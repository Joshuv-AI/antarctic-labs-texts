Anticodes in the Sum-Rank Metric
Eimear Byrne, Heide Gluesing-Luerssen, Alberto Ravagnani

Abstract
We study the structure of anticodes in the sum-rank metric for arbitrary fields and matrix blocks of arbitrary sizes. Our main result is a complete classification of optimal linear anticodes. We also compare the cardinality of the ball in the sum-rank metric with that of an optimal linear anticode, showing that the latter is strictly larger over sufficiently large finite fields. Finally, we give examples of parameters for which the largest anticode is neither a ball nor a linear anticode.

Introduction

Codes for the sum-rank metric have been proposed in several communication applications (cf.
[
8
]
,
[
9
]
,
[
12
]
,
[
14
]
). The question of code optimality in the sum-rank has been considered in
[
4
,
10
]
and the references therein.

In this note we will study anticodes in the sum-rank metric. More precisely, we consider the space

\bigoplus_{i=1}^{t}\mathbb{F}^{n_{i}\times m_{i}}
with an arbitrary field
\mathbb{F}
, and where the
sum-rank
of an element
(X_{1},...,X_{t})

is the sum of the ranks of the matrices
X_{i}
.
The resulting distance function generalizes both the Hamming metric (
n_{i}=m_{i}=1
) and the rank metric (
t=1
).
This hybrid nature is reflected in the classification result of optimal linear anticodes presented later in this note.

In a metric space, an
r
-
anticode
is a set of elements whose pairwise distances are upper bounded by
r
.
Anticodes naturally arise in the theory of error-correcting codes in two contexts:
the
Code-Anticode Bound

[
6
,
1
]
and for
generalized weights

[
13
]
.
In both of these topics, optimal anticodes play an important role.

The Code-Anticode Bound states that a code
C
of minimum distance
d
and a
(d-1)
-anticode
A
, both contained in a suitable
ambient space
V
, satisfy
|C||A|\leq|V|
.
This gives an upper bound on
|C|
, and the larger the cardinality of
A
, the tighter the bound is. Nonlinear anticodes for the Hamming metric were studied in a number of papers (cf.
[
2
,
1
]
), wherein the authors describe codes meeting the Code-Anticode Bound in the case of an optimal anticode as being
d
-
diameter perfect
.
Moreover, the authors establish the size of an optimal anticode as a consequence of their Diametric Theorem.

In
[
13
]
, the generalized Hamming and rank weights of a linear code
C
are defined in terms
of the dimensions of intersections of
C
with optimal linear anticodes.
This approach leads to duality results for generalized weights
[
13
]
and can be utilized to
characterize different classes of extremal codes
[
3
]
.

A natural question about anticodes is the classification of the optimal linear ones.
An optimal linear
r
-anticode over a field
\mathbb{F}
is a linear
r
-anticode whose dimension is maximal over all linear
r
-anticodes over
\mathbb{F}
.
In the Hamming metric, such linear spaces are isomorphic to
\bigoplus_{i=1}^{r}\mathbb{F}
(unless
|\mathbb{F}|=2
); see
[
13
]
.
In the rank metric on
\mathbb{F}^{n\times m}
, the optimal
r
-anticodes are the matrix spaces whose members have column
spaces contained in a fixed
r
-dimensional subspace
U\subseteq\mathbb{F}^{n}
if
n<m
, while for
n=m
their transposed spaces are also optimal
[
11
]
.

After the preliminaries, we provide in
Section

3
a classification of optimal linear anticodes in the sum-rank metric.
We show that any optimal linear anticode is the direct product of (a) optimal
anticodes in the rank metric (a certain number of which are necessarily full matrix spaces) and (b) an optimal anticode in the Hamming metric.
Conversely, we also characterize which of these products are indeed optimal linear anticodes.
For the precise formulation see
Corollary

3.8
.

In
Section

4
we turn to nonlinear anticodes over finite fields and the Code-Anticode Bound.
The latter generalizes the Sphere-Packing Bound by replacing the sphere of radius
r
with an optimal
2r
-anticode.
For example, in the Hamming metric the Singleton bound is sharper than the Hamming bound when the size of
the sphere of radius
r
is exceeded by that of an optimal linear
2r
-anticode (which is the case for sufficiently large field size).
In Section
4
, we consider this question for sum-rank metric codes and show that, for sufficiently
large field size, the sum-rank sphere of radius
r
is smaller than an optimal linear
2r
-anticode.

We conclude the paper by providing an example where the largest (possibly non-linear) anticodes in the sum-rank metric are, in general, neither the spheres nor the optimal linear anticodes.

2
Sum-Rank Metric Anticodes

Throughout the paper,
\mathbb{F}
denotes an arbitrary field. For
i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}=\{0,1,2,\ldots\}
we define
[i]=\{j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\mid 1\leq j\leq i\}
.
In order to define the ambient space for our sum-rank metric codes we fix positive integers
t
,
n_{1},\ldots,n_{t}
, and
m_{1},\ldots,m_{t}
that satisfy

(2.1)

n_{i}\leq m_{i}\ \text{ for all }\ i\in[t]\ \text{ and }\ m_{1}\geq\cdots\geq m_{t}.

We also set

(2.2)

N:=n_{1}+\ldots+n_{t}\ \text{ and }\ M:=m_{1}+\ldots+m_{t}.

The
sum-rank metric space
is the product of the
t
matrix spaces
\mathbb{F}^{n_{i}\times m_{i}}
, that is,

(2.3)

\Pi:=\Pi(n_{1}\times m_{1},\ldots,n_{t}\times m_{t}):=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{t}\mathbb{F}^{n_{i}\times m_{i}}.

The
sum-rank
of an element
X=(X_{1},...,X_{t})\in\Pi
is

\textnormal{srk}(X):=\sum_{i=1}^{t}\textnormal{rk}(X_{i}).

Note that
srk
induces a metric on
\Pi
via
(X,Y)\longmapsto\textnormal{srk}(X-Y)
.
In this paper we study anticodes in the sum-rank metric, which are defined as follows.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
0\leq r\leq N
be an integer. A (
sum-rank metric
)
r
-anticode
is a non-empty subset
A\subseteq\Pi
such that

\textnormal{srk}(X-Y)\leq r
for all
X,Y\in A
. We say that
A
is
linear
if it is an
\mathbb{F}
-linear subspace of
\Pi
. In that case we write
A\leq\Pi
.

By definition,
A\subseteq\Pi
is a
0
-anticode if and only if
|A|=1
.
Sum-rank metric anticodes may also be regarded as sets of
(N\times M)
-matrices supported on a particular profile.
More precisely, for any subset
\mathcal{P}\subseteq[N]\times[M]
, where
N,\,M
are as in (
2.2
), define
\mathbb{F}^{N\times M}[\mathcal{P}]
as the space of
(N\times M)
-matrices supported on
\mathcal{P}
(i.e., whose nonzero entries all have indices in
\mathcal{P}
).
If we define now
n_{0}=m_{0}=0
and

(2.4)

N_{i}=\sum_{j=0}^{i}n_{j},\ M_{i}=\sum_{j=0}^{i}m_{j},\ \mathcal{P}^{i}=[N_{i-1}+1,N_{i}]\times[M_{i-1}+1,M_{i}],\ \text{ and }\mathcal{P}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{t}\mathcal{P}^{i},

then
\mathbb{F}^{N\times M}[\mathcal{P}]
is the space of
(N\times M)
-block-diagonal matrices with
(n_{i}\times m_{i})
-blocks on the diagonal;
see also Figure
1
in Section
3
.
This provides us with an
\mathbb{F}
-linear isomorphism

(2.5)

\psi:\Pi\longrightarrow\mathbb{F}^{N\times M}[\mathcal{P}],\hskip 9.24994pt(X_{1},\ldots,X_{t})\longmapsto\begin{pmatrix}X_{1}&&\\
&\ddots&\\
&&X_{t}\end{pmatrix},

that satisfies
\textnormal{rk}(\psi(X))=\textnormal{srk}(X)
for all
X\in\Pi
.
Thus
\psi
is a linear isometry between the metric spaces
(\Pi,\textnormal{srk})
and
(\mathbb{F}^{N\times M}[\mathcal{P}],\textnormal{rk})
.
This isometry will be very useful in the next section when characterizing optimal anticodes.

Our first result is an upper bound on the dimension, and thus cardinality, of a
linear
anticode.
In Section
4
we will see that this bound is not true for anticodes that are not necessarily linear.

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
0\leq r\leq N
be an integer and set

\mathcal{U}_{r}=\bigg\{(u_{1},\ldots,u_{t})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{t}\,\bigg|\,0\leq u_{i}\leq n_{i}\mbox{ for all $i$ and }\sum_{i=1}^{t}u_{i}=r\bigg\}.

Then any linear
r
-anticode
C\leq\Pi
satisfies

\dim(C)\leq\max\bigg\{\sum_{i=1}^{t}m_{i}u_{i}\,\bigg|\,(u_{1},...,u_{t})\in\mathcal{U}_{r}\bigg\}.

Proof.

By (
2.5
) the space
\psi(C)
is a linear
r
-anticode in
\mathbb{F}^{N\times M}[\mathcal{P}]
with respect to the rank metric. Moreover,

C
and
\psi(C)
have the same cardinality.
Since
n_{i}\leq m_{i}
for all
i\in[t]
, we obtain from
[
7
, Theorem 46]
that this dimension cannot exceed
the stated maximum.
∎

Definition 2.3
.

We call a linear anticode
optimal
if it attains the bound in
Theorem

2.2
with equality.

We wish to point out that in
[
5
]
a weaker notion of anticode optimality is introduced.
The authors also describe explicitly their optimal anticodes.
The result is similar in nature to ours in the next section.

Clearly, anticodes in the sum-rank metric generalize those in the rank metric (the case
t=1
) and in the Hamming metric
(the case
n_{i}=m_{i}=1
for all
i\in[t]
).
In those instances, the optimal linear anticodes have been described explicitly.
We briefly survey these results.

Notation 2.4
.

Let
n,m\geq 1
be integers and let
U\leq\mathbb{F}^{n}
and
V\leq\mathbb{F}^{m}
be subspaces.
We denote by
\mathbb{F}^{{n}\times{m}}({U},{\rm c})
the space of matrices
X\in\mathbb{F}^{n\times m}
whose column space is contained in
U
. Similarly, we let
\mathbb{F}^{{n}\times{m}}({V},{\rm r})
denote space of matrices
X\in\mathbb{F}^{n\times m}
whose row space is contained in
V
.

It is not difficult to see that the spaces
\mathbb{F}^{{n}\times{m}}({U},{\rm c})
and
\mathbb{F}^{{n}\times{m}}({V},{\rm r})
in Notation
2.4
have dimension

m\dim(U)
and
n\dim(V)
, respectively.

The following result has been proven by Meshulam in
[
11
]
for square matrices,
but it is easy to verify that it is equally true for general rectangular matrices.

Theorem 2.5

(
[
11
, Thm. 3]
)
.

Let
m\geq n\geq 1
and
0\leq r\leq n
be integers.
Let
A\leq\mathbb{F}^{n\times m}
be an optimal linear
r
-anticode.
Then there exists an
r
-dimensional subspace
U\leq\mathbb{F}^{n}
such that
A=\mbox{$\mathbb{F}^{{n}\times{m}}({U},{\rm c})$}
or, if
n=m
,

A=\mbox{$\mathbb{F}^{{n}\times{m}}({U},{\rm r})$}
.

The next result from
[
13
]
describes optimal linear anticodes in the Hamming metric over fields with at least 3 elements.
In that paper the result has been stated for finite fields,
but the proof (and the proof of the accompanying lemma) shows that it is true for arbitrary fields.

Theorem 2.6

(
[
13
, Prop. 9]
)
.

Suppose that
|\mathbb{F}|\geq 3
and let
A\leq\mathbb{F}^{t}
be an optimal
r
-anticode in the Hamming metric.
Then
\dim(A)=r
(see also Theorem
2.2
) and there exists a subset
\mathcal{I}\subseteq[t]
such that
|\mathcal{I}|=r
and

A=A_{1}\oplus\ldots\oplus A_{t}
, where
A_{i}=\mathbb{F}
if
i\in\mathcal{I}
and
A_{i}=\{0\}
otherwise.
Note that, trivially, each
A_{i}
is an optimal anticode in
\mathbb{F}
.

The simple example
A=\mbox{$\langle{(1,0,1),(0,1,1)}\rangle$}
shows that the previous result is not true for the binary field
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{F}_{2}
.

3
Optimal Linear Anticodes

In this section we give a complete classification of optimal linear anticodes
A\leq\Pi
over any field
\mathbb{F}
.
We prove that every such anticode is the direct product of optimal rank-metric anticodes and an optimal Hamming-metric anticode.
More precisely, the following holds.
Recall the notation from (
2.1
)–(
2.2
).

Theorem 3.1
.

Suppose
m_{1}\geq\ldots\geq m_{s}>m_{s+1}=\ldots=m_{t}=1
, where the case
s=t
, thus
m_{i}>1
for all
i
, is allowed.
Let
A\leq\Pi
be an optimal linear anticode.
Then
A=A_{1}\oplus\ldots\oplus A_{s}\oplus A^{\sf h}
, where
A_{i}
is an optimal linear anticode in
\mathbb{F}^{n_{i}\times m_{i}}
for
i\in[s]
and

A^{\sf h}\leq\mathbb{F}^{1\times(t-s)}
is an optimal linear anticode with respect to the Hamming metric.
In particular, if
|\mathbb{F}|\geq 3
then
A^{\sf h}=A^{\sf h}_{1}\oplus\ldots\oplus A^{\sf h}_{t-s}
, where each
A^{\sf h}_{i}\in\{\mathbb{F},\{0\}\}
.

Before we turn to the proof we present a numerical lemma that describes the maximum in Theorem
2.2
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
0\leq r<N
and set
K:=\max\{\sum_{i=1}^{t}m_{i}u_{i}\mid(u_{1},\ldots,u_{t})\in\mathcal{U}_{r}\}
.
Let
j\in[t]
and
\delta\in\{0,\ldots,n_{j}-1\}

be the unique integers such that
r=\sum_{i=1}^{j-1}n_{i}+\delta
.
Then the following hold.

(a)

K=\sum_{i=1}^{j-1}m_{i}n_{i}+m_{j}\delta
.

(b)

Let
\ell,\ell^{\prime}
be the unique integers such that

m_{1}\geq\ldots\geq m_{\ell-1}>m_{\ell}=\ldots=m_{j}=\ldots=m_{\ell^{\prime}}>m_{\ell^{\prime}+1}\geq\ldots\geq m_{t}

and let
(u_{1},\ldots,u_{t})\in\mathcal{U}_{r}
. Then
\sum_{i=1}^{t}m_{i}u_{i}=K
if and only if

(i)

u_{i}=n_{i}
for
i<\ell
,

(ii)

\sum_{i=\ell}^{\ell^{\prime}}u_{i}=\sum_{i=\ell}^{j-1}n_{i}+\delta
,

(iii)

u_{i}=0
for
i>\ell^{\prime}
.

Proof.

Part (a) is
[
4
, Lemma 3.12]
. Let us prove part (b).
It is easy to see that if
(u_{1},\ldots,u_{t})\in\mathcal{U}_{r}
satisfies (i)–(iii), then
\sum_{i=1}^{t}m_{i}u_{i}=K
.
Thus, let
(u_{1},\ldots,u_{t})\in\mathcal{U}_{r}
be such that
\sum_{i=1}^{t}m_{i}u_{i}=K
.
We first show (i).
Write
u_{i}=n_{i}-\alpha_{i}
for all
i\in[t]
.
Assume by contradiction that
\sum_{i=1}^{\ell-1}\alpha_{i}>0
.
This means in particular that
\ell\geq 2
and thus the set
\{m_{1},\ldots,m_{\ell-1}\}
is not empty.
Since each element in this set satisfies

m_{i}>m_{\ell}=m_{j}
,
we obtain the following strict inequality

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{j-1}m_{i}u_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{j-1}m_{i}n_{i}-\sum_{i=1}^{j-1}m_{i}\alpha_{i}

\displaystyle=K-m_{j}\delta-\sum_{i=1}^{\ell-1}m_{i}\alpha_{i}-m_{j}\sum_{i=\ell}^{j-1}\alpha_{i}

\displaystyle<K-m_{j}(\delta+\sum_{i=1}^{j-1}\alpha_{i}).

As a consequence
m_{j}\sum_{i=j}^{t}u_{i}\geq\sum_{i=j}^{t}m_{i}u_{i}=K-\sum_{i=1}^{j-1}m_{i}u_{i}>m_{j}(\delta+\sum_{i=1}^{j-1}\alpha_{i})
and thus

\sum_{i=j}^{t}u_{i}>\delta+\sum_{i=1}^{j-1}\alpha_{i}
, which in turn yields

r=\sum_{i=1}^{t}u_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{j-1}(n_{i}-\alpha_{i})+\sum_{i=j}^{t}u_{i}>\sum_{i=1}^{j-1}(n_{i}-\alpha_{i})+\delta+\sum_{i=1}^{j-1}\alpha_{i}=r,

which is a contradiction. This proves (i).
Next, part (i) along with
\sum_{i=1}^{t}u_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{j-1}n_{i}+\delta
implies

(3.1)

\sum_{i=\ell}^{t}u_{i}=\sum_{i=\ell}^{j-1}n_{i}+\delta.

Now the assumption
K=\sum_{i=1}^{t}m_{i}u_{i}
together with (
3.1
) and (a) yields

K=\sum_{i=1}^{t}m_{i}u_{i}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{\ell-1}m_{i}n_{i}+m_{\ell}\sum_{i=\ell}^{t}u_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell-1}m_{i}n_{i}+m_{\ell}\sum_{i=\ell}^{j-1}n_{i}+m_{\ell}\delta=K.

As a consequence, the inequality is actually an equality, and this means
\sum_{i=\ell}^{t}(m_{\ell}-m_{i})u_{i}=0
.
Since all terms in this sum are non-negative and
m_{\ell}>m_{i}
for
i>\ell^{\prime}
, this implies
u_{i}=0
for
i>\ell^{\prime}
. This proves (iii) and now (ii) follows from (
3.1
).
∎

4
Nonlinear Sum-Rank Metric Anticodes

In this section we consider anticodes in the sum-rank metric that are not necessarily linear.
Since we have to measure the size of such anticodes by their cardinality, we restrict ourselves to codes over
finite
fields.
Thus from now on let
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{F}_{q}
be a finite field of order
q
.

In classical coding theory, a strong motivation for studying anticodes comes from the Code-Anticode Bound for distance-regular graphs. We prove a version of this result for linear spaces endowed with translation-invariant metrics.

Lemma 4.1

(
Code-Anticode Bound
)
.

Let
V
be a vector space over
\mathbb{F}
and let
\mbox{dist}:V\times V\to\mathbb{R}

be a translation-invariant distance function. Let

d
be a positive integer and let
C,A\subseteq V
be subsets with the following properties:
\mbox{dist}(X,Y)\geq d
for all distinct
X,Y\in C
and
\mbox{dist}(X,Y)\leq d-1
for all
X,Y\in A
.
Then
|C||A|\leq|V|
.

Proof.

The lemma is trivial if
|A|=1
or
|C|=1
. We henceforth assume
|A|,|C|\geq 2
.
For each
X\in V
define
X+A:=\{X+X^{\prime}\mid X^{\prime}\in A\}
. Then for any choice of distinct
X,Y\in C
the sets
X+A
and
Y+A
are disjoint.
Indeed, for any
X^{\prime},Y^{\prime}\in A
we have
X+X^{\prime}=Y+Y^{\prime}
if and only if
Y-X=X^{\prime}-Y^{\prime}
.
Translation invariance of the distance implies
{\rm dist}(X,Y)={\rm dist}(0,Y-X)={\rm dist}(0,X^{\prime}-Y^{\prime})={\rm dist}(Y^{\prime},X^{\prime})\leq d-1
, which contradicts our assumption on
C
.
It follows that
|C||A|=|\bigcup_{X\in\mathcal{C}}(X+A)|\leq|V|.
∎

A set
C
as in the previous lemma is called a
d
-code. In the sum-rank metric, this specializes to the following notion.

Definition 4.2
.

Let
1\leq d\leq N
be an integer. A (
sum-rank metric
)
d
-code
is a non-empty subset
C\subseteq\Pi
such that

\textnormal{srk}(X-Y)\geq d
for all distinct
X,Y\in C
. We say that
C
is
linear
if it is an
\mathbb{F}
-linear subspace of
\Pi
. In that case we write
C\leq\Pi
. When
|C|\geq 2
, we let the
sum-rank distance
of
C
be the integer

\textnormal{srk}(C):=\min\{\textnormal{srk}(X-Y)\mid X,Y\in C,\,X\neq Y\}
.

The Code-Anticode Bound is most commonly stated for distance-regular graphs, where
a code is a subset of the vertices and the (
geodesic
)
distance
between a pair of vertices is the minimum length of a path joining them. In that context, the Code-Anticode Bound is a special case of a result of Delsarte on association schemes;
see
[
1
,
6
]
.
The graph
\Gamma
is generally constructed from a finite metric space
(V,\mbox{dist})
taking as
V
the set of vertices, declaring
v,w
adjacent if
\mbox{dist}(v,w)=1
, and checking that for all
v,w\in V
the geodesic distance between
v
and
w
coincides with

\mbox{dist}(v,w)
.
We briefly consider this graph for the sum-rank metric.

Proposition 4.3
.

Define
\Gamma(\Pi)
to be the graph whose vertices are the distinct elements of
\Pi
and whose edges are the
pairs
(X,Y)\in\Pi
satisfying
\textnormal{srk}(X-Y)=1
. Then the geodesic distance in
\Gamma(\Pi)
coincides with the sum-rank distance.

Proof.

Let
X=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{t}),\,Y=(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{t})\in\Pi
be such that

\textnormal{rk}(X_{i}-Y_{i})=r_{i}
and
r=\sum_{i=1}^{t}r_{i}
, thus
\textnormal{srk}(X-Y)=r
.
Then there exist
r_{i}
(and no fewer) matrices
A_{ij}
of rank 1 satisfying
X_{i}-Y_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{r_{i}}A_{ij}
.
It follows that

X-Y
can be written as a sum of
r
matrices in
\Pi
of sum-rank 1, and
r
is the minimum number of sum-rank-1
matrices needed.
∎

It is interesting to note that our version of the Code-Anticode Bound does not rely on the distance regularity of the underlying graph.
Indeed,
\Gamma(\Pi)
is not distance regular, as the next example shows.
Recall that a graph
\Gamma
with geodesic distance
\gamma:\Gamma\times\Gamma\to\mathbb{N}_{0}
is
distance-regular
if

\big|\{v\in\Gamma\mid\gamma(v,a)=i,\,\gamma(v,b)=j\}\big|\;\text{ depends only on $i,j$, and $\gamma(a,b)$}.

Example 4.4
.

Let
\Pi=\mathbb{F}_{2}^{2\times 2}\oplus\mathbb{F}_{2}^{2\times 2}
and let
A,B\in\Pi
be the matrices

A=\left(\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}\right),\hskip 9.24994ptB=\left(\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}\right).

Then
\textnormal{srk}(A)=\textnormal{srk}(B)=2
, and one can check that
\big|\{Z\in\Pi\mid\textnormal{srk}(Z-A)=2,\,\textnormal{srk}(Z)=1\}\big|=3
, whereas

\big|\{Z\in\Pi\mid\textnormal{srk}(Z-B)=2,\,\textnormal{srk}(Z)=1\}\big|=8
.

The Singleton Bound and the Sphere-Packing Bound derived in
[
4
, Theorems 3.2 and 3.6]
are special instances of the
Code-Anticode Bound of Lemma
4.1
.
We first present these bounds.

Theorem 4.5

(
[
4
, Theorems 3.2 and 3.6]
)
.

Let
C\subseteq\Pi
be a code with
|C|\geq 2
and
\textnormal{srk}(C)=d
.

(a)

(
Singleton Bound
) Let
j
and
\delta
be the unique integers such that
d-1=\sum_{i=1}^{j-1}n_{i}+\delta
and
0\leq\delta\leq n_{j}-1
.
Then

|C|\leq q^{\sum_{i=j}^{t}m_{i}n_{i}-m_{j}\delta}.

Codes meeting the Singleton Bound are called
MSRD
.

(b)

(
Sphere-Packing Bound
) Let
r=\lfloor(d-1)/2\rfloor
. Then

|C|\leq\bigg\lfloor\frac{|\Pi|}{V_{r}(\Pi)}\bigg\rfloor,

where

(4.1)

V_{r}(\Pi):=\sum_{s=0}^{r}\ \sum_{(s_{1},...,s_{t})\in\mathcal{U}_{s}}\ \prod_{i=1}^{t}\left[\begin{matrix}n_{i}\\
s_{i}\end{matrix}\right]_{q}\prod_{j=0}^{s_{i}-1}(q^{m_{i}}-q^{j})

is the size of any sphere in
\Pi
of sum-rank radius
r

[
4
, Lem. 3.5]
. Codes meeting this bound are called
perfect
.

Clearly, the Sphere-Packing Bound is an instance of the Code-Anticode Bound because the ball of radius
\lfloor(d-1)/2\rfloor

gives the required
(d-1)
-anticode in Lemma
4.1
.

Proposition 4.6
.

The Singleton Bound is an instance of the Code-Anticode Bound of Lemma
4.1
.

Proof.

Recall the notation from (
2.3
) and the sets
\mathcal{U}_{r}
from
Theorem

2.2
.
For each
u\in\mathcal{U}_{d-1}
, define the projection

\tau_{u}:\Pi\longrightarrow\Pi\big((n_{1}-u_{1})\times m_{1},...,(n_{t}-u_{t})\times m_{t}\big),\hskip 18.49988ptX\longmapsto(X^{\prime}_{1},...,X^{\prime}_{t}),

where
X^{\prime}_{i}
is obtained from

X_{i}\in\mathbb{F}^{n_{i}\times m_{i}}
by deleting its last
u_{i}
rows. The kernel of this map is a (linear)
(d-1)
-anticode
A(u)
and so by Lemma
4.1
, we have
|\mathcal{C}||A(u)|\leq|\Pi|
for any code
C\subseteq\Pi
with
\textnormal{srk}(C)=d
and any choice of
u\in\mathcal{U}_{d-1}
. The Singleton Bound is obtained by choosing
u
such that
|A(u)|=q^{\sum_{i=1}^{t}u_{i}m_{i}}
is maximal.
By
Lemma

3.2
(a) this is the case for

u=(n_{1},...,n_{j-1},\delta,0,...,0)
for
j\in[t]
and
0\leq\delta<n_{j}
as in
Theorem

4.5
(a).
∎

Note that the maximal linear
(d-1)
-anticode appearing in the proof of the above result is the linear space

\mathcal{A}(n_{1},...,n_{j-1},\delta,0,...,0)=\Big(\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.5}{$\oplus$}}}_{i=1}^{j-1}\mathbb{F}^{n_{i}\times m_{i}}\Big)\oplus\mathbb{F}^{\delta\times m_{j}}.

The Sphere-Packing Bound is sharper than the Singleton Bound precisely for those parameters for which the size of an optimal linear
(d-1)
-anticode is less than the size of the sphere of radius
\lfloor(d-1)/2\rfloor
. In the remainder of the paper we compare the sizes of these two anticodes.
We first show that for sufficiently large field size
q
an optimal linear
r
-anticode is larger than the ball of radius
\lfloor r/2\rfloor
.

Proposition 4.7
.

Let
1\leq r\leq N
be an integer.
For sufficiently large
q
, the cardinality of the sum-rank sphere of radius
\lfloor r/2\rfloor
in
\Pi
is exceeded by the size of an optimal linear
r
-anticode.

Proof.

The result is clear if
r=1
. We henceforth assume
r\geq 2
and denote by
K
the dimension of an optimal linear

r
-anticode in
\Pi
.
Lemma
3.2
tells us that

(4.2)

K=\max\!\big\{\sum_{i=1}^{t}u_{i}m_{i}\,\big|\,u\in\mathcal{U}_{r}\big\}\ \text{ and }\ K>\sum_{i=1}^{t}u_{i}m_{i}\text{ for all }u\in\bigcup_{a=0}^{r-1}\mathcal{U}_{a}.

Let us now turn to the ball.
We can write the size of the ball of sum-rank radius
\rho:=\lfloor r/2\rfloor
in (
4.1
) as

V_{\rho}(\Pi)=\sum_{s=0}^{\rho}\ \sum_{(s_{1},...,s_{t})\in\mathcal{U}_{s}}S(s_{1},...,s_{t}),

where
S(s_{1},...,s_{t})=\prod_{i=1}^{t}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{1}{n_i}{s_i}_{q}\prod_{j=0}^{s_{i}-1}(q^{m_{i}}-q^{j})
, which is the cardinality of the set
of tuples
(X_{1},\ldots,X_{t})
in
\Pi
satisfying
\textnormal{rk}(X_{i})=s_{i}
for each
i\in[t]
.
It is straightforward to check that
S(s_{1},...,s_{t})\sim q^{\sum_{i=1}^{t}s_{i}(m_{i}+n_{i}-s_{i})}
as
q\to\infty
.
Therefore,
V_{\rho}(\Pi)\in\mathcal{O}\left(q^{\ell(\rho)}\right)
in Bachmann-Landau notation, where

\ell(\rho)=\max\bigg\{\sum_{i=1}^{t}s_{i}(m_{i}+n_{i}-s_{i})\,\bigg|\,(s_{1},...,s_{t})\in{\mathcal{U}}_{s},\,0\leq s\leq\rho\bigg\}.

Since
r\geq 2
by assumption, the maximum is clearly attained for some
s\in\{1,\ldots,\rho\}
.
Fix now any
s\in\{1,\ldots,\rho\}
and
(s_{1},...,s_{t})\in\mathcal{U}_{s}
.
Set
r_{i}=\min\{n_{i},2s_{i}\}
for
i\in[t]
. Then

(4.3)

\sum_{i=1}^{t}r_{i}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{t}2s_{i}\leq 2s\leq r.

Hence
(r_{1},\ldots,r_{t})\in\mathcal{U}_{a}
for some
a\leq r
. Furthermore we have

(4.4)

\left.\begin{array}[]{ccccl}s_{i}(m_{i}+n_{i}-s_{i})&\leq&n_{i}m_{i}&\text{ and }&[s_{i}(m_{i}+n_{i}-s_{i})=n_{i}m_{i}\Longleftrightarrow s_{i}=n_{i}],\\[1.93748pt]
s_{i}(m_{i}+n_{i}-s_{i})&\leq&2s_{i}m_{i}&\text{ and }&[s_{i}(m_{i}+n_{i}-s_{i})=2s_{i}m_{i}\Longleftrightarrow s_{i}=0].\end{array}\hskip 9.24994pt\right\}

The first inequality follows from maximizing the function
f(x)=x(m_{i}+n_{i}-x)

on the interval
[0,n_{i}]
, while recalling
m_{i}\geq n_{i}
, the second inequality is clear.
Note that since
\sum_{i=1}^{t}s_{i}=s\leq\rho<N
, there is at least one
i
such that
s_{i}<n_{i}
.

Case 1:
Suppose
0<s_{\lambda}<n_{\lambda}
for at least one
\lambda
.
Then
s_{\lambda}(m_{\lambda}+n_{\lambda}-s_{\lambda})<r_{\lambda}m_{\lambda}
, and
(
4.2
) – (
4.4
) imply

\sum_{i=1}^{t}s_{i}(m_{i}+n_{i}-s_{i})<\sum_{i=1}^{t}r_{i}m_{i}\leq K.

Case 2:
Suppose
s_{i}\in\{0,n_{i}\}
for all
i\in[t]
. Set
\mathcal{I}=\{i\mid s_{i}=n_{i}\}
. Then
\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}n_{i}=s\leq\rho
and

\sum_{i=1}^{t}s_{i}(m_{i}+n_{i}-s_{i})=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}n_{i}m_{i}<K

thanks to (
4.2
).
Hence we showed that in either case the ball is strictly smaller than the optimal linear anticode.
∎

The previous result implies that for sufficiently large
q
, the Code-Anticode Bound is sharper when the chosen anticode is an optimal linear anticode, rather than the sum-rank ball of the appropriate radius. For small values of
q
, this does not necessarily hold, as the following example shows.

Example 4.8
.

(a)

Let
\Pi=\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.5}{$\oplus$}}}_{i=1}^{3}\mathbb{F}_{2}^{2\times 2}
. Then

V_{2}(\Pi)=289
, while the size of an optimal linear
4
-anticode in
\Pi

is 256.

(b)

Now let
\Pi=\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.5}{$\oplus$}}}_{i=1}^{3}\mathbb{F}_{3}^{2\times 2}
. Then
V_{2}(\Pi)=3313
, while
the size of an optimal linear
4
-anticode in
\Pi
is 6561.

(c)

Finally, let
\Pi=\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.5}{$\oplus$}}}_{i=1}^{4}\mathbb{F}_{2}^{2\times 2}
.
Then
V_{2}(\Pi)=547
, while the size of an optimal linear
4
-anticode in
\Pi
is 256.

The Diametric Theorem of Ahlswede et al.
[
2
]
is reformulated in
[
1
, Theorem AK]

in order to show that the size of an optimal
r
-anticode in Hamming space equals the cardinality of the Cartesian product
of the Hamming ball of radius
r
in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n-\ell+2r}
and the optimal linear
(\ell-2r)
-anticode
\oplus_{j=1}^{\ell-2r}\mathbb{F}_{q}

for a suitable
\ell
.
For the sum-rank metric the following example suggests a similar behavior: the direct product of a suitable ball and full matrix spaces leads to a nonlinear anticode with larger cardinality than both the ball and the optimal linear anticode.

Example 4.9
.

Let
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{F}_{3}
and
\Pi=\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.5}{$\oplus$}}}_{i=1}^{7}\mathbb{F}^{2\times 2}
and
r=8
.
Denote by
B_{s}(k)
the ball of radius
s
in
\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.5}{$\oplus$}}}_{i=1}^{k}\mathbb{F}^{2\times 2}
.
We have the following
8
-anticodes, with
A_{0}
being the optimal linear
8
-anticode and
A_{4}
being the ball of radius
4
:

\begin{array}[]{ll}A_{0}=\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.5}{$\oplus$}}}_{i=1}^{4}\mathbb{F}^{2\times 2},&|A_{0}|=43,046,721,\\[3.87498pt]
A_{1}=B_{1}(4)\oplus\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.5}{$\oplus$}}}_{i=1}^{3}\mathbb{F}^{2\times 2},&|A_{1}|=68,555,889,\\[3.87498pt]
A_{2}=B_{2}(5)\oplus\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.5}{$\oplus$}}}_{i=1}^{2}\mathbb{F}^{2\times 2},&|A_{2}|=69,815,602,\\[3.87498pt]
A_{3}=B_{3}(6)\oplus\mathbb{F}^{2\times 2},&|A_{3}|=58,099,761,\\[3.87498pt]

Thus the “hybrid” anticodes
A_{1},\,A_{2},\,A_{3}
are strictly larger than the optimal linear anticode and the ball of radius

, and

is largest among those considered.
We also note that over the field

, the ball

\mathbb{F}_{4}

The explicit description of optimal (nonlinear) anticodes in the sum-rank metric appears to be an interesting open problem. We leave this to future research.
