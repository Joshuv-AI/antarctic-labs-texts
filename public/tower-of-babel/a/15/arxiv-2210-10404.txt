On the gain of entrainment in the n n -dimensional ribosome flow model
Ron Ofir and Thomas Kriecherbauer and Lars Grüne and Michael Margaliot

Abstract
The ribosome flow model (RFM) is a phenomenological model for the flow of particles along a 1D chain of n n sites. It has been extensively used to study ribosome flow along the mRNA molecule during translation. When the transition rates along the chain are time-varying and jointly T T -periodic the RFM entrains, i.e., every trajectory of the RFM converges to a unique T T -periodic solution that depends on the transition rates, but not on the initial condition. In general, entrainment to periodic excitations like the 24h solar day or the 50Hz frequency of the electric grid is important in numerous natural and artificial systems. An interesting question, called the gain of entrainment (GOE) in the RFM, is whether proper coordination of the periodic translation rates along the mRNA can lead to a larger average protein production rate. Analyzing the GOE in the RFM is non-trivial and partial results exist only for the RFM with dimensions n = 1 , 2 n=1,2 . We use a new approach to derive several results on the GOE in the general n n -dimensional RFM. Perhaps surprisingly, we rigorously characterize several cases where there is no GOE, so to maximize the average production rate in these cases, the best choice is to use constant transition rates all along the chain.



I
Introduction

The totally asymmetric
simple exclusion process (TASEP) is a
fundamental model in statistical mechanics
[
6
,
27
,
53
]
.
The basic version of
TASEP includes
n
sites, ordered along a 1D lattice, and particles that hop randomly in a unidirectional manner, that is, from site
i
to site
i+1
. Each site can be either empty or include a particle,
and a particle can only hop into an empty site. This generates an intricate coupling between the particles.
In particular, if a particle is “stuck” in a site for a long time then particles will accumulate in the sites behind it, thus generating a “traffic jam” of particles.

TASEP has been used to model the flow of ribosomes along the mRNA
[
63
]
and other processes of
intracellular transport
[
7
]
, pedestrian and vehicular traffic
[
20
]
, the movement of ants, and numerous other natural and artificial phenomena
[
53
]
.
However, rigorous analysis of TASEP is difficult and closed-form results are known only for very
special cases of transition rates along the lattice
[
6
]
.

The ribosome flow model (RFM) is the mean-field approximation of TASEP
[
49
]
. The RFM is a deterministic model composed of
n
non-linear, first-order ODEs.
The RFM is highly amenable to analysis using tools from systems and control theory: the RFM is a contracting system
[
2
]
, a cooperative system
[
34
]
,
and, moreover, it is also a totally positive differential system
[
33
]
.
The RFM can also be interpreted as a compartmental chemical reaction network (CRN)
with transition rates that depend on the amount
of particles
and free space in various compartments
[
57
]
. The RFM can also be derived via a special finite volume spatial discretization of widely used hyperbolic PDE
flow models
[
56
]
, and can also be represented as a port-Hamiltonian system
[
59
]
.

Understanding the regulation and dynamics of ribosome flow and, consequently, protein production rate is a fundamental problem in biology and medicine.
For example, “traffic jams” of ribosomes along the mRNA
have been implicated with various diseases
[
55
,
10
]
. Rigorous analysis of ribosome flow
is also important in scientific
fields that include manipulations of

mRNA viruses, and biotechnology.

The RFM and its variants has been extensively used to model and analyze the flow of ribosomes along the mRNA during translation (see, e.g.,
[

,

,

,

,

,

,
35
,

,

,

,

More recently, networks of interconnected RFMs have been used to model and analyze large-scale translation in the cell, and the effect of competition for shared resources like ribosomes and tRNA molecules
[

,

,

,
23
,

.
Models based on networks of RFMs

If we view the rates as a periodic excitation (e.g., due to the periodic cell-cycle process) then the

The cell-cycle is a periodic program that controls DNA synthesis and cell division.
Proper execution

[

The eukaryotic cell cycle is controlled by periodic gene expression
[

.
There is growing evidence that protein levels of cell-cycle related genes are regulated also via the translation machinery.
Ref.

[

Ref.
[

showed that the levels of 16 tRNA modifications, and thus the translation efficiencies of different codons oscillate during cell-cycle. Their results imply that translation regulation has a direct role in cell-cycle related oscillations.
Another possible regulation mechanism is via control of
the transcription rate of tRNA genes (and other genes), resulting in oscillations in intra-cellular tRNA
levels [12]. Since the decoding time of codons is affected by the available levels of the tRNA molecules
recognizing them, this may eventually lead to oscillations in the decoding times

A recent paper
[
60

used a sophisticated single-cell ribo-seq method
to show that
limitation for a particular amino acid causes ribosome pausing at a subset of the codons encoding the amino acid. However, this pausing is only observed in a sub-population of cells correlating to its cell cycle state.

Refs.
[
52
,
1
]
considered the gain of entrainment (GOE) in the RFM.
To explain the GOE problem, consider a general nonlinear
control system:

\displaystyle\dot{x}(t)

\displaystyle=f(x(t),u(t)),

\displaystyle y(t)

\displaystyle=g(x(t)),

(1)

where
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the state-vector,
u\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
is the input, and
y\in\mathbb{R}^{1}
is the scalar output. We assume that the output represents
a quantity that should be maximized (in some suitable sense). For example, in the context of biotechnology, the output may be the production rate of a desired protein.

A control
u
is called
T
-periodic if
u(t+T)=u(t)
for all
t\geq 0
.
Suppose that for any
T
-periodic control the system (
I
)
entrains, that is,
x
converges to a unique
T
-periodic solution
\gamma(t)
,
t\in[0,T)
, that depends on the control, but not on the initial condition
x(0)
. Then the output converges to the scalar
g(\gamma)
.
Now fix a
T
-periodic control
u
, and let

\displaystyle\overline{u}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}u(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t,

\displaystyle\overline{y}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}g(\gamma(t))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t,

that is, the averages of the input and the unique periodic output.
If we apply the constant
control
v(t):=\overline{u}
then the entrainment property implies that the state will converge to a unique equilibrium
e
,
that depends on
\overline{u}
, but not on the initial condition
x(0)
, and thus
y(t)
converges to
g(e)
. We say that the system admits a GOE for the input
u
if

\overline{y}>g(e).

In other words, we consider
two controls
u(t)
and
v(t):=\overline{u}
with the same average,
and compare the average of the corresponding asymptotic outputs. A GOE implies that entrainment does not only entail synchronization to periodic excitations, but also an
improvement in the average output
. This
general
property may be relevant in many fields including
protein production during the cell cycle division program,
seasonal cycles of infectious diseases
[
36
]
,
periodic fishery
[
5
]
,
and periodically-operated chemical reactors
[
54
]
.

Since periodic controls allow more flexibility than constant controls, one may intuitively expect that many systems admit GOE.
However, analysing the GOE in a nonlinear system is non-trivial. Many non-linear systems do not entrain at all, that is, the response to a periodic input is not necessarily convergence to a periodic solution (see, e.g.,
[
39
]
).
Even if the system does entrain, the periodic solution
\gamma(t)
(and its integral along a period) is
typically not known explicitly.

A dynamical system is called
contractive
if any two solutions approach each other at an exponential rate
[
8
,
2
]
.
Contractive systems entrain to periodic excitations
[
2
,
29
]
and also admit a well-defined
frequency response function
[
41
]
, but again this is not known explicitly. Thus, rigorous analysis of the GOE
is not immediate even for scalar contractive systems.
The next example demonstrates this in a simple synthetic system.

Example 1
.

Consider the scalar control system

\displaystyle\dot{x}(t)

\displaystyle=1-\frac{3}{2}\tanh(x(t))+u(t),

\displaystyle y(t)

\displaystyle=x(t).

Note that this is in the form in (
I
)
with
f(x,u)=1-\frac{3}{2}\tanh(x)+u
and
g(x)=x
.
Assume that
u(t)
takes values in
[-1,1]
. The state space of the dynamics is
\Omega:=\mathbb{R}_{+}
.
The Jacobian of the vector field is
J(x)=\frac{-3}{2\cosh^{2}(x)}
, so in particular
J(x)<0
for all
x\in\Omega
implying that the system is contractive and thus entrains to periodic excitations.
We consider two controls. The first is
u(t)=\sin(2\pi t)
that is
T
-periodic, with
T=1
, and has average
\overline{u}=0
, and the second is the constant control
v(t)\equiv\overline{u}=0
.
For the latter control the solution converges to the equilibrium point
e:=\tanh^{-1}(2/3)\approx 0.8047
, so the output converges to
g(e)=e
. Fig.
1
depicts the attracting periodic solution
\gamma(t)
,
t\in[0,1]
, for the control
u(t)
. Numerically computing

the average of this solution over a period yields
\overline{y}=\int_{0}^{1}\gamma(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t=0.8127

Periodic solution

as a function of

.

Analyzing GOE is closely related to
periodic optimal control
[
30
]
.
The basic idea is to pose the problem of
maximizing the average of the output,
along the periodic solution
, under an integral constraint on the control, namely,
\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}u(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t=\overline{u}
.
However, it seems that
the application of
standard analysis tools like Pontryagin’s maximum principle to this problem
provide explicit information only for low-dimensional systems, e.g., when
n=1

[
15
,
4
]
.
Another known approach for proving the optimality of equilibrium solutions is based on
passivity/dissipativity techniques
[
14
]
. However, so far for the RFM we have not been able to establish the kind of passivity/dissipativity that we would need for this kind of analysis. While dissipativity results for generalized RFMs exist
[
59
]
, it is not clear how they can be extended such that the transition rates play the role of the control, which would be needed for a GOE analysis with respect to periodic variations of these rates. Moreover, the integral constraints on the control cause technical difficulties.

Protein synthesis is known
to be one of the most energy consuming processes in the cell
[
28
]
. A GOE may make this process more efficient on average, and thus reduce the effective cost.
To study this problem in the context of translation, we develop a new approach to analyzing GOE in the
n
-dimensional RFM. This is based on integrating the differential equations and “higher-order moments” of these differential equations along the periodic solution.

It turns out that the algebraic derivations are simplified if we actually consider the differential equations for the centered variables obtained by subtracting from each state variable its equilibrium point corresponding to
the case of constant controls
u_{i}(t)\equiv\overline{u}_{i}
for all
i
. Using this, we prove several new results (see Theorem
1
below). For example, we show that for an
n
-dimensional RFM, with
n
odd, and equal
internal rates there
is no GOE.
To the best of our knowledge, these are the first analysis results on the GOE in a general
n
-dimensional RFM.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. The next section reviews the RFM and formally defines the GOE in the RFM.
Section
III
presents our main results. These show that in various
n
-dimensional RFMs there is no GOE.
The final section concludes the paper.

II
Preliminaries

In this section, we briefly review the RFM, and the problem of analyzing GOE.

II-A

The Ribosome Flow Model

The RFM
is a phenomenological
compartmental
model that includes
n
sites ordered along a 1D chain. Particles flow along the chain from left to right (i.e., from site
i
to site
i+1
).
The RFM includes
n
state variables
x_{i}(t)
,
i=1,\dots,n
.
Every state variable takes values in
[0,1]
, where
x_{i}(t)
represents the normalized density of particles at site
i
at time
t
. Thus,
x_{i}(t)=0

[
x_{i}(t)=1
] implies that site
i
is empty [completely full] at time
t
.
The state-space of the RFM is thus the
n
-dimensional unit cube
[0,1]^{n}
.

The RFM also includes
n
positive time-varying transition rates,
u_{0}(t),\dots,u_{n}(t)
,
where a large value of
u_{i}(t)
implies that it is relatively easy for a particle to move from site
i
to site
i+1
at time
t
.
In particular
u_{0}(t)
[
u_{n}(t)
]
is called the initiation rate [exit rate] at time
t
.
In the context of translation, every site corresponds to a group of
codons along the mRNA,
and
u_{i}(t)
models biophysical properties like the abundance of cognate tRNA molecules at time
t
.

The RFM consists of
n
deterministic first-order ODEs:

\dot{x}_{i}(t)=u_{i-1}(t)x_{i-1}(t)(1-x_{i}(t))-u_{i}(t)x_{i}(t)(1-x_{i+1}(t)),\quad i=1,\dots,n,

(2)

where
x_{0}(t):=1
and
x_{n+1}(t):=0
. If we view the transition rates as controls, then this is a nonlinear control system, as the equations include products of the state-variables and the controls.

For example, the RFM with
n=3
is given by

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{1}

\displaystyle=u_{0}(1-x_{1})-u_{1}x_{1}(1-x_{2}),

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{2}

\displaystyle=u_{1}x_{1}(1-x_{2})-u_{2}x_{2}(1-x_{3}),

(3)

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{3}

\displaystyle=u_{2}x_{2}(1-x_{3})-u_{3}x_{3},

where for the sake of simplicity we omit specifying the dependence on
t
.

To explain these equations, consider the equation for
\dot{x}_{2}
in (
II-A
).
The term
u_{1}x_{1}(1-x_{2})

is the flow from site
1
to site
2
. This is proportional to: the transition rate
u_{1}
from site
1
to site
2
;
the density
x_{1}
of particles in site
1
; and the “free space”
1-x_{2}
in site
2
.
Similarly,
the term
u_{2}x_{2}(1-x_{3})

is the flow from site
2
to site
3
. Thus, the equation for
\dot{x}_{2}
states that the change in density in site
2
is the flow into site
2
minus the flow out of site
2
.

The output rate from site
n
is
R(t):=u_{n}(t)x_{n}(t)
. When modeling translation, this corresponds to ribosomes exiting the mRNA, and thus to the protein production rate. In the context of biotechnology, a natural goal is to maximize this production rate. More generally,
in many transportation systems modeled using the RFM a natural goal is to maximize
R(t)
(in some well-defined sense).

Just like TASEP,
the RFM allows to model and analyze the evolution of traffic jams of particles.
This is due to the fact that the entry rate to site
i
depends on the free space
1-x_{i}
, which may be interpreted as a “soft version” of the simple exclusion principle.
To explain this, assume that
u_{2}(t)\equiv\varepsilon
, with
\varepsilon
positive and close to zero.
Then

\dot{x}_{2}\approx u_{1}x_{1}(1-x_{2})\geq 0,

so we expect
x_{2}
to increase towards one. Then
1-x_{2}
decreases towards zero, so

\dot{x}_{1}\approx u_{0}(1-x_{1})\geq 0,

so
x_{1}
will also increase towards one. In this way, a traffic jam of particles
is formed behind the slow transition rate.

Consider
positive and jointly
T
-periodic transition rates
u_{0}(t),\dots,u_{n}(t)
,
and denote the average of
u_{i}(t)
along a period by
\overline{u}_{i}
.
Ref.
[
32
]
showed
that in this case the RFM admits a unique globally exponentially stable (GES)
T
-periodic solution
\gamma
,
satisfying
\gamma(t)\in(0,1)^{n}
for all
t\in[0,T)
. The next example demonstrates this.

Example 2
.

Consider an RFM with dimension
n=3
, positive and
2\pi
-periodic rates

u_{0}(t)=3+\cos(t+5)
,

u_{1}(t)=1
,

u_{2}(t)=4+2\sin(t-4)
,

u_{3}(t)=2-\cos(t-1)
.
Fig.
2

depicts the solutions
x_{i}(t)
for
the (arbitrarily chosen) initial condition
x(0)=\begin{bmatrix}0.3&0.4&0.5\end{bmatrix}^{T}
. It may be seen that every
x_{i}(t)
converges to a
2\pi
-periodic solution
\gamma_{i}
. The convergence to this
\gamma
holds for any initial condition
x(0)\in[0,1]^{3}
.

Fig. 2:
Trajectories
x_{i}(t)
as a function of
t
in
an RFM with
n=3
and
2\pi
-periodic rates. Every state-variable converges to a periodic pattern, with period
2\pi
.

In particular, if the transition rates
are set to constant values
u_{i}(t)\equiv\overline{u}_{i}
for all
i
then the RFM admits a unique GES equilibrium point
e=\begin{bmatrix}e_{1}&\dots&e_{n}\end{bmatrix}^{T}\in(0,1)^{n}
.
It follows
from (

) that
e
satisfies

\overline{u}_{i-1}e_{i-1}(1-e_{i})=\overline{u}_{i}e_{i}(1-e_{i+1}),\quad i=1,\dots,n,

(4)

with
e_{0}:=1
and
e_{n+1}:=0
.

II-B

Gain of Entrainment in the RFM

Fix positive numbers
\overline{u}_{i}
,
i=0,\dots,n
. We compare two cases. In the first, we apply the constant control
u_{i}(t)\equiv\overline{u}_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,n
.
Then the RFM state-variables converge to an equilibrium
e:=\begin{bmatrix}e_{1}&\dots&e_{n}\end{bmatrix}^{T}\in(0,1)^{n}
satisfying (
4
). In particular, the protein production rate
R(t)
converges to

R_{C}:=\overline{u}_{n}e_{n},

where the subscript
C
stands for constant.

In the second case, we fix positive and
T
-periodic controls
u_{i}(t)
, such that
\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}u_{i}(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t=\overline{u}_{i}
. This constraint implies that we invest, on average, the same “control effort” as in the case of constant controls.
Then the RFM state-variables converge to a unique
T
-periodic solution
\gamma:[0,T)\to(0,1)^{n}
.
In particular, the average
production rate converges to

R_{P}:=\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}u_{n}(t)\gamma_{n}(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t,

where the subscript
P
stands for periodic.

We are interested in comparing
R_{C}
and
R_{P}
. We begin with a simple example.

Example 3
.

We simulated the RFM in Example
2

for the (arbitrarily chosen) initial
condition
\begin{bmatrix}0.3&0.4&0.5\end{bmatrix}^{T}
.
The solution at time
20\pi
is

x(20\pi)=\begin{bmatrix}0.7809&0.1624&0.3290\end{bmatrix}^{T},

and we assume that this point is very close to a point on
\gamma
.
Now simulating the dynamics from
x(0)=\begin{bmatrix}0.7809&0.1624&0.3290\end{bmatrix}^{T}
yields

x(2\pi)=\begin{bmatrix}0.7809&0.1624&0.3290\end{bmatrix}^{T}
,
and

R_{P}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}u_{3}(t)x_{3}(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t=0.5927.

The averages of the transition rates are

\overline{u}_{0}=3
,

\overline{u}_{1}=1
,

\overline{u}_{2}=4
,

\overline{u}_{3}=2
,
and (
4
) yields
e_{3}=0.3085
, so
R_{C}=\overline{u}_{3}e_{3}=0.6171
.
Thus, in this case
R_{C}>R_{P}
.

We say that the RFM admits a GOE for a control
u
if
R_{P}>R_{C}
. It is natural to expect that the added flexibility in using
T
-periodic, rather than constant controls, may yield a GOE. The difficulty in rigorously analyzing the GOE is due
to the fact that
\gamma
, which is the periodic solution of an
n
-dimensional nonlinear dynamical system, is not known explicitly.
Indeed, results that establish entrainment in various types of dynamical systems are typically based on implicit arguments like fixed point theorems
[
51
,
33
,
24
]
, and do not provide explicit information on the
periodic solution.

III
Main Results

In this section, we analyze several scenarios where the
n
-dimensional RFM admits no GOE for any periodic control and any period
T>0
.

Theorem 1
.

Fix an arbitrary
T>0
.
Consider the
n
-dimensional RFM with positive and jointly
T
-periodic controls
u_{i}(t)
,
i=0,\dots,n
. If any of the following six conditions holds then there is no GOE.

(I)

n
is odd, and for all
i\in\{1,3,\dots,n-2\}
there exists
\alpha_{i}>0
such that

u_{i}(t)=\alpha_{i}u_{i+1}(t),\text{ for all }t\in[0,T),

(5)

i.e., all the internal rates along the chain are “proportional in pairs”.

(II)

n
is even, and for all
i\in\{0,2,\dots,n-2
}
there exists
\alpha_{i}>0
such that

u_{i}(t)=\alpha_{i}u_{i+1}(t),\text{ for all }t\in[0,T).

(6)

(III)

n
is even, and for all
i\in\{1,3,\dots,n-1
}
there exists
\alpha_{i}>0
such that

u_{i}(t)=\alpha_{i}u_{i+1}(t),\text{ for all }t\in[0,T).

(7)

(IV)

n
is odd, and for all
i\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}
,

u_{i}(t)\equiv\overline{u}_{i},

i.e., all the transition rates are constant, except for the
initiation rate
u_{0}(t)
and the
exit rate
u_{n}(t)
.

(V)

n
is even, and for all
i\in\{0,1,\dots,n-1\}
,

u_{i}(t)\equiv\overline{u}_{i},

i.e., all the transition rates are constant, except for the exit rate
u_{n}(t)
.

(VI)

n
is even, and for all
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
,

u_{i}(t)\equiv\overline{u}_{i},

i.e., all the transition rates are constant, except for the initiation rate
u_{0}(t)
.

Before proving this result, we discuss some of its implications.
In some applications, it may be possible to control
u_{0}(t)
and/or
u_{n}(t)
, but not
the other, “internal” transition rates. For example,
it is often stated that control of translation in eukaryotic cells is exercised mainly at the initiation step, when ribosomes are recruited to mRNA
[
46
,
58
,
18
]
. Specifically, assume
that all the internal rates are constant, i.e.

u_{1}(t)\equiv\overline{u_{1}},\dots,u_{n-1}(t)\equiv\overline{u}_{n-1},

(8)

and our goal is to determine
T
-periodic and positive rates
u_{0}(t)
and
u_{n}(t)
, with fixed averages
\overline{u}_{0}
and
\overline{u}_{n}
,
that maximize the average output rate. If
n
is odd then
assertion
IV
in Theorem
1
states that
the constant rates
u_{0}(t)\equiv\overline{u}_{0}

and
u_{n}(t)\equiv\overline{u}_{n}

are an optimal choice. In the case of even
n
assertions
V
and
VI
in Theorem
1
provide the weaker result that constant rates are optimal if we allow only for one of the two rates
u_{0}
or
u_{n}
to be time-dependent.

The distinction between the cases of an RFM with
n
odd and
n
even is an artifact of our analysis approach. We conjecture that there is no GOE in the RFM for any
n
and any periodic controls, but we do not know how to prove this more general statement.

The remainder of this section is devoted to the proof of Theorem
1
.
This requires several auxiliary results. We begin by defining the “centered”
state-variables:

\displaystyle z_{i}(t):=x_{i}(t)-e_{i},\quad i=1,\dots,n.

(9)

Then (
2
) yields

\dot{z}_{i}(t)=u_{i-1}(t)(z_{i-1}(t)+e_{i-1})(1-z_{i}(t)-e_{i})-u_{i}(t)(z_{i}(t)+e_{i})(1-z_{i+1}(t)-e_{i+1}),\quad i=1,\dots,n,

(10)

with
z_{0}(t):=0
and
z_{n+1}(t):=0
.

Note that as
x
converges to the unique
T
-periodic solution
\gamma
,
z
converges to the unique
T
-periodic solution
\gamma-e
. Our analysis is based on computing integrals of
\frac{d}{dt}z_{i}(t)
and
\frac{d}{dt}z_{i}^{2}(t)

along this periodic solution
. To simplify the notation, we
write
\int_{0}^{T}\frac{d}{dt}(z_{i}(t))^{k}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t
,
k\in\{1,2\}
, but we
always
integrate along the unique
T
-periodic solution
\gamma
.

It is useful to introduce the following notation. For a
T
-periodic function
y(t)
, let

\overline{y}:=\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}y(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t,

that is, the average of
y(t)
along a period.
Note that
\overline{z}_{i}=\overline{x}_{i}-e_{i}
, so
\overline{z}_{i}>0
if and only if (iff) the average of
x_{i}
for the periodic transition rates is larger than
the equilibrium value
e_{i}
corresponding to the constant rates.
We also denote

\eta_{i,j}:=\overline{u_{i}z_{j}},\quad\eta_{i,j,k}:=\overline{u_{i}z_{j}z_{k}},

where, as noted above, the averages
are always computed along the unique periodic solution. Recall that
z_{0}(t)=z_{n+1}(t)\equiv 0
, so any
“moment”
\eta
that includes
z_{0}
or
z_{n+1}
(e.g.,
\eta_{i,j,n+1}=\overline{u_{i}z_{j}z_{n+1}}
) is zero.

The next result uses the compartmental structure of the RFM to derive a simple necessary and sufficient condition guaranteeing that there
is no GOE.

Proposition 2
.

We have

\eta_{0,1}=-\eta_{n,n},

(11)

and

R_{P}=-\eta_{0,1}+R_{C}.

(12)

In particular,
if
\eta_{0,1}\geq 0
then there is no GOE, and if
\eta_{0,1}>0
then
R_{P}
is strictly smaller than
R_{C}
.

Remark 1
.

Note that
\eta_{0,1}=\overline{u_{0}z_{1}}
may be interpreted as the “average correlation”
between the initiation rate
u_{0}
and the centered density
z_{1}=x_{1}-e_{1}
. Note also that (
11
) implies that there exists a time
\tau\in[0,T)
such that

u_{0}(\tau)z_{1}(\tau)=-u_{n}(\tau)z_{n}(\tau).

Proof.

It follows from (
10
) that

\dot{z}_{1}+\dots+\dot{z}_{n}=u_{0}(1-z_{1}-e_{1})-u_{n}(z_{n}+e_{n}).

Integrating this equation gives

0=\overline{u_{0}}(1-e_{1})-\eta_{0,1}-\overline{u_{n}}e_{n}-\eta_{n,n},

and using (
4
) proves (
11
).
To complete the proof note that

\displaystyle R_{P}

\displaystyle=\overline{u_{n}\gamma_{n}}

\displaystyle=\overline{u_{n}(z_{n}+e_{n})}

\displaystyle=\eta_{n,n}+R_{C},

and applying (
11
)
yields (
12
).
∎

The next result is based on integrating
\dot{z}_{i}(t)
,
i=1,\dots,n
. Roughly speaking, this allows to express the “third-order moment”
\eta_{i,i,i+1}=\overline{u_{i}z_{i}z_{i+1}}
as
a linear combination of three “second-order moments”.

Proposition 3
.

For any
i\in\{0,\dots,n\}
, we have

\eta_{i,i,i+1}=\eta_{0,1}+\eta_{i,i}(1-e_{i+1})-\eta_{i,i+1}e_{i}.

(13)

Proof.

First, we prove (
13
) for
i=0
and
i=n
. For
i=0
, (
13
) becomes

0=\eta_{0,1}+0-\eta_{0,1},

which clearly holds. Similarly, for
i=n
we get

0=\eta_{0,1}+\eta_{n,n},

which holds due to Prop.
2
.

The rest of the proof assume that
i\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}
. Integrating (
10
) and using (
4
) gives

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\overline{u_{i-1}(1-e_{i}-z_{i})(z_{i-1}+e_{i-1})}-\overline{u_{i}(z_{i}+e_{i})(1-e_{i+1}-z_{i+1})}

\displaystyle=-\eta_{i-1,i-1,i}+\eta_{i-1,i-1}(1-e_{i})-\eta_{i-1,i}e_{i-1}+\eta_{i,i,i+1}-\eta_{i,i}(1-e_{i+1})+\eta_{i,i+1}e_{i}.

(14)

For
i=1

this becomes

\displaystyle\eta_{1,1,2}

\displaystyle=\eta_{0,1}+\eta_{1,1}(1-e_{2})-\eta_{1,2}e_{1}

(15)

(recall that
z_{0}(t)=z_{n+1}(t)\equiv 0
).
For
i=2
, Eq. (
III
) becomes

\displaystyle\eta_{2,2,3}

\displaystyle=\eta_{1,1,2}-\eta_{1,1}(1-e_{2})+\eta_{1,2}e_{1}+\eta_{2,2}(1-e_{3})-\eta_{2,3}e_{2},

and using (
15
) gives

\displaystyle\eta_{2,2,3}

\displaystyle=\eta_{0,1}+\eta_{2,2}(1-e_{3})-\eta_{2,3}e_{2}.

Continuing in this manner
completes the proof.
∎

The next result is derived by integrating
\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}z_{i}^{2}(t)=z_{i}(t)\dot{z}_{i}(t)
,
i=1,\dots,n
. This yields
n
lower bounds on
\eta_{0,1}
.

Proposition 4
.

For any
i=1,\dots,n
, we have

\displaystyle\eta_{0,1}\geq\eta_{i,i+1}e_{i}^{2}-\eta_{i-1,i-1}(1-e_{i})^{2}.

(16)

Furthermore, equality is attained iff
z_{i}(t)\equiv 0
, that is, iff
x_{i}(t)\equiv e_{i}
.

Proof.

By (
10
),

\displaystyle\dot{z}_{i}z_{i}

\displaystyle=u_{i-1}z_{i}(1-e_{i}-z_{i})(z_{i-1}+e_{i-1})-u_{i}z_{i}(z_{i}+e_{i})(1-e_{i+1}-z_{i+1}).

Integrating this and rearranging terms gives

\displaystyle\overline{(u_{i-1}(z_{i-1}+e_{i-1})+u_{i}(1-e_{i+1}-z_{i+1}))z_{i}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\overline{u_{i-1}z_{i}(1-e_{i})(z_{i-1}+e_{i-1})}

\displaystyle-\overline{u_{i}z_{i}e_{i}(1-e_{i+1}-z_{i+1})}.

(17)

The left-hand side of this equation is non-negative because the controls
u_{i}(t)
are positive for all
t
, and along the periodic solution
z_{i}(t)+e_{i}=\gamma_{i}(t)\in(0,1)
for all
t
. Furthermore, the left-hand side is zero iff
z_{i}(t)\equiv 0
. We conclude that

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq\eta_{i-1,i-1,i}(1-e_{i})+\eta_{i-1,i}(1-e_{i})e_{i-1}+\eta_{i,i,i+1}e_{i}-\eta_{i,i}(1-e_{i+1})e_{i},

(18)

with equality iff
z_{i}(t)\equiv 0
, and substituting the expressions for the third-order moments (
13
) completes the proof.
∎

Note that for
i=1
and
i=n
,
Proposition
4

yields

\eta_{0,1}\geq\eta_{1,2}e_{1}^{2},

(19)

and

\eta_{0,1}\geq-\eta_{n-1,n-1}(1-e_{n})^{2},

(20)

respectively. Combining this with Proposition
2

implies that there is no GOE if
\eta_{1,2}\geq 0
or if
\eta_{n-1,n-1}\leq 0
.

For
n=1
,
z_{2}(t)\equiv 0
, so
\eta_{1,2}=\overline{u_{1}z_{2}}=0
and (
19
) becomes
\eta_{0,1}\geq 0
. Combining this with Prop.
2
yields the following result.

Corollary 5
.

In the RFM with
n=1
there is no GOE. Furthermore,
R_{P}=R_{C}
iff

u_{1}(t)=\frac{\overline{u}_{1}}{\overline{u}_{0}}u_{0}(t),\text{ for all }t\in[0,T).

(21)

Proof.

The proof that
R_{P}\leq R_{C}
follows immediately from Prop.
2
. Now assume that
R_{p}=R_{C}
. Then
z_{1}(t)\equiv 0
along the unique periodic solution, so

x_{1}(t)\equiv e_{1}=\frac{\overline{u}_{0}}{\overline{u}_{0}+\overline{u}_{1}}.

Substituting this in (
2
) yields

0=u_{0}(t)(1-e_{1})-u_{1}(t)e_{1}=\frac{\overline{u}_{1}u_{0}(t)-\overline{u}_{0}u_{1}(t)}{\overline{u}_{0}+\overline{u}_{1}},

and this completes the proof.
∎

The fact that in the 1D RFM there is no GOE was already proved in
[
1
]
using a more complicated argument (see also
[
25
,
52
]
).

We can now prove Theorem
1
for
n\geq 2
. To prove assertion
I
, suppose that
n
is odd, i.e.,
n=2k+1
, and that
all the internal rates are proportional in pairs, that is, for any
i\in\{1,3,\dots,n-2\}
there exists
\alpha_{i}>0
such that

u_{i}(t)=\alpha_{i}u_{i+1}(t),\text{ for all }t\in[0,T).

(22)

For
i=1
, Eq. (
16
) becomes

\eta_{0,1}\geq\eta_{1,2}e_{1}^{2}=\alpha_{1}\eta_{2,2}e_{1}^{2},

so if
\eta_{2,2}\geq 0
then there is no GOE and the proof is completed. We may thus assume that
\eta_{2,2}<0
.
For
i=3
, Eq. (
16
) becomes

\eta_{0,1}\geq\eta_{3,4}e_{3}^{2}-\eta_{2,2}(1-e_{3})^{2},

and using (
22
) gives
\eta_{0,1}\geq\alpha_{3}\eta_{4,4}e_{3}^{2}-\eta_{2,2}(1-e_{3})^{2}
.
Thus, if
\eta_{4,4}\geq 0
then there is no GOE, and we may assume that
\eta_{4,4}<0
.
Continuing in this manner, we may assume that
\eta_{2j,2j}<0
, for all
j\leq k
.
For
i=n=2k+1
, Eq. (
16
) becomes

\eta_{0,1}\geq-\eta_{2k,2k}(1-e_{2k+1})^{2}>0,

and this completes the proof of the assertion
I
in Theorem
1
.

To prove assertion
II
, suppose that
n
is even and that (
6
) holds. Then
\eta_{0,1}=\alpha_{0}\eta_{1,1}
, and we have established no GOE if
\eta_{1,1}\geq 0
. Therefore
we may assume that
\eta_{1,1}<0
. Considering (
16
) with
i=2
we conclude that there is no GOE if
\eta_{2,3}=\alpha_{2}\eta_{3,3}\geq 0
, and we may assume that
\eta_{3,3}<0
. Continuing in this manner, we have that
\eta_{n-1,n-1}<0
, and we already know from inequality (
20
) that this implies that there is no GOE. This completes the proof of assertion
II
in Theorem
1
.

To prove assertion
III
, suppose that
n
is even and that (
7
) holds. Then
\eta_{n-1,n}=\alpha_{n-1}\eta_{n,n}
, and due to Proposition
2
there is no GOE if
\eta_{n-1,n}\leq 0
. Therefore
we may assume that
\eta_{n-1,n}>0
. Considering (
16
) with
i=n-1
we conclude that there is no GOE if
\eta_{n-2,n-2}=\alpha_{n-3}^{-1}\eta_{n-3,n-2}\leq 0
, and we may assume that
\eta_{n-3,n-2}>0
. Continuing in this manner, we have that
\eta_{1,2}>0
, and we already know from inequality (
19
) that this implies that there is no GOE. This completes the proof of assertion
III
in Theorem
1
.

Assertions
IV
,
V
, and
VI
follow immediately from the assertions
I
,
II
, and
III
, respectively, as one may choose
\alpha_{i}=\overline{u}_{i}/\overline{u}_{i+1}
for the corresponding values of
i
.
This completes the proof of Theorem
1
.

Reconsidering the proof of
Proposition
4

allows to slightly strengthen Theorem
1
. To do this, we introduce another definition.

Definition 1
.

A
T
-periodic solution
\gamma:[0,T)\to(0,1)^{n}
of the RFM is called
degenerate
if there exists an index
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
such that
\gamma_{i}(t)
is constant.
Otherwise,
\gamma
is called
non-degenerate
.

Corollary 6
.

Suppose that one of the cases in Theorem
1
holds. Then for any
T
-periodic control
u(t)
that yields a non-degenerate
T
-periodic solution we have

R_{P}<R_{C}.

(23)

In other words, in this case constant rates are in fact strictly better than periodic ones.

Proof.

Fix an arbitrary
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
. Since the periodic solution is non-degenerate,
z_{i}(t)=x_{i}(t)-e_{i}

is not zero at some time
t_{i}\in[0,T)
, and this implies that the term on the left-hand side of (
III
)
is positive.
Thus, the inequality in (
18
) becomes strict. Now the arguments in the proof of Theorem
1
yield (
23
).
∎

While Theorem
1
proves that there is no GOE in certain cases, numerical experiments suggest that there is no GOE in general.

.

n=4
.

\int_{t}^{t+T}u_{4}(\tau)x_{4}(\tau)d\tau

u_{i}(t)=\overline{u}_{i}+A_{i}\cos(2\pi t+\phi_{i})
, where

were chosen at random. Thus, the rates are

.

.

IV
Discussion

We studied the GOE in the
n
-dimensional RFM. Our analysis approach is new and is based on calculating integrals of the (centered) state-variables and “higher-order moments” of these
variables along the periodic solution.

We proved that under certain conditions the
n
-dimensional RFM admits no GOE. We conjecture that this in fact always true, but even for the case
n=2
we are not able to prove this in the most general setting.

An intuitive explanation for the fact that constant controls are always optimal, based on numerical simulations, is as follows. When
u(t)
is
T
-periodic and non-constant there are times
t
such that
u(t)>\overline{u}
and times when the opposite inequality holds. In general, the “profit” to the average production rate in the
“good” times is less than the “loss” in the “bad” times. An interesting line for further research is to try and rigorously formulate and prove this observation. Note that in the case of optimal periodic control for scalar systems (i.e., when
n=1
), convexity theory
plays an important role
[
15
,
4
]
.

One case where we proved that there is no GOE is when
n
is odd and all
the internal rates are equal.
It is interesting to note that in TASEP, with all rates
constant
, the
case of equal internal hopping rates is the one that is amenable to analysis. Note also that it was recently shown that TASEP, just like the RFM, is a contractive system (in a suitable stochastic sense)
[
13
]
.

The RFM is a deterministic system, and it may be of interest to consider if the results on the GOE
hold also after adding some form of noise to the system.
Since the RFM is contractive,
the results in
[
42
]
suggest that an additive noise of small intensity (in the Ito-SDE-sense) will only slightly affect the output rate, which would imply that if the noise does lead to a GOE then it is likely to be small.
In any case, a precise notion of GOE in the presence of noise is, to the best of our knowledge, missing.

Finally, entrainment to periodic excitations is important in many natural and artificial systems.
For example, connecting several artificial biological systems that entrain to a common clock may lead to a well-functioning modular system
[
9
]
. Epidemic outbreaks entrain to seasonal forcing
[
50
]
.
Analysis of GOE in such models is related to interesting questions like: (1) is periodic production of synthetic biology constructs more efficient than
constant production?
and (2) does seasonality make the epidemics, on average, more or less severe?
