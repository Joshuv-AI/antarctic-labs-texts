Higher-order Common Information
Jan Østergaard

Abstract
Shannon mutual information quantifies dependence between two random variables. We introduce higher-order common information (HCI), a measure of statistical relevance across n n random variables. The HCI constructs a sequence of stochastically degraded representations. At each stage, the representation maximizes the amount of relevant information to the next source under a limited information budget. Due to this limited budget, some source-irrelevant information are potentially omitted at each stage. The construction can therefore be interpreted as a stochastic relaxation of the intersection of information. For finite-alphabet sources, the HCI is lower-bounded by the multivariate Gács–Körner common information and, for arbitrary sources, it is upper-bounded by their smallest pairwise mutual information. We derive closed-form HCI expressions for scalar jointly Gaussian sources and for a binary common-source model.



I
Introduction

Characterizing the information that is
shared
within a collection of
n
random variables
X_{1},\dotsc,X_{n}
remains challenging for
n>2
. For
n=2
, Shannon’s mutual information provides a measure of pairwise dependence, namely
I(X_{1};X_{2})

[
2
]
. However, for
n>2
there is no single canonical extension: different operational questions lead to different notions of “common information,” and pairwise summaries can be misleading.
A simple baseline is to quantify redundancy through the smallest pairwise mutual information. For example, for
n
variables one may define the redundancy
R
as:

R\triangleq\min_{i\neq j}I(X_{i};X_{j}).

(1)

While (
1
) captures the
amount
of pairwise dependence, it does not determine whether the
same
piece of information is present in all variables.

For two variables, several inequivalent notions of common information are already classical.
Wyner’s common information
[
3
]
(and related formulations in, e.g.,
[
4
,
5
]
) quantifies the minimum rate of common randomness needed to
simulate
the joint distribution of
(X_{1},X_{2})
via a latent variable
W
that renders them conditionally independent, i.e.,
X_{1}-W-X_{2}
. Formally,

\displaystyle C_{W}(X_{1},X_{2})

\displaystyle=\inf_{p(w|x_{1},x_{2})}I(X_{1},X_{2};W)

\displaystyle\quad\text{s.t.}\quad I(X_{1};X_{2}|W)=0,

(2)

which differs from mutual information, e.g.,
[
5
]
:

\displaystyle C_{W}(X_{1},X_{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\log_{2}\!\left(\frac{1+|\rho|}{1-|\rho|}\right),

(3)

\displaystyle I(X_{1};X_{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\log_{2}\!\left(\frac{1}{1-\rho^{2}}\right).

(4)

Extensions and variations of
C_{W}
to
n
variables exists, and in general it holds that
C_{W}(X_{1},X_{2})\geq I(X_{1};X_{2})

[
6
,
7
,
8
]
.

The Gács–Körner common information
[
9
]
captures the
deterministic
common part extractable from
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
without communication. It is defined as the maximum entropy of a random variable
V
that can be computed as a function of each observation, that is:

C_{\mathrm{GK}}(X_{1};X_{2})\triangleq\max_{f,g:\ f(X_{1})=g(X_{2})=V}H(V),

(5)

which can be zero even when
I(X_{1};X_{2})
is large.

For many jointly continuous distributions, entropy-based notions of common randomness can be infinite or degenerate, which motivates alternative characterizations.
Recently,
[
10
]
introduced the common information dimension, which replaces rate in bits by information dimension in order to quantify common randomness for continuous random variables in a manner that remains meaningful when entropy-based quantities diverge.

If one is instead interested in redundancy about a third variable
Y
(i.e., the information that
X_{1},\dotsc,X_{n}
share regarding
Y
), then the problem connects to partial information decomposition (PID) and related measures of redundant, unique, and synergistic information
[
11
,
12
,
13
,
14
,
15
,
16
,
17
]
. The BROJA redundancy
[
13
]
is an example of a PID redundancy measure defined for discrete alphabets. Extensions to continuous sources are nontrivial and typically rely on restrictive model-based assumptions, such as joint Gaussianity
[
18
,
15
]
.

There also exist multivariate measures that capture global dependencies such as the total correlation
[
19
,
20
]
and the dual total correlation
[
20
]
, which measures the total amount of statistical dependence among all variables and how much information each variable shares with the rest, respectively.

In this paper, we introduce a notion of higher-order common information (HCI),
R(X_{1},\dotsc,X_{n})
, for a
collection of
n
random variables. Unlike PID
frameworks, the HCI characterizes dependence intrinsic to the collection
\mathbf{X}=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})

without introducing an external target variable.
The construction is motivated by the idea of a sequential statistical
”intersection”. Starting from
T_{1}=X_{1}
, one would ideally retain in

T_{2}
only the information in
T_{1}
that is also present in
X_{2}
.
One would then retain in
T_{3}
only the information in
T_{2}
that is
also present in
X_{3}
, and continue in this manner through the complete
source collection. If we momentarily assume that ”present in” is interpreted as
deterministic
recoverability, then this sequential procedure corresponds to intersecting the
\sigma
-fields generated by the sources
[
21
]
. For discrete random
variables, this leads to the Gács–Körner common component. Thus, one would recover the multivariate notion
C_{\mathrm{GK}}(\mathbf{X})
of the Gács–Körner common information.
However, such a deterministic intersection is trivially equal to zero for both noisy discrete sources and for
many continuous sources even though they exhibit strong statistical dependence
[
9
]
. To be able to capture such statistical dependencies, our notion of HCI replaces this exact deterministic intersection by a sequential
information-bottleneck construction. In the first stage, set
T_{1}=X_{1}
. In the second stage, the next auxiliary is generated from
T_{1}

through a stochastic kernel satisfying
T_{2}-T_{1}-\mathbf{X}
.
The amount of information passed from
T_{1}
to
T_{2}
is constrained
by:

I(T_{2};T_{1})\leq I(T_{1};X_{2}),

(6)

and
T_{2}
is selected to maximize its
relevance to
X_{2}
:

T_{2}\in\arg\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}S-T_{1}-\mathbf{X}\\
I(S;T_{1})\leq I(T_{1};X_{2})\end{subarray}}I(S;X_{2}).

(7)

The procedure is then repeated: at stage
\ell
, the representation

T_{\ell+1}
is obtained by stochastically degrading
T_{\ell}
, its
information rate is limited by the relevance of
T_{\ell}
to the
current source, say
X_{\ell}
, and it is chosen to preserve as much of that relevance
as possible.

Our construction of HCI should be understood as a statistical relaxation of
set intersection rather than as a literal intersection of source
components. It does not generally produce a random variable that is
deterministically recoverable from every source. Instead, it measures
how much statistical relevance that survives through a sequence of
locally generated, source-dependent information bottlenecks.
If
T^{\star}
is an optimal final stage auxiliary for the source
\mathbf{X}
and the HCI is
R(X_{1},\dotsc,X_{n})=r
, then:

I(T^{\star};X_{j})\geq r,\quad j=1,\dotsc,n.

(8)

Thus, every individual source has at least
r
bits of
statistical information in common with a representation generated
locally
from
one source.

For finite-alphabet sources, we show that:

R(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})\geq C_{\mathrm{GK}}(X_{1};\ldots;X_{n}).

(9)

Thus, the HCI preserves every deterministically common component captured
by the Gács–Körner common information, while potentially assigning a
positive value to sources that share no deterministic
common function. For example, for correlated Bernoulli sources, the Gács–Körner common information is zero. In contrast, the proposed HCI is strictly positive for all finite
n
. Thus,
R(X_{1},\dotsc,X_{n})
can reveal shared structure that is not captured by existing measures.

To define and characterize
R(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})
for
n\geq 3
, we
introduce an iterative information-bottleneck construction based on
nested Markov-chain, information-matching, and target-minimality
constraints. We derive closed-form expressions for jointly Gaussian
sources and for a binary common-source model. In the equicorrelated
cases, these expressions reduce to:

R(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})=-\frac{1}{2}\log_{2}\!\left(1-|\rho|^{\,2^{n-1}}\right)

(10)

and

R(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})=1-h_{2}\!\left(\frac{1-c^{\,2^{n-1}}}{2}\right),

(11)

respectively, where
\rho
is the Gaussian correlation coefficient
and
c=1-2p
is the bias of the binary observation channel. For
general sources, we furthermore propose an HCI-inspired numerical approximation
and illustrate it using EEG recordings.

II
Higher-order common information

In this section, we formally introduce our notion of HCI, we provide upper and lower bounds, and finally present some key properties of the HCI.

II-A

Definition of HCI

For an arbitrarily distributed random variable
X
, define:

\displaystyle\mathsf{M}(X)\triangleq\bigl\{S:

\displaystyle\text{for every measurable }G=g(S),

(12)

\displaystyle X-G-S\ \Longrightarrow\ S=f(G)\ \text{a.s.}

\displaystyle\text{for some measurable }f\bigr\}.

Example 1

Let
E
and
X
be arbitrarily distributed and mutually independent. Set
T=X
and define
T^{\prime}=(T,E)
.
Then
T^{\prime}
contains a separately accessible source-irrelevant
component. Indeed, with
G=T
, we have
G=g(T^{\prime}),\mathbf{X}-G-T^{\prime}
,
but
T^{\prime}
is not a measurable function of
G
, since
E
is
independent of
G
. Hence
T^{\prime}\notin\mathsf{M}(X)
.
On the other hand,
T=X
belongs to

\mathsf{M}(X)
, since
T
is equal to the source
X
.

Example 2

Suppose that
X
is Gaussian,
T=X
, and
\widetilde{T}=aT+E,a\neq 0
,
where
E
is Gaussian and independent of
X
.
Then
\widetilde{T}
is target-minimal with respect to
X
.
Although
E
is target-independent, it is not available as a separate
component of
\widetilde{T}
, since it is mixed with
T
.
Moreover, the conditional Gaussian distribution

P_{X|\widetilde{T}=t}
has a mean that is a nonzero linear
function of
t\neq 0
. It follows that distinct values of
\widetilde{T}

results in distinct conditional distributions of
X
. Therefore,
any statistic
G=g(\widetilde{T})
satisfying
X-G-\widetilde{T}

must determine
\widetilde{T}
almost surely, proving that
\widetilde{T}\in\mathsf{M}(X)
.

Definition 1
(Higher-order common information)

Let
\mathbf{X}=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})
be a random vector whose components take values in standard Borel
spaces. Fix a reference index
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, let

\pi^{(i)}
be a permutation of
\mathcal{I}_{i}\triangleq\{1,\ldots,n\}\setminus\{i\}
,
and set
T_{1}^{(i)}=X_{i}
.
For
\ell=1,\ldots,n-2
, given
T_{\ell}^{(i)}
, define:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\ell}(T_{\ell}^{(i)},\pi^{(i)},\mathbf{X})

\displaystyle\triangleq\left\{S:\begin{array}[]{ll}\text{(MC)\ }&S-T_{\ell}^{(i)}-\mathbf{X},\\[1.99997pt]
\text{(IM)\ }&I(S;T_{\ell}^{(i)})\leq I(T_{\ell}^{(i)};X_{\pi^{(i)}(\ell)}),\\[1.99997pt]
\text{(TM)\ }&S\in\mathsf{M}(X_{\pi^{(i)}(\ell)}).\end{array}\right\}.

The three conditions in (
1
) are called the
Markov-chain (MC), information-matching (IM), and target-minimality (TM) constraints.
Define the optimal stage-wise source relevance by:

F_{\ell}(T_{\ell}^{(i)},\pi^{(i)},\mathbf{X})\triangleq\sup_{S\in\mathcal{F}_{\ell}(T_{\ell}^{(i)},\pi^{(i)},\mathbf{X})}I(S;X_{\pi^{(i)}(\ell)}),

(16)

and the optimizer set by:

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\ell}\bigl(T_{\ell}^{(i)},\pi^{(i)},\mathbf{X}\bigr)

\displaystyle\triangleq\Bigl\{S\in{}\mathcal{F}_{\ell}\bigl(T_{\ell}^{(i)},\pi^{(i)},\mathbf{X}\bigr):

(17)

\displaystyle I\bigl(S;X_{\pi^{(i)}(\ell)}\bigr)=F_{\ell}\bigl(T_{\ell}^{(i)},\pi^{(i)},\mathbf{X}\bigr)\Bigr\}.

The terminal set is defined as:

\mathcal{T}_{i}^{\pi^{(i)}}(\mathbf{X})\triangleq\left\{T_{n-1}^{(i)}:\begin{array}[]{l}T_{1}^{(i)}=X_{i},\\[1.99997pt]
T_{\ell+1}^{(i)}\in\mathcal{A}_{\ell}(T_{\ell}^{(i)},\pi^{(i)},\mathbf{X}),\\[1.99997pt]
\ell=1,\ldots,n-2\end{array}\right\}.

(18)

We can now finally define the HCI by:

R(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})\triangleq\min_{i}\max_{\pi^{(i)}}\sup_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{i}^{\pi^{(i)}}(\mathbf{X})}\min_{j}I(T;X_{j}).

(19)

Remark 1

Note that this HCI formulation has only a solution when all stage-wise suprema are attained, so that the corresponding optimizer sets are nonempty.
Throughout this paper,
R(X_{1},\dotsc,X_{n})
is therefore defined only for sources for which the stage-wise optimizer sets are nonempty. This condition is verified for the Gaussian and binary source models considered below. An extension to general standard Borel sources, where the suprema are not attained could, for example, be obtained by replacing the supremum by an
\varepsilon
-optimal maximizer. Such extensions are left for future work.

We will now discuss the constraints in the HCI definition in more details.
The nested MC and IM constraints in (
1
)
play complementary roles. The MC constraints:

T_{\ell+1}^{(i)}-T_{\ell}^{(i)}-\mathbf{X},\quad T_{1}^{(i)}=X_{i},

(20)

ensure that every auxiliary is generated by successively processing a
single reference source
X_{i}
. In particular, the construction has never access to multiple source variables and therefore cannot
introduce information that is available only through their joint observation such as synergistic information. Thus, the Markov chain
T_{\ell}^{(i)}-X_{i}-\mathbf{X}
holds for all
i
and
\ell
.

The IM constraints:

I(T_{\ell+1}^{(i)};T_{\ell}^{(i)})\leq I\!\left(T_{\ell}^{(i)};X_{\pi^{(i)}(\ell)}\right)

(21)

successively restrict the amount of information that can be passed from
one auxiliary to the next. These constraints prevent the construction
from carrying the reference variable unchanged through the entire
sequence. Indeed, if the IM constraints were omitted, the identity
chain:

T_{1}^{(i)}=T_{2}^{(i)}=\cdots=T_{n-1}^{(i)}=X_{i}

(22)

would be feasible. For a fixed reference
X_{i}
, the resulting terminal
optimization would then satisfy:

\sup_{T:\,T-X_{i}-\mathbf{X}}\min_{j}I(T;X_{j})=\min_{j}I(X_{i};X_{j}).

(23)

Thus, the sequential IM constraints prevent the HCI from
degenerating to the smallest pairwise mutual information of the source variables.

The interplay between the MC and IM constraints and the stage-wise relevance maximization admits an informal interpretation as a stochastic relaxation of
information intersection. At each stage, the new auxiliary is generated from the preceding representation, while the IM constraint
limits the information passed on. Subject to this budget, the optimization selects an auxiliary that preserves as much source-relevant information as possible and thereby potentially reduces the amount of source-irrelevant information. Note that unlike the case of exact intersection of deterministic information, our construction will not remove
all
source-irrelevant information. The MC and IM constraints restrict how information is propagated but do not, by themselves, enforce a minimal representation.
For example, suppose that
T
is feasible and define
T^{\prime}=(T,E)
, where
E\perp(T,\mathbf{X})
. Appending
E
does not change the
statistical relationship between the auxiliary and the sources:

I(T^{\prime};X_{j})=I(T;X_{j}),\quad j=1,\ldots,n,

(24)

and
I(T^{\prime};\mathbf{X})=I(T;\mathbf{X})
.
Although such private randomness does not change the current stage-wise supremum, it may change the feasible set
at subsequent stages, because a later auxiliary can be generated from the complete representation
T^{\prime}=(T,E)
. To remove deterministically
separable components that do not contribute to the statistical relationship with the current target, we impose the target-minimality
(TM) constraint. In the case
n=3
, there is only one auxiliary stage, and appending independent private randomness to the
terminal auxiliary does not change its mutual information with any source. For
n\geq 4
, however, intermediate auxiliaries are used to
generate subsequent representations, so redundant components can propagate through the sequence and complicate both the optimization and
the characterization of the optimal auxiliary chain. We therefore introduce the TM constraint to exclude such nonessential representations.

We allow both deterministic and stochastic mappings. For many sources, stochastic mappings are superior.
The following example shows that deterministic mappings do not necessarily saturate the IM constraint, whereas a stochastic mapping can attain
equality and yield a strictly larger HCI value.

Let

, and let
X_{1}
take values in
\{0,1,2\}
with
\mathbb{P}_{X_{1}}(0)=\frac{1}{2}
and
\mathbb{P}_{X_{1}}(1)=\mathbb{P}_{X_{1}}(2)=\frac{1}{4}
.
Moreover, define

X_{2}=X_{3}=\begin{cases}X_{1},&B=1,\\
\mathtt{e},&B=0,\end{cases}

where

, is independent of

II-B

Upper Bound to the HCI

In this section, we introduce an upper bound to the HCI by removing some of the constraints.
Specifically,
we define the following relaxed optimization problem by retaining the
MC and IM constraints from (
1
), while removing the TM and stage-wise optimality
requirements:

\displaystyle R_{\mathrm{ub}}(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})

\displaystyle\triangleq\min_{1\leq i\leq n}\max_{\pi^{(i)}}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}T_{1}^{(i)}=X_{i},\\
T_{\ell+1}^{(i)}-T_{\ell}^{(i)}-\mathbf{X},\\
I(T_{\ell+1}^{(i)};T_{\ell}^{(i)})\leq I\!\left(T_{\ell}^{(i)};X_{\pi^{(i)}(\ell)}\right),\\
\ell=1,\ldots,n-2\end{subarray}}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\min_{1\leq j\leq n}I(T_{n-1}^{(i)};X_{j}),

(43)

where the supremum is taken over the auxiliary sequence

(T_{2}^{(i)},\ldots,T_{n-1}^{(i)})
.

The following corollary follows directly from the fact that every auxiliary chain admissible for the HCI is also admissible for the
relaxed optimization problem in (
43
) which defines
R_{\mathrm{ub}}
.

Corollary 1

For any source
\mathbf{X}
, we have that:

R_{\mathrm{ub}}(\mathbf{X})\geq R(\mathbf{X}).

(44)

\triangle

Thus, an auxiliary may keep information that is irrelevant to the current source but relevant to subsequent sources, provided that doing so improves the supremum over the terminal stage auxiliary.
We provide an example of such a case below, where we further show that the upper bound in (
44
) can be strict.

Example 4

Let
A,B,C
be mutually independent fair bits and define
X_{1}=(A,B),X_{2}=(A,C),
and
X_{3}=(B,C)
.
Each pair of sources shares exactly one bit:

I(X_{1};X_{2})=I(X_{1};X_{3})=I(X_{2};X_{3})=1.

(45)

However, the three sources have no deterministic component in common, which implies that
C_{\mathrm{GK}}(\mathbf{X})=0
.

We first compute the HCI. Fix
X_{1}
as the reference and suppose that

X_{2}
is processed first. A feasible auxiliary
S
must satisfy:

S-X_{1}-(X_{1},X_{2},X_{3}),\quad I(S;X_{1})\leq I(X_{1};X_{2})=1.

(46)

Since
C\perp(A,B,S)
,
I(S;X_{2})=I(S;A)
.
Therefore,
I(S;X_{2})\leq H(A)=1
,
and the upper bound is attained by
S=A
. Hence the maximum
stage-wise relevance is one bit. Now let
S^{\star}
be any stage-wise optimizer. Then
I(S^{\star};A)=1
,
which implies that
H(A|S^{\star})=0
.
Moreover, we have that:

\displaystyle I(S^{\star};X_{1})

\displaystyle=I(S^{\star};A,B)

(47)

\displaystyle=I(S^{\star};A)+I(S^{\star};B|A)

(48)

\displaystyle=1+I(S^{\star};B|A).

(49)

The IM constraint
I(S^{\star};X_{1})\leq 1
therefore implies
I(S^{\star};B|A)=0
.
Thus,
S^{\star}-A-B
.
Since
A\perp B
, it follows that
S^{\star}\perp B
. In addition,

C\perp(A,B,S^{\star})
, and hence
I(S^{\star};X_{3})=I(S^{\star};B,C)=0
.
Therefore, every stage-wise optimizer has terminal value:

\min_{j\in\{1,2,3\}}I(S^{\star};X_{j})=0.

(50)

The same argument applies when
X_{3}
is processed first and, by
symmetry, for every choice of reference variable and ordering.
It therefore follows that:

R(X_{1},X_{2},X_{3})=C_{GK}(\mathbf{X})=0.

(51)

Let us now compute the relaxed upper bound
R_{\mathrm{ub}}
. Again fix

X_{1}
as the reference. Every relaxed feasible auxiliary
T

satisfies:

T-X_{1}-(X_{1},X_{2},X_{3}),\quad I(T;X_{1})\leq 1.

(52)

Because
C\perp(A,B,T)
, we have that:

I(T;X_{2})=I(T;A),\quad I(T;X_{3})=I(T;B).

(53)

Using
A\perp B
, we obtain:

\displaystyle I(T;X_{2})+I(T;X_{3})

\displaystyle=I(T;A)+I(T;B)

(54)

\displaystyle=I(T;A,B)-I(A;B|T)

(55)

\displaystyle\leq I(T;A,B)

(56)

\displaystyle=I(T;X_{1})

(57)

\displaystyle\leq 1.

(58)

It follows that:

\min_{j}I(T;X_{j})\leq\frac{1}{2}.

(59)

To attain the upper bound in (
59
), let

Q\sim\operatorname{Bern}(\tfrac{1}{2})
be independent of

(A,B,C)
, and define:

T=(Q,Z),\quad Z=\begin{cases}A,&Q=0,\\
B,&Q=1.\end{cases}

(60)

This auxiliary is generated locally from
X_{1}=(A,B)
, and hence:

T-X_{1}-(X_{1},X_{2},X_{3}).

(61)

Moreover,
I(T;X_{1})=I(T;A,B)=1
, so the IM constraint is satisfied with equality. Furthermore:

\displaystyle I(T;X_{2})

\displaystyle=I(T;A)=\frac{1}{2},

(62)

\displaystyle I(T;X_{3})

\displaystyle=I(T;B)=\frac{1}{2}.

(63)

Since
I(T;X_{1})=1
, the resulting terminal value is
\min_{j}I(T;X_{j})=\frac{1}{2}
.
Together with (
59
), this proves that for this reference the relaxed value is
1/2
. By symmetry, the same value is
obtained for every reference and ordering. Therefore:

R_{\mathrm{ub}}(X_{1},X_{2},X_{3})=\frac{1}{2},

(64)

and we have thus obtained:

\displaystyle R(X_{1},X_{2},X_{3})

\displaystyle=0

(65)

\displaystyle<\frac{1}{2}=R_{\mathrm{ub}}(X_{1},X_{2},X_{3})

(66)

\displaystyle<1=\min_{i\neq j}I(X_{i};X_{j}).

(67)

The relaxed upper bound balances the
A
- and
B
-components in a
single randomized representation, even though they are relevant to
different sources. On the other hand, stage-wise optimality prevents such a tradeoff:
processing
X_{2}
first requires retaining
A
maximally, which
removes the
B
-information which is irrelevant for
X_{2}
but relevant for
X_{3}
. This example therefore
illustrates both the strictness of
R_{\mathrm{ub}}
and the role of
stage-wise optimality in the HCI definition.

\triangle

II-C

Lower Bound to the HCI

The HCI is trivially lower bounded by zero, since the mutual information is non-negative. For discrete sources with finite alphabets, we can improve the lower bound by connecting the HCI to the Gács–Körner common information.

Theorem 1
(Gács–Körner lower bound)

Let
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
be finite-alphabet random variables. Then

R(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})\geq C_{\mathrm{GK}}(X_{1};\ldots;X_{n}),

(68)

where:

C_{\mathrm{GK}}(X_{1};\ldots;X_{n})\triangleq\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}V:\\
H(V|X_{j})=0,\;j=1,\ldots,n\end{subarray}}H(V).

(69)

Proof:

Let
V
be any deterministic common component of the sources such that:

H(V|X_{j})=0,\quad j=1,\ldots,n.

(70)

We first prove that if the current representation contains
V
, then
a stage-wise optimizer can be chosen to contain
V
as well.

Fix a stage with current representation
T
and current target
X
,
and suppose that:

H(V|T)=H(V|X)=0.

(71)

The available IM budget is:

\displaystyle\Delta

\displaystyle\triangleq I(T;X)

(72)

\displaystyle=I(V,T;X)

(73)

\displaystyle=H(V)+I(T;X|V).

(74)

We define
\delta\triangleq I(T;X|V)
,
so that:

\Delta=H(V)+\delta.

(75)

Let
S
be any auxiliary satisfying:

S-T-\mathbf{X},\quad I(S;T)\leq\Delta.

(76)

To simplify the notation, we define:

h\triangleq H(V|S),\quad r\triangleq I(S;T|V),\quad a\triangleq I(S;X|V).

(77)

Since
V
is a deterministic function of both
T
and
X
, it follows that:

\displaystyle I(S;T)

\displaystyle=H(V)-h+r,

(78)

\displaystyle I(S;X)

\displaystyle=H(V)-h+a.

(79)

Moreover,
S-T-X
remains a Markov chain conditionally on
V
, and
hence the data processing inequality yields:

a\leq r.

(80)

The feasibility condition and (
78
) imply
H(V)-h+r\leq H(V)+\delta
, which can equivalently be written as:

h\geq r-\delta.

(81)

We now construct a feasible auxiliary that explicitly retains
V
.
Conditionally on
V
, independently erase
S
with retention
probability
\lambda
, and denote the result by
W
. More precisely,
let
E\sim\operatorname{Bern}(\lambda)
be independent of

(S,T,\mathbf{X},V)
, and set:

W=\begin{cases}S,&E=1,\\
\mathtt{e},&E=0.\end{cases}

(82)

Moreover, define
S^{\prime}=(V,W)
. Then
S^{\prime}-T-\mathbf{X}
, and we have that:

\displaystyle I(S^{\prime};T)

\displaystyle=H(V)+\lambda r,

(83)

\displaystyle I(S^{\prime};X)

\displaystyle=H(V)+\lambda a.

(84)

We now choose
\lambda
such that:

\lambda\triangleq\begin{cases}1,&r\leq\delta,\\[1.99997pt]
\delta/r,&r>\delta.\end{cases}

(85)

It follows from (
83
) that:

I(S^{\prime};T)\leq H(V)+\delta=\Delta,

(86)

so
S^{\prime}
satisfies the IM constraint.

We next compare the relevance of
S^{\prime}
and
S
. If
r\leq\delta
,
then
\lambda=1
, and:

I(S^{\prime};X)=H(V)+a\geq H(V)-h+a=I(S;X).

(87)

If
r>\delta
, then
\lambda=\delta/r
. From
(
80
) and (
81
), it follows that:

a\left(1-\frac{\delta}{r}\right)\leq r-\delta\leq h.

(88)

Therefore, we finally obtain:

\displaystyle I(S^{\prime};X)

\displaystyle=H(V)+\frac{\delta}{r}a

(89)

\displaystyle\geq H(V)-h+a

(90)

\displaystyle=I(S;X).

(91)

Thus, every feasible
S
can be replaced by a feasible
S^{\prime}
that
contains
V
and has at least as much relevance to the current target.

It remains to enforce target minimality. Because the alphabets are
finite, let
G=g(S^{\prime})
be the minimal sufficient statistic of
S^{\prime}

for
X
, obtained by grouping values
s^{\prime}
that induce the same
conditional distribution
P_{X|S^{\prime}=s^{\prime}}
. Then, it follows that:

X-G-S^{\prime},\quad I(G;X)=I(S^{\prime};X),

(92)

and
G\in\mathsf{M}(X)
. Moreover, since
G
is a deterministic function of

S^{\prime}
:

I(G;T)\leq I(S^{\prime};T)\leq\Delta,

(93)

which implies that
G
remains feasible.

Since
V
is a deterministic function of both
X
and
S^{\prime}
and

X-G-S^{\prime}
, we obtain that:

H(V|G)=0.

(94)

Hence the target-minimal auxiliary
G
retains
V
.

We have so far proved that whenever the current
representation
T
contains
V
, every subsequent feasible auxiliary can be
replaced by a target-minimal feasible auxiliary that also contains

V
and has no smaller current-target relevance. Thus, a
stage-wise optimizer can always be selected such that it retains
V
.
By applying this result inductively for each stage, we can always find a feasible and optimal terminal auxiliary
T_{n-1}^{(i)}

such that
H(V|T_{n-1}^{(i)})=0
.
Since
V
is also a deterministic function of every
X_{j}
, we have that:

\displaystyle I(T_{n-1}^{(i)};X_{j})

\displaystyle\geq I(V;X_{j})

(95)

\displaystyle=H(V),\quad j=1,\ldots,n.

(96)

This holds for every reference index and every
ordering. Hence, we obtain:

R(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})\geq H(V).

(97)

Finally, maximizing over all deterministic common components
V
proves:

R(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})\geq C_{\mathrm{GK}}(X_{1};\ldots;X_{n}).

(98)

∎

II-D

Properties of the HCI

In this section we establish key properties of the HCI. We begin by the following lemma, which proves that when the supremum is attained in (
19
), then the IM constraint must be saturated. Thus, when restricting to sources where the supremum is attainable, one can in principle replace in the IM inequality constraint by an equality constraint without loss of optimality.
1
1

1

Equality in the IM constraint might not always be possible by deterministic functions, which is why we prefer to keep the inequality constraint.

Lemma 1

Let
S,T,X
be random variables satisfying
0<I(T;X)<\infty
.
If the stage-wise optimization:

\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}S-T-X\\
I(S;T)\leq I(T;X)\\
S\in\mathsf{M}(X)\end{subarray}}I(S;X)

(99)

is attained by
S^{\star}
, then
I(S^{\star};T)=I(T;X)
.

Proof:

We prove the lemma by forming a contradiction. Suppose, that
I(S^{\star};T)<I(T;X)
.
Since
S^{\star}-T-X
,

I(T;X)=I(S^{\star};X)+I(T;X|S^{\star}).

(100)

If
I(T;X|S^{\star})=0
, then
I(S^{\star};X)=I(T;X)
,
which contradicts
I(S^{\star};X)\leq I(S^{\star};T)<I(T;X)
.
Hence
I(T;X|S^{\star})>0
.

Because
T
takes values in a standard Borel space, there exists a
finite-valued measurable function
Q=q(T)
such that
I(Q;X|S^{\star})>0
.
Let
B\sim\operatorname{Bern}(\lambda)
be independent of

(S^{\star},T,X)
, and define
\widehat{S}=(S^{\star},W)
, where:

W=\begin{cases}Q,&B=1,\\
\mathtt{e},&B=0.\end{cases}

(101)

Then
\widehat{S}-T-X
, and we have that:

\displaystyle I(\widehat{S};T)

\displaystyle=I(W,S^{\star};T)

(102)

\displaystyle=I(S^{\star};T)+I(W;T|S^{\star})

(103)

\displaystyle=I(S^{\star};T)+H(W|S^{\star})-H(W|S^{\star},T)

(104)

\displaystyle=I(S^{\star};T)+\lambda H(Q|S^{\star})+(1-\lambda)H(\mathtt{e})

\displaystyle\quad-[\lambda H(Q|S^{\star},T)+(1-\lambda)H(\mathtt{e})]

(105)

\displaystyle=I(S^{\star};T)+\lambda H(Q|S^{\star}),

(106)

\displaystyle I(\widehat{S};X)

\displaystyle=I(S^{\star};X)+\lambda I(Q;X|S^{\star}).

(107)

Since
Q
is finite-valued,
H(Q|S^{\star})\leq\log_{2}|\mathcal{Q}|<\infty
.

Let
\eta\triangleq I(T;X)-I(S^{\star};T)>0
. We note that
H(Q|S^{\star})>0
, since otherwise

I(Q;X|S^{\star})=0
. Choose
\lambda
such that:

0<\lambda\leq\min\left\{1,\frac{\eta}{H(Q|S^{\star})}\right\}.

(108)

Then

I(\widehat{S};T)=I(S^{\star};T)+\lambda H(Q|S^{\star})\leq I(T;X),

(109)

whereas

I(\widehat{S};X)=I(S^{\star};X)+\lambda I(Q;X|S^{\star})>I(S^{\star};X).

(110)

Now if
\widehat{S}\notin\mathsf{M}(X)
, replace it by a target-minimal
sufficient statistic
G=g(\widehat{S})
for
X
. Then
X-G-\widehat{S}
, and hence
I(G;X)=I(\widehat{S};X)
and
I(G;T)\leq I(\widehat{S};T)
.
Thus
G
is feasible and satisfies
I(G;X)=I(\widehat{S};X)>I(S^{\star};X)
. However, this contradicts the stage-wise optimality of
S^{\star}
. Therefore,
I(S^{\star};T)=I(T;X)
.
∎

The following lemma demonstrates that if there are several terminal auxiliaries all achieving the optimal HCI value, then these optimal auxiliaries do not need to have much in common with each other.

Lemma 2

There exist finite-alphabet sources
X_{1},X_{2},X_{3}
for which two
target-minimal terminal auxiliaries
T_{2}^{\star}
and

\widetilde{T}_{2}^{\star}
both attain the HCI value, while
I(T_{2}^{\star};\widetilde{T}_{2}^{\star})=0
.

Proof:

Let
A,B,Q
be mutually independent

\operatorname{Bern}(\tfrac{1}{2})
random variables and define
X_{1}\triangleq(A,B)
, and
X_{2}=X_{3}\triangleq(Q,Y)
,
where:

Y\triangleq\begin{cases}A,&Q=0,\\
B,&Q=1.\end{cases}

(111)

We first choose
X_{1}
as the reference.
Since
X_{2}=X_{3}
, the two orderings are equivalent. Moreover,
I(X_{1};X_{2})=1
.

Let
S
be any feasible auxiliary at the
X_{2}
-stage. Then
S-(A,B)-(Q,Y)
and
I(S;A,B)\leq 1
.
Since
Q
is independent of
(S,A,B)
, we have that:

\displaystyle I(S;X_{2})

\displaystyle=I(S;Q,Y)

(112)

\displaystyle=I(S;Q)+I(S;Y|Q)

(113)

\displaystyle=I(S;Y|Q)

(114)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}I(S;A)+\frac{1}{2}I(S;B).

(115)

Because
A
and
B
are independent, we furthermore have that:

\displaystyle I(S;A)+I(S;B)

\displaystyle=I(S;A,B)+I(A;B)-I(A;B|S)

(116)

\displaystyle\leq I(S;A,B)

(117)

\displaystyle\leq 1.

(118)

Therefore,
I(S;X_{2})\leq\frac{1}{2}
.
Now consider
T_{2}^{\star}\triangleq A
and
\widetilde{T}_{2}^{\star}\triangleq B
.
Both auxiliaries satisfy the MC constraint and saturate the IM
constraint:

I(A;X_{1})=I(B;X_{1})=1=I(X_{1};X_{2}).

(119)

Furthermore,
I(A;X_{2})=I(B;X_{2})=\frac{1}{2}
.
Since
X_{3}=X_{2}
, we have that:

\displaystyle\min_{1\leq j\leq 3}I(A;X_{j})=\min_{1\leq j\leq 3}I(B;X_{j})=\frac{1}{2}.

(120)

The missing reference cases are simpler and in a similar way it can be shown that using
X_{2}
or
X_{3}
as reference yields the fixed-reference value 1, which is greater than when using
X_{1}
as the reference variable. Hence,
R(X_{1},X_{2},X_{3})=1/2
and both
A
and
B
attain it.
Nevertheless, because
A
and
B
are independent,
I(A;B)=0
. Therefore, even when the HCI is strictly positive, two terminal
optimizers do not necessarily share any mutual information.
∎

Lemma 3
(Pairwise mutual-information upper bound)

Let
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
be arbitrarily distributed random variables.
Then

R(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})\leq R_{\mathrm{ub}}(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})\leq\min_{i\neq j}I(X_{i};X_{j}).

(121)

Proof:

We have already establish that
R(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})\leq R_{\mathrm{ub}}(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})
. Thus, we only need to prove the last inequality.
Fix a reference index
i
, an ordering
\pi^{(i)}
, and an arbitrary
feasible auxiliary chain:

T_{1}^{(i)}=X_{i},\quad T_{2}^{(i)},\ldots,T_{n-1}^{(i)}.

(122)

The nested MC constraints imply, by composition, that the terminal
auxiliary satisfies:

T_{n-1}^{(i)}-X_{i}-\mathbf{X}.

(123)

Thus, for every
j\neq i
, the data-processing inequality
gives:

I(T_{n-1}^{(i)};X_{j})\leq I(X_{i};X_{j}).

(124)

Therefore, we must have that:

\displaystyle\min_{1\leq k\leq n}I(T_{n-1}^{(i)};X_{k})

\displaystyle\leq I(T_{n-1}^{(i)};X_{j})

(125)

\displaystyle\leq I(X_{i};X_{j}).

(126)

Since this holds for every
j\neq i
, we finally get:

\min_{k}I(T_{n-1}^{(i)};X_{k})\leq\min_{j\neq i}I(X_{i};X_{j}).

(127)

Taking the supremum over all feasible chains and then the maximum over
all orderings preserves the bound.
∎

It is well-known that the mutual information is invariant under invertible transformations
[
22
]
. We can show that a similar result holds for the HCI.

Lemma 4
(Invariance under componentwise invertible transformations)

Let
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
be arbitrarily distributed random variables.
For each
j
, let
Y_{j}=f_{j}(X_{j})
, where
f_{j}
is invertible, i.e.,
g_{j}(Y_{j})=X_{j}
exists.
Then:

R(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n})=R(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}).

(128)

Proof:

Let
\mathbf{X}=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})
and
\mathbf{Y}=(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n})
. Then, for every auxiliary random variable
T
:

I(T;Y_{j})=I(T;X_{j}),\quad j=1,\ldots,n.

(129)

Fix a reference index
i
, an ordering
\pi^{(i)}
, and a feasible
chain for
\mathbf{X}
:

T_{1}^{(i)}=X_{i},\quad T_{2}^{(i)},\ldots,T_{n-1}^{(i)}.

(130)

Construct a corresponding chain for
\mathbf{Y}
by setting:

\widehat{T}_{1}^{(i)}=Y_{i},\quad\widehat{T}_{\ell}^{(i)}=T_{\ell}^{(i)},\quad\ell=2,\ldots,n-1.

(131)

We first verify the MC constraints. Since
X_{i}
and
Y_{i}
determine
one another almost surely, the first-stage Markov chain
T_{2}^{(i)}-X_{i}-\mathbf{X}
is equivalent to
\widehat{T}_{2}^{(i)}-Y_{i}-\mathbf{Y}
.
For every subsequent stage, we similarly have that
T_{\ell+1}^{(i)}-T_{\ell}^{(i)}-\mathbf{X}

is equivalent to
\widehat{T}_{\ell+1}^{(i)}-\widehat{T}_{\ell}^{(i)}-\mathbf{Y}
.
Thus, the transformed chain satisfies all MC constraints.
We also have that the mutual-information invariance gives:

\displaystyle I(\widehat{T}_{2}^{(i)};\widehat{T}_{1}^{(i)})

\displaystyle=I(T_{2}^{(i)};Y_{i})

(132)

\displaystyle=I(T_{2}^{(i)};X_{i}),

(133)

and

I\!\left(\widehat{T}_{1}^{(i)};Y_{\pi^{(i)}(1)}\right)=I\!\left(X_{i};X_{\pi^{(i)}(1)}\right).

(134)

Hence the first IM constraint for the
\mathbf{Y}
-chain follows from
the corresponding IM constraint for the
\mathbf{X}
-chain.

For every
\ell\geq 2
,

I(\widehat{T}_{\ell+1}^{(i)};\widehat{T}_{\ell}^{(i)})=I(T_{\ell+1}^{(i)};T_{\ell}^{(i)}),

(135)

while

I\!\left(\widehat{T}_{\ell}^{(i)};Y_{\pi^{(i)}(\ell)}\right)=I\!\left(T_{\ell}^{(i)};X_{\pi^{(i)}(\ell)}\right).

(136)

Therefore, all subsequent IM constraints are also preserved. Moreover, all mutual information expressions are preserved.
Thus, every feasible chain for
\mathbf{X}
induces a feasible chain
for
\mathbf{Y}
with the same terminal value and vice versa. It follows that:

R(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n})=R(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}).

(137)

∎

Lemma 5
(Vanishing under pairwise independence)

Let
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
be arbitrarily distributed random variables. If
there exist indices
i\neq j
such that
I(X_{i};X_{j})=0
,
then
R(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})=0
.

Proof:

By the pairwise upper bound,
R(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})\leq\min_{k\neq\ell}I(X_{k};X_{\ell})\leq I(X_{i};X_{j})=0.

Moreover, since mutual information is nonnegative, the HCI is also nonnegative.
∎

Lemma 6
(Entropy upper bound)

Let
n\geq 2
, and let
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
be discrete random variables.
Then

0\leq R(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})\leq\min_{1\leq i\leq n}H(X_{i}).

(138)

Proof:

The upper bound follows from the pairwise upper bound in Lemma
3
, and the that fact that
I(X_{i};X_{j})\leq\min(H(X_{i}),H(X_{j}))
for discrete
X_{i},X_{j}
. The lower bound follows since the mutual information is non-negative.
∎

Lemma 7

Let
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
be discrete random variables satisfying
X_{1}=\cdots=X_{n}
and
H(X_{1})<\infty
. Then

R(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})=H(X_{1}).

(139)

Proof:

Before presenting the main theorem, we introduce a notion of order statistics of correlations, which will be needed in the sequel.
Let
X_{1},\dotsc,X_{n}
be jointly distributed random variables.
Let

and
X_{k}

Fix

The

(HCI for Gaussian sources)

is given by:

V
Approximating Common Information for General Sources

In this section, we consider general sources and propose a practical
procedure for constructing an HCI-inspired approximation from
finite-length data. The procedure is motivated by the iterative
information-bottleneck construction, in which each auxiliary must
satisfy the Markov-chain, information-matching, and target-minimality
constraints while maximizing relevance to the current target source.
Our approximation uses the additive Gaussian test channels that are
optimal for jointly Gaussian sources. At each stage, the auxiliary is
constructed by adding independent zero-mean Gaussian noise to the
current representation. The noise variance is selected so that the
information-matching constraint in (
1
) is approximately saturated:

I(T_{\ell+1};T_{\ell})\approx I(T_{\ell};X_{\pi(\ell)}).

(187)

This choice is also motivated by
Lemma
1
, which shows that, when the stage-wise
maximum is attained and the IM budget is finite, every stage-wise
maximizer must saturate the IM constraint.
Saturation is, however, only a necessary condition for stage-wise
optimality. For non-Gaussian sources, an additive Gaussian channel need
not maximize the source-relevance
I(T_{\ell+1};X_{\pi(\ell)})
among all feasible stochastic kernels, nor is target minimality
automatically guaranteed. In addition, mutual-information estimation
from finite data permits only approximate enforcement of the IM
constraint. Thus, the resulting quantity should be interpreted as an HCI-inspired numerical approximation
that is exact for the Gaussian models covered by
Theorem

Conclusions

We introduced the concept of higher-order common information for quantifying shared information among
n
random variables. Our results enable the assessment of multivariate dependencies beyond pairwise interactions. Additionally, we proposed a practical method for approximating the higher-order common information from arbitrary data, providing a new tool for uncovering inherent structure in complex, real-world datasets.
