Schwinger pair production in counterpropagating laser pulses: Identifying volume factors
A. G. Tkachev, I. A. Aleksandrov, V. M. Shabaev

Abstract
We investigate the nonperturbative process of vacuum pair production in a combination of two counterpropagating linearly polarized laser pulses of a finite spatial extent. By means of the locally-constant field approximation (LCFA), we calculate the total particle yield for the corresponding four-dimensional setup and compare it with the estimates obtained for simplified low-dimensional scenarios. Within the domain where the LCFA is well justified, we examine a combination of two plane-wave pulses, a standing electromagnetic wave, and a spatially uniform oscillating field and demonstrate that at each of these three levels of approximation, one can accurately predict the actual particle number by multiplying the results by properly chosen volume factors depending on the field parameters. We present closed-form expressions for these factors providing universal prescriptions for evaluating the particle yield. Our final formula connecting the spatially uniform setup with the four-dimensional scenario has a relative uncertainty of the level of 10 % 10\% . The explicit correspondences deduced in this study not only prove the relevance of the approximate predictions, but also allow one to quickly estimate the number of pairs for various realistic scenarios without performing multidimensional LCFA integrations.

II
External field configurations

A combination of two counterpropagating linearly polarized laser pulses will be described by the following expressions for the electric and magnetic field components:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{E}(t,\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle[\mathfrak{E}(\boldsymbol{x},\omega t-\omega z)+\mathfrak{E}(\boldsymbol{x},\omega t+\omega z)]\boldsymbol{e}_{x},

(1)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{H}(t,\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle[\mathfrak{E}(\boldsymbol{x},\omega t-\omega z)-\mathfrak{E}(\boldsymbol{x},\omega t+\omega z)]\boldsymbol{e}_{y},

(2)

where
\{\boldsymbol{e}_{i}\}
are the unit vectors along the Cartesian axes,
\boldsymbol{x}=x\boldsymbol{e}_{x}+y\boldsymbol{e}_{y}+z\boldsymbol{e}_{z}
,
\mathfrak{E}(\boldsymbol{x},\eta)
describes an individual laser pulse, and
\omega
is the corresponding carrier frequency. The most realistic field configuration within this study will involve two
Gaussian beams
of the following form:

\displaystyle\mathfrak{E}^{(\text{G})}(\boldsymbol{x},\eta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{E_{0}}{2}F(\eta)\frac{w_{0}}{w(z)}\,\mathrm{exp}\bigg[-\frac{x^{2}+y^{2}}{w^{2}(z)}\bigg]

(3)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\cos\bigg[\eta+\frac{\omega(x^{2}+y^{2})}{2R(z)}-\psi(z)\bigg],

where

\displaystyle w(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle w_{0}\sqrt{1+z^{2}/z_{\text{R}}^{2}},

(4)

\displaystyle R(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle z\Big(1+z_{\text{R}}^{2}/z^{2}\Big),

(5)

\displaystyle\psi(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{arctan}\penalty\ (z/z_{\text{R}}).

(6)

Here
w_{0}
is the waist radius,
z_{\text{R}}=\pi w_{0}^{2}/\lambda
is the Rayleigh range, and
\lambda=2\pi/\omega
. The function
F(\eta)
is a dimensionless smooth envelope function which vanishes for sufficiently large
|\eta|
. Within the paraxial approximation (
3
), we assume
w_{0}\gg\lambda
, which is equivalent to the condition
\theta\ll 1
, where
\theta=\lambda/(\pi w_{0})
is the beam divergence. We will specify the corresponding setup
\boldsymbol{E}^{(\text{G})}(t,\boldsymbol{x})
,
\boldsymbol{H}^{(\text{G})}(t,\boldsymbol{x})
by choosing the values of
E_{0}
,
\omega
, and
w_{0}
. The interaction volume is governed by the length scales
\lambda
and
w_{0}
.

In order to substantially simplify the structure of the external field, one can neglect the transverse coordinate dependence and set
w_{0}/w(z)=1
,
\psi(z)=0
. In this case, each Gaussian pulse turns into a plane electromagnetic wave which is infinite in the transverse
x
and
y

z
axis
[
22
,
30
,
31
,
32
,
33
]
. Within this
plane-wave approximation
(PWA), the resulting external field reads

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{E}^{(\text{PWA})}(t,z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{E_{0}}{2}\Big[F(\omega t-\omega z)\cos(\omega t-\omega z)

(7)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle F(\omega t+\omega z)\cos(\omega t+\omega z)\Big]\boldsymbol{e}_{x},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{H}^{(\text{PWA})}(t,z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{E_{0}}{2}\Big[F(\omega t-\omega z)\cos(\omega t-\omega z)

(8)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle F(\omega t+\omega z)\cos(\omega t+\omega z)\Big]\boldsymbol{e}_{y}.

To further simplify the setup, one can completely disregard the spatial dependence of the envelope function, i.e., one can replace
F(\omega t\pm\omega z)
with
F(\omega t)
. In this case, the external field will represent a standing electromagnetic wave, which is infinite in all of the spatial directions
[
22
,
18
,
27
,
49
,
51
,
52
]
. The field configuration within the
standing-wave approximation
(SWA) has the following form:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{E}^{(\text{SWA})}(t,z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E_{0}F(\omega t)\cos\omega t\cos\omega z\,\boldsymbol{e}_{x},

(9)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{H}^{(\text{SWA})}(t,z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E_{0}F(\omega t)\sin\omega t\sin\omega z\,\boldsymbol{e}_{y}.

(10)

Finally, one can approximate the external field by a spatially homogeneous background assuming that the particles are predominantly produced in the vicinity of the electric-field maxima. Here we set
z=0
and obtain

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{E}^{(\text{DA})}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E_{0}F(\omega t)\cos\omega t\,\boldsymbol{e}_{x},

(11)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{H}^{(\text{DA})}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0.

(12)

This approach will be called the

(DA).

We underline that while the two Gaussian beams (G) are localized along each of the three spatial axes, the PWA setup is finite only with respect to the
z
axis and the SWA and DA fields are infinite along each of the three directions. This means that while computing the number of
e^{+}e^{-}
pairs, one has to take into account the corresponding volume factor depending on the approximation chosen. In what follows, we will identify these factors and provide simple prescriptions allowing one to map the results obtained for the four scenarios described above (DA
\to
SWA
\to

III
Locally-constant field approximation

Here we will first present a general LCFA expression for the total number of pairs produced and then apply it to the specific field configurations described above.

III.1
General expression

The LCFA is based on the following closed-form expression for the total number of pairs produced per unit volume and time in the presence of a constant electromagnetic field
[
53
]
:

\frac{dN}{dtd\boldsymbol{x}}\,[\mathcal{E},\mathcal{H}]=\frac{e^{2}\mathcal{E}\mathcal{H}}{4\pi^{2}}\coth{\frac{\pi\mathcal{H}}{\mathcal{E}}}\,\mathrm{exp}\bigg(\!-\frac{\pi E_{\text{c}}}{\mathcal{E}}\bigg),

(13)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\sqrt{\mathcal{F}^{2}+\mathcal{G}^{2}}+\mathcal{F}},

(14)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\sqrt{\mathcal{F}^{2}+\mathcal{G}^{2}}-\mathcal{F}}.

(15)

The Lorentz invariant quantities
\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{G}
are defined via
\mathcal{F}=(\boldsymbol{E}^{2}-\boldsymbol{H}^{2})/2
and
\mathcal{G}=\boldsymbol{E}\cdot\boldsymbol{H}
.

Within the LCFA, one plugs the actual spatiotemporal dependence of the external inhomogeneous field into Eq. (
13
) and integrates it then over
t
and
\boldsymbol{x}
(see Refs.
[
40
,
41
,
42
,
43
,
44
,
45
,
46
,
47
,
49
,
48
,
50
]
):

N=\int d^{4}\mathrm{x}\,\frac{dN}{dtd\boldsymbol{x}}\,[\mathcal{E}(\mathrm{x}),\mathcal{H}(\mathrm{x})],

(16)

where
\mathrm{x}=(t,\,\boldsymbol{x})
.

In the present study, the total number of particles will be evaluated by means of Eq. (
16
). Let us now discuss the validity of the LCFA. Since the temporal and spatial variations of the external field are governed by the laser frequency
\omega
, the LCFA is generally well justified once
(E_{0}/E_{\text{c}})^{3/2}\gg\omega/m

[
49
]
. This criterion was reliably established in the case of a spatially uniform Sauter-like pulse in Ref.
[
49
]
(see also Ref.
[
48
]
) and also confirmed by means of numerical calculations for an oscillating laser field and a two-dimensional setup representing a standing electromagnetic wave
[
49
]
. In Appendix
A
, we explicitly derive exactly the same criterion in the case of a static
x
-dependent Sauter-like field. For a realistic laser wavelength of the order of
1\penalty\ $\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}$
, this condition yields
E_{0}/E_{\text{c}}\gg 10^{-4}
. As will be shown below the pair production threshold in terms of the ratio
E_{0}/E_{\text{c}}
for this wavelength amounts to several hundredth, so we will be mainly focused on the interval
0.01\leqslant E_{0}/E_{\text{c}}\leqslant 0.1
. Here the LCFA is definitely valid as even
0.01
is much larger than
10^{-4}
. On the other hand, if we consider the minimal value
E_{0}=0.01E_{\text{c}}
used in our calculations, then the laser frequency and wavelength will have to satisfy
\omega/m\ll 10^{-3}
and
\lambda\gg 4\times 10^{-4}\penalty\ $\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}$
, respectively, which is completely realistic. We also note that according to the numerical data presented in Fig. 1 of Ref.
[
49
]
in the case of a Sauter pulse, the relative error of the LCFA is less than
3\%
already for
(m/\omega)(E_{0}/E_{\text{c}})^{3/2}=10
. In our investigation, this parameter has a minimum value of the order of
10^{3}
for
E_{0}=0.01E_{\text{c}}
and a laser wavelength of
1\penalty\ $\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}$
. This suggests that the LCFA itself introduces a negligible uncertainty within the whole domain of the field parameters considered in our study. We also mention that the Keldysh parameter
\gamma\equiv m\omega/|eE_{0}|
does not govern the accuracy of the LCFA as the condition
\gamma\ll 1
is weaker than the above criterion
[
48
,
49
]
.

III.2
Dipole approximation (DA)

If the external field does not depend on the spatial coordinates, then Eq. (
16
) takes the following simple form:

\frac{N^{(\text{DA})}}{V}=\frac{e^{2}}{4\pi^{3}}\int\limits_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\!dt\,E^{2}(t)\,\mathrm{exp}\left[-\frac{\pi E_{\text{c}}}{|E(t)|}\right],

(17)

where
V=\int d\boldsymbol{x}
is the normalization volume and
E(t)
is the corresponding electric field strength. In our case,

E(t)=E_{0}F(\omega t)\cos\omega t.

(18)

Since the field depends on
t
via the product
\omega t
, one can substitute
\eta=\omega t
in Eq. (
17
) and immediately reveal that the right-hand side is inversely proportional to
\omega
. Accordingly, we will discuss the results in terms of the following dimensionless
\omega
-independent quantity:

\nu^{(\text{DA})}=\frac{\omega N^{(\text{DA})}}{m^{4}V}.

(19)

Its explicit form is given by

\nu^{(\text{DA})}=\frac{1}{4\pi^{3}}\frac{E_{0}^{2}}{E_{\text{c}}^{2}}\int\limits_{-\infty}^{+\infty}d\eta f^{2}(\eta)\,\mathrm{exp}\left[-\frac{\pi E_{\text{c}}}{E_{0}|f(\eta)|}\right],

(20)

where

f(\eta)=F(\eta)\cos\eta.

(21)

III.3
Standing-wave approximation (SWA)

Here we again factor out the
\omega
dependence:

\nu^{(\text{SWA})}=\frac{\omega N^{(\text{SWA})}}{m^{4}V}.

(22)

Let us introduce the following
\omega
-independent function representing a Lorentz invariant:

\mathcal{F}(\eta,\xi)=\frac{1}{2}\Big\{\big[\boldsymbol{E}^{(\text{SWA})}(\eta/\omega,\xi/\omega)\big]^{2}-\big[\boldsymbol{H}^{(\text{SWA})}(\eta/\omega,\xi/\omega)\big]^{2}\Big\}.

(23)

Taking into account Eqs. (
9
) and (
10
), one obtains

\mathcal{F}(\eta,\xi)=\frac{E_{0}^{2}}{2}\,F^{2}(\eta)\cos(\eta+\xi)\cos(\eta-\xi).

(24)

Then we will calculate

\displaystyle\nu^{(\text{SWA})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4\pi^{4}}\int\limits_{-\infty}^{+\infty}d\eta\int\limits_{-\pi}^{\pi}d\xi\theta(\mathcal{F}(\eta,\xi))

(25)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\frac{\mathcal{F}(\eta,\xi)}{E_{\text{c}}^{2}}\,\mathrm{exp}\left[-\frac{\pi E_{\text{c}}}{\sqrt{2\mathcal{F}(\eta,\xi)}}\right].

Here the Heaviside step function
\theta
indicates that
e^{+}e^{-}
pairs are generated only in the space-time regions where the electric field is stronger than the magnetic component. If the latter is not the case, one can Lorentz transform the external field, so that it becomes purely magnetic and, thus, does not produce pairs.

III.4
Plane-wave approximation (PWA)

Since the external field is now finite along the
z
axis, the total number of particles per unit transverse cross sectional area
S
is also finite. We will compute the following quantity:

\nu^{(\text{PWA})}=\frac{\omega^{2}N^{(\text{PWA})}}{m^{4}S}.

(26)

It also does not depend on
\omega
. Here it is convenient to introduce
\eta_{-}=\omega(t-z)
and
\eta_{+}=\omega(t+z)
. We find

\displaystyle\nu^{(\text{PWA})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{8\pi^{3}}\frac{E_{0}^{2}}{E_{\text{c}}^{2}}\int\limits_{-\infty}^{+\infty}d\eta_{-}\int\limits_{-\infty}^{+\infty}d\eta_{+}\theta(f(\eta_{-})f(\eta_{+}))

(27)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle f(\eta_{-})f(\eta_{+})\,\mathrm{exp}\left[-\frac{\pi E_{\text{c}}}{E_{0}\sqrt{f(\eta_{-})f(\eta_{+})}}\right],

where
f(\eta)
is defined in Eq. (
21
).

III.5
Gaussian beams (G)

The invariant
\mathcal{F}
is now given by

\mathcal{F}=2\mathfrak{E}(\boldsymbol{x},\omega t-\omega z)\mathfrak{E}(\boldsymbol{x},\omega t+\omega z).

(28)

Let us introduce

g(\boldsymbol{x},\eta)=F(\eta)\cos\bigg[\eta+\frac{\omega(x^{2}+y^{2})}{2R(z)}-\psi(z)\bigg].

(29)

Then

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{E_{0}^{2}}{2}\frac{w_{0}^{2}}{w^{2}(z)}\,\mathrm{exp}\bigg[-\frac{2(x^{2}+y^{2})}{w^{2}(z)}\bigg]

(30)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle g(\boldsymbol{x},\omega t-\omega z)g(\boldsymbol{x},\omega t+\omega z).

In order to calculate the total number of pairs
N
according to Eq. (
16
), we again introduce
\eta_{\pm}=\omega(t\pm z)
and also rescale the variables
x
and
y
as
x=w_{0}\rho_{x}
,
y=w_{0}\rho_{y}
. Since
z=(\eta_{+}-\eta_{-})/(2\omega)
, we represent
w(z)
as

w(z)=w_{0}\rho(\eta_{+}-\eta_{-}),

(31)

where

\rho(\eta)=\sqrt{1+\frac{\eta^{2}}{4\omega^{2}z_{\text{R}}^{2}}}=\sqrt{1+\bigg(\frac{\eta}{\omega^{2}w_{0}^{2}}\bigg)^{2}}.

(32)

We now obtain

\displaystyle N^{(\text{G})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{8\pi^{3}}\frac{m^{4}}{\omega^{4}}\frac{E_{0}^{2}}{E_{\text{c}}^{2}}\,(\omega w_{0})^{2}\int d\rho_{x}d\rho_{y}d\eta_{-}d\eta_{+}

(33)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\theta(g(\boldsymbol{x},\eta_{-})g(\boldsymbol{x},\eta_{+}))\frac{1}{\rho^{2}(\eta_{+}-\eta_{-})}

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\mathrm{exp}\bigg[-\frac{2(\rho_{x}^{2}+\rho_{y}^{2})}{\rho^{2}(\eta_{+}-\eta_{-})}\bigg]g(\boldsymbol{x},\eta_{-})g(\boldsymbol{x},\eta_{+})

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\mathrm{exp}\Bigg\{\!-\frac{\pi E_{\text{c}}}{E_{0}}\rho(\eta_{+}-\eta_{-})\,\mathrm{exp}\bigg[\frac{\rho_{x}^{2}+\rho_{y}^{2}}{\rho^{2}(\eta_{+}-\eta_{-})}\bigg]

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{g(\boldsymbol{x},\eta_{-})g(\boldsymbol{x},\eta_{+})}}\Bigg\},

where in the functions
g(\boldsymbol{x},\eta_{\pm})
we imply
x=w_{0}\rho_{x}
,
y=w_{0}\rho_{y}
, and
z=(\eta_{+}-\eta_{-})/(2\omega)
. Finally, we employ polar coordinates in the
\rho_{x}\rho_{y}
plane, integrate over the polar angle, and substitute
\chi=\rho^{2}
, where
\rho
is the corresponding radius,
\rho=\sqrt{\rho_{x}^{2}+\rho_{y}^{2}}
. This brings us to the following final expression:

\displaystyle N^{(\text{G})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{8\pi^{2}}\frac{m^{4}}{\omega^{4}}\frac{E_{0}^{2}}{E_{\text{c}}^{2}}\,(\omega w_{0})^{2}\int\limits_{0}^{+\infty}d\chi\int\limits_{-\infty}^{+\infty}d\eta_{-}\int\limits_{-\infty}^{+\infty}d\eta_{+}

(34)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\theta(\tilde{g}(\chi,z,\eta_{-})\tilde{g}(\chi,z,\eta_{+}))\frac{1}{\rho^{2}(\eta_{+}-\eta_{-})}

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\mathrm{exp}\bigg[-\frac{2\chi}{\rho^{2}(\eta_{+}-\eta_{-})}\bigg]\tilde{g}(\chi,z,\eta_{-})\tilde{g}(\chi,z,\eta_{+})

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\mathrm{exp}\Bigg\{\!-\frac{\pi E_{\text{c}}}{E_{0}}\rho(\eta_{+}-\eta_{-})\,\mathrm{exp}\bigg[\frac{\chi}{\rho^{2}(\eta_{+}-\eta_{-})}\bigg]

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{\tilde{g}(\chi,z,\eta_{-})\tilde{g}(\chi,z,\eta_{+})}}\Bigg\},

where

\tilde{g}(\chi,z,\eta)\equiv F(\eta)\cos\bigg[\eta+\frac{\omega w_{0}^{2}\chi}{2R(z)}-\psi(z)\bigg]

(35)

and
z=(\eta_{+}-\eta_{-})/(2\omega)
.

Here the result is finite and depends on the field parameters
E_{0}
,
\omega
, and
w_{0}
. Nevertheless, as can be easily seen, the integrand in Eq. (
34
) involves only
E_{0}
and the product
\omega w_{0}=2/\theta
, so it is convenient to define

\nu^{(\text{G})}=\frac{\omega^{4}}{m^{4}}\,N^{(\text{G})},

(36)

which depends only on
E_{0}
and
\theta
. As was mentioned above, the paraxial approximation is valid for sufficiently small values of
\theta
, i.e., large
\omega w_{0}
.

IV
Results

Here we will numerically evaluate Eqs. (

), (
25
), (

), and (

). By comparing the results of the different approximations, we aim at deriving simple closed-form expressions that establish the corresponding relations among them. In our numerical calculations, we employ the Gaussian envelope

F(\eta)=\mathrm{e}^{-(\eta/\sigma)^{2}},

where
\sigma
is a dimensionless parameter governing the laser pulse duration.

In what follows, we will separately discuss the transitions DA
\to
SWA, SWA
\to

\to

IV.1
SWA versus DA

According to our numerical results, the DA always overestimates the particle yield, which is no surprise as this approximation neglects the spatial profile of the external field by replacing it with unity and, thus, notably increases the local values of the field strength. Within the SWA, the particles are primarily produced in the vicinity of the maxima of the standing-wave electric component, which will be taken into account for identifying the ratio
\nu^{(\text{SWA})}/\nu^{(\text{DA})}
in the form of simple analytical expressions. To this end, we will proceed in two different directions: we will first develop a simple phenomenological model and then derive the asymptotic expressions for
\nu^{(\text{DA})}
and
\nu^{(\text{SWA})}
in the realistic regime
E_{0}\ll E_{\text{c}}
by means of Laplace’s method.

IV.1.1
Phenomenological model

As was pointed out above, within the SWA, the main contributions to the particle yield arise in the vicinities of the maxima of
\cos\omega z
in Eq. (
9
). Let us consider
z\sim 0
and identify the vicinity
(-\delta,\delta)
providing the dominant contribution to the particle number within the corresponding cycle of the cosine profile. According to Eq. (
13
), we suggest using the following relation:

\cos^{2}\omega\delta\,\mathrm{exp}\left(-\frac{\pi E_{\text{c}}}{E_{0}\cos\omega\delta}\right)=\zeta\,\mathrm{exp}\left(-\frac{\pi E_{\text{c}}}{E_{0}}\right),

(38)

which indicates that the particle density
dN/(dtd\boldsymbol{x})
at
z=\delta
equals its value at
z=0
multiplied by a small number
\zeta
. The latter will be determined by fitting our numerical results. Equation (
38
) can be solved numerically yielding the function
\delta=\delta_{\zeta}(E_{0})
. The corresponding “reduction factor” is found then via

\frac{\nu^{(\text{SWA})}}{\nu^{(\text{DA})}}\approx D_{\zeta}(E_{0})\equiv\frac{2\delta_{\zeta}(E_{0})}{(2\pi/\omega)}=\frac{\omega\delta_{\zeta}(E_{0})}{\pi}.

(39)

The crucial question here is whether one can find a
universal
value of
\zeta
, so that
\nu^{(\text{SWA})}/\nu^{(\text{DA})}\approx D_{\zeta}(E_{0})
for all
E_{0}
. By calculating the ratio
\nu^{(\text{SWA})}/\nu^{(\text{DA})}
numerically, we will identify
\zeta
which generates the most accurate approximation
D_{\zeta}(E_{0})
. In the weak-field limit
E_{0}\ll E_{\text{c}}
, one can construct an approximate closed-form solution to Eq. (
38
) (see Appendix
B
):

D_{\zeta}^{(0)}(E_{0})=\frac{\sqrt{2|\ln{\zeta}|}}{\pi^{3/2}}\,\sqrt{\frac{E_{0}}{E_{\text{c}}}}\\
\times\bigg[1-\frac{1}{\pi}\Big(1-\frac{5}{12}\ln{\zeta}\Big)\,\frac{E_{0}}{E_{\text{c}}}+\mathcal{O}\bigg(\frac{E_{0}^{2}}{E_{c}^{2}}\bigg)\bigg].

(40)

to only
1\%
. Our approximations can, of course, be further improved by incorporating the higher-order terms with respect to
E_{0}/E_{\text{c}}
, but it appears to be redundant since the accuracy is already very high, so we will opt to relatively simple analytical expressions. We also point out that, e.g., within the interval
0.01E_{\text{c}}\leqslant E_{0}\leqslant 0.1E_{\text{c}}

The analysis performed in this section also provides additional insights concerning the validity of the LCFA. The condition
(E_{0}/E_{\text{c}})^{3/2}\gg\omega/m
discussed above is equivalent to the requirement that the effective vicinity
\delta
be much larger than the pair formation length
m/|eE_{0}|
. This is equivalent to
D_{\zeta}(E_{0})\gg\gamma
, where
\gamma=m\omega/|eE_{0}|
is the Keldysh parameter characterizing the laser field. For a realistic laser wavelength of
1\penalty\ $\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}$

E_{0}>0.01E_{\text{c}}

\gamma<3.9\times 10^{-5}
, which definitely ensures
\gamma\ll D_{\zeta}(E_{0})

D_{\zeta}(E_{0})\gtrsim 0.05
according to Fig.

.

Although the above prescriptions (
39
) and (
40
) turn out to be very efficient for
\zeta=0.043
, the origin of the latter value of the fitting parameter is not yet clear as it was merely adjusted by comparing our numerical data. In what follows, we will estimate the ratio
\nu^{(\text{SWA})}/\nu^{(\text{DA})}
within the weak-field domain

in a systematic way by means of Laplace’s method and show why
\zeta
should correspond to
\mathrm{e}^{-\pi}\approx 0.0432
. The approach based on Laplace’s method will be our main analytical tool throughout the remaining part of the paper.

IV.1.2
Weak-field regime for DA and SWA via Laplace’s method

Since we are primarily interested in the domain of subcritical fields, we can consider the ratio
E_{0}/E_{\text{c}}
as a small parameter and invoke Laplace’s method for approximately evaluating the LCFA integrals. Let us begin with the analysis of
\nu^{(\text{DA})}
in Eq. (
20
). The main contribution comes from a small vicinity of the origin
\eta=0
, so we first construct the following expansions:

\displaystyle f(\eta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1-\bigg(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\bigg)\eta^{2}+\mathcal{O}(\eta^{4}),

(41)

\displaystyle f^{2}(\eta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1-\bigg(1+\frac{2}{\sigma^{2}}\bigg)\eta^{2}+\mathcal{O}(\eta^{4}),

(42)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|f(\eta)|}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1+\bigg(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\bigg)\eta^{2}

(43)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{12+12\sigma^{2}+5\sigma^{4}}{24\sigma^{4}}\,\eta^{4}+\mathcal{O}(\eta^{6}).

The last identity immediately leads to

\displaystyle\mathrm{exp}\left[-\frac{\pi E_{\text{c}}}{E_{0}|f(\eta)|}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{e}^{-\pi E_{\text{c}}/E_{0}}\,\mathrm{exp}\left(-\frac{\pi E_{\text{c}}}{E_{0}}\frac{2+\sigma^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\,\eta^{2}\right)

(44)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\bigg[1-\frac{\pi E_{\text{c}}}{E_{0}}\frac{12+12\sigma^{2}+5\sigma^{4}}{24\sigma^{4}}\,\eta^{4}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}\bigg(\frac{\eta^{6}E_{\text{c}}}{E_{0}}\bigg)+\mathcal{O}\bigg(\frac{\eta^{8}E^{2}_{\text{c}}}{E^{2}_{0}}\bigg)\bigg].

Here we keep in mind that
\eta\lesssim\sqrt{E_{0}/E_{\text{c}}}
according to the Gaussian profile in the first line of Eq. (
44
). Now we should only evaluate the integral in Eq. (
20
) using the expansions (
42
) and (
44
) (the vicinity of
\eta=0
can be stretched to the whole real axis). The result reads

\nu^{(\text{DA})}\approx\frac{1}{4\pi^{3}}\,\sqrt{\frac{2\sigma^{2}}{2+\sigma^{2}}}\bigg(\frac{E_{0}}{E_{\text{c}}}\bigg)^{5/2}\mathrm{e}^{-\pi E_{\text{c}}/E_{0}}\\
{}\times\bigg[1-\frac{44+44\sigma^{2}+13\sigma^{4}}{8\pi(2+\sigma^{2})^{2}}\frac{E_{0}}{E_{\text{c}}}+\mathcal{O}\bigg(\frac{E_{0}^{2}}{E_{\text{c}}^{2}}\bigg)\bigg].

(45)

The
\sigma
-dependent coefficient in the second line rapidly stabilizes with increasing
\sigma
, so we will use a simpler expression,

\nu^{(\text{DA})}\approx\frac{1}{4\pi^{3}}\,\sqrt{\frac{2\sigma^{2}}{2+\sigma^{2}}}\bigg(\frac{E_{0}}{E_{\text{c}}}\bigg)^{5/2}\mathrm{e}^{-\pi E_{\text{c}}/E_{0}}\\
{}\times\bigg[1-\frac{13}{8\pi}\frac{E_{0}}{E_{\text{c}}}+\mathcal{O}\bigg(\frac{E_{0}^{2}}{E_{\text{c}}^{2}}\bigg)\bigg].

(46)

As will be demonstrated below, this formula is already very accurate for
E_{0}\lesssim 0.1E_{\text{c}}
and sufficiently small
\sigma
(
\sigma\sim 5
), while for larger values of
\sigma
it is also necessary to take into account the side maxima of
|f(\eta)|
, i.e., those located near
\eta=\pm\pi
.

First, we easily find that any maximum
\eta_{0}
of
|f(\eta)|
obeys

\tan{\eta_{0}}=-\frac{2\eta_{0}}{\sigma^{2}}.

(47)

Since the side maxima play a non-negligible role only for large
\sigma
, we will use the following expansion for the first positive solution of Eq. (
47
):

\eta_{0}=\pi-\frac{2\pi}{\sigma^{2}}+\mathcal{O}\bigg(\frac{1}{\sigma^{4}}\bigg).

(48)

The rest part of the calculation is very similar to the above derivations, so we will only present the final expression:

\displaystyle\nu^{(\text{DA})}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4\pi^{3}}\,\sqrt{\frac{2\sigma^{2}}{2+\sigma^{2}}}\bigg(\frac{E_{0}}{E_{\text{c}}}\bigg)^{5/2}

(49)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\bigg\{\mathrm{e}^{-\pi E_{\text{c}}/E_{0}}\bigg[1-\frac{13}{8\pi}\frac{E_{0}}{E_{\text{c}}}+\mathcal{O}\bigg(\frac{E_{0}^{2}}{E_{\text{c}}^{2}}\bigg)\bigg]

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle 2\big(\mathrm{e}^{-\pi^{2}/\sigma^{2}}\big)^{5/2}\,\mathrm{exp}\left(-\frac{\pi E_{\text{c}}}{E_{0}}\,\mathrm{e}^{\pi^{2}/\sigma^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\bigg[1+\mathcal{O}\bigg(\frac{E_{0}}{E_{\text{c}}}\bigg)+\mathcal{O}\bigg(\frac{1}{\sigma^{4}}\bigg)\bigg]\bigg\}.

Here we have disregarded the linear terms with respect to the field strength in the prefactor of the side-maxima contributions. We observe that the additional term coincides with the previously found main contribution (
46
) with a rescaled field amplitude,
E_{0}\to E_{0}\,\mathrm{exp}(-\pi^{2}/\sigma^{2})
(it is multiplied by a factor of
2
since there are two side maxima near
\pm\pi
). This simple prescription is only valid for the leading-order contribution displayed in the third and fourth lines of Eq. (
49
).

The quality of this approximation is demonstrated in Fig.

. In principle, one can straightforwardly take into account the next maxima of the external field, but it is not necessary even for large value of

) and (

IV.1.3
SWA-to-DA ratio via Laplace’s method

Consider the ratio of the expressions (
49
) and (
50
). The side-maxima corrections to
\nu^{(\text{SWA})}/\nu^{(\text{DA})}
represent now the difference between the second terms in curly braces in Eqs. (
50
) and (
49
). The crucial point here is that they differ only in the
\sigma
-dependent exponential factors that satisfy
\mathrm{exp}[5\pi^{2}/(2\sigma^{2})]-\mathrm{exp}(3\pi^{2}/\sigma^{2})=\mathcal{O}(1/\sigma^{2})
for large
\sigma
. This means that the side-maxima corrections to great extent cancel each other out (for small
\sigma
these corrections are very small compared to the ratio of the main contributions). This explains now why the ratio
\nu^{(\text{SWA})}/\nu^{(\text{DA})}
was found in Sec.
IV.1.1
to be almost insensitive to varying
\sigma
. Accordingly, our prescription reads

\frac{\nu^{(\text{SWA})}}{\nu^{(\text{DA})}}\approx D(E_{0})\equiv\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\pi}\sqrt{\frac{E_{0}}{E_{\text{c}}}}\bigg(1-\frac{19}{8\pi}\frac{E_{0}}{E_{\text{c}}}\bigg).

(51)

Let us now compare this expression with Eq. (
40
). The leading-order terms in these two formulas coincide if
\zeta=\mathrm{e}^{-\pi}\approx 0.0432
, which explains why this parameter had this particular value in Sec.
IV.1.1
. Nevertheless, the next-order contributions prove to be slightly different:
19/(8\pi)\approx 0.735
in Eq. (
51
) versus
1/\pi+5/12\approx 0.756
in Eq. (
40
). In what follows, we will rely on Eq. (
51
) since it was obtained in a systematic way.

Our direct numerical calculations revealed that the relative error of Eq. (
51
) is indeed very small: even for
E_{0}=0.1E_{\text{c}}
it reaches only
1\%
independently of
\sigma
.

IV.2
PWA versus SWA

Our goal here is to take into account the finiteness of the laser pulses, which is neglected within the SWA. The total number of pairs
N^{(\text{PWA})}
obtained within the PWA is proportional to the cross sectional area
S
, while
N^{(\text{SWA})}
contains the volume
V=LS
. It is necessary to construct the effective length
L
as a function of the field parameters. It is natural to represent it as
L=(2\pi/\omega)N_{\text{eff}}
, where
N_{\text{eff}}
is a dimensionless quantity, which can be viewed as an effective number of cycles in the laser pulse. From Eqs. (
22
) and (
26
), it follows that the condition
N^{(\text{PWA})}=N^{(\text{SWA})}
is equivalent to

\frac{\nu^{(\text{PWA})}}{\nu^{(\text{SWA})}}=2\pi N_{\text{eff}}.

(52)

In the realistic subcritical domain
E_{0}\lesssim 0.1E_{\text{c}}
and quasistatic regime under consideration, the presence of the Gaussian envelope (
37
) makes the pair-production process efficiently occur only within
one
carrier cycle in the vicinity of the field maximum [
\eta=0
in Eq. (
37
)]. Since within the PWA the laser pulses do not fully overlap, one may expect that the correct estimate is
N_{\text{eff}}\approx 1/2
, so

\frac{\nu^{(\text{PWA})}}{\nu^{(\text{SWA})}}\approx\pi.

(53)

According to our calculations, this is indeed a quite accurate relation once
E_{0}\ll E_{\text{c}}
. However, it can be significantly improved by means of Laplace’s method along the lines of our analysis carried out in the previous section.

. First, we consider the main contribution arising from the vicinity of

Here we will again replace the

neglecting the terms of the order of

\displaystyle\nu^{(\text{PWA})}

\displaystyle+

IV.3
Gaussian beams versus PWA

Now our goal is to incorporate the transverse structure of the laser pulses. Let us first provide several preliminary remarks and then invoke Laplace’s method as was done in the previous sections. Note that Eqs. (
26
) and (
36
) lead to the following relation:

\frac{\nu^{(\text{G})}}{\nu^{(\text{PWA})}}=\omega^{2}S,

(57)

where we have assumed
N^{(\text{G})}=N^{(\text{PWA})}
. To estimate the effective area
S
, we first point out that it should be proportional to
w_{0}^{2}
, which is clear from the analysis of dimensions. This is also in agreement with the fact that
\nu^{(\text{PWA})}
is
\omega
-independent, while
\nu^{(\text{G})}
depends only on the product
\omega w_{0}=2/\theta
. Accordingly, the right-hand side of Eq. (
57
) should read
(\omega w_{0})^{2}h(E_{0}/E_{\text{c}},\sigma)
, where the function
h
is to be determined.

The pulse duration
\sigma
governs the longitudinal structure of the laser field, so we expect that the function
h(E_{0}/E_{\text{c}},\sigma)
is, in fact, almost insensitive to
\sigma
. On the other hand, the field amplitude
E_{0}
strongly affects the volume factor. Its qualitative behavior in the limit
E_{0}\ll E_{\text{c}}
can be identified by inspecting the Schwinger exponential
\mathrm{exp}(-\pi E_{\text{c}}/E_{0})
, where
E_{0}
is modified by the factor
\mathrm{e}^{-\chi}
[see Eq. (
34
)]. The effective radius of the vicinity of
x=y=0
(
\chi=0
) will possess the same scaling with respect to
E_{0}
as
\delta
in Eq. (
39
), i.e., it will be proportional to
\sqrt{E_{0}/E_{\text{c}}}
for
E_{0}\ll E_{\text{c}}
, which follows from Eq. (
40
). Therefore, the effective area is linear in
E_{0}
:

\frac{\nu^{(\text{G})}}{\nu^{(\text{PWA})}}\approx A(\omega w_{0})^{2}\,\frac{E_{0}}{E_{\text{c}}}.

(58)

In what follows, we will identify
A
and refine this approximation via Laplace’s method.

Let us now proceed to a systematic analysis of the integrals in Eq. (
34
). It turns out that the three-dimensional integration can also be accurately approximated in the weak-field domain via Laplace’s method (see Appendix
C
). The result reads

\displaystyle\nu^{(\text{G})}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi^{3}}\,(\omega w_{0})^{2}\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2+\sigma^{2}}\bigg(\frac{E_{0}}{E_{\text{c}}}\bigg)^{4}

(59)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\bigg\{\mathrm{e}^{-\pi E_{\text{c}}/E_{0}}\bigg[1-\frac{8}{\pi}\frac{E_{0}}{E_{\text{c}}}+\mathcal{O}\bigg(\frac{E_{0}^{2}}{E_{\text{c}}^{2}}\bigg)\bigg]

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle 4\big(\mathrm{e}^{-\pi^{2}/\sigma^{2}}\big)^{4}\,\mathrm{exp}\left(-\frac{\pi E_{\text{c}}}{E_{0}}\,\mathrm{e}^{\pi^{2}/\sigma^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\bigg[1+\mathcal{O}\bigg(\frac{E_{0}}{E_{\text{c}}}\bigg)+\mathcal{O}\bigg(\frac{1}{\sigma^{4}}\bigg)\bigg]\bigg\}.

V
Conclusion

In this study, we deduced simple analytical expressions connecting numerical results for the total particle yield within various approaches to modelling the external field of two counterpropagating laser pulses. Although the existence of an explicit correspondence between the dipole approximation and
(3+1)
-dimensional scenario is nontrivial itself, we derived a universal formula which ensures a relative uncertainty of less than
10\%
. It clearly indicates that the information on the total number of pairs produced by Gaussian beams is already encoded in the simplest setup depending solely on time. It turns out that the envelope function and the oscillating carrier incorporated in the dipole approximation provide one with the necessary estimates, so one should only map them onto the more involved scenario according to the prescription presented in this study. We underline here that the simplified spatially homogeneous setup completely fails to describe the actual momentum distributions of the particles as it disregards the nontrivial dynamics of the created electrons and positrons in the external inhomogeneous background field.

The explicit formulas for the volume factors identified in the present study are expected to be particularly useful for experimentalists as these expressions allow one to easily estimate the particle yield and do not require any specific numerical procedures or analytical calculations [it is not even necessary to carefully follow our derivation of the final formula (
63
)]. In addition, we demonstrated that the actual pair production threshold in terms of the peak intensity of an individual laser pulse is of the order of
10^{26}\penalty\ \text{W}/\text{cm}^{2}
.

Appendix A
Validity of the LCFA

Here we will discuss the LCFA validity in a bit more detail. Let us first recall a short simple derivation of the criterion
(E_{0}/E_{\text{c}})^{3/2}\gg\omega/m
in the case of a spatially homogeneous electric background
E(t)

[
49
]
. In a subcritical regime
E_{0}\ll E_{\text{c}}
, the main contribution to the particle number arises from the vicinity of the field maximum, where the field strength can be expanded according to

E(t)=E_{0}-\frac{1}{2}E_{0}\Omega^{2}t^{2}+\ldots

(68)

Here we assume that the field maximum is located at
t=0
, and
\Omega^{2}
comes from the second derivative of
E(t)
. For the setups considered in our study,
\Omega=\omega\sqrt{1+2/\sigma^{2}}\approx\omega
. The Schwinger exponential function then reads

\mathrm{exp}\left[\!-\frac{\pi E_{\text{c}}}{E(t)}\right]\approx\mathrm{exp}\left(\!-\frac{\pi E_{\text{c}}}{E_{0}}\right)\mathrm{exp}\left(\!-\frac{\pi E_{\text{c}}\omega^{2}t^{2}}{2E_{0}}\right).

(69)

This expression suggests that the effective timescale of the field variations within the pair production process is
\tau_{\text{eff}}\equiv(1/\omega)\sqrt{E_{0}/E_{\text{c}}}
. The external field can be treated as locally constant if
\tau_{\text{eff}}
is much larger than the characteristic pair formation length
2m/|eE_{0}|
. This criterion is equivalent to
(E_{0}/E_{\text{c}})^{3/2}\gg\omega/m
.

We see that the above discussion is quite universal and should also hold in the case of multidimensional setups since (a) the formation length remains the same and (b) the spatiotemporal variations of the external field are determined by a single parameter
\omega
(in principle, the temporal and spatial scales may be different, but this is only possible in specific setups that are not considered in our paper).

To provide a new explicit example that also confirms the above criterion, let us examine a static electric field of the following Sauter-like form:

E(x)=\frac{E_{0}}{\cosh^{2}\omega x}.

(70)

As was comprehensively discussed in Ref.
[
57
]
the Furry-picture quantization procedure in static fields significantly differs from the case of time-dependent backgrounds. The external field (
70
) can produce pairs only if
\xi\equiv 1/\gamma=|eE_{0}|/(m\omega)>1
, i.e., in the presence of a Klein zone. In this case, the electron number density depends on the transverse momentum
p_{\perp}=\sqrt{p_{y}^{2}+p_{z}^{2}}
and generalized energy
p^{0}
.

First, we note that the maximal number density corresponds to
p_{\perp}=p^{0}=0
and reads
[
57
]

\frac{\sinh^{2}[\pi(m/\omega)\sqrt{\xi^{2}-1}]}{\sinh^{2}(\pi\xi m/\omega)}.

(71)

By assuming
\xi\gg 1
and
m/\omega\gg 1
and comparing it with the LCFA value
\mathrm{exp}\,(-\pi E_{\text{c}}/E_{0})
, we find that the two results coincide once
(m/\omega)/\xi^{3}\ll 1
, which is equivalent to
(E_{0}/E_{\text{c}})^{3/2}\gg\omega/m
.

Second, we can directly compare the LCFA predictions for the
total
number of pairs with the exact result. According to Eq. (
16
), the LCFA prediction is exactly the same as in the case of a time-dependent Sauter pulse
E(t)=E_{0}/\cosh^{2}(\omega t)
(in the static case, the number of pairs is calculated with respect to a unit transverse area
S
and field duration
T
). The exact result is given by
[
57
]

\frac{N}{ST}=\frac{1}{\pi^{2}}\int\limits_{0}^{p^{*}_{\perp}}dp_{\perp}\,p_{\perp}\int\limits_{0}^{p^{0}_{*}}dp^{0}\,\frac{\sinh(\pi p^{\text{L}}/\omega)\sinh(\pi p^{\text{R}}/\omega)}{|\sinh\{(\pi/\omega)[eE_{0}/\omega+(1/2)(p^{\text{L}}-p^{\text{R}})]\}\sinh\{(\pi/\omega)[eE_{0}/\omega-(1/2)(p^{\text{L}}-p^{\text{R}})]\}|},

(72)

where

\displaystyle p^{*}_{\perp}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle m\sqrt{\xi^{2}-1},

(73)

\displaystyle p^{0}_{*}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle m(\xi-\sqrt{1+p_{\perp}^{2}/m^{2}}),

(74)

\displaystyle p^{\text{L}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{(p^{0}+eE/\omega)^{2}-m^{2}-p_{\perp}^{2}},

(75)

\displaystyle p^{\text{R}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{(p^{0}-eE/\omega)^{2}-m^{2}-p_{\perp}^{2}}.

(76)

By numerically evaluating Eq. (
72
) and computing the relative uncertainty of the LCFA prescription, we came to the same conclusion as that drawn concerning Fig. 1 of Ref.
[
49
]
: the numerical results based on the LCFA are accurate if
(E_{0}/E_{\text{c}})^{3/2}\gg\omega/m
.

We underline that the parameter
(m/\omega)(E_{0}/E_{\text{c}})^{3/2}
in the main text of our paper exceeds unity by, at least, three orders of magnitude, so the LCFA is extremely accurate and the relative errors of our estimates are solely governed by our approximations of the LCFA integrals.

We also point out that for very strong fields
E_{0}\gtrsim E_{\text{c}}
, the Pauli principle plays a vital role, which is completely neglected by the LCFA
[
49
,
50
]
. However, it is not relevant to the present study as we focus on the subcritical regime.

Finally, we mention that large
\omega
can be present in setups regarding dynamically assisted Schwinger effect
[
54
]
, where one of the field components can have a very small wavelength. Such scenarios are beyond the scope of the present investigation.

Approximation of the integrals for Gaussian beams

Here we derive an approximate closed-form expression for
\nu^{(\text{G})}
, which will allow us to identify the transverse volume factor that establishes the relation between
\nu^{(\text{PWA})}
and
\nu^{(\text{G})}
. First, we note that the Gouy phase
\psi(z)
and the term involving

in Eq. (

\tilde{g}(\chi,z,\eta)\approx f(\eta)
, where

is defined in Eq. (

\nu^{(\text{G})}
in the following form:

\nu^{(\text{G})}\approx\frac{1}{8\pi^{3}}\frac{E_{0}^{2}}{E_{\text{c}}^{2}}\int\limits_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\!d\eta_{-}\!\int\limits_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\!d\eta_{+}\,\theta(f(\eta_{-})f(\eta_{+}))f(\eta_{-})f(\eta_{+})\,\mathrm{exp}\left[-\frac{\pi E_{\text{c}}}{E_{0}\sqrt{f(\eta_{-})f(\eta_{+})}}\right]\Xi(\eta_{-},\eta_{+},E_{0}/(\pi E_{\text{c}}),\omega^{2}w_{0}^{2}),

(85)

where

\displaystyle\Xi(\eta_{-},\eta_{+},\varepsilon,\beta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\pi\beta\int\limits_{0}^{+\infty}d\chi\,\frac{1}{\rho^{2}(\eta_{+}-\eta_{-},\beta)}\,\mathrm{e}^{-2\chi/\rho^{2}(\eta_{+}-\eta_{-},\beta)}

(86)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\mathrm{exp}\left\{-\frac{1}{\varepsilon\sqrt{f(\eta_{-})f(\eta_{+})}}\bigg[\rho(\eta_{+}-\eta_{-},\beta)\,\mathrm{e}^{\chi/\rho^{2}(\eta_{+}-\eta_{-},\beta)}-1\bigg]\right\}.

To explicitly indicate the
\beta
dependence of the function (
32
), we have also introduced

\rho(\eta,\beta)=\sqrt{1+\frac{\eta^{2}}{\beta^{2}}}.

(87)

By substituting then
\mathrm{e}^{\chi/\rho^{2}}=x
, one obtains

\Xi(\eta_{-},\eta_{+},\varepsilon,\beta)=\pi\beta\int\limits_{1}^{+\infty}\frac{dx}{x^{3}}\,\mathrm{exp}\left\{-\frac{1}{\varepsilon\sqrt{f(\eta_{-})f(\eta_{+})}}\big[\rho(\eta_{+}-\eta_{-},\beta)x-1\big]\right\}.

(88)

Note that if we replace the function
\Xi
in Eq. (
85
) with unity, the expression will yield
\nu^{(\text{PWA})}
[see Eq. (
27
)]. To identify the transverse volume factor, we first calculate the integral in Eq. (

\Xi(\eta_{-},\eta_{+},\varepsilon,\beta)=\pi\beta\,\mathrm{e}^{1/q}\int\limits_{1}^{+\infty}\frac{dx}{x^{3}}\,\mathrm{e}^{-x/\mu}=\frac{\pi\beta}{2}\,\mathrm{e}^{1/q-1/\mu}\bigg[1-\frac{1}{\mu}-\frac{\mathrm{e}^{1/\mu}}{\mu^{2}}\,\mathrm{Ei}\,(-1/\mu)\bigg],

(89)

where

and
\mu\equiv q/\rho(\eta_{+}-\eta_{-},\beta)
. Since
\eta_{\pm}
are always small or of the order of unity (if we consider the side maxima of the external field) and
\beta\gg 1
, the function
\rho(\eta_{+}-\eta_{-},\beta)
is always very close to

. Accordingly,

and
\mu
are of the same order and small in the weak-field limit. Let us expand Eq. (

) as follows:

\Xi(\eta_{-},\eta_{+},\varepsilon,\beta)=\pi\beta\,\mathrm{e}^{1/q-1/\mu}\mu\big[1-3\mu+\mathcal{O}(\mu^{2})\big].

(90)

Although it is already clear that the simplest approximation for
\nu^{(\text{G})}/\nu^{(\text{PWA})}
has the form

, we will perform more accurate calculations. To this end, we first expand

similarly to Eq. (

We also need

\frac{1}{\mu}=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\,\bigg[1+\frac{2+\sigma^{2}}{4\sigma^{2}}\,\big(\eta_{-}^{2}+\eta_{+}^{2}\big)+\frac{12+12\sigma^{2}+7\sigma^{4}}{96\sigma^{4}}\,\big(\eta_{-}^{4}+\eta_{+}^{4}\big)+\frac{(2+\sigma^{2})^{2}}{16\sigma^{4}}\,\eta_{-}^{2}\eta_{+}^{2}+\mathcal{O}(\eta^{6})\bigg].

(92)

The first term gives rise to the main exponential factor

\eta_{\pm}
in Eq. (

affect the

\nu^{(\text{G})}\approx\frac{1}{2\pi^{3}}\,(\omega w_{0})^{2}\,\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2+\sigma^{2}}\bigg(\frac{E_{0}}{E_{\text{c}}}\bigg)^{4}\mathrm{e}^{-\pi E_{\text{c}}/E_{0}}\bigg[1-\frac{4(7+7\sigma^{2}+2\sigma^{4})}{\pi(2+\sigma^{2})^{2}}\frac{E_{0}}{E_{\text{c}}}+\mathcal{O}\bigg(\frac{E_{0}^{2}}{E_{\text{c}}^{2}}\bigg)\bigg].

(93)

We will again approximate the linear-term coefficient in square brackets as
-8/\pi+\mathcal{O}(1/\sigma^{2})
. By adding now the side-maxima contributions from
