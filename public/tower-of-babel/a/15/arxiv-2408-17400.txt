Blockwise Gluings And Amalgamation Failures in Integral Residuated Lattices
Valeria Giustarini and Sara Ugolini

Abstract
. We introduce the blockwise gluing construction. This describes residuated integral chains which can be decomposed into (possibly) partial algebras, stacked one on top of the other, and such that elements in a certain component multiply in blocks , i.e., in the same way, with respect to lower components. This construction generalizes that of 1-sums (or ordinal sums). As first main results, we provide finite axiomatizations for varieties generated by particular chains that are gluings of their archimedean components. For such varieties we also prove the finite embeddability property, and as a consequence, the decidability of their universal theory. Moreover, we solve in the negative several longstanding open problems in the literature about the amalgamation property (AP). Indeed, we provide denumerably many new examples of varieties lacking the AP, including: semilinear (commutative) integral residuated lattices, semilinear FL w -algebras, MTL-algebras, involutive and pseudocomplemented MTL-algebras, and all their n n -potent subvarieties for n ≥ 2 n\geq 2 . For the commutative varieties, this also entails that the associated substructural logics do not have the deductive interpolation property.



1.
Introduction

Residuated lattice-ordered monoids, or residuated lattices for short, are algebraic structures in the signature
(\lor,\land,\cdot,\backslash,/,1)
, where the monoidal reduct and the lattice order
\leq
are connected by the
residuation law
:

x\cdot y\leq z\;\;\Leftrightarrow\;\;y\leq x\backslash z\;\;\Leftrightarrow\;\;z\leq z/y,

in which the two divisions
(\backslash,/)
intuitively allow one to solve inequalities, in much of the same way as one would use inverses to solve equations in groups. First introduced by Ward and Dilworth to study ideals of rings
[
39
]
, residuated lattices have caught the interest of researchers in the field of algebraic logic. This is due in particular to the fact that they constitute a natural semantics for a wide framework of nonclassical logics, namely
substructural logics

[
20
,
31
]
. These are a large container of systems which include many of the interesting examples of logics amenable to the algebraic study: classical logic, intuitionistic logic, intermediate logics, many-valued logics, relevance logics to name a few. In this context, the monoidal operation acts as a conjunction, the two divisions play the role of implications, and the residuation law then reads as an algebraic version of a deduction theorem.

Residuated lattices comprise a variety of different structures; in fact, they do not satisfy any special purely lattice-theoretic nor monoid-theoretic identity, since any lattice and any monoid can be embedded into some residuated lattice
[
7
,
29
]
.
This wide spectrum of structures makes the study of residuated lattices both interesting and complicated, and while the literature on the subject is quite vast and many deep results have been obtained in the last decades (see e.g. the monographs
[
20
,
31
]
), at the present moment large classes of algebras still lack an effective structural understanding. One of such classes is given by commutative integral chains, i.e., totally ordered structures where the
unit of the monoid is the top element of the lattice, and the monoidal operation is commutative. This class is of particular interest in the field of nonclassical logics, since they give semantics to many-valued logics such as Łukasiewicz logic, Gödel-Dummett logic, Hájek Basic Logic, and Esteva-Godo’s monoidal t-norm based logic
[
25
,
12
]
.

This work aims to further our understanding of the structure of integral residuated lattices in general, and totally ordered ones in particular. A meaningful way of tackling this issue is to decompose these algebras in “simpler” components, which ideally are subalgebras or subreducts, and understand constructions that allow to put such components back together.
This approach has been particularly successful in varieties generated by totally ordered structures satisfying
divisibility
, i.e., such that if
x\leq y
, there are
z,z^{\prime}
such that
x=yz=z^{\prime}y
. The results in
[
2
]
, later extended to the non-commutative setting in
[
11
]
, use
1-sums
(or
ordinal sums
) to yield a description of totally ordered divisible chains. These results have been succesfully utilized to study, for instance, free algebras
[
6
]
, subvariety lattices
[
2
,
3
,
5
]
, and interesting algebraic properties such as amalgamation
[
16
]
.

While 1-sums have shown to be effective in divisible structures, they do not give a useful description for all integral chains. Indeed, the class of integral chains that are not decomposable in 1-sums is quite large –in the
0
-bounded cases, all involutive structures belong to it
[
34
]
– and it is not closed under subalgebras
[
23
]
, hence cannot be described by equations, quasiequations, nor universal sentences. It then becomes relevant to consider more general constructions, capable of effectively describing larger classes of chains. An example in this direction is in
[
22
]
; the authors first introduce a construction that
glues
together structures intersecting not just at
1
, like in 1-sums, but in a congruence filter
F
, and then obtain a construction involving partial algebras by removing said filter
F
.

Building on these latter ideas, we introduce the
blockwise gluing
, with which we are able to describe integral chains whose components are made of possibly partial algebras, stacked one on top of the other and intersecting at
1
, and such that elements in a certain component multiply
in blocks
, i.e., in the same way, with respect to lower components. This construction generalizes that of 1-sum and the one introduced in
[
22
]
. As first main results, we show that the blockwise gluing can be effectively used to study varieties generated by chains which are gluings of their archimedean components. We provide finite axiomatizations for
n
-potent varieties of this kind. Moreover, using
residuated frames

[
19
]
as a technical tool, we show that all the
n
-potent varieties we analyze have the finite embeddability property, as a result of the fact that the blockwise gluing construction is preserved by the residuated frames we make use of. This entails that all such varieties have decidable universal theories.

Finally, we show that, within the class of chains describable by means of blockwise gluings, one can construct a V-formation that does not have an amalgam in residuated chains. This solves in the negative several longstanding open problems in the literature about the amalgamation property (see the table in page 205 of
[
31
]
). In particular we establish that the following lack amalgamation: the varieties of semilinear (commutative) integral residuated lattices, semilinear FL
w
-algebras and MTL-algebras, involutive and pseudo-complemented MTL-algebras, and all their
n
-potent subvarieties for
n\geq 2
. For the commutative varieties, this also entails that the associated logics do not have the deductive interpolation property.

2.
Preliminaries

In this preliminary section we introduce the main notions of interest. First, we recall the basic facts about residuated lattices which will be of use in this paper. Secondly, we recall in some detail the residuated frames constructions, which will be a key tool to prove some interesting properties of the varieties generated by blockwise gluings, and will also be instrumental in some axiomatization results.

2.1.
Residuated lattices

A residuated lattice is an algebra
{\textbf{A}}=(A,\lor,\land,\cdot,\backslash,/,1)
of type
(2,2,2,2,2,0)
such that:

(1)

(A,\lor,\land)
is a lattice;

(2)

(A,\cdot,1)
is a monoid;

(3)

\backslash
and
/
are the right and left division of
\cdot
: for all
x,y,z\in A
,

x\cdot y\leq z\;\;\Leftrightarrow\;\;y\leq x\backslash z\;\;\Leftrightarrow\;\;x\leq z/y,

where
\leq
is the lattice ordering.

As usual, in what follows we write
xy
for
x\cdot y
.
Importantly, divisions in a residuated lattice can be characterized by means of the product and the order. Specifically, given a residuated lattice
A
,

(2.1)

x\backslash z=\max\{y\in A:xy\leq z\};\quad z/y=\max\{x\in A:xy\leq z\}.

A fact that will sometimes be used in this work is that a monoid with a complete lattice order is residuated if and only if multiplication distributes on both sides over arbitrary joins (
[
20
, Corollary 3.11]
). For a finite totally ordered structure, this is equivalent to the product preserving the order and the bottom element being absorbing
1
1

1

We observe for the sake of the reader that this last condition is wrongly omitted in Corollaries 3.12 and 3.13 of
[
20
]
.
(since the bottom element is the empty join).

Residuated lattices form a variety, denoted by
\mathsf{RL}
, as residuation can be expressed equationally; see
[
8
]
. When the monoidal identity is the top element of the lattice we say that the residuated lattice is
integral
or an
IRL
; we call the corresponding variety
\mathsf{IRL}
.
Residuated lattices with an additional constant
0
are called
pointed
.
0
-bounded
residuated lattices are pointed residuated lattices that satisfy the identity
0\leq x
. We call a residuated lattice
bounded
if it is integral and
0

The variety of bounded residuated lattices is called
\mathsf{FL_{w}}

(see
[
20
,
31
]

A residuated lattice is called
commutative
if the monoidal operation is commutative. In this case the two divisions coincide, and we write
x\to y
for
x\backslash y=y/x
. We write
\mathsf{CRL}
and
\mathsf{CIRL}
, respectively, for the commutative subvarieties of
\mathsf{RL}
and
\mathsf{IRL}

\mathsf{FL_{w}}
-algebras as
\mathsf{FL_{ew}}
-algebras, since commutativity of the monoidal operation corresponds to the structural rule of
exchange
. In
\mathsf{FL_{ew}}
-algebras we also write
\neg x
for
x\to 0
.

We call
chain
a totally ordered structure; hence a
residuated chain
is a residuated lattice which is totally ordered as a lattice.
Given a class of residuated lattices
\mathsf{K}
, we denote with
\mathsf{K}_{\rm c}
the class of structures in
\mathsf{K}
that are chains:

(2.2)

\mathsf{K}_{\rm c}=\{{\textbf{A}}\in\mathsf{K}:{\textbf{A}}\mbox{ is a chain}\}.

We say that a residuated lattice is
semilinear
if it is a subdirect product of chains. The variety of semilinear residuated lattices is called
\mathsf{SemRL}
and it is axiomatized by (see for instance
[
31
]
):

(sem)

[u\backslash((y\backslash x\land 1)u)\wedge 1]\lor[(v(x\backslash y\land 1))/v\wedge 1]=1,

or in the commutative case by
prelinearity
:

(prel)

(x\to y\land 1)\vee(y\to x\land 1)=1.

We call the variety of semilinear commutative residuated lattices
\mathsf{SemCRL}
and the subvariety of those satisfying also integrality
\mathsf{SemCIRL}
.
In particular the subvariety of prelinear
\mathsf{FL_{ew}}
-algebras is called
\mathsf{MTL}
since it is the equivalent algebraic semantics of the
monoidal t-norm based logic

[

]
.

A relevant subvariety of
\mathsf{MTL}
is the variety of BL-algebras, the equivalent algebraic semantics of Hájek’s Basic Logic
[
25
]
, which is axiomatized with respect to
\mathsf{MTL}
by the divisibility identity:

(div)

x\wedge y=x(x\backslash y)=(y/x)x.

The previous equation is equivalent to the following condition in chains: given a chain
A
, if
a\leq b
there are
c,d\in A
such that
a=cb=bd
. The variety of the
0

basic hoops
and it corresponds to divisible, semilinear CIRLs.
Among the most well known subvarieties of BL-algebras one finds
MV-algebras
and
Gödel algebras
, which are the equivalent algebraic semantics of, respectively, Łukasiewicz infinite-valued logic and Gödel-Dummett logic (which is the intermediate logic complete with respect to totally ordered Heyting algebras).
MV-algebras are BL-algebras satisfying
involution
:

(inv)

\neg\neg x=x;

while Gödel-algebras are BL-algebras satisfying

of the product:

(id)

x^{2}=x

Finite totally ordered MV-algebras and Gödel algebras are fully determined by their cardinality.

With respect to their structure theory, residuated lattices are quite well-behaved. Indeed, they are
1

and

In a residuated lattice

F\mapsto\theta_{F}=\{(x,y)\in A\times A:x\backslash y,y\backslash x\in F\}=\{(x,y)\in A\times A:x/y,y/x\in F\},

\theta\mapsto F_{\theta}=\{x\in A:(x,1)\in\theta\}

for all
F\in{\rm Fil}({\textbf{A}}),\theta\in{\rm Con}({\textbf{A}})
. In what follows, given an algebra

.

The following universal algebraic notion is going to be relevant for some results. An algebra

(for short, CEP) if, for any subalgebra

of

\Theta\in\mathsf{Con}({\textbf{B}})

[

) that all commutative subvarieties of residuated lattices or

-algebras have the CEP.

Finally, given that
partial
IRLs play a role in this manuscript, let us give a precise definition.
We call a
partial IRL
a partially ordered partial algebra
({\textbf{A}},\leq)
in the language of residuated lattices, such that:

(1)

1\in A
and
A
is integral:
x\leq 1
for all
x\in A
;

(2)

the three axioms of RLs are satisfied whenever they can be applied, in the following sense:

(a)

is the least common upper bound of

and
y
whenever it exists, and similarly

is the largest lower bound whenever it exists;

(b)

x1=1x=1
, and if

are defined then
(xy)z=x(yz)
;

(c)

if

if and only if

if and only if

.

(3)

and

ac\leq bc
, and likewise for left multiplication.

(4)

whenever defined, the division operations are order-preserving in the numerator and order-reversing in the denominator. That is:

x\leq y
and

z\backslash x\leq z\backslash y

x\leq y
and

While the above definition is quite general, most partial residuated lattices we will consider in this work will only possibly lack some divisions and some joins.

2.2.
Residuated frames

In order to establish key properties of some varieties under consideration, specifically, the finite model property (FMP) and the finite embeddability property (FEP), we will rely on the framework of residuated frames as developed in
[
19
]
. We recall the main facts formulated as needed in this work. For more details, we refer the reader to
[
19
]
.

In essence, a residuated frame is a multi-sorted structure
(W,W^{\prime},N,\circ,\bbslash,\sslash)
that abstracts key characteristics of a residuated lattice.
The ternary relations
\circ,\bbslash,\sslash
are analogous to the operations
(\cdot,\backslash,/)
, in so far that the former satisfy a residuation law with respect to the relation
N
, as the latter do with respect to the lattice order
\leq
. In a nutshell, residuated frames allow one to construct residuated structures with controlled properties by appropriately defining the above relations.

Definition 2.1
.

A
residuated frame
is a structure of the form
{\textbf{W}}=(W,W^{\prime},N,\circ,\bbslash,\sslash)
such that
\circ\subseteq W^{3}
,
\bbslash\subseteq W\times W^{\prime}\times W^{\prime}
,
\sslash\subseteq W^{\prime}\times W\times W^{\prime}
, and
N\subseteq W\times W^{\prime}
is a
nuclear relation
, i.e.:

(u\circ v)Nw\;\;\mbox{ iff }\;\;vN(u\bbslash w)\;\;\mbox{ iff }\;\;uN(w\sslash v)

for all
u,v\in W
and
w\in W^{\prime}
, where
x*y=\{z:(x,y,z)\in*\}
for
*\in\{\circ,\bbslash,\sslash\}
.

The relation
N
is a Galois relation and it induces a Galois connection for
\mathcal{P}(W)
and
\mathcal{P}(W^{\prime})
, given by the maps
{}^{\triangleright}:\mathcal{P}(W)\to\mathcal{P}(W^{\prime})
and
{}^{\triangleleft}:\mathcal{P}(W^{\prime})\to\mathcal{P}(W)
defined as follows:

X^{\triangleright}=\{z\in W^{\prime}:\forall x\in X,xNz\}\qquad Z^{\triangleleft}=\{x\in W:\forall z\in Z,xNz\},

for
X\subseteq W
and
Z\subseteq W^{\prime}
. Thus one has
X\subseteq Z^{\triangleleft}
if and only if
Z\subseteq X^{\triangleright}
. One can then consider the associated closure operator
\gamma_{N}(X)=X^{\triangleright\triangleleft}
on the powerset
\mathcal{P}(W)
. Importantly, the subsets
\{\{u\}^{\triangleleft}:u\in W^{\prime}\}
are a basis for
\gamma_{N}
, that is to say, the elements in
\gamma_{N}[\mathcal{P}(W)]
are exactly the intersections of elements in
\{\{u\}^{\triangleleft}:u\in W^{\prime}\}
. Let

X\circ Y=\{x\circ y:x\in X,y\in Y\},

then
\gamma_{N}
is a nucleus on
(\mathcal{P}(W),\circ)
, i.e., a closure operator such that
\gamma_{N}(X)\circ\gamma_{N}(Y)\subseteq\gamma_{N}(X\circ Y)
. We are ready to define the
Galois algebra
of
W
as
{\textbf{W}}^{+}=(\gamma_{N}[\mathcal{P}(W)],\cap,\cup_{\gamma_{N}},\circ_{\gamma_{N}},\backslash,/)
where

\displaystyle X\circ_{\gamma_{N}}Y=\gamma_{N}(X\circ Y),

\displaystyle X\cup_{\gamma_{N}}Y=\gamma_{N}(X\cup Y)

\displaystyle X\backslash Y=\{z:\forall x\in X,x\circ z\in Y\},

\displaystyle X/Y=\{z:\forall x\in X,z\circ x\in Y\}.

{\textbf{W}}^{+}
is in general a residuated lattice-ordered groupoid. In order to get a residuated lattice, we need to add further properties.

A
unital residuated frame
(ru-frame for short)
{\textbf{W}}=(W,W^{\prime},N,\circ,\bbslash,\sslash,E)
is a residuated frame with a set
E\subseteq W
such that
(x\circ E)^{\triangleright}=\{x\}^{\triangleright}=(E\circ x)^{\triangleright}
for all
x\in W
. A
ruz-frame
is a unital frame with an extra subset
D\subseteq W
. In both cases,
{\textbf{W}}^{+}
has a unit
\gamma_{N}(E)
and in the latter case
\gamma_{N}(D)
is the interpretation of the constant
0
. Moreover, a residuated frame is
associative
if for all
x,y,z\in W
,
[(x\circ y)\circ z]^{\triangleright}=[x\circ(y\circ z)]^{\triangleright}
. The Galois algebra of an associative ru-frame is a residuated lattice, while the one of an associative ruz-frame is an FL-algebra. We also observe that in what follows,
\circ
will always be a ternary relation obtained by a binary monoidal operation.

A typical application of residuated frames is to prove generation of a variety or quasivariety by its finite algebras. We say that a class of algebras
\mathsf{K}
has the
finite model property
(FMP) if any equation that fails in
\mathsf{K}
, fails in a finite member of
\mathsf{K}
. For a variety, this means being generated by its finite algebras. Moreover, we say that
\mathsf{K}
has the
finite embeddability property
(FEP) when for any given finite partial subalgebra
B
of an algebra
{\textbf{A}}\in\mathsf{K}
, there exists a finite algebra
{\textbf{D}}\in\mathsf{K}
into which
B
can be embedded. The FEP clearly implies the FMP, and for a variety, it coincides with being generated as a quasivariety by its finite members. We recall how residuated frames are used towards FMP and FEP, since we will use it in what follows.

Let
A
be a residuated lattice and
B
a partial subalgebra of
A
. Then
(W,\circ,1)
is taken to be the submonoid of
A
generated by
B
. Moreover, define a map
u
on
(W,\circ,1)
as
u(x)=v\circ x\circ w
for
v,w\in W
; we call such map a
section
, and we denote by
{\textbf{S}}_{W}
the set of all sections. Then set
W^{\prime}={\textbf{S}}_{W}\times{\textbf{B}}
. Finally, define the binary relation
N
as

xN(u,b)\mbox{ if and only if }u(x)\leq_{{\textbf{A}}}b.

N
is nuclear with respect to
\bbslash,\sslash
defined as follows:

x\bbslash(u,b)=\{(u(x\circ-),b)\}\;\;\mbox{ and }\;\;(u,b)\sslash y=\{(u(-\circ y),b)\},

where
u(x\circ-):z\mapsto u(x\circ z)
and
u(-\circ y):z\mapsto u(z\circ y)
.
Consider
{\textbf{W}}_{{\textbf{A}},{\textbf{B}}}=(W,W^{\prime},N,\circ,\bbslash,\sslash,\{1\})
, one has that:

Proposition 2.2

(
[
19
]
)
.

{\textbf{W}}_{{\textbf{A}},{\textbf{B}}}
is a residuated frame and the map

embeds

into

.

Thus, residuated frames can be used to construct a residuated lattice into which the initial partial subalgebra embeds. This is often used to prove that a certain variety or quasivariety is generated by its finite members, by showing that any (quasi)identity that fails in the (quasi)variety fails in a finite algebra. To this end, one needs to show that
{\textbf{W}}_{{\textbf{A}},{\textbf{B}}}^{+}
ends up in the desired class of algebras. This step may require an ad hoc proof, since not all properties of the initial residuated lattice need be preserved by the construction. The following is a useful observation. Note that we assumed the constant
1

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
A
be an integral residuated chain and let
B
be a finite partial subalgebra of

{\textbf{W}}_{{\textbf{A}},{\textbf{B}}}^{+}
is a finite integral residuated chain.

Proof.

The fact that
{\textbf{W}}_{{\textbf{A}},{\textbf{B}}}^{+}
is finite is shown in the proof of
[

, and integrality is easily seen to hold given that we consider

{\textbf{W}}_{{\textbf{A}},{\textbf{B}}}^{+}
is a chain. Recall that
\{\{(u,b)\}^{\triangleleft}:(u,b)\in W^{\prime}\}
forms a basis for
{\textbf{W}}_{{\textbf{A}},{\textbf{B}}}^{+}
. Moreover, by direct application of the definitions,

\{(u,b)\}^{\triangleleft}=\{x\in W:xN(u,b)\}=\{x\in W:u(x)\leq_{{\textbf{A}}}b\}.

Notice then that
\{(u,b)\}^{\triangleleft}
is a downset of
W
, which inherits the order of the chain

. Hence, the downsets in the basis and their intersections are totally ordered by inclusion, which implies that
{\textbf{W}}_{{\textbf{A}},{\textbf{B}}}^{+}
is a chain.
∎

3.
Blockwise gluings

The main results of this work aim to further our understanding of the structure of integral residuated lattices in general, and chains in particular. A meaningful way of tackling this issue is to decompose these algebras in “simpler” components; particularly, by seeing an IRL
A
as the union of two (or more) components, which ideally are its subalgebras or subreducts. Given that we are particularly interested in totally ordered structures, we shall consider constructions that assemble these components in a totally ordered fashion, effectively stacking one on top of another.

Say that a residuated lattice
A
can be seen as the union of two substructures
B
and
C
, one below the other, where we assume that
B\cap C=\{1\}
so that they share the constant. It has been shown in

(often called

in residuated structures
2

Therefore, in order to be able to combine the components in different ways, we must give up the requirement that the components be subalgebras, and instead allow them to be subreducts. A natural choice is to retain the product and order from the components, while allowing to omit closure under divisions, since the latter are entirely determined by the other operations.

We observe that every IRL chain can be seen as a union of subreducts closed under products and the lattice operations (it suffices to take as components the different archimedean classes). Therefore, if one were able to effectively describe all possible ways of redefining products between such components, this would yield a characterization of all IRL-chains. At the present moment, we are far from understanding this level of generality in a meaningful way. Nonetheless, in this work we take a step towards this direction. Specifically, we will consider the particular case in which elements belonging to a component multiply

with respect to lower components. Looking at Figure

An example of such a construction is of course the 1-sum, and more generally, the

, which will be the starting point of our investigation. We recall it here in some detail.
The key ingredient for the partial gluing construction is given by the partial algebra acting as the lower component in the gluing, called in
[

]
a

. This is a triple

(1)

and

\sigma(x)\leq y

.

(2)

(\sigma,\gamma)
is a residuated pair, i.e.
\sigma(x)\leq y
if and only if

, such that:

(a)

\sigma
is a

,

, and
\sigma(1)=1

(b)

\gamma

(c)

The partial gluing then considers a a lower compatible triple
({\textbf{K}},\sigma,\gamma)
, and an IRL

\displaystyle xy

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\sigma(x)&\mbox{ if }y\in L-\{1\},x\in K-\{1\}\\

\displaystyle x\backslash\,y

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}c_{L}&\mbox{ if }x,y\in K\mbox{ and $x\backslash^{{\textbf{K}}}y$ is undefined}\\

\displaystyle y/\,x

The 1-sum construction corresponds to the particular case where the lower compatible triple is a total IRL, and
\sigma
and

The intuition behind this construction in
[
22
]
comes from the idea of gluing together two IRLs, say
B
and

, which intersect not only at the constant

, but at a shared congruence filter (say

); then one “forgets” the filter, and what is left are the components of the partial gluing. Here we start from the partial gluing itself, and consider a more general version of it, where left and right products from

to

Later on, we will take these ideas one step further and introduce an iterated version of the construction which allows one to describe chains which are decomposable in an arbitrary (totally ordered) set of components.

Let us now be more precise. We start by defining the algebras we wish to characterize, which we will say are
blockwise gluings
of their components.

Definition 3.2
.

Let
{\textbf{A}},{\textbf{C}}
be IRLs, and
B
a (possibly) partial IRL. We say that
A
is a
blockwise gluing of
B
and
C
if:

(1)

A=B\cup C
and
B\cap C=\{1\}
;

(2)

the elements of
B-\{1\}
are strictly below the elements of
C
;

(3)

C
is a subalgebra of
A
;

(4)

given
x,y\in C-\{1\}
and
z\in B-\{1\}
,
xz=yz
and
zx=zy
.

We will see how to construct blockwise gluings by generalizing the partial gluing construction. Given a residuated structure
A
, we call an element
c\in A
a
splitting coatom
of
A
if for all
x\in A
,
x\neq 1
, we have that
x\leq c
.

Lemma 3.3
.

If
A
is a blockwise gluing of
B
and
C
, then:

(1)

B
, with the inherited operations from
A
, is a partial IRL where all operations are defined except:

(a)

x\backslash y
iff there is
z\in C
such that
xz\leq y
iff
x\backslash y
is a splitting coatom of
C
;

(b)

y/x
iff there is
z\in C
such that
zx\leq y
iff
y/x
is a splitting coatom of
C
;

(c)

x\lor y
iff
x\lor y\in C
, and then
C
has a bottom element
\perp_{{\textbf{C}}}
such that
x\vee y=\perp_{{\textbf{C}}}
.

(2)

let
b\in B-\{1\}
and
c,d\in C
, then
c\backslash b=d\backslash b
and
b/c=b/d
.

Proof.

(1) Notice that
B
is a partial IRL that contains
1
and is closed under products and meets. Let then
x,y\in B
, then
x\backslash y
and
y/x
can lie either in
B
or
C
. In particular,
x\backslash y\in C
if and only if
\max\{k\in A:xk\leq y\}
is the splitting coatom of
C
, since any element of
C
multiplies the same with respect to
B
by Definition
3.2
(4). This happens if and only if there exists
z\in C
such that
xz\leq y
, which proves (1a). With a similar argument one can show (1b).
For (1c), in
B
the only joins that are missing lie in
C
. Let then
x,y\in B-\{1\}
, if
x\vee y\in{\textbf{C}}
, necessarily
C
has a bottom element
\perp_{\textbf{C}}
and
x\vee y=\perp_{{\textbf{C}}}
. Indeed, all elements of
C
are strictly above
x
and
y
, and

is the smallest element with this property.

(2) Let
b\in B-\{1\}
and
c,d\in C
, then

c\backslash b=\max\{k\in A:ck\leq b\}=\max\{k\in B:ck\leq b\}=max\{k\in B:dk\leq b\}=d\backslash b,

where the second equality follows from the fact that
C
is closed under products and the elements of

are above the ones in

, while the third one follows from Definition
3.2
(4), since
k\in B
and

. Similarly one can show that also
b/c=b/d
. This concludes the proof.
∎

Remark 3.4
.

We further observe that, if
A
is a blockwise gluing of
B
and
C
,

can be seen as a partial IRL with only some division missing. Indeed, in the partial order inherited from
A
, the joins of elements of
B
that end up in
C
within

, exist and are set to
1
once restricting to
B
.

The lemma above shows a close similarity with algebras that are obtainable by partial gluings. However, in the blockwise gluing, left and right products between elements of the top component with elements of the lower component may differ. This observation inspires the following definition, which aims at characterizing the blockwise gluing abstractly. We will need two distinct operators,
\sigma^{\ell}
and
\sigma^{r}
, in order to store the information for left and right products respectively; accordingly, we shall also need two operators for the divisions,
\gamma^{\ell}
and
\gamma^{r}
.

Definition 3.5
.

We call
weak lower block
a tuple
({\textbf{B}},\sigma^{\ell},\sigma^{r},\gamma^{\ell},\gamma^{r})
such that:

(1)

B
is a partial IRL where all operations are defined except possibly for some divisions;

(2)

(\sigma^{\ell},\gamma^{\ell})
and
(\sigma^{r},\gamma^{r})
are residuated pairs;

(3)

\sigma^{\ell}
and
\sigma^{r}
are interior operators such that for
x,y\neq 1
:

(a)

x\sigma^{r}(y)=\sigma^{r}(xy)
and
\sigma^{\ell}(x)y=\sigma^{\ell}(xy)
;

(b)

x\sigma^{\ell}(y)=\sigma^{r}(x)y
;

(c)

\sigma^{r}(\sigma^{\ell}(x))=\sigma^{\ell}(\sigma^{r}(x))
;

(d)

\sigma^{\ell}(1)=\sigma^{r}(1)=1
;

(4)

\gamma^{\ell}
and
\gamma^{r}
are closure operators;

(5)

xy\leq\sigma^{r}(x),\sigma^{\ell}(y)
and
yx\leq\sigma^{r}(y),\sigma^{\ell}(x)
for all
x,y\in B
,
x,y\neq 1
.

We call
lower block
a weak lower block such that if
x\backslash y
is undefined then necessarily
\sigma^{r}(x)\leq y
and
x\not\leq y
and if
y/x
is undefined then necessarily
\sigma^{\ell}(x)\leq y
and
x\not\leq y
.

Note that both
\sigma^{\ell}
and
\sigma^{r}
are conuclei, i.e.
\sigma^{\ell}(x)\sigma^{\ell}(y)\leq\sigma^{\ell}(xy)
and
\sigma^{r}(x)\sigma^{r}(y)\leq\sigma^{r}(xy)
. Indeed
\sigma^{\ell}(xy)=\sigma^{\ell}(x)y\geq\sigma^{\ell}(x)\sigma^{\ell}(y)
since
y\geq\sigma^{\ell}(y)
and
\sigma^{r}(xy)=x\sigma^{r}(y)\geq\sigma^{r}(x)\sigma^{r}(y)
since
x\geq\sigma^{r}(x)
.

Let
A
be a blockwise gluing of
B
and
C
, and fix an element
c\in C-\{1\}
; we define

\sigma^{\ell}_{{\textbf{A}}}(x)=cx,\;\;\sigma^{r}_{{\textbf{A}}}(x)=xc,\;\;\gamma^{\ell}_{{\textbf{A}}}(x)=c\backslash x,\;\;\gamma^{r}_{{\textbf{A}}}(x)=x/c

for
x\in B-\{1\}
, and
\sigma^{\ell}(1)=\sigma^{r}(1)=\gamma^{r}(1)=\gamma^{\ell}(1)=1
.

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
A
be a blockwise gluing of
B
and
C
. Then
({\textbf{B}},\sigma^{\ell}_{{\textbf{A}}},\sigma^{r}_{{\textbf{A}}},\gamma^{\ell}_{{\textbf{A}}},\gamma^{r}_{{\textbf{A}}})
is a lower block.

Proof.

We proceed to prove properties (1)–(5) of Definition
3.5
. For simplicity, within this proof we write
\sigma^{\ell},\sigma^{r},\gamma^{\ell},\gamma^{r}
for
\sigma^{\ell}_{{\textbf{A}}},\sigma^{r}_{{\textbf{A}}},\gamma^{\ell}_{{\textbf{A}}},\gamma^{r}_{{\textbf{A}}}
.

(1) The fact that
B
is a partial IRL where all operations are defined except for some divisions follows from Lemma
3.3
and Remark
3.4
.

(2) Let us consider the pair
(\sigma^{\ell},\gamma^{\ell})
; one can easily show that it is a residuated pair. Indeed, if
\sigma^{\ell}(x)=cx\leq y
, then, by residuation in
A
,
x\leq c\backslash y=\gamma^{\ell}(y)
. Similarly, also
(\sigma^{r},\gamma^{r})
is a residuated pair.

(3) We now verify that
\sigma^{\ell}
and
\sigma^{r}
are interior operators satisfying the properties (a)-(d) in Definition
3.5
(3). First, notice that
\sigma^{\ell}
and
\sigma^{r}
are decreasing maps. Indeed, since
A
is integral,
\sigma^{\ell}(x)=cx\leq x
and
\sigma^{r}(x)=xc\leq x
. To prove that they are idempotent, note that
c
and
c^{2}
multiply the same with respect to the elements in
B
, by the definition of a blockwise gluing, and then we have that

\sigma^{\ell}(\sigma^{\ell}(x))=c^{2}x=cx=\sigma^{\ell}(x)\;\mbox{ and }\;\sigma^{r}(\sigma^{r}(x))=xc^{2}=xc=\sigma^{r}(x).

Let us now show order preservation. Suppose
x\leq y
, then, by order preservation of the product in
A
,
\sigma^{\ell}(x)=cx\leq cy=\sigma^{\ell}(y)
and
\sigma^{r}(x)=xc\leq yc=\sigma^{r}(y)
; hence
\sigma^{\ell}
and
\sigma^{r}
are interior operators.
Properties (a)-(c) follow from the associativity of the product in
A
. Finally, (d), i.e. the fact that
\sigma^{r}(1)=\sigma^{\ell}(1)
, holds by definition.

(4) We now want to show that both
\gamma^{\ell}
and
\gamma^{r}
are closure operators. First note that, as a consequence of integrality,
cx\leq x
and
xc\leq x
, hence by residuation
x\leq c\backslash x=\gamma^{\ell}(x)
and
x\leq x/c=\gamma^{r}(x)
. By order preservation, if
x\leq y
, then
\gamma^{\ell}(x)=c\backslash x\leq c\backslash y=\gamma^{\ell}(y)
and
\gamma^{r}(x)=x/c\leq y/c=\gamma^{r}(y)
. Moreover, by (2) of Lemma
3.3
, we get that
\gamma^{\ell}(\gamma^{\ell}(x))=c\backslash(c\backslash x)=c^{2}\backslash x=c\backslash x=\gamma^{\ell}(x)
and
\gamma^{r}(\gamma^{r}(x))=(x/c)/c=x/c^{2}=x/c=\gamma^{r}(x)
.

(5) The fact that, for all
x,y\in B-\{1\}
,
xy\leq\sigma^{r}(x),\sigma^{\ell}(y)
and
yx\leq\sigma^{r}(y),\sigma^{\ell}(x)
, follows by the order preservation of the product in
A
. Indeed,
xy\leq xc=\sigma^{r}(x)
,
xy\leq cy=\sigma^{\ell}(y)
,
yx\leq cx=\sigma^{\ell}(x)
and
yx\leq yc=\sigma^{r}(y)
.

Hence
({\textbf{B}},\sigma^{\ell},\sigma^{r},\gamma^{\ell},\gamma^{r})
is a weak lower block. It is left to show that it is a lower block. Suppose that
x\backslash y
is undefined in
B
, then
x\backslash y\in C-\{1\}
. Now,
\sigma^{r}(x)=x(x\backslash y)\leq y
, and also
x\not\leq y
since otherwise
x\backslash y
would be defined in
B
and equal to
1
. Similarly one can prove that if
y/x
is undefined then
\sigma^{\ell}(x)\leq y
and
x\not\leq y
. This concludes the proof.
∎

We now demonstrate how one can reconstruct a blockwise gluing starting from its abstract components.

Definition 3.7
.

Let
({\textbf{B}},\sigma^{\ell},\sigma^{r},\gamma^{\ell},\gamma^{r})
be a lower block, and
C
be an IRL such that
B\cap C=\{1\}
. We assume that
C
has a splitting coatom
c_{{\textbf{C}}}
in case there are

such that

, and
\sigma^{\ell}(x)\leq y
or
\sigma^{r}(x)\leq y
. Moreover, if
x\lor y=1
in

, then
C
has a bottom element
\perp_{{\textbf{C}}}
.
Let
\beta=(\sigma^{\ell},\sigma^{r},\gamma^{\ell},\gamma^{r})
, we define
{\textbf{B}}\boxplus_{\beta}{\textbf{C}}
to be the structure having domain
B\cup C
and with the operations
defined as follows:

\displaystyle xy

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}x\cdot^{{\textbf{B}}}y&\mbox{ if }x,y\in B\\
x\cdot^{{\textbf{C}}}y&\mbox{ if }x,y\in C\\
\sigma^{\ell}(y)&\mbox{ if }y\in B-\{1\},x\in C-\{1\}\\
\sigma^{r}(x)&\mbox{ if }x\in B-\{1\},y\in C-\{1\}\end{array}\right.

\displaystyle x\backslash\,y

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}x\backslash^{{\textbf{C}}}y&\mbox{ if }x,y\in C\\
x\backslash^{{\textbf{B}}}y&\mbox{ if }x,y\in B\mbox{ and }\sigma^{r}(x)\not\leq y\mbox{ or }x\leq y\\

\gamma^{\ell}(y)&\mbox{ if }x\in C-\{1\},y\in B-\{1\}\\

\end{array}\right.

\displaystyle y/\,x

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}y/\,^{{\textbf{C}}}x&\mbox{ if }x,y\in C\\
y/\,^{{\textbf{B}}}x&\mbox{ if }x,y\in B\mbox{ and }\sigma^{\ell}(x)\not\leq y\mbox{ or }x\leq y\\
c_{{\textbf{C}}}&\mbox{ if }x,y\in B-\{1\}\mbox{ and }\sigma^{\ell}(x)\leq y\mbox{ and }x\not\leq y\\
\gamma^{r}(y)&\mbox{ if }x\in C-\{1\},y\in B-\{1\}\\

\end{array}\right.

\displaystyle x\land y

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}x\land^{\textbf{B}}y&\mbox{ if }x,y\in B\\
x\land^{\textbf{C}}y&\mbox{ if }x,y\in C\\
x&\mbox{ if }x\in B-\{1\},y\in C\\
y&\mbox{ if }y\in B-\{1\},x\in C\end{array}\right.

\displaystyle x\lor y

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}x\vee^{\textbf{B}}y&\mbox{ if }x,y\in B\mbox{ and $x\vee y$ exists in }B\\
\perp_{{\textbf{C}}}&\mbox{ if }x,y\in B-\{1\}\mbox{ and $x\vee y=1$ in }B\\

x&\mbox{ if }y\in B-\{1\},x\in C\\
y&\mbox{ if }x\in B-\{1\},y\in C\end{array}\right.

We call
{\textbf{B}}\boxplus_{\beta}{\textbf{C}}
the

B
and
C

\beta=(\sigma^{\ell},\sigma^{r},\gamma^{\ell},\gamma^{r})
.

Proposition 3.8
.

{\textbf{B}}\boxplus_{\beta}{\textbf{C}}
is an IRL .

Proof.

It is straightforward to check that
({\textbf{B}}\boxplus_{\beta}{\textbf{C}},\lor,\land,1)
is a lattice with top element

. Consider
(B\cup C,\cdot,1)
, we show that it is a monoid. In particular we prove that the product is associative, i.e. for all
x,y,z

x(yz)=x(yz)
. First note that if

all belong to the same component, then associativity holds since

is a partial IRL with defined products, and

is an IRL. Thus, we need to consider the cases in which one of them belongs to a different component.

(1)

If
x,y\in B
and

, then:
(xy)z=\sigma^{r}(xy)=x\sigma^{r}(y)=x(yz)
where the second equality holds by Definition
3.5
(3a).

(2)

If
x,y\in C
and

(xy)z=\sigma^{\ell}(z)=\sigma^{\ell}(\sigma^{\ell}(z))=x(yz)
, where the second equality holds since
\sigma^{\ell}
is an interior operator by Definition
3.5
(3).

(3)

If
x,z\in B
and
y\in C
, then:

(xy)z=\sigma^{r}(x)z=x\sigma^{\ell}(z)=x(yz)
, where the second equality holds by Definition
3.5
(3b).

(4)

If
x,z\in C
and
y\in B
, then:

(xy)z=\sigma^{\ell}(y)z=\sigma^{r}(\sigma^{\ell}(y))=\sigma^{\ell}(\sigma^{r}(y))=x(yz)
,where the third equality holds by Definition
3.5
(3c);

(5)

If
x\in B
and
y,z\in C
, then:

(xy)z=\sigma^{r}(\sigma^{r}(x))=\sigma^{r}(x)=x(yz)
, where the second equality holds since
\sigma^{r}
is an interior operator by Definition
3.5
(3);

(6)

If
x\in C
and
y,z\in B
, then:

(xy)z=\sigma^{\ell}(y)z=\sigma^{\ell}(yz)=x(yz)
,where the second equality holds by Definition
3.5
(3a);

It is left to show that the residuation law holds, i.e., for all

xy\leq z\mbox{ if and only if }y\leq x\backslash z\mbox{ if and only if }x\leq z/y.

Notice that if at least one of the elements is

(1)

If

is an IRL.

(2)

Let

if and only if

(3)

Let

and

,
y\leq x\backslash z=1
, and

. Hence the three inequalities all hold. The same happens if

and

and if
x\in B
and

.

(4)

Let

and
z\in B
. Then
xy\in C

, hence

,
y\not\leq x\backslash z
, and
x\not\leq z/y
. Thus, the three inequalities are all false.

(5)

Let
x,z\in B
and
y\in C
. Let us start by the first equivalence. Suppose that

, then either
x\not\leq z
and
x\backslash z=c_{{\textbf{C}}}\geq y
, or
x\leq z
and then

. Conversely, suppose that

, then
x\backslash z
is not in

, hence either

or
xy=\sigma^{r}(x)\leq x\leq z
.

For the other equivalence, notice that since
(\sigma^{r},\gamma^{r})
is a residuated pair,
xy=\sigma^{r}(x)\leq z
iff
x\leq\gamma^{r}(z)=z/y
.

(6)

Let
x\in C
and
y,z\in B
. Then residuation can be proven similarly to the previous step, taking into account that
(\sigma^{\ell},\gamma^{\ell})
forms a residuated pair, and the definition of the divisions in
{\textbf{B}}\boxplus_{\beta}{\textbf{C}}
.

We can conclude that
{\textbf{B}}\boxplus_{\beta}{\textbf{C}}
is an IRL.
∎

Recall that given a blockwise gluing
A
of

and

, we defined, fixing any

,
\sigma^{\ell}_{{\textbf{A}}}(x)=cx,

\sigma^{r}_{{\textbf{A}}}(x)=xc
,
\gamma^{\ell}_{{\textbf{A}}}(x)=c\backslash x
,
\gamma^{r}_{{\textbf{A}}}(x)=x/c
.

Theorem 3.9
.

If
A
is a blockwise gluing of
B
and
C
, then
{\textbf{A}}={\textbf{B}}\boxplus_{\beta}{\textbf{C}}
, with
\beta=(\sigma^{\ell}_{{\textbf{A}}},\sigma^{r}_{{\textbf{A}}},\gamma^{\ell}_{{\textbf{A}}},\gamma^{r}_{{\textbf{A}}})
.

Proof.

If
A
is a blockwise gluing of
B
and
C
, then
({\textbf{B}},\sigma^{\ell}_{{\textbf{A}}},\sigma^{r}_{{\textbf{A}}},\gamma^{\ell}_{{\textbf{A}}},\gamma^{r}_{{\textbf{A}}})
is a lower block by Proposition
3.6
. Given that
B\cap C=\{1\}
by definition, and given Lemma
3.3
, one can define
{\textbf{B}}\boxplus_{\beta}{\textbf{C}}
. Notice that the domains of
{\textbf{B}}\boxplus_{\beta}{\textbf{C}}
and

coincide; let us verify that the operations of
{\textbf{B}}\boxplus_{\beta}{\textbf{C}}
coincide with those of

as well.
This is clear for the lattice operations. For the product, it follows from the fact that
B
and
C
are
\cdot
-subreduct of both
A
and
{\textbf{B}}\boxplus_{\beta}{\textbf{C}}
, and the observation that products between elements in
B
and

coincide in both algebras with:

bc=\sigma^{r}_{{\textbf{A}}}(b),\;\;cb=\sigma^{\ell}_{{\textbf{A}}}(b),

for any
b\in B-\{1\}
and
c\in C-\{1\}
.

Let us finally verify that the divisions coincide. If
x,y\in C
, there is nothing to prove since
C
is a subalgebra of both
{\textbf{B}}\oplus_{\beta}{\textbf{C}}
and
A
. Let then
x,y\in B
and consider
x\backslash y\in A
, then either
x\backslash y
is in

or it is in

. If
x\backslash y
is in

, then either

or there is no element

such that

. In the first case,

in both structures; in the latter case, necessarily
\sigma^{r}_{{\textbf{A}}}(x)\not\leq y
hence

is again the same in both structures given the definition of the division in
{\textbf{B}}\boxplus_{\beta}{\textbf{C}}
. If

is in

in

, then by residuation
\sigma^{r}_{{\textbf{A}}}(x)=xc\leq y
and then

both in

and in

, given respectively Lemma
3.3
(1a) and the definition of the division in
{\textbf{B}}\boxplus_{\beta}{\textbf{C}}
.
One can show in an analogous way that also

in
{\textbf{B}}\boxplus_{\beta}{\textbf{C}}
coincide with the one of

.
Lastly, the case
x\in B
and

and viceversa follow from Proposition
3.6

and the definition of the divisions in
{\textbf{B}}\boxplus_{\beta}{\textbf{C}}
.

We can conclude that
{\textbf{A}}={\textbf{B}}\boxplus_{\beta}{\textbf{C}}
.
∎

Observe that, unlike the 1-sum construction, the blockwise gluing is not an obviously associative operation, because one has to define each time the operators for the lower block. We hence introduce an iterated version of the blockwise construction.

The idea is to consider a family of (possibly partial) algebras
({\textbf{B}}_{i})_{i\in I}
indexed by some totally ordered set
(I;\leq)
, and a tuple of operators
(\sigma^{\ell},\sigma^{r},\gamma^{\ell},\gamma^{r})
for each pair of indices
i,j\in I
, in order to describe products and divisions among the two components.

Definition 3.10
.

Let
{\bf I}=(I,\leq)
be a totally ordered index set, and let
\{{\textbf{B}}_{i}\}_{i\in I}
be a set of partial IRLs. We call a family
\{({\textbf{B}}_{i},\beta_{ij})\}_{i<_{{\mathbf{I}}}j}
with
\beta_{ij}=(\sigma^{\ell}_{ij},\sigma^{r}_{ij},\gamma^{\ell}_{ij},\gamma^{r}_{ij})
a
family of blocks
if:

(1)

B_{i}\cap B_{j}=\{1\}
.

(2)

({\textbf{B}}_{i},\beta_{ij})
is a weak lower block for all
i,j\in I,i<j
.

(3)

For all
i\in I
, and
x,y\in B_{i}
such that
x\not\leq y
, if there exists an index
j>i
such that
\sigma^{r}_{ij}(x)\leq y
(or
\sigma^{\ell}_{ij}(x)\leq y
) then there is a maximum index
m_{r}(x,y)\in I
(or
m_{\ell}(x,y)\in I
) with such property.

(4)

For all
i\in I
, and
x,y\in B_{i}
:

(a)

if
x\backslash y
is undefined then
x\not\leq y
and there exists a maximum index
m_{r}(x,y)\in I
such that
\sigma^{r}_{im_{r}(x,y)}(x)\leq y
; in this case
{\textbf{B}}_{m_{r}(x,y)}
has a splitting coatom
c_{m_{r}(x,y)}
;

(b)

if
y/x
is undefined then there exist a maximum index
m_{\ell}(x,y)\in I
such that
\sigma^{\ell}_{im_{\ell}(x,y)}(x)\leq y
; in this case
{\textbf{B}}_{m_{\ell}(x,y)}
has a splitting coatom
c_{m_{\ell}(x,y)}
.

(5)

For any
x\in B_{k}
, and
k<j<i
we have:

(a)

\sigma^{\ell}_{ki}(\sigma^{\ell}_{kj}(x))=\sigma^{\ell}_{kj}(x)=\sigma^{\ell}_{kj}(\sigma^{\ell}_{ki}(x))
;

(b)

\sigma^{r}_{ki}(\sigma^{r}_{kj}(x))=\sigma^{r}_{kj}(x)=\sigma^{r}_{kj}(\sigma^{r}_{ki}(x))
;

(c)

\sigma^{\ell}_{ki}(\sigma^{r}_{kj}(x))=\sigma^{r}_{kj}(\sigma^{\ell}_{ki}(x))
;

(d)

\sigma^{\ell}_{kj}(\sigma^{r}_{ki}x)=\sigma^{r}_{ki}(\sigma^{\ell}_{kj}(x))
.

(6)

If
i<j<k
in
I
, then
\sigma^{r}_{ij}(x)\leq\sigma^{r}_{ik}(x)
and
\sigma^{\ell}_{ij}(x)\leq\sigma^{\ell}_{ik}(x)
.

(7)

If
x,y\in B_{i}
are such that
x\lor y=1
for some
i\in I
that is not the maximum in
I
, then there exist
j>i
that covers
i
in
{\bf I}
such that
{\textbf{B}}_{j}
has a bottom element
\bot_{j}
.

(8)

If there is a top element
i_{\top}\in I
,
{\textbf{B}}_{i_{\top}}
is a total algebra and we add to the family the pair
({\textbf{B}}_{i_{\top}},\beta_{i_{\top}\infty})
with
\beta_{i_{\top}\infty}=({\rm id},{\rm id},{\rm id},{\rm id})
.

Given such a family of blocks, we define the
(iterated) blockwise gluing

\boxplus_{\bf I}({\textbf{B}}_{i},\beta_{ij})
to be the algebra with domain
\bigcup_{i\in I}B_{i}

and operations defined as follows:

\displaystyle x\cdot y

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}x\cdot^{{\textbf{B}}_{i}}y&\mbox{ if }x,y\in B_{i}\mbox{ for some }i\in I\\
\sigma^{r}_{ij}(x)&\mbox{ if }x\in B_{i}-\{1\},y\in B_{j}\mbox{ and }i<j\\
\sigma^{\ell}_{ji}(y)&\mbox{ if }x\in B_{i}-\{1\},y\in B_{j}\mbox{ and }j<i\end{array}\right.

\displaystyle x\backslash\,y

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}x\backslash^{{\textbf{B}}_{i}}y&\mbox{ if }x,y\in B_{i},\mbox{ and }x\leq y\mbox{ or there is no }k\in I\mbox{ such that }\sigma^{r}_{ik}(x)\leq y\\
c_{m_{r}(x,y)}&\mbox{ if }x,y\in B_{i},x\not\leq y,\mbox{ and }\sigma^{r}_{im_{r}(x,y)}(x)\leq y\\
\gamma^{\ell}_{ji}(y)&\mbox{ if }x\in B_{i}-\{1\},y\in B_{j}\mbox{ and }j<i\\
1&\mbox{ if }x\leq y\\
\end{array}\right.

\displaystyle y/\,x

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}y/\,^{{\textbf{B}}_{i}}x&\mbox{ if }x,y\in B_{i},\mbox{ and }x\leq y\mbox{ or there is no }k\in I\mbox{ such that }\sigma^{\ell}_{ik}(x)\leq y\\
c_{m_{\ell}(x,y)}&\mbox{ if }x,y\in B_{i},x\not\leq y,\mbox{ and }\sigma^{\ell}_{im_{\ell}(x,y)}(x)\leq y\\
\gamma^{r}_{ji}(y)&\mbox{ if }x\in B_{i}-\{1\},y\in B_{j}\mbox{ and }j<i\\
1&\mbox{ if }x\leq y\\
\end{array}\right.

\displaystyle x\wedge y

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}x\wedge_{{\textbf{B}}_{i}}y&\mbox{ if }x,y\in B_{i}\\
x&\mbox{ if }x\in B_{j}\mbox{ and }y\in B_{i}\mbox{ with }j<i\\
y&\mbox{ if }x\in B_{j}\mbox{ and }y\in B_{i}\mbox{ with }i<j\\
\end{array}\right.

\displaystyle x\vee y

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\bot_{j}&\mbox{ if }x,y\in B_{i}\mbox{ with }i\neq\max{\textbf{I}},x\vee_{{\textbf{B}}_{i}}y=1,j\mbox{ covers }i\\
x\vee_{{\textbf{B}}_{i}}y&\mbox{ if }x,y\in B_{i},x\vee_{{\textbf{B}}_{i}}y\neq 1\\
x&\mbox{ if }x\in B_{i}\mbox{ and }y\in B_{j}\mbox{ with }j<i\\
y&\mbox{ if }x\in B_{i}\mbox{ and }y\in B_{j}\mbox{ with }i<j\\
\end{array}\right.

Theorem 3.11
.

Given a family of blocks
\{({\textbf{B}}_{i},\beta_{ij})\}_{i<_{{\mathbf{I}}}j}
, their blockwise gluing
\boxplus_{\bf I}({\textbf{B}}_{i},\beta_{ij})
is an IRL.

Proof.

First, as a straightforward consequence of the definitions,
\boxplus_{\bf I}({\textbf{B}}_{i},\beta_{ij})
has a lattice reduct with top element
1
. Let us then consider
(\bigcup_{i\in{I}}B_{i},\cdot,1)
, we proceed to show that it is a monoid. In particular, we verify that the product is associative. Notice that if
x,y,z
belong to the same component
B_{i}
, then associativity holds since
{\textbf{B}}_{i}
is a partial IRL in which products are always defined. If two among
x,y,z
belong to the same component, then associativity can be proved as in Proposition
3.8
. Thus, let
x,y,z
be in three different components
{\textbf{B}}_{i}
,
{\textbf{B}}_{j}
, and
{\textbf{B}}_{k}
with
i>j>k
, then we need to consider the following cases.

(1)

Let
x\in B_{i},y\in B_{j}
and
z\in B_{k}
, then:

(xy)z=\sigma^{\ell}_{ji}(y)z=\sigma^{\ell}_{kj}(z)
and
x(yz)=x\sigma^{\ell}_{kj}(z)=\sigma^{\ell}_{ki}(\sigma^{\ell}_{kj}(z))
; by Definition
3.10
(5a), we get
(xy)z=x(yz)
.

(2)

Let
y\in B_{i},x\in B_{j}
and
z\in B_{k}
, then:

(xy)z=\sigma^{r}_{ji}(x)z=\sigma^{\ell}_{kj}(z)
and
x(yz)=x\sigma^{\ell}_{ki}(z)=\sigma^{\ell}_{kj}(\sigma^{\ell}_{ki}(z))
; by Definition
3.10
(5a), we get
(xy)z=x(yz)
.

(3)

Let
x\in B_{i},z\in B_{j}
and
y\in B_{k}
, then:

(xy)z=\sigma^{\ell}_{ki}(y)z=\sigma^{r}_{kj}(\sigma^{\ell}_{ki}(y))
and
x(yz)=x\sigma^{r}_{kj}(y)=\sigma^{\ell}_{ki}(\sigma^{r}_{kj}(y))
; by Definition
3.10
(5c), we get
(xy)z=x(yz)
;

(4)

Let
z\in B_{i},x\in B_{j}
and
y\in B_{k}
, then:

(xy)z=\sigma^{\ell}_{kj}(y)z=\sigma^{r}_{ki}(\sigma^{\ell}_{kj}(y))
and
x(yz)=x\sigma^{r}_{ki}(y)=\sigma^{\ell}_{kj}({\sigma^{r}_{ki}(y)})
; thus, by Definition
3.10
(5d), we get
(xy)z=x(yz)
;

(5)

Let
z\in B_{i},y\in B_{j}
and
x\in B_{k}
, then:

(xy)z=\sigma^{r}_{kj}(x)z=\sigma^{r}_{ki}(\sigma^{r}_{kj}(x))
and
x(yz)=x\sigma^{r}_{ji}(y)=\sigma^{r}_{kj}(x)
; by Definition
3.10
(5b), we get
(xy)z=x(yz)
;

(6)

Let
y\in B_{i},z\in B_{j}
and
x\in B_{k}
, then:

(xy)z=\sigma^{r}_{ki}(x)z=\sigma^{r}_{kj}(\sigma^{r}_{ki}(x))
and
x(yz)=x\sigma^{\ell}_{ji}(z)=\sigma^{r}_{kj}(x)
; agin by Definition
3.10
(5b), we get
(xy)z=x(yz)
.

Let us now verify that the residuation law holds, i.e., for all
x,y,z
in the domain:
xy\leq z
iff
y\leq x\backslash z
iff
x\leq z/y
. If at least two elements are in the same component, the proof is analogous to the one in Proposition
3.8
, with some adjustments due to the new divisions in the following cases:

(1)

Let
x,z\in B_{i},y\in B_{j}
and
i<j
in
I
. If
xy=\sigma^{r}_{ij}(x)\leq z
, then either
x\leq z
and then
y\leq x\backslash z=1
, or
y\leq x\backslash z=c_{m_{r}(x,z)}
; indeed necessarily
j\leq m_{r}(x,z)
, since the latter is the largest index
m
such that
\sigma^{r}_{im}(x)\leq z
. Conversely, if
y\leq x\backslash z
, then either
x\leq z
or
\sigma^{r}_{im_{r}(x,z)}(x)\leq z
. In the former case, we get
xy=\sigma^{r}_{ij}(x)\leq x\leq z
, since
\sigma^{r}_{ij}
is an interior operator. In the latter,
y\leq x\backslash z=c_{m_{r}(x,z)}
, and therefore
j\leq m_{r}(x,z)
which implies that
xy=\sigma^{r}_{ij}(x)\leq\sigma^{r}_{im_{r}(x,z)}(x)\leq z
by Definition
3.10
(6). For the other equivalence,
xy=\sigma^{r}_{ij}(x)\leq z
iff
x\leq\gamma^{r}_{ij}(z)=z/y
by the fact that
(\sigma^{r}_{ij},\gamma^{r}_{ij})
is a residuated pair.

(2)

Let
x\in B_{j},y,z\in B_{i}
and
i<j
in
I
. Then residuation can be shown analogously to the case above.

Finally, if
x,y,z
are in three different components, then residuation can be easily seen to hold as a consequence of the definitions and the order. In particular, let
i<j<k
, then the three inequalities are verified in the following cases:
x\in B_{i},y\in B_{j},z\in B_{k}
;
x\in B_{i},z\in B_{j},y\in B_{k}
;
y\in B_{i},x\in B_{j},z\in B_{k}
;
y\in B_{i},z\in B_{j},x\in B_{k}
. The three inequalities are all invalid in the remaining cases:
z\in B_{i},x\in B_{j},y\in B_{k}
and
z\in B_{i},y\in B_{j},x\in B_{k}
.

We can conclude that
\boxplus_{\bf I}({\textbf{B}}_{i},\beta_{ij})
is an IRL.
∎

We will now show that the class of IRLs which can be characterized by the previous construction is the desired one, in which algebras can be seen as made by components stacked on top of each other, glued at
1
, and multiplying in blocks.

Definition 3.12
.

We define
\mathsf{BlockG}\subseteq\mathsf{IRL}
to be the class of IRLs
A
such that:

(1)

the domain of
A
can be seen as the union of a set
\{(B_{i})\}_{i\in I}
for some totally ordered index set
{\bf I}=(I,\leq)
, with
B_{i}\cap B_{j}=\{1\}
.

(2)

The elements of
B_{i}-\{1\}
are strictly below the elements of
B_{j}
iff
i<j
.

(3)

Each
B_{i}
, for
i\in I
, is the domain of a partial IRL
{\textbf{B}}_{i}
with the inherited operations from
A
, where all operations are defined except possibly some divisions and some joins.

(4)

if
x,y\in B_{i}-\{1\},z\in B_{j}
and
j<i
, then
xz=yz
and
zx=zy
.

Proposition 3.13
.

{\textbf{A}}\in\mathsf{BlockG}
if and only if it is a blockwise gluing of some family of blocks.

Proof.

Let
{\textbf{A}}\in\mathsf{BlockG}
, we will prove that it is a blockwise gluing of its components
\{{\textbf{B}}_{i}\}_{i\in I}
, with suitably defined operators
\beta_{ij}
. If
{\textbf{A}}\in\mathsf{BlockG}
, then its domain can be seen as
\bigcup_{i\in{I}}B_{i}
, where
{\bf I}
is a totally ordered index set. If there is a top element
i_{\top}\in I
, we set
\sigma^{\ell}_{i_{\top}\infty}=\sigma^{r}_{i_{\top}\infty}=\gamma^{\ell}_{i_{\top}\infty}=\gamma^{r}_{i_{\top}\infty}={\rm id}
. Now, recalling that if
x,y\in B_{j}
and
z\in B_{i}
with
i<j
, then
xz=yz
and
zx=zy
, we define the rest of the operators
\sigma^{\ell}_{ij}
,
\sigma^{r}_{ij},\gamma^{\ell}_{ij}
and
\gamma^{r}_{ij}
in the obvious way: given
c_{j}\in{\textbf{B}}_{j}
, we set

\sigma^{\ell}_{ij}(x)=c_{j}x\qquad\sigma^{r}_{ij}(x)=xc_{j}\qquad\gamma^{\ell}_{ij}(x)=c_{j}\backslash x\qquad\gamma^{r}_{ij}(x)=x/c_{j}

for any
x\in B_{i}-\{1\}
, and all operators are
1
at
1
.
We proceed to prove that
\{({\textbf{B}}_{i},\sigma^{\ell}_{ij},\sigma^{r}_{ij},\gamma^{\ell}_{ij},\gamma^{r}_{ij})\}_{i<_{{\textbf{I}}}j}
is a family of blocks according to Definition
3.10
.

First note, for property (1), that
B_{i}\cap B_{j}=\{1\}
by definition of the class
\mathsf{BlockG}
. For (2), one can show that
({\textbf{B}}_{i},\sigma^{\ell}_{ij},\sigma^{r}_{ij},\gamma^{\ell}_{ij},\gamma^{r}_{ij})
is a weak lower block for all
i,j\in I
with
i<j
, following the proof of Proposition
3.6
.

Let us check (3), i.e., for all
i\in I
, and
x,y\in B_{i}
such that
x\not\leq y
, if there exists an index
j>i
such that
\sigma^{r}_{ij}(x)\leq y
then there is a maximum index
m_{r}(x,y)\in I
with such property (the proof for the left operators being analogous). Notice that if
xc_{j}=\sigma^{r}_{ij}(x)\leq y
, we get by residuation that
c_{j}\leq x\backslash y
. It follows that the index
m
such that
x\backslash y\in B_{m}
is the desired
m_{r}(x,y)
.

We now verify (4a), the proof of (4b) being similar. Let
x,y\in B_{i}
, for some
i\in I
, such that
x\backslash y\not\in B_{i}
. Then necessarily
x\not\leq y
, since
1\in B_{i}
, and
x\backslash y
belongs to some
B_{j}
with
j>i
. Thus, since

\sigma^{r}_{ij}(x)=x(x\backslash y)\leq y
, as shown in the previous point there is a maximum index
m_{r}(x,y)\in I
such that
\sigma^{r}_{im_{r}(x,y)}(x)\leq y
; moreover, since all the elements in
B_{m_{r}(x,y)}
multiply the same with respect to the ones in
B_{i}
,
x\backslash y
is the splitting coatom of
{\textbf{B}}_{m_{r}(x,y)}
.

Notice that properties (5) and (6) follow straightforwardly from, respectively, associativity and order preservation of the product in
A
.

For (7), suppose that given
x,y\in{\textbf{B}}_{i}
,
x\vee y
is not defined in
{\textbf{B}}_{i}
. Then, since
{\textbf{B}}_{i}
is strictly below any
{\textbf{B}}_{j}
with
j>i
, necessarily
x\vee y=\perp_{j}
where
\perp_{j}
is the minimum of the component
{\textbf{B}}_{j}
, with
j
covering
i
.

Finally, for (8), if there is a top element
i_{\top}\in I
,
{\textbf{B}}_{i_{\top}}
necessarily is a total algebra, and we already defined
\beta_{i_{\top}\infty}=({\rm id},{\rm id},{\rm id},{\rm id})
.

Thus, given
{\textbf{A}}\in\mathsf{BlockG}
, one can define the blockwise gluing
\boxplus_{\bf I}({\textbf{B}}_{i},\beta_{ij})
where the
{\textbf{B}}_{i}
s are the components of
A
and the
\beta_{ij}
s are defined as above. It is left to show that
{\textbf{A}}=\boxplus_{\bf I}({\textbf{B}}_{i},\beta_{ij})
, that is to say, the operations of
A
coincides with the ones in
\boxplus_{\bf I}({\textbf{B}}_{i},\beta_{ij})
; this is a straightforward consequence of the definitions. Hence we can conclude that
{\textbf{A}}=\boxplus_{\bf I}({\textbf{B}}_{i},\beta_{ij})
.

For the converse, the fact that a blockwise gluing of a family of blocks is in
\mathsf{BlockG}
is a direct consequence of the definition.
∎

Remark 3.14
.

Proposition
3.13
shows that the iterated construction of blockwise gluing characterizes the class of algebras in
\mathsf{BlockG}
, which are intuitively gluings of some subcomponents whose products behave in blocks. We wish to point out that the relationship between algebras and family of blocks is not one-one. Precisely, different families of blocks can be used to construct the same algebra. This is due to the fact that some divisions present in the blocks can be “forgotten” in the blockwise gluing: it is possible that for
x,y\in B_{i}
,
x\backslash_{{\textbf{B}}_{i}}y
is defined in
B_{i}
, but there is a (maximum)
m\in I
such that
\sigma^{r}_{im}(x)\leq y
and hence
x\backslash y=c_{m}
in the gluing. Therefore, substituting
{\textbf{B}}_{i}
with a variation of it, where
x\backslash_{{\textbf{B}}_{i}}y
is not defined, would yield the same blockwise gluing.
This is more of a technicality than a conceptual point, but it allows one to use total algebras to construct a gluing, since it does not force the initial blocks to be partial algebras even when some of their divisions will be forgotten in the construction.

3.1.
Preservation of identities and
\mathsf{HSP_{u}}

In this subsection we investigate the interaction of the blockwise gluing construction with class operators and equational properties. For simplicity of exposition, we restrict to the non-iterated construction. This investigation is inspired by the analogous ones for 1-sums
[
2
]
and gluings of residuated lattices
[
22
]
.

3.1.1.
Preservation of identities

We would like to identify which kind of equations are preserved by the blockwise gluing construction. In particular, we characterize the cases in which commutativity, divisibility, and semilinearity are preserved. Observe that linearity is obviously maintained by the construction.

Let
B
be a partial integral residuated lattice. We call it semilinear (or divisible) if it satisfies the semilinearity (or divisibility) identity whenever all the terms involved are defined.

Proposition 3.15
.

Let
{\textbf{B}}\boxplus_{\beta}{\textbf{C}}
be a blockwise gluing, with
\beta=(\sigma^{\ell},\sigma^{r},\gamma^{\ell},\gamma^{r})
. Then:

(1)

{\textbf{B}}\boxplus_{\beta}{\textbf{C}}
is commutative if and only if
B
and
C
are commutative and
\sigma^{\ell}=\sigma^{r}
.

(2)

{\textbf{B}}\boxplus_{\beta}{\textbf{C}}
is divisible if and only if
C
is divisible,
B
is divisible, and the blockwise gluing coincides with the 1-sum.

(3)

{\textbf{B}}\boxplus_{\beta}{\textbf{C}}
is semilinear if and only if
B
is linear,
C
is semilinear, and either
C
is linear or the gluing is a 1-sum.

Proof.

(1)

Note that the right-to-left direction is obvious. For the left-to-right, assume
{\textbf{B}}\boxplus_{\beta}{\textbf{C}}
is commutative; necessarily
B
and
C
are commutative since they are subreducts under products. Moreover, for any
x\in B
and
y\in C
,
\sigma^{r}(x)=x\cdot y=y\cdot x=\sigma^{\ell}(x)
, by definition of the operations and by the fact that
{\textbf{B}}\boxplus_{\beta}{\textbf{C}}
is commutative.

(2)

Note again that the right-to-left direction is obvious. For the left-to-right direction, assume
{\textbf{B}}\boxplus_{\beta}{\textbf{C}}
is divisible; then necessarily
C
is divisible and
B
is divisible. Moreover, since
{\textbf{B}}\boxplus_{\beta}{\textbf{C}}
is divisible, for any
x\in B
and
y\in C-\{1\}
,

x=x\wedge y=y\cdot(y\backslash x)=\sigma^{\ell}(\gamma^{\ell}(x)).

From the fact that
x=\sigma^{\ell}(\gamma^{\ell}(x)
, one gets

\sigma^{\ell}(x)=\sigma^{\ell}(\sigma^{\ell}(\gamma^{\ell}(x)))=\sigma^{\ell}(\gamma^{\ell}(x))=x.

Similarly, one can show that also
\sigma^{r}
coincides with the identity map, yielding that
{\textbf{B}}\boxplus_{\beta}{\textbf{C}}
is the 1-sum of
B
and
C
.

(3)

Suppose that
{\textbf{B}}\boxplus_{\beta}{\textbf{C}}
is semilinear, then
C
is also semilinear since it is a subalgebra of the gluing. We now show that
B
is necessarily linear. By way of contradiction, consider two incomparable elements
x,y\in B
, i.e. such that
x\not\leq y
and
y\not\leq x
. Then
x\backslash y\neq 1
and
y\backslash x\neq 1
. Notice that by the definition of the operations in the gluing, either
x\backslash y\in B-\{1\}
or
x\backslash y=c_{{\textbf{C}}}
, the coatom of
C
; the same holds for
y\backslash x
. Thus, in any case,
(x\backslash y)\vee(y\backslash x)<1
. But since
{\textbf{B}}\boxplus_{\beta}{\textbf{C}}
is semilinear, and so in particular prelinear,
(x\backslash y)\vee(y\backslash x)=1
yielding a contradiction. Therefore,
B
is necessarily linear. For the last part, suppose that
C
is not linear. Then, there are incomparable elements in
C
, say
x,y\in C
such that
x\not\leq y
and
y\not\leq x
. We prove that, necessarily, both operators
\sigma^{\ell}
and
\sigma^{r}
must coincide with the identity map on
B
, or in other words, the gluing is a 1-sum, which will prove the claim. Suppose that there is an element
u\in B
such that
\sigma^{\ell}(u)<u
. Take then any
v\in B
, given that
{\textbf{B}}\boxplus_{\beta}{\textbf{C}}
is semilinear it holds that:

(u\backslash(y\backslash x)u)\lor(v(x\backslash y)/v)=1.

Notice that
u\backslash(y\backslash x)u=u\backslash\sigma^{\ell}(u)
and
v(x\backslash y)/v=\sigma^{r}(v)/v
. Since
\sigma^{\ell}(u)<u
, if also
\sigma^{r}(v)<v
then both
u\backslash\sigma^{\ell}(u)
and
\sigma^{r}(v)/v
are either elements of
B-\{1\}
or they coincide with the splitting coatom of
C
. In any case, their join is strictly smaller than
1
, a contradiction. Hence necessarily
\sigma^{r}(v)=v
, for all
v\in B
. This proves that at least one among
\sigma^{r}
and
\sigma^{\ell}
is the identity map. We proceed to prove that they must coincide. Indeed, by way of contradiction suppose, without loss of generality, that
\sigma^{r}
is the identity map but
\sigma^{\ell}
is not: i.e., there is
u\in B-\{1\}
such that
\sigma^{\ell}(u)<u
. Consider again the incomparable elements of
C
,
x
and
y
. An application of semilinearity yields that:

(u\backslash(y\backslash x)u)\lor(x(x\backslash y)/x)=(u\backslash\sigma^{\ell}(u))\lor(x(x\backslash y)/x)=1.

Since
\sigma^{r}(u)=u>\sigma^{\ell}(u)
, by the definition of the operations in the gluing we get that
u\backslash\sigma^{\ell}(u)\in B-\{1\}
. Hence necessarily, for the above join to be
1
,
x(x\backslash y)/x=1
. But then by residuation
x\leq x(x\backslash y)\leq y
, a contradiction since
x
and
y
are incomparable. Hence, there can be no element
u\in B-\{1\}
such that
\sigma^{\ell}(u)<u
, which proves that also
\sigma^{\ell}
is the identity map. This completes the left-to-right direction.

For the converse, notice first that if both
B
and
C
are linear, then the gluing is linear itself and hence semilinear. Suppose now that
B
is linear,
C
is semilinear, and the gluing is a 1-sum, i.e.,
\sigma^{\ell}=\sigma^{r}={\rm id}
.
We verify that
{\textbf{B}}\boxplus_{\beta}{\textbf{C}}
satisfies, for all
x,y,u,v
in the domain, the semilinearity identity:

(u\backslash(y\backslash x)u)\lor(v(x\backslash y)/v)=1.

We distinguish the following cases:

(a)

If
x,y,u,v\in C
, then the identity holds since
C
is semilinear.

(b)

If
x
and
y
are comparable, then either
x\leq y
or
y\leq x
which implies that the equation holds since either
u\backslash(y\backslash x)u=u\backslash u=1
or
v(x\backslash y)/v=v/v=1
, hence either way their join is
1
. Notice that this in particular covers the cases where at least one among
x
and
y
is in
B-\{1\}
.

(c)

Let now
x,y,v\in C
with
x
and
y
incomparable, and suppose
u\in B-\{1\}
. Then
u\backslash(y\backslash x)u=u\backslash\sigma^{\ell}(u)=u\backslash u=1
and the identity holds. The proof for
x,y,u\in C
and
v\in B-\{1\}
is completely analogous.

We covered all the possible cases, hence proving that
{\textbf{B}}\boxplus_{\beta}{\textbf{C}}
is semilinear.∎

Let us now observe that, since the upper component of the blockwise gluing is a subalgebra, and the lower component is a subreduct of the blockwise gluing, most of the one-variable identities are preserved.

Proposition 3.16
.

The blockwise gluing
{\textbf{B}}\boxplus_{\beta}{\textbf{C}}
preserves all the one-variable equations valid both in
B
and
C
not involving divisions. In case the gluing is a 1-sum, all the one-variable equations valid both in
B
and
C
are preserved.

Considering the previous proposition, we get that the blockwise gluing construction preserves
n
-potency, i.e.
x^{n}=x^{n+1}
for
n\geq 1
. It is also important to notice that, in general, the blockwise gluing construction does not preserve all the monoidal identities that are true both in
B
and
C
. For instance, as shown above, commutativity is only preserved when
\sigma^{\ell}=\sigma^{r}
. In fact:

Proposition 3.17
.

If
\sigma^{\ell}=\sigma^{r}
,
{\textbf{B}}\boxplus_{\beta}{\textbf{C}}
satisfies all the monoidal identities that are true in both
B
and
C
.

Proof.

The proof goes as the one of Proposition 5.3 in
[
22
]
.
∎

3.1.2.
\mathsf{HSP_{u}}

Constructions such as the blockwise gluing can help one better understand and describe the structure of the algebras. To this end, we now provide a description of the lattice of congruence filters of the blockwise gluing of two algebras, and we characterize when a blockwise gluing is subdirectly irreducible. Moreover, we describe the fundamental algebraic operators of subalgebras, homomorphic images, and ultrapowers of a blockwise gluing.

Definition 3.18
.

Let
B
be a partial integral residuated lattice. A
partial filter

F
of
B
is a nonempty upset of
B
that is closed under existing products and existing conjugates, i.e, for any
a\in B
and
x,y\in F
,
xy
,
yx
,
ax/a
, and
a\backslash xa
are in
F
whenever defined.

Let us consider the poset of partial filters of
B
ordered by inclusion; we observe that it is a lattice with intersection as meet, and the smallest partial filter containing the union as join.
Consider now a lower block
({\textbf{B}},\beta)
with
\beta=(\sigma^{\ell},\sigma^{r},\gamma^{\ell},\gamma^{r})
. We call
\beta
-filter
of
({\textbf{B}},\beta)
a partial filter
F
such that: for any
b\in B
such that
\sigma^{\ell}(b)\not\leq\sigma^{r}(b)
it holds that
\sigma^{r}(b)/b\in F
, and similarly, for any
b\in B
such that
\sigma^{r}(b)\not\leq\sigma^{\ell}(b)
it holds that
b\backslash\sigma^{\ell}(b)\in F
.
\beta
-filters also form a lattice, which we denote by
\mathbf{\beta Fil}({\textbf{B}})
. Notice that if
\sigma^{\ell}=\sigma^{r}
, then all partial filters are
\beta
-filters.

We are now ready to describe the lattice of congruence filters of a blockwise gluing.

Theorem 3.19
.

Let
{\textbf{B}}\boxplus_{\beta}{\textbf{C}}
with
\beta=(\sigma^{\ell},\sigma^{r},\gamma^{\ell},\sigma^{r})
. Then

(1)

if
\sigma^{\ell}=\sigma^{r}
,
\mathbf{Fil}({\textbf{B}}\boxplus_{\beta}{\textbf{C}})\cong\mathbf{Fil}({\textbf{C}})\oplus(\mathbf{\beta Fil}({\textbf{B}})-\{1\})
, where
\oplus
is the ordinal sum of posets;

(2)

if
\sigma^{\ell}\neq\sigma^{r}
,
\mathbf{Fil}({\textbf{B}}\boxplus_{\beta}{\textbf{C}})\cong\mathbf{\beta Fil}({\textbf{B}})
.

Proof.

(1) Let us consider the map
\varphi:\mathbf{Fil}({\textbf{B}}\boxplus_{\beta}{\textbf{C}})\to\mathbf{Fil}({\textbf{C}})\oplus(\mathbf{\beta Fil}({\textbf{B}})-\{1\})
defined by:

\displaystyle\varphi(F)=

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{l l}F&\mbox{ if }F\cap B=\{1\},\\
F\cap B&\mbox{ otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

The map
\varphi
is well defined. Indeed, let
F\in{\rm Fil}({\textbf{B}}\boxplus_{\beta}{\textbf{C}})
, then if
F\cap B=\{1\}
, clearly
F\in{\rm Fil}({\textbf{C}})
.
If instead there exists an
x\in F\cap B
with
x\neq 1
, then notice that
C\subseteq F
, since
F
needs to be closed upwards, and it is easy to see that
F\cap B\in{\rm\beta Fil}({\textbf{B}})-\{1\}
.

We prove that
\varphi
is the desired lattice isomorphism. It is a straightforward consequence of the definition that
\phi
is injective and preserves the order. To prove surjectivity, let
F\in{\rm Fil}({\textbf{C}})\oplus({\rm\beta Fil}({\textbf{B}})-\{1\})
, then either
F\in{\rm Fil}({\textbf{C}})
or
F\in{\rm\beta Fil}({\textbf{B}})-\{1\}
.

If
F\in{\rm Fil}({\textbf{C}})
, we show that
F
is also a congruence filter of
{\textbf{B}}\boxplus_{\beta}{\textbf{C}}
, and hence
\varphi(F)=F
. Since
C
is a subalgebra of the gluing, it suffices to check that
F
is closed under conjugates with respect to elements of
B
. Suppose there is
x\in F,x\neq 1
, and let
b\in B-\{1\}
. Then
b\backslash xb=b\backslash\sigma^{\ell}(b)=c_{{\textbf{C}}}
by the definition of the operations, since
\sigma^{r}(b)=\sigma^{\ell}(b)
by hypothesis. Similarly,
bx/b=c_{{\textbf{C}}}
. Notice that
x\leq c_{{\textbf{C}}}
hence
c_{{\textbf{C}}}\in F
. Thus,
F
is a filter of the gluing as desired.

Suppose now
F\in{\rm\beta Fil}({\textbf{B}})
,
F\neq\{1\}
, and let
F^{\prime}=F\cup C
. We prove that
F^{\prime}
is a congruence filter of the gluing. It suffices to check closure under conjugates involving elements of
B
, since
F^{\prime}
is clearly closed under products and upwards, and
C
is a subalgebra of the gluing. We need to consider the following cases:

•

If
x\in C,b\in B
, then
bx/b=\sigma^{r}(b)/b\in C\subseteq F^{\prime}
by the definition of the operations, since
\sigma^{\ell}(b)=\sigma^{r}(b)
. Similarly
b\backslash xb\in C\subseteq F^{\prime}
.

•

Let now
x\in F,b\in B
, then
bx/b
and
b\backslash xb
are either in
B
, hence in
F
given that
F
is a partial filter, or in
C
, hence in
F^{\prime}
.

•

Consider then
x\in F,c\in C
. Then
cx/c=\sigma^{\ell}(x)/c=\gamma^{r}\sigma^{\ell}(x)
. Now, since
\sigma^{r}(x)=\sigma^{\ell}(x)
by hypothesis, we get
x\leq\gamma^{r}\sigma^{\ell}(x)\in F\subseteq F^{\prime}
, using that
(\sigma^{r},\gamma^{r})
is a residuated pair.

Hence
F^{\prime}
is a filter of
{\textbf{B}}\boxplus_{\beta}{\textbf{C}}
, and clearly
\varphi(F^{\prime})=F
. We have shown that
\varphi
is an order-preserving bijection. To see that it is a lattice isomorphism, it suffices to observe that, given what we have shown above, the map
\varphi^{\prime}:\mathbf{Fil}({\textbf{C}})\oplus(\mathbf{\beta Fil}({\textbf{B}})-\{1\})\to\mathbf{Fil}({\textbf{B}}\boxplus_{\beta}{\textbf{C}})
defined by:

\displaystyle\varphi^{\prime}(F)=

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{l l}F&\mbox{ if }F\in{\rm Fil}({\textbf{C}}),\\
F\cup C&\mbox{ if }F\in{\rm\beta Fil}({\textbf{B}})\end{array}\right.

is the inverse of
\varphi
, and it is also order preserving.
This proves that
\varphi
is the desired lattice isomorphism.

(2) Suppose now that
\sigma^{\ell}\neq\sigma^{r}
. First, we notice that every nontrivial filter
F\in{\rm Fil}({\textbf{B}}\boxplus_{\beta}{\textbf{C}})
intersects
B-\{1\}
. Indeed, let
x\in F-\{1\}
. Since
F
is closed under conjugation, for any
y\in B
and
x\in F
,
yx/y
and
y\backslash xy
are in
F
. Recalling that
\sigma^{\ell}\neq\sigma^{r}
, there exists
b\in B
such that
\sigma^{\ell}(b)\neq\sigma^{r}(b)
, i.e. either
\sigma^{\ell}(b)\not\leq\sigma^{r}(b)
or
\sigma^{r}(b)\not\leq\sigma^{\ell}(b)
. If
\sigma^{\ell}(b)\not\leq\sigma^{r}(b)
, then, by definition of the operations,
bx/b=\sigma^{r}(b)/b\in B-\{1\}
. Similarly, if
\sigma^{r}(b)\not\leq\sigma^{\ell}(b)
, then
b\backslash xb=b\backslash\sigma^{\ell}(b)\in B-\{1\}
.

Let us then consider the map
\psi:\mathbf{Fil}({\textbf{B}}\boxplus_{\beta}{\textbf{C}})\to\mathbf{\beta Fil}({\textbf{B}})
sending
F\mapsto F\cap B
. To see that
\psi
is indeed a map from the filters of the gluing to the
\beta
-filters of the lower block
({\textbf{B}},\beta)
, consider any nontrivial
F\in{\rm Fil}({\textbf{B}}\boxplus_{\beta}{\textbf{C}})
. It is clear that
F\cap B
is a partial filter of
B
. To see that it is a
\beta
-filter, notice first that, as argued above, any
c\in C
is in
F
. Thus, by closure under conjugates, for every
b\in B
, both
b\backslash cb=b\backslash\sigma^{\ell}(b)
and
bc/b=\sigma^{r}(b)/b
are in
F
. Therefore, if
\sigma^{\ell}(b)\not\leq\sigma^{r}(b)
we have that
bc/b=\sigma^{r}(b)/b\in F\cap B
, and similarly, for any
b\in B
such that
\sigma^{r}(b)\not\leq\sigma^{\ell}(b)
we have that
b\backslash\sigma^{\ell}(b)\in F\cap B
. Hence,
F\cap B
is a
\beta
-filter.

We move to show that
\psi
is the desired lattice isomorphism. As above,
\psi
is clearly injective and order preserving. To prove surjectivity, Note first that
\psi(\{1\})=\{1\}
. Now
let
F\in{\rm\beta Fil}({\textbf{B}})-\{1\}
, and consider
F^{\prime}=F\cup C
. We prove that
F^{\prime}\in{\rm Fil}({\textbf{B}}\boxplus_{\beta}{\textbf{C}})
, by showing that it is closed under conjugates. We need to consider the following cases:

•

Let first
x\in C,b\in B
, then
bx/b=\sigma^{r}(b)/b
. If
\sigma^{\ell}(y)\leq\sigma^{r}(y)
then
\sigma^{r}(b)/b\in C\subseteq F^{\prime}
. If instead
\sigma^{\ell}(y)\not\leq\sigma^{r}(y)
, then
\sigma^{r}(b)/b\in F
since
F
is a
\beta
-filter. The proof that
b\backslash xb\in F
is similar.

•

Let now
x\in F,b\in B
, then
bx/b
and
b\backslash xb
are either in
B
, hence in
F\subseteq F^{\prime}
because
F
is closed under existing conjugates, or in
C
, hence in
F^{\prime}
.

•

Finally, let
x\in F,c\in C
. Then
cx/c=\sigma^{\ell}(x)/c\geq\sigma^{\ell}(x)\geq x^{2}\in F
, and then
cx/c\in F
. Similarly
c\backslash xc\in F
.

Thus
F^{\prime}
is a congruence filter of the gluing, and since
\psi(F^{\prime})=F
, we proved surjectivity of
\psi
. It also follows from what we have shown that the map
\psi^{\prime}:\mathbf{\beta Fil}({\textbf{B}})\to\mathbf{Fil}({\textbf{B}}\boxplus_{\beta}{\textbf{C}})
defined by
\psi^{\prime}(\{1\})=\{1\}
and
\psi^{\prime}(F)=F\cup C
is the inverse of
\psi
, and
\psi^{\prime}
is also clearly order-preserving. Therefore,
\psi
provides the lattice isomorphism showing
\mathbf{Fil}({\textbf{B}}\boxplus_{\beta}{\textbf{C}})\cong\mathbf{\beta Fil}({\textbf{B}})
.
∎

As a consequence:

Corollary 3.20
.

Let
{\textbf{B}}\boxplus_{\beta}{\textbf{C}}
be a blockwise gluing with
\beta=(\sigma^{\ell},\sigma^{r},\gamma^{\ell},\gamma^{r})
, then:

(1)

if
\sigma^{\ell}=\sigma^{r}
, then
{\textbf{B}}\boxplus_{\beta}{\textbf{C}}
is subdirectly irreducible if and only if
C
is subdirectly irreducible;

(2)

if
\sigma^{\ell}\neq\sigma^{r}
, then
{\textbf{B}}\boxplus_{\beta}{\textbf{C}}
is subdirectly irreducible if and only if
\mathbf{\beta Fil}(B)
has a monolith.

Let us now describe the homomorphic images (or equivalently, the quotients) of a blockwise gluing. Given a congruence filter
H
of a blockwise gluing
{\textbf{B}}\boxplus_{\beta}{\textbf{C}}
, we write
{\textbf{B}}/H
for the partial subalgebra of
({\textbf{B}}\boxplus_{\beta}{\textbf{C}})/H
with domain
B/H
.

Proposition 3.21
.

Let
H
be a congruence filter of a blockwise gluing
{\textbf{B}}\boxplus_{\beta}{\textbf{C}}
with
\beta=(\sigma^{\ell},\sigma^{r},\gamma^{\ell},\gamma^{r})
. Let
\beta_{H}=(\sigma^{\ell}_{H},\sigma^{r}_{H},\gamma^{\ell}_{H},\gamma^{r}_{H})
where
\alpha_{H}[x]=[\alpha(x)]
with
\alpha\in\{\sigma^{\ell},\sigma^{r},\gamma^{\ell},\gamma^{r}\}
.
Then:

(1)

({\textbf{B}}\boxplus_{\beta}{\textbf{C}})/H\cong{\textbf{B}}/H\boxplus_{\beta_{H}}{\textbf{C}}/H
, if
\sigma^{\ell}=\sigma^{r}
;

(2)

({\textbf{B}}\boxplus_{\beta}{\textbf{C}})/H\cong{\textbf{B}}/H
, if
\sigma^{\ell}\neq\sigma^{r}
.

Proof.

It is easy to check that
({\textbf{B}}\boxplus_{\beta}{\textbf{C}})/H
is a blockwise gluing of
{\textbf{B}}/H
and
{\textbf{C}}/H
in the sense of Definition
3.2
. Indeed:

(1)

the domain of the gluing is clearly
B/H\cup C/H
, and
B/H\cap C/H=\{[1]\}
.

(2)

the elements of
B/H-\{[1]\}
are strictly below those of
C/H
. Indeed, either
C/H=\{[1]\}
, or
B\cap H=\{1\}
. Hence, given
b\in B-\{1\}
and
c\in C
such that
[c]\neq[1]
,
c\backslash b\in B
and then
c\notin H
, which implies that
[b]\neq[c]
.

(3)

{\textbf{C}}/H
is a subalgebra of the gluing since it is closed under all operations.

(4)

Given
[x],[y]\in C/H
and
[z]\in B/H-\{[1]\}
,
[x][z]=[xz]=[yz]=[y][z]
and similarly
[z][x]=[zx]=[zy]=[z][y]
.

It then follows directly by Theorem
3.9
that
({\textbf{B}}\boxplus_{\beta}{\textbf{C}})/H\cong{\textbf{B}}/H\boxplus_{\beta_{H}}{\textbf{C}}/H
. Indeed, fixing

, we have that
\sigma^{\ell}_{{\textbf{B}}\boxplus_{\beta}{\textbf{C}}/H}([x])=[c][x]=[cx]=[\sigma^{\ell}(x)]=\sigma^{\ell}_{H}[x]
. The proof for the other operators is completely analogous. The points (1) and (2) then follow from the description of congruence filters in the gluing, Theorem
3.19
.
∎

-subreduct that is a total algebra. Moreover, let

.

Proposition 3.22
.

Let
{\textbf{B}}\boxplus_{\beta}{\textbf{C}}

and

. Then an IRL

is a subalgebra of

(1)

a subalgebra of
C
;

(2)

a (total) subalgebra of

(3)

•

•

{\textbf{B}}_{1}
is a
\beta

•

\beta_{1}

to
{\textbf{B}}_{1}
.

(4)

{\textbf{B}}_{2}\boxplus_{\beta_{2}}{\textbf{C}}_{2}
where

•

{\textbf{C}}_{2}
is a subalgebra of

•

{\textbf{B}}_{2}
is a
\beta
-subalgebra of

•

\beta_{2}
is given by the restrictions of the operators in
\beta
to
{\textbf{B}}_{2}
.

It is easy to check that all the structures in points (1)–(4) are subalgebras of
{\textbf{B}}\boxplus_{\beta}{\textbf{C}}

{\textbf{B}}\boxplus_{\beta}{\textbf{C}}

(2)

If

(3)

If

Lemma 4.8
.

Let

Proof.

We prove the claim by induction on

\displaystyle Y^{m}

\displaystyle=Y\circ_{\gamma_{N}}Y^{m-1}

\displaystyle=Y\circ_{\gamma_{N}}\;\gamma_{N}(Y\circ^{m-1}Y)

\displaystyle=\gamma_{N}(Y\circ\;\gamma_{N}(Y\circ^{m-1}Y))

\displaystyle=\gamma_{N}(\gamma_{N}(Y)\circ\;\gamma_{N}(Y\circ^{m-1}Y))

\displaystyle=\gamma_{N}(Y\circ(Y\circ^{m-1}Y))

\displaystyle=\gamma_{N}(Y\circ^{m}Y)

where the second equality holds by inductive hypothesis, the third by definition of

We are now ready to prove that the presented residuated frame construction applied to IRL chains preserves the blockwise gluing decomposition in archimedean components.
Given a class of algebras

Theorem 4.12

Since all chains in each

It is important to notice that, unlike what happens in each

not all chains are archimedean gluings, despite the latter being the generators of the variety. The key observation is that archimedeanity is not a first-order property, and this results in the fact that the class

is not closed under ultraproducts. Let us provide an example of a chain in
\mathsf{AG}
that is not an archimedean gluing.

Example 4.13
.

The variety of MV-algebras,
\mathsf{MV}
, is contained in
\mathsf{AG}
. Indeed,
\mathsf{MV}
is generated by its finite chains, which are all simple, hence, trivially, archimedean gluings. Not all MV-chains, however, are blockwise gluings of their archimedean components. An example of this is Chang’s MV-algebra, with domain

C=\{1,c,c^{2},\ldots c^{n}\ldots\neg c^{n},\ldots,\neg c^{2},\neg c,0\},

where
C^{+}=\{1,c,c^{2},\ldots c^{n}\ldots\neg c^{n}\}
is a copy of the negative cone of the lattice-ordered group of the integers
{\textbf{Z}}^{-}
, the elements in
C^{-}=\{\ldots\neg c^{n},\ldots,\neg c^{2},\neg c,0\}
all multiply to
0
, and
c^{n}\cdot\neg c^{m}=\neg(c^{m-n})
if

and
0
otherwise. Here,

and

are two different archimedean classes, and if

hence Chang’s MV-algebra is not an archimedean gluing, but it belongs to
\mathsf{AG}
(in fact, it can be obtained as an ultraproduct of the finite MV-chains).

One might then wonder whether all IRL-chains end up in the variety
\mathsf{AG}
, but this does not hold.
To show this, we first observe that all algebras in

validate all of the following formulas, for any

(

)

(x^{n}=x^{n+1}\penalty\ \&\penalty\ y\leq x^{n})\Rightarrow(xy=x^{n}y\penalty\ \&\penalty\ yx=yx^{n}),

(x^{n}=x^{n+1}\penalty\ \&\penalty\ y\leq x^{n})\Rightarrow(xy=x^{n}y)

.

\mathsf{AG}\models(\beta_{n})

Proof.

Since

Since

[a]

4.2.
Axiomatizations of subvarieties via archimedean gluings

In order to gain a better understanding of the subvariety lattice of
\mathsf{AG}
, we show how one can obtain finite axiomatizations for certain varieties arising naturally from finite archimedean gluings of simple IRL-chains. It is worth observing that not all subvarieties of
\mathsf{AG}
are finitely based. In fact, there are already non-finitely based subvarieties of basic hoops
\mathsf{BH}
(
[
2
, Example 7.2]
shows this in the
0
-bounded case, but it can be straightforwardly adapted), which is a proper subvariety of
\mathsf{AG}
. Indeed,
\mathsf{BH}
is generated for instance by the 1-sum of
\omega
copies of the
0
-free reduct of the standard MV-algebra
[
2
, Theorem 4.8]
, which can be seen as an archimedean gluing. Moreover, there are uncountably many subvarieties of
\mathsf{BH}
(see the analogous
0
-bounded case in
[
2
, Theorem 4.12]
, the same proof works for basic hoops), hence also of
\mathsf{AG}
.

The results in this section take inspiration from the axiomatizations of semilinear varieties generated by 1-sums in
[
2
,
23
]
. In contrast to these works, we here consider the diverse class of constructions given by gluings, hence we do not need the assumption of divisibility or its weakenings, and we do not assume commutativity. However, we do need to restrict to
n
-potent varieties.

We are able to prove in our context the equivalents to the main axiomatization results in
[
2
,
23
]
. In particular, we can:

(1)

define equations that characterize the varieties whose subdirectly irreducible algebras have a bounded number of components;

(2)

under certain assumptions, given varieties
\mathsf{V}_{1},\ldots,\mathsf{V}_{m}
, we can axiomatize the variety generated by archimedean gluings of the kind
{\textbf{A}}_{1}\boxplus\dots\boxplus{\textbf{A}}_{m}
for
{\textbf{A}}_{i}\in\mathsf{V}_{i}
.

Let us start by showing that it is possible to describe equationally the cardinality of the set
I
over which one decomposes in blockwise gluings of the archimedean components. Recall that by
(\mathsf{AG}_{n})_{\mathsf{c}}
we mean the class of chains in the variety
\mathsf{AG}_{n}
. Let then
{\textbf{A}}=\boxplus_{{\textbf{I}}}{\textbf{A}}_{i}
for
{\textbf{A}}\in(\mathsf{AG}_{n})_{\mathsf{c}}
. We write
a\ll b
if and only if
a
is in a lower component than
b
, i.e.

a\ll b\mbox{ if and only if }a\in A_{i}-\{1\}\mbox{ and }b\in A_{j}\mbox{ where }i<j.

The following technical lemma is the key to “talk about” the different components in the gluing.

Lemma 4.17
.

Let
A
be a chain in
\mathsf{AG}_{n}
,
{\textbf{A}}=\boxplus_{{\textbf{I}}}{\textbf{A}}_{i}
. The following holds:

(1)

(x^{n}\backslash y^{n})^{n}\backslash(x\vee y)=1
if and only if
x\gg y
or at least one between
x
and
y
is
1
;

(2)

(x^{n}\backslash y^{n})^{n}\backslash(x\vee y)=x\vee y
if and only if
x\ll y
,
x^{n}=y^{n}
, or
x=1
.

Proof.

(1) For the right to left direction, notice first that if one between
x
and
y
is
1
, then the equation trivially holds. While if
x\gg y
, since
{\textbf{A}}\in(\mathsf{AG}_{n})_{\mathsf{c}}
,
x^{n}\backslash y^{n}\in{\mathsf{arc}}(y^{n})
hence
(x^{n}\backslash y^{n})^{n}=y^{n}
, and so
(x^{n}\backslash y^{n})^{n}<(x\vee y)=x
, implying the thesis. For the left to right direction, we argue by contraposition. Suppose that none between
x
and
y
is
1
, and that
x^{n}=y^{n}
or
x\ll y
. Then,

(x^{n}\backslash y^{n})^{n}\backslash(x\vee y)=1\backslash(x\vee y)=(x\vee y)<1,

getting the thesis.

(2) For the right to left direction, if
x^{n}=y^{n}
or
x\ll y
then
x^{n}\backslash y^{n}=1
hence the identity becomes
x\lor y=x\lor y
. If
x=1
we get
1=1
.
For the left to right one, we argue by contraposition. Suppose that
x\gg y
with
x\neq 1
. Then, by point (1),

(x^{n}\backslash y^{n})^{n}\backslash(x\vee y)=1>x\vee y,

proving the thesis.
∎

The idea now is to use the above lemma to find an equation
\lambda_{k}^{n}
such that a subvariety
\mathsf{V}
of
\mathsf{AG}_{n}
satisfies it iff every chain in
\mathsf{V}
is an archimedean gluing of at most
k
components. Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
with
k>0
, we set:

(
\lambda_{k}^{n}
)

\bigwedge_{i=0}^{k-1}(x_{i+1}^{n}\backslash x_{i}^{n})^{n}\backslash(x_{i+1}\vee x_{i})\leq\bigvee_{i=0}^{k}x_{i}.

Lemma 4.18
.

Let
A
be a chain in
\mathsf{AG}_{n}
,
{\textbf{A}}=\boxplus_{{\textbf{I}}}{\textbf{A}}_{i}
.
{\textbf{A}}\models\lambda_{k}^{n}
if and only if
|I|\leq k
.

Proof.

Suppose that
|I|\leq k
, i.e., there are at most
k
components in the blockwise gluing. Thus, if one takes
k+1
elements in
A
, say
a_{0},\dots a_{k}
, there is
j\in\{0,\ldots,k\}
such that one of the following holds:

•

a_{j}=1
,

•

{\mathsf{arc}}(a_{j})={\mathsf{arc}}(a_{j+1})
, i.e.
a_{j}^{n}=a_{j+1}^{n}
,

•

a_{j+1}\ll a_{j}
.

In any case,
(a_{j+1}^{n}\backslash a_{j}^{n})^{n}\backslash(a_{j+1}\vee a_{j})=a_{j+1}\vee a_{j}
by Lemma
4.17
. Then

\bigwedge_{i=0}^{k-1}(a_{i+1}^{n}\backslash a_{i}^{n})^{n}\backslash(a_{i+1}\vee a_{i})\leq a_{j+1}\vee a_{j}\leq\bigvee_{i=0}^{k}a_{i},

meaning that
\lambda_{k}^{n}
holds.

Vice versa, suppose
|I|>k
. Then we have at least
k+1
components in the blockwise gluing. Hence, one can pick
k+1
elements different from
1
, all from distinct components, and order them from the smallest to the biggest. Precisely, let
a_{0},a_{1},\dots,a_{k}\in A
such that
a_{0}\ll a_{1}\ll\dots\ll a_{k}<1
; then by Lemma
4.17

\bigwedge_{i=0}^{k-1}(a_{i+1}^{n}\backslash a_{i}^{n})^{n}\backslash(a_{i+1}\vee a_{i})=1

while
\bigvee_{i=0}^{k}a_{i}<1
, which implies that
{\textbf{A}}\nvDash\lambda_{k}^{n}
.
∎

Let us recall that an algebra
A
is
generic
for a variety
\mathsf{V}
if it generates
\mathsf{V}
; as a consequence of the above lemma, we can get the analogous of the result
[
2
, Corollary 4.3]
for BL-algebras.

Theorem 4.19
.

Let
A
be a chain in
\mathsf{AG}_{n}
,
{\textbf{A}}=\boxplus_{{\textbf{I}}}{\textbf{A}}_{i}
. If
A
is generic, then
I
must be infinite.

Moreover, we observe that
\lambda_{1}^{k}
axiomatizes the subvariety
\mathsf{sAG}_{n}
generated by chains with only one archimedean component. Let us call algebras in
\mathsf{sAG}_{n}

semiarchimedean
. Notice that, in commutative chains, having one archimedean component is equivalent to being simple (we remind the reader that an algebra is simple if it has only two congruences). This is not the case without commutativity, since a chain may have more archimedean components but still be simple, because of closure under conjugates of the congruence filters.

We proceed to show how one can obtain finite axiomatizations for varieties generated by finite archimedean gluings, where the components belong to specific varieties of IRLs. The ideas are again inspired by the results in
[
2
,
23
]
, where the considered varieties are generated by 1-sums, and satisfy commutativity and divisibility, or a weak form of the latter. However, while we do restrict to
n
-potent varieties, we do not assume commutativity, and consider a wider class of constructions which include, but is not restricted to, 1-sums. This introduces some technical complications, and we will need to add some restrictions to the considered varieties. Let us now fix an
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
n\geq 1
.

Definition 4.20
.

Consider
\mathsf{V}_{1},\ldots,\mathsf{V}_{m}
to be subvarieties of
\mathsf{AG}_{n}
. We call
\mathsf{V}_{1},\ldots,\mathsf{V}_{m}

gluable
if, for
i=1,\ldots,m
:

(1)

\mathsf{V}_{i}
is semiarchimedean, i.e.,
\mathsf{V}_{i}\subseteq\mathsf{sAG}_{n}
;

(2)

\mathsf{V}_{i}
is axiomatizable, relatively to
\mathsf{sAG}_{n}
, by an identity in one variable
t_{i}(x)=1
, with
t_{i}
being a
(\backslash,/)
-free term.

The first condition ensures that it makes sense to take archimedean gluings where each component belongs to a specific variety, since chains of each
\mathsf{V}_{i}
will only have one archimedean component. Notice that this condition is verified, for instance, by
n
-potent varieties of MV-algebras or Wajsberg hoops. The second condition has to do with the nature of the blockwise gluing construction; recall indeed that the divisions are not necessarily preserved by the gluing. The need to restrict to 1-variable terms is purely technical (notice that all varieties of MV-algebras can be axiomatized by 1-variable terms).

Let then
\mathsf{V}_{1},\ldots,\mathsf{V}_{m}
be gluable subvarieties of
\mathsf{AG}_{n}
, we define:

(4.1)

\mathsf{V}_{1}\boxplus_{c}\dots\boxplus_{c}\mathsf{V}_{m}=\mathsf{V}(\{\boxplus_{\bf m}{\textbf{A}}_{i}:{\textbf{A}}_{i}\in(\mathsf{V}_{i})_{\mathsf{c}}\mbox{ for every }i=1,\dots,m\}).

We remind the reader from Notation
1
that we write
\boxplus_{\bf m}{\textbf{A}}_{i}
for a gluing over the ordered index set
1<\ldots<m
.
Notice that chains in
\mathsf{V}_{1}\boxplus_{c}\dots\boxplus_{c}\mathsf{V}_{m}
may have less than
m
components. In order to be able to describe equationally what happens in this situation, we need to consider subsequences of
\{1,\ldots,m\}
.
Let us then define
\mathsf{SEQ_{k}}
as the set of strictly increasing sequences of
k
natural numbers between
1
and
m
, i.e.

\mathsf{SEQ_{k}}=\{s=(s_{1},\dots,s_{k}):0<s_{1}<\dots<s_{k}\leq m\}.

Then for each
k=2,\dots m
, we consider
k
variables
x_{1},\dots x_{k}
and define two terms
p_{k}
and
q_{k}
as follows:

\displaystyle p_{k}^{n}:=\bigwedge_{i=1}^{k}(x_{i+1}^{n}\backslash x_{i}^{n})^{n}\backslash(x_{i+1}\vee x_{i})

\displaystyle q_{k}^{n}:=\bigvee_{i=1}^{k}x_{i}\vee\bigvee_{s\in\mathsf{SEQ_{k}}}\Biggl(\bigwedge_{j=1}^{k}t_{s_{j}}(x_{j})\Biggr)

Finally, we define the equations
\varepsilon_{k}
for
k=1,\dots,m
as:

(4.2)

\varepsilon_{1}^{n}(x):=\bigvee_{i=1}^{m}t_{i}(x)=1,\qquad\varepsilon_{k}^{n}:=p_{k}^{n}\leq q_{k}^{n}\;\;\mbox{ for }k\geq 2,

where we recall that
t_{i}(x)=1
is the axiomatization of
\mathsf{V}_{i}
relatively to
\mathsf{sAG}_{n}
.

While
\varepsilon_{1}
is quite self-explanatory, the following lemma provides an intuitive understanding of the identities
\varepsilon_{k}^{n}
for
k\geq 2
.

Lemma 4.21
.

Let
k\geq 2
. Then a chain
{\textbf{A}}\in\mathsf{AG}_{n}
satisfies
\varepsilon_{k}^{n}
iff, whenever one considers elements
a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}\in A
such that
a_{1}\ll\ldots\ll a_{k}<1
, there is a sequence
s=(s_{1},\dots,s_{k})\in\mathsf{SEQ_{k}}
such that
t_{s_{j}}(a_{j})=1
for
j=1,\dots,k
.

Proof.

Let
{\textbf{A}}\in\mathsf{AG}_{n}
. We first show that if one takes
a_{1},\dots,a_{k}\in A
that do not satisfy the hypothesis
a_{1}\ll\ldots\ll a_{k}<1
,
\varepsilon_{k}^{n}
holds with the assignment
x_{i}\mapsto a_{i}
.
First, if one of the
a_{i}
is equal to
1
, then
\bigvee_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}=1
, and so
p_{k}(a_{1},\dots,a_{k})\leq q_{k}(a_{1},\dots,a_{k})=1.

Moreover, if there is an
a_{i}
such that either
a_{i+1}\ll a_{i}
or
a_{i+1}^{n}=a_{i}^{n}
, then, by Lemma
4.17
,

p_{k}(a_{1},\dots,a_{k})\leq a_{i}\vee a_{i+1}\leq\bigvee_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}\leq q_{k}(a_{1},\dots,a_{k}).

Hence,
{\textbf{A}}\models\varepsilon_{k}^{n}
iff the identity holds whenever assigning the variables to elements
a_{1},\dots,a_{k}\in A
such that
a_{1}\ll a_{2}\ll\dots\ll a_{k}<1
. Notice that in this case,
p_{k}(a_{1},\dots,a_{k})=1
by Lemma
4.17
, and since none of the variables is set to
1
,
\bigvee_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}<1
. Thus,
p_{k}(a_{1},\dots,a_{k})\leq q_{k}(a_{1},\dots,a_{k})
iff
\bigvee_{s\in\mathsf{SEQ_{k}}}(\bigwedge_{j=1}^{k}t_{s_{j}}(x_{j}))=1
, which is equivalent to the existence of a sequence
s=(s_{1},\dots,s_{k})\in\mathsf{SEQ_{k}}
such that
t_{s_{j}}(a_{j})=1
for
j=1,\dots,k
. This completes the proof.
∎

In order to be able to prove the main results of this subsection, it will be important to establish that the subvariety of
\mathsf{AG}_{n}
axiomatized by
\lambda_{m}^{n}
and
\varepsilon_{k}^{n}
for
k=1,\dots,m
has the finite model property, and as such, it is generated by its finite chains. To do so, we need a technical lemma.

Lemma 4.22
.

Let
\mathsf{V}_{1},\ldots,\mathsf{V}_{m}
be gluable subvarieties of
\mathsf{AG}_{n}
. Let
A
be a chain in
\mathsf{AG}_{n}
,
{\textbf{A}}=\boxplus_{\bf m}{\textbf{A}}_{i}
, such that
{\textbf{A}}_{i}\in\mathsf{V}_{i}
for
i=1,\dots,m
. Then for any finite partial subalgebra
B
of
A
,
{\textbf{W}}_{{\textbf{A}},{\textbf{B}}}^{+}\in(\mathsf{AG}_{n})_{\rm fin}
and it satisfies
\lambda_{m}^{n}
and
\varepsilon_{k}^{n}
for
k=1,\dots,m
.

Proof.

The fact that
{\textbf{W}}_{{\textbf{A}},{\textbf{B}}}^{+}\in(\mathsf{AG}_{n})_{\rm fin}
follows from Lemma
4.9
. It is left to show that
{\textbf{W}}_{{\textbf{A}},{\textbf{B}}}^{+}\models\lambda_{m}^{n}
and
{\textbf{W}}_{{\textbf{A}},{\textbf{B}}}^{+}\models\varepsilon_{k}^{n}
for
k=1,\dots,m
. We prepare. First recall that the elements in
{\textbf{W}}_{{\textbf{A}},{\textbf{B}}}^{+}
are of the form

\{(u,b)\}^{\triangleleft}=\{x\in W:u(x)\leq_{{\textbf{A}}}b\}

which are downsets of
W
seen in
A
. It can be easily shown that they are principal downsets:

\{(u,b)\}^{\triangleleft}={\downarrow}_{{\textbf{A}}}m\cap W,\mbox{ with }m=\max\{x\in W:u(x)\leq_{{\textbf{A}}}b\}.

Indeed,
W
is the submonoid of
A
generated by
B
, so if
B=\{b_{1},\ldots,b_{q}\}
, all its elements are of the form
b_{1}^{k_{1}}\cdot\ldots\cdot b_{q}^{k_{q}}
. Hence
W
is a discretly totally ordered set whose elements are all below those of
B
, and therefore satisfies the ascending chain condition. Hence every nonempty set of its elements has a maximum, thus in particular the maximum of
\{x\in W:u(x)\leq b\}
exists; let us call such maximum
m
. The fact that
\{(u,b)\}^{\triangleleft}={\downarrow m}\cap W
then follows from the definition of
\{(u,b)\}^{\triangleleft}
.

Moreover, it can be shown that if
X,Z\in{\textbf{W}}_{{\textbf{A}},{\textbf{B}}}^{+}
where
X={\downarrow}_{{\textbf{A}}}x\cap W
and
Z={\downarrow}_{{\textbf{A}}}z\cap W
with
Z\ll X
, then
z\ll x
in
A
. Indeed if
x^{n}\leq z
in
A
, then
X\circ^{n}X\subseteq Z
. This implies that

X^{n}=\gamma_{N}(X\circ^{n}X)\subseteq\gamma_{N}(Z)=Z,

where the first equality follows from Lemma
4.8
, and the inclusion from the fact that
\gamma_{N}
is a nucleus hence order preserving. This contradicts the hypothesis, hence
Z\ll X
implies
z\ll x
.

Let us indicate with
\top
the
1
of the algebra
{\textbf{W}}_{{\textbf{A}},{\textbf{B}}}^{+}
to avoid confusion in what follows. We are ready to show that
{\textbf{W}}_{{\textbf{A}},{\textbf{B}}}^{+}\models\lambda_{m}^{n}
. Suppose by way of contradiction that
{\textbf{W}}_{{\textbf{A}},{\textbf{B}}}^{+}\nvDash\lambda_{m}^{n}
, then
{\textbf{W}}_{{\textbf{A}},{\textbf{B}}}^{+}
is blockwise gluing of at least
m+1
components. Hence, we can find a chain of
k+1
elements all belonging to different components and all different from
\top
, i.e.
W_{1}\ll\dots\ll W_{k}<\top
where
W_{i}={\downarrow}_{{\textbf{A}}}w_{i}\cap W
with
w_{i}\in W
. Thus, projecting back to
A
, we have a the sequence
w_{1}\ll w_{2}\ll\dots\ll w_{k}<1
having
k+1
elements all belonging to different components and all different from
1
which implies that
{\textbf{A}}\nvDash\lambda_{m}^{n}
, contradicting the hypothesis.

Let us now verify that
{\textbf{W}}_{{\textbf{A}},{\textbf{B}}}^{+}\models\varepsilon_{k}^{n}
with
k=1,\dots,m
. First suppose
k=1
and let
Y={\downarrow}_{\textbf{A}}y\cap W
be an element of
{\textbf{W}}_{{\textbf{A}},{\textbf{B}}}^{+}
. Projecting
Y
back to
A
, we have that
y
belongs to a certain archimedean component of
A
, say
{\textbf{A}}_{i}\in\mathsf{V}_{i}
; then
t_{i}(y)=1
. We prove that
t_{i}(y)=1
implies
t_{i}(Y)=\top
by induction on the complexity of the term
t_{i}
.

For the base case, if
t_{i}(x)=1
, then there is nothing to prove; if
t_{i}(x)=x
, then
t_{i}(y)=1
means that
y=1
and since
Y={\downarrow}_{\textbf{A}}1\cap W
,
t_{i}(Y)=\top
.

For the inductive case, suppose that
t_{i}(y)=1
implies that
t_{i}(Y)=\top
for terms of complexity
n
; we prove it for
n+1
. Recalling that the terms
t_{i}
of interest are divisions free, we need to consider the following cases:

•

If
t_{i}(x)=t_{1}(x)\star t_{2}(x)
, for
\star\in\{\cdot,\land\}
, then
t_{i}(y)=t_{1}(y)\star t_{2}(y)=1
implies that
t_{1}(y)=1
and
t_{2}(y)=1
, so, by inductive hypothesis,
t_{i}(Y)=t_{1}(Y)\star t_{2}(Y)=\top\star\top=\top
.

•

If
t_{i}(x)=t_{1}(x)\vee t_{2}(x)
, then
t_{i}(y)=t_{1}(y)\vee t_{2}(y)=1
implies that either
t_{1}(y)=1
or
t_{2}(y)=1
. Then by inductive hypothesis, either
t_{1}(Y)=1
or
t_{2}(Y)=1
, in any case
t_{i}(Y)=t_{1}(Y)\vee t_{2}(Y)=\top
.

Hence,
{\textbf{W}}_{{\textbf{A}},{\textbf{B}}}^{+}\models\varepsilon_{1}(x)
.

Let now
2\leq k\leq m
and let
W_{1},\dots,W_{k}\in{\textbf{W}}_{{\textbf{A}},{\textbf{B}}}^{+}
. By Lemma
4.21
, it suffices to check that
\varepsilon_{k}^{n}
holds for an assignment
x_{i}\mapsto W_{i}
with
W_{1}\ll\dots\ll W_{k}<\top
; precisely, it suffices to check that there is a sequence
s\in\mathsf{SEQ}_{k}
such that
\bigwedge_{j=0}^{k}t_{s_{j}}(W_{j})=\top
. Projecting back to
A
, we have a sequence
w_{1}\ll w_{2}\ll\dots\ll w_{k}<1
of
k
elements all belonging to different archimedean components and since
{\textbf{A}}\models\varepsilon_{k}^{n}
, there exists a sequence
s\in\mathsf{SEQ}_{k}
such that
t_{s_{i}}(w_{s_{i}})=1
. We have already shown above that
t(w_{i})=1
implies
t(W_{i})=\top
.
Hence, we have found a sequence
s\in\mathsf{SEQ}_{k}
such that
\bigwedge_{j=0}^{k}t_{s_{j}}(W_{j})=\top
, which implies that
{\textbf{W}}_{{\textbf{A}},{\textbf{B}}}^{+}\models\varepsilon_{k}^{n}
for
k=1,\dots,m
. This completes the proof.
∎

As a direct consequence:

Proposition 4.23
.

The subvariety of
\mathsf{AG}_{n}
axiomatized by
\lambda_{m}^{n}
and
\varepsilon_{k}^{n}
for
k=1,\dots,m
has the finite embeddability property.

We are now ready to state the main result of this subsection. The following proof is a non-trivial adaptation of the one in
[
23
]
, where the authors axiomatize varieties that satisfy a weak notion of divisibility, and which fixes some (minor) issues in the proof of the axiomatizations for subvarieties of BL-algebras in
[
2
, Theorem 5.1]
. We notice in particular that the situation in this case is complicated by the fact that we are dealing with (possibly) partial algebras. To bypass this issue, we utilize the above Proposition
4.23
.

Theorem 4.24
.

Let
\mathsf{V}_{1},\dots,\mathsf{V}_{m}
be gluable subvarieties of
\mathsf{AG}_{n}
. Then,
\mathsf{V}_{1}\boxplus_{c}\dots\boxplus_{c}\mathsf{V}_{m}
is axiomatized, relatively to
\mathsf{AG}_{n}
, by
\lambda_{m}^{n}
and
\varepsilon_{k}^{n}
for
k=1,\dots,m
.

Proof.

First observe that, by Lemma
4.18
,
\lambda_{m}
is a necessary condition to belong to the variety
\mathsf{V}_{1}\boxplus_{c}\dots\boxplus_{c}\mathsf{V}_{m}
. Note that then, one only needs to check that for every algebra
{\textbf{A}}\in\mathsf{AG}_{n}
such that
{\textbf{A}}\models\lambda_{m}
,

A
belongs to
\mathsf{V}_{1}\boxplus_{c}\dots\boxplus_{c}\mathsf{V}_{m}
if and only if
{\textbf{A}}\models\varepsilon_{k}^{n}
for all
k=1\ldots m
.

Observe that it is sufficient to check the left-to-right direction on the generators of the variety. Let
A
be a generator, then it is of the form
{\textbf{A}}=\boxplus_{\bf m}{\textbf{A}}_{i}
, where each
{\textbf{A}}_{i}
is a chain in
\mathsf{V}_{i}
for
i=1,\dots,m
.

Since each
\mathsf{V}_{i}\models\lambda_{1}
, each
{\textbf{A}}_{i}
only has one archimedean component. Moreover, since each
{\textbf{A}}_{i}\in\mathsf{V}_{i}
, it is clear that
\varepsilon_{1}^{n}
holds.
Let now
2\leq k\leq m
. By Lemma
4.21
, it suffices to consider
a_{1}\ll a_{2}\ll\dots\ll a_{k}<1
, and show that there is a sequence of
k
increasing natural numbers
s=(s_{1},\dots,s_{k})\in\mathsf{SEQ_{K}}
such that
t_{s_{j}}(a_{j})=1
for
j=1,\dots,k
. This clearly holds, again due to the fact each
{\textbf{A}}_{i}\in\mathsf{V}_{i}
. We have shown that
{\textbf{A}}\models\varepsilon_{k}^{n}
for
k=1,\dots,m
, for each generator
A
. Hence

\mathsf{V}_{1}\boxplus_{c}\dots\boxplus_{c}\mathsf{V}_{m}\models\varepsilon_{k}^{n}
, for
k=1.\dots,m
.

For the right-to-left direction, let us call
\mathsf{K}
the subvariety of
\mathsf{AG}_{n}
axiomatized by
\lambda_{m}^{n}
and
\varepsilon_{k}^{n}
for
k=1\ldots m
. It suffices to check that a set of generators for
\mathsf{K}
belong to
\mathsf{V}_{1}\boxplus_{c}\dots\boxplus_{c}\mathsf{V}_{m}
. Since
\mathsf{K}
has the finite embeddability property (Proposition
4.23
), and therefore the finite model property, one only needs to check that if
B
is a finite chain belonging to
\mathsf{K}
, then
{\textbf{B}}\in\mathsf{V}_{1}\boxplus_{c}\dots\boxplus_{c}\mathsf{V}_{m}
. We prove this by contrapositive.

Suppose
{\textbf{B}}\in\mathsf{AG}_{n}
is a finite chain such that
{\textbf{B}}\models\lambda_{m}
but
{\textbf{B}}\not\in\mathsf{V}_{1}\boxplus_{c}\dots\boxplus_{c}\mathsf{V}_{m}
. The goal is to show that
{\textbf{B}}\nvDash\varepsilon_{k}^{n}
for some
k\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
. Say that
{\textbf{B}}=\boxplus_{\bf k}{\textbf{B}}_{i}
, for some
k\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
, since
{\textbf{B}}\models\lambda_{m}
. The idea is to show that for any
i=1,\dots,k
, one can pick a
b_{i}\in{\textbf{B}}_{i}
such that if each
x_{i}
is assigned to
b_{i}
,
\varepsilon_{k}^{n}
is falsified by this assignment.

Since
B
is finite, then every
{\textbf{B}}_{i}
, for
i=1,\dots,k
, is also finite. Now, each
{\textbf{B}}_{i}
seen as a subreduct of
B
may be a partial algebra, but each
B_{i}
is in fact the domain of a residuated lattice whose operations extend those of
{\textbf{B}}_{i}
. Indeed, given that
{\textbf{B}}_{i}
is a finite chain whose product is order preserving, one can define the missing divisions in the obvious way:

x\backslash y:=\max\{z\in B_{i}:x\cdot z\leq y\},\;\;y/x:=\max\{z\in B_{i}:z\cdot x\leq y\}.

Let us denote with
{\textbf{B}}_{i}^{+}
this (total) residuated lattice associated to
{\textbf{B}}_{i}
over the same domain
B_{i}
.
Consider the family of blocks
\{({\textbf{B}}_{i},\beta_{ij})\}_{i<_{\bf k}j}
associated to
B
provided by Proposition
3.13
. Then, the same operators yield a family of blocks with the new total algebras over the same domains,
\{({\textbf{B}}^{+}_{i},\beta_{ij})\}_{i<_{\bf k}j}
, and it is straighforward that
{\textbf{B}}=\boxplus_{\bf k}{\textbf{B}}_{i}^{+}
. In other words, we can consider
B
as the blockwise gluing of the total algebras
{\textbf{B}}_{1}^{+},\dots,{\textbf{B}}_{k}^{+}
.

We are now ready to pick the elements
b_{i}
so that the assignment
x_{i}\mapsto b_{i}
makes
\varepsilon_{k}^{n}
fail. First, for each
i=1,\dots,k
, let

N_{i}=\{\mathsf{V}_{j}:{\textbf{B}}^{+}_{i}\not\in\mathsf{V}_{j}\mbox{ for some }j=1,\dots,m\}.

Observe that it is possible that some of the
N_{i}
are empty, but surely not all of them since
{\textbf{B}}\not\in\mathsf{V}_{1}\boxplus_{c}\dots\boxplus_{c}\mathsf{V}_{m}
. In case
N_{i}=\emptyset
, than one can choose whatever
b_{i}\in B_{i}
different from
1
. Otherwise, consider the join of the varieties in
N_{i}
,
\mathsf{N}_{i}
in what follows, and define

n_{i}(x)=\bigvee\{t_{j}(x):\mathsf{V}_{j}\in N_{i}\}

where
t_{j}(x)
is the term axiomatizing
\mathsf{V}_{j}
.
\mathsf{N}_{i}
is the variety axiomatized by
n_{i}(x)=1

[
18
, Corollary 4.3]
. Note that
{\textbf{B}}^{+}_{i}\not\in\mathsf{N}_{i}
. Indeed since
{\textbf{B}}^{+}_{i}
is subdirectly irreducible, as a consequence of Jónsson Lemma, if it belongs to the join of finitely many varieties then it should belong to one of them; but since we know that
{\textbf{B}}_{i}
does not belong to any of the
\mathsf{V}_{j}\in N_{i}
, we get that
{\textbf{B}}_{i}\not\in\mathsf{N}_{i}
. Thus there exists a
b_{i}\neq 1\in{\textbf{B}}^{+}_{i}
such that
n_{i}(b_{i})\neq 1
, and so such that
t_{j}(b_{i})\neq 1
for all
j
such that
{\textbf{B}}_{i}^{+}\notin\mathsf{V}_{j}
; let us choose exactly this
b_{i}
.

We claim that
\varepsilon_{k}^{n}
fails with the assignment mapping each
x_{i}
to
b_{i}
. Notice first that if
k=1
,
N_{1}
necessarily contains all
\mathsf{V}_{j}
for
j=1\ldots m
, which means that
\varepsilon_{1}(b_{1})=n_{1}(b_{1})\neq 1
.

Let now
k\geq 2
. Observe that
b_{1}\ll\dots\ll b_{k}<1
, thus, by Lemma
4.21
, it suffices to show that
\bigvee_{s\in\mathsf{SEQ_{k}}}\Bigl(\bigwedge_{j=1}^{k}t_{s_{j}}(b_{j})\Bigl)\neq 1
. Since
{\textbf{B}}\not\in\mathsf{V}_{1}\boxplus_{c}\dots\boxplus_{c}\mathsf{V}_{m}
, for any sequence
s=(s_{1},\dots,s_{k})\in\mathsf{SEQ_{k}}
there is an
s_{i}
such that
{\textbf{B}}_{i}^{+}\not\in\mathsf{V}_{s_{i}}
, and so
t_{s_{i}}(b_{i})\neq 1
. This implies that also
\bigvee_{s\in\mathsf{SEQ_{k}}}\Bigl(\bigwedge_{j=1}^{k}t_{s_{j}}(b_{j})\Bigl)\neq 1
, hence
\varepsilon_{k}^{n}
fails with the chosen assignment.
This completes the proof.
∎

Remark 4.25
.

We observe that one could rephrase the same results if all the gluable varieties
\mathsf{V}_{1},\ldots,\mathsf{V}_{m}
belong to a common subvariety
\mathsf{W}
of
\mathsf{AG}_{n}
; then the requirements of Definition
4.20
for
\mathsf{V}_{1},\ldots,\mathsf{V}_{m}
have to be satisfied relatively to
\mathsf{W}
. So for instance, the same results hold if one consider commutative varieties. We did not phrase the results in this more general case to avoid making the statements and definitions harder to read.

4.3.
The
0
-bounded case

All the results contained in this section can be adapted to the
0
-bounded case. Notice that here, if
A
is a chain in
\mathsf{F}\mathsf{L}_{w}
and it is a blockwise gluing
{\textbf{A}}=\boxplus_{\bf I}{\textbf{A}}_{i}
, the index set
\bf I
has a minimum
i_{0}
in the order, and
0\in A_{i_{0}}
. We call such a gluing
0
-bounded
. With this in mind, we can obtain all the same results as in Subsection
4.1
. In particular, let
\mathsf{AG}_{n}^{0}
be the subvariety of
\mathsf{SemFL}_{w}
axiomatized by (block
n
) for
n\geq 1
, and
\mathsf{C}\mathsf{AG}_{n}^{0}
its commutative subvariety. Then:

Corollary 4.26
.

(\mathsf{C})\mathsf{AG}_{n}^{0}
is the variety generated by
0
-bounded archimedean gluings of
n
-potent (commutative) chains, and it has the finite embeddability property, for all
n\geq 1
.
The join
\bigvee_{n\in\mathbb{N}}(\mathsf{C})\mathsf{AG}_{n}^{0}
is generated by all
0
-bounded archimedean gluings of (commutative) chains, and it is strictly contained in
\mathsf{SemFL}_{w}
(
\mathsf{MTL}
).

The results in Subsection
4.2
can also be adapted to the
0
-bounded case, but some proofs are required.
In particular, another important difference with respect to 1-sums becomes apparent here. In
0
-bounded 1-sums, the lowest component can be easily identified by the fact that it contains all and only the elements such that
\neg\neg x=x
(where we mean
\neg x:=x\backslash 0
). This is not the case for blockwise gluings. Nonetheless, we show that one can still identify equationally the lowest component in archimedean gluings. Let us fix an
n\in\mathbb{N}
. First, notice that:

Proposition 4.27
.

Let
A
be a chain in
\mathsf{AG}_{n}^{0}
,
{\textbf{A}}=\boxplus_{\bf I}{\textbf{A}}_{i}
; then
{\textbf{A}}\models\lambda_{k}^{n}
if and only if
|I|\leq k
. Moreover, if
A
is generic for
\mathsf{AG}_{n}^{0}
,
I
must be infinite.

Let us then call
\mathsf{sAG}_{n}^{0}
the subvariety of
\mathsf{AG}^{0}_{n}
axiomatized by
\lambda_{1}
, whose chains only have one archimedean component.

Lemma 4.28
.

Let
\mathsf{V}
be a subvariety of
\mathsf{AG}_{n}^{0}
generated by a class of
0
-bounded blockwise gluings whose lowest component belongs to a variety
\mathsf{W}\subseteq\mathsf{sAG}_{n}^{0}
, and suppose that
\mathsf{W}
is axiomatized, relatively to
\mathsf{sAG}_{n}^{0}
, by an equation in one variable
t_{0}(x)=1
. Then
\mathsf{V}\models(\neg x^{n})^{n}\backslash t_{0}(x)=1
.

Proof.

It suffices to show that the generators of
\mathsf{V}
satisfy the desired identity. Let then
A
be a
0
-bounded blockwise gluing,
{\textbf{A}}=\boxplus_{\bf I}{\textbf{A}}_{i}
, where the lowest component
{\textbf{A}}_{i_{0}}
belongs to
\mathsf{W}
. Since
\mathsf{V}\subseteq\mathsf{AG}_{n}^{0}
, recall that the
A_{i}
s are the archimedean components of
A
.

Let then
x\in A
. If
x\in A_{j}
with
j\neq i_{0}
, we have that
0\ll x
. Then also

5.
Amalgamation failures

In this final section we use blockwise gluings to construct some chains which provide a counterexample to the amalgamation property in several important varieties of residuated lattices. We solve in the negative several open problems in the literature; in particular, we show that the following varieties do not have the amalgamation property:
\mathsf{MTL}
,
\mathsf{SemFL}_{w}
, and their 0-free subreducts,
\mathsf{Sem(C)IRL}

Remark 5.13
.

We observe that one can obtain other failures of the AP by applying appropriate constructions to the algebras in
\mathcal{VS}
. For instance, the lifting and disconnected rotation constructions can be seen uniformly as
generalized
n
-rotations
. This more general construction was introduced in
[
9
]
, extending the
generalized rotation
of
[
4
]
, and later adapted to the non-commutative case in
[
22
]
. Intuitively, generalized
n
-rotations are obtained by taking an IRL
A
, attaching below it a rotated (possibly proper)
nuclear image
of
A
, and then adding a Łukasiewicz chain of
n
elements,
n-2
of which are between the original structure and its rotated nuclear image. This construction has as another particular case also the
connected rotation
construction. As above, one can obtain essentially the same proof as in Lemma
5.3
, and derive that the AP fails in all varieties generated by generalized
n
-rotations of (C)IRLs, for all
n\geq 2
.

To conclude, let us rephrase some of our results in terms of interpolation properties. Since, in the presence of the CEP, the amalgamation of a variety of residuated lattices
\mathsf{V}
corresponds to the deductive interpolation property of the corresponding logic
\mathcal{L}_{\mathsf{V}}
, we obtain the following consequences.

Corollary 5.14
.

Let
\mathsf{V}
be a semilinear variety of residuated lattices or FL-algebras with the CEP, such that the algebras (over the appropriate signature) in one among
\mathcal{VS},\mathcal{VS}_{0}
,
\mathcal{VS}_{\ell}
,
\mathcal{VS}_{\delta}
, belong to
\mathsf{V}
. Then the corresponding logic
\mathcal{L}_{\mathsf{V}}
does not have the deductive interpolation property.

To highlight the most relevant novel results:

Corollary 5.15
.

The following logics do not have the deductive interpolation property: MTL, IMTL, SMTL, and their
n
-potent extensions.
