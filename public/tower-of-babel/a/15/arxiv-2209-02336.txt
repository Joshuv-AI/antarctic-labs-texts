Emergent centrality in rank-based supplanting process
Kenji Shimomura 1 , Yasuhiro Ishitsuka 2 , and Hiroki Ohta 3

Abstract
We propose a stochastic process of interacting many agents, which is inspired by rank-based supplanting dynamics commonly observed in a group of Japanese macaques. In order to characterize the breaking of permutation symmetry with respect to agents’ rank in the stochastic process, we introduce a rank-dependent quantity, overlap centrality , which quantifies how often a given agent overlaps with the other agents. We give a sufficient condition in a wide class of the models such that overlap centrality shows perfect correlation in terms of the agents’ rank in zero-supplanting limit. We also discuss a singularity of the correlation in the case of interaction induced by a Potts energy.

One of promising candidates for going a step further in studying a many-body system is to construct a lattice model of interacting elements or agents which describes a many-body system.
This strategy is not only applied to equilibrium systems

Depending on the purpose of network analysis,

As an example of inhomogeneous agents,

In this paper, we focus on one of the intersections between lattice models and the network theory from the viewpoint of inhomogeneous agents. We propose a new type of nonequilibrium lattice model, which

The main objective of this paper is to show that a new type of macroscopic correlation appears when supplanting process, which is a rank-based interaction with broken detailed balance, is added to an equilibrium system. It turns out that this problem can be mapped

II.1
Equilibrium dynamics

We consider a general class of energy function

E(\bm{x})=E(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{N})

which is permutation symmetric in the following sense:

E(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{N})=E(x_{\sigma(1)},x_{\sigma(2)},\dots,x_{\sigma(N)}),

(1)

for any permutation
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{N}
of
N
elements, where
\mathfrak{S}_{N}
is the symmetric group of order
N
.
In addition, let
\beta
be a parameter determining the magnitude of the energy including the sign
\pm
.

As an example of the models belonging to the above class, one can consider the following
L
-state Potts energy
E({\bm{x}})
on the complete graph
where each agent connects with all agents
Potts
:

\displaystyle E({\bm{x}})=-\frac{2(L-1)\log(L-1)}{L-2}\dfrac{1}{2N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\delta(x_{i},x_{j}).

(2)

This case means that an agent interacts with the other agents only if they have overlaps. In this sense, this Potts model on the complete graph is equivalent to the agents with an on-site interaction in one dimension, which may be a simple model to describe interacting agents. The coefficient of (
2
) is adjusted so that the phase transition point
\beta_{c}
in the equilibrium state is equal to 1, which we will discuss in more detail in Section
III.1
.

Let us consider a Markov process with discrete time
t
, where during one time step between
t
and
t+1
, only one of the following possible transitions may occur. The transition probability
T_{0}({\bm{x}}\to f_{i}^{\pm}{\bm{x}})
from each configuration
{\bm{x}}
to the configuration
f_{i}^{\pm}{\bm{x}}
for any agent
i
is

\displaystyle T_{0}({\bm{x}}\to f_{i}^{\pm}{\bm{x}})=\dfrac{1}{2N}\dfrac{1}{1+\exp\left(\beta\mathcal{D}_{i}^{\pm}E({\bm{x}})\right)},

(3)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{i}^{\pm}E({\bm{x}}):=E(f_{i}^{\pm}{\bm{x}})-E({\bm{x}}).

(4)

This leads to that the joint probability
P_{t}({\bm{x}})
of configuration
{\bm{x}}
at time
t

satisfies the following master equation:

\displaystyle P_{t+1}({\bm{x}})=

\displaystyle\sum_{{\bm{x}}^{\prime}\neq{\bm{x}}}P_{t}({\bm{x}}^{\prime})T_{0}({\bm{x}}^{\prime}\to{\bm{x}})

\displaystyle+P_{t}({\bm{x}})\Big(1-\sum_{{\bm{x}}^{\prime}\neq{\bm{x}}}T_{0}({\bm{x}}\to{\bm{x}}^{\prime})\Big),

(5)

where the summation over
{\bm{x}^{\prime}}
is done
for all of the possible configurations such that
T_{0}({\bm{x}}\to{\bm{x}}^{\prime})
and
T_{0}({\bm{x}}^{\prime}\to{\bm{x}})
are defined above.

The Gibbs distribution

\displaystyle P_{\mathrm{can}}({\bm{x}}):=\dfrac{1}{Z_{N}(\beta)}\exp\left(-\beta E({\bm{x}})\right),

(6)

where
Z_{N}(\beta):=\sum_{{\bm{x}}}\exp(-\beta E({\bm{x}}))
,
is the stationary solution
P_{\mathrm{st}}({\bm{x}})
of the master equation (
5
), satisfying

\displaystyle\sum_{{\bm{x}}^{\prime}}P_{\mathrm{st}}({\bm{x}}^{\prime})T_{0}({\bm{x}}^{\prime}\to{\bm{x}})=P_{\mathrm{st}}({\bm{x}})\sum_{{\bm{x}}^{\prime}\neq{\bm{x}}}T_{0}({\bm{x}}\to{\bm{x}}^{\prime}),

(7)

because the Gibbs distribution satisfies the detailed balance condition:

\displaystyle P_{\mathrm{can}}({\bm{x}})T_{0}({\bm{x}}\to{\bm{x}^{\prime}})=P_{\mathrm{can}}({\bm{x}^{\prime}})T_{0}({\bm{x}^{\prime}}\to{\bm{x}}),

(8)

for any pair
{\bm{x}},{\bm{x}^{\prime}}
realized by the above dynamics.

II.2
Broken detailed balance by supplanting

Next, we consider to add a supplanting process to the equilibrium dynamics introduced above, which breaks the detailed balance condition.

Let us imagine a process in which an agent
i
hops to a position
y_{i}=x_{i}\pm 1
from position
x_{i}
in accordance with the equilibrium transition probability
T_{0}
, and then an agent at
y_{i}
, which has a rank
j
such that
i<j
, is stochastically forced to hop to the position
y_{i}+1
for
d=+
, or
y_{i}-1
for
d=-
.
In this process, an agent with a higher rank
i
supplants another agent with a lower rank
j
. This is why we call such a process the
supplanting process
. Note that the configuration
\bm{x}
turns to be the configuration
f_{j}^{d}f_{i}^{\pm}\bm{x}
through the whole supplanting process.
See Fig.
1
for a graphical reference of the process.
For convenience, let us introduce the following set

S(\bm{x},i,\pm)\coloneqq\{i<j\leq N\mid x_{j}=x_{i}\pm 1\},

(9)

which is a set of every agent whose rank is lower than
i
at position
x_{i}\pm 1
. That is, those agents could be supplanted by the agent
i
when the agent
i
hops to position
x_{i}\pm 1
.

Suppose that every agent in
S(\bm{x},i,\pm)
has the same chance to be chosen as the supplanted one, and that the direction
d
of hopping by supplanting is determined with equal probability
1/2
.
Explicitly, we define the transition probability from
{\bm{x}}
to
f_{j}^{d}f_{i}^{\pm}{\bm{x}}
for
d\in\{+,-\}
and
j\in S(\bm{x},i,\pm)
:

\displaystyle T({\bm{x}}\to f_{j}^{d}f_{i}^{\pm}{\bm{x}})=\dfrac{p}{2}\frac{1}{1+p\#S(\bm{x},i,\pm)}T_{0}({\bm{x}}\to f_{i}^{\pm}{\bm{x}}),

(10)

where
p\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\coloneqq[0,\infty)
is the parameter of supplanting rate and
\#S
is the number of all the elements of a set
S
.
When
p\to 0
, supplanting rarely occurs, and when
p\to\infty
, supplanting almost always occurs.
Note that at most one agent is supplanted in a single transition regardless of the value of
p
.

On the other hand, for the case of
j\notin S(\bm{x},i,\pm)
, it holds that

\displaystyle T({\bm{x}}\to f_{j}^{d}f_{i}^{\pm}{\bm{x}})=0.

(11)

Further, the probability of transition
T({\bm{x}}\to f_{i}^{\pm}{\bm{x}})
is modified from
T_{0}
as

\displaystyle T({\bm{x}}\to f_{i}^{\pm}{\bm{x}})=\frac{1}{1+p\#S(\bm{x},i,\pm)}T_{0}({\bm{x}}\to f_{i}^{\pm}{\bm{x}}).

(12)

Totally, the following holds:

\displaystyle T_{0}({\bm{x}}\to f_{i}^{\pm}{\bm{x}})

\displaystyle=T({\bm{x}}\to f_{i}^{\pm}{\bm{x}})+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\in S(\bm{x},i,\pm)\\
d=\pm\end{subarray}}T({\bm{x}}\to f_{j}^{d}f_{i}^{\pm}{\bm{x}}).

(13)

Then, the master equation for the joint probability
P_{t}({\bm{x}})

governing the above stochastic process is as follows:

\displaystyle P_{t+1}({\bm{x}})=

\displaystyle\sum_{{\bm{x}}^{\prime}}P_{t}({\bm{x}}^{\prime})T({\bm{x}}^{\prime}\to{\bm{x}})

\displaystyle+P_{t}({\bm{x}})(1-\sum_{{\bm{x}}^{\prime}\neq{\bm{x}}}T({\bm{x}}\to{\bm{x}}^{\prime})),

(14)

where the summation over
{\bm{x}^{\prime}}
is done
for all of the possible configurations such that
T({\bm{x}}^{\prime}\to{\bm{x}})
and

T({\bm{x}}\to{\bm{x}}^{\prime})
are defined.
Since
T(\bm{x}\to f_{i}^{\pm}\bm{x})
is positive for any
\bm{x}
and
i
with finite
\beta,p,N,L
, and
E
, any state can reach any state in this stochastic process;
that is, the stochastic process defined above is an irreducible Markov process.

In the presence of
p>0
, the supplanting process does not hold the detailed balance condition because of the asymmetric property in terms of agents rank; when a supplanting occurs in one step, i.e., an agent supplants another agent, the reverse process never occurs in any single step.
Thus, obviously the stationary solution
P_{\mathrm{st}}({\bm{x}})
is no longer the Gibbs distribution of a given energy function.
Note that in the limit of
p\to 0
,
the detailed balance condition in terms of a
given energy function is recovered.
Thus, we can also regard
p
as the strength of violation of detailed balance condition.

III
The case of the Potts energy

In this section, we focus on the
case of the Potts energy defined by (
2
).
We briefly review the known equilibrium properties and compute nonequilibrium stationary distribution by exact diagonalization of transition matrix corresponding to the master equation (
II.2
). Further, we introduce

with agents’ rank, which are calculated using the computed stationary distribution.

III.1
Computation of partition function at equilibrium

As a preliminary, we consider the equilibrium case with
p=0
.
The equilibrium ferromagnetic Potts model on the complete graph has two phases; one is the ordered phase for stronger interaction, and another is the disordered phase for weaker interaction, which are separated by a first-order transition point if
L\geq 3

Potts
.
In the context of this paper,
the ordered state can be regarded as the condensate state of agents, that is, the state where all agents are located at the same position.

Let us look into more detail for the computation of the above results. When
p=0
and interaction strength
\beta
is positive (
\beta>0
), corresponding to the case of attractive interaction,
the partition function
Z_{N}(\beta):=\sum_{{\bm{x}}}\exp\big({-}\beta E({\bm{x}})\big)
for
N\to\infty
can be explicitly expressed, by which the equilibrium transition point is computed exactly.
Concretely, by performing the Stratonovich–Hubbard transformation
mean-field_Potts2013
;
mean-field_Potts2020
with

\displaystyle\exp\left[{\frac{K\beta}{2N}\sum_{x\in X}\left({\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta(x_{i},x)}\right)^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle=\prod_{x\in X}\sqrt{\frac{NK\beta}{2\pi}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{d}q\exp\left[{-\frac{NK\beta}{2}q^{2}+K\beta q\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta(x_{i},x)}\right],

(15)

we obtain

\displaystyle Z_{N}(\beta)=\left({\frac{NK\beta}{2\pi}}\right)^{L/2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{L}}\mathrm{d}^{L}q\exp\left(-N\beta f_{\beta}({\bm{q}})\right),

(16)

\displaystyle f_{\beta}({\bm{q}})=\frac{K}{2}\sum_{x\in X}q_{x}^{2}-\beta^{-1}\log\left({\sum_{x\in X}\exp\left({K\beta q_{x}}\right)}\right),

(17)

where

\displaystyle K=\frac{2(L-1)\log(L-1)}{L-2}.

(18)

For
N\gg 1
, the minimal value of
f_{\beta}(\bm{q})
as a function of the order parameter
\bm{q}
behaves effectively as the free energy density of the Potts model as follows:

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{-\beta^{-1}\log Z_{N}(\beta)}{N}=\min_{\bm{q}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{L}}f_{\beta}(\bm{q}).

(19)

Taking

\frac{\partial f_{\beta}}{\partial q_{x}}(\bm{q})=0
to minimize
f_{\beta}(\bm{q})
,
we obtain the stationary condition

\displaystyle q_{y}\exp(-K\beta q_{y})=\Big(\sum_{x\in X}\exp(K\beta q_{x})\Big)^{-1}

(20)

for each
y\in X
.
From (
20
), we see that

\displaystyle q_{x}\exp(-K\beta q_{x})=q_{y}\exp(-K\beta q_{y}),

(21)

for any
x,y\in X
.
This indicates that there is a constant
c
such that
q_{x}\exp(-K\beta q_{x})=c
,
which has at most two real solutions
a,b
as an equation for
q_{x}
.
Then, we have
q_{x}\in\{a,b\}
for each
x\in X
.
From (
20
), we also have

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in X}q_{x}=1.

(22)

Thus, a necessary condition for the order parameter
\bm{q}
to minimize
f_{\beta}(\bm{q})
is described below. Keeping with (
21
) and (
22
), one of the following conditions (i) and (ii) is satisfied:

(i)

It holds that
\bm{q}=\tilde{\bm{q}}^{(0)}\coloneqq\dfrac{1}{L}(1,1,\dots,1).

(ii)

There exist an integer
n\in\left\{{1,\dots,L-1}\right\}
and two distinct real numbers
a_{n}=a_{n}(\beta),b_{n}=b_{n}(\beta)
satisfying

\displaystyle a_{n}\exp(-K\beta a_{n})=b_{n}\exp(-K\beta b_{n}),

(23)

\displaystyle na_{n}+(L-n)b_{n}=1.

(24)

Moreover,
n
components of
\bm{q}
are
a_{n}
and remaining
(L-n)
components of
\bm{q}
are
b_{n}
.

For example, if
n=1
, the solutions are described by
\bm{q}=\tilde{\bm{q}}^{(\alpha)}
for
1\leq\alpha\leq L
, where

\displaystyle\tilde{q}^{(\alpha)}_{x}\coloneqq\begin{cases}a_{1}(\beta)&(\text{if $x\equiv\alpha\mod L$})\\
b_{1}(\beta)&(\text{if $x\not\equiv\alpha\mod L$})\end{cases}.

(25)

In Ref.
Ellis
, it has been shown that the set of the global minimum points
\bm{q}
of
f_{\beta}(\bm{q})
corresponds to the case of
\bm{q}=\tilde{\bm{q}}^{(0)}
or
n=1
, depending on
\beta
. Concretely, the set is described as

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\left\{{\tilde{\bm{q}}^{(1)}(\beta),\tilde{\bm{q}}^{(2)}(\beta),\ldots,\tilde{\bm{q}}^{(L)}(\beta)}\right\}&(\text{if}\ 0<\beta<1)\\
\left\{{\tilde{\bm{q}}^{(0)},\tilde{\bm{q}}^{(1)}(1),\ldots,\tilde{\bm{q}}^{(L)}(1)}\right\}&(\text{if}\ \beta=1)\\
\left\{{\tilde{\bm{q}}^{(0)}}\right\}&(\text{if}\ \beta>1).\\
\end{cases}

(26)

Note that the equations (
23
) and (
24
) with

determine the value

uniquely, and the resulting functions

are differentiable in the region

.

The expectation value of energy density is also expressed as

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{\braket{E}_{\text{can}}}{N}=\begin{cases}\frac{\partial}{\partial\beta}\beta f_{\beta}(\tilde{\bm{q}}^{(1)}(\beta))&(\text{if}\ 0<\beta<1)\\
\frac{\partial}{\partial\beta}\beta f_{\beta}(\tilde{\bm{q}}^{(0)})&(\text{if}\ \beta>1).\end{cases}

(27)

Thus, one can show that the energy density (

) exhibits a discontinuous jump at
\beta=1
, which is the phase transition point of the Potts model.

III.2
State vector description

Let us move onto the model with general
p\geq 0
. In this case, we need to explicitly consider the dynamics
in order to compute the stationary distribution of the model.
We would like to describe
the stochastic process by transition matrices with some basic linear operators.
For more detailed description and derivation,
see Appendix
A.2
.

Let
H_{X}
be the one-agent state space,
which is of dimension
L
.
It is considered as a complex vector space with inner product
\braket{\cdot|\cdot}
,
and has an orthonormal basis
\{|x\rangle\mid x\in X\}
over
the set of complex numbers
\mathbb{C}
.
Then the
N
-times self-tensored space
H_{X}^{\otimes N}
can be
identified to the
N
-agent state spaces,
which is of dimension
L^{N}
.
For a configuration of agents
\bm{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{N})\in X^{N}
,
the corresponding state vector is
|\bm{x}\rangle=|x_{1}\rangle\otimes|x_{2}\rangle\otimes\dots\otimes|x_{N}\rangle
.
The space
H_{X}^{\otimes N}
has a natural inner product induced by

, and the set of the state vectors

is an orthonormal basis. We use the same symbol

to write the inner product on

We associate the probability

for agents’ configuration
\bm{x}
with a state
\ket{P}\in H_{X}^{\otimes N}
as follows:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\braket{\bm{x}|P}=P(\bm{x}),\end{split}

(28)

or

\displaystyle\begin{split}\ket{P}=\sum_{\bm{x}\in X^{N}}P(\bm{x})\ket{\bm{x}}.\end{split}

(29)

For a given state
\ket{P_{t}}
at time

, the time evolution of the state is described as follows:

\displaystyle\ket{P_{t+1}}=\widehat{T}\ket{P_{t}},

(30)

where
\widehat{T}
is a transition matrix on
H_{X}^{\otimes N}
such that (
30
)
is equivalent to the master equation (
II.2
) for the joint probability.
Similarly,
\widehat{T}_{0}
is the transition matrix

when

.

We introduce some basic operators.
A hopping map

to the right (resp. the left) corresponds to
the operator defined by
\widehat{\Delta}_{i}^{\pm}
;
for
\bm{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{N})\in X^{N}
,

\displaystyle\widehat{\Delta}_{i}^{+}|\bm{x}\rangle

\displaystyle=|f_{i}^{+}\bm{x}\rangle

\displaystyle=\ket{x_{1}}\otimes\ket{x_{2}}\otimes\dots\otimes\ket{x_{i}+1}\otimes\dots\otimes\ket{x_{N}},

(31)

\displaystyle\widehat{\Delta}_{i}^{-}|\bm{x}\rangle

\displaystyle=|f_{i}^{-}\bm{x}\rangle

\displaystyle=\ket{x_{1}}\otimes\ket{x_{2}}\otimes\dots\otimes\ket{x_{i}-1}\otimes\dots\otimes\ket{x_{N}}.

(32)

Next we define projection operators.
For a site
y\in X
and an agent
1\leq i\leq N
,
we define
\widehat{\Xi}_{i}^{y}
by

\widehat{\Xi}_{i}^{y}|\bm{x}\rangle=\begin{cases}|\bm{x}\rangle&\text{(if $x_{i}=y$)}\\
0&\text{(if $x_{i}\neq y$)},\end{cases}

(33)

for
\bm{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{N})\in X^{N}
.
Then for a configuration
\bm{y}\in X^{N}
,
we define the operator
\widehat{\Xi}^{\bm{y}}
as

\prod_{1\leq i\leq N}\widehat{\Xi}_{i}^{y_{i}}
.
Finally, we denote
\mathop{\widehat{\mathrm{id}}}\nolimits_{H}
(resp.
\mathop{\widehat{\mathrm{id}}}\nolimits_{H}^{\otimes N}
) as
the identity operator on
H_{X}
(resp.
H_{X}^{\otimes N}
).

Using these notions, we can describe the transition matrices

\widehat{T}_{0}
and
\widehat{T}
. First,
\widehat{T}_{0}
is

\displaystyle\widehat{T}_{0}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq i\leq N\\
d=\pm\end{subarray}}\sum_{\bm{x}\in X^{N}}\Big(T_{0}(\bm{x}\to f_{i}^{d}\bm{x})\widehat{\Delta}_{i}^{d}

\displaystyle\qquad+\left(\frac{1}{2N}-T_{0}(\bm{x}\to f_{i}^{d}\bm{x})\right)\mathop{\widehat{\mathrm{id}}}\nolimits_{H}^{\otimes N}\Big)\widehat{\Xi}^{\bm{x}}.

(34)

Then
\widehat{T}
is written as

\displaystyle\widehat{T}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq i\leq N\\
d=\pm\end{subarray}}\sum_{\bm{x}\in X^{N}}\Bigg(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\in S(\bm{x},i,d)\\
d^{\prime}=\pm\end{subarray}}T(\bm{x}\to f_{j}^{d^{\prime}}f_{i}^{d}\bm{x})\widehat{\Delta}_{j}^{d^{\prime}}\widehat{\Delta}_{i}^{d}+T(\bm{x}\to f_{i}^{d}\bm{x})\widehat{\Delta}_{i}^{d}+\Big(\frac{1}{2N}-T_{0}(\bm{x}\to f_{i}^{d}\bm{x})\Big)\mathop{\widehat{\mathrm{id}}}\nolimits_{H}^{\otimes N}\Bigg)\widehat{\Xi}^{\bm{x}}

(35)

\displaystyle=\widehat{T}_{0}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq i\leq N\\
d=\pm\end{subarray}}\sum_{\bm{x}\in X^{N}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\in S(\bm{x},i,d)\\
d^{\prime}=\pm\end{subarray}}T(\bm{x}\to f_{j}^{d^{\prime}}f_{i}^{d}\bm{x})(\widehat{\Delta}_{j}^{d^{\prime}}-\mathop{\widehat{\mathrm{id}}}\nolimits_{H}^{\otimes N})\widehat{\Delta}_{i}^{d}\widehat{\Xi}^{\bm{x}},

(36)

where we used (

).
For the definition of coefficients,
see (

), (

),
and (

On this settings, the transition matrix
\widehat{T}=\widehat{T}(\beta,p)
is naturally regarded as a linear operator on
H_{X}^{\otimes N}
. Let
\ket{P(\beta,p)}
be the unique stationary state of

\displaystyle\begin{split}\widehat{T}(\beta,p)\ket{P(\beta,p)}=\ket{P(\beta,p)}.\end{split}

(37)

One can show that, since

is irreducible,

exists and is uniquely determined by Perron–Frobenius theorem.

For the latter discussion, let us consider the symmetry of the transition matrices
\widehat{T}_{0}
and
\widehat{T}
.
We introduce permutation operators

\widehat{\Pi}_{\sigma}
on

For a given element

of

of agents,

where

Then, we have

\displaystyle\widehat{\Pi}_{\sigma}^{\dagger}\widehat{T}_{0}\widehat{\Pi}_{\sigma}=\widehat{T}_{0},

\displaystyle\widehat{\Pi}_{\sigma}^{\dagger}\widehat{T}\widehat{\Pi}_{\sigma}\neq\widehat{T},

(40)

(see (

In the sense of relation (

In contrast, the whole dynamics by

In order to characterize the correlation among agents,
we may consider the neighbor matrix
\mathcal{R}:=(r_{ij})_{i,j}
defined as

\displaystyle r_{ij}:=\sum_{{\bm{x}}}\delta(x_{i},x_{j})P({\bm{x}}).

(43)

For example, if
P(\bm{x})
is the uniform distribution then
r_{ij}=1/L
, and if

. The latter gives the maximum value of

. Note that

for any

The entry

In Fig.

In order to quantify how often a given agent overlaps with the other agents in total, we introduce the overlap centrality as a function of rank
i
using the entries of the neighbor matrix:

\displaystyle O_{i}:=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq j\leq N\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}r_{ij}.

(44)

That is, we regard the agent
i
having larger value of the overlap centrality
as more influential one compared to the other agents having lower values of the overlap centrality.
When the probability distribution
P(\bm{x})
is permutation symmetric, i.e.,
P(\sigma(\bm{x}))=P(\bm{x})
for any
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{N}
, the overlap centrality does not depend on rank
i
.
Note that

\displaystyle M=\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}O_{i}+\dfrac{1}{N}

(45)

holds by the definition.
As shown in Fig.
5
, we compute the overlap centrality

computed from the stationary distribution, showing that
the overlap centrality has a plus slope at attractive interaction of
\beta=1
and has a minus slope at repulsive interaction of
\beta=-1
.

In order to quantify the class of the overlap centrality in terms of the slope,
we measure the correlation coefficient
\phi
of the overlap centrality with respect to agents’ rank. This is defined as

\displaystyle\phi:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\dfrac{(O_{i}-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}O_{j})(i-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}j)}{s_{O}s_{I}},

(46)

where we define

\displaystyle s_{O}^{2}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i}(O_{i}-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}O_{j})^{2},

(47)

\displaystyle s_{I}^{2}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i}(i-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}j)^{2}=\frac{(N-1)(N+1)}{12}.

(48)

By definition, when
|\phi|=1
,

is a linear function of
i
.
We check the condition when this quantity
\phi
is not defined. Since we set

, the denominator of
\phi
is zero exactly when

. This corresponds to the case when

is constant as a function of

.
In this case, we say that the quantity
\phi
is

In Fig.

In the weak supplanting condition with

such as

or
p=0.01

\phi
is close to
+1
for negative
\beta
. As
\beta
increases,
\phi
sharply changes its sign around
\beta=0
, and turns out to be
-1
for positive
\beta
.
We will discuss this behavior in a more general setting in Section
IV
.

Let us discuss the weak-supplanting limit of

, where general results in terms of overlap centrality are available. Although we have focused on only the Potts energy as

in Section

we consider all of the models which belong to the general class of energy functions satisfying the permutation symmetry condition (
1
). The neighbor matrix
\mathcal{R}
(

), the overlap centrality
O_{i}\;(1\leq i\leq N)
(

\phi
of the overlap centrality with agents’ rank (

Hereafter, we use the state vector description, fix parameters

\beta,L,N
as arbitrary values, and consider

p
-dependence of the dynamics
unless otherwise specified.

The main goal in this section is
to derive perfect correlation, which means that the correlation coefficient (
46
) satisfies

,
in the weak-supplanting limit of

as long as
\phi
is not singular in the
sense mentioned after the definition of

.
In order to take a step forward, we start with introducing auxiliary stochastic processes (
49
), by which the transition matrix of the model can be completely reconstructed.
Then, using this decomposition property (
50
),
we construct another decomposition form (
53
) of the transition matrix, which we call

, where the asymptotic behaviors in terms of

Note that the assumption of (

) is essential in the derivation of key properties (

) and (

IV.1
Decompositions of transition matrix

In this subsection, we would like to introduce
beta decomposition
(
53
) of the operator
\widehat{T}
. This decomposition enables us to investigate an asymptotic behavior of
\widehat{T}
for
p\ll 1
because of an asymptotic property (
55
).
To describe it, first we introduce another decomposition, called
supplanting decomposition
. This is relatively easy to describe, and simplifies the description of beta decomposition.
For details, see Appendix
A.3
and
A.4
.

Let us define the partial sum in (
35
).
For an integer
1\leq n\leq N-1
, we define

\displaystyle\widehat{T}_{n}\coloneqq

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq i\leq N\\
d=\pm\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{x}\in X^{N}\end{subarray}}\delta(\#S(\bm{x},i,d),n)

(49)

\displaystyle\Bigg[\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\in S(\bm{x},i,d)\\
d^{\prime}=\pm\end{subarray}}T(\bm{x}\to f_{j}^{d^{\prime}}f_{i}^{d}\bm{x})(\widehat{\Delta}_{j}^{d^{\prime}}-\mathop{\widehat{\mathrm{id}}}\nolimits_{H}^{\otimes N})\Bigg]\widehat{\Delta}_{i}^{d}\widehat{\Xi}^{\bm{x}}.

This operator
\widehat{T}_{n}
is the second term in (
36
) restricting indices
\bm{x},i,d
to those with
\#S(\bm{x},i,d)=n
.
For any subset
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\{1,2,\dots,N-1\}
, the matrix
\widehat{T}_{0}+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}\widehat{T}_{i}
is also a stochastic matrix. For example, the matrix
\widehat{T}_{0}+\widehat{T}_{n}
represents the supplanting process only when
\#S(\bm{x},i,d)=n
.
By definition, we have

\widehat{T}=\widehat{T}_{0}+\widehat{T}_{1}+\widehat{T}_{2}+\dots+\widehat{T}_{N-1}.

(50)

This is a decomposition of the operator
\widehat{T}
, which we call
the
supplanting decomposition
.
Note that the coefficients
\langle\bm{y}|\widehat{T}_{n}|\bm{x}\rangle
of
n
-th term
\widehat{T}_{n}
for
n\geq 1
are estimated as

|\langle\bm{y}|\widehat{T}_{n}|\bm{x}\rangle|\leq\frac{p}{2}.

(51)

For details, see Appendix
A.3.2
.
In particular, the coefficients of
\widehat{T}_{n}
are estimated by
\mathcal{O}(p)
when
p\to+0
.
Moreover, since at most one of
\langle\bm{y}|\widehat{T}_{n}|\bm{x}\rangle\;(1\leq n\leq N-1)
is non-zero for fixed
\bm{x}
and
\bm{y}
, we also have

|\langle\bm{y}|(\widehat{T}-\widehat{T}_{0})|\bm{x}\rangle|\leq\frac{p}{2}.

(52)

Though we explicitly estimate the coefficients of
\widehat{T}_{n}
in (
51
), the supplanting decomposition does not give effective truncation of
\widehat{T}

in terms of small

.
Thus, we look for another decomposition of
\widehat{T}

\widehat{T}=\widehat{T}_{0}+\widehat{U}_{1}+\dots+\widehat{U}_{N-1}

(53)

satisfying

\langle\bm{y}|\widehat{U}_{m}|\bm{x}\rangle=\mathcal{O}(p^{m})\text{ as }p\to+0.

(54)

If we find such an expansion,
we have

\widehat{T}=\widehat{T}_{0}+\widehat{U}_{1}+\dots+\widehat{U}_{m}+\mathcal{O}(p^{m+1})

(55)

for
1\leq m\leq N-1
.

It would not be straightforward to practically find such an expansion. Nevertheless, in this case, through a rather tricky procedure as shown in Appendix
A.4.2
, one can prove that (
53
) and (
54
) are satisfied by the following definition of
\widehat{U}_{m}
:

\displaystyle\widehat{U}_{m}\coloneqq

\displaystyle\frac{(-1)^{m+1}B(m,1+1/p)}{p}

\displaystyle\quad\sum_{m\leq n\leq N}\binom{n-1}{m-1}(1+np)\widehat{T}_{n},

(56)

for
1\leq m\leq N-1
.
Here,
B(a,b)
is the beta function
and
\displaystyle\binom{n-1}{m-1}
is the binomial coefficient. See (
204
) and (
221
) of Appendix
A
for the detail of the derivation; see also Remark
A.2
for the motivation of this decomposition. We call this expansion

. Note that, for an integer
m\geq 1
,

B\left(m,1+\frac{1}{p}\right)=\frac{(m-1)!p^{m}}{(1+p)(1+2p)\dots(1+mp)}.

(57)

By substituting (
49
) and (
57
) into (
IV.1
) with certain sets of transformations in Appendix
A.3.3
and
A.4.4
,
we reach another representation of
\widehat{U}_{m}

as in (
195
) and (
231
):

\displaystyle\widehat{U}_{m}=

\displaystyle\frac{(-1)^{m-1}(m-1)!p^{m}}{2(1+p)(1+2p)\dots(1+mp)}

(58)

\displaystyle\times\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq i<i_{1}<\dots<i_{m}\leq N\end{subarray}}\left[\sum_{1\leq k\leq m}(\widehat{\Delta}_{i_{k}}^{+}+\widehat{\Delta}_{i_{k}}^{-}-2\mathop{\widehat{\mathrm{id}}}\nolimits^{\otimes N}_{H})\right]

\displaystyle\times\widehat{\Xi}_{i,i_{1},\dots,i_{m}}\widehat{T}_{0,\text{move}}^{i},

where we define

\displaystyle\widehat{T}_{0,\text{move}}^{i}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum_{d=\pm}\sum_{\bm{x}\in X^{N}}T_{0}(\bm{x}\to f_{i}^{d}\bm{x})\widehat{\Delta}_{i}^{d}\widehat{\Xi}^{\bm{x}},

(59)

\displaystyle\widehat{\Xi}_{i,i_{1},\dots,i_{m}}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum_{x\in X}\widehat{\Xi}_{i}^{x}\widehat{\Xi}_{i_{1}}^{x}\dots\widehat{\Xi}_{i_{m}}^{x}.

(60)

This representation of
\widehat{U}_{m}

is suitable for the further calculation related to permutation symmetry in Section
IV.2
.
In the following sections,
\widehat{U}_{1}
is the one
we mainly consider in the weak supplanting limit

.

Recall that

defined in (

In Appendix
A.2.5
and

,

IV.2
Existence of perfect correlation

By using beta decomposition of the transition matrix obtained above,
we are going to show that the correlation coefficient
\phi
exhibits perfect correlation
|\phi|=1

in the limit of
p\to 0
.

One can use the Brillouin–Wigner type perturbation theory
Brilloin-Wigner-type perturbation theory
to rewrite the stationary state
\ket{P(\beta,p)}
of
\widehat{T}(\beta,p)
as a perturbation expansion from the stationary state of
\widehat{T}_{0}
. For that purpose, we introduce some symbols. Let
\ket{P_{\mathrm{can}}(\beta)}
be the stationary state of
\widehat{T}_{0}(\beta)
, i.e., the state corresponding to the Gibbs distribution (
6
):

\displaystyle\begin{split}\ket{P_{\mathrm{can}}(\beta)}\coloneqq\sum_{\bm{x}}\frac{\exp(-\beta E(\bm{x}))}{Z(\beta)}\ket{\bm{x}},\end{split}

(64)

which satisfies

\displaystyle\begin{split}\widehat{T}_{0}(\beta)\ket{P_{\mathrm{can}}(\beta)}=\ket{P_{\mathrm{can}}(\beta)},\end{split}

(65)

and

\displaystyle\sum_{\bm{x}}\braket{\bm{x}|P_{\mathrm{can}}(\beta)}=1.

(66)

Note that
\ket{P_{\mathrm{can}}(\beta)}
is invariant under a permutation, that is, it holds that

\displaystyle\widehat{\Pi}_{\sigma}\ket{P_{\mathrm{can}}(\beta)}=\ket{P_{\mathrm{can}}(\beta)}

(67)

for any
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{N}
, because of the permutation symmetry condition (
1
) for energy function.
Let
\widehat{\mathrm{pr}}(\beta)
be a projection operator on
H_{X}^{\otimes N}
to the orthogonal complement of the subspace
{\mathbb{C}}\ket{P_{\mathrm{can}}(\beta)}
:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\widehat{\mathrm{pr}}(\beta)\coloneqq\mathop{\widehat{\mathrm{id}}}\nolimits_{H}^{\otimes N}-\frac{\ket{P_{\mathrm{can}}(\beta)}\bra{P_{\mathrm{can}}(\beta)}}{\braket{P_{\mathrm{can}}(\beta)|P_{\mathrm{can}}(\beta)}},\end{split}

(68)

and
\widehat{G}_{0}(\beta)
be a linear operator from
H_{X}^{\otimes N}
to itself:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\widehat{G}_{0}(\beta)\coloneqq\left({\mathop{\widehat{\mathrm{id}}}\nolimits_{H}^{\otimes N}-\widehat{T}_{0}(\beta)}\right)^{-1}\widehat{\mathrm{pr}}(\beta).\end{split}

(69)

Here,
we need to set the coefficient of the term
\mathop{\widehat{\mathrm{id}}}\nolimits_{H}^{\otimes N}
in
\widehat{G}_{0}(\beta)
as the eigenvalue
1
corresponding to
the eigenvector
\ket{P(\beta,p)}
of
\widehat{T}(\beta,p)
(see (
37
)).
With the above notations,
\ket{P(\beta,p)}
can be written as follows:

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\ket{P(\beta,p)}\\
&=C(\beta,p)\left[{\ket{P_{\mathrm{can}}(\beta)}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left({\widehat{G}_{0}(\widehat{T}-\widehat{T}_{0})}\right)^{n}\ket{P_{\mathrm{can}}(\beta)}}\right],\end{split}

(70)

where
C(\beta,p)
is the positive normalization factor of
\ket{P(\beta,p)}
such that
\sum_{\bm{x}\in X^{N}}\braket{\bm{x}|P(\beta,p)}=1
.
Since
\widehat{T}-\widehat{T}_{0}=\mathcal{O}(p)
, one can estimate that
C(\beta,p)=1+\mathcal{O}(p)
.

As obtained in (
53
) and (
58
), within the asymptotic regime of small
p
,
\widehat{T}-\widehat{T}_{0}
is can be expanded with powers of
p
, and then we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}\widehat{T}-\widehat{T}_{0}=\widehat{U}_{1}+\mathcal{O}(p^{2}),\end{split}

(71)

where

\displaystyle\begin{split}\widehat{U}_{1}=\frac{p/2}{1+p}\sum_{1\leq i_{0}<i_{1}\leq N}\left({\widehat{\Delta}_{i_{1}}^{+}+\widehat{\Delta}_{i_{1}}^{-}-2\mathop{\widehat{\mathrm{id}}}\nolimits_{H}^{\otimes N}}\right)\widehat{\Xi}_{i_{0},i_{1}}\widehat{T}_{0,\text{move}}^{i_{0}}.\end{split}

(72)

By substituting (
72
) into (
70
), we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\ket{P(\beta,p)}\\
&=C(\beta,p)\left[{\ket{P_{\mathrm{can}}(\beta)}+\widehat{G}_{0}\widehat{U}_{1}\ket{P_{\mathrm{can}}(\beta)}+\mathcal{O}(p^{2})}\right]\\
&=C(\beta,p)\left[{\ket{P_{\mathrm{can}}(\beta)}+\frac{p}{1+p}\sum_{1\leq i_{0}<i_{1}\leq N}\widehat{B}_{i_{0},i_{1}}\ket{P_{\mathrm{can}}(\beta)}}\right]\\
&\hskip 199.16928pt+\mathcal{O}(p^{2}),\end{split}

(73)

where we define:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\widehat{B}_{i_{0},i_{1}}\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\widehat{G}_{0}\left({\widehat{\Delta}_{i_{1}}^{+}+\widehat{\Delta}_{i_{1}}^{-}-2\mathop{\widehat{\mathrm{id}}}\nolimits_{H}^{\otimes N}}\right)\widehat{\Xi}_{i_{0},i_{1}}\widehat{T}_{0,\text{move}}^{i_{0}}.\end{split}

(74)

This operator
\widehat{B}_{i_{0},i_{1}}
is dependent on
\beta
but independent of
p
.
Thus, using
r_{ii}=1
for any
i
, the overlap centrality can be written as follows:

IV.5
Correspondence between Overlap centrality and Eigenvector centrality

Let us discuss the relation between the overlap centrality
{\bm{O}}=(O_{i})_{i=1}^{N}
and the other existing ways to define centrality.

First, the overlap centrality has a connection to another existing centrality in the following sense. Let us consider a weighted complete graph, where each agent is regarded as a vertex, and an element
r_{ij}
of the neighbor matrix
\mathcal{R}
for the pair of agents
i,j
is regarded as the weight of the edge
(i,j)
.
Then, the overlap centrality defined above is equivalent to the strength centrality of the complete graph constructed above, which has been introduced in the field of network science
Freeman
;
Barrat
.

Second,
one can also define the eigenvector centrality of the complete graph mentioned above as the eigenvector
{\bm{V}}=(V_{i})_{1\leq i\leq N}
of
\mathcal{R}

with the maximum eigenvalue.
Indeed, one may show that the eigenvector centrality is
directly related to the overlap centrality when
p\ll 1
in the following manner:

\displaystyle{\bm{V}}\propto\frac{1}{N^{3/2}c}{\bm{O}}-\gamma\times(1,1,\ldots,1)^{T}+\mathcal{O}(p^{2}),

(105)

where
c
and
\gamma
are constants with
\mathcal{O}(1)
.
In particular, combining with (
85
), we have

\displaystyle V_{i}

\displaystyle\propto\frac{p}{1+p}\frac{\mathcal{B}_{2}-\mathcal{B}_{1}}{N^{3/2}c}i+\left(\frac{c_{0}}{N^{3/2}c}-\gamma\right)+\mathcal{O}(p^{2})

(106)

where the coefficient of proportionality is independent of
i
.
Thus, the eigenvector centrality as well as the overlap centrality depends on the rank
i
linearly if the term of
\mathcal{O}(p^{2})
is ignored in (
105
).
Remarkably, that relation (
105
) holds for general probability distribution which breaks the permutation symmetry of agents weakly. See Appendix
B

for the explicit two conditions to hold the relation (
105
) in a
more general form.
As shown in Fig.

one can see better agreement of both centralities for smaller

V
Concluding remarks

In this paper, we have proposed a stochastic process without both detailed balance and permutation symmetry, which is inspired by the supplanting phenomenon of Japanese macaques.
We have introduced a new type of centrality, which we call overlap centrality, to characterize a rank-dependent correlation of agents’ positions. Then we have found that the new model of interacting ranked agents exhibits the unexpected linearity (
85
) with respect to agent’s rank
i
in overlap centrality
O_{i}
at small supplanting condition, which could be regarded as a new type of collective phenomenon.
Precisely speaking, when
\mathcal{B}_{1}\neq\mathcal{B}_{2}
, the perfect correlation corresponding to the linearity mentioned above appears between
O_{i}
and rank
i
in the regime of weak supplanting limit
p\to+0
.
Even for small but non-zero
p
, concrete analysis by exact diagonalization shows that
\phi
is very close to the perfect correlation in the case of the Potts energy if
\beta
is far from
0
.

One might ask about the meaning of perfect correlation. So far, we do not have clear answer this question. However, one might have speculation that the perfect correlation implies divergence of characteristic scale in ranking. From this viewpoint, it could be intriguing to quantify such a characteristic scale as future studies.

Another problem on the singular behaviors of
\phi
around
\beta=0
is to identify the effects which essentially cause those singular behaviors. Compared to the equilibrium Potts model, the model with supplanting process does not have permutation symmetry in terms of agents, and also not have detailed balance.
In our derivation of perfect correlation, broken permutation symmetry is one of essential parts, but we are not aware of the effects from broken detailed balance. Concerning this point, one can consider an equilibrium model keeping with detailed balance without permutation symmetry by, for example,
an energy function
\sum_{i,j}J_{i,j}\delta(x_{i},x_{j})
, where
J_{i,j}
is a function of agents
i,j
such as
i\times j
.
If one could prove that there does not exist the singularity of
\phi
for such general equilibrium models, one could presumably expect that
both permutation symmetry breaking and broken detailed balance are essential for causing the singularity. Such a motivation has been raised for broken rotational symmetry observed in active matter
V-Tasaki
. Indeed, it has been proven for a general class of systems having potentials
dependent on position and velocity that rotational symmetry cannot be broken in equilibrium. This implies that the observed phase transitions associating with broken rotational symmetry in active matters are caused purely by nonequilibrium effects such as broken detailed balance.
We will need somewhat similar ideas.

permutation symmetry

connect those quantities with the other known quantities, which remains as an open problem.

Whereas our above discussions focus on the case of small
p\ll 1
,
we move onto the case of any
p
. The result of exact diagonalization implies that perfect correlation with
\phi=1
would hold for negative
\beta
sufficiently far from
0
.
This behavior is of interest in that the strong correlation effect originating from hard core repulsion may stabilize perfect correlation with
\phi=1
.
However,
our strategy of the perturbation with respect to
p
is unavailable for not small
p\not\ll 1
.
For this reason, it is not clear whether the perfect correlation is derived by use of permutation symmetry
in a similar way to the case of
p\ll 1
.
In order to tackle this problem, the situation with restricted values of
L
and
N
is to be considered. As an example, let us take the case satisfying
N=L+1
with the Potts energy.
In this case, if the repulsion is sufficiently strong, then each site is occupied by at least an agent, and there is a single site occupied by two agents.
Then, one of the two agents occupying the site can hop in accordance with the equilibrium dynamics, while all of the other agents cannot hop effectively.
This leads to reduction of the transition of states and could help us to analyze the overlap centrality of this model.
Note that the above discussion is based on the Potts energy, and is not necessarily applied to the other case with general energy form.
It remains for future work to perform further numerical calculation for energy functions other than the Potts energy as well as to explore analytic methods for general
p
.

Let us briefly discuss the possibility of phase transition lines
in parameter space
(\beta,p)
for non-zero
p
.
First, the equilibrium phase transition point
\beta=\beta_{c}
with
p=0
for the case of Potts energy might extend toward non-zero
p
as
a nonequilibrium phase transition line
\beta=\beta_{c}(p)
where
M
shows singular jump.
Second, the point
\beta=0
with
p\to+0
where correlation coefficient shows singularity might also extend toward non-zero
p

as another nonequilibrium phase transition line
\beta=\beta_{0}(p)
where
\phi
shows a singular jump.
However, since our analysis is limited to the
parameter region close to
p=0
, it is necessary to perform large scale numerical simulations or develop another analytical methods in order to capture the true limiting behavior of large system size for non-zero
p
. This remains to be an intriguing future study.

From a mathematical viewpoint,
beta decomposition
of the transition matrix could be useful in more general context. This expression gives the exact lowest order of transition matrix in terms of
p
. Nevertheless, the concrete expression of it looks complicated and then it would not be straightforward to use the expression for a given purpose. Further, it should be noted that the minor modification of supplanting process prevents us to obtain the beta decomposition. In this sense, the present version of the supplanting process can be regarded as a specially tractable case.
It remains as an open question how beta decomposition can be applied to calculation of the other quantities and whether one can find other tractable cases in this direction.

Let us remark on the direct relation between eigenvector centrality and strength centrality.
Unifying two relations among centralities discussed in Section
IV.5
, one can also have another conclusion in the context of network theory.
Consider a complete graph whose edges are weighted by the same value with small fluctuation.
Then the neighbor matrix
\mathcal{R}
whose entries are weights of edges has a decomposition
\tilde{\mathcal{R}}^{(1)}+\tilde{\mathcal{R}}^{(2)}
similar to (

),
and the graph holds a linear relation similar to (

Lastly, we mention the results in this paper relevant to behaviors of members in a group of primate species.
Ranking has been widely known to be one characteristic structure which primates species has when they live as a group
primatebook
.
Indeed, it has been found that ranking affects the spatial location of the members in a group

pecentrality1

and the distance between the members
rank_distance1
;

The overlap centrality observed in the model proposed in this paper
might shed light on how one could estimate the ranking structures of such a group
and its environmental condition.

Derivation of two decompositions of transition matrix

In this section, we would like to explain, in detail, the derivations of two decompositions of transition matrix
\widehat{T}
, which we call
supplanting decomposition
and
beta decomposition
as mentioned in Section
IV.1
.
To do this, we give a detailed account of our models in terms of operators.
Hereafter, in order to make this Appendix self-contained,
many definitions in the main text are repeated.

Notations

For a set
\bm{S}
, the number of elements is denoted as
\#\bm{S}
.
The Kronecker’s delta
\delta(i,j)
is defined as

\delta(i,j)=\begin{cases}1&(\text{if $i=j$})\\
0&(\text{if $i\neq j$}).\\
\end{cases}

(109)

We consider any vector space appearing in this section as complex vector space.
We denote the set of complex numbers as
\mathbb{C}
,
and the set of real numbers as
\mathbb{R}
.

A.1
Describing the model

In this subsection, as a preliminary, we introduce basic concepts which are necessary to explain the model.

A.1.1
Agents and states

We consider
N\geq 2
agents labeled by
1,2,\dots,N
.
They have a total ordering (i.e. linear dominance) meaning that if
1\leq i<j\leq N
, agent
i
is higher than agent
j
.
The set of all agents is written as

[N]\coloneqq\{1,2,\dots,N\}.

(110)

The agents lie in the lattice
X={\mathbb{Z}}/L{\mathbb{Z}}=\{0,1,\dots,L-1\}
with
L\geq 3
.
For an agent
i
, the position is written as
x_{i}\in X
.
Thus, the configuration space of agents is described by

X^{N}=\left\{\bm{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{N})\mid x_{i}\in X\right\}.

(111)

A.1.2
Hopping map

We define a map
f_{i}^{+}\colon X^{N}\to X^{N}
(resp.
f_{i}^{-}\colon X^{N}\to X^{N}
) of configurations as
the increment (resp. decrement) of position of agent
i
under periodic boundary condition.
Explicitly, we have

\displaystyle f_{i}^{+}(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{i},\dots,x_{N})

\displaystyle\coloneqq(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{i}+1,\dots,x_{N}),

(112)

\displaystyle f_{i}^{-}(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{i},\dots,x_{N})

\displaystyle\coloneqq(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{i}-1,\dots,x_{N}).

(113)

A.1.3
The permutation-invariant energy function

We take an energy function
E(\bm{x})=E(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{N})
which is symmetric in the following sense:

E(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{N})=E(x_{\sigma(1)},x_{\sigma(2)},\dots,x_{\sigma(N)})

(114)

for any permutation
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{N}
of
N
elements in
[N]
.
The symmetry will be used in
Appendix
A.2.5
and
A.4.5
,
and is essential in
(
IV.2
) and (
IV.2
).
For example, in (
2
),
we take the
normalized Potts model

\displaystyle E(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=-\frac{(L-1)\log(L-1)}{N(L-2)}\sum_{1\leq i,j\leq N}\delta(x_{i},x_{j})

(115)

as an energy function.

To simplify the notation, we introduce the
(i,d)
-th difference of the energy function
E

for
1\leq i\leq N
and
d\in\{+,-\}
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{i}^{d}E(\bm{x})

\displaystyle\coloneqq E(f_{i}^{d}\bm{x})-E(\bm{x}).

(116)

A.1.4
Describing the hopping by equilibrium dynamics

First we describe the hopping by equilibrium dynamics as follows.

(i)

Determine an agent
1\leq i\leq N
with equal probability
1/N
.

(ii)

Choose a direction
d=+
or
-
with equal probability
1/2
.

(iii)

Decide whether the agent
i
stays or hops to the direction
d
.

•

The probability that the agent
i
hops to direction
d
is

\frac{1}{1+\exp(\beta\mathcal{D}_{i}^{d}E(\bm{x}))}.

(117)

•

The probability that the agent
i
stays is

1-\frac{1}{1+\exp(\beta\mathcal{D}_{i}^{d}E(\bm{x}))}=\frac{1}{1+\exp(-\beta\mathcal{D}_{i}^{d}E(\bm{x}))}.

(118)

For a graphical explanation, see Fig.
10
.

A.1.5
Describing the hopping by supplanting process

For a given configuration
\bm{x}\in X^{N}
, an agent
i\in[N]
, and a direction
d\in\{+,-\}
, we define the set
S(\bm{x},i,d)\subseteq[N]
as follows:

\displaystyle S(\bm{x},i,\pm)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\left\{i<j\leq N\mid x_{j}=x_{i}\pm 1\right\}.

(119)

In other words,
S(\bm{x},i,d)
is defined as a set of agents
j\in[N]
satisfying the two conditions: (1)
j\in S(\bm{x},i,d)
is lower than
i
(in other words
j>i
), and (2) sits on the site
x_{i}+1
(when
d=+
) or
x_{i}-1
(when
d=-
).

Let us fix the supplanting rate
0\leq p<\infty
.
After the step (iii) of the first hopping,
we define the
supplanting process
as described below.

(iv)

One of the following events occurs in the probabilities described below.

•

The agent
j\in S(\bm{x},i,d)
hops
with the probability

\frac{p}{1+p\#S(\bm{x},i,d)}.

(120)

•

In a probability

\frac{1}{1+p\#S(\bm{x},i,d)},

(121)

no one hops.

(v)

If a hopping occurs in (iv), choose a direction
d^{\prime}=+
or
-
of the hopping of the agent
j
with uniform probability
1/2
.

Note that if
p=0
or
S(\bm{x},i,d)
is empty, then no supplanting occurs.

describes the probability tree after the first hopping of the agent
i
.

A.2
Transition matrices

In this subsection, we write down the transition matrices as linear operators on a state space.

A.2.1
State vector spaces

The state space
H_{X}
is the complex vector space with a basis

|0\rangle,|1\rangle,\dots,|{L-1}\rangle

(122)

corresponding to sites
0,1,\dots,L-1\in X
, respectively.

Similarly the multi-state space
H_{X^{N}}
is
the complex vector space with a basis
|\bm{x}\rangle
corresponding to configurations
\bm{x}\in X^{N}
.
It is identified to the tensor space
H^{\otimes N}_{X}
with a map

H_{X^{N}}\to H_{X}^{\otimes N}\coloneqq\underbrace{H_{X}\otimes H_{X}\otimes\dots\otimes H_{X}}_{N}\quad;\quad|\bm{x}\rangle\mapsto|x_{1}\rangle\otimes|x_{2}\rangle\otimes\dots\otimes|x_{N}\rangle.

(123)

With this identification, we freely use tensor notation to write down operators.

We introduce the inner product
\braket{\cdot|\cdot}
on
H_{X}
such that the basis
(|x\rangle)_{x\in X}
is orthonormal. This induces an inner product on
H_{X}^{\otimes N}
, and the basis
(|\bm{x}\rangle)_{\bm{x}\in X^{N}}
on
H_{X}^{\otimes N}
is orthonormal.
We use the same symbol
\langle\cdot|\cdot\rangle
to write this induced inner product on
H_{X}^{\otimes N}
.

A.2.2
Coefficients of operators

An operator
\widehat{A}\colon H_{X}^{\otimes N}\to H_{X}^{\otimes N}
is described by its coefficients.
The
(\bm{x},\bm{y})
-th coefficient of
\widehat{A}
is written by

\langle\bm{y}|\widehat{A}|\bm{x}\rangle
.
In other words, we can write

\widehat{A}|\bm{x}\rangle=\sum_{\bm{y}\in X^{N}}|\bm{y}\rangle\langle\bm{y}|\widehat{A}|\bm{x}\rangle.

(124)

A.2.3
Shift operators

As pieces of transition matrices, we define notations of shift operators.

The shift operators
\widehat{\Delta}_{i}^{+}
and
\widehat{\Delta}_{i}^{-}
on
H_{X}^{\otimes N}

for
1\leq i\leq N
are defined as

\displaystyle\widehat{\Delta}_{i}^{+}|\bm{x}\rangle

\displaystyle\coloneqq|f_{i}^{+}\bm{x}\rangle

(125)

\displaystyle=|x_{1}\rangle\otimes|x_{2}\rangle\dots\otimes|x_{i}+1\rangle\otimes\dots\otimes|x_{N}\rangle,

(126)

\displaystyle\widehat{\Delta}_{i}^{-}|\bm{x}\rangle

\displaystyle\coloneqq|f_{i}^{-}\bm{x}\rangle

(127)

\displaystyle=|x_{1}\rangle\otimes|x_{2}\rangle\dots\otimes|x_{i}-1\rangle\otimes\dots\otimes|x_{N}\rangle,

(128)

for
\bm{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{N})\in X^{N}
.
In terms of coefficients, the followings hold:

\displaystyle\langle\bm{y}|\widehat{\Delta}_{i}^{+}|\bm{x}\rangle

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}1&(\text{if $\bm{y}=f_{i}^{+}\bm{x}$})\\
0&(\text{otherwise}),\end{cases}

(129)

\displaystyle\langle\bm{y}|\widehat{\Delta}_{i}^{-}|\bm{x}\rangle

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}1&(\text{if $\bm{y}=f_{i}^{-}\bm{x}$})\\
0&(\text{otherwise}).\end{cases}

(130)

A.2.4
Projection operators

We denote the identity operator on the state space
H_{X}
as
\mathop{\widehat{\mathrm{id}}}\nolimits_{H}
.
Then we can write the identity operator on the multi-state space
H_{X}^{\otimes N}
as
\mathop{\widehat{\mathrm{id}}}\nolimits_{H}^{\otimes N}
.

The projection operator
\widehat{\Xi}_{i}^{y}
on
H_{X}^{\otimes N}
for
1\leq i\leq N
and
y\in X
is defined as

\widehat{\Xi}_{i}^{y}|\bm{x}\rangle\coloneqq\begin{cases}|\bm{x}\rangle&(\text{if $x_{i}=y$})\\
0&(\text{otherwise}).\end{cases}

(131)

This projection operator checks whether
x_{i}=y
or not; if
x_{i}=y
return
|\bm{x}\rangle
, otherwise return the zero vector.
For a configuration
\bm{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{N})\in X^{N}
,
we define

\widehat{\Xi}^{\bm{x}}\coloneqq\prod_{1\leq j\leq N}\widehat{\Xi}_{j}^{x_{j}}.

(132)

It satisfies

\widehat{\Xi}^{\bm{y}}|\bm{x}\rangle=\begin{cases}|\bm{x}\rangle&(\text{if $\bm{x}=\bm{y}$})\\
0&(\text{otherwise}).\end{cases}

(133)

and, in terms of coefficients,

\displaystyle\langle\bm{y}|\widehat{\Xi}^{\bm{z}}|\bm{x}\rangle

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}1&(\text{if $\bm{y}=\bm{x}=\bm{z}$})\\
0&(\text{otherwise}).\end{cases}

(134)

The operator
\widehat{\Xi}^{\bm{z}}
checks whether
\bm{x}=\bm{z}
.

We define some other projection operators: first, for
x\in X
and
\emptyset\neq\bm{S}\subseteq[N]
,

\displaystyle\widehat{\Xi}_{\bm{S}}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\begin{cases}\sum_{x\in X}\prod_{i\in\bm{S}}\widehat{\Xi}_{i}^{x}&(\text{if $\bm{S}\neq\emptyset$})\\
\mathop{\widehat{\mathrm{id}}}\nolimits^{\otimes N}_{H}&(\text{if $\bm{S}=\emptyset$}).\end{cases}

(135)

The operator
\widehat{\Xi}_{\bm{S}}
checks whether all of the agents in
\bm{S}
sit on the same site. Explicitly,

\displaystyle\widehat{\Xi}_{\bm{S}}|\bm{x}\rangle=\begin{cases}|\bm{x}\rangle&(\text{if $x_{i}=x_{j}$ for any $i,j\in\bm{S}$})\\
0&(\text{otherwise}).\end{cases}

(136)

If
\bm{S}
is empty, it checks nothing; it is the identity operator.

Another projection operator
\widehat{\Upsilon}_{i;\bm{S}}
for
1\leq i\leq N
and
\bm{S}\subseteq\{i+1,i+2,\dots,N\}

checks two conditions: (1)
x_{j}=x_{i}
if
j\in\bm{S}
, and (2)
x_{j^{\prime}}\neq x_{i}
if
j^{\prime}\not\in\bm{S}
and
j^{\prime}>i
.
Explicitly, it is defined as

\displaystyle\widehat{\Upsilon}_{i;\bm{S}}|\bm{x}\rangle

\displaystyle\coloneqq\begin{cases}|\bm{x}\rangle&(\text{if
$x_{j}=x_{i}$ for $j\in\bm{S}$,
and $x_{i}\not\in\{x_{j^{\prime}}\}_{\begin{subarray}{c}j^{\prime}>i\\
j^{\prime}\not\in\bm{S}\end{subarray}}$})\\
0&(\text{otherwise}).\end{cases}

(137)

In terms of
\widehat{\Xi}_{i}^{x}
and
\widehat{\Xi}_{\bm{S}}
, this operator is written as

\displaystyle\widehat{\Upsilon}_{i;\bm{S}}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\sum_{x\in X}\widehat{\Xi}_{i}^{x}\cdot\prod_{j\in\bm{S}}\widehat{\Xi}_{j}^{x}\cdot\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}j^{\prime}>i\\
j^{\prime}\not\in\bm{S}\end{subarray}}(\mathop{\widehat{\mathrm{id}}}\nolimits_{H}^{\otimes N}-\widehat{\Xi}_{j^{\prime}}^{x})&(\text{if $\bm{S}\neq\emptyset$ and $\bm{S}\neq\{i+1,i+2,\dots,N\}$})\\
\sum_{x\in X}\widehat{\Xi}_{i}^{x}\cdot\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}j^{\prime}>i\end{subarray}}(\mathop{\widehat{\mathrm{id}}}\nolimits_{H}^{\otimes N}-\widehat{\Xi}_{j^{\prime}}^{x})&(\text{if $\bm{S}=\emptyset$ and $i\leq N-1$})\\
\sum_{x\in X}\widehat{\Xi}_{i}^{x}\cdot\prod_{j\in\bm{S}}\widehat{\Xi}_{j}^{x}&(\text{if $\bm{S}\neq\emptyset$ and $\bm{S}=\{i+1,i+2,\dots,N\}$})\\
\sum_{x\in X}\widehat{\Xi}_{i}^{x}&(\text{if $\bm{S}=\emptyset$ and $i=N$})\end{cases}

(138)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{S}\subseteq\bm{S}^{\prime}\subseteq\{i+1,\dots,N\}\end{subarray}}(-1)^{\#\bm{S}^{\prime}-\#\bm{S}}\widehat{\Xi}_{\bm{S}^{\prime}}.

(139)

A relation between these operators is

\displaystyle\widehat{\Xi}_{\{i\}\cup\bm{S}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{S}\subseteq\bm{S}^{\prime}\subseteq\{i+1,\dots,N\}\end{subarray}}\widehat{\Upsilon}_{i;\bm{S}}.

(140)

A.2.5
Permutation operators

For a permutation
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{N}
of
[N]=\{1,2,\dots,N\}
,
we define

\sigma(\bm{x})\coloneqq(x_{\sigma(1)},x_{\sigma(2)},\dots,x_{\sigma(n)})

(141)

for
\bm{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n})\in X^{N}
.
Let
\widehat{\Pi}_{\sigma}
be the corresponding matrix to
\sigma^{-1}
.
Explicitly,

\displaystyle\widehat{\Pi}_{\sigma}|(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n})\rangle

\displaystyle=|\sigma^{-1}(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n})\rangle

(142)

\displaystyle=|(x_{\sigma^{-1}(1)},x_{\sigma^{-1}(2)},\dots,x_{\sigma^{-1}(N)})\rangle.

(143)

For an operator
\widehat{A}
on
H_{X}^{\otimes N}
,
the daggered symbol
\widehat{A}^{\dagger}
denotes the Hermitian conjugate of
\widehat{A}
. Then we have

\widehat{\Pi}_{\sigma}^{\dagger}=\widehat{\Pi}_{\sigma}^{-1}=\widehat{\Pi}_{\sigma^{-1}}
.
We also have some commutation relations as follows:

\displaystyle\widehat{\Delta}_{j}^{\pm}\widehat{\Pi}_{\sigma}

\displaystyle=\widehat{\Pi}_{\sigma}\widehat{\Delta}_{\sigma(j)}^{\pm},

(144)

\displaystyle\widehat{\Xi}_{j}^{x}\widehat{\Pi}_{\sigma}

\displaystyle=\widehat{\Pi}_{\sigma}\widehat{\Xi}_{\sigma(j)}^{x},

(145)

\displaystyle\widehat{\Xi}_{\bm{S}}\widehat{\Pi}_{\sigma}

\displaystyle=\widehat{\Pi}_{\sigma}\widehat{\Xi}_{\sigma(\bm{S})}.

(146)

We remark that
\widehat{\Upsilon}_{i;\bm{S}}
has a factor
\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}j>i\\
j\not\in\bm{S}\end{subarray}}(\mathop{\widehat{\mathrm{id}}}\nolimits_{H}^{\otimes N}-\widehat{\Xi}_{x}^{j})
.
Since this factor involves an ordering
j>i
,
it does not satisfy simple relation in terms of permutation operators.
A key procedure in our calculation is to rewrite an operator
\widehat{\Upsilon}_{i;\bm{S}}

in terms of
\widehat{\Xi}_{\{i\}\cup\bm{S}}
. This enables us to use the commutation relations in terms of permutations.

Besides, since the energy function depends only on the numbers of agents on each site,
the energy function does not depend on the labeling of the agents. In other terms,

\displaystyle E(f_{\sigma(i)}^{d}(x_{\sigma^{-1}(1)},x_{\sigma^{-1}(2)},\dots,x_{\sigma^{-1}(N)}))

\displaystyle=E(f_{i}^{d}(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{N})).

(147)

A.2.6
Other commutation relations of operators

The shift operators (resp. the projection operators) are commutative at each other,
while a shift operator and a projection operator does not necessarily commute. Explicitly, it holds that

\displaystyle\widehat{\Xi}_{i}^{x}\widehat{\Delta}_{j}^{+}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\widehat{\Delta}_{j}^{+}\widehat{\Xi}_{i}^{x}&(\text{if $i\neq j$})\\
\widehat{\Delta}_{j}^{+}\widehat{\Xi}_{i}^{x-1}&(\text{if $i=j$}),\end{cases}

(148)

\displaystyle\widehat{\Xi}_{i}^{x}\widehat{\Delta}_{j}^{-}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\widehat{\Delta}_{j}^{-}\widehat{\Xi}_{i}^{x}&(\text{if $i\neq j$})\\
\widehat{\Delta}_{j}^{-}\widehat{\Xi}_{i}^{x+1}&(\text{if $i=j$}).\end{cases}

(149)

A.2.7
Transition matrix for the first hopping

According to the description in Appendix
A.1.4
,
the transition matrix
\widehat{T}_{0}
for the first hopping can be described as

\widehat{T}_{0}\coloneqq\sum_{1\leq i\leq N}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{d=\pm}\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\bm{x}\in X^{N}}\Big(\frac{\widehat{\Delta}_{i}^{d}}{1+\exp(\beta\mathcal{D}_{i}^{d}E(\bm{x}))}+\frac{\mathop{\widehat{\mathrm{id}}}\nolimits_{H}^{\otimes N}}{1+\exp(-\beta\mathcal{D}_{i}^{d}E(\bm{x}))}\Big)\widehat{\Xi}^{\bm{x}}.

(150)

For the latter use, we introduce some symbols. First,
for
\bm{x}\in X^{N}
,
1\leq i\leq N
, and
d\in\{+,-\}
,
we write the coefficient of
\widehat{T}_{0}
as

c_{\beta}(\bm{x},i,d)\coloneqq\frac{1}{2N(1+\exp(\beta\mathcal{D}_{i}^{d}E(\bm{x})))}.

(151)

Then we have

\frac{1}{2N}-c_{\beta}(\bm{x},i,d)=\frac{1}{2N(1+\exp(-\beta\mathcal{D}_{i}^{d}E(\bm{x})))}.

(152)

Since
\exp(x)>0
for any real
x\in\mathbb{R}
, we can estimate

0<c_{\beta}(\bm{x},i,d)\leq\frac{1}{2N}

(153)

for any
\beta,E,\bm{x},i,d,N
, and
L
.
We define a set of operators: for
d\in\{+,-\}
,

\displaystyle\widehat{T}_{0,\text{move}}^{i,d}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum_{\bm{x}\in X^{N}}c_{\beta}(\bm{x},i,d)\widehat{\Delta}_{i}^{d}\widehat{\Xi}^{\bm{x}},

(154)

\displaystyle\widehat{T}_{0,\text{stay}}^{i,d}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum_{\bm{x}\in X^{N}}\left(\frac{1}{2N}-c_{\beta}(\bm{x},i,d)\right)\widehat{\Xi}^{\bm{x}},

(155)

\displaystyle\widehat{T}_{0,\text{move}}^{i}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\widehat{T}_{0,\text{move}}^{i,+}+\widehat{T}_{0,\text{move}}^{i,-},

(156)

\displaystyle\widehat{T}_{0,\text{stay}}^{i}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\widehat{T}_{0,\text{stay}}^{i,+}+\widehat{T}_{0,\text{stay}}^{i,-}.

(157)

Then we have

\displaystyle\widehat{T}_{0}

\displaystyle=\sum_{1\leq i\leq N}(\widehat{T}_{0,\text{move}}^{i}+\widehat{T}_{0,\text{stay}}^{i})

(158)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq i\leq N\\
d=\pm\end{subarray}}(\widehat{T}_{0,\text{move}}^{i,d}+\widehat{T}_{0,\text{stay}}^{i,d}).

(159)

Note that, using relations in Appendix
A.2.5
, we have

\displaystyle\widehat{\Pi}_{\sigma}^{\dagger}\widehat{T}_{0,\text{move}}^{i,d}\widehat{\Pi}_{\sigma}

\displaystyle=\widehat{T}_{0,\text{move}}^{\sigma(i),d},

(160)

\displaystyle\widehat{\Pi}_{\sigma}^{\dagger}\widehat{T}_{0,\text{stay}}^{i,d}\widehat{\Pi}_{\sigma}

\displaystyle=\widehat{T}_{0,\text{stay}}^{\sigma(i),d},

(161)

\displaystyle\widehat{\Pi}_{\sigma}^{\dagger}\widehat{T}_{0}\widehat{\Pi}_{\sigma}

\displaystyle=\widehat{T}_{0}.

(162)

A.2.8
Transition matrix for supplanting process

According to Appendix
A.1.5
,
in order to describe the transition matrix
\widehat{T}
for the supplanting process,
it suffices to replace
\widehat{\Delta}_{i}^{d}\widehat{\Xi}^{\bm{x}}
appearing in

A.2.10
The broken permutation symmetry of transition matrix

In this subsection, we explain an example of the broken permutation symmetry of transition matrix
\widehat{T}
by using commutative relations.
Recall that, for an operator
\widehat{A}
on
H_{X}^{\otimes N}
,
the daggered symbol
\widehat{A}^{\dagger}
denotes the Hermitian conjugate of
\widehat{A}
.

By (

), for

and

, we have

(172)

On the other hand, for a transposition
\sigma=(i\;j)\in\mathfrak{S}_{N}
, one has

\displaystyle\langle f_{j}^{d^{\prime}}f_{i}^{d}\bm{x}|\widehat{\Pi}_{\sigma}^{\dagger}\widehat{T}\widehat{\Pi}_{\sigma}|\bm{x}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\bm{x}|\widehat{\Delta}_{i}^{d,{\dagger}}\widehat{\Delta}_{j}^{d^{\prime},{\dagger}}\widehat{\Pi}_{\sigma}^{\dagger}\widehat{T}\widehat{\Pi}_{\sigma}|\bm{x}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\bm{x}|(\widehat{\Pi}_{\sigma}\widehat{\Delta}_{j}^{d^{\prime}}\widehat{\Delta}_{i}^{d})^{\dagger}\widehat{T}\widehat{\Pi}_{\sigma}|\bm{x}\rangle.

(174)

Using the commutative relation (

), one can see

\displaystyle\langle\bm{x}|(\widehat{\Pi}_{\sigma}\widehat{\Delta}_{j}^{d^{\prime}}\widehat{\Delta}_{i}^{d})^{\dagger}\widehat{T}\widehat{\Pi}_{\sigma}|\bm{x}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\bm{x}|(\widehat{\Delta}_{\sigma(j)}^{d^{\prime}}\widehat{\Delta}_{\sigma(i)}^{d}\widehat{\Pi}_{\sigma})^{\dagger}\widehat{T}\widehat{\Pi}_{\sigma}|\bm{x}\rangle

(175)

\displaystyle=\langle\bm{x}|(\widehat{\Delta}_{i}^{d^{\prime}}\widehat{\Delta}_{j}^{d}\widehat{\Pi}_{\sigma})^{\dagger}\widehat{T}\widehat{\Pi}_{\sigma}|\bm{x}\rangle

(176)

\displaystyle=\langle\bm{x}|\widehat{\Pi}_{\sigma}^{\dagger}\widehat{\Delta}_{j}^{d,{\dagger}}\widehat{\Delta}_{i}^{d^{\prime},{\dagger}}\widehat{T}\widehat{\Pi}_{\sigma}|\bm{x}\rangle.

(177)

Proceeding with the transformation using (
141
), it follows that

\displaystyle\langle\bm{x}|\widehat{\Pi}_{\sigma}^{\dagger}\widehat{\Delta}_{j}^{d,{\dagger}}\widehat{\Delta}_{i}^{d^{\prime},{\dagger}}\widehat{T}\widehat{\Pi}_{\sigma}|\bm{x}\rangle

(180)

Thus, since

,
we obtain the broken permutation symmetry of

with respect to the permutation of components:

\langle f_{j}^{d^{\prime}}f_{i}^{d}\bm{x}|\widehat{\Pi}_{\sigma}^{\dagger}\widehat{T}\widehat{\Pi}_{\sigma}|\bm{x}\rangle\neq\langle f_{j}^{d^{\prime}}f_{i}^{d}\bm{x}|\widehat{T}|\bm{x}\rangle.

(181)

This shows that, for any energy function

as in Appendix

and any transposition

\widehat{\Pi}_{\sigma}^{\dagger}\widehat{T}\widehat{\Pi}_{\sigma}\neq\widehat{T}.

(182)

In fact, for any non-trivial permutation

182

Explicitly, for a non-trivial permutation

A.4.1
Definition of beta terms

Here we give a definition of
m
-th term
\widehat{U}_{m}
of beta decomposition
by using
\widehat{T}_{n}
which appear in the supplanting decomposition (see (

For
1\leq m\leq N-1

\displaystyle\widehat{U}_{m}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{(-1)^{m-1}B(m,1+1/p)}{p}\sum_{m\leq n\leq N-1}\binom{n-1}{m-1}(1+np)\widehat{T}_{n}.

(197)

Here
B(a,b)
is the beta function:

\displaystyle B(a,b)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{\Gamma(a)\Gamma(b)}{\Gamma(a+b)}

(198)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}x^{a-1}(1-x)^{b-1}dx.

(199)

In particular, we have

\displaystyle B\left(m,1+\frac{1}{p}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma(m)\Gamma(1+1/p)}{\Gamma(m+1+1/p)}

(200)

\displaystyle=\frac{(m-1)!}{(1+1/p)(2+1/p)\dots(m+1/p)}

(201)

\displaystyle=\frac{(m-1)!p^{m}}{(1+p)(1+2p)\dots(1+mp)}.

(202)

By these descriptions, one can write

\displaystyle\widehat{U}_{m}

\displaystyle=\frac{(-1)^{m-1}(m-1)!p^{m-1}}{(1+p)(1+2p)\dots(1+mp)}\sum_{m\leq n\leq N-1}\binom{n-1}{m-1}(1+np)\widehat{T}_{n}.

(203)

A.4.2
The beta decomposition

We can prove the following decomposition:

\widehat{T}=\widehat{T}_{0}+\sum_{1\leq m\leq N-1}\widehat{U}_{m}.

(204)

In fact,

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq m\leq N-1}\widehat{U}_{m}

\displaystyle=\sum_{1\leq m\leq N-1}\frac{(-1)^{m-1}B(1+1/p,m)}{p}\sum_{m\leq n\leq N-1}\binom{n-1}{m-1}(1+np)\widehat{T}_{n}

(205)

\displaystyle=\sum_{1\leq n\leq N-1}\left[\sum_{1\leq m\leq n}(-1)^{m-1}\frac{B(1+1/p,m)}{p}\binom{n-1}{m-1}\right](1+np)\widehat{T}_{n}.

(206)

Then the following lemma is enough to show the desired decomposition (
204
).

Lemma A.1
.

\sum_{1\leq m\leq n}\frac{(-1)^{m-1}B(1+1/p,m)}{p}\binom{n-1}{m-1}=\frac{1}{1+np}.

(207)

Sketch of proof.

We only sketch the proof. With two generating series of exponential type

\displaystyle F_{0}(t)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum_{m\geq 0}a_{m}\frac{t^{m}}{m!},

(208)

\displaystyle F_{1}(t)

\displaystyle\coloneqq F_{0}(t)e^{t},

(209)

where the coefficients of
F_{0}(t)
is

\displaystyle a_{m}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{(-1)^{m}B(1+1/p,m+1)}{p}

(210)

\displaystyle=\frac{(-1)^{m}m!p^{m}}{(1+p)(1+2p)\dots(1+(m+1)p)},

(211)

it is enough to prove

F_{1}(t)=\sum_{r\geq 0}\frac{1}{1+(r+1)p}\frac{t^{r}}{r!}.

(212)

Since
a_{0}=F_{0}(0)={(1+p)^{-1}}
and

\displaystyle(1+(m+2)p)a_{m+1}=(-1)\cdot(m+1)pa_{m}

(213)

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle(m+1)a_{m+1}+(m+1)a_{m}=-\left(1+\frac{1}{p}\right)a_{m+1}

(214)

for
m\geq 0
, we have a differential equation for

\displaystyle t\left(\frac{dF_{0}}{dt}(t)+F_{0}(t)\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{p}({(1+p)}F_{0}(t)-1).

(215)

Thus
F_{1}(t)=F_{0}(t)e^{t}
satisfies

\displaystyle t\frac{dF_{1}}{dt}(t)

\displaystyle=-{\left(1+\frac{1}{p}\right)}F_{1}(t)+\frac{1}{p}e^{t}.

(216)

Now the equation (
212

∎

Remark A.2

(The origin of the beta decomposition)
.

With the operators
\widehat{U}_{i_{0};\bm{S}}
,
we can define a variant model of supplanting process.
For a subset
\bm{S}_{0}\subseteq\{1,\dots,N\}
, we define

\widehat{T}_{\bm{S}_{0}}\coloneqq\sum_{i_{0}\in\bm{S}_{0}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{S}^{\prime}\subseteq\{i_{0}+1,\dots,N\}\cap\bm{S}_{0}\end{subarray}}\widehat{U}_{i_{0};\bm{S}^{\prime}}.

(237)

This operator
\widehat{T}_{\bm{S}_{0}}
represents the model
where supplanting process only occurs on pairs of agents
i,j

with
i,j\in\bm{S}_{0}
.

Conversely, we can define
\widehat{U}_{i_{0};\bm{S}}
from these variants
\widehat{T}_{\bm{S}_{0}}

by inclusion-exclusion principle:

\widehat{U}_{i_{0};\bm{S}_{0}}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{S}^{\prime}\subsetneq\{i_{0}+1,\dots,N\}\cap\bm{S}_{0}\end{subarray}}(-1)^{\#\bm{S}^{\prime}-\#\bm{S}_{0}}\widehat{T}_{\{i_{0}\}\cup\bm{S}^{\prime}}.

(238)

The beta decomposition was originally derived from this point of view.

A.4.5
Commutation relation between beta terms and permutation operators

Though it is not used in the main text, we write down a commutation relation between beta terms and permutation operators.

Using relations in Section
A.2.5
, we have the following forms (the third equation is equivalent to (
160
)):

\displaystyle\widehat{P}_{\bm{S}}\widehat{\Pi}_{\sigma}

\displaystyle=\widehat{\Pi}_{\sigma}\widehat{P}_{\sigma(\bm{S})},

(239)

\displaystyle\widehat{\Xi}_{\{i_{0}\}\cup\bm{S}}\widehat{\Pi}_{\sigma}

\displaystyle=\widehat{\Pi}_{\sigma}\widehat{\Xi}_{\{\sigma(i_{0})\}\cup\sigma(\bm{S})},

(240)

\displaystyle\widehat{T}_{0,\text{move}}^{i_{0}}\widehat{\Pi}_{\sigma}

\displaystyle=\widehat{\Pi}_{\sigma}\widehat{T}_{0,\text{move}}^{\sigma(i_{0})}.

(241)

With these three relations, we obtain that

\widehat{U}_{i_{0};\bm{S}}\widehat{\Pi}_{\sigma}=\widehat{\Pi}_{\sigma}\widehat{U}_{\sigma(i_{0});\sigma(\bm{S})}.

(242)

This enables us to investigate the commutation relation between
\widehat{U}_{m}
and permutation operators with using (
236
) or between
\widehat{T}_{\bm{S}_{0}}
and permutation operators with using (
237
).

Appendix B
Eigenvector centrality and overlap centrality

In this section we see a relation between the eigenvector centrality and the overlap centrality of the neighbor matrix.
Let us recall the definitions of their centrality in our context.
The eigenvector centrality is defined as a normalized eigenvector with the maximum eigenvalue of the neighbor matrix
\mathcal{R}
. Such an eigenvector exists uniquely and its components can be taken to be real and positive because of the Perron–Frobenius theorem
Bonacich
;
Newman
.
The overlap centrality (
44
) is the expectation value of how many agents are at the same site with a given agent.

First we describe our settings. We take a state
\ket{P}\in H_{X}^{\otimes N}
corresponding to a probability distribution
P(\bm{x})
.

\ket{P}
does not have to be a stationary state of a certain stochastic process.
The neighbor matrix
\mathcal{R}=(r_{ij})_{1\leq i,j\leq N}
of
\ket{P}
is defined similar to (
43
), explicitly

r_{ij}=\sum_{\bm{x}}\delta(x_{i},x_{j})\braket{\bm{x}|P}.

(243)

We consider a decomposition of the state
\ket{P}

\displaystyle\ket{P}=\ket{P_{1}}+\ket{P_{2}},

(244)

and define matrices
\mathcal{R}^{(\ell)}=(r^{(\ell)}_{ij})
as

\displaystyle r_{ij}^{(\ell)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{x}}\delta(x_{i},x_{j})\braket{\bm{x}|P_{\ell}}

(245)

for
\ell=1,2
. By definition, we also have a decomposition of the neighbor matrix
\mathcal{R}

\mathcal{R}={\mathcal{R}}^{(1)}+{\mathcal{R}}^{(2)}.

(246)

We assume that the decomposition (
244
)
satisfies the following two conditions.

(i)

\ket{P_{1}}
is symmetric under permutations: that is,
\widehat{\Pi}_{\sigma}\ket{P_{1}}=\ket{P_{1}}
for any
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{N}
. From this assumption there is a constant
c
with
r_{ij}^{(1)}=c
for any
i\neq j
.
We assume that
c\neq 0
.

(ii)

The off-diagonal entries
r_{ij}^{(2)}
of
\mathcal{R}^{(2)}
are sufficiently smaller than
\left|{c}\right|
: that is,
|r_{ij}^{(2)}|\ll|c|
for any
i\neq j
.

We modify the matrices
\mathcal{R}^{(1)},\mathcal{R}^{(2)}
to
\tilde{\mathcal{R}}^{(1)}=(\tilde{r}_{ij}^{(1)}),\tilde{\mathcal{R}}^{(2)}=(\tilde{r}_{ij}^{(2)})

such that all of the diagonal entries of
\tilde{\mathcal{R}}^{(2)}
are zero and the following decomposition of
\mathcal{R}
holds:

\mathcal{R}=\tilde{\mathcal{R}}^{(1)}+\tilde{\mathcal{R}}^{(2)}.

(247)

Considering all of the diagonal entries of
\mathcal{R}
are one, we can take

\displaystyle\tilde{r}_{ij}^{(1)}=\begin{cases}1&\text{(if $i=j$)}\\
r_{ij}^{(1)}=c&\text{(if $i\neq j$)}\end{cases}\quad\text{ and }\quad\tilde{r}_{ij}^{(2)}=\begin{cases}0&\text{(if $i=j$)}\\
r_{ij}^{(2)}&\text{(if $i\neq j$)}.\end{cases}

(248)

From the assumption (
ii
), the matrix
\tilde{\mathcal{R}}^{(2)}
can be regarded as a perturbative part in
\mathcal{R}
.

The eigenvalue problem of
\tilde{\mathcal{R}}^{(1)}
can be solved easily: eigenvalues of
\tilde{\mathcal{R}}^{(1)}
are
1+(N-1)c
and
1-c
, and their corresponding eigenspaces are
{\mathbb{C}}\bm{v}_{0}
and the orthogonal complement
({\mathbb{C}}\bm{v}_{0})^{\perp}
, respectively, where
\bm{v}_{0}\coloneqq\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}(1,1,\ldots,1)^{T}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{N}
. In particular, if
\tilde{\mathcal{R}}^{(2)}
vanishes, then the eigenvector centrality of
\mathcal{R}=\tilde{\mathcal{R}}^{(1)}
is the vector
\bm{v}_{0}
.

Let us use a first-order perturbation theory to calculate the eigenvector centrality
{\bm{V}}
of
\mathcal{R}
. Here we introduce an orthonormal system
(\bm{u}_{i})_{i=1}^{N-1}
of the vector space
({\mathbb{C}}\bm{v}_{0})^{\perp}
. According to the Rayleigh–Schrödinger type perturbation theory, we have

\displaystyle{\bm{V}}

\displaystyle\propto\bm{v}_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}\frac{\bm{u}_{i}\bm{u}_{i}^{\dagger}\tilde{\mathcal{R}}^{(2)}\bm{v}_{0}}{1+(N-1)c-(1-c)}+\mathcal{O}(|\tilde{r}^{(2)}/c|^{2})

(249)

\displaystyle=\bm{v}_{0}+\frac{(1-\bm{v}_{0}\bm{v}_{0}^{\dagger})\tilde{\mathcal{R}}^{(2)}\bm{v}_{0}}{Nc}+\mathcal{O}(|\tilde{r}^{(2)}/c|^{2})

(250)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Nc}\mathcal{R}\bm{v}_{0}-\left({\frac{\bm{v}_{0}^{\dagger}\tilde{\mathcal{R}}^{(2)}\bm{v}_{0}}{Nc}+\frac{1-c}{Nc}}\right)\bm{v}_{0}+\mathcal{O}(|\tilde{r}^{(2)}/c|^{2})

(251)

up to the first order of
|\tilde{r}^{(2)}/c|\coloneqq\max_{1\leq i,j\leq N}|\tilde{r}_{ij}^{(2)}/c|
.
Since
(\mathcal{R}\bm{v}_{0})_{i}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{j=1}^{N}r_{ij}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}(O_{i}-1)
, we obtain

\displaystyle{\bm{V}}\propto\frac{1}{N^{3/2}c}{\bm{O}}-\gamma\times(1,1,\ldots,1)^{T}+\mathcal{O}(|\tilde{r}^{(2)}/c|^{2}),

(252)

where
{\bm{O}}=(O_{i})_{i=1}^{N}
is the vector consisting of the overlap centrality, and
\gamma
is a constant which is expressed by

\displaystyle\gamma=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\left({\frac{\bm{v}_{0}^{\dagger}\tilde{\mathcal{R}}^{(2)}\bm{v}_{0}}{Nc}+\frac{2-c}{Nc}}\right).

(253)

Thus we find that the eigenvector centrality and the overlap centrality are equal up to multiplying by a constant and adding a vector in
{\mathbb{C}}(1,1,\ldots,1)^{T}
. Note that the higher order terms of this perturbation can have a nontrivial
{\bm{O}}
-dependence, but are ignored in the approximation.

Let us apply this result to the case of the stationary state
\ket{P(\beta,p)}
of the transition matrix
\widehat{T}(\beta,p)
.
Suppose that
p
is small enough to be able to perform the perturbation expansion (
70
).
Under this assumption, we give the decomposition of
\ket{P(\beta,p)}
as follows:

\displaystyle\ket{P(\beta,p)}

\displaystyle=\ket{P_{1}(\beta,p)}+\ket{P_{2}(\beta,p)},

(254)

\displaystyle\ket{P_{1}(\beta,p)}

\displaystyle=C(\beta,p)\ket{P_{\text{can}}(\beta)},

(255)

\displaystyle\ket{P_{2}(\beta,p)}

\displaystyle=C(\beta,p)\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left({\widehat{G}_{0}(\widehat{T}-\widehat{T}_{0})}\right)^{n}\ket{P_{\mathrm{can}}(\beta)}.

(256)

Let us check that this decomposition satisfies the above condition (i) and (ii).

(i)

From (
67
),
\ket{P_{1}(\beta,p)}
is symmetric under permutations. Since
\beta,E(\bm{x})\in{\mathbb{R}}
, we have

\displaystyle c=\dfrac{C(\beta,p)}{Z_{N}(\beta)}\sum_{\bm{x}\in X^{N}}\delta(x_{1},x_{2})e^{-\beta E(\bm{x})}\neq 0.

(257)

(ii)

\widehat{T}-\widehat{T}_{0}=\mathcal{O}(p)
leads to
\tilde{r}_{ij}^{(2)}=\mathcal{O}(p)
,
and
c
is of order of unity in terms of
p
, which follows that
\left|{\tilde{r}^{(2)}/c}\right|=\mathcal{O}(p)
.

Hence, the relation (
252
) can be applied in the present case, and we obtain the expression (
105
), in which the term of
\mathcal{O}(\left|{\tilde{r}^{(2)}/c}\right|^{2})
in (
252
) is replaced
with
\mathcal{O}(p^{2})
.
Note that the manner of such a decomposition (
254
) is not unique. In particular, one can choose the coefficient of
\ket{P_{\mathrm{can}}(\beta)}
in
\ket{P_{1}(\beta,p)}
as any form of
1+\mathcal{O}(p)
, though the values of
c
and
\gamma
depend on the manner.
