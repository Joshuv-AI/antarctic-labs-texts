Introduction

For any graph
G
, the
ramsey number

r(G)
is the smallest positive integer
r
such that any
2
-coloring of the edges of the complete graph on
r
vertices (
K_{r}
) contains a monochromatic subgraph isomorphic to
G
. Famously, the exact determination of ramsey numbers is an exceedingly difficult computational problem that becomes intractable beyond small cases. As such, the ramsey numbers of various classes of graphs must be determined analytically, and some (such as the ramsey numbers of complete graphs,
r(K_{n})
) have proved to be notoriously difficult to determine; as such, there are many open or partially open problems. However, the ramsey numbers of many classes of simple graphs (including stars, paths, and cycles) have been determined exactly: see
[
1
]
for a survey of known results of ramsey numbers as of January 2021.

In 1979, Grossman, Harary, and Klawe investigated the ramsey numbers of one such family of graphs known as double stars (
[
2
]
). A
double star
is defined as the union of two disjoint stars with an edge joining their centers. Grossman et al. solved the ramsey numbers of double stars in most cases, and more recently Norin, Sun, and Zhao have made some progress in the remaining open cases for double stars (
[
3
]
). See Section
4
for more information. Motivated by the results of Grossman et al. and Norin et al., here we investigate the ramsey numbers of a generalization of double stars, which we refer to as
linked double stars
.

Figure 1:
Example of a
S_{5}(7,4)

We define a linked double star as the union of two disjoint stars with a
path
joining their centers. In particular, for
n\geq m\geq 0
, the
linked double star

S_{c}(n,m)
is the graph on the points
\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{c},v_{1},\ldots,v_{n},w_{1},\ldots w_{m}\}
with the edges

\{(a_{1},v_{i}),(a_{1},a_{2}),(a_{2},a_{3}),\ldots,(a_{c-1},a_{c}),(a_{c},w_{j}):1\leq i\leq n,1\leq j\leq m\}.

Note that above,
a_{1}
and
a_{c}
are the centers of the
n
-star and
m
-star respectively, and the path from
a_{1}
to
a_{c}
is the link between the two stars. For reference, Figure 1 shows an example of a
S_{5}(7,4)
.

In this paper, we will primarily be studying the ramsey numbers of
odd-linked double stars
. Given the above definition of a linked double star, an odd-linked double star simply refers to a linked double star wherein the link has an odd number of vertices (i.e.
c=2p+1
for some
p\in\mathbb{N}
).

We exactly determine the ramsey numbers of two groups of odd-linked double stars. In particular, if
c=2p+1
, we find that

1.

r(S_{c}(n,m))=2(n+m)+c-2
if
n\geq c

2.

r(S_{c}(n,2))=n+3p+3
if
n\leq p-2
.

In Section 2, we establish lower bounds on the ramsey numbers of odd-linked double stars, which hold irrespective of the relative size of the stars and the link. In Section 3, we establish upper bounds, which in the particular cases of the sizes of
n
and
m
described above, happen to be precisely equal to the lower bounds determined in Section 2 (thus determining the ramsey numbers exactly). Finally, in Section 4, we list open problems suggested by this work.

2
Lower bounds

In this section, we will establish lower bounds on the ramsey numbers of all odd-linked double stars. From this point forth, we will assume (and use interchangeably)
c=2p+1
for some
p\in\mathbb{N}
, where again
c
is the number of vertices on the link. The general statement of the lower bounds are presented in Theorem 2.1, the proof of which will be provided by applying the subsequent lemmas.

Theorem 2.1
.

The ramsey numbers of the odd-linked double stars satisfy

r(S_{c}(n,m))\geq\begin{cases}2(n+m+p)-1&\mbox{for }n+m\geq p+2\\
n+m+3p+1&\mbox{for }n+m\leq p+2\end{cases}.

Lemma 2.2
.

r(S_{c}(n,m))\geq 2(n+m+p)-1
.

Proof.

Consider a
2
-coloring of
K_{2(n+m+p)-2}
, where the red subgraph consists of
K_{n+m+p-1}\cup K_{n+m+p-1}
, so that the blue subgraph is the complete bipartite graph
K(n+m+p-1,n+m+p-1)
. It is easy to see that there is no red
S_{c}(n,m)
, since
S_{c}(n,m)
is connected and has
n+m+c
vertices. But there also cannot be a blue
S_{c}(n,m)
, since when a
S_{c}(n,m)
is presented as a subgraph of a bipartite graph there must be
n+m+p
vertices that belong to the same partition.
∎

Lemma 2.3
.

r(S_{c}(n,m))\geq n+m+3p+1
.

Proof.

Consider a
2
-coloring of
K_{n+m+3p}
, where the red subgraph consists of
K_{p}\cup K_{n+m+2p}
, so that the blue subgraph is the complete bipartite graph
K(p,n+m+2p)
. It is easy to see that there is no red
S_{c}(n,m)
since
S_{c}(n,m)
is connected and has
n+m+c
vertices. But there also cannot be a blue
S_{c}(n,m)
, since when a
S_{c}(n,m)
is presented as subgraph of a bipartite graph there must be
n+m+p
vertices that belong to one partition, and at least
p+1
vertices belonging to the other partition.
∎

Combining the results of Lemmas
2.2
and
2.3
, the proof of Theorem
2.1
is completed.

3
Upper bounds

In this section, we will establish upper bounds on the ramsey numbers of odd-linked double stars.

We begin, as before, by stating the main theorem(s), and then prove the result(s) by a series of lemmas which together imply the desired result(s).

Theorem 3.1
.

If
n\geq 2p+1
, the ramsey numbers of odd-linked double stars satisfy

r(S_{c}(n,m))\leq 2(n+m+p)-1.

Theorem 3.2
.

If
n\leq p-2
, the ramsey numbers of odd-linked double stars satisfy

r(S_{c}(n,2))\leq n+3p+3.

Proof of Theorem
3.1

We will first introduce some notation which will be used throughout this section, the approach taken here is largely motivated by the strategy used by Grossman et al. in
[
2
]
. Consider some fixed
2
-coloring of a complete graph

, and let

denote the vertex-set of

. For a particular vertex

, let

and
B(v)
denote the sets of vertices joined to

by a red and blue edge respectively. In this section, we may often use red-
d(v)
to denote

and similarly for blue-
d(v)
. For a particular subset of points,

, define red-
d_{W}(v)
as

\text{red-}d_{W}(v)=|R(v)\cap W|

and similarly for blue-
d_{W}(v)
. We will also consider a particular vertex

, where

is chosen to have maximum monochromatic degree. Thus we may assume without loss of generality that
\text{red-}d(u)\geq\text{red-}d(v)
and
\text{red-}d(u)\geq\text{blue-}d(v)
for all

. For convenience, we will define

and

, and write

|A|=n+m+p-1+k

for some integer

. Finally, unless explicitly stated otherwise, we shall assume that

and

.

We will now provide a series of lemmas that will be used repeatedly in this section.

Lemma 3.3
.

(
Due to Yu and Li
, see
[
4
]
)
A broom graph,

, is equivalent to

. The ramsey numbers of brooms are

r(B(n,c))=\begin{cases}n+c+\left\lceil\frac{c}{2}\right\rceil-1&\text{if }c\geq 2n-1\\
2(n+c)-2\left\lceil\frac{c}{2}\right\rceil-1&\text{if }4\leq c\leq 2n-2\end{cases}.

Note that if
n=1
, or
1\leq c\leq 2
, then the ramsey numbers of the corresponding brooms can be solved exactly by the ramsey numbers of paths and stars. See
[
4
]
for more information. If

, then since

, the ramsey numbers of the corresponding brooms are solved by the results of Grossman et al. See
[
2
]
for more information.

Lemma 3.4
.

If

, and

, then every such

-coloring of a

Clearly it is enough to prove the lemma for

Consider some fixed

\displaystyle\leq n-p-1

\displaystyle<(n+1)-p-1

Lemma 3.16

.

Lemma 3.17

Suppose

First, observe that

Consider some fixed

Now observe that if there are any four consecutive vertices

c_{3}
and
c_{2p+1}
, the path

P=\{c_{1},c_{2},c_{3},\ldots,c_{i-1},c_{2p+1},c_{2p},\ldots,c_{i+1},c_{i}\},

gives the desired blue path of length
2p+1
(note a similar path exists if instead

or
c_{i}=c_{2p+2}
. Thus, if any

, there exists a blue

(note there is one vertex left unused on

,

, that may be used to complete the

, if needed). Thus we may assume that
\forall c\in C\backslash\{c_{1}\}
,
\text{blue-}d_{Z_{C}}(c)\leq 1
and so
\text{red-}d_{Z_{C}}(c)\geq n+p
.

Now since
n<p
we have

\frac{(n+p)(2p+1)}{n+p+1}>n+p+1,

and so there must be a vertex
z^{\prime}\in Z_{C}
such that
\text{red-}d_{C}(z^{\prime})\geq n+p+2
. Let
W\subseteq C
be a subset of
R(z^{\prime})\cap C
of size
n+p+2
. Now since

\frac{(n+p-1)(n+p+2)}{n+p}>2

there must exist a vertex
z\in Z_{C}\backslash\{z^{\prime}\}
such that
\text{red-}d_{W}(z)\geq 3
. Let
\{w_{1},w_{2},w_{3}\}\subseteq R(z)\cap W
. Choose any
p
vertices of
W
including the vertex
w_{1}
, but excluding
w_{2}
and
w_{3}
; call this set of vertices

. Observe that since

, we know
\forall a\in A
,
\text{red-}d_{Z_{C}\backslash\{z^{\prime}\}}(a)\geq n+p-1\geq p+1
. Furthermore, since

p+1\leq n+p\leq 2(p+1)-2,

by Lemma
3.5
there must be a red cycle
C^{\prime}
of length
2p
between
W
and
Z_{C}\backslash\{z^{\prime}\}
(where above we have used the fact that

and

). Since

contains
w_{1}
, avoids
w_{2}
and
w_{3}
, and
z

\{w_{1},w_{2},w_{3}\}
, this defines a red
S_{2p}(2,0)
, with one endpoint at

and the other endpoint somewhere in
W
. But since

, we may append
z^{\prime}
to the endpoint in

,

without interference.

∎

Corollary 3.20.1
.

Let
C
be a blue cycle of length
2p+2
. If
|S_{C}|\geq p+1
and
|T_{C}^{2p+1}|\geq p+2
, then if there exists a vertex
c\in C
such that
\text{blue-}d_{Z_{C}}(c)\geq n+1
, then this
2
-coloring admits a monochromatic

.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.19
, we may assume that every
t\in T^{2p+1}
satisfies
\text{red-}d_{Z_{C}}(t)\geq p+1
. In particular, if
T=\{c\in C:\text{ red-}d_{Z_{C}}(c)\geq p+1\}
, then

. Therefore if
|T_{C}^{2p+1}|\geq p+2
, then

∎

Lemma 3.21
.

Let
C
be a blue cycle of length
2p+2
. If
|S_{C}|=p+1
and
|T_{C}^{2p+1}|=p+1
, then if there exists a vertex
c\in C
such that
\text{blue-}d_{Z_{C}}(c)\geq n+1
, then this
2
-coloring admits a monochromatic
S_{c}(n,2)
.

Proof.

Suppose there existed a vertex
c_{1}\in C
, with
\text{blue-}d_{Z_{C}}(q_{1})\geq n+1
. Let
C^{\prime}=C\backslash T_{C}^{2p+1}
. If any
c\in C^{\prime}
satisfied
\text{red-}d_{Z_{C}}(c)\geq p+1
, then by Lemma
3.20
, this
2
-coloring would admit a monochromatic
S_{c}(n,2)
. Therefore, we may assume
\forall c\in C^{\prime}
that
\text{red-}d_{Z_{C}}(c)\leq p
and so
\text{blue-}d_{Z_{C}}(q)\geq n+1
.

Define

X=\{c_{i}\in C:\exists c_{j}\in C^{\prime}\text{ such that }c_{i}\text{ and }c_{j}\text{ are endpoints of a path of length }2p+1\text{ on }C\}.

Conceptually,
X
is the set of all the vertices on
C
that are found by following
C
for
2p+1
vertices, starting from a vertex in
C^{\prime}
. Note that
|X|\geq|C^{\prime}|=p+1
. Suppose some
x\in X
satisfied
\text{blue-}d_{Z_{C}}(x)\geq 1
, and let
c\in C^{\prime}
be the vertex found by following
C
for
2p+1
vertices from
x
. Then
c
and
x
would define a blue
S_{c}(n,2)
, with
n
-star at
c
, and
2
-star at
x
(where we have used one vertex in
Z_{C}
, and the remaining vertex on
C
between
x
and
c
to form the
2
-star). Therefore, we may assume that there is a red complete bipartite graph between
X
and
Z_{C}
.

Let
X^{\prime}\subseteq X
be some subset of
p+1
vertices of
X
, and set
Y=C\backslash X^{\prime}
. Note that
|X^{\prime}|=|Y|=p+1
. Since
X^{\prime}\subset X
, we may assume by the argument above that there is a red complete bipartite graph between
X^{\prime}
and
Z_{C}
. Now suppose that some
x\in X^{\prime}
was connected to some
y\in Y
by a red edge. Then, using the complete bipartite graph between
X^{\prime}
and
Z_{C}
, we may form a red
S_{c}(n,1)
, with the
n
-star centered at some vertex in
X^{\prime}\backslash\{x\}
, and the
1
-star centered at
x
. Using

, this
1
-star can be made into a
2
-star, completing the red
S_{c}(n,2)
. Therefore, we may assume there is a blue complete bipartite graph between
X^{\prime}
and
Y
.

Suppose that there is a vertex
y\in Y
such that
\text{blue-}d_{Z_{C}}(y)\geq n+1
. Now if any vertex
y^{\prime}\in Y\backslash\{y\}
has
\text{blue-}d_{Z_{C}}(y^{\prime})\geq 1
, then it will complete a blue
S_{c}(n,2)
. This blue
S_{c}(n,2)
can be formed using the blue complete bipartite graph between
X^{\prime}
and
Y
, to build a blue path of length
2p+1
with one endpoint at
y
and the other endpoint at
y^{\prime}
; this path defines a blue
S_{c}(n,2)
with
n
-star at
y
, and
2
-star at
y^{\prime}
(where we have used the remaining unused vertex in
X^{\prime}
to complete the
2
-star, if necessary). Therefore, we may assume there is a red complete bipartite graph between
Y\backslash\{y\}
and
Z_{C}
. Now this implies that
\forall z\in Z_{C}
,

\displaystyle\text{red-}d_{Y}(z)

\displaystyle\geq p

\displaystyle\geq n+2.

Now using the red complete bipartite graph between
X^{\prime}
and
Z_{C}
, there must exist a red path of length
2p+1
with both endpoints in
Z_{C}
. By the above reasoning, both endpoints have sufficient red degree to complete the red
S_{c}(n,2)
.

Therefore we may assume that
\forall y\in Y
,
\text{red-}d_{Z_{C}}(y)\geq p+1
. Since

p+1\leq|Z_{C}|\leq 2(p+1)-2,

by Lemma
3.5
there must be a blue cycle of length
2p+2
between

and
Z_{C}
. This cycle contains a red path of length

with both endpoints in

. Since there is a red complete bipartite graph between

, the endpoints have sufficient red degree to complete a red

Lemma 3.22
.

If
|S|\geq p+1
, and
n\leq p-2
, then every such
2
-coloring of a
K_{r}
admits a monochromatic
S_{c}(n,2)
, for
r\geq n+3p+3
.

Proof.

Clearly it is enough to prove the lemma for
r=n+3p+3
. If
|S|\geq p+1
, then by the results of Corollary
3.20.1
and Lemma
3.21
, we may assume that
\forall q\in Q
,
\text{blue-}d_{Z}(q)\leq n
and so
\text{red-}d_{Z}(q)\geq p+1
. Since

\frac{(p+1)(2p+2)}{n+p+1}>p+1,

there must be a vertex
z^{\prime}\in Z
such that
\text{red-}d_{Q}(z^{\prime})\geq p+2
. We shall now consider two cases based on the size of
|S|
.

•

If
|S|=p+1
, then let
W\subset Q
be a subset of

of size

, such that there exists a vertex

that is not in
S
. Such a subset must exist since
|R(z^{\prime})\cap Q|\geq p+2
, but

, where

. Finally, let

be chosen so that

is not adjacent to

on

•

If

, then let

be any subset of

, where as above,

. Similarly, choose

so that it is not adjacent to

on

.

Given the above definitions of

vertices of

including

, but excluding

. Furthermore, choose these

, nor the two vertices adjacent to

on

are chosen. If

, then removing

,
q_{i}
,

, and

from consideration leaves at least

\displaystyle 2p+2-(n+1)-2

\displaystyle=2p-n-1

\displaystyle\geq p+1

vertices to choose from. Call this set of

chosen vertices

. Since

, it follows that
\forall a\in A
,
\text{red-}d_{Z}(a)\geq p+1
. Since

2p+2
between

and

, call this red cycle

.

Now, either

. First, suppose that

. Then following

4
Unsolved Problems and Further Results

Here we have proved the ramsey numbers of odd-linked double stars in all cases where
n\geq c
, and
n\leq\lfloor\frac{c}{2}\rfloor-2
,
m=2
. However, our work leaves open the result for all other cases with
n<c
. We possess partial results in some other cases with
n<c
, but most cases remain open.

Beyond the remaining cases of odd-linked double stars, this result more generally prompts the question of the ramsey numbers of the even-linked double stars. Surprisingly, the ramsey numbers of even-linked double stars have proved to be significantly more challenging to determine than odd-linked double stars. There are, however, some results for specific cases of even-linked double stars:

•

First we simply note that if
c=2p
the ramsey numbers of
S_{c}(n,0)
or
S_{c}(n,1)
are solved exactly by the result of Lemma
3.3
.

•

The ramsey numbers of
S_{4}(n,m)
(example in Figure 2 below) were solved by Burr and Erdős to be

r(S_{4}(n,m))=\text{max}(2n+3,n+2m+5).

See in
[
9
]
for detailed proof.

Example of a
S_{4}(6,4)

•

Finally, the ramsey numbers of
S_{2}(n,m)
(example in Figure 3 below) were solved, in most cases, by Grossman et. Al. In particular, they show that for all
n\leq\sqrt{2}m
and
n\geq 3m
, the ramsey numbers of
S_{2}(n,m)
satisfy

r(S_{2}(n,m))=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lr}\text{max}(n+2m+1,2n+2),&\text{for }n\text{ odd},m\leq 2\\
\text{max}(n+2m+2,2n+2),&\text{otherwise}\end{array}\right\}.

See
[
2
]
for detailed proof. It was conjectured in
[
2
]
that the above result holds in the range
\sqrt{2}m<n<3m
as well. Surprisingly however, Norin et. Al showed in
[
3
]
that the conjecture is false, as well as providing asymptotic results for the ramsey numbers of
S_{2}(n,m)
in the gap. The exact value of
r(S_{2}(n,m))
in the range
\sqrt{2}m<n<3m
, however, remains an open question.

S_{2}(8,5)

We generally ask then, for either
c\geq 6
or
c=2
, what are the ramsey numbers of the
even-linked double stars
?
