Curie–Weiss Model under ℓ p \ell^{p} constraint and a Generalized Hubbard–Stratonovich Transform
Partha S. Dey 1, Daesung Kim 2

Abstract
. We consider the Ising Curie–Weiss model on the complete graph constrained under a given ℓ p \ell^{p} norm for some p > 0 p>0 . For p = ∞ p=\infty , it reduces to the classical Ising Curie–Weiss model. We prove that for all p > 2 p>2 , there exists β c ​ ( p ) \beta_{c}(p) such that for β < β c ​ ( p ) \beta<\beta_{c}(p) , the magnetization is concentrated at zero and satisfies an appropriate Gaussian CLT. We have β c ​ ( p ) > 1 \beta_{c}(p)>1 for p > 2 p>2 and lim p → ∞ β c ​ ( p ) = 3 \lim_{p\to\infty}\beta_{c}(p)=3 . 1.
Introduction

1.1.
The Model

We consider the mean-field Curie–Weiss model under an
\ell^{p}
constraint. The model has the same formulas for the Hamiltonian and the Gibbs measure as in the classical Curie–Weiss model. However, the spins in this model are taken from the
n
-dimensional
\ell^{p}
sphere instead of the hypercube as in the classical case.

We fix a positive real number
p>0
and define the spin configuration space
S_{n,p}
to be the
n
-dimensional
\ell^{p}
-sphere of radius
n^{1/p}
,
i.e.,

\displaystyle S_{n,p}:=\left\{\bm{\sigma}\in\mathds{R}^{n}\ \biggl|\ \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|\sigma_{i}\right|^{p}=1\right\}.

Consider the Gibbs measure at inverse temperature
\beta>0
given by

(1.1)

\displaystyle d\mu_{n,\beta,p}(\bm{\sigma}):=\mathcal{Z}_{n,p}(\beta)^{-1}\exp(\beta H_{n}(\bm{\sigma}))\,d_{n,p}\bm{\sigma},

where
d_{n,p}
is the uniform measure on
S_{n,p}
,
H_{n}(\bm{\sigma}):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i<j}\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}
is the Hamiltonian, and

(1.2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}_{n,p}(\beta)

\displaystyle:=\int_{S_{n,p}}\exp(\beta H_{n}(\bm{\sigma}))\,d_{n,p}\bm{\sigma}

is the partition function.

The classical Curie–Weiss model can be considered as the
p=\infty
version of this model, where the critical inverse temperature is
\beta_{c}(\infty)=1
. When
p=2
, this model is equivalent to the self-scaled version with standard Gaussian base measure, and it is known that a similar phase transition happens at
\beta_{c}(2)=1
(see
Cerf and Gorny (2016)
;
Gorny (2014)
for more details at the critical
\beta_{c}(2)
).

1.2.
Motivation

The classical Curie–Weiss model and its generalization by
Ellis and
Newman (1978)
consider the product base measure, which leads to the independent structure. A natural question is whether there is a similar phase transition when the spin configuration has a dependence structure. In particular, in the
\ell^{p}
constraint model, each spin has a global interaction with other spins and one question is whether there exists some
\beta_{c}(p)\in(0,\infty)
such that for
\beta<\beta_{c}(p)
the typical magnetization is concentrated at
0
and for
\beta>\beta_{c}(p)
it is not. Another question is whether developing a general framework to analyze constrained spin models is possible.

The classical model can be understood as the special case
p=\infty
in our model, and we are interested in the asymptotic behavior when
n
goes to infinity for finite
p>0
. A natural question is whether the limits in
n
and
p
are interchangeable and whether there is a discontinuity. Specifically, we are interested in the behavior of the function
p\mapsto\beta_{c}(p)
as
p
varies from
0
to
\infty
, for example, whether it is continuous and type of discontinuity if it is not. We clearly know that
\beta_{c}(2)=\beta_{c}(\infty)=1
. One might suspect that
\beta_{c}(p)
is a constant or a continuous function for
p\in[2,\infty]
. However, the actual picture is different and needs detailed analysis.

Another motivation of the
\ell^{p}
-constraint model is the connection with the
\ell^{p}
-Gaussian Grothendieck problem. The
\ell^{p}
-Grothendieck problem is the optimization problem regarding the quadratic forms of a matrix over
\ell^{p}
. The corresponding disorder model of the
\ell^{p}
Curie–Weiss model is closely related to the
\ell^{p}
-Grothendieck problem with a random matrix (see
Chen and Sen (2023)
;
Dominguez (2022)
;
Khot and Naor (2012)
).

1.3.
Simplification

We can rewrite the model (
1.1
) with respect to a sequence of i.i.d. random variables to remove the dependency arising from the
\ell^{p}
constraint, allowing us to generalize the model further. We define the norm
\left|\!\left|\!\left|\cdot\right|\!\right|\!\right|_{n,p}
on
\mathds{R}^{n}
as follows

(1.3)

\displaystyle\left|\!\left|\!\left|\bm{x}\right|\!\right|\!\right|_{n,p}:=\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|x_{i}\right|^{p}\right)^{1/p}\text{ for }\bm{x}\in\mathds{R}^{n}.

Next, we define a collection of symmetric probability densities indexed by
p>0
given by

\displaystyle\rho_{p}(x):=c_{p}\cdot e^{-\left|x\right|^{p}/p},\quad x\in\mathds{R}

where
c_{p}:=\frac{p^{-1/p}}{2\Gamma(1+1/p)}

for
p>0
and use
Z_{p}
to denote a typical random variable having density
\rho_{p}
. Note that
|Z_{1}|\sim\text{Exp}(1)
and
Z_{2}\sim\N(0,1)
. Moreover, it is easy to check that

\E\left|Z_{p}\right|^{p}=1\text{ for all }p>0.

One can easily verify that
\left|Z_{p}\right|^{p}/p
is distributed as a Gamma random variable with shape parameter
1/p
and scale parameter
1
, enabling us to compute all of its polynomial moments; in particular, the variance of
Z_{p}
can be explicitly written as

\displaystyle\nu_{p}^{2}:=\var(Z_{p})=\E Z_{p}^{2}=p^{2/p}\cdot\frac{\Gamma(3/p)}{\Gamma(1/p)}\quad\text{ for }p>0.

Let the coordinates of the random vector
\bm{X}=(X_{1},X_{2},\ldots,X_{n})
be
n
i.i.d. random variables with density
\rho_{p}
. Define

\displaystyle\widehat{X}_{i}:=\frac{X_{i}\;\;\;}{\left|\!\left|\!\left|\bm{X}\right|\!\right|\!\right|_{n,p}},\quad i=1,2,\ldots,n.

It is well-known (see
Schechtman and
Zinn (1990)
;
Barthe et al. (2005)
) that the vector
\widehat{\bm{X}}=(\widehat{X}_{1},\widehat{X}_{2},\ldots,\widehat{X}_{n})
is uniformly distributed on the
n
-dimensional
\ell^{p}
-sphere
S_{n,p}
. In particular, for the partition function (
1.2
), we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}_{n,p}(\beta)

\displaystyle=\E\exp(\beta H_{n}(\widehat{\bm{X}}))

(1.4)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathds{R}^{n}}\exp\left(\frac{\beta n}{2}\cdot\frac{\left|n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\right|^{2}}{\left|\!\left|\!\left|\bm{x}\right|\!\right|\!\right|_{n,p}^{2}}-\frac{\beta}{2}\cdot\frac{n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{2}}{\left|\!\left|\!\left|\bm{x}\right|\!\right|\!\right|_{n,p}^{2}}\right)\prod_{i=1}^{n}\rho_{p}(x_{i})\,dx_{i}.

When
p\geqslant 1
, clearly

\displaystyle\frac{\left|n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\right|}{\left|\!\left|\!\left|\bm{x}\right|\!\right|\!\right|_{n,p}}\leqslant 1\text{ for all }\bm{x}\in\mathds{R}^{n}.

In particular,
{H_{n}(\bm{\sigma})}\leqslant n/2
and for the log-partition function we get
\log\mathcal{Z}_{n,p}(\beta)\leqslant n\beta/2
for all
\beta>0
. However, for
p\in(0,1)
the Hamiltonian grows at the order of
n^{2/p-1}\gg n
when finitely many of the spins are
\Theta(n^{1/p})
. The representation (
1.4
) also allows us to generalize the model further by replacing the base measure
\rho_{p}(x)dx
with a general symmetric probability measure
d\rho(x)
; we postpone the discussion to Section
1.4.3
.

By the strong law of large numbers, when
X_{1},X_{2},\ldots,X_{n}
are i.i.d. from
\rho_{p}
, we have

\displaystyle\left|\!\left|\!\left|\bm{X}\right|\!\right|\!\right|_{n,p}^{p}\text{ is concentrated around }\E|X_{1}|^{p}=1\text{ for large }n.

Thus, motivated by the classical results by
Ellis and
Newman (1978)
, one expects the critical inverse temperature to be
\beta_{c}(p)
, where

(1.5)

\displaystyle\beta_{c}(p):=\nu_{p}^{-2}=\frac{3}{p^{2/p}}\cdot\frac{\Gamma(1+1/p)}{\Gamma(1+3/p)}

for
p\in[1,\infty)
. In Theorem
1.2
and Theorem
1.17
, we prove that
\beta_{c}(p)
is indeed the critical inverse temperature for the
\ell^{p}
constraint Curie–Weiss model and its generalization to the
\ell^{p}
self-scaled model (defined in Section
1.4.3
) for
p\geqslant 2
(see also Subsection
1.6
). Note that
\beta_{c}(2)
coincides with the classical critical inverse temperature,
i.e.,
\beta_{c}(\infty)=1=\beta_{c}(2)
.

However,
\beta_{c}(p)
converges to
3=1/\var(U([-1,1]))
as
p\to\infty
, which is different from
\beta_{c}(\infty)=1=1/\var(U(\{+1,-1\}))
. Here,
U([-1,1])
and
U(\{+1,-1\})
are uniform distributions over
[-1,1]
and
\{+1,-1\}
respectively. This is due to the fact that
S_{n,p}
can converge to the discrete hypercube or the continuous
\ell^{\infty}
-sphere depending on how we take the limit
p\to\infty
. For fixed
n
, if we take the limit
p\to\infty
on the approximate constraint
\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}|x_{i}|^{p}\right)^{1/p}\approx n^{1/p}
, then the limiting constraint defines the
\ell^{\infty}
-sphere
\{\bm{x}\in\mathds{R}^{n}:\max_{i}|x_{i}|\approx 1\}
. On the other hand, for fixed
n
if we take the limit
p\to\infty
on the exact constraint
\sum_{i=1}^{n}|x_{i}|^{p}=n
, then we obtain the discrete hypercube

\left\{\bm{x}\in\mathds{R}^{n}:\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lim_{p\to\infty}|x_{i}|^{p}=n\right\}=\{\bm{x}\in\mathds{R}^{n}:|x_{i}|=1\text{ for all }i=1,2,\ldots,n\}.

Here, we do not pursue a detailed analysis of the transition at
p=\infty
and put it as an open question.

1.4.
Main Results

We first describe the main results before discussing the heuristics, background literature, and ideas behind the proofs. In Section
1.4.1
, we state our main results about the
\ell^{p}
constraint model for general
p>0
. For
p\geqslant 2
, fluctuation results for the magnetization are similar to the classical Curie-Weiss model. However, a direct application of the existing tools does not work in this model due to the structure of the
\ell^{p}
self-scaled Hamiltonian. In Section
1.4.2
, we develop the generalized Hubbard–Stratonovich (GHS) transform based on the existence of a particular additive process with independent increments. GHS transform plays the leading role in analyzing the
\ell^{p}
contained model. In Section
1.4.3
, we extend the analysis to a general class of
\ell^{p}
self-scaled model in a similar spirit to
Ellis and
Newman (1978)
.

1.4.1.
\ell^{p}
constraint model

Our first main result is a phase transition of the Curie–Weiss model under
\ell^{p}
constraint. Let
m_{n,\beta,p}(\bm{\sigma})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{i}
be the magnetization where
\bm{\sigma}
is chosen according to the Gibbs measure (
1.1
) and
\beta_{c}(p)
be defined as (
1.5
). The cumulant generating function of
Z_{p}\sim\rho_{p}
is given by

(1.6)

\displaystyle\psi_{p}(t):=\log\E[e^{tZ_{p}}],\quad t\in\mathds{R}\,\text{ where }Z_{p}\sim\rho_{p}.

First, we have the variational representation of the limiting free energy for arbitrary
p\geqslant 2
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
p\in[2,\infty)
be fixed and
\beta_{c}(p)
be as defined in (
1.5
). For all
\beta\geqslant 0
, we have

(1.7)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\lim_{n\to\infty}&\frac{1}{n}\log\mathcal{Z}_{n,p}(\beta)\\
&=\sup_{z,w\geqslant 0}\left\{\psi_{p}\left(\sqrt{\beta}{zw}{(1+z^{p})^{-1/p}}\right)-\frac{1}{p}\log(1+z^{p})-\frac{p-2}{2p}w^{2p/(p-2)}\right\}\\
&=\sup_{0\leqslant u<1,w\geqslant 0}\left\{\psi_{p}\left(\sqrt{\beta}\cdot uw\right)+\frac{1}{p}\log(1-u^{p})-\frac{p-2}{2p}w^{2p/(p-2)}\right\}.\end{split}

In Theorem
1.17
, we present a general version of this result. The following result says that for
p\geqslant 2
, we have a phase transition of the magnetization
m_{n,\beta,p}
at
\beta=\beta_{c}(p)
similar to the classical model.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
p\in[2,\infty)
be fixed and
\beta_{c}(p)
be as defined in (
1.5
).

a.

If
\beta<\beta_{c}(p)
, then
m_{n,\beta,p}
is concentrated at zero and has a Gaussian limit as
n\to\infty
. More precisely,

\sqrt{n}\cdot m_{n,\beta,p}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{i}\implies\mathrm{N}(0,(\beta_{c}(p)-\beta)^{-1})

as
n\to\infty
and
\mathcal{Z}_{n,p}(\beta)
is uniformly bounded in
n
. In particular,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\mathcal{Z}_{n,p}(\beta)=0.

b.

If
\beta=\beta_{c}(p)
, then

n^{1/4}m_{n,\beta,p}(\bm{\sigma})\implies A^{-1/4}\cdot Z_{4},

where

\displaystyle A=\beta_{c}^{2}\left(\frac{2}{p}+\frac{\kappa_{p}}{6}\right),\quad\kappa_{p}:=3-\E Z_{p}^{4}/(\E Z_{p}^{2})^{2}=3-\Gamma(5/p)\Gamma(1/p)\Gamma(3/p)^{-2}\geqslant 0.

c.

If
\beta>\beta_{c}(p)
, then

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\mathcal{Z}_{n,p}(\beta)>0,

and
m_{n,\beta,p}
is concentrated at
\pm m_{\ast}
where
m_{\ast}=\frac{w_{\ast}}{\sqrt{\beta}}
and
w=w_{\ast}
is the unique positive solution to the equation

\displaystyle\psi_{p}^{\prime}\left(\frac{\sqrt{\beta}w\ }{(1+w^{2})^{1/p}}\right)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\beta}}w(1+w^{2})^{1/p}.

Remark 1.3
.

If
p=2
and
\beta>\beta_{c}(2)=1
, then
\psi_{2}^{\prime}(t)=t
and so
w_{\ast}=\sqrt{\beta-1}
and
m_{\ast}=\sqrt{1-1/\beta}
. However, for general
p>2
, there is no explicit formula for
m_{*}
. It will also be interesting to find rates of convergence for the above distributional limits.

The main tool used in the proof of Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
is the GHS transform introduced in Section
1.4.2
. As an application of the GHS transform (see Lemma
2.1
), we have for all
p\geqslant 2,t\in\mathds{R}
,

\psi_{p}(t)\leqslant\psi_{2}(t\nu_{p})=t^{2}\nu_{p}^{2}/2.

Using Young’s inequality
xy\leqslant\frac{2}{p}x^{p/2}+\frac{p-2}{p}y^{p/(p-2)}
for
p>2,x,y\geqslant 0
, it is an easy exercise to see that for
\beta\leqslant\beta_{c}(p)=1/\nu_{p}^{2}
, we have

\displaystyle\psi_{p}\left(\sqrt{\beta}\cdot uw\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{p}\log(1-u^{p})-\frac{p-2}{2p}w^{2p/(p-2)}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{2}u^{2}w^{2}+\frac{1}{p}\log(1-u^{p})-\frac{p-2}{2p}w^{2p/(p-2)}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{p}u^{p}+\frac{1}{p}\log(1-u^{p})\leqslant 0

for all
w\geqslant 0,u\in[0,1)
. However, it is nontrivial to show that the supremum in equation (
1.7
) is indeed strictly positive for
\beta>\beta_{c}(p)
. The proof is presented in Section
5.2.1
.

The GHS transform allows us to describe the typical spin configuration under the Gibbs measure by introducing hidden random variables.
Note that a similar analysis for typical configuration of the classical Curie-Weiss model can be found in
Papangelou (1989)
.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
\beta<\beta_{c}
and
p\geqslant 2
. Let
(X_{1},X_{2,}\ldots,X_{n})
be random variables under the Gibbs measure

(1.8)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\widehat{\mathcal{Z}}_{n,p}(\beta)}\exp\left(\frac{\beta n}{2}\cdot\frac{\left|n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\right|^{2}}{\left|\!\left|\!\left|\bm{x}\right|\!\right|\!\right|_{n,p}^{2}}\right)\prod_{i=1}^{n}\rho_{p}(x_{i})\,dx_{i}

where
\widehat{\mathcal{Z}}_{n,p}(\beta)
is the normalizing constant. Then, there exist random variables
(Z^{(n)},U^{(n)})
such that conditioned on
(Z^{(n)},U^{(n)})=(z,u)
,
(X_{1},X_{2},\ldots,X_{n})
is an i.i.d. sequence of random variables and

\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}-\sqrt{\beta/\beta_{c}^{2}}\cdot zu\implies\N(0,1/\beta_{c}).

Furthermore,
Z^{(n)}U^{(n)}\implies\N(0,\beta_{c}/(\beta_{c}-\beta))
as
n\to\infty
. Consequently, unconditionally,
\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\implies\N(0,1/(\beta_{c}-\beta))
.

Remark 1.5
.

Let
\beta\leqslant\beta_{c}(p)
and
p\geqslant 2
. The typical behavior of
\bm{\sigma}=(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\ldots,\sigma_{n})
under the Gibbs measure (
1.1
) and that of
\bm{X}=(X_{1},X_{2},\ldots,X_{n})
under (
1.8
) is not exactly the same because
\bm{X}
is not scaled by the
\ell^{p}
norm of
\bm{X}
and the Gibbs measures are slightly different. However, one can see that their asymptotic behaviors agree for the following reasons. First, the difference between the Gibbs measures is the term

\frac{\beta}{2}\cdot\frac{n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{2}}{\left|\!\left|\!\left|\bm{x}\right|\!\right|\!\right|_{n,p}^{2}}

in the exponent. This term is upper bounded by
\beta/2
for
p\geqslant 2
and converges to a constant almost surely as
n\to\infty
. Modulo this difference, we have
\bm{\sigma}=\bm{X}/\left|\!\left|\!\left|\bm{X}\right|\!\right|\!\right|_{n,p}
. Since
\bm{X}/\left|\!\left|\!\left|\bm{X}\right|\!\right|\!\right|_{n,p}
and
\left|\!\left|\!\left|\bm{X}\right|\!\right|\!\right|_{n,p}
are independent and
\left|\!\left|\!\left|\bm{X}\right|\!\right|\!\right|_{n,p}
converges to 1 in probability, we see that the finite-dimensional marginals of
\bm{\sigma}
and
\bm{X}
(and also their magnetizations) have the same asymptotic behavior.

Here, we note that the result in Theorem
1.1
cannot hold for
p<2
, as the right-hand side of equation (
1.7
) is infinity for
p<2
. However, in the
p>1
case, we can use the theory of self-normalized processes (see
de la Peña
et al. (2009)
) to get the following variational representation of the limiting free energy.

Proposition 1.6
.

For
p>1
, we have

(1.9)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\mathcal{Z}_{n,p}(\beta)\\
&\qquad=\sup_{y,c\geqslant 0}\inf_{t\geqslant 0}\left\{\frac{\beta y^{2}}{2}+\psi_{p}\left(\frac{ct}{(1+ty)^{1/p}}\right)-\frac{1}{p}\log(1+ty)-\frac{p-1}{p}tyc^{p/(p-1)}\right\}.\end{split}

For
p\neq 2
, the right-hand side of equation (
1.9
) in Proposition
1.6
can be written in a way similar to equation (
1.7
). By doing a change of variables,
w=(\beta y^{2})^{1/2-1/p},z=(\beta y^{2})^{1/p}c^{-1/(p-1)},t=s\cdot z^{p}/y
, we get

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\mathcal{Z}_{n,p}(\beta)

\displaystyle\qquad=\sup_{z,w\geqslant 0}\inf_{s\geqslant 0}\left\{\psi_{p}\left(\frac{\sqrt{\beta}\cdot szw}{(1+sz^{p})^{1/p}}\right)-\frac{1}{p}\log(1+sz^{p})-\frac{2ps-2s-p}{2p}w^{2p/(p-2)}\right\}.

By taking
s=1
, we thus get for
p>1
,

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\mathcal{Z}_{n,p}(\beta)\leqslant\sup_{z,w\geqslant 0}\left\{\psi_{p}\left(\frac{\sqrt{\beta}\cdot zw}{(1+z^{p})^{1/p}}\right)-\frac{1}{p}\log(1+z^{p})+\frac{2-p}{2p}w^{2p/(p-2)}\right\}.

For
p\in(1,2)
, we can use the GHS transform to get the following result. Since it follows from Theorem
1.17
with a change of variables, we omit the proof.

Theorem 1.7
.

Let
p\in(1,2)
be fixed and
F_{p}(\beta):=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\mathcal{Z}_{n,p}(\beta)
for
\beta\geqslant 0
. We have

\sup_{z\geqslant 0}\left(\psi_{p}(\sqrt{y}z/(1+|z|^{p})^{1/p})-\frac{1}{p}\log(1+|z|^{p})\right)=\sup_{x\geqslant 0}\left\{F_{p}(xy)-\frac{2-p}{2p}x^{\frac{p}{2-p}}\right\}.

In particular, we have

(1.10)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\mathcal{Z}_{n,p}(\beta)\leqslant\inf_{w>0}\sup_{z\geqslant 0}\left\{\psi_{p}\left(\frac{\sqrt{\beta}\cdot zw}{(1+z^{p})^{1/p}}\right)-\frac{1}{p}\log(1+|z|^{p})+\frac{2-p}{2p}w^{\frac{2p}{p-2}}\right\}.

Remark 1.8
.

Note that the right-hand side of (
1.10
) is not a convex function of
\beta
, in general, because it is defined by an infimum of convex functions. Since the limiting free energy is convex in
\beta
, the inequality (
1.10
) may not be a tight bound for the limiting free energy. Proposition
1.6
tells us that the log-partition function converges as
n\to\infty
to a well-defined function of
\beta
. However, the limit is given by supremum and infimum over three variables, so the variational formula does not give us much information about the phase transition and the critical temperature. It is open to find a variational formula for the limiting free energy similar to the
p\geqslant 2
case and the critical temperature for
1<p<2
. We believe that similar to the
p\geqslant 2
case, a phase transition happens in the
p\in(1,2)
case at
\beta=\beta_{c}(p)
.

When
0<p<1
, the log-partition function has a super-linear scaling, and the limiting free energy is an explicit function of
p
.

Theorem 1.9
.

Define
p_{1}:=0
and
p_{k}:=\frac{2\log(1+1/k)}{\log(1+2/(k-1))}
for
k\geqslant 2
. For
p\in(p_{k-1},p_{k}]
, define
\tau(p):=k^{1-2/p}\cdot(k-1)
. For
p\in(0,1)
, we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}n^{1-2/p}\log\mathcal{Z}_{n,p}(\beta)=\frac{\beta}{2}\tau(p).

The result follows from Theorem
1.18
as a particular case with
X\sim\rho_{p}
.

1.4.2.
Generalized Hubbard–Stratonovich transform

The following result plays a crucial role in analyzing the
\ell_{p}
constraint Curie–Weiss model. In the classical Curie–Weiss model, the phase transition and the Gaussian fluctuation can be investigated by the Hubbard–Stratonovich transform, which enables us to linearize the Hamiltonian (see Section
1.8
). It turned out that we can use the following generalized version of the Hubbard–Stratonovich (GHS) transform to linearize the
\ell^{p}
self-scaled Hamiltonian in our model.

Theorem 1.10

(GHS transform)
.

For any
p\geqslant 2
, there exists a positive r.v.
U=U_{p,2}
, such that for all
x\in\mathds{R},y>0
we have

y^{-1/p}\exp\left(x^{2}y^{-2/p}\right)=c_{p}\cdot\E_{U}\int_{\mathds{R}}e^{\sqrt{2}xzU-y\left|z\right|^{p}/p}\,dz

where
c_{p}=\frac{p^{-1/p}}{2\Gamma(1+1/p)}
.

Corollary 1.11
.

In particular, for
(\omega_{i},\nu_{i})_{i\geqslant 1}
i.i.d. random vectors with
\nu_{1}>0
a.s. and
p\geqslant 2
, we have

\displaystyle\E\left[\biggl(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\nu_{i}\biggr)^{-1/p}\exp\left(\frac{\beta}{2n}\biggl(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\omega_{i}\biggr)^{2}\biggl(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\nu_{i}\biggr)^{-2/p}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=c_{p}\cdot\E_{U}\int_{\mathds{R}}\left(\E\exp\left(\sqrt{\frac{\beta}{n}}\cdot\omega_{1}zU-\frac{\nu_{1}}{n}\cdot\frac{1}{p}\left|z\right|^{p}\right)\right)^{n}\,dz.

The key ingredient of the GHS transform is the existence of a positive random variable
U
independent of
X\sim\rho_{p}
,
p\geqslant 2
, satisfying
X\cdot U\sim\mathrm{N}(0,1)=\rho_{2}
. Indeed, we construct a stochastic process
U_{p,q}
indexed by
0<q<p
such that
Z_{p}\cdot U_{p,q}\sim\rho_{q}
where
Z_{p}\sim\rho_{p}
. The proof is based on the following construction of an additive process.

Theorem 1.12
.

There exists an additive process
(Y_{t})_{t>0}
with independent increments such that
Y_{t}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{d}}}{{=}}t\log X_{t}
and
X_{t}\sim\textsc{Gamma}(t,1)
for all
t>0
. Moreover,
Y_{0}\sim-\textsc{Exp}(1)
.

By Theorem
1.12
and letting
U_{p,q}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{d}}}{{=}}\exp(Y_{1/q}-Y_{1/p})
, one can show the following.

Proposition 1.13
.

Let
Z_{p}\sim\rho_{p}
for
p>0
. For every
0<q<p
, there exists a positive r.v.
U=U_{p,q}
independent of
Z_{p}
such that

Z_{p}\cdot U_{p,q}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{d}}}{{=}}Z_{q}.

Moreover,
U_{p,q}
has a strictly positive density
\theta=\theta_{p,q}
on
(0,\infty)
which satisfies

\displaystyle g(x):=\frac{\theta(x)}{x^{\alpha/2}\exp(-x^{\alpha}/\alpha)}\to C_{p,q}:=\frac{pc_{q}}{c_{p}\sqrt{2\pi(p-q)}}\text{ as $x\to\infty$},

where
\alpha=pq/(p-q)
.

Remark 1.14
.

In the case of
p=4
, one can explicitly compute the density of
U_{4,2}
such that
Z_{4}\cdot U_{4,2}\sim\N(0,1)
, where
Z_{4}\sim\rho_{4}
with

\rho_{4}(x)=\sqrt{2}\exp(-x^{4}/4)/\Gamma(1/4),x\in\mathds{R}.

Let
\theta_{4,2}
be the density given by

\theta_{4,2}(u):=\sqrt{2}u^{2}\exp(-u^{4}/4)/\Gamma(3/4),u>0.

One can check that if
\theta_{4,2}
is the density of
U_{4,2}
and
U_{4,2}\perp Z_{4}
, then
U_{4,2}\cdot Z_{4}\sim\N(0,1)
.

Proposition
1.13
says that a random variable
Z_{q}
can be written as a product of two independent random variables. It is well-known (see
Dufresne (2010)
for example) that there are various identities regarding families of Gamma and Beta random variables, for example,

G^{(a)}+G^{(b)}=G^{(a+b)},\qquad B^{(a,b)}G^{(a+b)}=G^{(a)}

where
G^{(a)}
is a Gamma random variable with the shape parameter
a
and the scale parameter 1, and
B^{(a,b)}
is a Beta random variable with parameters
a,b
. This gives rise to the so-called beta-gamma algebra. Since the
p
-th power of
Z_{p}\sim\rho_{p}
is Gamma distributed, the proposition can be understood as a variant of the beta-gamma algebra for the powers of Gamma distributions.

For general
p>q>0
, using Mellin transform and the facts that
\left|Z_{p}\right|^{p}/p\sim\text{Gamma}(1/p,1)
,
\left|Z_{p}\right|\cdot U_{p,q}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{d}}}{{=}}\left|Z_{q}\right|
, one can check that the random variable
p^{1/p}q^{-1/q}\cdot U_{p,q}
has density given by

\frac{1}{2\pi{i\mkern 1.0mu}}\int_{1-{i\mkern 1.0mu}\infty}^{1+{i\mkern 1.0mu}\infty}x^{-z}\cdot\frac{\Gamma(1/p)}{\Gamma(1/q)}\frac{\Gamma(z/q)}{\Gamma(z/p)}\,dz,\text{ for }x>0.

Note that, for
a>0
, we have

\left|\Gamma(a+{i\mkern 1.0mu}b)\right|/\Gamma(a)=\prod_{k=0}^{\infty}\left(1+b^{2}/(a+k)^{2}\right)^{-1/2}\approx\exp(-b^{2}/2a),

which implies that the density is well-defined for
p>q
. The asymptotic behavior of the density also follows from the relation
\left|Z_{p}\right|\cdot U_{p,q}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{d}}}{{=}}\left|Z_{q}\right|
. Using the density formula for the product of two independent rvs, we get for any
x>0
,

\displaystyle{c_{q}}/{c_{p}}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp\left({x^{q}}/{q}-{(x/y)^{p}}/{p}\right)\cdot{\theta(y)}/{y}\cdot dy

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}y^{\alpha/2-1}\exp\left(x^{q}/q-(x/y)^{p}/p-y^{\alpha}/\alpha\right)\cdot g(y)\,dy

where
\alpha=pq/(p-q)
. Doing a change of variable
y=x^{(p-q)/p}\cdot(1+tx^{-q/2}),x=\varepsilon^{-2/q}
, we get for all
\varepsilon>0
,

\displaystyle\int_{-1/\varepsilon}^{\infty}\exp(-\varepsilon^{-2}h(t\varepsilon))\cdot(1+t\varepsilon)^{\alpha/2-1}g\left(\varepsilon^{-2/\alpha}(1+t\varepsilon)\right)\,dt={c_{q}}/{c_{p}}

where

h(u):=(1+u)^{-p}/p+(1+u)^{\alpha}/\alpha-1/q.

Note that,
h(0)=h^{\prime}(0)=0
and
h^{\prime\prime}(u)\geqslant h^{\prime\prime}(0)=p^{2}/(p-q)
for all
u\geqslant 0
. Thus, we have

\displaystyle 2u^{-2}h(u)\geqslant\lim_{u\to 0}2u^{-2}h(u)={p^{2}}/(p-q).

Using DCT with
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
and the Gaussian integral formula, thus we expect that

\sqrt{2\pi(p-q)/p^{2}}\cdot\lim_{x\to\infty}g(x)=c_{q}/c_{p}.

A rigorous argument is presented in Section
4.3
in the proof of Proposition
1.13
.

In the proofs of the main results, we mainly used the existence of a random variable
U_{p,2}
independent of
Z_{p}
in Proposition
1.13
that satisfies
Z_{p}\cdot U_{p,2}\sim\N(0,1)
, whereas we indeed constructed a stochastic process
U_{p,q}
in
p
for fixed
q
in Theorem
1.12
. A geometric interpretation and other possible applications of this stochastic process are of independent interest.

1.4.3.
The
\ell^{p}
self-scaled model

One can generalize the
\ell^{p}
constraint model to the
\ell^{p}
self-scaled model. As we have seen, the
\ell^{p}
constraint Curie–Weiss model can be written in terms of self-normalized process
\widehat{\bm{X}}=(\widehat{X}_{1},\widehat{X}_{2},\ldots,\widehat{X}_{n})
where

\displaystyle\widehat{X}_{i}:=\frac{X_{i}\;\;\;}{\left|\!\left|\!\left|\bm{X}\right|\!\right|\!\right|_{n,p}},\quad i=1,2,\ldots,n,

and the random vector
\bm{X}=(X_{1},X_{2},\ldots,X_{n})
be
n
i.i.d. random variables from
\rho_{p}
. Motivated by (
1.4
), one can consider
\ell^{p}
self-scaled model with a symmetric base measure
\rho
, defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}_{n,p}(\beta;\rho)

\displaystyle:=\E\left[\frac{\beta}{n}\cdot\frac{\sum_{i<j}X_{i}X_{j}}{\left|\!\left|\!\left|\bm{X}\right|\!\right|\!\right|^{2}_{n,p}}\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathds{R}^{n}}\exp\left(\frac{\beta n}{2}\cdot\frac{\left|n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\right|^{2}}{\left|\!\left|\!\left|\bm{x}\right|\!\right|\!\right|_{n,p}^{2}}-\frac{\beta}{2}\cdot\frac{n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{2}}{\left|\!\left|\!\left|\bm{x}\right|\!\right|\!\right|_{n,p}^{2}}\right)\prod_{i=1}^{n}d\rho(x_{i}).

The above
\ell^{p}
constraint model can be generalized with respect to any symmetric base measure
\rho
on
\mathds{R}
with
\mathbb{P}(X=0)=0
,
X\sim\rho
, and finite
p
-th moment under the self-scaled Hamiltonian

\displaystyle H_{n,p}(\bm{x}):=\frac{n}{2}\cdot\frac{\left|n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\right|^{2}}{\left|\!\left|\!\left|\bm{x}\right|\!\right|\!\right|_{n,p}^{2}}-\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{2}}{\left|\!\left|\!\left|\bm{x}\right|\!\right|\!\right|_{n,p}^{2}}.

Assumption 1.15
.

Let
X\sim\rho
with
\E_{\rho}|X|^{p}=1
. In what follows, we assume one of the following:

(A)

\pr(|X|>0)=1
and
\E_{\rho}e^{\theta|X|^{p}}<\infty
for some
\theta>0
.

(B)

\pr(|X|>0)=1
and
\E_{\rho}[|X|^{-\alpha}]<\infty
for some
\alpha>0
.

Note that Assumption
(A)
is stronger than Assumption
(B)
and is needed only for the large deviation argument (Theorem
1.16
). Using the GHS transform, we obtain a simpler variational form of the limiting free energy (Theorem
1.17
) only under Assumption
(B)
. Since the Hamiltonian is normalized in the
\ell^{p}
norm, it is necessary to assume that the base measure
\rho
does not give a mass at 0 and decays fast enough near 0. Note that for
\rho_{p}
distribution, Assumption
(B)
holds for any
0<\alpha<1
. For Assumption
(A)
, one can find the same condition in
Ellis and
Newman (1978)
, where the generalized Curie–Weiss model was investigated.

Large deviation based analysis.
We write
\mathcal{Z}_{n,p}(\beta;\rho)
to denote the corresponding partition function with
\sigma_{i},i\in[n]
being i.i.d. from
\rho
. That is,

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}_{n,p}(\beta;\rho)=\int_{\mathds{R}^{n}}\exp\left(\frac{\beta n}{2}\cdot\frac{\left|n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\right|^{2}}{\left|\!\left|\!\left|\bm{x}\right|\!\right|\!\right|_{n,p}^{2}}-\frac{\beta}{2}\cdot\frac{n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{2}}{\left|\!\left|\!\left|\bm{x}\right|\!\right|\!\right|_{n,p}^{2}}\right)\prod_{i=1}^{n}d\rho(x_{i}).

Since

\frac{n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{2}}{\left|\!\left|\!\left|\bm{x}\right|\!\right|\!\right|_{n,p}^{2}}\leqslant\begin{cases}1,&p\geqslant 2,\\
n^{\tfrac{2}{p}-1},&1<p<2,\end{cases}=o(n)\text{ for }p>1,

as explained in Remark
1.5
, it suffices to consider the following partition function

(1.11)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}}_{n,p}(\beta;\rho)=\int_{\mathds{R}^{n}}\exp\left(\frac{\beta n}{2}\cdot\frac{\left|n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\right|^{2}}{\left|\!\left|\!\left|\bm{x}\right|\!\right|\!\right|_{n,p}^{2}}\right)\prod_{i=1}^{n}d\rho(x_{i}).

Let
F(x,y)=\frac{\beta}{2}x^{2}y^{-2/p}
and
\mathcal{X}:=\{(x,y)\in\mathds{R}^{2}:0\leqslant|x|\leqslant y^{1/p}\}
. Let
(X_{i})_{i\geqslant 1}
be an i.i.d. sequence of random variables with density
\rho
,
S_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}
, and
T_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}|X_{i}|^{p}
. Let
Q_{n}
be the joint distribution of
(S_{n}/n,T_{n}/n)
. The log Laplacian and the Cramér transform of
(X,|X|^{p})
are given by

\displaystyle\psi_{\rho}(u,v):=\log\E_{\rho}e^{uX+v|X|^{p}},\qquad I(x,y):=\sup_{u,v}(ux+vy-\psi_{\rho}(u,v)).

Then, the partition function
\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}}_{n,p}(\beta;\rho)
can be written as

\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}}_{n,p}(\beta;\rho)=\int_{\mathcal{X}}e^{nF(x,y)}Q_{n}(dx,dy).

Let
Q_{n,F}(A)=\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}}_{n,p}(\beta;\rho)^{-1}\int_{A}e^{nF}dQ_{n}
.
We refer to the book
Dembo and
Zeitouni (2010)
for detailed discussions on large deviation principles.

Theorem 1.16
.

Let
X\sim\rho
with
\E_{\rho}|X|^{p}=1
and
p\geqslant 1
be fixed. Assume that Assumption
(A)
holds. Then,
\{Q_{n,F}\}_{n\geqslant 1}
has a large deviation property with speed
n
and a rate function
I_{F}(x,y):=I(x,y)-F(x,y)
and

(1.12)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\mathcal{Z}_{n,p}(\beta;\rho)=\sup_{0\leqslant|x|\leqslant y^{1/p}}\left(F(x,y)-I(x,y)\right).

Furthermore, if
K
is a closed subset in
\mathds{R}^{2}
that does not contain any minimum points of
I_{F}
, then
Q_{n,F}(K)\leqslant e^{-Cn}
for large
n
and some constant
C=C(K)>0
.

Analysis using Generalized Hubbard–Stratonovich Transform.
For
X\sim\rho_{p}
, it is easy to check that

\displaystyle\sup_{0\leqslant|x|\leqslant y^{1/p}}

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{2}\beta x^{2}y^{-2/p}-I(x,y)\right)

\displaystyle=\sup_{0\leqslant x\leqslant y^{1/p}}\inf_{u,v}\left(\frac{1}{2}\beta x^{2}y^{-2/p}+\log\E_{\rho}e^{uX+v|X|^{p}}-ux-vy\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\sup_{z,w\geqslant 0}\left(\log\E_{\rho}e^{\sqrt{\beta}zwX-|zX|^{p}/p}-\frac{p-2}{2p}\cdot w^{2p/(p-2)}\right)

by taking

u=\sqrt{\beta}zw,v=-z^{p}/p\text{ where }x=w^{2p/(p-2)}/(\sqrt{\beta}zw),y=w^{2p/(p-2)}/z^{p}.

It turns out that the equality holds under a weaker assumption on
\rho
.

Theorem 1.17
.

Let
X\sim\rho
with
\E_{\rho}|X|^{p}=1
and Assumption
(B)
holds.

a.

If
p>2
, then

(1.13)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\mathcal{Z}_{n,p}(\beta;\rho)=\sup_{z,w\geqslant 0}\left\{\psi_{\rho}(\sqrt{\beta}zw,-|z|^{p}/p)-\frac{p-2}{2p}\cdot w^{\frac{2p}{p-2}}\right\}.

Furthermore, the limit is strictly positive if
\beta>\beta_{c}(\rho):=1/\E_{\rho}[X^{2}]
and
0
if
\beta\leqslant 1
.

b.

For
p=2
, we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\mathcal{Z}_{n,2}(\beta;\rho)=\sup_{z\geqslant 0}\psi_{\rho}(\sqrt{\beta}z,-z^{2}/2).

Furthermore, the limit is strictly positive for
\beta>\beta_{c}(\rho)
, and the limit is zero for
\beta<\beta_{c}(\rho)
.

c.

If
1<p<2
, then

\displaystyle F_{p}(\beta;\rho)

\displaystyle:=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\mathcal{Z}_{n,p}(\beta;\rho)

\displaystyle=\sup_{y,c\geqslant 0}\inf_{t\geqslant 0}\left\{\frac{1}{2}\beta y^{2}+\psi_{\rho}\left(ct,-ty/p\right)-\frac{p-1}{p}tyc^{p/(p-1)}\right\}

and

\sup_{z\geqslant 0}\psi_{\rho}(\sqrt{y}z,-|z|^{p}/p)=\sup_{x\geqslant 0}\left\{F_{p}(xy;\rho)-\frac{2-p}{2p}x^{\frac{p}{2-p}}\right\}.

In particular,

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\mathcal{Z}_{n,p}(\beta;\rho)\leqslant\inf_{w>0}\sup_{z\geqslant 0}\left\{\psi_{\rho}\left({\sqrt{\beta}zw},-|z|^{p}/p\right)-\frac{p-2}{2p}w^{\frac{2p}{p-2}}\right\}.

Note that the representation (
1.13
) for
p>2
case is a generalization of (
1.16
) in the classical Curie–Weiss model. One can prove a Gaussian CLT for the scaled magnetization in the setting of Theorem
1.17
for
\beta<1,p>2
case. It is interesting to see if the maximizers in the right-hand side of (
1.13
) give us the information of the concentration of the magnetization as the large deviation results do in Theorem
1.16
for all
\beta<\beta_{c}(\rho)
.

For
p\in(0,1)
, we generalize the results in Theorem
1.9
to the
\ell^{p}
self-scaled model as follows.

Theorem 1.18
.

Let
(p_{k})_{k\geqslant 1}
and the function
\tau(\cdot)
be as defined in Theorem
1.9
. Assume that for
X\sim\rho
,
\mathbb{P}(X=0)=0
, and one of the following holds:

1.

for some
C>0
,

, and
\alpha_{1}<2-p
, as

.

1.5.
Heuristics behind the proof

In this section, we provide some heuristics behind the analysis of the
\ell^{p}
constraint model. A similar analysis can also be used for the
\ell^{p}
self-scaled model. Let
\bm{X}=(X_{1},X_{2},\ldots,X_{n})
be distributed with density proportional to

\exp\left(\frac{\beta n}{2}\cdot\frac{\left|n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\right|^{2}}{\left|\!\left|\!\left|\bm{x}\right|\!\right|\!\right|_{n,p}^{2}}-\frac{\beta}{2}\cdot\frac{n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{2}}{\left|\!\left|\!\left|\bm{x}\right|\!\right|\!\right|_{n,p}^{2}}\right)\prod_{i=1}^{n}\rho_{p}(x_{i}).

One can easily check that the conditional density of
X_{i}
given
\{(X_{j})_{j\neq i}=(x_{j})_{j\neq i}\}
at
x
is proportional to

\exp\left(\beta\cdot\frac{n^{-1}\sum_{j\neq i}x_{j}}{\left|\!\left|\!\left|\bm{x}\right|\!\right|\!\right|_{n,p}^{2}}\cdot x-\frac{|x|^{p}}{p}-O(1/n)\right),\qquad x\in\mathds{R}.

Due to the mean-field structure, if the following concentration behavior (unproven) is valid for large
n
:

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}X_{i}\approx a_{1},\text{ and }\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}|X_{i}|^{p}\approx a_{p}^{p},

then we should have
a_{1}=\E Y\text{ and }a_{p}^{p}=\E|Y|^{p},
where
Y
has density proportional to

\exp(\beta\cdot a_{1}/a_{p}^{2}\cdot y-|y|^{p}/p),y\in\mathds{R}.

Defining,
\theta=\beta\cdot a_{1}/a_{p}^{2}
, we must have (see Lemma
2.4
)

a_{1}=\E[Y]=\psi_{p}^{\prime}(\theta),\text{ and }a_{p}^{p}=\E[|Y|^{p}]=1+\theta\psi^{\prime}_{p}(\theta)

where
\psi_{p}(t)=\log\E\exp(tZ_{p})
. Thus,
\theta
should satisfy the equation

(1.14)

\displaystyle\theta

\displaystyle=\beta\cdot\frac{\psi_{p}^{\prime}(\theta)}{(1+\theta\psi^{\prime}_{p}(\theta))^{2/p}}.

Lemma
2.2
shows that the function

t\mapsto\frac{t}{\psi_{p}^{\prime}(t)}\cdot(1+t\psi^{\prime}_{p}(t))^{2/p},\qquad t>0

is strictly increasing for
p\geqslant 2
.
Now,
\theta=0
is always a solution to (
1.14
) and there is a unique positive solution if
p\geqslant 2
and

\displaystyle\beta>\widehat{\beta}_{c}:=\inf_{\theta\geqslant 0}\theta\cdot{(1+\theta\psi^{\prime}_{p}(\theta))^{2/p}}/\psi_{p}^{\prime}(\theta).

Thus, for
\beta\leqslant\widehat{\beta}_{c}
, we expect the magnetization to be concentrated at
0
and a typical coordinate
X_{i}
to be distributed according to
\rho_{p}
. Whereas, for
\beta>\widehat{\beta}_{c}
, the magnetization should not be concentrated at zero. For
p\geqslant 2,\beta>\beta_{c}
, this can be seen from the following computation. For
\theta>0
satisfying (
1.14
) and with
a:=\psi_{p}^{\prime}(\theta),b:=1+\theta\psi^{\prime}_{p}(\theta)
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}_{n,p}(\beta)

\displaystyle\approx\int_{\mathds{R}^{n}}b^{-n/p}\exp\left(\frac{\beta n}{2}\cdot\frac{\left|n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\right|^{2}}{\left|\!\left|\!\left|\bm{x}\right|\!\right|\!\right|_{n,p}^{2}}-\frac{1}{pb}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|x_{i}|^{p}\right)c_{p}^{n}\prod_{i=1}^{n}dx_{i}

\displaystyle\geqslant\exp\left(n\psi_{p}(\theta)-\frac{n}{2}\theta a+\frac{n}{p}(b-1)-\frac{n}{p}\log b\right)\cdot

\displaystyle\qquad\int_{\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\approx na+O(\sqrt{n}),\sum_{i=1}^{n}|x_{i}|^{p}\approx nb+O(\sqrt{n})}\prod_{i=1}^{n}c_{p}e^{\theta x_{i}-|x_{i}|^{p}/p-\psi_{p}(\theta)}dx_{i}.

Note that the last integral is
\Theta(1)
by CLT and by Lemma
2.2
, we have

\displaystyle\psi_{p}(\theta)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\theta a+\frac{\theta}{p}(b-1)-\frac{1}{p}\log b

\displaystyle=\psi_{p}(\theta)-\frac{1}{2}\theta\psi^{\prime}_{p}(\theta)+\frac{1}{p}\theta\psi^{\prime}_{p}(\theta)-\frac{1}{p}\log(1+\theta\psi^{\prime}_{p}(\theta))

\displaystyle\geqslant\psi_{p}(\theta)-\frac{1}{2}\theta\psi_{p}^{\prime}(\theta)=\int_{0}^{\theta}t\cdot\left(\frac{\psi_{p}^{\prime}(t)}{t}-\frac{\psi_{p}^{\prime}(\theta)}{\theta}\right)dt>0.

The generalized Hubbard–Stratonovich transform is used to understand the distribution of the typical coordinates in a structured way and make the above heuristics rigorous.

1.6.
Discussion on the Critical Temperatures

We have seen that the
\ell^{p}
constraint Curie–Weiss model for
p>2
has the critical inverse temperature
\beta_{c}(p)
as in (
1.5
) in a sense that if
\beta<\beta_{c}(p)
then the limiting free energy

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\mathcal{Z}_{n,p}(\beta)=0

and for
\beta>\beta_{c}(p)
then the limiting free energy is strictly positive.

In the
\ell^{p}
self-scaled model with general base measure
\rho
, Theorem
1.17
(a) tells us that the limiting free energy is strictly positive when
\beta>\beta_{c}(\rho):=1/\var_{\rho}(X)
. However, it is unclear that
\beta_{c}(\rho)
is indeed the critical inverse temperature. To show this, it follows from (
1.13
) that we need to show

(1.15)

\displaystyle\E\exp\left(\sqrt{\beta}zwX-z^{p}|X|^{p}/p\right)\leqslant\exp\left(\frac{p-2}{2p}w^{2p/(p-2)}\right)

for all
z,w\geqslant 0,\beta\leqslant\beta_{c}(\rho)
, where
X\sim\rho
.

One can derive the inequality for
\rho=\rho_{p}
, as mentioned in the discussion after Theorem
1.2
. The inequality (
1.15
) holds true for
\rho=\frac{1}{2}\delta_{1}+\frac{1}{2}\delta_{-1}
, which boils down to the classical Curie-Weiss model. Indeed,

\displaystyle\psi_{\rho}(\sqrt{\beta_{c}(\rho)}zw,-z^{p}/p)

\displaystyle=\E_{\rho}\exp\left(\sqrt{\beta_{c}(\rho)}zwX-z^{p}|X|^{p}/p\right)

\displaystyle=\cosh(zw)e^{-z^{p}/p}\leqslant\exp\left(\frac{z^{2}w^{2}}{2}-\frac{z^{p}}{p}\right)\leqslant\exp\left(\frac{p-2}{2p}w^{2p/(p-2)}\right).

Here, we used the bound
\cosh(t)\leqslant\exp(t^{2}/2)
and Young’s inequality
ab\leqslant a^{q}/q+b^{r}/r
for
q=p/2
and
1/q+1/r=1
. The inequality (
1.15
) remains open for general symmetric measures. Note that one can show (
1.15
) for
\beta\leqslant 1
using Young’s inequality.

For
p=2
case, Theorem
1.17
(b) shows that
\beta_{c}(\rho)
is the critical inverse temperature for the
\ell^{p}
self-scaled model. In the case where
1<p<2
, it is not clear whether
\beta_{c}(p)
is the critical inverse temperature even for the
\rho_{p}
case.

For
\rho=\rho_{1}
, one can show that
\beta_{c}(1)=\frac{1}{2}
is the critical inverse temperature. For
X\sim\rho_{1}
and
0\leqslant x<y
, we have

\displaystyle I(x,y)

\displaystyle=\sup_{u,v}(ux+vy-\log\E\exp(uX+v|X|))

\displaystyle=\sup_{0\leqslant u<w}(ux+(1-w)y-\log w+\log(w^{2}-u^{2}))

\displaystyle=y-\log y+\sup_{0\leqslant s<1,z>0}(zst-z+\log z+\log(1-s^{2}))

\displaystyle=y-1-\log y+\sup_{0\leqslant s<1}(\log(1-s^{2})-\log(1-st))

\displaystyle=y-1-\log y-\log\frac{1}{2}(1+\sqrt{1-t^{2}})

where
t:=x/y
and the third equality follows by a change of variable
t=u/w,z=wy
. In particular, we get

\displaystyle\sup_{0\leqslant x\leqslant y}(\beta x^{2}/2y^{2}-I(x,y))=\sup_{0\leqslant t\leqslant 1}\left(\frac{1}{2}\beta t^{2}+\log\frac{1}{2}(1+\sqrt{1-t^{2}})\right).

By changing the variable
t=\sin\theta
, one can quickly check that the optimizer is attained at
t_{*}
where

t_{*}^{2}:=\frac{2(2-1/\beta)_{+}}{\sqrt{\beta^{2}+4\beta}+2-\beta}.

If
\beta<\beta_{c}(1)=\frac{1}{2}
, then
t_{*}=0
and the limiting free energy is zero. On the other hand, if
\beta>\beta_{c}(1)=\frac{1}{2}
, then one can see that the limiting free energy is strictly positive.

1.7.
Related Literature

If we remove the
\ell^{p}
scaling in the partition function (
1.4
), our model boils down to the generalized Curie–Weiss model in
Ellis and
Newman (1978)
, where the base measures are given by the product measure with sub-Gaussian tail decay. From this perspective, our model generalizes to the one in
Ellis and
Newman (1978)
by introducing an extra dependence structure on the spin configuration, which arises from the
\ell^{p}
constraint. Under Gaussian tail decay assumption for
\rho
, Ellis and Newman
Ellis and
Newman (1978)
proved that the Curie–Weiss model without constraint exhibits the phase transition at
\beta_{c}(\rho)=1/\var_{\rho}(X)
. It was shown in
Eisele and
Ellis (1988)
that the generalized Curie–Weiss model can have a sequence of phase transitions at different critical temperatures.

Since the self-organized criticality model in
Cerf and Gorny (2016)
is the special case where
p=2
and
\beta=\beta_{c}(2)
is the critical inverse temperature, our model can also be considered as a generalization of the self-organized criticality model. Detailed analysis at criticality
\beta_{c}(2)
was investigated in
Gorny (2014)
;
Gorny and
Varadhan (2015)
.

We simplified our model with self-scaled random vectors with
\rho_{p}
distribution and extended to a general
\ell^{p}
self-scaled Curie–Weiss model. From this perspective, our model is closely connected to self-normalized processes in
de la Peña
et al. (2009)
.

Corresponding disordered model is known as
\ell^{p}
–Grothendieck spin–glass model
Chen and Sen (2023)
;
Dominguez (2022)
;
Khot and Naor (2012)
. For an
n\times n
matrix
A
, it is well-known that the maximum of
\langle Ax,x\rangle
for
\|x\|_{2}\leqslant 1
is the largest eigenvalue of
\frac{1}{2}(A+A^{T})
. A natural question is the optimization of
\langle Ax,x\rangle
for
\|x\|_{p}\leqslant 1
and
1\leqslant p\leqslant\infty
. This is called the
\ell^{p}
-Grothendieck problem. The same optimization problem with random matrix
A
, where the entries are i.i.d. standard Gaussian, has been studied in
Chen and Sen (2023)
;
Dominguez (2022)
;
Khot and Naor (2012)
. The limiting free energy is given in
Chen and Sen (2023)
.

The distribution of a typical point from the intersection of high-dimensional symmetric convex bodies shows phase transition.
Chatterjee (2017)
considered the uniform probability distribution on the intersection of
\ell^{1}
-ball and
\ell^{2}
-ball and showed that phase transitions and localization phenomena occur depending on the radius of
\ell^{2}
-ball. Suppose that
X=(X_{1},X_{2},\ldots,X_{n})
is a random vector uniformly chosen from
\{x\in\mathds{R}^{n}:\sum_{i=1}^{n}|x_{i}|=n,\sum_{i=1}^{n}|x_{i}|^{2}=bn\}
for
b>1
. If
1<b\leqslant 2
, then the first
k
random variables in
(X_{1},X_{2},\ldots,X_{n})
converges to an i.i.d. sequence of some random variables depending on
b
in law and in joint moments as
n\to\infty
. On the other hand, if
b>2
, then
(X_{1},X_{2},\ldots,X_{k})
converges to an i.i.d sequence of exponential random variables, and the localization phenomenon appears in a sense that most of the mass is concentrated on the maximum component of
(X_{1},X_{2},\ldots,X_{n})
and the other components have negligible masses as
n\to\infty
.

The result of
Chatterjee (2017)
has been extended to multiple constraints by
Nam (2020)
. He considered the microcanonical ensembles with multiple constraints with unbounded observables and showed that a sequence
(X_{1},X_{2},\ldots,X_{n})
sampled from the multiple constraints exhibits a phase transition, localization, and delocalization phenomena. Note that the microcanonical ensemble with unbounded multiple constraints has been of great interest due to its connection to nonlinear Schrödinger equations.

1.8.
The Classical Curie–Weiss Model

Curie–Weiss model or Ising model on the complete graph is one of the simplest spin models exhibiting phase transition with respect to the temperature in terms of magnetization or average spin. The spins are arranged on the vertices of the complete graph
K_{n}=([n],\mathcal{E}_{n})
on
n
-vertices. The spin at vertex
i\in[n]
is denoted by
\sigma_{i}
and the Hamiltonian corresponding to a spin configuration
\bm{\sigma}=(\sigma_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
is given by

\displaystyle H_{n}(\bm{\sigma})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i<j}\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}=\frac{n}{2}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{i}\right)^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\quad\text{ for }\bm{\sigma}\in S_{n,\infty}:=\{-1,+1\}^{n}.

The Gibbs measure on
\{-1,+1\}^{n}
at inverse temperature
\beta>0
is defined as

\displaystyle d\mu_{n,\beta}(\bm{\sigma})=\mathcal{Z}_{n}(\beta)^{-1}\exp(\beta H_{n}(\bm{\sigma}))d\bm{\sigma}

where
d\bm{\sigma}
is the uniform measure on
\{-1,+1\}^{n}
and
\mathcal{Z}_{n}(\beta)
is the partition function. Several approaches are available in the literature to analyze phase transition in the Curie–Weiss model. Next, we describe three of the approaches.

1.8.1.
Combinatorial approach

One can prove the phase transition of the magnetization using direct combinatorial arguments, equivalent to large deviation analysis. Indeed, it follows from an elementary counting and Stirling’s formula that for any closed subset
K\subset[-1,1]
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\log\mu_{n,\beta}(\{m(\bm{\sigma})\in K\})=-\min_{x\in K}\left\{(I(x)-F(x))-\min_{y\in[-1,1]}(I(y)-F(y))\right\}

where
I(x)=\left(\tfrac{1+x}{2}\right)\log\left(\tfrac{1+x}{2}\right)+\left(\tfrac{1-x}{2}\right)\log\left(\tfrac{1-x}{2}\right)
and
F(x)=\tfrac{\beta x^{2}}{2}
. Thus, the magnetization is concentrated on the minimum points of
I-F
, and the minimizer satisfies
x=\tanh(\beta x)
. If
\beta<1
, then the equation has the unique solution
x=0
so that the magnetization is concentrated at
0
. On the other hand, for
\beta>1
, the magnetization is concentrated at
\pm x_{\beta}^{\ast}
, which are nonzero solutions to
x=\tanh(\beta x)
.

1.8.2.
Exchangeable pair approach

Another approach is to use the Glauber dynamics. Here, we describe Stein’s method for exchangeable pairs using Glauber dynamics. Suppose
\bm{\sigma}
is sampled from
\mu_{n,\beta}
and
I\in[n]
is chosen at random. We define
\bm{\sigma}^{\prime}
as follows. If
I=j
, then
\sigma_{i}^{\prime}:=\sigma_{i}
for
i\neq j
and
\sigma_{j}^{\prime}
is sampled from
\mu_{n,\beta}
conditioned to
(\sigma_{i})_{i\neq j}
. Then,
(\bm{\sigma},\bm{\sigma}^{\prime})
is an exchangeable pair. For
W=n^{-1/2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{i}
and
W^{\prime}=n^{-1/2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{i}^{\prime}
, we get
\E[W-W^{\prime}\mid W]\approx n^{-1}(1-\beta)W
for
\beta<1
and
\E[W-W^{\prime}\mid W]\approx n^{-2}W^{3}/3
for
\beta=1
. Classical Stein’s method argument shows that
W
converges to normal for
\beta<1
, and it converges to a non-normal distribution for
\beta=1
under suitable scaling (see
Chatterjee and
Shao (2011)
). We also note that
Chatterjee (2007)
used this exchangeable pair approach to obtain concentration inequalities for the magnetization.

1.8.3.
Hubbard–Stratonovich transform

One can study the phase transition of the Curie–Weiss model using analytic methods. Hubbard–Stratonovich transform is

\displaystyle e^{{x^{2}}/{2}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{\mathds{R}}\exp(-{y^{2}}/{2}+xy)\,dy.

Since
H_{n}(\bm{\sigma})=\tfrac{1}{2n}(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{i})^{2}-\tfrac{1}{2n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{i}^{2}=\tfrac{1}{2n}(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{i})^{2}-\tfrac{1}{2}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}_{n}(\beta)=\E[e^{\beta H_{n}(\bm{\sigma})}]

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{-{\beta}/{2}}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{\mathds{R}}\exp\left(-y^{2}/{2}+n\log\cosh\left(\sqrt{\beta/n}\cdot y\right)\right)\,dy

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{-{\beta}/{2}}\sqrt{n}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{\mathds{R}}\exp\left(n\left(-{y^{2}}/{2}+\log\cosh\left(\sqrt{\beta}\cdot y\right)\right)\right)\,dy.

Thus,

(1.16)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\mathcal{Z}_{n}(\beta)=\sup_{y}\left\{-{y^{2}}/{2}+\log\cosh\left(\sqrt{\beta}y\right)\right\}.

If
\beta<1
,
W=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{i}
, and
t>0
, it follows from Hubbard–Stratonovich transform and the dominated convergence theorem that

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\E_{\mu_{n,\beta}}[e^{tW}]

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\int_{\mathds{R}}\exp\left(-{y^{2}}/{2}+n\log\cosh\left(n^{-1/2}(\sqrt{\beta}\cdot y-t)\right)\right)\,dy}{\int_{\mathds{R}}\exp\left(-{y^{2}}/{2}+n\log\cosh\left(\sqrt{\beta/n}\cdot y\right)\right)\,dy}

\displaystyle=\exp\left({t^{2}}/{2(1-\beta)}\right).

This shows that
W
converges to a normal distribution as
n\to\infty
. For further details on the classical Curie–Weiss model, we refer the reader to
(
Friedli and
Velenik, 2018
, Chap. 2)
.

In this paper, we mainly focus on developing and applying the generalized Hubbard–Stratonovich (GHS) transform to our model and using the classical large deviation tools. Appropriate stationary dynamics and application of Stein’s method will be discussed in a future article.

2.1.
Cumulant generating function

Let
0<p<\infty
and
Z_{p}
be a random variable with density
\rho_{p}(x)=c_{p}e^{-|x|^{p}/p}
. Note that
\E[Z_{p}^{2}]=\nu_{p}^{2}=1/\beta_{c}
and
\E[|Z_{p}|^{p}]=1
. Recall the cumulant-generating function

\psi_{p}(t)=\log\E[e^{tZ_{p}}].

Lemma 2.1
.

For all real number
\ell\geqslant 2
we have

\nu_{p}^{-\ell}\E\left|Z_{p}\right|^{\ell}\text{ is decreasing in }p>0.

In particular, for all
t\in\mathds{R},p\geqslant 2
we have
\psi_{p}(t)\leqslant\frac{1}{2}\nu_{p}^{2}t^{2}
and
\lim_{t\to 0}\psi_{p}(t)/t^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\nu_{p}^{2}
.

Proof.

Fix
\ell\geqslant 2
and
p\geqslant q>0
. To prove that
\nu_{p}^{-\ell}\E\left|Z_{p}\right|^{\ell}\leqslant\nu_{q}^{-\ell}\E\left|Z_{q}\right|^{\ell}
or equivalently,
\E\left|Z_{q}\right|^{\ell}/\E\left|Z_{p}\right|^{\ell}\geqslant(\E Z_{q}^{2}/\E Z_{p}^{2})^{\ell/2}
, we use Proposition
1.13
. We have
Z_{q}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{d}}}{{=}}Z_{p}\cdot U_{p,q}
where
U_{p,q}
is independent of
Z_{p}
. Thus,

\E\left|Z_{q}\right|^{\ell}/\E\left|Z_{p}\right|^{\ell}=\E U_{p,q}^{\ell}\geqslant(\E U_{p,q}^{2})^{\ell/2}=(\E Z_{q}^{2}/\E Z_{p}^{2})^{\ell/2}.

Using symmetry of
Z_{p}
, we have, for all
t\in\mathds{R}
,

\E\exp(tZ_{p})=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{2k}}{(2k)!}\E Z_{p}^{2k}.

Combining, we get for
p\geqslant 2
,

\E\exp(tZ_{p})\leqslant\E\exp(t\nu_{p}Z_{2})=\exp(\nu_{p}^{2}t^{2}/2).

This completes the proof.

\blacksquare

Lemma 2.2
.

The function
\psi_{p}^{\prime}(t)/t
is decreasing in
t\in[0,\infty)
for
p\geqslant 2
, and increasing in
t\in[0,\infty)
for
0<p<2
.

Corollary 2.3
.

Let
X
be a r.v. with density proportional to
\exp(x-c|x|^{p}),x\in\mathds{R}
for some
c>0,p\geqslant 2
. Then, we have

\var(X)\leqslant\E X.

Proof of Lemma
2.2
.

If
p=2
, then
\psi_{2}^{\prime}(t)/t=\E Z_{2}^{2}
is constant and the result holds. For the rest of the proof, we fix
p>0
.

Since
|Z_{p}|^{p}/p
is Gamma distributed with parameters
1/p
and
1
, we get that
\E Z_{p}^{2k}=p^{(2k+1)/p}\Gamma((2k+1)/p)/\Gamma(1/p)
for all

. In particular, we have

\displaystyle e^{\psi_{p}(t)}=\E e^{tZ_{p}}

\displaystyle=\frac{p^{1/p}}{\Gamma(1/p)}\cdot\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{2k}p^{2k/p}}{(2k)!}\cdot\Gamma((2k+1)/p),\qquad t>0.

Thus, it is enough to show that the function
f(t):=\log M^{\prime}(t)-\log(tM(t))
is decreasing (resp. increasing) for
p>2
(resp.
p<2

\displaystyle M(t)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{2k}}{(2k)!}\cdot\Gamma((2k+1)/p),\qquad t>0.

We have,

\displaystyle tM(t)

\displaystyle=p\cdot\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{2k+1}}{(2k+1)!}\cdot\Gamma((2k+1+p)/p)=p\cdot\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{2k+1}}{(2k+1)!}\cdot a_{p}(k),

\displaystyle M^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{2k+1}}{(2k+1)!}\cdot\Gamma((2k+3)/p)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{2k+1}}{(2k+1)!}\cdot a_{2}(k).

where

for

M^{\prime\prime}(t)\cdot tM(t)-M^{\prime}(t)\cdot(tM(t))^{\prime}\leqslant 0.
Moreover, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p}\cdot\left[M^{\prime\prime}(t)\cdot tM(t)-(tM(t))^{\prime}\cdot M^{\prime}(t)\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{2k}}{(2k)!}\cdot a_{2}(k)\cdot\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{2\ell+1}}{(2\ell+1)!}\cdot a_{p}(\ell)-\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{2k}}{(2k)!}\cdot a_{p}(k)\cdot\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{2\ell+1}}{(2\ell+1)!}\cdot a_{2}(\ell).

For

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{1}{(2k)!(2(n-k)+1)!}\biggl(a_{2}(k)a_{p}(n-k)-a_{p}(k)a_{2}(n-k)\biggr)

\displaystyle\qquad=\frac{1}{2}\Bigg(\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{2k+1}{(2k+1)!(2(n-k)+1)!}(a_{2}(k)a_{p}(n-k)-a_{p}(k)a_{2}(n-k))

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{2(n-k)+1}{(2k+1)!(2(n-k)+1)!}(a_{2}(n-k)a_{p}(k)-a_{p}(n-k)a_{2}(k))\Bigg)

\displaystyle\qquad=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{(k-(n-k))a_{2}(n-k)a_{2}(k)}{(2k+1)!(2(n-k)+1)!}\left(\frac{a_{p}(k)}{a_{2}(k)}-\frac{a_{p}(n-k)}{a_{2}(n-k)}\right).

Since the Gamma function is log-convex, one can check that the function

\blacksquare

(Z^{(n)},U^{(n)})
is given by

\displaystyle f_{Z^{(n)},U^{(n)}}(z,u)=\frac{c_{p}}{\widehat{\mathcal{Z}}_{n,p}(\beta)}\exp\left(n\left(\psi_{p}\left(\sqrt{\frac{\beta}{n}}\frac{zu}{(1+\frac{|z|^{p}}{n})^{1/p}}\right)-\frac{1}{p}\log\left(1+\frac{|z|^{p}}{n}\right)\right)\right)\theta(u),

it is easy to see that
X_{1},X_{2},\ldots,X_{n}
are conditionally i.i.d. given
Z^{(n)}=z,U^{(n)}=u
with the common density (
2.1
). By change of variables,

\displaystyle\E[X_{1}\mid Z^{(n)}=z,U^{(n)}=u]

\displaystyle=\left(1+\frac{|z|^{p}}{n}\right)^{-1/p}\int_{\mathds{R}}x\exp(\theta x-|x|^{p}/p-\psi_{p}(\theta))\,dx

\displaystyle=\left(1+\frac{|z|^{p}}{n}\right)^{-1/p}\psi_{p}^{\prime}(\theta),

\displaystyle\theta

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{\beta}{n}}\frac{zu}{(1+\frac{|z|^{p}}{n})^{1/p}}.

Since
\theta\to 0
as
n\to\infty
and
\lim_{\theta\to 0}\psi_{p}^{\prime}(\theta)/\theta=\nu_{p}^{2}=1/\beta_{c}
, we get

\displaystyle\E[X_{1}\mid Z^{(n)}=z,U^{(n)}=u]

\displaystyle=\left(1+\frac{|z|^{p}}{n}\right)^{-1/p}\psi_{p}^{\prime}(\theta)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\left(\sqrt{\frac{\beta}{\beta_{c}^{2}}}\cdot zu+o(1)\right).

Similarly, since
\psi_{p}^{\prime\prime}(\theta)\to\nu_{p}^{2}
as
\theta\to 0
,

\displaystyle\var(X_{1}\mid Z^{(n)}=z,U^{(n)}=u)

\displaystyle=\left(1+\frac{|z|^{p}}{n}\right)^{-2/p}\psi_{p}^{\prime\prime}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\left(1+\frac{|z|^{p}}{n}\right)^{-2/p}\nu_{p}^{2}\cdot(1+o(1))=\frac{1}{\beta_{c}}+o(1).

This completes the proof.

\blacksquare

3.
Proofs for the
\ell^{p}
constraint Curie–Weiss model

In this section, we provide the proofs of Theorems
1.2
,
1.4
, and Proposition
1.6
, where the limiting free energy, Gaussian fluctuation, and the typical configuration of the
\ell^{p}
constraint Curie–Weiss model are discussed.

3.1.
Proof of Theorem
1.2
(a)

First, we show that
\mathcal{Z}_{n,p}(\beta)
is uniformly bounded in
n
. Fix
p\geqslant 2
. Let
X,X_{1},X_{2},\ldots,X_{n}
be i.i.d. random variables from
\rho_{p}
. Define the random variables

S_{n}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\text{ and }T_{n}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|X_{i}\right|^{p}.

Now, we define

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{Z}}_{n,p}(\beta)

\displaystyle:=\E\left[\bigl(T_{n}/n\bigr)^{-1/p}\exp\left(\frac{\beta}{2n}\cdot S_{n}^{2}\cdot\bigl(T_{n}/n\bigr)^{-2/p}\right)\right].

Since
T_{n}/p
is a Gamma random variable with shape parameter
n/p
and the rate parameter 1, we have

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{Z}}_{n,p}(0)=n^{1/p}\E[T_{n}^{-1/p}]=\frac{(n/p)^{1/p}\cdot\Gamma((n-1)/p)}{\Gamma(n/p)}\leqslant(n/(n-p))^{1/p}\leqslant\exp(1/(n-p)),

where the first inequality follows by convexity of the log-Gamma function (also known as Gautschi’s inequality) and thus, is uniformly bounded for
n>p
.

Let
\beta>0
and
U\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{d}}}{{=}}U_{p,2}
independent of
X
such that
X\cdot U\sim\N(0,1)
which is constructed in Proposition
1.13
. Using Theorem
1.10
, we get

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{Z}}_{n,p}(\beta)=c_{p}\cdot\E_{U}\int_{\mathds{R}}\left(\E_{X}\exp\left(\sqrt{\frac{\beta}{n}}\cdot XzU-\frac{1}{np}\left|Xz\right|^{p}\right)\right)^{n}\,dz.

Using the fact that
X\sim\rho_{p}
, we simplify

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{Z}}_{n,p}(\beta)

\displaystyle=c_{p}\cdot\E_{U}\int_{\mathds{R}}(1+|z|^{p}/n)^{-n/p}\exp\left(n\psi_{p}\left(\sqrt{\beta/n}\cdot(1+|z|^{p}/n)^{-1/p}\cdot zU\right)\right)\,dz

\displaystyle=c_{p}\cdot\E_{U}\int_{\mathds{R}}\exp\left(-\frac{n}{p}f(|z|^{p}/n)-\frac{|z|^{p}}{p}\cdot\frac{1}{1+|z|^{p}/2n}+n\psi_{p}\left(\sqrt{\beta/n}\cdot\frac{zU}{(1+|z|^{p}/n)^{1/p}}\right)\right)\,dz,

where

f(x)=\log(1+x)-2x/(2+x)

is a positive increasing function on
[0,\infty)
.

Note that,

f^{\prime}(x)=1/(1+x)-4/(2+x)^{2}=x^{2}/(1+x)(2+x)^{2}>0\text{ for }x>0.

Heuristically, the term inside the exponential is approximately
-|z|^{p}/p+{\beta\nu_{p}^{2}}/{2}\cdot z^{2}U^{2}
. Thus, using the fact that
XU\sim\N(0,1)
, the upper bound is roughly

\E_{X,U}\exp({\beta\nu_{p}^{2}}/{2}\cdot X^{2}U^{2})=(1-\beta\nu_{p}^{2})^{-1/2}<\infty\text{ when }\beta<\beta_{c}=\nu_{p}^{-2}.

To rigorously upper bound the term, choose
\delta>0
such that
\beta^{\prime}:=\beta(1+\delta)^{2/p}<\beta_{c}(p)=1/\nu_{p}^{2}
and let
\widehat{\mathcal{Z}}_{n,p}(\beta)=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}J_{i}
where

\displaystyle J_{0}

\displaystyle=c_{p}\cdot\E_{U}\int_{|z|^{p}\leqslant 2n\delta}K_{n,p,\beta}(z,U)\,dz,

\displaystyle J_{i}

\displaystyle=c_{p}\cdot\E_{U}\int_{2^{i}n\delta\leqslant|z|^{p}\leqslant 2^{i+1}n\delta}K_{n,p,\beta}(z,U)\,dz,\qquad i\geqslant 1,

\displaystyle K_{n,p,\beta}(z,U)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-\frac{n}{p}f(|z|^{p}/n)-\frac{|z|^{p}}{p(1+{|z|^{p}}/{2n})}+n\psi_{p}\left(\sqrt{\frac{\beta}{n}}\cdot\frac{zU}{(1+|z|^{p}/n)^{1/p}}\right)\right).

For
i=0
, we have

\displaystyle J_{0}\leqslant c_{p}\cdot\E_{U}\int_{\mathds{R}}\exp\left(-\frac{|z|^{p}}{p}\cdot\frac{1}{1+\delta}+n\psi_{p}\left(\sqrt{\beta/n}\cdot{zU}\right)\right)\,dz.

Since
Z_{p}\cdot U=\eta\sim\N(0,1)
,

\displaystyle J_{0}\leqslant(1+\delta)^{1/p}\E_{\eta}\exp\left(n\psi_{p}\left(\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\beta(1+\delta)^{2/p}}\cdot{\eta}\right)\right)<\infty.

In general, for
i\geqslant 1
we get

\displaystyle J_{i}

\displaystyle\leqslant c_{p}e^{-nf(2^{i}\delta)/p}\cdot\E_{U}\int_{\mathds{R}}\exp\left(-\frac{|z|^{p}}{p}\cdot\frac{1}{1+2^{i}\delta}+n\psi_{p}\left(\sqrt{\beta/n}\cdot\frac{zU}{(1+2^{i}\delta)^{1/p}}\right)\right)\,dz

\displaystyle\leqslant 2^{1/p}e^{-(n-1)f(2^{i}\delta)/p}\E_{\eta}\exp\left(n\psi_{p}\left(\sqrt{\beta/n}\cdot\eta\right)\right).

Thus, we get that
\widehat{\mathcal{Z}}_{n,p}(\beta)
is uniformly bounded in
n
when
\beta<\beta_{c}
.

To see the Gaussian fluctuation of the magnetization, let

\displaystyle d\widehat{\mu}_{n,\beta,p}

\displaystyle:=\widehat{\mathcal{Z}}_{n,p}(\beta)^{-1}\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{n}|x_{i}|^{p}/n\bigg)^{-1/p}

\displaystyle\quad\exp\left(\frac{\beta}{2n}\cdot\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\bigg)^{2}\cdot\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{n}|x_{i}|^{p}/n\bigg)^{-2/p}\right)\prod_{i=1}^{n}\rho_{p}(x_{i})dx_{i}.

For
u,v>0
, we consider

\displaystyle\left\langle\exp\left(-u\frac{S_{n}}{\sqrt{n}}-v\frac{T_{n}}{n}\right)\right\rangle_{\widehat{\mu}_{n,\beta,p}}

\displaystyle\qquad=\widehat{\mathcal{Z}}_{n,p}(\beta)^{-1}\E\left[\bigl(T_{n}/n\bigr)^{-1/p}\exp\left(\frac{\beta}{2n}\cdot S_{n}^{2}\cdot\bigl(T_{n}/n\bigr)^{-2/p}-u\frac{S_{n}}{\sqrt{n}}-v\frac{T_{n}}{n}\right)\right].

Using Theorem
1.10
, we get

\displaystyle\E\left[\bigl(T_{n}/n\bigr)^{-1/p}\exp\left(\frac{\beta}{2n}\cdot S_{n}^{2}\cdot\bigl(T_{n}/n\bigr)^{-2/p}-u\frac{S_{n}}{\sqrt{n}}-v\frac{T_{n}}{n}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=c_{p}\cdot\E_{U}\int_{\mathds{R}}\left(\E_{X}\exp\left(\frac{X}{\sqrt{n}}\cdot(\sqrt{\beta}zU-u)-\frac{\left|X\right|^{p}}{np}(\left|z\right|^{p}+vp)\right)\right)^{n}\,dz

\displaystyle=c_{p}\cdot\E_{U}\int_{\mathds{R}}\left(1+\frac{(|z|^{p}+vp)}{n}\right)^{-n/p}\exp\left(n\psi_{p}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\cdot\frac{(\sqrt{\beta}zU-u)}{(1+(|z|^{p}+vp)/n)^{1/p}}\right)\right)\,dz

\displaystyle=c_{p}\cdot\E_{U}\int_{\mathds{R}}\widetilde{K}_{n,p,\beta}(z,U)\,dz

where
f(x)=\log(1+x)-2x/(2+x)
and

\displaystyle\widetilde{K}_{n,p,\beta}(z,U)

\displaystyle\quad=\exp\left(-\frac{n}{p}f\left(\frac{|z|^{p}+vp}{n}\right)-\frac{(|z|^{p}+vp)}{p\left(1+\frac{|z|^{p}+vp}{2n}\right)}+n\psi_{p}\left(\frac{(\sqrt{\beta}zU-u)}{\sqrt{n}\left(1+\frac{|z|^{p}+vp}{n}\right)^{1/p}}\right)\right).

Let
\delta>0
be such that
\beta^{\prime}:=\beta(1+\delta)^{2/p}<\beta_{c}(p)
, then

\displaystyle c_{p}\cdot\E_{U}\int_{\{|z|^{p}+vp\leqslant 2n\delta\}}\widetilde{K}_{n,p,\beta}(z,U)\,dz

\displaystyle\leqslant c_{p}\cdot\E_{U}\int_{\mathds{R}}\exp\left(-\frac{(|z|^{p}+vp)}{p}\cdot\frac{1}{1+\delta}+\frac{\nu_{p}^{2}}{2}\cdot(\sqrt{\beta}zU-u)^{2}\right)\,dz

\displaystyle=c_{p}\cdot(1+\delta)^{1/p}\E_{U,X}\exp\left(-\frac{v}{1+\delta}+\frac{1}{2}\nu_{p}^{2}((1+\delta)^{1/p}\sqrt{\beta}XU-u)^{2}\right)<\infty.

For each
i\geqslant 1
, let
a=2^{i}\delta
, then we get

\displaystyle c_{p}\cdot\E_{U}\int_{\mathds{R}}\mathds{1}_{\{na\leqslant|z|^{p}+vp\leqslant 2na\}}\widetilde{K}_{n,p,\beta}(z,U)\,dz

\displaystyle\leqslant c_{p}e^{-nf(a)/p}\cdot\E_{U}\int_{\mathds{R}}\exp\left(-\frac{(|z|^{p}+vp)}{p}\cdot\frac{1}{1+a}+\frac{\nu_{p}^{2}}{2}(\sqrt{\beta}zU-u)^{2}\right)\,dz

\displaystyle\leqslant 2^{1/p}e^{-(n-1)f(a)/p}\E_{U,X}\exp\left(-\frac{v}{1+a}+\frac{1}{2}\nu_{p}^{2}(\sqrt{\beta}XU(1+a)^{-1/p}-u)^{2}\right).

Since this is summable in
i
, we get that
\langle\exp\left(-uS_{n}/\sqrt{n}-vT_{n}/n\right)\rangle_{\widehat{\mu}_{n,\beta,p}}
is uniformly bounded in
n
when
\beta<\beta_{c}
.
Since
\lim_{t\to 0}\psi_{p}(t)/t^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\nu_{p}^{2}t^{2}
by Lemma
2.1
, we get

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\cdot(1+(|z|^{p}+vp)/n)^{-1/p}\cdot(\sqrt{\beta}zU-u)\right)\right)^{n}=\exp\left(\frac{\nu_{p}^{2}}{2}(\sqrt{\beta}zU-u)^{2}\right).

Thus, it follows from the DCT that

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\E\left[\bigl(T_{n}/n\bigr)^{-1/p}\exp\left(\frac{\beta}{2n}\cdot S_{n}^{2}\cdot\bigl(T_{n}/n\bigr)^{-2/p}-\frac{uS_{n}}{\sqrt{n}}-\frac{vT_{n}}{n}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=c_{p}\lim_{n\to\infty}\E_{U}\int_{\mathds{R}}\left(1+\frac{(|z|^{p}+vp)}{n}\right)^{-n/p}\exp\left(n\psi_{p}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\cdot\frac{(\sqrt{\beta}zU-u)}{(1+(|z|^{p}+vp)/n)^{1/p}}\right)\right)\,dz

\displaystyle=\E_{Z_{2}}\left[\exp\left(-v+\frac{\beta\nu_{p}^{2}}{2}\left(Z_{2}-\frac{u}{\sqrt{\beta}}\right)^{2}\right)\right]=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\beta\nu_{p}^{2}}}\exp\left(-v+\frac{\nu_{p}^{2}}{2(1-\beta\nu_{p}^{2})}u^{2}\right).

Therefore, we conclude that

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\langle\exp\left(-uS_{n}/\sqrt{n}-vT_{n}/n\right)\rangle_{\widehat{\mu}_{n,\beta,p}}=\exp\left(-v+\frac{\nu_{p}^{2}}{2(1-\beta\nu_{p}^{2})}u^{2}\right).

The same argument works for pure imaginary exponents
u,v\in i\mathds{R}
. Thus, we see that
S_{n}/\sqrt{n}
converges to
\N(0,\nu_{p}^{2}/(1-\beta\nu_{p}^{2}))
. Also
T_{n}/n
converges to 1 under the measure
\widehat{\mu}_{n,\beta,p}
, which yields the same convergence results under
\mu_{n,\beta,p}
.

3.2.
Proof of Theorem
1.2
Part (b)

Consider the case where
\beta_{c}=\beta
and
p\geqslant 2
.
By Remark
1.5
, it suffices to prove the result for
\bm{X}=(X_{1},X_{2},\ldots,X_{n})
under the Gibbs measure (
1.8
).
From Lemma
2.5
and CLT, we know that

\displaystyle\frac{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}-\E[Y_{1}]}{\sqrt{\var(Y_{1})/n}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\frac{1}{n^{3/4}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}-n^{1/4}\E[Y_{1}]}{n^{-1/4}\sqrt{\var(Y_{1})}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\frac{1}{n^{3/4}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\beta_{c}}}\frac{Z^{(n)}U^{(n)}}{n^{1/4}}}{n^{-1/4}/\sqrt{\beta_{c}}}+o(1)\implies\N(0,1).

Thus, it suffices to consider the limit of
\frac{Z^{(n)}U^{(n)}}{n^{1/4}}
. Let
\phi:\mathds{R}\to\mathds{R}
be a smooth, even function (that is,
\phi(t)=\phi(-t)
for all
t\in\mathds{R}
) with compact support, then

\displaystyle\E[\phi(n^{-1/4}Z^{(n)}U^{(n)})]

\displaystyle=\frac{2c_{p}}{\widehat{Z}_{n,p}(\beta)}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\phi(n^{-1/4}zu)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\exp\left(n\left(\psi_{p}\left(\sqrt{\frac{\beta}{n}}\frac{zu}{(1+\frac{|z|^{p}}{n})^{1/p}}\right)-\frac{1}{p}\log\left(1+\frac{|z|^{p}}{n}\right)\right)\right)\theta(u)\,dudz.

By change of variable,
z\mapsto n^{\frac{1}{2p}}z
and
u\mapsto n^{\frac{1}{4}-\frac{1}{2p}}u
, the Taylor expansion of
\psi_{p}
, and Proposition
1.13
,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\phi(n^{-1/4}zu)\exp\left(n\left(\psi_{p}\left(\sqrt{\frac{\beta}{n}}\frac{zu}{(1+\frac{|z|^{p}}{n})^{1/p}}\right)-\frac{1}{p}\log\left(1+\frac{|z|^{p}}{n}\right)\right)\right)\theta(u)\,dudz

\displaystyle\qquad=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\phi(zu)\exp\Bigg(\sqrt{n}\left(\frac{1}{2}z^{2}u^{2}-\frac{1}{p}z^{p}-\frac{p-2}{2p}u^{\frac{2p}{p-2}}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad-\left(\frac{1}{p}z^{p+2}u^{2}+\frac{\kappa}{24}z^{4}u^{4}-\frac{1}{2p}z^{2p}\right)+o(1)\Bigg)g(n^{\frac{p-2}{4p}}u)n^{1/2}u^{\frac{p}{p-2}}\,dzdu

where

\kappa_{p}=\beta_{c}^{2}\cdot(3(\E Z_{p}^{2})^{2}-\E Z_{p}^{4})=3-\frac{\E Z_{p}^{4}}{(\E Z_{p}^{2})^{2}}=3-\frac{\Gamma(5/p)\Gamma(1/p)}{\Gamma(3/p)^{2}}>0.

Here, we note that
-\kappa_{p}
is the excess kurtosis of the r.v.
Z_{p}
.
For fixed
u\geqslant 0
, let
z=u^{2/(p-2)}(1+n^{1/4}y)
, then

\displaystyle\E[\phi(n^{-1/4}Z^{(n)}U^{(n)})]

\displaystyle=\frac{2c_{p}}{\widehat{Z}_{n,p}(\beta)}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{-n^{1/4}}^{\infty}\phi(u^{p/(p-2)}(1+n^{1/4}y))

\displaystyle\qquad\exp\Bigg(\sqrt{n}u^{2p/(p-2)}\left(\frac{1}{2}(1+n^{1/4}y)^{2}-\frac{1}{p}(1+n^{1/4}y)^{p}-\frac{p-2}{2p}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad-u^{4p/(p-2)}\left(\frac{1}{p}(1+n^{1/4}y)^{p+2}+\frac{\kappa}{24}(1+n^{1/4}y)^{4}-\frac{1}{2p}(1+n^{1/4}y)^{2p}\right)+o(1)\Bigg)

\displaystyle\qquad g(n^{\frac{p-2}{4p}}u)n^{1/4}u^{\frac{p+2}{p-2}}\,dydu.

Note that for fixed
u,y
, as
n\to\infty
, we get

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}u^{2p/(p-2)}\left(\frac{1}{2}(1+n^{1/4}y)^{2}-\frac{1}{p}(1+n^{1/4}y)^{p}-\frac{p-2}{2p}\right)\to-\frac{p}{2}u^{2p/(p-2)}y^{2}.

Let
\phi_{0}=1
, then it follows from Proposition
1.13
and the DCT that

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\E[\phi(n^{-1/4}Z^{(n)}U^{(n)})]

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\E[\phi(n^{-1/4}Z^{(n)}U^{(n)})]}{\E[\phi_{0}(n^{-1/4}Z^{(n)}U^{(n)})]}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathcal{Z}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\phi(u^{p/(p-2)})\exp\Bigg(-\frac{p}{2}u^{2p/(p-2)}y^{2}-u^{4p/(p-2)}\left(\frac{1}{p}+\frac{\kappa}{24}-\frac{1}{2p}\right)\Bigg)u^{\frac{p+2}{p-2}}\,dydu

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{2\pi}}{\sqrt{p}\mathcal{Z}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\phi(u^{p/(p-2)})\exp\Bigg(-u^{4p/(p-2)}\left(\frac{1}{2p}+\frac{\kappa}{24}\right)\Bigg)u^{\frac{2}{p-2}}\,du

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{2\pi}(p-2)}{p^{3/2}\mathcal{Z}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\phi(x)\exp\Bigg(-\left(\frac{1}{2p}+\frac{\kappa}{24}\right)x^{4}\Bigg)\,dx

where

\mathcal{Z}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\exp\Bigg(-\frac{p}{2}u^{2p/(p-2)}y^{2}-u^{4p/(p-2)}\left(\frac{1}{2p}+\frac{\kappa}{24}\right)\Bigg)u^{\frac{p+2}{p-2}}\,dydu.

For general smooth and compactly supported function
\phi
, we apply the same argument for
\widetilde{\phi}(t)=\phi(t)+\phi(-t)
. Therefore, we conclude that
\frac{1}{n^{3/4}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}
converges in distribution to the distribution limit of
\frac{1}{\sqrt{\beta_{c}}n^{1/4}}Z^{(n)}U^{(n)}
, whose density is given by

C\exp\Bigg(-\beta_{c}^{2}\left(\frac{1}{2p}+\frac{\kappa}{24}\right)x^{4}\Bigg)

as desired.

3.3.
Proof of Theorem
1.2
Part (c)

By (
1.12
) and Theorem
1.17
(a), we see that the infimum of
I_{F}=I-F
is strictly negative when
\beta>\beta_{c}
. Thus,
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\mathcal{Z}_{n,p}(\beta)>0
.

We claim that

\psi_{p}\left(\sqrt{\beta}\cdot uv\right)+\frac{1}{p}\log(1-u^{p})-\frac{p-2}{2p}v^{2p/(p-2)}

has a unique maximizer
0<u_{\ast}<1,0<v_{\ast}<\infty
. The maximizers should satisfy

\displaystyle\psi_{p}^{\prime}(\sqrt{\beta}uv)\sqrt{\beta}uv

\displaystyle=v^{2p/(p-2)},\text{ and }

\displaystyle\psi_{p}^{\prime}(\sqrt{\beta}uv)\sqrt{\beta}uv

\displaystyle=\frac{u^{p}}{1-u^{p}}.

From this, we obtain the equation

(3.1)

\displaystyle\frac{(1+w^{2})^{1/p}}{\sqrt{\beta}w\ }\cdot\psi_{p}^{\prime}(\frac{\sqrt{\beta}w\ }{(1+w^{2})^{1/p}})=\frac{1}{\beta}(1+w^{2})^{2/p},\qquad w=v^{p/(p-2)}.

The left-hand side in (
3.1
) converges to
1/\beta_{c}
as
w\to 0
and the right-hand side equals
1/\beta
when
w=0
. Since
\beta_{c}<\beta
, and
\frac{w}{(1+w^{2})^{1/p}},(1+w^{2})^{2/p}
are increasing in
w
for
p\geqslant 2
, it follows from Lemma
2.2
that there exists a unique nonzero solution
w_{\ast}
to the equation (
3.1
). Let
u_{\ast}=w_{\ast}^{2/p}(1+w_{\ast}^{2})^{-1/p}
and
v_{\ast}=w_{\ast}^{(p-2)/p}
, then

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\mathcal{Z}_{n,p}(\beta)

\displaystyle=\sup_{0\leqslant u<1,w\geqslant 0}\left\{\psi_{p}\left(\sqrt{\beta}\cdot uv\right)+\frac{1}{p}\log(1-u^{p})-\frac{p-2}{2p}v^{2p/(p-2)}\right\}

\displaystyle=\psi_{p}\left(\sqrt{\beta}\cdot u_{\ast}v_{\ast}\right)+\frac{1}{p}\log(1-u_{\ast}^{p})-\frac{p-2}{2p}v_{\ast}^{2p/(p-2)}.

Let
(X_{1},X_{2}\ldots,X_{n},Z^{(n)},U^{(n)})
be random variables with joint density

f(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n},z,u)=C\exp\left(\sqrt{\beta}zu\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}-\frac{|z|^{p}}{p}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|x_{i}|^{p}+\log\theta(n^{\frac{p-2}{2p}}u)\right)\prod_{i=1}^{n}\rho_{p}(x_{i}).

From the above computations, we see that
(Z^{(n)},U^{(n)})
converges in distribution to a r.v. supported on
(\pm w_{\ast}^{2/p},w_{\ast}^{(p-2)/p})
with probability
1/2
. Moreover, Lemma
2.5
tells us that
X_{1},X_{2},\ldots,X_{n}
given
(Z^{(n)},U^{(n)})=(w_{\ast}^{2/p},w_{\ast}^{(p-2)/p})
are i.i.d. with conditional density

\exp\left(\sqrt{\beta}w_{\ast}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}-\frac{w_{\ast}^{2}}{p}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|x_{i}|^{p}-n\psi_{p}\left(\theta_{\ast}\right)\right)(1+w_{\ast}^{2})^{n/p}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\rho_{p}(x_{i}).

where
\theta_{\ast}=\sqrt{\beta}w_{\ast}(1+w_{\ast}^{2})^{-1/p}
. By Lemmas
2.4
and
2.5
, we have

\displaystyle\E[X_{1}]

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathds{R}}x\exp\left(\sqrt{\beta}w_{\ast}x-\frac{w_{\ast}^{2}}{p}|x|^{p}-\psi_{p}\left(\theta_{\ast}\right)\right)(1+w_{\ast}^{2})^{1/p}\rho_{p}(x)\,dx

\displaystyle=(1+w_{\ast}^{2})^{-1/p}\int_{\mathds{R}}y\exp\left(\theta_{\ast}y-\frac{1}{p}|y|^{p}-\psi_{p}\left(\theta_{\ast}\right)\right)\,dy

\displaystyle=(1+w_{\ast}^{2})^{-1/p}\psi_{p}^{\prime}\left(\theta_{\ast}\right)=m_{\ast},

\displaystyle\E[|X_{1}|^{p}]

\displaystyle=(1+w_{\ast}^{2})^{-1}\int_{\mathds{R}}|y|^{p}\exp\left(\theta_{\ast}y-\frac{1}{p}|y|^{p}-\psi_{p}\left(\theta_{\ast}\right)\right)\,dy

\displaystyle=(1+w_{\ast}^{2})^{-1}(1+\theta_{\ast}\psi^{\prime}_{p}(\theta_{\ast}))=1.

Here, we used the fact that
w_{\ast}
is the unique positive solution to the equation (
3.1
). From this and Remark
1.5
, we deduce that the magnetization is concentrated at
\E[X_{1}]=m_{\ast}
when
Z^{(n)}\to w_{\ast}^{2/p}
. By conditioning on
Z^{(n)}=-w_{\ast}^{2/p},U^{(n)}=w_{\ast}^{(p-2)/p}
, we see that the magnetization is concentrated at
-m_{\ast}
as desired.

3.4.
Proof of Theorem
1.4

Define

\displaystyle(Y_{1},Y_{2},\ldots,Y_{n}):\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{d}}}{{=}}(X_{1},X_{2},\ldots,X_{n})\mid(Z^{(n)},U^{(n)})=(z,u)

for
z\in\mathds{R},u>0
fixed. That is, the joint density of
Y_{1},Y_{2},\ldots,Y_{n}
is given by

\displaystyle f_{Y}(y_{1},y_{2},\ldots,y_{n})

\displaystyle=\frac{f(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n},z,u)}{f_{Z^{(n)},U^{(n)}}(z,u)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\exp\left(\sqrt{\frac{\beta}{n}}\cdot zu\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}-\frac{1}{np}\left|z\right|^{p}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|y_{i}|^{p}\right)\,\prod_{i=1}^{n}\rho_{p}(y_{i})}{\exp\left(n\left(\psi_{p}\left(\sqrt{\frac{\beta}{n}}\frac{zu}{(1+\frac{|z|^{p}}{n})^{1/p}}\right)-\frac{1}{p}\log\left(1+\frac{|z|^{p}}{n}\right)\right)\right)}.

Since
Y_{1},Y_{2},\ldots,Y_{n}
are i.i.d., the CLT tells us that

\displaystyle\frac{\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}-\sqrt{n}\E[Y_{1}]}{\sqrt{\var(Y_{1})}}\implies\N(0,1).

It is easy to see that
(Z_{n},U_{n})
converges in distribution to a random vector
(Z,U)
with joint density for
(Z,U)
given by

\displaystyle f_{Z,U}(z,u)=C_{1}\exp\left(\frac{\beta}{2\beta_{c}}z^{2}u^{2}-\frac{|z|^{p}}{p}\right)\theta(u),\qquad z\in\mathds{R},u>0,

where
C_{1}
is the normalizing constant. Since
\widehat{\mathcal{Z}}_{n,p}(\beta)
is uniformly bounded in
n
by the proof of Theorem
1.2
, Part a, we can take a limit along a subsequence. From this and the fact that
X\cdot U\sim\N(0,1)
, one can see that the density of the product
Z\cdot U
is

\displaystyle f_{Z\cdot U}(t)=C_{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{u}\exp\left(\frac{\beta}{2\beta_{c}}t^{2}\right)\exp\left(-\frac{|t|^{p}}{pu^{p}}\right)\theta(u)\,du=C_{2}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{\beta}{\beta_{c}}\right)t^{2}\right).

Thus,
Z\cdot U\sim\N(0,\beta_{c}/(\beta_{c}-\beta))
. By Lemma
2.5
, we have
\lim_{n\to\infty}\sqrt{n}\E[Y_{1}]=\sqrt{\frac{\beta}{\beta_{c}^{2}}}\cdot ZU
and
\lim_{n\to\infty}\var(Y_{1})=\frac{1}{\beta_{c}}
. Thus,
\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}(Y_{1}+Y_{2}+\cdots+Y_{n})
converges to a normal distribution with mean 0 and variance

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\beta_{c}}+\frac{\beta}{\beta_{c}^{2}}\frac{\beta_{c}}{\beta_{c}-\beta}=\frac{1}{\beta_{c}-\beta}

in distribution.

3.5.
Proof of Proposition
1.6

Since

\frac{n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{i}^{2}}{\left|\!\left|\!\left|\bm{\sigma}\right|\!\right|\!\right|_{n,p}^{2}}\leqslant n^{\tfrac{2}{p}-1},

it suffices to consider the partition function

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}}_{n,p}(\beta)=\E\left[\exp\left(\frac{\beta n}{2}\left(\frac{S_{n}}{T_{n}^{1/p}}\right)^{2}\right)\right]

where
S_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}
and
T_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|X_{i}|^{p}
. It was shown in
(
de la Peña
et al., 2009
, p.31)
that

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\pr\left(\frac{S_{n}}{T_{n}^{1/p}}\geqslant x\right)^{1/n}=\sup_{c\geqslant 0}\inf_{t\geqslant 0}\E\left[\exp\left(t\left(cX-x\left(\frac{|X|^{p}}{p}+\frac{p-1}{p}c^{p/(p-1)}\right)\right)\right)\right]

for all
x\geqslant 0
. Let
g(y)=\exp\left(\tfrac{\beta n}{2}y^{2}\right)
and
Y_{n}=\frac{S_{n}}{T_{n}^{1/p}}
, then by symmetry we have

\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}}_{n,p}(\beta)=\E[g(Y_{n})]=2\int_{0}^{\infty}g^{\prime}(y)\pr(Y_{n}\geqslant y)\,dy.

Thus, we get

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\mathcal{Z}_{n,p}(\beta)

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}}_{n,p}(\beta)

\displaystyle=\sup_{y\geqslant 0}\left\{\frac{\beta y^{2}}{2}+\sup_{c\geqslant 0}\inf_{t\geqslant 0}\log\E\left[\exp\left(t\left(cX-y\left(\frac{|X|^{p}}{p}+\frac{p-1}{p}c^{p/(p-1)}\right)\right)\right)\right]\right\}

\displaystyle=\sup_{y,c\geqslant 0}\inf_{t\geqslant 0}\left\{\frac{\beta y^{2}}{2}+\psi_{p}\left(\frac{ct}{(1+ty)^{1/p}}\right)-\frac{1}{p}\log(1+ty)-\frac{p-1}{p}tyc^{p/(p-1)}\right\}

as desired.

4.
Proofs for the Generalized Hubbard–Stratonovich transform

In this section, we prove the Generalized Hubbard–Stratonovich (GHS) transform (Theorem
1.10
). The main ingredient of the GHS transform is the existence of a particular additive process, which will be proved in Theorem
1.12
. Based on the additive process, we construct a random variable
U_{p,q}
such that
Z_{p}\cdot U_{p,q}=Z_{q}
and provide a detailed analysis on the density of
U_{p,q}
in Proposition
1.13
.

Recall the collection of symmetric densities

\displaystyle\rho_{p}(x):=c_{p}\cdot e^{-\left|x\right|^{p}/p},\quad x\in\mathds{R}

where
c_{p}:=\frac{p^{-1/p}}{2\Gamma(1+1/p)}

for
p>0
. We use
Z_{p}
to denote a typical random variable with density
\rho_{p}
. Now, fix
p\geqslant 2
. Using Proposition
1.13
, we can prove the GHS transform for
p>0
.

4.1.
Proof of Theorem
1.10

Recall that
Z_{2}\sim\N(0,1)
satisfies
\E\exp(tZ_{2})=\exp(t^{2}/2)
for all
t\in\mathds{R}
. By Proposition
1.13
, there exist independent random variables
Z_{p}\sim\rho_{p}
and
U\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{d}}}{{=}}U_{p,2}
such that
Z_{p}\cdot U\sim\N(0,1)
. Thus, we have

\displaystyle\exp\left(x^{2}y^{-2/p}\right)

\displaystyle=\E\exp\left(\sqrt{2}xy^{-1/p}\cdot Z_{2})\right)

\displaystyle=\E_{U}\int_{\mathds{R}}c_{p}\exp\left(\sqrt{2}x\cdot y^{-1/p}z\cdot U-\left|z\right|^{p}/p\right)\,dz

\displaystyle=c_{p}y^{1/p}\E_{U}\int_{\mathds{R}}\exp\left(\sqrt{2}xzU-y\left|z\right|^{p}/p\right)\,dz.

Here we used a change of variable
z\to y^{1/p}z
in the last equality.

4.2.
Proof of Theorem
1.12

Let
f:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
be a continuously differentiable, increasing function with
f(0)=0
. Let
(\mathcal{P}_{f},w_{x})
be a marked Poisson point process where
\mathcal{P}_{f}
is a Poisson point process with intensity
\mu_{f}(x):=f^{\prime}(x)/f(x)
,
x>0
, and the mark
w_{x}
is an exponential random variable with mean
f(x)
. Define

\displaystyle V_{t}^{(f)}=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{P}_{f}\cap[0,t]}w_{x}=\int_{0}^{t}w_{x}\,\mathcal{P}_{f}(dx),\qquad t\geqslant 0,

and
V_{t,s}^{(f)}:=V_{s}^{(f)}-V_{t}^{(f)}
for
0\leqslant t<s<\infty
. For an integer
k\geqslant 1
, let
f_{k}(x):=f(x/k)
and
V^{(k)}_{t}
be an independent copy of
V^{(f_{k})}_{t}
. Note that

\displaystyle\E e^{i\xi V_{t}^{(f)}}=\E\left(\prod_{x\in\mathcal{P}_{f}\cap[0,t]}e^{i\xi w_{x}}\right)

\displaystyle=\E\left(\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{t}\log(1-i\xi f(x))\,\mathcal{P}_{f}(dx)\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{t}\left(1-\frac{1}{1-i\xi f(x)}\right)\,\frac{f^{\prime}(x)}{f(x)}\,dx\right)=\frac{1}{1-i\xi f(t)}.

Define

\displaystyle U_{t}^{(m)}:=\sum_{k=1}^{m}(V_{t}^{(k)}-\E V_{t}^{(k)}).

We assume that
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}f(t/k)^{2}<\infty
for each
t>0
. Then,
\E[U_{t}^{(m)}]=0
and
\var(U_{t}^{(m)})<\infty
, which yields that
U_{t}^{(m)}\implies U_{t}
as
m\to\infty
in distribution, for all
t\geqslant 0
. Note that the characteristic function of
U_{t}
can be computed as

\displaystyle\E e^{i\xi U_{t}}=\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}\E e^{i\xi V_{t}^{(k)}}\cdot e^{-i\xi\E V_{t}^{(k)}}=\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}(1-i\xi f(t/k))^{-1}e^{-i\xi f(t/k)}.

Let
f(x):=x/(1+x)
, then

\displaystyle\E e^{i\xi U_{t}}=\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}\left(1-\frac{i\xi t}{k+t}\right)^{-1}e^{-i\xi t/(k+t)}.

Note that

\displaystyle e^{-\gamma z}\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}(1+z/k)^{-1}e^{z/k}=\frac{\Gamma^{\prime}(z+1)}{\Gamma(z+1)}=-\gamma+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{z}{k(k+z)},

where
\Gamma
is the Gamma function and
\gamma
is the Euler constant. It then follows that

\displaystyle\E e^{i\xi U_{t}}

\displaystyle=\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}\left(1-\frac{i\xi t}{k+t}\right)^{-1}e^{-i\xi t/(k+t)}

\displaystyle=\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}\left(1+(1-i\xi)t/k\right)^{-1}e^{(1-i\xi)t/k}\cdot\left(1+{t}/{k}\right)e^{-t/k}\cdot e^{i\xi t^{2}/(k(k+t))}

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{\gamma(1-i\xi)t}\Gamma((1-i\xi)t+1)}{e^{\gamma t}\Gamma(t+1)}\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}e^{i\xi t^{2}/(k(k+t))}.

Simplifying, we get

\displaystyle\E e^{i\xi U_{t}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma((1-i\xi)t+1)}{\Gamma(t+1)}\exp\left(i\xi t\left(-\gamma+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{t}{k(k+t)}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma((1-i\xi)t+1)}{\Gamma(t+1)}\exp\left(i\xi t\frac{\Gamma^{\prime}(t+1)}{\Gamma(t+1)}\right)=(1-i\xi)e^{i\xi}\cdot\frac{\Gamma((1-i\xi)t)}{\Gamma(t)}\exp\left(i\xi t\frac{\Gamma^{\prime}(t)}{\Gamma(t)}\right).

Let
X_{t}
be the Gamma random variable with shape parameter
t
and rate parameter
1
. Then

\displaystyle\E e^{-i\xi t\log X_{t}}=\E X_{t}^{-i\xi t}={\Gamma((1-i\xi)t)}/{\Gamma(t)},\quad\E\log X_{t}=\frac{d}{du}\E e^{u\log X_{t}}|_{u=0}={\Gamma^{\prime}(t)}/{\Gamma(t)}.

Let
Y
be an exponential random variable with rate 1, independent of
(U_{t})_{t}\geqslant 0
. Thus,

\displaystyle\E e^{i\xi(U_{t}+Y-1)}

\displaystyle=e^{-i\xi}\cdot\E e^{i\xi Y}\cdot\E e^{i\xi U_{t}}

\displaystyle=e^{-i\xi}\cdot(1-i\xi)^{-1}\cdot(1-i\xi)e^{i\xi}\cdot\frac{\Gamma((1-i\xi)t)}{\Gamma(t)}\cdot e^{i\xi t\Gamma^{\prime}(t)/\Gamma(t)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma((1-i\xi)t)}{\Gamma(t)}\cdot e^{i\xi t\Gamma^{\prime}(t)/\Gamma(t)}=\E e^{-i\xi(t\log X_{t}-t\E\log X_{t})}.

Thus, we have

-U_{t}+1-Y\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{d}}}{{=}}t\log X_{t}-t\E\log X_{t},\text{ for all }t>0.

In particular, if we define the process

(4.1)

\displaystyle Y_{t}:=-U_{t}-Y+t\Gamma^{\prime}(t+1)/\Gamma(t+1),\text{ for all }t\geqslant 0,

it has independent increments and
Y_{t}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{d}}}{{=}}t\log X_{t}
for all
t>0
.

From the proof, we can construct the marginals for the process
Y
at any finitely many time points. We get the existence of the process
Y
over the positive real line by Theorem 9.7(ii) in
(
Sato, 2013
, page 51)
, which states that if a system of probability measures
\{\mu_{s,t}:0\leqslant s\leqslant t<\infty\}
satisfies

\displaystyle\mu_{s,t}\ast\mu_{t,u}

\displaystyle=\mu_{s,u}\text{ for all }0\leqslant s\leqslant t\leqslant u<\infty,

\displaystyle\mu_{s,s}

\displaystyle=\delta_{0}\text{ for }0\leqslant s<\infty,

\displaystyle\lim_{s\uparrow t}\mu_{s,t}

\displaystyle=\delta_{0}\text{ for }0<t<\infty,

\displaystyle\lim_{t\downarrow s}\mu_{s,t}

\displaystyle=\delta_{0}\text{ for }0\leqslant s<\infty,

then there exists an additive process
X_{t}
such that the law of
X_{t}-X_{s}
is
\mu_{s,t}
.

4.3.
Proof of Proposition
1.13

According to the proof of Theorem
1.12
, for the existence of the density, it suffices to show that

\displaystyle\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}\left(1-\frac{{i\mkern 1.0mu}\xi t}{k+t}\right)^{-1}e^{-{i\mkern 1.0mu}\xi t/(k+t)}

converges absolutely. Indeed, let
a_{k}=\frac{{i\mkern 1.0mu}\xi t}{k+t}
, then

\displaystyle\left|\frac{e^{-a_{k}}}{1-a_{k}}-1\right|=\frac{|e^{-a_{k}}-1+a_{k}|}{1-|a_{k}|}\leqslant c\frac{|a_{k}|^{2}}{1-|a_{k}|}

for large
k
and it is summable. Thus, the infinite product converges absolutely. Since
Y_{t}
is defined by the independent sum of nonnegative random variables (see (
4.1
)) and one of them is an exponential random variable, it follows that the density is strictly positive.

Let
p>q>0
be fixed. Let
\theta:=\theta_{p,q}
be the density of
U_{p,q}
. We can explicitly write down an expression for
\theta
using M–Wright functions.

Using Mellin transform and the facts that
\left|Z_{p}\right|^{p}/p\sim\text{Gamma}(1/p,1)
,
\left|Z_{p}\right|\cdot U_{p,q}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{d}}}{{=}}\left|Z_{q}\right|
, one can check that the random variable
V:=p^{1/p}q^{-1/q}\cdot U_{p,q}
has density given by

f_{V}(x)=\frac{\Gamma(1/p)}{\Gamma(1/q)}\cdot\frac{1}{2\pi{i\mkern 1.0mu}}\int_{1-{i\mkern 1.0mu}\infty}^{1+{i\mkern 1.0mu}\infty}x^{-z}\cdot\frac{\Gamma(z/q)}{\Gamma(z/p)}\,dz,\text{ for }x>0.

Gamma function has simple poles at all non-positive integers
0,-1,-2,\ldots
. Thus, evaluating the contour integral as a sum over residues we get for
x>0
,

f_{V}(x)=\frac{\Gamma(1/p)}{\Gamma(1/q)}\cdot\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{x^{qk}}{\Gamma(-\nu k)}\cdot\lim_{z\to-qk}(z+qk)\Gamma(z/q)=\frac{q\Gamma(1/p)}{\Gamma(1/q)}\cdot\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-x^{q})^{k}}{k!\cdot\Gamma(-\nu k)},

where
\nu:=q/p\in(0,1)
. In particular, we have for
x>0
,

\theta(x)=\frac{qp^{1/p}\Gamma(1/p)}{q^{1/q}\Gamma(1/q)}\cdot\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-r/\nu)^{k}}{k!\cdot\Gamma(-\nu k)}=\frac{pc_{q}}{c_{p}}\cdot rM_{\nu}(r/\nu)

where
r=x^{q}/p^{1-\nu}
and

M_{\nu}(x):=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-z)^{n}}{n!\cdot\Gamma(1-\nu-\nu n)}

is the M–Wright function, a particular example of the Wright function of the second kind. Asymptotic representation of M–Wright functions for large arguments using a saddle-point analysis is well-known in the literature (see
(
Mainardi
et al., 2010
, eq. 4.3)
and references within),

(4.2)

\displaystyle M_{\nu}(r/\nu)\cdot r^{1-1/(2(1-\nu))}\cdot\exp((1-\nu)/\nu\cdot r^{1/(1-\nu)})\to\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi(1-\nu)}}

as
r\to\infty
. Simplifying, we get the result.

5.
Proofs for the
\ell^{p}
Self-Scaled Model

In this section, we present the proofs of Theorems
1.16
,
1.17
, and
1.18
, where the limiting free energy of the
\ell^{p}
self-scaled model is analyzed. Note that, Theorems
1.2
and
1.7
follow from the proof of Theorem
1.17
, and Theorem
1.9
follows from the proof of Theorem
1.18

5.1.
Proof of Theorem
1.16

Since
Q_{n}
has the large deviation property (which follows from the moment assumption and
(
Ellis, 2006
, Theorem II.4.1)
and
\sup_{(x,y)\in\mathcal{X}}F(x,y)<\infty
, it follows from
(
Ellis, 2006
, Theorem II.7.1)
that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\mathcal{Z}_{n,p}(\beta;\rho)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}}_{n,p}(\beta;\rho)=\sup_{0\leqslant|x|\leqslant y^{1/p}}\left(F(x,y)-I(x,y)\right),

where
\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}}_{n,p}(\beta;\rho)
is defined as in (
1.11
). Since
F
is continuous on
\mathcal{X}
,
\int_{\mathcal{X}}e^{nF(x,y)}\,Q_{n}(dx,dy)
is finite, and

\lim_{L\to\infty}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\int_{F\geqslant L}e^{nF(x,y)}\,Q_{n}(dx,dy)=-\infty,

we apply
(
Ellis, 2006
, Theorem II.7.2)
to conclude the proof.

5.2.
Proof of Theorem
1.17

5.2.1.
Part (a)

Let
X,X_{1},X_{2},\ldots,X_{n}
be i.i.d. random variables from
\rho
. As in the proof of Theorem
1.2
(a), we consider

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{Z}}_{n,p}(\beta;\rho)=\E_{\rho}\left[\bigl(T_{n}/n\bigr)^{-1/p}\exp\left(\frac{\beta}{2n}\cdot S_{n}^{2}\cdot\bigl(T_{n}/n\bigr)^{-2/p}\right)\right]

where

S_{n}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\text{ and }T_{n}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|X_{i}\right|^{p}.

Then, it follows from Theorem
1.10
that

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{Z}}_{n,p}(\beta;\rho)

\displaystyle=c_{p}\iint\E_{U}\left[\exp\left(\sqrt{\beta n}S_{n}\cdot z\cdot U-T_{n}|z|^{p}/p\right)\right]\,dz\prod_{i=1}^{n}d\rho(x_{i})

\displaystyle=c_{p}\iint\left(\int\exp\left(\sqrt{\frac{\beta}{n}}x\cdot z\cdot u-\frac{1}{np}|x|^{p}|z|^{p}\right)d\rho(x)\right)^{n}\theta(u)\,dudz

\displaystyle=c_{p}\iint\exp\left(n\psi_{\rho}(\sqrt{\beta}zu,-|z|^{p}/p)\right)\theta(n^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p}}u)n^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{2}{p}}\,dudz

where
U\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{d}}}{{=}}U_{p,2}
is independent of
X_{i},i=1,2,\ldots,n
such that
X\cdot U\sim\N(0,1)
. Since
\log\theta(n^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p}}u)=-\frac{p-2}{2p}nu^{2p/(p-2)}+o(1)
by Proposition
1.13
, we get

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\widehat{\mathcal{Z}}_{n,p}(\beta;\rho)=\sup_{z,u}\left\{\psi_{\rho}(\sqrt{\beta}zu,-|z|^{p}/p)-\frac{p-2}{2p}u^{\frac{2p}{p-2}}\right\}.

Note that

\pr_{\rho}(|X_{1}|\leqslant x)\leqslant\frac{\E_{\rho}|X_{1}|^{-\alpha}}{x^{-\alpha}}\leqslant Cx^{\alpha}.

Choose
k>\frac{pq}{\alpha}
, then for all
n>k
, we have

(5.1)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\E_{\rho}[|T_{n}|^{-q}]&\leqslant\E_{\rho}[|\max_{i}|X_{i}|^{-pq}]\\
&=pq\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{-pq-1}\pr_{\rho}(|X_{1}|\leqslant x)^{n}\,dx\\
&\leqslant pq\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{-pq-1}\pr_{\rho}(|X_{1}|\leqslant x)^{k}\,dx\leqslant C\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{-pq+\alpha k-1}\,dx<\infty.\end{split}

Let
\widehat{\beta}<\beta
and
r=\beta/\widehat{\beta}
, then we have

\log\widehat{\mathcal{Z}}_{n,p}(\widehat{\beta};\rho)=\frac{1}{r}\log\mathcal{Z}_{n,p}(\beta;\rho)+\left(1-\frac{1}{r}\right)\log\E_{\rho}[|T_{n}|^{-q}]

where
q=\frac{r}{p(r-1)}=\frac{\beta}{p(\beta-\widehat{\beta})}
. Letting
\widehat{\beta}\to\beta
(that is,
r\to 1
), it follows from the continuity of
\psi
that

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\mathcal{Z}_{n,p}(\beta;\rho)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\widehat{\beta}\to\beta}\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\widehat{\mathcal{Z}}_{n,p}(\widehat{\beta};\rho)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\widehat{\beta}\to\beta}\sup_{z,u}\left\{\psi_{\rho}(\sqrt{\widehat{\beta}}zu,-|z|^{p}/p)-\frac{p-2}{2p}u^{\frac{2p}{p-2}}\right\}

\displaystyle=\sup_{z,u}\left\{\psi_{\rho}(\sqrt{\beta}zu,-|z|^{p}/p)-\frac{p-2}{2p}u^{\frac{2p}{p-2}}\right\}.

Define the function
f_{\beta}:[0,\infty)^{2}\to\mathds{R}
given by

f_{\beta}(z,w):=\psi_{\rho}(\sqrt{\beta}zw,-z^{p}/p)-\frac{p-2}{2p}\cdot w^{2p/(p-2)}.

One can see that
\sup_{z,w\geqslant 0}f_{\beta}(z,w)>0
when
\beta>\beta_{c}(\rho)
.
Indeed, fix
\beta>\beta_{c}(\rho)
, a constant
a>0
and consider the function

g(t)=p\cdot f_{\beta}(t^{2/p},at^{1-2/p}),\ t\geqslant 0.

It is easy to check that
g(0)=g^{\prime}(0)=0
and

g^{\prime\prime}(0)=-2+p\beta\sigma_{X}^{2}\cdot a^{2}-(p-2)\cdot a^{2p/(p-2)},

where
\sigma_{X}^{2}=\E[X^{2}]
. In particular,
g^{\prime\prime}(0)>0
iff

\beta\sigma_{X}^{2}\cdot a^{2}>2/p+(1-2/p)\cdot a^{2p/(p-2)}.

We can take
a_{0}=1
so that

\frac{2/p+(1-2/p)\cdot a_{0}^{2p/(p-2)}}{a_{0}^{2}}=1<\beta\sigma_{X}^{2}.

Thus, there exists
t>0
such that
f_{\beta}(t^{2/p},t^{1-2/p})>0
.

5.2.2.
Part (b)

As in the proof of Theorem
1.17
, it suffices to consider

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{Z}}_{n,2}(\beta;\rho)=\E_{\rho}\left[\bigl(T_{n}/n\bigr)^{-1/2}\exp\left(\frac{\beta}{2n}\cdot S_{n}^{2}\cdot\bigl(T_{n}/n\bigr)^{-1}\right)\right].

Using the GHS transform, we get

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{Z}}_{n,2}(\beta;\rho)

\displaystyle=\E_{\rho}\left[\bigl(T_{n}/n\bigr)^{-1/2}\exp\left(\sqrt{\frac{\beta}{n}}\cdot S_{n}\cdot\bigl(T_{n}/n\bigr)^{-1/2}\cdot\eta\right)\right]

\displaystyle=c_{2}\int\E_{\rho}\left[\exp\left(\sqrt{\frac{\beta}{n}}\cdot S_{n}\cdot y-\frac{1}{2}\frac{T_{n}}{n}y^{2}\right)\right]\,dy

\displaystyle=c_{2}\sqrt{n}\int\left(\E_{\rho}\left[\exp\left(\sqrt{\beta}\cdot X\cdot y-\frac{1}{2}X^{2}\cdot y^{2}\right)\right]\right)^{n}\,dy

where
\eta\sim\N(0,1)
. Thus, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\widehat{\mathcal{Z}}_{n,2}(\beta;\rho)=\sup_{y}\log\E_{\rho}\left[\exp\left(\sqrt{\beta}y\cdot X-\frac{1}{2}y^{2}X^{2}\right)\right].

Let
f(z)=\psi_{\rho}(\sqrt{\beta}z,-z^{2}/2)
. One can see that
\sup_{z\geqslant 0}f(z)>0
for
\beta>\beta_{c}(\rho)=1
because
f^{\prime}(0)=0
and

6.
Multi-species Curie–Weiss model under
\ell^{p}
constraint

One of the natural generalizations of the
\ell^{p}
constraint Curie–Weiss model is a multi-group or multi-species version. A multi-group version of the Curie–Weiss model was introduced in
Gallo and
Contucci (2008)
and developed in
Berthet
et al. (2019)
;
Knöpfel et al. (2020)
;
Kirsch and
Toth (2022)
;
Fedele and
Contucci (2011)
. In this model, there are
d
many groups, and a spin interaction between groups
s
and
t
for
s,t\in[d]
is given by
\delta_{s,t}
. One can study this model with
\ell^{p}
ball spin configuration.

To be precise, we consider the complete graph with
N
vertices. Suppose there are
d
groups in the set of vertices, and each group has
N_{1},N_{2},\ldots,N_{d}
many vertices. That is,
N=N_{1}+\cdots+N_{d}
. We assume that as
N\to\infty
, the ratio
\frac{N_{s}}{N}
converges to
\alpha_{s}
, with
\alpha_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{d}=1
. Let
I_{s}
be the index set of
s
-th group (so
|I_{s}|=N_{s}
), then the Hamiltonian is defined by

H_{N}(\bm{\sigma})=\exp\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{s,t=1}^{d}\delta_{s,t}\sum_{i\in I_{s}}\sum_{j\in I_{t}}\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}\right)

for
\bm{\sigma}\in\Omega_{N}=\{-1,1\}^{N}=\prod_{s=1}^{d}\{-1,1\}^{N_{s}}
and a
d\times d
inverse temperature matrix
\bm{\delta}=(\delta_{s,t})_{s,t=1,\ldots,d}
. The phase transition of the magnetizations of each group
(S_{1},\ldots,S_{d})
,
S_{t}=\frac{1}{N_{t}}\sum_{i\in I_{t}}\sigma_{i}
, has been studied in
Fedele and
Contucci (2011)
;
Kirsch and
Toth (2022)
, which proved that the multi-group Curie–Weiss model has similar phase transition as in the classical model. The critical temperature is characterized by the matrix
J:=\bm{\delta}^{-1}-\bm{\alpha}
, where
\bm{\alpha}
is a diagonal matrix whose entries are
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{d}
. In the high-temperature regime where
J
is positive definite, it was shown that the magnetizations
(S_{1},\ldots,S_{d})
are concentrated on 0 with Gaussian fluctuation.

One can consider the multi-group Curie–Weiss model under
\ell^{p}
constraint by replacing the spin configuration space with
\Omega_{N,p}
and the Gibbs measure with the uniform measure on
\Omega_{N,p}
. As above, one can rewrite the Hamiltonian and the Gibbs measure regarding the self-scaled vector with
\rho_{p}
distribution. That is, the partition function has the following form

\mathcal{Z}=\int\exp\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{s,t=1}^{d}\delta_{s,t}\sum_{i\in I_{s}}\sum_{j\in I_{t}}\frac{x_{i}x_{j}}{\left|\!\left|\!\left|\bm{x}\right|\!\right|\!\right|_{n,p}^{2}}\right)d\rho_{p}(\bm{x}).

Applying the GHS transform to linearize the Hamiltonian as before, one can perform a similar analysis in the high-temperature regime and
p\geqslant 2
. Note that the Hubbard–Stratonovich transform method has been used in
Kirsch and
Toth (2022)
for multi-group Curie–Weiss model with
\pm 1
spin case
\Omega_{N}
.

7.
Discussions and Further Questions

1.

For
p\geqslant 2
and
\beta<\beta_{c}(p)
(the high-temperature regime), we have shown that the magnetization has a Gaussian fluctuation. The natural following question is to obtain a rate of convergence for CLT. This will be done in a forthcoming paper.

2.

Another generalization one can investigate is a multiple
\ell^{p}
constraints version. One can consider the Curie–Weiss model, where the spin configuration is the intersection of several
\ell^{p}
balls for different

Nam (2020)
and nonlinear Schrödinger equations.

3.

One can generalize the
\ell^{p}
constraint model to
p
-spin model, where the Hamiltonian is defined by the interaction between
k
many spins for
k\geqslant 3
.

4.

It is interesting to study a geometric interpretation for the GHS transformation. To prove the GHS transform, we introduced a random variable

independent of

in Proposition
1.13
that satisfies
Z_{p}U_{p,q}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{d}}}{{=}}Z_{q}
. For the existence of such random variables, we constructed a stochastic process
U_{p,q}
in
p
for fixed
