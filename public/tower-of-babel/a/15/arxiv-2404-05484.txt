On Computational Modeling of Sleep-Wake Cycle
Xin Li

Abstract
Why do mammals need to sleep? Neuroscience treats sleep and wake as default and perturbation modes of the brain. It is hypothesized that the brain self-organizes neural activities without environmental inputs. This paper presents a new computational model of the sleep-wake cycle (SWC) for learning and memory. During the sleep mode, the memory consolidation by the thalamocortical system is abstracted by a disentangling operator that maps context-dependent representations (CDR) to context-independent representations (CIR) for generalization. Such a disentangling operator can be mathematically formalized by an integral transform that integrates the context variable from CDR. During the wake mode, the memory formation by the hippocampal-neocortical system is abstracted by an entangling operator from CIR to CDR where the context is introduced by physical motion. When designed as inductive bias, entangled CDR linearizes the problem of unsupervised learning for sensory memory by direct-fit. The concatenation of disentangling and entangling operators forms a disentangling-entangling cycle (DEC) as the building block for sensorimotor learning. We also discuss the relationship of DEC and SWC to the perception-action cycle (PAC) for internal model learning and perceptual control theory for the ecological origin of natural languages.

Geometric Intuition

Graph theory has a well-known educational puzzle named “Three Utilities Problem". This puzzle can be formalized as a problem in topological graph theory by asking whether the complete bipartite graph

K_{3,3}
, has a graph embedding in the plane. The impossibility of the puzzle corresponds to the fact that
K_{3,3}
is not a planar graph. However, removing the planarity constraint can easily solve such a puzzle. This is a simple example illustrating the blessing of dimensionality to the problem of manifold disentanglement/untangling. In our daily lives, the overpass at the intersection is another example of entanglement/tangling - the traffic jam problem can be effectively solved by lifting the space along the vertical dimension. By analogy, we can imagine that the pattern classification problem can be facilitated by a similar strategy of dimensionality increase (i.e., entanglement). We observe that such inductive bias can be exploited to discriminate patterns in a higher dimensional space by inducing motion as the contextual variable.

3
Entanglement and Disentanglement for Wake and Sleep

Our biological intuition is based on observing the alternating default and perturbation modes in SWC. It has been hypothesized in
[
9
]
that “sleep may serve to cement or consolidate memories by replaying parts and details of the waking experience”. The process of memory consolidation is believed to occur during REM sleep
[
74
]
, which transforms episodic memory into semantic memory. Episodic memories are context-dependent representations (CDR) in which context is specified by the spatiotemporal position associated with the animal’s behavior. Semantic memories are context-independent representation (CIR) as the consequence of generalization. Geometrically,
CIR\rightarrow CDR
corresponds to dimensionality expansion because context is induced as design bias
[
25
]
;
CDR\rightarrow CIR
is implemented by dimensionality reduction because aliasing or collapse of contextual variables leads to generalization
[
46
]
.

Computationally, we abstract the pair of dimensionality expansion and reduction operators into a cycle between CIR and CDR, as shown in Fig.
3
a. Assuming sensory raw inputs are data belonging to a low-dimensional manifold embedded in a high-dimensional space, the objective of manifold entanglement or tangling is to overfit the data for specification by introducing context as design/inductive bias
[
3
]
. This objective can be facilitated by embedding the manifold into a high-dimensional space, as Whitney’s embedding theorem suggests. Therefore, manifold entanglement/tangling maps sensory raw input to a high-dimensional latent space where the task of separating different objects can be facilitated by linearization (e.g., by linear dichotomy
[
12
]
). The dual process of manifold disentanglement/untangling is to reduce the dimensionality of data representation for generalization. A common example is aliasing
[
46
]
, which collapses the variables with a common state (context). This section will study the pair of manifold disentanglement and entanglement separately.

3.1
Wake Mode for Specification: Dimensionality Expansion via Context Dependency

The intuition behind context dependency can be easily explained by the toy example of binary classification in supervised learning (e.g., cats vs. dogs). Suppose we collect sensory raw data associated with two classes
\textbf{X}_{0},\textbf{X}_{1}\in R^{n}
corresponding to classes 0 and 1, respectively. The raw data in
R^{n}
is generally not linearly separable but they can be made linearly separable by lifting the data distribution to a new dimension - i.e.,
(\textbf{X}_{c},c)\in R^{n+1}
where
c=0,1
(a linear classifier
c=1/2
is sufficient). Such an idea of dimensionality expansion can be better articulated as a strategy of context dependency that transforms a supervised learning problem into an unsupervised learning problem
[
2
]
. The key idea is that the class labels associated with different objects can be explicitly introduced as inductive bias or context variables.

Alternatively, we note that the spatio-temporal locality of the object often enforces the context information in the physical world. In other words, the local receptive field of a mammalian brain can only pay attention to a single object in space at any time. This strategy of overfitting with design bias implements recently proposed “direct-fit-to-nature”
[
30
]
, which relies on over-parameterized models to provide mindless generalization. The shared vision between our context dependency and direct-fit is to densely sample the problem space so that local heuristics (e.g., nearest neighbor or averaging) will be sufficient for situating novel observations within the context of past observations. This vision is also consistent with intelligence without representation
[
5
]
which advocates for learning without explicitly representing underlying rules and principles in a human-interpretable way
[
30
]
. We can generalize the strategy of direct-fit by context-dependency for high-dimensional labels/context as follows.

Proposition 1 (Specification). Context Dependency for Direct-Fit
.

Given sensory raw data
\textbf{X}\in R^{n}
and its associated label
\textbf{C}\in R^{k}
, we can directly fit the data distribution
P(\textbf{X})
by introducing context dependency as a joint PDF
P(\textbf{X},\textbf{C})\in R^{n+k}
.

The proof of Proposition 1 is a straightforward extension of the strategy used in binary classification. By considering the discrete representation of label or context
C
, linear separability is guaranteed in the expanded higher-dimensional space. Note that when the dimensionality of
C
is higher than that of
X
, we will observe high-dimensional encoding space as envisioned in direct-fit
[
30
]
, which turns an extrapolation problem into an interpolation problem.

To facilitate our discussion, we will call the raw sensory observation
X
context-independent representation (CIR) and the entangled data after encoding
(\textbf{X},\textbf{C})
context-dependent representation (CDR). A moment of thought will reveal the overfitting trouble with CDR immediately. This is indeed the consequence of entangling the data manifold
P(\textbf{X})
by dimensionality expansion during the wake mode. It achieved the goal of direct fit by over-fitting the data in the lifted multi-dimensional embedding space of CDR. without ecological regularization, CDR will have an undesirable generalization performance (e.g., a random perturbation along the decision boundary by observation noise will cause the incorrect classification result). Fortunately, we have a remedy for such mishaps in the sleep mode, as we will discuss next.

3.2
Sleep Mode for Generalization: Dimensionality Reduction via Integral Transform

The dual operation of sleep mode can be understood as the extension of aliasing
[
46
]
by memory consolidation. The basic idea behind aliasing is to provide useful invariants or generalizations by representing equivalent states in which the same action/class is needed. Aliasing is usually achieved by dimensionality reduction, which collapses the distribution with shared context variables. More generally, we suggest that generalization can be achieved by mapping from CDR to CIR using integral transforms. The trick is to selectively remove or collapse hidden states
C
where the context dependency dictates the criterion of selectivity. Using contextual information
C
as the guardrail, we are guaranteed a faithful generalization without the risk of over-generalization. Formally, we have the following results.

Proposition 2 (Generalization). Integral Transform for Interpolation-based Generalization
.

Given an entangled CDR
P(\textbf{X},\textbf{C})
, we can restore the disentangled CIR
P(\textbf{X})
by an integral transform
P(\textbf{X})=\int P(\textbf{X},\textbf{C})d\textbf{C}
.

Note that integral transforms are intrinsically interpolation or averaging in high-dimensional space. From the perspective of distributed representation,
[
33
]
, we can interpret context
C
as a distributed set of representing
X
. In distributed representations, interpolation-based generalization happens when a neural network learns to map inputs to a high-dimensional space where similar inputs are closer together and dissimilar inputs are farther apart. Integral transform offers one step beyond interpolation-based generalization
[
33
]
because all noise and interference in task-irrelevant dimensions will be filtered out for improved robustness.

The pairing of specification and generalization operators forms a complete model for SWC, as shown in Fig.
3
a. The connection of SWC with direct-fit models
[
30
]
is appealing - the objective of learning is not human-interpretable rules or world representations but rational behaviors for survival and prosperity in the physical world. Toward this objective, evolution has invented a localized computation for embodied cognition to interpolate over task-relevant manifolds (we will elaborate on this issue later when studying sensorimotor interaction). Direct-fit implemenyed by SWC offers a constructive solution to obtain over-parameterized models that are simple and parsimonious. More importantly, the connection of SWC with the evolutionary process is so powerful that we strongly believe it offers an important clue to unlock hidden secrets in developmental and ecological psychology. In summary, SWC forms the building block for our solution to efficient learning problems in general. Before we study the application of this new theory into neuroscience, we report hierarchical extensions of SWC into chains and fundamental group of torus
[
31
]
to enrich our weaponry of constructing over-parameterized direct-fit models.

Biological Implementations and Applications

The biological implementation of both entanglment and disentanglement operators is based on the
polychronization neural groups (PNG) theory
[
36
]
. A direct consequence of direct-fit-to-nature is over-parameterization
[
30
]
to increase the predictive power without explicitly modeling the underlying generative structure of the world. PNG offers a plausible computational model for supporting such mindless yet powerful generalizations. The number of PNG
[
36
]
can exponentially increase as more neurons are involved. Intuitively, one can argue that a high-level PNG with more neurons will have more representation power than a low-level PNG, which makes it a promising candidate for implementing the over-parameterized model required by direc-fit
[
30
]
.

We first extend Hebbian postulate
[
32
]
to make it work for PNG as follows - “A low-level PNG that
drives
a high-level PNG to fire tends to wire together”
[
19
]
. Driving is hypothesized as a mechanism for generalizing cloning
[
75
]
to achieve specificity (e.g., unidirectional place cells
[
9
]
and context-dependent concept cells). The opposite direction from high-level to low-level generalizes aliasing
[
46
]
to achieve invariance or generalization (e.g., omnidirectional place cells and context-independent concept cells
[
59
,
58
]
). Due to dimensionality expansion (the consequence of recursive untangling), the support of CDR would increase exponentially. PNG-based implementation can handle this combinatorial explosion because of its enormous capacity
[
36
]
.

The connection of PNG with SWC
[

]
allows us to study cognitive functions from a novel DEC perspective. Using DEC as the vocabulary, we attempt to construct a computational model for SWC as the first step toward a more comprehensive neural syntax

]

. Navigation represents an easier learning problem that evolution solved long before the vision problem. Our understanding about this task has converged to a theory based on cognitive maps

Cognitive maps organize knowledge to support flexible and adaptive behavior. How do mammalian brains encode 2D physical space information into 1D spike sequence? This question is at the core of understanding the relationship between space and time in its simplest form. The representation of spatial information (Euclidean or non-Euclidean) plays an essential role in the building of cognitive maps. A graph-based representation such as clone-structured causal graphs (CSCG)
[

has been widely adopted for encoding the physical space into action-related state space. During the construction of the CSCG model, the aliasing problem is solved by the cloning trick, which can be interpreted as a degenerated untangling operator.

Computational Modeling of Sensorimotor and Social Interactions

DEC-based local computation for direct-fit inspired us to propose a new conceptual framework called “localized embodied cognition” with the locality principle in mind from problem formulation to solution algorithms. We still use the world “as its own model”
[
5
]
, but the way local embodied cognition never attempts to solve a global problem locally. An organism needs to couple sensory with motor systems to achieve various goals but never stores knowledge about the global environment. Instead, the bodily interactions with the environment (situatedness) are persistently maintained by the neocortex (a single book)’s interaction with the hippocampus (a large library). The cognitive map in the hippocampus offers a situational context to interpret the sensorimotor interaction occurring in the neocortex. In this section, we study sensorimotor interaction and hippocampal-neocortical interaction using the newly-constructed neural syntax.

SMC-based Social Interaction.

Carrying the “more is different” principle one step further, we can envision a bigger cycle connecting a pair of SMCs. When one person becomes another’s environment, the communication between two SMCs forms another bigger cycle known as non-verbal communication. The communication bandwidth of such non-verbal communication is limited by the locality of our sensory organs. Even though within each neocortex, the hierarchical organization maximizes the information flow inward, social communication (flowing outward) has to wait for evolution to improve its efficiency.

The fundamental role played by natural language is to facilitate communication among people - from non-verbal to verbal. If one concatenates the SMCs of two people (for communication purposes), we observe a larger cycle
SMC_{A}\leftrightarrows SMC_{B}

Modeling the bigger cycle (refer to Appendix A for a more detailed discussion about the ecological origin of languages) for understanding the social nature of language might offer new insight to the neural exploitation hypothesis

Conclusions, Limitations, and Broader Impact

Conclusions:

This paper studies the computational modeling of sleep-wake cycle (SWC) from a direct-fit perspective.
The wake mode entangles the sensory observations by binding them into gnostic cells. The sleep mode may consolidate memories by replaying the waking experience. Memory consolidation is abstracted by an integral transform in this paper, which disentangles the manifold for generalization. The pair of entanglement and disentanglement operators implement the mapping between CIR and CDR, demonstrating the importance of context dependency to learning and memory. The hierarchical extension of disentanglement-entanglement cycles into high-order topological structures (e.g., torus and chain) is an attempt to enhance the computational modeling capability of SWC for more sophisticated cognitive tasks.

Limitations:

Our paper is inspired by many cycles discovered by Buzsaki in the rhythms of the brain
[
9
]
. Its biggest limitation is the lack of experimental verification. As pointed out in
[
9
]
, studying the brain’s default mode is even more challenging than the perturbation mode. Experiments related to sleep-dependent memory consolidation
[
69
]
only indirectly provide evidence to support the role of SWC in semantic memory. Many cycles discovered in this paper are limited to theoretical appeal, whose practical implications and connections remain to be established. Due to space limitation, we have skipped the connection of DEC with limit-cycle behavior of Wilson-Cowan model
[
76
]
and its related hysteresis cycle (the physical origin of memory).

Broader Impacts:

Understanding the sleep-wake cycle paves the path to unlocking the secret of learning and memory. At the device level of individual neurons, limit-cycle behavior has been well studied by the Wilson-Cowan model
[
76
]
. At the circuit level of neural groups, canonical circuits
[
39
]
have formed the cycle for predictive processing. This paper marks the first step toward discovering cycles at the system level of neural areas. Since SWC is fundamental to almost all cognitive functions, its computational modeling might facilitate the study of both normal behaviors and neurological disorders such as autism and anxiety. Studying the default state of the brain (sleep mode) is deemed to complement the study of the perturbation state (wake mode).

Appendix A
Ecological View of Sleep/Wake Modes and Sleep-Wake Cycle

The Brain’s Default State: Sleep Mode.

According to Gyorgy Buzsaki
[
9
]
, rest and sleep are characterized by self-organized oscillations in the absence of environmental inputs. Without an outside influence, the evolving state in the sleep mode is deterministic (i.e., completely predictable). The role of sleep in memory consolidation has been observed in the experiments with sleep spindles
[
64
]
. It was hypothesized that during spindles, specific neuronal populations become more strongly modulated through virtual replay
[
37
]
. Experiments with the visual cortex and hippocampus have suggested that they support simultaneous reactivation of coherent memory traces during slow-wave sleep (SWS) and rapid-eye-movement (REM) sleep
[
37
]
. All these experimental findings are consistent with Proposition 2 in our SWC theory. In summary, Memory consolidation is a process by which the hippocampus guides the organization of the information stored in the neocortex
[
68
]
. The consequence of self-organization is that the consolidated memory becomes independent of the hippocampus - e.g., from episodic memory (context-dependent) to semantic memory (context-independent).

The Brain’s Perturbation State: Wake Mode.

According to Karl Friston, “brain dynamics constantly shift from the complex to the predictable” (pp. 111,
[
9
]
). The waking brain has been hypothesized to be optimized for encoding memories
[
60
]
. From sensory perception to motor control, goal-oriented behaviors offer a unifying perspective of understanding the brain’s state during the wake mode. According to Hebbian postulate
[
32
]
, the synchronization of cell assemblies (a.k.a., polychronization of neural groups
[
36
]
) drives the brain dynamics “from stable incoherence through dynamic instability to complete entrainment”
[
20
]
. Unlike the deterministic default mode, the stochastic nature of environmental inputs dictates the brain to strike a good balance between specification and generalization. By encoding history into memory, complex systems manage to make reliable predictions. Proposition 1 in our SWC theory is consistent with this line of reasoning because CDR facilitates the prediction by inductive bias.

Sleep-Wake Cycle and Memory Consolidation.

Not only humans but also mammals go to sleep
[
66
]
. Across mammals, the amount and nature of sleep are often correlated with their body size, age, and ecological variables (e.g., terrestrial or aquatic). The role of the SWC in energy conservation and nervous system recuperation has been widely observed in mammals. The recognition of SWC as a biological rhythm controlled by brain oscillators (i.e., beyond the framework of homeostatic principles) has only been more widely accepted since the 1960s
[
42
]
. The basis of SWC has been related to the process of toxins’ accumulation and removal
[
42
]
. The neurobiology of SWC was reviewed in
[
21
]
, highlighting the sleep architecture, circadian regulation, and regulatory feedback. The close relationship between memory encoding/consolidation and SWC was studied by Buzsaki in 1998
[
7
]
(a more recent review can be found in
[
17
]
). The integral transform in Proposition 2 might be a mathematical abstraction of synaptic modifications in the hippocampus during sleep. The importance of sleep patterns to the preservation of experience-induced synaptic modifications has been documented by various studied on sleep deprivation
[
40
]
.

Appendix B
On Ecological Origin of Languages

The origin of language was once a taboo among linguists due to a lack of scientific methodology
[
34
]
. In 1950, Alan Turing invented an imitation game (later known as the Turing test) for artificial intelligence (AI)
[
72
]
. In the Turing test, a human evaluator would judge natural language conversations between a human and a machine designed to generate human-like responses. Recent breakthroughs in generative AI (e.g., ChatGPT developed by OpenAI
[
6
]
) have shown convincing evidence for machine’s nearly passing the Turing test (“Essentially, whether I pass the Turing Test might depend on the context and the criteria set by the human interacting with me.” - quoted from ChatGPT). However, passing a Turing test does not imply the possession of minds like humans do, as demonstrated by John Seale in Chinese room thought experiments in 1980.
The fundamental reason behind the Chinese room argument is that the Turing test is a top-down approach to understanding intelligence
[
9
]
.

An alternative approach is bottom-up (a.k.a. inside-out), which requires a deeper understanding of the mechanism underlying the structure and function of neural systems.
As discovered by Vernon Moutcastle in 1957
[
52
]
, there is no visible difference among the six-layer organization of the neocortex no matter which area we look into. Based on this observation, he hypothesized that all cognitive functions, from spatial navigation and motor control to vision and language, result from a universal cortical processing algorithm. According to Theodosius Dobzhansky, “nothing in biology makes sense except in the light of evolution.” The evolution of mammalian brains offers fruitful insight into the functional organization of the neocortex. As Hans Moravec says in
[
49
]
, abstract thought “is a new trick, perhaps less than 100 thousand years old….effective only because it is supported by this much older and much more powerful, though usually unconscious, sensorimotor knowledge.” To understand language, we must understand sensorimotor learning first.

Following the clue offered by the evolution of mammalian brains, one can understand the allocortex that is sandwiched between the old and new brain for spatial navigation
[
71
]
. The discovery of place cells in the hippocampus in 1978
[
55
]
marked a milestone in our journey to a better understanding of how the brain works. This line of research has accumulated many more discoveries (e.g., grid cells
[
50
,
51
]
) and computational models related to navigation, contributing to our knowledge about how to build cognitive maps
[
75
]
. Along this line of reasoning, the relationship between place cells and memory consolidation
[
69
]
is particularly fascinating because if Mountcastle were correct, the trick used by nature to create gnostic units such as place cells is likely to be recycled for other cognitive functions (e.g., concept cells in object recognition
[
59
,
58
]
).

To solve the navigation problem, context (for specification) and aliasing (for generalization) are two clever tricks invented by nature. As shown in
[
9
]
(pp. 327), oscillatory timing of cell assemblies defines the spatiotemporal context for a currently coded item (e.g., the current position of the animal). During the wake mode (perturbation state), animals generate unidirectional place fields as context-dependent episodic memories about one-dimensional travel routes. During the sleep mode (default state), memory consolidation uses an aliasing trick to collapse unidirectional place cells with identical positions but different contexts into omnidirectional place cells. Unlike unidirectional place cells that are context-dependent, omnidirectional place cells are context-independent, implying better generalization. Such an aliasing trick becomes Maslow’s hammer
[
45
]
to tackle other cognitive tasks.

Nature has cleverly used the aliasing trick, based on Maslow’s hammer, to solve the learning problem by memory. In simple terms, the spatio-temporal context in navigation becomes a response vector to a feature input in perception or action. The problem of sensorimotor learning is then transformed into a generalized navigation problem of memorizing a “travel sequence” in the latent space
[
67
]
that comprises the concatenation of feature input and response vectors.
During the wake mode, the brain encodes many such travel sequences, which are known to suffer from overfitting. However, during sleep mode, memory consolidation collapses episodic memory into semantic memory. This is achieved by binding polychronization neural groups with the same feature input but different responses (contextual variables) together for generalization. During sleep-wake cycles
[
74
,
69
]
, overfitted context-dependent representations (CDR) are self-organized into context-independent representations (CIR) without the bias-variance barrier
[
25
]
or the curse of dimensionality
[
4
]
because the brain does not solve any optimization problem but count on memory (Maslow’s Hammer) for adaptive behaviors.

The evolution of sensorimotor learning greatly contributes to human flexible behaviors in terms of perception and action
[
67
]
. However, social communication between two people requires another clever recycling of Maslow’s Hammer. Unlike abstract thoughts that can be done internally in our mind, social communication requires the completion of an external cycle connecting the sensorimotor systems of two people. Such an external loop is at the mercy of the information bottleneck of our sensory organs because we cannot directly communicate with others like a three-body civilization (in SCIFI fiction “Three-Body”) that cannot lie. Instead, we have to count on external communication channels to exchange our thoughts. Visual communication such as sign language is limited by its capacity; natural language is more effective because the neocortex can recycle its sensorimotor skills learned from interacting with the world (corresponding to the internal cycle, as shown Fig.
6
) to interacting with the peer (corresponding to the external cycle, as shown Fig.
A1
a). Such ecological explanation for the origin of natural languages is another evidence supporting Maslow’s Hammer used by evolution.

This line of reasoning explains the hierarchical organization discovered by Noam Chomsky in 1957
[
10
]
and supports the Sapir–Whorf hypothesis
[
38
]
in linguistic relativity. The Sapir–Whorf hypothesis, also known as
linguistic relativity
, is a concept in linguistics that suggests the structure of a language affects its speakers’ worldview or cognition. In summary, Chomsky hierarchy (Fig.
A1
b) mirrors the nested organization of the physical world.
