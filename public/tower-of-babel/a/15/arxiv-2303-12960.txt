Abstract
. We develop a new method leading to an elementary proof of a generalization of Gromov’s theorem about non existence of Hölder embeddings into the Heisenberg group.



2020 Mathematics Subject Classification
Primary 53C17, 53C23; Secondary 55M05, 57R40, 58A10

1.
Introduction

The Heisenberg group
\mathbb{H}^{n}
equipped with the Carnot-Carathéodory metric
d_{c}
is homeomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{2n+1}
, but its Hausdorff dimension equals
2n+2
. On compact sets the metric
d_{c}
satisfies
|p-q|\lesssim d_{c}(p,q)\lesssim|p-q|^{1/2}
. In fact, in some directions the metric
d_{c}
is comparable to the Euclidean metric, while in other directions it is of fractal nature. The Heisenberg group is also isomorphic to the standard contact structure on
\mathbb{R}^{2n+1}
, and the metric
d_{c}
gives a natural way of measuring distances between points along curves tangent to the contact distribution. It is well known that every Legendrian (i.e., horizontal) map
f:U\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{H}^{n}\cong\mathbb{R}^{2n+1}
, that is smooth or Lipschitz, must satisfy
\operatorname{rank}Df\leq n
, and hence there is no Lipschitz embedding of
U
into
\mathbb{H}^{n}
, if
m\geq n+1
. On the other hand, any smooth or Lipschitz map
f:U\to\mathbb{R}^{2n+1}
, is
\frac{1}{2}
-Hölder continuous as a map into
\mathbb{H}^{n}
. In this context Gromov in his seminal work
[
7
]
, initiated the program of investigating properties of Hölder continuous mappings into the Heisenberg group. He proved the following non-embedding result
[
7
, 3.1.A]
:

Theorem 1.1
.

Every
\gamma
-Hölder continuous embedding
f:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{H}^{n}
,
m\geq n+1
, satisfies
\gamma\leq\frac{n+1}{n+2}
.

The original proof is actually quite difficult since it requires the
h
-principle and microflexibility.
For a more detailed presentation of this proof and another proof based on the Rumin complex, see
[
12
]
. There is also a proof of the non-existence of an embedding
f\in C^{0,\gamma}(\mathbb{R}^{2},\mathbb{H}^{1})
,
\gamma>2/3
, due to Züst
[
17
]
, based on a factorization through a topological tree. However, the argument cannot be adapted to the general case of Theorem
1.1
.

Gromov
[
7
]
, conjectured that if
f:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{H}^{1}
is a
\gamma
-Hölder embedding, then
\gamma\leq\frac{1}{2}
. Back in 2015, Hajłasz, Mirra, and Schikorra ran numerical experiments suggesting that in fact an embedding could possibly exist for all
\gamma<\frac{2}{3}
. They provided numerical support indicating that any smooth map
f:\mathbb{S}^{1}\to\mathbb{H}^{1}
admits a
\gamma
-Hölder extension
f:\mathbb{B}^{2}\to\mathbb{H}^{1}
if
\gamma\in(0,\frac{2}{3})
. Recently, Wenger and Young
[
16
]
, proved that the pair
(\mathbb{R}^{2},\mathbb{H}^{1})
has the
\gamma
-Hölder extension property for any
\gamma\in(0,\frac{2}{3})
. In particular, any mapping
f\in C^{0,\gamma}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathbb{H}^{1})
admits a
C^{0,\gamma}
-extension to
\mathbb{B}^{2}
. However, their construction will not lead to an embedding.

In the last two decades, there has been a substantial interest in studying Hölder continuous functions and mappings from the analytic and geometric point of view.
While Hölder continuous mappings need not be differentiable at any point, a new approach to analysis of such maps has been developed by Brezis and Nguyen
[
2
,
3
]
, as an abstract theory, Conti, De Lellis and Székelyhidi
[
6
]
, with applications to rigidity of isometric embeddings, and Züst
[
18
]
, in the context of currents.
The theory plays also an important role in Isett’s solution of the Onsager conjecture
[
10
]
.
See also
[
15
,
14
,
11
]
, for related research.
For a detailed study of Jacobians and pullbacks of differential forms by Hölder continuous mappings with applications to the geometry and topology of the Heisenberg groups, see
[
8
]
.
This paper develops at depth the theory related to the proof of Theorem
1.2
presented below.

The purpose of this work is to provide an elementary proof of the following generalization of Gromov’s non-embedding theorem.

Theorem 1.2
.

Suppose that
m\geq n+1
,

\frac{1}{2}\leq\gamma\leq\frac{n+1}{n+2}\quad\text{and}\quad\theta=\frac{n+1}{n}-\frac{2\gamma}{n}.

Then, there does not exist a map

f\in C^{0,\gamma+}({\mathbb{B}}^{m},\mathbb{H}^{n})\cap C^{0,\theta}({\mathbb{B}}^{m},\mathbb{R}^{2n+1})

or a map

f\in C^{0,\gamma}({\mathbb{B}}^{m},\mathbb{H}^{n})\cap C^{0,\theta+}({\mathbb{B}}^{m},\mathbb{R}^{2n+1}),

such that
f|_{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}
is a topological embedding.

Here,
C^{0,\alpha}({\mathbb{B}}^{m},X)
stands for the class of Hölder continuous mappings into a metric space
X
.
Moreover,
f\in C^{0,\alpha+}({\mathbb{B}}^{m},X)
means that

\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\ \sup\left\{\frac{d(f(x),f(y))}{|x-y|^{\alpha}}:\ x,y\in{\mathbb{B}}^{m},\ 0<|x-y|\leq t\right\}=0.

This is a natural class of mappings: uniformly continuous mappings are precisely mappings of the class
C^{0,0+}
and if
\alpha\in(0,1)
, then
C^{0,\alpha+}(\mathbb{R}^{m})
is the subspace of
C^{0,\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{m})

that consists of functions that can be approximated by
C^{\infty}
functions in the
C^{0,\alpha}
norm on compact sets.

If
\gamma=\frac{n+1}{n+2}
, then
\theta=\frac{n+1}{n+2}
, and we obtain the following strengthened version of Gromov’s theorem:

Corollary 1.3
.

If
m\geq n+1
, then there is no map
f\in C^{0,\frac{n+1}{n+2}+}(\mathbb{B}^{m},\mathbb{H}^{n})
such that
f|_{\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}
is a topological embedding.

The recent work of Wenger and Young
[
16
]
, shows that
Corollary

1.3
is sharp when
n=1
: for any
\gamma<\frac{2}{3}
, any
C^{0,\gamma}
-embedding of the the
\mathbb{S}^{1}
-sphere into
\mathbb{H}^{1}
can be extended to a map
f\in C^{0,\gamma}({\mathbb{B}^{2}},\mathbb{H}^{1})
.

When
\gamma=\frac{1}{2}
, then
\theta=1
, and we obtain in particular the following result from
[
1
, Theorem 1.11]
that was proved by different techniques:

Corollary 1.4
.

If
m\geq n+1
, then there is no embedding
f\in C^{0,\frac{1}{2}+}({\mathbb{B}^{m}},\mathbb{H}^{n})\cap C^{0,1}({\mathbb{B}^{m}},\mathbb{R}^{2n+1})
.

Remark 1.5
.

In this work we focus our attention on Hölder continuous maps both for simplicity and because Gromov’s theorem is stated for Hölder maps.
However, since the proof implicitly relies on distributional Jacobians and distributional pullbacks of forms by Hölder mappings, the results obtained here can likely be generalized to limiting fractional Sobolev spaces.
For this one should combine the present arguments with the techniques developed in
[
14
,
2
,
11
,
13
]
. Also, there is likely a limiting Besov-space result containing all previous examples similar to the limiting Besov space in the Onsager conjecture,
[
5
]
.

2.
Preliminaries

In this section we recall basic definitions and we collect auxiliary results needed in the proof of Theorem
1.2
.

For non-negative expressions
A
and
B
we write
A\lesssim B
if
A\leq CB
for some constant
C>0
and
A\approx B
if
A\lesssim B\lesssim A
. The unit ball and the balls of radius
r
centered at
0
will be denoted by
\mathbb{B}^{m}
and
\mathbb{B}^{m}_{r}
.
\mathbb{S}^{m}
will stand for the unit sphere centered at the origin.
U
will always be an open subset in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
. We will denote the
L^{p}
norm by
\|f\|_{p}
or
\|f\|_{L^{p}(U)}
.

If
f\in C^{0,\gamma}(U)
, then we write

[f]_{\gamma}=[f]_{\gamma,U}=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y\in U\\
x\neq y\end{subarray}}\frac{|f(x)-f(y)|}{|x-y|^{\gamma}}\,,\quad\text{and}\quad[f]_{\gamma,\varepsilon}=[f]_{\gamma,\varepsilon,U}=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y\in U\\
0<|x-y|\leq\varepsilon\end{subarray}}\frac{|f(x)-f(y)|}{|x-y|^{\gamma}}\,.

Note that if
f\in C^{0,\gamma+}_{\rm loc}(U)
, then for any
U^{\prime}\Subset U
we have that
[f]_{\gamma,\varepsilon,U^{\prime}}\to 0
as
\varepsilon\to 0
.

2.1.
Pullbacks of differential forms

The spaces of smooth differential
k
-forms on
U
, and smooth
k
-forms with compact support in
U
, will be denoted by
\Omega^{k}(U)
and
\Omega_{c}^{k}(U)
respectively. If the coefficients of the differential forms will be in
L^{\infty}
,
C^{0,\gamma}
etc. we will write

\Omega^{k}L^{\infty}(U)
,
\Omega^{k}C^{0,\gamma}(U)
etc.

Let
\phi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{B}^{m})
be a standard mollifier and
\phi_{\varepsilon}(x)=\varepsilon^{-m}\phi(x/\varepsilon)
. Then

f_{\varepsilon}:=f*\phi_{\varepsilon}
satisfies

Df_{\varepsilon}(x)=\varepsilon^{-m-1}\int_{\mathbb{B}^{m}_{\varepsilon}}(f(x-z)-f(x))D\phi\Big(\frac{z}{\varepsilon}\Big)\,dz,\quad\|Df_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{B}^{m}(x_{o},r))}\lesssim\varepsilon^{\gamma-1}[f]_{\gamma,\varepsilon,\mathbb{B}^{m}(x_{o},2r)},

provided
0<\varepsilon<r
. This estimate easily implies

Lemma 2.1
.

If
f\in C^{0,\gamma}(U,\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
\gamma\in(0,1]
, and
B(x_{o},2r)\subset U
, then for any

\kappa\in\Omega^{k}L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
we have

\|f_{\varepsilon}^{*}\kappa\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{B}^{m}(x_{o},r))}\lesssim\|\kappa\|_{\infty}[f]^{k}_{\gamma,\varepsilon,\mathbb{B}^{m}(x_{o},2r)}\varepsilon^{k(\gamma-1)},\quad\text{provided }0<\varepsilon<r.

The implied constant depends on
m
,
d
,
k
and
\phi
only.

2.2.
The Heisenberg group

We assume that the reader is familiar with the Heisenberg groups, but we recall some definitions to fix notation.
Denote coordinates in
\mathbb{R}^{2n+1}
by
(x,y,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}
.
The Heisenberg group
\mathbb{H}^{n}=\mathbb{R}^{2n+1}
is a Lie group with the group law

(x,y,t)*(x^{\prime},y^{\prime},t^{\prime})=\Big(x+x^{\prime},y+y^{\prime},t+t^{\prime}+2\sum_{j=1}^{n}(y_{j}x_{j}^{\prime}-x_{j}y_{j}^{\prime})\Big).

The Heisenberg group is equipped with the
2n
-dimensional horizontal distribution which is the kernel of the standard contact form

\upalpha=dt+2\sum_{j=1}^{n}(x_{j}dy_{j}-y_{j}dx_{j}).

The Carnot-Carathéodory metric is defined as infimum of lengths of horizontal curves (i.e. curves tangent to the horizontal distribution) that connect given two points. The length of such curves is computed with respect to a left invariant Riemannian metric. In other words the Heisenberg group is the standard contact structure on
\mathbb{R}^{2n+1}
equipped with a natural metric of measuring distances along Legendrian curves.

It turns out that the metric
d_{c}
is bi-Lipschitz equivalent with the Korányi metric

d_{K}(p,q):=\|q^{-1}*p\|_{K},\quad\text{where}\quad\|(x,y,t)\|_{K}:=(|(x,y)|^{4}+t^{2})^{1/4}.

In what follows we will assume that
\mathbb{H}^{n}
is a metric space with respect to the metric
d_{K}
. It will be convenient to write

(1)

d_{K}(p,q)=(|\pi(p)-\pi(q)|^{4}+|\varphi(p,q)|^{2})^{1/4}\approx|\pi(p)-\pi(q)|+|\varphi(p,q)|^{1/2},

where
\pi:\mathbb{R}^{2n+1}\to\mathbb{R}^{2n}
,
\pi(x,y,t)=(x,y)
is the orthogonal projection and

(2)

\varphi(p,q)=t-t^{\prime}+2\sum_{j=1}^{n}(x_{j}^{\prime}y_{j}-x_{j}y_{j}^{\prime})\quad\text{for $p=(x,y,t)$, $q=(x^{\prime},y^{\prime},t^{\prime})$}.

It is important to note that the expression
\varphi(p,q)
is somewhat similar to the contact form
\upalpha
. It will play an important role in our proofs.

Recall that if
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{2n+1}
is a compact set, then there is a constant
C\geq 1
such that

(3)

C^{-1}|p-q|\leq d_{K}(p,q)\leq C|p-q|^{1/2}\quad\text{for }p,q\in K.

In particular, the identity map
\operatorname{id}:\mathbb{H}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{2n+1}
is locally Lipschitz continuous.

Lemma 2.2
.

Suppose that

f\in C^{0,\gamma}(U,\mathbb{H}^{n})
,
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
, and

\gamma\in(0,1]
. If
\mathbb{B}^{m}(x_{o},2r)\subset U
, then

\|f^{*}_{\varepsilon}\upalpha\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{B}^{m}(x_{o},r))}\leq 4^{\gamma}\|D\phi\|_{1}[f]_{\gamma,2\varepsilon,\mathbb{B}^{m}(x_{o},2r)}^{2}\,\varepsilon^{2\gamma-1}\quad\text{for all $0<\varepsilon<r$.}

Proof.

Using notation
f=(f^{x},f^{y},f^{t})
, (
2
), and the fact that for a fixed point
p\in\mathbb{B}^{m}(x_{o},r)
, we have

\int_{\mathbb{B}^{m}_{\varepsilon}}f_{\varepsilon}^{t}(p)d\phi(z/\varepsilon)\,dz=0
, the following equalities are easy to verify

\begin{split}&(f^{*}_{\varepsilon}\upalpha)(p)=df^{t}_{\varepsilon}(p)+2\sum_{j=1}^{n}(f_{\varepsilon}^{x_{j}}(p)df_{\varepsilon}^{y_{j}}(p)-f_{\varepsilon}^{y_{j}}(p)df_{\varepsilon}^{x_{j}}(p))=\\
&\varepsilon^{-m-1}\int_{\mathbb{B}^{m}_{\varepsilon}}\left(f^{t}(p-z)-f_{\varepsilon}^{t}(p)+2\sum_{j=1}^{n}\big((f_{\varepsilon}^{x_{j}}(p)f^{y_{j}}(p-z)-f_{\varepsilon}^{y_{j}}(p)f^{x_{j}}(p-z)\big)\right)d\phi\left(\frac{z}{\varepsilon}\right)\,dz\\
&=\varepsilon^{-m-1}\int_{\mathbb{B}^{m}_{\varepsilon}}\int_{\mathbb{B}^{m}_{\varepsilon}}\varphi(f(p-z),f(p-w))\phi_{\varepsilon}(w)d\phi\left(\frac{z}{\varepsilon}\right)\,dw\,dz.\end{split}

Since

|\varphi(f(p-z),f(p-w))|\leq d_{K}(f(p-z),f(p-w))^{2}\leq[f]_{\gamma,2\varepsilon,\mathbb{B}^{m}(x_{o},2r)}^{2}(2\varepsilon)^{2\gamma},

the result easily follows.
∎

Remark 2.3
.

One can show that
Lemma

2.2
implies that for
f\in C^{0,\frac{1}{2}+}(U,\mathbb{H}^{n})
, we have

f^{\ast}(\upalpha)=0

in the distributional sense. If
f\in C^{0,1}(U,\mathbb{H}^{n})
, this is also true in a.e. sense. For more details, see
[
8
]
.

2.3.
Decomposition of forms

In what follows
\upomega=\sum_{j=1}^{n}dx_{j}\wedge dy_{j}\in\bigwedge\nolimits^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2n})^{*}
will denote the standard symplectic form. Then next algebraic result due to Lefschetz is well known, see
[
4
, Proposition 1.1a]
or
[
9
, Proposition 1.2.30]
.

Lemma 2.4
.

The wedge product with the symplectic form

(4)

\upomega\wedge\cdot:\bigwedge\nolimits^{n-1}(\mathbb{R}^{2n})^{*}\to\bigwedge\nolimits^{n+1}(\mathbb{R}^{2n})^{*},

is an isomorphism.

As a straightforward application of Lemma
2.4
we obtain

Lemma 2.5
.

If
\kappa\in\Omega^{n+1}(\mathbb{R}^{2n+1})
, then there are smooth forms

\beta\in\Omega^{n}(\mathbb{R}^{2n+1})
and

\delta\in\Omega^{n-1}(\mathbb{R}^{2n+1})

that do not contain components with
dt
and satisfy

\kappa=\upalpha\wedge\beta+d\upalpha\wedge\delta.

Moreover, if
\kappa(p)=0
, then
\beta(p)=0
and
\delta(p)=0
, i.e.,
{\rm supp}\,\beta\cup{\rm supp}\,\delta={\rm supp}\,\kappa
.

Proof.

If
\pi:\mathbb{R}^{2n+1}\to\mathbb{R}^{2n}
,
\pi(x,y,t)=(x,y)
, then
\pi^{*}\upomega\in\Omega^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2n+1})
is a natural identification of the symplectic form with a
2
-form on
\mathbb{R}^{2n+1}
. Note that

d\upalpha=4\sum_{j=1}^{n}dx_{j}\wedge dy_{j}=4\pi^{*}\upomega
.

With the notation
dx_{I}:=dx_{i_{1}}\wedge\ldots\wedge dx_{i_{k}}
,
I=(i_{1},\ldots,i_{k})
,
1\leq i_{1}<\ldots<i_{k}\leq n
,
|I|=k
, it easily follows from Lemma
2.4
that if
\eta\in\Omega^{n+1}(\mathbb{R}^{2n+1})
, and

\eta=\sum_{|I|+|J|=n+1}\eta_{IJ}\,dx_{I}\wedge dy_{J},

i.e., if
\eta
is a form that does not contain components with
dt
, then
\eta
can be uniquely written as
\eta=d\upalpha\wedge\delta
, where
\delta
does not contain components with
dt
. Since the coefficients of
\delta
are obtained from the coefficients of
\eta
through the isomorphism (
4
) of linear spaces, it follows that
\delta\in\Omega^{n-1}(\mathbb{R}^{2n+1})
and
\delta(p)=0
whenever
\eta(p)=0
.

Since
\{\upalpha(p),dx_{1},dy_{1},\ldots,dx_{n},dy_{n}\}
is a basis of
T^{*}_{p}\mathbb{R}^{2n+1}
, it follows that
elements of the form

dx_{I}\wedge dy_{J},\ |I|+|J|=n+1,\quad\text{and}\quad\upalpha(p)\wedge dx_{I^{\prime}}\wedge dy_{J^{\prime}},\ |I^{\prime}|+|J^{\prime}|=n,

form a basis of
\bigwedge\nolimits^{n+1}T_{p}^{*}\mathbb{R}^{2n+1}
. Therefore, any form
\kappa\in\Omega^{n+1}(\mathbb{R}^{2n+1})
can be uniquely represented as
\eta+\upalpha\wedge\beta
, where at every point,
\eta
belongs to the span of
dx_{I}\wedge dy_{J}
,
|I|+|J|=n+1
and
\beta
belongs to span of
dx_{I^{\prime}}\wedge dy_{J^{\prime}}
,
|I^{\prime}|+|J^{\prime}|=n
. Hence,

\kappa=\eta+\upalpha\wedge\beta=d\upalpha\wedge\delta+\upalpha\wedge\beta.

Since the coefficients of the forms
\eta
and
\beta
, and hence coefficients of the forms
\delta
and
\beta
are obtained from coefficients of
\kappa
(in the standard basis) by linear isomorphisms, it follows that the forms
\delta\in\Omega^{n-1}(\mathbb{R}^{2n+1})
,
\beta\in\Omega^{n}(\mathbb{R}^{2n+1})
are smooth, and
\delta(p)=0
,
\beta(p)=0
, whenever
\kappa(p)=0
.
∎

2.4.
Poincaré and Alexander dualities

Let
f:\mathbb{S}^{k}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
k<m
, be a topological embedding. It follows from the Poincaré and the Alexander dualities that
H^{k+1}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{m}\setminus{}f(\mathbb{S}^{k}))=\mathbb{Z}
. Thus, translating it into the de Rham cohomology, there is a closed form
\kappa\in\Omega_{c}^{k+1}(\mathbb{R}^{m}\setminus f(\mathbb{S}^{k}))
that cannot be written as
\kappa=d\eta
, for any
\eta\in\Omega^{k}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{m}\setminus f(\mathbb{S}^{k}))
. The existence of
\kappa
can be proved using the Mayer-Vietoris sequence. In the next result we construct
\kappa
directly and explicitly. Our proof is elementary and self-contained, but it mimics the construction of the Mayer-Vietoris sequence.

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
f:\mathbb{S}^{k}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
k<m
, be a topological embedding. Then, there is a closed form
\kappa\in\Omega_{c}^{k+1}(\mathbb{R}^{m}\setminus f(\mathbb{S}^{k}))
such that if
f_{i}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{k+1},\mathbb{R}^{m})

and
f_{i}|_{\mathbb{S}^{k}}
converges uniformly to
f
, then there is
i_{o}
such that

(5)

\int_{\mathbb{B}^{k+1}}f_{i}^{*}\kappa=1\quad\text{for all }i\geq i_{o}.

Remark 2.7
.

Note that
\kappa\neq d\eta
for all

\eta\in\Omega_{c}^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{m}\setminus f(\mathbb{S}^{k}))
, as otherwise integral (
5
) would be zero for large
i
, by Stokes’ theorem.

Proof.

Fix
m
throughout the proof and proceed by induction on
k
.
For
k=0
we have
\mathbb{S}^{0}=\{-1,+1\}
and we take
\eta\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{m})
that equals
1
in a neighborhood of
f(+1)
and equal
0
in a neighborhood of
f(-1)
. Then, we define
\kappa=d\eta\in\Omega_{c}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{m}\setminus f(\mathbb{S}^{0}))
. Clearly,
\kappa
is closed. If
i
is sufficiently large, then

\int_{\mathbb{B}^{1}}f_{i}^{*}\kappa=\int_{-1}^{1}f_{i}^{*}d\eta=\eta(f_{i}(+1))-\eta(f_{i}(-1))=1.

Suppose now that the result is true for
k-1
and we will prove it for
k
.
Let
\mathbb{S}^{k}_{+}
,
\mathbb{S}^{k}_{-}
and
\mathbb{S}^{k-1}=\mathbb{S}^{k}_{+}\cap\mathbb{S}^{k}_{-}
, be the closed hemispheres and the equator of
\mathbb{S}^{k}
, respectively. Let
\mathbb{B}^{k}\subset\mathbb{B}^{k+1}
,
\partial\mathbb{B}^{k}=\mathbb{S}^{k-1}
, be the intersection of
\mathbb{B}^{k+1}
with the equatorial hyperplane.

By the induction hypothesis we can find a closed form

\tilde{\kappa}\in\Omega_{c}^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{m}\setminus f(\mathbb{S}^{k-1}))
and
i_{o}
, such that

(6)

\int_{\mathbb{S}^{k}_{+}}f_{i}^{*}\tilde{\kappa}=\int_{\mathbb{B}^{k}}f_{i}^{*}\tilde{\kappa}=1,\qquad\text{for all }i\geq i_{o},

where the first equality follows from the fact that
\mathbb{S}^{k}_{+}\cup\mathbb{B}^{k}
is the boundary of a half-ball and the integral of the closed form
f_{i}^{*}\tilde{\kappa}
on that boundary of the half-ball equals zero. Of course, we need to choose a proper orientation of
\mathbb{S}^{k}_{+}
.

Let
U
be an open neighborhood of
f(\mathbb{S}^{k-1})
such that
{\rm supp}\,\tilde{\kappa}\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}\setminus\mkern 1.7mu\overline{\mkern-1.7muU\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
. Since

\{\mathbb{R}^{m}\setminus f({\mathbb{S}}^{k}_{-}),\ \mathbb{R}^{m}\setminus f({\mathbb{S}}^{k}_{+}),\ U\}

is an open covering of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
, we may choose a smooth partition of unity
\{\psi_{+},\psi_{-},\psi_{U}\}
subordinate to that covering.
Let
\sigma=\psi_{+}\tilde{\kappa}
, and
\delta=\psi_{-}\tilde{\kappa}
. Since
\psi_{U}\tilde{\kappa}=0
, we have that
\tilde{\kappa}=\sigma+\delta
.
Since
d\tilde{\kappa}=0
, it follows that
d\sigma=-d\delta
, so
{\rm supp}\,d\sigma={\rm supp}\,d\delta
.
Note that
\sigma=0
is a neighborhood of
f({\mathbb{S}}^{k}_{-})
and
\delta=0
in a neighborhood of
f({\mathbb{S}}^{k}_{+})
, so
d\sigma=-d\delta=0
in a neighborhood of
f(\mathbb{S}^{k})
i.e.,

d\sigma\in\Omega_{c}^{k+1}(\mathbb{R}^{m}\setminus f(\mathbb{S}^{k}))
. Moreover, the observation about supports of the forms
\sigma
and
\delta
along with (
6
) yields

\int_{\mathbb{B}^{k+1}}f_{i}^{*}d\sigma=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{k}}f_{i}^{*}\sigma=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{k}_{+}}f_{i}^{*}\sigma=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{k}_{+}}f_{i}^{*}(\sigma+\delta)=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{k}_{+}}f_{i}^{*}\tilde{\kappa}=1\quad\text{for all }i\geq i_{o}.

Therefore, the closed form

\kappa:=d\sigma\in\Omega_{c}^{k+1}(\mathbb{R}^{m}\setminus f(\mathbb{S}^{k}))
satisfies the claim.
The proof is complete.
∎

3.
Proof of Theorem
1.2

Since
\mkern 1.7mu\overline{\mkern-1.7mu\mathbb{B}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu^{n+1}\subset\mkern 1.7mu\overline{\mkern-1.7mu\mathbb{B}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu^{m}
, an embedding of
\mathbb{S}^{m-1}\subset\mkern 1.7mu\overline{\mkern-1.7mu\mathbb{B}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu^{m}
yields an embedding of
\mathbb{S}^{n}\subset\mkern 1.7mu\overline{\mkern-1.7mu\mathbb{B}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu^{n+1}
. Therefore, without loss of generality, we may assume that
m=n+1
. Suppose, for the sake of contradiction, that there is a map

f\in C^{0,\gamma+}({\mathbb{B}}^{n+1},\mathbb{H}^{n})\cap C^{0,\theta}({\mathbb{B}}^{n+1},\mathbb{R}^{2n+1})\ \ \text{or}\ \ f\in C^{0,\gamma}({\mathbb{B}}^{n+1},\mathbb{H}^{n})\cap C^{0,\theta+}({\mathbb{B}}^{n+1},\mathbb{R}^{2n+1}),

such that
f|_{\mathbb{S}^{n}}
is an embedding. In order to approximate
f
by convolution all the way up to the boundary, it would be convenient to assume that
f
is defined on
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, but we can easily do that, because the extension of
f
by
f(x)=f(x/|x|)
, when
|x|>1
, belongs to the same Hölder classes as the original map
f
. Let
f_{i}=f*\phi_{1/i}
. Clearly,
f_{i}
restricted to
\mathbb{S}^{n}
converges uniformly to
f|_{\mathbb{S}^{n}}
.

Let
\kappa\in\Omega_{c}^{n+1}(\mathbb{R}^{2n+1}\setminus f(\mathbb{S}^{n}))
be as in Lemma
2.6
(with
m=2n+1
and
k=n
), so

\int_{\mathbb{B}^{n+1}}f_{i}^{*}\kappa=1\quad\text{for all }i\geq i_{o}.

According to Lemma
2.5
, we have

\kappa=\upalpha\wedge\beta+d\upalpha\wedge\delta
for some
\beta\in\Omega_{c}^{n}(\mathbb{R}^{2n+1}\setminus f(\mathbb{S}^{n}))
and
\delta\in\Omega_{c}^{n-1}(\mathbb{R}^{2n+1}\setminus f(\mathbb{S}^{n}))
.
By increasing
i_{o}
if necessary, we may assume that
f_{i}(\mathbb{S}^{n})\cap{\rm supp}\,\delta=\varnothing
, and hence,
f_{i}^{*}\delta|_{\mathbb{B}^{n+1}}\in\Omega_{c}^{n-1}(\mathbb{B}^{n+1})
for
i\geq i_{o}
, so integration by parts yields

\int_{\mathbb{B}^{n+1}}df_{i}^{*}\upalpha\wedge f_{i}^{*}\delta=\int_{\mathbb{B}^{n+1}}f_{i}^{*}\upalpha\wedge f_{i}^{*}(d\delta).

Therefore,

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{B}^{n+1}}f_{i}^{*}\kappa=\int_{\mathbb{B}^{n+1}}\big(f^{*}_{i}\upalpha\wedge f_{i}^{*}\beta+df_{i}^{*}\upalpha\wedge f_{i}^{*}\delta\big)=\int_{\mathbb{B}^{n+1}}f_{i}^{*}\upalpha\wedge f_{i}^{*}(\beta+d\delta)

\displaystyle\lesssim\|f_{i}^{*}\upalpha\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{B}^{n+1})}\|f_{i}^{*}(\beta+d\delta)\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{B}^{n+1})}.

This however, yields a contradiction, because
Lemmata
2.1
and
2.2
yield the estimates

\begin{split}\|f_{i}^{*}\upalpha\|_{\infty}&\|f_{i}^{*}(\beta+d\delta)\|_{\infty}\lesssim i^{1-2\gamma}[f]^{2}_{\gamma,2i^{-1},\mathbb{B}^{n+1}_{2}}\cdot i^{n(1-\theta)}[f]^{n}_{\theta,i^{-1},\mathbb{B}^{n+1}_{2}}\\
&=[f]^{2}_{\gamma,2i^{-1},\mathbb{B}^{n+1}_{2}}[f]^{n}_{\theta,i^{-1},\mathbb{B}^{n+1}_{2}}\to 0\quad\text{as $i\to\infty$,}\end{split}

because
f\in C^{0,\gamma}\cap C^{0,\theta}
and
f\in C^{0,\gamma+}
or
f\in C^{0,\theta+}
.

∎
