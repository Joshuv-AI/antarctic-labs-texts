Multivariate Predictors of LyC Escape II: Predicting LyC Escape Fractions for High-Redshift Galaxies
Anne E. Jaskot, Anneliese C. Silveyra, Anna Plantinga, Sophia R. Flury, Matthew Hayes, John Chisholm, Timothy Heckman, Laura Pentericci, Daniel Schaerer, Maxime Trebitsch, Anne Verhamme, Cody Carr, Henry C. Ferguson, Zhiyuan Ji, Mauro Giavalisco, Alaina Henry, Rui Marques-Chaves, Göran Östlin, Alberto Saldana-Lopez, Claudia Scarlata, Gábor Worseck, Xinfeng Xu

Abstract
JWST is uncovering the properties of ever increasing numbers of galaxies at z > 6 z>6 , during the epoch of reionization. Connecting these observed populations to the process of reionization requires understanding how efficiently they produce Lyman continuum (LyC) photons and what fraction ( f esc f_{\rm esc} ) of these photons escape into the intergalactic medium. By applying the Cox proportional hazards model, a survival analysis technique, to the Low-redshift Lyman Continuum Survey (LzLCS), we develop new, empirical, multivariate predictions for f esc f_{\rm esc} . The models developed from the LzLCS reproduce the observed f esc f_{\rm esc} for z ∼ 3 z\sim 3 samples, which suggests that LyC emitters may share similar properties at low and high redshift. Our best-performing models for the z ∼ 3 z\sim 3 galaxies include information about dust attenuation, ionization, and/or morphology. We then apply these models to z ≳ 6 z\gtrsim 6 galaxies. For large photometric samples, we find a median predicted f esc f_{\rm esc} =0.047-0.14. For smaller spectroscopic samples, which may include stronger emission line galaxies, we find that ≥ 33 \geq 33 % of the galaxies have f esc f_{\rm esc} > 0.2 >0.2 , and we identify several candidate extreme leakers with f esc f_{\rm esc} ≥ 0.5 \geq 0.5 . The current samples show no strong trend between predicted f esc f_{\rm esc} and UV magnitude, but limited spectroscopic information makes this result uncertain. Multivariate predictions can give significantly different results from single variable predictions, and the predicted f esc f_{\rm esc} for high-redshift galaxies can differ significantly depending on whether star formation rate surface density or radius is used as a measure of galaxy morphology. We provide all parameters necessary to predict f esc f_{\rm esc} for additional samples of high-redshift galaxies using these models.

II.2
Multivariate Survival Analysis: The Cox Proportional Hazards Model

In order to generate multivariate diagnostics for
f_{\rm esc}
, we need to incorporate information from both the
f_{\rm esc}
detections and the upper limits. Within the field of statistics, survival analysis techniques are appropriate for censored datasets that contain limits. One such survival analysis method is the Cox proportional hazards model (
Cox 22
; see
Clark et al. 21
and
Bradburn et al. 13
for reviews and
Feigelson & Nelson 33
and
Isobe et al. 48
for examples in astronomy). Here we describe the basic form of the Cox model and its assumptions. We refer the reader to Paper I for a more thorough discussion of this model and its application to the LzLCS+. To implement the Cox model, we use the CoxPHFitter routine in the
lifelines
python package
[
24
]
.

The Cox proportional hazards model predicts the probability of a particular
f_{\rm esc}
value given a set of input variables. Like many other survival analysis techniques, implementations of the Cox model typically assume the dataset contains measurements and lower limits (so-called “right-censored” data). In contrast, our dependent variable data consist of
f_{\rm esc}
measurements and associated upper limits. Consequently, we transform our
f_{\rm esc}
values to the absorbed fraction of LyC,
f_{\rm abs}
= 1 -
f_{\rm esc}
, for use in the Cox model. However, for ease of interpretation, we put the
f_{\rm abs}
results back into the form of
f_{\rm esc}
in all figures and in the tabulated results in the Appendix.

As applied to the LzLCS+, the Cox proportional hazards regression model fits for the probability of a LyC detection in an infinitesimally small increment of
f_{\rm abs}
, for a given set of independent variables and assuming no detection at a lower value of
f_{\rm abs}
(higher value of
f_{\rm esc}
). The Cox model assumes a particular functional form for this
f_{\rm esc}
probability, which is known as the “hazard function”:

h(f_{\rm abs}|x)=h_{0}(f_{\rm abs})\exp[\sum_{i=1}^{n}b_{i}(x_{i}-{\bar{x}_{i}})].

(2)

In this equation,
b_{i}
are the best-fit coefficients for each input variable
x_{i}
, and
\bar{x}_{i}
is the mean value of each input variable in the reference LzLCS+ dataset. The term
h_{0}
(
f_{\rm abs}
) is the baseline hazard function, the probability of having
f_{\rm abs}
in the case where all input variables match their average values within the LZLCS+. The Cox model is semi-parametric, in that the dependence on the input variables has a fixed exponential functional form, but the baseline hazard function,
h_{0}
, is estimated non-parametrically. Despite its fixed functional form for the input variable dependence, the Cox model allows the input variables to take any form. For instance, we could define an input variable
x_{i}
as a measured value
j
, the logarithm of that measurement
\log_{\rm 10}
(
j
), the square of that measurement
j^{2}
, or any other functional form of our choosing. In our case, we opt to use logarithmic forms of our input variables where possible (e.g,
\log_{\rm 10}
(O32),
M_{\rm 1500}
,
\log_{\rm 10}
(
M_{*}
)). In this logarithmic form, most variables have a similar order of magnitude and scale in the same manner, and an order of magnitude increase in a particular input variable simply translates into increasing the probability
h(f_{\rm abs}|x)
by a factor of
e^{b_{i}}
. In this paper, the only variables that we do not use in a logarithmic fashion are EW(Ly
\alpha
) and
f_{{\rm esc,Ly}\alpha}
, because they range from negative (Ly
\alpha
absorption) to positive (Ly
\alpha
emission) within the LzLCS+.

Although Equation
2
gives the
probability
of
f_{\rm abs}
, we would like to predict the expected
values
of
f_{\rm abs}
and
f_{\rm esc}
and their associated uncertainty. We adopt the median of the probability distribution as this expected value
[
13
,
24

will be above this value 50% of the time and below it 50% of the time. To determine this median
f_{\rm abs}

(
f_{\rm abs}

S(f_{\rm abs})=\exp[-{\rm HF}_{0}(f_{\rm abs})\cdot{\rm ph}(x)],

(3)

where HF
0
is the baseline cumulative hazard function

{\rm HF}_{0}(f_{\rm abs})=\int_{0}^{f_{\rm abs}}h_{0}(f)df

(4)

and ph(
x

{\rm ph}(x)=\exp[\sum_{i=1}^{n}b_{i}(x_{i}-{\bar{x}_{i}})].

(5)

[
22
,
13
,
24
,
86

. The median

(

)

II.2.1
Uncertainty in the Predicted
f_{\rm esc}

Because the survival function represents a probability distribution, we can also use it to calculate the expected uncertainty in our predicted
f_{\rm esc}
estimate. The
f_{\rm abs}
values where
S
(
f_{\rm abs}
) = 0.159 and
S
(
f_{\rm abs}
) = 0.841 represent the range in the predicted
f_{\rm esc}
corresponding to the Normal theory 1-
\sigma
uncertainty. This uncertainty range reflects the inherent scatter of the relationships between
f_{\rm esc}
and the input variables, where this scatter can come both from measurement uncertainties and from genuine variation among the galaxy population. In Paper I, we tested the effect of measurement uncertainty by performing a Monte Carlo (MC) resampling of each independent and dependent variable according to its observational uncertainty. We found that the distribution of predicted
f_{\rm esc}
from the resampled inputs was nearly always smaller than the 1-
\sigma
bounds inferred from the survival function. In other words, the inherent scatter in the correlations, not the measurement error, is the dominant source of uncertainty in the predicted
f_{\rm esc}
, and the survival function bounds serve as a reasonable estimate of the uncertainty in the
f_{\rm esc}
predictions.

II.2.2
Goodness-of-Fit Metrics

As in Paper I, we evaluate the Cox models’ performance using several complementary metrics. The concordance index,
C
, is particularly useful, as it is appropriate for censored data. The concordance index assesses whether the model
f_{\rm esc}
predictions correctly sort the dataset in the order of its observed
f_{\rm esc}
. To evaluate this sorting, the concordance index calculation compares each possible pair of data points. Concordant pairs are those where the galaxy with higher observed
f_{\rm esc}
also has higher predicted
f_{\rm esc}
, discordant pairs are the opposite, and tied pairs have identical predicted
f_{\rm esc}
. Some pairs with upper limits in
f_{\rm esc}
lead to ambiguous rankings and do not appear in the concordance index calculation. With all pairs evaluated, the concordance index is calculated as

C=\frac{n_{c}+0.5n_{t}}{n_{c}+n_{d}+n_{t}},

(6)

where
n_{c}
,
n_{d}
, and
n_{t}
are the number of concordant, discordant, and tied pairs, respectively. A value of
C=1.0
indicates a perfect rank ordering, 0.5 is perfectly random ordering, and 0 is perfect disagreement. The major advantage of
C
is that it includes both LyC detections and non-detections. However, it only assesses the relative order of the predicted
f_{\rm esc}
values, not their accuracy. To measure the latter, we turn to alternative quantities. One such metric is the
R^{2}
statistic

R^{2}=1-\frac{\sum_{i}(y_{i}-f_{i})^{2}}{\sum_{i}(y_{i}-{\bar{y}})^{2}},

(7)

where
y_{i}
are the observed values of
\log_{\rm 10}
(
f_{\rm esc}
),
{\bar{y}}
is their mean value, and
f_{i}
are the predicted
f_{\rm esc}
values from the Cox model. As in Paper I and
Maji et al. [74]
, we also calculate a variant of
R^{2}
, the adjusted
R^{2}
, which accounts for the number of free parameters
p
in the model and number of data points
n
:

R^{2}_{\rm adj}=1-(1-R^{2})\frac{n-1}{n-p-1}.

(8)

When increasing the number of variables,
R^{2}_{\rm adj}
increases only if a variable improves the predictions more than expected by chance. Finally, we report the root-mean-square (RMS) dispersion

{\rm RMS}=\sqrt{\frac{\sum_{i}(y_{i}-f_{i})^{2}}{n}}.

(9)

As explained in Paper I, we evaluate these three quantities (
R^{2}
,
R^{2}_{\rm adj}
, and RMS) using
\log_{\rm 10}
(
f_{\rm esc}
). The scatter in the
\log_{\rm 10}
(
f_{\rm esc}
) predictions is relatively consistent across the full range of observed
f_{\rm esc}
, whereas the scatter in the linear
f_{\rm esc}
changes systematically across this range. We can only calculate the
R^{2}
,
R^{2}_{\rm adj}
, and RMS metrics for the LzLCS+ galaxies with both detected LyC and non-zero predicted
f_{\rm esc}
, since these metrics require observed values of
\log_{\rm 10}
(
f_{\rm esc}
) and finite predicted
\log_{\rm 10}
(
f_{\rm esc}
). Hence, these metrics only indicate the accuracy of the model predictions for LCEs. However, the most successful models according to the
R^{2}
,
R^{2}_{\rm adj}
, and/or RMS metrics also tend to have high
C
and vice versa (see Paper I).

II.2.3
Choice of Input Variables

In generating Cox models from the LzLCS+, we consider a limited set of input variables. Although the dependent variable in the Cox model can contain measurement limits, the independent variables cannot. We therefore only use independent variables that have measurements for nearly all the LzLCS+ galaxies, which requires us to exclude fainter emission line measurements (e.g., [O
i
]
\lambda
6300 and [S
ii
]
\lambda\lambda
6716,6731) or measurements not widely available for the full sample (e.g., Ly
\alpha
velocity peak separation). In this paper, all 88 galaxies in the LzLCS+ have measurements for our chosen input variables with one exception. The non-leaker J1046+5827 does not have a reported
\Sigma_{\rm SFR}
or
r_{\rm 50,NUV}
, and we do not include it in deriving Cox models that use these variables. The Cox model may also fail to converge if we include highly collinear variables, which trace nearly identical properties. Given this limitation, we choose to use only one measure of UV dust attenuation (E(B-V)
UV
or
\beta_{\rm 1550}
) per model, and we do not include both
\Sigma_{\rm SFR}
and
r_{\rm 50,NUV}
in a single model.

We explored a variety of variable combinations in Paper I, and our best model attained
R^{2}=0.69
,
R^{2}_{\rm adj}=0.60
, RMS
=0.31
dex, and
C=0.91
. We provide the parameters for this model in the Appendix. However, this model included the average EW of Lyman-series absorption lines (EW(H
i
,abs)) and
f_{{\rm esc,Ly}\alpha}
, both of which will be affected by the IGM at
z>6
. Models in Paper I without UV absorption lines or Ly
\alpha
showed higher scatter but an overall ability to identify LCEs, with
R^{2}=0.29-0.40
,
R^{2}_{\rm adj}=0.14-0.35
, RMS
=0.44-0.47
dex, and
C=0.83
(Paper I; see Appendix for these model parameters). In this paper, we are specifically concerned with variables that are easily observable at
z>6
or measured in large samples at
z\sim 3
. Hence, we likewise omit UV absorption line measurements, and we avoid Ly
\alpha
measurements for most of the models tailored to
z>6
galaxies.

z\sim 3

values for the LzLCS+ sample alone. Notably, predictions for the two strongest high-redshift LCEs, Ion2 and J1316+2614, match the observations nearly perfectly in most cases (Figures
1
and
2
), demonstrating the success of the model in identifying extreme LCEs.

The goodness-of-fit metrics give clues as to which variables are most important in predicting
f_{\rm esc}
. As seen in Table
3
, the models with the highest
R^{2}
and lowest RMS for the LzLCS+ sample are the TopThree, LAE-O32, ELG-O32 and variants, and the R50-
\beta
models. These same models are the only ones that include both a measure of UV dust attenuation and either O32 or radius as variables. Turning to the
C
metric, which includes non-detections, the best-performing models for the LzLCS+ sample are the R50-
\beta
, TopThree, LAE-O32, ELG-O32-
\beta
-Ly
\alpha
, and LAE models. To properly estimate
f_{\rm esc}
for weak and non-LCEs, UV dust attenuation again appears important, but each of the best-performing models also includes either morphological information or Ly
\alpha
measurements. These additional variables may help distinguish non-LCEs from LCEs.

By all metrics, the LAE-O32-nodust and
\beta
-Metals models perform the worst for the LzLCS+ galaxies. The LAE-O32-nodust model is the only one that lacks E(B-V)
UV
or
\beta_{\rm 1550}
, which highlights the crucial role of dust extinction in LyC escape. Conversely, the
\beta
-Metals model relies almost exclusively on dust attenuation to infer
f_{\rm esc}
and lacks information on O32, Ly
\alpha
, or morphology, which may better constrain the LyC absorption due to H
i
.

III.2.1
Model Performance for
z\sim 3
Samples

Comparing the models’ performance for the high-redshift and combined low- and high-redshift samples is difficult, because each high-redshift model applies to a different set of galaxies. For example, the ELG-O32-
\beta
model has the lowest RMS for the high-redshift galaxies, but this RMS is based on a single galaxy: Ion2. Ion2 is the only galaxy that is included in most of the models (all but ELG-O32-
\beta
-Ly
\alpha
, where it has an input limit). For Ion2, we adopt an observed absolute
f_{\rm esc}
=0.75, in the middle of the 0.5-1 bounds reported by
Vanzella et al. [120]
. Using this
f_{\rm esc}
, Ion2’s LyC escape is predicted most accurately by the ELG-O32-
\beta
(RMS=0.02 dex) and ELG-O32 (RMS=0.04 dex) models. For Ion2, dust attenuation, luminosity, and ionization appear to be key factors in predicting its
f_{\rm esc}
, although most other models are still consistent with its reported range of
f_{\rm esc}
(RMS=0.09-0.15 dex for the next six models). The
\beta
-Metals model, which lacks information about O32 and morphology, is the only inaccurate model (RMS=0.43 dex). More surprisingly, however, the TopThree model gives the lowest predicted
f_{\rm esc}
=0.53 (RMS=0.15 dex), which highlights the role of UV magnitude in the
f_{\rm esc}
predictions, a point we discuss further in §
IV
.

Nine galaxies, including four LCEs, appear in each of the LAE, LAE-O32, LAE-O32-nodust, and ELG-O32 model test samples. Although the LAE-O32-nodust model performed worst for the LzLCS+, for this common subsample of nine high-redshift galaxies, the LAE-O32-nodust model has the lowest RMS (0.09 dex) and highest
C
(0.76), compared to RMS values of 0.27-0.32 dex and
C
values of 0.65-0.71 for these galaxies in the other three models. Given the generally successful performance of all four of these models, models with O32 and/or EW(Ly
\alpha
) appear able to predict
f_{\rm esc}
for high-redshift galaxies equally well. Hence, while models with E(B-V)
UV
and O32 or morphology work best for the LzLCS+ sample, we cannot rule out the possibility that EW(Ly
\alpha
) may also be important in predicting
f_{\rm esc}
at
z\sim 3
.

Nevertheless, using EW(Ly
\alpha
) as the sole nebular measurement is not sufficient to accurately predict
f_{\rm esc}
. The LAE model (Figure
1
b), which applies to the largest set of
z\sim 3
LyC measurements, is one of the least successful models at predicting
f_{\rm esc}
in the high-redshift galaxies. As seen in Table
3
, this model has the largest RMS scatter for the
z\sim 3
sample (0.9 dex), lowest
C
(0.49), and the second worst
R^{2}
(-4.2). The other model with comparably poor
R^{2}
and
C
for
z\sim 3
galaxies is the ELG-EW model, which is the only other model that exclusively relies on an emission line EW for its input nebular information. Both models also have some of the lowest
R^{2}
values for the LzLCS+ sample, which reflects the fact that these models substantially under-predict the
f_{\rm esc}
of the strongest LCEs in the LzLCS+. The LAE model shows this same tendency towards under-prediction for the
z\sim 3
LCEs as well; 13
z\sim 3
galaxies with observed
f_{\rm esc}

\geq 0.2
are predicted to have
f_{\rm esc}
of only
\leq 0.03
by the model.

The poor performance of the LAE model may result from limitations in both the model itself and in the
z\sim 3
data. The fact that both the LAE and ELG-EW models fare poorly for the LzLCS+ and for the high-redshift galaxies, especially for strong LCEs, suggests that nebular EW is a flawed tracer of
f_{\rm esc}
. Indeed, [O
iii
] EW and Ly
\alpha
EW should be low both for the weakest and for the strongest LCEs. In non-leakers, a high optical depth and corresponding lack of Ly
\alpha
escape should lead to a low Ly
\alpha
EW. Low [O
iii
] and Ly
\alpha
EWs could also indicate a weak current burst of star formation, without significant feedback or LyC production, which might result in undetected LyC. At lower optical depths and moderate
f_{\rm esc}
, Ly
\alpha
EW may increase due to enhanced escape, and [O
iii
] EWs may likewise increase if these lower optical depths are preferentially associated with stronger starbursts. However, the [O
iii
] and Ly
\alpha
EWs will decrease again in the strongest LCEs, as a general lack of nebular gas results in the limited production of nebular emission lines
[
133
,
93
, e.g.,]
. Thus, EW may be an ambiguous indicator of LyC escape.

The
z\sim 3
data also have limitations. The strong LCEs that are most severely under-predicted by the LAE model all come from the
Liu et al. [71]
and
Kerutt et al. [61]
samples of individual LAEs with LyC detections. Both papers note that their LyC detections represent the extreme of the population and may not be representative of the average galaxy population with these parameters, whose
f_{\rm esc}
may be much lower. In Paper I, we found a similar result for the strongest LCEs in the LzLCS+, whose only distinguishing feature compared to more moderate LCEs is their low line-of-sight H
i
content, suggestive of a favorable orientation. Another limitation of the high-redshift data is the uncertainty in the IGM attenuation. At
z\sim 3
, this attenuation is significant and varies along different lines of sight
[
103
,
47
,
120
,
114
, e.g.,]
. This unknown sightline dependence leads to additional uncertainty in the reported
f_{\rm esc}
for individual galaxies and could cause some genuine LCEs to appear as non-detections or to have overestimated
f_{\rm esc}
.

The stacked samples
[
114
,
92
,
7
]
shown circled in Figure
1
, average over these variations and are less subject to this uncertainty. In the LAE model in Figure
1
b, we see that the stacked samples do indeed show less scatter than the individual high-redshift detections. However, the model does not perfectly predict
f_{\rm esc}
for the stacked samples, and their scatter is still comparable to the scatter in the individual LzLCS+ galaxies. In addition to averaging over IGM attenuation variations, the stacks also average over any galaxy-to-galaxy variation in physical properties. Hence, the input variables may not represent the true set of properties of an individual system, leading to some uncertainty in the predicted
f_{\rm esc}
.

Unfortunately, the small sample sizes in most models and the lack of a common high-redshift sample across the models make it difficult to discern which model parameters are most important for predicting
f_{\rm esc}
at high redshift. Our results suggest that if high-redshift galaxies behave like their lower redshift counterparts in the LzLCS+ sample, E(B-V)
UV
or
\beta_{\rm 1550}
, O32, and
\Sigma_{\rm SFR}
or half-light radius are essential variables to include. For the existing
z\sim 3
samples, models with at least two of these parameters do successfully predict the
f_{\rm esc}
of the available high-redshift galaxies to within
\sim
0.3 dex.

This performance also shows that the LzLCS+ galaxies may indeed be reasonable analogs for high-redshift galaxies and that the same observable and physical properties may correlate with LyC escape at both low and high redshift
[
105
,
111
,
81
, e.g.,]
. This agreement is not trivial, as in principle, the
z\sim 0.3
and
z\sim 3
galaxies could have different star formation histories, dust properties, morphologies, or other properties, any of which could affect
f_{\rm esc}
. The LzLCS+ parameter space does cover the properties of the
z\sim 3
samples, with some exceptions. Seventeen
z\sim 3
targets (8 LCEs and 9 non-LCEs) are brighter than the LzLCS+ UV magnitude range by 0.1-3.18 magnitudes, including the strong LCEs Ion2 and J1316+2614. An additional two LCEs in the LAE model are fainter than the LzLCS+ by 0.5-0.7 magnitudes. The predictions for most models, all those with
M_{\rm 1500}
as a variable, therefore extrapolate to an unobserved part of parameter space, yet still perform well.

The data in Figures

and
2
and metrics in Table

include
z\sim 3
galaxies that have measured values for all of the input independent variables in the models

Finally, we note that our model predictions do not account for systematic uncertainties, including differences in methodology. We adopt the published values for all data. However, each paper makes different assumptions, which could affect the tabulated values. For instance, papers differ in their adopted IGM transmission models, the models used in SED fits, and adopted dust attenuation laws. These assumptions could lead to systematic differences in properties like
f_{\rm esc}
,
M_{*}
, and E(B-V)
UV
among the different samples. In addition, LyC measurements may be photometric or spectroscopic and may cover different wavelength ranges. Despite these systematics, the models work well in predicting

. Adopting consistent methodologies could potentially result in better predictions; however, as shown by the scatter in the LzLCS+ sample, which has a consistent methodology, the inherent uncertainty in the model itself also limits the possible accuracy of predictions.

III.2.2
Summary of
z\sim 3
Results

In conclusion, Cox models derived using the
z\sim 0.3
LzLCS+ sample can successfully predict
f_{\rm esc}
in
z\sim 3
LCEs. This agreement suggests that LCEs may have similar physical properties at both low and high redshift
[
108
, e.g.]
. We find that the most accurate models include E(B-V)
UV
and O32 or morphology measurements as variables. However, larger high-redshift samples with a full suite of measurements are required to test this result. Future observations of
z\sim 3
LCEs with
JWST
will further clarify whether the relationship between
f_{\rm esc}
and physical properties evolves with redshift or remains constant. Preliminary
JWST
observations suggest that
z\gtrsim 6
galaxies may indeed share numerous physical properties with low-redshift analog samples like the LzLCS
[
111
,
31
,
81
,
70
, e.g.,]
. The models developed on the LzLCS+ sample and tested on
z\sim 3
LCEs may therefore apply equally well at the reionization epoch.

at

f_{\rm esc}
(see Section

and Table

M_{\rm 1500}=-19
to

f_{\rm esc}
. The ELG-O32-
\beta

f_{\rm esc}
and magnitude exists, however.

At the faintest magnitudes, the
Atek et al. [1]
sample, included in the
\beta
-Metals model includes several extremely low-luminosity lensed galaxies observed as part of the
JWST
UNCOVER survey
[
6
,
127
]
. For these galaxies, the
\beta
-Metals model therefore extrapolates the LzLCS+ sample to an unobserved parameter space. The only statistically significant predictor variable in the
\beta
-Metals model is
\beta_{\rm 1550}
, and Figure
4
illustrates that these galaxies’ blue slopes suggest at least moderate
f_{\rm esc}
(0.02-0.18; Table
8
). Crucially, even these moderate
f_{\rm esc}
values, which may be under-predicted, are still greater than what is required for such faint galaxies to dominate reionization given their high LyC photon production rates
[
1
]
.

While moderate or high
f_{\rm esc}
for faint, blue galaxies matches past predictions
[
19
, e.g.,]
, the high
f_{\rm esc}
in the brighter sources in Figure
4
may seem surprising at first glance. In all four models, the fitted coefficients (see the Appendix) show that a brighter
M_{\rm 1500}
results in a higher
f_{\rm esc}

at fixed values of the other inputs (fixed E(B-V), fixed O32, etc).
This effect does not come from the association of higher observed UV luminosity with lower dust content. The data do not show such a correlation, and, because the models already include dust measurements, the model implies that brighter UV luminosities increase
f_{\rm esc}
at
fixed
dust attenuation. In addition, we see a similar relationship with
M_{*}
, where higher
M_{*}
is also associated with higher
f_{\rm esc}
at fixed values of the other parameters.
Lin et al. [70]
find a similar result using a different technique. Using the LzLCS+ data, they fit a logistic regression model for the probability of having LyC escape as a function of
M_{\rm 1500}
,
\beta_{\rm 1550}
, and O32 and likewise find a brighter
M_{\rm 1500}
increases the probability of LyC escape for fixed values of the other variables. This scaling does not imply that higher UV luminosities correlate with
f_{\rm esc}
in the LzLCS+ sample; if anything, the LzLCS+ indicates a slight trend in the opposite direction
[
41
]
. Instead, this scaling implies that a bright galaxy with LCE-like properties, such as high O32 and blue UV slope, is a more extreme object than a faint galaxy with identical properties. For instance, at fixed O32, the LzLCS+ galaxies with higher luminosities tend to have higher
f_{\rm esc}
, but high O32 values are also uncommon among the bright galaxies as a whole, leading to an overall trend of decreasing
f_{\rm esc}
with luminosity
[
41
, e.g.,]
.

The enhanced
f_{\rm esc}
for bright LCE candidates affects the predictions in Figure
4
by boosting the
f_{\rm esc}
of a bright, dust-poor emission-line galaxy or compact galaxy relative to an otherwise similar faint galaxy. For instance, for the ELG-EW model, the brightest galaxies should have higher predicted
f_{\rm esc}
only if they have equally low E(B-V) and equally high EW as the fainter galaxies. Indeed, the ELG-EW samples, mostly galaxies from
Endsley et al. [30]
,
Endsley et al. [31]
, show no trend between E(B-V)
UV
and
M_{\rm 1500}
or between EW([O
iii
]+H
\beta
) and
M_{\rm 1500}
; with comparable dust and emission line strengths, the brighter galaxies therefore end up with higher predicted
f_{\rm esc}
.
Endsley et al. [31]
note that lower metallicities or higher
f_{\rm esc}
among the fainter galaxies could suppress their EWs and account for the lack of an observed trend with
M_{\rm 1500}
. Hence, other properties, such as O32, might be necessary to distinguish faint galaxies with high
f_{\rm esc}
. The EWs in the
Endsley et al. [30]
,
Endsley et al. [31]
samples also come from photometry, which may be more uncertain than spectroscopic measurements
[
28
, e.g.,]
.

The ELG-O32-
\beta
samples have O32 measurements, but they similarly show no strong trend between
\beta_{\rm 1550}
and
M_{\rm 1500}
or between O32 and
M_{\rm 1500}
. Likewise, the
Morishita et al. [87]
sample, which constitutes the bulk of the R50-
\beta
model sample, exhibits a relatively flat trend between radius and
M_{\rm 1500}
. Only the
\beta
-Metals samples, mostly from
Atek et al. [1]
, show any trend between physical properties and
M_{\rm 1500}
, with the lowest metallicities and bluest slopes appearing among the faintest galaxies.

The limitations of the models and the available
z\gtrsim 6
datasets make it difficult to discern any trends between
M_{\rm 1500}
and
f_{\rm esc}
. Limited measurements for galaxies at the brightest and faintest magnitudes restrict us to some of the less accurate Cox models. The models also require extrapolating to make predictions for galaxies outside the magnitude or radius range of the LzLCS+ sample. Better constraints on
f_{\rm esc}
within the
z>6
population will require larger spectroscopic samples across a wide range of galaxy magnitudes as well as better observational constraints on
f_{\rm esc}
in a more diverse set of galaxies at lower redshifts.

IV.3
Notes on Individual Sources

IV.3.1
Strong LCES

Several galaxies in the ELG-O32 and ELG-O32-
\beta
models appear to be extremely strong LCEs (Tables
6
and
7
). One unusual galaxy, CEERS-1019, has a predicted
f_{\rm esc}

=1
. Such a high
f_{\rm esc}
would seemingly conflict with the presence of strong emission lines, since it should correspond to a complete absence of absorbing gas. However, given the uncertainty in the model predictions, the
f_{\rm esc}
of CEERS-1019 could be as low as 0.6. Two other galaxies in the ELG-O32 model sample, CEERS-698 and CEERS-1027, have
f_{\rm esc}

\geq 0.5
, and
f_{\rm esc}
as high as 1 is consistent with their uncertainties. Interestingly, all three of these strong LCE candidates are strong Ly
\alpha
emitters, which suggests that they reside within ionized bubbles
[
117
]
. Similarly, two galaxies in the ELG-O32-
\beta
samples have predicted
f_{\rm esc}

>0.5
: 18846 and 9422 from JADES
[
109
]
. With O32
=70.6
, the ELG-O32-
\beta
model infers
f_{\rm esc}

=0.85
for 9422, much higher than the
f_{\rm esc}

=0.01
value derived by
Saxena et al. [109]
using the multivariate prediction method of
Choustikov et al. [20]
; we discuss the differences between our predictions and those of other models in §
V
.

The true
f_{\rm esc}
of some of these extreme galaxies may not be quite as high as the models predict, however. Because two of these strong LCEs, CEERS-1019 (
f_{\rm esc}

=1
) and CEERS-698 (
f_{\rm esc}

\sim 0.7
), have brighter UV magnitudes than the LzLCS+ sample by 0.4-0.9, their high predicted
f_{\rm esc}
could be unreliable. However, these same models successfully predicted the high
f_{\rm esc}
of the
z\sim 3
galaxies Ion2 and J1316+2614 (Figure
2
), which are as bright as these
z>6
galaxies. The LzLCS+ sample itself also contains only three LCEs with
f_{\rm esc}

>0.5
, which limits the models’ ability to accurately determine predictive relationships in the high
f_{\rm esc}
regime
[
81
]
. CEERS-1019 may differ from the LzLCS+ galaxies for additional reasons; it has unusually strong nitrogen emission and is a possible candidate for supermassive star formation
[
79
]
or an AGN
[
63
]
. The physical conditions occurring in extreme galaxies like CEERS-1019 likely are not present within the LzLCS+ sample. Empirical predictions based on
z=0.3
galaxies would not be suitable for such objects.

The predictions for the other strong LCE candidates are likely more reliable, provided their star-formation and nebular conditions are not significantly different from the LCEs at
z\sim 3
. Galaxy 9422 has a higher O32 ratio than the LzLCS+, but the other strong leaker candidates fall within the LzLCS+ parameter space, so that the model is not extrapolating to an unobserved regime. Hence, our models suggest that several strong emission-line galaxies identified in the epoch of reionization may be strong LCEs with
f_{\rm esc}
well above 0.2.

IV.3.2
JADES-GS-z7-LA

Saxena et al. [108]
report the detection of a
z=7.3
galaxy with strong Ly
\alpha
emission, JADES-GS-z7-LA, which is presumably located within an ionized bubble. With
f_{{\rm esc,Ly}\alpha}
=0.96
, IGM absorption seems to have had little effect on the galaxy’s Ly
\alpha
emission. We therefore choose to incorporate the measured EW(Ly
\alpha
) and
f_{{\rm esc,Ly}\alpha}
in our model predictions. We can apply most of the models in Table
1
to JADES-GS-z7-LA, omitting only the TopThree, R50-
\beta
, and
\beta
-Metals models due to a lack of reported
\Sigma_{\rm SFR}
, radius, and 12+
\log_{\rm 10}
(O/H). We compare the different
f_{\rm esc}
predictions in Table
9
. JADES-GS-z7-LA does fall outside of the LzLCS+ parameter space, which may limit the accuracy of the
f_{\rm esc}
predictions; it is fainter than the LzLCS+ galaxies by 1.6 mag, and it has a higher EW(Ly
\alpha
) by 144.1 Å.

The different Cox models disagree regarding the
f_{\rm esc}
of JADES-GS-z7-LA, with predicted
f_{\rm esc}
ranging from 0 to 0.40. The only nonzero predictions come from models that have information about UV dust attenuation and Ly
\alpha
, but even the extreme Ly
\alpha
properties of JADES-GS-z7-LA do not guarantee
f_{\rm esc}

>0.1
. The LAE-O32 and ELG-O32-
\beta
-Ly
\alpha
models include O32 and stellar mass as well as Ly
\alpha
and find
f_{\rm esc}

=0.017-0.085
. These two models are also among the best-performing models for the LzLCS+ sample (Table
3
). JADES-GS-z7-LA is not devoid of dust, with E(B-V)
{}_{\rm UV}=0.10
, and its UV slope
\beta=-2.1
is not extreme. These properties are consistent with the properties of
z\sim 0.3
galaxies with moderate
f_{\rm esc}
; all the LzLCS+ galaxies with higher
f_{\rm esc}

>0.1
have lower E(B-V)
{}_{\rm UV}<0.1
, and a slope of
\beta_{\rm 1550}=-2.1
matches the median value for the moderate LCEs with
f_{\rm esc}

=0.05-0.1
.

Furthermore, as discussed above, given JADES-GS-z7-LA’s low mass (
\log_{\rm 10}
(
M_{*}/
M
☉
)=
7.15
), its O32 value of 8.8 may not be extreme. Even its strong Ly
\alpha
emission does not necessarily imply a low optical depth along the line of sight, since Ly
\alpha
photons can scatter. For instance, the non-leaking
z\sim 0.3
galaxy J1248+4259
[
53
]
has
f_{\rm esc}

\leq 0.013
and EW(Ly
\alpha
)
=256
Å. One piece of evidence that favors a high
f_{\rm esc}
for JADES-GS-z7-LA is the small offset between the Ly
\alpha
velocity and the systemic redshift
[
108
]
. With the low resolution of most of the LzLCS+ UV spectra, we cannot currently include this parameter in our models, and it may boost the predicted
f_{\rm esc}
for JADES-GS-z7-LA. However, this low velocity offset could still be consistent with little to no LyC escape; its offset of
120\pm 80

{\rm\thinspace km}{\rm\thinspace s}^{-1}\,
also resembles the Ly
\alpha
profile of the non-leaker J1248+4259, whose red peak is offset from the systemic velocity by
<200

{\rm\thinspace km}{\rm\thinspace s}^{-1}\,
.

In agreement with the assessment of
Saxena et al. [108]
, we thus find that the evidence for LyC escape in JADES-GS-z7-LA is ambiguous. The multivariate models that incorporate the most information suggest that it likely has a moderate
f_{\rm esc}

\sim 0.017-0.085
and may therefore not be solely responsible for producing its ionized bubble in the IGM
[
130
, e.g.,]
.

IV.3.3
Galaxies with High O32

The case of JADES-GS-z7-LA highlights the fact that the Cox multivariate predictions can differ from single-variable estimates (e.g., based on Ly
\alpha
alone). Galaxies with high O32 provide another example of this result. For instance,
Williams et al. [128]
present
JWST
observations of a lensed low-mass (
\log_{\rm 10}
(
M_{*}
/
M
☉
) = 7.7) galaxy, 11027, at

and hypothesize that the galaxy has a high
f_{\rm esc}

>0.1
based on its high O32 ratio of 12 ([O

]
\lambda\lambda
5007+4959/[O

]=16). However
Lin et al. [70]
suggest that 11027 is not likely to be a strong LCE considering the combination of O32, UV magnitude, and UV slope. Our ELG-O32 and ELG-O32-
\beta

6
-
7
) likewise predict
f_{\rm esc}
of only 0.012-0.021. O32 alone is not sufficient to constrain
f_{\rm esc}
, and high O32 values are common among the lowest mass galaxies in the LzLCS+ sample
[
41
]
, including weak LyC emitters. In contrast, the ELG-O32-
\beta
model predicts that several galaxies with lower O32 than galaxy 11027 (e.g., 6355, 10000, 10612, 12637 with O32=6.3-10.6,
Schaerer et al. 111
,
Mascia et al. 81
,
Saxena et al. 109
) are actually more likely to be LCEs because of their higher luminosities and/or bluer UV slopes (Table
7
). The Cox predictions emphasize the fact that not all galaxies with high O32 have high
f_{\rm esc}

[
53
,
41
, e.g.,]
and additional properties such as mass, UV luminosity, and dust extinction are important to consider.

V
Comparison with Other Studies

V.1
Single-Variable Predictions

Among the observable properties measured for the LzLCS+ sample, the UV slope
\beta_{\rm 1550}
shows one of the strongest correlations with
f_{\rm esc}
. Consequently,

VI
Implications for Reionization

As demonstrated in §
III
, the empirical Cox models derived from the LzLCS+ show promise as diagnostics of
f_{\rm esc}
at high redshift. However, with the limited measurements at
z>6
available so far, the models do not decisively show which galaxy populations dominate reionization (§
IV.2
). Because a combination of properties regulates a galaxy’s optical depth, we need estimates of many factors to accurately predict
f_{\rm esc}
. Dust attenuation, galaxy morphology, ionization, and UV luminosity all play a role in promoting LyC escape. Based primarily on dust attenuation, we would expect faint galaxies to be strong LCEs
[
19
,
1
, e.g.,]
. Nevertheless, at a fixed value of
\beta_{\rm 1550}
,
f_{\rm esc}
still shows considerable spread in the LzLCS+ and in multivariate predictions (Figure
6
), spanning a range of up to
\sim 0.7
in
f_{\rm esc}
. Without estimates of other properties (e.g.,
\Sigma_{\rm SFR}
, O32), we therefore cannot determine whether faint galaxies have
f_{\rm esc}
above or below the average LzLCS+ value for the same UV color.

At the same time, we cannot yet rule out the contribution of more luminous galaxies. Several galaxies with
M_{\rm 1500}\leq-20
(e.g., CEERS 498, 1027, 698, 1019; JADES 18846; GLASS-JWST 100003, 10021) have the low dust content and high ionization suggestive of extreme
f_{\rm esc}

\geq 0.5
. The LzLCS+ data seem to suggest that for the same O32 ratio and
\beta_{\rm 1500}
, a more luminous galaxy will have higher
f_{\rm esc}
(see also
Lin et al. 70
). This effect could possibly be an observational bias, as the LyC flux should be easier to detect for more luminous objects. However, we note that at fixed O32, brighter LzLCS+ galaxies show higher ratios of 900 Å to 1100 Å flux, which suggests that their high
f_{\rm esc}
is genuine. Moreover, the trend of higher
f_{\rm esc}
with luminosity only appears in galaxies that have properties associated with LyC escape, such as high O32. Overall, the brighter galaxies in the LzLCS+ have fewer LyC detections
[
41
]
, which suggests that the detections are not biased toward the brightest galaxies. Furthermore, including UV luminosity in the multivariate models appears necessary to reproduce the
f_{\rm esc}
of Ion2 (§
III
and §
V
), which suggests that the increase of
f_{\rm esc}
with luminosity may be a real phenomenon.

In the multivariate models,
M_{\rm 1500}
might help to break the degeneracy between ionization parameter and optical depth in galaxies with high O32. For instance, low-metallicity galaxies tend to have both low luminosities and inherently high ionization parameters
[
89
,
27
, e.g.,]
, such that they may be more likely to have high O32 even at high optical depth. Alternatively, LCEs with higher UV luminosities could be able to ionize gas over a wider opening angle, so that we are statistically more likely to observe a high
f_{\rm esc}
line of sight.

Of course, to evaluate a galaxy population’s influence on reionization, we must also consider how many ionizing photons are produced and the total LyC input into the IGM, not just
f_{\rm esc}
. Unfortunately, the Cox models do not do as well at predicting the ionizing to non-ionizing UV flux ratio or the total LyC luminosity (Paper I). Galaxies with high O32 and high nebular EWs may possess elevated
\xi_{\rm ion}
values
[
110
,
116
,
83
,
91
, e.g.,]
, such that at fixed
f_{\rm esc}
, these galaxies will provide more LyC photons to the IGM. These same properties scale with
f_{\rm esc}
in the multivariate Cox models, and our identified candidate strong LCEs at
z>6
generally have O32
>10
and/or EW([O
iii
]+H
\beta
)
>1500
Å. High
f_{\rm esc}
may therefore be coupled with high
\xi_{\rm ion}

[
110
,
91
, e.g.,]
.

However, ionizing photon production may vary with other parameters as well, such as galaxy luminosity
[
11
,
35
, e.g.,]
.
Fujimoto et al. [42]
estimate
\xi_{\rm ion}
for spectroscopically confirmed galaxies at
z\sim 8-9
and find that
\xi_{\rm ion}
is two times higher at
M_{\rm UV}\sim-19.5
compared to
M_{\rm UV}\sim-21.5
. Although this enhanced efficiency is not enough to compensate for the six times fainter UV luminosity at
M_{\rm UV}\sim-19.5
vs. -21.5, these fainter galaxies are also approximately 40 times more numerous
[
10
]
.
Atek et al. [1]
infer similarly high
\xi_{\rm ion}
for even fainter (
M_{\rm UV}\sim-17
to -15) and even more numerous galaxies, which only need
f_{\rm esc}
<0.05
to drive reionization. For the faintest galaxies we consider (
M_{\rm 1500}>-17.5
), the median of the Cox model predicted
f_{\rm esc}
values is near this threshold, with median
f_{\rm esc}
\sim 0.04
. However, these
f_{\rm esc}
estimates require refinement with additional parameters (e.g., O32,
\Sigma_{\rm SFR}
). If
f_{\rm esc}
does not vary strongly with luminosity, as suggested by our preliminary, limited models, the fainter population would dominate reionization due to their higher
\xi_{\rm ion}
and greater numbers.

Assessing the main contributors to reionization requires progress on several fronts. At
z>6
, we need estimates of nebular and morphological parameters in addition to
\beta_{\rm 1550}
and
M_{\rm 1500}
for galaxy samples spanning a wide range of luminosities. Parameters such as O32 and
\Sigma_{\rm SFR}
have some of the greatest effects on the
f_{\rm esc}
prediction accuracy for both the LzLCS+ (Paper I) and galaxies at
z\sim 3
(§
III
). The limited magnitude range of the LzLCS+ reference sample (
M_{\rm 1500}\sim-18.5
to
\sim-21.5
) also introduces uncertainty. Our
f_{\rm esc}
estimates for spectroscopic samples within this magnitude range are likely reasonable and suggest moderately high median
f_{\rm esc}

\sim 0.04-0.05
, with some galaxies reaching
f_{\rm esc}
as high as 0.6-0.7 (see Figure
4
and predictions in Table
6
for the ELG-O32 model and Table
7
for the ELG-O32-
\beta
model). However, our
f_{\rm esc}
estimates for fainter and brighter galaxy populations rely on extrapolation and are less trustworthy. To confirm these estimates, we need to explore
f_{\rm esc}
and its dependence on galaxy properties across a wider magnitude range at
z<6
. Lastly, observations of the IGM and galaxy population at
z>6
will provide a further test of
f_{\rm esc}
predictions. An accurate model of
f_{\rm esc}
should reproduce both the timing and topology of reionization with the
z>6
galaxy population. While much progress is needed to both improve and confirm
f_{\rm esc}
estimates, our current
f_{\rm esc}
predictions imply that plausible contributors to reionization appear at all magnitude ranges, and star-forming galaxies with the required levels of LyC escape do indeed exist at
z>6
.

VII
Summary

Quantifying the LyC escape fraction (
f_{\rm esc}
) of galaxies is critical to understand the sources of cosmic reionization. We have developed a flexible tool for predicting
f_{\rm esc}
empirically using combinations of observable variables available in the
z\sim 0.3
LzLCS+ reference sample (Paper I). We generate multivariate diagnostics for
f_{\rm esc}
with the Cox proportional hazards model
[
22
]
, a survival analysis technique that appropriately treats data with upper limits. We test Cox models developed from the
z\sim 0.3
galaxies on
f_{\rm esc}
observations at
z\sim 3
, and we apply the models to several samples of
z\gtrsim 6
galaxies to predict
f_{\rm esc}
for galaxies in the epoch of reionization. The Appendix provides all the information necessary to use the multivariate Cox models to predict
f_{\rm esc}
for other samples of galaxies, including samples at
z\sim 3
and samples at
z\gtrsim 6
, where the LyC is unobservable.
2
2

2

We have also developed a python script, available at
https://github.com/sflury/LyCsurv
, that allows the user to generate new Cox proportional hazards models to predict
f_{\rm esc}
using custom combinations of variables for any given galaxy population. Version 0.1.0 of the code is archived in Zenodo
[
39
]
.

We summarize our main findings below.

1.

The models successfully reproduce the observed
f_{\rm esc}
values for the high-redshift
z\sim 3
galaxies and often have a lower RMS for the
z\sim 3
samples than for the
z\sim 0.3
galaxies. The success of these models suggests that low-redshift and high-redshift LCEs may share similar properties. (§
III
)

2.

The best-performing models for the
z\sim 3
galaxies include the dust attenuation inferred from the UV SED (E(B-V)
UV
) or the UV slope
\beta_{\rm 1550}
, plus either O32 or a morphological measurement (
r_{\rm 50,NUV}
or
\Sigma_{\rm SFR}
). However, this result is tentative; each model is tested on a different subset of high-redshift galaxies, which makes comparing the models difficult. To determine which variable combinations best predict
f_{\rm esc}
at high redshift, we require larger
z\sim 3
samples of LyC-emitters with the full suite of input variables. (§
III
)

3.

We generate new Cox models based on the LzLCS+ observations, which incorporate variables measured for
z\gtrsim 6
samples. One model, using
M_{\rm 1500}
,
M_{*}
, E(B-V)
UV
and EW([O
iii
]+H
\beta
) as input variables, applies to 180 galaxies from
Endsley et al. [30]
,
Endsley et al. [31]
,
Bouwens et al. [12]
,
Tang et al. [117]
,
Fujimoto et al. [42]
,
Saxena et al. [108]
and predicts a median
f_{\rm esc}

=0.047
for this combined sample. Of these 180 galaxies, 27% have
f_{\rm esc}

>0.1
and only 6% have
f_{\rm esc}

>0.2
. However, this set of variables results in predictions that tend to underestimate
f_{\rm esc}
, and the galaxy samples mostly consist of photometric measurements. A second model, using
M_{\rm 1500}
,
M_{*}
,
\beta_{\rm 1550}
, and
r_{\rm 50,NUV}
as input variables, applies to 278 galaxies, mostly with photometric redshifts, from
Morishita et al. [87]
and
Mascia et al. [81]
. This model predicts a higher median
f_{\rm esc}

=0.14
, with 56% and 39% of the galaxies having
f_{\rm esc}

>0.1
and
f_{\rm esc}

>0.2
, respectively. Many of these galaxies are more compact than the LzLCS+ sample, and this model therefore requires extrapolating beyond the LzLCS+ parameter space. (§
IV.1
)

4.

Smaller samples of spectroscopically-confirmed
z\gtrsim 6
galaxies have higher predicted
f_{\rm esc}
, likely because they tend to include stronger emission-line galaxies. We use a model with
\beta_{\rm 1550}
,
M_{\rm 1500}
, and O32 to predict
f_{\rm esc}
for 27 spectroscopically-confirmed galaxies
[
128
,
111
,
81
,
108
,
109
]
and find that 33-41% of these galaxies have
f_{\rm esc}

>0.2
. For a smaller sample of 17 galaxies
[
128
,
117
,
42
,
108
]
, using
M_{*}
as an additional input variable and E(B-V)
UV
instead of
\beta_{\rm 1550}
, we find that 46-85% of the galaxies have
f_{\rm esc}

>0.2
. These models identify five galaxies at
z>5.9
that may have a line-of-sight
f_{\rm esc}
as high as 0.5-1: CEERS-698, CEERS-1019, CEERS-1027 from
Tang et al. [117]
and JADES 18846 and JADES 9422 from
Saxena et al. [109]
. (§§
IV.1
,
IV.3
).

5.

Currently, the predicted
f_{\rm esc}
values for different galaxy samples show no strong trend with UV magnitude across the range of
M_{\rm 1500}=-16
to
-22
. However, many of these models are limited by a lack of spectroscopic information. The models suggest that low-luminosity galaxies (
M_{\rm 1500}>-18
) have at least moderate
f_{\rm esc}

\sim 0.05
. If this
f_{\rm esc}
is coupled with a high ionizing photon production efficiency, such faint galaxies could substantially contribute to reionization
[
1
,
113
, e.g.,]
. Additional measurements of variables such as O32 and
\Sigma_{\rm SFR}
for more
z\gtrsim 6
galaxies could enable more accurate
f_{\rm esc}
predictions for both bright and faint galaxies and might increase the numbers of suspected strong LCEs. (§
IV.2
)

6.

The multivariate predictions for
z\gtrsim 6
galaxies can differ strongly from
f_{\rm esc}
predictions based on a single variable. We predict
f_{\rm esc}
for the strong Ly
\alpha
Emitter, JADES-GS-z7-LA, discovered at
z=7.278

[
108
]
. Despite its exceedingly high
f_{{\rm esc,Ly}\alpha}
, which would seem to imply a high
f_{\rm esc}
, the two Cox models that include the most available information (dust, Ly
\alpha
, O32, and luminosity) predict that JADES-GS-z7-LA is a moderate LCE with
f_{\rm esc}

=0.017-0.085
. Another example is the galaxy 11027 from
Williams et al. [128]
, which has O32
=12
, similar to the
z\gtrsim 6
galaxies with predicted
f_{\rm esc}

\geq 0.6
. Yet, models incorporating other information about the galaxy, such as its mass (
\log_{\rm 10}
(
M_{*}
/
M
☉
)=7.7), UV magnitude (
M_{\rm 1500}=-17.4
), and dust attenuation (
\beta_{\rm 1550}=-2
) predict
f_{\rm esc}

\leq 0.02
. However, we caution that some
z\gtrsim 6
galaxies, including these ones, fall outside the parameter space covered by the
z\sim 0.3
galaxy sample, which make make our
f_{\rm esc}
predictions less reliable. (§
IV.3
)

7.

We compare our model predictions to single variable predictions
[
19
]
and to other multivariate
f_{\rm esc}
predictions derived from simulations
[
20
]
or observations
[
81
,
82
]
. The Cox models more accurately predict
f_{\rm esc}
in non-detections than the
\beta_{\rm 1550}
model from
Chisholm et al. [19]
, and the inclusion of feedback-sensitive variables such as O32 and
\Sigma_{\rm SFR}
may better trace the effect of absorption from H
i
in LCEs with low dust content. Turning to multivariate models, the LzLCS+ sample implies a correlation between O32 and
f_{\rm esc}
, rather than the anti-correlation adopted in the
Choustikov et al. [20]
model. As a result, we find different predicted
f_{\rm esc}
for high-redshift galaxies, and the
Choustikov et al. [20]
model fails to reproduce the
f_{\rm esc}
of the LzLCS+ galaxies. In contrast, our predictions agree with the
Mascia et al. [81]
model, when we use their same set of input variables. However, our high-redshift
f_{\rm esc}
predictions differ strongly when we add UV luminosity or
\Sigma_{\rm SFR}
as input variables.

LyC-emitting galaxies show similar physical characteristics at both low and high redshift, which suggests that the same processes may govern LyC escape across cosmic time. Because a variety of factors can influence LyC escape, accurately predicting
f_{\rm esc}
requires information about galaxy luminosities, dust, nebular properties, and ionization. Direct measurements of LyC at low redshift, combined with multivariate statistical models for
f_{\rm esc}
, can give insight into the possible
f_{\rm esc}
of galaxies in the epoch of reionization. Nevertheless, additional observations are necessary before these techniques can reach their full potential. At low redshift, LyC observations that push to new parameter space, especially fainter and brighter UV magnitudes, will aid the application of low-redshift results to high-redshift samples. Measurements of relevant galaxy properties, especially in the rest-frame optical, for larger samples of
z\sim 3
LCEs will better test the relative performance of these models at high redshift. Based on our current results, models using O32 and
\Sigma_{\rm SFR}
appear promising but are not yet applicable to large samples at
z>6
. By measuring these properties for larger, representative galaxy samples at
z>6
, high-redshift surveys can connect galaxies in the epoch of reionization with their LyC-emitting counterparts at low redshift.

Each table first lists the goodness-of-fit statistics for the LzLCS+ galaxies as a measure of the model performance. It then provides the fitted coefficients (
b_{i}
) for each included variable and the reference values
{\bar{x}_{i}}
, which are the mean of the LzLCS+
x_{i}
values, where
x_{i}
is the input variable (
\beta_{\rm 1550}
,
\log_{\rm 10}
(O32), etc). Finally, the table lists the baseline cumulative hazard function, HF
0
, calculated by the
lifelines
Cox Model fitting routine for each of the observed
f_{\rm esc}
values for LCEs in the LzLCS+. Together, these parameters predict
f_{\rm esc}
for a given galaxy with a set of input variables
x
as follows (see Section §
II.2
for more detail).

To predict
f_{\rm esc}
for a galaxy, we first calculate the partial hazards function ph(
x
) for its set of variables:

{\rm ph}(x)=\exp[\sum_{i=1}^{n}b_{i}(x_{i}-{\bar{x}_{i}})].

(10)

We use the coefficients
b_{i}
given in the model table, the galaxy’s observed values
x_{i}
for each variable, and the mean values
{\bar{x}_{i}}
also given in the model table. For example, for the galaxy Ion2, we have observed values of E(B-V)
{}_{\rm UV}=0
,
\log_{\rm 10}
(O32)=0.851, and
\log_{\rm 10}
(
\Sigma_{\rm SFR}
/(
M
☉
yr
-1
kpc
-2
))=2.03. Using
b_{i}
(-10.083, 1.240, 1.369) and
{\bar{x}_{i}}
(0.158, 0.521, 0.705) from Table
16
, we calculate

{\rm ph}(x)=\exp[-10.083*(0-0.158)+1.240*(0.851-0.521)+1.369*(2.03-0.705)]=45.43.

(11)

We then use this value of ph(
x)
to scale the baseline cumulative hazard function HF
0
(
f_{\rm esc}
) and calculate the Survival Function
S
(
f_{\rm esc}
), which represents the probability that the true escape fraction,
f_{\rm esc,true}
, is lower than each tabulated
f_{\rm esc}
:

S(f_{\rm esc})=\exp[-HF_{0}(f_{\rm esc})*{\rm ph}(x)].

(12)

From Table
16
, for
f_{\rm esc}

=0.8890
, HF
0
is 0.003949 and
S
(0.8890) is therefore
\exp[-0.003949*45.43]=0.8358
. For
f_{\rm esc}

=0.6247
,
HF_{0}=0.008253
and
S(0.6247)=\exp[-0.008253*45.43]=0.6873
, and so on, for each value of
f_{\rm esc}
. Thus, according to the “TopThree model, there is a 68.73% probability that Ion2 has a measured
f_{\rm esc}

<0.6247
and an 83.58% probability that Ion2’s
f_{\rm esc}

<0.8890
. To find the median predicted
f_{\rm esc}
, we calculate
S
for each value of
f_{\rm esc}
in the table and find the value where
S
reaches 0.5;
f_{\rm esc}
is predicted to be above this value 50% of the time and below this value 50% of the time. For Ion2, we see that
S(0.5838)=0.5629
and
S(0.4911)=0.4577
. We linearly interpolate to find where
S
(
f_{\rm esc}
)
=0.5
, finding that
S(0.53)\sim 0.5
. The predicted
f_{\rm esc}
of Ion2 is then
f_{\rm esc}

=0.53
. Similarly, to determine the uncertainties in the predicted
f_{\rm esc}
, we find where
S
(
f_{\rm esc}
) reaches 0.159 and 0.841. According to the model,
f_{\rm esc}
will be between these
f_{\rm esc}
values 68% of the time. For some galaxies,
S
(
f_{\rm esc}
) is always
>0.5
for the tabulated
f_{\rm esc}
values, indicating a
>
50% probability that
f_{\rm esc}
is smaller than the smallest tabulated
f_{\rm esc}
. This situation corresponds to an arbitrarily small predicted
f_{\rm esc}
,
f_{\rm esc}

\sim 0
. Conversely, if
S
(
f_{\rm esc}
) is always
<0.5
,
f_{\rm esc}
is arbitrarily large and
f_{\rm esc}

\sim 1
.
