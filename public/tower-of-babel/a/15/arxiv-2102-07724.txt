Abstract
. We study the variety 𝐙𝐆 \mathbf{ZG} of monoids where the elements that belong to a group are central , i.e., commute with all other elements. We show that 𝐙𝐆 \mathbf{ZG} is local , that is, the semidirect product 𝐙𝐆 ∗ 𝐃 \mathbf{ZG}*\mathbf{D} of 𝐙𝐆 \mathbf{ZG} by definite semigroups is equal to 𝐋𝐙𝐆 \mathbf{LZG} , the variety of semigroups where all local monoids are in 𝐙𝐆 \mathbf{ZG} . Our main result is thus: 𝐙𝐆 ∗ 𝐃 = 𝐋𝐙𝐆 \mathbf{ZG}*\mathbf{D}=\mathbf{LZG} . We prove this result using Straubing’s delay theorem, by considering paths in the category of idempotents. In the process, we obtain the characterization 𝐙𝐆 = 𝐌𝐍𝐢𝐥 ∨ 𝐂𝐨𝐦 \mathbf{ZG}=\mathbf{MNil}\lor\mathbf{Com} , and also characterize the 𝐙𝐆 \mathbf{ZG} languages, i.e., the languages whose syntactic monoid is in 𝐙𝐆 \mathbf{ZG} : they are precisely the languages that are finite unions of disjoint shuffles of singleton languages and regular commutative languages.

1.
Introduction

In this paper, we study a variety of monoids called
\mathbf{ZG}
.
It is defined by requiring that the elements of the monoid that belong
to a group are
central
, i.e., commute with all other elements of the
monoid. The notation
\mathbf{ZG}
thus stands for
Zentral Group
,
inspired by the classical notion of centrality in group theory.
We can also define
\mathbf{ZG}
with the equation
x^{\omega+1}y=yx^{\omega+1}
on all elements
x
and
y
, where
\omega
is the idempotent power of the monoid.

The variety
\mathbf{ZG}
has been introduced by Auinger
[
Aui00
]
as a subvariety of interest
of the broader and better-known class
\mathbf{ZE}
of semigroups where the idempotent elements are
central.
The study of
\mathbf{ZE}
was initiated by Almeida and
Azevedo
[
AA87
]
.
Straubing studied in particular the variety
\mathbf{MNil}
(called simply
\mathbf{V}

in
[
Str82
]
)
of regular languages generated by finite languages,
and showed that it is exactly the variety of aperiodic monoids in
\mathbf{ZE}
.
From this, a systematic investigation of the subclasses of
\mathbf{ZE}

was started by Almeida and pursued by Auinger: see
[
Alm96
, page 211]

and
[
Aui00
,
Aui02
]
.

Our specific motivation to investigate
\mathbf{ZG}
is our recently published
study
[
AJP21
]

of the
dynamic membership problem for regular languages
.
In this problem, introduced in
[
SFMS97
]
,
we study how to apply substitution update operations on an input word
while maintaining the information of whether it belongs to a fixed regular
language. In
[
AJP21
]
, we show that this can be performed in
constant time per update for monoids in
\mathbf{ZG}
, and extend this to
semigroups and languages. Further,
\mathbf{ZG}
turns out to be a plausible
boundary to characterize constant-time complexity for monoids, semigroups, and
languages. However, the case of semigroups requires a study of the
so-called
semidirect product
of
\mathbf{ZG}
by definite (
\mathbf{D}
) semigroups, which
we denote by
\mathbf{ZG}*\mathbf{D}
. Thus, some of our results on semigroups and languages
in
[
AJP21
]
require an deeper understanding of
\mathbf{ZG}
and
\mathbf{ZG}*\mathbf{D}
,
which is the focus of this work.

The semidirect product operation on varieties used to define
\mathbf{ZG}*\mathbf{D}
intuitively corresponds to composing finite
automata via a kind of cascade operation. Its study is the subject of a large
portion of semigroup theory, inspired by the classical study
of semidirect products in group theory.
There are also known results to understand
the semidirect product specifically
by

\mathbf{D}
.
For instance, the

Derived category theorem

[
Til87
]

studies it as a decisive step towards proving the decidability of membership
to an arbitrary semidirect product, i.e., deciding if a given monoid belongs to the product.
The product by
\mathbf{D}
also arises naturally in several other
contexts:
the dotdepth hierarchies
[
Str85
]
,
the circuit complexity
of regular languages
[
Str94
]
,
or the study of the successor relations in first-order logic
[
TW98
,
TW01
,
KL13
]
.

Understanding this product with
\mathbf{D}
is notoriously complicated. For instance,
it requires specific dedicated work for some varieties like
\mathbf{J}
or
\mathbf{Com}

[
Kna83b
,
Kna83a
,
TW85
]
.
Also, this product does not preserve the decidability of membership,
i.e.,
Auinger
[
Aui10
]
proved that
there are varieties
\mathbf{V}
such that membership in
\mathbf{V}
is decidable, but
the analogous problem for
\mathbf{V}*\mathbf{D}
is undecidable.
For the specific case of the varieties
\mathbf{ZG}
,
\mathbf{ZE}
, or even
\mathbf{MNil}
,
we are not aware of prior results describing their semidirect product with
\mathbf{D}
.

Locality.

Existing work has nevertheless identified some cases where the
*\,\mathbf{D}
operator can be
simplified to a much nicer
local operator
, that preserves the decidability of membership and
is easier to understand.
For any semigroup
S
, the
local monoids
of
S
are the subsemigroups
of
S
of the form
eSe
with
e
an idempotent element of
S
.
For a variety
\mathbf{V}
,
we say that a semigroup belongs to
\mathbf{LV}
if all its local monoids are in
\mathbf{V}
.
It is not hard to notice that the variety
\mathbf{V}*\mathbf{D}
is always a subvariety of

\mathbf{LV}
, i.e., that every monoid in
\mathbf{V}*\mathbf{D}
must also be in
\mathbf{LV}
.
In some cases,
we can show a
locality result
stating that the other
direction also holds, so that
\mathbf{V}*\mathbf{D}=\mathbf{LV}
. In those cases we say that
the variety
\mathbf{V}
is
local
.
The locality of the variety of monoids
\mathbf{DA}

[
Alm96
]

is a famous result that has deep implications
in logic and complexity
[
TW98
,
DP15
,
GMS17
]

and has inspired recent follow-up work
[
PS16
]
.
Locality results are also known for other varieties, for instance
the variety of
semi-lattice monoids
(monoids that are both idempotent and
commutative)
[
McN74
,
BS73
]
, any
sub-varieties
of groups

[
Str85
, Theorem 10.2]
, or the

\mathcal{R}
-trivial variety

[
SJ73
,
Ste04
,
Str15
]
.
Thus, a hope to understand the variety
\mathbf{ZG}*\mathbf{D}
is to establish a locality result
of this type for
\mathbf{ZG}
.

Contributions.

This paper shows the locality of the variety
\mathbf{ZG}
.
This is achieved by showing a slightly stronger statement: each variety
\mathbf{ZG}_{p}

is local, where
\mathbf{ZG}_{p}
is the variety of
\mathbf{ZG}
monoids where each subgroup has
a period dividing
p
:

Theorem 1
.

For every
p>0
, we have
\mathbf{LZG}_{p}=\mathbf{ZG}_{p}*\mathbf{D}
.

As
\mathbf{ZG}
is the union of the
\mathbf{ZG}_{p}
, this easily implies:

Corollary 2
.

We have
\mathbf{LZG}=\mathbf{ZG}*\mathbf{D}
.

Further, the variety
\mathbf{MNil}
of aperiodic monoids in
\mathbf{ZE}

(introduced in
[
Str82
]
and
mentioned earlier) is exactly
\mathbf{ZG}_{1}
. Thus:

Corollary 3
.

We have
\mathbf{LMNil}=\mathbf{MNil}*\mathbf{D}
.

In the process of proving Theorem
1
, we obtain a characterization of
\mathbf{ZG}_{p}
-congruences,
i.e., congruences
\sim
on
\Sigma^{*}
where the quotient

\Sigma^{*}/\sim
is a monoid of
\mathbf{ZG}_{p}
. We show that they are always refined by a
so-called
n,p
-congruence
, which identifies the number of occurrences of
the
frequent letters
(the ones occurring
>n
times in the word)
modulo
p
, and also identifies the exact subword formed by the
rare letters
(the ones
occurring
\leq n
times). Thanks to this
(Theorem
4
), we also obtain a characterization of the
languages of
\mathbf{ZG}
, i.e., the languages whose syntactic monoid is in
\mathbf{ZG}
: they
are exactly the finite unions of disjoint shuffles of singleton languages and
commutative languages (Corollary
5
). We also characterize

\mathbf{ZG}
as a variety of monoids
(Corollary
6
):
\mathbf{ZG}=\mathbf{MNil}\lor\mathbf{Com}
, for

\mathbf{MNil}
defined in
[
Str82
]
and
\mathbf{Com}
the variety of commutative
monoids.

Paper structure.

We give preliminaries in Section
2
and formally define
the variety
\mathbf{ZG}
. We then give in Section
3
our characterizations
of
\mathbf{ZG}

via
n,p
-congruences. We then define in
Section
4
the varieties
\mathbf{ZG}*\mathbf{D}
,
\mathbf{LZG}
,
\mathbf{ZG}_{p}*\mathbf{D}
, and
\mathbf{LZG}_{p}

used in Theorem
1
and Corollary
2
, which we prove in the
rest of the paper. We first introduce the framework of Straubing’s
delay theorem used for our proof in Section
5
, and
rephrase our result as a claim
(Claim
5
)
on paths in the category of idempotents. We then
study in Section
6
how to pick a sufficiently large value of
n

as a choice of our
n,p
-congruence,
show in Section
7
two lemmas on paths in the
category of idempotents, and finish the proof in Section
8

by two nested inductions. We
conclude in Section
9
.

2.
Preliminaries

For a complete presentation of the basic concepts (automata, monoids,
semigroups, groups, etc.) the reader can refer to
the book of Pin
[
Pin86
]
or to the more recent lecture notes
[
Pin19
]
.
Except for the free monoid,
all semigroups, groups, and monoids that we consider are finite.

Semigroups, monoids, varieties.

A
semigroup

S
is a set equipped with an internal associative law
(written multiplicatively). A

monoid

M
is a semigroup with an
identity element

1
, i.e.,
an element with
1x=x1=x
for all
x\in M
: note that the identity
element is necessarily unique.
A
variety of semigroups
(resp.,
variety of monoids
) is a class of
semigroups (resp., monoids)
closed under direct product, quotient, and subsemigroup (resp., submonoid).

For a semigroup
S
, we call
x\in S

idempotent
if
x^{2}=x
.
We call the
idempotent power
of
x\in S
the unique idempotent element which is a power of
x
. (This means that
x
is idempotent iff it is its own idempotent power.)
Now, the
idempotent power of
S
is the least integer
\omega>0

such that for any element
x\in S
,
the element
x^{\omega}
is an idempotent power of
x
.
We write
x^{\omega+k}
for any
k\in\mathbb{Z}

to mean
x^{\omega+k^{\prime}}
where
k^{\prime}\in\{0,\ldots,\omega-1\}
is the remainder of
k

in the integer division by
\omega
.
For example,
x^{\omega-1}
simply denotes
x^{2\omega-1}
, where we have

2\omega-1>0
; in particular, as expected, we have
x^{\omega-1}x=x^{\omega}
.

Languages and congruences.

We denote by
\Sigma
an alphabet and by
\Sigma^{*}
the set of all finite words on
\Sigma
.
A
factor
of a word is a contiguous subword of that word.
For
w\in\Sigma^{*}
, we denote by
\left|w\right|
the length of
w
.
For
u,v\in\Sigma^{*}
, we say that
u
is a
subword
of
v
if, letting

n\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\left|u\right|
, there are

1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{n}\leq\left|v\right|
such that
u=v_{i_{1}}\cdots v_{i_{n}}
.
For
w\in\Sigma^{*}
and
a\in\Sigma
, we denote by
\left|w\right|_{a}
the number of occurrences of
a
in
w
.
A
language

L
is a subset of
\Sigma^{*}
.

A
congruence
on a finite alphabet
\Sigma
is an equivalence relation
on
\Sigma^{*}
which satisfies
compositionality
, i.e., it is compatible
with the concatenation of words in the following sense: for any words
x
,
y
,

z
, and
t
of
\Sigma^{*}
, if
x\sim y
and
z\sim t
, then
xz\sim yt
.
It is a
finite index congruence
if it has a finite number of equivalence classes.
For a given finite index congruence
\sim
, the quotient
\Sigma^{*}/{\sim}
is a
finite monoid, whose law corresponds to concatenation over
\Sigma^{*}
and whose
identity element is the class of the empty word.

The
syntactic congruence
of a regular language
L
over an alphabet
\Sigma
is
the congruence on
\Sigma
where
u,v\in\Sigma^{*}
are equivalent if for all

s,t\in\Sigma^{*}
, we have
sut\in L
iff
svt\in L
. As
L
is regular,
this congruence is a finite index congruence.
The
syntactic monoid
of
L
over
\Sigma^{*}
is then
the quotient of
\Sigma^{*}
by the
syntactic congruence for
L
.
The
syntactic semigroup
of
L
is the quotient of
\Sigma^{+}
, the
non-empty words over
\Sigma
, by the syntactic congruence.

A
variety of regular languages
is a class of regular languages which is
closed under Boolean operations, left and right derivatives (also called left
and right quotients), and inverse homomorphisms.
By Eilenberg’s theorem
[
Eil76
]
, a variety
\mathbf{V}
of monoids
defines a
variety of regular languages
, namely, the languages whose syntactic
monoid is in
\mathbf{V}
.
Following standard practice, we abuse
notation and identify varieties of monoids with varieties of languages following
this correspondence, i.e., we write
\mathbf{V}
both for the variety of monoids and
the variety of languages.

Letting
\mathbf{V}
be a variety of monoids,
we say that a finite index congruence
\sim
on
\Sigma
is a

\mathbf{V}
-congruence
if the quotient

\Sigma^{*}/{\sim}
is a monoid in
\mathbf{V}
.
For a given
\mathbf{V}
-congruence
\sim
, the map
\eta:\Sigma^{*}\to\Sigma^{*}/{\sim}
,
defined by associating each word with its equivalence class, is a surjective
morphism to a monoid of
\mathbf{V}
.
Hence, each equivalence class is a language of
\mathbf{V}
, since it is recognized by

\Sigma^{*}/{\sim}
.

The variety
\mathbf{ZG}
.

In this paper, we study the variety of monoids
\mathbf{ZG}

defined (via Reiterman’s theorem
[
Rei82
]
)
by the equation:
x^{\omega+1}y=yx^{\omega+1}
.
Intuitively, this says that the elements
of the form
x^{\omega+1}
are central, i.e., commute with all other elements.
This clearly implies the same for elements of
the form
x^{\omega+k}
for any
k\in\mathbb{Z}
, as we will
implicitly use throughout the paper:

{clm}

For any monoid
M
in
\mathbf{ZG}
, for
x,y\in M
, and
k\in\mathbb{Z}
, we have:

x^{\omega+k}y=yx^{\omega+k}
.

Proof 2.1
.

Recall that
x^{\omega+k}
denotes
x^{\omega+k^{\prime}}
where
k^{\prime}\in\{0,\ldots,\omega-1\}
is the remainder of
k

in the integer division by
\omega
.
Now, we can write
x^{\omega+k^{\prime}}
as
(x^{\omega+k^{\prime}})^{\omega+1}
, because the latter is equal to

x^{(\omega+k^{\prime})\times(\omega+1)}
which is indeed equal to
x^{\omega+k^{\prime}}
.
Thus, by setting
x^{\prime}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}(x^{\omega+k^{\prime}})
and applying the equation
of
\mathbf{ZG}
, we conclude.

Note that the elements of the form
x^{\omega+1}

are precisely the
group elements
, namely, the elements of the monoid that are within a (possibly trivial)
group that is a subsemigroup of the monoid. In particular, the neutral
elements of these groups are not necessarily the neutral element of the
monoid.

Claim
2

motivates the name
\mathbf{ZG}
, which stands for “Zentral Group”: it
follows the
traditional notation
Z(\cdot)
for central subgroups, and extends the variety
\mathbf{ZE}

introduced in
[
Alm94
, p211]
which only requires idempotents to
be central.
Thus, we have
\mathbf{ZG}\subsetneq\mathbf{ZE}
, and
non-commutative groups are examples of monoids that are in
\mathbf{ZE}
but not in
\mathbf{ZG}
.

In addition to the class
\mathbf{ZG}
, we will specifically study
the subclasses
\mathbf{ZG}_{p}
for
p>0
defined by imposing the equation

x^{\omega+p}=x^{\omega}
in addition to the
\mathbf{ZG}
equation. Intuitively,

\mathbf{ZG}_{p}
is the variety of monoids (and associated regular languages) where group
elements commute and where the
period
of all group elements divides
p
,
where the
period
of a group element
x
is the smallest integer
p^{\prime}
such that

x^{p^{\prime}}=x
.
Clearly,
\mathbf{ZG}=\bigcup_{p>0}\mathbf{ZG}_{p}
. Further, any finite language is in

\mathbf{ZG}_{p}
for any
p>0
.

The
period
of a semigroup is the least common multiple of all periods
of group elements. Note that the period of a semigroup always divides
\omega
,
because the period of any group element is equal to its idempotent power, hence
divides
\omega
.
Further,
the period of a monoid in
\mathbf{ZG}_{p}
always divides
p
.

3.
Characterizations of
\mathbf{ZG}

In this section, we present our characterizations of
\mathbf{ZG}_{p}
and of
\mathbf{ZG}
, which we will use
to prove Theorem
1
.
We will show that
\mathbf{ZG}_{p}
is intimately linked to a congruence on words called the

n,p
-congruence. Intuitively, two words are identified by this congruence if the
subwords of the
rare letters
(occurring less than
n
times) are the
same, and the numbers of occurrences of the
frequent letters
(occurring
more than
n
times) are congruent modulo
p
. This is the standard technique of

stratification
, used also, e.g., in
[
TW85
, Example 0.1]
or
in
[
Alm94
, Section 10.8]
.
Formally:

{defi}

[Rare and frequent letters,
n,p
-congruence]

Fix an alphabet
\Sigma
and a word
w\in\Sigma^{*}
.
Given a integer
n\in\mathbb{N}
called the
threshold
, we call
a\in\Sigma

rare
in
w
if
\left|w\right|_{a}\leq n
, and
frequent
in
w
if
\left|w\right|_{a}>n
.
The
rare alphabet
is the (possibly empty) set
\{a\in\Sigma\mid\left|w\right|_{a}\leq n\}
.
We define the
rare subword

{\kern-1.2ptw_{|\leq n}}
to be the subword of
w

obtained by keeping only the rare letters of
w
.

For
n>0
and for any integer
p>0
called the
period
, the
n,p
-congruence

\sim_{n,p}

is defined by writing
u\sim_{n,p}v
for

u,v\in\Sigma^{*}
iff:

•

The rare subwords are equal:
{\kern-1.2ptu_{|\leq n}}={\kern-1.2ptv_{|\leq n}}
;

•

The rare alphabets are the same: for all
a\in\Sigma
, we have
\left|u\right|_{a}>n
iff
\left|v\right|_{a}>n
;

•

The number of letter occurrences modulo
p
are the same: for all
a\in\Sigma
, we have that
\left|u\right|_{a}
and
\left|v\right|_{a}
are congruent
modulo
p
. (Note that we already know this for rare letters
using the previous conditions.)

We first remark that two
n,p
-equivalent words are also
m,q
-equivalent for any
divisor
q
of
p
and any
m\leq n
:

{clm}

For any alphabet
\Sigma
, for any
0<m\leq n
and
p,q>0
such
that
q
divides
p
,
the
n,p
-congruence refines the
m,q
-congruence.

Intuitively, it is less precise to look for exact subwords up to a lower
threshold and with a modulo that divides the original modulo. Here is the formal
proof:

Proof 3.1

(Proof of Claim
3
)
.

We first assume that
p=q
.
The claim is trivial for
m=n
, so we assume
n>m
.
As
n>m
, if two words
u
and
v
have the same rare alphabet for
n
, then they have the same rare alphabet for
m
, because the number of occurrences of all rare letters for
n
is the same, so the same ones are also rare for
m
.
Furthermore, if
u
and
v
have the same rare subword for
n
,
the restriction of this same rare subword to the rare letters for
m

yields the same rare subword for
m
.
Last, the number of occurrences of the frequent letters modulo
q
is
the same. Indeed, for the letters that were frequent for
n
, this is
the case because their number of occurrences is congruent modulo
p=q
.
For the letters that were not
frequent for
n
, this is because their number of occurrences has to be
the same because the rare subwords for
n
were the same.

We now assume that
q<p
. Let us now assume that
m=n
. Then the

n,p
-congruence refines the
m,q
-congruence because the rare
alphabets and subwords must be equal (the threshold is unchanged), and
the counts of the frequent letters modulo
p
determine these counts
modulo
q
as
q
divides
p
.

To conclude in the general case, we know that the
n,p
-congruence
refines the
n,q
-congruence which refines the
m,q
-congruence, so we
conclude by transitivity.

What is more, observe that
n,p
-congruences are a particular case of

\mathbf{ZG}_{p}
-congruences:

{clm}

For any alphabet
\Sigma
and
n>0
and
p>0
,
the
n,p
-congruence over
\Sigma^{*}
is a
\mathbf{ZG}_{p}
-congruence.

Proof 3.2
.

Let
E
be an equivalence class of the
n,p
-congruence, which we see as a
language of
\Sigma^{*}
, and let us show that
E
is a language of
\mathbf{ZG}_{p}
.
Intuitively,
E
defines a language where the number of occurrences of
each letter modulo
p
is fixed, where the set of letters occurring

\leq n
times (the rare letters) is fixed, and the
subword that they achieve is also fixed.
Formally, let
\Sigma=A\sqcup B
the partition of
\Sigma
in rare and
frequent letters for the class
E
, let
u
be the word over
A^{*}

associated to the class
E
, and let
\vec{k}
be the
\left|B\right|
-tuple
describing the modulo values for
E
.

We know that the singleton language

by

Similarly, the language

The goal of this section is to show the following result. Intuitively, it states

For any

-congruence

We first present some consequences of Theorem

3.1.
Consequences of Theorem
4

The most important consequence of Theorem
4
is a
characterization of the languages in
\mathbf{ZG}
, which is
similar
to the one obtained by Straubing in
[
Str82
]
for the variety
\mathbf{MNil}
.
To define
\mathbf{MNil}
, first define the operation
S^{1}
on a semigroup
S
: this is
the monoid obtained from
S
by adding an identity element
1
to
S

if
S
does not have one. Now,
define a
nilpotent semigroup

S
to be a semigroup
satisfying the equation
x^{\omega}y=yx^{\omega}=x^{\omega}
.
The variety
\mathbf{MNil}
is generated by monoids of the form
S^{1}
for

S
a nilpotent semigroup. Note that
\mathbf{MNil}\subseteq\mathbf{ZG}
, because the equation
of
\mathbf{MNil}
implies the equation of
\mathbf{ZG}
.
It was shown in
[
Str82
]
that the languages of
\mathbf{MNil}
are generated
by
disjoint
monomials
,
that is, they are Boolean combinations of languages of the form
B^{*}a_{1}B^{*}a_{2}\cdots a_{k}B^{*}
with
B\cap\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}\}=\emptyset
.

Our analogous characterization for
\mathbf{ZG}
is the following, obtained via
Theorem
4
:

Corollary 5
.

A language is in
\mathbf{ZG}
if and only if it can be expressed as a finite union of languages of the form

B^{*}a_{1}B^{*}a_{2}\cdots a_{k}B^{*}\cap K

where
B\cap\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}\}=\emptyset
and
K

is a regular commutative language.

Equivalently, we can say that every language of
\mathbf{ZG}
is a
finite union of
disjoint shuffles
of a singleton language (containing
only one word) and of a regular commutative language, where the
disjoint
shuffle
operator interleaves two languages (i.e., it describes the sets of words
that can be achieved as interleavings of one word in each language) while
requiring that the two languages are on disjoint alphabets.
Intuitively, these characterizations are because the syntactic congruence of a

\mathbf{ZG}
language is a
\mathbf{ZG}
-congruence, and Theorem
4
tells us
that it is refined by an
n,p
-congruence, whose classes can be expressed as
stated.
Let us formally prove Corollary
5
using
Theorem
4
:

Proof 3.3

(Proof of Corollary
5
)
.

One direction is easy: if a language

is of the prescribed form, then it is a
Boolean combination of languages of
\mathbf{MNil}
and regular commutative languages.
These languages are in
\mathbf{ZG}
and
\mathbf{ZG}
is closed under Boolean operations, so
indeed
L
is in
\mathbf{ZG}
.

For the converse direction,
fix a language

in
\mathbf{ZG}
, and consider the syntactic congruence
\sim

of

\mathbf{ZG}
-congruence,

\mathbf{ZG}_{p}
-congruence for some value

-congruence

Now, an equivalence class of the

second language is commutative, and the disjointness of rare and frequent

Thus, we have shown that

is a union of disjoint shuffles of a singleton

Alternatively, the join is the least variety containing both varieties. We then

Corollary 6

The variety

is generated by commutative monoids and monoids of the form

a nilpotent semigroup. In other words, we have:
\mathbf{ZG}=\mathbf{MNil}\lor\mathbf{Com}

Proof 3.4
.

Clearly

3.2.
Proof of Theorem

Having spelled out the consequences of Theorem

, we prove it
in the rest of this section. It crucially relies on a general result about
\mathbf{ZG}
that we will use

Lemma 8

Let

be a monoid of
\mathbf{ZG}
, let

(xy)^{\omega}=x^{\omega}y^{\omega}

Proof 3.6
.

We show this claim by showing that two equalities establishing that each side
of the equation is equal to the same term, namely,
x^{\omega}y^{\omega}(xy)^{\omega}

(xy)^{\omega}=x^{\omega}y^{\omega}(xy)^{\omega}.

(1)

To show Equation
1
, remember that we have
(xy)^{\omega}=xy(xy)^{\omega-1}
from the definition of
(xy)^{\omega-1}
in the preliminaries. Now, as
(xy)^{\omega-1}
is

x(xy)^{\omega-1}y

By injecting an
(xy)^{\omega}
in the latter, we obtain:

(xy)^{\omega}=x(xy)^{\omega-1}(xy)^{\omega}y.

Applying this equality
\omega
times gives:

(xy)^{\omega}=(x(xy)^{\omega-1})^{\omega}(xy)^{\omega}y^{\omega}.

Now, we can expand
(x(xy)^{\omega-1})^{\omega}
, commuting the

(xy)^{\omega-1}
to regroup the

into

and regroup the

(xy)^{\omega-1}
into

((xy)^{\omega-1})^{\omega}
which is equal to
(xy)^{\omega}
, so that the first factor of the
right-hand side is equal to
x^{\omega}(xy)^{\omega}
.
By commuting, we obtain Equation
1
.

The second equality is:

x^{\omega}y^{\omega}=x^{\omega}y^{\omega}(xy)^{\omega}.

(2)

To show Equation
2
,
note that we have
x^{\omega}y^{\omega}=x^{\omega-1}xy^{\omega-1}y
, so by
the equation of
\mathbf{ZG}
we get:

x^{\omega}y^{\omega}=x^{\omega-1}y^{\omega-1}xy.

Now, we have
x^{\omega-1}y^{\omega-1}=x^{\omega-1}x^{\omega}y^{\omega-1}y^{\omega}
,
and by the equation of
\mathbf{ZG}
we have:

x^{\omega-1}y^{\omega-1}=x^{\omega-1}y^{\omega-1}x^{\omega}y^{\omega}.

Inserting the second equality in the first, we have:

x^{\omega}y^{\omega}=x^{\omega-1}y^{\omega-1}x^{\omega}y^{\omega}xy.

Now, applying this equality
\omega
times gives

x^{\omega}y^{\omega}=(x^{\omega-1}y^{\omega-1})^{\omega}x^{\omega}y^{\omega}(xy)^{\omega}
.
As the first factor of the right-hand side is equal to
x^{\omega}y^{\omega}
, we get

x^{\omega}y^{\omega}=x^{\omega}y^{\omega}(xy)^{\omega}
. This establishes
Equation
2
.

From Equations
1
and

the proof.

Proof 3.7
.

Define

Thus, fixing the value
p>0
for the rest of this paper, the only remaining
task is to prove Theorem
1
on
\mathbf{LZG}_{p}
and
\mathbf{ZG}_{p}*\mathbf{D}
.
Note that we can freely use all equational results shown about monoids of
\mathbf{ZG}

(e.g., Lemma
8
), as
they also hold for monoids in
\mathbf{ZG}_{p}
.

To prove Theorem
1
, we will first present the general framework of Straubing’s
delay theorem in the next section and show the easy inclusion
\mathbf{ZG}_{p}*\mathbf{D}\subseteq\mathbf{LZG}_{p}
,
before moving on with the rest of the proof.

5.
Straubing’s Delay Theorem

To show our main result, we use Straubing’s delay theorem from
[
Str85
]
.
We first give some prerequisites to recall this result.
To this end, let us first define a general notion of
finite category
:

{defi}

A
finite category
on a finite set of
objects

O
defines, for every pair
(o,o^{\prime})\in O
of objects, a finite set
\mathrm{C}_{o,o^{\prime}}
of
arrows
. An arrow in
\mathrm{C}_{o,o^{\prime}}
is said to be
going from
o
to
o^{\prime}
; we call
o
the

starting object
and
o^{\prime}
the
ending object
.

The overall set of arrows
\bigcup_{o,o^{\prime}\in O}\mathrm{C}_{o,o^{\prime}}
is equipped with a composition law: for any objects
o,o^{\prime},o^{\prime\prime}
, for
any arrows
a\in\mathrm{C}_{o,o^{\prime}}
and
b\in\mathrm{C}_{o^{\prime},o^{\prime\prime}}
, the composition law
gives us
ab
which must be an arrow of
\mathrm{C}_{o,o^{\prime\prime}}
. (Note that the objects

o
,
o^{\prime}
, and
o^{\prime\prime}
are not necessarily pairwise distinct.)
Further, this composition law must be associative. What is more, we require
that for any object
o
, there exists an arrow in
\mathrm{C}_{o,o}
which is the identity
for all elements that it can be combined with (note that these arrows are in
particular unique).

We now define the notion of
category of idempotents
of a semigroup:

{defi}

[Category of idempotents]
Let
S
be a semigroup. The
category of idempotents

S_{\mathrm{E}}
of
S
is the finite category defined as follows:

•

The objects of
S
are the idempotents of
S
.

•

For any idempotents
e_{1}
and
e_{2}
and any element
x
of
S
such
that
x\in e_{1}Se_{2}
, we have an arrow labeled by
x
going from
e_{1}

to
e_{2}
, which we will denote by
(e_{1},x,e_{2})
. Formally,
\mathrm{C}_{e_{1},e_{2}}=\{(e_{1},x,e_{2})\mid x\in e_{1}Se_{2}\}
.

The composition law of the category is
(e_{1},x,e_{2})(e_{2},y,e_{3})=(e_{1},xy,e_{3})
.
Note that it is clearly associative thanks to the associativity of the
composition law on
S
. The identity element in
\mathrm{C}_{e,e}
for an
idempotent
e
is simply
(e,e,e)
.

Let us now study
S_{\mathrm{E}}
in more detail.
We denote by

B\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\{(e_{1},x,e_{2})\mid x\in e_{1}Se_{2}\}
the set of arrows of
the category of idempotents.

A
path
of
S_{\mathrm{E}}
is a nonempty word of
B^{*}
whose sequence of arrows is

valid
, i.e., the ending object of each arrow except the last one is equal to the starting object of the next arrow.
Because
S_{\mathrm{E}}
is a category, each path is equivalent to an element of the
category, i.e., composing the arrows of the path according to the composition
law of the category will give one arrow of the category, whose starting and
ending objects will be the
starting object of the path
(i.e., that of the
first arrow) and the
ending object of the path
(i.e., of the last arrow).
Two paths are
coterminal
if they have the same starting and ending object.
Two paths
\pi_{1}
and
\pi_{2}
are
S_{\mathrm{E}}
-equal
if they evaluate to the same
category element, which we write
\pi_{1}\equiv\pi_{2}
.
Note that if two paths are
S_{\mathrm{E}}
-equal then they must be coterminal.
A
loop
is a path whose starting and ending objects are the same.

A
congruence
on
B^{*}
is an equivalence relation
\sim
over
B^{*}
that
satisfies compositionality as previously defined.
Note that the relation is also defined on
words of
B^{*}
that are not valid, i.e., that do not correspond to paths; the
compositionality requirement also applies to such words.

{defi}

[Compatible congruence]
A congruence
\sim
on
B^{*}
is said to be
compatible
with
S_{\mathrm{E}}

if
for any two coterminal paths
\pi_{1}
and
\pi_{2}
of
S_{\mathrm{E}}
such that

\pi_{1}\sim\pi_{2}
,
we have
\pi_{1}\equiv\pi_{2}
.
In other words,
\sim
is compatible with
S_{\mathrm{E}}
iff, on words
of
B^{*}
that are coterminal paths, it refines

S_{\mathrm{E}}
-equality.

Recall the notion of a
\mathbf{ZG}_{p}
-congruence from Section
2
.
We are now ready to state Straubing’s delay theorem, which was introduced
in
[
Str85
]
and rephrased in
[
Til87
]
.
The theorem applies to any variety, but we state it specifically for
\mathbf{ZG}_{p}
for
our purposes. The theorem gives us an alternative characterization of
\mathbf{ZG}_{p}*\mathbf{D}
:

Theorem 9

(Straubing’s delay theorem, Theorem 5.2 of
[
Str85
]
)
.

A semigroup
S
is in
\mathbf{ZG}_{p}*\mathbf{D}
iff,
writing
S_{\mathrm{E}}
the category of idempotents of
S
and
B
its set
of arrows,
there exists a
\mathbf{ZG}_{p}
-congruence on
B^{*}
which is compatible with
S_{\mathrm{E}}
.

Using our notion of
n,p
-congruence, via
Claim
3
and
Theorem
4
,
we rephrase it again:

Corollary 10
.

A semigroup
S
is in
\mathbf{ZG}_{p}*\mathbf{D}
iff, writing
S_{\mathrm{E}}
and
B
as above,
there exists an
n,p
-congruence on
B^{*}
which is compatible with
S_{\mathrm{E}}
.

Before moving on to the full proof of our main theorem
(Theorem
1
),
we conclude the section by noticing that the Straubing delay theorem implies the easy direction
of our result, namely, if
L
is in
\mathbf{ZG}_{p}*\mathbf{D}
then
L
is in
\mathbf{LZG}_{p}
. This easy direction
follows directly from
[
Til87
]
, but let us provide a self-contained
argument:

{clm}

We have
\mathbf{ZG}_{p}*\mathbf{D}\subseteq\mathbf{LZG}_{p}
.

Proof 5.1
.

If
S
is in
\mathbf{ZG}_{p}*\mathbf{D}
, then by Theorem
9
, there exists a

\mathbf{ZG}_{p}
-congruence
\sim
compatible with
S_{\mathrm{E}}
.
Let us now show that
S
is in
\mathbf{LZG}_{p}
by showing that,
for any idempotent
e
, the local monoid
eSe
is in
\mathbf{ZG}_{p}
.
Let
e
be an idempotent. By definition of
S_{\mathrm{E}}
, the local monoid
eSe
is isomorphic to the subset of arrows of
S_{\mathrm{E}}
going from
e
to
e
, with their composition law.
Let us denote this subset by
B_{e}
.
Define
\sim_{e}
to be the specialization of the relation
\sim
to
B_{e}
. Remark
that
M=B_{e}^{*}/{\sim_{e}}
is a submonoid of
B^{*}/{\sim}
,
and is hence in
\mathbf{ZG}_{p}
because
B^{*}/{\sim}
is, and
\mathbf{ZG}_{p}
is a variety.
Remark that since all words in
B_{e}^{*}
are valid paths in
S_{\mathrm{E}}
, the local
monoid
N\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}eSe
defines a congruence
\sim_{2}
over
B_{e}^{*}
where two paths are equivalent if they evaluate to the same monoid element.
We know that
\sim
, hence
\sim_{e}
, refines this congruence
\sim_{2}
. Hence,
N
is a quotient of
M
.
Thus,
eSe
is a quotient of
B_{e}^{*}/{\sim_{e}}
, which is a submonoid of a
monoid in
\mathbf{ZG}_{p}
, concluding the proof.

In the rest of this paper, we show the much harder direction, i.e., if
S
is in

\mathbf{LZG}_{p}
then
S
is in
\mathbf{ZG}_{p}*\mathbf{D}
.
To prove this, using
Corollary
10
, it suffices to show:

{clm}

Let
S
be a semigroup of
\mathbf{LZG}_{p}
, write
S_{\mathrm{E}}
its category of idempotents and

B
the set of arrows of
S_{\mathrm{E}}
.
There exists an integer
n>0
such that the
n,p
-congruence on
B^{*}
is compatible with
S_{\mathrm{E}}
.

This result then implies, by our rephrasing of Straubing’s result
(Corollary
10
), that
S
is in
\mathbf{ZG}_{p}*\mathbf{D}
. So in the rest of this paper we prove Claim
5
.
The proof is structured in three sections. First, in Section
6
,
we carefully choose the threshold
n
of the
n,p
-congruence to be “large
enough” so that we can enforce a gap between the number of occurrences of the

rare
and
frequent
arrows according to the
n,p
-congruence, also
ensuring a strong connectedness property on the category of idempotents.
Second, in Section
7
, we show auxiliary results about paths in
the category of idempotents, using the
\mathbf{ZG}
equation and the properties of the
threshold.
Third, in Section
8
, we conclude the proof, doing first an
outer induction on the total number of rare arrow occurrences, and then an inner
induction on the number of frequent arrows, using
a so-called
ear decomposition
of the category.

6.
Choosing the Threshold of the Congruence

In this section, we explain how to choose the threshold
n

to prove Claim
5
. Our threshold will
enforce a large enough “gap” between the total number of occurrences of the
rare letters and the number of occurrences of the frequent letters. This is
called a
distant
threshold and will be useful in the rest of the
argument.

This section is split into two subsections.
In the first subsection, we formally define the notion of a
distant rare-frequent threshold, and show how such a threshold can be used to
find factors with no rare letters and a large number of copies of a frequent
letter.
We further explain that we can indeed
find a sufficiently distant rare-frequent threshold. This is a
generic result on words and alphabets that does not depend on
\mathbf{ZG}
or
on the category of idempotents.
In the second subsection, we instantiate this result for
paths in the category of idempotents, and
explain how a distant rare-frequent threshold incidentally ensures
a strong connectedness property on the category. This last subsection
focuses on the category of idempotents, but is also generic in the sense that it
does not use the
\mathbf{ZG}
equation. Hence, all of the present section applies to arbitrary
semigroups.

6.1.
Finding sufficiently distant thresholds

We now give our formal definition of a
distant
rare-frequent threshold:

{defi}

For
\Sigma
an alphabet,
u\in\Sigma^{*}
, and
m>0
,
we say that an integer
n>0
is an
m
-distant rare-frequent threshold
for
u
if,
letting
\Sigma_{r}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\{a\in\Sigma\mid\left|u\right|_{a}\leq n\}

be the rare alphabet of
u
for
n
,
then the
total
number of occurrences of rare letters in
u
is less
than a proportion
1/m
of the threshold
n
, minus one. Formally:

\sum_{a\in\Sigma_{r}}\left|u\right|_{a}\leq\frac{n}{m}-1
.

Note that if
n>0
is an
m
-distant rare-frequent threshold for
u
, then it
is in particular an
m^{\prime}
-distant rare-frequent threshold for any
0<m^{\prime}\leq m
.

If a word has a distant rare-frequent threshold, then any frequent letter can be
found in sufficiently many occurrences in some factor containing no rare letter.
This will be useful in pumping arguments, and is the motivation for the definition.
Formally:

Lemma 11
.

For any alphabet
\Sigma
and
m>0
, if a word
u\in\Sigma^{*}
has an

m
-distant rare-frequent threshold, then for any frequent letter
a
, the
word
u
has a factor containing no rare letter and containing
at least
m+1
occurrences of
a
.

Proof 6.1
.

As
a
is a frequent letter, by definition, its number
\left|u\right|_{a}
of
occurrences in
u
is such that
\left|u\right|_{a}>n
. Let
r\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\sum_{a\in\Sigma_{r}}\left|u\right|_{a}
be the total number of occurrences of the
rare letters. The definition of an
m
-distant rare-frequent threshold ensures
that
r\leq\frac{n}{m}-1
. Thus,
r+1\leq\frac{n}{m}
. Multiplying by
m
,
we obtain
(r+1)m\leq n
. Thus,
\left|u\right|_{a}>(r+1)m
. As there are
r
rare
letters in total, there are
r+1
subwords between them containing no rare
letter, so the inequality implies that one of them contains
>m
occurrences
of
a
, i.e.,
\geq m+1
occurrences of
a
. This concludes the proof.

We now intuitively state the existence, for any
m>0
,
of an
m
-distant rare-frequent threshold that can be used for any word
u
.
The precise claim is more complicated to phrase,
as we cannot pick one
n
which can serve as a
m
-distant rare-frequent threshold
for any word
u
. Indeed, for any choice of
n
,
there will always be words
u
where the number of rare letter
occurrences happens to be close to
n
. However, we can pick a large enough
n

such that, given any word
u
, we can pick some
0<n^{\prime}\leq n
as an

m
-distant rare-frequent threshold for
u
. We will state the claim more
generally about picking a threshold for a pair of words
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
.

Here is the formal claim. We repeat that this result is a general claim about
words, which is not specific to
\mathbf{ZG}
.

Lemma 12
.

For any alphabet
\Sigma
and
m>0
, there exists an integer
n>0
ensuring the
following: for any words
u_{1},u_{2}\in\Sigma^{*}
, there exists an
integer
n^{\prime}
with
0<n^{\prime}\leq n
which is an
m
-distant rare-frequent threshold for
u_{1}
and for
u_{2}
.

Let us now prove Lemma
12
in the rest of this subsection.
We first show an abstract result capturing the essence of the underlying
pigeonhole principle argument:

{clm}

For any
d>0
and
m^{\prime}>0
,
there exists an integer
n\geq m^{\prime}
ensuring the following:
for any
d
-tuple
T
of integers,
there exists an integer
n^{\prime}
with
m^{\prime}\leq n^{\prime}\leq n
such that

\sum_{i\in F}T_{i}\leq\frac{n^{\prime}}{m^{\prime}}
,
where
F=\{i\mid T_{i}\leq n^{\prime}\}
.

Intuitively, the value
m^{\prime}
will be computed from
m
to
ensure the “minus one” gap in Definition
6.1
,
the “dimension”
d
will be the cardinality of the
alphabet
\Sigma
(multiplied by 2 as we consider two words),
the integer
n
is the threshold that we will choose, and
n^{\prime}
is the value that
we wish to obtain.
Let us prove Claim
6.1
:

Proof 6.2

(Proof of Claim
6.1
)
.

Let us take
n\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}(m^{\prime}d)^{d+2}
, which ensures
n\geq m^{\prime}
.
The candidate values of
n^{\prime}
that we will consider are intuitively the
following:

m^{\prime}d
,
(m^{\prime}d)^{2},\ldots,(m^{\prime}d)^{d+2}
.
Now take any
d
-tuple
T
.
For any
1\leq i\leq d+2
, let
R_{i}=\{i^{\prime}\mid T_{i^{\prime}}\leq(m^{\prime}d)^{i}\}
be
the coordinates where
T
has a value
\leq(m^{\prime}d)^{i}
. By definition, we have

\emptyset\subseteq R_{1}\subseteq\cdots\subseteq R_{d+2}\subseteq\{1,\ldots,d\}
.
Applying the pigeonhole principle on the cardinalities of these sets, there
are
1\leq i<j\leq d+2
such that
R_{i}=R_{j}
.

Let us set
n^{\prime}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}(m^{\prime}d)^{j}
. By
construction, we have
n^{\prime}\geq m^{\prime}
. Now,
consider the sum
\sum_{i^{\prime}\in R_{j}}T_{i^{\prime}}
.
As
R_{j}=R_{i}
, we know that for every
i^{\prime}\in R_{j}
, we have
T_{i^{\prime}}\leq(m^{\prime}d)^{i}
. Thus, the sum is at most

d
times this value because
T
is a
d
-tuple. Formally,

\sum_{i^{\prime}\in R_{j}}T_{i^{\prime}}
is at most
d(m^{\prime}d)^{i}
, hence it is
\leq\frac{(m^{\prime}d)^{i+1}}{m^{\prime}}
, so it is
\leq\frac{n^{\prime}}{m^{\prime}}
by definition of

n^{\prime}=(m^{\prime}d)^{j}
because
i<j
. This concludes the proof.

With Claim
6.1
, it is now easy to show Lemma
12
:

Proof 6.3

(Proof of Lemma
12
)
.

Let
n
be the value given by Claim
6.1
when taking
d\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}2\left|\Sigma\right|
and
m^{\prime}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}(m+1)m
.

Let us consider any pair of words
u_{1},u_{2}\in\Sigma^{*}
.
Let
T
be the
d
-tuple of the letter occurrences of
u_{1}
, followed by those
of
u_{2}
.
The statement of Claim
6.1
ensures that there exists an integer
n^{\prime}

with
n^{\prime}\geq m^{\prime}>m>0
such that, when using
n^{\prime}
as the
rare-frequent threshold, the total number of rare letters
in
u_{1}
plus in
u_{2}
is

\leq\frac{n^{\prime}}{m^{\prime}}
.

Now, let us show that
\frac{n^{\prime}}{m^{\prime}}\leq\frac{n^{\prime}}{m}-1
. To
this end, as
m^{\prime}=m(m+1)
, first note that
\frac{n^{\prime}}{m^{\prime}}\leq\frac{n^{\prime}}{m+1}
. To
show now that
\frac{n^{\prime}}{m+1}\leq\frac{n^{\prime}}{m}-1
, let
us evaluate
\frac{n^{\prime}}{m}-1-\frac{n^{\prime}}{m+1}
and check that it is nonnegative.
This difference evaluates to
\frac{n^{\prime}-m(m+1)}{m(m+1)}
. Now, we have
n^{\prime}\geq m^{\prime}
, so
n^{\prime}\geq m(m+1)
, which is
\geq 0
. So indeed
\frac{n^{\prime}}{m^{\prime}}\leq\frac{n^{\prime}}{m}-1
.

Combining the conclusions of the two previous paragraphs, we obtain that
the total number of rare letters
u_{1}
plus in
u_{2}
is
\leq\frac{n^{\prime}}{m}-1
.
So the same is true of the rare letters in
u_{1}
, and of the rare letters
in
u_{2}
. By contrast, the frequent letters in
u_{1}
occur
>n^{\prime}
times by
definition, and
the same is true of the frequent letters in
u_{2}
.
Hence, by
Definition
6.1
, the value
n^{\prime}
is an
m
-distant rare-frequent
threshold for
u_{1}
and for
u_{2}
,
concluding the proof.

Thus, we have shown that, for any alphabet size and desired distance
m
, we can
pick a value
n
ensuring that for any two words we can use some
0<n^{\prime}\leq n

as an
m
-distant rare-frequent threshold for them.

6.2.
Choice of threshold and resulting properties

In the rest of this section, we instantiate the generic argument of the previous
section to the choice of an arbitrary semigroup
S
and its category of idempotents

S_{\mathrm{E}}
: we claim the existence of a suitably distant rare-frequent threshold,
and observe that it additionally ensures the strong connectedness of the
category. However, the argument is still generic in the sense
that it applies to an arbitrary semigroup
S
, even if it is not in
\mathbf{ZG}
.

Let us first rephrase Lemma
12
to this setting by picking as
alphabet the arrows
B
of the category of idempotents
S_{\mathrm{E}}
of
S
and by
choosing
m\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\left|S\right|
. We immediately obtain:

{clm}

Let
S
be a semigroup and let

S_{\mathrm{E}}
be its category of idempotents.
There exists an integer
n>0
ensuring the following:
for any paths
u_{1},u_{2}
in
S_{\mathrm{E}}
, there is
0<n^{\prime}\leq n
which is a

\left|S\right|
-distant rare-frequent threshold for
u_{1}
and for
u_{2}
.

The rephrasing of Lemma
11
is:

{clm}

If a path
u
in
S_{\mathrm{E}}
has an
\left|S\right|
-distant rare-frequent threshold,
then for any frequent arrow
a
, the
path
u
has a factor containing no rare arrow and containing
at least
m+1
occurrences of
a
.

We now close the section by observing that having an
m
-distant rare-frequent
threshold for a path
w
, indeed having simply an
1
-distant rare-frequent
threshold for
w
, enforces a strong connectedness property on the category.
Specifically, the set of frequent arrows in this path for this threshold must form a
so-called
union of strongly connected components (SCCs)
:

{defi}

Given
S_{\mathrm{E}}
and a subset
B^{\prime}
of its set of arrows
B
, we say that
B^{\prime}

is a union of SCCs
if,
letting
G
be the directed graph on the objects of
S_{\mathrm{E}}
formed of the arrows
of
B^{\prime}
, then all connected components of
G
are strongly connected.

{clm}

Fix
S
and
S_{\mathrm{E}}
and
B
, let
w\in B^{*}
be a path of
S_{\mathrm{E}}
, and let

n^{\prime}>0
be a 1-distant rare-frequent threshold of
w
.
Then the set of frequent arrows of
w
for
n^{\prime}
is a union of SCCs.

Proof 6.4
.

Consider
G
the directed graph of Definition
6.2
.
Let us assume by way of contradiction that
G
has a connected component which is not strongly connected. This means that there exists an edge
(u,v)
of
G
such that there is no path from
u
to
v
in
G
. Consider any frequent arrow
a
in
S_{\mathrm{E}}
achieving the edge
(u,v)
of
G
.

As
a
is frequent in
w
, we know that
a
occurs strictly more than

n^{\prime}
times in
w
, hence
w
contains at least
n^{\prime}

return paths
,
i.e., paths from the ending object
v
of
a
back to the starting object
u
of
a
. As there is no path from
v
to
u
in
G
, each one of these paths must contain an arrow of
B
which is rare in
w
.

Hence, the total number of rare arrows in
w
is at least
n^{\prime}
. But the 1-distant rare-frequent threshold condition imposes that the total number of rare arrow occurrences in
w
is
\leq n^{\prime}-1
. We have thus reached a contradiction.

7.
The Loop Insertion and Prefix Substitution Lemmas

We now show two auxiliary results on the category of idempotents of
\mathbf{LZG}

semigroups, to be used in the sequel.
The first result is the
loop insertion lemma
: it allows us to insert
any loop of frequent arrows to the power
\omega
without affecting equivalence.
The second is
the
prefix substitution lemma
: it allows us to replace a prefix of
frequent arrows by another, without affecting equivalence, up to inserting a loop later in the path.

The results shown in this section hold for semigroups in
\mathbf{LZG}
, hence for
those in
\mathbf{LZG}_{p}
for any
p>0
. Thus, let us fix a semigroup
S
in
\mathbf{LZG}
.
Recall that
S_{\mathrm{E}}
denotes the category of idempotents of
S
,
and denote by
B
the set of arrows of
S_{\mathrm{E}}
.

Remember that, by definition of the category of idempotents
S_{\mathrm{E}}
, for any idempotent

e
of
S
, the set of loops with starting and ending object
e
under the
product law of the category forms a monoid which is isomorphic to the local
monoid
eSe
of
S
.
As we know that
S
is in
\mathbf{LZG}
, all its local monoids are
in
\mathbf{ZG}
. Hence the
\mathbf{ZG}
equation immediately applies to loops in the
category of idempotents, which we will often use in the results of this section:

{clm}

Let
x
and
y
be two coterminal loops of
S_{\mathrm{E}}
, let
k\in\mathbb{Z}
, and
let
\omega
be an idempotent power of
S
. We have:

x^{\omega+k}y\equiv yx^{\omega+k}
.

7.1.
Loop insertion lemma

The loop insertion lemma allows us, when we have a sufficiently distant rare-frequent
threshold
n^{\prime}
,
to insert any arbitrary loop raised to the power
\omega
without changing the
category element to which a path evaluates.
We also show that this change does not affect
n^{\prime},\omega
-equivalence:
this will suffice to preserve
n^{\prime},p
-equivalence (where
p
is the period
of
S
),
which will be useful later (we come back to this at the beginning of
Section
8
).
Formally:

Lemma 13

(Loop insertion lemma)
.

Let
\pi
be a path, and assume that
n^{\prime}
is an
\left|S\right|
-distant rare-frequent threshold
for
\pi
.
Let
\pi=rt
be a decomposition of
\pi
(with
r
or
t
possibly empty),
let
o
be the object between
r
and
t
(i.e., the ending object
of
r
, or the starting object of
t
if
r
is empty),
and let
\pi^{\prime}
be a loop on
o
that only uses frequent arrows.
Then
\pi\equiv r(\pi^{\prime})^{\omega}t
.
(Note that these two paths are also
n^{\prime},\omega
-equivalent by construction.)

In the rest of this subsection, we show Lemma
13
.
We first rephrase the claim to the following auxiliary result:

{clm}

Let
\pi
be a path, and assume that
n^{\prime}
is an
\left|S\right|
-distant rare-frequent
threshold for
\pi
.
Let
\pi=rt
be a decomposition of
\pi
and
o
be the object between
r

and
t
.
Let
X
be the set of elements of the local monoid on
o
that can be
achieved as some loop
q_{x}
of frequent arrows raised to the power
\omega
: formally,
X
is
the set of elements
x\in S
such that there is a loop
q_{x}
of frequent
arrows such that
q_{x}^{\omega}
evaluates to
(o,x,o)
, noting that this
implies that
x
is idempotent.
Then letting
q\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\prod_{x\in X}q_{x}^{\omega}
, we have
that
\pi\equiv rqt
.
(Note that these two paths are also
n^{\prime},\omega
-equivalent by construction.)

Intuitively, in this claim,
X
stands for the set of elements of
S
that we
can achieve as a loop on
o
raised at the power
\omega
and using frequent arrows
only. The claim states that inserting loops of this form at
o
to achieve all
such elements
x
will preserve equivalence.

We first explain why Claim
7.1
implies Lemma
13
. Indeed, when taking
p=rt
, letting
o
be the object between
r
and
t
,
and taking
r(\pi^{\prime})^{\omega}t
,
the sets
X
defined in Claim
7.1
after the occurrence of
r
in

p=rt
and
r(\pi^{\prime})^{\omega}t
will be the same for both
paths (because
X
only depends on
o
).
Thus, Claim
7.1
implies that there is a loop
q
such that
rt\equiv rqt
and
r(\pi^{\prime})^{\omega}t\equiv rq(\pi^{\prime})^{\omega}t
.
Now, as
(\pi^{\prime})^{\omega}
must correspond to an arrow of the form
(o,x,o)
for
x\in X
, it must be the same idempotent as one of the idempotents achieved by one of the loops in the definition of
q
, and
as the local monoid is in
\mathbf{ZG}
these idempotents commute and
q\equiv q(\pi^{\prime})^{\omega}
.
Hence, we have

rqt\equiv rq(\pi^{\prime})^{\omega}t
.
We know that
rqt\equiv rt
, and
rq(\pi^{\prime})^{\omega}t\equiv r(\pi^{\prime})^{\omega}t
.
Thus we obtain
rt\equiv r(\pi^{\prime})^{\omega}t
.
Thus,
Lemma
13
is proved once we have shown Claim
7.1
.

Hence, all that remains is to show Claim
7.1
. We will do so by
establishing a number of claims.

We first show that, for any frequent arrow
x
, we can
insert some loop of the form
xu
where
u
is a return path using only frequent
arrows, while preserving equivalence.
This uses the notion of distant rare-frequent
threshold; specifically, this is where we perform the pumping made possible by
Claim
6.2
.

{clm}

Let
\pi=rt
be a path
with an
\left|S\right|
-distant rare-frequent threshold,
let
o
be the object between
r
and
t
,
and let
x
be any frequent arrow starting at
o
.
Then we have
\pi\equiv r(xu)^{\omega}t
for some return path
u
using only
frequent arrows.

Proof 7.1
.

As
x
is a frequent arrow and
\pi
has an
\left|S\right|
-distant threshold, we know by
Claim
6.2
that
\pi
contains a factor

\rho
that contains only frequent arrows and contains
k>\left|S\right|
occurrences of
x
.
This provides a decomposition of
\rho
in the form:

\rho=\rho_{1}x\rho_{2}x\cdots\rho_{k}xs
.

By the pigeonhole principle, there exists
i<j
such that

\rho_{1}x\cdots\rho_{i}x\equiv\rho_{1}x\cdots\rho_{j}x
.
Hence, iterating, we obtain:

\rho_{1}x\cdots\rho_{j}x\equiv\rho_{1}x\cdots\rho_{i}x(\rho_{i+1}x\cdots\rho_{j}x)^{\omega}.

Moving the
\omega
, we get:

\rho_{1}x\cdots\rho_{j}x\equiv\rho_{1}x\cdots\rho_{i-1}x\rho_{i}(x\rho_{i+1}x\cdots\rho_{j})^{\omega}x.

This proves that
\rho
and
h(xu)^{\omega}g
achieve the same category element
when taking
u\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\rho_{i+1}x\cdots\rho_{j}
,
h\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\rho_{1}x\cdots\rho_{i-1}x\rho_{i}

and
g\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}x\rho_{j+1}x\cdots\rho_{k}xs
.
By compositionality,
\pi
and
h^{\prime}(xu)^{\omega}g^{\prime}
achieve the same
category element, where
h^{\prime}
is the part of
\pi
preceding
\rho

followed by
h
, and
g^{\prime}
is
g
followed by the part of
\pi

following
\rho
.

Now, recall that we must show the result for our decomposition
\pi=rt
,
where the object
o
between
r
and
t
is the starting object of the
arrow
x
and the ending object of
h
.
Either
h^{\prime}
is a prefix of
r
, or vice-versa. Assume first that we are in the
first case, so
h^{\prime}=rw
for some path
w
. Then,
w
and
(xu)^{\omega}

belong to the local monoid of
o
which is in
\mathbf{ZG}
. Since idempotents commute
with all elements, we have
w(xu)^{\omega}\equiv(xu)^{\omega}w
establishing that

rw(xu)^{\omega}g^{\prime}\equiv r(xu)^{\omega}wg^{\prime}\equiv r(xu)^{\omega}t
since

wg^{\prime}=t
.
The other case is symmetrical. This concludes the proof of
Claim
7.1
.

We then prove a generalization of the previous claim, going from a single
frequent arrow to an arbitrary path of frequent arrows:

{clm}

Let
\pi=rt
be a path
with an
\left|S\right|
-distant rare-frequent threshold.
Let
o
be the object between
r
and
t
.
Let
h
be any path starting at
o
which
only uses frequent arrows.
Then we have
\pi\equiv r(hg)^{\omega}t
for some return path
g
using only
frequent arrows.

Proof 7.2
.

We show the claim by induction on the length of
h
.
The base case of the induction, with
h
of length
0
, is trivial with
g
also having length
0
.

For the inductive claim, write
h=h^{\prime}a
. Intuitively, we will insert a loop
starting with the path
h^{\prime}
, then insert a loop starting with the
arrow
a
within that loop, and then recombine.

Formally,
by induction hypothesis, there exists a
g^{\prime}
using only frequent arrows
such that:

\pi\equiv r(h^{\prime}g^{\prime})^{\omega}t.

Furthermore, by applying Claim
7.1
to the decomposition

r^{\prime}=rh^{\prime}
and
t^{\prime}=g^{\prime}(h^{\prime}g^{\prime})^{\omega-1}t
and with the frequent arrow
a
we get a return path
u
using only
frequent arrows
such that:

r(h^{\prime}g^{\prime})^{\omega}t\equiv rh^{\prime}(au)^{\omega}g^{\prime}(h^{\prime}g^{\prime})^{\omega-1}t

So, iterating the
\omega
power, and combining with the preceding equation, we
get:

\pi\equiv rh^{\prime}((au)^{\omega})^{\omega}g^{\prime}(h^{\prime}g^{\prime})^{\omega-1}t.

Now, by applying
\omega-1
times Claim
7
to each
(au)^{\omega}
except the first and to each loop going from after this
(au)^{\omega}
to the position between an occurrence of
h^{\prime}
and
g^{\prime}
,
we get that:

\pi\equiv rh^{\prime}(au)^{\omega}g^{\prime}(h^{\prime}(au)^{\omega}g^{\prime})^{\omega-1}t.

Note the right-hand side is equal to:

r(h^{\prime}(au)^{\omega}g^{\prime})^{\omega}t
.
So we have shown:

\pi\equiv r(h^{\prime}(au)^{\omega}g^{\prime})^{\omega}t.

So this establishes the inductive claim by taking
g\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}u(au)^{\omega-1}g^{\prime}
.

We are interested in the specialization of this result when the path of
frequent arrows to insert is a loop. In this case, the return path is also a
loop. Formally, the specialization is the following:

Corollary 14
.

Let
\pi=rt
be a path
with an
\left|S\right|
-distant rare-frequent threshold
n^{\prime}
,
let
o
be the object between
r
and
t
,
and let
q
be a loop on
o
using only frequent arrows.
We have that
rt\equiv rq^{\omega}(q^{\prime})^{\omega}t
for some loop
q^{\prime}

on
o
using only frequent arrows
(note that the two are also
n^{\prime},\omega
-equivalent).

Proof 7.3
.

We use Claim
7.1
with
h\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}q
. This gives us the
existence of a return path
g
using only frequent arrows, which is then also a loop on
o
, such that

rt\equiv r(qg)^{\omega}t
.
Now, applying Lemma
8
to the local monoid on object
o
, we know that this evaluates to the same category element as

rq^{\omega}g^{\omega}t
.
Taking
q^{\prime}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}g
concludes the proof of Corollary
14
.

The only step left is to argue that Corollary
14
implies our rephrasing
(Claim
7.1
) of the loop insertion lemma
(Lemma
13
).
To do this, let
\pi=rt
be the path,
let
o
be the object between
r
and
t
,
and let
X
be the set of idempotents definable from frequent arrows.
Let us write
X=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}\}
with
k=\left|X\right|
.
For each
1\leq i\leq k
, take a loop
q_{i}
on
o
consisting only of frequent arrows
that achieves
x_{i}
, i.e., which ensures that
q_{i}^{\omega}
evaluates to
(o,x_{i},o)
.
Now, we apply Corollary
14

k
times with
q
being each of the
loops

q_{1},\ldots,q_{k}
. We get the following, where
q^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,q^{\prime}_{k}
are the
loops
q^{\prime}
on
o
of frequent arrows obtained by the statement of Corollary
14
:

rt\equiv rq_{1}^{\omega}(q^{\prime}_{1})^{\omega}\cdots q_{k}^{\omega}(q_{k}^{\prime})^{\omega}t

Note that the left-hand-side and right-hand-side are also

n^{\prime},\omega
-equivalent.

Now, since the
q^{\prime}_{i}
are loops on
o
consisting of frequent arrows, each

(q^{\prime}_{i})^{\omega}
evaluates to
(o,x,o)
for some
x\in X
.
As the elements of
X
are in the local monoid of
o
which is in
\mathbf{ZG}
,
commuting the loops using Claim
7
,
we can combine each
(q^{\prime}_{i})^{\omega}
with some
q_{j}^{\omega}
such that

(q^{\prime}_{i})^{\omega}\equiv q_{j}^{\omega}
, hence
(q^{\prime}_{i})^{\omega}q_{j}^{\omega}=q_{j}^{\omega}
.
Thus, we
get that
rt
is
n^{\prime},\omega
-equivalent to, and evaluates to the same category
element as, the path:

r\prod_{x\in X}q_{x}^{\omega}t.

This concludes the proof of Claim
7.1
, and thus establishes our
desired result, Lemma
13
.

7.2.
Prefix substitution lemma

The prefix substitution lemma allows us to change any prefix of frequent arrows of a
path, up to inserting a loop of frequent arrows elsewhere:

Lemma 15
.

Let
\pi=xry
be a path, and assume that
n^{\prime}
is an
\left|S\right|
-distant rare-frequent threshold
for
\pi
.
Let
x^{\prime}
be a path coterminal with
x
. Assume that every arrow in
x

and in
x^{\prime}
is frequent.
Assume that some object
o
in the SCC of
frequent arrows of the starting object of
r
occurs again in
y
, say as the
intermediate object of
y=y_{1}y_{2}
.
Then there exists
y^{\prime}=y_{1}y^{\prime\prime}y_{2}
for some loop
y^{\prime\prime}
consisting only of
frequent arrows such that
\pi\equiv x^{\prime}ry^{\prime}
and such that
\pi
and
x^{\prime}ry^{\prime}
are
n^{\prime},\omega
-equivalent.

This lemma uses Claim
6.2
to argue that
frequent arrows are a union of SCCs. Its proof relies on the loop insertion
lemma (Lemma
13
), but with extra technical work using the
\mathbf{ZG}

equation.

To prove Lemma
15
, we will first show
that frequent loops can be “recombined” without changing the category image,
simply by equation manipulation:

{clm}

For
x
,
x^{\prime}
two coterminal paths in
S_{\mathrm{E}}
and
y
,
y^{\prime}
coterminal paths in
S_{\mathrm{E}}
such that
xy
and
x^{\prime}y^{\prime}
are valid loops, we have:

(xy)^{\omega}(x^{\prime}y^{\prime})^{\omega}\equiv(xy^{\prime})^{\omega}(x^{\prime}y)^{\omega}(xy)^{\omega}(x^{\prime}y^{\prime})^{\omega}
.

Proof 7.4
.

Let us first show that:

(xy)^{\omega}(x^{\prime}y^{\prime})^{\omega}\equiv x^{\prime}y(xy)^{\omega-1}(x^{\prime}y^{\prime})^{\omega-1}xy^{\prime}

(3)

To show Equation
3
, first rewrite
(xy)^{\omega}
as
x(yx)^{\omega-1}y
and likewise for
(y^{\prime}x^{\prime})^{\omega}
, to get:

(xy)^{\omega}(x^{\prime}y^{\prime})^{\omega}\equiv x(yx)^{\omega-1}yx^{\prime}(y^{\prime}x^{\prime})^{\omega-1}y^{\prime}

Then, we use Claim
7
to move
(yx)^{\omega-1}
, so the above
evaluates to the same category element as:

xyx^{\prime}(yx)^{\omega-1}(y^{\prime}x^{\prime})^{\omega-1}y^{\prime}

We again rewrite
(yx)^{\omega-1}
to
y(xy)^{\omega-2}x
, yielding:

xyx^{\prime}y(xy)^{\omega-2}x(y^{\prime}x^{\prime})^{\omega-1}y^{\prime}

We again use Claim
7
to move
(xy)^{\omega-2}
, merge it with the prefix
xy
, and move it back to its place, yielding:

x^{\prime}y(xy)^{\omega-1}x(y^{\prime}x^{\prime})^{\omega-1}y^{\prime}

We rewrite
(y^{\prime}x^{\prime})^{\omega-1}
to
y^{\prime}(x^{\prime}y^{\prime})^{\omega-2}x^{\prime}
, yielding:

x^{\prime}y(xy)^{\omega-1}xy^{\prime}(x^{\prime}y^{\prime})^{\omega-2}x^{\prime}y^{\prime}

Again by Claim
7
, we can merge
(x^{\prime}y^{\prime})^{\omega-1}
with
x^{\prime}y^{\prime}
and move it to finally get:

x^{\prime}y(xy)^{\omega-1}(x^{\prime}y^{\prime})^{\omega-1}xy^{\prime}

Thus, the left-hand side of Equation
3
evaluates to the same category
element as the right-hand side, and we have shown Equation
3
.

Now, we have
(xy)^{\omega}(x^{\prime}y^{\prime})^{\omega}\equiv(xy)^{2\omega}(x^{\prime}y^{\prime})^{2\omega}
, so using Claim
7
again, Equation
3
gives:

(xy)^{\omega}(x^{\prime}y^{\prime})^{\omega}\equiv x^{\prime}y(xy)^{\omega}(x^{\prime}y^{\prime})^{\omega}(xy)^{\omega-1}(x^{\prime}y^{\prime})^{\omega-1}xy^{\prime}

We can now use Equation
3
to replace
(xy)^{\omega}(x^{\prime}y^{\prime})^{\omega}
by
the right-hand side of Equation
3
and use Claim

to
commute, yielding:

(xy)^{\omega}(x^{\prime}y^{\prime})^{\omega}\equiv(x^{\prime}y)^{2}(xy)^{\omega-2}(x^{\prime}y^{\prime})^{\omega-2}(xy^{\prime})^{2}

By definition we have
(xy)^{\omega-2}\equiv(xy)^{\omega-2}(xy)^{\omega}
and

(x^{\prime}y^{\prime})^{\omega-2}\equiv(x^{\prime}y^{\prime})^{\omega}(x^{\prime}y^{\prime})^{\omega-2}
. Injecting these in
the equation above, we get:

(xy)^{\omega}(x^{\prime}y^{\prime})^{\omega}\equiv(x^{\prime}y)^{2}(xy)^{\omega-2}\underbrace{(xy)^{\omega}(x^{\prime}y^{\prime})^{\omega}}_{(\star)}(x^{\prime}y^{\prime})^{\omega-2}(xy^{\prime})^{2}

Note that
(\star)
is now equal to the left-hand-side of the equation.
Substituting
\omega
times the right-hand-side into

, we obtain:

(xy)^{\omega}(x^{\prime}y^{\prime})^{\omega}\equiv(x^{\prime}y)^{\omega}(xy)^{\omega}(x^{\prime}y^{\prime})^{\omega}(xy^{\prime})^{\omega}

As these elements commute (thanks to Claim
7
), we have shown the desired equality.

We will now extend this result to show that we can change
the initial part of a path, even if it is not a loop, provided that there is a
coterminal path under an
\omega
-power with which we can swap it.

{clm}

For
x
,
x^{\prime}
two coterminal paths in
S_{\mathrm{E}}
and
y
,
y^{\prime}
coterminal paths in
S_{\mathrm{E}}
such that

and

are valid loops, and for any path

xt(xy)^{\omega}(x^{\prime}y^{\prime})^{\omega}\equiv x^{\prime}t(xy)^{\omega}xy^{\prime}(x^{\prime}y^{\prime})^{\omega-1}.

We establish this again by equation manipulation.

Proof 7.5

(Proof of Claim
7.2
)
.

We apply Claim
7.2
to show the following equality about the left-hand side:

xt(xy)^{\omega}(x^{\prime}y^{\prime})^{\omega}\equiv xt(xy^{\prime})^{\omega}(x^{\prime}y)^{\omega}(xy)^{\omega}(x^{\prime}y^{\prime})^{\omega}

By commutation of
(x^{\prime}y)^{\omega}
thanks to Claim

(x^{\prime}y)^{\omega}xt(xy^{\prime})^{\omega}(x^{\prime}y^{\prime})^{\omega}(xy)^{\omega}

By expanding

, we get:

x^{\prime}(yx^{\prime})^{\omega-1}yxt(xy^{\prime})^{\omega}(x^{\prime}y^{\prime})^{\omega}(xy)^{\omega}

By commutation of
(xy)^{\omega}

x^{\prime}(yx^{\prime})^{\omega-1}yx(yx)^{\omega-1}yxt(xy^{\prime})^{\omega}(x^{\prime}y^{\prime})^{\omega}

x^{\prime}(yx^{\prime})^{\omega-1}(yx)^{\omega+1}t(xy^{\prime})^{\omega}(x^{\prime}y^{\prime})^{\omega}

x^{\prime}t(xy^{\prime})^{\omega}x^{\prime}(yx^{\prime})^{\omega-1}(yx)^{\omega+1}(y^{\prime}x^{\prime})^{\omega-1}y^{\prime}

x^{\prime}(yx^{\prime})^{\omega-1}=(x^{\prime}y)^{\omega-1}x^{\prime}

x^{\prime}t(xy^{\prime})^{\omega}(x^{\prime}y)^{\omega-1}x^{\prime}(yx)^{\omega+1}(y^{\prime}x^{\prime})^{\omega-1}y^{\prime}

x^{\prime}t(xy^{\prime})^{\omega}(x^{\prime}y)^{\omega-1}(x^{\prime}y^{\prime})^{\omega-1}x^{\prime}(yx)^{\omega+1}y^{\prime}

Now, expanding

, we get:

x^{\prime}t(xy^{\prime})^{\omega}(x^{\prime}y)^{\omega-1}(x^{\prime}y^{\prime})^{\omega-1}x^{\prime}y(xy)^{\omega}xy^{\prime}

(x^{\prime}y)^{\omega-1}

x^{\prime}t(xy^{\prime})^{\omega}(x^{\prime}y^{\prime})^{\omega-1}(x^{\prime}y)^{\omega}(xy)^{\omega}xy^{\prime}

Note that

x^{\prime}t(xy^{\prime})^{\omega}(x^{\prime}y^{\prime})^{\omega}(x^{\prime}y)^{\omega}(xy)^{\omega}(x^{\prime}y^{\prime})^{\omega-1}xy^{\prime}

Now, applying Claim
7.2
in reverse (using commutation again), we can obtain:

x^{\prime}t(x^{\prime}y^{\prime})^{\omega}(xy)^{\omega}(x^{\prime}y^{\prime})^{\omega-1}xy^{\prime}

Then commuting
(x^{\prime}y^{\prime})^{\omega}

x^{\prime}t(xy)^{\omega}(x^{\prime}y^{\prime})^{\omega-1}xy^{\prime}

A final commutation of
(x^{\prime}y^{\prime})^{\omega-1}

and we have preserved equivalence in the category of idempotents, establishing the result.

With Claim
7.2
in hand, and using Lemma
13
, we can now prove

15
:

Proof 7.6

(Proof of Lemma

, we can assume that
x_{1}=y_{1}
.

By Lemma
13
,
we insert a loop
(\rho\kappa)^{\omega}
after every
occurrence of
\tau

without changing the category element and still respecting the

n^{\prime},\omega
-congruence,
hence the
n^{\prime},p^{\prime}
-congruence because
p^{\prime}
divides
\omega
.
By expanding
(\rho\kappa)^{\omega}=\rho\kappa(\rho\kappa)^{\omega-1}
, it suffices to show that the following paths evaluate to the same category element, with
x_{1}=y_{1}
:

\displaystyle\pi_{1}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=x_{1}\tau\rho\kappa(\rho\kappa)^{\omega-1}\cdots x_{t-1}\tau\rho\kappa(\rho\kappa)^{\omega-1}x_{t}

\displaystyle\pi_{2}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=y_{1}\tau\rho\kappa(\rho\kappa)^{\omega-1}\cdots y_{t^{\prime}-1}\tau\rho\kappa(\rho\kappa)^{\omega-1}y_{t^{\prime}}

We can now regroup the occurrences of
\tau\rho
, which are loops such that
some edges (namely, the edges of
\tau
) only occur in these factors.
This means that we can conclude as in case 2 for the cycle
\tau\rho
, as this
cycle contains some edges that only occur there; we can choose
\beta
at the
end of the proof to be a loop on
x
visiting all edges of
G
except those
of
\tau
,
which is again possible because
G
is still strongly connected even after the
removal of
\tau
.

This establishes case 3 and concludes the induction step of the proof,
establishing Claim
8.1
.

We have thus proved by induction that
\pi_{1}
and
\pi_{2}
evaluate to the same
category element, in the base case (Claim
8.1
) of the outer induction
(Claim
8
) where all edges of
\pi_{1}
and
\pi_{2}
are frequent.

8.2.
Outer induction step (I): some arrows are rare.

Let us now show the induction step for the outer induction
(Claim
8
), namely, the
induction on the number of occurrences of rare arrows.
We assume the claim of the outer induction for
r\in\mathbb{N}
.
Consider two paths
\pi_{1}
and
\pi_{2}
and an
\left|S\right|
-distant rare-frequent
threshold
n^{\prime}
such that
\pi_{1}
and
\pi_{2}
are
n^{\prime},p^{\prime}
-equivalent and such that they contain

r+1
rare letters. Let us partition them as
\pi_{1}=q_{1}as_{1}

and

\pi_{2}=q_{2}as_{2}

where
q_{1}
and
q_{2}
all consist of frequent arrows, and
a
is the first rare
arrow of
\pi_{1}
and
\pi_{2}
(note that
n^{\prime},p^{\prime}
-equivalence implies that the first rare
arrow is the same in both paths).
In this case,
q_{1}
and
q_{2}
are two coterminal paths consisting only of
frequent arrows (or they are empty), and
s_{1}
and
s_{2}
are two coterminal
paths (possibly empty) with
r
rare letter occurrences.

Remember that, as
n^{\prime}
is an
\left|S\right|
-distant rare-frequent threshold for

\pi_{1}

and
\pi_{2}
, then we know that the frequent arrows of
\pi_{1}
form a union of SCCs
(Claim
6.2
); note that, thanks to

n^{\prime},p^{\prime}
-equivalence, the same is true of
\pi_{2}
with the same SCCs.
Consider the SCC
C
of frequent arrows that contains the starting object of
a
. There are two cases, depending on whether some object of
C
occurs again in
s_{1}
or not.
Note that some object of
C
occurs again in
s_{1}
iff the same is true
of
s_{2}
, because which frequent arrow components occur again is entirely
determined by the ending objects of the rare arrows of
s_{1}
and
s_{2}
, which
are identical thanks to
n^{\prime},p^{\prime}
-equivalence.

Case I.1:
C
occurs again after
a
.

In this case, we are in a situation where we can apply Lemma
15
, because
q_{1}
and
q_{2}
only
consist of frequent arrows and some object of the SCC
C
of the starting object
of
a
occurs again in
s_{1}
. The lemma tells us that
there is a path:

\pi_{1}^{\prime}=q_{2}as_{1}^{\prime}

which evaluates to the same category element as
\pi_{1}
and is
n^{\prime},\omega
-equivalent to
it, hence
n^{\prime},p^{\prime}
-equivalent.
Hence, by compositionality, it suffices to show that
s_{1}^{\prime}
and
s_{2}
evaluate to the same category element.

To apply the induction hypothesis, we simply need to ensure that
n^{\prime}
is still a

\left|S\right|
-distant rare-frequent threshold for
s_{1}^{\prime}
and
s_{2}
. To do this, we
need to ensure that the arrows that are frequent in
\pi_{1}
and
\pi_{2}
are still
frequent there, and still satisfy the
\left|S\right|
-distant condition. Fortunately,
we can simply ensure this by inserting a loop using Lemma
13
.
Formally, write
s_{1}^{\prime}=r_{1}t_{1}
where the intermediate object is the object of the
SCC
C
that occurred in
s_{1}
(the existence of such a decomposition is
a consequence of the statement of Lemma
15
), and write
s_{2}=r_{2}t_{2}
in the same way (which we already discussed must be possible with
s_{2}
).
Let
\beta
be an arbitrary loop of frequent arrows where all arrows of
C
occur:
this is possible because
C
is strongly connected. We know by
Lemma
13
that

s_{1}^{\prime}=r_{1}t_{1}
and
r_{1}\beta^{\omega}t_{1}
are both

n^{\prime},\omega
-equivalent, hence
n^{\prime},p^{\prime}
-equivalent, and evaluate to the same
category element: this is also true with

w_{1}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}r_{1}\beta^{n^{\prime\prime}\omega}t_{1}
for a sufficiently large
n^{\prime\prime}
such that every frequent arrow
of
C
occurs as many times as it did in
\pi_{1}
. Likewise,
s_{2}=r_{2}t_{2}
and

w_{2}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}r_{2}\beta^{n^{\prime\prime}\omega}t_{2}
are both
n^{\prime},p^{\prime}
-equivalent and evaluate to the same category
element. So it suffices to consider
w_{1}
and
w_{2}
.

Let us apply the induction hypothesis to them. They are two coterminal paths,
and they are
n^{\prime},p^{\prime}
-equivalent because
w_{1}\sim_{n^{\prime},p^{\prime}}\pi_{1}^{\prime}\sim_{n^{\prime},p^{\prime}}\pi_{1}
and
w_{2}\sim_{n^{\prime},p^{\prime}}\pi_{2}
and by hypothesis
\pi_{1}\sim_{n^{\prime},p^{\prime}}\pi_{2}
. What is more, the arrows
that were rare in
\pi_{1}
and
\pi_{2}
are still rare for them, and they have
r

occurrences in total: this was true by construction of
s_{1}
and
s_{2}
and is
true of
s_{1}^{\prime}
because
\pi_{1}=q_{1}as_{1}\sim_{n^{\prime},p^{\prime}}q_{2}as_{1}^{\prime}
and all arrows
of
q_{2}
are frequent so the rare subwords of
s_{1}
and
s_{1}^{\prime}
are the same.
The arrows that were frequent in
\pi_{1}
and
\pi_{2}
are still frequent in
s_{1}
and

s_{2}
and occur at least as many times as they did in
\pi_{1}
and
\pi_{2}

respectively: we have guaranteed this for the arrows of
C
by inserting

\beta^{n^{\prime\prime}\omega}
, and this is clear for the arrows outside of
C
as
all their occurrences in
\pi_{1}
and
\pi_{2}
were in
s_{1}
and
s_{2}
respectively,
and
s_{1}^{\prime}
has at least as many occurrences of every letter as
s_{1}
does (this
is a consequence of the statement of Lemma
15
). This ensures
that
s_{1}^{\prime}\sim_{n^{\prime},p^{\prime}}s_{2}
, and that
n^{\prime}
is still an
\left|S\right|
-distant
rare-frequent threshold for them.

Hence, by the induction hypothesis, we have
s_{1}^{\prime}\equiv s_{2}
, so that by
compositionality we have
\pi_{1}\equiv\pi_{2}
.

Case I.2:
C
does not occur again after
a
.

In this situation, we cannot apply Lemma
15
. However,
intuitively, the arrows visited in
q_{1}
and in
q_{2}
must be disjoint from
those visited in the rest of the paths, so we can independently reason on
q_{1}

and
q_{2}
, and on
s_{1}
and
s_{2}
.

Formally, we first claim that
q_{1}\equiv q_{2}
by the base case (Claim
8.1
) of the
outer induction (Claim
8
). Indeed, first note that they are two coterminal paths. Now,
there are two cases: every arrow
x
which is frequent in
\pi_{1}
and
\pi_{2}
is
either in the SCC
C
of the
starting object of
a
or not. In the first case, all the occurrences of
x

in
\pi_{1}
must be in
q_{1}
, as any occurrence of
x
in
s_{1}
would witness that
we are in Case I.1; and likewise all its occurrences in
\pi_{2}
must be in
q_{2}
.
In the second case, all its occurrences in
\pi_{1}
must be in
s_{1}
and all
its occurrences in
\pi_{2}
must be in
s_{2}
, for the same reason. Thus,
q_{1}
and

q_{2}
contain no letter which was rare in
\pi_{1}
and
\pi_{2}
, some of the frequent
letters of
\pi_{1}
and
\pi_{2}
(those of the other SCCs) do not occur there at all,
and the others occur there with the same number of occurrences. Thus indeed
q_{1}\sim_{n^{\prime},p^{\prime}}q_{2}
, they contain no rare arrows, and
n^{\prime}
is still
an
\left|S\right|
-distant rare-frequent threshold for them. Thus, the base case of
the outer induction concludes that they
evaluate to the same category element.

We now claim that
s_{1}\equiv s_{2}
by the induction case of the outer induction
(Claim
8
).
Indeed, they are again two coterminal paths. What is more, by the previous
reasoning, the arrows that are frequent in
\pi_{1}
and
\pi_{2}
either occur only
in
s_{1}
and
s_{2}
or do not occur there at all. Thus,
s_{1}
and
s_{2}
contain

r
rare arrows (for the arrows that were already rare in
\pi_{1}
and
\pi_{2}
),
and the frequent arrows either occur in
s_{1}
and
s_{2}
with the same number of
occurrences as in
\pi_{1}
and
\pi_{2}
or not at all. This implies that
n^{\prime}
is still
an
\left|S\right|
-distant rare-frequent threshold for
s_{1}
and
s_{2}
. Thus, we have

s_{1}\sim_{n^{\prime},p^{\prime}}s_{2}
and the induction case of the outer induction establishes
that
s_{1}\equiv s_{2}
.

Thus, by compositionality, we know that
\pi_{1}
and
\pi_{2}
evaluate to the same category element.
We have concluded both cases of the outer induction proof and shown
Claim
8
.

8.3.
Concluding the proof.

We have proven Claim
8
, and explained afterwards how to use it to
show that
\pi_{1}
and
\pi_{2}
evaluate to the same category element.
This implies that
n^{\prime},p^{\prime}
-equivalence for our choice of
n^{\prime}
, hence also

n,p
-equivalence, is compatible with
S_{\mathrm{E}}
. Thus, by
Corollary
10
we know that
L
is in
\mathbf{ZG}*\mathbf{D}_{p}
. Hence,
L\in\mathbf{LZG}_{p}

implies that
L\in\mathbf{ZG}*\mathbf{D}_{p}
, so we have shown Claim
5
. Together with
Claim
5
, it establishes the locality result
\mathbf{LZG}_{p}=\mathbf{ZG}*\mathbf{D}_{p}
,
and we have shown Theorem
1
.

9.
Conclusion

In this paper, we have given a characterization of the languages of
\mathbf{ZG}
, and
proved that the variety
\mathbf{ZG}
is local. More specifically, we have shown this
for all the varieties
\mathbf{ZG}_{p}
for
p>0
, in particular
\mathbf{MNil}=\mathbf{ZG}_{1}=\mathbf{ZG}\cap\mathbf{A}
.

A natural question for further study is whether the variety
\mathbf{ZE}
is also local.
This question seems more complicated. Indeed, as proved by
Almeida
[
Alm94
]
, we have
\mathbf{ZE}=\mathbf{G}\lor\mathbf{Com}
,
that is,
\mathbf{ZE}
is the variety of monoids generated by the variety
\mathbf{G}

of groups and the variety
\mathbf{Com}
of commutative languages.
Now,
\mathbf{G}
is a local variety
[
TW85
, Example 1.3]
,
while
\mathbf{Com}
is not
[
TW85
, Example 1.4]
.
Further, as we have shown that
\mathbf{LZG}=\mathbf{ZG}*\mathbf{D}
and
\mathbf{Com}
is a subset of
\mathbf{ZG}
,
the counter-example languages (e.g.,
e^{*}af^{*}be^{*}cf^{*}
) to the locality of

\mathbf{Com}
(i.e., that are in
\mathbf{LCom}
but not in
\mathbf{Com}*\mathbf{D}
) cannot be
counter-examples to the locality of
\mathbf{ZE}
(because they are in
\mathbf{LCom}
,
hence
\mathbf{LZG}
, hence
\mathbf{ZG}*\mathbf{D}
, hence
\mathbf{ZE}*\mathbf{D}
).
This being said, if
\mathbf{ZE}
is indeed local, a proof would probably require
different techniques from ours, given that we do not see how our
techniques could be used even to reprove the locality of
\mathbf{G}
.

We hope that extending our approach to a study of locality for centrally defined
varieties in general could lead to such general results on the interplay of join
operations and of the locality or non-locality for arbitrary varieties, in the
spirit of the results shown in
[
CE13
]
for various Mal’cev
products.
