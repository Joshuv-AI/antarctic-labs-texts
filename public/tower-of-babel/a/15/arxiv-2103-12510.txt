The Newton Product of Polynomial Projectors. Part 2 : approximation properties
François Bertrand, Jean-Paul Calvi

Abstract
. We prove that the Newton product of efficient polynomial projectors is still efficient. Various polynomial approximation theorems are established involving Newton product projectors on spaces of holomorphic functions on a neighborhood of a regular compact set, on spaces of entire functions of given growth and on spaces of differentiable functions. Efficient explicit new projectors are presented.

1.
Introduction

In a recent paper
[
3
]
we introduced a new way of forming an approximation operator, precisely a polynomial projector, acting on spaces of functions defined on a (subset of a) space of dimension
n
out of two polynomials projectors on spaces of functions defined on a space of smaller dimension
n_{s}
,
s=1,2
,
n=n_{1}+n_{2}
. Our process was called the
Newton product
of the (smaller dimensional) projectors. It is related but considerably different from the classical method based on the tensor product of operators. The construction of the Newton product relies on a suitable graduation, called a
Newton structure
, of the interpolation conditions that define the projector. For instance, in the simplest case of a (univariate) Lagrange polynomial projector
\mathbf{L}[a_{0},\dots,a_{d}]
with respect to the
d+1
points
a_{0},\dots,a_{d}
, the specification of a (useful) Newton structure is equivalent to the specification of an ordering of the interpolations points. In fact, such a specification is already required in the Newton formula (based on divided differences) for classical Lagrange interpolation and there lies the origin of our terminology. In general, see below, a Newton structure is obtained in a more general way but, as a first approximation, the reader may retain that as far as the projectors are determined by a set of points (for instance, Lagrange, Kergin, Hakopian projectors) a (natural) Newton structure is determined by an ordering of these points. For example, if we stay with the ordering of
A=\{a_{0},\dots,a_{d}\}
and
B=\{b_{0},\dots,b_{d}\}
induced by the indexes,

(1.1)

\mathbf{L}[a_{0},\dots,a_{d}]\ntimes\mathbf{L}[b_{0},\dots,b_{d}],

(where
\ntimes
indicates the Newton product) is the bivariate Lagrange interpolation projector at the points
(a_{i},b_{j})
,
i+j\leq d
; this is a set of interpolation points first considered a long time ago by Bierman
[
5
]
. The main goal of the present work is to validate the previous one in answering in the affirmative the obvious question : if the partial projectors are efficient (in a certain sense) approximation operators, will it be the same for their Newton product ? Actually, we tried to write the present paper in order that it could work in the other direction : we relied as little as possible on the previous work in order that the reader interested by the results obtained in this one might decide to go back to
[
3
]
for a deeper understanding of the underlying algebraic machinery. The above question is answered positively for projectors on classical spaces of holomorphic functions on a neighborhood of a regular compact set (in the sense of pluripotential theory), on spaces of entire functions of given growth, on spaces of differentiable functions, the results in the latter case being however less precise. The proofs rest on an identical principle that is explained in the next section.

For the convenience of the reader and make the paper self contained, we will conclude this introduction with a definition of the Newton product as well as a few important examples.

The symbol
\oplus
denotes a direct sum of vector spaces,
\otimes
is used for the usual tensor product, while the symbol
\ntimes
is reserved for the Newton product.

A polynomial projector
\Pi
of degree
d
on a space of (real or complex) functions
\mathop{\hbox{$\mathcal{E}$}}=\mathop{\hbox{$\mathcal{E}$}}(X)
defined on

,

or
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{C}

\mathop{\hbox{$\mathcal{E}$}}
with values in the
\mathop{\hbox{$\mathcal{E}$}}
-subspace
\mathcal{P}_{d}(\mathbb{K}^{n})
of real or complex polynomials of total degree at most
d
, such that
\Pi\circ\Pi=\Pi
. We set

\texttt{\rm\footnotesize CND}(\Pi)=\big\{\nu\in{\mathop{\hbox{$\mathcal{E}$}}}^{\prime}\;:\;\nu(f)=\nu(\Pi(f)),\;\text{all $f\in\mathop{\hbox{$\mathcal{E}$}}$}\big\}

where
{\mathop{\hbox{$\mathcal{E}$}}}^{\prime}
is the space of continuous linear forms on
\mathop{\hbox{$\mathcal{E}$}}
. We call
\texttt{\rm\footnotesize CND}(\Pi)
the
space of interpolation conditions
for
\Pi
. For instance if
\Pi
is a Lagrange interpolation projector then
\texttt{\rm\footnotesize CND}(\Pi)
is the space of functionals spanned by the Dirac functionals
\delta_{a}:f\to f(a)
when
a
runs among the interpolation points. A list of the classical spaces
\mathop{\hbox{$\mathcal{E}$}}
that that will be considered can be found in Table
1
below.

A
Newton structure
for
\Pi
is a direct sum of subspaces
J_{i}\subset\texttt{\rm\footnotesize CND}(\Pi)
, that is,

\texttt{\rm\footnotesize CND}(\Pi)=J_{0}\oplus J_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus J_{d},

such that, for
j=0,\dots,d
, there exists a projector
\Pi_{j}
of degree
j
with

\texttt{\rm\footnotesize CND}(\Pi_{j})=J_{0}\oplus J_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus J_{j}.

It is shown in
[
3
]
that all projectors possess Newton structures. In fact, to obtain a Newton structure is equivalent to find a basis
(\mu_{\alpha}\;:\;|\alpha|\leq d)
where

denotes the length of
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
such that for
j=0,\dots,d
, the sub-list
(\mu_{\alpha}\;:\;|\alpha|\leq j)
is linearly independent on

and the link with the previous definition is given by

\texttt{\rm\footnotesize CND}(\Pi_{j})={\text{\rm span}}\left\{\mu_{\alpha}\;:\;|\alpha|\leq j\right\}\quad\text{or}\quad J_{j}={\text{\rm span}}\left\{\mu_{\alpha}\;:\;|\alpha|=j\right\},\quad j=0,\dots,d.

A polynomial projector together with a specific Newton structure is called a
Newton-structured projector
. It is important not to confuse a polynomial projector with the richer notion of Newton-structured polynomial projector. To distinguish a mere projector from a Newton structured projector, we use the notation

[\Pi]=(\Pi_{0},\dots,\Pi_{d})

to denote the latter.

Now, given two Newton structured projectors
[\Pi^{i}]

i=1,2

\mathop{\hbox{$\mathcal{E}$}}(X_{i})
with respective Newton structure corresponding to the basis

\Pi

such that

\texttt{\rm\footnotesize CND}(\Pi)={\text{\rm span}}\left\{\mu^{1}_{\alpha}\otimes\mu^{2}_{\beta}\;:\;|\alpha|+|\beta|=0,\dots,d\right\}.

This projector
\Pi
is called the
Newton product
of
[\Pi^{1}]
and
[\Pi^{2}]
and is denoted by
[\Pi^{1}]\ntimes[\Pi^{2}]
.

For the sake of notational simplicity, we will sometimes (abusively) write

\Pi^{1}\ntimes\Pi^{2}
instead of
[\Pi^{1}]\ntimes[\Pi^{2}]
, especially when
the Newton structure we use is clear in the context but, in any case, it must
be remembered that the use of the symbol
\ntimes
(for the Newton product)
implies that Newton structures have been chosen. The main algebraic formula
regarding the computation of a Newton product on a product function is recalled
in Theorem
2.5
below.

2.2.
The Newton product of Newton sequences

2.3.
Strategy of proof

We describe the simple general strategy that we follow in answering Problem
1

Assume that we work with
\mathop{\hbox{$\mathcal{E}$}}(X_{1})
,
\mathop{\hbox{$\mathcal{E}$}}(X_{2})
and
\mathop{\hbox{$\mathcal{E}$}}(X_{1}\times X_{2})

Step 1
.

We find product polynomials

(2.7)

p_{\alpha,\beta}(z^{1},z^{2})=p^{1}_{\alpha}(z^{1})p^{2}_{\beta}(z^{2})\quad\text{with $\deg p^{1}_{\alpha}=|\alpha|$ and $\deg p^{2}_{\beta}=|\beta|$},

such that, for all

Step 2

Since
\Pi_{d}
is a projector of degree

(2.9)

\displaystyle f-\Pi_{d}(f)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=d+1}^{\infty}\sum_{|\alpha|+|\beta|=j}c_{\alpha\beta}(f)\big\{p_{\alpha,\beta}-\Pi_{d}(p_{\alpha,\beta})\big\}

(2.10)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=d+1}^{\infty}\sum_{|\alpha|+|\beta|=j}c_{\alpha\beta}(f)p_{\alpha,\beta}-\sum_{|\alpha|+|\beta|=d+1}^{\infty}c_{\alpha\beta}(f)\Pi_{d}(p_{\alpha,\beta}),

\mathcal{F}

Observe that, since the first series tends to

\mathcal{F}

d\to\infty
, we might restrict ourselves to show that
\sum_{j=d+1}^{\infty}\sum_{|\alpha|+|\beta|=j}c_{\alpha\beta}(f)\Pi_{d}(p_{\alpha,\beta})
goes to
0
as
d
tends to
\infty
in (
2.10
). It is however equally simple and somewhat more elegant to work with the right hand side of (
2.9
).

At this point, we need an algebraic formula established in
[
3
]
for the computation of a Newton product projector applied to a product function :

Theorem 2.5
.

Let, for
i=1,2
,
[\Pi^{i}]=(\Pi^{i}_{0},\dots,\Pi^{i}_{d})
be a Newton structured polynomial projector of degree
d
on
\mathop{\hbox{$\mathcal{E}$}}(X_{i})
,
X_{i}\in\mathbb{K}^{n_{i}}
. If
f_{i}\in\mathop{\hbox{$\mathcal{E}$}}(X_{i})
we denote by
f_{1}f_{2}
the product function defined by
(f_{1}f_{2})(x^{1},x^{2})=f_{1}(x^{1})f_{2}(x^{2})
then we have

(2.11)

\Pi(f_{1}f_{2})=\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathbb{N}_{d}(2)}\pi_{i}^{1}(f_{1})\pi_{j}^{2}(f_{2}),\quad f_{i}\in\mathop{\hbox{$\mathcal{E}$}}(X^{i}),

where the
\pi^{s}_{i}
denote the Newton summands corresponding to
\Pi^{s}
, see (
2.3
).

Proof.

See
[
3
, Theorem 4.5 (2)]
.
∎

We may now state the key computational lemma.

Lemma 2.6
.

We use the notation above, see in particular (
2.7
).
Assume that
|\alpha|+|\beta|\geq d+1
and
let

(2.12)

B(d,\alpha,\beta)=\{(i_{1},i_{2})\in\mathbb{N}^{2}\;:\;d+1\leq i_{1}+i_{2};\;i_{1}\leq|\alpha|;\;i_{2}\leq|\beta|\}.

We have

p_{\alpha,\beta}-\Pi_{d}(p_{\alpha,\beta})=\sum_{(i_{1},i_{2})\in B(d,\alpha,\beta)}\pi^{1}_{i_{1}}(p^{1}_{\alpha})\pi^{2}_{i_{2}}(p^{2}_{\beta}).

Proof.

Let
d_{1}=|\alpha|
and
d_{2}=|\beta|
. Since
\Pi_{d_{s}}
is a projector of degree
d_{s}
we have

p_{\alpha,\beta}=p^{1}_{\alpha}p^{2}_{\beta}=\Pi_{d_{1}}(p^{1}_{\alpha})\Pi_{d_{2}}(p^{2}_{\beta}),

and, in view of (
2.3
),

(2.13)

\displaystyle p_{\alpha,\beta}=\left(\sum_{i_{1}\leq d_{1}}\pi_{i_{1}}(p^{1}_{\alpha})\right)\cdot\left(\sum_{i_{2}\leq d_{2}}\pi_{i_{2}}(p^{2}_{\beta})\right)=\sum_{i_{1}\leq d_{1},i_{2}\leq d_{2}}\pi_{i_{1}}(p^{1}_{\alpha})\pi_{i_{2}}(p^{2}_{\beta}).

On the other hand, since
p_{\alpha,\beta}
is a product function, the product formula
in Theorem
2.5
yields

(2.14)

\Pi_{d}(p_{\alpha,\beta})=\sum_{i_{1}+i_{2}\leq d}\pi^{1}_{i_{1}}(p^{1}_{\alpha})\pi^{2}_{i_{2}}(p^{2}_{\beta}).

The relation follows immediately on subtracting (
2.14
) from (
2.13
).
∎

Step 3
.

The properties of the factors
\mathcal{N}^{s}
enter into play when estimating

p_{\alpha,\beta}-\Pi_{d}(p_{\alpha,\beta})
with the help of the formula given in the previous lemma.
To estimate the terms in the sum, we invoke the assumption that the divisor sequences
\mathcal{N}^{s}
,
s=1,2
, are converging via the use of a uniform boundedness principle (Banach-Steinhaus theorem),
[
27
, Chapter 2]
. It is therefore essential to work on spaces where the principle holds.

3.
Spaces of holomorphic functions on a neighborhood of a regular compact set

3.1.
The space
\mathop{\hbox{$\mathcal{H}$}}(K)

The space
\mathop{\hbox{$\mathcal{H}$}}(K)
formed of all functions holomorphic on a neighborhood of the compact set
K
is endowed with the usual inductive limit of the spaces
H(\Omega)
or, equivalently,
A(\Omega)
where
\Omega
is an open (bounded) neighborhood of
K
and
H(\Omega)
denotes the space of holomorphic functions on
\Omega
with the topology of uniform convergence on compact subsets, while
A(\Omega)
is the
Banach space
of the functions continuous on
\overline{\Omega}
and holomorphic on
\Omega
with the usual sup-norm on
\overline{\Omega}
. Thus, for instance, a sequence

f_{n}
converge to
f
in
\mathop{\hbox{$\mathcal{H}$}}(K)
if and only if there exists an open bounded neighborhood
\Omega
of
K
such that
f
and
f_{n}
are in
A(\Omega)
for
all
n
and
f_{n}
converges uniformly to
f
on
\overline{\Omega}
. A linear map from
\mathop{\hbox{$\mathcal{H}$}}(K)
onto another topological vector space
\mathcal{F}

is continuous if and only if all its restrictions to the spaces
A(\Omega)
,
\Omega\supset K
are continuous.

3.2.
Some tools from pluripotential theory

We recall some basic facts from pluripotential complex theory which are required in the sequel. A good general reference is the book by Klimek
[
17
]
. The survey by Levenberg
[
18
]
also contains the required material (and much more) and is more approximation-theory oriented. The non-specialist reader may freely assume that the compact sets
K
we work with are convex in the ordinary geometrical sense. This would imply a limited loss of generality only, at least from a practical point of view.

Let
K
be a regular polynomially convex compact set in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
. Its continuous pluri-subharmonic Green-Siciak extremal function is denoted by
V_{K}
. Recall that this function can be defined for instance as

V_{K}(z)=\max\left(0,\sup\Big\{\frac{1}{\deg p}\ln|p(z)|\;:\;p\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{C}^{n}),\;\|p\|_{K}\leq 1\Big\}\right).

For
R>1
, we define the bounded open set
K_{R}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}^{n}\;:\;V_{K}(z)<\ln R\}
.

A first use of these level sets appears in the Bernstein-Walsh-Siciak inequality
[
17
]
which states that

(3.1)

\|p\|_{\overline{K_{R}}}\leq R^{\deg p}\|p\|_{K},\quad p\in\mathcal{P}_{d}(\mathbb{C}^{n}),\quad R>1.

The level sets
K_{R}
are further related to the rate of polynomial approximation of a holomorphic function on
K
. Namely, if

\text{dist}(f,\mathcal{P}_{d}(\mathbb{C}^{n}))=\inf\{\|f-p\|_{K}\;:\;p\in\mathcal{P}_{d}(\mathbb{C}^{n})\},

then
f
is holomorphic (or extends to a holomorphic function) on
K_{R}
if and only if

\limsup_{d\to\infty}\text{dist}\Big(f,\mathcal{P}_{d}(\mathbb{C}^{n})\Big)^{1/d}<1/R.

In particular, setting

(3.2)

\limsup_{d\to\infty}\text{dist}\Big(f,\mathcal{P}_{d}(\mathbb{C}^{n})\Big)^{1/d}=\dfrac{1}{\rho(f)},

a continuous function
f
on
K
extends to a function in
\mathop{\hbox{$\mathcal{H}$}}(K)
if and only if
\rho(f)>1

and, more precisely, for all
R<\rho(f)
,
f
extends to a function in
A(K_{R})
.

3.3.
Bernstein-Markov measures

An asymptotically optimal approximation polynomial for functions in
\mathop{\hbox{$\mathcal{H}$}}(K)
,
K
as above, can be obtained as a Fourier expansion with respect to a suitable probability measure as follows. One says that a probability measure
\mu
with compact support on
K
is a
Bernstein-Markov measure
if, for all
\epsilon>0
, there exists a constant
C(\epsilon)=C(\epsilon,\mu)
such that for all polynomial
p
we have

(3.3)

\|p\|_{K}\leq C(\epsilon)(1+\varepsilon)^{\deg(p)}\|p\|_{2},\quad\|p\|_{2}=\sqrt{\int_{K}|p(z)|^{2}d\mu(z)}.

In other words, the
L^{2}
-norm of polynomial behaves asymptotically essentially like the sup-norm on
K
. By a theorem of Nguyen and Zériahi
[
20
]
, on a regular compact set
K
as above, such a measure always exists. See also
[
11
]
for a general discussion on Bernstein-Markov measures.

Given such a measure, we denote by
(b_{\alpha})
the orthonormal sequence of polynomials obtained by the Gram-Schmidt process from the monomial sequence
(z^{\alpha})
ordered with respect to the graded lexicographic order. In particular,
z^{\alpha}
is the leading monomial in
b_{\alpha}
and
\deg b_{\alpha}=|\alpha|
. When it is necessary to clarify the measure we use, instead of
b_{\alpha}(z)
, we use the notation
b(\alpha,\mu,z)
as in the introduction. The corresponding orthogonal projection is

(3.4)

\mathbf{P}_{d,\mathbf{dm}}(f)=\sum_{|\alpha|\leq d}c_{\alpha}(f)b_{\alpha},\\
\quad c_{\alpha}(f)=\langle f,b_{\alpha}\rangle=\int_{K}f(z)\overline{b}_{\alpha}(z)\mathbf{dm}(z),\quad f\in\mathop{\hbox{$\mathcal{H}$}}(K).

It is well known, see
[
33
]
, that,
\mathbf{dm}
being a Bernstein-Markov measure,

(3.5)

\limsup_{d\to\infty}\{\|f-\mathbf{P}_{d,\mathbf{dm}}(f)\|_{K}\}^{1/d}=\limsup_{d\to\infty}\text{dist}(f,\mathcal{P}_{d}(\mathbb{C}^{n}))^{1/d}.

This is easily shown as follows. Taking
t_{d}
, a best (uniform) approximation of degree
d
of
f
on
K
, i.e. such that
\text{dist}(f,\mathcal{P}_{d}(\mathbb{C}^{n}))=\|f-t_{d}\|_{K}
, we have

(3.6)

\displaystyle\|f-t_{d}\|_{K}

\displaystyle\leq\|f-\mathbf{P}_{d,\mathbf{dm}}(f)\|_{K}\leq C(1+\epsilon)^{d}\|f-\mathbf{P}_{d,\mathbf{dm}}(f)\|_{2}

(3.7)

\displaystyle\leq C(1+\epsilon)^{d}\|f-t_{d}\|_{2}\leq C(1+\epsilon)^{d}\|f-t_{d}\|_{K},

where we use that the orthogonal projection furnishes the best
L^{2}
approximant of
f
on the second line. Now, (
3.5
) immediately follows.

Likewise, since, by orthogonality,

c_{\alpha}(f)=\langle f,b_{\alpha}\rangle=\langle f-\mathbf{P}_{|\alpha|-1,\mathbf{dm}}(f),b_{\alpha}\rangle,

by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we have

(3.8)

|c_{\alpha}(f)|\leq\|f-\mathbf{P}_{|\alpha|-1,\mathbf{dm}}(f)\|_{2}\leq\|f-t_{|\alpha|-1}\|_{2}\\
\leq\|f-t_{|\alpha|-1}\|_{2}\leq\text{dist}(f,\mathcal{P}_{|\alpha|-1}(\mathbb{C}^{n})).

Note that, in fact, we have the series expansion

(3.9)

f=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\sum_{|\alpha|=j}c_{\alpha}(f)b_{\alpha},\quad f\in\mathop{\hbox{$\mathcal{H}$}}(K),

where the convergence holds in
\mathop{\hbox{$\mathcal{H}$}}(K)
. For this and other applications to pluripotential theory, we refer to
[
33
]
.

Observe finally that, when applied with
p=b_{\alpha}
, inequality (
3.3
) reads as

(3.10)

\|b_{\alpha}\|_{K}\leq C(\epsilon)(1+\varepsilon)^{|\alpha|},\quad|\alpha|=0,1,2,\dots.

3.4.
Cartesian products

We now collect a few facts that we will need later about the above notions in relation to Cartesian products of sets.

(i)

The Cartesian product
K_{1}\times K_{2}\subset\mathbb{C}^{n_{1}}\times\mathbb{C}^{n_{2}}
of two regular polynomially convex sets is still regular polynomially convex and, see
[
17
]
,

V_{K_{1}\times K_{2}}(z^{1},z^{2})=\max\big(V_{K_{1}}(z^{1}),V_{K_{2}}(z^{2})\big).

In particular,

{(K_{1}\times K_{2})}_{R}={K_{1}}_{R}\times{K_{2}}_{R}.

(ii)

The product
\mu_{1}\times\mu_{2}
of a Bernstein-Markov probability measure
\mu_{1}
on
K_{1}
by a Bernstein-Markov probability measure
\mu_{2}
on
K_{2}
is a Bernstein-Markov measure on
K_{1}\times K_{2}
, see
[
10
, Lemma 2, p. 290]
. Moreover, as is readily checked, the corresponding orthonormal polynomials satisfy the relation

(3.11)

b\left((\alpha^{1},\alpha^{2}),\mu_{1}\times\mu_{2},z\right)=b(\alpha^{1},\mu_{1},z^{1})\times b(\alpha^{2},\mu_{2},z^{2}),\quad z=(z^{1},z^{2}).

3.5.
The convergence theorem

Roughly, we prove that the Newton product of projectors that approximate efficiently holomorphic functions on regular compact sets itself efficiently approximates holomorphic function on the Cartesian product of the compact sets.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
K_{s}
be a regular polynomially convex set in
\mathbb{C}^{n_{s}}
and
\mathcal{N}^{s}=(\Pi_{0}^{s},\Pi_{1}^{s},\dots)
a Newton sequence on
\mathop{\hbox{$\mathcal{H}$}}(K_{s})
,
s=1,2
. If
\mathcal{N}^{s}
is converging on
\mathop{\hbox{$\mathcal{H}$}}(K_{s})
for
s=1,2
then
\mathcal{N}^{1}\ntimes\mathcal{N}^{2}
is converging on
\mathop{\hbox{$\mathcal{H}$}}(K_{1}\times K_{2})
.

Lemma 3.2
.

With the assumptions of the theorem, for
s=1,2
and for all
R>1
, there exists a constant
\gamma_{s}(R)
such that

\|\Pi^{s}_{d}(f)\|_{K}\leq\gamma_{s}(R)\|f\|_{\overline{{K_{s}}_{R}}},\quad f\in A({K_{s}}_{R}).

Proof.

Each projector
\Pi^{s}_{d}
is a continuous projector from
\mathop{\hbox{$\mathcal{H}$}}(K_{s})
onto
\mathcal{P}_{d}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
. Using the definition of the topology on
\mathop{\hbox{$\mathcal{H}$}}(K_{s})
and the fact that every norm is equivalent on
\mathcal{P}_{d}(\mathbb{C}^{n_{s}})
,
\Pi_{d}
defines a continuous projector from the Banach space
A(K_{R})
to the Banach space
\mathop{\hbox{$\mathcal{C}$}}(K)
of continuous function on
K
. The fact that
\mathcal{N}^{s}
is converging yields that
\Pi^{s}_{d}(f)
converges to
f
uniformly on
K
for all
f\in A(K_{R})
hence,
for such
f
, the sequence
(\Pi^{s}_{d}(f))
is bounded in
\mathop{\hbox{$\mathcal{C}$}}(K)
. Now, the claim results from an application of the uniform boundedness principle (Banach-Steinhaus Theorem) on the Banach space
A(K_{R})
.
∎

Corollary 3.3
.

Likewise, using the Newton summands,
\pi_{d}^{s}
, see (
2.3
), for
s=1,2
and for all
R>1
, there exists a constant
\delta_{s}(R)
such that

\|\pi^{s}_{d}(f)\|_{K}\leq\delta_{s}(R)\|f\|_{\overline{{K_{s}}_{R}}},\quad f\in A({K_{s}}_{R}).

Proof.

Use the lemma and the fact that
\pi_{d}^{s}=\Pi^{s}_{d+1}-\Pi^{s}_{d}
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

We take a Bernstein-Markov measure
\mu_{s}
on
K_{s}
and consider its product
\mu=\mu_{1}\times\mu_{2}
which is Bernstein-Markov on
K_{1}\times K_{2}
, see Subsection
3.3
. Following the strategy explained in Subsection
2.3
, we start from from the expansion

(3.12)

f=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\sum_{|\alpha|+|\beta|=j}c_{\alpha\beta}(f)b_{\alpha,\beta},\quad c_{\alpha\beta}(f)=\iint\limits_{K_{1}\times K_{2}}f(z)\overline{b}_{\alpha,\beta}(z)d\mu_{1}(z^{1})d\mu_{2}(z^{2}),

where
f
is any fixed element in
\mathop{\hbox{$\mathcal{H}$}}(K)
,
z=(z^{1},z^{2})
,
z^{s}\in K_{s}
, and, see (
3.11
),

(3.13)

b_{\alpha,\beta}(z^{1},z^{2})=b(\alpha,\mu_{1},z^{1})\times b(\beta,\mu_{2},z^{2}).

Recall that convergence in (
3.12
) holds in
\mathop{\hbox{$\mathcal{H}$}}(K)
. Next, a use of equation (
2.9
) and Lemma
2.6
gives

(3.14)

f-\Pi_{d}(f)=\sum_{j=d+1}^{\infty}\sum_{|\alpha|+|\beta|=j}c_{\alpha\beta}(f)\sum_{(i_{1},i_{2})\in B(d,\alpha,\beta)}\pi^{1}_{i_{1}}\big(b(\alpha,\mu_{1},\cdot)\big)\pi^{2}_{i_{2}}\big(b(\beta,\mu_{2},\cdot)\big),

where
B(d,\alpha,\beta)
is defined in (
2.12
). We will use this expression to show that
f-\Pi_{d}(f)
converges uniformly to
0
on
K
. Define as above,

\limsup_{d\to\infty}\text{dist}\big(f,\mathcal{P}_{d}(\mathbb{C}^{n})\big)^{1/d}=\dfrac{1}{\rho(f)},

so that since
f\in\mathop{\hbox{$\mathcal{H}$}}(K)
,
\rho(f)>1
. Choose
1<R_{1}<R_{2}<\rho(f)
and
\epsilon>0
to be fixed later. A use of Corollary
3.3
with
R=R_{1}
gives

(3.15)

\|\pi^{1}_{i}\big(b(\alpha,\mu^{1},\cdot)\big)\pi^{2}_{j}\big(b(\beta,\mu_{2},\cdot)\big)\|_{K}\leq\|\pi^{1}_{i}\big(b(\alpha,\mu^{1},\cdot)\big)\|_{K_{1}}\|\pi^{2}_{j}\big(b(\beta,\mu_{2},\cdot)\big)\|_{K_{2}}\\
\leq\delta_{1}(R_{1})\|b(\alpha,\mu^{1},\cdot)\|_{\overline{{K_{1}}_{R_{1}}}}\cdot\delta_{2}(R_{1})\|b(\alpha,\mu^{1},\cdot)\|_{\overline{{K_{2}}_{R_{1}}}},

Now, applying inequality (
3.1
) to the right hand side together with the bound (
3.10
) for the orthonormal polynomials (with the current
\epsilon
), we get the following estimate :

(3.16)

\|\pi^{1}_{i}\big(b(\alpha,\mu^{1},\cdot)\big)\pi^{2}_{j}\big(b(\beta,\mu_{2},\cdot)\big)\|_{K}\\
\leq\delta_{1}(R_{1})\delta_{2}(R_{1})R_{1}^{|\alpha|+|\beta|}C(\epsilon,\mu_{1})C(\epsilon,\mu_{2})(1+\epsilon)^{|\alpha|+|\beta|}\\
\leq\delta_{1}(R_{1})\delta_{2}(R_{1})R_{1}^{j}C(\epsilon,\mu_{1})C(\epsilon,\mu_{2})(1+\epsilon)^{j},

where we used
|\alpha|+|\beta|=j
.
Using, this estimate in (
3.14
) and setting

\tau=\delta_{1}(R_{1})\delta_{2}(R_{1})C(\epsilon,\mu_{1})C(\epsilon,\mu_{2}),

we obtain

(3.17)

\|f-\Pi_{d}(f)\|_{K}\leq\tau\sum_{j=d+1}^{\infty}(R_{1}(1+\epsilon))^{j}\sum_{|\alpha|+|\beta|=j}c_{\alpha\beta}(f)\text{card}(B(d,\alpha,\beta)),

where
card
is used to denote the cardinality.
Now, since
R_{2}<\rho(f)
, in view of
(
3.8
), there exists
\xi=\xi(R_{2})

(3.18)

|c_{\alpha\beta}(f)|\leq\text{dist}(f,\mathcal{P}_{|\alpha|+|\beta|-1}(\mathbb{C}^{n}))\leq\xi\left(\dfrac{1}{R_{2}}\right)^{|\alpha|+|\beta|}=\xi\dfrac{1}{R_{2}^{j}}.

(Note that, here,
\xi
is needed only for notational convenience, to have a bound valid for all
\alpha
and
\beta
, rather than for
|\alpha|+|\beta|
large enough.)
Hence, together with (
3.17
), we obtain

(3.19)

\|f-\Pi_{d}(f)\|_{K}\leq\tau\xi\sum_{j=d+1}^{\infty}\left(\dfrac{R_{1}(1+\epsilon)}{R_{2}}\right)^{j}\sum_{|\alpha|+|\beta|=j}\text{card}(B(d,\alpha,\beta)).

It remains to observe that the right hand side sum over
\alpha
and
\beta
grows polynomially in
j
. This is readily seen. Indeed,

(3.20)

\text{card}(B(d,\alpha,\beta))\leq(|\alpha|+1)\times(|\beta|+1)\leq(j+1)^{2}\quad\text{(since $|\alpha|+|\beta|=j$)}.

Hence,

(3.21)

\sum_{|\alpha|+|\beta|=j}\text{card}(B(d,\alpha,\beta))\leq(j+1)^{2}\binom{n+j}{j-1}=O(j^{n+2}),\quad n=n_{1}+n_{2}.

Now take
\epsilon
so that
R_{1}(1+\epsilon)<R_{2}
. This is possible since
R_{1}<R_{2}
and
\epsilon
can be taken arbitrarily small.
In view of (
3.21
), the right hand side of (
3.19
) is the remainder of a converging series and this shows that
\|f-\Pi_{d}(f)\|_{K}\to 0
as
d
tends to
\infty
. Thus, at this point, using the terminology introduced in definition
2.2
, we proved that
\mathcal{N}^{1}\ntimes\mathcal{N}^{2}
is
\mathop{\hbox{$\mathcal{C}$}}(K)
-converging on
\mathop{\hbox{$\mathcal{H}$}}(K_{1}\times K_{2})
.

Actually, since
\Pi_{d}(f)
is a polynomial projector the uniform convergence on
K
implies the convergence on a compact neighborhood of
K
, hence in
\mathop{\hbox{$\mathcal{H}$}}(K)
.
Such a reasoning is detailed in
[
9
, Section 4, p. 17]
.
In fact, since
R_{1}(1+\epsilon)
can be taken arbitrarily close to
1
and
R_{2}
arbitrarily close to
\rho(f)
, our proof actually shows that

(3.22)

\limsup_{d\to\infty}\|f-\Pi_{d}(f)\|_{K}^{1/d}=\dfrac{1}{\rho(f)},

so that
\Pi_{d}(f)
provides an asymptotically optimal approximation. This fact also classically implies convergence in
\mathop{\hbox{$\mathcal{H}$}}(K)
. Here is a sketch of the proof.
The series

L=\Pi_{0}(f)+\sum_{d=0}^{\infty}\Pi_{d+1}(f)-\Pi_{d}(f)

is normally converging on
\overline{K_{R}}
, for
R<\rho(f)
. Indeed, by Bernstein Walsh Siciak inequality,
(
3.1
)

\displaystyle\|\Pi_{d+1}(f)-\Pi_{d}(f)\|_{K_{R}}

\displaystyle\leq R^{d+1}\|\Pi_{d+1}(f)-\Pi_{d}(f)\|_{K}

\displaystyle\leq R^{d+1}\|f-\Pi_{d}(f)\|_{K}+R^{d+1}\|f-\Pi_{d+1}(f)\|_{K},

which in view (
3.22
) is the general term of a converging series. Now the limit
L
must equal
f
on
K_{R}
since it coincides with
f
on
K
.
∎

3.6.
Examples

Of course, most classical constructive polynomial approximation results in the complex domain can be used together with the above theorem to get efficient multivariate projectors. We just point out three very natural examples. The first one shows how a well-known result is re-captured with our theorem, the second and third ones provide new projectors which, it seems, deserve particular attention. All the Newton products we consider below are constructed using the Newton structure induced by the ordering of the interpolation points.

(A)

For
i=1,\dots,n
, we let
K_{i}
denote a regular polynomially convex plane compact set, and
a_{d}^{i}
,
d\in\mathbb{N}
, be a sequence of points on the boundary of
K_{i}
such that the discrete measure

\mu_{d}^{i}=\dfrac{1}{d+1}\sum_{j=0}^{d}[a_{d}^{j}]

converges weakly to the
equilibrium measure
on
K_{i}
.
Then, for every
f\in\mathop{\hbox{$\mathcal{H}$}}(K_{1}\times\cdots\times K_{n})
, we have

(3.23)

\mathbf{L}[a_{0}^{1},\dots,a_{d}^{1}]\ntimes\mathbf{L}[a_{0}^{2},\dots,a_{d}^{2}]\ntimes\cdots\ntimes\mathbf{L}[a_{0}^{n},\dots,a_{d}^{n}](f)

converges to
f
in
\mathop{\hbox{$\mathcal{H}$}}(K_{1}\times\cdots\times K_{n})
. This follows from classical univariate Lagrange interpolation theory, see the references given above, together with (iterated applications of) Theorem
3.1
. This well-known result was first published (in another presentation) in
[
28
]
. Siciak’s proof used a multivariate version of the classical (complex) Hermite error formula for Lagrange-Hermite interpolation. Note that the projector on the left hand side of (
3.23
) is itself a multivariate Lagrange interpolation
\mathbf{L}[A^{d}]
with the Bierman set of interpolation points, see also the introduction,

A_{d}=\left\{\left(a_{i_{1}}^{1},a_{i_{2}}^{2},\dots,a_{i_{n}}^{n}\right)\;:\;i_{1}+\dots i_{n}\leq d\right\}.

We refer to
[
3
, Section 6.3, p. 37]
for details and earlier references.

(B)

Let
K
be a convex circular compact set in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
(i.e.
z\in K,\;\theta\in\mathbb{R}\implies e^{i\theta}z\in K
). If
(a_{d})
is a sequence of points in
K
such that

\mu_{d}=\dfrac{1}{d+1}\sum_{j=0}^{d}[a_{j}]

converges weakly to a
e^{i\theta}
-invariant measure
\mu
, that is, such that

\int_{K}f(z)d\mu(z)=\int_{k}f(e^{i\theta}z)d\mu(z)\quad\text{for all $f\in\mathop{\hbox{$\mathcal{C}$}}(K)$ and $\theta\in\mathbb{R}$},

then the sequence of Kergin projectors
(\mathbf{K}[a_{0},\dots,a_{d}])
is converging on

4.
Spaces of entire functions

The effectiveness of a polynomial projector defined on the space of entire
functions usually depends on the growth (order
\omega
, type
\tau
) of the approximated functions; roughly, the farther the function is from the space of polynomials the stronger are the requirements on the projector. When
the projectors are univariate Lagrangian projectors at a sequence of points
(a_{d})
(of increasing modulus), the acceptable values for
\omega
and
\tau
depend on the velocity at which
|a_{d}|
goes to infinity. For instance, a classical result of Polya states that all entire functions of exponential type
<\ln 2
can be approximated (in
\mathop{\hbox{$\mathcal{H}$}}(\mathbb{C})
) by Lagrange interpolation at the points
a_{d}=d
and the upper bound
\ln 2
is optimal. Precise statements will be recalled below. As indicated above, the book by Gelfond
[
15
]
contains many results on this subject.

Here, assuming that
\mathcal{N}^{s}
is
\mathop{\hbox{$\mathcal{H}$}}(\mathbb{C}^{n_{s}})
-converging on a space
E_{s}
of entire functions on
\mathbb{C}^{n_{s}}
for
s=1,2
, we will look for a space of entire functions
E
on which
\mathcal{N}^{1}\ntimes\mathcal{N}^{2}
is
\mathop{\hbox{$\mathcal{H}$}}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
-converging,
n=n_{1}+n_{2}
.

We will begin by recalling the required material on the study of growth of entire functions of several variables.

4.1.
Growth of entire functions

Let
f\in\mathop{\hbox{$\mathcal{H}$}}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
and
N
a norm on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
. We set

M_{N}(f,r)=\max_{N(z)\leq r}|f(z)|,\quad r\geq 0.

Given
\omega>0
, the
\omega
-type

\tau=\tau(f,\omega,N)
of
f\in\mathop{\hbox{$\mathcal{H}$}}(\mathbb{C}^{m})
is defined as

\tau=\limsup_{r\to\infty}\dfrac{\ln M_{N}(f,r)}{t^{\omega}}.

When
\tau
is finite, it is the infimum of all
s
such that
\ln M_{N}(f,r)\leq sr^{\omega}+O(1)
as
r\to\infty
. The type may be infinite (when no such
\tau
exists) and it depends on the norm
N
we work with. For instance, for
\lambda>0
,

(4.1)

\tau(f,\omega,\lambda N)=\dfrac{1}{\lambda}\tau(f,\omega,N).

The interesting
\omega
-type is computed when
\omega
is the
order
of
f
, that is, the infimum of all
s
, if there exists, such that
\ln M_{N}(f,r)=O(w^{r})
as
r\to\infty
,

\omega=\limsup_{r\to\infty}\dfrac{\ln\ln M_{N}(f,r)}{\ln r}.

In contrast to the
\omega
-type, the order of an entire function does not depend on the norm
N
. A function of finite
1
-type
\tau
is said to be of
exponential type

\tau
.

In the sequel,

we will assume we work with norms the ball of which are poly-circular, that is to say,
N(z_{1},\dots,z_{n})\leq 1\implies N(\lambda_{1}z_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n}z_{n})\leq 1
for
|\lambda_{i}|=1
,
i=1,\dots,n
.

For sake of reference, we will call such a norm a
PC-norm
. The common
l_{p}
norms satisfy this condition. The usefulness of this condition appears in the lemma below which will enable us to use the strategy described in Subsection
2.3
by using the standard power series expansion as the starting expansion (
2.8
). Namely the lemma shows how the growth of an entire function is reflected into the behavior of the coefficients of its power series expansion. At the end of this section, we briefly explain a way to circumvent the condition for the norm to be PC, at least in certain important cases.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
f(z)=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}a_{\alpha}z^{\alpha}
be an entire function and
N
a PC-norm on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
. We have

(4.2)

|a_{\alpha}|\leq t^{-|\alpha|}\dfrac{M_{N}(f,t)}{\delta_{N}(\alpha)},\quad t>0,

where

(4.3)

\delta_{N}(\alpha)=\max\{|z^{\alpha}|\,:\,N(z)\leq 1\},\quad\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n}.

Proof.

The reasoning is taken from
[
26
]
. Let
P_{k}(z)=\sum_{|\alpha|=k}a_{\alpha}z^{\alpha}
and assume that, for a fixed
k
,
C_{k}=\max\{|P_{k}(z)\,:\,N(z)=1\}
is attained at
z=u
. Applying the Cauchy inequalities to the univariate function
g(w)=f(wu)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}P_{k}(u)w^{k}
, we get

C_{k}\leq t^{-k}\max\{|g(w)|\;:\;|w|=t\}\leq t^{-k}M_{N}(f,t),\quad t>0.

Now, the multivariate Cauchy inequalities applied to the polynomial
P_{k}
give

|a_{\alpha}|\leq r^{-\alpha}\max\{\left|P_{k}(u_{1},\dots,u_{n})\right|\;:\;|u_{i}|=r_{i},\;i=1,\dots,n\},\\
\quad r=(r_{1},\dots,r_{n}),\quad r_{i}>0.

We apply this inequality with

r\in L=\{(|u_{1}|,\dots,|u_{n}|)\;:\;N(u_{1},\dots,u_{n})\leq 1\}\subset\{N(u)\leq 1\},

where the inclusion holds because
N
is a PC-norm, thus arriving to

|a_{\alpha}|r^{\alpha}\leq C_{k},\quad|\alpha|=k,\quad r\in L.

Inequality (
4.2
) is now obtained by passing to the supremum over
r
in
L
on the left hand term.
∎

It is convenient to introduce the following spaces of entire functions.

Definition 4.2
.

Let
N
be a PC-norm on
\mathop{\hbox{$\mathcal{H}$}}(\mathbb{C}^{m})
,
\omega\geq 0
and
A>0
. We denote by
E_{\omega}^{m}(A,N)
the subspace of entire functions on
\mathbb{C}^{m}
for which there exists a constant
M
such that

M_{P}(f,r)\leq M\exp(Ar^{\omega})),\quad r\in\mathbb{R}.

There is an obvious connection between these spaces and the classical notions of order and type recalled above. We state it as a remark for future reference.

Remark 1
.

Given
\omega>0
and
\tau<\infty
, the following statements are equivalent.

(1)

f\in\mathop{\hbox{$\mathcal{H}$}}(\mathbb{C}^{m})
is
\omega
-finite type
<\tau
with respect to the norm
N
.

(2)

f\in\cup_{A<\tau}E_{\omega}^{m}(A,N)
.

We define a norm on
E_{\omega}^{m}(A,N)

by setting

\|f\|_{A,N}^{\omega}=\sup_{r>0}M_{N}(f,r)\exp(-Ar^{\omega}).

Observe that for all compact
K
in
\mathbb{C}^{m}
, there exists a constant
C(K)
such
that all
f\in E_{\omega}^{m}(A,N)
,

(4.4)

\|f\|_{K}\leq C(K)\|f\|_{A,N}^{\omega},

so that convergence with respect to the norm
\|\cdot\|_{A,N}^{w}
implies uniform convergence on all compact subsets of
\mathbb{C}^{m}
. From this, we deduce the following lemma whose standard proof is only sketched.

Lemma 4.3
.

When endowed with the norm
\|\cdot\|_{A,N}^{w}
, the space
E_{w}^{m}(A,N)
is a Banach space.

Proof.

Let
(f_{n})
be a Cauchy sequence in
E_{w}^{m}(A,N)
. In view of (
4.4
), it is also a Cauchy sequence in
\mathop{\hbox{$\mathcal{H}$}}(\mathbb{C}^{m})
and therefore converges to an entire function
f
. To show that such
f
is in
E_{w}^{m}(A,N)
, fix
r>0
, the uniform convergence on the
N
-ball of radius
r
shows that there exists
n_{0}=n_{0}(r)
such that
M_{N}(f-f_{n_{0}},r)\leq 1
so that

M_{N}(f,r)\exp(-Ar^{\omega})\leq M_{N}(f_{n_{0}},r)\exp(-Ar^{\omega})+\exp(-Ar^{\omega})\leq\|f_{n_{0}}\|_{A,N}^{\omega}+1\leq C,

the latter since the sequence
\|f_{n}\|_{A,N}^{\omega}
, being itself Cauchy, is bounded. Passing to the supremum over
r
, we obtain that
f\in E_{w}^{m}(A,N)
. A similar reasoning shows that the convergence of
f_{n}
to
f
holds in
E_{w}^{m}(A,N)
.
∎

Likewise, since, for any functional
\mu
(continuous linear form) on
\mathop{\hbox{$\mathcal{H}$}}(\mathbb{C}^{m})
, there exist a compact
K
and a constant
C(K)
such that, for all
f\in\mathop{\hbox{$\mathcal{H}$}}(\mathbb{C}^{m})
,
|\mu(f)|\leq C(K)\||f||_{K}
, the restriction of such a functional to
E_{w}^{m}(A,N)
is continuous with its topology of Banach space. We therefore have :

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
\Pi
denote a polynomial projector on
\mathop{\hbox{$\mathcal{H}$}}(\mathbb{C}^{m})
.
The restriction of
\Pi
to
E_{w}^{m}(A,N)
is continuous for its topology of Banach space.

We will need to know the
\|\cdot\|_{A,N}^{\omega}
-norm of the monomial
e_{\alpha}:z\to z^{\alpha}

in
\mathbb{C}^{m}
.

Lemma 4.5
.

We have

\|e_{\alpha}\|_{A,N}^{\omega}=\delta_{N}(\alpha)\left(\dfrac{|\alpha|}{e\omega A}\right)^{|\alpha|/\omega},

where
\delta_{N}(\alpha)=M_{N}(e_{\alpha},1)
is defined in (
4.3
).

Proof.

By homogeneity of
e_{\alpha}
, we have

M_{N}(e_{\alpha},t)\exp(-At^{\omega})=M_{N}(e_{\alpha},1)t^{|\alpha|}\exp(-At^{\omega})=\delta_{N}(\alpha)t^{|\alpha|}\exp(-At^{\omega}).

The claim follows by observing that the function
t\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\to t^{|\alpha|}\exp(-At^{\omega})
reaches its maximum at
t=(|\alpha|/(\omega A))^{1/\omega}
.
∎

4.2.
The convergence theorems

As above, we work with
\mathbb{C}^{n}=\mathbb{C}^{n_{1}}\times\mathbb{C}^{n_{2}},\quad n_{s}\geq 1
and let
N_{s}
denote a PC-norm on
\mathbb{C}^{n_{s}}
.
Moreover, given
w>0
and

For the sake of simplicity, we will also write

(4.5)

\|\cdot\|_{s}=\|\cdot\|_{A_{s},N_{s}}^{\omega},\quad s=1,2.

We first treat the case

, the case

Theorem 4.6
.

We use the notation above and assume

. Let

If
\mathcal{N}^{s}

for

,

-type

\mathop{\hbox{$\mathcal{H}$}}(\mathbb{C}^{n_{s}})

According to Remark

\mathcal{N}^{1}\ntimes\mathcal{N}^{2}

,

The theorem therefore implies convergence on

∎

Corollary 4.8

(Case
\tau_{1}=\tau_{2}
)
.

Let
0<\omega\leq 1
and
\tau>0
. If
\mathcal{N}^{s}

-type
<\tau
with respect to
N_{s}

\mathop{\hbox{$\mathcal{H}$}}(\mathbb{C}^{n_{s}})

Proof.

Use the previous corollary taking (
4.1
) into account.
∎

Note that the result is optimal if the assumption is. That is to say if there exists a function
f
of
\omega
-type
=\tau
with respect to

for which
\Pi^{1}_{d}(f)
does not converge to

A careful examination of the proof of Theorem

below would show that a version of the theorem holds true when the spaces

,

Such a condition is not satisfying since it does not ensure suitable convergence for all functions depending only on the group of variables

Given
\alpha_{s}\in\mathbb{N}^{n_{s}}
,

, we form
\alpha=(\alpha^{1},\alpha^{2})\in\mathbb{N}^{n}

\delta_{N}(\alpha^{1},\alpha^{2})

Lemma 4.9
.

With the notation of the theorem,

\delta_{N}(\alpha)=\dfrac{|\alpha^{1}|^{|\alpha^{1}}|\alpha^{2}|^{|\alpha^{2}|}}{|\alpha|^{|\alpha|}}\;\cdot\;\dfrac{\delta_{N_{1}}(\alpha^{1})\delta_{N_{2}}(\alpha^{2})}{{a_{1}}^{|\alpha^{1}|}{a_{2}}^{|\alpha^{2}|}}.

Proof.

In view of (
4.3
), we have

\delta_{N}(\alpha^{1},\alpha^{2})=\max\left\{|u_{1}|^{|\alpha^{1}|}|u_{2}|^{|\alpha^{2}|}\;:\;a_{1}N_{1}(u_{1})+a_{2}N_{2}(u_{2})=1\right\}.

Setting
u_{s}=r_{s}v_{s}
with
N_{s}(v_{s})=1
,

, the above relation translates into

\delta_{N}(\alpha^{1},\alpha^{2})=\delta_{N_{1}}(\alpha^{1})\delta_{N_{2}}(\alpha^{2})\max\left\{r_{1}^{|\alpha^{1}|}r_{2}^{|\alpha^{2}|}\;:\;a_{1}r_{1}+a_{2}r_{2}=1\right\}.

Now, it is readily seen that the function

r_{1}\in[0,1/a_{1}]\longrightarrow r_{1}^{|\alpha^{1}|}\left(\dfrac{1-a_{1}r_{1}}{a_{2}}\right)^{|\alpha^{2}|}

reaches its maximum at

r_{1}=\dfrac{1}{a_{1}}\;\cdot\;\dfrac{|\alpha^{1}|}{|\alpha|},

from wich the lemma immediately follows.
∎

We turn to the proof of Theorem
4.6
.

Proof.

Using the notation (
2.5
), we need to show that for any
f\in E_{\omega}^{n}(A,N)
,

\Pi_{d}(f)
converges to
f
in
\mathop{\hbox{$\mathcal{H}$}}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
as

, that is,
\Pi_{d}(f)
converges to
f
uniformly on every compact subset of
\mathbb{C}^{n}
. We fix such a
f
and
K=K_{1}\times K_{2}
a compact set in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
with
K_{s}\in\mathbb{C}^{n_{s}}
and prove that
\|f-\Pi_{d}(f)\|_{K}\to 0
as

.

Step 4

(Banach-Steinhaus)
.

In view of Lemma

,
\Pi^{s}_{d}:E_{s}\to\mathop{\hbox{$\mathcal{C}$}}(K_{s})

. Hence, by the Banach-Steinhaus Theorem, there exists a constant

such that, see (

\|\Pi^{s}_{d}(f_{s})\|_{K_{s}}\leq\Gamma_{s}\|f_{s}\|_{s},\quad d\in\mathbb{N},\quad f_{s}\in E_{s},\quad s=1,2.

Likewise, using the Newton summands
\pi_{d}^{s}=\Pi^{s}_{d}-\Pi^{s}_{d-1}
, just as in Corollary
3.3
,

(4.9)

\|\pi^{s}_{d}(f_{s})\|_{K_{s}}\leq C_{s}\|f_{s}\|_{s},\quad d\in\mathbb{N},\quad f_{s}\in E_{s},\quad s=1,2,

with
C_{s}=2\Gamma_{s}
.

Step 5

(Using the strategy described in Subsection
2.3
)
.

Writing
e^{s}_{\alpha}:z^{s}\in\mathbb{C}^{n_{s}}\to z^{\alpha}
, we start from the power series expansion of

,

f(z^{1},z^{2})=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\sum_{|\alpha^{1}|+|\alpha^{2}|=j}c_{(\alpha^{1},\alpha^{2})}e_{\alpha^{1}}(z^{1})e_{\alpha^{2}}(z^{2}).

f-\Pi_{d}(f)=\sum_{j=d+1}^{\infty}\sum_{|\alpha^{1}|+|\alpha^{2}|=j}c_{\alpha^{1}\alpha^{2}}\left\{\sum_{(i_{1},i_{2})\in B(d,\alpha^{1},\alpha^{2})}\pi_{i_{1}}^{1}(e_{\alpha^{1}})\pi_{i_{2}}^{2}(e_{\alpha^{2}})\right\}.

We will denote by

the sum into brackets in the above equation.
We will prove that the series

\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\sum_{|\alpha^{1}|+|\alpha^{2}|=j}\left|c_{\alpha^{1}\alpha^{2}}\right|\|R_{\alpha^{1},\alpha^{2}}\|_{K}

is converging and

will therefore go to

Step 6

(Estimating
\|R_{\alpha^{1},\alpha^{2}}\|_{K}
)
.

Using (

) in the above equation for

, we obtain

(4.10)

\displaystyle\|R_{\alpha^{1},\alpha^{2}}\|_{K}

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}C_{2}\text{card}\left(B(d,\alpha^{1},\alpha^{2})\right)\|e_{\alpha^{1}}\|_{1}\;\|e_{\alpha^{2}}\|_{2}

and, using the estimate (
3.20
) for

,

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}C_{2}(|\alpha^{1}|+1)(|\alpha^{2}|+1)\|e_{\alpha^{1}}\|_{1}\;\|e_{\alpha^{2}}\|_{2}

Now a use of Lemma
4.5
gives

\|R_{\alpha^{1},\alpha^{2}}\|_{K}\leq C_{1}C_{2}(|\alpha^{1}|+1)(|\alpha^{2}|+1)\delta_{N_{1}}(\alpha^{1})\delta_{N_{2}}(\alpha^{2})\\
\times\left(\dfrac{|\alpha^{1}|}{e\omega A_{1}}\right)^{\frac{|\alpha^{1}|}{\omega}}\left(\dfrac{|\alpha^{2}|}{e\omega A_{2}}\right)^{\frac{|\alpha^{2}|}{\omega}}.

Step 7

(Estimating
\left|c_{\alpha^{1}\alpha^{2}}\right|
)
.

Since
N_{s}
is a PC-norm for

so is the norm
N
and we may therefore apply estimate (

) in Lemma

to get

\left|c_{\alpha^{1}\alpha^{2}}\right|\leq\dfrac{t^{-|\alpha|}}{\delta_{N}(\alpha)}M_{N}(f,t)\leq M\dfrac{t^{-|\alpha|}}{\delta_{N}(\alpha)}e^{tA},\quad\alpha=(\alpha^{1},\alpha^{2}),\quad t\geq 0,

where we used

. Since, as in the proof of Lemma
4.5
, the function

reaches its minimum for

, we have

\left|c_{\alpha^{1}\alpha^{2}}\right|\leq M\left(\dfrac{|\alpha|}{e\omega A}\right)^{-|\alpha|/\omega}\cdot\dfrac{1}{\delta_{N}(\alpha)}.

Next, we use Lemma
4.9

\delta_{N}(\alpha)
and, after some simple calculation, we arrive to

(4.14)

\left|c_{\alpha^{1}\alpha^{2}}\right|\leq M\left(\dfrac{1}{e\omega A}\right)^{-|\alpha|/\omega}\cdot|\alpha|^{|\alpha|-1/\omega}\cdot\dfrac{{a_{1}}^{|\alpha^{1}|}{a_{2}}^{|\alpha^{2}|}}{|\alpha^{1}|^{|\alpha^{1}|}|\alpha^{2}|^{|\alpha^{2}|}\delta_{N_{1}}(\alpha^{1})\delta_{N_{2}}(\alpha^{2})}.

Step 8

(Conclusion)
.

Putting (
4.12
) and (
4.14
) together, setting

, we obtain

\left|c_{\alpha^{1}\alpha^{2}}\right|\|R_{\alpha^{1},\alpha^{2}}\|_{K}\leq C(|\alpha^{1}|+1)(|\alpha^{2}|+1)\\
\cdot\left(\dfrac{a_{1}^{\omega}A}{A_{1}}\right)^{|\alpha^{1}|/\omega}\left(\dfrac{a_{2}^{\omega}A}{A_{2}}\right)^{|\alpha^{2}|/\omega}\times\left\{\dfrac{|\alpha^{1}|^{|\alpha^{1}|}|\alpha^{2}|^{|\alpha^{2}|}}{|\alpha|^{|\alpha|}}\right\}^{\frac{1}{\omega}-1}.

The terms into brackets is obviously smaller than one
(since
|\alpha^{s}|\leq|\alpha|
), hence we have

\left|c_{\alpha^{1}\alpha^{2}}\right|\|R_{\alpha^{1},\alpha^{2}}\|_{K}\leq M(|\alpha|+1)^{2}q_{1}^{|\alpha^{1}|}q_{2}^{|\alpha^{2}|}\quad\text{with}\quad q_{s}=\left(\dfrac{a_{s}^{1/\omega}A}{A_{s}}\right)^{1/\omega}<1,

the latter by assumption (
4.7
). The convergence of the series of general term

follows and according to the second step, this concludes the proof of the theorem.

∎

Remark 2

(About the Assumption on the norm
N
.)
.

(A)

Any norm
N_{s}
is dominated by the PC-norm
\mathbf{N}_{s}
defined by

\mathbf{N_{s}}(z)=\max\{N(\lambda_{1}z_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n}z_{n}),\;|\lambda_{i}|=1,\;i=1,\dots,n\},

and
\mathbf{N}_{s}
is the smallest norm satisfying the property. Since

E_{\omega}^{n_{s}}(A_{s},\mathbf{N}_{s})\subset E_{\omega}^{n_{s}}(A,N_{s}),

we may apply Theorem
4.6
to get a space depending on
\mathbf{N}
(defined from the norms
\mathbf{N}_{s}
as in the above theorems) on which the Newton product converges. This space however is unlikely to be optimal, as follows from the next remark.

(B)

In the case of entire functions of exponential type, another approach is available that enable to eliminate the assumption on the norm
N
to be
PC
. By representing such a function
f
(of exponential type
\tau<1
with respect to
N
) by a Laplace transform of an analytic functional,
see
[
16
, Section 4.5]
, we can write
f
as

f(w)=\int_{\{N^{\star}(z)\leq\tau\}}\exp\langle z,w\rangle d\mu(z),

where
\mu
is a complex measure supported on the ball
\{N^{\star}(z)\leq\tau\}
and
N^{\star}
is the
dual norm
of
N
(without assuming that
N
is PC). Recall that

N^{\star}(z)=\max\big\{|\langle z,\xi\rangle|,\;N(\xi)=1\big\}.

This yields a new way of computing
\Pi(f)
, hence
f-\Pi(f)
, namely

\Pi(f)(w)=\int_{\{N^{\star}(z)\leq\tau\}}\Pi\big(\exp\langle z,\cdot\rangle\big)(w)d\mu(z),

which is interesting since
w\to\exp\langle z,w\rangle
is the product
\exp\langle z^{1},w^{1}\rangle\times\exp\langle z^{2},w^{2}\rangle
so that we may use the available formula for the Newton projector of a product function (Theorem
2.5
). Let us just point out how the formula for
N
naturally comes from the norms
N_{s}
through duality. In fact, if
N=\tau_{1}N_{1}+\tau_{2}N_{2}
as in Corollary
4.7
then one readily checks that

N^{\star}(z^{1},z^{2})=\max\left\{(\tau_{1}N_{1})^{\star}(z^{1}),(\tau_{2}N_{2})^{\star}(z^{2})\right\}=\max\left\{\frac{N_{1}^{\star}(z^{1})}{\tau_{1}},\frac{N_{2}^{\star}(z^{2})}{\tau_{2}}\right\}.

Hence if
F
is an entire function of exponential type
<1
with respect to
N
, for some measure
\mu
we have

\displaystyle f(w)

\displaystyle=\int_{\{N^{\star}(z)\leq 1\}}\exp\langle z,w\rangle d\mu(z^{1},z^{2})

\displaystyle=\iint_{\{N_{1}^{\star}(z_{1})\leq\tau_{1}\}\times\{N_{2}^{\star}(z_{2})\leq\tau_{2}\}}\exp\langle z^{1},w^{1}\rangle\exp\langle z^{2},w^{2}\rangle d\mu(z^{1},z^{2}).

And the convergence of
\Pi_{d}(f)
to
f
can be obtained as from the convergence of

\Pi_{d}
for the product function
w\to\exp\langle z^{1},w^{1}\rangle\times\exp\langle z^{2},w^{2}\rangle
(the convergence being uniform in
w^{s}
). We omit the details.

4.3.
Example

We illustrate the above convergence theorems in the case
of the product of two Newton sequences of Kergin interpolation projectors as in Subsection

3.6
for which deep approximation results are available for entire functions. Let us first recall such a result. We use the notation introduced above. Give
\omega>0
, we set

(4.17)

c=c(\omega)=\int_{0}^{1/2}\dfrac{t^{\omega-1}}{1-t}dt.

In particular,

c(1)=\ln 2.

Given a sequence of points
a^{s}_{d}
in
\mathbb{C}^{n_{s}}
,
d\in\mathbb{N}
, such that the sequence of their norms
N_{s}(a^{s}_{d})
is non decreasing, we define the counting function
\mathcal{N}_{s}(r)
as the number of interpolation points whose
N^{s}
-norm is not bigger than
r
, that is

\mathcal{N}_{s}(r)=\text{card}\{i\in\mathbb{N}\;:\;N^{s}(a^{s}_{i})\leq r\}.

The
\omega
-
density

\Delta_{s}
, with respect to
N_{s}
, of the sequence
a^{s}_{d}
is then defined as

\Delta_{s}=\liminf_{r\to\infty}\dfrac{\mathcal{N}_{s}(r)}{r^{\omega}}.

It is known that, for all entire function of
\omega
-type
\tau^{s}
such that

\tau_{s}<c\Delta_{s}
, the sequence of Kergin interpolation polynomials
K[a_{0}^{s},\dots,a_{d}^{s};f]
converges uniformly to
f
on every compact subset of
\mathbb{C}^{n_{s}}
. This is a multivariate generalization of a Theorem of Gelfond
[
15
]
. It was first proved in the case of the standard euclidean norm by Bloom
[
7
]
and then extended to an arbitrary norm by Andersson and Passare
[
1
]
.

Here, a direct application of Corollary
4.8
yields the following.

Theorem 4.12
.

Let
(a^{s}_{d})
,
s=1,2
be two sequences of points as above. If their density are equal, i.e.
\Delta_{1}=\Delta_{2}(=\Delta)
, then, for all entire functions on
\mathbb{C}^{n}=\mathbb{C}^{n_{1}}\times\mathbb{C}^{n_{2}}
of
\omega
-type
\tau
with respect to the norm

, see below, satisfying

\tau<\Delta c,

where

is defined in (

), then the sequence of polynomials

(\mathbf{K}[a_{0}^{1},\dots,a_{d}^{1}])\ntimes(\mathbf{K}[a_{0}^{2},\dots,a_{d}^{2}])(f)

converges uniformly to

on every compact subset of
\mathbb{C}^{n}
.
Here

if

and
N=(N_{1}^{\omega}+N_{2}^{\omega})^{1/\omega}
if

.

Of course, the case

is handled by Corollary

4.7
.

5.
Spaces of smooth functions

5.1.
Adapting the tools

Roughly the same technique as in Section
3
can be used to derive results on spaces of differentiable functions. We will omit some details of the proofs where they are similar to those previously given. The results obtained are not optimal, this will be explained below. Although we will present the results in a more general setting, the reader may assume that, in what follows, all the compact sets considered are convex bodies (compact convex sets of non empty interior). Given a fat compact set
\kappa
(
\kappa
is the closure of its interior), we denote by
\mathop{\hbox{$\mathcal{C}$}}^{m}(\kappa)
the space of all functions which are
m
-times continuously differentiable on the interior of
\kappa
and whose all derivatives of order
\leq m
extend continuously to
\kappa
. It is a Banach space when endowed with the norm

(5.1)

\|f\|_{m,\kappa}=\max_{|\alpha|\leq m}\|D^{\alpha}f\|_{\kappa}.

First, we need a stronger notion of Bernstein-Markov measure, see Subsection
3.3
. Let
\mu
be a probability measure on
\kappa
. If there exists
\theta>0
such that, for some constant
C_{\mu}
, we have

(5.2)

\|p\|_{\kappa}\leq C_{\mu}(\deg p)^{\theta}\|p\|_{2},\quad p\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),

where as usual
\|p\|^{2}_{2}=\int_{\kappa}p^{2}(x)d\mu(x)
,
we say that
\mu
is a
\theta
-
strong Bernstein-Markov measure
(
SBM
) on
\kappa
. When there exists such a measure on
\kappa
, we say that
\kappa
is a
\theta
-SBM compact.
Observe that the sub-exponential term
(1+\varepsilon)^{\deg(p)}
in (
3.3
) is replaced in (
5.2
) by a polynomial term in the degree.
The classical Nikolskii inequality states that
dx
(Lebesgue measure) is
1
-SBM for
[-1,1]
, see
[
19
, Theorem 3.1.4]
. Zeriahi showed in
[
34
]
that the Lebesgue measure is SBM for a large class of compact sets. From the bounds in
[
34
, p. 686]
, we deduce that, when
K
is a convex body in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, then
\theta
can be taken as
3n/2
. Such a
\theta
is probably not optimal but we are not aware of more precise bounds. We omit the proof of the following lemma which is similar to that of [8, Lemma 2, p. 290], see also Subsection
3.3
.

Lemma 5.1
.

If
\mu_{s}
is a
\theta_{s}
-SBM measure on
K_{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{s}}
for
s=1,2
then
\mu_{1}\times\mu_{2}
is a
(\theta_{1}+\theta_{2})
-SBM measure on
K_{1}\times K_{2}
.

Recall now that a compact
\kappa
satisfies a
Markov inequality
of
exponent

r
if, for some positive constant
M_{\kappa}
, we have

(5.3)

\|D^{\alpha}p\|_{\kappa}\leq M_{\kappa}(\deg p)^{r|\alpha|}\|P\|_{\kappa},\quad\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n},\quad p\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{n}).

We refer to
[
21
]
for a large class of compact sets satisfying a Markov inequality, see also the surveys
[
25
,
23
]
. In the case of a convex body, the exponent
r
can be taken as
2
(in any dimension), see
[
32
]
.

It is readily seen that if
\kappa_{s}
satisfies a Markov inequality of exponent
r_{s}
,
s=1,2
, then
\kappa_{1}\times\kappa_{2}
satisfies a Markov inequality of exponent
r=\max(r_{1},r_{2})
.

If
\kappa
is a
\theta
-SBM compact satisfying a Markov inequality of exponent
r
, then there exists a constant
C_{\mu,\kappa}=C(\mu)M(\kappa)
, such that for all derivatives
D^{\alpha}
we have

(5.4)

\|D^{\alpha}p\|_{\kappa}\leq C_{\mu,\kappa}(\deg p)^{\theta+r|\alpha|}\|p\|_{2},\quad p\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{n}).

Finally, we say that
\kappa
is a
Jackson compact set
if for all function
f
in
\mathop{\hbox{$\mathcal{C}$}}^{m}(\kappa)
, we have

(5.5)

\text{dist}(f,\mathcal{P}_{d}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))\leq M_{J}d^{-m}

where the distance is with respect to the uniform norm on
\kappa
and
M_{J}
depends only on
f
,
m
and
\kappa
. For this notion of Jackson sets we refer to
[
24
]
and the references therein. In view of
[
2
, Theorem 2]
if
\kappa
is quasi-convex (i.e. satisfies the Whitney property P), so that, by the Whitney extension Theorem
[
31
,
6
]
, all
f
in
\mathop{\hbox{$\mathcal{C}$}}^{m}(\kappa)
extends to a function
\mathop{\hbox{$\mathcal{C}$}}^{m}
on a neighborhood of
\kappa
then for all degree
d
, there exists a polynomial
t_{d}
(of near-best simultaneous approximation of
f
) such that

(5.6)

\|D^{\alpha}(f-t_{d})\|_{\kappa}\leq\mathbf{M}_{J}/d^{m-|\alpha|},\quad d\in\mathbb{N},

where
\mathbf{M}_{J}=M_{J}(m,f)\geq M_{J}
depends only on
f
and
m
and
\kappa
. Such sets, in particular, convex bodies, are therefore Jackson sets.

Given a
\theta
-SBM measure
\mu
on
\kappa
, we may construct the sequence of orthonormal polynomials
b_{\alpha}
and the corresponding orthogonal projection

\mathbf{P}_{d,\mu}(f)=\sum_{|\alpha|\leq d}c_{\alpha}(f)b_{\alpha},\quad c_{\alpha}(f)=\langle f,b_{\alpha}\rangle=\int_{K}f(x){b}_{\alpha}(x)d\mu(x),

as in Subsection
3.3
.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a
\theta
-SBM compact set with measure
\mu
as well as a Jackson and Markov compact set with exponent
r
. For all
f\in\mathop{\hbox{$\mathcal{C}$}}^{\mathbf{m}}(K)
, we have

(5.7)

f=\sum_{d=0}^{\infty}\sum_{|\alpha|=d}c_{\alpha}(f)b_{\alpha}\quad\text{in \; ${\mathop{\hbox{$\mathcal{C}$}}}^{\overline{m}}(K)$,}

whenever
\mathbf{m}>\theta+r\overline{m}+1
.

Proof.

We denote by
\mu
the
\theta
-SBM measure on
K
. The reasoning is similar to that given in Subsection
3.3
, see also
[
34
]
. Let us write
H_{d}=\sum_{|\alpha|=d}c_{\alpha}(f)b_{\alpha}
. We prove that
\sum_{d=0}^{\infty}D^{\beta}H_{d}
uniformly converges on
K
for
\|\alpha\leq\overline{m}
. To explain the condition on
\mathbf{m}
, let us just observe that, the assumptions on
K
enable us to use (
5.4
) so that

(5.8)

\|D^{\beta}H_{d}\|_{K}\leq C_{\mu,K}d^{\theta+r\overline{m}}\|H_{d}\|_{2},

Now, let us just observe that here, calling
t_{d-1}
a best uniform approximation polynomial of
f
in
\mathcal{P}_{d-1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, see (
5.6
),
we have

(5.9)

\displaystyle\|H_{d}\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\|\mathbf{P}_{d,\mu}(f)-\mathbf{P}_{d-1,\mu}(f)\|_{2}

(5.10)

\displaystyle\leq\|f-\mathbf{P}_{d,\mu}(f)\|_{2}+\|f-\mathbf{P}_{d-1,\mu}(f)\|_{2}

(5.11)

\displaystyle\leq 2\|f-\mathbf{P}_{d-1,\mu}(f)\|_{2}

(
\mathbf{P}_{d,\mu}(f)
is best in
\mathcal{P}_{d}
)

(5.12)

\displaystyle\leq 2\|f-t_{d-1}\|_{2}

(
\mathbf{P}_{d-1,\mu}(f)
is best in
\mathcal{P}_{d-1}
)

(5.13)

\displaystyle\leq 2\|f-t_{d-1}\|_{K}\leq\frac{2M_{J}}{(d-1)^{\mathbf{m}}}\leq\frac{M^{\prime}}{d^{\mathbf{m}}}

\displaystyle\text{($K$ is Jackson and $f\in{\mathop{\hbox{$\mathcal{C}$}}}^{\mathbf{m}}(K)$)}.

This bound together with inequality (
5.8
) now yields

\|D^{\beta}H_{d}\|_{K}\leq C_{\mu,K}M^{\prime}d^{\theta+r\overline{m}-\mathbf{m}},

so that the series
\sum_{d=0}^{\infty}D^{\beta}H_{d}
converges normally on
K
as soon as

\theta+r\overline{m}-\mathbf{m}<-1
.
This shows that, under the assumption on
\mathbf{m}
, the right hand side of (
5.7
) converges in
\mathop{\hbox{$\mathcal{C}$}}^{\overline{m}}(K)
to a certain function
g
. Since
the convergence of the series to
f
plainly holds in
L^{2}
,
g
coincides almost everywhere with
f
on
K
, hence everywhere by continuity.
∎

5.2.
The convergence theorem

It is important to note the three levels of differentiability
\mathbf{m}
,

and

that occur in the following statement.

Theorem 5.3
.

For

\mathbb{R}^{n_{s}}

-SBM compact,

(2)

Let

\mathop{\hbox{$\mathcal{C}$}}^{\overline{m}}(K_{s})

\mathop{\hbox{$\mathcal{C}$}}(\kappa_{s})
-converging on
\mathop{\hbox{$\mathcal{C}$}}^{m}(K_{s})
for
