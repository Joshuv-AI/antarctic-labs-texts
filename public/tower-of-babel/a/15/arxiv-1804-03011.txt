Abstract
. The syntactic monoid of a language is generalized to the level of a symmetric monoidal closed category 𝒟 \mathscr{D} . This allows for a uniform treatment of several notions of syntactic algebras known in the literature, including the syntactic monoids of Rabin and Scott ( 𝒟 = \mathscr{D}= sets), the syntactic ordered monoids of Pin ( 𝒟 = \mathscr{D}= posets), the syntactic semirings of Polák ( 𝒟 = \mathscr{D}= semilattices), and the syntactic associative algebras of Reutenauer ( 𝒟 \mathscr{D} = vector spaces). Assuming that 𝒟 \mathscr{D} is a commutative variety of algebras or ordered algebras, we prove that the syntactic 𝒟 \mathscr{D} -monoid of a language L L can be constructed as a quotient of a free 𝒟 \mathscr{D} -monoid modulo the syntactic congruence of L L , and that it is isomorphic to the transition 𝒟 \mathscr{D} -monoid of the minimal automaton for L L in 𝒟 \mathscr{D} . Furthermore, in the case where the variety 𝒟 \mathscr{D} is locally finite, we characterize the regular languages as precisely the languages with finite syntactic 𝒟 \mathscr{D} -monoids.

1.
Introduction

One of the successes of the theory of coalgebras is that ideas from automata theory can be developed at a level of abstraction where they apply uniformly to many different types of systems. In fact, classical deterministic automata are a standard example of coalgebras for an endofunctor. And that automata theory can be studied with coalgebraic methods rests on the observation that formal languages form the final coalgebra.

The present paper contributes to a new category-theoretic view of
algebraic
automata theory. In this theory one starts with an elegant machine-independent notion of language recognition: a language
L\subseteq X^{*}
is
recognized
by a monoid morphism
e:X^{*}\to M
if it is the preimage under
e
of some subset of
M
. Regular languages are then characterized as precisely the languages recognized by finite monoids. A key concept, introduced by Rabin and Scott
[
19
]
(and earlier in unpublished work of Myhill), is the
syntactic monoid
of a language
L
. It serves as a canonical algebraic recognizer of
L
, namely the smallest
X
-generated monoid recognizing
L
. Two standard methods to construct the syntactic monoid are:

(1)

as a quotient of the free monoid
X^{*}
modulo the
syntactic congruence
of
L
, which is a two-sided version of the well-known Myhill-Nerode equivalence, and

(2)

as the
transition monoid
of the minimal automaton for
L
.

In addition to syntactic monoids there are several related notions of
syntactic algebras for (weighted) languages in the literature, most
prominently the syntactic ordered monoids of Pin
[
16
]
, the syntactic
idempotent semirings of Polák
[
18
]
and the syntactic associative
algebras of Reutenauer
[
20
]
, all of which admit constructions similar
to (1) and (2). A crucial observation is that monoids, ordered monoids,
idempotent semirings and associative algebras are precisely the monoid objects
in the categories of sets, posets, semilattices and vector spaces,
respectively. Moreover, all these categories are symmetric monoidal closed
w.r.t. their usual tensor product.

The main goal of our paper is to develop a theory of algebraic recognition in
a general symmetric monoidal closed category
\mathscr{D}=(\mathscr{D},\otimes,I)
, covering all the above syntactic algebras uniformly. Following Goguen

[
12
]
, a
language
in
\mathscr{D}
is a morphism
L:X^{\scriptscriptstyle\oasterisk}\rightarrow Y
where

X
is a fixed object of inputs,
Y
is a fixed object of outputs, and
X^{\scriptscriptstyle\oasterisk}

denotes the free
\mathscr{D}
-monoid on
X
. And a
\mathscr{D}
-automaton
is given by
the picture below: it consists of an object of states
Q
, a morphism
i

representing the initial state, an output morphism
f
, and a transition
morphism
\delta
which may be presented in its curried form
\lambda\delta
.

\textstyle{X\otimes Q\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\delta}
\textstyle{I\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{i}
\textstyle{Q\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{f}
\scriptstyle{\lambda\delta}
\textstyle{Y}
\textstyle{[X,Q]}

(1.1)

As observed by Rutten
[
21
]
, this means that an automaton is at the same time an
algebra

I+X\otimes Q\xrightarrow{[i,\delta]}Q
for the functor
FQ=I+X\otimes Q
, and a
coalgebra

Q\xrightarrow{\langle f,\lambda\delta\rangle}Y\times[X,Q]
for the functor
TQ=Y\times[X,Q]
. It turns out that much of the classical (co-)algebraic theory of automata in the category of sets extends to this level of generality. Thus Goguen
[
12
]
demonstrated that the initial algebra for
F
coincides with the free
\mathscr{D}
-monoid
X^{\scriptscriptstyle\oasterisk}
, and that every language is accepted by a unique minimal
\mathscr{D}
-automaton. We will add to this picture the observation that the final coalgebra for
T
is carried by the object of languages
[X^{\scriptscriptstyle\oasterisk},Y]
, see Proposition
2.26
.

In Section
3
we introduce the central concept of our paper, the

syntactic
\mathscr{D}
-monoid
of a language
L:X^{\scriptscriptstyle\oasterisk}\rightarrow Y
, which by definition
is the smallest
X
-generated
\mathscr{D}
-monoid recognizing
L
. In Corollary
3.10
we give a natural condition on a monoidal category
that ensures the existence of a syntactic
\mathscr{D}
-monoid for every
language
L
. Furthermore, assuming that
\mathscr{D}
is a commutative variety of
algebras or ordered algebras, we will show that the above constructions (1)
and (2) for the classical syntactic monoid adapt to our general setting: the
syntactic
\mathscr{D}
-monoid is (1) the quotient of
X^{\scriptscriptstyle\oasterisk}
modulo the syntactic
congruence of
L
(Theorem
3.14
), and (2) the transition

\mathscr{D}
-monoid
of the minimal
\mathscr{D}
-automaton for
L
(Theorem
4.7
). As special
instances we recover syntactic monoids (
\mathscr{D}=
sets),
syntactic ordered monoids (
\mathscr{D}=
posets), syntactic semirings
(
\mathscr{D}=
semilattices) and syntactic associative algebras (
\mathscr{D}=
vector spaces). In addition, our
categorical setting yields
new types of syntactic algebras “for free”. For example, we will identify
monoids with zero as the algebraic structures representing partial automata
(the case
\mathscr{D}=
pointed sets) and obtain the
syntactic monoid with
zero
for a given language. Similarly, by taking as
\mathscr{D}
the variety of
algebras with an involutive unary operation we obtain
syntactic
involution monoids
.

Most of the results of our paper apply to arbitrary languages. In Section
5
we will investigate
\mathscr{D}
-regular languages
, that is, languages accepted by
\mathscr{D}
-automata with a finitely presentable object of states. Under suitable assumptions on
\mathscr{D}
, we will prove that a language is
\mathscr{D}
-regular iff its syntactic
\mathscr{D}
-monoid is carried by a finitely presentable object (Theorem
5.6
). We will also derive a dual characterization of the syntactic
\mathscr{D}
-monoid which is new even in the “classical” case
\mathscr{D}=
sets: if
\mathscr{D}
is a locally finite variety, i.e. all finitely generated algebras are finite, and if moreover some other locally finite variety
\mathscr{C}
is dual to
\mathscr{D}
on the level of finite objects, the syntactic
\mathscr{D}
-monoid of
L
dualizes to the local variety of languages in
\mathscr{C}
generated by the reversed language of
L
.

Related work.

This paper is a reworked full version of the conference paper
[
2
]
.
Apart from including full proofs, it has two new contributions. First, we
present in Section
3.1
a purely categorical existence
criterion for syntactic monoids in an abstract symmetric monoidal closed category, whereas in

[
2
]
only a set-theoretic construction was given. Secondly, we treat the
case of ordered algebras explicitly, covering new examples such as the
syntactic ordered monoids of Pin
[
16
]
.

Another categorical
approach to (classical) syntactic monoids appears in the work of
Ballester-Bolinches, Cosme-Llopez and Rutten
[
4
]
. These authors
consider automata in the category of sets specified by
equations
or
dually by
coequations
, which leads to a construction of the automaton
underlying the syntactic monoid of a language. The fact that it forms the
transition monoid of a minimal automaton is also interpreted in that setting.
In the present paper we take a more general approach by
studying algebraic recognition in an arbitrary symmetric monoidal closed
category
\mathscr{D}
. One important source of inspiration for our categorical setting
was the work of Goguen
[
12
]
.

In the recent papers
[
1
,
3
]
we presented a categorical view of
varieties of languages
, another central topic of algebraic automata theory. Building on the duality-based approach of Gehrke, Grigorieff and Pin
[
11
]
, we generalized Eilenberg’s variety theorem and its local version to the level of an abstract (pre-)duality between algebraic categories. The idea to replace monoids by monoid objects in a commutative variety
\mathscr{D}
originates in this work.

Another categorical setting for algebraic language theory can be found in Bojánczyk

[
8
]
. He considers, in lieu of monoids in commutative varieties,
Eilenberg-Moore algebras for an arbitrary monad on sorted sets, and defines
syntactic congruences in this more general setting. Our Theorem

3.14
is a special case of
[
8
, Theorem 3.1]
.

2.
Preliminaries

Throughout this paper we investigate deterministic automata in a symmetric monoidal closed
category. In all concrete applications, this category will be a commutative
variety
of algebras or ordered algebras. Recall that
for a finitary signature
\Sigma
a
variety of

\Sigma
-algebras
is a class of

\Sigma
-algebras specified by equations
s=t
between
\Sigma
-terms.
Equivalently, by
Birkhoff’s HSP theorem, it is a class of
\Sigma
-algebras closed
under products, subalgebras and quotients (= homomorphic images). Similarly,

ordered
\Sigma
-algebras
are posets equipped with order-preserving

\Sigma
-operations, and their morphisms are order-preserving

\Sigma
-homomorphisms. A

quotient
of an ordered algebra
B
is represented by a surjective morphism

e\colon B\twoheadrightarrow A
, and a
subalgebra
of
B
is represented by an

order-reflecting

morphism
m\colon A\rightarrowtail B
, i.e.
mx\leq my
iff
x\leq y
. A
variety of ordered
\Sigma
-algebras
is a class
of ordered
\Sigma
-algebras
specified by inequations
s\leq t
between
\Sigma
-terms. Equivalently, by
Bloom’s HSP
theorem
[
7
]
, it is a class of ordered
\Sigma
-algebras closed under
products,
subalgebras and quotients.

A variety
\mathscr{D}
of algebras or ordered algebras is called
commutative
(a.k.a.
entropic
)
if, for any two algebras
A
and
B
in
\mathscr{D}
, the set

[A,B]
of all homomorphisms from
A
to
B
forms a subalgebra
[A,B]\rightarrowtail B^{|A|}
of the product of
|A|
copies of
B
. Equivalently, the
corresponding monad over
\mathbf{Set}
(resp.
\mathbf{Pos}
, the category of posets) is
commutative in the sense of Kock
[
13
]
.

Example 2.1
.

In our applications, we shall work with the following commutative varieties:

(1)

\mathbf{Set}
is a commutative variety (over the empty signature) with
[A,B]=B^{A}
.

(2)

\mathbf{Pos}
is a commutative variety of ordered algebras (over the empty signature). Here
[A,B]
is the poset of monotone functions from
A
to
B
, ordered pointwise.

(3)

A
pointed set

(A,\bot)
is a set
A
together with a chosen point
\bot\in A
. The category
\mathbf{Set}_{\bot}
of pointed sets and point-preserving functions is a commutative variety. The point of
[(A,\bot_{A}),(B,\bot_{B})]
is the constant function with value
\bot_{B}
.

(4)

An
involution algebra
is a set with an involutive unary operation
x\mapsto\widetilde{x}
, i.e.
\widetilde{\widetilde{x}}=x
. We call
\widetilde{x}
the
complement
of
x
. Morphisms are functions
f
preserving complements, i.e.
f(\widetilde{x})=\widetilde{f(x)}
. The variety
\mathbf{Inv}
of involution algebras is commutative. Indeed, the set
[A,B]
of all homomorphisms is an involution algebra with pointwise complementation.

(5)

All other examples we treat in our paper are varieties of modules over a semiring. Recall that a
semiring

\mathds{S}=(S,+,\cdot,0,1)
consists of a commutative monoid
(S,+,0)
and a monoid
(S,\cdot,1)
subject the following equational laws:

0s=s0=0,\quad r(s+t)=rs+rt\quad\text{and}\quad(r+s)t=rt+st.

A
module
over a semiring
\mathds{S}
is a commutative monoid
(M,+,0)
together with a scalar multiplication
\cdot:S\times M\rightarrow M
(written simply as juxtaposition
(s,x)\mapsto sx
, as usual) such that the following laws hold:

\begin{array}[]{r@{\ }c@{\ }l@{\qquad}r@{\ }c@{\ }l@{\qquad}r@{\ }c@{\ }l}(r+s)x&=&rx+sx,&r(x+y)&=&rx+ry,&(rs)x&=&r(sx),\\
0x&=&0,&1x&=&1,&r0&=&0.\end{array}

We denote by
\mathbf{Mod}(\mathds{S})
the category of
\mathds{S}
-modules and module homomorphisms (i.e.
\mathds{S}
-linear maps). This is a commutative variety; here
[A,B]
is the set of all module homomorphisms with pointwise module structure. Three interesting special cases of
\mathbf{Mod}(\mathds{S})
are:

(a)

\mathds{S}=\{0,1\}
, the boolean semiring with
1+1=1
: the category
{\mathbf{JSL}_{0}}
of join-semilattices with the least element
0
, and homomorphisms preserving joins and
0
;

(b)

\mathds{S}=\mathds{Z}
: the category
\mathbf{Ab}
of abelian groups and group homomorphisms;

(c)

\mathds{S}=\mathds{K}
(a field): the category
\mathbf{Vec}(\mathds{K})
of vector spaces over
\mathds{K}
and linear maps.

Notation 2.2
.

For any variety
\mathscr{D}
of algebras or ordered algebras, we denote by
\Psi:\mathbf{Set}\rightarrow\mathscr{D}
the left adjoint to the forgetful functor

|\mathord{-}|:\mathscr{D}\rightarrow\mathbf{Set}
. Thus
\Psi X_{0}
is the free object of
\mathscr{D}
on

X_{0}
.

Example 2.3
.

(1)

For
\mathscr{D}=\mathbf{Set}
or
\mathbf{Pos}
we have
\Psi X_{0}=X_{0}
(discretely ordered).

(2)

For
\mathscr{D}=\mathbf{Set}_{\bot}

.

(3)

For

(whose elements are denoted

, and the universal arrow

(4)

For
\mathscr{D}=\mathbf{Mod}(\mathds{S})

is the submodule of
\mathds{S}^{X_{0}}
on all functions

Definition 2.4
.

Given objects
A
,
B
and
C
of a variety
\mathscr{D}
, a
bimorphism
from

A
,
B
to

C
is a function
f:|A|\times|B|\rightarrow|C|
such that the maps

f(a,\mathord{-}):|B|\rightarrow|C|
and
f(\mathord{-},b):|A|\rightarrow|C|
carry morphisms of
\mathscr{D}
for every
a\in|A|
and
b\in|B|
. A
tensor product
of
A
and
B
is a universal bimorphism

\eta_{A,B}:|A|\times|B|\rightarrow|A\otimes B|
, which means that
for every bimorphism
f:|A|\times|B|\rightarrow|C|
there is a
unique morphism
f^{\prime}:A\otimes B\rightarrow C
in
\mathscr{D}
with
f^{\prime}\cdot\eta_{A,B}=f
.

Theorem 2.5

(Banaschewski and Nelson
[
5
]
)
.

Every commutative variety
\mathscr{D}
of algebras or ordered algebras has tensor
products, making

(\mathscr{D},\otimes,I)
with
I=\Psi 1
a symmetric monoidal closed category. That is,
we have the following bijective correspondence of morphisms, natural in

A,B,C\in\mathscr{D}
:

\begin{array}[]{rl}f:&A\otimes B\rightarrow C\\
\hline\cr\lambda f:&A\rightarrow[B,C]\end{array}

Remark 2.6
.

We will use the following properties of the tensor product, see
[
5
]
:

(1)

-\otimes B
and
A\otimes-
are left-adjoints and hence
preserve all colimits.

(2)

The morphism
\lambda f
is obtained by currying the bimorphism
f^{\prime}=f\cdot\eta_{A,B}:|A|\times|B|\to|C|
corresponding to
f
, that is,
\lambda f(a)(b)=f^{\prime}(a,b)
for
a\in|A|
and
b\in|B|
.

(3)

The evaluation morphism
\mathsf{ev}=\mathsf{ev}_{A,B}:[A,B]\otimes A\to B
,
i.e. the counit of the adjunction
-\otimes A\dashv[A,-]
, is the morphism in

\mathscr{D}
corresponding to the bimorphism

\mathsf{ev}^{\prime}:|[A,B]|\times|A|\to|B|,\quad(h,a)\mapsto h(a).

(4)

The right unit isomorphism
i:A\cong A\otimes I
is given by

i~=~(|A|\xrightarrow{\langle{A,c_{1}}\rangle}|A|\times|I|\xrightarrow{\eta}|A\otimes I|)

where
c_{1}:|A|\to|I|
is the constant map that sends
a\in A
to
the generator
of
I=\Psi 1
.

Assumptions 2.7
.

For the rest of this paper let
\mathscr{D}
be a commutative variety of algebras or
ordered algebras, equipped with the monoidal structure
(\mathscr{D},\otimes,I)
of the above theorem. Furthermore, we fix an
object
X
(of inputs) and an object
Y
(of outputs) in
\mathscr{D}
.

Remark 2.8
.

Despite the above algebraic assumptions we will state many of our
definitions, theorems and proofs for an arbitrary symmetric monoidal closed
category

(\mathscr{D},\otimes,I)
subject to
additional properties.

Remark 2.9
.

Recall that a
monoid

(M,m,i)
in a monoidal category
(\mathscr{D},\otimes,I)

(with tensor product
\otimes:\mathscr{D}\times\mathscr{D}\rightarrow\mathscr{D}
and tensor unit
I\in\mathscr{D}
) is
an object
M
equipped with a multiplication
m:M\otimes M\rightarrow M
and unit
i:I\rightarrow M
satisfying the usual associative and unit laws. Due to
\otimes
and
I=\Psi 1
representing bimorphisms, this categorical definition is equivalent to the
following algebraic one in our setting: a
\mathscr{D}
-monoid
is a triple

(M,\bullet,i)
where
M
is an object of
\mathscr{D}
and
(|M|,\bullet,i)

is a monoid (in the usual sense) with
\bullet:|M|\times|M|\rightarrow|M|
a
bimorphism of
\mathscr{D}
. A
morphism

h:(M,\bullet,i)\rightarrow(M^{\prime},\bullet^{\prime},i^{\prime})

of
\mathscr{D}
-monoids is a morphism
h:M\rightarrow M^{\prime}
of
\mathscr{D}
such that
|h|:|M|\rightarrow|M^{\prime}|
is a monoid morphism. We denote by
\mathbf{Mon}(\mathscr{D})

the category of
\mathscr{D}
-monoids and their homomorphisms. In the following we will
freely work with
\mathscr{D}
-monoids in both categorical and algebraic disguise.

Example 2.10
.

(1)

In
\mathbf{Set}
the tensor product is the cartesian product,
I=\{\ast\}
, and
\mathbf{Set}
-monoids are ordinary monoids.

(2)

In
\mathbf{Pos}
the tensor product is the cartesian product,
I=\{\ast\}
, and
\mathbf{Pos}
-monoids are
ordered monoids
, that is, posets equipped with a monoid structure whose multiplication
\bullet
is monotone.

(3)

In
\mathbf{Set}_{\bot}
we have
I=\{\bot,\ast\}
, and the tensor product of pointed sets
(A,\bot_{A})
and
(B,\bot_{B})
is
A\otimes B=(A\setminus\{\bot_{A}\})\times(B\setminus\{\bot_{B}\})+\{\bot\}
.
\mathbf{Set}_{\bot}
-monoids are precisely monoids with zero. Indeed, given a
\mathbf{Set}_{\bot}
-monoid structure on
(A,\bot)
we have
x\bullet\bot=\bot=\bot\bullet x
for all

because
\bullet
is a bimorphism, i.e.
\bot
is a zero element. Morphisms of
\mathbf{Mon}(\mathbf{Set}_{\bot})
are zero-preserving monoid morphisms.

(4)

An
\mathbf{Inv}
-monoid (also called an
involution monoid
) is a monoid equipped with an involution
x\mapsto\widetilde{x}
such that
x\bullet\widetilde{y}=\widetilde{x}\bullet y=\widetilde{x\bullet y}
. For example, for any set
A
the power set
\mathcal{P}A
naturally carries the structure of an involution monoid: the involution takes complements,
\widetilde{S}=A\setminus S
, and the monoid multiplication is the symmetric difference
S\oplus T=(S\setminus T)\cup(T\setminus S)
.

(5)

{\mathbf{JSL}_{0}}
-monoids are precisely idempotent semirings (with
0
and
1
). Indeed, a
{\mathbf{JSL}_{0}}
-monoid on a semilattice (i.e. a commutative idempotent monoid)
(D,+,0)
is given by a unit
1
and a monoid multiplication that, being a bimorphism, distributes over
+
and
0
.

(6)

More generally, a
\mathbf{Mod}(\mathds{S})
-monoid is precisely an associative algebra over
\mathds{S}
: it consists of an
\mathds{S}
-module together with a unit
1
and a monoid multiplication that distributes over
+
and
0
and moreover preserves scalar multiplication in both components.

.

Proposition 2.12

(see Mac Lane
[
14
]
)
.

Let
\mathscr{D}
be a symmetric monoidal closed category with countable coproducts. Then the forgetful functor

has a left adjoint assigning to every object

the free

. The monoid structure

is given by the coproduct injection

and

and

has as its

is the first coproduct injection.

Remark 2.13
.

For any
\mathscr{D}
-monoid
(M,m,i)
and any morphism

\mathscr{D}
-monoid morphism

is defined inductively on the components of the coproduct

Proposition 2.14
.

The free
\mathscr{D}
-monoid on
X=\Psi X_{0}
is
X^{\scriptscriptstyle\oasterisk}=\Psi X_{0}^{*}
. Its monoid multiplication extends the concatenation of words in
X_{0}^{*}
, and its unit is the empty word

Proof.

A constructive proof can be found in
[
1
]
. Here we sketch a more conceptual argument, using the universal property of the tensor product. Observe that the free-algebra functor
\Psi:\mathbf{Set}\rightarrow\mathscr{D}
is strongly monoidal, i.e. it preserves the unit and tensor product up to natural isomorphism. Indeed, we have
\Psi 1=I
by definition. To see that
\Psi(A\times B)\cong\Psi A\otimes\Psi B
for all sets
A
and
B
, consider the following bijections (natural in
D\in\mathscr{D}
):

\displaystyle\mathscr{D}(\Psi(A\times B),D)

\displaystyle\cong\mathbf{Set}(A\times B,|D|)

\displaystyle\cong\mathbf{Set}(A,|D|^{B})

\displaystyle\cong\mathbf{Set}(A,|[\Psi B,D]|)

\displaystyle\cong\mathscr{D}(\Psi A,[\Psi B,D])

\displaystyle\cong\mathscr{D}(\Psi A\otimes\Psi B,D).

This implies
\Psi(A\times B)\cong\Psi A\otimes\Psi B
by the Yoneda lemma. Using the fact that

X^{\scriptscriptstyle\oasterisk}\cong\coprod_{n<\omega}X^{{\otimes n}}\cong\coprod_{n<\omega}\Psi X_{0}^{n}\cong\Psi(\coprod_{n<\omega}X_{0}^{n})=\Psi X_{0}^{*}.

∎

3.
Algebraic Recognition and Syntactic
\mathscr{D}
-Monoids

In classical algebraic automata theory one considers recognition of
languages by (ordinary) monoids in lieu of automata. The key concept
is the
syntactic monoid
which is characterized as the smallest
monoid recognizing a given language. There are also related concepts of
canonical algebraic recognizers in the literature, e.g. the syntactic ordered monoid, the syntactic
idempotent semiring and the syntactic associative algebra. In this section we will give a uniform account of algebraic
language recognition in our categorical setting. Our main result is the
definition and construction of a minimal algebraic
recognizer, the
syntactic
\mathscr{D}
-monoid
of a language.

Assumptions 3.1
.

Throughout this section
(\mathscr{D},\otimes,I)
is an arbitrary symmetric monoidal closed category, and

\mathcal{E}
is a class of epimorphisms in
\mathscr{D}
that contains
all isomorphisms and is closed under composition. In the case where
\mathscr{D}

is a variety of algebras or ordered algebras, we always choose

\mathcal{E}=\text{surjective homomorphisms}.

Definition 3.2
.

A
\mathscr{D}
-monoid morphism

recognizes
the language

with

.

Remark 3.12

(1)

(2)

associative
\mathds{S}
-algebra
of a weighted language
L_{0}:X_{0}^{*}\rightarrow\mathds{S}
is the
quotient of
\mathds{S}[X_{0}]
modulo the congruence defined for
U,V\in\mathds{S}[X_{0}]
as
follows:

U\equiv_{L}V\quad\text{iff}\quad\text{for all $x,y\in X_{0}^{*}$}:L(xUy)=L(xVy)

(3.2)

Indeed, since
L:\mathds{S}[X_{0}]\rightarrow\mathds{S}
is linear, (

) implies

for all

Definition

.

(5)

In particular, for
\mathscr{D}={\mathbf{JSL}_{0}}
with

and

(6)

For

, the

\mathds{K}
-algebra

-weighted language

-algebra of finite weighted languages modulo the ideal

Indeed, the congruence this ideal

)
is precisely (

). Syntactic

.

(7)

Analogously, for

.

3.3.
Syntactic congruences for ordered algebras

Next, we consider the case where
\mathscr{D}
is a variety of ordered algebras.

Remark 3.16
.

(1)

A
congruence
on an ordered
\Sigma
-algebra
(A,\leq)
is a
preorder
\preccurlyeq
on
A
such that
a\leq a^{\prime}
implies
a\preccurlyeq a^{\prime}
, and all
\Sigma
-operations of
A
are monotone w.r.t.

\preccurlyeq
(equivalently,
\preccurlyeq
forms a subalgebra of

A\times A
). Then
\preccurlyeq\cap\succcurlyeq
is a
congruence in the unordered sense, and the quotient algebra
e:A\twoheadrightarrow A/(\preccurlyeq\cap\succcurlyeq)
forms an ordered algebra with partial
order induced by
e
:

e(u)\preccurlyeq^{\prime}e(v)\quad\text{iff}\quad u\preccurlyeq v.

This ordered algebra is called the
quotient
of
A
modulo

\preccurlyeq
, and is denoted by
A/\mathord{\preccurlyeq}
.

(2)

For any
homomorphism
h:A\to B
between ordered
\Sigma
-algebras, the
kernel congruence
of
h
is the congruence on
A
defined by

a\leq_{h}a^{\prime}\quad\text{iff}\quad h(a)\leq h(a^{\prime}).

(3)

The
homomorphism theorem
has the following version for ordered
algebras, see e.g.
[
17
, Proposition 1.1]
: given
homomorphisms of ordered
\Sigma
-algebras
e:A\twoheadrightarrow B
and
h:A\to C
,
where

e

is surjective, there exists a homomorphism
h^{\prime}:B\to C
with
h=h^{\prime}\cdot e
iff,
for all
a,a^{\prime}\in A
,

e(a)\leq e(a^{\prime})\quad\text{implies}\quad h(a)\leq h(a^{\prime}).

Definition 3.17
.

Let
\mathscr{D}
be a variety of ordered algebras. The
syntactic congruence
of
a language
L:X^{\scriptscriptstyle\oasterisk}\to Y
is the relation
\leq_{L}
on
|X^{\scriptscriptstyle\oasterisk}|
defined by

u\leq_{L}v\quad\text{iff}\quad\forall x,y\in|X^{\scriptscriptstyle\oasterisk}|:L(x\bullet u\bullet y)\leq L(x\bullet v\bullet y)

(3.3)

Theorem 3.18
.

\leq_{L}
is a congruence of the free
\mathscr{D}
-monoid
X^{\scriptscriptstyle\oasterisk}
, and

\mathsf{Syn}(L)=X^{\scriptscriptstyle\oasterisk}/\mathord{\leq_{L}}.

Proof.

Clearly
\leq_{L}
is a preorder. Let us verify the conditions of
Remark
3.16
(1). First, if
u\leq v
in
X^{\scriptscriptstyle\oasterisk}
, then
x\bullet u\bullet y\leq x\bullet v\bullet y
for all
x,y\in X^{\scriptscriptstyle\oasterisk}
because
\bullet
is
monotone. Since also
L
is monotone, we conclude
L(x\bullet u\bullet y)\leq L(x\bullet v\bullet y)
in
Y
for all
x,y
, i.e.
u\leq_{L}v
. Next, observe
that
\leq_{L}
is a subobject of
X^{\scriptscriptstyle\oasterisk}\times X^{\scriptscriptstyle\oasterisk}
in
\mathscr{D}
, namely

\mathord{\leq_{L}}=\bigcap\leq_{x,y}
, where for fixed
x,y\in X^{\scriptscriptstyle\oasterisk}
the
object

\leq_{x,y}
is the kernel congruence of the
\mathscr{D}
-morphism

\textstyle{X^{\scriptscriptstyle\oasterisk}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{x\bullet-}
\textstyle{X^{\scriptscriptstyle\oasterisk}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{-\bullet y}
\textstyle{X^{\scriptscriptstyle\oasterisk}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{L}
\textstyle{Y}
. Moreover, the monoid multiplication
\bullet
is
monotone w.r.t.
\leq_{L}
: given
u\leq_{L}v
and
u^{\prime}\leq_{L}v^{\prime}
we have for
all
x,y\in X^{\scriptscriptstyle\oasterisk}
that

L(x\bullet u\bullet u^{\prime}\bullet y)\leq L(x\bullet v\bullet u^{\prime}\bullet y)\leq L(x\bullet v\bullet v^{\prime}\bullet y).

Hence
u\bullet u^{\prime}\leq_{L}v\bullet v^{\prime}
, and
therefore
\leq_{L}
is a
\mathscr{D}
-submonoid of
X^{\scriptscriptstyle\oasterisk}\times X^{\scriptscriptstyle\oasterisk}
, as required.

The proof that
\mathsf{Syn}(L)=X^{\scriptscriptstyle\oasterisk}/\mathord{\leq_{L}}
is completely analogous to
that of Proposition
3.14
: replace equations by inequations,
and use the homomorphism theorem for ordered algebras to construct the
morphisms
f_{L}
and
h
.
∎

Example 3.19
.

In
\mathscr{D}=\mathbf{Pos}
with
Y=\{0<1\}
, the
syntactic ordered
monoid
of a language
L\subseteq X^{*}
is the ordered quotient monoid

X^{*}/\mathord{\leq_{L}}
where for
u,v\in X^{*}
,

u\leq_{L}v\quad\text{iff}\quad\text{for
all
$x,y\in X^{*}$: }xuy\in L\Rightarrow xvy\in L.

This construction is due to Pin
[
16
]
.

4.
Transition
\mathscr{D}
-Monoids

In this section we present another construction of the syntactic
\mathscr{D}
-monoid of a language: it is the transition
\mathscr{D}
-monoid of the minimal
\mathscr{D}
-automaton for this language. We continue to work under the Assumptions
2.7
. Recall from Remark
2.22
the
\mathscr{D}
-monoid
[Q,Q]
and the
\mathscr{D}
-monoid morphism
(\lambda\delta)^{+}:X^{\scriptscriptstyle\oasterisk}\to[Q,Q]
.

Definition 4.1
.

The
transition
\mathscr{D}
-monoid

\mathsf{T}(Q)
of an
F
-algebra
(Q,\delta,i)
is the image of the
\mathscr{D}
-monoid morphism
(\lambda\delta)^{+}:X^{\scriptscriptstyle\oasterisk}\rightarrow[Q,Q]
:

\textstyle{X^{\scriptscriptstyle\oasterisk}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{e_{\mathsf{T}(Q)}}
\scriptstyle{(\lambda\delta)^{+}}
\textstyle{[Q,Q]}
\textstyle{\mathsf{T}(Q)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{m_{\mathsf{T}(Q)}}

Example 4.2
.

(1)

In
\mathbf{Set}
or
\mathbf{Pos}
the
(ordered) transition monoid
of an
F
-algebra
Q
, i.e. an
(ordered) automaton without final states, is the (ordered) monoid of all extended
transition maps
\delta_{w}=\delta_{a_{n}}\cdot\ldots\cdot\delta_{a_{1}}:Q\rightarrow Q
for

w=a_{1}\cdots a_{n}\in X^{*}
. The unit is
\mathsf{id}_{Q}=\delta_{\varepsilon}
and the monoid multiplication is composition.

(2)

In
\mathbf{Set}_{\bot}
with
X=X_{0}+\{\bot\}
(the setting for partial automata) this is completely analogous, except that we add the constant endomap of
Q
with value
\bot
.

(3)

In
\mathbf{Inv}
with
X=X_{0}+\widetilde{X_{0}}
we get the involution monoid of all
\delta_{w}
and
\widetilde{\delta_{w}}
. Again the unit is
\delta_{\varepsilon}
, and the multiplication is determined by composition plus the equations
x\widetilde{y}=\widetilde{xy}=\widetilde{x}y
.

(4)

In
{\mathbf{JSL}_{0}}
with
X=\mathcal{P}_{f}X_{0}
the
transition semiring
consists of all finite joins of extended transitions, i.e. all semilattice homomorphisms of the form

\delta_{w_{1}}\vee\cdots\vee\delta_{w_{n}}
for
\{w_{1},\ldots,w_{n}\}\in\mathcal{P}_{f}X_{0}^{*}
.
The transition semiring was introduced by Polák
[
18
]
.

(5)

In
\mathbf{Mod}(\mathds{S})
with
X=\Psi X_{0}
the associative transition algebra consists of all linear maps of the form

\sum_{i=1}^{n}s_{i}\delta_{w_{i}}
with
s_{i}\in\mathds{S}
and
w_{i}\in X_{0}^{*}
.

Recall from Definition
2.16
that a
\mathscr{D}
-automaton is an
F
-algebra
Q
together with an output morphism
f:Q\to Y
. Hence we can speak of the transition
\mathscr{D}
-monoid of a
\mathscr{D}
-automaton.

Proposition 4.3
.

The language accepted by a
\mathscr{D}
-automaton
(Q,\delta,f,i)
is recognized by the
\mathscr{D}
-monoid morphism
e_{\mathsf{T}(Q)}:X^{\scriptscriptstyle\oasterisk}\twoheadrightarrow\mathsf{T}(Q)
.

Proof.

Let
(Q,\delta,i,f)
be a
\mathscr{D}
-automaton. By definition it accepts the language
L_{Q}=(X^{\scriptscriptstyle\oasterisk}\xrightarrow{e_{Q}}Q\xrightarrow{f}Y)
where
e_{Q}
is the unique
F
-algebra homomorphism. Consider the morphism that evaluates any endomorphism of
Q
at the initial state:

\mathsf{ev}_{i}=([Q,Q]\cong[Q,Q]\otimes I\xrightarrow{[Q,Q]\otimes i}[Q,Q]\otimes Q\xrightarrow{\mathsf{ev}}Q).

Now let

f_{\mathsf{T}(Q)}=(\mathsf{T}(Q)\xrightarrow{m_{\mathsf{T}(Q)}}[Q,Q]\xrightarrow{\mathsf{ev}_{i}}Q\xrightarrow{f}Y).

With this morphism
\mathsf{T}(Q)
recognizes
L
; indeed, using the right unit isomorphism
\iota_{Z}:Z\to Z\otimes I
we compute:

\displaystyle L_{Q}

\displaystyle=f\cdot e_{Q}

\displaystyle=f\cdot\mathsf{ev}\cdot((\lambda\delta)^{+}\otimes i)\cdot\iota_{X^{\scriptscriptstyle\oasterisk}}

(Remark
2.22
)

\displaystyle=f\cdot\mathsf{ev}_{i}\cdot\iota_{[Q,Q]}^{-1}\cdot((\lambda\delta)^{+}\otimes I)\cdot\iota_{X^{\scriptscriptstyle\oasterisk}}

(def. of
\mathsf{ev}_{i}
)

\displaystyle=f\cdot\mathsf{ev}_{i}\cdot(\lambda\delta)^{+}

(naturality of
\iota
)

\displaystyle=f\cdot\mathsf{ev}_{i}\cdot m_{\mathsf{T}(Q)}\cdot e_{\mathsf{T}(Q)}

(Definition
4.1
)

\displaystyle=f_{\mathsf{T}(Q)}\cdot e_{\mathsf{T}(Q)}

(def. of
f_{\mathsf{T}(Q)}
)

This completes the proof.
∎

Definition 4.4
.

A
\mathscr{D}
-automaton
(Q,\delta,i,f)
is called
minimal
iff it is

(a)

reachable
: the unique
F
-algebra homomorphism
X^{\scriptscriptstyle\oasterisk}\rightarrow Q
is surjective;

(b)

simple
: the unique
T
-coalgebra homomorphism
Q\rightarrow[X^{\scriptscriptstyle\oasterisk},Y]
is injective.

Theorem 4.5

(Goguen
[
12
]
)
.

Every language
L:X^{\scriptscriptstyle\oasterisk}\rightarrow Y
is accepted by a minimal
\mathscr{D}
-automaton
\mathsf{Min}(L)
, unique up to isomorphism. Given any reachable automaton
Q
accepting
L
, there is a unique surjective automata homomorphism from
Q
into
\mathsf{Min}(L)
.

This leads to the announced construction of syntactic
\mathscr{D}
-monoids via transition
\mathscr{D}
-monoids. The case
\mathscr{D}=\mathbf{Set}
and
\mathbf{Pos}
are standard results of algebraic automata theory (see e.g. Pin
[
15
]
), and the case
\mathscr{D}={\mathbf{JSL}_{0}}
is due to Polák
[
18
]
. For the other instances in Example
4.2
this appears to be new.

Remark 4.6
.

Recall that in our algebraic setting the tensor unit
I
is
\Psi 1
. Hence
elements
a\in A
of a
\mathscr{D}
-object are in 1-1-correspondence with morphisms

a:I\to A
; we shall henceforth not distinguish these.

Theorem 4.7
.

The syntactic
\mathscr{D}
-monoid of a language
L:X^{\scriptscriptstyle\oasterisk}\rightarrow Y
is isomorphic to the transition
\mathscr{D}
-monoid of its minimal
\mathscr{D}
-automaton:

\mathsf{Syn}(L)\cong\mathsf{T}(\mathsf{Min}(L)).

Proof.

Let
\mathsf{Min}(L)=(Q,\delta,i,f)
, and write
\delta_{x}:Q\rightarrow Q
for
e_{\mathsf{T}(Q)}(x)

(
x\in X^{\scriptscriptstyle\oasterisk}
). Note that
\delta_{x\bullet y}=\delta_{y}\cdot\delta_{x}
for all

x,y\in X^{\scriptscriptstyle\oasterisk}
since
e_{\mathsf{T}(Q)}
is a
\mathscr{D}
-monoid morphism. Recall also that
the unique
F
-algebra homomorphism
e_{Q}:X^{\scriptscriptstyle\oasterisk}\rightarrow Q
assigns to
x\in X^{\scriptscriptstyle\oasterisk}
the
element
\delta_{x}\cdot i:I\rightarrow Q
(see Remark
2.22
(2)), and the
unique
T
-coalgebra homomorphism

m_{Q}:Q\rightarrow[X^{\scriptscriptstyle\oasterisk},Y]
assigns to a state
q:I\rightarrow Q
the language
x\mapsto f\cdot\delta_{x}\cdot q
(see Remark
2.27
). It suffices to show
that the kernel congruence of

e_{\mathsf{T}(Q)}
coincides with the syntactic congruence of
L
. If
\mathscr{D}
is a variety of
algebras, this requires to prove that for all
u,v\in|X^{\scriptscriptstyle\oasterisk}|

one has

\delta_{u}=\delta_{v}\quad\text{iff}\quad\forall x,y\in X^{\scriptscriptstyle\oasterisk}:L(x\bullet u\bullet y)=L(x\bullet v\bullet y).

To see this, we compute

\displaystyle\delta_{u}=\delta_{v}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\forall x:\delta_{u}\cdot e_{Q}(x)=\delta_{v}\cdot e_{Q}(x)

(
e_{Q}
surjective)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\forall x:\delta_{u}\cdot\delta_{x}\cdot i=\delta_{v}\cdot\delta_{x}\cdot i

(def.
e_{Q}
)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\forall x:m_{Q}\cdot\delta_{u}\cdot\delta_{x}\cdot i=m_{Q}\cdot\delta_{v}\cdot\delta_{x}\cdot i

(
m_{Q}
injective)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\forall x,y:f\cdot\delta_{y}\cdot\delta_{u}\cdot\delta_{x}\cdot i=f\cdot\delta_{y}\cdot\delta_{v}\cdot\delta_{x}\cdot i

(def.
m_{Q}
)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\forall x,y:f\cdot\delta_{x\bullet u\bullet y}\cdot i=f\cdot\delta_{x\bullet v\bullet y}\cdot i

(def.
\delta_{(\mathord{-})}
)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\forall x,y:f\cdot e_{Q}(x\bullet u\bullet y)=f\cdot e_{Q}(x\bullet v\bullet y)

(def.
e_{Q}
)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\forall x,y:L(x\bullet u\bullet y)=L(x\bullet v\bullet y)

(
L=L_{Q}
)

The case where
\mathscr{D}
is a variety of ordered algebras is completely analogous:
just replace equations by inequations.
∎

5.
\mathscr{D}
-Regular Languages

Our results so far apply to arbitrary languages in
\mathscr{D}
. In the present section we focus on
regular languages
, which in
\mathscr{D}=\mathbf{Set}
are the languages accepted by finite automata, or equivalently the languages recognized by finite monoids. For arbitrary
\mathscr{D}
the role of finite sets is taken over by finitely presentable objects. Recall that an object
D
of
\mathscr{D}
is
finitely presentable
if the hom-functor
\mathscr{D}(D,\mathord{-}):\mathscr{D}\rightarrow\mathbf{Set}
preserves filtered colimits. Equivalently,
D
can be presented with finitely many generators and relations. For example, the tensor unit
I=\Psi 1
is finitely presentable, since it is presented with one generator and no relations.

Definition 5.1
.

A language
L:X^{\scriptscriptstyle\oasterisk}\rightarrow Y
is called
\mathscr{D}
-regular
if it is accepted by some
\mathscr{D}
-automaton with a finitely presentable object of states.

To work with this definition, we need the following

Assumptions 5.2
.

We assume that the full subcategory
\mathscr{D}_{f}
of finitely presentable objects of
\mathscr{D}
is closed under subobjects, quotients and finite products.

Example 5.3
.

(1)

Recall that a variety is
locally finite
if all finitely presentable algebras (equivalently all finitely generated free algebras) are finite. Every locally finite variety satisfies the above assumptions. This includes our examples
\mathbf{Set}
,
\mathbf{Set}_{\bot}
,
\mathbf{Inv}
and
{\mathbf{JSL}_{0}}
.

(2)

A semiring
\mathds{S}
is called
Noetherian
if all submodules of finitely generated
\mathds{S}
-modules are finitely generated. In this case, as shown in
[
10
]
, the category
\mathbf{Mod}(\mathds{S})
satisfies our assumptions.
Every field is Noetherian, as is every finitely generated commutative ring, so
\mathbf{Vec}(\mathds{K})
and
\mathbf{Ab}=\mathbf{Mod}(\mathds{Z})
are special instances.

Example 5.4
.

As we have mentioned already, for
\mathscr{D}=\mathbf{Set}
the
\mathscr{D}
-regular languages are precisely the classical regular languages. The same is true for
\mathbf{Set}_{\bot}
,
\mathbf{Pos}
,
\mathbf{Inv}
and
{\mathbf{JSL}_{0}}
. For
\mathscr{D}=\mathbf{Mod}(\mathds{S})
for a Noetherian semiring
\mathds{S}
it is known that the
\mathscr{D}
-regular languages are precisely the rational weighted languages, see e.g.
[
10
]
.

Remark 5.5
.

(1)

The functor
FQ=I+X\otimes Q
preserves surjective homomorphisms. Therefore the factorization system of
\mathscr{D}
lifts to the category of
F
-algebras, that is, every
F
-algebra homomorphism factorizes into a surjective homomorphism followed by an injective (resp. order-reflecting) one. By the
reachable part

Q_{r}
of a
\mathscr{D}
-automaton
(Q,\delta,f,i)
we mean the image of the initial
F
-algebra homomorphism, i.e.
\textstyle{e_{Q}=(X^{\scriptscriptstyle\oasterisk}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{e_{r}}
\textstyle{Q_{r}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{m_{r}}
\textstyle{Q).}

Putting
f_{r}:=f\cdot m_{r}:Q_{r}\rightarrow Y
, the
F
-algebra
Q_{r}
becomes an automaton, and
m_{r}
an automata homomorphism.∎

(2)

In the following automata, (co-)algebras and monoids with finitely presentable carrier are referred to as
fp-automata
,
fp-(co-)algebras
and
fp-monoids
, respectively.

Theorem 5.6
.

For any language
L:X^{\scriptscriptstyle\oasterisk}\rightarrow Y
the following statements are equivalent:

(a)

L
is
\mathscr{D}
-regular.

(b)

The minimal
\mathscr{D}
-automaton
\mathsf{Min}(L)
has a finitely presentable carrier.

(c)

L
is recognized by some
\mathscr{D}
-monoid with a finitely presentable carrier.

(d)

The syntactic
\mathscr{D}
-monoid
\mathsf{Syn}(L)
has a finitely presentable carrier.

Proof.

(a)
\Leftrightarrow
(b) follows from Theorem
4.5
and our assumption that
\mathscr{D}_{f}
is closed under subobjects and quotients. Similarly,
(c)\Leftrightarrow(d)
follows from the universal property of the syntactic monoid (see Definition
3.6
) and again closure of
\mathscr{D}_{f}
under subobjects and quotients. (c)
\Rightarrow
(a) is a consequence of Lemma
3.5
. To prove (a)
\Rightarrow
(c), let
Q
be any fp-automaton accepting
L
. Then by Proposition
4.3
the transition monoid
\mathsf{T}(Q)\rightarrowtail[Q,Q]
recognizes
L
, so by closedness of
\mathscr{D}_{f}
under subobjects it suffices to show that
[Q,Q]
is a finitely presentable object of
\mathscr{D}
. Assuming that
Q
has
n
generators as an algebra of
\mathscr{D}
, the map
[Q,Q]\rightarrow Q^{n}
defined by restriction to the set of generators is an injective (resp. order-reflecting) morphism of
\mathscr{D}
. Since
\mathscr{D}_{f}
is closed under subobjects and finite products, it follows that
[Q,Q]
is finitely presentable.
∎

Just as the collection of all languages is internalized by the final coalgebra
[X^{\scriptscriptstyle\oasterisk},Y]
, see Proposition
2.26
, we can internalize the regular languages by means of the
rational coalgebra
.

Definition 5.7
.

The
rational coalgebra

\varrho T
for
T
is the colimit (taken in the category of
T
-coalgebras and homomorphisms) of all
T
-coalgebras with finitely presentable carrier.

Example 5.8
.

In
\mathscr{D}=\mathbf{Set}
, the rational coalgebra is the subcoalgebra of the final coalgebra
\mathcal{P}X_{0}^{*}
given by the set of all regular languages. Analogously for
\mathscr{D}=\mathbf{Pos}
where the order is given by inclusion of languages. In general,
\varrho T
always has the
\mathscr{D}
-regular languages as states:

Proposition 5.9
.

There is a one-to-one correspondence between
\mathscr{D}
-regular languages and elements
I\rightarrow\varrho T
of the rational coalgebra.

Proof.

We describe mutually inverse maps

(I\xrightarrow{x}\varrho T)\mapsto(X^{\scriptscriptstyle\oasterisk}\xrightarrow{L_{x}}Y)\qquad\text{and}\qquad(X^{\scriptscriptstyle\oasterisk}\xrightarrow{L}Y)\mapsto(I\xrightarrow{x_{L}}\varrho T)

between the elements of
\varrho T
and the
\mathscr{D}
-regular languages.
Let
h_{Q}:Q\to\varrho T
be the injections of the colimit
\varrho T
, where
Q=(Q,\delta_{Q},f_{Q})
ranges over all fp-coalgebras. Note that this colimit is filtered because
\mathscr{D}_{f}
is closed under finite colimits in
\mathscr{D}
. Moreover, since colimits of coalgebras are formed in the underlying category, the morphisms
h_{Q}
also form a colimit cocone in
\mathscr{D}
. Given an element
I\xrightarrow{x}\varrho T
of the rational coalgebra we define a
\mathscr{D}

7.
Conclusions and Future Work

We proposed the first steps of a categorical theory of algebraic language
recognition. Despite our assumption that
\mathscr{D}
be a commutative variety, we have seen that a number of our definitions, constructions and proofs work in any symmetric
monoidal closed category with enough structure. The next step should be the treatment
of algebraic recognition beyond languages of finite words, including such
examples as Wilke
algebras
[
22
]
(representing
\omega
-languages) or forest algebras

[
9
]
(representing tree languages). A first categorical approach to such structures appears in the
work of Bojánczyk
[
8
]
who works with monads on sorted sets rather than
monoids in a variety
\mathscr{D}
. We expect that a unification of these two
directions is possible.

One of the leading themes of algebraic automata theory is the classification of languages in terms of their syntactic algebras. For instance, by Schützenberger’s theorem a language is star-free iff its syntactic monoid is aperiodic. We hope that our conceptual view of syntactic monoids (notably their dual characterization in Theorem
6.10
) can contribute to a duality-based approach to such results.
