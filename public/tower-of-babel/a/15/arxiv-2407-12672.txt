A concentration inequality for random combinatorial optimisation problems
Joel Larsson Danielsson

Abstract
Given a finite set S S , i.i.d. random weights { X i } i ∈ S \{X_{i}\}_{i\in S} , and a family of subsets ℱ ⊆ 2 S \mathcal{F}\subseteq 2^{S} , we consider the minimum weight of an F ∈ ℱ F\in\mathcal{F} : M ⁡ ( ℱ ) := min ⁡ ∑ i ∈ F F ∈ ℱ ⁡ X i . M(\mathcal{F}):=\min_{F\in\mathcal{F}}\sum_{i\in F}X_{i}. In particular, we investigate under what conditions this random variable is sharply concentrated around its mean. We define the patchability of a family ℱ \mathcal{F} : essentially, how expensive is it to finish an almost-complete F F (that is, F F is close to ℱ \mathcal{F} in Hamming distance) if the edge weights are re-randomized? Combining the patchability of ℱ \mathcal{F} , applying the Talagrand inequality to a dual problem, and a sprinkling-type argument, we prove a concentration inequality for the random variable M ⁡ ( ℱ ) M(\mathcal{F}) .

Combinatorial minimum weight problems

The class of optimization problems that we are interested in does not necessarily involve graphs, but before giving the general definition we will first discuss them in a graph setting.

Concentration inequalities

In probabilistic combinatorics, one often needs to show that the distribution of some random variable
Z
concentrates around some value
c
: that for any small
\varepsilon>0
,

Z
lies in the interval
[(1-\varepsilon)c,(1+\varepsilon)c]
with high probability.
2
2

2

If such concentration holds, the median has to be close to
c
, and it is usually easy to show that the expected value is also close to
c
.

In particular, it is a common situation that one has a product space
\Omega=\prod_{i\in S}\Omega_{i}
, random variables
X_{i}
on the
\Omega_{i}
’s, and a function
g:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
. One then wants to show concentration of the random variable
Z=g(X_{1},X_{2},\ldots X_{N})
. With some abuse of notation, we can also refer to
Z(\omega)=g(X_{1}(\omega),X_{2}(\omega),\ldots X_{N}(\omega))
by
g(\omega)
.

For many such functions
g
of interest, it turns out that while
g
does depend on all
N

Many concentration inequalities quantify in different ways this intuitive sense of

not depending ‘too much’ on any specific random variable
X_{i}
, or on any small set of these random variables.

The method of bounded differences considers the Lipschitz constant of
g
: How much can
g
change, if only one of its inputs is changed? More precisely, we say that
g
is
K

|g(\omega)-g(\omega^{\prime})|\leq K
whenever
\omega,\omega^{\prime}
differ in only one coordinate. The McDiarmid inequality (based on the Azuma-Hoeffding martingale concentration inequality) bounds the size of the fluctuations around the mean by
O(K\sqrt{N})
.
This Lipschitz condition considers the worst-case change, which might very different from the
typical

|g(\omega)-g(\omega^{\prime})|
could be significantly smaller than
K
for most pairs
\omega,\omega^{\prime}
. (This tends to be the case for the random variable
M(\mathcal{F})
, for families
\mathcal{F}
that are not very small.) In a 2016 paper by Warnke
[
13
]
, several variations on the McDiarmid inequality can be found, involving various

Lipschitz conditions. While these can greatly improve upon the inequalities based on worst-case Lipschitz constants for some functions

Another powerful tool is the Talagrand inequality
[
11

[
2

.
This inequality captures the intuition of a function not depending ‘too much’ on any coordinates in a different way, by considering the ‘certifiability’ of

that one can show to an observer to verify that the event

A major benefit of the Talagrand inequality is that it does not depend on the dimension

Asymptotic notation

In addition to the commonly used asymptotic notation of
O,o,\omega,\Omega
, we will also use
O_{\mathbb{P}},o_{\mathbb{P}},\omega_{\mathbb{P}}
and
\Omega_{\mathbb{P}}
to denote the probabilistic versions:
For sequences
X_{n},Y_{n}
of random variables, we say that
X_{n}=o_{\mathbb{P}}(Y_{n})
and
Y_{n}=\omega_{\mathbb{P}}(X_{n})
if
X_{n}/Y_{n}\to 0
in probability as
n\to\infty
. We say that
X_{n}=O_{\mathbb{P}}(Y_{n})
and
Y_{n}=\Omega_{\mathbb{P}}(X_{n})
if there for any
\varepsilon>0
exists a constant
C>0
such that
X_{n}\leq CY_{n}
with probability at least
1-\varepsilon
for all sufficiently large
n
. Furthermore,
X_{n}=\Theta_{\mathbb{P}}(Y_{n})
iff
X_{n}=O_{\mathbb{P}}(Y_{n})
and
X_{n}=\Omega_{\mathbb{P}}(Y_{n})
.

Finally, we use
f(n)\ll g(n)
to denote
f(n)=o(g(n))
. And unless otherwise specified, the asymptotics will always be implicitly ‘as
n\to\infty
’ (or ‘as
N\to\infty
’).

Results

2.1

Patchability condition

Loosely speaking, our concentration inequality says that if any ‘almost-complete’ member of
\mathcal{F}
(missing on the order of
\sqrt{\ell(\mathcal{F})}
elements) can be completed at cost
o(\mathbb{E}M(\mathcal{F}))
(whp), then the optimal cost
M(\mathcal{F})
has to be sharply concentrated.
Before stating the theorem, we need to make this notion more precise.

As noted earlier, we can assume without loss of generality that
\mathcal{F}
contains only minimal sets.
We will let
\langle\mathcal{F}\rangle
denote the upwards closure of
\mathcal{F}
:
\langle\mathcal{F}\rangle=\{G\subseteq S:\exists F\in\mathcal{F}:F\subseteq G\}
.

Since the weights are non-negative,
M(\mathcal{F})=M(\langle\mathcal{F}\rangle)
.

For any
G,P\subseteq S
, we say that
P
is a
G
-patch
if
G\cup P
contains a member of
\mathcal{F}
, i.e.
G\cup P\in\langle\mathcal{F}\rangle
. Define the function
\rho:2^{S}\mapsto\mathbb{N}
by

\displaystyle\rho(G):=d(G,\langle\mathcal{F}\rangle)=\min\{|P|:P\textrm{ is a $G$-patch}\},

(1)

where
d
denotes Hamming distance:
d(G,F)=|G\Delta F|
and
d(G,\mathcal{F})=\min_{F\in F}|G\Delta F|
.

Let
\mathcal{B}_{r}=\mathcal{B}_{r}(\langle\mathcal{F}\rangle)
be the
r
-neighbourhood of
\langle\mathcal{F}\rangle
in the Hamming metric, i.e. the set of all
G\subseteq S
with
\rho(G)\leq r
.
For any set
G\subseteq S
, let the random variable
\mathrm{Patch}(G)
be the minimum weight of a
G
-patch:

\mathrm{Patch}(G):=\min\{X_{P}:P\textrm{ is a }G\textrm{-patch}\}.

Definition 2.1
:

We say that a
G\subseteq S
is
(\lambda,\varepsilon)
-
patchable
(with respect to the random weights
X_{i}
) if
G
can be patched at cost at most
\lambda
with probability at least
1-\varepsilon
. That is,

\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{Patch}(G)\leq\lambda)\geq 1-\varepsilon.

The family
\mathcal{F}
is said to be
(r,\lambda,\varepsilon)
-
patchable
if every
G\in\mathcal{B}_{r}
(that is,
G\subseteq S
with
\rho(G)\leq r
) is
(\lambda,\varepsilon)
-patchable.

Remark 2.2
:

\mathrm{Patch}(G)
can also be seen as a Hamming distance to
\langle\mathcal{F}\rangle
: if we define the randomly weighted Hamming distance by
D(G,F):=X_{G\Delta F}
, then
\mathrm{Patch}(G)=D(G,\langle\mathcal{F}\rangle)
.
In terms of these Hamming distances,
\mathcal{F}
is
(r,\lambda,\varepsilon)
-patchable if any
G
in the
r
-neighbourhood of
\langle\mathcal{F}\rangle
w.r.t. the metric
d
lies in the
\lambda
-neighbourhood of
\langle\mathcal{F}\rangle
w.r.t. the metric
D
, with probability at least
1-\varepsilon
.

In other words,
\mathcal{F}
is
(r,\lambda,\varepsilon)
-
patchable
if any
F\in\mathcal{F}
from which an arbitrary
r
elements has been removed (giving us a
G
with
\rho(G)=r
), can be patched at cost at most
\lambda
, with probability at least
1-\varepsilon
. Patching
G
will not necessarily restore the
same

F
, as we only require that our ‘patched’ set
G\cup P
contains
some
member of
\mathcal{F}
.
It is important to note here that the set
G
is not random, and in particular it is not chosen in a way that depends on the weights
X_{i}
.

When applying our inequality (theorem
2.5
), the main effort will usually be to show that this patchability condition is met, for some
r,\lambda
and
\varepsilon
.
Note that if
X_{i}\leq 1
with probability
1
, then any family
\mathcal{F}
is trivially
(r,r,0)
-patchable for any
r
– simply put back the
r
elements that were removed, at cost at most
r
. However, it is only for families that are
(r,\lambda,\varepsilon)
-patchable for some
\lambda\ll r
that our inequality (theorem
2.5
) improves upon a ‘standard’ application of the Talagrand inequality.

2.2

Probability distributions of the weights

Most commonly, the edge weights
X_{i}
are exponential or uniform
U(0,1)
. But the proof of our inequality can easily be generalised to a larger class of edge weight distributions, such as the positive powers of such random variables. The only assumption we will make on the distribution of
X_{i}
is the following.

Assumption 2.3
:

For
q>0
we say that the distribution of a random variable
X
satisfies assumption
A(q)
if the following holds:
For any
s\in(0,1)
,
X
can be coupled to two copies
Y\overset{d}{=}Y^{\prime}\overset{d}{=}X
following the same distribution, such that
Y
and
Y^{\prime}
are independent and surely

X\leq\min\left(\frac{Y}{(1-s)^{1/q}},\frac{Y^{\prime}}{s^{1/q}}\right).

(2)

For instance, the
(1/q)
:th power of exponential or uniform random variables satisfies
A(q)
. The assumption is also closely related to the so-called
pseudo-dimension
of a random variable. We will discuss this in section
5
.

Remark 2.4
:

Note also
that
eq.

2
can be iterated, so that for any
\{s_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
with
\sum_{i=1}^{k}s_{i}=1
we have that i.i.d. random variables
Y^{(i)}\overset{d}{=}X
can be coupled to
X
in such a way that
X\leq\min_{i\in[k]}\left({Y^{(i)}}/{s_{i}^{1/q}}\right).

In particular, for
s_{i}=k^{-1}
,
X\leq k^{1/q}\min_{i\in[k]}\left({Y^{(i)}}\right).

2.3

A ‘patchability’ concentration inequality

We are now ready to state our concentration inequality.

Theorem 2.5
:

Assume the distribution of the
X_{i}
’s satisfies
A(q)
in assumption (
2.3
).
Given
\varepsilon>0
, let
L
be such that
\mathbb{P}(M(\mathcal{F})>L)\geq\varepsilon
.
If
\mathcal{F}
is
(r,\lambda,\varepsilon)
-patchable for some
r\geq\sqrt{8\log(\varepsilon^{-1})\cdot\ell(\mathcal{F})}
and
\lambda>0
, then with probability at least
1-2\varepsilon
,

\displaystyle M(\mathcal{F})

\displaystyle\leq\left(L^{\frac{q}{q+1}}+\lambda^{\frac{q}{q+1}}\right)^{\frac{q+1}{q}}.

(3)

In particular, if
m
is the median of
M(\mathcal{F})
,
\varepsilon<1/4
, and
\lambda\leq m
, then for some constant
C=C(q)
,
with probability at least
1-3\varepsilon
,

\displaystyle\frac{|M(\mathcal{F})-m|}{m}

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\frac{\lambda}{m}\right)^{\frac{q}{q+1}}.

(4)

The theorem gives sharp concentration if
\lambda=o(m)
as
N\to\infty
.

Note also that
r
is an increasing function of
\ell(\mathcal{F})
, the largest size of a member of
\mathcal{F}
, and
\lambda
in turn is a non-decreasing function of
r
. One therefore want to make
\ell(\mathcal{F})
as small as possible.

A less general version of theorem
2.5
, appeared in a previous paper
[
3
]
by the author and L. Federico. There we studied a specific family
\mathcal{F}
(the family of
H
-factors in
K_{n}
, for some small graph
H
), for which a property very similar to patchability holds trivially. We discuss this family in section
3.2
.

Remark 2.6
:

Some families
\mathcal{F}
have a large variation in the sizes of its members, and while smaller sets
F
are more likely to have low weight
X_{F}
, there might be a much larger number of bigger sets in
\mathcal{F}
. If the
F
achieving the optimal weight
M(\mathcal{F})
tends to be a small set, it can sometimes be helpful to sort the members of
\mathcal{F}
into two families:
\mathcal{F}_{small}
with small sets and
\mathcal{F}_{big}
with big sets.
If one can (with high probability) upper bound
M(\mathcal{F}_{small})<t
and lower bound
M(\mathcal{F}_{big})>t
with the same
t=t(n)
(for instance by the methods discussed in sections
6
and
7
), then with high probability
M(\mathcal{F})=M(\mathcal{F}_{small})
and it suffices to show concentration of
M(\mathcal{F}_{small})
.

2.4

Proof strategy for theorem
2.5

This proof will follow a similar strategy to that in
[
3
]
.
The main novel ideas (here and in
[
3
]
) are (i) the patchability condition, and (ii) to apply the Talagrand inequality to a dual problem:
Setting a ‘budget’
L>0
, how close (in Hamming distance) to a member of
\mathcal{F}
can we get while staying within budget?
More precisely, we define

Z_{L}:=\min\{\rho(G):G\subseteq S\textrm{ and }X_{G}\leq L\}.

Talagrand’s concentration inequality is much better suited to this random variable, and we use it to show that
Z_{L}
is typically ‘small’ (roughly of order
\sqrt{\ell(\mathcal{F})}
) for a suitable
L
.
That is, that there exists a
G\subseteq S
with weight
X_{G}\leq L
, and which can be turned into a member of
\mathcal{F}
by adding at most a small number of elements from
S
.

For the next step, given such a
G
,
we would like to find a cheap ‘patch’
P\subseteq S
such that
G\cup P\in\mathcal{F}
. However, here we run in to an obstacle:
G
is now a random set, and the weights of the elements not in
G
will not be independent from
G
, because
G
was chosen in a way that depends on the weights
X_{i}
.

To get around this obstacle, we perform a trick originally due to Walkup
[
12
]
, which we call the
red-green split
.
Split each element
x\in S
into two, a green and a red copy.
For some small
s>0
, give these independent random weights
{Y_{i}}/({1-s})^{1/q},{Y_{i}^{\prime}}/{s}^{1/q}
, where
Y_{i}
and
Y_{i}^{\prime}
follow the same distribution as
X_{i}
, and couple them to
X_{i}
as in assumption
A(q)
.
With this coupling, the green weights
Y_{i}
are typically close to
X_{i}
, while the red weights
Y^{\prime}_{i}
tend to be larger. Crucially, the red weights are independent from the green weights.

We then study the dual problem on the green weights, and use Talagrand’s inequality as described above to show that there probably exists a green
G\subseteq S
with
\rho(G)\leq r
and
Y_{G}\leq L
, with
r
of order roughly
\sqrt{\ell(\mathcal{F})}
.
Next we use that
\mathcal{F}
is
(r,\lambda,\varepsilon)
-patchable to find a red
G
-patch
P
with
Y^{\prime}_{P}\leq\lambda
. Since
G\cup P\in\langle\mathcal{F}\rangle
, we have that

M(\mathcal{F})\leq X_{G}+X_{P}\leq\frac{Y_{G}}{(1-s)^{1/q}}+\frac{Y_{P}^{\prime}}{s^{1/q}}.

(5)

Using that
Y_{G}\leq L
and
Y^{\prime}_{P}\leq\lambda
(with high probability), and optimizing over
s
gives us the upper bound (
3
) in theorem
2.5
.

Theorem 3.1
:

Let
\mathcal{T}

Then there exists a constant

L:=c_{q}n^{1-1/q}

\frac{|M(\mathcal{T})-L|}{L}=O_{\mathbb{P}}\Big(n^{-\frac{q}{2(q+1)}}\Big)=o_{\mathbb{P}}(1).

In particular, for
q=1
, it is known that
\mathbb{E}[M(\mathcal{T})]\to\zeta(3)\approx 1.202
, and
\Var[M(\mathcal{T})]=\Theta(1/n)
, so that the fluctuations of
M(\mathcal{T})
around its expected value is of order

. Our theorem gives a weaker upper bound, of order
n^{-1/4}
.

For
q\geq 1
the theorem follows from
[
6

q<1
.

Proof.
In
[
5
]
it is shown that
\mathbb{E}[M(\mathcal{T})]\to\zeta(3)
when
q=1
. Assuming that the edge weights are such that
X_{i}^{q}\sim U(0,1)
, it is easy to adapt this argument to show that
\mathbb{E}[M(\mathcal{T})]/n^{1-1/q}
converges to a constant as
n\to\infty
.

We will prove that
\patch(G)=O_{\mathbb{P}}(rn^{-1/q})
for any
G
with
\rho(G)=r
. The theorem then follows by plugging this into theorem
2.5
, by noting that (i) since a spanning tree has
n-1
edges,
r=\Theta(\sqrt{n})
(for
\varepsilon

M(\mathcal{T})
around its median implies that the expected value is close to the median.

Pick any
G
with
\rho(G)=r=\Theta(\sqrt{n})
.
A graph with
\rho(G)=r
has
r+1
connected components, as
r
edges must be added to it in order to connect it.
Let
C_{1},\ldots,C_{r+1}
be these connected components, sorted in increasing order by their number of vertices, and with ties broken arbitrarily.
For each edge in
E(K_{n})-E(G)
that goes between two components, orient it according to the order of the components above: from
C_{i}
to
C_{j}
when
i<j
.
We will find a
G
-patch

by, for each
1\leq i\leq r
, picking the cheapest outgoing edge from
C_{i}
. Note that such a
P
is indeed a
G
-patch, because
\langle\mathcal{T}\rangle
is the family of connected graphs, and there is a path in
G\cup P
from any
C_{i}
(
i\leq r
) to
C_{r+1}
.

Claim 1
:

For
1\leq i\leq r
,
C_{i}
has at least
s:=\min(n/2,n^{2}/4r^{2})=\Theta(n)
outgoing edges.

Proof.
Consider a component
C_{i}
with
k
vertices. If
k\leq n/2r
, then
C_{1},\ldots,C_{i}

Given a fixed graph

In the random assignment problem,

This problem has been studied when the edge weights satisfy condition

Other weight distributions

Assumption

is known to be
\mu(A)=\frac{\Gamma(1+q)^{m}}{\Gamma(1+qm)}L^{qm}
(see
[
7
]
for a concise derivation).
The asymptotic expression comes from noting that the numerator is
2^{O(m)}
, and using Stirling’s approximation on the denominator.
∎

From the claim we have that
\mathbb{P}(X_{F}\leq L)\leq 2^{O(m)}(L/m)^{qm}
(for
F
with
|F|=m
) and hence
\mathbb{E}[R_{m}]\leq(c_{0}L/m^{1-\beta/q})^{qm}
for some
c_{0}
.
If we pick
L:=c_{1}c_{0}^{-1}\cdot\min(\ell_{0}^{1-\beta/q},\ell_{1}^{1-\beta/q})
for some
c_{1}>0
, we get that
c_{0}L/m^{1-\beta/q}\leq c_{1}
for all
m
, and hence
\mathbb{E}[R_{m}]\leq c_{1}^{qm}
.
Thus
\mathbb{E}[X]=\sum_{m=\ell_{0}}^{\ell_{1}}\mathbb{E}[R_{m}]<c_{1}^{q\ell_{0}}/(1-c_{1}^{q})
, which is less than
\exp(-\lambda\ell_{0})
if
c_{1}
is sufficiently small.
∎

7

Upper tail bound

To apply Theorem
2.5
it is sometimes helpful to first have rougher bounds on the tails of
M(\mathcal{F})
. Here we provide a simple bound on the upper tail.

Proposition 7.1
:

If
\mu
is the median of
M(\mathcal{F})
, then for any
t\geq 0
,

\mathbb{P}(M(\mathcal{F})>t\mu)\leq 2^{1-t^{q}}.

(11)

Furthermore,
\mathbb{E}M(\mathcal{F})\leq C_{q}\mu
for some constant
C_{q}
only depending on
q
, and thus
eq.

11
holds with
\mu
replaced with
\mathbb{E}M(\mathcal{F})
and the
2
in the right-hand side replaced with some
r>1
.

Proof.
Let
k:=\lfloor t^{q}\rfloor
, and assume WLOG that
k\geq 2
: If
t^{q}\leq 1
, then
2^{1-t^{q}}\geq 1
and there is nothing to prove, and if
1<t^{q}<2
, then
t\mu>\mu
and hence
\mathbb{P}(M(\mathcal{F})>t\mu)\leq 1/2<2^{1-t^{q}}
. For each
i\in S
, and
j\in[k]
, let
X_{i}^{(j)}
be i.i.d random variables following the same distribution as
X_{i}
.
As noted in remark
2.4
, we can couple these random variables
X_{i}^{(j)}
to
X_{i}
such that surely

X_{i}\leq k^{1/q}\cdot\min_{j\in[k]}(X_{i}^{(j)}).

Let
M^{(j)}
be defined as
M(\mathcal{F})
but with edge weights
X_{i}^{(j)}
. Now, if
M^{(j)}=x
for some
j,x
, by definition there exists an
F\in\mathcal{F}
with
X_{F}^{(j)}=x
, and this
F
then has weight
X_{F}=\sum_{i\in F}X_{i}\leq\sum_{i\in F}k\cdot X_{i}^{(j)}=kx
. This implies that surely

M(\mathcal{F})\leq X_{F}\leq k^{1/q}\cdot\min_{j\in[k]}(M^{(j)}),

and
k^{1/q}\leq t
by the choice of
k
.
Noting that
\mathbb{P}(\min_{j\in[k]}(M^{(j)})>\mu)\leq 2^{-k}\leq 2^{1-t^{q}}
, the first part of the claim follows.

For the ‘furthermore’ part, we will use
\mathbb{E}M(\mathcal{F})=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(M(\mathcal{F})>x)dx
. From the first part the integrand is at most
2^{1-(x/\mu)^{q}}
. After a change of variables
z=\ln(2)(x/\mu)^{q}
, its integral becomes
\int_{0}^{\infty}2^{1-(x/\mu)^{q}}dx={2}{\ln(2)^{-1/q}}\Gamma(1+1/q)\cdot\mu
.
∎
