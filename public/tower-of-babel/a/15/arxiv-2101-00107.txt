Abstract
. A central question in random matrix theory is universality. When an emergent phenomena is observed from a large collection of chosen random variables it is natural to ask if this behavior is specific to the chosen random variable or if the behavior occurs for a larger class of random variables. The rank statistics of random matrices chosen uniformly from Mat ⁡ ( 𝐅 q ) \operatorname{Mat}(\mathbf{F}_{q}) over a finite field are well understood. The universality properties of these statistics are not yet fully understood however. Recently Wood [ 39 ] and Maples [ 26 ] considered a natural requirement where the random variables are not allowed to be too close to constant and they showed that the rank statistics match with the uniform model up to an error of type e − c ​ n e^{-cn} . In this paper we explore a condition called near uniform, under which we are able to prove tighter bounds q − c ​ n q^{-cn} on the asymptotic convergence of the rank statistics. Our method is completely elementary, and allows for a small number of the entries to be deterministic, and for the entries to not be identically distributed so long as they are independent. More importantly, the method also extends to near uniform symmetric, alternating matrices. Our method also applies to two models of perturbations of random matrices sampled uniformly over GL n ⁡ ( 𝐅 q ) \operatorname{GL}_{n}(\mathbf{F}_{q}) : subtracting the identity or taking a minor of a uniformly sampled invertible matrix.

Introduction

A central question in random matrix theory is that of universality. If instead of taking a Haar-uniform random matrix, we sample by some other methods, under what conditions will we observe similar statistics in some large scale matrix phenomena? While there have been many results addressing universality of random matrices in characteristic zero to study the spectral behavior of various models of random matrices, we have not seen much in the literature addressing universality behavior in the finite fields setting. 1.12
)
Let
M_{0}
be the upper left
m_{0}\times m_{0}
corner of our near uniform random matrix
M_{n}
. By our rank relations
3.11
and
3.12
at each of the remaining
n-m_{0}
steps the evolution of the uniform and near uniform models differ by an error of at most
(C^{\prime}/q)^{cm_{0}}
. Therefore summing over all possible indices of rank drops we have,

\displaystyle\|Q(M_{n}(M_{0}))-Q(M_{n})\|_{TV}\leq 2^{n}(\frac{C^{\prime}}{q})^{cm_{0}}.

By the previous Corollary we have,

\displaystyle\|Q(M_{n}(M_{0})-Q_{\bullet}\|_{TV}\leq\frac{3^{n/2}}{q^{n/2-m_{0}}}.

Therefore by the triangle inequality we have,

\displaystyle\|Q(M_{n})-Q_{\bullet}\|_{TV}\leq 2^{n}(\frac{C^{\prime}}{q})^{cm_{0}}+\frac{3^{n/2}}{q^{n/2-m_{0}}}.

∎

4.
Proof of Theorem
1.15
and Theorem
1.16

Let
A_{n}\in\operatorname{GL}_{n}({\mathbf{F}}_{q})
be chosen uniformly. Let
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
denote the columns of
A_{n}
. We start with two simple results which will be important for both theorems: a uniform element of
\operatorname{GL}_{n}({\mathbf{F}}_{q})
can be sampled using the column exposure process and a bound on the probability a rectangular submatrix of
A_{n}
is full rank.

Claim 4.1
.

Sample vectors
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
one at a time as follows: for
0\leq k\leq n-1
sample
X_{k+1}
uniformly from
{\mathbf{F}}_{q}^{n}\backslash\langle X_{1},\dots,X_{k}\rangle
. Let
A
be the matrix with
X_{i}
as columns. Then for each
B\in\operatorname{GL}_{n}({\mathbf{F}}_{q})

{\mathbf{P}}(A=B)=\frac{1}{|\operatorname{GL}_{n}({\mathbf{F}}_{q})|}.

Proof.

Let
{\mathbf{c}}_{i}
be the
i
-th column vector of
B
. By definition
{\mathbf{P}}(X_{k+1}={\mathbf{c}}_{k+1}(B)|X_{1}={\mathbf{c}}_{1}(B),\dots,X_{k}={\mathbf{c}}_{k}(B))=\frac{1}{q^{n}-q^{k}}
, and so
{\mathbf{P}}(A=B)=\prod_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{1}{q^{n}-q^{k}}.

∎

Lemma 4.2
.

For
1\leq k\leq l\leq n
, let
X_{1}^{\prime},\dots,X_{k}^{\prime}
be the vectors obtained from
X_{1},\dots,X_{k}
by restricting to the first (or last)
l
coordinates. We have

{\mathbf{P}}(X_{1}^{\prime},\dots,X_{k}^{\prime}\mbox{ are linearly independent})\geq 1-2/q^{l-k}.

By symmetry for
X_{1}^{\prime\prime},\dots,X_{l}^{\prime\prime}
the restrictions of
X_{1},\dots X_{l}
to the first (or last)
k
coordinates we have,

{\mathbf{P}}(X_{1}^{\prime\prime},\dots,X_{l}^{\prime\prime}\mbox{ span ${\mathbf{F}}_{q}^{k}$})\geq 1-2/q^{l-k}.

Proof.

We show that the event that the
X_{i}^{\prime}
are dependent is bounded by
2/q^{l-k}
. If the
X_{i}^{\prime}
are dependent there exists
t<k
such that
X^{\prime}_{1},\dots,X^{\prime}_{t}
are linearly independent and
X^{\prime}_{t+1}\in\langle X^{\prime}_{1},\dots,X^{\prime}_{t}\rangle
.

We have that
X_{t+1}^{\prime}\in\langle X_{1}^{\prime},\dots,X_{t}^{\prime}\rangle
if
X^{\prime}_{t+1}
belongs to the set
S_{t}
, where
S_{t}
is the set whose projection onto the first
l
coordinates is a subset of
\langle X_{1}^{\prime},\dots,X_{t}^{\prime}\rangle
. If
X_{1}^{\prime},\dots X_{t}^{\prime}
are linearly independent then
S_{t}
has size
q^{t}\times q^{n-l}=q^{n+t-l}
. Therefore summing over the indices
t<k
we obtain,

\displaystyle{\mathbf{P}}(X_{1}^{\prime},\dots,X_{k}^{\prime}\mbox{ are linearly dependent})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{S_{0}}{|\overline{W}_{0}|}+\dots+\frac{S_{k-1}}{|\overline{W}_{k-1}|}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{q^{n-l}}{q^{n}-1}+\dots+\frac{q^{n+(k-1)-l}}{q^{n}-q^{k-1}}

\displaystyle\leq 2(q^{-l}+\dots+q^{k-l-1})

\displaystyle\leq 2q^{k-l}.

For the symmetric statement note that
X_{1}^{\prime\prime},\dots,X_{l}^{\prime\prime}
spanning
{\mathbf{F}}_{q}^{k}
is equivalent to the first
k
rows’ restriction to the first
l
coordinates being linearly independent.
∎

4.3.
Proof of Theorem
1.15

Let
M_{n}=A_{n}-I
. The method is very similar to the one in Section
2
. Claim
4.1
justifies our use of a column exposure process. From Lemma
4.2
it follows
X_{1}-{\mathbf{e}}_{1},\dots,X_{k}-{\mathbf{e}}_{k}
are linearly independent for
k<(1/2-c)n
with probability
1-q^{-cn}
. By Proposition
4.4
the normal vectors to
W_{k}
are likely unstructured. The proof concludes similarly to the proof of Theorem
1.4
.

From Lemma
4.2
it follows that
W_{k}(M_{n})
has dimension
k
with probability at least
1-q^{-cn}
for
k\leq 1/2(1-c)n
so we may start analyzing the rank evolution process there. For
k\geq(1/2+\varepsilon)n
, let
{\mathcal{F}}_{GL,k}
be the event that
X_{1}^{\prime},\dots,X_{k}^{\prime}
span
{\mathbf{F}}_{q}^{n-k}
where
X_{1}^{\prime},\dots,X_{k}^{\prime}
are the restrictions of
X_{1},\dots,X_{k}^{\prime}
to the last
n-k
coordinates. For other
k
let
{\mathcal{F}}_{GL,k}
be empty. By Lemma
4.2
,
{\mathbf{P}}({\mathcal{F}}_{GL,k})>1-q^{n-2k}
. We now study how the rank evolves after exposing
X=X_{k+1}
.

Proposition 4.4
.

Let
k\geq(1/2-\varepsilon)n
. For any nonzero normal vector
{\mathbf{w}}
of
\langle X_{1}-{\mathbf{e}}_{1},\dots,X_{k}-{\mathbf{e}}_{k}\rangle
we have

\Big|{\mathbf{P}}((X_{k+1}-{\mathbf{e}}_{k+1})\cdot{\mathbf{w}}=0|{\mathcal{F}}_{GL,k})-1/q\Big|\leq q^{(-1/2+\varepsilon)n}.

Proof.

Let
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n-k}
be an orthonormal basis for
\overline{W_{k}}
. We can view
X_{k+1}
as a random vector
\sum_{i=1}^{k}\beta_{i}X_{i}+\sum_{i=1}^{n-k}\alpha_{i}Y_{i}
where
(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n-k},\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{k})
is chosen uniformly from the set of vectors where at least one of
\alpha_{i}
is nonzero. In particular each
\beta_{i}
is independent of
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n-k},\beta_{1},\dots,\hat{\beta_{i}},\dots,\beta_{k}
.

Case 1:

k\geq(1/2+\varepsilon)n
.

Because we conditioned on
X_{1}^{\prime},\dots,X_{k}^{\prime}
spanning
{\mathbf{F}}_{q}^{n-k}
,
{\mathbf{w}}
is not orthogonal to one of
{\mathbf{e}}_{1},\dots,{\mathbf{e}}_{k}
. Without loss of generality assume that
{\mathbf{w}}
is not orthogonal to
{\mathbf{e}}_{1}
. Now as
{\mathbf{w}}
is orthogonal to
X_{1}-{\mathbf{e}}_{1},\dots,X_{k}-{\mathbf{e}}_{k}
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbf{P}}_{\beta_{1}}((X_{k+1}-{\mathbf{e}}_{k+1})\cdot{\mathbf{w}}=0)

\displaystyle={\mathbf{P}}_{\beta_{1}}\Big((\sum_{i=1}^{k}\beta_{i}X_{i}+\sum_{i=1}^{n-k}\alpha_{i}Y_{i}-{\mathbf{e}}_{k+1})\cdot{\mathbf{w}}=0\Big)

\displaystyle={\mathbf{P}}_{\beta_{1}}\Big(\beta_{1}X_{1}\cdot{\mathbf{w}}=a\Big)

\displaystyle={\mathbf{P}}_{\beta_{1}}(\beta_{1}{\mathbf{e}}_{1}\cdot{\mathbf{w}}=a)

\displaystyle=1/q.

Note
a=-\sum_{i=2}^{k}\beta_{i}X_{i}-\sum_{i=1}^{n-k}+{\mathbf{e}}_{k+1}
is independent of
\beta_{1}
and
{\mathbf{e}}_{1}\cdot{\mathbf{w}}=X_{1}\cdot{\mathbf{w}}\neq 0
.

Case 2:

(1/2-\varepsilon)n\leq k\leq(1/2+\varepsilon)n
.

If
{\mathbf{w}}
is not orthogonal to some
{\mathbf{e}}_{i},1\leq i\leq k
we can use the argument from Case 1. So assume
{\mathbf{e}}_{i}\cdot{\mathbf{w}}=0
for
i\leq k
. Because
{\mathbf{w}}\cdot(X_{i}-{\mathbf{e}}_{i})=0
for
i\leq k
this also implies
X_{i}\cdot{\mathbf{w}}=0
for
i\leq k
. Write
{\mathbf{w}}=(w_{1},\dots,w_{n})=(0,\dots,0,w_{k+1},\dots,w_{n})
with respect to the basis
\{X_{1},\dots,X_{k},Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n-k}\}
. The tuple
(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n-k})
is chosen uniformly from
{\mathbf{F}}_{q}^{n-k}\backslash\{\mathbf{0}\}
, and
(w_{k+1},\dots,w_{n})
is a fixed vector in
{\mathbf{F}}_{q}^{n-k}\backslash\{\mathbf{0}\}
. If
X
was chosen uniformly from
{\mathbf{F}}_{q}^{n-k}
then
{\mathbf{P}}((X-{\mathbf{e}}_{k+1})\cdot{\mathbf{w}}=0)=1/q
. Accounting for whether
X=0
would or would not have given an equality we get,

\displaystyle\Big|{\mathbf{P}}\Big((X-{\mathbf{e}}_{k+1})\cdot{\mathbf{w}}=0\Big)-1/q\Big|

\displaystyle\leq\max\Big\{\frac{q^{n-k-1}}{q^{n-k}-1}-1/q,1/q-\frac{q^{n-k-1}-1}{q^{n-k}-1}\Big\}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{q^{n-k-1}}.

∎

Proof.

(of Theorem
1.15
) Combining Proposition
4.4
and Lemma
2.11
we then obtain that for any
k\geq(1/2-\varepsilon)n
, with
X_{k+1}
being chosen uniformly from
{\mathbf{F}}_{q}^{n}\backslash W_{k}(A_{n})
we have

\Big|{\mathbf{P}}_{X_{k+1}}(X_{k+1}-{\mathbf{e}}_{k+1}\in W_{k}(M_{n})|\operatorname{codim}(W_{k}(M_{n}))=l)-\frac{1}{q^{l}}\Big|\leq q^{(-1/2+\varepsilon)n}.

Using this result together with the fact that
\operatorname{codim}(W_{\lfloor(1/2-\varepsilon)n\rfloor}(M_{k}))=0
with probability
1-p^{-\varepsilon n}
, we can complete the proof of Theorem
1.15
the same way we concluded Theorem
1.4
. Namely there are
2^{n}
ways the rank could evolve during the column exposure process. Choosing
\varepsilon=1/4
each occurs with the probability one would predict from the uniform model except for an error bounded by,

\displaystyle 2q^{-n/2}+(1-1/4)nq^{-n/4}.

Where the errors come from the probability
W_{\lfloor n/4\rfloor}(M_{n})
is not full rank and the error we accumulate in the remaining
\lfloor(1-1/4)n\rfloor
steps. So for
q>2^{4}
we can sum our errors and get our desired bound.
∎

4.5.
Proof of Theorem
1.16

In this subsection, let
M_{n^{\prime}}
denote the upper left
n^{\prime}\times n^{\prime}
corner of
A_{n}
where
n^{\prime}\leq(1-\varepsilon)n
and let
X_{1}^{\prime},\dots,X_{n^{\prime}}^{\prime}
denote its columns.

Just as in the independent and perturbed models the goal is to apply Proposition
2.11
to determine the transition probabilities. With that in mind we show the following.

Proposition 4.6
.

For all nonzero normal vectors
{\mathbf{w}}
to
W_{k}(M_{n^{\prime}})
we have

|{\mathbf{P}}({\mathbf{w}}\cdot X_{k+1}^{\prime}=0)-1/q|<\frac{1}{q^{n-k-1}}.

Proof.

Let
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n-k}
be a basis for
W_{k}(A_{n})
. As in Proposition
4.4

X_{k+1}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\beta_{i}X_{i}+\sum_{i=1}^{n-k}\alpha_{i}Y_{i}
where
(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n-k},\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{k})
is chosen uniformly from the set of vectors where at least one of
\alpha_{i}
is nonzero. Now consider the restriction of this equation to the first
n^{\prime}
entries,

\displaystyle X_{k+1}^{\prime}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\beta_{i}X_{i}^{\prime}+\sum_{i=1}^{n-k}\alpha_{i}Y_{i}^{\prime}.

Taking the dot product with
{\mathbf{w}}
we get,

\displaystyle{\mathbf{w}}\cdot X_{k+1}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\beta_{i}X_{i}^{\prime}\cdot{\mathbf{w}}+\sum_{i=1}^{n-k}\alpha_{i}Y_{i}^{\prime}\cdot{\mathbf{w}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n-k}\alpha_{i}Y_{i}^{\prime}\cdot{\mathbf{w}}

At least one
Y_{i}
must have
Y_{i}\cdot{\mathbf{w}}\neq 0
. Therefore as in Proposition
4.4
we get,

\displaystyle\Big|{\mathbf{P}}\Big((X_{k+1}^{\prime}\cdot{\mathbf{w}}=0\Big)-1/q\Big|

\displaystyle\leq\max\Big\{\frac{q^{n-k-1}}{q^{n-k}-1}-1/q,1/q-\frac{q^{n-k-1}-1}{q^{n-k}-1}\Big\}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{q^{n-k-1}}.

∎

Proof.

(of Theorem
1.16
)
Combining Proposition
4.6
and Proposition
2.11
we obtain,

\displaystyle|{\mathbf{P}}(X_{k+1}^{\prime}\in W_{k}(M_{n^{\prime}})|\operatorname{codim}(W_{k}(M_{n^{\prime}}))=d)-1/p^{d}|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{q^{n-k-1}}

\displaystyle\leq q^{-\varepsilon n}.

As a consequence, by directly comparing with the rank evolution of the uniform model over size
n^{\prime}
(via (
2
)) and by taking union bound, we obtain a total variation distance of
2^{n^{\prime}}q^{-\varepsilon n}
for the rank distribution of this model and the uniform
n^{\prime}\times n^{\prime}
model. This bound can be improved if
n^{\prime}
is large, for instance when
n^{\prime}\geq\varepsilon n
. Indeed, in this case, by Lemma
4.2

W_{n^{\prime}-\lfloor\varepsilon n\rfloor}(M_{n^{\prime}})
has full dimension
n^{\prime}-\lfloor\varepsilon n\rfloor
with probability at least
1-2q^{-\lfloor\varepsilon n\rfloor}
. Starting at this step leaves
2^{\lfloor\varepsilon n\rfloor}
ways for the rank to evolve. As such we obtain a total variation distance of
2q^{-\lfloor\varepsilon n\rfloor}+2^{\lfloor\varepsilon n\rfloor}q^{-\varepsilon n}
between the rank distribution of this model and the uniform
n^{\prime}\times n^{\prime}
model. Finally, using Fulman and Goldstein’s result we add another term of
\frac{3}{q^{n^{\prime}}}
for the total variation distance to the limiting distribution
Q_{\infty}
.
∎
