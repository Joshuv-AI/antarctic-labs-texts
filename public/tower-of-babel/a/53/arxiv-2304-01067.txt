A nodally bound-preserving finite element method
Gabriel R. Barrenechea, Emmanuil Georgoulis, Tristan Pryer, Andreas Veeser

Abstract
. This work proposes a nonlinear finite element method whose nodal values preserve bounds known for the exact solution. The discrete problem involves a nonlinear projection operator mapping arbitrary nodal values into bound-preserving ones and seeks the numerical solution in the range of this projection. As the projection is not injective, a stabilisation based upon the complementary projection is added in order to restore well-posedness. Within the framework of elliptic problems, the discrete problem may be viewed as a reformulation of a discrete obstacle problem, incorporating the inequality constraints through Lipschitz projections. The derivation of the proposed method is exemplified for linear and nonlinear reaction-diffusion problems. Near-best approximation results in suitable norms are established. In particular, we prove that, in the linear case, the numerical solution is the best approximation in the energy norm among all nodally bound-preserving finite element functions. A series of numerical experiments for such problems showcase the good behaviour of the proposed bound-preserving finite element method.



1.
Introduction

Structure-preserving numerical methods have been an overarching theme in computational partial differential equations (PDEs) over the
years. By structure-preserving we mean methods that produce approximations satisfying certain desired properties of the underlying exact problem, e.g., local conservation, entropy
inequalities, maximum principle, pointwise divergence-free constraints, or exactly
symmetric stress tensor approximations, just to name a few.

Numerical methods satisfying
Discrete Maximum Principles (DMP)
and/or
monotonicity
properties have been studied extensively in the finite element literature; see
[
11
,
12
,
22
,
27
,
8
,
7
,
1
,
2
]
for a (very) non-exhaustive list, and
[
3
]
for a recent review and more related references. Methods satisfying the latter properties imply
positivity preservation
or, more generally,
bound preservation
of the resulting numerical solutions.

Crucially, however, bound-preservation appears to be a weaker structural requirement than the DMP. Nonetheless, bound-preserving numerical solutions are indispensable for the numerical stability of many complex nonlinear phenomena modelled by systems of PDEs. For instance, many such PDE models are valid only for positive solutions of the constituent equations. Examples include nonlinear reaction-diffusion systems modelling concentrations of reactants, or turbulence-inducing fields. Also, phase-field PDE models are usually characterised by solutions satisfying pointwise global maxima and minima. Although failure to preserve the same bounds for the numerical solutions is not typically catastrophic for scalar PDE problems, they may have a compounding effect when such PDEs are part of a more complex system of equations.

There is thus an interest in bound-preserving methods which, when appropriately designed, may be less “stiff” than current schemes striving to satisfy the DMP or monotonicity.

This work proposes a new finite element method in this spirit. To this end, assume that we are given a partial differential equation (with its associated weak form), and a Lagrange finite element space of arbitrary order.
Moreover, for simplicity, assume that the exact solution is positive, i.e. the zero function is a lower bound.
Then the formal derivation of the new method can be summarized by the following three steps:

•

Introduce a projection operator
v_{h}\mapsto v_{h}^{+}
on the finite element space so that the nodal values of the projected function
v_{h}^{+}
are positive.

•

As usual, replace the test space in the weak formulation of the continuous problem with the finite element space, but, instead of looking for a finite element function as the numerical solution, seek for a projected one in the form
u_{h}^{+}
.

•

Add a stabilisation based upon the complementary projection
v_{h}\mapsto v_{h}^{-}
onto the finite element space.

Notice that the projection operator and so the proposed method are nonlinear. The latter is expected in light of the classical “Godunov barrier” principle. The solution of the proposed method is sought in the range of the projection, where the projection acts as a parametrisation and the parameter domain is the finite element space. The fact that the projection is not injective entails the need of the stabilisation in the third step.

Interestingly, if its nodal values are positive, the classical finite element solution coincides with the one of the proposed method. More precisely, and more importantly, the solution to the present method
turns out to be the orthogonal projection of the exact solution
onto the closed and convex set of finite element functions with positive
nodal values. This observation is instrumental in this work and yields the following two important consequences:

•

The proposed method can be viewed as a reformulation of a discrete obstacle problem. In contrast to the latter, the reformulation consists of a discrete variational equality and encodes the crucial inequality constraints in Lipschitz-continuous projections.
This difference may prepare the ground for solvers that are an alternative to the usually used constrained optimisation techniques; see, e.g.,
[
16
]
. Here, we employ a
simple approach based upon Richardson-like iterations.

•

Near best approximation for suitable error notions follows as a corollary. In particular, for the linear reaction-diffusion problem, the solution of the proposed method is actually the best approximation to the exact solution in the energy norm from the aforementioned convex subset. For a more general, semilinear problem with a power-like nonlinear reaction, the proposed method is shown to be quasi-optimal with respect to the sum of the
H^{1}
-seminorm and a quasinorm associated with the reaction term.

To illustrate both aspects, a series of numerical experiments is presented, showcasing the superior performance of the proposed method compared to standard finite element approximations.

It is worth mentioning that related approaches (albeit with distinct differences to the present one) have been advocated in the literature.
In
[
5
]
the bounds on the continuous solution are imposed as a restriction in a constraint optimisation problem, and a link to
nonlinear stabilisation is presented. Also, in
[
28
]
a constrained optimisation problem involving a mixed weak formulation is employed to enforce bound-preservation. In
[
9
]
the link between positivity-preservation and the contact problem
is used to motivate a nonlinear stabilised method that enforces the positivity of the solution in a weak way.
In addition, the

cut-off
finite element method
[
26
]

truncates the finite element function
after
it is computed at a given time step, so as
to input the truncated function as approximation of the current time step;
see also
[
32
]
for an application of a related idea to the Allen-Cahn equation.
In the steady state case, the idea of
truncating

the finite element solution to respect given bounds has been justified for reaction-diffusion equations in
[
23
]
using energy arguments.
In
[
24
]
a conservative recovery strategy is proposed and tested numerically.
Finally, in the context of the Joule heating problem a truncation of one of the variables is introduced in order to
regularise a rough right-hand side in
[
21
]
.

The remainder of the manuscript is organised as follows. The rest of introduction is devoted to setting up notations and main
assumptions of this work. The finite element method is presented in Section
3
, and its stability and optimal error
estimates are proven. Then, in Section
4
we extend this framework to a nonlinear reaction-diffusion equation, and
finally Section
5
is devoted to presenting numerical experiments.

2.
General setting and the linear model problem

We will use standard notation for Sobolev spaces, in line with, e.g.,
[
15
]
. More precisely,
for
D\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d=1,2,3
, we denote by
\|\cdot\|_{0,p,D}
the
L^{p}(D)
-norm, when
p=2

the subscript
p
will be often omitted, and we only write
\|\cdot\|_{0,D}
. In addition, for
s\geq 0,p\in(1,\infty)
,
we denote by
\|\cdot\|_{s,p,D}
(
|\cdot|_{s,p,D}
) the norm (seminorm) in
W^{s,p}(D)
; when

p=2
, we will again often omit the subscript
p
.
In addition,
we denote by

H^{-1}(D)
the dual of
H^{1}_{0}(D)

while identifying
L^{2}(D)
with its dual. Thus, writing
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{D}
for the duality pairing, we have

\langle f,v\rangle_{\Omega}=\int_{\Omega}f(\bm{x})v(\bm{x})\textrm{d}\bm{x}\qquad\forall\,v\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\,,

whenever
f\in H^{-1}(D)
is regular enough.
The
L^{2}(D)
-inner product is denoted by
(\cdot,\cdot)_{D}
.
We will not distinguish between inner product and duality pairing for scalar and for vector-valued functions.

The boundary-value problems we will be concerned with are posed in an open, bounded domain
\Omega
with polyhedral Lipschitz boundary
\partial\Omega
.
Let
\mathcal{P}
be a conforming, shape-regular partition of
\Omega
into simplices (or quadrilaterals/hexahedra)
K
.
Over
\mathcal{P}
, and for
k\geq 1
, we define the finite element space

(1)

V_{\mathcal{P}}:=\{v_{h}\in C^{0}(\Omega):v_{h}|_{K}\in\mathcal{R}(K)\;\forall\,K\in\mathcal{P}\}\cap H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\,,

where

(2)

\mathcal{R}(K)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\mathbb{P}_{k}(K)&\;\textrm{if}\;K\;\textrm{is a simplex}\,,\\
\mathbb{Q}_{k}(K)&\;\textrm{if}\;K\;\textrm{is {an affine} quadrilateral/hexahedron}\,,\end{array}\right.

with
\mathbb{P}_{k}(K)
denoting the polynomials of total degree
k
on
K
and
\mathbb{Q}_{k}(K)
denoting the,
mapped from a reference element through an affine mapping, polynomials of degree
\leq k
in each variable.
We denote by
\bm{x}_{i},i=1,\ldots,N
the set of internal nodes of
\mathcal{P}
, and by
\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{N}
the set
of usual Lagrangian basis functions spanning the space
V_{\mathcal{P}}
.

The diameter of
K
is denoted by
h_{K}
,
h=\max\{h_{K}:K\in\mathcal{P}\}
, and we define the mesh function
\mathfrak{h}
as a continuous, element-wise
linear function defined as a local average. More precisely, for a node
\bm{x}_{i}
of
\mathcal{P}
we define the local neighbourhood
K_{\bm{x}_{i}}=\{K\in\mathcal{P}:\bm{x}_{i}\in K\}
, and define

\mathfrak{h}
as the only element of
V_{\mathcal{P}}
(with
k=1
) given by the nodal values

(3)

\mathfrak{h}(\bm{x}_{i})=\frac{\sum_{K\in K_{\bm{x}_{i}}}h_{K}}{\#K_{\bm{x}_{i}}}\,.

We recall the inverse inequality (see, e.g.,
[
15
, Lemma 12.1]
): for all
m,\ell\in\mathbb{N},m\leq\ell
and all
p,q\in[1,+\infty]
, there
exists a constant
C
, independent of
K\in\mathcal{P}
such that

(4)

|q|_{\ell,p,K}\leq Ch_{K}^{m-\ell+d\left(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q}\right)}\,|q|_{m,q,K}\,,

for every polynomial function
q
defined on
K
.

In the space
V_{\mathcal{P}}
we denote by
(\cdot,\cdot)_{h}
the lumped
L^{2}(\Omega)
-inner product given by

(5)

(v_{h},w_{h})_{h}:=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathfrak{h}^{d}(\bm{x}_{i})v_{h}(\bm{x}_{i})w_{h}(\bm{x}_{i})\,,

with associated norm
|v_{h}|_{h}=(v_{h},v_{h})_{h}^{\frac{1}{2}}
.

Remark 2.1
.

The following result, whose proof can be found in
[
15
, Proposition 12.5]
,
will be of importance for us in the analysis of the method
proposed in Section
3
: there exist
C,c>0
, independent of
\mathfrak{h}
, such that

(6)

c\,|v_{h}|_{h}^{2}\leq\|v_{h}\|^{2}_{0,\Omega}\leq C\,|v_{h}|_{h}^{2}\,,

for all
v_{h}\in V_{\mathcal{P}}
.

2.1.
Linear model problem

Given
f\in H^{-1}(\Omega)
we consider the following reaction-diffusion
equation: find
u:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
such that

(7)

\displaystyle-{\mathop{\mathrm{div}\,}}\big(\mathcal{D}\nabla u)+\mu u

\displaystyle=f\qquad\textrm{in}\;\Omega\,,

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle=0\qquad\textrm{on}\;\partial\Omega\,,

with
\mathcal{D}=\big(d_{ij}\big)_{i,j=1}^{d}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)^{d\times d}
and
\mu\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
stand for the diffusion tensor and reaction coefficient, respectively. We assume that
\mu(\bm{x})\geq\mu_{0}\geq 0
a.e. in
\Omega
, and that the diffusion tensor
\mathcal{D}
is symmetric and uniformly strictly positive-definite in
\Omega
, viz.,
there exists a positive constant
d_{0}>0
such that, for almost all
\bm{x}\in\Omega
, we have

(8)

\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}y_{i}d_{ij}(\bm{x})y_{j}\geq d_{0}\sum_{i=1}^{d}y_{i}^{2}\qquad\forall\,(y_{1},\ldots,y_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,.

The weak formulation of (
7
) reads: find
u\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
, such that

(9)

a(u,v)=\langle f,v\rangle_{\Omega}\qquad\forall\,v\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\,,

where
a(\cdot,\cdot):H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\times H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\to\mathbb{R}
is the bilinear form defined by

(10)

a(v,w):=(\mathcal{D}\nabla v,\nabla w)_{\Omega}+(\mu v,w)_{\Omega}\,.

Defining the
energy norm

(11)

\|v\|_{a}:=\sqrt{a(v,v)}\,,

the well-posedness of (
9
) follows from the Lax-Milgram Lemma (see, e.g.,
[
15
]
).

Remark 2.2
.

At the core of this work is the following property of the solution of (
9
):
as a consequence of maximum and comparison principles (see
[
17
]
, or
[
29
, Corollary 4.4]
)
the following bounds can be proven: for almost all
\bm{x}\in\Omega

the solution
u
of (
9
) satisfies

(12)

-\frac{\|f\|_{0,\infty,\Omega}}{\mu_{0}}\leq u(\bm{x})\leq\frac{\|f\|_{0,\infty,\Omega}}{\mu_{0}}\,.

This last statement can be made more precise if
f\geq 0
in
\Omega
. In fact, in this case for almost all
\bm{x}\in\Omega

the following inequality holds:

(13)

0\leq u(\bm{x})\leq\frac{\|f\|_{0,\infty,\Omega}}{\mu_{0}}\,.

The results given in the above remark motivate the introduction of the following assumption.

Assumption (A1):

We will suppose that the solution of (
7
) satisfies

(14)

0\leq u(x)\leq\mathfrak{a}\qquad\textrm{for almost all}\;x\in\,\Omega\,,

where
\mathfrak{a}
is a known constant.

Remark 2.3
.

The lower bound in
{\mathop{\mathrm{Assumption~(A1)}}}
is not required to be equal to zero and
all the results proven below hold for a more general bounding
box without major modifications. In particular, the value
\mathfrak{a}
can be replaced by a
non-negative, continuous function
\mathfrak{a}(\bm{x})
.

The standard Galerkin finite element method for (
7
) is given by: find
u_{h}^{\rm FEM}\in V_{\mathcal{P}}
such that

(15)

a(u_{h}^{\rm FEM},v_{h})=\langle f,v_{h}\rangle_{\Omega}\qquad\forall\,v_{h}\in V_{\mathcal{P}}\,.

It is well-known that
u_{h}^{\rm FEM}
is a best approximation in the following sense:

(16)

\|u-u_{h}^{\rm FEM}\|_{a}=\inf_{v_{h}\in V_{\mathcal{P}}}\|u-v_{h}\|_{a}.

However, this nice property does not prevent that, when the reaction
\mu
dominates the diffusion
\mathcal{D}
, that is, e.g. if
\|\mathcal{D}\|_{0,\infty,\Omega}\ll\mu
, then
u_{h}^{\rm FEM}
may exhibit spurious oscillations (see, e.g.,
[
30
]
) and,
thus,
u_{h}^{\rm FEM}
may fail to satisfy
{\mathop{\mathrm{Assumption~(A1)}}}
. In fact, very stringent conditions need to be imposed on the mesh for the solution of a reaction-diffusion equation
such as (
9
) to satisfy
{\mathop{\mathrm{Assumption~(A1)}}}
(see, e.g.,
[
7
]
for details), even in the case of scalar (isotropic) diffusion. For the case of a general diffusion
\mathcal{D}
,
conditions on the mesh related to the weighted inner product
(\mathcal{D}\cdot,\cdot)
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
need to be imposed, in addition to a mesh size restriction
(see, e.g.,
[
20
]
for details).

3.
The finite element method for the linear problem

The goal of this section is to derive a method that, on the one hand, essentially preserves the bounds in Assumption (A1) without restrictions on the mesh and, on the other hand, maintains the good approximation properties (
16
) of the classical finite element solution.

To this end,
we define the following closed convex subset of
V_{\mathcal{P}}
:

(17)

V_{\mathcal{P}}^{+}:=\{v_{h}\in V_{\mathcal{P}}:v_{h}(\bm{x}_{i})\in[0,\mathfrak{a}]\;\textrm{for all}\,\bm{x}_{i}=1,\ldots,N\}\,,

that is, the set of finite element functions that respect the bound (
14
) at their degrees of freedom.
With this convex set in mind, every finite element function is split as
v_{h}=v_{h}^{+}+v_{h}^{-}
, where
v_{h}^{+}\in V_{\mathcal{P}}^{+}
is defined by

(18)

v_{h}^{+}:=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\max\big\{0,\min\{v_{h}(\bm{x}_{i}),\mathfrak{a}\}\big\}\phi_{i}\,,

and

(19)

v_{h}^{-}:=v_{h}-v_{h}^{+}\,,

is the part of the function residing outside
V_{\mathcal{P}}^{+}
.
From now on, we refer to the functions
v_{h}^{+}
and
v_{h}^{-}
as the
constrained

and
complementary
parts of
v_{h}
.

The finite element method proposed in this work reads: find
u_{h}\in V_{\mathcal{P}}
, such that

(20)

a_{h}(u_{h};v_{h})=\langle f,v_{h}\rangle_{\Omega}\qquad\forall\,v_{h}\in V_{\mathcal{P}}\,,

where
a_{h}(\cdot;\cdot)
is the nonlinear form given by

(21)

a_{h}(u_{h};v_{h}):=a(u_{h}^{+},v_{h})+s(u_{h}^{-},v_{h})\,,

with
a(\cdot,\cdot)
defined in (
10
), and
s(\cdot,\cdot):C(\bar{\Omega})\times C(\bar{\Omega})\to\mathbb{R}
is the stabilising bilinear form defined by

(22)

s(v_{h},w_{h})=\alpha\sum_{i=1}^{N}\big(\|\mathcal{D}\|_{0,\infty,\omega_{i}}\mathfrak{h}(\bm{x}_{i})^{d-2}+\|\mu\|_{0,\infty,\omega_{i}}\mathfrak{h}(\bm{x}_{i})^{d}\big)\,v_{h}(\bm{x}_{i})w_{h}(\bm{x}_{i})\,,

where

\alpha>0
is a non-dimensional constant to be determined precisely in Theorem
3.2
, and
\omega_{i}:=\cup_{K\in\mathcal{P}:K\cap K_{\bm{x}_{i}}\neq\emptyset}K
denotes an extended patch. The definition of
\omega_{i}
will be exploited in establishing (
24
) below.

Defining the stabilisation norm as

(23)

\|v_{h}\|_{s}:=\sqrt{s(v_{h},v_{h})}\,,

and using (
6
), there
exists a
C_{\rm equiv}>0
, depending only on the shape-regularity constant, such that

(24)

\|v_{h}\|_{a}^{2}\leq\frac{C_{\rm equiv}}{\alpha}\,\|v_{h}\|_{s}^{2}\qquad\forall\,v_{h}\in V_{\mathcal{P}}\,,

where
\alpha>0
is the stabilisation parameter appearing in the definition (
22
) of
s(\cdot,\cdot)
.

3.1.
Well-posedness and consistency

In this section we will analyse the existence, uniqueness, and stability results for the proposed method (
20
). We start with
the following monotonicity result for the stabilising form
s(\cdot,\cdot)
.

Lemma 3.1
.

The bilinear form
s(\cdot,\cdot)
satisfies the following inequalities:

(25)

\displaystyle s(v_{h}^{-}-w_{h}^{-},v_{h}^{+}-w_{h}^{+})

\displaystyle\geq 0\qquad\forall\,v_{h},w_{h}\in V_{\mathcal{P}}\,,

(26)

\displaystyle s(v_{h}^{-},w_{h}-v_{h}^{+})

\displaystyle\leq 0\qquad\forall\,v_{h}\in V_{\mathcal{P}},w_{h}\in V_{\mathcal{P}}^{+}\,.

Proof.

Let
\bm{x}_{i}\in\mathcal{P}
be any internal node. If

v_{h}(\bm{x}_{i})\geq w_{h}(\bm{x}_{i})
, then
v_{h}^{+}(\bm{x}_{i})\geq w_{h}^{+}(\bm{x}_{i})
. Moreover, if
v_{h}(\bm{x}_{i})\geq w_{h}(\bm{x}_{i})>\mathfrak{a}
, then

v_{h}^{-}(\bm{x}_{i})=v_{h}(\bm{x}_{i})-\mathfrak{a}\geq w_{h}(\bm{x}_{i})-\mathfrak{a}=w_{h}^{-}(\bm{x}_{i})
. If
v_{h}(\bm{x}_{i})>\mathfrak{a}
and

w_{h}(\bm{x}_{i})\leq\mathfrak{a}
, then

v_{h}^{-}(\bm{x}_{i})=v_{h}(\bm{x}_{i})-\mathfrak{a}\geq 0\geq w_{h}^{-}(\bm{x}_{i})
. If
v_{h}(\bm{x}_{i})\leq\mathfrak{a}
, then either
v_{h}^{-}(\bm{x}_{i})=0\geq w_{h}^{-}(\bm{x}_{i})
,
or
v_{h}^{-}(\bm{x}_{i})=v_{h}(\bm{x}_{i})\geq w_{h}(\bm{x}_{i})=w_{h}^{-}(\bm{x}_{i})
.
Thus

\displaystyle s(v_{h}^{-}-w_{h}^{-},v_{h}^{+}-w_{h}^{+})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \alpha\sum_{i=1}^{N}\big(\|\mathcal{D}\|_{0,\infty,\omega_{i}}\mathfrak{h}(\bm{x}_{i})^{d-2}+\|\mu\|_{0,\infty,\omega_{i}}\mathfrak{h}(\bm{x}_{i})^{d}\big)\,(v_{h}^{-}-w_{h}^{-})(\bm{x}_{i})(v_{h}^{+}-w_{h}^{+})(\bm{x}_{i})

(27)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\ 0,

which proves (
25
). Taking
w_{h}\in V_{\mathcal{P}}^{+}
gives
w_{h}^{-}=0
, and then (
26
) follows from (
25
).
∎

Theorem 3.2

(Well-posedness)
.

Let
T:V_{\mathcal{P}}\to[V_{\mathcal{P}}]^{\prime}
be the mapping defined by

(28)

[Tv_{h},w_{h}]=a(v_{h}^{+},w_{h})+s(v_{h}^{-},w_{h})\,.

Then,
T
is continuous and, if the non-dimensional parameter
\alpha
is chosen such that
\alpha\geq C_{\rm equiv}
, it is also strongly monotone, since then
T
satisfies: there exists
\beta>0
, independent
of
h
, such that

(29)

[Tv_{h}-Tw_{h},v_{h}-w_{h}]\geq\beta\,\|v_{h}-w_{h}\|^{2}_{a}\,,

for all
v_{h},w_{h}\in V_{\mathcal{P}}
.
As a consequence, (
20
) has a unique solution
u_{h}\in V_{\mathcal{P}}
.

Proof.

We start defining the mesh-dependent norm
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{P}}
by

(30)

\|v_{h}\|_{\mathcal{P}}:=\left\{\|v_{h}\|_{a}^{2}+\|v_{h}\|_{s}^{2}\right\}^{\frac{1}{2}}\,.

Then, for all
v_{h},w_{h},z_{h}\in V_{\mathcal{P}}
using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and (
24
) we get to

\displaystyle[Tv_{h}-Tw_{h},z_{h}]

\displaystyle=a\big(v_{h}^{+}-w_{h}^{+},z_{h}\big)+s\big(v_{h}^{-}-w_{h}^{-},z_{h}\big)

(31)

\displaystyle\leq\big(\|v_{h}^{+}-w_{h}^{+}\|_{a}^{2}+\|v_{h}^{-}-w_{h}^{-}\|_{s}^{2}\big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\,\|z_{h}\|_{\mathcal{P}}\,,

which proves the continuity of
T
. To prove the monotonicity of
T
, let
v_{h},w_{h}\in V_{\mathcal{P}}
. Using the Cauchy-Schwarz and Young inequalities, and
(
24
), we obtain

\displaystyle[Tv_{h}-Tw_{h},v_{h}-w_{h}]=a(v_{h}^{+}-w_{h}^{+},v_{h}-w_{h})+s(v_{h}^{-}-w_{h}^{-},v_{h}-w_{h})

\displaystyle=\|v_{h}^{+}-w_{h}^{+}\|_{a}^{2}+a(v_{h}^{+}-w_{h}^{+},v_{h}^{-}-w_{h}^{-})+\|v_{h}^{-}-w_{h}^{-}\|_{s}^{2}+s(v_{h}^{-}-w_{h}^{-},v_{h}^{+}-w_{h}^{+})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\|v_{h}^{+}-w_{h}^{+}\|^{2}_{a}+\left(1-\frac{C_{\rm equiv}}{2\alpha}\right)\|v_{h}^{-}-w_{h}^{-}\|_{s}^{2}+s(v_{h}^{-}-w_{h}^{-},v_{h}^{+}-w_{h}^{+})

(32)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\|v_{h}^{+}-w_{h}^{+}\|^{2}_{a}+\left(1-\frac{C_{\rm equiv}}{2\alpha}\right)\|v_{h}^{-}-w_{h}^{-}\|_{s}^{2}\,,

where in the last inequality we used (
25
) in Lemma
3.1
.
Next, using that
\alpha\geq C_{\rm equiv}
and (
24
) we arrive at

(33)

\left(1-\frac{C_{\rm equiv}}{2\alpha}\right)\|v_{h}^{-}-w_{h}^{-}\|_{s}^{2}\geq\frac{\alpha}{2C_{\rm equiv}}\|v_{h}^{-}-w_{h}^{-}\|_{a}^{2}\,.

Hence, (
29
) follows replacing (
33
) in (
32
) and the fact that, since

a(\cdot,\cdot)
is symmetric and elliptic, we have
\|v_{h}\|_{a}\leq\sqrt{2}\,(\|v_{h}^{+}\|_{a}+\|v_{h}^{-}\|_{a})
.

Finally, the existence and uniqueness of solutions follows by using classical results in monotone operator theory (see, e.g.,
[
29
, Theorem 10.49]
).
∎

For the next observation, it is useful to recall that, for Galerkin methods, consistency can be expressed as an invariance property of the operator mapping the exact solution to the Galerkin one.

Lemma 3.3

(Consistency)
.

Under
{\mathop{\mathrm{Assumption~(A1)}}}
, the method (
20
) enjoys the following invariance property: if the exact solution
u
belongs to
V_{\mathcal{P}}{}
, then
u_{h}^{+}=u_{h}=u
.

Proof.

As
u\in V_{\mathcal{P}}{}
, the functions
u^{+}
and
u^{-}
are defined and, due to
{\mathop{\mathrm{Assumption~(A1)}}}
, we have
u^{+}=u

and
u^{-}=0
. As a result, we get

a_{h}(u;v_{h})=a(u^{+},v_{h})=a(u,v_{h})=\langle f,v_{h}\rangle_{\Omega}\,,

for all
v_{h}\in V_{\mathcal{P}}
. Thanks to Theorem
3.2
, the solution of (
20
) is unique and therefore we obtain
u_{h}=u
. Using that
u_{h}^{-}=u^{-}=0
, we conclude that
u_{h}^{+}=u_{h}
.
∎

Remark 3.4
.

Whenever
k=1
the method (
20
) is actually
bound-preserving throughout the domain
\Omega
, not only in the
nodes. Furthermore, if
\mu>0
, in addition it also respects the
discrete maximum principle. That is, if
f\geq 0
then
u_{h}^{+}

cannot attain an interior negative minimum, and reaches its
minimum at the boundary. If
\mu=0
, it appears that the method
by construction does not guarantee that
u_{h}^{+}
cannot attain an
interior minimum. Nonetheless, all our numerical experiments to
date have failed to produce such a case.

3.2.
Characterisation of the constrained part and error estimates

One of the salient features of the method (
20
) is that
u_{h}^{+}

is characterised as being the unique solution of variational
inequality posed on the closed convex set
V_{\mathcal{P}}^{+}
. This is proven
in the next result.

Theorem 3.5

(Characterization of constrained part)
.

Let
u_{h}\in V_{\mathcal{P}}
be the unique solution of (
20
). Then,
u_{h}^{+}
satisfies the following variational inequality:
u_{h}^{+}\in V_{\mathcal{P}}^{+}
and satisfies

(34)

a(u_{h}^{+},v_{h}-u_{h}^{+})\geq\langle f,v_{h}-u_{h}^{+}\rangle_{\Omega}\qquad\forall\,v_{h}\in V_{\mathcal{P}}^{+}\,.

Proof.

Using (
20
) with
v_{h}\in V_{\mathcal{P}}
,
v_{h}^{-}
, and
u_{h}^{+}
as test functions we have the following
equalities

\displaystyle a(u_{h}^{+},v_{h})+s(u_{h}^{-},v_{h})

\displaystyle=\langle f,v_{h}\rangle_{\Omega}\,,

\displaystyle a(u_{h}^{+},v_{h}^{-})+s(u_{h}^{-},v_{h}^{-})

\displaystyle=\langle f,v_{h}^{-}\rangle_{\Omega}\,,

\displaystyle a(u_{h}^{+},u_{h}^{+})+s(u_{h}^{-},u_{h}^{+})

\displaystyle=\langle f,u_{h}^{+}\rangle_{\Omega}\,.

Subtracting the second and third equation from the first one, and using that
v_{h}^{+}=v_{h}-v_{h}^{-}
we get

(35)

a(u_{h}^{+},v_{h}^{+}-u_{h}^{+})+s(u_{h}^{-},v_{h}^{+}-u_{h}^{+})=\langle f,v_{h}^{+}-u_{h}^{+}\rangle_{\Omega}\,,

for all
v_{h}^{+}\in V_{\mathcal{P}}^{+}
. Finally, using (
26
) in Lemma
3.1
we get that
u_{h}^{+}\in V_{\mathcal{P}}^{+}

satisfies (
34
), thus completing the proof.
∎

Remark 3.6
.

Due to Stampacchia’s Theorem, problem (
34
) can be proven directly to have a unique solution.
This proves that both the stabilised method (
20
) and the variational inequality (
34
) are, in fact, equivalent.

The equivalence of the method as a variational inequality enables us to prove best approximation error estimates in a standard fashion.

Theorem 3.7

(Abstract error analysis)
.

Let
u
be the solution of (
7
) and
u_{h}\in V_{\mathcal{P}}
be the unique solution of (
20
). Then,

(36)

\|u-u_{h}^{+}\|_{a}=\min_{v_{h}\in V_{\mathcal{P}}^{+}}\|u-v_{h}\|_{a}\,.

Moreover, let
u_{h}^{\rm FEM}
be the solution of the (standard) finite element method (
15
). Then,
the negative part
u_{h}^{-}
satisfies the following error estimate

(37)

s(u_{h}^{-},u_{h}^{-})^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq\sqrt{\frac{C_{\rm equiv}}{\alpha}}\min\big\{\|u-u_{h}^{+}\|_{a},\|u_{h}^{\rm FEM}-u_{h}^{+}\|_{a}\big\}\,.

Proof.

Since
u_{h}^{+}
is the solution of (
34
), it satisfies in particular

(38)

a(u_{h}^{+}-u,v_{h}-u_{h}^{+})\geq 0\qquad\forall\,v_{h}\in V_{\mathcal{P}}^{+}\,.

Thus,
u_{h}^{+}
is the best approximation of
u
in
V_{\mathcal{P}}^{+}
with respect to the norm induced by

a(\cdot,\cdot)
, thus proving the quasi-optimality for
u_{h}^{+}
stated in (
36
).

The proof of (
37
) is similar. In fact, using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and (
24
) we get

(39)

s(u_{h}^{-},u_{h}^{-})=a(u-u_{h}^{+},u_{h}^{-})\leq\sqrt{\frac{C_{\rm equiv}}{\alpha}}\,\|u-u_{h}^{+}\|_{a}\sqrt{s(u_{h}^{-},u_{h}^{-})}\,.

Alternatively, using that
a(u-u_{h}^{\rm FEM},u_{h}^{-})=0
, then, we also have

(40)

s(u_{h}^{-},u_{h}^{-})=a(u_{h}^{\rm FEM}-u_{h}^{+},u_{h}^{-})\leq\sqrt{\frac{C_{\rm equiv}}{\alpha}}\|u_{h}^{\rm FEM}-u_{h}^{+}\|_{a}\sqrt{s(u_{h}^{-},u_{h}^{-})}\,,

which proves (
37
).
∎

Remark 3.8

(Convergence of complementary part)
.

We may interpret the last result in two ways. First,
u_{h}^{-}
converges to zero at least at the same speed as
u_{h}^{+}
converges to
u
.
Moreover, (
37
) implies that in certain cases this convergence is much faster than the one for
u_{h}^{+}
.
More precisely, focusing on the case of piecewise linear finite element functions, if the mesh satisfies the conditions
for the plain Galerkin method to admit discrete maximum principle (see, e.g.,
[
7
]
), then
u_{h}^{\rm FEM}\in V_{\mathcal{P}}^{+}
, which implies
that
u_{h}=u_{h}^{\rm FEM}
is also the solution to (
20
). Thus, thanks to (
37
), for certain meshes,
and their regular refinements, we have that
u_{h}^{-}=0
.

Remark 3.9

(Best approximation of constrained part)
.

In view of the best approximation result (
36
) for the constrained part
u_{h}^{+}
, there is no better finite element function with bound-preserving nodal values in the energy norm. Thus, (
36
) is the counterpart of the classical best approximation result (
16
) for the proposed method.

We finish this section by discussing briefly the notion of numerical solution. Since our main interest is the part of the solution

u_{h}
that belongs to
V_{\mathcal{P}}^{+}
, namely
u_{h}^{+}
, then the latter will be considered to be the numerical solution in the remaining
of the manuscript. The “intermediate” solution
u_{h}
appears mostly as a tool to be able to replace the variational inequality
by an equality posed over the whole vector space
V_{h}
.

4.
A problem with nonlinear reaction

To showcase the potential generality of the proposed bound-preserving approach, we now extend (
20
) to a
semilinear problem with monotone nonlinearity. Specifically, for
p\in(1,\infty)
we consider
the problem of finding
u
such that

(41)

\begin{split}-{\mathop{\mathrm{div}\,}}\big(\mathcal{D}\,\nabla u\big)+\left|u\right|^{p-2}u&=f\qquad\text{ in }\Omega\,,\\
u&=0\qquad\text{ on }\partial\Omega\,,\end{split}

where
\mathcal{D}
satisfies the same assumptions as above.
To avoid technical diversions, we will only consider
p\geq 2
.
This class of equation is sometimes referred to as the
Lane-Emden-Fowler equation and is related to problems with critical
exponents
[
13
]
. Furthermore, they arise in
the theory of boundary layers of viscous fluids

[
31
]
, among other application areas.

The weak form of this problem is given by: find
u\in\mathcal{X}:=H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\cap L^{p}(\Omega)
such that

(42)

a\!\left({u,v}\right)+b\!\left({u;u,v}\right)=\left\langle{f,v}\right\rangle_{\Omega}\qquad\forall\,v\in\mathcal{X}\,,

where
a(\cdot,\cdot)
is given by (
10
) (with
\mu=0
in this case), and the semilinear form
b
is given by

(43)

b\!\left({w;u,v}\right):=(\left|w\right|^{p-2}u,v)_{\Omega}\,.

The space
\mathcal{X}
is provided with the norm

(44)

\|v\|_{\mathcal{X}}:=|v|_{1,\Omega}+\|v\|_{0,p,\Omega}\,,

thus making it a reflexive Banach space. So, using monotone operator theory (see, e.g.,
[
29
, Chap 10]
), this problem can be proven to have a unique solution.

The error analysis of this type of problem has been carried out in several works, as early
as
[
18
,
19
]
(in the context of the
p
-Laplacian). In there, the estimates are suboptimal for some values of the exponent
p
.
So, later approaches (see, e.g.,
[
4
]
) have made use of the concept of quasinorm in order to obtain
optimal error estimates. As this is the approach we will follow in this work, we start recalling the definition of a quasinorm.

Definition 4.1

(Quasinorm)
.

Let
V
be a real vector space. A
quasinorm

\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\!\left({q}\right)}
in
V
is a mapping
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\!\left({q}\right)}:V\to\mathbb{R}

that satisfies

(45)

\left\|v\right\|_{\!\left({q}\right)}\geq 0\text{ and }\left\|v\right\|_{\!\left({q}\right)}=0\iff v=0,

for all
v\in V
.
However, the usual triangle inequality is replaced by

(46)

\left\|v+w\right\|_{\!\left({q}\right)}\leq C\!\left({\left\|v\right\|_{\!\left({q}\right)}+\left\|w\right\|_{\!\left({q}\right)}}\right),

for all
v,w\in V
, where
C
may depend on the definition of
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\!\left({q}\right)}
, and the elements
v
and
w
themselves.

Remark 4.2
.

Strictly speaking, we should also demand that the quasinorm is homogeneous, that is,
\left\|\alpha v\right\|_{\!\left({q}\right)}=|\alpha|\left\|v\right\|_{\!\left({q}\right)}
for
all
v\in V
and all
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
. The mapping we will use to measure the error does not satisfy this last property, but
this will not affect the error estimates presented below.

In our analysis below we will make use of the following quasinorm in
L^{p}(\Omega)
: for a given
w\in L^{p}(\Omega)

we define

(47)

\left\|v\right\|_{\!\left({w,p}\right)}^{2}:=\int_{\Omega}\left|v\right|^{2}\!\left({\left|w\right|+\left|v\right|}\right)^{p-2}\textrm{d}x\,,

for all
v\in L^{p}(\Omega)
. It has the following properties.

Lemma 4.3
.

The mapping
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\!\left({w,p}\right)}
is a quasinorm in
L^{p}(\Omega)
. Moreover,
for all
v,w\in L^{p}(\Omega)
, the following equivalence holds

(48)

\left\|v\right\|_{0,p,\Omega}^{p}\leq\left\|v\right\|_{\!\left({w,p}\right)}^{2}\leq\||v|+|w|\|_{0,p,\Omega}^{p-2}\,\left\|v\right\|_{0,p,\Omega}^{2}\,.

Proof.

The proof that
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\!\left({w,p}\right)}
is a quasinorm is completely analogous to that of
[
14
, Proposition 2.1]
. The equivalence (
48
)
is proven following exactly the same steps as in the proof of
[
14
, Equation (10)]
(see also
[
25
, Proposition 3.1]
).
∎

In addition, the following monotonicity and continuity results can be proven for the nonlinear form
b(\cdot;\cdot,\cdot)
.

Lemma 4.4
.

The nonlinear form
b(\cdot;\cdot,\cdot)
is strongly monotone with respect to the
quasinorm (
47
). More precisely, there exists a constant
C_{C}>0
such that

(49)

b\!\left({u;u,u-v}\right)-b\!\left({v;v,u-v}\right)\geq C_{C}\left\|u-v\right\|_{\!\left({u,p}\right)}^{2}.

Moreover, for any
\theta\in(0,1]
the following holds

(50)

\left|b\!\left({u;u,w}\right)-b\!\left({v;v,w}\right)\right|\leq C_{B}\!\left({\theta\left\|u-v\right\|_{\!\left({u,p}\right)}^{2}+\theta^{{-p+1}}\left\|w\right\|_{\!\left({u,p}\right)}^{2}}\right)\qquad\forall\,u,v,w\in L^{p}(\Omega)\,,

where
C_{B}
depends only on
p
.

Proof.

In
[
4
, Lemma 2.1]
the following bounds are proven

(51)

\displaystyle\big(|x|^{p-2}x-|y|^{p-2}y\big)\,(x-y)

\displaystyle\geq C_{2}\big(|x|+|y|\big)^{p-2}|x-y|^{2}\,,

(52)

\displaystyle\big||x|^{p-2}x-|y|^{p-2}y\big|

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}\big(|x|+|y|\big)^{p-2}|x-y|\,,

for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
. Next, (
49
) follows by from (
51
) and noticing that

|x|+|y|\geq\big(|x|+|x-y|\big)/2
.
To prove (
50
) we combine (
52
) with the result from
[
25
, Lemma 2.2]
.
∎

4.1.
The finite element method

The finite element method we consider is the natural extension of
(
20
), that is: find
u_{h}\in V_{\mathcal{P}}
such that

(53)

a\!\left({u_{h}^{+},v_{h}}\right)+b\!\left({u_{h}^{+};u_{h}^{+},v_{h}}\right)+s\!\left({u_{h}^{-},v_{h}}\right)=\left\langle{f,v_{h}}\right\rangle_{\Omega}\qquad\forall\,v_{h}\in V_{\mathcal{P}}\,.

Here, the stabilisation term is given by

(54)

s(v_{h},w_{h}):=\alpha\sum_{i=1}^{N}\|\mathcal{D}\|_{0,\infty,\omega_{i}}\mathfrak{h}(\bm{x}_{i})^{d-2}\,v_{h}(\bm{x}_{i})w_{h}(\bm{x}_{i})\,,

where, once again,

\alpha>0
is an non-dimensional constant. Following very similar steps to those from the proof
of Theorem
3.2
, Method (
53
) can be proven to have a unique solution
u_{h}\in V_{\mathcal{P}}
. We note that there appears to be no traceable numerical advantage in including a linearised reaction term in the stabilisation (
54
), at least for modestly large values of
p
, so we omit it for simplicity.

As in the linear case, (
53
) can be linked to a variational inequality, as the following result
(whose proof is totally analogous to that of Theorem
3.5
) shows.

Theorem 4.5
.

Let
u_{h}
be the unique solution of (
53
). Then,
u_{h}^{+}

satisfies the variational inequality

(55)

a\!\left({u_{h}^{+},v_{h}-u_{h}^{+}}\right)+b\!\left({u_{h}^{+};u_{h}^{+},v_{h}-u_{h}^{+}}\right)\geq\left\langle{f,v_{h}-u_{h}^{+}}\right\rangle_{\Omega}\qquad\forall\,v_{h}\in V_{\mathcal{P}}^{+}.

The following result is the main reason for the use of a quasinorm instead of the norm induced by
the problem. In fact, starting from the last result a Céa type estimate can be obtained, but, analogously
to what is reported in
[
10
]
, that would lead to a suboptimal estimate for certain values of
p
. So,
in the next result we provide an optimal estimate with respect to the quasinorm (
47
).

Theorem 4.6
.

Let
u
solve (
41
) and
u_{h}\in V_{\mathcal{P}}
be the solution of (
53
). Then

(56)

\left\|u-u_{h}^{+}\right\|_{a}^{2}+\left\|u-u_{h}^{+}\right\|_{\!\left({u,p}\right)}^{2}\leq C\inf_{v_{h}\in V_{\mathcal{P}}^{+}}\!\left({\left\|u-v_{h}\right\|_{a}^{2}+\left\|u-v_{h}\right\|_{\!\left({u,p}\right)}^{2}}\right).

Proof.

Using the coercivity (
49
) of
b
followed by Theorem
4.5
we get

(57)

\begin{split}C_{C}\!\left({\left\|u-u_{h}^{+}\right\|_{a}^{2}+\left\|u-u_{h}^{+}\right\|_{\!\left({u,p}\right)}^{2}}\right)&\leq a\!\left({u-u_{h}^{+},u-u_{h}^{+}}\right)+b\!\left({u;u,u-u_{h}^{+}}\right)-b\!\left({u_{h}^{+};u_{h}^{+},u-u_{h}^{+}}\right)\\
&\leq a\!\left({u-u_{h}^{+},u-v_{h}}\right)+b\!\left({u;u,u-v_{h}}\right)-b\!\left({u_{h}^{+};u_{h}^{+},u-v_{h}}\right),\end{split}

for every
v_{h}\in V_{\mathcal{P}}^{+}
. Next, using Young’s inequality and
(
50
) we have, for all
\theta\in(0,1]
,

(58)

\begin{split}C_{C}\!\left({\left\|u-u_{h}^{+}\right\|_{a}^{2}+\left\|u-u_{h}^{+}\right\|_{\!\left({u,p}\right)}^{2}}\right)&\leq\frac{C_{C}}{4}\left\|u-u_{h}^{+}\right\|_{a}^{2}+C_{C}\left\|u-v_{h}\right\|_{a}^{2}\\
&\qquad+C_{B}\!\left({\theta\left\|u-u_{h}^{+}\right\|_{\!\left({u,p}\right)}^{2}+\theta^{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}-p+1}}\left\|u-v_{h}\right\|_{\!\left({u,p}\right)}^{2}}\right).\end{split}

Choosing
\theta=\min\!\left({\frac{C_{C}}{4C_{B}},\frac{1}{2}}\right)
and rearranging the inequality yields the desired result.
∎

6.
Concluding remarks

We proposed an inexpensive and simple way to impose hard bounds on the range of a finite element solution. This is achieved through the definition of a nonlinear stabilised Galerkin approach, which is designed to provide the orthogonal projection into the closed convex set of physically admissible solutions satisfying hard bounds. In an effort to highlight the key ideas, we have confined the presentation to linear and monotone semilinear reaction-diffusion equations. We stress, however, that the framework is general enough to allow other model problems, as well as bound-preserving variants of other known finite element methods, to be constructed following the methodology presented here. In particular, an interesting extension of the proposed methodology to convection-dominated problems is both relevant and, we believe, within reach. Moreover, the extension to discontinuous Galerkin methods posed on general polygonal/polyhedral meshes is also conceivable. Both these, and other, topics are ongoing, and will be discussed elsewhere.
