Dobrushin and Steif metrics are equal
J. Armstrong-Goodall and R.S.MacKay

Abstract
. It is proved that two useful and apparently different metrics on the set of Borel probabilities on countable products of Polish spaces of bounded diameters are equal. This paves the way for advances in their computation.



1.
Introduction

It is well-known that standard metrics on spaces of multivariate probability distributions with many or countably infinite number of variables are of limited use. For example, Liggett
[
L
]
laments on p.70 that “total variation convergence essentially never occurs for particle systems”. Examples of other metrics that fail similarly are given in
[
M1
,
M2
]
.

To rectify this, one of us
[
M1
]
introduced a metric on multivariate probability distributions that
does
give convergence for many systems (the examples treated there were weakly dependent probabilistic cellular automata, but the same applies to particle systems). It was based on ingredients from Dobrushin
[
D
]
(following
[
Va
]
), which give a type of weak convergence for such systems, but it appears that Dobrushin did not take the final step of metrising the weak topology (perhaps because it requires an assumption of bounded diameters).

It turned out, however, that Steif had proposed a metric that achieves the same goal many years before
[
S
]
. Its definition extends one of Ornstein
[
OW
]
from the case of translation-invariant probabilities on
A^{\mathbb{Z}}
for a finite set
A
to general Borel probabilities on
A^{\mathbb{Z}}
.
[
M1
]
had dismissed extension of Ornstein’s metric but had missed Steif’s way of achieving it.

Superficially, the two metrics look different. Yet in the Appendix to
[
M2
]
it was shown that for finite spaces they are not only equivalent but equal.
This led to the conjecture that they are always equal.

Dobrushin metric was defined on any countable product of Polish (complete separable metric) spaces with bounded diameters.
Steif’s metric can easily be generalised to the same context. In this paper it is proved that they are equal.

This result is significant because they are useful for proving and quantifying convergence of interacting particle systems and parameter-dependence of the stationary probabilities. Their computation for explicit multivariate probabilities, however, is not easy (though see
[
DM
]
for some successes). It is helpful to have two alternative formulations of the same metric.

Before closing the introduction, a comment is appropriate on
[
FH
]
. It extends the weak convergence proof of
[
Va
]
to allow a global component of interaction. In our opinion, this is, however, already covered by the results of
[
Va
,
D
]
, because the dependency matrix of
[
Va
,
D
]
gains a contribution
\varepsilon/N
in each of the
N
components, which still sums to only
\varepsilon
. Furthermore, the metric of
[
FH
]
requires an artificial enumeration and weighting of the components, and they use the oscillation of a function rather than its Lipschitz constant.

2.
Statement of result

Let
S
be a countable set. For each
s\in S
, let
(X_{s},d_{s})
be a Polish (complete separable metric) space. Suppose
\sup_{s\in S}\mbox{diam}(X_{s})<\infty
. Let
X=\prod_{s\in S}X_{s}
with product topology. Let
\mathcal{P}
be the set of Borel probabilities on
X
. For
\mu\in\mathcal{P}
(or a signed Borel measure) and measurable
f:X\to\mathbb{R}
, denote the integral of
f
with respect to
\mu
by
\mu(f)
.
For
x\in X
denote the component in
X_{s}
by
x_{s}
.
Extend
d_{s}:X_{s}\times X_{s}\to\mathbb{R}
to a semi-metric
d_{s}:X\times X\to\mathbb{R}
(denoted by the same symbol) defined by
d_{s}(x,y)=d_{s}(x_{s},y_{s})
for all
x,y\in X
.

For
f:X\to\mathbb{R}
and
s\in S
define the
partial Lipschitz constant

\Delta_{s}(f)=\sup\frac{f(x)-f(y)}{d_{s}(x,y)}

over pairs
x\neq y\in X
agreeing off
s
. Let
the
Dobrushin semi-norm

\|f\|=\sum_{s}\Delta_{s}(f)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\cup\{\infty\}.

Let the
Dobrushin smooth functions

\mathcal{F}
be the set of
f:X\to\mathbb{R}
with
\|f\|<\infty
, and
\mathcal{C}
be the constant functions
X\to\mathbb{R}
. From these ingredients,
[
M1
]
made the following

Definition
: The
Dobrushin distance
between
\mu
and
\nu\in\mathcal{P}
is

(1)

D(\mu,\nu)=\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}\setminus\mathcal{C}}\frac{\mu(f)-\nu(f)}{\|f\|}.

For
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}
let
M
be the set of
joinings
of
\mu
to
\nu
(often called couplings), i.e. the set of Borel probabilities
m
on
X\times X
whose marginals on the first and second factors are
\mu,\nu
, respectively. Extending
[
S
]
from the case where each
X_{s}
was finite with discrete metric, define

Definition
: The
Steif distance
between
\mu
and
\nu\in\mathcal{P}
is

(2)

\bar{d}(\mu,\nu)=\inf_{m\in M}\sup_{s\in S}m(d_{s}).

It is not difficult to check (see
[
M1
]
for
D
) that both
D
and
\bar{d}
are metrics on
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{P}
is complete with respect to each.

Theorem
:

D=\bar{d}

3.
Proof

Proof
: Firstly, by homogeneity of degree one,

D(\mu,\nu)=\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}:\|f\|\leq 1}(\mu-\nu)(f).

Let
E=\{e=(e_{s})_{s\in S}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{S}:\sum_{s\in S}e_{s}\leq 1\}
. For
e\in E
, let

c_{e}(x,y)=\sum_{s\in S}e_{s}d_{s}(x,y)

and

F_{e}=\{f:X\to\mathbb{R}:\forall x,y\in X,f(x)-f(y)\leq c_{e}(x,y)\}.

Then
f\in\cup_{e\in E}F_{e}
implies
\|f\|\leq 1
because if
f\in F_{e}
then for all
s\in S
and
x,y\in X
agreeing on
s
,
f(x)-f(y)\leq e_{s}d_{s}(x,y)
. So
\Delta_{s}(f)\leq e_{s}
, thus summing over
s\in S
,
\|f\|\leq 1
.
Conversely,
\|f\|\leq 1
implies
f\in\cup_{e\in E}F_{e}
because choose an enumeration of
S
and change sequentially the components of
x
to those of
y
to obtain
f(x)-f(y)\leq\sum_{s\in S}\Delta_{s}(f)d_{s}(x,y)
.
But
\sum_{s\in S}\Delta_{s}(f)=\|f\|\leq 1
and
\Delta_{s}(f)\geq 0
, so
\Delta(f)\in E
, thus
f\in F_{\Delta(f)}
.
The supremum over
f\in\cup_{e\in E}F_{e}
is the same as the supremum over
e\in E
of the supremum over
f\in F_{e}
.
Thus,

(3)

D(\mu,\nu)=\sup_{e\in E}\sup_{f\in F_{e}}(\mu-\nu)(f).

Secondly,
\sup_{s\in S}m(d_{s})=\sup_{e\in E}\sum_{s\in S}e_{s}m(d_{s})
, because denote the lefthand side by
u
and let
\varepsilon>0
then
\exists s^{\prime}\in S
with
m(d_{s^{\prime}})\geq u-\varepsilon
, so choose
e_{s^{\prime}}=1
and the rest of
e_{s}=0
to get the righthand side at least
u-\varepsilon
; conversely,
\sum_{s\in S}m(d_{s})\leq\sum_{s\in S}e_{s}u\leq u
. So

\bar{d}(\mu,\nu)=\inf_{m\in M}\sup_{e\in E}\sum_{s\in S}e_{s}m(d_{s}).

This is at least
\sup_{e\in E}\inf_{m\in M}\sum_{s\in S}e_{s}m(d_{s})
because for any
e^{\prime}\in E,m\in M
,

\sup_{e\in E}\sum_{s\in S}e_{s}m(d_{s})\geq\sum_{s\in S}e^{\prime}_{s}m(d_{s}).

Taking the infimum over
m\in M
,
\inf_{m\in M}\sup_{e\in E}\sum_{s\in S}e_{s}m(d_{s})\geq\inf_{m\in M}\sum_{s\in S}e^{\prime}_{s}m(d_{s})
. Taking the supremum over
e^{\prime}\in E
,

\inf_{m\in M}\sup_{e\in E}\sum_{s\in S}e_{s}m(d_{s})\geq\sup_{e^{\prime}\in E}\inf_{m\in M}\sum_{s\in S}e^{\prime}_{s}m(d_{s}).

\bar{d}(\mu,\nu)
is also at most
\sup_{e\in E}\inf_{m\in M}\sum_{s\in S}e_{s}m(d_{s})
because for all
\varepsilon>0
,
e\in E
, there exists
m^{\prime}_{e,\varepsilon}\in M
such that

\inf_{m\in M}\sum_{s\in S}e_{s}m(d_{s})+\varepsilon\geq\sum_{s\in S}e_{s}m^{\prime}_{e,\varepsilon}(d_{s}).

So for all
\varepsilon>0
,

\sup_{e\in E}\inf_{m\in M}\sum_{s\in S}e_{s}m(d_{s})+\varepsilon\geq\sup_{e\in E}\sum_{s\in S}e_{s}m^{\prime}_{e,\varepsilon}(d_{s})\geq\inf_{m\in M}\sup_{e\in E}\sum_{s\in S}e_{s}m(d_{s}).

This holds for all
\varepsilon>0
so the result
\bar{d}(\mu,\nu)\leq\sup_{e\in E}\inf_{m\in M}\sum_{s\in S}e_{s}m(d_{s})
follows.
Combining the above two results,

\bar{d}(\mu,\nu)=\sup_{e\in E}\inf_{m\in M}\sum_{s\in S}e_{s}m(d_{s}).

Thus, using linearity of integration and the definition of
c_{e}
,

(4)

\bar{d}(\mu,\nu)=\sup_{e\in E}\inf_{m\in M}m(c_{e}).

Thirdly, for all
e\in E
,
c_{e}
is a semi-metric on
X
, so by Kantorovich-Rubinstein duality, e.g. Theorem 5.10(i) of
[
Vi
]
,

(5)

\sup_{f\in F_{e}}(\mu-\nu)(f)=\inf_{m\in M}m(c_{e}).

Taking the supremum over
e\in E
and using equations (
3
,
4
) yields the desired result:

D(\mu,\nu)=\bar{d}(\mu,\nu).

∎

Appendix: Clarification of Appendix of
[
M2
]

We take the opportunity to clarify the Appendix to
[
M2
]
. For
c_{e}=\sum_{s\in S}e_{s}d_{s}
and
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}
, it addressed maximising
\mu(f)+\nu(g)
over pairs of functions
f,g:X\to\mathbb{R}
subject to
f(x)+g(y)\leq c_{e}(x,y)
for all
x,y\in X
. To use linear programming results, the discussion there was restricted to the case of
X
finite, but the analysis to follow here applies in full generality if maximum is replaced by supremum.

It was stated that “For fixed
e
, the maximum is attained by
g=-f
, by the Kantorovich-Rubinstein theorem applied to cost function
\sum_{s\in S}e_{s}d_{s}(x_{s},y_{s})
.” What was intended to be cited is the third inequality in Theorem 5.10(i) of
[
Vi
]
, combined with the statement there that one can impose
\psi
to be
c
-convex, and an extension of the Particular Case 5.4 of
[
Vi
]
to semi-metrics. The extension was sketched in parentheses at the end of Particular Case 5.4 of
[
Vi
]
, but we believe is missing a hypothesis, so we spell it out here.

Say
c:X\times X\to\mathbb{R}
is a
semi-metric
if
\forall x,y,z\in X,c(x,z)\leq c(x,y)+c(y,z)
and
c(x,x)=0
. Note that we do not require symmetry, nor non-negativity.
A function
\psi:X\to\mathbb{R}
is called
c
-convex
if there exists a function
\zeta:X\to\mathbb{R}
such that
\psi(x)=\sup_{y}(\zeta(y)-c(x,y))
.

\psi:X\to\mathbb{R}
is called
1-Lipschitz
(with respect to
c
) if for all
x,x^{\prime}\in X
,
\psi(x)-\psi(x^{\prime})\leq c(x^{\prime},x)
(note that by reversing the roles of
x,x^{\prime}
, it also implies
\psi(x)-\psi(x^{\prime})\geq-c(x,x^{\prime})
).
The
c
-transform
of a function
\psi:X\to\mathbb{R}
is the function
\psi^{c}(x)=\inf_{y}(\psi(y)+c(y,x))
.

Proposition 1
: If
c
is a semi-metric on
X
and
\psi:X\to\mathbb{R}
, then the following are equivalent:

(1)

\psi
is
c
-convex;

(2)

\psi
is 1-Lipschitz;

(3)

\psi^{c}=\psi
.

Proof
: Suppose
\psi
is
c
-convex. Then for all
\varepsilon>0
and
x\in X
there exists
y\in X
such that
\psi(x)\leq\zeta(y)-c(x,y)+\varepsilon
.
Also, for all
x^{\prime}\in X
,
\psi(x^{\prime})\geq\zeta(y)-c(x^{\prime},y)
, so
\psi(x)-\psi(x^{\prime})\leq c(x^{\prime},y)-c(x,y)+\varepsilon\leq c(x^{\prime},x)+\varepsilon
by the triangle inequality.
So for all
x,x^{\prime}\in X
,
\psi(x)-\psi(x^{\prime})\leq c(x^{\prime},x)
, which is the definition of
\psi
being 1-Lipschitz.

In the other direction, if
\psi
is 1-Lipschitz, then for all
x,y\in X
,
\psi(y)-c(x,y)\leq\psi(x)
so
\sup_{y}(\psi(y)-c(x,y))\leq\psi(x)
. But if
y=x
then
\psi(y)-c(x,y)=\psi(x)
, using
c(x,x)=0
. Thus
\sup_{y}(\psi(y)-c(x,y))=\psi(x)
, showing that
\psi
is
c
-convex with
\zeta=\psi
.

Next, suppose
\psi
is 1-Lipschitz. Then for all
x,y\in X
,
\psi(x)\leq\psi(y)+c(y,x)
. So
\psi(x)\leq\inf_{y}(\psi(y)+c(y,x))
. The right hand side is the definition of
\psi^{c}(x)
.
Inserting
y=x
and using
c(x,x)=0
we see also that
\psi(x)\geq\psi^{c}(x)
. So
\psi^{c}=\psi
.

Conversely, if
\psi=\psi^{c}
then for all
x\in X
,
\psi(x)=\inf_{y}(\psi(y)+c(y,x))
. Thus for all
\varepsilon>0
and
x\in X
, there exists
y
with
\psi(y)+c(y,x)\leq\psi(x)+\varepsilon
. Also, for all
x^{\prime}\in X
,
\psi(x^{\prime})\leq\psi(y)+c(y,x^{\prime})
. So
\psi(x^{\prime})-\psi(x)\leq c(y,x^{\prime})-c(y,x)+\varepsilon\leq c(x,x^{\prime})+\varepsilon
by the triangle inequality. Hence
\psi(x^{\prime})-\psi(x)\leq c(x,x^{\prime})
, so
\psi
is 1-Lipschitz.
∎

Then the third inequality of Theorem 5.10(i) of
[
Vi
]
allows one to replace
g
by
(-f)^{c}

(the correspondence with his notation is
\phi=g,\psi=-f
).
His statement there that one can impose that
\psi
be
c
-convex and the above proposition allow one to replace
(-f)^{c}
by
-f
. Hence one can take
g=-f
, as claimed.

Note that Prop.5.8 of
[
Vi
]
gives yet another equivalence to
c
-convexity:
\psi
is
c
-convex iff
\psi^{cc}=\psi
.

We now give a simple proof that does not refer to the Kantorovich-Rubinstein theorem, using only the above Proposition 1.

Proposition 2
: Given
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}
and semi-metric
c
on
X
, the supremum of
\mu(f)+\nu(g)
over pairs of functions
f,g:X\to\mathbb{R}
subject to
f(x)+g(y)\leq c(x,y)
for all
x,y\in X
is equal to the supremum over cases with
g=-f
.

Proof
: Firstly, the constraint
f(x)+g(y)\leq c(x,y)
implies that for all
y
,
g(y)\leq\inf_{x}(c(x,y)-f(x))
, which is the definition of
(-f)^{c}(y)
. So
\nu(g)\leq\nu((-f)^{c})
and for all
x,y\in X
,
f(x)+(-f)^{c}(y)\leq c(x,y)
. Thus replacing
g
by
(-f)^{c}
satisfies the constraint and does not decrease the objective function.

Secondly, by the same argument one can replace
f
by
\tilde{f}=(-g)^{c}
. But
-\tilde{f}
is
c
-convex, because
(-g)^{c}(x)=\inf_{y}(-g(y)+c(y,x))
iff
-(-g)^{c}(x)=\sup_{y}(g(y)-c(y,x))
. Hence, by Proposition 1,
(-\tilde{f})^{c}=-\tilde{f}
. So one can restrict to
f
satisfying
(-f)^{c}=-f
.

Combining these, one can restrict
g
to be
(-f)^{c}
and we can restrict
f
to be
(-(-f)^{c})^{c}=f^{c}
, so we can restrict to
g=-f
.
∎
