Introduction

The aim of this paper is to obtain optimal estimates, in terms of a one-dimensional eigenvalue problem, for the first Robin eigenvalue of the anisotropic
p
-Laplacian operator, namely:

\lambda_{1}(\beta,\Omega)=\min_{\psi\in W^{1,p}(\Omega)\setminus\{0\}}\frac{\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}F(\nabla\psi)^{p}dx+\beta\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Omega}|\psi|^{p}F(\nu_{\Omega})d\mathcal{H}^{N-1}}{\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}|\psi|^{p}dx},

(1.1)

where
p\in]1,+\infty[
,
\Omega
is a bounded,
C^{2}
domain in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
,
\nu_{\Omega}
is its Euclidean outward normal,
\beta
is a real number,
F:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to[0,+\infty[
,
N\geq 2
, is a convex, even,
1
-homogeneous and
C^{3,\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\{0\})
function such that
[F^{p}]_{\xi\xi}
is positive definite in
\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\{0\}
(we refer the reader to Section
2
for all the definitions, here and below used, related the Finsler metric
F
). When
\beta=0
,
\lambda_{1}(\beta,\Omega)=0
is the first (trivial) Neumann eigenvalue, while, when
\beta
goes to
+\infty
, it reduces to the first Dirichlet eigenvalue.

If
u\in W^{1,p}(\Omega)
is a minimizer of (
1.1
), then
u
satisfies

\begin{cases}-\mathcal{Q}_{p}u=\lambda_{1}(\beta,\Omega)\left|u\right|^{p-2}u\quad&\text{in}\ \Omega,\\
F(\nabla u)^{p-1}F_{\xi}(\nabla u)\cdot\nu_{\Omega}+\beta F(\nu_{\Omega})|u|^{p-2}u=0&\text{on}\ \partial\Omega,\end{cases}

(1.2)

where

\mathcal{Q}_{p}u=\dive(F^{p-1}(\nabla u)F_{\xi}(\nabla u)).

We remark that when
F(\xi)=\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\xi_{i}^{2}}
, the Euclidean norm, problem (
1.2
) corresponds to the classical eigenvalue problem for the Euclidean
p
-Laplace operator.

The problem of estimating (
1.1
) in terms of geometrical quantities related to
\Omega
is well studied; actually, there is a striking difference between the cases
\beta\geq 0
and
\beta<0
.

For positive values of the parameter
\beta
, if
\Omega
is a bounded Lipschitz open set of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, a Faber-Krahn inequality holds:

\lambda_{1}(\beta,\Omega)\geq\lambda_{1}(\beta,\mathcal{W})

where
\mathcal{W}
is the so-called Wulff shape, with the same Lebesgue measure of
\Omega
. We refer the reader to
[
7
]
and the references therein contained.
Furthermore, in
[
7
]
it has been also proved that, in the class of convex sets,
an estimate in terms of the anisotropic inradius of
\Omega
(that is the radius of the greatest Wulff shape that can be contained in
\Omega
), denoted by
R_{F}(\Omega)
, can be proved. Indeed, if
\Omega
is a convex, bounded domain of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, it holds that

\lambda_{1}(\beta,\Omega)\geq\left(\frac{p-1}{p}\right)^{p}\frac{\beta}{R_{F}(\Omega)\left(1+\beta^{\frac{1}{p-1}}R_{F}(\Omega)\right)^{p-1}}

(see also
[
17
]
for
p=2
in the Euclidean case). Actually, this estimate is far to be optimal. On the other hand, in the Dirichlet case (i.e. with
\beta=+\infty
) a sharp estimate holds for smooth bounded anisotropic mean convex domains, that is with nonnegative anisotropic mean curvature:

\lambda_{1}^{D}(\Omega)\geq(p-1)\left(\frac{\pi_{p}}{2}\right)^{p}\frac{1}{R_{F}(\Omega)^{p}}

(1.3)

where
\lambda_{1}^{D}(\Omega)=\lim_{\beta\to+\infty}\lambda_{1}(\beta,\Omega)
is the first Dirichlet eigenvalue of
\mathcal{Q}_{p}
, and

\pi_{p}=2\int_{0}^{1}(1-t^{p})^{-\frac{1}{p}}dt=\frac{2\pi}{p\sin\frac{\pi}{p}}.

Moreover, the estimate (
1.3
) is optimal on a suitable infinite slab. Inequality (
1.3
) is a generalization, for a large class of nonlinear operators, of a well-known inequality for the first Dirichlet eigenvalue (
p=2
) of planar convex domains in the Euclidean Laplacian case, proved by Hersch
[
16
]
, and then generalized by Protter
[
28
]
in the case
N\geq 2
. The Finsler-Laplacian case has been considered in
[
4
]
for
p=2
and convex domains, while the general case with
1<p<+\infty
and
\Omega
anisotropic mean convex in
[
9
]
, as well as the optimality of the inequality.

Various other kind of estimates, in terms of geometrical quantities depending on
\Omega
, can be given for the eigenvalues of nonlinear operators with Robin boundary conditions, Neumann conditions, as well as in “holed” domains with different conditions on the various components of the boundary. We refer the reader, for example, to
[
10
,
11
,
25
]
and the references therein contained.

For negative values of the parameter
\beta
, the situation is very different. For
p=2
and in the Euclidean setting, in
[
1
]
, Bareket conjectured that, among all smooth bounded domains of given volume, the first eigenvalue is maximized when
\Omega
is a ball. The conjecture remained open for a long time. In
[
12
]
it has been showed that it is true for domains close, in a suitable sense, to a ball. Moreover, the conjecture has been disproved in
[
13
]
for
p=2
, and in
[
18
]
for
p\in]1,+\infty[
, for
\left|\beta\right|
large enough. Furthermore, in
[
13
]
it was proved that, in two dimensions and
p=2
, the conjecture is true for small values of
\left|\beta\right|
, and in
[
18
]
, in any dimension and
1<p<+\infty
for starshaped domains, always for
\left|\beta\right|
small. Actually, the optimal value of
\beta
is still unknown. As regards the Finsler setting, in
[
26
]
it has been showed that for
\left|\beta\right|
small the Wulff shape, among all the sufficiently smooth domains with given volume, is a maximizer.

The aim of this paper is to obtain several estimates for
\lambda_{1}(\beta,\Omega)
in terms of geometric quantities related to
\Omega
, both in the cases
\beta\geq 0
and
\beta<0
.

For
\beta\geq 0
, we prove an optimal lower bound for
\lambda_{1}(\beta,\Omega)
, with
\Omega
smooth, bounded domain, which is anisotropic mean convex (that is, with nonnegative anisotropic mean curvature
\mathcal{H}_{F}
, see Section 2.2 for the precise definition), in terms of the first eigenvalue of a nonlinear, one dimensional problem.
As a consequence, we obtain an estimate in terms of the anisotropic inradius of
\Omega
, which reduces to (
1.3
) when
\beta
tends to
+\infty
. The approach we adopt makes use of the
\mathcal{P}
-function method, well known in the classical Euclidean case for the Laplace operator (see
[
31
]
and the references therein) and recently considered in the Finsler case, for example, in
[
6
,
8
,
9
]
.

More precisely, we prove the following result.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\Omega
be a bounded
C^{2}
domain in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
,
N\geq 2
, such that
\partial\Omega
has nonnegative anisotropic mean curvature
\mathcal{H}_{F}
, and let be
\beta>0
. Then

\lambda_{1}(\beta,\Omega)\geq\mu_{1}(\beta,s_{0})

(1.4)

where
\mu_{1}(\beta,s_{0})
is the first positive root of

\frac{\mu}{p-1}=\frac{\beta^{p^{\prime}}}{\cos_{p}^{-p}\left(\left(\frac{\mu}{p-1}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}s_{0}\right)-1}.

with
p^{\prime}=\frac{p}{p-1}
,
s_{0}=(p^{\prime}M(\Omega))^{\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}}
, and
M(\Omega)
is the maximum value of the stress function
w_{\Omega}
, that is the solution of

\begin{cases}-\mathcal{Q}_{p}w_{\Omega}=1\quad&\text{in}\ \Omega,\\
w_{\Omega}=0&\text{on}\ \partial\Omega.\end{cases}

(1.5)

Moreover, the estimate (
1.4
) is optimal for a suitable infinite slab.

The definition of the generalized trigonometric function
\cos_{p}
will be recalled in Section
3
.

Inequality (
1.4
) was first proved, when
F
is the Euclidean norm and
p=2
, by Sperb in
[
32
]
.
The value
\mu_{1}(\beta,s_{0})
is the first eigenvalue of a suitable nonlinear one dimensional eigenvalue problem, namely

\begin{cases}(|X^{\prime}|^{p-2}X^{\prime})^{\prime}+\mu_{1}(\beta,s_{0})|X|^{p-2}X=0\quad\text{in}\ (0,s_{0}),\\
|X^{\prime}(0)|^{p-2}X^{\prime}(0)=0,\\
|X^{\prime}(s_{0})|^{p-2}X^{\prime}(s_{0})+\beta|X(s_{0})|^{p-2}X(s_{0})=0,\end{cases}

(1.6)

where
s_{0}=(p^{\prime}M(\Omega))^{\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}}
(this problem is studied in Section 3). The inequality (
1.4
) was not known also in the Euclidean case, when
p\neq 2
; we mention that optimal estimates of
\lambda_{1}(\beta,\Omega)
with respect to the first eigenvalue of a one dimensional problem have been obtained by
[
30
]
for the case of the Euclidean Laplacian eigenvalue, and
[
22
]
for the
p
-Laplacian, both on compact manifolds. In these cases, when
\Omega
is a domain of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, our estimate (
1.4
) improves the quoted results in
[
22
,
30
]
. Moreover, the class of the operators we consider is very general and can be highly nonlinear.

Furthermore, Theorem
1.1
allows to achieve an estimate in terms of the inradius
R_{F}(\Omega)
, by using the following bounds for the maximum
M(\Omega)
of the Saint-Venant torsional rigidity problem and the anisotropic inradius
R_{F}(\Omega)
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\Omega
be a bounded
C^{2}
domain in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
,
N\geq 2
, such that
\partial\Omega
has nonnegative anisotropic mean curvature
\mathcal{H}_{F}
. Then

\frac{1}{N^{p^{\prime}-1}}\frac{R_{F}^{p^{\prime}}(\Omega)}{p^{\prime}}\leq M(\Omega)\leq\frac{R_{F}^{p^{\prime}}(\Omega)}{p^{\prime}}.

Moreover, the right-hand side inequality is optimal, as
\Omega
approaches a suitable infinite
N
-dimensional slab; the left-hand side inequality holds as an equality if and only if
\Omega
is a Wulff shape.

The upper bound, in the case of planar Euclidean Laplacian, goes back to a result proved in
[
27
]
; the two inequalities in the general
p
-Finsler case were proved in
[
8
]
.

Then as a corollary of Theorem
1.1
, we obtain the following explicit estimate of
\lambda_{1}(\beta,\Omega)
in terms of
R_{F}(\Omega)
.

Corollary 1.3
.

Under the same hypotheses of Theorem
1.1
, it holds that

\lambda_{1}(\beta,\Omega)\geq(p-1)\left(\frac{\pi_{p}}{2}\right)^{p}\frac{1}{\left(R_{F}(\Omega)+\frac{\pi_{p}}{2}\beta^{-\frac{1}{p-1}}\right)^{p}},

(1.7)

where
R_{F}(\Omega)
is the anisotropic inradius of
\Omega
.

Incidentally, the inequality (
1.7
) reduces to (
1.3
) as
\beta\to+\infty
, since
\lambda_{1}(\beta,\Omega)
converges to the first Dirichlet eigenvalue.

An estimate of the type (
1.7
) has been given also in
[
30
]
in the Euclidean,
p=2
case, when
\Omega
is a compact Riemannian manifold with smooth boundary and nonnegative Ricci curvature of
\Omega
and mean curvature of
\partial\Omega
.

In the case
\beta<0
, we get the following upper bound.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
\Omega
be a bounded
C^{2}
domain in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
,
N\geq 2
, with nonnegative anisotropic mean curvature
\mathcal{H}_{F}
of
\partial\Omega
, and let
\beta<0
.
Then

\lambda_{1}(\beta,\Omega)\leq\mu_{1}(\beta,R_{F}(\Omega))

(1.8)

where
\mu_{1}(\beta,R_{F}(\Omega))
is the unique negative value that solves

-\frac{\mu}{{p-1}}=\frac{|\beta|^{p^{\prime}}}{1-\cosh_{p}^{-p}\left(\left(\frac{-\mu}{p-1}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}R_{F}(\Omega)\right)}.

Moreover, the estimate (
1.8
) is optimal for a suitable slab.

The value
\mu_{1}(\beta,R_{F}(\Omega))
is the first eigenvalue of (
1.6
) when
\beta<0
and

.

Finally, by using Theorem
1.4
we get the following estimate for
\lambda_{1}
in terms of
\beta
.

Corollary 1.5
.

Under the hypotheses of Theorem
1.4
, it holds that

\lambda_{1}(\beta,\Omega)\leq(1-p)|\beta|^{\frac{p}{p-1}}.

(1.9)

The estimate (
1.9

[
14

]
for

[

for

2
Notation and preliminaries

Let
1<p<+\infty
; throughout this paper we will denote by
p^{\prime}
the conjugate exponent of
p
, that is the number such that
\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}=1
. Moreover, we will call a domain of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
a connected open set.

2.1
The Finsler norm

Throughout the paper we will consider a convex, even,
1-
homogeneous function

\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\mapsto F(\xi)\in[0,+\infty[,

that is a convex function such that

F(t\xi)=|t|F(\xi),\quad t\in\mathbb{R},\,\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{N},

(2.1)

and such that

a|\xi|\leq F(\xi),\quad\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{N},

(2.2)

for some constant
a>0
. Under this hypothesis it is easy to see that there exists
b\geq a
such that

F(\xi)\leq b|\xi|,\quad\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{N}.

Throughout the paper we will also assume that
F
belongs to
C^{3,\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\{0\})
, and that

\nabla^{2}_{\xi}[F^{p}](\xi)\text{ is positive definite in }\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\{0\}.

(2.3)

The hypothesis (
2.3
) on
F
ensures that the operator

\Qp[u]:=\dive\left(\frac{1}{p}\nabla_{\xi}[F^{p}](\nabla u)\right)

is elliptic, hence there exists a positive constant
\gamma
such that

\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}{\nabla^{2}_{\xi_{i}\xi_{j}}[F^{p}](\eta)\xi_{i}\xi_{j}}\geq\gamma|\eta|^{p-2}|\xi|^{2},

for some positive constant
\gamma
, for any
\eta\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\{0\}
and for any
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
.

For
p\geq 2
, the condition

\nabla^{2}_{\xi}[F^{2}](\xi)\text{ is positive definite in }\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\{0\},

is sufficient for the validity of (
2.3
).

The polar function
F^{o}\colon\mathbb{R}^{N}\rightarrow[0,+\infty[

of
F
is defined as

F^{o}(v)=\sup_{\xi\neq 0}\frac{\langle\xi,v\rangle}{F(\xi)}.

It is easy to verify that also
F^{o}
is a convex function
which satisfies properties (
2.1
) and
(
2.2
). Furthermore,

F(v)=\sup_{\xi\neq 0}\frac{\langle\xi,v\rangle}{F^{o}(\xi)}.

From the above property it holds that

|\xi\cdot\eta|\leq F(\xi)F^{o}(\eta)\qquad\forall\xi,\eta\in\mathbb{R}^{N}.

(2.4)

The set

\mathcal{W}=\{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\colon F^{o}(\xi)<1\}

is the so-called Wulff shape centered at the origin. We put

\kappa_{N}=|\mathcal{W}|
, where
|\mathcal{W}|
denotes the Lebesgue measure
of
\mathcal{W}
. More generally, we denote with
\mathcal{W}_{r}(x_{0})

the set
r\mathcal{W}+x_{0}
, that is the Wulff shape centered at
x_{0}

with measure
\kappa_{N}r^{N}
, and
\mathcal{W}_{r}(0)=\mathcal{W}_{r}
.

The following properties of
F
and
F^{o}
hold true:

\begin{array}[]{ll}F_{\xi}(\xi)\cdot\xi=F(\xi),\quad F_{\xi}^{o}(\xi)\cdot\xi=F^{o}(\xi),&\forall\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\{0\},\\[4.26773pt]
F(F_{\xi}^{o}(\xi))=F^{o}(F_{\xi}(\xi))=1,&\forall\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\{0\},\\[4.26773pt]
F^{o}(\xi)F_{\xi}(F_{\xi}^{o}(\xi))=F(\xi)F_{\xi}^{o}(F_{\xi}(\xi))=\xi&\forall\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\{0\},\end{array}

where
F_{\xi}=\nabla F(\xi)
. The anisotropic distance of
x\in\overline{\Omega}
to the boundary of a bounded domain
\Omega
is the function

d_{F}(x)=\inf_{y\in\partial\Omega}F^{o}(x-y),\quad x\in\overline{\Omega}.

We stress that when
F=|\cdot|
then
d_{F}=d_{\mathcal{E}}
, the Euclidean distance function from the boundary.

It is not difficult to prove that
d_{F}
is a uniform Lipschitz function in
\overline{\Omega}
and

F(\nabla d_{F}(x))=1\quad\text{a.e. in }\Omega.

Obviously,
d_{F}\in W_{0}^{1,\infty}(\Omega)
.
Finally, the anisotropic inradius of
\Omega
is the quantity

R_{F}(\Omega)=\max\{d_{F}(x),\;x\in\overline{\Omega}\},

that is the radius of the largest Wulff shape
\mathcal{W}_{r}(x)
contained in
\Omega
.

2.2
Anisotropic curvatures

We recall here some basic facts on anisotropic curvatures and on an integration formula in anisotropic normal coordinates, referring the reader mainly to
[
5
]
for the details.

Let
\Omega
be a
C^{2}
bounded domain, and
x\in\partial\Omega
.
The anisotropic outer normal
n^{F}_{\Omega}
to
\partial\Omega
is given by

n^{F}_{\Omega}(y)=F_{\xi}(\nu_{\Omega}(y)),\qquad y\in\partial\Omega,

where
\nu_{\Omega}(y)
is the Euclidean outer normal to
\partial\Omega
at
y
.
It holds

F^{o}(n^{F}_{\Omega})=1.

The anisotropic principal curvatures
\kappa^{F}_{1},\ldots,\kappa_{N-1}^{F}
are defined as the eigenvalues of the anisotropic Weingarten map

dn^{F}_{\Omega}\colon T_{y}\partial\Omega\to T_{n_{\Omega}^{F}(y)}\mathcal{W},

where
T_{y}\partial\Omega
is the tangent space to
\partial\Omega
at
y
(see
[
34
]
). The anisotropic mean curvature of
\partial\Omega
at a point
y
is defined as

\mathcal{H}_{F}(y)=\kappa_{1}^{F}(y)+\ldots\kappa_{N-1}^{F}(y),\quad y\in\partial\Omega.

A domain such that
\mathcal{H}_{F}(y)\geq 0
for any
y\in\partial\Omega
is said to be anisotropic mean convex.

Actually, the anisotropic principal curvatures can be equivalently defined as follows (
[
5
]
, in particular Remark 5.9). Let us recall that the anisotropic distance function
d_{F}
is
C^{2}
in a tubular neighborhood of
\partial\Omega
, hence we can define the matrix-valued function

W(y)=-F_{\xi\xi}(\nabla d_{\Omega}(y))\nabla^{2}d_{\Omega}(y),\quad y\in\partial\Omega.

It holds that for any
v\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
,
W(y)v
belongs to
T_{y}
. Then it is possible to define the map
\overline{W}(y)\colon T_{y}\to T_{y}
, as
\overline{W}(y)w=W(y)w
,
w\in T_{y}
.
The matrix
\overline{W}(y)
is, in general, not symmetric, but the eigenvalues are real numbers. These eigenvalues
\kappa_{1}^{F}(y)\leq\kappa_{2}^{F}(y)\leq\ldots\leq\kappa_{N-1}^{F}(y)
are the anisotropic principal curvatures of
\partial\Omega
at the point
y
, and the definition is equivalent to the preceding one. Then it holds that

\mathcal{H}_{F}(y)=\dive\left[F_{\xi}\left(-{\nabla d_{F}(y)}\right)\right]=\tr(W(y)).

(see also
[
34
, Section 3]
).

One of the tools that will be used in what follows, is a change of variable formula in anisotropic normal coordinates, based on the fact that the set of the singular points of the anisotropic distance function from the boundary has Lebesgue measure zero (
[
5
]
). To state this result, let us define the function

\Phi(y,t)=y-tF_{\xi}(\nu_{\Omega}(y)),\qquad y\in\partial\Omega,\quad t\in\mathbb{R},

and the function
\ell(y)
,
y\in\partial\Omega
,

\ell(y)=\sup\{d_{F}(z),\,z\in\Omega\text{ and }y\in\Pi(z))\},

where
\Pi(z)=\{\eta\in\partial\Omega\colon d_{F}(z)=F^{o}(z-\eta)\}
is the set of projections of
z
in
\partial\Omega
.

Theorem 2.1
.

[
5
, Theorem 7.1]
.
For every
h\in L^{1}(\Omega)
, it holds

\int_{\Omega}h(x)dx=\int_{\partial\Omega}F(\nu_{\Omega}(y))\int_{0}^{\ell(y)}h(\Phi(y,t))J(y,t)\,dt\,d\mathcal{H}^{N-1}(y),

where
J(y,t)=\Pi_{i=1}^{N-1}(1-t\kappa^{F}_{i}(y))
.

The Jacobian of
\Phi
, that is
J(y,t)=\Pi_{i=1}^{N-1}(1-t\kappa^{F}_{i}(y))
, is positive, being
1-t\kappa^{F}_{i}(y)>0
for any
i=1,\ldots,N-1
(
[
5
, Lemma 5.4]
), and if
\Omega
is anisotropic mean convex it holds that

\frac{-\frac{d}{dt}\left[J(y,t)\right]}{J(y,t)}=\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}\frac{\kappa^{F}_{i}(y)}{1-t\kappa^{F}_{i}(y)}\geq\frac{\mathcal{H}_{F}(y)}{1-\frac{\mathcal{H}_{F}(y)}{N-1}t}\geq 0\qquad\forall y\in\partial\Omega,\;t\in[0,\ell(y)[.

(2.5)

The equality in (
2.5
) is a straightforward computation, while the first inequality follows by applying the Newton inequalities exactly as done in
[
21
, Proposition 2.6]
.

To conclude this subsection, we recall an observation that will be used in the following. For any
x\in\Omega
such that
\Pi(x)=\{y\}
, we have (
[
5
, Lemma 4.3]
) that

\nabla d_{F}(x)=-\frac{\nu_{\Omega}(y)}{F(\nu_{\Omega}(y))}.

(2.6)

2.3
The first eigenvalue of the Robin Finsler
p
-Laplacian

In this section we review an existence and characterization result for the first eigenpair of the Robin Finsler
p-
Laplacian. Recall that, in the case
\beta>0
, the existence result has been proved for example in
[
7
]
; for the negative values of the boundary parameter, it follows by adapting the proof of
[
18
]
for the Euclidean
p
-Laplacian.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
1<p<+\infty
,
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
, and let
\Omega
be a bounded Lipschitz domain in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
. Then there exists a function
u\in C^{1,\alpha}(\Omega)
which realizes the minimum in (
1.1
) and satisfies problem (
1.2
).
Moreover, the first eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}(\beta,\Omega)
of (
1.2
) is simple and the corresponding eigenfunctions are positive (or negative) in
\Omega
.

Proof.

For any
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
, let us consider
\{u_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
a minimizing normalized sequence for
\lambda_{1}(\beta,\Omega)
, that is

\lim_{n\to+\infty}\int_{\Omega}F(\nabla u_{n})^{p}dx+\beta\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Omega}|u_{n}|^{p}F(\nu_{\Omega})d\mathcal{H}^{N-1}=\lambda_{1}(\beta,\Omega),\\
\text{with}\int_{\Omega}|u_{n}|^{p}dx=1\ \forall n\in\mathbb{N}.

(2.7)

For any
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, there exists a constant
K_{\varepsilon}>0
(see
[
15
, Th. 1.5.1.10]
) such that

\int_{\partial\Omega}|u_{n}|^{p}dx\leq\varepsilon\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u_{n}|^{p}dx+K_{\varepsilon}\int_{\Omega}|u_{n}|^{p}dx\quad\forall n\in\mathbb{N}.

(2.8)

By using (
2.2
), (
2.7
) and (
2.8
) for suitable choice of
\varepsilon
depending on
\beta
, we deduce that

\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u_{n}|^{p}dx<+\infty.

Therefore
\{u_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is bounded in
W^{1,p}(\Omega)
and hence there exists a subsequence
\{u_{n_{j}}\}
and
u\in W^{1,p}(\Omega)
such that
u_{n_{j}}\to u
strongly in
L^{p}(\Omega)
and almost everywhere, and
\nabla u_{n_{j}}\rightharpoonup\nabla u
weakly in
L^{p}(\Omega)
. As a consequence, by the compactness of the
trace operator (see for example
[
23
, Cor. 18.4]
),
u_{n_{j}}
(up to another subsequence) strongly converges to
u
in
L^{p}(\partial\Omega)
.

Hence, the existence of the minimizer of (
1.1
) follows by the lower semicontinuity:

\begin{split}\lambda_{1}(\beta,\Omega)&\leq\int_{\Omega}F(\nabla u)^{p}dx+\beta\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Omega}|u|^{p}F(\nu_{\Omega})d\mathcal{H}^{N-1}\\
&\leq\liminf_{j\to+\infty}\int_{\Omega}F(\nabla u_{n_{j}})^{p}dx+\beta\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Omega}|u_{n_{j}}|^{p}F(\nu_{\Omega})d\mathcal{H}^{N-1}=\lambda_{1}(\beta,\Omega).\end{split}

Now, let us observe that if
u
is a minimizer of (
1.1
), then
|u|
is also a minimizer, so we can assume
u\geq 0
. Moreover,
u
is in
L^{\infty}(\bar{\Omega})
(see
[
7
]
if
\beta\geq 0
, or argue as in
[
20
, Theorem 4.3]
if
\beta<0
). Hence by Harnack inequality,
u>0
in
\Omega
. Moreover, by standard regularity results (see for example
[
33
]
),
u\in C^{1,\alpha}(\Omega)
. Finally, to prove the simplicity we follow an argument contained in
[
3
]
. So, let fix two positive eigenfunctions
u
and
v
, normalized as
\|u\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}=\|v\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}=1
, and define
w_{t}=\left(tu^{p}+(1-t)v^{p}\right)^{1/p}
, with
t\in[0,1]
. We have that
\|w_{t}\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}=1
. Then, by the convexity of
F^{p}
, it holds

\begin{split}F(\nabla w_{t})^{p}&=w_{t}^{p}F\left(\frac{tu^{p}\frac{\nabla u}{u}+(1-t)v^{p}\frac{\nabla v}{v}}{tu^{p}+(1-t)v^{p}}\right)^{p}\\
&\leq\mu_{t}^{p}\left[\frac{tu^{p}}{tu^{p}+(1-t)v^{p}}F\left(\frac{\nabla u}{u}\right)^{p}+\frac{(1-t)v^{p}}{tu^{p}+(1-t)v^{p}}F\left(\frac{\nabla v}{v}\right)^{p}\right]\\
&=tF(\nabla u)^{p}+(1-t)F(\nabla v)^{p}.\end{split}

(2.9)

By using (
2.9
) and the fact that
u
and
v
are both minimizers, we obtain

\begin{split}\lambda_{1}(\beta,\Omega)&\leq\int_{\Omega}F(\nabla w_{t})^{p}dx+\beta\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Omega}w_{t}^{p}F(\nu_{\Omega})d\mathcal{H}^{N-1}\\
&\leq t\left(\int_{\Omega}F(\nabla u)^{p}dx+\beta\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Omega}u^{p}F(\nu_{\Omega})d\mathcal{H}^{N-1}\right)\\
&+(1-t)\left(\int_{\Omega}F(\nabla v)^{p}dx+\beta\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Omega}v^{p}F(\nu_{\Omega})d\mathcal{H}^{N-1}\right)\\
&=\lambda_{1}(\beta,\Omega)\end{split}

So
w_{t}
is a minimizer for (
1.1
) and the inequality (
2.9
) holds as equality. Therefore
\frac{\nabla u}{u}=\frac{\nabla v}{v}
and, consequently,
\nabla(\log u-\log v)=0
, that implies the result.
∎

2.4
The
\mathcal{P}
-function method

In order to give an optimal lower estimate for
\lambda_{1}(\beta,\Omega)

in the case
\beta>0
, we will use the so-called
\mathcal{P}
-function method.
Let us consider the Dirichlet boundary value problem

\begin{cases}-\mathcal{Q}_{p}w=f(w)&\text{in }\Omega\\
w=0&\text{on }\partial\Omega,\end{cases}

(2.10)

where
f
is a nonnegative
C^{1}(0,+\infty)\cap C^{0}([0,+\infty[)
function. We recall the following result, based on
[
6
, Prop. 4.1]
.

Theorem 2.3

(
[
8
,
9
]
)
.

Let
\Omega
be a bounded
C^{2}
domain in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
,
N\geq 2
, with nonnegative anisotropic mean curvature
\mathcal{H}_{F}
of
\partial\Omega
, and let
w>0
be a solution to the problem (
2.10
),
then

\mathcal{P}(x):=\frac{p-1}{p}F^{p}(\nabla w(x))-\int_{w(x)}^{\max_{\bar{\Omega}}w}f(s)ds\leq 0\quad\text{ in }\overline{\Omega}.

Remark 2.4
.

Theorem
2.3
implies that if
f=1
and then
w=w_{\Omega}\in W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega)
is the stress function for
\Omega
, that is the solution of (
1.5
) then

F(\nabla w_{\Omega}(x))\leq\left[\frac{p}{p-1}(M(\Omega)-w_{\Omega}(x))\right]^{\frac{1}{p}}

(2.11)

where
M(\Omega)=\max_{\Omega}w_{\Omega}
.

3
A one dimensional
p
-Laplacian eigenvalue problem

In this Section, we study a one-dimensional eigenvalue problem that will be useful to estimate
\lambda_{1}(\beta,\Omega)
for both positive and negative values of the boundary parameter.

We first briefly summarize the definitions and some properties of the
p
-trigonometric and
p
-hyperbolic functions (see for example to
[
19
,
24
,
29
]
). We observe that these functions coincide with the standard trigonometric and hyperbolic functions when
p=2
.

Let us consider the function
\arccos_{p}:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
defined as

\arccos_{p}(x)=\displaystyle\int_{x}^{1}\frac{dt}{\displaystyle\left(1-{t^{p}}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}}.

Denote by
z(t)
the inverse function of
\arccos_{p}
which is defined on the interval
\left[0,\frac{\pi_{p}}{2}\right]
, where

\pi_{p}=2\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\frac{dt}{\displaystyle\left(1-{t^{p}}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}}=\frac{2\pi}{p\sin\frac{\pi}{p}}.

Therefore, the
p
-cosine function
\cos_{p}
is the even function defined as the following periodic extension of
z(t)
:

\cos_{p}(t)=\left\{\begin{split}&z(t)&&\text{if}\ \ t\in\left[0,\frac{\pi_{p}}{2}\right],\\
&-z(\pi_{p}-t)&&\text{if}\ \ t\in\left[\frac{\pi_{p}}{2},\pi_{p}\right],\\
&\cos_{p}(-t)\ &&\text{if}\ \ t\in\left[-\pi_{p},0\right],\\
\end{split}\right.

and extended periodically to all
\mathbb{R}
, with period
2\pi_{p}
; the extension is continuosly differentiable on
\mathbb{R}
. If
p=2
, then
\cos_{p}x
and
\arccos_{p}x
coincides with the standard trigonometric functions
\cos x
and
\arccos x
.

Let now also recall what the generalized hyperbolic cosine and arccosine functions are. The function
\arccosh_{p}
is defined as

\arccosh_{p}(x)=\displaystyle\int_{1}^{x}\frac{1}{(t^{p}-1)^{\frac{1}{p}}}dt,\ x\in[1,+\infty[.

Then
\cosh_{p}\colon t\in[0,+\infty[\mapsto[1,+\infty[
is its inverse function. It is strictly increasing in
[0,+\infty[
; it can be extended on all
\mathbb{R}
as
\cosh_{p}(-t)=\cosh_{p}(t)
,
t>0
.
If
p=2
,
\cosh_{p}
and
\arccosh_{p}
coincides with the standard hyperbolic functions.

Now we consider the following eigenvalue problem in the unknown
X=X(s)
:

\begin{cases}(|X^{\prime}|^{p-2}X^{\prime})^{\prime}+\mu|X|^{p-2}X=0\quad\text{in}\ (0,s_{0}),\\
|X^{\prime}(0)|^{p-2}X^{\prime}(0)=0,\\
|X^{\prime}(s_{0})|^{p-2}X^{\prime}(s_{0})+\beta|X(s_{0})|^{p-2}X(s_{0})=0,\end{cases}

(3.1)

where
s_{0}
is a given positive number.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
1<p<+\infty
and
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
. Then there exists the smallest eigenvalue
\mu
of (
3.1
), which has the following variational characterization:

\mu_{1}(\beta,s_{0})=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in W^{1,p}(0,s_{0})\\
\left|v^{\prime}(0)\right|^{p-2}v^{\prime}(0)=0\end{subarray}}\frac{\int_{0}^{s_{0}}\left|v^{\prime}(s)\right|^{p}ds+\beta v(s_{0})^{p}}{\int_{0}^{s_{0}}\left|v(s)\right|^{p}ds}.

Moreover, the corresponding eigenfunctions are unique up to a multiplicative constant and have constant sign. The first eigenvalue
\mu_{1}(\beta,s_{0})
has the sign of
\beta
. Moreover if
\beta>0
, the first eigenfunction is

X(s)=\cos_{p}\left(\left(\frac{\mu_{1}(\beta,s_{0})}{p-1}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}s\right),\quad s\in(0,s_{0})

and the eigenvalue
\mu_{1}(\beta,s_{0})
is the first positive value that satisfies

\frac{\mu}{p-1}=\frac{\beta^{p^{\prime}}}{\cos_{p}^{-p}\left(\left(\frac{\mu}{p-1}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}s_{0}\right)-1}.

If
\beta<0
, the first eigenfunction is

X(s)=\cosh_{p}\left(\left(\frac{-\mu_{1}(\beta,s_{0})}{p-1}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}s\right),\quad s\in(0,s_{0})

and in this case
\mu_{1}(\beta,s_{0})
is the unique negative value that satisfies

-\frac{\mu}{p-1}=\frac{|\beta|^{p^{\prime}}}{1-\cosh_{p}^{-p}\left(\left(\frac{-\mu}{p-1}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}s_{0}\right)}.

Proof.

By standard arguments of Calculus of Variations, it is possible to show the existence of the first eigenvalue
\mu_{1}(\beta,s_{0})
in correspondence with a unique (up to a constant) positive solution of (
3.1
), that we denote by
X
. Moreover,
\mu_{1}(\beta,s_{0})
has the sign of
\beta
, and integrating (
3.1
) we get

|X^{\prime}(s)|^{p-2}X^{\prime}(s)=-\mu_{1}(\beta,s_{0})\int_{0}^{s}X^{p-1}dt.

(3.2)

Moreover, by multiplying the first line of (
3.1
) for
X^{\prime}
and integrating from
0
to
s
, we have

\int_{0}^{s}(|X^{\prime}|^{p-2}X^{\prime})^{\prime}X^{\prime}dt+\mu_{1}(\beta,s_{0})\int_{0}^{s}X^{p-1}X^{\prime}dt=0

and hence

\frac{|X^{\prime}(s)|^{p}}{p^{\prime}}+\frac{\mu_{1}(\beta,s_{0})}{p}X(s)^{p}=\frac{\mu_{1}(\beta,s_{0})}{p}X(0)^{p}\qquad\text{in}\ (0,s_{0}).

(3.3)

Now, let
\beta>0
. By (
3.2
),
X
is decreasing; we fix
X(0)=1
. Therefore, from (
3.3
), we have

X^{\prime}=-\tilde{\mu}^{\frac{1}{p}}(1-X^{p})^{\frac{1}{p}},

(3.4)

where
\tilde{\mu}=\frac{\mu_{1}(\beta,s_{0})}{p-1}
. By integrating (
3.4
) from
0
to
s
, we have

\arccos_{p}\left(X(s)\right)=\displaystyle\int_{X(s)}^{1}\frac{1}{(1-X^{p})^{\frac{1}{p}}}dX=\tilde{\mu}^{\frac{1}{p}}s

(3.5)

Then the solution of problem (
3.1
) is

X(s)=\cos_{p}\left(\tilde{\mu}^{\frac{1}{p}}s\right).

(3.6)

Finally, from the boundary condition in (
3.1
), we have
X^{\prime}(s_{0})=-\beta^{\frac{1}{p-1}}X(s_{0})
, that, by considering (
3.4
) and (
3.6
), gives

\frac{\tilde{\mu}}{\beta^{p^{\prime}}}=\frac{1}{\cos_{p}^{-p}\left(\tilde{\mu}^{\frac{1}{p}}s_{0}\right)-1}.

When
\beta<0
, then
X>0
is increasing and defined up to a multiplicative constant, we set the minimum
X(0)=1
. From (
3.3
), we have

X^{\prime}=(-\tilde{\mu})^{\frac{1}{p}}(X^{p}-1)^{\frac{1}{p}}.

(3.7)

By integrating from
0
to
s
, we have

\arccosh_{p}\left(X(s)\right)=\displaystyle\int_{1}^{X(s)}\frac{1}{(X^{p}-1)^{\frac{1}{p}}}dX=(-\tilde{\mu})^{\frac{1}{p}}s.

Therefore, the solution of (
3.1
), for
\beta<0
, is

X(s)=\cosh_{p}\left((-\tilde{\mu})^{\frac{1}{p}}s\right).

(3.8)

Moreover, from the boundary condition in (
3.1
), we have
X^{\prime}({s_{0}})=|\beta|^{\frac{1}{p-1}}X({s_{0}})
that, by considering (
3.7
) and (
3.8
), gives

\frac{-\tilde{\mu}}{|\beta|^{p^{\prime}}}=\frac{1}{1-\cosh_{p}^{-p}\left((-\tilde{\mu})^{\frac{1}{p}}s_{0}\right)}.

(3.9)

We observe that, being the function
\cosh_{p}(t)
strictly monotone increasing as
t>0
, and
\cosh_{p}(0)=1
,
\cosh_{p}(t)\to+\infty
as
t\to+\infty
, then

\frac{x}{|\beta|^{p^{\prime}}}\left[1-\cosh_{p}^{-p}\left(x^{\frac{1}{p}}s_{0}\right)\right]=1
has always a unique positive solution
x
.
∎

Remark 3.2
.

Since
X
is decreasing when
\beta>0
, it holds that
0\leq X\leq 1
. Hence
0\leq\arccos_{p}\left(X(s)\right)\leq\frac{\pi_{p}}{2}
. Therefore, from (
3.5
), we have
0\leq\tilde{\mu}^{\frac{1}{p}}s\leq\frac{\pi_{p}}{2}
, and then

\mu_{1}(\beta,s_{0})\leq(p-1)\left(\frac{\pi_{p}}{2s_{0}}\right)^{p}.

On the other hand, in the case
\beta<0
, then by (
3.9
) it holds

\mu_{1}(\beta,s_{0})\leq-(p-1)|\beta|^{p^{\prime}}.

4
Comparison results

In this section we will prove a comparison result that will be used to get the main theorems. We remark that the two estimates stated below do not depend on the sign of
\beta
.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
\Omega
be a bounded Lipschitz domain in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
,
N\geq 2
,
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
and
\lambda_{1}(\beta,\Omega)
be the first eigenvalue of (
1.2
).

If
v
is a nonnegative function satisfying

\begin{cases}-\mathcal{Q}_{p}v\geq\mu v^{p-1}\quad&\text{in}\ \Omega\\
F(\nabla v)^{p-1}F_{\xi}(\nabla v)\cdot\nu_{\Omega}+\beta F(\nu_{\Omega})v^{p-1}\geq 0&\text{on}\ \partial\Omega,\end{cases}

(4.1)

in weak sense, that is

\int_{\Omega}F(\nabla v)^{p-1}F_{\xi}(\nabla v)\cdot\nabla\varphi dx+\beta\int_{\Omega}F(\nu_{\Omega})v^{p-1}d\mathcal{H}^{N-1}\geq\mu\int_{\Omega}v^{p-1}\varphi dx,\\
\forall\varphi\in W^{1,p}(\Omega),\;\varphi\geq 0,

then

\lambda_{1}(\beta,\Omega)\geq\mu.

If
v
is a nonnegative function satisfying

\begin{cases}-\mathcal{Q}_{p}v\leq\mu v^{p-1}\quad&\text{in}\ \Omega\\
F(\nabla v)^{p-1}F_{\xi}(\nabla v)\cdot\nu_{\Omega}+\beta F(\nu_{\Omega})v^{p-1}\leq 0&\text{on}\ \partial\Omega,\end{cases}

(4.2)

in weak sense, that is

\int_{\Omega}F(\nabla v)^{p-1}F_{\xi}(\nabla v)\cdot\nabla\varphi dx+\beta\int_{\Omega}F(\nu_{\Omega})v^{p-1}d\mathcal{H}^{N-1}\leq\mu\int_{\Omega}v^{p-1}\varphi dx,\\
\forall\varphi\in W^{1,p}(\Omega),\;\varphi\geq 0,

Then

\lambda_{1}(\beta,\Omega)\leq\mu.

Proof.

Let us consider a positive first eigenfunction
u
of (
1.2
). Hence

\int_{\Omega}F(\nabla u)^{p}dx+\beta\int_{\partial\Omega}u^{p}F(\nu_{\Omega})d\mathcal{H}^{N-1}=\lambda_{1}(\beta,\Omega)\int_{\Omega}u^{p}dx

(4.3)

while, choosing as test function
\varphi=\frac{u^{p}}{(v+\varepsilon)^{p-1}}
in (
4.1
) we get

\begin{split}\int_{\Omega}p\left(\frac{u}{v+\varepsilon}\right)^{p-1}F(\nabla v)^{p-1}F_{\xi}(\nabla v)\cdot\nabla u\,dx-(p-1)\int_{\Omega}\left(\frac{u}{v+\varepsilon}\right)^{p}F(\nabla v)^{p}dx\\
+\beta\int_{\partial\Omega}\frac{v^{p-1}}{(v+\varepsilon)^{p-1}}u^{p}F(\nu_{\Omega})d\mathcal{H}^{N-1}\geq\mu\int_{\Omega}\frac{v^{p-1}}{(v+\varepsilon)^{p-1}}u^{p}dx\end{split}

(4.4)

Hence by subtracting (
4.4
) to (
4.3
), we get

\int_{\Omega}\left\{[F(\nabla u)]^{p}-pF\left(\frac{u}{v+\varepsilon}\nabla v\right)^{p-1}F_{\xi}\left(\frac{u}{v+\varepsilon}\nabla v\right)\cdot\nabla u+\right.\\
\left.+(p-1)F\Big(\frac{u}{v+\varepsilon}\nabla v\Big)^{p}\right\}dx\leq\int_{\Omega}\left[\lambda_{1}(\beta,\Omega)-\frac{v^{p-1}}{(v+\varepsilon)^{{p-1}}}\mu\right]u^{p}\,dx.

From the convexity of
F^{p}
, we get that the left-hand side in the above inequality is nonnegative. Hence, as
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
, the monotone convergence gives that

0\leq(\lambda_{1}(\beta,\Omega)-\mu)\int_{\Omega}u^{p}\,dx,

and the first inequality follows.

As regards the second inequality, the proof follows in a similar way: by using
\frac{v^{p}}{(u+\varepsilon)^{p-1}}
and
v
as test functions in (
1.2
) and (
4.2
) respectively, we get

\begin{split}\int_{\Omega}p\left(\frac{v}{u+\varepsilon}\right)^{p-1}F(\nabla u)^{p-1}F_{\xi}(\nabla u)\cdot\nabla v\,dx-(p-1)\int_{\Omega}\left(\frac{v}{u+\varepsilon}\right)^{p}F(\nabla u)^{p}dx\\
+\beta\int_{\partial\Omega}\frac{u^{p-1}}{(u+\varepsilon)^{p-1}}v^{p}F(\nu_{\Omega})d\mathcal{H}^{N-1}=\lambda_{1}(\beta,\Omega)\int_{\Omega}\frac{u^{p-1}}{(u+\varepsilon)^{p-1}}v^{p}dx.\end{split}

(4.5)

and

\int_{\Omega}F(\nabla v)^{p}dx+\beta\int_{\partial\Omega}v^{p}F(\nu_{\Omega})d\mathcal{H}^{N-1}\leq\mu\int_{\Omega}v^{p}dx;

(4.6)

hence by subtracting (
4.5
) to (
4.6
) we get

\int_{\Omega}\left\{[F(\nabla v)]^{p}-pF\left(\frac{v}{u+\varepsilon}\nabla u\right)^{p-1}F_{\xi}\left(\frac{v}{u+\varepsilon}\nabla u\right)\cdot\nabla v+\right.\\
\left.+(p-1)F\Big(\frac{v}{u+\varepsilon}\nabla u\Big)^{p}\right\}dx\leq\int_{\Omega}\left[\mu-\frac{u^{p-1}}{(u+\varepsilon)^{{p-1}}}\lambda_{1}(\beta,\Omega)\right]v^{p}\,dx.

and passing to the limit as
\varepsilon\to 0

(\mu-\lambda_{1}(\beta,\Omega))\int_{\Omega}v^{p}\,dx\geq 0,

and the conclusion follows.
∎

5
Proof of the main results

In this section we give the proof of the main results of the paper.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Let us consider the anisotropic Dirichlet
p
-torsional rigidity problem (
1.5
) and let us denote by
M
the maximum achieved by the anisotropic stress function
w_{\Omega}
, and fix
s_{0}=(p^{\prime}M)^{\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}}
. The first eigenfunction
X=X(s)
of problem (
3.1
) with
\mu=\mu_{1}(\beta,s_{0})
has a sign, then we can assume that
X>0
(see also the discussion in Section
3
). We set

s(x):=\left[p^{\prime}(M-w_{\Omega}(x))\right]^{\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}}\qquad\text{in}\ \Omega,

and let us observe that
s(x)=s_{0}
if
x\in\partial\Omega
.
We denote

v(x):=X(s(x))\qquad\text{in}\ \Omega.

Therefore, we have
\nabla v(x)=X^{\prime}(s(x))\nabla s(x)
, and

\nabla s=-\frac{\nabla w_{\Omega}}{s^{p^{\prime}-1}}\qquad\text{in}\ \Omega.

We first show that the function
v
satisfies

\begin{cases}-\mathcal{Q}_{p}v\geq\mu_{1}(\beta,s_{0})v^{p-1}\quad&\text{in}\ \Omega\\
F(\nabla v)^{p-1}F_{\xi}(\nabla v)\cdot\nu_{\Omega}+\beta F(\nu_{\Omega})v^{p-1}\geq 0&\text{on}\ \partial\Omega.\end{cases}

Indeed, the computation of the anisotropic
p
-Laplacian of
v
gives:

\begin{split}\mathcal{Q}_{p}v&=\dive(F^{p-1}(\nabla v)F_{\xi}(\nabla v))=\dive(|X^{\prime}|^{p-2}X^{\prime}F^{p-1}(\nabla s)F_{\xi}(\nabla s))\\
&=|X^{\prime}|^{p-2}X^{\prime}\dive(F^{p-1}(\nabla s)F_{\xi}(\nabla s))+(|X^{\prime}|^{p-2}X^{\prime})^{\prime}F^{p}(\nabla s)\\
&=|X^{\prime}|^{p-2}X^{\prime}\dive\left(-\frac{F^{p-1}(\nabla w_{\Omega})F_{\xi}(\nabla w_{\Omega})}{s}\right)+(|X^{\prime}|^{p-2}X^{\prime})^{\prime}\frac{F^{p}(\nabla w_{\Omega})}{s^{p^{\prime}}}\\
&=|X^{\prime}|^{p-2}X^{\prime}\left(-\frac{\mathcal{Q}_{p}w_{\Omega}}{s}+\frac{F_{\xi}(\nabla w_{\Omega})F^{p-1}(\nabla w_{\Omega})\nabla s}{s^{2}}\right)-\mu_{1}(\beta,s_{0})X^{p-1}\frac{F^{p}(\nabla w_{\Omega})}{s^{p^{\prime}}}\\
&=|X^{\prime}|^{p-2}X^{\prime}\left(\frac{1}{s}-\frac{F^{p}(\nabla w_{\Omega})}{s^{p^{\prime}+1}}\right)-\mu_{1}(\beta,s_{0})X^{p-1}\frac{F^{p}(\nabla w_{\Omega})}{s^{p^{\prime}}}.\end{split}

Hence, we have the following:

\begin{split}\mathcal{Q}_{p}v+\mu_{1}v^{p-1}&=\frac{|X^{\prime}|^{p-2}X^{\prime}}{s}\left(1-\frac{F^{p}(\nabla w_{\Omega})}{s^{p^{\prime}}}\right)+\mu_{1}(\beta,s_{0})X^{p-1}\left(1-\frac{F^{p}(\nabla w_{\Omega})}{s^{p^{\prime}}}\right)\\
&=\left(\frac{|X^{\prime}|^{p-2}X^{\prime}}{s}+\mu_{1}(\beta,s_{0})X^{p-1}\right)\left(1-\frac{F^{p}(\nabla w_{\Omega})}{s^{p^{\prime}}}\right).\end{split}

(5.1)

We claim that

\mathcal{Q}_{p}v+\mu_{1}(\beta,s_{0})v^{p-1}\leq 0.

Indeed, the
\mathcal{P}
-function method assures that the term
