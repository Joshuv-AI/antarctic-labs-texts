Low-Energy Signals from the Formation of Dark Matter-Nuclear Bound States
Asher Berlin, Hongwan Liu, Maxim Pospelov, Harikrishnan Ramani

Abstract
Dark matter particles may bind with nuclei if there exists an attractive force of sufficient strength. We show that a dark photon mediator of mass ∼ ( 10 − 100 ) ​ MeV \sim(10-100)\ \text{MeV} that kinetically mixes with Standard Model electromagnetism at the level of ∼ 10 − 3 \sim 10^{-3} generates keV-scale binding energies between dark matter and heavy elements, while forbidding the ability to bind with light elements. In underground direct detection experiments, the formation of such bound states liberates keV-scale energy in the form of electrons and photons, giving rise to mono-energetic electronic signals with a time-structure that may contain daily and seasonal modulations. We show that data from liquid-xenon detectors provides exquisite sensitivity to this scenario, constraining the galactic abundance of such dark particles to be at most ∼ 10 − 18 − 10 − 12 \sim 10^{-18}-10^{-12} of the galactic dark matter density for masses spanning ∼ ( 1 − 10 5 ) ​ GeV \sim(1-10^{5})\ \text{GeV} . However, an exponentially small fractional abundance of these dark particles is enough to explain the observed electron recoil excess at XENON1T.



Introduction.
Over the years, dark matter (DM) direct detection experiments have become extraordinarily sensitive to sub-MeV energy deposition by exotic sources, with thresholds recently extending down to sub-keV energies. While the primary motivation for these experiments is to search for the elastic scattering of weakly interacting massive particles (WIMPs) off nuclei, the scope of these searches now includes, e.g., electron scattering, DM absorption, and exo- and endo-thermic inelasticity in DM-nucleus scattering (see, e.g., Ref.
Lin 2019
for a review). Each of these searches seeks to measure the energy deposited from either the kinetic (scattering) or mass energy (absorption, inelasticity) of the incoming DM particle. In this paper, we point out a third, distinct alternative: the energy released due to the formation of a bound state between DM and a Standard Model (SM) nucleus.

The phenomenology of DM-nuclear bound state formation has been studied previously in the literature. For instance,
\text{\,}\mathrm{Me\kern-0.50003ptV}

Pospelov and Ritz 2007
;

;

;

Bernabei et al. 2008
;
Bernabei et al. 2010
;
Bernabei et al. 2018

Terrestrial Thermalization.
Before ultimately binding to a heavy nucleus in an underground direct detection experiment,
\chi
thermalizes to terrestrial temperatures after elastically scatters many times off the much lighter elements in Earth’s atmosphere and crust. This is governed by the transfer cross section for elastic DM-nuclear scattering
\chi N\to\chi N
, which in the perturbative Born limit (
m_{A^{\prime}}\gg q_{\text{eff}}Z\alpha\mu
)
is approximately

\displaystyle\sigma_{T}^{(\text{Born})}

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{64\pi\,Z^{2}\alpha^{2}\,q_{\text{eff}}^{2}\,\mu^{2}}{3\,m_{A^{\prime}}^{4}}

\displaystyle\simeq$3\text{\times}{10}^{-26}\text{\,}{\mathrm{cm}}^{2}$\times\bigg(\frac{q_{\text{eff}}}{10^{-3}}\bigg)^{2}\bigg(\frac{$50\text{\,}\mathrm{Me\kern-0.50003ptV}$}{m_{A^{\prime}}}\bigg)^{4}~,

(4)

where in the second line we have taken
m_{\chi}\gg m_{N}
and set the nuclear parameters equal to that of silicon, one of the most abundant elements in Earth’s crust. Taking a terrestrial silicon density of
n_{\text{Si}}\sim${10}^{22}\text{\,}{\mathrm{cm}}^{-3}$

\ell_{\text{therm}}\sim(m_{\chi}\,m_{N}/\mu^{2})\,(n_{\text{Si}}\,\sigma_{T})^{-1}
for
\chi
to equilibrate to room temperature is much smaller than
1\ \text{km}
for sub-TeV DM masses.

Earth’s gravitational field

\chi

Rate of Bound State Formation.
The formation of DM-nuclear bound states occurs by the Migdal/Auger-like ejection of an atomic electron,
\chi+A\to(\chi A^{+})+e^{-}
, followed by subsequent relaxation of the non-ejected atomic electrons to the ground state. Bound state formation by bremsstrahlung emission of a final state photon,
\chi+A\to(\chi A)+\gamma
, is subdominant, as is the case for the elastic scattering of light DM, since the rate is suppressed by
\sim$\text{\,}\mathrm{ke\kern-0.50003ptV}$/m_{N}

Bell et al. 2020
;
Ibe et al. 2018
.

We estimate the probability of the transition
\chi+A\to(\chi A^{+})+e^{-}
using quantum mechanical perturbation theory. In the Supplemental Material, we derive the cross section for this process. We focus on the
s

s
-wave, the cross section for this
s
-wave to
s
-wave transition is

\sigma_{B}v_{\text{rel}}\simeq\frac{4\pi}{9}\,\frac{(Z\alpha\,m_{e})^{5}}{(2\mu\,E_{B})^{7/2}}\,\Big(\frac{\mu}{m_{N}}\Big)^{4}\,F_{\chi}^{2}\,F_{e}^{2}~.

(6)

and

\chi

E_{B}=$2.5\text{\,}\mathrm{ke\kern-0.50003ptV}$

Recombination Signal.
Direct detection experiments that employ heavy elements, such as liquid noble targets, have remarkable sensitivity to the formation of these DM-nuclear bound states. For a detector employing a target of atomic mass
A
, the signal rate per unit target mass per unit time is

R_{\text{sig}}=P_{\text{surv}}\,n_{\chi}\,\sigma_{B}v_{\text{rel}}\,\left(\frac{N_{A}}{A\ \text{grams}}\right)~,

(8)

where
N_{A}
is Avogadro’s number and
P_{\text{surv}}
is the survival probability that an incoming
\chi
is not captured by naturally occurring elements in the terrestrial overburden. Since we are interested in dark photon masses that forbid
\chi
from binding with elements much lighter than xenon, premature capture in the terrestrial crust can only occur from binding with rare heavy elements.

The most abundant of such elements is barium, which possesses an atomic number and mass slightly greater than xenon. The average density of barium in Earth’s crust is
n_{\text{Ba}}\sim${10}^{18}\text{\,}{\mathrm{cm}}^{-3}$
with an exact value that varies geographically. For our estimates below, we adopt a range
n_{\text{Ba}}\in(0.7-6)\times${10}^{18}\text{\,}{\mathrm{cm}}^{-3}$
, whose log-central value of
2\times${10}^{18}\text{\,}{\mathrm{cm}}^{-3}$
is representative of limestone
Wedepohl 2014
, the dominant rock in the overburden at Gran Sasso National Laboratory. The survival probability is then given by
P_{\text{surv}}\simeq\text{exp}[-n_{\text{Ba}}\,\sigma_{\text{cap}}v_{\text{rel}}\,\Delta t]
, where
\sigma_{\text{cap}}
is the cross section for
\chi
to bind with barium and
\Delta t
is the time it takes for
\chi
to penetrate the XENON1T overburden, which consists of
h\simeq 1.4\ \text{km}
of rock. The transit time is given by the minimum of either the diffusion or gravitational-drift timescales,
\Delta t\sim\min(t_{\text{diff}},t_{\text{drift}})
, where the diffusion time
t_{\text{diff}}
and the gravitational-drift timescale
t_{\text{drift}}
are approximately

t_{\text{diff}}\sim h^{2}\,\frac{\Gamma_{\text{drag}}\,m_{\chi}}{$300\text{\,}\mathrm{K}$}~~,~~t_{\text{drift}}\sim h\,\frac{\Gamma_{\text{drag}}}{g}~,

(9)

and
\Gamma_{\text{drag}}\sim(\mu/m_{\chi})n_{N}\sigma_{T}v_{\text{rel}}
is the drag rate (the inverse timescale for a particle to change its momentum by an
\mathcal{O}(1)
amount) from elastic scattering off of light nuclei with density
n_{N}
in the crust. Fixing
E_{B}=2.5\ \text{keV}
in xenon and
m_{A^{\prime}}=50\ \text{MeV}
, we find that diffusion is efficient (
t_{\text{diff}}\ll t_{\text{drift}}
) for
m_{\chi}\ll$100\text{\,}\mathrm{Ge\kern-0.50003ptV}$
, while gravitational drift is most efficient (
t_{\text{drift}}\ll t_{\text{diff}}
) for
m_{\chi}\gg$100\text{\,}\mathrm{Ge\kern-0.50003ptV}$
, such that
P_{\text{surv}}\simeq 1
in either case. Instead, for DM masses of
m_{\chi}\sim$100\text{\,}\mathrm{Ge\kern-0.50003ptV}$
,
P_{\text{surv}}\ll 1
and premature capture by naturally occurring barium may exponentially reduce the DM flux that is able to reach underground detectors.

m_{\chi}\ll 10\ \text{GeV}

m_{\chi}\ll 100\ \text{GeV}

Discussion.
We have explored the intriguing possibility that the excess of events at XENON1T is explained by an exponentially small subcomponent of DM that binds with heavy nuclei through a dark photon mediator of mass
m_{A^{\prime}}\sim\mathcal{O}(10-100)\ \text{MeV}
that generates a sizeable DM-SM coupling
q_{\text{eff}}=\epsilon\sqrt{\alpha_{D}/\alpha}\gtrsim 10^{-3}
. For a perturbative model in which
\alpha_{D}\lesssim\mathcal{O}(1)
, we are thus motivated to consider
\epsilon\gtrsim 10^{-4}
. This parameter space can be efficiently probed by searching for visible decays of dark photons that are produced in the collisions of dedicated accelerator experiments.

For
m_{A^{\prime}}\ll 1\ \text{MeV}

We have focused on exhibiting the salient features of how DM-SM bound state formation can give rise to signals in the XENON1T S1-S2 data of Ref.
Aprile et al. 2020
since this search has the largest exposure among experiments sensitive to
\mathcal{O}(\text{keV})
electron recoils. However, other experiments may have sensitivity to new parameter space for two reasons. First, when threshold energies are reduced to below
\sim 1\ \text{keV}
at the expense of reduced exposure or larger backgrounds, smaller couplings

can be probed for larger

\mathcal{O}(100)\ \text{eV}

\mathcal{O}(10)\ \text{eV}

;
Bernabei et al. 2010
;
Bernabei et al. 2018

The DAMA experiment has reported a long-standing annual modulation signal in the
(1-6)\ \text{keV}
energy range
Bernabei et al. 2008
;
Bernabei et al. 2010
;
Bernabei et al. 2018
. While the primary target materials (sodium and iodine) are lighter than xenon, thallium is present at the

level. Since thallium nuclei are much heavier than xenon, this raises the tantalizing possibility that
q_{\text{eff}}
is large enough such that
\chi
binds deeply in thallium but is too small to deposit a significant energy above threshold in xenon-based experiments, thus explaining the DAMA observation. For
m_{\chi}\gtrsim 100\ \text{GeV}
, explaining the modulated rate at DAMA requires fractional abundances of
f_{\chi}\gtrsim 10^{-9}
, which is in conflict with limits derived from CDMS
Abrams et al. 2002

m_{\chi}\lesssim 100\ \text{GeV}

without being in tension with an S2-only search at XENON1T
Aprile et al. 2019

Although strongly interacting relics cannot accommodate this excess of events, it is interesting to note that DAMA can place some of the most stringent constraints on models in which

\chi+A\to(\chi A^{+})+e^{-}

Perturbation Theory Set-Up

The Hamiltonian for the system can be written as
\hat{H}=\hat{H}_{0}+\hat{V}_{\text{pert}}
, with
\hat{V}_{\text{pert}}
being a small perturbation to the unperturbed Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{0}
. The cross section for transitioning from an initial state
\ket{i}
to a continuum final state
\ket{f}
, both eigenstates of
\hat{H}_{0}
, is given by Fermi’s golden rule. In the process we are considering, an electron is ejected into a continuum final state, with a change in energy given by
E_{B}
, the binding energy of
(\chi A)
, so that Fermi’s golden rule reads
Landau and Lifshitz 1981

\displaystyle\textrm{d}\sigma=\frac{\textrm{d}^{3}\mathbf{p}_{e}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\,2\pi\,\left|\braket{f|\hat{V}_{\text{pert}}|i}\right|^{2}\delta\left(E_{e}-E_{B}+\omega_{e,i}\right)=\frac{\textrm{d}\Omega_{e}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,m_{e}p_{e}\,\left|\braket{f|\hat{V}_{\text{pert}}|i}\right|^{2}\,,

(S1)

where
\mathbf{p}_{e}
and
E_{e}
are the momentum and energy of the final state electron respectively, and
\omega_{e,i}
is the binding energy of the initial state electron. In the second equality, we have integrated over the delta function, which leaves just the solid angle of the electron momentum
\Omega_{e}
and fixes
p_{e}=\sqrt{2m_{e}(E_{B}-\omega_{e,i})}
. We neglect the kinetic energy of the incoming thermalized
\chi
particle, which is much smaller than
E_{B}
. The appropriate normalization of the states
\ket{i}
and
\ket{f}
will be discussed below.

The total Hamiltonian involving the nucleus
N
, the DM particle
\chi
, and the ejected electron
e
contains the following terms:

\displaystyle\hat{H}=\hat{T}+\hat{V}_{\chi N}+\hat{V}_{eA}\,,

(S2)

where
\hat{T}
is the kinetic energy terms of all the particles involved, and the various potential energy contributions are
\hat{V}_{\chi N}
between
\chi
and the nucleus
N
and
\hat{V}_{eA}
between the ejected electron and the atom (including both
N
and the other unejected electrons).
9
9

9

The other potential energy terms involving solely the unejected electrons are unimportant, since either the ejected electron states or the
\chi
states are eigenstates of these operators, and the initial and final wavefunctions are orthogonal. Furthermore, we do not include the potential arising from interactions between
\chi
and electrons because
\chi
becomes tightly bound to the atom and thus dominantly experiences just the bare nucleus: the typical size of the bound state, given in Eq. (
S6
), is much smaller than the size of the xenon atom.
Let us now discuss both of these contributions.

We model

by adopting the Thomas-Fermi model for the neutral atom, which gives an effective screened potential energy of the nucleus and the atomic electrons as a function of the distance to the nucleus. In this model, the effective screened potential energy of the entire atom

is
Landau and Lifshitz 1981

\displaystyle V_{A}(|\mathbf{r}_{e}-\mathbf{r}_{N}|)=-\,\frac{Z\alpha}{|\mathbf{r}_{e}-\mathbf{r}_{N}|}~\phi\left(\frac{|\mathbf{r}_{e}-\mathbf{r}_{N}|}{b}\right)\,,

(S3)

where

\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}\phi}{d\xi^{2}}=\frac{\phi^{3/2}}{\sqrt{\xi}}\,,

(S4)

with the boundary conditions
\phi(0)=1
and
\lim_{\xi\to\infty}\phi(\xi)=0
, which can be solved numerically.
V_{eA}
, the potential energy between the ejected electron and the rest of the atom, can be obtained by subtracting the contribution from the initial bound state electron, i.e.,

\displaystyle V_{eA}(|\mathbf{r}_{e}-\mathbf{r}_{N}|)=V_{A}(|\mathbf{r}_{e}-\mathbf{r}_{N}|)-\alpha\int\textrm{d}^{3}\mathbf{r}^{\prime}\frac{|\psi_{e,i}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})|^{2}}{|(\mathbf{r}_{e}-\mathbf{r}_{N})-\mathbf{r}^{\prime}|}\,,

(S5)

where

The characteristic size of the

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\mu E_{B}}}\simeq$9\text{\,}\mathrm{fm}$\times\left(\frac{$100\text{\,}\mathrm{Ge\kern-0.50003ptV}$}{\mu}\right)^{1/2}\left(\frac{$2.5\text{\,}\mathrm{ke\kern-0.50003ptV}$}{E_{B}}\right)^{1/2}\,,

(S6)

which is comparable to typical nuclear radii

\mu
is the reduced mass of
\chi
and

between
\chi

N

\displaystyle V_{\chi N}(|\mathbf{r}_{\chi}-\mathbf{r}_{N}|)=-\int_{N}\textrm{d}^{3}\mathbf{r}^{\prime}~\frac{\rho}{|(\mathbf{r}_{\chi}-\mathbf{r}_{N})-\mathbf{r}^{\prime}|}~e^{-m_{A^{\prime}}|(\mathbf{r}_{\chi}-\mathbf{r}_{N})-\mathbf{r}^{\prime}|}~,

\displaystyle V_{\chi N}(r)=-\,\frac{4\pi\,\rho}{m_{A^{\prime}}^{3}\,r}\begin{cases}m_{A^{\prime}}\,r-e^{-m_{A^{\prime}}R_{\text{nuc}}}(1+m_{A^{\prime}}\,R_{\text{nuc}})\,\sinh\left(m_{A^{\prime}}\,r\right)&(r<R_{\text{nuc}})\\

(S8)

\displaystyle V_{eA}(|\mathbf{r}_{e}-\mathbf{r}_{N}|)

\displaystyle=V_{eA}(r_{e})+\mathbf{r}_{N}^{i}\,\partial^{i}\,V_{eA}(r_{e})+\frac{1}{2}\,\mathbf{r}_{N}^{i}\mathbf{r}_{N}^{j}\,\partial^{i}\partial^{j}\,V_{eA}(r_{e})+\cdots

(S9)

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{0}\equiv\hat{T}+\hat{V}_{\chi N}+\hat{V}_{eA}^{(0)}\,.

(S10)

The initial and final states of both
\chi

\displaystyle\hat{V}_{\text{pert}}\equiv\hat{V}_{eA}^{(1)}+\hat{V}_{eA}^{(2)}\,,

(S11)

keeping just the first two terms in the expansion of

Wavefunctions in a Central Potential

Before we investigate each transition separately, we note that
\chi
and
e
are both subject to a two-body central potential with the nucleus. In each case, the Schrödinger equation is separable into an angular and a radial component. For a central potential
V
, and a well-defined energy
E
and angular momentum quantum number
l

R(\hskip-1.70709pt\text{\LARGE r})
satisfies the following equation:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\hskip-1.70709pt\text{\LARGE r}^{\,2}}\,\frac{\textrm{d}}{\textrm{d}\hskip-1.70709pt\text{\LARGE r}}\left(\hskip-1.70709pt\text{\LARGE r}^{\,2}\,\frac{\textrm{d}R}{\textrm{d}\hskip-1.70709pt\text{\LARGE r}}\right)-\left[\frac{l(l+1)}{\hskip-1.70709pt\text{\LARGE r}^{\,2}}+2\mu\,V\right]R=-2\mu E\,R~,

(S12)

where
r
is the relative coordinate between the two bodies, and
\mu
is the reduced mass of two bodies. For a bound state wavefunction

such that
\mathbf{k}
is the momentum of the particle far away from the nuclear potential.

For bound wavefunctions, the full wavefunction is
R
times the appropriate spherical harmonic for angular momentum
l
and azimuthal quantum number

can be expanded as
Landau and Lifshitz 1981

\displaystyle\psi(\hskip-1.70709pt\text{\LARGE r}\,,\theta)=\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\,\frac{2l+1}{2k}\,i^{l}\,P_{l}(\cos\theta)\,R_{kl}(\hskip-1.70709pt\text{\LARGE r})\,,

(S13)

where

S12

until we obtain a solution that goes to zero as
r\to\infty
with no nodes. Including the angular piece of the wavefunction (i.e., the spherical harmonic
Y_{00}=1/\sqrt{4\pi}
), we have

\displaystyle\psi_{\chi,f}(r)=R_{\text{b.s.}}^{\chi}(r)/\sqrt{4\pi}\,.

(S22)

Next, we consider the initial and final state wavefunctions for the ejected electron. From Eq. (
s-to-s Capture Cross Section
), we see that the capture cross section is enhanced by
Z^{2}
for electronic wavefunctions that have non-vanishing weight at the origin. Since wavefunctions of angular momentum
\ell
scale as
\sim r^{\ell}
at small radii, we focus on
s
-wave electronic wavefunctions. The electron is treated as initially occupying a bound eigenstate of
\hat{T}+\hat{V}_{eA}^{(0)}
. For simplicity, we adopt the Roothaan-Hartree-Fock electronic wavefunctions for the atomic orbitals, where the radial part of the wavefunctions is decomposed into a linear combination of Slater orbitals, as computed in Refs.
Bunge et al. 1993
;
McLean and McLean 1981
(see also Ref.
Xeni 2018
). This decomposition can be written as

\displaystyle R_{nl}^{e}(r_{e})=\sum_{j}C_{jnl}\,S_{jl}(r_{e})~~,~~S_{jl}(r_{e})=\frac{(2Z_{jl})^{n_{jl}+1/2}}{\sqrt{(2n_{jl})!}}\,a_{0}^{-3/2}\left(\frac{r_{e}}{a_{0}}\right)^{n_{jl}-1}e^{-Z_{jl}\,r_{e}/a_{0}}~,

(S23)

where
C_{jnl}
is the weight given to each Slater orbital (indexed by
j
),
Z_{jl}
is an effective charge,
n_{jl}
is the principal quantum number of that Slater orbital (see, e.g., Table 4.1 of Ref.
Xeni 2018
), and
a_{0}=1/\alpha m_{e}
is the Bohr radius. For a
\chi-\text{Xe}
binding energy of
E_{B}=$2.5\text{\,}\mathrm{ke\kern-0.50003ptV}$
, only the
3s
,
4s
, and
5s
electrons in xenon are shallowly bound enough to be ejected in an
s
-to-
s
transition. The full initial state wavefunction of the electron for each of these states is then simply

\displaystyle\psi_{e,i}=\frac{R_{n0}^{e}(r_{e})}{\sqrt{4\pi}}\,.

(S24)

after including the appropriate spherical harmonic.

For the outgoing electron, we obtain the radial wavefunction
R_{p_{e}0}^{e}(r_{e})
by numerically solving Eq. (
S12
) for the
e-A
system, i.e., with
\mu=m_{e}
,
V=V_{eA}^{(0)}
,
l=0
, and
E=p_{e}^{2}/2m_{e}=E_{B}-\omega_{e,i}
. Asymptotically far away from the nucleus, we again expect
R_{p_{e}0}^{e}(r_{e})
to approach the free continuum solution
2p_{e}j_{0}(p_{e}r)
up to a phase, which sets the normalization of
R^{e}_{p_{e}0}(r_{e})
. The full final electron wavefunction is then (see Eq. (
S13
) for the normalization factor)

\displaystyle\psi_{e,f}(r_{e})=\frac{R^{e}_{p_{e}0}(r_{e})}{2p_{e}}\,.

(S25)

Having determined the wavefunctions in Eq. (
s-to-s Capture Cross Section
), we are now ready to compute
\sigma_{s}
, the
s
-to-
s
capture cross section after summing over all possible initial electron states. Returning to Eq. (
s-to-s Capture Cross Section
), we find that the contribution to
V_{eA}^{(2)}
from the screening electrons (the second term in the first set of vertical brackets) is subdominant (at the level of
\sim 0.5\%
) to the
Z^{2}
contribution from the nucleus itself (the first term in the first set of vertical brackets). Keeping only this
Z^{2}
contribution yields

\displaystyle\textrm{d}\sigma_{s}

\displaystyle=2\,\sum_{n}\frac{\textrm{d}\Omega_{e}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,m_{e}p_{e}\,\left(\frac{2\pi\,Z\alpha}{3}\right)^{2}\left(\frac{\mu}{m_{N}}\right)^{4}\,\left|\psi_{e,f}^{*}(0)\,\frac{R_{n0}^{e}(0)}{\sqrt{4\pi}}\,\int\textrm{d}r~4\pi r^{4}\,\frac{R_{\text{b.s.}}^{\chi}(r)}{\sqrt{4\pi}}\,\frac{G(r)}{\sqrt{v_{\text{rel}}}}\right|^{2}\,,

(S26)

where for
E_{B}=2.5\ \text{keV}
in xenon the sum is over the
n=
3, 4, and 5 electrons, and the factor of two accounts for the pair of electrons in each of these
s
orbitals. Also note that
p_{e}=\sqrt{2m_{e}(E_{B}-\omega_{e,i})}
depends on
n
through the orbital-dependent electron binding energy
\omega_{e,i}
. Integrating over the outgoing electron solid angle, the above expression simplifies to

\displaystyle\sigma_{s}v_{\text{rel}}=\frac{4\pi\,(Z\alpha)^{2}}{9}\,\left(\frac{\mu}{m_{N}}\right)^{4}\left|\int\textrm{d}r~r^{4}\,R_{\text{b.s.}}^{\chi}(r)\,G(r)\right|^{2}~\sum_{n}\,2\,m_{e}p_{e}\,\left|\psi_{e,f}^{*}(0)\,R_{n0}(0)\right|^{2}\,.

(S27)

Noting that the continuum wavefunctions
G
and
\psi_{e,f}
are dimensionless, while the bound state wavefunctions
R_{\text{b.s.}}^{\chi}
and
R_{n0}
have dimension
[\text{length}]^{-3/2}
, we can use the characteristic length scale of the
\chi
-
N
bound state
1/\sqrt{2\mu E_{B}}
as well as the size of the atom
1/(Z\alpha m_{e})
to construct the following dimensionless form factors:

\displaystyle F_{\chi,s}^{2}=(2\mu E_{B})^{7/2}\,\left|\int\textrm{d}r~r^{4}\,R_{\text{b.s.}}^{\chi}(r)\,G(r)\right|^{2}~~,~~F_{e}^{2}=\frac{2}{(Z\alpha m_{e})^{3}}\sum_{n}\frac{p_{e}}{m_{e}}\,\left|\psi^{*}_{e,f}(0)\,R_{n0}(0)\right|^{2}~.

(S28)

Our final result for the cross section is then

\displaystyle\sigma_{s}v_{\text{rel}}

\displaystyle=\frac{4\pi}{9}\,\frac{(Z\alpha m_{e})^{5}}{(2\mu E_{B})^{7/2}}\,\Big(\frac{\mu}{m_{N}}\Big)^{4}\,F_{\chi,s}^{2}\,F_{e}^{2}\,,

(S29)

or numerically

\displaystyle\sigma_{s}v_{\text{rel}}

\displaystyle\simeq$7\text{\times}{10}^{-34}\text{\,}{\mathrm{cm}}^{2}$\times\left(\frac{Z}{54}\right)^{5}\bigg(\frac{$122\text{\,}\mathrm{Ge\kern-0.50003ptV}$}{m_{N}}\bigg)^{4}\bigg(\frac{$2.5\text{\,}\mathrm{ke\kern-0.50003ptV}$}{E_{B}}\bigg)^{7/2}\bigg(\frac{\mu}{$100\text{\,}\mathrm{Ge\kern-0.50003ptV}$}\bigg)^{1/2}\bigg(\frac{F_{\chi,s}^{2}}{49}\bigg)\bigg(\frac{F_{e}^{2}}{0.5}\bigg)\,.

(S30)

Taking the ionization potentials for the
3s
,
4s
, and
5s
xenon states to be
1148.7\text{\,}\mathrm{e\kern-0.50003ptV}
,
213.2\text{\,}\mathrm{e\kern-0.50003ptV}
, and
23.3\text{\,}\mathrm{e\kern-0.50003ptV}

Cardona and Ley 1978
, respectively, for
E_{B}=$2.5\text{\,}\mathrm{ke\kern-0.50003ptV}$
we find
F_{e}^{2}=0.5
with a relative contribution of 81%, 17% and 2% from each state. For
F_{\chi,s}^{2}
, we find only a weak dependence on
m_{\chi}
, ranging from
F_{\chi,s}^{2}\approx 35
for
m_{\chi}=$1\text{\,}\mathrm{Ge\kern-0.50003ptV}$
to
F_{\chi,s}^{2}\approx 62

m_{\chi}\gg m_{\text{Xe}}

E_{B}=$2.5\text{\,}\mathrm{ke\kern-0.50003ptV}$
gives

\displaystyle\sigma_{\text{Xe},s}v_{\text{rel}}\simeq$6\text{\times}{10}^{-34}\text{\,}{\mathrm{cm}}^{2}$\times\left(\frac{\mu}{$100\text{\,}\mathrm{Ge\kern-0.50003ptV}$}\right)^{0.55}\,.

(S31)

p-to-s Capture Cross Section

We now consider the case of
p
-to-
s
capture through
\hat{V}_{eA}^{(1)}
. This process results in a dipole transition of the final state electron, and bears many similarities to the photoelectric cross section of the atom.

To begin, let us consider the matrix element of Eq. (
S1
), which in position space involves
V_{eN}^{(1)}=\mathbf{r}_{N}^{i}\,\partial^{i}\,V_{eA}^{(0)}
. In the language of operators, this corresponds to
\hat{V}_{eN}^{(1)}=i\,\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{N}^{i}\,\,[\,\hat{\mathbf{p}}_{e}^{i},\hat{V}_{eA}^{(0)}\,]
. The matrix element of
\hat{V}_{eN}^{(1)}
between the initial state
|i\rangle=|\chi_{i}\rangle\,|e_{i}\rangle
and final state
|f\rangle=|\chi_{f}\rangle\,|e_{f}\rangle
is then

\langle f|\hat{V}_{eN}^{(1)}|i\rangle=i\,\langle\chi_{f}|\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{N}^{i}|\chi_{i}\rangle~\langle e_{f}|\,[\,\hat{\mathbf{p}}_{e}^{i},\hat{V}_{eA}^{(0)}\,]\,|e_{i}\rangle=i\,\langle\chi_{f}|\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{N}^{i}|\chi_{i}\rangle~\langle e_{f}|\,[\,\hat{\mathbf{p}}_{e}^{i},\hat{H}^{(0)}\,]\,|e_{i}\rangle=-iE_{B}\,\langle\chi_{f}|\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{N}^{i}|\chi_{i}\rangle~\langle e_{f}|\hat{\mathbf{p}}_{e}^{i}|e_{i}\rangle~,

(S32)

where in the second and third equalities we have made use of the fact that the only contribution to
\hat{H}_{0}
that does not commute with
\hat{\mathbf{p}}_{e}
is
\hat{V}_{eA}
, and that the initial and final electron states are both eigenstates of
\hat{H}_{0}
, with the difference in energy between the two states being given by the
\chi
-
N
binding energy,
E_{B}
. The square of this matrix element can be simplified by averaging over the direction of the electron momentum and using Eq. (
S15
), which allows us to replace

\big|\langle f|\hat{V}_{eN}^{(1)}|i\rangle\big|^{2}\to\frac{1}{3}\,E_{B}^{2}\,\big|\langle\chi_{f}|\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{N}|\chi_{i}\rangle\big|^{2}~\big|\langle e_{f}|\hat{\mathbf{p}}_{e}|e_{i}\rangle\big|^{2}~.

(S33)

Notice that we have successfully factored the contributions into a DM-only piece and an electron-only piece. The electron part of the matrix element is the same one that appears in electromagnetic dipole transitions. In particular, the photoelectric cross section in the long wavelength limit (averaging over photon polarization) is
\sigma_{\text{pe}}=2\alpha p_{e}\,\big|\langle e_{f}|\hat{\mathbf{p}}_{e}|e_{i}\rangle\big|^{2}/(3m_{e}E_{B})

Sakurai and Napolitano 2020
for an incoming photon with energy
E_{B}
and outgoing electron with momentum
p_{e}
. This allows Eq. (
S33
) to be rewritten as

\big|\langle f|\hat{V}_{eN}^{(1)}|i\rangle\big|^{2}\to~\frac{m_{e}E_{B}^{3}}{2\alpha p_{e}}\,\sigma_{\text{pe}}~\big|\langle\chi_{f}|\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{N}|\chi_{i}\rangle\big|^{2}~.

(S34)

The capture cross section is determined from this matrix element as in Eq. (
S1
). Rewriting the DM matrix element in position basis and using
r_{N}=(\mu/m_{N})\,r
, we have

\displaystyle\textrm{d}\sigma_{p}=\frac{\textrm{d}\Omega_{e}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,\frac{m_{e}^{2}E_{B}^{3}}{2\alpha}\,\left(\frac{\mu}{m_{N}}\right)^{2}\,\sigma_{\text{pe}}\,~\left|\int\textrm{d}^{3}\mathbf{r}~\psi_{\chi,f}^{*}(\mathbf{r})\,\psi_{\chi,i}(\mathbf{r})~\mathbf{r}\right|^{2}\,.

(S35)

The DM wavefunctions can be derived in a similar manner to the the previous section. The final bound state wavefunction
\psi_{\chi,f}
is identically given by the
s
-wave state in Eq. (
S22
). Since
\psi_{\chi,f}
is spherically symmetric, we see that the integral in Eq. (
S35
) vanishes if
\psi_{\chi,i}
is also
s
-wave. Hence, we take the incoming wavefunction
\psi_{\chi,i}
as the
\ell=1
term in the continuum wavefunction expansion in Eq. (
S13
), which gives

\displaystyle\psi_{\chi,i}=\frac{3i}{2k\sqrt{v_{\text{rel}}}}\,(\hat{\mathbf{k}}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{r}})\,R_{k1}(r)\,,

(S36)

where
R_{k1}(r)
is the solution to Eq. (
S12
) with
l=1
,
V=V_{\chi N}
, and
E\simeq 0
. Far away from the nucleus,
R_{k1}
should tend toward the free
l=1
solution
R_{k1}(r)\to 2kj_{1}(kr)\simeq 2k^{2}r/3
for
k\ll 1/r
. As in the last section, to incorporate the effect of the potential
V_{\chi N}
on
\psi_{\chi,i}(r)
at smaller radii, we parametrize the general solution as

\displaystyle\psi_{\chi,i}=i(\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r})\,F(r)/\sqrt{v_{\text{rel}}}~~,~~\lim_{r\to\infty}F(r)=1~,

(S37)

for some dimensionless function
F(r)
that we compute numerically by solving Eq. (
S12
) for the
\chi-N
system.

Having determined the DM wavefunctions, Eq. (
S35
) reduces to

\displaystyle\sigma_{p}v_{\text{rel}}=\frac{2m_{e}^{2}\,E_{B}^{3}\,\mu^{3}\,T}{3\alpha\,m_{N}^{2}}\,\sigma_{\text{pe}}\,\left|\int\textrm{d}r~R_{\text{b.s.}}^{\chi}(r)\,F(r)\,r^{4}\right|^{2}\,,

(S38)

where we integrated over the outgoing electron solid angle and used Eq. (
S15
) to replace
(\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r})\,\mathbf{r}\to(r^{2}/3)\,\mathbf{k}
in the integral over
r
. Finally, we replaced
k^{2}
with its thermally-averaged value
k^{2}\to 3\mu T
, where
T\simeq 300\ \text{K}
is the temperature of the thermalized
\chi
particle; as a result, the cross section is suppressed by the small thermal velocity. As in the last section, we can construct the following dimensionless quantity

\displaystyle F_{\chi,p}^{2}=(2\mu E_{B})^{7/2}\left|\int\textrm{d}r~R_{\text{b.s.}}^{\chi}(r)\,F(r)\,r^{4}\right|^{2}\,,

(S39)

such that the
p
-to-
s
capture cross section is given by

\displaystyle\sigma_{p}v_{\text{rel}}=\frac{m_{e}^{2}\,T\,F_{\chi,p}^{2}\,\sigma_{\text{pe}}}{12\alpha\,m_{N}^{2}\,(2\mu E_{B})^{1/2}}~.

(S40)

For parameters that are representative of
E_{B}=2.5\ \text{keV}
in xenon, we find

\displaystyle\sigma_{p}v_{\text{rel}}\simeq${10}^{-35}\text{\,}{\mathrm{cm}}^{2}$\times\bigg(\frac{T}{$300\text{\,}\mathrm{K}$}\bigg)\bigg(\frac{$122\text{\,}\mathrm{Ge\kern-0.50003ptV}$}{m_{N}}\bigg)^{2}\bigg(\frac{$2.5\text{\,}\mathrm{ke\kern-0.50003ptV}$}{E_{B}}\bigg)^{1/2}\bigg(\frac{$100\text{\,}\mathrm{Ge\kern-0.50003ptV}$}{\mu}\bigg)^{1/2}\bigg(\frac{F_{\chi,p}^{2}}{176}\bigg)\bigg(\frac{\sigma_{pe}}{$2.5\text{\times}{10}^{-19}\text{\,}{\mathrm{cm}}^{2}$}\bigg)\,.

(S41)

The photoelectric cross section
\sigma_{\text{pe}}
for various elements, including xenon, with a photon energy of
2.5\text{\,}\mathrm{ke\kern-0.50003ptV}
can be interpolated from data in Refs.
West and Morton 1978
;
Veigele 1973

E_{B}=$2.5\text{\,}\mathrm{ke\kern-0.50003ptV}$
in xenon, we find only a weak dependence of
F_{\chi,p}^{2}
on
m_{\chi}
, ranging from
F_{\chi,p}^{2}\approx 81

m_{\chi}=$1\text{\,}\mathrm{Ge\kern-0.50003ptV}$

m_{\chi}\gg m_{N}
.
