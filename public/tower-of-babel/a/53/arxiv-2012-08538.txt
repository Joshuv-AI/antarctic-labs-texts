Two-point Functions and Bootstrap Applications in Quantum Field Theories
Denis Karateev

Abstract
We study two-point functions of local operators and their spectral representation in UV complete quantum field theories in generic dimensions focusing on conserved currents and the stress-tensor. We establish the connection with the central charges of the UV and IR fixed points. We re-derive “c-theorems” in 2d and show the absence of their direct analogs in higher dimensions. We conclude by focusing on quantum field theories with a mass gap. We study the stress tensor two-particle form factor, derive implications of unitarity and define concrete bootstrap problems in generic dimensions.

2.2
Stress-tensor

Let us now turn our attention to the local stress-tensor
T^{ab}(x)
totally symmetric in its indices, namely
T^{ab}(x)=T^{ba}(x)
. In a
d

SO(d)
. It can be decomposed as a direct sum of the trivial and the symmetric traceless representations:
\bullet\oplus\,\bf{d}
. The trivial representation corresponds to the trace of the stress-tensor which we denote by

\Theta(x)\equiv T^{aa}(x).

(27)

Logically the discussion in this section will be identical to the one of conserved currents with several minor complications. The most general Euclidean two-point function consistent with the rotational symmetry and the translational invariance has the following form

\displaystyle\langle 0|T^{ab}(x_{1})T^{cd}(x_{2})|0\rangle_{E}=\frac{1}{r^{2d}}\left(\sum_{m}h_{m}(r)\mathbb{T}_{m}^{abcd}(x_{1},x_{2})+\sum_{n}ig_{n}(r)\mathbf{T}_{n}^{abcd}(x_{1},x_{2})\right).

(28)

Here
\mathbb{T}_{m}^{abcd}
and
\mathbf{T}_{n}^{abcd}
denote parity even and odd tensor structures respectively. The imaginary unit
i
is introduced in the parity odd part for the later convenience. The functions
h_{m}(r)
and
g_{n}(r)
multiplying these structures are dimensionless. Since the Euclidean correlation functions are time-ordered one has the following symmetry condition

\langle 0|T^{ab}(x_{1})T^{cd}(x_{2})|0\rangle_{E}=\langle 0|T^{cd}(x_{2})T^{ab}(x_{1})|0\rangle_{E}.

(29)

In the presence of the conformal symmetry the form of the two-point function (
28

\displaystyle\langle 0|T^{ab}(x_{1})T^{cd}(x_{2})|0\rangle_{E}

\displaystyle=\frac{C_{T}}{x_{12}^{2d}}\times\left(\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathcal{I}^{ac}(x_{1},x_{2})\mathcal{I}^{bd}(x_{1},x_{2})+\mathcal{I}^{ad}(x_{1},x_{2})\mathcal{I}^{bc}(x_{1},x_{2})\right)-\frac{1}{d}\,\delta^{ab}\delta^{cd}\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{i\,C^{\prime}_{T}}{x_{12}^{2d}}\times\frac{\delta_{d,2}}{4}\,\bigg(\mathcal{I}^{ac}(x_{1},x_{2})\mathcal{E}^{bd}(x_{1},x_{2})+\mathcal{I}^{ad}(x_{1},x_{2})\mathcal{E}^{bc}(x_{1},x_{2})

\displaystyle+\mathcal{I}^{bc}(x_{1},x_{2})\mathcal{E}^{ad}(x_{1},x_{2})+\mathcal{I}^{bd}(x_{1},x_{2})\mathcal{E}^{ac}(x_{1},x_{2})\bigg).

\mathcal{E}^{ab}
were defined in (
10

C_{T}
is called the stress-tensor central charge. It was first introduced in

.

15

15

The stress-tensor defines the conformal algebra. For instance the dilatation operator is defined as
D\equiv-\int d\Omega_{d}r^{d-2}x^{a}x^{b}T^{ab}(x)

is the sphere in

is the scaling dimension. Once the scaling dimensions are fixed there is no possibility to rescale the stress-tensor. Thus, the value of
C_{T}
cannot be changed in contrast to the coefficients appearing in two-point functions of generic operators. See also footnote
10

It is a universal quantity in any number of dimensions. In unitary theories
C_{T}>0
. The quantity

3
Lorentzian two-point functions

In this section we discuss two-point functions in the Lorentzian signature.
Contrary to the Euclidean signature where only the time-ordered two-point functions exist, in the Lorentzian signature we can define Wightman, time-ordered, advanced and retarded two-point functions. In what follows we will discuss the first two. The Wightman two-point functions suit best for defining spectral densities. They are automatically well defined at coincident points and do not have any contact terms. Time-ordered Lorentzian two-point functions will be employed in section
5
due to the following property: they simply become the Euclidean two-point functions under the Wick rotation. It will be sufficient to work with time-ordered correlators at non coincident points. This allows to avoid complications due to presence of contact terms.

The discussion presented in this section is completely generic for
d\geq 4
. In
d=2
and
d=3
two-point functions are allowed to have a parity odd contribution. Bellow we will completely ignore this possibility.

3.2
Time-ordered two-point functions

Time-ordered correlators are widely used because of several reasons. First they can be straightforwardly computed in perturbation theory. Second, they appear in the LSZ reduction formula and third they can be easily mapped to Euclidean correlators using the Wick rotation. In this section we will discuss two-point time-ordered correlaotrs.

Given two real scalar operators
\mathcal{O}_{1}(x)
and
\mathcal{O}_{2}(x)
the time-ordered two-point function is defined as

\langle 0|\mathcal{O}_{1}(x_{1})\mathcal{O}_{2}(x_{2})|0\rangle_{T}\equiv\theta(x_{12}^{0})\langle 0|\mathcal{O}_{1}(x_{1})\mathcal{O}_{2}(x_{2})|0\rangle_{W}+\theta(x_{21}^{0})\langle 0|\mathcal{O}_{2}(x_{2})\mathcal{O}_{1}(x_{1})|0\rangle_{W}.

(128)

Plugging the expression for the Wightman two-point functions in terms of the spectral density (
58
) we get

\displaystyle\langle 0|\mathcal{O}(x)\mathcal{O}(0)|0\rangle_{T}

\displaystyle=-i\int_{0}^{\infty}ds\rho_{\mathcal{O}}(s)\Delta_{F}(x;s),

(129)

\displaystyle-i\Delta_{F}(x;s)

\displaystyle\equiv\theta(x^{0})\Delta_{W}(x;s)+\theta(-x^{0})\Delta_{W}(-x;s)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}\int\frac{d^{d}p}{(2\pi)^{d}}e^{ip\cdot x}\frac{-i}{p^{2}+s-i\epsilon},

where
\Delta_{F}
is called the Feynman propagator.

\theta(t)=-\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}ds\,\frac{e^{-ist}}{s+i\epsilon}

Spectral densities in Lorentzian CFTs

In the previous section we defined spectral densities as the Fourier transform of Wightman two-point functions. In the presence of conformal symmetry the two-point functions are purely kinematic objects. In other words their form is completely fixed by the conformal symmetry. As a consequence we can straightforwardly compute the CFT spectral densities.

Spectral densities and central charges

As explained in section
3
, in the case of a conserved current
J^{\mu}(x)
there is a single (Little group spin one) component of the spectral density denoted by
\rho_{J}^{1}(s)
. Analogously, in the case of the stress-tensor
T^{\mu\nu}(x)
there are two components of the spectral density, namely the trace part
\rho_{\Theta}(s)
and the Little group spin two part
\rho_{\hat{T}}^{2}(s)
.
27
27

27

Spectral densities give an alternative description of the two-point functions to the position space functions

introduced in section

and

with three differential constraints.

In what follows we will explain how the information about the UV and IR central charges (for their precise definition see either section

or section
2
) are encoded in the components of spectral densities. We will see that
d=2
and

28

Conserved currents

Taking into account (
150
), the requirement that the quantum field theory under consideration has the UV and IR fixed points described by the UV and IR conformal field theories respectively at the level of spectral densities is imposed by the conditions

29

We remind that
s

\displaystyle\lim_{s\rightarrow 0}\,s^{2-d/2}

\displaystyle\times\left(\rho_{J}^{1}(s)-\rho_{J}^{1}(s)\big|_{IR\;CFT}\right)=0,

\displaystyle\lim_{s\rightarrow\infty}\,s^{2-d/2}

\displaystyle\times\left(\rho_{J}^{1}(s)-\rho_{J}^{1}(s)\big|_{UV\;CFT}\right)=0.

\displaystyle\lim_{s\rightarrow 0}s^{2-d/2}\times\rho_{J}^{1}(s)

\displaystyle\lim_{s\rightarrow\infty}s^{2-d/2}\times\rho_{J}^{1}(s)

\displaystyle=C^{UV}_{J}\times(2\pi)^{-1}\frac{d-2}{d-1}\,\kappa(d,d-1),

In order to understand what is happening in

(156)

For more details see section

Stress-tensor

The identical discussion holds for the stress-tenors. The requirement that the UV and IR fixed points are governed by the UV and IR conformal field theories translates into the conditions on the components of the stress-tensor spectral density.

We start with the spectral density of the trace of the stress-tensor
\rho_{\Theta}(s)
. In a conformal theory we strictly have
\Theta(x)=0

(161)

\lim_{s\rightarrow\infty}\,s^{d/2-\Delta_{\mathcal{O}}}\times\left(\rho_{\Theta}(s)-g^{2}\rho_{\mathcal{O}}(s)\big|_{UV\;CFT}\right)=0

\lim_{s\rightarrow\infty}s^{d/2-\Delta_{\mathcal{O}}}\times\rho_{\Theta}(s)=g^{2}(2\pi)^{-1}\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{O}}\;\kappa(d,\Delta_{\mathcal{O}}).

(163)

143

\mathcal{O}

140

\lim_{s\rightarrow 0}s^{d/2-\Delta_{\mathcal{O}}^{\prime}}\times\rho_{\Theta}(s)=g^{\prime\,2}(2\pi)^{-1}\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{O}^{\prime}}\;\kappa(d,\Delta_{\mathcal{O}}).

(165)

\mathcal{O}

6
Applications to bootstrap

In section 3 of
Karateev:2019ymz
it was shown how to use unitarity to construct non-trivial constraints on partial amplitudes, form factors and spectral densities. This was done in a presence of a single scalar local operator. Here we extend the analysis of section 3 in
Karateev:2019ymz
to include the full stress-tensor. We will conclude this section by defining concrete bootstrap problems.

We will focus on quantum field theory with a mass gap (or equivalently on the QFTs with an empty IR fixed point). The spectrum of such theories is described by one-particle asymptotic in and out states. We will work here with identical scalar particles for simplicity. For precise definitions of asymptotic states see section 2.1 in
Karateev:2019ymz
. One can build the two-particle asymptotic in and out states by taking the symmetrized tensor product of two one-particle states.
30
30

30

Symmetrization is required for identical particles in order to make the state invariant under the exchange of two particles.
We denote such two-particle states by

|m,\vec{p}_{1};m,\vec{p}_{2}\rangle_{in},\qquad|m,\vec{p}_{1};m,\vec{p}_{2}\rangle_{out}.

(174)

The four-momenta of the one-particle asymptotic states by definition obey

p_{1}^{2}=-m^{2},\qquad p_{2}^{2}=-m^{2}.

(175)

We also define

p^{\mu}\equiv p_{1}^{\mu}+p_{2}^{\mu},\qquad(p_{1}+p_{2})^{2}=-s,\qquad(p_{1}-p_{2})^{2}=s-4m^{2},

(176)

where
s
is the squared total energy of the two-particle state.

Sylvester’s criterion

A semi-positive definite matrix

has only non-negative eigenvalues. In order to address semi-positive constraints in practice we use the Sylvester’s criterion: a matrix

is semi-positive definite if and only if all its principal minors (including the determinant) are non-negative.

Appendix B
Unitarity

Unitary quantum field theories are defined to have non-negative norms of all its states. Consider some state
|\psi\rangle
. The unitarity then requires

\langle\psi|\psi\rangle\geq 0.

(254)

In a more complicated situation when we have

{}^{I}\langle\psi|\psi\rangle^{J}\succeq 0.

(255)

In what follows we will use (

255

Appendix C
Euclidean vs. Lorentzian operators

Here we will discuss Euclidean and Lorentzian correlators. We then provide a formal way to define the latter as various analytic continuations of the former.
Part of the discussion here is based on section 7 and appendix B of
Simmons-Duffin:2016gjk
.

Euclidean correlators

In the Euclidean space two- (and higher-) point correlation functions are computed using the path integral approach. They are denoted by

\langle\mathcal{O}_{E}(x_{E})\mathcal{O}_{E}(y_{E})\rangle.

(283)

We introduced the subscript
E
for both the coordinates and the operators in order to emphasise that we work in the Euclidean metric. By construction the correlation function (
283
) is time-ordered with respect to Euclidean time, namely

\langle\mathcal{O}_{E}(x_{E})\mathcal{O}_{E}(y_{E})\rangle=\langle\mathcal{O}_{E}(y_{E})\mathcal{O}_{E}(x_{E})\rangle.

(284)

We can also reinterpret the correlator (
283
) as the vacuum expectation value of local operators in some Hilbert space.
48
48

48

One can think about states and operators as vectors and matrices in the infinite-dimensional space. The vacuum state is the state with the lowest energy.
This is done as follows. The vacuum expectation value of two local operators is denote by

\langle 0|\mathcal{O}_{E}(x_{E})\mathcal{O}_{E}(y_{E})|0\rangle.

(285)

The order of operators in this expression is important.
The correlator (
285
) makes sense only if
x_{E}^{0}>y_{E}^{0}
. This is easy to see by rewriting (
285
) in the following way

\langle 0|\mathcal{O}_{E}(x_{E})\mathcal{O}_{E}(y_{E})|0\rangle=\langle 0|\mathcal{O}_{E}(0)e^{-H(x_{E}^{0}-y_{E}^{0})}\mathcal{O}_{E}(0)|0\rangle,

(286)

where
H
is the Hamiltonian of the system. Here we simply used the translation invariance

\mathcal{O}_{E}(x_{E})=e^{+P\cdot x_{E}}\mathcal{O}_{E}(0)e^{-P\cdot x_{E}},

(287)

where
P^{a}
are the generators of translations and
H\equiv P^{0}
. The Hamiltonian
H
is an infinite-dimensional matrix with non-negative eigenvalues. In other words the eigenvalues of
H
are bounded from below. The operator
e^{-H(x_{E}^{0}-y_{E}^{0})}
has all finite eigenvalues only if
x_{E}^{0}>y_{E}^{0}
. It becomes unbounded from above if
x_{E}^{0}<y_{E}^{0}
. In this case (
286
) formally diverges. The only option to avoid this and to define the two-point correlation function for any values of
x_{E}
and
y_{E}
is as follows

\langle 0|\mathcal{O}_{E}(x_{E})\mathcal{O}_{E}(y_{E})|0\rangle_{E}\equiv\theta(x_{E}^{0}-y_{E}^{0})\langle 0|\mathcal{O}_{E}(x_{E})\mathcal{O}_{E}(y_{E})|0\rangle+\\
\theta(y_{E}^{0}-x_{E}^{0})\langle 0|\mathcal{O}_{E}(y_{E})\mathcal{O}_{E}(x_{E})|0\rangle

(288)

By construction this is the time-ordered (with respect to Euclidean time) correlation function. We refer to it as the Euclidean correlator. The equivalence between the path integral formulation (
283
) and the operator formulation (
288
) leads to

\langle\mathcal{O}_{E}(x_{E})\mathcal{O}_{E}(y_{E})\rangle=\langle 0|\mathcal{O}_{E}(x_{E})\mathcal{O}_{E}(y_{E})|0\rangle_{E}.

(289)

Lorentzian correlators

Let us now consider the vacuum expectation value of the local operators in the Lorentzian signature

\langle 0|\mathcal{O}_{L}(x_{L})\mathcal{O}_{L}(y_{L})|0\rangle.

(290)

This quantity is not well-defined since it generically contains poles when
(x_{L}-y_{L})^{2}=0
at
x_{L}^{\mu}\neq y_{L}^{\mu}
. In order to define the above vacuum expectation value correctly one should specify how to deal with these poles. In practice we allow for a small imaginary part for the Lorentzian time and then send it to zero. Several different ways (prescriptions) exist. They define different types of correlators, namely Wightman, time-ordered (Feynman), advanced and retarded correlators.
For instance the Wightman function is defined as follows

\langle 0|\mathcal{O}_{L}(x_{L})\mathcal{O}_{L}(y_{L})|0\rangle_{W}\equiv\\
\lim_{\epsilon_{1}\rightarrow 0}\lim_{\epsilon_{2}\rightarrow 0}\langle 0|\mathcal{O}_{L}(x^{0}_{L}-i\epsilon_{1},\vec{x})\mathcal{O}_{L}(y^{0}_{L}-i\epsilon_{2},\vec{x}_{2})|0\rangle,

(291)

where
\epsilon_{1}>\epsilon_{2}
. This is the simplest possible prescription. One can use now the Wightman correlator to define all the other types of correlators (instead of going though various prescriptions). For example the time-ordered (Feynman) correlator is defined as

\langle 0|\mathcal{O}_{L}(x_{L})\mathcal{O}_{L}(y_{L})|0\rangle_{F}\equiv\theta(x^{0}_{L}-y^{0}_{L})\langle 0|\mathcal{O}_{L}(x_{L})\mathcal{O}_{L}(y_{L})|0\rangle_{W}+\\
\theta(y_{L}-x_{L})\langle 0|\mathcal{O}_{L}(y_{L})\mathcal{O}_{L}(x_{L})|0\rangle_{W}.

(292)

By definition the Wightman two-point function (
291
) is a distribution. When integrated with a test-function the
i\epsilon
prescription leads to an unambiguous result. The time-ordered two-point function (
292
) instead is not a distribution due to the presence of the step functions.

Relation between Euclidean and Lorentzian correlators

We can formalise the discussion of Lorentzian two-point functions by defining them as various analytic continuations of the Euclidean two-point function in time. Let us denote the Euclidean time by
t_{E}\equiv x_{E}^{0}
and the Lorentzian time by
t_{L}\equiv x_{L}^{0}
.

Let us start from the following Euclidean correlator

\langle 0|\mathcal{O}_{E}(\epsilon_{1},\vec{x})\mathcal{O}_{E}(\epsilon_{2},\vec{x}_{2})|0\rangle_{E},

(293)

where
\epsilon_{1}
and
\epsilon_{2}
are real Euclidean times which obey
\epsilon_{1}>\epsilon_{2}
. For
\epsilon_{1}>\epsilon_{2}
only the first term in (
288
) survives and we can thus drop the subscript
E
. We then analytically continue this object to complex times and send both
\epsilon_{1}
and
\epsilon_{2}
to zero. The resulting object formally defines the Wightman two-point function, namely

\displaystyle\langle 0|\mathcal{O}_{L}(x^{0}_{L},\,\vec{x})\mathcal{O}_{L}(y^{0}_{L},\,\vec{y})|0\rangle_{W}

\displaystyle\equiv\lim_{\epsilon_{1}\rightarrow 0}\lim_{\epsilon_{2}\rightarrow 0}\langle 0|\mathcal{O}_{E}(\epsilon_{1}+ix^{0}_{L},\vec{x})\mathcal{O}_{E}(\epsilon_{2}+iy^{0}_{L},\vec{x}_{2})|0\rangle

\displaystyle=\lim_{\epsilon_{1}\rightarrow 0}\lim_{\epsilon_{2}\rightarrow 0}\langle 0|\mathcal{O}_{E}\left(i(x^{0}_{L}-i\epsilon_{1}),\,\vec{x}\right)\mathcal{O}_{E}\left(i(y^{0}_{L}-i\epsilon_{2}),\,\vec{x}_{2}\right)|0\rangle

\displaystyle=\lim_{\epsilon_{1}\rightarrow 0}\lim_{\epsilon_{2}\rightarrow 0}\langle 0|\mathcal{O}_{L}(x^{0}_{L}-i\epsilon_{1},\,\vec{x})\mathcal{O}_{L}(y^{0}_{L}-i\epsilon_{2},\,\vec{x}_{2})|0\rangle.

(294)

Here we have decided to relate the Euclidean and Lorentzian times as

t_{E}=+it_{L},\qquad t_{L}=-it_{E}

(295)

The equality between the last two entries in (
294
) lead to the formal relation between the Euclidean and Lorentian scalar local operators

\mathcal{O}_{E}(t_{E},\vec{x})=\mathcal{O}_{L}(-it_{E},\vec{x}),\qquad\mathcal{O}_{L}(t_{L},\vec{x})=\mathcal{O}_{E}(it_{L},\vec{x}).

(296)

For local operators with spin the relation between Euclidean and Lorentzian operators is more complicated. For instance for the vector operators we have

J^{0}_{E}(t_{E},\vec{x})=-iJ^{0}_{L}(-it_{E},\vec{x}),\qquad\vec{J}_{E}(t_{E},\vec{x})=\vec{J}_{L}(-it_{E},\vec{x}).

(297)

The analytic continuation which follows the path

z\rightarrow z^{\prime}\equiv ze^{i(\pi/2-\epsilon)}\approx z(i+\epsilon)

(298)

is known as the Wick rotation. Without loss of generality let us set
y=0
by using translation invariance.
Using the Wick rotation one can define the (Feynman) time-ordered two-point function as

\displaystyle\langle 0|\mathcal{O}_{L}(x^{0}_{L},\,\vec{x})\mathcal{O}_{L}(0)|0\rangle_{F}

\displaystyle\equiv\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\langle 0|\mathcal{O}_{E}(x^{0}_{L}e^{i(\pi/2-\epsilon_{1})},\,\vec{x})\;\mathcal{O}_{E}(0)|0\rangle_{E}

(299)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\langle 0|\mathcal{O}_{E}(x^{0}_{L}(i+\epsilon),\,\vec{x})\;\mathcal{O}_{E}(0)|0\rangle_{E}.

(300)

Using the definition of the Euclidean propagator in the right-hand side of (
300
) the last equality can be written as

\theta(+x^{0}_{L})\langle 0|\mathcal{O}_{E}(ix^{0}_{L}+\epsilon,\,\vec{x})\;\mathcal{O}_{E}(0)|0\rangle_{E}+\theta(-x^{0}_{L})\langle 0|\mathcal{O}_{E}(ix^{0}_{L}-\epsilon,\,\vec{x})\;\mathcal{O}_{E}(0)|0\rangle_{E},

(301)

where we have used the fact that
\epsilon\,t_{L}\approx+\epsilon
if
t_{L}>0
and
\epsilon\,t_{L}\approx-\epsilon
if
t_{L}<0
.
Using the first line of (
294
) one sees the equivalence between the above expression and (
292
).

Appendix D
Källén-Lehmann representation in Euclidean signature

The Källén-Lehmann representation of the Lorentzian time-ordered two-point functions was derived in section
3.2
. In this section we would like to translate those result to the Euclidean signature. For that we apply the following change of variables

x_{L}^{0}\rightarrow-ix^{0}_{E},\qquad p_{L}^{0}\rightarrow-ip^{0}_{E}.

(302)

Which is in agreement with (
295
).

Let us start with the Källén-Lehmann representation for the scalar operators given by (
129
). Performing the above change of variables we get

\displaystyle\langle 0|\mathcal{O}(x)\mathcal{O}(0)|0\rangle_{E}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}ds\rho_{\mathcal{O}}(s)\Delta_{E}(x;s),

(303)

\displaystyle\Delta_{E}(x;s)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}\int\frac{d^{d}p_{E}}{(2\pi)^{d}}e^{ip_{E}\cdot x_{E}}\frac{1}{p_{E}^{2}+s}.

See appendix B of
Karateev:2019ymz
for some additional details. Notice the absence of the
i\epsilon
since there are no poles for real

to be regularized.

The Källén-Lehmann for the conserved currents and the stress-tensor were derived in (

) and (

). Analogously to the scalar case one gets

\displaystyle\langle 0|J^{a}(x)J^{b}(0)|0\rangle_{T}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}ds\rho_{J}^{1}(s)\Delta_{E,\,1}^{ab}(x;s),

(304)

\displaystyle\langle 0|T^{ab}(x)T^{cd}(0)|0\rangle_{T}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}ds\left(\frac{\rho_{\Theta}(s)}{(d-1)^{2}}\,\Delta_{E,\Theta}^{ab;\,cd}(x;s)+\rho_{\hat{T}}^{2}(s)\Delta_{E,\,2}^{ab;\,cd}(x;s)\right),

(305)

where the Euclidean propagators
\Delta_{E}
are obtained from (
137
) and (
139
). The Euclidean correlator for the conserved current is obtained from (
137
), it reads

\Delta_{E,\,1}^{ab}(x;s)=(s\,\delta^{ab}-\partial^{a}\partial^{b})\Delta_{E}(x;s)

(306)

and the Euclidean Feynman propagators for the stress-tensor read as

\displaystyle\Delta_{E,\Theta}^{ab;cd}(x;s)

\displaystyle=(s\,\delta^{ab}-\partial^{a}\partial^{b})(s\,\delta^{cd}-\partial^{c}\partial^{d})\Delta_{E}(x;s),

(307)

\displaystyle\Delta^{ab;\,cd}_{E,\,2}(x;s)

\displaystyle=s^{2}\,\Pi^{ab;\,cd}_{2}(\partial;s)\Delta_{E}(x;s),

where we have defined the Euclidean projectors in the coordinate space

\displaystyle\Pi_{1}^{ab}(\partial;s)

\displaystyle\equiv\delta^{ab}-s^{-1}\partial^{a}\partial^{b},

(308)

\displaystyle\Pi_{2}^{ab;\,cd}(\partial;s)

\displaystyle\equiv-\frac{1}{d-1}\Pi_{1}^{ab}(\partial;s)\Pi_{1}^{cd}(\partial;s)+\frac{1}{2}\,\Pi_{1}^{ac}(\partial;s)\Pi_{1}^{bd}(\partial;s)+\frac{1}{2}\,\Pi_{1}^{ad}(\partial;s)\Pi_{1}^{bc}(\partial;s).

Appendix E
Scalar propagators

In this appendix we compute the explicit form of the scalar Euclidean, Wightman, Feynman, retarded and advanced propagators. In what follows we completely ignore the case of coincident points, in other words
x\neq 0
.

Euclidean propagator

Let us start with the scalar Euclidean propagator in position space. It was defined in (
303
). Let us repeat its definition here again for convenience

\Delta_{E}(x;s)\equiv\int\frac{d^{d}p}{(2\pi)^{d}}\frac{e^{ip\cdot x}}{p^{2}+s}.

(309)

We emphasise that in the Euclidean signature
x^{2}>0
and
p^{2}\geq 0
.
Switching to spherical coordinates we get

\displaystyle\Delta_{E}(x;s)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Omega_{d-1}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{p^{d-1}dp}{p^{2}+s}\int_{-1}^{+1}d\xi(1-\xi^{2})^{\frac{d-3}{2}}\,e^{ipr\xi}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d/2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{p^{d-1}dp}{p^{2}+s}\;\frac{J_{\frac{d-2}{2}}(pr)}{(pr)^{\frac{d-2}{2}}}

(310)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d/2}}\left(\frac{\sqrt{s}}{r}\right)^{\frac{d-2}{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{k^{\frac{d}{2}}dk}{k^{2}+1}J_{\frac{d-2}{2}}(rk\sqrt{s}).

In the second line we have defined
r\equiv\sqrt{x^{2}}
and used the result 3.387 2. from
Gradshteyn:1702455
. The symbol
J_{n}(x)
stands for the Bessel function of the first kind. The last integral in (
310
) is given by 6.565 4. in
Gradshteyn:1702455
. It converges only for
d<5
. Performing it we obtain the final expression

\Delta_{E}(x;s)=\frac{s^{\frac{d-2}{2}}}{(2\pi)^{d/2}}\times\frac{K_{\frac{d-2}{2}}(\sqrt{sx^{2}})}{\left(\sqrt{sx^{2}}\right)^{\frac{d-2}{2}}},

(311)

where
K_{n}(x)
denotes the Bessel function of the second kind. The result (
311
) is analytic in
d
. Thus, even though the integral in (
310
) does not converge, we can define it via the analytic continuation of (
311
).

Wightman propagator

The Wightman propagator is defined in (
58
). We remind that
s>0
. Integrating over the
\delta
-function we get

\displaystyle\Delta_{W}(x;s)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}\int\frac{d^{d-1}\vec{k}}{(2\pi)^{d-1}}\;\frac{e^{-i\hat{x}^{0}\sqrt{s+\vec{k}^{2}}}e^{i\vec{k}\cdot\vec{x}}}{2\sqrt{s+\vec{k}^{2}}},

(312)

where as usual time
x^{0}
has a small imaginary part according to

\hat{x}^{0}\equiv x^{0}-i\epsilon.

(313)

To perform the rest of integrals in (
312
) we split the discussion into two distinct situations: when
x^{2}>0
(space-like separation) and when
x^{2}<0
(time-like separation). As indicated in the beginning of the section we will completely exclude the light-like separation
x^{2}=0
from the discussion.

Let us consider first the space-like separation of points. In this situation we can perform the Lorentz transformation to set
x^{0}=0
. We can then perform the integral (
312
) as follows

\displaystyle\Delta_{W}(x;s)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Omega_{d-2}}{2\,(2\pi)^{d-1}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{dk\,k^{d-2}}{\sqrt{s+k^{2}}}\int_{-1}^{+1}d\eta\,(1-\eta^{2})^{\frac{d-4}{2}}e^{ik\chi\eta}

(314)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}\;(2\pi)^{-d/2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{dk\,k^{d-2}}{\sqrt{s+k^{2}}}\frac{J_{\frac{d-3}{2}}(k\chi)}{(k\chi)^{\frac{d-3}{2}}}

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{-d/2}\,s^{\frac{d-2}{2}}\frac{K_{\frac{d-2}{2}}(\chi\sqrt{s})}{(\chi\sqrt{s})^{\frac{d-2}{2}}}.

In the first line of (
314
) we have switched to the spherical coordinates in
d-1
dimensions and defined
k\equiv|\vec{k}|
together with
\chi\equiv|\vec{x}|
. We performed the integration over
d-3
angles to get the spherical angle
\Omega_{d-2}
, where

\Omega_{n}\equiv\frac{n\,\pi^{n/2}}{\Gamma(n/2+1)}.

(315)

The variable
\eta
reflects the integration over the last remaining angle. For details see formulas (A.3) - (A.6) in
Karateev:2019ymz
.
In the second line of (
314
) we use the result 3.387 2. from
Gradshteyn:1702455
. The function
J_{n}(x)
stands for the Bessel function of the first kind. Finally in the third line of (
314
) we use formula 6.564 1. from
Gradshteyn:1702455
. The function
K_{n}
is called the Bessel function of the second kind.

Let us now consider the time-like separation of points, namely when
x^{2}<0
. In this situation we can perform the Lorentz transformation to set
\vec{x}=0
. We can then perform the integral (
312
) as follows

\displaystyle\Delta_{W}(x;s)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Omega_{d-1}}{2\,(2\pi)^{d-1}}\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}\int_{0}^{\infty}dk\,k^{d-2}\frac{e^{-i\hat{x}^{0}\sqrt{s+k^{2}}}}{\sqrt{s+k^{2}}},

(316)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Omega_{d-1}\,s^{\frac{d-2}{2}}}{2\,(2\pi)^{d-1}}\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}\int_{1}^{\infty}d\xi\,(\xi^{2}-1)^{\frac{d-3}{2}}\,e^{-i\hat{x}^{0}\sqrt{s}\,\xi}

\displaystyle=\frac{i\pi}{2}(2\pi)^{-d/2}s^{\frac{d-2}{2}}\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{H^{(1)}_{\frac{2-d}{2}}(i\epsilon-x^{0}\sqrt{s})}{(i\epsilon-x^{0}\sqrt{s})^{\frac{d-2}{2}}}.

In the first line of (
316
) we switch to the spherical coordinates in
d-1
dimensions and defined
k\equiv|\vec{k}|
.
In the second line of (
316
) we performed yet another change of variables where
\xi\equiv\sqrt{1-s^{-1}k^{2}}
. Finally in the last line of (
316
) we use formula 3.387 4. from
Gradshteyn:1702455
. Notice that it was crucial to have a small imaginary part in order to define the integral properly. The function
H_{n}^{(1)}
is called the Hankel function of the first kind. Notice also that the time component
x^{0}
does not have a definite sign.

We still need to perform some work to bring the result (
316
) to its final form. To do that we split (
316
) into two parts: one with
x^{0}>0
and one with
x^{0}<0
. Using properties of the Hankel function we get then

\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{H^{(1)}_{\frac{2-d}{2}}(i\epsilon-x^{0}\sqrt{s})}{(i\epsilon-x^{0}\sqrt{s})^{\frac{d-2}{2}}}=-\theta(x^{0})\frac{H^{(2)}_{\frac{2-d}{2}}(x^{0}\sqrt{s})}{(x^{0}\sqrt{s})^{\frac{d-2}{2}}}+\theta(-x^{0})\frac{H^{(1)}_{\frac{2-d}{2}}(-x^{0}\sqrt{s})}{(-x^{0}\sqrt{s})^{\frac{d-2}{2}}}.

(317)

We can now perform the Lorentz transformation in order to write the expressions (
314
) and (
316
) in a generic frame. Effectively this is done by replacing
\chi\rightarrow\sqrt{x^{2}}
and
x^{0}\rightarrow\pm\sqrt{-x^{2}}
. Taking this and (
317
) into account we arrive at the final expression for the scalar Wightman propagator

\displaystyle\Delta_{W}

\displaystyle(x;s)=(2\pi)^{-d/2}\,s^{\frac{d-2}{2}}\times

(318)

\displaystyle\left(\theta(+x^{2})\,\frac{K_{\frac{d-2}{2}}(\sqrt{sx^{2}})}{(\sqrt{sx^{2}})^{\frac{d-2}{2}}}-\frac{i\pi}{2}\theta(-x^{2})\frac{\theta(+x^{0})H^{(2)}_{\frac{2-d}{2}}(\sqrt{-sx^{2}})-\theta(-x^{0})H^{(1)}_{\frac{2-d}{2}}(\sqrt{-sx^{2}})}{(\sqrt{-sx^{2}})^{\frac{d-2}{2}}}\right).

Feynman propagator

The scalar Feynman propagator was defined in (
129
). Using (
318
) we can get its explicit expression which reads as

\displaystyle-i\Delta_{F}(x;s)

\displaystyle\equiv\theta(x^{0})\Delta_{W}(x;s)+\theta(-x^{0})\Delta_{W}(-x;s)

(319)

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{-d/2}\,s^{\frac{d-2}{2}}\times\Bigg(\theta(+x^{2})\,\frac{K_{\frac{d-2}{2}}(\sqrt{sx^{2}})}{(\sqrt{sx^{2}})^{\frac{d-2}{2}}}-\frac{i\pi}{2}\theta(-x^{2})\frac{H^{(2)}_{\frac{2-d}{2}}(\sqrt{-sx^{2}})}{(\sqrt{-sx^{2}})^{\frac{d-2}{2}}}\Bigg).

For completeness let us also introduce the retarded and advanced propagators

\displaystyle-i\Delta_{R}(x;s)\equiv+\theta(+x^{0})D(x;s),

(320)

\displaystyle-i\Delta_{A}(x;s)\equiv-\theta(-x^{0})D(x;s),

where we have defined

\displaystyle D(x;s)

\displaystyle\equiv\Delta_{W}(x;s)-\Delta_{W}(-x;s)

(321)

\displaystyle=-(2\pi)^{-d/2}\,s^{\frac{d-2}{2}}\theta(-x^{2})\times i\pi\Big(\theta(x^{0})-\theta(-x^{0})\Big)\,\frac{J_{\frac{2-d}{2}}(\sqrt{-sx^{2}})}{\sqrt{-sx^{2}}^{\frac{d-2}{2}}}.

To obtain this result we have plugged (
318
) into the first line of (
321
) and rewrote the sum of two Hankel functions as a Bessel function of the first kind.

The results for Feynman, retarded and advance propagators are well known in
d=2
and
d=4
dimensions, see for example
Johnston:2009fr
for the clean summary. The scalar Feynman propagator in general dimensions was also computed in
Zhang:2008jy
, see formula (27). Our results match the ones present in the literature. In order to perform the comparison note the following properties of the special functions

d\geq 2:\quad H^{(1)}_{\frac{2-d}{2}}(z)=(+i)^{d-2}H^{(1)}_{\frac{d-2}{2}}(z),\qquad H^{(2)}_{\frac{2-d}{2}}(z)=(-i)^{d-2}H^{(2)}_{\frac{d-2}{2}}(z),

(322)

together with

\displaystyle d\geq 2\;\;\text{(even)}:\quad

\displaystyle J_{\frac{2-d}{2}}(z)=i^{d-2}J_{\frac{d-2}{2}}(z),

(323)

\displaystyle d\geq 3\;\;\text{(odd)}:\quad

\displaystyle J_{\frac{2-d}{2}}(z)=i^{d-1}Y_{\frac{d-2}{2}}(z),

(324)

where
Y_{n}
is the Neumann function and
z
is real.

Consistency check

For the space-like separation of points
x^{2}>0
the Euclidean, the Wightman and the Feynman propagators are simply related as

x^{2}>0:\qquad\Delta_{E}(x;s)=\Delta_{W}(x;s)=-i\Delta_{F}(x;s).

(325)

For the time-like separation of points
x^{2}<0
one should be able to obtain the expressions (
318
) and (
319
) using the analytic continuation of the Euclidean result. This re-derivation should be seen as the consistency check of the above computations. For simplicity let us work in the frame where
\vec{x}=0
, in other words
x^{2}=-t_{L}^{2}
, where
t_{L}
is the Lorentzian time.

Let us start from the Wightman propagator. Using the first line of (
294
) we can write

\Delta_{W}(t_{L};s)=\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\Delta_{E}(\epsilon+it_{L};s).

(326)

Using the explicit expression (
311
) we get

\Delta_{W}(t_{L};s)=\frac{s^{\frac{d-2}{2}}}{(2\pi)^{d/2}}\times\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\frac{K_{\frac{d-2}{2}}(\sqrt{s(\epsilon+it_{L})^{2}})}{\left(\sqrt{s(\epsilon+it_{L})^{2}}\right)^{\frac{d-2}{2}}}.

(327)

By splitting this expression in two distinct cases of
t_{L}<0
and
t_{L}>0
we can take the limit explicitly

\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\sqrt{s(\epsilon+it_{L})^{2}}=\begin{cases}+i\sqrt{s\,t_{L}^{2}},\quad t_{L}>0\\
-i\sqrt{s\,t_{L}^{2}},\quad t_{L}<0.\end{cases}

(328)

The Bessel functions of the second kind are related to the Hankel functions via the following relations

\displaystyle K_{n}(+iz)=-\frac{i\pi}{2}(-i)^{n}H_{n}^{(2)}(z),\qquad K_{n}(-iz)=+\frac{i\pi}{2}(+i)^{n}H_{n}^{(1)}(z),

(329)

where
z>0
. The equivalence between the expressions (
327
) and (
318
) for
x^{2}<0
becomes obvious once the relations (
328
) and (
329
) together with (
322

Due to (
299
), (
309
) (
129
) the Feynman propagator can be obtained from the Euclidean one by means of the Wick rotation as

-i\Delta_{F}(t_{L};s)=\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\Delta_{E}(t_{L}e^{i(\pi/2-\epsilon)};s).

(330)

Using the explicit expression (
311
) and (
298
) we get

-i\Delta_{F}(t_{L};s)=\frac{s^{\frac{d-2}{2}}}{(2\pi)^{d/2}}\times\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\frac{K_{\frac{d-2}{2}}(\sqrt{-st_{L}^{2}+i\epsilon})}{\left(\sqrt{-st_{L}^{2}+i\epsilon}\right)^{\frac{d-2}{2}}}.

(331)

As before using the fact that
\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\sqrt{-st_{L}^{2}+i\epsilon}=+i\sqrt{st_{L}^{2}}
and the first entry in (
329
) we conclude that

-i\Delta_{F}(t_{L};s)=\frac{s^{\frac{d-2}{2}}}{(2\pi)^{d/2}}\times\left(-\frac{i\pi}{2}\right)\frac{H^{(2)}_{\frac{2-d}{2}}(\sqrt{st_{L}^{2}})}{\left(\sqrt{st_{L}^{2}}\right)^{\frac{d-2}{2}}}.

(332)

Here we have also used the second entry in (
322
). This expression is equivalent to (
319
) when
\vec{x}=0
or in other words when
-x^{2}=t_{L}^{2}
.

Massless limit

Let us study the massless limit
s\rightarrow 0
of the explicit expressions for the Euclidean, Wightman and Feynman propagators given by (
311
), (
318
) and (
319
) respectively. We start with the Wightman propagator (
318
). Expanding it at
s=0
to the leading order in

we get

\displaystyle d=2\quad\text{and}\quad x^{2}>0:

\displaystyle\Delta_{W}(x;s)=-\frac{\log(x^{2})}{4\pi}-\frac{2\gamma+\log(s/4)}{4\pi}+O(s),

(333)

\displaystyle d\geq 3\quad\text{and}\quad x^{2}>0:

\displaystyle\Delta_{W}(x;s)=\frac{\Gamma(\frac{d-2}{2})}{4\pi^{d/2}(x^{2})^{\frac{d-2}{2}}}+O(s).

In the first expression
\gamma
is the Euler constant. We see that for
d\geq 3
the scalar Wightman propagator is completely finite at
s=0
. In
d=2
it has a divergent part. For the space-like separation of points
x^{2}>0
due to the relation (
325

For the time-like separation of points in
d\geq 3
we get

\displaystyle x^{2}<0:

\displaystyle\Delta_{W}(x;s)=\frac{(-i)^{d-2}\Gamma(\frac{d-2}{2})}{4\pi^{d/2}(-x^{2})^{\frac{d-2}{2}}}\left(\theta(x^{0})+(-1)^{d-2}\theta(-x^{0})\right)+O(s),

(334)

\displaystyle x^{2}<0:

\displaystyle-i\Delta_{F}(x;s)=\frac{(-i)^{d-2}\Gamma(\frac{d-2}{2})}{4\pi^{d/2}(-x^{2})^{\frac{d-2}{2}}}+O(s).

Spinning Wightman propagators are obtained by taking derivatives with respect to coordinates. Thus, spinning propagators are finite at

Appendix F
Spectral densities and central charges: technical details

In section
2
we have derived the sum rules for the central charges
C_{J}
and
C_{T}
in terms of the Euclidean two-point functions of the conserved current and the stress-tensor respectively. They are given in (
17
) and (
37
). In this appendix we will derive their implications for the spectral densities.

We can plug the expressions (
304
) and (
305
) into the sum rules (
17
) and (
37
), and obtain the desired relation between the central charges and spectral densities. The details of this manipulations are subtle. In what follows we carefully derive the result for conserved currents and then simply state the answer for the stress-tensor.

Conserved currents

Using (
304
) and (
306
) we can rewire (
17
) as

\displaystyle C_{J}^{UV}-C_{J}^{IR}=\lim_{r_{min}\rightarrow 0}\lim_{r_{max}\rightarrow\infty}\int_{r_{min}}^{r_{max}}dr\,\int_{0}^{\infty}ds\rho_{J}^{1}(s)\,f_{d}(r;s),

(335)

where
r\equiv\sqrt{x^{2}}
and we have defined

\displaystyle f_{d}(r;s)=r^{2d-3}\,(\delta^{ab}+(d-2)r^{-2}x^{a}x^{b})(s\,\delta^{ab}-\partial^{a}\partial^{b})\Delta_{E}(x;s).

(336)

We can explicitly evaluate this function by using (
311
) and taking its derivatives. Performing the straightforward algebra we get

f_{d}(r;s)=\frac{d-1}{(2\pi)^{d/2}}\times s^{\frac{3-d}{2}}(r\sqrt{s})^{\frac{3(d-2)}{2}}\times\left((r\sqrt{s})K_{\frac{d+2}{2}}(r\sqrt{s})-2(d-1)K_{\frac{d}{2}}(r\sqrt{s})\right).

(337)

We can now plug the function (
337
) into (
335
), exchange the order of integrals and make a change of variables from
r
to the dimensionless quantity
u\equiv r\sqrt{s}
. We get

C_{J}^{UV}-C_{J}^{IR}=\frac{d-1}{(2\pi)^{d/2}}\lim_{r_{min}\rightarrow 0}\lim_{r_{max}\rightarrow\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{ds}{s^{d/2-1}}\rho_{J}^{1}(s)\,\Big(F_{d}(r_{max}\sqrt{s})-F_{d}(r_{min}\sqrt{s})\Big),

(338)

where we have defined the function
F_{d}
as an indefinite (primitive) integral

F_{d}(u)\equiv\int du\,u^{\frac{3(d-2)}{2}}\left(uK_{\frac{d+2}{2}}(u)-2(d-1)K_{\frac{d}{2}}(u)\right).

(339)

In order to obtain the final version of the sum rule (
338
) first, we need to know the limits of the
F_{d}(u)
functions. One can find that (up to an irrelevant additive constant)

Form factor normalization

The stress-tensor defines the generators of translations as

P^{\mu}\equiv\int d^{d-1}xT^{0\mu}(x).

(351)

Let us now evaluate the matrix element of
P^{\mu}
with one-particle states. By convention the one-particle states obey the following normalization

\langle m_{1},\vec{p}_{1}|m_{2},\vec{p}_{2}\rangle=2p_{1}^{0}\delta_{m_{1}m_{2}}\times(2\pi)^{d-1}\delta^{(d-1)}(\vec{p}_{1}-\vec{p}_{2}).

(352)

Since the one-particle states are the eigenstates of translations, for identical particles one gets

\langle m,\vec{p}_{1}|P^{\mu}|m,\vec{p}_{2}\rangle=2p_{1}^{0}p_{1}^{\mu}\times(2\pi)^{d-1}\delta^{(d-1)}(\vec{p}_{1}-\vec{p}_{2}),

(353)

where from the definition of one-particle states one has

p_{1}^{2}=p_{2}^{2}=-m^{2}.

(354)

On the other hand using (
351
) and (
63

\displaystyle\langle m,\vec{p}_{1}|P^{\mu}|m,\vec{p}_{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle m,\vec{p}_{1}|T^{0\mu}(0)|m,\vec{p}_{2}\rangle\times\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}d^{d-1}x\,e^{i(p_{2}-p_{1})\cdot x}

\displaystyle=\langle m,\vec{p}_{1}|T^{0\mu}(0)|m,\vec{p}_{2}\rangle\times(2\pi)^{d-1}\delta^{(d-1)}(\vec{p}_{1}-\vec{p}_{2}).

(355)
