Large sets avoiding infinite arithmetic / geometric progressions Thanks: The third author was supported by the Hungarian National Research, Development ad Innovation Office - NKFIH, 124749 and 129335.
Alex Burgin, Samuel Goldberg, Tamás Keleti, Connor MacMahon, Xianzhi Wang

Abstract
. We study some variants of the Erdős similarity problem. We pose the question if every measurable subset of the real line with positive measure contains a similar copy of an infinite geometric progression. We construct a compact subset E E of the real line such that 0 0 is a Lebesgue density point of E E , but E E does not contain any (non-constant) infinite geometric progression. We give a sufficient density type condition that guarantees that a set contains an infinite geometric progression. By slightly improving a recent result of Bradford, Kohut and Mooroogen [ 1 ] , we construct a closed set F ⊂ [ 0 , ∞ ) F\subset[0,\infty) such that the measure of F ∩ [ t , t + 1 ] F\cap[t,t+1] tends to 1 1 at infinity but F F does not contain any infinite arithmetic progression. We also slightly improve a more general recent result by Kolountzakis and Papageorgiou [ 2 ] for more general sequences. We give a sufficient condition that guarantees that a given Cantor type set contains at least one infinite geometric progression with any quotient between 0 0 and 1 1 . This can be applied to most symmetric Cantor sets of positive measure.



1.
Introduction

In 1974 Pál Erdős
[
3
]
posed the question if for any infinite set
A\subset\mathbb{R}

there exists a set
E\subset\mathbb{R}
with positive Lebesgue measure such that
E
contains no similar copies of
A
(here by a
similar copy
of a set
S\subset\mathbb{R}
we mean a set of the form
aS+b=\{ax+b:x\in S\}
, where
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
and
a\neq 0
). The statement that the answer is positive is called the
Erdős similarity conjecture
, although it is not clear if Erdős himself expected an affirmative answer.
There are a lot of partial results (see e.g.
[
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
,
8
]
), but none so far that conclusively answer the question.

It is easy to see that it is enough to check the conjecture for
A=\{a_{1},a_{2},\ldots\}
, where
(a_{n})
is a sequence converging to zero.
In 1984 Falconer
[
4
]
showed the conjecture for all slowly converging sequences (in the sense that
a_{n+1}/a_{n}\rightarrow 1
).
Surprisingly, there is no single exponentially quickly convergent sequence
(a_{n})
for which it is known if the Erdős similarity conjecture holds.
In particular it is not known if there exists a
q\in(0,1)
such that every measurable set
E\subset\mathbb{R}
with positive Lebesgue measure contains a similar copy of the sequence
(q^{n})
.
Clearly, the Erdős similarity conjecture would imply that such
q
cannot exist.
Here we pose the following possibly easier question:

Question 1
.

Does every measurable set
E\subset\mathbb{R}
of positive Lebesgue measure contain a sequence of the form
(aq^{n}+b)_{n=1}^{\infty}
for some
a,b,q\in\mathbb{R}
,
a\neq 0
,
q\in(0,1)
?

We do not know the answer to Question
1
.
A natural attempt to prove an affirmative answer could be taking a Lebesgue density point
b
of
E
and showing that for such a
b
we can find
a\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
and
q\in(0,1)
such that
(aq^{n}+b)_{n=1}^{\infty}\subset E
.
One of the main results of this paper is the following result, which shows that this approach does not work.

Theorem 1.1
.

There exists a compact set
E\subset\mathbb{R}
such that

0
is a Lebesgue density point of
E
but

E
does not contain any (non-constant) infinite
geometric progression
(aq^{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
.

After seeing that a density point at zero does not guarantee that the set contains an infinite
geometric progression, it is natural to seek for other density type conditions that do guarantee geometric progressions.
In Proposition
3.1
we
give such a sufficient condition.
Then we show that there exist measurable sets of
positive Lebesgue measure for which this density type condition does not hold for any point.
Thus this approach cannot give an answer to Question
1
either.

In
[
6
]
Kolountzakis proved that for any infinite set
A\subset\mathbb{R}
there exists a measurable set
E\subset[0,1]
of measure arbitrarily close to
1
such that almost every similar copy of
A
is not contained in
E
.
By a slight modification of the original proof of Kolountzakis
[
6
, Theorem 1]
we show that
there exists a measurable set
E\subset[0,1]
of measure arbitrarily close to
1
such that almost every similar copy of almost every infinite geometric progression is not contained in
E
.

Note that
E
contains an infinite
geometric progression if and only if

\log(E\cap(0,\infty))
or
\log((-E)\cap(0,\infty))

contains an infinite arithmetic progression.
Therefore the problem of finding a sufficient condition that guarantees that a set contains an infinite
geometric progression is closely related to finding a sufficient condition that guarantees that a set contains an infinite arithmetic progression.
This latter problem has been studied by L. Bradford, H. Kohut and Y. Mooroogen in a very recent paper
[
1
]
.
They proved the following result.
Here and in the sequel
\lambda
denotes Lebesgue measure.

Theorem 1.2

(Bradford et al.
[
1
]
)
.

For any
\varepsilon>0

there exists
S\subset\mathbb{R}
satisfying

\displaystyle\lambda(S\cap[t,t+1])\geq 1-\varepsilon

for each real
t
such that
S
does not contain any infinite arithmetic progression.

We slightly improve the above result by proving the following.

Theorem 1.3
.

There exists a closed set
F\subset[0,\infty)
satisfying

(1)

\lim_{t\to\infty}\lambda(F\cap[t,t+1])=1

such that
F
does not contain any infinite arithmetic progression.

The importance of this improvement is that in Lemma
2.5
we prove that (
1
) holds if and only if
0
is a density point of
\exp(-F)\cup-\exp(-F)
.
Thus Theorem
1.3
will easily imply Theorem
1.1
.

In an even more recent paper Kolountzakis and Papageorgiou
[
2
]
improved Theorem
1.2
in the following different way.

Theorem 1.4

(Koluntzakis-Papageorgiou
[
2
]
)
.

Let
A=\{a_{1},a_{2},\ldots\}\subset(0,\infty)
such that

(2)

a_{n+1}-a_{n}\geq 1\quad(\forall n\in\mathbb{N})\qquad\text{and}\qquad\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log a_{n}}{n}=0.

Then for every
\varepsilon>0
there exists a Lebesgue measurable set
E\subset\mathbb{R}
such that

(3)

\lambda(E\cap[t,t+1])\geq 1-\varepsilon\qquad(\forall t\in\mathbb{R}),

but
E
does not contain any similar copy of
A
.

We slightly improve this result by showing Theorem
2.4
, which states that, similarly as above, in Theorem
1.4
we can replace (
3
) by the requirement

\lim_{t\to\infty}\lambda(E\cap[t,t+1])=1.

Finally, in Section
4
we study the following two closely related problems:
How can we guarantee that a given subset of
\mathbb{R}
contains infinite arithmetic progressions with every positive common difference,
or infinite geometric progressions with every quotient between
0
and
1
?
As an application we show (Corollaries
4.4
and
4.6
) that under mild conditions all
symmetric Cantor sets with positive measure
contain geometric progressions with any given quotient between
0
and
1
.

To avoid trivial examples, by arithmetic progressions and geometric progressions we mean only non-constant sequences.
Since any infinite geometric progression with negative ratio contains an infinite geometric progression with positive ratio, and since geometric progressions with ratio greater than
1
are not contained in any bounded set, we consider only geometric progressions with ratio between
0
and
1
.

First we prove two simple lemmas from which Theorem

Lemma 2.1
.

Given
\varepsilon>0
, we can find
E\subset[0,\infty)
which contains no arithmetic progression of irrational common difference such that
\lambda(E\cap[t,t+1])\geq 1-\varepsilon
for
every
t\geq 0

Proof.

Fix
\varepsilon>0
. Let
E:=\bigcup_{n=0}^{\infty}[n,n+1-\varepsilon]
. Clearly,
\lambda(E\cap[t,t+1])\geq 1-\varepsilon
for
every
t\geq 0
.
Let
(b+n\Delta)_{n=0}^{\infty}
be an arithmetic progression with
\Delta
irrational. Since
\Delta
is irrational,
(\{b+n\Delta\})_{n=0}^{\infty}
is dense in
[0,1)
, where
\{\cdot\}
denotes the fractional part. Hence,
E
contains no arithmetic progression of irrational common difference
\Delta
.
∎

Lemma 2.2
.

Fix
\varepsilon>0
.
Let
\Delta_{1},\Delta_{2},\dots
be a countable collection of positive real numbers. Then there exists a measurable
E\subset[0,\infty)
containing no arithmetic progression with common difference
\Delta_{n}
for all
n
, and such that
\lambda(E\cap[t,t+1])\geq 1-\varepsilon
for every non-negative real
t
.

Proof.

Let
\phi:\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}
be onto and denote
\phi(n)=(\phi_{1}(n),\phi_{2}(n))
.
Let
m_{1},m_{2},\ldots
be a quickly increasing sequence of positive integers that we choose later. Let

J_{n}=((\phi_{2}(n)-1)\varepsilon/2,(\phi_{2}(n)+1)\varepsilon/2)\qquad\text{and}\qquad I_{n}=J_{n}+m_{n}\Delta_{\phi_{1}(n)}.

Since
I_{n}
intersects every arithmetic progression
(b+k\Delta_{\phi_{1}(n)})_{k=0}^{\infty}
for
every
b\in J_{n}
and
\phi
is onto,
the set
E=[0,\infty)\setminus\cup_{n=1}^{\infty}I_{n}
does not contain an infinite arithmetic progression with common difference
\Delta_{n}
for all
n
.

By induction we can make the sequence
(m_{n})
grow fast enough to guarantee that
\inf I_{n}>1+\sup I_{n-1}
for every
n
.
This property clearly guarantees that

\lambda(E\cap[t,t+1])\geq 1-\varepsilon
for every non-negative real
t
.
∎

1.2

Fix

To get our improvements we need the following lemma.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
\alpha_{n}
be an increasing sequence of positive reals such that
\alpha_{n}\to\infty
.
Suppose that for any
\varepsilon>0
and
n\in\mathbb{N}

there exists a measurable set
E(\varepsilon,n)\subset\mathbb{R}

such that
E(\varepsilon,n)
does not contain any similiar copy
of
\{\alpha_{n},\alpha_{n+1},\ldots\}
and

\lambda(E(\varepsilon,n)\cap[t,t+1])>1-\varepsilon\qquad(\forall t>0).

Then there exists a closed set
F\subset\mathbb{R}
that does
not contain any similiar copy
of
\{\alpha_{n},\alpha_{n+1},\ldots\}
for any
n
and

\lim_{t\to\infty}\lambda(F\cap[t,t+1])=1.

Proof.

For
k\in\mathbb{N}
let
E_{k}=\cap_{n=1}^{\infty}E(1/k,n)
.
Then
E_{k}
does not contain any similar copy of
\{\alpha_{n},\alpha_{n+1},\ldots\}
for any

\lambda(E_{k}\cap[t,t+1])>1-\frac{1}{k}\qquad(\forall t>0).

By taking a closed subset we can suppose that
E_{k}
is closed.
Let
G_{k}=\mathbb{R}\setminus E_{k}
.

For any set
X\subset\mathbb{R}
let

H_{m}(X)=\{(a,b)\in(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}\ :\ (\exists n>m)\ a\alpha_{n}+b\in X\}.

Note that
H_{m}(X)
is open if
X
is open and by assumption

H_{m}(G_{k})=(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}
for any

and

Hence

[1/k,k]\times[-k,k]\subset\bigcup_{h>0}H_{m}(G_{k}\cap(-\infty,h)),

Since for any
(a,b)\in[1/k,k]\times[-k,k]
and

a\alpha_{n}+b>\frac{\alpha_{m}}{k}-k
, we obtain that

[1/k,k]\times[-k,k]\subset H_{m}\left(G_{k}\cap\left(\frac{\alpha_{m}}{k}-k,h_{k,m}\right)\right).

By induction, since
\alpha_{n}\to\infty
, we can choose a sequence of positive integers

such that

\frac{\alpha_{m_{k}}}{k}-k>h_{k-1,m_{k-1}}+1.

Finally, let

G=\bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty}G_{k}\cap\left(\frac{\alpha_{m_{k}}}{k}-k,h_{k,m_{k}}\right)

and take

.
It is easy to check that

In order to show that Theorem
1.3
implies Theorem
1.1
we need the following Lemma.

Lemma 2.5
.

The following three conditions are equivalent for any measurable
E\subset(0,\infty)
:

\displaystyle\text{(i)}\quad\lim_{t\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{\lambda(E\cap[0,t])}{t}=1,

\displaystyle\text{(ii)}\quad\lim_{\mu\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\lambda(-\log E\cap[\mu,\mu+\nu])}{\nu}=1\quad\text{for all fixed}\quad\nu>0,

\displaystyle\text{(iii)}\quad\lim_{\mu\rightarrow\infty}{\lambda(-\log E\cap[\mu,\mu+1])}=1.

Proof.

Let
F=(0,\infty)\setminus E
.
Writing
\lambda(E\cap[0,t])/t=1-\lambda(F\cap[0,t])/t=1-\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}\mathds{1}_{F}(r)d\lambda(r)
and applying a change of variable
x=-\log r
, condition
(i)
is equivalent to

\displaystyle(i^{\prime})\qquad\qquad\lim_{\mu\rightarrow\infty}e^{\mu}\int_{\mu}^{\infty}\mathds{1}_{-\log F}(x)e^{-x}d\lambda(x)=0,

where
t=e^{-\mu}
.
We can clearly rewrite
(ii) as

\displaystyle(ii^{\prime})\qquad(\forall\nu>0)\quad\lim_{\mu\rightarrow\infty}\int_{\mu}^{\mu+\nu}\mathds{1}_{-\log F}(x)d\lambda(x)=0,

and (iii) as

\displaystyle(iii^{\prime})\qquad\qquad\qquad\lim_{\mu\rightarrow\infty}\int_{\mu}^{\mu+1}\mathds{1}_{-\log F}(x)d\lambda(x)=0,

therefore it is enough to prove
(i^{\prime})\Longleftrightarrow(ii^{\prime})\Longleftrightarrow(iii^{\prime})
.

First we show
(i^{\prime})\implies(ii^{\prime})
.
We have the following for all
\mu,\nu>0
by the non-negativity of the function
\mathds{1}_{-\log F}(x)e^{-x}
and the monotonicity of the integral:

\displaystyle e^{\mu+\nu}\int_{\mu}^{\infty}\mathds{1}_{-\log F}(x)e^{-x}d\lambda(x)\geq\int_{\mu}^{\mu+\nu}\mathds{1}_{-\log F}(x)e^{\mu+\nu-x}d\lambda(x)\geq\int_{\mu}^{\mu+\nu}\mathds{1}_{-\log F}(x)d\lambda(x)\geq 0.

Now, letting
\mu\rightarrow\infty
and applying the Squeeze Theorem with condition
(i^{\prime})
, we obtain
(ii^{\prime})
.

Since
(ii^{\prime})\implies(iii^{\prime})
is clear it remains to show
(iii^{\prime})\implies(i^{\prime})
.
For any positive integer
k
we have

\displaystyle e^{\mu}\int_{\mu}^{\infty}\mathds{1}_{-\log F}(x)e^{-x}d\lambda(x)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{k}\int_{\mu+j-1}^{\mu+j}\mathds{1}_{-\log F}(x)e^{\mu-x}d\lambda(x)+\int_{\mu+k}^{\infty}e^{\mu-x}d\lambda(x)

\displaystyle\leq k\cdot\int_{\mu}^{\mu+1}\mathds{1}_{-\log F}(x)d\lambda(x)+e^{-k}.

Letting
\mu\rightarrow\infty
and using
(iii^{\prime})
we obtain that
the limit of the left-hand side is at most
e^{-k}

for all
k
, so
the limit is zero as desired.
∎

3.
A sufficient density condition that guarantees infinite geometric progression

We give a sufficient condition for a measurable set to contain an infinite geometric progression.

Proposition 3.1
.

Suppose that
E\subset\mathbb{R}
is a measurable set and for some fixed
r_{0}>0
we have

(7)

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\int_{E^{c}\cap[0,r_{0}^{n}]}\frac{1}{n}x^{\frac{1}{n}-1}\,d\lambda(x)<\infty,

where
E^{c}
denotes the complement of
E
.
Then there exists
a>0
and
r\in(0,1)
such that
(ar^{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset E
.

Proof.

We proceed by proving the contrapositive,
so we suppose that there exists no
a>0
and
r\in(0,1)
such that
(ar^{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset E
.
Then for each
r\in(0,1)
, there exists a strictly increasing sequence of natural numbers
(n_{m})_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
dependent on
r
such that
(r^{n_{m}})_{m\in\mathbb{N}}\subset E^{c}
. Now, fix
m\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{0\}
.
Let
G_{n}(x)=x^{\frac{1}{n}}
.
By
\sigma
-subadditivity
and by applying a change of variables
r=G_{n}(x)=x^{\frac{1}{n}}

we obtain

\displaystyle r_{0}

\displaystyle=\lambda([0,r_{0}])=\lambda(\{r\in[0,r_{0}]:r^{n}\in E^{c}\text{ for some }n\geq m\})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=m}^{\infty}\lambda(\{r\in[0,r_{0}]:r^{n}\in E^{c}\}=\sum_{n=m}^{\infty}\int_{G_{n}(E^{c})\cap[0,r_{0}]}\,d\lambda(r)=\sum_{n=m}^{\infty}\int_{E^{c}\cap[0,r_{0}^{n}]}\frac{1}{n}x^{\frac{1}{n}-1}\,d\lambda(x).

Since we have bounded the tail of the non-negative series below by a positive constant, it diverges to infinity.
∎

Remark 1
.

Since
\int_{0}^{r_{0}^{n}}\frac{1}{n}x^{\frac{1}{n}-1}\ dx=r_{0}
,
the series of (
7
) can be viewed as
r_{0}
times a series of increasingly narrower weighted densities of
E^{c}
at 0. Thus, the sufficient condition is that these densities of
E^{c}
converge to
0
quickly enough
to make this series converge. From Theorem
1.1
, we know there exists a set with
0
as a density point which does not satisfy the sufficient condition. Yet, given measurable
E\subset\mathbb{R}
, we might hope that by translating the condition in the above proposition, we could find a viable translate for a copy of a geometric progression. However, there exist sets of large measure which do not satisfy this condition at any point with density 1, for we can make the density converge as slowly as we like:
indeed, it is well known that there is no bound for the rate of convergence at a Lebesgue density point, see for example
[
9
]
. For our purposes, this fact manifests itself in the following manner.

Lemma 3.2
.

Fix
\varepsilon>0
and let
f:(0,1)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}
be a continuous, monotone
non-decreasing
function such that
\lim_{x\rightarrow 0^{+}}f(x)=0
. Then there exists an open
G\subset(0,1)
with measure not exceeding
\varepsilon
, so that for any
p\in[0,1)
and
t\in(0,1-p)
we have

\frac{\lambda(G\cap[p,p+t])}{t}\geq f(t).

Proof.

Fix
\varepsilon>0
. Choose a sequence
1=\delta_{1}>\delta_{2}>\ldots
such that
f(\delta_{n})<\varepsilon/2^{n+1}
for every
n
.
For each
n
, choose
M_{n}\gg 0
satisfying

\displaystyle\frac{(\lfloor M_{n}x\rfloor-2)}{M_{n}x}>\frac{1}{2}

for all
x\in[\delta_{n+1},\delta_{n})
. Set
G=\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}\bigcup_{k=0}^{M_{n}-1}(\frac{k}{M_{n}},\frac{1}{M_{n}}(k+\frac{\varepsilon}{2^{n}}))
. By subadditivity, the measure of
G
does not exceed
\varepsilon
. Now, fix
p\in[0,1)
and

t\in(0,1-p)
.
Since

0<t<1=\delta_{1}
,
there exists a natural number
n_{0}
so that
t\in[\delta_{n_{0}+1},\delta_{n_{0}})
. Clearly,
(\frac{k}{M_{n_{0}}},\frac{k+1}{M_{n_{0}}})\subset[p,p+t]
for at least
\lfloor M_{n_{0}}t\rfloor-2
integers
0\leq k\leq M_{n_{0}}-1
. Hence, we have the following:

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda(G\cap[p,p+t])}{t}\geq\frac{1}{t}\lambda\bigg[\bigcup_{k=0}^{M_{n_{0}}-1}\bigg(\frac{k}{M_{n_{0}}},\frac{1}{M_{n_{0}}}\bigg(k+\frac{\varepsilon}{2^{n_{0}}}\bigg)\bigg)\cap[p,p+t]\bigg]

\displaystyle\geq\frac{(\lfloor M_{n_{0}}t\rfloor-2)\varepsilon}{M_{n_{0}}t2^{n_{0}}}>\frac{\varepsilon}{2^{n_{0}+1}}\geq f(\delta_{n_{0}})\geq f(t).

∎

Applying the above with

as follows

\displaystyle f(x)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{\log( \log(1/x))}&x<e^{-e}\\
1&x\geq e^{-e}\end{cases}.

one can construct a closed subset

of
\mathbb{R}
with cofinite measure such that

does not satisfy the condition in Proposition

for all

3.1.
A Remark on a Result of Kolountzakis

In

6

Kolountzakis proved that for any infinite set

such that almost every similar copy of
A
is not contained in
E
; that is,

\lambda(\{(a,b)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\ :\ aA+b\subset E\})=0.

Since in this paper our goal (see Question
1
) is to find at least one similar copy of at least one infinite geometric progression it is natural to ask if there exists a set
E
as in the above theorem that works for
A=\{r^{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\}
simultaneously for every
r\in(0,1)
or at least for almost every
r\in(0,1)
.
It turns out that for almost every
r\in[0,1]
this can be obtained by a slight modification of the original proof of Kolountzakis
[
6
, Theorem 1]
.

Proposition 3.3
.

There exists a measurable set
E\subset[0,1]
of measure arbitrarily close to
1
such that almost every similar copy of almost every infinite geometric progression is not contained in
E
; that is,

\lambda(\{(r,a,b):(ar^{n}+b)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset E\})=0.

Proof.

The proof is nearly the same as in
[
6
, Theorem 1]
, so we just take note of the modifications. First, notice that, the same argument that shows that we can suppose that the scaling factor
a
is in some fixed interval
[\alpha,\beta]
for some
0<\alpha<\beta<\infty
, also shows that it suffices to consider only
r\in[r_{0},r_{1}]
where
0<r_{0}<r_{1}<1
.
Choose
m_{j}
such that
1/m_{j}<\frac{1}{2}\alpha r_{0}^{j}(1-r_{1}).
Then
1/m_{j}
is smaller than half of the minimum gap of the numbers
\alpha r^{0},\ldots,\alpha r^{j-1}
any
r\in[r_{0},r_{1}]
as in the original proof.
For the final step, observe from the last computation in the proof that we have

\mathbb{E}\lambda(\{(a,b,r):(a+br^{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset E\})=\int_{r_{0}}^{r_{1}}\mathbb{E}\lambda(\{(a,b):(a+br^{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset E\})d\lambda(r)=0,

and obtain our desired set with positive probability as in the original proof.
∎

4.
A sufficient condition for Cantor-type sets to contain scaled copies of all convergent infinite geometric progressions.

First we give a sufficient condition that guarantees arithmetic progressions with any positive common difference.

Lemma 4.1
.

If
A\subset\mathbb{R}
is a measurable set of finite Lebesgue measure
then for every
\Delta>0
the set
(0,\infty)\setminus A

contains infinite arithmetic progression with difference
\Delta
.

Proof.

Without loss of generality we can suppose that
\Delta=1
.
Let

B_{n}=A\cap(n,n+1]-n\subset(0,1].

Then

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\lambda(B_{n})=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\lambda(A\cap(n,n+1])=\lambda(A\cap(0,\infty))<\infty.

Thus, by the Borel-Cantelli lemma, almost every
a\in(0,1]

is contained only in finitely many
B_{n}
.
On the other hand, if

a\in(0,1]
is contained only in finitely many
B_{n}

then for large enough
N
the arithmetic progression

a+N,a+N+1,a+N+2,\ldots
is contained in
(0,\infty)\setminus A
.
∎

Now we can give a condition that guarantees an infinite converging geometric progression for every possible quotient.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
I_{k}=(u_{k},v_{k})\subset(0,\infty)

(k=1,2,\ldots)
be disjoint open intervals and

G=\cup_{k=1}^{\infty}I_{k}
. If

(8)

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\log v_{k}-\log u_{k}<\infty\qquad\text{or}\qquad\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{v_{k}-u_{k}}{v_{k}}<\infty

then for every
q\in(0,1)
the set

E=(0,\infty)\setminus G

contains a similar copy of
\left\{q^{n}\right\}_{n\geq 0}
converging to
0
.

Proof.

We claim that the second inequality of (
8
) implies the first one, so it is enough to prove the lemma when the first inequality of (
8
) holds.
Indeed, the second inequality implies that
u_{k}/v_{k}\to 1
,
so
v_{k}\leq 2u_{k}
for large enough
n
.
Thus, using that for any
0<u<v\leq 2u
we have

\log v-\log u=\log\left(1+\frac{v-u}{u}\right)\leq\frac{v-u}{u}\leq 2\frac{v-u}{v},

we obtain the claim.

Let
A=-\log(G)
. Then

\lambda(A)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}(\log v_{k}-\log u_{k})<\infty.

By Lemma
4.1
this implies that
(0,\infty)\setminus A
contains
infinite arithmetic progression with any given difference
\Delta>0
.
This clearly implies that

E=(0,\infty)\setminus G

contains an infinite geometric progression with any quotient
q\in(0,1)
.
∎

Definition 4.3
.

Fix a sequence
t_{0},t_{1},\ldots
such that

(9)

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}2^{k}t_{k}\leq 1.

We define the sets
E_{0}\supset E_{1}\supset\ldots
by induction such that each
E_{k}
is the union of
2^{k}
closed intervals of equal length.
Let
E_{0}=[0,1]
.
If
E_{k}
is defined then
E_{k+1}
is obtained by removing
an open interval of length
t_{k}
from the middle of each closed
interval of
E_{k}
.
Note that condition (
9
) guarantees that the length of the intervals of
E_{k}
is greater than
t_{k}
.
We call the set
E=\cap_{k=0}^{\infty}E_{k}
a
symmetric Cantor set
with parameters
(t_{k})
.

Corollary 4.4
.

If

(10)

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}2^{k}t_{k}<1\qquad\text{and}\qquad\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}2^{k}\cdot t_{k}\cdot k<\infty

then the above defined symmetric Cantor set
E=\cap_{k=0}^{\infty}E_{k}
with
parameters
(t_{k})
contains an infinite
geometric progression with every quotient
q\in(0,1)
.

Proof.

For each
k
let
I_{k,j}=(u_{k,j},v_{k,j})

with
u_{k,1}<\ldots<u_{k,2^{k}}

be the open intervals of length
t_{k}
we removed from the middle of the closed intervals of
E_{k}
.
Thus
v_{k,j}-u_{k,j}=t_{k}
for each
k
and
j
and we have

E=[0,1]\setminus\cup_{k,j}I_{k,j}
.

Let
m=\lambda(E)
. Note that the first inequality of (
10
) gives that
m>0
.
By definition, the closed intervals of
E_{k}
have length

\lambda(E_{k})/2^{k}\geq\lambda(E)/2^{k}=m/2^{k}
.
This implies that
v_{k,j}\geq(j-1/2)\cdot m/2^{k}
for each
k,j
.

Therefore

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{2^{k}}\frac{v_{k,j}-u_{k,j}}{v_{k,j}}\leq\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{2^{k}}\frac{t_{k}}{(j-1/2)\cdot m/2^{k}}=\frac{1}{m}\cdot\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}2^{k}\cdot t_{k}\cdot\sum_{j=1}^{2^{k}}\frac{1}{(j-1/2)}.

Since the inner sum of the right-handside is at most

C\log(2^k)=Ck\log 2
for some constant
C
,
the second inequality of (
10
) implies
that Lemma
4.2
can be applied to complete the proof.
∎

Definition 4.5
.

We call a symmetric Cantor set
E\subset[0,1]
a
middle-
a
fat Cantor set
if it is a symmetric Cantor set (see Definition
4.3
) with parameters
t_{k}=a^{k+1}
for some
0<a<1/3
. Note that we necessarily need
a<1/3
to preserve positive measure of our resultant set.

Corollary 4.6
.

All middle-
a
fat Cantor sets contain an infinite
geometric progression with every quotient
q\in(0,1)
.

Proof.

It is easy to see that (
10
) holds for

t_{k}=a^{k+1}
if
a\in(0,1/3)
, so Corollary
4.4
can be applied.
∎
