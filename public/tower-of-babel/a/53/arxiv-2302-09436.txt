Rarefied Thue-Morse Sums Via Automata Theory and Logic
Jeffrey Shallit

Abstract
Let t ⁡ ( n ) t(n) denote the number of 1 1 -bits in the base- 2 2 representation of n n , taken modulo 2 2 . We show how to prove the classic conjecture of Leo Moser, on the rarefied sum ∑ 0 ≤ i < n ( − 1 ) t ⁡ ( 3 ​ i ) \sum_{0\leq i<n}(-1)^{t(3i)} , using tools from automata theory and logic. The same technique can be used to prove results about analogous sums.



1
Introduction

In the 1960’s Leo Moser observed that among the first few multiples of

3
, most of them have an even number of
1
’s when expressed in base
2
.
This is true, for example, for
0,3,6,9,12,15,
and
18
; the first
counterexample is
21
. He conjectured that among the first

n
multiples of
3
, those with an even number of
1
’s always
predominate over those with an odd number of
1
’s. This is

Moser’s conjecture
.

Let
t(n)
denote the number of
1
’s appearing in the base-
2

representation of
n
, taken modulo
2
.
Then
(t(n))_{n\geq 0}
is
the classic Thue-Morse sequence
[
1
]
.
Let
b
be a positive integer,
let
0\leq j<b
, and for
n\geq 0
define

f_{b,j}(n)=\sum_{0\leq i<n}(-1)^{t(bi+j)}
.
An expression like
f_{b,j}(n)
is called a

rarefied Thue-Morse sum

[
9
]
,
and Moser’s conjecture can be restated as the claim that

f_{3,0}(n)>0
for all
n\geq 1
.

Moser’s conjecture was proved by Newman
[
16
]
, who
proved even more: he determined the asymptotic behavior of the
excess.
Newman proved that

\text{\scalebox{.8}{$\frac{1}{20}$}}\leq f_{3,0}(n)/n^{e}\leq 5,

where
e=(\log 3)/(\log 4)\doteq 0.79248
.
Later, the exact behavior of
f_{3,0}(n)
was determined
by Coquet
[
3
]
.

Since Newman’s 1969 paper, our knowledge of the behavior of
f_{b,j}(n)
has
greatly increased. See, for example,

[
17
,
3
,
8
,
9
,
10
,
5
,
11
,
25
,
4
,
7
,
22
,
23
,
12
,
2
]
.

In this paper we show how to obtain bounds like Newman’s through an
entirely different approach, via automata theory and logic.
The crucial idea is that, in some cases, there is a deterministic
finite automaton (DFA) that computes
f_{b,j}(n)
in the
following sense: it takes representations of
integers
n
and
y
in parallel, as inputs, and accepts
iff
y=f_{b,j}(n)
. Such an automaton is sometimes
called
synchronized

[
19
]
.
The novelty is that the particular
bases of representation for
n
and
y
may be different, and
they depend on
b
. Once we have the synchronized automaton, we can use
existing theorems about synchronization to understand the growth
rate of
f_{b,j}(n)
. Although typically our method does not
provide the best possible constants, in many cases it provides
decent estimates with very little work.

2
Notation

We need some notation to express base-
b
representation.
Define
\Sigma_{b}=\{0,1,\ldots,b-1\}
, the usual set of
digits for base
b
. If
x\in\Sigma_{b}^{*}
, say

x=a_{1}a_{2}\cdots a_{t}
, then we define

[x]_{b}=\sum_{1\leq i\leq t}a_{i}b^{t-i}
.
By
(n)_{b}
for
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we mean the word in
\Sigma_{b}^{*}

giving the (unique) most-significant-digit-first
representation of
n
in base
b
, with no leading zeros.
Thus, for example,
[00101011]_{2}=43
and

(43)_{2}=101011
.

In Section
6
we will also need representations for negative
integers. To do so, we (analogously) define representation in
base
(-b)
using the same digit set
\Sigma_{b}

If
x=a_{1}a_{2}\cdots a_{t}\in\Sigma_{b}^{*}
, then we define

[x]_{b}=\sum_{1\leq i\leq t}a_{i}(-b)^{t-i}
, and we
also define
(n)_{-b}
to be the (unique) word representing

n
in base
(-b)
, with no leading zeros.
Thus, for example,
[010011]_{-2}=-15
and
(-15)_{-2}=10011
.

We use regular expressions to describe the base-
b
and base-
(-b)

representations of integers. For
|b|>10
, however, the individual digits
may consist of numbers that require more than one digit to
write down in base
10
, which creates an ambiguity of how to
interpret, for example, an expression like
12
: does it represent
a two-digit number or a one-digit number using the single digit
12
?
To avoid this problem, we use brackets to indicate single base-
b

digits, when necessary. Thus for a single digit
12
we write
[12]
.

3
Automata

The first step is to show that
f_{3,0}(n)
is a
(4,3)
-synchronized
function of
n
. This means there is a DFA
that takes as input, in parallel, the base-
4

representation of
n
and the base-
3
representation of
y
and
accepts iff
y=f_{3,0}(n)
.

To get the
(4,3)
-synchronized automaton for
f_{3,0}(n)
, we
first “guess” it from empirical
data using a version of the Myhill-Nerode theorem
(see
[
13
, §3.4]
and

[
20
, §5.7]
).
It has 16 states, and is displayed in Figure
1
.

Figure 1:
(4,3)
-synchronized automaton for
f_{3,0}(n)
.

Next, we need to rigorously
prove
that our guessed automaton is correct.
This could be done tediously with a long induction, but instead
we use
Walnut

[
15
,
20
]
,
a tool that implements a decision procedure
for the first-order logical theory of the structure
\langle\mathbb{N},+,V_{k}\rangle
, where
V_{k}(n)=k^{e}
if
k^{e}
is the largest power
of
k
dividing
n
. With
Walnut
we can simply state propositions,
phrased in first-order logic, about
automata and automatic sequences, and have
Walnut
verify or
falsify them.

First, let’s check that the automaton above
really defines a function from
\mathbb{N}
to
\mathbb{N}
; that is,
that for each
n
there is exactly one
y
such that the pair
of inputs
(n,y)
is
accepted, and there are no
n
for which
(n,y_{1})
and
(n,y_{2})
are
both accepted with
y_{1}\not=y_{2}
:

eval test30_1 "An Ey $f30(n,y)":
eval test30_2 "~En,y1,y2 $f30(n,y1) & $f30(n,y2) & ?msd_3 y1!=y2":

and
Walnut
returns
TRUE
for both.

We should briefly explain the syntax of
Walnut
:

•

A
represents the universal quantifier
\forall
and

E
represents the existential quantifier
\exists

•

&
is logical
AND

•

|
is logical
OR

•

~
is logical
NOT

•

=>
is logical implication

•

msd_
b
indicates that the numbers in the expression
that follow are expressed in base
b

•

@
n
refers to the value
n
of an automatic sequence.

Next, let’s prove by induction on
n
that the automaton we found
really does compute
f_{3,0}(n)
. To do so we simply verify
the base case
f_{3,0}(0)=0
and the induction step

f_{3,0}(n+1)=f_{3,0}(n)+\begin{cases}1,&\text{if $t(3n)=0$};\\
-1,&\text{if $t(3n)=1$}.\end{cases}.

(1)

We can verify the induction step with the following
Walnut
code:

def tm4 "0->0110 1->1001":
promote TM4 tm4:
eval test30_3 "?msd_4 An,x (n>=1 & $f30(n, ?msd_3 x) &
TM4[3*n]=@0) => $f30(n+1, ?msd_3 x+1)":
eval test30_4 "?msd_4 An,x (n>=1 & $f30(n, ?msd_3 x) &
TM4[3*n]=@1) => $f30(n+1, ?msd_3 x-1)":

Here the first two commands define the Thue-Morse sequence

[
1
]
in base
4
, and the second two
commands check the correctness of Eq. (
1
).

Walnut
returns
TRUE
for both commands.
This completes the proof that our automaton is correct.

4
Results for
f_{3,0}(n)

Now that we know the automaton really does compute
f_{3,0}(n)
,
we can re-prove the following result of Newman
[
16
]
:

Theorem 1
.

We have
f_{3,0}(n)>0
for all
n\geq 1
. Furthermore,

f_{3,0}(n)=O(n^{{\log 4}\over{\log 3}})
and
this upper bound is optimal.

Proof.

For the first claim, let’s check that
f_{3,0}(n)>0
for all
n\geq 1
:

eval test30_5 "?msd_4 An,y (n>=1 & $f30(n,y)) => ?msd_3 y>0":

Next, by a theorem about synchronized sequences
[
19
, Thm. 8]
,
we know that if a function

g(n)
is
(a,b)
-synchronized for integers
a,b\geq 2
, then either

(a)

g(n)=O(n^{{\log b}\over{\log a}})
and there is a positive
constant
c
such that
g(n)>cn^{{\log b}\over{\log a}}
infinitely
often; or

(b)

g(n)=O(1)
.

In our case,
(a,b)=(4,3)
. Furthermore, we can verify that

f_{3,0}(n)\not=O(1)
as follows:

eval test30_6 "?msd_4 Ay En,x $f30(n,x) & ?msd_3 x>y":

which asserts that for every integer
y
we can find an
n

such that
f_{3,0}(n)>y
.
Walnut
returns
TRUE

for this command. Hence alternative (b) is impossible and
alternative (a) holds.
∎

The next thing we would like to do is to find, as Newman did,
upper and lower
bounds on
f_{3,0}(n)/n^{{\log 3}/{\log 4}}
.
However, if we want to compare
f_{3,0}(n)

to a function like
n^{{\log 3}/{\log 4}}
using our method, we run into
the difficulty that no finite automaton can compute arbitrary real powers
like this. To get around this difficulty, we define a kind of
“pseudopower” that an automaton can compute. Using this, we can
prove an improved version of
Newman’s original bounds, with very little effort.

Let
a,b
be integers with
2\leq a\leq b
. The particular
pseudopower that we define is

p_{a,b}(n):=[(n)_{a}]_{b}
. In other words, we first express
n
in base

a
, and then consider it as a number expressed in
base
b
.

The following theorem quantifies
how close
p_{a,b}(n)
is to
n^{{\log b}\over{\log a}}
.
(The same idea was used in
[
18
]
, for the
special case where
a=2
and
b=4
.)

Theorem 2
.

Suppose
a,b
are integers with
2\leq a\leq b
.
Let
e=(\log b)/(\log a)
.
Then

{\text{\scalebox{.8}{$\frac{a-1}{b-1}$}}}n^{e}\leq{\text{\scalebox{.8}{$\frac{a-1}{b-1}$}}}((n+1)^{e}-1)\leq[(n)_{a}]_{b}\leq n^{e}

for all
n\geq 0
.

For a proof, see Section
8
.

Now we can prove upper and lower bounds on
f_{3,0}(n)
.
Let us define
p_{a,b}(n)=[(n)_{a}]_{b}
for
n\geq 0
.
Then
p_{3,4}
is computed by the
Walnut
automaton
defined as follows:

reg p34 msd_3 msd_4 "([0,0]|[1,1]|[2,2])*":

The idea now is to compute
p_{3,4}(f_{3,0}(n))
, which will
approximate
(f_{3,0}(n))^{{\log 4}\over{\log 3}}
. The
degree of approximation is given by Theorem
2
.

Next we prove

Theorem 3
.

For
n\geq 1
we have
n\leq p_{3,4}(f_{3,0}(n))\leq(3n-1)/2
,
and both bounds are achieved infinitely often.

Proof.

We use the following
Walnut
code:

eval bnd1 "?msd_4 An,x,m ($f30(n,x) & $p34(x,m)) => n<=m":
eval bnd2 "?msd_4 An,x,m (n>=1 & $f30(n,x) & $p34(x,m)) => 2*m+1<=3*n":
def bnd3 "?msd_4 Ex $f30(n,x) & $p34(x,n)":
def bnd4 "?msd_4 Ex,m $f30(n,x) & $p34(x,m) & 2*m+1=3*n":

and
Walnut
returns
TRUE
for the first two commands.

The third command creates an automaton accepting the base-
4

representation of those
n
for which
p_{3,4}(f_{3,0}(n))=n
.
Inspection of this automaton reveals that

n
is accepted if and only if
(n)_{4}\in\{0,2\}^{*}\{\epsilon,1\}
.

The fourth command creates an automaton accepting the base-
4

representation of those
n
for which
p_{3,4}(f_{3,0}(n))=(3n-1)/2
.
Inspection of this automaton reveals that

n
is accepted if and only if
(n)_{4}\in\{1\}\,\cup\,2^{*}3
.
∎

We can now prove

Theorem 4
.

Let
e={{\log 3}\over{\log 4}}
.
Then
c_{1}n^{e}\leq f_{3,0}(n)\leq c_{2}n^{e}
,
where
c_{1}=1
and
c_{2}=(9/4)^{e}\doteq 1.9015
.

Proof.

From Theorem
3
we have

n\leq p_{3,4}(f_{3,0}(n))
. By substituting

f_{3,0}(n)
for
n
in Theorem
2
, we get

p_{3,4}(f_{3,0}(n))\leq(f_{3,0}(n))^{1/e}
.
Hence
n\leq(f_{3,0}(n))^{1/e}
, and the
lower bound follows by raising both sides to the
e
power.

From Theorem
3
we have

p_{3,4}(f_{3,0}(n))\leq{3\over 2}n
.
By substituting

f_{3,0}(n)
for
n
in Theorem
2
, we get

{2\over 3}(f_{3,0}(n))^{1/e}\leq p_{3,4}(f_{3,0}(n))
.
Hence
{2\over 3}(f_{3,0}(n))^{1/e}\leq{3\over 2}n
,
so
(f_{3,0}(n))^{1/e}\leq{9\over 4}n
, and
raising both sides to the
e
power
gives the bound.
∎

.

The constants
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
we obtained can be compared to the

Theorem 6

(a)

For

.

and

returns
TRUE
for both. Note that

asserts that

.
∎

Corollary 7
.

Let
e={{\log 3}\over{\log 4}}
.

(a)

\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}f_{3,0}(n)/n^{e}\geq 55/(260/3)^{e}\doteq 1.601958
.

5
Results for
f_{3,1}(n)
and
f_{3,2}(n)

Exactly the same approach can be applied to study
both
f_{3,1}(n)
and
f_{3,2}(n)
, except
now these functions take non-positive values.
These were studied previously by Dumont
[
8
]

and Drmota and Stoll
[
7
]
.

Since
Walnut
’s fundamental domain is
\mathbb{N}
,
the natural numbers, it is easiest
to study the functions
-f_{3,1}(n)
and
-f_{3,2}(n)
instead.
Once again we can show that these two functions are
synchronized, with automata of 15 and 14 states, respectively.

Theorem 8
.

We have

(a)

n/2\leq p_{3,4}(-f_{3,1}(n))\leq(3n-1)/2

for all
\geq 1
. The lower bound is achieved precisely
when
(n)_{4}\in\{0,1\}^{*}0
, and the upper bound precisely when

(n)_{4}\in\{1\}\,\cup\,2^{*}3
.

(b)

(n/2)^{{\log 3}\over{\log 4}}\leq-f_{3,1}(n)\leq((9n-3)/4)^{{\log 3}\over{\log 4}}
for all
n\geq 1
.

Proof.

Once we have guessed the automaton for
-f_{3,1}(n)
—it has 15 states–we can
verify its correctness as we did for
f_{3,0}(n)
:

eval test31_1 "An Ey $mf31(n,y)":
eval test31_2 "~En,y1,y2 $mf31(n,y1) & $mf31(n,y2) & ?msd_3 y1!=y2":
eval test31_3 "?msd_4 An,x (n>=1 & $mf31(n, ?msd_3 x) &
TM4[3*n+1]=@1) => $mf31(n+1, ?msd_3 x+1)":
eval test31_4 "?msd_4 An,x (n>=1 & $mf31(n, ?msd_3 x) &
TM4[3*n+1]=@0) => $mf31(n+1, ?msd_3 x-1)":
eval test31_5 "?msd_4 An,y (n>=1 & $mf31(n,y)) => ?msd_3 y>0":
eval test31_6 "?msd_4 Ay En,x $mf31(n,x) & ?msd_3 x>y":

Then we use
Walnut
to verify the bounds in the theorem.

eval test31_bnd1 "?msd_4 An,x,m ($mf31(n,x) & $p34(x,m)) => n<=2*m":
eval test31_bnd2 "?msd_4 An,x,m (n>=1 & $mf31(n,x) & $p34(x,m)) => 2*m+1<=3*n":
def test31_bnd3 "?msd_4 Ex $mf31(n,x) & $p34(x,n)":
def test31_bnd4 "?msd_4 Ex,m $mf31(n,x) & $p34(x,m) & 2*m+1=3*n":

The last two commands produce automata accepting the sets of the theorem.
∎

8
Proof of Theorem
2

In this section, we prove the needed technical result, Theorem
2
.
We start with some useful lemmas.

Lemma 13
.

Suppose
n\geq 1
,
0\leq x\leq 1
, and
e\geq 1
.
Then
(n+x)^{e}\leq(n+1)^{e}+e(x-1)
.

Proof.

For
x\in[0,1]
, define
h(x):=(n+x)^{e}-(n+1)^{e}-e(x-1)
.
We want to prove that
h(x)\leq 0
.
Now
h^{\prime}(x)=e(n+x)^{e-1}-e
. From the hypotheses on
n,x,
and
e
, we
see that
h^{\prime}(x)\geq 0
. Hence
h
is increasing on
the interval
[0,1]
. Thus
h(x)\leq h(1)=0
.
∎

Lemma 14
.

Suppose
1\leq a\leq b
.
Then
{{a-1}\over a}\log b\geq{{b-1}\over b}\log a
.

Proof.

Writing
b=a+x
with
x\geq 0
, we must show

h(x)={{a-1}\over a}\log(a+x)-{{a+x-1}\over{a+x}}\log a\geq 0
.
But
h^{\prime}(x)=((a-1)(a+x)-a\log a)/(a(a+x)^{2})
.
Now from the Taylor series expansion we have

e^{a-1}\geq a
, so
a-1\geq\log a
, so

a(a-1)\geq a\log a
, so
h^{\prime}(x)\geq 0
.
Now
h(0)=0
, so
h(x)\geq 0
for all
x
.
∎

Lemma 15
.

Suppose
1<a\leq b
and
x\in[0,1]
. Then

((a-1)x+1)^{\log b}\leq((b-1)x+1)^{\log a}.

Proof.

By taking logarithms, it suffices to show that

(\log b)(\log((a-1)x+1))\leq(\log a)(\log((b-1)x+1)).

This is true for
x=0
, so it suffices to prove

{{\log b}\over{\log a}}\leq f(x)

for
f(x)={{\log((b-1)x+1)}\over{\log((a-1)x+1)}}

and
x\in(0,1]
.
If we show that
f(x)
is decreasing on
x\in(0,1]
then

f(x)\geq f(1)={{\log b}\over{\log a}}
, as desired.

To show
f(x)
is decreasing on
x\in(0,1]
, we should
show
f^{\prime}(x)\leq 0
.
We have

f^{\prime}(x)={{g(x)}\over{((a-1)x+1)((b-1)x+1)(\log((a-1)x+1))^{2}}}.

where

g(x)=(b-1)((a-1)x+1)\log((a-1)x+1)-(a-1)((b-1)x+1)\log((b-1)x+1).

Since the denominator of
f^{\prime}(x)
is positive, it suffices to show
that
g(x)\leq 0
. To show this, since
g(0)=0
,
it suffices to show
g(x)
is decreasing on
[0,1]
.
Now

g^{\prime}(x)=(a-1)(b-1)(\log(1+(a-1)x)-\log(1+(b-1)x))

which, since
a\leq b
, gives
g^{\prime}(x)\leq 0
.
The result now follows.
∎

We are now ready to prove Theorem
2
.

Proof of Theorem
2
.

The first inequality

{{a-1}\over{b-1}}n^{e}\leq{{a-1}\over{b-1}}((n+1)^{e}-1)

follows from Lemma
13
with
x=0
. So we focus
on the remaining inequalities.

The upper and lower bounds clearly hold for
n=0
.

Now suppose
n\geq 1
. For the upper bound, express
n

in base
a
as follows:
n=c_{t-1}a^{t-1}+\cdots+c_{1}a+c_{0}
.
Then
[(n)_{a}]_{b}=c_{t-1}b^{t-1}+\cdots+c_{1}b+c_{0}
.
Then

\displaystyle n^{e}

\displaystyle=(c_{t-1}a^{t-1}+\cdots+c_{1}a+c_{0})^{e}

\displaystyle\geq c_{t-1}(a^{e})^{t-1}+\cdots+c_{1}(a^{e})+c_{0}

\displaystyle=c_{t-1}b^{t-1}+\cdots+c_{1}b+c_{0}

\displaystyle=[(n)_{a}]_{b}.

Next, let us prove the lower bound by induction on
n
.
The base case is
1\leq n\leq a-1
. For these
n
we need to show

\text{\scalebox{.8}{$\frac{a-1}{b-1}$}}((n+1)^{e}-1)\leq n,

or, equivalently by
setting
x=n/(a-1)
and rearranging, we need to show

(1+(a-1)x)^{\log b}\leq(1+(b-1)x)^{\log a}
for
x\in[0,1]
. But
this is Lemma
15
.

Now assume the lower bound holds for
n^{\prime}<n
; we prove it for
n
.
Write
n=ak+c
for some integer
c
,
0\leq c\leq a-1
.
Then

\displaystyle\text{\scalebox{.8}{$\frac{a-1}{b-1}$}}((n+1)^{e}-1)

(2)

\displaystyle\quad=\text{\scalebox{.8}{$\frac{a-1}{b-1}$}}((ak+c+1)^{e}-1)

\displaystyle\quad=\text{\scalebox{.8}{$\frac{a-1}{b-1}$}}\bigl(a^{e}\bigl(k+\text{\scalebox{.8}{$\frac{c+1}{a}$}}\bigr)^{e}-1\bigr)

\displaystyle\quad=\text{\scalebox{.8}{$\frac{a-1}{b-1}$}}\bigl(b\bigl(k+\text{\scalebox{.8}{$\frac{c+1}{a}$}}\bigr)^{e}-1\bigr)

\displaystyle\quad\leq\text{\scalebox{.8}{$\frac{a-1}{b-1}$}}\bigl(b\bigl((k+1)^{e}+e\text{\scalebox{.8}{$\frac{c+1-a}{a}$}}\bigr)-1\bigr)\quad\text{(by Lemma~\ref{lem1} with $n=k$, $x=(c+1)/a$))}

\displaystyle\quad\leq b\bigl(\text{\scalebox{.8}{$\frac{a-1}{b-1}$}}((k+1)^{e}-1)\bigr)+c

(3)

\displaystyle\quad\leq b[(k)_{a}]_{b}+c\quad\text{(by induction)}

\displaystyle\quad=[(ak+c)_{a}]_{b}

\displaystyle\quad=[(n)_{a}]_{b}.

However, the inequality (
3
)

\text{\scalebox{.8}{$\frac{a-1}{b-1}$}}\bigl(b\bigl((k+1)^{e}+e\text{\scalebox{.8}{$\frac{c+1-a}{a}$}}\bigr)-1\bigr)\leq b\bigl(\text{\scalebox{.8}{$\frac{a-1}{b-1}$}}((k+1)^{e}-1)\bigr)+c

still needs justification. To see it, note that by simplification
it is equivalent to the claim
that
(abe-ab-be+a)(a-1-c)\geq 0
. The second term is non-negative,
since
c\leq a-1
, so it suffices to show

abe-ab-be+a\geq 0
,
which is equivalent to the claim

{{a-1}\over a}\log b\geq{{b-1}\over b}\log a
. But this
is Lemma
14
.
∎

Remark 16

We remark that the bounds in Theorem

The lower bound, for example, is approached by

, while the upper bound is
reached for numbers of the form
