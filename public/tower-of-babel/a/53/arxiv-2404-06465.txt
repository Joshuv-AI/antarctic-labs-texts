Phase space contraction of degenerately damped random splittings
David P. Herzog 1 and Jonathan C. Mattingly 2

Abstract
. When studying out-of-equilibrium systems, one often excites the dynamics in some degrees of freedom while removing the excitation in others through damping. In order for the system to converge to a statistical steady state, the dynamics must transfer the energy from the excited modes to the dissipative directions. The precise mechanisms underlying this transfer are of particular interest and are the topic of this paper. We explore a class of randomly switched models introduced in [ 2 , 3 ] and provide some of the first results showing that minimal damping is sufficient to stabilize the system in a fluids model.



1.
Introduction and Motivation

Many dynamical systems of scientific importance are expected to
be ergodic, where time averages of observables with respect to the system converge in the long-run to spatial averages against an
invariant measure. We are interested in
settings where such statistical steady states are not in local equilibrium,
but rather have a nontrivial flux through the system. Particular examples include
chains of coupled oscillators with ends
connected to heat baths at different temperatures
[
9
,
16
,
18
]
and
Euler equations with forcing and dissipation acting
on limited degrees of freedom
[
7
,
25
]
. See also
[
11
]
for graphs of coupled oscillators with only some oscillators containing damping and random forcing. In such examples, energy is injected
in some directions in space and then transferred by the
conservative part of the dynamics to the dissapative degrees of freedom. This process typically produces an equilibrium steady state
with a flux from the forced degrees of freedom to the dissipated
ones. We will refer to such forced-dissipative systems
where the dissipation only acts on a subset of the degrees of freedom as

partially dissipative
.

In partially dissipative systems, establishing the existence of a statistical steady state can be highly nontrivial. One of the main goals of this paper is to prove the
existence of such steady states under a randomly modified
dynamics, in settings such as finite-dimensional projections of the partially damped-driven Euler equations.

To make the discussion more concrete, fix an ordinary differential equation (ODE)

(1.1)

\displaystyle\dot{x}=V(x)

on a Hilbert space
\mathbf{X}
which is nonexplosive for all initial conditions
x_{0}\in\mathbf{X}
and conserves a quantity
H\colon\mathbf{X}\rightarrow[0,\infty)
. Note that conservation simply means that

H(x_{t})=H(x_{0})
for all
t\geq 0
. Often, the vector field
V
is
the Hamiltonian flow associated with Hamiltonian
H
. To the dynamics (
1.1
), one can then add dissipation
and forcing to arrive at the forced-damped system

\displaystyle\dot{x}=V(x)-\Lambda x+F\,

where
\Lambda:\mathbf{X}\rightarrow\mathbf{X}
is symmetric, non-negative definite and
F:[0,\infty)\rightarrow\mathbf{X}
is time-dependent.
When
\Lambda
is furthermore assumed strictly positive-definite, in many scenarios it is
not hard to show that that there exist constants
\alpha\in(0,1)

and
f\geq 0
so that for some fixed time
t

(1.2)

\displaystyle H(x_{t})\leq\alpha H(x_{0})+f\,.

If the sublevel sets
\{x:H(x)\leq c\}
,
c\geq 0
, are furthermore compact in
\mathbf{X}
, then
(
1.2
) quickly implies the existence of a stationary
measure. In random systems with sufficient noise to ensure
that the stationary measure is unique, the bound
(
1.2
) in expectation is critical in proving that the system
converges to the equilibrium at a prescribed rate.

In scenarios where
\Lambda
has a nontrivial kernel, proving estimates like
(
1.2
) can be difficult because it requires
detailed knowledge of the conservative dynamics (
1.1
). Even in this context, however, there are examples where the forcing allows the conservative part (
1.1
) to transport energy to the dissipative directions, keeping it from building up in the undamped degrees of freedom. When this phenomenon
happens at a sufficiently fast rate, one can ensure the existence of a
stationary state and its uniqueness if additional conditions are
satisfied.
We refer to
[
6
,
7
,
10
,
25
]
for concrete examples in fluid-like models on Euclidean space and to
[
9
,
11
,
16
,
18
]
for Langevin-like systems. More closely related to the current paper is the very interesting work
[
6
]
which appeared after this work was finished. It considers hypoelliptic SDEs with “generic” Euler-type nonlinearities in the degenerately damped setting. There, under the assumption that the dimension of the damped modes is at least one third of the dimension of the phase space, for a set of Euler type nonlinearities of full Lebesgue measure the resulting SDE has a unique stationary measure. Note that this result does not identify which nonlinearities within this class have this property, but it is tantalizing because almost all of them have to have it. It is related to
[
25
]
which also considered “generic” nonlinearities. In
[
25
]
and most other fluid models
[
7
,
10
,
25
]
, a significant number of directions need damping. In contrast, one needs far less dissipative directions in the situation of oscillators provided the interaction potentials are strong enough relative to the pinning potentials
[
9
,
11
,
16
]
. However, when the interactions are weak, even in the case of a short chain of oscillators, the propagation of energy to dissipative modes can be highly nontrivial
[
18
]
.

In contexts similar to
[
7
,
10
,
25
]
, this paper will present some of the first results establishing the existence
of steady states where the dissipative directions are restricted to a small number
of degrees of freedom, even when the system’s dimension is large. This will be done for
randomly split
versions of equations such as Galerkin projections of two-dimensional Euler and the Lorenz ’96 model. Random splittings are simplified models which afford stronger results at the cost of disrupting the original dynamics. However, they are insightful as they help build intuition about the full dynamics and the finer mechanisms involving the transport of energy to these isolated dissipative directions. For example, our main results
establish the existence of statistical steady states for the randomly
split Galerkin 2D Euler equations and the randomly split Lorenz ’96
models under the hypothesis that the dissipative operator is restricted to a finite
number of well-placed modes. Furthermore, the number of dissipative modes does not
grow with the dimension of the system, which is new in these
settings.

Random splittings can be thought of as a “randomly teased” version of the original dynamics at every time step, but in way that is natural to the dynamics. One can view one cycle of the splitting as “generic in some sense” evolution that is close to the true evolution. On each time step, we chose a different flow near by to the true flow. On average, the resulting random dynamics is very close to the deterministic dynamics and all of the fluctuations are built from the base vector fields that define the
dynamics, but in a different mixture than the deterministic system. To build the random splittings, we choose
elements of the dynamics that are thought to be fundamental to the structure of the flow of the base dynamics. For example, for the 2D Euler equation, we use
the three-mode resonant interactions as our dynamical building
blocks which maintain many of the invariant
structures of the original system. This is in contrast to more traditional
stochastic agitation methods which use additive Brownian forcing.

Although some unlikely stochastic realizations are leveraged in the proof of the
existence of a stationary measure, we will see that the arguments leading to energy dissipation align well with the scenarios people often
invoke in heuristic explanations of why such systems with partial
dissipation should be stable. In particular, we will see how energy
transports via the building blocks, e.g. the three-mode resonant interactions in Euler, to the dissipative directions. Additionally, the
probabilities of the identified dissipative events scale
sufficiently well with the energy that the implied convergence rates would
be geometric for the Lorenz ’96 system and stretched exponential for
the Euler system. The stretched exponential convergence is not
completely surprising given that the fixed points of Euler and their
nearby slow dynamics persist in the random splitting.
It is also worth noting that we make no use of any additional
stochastic forcing. Consequently, we
do not deduce any convergence-to-equilibrium theorems. Rather, we only
give existence of stationary measures. In existing, related
works
[
2
,
3
,
25
]
, the uniqueness of the invariant measure for a
stochastically switched Euler system was shown within the class of
measures absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure on the constraint
surface. While these results could almost certainly be adapted to
this setting, they do not preclude the system converging to one of the
fixed points preserved by the random splitting. Random forcing is
important to show that the system leaves the neighborhood of the fixed
points in an efficient way. In future work, it would be interesting to combine random
switching and random forcing, though we do not explore that here.

Paper Organization:

In Section
2
, we
introduce some general results for proving the tightness needed
to guarantee the existence of an invariant measure for the system. We
also make some comments on the return times to compact sets that give
an indication what the rates of convergence to equilibrium might
be. In Section
3
, we introduce randomly split systems and the first of our two
examples; namely, the Lorenz ’96 model. We demonstrate most of the ideas
introduced in this section by proving the needed assumptions for the
Lorenz ’96 model. In Section
4
, we establish the required uniform
lower bound
on the probability of entering into a dissipative region in the
Lorenz ’96 model. In Section
5
, we turn to the Euler
equations and prove all of the needed estimates in that setting by
adapting the calculations used for the Lorenz ’96. In many ways, however, the
Euler setting is significantly more complicated.

2.
General Results for Partially Dissipative Systems

In this section,
(\mathbf{X},\rho)
denotes a complete, separable metric space and
\mathcal{B}
its associated Borel
\sigma
-algebra of subsets. We let
(X_{n})
be a discrete-time Markov chain on the state space
\mathbf{X}
with transition semigroup
(\mathcal{P}_{n})
defined over the probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbf{P},\mathbf{E})
. The operator
\mathcal{P}_{n}
acts on bounded measurable functions
\phi:\mathbf{X}\rightarrow\mathbf{R}
and finite
\mathcal{B}
measures
\mu
via

(2.1)

\displaystyle(\mathcal{P}_{n}\phi)(x):=\mathbf{E}_{x}\phi(X_{n})\qquad\text{ and }\qquad(\mu\mathcal{P}_{n})(B):=\int_{\mathbf{X}}\mathbf{P}_{x}(X_{n}\in B)\mu(dx)

for
x\in\mathbf{X}
and
B\in\mathcal{B}
. In (
2.1
), the notation
\mathbf{E}_{x}
and
\mathbf{P}_{x}
means
\mathbf{E}
and
\mathbf{P}
, but indicates that the Markov chain has initial state
X_{0}=x
. For any
n\geq 0
,
B\in\mathcal{B}
and
x\in\mathbf{X}
, we let

(2.2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{n}(x,B)=(\mathcal{P}_{n}\mathbf{1}_{B})(x)=\mathbf{P}_{x}(X_{n}\in B)

denote the Markov transition probabilities. Throughout,
\mathcal{P}_{1}
will be denoted by
\mathcal{P}
.

The following elementary result is the beginning of our analysis.

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
H\colon\mathbf{X}\rightarrow[1,\infty)
and
p\colon[1,\infty)\rightarrow(0,1]
be measurable, and let
\alpha\in(0,1)
and
f_{1},f_{2}\geq 0
be constants. Suppose for any
x\in\mathbf{X}
there exists an event
A=A(x)\in\mathcal{F}
such that

\mathbf{P}_{x}(A)\geq p(H(x))
and such that the following bounds hold

(2.4)

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{x}\big[H(X_{1})\,\mathbf{1}_{A}\big]

\displaystyle\leq\alpha H(x)\mathbf{P}_{x}(A)+f_{1},

(2.5)

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{x}\big[H(X_{1})\,\mathbf{1}_{A^{c}}\big]

\displaystyle\leq H(x)\mathbf{P}_{x}(A^{c})+f_{2}

for every
x\in\mathbf{X}
.
Then the global estimate

(2.6)

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}H-H

\displaystyle\leq-(1-\alpha)p(H)\,H\,+f_{1}+f_{2}

holds on
\mathbf{X}
.

Proof.

Observe for any
x\in\mathbf{X}
we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}H(x)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}_{x}\big[H\big(X_{1}\big)\,\mathbf{1}_{A}\big]+\mathbf{E}_{x}\big[H\big(X_{1}\big)\,\mathbf{1}_{A^{c}}\big]

\displaystyle\leq[\alpha\mathbf{P}_{x}(A)+(1-\mathbf{P}_{x}(A))]H(x)+f_{1}+f_{2}

\displaystyle\leq[1-\mathbf{P}_{x}(A)(1-\alpha)]H(x)+f_{1}+f_{2}

\displaystyle\leq[1-(1-\alpha)p(H(x))]H(x)+f_{1}+f_{2}.

This concludes the proof.
∎

The next lemma shows that the conclusion
of Proposition
2.3
guarantees the existence of (at least one)
stationary distribution for the Markov chain provided
x\mapsto p(H(x))H(x)
has compact sublevel sets and the semigroup
(\mathcal{P}_{n})
is
Feller
; that is,
x\mapsto\mathcal{P}\phi(x)
is continuous whenever
\phi:\mathbf{X}\rightarrow\mathbf{R}
is bounded, continuous.

Lemma 2.7
.

Assume
(\mathcal{P}_{n})
is Feller. Suppose there exist measurable
H\colon\mathbf{X}\rightarrow[1,\infty)
and
G\colon[1,\infty)\rightarrow(0,\infty)
such that
\{x\in\mathbf{X}\,:\,G(H(x))\leq\alpha\}
is compact for every
\alpha>0
, and for some constant
f\geq 0
we have the following estimate on
\mathbf{X}
:

(2.8)

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}H-H\leq-G(H)+f.

Then the Markov chain
(X_{n})
has at least one stationary distribution.

Remark 2.9
.

The result above remains true if we replace (
2.8
) with the perhaps more familiar bound

(2.10)

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}H-H\leq-\widetilde{H}+f

where
\widetilde{H}:\mathbf{X}\rightarrow[1,\infty)
is any measurable function such that
\{x\in\mathbf{X}\,:\,\widetilde{H}(x)\leq R\}
is compact for every
R\geq R_{*}>0
, for some
R_{*}>0
. In other words, the righthand side of (
2.8
) need not be a function of
H
, but we will use this specific form several times below. Weaker bounds than (
2.10
) are sometimes used to show the existence of a stationary distribution, but they usually follow the Hasminskii cycle construction which is different than the Krylov-Bogolyubov existence method used below. See, for example,
[
23
]
.

Proof of Lemma
2.7
.

Because this result is essentially the well-known Krylov-Bogolyubov
theorem for Feller Markov semigroups, we sketch the proof and
refer the reader to
[
13
,
19
,
22
]
for further details.
Fix
x\in\mathbf{X}
and define the sequence of
\mathcal{B}
probability measures
(\mu_{n})
by

\displaystyle\mu_{n}(B)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\ell=0}^{n-1}\mathbf{P}_{x}(X_{\ell}\in B),\,\,n\geq 1,\,B\in\mathcal{B}.

Using (
2.8
) and the Markov property we find that

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=0}^{n-1}\mathbf{E}_{x}G(H(X_{\ell}))\leq H(x)-\mathcal{P}_{n}H(x)+nf\leq H(x)+nf\,.

Hence for any
R>0
,

\displaystyle\mu_{n}(x:G(H(x))>R)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\ell=0}^{n-1}\mathbf{P}_{x}(G(H(X_{\ell}))>R)\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\ell=0}^{n-1}\frac{\mathbf{E}_{x}G(H(X_{\ell}))}{R}\leq\frac{H(x)+f}{R},

which shows that the family of measures
(\mu_{n})
is tight since
\{x\,:\,G(H(x))\leq R\}
is compact by hypothesis. Let

\mu
be a weak limit point of this family and, when they exist, let
\mu_{n}(\phi),\mu_{n}\mathcal{P}(\phi)
and
\mu(\phi)
denote the expectations of
\mathcal{B}
-measurable
\phi:\mathbf{X}\rightarrow\mathbf{R}
with respect to
\mu_{n}
,
\mu_{n}\mathcal{P}
and
\mu
. To see that

\mu
is stationary, observe that for any bounded,
\mathcal{B}
-measurable
\phi:\mathbf{X}\rightarrow\mathbf{R}
we have by definition of
\mu_{n}

\displaystyle|\mu_{n}\mathcal{P}(\phi)-\mu_{n}(\phi)|=\frac{1}{n}\big|\mathbf{E}_{x}\phi(X_{n})-\phi(x)\big|\rightarrow 0

as
n\rightarrow\infty
.
Hence if
(\mu_{n_{k}})
is a subsequence converging weakly to
\mu
, then the Fubini-Tonelli theorem, the Feller property and weak convergence implies

\displaystyle\mu\mathcal{P}(\phi)=\mu(\mathcal{P}\phi)=\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\mu_{n_{k}}(\mathcal{P}\phi)=\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}(\mu_{n_{k}}\mathcal{P}(\phi)-\mu_{n_{k}}(\phi))+\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\mu_{n_{k}}(\phi)=\mu(\phi).

Thus,
\mu\mathcal{P}(\phi)=\mu(\phi)
for all bounded, continuous
\phi:\mathbf{X}\rightarrow\mathbf{R}
, finishing the proof.

∎

Remark 2.11
.

Suppose that (
2.8
) is satisfied and
G(t)\rightarrow\infty
as
t\rightarrow\infty
. Then for any
\epsilon\in(0,1)
there exists
R>0
large enough so that the global bound holds

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}H-H\leq-(1-\epsilon)G(H)+(1+\epsilon)f\mathbf{1}_{\{H\leq R\}}.

Thus under this hypothesis on
G
, the bound (
2.8
) is equivalent to the bound

(2.12)

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}H-H\leq-G(H)+f\mathbf{1}_{\{H\leq R\}}

provided the terms
G
and
f
on the righthand side of (
2.12
) are redefined accordingly.

2.1.
Return Times and Convergence

Let
R,f\geq 0
and suppose that (
2.12
) is satisfied for some
H:\mathbf{X}\rightarrow[1,\infty)
measurable and some concave nondecreasing differentiable function
G:[1,\infty)\rightarrow(0,\infty)
. We next consider implications of this estimate for return times of the Markov chain
(X_{n})
to the set

(2.13)

\displaystyle H_{\leq R}=\{x\in\mathbf{X}\,:\,H(x)\leq R\}.

Let

(2.14)

\displaystyle T_{R}=\min\{n\geq 0\,:\,H(X_{n})\leq R\}

and note that in the special case when
G(t)=\alpha t
for some constant
\alpha>0
, relation (
2.12
) implies

(2.15)

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{x}e^{\alpha T_{R}}<\infty\,\,\text{ for all }\,\,x\in\mathbf{X}.

This fact is well-known. However, when
G
is a general concave, nondecreasing function, the corresponding form of the moment bound changes.

To describe how the estimates change in this more general context, define
K:[1,\infty)\rightarrow[0,\infty)
by

(2.16)

\displaystyle K(t)=\int_{1}^{t}\frac{1}{G(s)}\,ds.

Since
G
is assumed concave, we have that

\displaystyle G(t)\leq G(1)+G^{\prime}(1)(t-1).

This fact, along with the condition that
G
is nondecreasing and
positive, implies that
K
is strictly increasing and
K(t)\rightarrow\infty
as
t\rightarrow\infty
. In particular,
K
has
inverse function
K^{-1}:[0,\infty)\rightarrow[1,\infty)
with

K^{-1}(0)=1
which is also strictly increasing. Furthermore,
K
itself is concave as
G
is nondecreasing. Define
r:[0,\infty)\rightarrow(0,\infty)
by

(2.17)

\displaystyle r(t):=(K^{-1})^{\prime}(t)=G(K^{-1}(t)).

It is worth noting that the properties of
r
and
K^{-1}
imply that

K^{-1}(n)
,
n\in\mathbf{Z}_{\geq 1}
, is comparable to
\sum_{k=1}^{n}r(k)
. In particular, it holds that

(2.18)

\displaystyle K^{-1}(n)-1=\int_{0}^{n}r(s)\,ds\leq\sum_{k=1}^{n}r(k).

The following result also holds:

Theorem 2.19
.

Let
R,f\geq 0
and suppose that the estimate (
2.12
) holds for some measurable
H:\mathbf{X}\rightarrow[1,\infty)
and some concave nondecreasing differentiable function
G:[1,\infty)\rightarrow(0,\infty)
. Then for all
x\notin H_{\leq R}

(2.20)

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{x}\bigg[\sum_{k=0}^{T_{R}-1}r(k)\bigg]\leq H(x)

and for all
x\notin H_{\leq R}
,
n\geq 0

(2.21)

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{x}(T_{R}\geq n+1)\leq\frac{H(x)+1}{K^{-1}(n)}.

The bound (
2.20
) follows directly from
[
12
,
24
]
while relation (
2.21
) follows from (
2.20
) using (
2.18
). Furthermore, if in addition to the conclusions of Theorem
2.19
one has a minorization condition of the form

\displaystyle\inf_{x\in H_{\leq R^{\prime}}}\mathcal{P}(x,\;\cdot\;)\geq c\lambda(\;\cdot\;)

for some constant
c>0
,
R^{\prime}>2R
and
\mathcal{B}
-probability measure
\lambda
, then one can prove that (see
[
12
,
24
]
)

\mathcal{P}_{n}(x,\;\cdot\;)
converges in total variation to the unique stationary distribution of the chain as
n\rightarrow\infty
at a rate proportional to
1/r(n)
. In Example
2.22
and Example
2.23
below, this will in turn correspond to exponential, polynomial and stretched exponential rates of convergence to equilibrium, depending on the structure of
G
. This fits with the intuition that one mechanism slowing
convergence to a subexponential rate is slow return times to the
minorizing set
H_{\leq R}
. See also
[
15
,
17
]
for subgeometric rates of convergence for Markov processes in the continuous-time setting.

Example 2.22
.

Suppose that
G(t)=\alpha t^{a}
for some
\alpha>0
and
a\in(0,1]
. Thus
G
is strictly increasing and concave on
[1,\infty)
and
K
,
K^{-1}
are given by

\displaystyle K(t)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{\alpha}\log(t)&\text{ if }a=1\\
\frac{t^{1-a}-1}{\alpha(1-a)}&\text{ if }a\in(0,1)\end{cases}\quad\text{ and }\quad K^{-1}(t)=\begin{cases}e^{\alpha t}&\text{ if }a=1\\
(\alpha(1-a)t+1)^{1/(1-a)}&\text{ if }a\in(0,1)\end{cases}.

Furthermore,

\displaystyle r(t)=\begin{cases}\alpha e^{\alpha t}&\text{ if }\alpha=1\\
\alpha(\alpha(1-a)t+1)^{\frac{a}{1-a}}&\text{ if }a\in(0,1)\end{cases}.

In particular, in the case when
a=1
, the bound (
2.20
) recovers (
2.15
).

The next example will be particularly important in this paper.

Example 2.23
.

Let
\alpha>0
and
G_{0}:(1,\infty)\rightarrow(0,\infty)
be given by

\displaystyle G_{0}(t)=\frac{\alpha t}{\log(t)}

and note that
G_{0}
is nondecreasing and concave for
t\geq e^{2}
. For
t\geq 1
, we define
G(t)=G_{0}(t+e^{2})
and observe that

\displaystyle K(t)=\int_{1}^{t}\frac{1}{G(s)}\,ds=\int_{1+e^{2}}^{t+e^{2}}\frac{1}{G_{0}(s)}\,ds=\frac{1}{2\alpha}\bigg[(\log(t+e^{2}))^{2}-(\log(1+e^{2}))^{2}\bigg],

and it follows that

\displaystyle K^{-1}(t)

\displaystyle=\exp\big((2\alpha t+[\log(1+e^{2})]^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}\big)-e^{2},

\displaystyle r(t)

\displaystyle=\alpha\frac{\exp\big((2\alpha t+[\log(1+e^{2})]^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}\big)}{(2\alpha t+[\log(1+e^{2})]^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}}.

3.
Random Splittings

We now specialize to the setting of a discrete-time Markov chain
(X_{n})
with state space
(\mathbf{X},\rho)=(\mathbf{R}^{d},|\cdot|)
where
|\cdot|
is the usual Euclidean distance. In order to describe the specific Markov chains to be studied in the rest of the paper, we first fix some notation convenient to this setting.

3.1.
Notation

For
x,y\in\mathbf{R}^{d}
, we use
\langle x,y\rangle
to denote the standard inner product between
x
and
y
so that
|x|=\sqrt{\langle x,x\rangle}
. The symbol
\mathscr{V}_{d}
denotes the set of complete,
C^{\infty}
vector fields on
\mathbf{R}^{d}
; that is,
\mathscr{V}_{d}
denotes the set of
C^{\infty}
vector fields whose flows are define globally in time (on
\mathbf{R}
) and space (on
\mathbf{R}^{d}
). For any
V\in\mathscr{V}_{d}
, we let
(t,x)\mapsto\varphi_{t}^{V}(x):\mathbf{R}\times\mathbf{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbf{R}^{d}
denote the flow map associated to the differential equation

(3.1)

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{t}=V(x_{t}).

That is, for
(t,x)\in\mathbf{R}\times\mathbf{R}^{d}
,
\varphi_{t}^{V}(x)
denotes the solution of (
3.1
) at time
t
with initial condition
x_{0}=x\in\mathbf{R}^{d}
. We emphasize that since
V
is complete, the mapping
(t,x)\mapsto\varphi_{t}(x)
is defined for all
t\in\mathbf{R}
and
x\in\mathbf{R}^{d}
. For any
V\in\mathscr{V}_{d}
, we say that a finite collection
\mathscr{S}=\{V_{1},V_{2},\ldots,V_{m}\}\subset\mathscr{V}_{d}

is a
splitting
of
V
provided

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}V_{i}=V.

We will use
S_{m}
to denote the set of permutations of
\{1,2,\ldots,m\}
. Given a splitting
\mathscr{S}=\{V_{1},\ldots,V_{m}\}\subset\mathscr{V}_{d}
of
V\in\mathscr{V}_{d}
, we will often use
\varphi_{t}^{i}(x)
,
x\in\mathbf{R}^{d}
, to denote
\varphi_{t}^{V_{i}}(x)
. In this setting, for any
\sigma=(\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{m})\in S_{m}
,
x\in\mathbf{R}^{d}
and
t=(t_{1},\ldots,t_{m})\in[0,\infty)^{m}
we let

(3.2)

\displaystyle\Phi^{\sigma}_{t}(x):=\varphi_{t_{m}}^{\sigma_{m}}\circ\cdots\circ\varphi_{t_{1}}^{\sigma_{1}}(x)

where
\circ
denotes composition.
In words,
\Phi^{\sigma}_{t}(x)
is the result of flowing along the vector fields in the splitting
\mathscr{S}
in the order prescribed by the permutation
\sigma
starting from
x\in\mathbf{R}^{d}
, with corresponding times spent along each trajectory given by
t=(t_{1},t_{2},\ldots,t_{m})
. For
m\in\mathbf{N}
as in
\mathscr{S}=\{V_{1},V_{2},\ldots,V_{m}\}
, we let

(3.3)

\displaystyle u_{n}=(u_{1n},u_{2n},\ldots,u_{mn}),\,\,\,n\in\mathbf{Z}_{\geq 0},

denote a countably infinite collection of independent, uniformly distributed random variables on
S_{m}
. Also, let

(3.4)

\displaystyle\tau_{n}=(\tau_{1n},\tau_{2n},\ldots,\tau_{mn}),\,\,\,n\in\mathbf{Z}_{\geq 0},

be an independent collection of random vectors such that for each
i
and
j
,
\tau_{ij}\sim\exp(1/h)
. We furthermore suppose that the collection
\{\tau_{n}\,:\,n\in\mathbf{Z}_{\geq 0}\}
is independent of the collection
\{u_{n}\,:\,n\in\mathbf{Z}_{\geq 0}\}
. Throughout the paper, we let
\tau
be a generic
\exp(1/h)
distributed random variable.

3.2.
Random Splittings

We can construct a discrete-time Markov chain
(X_{n})
with state space
\mathbf{R}^{d}
associated to the splitting
\mathscr{S}
inductively by

(3.5)

\displaystyle X_{n+1}=\Phi^{u_{n}}_{\tau_{n}}\circ X_{n}=\varphi^{u_{mn}}_{\tau_{mn}}\circ\cdots\circ\varphi^{u_{1n}}_{\tau_{1n}}\circ X_{n}.

That is, starting from state
X_{n}
, the state
X_{n+1}
is determined by selecting a uniformly distributed ordering of the vector fields in
\mathscr{S}
and then flowing along each of these in the selected order for an independent,
\exp(1/h)
amount of time. We call the Markov chain
(X_{n})
the
random splitting
associated to
\mathscr{S}
.
For notational convenience, we will also make use of the random variables
X_{n,k}
,
n\geq 0
,
k=0,1,2,\ldots,m
, defined by

\displaystyle X_{n,k}=\begin{cases}X_{n}&\text{ if }k=0,n\geq 0\\
\varphi^{u_{kn}}_{\tau_{kn}}\circ\cdots\circ\varphi^{u_{1n}}_{\tau_{1n}}\circ X_{n},&\text{ if }k=1,2,\ldots,m,\,\,n\geq 0.\end{cases}

Note that the stochastic sequence
(X_{n,k})_{k=0}^{m}
keeps track of
the dynamics between steps
n
and
n+1
of the random splitting. The
sequence
(X_{n,k})
will be referred to as the
random dynamics

associated to the splitting
\mathscr{S}
. Dynamics generated by a
random splitting can be understood as a
random dynamical system

[
4
]
or a collection of random maps
[
20
]
. We use the
term “random splitting” to emphasize the origin of the random maps and
because we will vary the time step parameter
h
. The concept of a random splitting is also closely related to piecewise
deterministic Markov processes (PDMPs)
[
5
,
8
,
14
]
.

3.2.1.
Random splitting of Lorenz ’96

As a non-trivial, illustrative example in this section, we consider a splitting of the forced and partially damped Lorenz ’96 equation on
\mathbf{R}^{d}

(3.6)

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{i}=(x_{i+1}-x_{i-2})x_{i-1}-\delta_{1i}x_{i}+\beta_{i}

where
i=1,\ldots,d
,
d\geq 4
. The indices in equation (
3.6
) live on the discrete circle so that, in particular,
x_{d+1}\equiv x_{1}
,
x_{0}\equiv x_{d}
, and
x_{-1}\equiv x_{d-1}
. We assume that the system (
3.6
) is forced externally by real-valued constants
\beta_{i}\neq 0
,
i=1,\ldots,d
, and that dissipation enters through the first mode only; that is,
\delta_{1i}=1
if
i=1\,(\text{mod}\,d)
and
\delta_{1i}=0
otherwise.

To define the splitting, let
\{e_{i}\}_{i=1}^{d}
denote the standard orthornormal basis of
\mathbf{R}^{d}
and introduce the following vector fields on
\mathbf{R}^{d}

(3.7)

\displaystyle V_{i}(x)=x_{i+1}x_{i-1}e_{i}-x_{i}x_{i-1}e_{i+1},\,\,i=1,\ldots,d,\qquad V_{\star}(x)=-x_{1}e_{1}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\beta_{i}e_{i},

(3.8)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad V(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{d}[(x_{i+1}-x_{i-2})x_{i-1}-\delta_{1i}x_{i}+\beta_{i}]e_{i}.

One can show that each of the vector fields in (
3.7
)-(
3.8
) belongs to
\mathscr{V}_{d}
since the flows along each
V_{i}
,
i=1,2,\ldots,d
, conserve the Euclidean length
|\cdot|
. Furthermore,
\mathscr{S}:=\{V_{1},\ldots,V_{d},V_{\star}\}
forms a splitting of
V\in\mathscr{V}_{d}
since

(3.9)

\displaystyle V(x)=V_{\star}(x)+\sum_{i=1}^{d}V_{i}(x).

Let
(X_{n})
denote the random splitting associated to
\mathscr{S}
and
(\mathcal{P}_{n})
be the associated Markov semigroup. If
H:\mathbf{R}^{d}\rightarrow[1,\infty)
is given by

(3.10)

\displaystyle H(x)=|x|+1,

one of our main goals below will be to prove the following result.

Theorem 3.11
.

For every
h>0
small enough but independent of the dimension
d
, there exist constants
\alpha=\alpha(d,h)\in(0,1)
and
f=f(d,h)>0
such that the bound is satisfied

(3.12)

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{d+1}H(x)\leq\alpha H(x)+f

for all
x\in\mathbf{R}^{d}
.

The proof of Theorem
3.11
will be established partly here in Section
3
and partly in Section
4
by validating the hypotheses of Proposition
2.3
with a constant function
p\equiv p_{*}>0
. Surprisingly, even though damping is externally present only on the first mode, it propagates through the random dynamics to produce the globally contractive-type estimate (
3.12
) for the random splitting. In this regard, the randomization of the ordering of the vector fields is important in the arguments, as it allows us more easily see how energy is transported from one direction to another using a convenient ordering of the fields. Such an ordering necessarily happens with positive probability.

Remark 3.13
.

We recall that Theorem
3.11
ensures the existence of a stationary distribution for the random splitting and also implies that return times to large compact sets have exponential moments (see Section
2
). Note that in previous work
[
3
]
it was shown that the randomly split dynamics in general converges on finite time windows to the underlying deterministic ODE as the switching rate tends to infinity (equivalently, as the mean step size
h>0
converges to
0
). This implies that finite segments of the stochastic trajectory will, as
h\rightarrow 0
, increasingly look like the solution of the deterministic ODE. Consequently, the random splitting will likely affect the structure of the associated invariant measure since this is “time infinity” object. Nonetheless, studying the random splitting helps illuminate some of the mechanisms involved with the transfer of energy leading to dissipative effects.

3.3.
Section overview

Returning to the setting of a general random splitting, in Section
3.4
, Section
3.5
and Section
3.6
we describe the important structural features that produce the bounds required by Proposition
2.3
, and in particular those that lead to estimates like (
3.12
). Namely:

•

A quantity approximately conserved under each vector field in the splitting, i.e. the function
H
in this discussion. This will be further elaborated in Section
3.4
using the notion of
subconservation
. Importantly, subconservation produces the bound (
2.5
) for some constant
f_{2}\geq 0
, for any event
A\in\mathcal{F}
.

•

Dissipation present in at least one vector field in the splitting
\mathscr{S}
. This will be discussed in Section
3.5
below. This structure allows one to establish a bound of the form (
2.4
) for a collection of events
A=A(x)
. These events correspond to certain entrances of the random dynamics to a “dissipative region”
D
in space.

•

Such entrances to the dissipative region
D
must have sufficient probability so that the estimate
\mathbf{P}_{x}(A)\geq p(H(x))
in Proposition
2.3
is satisfied for all
x\in\mathbf{R}^{d}
for some “reasonable” function
p:[1,\infty)\rightarrow(0,1]
. In the case of the splitting for the Lorenz ’96 equation above, we need to be able to take
p
in Proposition
2.3
to be a constant function, i.e.
p\equiv p_{*}>0
. This point will be discussed in more detail in Section
3.6
.

Unless otherwise specified, let
\mathscr{S}=\{V_{1},V_{2},\ldots,V_{m}\}\subset\mathscr{V}_{d}
be a splitting of
V\in\mathscr{V}_{d}
, and let
H:\mathbf{R}^{d}\rightarrow[1,\infty)
be measureable.

3.4.
Subconservation

Perhaps the most basic structural feature of the splittings considered in this paper is that each vector field in
\mathscr{S}
“approximately conserves” the function
H
. To be more precise:

Definition 3.14
.

We call a splitting
\mathscr{S}

subconservative
with respect to
H
if for every
i=1,2,\ldots,m
there exists measurable
F_{i}:[0,\infty)\rightarrow[0,\infty)
such that
f_{i}:=\mathbf{E}F_{i}(\tau)<\infty
and

(3.15)

\displaystyle H(\varphi_{t}^{i}(x))\leq H(x)+F_{i}(t)

for all
x\in\mathbf{R}^{d}
,
t\geq 0
.

Remark 3.16
.

Observe that if a vector field
W\in\mathscr{V}_{d}
conserves
H
, then
H(\varphi_{t}^{W}(x))=H(x)
for all
x\in\mathbf{R}^{d}
,
t\geq 0
. Thus subconservation is a slight deviation from this concept where the function
H
along all of the vector fields in the splitting does not grow “too fast”.

Example 3.17
.

Consider the splitting
\mathscr{S}=\{V_{1},\ldots,V_{d},V_{\star}\}
of the Lorenz ’96 equation (
3.6
) introduced in (
3.7
), and let
H
be as in (
3.10
). We claim that
\mathscr{S}
is subconservative with respect to
H
. To see why, note first that for
i=1,2,\ldots,d
and
t\geq 0

\displaystyle H(\varphi^{i}_{t}(x))=H(x)

since each of the vector fields
V_{1},V_{2},\ldots,V_{d}
conserves length
|x|
. In particular, we can take
F_{i}\equiv 0
in (
3.15
). On the other hand, letting
\varphi_{t}^{\star}(x):=\varphi_{t}^{V_{\star}}(x)
and using the triangle inequality we have

\displaystyle H(\varphi_{t}^{\star}(x))

\displaystyle=|(e^{-t}x_{1}+(1-e^{-t})\beta_{1},x_{2}+\beta_{2}t,\ldots,x_{d}+\beta_{d}t)|+1

\displaystyle\leq H(x)+|(\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\ldots,\beta_{d})|t,\quad t\geq 0.

Hence we can take
F_{\star}(t)=|(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{d})|t
as in (
3.15
) and the claim is established.

As a basic consequence of subconservation, we have the following result. See also (
2.5
).

Proposition 3.18
.

Suppose that
H
is subconservative for
\mathscr{S}
and let
(X_{n})
denote the random splitting associated to
\mathscr{S}
. Then

(3.19)

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{x}\Big[\mathbf{1}_{A}H(X_{n})\Big]\leq\mathbf{P}_{x}(A)H(x)+n\sum_{i=1}^{m}f_{i}

for all
x\in\mathbf{R}^{d}
,
n\geq 0
and
A\in\mathcal{F}
. In the above,
f_{i}=\mathbf{E}F_{i}(\tau)\geq 0
is as in (
3.15
).

Proof.

The bound (
3.19
) is trivially satisfied when
n=0
. Thus suppose
n\geq 1
. Since
\mathscr{S}
is subconservative with respect to
H
, let
F_{i}
and
f_{i}
be as in (
3.15
) and observe that for any
i\in\{1,2,\ldots,m-1\}
we have by definition of the random splitting and subconservation

(3.20)

\displaystyle H(X_{n})=H(X_{n-1,m})

\displaystyle\leq H(X_{n-1,m-i})+\textstyle{\sum_{k=m-i+1}^{m}}F_{u_{k(n-1)}}(\tau_{k(n-1)})

\displaystyle\leq H(X_{n-1})+\textstyle{\sum}_{k=1}^{m}F_{u_{k(n-1)}}(\tau_{k(n-1)}).

Hence using independence it follows for any
A\in\mathcal{F}

(3.21)

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{x}[\mathbf{1}_{A}H(X_{n})]\leq\mathbf{E}_{x}[\mathbf{1}_{A}H(X_{n-1})]+\textstyle{\sum}_{i=1}^{m}f_{i}.

The bound (
3.19
) follows by iterating (
3.21
).
∎

3.5.
Partial dissipation

We next explore the basic structural feature of the random splittings in this paper which give rise to estimates of the form (
2.4
). Recall that
\mathcal{B}
denotes the Borel measurable subsets of the state space
\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{R}^{d}
.

Definition 3.22
.

Let
D\in\mathcal{B}
be non-empty. We call the splitting
\mathscr{S}

partially dissipative for

H

on the set

D

with index

\ell\in\{1,2,\ldots,m\}
if there exists a measurable function
G_{\ell}:[0,\infty)\rightarrow[0,\infty)
with
g_{\ell}:=\mathbf{E}G_{\ell}(\tau)<\infty
and a strictly decreasing function
\alpha_{\ell}:[0,\infty)\rightarrow(0,1]
for which the bound holds

(3.23)

\displaystyle H(\varphi^{\ell}_{t}(x))\leq\alpha_{\ell}(t)H(x)+G_{\ell}(t),\,\,\,x\in D,\,\,t\geq 0.

Notationally, we set
a_{\ell}=\mathbf{E}\alpha_{\ell}(\tau)\in(0,1)
. The set
D
will be called a
dissipative region
for the random splitting
(X_{n})
. The index
\ell
will be called a
dissipative direction
.

Example 3.24
.

Consider again the splitting
\mathscr{S}=\{V_{1},\ldots,V_{d},V_{\star}\}
of the Lorenz ’96 equation (
3.6
) introduced in (
3.7
), and let
H
be as in (
3.10
). Fixing
\eta\in(0,1)
, we claim that
\mathscr{S}
is partially dissipative with respect to
H
on the set

(3.25)

\displaystyle D_{\eta}=\{x\in\mathbf{R}^{n}\,:\,|x_{1}|^{2}\geq\eta|x|^{2}\}

with index
\ell=\star
. Indeed, if
F_{\star}(t)=|(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{d})|t
, then for
t\geq 0
we have

\displaystyle H(\varphi_{t}^{\star}(x))

\displaystyle=|(e^{-t}x_{1}+(1-e^{-t})\beta_{1},x_{2}+\beta_{2}t,\ldots,x_{d}+\beta_{d}t)|+1

\displaystyle\leq H(e^{-t}x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{d})+F_{\star}(t)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{(e^{-2t}-1)x_{1}^{2}+|x|^{2}}+1+F_{\star}(t)

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{1-\eta(1-e^{-2t})}|x|+1+F_{\star}(t)\leq\alpha_{\star}(t)H(x)+F_{\star}(t)+1

where
\alpha_{\star}(t)=\sqrt{1-\eta(1-e^{-2t})}
is strictly decreasing on
[0,\infty)
and maps
[0,\infty)
into
(0,1]
. This finishes the proof of the claim.

Our next result relates partial dissipation and subconservation to an estimate of the form (
2.4
). To state it, recalling that
(X_{n,k})
denotes the random dynamics associated to the splitting
\mathscr{S}
, for any
D\in\mathcal{B}
,
\ell\in\{1,2,\ldots,m\}
and
n\geq 0
, let

\displaystyle T_{n,D}=\min\big\{k\in\{0,1,\ldots,m-1\}\,:\,X_{n,k}\in D\big\},

where we understand
\min\emptyset=\infty
,
and

(3.26)

\displaystyle A_{n}^{\ell}(D)

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{k=0}^{m-1}\Big\{T_{n,D}=k\Big\}\cap\Big\{\ell=u_{(k+1)n}\Big\}=:\bigcup_{k=0}^{m-1}A_{n,k}^{\ell}(D).

Note that
A_{n,k}^{\ell}(D)
is the event that the random sequence
(X_{n,j})_{j=0}^{m-1}
first enters the set
D
after the
k
th flow and the next flow, namely the
(k+1)
st flow, follows the vector field
V_{\ell}
.

Proposition 3.27
.

Let
D\in\mathcal{B}
be nonempty and suppose that the splitting
\mathscr{S}
is subconservative for
H
and partially dissipative for
H
on the set
D
with index
\ell
, and let
a_{\ell}\in(0,1)
be as in Definition
3.22
. Then there exists constants
f_{n,k}\geq 0
such that

(3.28)

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{x}\big[\mathbf{1}_{A_{n}^{\ell}(D)}H(X_{k})\big]\leq a_{\ell}\mathbf{P}_{x}(A_{n}^{\ell}(D))H(x)+f_{n,k}

for all
x\in\mathbf{R}^{d}
,
k\geq n+1
.

Proof.

Let
D\in\mathcal{B}
be nonempty and suppose that the bound (
3.23
) holds for some index
\ell\in\{1,2,\ldots,m\}
, measurable
G_{\ell}:[0,\infty)\rightarrow[0,\infty)
with
g_{\ell}=\mathbf{E}G_{\ell}(\tau)<\infty
and strictly decreasing function
\alpha_{\ell}:[0,\infty)\rightarrow(0,1]
with
a_{\ell}=\mathbf{E}\alpha_{\ell}(\tau)
. Since
\mathscr{S}
is subconservative, suppose the bounds (
3.15
) hold for some
F_{i}:[0,\infty)\rightarrow[0,\infty)
measurable with
f_{i}=\mathbf{E}F_{i}(\tau)<\infty
. For simplicity in the arguments below, set
A=A_{n}^{\ell}(D)
and
A_{j}=A_{n,j}^{\ell}(D)
.

Since
k\geq n+1
and the union (
3.26
) is disjoint, relation (
3.21
) gives

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{x}\mathbf{1}_{A}H(X_{k})

\displaystyle\leq\mathbf{E}_{x}\mathbf{1}_{A}H(X_{n+1})+(k-n-1)\textstyle{\sum_{i=1}^{m}f_{i}}

\displaystyle=\textstyle{\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}}\mathbf{E}_{x}\mathbf{1}_{A_{j}}H(X_{n+1})+(k-n-1)\textstyle{\sum_{i=1}^{m}f_{i}}

(3.29)

\displaystyle\leq\textstyle{\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}}\mathbf{E}_{x}\mathbf{1}_{A_{j}}H(X_{n,j+1})+(k-n)\textstyle{\sum_{i=1}^{m}f_{i}}.

Next, observe that

\displaystyle\mathbf{1}_{A_{j}}H(X_{n,j+1})\leq\mathbf{1}_{A_{j}}\alpha_{\ell}(\tau_{(j+1)n})H(X_{n,j})+\mathbf{1}_{A_{j}}G_{\ell}(\tau_{(j+1)n}).

Using independence of
\tau_{(j+1)n}
and
\mathbf{1}_{A_{j}}H(X_{n,j})
, and independence of
\tau_{(j+1)n}
and
\mathbf{1}_{A_{j}}
, we thus obtain

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{x}\mathbf{1}_{A_{j}}H(X_{n,j+1})\leq a_{\ell}\mathbf{E}_{x}[\mathbf{1}_{A_{j}}H(X_{n,j})]+g_{\ell}\mathbf{P}(A_{j}).

Hence, this estimate with subconservation implies

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{x}\mathbf{1}_{A_{j}}H(X_{n,j+1})

\displaystyle\leq a_{\ell}\mathbf{E}_{x}[\mathbf{1}_{A_{j}}H(X_{n,j})]+g_{\ell}\mathbf{P}(A_{j})

(3.30)

\displaystyle\leq a_{\ell}\mathbf{P}_{x}(A_{j})H(x)+(n+1)\textstyle{\sum_{j=1}^{m}}f_{i}+g_{\ell}\mathbf{P}(A_{j}).

Combining (
3.29
) with (
3.30
), we obtain the claimed estimate (
3.28
) with
f_{n,k}
given by

(3.31)

\displaystyle f_{n,k}=g_{\ell}+((m-1)n+m+k)\textstyle{\sum_{i=1}^{m}f_{i}}.

∎

3.6.
Entrances to a dissipative region

As we have seen in the case of the random splitting of the Lorenz ’96 equation (
3.6
), checking subconservation and partial dissipation for the random splitting is relatively straightforward. See Example
3.17
and Example
3.24
for further details of this point. By way of Proposition
2.3
, the central difficulty in showing a bound like (
2.6
) is estimating
\mathbf{P}_{x}(A_{n}^{\ell}(D))
for a dissipative set
D
and a dissipative direction
\ell\in\{1,2,\ldots,m\}
. Such an estimate comes from the specific nature of the dynamics of the splitting, as the system transports an initial condition
x\in\mathbf{R}^{d}
to
D
in a certain number of steps
n
. The way in which this transport happens inherently depends on
x
. In this section, we provide the condition (Theorem
3.33
below) we will check in subsequent sections to establish the needed lower bound on this probability.

To state the result, define for
R>0

(3.32)

\displaystyle H_{>R}=\{x\in\mathbf{R}^{d}\,:\,H(x)>R\}.

Theorem 3.33
.

Let
D\in\mathcal{B}
be nonempty and suppose that the splitting
\mathscr{S}
is subconservative with respect to
H
and partially dissipative with respect to
H
on the set
D
with index
\ell
. Suppose there exist
n\in\mathbf{Z}_{\geq 1}
,
R>0
and
p:[1,\infty)\rightarrow(0,1]
such that

(3.34)

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{x}(A_{j(x)}^{\ell}(D))\geq p(H(x))\,\,\,\text{ for all }\,\,\,x\in H_{>R},

for some index
j(x)\leq n-1
. Then the Markov chain
(\tilde{X}_{j}):=(X_{jn})
satisfies the hypotheses of Proposition
2.3
with the same choice of
p
.

Remark 3.35
.

We emphasize that the index
j(x)
in (
3.34
) depends on the initial state
x
but has the uniform upper bound
j(x)\leq n-1
over all
x\in H_{>R}
.

Proof of Theorem
3.33
.

Since
\mathscr{S}
is subconservative with respect to
H
, pick
F_{i}
and
f_{i}=\mathbf{E}F_{i}(\tau)<\infty
so that the bounds (
3.15
) hold for
i=1,2,\ldots,m
. Since
\mathscr{S}
is partially dissipative with respect to
H
on
D
with index
\ell
, suppose that the bound (
3.23
) is satisfied for some measurable
G_{\ell}:[0,\infty)\rightarrow[0,\infty)
and
\alpha_{\ell}:[0,\infty)\rightarrow(0,1]
with
\alpha_{\ell}
strictly decreasing and
g_{\ell}=\mathbf{E}G_{\ell}(\tau)<\infty
and
a_{\ell}=\mathbf{E}\alpha_{\ell}(\tau)\in(0,1)
. By hypothesis, pick
n\in\mathbf{Z}_{\geq 1}
and
R>0
such that for every
x\in H_{>R}
we have (
3.34
). We claim that the Markov chain
(\tilde{X}_{j}):=(X_{jn})
satisfies the hypothesis of Proposition
2.3
.

For
x\in H_{>R}
, define the event
A=A(x)
in the statement of Proposition
2.3
by
A(x)=A_{j(x)}^{\ell}(D)
. Then

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{x}(A)=\mathbf{P}(A(x))\geq p(H(x))\,\,\text{ for all }x\in H_{>R}.

On the other hand, for
x\in H_{\leq R}
we define
A=A(x)=\Omega
, so that

\mathbf{P}_{x}(A)=\mathbf{P}(A(x))=1\geq p(H(x))\,\,\text{ for all }\,\,x\in H_{\leq R}.

Next, by Proposition
3.27
, for any
x\in H_{>R}
we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{x}\mathbf{1}_{A}H(\tilde{X}_{1})=\mathbf{E}_{x}\mathbf{1}_{A}H(X_{n})

\displaystyle\leq a_{\ell}\mathbf{P}_{x}(A)H(x)+c_{n}

for some constant
c_{n}\geq 0
.
By Proposition
3.18
, we have for any
B\in\mathcal{F}
and
x\in\mathbf{R}^{d}

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{x}\mathbf{1}_{B}H(\tilde{X}_{1})=\mathbf{E}_{x}\mathbf{1}_{B}H(X_{n})

\displaystyle\leq\mathbf{P}_{x}(B)H(x)+n\textstyle{\sum_{i=1}^{m}f_{i}}.

In particular, for any
x\in H_{\leq R}

∎

Lemma 4.6

(Downward energy transfer)
.

Let

(4.7)

\displaystyle|\sin(|x_{j-2}|\tau)|\leq h.

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}(\tilde{B}_{1}(x_{j-2})^{c})=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathbf{P}\big(a\tau\in(3h+\pi k,-3h+\pi(k+1))\big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{\pi k}{ha}}\bigg\{e^{-\frac{3}{a}}-e^{\frac{3}{a}-\frac{\pi}{ha}}\bigg\}

Lemma 4.20

Remark 5.24
.

Assumption
5.22
aligns with study of out-of-equilibrium fluxes in turbulence. The damping assumption on high frequencies corresponds to the effect of viscosity on high Fourier modes. Damping on low modes corresponds to the effect of friction at large scales. Damping on the large scales is needed to prevent energy build up since our model is on the two-dimensional periodic box. The absence of dissipation on the intermediate modes, which should be thought of as the systems’ inertial range in turbulence theory, is important here because we want to study energy transfer between low modes and high modes created by the nonlinearity. Since the system is dissipative, we must force the system to keep it out of the zero state. We choose to only force at the large scales to emphasize that we are interested in studying in how energy is transferred through the inertial range (i.e. the middle modes) to the dissipative scale (i.e. the high modes).

Remark 5.27

The difference between the conclusions of Theorem

Recall that in Section 2, this correction leads to stretched exponential moments, versus exponential moments in the case of Lorenz ’96, of the first return time to a large compact set in the phase space. Lemma 5.35

Suppose that

Proof.

We suppose without loss of generality that

\displaystyle B_{4}=B_{4}(\mathbf{j},\mathbf{l}):=\bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty}\bigg\{2\pi k+\frac{\pi}{6}\leq\frac{\theta_{\mathbf{j}\mathbf{l}}z_{0}\tau}{\delta}+\theta_{*}\leq\frac{\pi}{4}+2\pi k\bigg\}.

\displaystyle|x_{\tau}|=\rho_{0}\bigg|\cos\bigg(\frac{\theta_{\mathbf{j}\mathbf{l}}z_{0}\tau}{\delta}+\theta_{*}\bigg)\bigg|\geq\frac{\rho_{0}}{\sqrt{2}}\geq\frac{c_{0}}{2}\,\,\,\text{ and }\,\,\,|y_{\tau}|=\rho_{0}\bigg|\sin\bigg(\frac{\theta_{\mathbf{j}\mathbf{l}}z_{0}\tau}{\delta}+\theta_{*}\bigg)\bigg|\geq\frac{\rho_{0}}{2}\geq\frac{c_{0}}{2}.

such that

\displaystyle\xi\leq\mathcal{E}\leq 1\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\frac{\mathcal{E}}{|\mathbf{l}|^{2}}+\zeta\leq E\leq\frac{\mathcal{E}}{|\mathbf{k}|^{2}}-\zeta\delta^{2}.

(A2)

There exists constants
\xi\in(0,1),\zeta\in(0,\zeta_{0}(\mathbf{j},\mathbf{k})d)
independent of
\delta
such that

\displaystyle\xi\leq\mathcal{E}\leq 1\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\frac{\mathcal{E}}{|\mathbf{k}|^{2}}+\zeta\delta^{2}\leq E\leq\frac{\mathcal{E}}{|\mathbf{j}|^{2}}-\zeta.

Remark 5.43
.

Although it is suggestive, Assumption
5.42
is
not
equivalent to the condition that there exists a constant
c>0
small, independent of
\delta\in(0,1)
for which

(5.44)

\displaystyle c\leq y^{2}_{0}\leq\mathcal{E}-c\delta^{2}.

The above relation (
5.44
) means that, initially, the middle mode
y
in (
5.32
) contains some, but not all of the relative enstrophy
\mathcal{E}
. To see why they are not equivalent, note that the condition
E=\mathcal{E}/|\mathbf{k}|^{2}
is equivalent to the initial data
x_{0}
and
z_{0}
in (
5.32
) satisfying

(5.45)

\displaystyle x^{2}_{0}=z^{2}_{0}\frac{\frac{1}{|\mathbf{k}|^{2}}-\frac{1}{|\mathbf{l}|^{2}}}{\frac{1}{|\mathbf{j}|^{2}}-\frac{1}{|\mathbf{k}|^{2}}}.

Note that if
y_{0}
satisfies (
5.44
), then using the fact that
|\mathbf{l}|^{2}>|\mathbf{k}|^{2}>|\mathbf{j}|^{2}
we can find
x_{0},z_{0}
satisfying (
5.45
). When returning back to the randomly switched system, in order to deal with this family of
bad
initial conditions, we will employ the assumption that
\mathbf{j}\in\mathscr{F}
. In particular, this will allow us to ensure, with positive probability, that we can initially perturb the dynamics away from the surface (
5.45
) so that for some constant
\zeta^{\prime}>0
independent of
\delta
, the resulting coordinates
(x_{0},y_{0},z_{0})
satisfy either

\displaystyle x^{2}_{0}\bigg(\frac{1}{|\mathbf{j}|^{2}}-\frac{1}{|\mathbf{k}|^{2}}\bigg)\leq z^{2}_{0}\bigg(\frac{1}{|\mathbf{k}|^{2}}-\frac{1}{|\mathbf{l}|^{2}}\bigg)-\zeta^{\prime}\delta^{2},

which is equivalent to
E\leq\tfrac{\mathcal{E}}{|\mathbf{k}|^{2}}-\zeta^{\prime}\delta^{2}
, or

\displaystyle x^{2}_{0}\bigg(\frac{1}{|\mathbf{j}|^{2}}-\frac{1}{|\mathbf{k}|^{2}}\bigg)\geq z^{2}_{0}\bigg(\frac{1}{|\mathbf{k}|^{2}}-\frac{1}{|\mathbf{l}|^{2}}\bigg)+\zeta^{\prime}\delta^{2},

which is equivalent to
E\geq\frac{\mathcal{E}}{|k|^{2}}+\zeta^{\prime}\delta^{2}.
This will then imply Assumption
5.42
.

In order to state our main result of this section, for
\eta\in(0,1/2)
and
\delta\in(0,1)
, consider the following set

(5.46)

\displaystyle B_{\eta,\delta}=\{s\geq 0\,:\,\min\{|x_{s}|,|y_{s}|,|z_{s}|\}\geq\eta\}

where
(x_{s},y_{s},z_{s})
denotes the solution of (
5.32
). Of course, the solution
(x_{s},y_{s},z_{s})
depends on
\delta
, but we suppress this dependence for notational simplicity.

Theorem 5.47

(Thermalization)
.

Suppose that Assumption
5.42
is satisfied. Then there exist constants
\eta\in(0,1/4),c>0
and
\delta_{*}\in(0,1)
depending only on
|\mathbf{j}|,|\mathbf{k}|,|\mathbf{l}|,\zeta,\xi,h
so that

(5.48)

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\{\tau\in B_{\eta,\delta}\}\geq\frac{c}{|\log\delta|}\,\,\,\text{ for all }\delta\in(0,\delta_{*}).

Remark 5.49
.

Note that conclusion (
5.48
) is
thermalization
of the triple
(x_{s},y_{s},z_{s})
with probability
\geq c/|\log\delta|
.

In order to prove Theorem
5.47
, we first introduce and deduce properties of the so-called
Jacobi elliptic functions
. As we will see below, these functions can be used to arrive at explicit expressions for solutions of equation (
5.32
).

To begin, for
(\mathbf{j},\mathbf{k},\mathbf{l})\in\mathscr{I}
with
|\mathbf{k}|>|\mathbf{j}|
, we first define the following parameters

(5.50)

\displaystyle\kappa_{1}=\frac{E-\frac{\mathcal{E}}{|\mathbf{l}|^{2}}}{\frac{1}{|\mathbf{j}|^{2}}-\frac{1}{|\mathbf{l}|^{2}}},\qquad\gamma_{1}=\frac{\frac{1}{|\mathbf{k}|^{2}}-\frac{1}{|\mathbf{l}|^{2}}}{E-\frac{\mathcal{E}}{|\mathbf{l}|^{2}}},\qquad\kappa_{2}=\frac{\frac{\mathcal{E}}{|\mathbf{j}|^{2}}-E}{\frac{1}{|\mathbf{j}|^{2}}-\frac{1}{|\mathbf{l}|^{2}}},\qquad\gamma_{2}=\frac{{\frac{1}{|\mathbf{j}|^{2}}-\frac{1}{|\mathbf{k}|^{2}}}}{\frac{\mathcal{E}}{|\mathbf{j}|^{2}}-E}

where
E,\mathcal{E}
are the conserved quantities in (
5.38
). Observe that each of these parameters is strictly positive under Assumption
5.42
. Furthermore, these parameters arise from relations (
5.38
)-(
5.39
) by solving for
x
and
z
in terms of
y
. Indeed, we see that (
5.38
)-(
5.39
) together imply

(5.51)

\displaystyle x^{2}=\kappa_{1}(1-\gamma_{1}y^{2})\qquad\text{ and }\qquad z^{2}=\kappa_{2}(1-\gamma_{2}y^{2}).

Next, define

(5.52)

\displaystyle\gamma_{\text{min}}=\gamma_{1}\wedge\gamma_{2},\qquad\gamma_{\text{max}}=\gamma_{1}\vee\gamma_{2},\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\rho=\frac{\gamma_{\text{min}}}{\gamma_{\text{max}}}.

One can check that

\displaystyle\frac{\mathcal{E}}{|\mathbf{l}|^{2}}<E<\frac{\mathcal{E}}{|\mathbf{k}|^{2}}\,\,\,\text{ implies }\,\,\,\gamma_{1}>\mathcal{E}^{-1}\text{ and }\gamma_{2}<\mathcal{E}^{-1},\text{ so }\rho=\frac{\gamma_{2}}{\gamma_{1}}<1.

Similarly,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathcal{E}}{|\mathbf{k}|^{2}}<E<\frac{\mathcal{E}}{|\mathbf{j}|^{2}}\,\,\,\text{ implies }\,\,\,\gamma_{2}>\mathcal{E}^{-1}\text{ and }\gamma_{1}<\mathcal{E}^{-1},\text{ so }\rho=\frac{\gamma_{1}}{\gamma_{2}}<1.

Remark 5.53
.

To motivate the use of Jacobi elliptic functions with the parameters above, we observe that, formally, if
b:=y\sqrt{\gamma_{\text{max}}}
where
y
is as in (
5.32
), then

\displaystyle\dot{b}=\frac{\sqrt{\gamma_{\text{max}}\kappa_{1}\kappa_{2}}\theta_{\mathbf{j}\mathbf{l}}}{\delta}\sqrt{1-\rho b^{2}}\sqrt{1-b^{2}}.

Since
\rho\in(0,1)
, the above system is explicitly (locally) integrable but we need to be careful about the signs in the square roots. These signs depend on the initial data as well as the periodicity in the system (
5.32
). A similar line of reasoning can also be used to arrive at formal explicitly integrable equations for
x
and
z
in (
5.32
).

Following the previous remark, define a function
T_{\rho}
on
[0,1]
and a number
K_{\rho}
by

(5.54)

\displaystyle T_{\rho}(s)=\int_{0}^{s}\frac{db}{\sqrt{1-\rho b^{2}}\sqrt{1-b^{2}}}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad K_{\rho}:=T_{\rho}(1).

Observe that since
\rho<1
,
T_{\rho}
is a well-defined, strictly increasing function on
[0,1]
with range
[0,K_{\rho}]
. Let
\text{sn}_{\rho}:[0,K_{\rho}]\rightarrow[0,1]
denote the inverse of
T_{\rho}
. We extend
\text{sn}_{\rho}
to the larger interval
[0,4K_{r}]
by defining

(5.55)

\displaystyle\text{sn}_{\rho}(x):=\text{sn}_{\rho}(2K_{\rho}-x),\qquad x\in[K_{\rho},2K_{\rho}],

\displaystyle\text{sn}_{\rho}(x):=-\text{sn}_{\rho}(4K_{\rho}-x)\qquad x\in[2K_{\rho},4K_{\rho}].

We extend
\text{sn}_{\rho}
periodically to all of
\mathbf{R}
with period
4K_{\rho}
. Similarly, the function
\text{cn}_{\rho}
is defined initially on the interval
[0,4K_{r}]
by

(5.56)

\displaystyle\text{cn}_{\rho}(x)

\displaystyle:=\sqrt{1-\text{sn}_{\rho}^{2}(x)},\qquad x\in[0,K_{\rho}],

\displaystyle\text{cn}_{\rho}(x)

\displaystyle:=-\text{cn}_{\rho}(2K_{\rho}-x),\quad x\in[K_{\rho},2K_{\rho}],

\displaystyle\text{cn}_{\rho}(x)

\displaystyle:=\text{cn}_{\rho}(4K_{\rho}-x),\quad x\in[2K_{\rho},4K_{\rho}]

and then extended to all of
\mathbf{R}
to a function which is periodic with period
4K_{\rho}
. The function
\text{dn}_{\rho}
is defined by

(5.57)

\displaystyle\text{dn}_{\rho}(x):=\sqrt{1-\rho\text{sn}_{\rho}(x)^{2}},\qquad x\in\mathbf{R}.

Note that because
\rho<1
,
\text{dn}_{\rho}
remains positive for all values of
x
whereas
\text{sn}_{\rho}
and
\text{cn}_{\rho}
oscillate between
1
and
-1
, similar to the usual sine and cosine. The function
\text{sn}_{\rho}
,

\text{cn}_{\rho}
, and
\text{dn}_{\rho}
are called
Jacobi elliptic functions
. One can check that (see
[
1
]
) the functions
\text{sn}_{\rho}
,
\text{cn}_{\rho}
and
\text{dn}_{\rho}
are differentiable and satisfy the following differential identities

(5.58)

\displaystyle\text{sn}_{\rho}^{\prime}(x)=\text{cn}_{\rho}(x)\text{dn}_{\rho}(x),\quad\text{cn}_{\rho}^{\prime}(x)=-\text{sn}_{\rho}(x)\text{dn}_{\rho}(x),\quad\text{dn}_{\rho}^{\prime}(x)=-\rho\text{sn}_{\rho}(x)\text{cn}_{\rho}(x).

Using the above facts, we can now deduce explicit expressions involving the Jacobi elliptic functions for solutions of (
5.32
). The explicit expressions vary slightly depending on the value of the relative energy
\mathcal{E}
and the initial data, as we now note.

Assumption
(A1)

Suppose that Assumption
5.42
(A1) is satisfied. First observe that
\mathcal{E}/|\mathbf{l}|^{2}<E<\mathcal{E}/|\mathbf{k}|^{2}
implies that the initial condition
z_{0}
in (
5.32
) is nonzero. Indeed, if
z_{0}=0
, then using
|\mathbf{l}|>|\mathbf{k}|>|\mathbf{j}|
gives

\displaystyle E=\frac{x^{2}_{0}}{|\mathbf{j}|^{2}}+\frac{y^{2}_{0}}{|\mathbf{k}|^{2}}\geq\frac{\mathcal{E}}{|\mathbf{k}|^{2}}.

Now, if
z_{0}>0
, then we can express
x
,
y
and
z
as

\displaystyle x_{t}=\sqrt{\kappa_{1}}\text{cn}_{\rho}\bigg[

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{\gamma_{1}\kappa_{1}\kappa_{2}}\theta_{\mathbf{j}\mathbf{l}}}{\delta}t+\theta_{0}\bigg],\quad y_{t}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\gamma_{1}}}\text{sn}_{\rho}\bigg[\frac{\sqrt{\gamma_{1}\kappa_{1}\kappa_{2}}\theta_{\mathbf{j}\mathbf{l}}}{\delta}t+\theta_{0}\bigg],

\displaystyle z_{t}=\sqrt{\kappa_{2}}\text{dn}_{\rho}\bigg[\frac{\sqrt{\gamma_{1}\kappa_{1}\kappa_{2}}\theta_{\mathbf{j}\mathbf{l}}}{\delta}t+\theta_{0}\bigg],

where
\theta_{0}\in[0,4K_{\rho})
is the unique point so that

(5.59)

\displaystyle x_{0}=\sqrt{\kappa_{1}}\text{cn}_{\rho}(\theta_{0})\qquad\text{ and }\qquad y_{0}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\gamma_{1}}}\text{sn}_{\rho}(\theta_{0}).

On the other hand, if
z_{0}<0
, then the solution of (
5.32
) can be written as

\displaystyle x_{t}=\sqrt{\kappa_{1}}\text{cn}_{\rho}\bigg[

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{\gamma_{1}\kappa_{1}\kappa_{2}}\theta_{\mathbf{j}\mathbf{l}}}{\delta}t+\theta_{0}\bigg],\quad y_{t}=-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\gamma_{1}}}\text{sn}_{\rho}\bigg[\frac{\sqrt{\gamma_{1}\kappa_{1}\kappa_{2}}\theta_{\mathbf{j}\mathbf{l}}}{\delta}t+\theta_{0}\bigg],

\displaystyle z_{t}=-\sqrt{\kappa_{2}}\text{dn}_{\rho}\bigg[\frac{\sqrt{\gamma_{1}\kappa_{1}\kappa_{2}}\theta_{\mathbf{j}\mathbf{l}}}{\delta}t+\theta_{0}\bigg],

where
\theta_{0}\in[0,4K_{\rho})
is the unique point so that

(5.60)

\displaystyle x_{0}=\sqrt{\kappa_{1}}\text{cn}_{\rho}(\theta_{0})\qquad\text{ and }\qquad y_{0}=-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\gamma_{1}}}\text{sn}_{\rho}(\theta_{0}).

Additionally, it is not hard to check that the conditions
\mathcal{E}\in[\xi,1]
and
\mathcal{E}/|\mathbf{l}|^{2}+\zeta\leq E\leq\mathcal{E}/|\mathbf{k}|^{2}-\zeta\delta^{2}
imply that the parameters
\kappa_{1},\gamma_{1},\kappa_{2},\gamma_{2}
are bounded above and below by positive constants that do not depend on
\delta
. Indeed, we have the following bounds

(5.61)

\displaystyle\frac{\zeta}{\frac{1}{|\mathbf{j}|^{2}}-\frac{1}{|\mathbf{l}|^{2}}}

\displaystyle\leq\kappa_{1}\leq\frac{\frac{1}{|\mathbf{k}|^{2}}-\frac{1}{|\mathbf{l}|^{2}}}{\frac{1}{|\mathbf{j}|^{2}}-\frac{1}{|\mathbf{l}|^{2}}},\qquad\,\,\,1\leq\gamma_{1}\leq\frac{\frac{1}{|\mathbf{k}|^{2}}-\frac{1}{|\mathbf{l}|^{2}}}{\zeta},

\displaystyle\frac{\frac{\xi}{|\mathbf{j}|^{2}}-\frac{\xi}{|\mathbf{k}|^{2}}}{\frac{1}{|\mathbf{j}|^{2}}-\frac{1}{|\mathbf{l}|^{2}}}

\displaystyle\leq\kappa_{2}\leq\frac{\frac{1}{|\mathbf{j}|^{2}}-\frac{1}{|\mathbf{l}|^{2}}-\zeta}{\frac{1}{|\mathbf{j}|^{2}}-\frac{1}{|\mathbf{l}|^{2}}},\qquad\frac{\frac{1}{|\mathbf{j}|^{2}}-\frac{1}{|\mathbf{k}|^{2}}}{\frac{1}{|\mathbf{j}|^{2}}-\frac{1}{|\mathbf{l}|^{2}}-\zeta}\leq\gamma_{2}\leq 1/\xi.

Assumption
(A2)

Suppose now that Assumption
5.42
(A2) is satisfied. Then a similar argument to the one used above implies that
x_{0}\neq 0
. If
x_{0}>0
, then the unique solution of (
5.32
) is given by

\displaystyle x_{t}=\sqrt{\kappa}_{1}\text{dn}_{\rho}\bigg[

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{\gamma_{2}\kappa_{1}\kappa_{2}}\theta_{\mathbf{j}\mathbf{l}}}{\delta}t+\theta_{0}\bigg],\,\,\,\,y_{t}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\gamma_{2}}}\text{sn}_{\rho}\bigg[\frac{\sqrt{\gamma_{2}\kappa_{1}\kappa_{2}}\theta_{\mathbf{j}\mathbf{l}}}{\delta}t+\theta_{0}\bigg],

\displaystyle z_{t}=\sqrt{\kappa_{2}}\text{cn}_{\rho}\bigg[\frac{\sqrt{\gamma_{2}\kappa_{1}\kappa_{2}}\theta_{\mathbf{j}\mathbf{l}}}{\delta}t+\theta_{0}\bigg],

where
\theta_{0}\in[0,4K_{\rho})
is defined by

(5.62)

\displaystyle y_{0}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\gamma_{2}}}\text{sn}_{\rho}(\theta_{0})\qquad\text{ and }\qquad z_{0}=\sqrt{\kappa_{2}}\text{cn}_{\rho}(\theta_{0}).

On the other hand if
x_{0}<0
, then the unique solution of (
5.32
) is given by

\displaystyle x_{t}=-\sqrt{\kappa}_{1}\text{dn}_{\rho}\bigg[

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{\gamma_{2}\kappa_{1}\kappa_{2}}\theta_{\mathbf{j}\mathbf{l}}}{\delta}t+\theta_{0}\bigg],\,\,\,\,y_{t}=-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\gamma_{2}}}\text{sn}_{\rho}\bigg[\frac{\sqrt{\gamma_{2}\kappa_{1}\kappa_{2}}\theta_{\mathbf{j}\mathbf{l}}}{\delta}t+\theta_{0}\bigg],

\displaystyle z_{t}=\sqrt{\kappa_{2}}\text{cn}_{\rho}\bigg[\frac{\sqrt{\gamma_{2}\kappa_{1}\kappa_{2}}\theta_{\mathbf{j}\mathbf{l}}}{\delta}t+\theta_{0}\bigg],

where
\theta_{0}\in[0,4K_{\rho})
is defined by

(5.63)

\displaystyle y_{0}=-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\gamma_{2}}}\text{sn}_{\rho}(\theta_{0})\qquad\text{ and }\qquad z_{0}=\sqrt{\kappa_{2}}\text{cn}_{\rho}(\theta_{0}).

In this case, we also have bounds for the positive parameters
\kappa_{1},\gamma_{1},\kappa_{2},\gamma_{2}
analogous to those in (
5.61
):

(5.64)

\displaystyle\frac{\frac{\xi}{|\mathbf{k}|^{2}}-\frac{\xi}{|\mathbf{l}|^{2}}}{\frac{1}{|\mathbf{j}|^{2}}-\frac{1}{|\mathbf{l}|^{2}}}

\displaystyle\leq\kappa_{1}\leq\frac{\frac{1}{|\mathbf{j}|^{2}}-\frac{1}{|\mathbf{l}|^{2}}-\zeta}{\frac{1}{|\mathbf{j}|^{2}}-\frac{1}{|\mathbf{l}|^{2}}},\qquad\frac{\frac{1}{|\mathbf{k}|^{2}}-\frac{1}{|\mathbf{l}|^{2}}}{\frac{1}{|\mathbf{j}|^{2}}-\frac{1}{|\mathbf{l}|^{2}}-\zeta}\leq\gamma_{1}\leq\frac{1}{\xi},

\displaystyle\frac{\zeta}{\frac{1}{|\mathbf{j}|^{2}}-\frac{1}{|\mathbf{l}|^{2}}}

\displaystyle\leq\kappa_{2}\leq\frac{\frac{1}{|\mathbf{j}|^{2}}-\frac{1}{|\mathbf{k}|^{2}}}{\frac{1}{|\mathbf{j}|^{2}}-\frac{1}{|\mathbf{l}|^{2}}},\qquad 1\leq\gamma_{2}\leq\frac{\frac{1}{|\mathbf{j}|^{2}}-\frac{1}{|\mathbf{k}|^{2}}}{\zeta}.

Given the expressions above for solutions of (
5.32
) as well as the bounds (
5.61
) and (
5.64
), we are almost ready to prove Theorem
5.47
. The last ingredient needed is a lower asymptotic bound on the quantity
K_{\rho}
in certain regimes in Assumption
5.42
. We recall that
K_{\rho}
is
1/4
of the period of the Jacobi elliptic functions
\text{sn}_{\rho}
and
\text{cn}_{\rho}
. We do this in the following lemma.

Lemma 5.65
.

Suppose that Assumption
5.42
is satisfied and, furthermore,

(5.66)

\displaystyle E=\frac{\mathcal{E}}{|\mathbf{k}|^{2}}+\epsilon(\delta)\qquad\text{ or }\qquad E=\frac{\mathcal{E}}{|\mathbf{k}|^{2}}-\epsilon(\delta)

where
\epsilon(\delta)>0
and
\epsilon(\delta)\rightarrow 0
as
\delta\rightarrow 0
. Then as
\delta\rightarrow 0

(5.67)

\displaystyle K_{\rho}=-\frac{\log\epsilon(\delta)}{2}+O(1).

Remark 5.68
.

If Assumption
5.42
is satisfied and
E
is uniformly bounded away from
\mathcal{E}/|\mathbf{k}|^{2}
as
\delta\rightarrow 0
, then the parameter
\rho=\gamma_{\text{min}}/\gamma_{\text{max}}<1
is then uniformly bounded away from
1
as
\delta\rightarrow 0
. Hence,
K_{\rho}
is order one, positive as
\delta\rightarrow 0
. Thus the analysis in the lemma above focuses on the case when
E\rightarrow\mathcal{E}/|\mathbf{k}|^{2}
as
\delta\rightarrow 0
.

Proof of Lemma
5.65
.

Let
E=\mathcal{E}/|\mathbf{k}|^{2}\pm\epsilon(\delta)
where
\epsilon(\delta)>0
satisfies
\epsilon(\delta)\rightarrow 0
as
\delta\rightarrow 0
. Without loss of generality, we study the case when
E=\mathcal{E}/|k|^{2}-\epsilon(\delta)
as the other case is nearly identical. Observe that as
\delta\rightarrow 0

\displaystyle\rho=\frac{\gamma_{\text{min}}}{\gamma_{\text{max}}}=\frac{\frac{1}{1+\frac{\epsilon(\delta)}{\frac{\mathcal{E}}{|\mathbf{j}|^{2}}-\frac{\mathcal{E}}{|\mathbf{k}|^{2}}}}}{\frac{1}{1-\frac{\epsilon(\delta)}{\frac{\mathcal{E}}{|\mathbf{k}|^{2}}-\frac{\mathcal{E}}{|\mathbf{l}|^{2}}}}}=1-\bigg(\frac{1}{\mathcal{E}(\frac{1}{|\mathbf{j}|^{2}}-\frac{1}{|\mathbf{k}|^{2}})}+\frac{1}{\mathcal{E}(\frac{1}{|\mathbf{k}|^{2}}-\frac{1}{|\mathbf{l}|^{2}})}\bigg)\epsilon(\delta)+O(\epsilon^{2}(\delta)).

Using the asymptotics in
[
21
]
, we thus obtain

\displaystyle K_{\rho}=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{db}{\sqrt{1-\rho b^{2}}\sqrt{1-b^{2}}}\,db=-\frac{\log\epsilon(\delta)}{2}+O(1)

as
\delta\rightarrow 0
.
∎

Given the previous result, we now turn to the proof of Theorem
5.47
.

Proof of Theorem
5.47
.

Set
C=\theta_{\mathbf{j}\mathbf{l}}\sqrt{\gamma_{\text{max}}\kappa_{1}\kappa_{2}}
and assume without loss of generality that
C>0
. Observe that for
\eta>0
,
\delta_{*}>0
small enough depending only on
|\mathbf{j}|,|\mathbf{k}|,|\mathbf{l}|,\zeta,\xi
, there exists
\eta^{\prime}>0
with
\eta^{\prime}<1-\eta^{\prime}
depending only on
|\mathbf{j}|,|\mathbf{k}|,|\mathbf{l}|,\zeta,\xi
such that
\delta\in(0,\delta_{*})
implies

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\{\tau\in B_{\eta,\delta}\}=\int_{B_{\eta,\delta}}\frac{1}{h}e^{-t/h}\,dt

\displaystyle\geq\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbf{1}\{\eta^{\prime}\leq|\text{sn}_{\rho}(Ct/\delta+\theta_{0})|\leq 1-\eta^{\prime}\}\frac{1}{h}e^{-t/h}\,dt

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta}{C}e^{\theta_{0}\frac{\delta}{Ch}}\int_{\theta_{0}}^{\infty}\mathbf{1}\{\eta^{\prime}\leq|\text{sn}_{\rho}(t)|\leq 1-\eta^{\prime}\}\frac{1}{h}e^{-\frac{\delta t}{Ch}}\,dt

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\delta}{C}e^{\theta_{0}\frac{\delta}{Ch}}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\int_{4jK_{\rho}}^{(4j+1)K_{\rho}}\mathbf{1}\{\eta^{\prime}\leq\text{sn}_{\rho}(t)\leq 1-\eta^{\prime}\}\frac{1}{h}e^{-\frac{\delta t}{Ch}}\,dt

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\delta}{C}e^{\theta_{0}\frac{\delta}{Ch}}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{\delta K_{\rho}}{Ch}(4j+1)}\int_{4jK_{\rho}}^{(4j+1)K_{\rho}}\mathbf{1}\{\eta^{\prime}\leq\text{sn}_{\rho}(t)\leq 1-\eta^{\prime}\}dt

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta}{C}\exp\Big(\frac{\theta_{0}\delta}{Ch}\Big)(T_{\rho}(1-\eta^{\prime})-T_{\rho}(\eta^{\prime}))\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{\delta K_{\rho}}{Ch}(4j+1)}.

Next, we note that

\displaystyle\frac{\delta}{C}\exp\Big(\frac{\theta_{0}\delta}{Ch}\Big)(T_{\rho}(1-\eta^{\prime})-T_{\rho}(\eta^{\prime}))\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{\delta K_{\rho}}{Ch}(4j+1)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\frac{\delta}{C}}{1-e^{-4\delta K_{\rho}/Ch}}\exp\Big(\frac{\theta_{0}\delta}{Ch}-\frac{5\delta K_{\rho}}{Ch}\Big)[T_{\rho}(1-\eta^{\prime})-T_{\rho}(\eta^{\prime})].

Using Lemma
5.65
and the definition of
T_{\rho}(s)
we see that there exists
c_{1}=c_{1}(h)>0
independent of
\delta\in(0,\delta_{*})
for which

(5.69)

\displaystyle\exp\Big(\frac{\theta_{0}\delta}{Ch}-\frac{t\delta K_{\rho}}{Ch}\Big)[T_{\rho}(1-\eta^{\prime})-T_{\rho}(\eta^{\prime})]\geq c_{1}.

Also, using Lemma
5.65
, we find that as
\delta\rightarrow 0
,

\displaystyle 1-e^{-4\delta K_{\rho}/Ch}=\frac{4\delta K_{\rho}}{Ch}+O(\delta^{2}K_{\rho}^{2}).

Hence, there exists
\delta_{*}>0
small enough depending only on
|\mathbf{j}|,|\mathbf{k}|,|\mathbf{l}|,\zeta,\xi,h
and a constant
c_{2}=c_{2}(h)>0
independent of
\delta\in(0,\delta_{*})
for which

(5.70)

\displaystyle\frac{\frac{\delta}{C}}{1-e^{-4\delta K_{\rho}/Ch}}\geq\frac{c_{2}}{|\log\delta|}\,\,\,\text{ for all }\delta\in(0,\delta_{*}).

Setting
c=c_{1}c_{2}
and combining (
5.69
) with (
5.70
) completes the proof.
∎

5.5.
Proof of Theorem
5.29

Given the analysis of the transfer of energy in the triples (
5.105
) of the previous section, we are now ready to prove the main result of this section, Theorem
5.29
. Due to its length, the proof will be split into two parts depending on what assumption on the forced and damped modes, either (DF1) or (DF2), is satisfied.

Proof of Theorem
5.29

Most of the derivation is done in

Recall that in vorticity formulation, the damped and forced two-dimensional Euler equation on
