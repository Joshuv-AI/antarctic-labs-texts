Matroid Horn Functions
Kristóf Bérczi, Endre Boros, Kazuhisa Makino

Abstract
Hypergraph Horn functions were introduced as a subclass of Horn functions that can be represented by a collection of circular implication rules. These functions possess distinguished structural and computational properties. In particular, their characterizations in terms of implicate-duality and the closure operator provide extensions of matroid duality and the Mac Lane – Steinitz exchange property of matroid closure, respectively. In the present paper, we introduce a subclass of hypergraph Horn functions that we call matroid Horn functions. We provide multiple characterizations of matroid Horn functions in terms of their canonical and complete CNF representations. We also study the Boolean minimization problem for this class, where the goal is to find a minimum size representation of a matroid Horn function given by a CNF representation. While there are various ways to measure the size of a CNF, we focus on the number of circuits and circuit clauses . We determine the size of an optimal representation for binary matroids, and give lower and upper bounds in the uniform case. For uniform matroids, we show a strong connection between our problem and Turán systems that might be of independent combinatorial interest. Keywords: Boolean minimization, Horn functions, Matroids, Turán systems



1
Introduction

Hypergraph Horn functions were introduced in
[
2
]
as a special subclass of Horn functions that is rich in mathematical structure and has advantageous algorithmic properties. The family of hypergraph Horn functions turned out to be highly structured yet general enough to provide insight into the structure of Horn functions, e.g., they generalize equivalence relations, and more generally matroids. The authors introduced the notion of implicate-duality or
i
-duality, and showed that every Boolean function has a unique
i
-dual, which is always hypergraph Horn. In particular,
i
-duality generalizes matroid duality in the sense that if a hypergraph Horn function corresponds to a matroid, then its
i
-dual corresponds to the dual matroid.

In this paper, we consider hypergraph Horn functions for which the underlying hypergraph corresponds to the circuits of a matroid, and call them
matroid Horn
functions. These functions possess many interesting properties. Some are functional properties that are related only to the structure of true and false sets of the function, while others are related to the structure of possible representations of the function. We provide characterizations of matroid Horn functions in terms of prime implicates, the closure operator and the rank function, and the
i
-dual. Meanwhile, we also explain the correspondence between the basic notions of matroid theory and matroid Horn logic.

One of the most intriguing questions related to Boolean functions is the so called
Boolean minimization problem
(BM): given a representation of a Boolean function in a conjunctive normal form (CNF), find an equivalent CNF representation of minimum size with respect to a certain objective. There are different ways to measure the size of a CNF, standard examples being the number of clauses and the total number of literals. Finding a short representation is motivated by many applications, for example, such a representation can be used to reduce the size of the knowledge base in a propositional expert system, which in turn improves the performance of the system. An unsatisfiable formula can be easily recognized from its shortest CNF representation for both above measures of the output size. Therefore BM contains the CNF satisfiability problem (SAT) as a special case, implying that it is NP-hard. However, while SAT is NP-complete (i.e.
\Sigma^{p}_{1}
-complete
[
3
]
), BM is
\Sigma^{p}_{2}
-complete
[
15
,
16
]
, suggesting that BM is probably more difficult than SAT. We consider the problem of finding short representations of matroid Horn functions. We also discuss minimum representations of matroids in terms of circuit generation. We show that for binary matroids, the set of chordless cycles provides the unique optimal representation for all objectives considered. For uniform matroids, we prove that our problem is closely related to the minimum size of Turán systems and provide bounds as a function of Turán numbers; this result might be of independent combinatorial interest.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Basic definitions and results on hypergraphs, Horn functions and matroids are presented in Section
2
as in
[
2
]
. In Section
3
, we introduce the class of matroid Horn functions and provide several equivalent characterizations. The Boolean minimization problem for matroid Horn functions is discussed in Section
4
. Finally, we close the paper by some open problems in Section
5
.

Hypergraph Horn functions.

We denote by

Truth assignments (i.e., Boolean vectors)

we denote by

and
\Phi(Z)
instead of

and
\Phi(\chi_{Z})
, to denote the evaluation of

and
\Phi

\chi_{Z}
, respectively. We say that a set

is a

of

if

, and a
false set
otherwise. We denote by
\mathcal{T}(f)
and
\mathcal{F}(f)
the
families of true sets
and

of

, respectively. Every Boolean expression defines/represents a unique Boolean function. If
A

if

for all

, and

if

, where

is called a

.

A clause is called

if it contains exactly one positive literal. It is easy to see that definite Horn clauses represent simple implications. Namely, for a proper subset
B\subsetneq V
and

B\to v
is equivalent to the definite Horn clause
C=v\vee\left(\bigvee_{u\in B}\overline{u}\right)
: the true sets of both expressions are exactly the sets

such that either

or
T\supseteq B\cup\{v\}
. We call
B
the
body
of the Horn clause. A CNF is called
definite Horn
if it consists of definite Horn clauses, and a Boolean function is called
definite Horn
if it can be represented by a definite Horn CNF. The following characterization of definite Horn functions is well-known.

Lemma 1

(see e.g.,
[
10
,
4
]
)
.

A Boolean function

is definite Horn if and only if the family
\mathcal{T}(f)
of true sets of

is closed under intersection and contains

.

\Box

Lemma
1
implies that for any set
Z\subseteq V
there exists a unique minimal true set containing

, the so-called

of

denoted by
\mathbb{T}_{h}(Z)
. In fact such a closure can be computed efficiently from any definite Horn CNF representation

by the so-called

(see e.g.,

denote the set of all variables

in
\Phi

and
v\in V\setminus Z
, and define
\mathbb{T}^{1}_{\Phi}(Z)=Z\cup A
. For

we define
\mathbb{T}^{i}_{\Phi}(Z)=\mathbb{T}^{1}_{\Phi}(\mathbb{T}^{i-1}_{\Phi}(Z))
. Since
Z\subseteq\mathbb{T}^{1}_{\Phi}(Z)\subseteq\mathbb{T}^{2}_{\Phi}(Z)\subseteq\dots\subseteq V
,
\mathbb{T}^{i+1}_{\Phi}(Z)=\mathbb{T}^{i}_{\Phi}(Z)
holds for some integer

. Let

be the smallest such index

, and

. Thus we can define

, and we say that

, it can be shown (see e.g.,

]
) that the resulting set

of

, but only on the underlying function

, that is, we can write

The conjunction of two hypergraph Horn functions

Matroids.

We give a brief introduction into matroid theory, and refer the reader to
[
11
]
for further details. A
matroid

\mathbb{M}=(E,\mathcal{C})
is defined by its
ground set

E
and its
family of circuits

\mathcal{C}\subseteq 2^{E}
that satisfies the following
circuit axioms
.

(C1)

\emptyset\notin\mathcal{C}
.

(C2)

If
C_{1},C_{2}\in\mathcal{C}
, then
C_{1}\not\subset C_{2}
.

(C3)

If
C_{1},C_{2}\in\mathcal{C}
are distinct and
u\in C_{1}\cap C_{2}
, then there exists
C_{3}\in\mathcal{C}
such that
C_{3}\subseteq(C_{1}\cup C_{2})-u
.

For a family
\mathcal{C}\subseteq 2^{V}
that satisfies satisfies the circuit axioms, i.e. it is the family of circuits of some matroid
\mathbb{M}
, let
\mathcal{I}
denote the family of independent sets of
\mathcal{C}
. Then it is known that it satisfies so-called
independence axioms
: (I1)
\emptyset\in\mathcal{I}
, (I2)
X\subseteq Y\in\mathcal{I}\Rightarrow X\in\mathcal{I}
, and (I3)
X,Y\in\mathcal{I},\ |X|<|Y|\Rightarrow\exists e\in Y-X\ \text{s.t.}\ X+e\in\mathcal{I}
. The
rank
of a set
X\subseteq V
is the maximum size of an independent subset of
X
and is denoted by
{\it rank}_{\mathbb{M}}(X)
. The maximal independent sets of
M
are called
bases
. A set
X\subseteq V
is called
closed
(or a
flat
or a
subspace
) if
{\it rank}_{\mathbb{M}}(X+v)>{\it rank}_{\mathbb{M}}(X)
for every
v\in V\setminus X
. The
closure
(or
span
) of a set
X\subseteq V
is defined as
\cl_{\mathbb{M}}(X)=\{v\in V\mid{\it rank}_{\mathbb{M}}(X+v)={\it rank}_{\mathbb{M}}(X)\}
. It is known that
X
is closed if and only if
\cl_{\mathbb{M}}(X)=X
. A
hyperplane
is a closed set of rank
{\it rank}_{\mathbb{M}}(V)-1
.

Like circuit and independence axioms, each concept has axioms for matroids. For example, the closure operator of a matroid
\mathbb{M}
satisfies the following
closure axioms
.

(CL1)

X\subseteq\cl_{\mathbb{M}}(X)
for all
X\subseteq V
.

(CL2)

\cl_{\mathbb{M}}(X)=\cl_{\mathbb{M}}(\cl_{\mathbb{M}}(X))
for all
X\subseteq V
.

(CL3)

\cl_{\mathbb{M}}(X)\subseteq\cl_{\mathbb{M}}(Y)
for all
X,Y\subseteq V
with
X\subseteq Y
.

(CL4)

v\in\cl_{\mathbb{M}}(X+u)\setminus\cl_{\mathbb{M}}(X)
implies that
u\in\cl_{\mathbb{M}}(X+v)\setminus\cl_{\mathbb{M}}(X)
for all
u,v\in V
and all
X\subseteq V
.

It is known that general closure operators are defined as the ones that satisfy the axioms
(CL1)
,
(CL2)
and
(CL3)
, and are equivalent to operators
\mathbb{T}_{h}
for definite Horn functions
f
. Axiom
(CL4)
is called
Mac Lane–Steinitz exchange property

[
17
]
. As we have seen in Theorem
4
(a)
, it is generalized to closure operators of hypergraph Horn functions.

3
Matroid Horn Functions

We consider hypergraph Horn functions associated to families of circuits of a matroid, and study their properties and relations to matroid theory. We call a definite Horn function
matroidal
or
matroid Horn
if
h=\Phi_{\mathcal{C}}
for the family of circuits
\mathcal{C}
of a matroid
\mathbb{M}=(V,\mathcal{C})
.

For a matroid Horn function
h
, let us call its CNF representation
h=\Phi_{\mathcal{C}}

canonical
if the hypergraph
\mathcal{C}
satisfies the circuit axioms
(C1)
–
(C3)
, i.e.,
\mathcal{C}
is the family of circuits of some matroid. Our first main result provides characterizations of matroid Horn functions in terms of their canonical representations.

Theorem 5
.

Let
\mathcal{C}\subseteq 2^{V}
be a nontrivial Sperner hypergraph and let
h
be the hypergraph Horn function represented by
\Phi_{\mathcal{C}}
. Then the following are equivalent.

(i)

\mathcal{C}
satisfies the circuit axiom
(C3)
.

(ii)

\mathcal{K}(h)=\mathcal{C}^{dc}
.

(iii)

\mathcal{M}(h)=\mathcal{C}^{dcdc}
.

(iv)

\mathcal{T}(h)=\left(\mathcal{C}^{dcdc}\right)^{\cap}
.

Let us remark that claim
(ii)
in the above theorem means that the minimal keys of a matroid Horn function are exactly the bases of the corresponding matroid by (
1
). Furthermore, claims
(iii)
and
(iv)
imply that the set of characteristic models of a matroid Horn function are exactly its maximal nontrivial true sets. Note that while
\mathcal{C}^{dcdc}
can be the characteristic set of
\Phi_{\mathcal{C}}
only for matroid Horn functions,
\mathcal{M}(h)
may be the characteristic set of a non-matroidal hypergraph Horn function, too, see Example
12
.

Let us recall that every Boolean function
f
has a unique set of prime implicates, and the unique CNF representation that contains all prime implicates of
f
is called the
complete CNF
of
f
, see e.g.
[
4
]
. Our next main result provides characterizations of matroid Horn functions in terms of their complete CNF.

Theorem 6
.

For a definite Horn function
h
, the following are equivalent.

(i)

The function
h
is matroid Horn.

(ii)

The complete CNF of
h
is circular.

(iii)

The implicate-dual function
h^{i}
is matroid Horn.

(iv)

The complete CNF of
h^{i}
is circular.

For the proof of the theorems, we need a number of technical lemmas providing additional characterizations of matroid Horn functions that might be of interest on their own. In fact, we will show that if
h=\Phi_{\mathcal{C}}
for the family of circuits
\mathcal{C}

\mathcal{K}(\Phi_{\mathcal{H}})=\left\{K\subseteq V\bigm||K\cap X|=3~\text{and}~K\cap Y\in F\right\}\cup\left\{K\subseteq V\bigm||K\cap Y|=3~\text{and}~K\cap X\in E\right\}.

From this, one can derive that

\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{K}(\Phi_{\mathcal{H}})^{cd}=\{X,Y\}\cup\left\{\{a,b\},\{c,d\}\right\}\times\left\{\{e,f\},\{g,h\}\right\}

and that it satisfies the circuit axioms
(C1)
–
(C3)
. Thus, by Table

, we have
\mathcal{K}(\Phi_{\mathcal{C}})=\mathcal{C}^{dc}=\left(\mathcal{K}(\Phi_{\mathcal{H}})^{cd}\right)^{dc}=\mathcal{K}(\Phi_{\mathcal{H}})
, since both operators “

” and “

\{1,2,3,5\}{\rightarrow}6
is a prime implicate of

4
Minimum representations

Given a system
\mathcal{F}\subseteq 2^{V}
and a subsystem
\mathcal{G}\subseteq\mathcal{F}
, let
\langle\mathcal{G}\rangle^{1}_{\mathcal{F}}
denote the family of sets in
\mathcal{F}
that
\mathcal{G}

generates in a single step
, that is,

\langle\mathcal{G}\rangle^{1}_{\mathcal{F}}=\mathcal{G}\cup\{X\in\mathcal{F}\mid X=(X_{1}\cup X_{2})-v\ \text{for distinct}\ X_{1},X_{2}\in\mathcal{G},\ v\in X_{1}\cap X_{2}\}.

We denote the repeated application of operator
\langle\ \cdot\ \rangle^{1}_{\mathcal{F}}
for
k\geq 2
times by
\langle\ \cdot\ \rangle^{k}_{\mathcal{F}}
, that is,

\langle\mathcal{G}\rangle^{k}_{\mathcal{F}}=\langle\langle\mathcal{G}\rangle^{k-1}_{\mathcal{F}}\rangle^{1}_{\mathcal{F}}.

Since
V
is finite, for some
k
we have
\langle\mathcal{G}\rangle^{k}_{\mathcal{F}}=\langle\mathcal{G}\rangle^{k+1}_{\mathcal{F}}
. We denote this final system by
\langle\mathcal{G}\rangle_{\mathcal{F}}
and call
\mathcal{G}
a
generator
of
\mathcal{F}
if
\langle\mathcal{G}\rangle_{\mathcal{F}}=\mathcal{F}
.

Let
\mathbb{M}=(V,\mathcal{C})
be a matroid and
h_{\mathbb{M}}
be the corresponding matroid Horn function. For a circuit
C\in\mathcal{C}
and
v\in C
, the clause
(C-v)\rightarrow v
is called a
circuit clause
. Our goal is to find compact representations of
h_{\mathbb{M}}
, and therefore of
\mathbb{M}
as well. To this end, we consider three different objectives:

(G) circuit generator:

|\mathbb{M}|_{G}=
minimum cardinality of a generator of
\mathcal{C}
,

(C) number of circuits:

|\mathbb{M}|_{C}=
minimum cardinality of a subsystem
\mathcal{D}\subseteq\mathcal{C}
s.t.
h_{\mathbb{M}}=\Phi_{\mathcal{D}}
,

(K) number of circuit clauses:

|\mathbb{M}|_{K}=
minimum number of circuit clauses needed to represent
h_{\mathbb{M}}
.

Objective (G) characterizes the complexity of the circuit family of
\mathbb{M}
. The number of circuits (C) denotes the minimum size of a subsystem of
\mathcal{C}
for which the corresponding hypergraph Horn CNF provides a representation of
h_{\mathbb{M}}
. The number of clauses is an important parameter for SAT solvers when a Horn formula encodes a constraint which is part of a larger problem; objective (K) captures an analogous notion when the set of usable clauses is restricted to circuit clauses.

4.1
Binary matroids

A matroid
\mathbb{M}=(V,\mathcal{C})
is called
binary
if it can be represented over the finite field GF(2), or in other words, its elements corresponds to the columns of a
0,1
-matrix such that a set
X\subseteq V
is independent in
\mathbb{M}
if and only if the corresponding columns are linearly independent over GF(2). By abuse of notation, we denote the binary matrix representing the matroid also by
\mathbb{M}
, and for a subset
X\subseteq V
, the sum of the corresponding columns of
\mathbb{M}
is written as
\sum_{u\in X}u
. The class of binary matroids contains several important subclasses, such as regular or graphic matroids.

We will need the following easy observations on binary matroids.

Lemma 14
.

Let
\mathbb{M}=(V,\mathcal{C})
be a binary matroid and
X\subseteq V
be an independent set. Then there is at most one
v\in V
for which
X+v
forms a circuit of
\mathbb{M}
.

Proof.

Let
v=\sum_{u\in X}u
. We claim that if
v
is contained in
V
, then it is the unique element for which
X+v
forms a circuit. The set
X+v
is clearly dependent, but any proper subset
X^{\prime}\subsetneq X+v
is independent. Indeed, if
X^{\prime}\subsetneq X+v
is dependent, then necessarily
v\in X^{\prime}
. But then
(X+v)\setminus X^{\prime}\subseteq X
is a non-empty dependent subset of
X
, a contradiction.
∎

Lemma 15
.

Let
\mathbb{M}=(V,\mathcal{C})
be a binary matroid without parallel elements. If
C_{1},C_{2}\in\mathcal{C}
are such that
|C_{1}\setminus C_{2}|=1
, then
|C_{1}|<|C_{2}|
.

Proof.

As
C_{2}\nsubseteq C_{1}
and
|C_{1}\setminus C_{2}|=1
, we have
|C_{1}|\leq|C_{2}|
. Suppose indirectly that
|C_{1}|=|C_{2}|
, implying that
|C_{2}\setminus C_{1}|=1
. Let
C_{1}\setminus C_{2}=\{v\}
and
C_{2}\setminus C_{1}=\{w\}
. The intersection
X=C_{1}\cap C_{2}
is an independent set of
\mathbb{M}
for which both
X+v
and
X+w
are circuits. By Lemma
14
,
v=w=\sum_{u\in X}u
, contradicting the assumption that
\mathbb{M}
does not contain parallel elements.
∎

Motivated by the example of graphic matroids, we call a circuit
C\in\mathcal{C}
of a simple binary matroid
chordless
if there exists no
C^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}
such that
|C^{\prime}\setminus C|=1
and
|C^{\prime}|<|C|
. Our first result characterizes the minimum size of a generator of
\mathcal{C}
, i.e. objective (G), for simple binary matroids.

Theorem 16
.

Let
\mathbb{M}=(V,\mathcal{C})
be a simple binary matroid. Then the set of chordless cycles is the unique minimum generator of
\mathcal{C}
.

Proof.

Let
C_{1},C_{2}\in C
and
v\in C_{1}\cap C_{2}
. If
C=(C_{1}\cup C_{2})-v
is a circuit of
\mathbb{M}
, then
|C_{1}\setminus C|=|\{v\}|=1
and so
|C_{1}|<|C|
by Lemma
15
. That is, every circuit obtained by the generation step is non-chordless, hence all the chordless circuits must be contained in any generator of
\mathbb{M}
.

Let us denote by
\mathcal{D}
the set of chordless cycles of
\mathbb{M}
, and take an arbitrary circuit
C\in\mathcal{C}
. We prove by induction on the size of
|C|
that it can be generated from
\mathcal{D}
, that is,
C\in\langle\mathcal{D}\rangle_{\mathcal{C}}
. This clearly holds for chordless circuits. Assume now that
C\in\mathcal{C}
is not chordless. By definition, there exists
C_{1}\in\mathcal{C}
such that
|C_{1}\setminus C|=1
and
|C_{1}|<|C|
. Let
C_{1}\setminus C=\{v\}
and take an arbitrary element
w\in C\cap C_{1}
. Notice that such an element exists as
|C_{1}|>1
by the simplicity of
\mathbb{M}
. By the third circuit axiom
(C3)
, there exists a circuit
C_{2}\subseteq(C\cup C_{1})-w
. As
C_{2}\nsubseteq C
, we have
v\in C_{2}
and so
|C_{2}\setminus C|=1
. Therefore
|C_{2}|<|C|
by Lemma
15
. By the induction hypothesis, both
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
can be generated from
\mathcal{D}
, implying that
C
can be generated as well.
∎

It turns out that the set of chordless circuits provides an optimal representation in terms of
|\cdot|_{C}
and
|\cdot|_{K}
as well. The proof is analogous to that of Theorem
16
.

Theorem 17
.

Let
\mathbb{M}=(V,\mathcal{C})
be a simple binary matroid. Then the set of chordless cycles is the unique minimum subsystem of
\mathcal{C}
with respect to
|\mathbb{M}|_{C}
. Furthermore,
\{(C-v)\rightarrow v\mid C\in\mathcal{C}\ \text{is chordless},\ v\in C\}
is the unique minimum set of circuit clauses with respect to
|\mathbb{M}|_{K}
.

Proof.

Let us denote by
\mathcal{D}
the set of chordless cycles of
\mathbb{M}
. Take a chordless circuit
C\in\mathcal{D}
, and consider an arbitrary
u\in C
. Then
(C-u)\rightarrow u
is an implicate of
h_{\mathbb{M}}
, hence for any representation of
h_{\mathbb{M}}
there exists a circuit
C^{\prime}
and
v\in C^{\prime}
such that
C^{\prime}-v\subseteq C-u
. If
v\in C
then necessarily
C=C^{\prime}
by circuit axiom
(C2)
. Otherwise
|C^{\prime}\setminus C|=1
, and so
|C^{\prime}|<|C|
by Lemma
15
, contradicting
C
being chordless. This implies that every representation of
h_{\mathbb{M}}
contains the circuit clause
(C-u)\rightarrow u
.

We claim that
h_{\mathbb{M}}=\Phi_{\mathcal{D}}
. To see this, take an arbitrary circuit
C\in\mathcal{C}
and
u\in C
. We prove by induction on the size of
|C|
that
(C-u)\rightarrow u
is an implicate of
\Phi_{\mathcal{D}}
. This clearly holds for chordless circuits. Assume now that
C\in\mathcal{C}
is not chordless. By definition, there exists
C_{1}\in\mathcal{C}
such that
|C_{1}\setminus C|=1
and
|C_{1}|<|C|
. Let
C_{1}\setminus C=\{v\}
and take an arbitrary element
w\in C\cap C_{1}
. Notice that such an element exists as
|C_{1}|>1
by the simplicity of
\mathbb{M}
. By the third circuit axiom
(C3)
, there exists a circuit
C_{2}\subseteq(C\cup C_{1})-w
. As
C_{2}\nsubseteq C
, we have
v\in C_{2}
and so
|C_{2}\setminus C|=1
. Therefore
|C_{2}|<|C|
by Lemma
15
. By the induction hypothesis,
(C_{1}-v)\rightarrow v
and
(C_{2}-u)\rightarrow u
are both implicates of
\Phi_{\mathcal{D}}
, implying that
(C-u)\rightarrow u
is also an implicate.
∎

Remark 18
.

The gap between the number of circuits and the number of chordless circuits can be exponentially large. For example, consider a complete bipartite graph
G=(S,T;E)
with
|S|=|T|=n
, and let
\mathbb{M}=(E,\mathcal{C})
be the graphic matroid of
G
. Then the number of cycles in
G
is clearly exponential in
n
, while the number of chordless cycles is
{n\choose 2}^{2}
.

4.2
Uniform matroids

Given a positive integer
r
, the
uniform matroid

\mathbb{M}=(V,\mathcal{C})
of rank
r
is defined by
\mathcal{C}=\{C\subseteq V\mid|C|=r+1\}
. Although uniform matroids form one of the simplest matroid classes, determining the exact value of
|\mathbb{M}|_{C}
seems to be a difficult problem. For
|\mathbb{M}|_{G}
and
|\mathbb{M}|_{K}
we give closed formulas for the case of uniform matroids. It is worth mentioning that
h_{\mathbb{M}}
in this case is a key Horn function
[
1
]
for which
F_{h_{\mathbb{M}}}(X)=X
if
|X|\leq r-2
and
F_{h_{\mathbb{M}}}(X)=V
otherwise.

Theorem 19
.

Let
\mathbb{M}=(V,\mathcal{C})
be a rank-
r
uniform matroid. Then
|\mathbb{M}|_{G}=n-r
.

Proof.

Let
v_{1},\dots,v_{n}
denote the elements of the ground set
V
. Consider the system
\mathcal{D}=\{\{v_{i},\dots,v_{i+r}\}\mid i\in[n-r]\}
. We claim that
\mathcal{D}
is a generator of
\mathcal{C}
. Take an arbitrary
C\in\mathcal{C}
, and assume that
C=\bigcup_{j=1}^{q}\{v_{i_{j}},\dots,v_{i_{j}+\delta_{j}}\}
, where
i_{j}+\delta_{j}<i_{j+1}
for
j\in[q-1]
. We prove by induction on
\delta_{1}
, and with respect to this, on
q
that
C
can be generated from
\mathcal{D}
. If
\delta_{1}=0
then
q=1
and
C\in\mathcal{D}
, hence assume that
\delta_{1}>0
. Let
C_{1}=C-v_{i_{q}+\delta_{q}}+v_{i_{1}+\delta_{1}+1}
and
C_{2}=C-v_{1}+v_{i_{1}+\delta_{1}+1}
. It is not difficult to see that either
\delta_{1}
or
q
decreases in both cases, hence the circuits
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
can be generated starting from
\mathcal{D}
. Notice that
C=(C_{1}\cup C_{2})-v_{i_{1}+\delta_{1}+1}
, therefore
C
can be generated as well.

To see that
\mathcal{D}
is an optimal solution, consider any generator
\mathcal{G}
. Let us call a pair
C_{1},C_{2}\in\mathcal{G}
connected if
|C_{1}\setminus C_{2}|=|C_{2}\setminus C_{1}|=1
. Accordingly, one can define the connected components
\mathcal{G}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{G}_{s}
of
\mathcal{G}
. We denote the number of circuits in
\mathcal{G}_{i}
by
q_{i}
. By the definition of connectivity, the union of the circuits in
\mathcal{G}_{i}
contains at most
r+q_{i}
elements of
V
. Moreover,
|\bigcup_{C\in\mathcal{G}_{i}}C\cap(\bigcup_{C\in\mathcal{G}\setminus\mathcal{G}_{i}}C)|\geq r
for
i\in[p]
. Indeed, if this does not hold then the generation step cannot be applied to
C_{1}\in\langle\mathcal{G}_{i}\rangle_{\mathcal{C}}
and
C_{2}\in\langle\mathcal{G}\setminus\mathcal{G}_{i}\rangle_{\mathcal{C}}
as
|C_{1}\cap C_{2}|\leq r-1
. This implies that no circuit
C
with
|C\cap\bigcup_{C\in\mathcal{G}\setminus\mathcal{G}_{i}}|=r
can be generated, contradicting
\mathcal{G}
being a generator. As the circuits in
\mathcal{G}
must cover all the
n
elements of
V
, we get

\displaystyle n{}

\displaystyle{}\leq\left|\bigcup_{C\in\mathcal{G}_{1}}C\right|+\sum_{i=2}^{s}\left|\left(\bigcup_{C\in\mathcal{G}_{i}}C\right)\setminus\left(\bigcup_{C\in\mathcal{G}\setminus\mathcal{G}_{i}}C\right)\right|

\displaystyle{}\leq(r+q_{1})+\sum_{i=2}^{s}[r+q_{i}-r]

\displaystyle{}=r+\sum_{i=1}^{s}q_{i}.

This implies
|\mathcal{G}|=\sum_{i=1}^{s}|\mathcal{G}_{i}|=\sum_{i=1}^{s}q_{i}\geq n-r
, concluding the proof of the theorem.
∎

Let us now turn to the case of
|\cdot|_{C}
. We give two results, the first being based on a counting argument, while the second showing a connection to Turán systems.

Theorem 20
.

Let
\mathbb{M}=(V,\mathcal{C})
be a rank-
r
uniform matroid on
n\geq r+2
elements. Then
{n\choose r}/(r+\frac{1}{2})\leq|\mathbb{M}|_{C}\leq{n\choose r}
.

Proof.

We prove the lower bound by a token counting argument. Let us give one token to every
r
-element subset of
V
, and consider an arbitrary subsystem
\mathcal{D}
of
\mathcal{C}
such that
\Phi_{\mathcal{D}}=h_{\mathbb{M}}
. We redistribute the tokens as follows: every
r
-element set shares its token evenly among the sets in
\mathcal{D}
containing it. Then every set in
\mathcal{D}
receives at most
r+\frac{1}{2}
tokens in total. Indeed, for every circuit
C_{1}\in\mathcal{D}
there must be another circuit
C_{2}\in\mathcal{D}
with
|C_{1}\cap C_{2}|=r
, as otherwise
F_{\Phi_{\mathcal{D}}}(C)=C\neq V
by
r<n-1
, a contradiction. This proves the lower bound.

The upper bound follows from an easy construction: pick an arbitrary element
v\in V
and consider the system
\mathcal{D}=\{X+v\mid X\subseteq V-v,|X|=r\}
. It is not difficult to check that
F_{\Phi_{\mathcal{D}}}(X)=X
if
|X|\leq r-1
and
F_{\Phi_{\mathcal{D}}}(X)=V
otherwise, hence
\Phi_{\mathcal{D}}=h_{\mathbb{M}}
as required.
∎

Interestingly, for uniform matroids, the objective
|\cdot|_{C}
is closely related to Turán and covering numbers. A
Turán
(n,t,k)
-system
is an
k
-uniform hypergraph of
n
vertices such that every
t
-element subset of vertices contains at least one hyperedge. The
Turán number

t(n,t,k)
asks for the minimum size of such a hypergraph. Determining the exact value of
t(n,t,k)
is a problem posed by Turán
[
14
]
. The simplest case
t=3
,
k=2
was answered by Mantel’s theorem
[
9
]
from 1907, showing that the largest triangle-free graph on
n
vertices is a complete bipartite graph with color classes having sizes
\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor
and
\lceil\frac{n}{2}\rceil
. The complementary family of a Turán
(n,t,k)
-system is called a
covering
(n,n-k,n-t)
-system
. In other words, a covering
(n,q,r)
-system is a
q
-uniform hypergraph such that every
r
element subset is contained in at least one of the hyperedges. The minimum size of such a system is denoted by
c(n,q,r)
, which by definition is the same as
t(n,n-r,n-q)
.

Let
\ell(n,q,r)=\left\lceil\frac{n}{q}\left\lceil\frac{n-1}{q-1}\left\lceil\dots\left\lceil\frac{n-r+1}{q-r}\right\rceil\dots\right\rceil\right\rceil\right\rceil
. In
[
12
]
, Schönheim verified that

c(n,q,r)\geq\ell(n,q,r)
for all
n\geq q\geq r\geq 1
. The special case when
q=3
and
r=2
was studied by Fort Jr. and Hedlund
[
5
]
, who showed that Schönheim’s bound is tight, implying

c(n,3,2)=\begin{cases}n^{2}/6&\text{if $n\equiv 0$}\\
(n^{2}-n)/6&\text{if $n\equiv 0$}\\
(n^{2}+2)/6&\text{if $n\equiv 2$ or $4$}\\
(n^{2}-n+4)/6&\text{if $n\equiv 5$}\end{cases}\ (\bmod\ 6).\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:fh}

For further details on covering and Turán systems, we refer the interested reader to
[
6
]
and
[

]
, respectively.

We consider two further set families with different structural properties. Given a finite set
V
with
|V|=n
, let
\mathcal{H}\subseteq 2^{V}
be an
(r+1)
-uniform hypergraph. We call
\mathcal{H}
a

(n,r+1,r)
-system
if every subset
X\subseteq V
of size

is contained in exactly one hyperedge, and call it a

(n,r+1,r)
-system
if for every subset of

of size at least

there exists a hyperedge

with
|H\setminus X|=1
. We denote by
s(n,r+1,r)
and

, the minimum cardinality of such systems, respectively. The definitions imply that every implication system is a covering system, which in turn is a Steiner system with respect to the same parameters
(n,r+1,r)
. Therefore, we have

s(n,r+1,r)\leq c(n,r+1,r)\leq b(n,r+1,r).

The interpretation of these systems using Horn-logic is as follows. An
(r+1)
-uniform hypergraph
\mathcal{H}\subseteq 2^{V}
is a covering
(n,r+1,r)
-system if
\mathbb{T}_{\Phi_{\mathcal{H}}}(X)\neq X

, and it is an implication

\mathbb{T}_{\Phi_{\mathcal{H}}}(X)=V

. In particular, for a rank-

\mathbb{M}=(V,\mathcal{C})

\mathcal{D}

First we give lower and upper bounds on

\mathbb{M}=(V,\mathcal{C})

The lower bound follows from the observations that

2
uniform matroid on
n
elements. The combination of Theorem
21
and (
4
) gives
|\mathbb{M}|_{C}=\kappa\cdot n^{2}+O(n)
for some
1/6\leq\kappa\leq 1/3
, where the lower bound on
\kappa
can be improved to
1/5
by Theorem
20
. Next we prove that the right order of magnitude of
|\mathbb{M}|_{C}
coincides with this lower bound.

Theorem 22
.

Let
\mathbb{M}=(V,\mathcal{C})
be a rank-
2
uniform matroid on
n\geq 46
elements. Then

|\mathbb{M}|_{C}=\frac{n^{2}}{5}+O(n).

Proof.

Assume that
p
and
b
are positive integers such that
n=a\cdot p+b
,
p\geq 3
and
b\geq 2
. We also assume that
b
is a constant; its value will be specified later. Given such numbers, we take an arbitrary partition into
p+1
sets
V=A_{1}\cup\dots\cup A_{p}\cup B
where
|A_{i}|=5
for
i\in[p]
and
|B|=b
. We call the
A_{i}
s
groups
and the set
B
as
residual
. Furthermore, we denote the elements of the
i
th group by
A_{i}=\{a^{i}_{0},\dots,a^{i}_{4}\}
for
i\in[p]
. Throughout the proof, we work with the indices of the elements modulo
5
.

The high-level idea of the construction is as follows. As a first step, we show that there exists a hypergraph
\mathcal{C}_{1}
of size
O(n)
for which
\mathbb{T}_{\Phi_{\mathcal{C}_{1}}}(\{u,v\})=V
whenever
\{u,v\}\subseteq A_{i}\cup B
for some
i\in[p]
, that is, if
u
and
v
are not in distinct groups. Then it remains to consider pairs that have one-one element in two distinct groups. Hence in a second step, we consider a Steiner
(p,3,2)
-system on the set of groups, and define a hypergraph
\mathcal{C}_{2}
of size
n^{2}/5+O(n)
which ensures that
|\mathbb{T}_{\Phi_{\mathcal{C}_{2}}}(\{u,v\})\cap A_{i}|\geq 2
for some group
i
, whenever
u
and
v
are in distinct groups. For this step, we have to choose the values of
p
and
b
carefully in order to ensure the existence of a Steiner
(p,3,2)
-system and keep
b
to be a constant. At the end, the hypergraph
\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{C}_{1}\cup\mathcal{C}_{2}
has total size
n^{2}/5+O(n)
and form an implication
(n,3,2)
-system, proving the claim.

For the first step, define

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{1}=

\displaystyle\left\{\{u,v,w\}\mid u,v\in A_{i}\ \text{for some $i\in[p]$},\ w\in B\right\}

\displaystyle\cup

\displaystyle\left\{\{u,v,w\}\mid u,v\in B,\ w\notin B\right\}.

By the assumption that
b
is a constant, we have
|\mathcal{C}_{1}|=p\cdot{5\choose 2}\cdot b+(n-b)\cdot{b\choose 2}=O(n)
.

Claim 23
.

Assume that
\{u,v\}\subseteq A_{i}\cup B
for some
i\in[p]
. Then
\mathbb{T}_{\Phi_{\mathcal{C}_{1}}}(\{u,v\})=V
.

Proof.

If
\{u,v\}\subseteq B
, then
w\in\mathbb{T}_{\Phi_{\mathcal{C}_{1}}}(\{u,v\})
for every
w\in V\setminus B
since
\{u,v,w\}\in\mathcal{C}_{1}
. As
|V\setminus B|=5\cdot p\geq 2
, there exist distinct
w_{1},w_{2}\in V\setminus B
, implying that
z\in\mathbb{T}_{\Phi_{\mathcal{C}_{1}}}(\{u,v\})
for every
z\in B
since
\{w_{1},w_{2},z\}\in\mathcal{C}_{1}
.

If
\{u,v\}\in A_{i}
for some
i\in[p]
, then
w\in\mathbb{T}_{\Phi_{\mathcal{C}_{1}}}(\{u,v\})
for
w\in B
since
\{u,v,w\}\in\mathcal{C}_{1}
. As
|B|=b\geq 2
, there exist distinct
w_{1},w_{2}\in B
, implying that
z\in\mathbb{T}_{\Phi_{\mathcal{C}_{1}}}(\{u,v\})
for every
z\in V\setminus B
since
\{w_{1},w_{2},z\}\in\mathcal{C}_{1}
.
∎

For the second step, we need to choose
p
in such a way that there exists a Steiner
(p,3,2)
-system, usually called a
Steiner triple system
. It is known that such a system exists whenever
p\equiv 1
or
3\ (\bmod\ 6)
, see more about Steiner triple systems in
[
7
]
. To achieve this, let us fix the value of
b
such that

b=\begin{cases}(n\bmod 30)-15&\text{if $(n\bmod\ 30)\geq 17$},\\
(n\bmod 30)+15&\text{if $(n\bmod\ 30)\leq 16$},\end{cases}

and set
p=(n-b)/5
. By the assumption
n\geq 46
and the definition of
b
, we get
p\equiv 3\ (\bmod\ 6)
,
p\geq 3
and
2\leq b\leq 31
. Let
\mathcal{T}\subseteq 2^{[p]}
be a Steiner triple system that is defined on the groups. For each triple
T=\{x,y,z\}\in\mathcal{T}
, set

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{T}=

\displaystyle\left\{\{a^{x}_{i},a^{y}_{i},a^{z}_{i+1}\},\{a^{x}_{i},a^{y}_{i},a^{z}_{i+2}\}\mid 0\leq i\leq 4\right\}

\displaystyle\cup

\displaystyle\left\{\{a^{y}_{i},a^{z}_{i},a^{x}_{i+1}\},\{a^{y}_{i},a^{z}_{i},a^{x}_{i+2}\}\mid 0\leq i\leq 4\right\}

\displaystyle\cup

\displaystyle\left\{\{a^{x}_{i},a^{z}_{i},a^{y}_{i+1}\},\{a^{x}_{i},a^{z}_{i},a^{y}_{i+2}\}\mid 0\leq i\leq 4\right\},

and define
\mathcal{C}_{2}=\bigcup_{T\in\mathcal{T}}\mathcal{C}_{T}
, see Figure
2
for an example. Since
p=n/5+O(1)
, we have
|\mathcal{C}_{2}|=30\cdot|\mathcal{T}|=30\cdot 1/3\cdot{p\choose 2}=\frac{n^{2}}{5}+O(n)
.

Figure 2
:
Construction of the hyperedges appearing in
\mathcal{C}_{1}
(green triangles) and in
\mathcal{C}_{T}
(red triangles), where
T=\{x,y,z\}
is a triple of the Steiner
(p,3,2)
-system.

Claim 24
.

Assume that
u\in A_{x}
and
v\in A_{y}
where
T=\{x,y,z\}
is a triple in
\mathcal{T}
. Then
\mathbb{T}_{\Phi_{\mathcal{C}_{1}\cup\mathcal{C}_{2}}}(\{u,v\})=V
.

Proof.

By Claim
23
, it suffices to show that
\mathbb{T}_{\Phi_{\mathcal{C}_{2}}}(\{u,v\})
contains two elements of the same group. If
u=a^{x}_{i}
and
v=a^{y}_{i}
for some
0\leq i\leq 4
, then
\mathcal{C}_{2}
contains both
\{a^{x}_{i},a^{y}_{i},a^{z}_{i+1}\}
and
\{a^{x}_{i},a^{y}_{i},a^{z}_{i+2}\}
, and we are done. Otherwise, we may assume without loss of generality that
u=a^{x}_{i}
and
v=a^{y}_{j}
where
(j-i\bmod\ 5)\leq 2
. Then
\mathcal{C}_{2}
contains both
\{a^{x}_{i},a^{z}_{i},a^{y}_{j}\}
and
\{a^{x}_{i},a^{z}_{i},a^{y}_{3+2i-j}\}
. This finishes the proof of the claim.
∎

By Claims
23
and
24
, the hypergraph
\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{C}_{1}\cup\mathcal{C}_{2}
satisfies
h_{\mathbb{M}}=\Phi_{\mathcal{D}}
and has total size
n^{2}/5+O(n)
, concluding the proof of the theorem.
∎

Finally, we consider the objective
|\cdot|_{K}
for the uniform case.

Theorem 25
.

Let
\mathbb{M}=(V,\mathcal{C})
be a rank-
r
uniform matroid on
n\geq r+1
elements. Then
|\mathbb{M}|_{K}={n\choose r}
.

Proof.

Recall that
F_{h_{\mathbb{M}}}(X)=V
for any subset
X\subseteq V
of size
r
. This implies that each
r
-element subset has to be the body of at least one hyperedge in any representation of
h_{\mathbb{M}}
using circuit clauses, implying
|\mathbb{M}|_{K}\geq{n\choose r}
.

For the upper bound, let
v_{1},\dots,v_{n}
denote the elements of
V
. Take an arbitrary set
X\subseteq V
of size
r
. Each such set has the form
X=\bigcup_{j=1}^{q}\{v_{i_{j}},\dots,v_{i_{j}+\delta_{j}}\}
for some
q\geq 1
, where
i_{j}+\delta_{j}+1<i_{j+1}
for
j\in[q-1]
and indices are meant in a cyclic order. Define
v_{X}=v_{i_{1}+\delta_{1}+1}
and

\Phi=\bigwedge_{\begin{subarray}{c}X\subseteq V:\\
|X|=r\end{subarray}}X\rightarrow v_{X}.

Then
F_{\Phi}(X)=X
if
|X|\leq r-1
and
F_{\Phi}(X)=V
otherwise, therefore
\Phi
is a representation of
h_{\mathbb{M}}
that uses circular clauses only, concluding the proof.
∎

5
Conclusions

In the present paper, as a continuation of the work started in
[
2
]
, we considered hypergraph Horn functions associated to families of circuits of a matroid, and introduced the notion of matroid Horn functions. We gave several equivalent characterizations of matroid Horn functions in terms of their canonical and complete CNF representations, and studied minimum representations of matroids and matroid Horn functions with respect to various objectives.

The proposed subclass opens up new research directions, hence we conclude our paper with listing a few open problems.

Question 1
.

What is the computational complexity of checking if a given a definite Horn function
h
represented by a definite Horn CNF
\Psi
is matroid Horn or not?

Question 2
.

Given a rank-
r
uniform matroid
\mathbb{M}
, what is the right order of magnitude of
|\mathbb{M}|_{C}=b(n,r+1,r)
?

We conjecture that the answer to this question is in fact the lower bound provided by Theorem
20
, that is,
\frac{2\cdot n^{r}}{(2\cdot r+1)\cdot r!}+O(n^{r-1})
.
