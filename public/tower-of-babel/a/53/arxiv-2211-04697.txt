L ∞ L^{\infty} - and L 2 L^{2} -sensitivity analysis for causal inference with unmeasured confounding
Yao Zhang, Qingyuan Zhao

Abstract
Sensitivity analysis for the unconfoundedness assumption is crucial in observational studies. For this purpose, the marginal sensitivity model (MSM) gained popularity recently due to its good interpretability and mathematical properties. However, as a quantification of confounding strength, the L ∞ L^{\infty} -bound it puts on the logit difference between the observed and full data propensity scores may render the analysis conservative. In this article, we propose a new sensitivity model that restricts the L 2 L^{2} -norm of the propensity score ratio, requiring only the average strength of unmeasured confounding to be bounded. By characterizing sensitivity analysis as an optimization problem, we derive closed-form sharp bounds of the average potential outcomes under our model. We propose efficient one-step estimators for these bounds based on the corresponding efficient influence functions. Additionally, we apply multiplier bootstrap to construct simultaneous confidence bands to cover the sensitivity curve that consists of bounds at different sensitivity parameters. Through a real-data study, we illustrate how the new L 2 L^{2} -sensitivity analysis can improve calibration using observed confounders and provide tighter bounds when the unmeasured confounder is additionally assumed to be independent of the measured confounders and only have an additive effect on the potential outcomes.

Related works

Modern sensitivity analysis methods for observational studies can be
traced back to
Cornfield et al. 1959
, who studied how strong an
unmeasured confounder needs to be associated with smoking to fully
explain the association between smoking and lung cancer. One common approach is to augment the statistical model with one or more sensitivity parameters in relation
to an unmeasured variable
U
, e.g.,

Rosenbaum and Rubin 1983a
;
Imbens 2003
;
VanderWeele and Arah 2011
;
VanderWeele and Ding 2017
.
These parameters, if not carefully specified, may be identifiable from
the observed data and cannot be varied freely in a sensitivity analysis

[
Scharfstein et al. 1999
;
Gustafson and McCandless 2018
;
Zhang and Tchetgen Tchetgen 2019
]
.

Without explicitly specifying a confounder
U
, authors have sought to
relax the unconfoundedness assumption by specifying the contrast,
parameterized by
\delta
,
between the counterfactual distribution of
Y(z)
given
X,Z=1-z
and
the factual distribution of
Y(z)
given
X,Z=z

[
Robins et al. 2000
;
Birmingham et al. 2003
;
Vansteelandt et al. 2006
;
Scharfstein et al. 2021
]
,
or between the distributions of
Z
given
X,Y(z)
and the
distribution of
Z
given
X

[
Scharfstein et al. 1999
;
Gilbert et al. 2003
;
Gilbert et al. 2013
;
Franks et al. 2019
;
Masten et al. 2024
]
.
Unconfoundedness is usually characterized by the assumption that

\delta=0
, but the causal effect can be identified given any value of

\delta
and the factual distribution.
In the pair-matched setting, the sensitivity analysis method developed by

Rosenbaum 1987
;
Rosenbaum 2002
can consider a range
of differences simultaneously and return a worst-case p-value or
confidence interval for inference.
In an extension of Rosenbaum’s sensitivity model to the i.i.d. setup,

Yadlowsky et al. 2022
introduced a method based on empirical loss minimization to estimate bounds for the (conditional) average treatment
effect.

Some recent articles attempted to relax the boundedness assumptions in
Rosenbaum’s sensitivity
model
[
Hasegawa and Small 2017
;
Fogarty and Hasegawa 2019
]
and
marginal sensitivity model

[
Tan 2006
;
Zhao et al. 2019
]
.
Bonvini and Kennedy 2021

proposed an alternative
sensitivity model which fixes the proportion of unconfounded units in
a study and assumed that
Y\in[y_{\text{m}in},y_{\text{m}ax}]
for
upper bounding the effect size on those units. Both

Jin et al. 2022
and
Bertsimas et al. 2022

proposed a generalization of MSM through the lens of distributionally
robust optimization.

Huang and Pimentel 2022
derived an upper bound for the bias in estimating the average treatment effect on the treated (ATT) under a variance-based sensitivity model:

\frac{\mathbb{V}[W\mid Z=0]}{\mathbb{V}[W^{\prime}\mid Z=0]}:=\frac{\mathbb{V}\{e(X)/[1-e(X)]\mid Z=0\}}{\mathbb{V}\{e(X,U)/[1-e(X,U)]\mid Z=0\}}\in\left[1,1/(1-R^{2})\right],

They obtained a closed-form expression of the maximum bias, assuming that

\text{Corr}(W-W^{*},Y\mid W,Z=1)=1.
Given that this assumption may be incompatible with the model constraint, the expression is an upper bound of the bias.

The sensitivity models discussed above restrict the confounding strength with the same constraint for all values of
X
, and/or can only provide non-sharp (i.e., conservative) bounds due to omitting constraints. Our work measures the average strength of confounding using the second moment (i.e., variance) of the propensity score ratio. The mean 1 constraint in (
3
) plays a crucial role in our motivation. The variance of 0 is equivalent to the unconfoundedness assumption. In our method, we directly solve the optimization problem with all the necessary constraints in place.

3.1
Lagrangian formulation

The optimization problem in (
8
) is challenging to solve because the second moment constraint is marginalized over
X
, while the other constraints need to hold for all values of
X
. To address this challenge, we first approach its Lagrangian formulation, where the marginal constraint becomes part of the objective function. This enables us to decompose the optimization problem and solve it after conditioning on
X
. Combining the solutions for all values of
X
can still lead to the same sensitivity curve (
Proposition

2
). The proof is straightforward and can be found in
Appendix

.

Consider
\mathbb{E}_{Y\mid X,1}[h(X,Y)Y]
and
\mathbb{E}_{Y\mid X,1}[h^{2}(X,Y)]
as two competing objectives.
Emulating the Markowitz portfolio optimization problem in finance

[
Markowitz 1952
]
, we define the conditional optimization
problem through a trade-off parameter
\lambda>0

\begin{split}\inf_{h}&\quad\ \frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{Y\mid X,1}\left[h^{2}(X,Y)\right]+\lambda\mathbb{E}_{Y\mid X,1}\left[h(X,Y)Y\right]\\
\text{subject to}&\quad\ \mathbb{E}_{Y\mid X,1}\left[h(X,Y)\right]=1,\\
&\quad\ h(X,Y)\geq e(X).\end{split}

(9)

To clarify, we are solving (
9
) given
Z=1
and any value of
X.

The last constraint needs to hold for all values of
Y\mid Z=1
. The solution is provided as follows.

Proposition 1
.

Under
2
, the program (
9
) with
\lambda>0
is solved by

h_{*}(X,Y)=e(X)+\lambda g(X,Y)

(10)

where
g(X,Y)=(\xi_{X}-Y)\mathbbold{1}_{\{Y\leq\xi_{X}\}}
and

\xi_{X}
is the unique root of the following function that is strictly
increasing:

f_{\lambda,X}(\xi):=\mathbb{E}_{Y\mid X,1}\left[(\xi-Y)\mathbbold{1}_{\{Y\leq\xi\}}\right]-\left[1-e(X)\right]/\lambda.

(11)

For the solution

in (
10
), we define
the sensitivity value and the lower bound:

\psi_{1}(\lambda):=\mathbb{E}_{X,Y\mid Z=1}\left[h_{*}^{2}(X,Y)\right]\ \text{ and }\ \psi_{2}(\lambda):=\mathbb{E}_{X,Y\mid Z=1}\left[h_{*}(X,Y)Y\right],

(12)

The sensitivity curve for
\mathbb{E}[Y(1)]
is given by
joining the points

for a range of
\lambda

The proposition below confirms that this curve is the same as the one we would obtain by connecting the optimal values of (

Proposition 2

For any

induces tighter bounds under an additional assumption. We call it the sensitivity value formulation, because it
involves finding the minimum strength of unmeasured confounding needed for

Assume that

4
Semiparametric inference

After solving the optimization problems, the remaining challenge in
sensitivity analysis is to conduct statistical inference for the
bounds and the sensitivity values. We will first introduce the idea of
one-step estimation and cross-fitting, using the
L^{\infty}
-model as
an example. Then, we will provide similar results for the
L^{2}
-model
and discuss how to combine our results with multiplier bootstrap to
obtain simultaneous confidence bands of sensitivity curves.

4.1
One-step estimation:
L^{\infty}
-model

The sharp upper bound for
\mathbb{E}[Y(1)]

based on the solution
h_{*}(X,Y)
in
Proposition

5
can be written as
\psi=\psi_{+}+\psi_{-}
with

\psi_{+}:=\mathbb{E}\big[W_{+}(X)\mu_{+}(X)\big]\quad\text{and}\quad\psi_{-}:=\mathbb{E}\big[W_{-}(X)\mu_{-}(X)\big],

(19)

where

\mu_{+}(X)=\mathbb{E}_{Y\mid X,1}\big[Y\mathbbold{1}_{\{Y>Q(X)\}}\big]

and
\mu_{-}(X)=\mathbb{E}_{Y\mid X,1}\big[Y\mathbbold{1}_{\{Y<Q(X)\}}\big]
are called expected shortfalls in the field of financial risk measurement.
To estimate
\psi
, we need to estimate the propensity score
e
, the quantile
Q

and the truncated expectations above and below
Q
respectively. We denote the nuisance parameter by
\eta=(e,Q,\mu_{+},\mu_{-})
.

Suppose that we construct the estimator
\hat{\eta}=(\hat{e},\hat{Q},\hat{\hat{\mu}}_{+},\hat{\hat{\mu}}_{-})
using the first
K-1
folds of the observations. Using
\hat{e}
, we can estimate the weight functions
W_{+}
and
W_{-}
in
\psi
.
Evaluated them at the
K
-th fold empirical measure
\mathbb{P}_{n}^{(K)}=m^{-1}\sum_{i=n-m+1}^{n}\delta_{O_{i}}
gives the direct plug-in estimator of
\psi
, defined as

\hat{\psi}_{\text{direct}}=\mathbb{P}_{n}^{(K)}\big\{\hat{W}_{+}\hat{\hat{\mu}}_{+}+\hat{W}_{-}\hat{\hat{\mu}}_{-}\big\}.

(20)

Suppose that the nuisance estimator
\hat{\eta}
is nonparametric, then
it follows from the classical theory of nonparametric regression

[
Wasserman 2006
]
that
\hat{\eta}
will converge to
\eta
at a
rate slower than
n^{-1/2}
, regardless of the dimension of the
problem. The consequence of this is that the direct estimator can not
be used for asymptotic inference because its bias also goes to
0

slower than
n^{-1/2}
. Fortunately, it is well known from
semiparametric theory
[
Bickel et al. 1993
]
that we can often
obtain estimators that converge in faster rates by using the
efficient
influence functions
(EIFs) of the target parameter, e.g.,
\psi.

The idea is a nonparametric analogue of the one-step Newton-Raphson method for solving the MLE problem for parametric models; see Section 4.5.3 in
Shao 2003

for an introduction. However, the transition from parametric models to
nonparametric models is nontrivial since it is not straightforward to define the score
function and tangent space for the infinite-dimensional nuisance
parameter
\eta
.
In our case, deriving EIFs for stochastic programs with nuisance parameters is a new challenge. Using the EIF of

\psi
as an example, we briefly explain how EIF leads to bias correction. More background can be found in
Kennedy 2022
and
Appendix

F
.
In what follows, all the EIFs have a mean of 0. In other words, the uncentered EIF of
\psi,EIF\{\psi\}+\psi,
has its mean at the target parameter
\psi
.

Theorem 1
.

The uncentered EIF of
\psi
is given by
\phi(O)=\phi_{+}(O)+\phi_{-}(O)
with

\displaystyle\phi_{+}(O)=

\displaystyle\ \frac{ZW_{+}(X)}{e(X)}\left[\left(1-\alpha_{*}-\mathbbold{1}_{\{Y>Q(X)\}}\right)Q(X)+Y\mathbbold{1}_{\{Y>Q(X)\}}-\mu_{+}(X)\right]

\displaystyle\ +\left[(1-\Gamma)Z+\Gamma\right]\mu_{+}(X)\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle\phi_{-}(O)=

\displaystyle\ \frac{ZW_{-}(X)}{e(X)}\left[\left(\alpha_{*}-\mathbbold{1}_{\{Y<Q(X)\}}\right)Q(X)+Y\mathbbold{1}_{\{Y<Q(X)\}}-\mu_{-}(X)\right]

\displaystyle\ +\left[(1-\Gamma^{-1})Z+\Gamma^{-1}\right]\mu_{-}(X).

Notice that the nuisance parameter in the uncentered EIFs above is the same as the one in the definition of

This is because the EIF is the first-order functional derivative of

Making the dependence of

Cross-fitting.
By sample splitting, we ensure that the estimator
\hat{\phi}(\cdot)=\phi(\cdot;\hat{\eta})
based the first
K-1
folds of our sample is independent of the empirical measure
\mathbb{P}_{n}^{(K)}
used for inference. Then the central limit theorem can hold without additional regularity assumptions on the nuisance. To remedy the asymptotic efficiency loss, we can use the popular
K
-fold cross-fitting strategy in
Schick 1986
;
Chernozhukov et al. 2018
. The cross-fitted estimator of
\psi
is obtained by averaging
K
cross-fitted one-step estimators:

\hat{\psi}_{\text{cf}}:=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\hat{\psi}^{(k)}\equiv\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbb{P}_{n}^{(k)}\hat{\phi}^{(k)}\equiv\mathbb{P}_{n}\hat{\phi}_{\text{cf}},

where
\mathbb{P}_{n}^{(k)}
is the
k
-th fold empirical measure and the function
\hat{\phi}^{(k)}(O)=\hat{\phi}(O;\hat{\eta}_{-k})
is based on the nuisance estimator
\hat{\eta}_{-k}
fitted to the other folds. After obtaining
K
different nuisance estimators
\hat{\eta}_{-1},\dotsc,\hat{\eta}_{-K},
the cross-fitted estimator above can be simply written as an average of
\hat{\phi}(O_{1};\hat{\eta}_{-k_{1}}),\dotsc,\hat{\phi}(O_{n};\hat{\eta}_{-k_{n}}),
where
k_{i}
is the fold that contains
i
-th observation
O_{i}
, i.e., the fold that is not used by the estimator
\hat{\eta}_{-k_{i}}.
This adaptive average is denoted by
\mathbb{P}_{n}\hat{\phi}_{\text{cf}}
in the equation above.

Cross-fitting allows us to make use of every single observation in computing the empirical average and estimating the nuisance parameter but not simultaneously. The bias of the cross-fitted estimator converges as fast as the one-step estimator.
Our sample-splitting and cross-fitting overlap in the
K
-th fold. Then,
\hat{\psi}^{(K)}=\hat{\psi},\hat{\phi}^{(K)}=\hat{\phi}
and
\hat{\eta}_{-K}=\hat{\eta}
.
Then by
Proposition

7
, we have

\hat{\psi}_{\text{cf}}-\psi=\left(\mathbb{P}_{n}-\mathbb{P}\right)\hat{\phi}_{\text{cf}}+\Bias(\hat{\phi}_{\text{cf}}\mid\hat{\eta}_{\text{cf}})=\left(\mathbb{P}_{n}-\mathbb{P}\right)\phi+o_{\mathbb{P}}(n^{-1/2}),

as long as

\Bias(\hat{\phi}^{(k)}\mid\hat{\eta})=o_{\mathbb{P}}(n^{-1/2})
for all
k\in[K].

This result implies that

\hat{\psi}_{\text{cf}}

is a root-
n
consistent and asymptotically normal (CAN) estimator of
\psi
, i.e.,

\sqrt{n}\big(\hat{\psi}_{\text{cf}}-\psi\big)\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal{N}\left(0,\sigma^{2}:=\Var\left[\phi(O)\right]\right).

It is worth noting that the uncentered EIF
\phi(O)
achieves the lowest variance in the central limit theorem (CLT) above, which implies that

\hat{\psi}_{\text{cf}}
is an efficient estimator of
\psi

[
Bickel et al. 1993
;
Tsiatis 2006
]
. The (
1-\alpha
)-confidence interval of
\psi
is given by

\big[\hat{\psi}_{\text{cf}}\pm z_{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\hat{\sigma}_{\text{cf}}/\sqrt{n}\big],

where
z_{\frac{\alpha}{2}}
is the upper
\alpha/2
-quantile of the standard normal and
\hat{\sigma}_{\text{cf}}^{2}
is the cross-fitted estimator of the variance
\sigma^{2}
in the CLT above.

We can estimate all the nuisance parameters through a conditional density estimator
\mathbb{\hat{E}}_{Y\mid X,1}[\cdot]=\int\cdot\ \hat{p}_{Y\mid X,1}(y)dy.
For example, we estimate the second moment in (
21
) by
\int y^{2}\mathbbold{1}_{\{y\leq\hat{\xi}_{X}\}}\hat{p}_{Y\mid X,1}(y)dy.

In the literature, there are
advanced methods

[
Chernozhukov et al. 2013
;
Belloni et al. 2019
;
Gao and Hastie 2021
]

and
models
[
Meinshausen and Ridgeway 2006
;
Friedman 2020
]
to do so.
As an illustrative example, we can use the Nadaraya-Waston (NW) kernel estimator
[
Nadaraya 1964
;
Watson 1964
]
,

\hat{p}_{Y\mid X,1}(y)=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n-m}Z_{i}K_{1}(X-X_{i})K_{2}(y-Y_{i})}{\sum_{j=1}^{n-m}Z_{j}K_{1}(X-X_{j})},

(22)

where
K_{1}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow[0,\infty]
and

K_{2}:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow[0,\infty]
are two continuous and
nonnegative kernel functions.
Alternatively, we can use an additive regression model: given
Z_{i}=1,

Y_{i}=f(X_{i})+\epsilon_{i}\sigma^{2}(X_{i}),

where
\epsilon_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1).
We can first fit a regression model
\hat{f}
to estimate
f
.
Given a mean estimate
\hat{f}
and a variance estimate using the residuals,
the (truncated) first and second moments of the Gaussian outcome
Y
given
X,Z=1
can be estimated with closed-form expressions. We can also combine both approaches by modelling the density of the residual
Y_{i}-\hat{f}(X_{i})
using NW; see Section 4 of
Hansen 2004
and Section 3 of
Fan and Yao 1998
for this two-step modelling approach.

To estimate the root
\xi_{X}
, we first estimate the function
f_{\lambda,X}(\xi)
in (
11
) or
f_{\theta,X}(\xi)
in (
15
)
using
\hat{e}
and the moments estimated by
\hat{p}_{Y\mid X,1}
.
The estimator is an increasing function of
\xi
when
\hat{p}_{Y\mid X,1}>0.
We
can find
\hat{\xi}_{X}
via the bisection method; the root-finding
error decays exponentially fast in the number of iterations.
The results below show that one-step correction can improve the accuracy
of the direct plug-in estimator.

and

4.3
Simultaneous confidence bands

In practice, it is often desirable to report multiple bounds under
different levels of unmeasured confounding.

Belloni et al. 2018
proposed an inference procedure based on multiplier bootstrap (MB)
[
Giné and Zinn 1984
;
Vaart and Wellner 1996
]
to construct
simultaneous confidence bands for a range of parameters. MB has been applied in causal inference by
Kennedy 2019
;
Bonvini and Kennedy 2021
.
Here we apply MB to construct confidence bands for the sensitivity curves,

\begin{split}\Psi(\mathcal{D})&:=\left\{(\Gamma,\psi(\Gamma)):\Gamma\in\mathcal{D}\subset[1,\infty)\right\},\\
\Psi_{12}(\mathcal{D}_{12})&:=\{(\psi_{1}(\lambda),\psi_{2}(\lambda)):\lambda\in\mathcal{D}_{1}\subset[0,\infty)\},\\
\Psi_{3}(\mathcal{D}_{3})&:=\{(\psi_{3}(\theta),\mathbb{E}_{Y\mid X,1}[Y]-\theta):\theta\in\mathcal{D}_{3}\subset[0,\infty)\},\end{split}

(23)

for some pre-specified ranges
\mathcal{D}
’s.
Kennedy 2019

proved the validity of MB for influence function-based estimators under some standard regularity conditions; see Theorem 3 and 4 in his paper. We next restate his results in our context.

Suppose that we want to construct a confidence band for the
sensitivity curve
\psi_{0}(\beta)
defined using a sensitivity
parameter
\beta\in\mathcal{D}_{0}.
Following the notation in our
last section, we denote the uncentered EIF of
\psi_{0}
by
\phi_{0}

and its variance
\sigma_{0}^{2}(\beta).
In one-step estimation, we
estimate the EIF by
\hat{\phi}_{0}(\cdot)=\hat{\phi}(\cdot;\hat{\eta})
using the nuisance estimator
\hat{\eta}=(\hat{e},\hat{p}_{Y\mid X,1})
; we can also estimate the quantile
functions and their truncated expectations using
\hat{\eta}
. We
implement cross-fitting and denote the estimators similarly. We state the regularity conditions on
\hat{\phi}_{0}(\cdot)
instead of every
\hat{\phi}^{(k)}(\cdot)=\hat{\phi}(\cdot;\hat{\eta}_{-k})
for all
k\in[K]
in cross-fitting.
The following results apply to three formulations of sensitivity analysis problems (
L^{\infty}
-MSM,
L^{2}
-MSM with the Lagrangian formulation, and
L^{2}
-MSM with the sensitivity value formulation) and their corresponding efficient influence function-based estimators considered in this article.

Theorem 4
.

Under
2
,
4
and the conditions

(i)

\sup_{\beta\in\mathcal{D}_{0}}|\hat{\sigma}_{0,\text{cf}}^{2}(\beta)/\sigma_{0}^{2}(\beta)-1|=o_{\mathbb{P}}(1),

(ii)

\mathbb{E}\big\{\big[\sup_{\beta\in\mathcal{D}_{0}}|\hat{\phi}_{0}(O;\beta)-\phi_{0}(O;\beta)\big|\big]^{2}\ \big|\ \hat{\eta}\big\}=o_{\mathbb{P}}(1),

\beta

it holds that

\sqrt{n}\Big[\hat{\psi}_{0,\text{cf}}(\cdot)-\psi_{0}(\cdot)\Big]/\hat{\sigma}_{0,\text{cf}}^{2}(\cdot)\xrightarrow{d}\mathbb{G}_{0}(\cdot)=\left[\phi_{0}(O;\cdot)-\psi_{0}(\cdot)\right]/\sigma_{0}(\cdot),

6
Real data study

We next compare the analysis methods on a publicly available observational study
[
Zhao et al. 2018
]
for estimating the ATE of fish consumption on the blood mercury level.
The outcome variable “blood mercury” is obtained from the individuals who answered questionnaires about seafood consumption in the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) 2013-2014. The binary “treamtent” variable indicates if an individual has consumed more than 12 servings of fish or shellfish in the previous month. The study has 234 treated individuals (high consumption) and 873 controls (low consumption), and 8 covariates: gender, age, income, whether income is missing, race, education, ever smoked, and the number of cigarettes smoked last month.

We use the same nuisance estimators as in the last section. We one-step estimate all the sensitivity curves with 9-fold cross-fitting. Fixing the sensitivity parameter at the same value, we obtain the upper bound of
\mathbb{E}[Y(0)]
as follows.
We change
Z=1
to
Z=0
and
Y
to
-Y
in the dataset, then apply our proposed estimator for
\mathbb{E}[Y(1)]
and multiply it by
-1
at the end. We average the treated and control sensitivity values weighted by

and

, which measures the total deviation from
2
. Connecting all the ATE bounds gives the sensitivity curve of the ATE. We construct lower confidence bands for all the sensitivity curves, using MB on the EIFs of the ATE lower bounds (the difference between the EIFs of treated and control).
For

-analysis, it follows from
Theorem

that joining the points

gives a valid confidence band for
\Psi_{12}(\mathcal{D}_{12}).
We can compute the band for
\Psi_{3}(\mathcal{D}_{3})
similarly.

illustrates the sensitivity curves obtained under MSM (the

-model) and our new

\mathcal{H}_{\infty}(\Gamma)
in (

) holds for

.

\Gamma=7.5
, we can compare it
with the corresponding odd ratio for some observed covariates(s) in

7
Conclusion & Discussion

In this article, we extend sensitivity analysis based on the
previously proposed
L^{\infty}
-marginal sensitivity model (MSM) to a more
flexible
L^{2}
-sensitivity model, which only assumes the averaged
strength of unmeasured confounding is bounded. We provide analytic solutions to the optimization problems under the
L^{2}
-model and establish the connections between
the solutions and sharp bounds obtained under the
L^{\infty}
- and
L^{2}
-model.
Our new analysis framework is further complemented by methodology for constructing efficient estimators and simultaneously valid confidence bands.

Under the unconfoundedness assumption, the IPW estimator and its
variants have been applied in a variety of observational studies.
Recently, MSM has extended to deal with sequential unconfounding

[
Bonvini et al. 2022
;
Tan 2023
]
. Similarly,
it would be interesting to extend the
L^{2}
-model to studies over time and space
[
Brumback et al. 2004
;
Lee and Ogburn 2021
]
or with
imperfect proxies of confounding mechanisms

[
Tchetgen et al. 2020
]
. The instability issue of IPW estimators and
the associated sensitivity analysis are often addressed by trimming
the propensity scores, which changes the estimand. It would be also
useful to consider relaxations of the optimization problems considered
here and develop simpler surrogates. Such relaxations may allow us to
include more constraints based on domain knowledge of the unmeasured
confounders in the sensitivity model, leading to tighter bounds for partial
identification.

The term “sensitivity value” was first introduced in
Zhao 2018
within the framework of
Rosenbaum 2002
’s sensitivity model for pair-matched observational studies. However, the sensitivity values proposed in this article are applicable to a broader range of study designs and statistical problems. For example, they can be used in constructing confidence intervals
[
Owen 2001
;
Duchi et al. 2021
]
and evaluating stability
[
Gupta and Rothenhäusler 2021
]
. Given their connection to sensitivity values and partial identification through constrained optimization, our results in semiparametric inference may have applications beyond causal inference.

Appendix F
Background: influence functions

The theory of influence functions
[
van der Vaart and Bernard 2002
]
is crucial to removing the first-order bias in semiparametric estimation. It starts by treating a one-dimensional parameter
\tau
as a mapping
\tau(\mathbb{P})
from
\mathcal{P}
to
\mathbb{R}
where
\mathcal{P}
is the set of all possible observed data distributions. For any
\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{P}
, we define a path through
\mathbb{P}
as a one-dimensional submodel that passes through

\mathbb{P}
at
\epsilon=0
in the direction of a zero-mean function
s
satisfying that
\|s\|_{2}\leq C^{\prime}
and
\epsilon\leq 1/C^{\prime}
for some constant
C^{\prime}>0
. The submodel
\mathbb{P}_{\epsilon}
takes a density

p_{O,\epsilon}(o):=p_{O}(o)[1+\epsilon s(o)]
for
o\in\mathcal{O}
.

The tangent space
\mathcal{S}
is defined as the set of zero-mean functions
s
for any paths through
\mathbb{P}
; and
\mathcal{S}
is known to be the Hilbert space of zero-mean functions when we use a nonparametric model.
Suppose that the mapping
\tau(\mathbb{P})
is differentiable at
\mathbb{P}
relative to
\mathcal{S}
, meaning that there is a linear mapping
\dot{\tau}(\cdot;\mathbb{P}):\mathcal{S}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that for any
s\in\mathcal{S}
and submodel
\mathbb{P}_{\epsilon}
, we have

\dot{\tau}(s;\mathbb{P})=\frac{d\psi_{\epsilon}}{d\epsilon}\Big|_{\epsilon=0}=\mathbb{E}\big[EIF(\tau)(O)s(O)\big],

(31)

where
\tau_{\epsilon}:=\tau(\mathbb{P}_{\epsilon})
and
s(o):=\frac{d}{d\epsilon}\log p_{O,\epsilon}(o)\big|_{\epsilon=0}
.
In this article, we let the subscript “
\epsilon
” denote the distribution shift from
\mathbb{P}
to
\mathbb{P}_{\epsilon}
and “
s
” denote the score function for a random variable.
The second equality above is established by the Riesz representation theorem for the Hilbert space
\mathcal{S}
that expresses
\dot{\tau}(\cdot;\mathbb{P})
as an inner product with the unique
efficient influence function

EIF(\tau):\mathcal{O}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
that lies in the closed linear span of
\mathcal{S}
. The efficiency of
EIF(\tau)

K.1

We first let

Lemma 4
.

Under
2
and
3

\mathcal{F}=\left\{\phi(O;\Gamma):\Gamma\in\mathcal{D}=[\Gamma_{\min},\Gamma_{\max}]\right\}

|\phi(O;\check{\Gamma})-\phi(O;\Gamma)|\lesssim_{\mathbb{P}}|\check{\Gamma}-\Gamma|

Proof.

From

1

\displaystyle\ \frac{ZW_{+}(X)}{e(X)}\left\{\left[Y-Q(X)\right]\mathbbold{1}_{\{Y>Q(X)\}}-\mathbb{E}_{Y\mid X,1}\left[\left[Y-Q(X)\right]\mathbbold{1}_{\{Y>Q(X)\}}\right]\right\}

\displaystyle\ +\left[(1-\Gamma)Z+\Gamma\right]\left(\mathbb{E}_{Y\mid X,1}\left[\left[Y-Q(X)\right]\mathbbold{1}_{\{Y>Q(X)\}}\right]\ +Q(X)\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid X,1}\{Y>Q(X)\}\right).

5

\Gamma/(1+\Gamma)
-quantile. Let
\check{Q}(X)

\check{\Gamma}/(1+\check{\Gamma})

which shows that

|\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid X,1}\{Y>\check{Q}(X)\}-\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid X,1}\{Y>Q(X)\}|\lesssim|\check{Q}(X)-Q(X)|.
