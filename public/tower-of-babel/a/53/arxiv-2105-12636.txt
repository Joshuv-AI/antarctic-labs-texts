Saddle solutions for the Choquard equation with a general nonlinearity
Jiankang Xia

Abstract




Abstract:
In the spirit of Berestycki and Lions, we prove the existence of saddle type nodal solutions for the Choquard equation

-\Delta u+u=\big(I_{\alpha}\ast F(u)\big)F^{\prime}(u)\hskip 18.49988pt\text{ in }\;\mathbb{R}^{N}

where
N\geq 2
and
I_{\alpha}
is the Riesz potential of order
\alpha\in(0,N)
. Without any compact setting, we construct saddle solutions in a unified way for the Choquard equation whose nodal domains are of cone shapes demonstrating Coxeter’s symmetric configurations in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
.
Moreover, if
F^{\prime}
is odd and has constant sign on
(0,+\infty)
, then the saddle solution maintains signed on the fundamental domain of the corresponding Coxeter group and receives opposite signs on any two adjacent domains.
These results further complete the variational framework in constructing sign-changing solutions for the Choquard equation but still require a quadratic or super-quadratic growth on
F
near the origin.

Key words:
Choquard equation; saddle solutions; Coxeter group; nodal domains.

Introduction and main results

We consider nodal solutions for the Choquard type nonlinear equation

-\Delta u+u=\big(I_{\alpha}\ast F(u)\big)f(u),\qquad u\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N}),

(
\mathcal{C}
)

which has been widely studied in recent years for its diverse applications in physics.
Here the dimension
N\geq 2
and
F\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R})
is the primitive function of
f
. The Riesz potential
I_{\alpha}
with order
\alpha\in(0,N)
is defined for

x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\{0\}
by

I_{\alpha}(x)=\frac{A_{\alpha}}{\lvert x\rvert^{N-\alpha}}\quad\text{ with }\quad A_{\alpha}=\frac{\Gamma(\frac{N-\alpha}{2})}{2^{\alpha}\pi^{\frac{N}{2}}\Gamma(\frac{\alpha}{2})}

where
\Gamma
denotes the classical Gamma function and
\ast
stands for the convolution on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
.

The Choquard equation (

C

) is a canonically nonlocal equation due to the appearance of the convolution. When
N=3
,
\alpha=2
and
F(u)=\lvert u\rvert^{2}
, as an important model, it arises in various physics contexts, such as the theory of one-component plasma in describing the electron trapped in its own hole
[
20
]
and the model of a polaron at rest in the quantum physics
[
29
]
. This equation also has applications in Einstein–Klein–Gordon system and Einstein–Dirac system

[
11
]
. It is usually referred to as the Schrödinger–Newton equation in the description of self-gravitating matter
[
25
]
. We refer to
[
6
,
18
,
28
]
and the references therein for more physical interpretations.

The
p
-homogeneous Choquard equation, e.g.,
F(u)=\lvert u\rvert^{p}
, has already been extensively investigated by a plenty of literatures during last decades. We refer to
[
20
,
22
,
23
]
for the existence and multiplicity results for the Choquard equation (

C

) in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
with
F(u)=\lvert u\rvert^{2}
and
\alpha=2
. Moroz and Van Schaftingen
[
26
]
obtained the ground state solution for the
p
-homogeneous Choquard equation. Here and in the sequel, the ground state solution is understood in the sense that it minimizes the energy functional among all nontrivial solutions.
It is shown that nontrivial solutions with finite energies to the
p
-homogeneous Choquard equation can exist if and only if the exponent
p
satisfies
\frac{N-2}{N+\alpha}<\frac{1}{p}<\frac{N}{N+\alpha}
, see e.g.,
[
26
,
27
,
32
]
. Several papers have also studied the existence of sign-changing
solutions to the
p
-homogeneous Choquard equation in recent years. In a compact setting, we mention
[
32
]
for the least energy sign-changing solution and refer to
[

,
15
]
for the multiple radially symmetric solutions with prescribed number of nodal domains. We refer readers to the survey paper
[
28
]
and the references therein for more recent results in this field.

For the Choquard equation (

C

), Moroz and Van Schaftingen
[
27
]
investigated the ground state solution under the so-called Berestycki–Lions conditions as
N\geq 3
,

(
F_{0}
)

F\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R})
and there exists
s_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
such that
F(s_{0})\neq 0
.

(
f_{1}
)

There exists

such that for every

,
\lvert sF^{\prime}(s)\rvert\leq C(\lvert s\rvert^{\frac{N+\alpha}{N}}+\lvert s\rvert^{\frac{N+\alpha}{N-2}}).

(

\lim\limits_{s\to 0}\frac{F(s)}{\lvert s\rvert^{\frac{N+\alpha}{N}}}=0
and
\lim\limits_{s\to\infty}\frac{F(s)}{\lvert s\rvert^{\frac{N+\alpha}{N-2}}}=0

Besides the nontrivial assumption

, Battaglia and Van Schaftingen
[

(

)

We emphasis that the above assumptions on the nonlinearity are quit mild and reasonable. It turns out that these assumptions are actually "almost necessary" for the existence of nontrivial finite energy solutions

On the other hand, some progresses have been made on the saddle type nodal solutions. It was Ghimenti and Van Schaftingen who constructed a surprisingly odd solution for the
p
-homogeneous Choquard equation in their seminal work
[
8
]
, this kind of odd solution has exactly two half-space nodal domains and makes the issue of constructing sign-changing solutions become more interesting because the compact assumption there is no longer needed.
Actually they took advantage of the reflection group with order

Motivated by the highlight results in

In a very recent joint work with Zhang

Whereas all of the saddle solutions constructed in

In the present paper, we shall deal with the existence of saddle solutions for the Choquard equation (

C

) with a general nonlinearity by introducing the Coxeter symmetry.
The approach for seeking saddle solutions in the previous work heavily depends upon the Nehari manifold method, in turn, the homogeneous or the monotonic property of the nonlinearity plays a significant role. As a consequence, the Nehari manifold method can not work well in tacking with the general Choquard equation (

C

) if we do not have any additional monotonic assumptions on

. This has been observed in the process of finding ground state solutions in
[

,
27
]
.

To state our results, we shall first recall the Coxeter group and some notations.
Let
G
be a finite Coxeter group with rank
k\leq N
and
\lvert G\rvert
denote its cardinality.
It follows that
G=\langle\mathcal{S}\rangle
with
\mathcal{S}
being the generating set and having

distinct reflections in
\mathcal{S}
. More precisely, there exists a unique epimorphism
\psi:G\to\{\pm 1\}
induced by
\psi(s)=-1
for all

. A function

is said to be

g\diamond u(x)=\psi(g)u(x),\quad\text{ for }g\in G,\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}.

Here
\diamond
denotes the group action which will be explained in Section
2
. With this kind of symmetric groups, the symmetric critical principle is applicable and we shall consider our problem in a closed
G
-subspace defined by

\mathcal{H}_{G}=\{u\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})\,|\,g\diamond u=\psi(g)u,\forall g\in G\}.

It turns out that all of the symmetric groups used in
[
8
,
33
,
36
]
are of Coxeter with their ranks less than
4
yielding different symmetric nodal structures. In what follows, we say
u\in\mathcal{H}_{G}\setminus\{0\}
is a
G
-saddle solution if
u
solves the Choquard equation (

C

) and

is called

-groundstate if in addition

minimizes the energy functional amongst all the
G
-saddle solutions.

The first result is about the existence of
G
-groundstate. Besides the nontrivial assumption
(F_{0})
, in the present paper, we assume a little stronger assumptions than that in

on the nonlinearity

(

)

\lim\limits_{s\to 0}\frac{f(s)}{s}=\mu\in[0,+\infty)
.

(

)

\lim\limits_{s\to\infty}\frac{F(s)}{\lvert s\rvert^{\frac{N+\alpha}{N-2}}}=0

\lim\limits_{s\to\infty}\frac{F(s)}{\lvert s\rvert^{\bar{p}}}=0

2
Variational framework and minimax characterizations

The Choquard equation (

C

) has a variational structure and corresponds to an energy functional
\mathcal{E}
, defined for each function
u\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
by

\mathcal{E}(u)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\lvert\nabla u\rvert^{2}+\lvert u\rvert^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}(I_{\alpha}*F(u))F(u).

According to the arguments in
[
1
,
27
]
, this functional is well defined and is of
C^{1}
in
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
.
In the sprite of Berestycki–Lions assumptions, the authors in
[
1
,
27
]
investigated the existence of ground state solutions for the Choquard equation (

C

) by employing a scaling technique introduced by Jeanjean
[
16
]
to construct the so called Pohozaev–Palais–Smale sequence. Precisely, they proved that
the mountain pass level defined below actually yields the ground state solution, it takes the form of

c_{0}=\inf_{\gamma\in\Gamma_{0}}\max_{t\in[0,1]}\mathcal{E}(\gamma(t))

where the paths set is defined by

\Gamma_{0}=\{\gamma\in C([0,1];H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N}))\,|\,\gamma(0)=0\text{ and }\mathcal{E}(\gamma(1))<0\}.

To obtain the symmetric nodal structures, we shall introduce the Coxeter symmetry, see the details in the Appendix.
For
k\geq 1
, we denote
\mathcal{G}_{k}
and
\mathcal{W}_{k}
be the collections of finite Coxeter groups and the irreducible Coxeter groups with rank
k
, respectively. Given
G\in\mathcal{G}_{k}
,
by Proposition
A.4
, we see that
G=W_{k_{1}}\times\cdots\times W_{k_{\ell}}
where
W_{k_{i}}\in\mathcal{W}_{k_{i}}
for each
k_{i}\geq 1
and
\sum_{i}k_{i}=k
.
Recalling the unique epimorphism
\psi:G\to\{\pm 1\}
defined in Lemma
A.6
, we now set up the framework space

\mathcal{H}_{G}:=\{u\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})\,|\,\texttt{g}\diamond u=\psi(g)u\text{ for all }\texttt{g}\in G\}.

Here the group action on a function
u:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
is defined for each
g\in G
by

g\diamond u(x)=u(g^{-1}x)\quad\text{
with
}\quad gx:=(g\oplus 1_{N-k})x.

The energy functional permits the mountain pass geometry, so that the mountain pass level is a natural candidate for seeking weak solutions. Namely, we consider

c_{G}=\inf_{\gamma\in\Gamma_{G}}\max_{t\in[0,1]}\mathcal{E}(\gamma(t))

(2.1)

where paths set belongs to
\mathcal{H}_{G}
, i.e.,
\Gamma_{G}=\{\gamma\in\Gamma_{0}\,|\,\gamma(t)\in\mathcal{H}_{G}\text{ for all }t\in[0,1]\}
.
Comparing with the saddle type nodal solutions obtained in
[
26
,
36
,
33
]
, we have neither monotonic assumption nor Ambrosetti–Rabinowitz type growth condition on the nonlinearity. To overcome this, we recall the Pohozaev identity, which is allowed by the regularities exhibited in Theorem
1.2
. For the detailed proofs, we refer to
[
27

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
N\geq 2
. If
F
satisfies
(F_{1})
–
(F_{2})
and
u\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})\cap W^{2,2}_{\rm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
solves the equation (

\mathcal{P}(u)=\frac{N-2}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\lvert\nabla u\rvert^{2}+\frac{N}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\lvert u\rvert^{2}-\frac{N+\alpha}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}(I_{\alpha}*F(u))F(u)=0.

To simplify our notations, we define

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
N\geq 2
and
F\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R};\mathbb{R})
satisfy the assumptions
(F_{1})
–
(F_{2})
. Then for each
u\in\mathcal{H}_{G}\setminus\{0\}
that satisfies
Q(u)>0
, there exist a path
\gamma_{u}\in C([0,+\infty);\mathcal{H}_{G})
and a unique
t_{u}\in(0,+\infty)
such that

\gamma_{u}(0)=0
,
\gamma_{u}(t_{u})\in\mathcal{P}_{G}
and
\mathcal{E}(\gamma_{u}(t_{u}))=\max_{t\geq 0}\mathcal{E}(\gamma_{u}(t))
. Particularly, if
\mathcal{P}(u)\leq 0
, then
t_{u}\leq 1
.

This lemma essentially is the results of
[
27
, Propositons 4.1]
(see
[
1
, Propositons 5.1]
for
N=2
) and the proof relies on the scaling technique developed by Jeanjean and Tanaka
[
17
]
.

Proof.

Let
u\in\mathcal{H}_{G}\setminus\{0\}
that satisfies
Q(u)>0
. We define a path

\gamma_{u}:[0,+\infty)\to\mathcal{H}_{G}
such that for
N\geq 3

\gamma_{u}(t)(x)=\begin{cases}u(x/t),&\text{if }t>0,\\
0,&\text{if }t=0.\end{cases}

Then the path
\gamma_{u}(t)\in C([0,+\infty);\mathcal{H}_{G})
and satisfies
\gamma_{u}(0)=0
. Direct computations show us two functions that

\alpha(t):=\mathcal{E}(\gamma_{u}(t))=\frac{t^{N-2}}{2}A(u)+\frac{t^{N}}{2}B(u)-\frac{t^{N+\alpha}}{2}Q(u)

and

\beta(t):=\mathcal{P}(\gamma_{u}(t))=\frac{N-2}{2}t^{N-2}A(u)+\frac{N}{2}t^{N}B(u)-\frac{N+\alpha}{2}t^{N+\alpha}Q(u).

It is easy to see that
\beta(t)=t\alpha^{\prime}(t)
and

\alpha(t)
admits a global maxima because
\alpha(t)>0
for small
t
and
\alpha(t)<0
when
t
is sufficient large. It then follows that there exists
t_{u}\in(0,+\infty)
such that
\alpha(t_{u})=\max_{t\geq 0}\alpha(t)
. Moreover,
t_{u}
is unique since the function
\beta(t)
only has one zero point so that
\beta(t_{u})=0
and
\gamma_{u}(t_{u})\in\mathcal{P}_{G}
. In fact, suppose in contrary that
t_{u}^{1}<t_{u}^{2}
such that

\beta(t_{u}^{1})=\beta(t_{u}^{2})=0
, we then deduce a contradiction that

\Big(\frac{1}{(t_{u}^{1})^{2}}-\frac{1}{(t_{u}^{2})^{2}}\Big)\frac{N-2}{2}A(u)=\Big((t_{u}^{1})^{\alpha}-(t_{u}^{2})^{\alpha}\Big)\frac{N+\alpha}{2}Q(u).

Note that
\beta(t)>0
on the interval
(0,t_{u})
.
\mathcal{P}(u)\leq 0
implies that
\beta(1)\leq 0
, this brings us that
(0,t_{u})\subset(0,1)
, which amounts to
t_{u}\leq 1
.

For the case
N=2
, the above path
\gamma_{u}
is not anymore continuous at
0
in
\mathcal{H}_{G}
, we shall connect the function
\gamma_{u}(t_{u})
with
0
by the multiplication. Since
Q(u)>0
, we can find a
t_{u}>0
such that
\beta(t_{u})=0
, so that
\gamma_{u}(t_{u})\in\mathcal{P}_{G}
. We then define that

\tilde{\gamma}_{u}(t)(x)=\begin{cases}\frac{t}{\tau_{0}}u(\frac{x}{\tau_{0}t_{u}}),&\text{if }t\leq\tau_{0},\\
u(\frac{x}{tt_{u}}),&\text{if }t\geq\tau_{0}.\end{cases}

The parameter
\tau_{0}\in(0,1)
will be determined later. Clearly, we see that
\gamma_{u}
is continuous in
\mathcal{H}_{G}
. For
t\geq\tau_{0}
, the Pohozaev identity gives that

\displaystyle\tilde{\alpha}(t)=\mathcal{E}(\tilde{\gamma}_{u}(t))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}A(u)+\frac{(t_{u}t)^{2}}{2}B(u)-\frac{(tt_{u})^{2+\alpha}}{2}Q(u)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}A(u)+\Big(\frac{t^{2}}{2}-\frac{t^{2+\alpha}}{2+\alpha}\Big)t_{u}^{2}B(u).

It is not difficult to check that
\tilde{\alpha}(t)
achieves its strict maximum at
t=1
and is negative for sufficient large
t
.
Without loss of generality, we assume that
t_{u}=1
. For

t\leq\tau_{0}
, by the assumptions
(F_{1})
–
(F_{2})
and the Hardy–Littlewood–Sobolev inequality, we deduce that

\displaystyle\frac{\tau_{0}}{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}(I_{\alpha}*

\displaystyle F(tu/\tau_{0})f(tu/\tau_{0})u=\frac{\tau^{2}_{0}}{t^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}(I_{\alpha}*F(tu/\tau_{0})f(tu/\tau_{0})tu/\tau_{0}

\displaystyle\leq C\frac{\tau^{2}_{0}}{t^{2}}\Big(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\lvert F(tu/\tau_{0})\rvert^{\frac{4}{2+\alpha}}\Big)^{\frac{2+\alpha}{4}}\Big(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\lvert f(tu/\tau_{0})tu/\tau_{0}\rvert^{\frac{4}{2+\alpha}}\Big)^{\frac{2+\alpha}{4}}

\displaystyle\leq C\frac{\tau^{2}_{0}}{t^{2}}\Big(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\lvert tu/\tau_{0}\rvert^{\frac{8}{2+\alpha}}+\lvert tu/\tau_{0}\rvert^{\frac{4\bar{p}}{2+\alpha}}\Big)^{\frac{2+\alpha}{2}}\leq C\big(\|u\|^{4}+\|u\|^{2\bar{p}}\big).

We conclude our proof by fixing a proper
\tau_{0}<1
such that
\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}\tau}\mathcal{E}(\tilde{\gamma}_{u}(t))>0
for
t\leq\tau_{0}
. Actually, we have for sufficiently small
\tau_{0}
,

\displaystyle\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\tilde{\alpha}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\Big(\frac{t^{2}}{2\tau_{0}^{2}}A(u)+\frac{t^{2}}{2}B(u)-\frac{\tau_{0}^{2+\alpha}}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}(I_{\alpha}*F(tu/\tau_{0}))F(tu/\tau_{0})\Big)

\displaystyle=t\Big(\frac{1}{\tau_{0}^{2}}A(u)+B(u)-\tau_{0}^{\alpha}\frac{\tau_{0}}{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}(I_{\alpha}*F(tu/\tau_{0})f(tu/\tau_{0})u\Big)

\displaystyle\geq t\Big(\frac{1}{\tau_{0}^{2}}A(u)+B(u)-C\tau_{0}^{\alpha}(\|u\|^{4}+\|u\|^{2p})\Big)>0.

Hence,
\tilde{\alpha}(t)
is strictly increasing when
t\leq\tau_{0}
, which allows
t=t_{u}
being the unique global maximum point.
∎

A standard argument shows that the functional
\mathcal{E}
has the mountain pass geometry, which furnishes a Pohozaev–Palais–Smale sequence at the energy level

defined by (

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
N\geq 2
,
\alpha\in(0,N)
. Assume the growth assumptions
(F_{0})
–
(F_{2})
hold.
Then there exists a sequence

in
\mathcal{H}_{G}
such that, as

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(u_{n})\to c_{G}\in(0,+\infty),\;\mathcal{P}(u_{n})\to 0,\;\text{\rm{ and} }\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(u_{n})\to 0\;\text{\rm{ strongly in the dual space }}\mathcal{H}_{G}^{*}.

Proof.

To show

, we need construct a path in
\mathcal{H}_{G}
. By Lemma
2.2
, this turns out to find a function

such that

.
In order to derive this, by recalling the nontrivial assumption
(F_{0})
and the unit ball

centered at the origin, we take

so that

and

. Here

is the strict fundamental domain of the Coxeter group

and
\chi_{B_{1}\cap\mathcal{F}}
denotes the characteristic function on

. In fact, for any

, we have

\bar{g}\diamond w_{0}=s_{0}\sum_{g\in G}\psi(g)(\bar{g}g)\diamond\chi_{B_{1}\cap\mathcal{F}}(x)=s_{0}\psi(\bar{g})\sum_{g\in G}\psi(\bar{g}g)(\bar{g}g)\diamond\chi_{B_{1}\cap\mathcal{F}}(x)=\psi(\bar{g})w_{0}.

The remanent proof can be verbatim copied from
[

, Proposition 2.1]
and
[
1
, Proposition 3.1]
, respectively. The only difference is the Sobolev space

should be replaced by the framework subspace

, so we omit the details but emphasis that the scaling technique of Jeanjean
[

]
can be used to get the additional asymptotic property for the Palais–Smale sequence that

as

.
∎

We remark that the nontrivial assumption
(F_{0})
indicates both the paths set

and the Pohozaev manifold

are nonempty. We shall prove the following characterizations.

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
N\geq 2
,
\alpha\in(0,N)
. Assume that the assumptions
(F_{0})
–
(F_{2})

c_{G}=p_{G}=m_{G}.

Here

For any

so that

. By Lemma

and a suitable change of variable, we conclude that

Proof of Theorem

–

It then follows that

for any

.

We denote for convenience
\frac{2N}{N-2}=\infty
in case that
N=2
. By the growth assumptions
(F_{1})
–
(F_{2})
, there exists a constant
C>0
such that for all

For the planar case, the exponent

will be replaced by fixed
\bar{p}\in[2,\infty)
.
We then conclude that

by proving that

In fact, we have by direct computations that

\begin{split}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{\lvert u(y)\rvert^{s}}{\lvert x-y\rvert^{N-\alpha}}\,\mathrm{d}y&=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{\lvert u(x-y)\rvert^{s}}{\lvert y\rvert^{N-\alpha}}\,\mathrm{d}y\\
&=\int_{B_{1}}\frac{\lvert u(x-y)\rvert^{s}}{\lvert y\rvert^{N-\alpha}}\,\mathrm{d}y+\int_{B_{1}^{c}}\frac{\lvert u(x-y)\rvert^{s}}{\lvert y\rvert^{N-\alpha}}\,\mathrm{d}y.\end{split}

(3.1)

By choosing
t\in(\frac{N}{\alpha},\frac{N}{s\alpha}\frac{2N}{N-2})
and

such that

,
we deduce that
\lvert x\rvert^{\alpha-N}\in L^{\frac{t}{t-1}}(B_{1})
and
\lvert x\rvert^{\alpha-N}\in L^{\frac{r}{r-1}}(B^{c}_{1})
.
Here and in the sequel, the notation
B_{r}
denotes the ball in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
with radius

and centered at the origin.
By the Hölder inequality, we have

\begin{split}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{\lvert u(y)\rvert^{s}}{\lvert x-y\rvert^{N-\alpha}}\,\mathrm{d}y&\leq\Big(\int_{B_{1}}\lvert x\rvert^{\frac{t(\alpha-N)}{t-1}}\,\mathrm{d}x\Big)^{\frac{t-1}{t}}\Big(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\lvert u\rvert^{st}\Big)^{\frac{1}{t}}\\
&\quad+\Big(\int_{B^{c}_{1}}\lvert x\rvert^{\frac{r(\alpha-N)}{r-1}}\,\mathrm{d}x\Big)^{\frac{r-1}{r}}\Big(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\lvert u\rvert^{sr}\Big)^{\frac{1}{r}}.\end{split}

(3.2)

Hence, (
3.1
) and (
3.2
) conclude the claim.
Note that
u\in W^{2,s}_{\rm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
for
s\geq 1
. We then derive by combining the Rellich–Kondrachov embedding theorem and a standard Schauder estimate that
u\in C^{2,\lambda}(B_{1}(x))
for some
\lambda\in(0,1)
and there exists a constant
C

such that

\lvert u(x)\rvert\leq\|u\|_{C^{1,\lambda}(B_{1}(x))}\leq C\|u\|_{W^{2,s}(B_{1}(x))}.

This leads to
\lim_{\lvert x\rvert\to+\infty}\lvert u(x)\rvert=0
. We thus have

since

.
Moreover, for

\begin{split}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}&\frac{\lvert u(y)\rvert^{s}}{\lvert x-y\rvert^{N-\alpha}}\,\mathrm{d}y=\int_{B_{\lvert x\rvert/2}}\frac{\lvert u(y)\rvert^{s}}{\lvert x-y\rvert^{N-\alpha}}\,\mathrm{d}y+\int_{B^{c}_{\lvert x\rvert/2}}\frac{\lvert u(y)\rvert^{s}}{\lvert x-y\rvert^{N-\alpha}}\,\mathrm{d}y\\

This gives that

We claim that

.
By the facts that
wW_{k_{1}}=W_{k_{1}}w
and

, we deduce

On the other hand, for every

, so that

.
We then have

\begin{split}&s\diamond U^{q}_{R}=\frac{1}{\lvert S_{q}\rvert}\sum_{n\in N_{k}}\big(\zeta_{R}U_{S_{q}}\big)(n^{-1}s_{1}sx-l_{G}Rq)-\big(\zeta_{R}U_{S_{q}}\big)(n^{-1}sx-l_{G}Rq)\\
&=\frac{1}{\lvert S_{q}\rvert}\sum_{n\in N_{k}}\big(\zeta_{R}U_{S_{q}}\big)(n^{-1}n_{s}x-l_{G}Rq)-\frac{1}{\lvert S_{q}\rvert}\sum_{n\in N_{k}}\big(\zeta_{R}U_{S_{q}}\big)(n^{-1}n_{s}^{\prime}s_{1}x-l_{G}Rq)\\
&=-\frac{1}{\lvert S_{q}\rvert}\sum_{n\in N_{k}}\big(\zeta_{R}U_{S_{q}}\big)(n^{-1}s_{1}x-l_{G}Rq)+\frac{1}{\lvert S_{q}\rvert}\sum_{n\in N_{k}}\big(\zeta_{R}U_{S_{q}}\big)(n^{-1}x-l_{G}Rq)=-U_{R}^{q}.\end{split}

By the construction, we easily see

for any

We now prove that

for sufficiently large

. To this end, we denote

for given

and
{z}_{j}\in Gq
. Note that all of the balls
B^{j}_{2R}
for

are separated from each other.
We then deduce that

\begin{split}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}&(I_{\alpha}*F(U_{R}^{q}))F(U_{R}^{q})=A_{\alpha}\sum_{z_{j}\in Gq}\sum_{z_{m}\in Gq}\int_{B^{j}_{2R}}\int_{B^{m}_{2R}}K_{jm}(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y\\
&=A_{\alpha}\sum_{z_{j}\in Gq}\int_{B^{j}_{2R}}\int_{B^{j}_{2R}}K_{jj}(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y+A_{\alpha}\sum_{z_{j}\neq z_{m}\in Gq}\int_{B^{j}_{2R}}\int_{B^{m}_{2R}}K_{jm}(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y\end{split}

where the function
K_{jm}:\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
is defined by

K_{jm}(x,y):=\frac{F\big((\zeta_{R}U_{R}^{q})(x-l_{G}Rz_{j})\big)F\big((\zeta_{R}U_{R}^{q})(y-l_{G}Rz_{m})\big)}{\lvert x-y\rvert^{N-\alpha}}.

Moreover, we observe that

(K_{1}l_{G}-4)R\leq\lvert x-y\rvert\leq(K_{2}l_{G}+4)R

for any

and
y\in B^{m}_{2R}
with

.
It then follows that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}(I_{\alpha}*F(U^{q}_{R}))F(U^{q}_{R})

\displaystyle\geq{\lvert Gq\rvert}\int_{B_{2R}}\int_{B_{2R}}A_{\alpha}\frac{F\big((\zeta_{R}U_{S_{q}})(x)\big)F\big((\zeta_{R}U_{S_{q}})(y)\big)}{\lvert x-y\rvert^{N-\alpha}}

(4.1)

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{z_{j}\neq z_{m}\in Gq}\frac{}{R^{N-\alpha}}\int_{B^{j}_{2R}}F\big((\zeta_{R}U_{S_{q}})(x-l_{G}Rz_{j})\big)\,\mathrm{d}x\int_{B^{m}_{2R}}F\big((\zeta_{R}U_{S_{q}})(y-l_{G}Rz_{m})\big)\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle={\lvert Gq\rvert}\int_{B_{2R}}\int_{B_{2R}}A_{\alpha}\frac{F\big((\zeta_{R}U_{S_{q}})(x)\big)F\big(\zeta_{R}U_{S_{q}})(y)\big)}{\lvert x-y\rvert^{N-\alpha}}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y+\frac{}{R^{N-\alpha}}\Big(\int_{B_{2R}}F\big(\zeta_{R}U_{S_{q}}\big)\Big)^{2}.

We now estimate the first term that

\begin{split}&\int_{B_{2R}}\int_{B_{2R}}A_{\alpha}\frac{F\big((\zeta_{R}U_{S_{q}})(x)\big)F\big(\zeta_{R}U_{S_{q}})(y)\big)}{\lvert x-y\rvert^{N-\alpha}}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}(I_{\alpha}*F(\zeta_{R}U_{S_{q}})F(\zeta_{R}U_{S_{q}})\\
&\geq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}(I_{\alpha}*F(U_{S_{q}})F(U_{S_{q}})-2\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\big(I_{\alpha}*F(U_{S_{q}})\big)\big(F(U_{S_{q}})-F(\zeta_{R}U_{S_{q}})\big).\end{split}

(4.2)

In view of the Hardy–Littlewood–Sobolev inequality
[
21
, Theorem 4.3]
, we deduce that

\displaystyle\Big|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}(I_{\alpha}*F(U_{S_{q}})

\displaystyle\big(F(U_{S_{q}})-F(\zeta_{R}U_{S_{q}})\big)\Big|

\displaystyle\leq\Big(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\lvert F(U_{S_{q}})\rvert^{\frac{2N}{N+\alpha}}\int_{B_{R}^{c}}\big(\lvert F(U_{S_{q}})\rvert+\lvert F(\zeta_{R}U_{S_{q}})\rvert\big)^{\frac{2N}{N+\alpha}}\Big)^{\frac{N+\alpha}{2N}}.

By the assumptions
(F_{1})
–
(F_{2})
, we see that there exists

such that for all

this implies that
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\lvert F(U_{S_{q}})\rvert^{\frac{2N}{N+\alpha}}
is bounded
and

\int_{B_{R}^{c}}\lvert F(\zeta_{R}U_{S_{q}})\rvert^{\frac{2N}{N+\alpha}},\;\int_{B_{R}^{c}}\lvert F(U_{S_{q}})\rvert^{\frac{2N}{N+\alpha}}\leq\int_{B_{R}^{c}}\lvert U_{S_{q}}\rvert^{2}+\int_{B_{R}^{c}}\lvert U_{S_{q}}\rvert^{\frac{2N}{N-2}}.

Noticing that
U_{S_{q}}
decays exponential at infinity, we conclude that

2\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}(I_{\alpha}*F(U_{S_{q}}))\big(F(U_{S_{q}})-F(\zeta_{R}U_{S_{q}})\big)=o(\frac{1}{R^{N-\alpha}}).

(4.3)

Similarly, by the assumptions
(F_{1})
and
(F_{2})
again we derive that

\lvert F(t)\rvert\leq C(\lvert t\rvert^{2}+\lvert t\rvert^{\frac{N+\alpha}{N-2}})
for some positive constant
C
,
so that

\frac{1}{R^{N-\alpha}}\Big(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}F(U_{S_{q}})\Big)^{2}-\frac{1}{R^{N-\alpha}}\Big(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}F(\zeta_{R}U_{S_{q}})\Big)^{2}=o(\frac{1}{R^{N-\alpha}}).

(4.4)

By combining the above (
4
)–(
4.4
), we arrive at an asymptotic lower bound for

that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}(I_{\alpha}*F(U^{q}_{R}))F(U_{R}^{q})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\lvert G\rvert}{\lvert S_{q}\rvert}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}(I_{\alpha}*F(U_{S_{q}}))F(U_{S_{q}})+\frac{}{R^{N-\alpha}}\Big(\int_{B_{R}}F(\zeta_{R}U_{S_{q}})\Big)^{2}+o(\frac{1}{R^{N-\alpha}})

\displaystyle=\frac{\lvert G\rvert}{\lvert S_{q}\rvert}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}(I_{\alpha}*F(U_{S_{q}}))F(U_{S_{q}})+\frac{}{R^{N-\alpha}}\Big(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}F(U_{S_{q}})\Big)^{2}+o\big(\frac{1}{R^{N-\alpha}}\big).

(4.5)

This completes the claim. By Lemma
2.2
, there exist a path
\gamma_{R}\in C([0,+\infty);\mathcal{H}_{G})
and a unique

such that

\mathcal{P}(\gamma_{R}(t_{R}))=0
so that
\gamma_{R}(t_{R})\in\mathcal{P}_{G}
.
Direct calculations give us that

\frac{N-2}{2}t_{R}^{N-2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\lvert\nabla U^{q}_{R}\rvert^{2}+\frac{N}{2}t_{R}^{N}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\lvert U_{R}^{q}\rvert^{2}=\frac{N+\alpha}{2}t_{R}^{N+\alpha}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}(I_{\alpha}*F(U^{q}_{R}))F(U^{q}_{R}).

(4.6)

On the other hand, we deduce by integration by parts that

\begin{split}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\lvert\nabla U^{q}_{R}\rvert^{2}+\lvert U^{q}_{R}\rvert^{2}&=\sum_{z_{j}\in Gq}\int_{B_{2R}^{j}}\lvert\nabla U^{q}_{R}\rvert^{2}+\lvert U^{q}_{R}\rvert^{2}=\frac{\lvert G\rvert}{\lvert S_{q}\rvert}\int_{B_{2R}}\lvert\nabla\big(\zeta_{R}U_{S_{q}}\big)\rvert^{2}+\lvert\zeta_{R}U_{S_{q}}\rvert^{2}\\
&=\frac{\lvert G\rvert}{\lvert S_{q}\rvert}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\zeta_{R}^{2}(\lvert\nabla U_{S_{q}}\rvert^{2}+\lvert U_{S_{q}}\rvert^{2})-\frac{\lvert G\rvert}{\lvert S_{q}\rvert}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\zeta_{R}(\Delta\zeta_{R})\lvert U_{S_{q}}\rvert^{2}\\
&\leq\frac{\lvert G\rvert}{\lvert S_{q}\rvert}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\lvert\nabla U_{S_{q}}\rvert^{2}+\lvert U_{S_{q}}\rvert^{2}+\frac{}{R^{2}}\int_{B_{2R}\setminus B_{R}}\lvert U_{S_{q}}\rvert^{2}.\end{split}

Again, the exponential decay of the solution
U_{S_{q}}
implies that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\big(\frac{N-2}{2}\lvert\nabla U^{q}_{R}\rvert^{2}+\frac{N}{2}\lvert U^{q}_{R}\rvert^{2}\big)\leq\lvert Gq\rvert\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\big(\frac{N-2}{2}\lvert\nabla U_{S_{q}}\rvert^{2}+\frac{N}{2}\lvert U_{S_{q}}\rvert^{2}\big)+o\big(\frac{1}{R^{N-\alpha}}\big).

(4.7)

Taking into account of these estimates (
4
) and (
4.7
), we then derive from (
4.6
)
that

is uniformly bounded in

. We thus deduce an asymptotic bound that

\begin{split}\mathcal{E}(\gamma_{R}(t_{R}))&=\frac{t_{R}^{N-2}}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\lvert\nabla U_{R}^{q}\rvert+\frac{t_{R}^{N}}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\lvert U_{R}^{q}\rvert^{2}-\frac{t_{R}^{N+\alpha}}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}(I_{\alpha}*F(U_{R}^{q}))F(U_{R}^{q})\\
&\leq\lvert Gq\rvert\mathcal{E}(\gamma_{U_{S_{q}}}(t_{R}))-\frac{C}{R^{N-\alpha}}+o(\frac{1}{R^{N-\alpha}})\\
&\leq\lvert Gq\rvert\max_{t\geq 0}\mathcal{E}(\gamma_{U_{S_{q}}}(t))-\frac{C}{R^{N-\alpha}}+o(\frac{1}{R^{N-\alpha}})\\
&=\lvert Gq\rvert\mathcal{E}(U_{S_{q}})-\frac{C}{R^{N-\alpha}}+o(\frac{1}{R^{N-\alpha}}).\end{split}

The desired conclusion
c_{G}<{\lvert Gq\rvert}c_{S_{q}}
follows provided that

is sufficiently large.
We note that the Proposition
2.4
is used in the final equality that

achieves

. We also mention the Lagrange’s theorem on the finite group that
\lvert G\rvert=\lvert Gq\rvert\lvert S_{q}\rvert.

We now prove that, up to translations, the Pohozaev–Palais–Smale sequence constructed in Proposition
2.3
has a nontrivial weak limit below
the energy level
c_{G}
.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
N\geq 2
,
\alpha\in(0,N)
and
G\in\mathcal{G}_{k}
with
1\leq k\leq N
. Assume that the sequence

(u_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathcal{H}_{G}
satisfies as
n\to\infty
,

\mathcal{E}(u_{n})\to c_{G},\quad\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(u_{n})\to 0\quad\text{\rm{ strongly in } }\mathcal{H}_{G}^{*}\quad\text{ \rm{and} }\mathcal{P}(u_{n})\to 0.

Then, there exists a sequence of points
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\{0\}\times\mathbb{R}^{N-k}\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
such that, up to a subsequence,

(u_{n}(\cdot-a_{n}))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converges weakly to
u\in\mathcal{H}_{G}
.
Moreover,

0<\mathcal{E}(u)\leq c_{G}\qquad\text{\rm{and}}\qquad\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(u)=0\quad\text{\rm{in}}\quad\mathcal{H}_{G}^{*}.

Proof.

The sequence
(u_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is bounded in
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
because we have

(\alpha+2)A(u_{n})+\alpha B(u_{n})=2(N+\alpha)\mathcal{E}(u_{n})-2\mathcal{P}(u_{n})+o(1).

We claim that there exists
R>0
such that for some
p\in(\frac{N+\alpha}{N},\frac{N+\alpha}{N-2})

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\int_{O_{R}}\lvert u_{n}\rvert^{\frac{2Np}{N+\alpha}}>0.

(5.1)

Here
O_{R}:=[-R,R]^{k}\times\mathbb{R}^{N-k}
. We acknowledge this claim (
5.1
) temporarily and first finish the proof of the lemma.
Taking a function
\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
such that
\varphi=1
on
O_{R}
,
\supp\varphi\subset O_{3R/2}
,
0\leq\varphi\leq 1
on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
and

\nabla\varphi\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
, we then deduce from
[
23
, Lemma 1.1]
(see also
[
26
, Lemma 2.3]
or
[
35
, Lemma 1.21]
)
that there exists a constant
K_{G}>0
such that

\begin{split}\int_{O_{R}}\lvert u_{n}\rvert^{\frac{2Np}{N+\alpha}}&\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\lvert\varphi u_{n}\rvert^{\frac{2Np}{N+\alpha}}\\
&\leq\Big(\sup_{a\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\int_{B_{R/2}(a)}\lvert\varphi u_{n}\rvert^{\frac{2Np}{N+\alpha}}\Big)^{1-\frac{N+\alpha}{Np}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\lvert\nabla(\varphi u_{n})\rvert^{2}+\lvert\varphi u_{n}\rvert^{2}\\
&\leq\Big(\sup_{a\in\{0\}\times\mathbb{R}^{N-k}}\int_{B_{K_{G}R}(a)}\lvert u_{n}\rvert^{\frac{2Np}{N+\alpha}}\Big)^{1-\frac{N+\alpha}{Np}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\lvert\nabla u_{n}\rvert^{2}+\lvert u_{n}\rvert^{2}.\end{split}

Hence, there exists a sequence of points
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\{0\}\times\mathbb{R}^{N-k}
such that

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\int_{B_{K_{G}R}(a_{n})}\lvert u_{n}\rvert^{\frac{2Np}{N+\alpha}}>0.

We then conclude by a similar argument as in
[
27
, Proposition 2.2]
that, up to translations and a subsequence,
(u_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converges weakly to some function
u\in\mathcal{H}_{G}\setminus\{0\}
and
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(u)=0
in
\mathcal{H}_{G}^{*}
. We thus conclude that
\mathcal{P}(u)=0
.
By the weakly lower semicontinuity of the norm, we are left that
\mathcal{E}(u)\leq c_{G}
. In fact, we have

\begin{split}c_{G}&=\lim_{n\to\infty}\Big(\mathcal{E}(u_{n})-\frac{1}{N+\alpha}\mathcal{P}(u_{n})\Big)\\
&=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\alpha}{2(N+\alpha)}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\lvert\nabla u_{n}\rvert^{2}+\frac{\alpha}{2(N+\alpha)}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\lvert u_{n}\rvert^{2}\\
&\geq\frac{\alpha}{2(N+\alpha)}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\lvert\nabla u\rvert^{2}+\lvert u\rvert^{2}=\mathcal{E}(u)-\frac{1}{N+\alpha}\mathcal{P}(u)=\mathcal{E}(u).\end{split}

We now return to finish the proof of the claim (
5.1
). Up to a subsequence, there exists
\Lambda\in(0,+\infty)
such that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\lvert u_{n}\rvert^{\frac{2Np}{N+\alpha}}=\Lambda.

Note that the sequence
(u_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is bounded in
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
. The Rellich–Kondrachov embedding theorem guarantees the finiteness of
\Lambda
.
We suppose that
\Lambda=0
. By
(F_{1})
and
(F_{2})
, we deduce that for any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
C_{\varepsilon}
such that

\lvert F(u)\rvert\leq\varepsilon(\lvert u\rvert^{\frac{N+\alpha}{N}}+\lvert u\rvert^{\frac{N+\alpha}{N-2}})+C_{\varepsilon}\lvert u\rvert^{p}.

By the Hardy–Littlewood–Sobolev inequality and by the Rellich–Kondrachov embedding theorem, we derive that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}(I_{\alpha}*F(u_{n}))F(u_{n})=0.

Inserting this into

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{P}(u_{n})=0
, we deduce
\lim_{n\to\infty}\|u_{n}\|=0
, then a contradiction follows that

c_{G}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{E}(u_{n})=0.

Since
G=\langle\mathcal{S}\rangle
, in the subsequent parts of this paper, we
assume each of the hyperplane fixed by the reflection

passing through the origin and
\mathcal{F}

always refer to the strict fundamental domain under the group action

, see Appendix. Without loss of generality, we can fix

to be the infinite cone formed by

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\int_{B_{r}(y)}\lvert u_{n}\rvert^{\frac{2Np}{N+\alpha}}>0.

Otherwise, by Lions’ Lemma (e.g.,
[

In fact, if

, we can choose any

; if

, then we take a reflection

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\int_{(B_{R}(\mathcal{R}x_{n}))}\lvert u_{n}\rvert^{\frac{2Np}{N+\alpha}}=\liminf_{n\to\infty}\int_{B_{R}(x_{n})}\lvert u_{n}\rvert^{\frac{2Np}{N+\alpha}}\geq\Lambda-\varepsilon.

For this situation, we also assume by the symmetry that

.

Case 1
: If there exists

such that

(y_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset B_{R_{1}}\cap\mathcal{F}
,
we then conclude the claim (

.

For the subsequent proofs, we always assume the cut-off function

.

The subsequent proofs will be completed by splitting into several cases according to the asymptotic distance between the sequence

Case 2

u=\mathcal{U}(\chi_{\mathcal{F}}u),\;\;\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{U}(v))=\mathcal{E}_{0}(v),\;\;\text{ and }\;\;\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{U}(v))=\mathcal{P}_{0}(v).

These facts suggest that
\inf_{\mathcal{P}_{0}}\mathcal{E}_{0}=\inf_{\mathcal{P}_{G}}\mathcal{E}=c_{G}
,
where

\mathcal{P}_{0}=\{v\in H^{1}_{0}(\mathcal{F})\setminus\{0\}\,|\,\mathcal{P}_{0}(v)=0\}
.

Let
u
be the nodal solution that achieves
c_{G}
, then
w:=\chi_{\mathcal{F}}u\in H^{1}_{0}(\mathcal{F})
and

\mathcal{E}_{0}(w)=\inf_{\mathcal{P}_{0}}\mathcal{E}_{0}=c_{G}
.
Note that
\lvert w\rvert\in H^{1}_{0}(\mathcal{F})
. Since
f
is odd,
F
is even and we thus deduce that

\mathcal{E}_{0}(\lvert w\rvert)=\mathcal{E}_{0}(w)=c_{G}\;\;\text{ and }\;\;\mathcal{P}_{0}(\lvert w\rvert)=\mathcal{P}_{0}(w)=0.

We thus assume that
w\geq 0
in
\mathcal{F}
. On the other hand,

\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{U}(w))=\mathcal{P}_{0}(w)=0
and
\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{U}(w))=\mathcal{E}_{0}(w)=c_{G}
imply that
\mathcal{U}(w)\in\mathcal{P}_{G}
also achieves
c_{G}=p_{G}
, thus
\mathcal{U}(w)
is a weak solution to the Choquard equation
(

C

), see
[
27
, Lemma 5.1]
.
By Theorem
1.2
, we infer that
\mathcal{U}(w)\in C^{2}
. Therefore,
\mathcal{U}(w)

satisfies in the classical sense that
-\Delta\mathcal{U}(w)+\mathcal{U}(w)\geq 0
in
\mathcal{F}
,
which, together with the strong maximum principle for classical supersolutions (see
[
10
, Theorem 3.5]
), yields
\mathcal{U}(w)>0
everywhere in

{\rm{int}}(\mathcal{F})
.
From the definition of
\mathcal{U}
, we see that
u>0
everywhere in
{\rm{int}}(\mathcal{F})
. Thus
u
has exactly

\lvert G\rvert
number of nodal domains in the whole space
\mathbb{R}^{N}
.

We finally complete the existence of the global minimal nodal solutions.

Proof of Theorem
1.4
.

We consider the minimization problem on the nodal solution set,

c_{\rm{nod}}=\inf_{u\in\mathcal{M}_{\rm{nod}}}\mathcal{E}(u),

with the constraint being defined by
\mathcal{M}_{\rm{nod}}=\{u\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})\,|\,u^{\pm}\neq 0,\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(u)=0\}
.
Clearly we have by Theorem
1.1
that the
{\bf{A}_{1}}
-groundstates do exist, which leads us
c_{0}\leq c_{\rm{nod}}\leq c_{\bf{A}_{1}}<2c_{0}
, here
c_{0}
refers to the ground state energy of the Choquard equation (

C

). Moreover,
c_{0}=\inf_{\mathcal{N}}\mathcal{E}
where
\mathcal{N}
is the Neari manifold. This can be done due to our monotonic assumption
(F^{\prime}_{1})
.

Taking a minimizing sequence
(v_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathcal{M}_{\rm{nod}}
so that
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(v_{n})=0
and
\mathcal{E}(v_{n})\to c_{\rm{nod}}
as
n\to\infty
. By combining the Pohozaev identity, we obtain the boundedness of
(v_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
.
By the assumptions
(F^{\prime}_{1})
and
(F^{\prime}_{2})
, there exists some constant
C>0
such that

\lvert F(t)\rvert,\lvert f(t)t\rvert\leq C(\lvert t\rvert^{r}+\lvert t\rvert^{p}).

This, together with the facts that
\langle\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(v_{n}),v_{n}^{\pm}\rangle=0
and the Hardy–Littlewood–Sobolev inequality, implies that

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\|v_{n}^{\pm}\|>0
.
We now claim that

\sum_{p_{1},p_{2}\in\{r,p\}}\liminf_{n\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}(I_{\alpha}*\lvert v_{n}^{+}\rvert^{p_{1}})\lvert v_{n}^{-}\rvert^{p_{2}}>0.

Otherwise, we deduce by the assumptions
(F^{\prime}_{1})
and
(F^{\prime}_{2})
again that

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}(I_{\alpha}*F(v_{n}^{\pm}))F(v_{n}^{\mp})\leq C\sum_{p_{1},p_{2}\in\{r,p\}}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}(I_{\alpha}*\lvert v_{n}^{+}\rvert^{p_{1}})\lvert v_{n}^{-}\rvert^{p_{2}}=0

and

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}(I_{\alpha}*F(v_{n}^{\pm}))f(v_{n}^{\mp})v_{n}^{\mp}\leq C\sum_{p_{1},p_{2}\in\{r,p\}}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}(I_{\alpha}*\lvert v_{n}^{+}\rvert^{p_{1}})\lvert v_{n}^{-}\rvert^{p_{2}}=0

We therefrom obtain that

\mathcal{E}(v_{n})=\mathcal{E}(v_{n}^{+})+\mathcal{E}(v_{n}^{-})+o(1),\;\text{ and }\;\langle\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(v_{n}^{\pm}),v_{n}^{\pm}\rangle=\langle\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(v_{n}),v_{n}^{\pm}\rangle+o(1)=o(1).

It then follows that there exist
s_{n}^{\pm}\in(0,+\infty)
with

\lim_{n\to\infty}s_{n}^{\pm}=1
such that
s^{\pm}_{n}v_{n}^{\pm}\in\mathcal{N}
. This leads a contradiction that

\displaystyle 2c_{0}>c_{\rm{nod}}+o(1)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{E}(v_{n})=\mathcal{E}(v_{n}^{+})+\mathcal{E}(v_{n}^{-})+o(1)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{E}(s_{n}^{+}v_{n}^{+})+\mathcal{E}(s_{n}^{-}v_{n}^{-})+o(1)\geq 2c_{0}+o(1).

By adopting the proof of
[
8
, Lemma 3.6]
, we conclude that there exist
R>0
and one pair
(p_{1},p_{2})\in\{(r,r),(r,p),(p,r),(p,p)\}
such that

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\sup_{a\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\int_{B_{R}(a)}\lvert v^{+}_{n}\rvert^{\frac{2Np_{1}}{N+\alpha}}\int_{B_{R}(a)}\lvert v_{n}^{-}\rvert^{\frac{2Np_{2}}{N+\alpha}}>0.

Up to a translation and a subsequence, we can assume that

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\int_{B_{R}(0)}\lvert v_{n}^{+}\rvert^{\frac{2Np_{1}}{N+\alpha}}>0,\text{ and }\liminf_{n\to\infty}\int_{B_{R}(0)}\lvert v_{n}^{-}\rvert^{\frac{2Np_{2}}{N+\alpha}}>0.

This means that
v_{n}\rightharpoonup v\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
and
v^{\pm}\neq 0
. In particular,
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(v)=0
and
\mathcal{P}(v)=0
. We then deduce by the weakly lower semicontinuity of the norm that

\displaystyle c_{\rm{nod}}

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{E}(v)=\mathcal{E}(v)-\frac{1}{N+\alpha}\mathcal{P}(v)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha}{2(N+\alpha)}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\lvert\nabla v\rvert^{2}+\frac{2+\alpha}{2(N+\alpha)}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\lvert v\rvert^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\alpha}{2(N+\alpha)}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\lvert\nabla v_{n}\rvert^{2}+\frac{2+\alpha}{2(N+\alpha)}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\lvert v_{n}\rvert^{2}+o(1)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{E}(v_{n})-\frac{1}{N+\alpha}\mathcal{P}(v_{n})+o(1)=\mathcal{E}(v_{n})+o(1).

Hence, the infimum value
c_{\rm{nod}}
is achieved by the sign-changing solution
v
and then the strongly convergence follows.

Appendix A
Finite Coxeter groups

In this section, we shall recall some fundamental theory on Coxeter groups including the classification result and the Coxeter diagram. Most of these background knowledge are contained in the references
[
5
,
31
]
. We only list some definitions and results because the related proofs can be found either in
[
5
,
31
]
or in the previous work
[
37
]
. The first concept is the reflection.

Definition A.1
.

[
5
, Definition 6.1.1]

A linear reflection on a vector space
V
is a linear automorphism
r:V\to V
such that
r^{2}=1
and such the fixed subspace
H_{r}
is a hyperplane.

With the help of reflections, we introduce the formal definition of Coxeter groups.

Definition A.2
.

[
31
, Definition 1.18]

Let
I
be a finite indexing set with cardinal number
\lvert I\rvert=k
and
\mathcal{S}=\{s_{i}\}_{i\in I}
.
A Coxeter matrix
M=(m_{ij})_{i,j\in I}
on the set
\mathcal{S}
is a
k\times k

symmetric matrix with entries in
\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
such that
m_{ii}=1
for all
i\in I
and
m_{ij}\geq 2
for all distinct
i,j\in I
.
The associated Coxeter group
G=G_{\mathcal{S}}
is defined by the presentation

G=\langle\mathcal{S}\,|\,s_{i}^{2}=1,\,\forall i\in I\;\text{ and }\;(s_{i}s_{j})^{m_{ij}}=1,\;\text{ for }i\neq j\in I\rangle.

The set
\mathcal{S}
is a Coxeter generating set for
G
and the cardinality
\lvert I\rvert
is called the rank of
G
.

The following Coxeter diagram is a useful tool in classifying the finite Coxeter groups.

Definition A.3
.

[
5
, Definition 3.5.1]

Suppose that
M=(m_{ij})
is a Coxeter matrix on a set
I
.
We associate to
M
a graph
\Upsilon
called its Coxeter graph. The vertex set
of
\Upsilon
is
I
. Distinct vertices
i
and
j
are connected by an edge if and only if

m_{ij}\geq 3
. The edge
{i,j}
is labeled by
m_{ij}
if
m_{ij}\geq 4
. If
m_{ij}=3
, the edge is left
unlabeled. The graph together with the labeling of its edges is called the Coxeter diagram associated to
M
.

The following proposition (see e.g.,
[
5
, Theorem 4.1.6 and Proposition 4.1.7]
) shows that
the study of finite Coxeter groups can be largely reduced to the case when the Coxeter is irreducible, which means its diagram is connected.

Proposition A.4
.

For each
\mathcal{T}\subset\mathcal{S}
,
G_{\mathcal{T}}
is a Coxeter group. Moreover,
suppose
\mathcal{S}
can be partitioned into two nonempty disjoint subsets
\mathcal{T}
and
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
such that
(st)^{2}=1
for all
s\in\mathcal{T}
and
t\in\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
. Then

G=G_{\mathcal{T}}\times G_{\mathcal{T}^{\prime}}
.

As a result, any finite Coxeter group
G
is the direct products of some irreducible Coxeter groups.

.

For every finite irreducible Coxeter group

We finally recall the fundamental domain for the Coxeter group

\partial^{i}\mathcal{F}

\mathcal{F}

Let

Lemma A.9

Let
