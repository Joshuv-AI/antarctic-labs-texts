Scalar products of Bethe vectors in the generalized algebraic Bethe ansatz


Abstract
We consider an X ​ Y ​ Z XYZ spin chain within the framework of the generalized algebraic Bethe ansatz. We study scalar products of the transfer matrix eigenvectors and arbitrary Bethe vectors. In the particular case of free fermions we obtain explicit expressions for the scalar products with different number of parameters in two Bethe vectors.

The calculation of scalar products within the framework of the algebraic Bethe ansatz

is an important task. The availability of simple and compact formulas for scalar products of Bethe vectors allows one to study the form factors and correlation functions of quantum integrable models. Several interesting results in this direction have been obtained in models with rational and trigonometric
R
-matrices

[
4
,
5
,
6
,

,

,

,

To study the fully anisotropic
XYZ
Heisenberg chain
[
20
]
, the generalized algebraic Bethe ansatz is used
[
2
]
. This is because the
XYZ
chain
has an
R
-matrix of an 8-vertex model
[
21
,
22
,
23
,
24
]
. As a result, the corresponding monodromy matrix does not have a vacuum vector, which is necessary for constructing the eigenvectors of the transfer matrix in the framework of the traditional algebraic Bethe ansatz.

The generalized algebraic Bethe ansatz allows one to construct eigenvectors of the transfer matrix, as well as to obtain Bethe equations that determine the spectrum of the Hamiltonian. At the same time, the problem of studying the scalar products of generalized Bethe vectors acquires an extremely complex technical character and, until recently, practically has not been researched. Progress was made after the development of a new method based on the reduction of scalar products to a system of linear equations
[
25
]
. For the generalized algebraic Bethe ansatz, this method allowed us to obtain compact determinant representations for the scalar products of the transfer matrix eigenvectors (on-shell Bethe vectors) and arbitrary (off-shell) Bethe vectors depending on the same number of parameters
[
26
]
. In this paper, we call such scalar products
balanced
.

However, further research in this direction showed that such scalar products are not enough to calculate the form factors of local spin operators. The latter can be reduced to the form factors of the matrix elements of the monodromy matrix using the quantum inverse problem
[
28
,
29
,
30
]
. In turn, actions of the elements of the monodromy matrix on the initial on-shell Bethe vector generate linear combinations of off-shell Bethe vectors, in which the number of parameters may differ by one from the original one
[
27
]
. As a result, scalar products arise in which the number of parameters in the left and right vectors do not coincide. This article is devoted to the calculation of such
imbalanced
scalar products.

We use the method of reducing scalar products to a system of linear equations. In principle, this method allows one to obtain the result for the
XYZ
chain for general values of the coupling constants. However, we are facing technical difficulties that have not yet been overcome. Therefore, in this paper, we consider a special case of the
XYZ
chain, in which it is equivalent to free fermions (the
XY
chain)
[
32
,
34
,
33
,
35
,
36
,
37
,
38
,
39
,
40
]
. We hope to generalize the obtained results in the future.

The paper is organized as follows. In section
2
, we give a brief description of the generalized algebraic Bethe ansatz. Here we introduce a gauge transformation of the monodromy matrix and construct the Bethe vectors. We also introduce here scalar products of Bethe vectors. In section
3
we derive a cascading system of equations, which relates scalar products with different imbalances. In section
4
we consider scalar products with imbalance
\pm 2
(that is, the number of parameters in the left and right vectors differs by
\pm 2
). We prove that these scalar products vanish. This observation allows us
to express scalar products with imbalance
\pm 1

At the end of this paper we have collected basic information about Jacobi theta functions in appendix
A

In appendix

we discuss non-trivial solutions of special homogenous systems. Finally, in appendix

integral method that allows us to calculate certain sums containing theta functions.

2
Generalized algebraic Bethe ansatz for the XYZ model

In this section, we provide basic information about the description of the
XYZ
model by the generalized algebraic Bethe ansatz.
The reader can get acquainted with this method in more detail in works
[
2
,
26
]
.

2.1
R
-matrix and monodromy matrix

The
XYZ
spin chain is equivalent to an 8-vertex model. The corresponding
8-vertex
R
-matrix has the following form:

R(u)=\begin{pmatrix}a(u)&0&0&d(u)\\
0&b(u)&c(u)&0\\
0&c(u)&b(u)&0\\
d(u)&0&0&a(u)\\
\end{pmatrix},

(2.1)

where

\displaystyle a(u)=\frac{2\theta_{4}(\eta|2\tau)\,\theta_{1}(u+\eta|2\tau)\,\theta_{4}(u|2\tau)}{\theta_{2}(0|\tau)\,\theta_{4}(0|2\tau)},

(2.2)

\displaystyle b(u)=\frac{2\theta_{4}(\eta|2\tau)\,\theta_{4}(u+\eta|2\tau)\,\theta_{1}(u|2\tau)}{\theta_{2}(0|\tau)\,\theta_{4}(0|2\tau)},

\displaystyle c(u)=\frac{2\theta_{1}(\eta|2\tau)\,\theta_{4}(u+\eta|2\tau)\,\theta_{4}(u|2\tau)}{\theta_{2}(0|\tau)\,\theta_{4}(0|2\tau)},

\displaystyle d(u)=\frac{2\theta_{1}(\eta|2\tau)\,\theta_{1}(u+\eta|2\tau)\,\theta_{1}(u|2\tau)}{\theta_{2}(0|\tau)\,\theta_{4}(0|2\tau)}.

The definition of the Jacobi theta functions is given in appendix
A
.

To define a monodromy matrix we first introduce a quantum Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
and an auxiliary space
\mathcal{H}_{0}
. The first one is a tensor
product of local quantum spaces
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{2}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathcal{H}_{N}
. In its turn,
\mathcal{H}_{0}\cong\mathbb{C}^{2}
, and each
\mathcal{H}_{k}\cong\mathbb{C}^{2}
.

Then the monodromy matrix of the
XYZ
chain of the length
N
is defined as a product of the
R
-matrices acting in
\mathcal{H}_{0}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{k}
:

\mathcal{T}(u)=R_{01}(u-\xi_{1})R_{02}(u-\xi_{2})\cdots R_{0N}(u-\xi_{N}),

(2.3)

where complex parameters
\xi_{k}
are called inhomogeneities. In what follows, we will consider only chains with an even number of sites
N
.

The monodromy matrix (
2.3
) satisfies an

RTT
-relation

R_{12}(u-v)\mathcal{T}_{1}(u)\mathcal{T}_{2}(v)=\mathcal{T}_{2}(v)\mathcal{T}_{1}(u)R_{12}(u-v),

(2.4)

which holds in the tensor product
\mathbb{C}^{2}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{2}\otimes\mathcal{H}
. The subscripts in (
2.4
) show in which of the two auxiliary
spaces
\mathbb{C}^{2}
the monodromy matrix
\mathcal{T}_{k}
acts nontrivially. If we write down
the monodromy matrix as a
2\times 2
matrix in the auxiliary space
\mathcal{H}_{0}

\mathcal{T}(u)=\begin{pmatrix}A(u)&B(u)\\
C(u)&D(u)\end{pmatrix},

(2.5)

then relation (
2.4
) defines commutation relations between the
operators
A(u)
,
B(u)
,
C(u)
, and
D(u)
acting in
\mathcal{H}
.

The Hamiltonian
H
of the
XYZ
chain can be extracted from a transfer matrix
{\sf T}(u)
. The latter is the trace of the monodromy matrix with respect to the auxiliary space

{\sf T}(u)={\mathop{\rm tr}}_{0}\mathcal{T}(u)=A(u)+D(u).

(2.6)

It is the generating function of the integrals of motion. In particular, let us define
H
by

H=\frac{2\theta_{1}(\eta|\tau)}{\theta_{1}^{\prime}(0|\tau)}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}u}\log{\sf T}(u)\Bigr|_{u=0}-\frac{\theta_{1}^{\prime}(\eta|\tau)}{\theta^{\prime}_{1}(0|\tau)}N\mathbf{1},

(2.7)

where
\mathbf{1}
is the identity operator. Then in the homogeneous limit
\xi_{k}=0
,
k=1,\dots,N
, we obtain

H=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\Bigl(J_{x}\sigma^{x}_{j}\sigma^{x}_{j+1}+J_{y}\sigma^{y}_{j}\sigma^{y}_{j+1}+J_{z}\sigma^{z}_{j}\sigma^{z}_{j+1}\Bigr),\qquad\sigma^{x,y,z}_{N+1}=\sigma^{x,y,z}_{1},

(2.8)

where spin operators
\sigma^{x,y,z}_{k}
are Pauli matrices acting non-trivially in
\mathcal{H}_{k}
. Numerical coefficients
J_{x,y,z}
are given by

J_{x}=\frac{\theta_{4}(\eta|\tau)}{\theta_{4}(0|\tau)}\,,\quad J_{y}=\frac{\theta_{3}(\eta|\tau)}{\theta_{3}(0|\tau)}\,,\quad J_{z}=\frac{\theta_{2}(\eta|\tau)}{\theta_{2}(0|\tau)}\,.

They play the role of interaction strength along the axis
x
,
y
, and
z
.

Although only a homogeneous case is needed to construct the Hamiltonian of the
XYZ
chain, in what follows we will consider a more general
inhomogeneous model (
2.3
) with arbitrary complex inhomogeneities
\xi_{k}
. We emphasize, however, that we do this solely for reasons of generality.
In all the formulas below, the homogeneous limit is trivial.

In our calculations, we will mainly focus on the case
\eta=1/2
, which corresponds to
J_{z}=0
. However, many of the formulas below remain valid for
more general case of rational
\eta
.

2.2
Gauge transformed monodromy matrix and vacuum vectors

In models with a 6-vertex
R
-matrix, the eigenvectors of the transfer matrix (on-shell Bethe vectors) are constructed by applying creation operators to the vacuum
vector. There is no such vector in the
XYZ
model. Therefore, to construct Bethe vectors within the framework of the generalized algebraic Bethe ansatz, we need first to introduce generalized gauge-transformed monodromy matrices

[
2
,
26
]
. Let

\mathcal{T}_{k,l}(u)=M^{-1}_{k}(u)\mathcal{T}(u)M_{l}(u)=\begin{pmatrix}A_{k,l}(u)&B_{k,l}(u)\\
C_{k,l}(u)&D_{k,l}(u)\end{pmatrix}.

(2.9)

Here

M_{k}(u)=\begin{pmatrix}\theta_{1}(s_{k}+u|2\tau)&\gamma_{k}\theta_{1}(t_{k}-u|2\tau)\\
\theta_{4}(s_{k}+u|2\tau)&\gamma_{k}\theta_{4}(t_{k}-u|2\tau)\end{pmatrix},

(2.10)

where
s_{k}=s+k\eta
,
t_{k}=t+k\eta
,

s,t\in\mathbb{C}
are arbitrary parameters and

\gamma_{k}=\frac{2}{\theta_{2}(x_{k}|\tau)\theta_{2}(0|\tau)},\qquad\text{where}\qquad x_{k}=x+k\eta,\qquad x=\frac{s+t}{2}.

(2.11)

It is easy to check that

\det M_{k}(u)=\frac{2\theta_{1}(y+u|\tau)}{\theta_{2}(0|\tau)},\qquad\text{where}\qquad y=\frac{s-t}{2}.

(2.12)

For the gauge transformed monodromy matrices, there exists a vector whose properties are similar to those of the vacuum vector in the traditional algebraic Bethe ansatz.
Let us introduce a family of local vacuum vectors
|\omega_{k}^{l}\rangle
parameterized by integer
l
:

|\omega_{k}^{l}\rangle=\begin{pmatrix}\theta_{1}(s_{k+l-1}+\xi_{k}|2\tau)\\
\theta_{4}(s_{k+l-1}+\xi_{k}|2\tau)\end{pmatrix}\in\mathcal{H}_{k}.

(2.13)

The global vacuum vectors are then defined as

|\Omega^{l}\rangle=|\omega_{1}^{l}\rangle\otimes|\omega_{2}^{l}\rangle\otimes\ldots\otimes|\omega_{N}^{l}\rangle\quad\in\mathcal{H}.

(2.14)

Then one can check that

\displaystyle C_{l,l+N}(u)|\Omega^{l}\rangle=0,

(2.15)

\displaystyle A_{l,l+N}(u)|\Omega^{l}\rangle=a(u)|\Omega^{l+1}\rangle,

\displaystyle D_{l,l+N}(u)|\Omega^{l}\rangle=d(u)|\Omega^{l-1}\rangle,

where

a(u)=\prod_{k=1}^{N}\theta_{1}(u-\xi_{k}+\eta|\tau),\qquad d(u)=\prod_{k=1}^{N}\theta_{1}(u-\xi_{k}|\tau).

(2.16)

Similarly, we can define dual (left) local vacuum vectors by

\langle\bar{\omega}_{k}^{l}|=\Big(-\theta_{4}(t_{k+l}-\xi_{k}|2\tau);\theta_{1}(t_{k+l}-\xi_{k}|2\tau)\Big)\in\mathcal{H}^{*}_{k}\;.

(2.17)

The global dual (left) vacuum vectors are defined as tensor products of the local ones:

\langle\bar{\Omega}^{l}|=\langle\bar{\omega}_{1}^{l}|\otimes\langle\bar{\omega}_{2}^{l}|\otimes\ldots\otimes\langle\bar{\omega}_{N}^{l}|\quad\in\mathcal{H}^{*}.

(2.18)

The action of the operators
A_{l,l+N}(u)
,
D_{l,l+N}(u)
,
B_{l,l+N}(u)
to the left vacuum is given by

\displaystyle\langle\bar{\Omega}^{l}|B_{l,l+N}(u)=0,

(2.19)

\displaystyle\langle\bar{\Omega}^{l}|A_{l,l+N}(u)=\gamma_{l}\gamma_{l+N}^{-1}a(u)\langle\bar{\Omega}^{l-1}|,

\displaystyle\langle\bar{\Omega}^{l}|D_{l,l+N}(u)=\gamma_{l+N}\gamma_{l}^{-1}d(u)\langle\bar{\Omega}^{l+1}|.

The Bethe vectors are constructed by the successive action of the operators
B_{k,l}(u)
on the global vacuum vector.
The dual Bethe vectors are constructed by means of the operators
C_{k,l}(v)
(see below).

2.2.1
Bethe vectors

Before moving on, we introduce some new notation. Henceforth we will omit the modular parameter in the notation of theta functions
whenever it is equal to
\tau
, namely,
\theta_{a}(\cdot)\equiv\theta_{a}(\cdot|\tau)
.

Let us also introduce two functions that will be often used below

f(u,v)=\frac{\theta_{1}(u-v+\eta)}{\theta_{1}(u-v)},\qquad h(u,v)=\frac{\theta_{1}(u-v+\eta)}{\theta_{1}(\eta)}.

(2.20)

In what follows, we will constantly deal with sets of complex variables.
We denote these sets by a bar:
\bar{u}=\{u_{1},\dots,u_{m}\}
,
\bar{v}=\{v_{1},\dots,v_{n}\}
, etc. As a rule, the number of elements in the
sets is not shown explicitly in the equations, however, we give these cardinalities in
special comments to the formulas. We also introduce special subsets
\bar{u}_{j}=\bar{u}\setminus\{u_{j}\}
,
\bar{u}_{j,k}=\bar{u}\setminus\{u_{j},u_{k}\}
and so on.

In order to make the formulas more compact we use a shorthand notation for products of functions (
2.20
).
Namely, if the functions
f
or
h
depend on a set (or two sets) of variables, this means that one should take the product over the corresponding set.
For example,

f(u_{j},\bar{u}_{j})=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}u_{l}\in\bar{u}\\
l\neq j\end{subarray}}f(u_{j},u_{l}),\quad f(\bar{v},\bar{u})=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}u_{l}\in\bar{u}\\
v_{k}\in\bar{v}\end{subarray}}f(v_{k},u_{l})\qquad\text{etc.}

(2.21)

By definition, any product over the empty set is equal to
1
. A double product is equal to
1
if at least one of the sets
is empty.

We also apply this convention to the products of theta functions, for example,

\theta_{2}(u-\bar{v})=\prod_{v_{k}\in\bar{v}}\theta_{2}(u-v_{k}),\qquad\theta_{1}(u_{j}-\bar{u}_{j})=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}u_{l}\in\bar{u}\\
l\neq j\end{subarray}}\theta_{1}(u_{j}-u_{l}),\qquad\text{etc.}

(2.22)

In particular, expressions (
2.16
) for the functions
a(u)
and
d(u)
take the form

a(u)=\theta_{1}(u-\bar{\xi}+\eta),\qquad d(u)=\theta_{1}(u-\bar{\xi}).

(2.23)

To construct Bethe vectors we first introduce a generalized pre-Bethe vector as

|\psi_{n-r}^{l}(\bar{u})\rangle=B_{l-r-1,l+r+1}(u_{n-r})B_{l-r-2,l+r+2}(u_{n-r-1})\cdots B_{l-n,l+n}(u_{1})|\Omega^{l-n}\rangle,

(2.24)

where
\bar{u}=\{u_{1},\dots,u_{n-r}\}
is a set of arbitrary complex numbers,
n=N/2
, and
r\in\mathbb{Z}
. A generalized
Bethe vector is then defined as a Fourier transform of (
2.24
). For
\eta=1/2
, it has the following form:

|\hat{\Psi}^{\nu}_{n-r}(\bar{u})\rangle=\sum_{l=0}^{3}e^{-\pi i\nu l\eta}|\psi_{n-r}^{l}(\bar{u})\rangle,\qquad\nu\in\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}.

(2.25)

If the parameters
\bar{u}
satisfy a system of Bethe equations (see below), then the generalized
Bethe vector becomes an eigenvector of the
transfer matrix
{\sf T}(u)

[
2
]
. The Bethe vectors are symmetric with respect to the parameters
\bar{u}
due to the commutation relations between the operators
B_{l-k,l+k}
and
B_{l-k-1,l+k+1}

[
2
,
26
]
.

Similarly, we can define generalized dual pre-Bethe vectors

\langle\psi_{n-r}^{l}(\bar{v})|=\langle\bar{\Omega}^{l-n}|\bar{C}_{l-n,l+n}(v_{1})\ldots\bar{C}_{l-r-2,l+r+2}(v_{n-r-1})\bar{C}_{l-r-1,l+r+1}(v_{n-r}),

(2.26)

where
\bar{C}_{kl}=\gamma_{k}\gamma_{l}C_{kl}
, and
\bar{v}=\{v_{1},\dots,v_{n-r}\}
is a set of arbitrary complex numbers.
Then dual Bethe vectors are

\langle\hat{\Psi}^{\nu}_{n-r}(\bar{v})|=\sum_{l=0}^{3}e^{\pi i\nu l\eta}\langle\psi_{n-r}^{l}(\bar{v})|\qquad\nu\in\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}.

(2.27)

Just like the vectors (
2.25
), the dual Bethe vectors are symmetric with respect to the parameters
\bar{v}
.

Generically, (dual) Bethe vectors become eigenvectors of the transfer matrix (on-shell Bethe vectors) for
r=0
. Let us introduce

\chi_{\nu}(z)=(-1)^{n}e^{i\pi\eta\nu}a(z)+e^{-i\pi\eta\nu}d(z).

(2.28)

Then
|\hat{\Psi}^{\nu}_{n}(\bar{u})\rangle
is an on-shell Bethe vector provided

\chi_{\nu}(u_{j})=0,\qquad j=1,\dots,n.

(2.29)

A set of equations (
2.29
) is nothing but a system of Bethe equations in the case of free fermions
\eta=1/2
.

Similarly,
\langle\hat{\Psi}^{\nu}_{n}(\bar{v})|
is a dual eigenvector of the transfer matrix (dual on-shell Bethe vector) provided

\chi_{\nu}(v_{j})=0,\qquad j=1,\dots,n.

(2.30)

Then

\displaystyle\sf{\displaystyle T}(z)|\hat{\Psi}^{\nu}_{n}(\bar{u})\rangle=T_{\nu}(z|\bar{u})|\hat{\Psi}^{\nu}_{n}(\bar{u})\rangle,

(2.31)

\displaystyle\langle\hat{\Psi}^{\nu}_{n}(\bar{v})|{\sf T}(z)=T_{\nu}(z|\bar{v})\langle\hat{\Psi}^{\nu}_{n}(\bar{v})|.

where

T_{\nu}(z|\bar{u})=\chi_{\nu}(z)f(z,\bar{u}),\qquad T_{\nu}(z|\bar{v})=\chi_{\nu}(z)f(z,\bar{v}).

(2.32)

In the
XYZ
model with a rational value of
\eta
, there is a degeneracy of the spectrum
[
41
,
42
,
43
]
. In particular, for
\eta=1/2
, the degeneracy is due to the presence of roots of Bethe equations differing from each other by
1/2
.
Let us define the following mapping over the fundamental domain
z\in\mathbb{C}/(\mathbb{Z}+\tau\mathbb{Z})

z^{\ast}=z+\frac{(-1)^{\epsilon}}{2},

(2.33)

where
\epsilon=0
if
0\leq\Re z<\frac{1}{2}
and
\epsilon=1
otherwise.
It is easy to check that
\chi_{\nu}(z^{\ast})=(-1)^{\nu}\chi_{\nu}(z)
. Therefore, if
v_{a}
is a root
\chi_{\nu}(z)
, then
v_{a}^{\ast}
is also a root
\chi_{\nu}(z)
. We will call such elements twins. Since equation (
2.28
) is an elliptic polynomial of degree
N
, we conclude that
we have
N/2
vacancies (i.e. possible solutions) of (
2.30
) in the half domain
0\leq\Re z<\frac{1}{2}
and
N/2
vacancies in the remaining half
\frac{1}{2}\leq\Re z<1
of the fundamental domain.

In what follows, we will work only with twin-free on-shell Bethe vectors that correspond to singlet eigenstates. Consideration of
vectors with twins requires a special study (see e.g.
[
44
,
45
,
46
,
47
]
.

If a twin-free (dual) on-shell Bethe vector is parameterized by the roots
\bar{v}
, then it follows from (
2.32
) that the eigenvalues are zero for any of its twin roots:

T_{\nu}(v_{a}^{\ast})=0,\,\forall a.

(2.34)

Whereas the eigenvalues of the transfer matrix can be evaluated at its Bethe roots and written as
T_{\nu}(v_{a})=\theta_{2}(0)\Omega^{\nu}_{a}
where

\Omega^{\nu}_{a}=\frac{1}{\theta_{2}(0)}\lim_{z\to v_{a}}T_{\nu}(z)=\frac{(-1)^{n}e^{i\pi\nu\eta}a(v_{a})f(v_{a},\bar{v}_{a})\mathcal{V}_{a}}{\theta_{1}^{\prime}(0)}=\frac{-e^{-i\pi\nu\eta}d(v_{a})f(v_{a},\bar{v}_{a})\mathcal{V}_{a}}{\theta_{1}^{\prime}(0)}.

(2.35)

Here we introduced a logarithmic derivative

\mathcal{V}_{a}=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}z}\left(\frac{a(z)}{d(z)}\right)\bigg|_{z=v_{a}},

(2.36)

which appears in normalized expressions for scalar products (see below).

2.3
Scalar products, selection rule

In
[
26
]
, scalar products of the following form were considered:

\mathbf{S}^{\nu,\lambda}_{n,n}(\bar{v}|\bar{u})=\mathcal{N}_{n}^{\nu}(\bar{v})\langle\Psi^{\nu}_{n}(\bar{v})|\Psi^{\lambda}_{n}(\bar{u})\rangle,

(2.37)

where

\mathcal{N}_{n}^{\nu}(\bar{v})=\left(\langle\Psi^{\nu}_{n}(\bar{v})|\Psi^{\nu}_{n}(\bar{v})\rangle\right)^{-1}.

(2.38)

In these equations,
\langle\Psi^{\nu}_{n}(\bar{v})|
is a dual on-shell Bethe vector, that is the set
\bar{v}
satisfies Bethe equations (
2.30
). At the same time,
|\Psi^{\lambda}_{n}(\bar{u})\rangle
is an off-shell Bethe vector, that is, the set
\bar{u}
does not enjoy any constraint. However, we require that
\#\bar{v}=\#\bar{u}=n
.
We call such scalar products balanced.

As we have shown in
[
27
]
, the scalar products (
2.37
) are not enough for the description of the form factors of local operators. Therefore, in this paper, we consider scalar products of a more general form

\mathbf{S}^{\nu,\lambda}_{n,m}(\bar{v}|\bar{u})=\mathcal{N}_{n}^{\nu}(\bar{v})\langle\Psi^{\nu}_{n}(\bar{v})|\Psi^{\lambda}_{m}(\bar{u})\rangle.

(2.39)

As before,
\langle\Psi^{\nu}_{n}(\bar{v})|
is a dual on-shell Bethe vector. Moreover, we assume that the set
\bar{v}
does not contain twins. As for the vector
|\Psi^{\lambda}_{m}(\bar{u})\rangle
, it is still an off-shell Bethe vector, however,
\#\bar{u}=m
, and
m

may be different from
n
. We will call
\varkappa=n-m
an imbalance and the corresponding scalar products imbalanced.

It is easy to establish some properties of scalar products with respect to the imbalance. They are based on the properties of the operator
{\sf U}_{3}
:

{\sf U}_{3}=\sigma_{1}^{z}\otimes\cdots\otimes\sigma_{N}^{z}.

(2.40)

It was shown in
[
26
]
that generic (dual) off-shell Bethe vectors are eigenvectors of
{\sf U}_{3}
:

{\sf U}_{3}|\Psi^{\lambda}_{m}(\bar{u})\rangle=(-1)^{\lambda+m}|\Psi^{\lambda}_{m}(\bar{u})\rangle,\qquad\langle\Psi^{\nu}_{n}(\bar{v})|{\sf U}_{3}=(-1)^{\nu+n}\langle\Psi^{\nu}_{n}(\bar{v})|.

(2.41)

This implies a selection rule

\mathbf{S}^{\nu,\lambda}_{n,m}(\bar{v}|\bar{u})\cong\delta_{\nu+\varkappa,\lambda\;(\hskip-8.53581pt\mod 2)}\;.

(2.42)

In the case of free fermions, it follows from (
2.42
) that either
\lambda=\nu
or
\lambda=\nu+2\hskip-2.84526pt\mod 2
.

Note also that scalar products are symmetric in
\bar{v}
and symmetric in
\bar{u}
.

3
Cascading systems of linear equations for scalar products

We use the approach developed in
[
25
]
that was also used in
[
26
]
for the balanced scalar products of the XYZ model. From now on, we consider the free fermion case only, so the parameter
\eta=1/2
is fixed. However some part of the arguments, including initial remarks, still holds for rational
\eta
models.

\times\frac{\theta_{1}(u_{j}-\bar{v})\theta_{1}(u_{j}-\bar{z})\theta_{1}(\bar{u}-z_{l})}{\theta_{2}(u_{j}-\bar{u}_{j})\theta_{1}(\bar{v}-z_{l})\theta_{1}(\bar{z}_{l}-z_{l})},\qquad l=1,2,3,

(4.36)

where

\tilde{S}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}(v_{j}-u_{j})+\sum_{l=1}^{3}(z_{l}-u_{n+l}).

(4.37)

Thus, we find

Y^{1}_{j}=\sum_{l=1}^{3}C_{l}Y^{1}_{j;l},

(4.38)

where

Y^{1}_{j;l}=\theta_{2}(x+u_{j}-z_{l}-\tilde{S})\frac{\theta_{1}(u_{j}-\bar{z}_{l})\theta_{1}(u_{j}-\bar{v})}{\theta_{2}(u_{j}-\bar{u}_{j})},

(4.39)

and
C_{l}
are some functions that depend on the parameters
\bar{u}
in such a way that
C_{l}Y^{1}_{j;l}
does not depend on
u_{j}
.

Substituting
Y^{1}_{j;l}
(
4.39
) into equation (
4.10
) with
\epsilon=0
we obtain
Y^{0}_{j;l}
:

Y_{j;l}^{0}=-\frac{C_{l}\theta_{2}(0)}{f(u_{j},\bar{v})}\sum_{k=1}^{n+3}\frac{\theta_{1}(u_{k}-\bar{z})\theta_{1}(u_{k}-\bar{v})}{\theta_{1}(u_{k}-\bar{u}_{k})}\frac{\theta_{1}(u_{k}-u_{j}-x)}{\theta_{2}(u_{k}-u_{j})\theta_{1}(x)}\frac{\theta_{2}(x+u_{k}-z_{l}-\tilde{S})}{\theta_{1}(u_{k}-z_{l})}.

(4.40)

Computing this sum via the contour integral method we find

Y_{j;l}^{0}=f(u_{j},\bar{z}_{l})\frac{\theta_{2}(x)}{\theta_{1}(x)}\;C_{l}\frac{\theta_{1}(u_{j}-\bar{z}_{l})\theta_{1}(u_{j}-\bar{v})}{\theta_{2}(u_{j}-\bar{u}_{j})}\theta_{1}(x+u_{j}-z_{l}-\tilde{S}).

(4.41)

We see now that we can not satisfy the condition that
Y^{\epsilon}_{j}
does not depend on
u_{j}
. Indeed, to provide this condition for
Y_{j;l}^{1}
, we
should choose

C_{l}=\tilde{C}_{l}\frac{\prod_{a>b}^{n+3}\theta_{2}(u_{a}-u_{b})}{\theta_{1}(\bar{u}-\bar{v})\theta_{1}(\bar{u}-\bar{z}_{l})},

(4.42)

where
\tilde{C}_{l}
does not depend on
\bar{u}
. However, it follows form (
4.41
) that
Y_{j;l}^{0}
becomes proportional to the product

f(u_{j},\bar{z}_{l})
in this case. And vice versa: choosing the function
C_{l}
in (
4.41
) to eliminate the dependence on

u_{j}
, we thereby create a dependence on
u_{j}
in (
4.39
).

Thus, the only solution to system (
4.10
) that satisfies the necessary condition is the trivial solution.
Hence,

\langle\hat{\Psi}^{\nu}_{n}(\bar{v})|\hat{\Psi}^{\lambda}_{m}(\bar{u})\rangle=0,

(4.43)

for
\varkappa=n-m=-2
. Thus, proposition
4.1
is proved.

Through proposition
4.1
we have obtained a strong selection rule for the scalar products of twin-free on-shell Bethe vectors. We find that they are orthogonal to all Bethe vectors from the sector
\varkappa=\pm 2
. However, this does not tell anything about their scalar products with Bethe vectors for
\varkappa=\pm 1
. In this case, the system of equations (

) is inhomogeneous. The next section is devoted to this system.

5
Imbalanced scalar products with
\varkappa=\pm 1

It is convenient to make a change of variables in this section. Namely, we replace the set
\bar{u}=\{u_{1},\dots,u_{m+1}\}

\bar{w}=\{w_{1},\dots,w_{m+1}\}

For odd values of parameter

To describe them, we first introduce coefficients

Let us also define two functions

\displaystyle\beta^{+}_{l}(z)

(5.2)

\displaystyle\beta^{-}_{l}(z;u,v)

\displaystyle=\theta_{2}(z-t_{l})\theta_{2}(z-u+x_{l})\theta_{2}(z-v+x_{l}),

Recall that

In its turn, the homogeneous part of (

Obviously,

Let

Since all the scalar products

Conclusion

In
[
27
]
, we argued that balanced scalar products are not enough to calculate form factors. One also needs to know
scalar products with imbalance
\varkappa=\pm 1
. In this paper, we have shown that in the
XY
model, the latter are expressed in a simple way through
the balanced scalar products. Thus, we have prepared all the necessary tools for calculating the form factors of local
spin operators in the
XY
model. Indeed, due to the explicit solution of the inverse problem, the latter are reduced to the form factors of the
monodromy matrix elements. For example, the form factor of the operator
\sigma_{k}^{z}
is reduced to the matrix element of
A(\xi_{k})-D(\xi_{k})
:

\frac{\langle\Psi^{\nu}_{n}(\bar{v})|\sigma_{k}^{z}|\Psi^{\lambda}_{n}(\bar{u})\rangle}{\|\Psi^{\nu}_{n}(\bar{v})\|\|\Psi^{\lambda}_{n}(\bar{u})\|}=\left(\frac{\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}T_{\lambda}(\xi_{j}|\bar{u})}{\prod_{j=1}^{k}T_{\nu}(\xi_{j}|\bar{v})}\right)\frac{\langle\Psi^{\nu}_{n}(\bar{v})|\big(A(\xi_{k})-D(\xi_{k})\big)|\Psi^{\lambda}_{n}(\bar{u})\rangle}{\|\Psi^{\nu}_{n}(\bar{v})\|\|\Psi^{\lambda}_{n}(\bar{u})\|}.

(5.17)

The action of
A(\xi_{k})-D(\xi_{k})
on the vector
|\Psi^{\lambda}_{n}(\bar{u})\rangle
is schematically given by formula (
3.3
)
(with other coefficients
\mathbf{W}^{(\lambda)}_{\varkappa;k}
). Thus, the form factor (
5.17
) is reduced to a linear combination of scalar products with imbalance
\varkappa=0,\pm 1
.
As for the correct normalization, it is easy to see that it is restored in expressions that are quadratic in form factors.

We believe that the method proposed in this paper can also be generalized to the case of an arbitrary rational value of the parameter
\eta
. As already noted, the cascade system of equations for scalar products (
3.5
) gives homogeneous equations with an imbalance
\varkappa=0\mod M(Q)
, where
M(Q)=Q
for
Q
odd and
M(Q)=Q/2
for Q even. It is most probable that these inhomogeneous equations have only trivial solutions for the same reason as in the case of free fermions. If so, then we get a closed system of inhomogeneous linear equations for scalar products with nonzero imbalance. It remains only to write down the solution of this system in a form convenient for further applications. We will study this issue in our future publications.

Appendix A
Jacobi theta functions

Here we only give some basic properties of Jacobi theta functions used in the paper. See
[
31
]
for more details.

The Jacobi theta functions are defined as follows:

\displaystyle\theta_{1}(u|\tau)=-i\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}(-1)^{k}q^{(k+\frac{1}{2})^{2}}e^{\pi i(2k+1)u},

(A.1)

\displaystyle\theta_{2}(u|\tau)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}q^{(k+\frac{1}{2})^{2}}e^{\pi i(2k+1)u},

\displaystyle\theta_{3}(u|\tau)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}q^{k^{2}}e^{2\pi iku},

\displaystyle\theta_{4}(u|\tau)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}(-1)^{k}q^{k^{2}}e^{2\pi iku},

where
\tau\in\mathbb{C}
,
\Im\tau>0
, and

q=e^{\pi i\tau}
.

They satisfy the following shift properties:

\displaystyle\theta_{1}(u+1/2|\tau)=\theta_{2}(u|\tau),

\displaystyle\theta_{2}(u+1/2|\tau)=-\theta_{1}(u|\tau),

(A.2)

\displaystyle\theta_{1}(u+1|\tau)=-\theta_{1}(u|\tau),

\displaystyle\theta_{2}(u+1|\tau)=-\theta_{2}(u|\tau),

\displaystyle\theta_{1}(u+\tau|\tau)=-e^{-\pi i(2u+\tau)}\theta_{1}(u|\tau),

\displaystyle\theta_{2}(u+\tau|\tau)=e^{-\pi i(2u+\tau)}\theta_{2}(u|\tau).

In this paper, we use an identity

2\theta_{1}(u+v|2\tau)\theta_{4}(u-v|2\tau)=\theta_{1}(u|\tau)\theta_{2}(v|\tau)+\theta_{2}(u|\tau)\theta_{1}(v|\tau).

(A.3)

It follows from this identity that

\theta_{1}(u|\tau)\theta_{2}(v|\tau)=\theta_{1}(u+v|2\tau)\theta_{4}(u-v|2\tau)+\theta_{4}(u+v|2\tau)\theta_{1}(u-v|2\tau).

(A.4)

In particular, setting
v=u
in (
A.4
) we obtain

\theta_{1}(u|\tau)\theta_{2}(u|\tau)=\theta_{1}(2u|2\tau)\theta_{4}(0|2\tau).

(A.5)

Differentiating (
A.5
) with respect to
u
at zero, we arrive at

\frac{\theta^{\prime}_{1}(0|\tau)}{\theta^{\prime}_{1}(0|2\tau)}=2\frac{\theta_{4}(0|2\tau)}{\theta_{2}(0|\tau)}.

(A.6)

Appendix B
Zero eigenvectors

Let
M
be an
n\times n
matrix. Let its matrix elements can be presented in the form

M_{jk}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}A_{j\ell}B_{\ell k},

(B.1)

where
m<n
. In other words, the matrix
M
is the product of two rectangular matrices of size
n\times m
and
m\times n
. Assume that
\mathop{\rm rank}B=m
. Otherwise,
if
\mathop{\rm rank}B=m^{\prime}
with
m^{\prime}<m
, then the rows of
B
are linearly dependent, and we can present
M
as a sum of
m^{\prime}
terms:

M_{jk}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{m^{\prime}}\tilde{A}_{j\ell}B_{\ell k}.

(B.2)

Obviously,
\det M=0
. Let us find zero eigenvectors of
M
. For this, we extend the matrix
B_{jk}
by adding
n-m
rows with elements
B_{m+1,k},B_{m+2,k},\dots,B_{n,k}
. Let us
denote this extended
n\times n
matrix by
\tilde{B}_{jk}
. We require that this extended matrix is invertible. Then zero eigenvectors
\Psi^{(a)}
,

a=1,\dots,n-m
have the following components

\Psi^{(a)}_{j}=(\tilde{B}^{-1})_{j,m+a}.

(B.3)

The proof is elementary:

\sum_{k=1}^{n}M_{jk}\Psi^{(a)}_{k}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}A_{j\ell}B_{\ell k}(\tilde{B}^{-1})_{k,m+a}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}A_{j\ell}\delta_{\ell,m+a}=0,

(B.4)

because
\ell<m+a
.

Since we assume that
\tilde{B}_{jk}
is invertible, the vectors
\Psi^{(a)}
are linearly independent.

In particular, let
B
be a rectangular Cauchy matrix

B_{jk}=\frac{\theta_{1}(x_{j}-y_{k}+\lambda)}{\theta_{1}(x_{j}-y_{k})},\qquad j=1,\dots,m,\qquad k=1,\dots n,

(B.5)

dependent on pairwise distinct complex numbers
x_{1},\dots,x_{m}
and
y_{1},\dots,y_{n}
.
We can create an extended matrix as follows:

\tilde{B}_{jk}=\frac{\theta_{1}(x_{j}-y_{k}+\lambda)}{\theta_{1}(x_{j}-y_{k})},\qquad j,k=1,\dots n.

(B.6)

Here
x_{m+1},\dots,x_{n}
are generic complex numbers not equal to
x_{1},\dots,x_{m}
and
y_{1},\dots,y_{n}
.
Then the extended matrix
\tilde{B}_{jk}
is non-degenerated, and the inverse matrix is

(\tilde{B}^{-1})_{jk}=\frac{1}{\theta_{1}(\lambda)\theta_{1}(S+\lambda)}\frac{\theta_{1}(S+\lambda-x_{k}+y_{j})}{\theta_{1}(x_{k}-y_{j})}\frac{\theta_{1}(x_{k}-\bar{y})\theta_{1}(\bar{x}-y_{j})}{\theta_{1}(x_{k}-\bar{x}_{k})\theta_{1}(\bar{y}_{j}-y_{j})},

(B.7)

where

S=\sum_{j=1}^{n}(x_{j}-y_{j}).

Respectively, zero eigenvectors of
M
are

\Psi^{(l)}_{j}=C_{l}\theta_{1}(S+\lambda-x_{m+l}+y_{j})\frac{\theta_{1}(\bar{x}_{m+l}-y_{j})}{\theta_{1}(\bar{y}_{j}-y_{j})},

(B.8)

where
C_{l}
are some constants. We see that different eigenvectors are parameterized by the numbers
x_{m+1},\dots,x_{n}
.

Appendix C
Contour integral method

A contour integral method allows one to calculate or transform sums of a special form containing the Jacobi theta functions. To illustrate this method, we
consider two examples. The first example is related to the transformation of the matrix product
\mathbf{\Omega}^{1}\mathbf{\Omega}^{0}
.

It follows from (
4.12
) that the product
\mathbf{\Omega}^{1}\mathbf{\Omega}^{0}
has the following form:

\left(\mathbf{\Omega}^{1}\mathbf{\Omega}^{0}\right)_{jk}=\frac{\theta_{2}(0)f(u_{k},\bar{u}_{k})}{\theta_{1}(x)\theta_{2}(x)f(u_{j},\bar{v})}H_{jk},

(C.1)

where

H_{jk}=\sum_{a=1}^{n-2p+1}\frac{\theta_{2}(u_{a}-\bar{u})\theta_{1}(u_{a}-\bar{v})}{\theta_{1}(u_{a}-\bar{u}_{a})\theta_{2}(u_{a}-\bar{v})}\frac{\theta_{2}(u_{\ell j}-x)\theta_{1}(u_{\ell k}+x)}{\theta_{2}(u_{a}-u_{j})\theta_{2}(u_{a}-u_{k})}.

(C.2)

Consider a contour integral

J=\frac{\theta^{\prime}_{1}(0)}{2\pi i}\oint\,dz\frac{\theta_{2}(z-\bar{u})\theta_{1}(z-\bar{v})}{\theta_{1}(z-\bar{u})\theta_{2}(z-\bar{v})}\frac{\theta_{2}(z-u_{j}-x)\theta_{1}(z-u_{k}+x)}{\theta_{2}(z-u_{j})\theta_{2}(z-u_{k})}.

(C.3)

Using (
A.2
) we obtain that
J=0
due to periodicity (recall that
\#\bar{u}=n-2p+1
and
\#\bar{v}=n
). On the other hand, this integral equals the sum of the residues within the integration contour. The sum of the residues in
z=u_{a}
gives
H_{jk}
. There are also poles in
z=v_{q}+1/2
,
q=1,\dots,n
.
Finally, there is a pole at
z=u_{j}+1/2
for
j=k
. Thus, we obtain

H_{jk}=\delta_{jk}\frac{f(u_{j},\bar{v})}{f(u_{j},\bar{u}_{j})}\frac{\theta_{1}(x)\theta_{2}(x)}{\theta_{2}(0)}+\theta_{2}(0)\sum_{q=1}^{n}\frac{f(v_{q},\bar{v}_{q})}{f(v_{q},\bar{u})}\frac{\theta_{1}(u_{j}-v_{q}+x)\theta_{2}(u_{k}-v_{q}-x)}{\theta_{1}(u_{j}-v_{q})\theta_{1}(u_{k}-v_{q})}.

(C.4)

This implies

\left(\mathbf{\Omega}^{1}\mathbf{\Omega}^{0}\right)_{jk}=\delta_{jk}+\frac{\theta_{2}^{2}(0)f(u_{k},\bar{u}_{k})}{\theta_{1}(x)\theta_{2}(x)f(u_{j},\bar{v})}\sum_{q=1}^{n}\frac{f(v_{q},\bar{v}_{q})}{f(v_{q},\bar{u})}\frac{\theta_{1}(u_{j}-v_{q}+x)\theta_{2}(u_{k}-v_{q}-x)}{\theta_{1}(u_{j}-v_{q})\theta_{1}(u_{k}-v_{q})}.

(C.5)

Hence, we arrive at (
4.15
).

In the second example, we calculate the coefficients
\mathcal{A}_{j}
(
4.27
). We have

\mathcal{A}_{j}=\frac{-1}{f(v_{n},v_{j})\theta_{1}(x)\theta_{1}(x-S)}\frac{\theta_{2}(\bar{u}-v_{j})}{\theta_{2}(\bar{v}_{n,j}-v_{j})}\frac{f(v_{n},\bar{v}_{n})}{f(v_{n},\bar{u})}G^{a}_{j},

(C.6)

where

G^{a}_{j}=\sum_{a=1}^{n-1}\frac{\theta_{1}(u_{a}-v_{j}-x+S)\theta_{1}(u_{a}-v_{n}+x)}{\theta_{1}(u_{a}-v_{j})\theta_{1}(u_{a}-v_{n})}\frac{\theta_{1}(u_{a}-\bar{v}_{n})}{\theta_{1}(u_{a}-\bar{u}_{a})}.

(C.7)

The function
