Growing Schrödinger’s cat states by local unitary time evolution of product states
Saverio Bocini, Maurizio Fagotti

Abstract
We envisage many-body systems that can be described by quantum spin-chain Hamiltonians with a trivial separable eigenstate. For generic Hamiltonians, such a state represents a quantum scar. We show that, typically, a macroscopically-entangled state naturally grows after a single projective measurement of just one spin in the quantum scar. Moreover, we identify a condition under which what is growing is a “Schrödinger’s cat state”. Our analysis does not reveal any particular requirement for the entangled state to develop, provided that the quantum scar does not minimise/maximise a local conservation law. We study two examples explicitly: systems described by generic Hamiltonians and a model that exhibits a U ⁡ ( 1 ) U(1) hidden symmetry. The latter can be reinterpreted as a 2-leg ladder in which the interactions along the legs are controlled by the local state on the other leg through transistor-like building blocks.

We propose a theoretical protocol that stands out for its naturalness, in the sense that the growth of macroscopic entanglement is a manifestation of an intriguing physical phenomenon rather than of clever manipulations of a system.
The most general system that we consider is described by a quantum spin-
\frac{1}{2}
chain Hamiltonian
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{H}}
with local interactions and a trivial separable eigenstate.
Without loss of generality, the latter can be set equal to

\ket{\Psi(0)}=\ket{\Uparrow}\equiv\ket{\uparrow\cdots\uparrow}\,,

(1)

where
\ket{\uparrow}
denotes the eigenvector of the Pauli matrix
\sigma^{z}
with eigenvalue
1

\Uparrow
will represent in this paper a generic number of adjacent
\uparrow

U(1)
symmetry, e.g., the conservation of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{S}}^{z}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\ell}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}_{\ell}^{z}
, where
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}_{\ell}^{\alpha}
are local operators acting like the Pauli matrices
\sigma^{\alpha}
on site
\ell
and like the identity elsewhere. As we will see before long, the most interesting cases are however those in which
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{S}}^{z}
is not conserved. If there are no additional relevant conserved operators, either
\ket{\Uparrow}
is the ground state or it is an exact
quantum scar

[
19
,
20
,
21
]
: its properties contrast with those of the eigenstates with similar energy. Quantum scars have recently attracted a lot of attention
[
22
,
23
,
24
]
, also in connection with their unusual entanglement properties, displaying low bipartite entanglement, still potentially featuring extensive multipartite entanglement
[
25
]
. In our case the quantum scar is exceptional, as it is fully separable.

The basic idea is that the quantum scar is essentially metastable and could be transmuted into a state with extensive multipartite entanglement just by a local perturbation.
Specifically, we consider the effect of a quantum measurement of a spin in a tilted direction with respect to
z
, so that the state is projected into

\ket{\Psi_{\theta}(0^{+})}=e^{i\theta\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}_{0}^{y}}\ket{\Uparrow}=\cos\theta\ket{\Uparrow}+\sin\theta\ket{\Uparrow\downarrow\Uparrow}

(2)

with probability
\cos^{2}\theta
.
Time evolution affects only the second term on the right hand side of Eq. [
2
], hence

\ket{\Psi_{\theta}(t)}=\cos\theta\ket{\Uparrow}+\sin\theta e^{-i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{H}}t}\ket{\Uparrow\downarrow\Uparrow},\quad t>0\,.

(3)

The Lieb-Robinson bounds
[
26
]
ensure that the perturbation is irrelevant outside a light cone emerging from the space-time point of the measurement. Since the state before the measurement is an eigenstate, the measurement triggers a nontrivial time evolution only within a region whose length grows linearly with the time. Hence, neglecting the part of the system outside that region does not significantly affect the purity of the state.
We call
\Omega_{\epsilon}(t)
the smallest subsystem that contains the spin that is measured and that, as clarified later, can be considered pure at time
t
with accuracy
1-\epsilon
.
In the following, most of our considerations, including the use of terms such as “macroscopic” and “extensive”, will be referred to subsystem
\Omega_{\epsilon}(t)
. This allows us to treat on the same footing infinite systems, which are arguably more interesting from a theoretical point of view, and finite ones (provided that the actual system’s size is larger than
\Omega_{\epsilon}(t)
), which are instead more relevant for the experiments.
The situation is pictorially described in Fig.
1
: after the spin flip at the origin, the component
\ket{\Uparrow\downarrow\Uparrow}
of the state evolves in time, while the component
\ket{\Uparrow}
stays constant; the effective size of the system is given by the spins that are inside the light cone originating from the local perturbation, while the rest of the spins are neglected.

First we show that, quite generally, in the settings considered the perturbation has everlasting effects on the expectation values of local observables arbitrarily far from the position of the measurement. That is to say,
e^{-i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{H}}t}\ket{\Uparrow\downarrow\Uparrow}\equiv\ket{\Psi_{\pi/2}(t)}
is macroscopically different from
\ket{\Uparrow}
, hence there exists an observable
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}=\sum_{\ell}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{a}}_{\ell}
, where
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{a}}_{\ell}
are finite-support observables, such that
\braket{\Uparrow|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}|\Uparrow}-\braket{\Psi_{\pi/2}(t)|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}|\Psi_{\pi/2}(t)}
is extensive, i.e., it scales linearly with the (effective) system size
|\Omega_{\epsilon}(t)|

[
27
]
.
Such an effect is already remarkable, as, generally, local perturbations in quantum many-body systems either fade away or remain localised.
We take it as the starting point of our investigation into the multipartite entanglement properties of
\ket{\Psi_{\theta}(t)}
.
We remind the reader that a state is said to have extensive multipartite entanglement if and only if the variance scales as the square of the system’s length for at least one extensive operator
[
28
,
29
,
30
]
.
Being
\ket{\Psi_{\pi/2}(t)}
macroscopically different from
\ket{\Uparrow}
, the reader is already in the position to infer that the projective measurement on the quantum scar
\ket{\Uparrow}
will generate a state with extensive multipartite entanglement.
But the situation is even more interesting.
We provide evidence and then conjecture that, if the model has a hidden
U(1)
symmetry (see below for the definition),
\ket{\Psi_{\pi/2}(t)}
is not macroscopically entangled;
under those conditions, the projective measurement generates a genuine Schrödinger cat’s state consisting of an (ideally) arbitrarily large number of sites.

1
The system

We investigate a variety of Hamiltonians with local densities.
Our requirement of
\ket{\Uparrow}
being eigenstate of the Hamiltonian allows for any kind of interactions that commute with the total spin in the
z
direction,
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{S}}^{z}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\ell}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}^{z}_{\ell}
, (such as the hopping term
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}_{\ell}^{x}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}_{\ell+n}^{x}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}_{\ell}^{y}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}_{\ell+n}^{y}
or the longitudinal Dzyaloshinskii–Moriya interaction
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}_{\ell}^{x}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}_{\ell+n}^{y}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}_{\ell}^{y}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}_{\ell+n}^{x}
) but not only that. The constraint is much weaker than a
U(1)
symmetry and the Hamiltonian density can feature any term of the form
\frac{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{1}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}_{\ell}^{z}}{2}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}_{\ell}\frac{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{1}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}_{\ell}^{z}}{2}
, where
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}_{\ell}
is a local operator with support in a region around
\ell
. Among the allowed operators,
those with a hidden
U(1)
symmetry (such as
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}_{\ell-1}^{x}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{1}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}_{\ell}^{z})\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}_{\ell+1}^{x}
or
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}_{\ell-1}^{x}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}_{\ell}^{x}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}_{\ell+1}^{x}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}_{\ell}^{y}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}_{\ell+1}^{y})\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}_{\ell+2}^{x}
) will play a special role.

1.1
Hidden
U(1)
symmetry

The first class of Hamiltonians we consider has recently sparked some attention because it describes systems that are macroscopically sensitive to local perturbations
[
31
,
32
]
.
Such a sensitivity is triggered by so-called “semilocal conservation laws”, whose densities act as local observables only in a restricted space of operators with a particular symmetry.
This unusual property could seem, at first glance, innocuous, but it allows the state to retain memory of so-called “string order”
[
33
,
34
,
35
]
, making in turn symmetry-protected topological order survive the limit of infinite time
[
32
]
.
An example of operator with a semilocal density in a system that is symmetric under the spin flip
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}_{\ell}^{x,y}\rightarrow-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}_{\ell}^{x,y}
is

\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{S}}}^{z}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\ell}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Pi}}^{z}(\ell)\,,

(4)

where
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Pi}}^{z}(\ell)
can be thought of as a semi-infinite string of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}^{z}
and is such that
[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Pi}}^{z}(\ell)]^{2}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{I}}
,
[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Pi}}^{z}(\ell),\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}_{j}^{z}]=0
and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Pi}}^{z}(\ell)\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}_{j}^{x,y}=\mathrm{sgn}(\ell-j-\frac{1}{2})\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}_{j}^{x,y}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Pi}}^{z}(\ell)
. In spin-flip invariant settings, the density
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Pi}}^{z}(\ell)
of
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{S}}}^{z}
is a local observable and, if
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{S}}}^{z}
commutes with the Hamiltonian, it has important consequences on the evolution of the system, similarly to any other local conservation law — we refer the reader to Ref.
[
32
]
for additional details.

The (two) symmetry sectors of the spin-flip symmetry are connected by any odd local operator, e.g.,
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}_{j}^{x}
. Acting with it on the state splits the latter in two parts where the expectation value of semilocal operators takes opposite signs. This is at the origin of the macroscopic effects of local perturbations pointed out in Ref.
[
31
]
.
The behaviour of fluctuations in these systems has not yet been investigated but the sensitivity of local observables to a single spin flip is a strong indication that the quantum measurement of a local observable such as
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}_{j}^{x}
could result in macroscopically entangled states.
As a specific example, we consider the following Hamiltonian

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{H}}_{1}=\sum_{\ell}\frac{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{1}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}_{\ell}^{z}}{8}[J\vec{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}}_{\ell-1}\overset{S}{\cdot}\vec{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}}_{\ell+1}+\\
+\vec{D}\cdot(\vec{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}}_{\ell-1}\times\vec{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}}_{\ell+1})]-\frac{\vec{h}}{2}\cdot\vec{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}}_{\ell}

(5)

where
\vec{a}\overset{S}{\cdot}\vec{b}=\vec{a}\cdot(S\vec{b})
. This Hamiltonian commutes with the semilocal charge
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{S}}}^{z}
— Eq. [
4
] — provided that

S=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{1+\gamma}{2}&w&0\\
w&\frac{1-\gamma}{2}&0\\
0&0&\Delta\end{bmatrix}\quad\vec{D}=\begin{bmatrix}0\\
0\\
D^{z}\end{bmatrix}\quad\vec{h}=\begin{bmatrix}0\\
0\\
h^{z}\end{bmatrix}\,.

(6)

It describes a system that can be interpreted as a quantum transistor chain, in which the building blocks
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{T}}^{\alpha\beta}_{\ell}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}_{\ell-1}^{\alpha}\frac{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{I}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}_{\ell}^{z}}{2}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}_{\ell+1}^{\beta}
, with
(\alpha,\beta)\in\{(x,x),(x,y),(y,x),(y,y),(z,z),(0,0)\}
(
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}^{0}_{\ell}\equiv\boldsymbol{\mathbf{1}}
), commute with
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{S}}}^{z}
— see Fig.

1.2
Generic model

We establish contact with the systems currently studied in quantum simulators, for example with trapped ions
[
43
]
, by considering the following Hamiltonian

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{H}}_{2}=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{\ell}\Bigl[\sum_{r=1}J_{r}\vec{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}}_{\ell}\overset{S_{r}}{\cdot}\vec{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}}_{\ell+r}+\vec{D}_{r}\cdot(\vec{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}}_{\ell}\times\vec{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}}_{\ell+r})\Bigr]-\frac{\vec{h}}{2}\cdot\vec{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}}_{\ell}.

(7)

This is the most general Hamiltonian where spins interact in pairs: there’s a Heisenberg exchange term, a Dzyaloshinskii-Moriya interaction, and a coupling with an external field.
The trivial quantum scar
\ket{\Uparrow}
appears when

S_{r}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&\frac{1}{2}\gamma_{r}^{x}\\
0&1&\frac{1}{2}\gamma_{r}^{y}\\
\frac{1}{2}\gamma_{r}^{x}&\frac{1}{2}\gamma_{r}^{y}&1+\gamma_{r}^{z}\end{bmatrix}\quad\vec{h}=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{1}{2}\sum_{r=1}J_{r}\gamma^{x}_{r}\\
\frac{1}{2}\sum_{r=1}J_{r}\gamma^{y}_{r}\\
h^{z}\end{bmatrix}.

(8)

The parameter
\vec{\gamma}
incorporates both an anisotropy in the Heisenberg interaction and a rotation of the axes (which is relevant because we have fixed the orientation of the spins of the separable eigenstate).
As will be clarified before long, the effect we exploit to generate macroscopically entangled states requires a
U(1)

,
D_{r}^{y}
,
\gamma_{r}^{x}

\gamma_{r}^{y}

In such a generic setting there is no a-priori reason to expect the localised perturbation to produce macroscopic effects; for example, the system does not exhibit semilocal charges or special constraining interactions. Is the presence of a simple quantum scar sufficient to trigger the phenomenon?

2
Results

2.1
The effective system
\Omega(t)

We have introduced
\Omega_{\epsilon}(t)
as the smallest subsystem, containing the measured spin, that can be considered pure at time
t
with accuracy
1-\epsilon
. We clarify here this point.
First of all, it can be readily proven that the initial perturbation is irrelevant outside a light cone emerging from the space-time point of the measurement using a corollary of the Lieb-Robinson bounds derived in Ref.
[
48
]
. Indeed, the Heisenberg representation of a local operator is exponentially close to an operator with support in a finite region including the support of the operator, the argument of the exponential being proportional to
d-v_{LR}|t|
, where
d
is the smallest distance between the observable and the boundary of the region and
v_{LR}
is the Lieb-Robinson velocity. In our specific case, this means that the following decomposition holds:
\exp[i\theta\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}_{0}^{y}(-t)]=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}_{\Omega}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{\Omega}
, where
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}_{\Omega}
is a unitary operator with support in a region
\Omega
centered at the measurement position and
\parallel\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{\Omega}\parallel\lesssim\exp[-(|\Omega|/2-vt)/\xi]
, with
\xi
a non-universal constant. This gives

\parallel\ket{\Psi(t)}\bra{\Psi(t)}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}_{\Omega}\ket{\Uparrow}\bra{\Uparrow}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}_{\Omega}^{\dagger}\parallel=\\
\parallel\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}_{\Omega}\ket{\Uparrow}\bra{\Uparrow}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{\Omega}^{\dagger}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{\Omega}\ket{\Uparrow}\bra{\Uparrow}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}_{\Omega}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{\Omega}\ket{\Uparrow}\bra{\Uparrow}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{\Omega}^{\dagger}\parallel\\
\leq 2\parallel\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{\Omega}\parallel+\parallel\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{\Omega}\parallel^{2}\lesssim e^{-(|\Omega|/2-vt)}\,;

(9)

that is to say, up to exponentially small corrections the reduced density matrix of
\Omega
is pure.

We call
\Omega_{\epsilon}(t)
the smallest spin block for which
\parallel\mathrm{tr}_{\Omega_{\epsilon}(t)}[e^{-i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{H}}t}\ket{\Uparrow\downarrow\Uparrow}\bra{\Uparrow\downarrow\Uparrow}e^{-i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{H}}t}]-(\ket{\Uparrow}\bra{\Uparrow})_{\overline{\Omega_{\epsilon}(t)}}\parallel
is smaller than
\epsilon
, where
(\ket{\Uparrow}\bra{\Uparrow})_{\overline{\Omega_{\epsilon}(t)}}
is the state
\ket{\Uparrow}
restricted to the complement of
\Omega_{\epsilon}(t)
. Note that
\Omega_{\epsilon}(0)
contains only the spin at the origin for any choice of
\epsilon
.

2.2
U(1)
symmetry

We point out here that, if the total magnetisation
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{S}}^{z}
is conserved, the states
\ket{\Uparrow}
and
\ket{\Psi_{\pi/2}(t)}
can not become macroscopically different and their linear combination is not macroscopically entangled.
Specifically, Appendix
C
provides a proof that a state
\ket{\Psi}
obtained by time evolution under a
U(1)
-symmetric Hamiltonian after a local perturbation to
\ket{\Uparrow}

is macroscopically equivalent to
\ket{\Uparrow}
; that is to say, the quantity
\braket{\Psi|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}|\Psi}-\braket{\Uparrow|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}|\Uparrow}
is subextensive for any translational-invariant operator
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}=\sum_{\ell}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}_{A_{\ell}}
, with
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}_{A_{\ell}}
local operators.
We also show that the variance of any of such operators
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}
with respect to the state
\ket{\Psi}
grows at most linearly with system size
|\Omega_{\epsilon}(t)|
, ruling out the possibility to obtain a macroscopically entangled state from time evolution of a locally-perturbed
\ket{\Uparrow}
. And this holds true for any local perturbation.
Physically, we can understand this result from the fact that, first, a local operator can only flip a finite number of spins, and, second, the initial state,
\ket{\Uparrow}
, has maximal
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{S}}^{z}
. As a result, the Hilbert space accessible to time evolution is too small for macroscopic entanglement to develop.
This is the first clue supporting the expectation that, in order to build up macroscopic entanglement,
\ket{\Uparrow}

can not be the ground state of a local conservation law. In generic systems, we can read this as a statement that
\ket{\Uparrow}
should be a quantum scar.

2.3
Hidden
U(1)
symmetry

We start with
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{H}}_{1}
— Eq. [
5
] — in the integrability region in which, as discussed before, we can take for granted that
\ket{\Psi_{\pi/2}(t)}
becomes macroscopically different from
\ket{\Uparrow}
:

|\braket{\Uparrow\downarrow\Uparrow|e^{i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{H}}_{1}t}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}e^{-i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{H}}_{1}t}|\Uparrow\downarrow\Uparrow}-\braket{\Uparrow|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}|\Uparrow}|\sim t\,,

(10)

with
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}
a generic (spin-flip invariant) extensive operator. This is expected as long as the initial state
\ket{\Uparrow}
is in the middle of the spectrum of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{H}}_{1}
. Incidentally, we can understand that this latter condition is necessary by considering the dual XXZ model — Eq. [
1
] — in which, for
|h^{z}|
large enough,
\ket{\Uparrow}
becomes the ground state (or the maximum energy state). By applying the Kramers-Wannier duality mapping proposed in Ref.
[
31
]
the system is mapped into the time evolution of a domain wall in the XXZ model, where the magnetic field
h^{z}
plays the role of the anisotropy. As proven in Ref.
[
49
]
, the domain wall does not spread when the anisotropy is larger than a critical value, which is equivalent to say that the perturbation remains localised when
|h^{z}|
is large enough to move
\ket{\Uparrow}
at the boundaries of the energy spectrum.

In the integrability region of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{H}}_{1}
there is just one piece of information that we cannot retrieve from the scientific literature: the behaviour of fluctuations. This is important to us because the Lieb-Robinson bound guarantees
|\Omega_{\epsilon}(t)|=\mathcal{O}(t)
, and hence, if the variance of extensive observables in
\ket{\Psi_{\pi/2}(t)}
were
\mathcal{O}(t)
, we could readily conclude that the projective measurement generates a genuine cat state.

We measure macroscopic entanglement with the so called
quantumness
, which characterises the asymptotic behaviour of the maximum quantum Fisher information among all extensive observables (see
Materials and Methods
).
In fact, we only compute a lower bound of it,
\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{(1)}
, resulting from reducing the space of observables.
An example is reported in Fig.
3
, where we plot
\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{(1)}
as a function of the effective system size
|\Omega_{\epsilon}(t)|
. To study the asymptotic behavior of
\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{(1)}
we fit the data with a curve parametrised as
\beta_{0}+\beta_{1}|\Omega_{\epsilon}(t)|+\beta_{2}\frac{1}{|\Omega_{\epsilon}(t)|}
, where the term
\frac{1}{|\Omega_{\epsilon}(t)|}
stands for a potential sub-leading term. The estimated leading order is consistent with a constant (
\beta_{1}\approx 0
) in the case
\theta=\pi/2
and with a linear growth (
\beta_{1}\neq 0
) in the generic case.
The figure also shows how the difference of magnetization between flipping or not the spin is proportional to
|\Omega_{\epsilon}(t)|
, meaning that the two states
\ket{\Uparrow}
and
\ket{\Psi_{\pi/2}(t)}
are macroscopically different and therefore the state
\ket{\Psi_{\theta}(t)}
is macroscopically entangled for generic
\theta

2.5
Imperfections in the preparation of the state

We investigate the effect of perturbing the state by time evolution under a homogeneous Hamiltonian
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{H}}_{0}
for a fixed time
t_{0}
, i.e.,
\ket{\Psi_{0}(0^{-})}=e^{-i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{H}}_{0}t_{0}}\ket{\Uparrow}
.
We then study the time evolution of
\ket{\Psi_{\theta}(0^{+})}=e^{i\theta\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}_{0}^{y}}\ket{\Psi_{0}(0^{-})}
, which is the analog of Eq. [
2
].
We warn the reader of a complication: the initial evolution affects the whole chain, so, strictly speaking, there is no approximately pure subsystem.
If we insist on considering the subsystem associated with the spreading of the local perturbation (corresponding to the sharp change of behaviour in the profile of the magnetization), we should analyse the quantum Fisher information of a mixed state, which is a harder quantity to compute.
In this preliminary study of the stability of our protocol, we limit ourselves to check the qualitative behaviour of the probability distribution of the magnetization.

We distinguish two classes of perturbations: in the first class, the perturbation is spin-flip symmetric (thus the hidden
U(1)
symmetry is preserved), e.g.,
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{H}}_{0}
could be the integrable Ising Hamiltonian

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{H}}_{0,1}=-\frac{1}{4}\sum_{\ell}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}_{\ell}^{x}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}_{\ell+1}^{x}+h_{0}^{z}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}_{\ell}^{z}\right)\,;

(11)

in the second class, the pre-measuerement Hamiltonian breaks the spin-flip invariance behind the hidden
U(1)
symmetry, e.g.

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{H}}_{0,2}=-\frac{1}{4}\sum_{\ell}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}_{\ell}^{x}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}_{\ell+1}^{x}+h_{0}^{z}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}_{\ell}^{z}+h_{0}^{x}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}_{\ell}^{x}\right)\,,

(12)

which is a non-integrable version of the Ising model.
Note that, in both cases, the larger
h_{0}^{z}
, the smaller the expected effect, therefore
h_{0}^{z}
can be used as a control parameter.

The first class of perturbations is not expected to destabilize the growth of a cat state: On the one hand, Ref.
[
31
]
showed that the perturbation has everlasting macroscopic effects even after so-called “global quenches” from a spin-flip invariant initial state. On the other hand, Ref.
[
50
]
indirectly confirms clustering in
\ket{\Psi_{\pi/2}(t)}
at long times with noninteracting transistor Hamiltonians, indeed the mutual information approaches zero at large distances.
Our numerical analysis is consistent with these expectations, indeed we still see the formation of a bimodal probability distribution (with two well separated peaks).

On the contrary, for the second class of perturbations the probability density does not present two well separated peaks, as shown e.g. in the bottom row of Fig.
6
.
This rules out the formation of a cat state, but the basic features pointing to macroscopic entanglement (such as the linear growth in time of the difference of magnetization between flipping or not the spin) are still present for times longer than our maximum simulation times.

3
Discussion

3.1
Local perturbations with macroscopic effects

In ordinary 1D systems a spin flip does not change the macroscopic properties of a state whatever long the time is. The naive expectation is that its effect spreads out across the chain as
t^{\alpha}
but fades away as
t^{-\alpha}
, with
\alpha=1
in integrable models and
\alpha=\tfrac{1}{2}
in most of the generic systems.
Recently, three situations have been pointed out in which this picture can break down:

•

ground states in symmetry-broken phases
[
51
,
52
]
.

•

quantum jammed states in systems with Hilbert space fragmentation
[
41
]
.

•

systems with semilocal conservation laws
[
31
]
.

To these three known cases, we have added models with a fully separable quantum scar.
This novel scenario has two advantages: first, a product state can be easily prepared experimentally, and, second, its stationarity
gives the freedom to stop the (virtual) experiment at any time without compromising the outcome.

Remarkably, the norm is that the perturbation generates macroscopic entanglement, indeed, the possible but improbable operators breaking that rule can be cured by the addition of a local operator that does not change the excited state — e.g.,
\epsilon\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}_{\ell}^{z}
for our initial state
\ket{\Uparrow}
.

In the special case in which there is a hidden
U(1)
symmetry our analysis of the fluctuations reveals that the entangled part of the system is in a Schrödinger’s cat state.

3.2
On the spreading

The existence of the semilocal charge
\sum_{\ell}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Pi}}^{z}(\ell)
ensures that the effect of the spin flip does not fade away at long time
[
31
,
32
]
, but it is not sufficient to exclude the effect to remain confined around the position of the measurement. This is indeed what happens when
h
is too large, in which case the Hamiltonian exhibits localised excitations (see Appendix
B
). In the generic case, however, the perturbation spreads out. Using the underlying integrable structure, Ref.
[
31
]
showed that the spreading is ballistic for
\gamma=1
,
w=\Delta=D^{z}=0
, and
|h^{z}|<\frac{1}{4}
. And for slightly different initial product states, ballistic behaviour was observed
[
41
,
42
]
also for
\gamma=w=\Delta=D^{z}=h^{z}=0
, for which the model is integrable as well.
A priori one would not expect ballistic spreading in generic models, but rather diffusive. In Ref.
[
41
]
, in particular, a preliminary analysis of the effect of some integrability-breaking perturbations pointed to a non-ballistic spreading. In contrast, we find numerical evidence that ballistic behaviour extends to a wide range of parameters, at least, within the time window investigated. In order to rule integrability out, we have checked the statistics of the energy levels, and, for generic parameters, our data are in excellent agreement with a Wigner-Dyson distribution (see the Appendix
A
), supporting, in turn, the non-integrability of the generic models investigated.

Materials and Methods

4.1
Simulations of the dynamics

Numerical simulations are performed with Julia ITensor library
[
53
]
.
We use a time-evolving block decimation (TEBD) algorithm and, in all data reported, time evolution is discretized in time steps
\delta t=0.01
with 2nd order Trotter-Suzuki gates
[
54
]
.

4.2
Full counting statistics

The full probability distribution of a given observable encodes the entire information about its moments.
Let
L
be the chain’s length.
We define
P_{\theta}(m)
as the probability to get
m
from a measurement of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{S}}^{z}
given that the system is in the state
\ket{\Psi_{\theta}(t)}
.
Note that
m\in\{-\frac{L}{2},-\frac{L}{2}+1,...,\frac{L}{2}\}
.
To set a convention, we assume
L
to be divisible by
4
, so that the maximum possible value
m
of the magnetization is an integer even number.
The generating function of the moments of the probability distribution is defined as

G_{\theta}(k)=\braket{\Psi_{\theta}(t)|e^{i\frac{2\pi k}{L+1}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{S}}^{z}}|\Psi_{\theta}(t)}.

(13)

The probability
P_{\theta}(m)
is then the Fourier transform of the generating function:

P_{\theta}(m)=\frac{1}{L+1}\sum_{k=-L/2}^{L/2}e^{-i\frac{2\pi k}{L+1}m}G_{\theta}(k).

(14)

We compute the generating function numerically using Julia ITensor library
[
53
]
.

We point out that, if the model is invariant under spin flip
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}^{x,y}\rightarrow-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}^{x,y}
, the state
\ket{\Psi_{\pi/2}(t)}
belongs to the sector in which the parity operator
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Pi}}^{z}=\prod_{\ell}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}^{z}_{\ell}
has eigenvalue
-1
, which means
P_{\pi/2}(m)=0
for any even
m
.
This implies that, in the generic case of
\ket{\Psi_{\theta}(t)}
with
\theta\neq\frac{\pi}{2}
,
P_{\theta}(m)=0
for any even
m
except for
P_{\theta}(L/2)=\cos^{2}\theta
.
This is why, in the plots of the probability density for models with spin-flip invariance, we report only odd values of
m
and
m=L/2
.

4.3
Quantumness and Quantum Fisher information

Ref.
[
55
]
proposed the quantum Fisher information as a measure of the “macroscopicity” of quantum effects in lattice systems.
Its computation is however rather complicated when addressed in full generality, both analytically and numerically.
For the sake of simplicity, we follow the approach of Refs
[
56
,
28
,
29
]
and restrict ourselves to examining a sufficient condition. Specifically, we investigate the quantum Fisher information
\mathcal{F}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}})
of extensive operators whose densities have support on a single site, i.e.,
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}[\{\vec{n}\}]=\sum_{j\in\Omega_{\epsilon}(t)}\vec{n}_{j}\cdot\vec{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}}_{j}
, where
\vec{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}}_{j}\equiv\{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}^{x}_{j},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}^{y}_{j},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}^{z}_{j}\}
and the coefficients are normalised as
|\vec{n}_{j}|^{2}=1
.
In pure states, which is the case we consider,
\mathcal{F}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}})
equals four times the variance of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}
.
Using the notation of
[
30
]
, we introduce the quantumness
N_{\mathrm{eff}}
of a state as its maximal quantum Fisher information with respect to all extensive observables:

N_{\mathrm{eff}}=\frac{1}{4|\Omega_{\epsilon}(t)|}\max_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}}\mathcal{F}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}).

(15)

We denote by
N_{\mathrm{eff}}^{(1)}
the maximisation restricted to the observables with single-site density as introduced above.

N_{\mathrm{eff}}
was interpreted as an effective size of the macroscopic quantum state, thus
N_{\mathrm{eff}}^{(1)}
is a lower bound for the effective size.
We introduce the covariance matrix

[\mathrm{K}(t)]_{n,\alpha;m,\beta}=\frac{1}{2}\braket{\Psi_{\pi/2}(t)|\{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}_{n}^{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}_{m}^{\beta}\}|\Psi_{\pi/2}(t)}+\\
-\braket{\Psi_{\pi/2}(t)|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}_{n}^{\alpha}|\Psi_{\pi/2}(t)}\braket{\Psi_{\pi/2}(t)|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}_{m}^{\beta}|\Psi_{\pi/2}(t)}\,,

(16)

where
\{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}_{n}^{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}_{m}^{\beta}\}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}_{n}^{\alpha}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}_{m}^{\beta}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}_{m}^{\beta}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}_{n}^{\alpha}
.
In pure states,
\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{(1)}
satisfies

\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{(1)}=\frac{\mathrm{tr}[\mathrm{D}(t)]}{|\Omega_{\epsilon}(t)|}\,,\quad\text{with}\quad\mathrm{K}(t)\vec{v}(t)=[\mathrm{D}(t)\otimes\mathrm{I}_{3}]\vec{v}(t)\,,

(17)

where
n,m\in\Omega_{\epsilon}(t)
(the vector space is
3|\Omega_{\epsilon}(t)|
-dimensional) and
\mathrm{D}(t)
is required to be diagonal (
[\vec{v}]_{n}
are normalised).
We solve Eq. [
17
] using a Lanczos algorithm with three basic iterative steps:

1.

\vec{w}^{(n)}=\mathrm{K}\vec{v}^{(n)}

2.

[\mathrm{D}^{(n)}]_{j}=\parallel\vec{w}_{j}^{(n)}\parallel^{-1}

3.

\vec{v}^{(n+1)}=(\mathrm{D}^{(n)}\otimes\mathrm{I}_{3})\vec{w}^{(n)}

In most of the cases this procedure worked well without particular stabilizers.

5
Conclusion

We have shown that, quite generally, macroscopic entanglement emerges from a localised inhomogeneity in a state that would be otherwise stationary. This can not happen in an ordinary excited state that maximises the entropy under the constraints of the local conservation laws. In generic systems, indeed, we observe the phenomenon in quantum scars with anomalously low bipartite entanglement. We also argue that, in the specific case in which the excited state maximises instead the entropy under the constraints of also semi-local conservation laws, macroscopic entanglement takes the simple form of a cat state, providing in turn a novel way to generate cat states in a class of quantum many-body systems with local interactions.

This work leaves several open questions. First, a rigorous proof of the generation of cat states is missing. Second, the observation of ballistic behaviour in generic systems could be questioned as a finite-time effect, so additional investigations are imperative. Third, we have only provided a preliminary and incomplete check of the stability of our protocol, but this is clearly an important issue and does require additional investigations.

Appendix C
Absence of macroscopic effects in models with a
U(1)
symmetry

In this section we prove that a state
\ket{\Psi}
obtained by time evolution under a
U(1)
-symmetric Hamiltonian after a local perturbation to
\ket{\Uparrow}

is macroscopically equivalent to
\ket{\Uparrow}
; that is to say, the quantity
\braket{\Psi|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}|\Psi}-\braket{\Uparrow|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}|\Uparrow}
is subextensive for any translational-invariant operator
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}=\sum_{\ell}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}_{A_{\ell}}
, with
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}_{A_{\ell}}
local operators.
We also prove that the variance of any of such operators
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}
with respect to the state
\ket{\Psi}
grows at most linearly with system
|\Omega_{\epsilon}(t)|
, ruling out the possibility to obtain a macroscopically entangled state from time-evolution of a locally-perturbed
\ket{\Uparrow}
.

Let us denote by
\ket{\Psi_{s}}
a state obtained by flipping
s
spins in
\ket{\Uparrow}
, or linear combination of them.
We start by observing that, if
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{S}}^{z}
is conserved, the state time-evolves as

\ket{\Psi_{s}(t)}=\sum_{n_{1}<n_{2}<...<n_{s}}w_{\{n\}}(t)\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}_{n_{1}}^{-}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}_{n_{2}}^{-}...\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}_{n_{s}}^{-}\ket{\Uparrow}\,,

for some coefficients such that
\sum_{n_{1}<n_{2}<...<n_{s}}|w_{\{n\}}|^{2}=1
.
Let
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}_{A_{\ell}}
be an operator with support in
A
, where
A_{\ell}
is a set of
|A|
adjacent sites starting with the
\ell
-th spin:
A=\{\ell,...,\ell+|A|-1\}
. We have

\braket{\Psi_{s}(t)|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}_{A_{\ell}}|\Psi_{s}(t)}-\braket{\Uparrow|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}_{A_{\ell}}|\Uparrow}\\
=\sum_{i=1}^{s}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n_{1}<...<n_{s}\\
n_{k}\in A_{\ell}\Leftrightarrow k=i\end{subarray}}\biggl(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n_{i}^{\prime}\\
n_{k}^{\prime}\in A_{\ell},\forall k\in\{i\}\end{subarray}}w_{\{n\}}^{*}(t)w_{\{n\},n_{i}\rightarrow n^{\prime}_{i}}(t)\braket{\Uparrow|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}^{+}_{n^{\prime}_{i}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}_{A_{\ell}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}^{-}_{n_{i}}|\Uparrow}-|w_{\{n\}}(t)|^{2}\braket{\Uparrow|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}_{A_{\ell}}|\Uparrow}\biggr)\\
+\sum_{i=1}^{s-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n_{1}<...<n_{s}\\
n_{k}\in A_{\ell}\Leftrightarrow k\in\{i,i+1\}\end{subarray}}\biggl(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n_{i}^{\prime}<n^{\prime}_{i+1}\\
n^{\prime}_{k}\in A_{\ell},\forall k\in\{i,i+1\}\end{subarray}}w_{\{n\}}^{*}(t)w_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{n\},n_{i}\rightarrow n^{\prime}_{i}\\
n_{i+1}\rightarrow n^{\prime}_{i+1}\end{subarray}}(t)\braket{\Uparrow|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}^{+}_{n^{\prime}_{i}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}^{+}_{n^{\prime}_{i+1}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}_{A_{\ell}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}^{-}_{n_{i}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}^{-}_{n_{i+1}}|\Uparrow}-|w_{\{n\}}(t)|^{2}\braket{\Uparrow|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}_{A_{\ell}}|\Uparrow}\biggr)\\
+...+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n_{1}<...<n_{s}\\
n_{k}\in A_{\ell},\forall k\in\{1,...,s\}\end{subarray}}\biggl(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n^{\prime}_{1}<...<n^{\prime}_{s}\\
n^{\prime}_{k}\in A_{\ell},\forall k\in\{1,...,s\}\end{subarray}}w_{\{n\}}^{*}(t)w_{\{n^{\prime}\}}(t)\braket{\Uparrow|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}^{+}_{n_{1}^{\prime}}...\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}^{+}_{n_{s}^{\prime}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}_{A_{\ell}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}^{-}_{n_{1}}...\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}^{-}_{n_{s}}|\Uparrow}-|w_{\{n\}}(t)|^{2}\braket{\Uparrow|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}_{A_{\ell}}|\Uparrow}\biggr)\,.

(23)

An upper bound for the absolute value of the left hand side of the equation is obtained using
\braket{v|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}|v}\leq\parallel\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}\parallel\braket{v|v}
, where
\parallel\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}\parallel
denotes the operator norm, indeed we have

|\braket{\Psi_{s}(t)|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}_{A_{\ell}}|\Psi_{s}(t)}-\braket{\Uparrow|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}_{A_{\ell}}|\Uparrow}|\leq\\
\leq 2\parallel\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}_{A_{\ell}}\parallel\left(\sum_{i=1}^{s}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n_{1}<...<n_{s}\\
n_{k}\in A_{\ell}\Leftrightarrow k=i\end{subarray}}|w_{\{n\}}(t)|^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{s-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n_{1}<...<n_{s}\\
n_{k}\in A_{\ell}\Leftrightarrow k\in\{i,i+1\}\end{subarray}}|w_{\{n\}}(t)|^{2}+...+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n_{1}<...<n_{s}\\
n_{k}\in A_{\ell},\forall k\in\{1,...,s\}\end{subarray}}|w_{\{n\}}(t)|^{2}\right)\leq\\
\leq 2\parallel\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}_{A_{\ell}}\parallel\sum_{i=1}^{s}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n_{1}<...<n_{s}\\
n_{i}\in A_{\ell}\end{subarray}}|w_{\{n\}}(t)|^{2}\,,

(24)

where the last step is not an equality because there are some coefficients
w
that are counted more than once in the last line (e.g., if we take
s=2
and
A_{\ell}=\{\ell,\ell+1\}
, there is originally just one term
|w_{\ell,\ell+1}|^{2}
, coming from the sum
\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n_{1}<...<n_{s}\\
n_{k}\in A_{\ell},\forall k\in\{1,...,s\}\end{subarray}}
, but the sum
\sum_{i=1}^{s}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n_{1}<...<n_{s}\\
n_{i}\in A_{\ell}\end{subarray}}
has two of such terms, that we get for
i=\ell
and
i=\ell+1
).
Let us now consider the corresponding translational-invariant extensive operator
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}=\sum_{\ell\in\Omega_{\epsilon}(t)}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}_{A_{\ell}}
, where here
\Omega_{\epsilon}
has a generalised definition with respect to the main text, since we want it to include all the light cones steaming from each spin flip. This gives

|\braket{\Psi_{s}(t)|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}|\Psi_{s}(t)}-\braket{\Uparrow|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}|\Uparrow}|\leq\sum_{\ell\in\Omega_{\epsilon}(t)}|\braket{\Psi_{s}(t)|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}_{A_{\ell}}|\Psi_{s}(t)}-\braket{\Uparrow|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}_{A_{\ell}}|\Uparrow}|\leq\\
\leq 2\parallel\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}_{A_{1}}\parallel\sum_{\ell\in\Omega_{\epsilon}(t)}\sum_{i=1}^{s}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n_{1}<...<n_{s}\\
n_{i}\in A_{\ell}\end{subarray}}|w_{\{n\}}(t)|^{2}\lesssim 2\parallel\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}_{A_{1}}\parallel|A|\sum_{i=1}^{s}\sum_{n_{1}<...<n_{s}}|w_{\{n\}}(t)|^{2}=2s\parallel\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}_{A_{1}}\parallel|A|\,,

(25)

where we used translational invariance and
\lesssim
rather than
\leq
because
\Omega_{\epsilon}(t)
is only approximately pure.
This shows that
\ket{\Psi_{s}(t)}
and
\ket{\Uparrow}
are macroscopically indistinguishable for any time
t
.

We want now to extend the result to the square of the operator. We follow the same steps:

\braket{\Psi_{s}(t)|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}_{A_{\ell}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}_{A_{\ell^{\prime}}}|\Psi_{s}(t)}-\braket{\Uparrow|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}_{A_{\ell}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}_{A_{\ell^{\prime}}}|\Uparrow}\\
=\sum_{i=1}^{s}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n_{1}<...<n_{s}\\
n_{k}\in A_{\ell}\cup A_{\ell^{\prime}}\Leftrightarrow k=i\end{subarray}}\biggl(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n_{i}^{\prime}\\
n_{k}^{\prime}\in A_{\ell}\cup A_{\ell^{\prime}},\forall k\in\{i\}\end{subarray}}w_{\{n\}}^{*}(t)w_{\{n\},n_{i}\rightarrow n^{\prime}_{i}}(t)\braket{\Uparrow|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}^{+}_{n^{\prime}_{i}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}_{A_{\ell}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}_{A_{\ell^{\prime}}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}^{-}_{n_{i}}|\Uparrow}-|w_{\{n\}}(t)|^{2}\braket{\Uparrow|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}_{A_{\ell}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}_{A_{\ell^{\prime}}}|\Uparrow}\biggr)\\
+\sum_{i<j=1}^{s}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n_{1}<...<n_{s}\\
n_{k}\in A_{\ell}\cup A_{\ell^{\prime}}\Leftrightarrow k\in\{i,j\}\end{subarray}}\biggl(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n_{i}^{\prime}<n^{\prime}_{j}\\
n^{\prime}_{k}\in A_{\ell}\cup A_{\ell^{\prime}},\forall k\in\{i,j\}\end{subarray}}w_{\{n\}}^{*}(t)w_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{n\},n_{i}\rightarrow n^{\prime}_{i}\\
n_{j}\rightarrow n^{\prime}_{j}\end{subarray}}(t)\braket{\Uparrow|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}^{+}_{n^{\prime}_{i}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}^{+}_{n^{\prime}_{j}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}_{A_{\ell}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}_{A_{\ell^{\prime}}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}^{-}_{n_{i}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}^{-}_{n_{j}}|\Uparrow}+\\
-|w_{\{n\}}(t)|^{2}\braket{\Uparrow|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}_{A_{\ell}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}_{A_{\ell^{\prime}}}|\Uparrow}\biggr)+\dots+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n_{1}<...<n_{s}\\
n_{k}\in A_{\ell}\cup A_{\ell^{\prime}},\forall k\in\{1,...,s\}\end{subarray}}\biggl(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n^{\prime}_{1}<...<n^{\prime}_{s}\\
n^{\prime}_{k}\in A_{\ell}\cup A_{\ell^{\prime}},\forall k\in\{1,...,s\}\end{subarray}}w_{\{n\}}^{*}(t)w_{\{n^{\prime}\}}(t)\times\\
\times\braket{\Uparrow|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}^{+}_{n_{1}^{\prime}}...\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}^{+}_{n_{s}^{\prime}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}_{A_{\ell}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}_{A_{\ell^{\prime}}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}^{-}_{n_{1}}...\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}^{-}_{n_{s}}|\Uparrow}-|w_{\{n\}}(t)|^{2}\braket{\Uparrow|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}_{A_{\ell}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}_{A_{\ell^{\prime}}}|\Uparrow}\biggr)\,,

(26)

from which

|\braket{\Psi_{s}(t)|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}_{A_{\ell}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}_{A_{\ell^{\prime}}}|\Psi_{s}(t)}-\braket{\Uparrow|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}_{A_{\ell}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}_{A_{\ell^{\prime}}}|\Uparrow}|\leq 2\parallel\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}_{A_{\ell}}\parallel^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{s}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n_{1}<...<n_{s}\\
n_{k}\in A_{\ell}\cup A_{\ell^{\prime}}\Leftrightarrow k=i\end{subarray}}|w_{\{n\}}(t)|^{2}+\\
+2\parallel\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}_{A_{\ell}}\parallel^{2}\sum_{i<j=1}^{s}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n_{1}<...<n_{s}\\
n_{k}\in A_{\ell}\cup A_{\ell^{\prime}}\Leftrightarrow k\in\{i,j\}\end{subarray}}|w_{\{n\}}(t)|^{2}+\dots+2\parallel\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}_{A_{\ell}}\parallel^{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n_{1}<...<n_{s}\\
n_{k}\in A_{\ell}\cup A_{\ell^{\prime}},\forall k\in\{1,...,s\}\end{subarray}}|w_{\{n\}}(t)|^{2}\\
\leq 2\parallel\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}_{A_{\ell}}\parallel^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{s}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n_{1}<...<n_{s}\\
n_{i}\in A_{\ell}\cup A_{\ell^{\prime}}\end{subarray}}|w_{\{n\}}(t)|^{2}\,,

(27)

and finally

|\braket{\Psi_{s}(t)|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}^{2}|\Psi_{s}(t)}-\braket{\Uparrow|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}^{2}|\Uparrow}|\leq\sum_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}\in\Omega_{\epsilon}(t)}|\braket{\Psi_{s}(t)|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}_{A_{\ell}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}_{A_{\ell^{\prime}}}|\Psi_{s}(t)}-\braket{\Uparrow|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}_{A_{\ell}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}_{A_{\ell^{\prime}}}|\Uparrow}|\\
\leq 2\parallel\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}_{A_{1}}\parallel^{2}\sum_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}\in\Omega_{\epsilon}(t)}\sum_{i=1}^{s}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n_{1}<...<n_{s}\\
n_{i}\in A_{\ell}\cup A_{\ell^{\prime}}\end{subarray}}|w_{\{n\}}(t)|^{2}\leq 4\parallel\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}_{A_{1}}\parallel^{2}\sum_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}\in\Omega_{\epsilon}(t)}\sum_{i=1}^{s}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n_{1}<...<n_{s}\\
n_{i}\in A_{\ell}\end{subarray}}|w_{\{n\}}(t)|^{2}\\
\lesssim 4\parallel\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}_{A_{1}}\parallel^{2}|A||\Omega_{\epsilon}(t)|\sum_{i=1}^{s}\sum_{n_{1}<...<n_{s}}|w_{\{n\}}(t)|^{2}=4s\parallel\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}_{A_{1}}\parallel^{2}|A||\Omega_{\epsilon}(t)|\,.

(28)

This shows that the variance of the any operator
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}
on the state
\ket{\Psi_{s}(t)}
coincides with the one on the state
\ket{\Uparrow}
modulo corrections of order
\mathcal{O}(|\Omega_{\epsilon}(t)|)
.
Since
\ket{\Uparrow}
is a state for which clustering of correlations holds and his variance cannot be larger than system’s size, we obtain that the state
\ket{\Psi_{s}(t)}
has the same properties, implying that it is not macroscopically entangled.
Our proof can be easily generalised to any power of the operator, but higher powers are not needed to our purposes.

We now extend the results above to more general states. We consider, first, the quantity
\braket{\Psi_{s^{\prime}}|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}|\Psi_{s}}
,
s\neq s^{\prime}
, for two different states
\ket{\Psi_{s}}
and
\ket{\Psi_{s^{\prime}}}
.
Without loss of generality, we can assume
s>s^{\prime}
.
We start from

|\braket{\Psi_{s^{\prime}}|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}|\Psi_{s}}|\leq\sum_{\ell\in\Omega_{\epsilon}(t)}\sum_{n^{\prime}_{1}<...<n^{\prime}_{s^{\prime}}}\sum_{n_{1}<...<n_{s}}|\braket{\Uparrow|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}^{+}_{n^{\prime}_{1}}...\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}^{+}_{n^{\prime}_{s^{\prime}}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}_{A_{\ell}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}^{-}_{n_{1}}...\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}^{-}_{n_{s}}|\Uparrow}||w^{\prime}_{\{n^{\prime}\}}(t)||w_{\{n\}}(t)|,

(29)

and rewrite the sums regrouping them, for fixed
\ell
, according to how many of the primed indices
\{n^{\prime}\}
are outside the set
A_{\ell}
. This is a number
p
that goes from 0 to
s^{\prime}
. In order to get a non-zero contribution, the number of indices
\{n\}
outside
A_{\ell}
should also be
p
. The number of indices
\{n^{\prime}\}\in A_{\ell}
is then
s^{\prime}-p\in\{0,...,s^{\prime}\}
, while the number of indices
\{n\}\in A_{\ell}
is
s-p\in\{s-s^{\prime},...,s\}
; note that there is no contribution from
|A_{\ell}|<s-p
.
For given
p
, there are
p+1
distinct subsets of consecutive
n
that can be in
A_{\ell}
(they are characterised by the position of the first element of the subset); to keep track of this fact, we introduce a sum over an index
j
, such that the index
n_{j}
is the smallest of the indices
\{n\}\in A_{\ell}
.
Such a decomposition leads to

|\braket{\Psi_{s^{\prime}}|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}|\Psi_{s}}|\leq\sum_{\ell\in\Omega_{\epsilon}(t)}\sum_{p=0}^{s^{\prime}}\sum_{j=1}^{p+1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n_{1}<...<n_{s}\\
n_{k}\in A_{\ell}\Leftrightarrow j\leq k<j+s-p\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n^{\prime}_{1}<...<n^{\prime}_{s^{\prime}-p}\\
n^{\prime}_{k}\in A_{\ell},\forall k\in\{1,...,s^{\prime}-p\}\end{subarray}}\\
|\braket{\Uparrow|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}^{+}_{n^{\prime}_{1}}...\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}^{+}_{n^{\prime}_{s^{\prime}-p}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}_{A_{\ell}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}^{-}_{n_{j}}...\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}^{-}_{n_{j+s-p-1}}|\Uparrow}||w^{\prime}_{\{n\}\setminus\{n_{j},...,n_{j+s-p-1}\}\cup\{n^{\prime}_{1},...,n^{\prime}_{s^{\prime}-p}\}}(t)||w_{\{n\}}(t)|\leq\\
\leq\parallel\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}_{A_{\ell}}\parallel\sum_{p=0}^{s^{\prime}}\sum_{j=1}^{p+1}\sum_{\ell\in\Omega_{\epsilon}(t)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n_{1}<...<n_{s}\\
n_{k}\in A_{\ell}\Leftrightarrow j\leq k<j+s-p\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n^{\prime}_{1}<...<n^{\prime}_{s^{\prime}-p}\\
n^{\prime}_{k}\in A_{\ell},\forall k\in\{1,...,s^{\prime}-p\}\end{subarray}}|w^{\prime}_{\{n\}\setminus\{n_{j},...,n_{j+s-p-1}\}\cup\{n^{\prime}_{1},...,n^{\prime}_{s^{\prime}-p}\}}(t)||w_{\{n\}}(t)|.

(30)

It is now convenient to write the sum over the indices
n,n^{\prime}
in
A_{\ell}
in another way. We introduce
P_{k}^{K}
as the set of all the
k
-tuples of increasing numbers in
\{0,...,K-1\}
. Note that
P_{k}^{K}
has
\binom{K}{k}
elements. We then have

|\braket{\Psi_{s^{\prime}}|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}|\Psi_{s}}|\leq\parallel\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}_{A_{\ell}}\parallel\sum_{p=0}^{s^{\prime}}\sum_{j=1}^{p+1}\sum_{\ell\in\Omega_{\epsilon}(t)}\sum_{(k_{1},...,k_{s-p})\in P_{s-p}^{|A|}}\sum_{(k^{\prime}_{1},...,k^{\prime}_{s^{\prime}-p})\in P_{s^{\prime}-p}^{|A|}}\sum_{n_{1}<...<n_{j-1}<A_{\ell}<n_{j+s-p}<...<n_{s}}\\
|w^{\prime}_{\{n_{1},...,n_{j-1},\ell+k^{\prime}_{1},...,\ell+k^{\prime}_{s^{\prime}-p},n_{j+s-p},...,n_{s}\}}(t)||w_{\{n_{1},...,n_{j-1},\ell+k_{1},...,\ell+k_{s-p},n_{j+s-p},...,n_{s}}(t)|,

(31)

which we rewrite isolating the term with
p=s^{\prime}
(the case in which all the indices
\{n^{\prime}\}
are outside
A_{\ell}
), which is special:

|\braket{\Psi_{s^{\prime}}|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}|\Psi_{s}}|\leq\parallel\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}_{A_{\ell}}\parallel\sum_{p=0}^{s^{\prime}-1}\sum_{j=1}^{p+1}\sum_{(k_{1},...,k_{s-p})\in P_{s-p}^{|A|}}\sum_{(k^{\prime}_{1},...,k^{\prime}_{s^{\prime}-p})\in P_{s^{\prime}-p}^{|A|}}\sum_{\ell\in\Omega_{\epsilon}(t)}\sum_{n_{1}<...<n_{j-1}<A_{\ell}<n_{j+s-p}<...<n_{s}}\\
|w^{\prime}_{\{n_{1},...,n_{j-1},\ell+k^{\prime}_{1},...,\ell+k^{\prime}_{s^{\prime}-p},n_{j+s-p},...,n_{s}\}}(t)||w_{\{n_{1},...,n_{j-1},\ell+k_{1},...,\ell+k_{s-p},n_{j+s-p},...,n_{s}}(t)|\\
+\parallel\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}_{A_{\ell}}\parallel\sum_{j=1}^{s^{\prime}+1}\sum_{(k_{1},...,k_{s-s^{\prime}})\in P_{s-s^{\prime}}^{|A|}}\sum_{\ell\in\Omega_{\epsilon}(t)}\sum_{n_{1}<...<n_{j-1}<A_{\ell}<n_{j+s-s^{\prime}}<...<n_{s}}\\
|w^{\prime}_{\{n_{1},...,n_{j-1},n_{j+s-s^{\prime}},...,n_{s}\}}(t)||w_{\{n_{1},...,n_{j-1},\ell+k_{1},...,\ell+k_{s-s^{\prime}},n_{j+s-s^{\prime}},...,n_{s}}(t)|.

(32)

Now, for fixed
p,j,\{k\},\{k^{\prime}\}
, we want to maximize the sums over
\ell,\{n\}
independently, which gives an upper bound to the full quantity. To that aim, we introduce the following compact notation: for the terms with
p<s^{\prime}

\sum_{\ell\in\Omega_{\epsilon}(t)}\sum_{n_{1}<...<n_{j-1}<A_{\ell}<n_{j+s-p}<...<n_{s}}|w^{\prime}_{\{n_{1},...,n_{j-1},\ell+k^{\prime}_{1},...,\ell+k^{\prime}_{s^{\prime}-p},n_{j+s-p},...,n_{s}\}}(t)|\times\\
\times|w_{\{n_{1},...,n_{j-1},\ell+k_{1},...,\ell+k_{s-p},n_{j+s-p},...,n_{s}}(t)|\quad\equiv\sum_{b\in B}W^{\prime}_{b}W_{b}\,,

(33)

and for the term
p=s^{\prime}

\sum_{\ell\in\Omega_{\epsilon}(t)}\sum_{n_{1}<...<n_{j-1}<A_{\ell}<n_{j+s-s^{\prime}}<...<n_{s}}|w^{\prime}_{\{n_{1},...,n_{j-1},n_{j+s-s^{\prime}},...,n_{s}\}}(t)||w_{\{n_{1},...,n_{j-1},\ell+k_{1},...,\ell+k_{s-s^{\prime}},n_{j+s-s^{\prime}},...,n_{s}}(t)|\\
\equiv\sum_{\ell\in\Omega_{\epsilon}(t)}\sum_{c\in C}W^{\prime}_{c}W_{c,\ell}\,,

(34)

where
b
and
c
regroup all the indices that
w
and
w^{\prime}
have in common and
W,W^{\prime}
are a synthetic expression for the coefficients that report only the indices we are interested in; note that in the first case there are as many independent
w
as
w^{\prime}
, while, for
p=s^{\prime}
,
w^{\prime}
does not depend on
\ell
anymore.
In order to maximize the expressions above, we should take into accounts the constraints
\sum_{n^{\prime}_{1}<...<n^{\prime}_{s^{\prime}}}|w^{\prime}_{\{n^{\prime}\}}|^{2}=1
and
\sum_{n_{1}<...<n_{s}}|w_{\{n\}}|^{2}=1
. In our search for the maximum, we can use the stronger conditions
\sum_{b\in B}W_{b}^{2}=1
and
\sum_{b\in B}(W^{\prime}_{b})^{2}=1
in the first case and
\sum_{c\in C}(W_{c}^{\prime})^{2}=1
and
\sum_{c\in C,\ell\in\Omega_{\epsilon}(t)}W_{c,\ell}^{2}=1
in the second case, which amount to require that the coefficients that are not involved in the sum are zero (given the original constraint, this maximises the sum because all the terms are positive).
Using the method of Lagrange multipliers, one can show that, under these constraints,
\sum_{b\in B}W^{\prime}_{b}W_{b}
is maximised by
W_{b}=W^{\prime}_{b}
and equals 1, while
\sum_{\ell\in\Omega_{\epsilon}(t)}\sum_{c\in C}W^{\prime}_{c}W_{c,\ell}
has degenerate points of maximum in
W_{c,\ell}=|\Omega_{\epsilon}(t)|^{-1/2}W^{\prime}_{c}
and gives
|\Omega_{\epsilon}(t)|^{1/2}
.
In the end

|\braket{\Psi_{s^{\prime}}|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}|\Psi_{s}}|\leq\parallel\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}_{A_{\ell}}\parallel\sum_{j=1}^{s^{\prime}+1}\sum_{(k_{1},...,k_{s-s^{\prime}})\in P_{s-s^{\prime}}^{|A|}}|\Omega_{\epsilon}(t)|^{1/2}+\mathcal{O}(|\Omega_{\epsilon}(t)|^{0})=\\
=\parallel\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}_{A_{\ell}}\parallel(s^{\prime}+1)\binom{|A|}{s-s^{\prime}}|\Omega_{\epsilon}(t)|^{1/2}+\mathcal{O}(|\Omega_{\epsilon}(t)|^{0})\,.

(35)

This result will be used below.

We finally consider a finite sum of states obtained by flipping different numbers of spins:
\ket{\Psi^{(S)}}=\sum_{s=1}^{S}a_{s}\ket{\Psi_{s}}
, where
a_{j}\in\mathbb{R},\forall j
(any potential phase can be absorbed in the states), and
\sum_{s=1}^{S}a_{j}^{2}=1
.
From Eq. [
35
] it readily follows

|\braket{\Psi_{S+1}|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}|\Psi^{(S)}}|\leq\sum_{s=1}^{S}|\braket{\Psi_{S+1}|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}|\Psi_{s}}|\lesssim\mathcal{O}(|\Omega_{\epsilon}(t)|^{1/2})\,.

(36)

Let us now consider the state
\ket{\Psi^{(2)}}=a_{1}\ket{\Psi_{1}}+a_{2}\ket{\Psi_{2}}
, with
a_{1},a_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
and
a_{1}^{2}+a_{2}^{2}=1
. First of all, note that such a state is macroscopically equivalent to
\ket{\Uparrow}
. Indeed, using
\braket{\Psi_{2}|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}|\Psi_{2}}=\braket{\Psi_{1}|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}|\Psi_{1}}+\mathcal{O}(|\Omega_{\epsilon}(t)|^{0})
(a corollary of Eq. [
25
]), we have

\braket{\Psi^{(2)}|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}|\Psi^{(2)}}-\braket{\Uparrow|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}|\Uparrow}=a_{1}^{2}\braket{\Psi_{1}|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}|\Psi_{1}}+a_{2}^{2}\braket{\Psi_{2}|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}|\Psi_{2}}+2a_{1}a_{2}Re(\braket{\Psi_{1}|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}|\Psi_{2}})-\braket{\Uparrow|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}|\Uparrow}\\
=2a_{1}a_{2}Re(\braket{\Psi_{1}|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}|\Psi_{2}})+\mathcal{O}(|\Omega_{\epsilon}(t)|^{0})\lesssim\mathcal{O}(|\Omega_{\epsilon}(t)|^{1/2})\,,

(37)

which is sub-extensive. Similarly, we can conclude that
\braket{\Psi^{(S)}|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}|\Psi^{(S)}}-\braket{\Uparrow|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}|\Uparrow}\lesssim\mathcal{O}(|\Omega_{\epsilon}(t)|^{1/2})
, meaning that
\ket{\Psi^{(S)}}
is macroscopically equivalent to
\ket{\Uparrow}
for any finite
S
.
Let us now look at the variance of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}
in
\ket{\Psi^{(2)}}
:

\braket{\Psi^{(2)}(t)|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}^{2}|\Psi^{(2)}(t)}-\braket{\Psi^{(2)}(t)|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}|\Psi^{(2)}(t)}^{2}=a_{1}^{2}\braket{\Psi_{1}|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}^{2}|\Psi_{1}}+a_{2}^{2}\braket{\Psi_{2}|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}^{2}|\Psi_{2}}+2a_{1}a_{2}Re\left(\braket{\Psi_{1}|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}^{2}|\Psi_{2}}\right)+\\
-\left(a_{1}^{2}\braket{\Psi_{1}|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}|\Psi_{1}}+a_{2}^{2}\braket{\Psi_{2}(t)|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}|\Psi_{2}(t)}+2a_{1}a_{2}Re\left(\braket{\Psi_{1}|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}|\Psi_{2}(t)}\right)\right)^{2}=\\
=\braket{\Psi_{1}|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}^{2}|\Psi_{1}}+2a_{1}a_{2}Re\left(\braket{\Psi_{1}|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}^{2}|\Psi_{2}(t)}\right)+\mathcal{O}(|\Omega_{\epsilon}(t)|)+\\
-\left(\braket{\Psi_{1}|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}|\Psi_{1}}+2a_{1}a_{2}Re\left(\braket{\Psi_{1}|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}|\Psi_{2}(t)}\right)+\mathcal{O}(|\Omega_{\epsilon}(t)|^{0})\right)^{2}\,,

(38)

where we used
\braket{\Psi_{2}|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}|\Psi_{2}}=\braket{\Psi_{1}|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}|\Psi_{1}}+\mathcal{O}(|\Omega_{\epsilon}(t)|^{0})
and
\braket{\Psi_{2}|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}^{2}|\Psi_{2}}=\braket{\Psi_{1}|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}^{2}|\Psi_{1}}+\mathcal{O}(|\Omega_{\epsilon}(t)|)
(corollaries of Eq. [
25
] and Eq. [
28
]).
Using that the variance of any state
\ket{\Psi_{s}}
can not grow faster than
|\Omega_{\epsilon}(t)|
, we get

\braket{\Psi^{(2)}(t)|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}^{2}|\Psi^{(2)}(t)}-\braket{\Psi^{(2)}(t)|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}|\Psi^{(2)}(t)}^{2}\\
=2a_{1}a_{2}\left[Re\left(\braket{\Psi_{1}|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}^{2}|\Psi_{2}(t)}\right)-\braket{\Psi_{1}|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}|\Psi_{1}}Re\left(\braket{\Psi_{1}|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}|\Psi_{2}(t)}\right)\right]+\mathcal{O}(|\Omega_{\epsilon}(t)|)\,.

(39)

If the leading contribution to the variance were larger than
\mathcal{O}(|\Omega_{\epsilon}(t)|)
, by changing the sign of
a_{1}
we could make the variance negative; since the variance is by definition positive, by contradiction, we have that the leading order cannot be larger than
\mathcal{O}(|\Omega_{\epsilon}(t)|)
.
We can now assume that the variance in the state
\ket{\Psi^{(S-1)}}
is of order
\mathcal{O}(|\Omega_{\epsilon}(t)|)
and show that this implies that also the variance in
\ket{\Psi^{(S)}}
is.
The proof is essentially the same as above with just a small modification, namely,
\braket{\Psi^{(S-1)}|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}|\Psi^{(S-1)}}-\braket{\Uparrow|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}|\Uparrow}
is
\mathcal{O}(|\Omega_{\epsilon}(t)|^{1/2})
rather than
\mathcal{O}(|\Omega_{\epsilon}(t)|^{1/2})
. This gives an extra term in the potential leading order, which however does not affect the final result: calling
\ket{\Psi^{(S)}}=a_{1}\ket{{\Psi_{S}}}+a_{2}\ket{\Psi^{(S-1)}}
, we have

\braket{\Psi^{(S)}(t)|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}^{2}|\Psi^{(S)}(t)}-\braket{\Psi^{(S)}(t)|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}|\Psi^{(S)}(t)}^{2}\\
=a_{1}^{2}\braket{\Psi_{S}|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}^{2}|\Psi_{S}}+a_{2}^{2}\braket{\Psi^{(S-1)}|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}^{2}|\Psi^{(S-1)}}+2a_{1}a_{2}Re\left(\braket{\Psi_{S}|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}^{2}|\Psi^{(S-1)}}\right)\\
-\left(a_{1}^{2}\braket{\Psi_{S}|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}|\Psi_{S}}+a_{2}^{2}\braket{\Psi^{(S-1)}(t)|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}|\Psi^{(S-1)}(t)}+2a_{1}a_{2}Re\left(\braket{\Psi_{S}|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{O}}|\Psi^{(S-1)}(t)}\right)\right)^{2}\\
