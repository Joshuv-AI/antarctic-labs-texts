Information Geometry of Wasserstein Statistics on Shapes and Affine Deformations
Shun-ichi Amari, Takeru Matsuda

Abstract
Information geometry and Wasserstein geometry are two main structures introduced in a manifold of probability distributions, and they capture its different characteristics. We study characteristics of Wasserstein geometry in the framework of [ 34 ] for the affine deformation statistical model, which is a multi-dimensional generalization of the location-scale model. We compare merits and demerits of estimators based on information geometry and Wasserstein geometry. The shape of a probability distribution and its affine deformation are separated in the Wasserstein geometry, showing its robustness against the waveform perturbation in exchange for the loss in Fisher efficiency. We show that the Wasserstein estimator is the moment estimator in the case of the elliptically symmetric affine deformation model. It coincides with the information-geometrical estimator (maximum-likelihood estimator) when the waveform is Gaussian. The role of the Wasserstein efficiency is elucidated in terms of robustness against waveform change.

Information geometry is constructed based on the invariance principle of Chentsov

Wasserstein geometry has an old origin, proposed by G. Monge in 1781 as a problem of transporting mass distributed in the form
p({\bm{x}})
to another

such that the total transportation cost is minimized. It depends on the transportation cost

{\bm{y}}

However, it is in general difficult to calculate the Wasserstein distance
[
45
]

See recent papers
[
27
,
45
]
for computational algorithms of the Wasserstein distance.

It is natural to search for the relation between the two geometries. There are a number of such trials, including

,

,

,

,

Chen and Li 2020
studied a general regular statistical model specified by a finite number of parameters and pulled-back a Riemannian metric from the Otto metric in the space consisting of all smooth and positive density functions on

Using this metric (W-metric), they studied the natural gradient estimation procedures, obtaining the result that the W-natural gradient has better performances compared to the natural gradient procedures due to the Fisher-Rao metric.
This is remarkable observation of showing the usefulness of W-metric in statistical inference.
We follow this idea of pulling-back the Otto Riemannian metric to a regular statistical model, using their regularity conditions which guarantee the existence and uniqueness of the pulled-back Riemannian metric.

Li and Zhao 2023
gave a unified framework for the two geometries. The present article is based on their framework and focuses on the affine deformation model, for which the standard waveform
f
and the deformation parameter
{\bm{\theta}}
are separated.

Li and Zhao 2023
further introduced the Wasserstein score function in parallel to the Fisher score function, defining two estimators

and

thereby.
The former

is the maximum likelihood estimator that maximizes the log likelihood.
This is the one that minimizes an invariant divergence from the empirical distribution

to a parametric model, where the empirical distribution is given based on

The latter Wasserstein estimator

is defined as the zero point of the Wasserstein score.
It is asymptotically equivalent to the minimizer of the
W
-divergence between the empirical distribution and model.
Also,

further defined the

-efficiency and

-efficiency of a consistent estimator

given a statistical model

Riemannian structures in the space of probability densities

We consider the space
{\mathcal{F}}=\left\{p({\bm{x}})\right\}
of all smooth positive probability density functions on
{\bm{R}}^{d}
that have finite second moments.
Later, we may relax the conditions of positivity and smoothness, when we discuss a parametric model, in particular the deformation model
1
1

1

For example, the probability density of the uniform distribution inside an ellipse takes zero outside of the ellipse and thus non-smooth on the boundary.
.
We define a divergence function
D[p({\bm{x}}),q({\bm{x}})]
, which represents the degree of difference between
p({\bm{x}})
and
q({\bm{x}})
. The square of the
L^{2}

p({\bm{x}})
and

and

1.

D[p({\bm{x}}),q({\bm{x}})]\geq 0
and the equality holds if and only if
p({\bm{x}})=q({\bm{x}})
.

2.

Let

be an infinitesimally small deviation of

. Then,

A divergence is said to be invariant if

of the coordinates from
{\bm{x}}\in{\bm{R}}^{d}
to

\tilde{p}({\bm{y}})=\left|\frac{\partial{\bm{x}}}{\partial{\bm{y}}}\right|p({\bm{x}}).

A typical invariant divergence is the

\displaystyle D_{\alpha}[p({\bm{x}}),q({\bm{x}})]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{4}{1-\alpha^{2}}\left(1-\int p({\bm{x}})^{(1+\alpha)/2}q({\bm{x}})^{(1-\alpha)/2}{\rm d}\bm{x}\right)

\alpha\neq\pm 1

For

.
The case

H^{2}[p({\bm{x}}),q({\bm{x}})]=\frac{1}{2}\int\left(\sqrt{p({\bm{x}})}-\sqrt{q({\bm{x}})}\right)^{2}{\rm d}\bm{x}.

\partial_{t}\rho({\bm{x}},t)+\nabla_{\bm{x}}\cdot\left\{\rho({\bm{x}},t)\nabla_{\bm{x}}\Phi({\bm{x}},t)\right\}=0.

The Wasserstein divergence is written in the dynamic formulation as

D_{W}\left[p({\bm{x}}),q({\bm{x}})\right]={\mathop{\inf}_{\Phi}}\int^{1}_{0}\int\|\nabla_{\bm{x}}\Phi({\bm{x}},t)\|^{2}\rho({\bm{x}},t){\rm d}\bm{x}{\rm d}t.

\mathcal{F}
by the Taylor expansion of

The Riemannian metric

In the case of the invariant divergence,

so that

-Wasserstein divergence, consider the change of density from

By using the potential

Score functions and estimators

g^{F}_{ij}({\bm{\theta}})={\rm{E}}\left[\partial_{i}l(\bm{x},{\bm{\theta}})\partial_{j}l(\bm{x},{\bm{\theta}})\right]=\int p(\bm{x},{\bm{\theta}})\partial_{i}l({\bm{x}},{\bm{\theta}})\partial_{j}l({\bm{x}},{\bm{\theta}}){\rm d}\bm{x}.

In the Wasserstein case,

\displaystyle g^{W}_{ij}({\bm{\theta}})

\displaystyle=-\int S^{W}_{i}({\bm{x}},{\bm{\theta}})\Delta_{p}S^{W}_{j}({\bm{x}},{\bm{\theta}}){\rm d}\bm{x}

\displaystyle=\int p({\bm{x}},{\bm{\theta}})\nabla_{\bm{x}}S^{W}_{i}({\bm{x}},{\bm{\theta}})^{\top}\nabla_{\bm{x}}S^{W}_{j}({\bm{x}},{\bm{\theta}}){\rm d}\bm{x}

\displaystyle={\rm E}[\nabla_{\bm{x}}S^{W}_{i}({\bm{x}},{\bm{\theta}})^{\top}\nabla_{\bm{x}}S^{W}_{j}({\bm{x}},{\bm{\theta}})],

(14)

where identity (
9
) is used.

The score functions
S_{i}({\bm{x}},{\bm{\theta}})
give a set of estimating functions from (
13
), which are used to obtain an estimator
\hat{\bm{\theta}}
.
Suppose that we have
n
independent observations
{\bm{x}}_{1},\cdots,{\bm{x}}_{n}
from

.
Let
\hat{p}_{\rm{emp}}({\bm{x}})
be the empirical distribution given by

\hat{p}_{\rm{emp}}({\bm{x}})=\frac{1}{n}\sum^{n}_{t=1}\delta\left({\bm{x}}-{\bm{x}}_{t}\right).

Then, replacing expectation
{\rm{E}}
in (
13
) by the expectation with respect to the empirical distribution, we have estimating equations

{\rm{E}}_{\rm{emp}}[S_{i}({\bm{x}},\hat{\bm{\theta}})]=\frac{1}{n}\sum^{n}_{t=1}S_{i}({\bm{x}}_{t},\hat{\bm{\theta}})=0,\quad i=1,\cdots,m.

(15)

The solution
\hat{\bm{\theta}}
gives a consistent estimator for large

under regularity conditions on the score function
[
51
]
.
Roughly speaking,
\hat{\bm{\theta}}
is the projection of
\hat{p}_{\rm{emp}}({\bm{x}})
to the model
\mathcal{M}
with respect to the metric
g
(see Figure
3
).
It is the solution of

\langle\hat{p}_{\rm{emp}}({\bm{x}}),S_{i}({\bm{x}},{\bm{\theta}})\rangle=0,

giving a consistent estimator
\hat{\bm{\theta}}
.
A consistent estimator is Fisher efficient when the projection is orthogonal with respect to the Fisher–Rao metric
[
5
]
.

.

For the invariant Fisherian case, the estimator
\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{F}
defined by (
15

\frac{1}{n}\sum^{n}_{t=1}S_{i}^{F}({\bm{x}}_{t},\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{F})=0.

The Cramér-Rao theorem gives a matrix inequality for any unbiased estimator
\hat{\bm{\theta}}
,

{\rm{Cov}}[\hat{\bm{\theta}}]\succeq\frac{1}{n}g^{-1}_{F}({\bm{\theta}}),

where
{\rm{Cov}}[\cdot]
is the covariance matrix and
\succeq
denotes the matrix order defined by the positive semidefiniteness.
The maximum likelihood estimator
\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{F}
satisfies

{\rm{Cov}}[\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{F}]\approx\frac{1}{n}g^{-1}_{F}({\bm{\theta}})

asymptotically. Hence, it minimizes the error covariance matrix and the minimized error covariance is given asymptotically by the inverse of the Fisher metric tensor
g_{F}
divided by
n
.
Such a property is called the Fisher efficiency.

In the following, we study the characteristics of the estimator
\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{W}
defined by (
15
) with the Wasserstein score:

\frac{1}{n}\sum^{n}_{t=1}S_{i}^{W}({\bm{x}}_{t},\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{W})=0.

We call

-estimator) following

]

In the case of the one-dimensional location-scale model, the Wasserstein estimator is asymptotically equivalent to the estimator obtained by minimizing the Wasserstein divergence (transportation cost) from the empirical distribution
\hat{p}_{\rm{emp}}({\bm{x}})
to model
\mathcal{M}
:

\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{Wp}={\mathop{\arg\min}_{{\bm{\theta}}}}\;D_{W}\left[\hat{p}_{\rm{emp}}({\bm{x}}),p({\bm{x}},{\bm{\theta}})\right].

(16)

See the end of Section

The properties of

were studied in detail by

Note that

Affine deformation model

Now, we focus on the affine deformation model.

In the following, we denote the log probability of standard shape

For each standard shape function

For a general

and

is a diagonal matrix with diagonal elements

Thus,

Since

\mathcal{M}_{f}={\bm{R}}^{d}\times\bm{R}_{+}^{d}\times{\mathcal{O}}(d)\times{\mathcal{O}}(d)/\sim.

See

When

All ellipsoidal shapes are equivalent to a spherical shape. A family of special parallel-piped shapes are equivalent to a cubic form (see Figure

Here, we focus on the cases where

which is elliptically symmetric.
We restrict the parameter

Namely, the dimension of

First, we consider the
F

(maximum likelihood estimator).
The log-likelihood is given by

\log p({\bm{x}},{\bm{\theta}})=\log|\Lambda|+\log g(\|\Lambda({\bm{x}}-{\bm{\mu}})\|).

When there are
n

\sum^{n}_{j=1}\partial_{\bm{\theta}}\log p\left({\bm{x}}_{j},{\bm{\theta}}\right)=0.

The solution
\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{F}
strongly depends of the shape
g
.

Contrary to this, the
W
-estimator
\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{W}
does not depend on the shape
g
as follows.
We write the
i

\bm{e}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
so that
(\bm{e}_{i})_{j}=\delta_{ij}
.

Lemma 1
.

For a symmetric
A\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
,
\bm{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
c\in\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{x}}\left(\frac{1}{2}\bm{x}^{\top}A\bm{x}+\bm{b}^{\top}\bm{x}+c\right)=A\bm{x}+\bm{b},\quad\Delta_{\bm{x}}\left(\frac{1}{2}\bm{x}^{\top}A\bm{x}+\bm{b}^{\top}\bm{x}+c\right)={\rm tr}(A).

Proof.

Straightforward calculation.
∎

Lemma 2
.

Let
A,B\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
be symmetric matrices.
If
A
is positive definite, then the Sylvester equation
AX+XA=B
has the unique solution
X
, which satisfies
X^{\top}=X
and
{\rm tr}(X)={\rm tr}(A^{-1}B)/2
.

Proof.

From the positive semidefiniteness of
A
, the spectra of
A
and
-A
are disjoint. Thus, from Theorem VII.2.1 of
[
12
]
, the Sylvester equation
AX+XA=B
has a unique solution.

Let
X
be the solution of the Sylvester equation.
From
A^{\top}=A
, we have
AX^{\top}+X^{\top}A=(AX+XA)^{\top}=B^{\top}=B
, which means that
X^{\top}
is also a solution of the Sylvester equation.
Since the solution is unique, it implies
X^{\top}=X
.
Also, from the positive semidefiniteness of
A
and
AX+XA=B
, we have
X+A^{-1}XA=A^{-1}B
.
Taking the trace and using
{\rm tr}(A^{-1}XA)={\rm tr}(X)
, we obtain
{\rm tr}X={\rm tr}(A^{-1}B)/2
.
∎

Theorem 1
.

For the elliptically symmetric deformation model (
18
), the Wasserstein score functions are quadratic in
\bm{x}
.
Specifically, the Wasserstein score function for
\mu_{i}
is

\displaystyle S_{\mu_{i}}^{{W}}(\bm{x},\bm{\theta})=x_{i}-\mu_{i},

and the Wasserstein score function for
\Lambda_{ij}
is

\displaystyle S_{\Lambda_{ij}}^{W}(\bm{x},\bm{\theta})=\frac{1}{2}\bm{x}^{\top}A\bm{x}+\bm{b}^{\top}\bm{x}-{\rm E}_{\bm{\theta}}\left[\frac{1}{2}\bm{x}^{\top}A\bm{x}+\bm{b}^{\top}\bm{x}\right],

where
A
is the unique solution of the Sylvester equation
\Lambda^{2}A+A\Lambda^{2}=-\Lambda\bm{e}_{i}\bm{e}_{j}^{\top}-\bm{e}_{i}\bm{e}_{j}^{\top}\Lambda
and
\bm{b}=-A\bm{\mu}
.

Proof.

We show that the above
S^{W}
’s satisfy the Poisson equation (
11
) directly.

First, we consider the mean parameter
\mu_{i}
.
From (
18
),

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\mu_{i}}\log p(\bm{x},\bm{\theta})=-\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}\log p(\bm{x},\bm{\theta})=-\nabla_{\bm{x}}\log p(\bm{x},\bm{\theta})^{\top}\bm{e}_{i}.

Also, from Lemma
1
,

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{x}}(x_{i}-\mu_{i})=\bm{e}_{i},\quad\Delta_{\bm{x}}(x_{i}-\mu_{i})=0.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{x}}\log p({\bm{x}},{\bm{\theta}})^{\top}\nabla_{\bm{x}}(x_{i}-\mu_{i})+\Delta_{\bm{x}}(x_{i}-\mu_{i})+\frac{\partial}{\partial\mu_{i}}\log p({\bm{x}},{\bm{\theta}})=0.

Thus, the Wasserstein score function for the mean parameter
\mu_{i}
is

\displaystyle S_{\mu_{i}}^{{W}}(\bm{x},\bm{\theta})=x_{i}-\mu_{i}.

Next, we consider the deformation parameter
\Lambda_{ij}
.
Since

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\Lambda_{ij}}\|\Lambda(\bm{x}-\bm{\mu})\|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\|\Lambda(\bm{x}-\bm{\mu})\|^{-1}\frac{\partial}{\partial\Lambda_{ij}}(\bm{x}-\bm{\mu})^{\top}\Lambda^{2}(\bm{x}-\bm{\mu})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\|\Lambda(\bm{x}-\bm{\mu})\|^{-1}(\bm{x}-\bm{\mu})^{\top}\frac{\partial\Lambda^{2}}{\partial\Lambda_{ij}}(\bm{x}-\bm{\mu})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\|\Lambda(\bm{x}-\bm{\mu})\|^{-1}(\bm{x}-\bm{\mu})^{\top}(\Lambda\bm{e}_{i}\bm{e}_{j}^{\top}+\bm{e}_{i}\bm{e}_{j}^{\top}\Lambda)(\bm{x}-\bm{\mu}),

we have

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\Lambda_{ij}}\log p(\bm{x},\theta)

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial\Lambda_{ij}}\log\det\Lambda+\frac{\partial}{\partial\Lambda_{ij}}\log g(\|\Lambda(\bm{x}-\bm{\mu})\|)

\displaystyle=(\Lambda^{-1})_{ij}+\frac{g^{\prime}(\|\Lambda(\bm{x}-\bm{\mu})\|)}{g(\|\Lambda(\bm{x}-\bm{\mu})\|)}\frac{\partial}{\partial\Lambda_{ij}}\|\Lambda(\bm{x}-\bm{\mu})\|

\displaystyle=(\Lambda^{-1})_{ij}+\frac{g^{\prime}(\|\Lambda(\bm{x}-\bm{\mu})\|)}{2\|\Lambda(\bm{x}-\bm{\mu})\|g(\|\Lambda(\bm{x}-\bm{\mu})\|)}(\bm{x}-\bm{\mu})^{\top}(\Lambda\bm{e}_{i}\bm{e}_{j}^{\top}+\bm{e}_{i}\bm{e}_{j}^{\top}\Lambda)(\bm{x}-\bm{\mu})

\displaystyle=-\frac{g^{\prime}(\|\Lambda(\bm{x}-\bm{\mu})\|)}{\|\Lambda(\bm{x}-\bm{\mu})\|g(\|\Lambda(\bm{x}-\bm{\mu})\|)}\left(-\frac{1}{2}\bm{x}^{\top}L\bm{x}+\bm{x}^{\top}L\bm{\mu}-\frac{1}{2}\bm{\mu}^{\top}L\bm{\mu}\right)+(\Lambda^{-1})_{ij},

where
L=\Lambda\bm{e}_{i}\bm{e}_{j}^{\top}+\bm{e}_{i}\bm{e}_{j}^{\top}\Lambda
.
Let

\displaystyle S(\bm{x})=\frac{1}{2}\bm{x}^{\top}A\bm{x}+\bm{b}^{\top}\bm{x}-{\rm E}_{\theta}\left[\frac{1}{2}\bm{x}^{\top}A\bm{x}+\bm{b}^{\top}\bm{x}\right],

where
A
is the unique solution of the Sylvester equation
\Lambda^{2}A+A\Lambda^{2}=-L
and
\bm{b}=-A\bm{\mu}
.
From Lemma
1
and Lemma
2
,

\displaystyle\Delta_{\bm{x}}S(\bm{x})={\rm tr}A=-\frac{1}{2}{\rm tr}(\Lambda^{-2}L)=-\frac{1}{2}{\rm tr}(\Lambda^{-2}(\Lambda\bm{e}_{i}\bm{e}_{j}^{\top}+\bm{e}_{i}\bm{e}_{i}^{\top}\Lambda))=-(\Lambda^{-1})_{ij}.

Also,

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{x}}\log p({\bm{x}},{\bm{\theta}})^{\top}\nabla_{\bm{x}}S(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{g^{\prime}(\|\Lambda(\bm{x}-\bm{\mu})\|)}{g(\|\Lambda(\bm{x}-\bm{\mu})\|)}\nabla_{\bm{x}}(\|\Lambda(\bm{x}-\bm{\mu})\|)^{\top}(A\bm{x}+\bm{b})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{g^{\prime}(\|\Lambda(\bm{x}-\bm{\mu})\|)}{\|\Lambda(\bm{x}-\bm{\mu})\|g(\|\Lambda(\bm{x}-\bm{\mu})\|)}(\Lambda^{2}(\bm{x}-\bm{\mu}))^{\top}(A\bm{x}+\bm{b})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{g^{\prime}(\|\Lambda(\bm{x}-\bm{\mu})\|)}{\|\Lambda(\bm{x}-\bm{\mu})\|g(\|\Lambda(\bm{x}-\bm{\mu})\|)}\left(\frac{1}{2}\bm{x}^{\top}(\Lambda^{2}A+A\Lambda^{2})\bm{x}+\bm{x}^{\top}(\Lambda^{2}\bm{b}-A\Lambda^{2}\bm{\mu})-\bm{\mu}^{\top}\Lambda^{2}\bm{b}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{g^{\prime}(\|\Lambda(\bm{x}-\bm{\mu})\|)}{\|\Lambda(\bm{x}-\bm{\mu})\|g(\|\Lambda(\bm{x}-\bm{\mu})\|)}\left(-\frac{1}{2}\bm{x}^{\top}L\bm{x}+\bm{x}^{\top}L\bm{\mu}-\frac{1}{2}\bm{\mu}^{\top}L\bm{\mu}\right),

where we used

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{x}}(\|\Lambda(\bm{x}-\bm{\mu})\|)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\|\Lambda(\bm{x}-\bm{\mu})\|}\nabla_{\bm{x}}(\|\Lambda(\bm{x}-\bm{\mu})\|^{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\|\Lambda(\bm{x}-\bm{\mu})\|}\nabla_{\bm{x}}((\bm{x}-\bm{\mu})^{\top}\Lambda^{2}(\bm{x}-\bm{\mu}))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\|\Lambda(\bm{x}-\bm{\mu})\|}\Lambda^{2}(\bm{x}-\bm{\mu}).

Therefore,

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{x}}\log p({\bm{x}},{\bm{\theta}})^{\top}\nabla_{\bm{x}}S(\bm{x})+\Delta_{\bm{x}}S(\bm{x})+\frac{\partial}{\partial\Lambda_{ij}}\log p({\bm{x}},{\bm{\theta}})=0,

which means that
S(\bm{x})
is the Wasserstein score function for
\Lambda_{ij}
.

∎

Theorem 3

When

Here, we discuss the relation between the current Wasserstein estimator

We assume

The estimator (

which is the empirical mean and coincides with the moment estimator.
Also, the estimator (

From

which leads to

\hat{\sigma}_{Wp}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}k_{i}x_{(i)}\approx\frac{1}{n\sigma}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x^{2}_{(i)}=\frac{1}{n\sigma}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x^{2}_{i}.

Since
\hat{\sigma}_{Wp}\approx\sigma
asymptotically,

\hat{\sigma}_{Wp}^{2}\approx\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x^{2}_{i}.

This shows that
\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{Wp}=(\hat{{\bm{\mu}}}_{Wp},\hat{\sigma}_{Wp})
asymptotically coincides with the second-order moment estimator
\hat{{\bm{\theta}}}_{W}=(\hat{{\bm{\mu}}}_{W},\hat{\sigma}_{W})
.

6
Wasserstein covariance and robustness

Following
[
34
]
, we define the Wasserstein covariance (
W
-covariance) matrix
{\rm Var}_{\theta}^{\mathrm{W}}[\hat{\bm{\theta}}]
of an estimator
\hat{\bm{\theta}}
by the positive semidefinite matrix given by

\displaystyle{\rm Var}_{\theta}^{\mathrm{W}}[\hat{\bm{\theta}}]=({\rm E}_{\theta}[(\nabla_{\bm{x}}\hat{{\theta}}_{a})^{\top}(\nabla_{\bm{x}}\hat{{\theta}}_{b})])_{ab},

(21)

where
\nabla_{\bm{x}}\hat{\bm{\theta}}
is assumed to be square integrable.

Li and Zhao 2023
showed the Wasserstein–Cramer–Rao inequality

{\rm Var}_{\theta}^{\mathrm{W}}(\hat{\bm{\theta}})\succeq\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}{\rm E}_{\theta}[\hat{\bm{\theta}}]\right)^{\top}G_{\mathrm{W}}(\theta)^{-1}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}{\rm E}_{\theta}[\hat{\bm{\theta}}]\right),

(22)

where

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}{\rm E}_{\theta}[\hat{\bm{\theta}}]:=\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{b}}{\rm E}_{\theta}[\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{a}]\right)_{ab}.

A consistent estimator
\hat{\bm{\theta}}
is said to be Wasserstein efficient (
W
-efficient) if its Wasserstein covariance asymptotically satisfies (
22
) with equality.
We give a proof of the Wasserstein–Cramer–Rao inequality based on the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality in the Appendix.

We show that the Wasserstein covariance of an estimator can be viewed as a measure of robustness against additive noise.
Suppose that
X\sim p({\bm{x}},{\bm{\theta}})
and we estimate
\bm{\theta}
from the noisy observation
\widetilde{X}=X+Z
, where
Z
is independent from
X
,
{\rm E}[Z]=0
and
{\rm Var}[Z]=\sigma^{2}I
with
\sigma^{2}
sufficiently small.
The probability density of
\widetilde{X}
is given by
\widetilde{p}({\bm{x}},{\bm{\theta}})=p({\bm{x}},{\bm{\theta}})\ast q(\bm{x})
, where
\ast
is the convolution and
q
is the probability density of the noise
Z
.
Namely, the noise changes the waveform from
p
to
\widetilde{p}
.
Generally, the estimator degrades when the noise is added.
Here, we quantify the robustness of an estimator against noise based on how much its variance increases due to noise.
Namely, we focus on
{{\rm Var}_{\theta}[\hat{\bm{\theta}}(X+Z)]-{\rm Var}_{\theta}[\hat{\bm{\theta}}(X)]}
, which does not depend on the exact distribution of
Z
but only its first two moments.
If this quantity is small, it implies that the estimator is not much affected by noise contamination, which can be viewed as its robustness.
This quantity is closely related to the Wasserstein covariance as follows.

Theorem 4
.

The Wasserstein covariance satisfies

\displaystyle{\rm Var}^{\mathrm{W}}_{\theta}[\hat{\bm{\theta}}]_{ab}=

\displaystyle\lim_{\sigma^{2}\to 0}\frac{{\rm Var}_{\theta}[\hat{\bm{\theta}}(X+Z)]_{ab}-{\rm Var}_{\theta}[\hat{\bm{\theta}}(X)]_{ab}}{\sigma^{2}}

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{1}{2}\left({\rm Cov}_{\theta}[\hat{{\theta}}_{a}(X),\Delta\hat{{\theta}}_{b}(X)]+{\rm Cov}_{\theta}[\hat{{\theta}}_{b}(X),\Delta\hat{{\theta}}_{a}(X)]\right),

where
\Delta
is the Laplacian.
In particular, if
\Delta\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{a}(X)
is constant for every
a
(e.g.
\hat{\bm{\theta}}
is quadratic in
\bm{x}
), then

{\rm Var}^{\mathrm{W}}_{\theta}[\hat{\bm{\theta}}]=\lim_{\sigma^{2}\to 0}\frac{{\rm Var}_{\theta}[\hat{\bm{\theta}}(X+Z)]-{\rm Var}_{\theta}[\hat{\bm{\theta}}(X)]}{\sigma^{2}}.

Proof.

By Taylor expansion, for sufficiently small
\bm{z}
,

\displaystyle\hat{{\theta}}_{a}(\bm{x}+\bm{z})\approx\hat{{\theta}}_{a}(\bm{x})+\sum_{i}\frac{\partial\hat{{\theta}}_{a}}{\partial x_{i}}(\bm{x})z_{i}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j}\frac{\partial^{2}\hat{{\theta}}_{a}}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}(\bm{x})z_{i}z_{j}.

From
{\rm E}[Z]=0
,
{\rm Var}[Z]=\sigma^{2}I
and the independence of
X
and
Z
,

\displaystyle{\rm E}_{\theta}[\hat{{\theta}}_{a}(X+Z)]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\rm E}_{\theta}[\hat{{\theta}}_{a}(X)]+\sum_{i}{\rm E}_{\theta}\left[\frac{\partial\hat{{\theta}}_{a}}{\partial x_{i}}(X)\right]{\rm E}[z_{i}]+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j}{\rm E}_{\theta}\left[\frac{\partial^{2}\hat{{\theta}}_{a}}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}(X)\right]{\rm E}[z_{i}z_{j}]+o(\sigma^{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\rm E}_{\theta}[\hat{{\theta}}_{a}(X)]+\frac{1}{2}{\rm E}_{\theta}[\Delta\hat{{\theta}}_{a}(X)]\sigma^{2}+o(\sigma^{2}).

Also,

\displaystyle{\rm E}_{\theta}[\hat{{\theta}}_{a}(X+Z)\hat{{\theta}}_{b}(X+Z)]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\rm E}_{\theta}[\hat{{\theta}}_{a}(X)\hat{{\theta}}_{b}(X)]+\frac{1}{2}{\rm E}_{\theta}[\hat{{\theta}}_{a}(X)\Delta\hat{{\theta}}_{b}(X)+\hat{{\theta}}_{b}(X)\Delta\hat{{\theta}}_{a}(X)]\sigma^{2}+{\rm Var}_{\theta}^{\mathrm{W}}[\hat{\bm{\theta}}]_{ab}\sigma^{2}+o(\sigma^{2}).

Then,

\displaystyle{\rm Var}_{\theta}[\hat{\bm{\theta}}(X+Z)]_{ab}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\rm E}_{\theta}[\hat{{\theta}}_{a}(X+Z)\hat{{\theta}}_{b}(X+Z)]-{\rm E}_{\theta}[\hat{{\theta}}_{a}(X+Z)]{\rm E}_{\theta}[\hat{{\theta}}_{b}(X+Z)]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\rm Var}_{\theta}[\hat{\bm{\theta}}(X)]_{ab}+{\rm Var}^{\mathrm{W}}_{\theta}[\hat{\bm{\theta}}]_{ab}\sigma^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{1}{2}\left({\rm Cov}_{\theta}[\hat{{\theta}}_{a}(X),\Delta\hat{{\theta}}_{b}(X)]+{\rm Cov}_{\theta}[\hat{{\theta}}_{b}(X),\Delta\hat{{\theta}}_{a}(X)]\right)\sigma^{2}+o(\sigma^{2}),

where the covariance term vanishes when
\hat{\bm{\theta}}
is quadratic in
\bm{x}
.
∎

For the elliptically symmetric deformation model (
18
), from Theorem
4
and Corollary
1
, the Wasserstein covariance quantifies the robustness of the Wasserstein estimator and the Wasserstein–Cramer–Rao inequality gives its limit.
It is an interesting future problem to investigate when the Wasserstein estimator attains the Wasserstein efficiency.
Note that the Fisher efficiency (in finite samples), which is defined by the usual Cramer–Rao inequality, is attained by the maximum likelihood estimator if and only if the estimand is the expectation parameter of an exponential family.

7
Conclusion

Statistical inference based on the likelihood principle enjoyed great successes, and information geometry has played an important role in it. However, Wasserstein divergence gives another viewpoint, which is based on the geometric structure of the sample space
X
. There are many applications of the Wasserstein geometry not only to the transportation problem but to computer vision and image analysis and recently deep learning in AI.

We studied the Wasserstein statistics using the framework of
[
34
]
, proving that the Wasserstein covariance quantifies robustness against the convolutional waveform deformation due to observation noise.
We further studied
W
-statistics of the affine deformation model.
We showed
F
-efficiency and
W
-efficiency of estimators
\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{F}
and
\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{W}
. We elucidated how the waveform
f
contributes to the efficiencies. The Gaussian distribution gives the only waveform in which the
F
-estimator and
W
-estimator coincide.

Estimation of a covariance matrix, especially in high dimensions under special structure (e.g. low-rankness, sparsity), has been an important problem in statistics.
It is an interesting future problem to investigate whether the Wasserstein geometry is helpful in covariance estimation.

Other than the elliptically symmetric deformation model, it is difficult in general to derive the Wasserstein score, which corresponds to the infinitesimal optimal transport. It is an interesting future problem to explore other statistical models for which the Wasserstein score is obtained in closed form. Also, it would be useful to develop approximation techniques for the Wasserstein score.

The present paper is only a first step to construct general Wasserstein statistics. In future work, we need to use more general statistical models.
We also need to extend our approach to statistical theories of hypothesis testing, pattern classification, clustering and many other statistical problems based on the Wasserstein geometry.

Appendix A
Proof of Wasserstein–Cramer–Rao inequality

For random vectors
U
and
V
,

\displaystyle 0\leq{\rm E}\|tU+V\|^{2}={\rm E}[\|U\|^{2}]t^{2}+2{\rm E}[U^{\top}V]t+{\rm E}[\|V\|^{2}]

for every
t
.
Thus, by considering the discriminant of the quadratic equation,

\displaystyle{\rm E}[U^{\top}V]^{2}\leq{\rm E}[\|U\|^{2}]{\rm E}[\|V\|^{2}].

(23)

Substituting

\displaystyle U=\sum_{i}a_{i}\nabla_{\bm{x}}\hat{\theta}_{i},\quad V=\sum_{j}b_{j}\nabla_{\bm{x}}S_{j}^{W}

into (
23
) yields

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{i,j}a_{i}b_{j}{\rm E}_{\theta}[(\nabla_{\bm{x}}\hat{\theta}_{i})^{\top}(\nabla_{\bm{x}}S_{j}^{W})]\right)^{2}\leq{\rm E}_{\theta}\left[\left\|\sum_{i}a_{i}\nabla_{\bm{x}}\hat{\theta}_{i}\right\|^{2}\right]{\rm E}_{\theta}\left[\left\|\sum_{j}b_{j}\nabla_{\bm{x}}S_{j}^{W}\right\|^{2}\right].

(24)

From the property (
11
) of the Wasserstein score function,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{j}}{\rm E}_{\theta}[\hat{\theta}_{i}]

\displaystyle=\int\hat{\theta}_{i}(x)\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{j}}p(x,\theta){\rm d}x

\displaystyle=-\int\hat{\theta}_{i}(x)\nabla_{\bm{x}}\cdot(p(x,\theta)\nabla_{\bm{x}}S_{j}^{W}(x,\theta)){\rm d}x

\displaystyle=-\int(\nabla_{\bm{x}}\cdot(\hat{\theta}_{i}(x)p(x,\theta)\nabla_{\bm{x}}S_{j}^{W}(x,\theta))-(\nabla_{\bm{x}}\hat{\theta}_{i}(x))^{\top}(\nabla_{\bm{x}}S_{j}^{W}(x,\theta))p(x,\theta)){\rm d}x

\displaystyle={\rm E}_{\theta}[(\nabla_{\bm{x}}\hat{\theta}_{i})^{\top}(\nabla_{\bm{x}}S_{j}^{W})],

which yields

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j}a_{i}b_{j}{\rm E}_{\theta}[(\nabla_{\bm{x}}\hat{\theta}_{i})^{\top}(\nabla_{\bm{x}}S_{j}^{W})]=\sum_{i,j}a_{i}b_{j}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{j}}{\rm E}_{\theta}[\hat{\theta}_{i}]=\bm{a}^{\top}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}{\rm E}_{\theta}[\hat{\theta}]\right)\bm{b}.

(25)

From the definition of the Wasserstein covariance (
21
),

\displaystyle{\rm E}_{\theta}\left[\left\|\sum_{i}a_{i}\nabla_{\bm{x}}\hat{\theta}_{i}\right\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle={\rm E}_{\theta}\left[\sum_{i,j}a_{i}a_{j}(\nabla_{\bm{x}}\hat{\theta}_{i})^{\top}(\nabla_{\bm{x}}\hat{\theta}_{j})\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}a_{i}a_{j}{\rm Var}_{\theta}^{\mathrm{W}}(\hat{\theta})_{ij}

\displaystyle=\bm{a}^{\top}{\rm Var}_{\theta}^{\mathrm{W}}(\hat{\theta})\bm{a}.

(26)

From the definition of the Wasserstein information matrix (
14
),

\displaystyle{\rm E}_{\theta}\left[\left\|\sum_{j}b_{j}\nabla_{\bm{x}}S_{j}^{W}\right\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle={\rm E}_{\theta}\left[\sum_{i,j}b_{i}b_{j}(\nabla_{\bm{x}}S_{i}^{W})^{\top}(\nabla_{\bm{x}}S_{j}^{W})\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}b_{i}b_{j}G_{\mathrm{W}}(\theta)_{ij}

\displaystyle=\bm{b}^{\top}G_{\mathrm{W}}(\theta)\bm{b}.

(27)

Substituting (
25
), (
26
) and (
27
) into (
24
), we obtain

\displaystyle\left(\bm{a}^{\top}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}{\rm E}_{\theta}[\hat{\theta}]\right)\bm{b}\right)^{2}\leq\bm{a}^{\top}{\rm Var}_{\theta}^{\mathrm{W}}(\hat{\theta})\bm{a}\cdot\bm{b}^{\top}G_{\mathrm{W}}(\theta)\bm{b}.

By putting

\displaystyle\bm{b}=G_{\mathrm{W}}(\theta)^{-1}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}{\rm E}_{\theta}[\hat{\theta}]\right)\bm{a},

it leads to

\displaystyle\bm{a}^{\top}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}{\rm E}_{\theta}[\hat{\theta}]\right)G_{\mathrm{W}}(\theta)^{-1}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}{\rm E}_{\theta}[\hat{\theta}]\right)\bm{a}\leq\bm{a}^{\top}{\rm Var}_{\theta}^{\mathrm{W}}(\hat{\theta})\bm{a},

which is equal to the Wasserstein Cramer–Rao inequality (
22
).
