Probing cosmic strings by reconstructing polarization rotation of the cosmic microwave background
and Simone Ferraro

Abstract
Cosmic birefringence—the rotation of the polarization of the cosmic microwave background (CMB) photons as they travel to the Earth—is a smoking gun for axion-like particles (ALPs) that interact with the photon. It has recently been suggested that topological defects in the ALP field called cosmic strings can cause polarization rotation in quantized amounts that are proportional to the electromagnetic chiral anomaly coefficient 𝒜 \mathcal{A} , which holds direct information about physics at very high energies. In this work, we study the detectability of a random network of cosmic strings through estimating rotation using quadratic estimators (QEs). We show that the QE derived from the maximum likelihood estimator is equivalent to the recently proposed global-minimum-variance QE; the classic Hu-Okamoto QE equals the global-minimum-variance QE under special conditions, but is otherwise still nearly globally optimal. We calculate the sensitivity of QEs to cosmic birefringence from string networks, for the Planck satellite mission, as well as for third- and fourth-generation ground-based CMB experiments. Using published binned rotation power spectrum derived from the Planck 2015 polarization data, we obtain a constraint 𝒜 2 ​ ξ 0 < 0.93 \mathcal{A}^{2}\,\xi_{0}<0.93 at the 95% confidence level, where ξ 0 \xi_{0} is the total length of strings in units of the Hubble scale per Hubble volume, for a phenomenological but reasonable string network model describing a continuous distribution of string sizes. Looking forward, experiments such as the Simons Observatory and CMB-S4 will either discover or falsify the existence of an ALP string network for the theoretically plausible parameter space 𝒜 2 ​ ξ 0 ≳ 0.01 \mathcal{A}^{2}\,\xi_{0}\gtrsim 0.01 .

Introduction

The intensity and polarization anistropies of the cosmic microwave background (CMB) are the most stringently tested physical observables in cosmology. At the leading order, the anisotropies are Gaussian random quantities and follow isotropic power spectra that are precisely predictable from physical models of the primordial Universe. Nearly originated from the cosmic horizon, the CMB photons traverse the longest distances in the Universe without major absorption or scattering.

The above two reasons render the CMB a sensitive probe of cosmic birefringence—the coherent rotation of the polarization planes as CMB photons travel along the line of sight. This effect can be mediated by an ultralight axion-like particles (ALPs, or simply “axions” in this work) that couples to the photon via a Chern-Simons coupling
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
. Such ALPs are a ubiquitous outcome of string theory compactifications
[
4
]
; they may account for the dark matter
[
5
]
, or hold clues to the mystery of the Dark Energy
[
6
,
7
]
. Recently, Ref.
[
8
]
pointed out that topological defects in the ALP field, termed cosmic strings, can imprint birefringence patterns in the CMB in a quantized manner. Remarkably, the observable rotation angle is insensitive to the ALP-photon coupling constant, which is unconstrained and subject to renormalization. In fact, the rotation angle varies in quantized amounts as the line of sight crosses individual strings, which is determined by the electromagnetic chiral anomaly coefficient
\mathcal{A}
, free from renormalization
[
9
]
. Not only does
\mathcal{A}
offer insight into the smallest unit of electric charge
[
10
]
, it is also useful for distinguishing between beyond-the-Standard-Model theories, whose matter content and charge assignment at very high energies lead to predictions for the value of
\mathcal{A}

[
8
]
.

Recently, the study of CMB birefringence has heated up after tentative evidence for birefringence that is highly coherent across the sky was reported in a refined analysis of the Planck 2018 polarization data
[
11
]
, with implications for ALP searches
[
12
]
. While an isotropic polarization rotation would be evidence for symmetry-breaking phenomena beyond the Standard Model, it is the power spectrum of direction-dependent rotations that will inform us about the spatial structure of birefringence sources.

While the anisotropic rotation induced by the strings is non-Gaussian by nature, in this work we study detectability using quadratic estimators which are optimal for Gaussian random fields. In this method, one seeks statistical correlation between Fourier modes of the anisotropies with different Fourier wave vectors. This method is appropriate for the scenario of a large number of strings within the observable Universe, but should also offer decent detection statistics in the case of relatively few strings. This simplification may be adequate if one aims for a first statistical detection of rotation, before characterization of the non-Gaussian behaviors of the rotation pattern is carried out.

Quadratic estimators for the purpose of estimating direction-dependent birefringence were first investigated in Refs.
[
13
,
14
]
in the full-sky formalism and in Ref.
[
15
]
in the flat-sky approximation. This method was used to derive bounds on the rotation power spectrum in the WMAP data
[
16
]
. In this work, we compare a few differently constructed quadratic estimators in general as well as in the context of cosmic birefringence. We study the detectability of direction-dependent birefringence as a result of a network of ALP cosmic strings
[
8
,
17
]
, for CMB anisotropy data collected by the Planck mission as well as forthcoming data to be collected by third and fourth generation ground-based CMB experiments. Instead of using the
EB

[

]

[

\mathcal{A}^{2}\,\xi_{0}
through these CMB experiments, where
\xi_{0}
is the total length of strings per Hubble volume in units of the Hubble length, as described in Section
7
.

In the absence of cosmic birefringence, the intervening large-scale structure of the Universe induces cross-correlation between Fourier modes of the CMB anisotropies via gravitational lensing. Nevertheless, we will show that estimation of polarization rotation is nearly orthogonal to the estimation of the lensing potential, as was also discussed in Ref.
[
14
]
. Therefore, for the purpose of detecting polarization rotation, it is a good approximation to treat the lensed anistropies as Gaussian random fields with statistical isotropy and to completely neglect mode correlations induced by lensing.

We study three ways to construct quadratic estimators for the rotation field using primary anisotropies. The first one is the famous Hu-Okamoto (HO) estimator
[
18
]
, which has been widely employed in weak lensing reconstruction of the CMB. In the second way, the so-called global-minimum-variance (GMV) estimator is constructed following the recent work of Ref.
[
19
]
, which improves upon the HO estimator. For the third one, we derive an optimal quadratic estimator following the maximization of the full likelihood function for the anisotropy observables.

We explicitly show that the third quadratic estimator, obtained as an approximation to the maximal likelihood estimator, is equivalent to the GMV estimator, as one would anticipate based on the definition of the GMV estimator. Although the GMV estimator is in general distinct from the HO estimator and is mathematically guaranteed to be at least as sensitive as the latter
[
19
]
, we show that the two are equivalent when rotation estimators are constructed only from the
E
- and
B
-mode polarization anisotropies, which includes the dominant
EB
estimators. We phrase a general sufficient condition for such equivalence. Furthermore, we numerically find that if the temperature anisotropies are also included, then the difference in the
reconstruction noise between the GMV and HO estimators for the rotation field is insignificant at both current and forthcoming CMB experiments, with less than 0.5% improvement from GMV across the range of angular scales.

We find that information about the plausible cosmic string network is dominated by rotation reconstruction at large scales
L\lesssim 100
, and that data from the Planck mission should already have constraining power for a significant part of the theoretically favored parameter space,
\mathcal{A}^{2}\,\xi_{0}\gtrsim\mathcal{O}(1)
. For the entire theoretically plausible parameter range
\mathcal{A}^{2}\,\xi_{0}\gtrsim 0.01
, we find that future CMB experiments will have the definitive statistical power to either substantiate or falsify the existence of cosmic strings from ALPs that couple to electromagnetism.

Using published birefringence power spectrum extracted from Planck 2015 data
[
20
]
, we place upper bounds on the parameter
\mathcal{A}^{2}\,\xi_{0}
. For two phenomenological string network models developed in
[
17
]
, we find that
\mathcal{A}^{2}\,\xi_{0}<8.0
if the Universe is populated by string loops of the same but arbitrary size, and
\mathcal{A}^{2}\,\xi_{0}<0.93
if, more realistically, the string loops have a continuous distribution of sizes, both at the 95% confidence level. These results are consistent with the absence of a cosmic string network.

2
Quadratic estimators

As the CMB photons travel from the last scattering surface, they follow deflected trajectories due to gravitational deflection by matter overdensity and underdensity. Along the perturbed trajectories, the axion-photon coupling causes a rotation of the plane of polarization. At the leading order, it suffices to compute the rotation effect along the unperturbed line of sight. The absence of interference between these two effects at the leading order is discussed and justified in Section
5
. As we focus on the rotation effect, we start with the
lensed but unrotated
CMB anisotropies, and consider the effect of rotation on them.

2.1
Notation

Throughout this paper, we assume a flat-sky approximation, in which the observables are functions of
\bm{\mathbf{\hat{n}}}=n_{x}\,\bm{\mathbf{\hat{x}}}+n_{y}\,\bm{\mathbf{\hat{y}}}
. We use the tilded notation to indicate lensed but unrotated quantities: the temperature anisotropies
\tilde{T}(\bm{\mathbf{\hat{n}}})
, and the polarization anisotropies
\tilde{E}(\bm{\mathbf{\hat{n}}})
and
\tilde{B}(\bm{\mathbf{\hat{n}}})
. Their power spectra in Fourier space
\tilde{C}^{XY}_{l}
are defined through:

\expectationvalue{\tilde X(\vect l_1)\tilde Y^*(\vect l_2)}=(2\pi)^{2}\,\delta_{D}(\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{1}-\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{2})\,\tilde{C}^{XY}_{l_{1}}.

(2.1)

We parameterize cosmic birefringence using an anisotropic rotation angle for the polarization
\alpha(\bm{\mathbf{\hat{n}}})
. The lensed and rotated quantities are denoted by
T(\bm{\mathbf{\hat{n}}})
,
E(\bm{\mathbf{\hat{n}}})
, and
B(\bm{\mathbf{\hat{n}}})
(birefringence leaves the temperature anisotropies unchanged). The cross correlations are consequently modified to

\expectationvalue{X(\vect l_1)Y^*(\vect l_2)}=\expectationvalue{\tilde X(\vect l_1)\tilde Y^*(\vect l_2)}+f_{XY}(\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{1},\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{2})\,\alpha(\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{1}-\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{2})+O(\alpha^{2}).

(2.2)

Based on the latest constraints from the CMB data
[
21
]
and predictions of theoretical models
[
17
]
, we expect
\alpha(\bm{\mathbf{\hat{n}}})
to be small angles (in radians). Therefore, it suffices to expand the expression up to the linear order.

The observed anisotropies (denoted with a subscript
o
) include both the cosmological signals and noise (denoted with a subscript
n
):

X_{o}(\bm{\mathbf{l}})=X(\bm{\mathbf{l}})+X_{n}(\bm{\mathbf{l}}).

(2.3)

The Fourier space power spectra can be separately defined for the observed anisotropies and for the noise, respectively:

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{X_o(\vect l_1)Y_o^*(\vect l_2)}

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{2}\,\delta_{D}(\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{1}-\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{2})\,C^{XY}_{l_{1}}

(2.4)

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{X_n(\vect l_1)Y_n^*(\vect l_2)}

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{2}\,\delta_{D}(\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{1}-\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{2})\,C^{XY,n}_{l_{1}}.

(2.5)

In this paper, we concern the variance of rotation estimators under the null hypothesis,
i.e.

\alpha(\bm{\mathbf{\hat{n}}})=0
. Since cosmic signals and noise are uncorrelated, we have

C^{XY}_{l}=\tilde{C}^{XY}_{l}+C^{XY,n}_{l}.

(2.6)

2.2
Rotation of polarization

Let
\tilde{Q}(\bm{\mathbf{\hat{n}}})\pm i\tilde{U}(\bm{\mathbf{\hat{n}}})
be the Stokes’ parameters for the lensed but unrotated polarizations. For a line-of-sight specific polarization rotation
\alpha(\bm{\mathbf{n}})
, the perturbed Stokes fields are given by:

Q(\bm{\mathbf{\hat{n}}})\pm iU(\bm{\mathbf{\hat{n}}})=\left[\tilde{Q}(\bm{\mathbf{\hat{n}}})\pm i\tilde{U}(\bm{\mathbf{\hat{n}}})\right]\,e^{\pm 2\,i\,\alpha(\bm{\mathbf{\hat{n}}})},

(2.7)

The

E(\bm{\mathbf{l}})\pm iB(\bm{\mathbf{l}})=\int\mathrm{d}^{2}\bm{\mathbf{\hat{n}}}\,e^{-i\bm{\mathbf{l}}\cdot\bm{\mathbf{\hat{n}}}}\,e^{\mp 2\,i\,\varphi_{\bm{\mathbf{l}}}}\,[Q(\bm{\mathbf{\hat{n}}})\pm iU(\bm{\mathbf{\hat{n}}})],

(2.8)

where
\varphi_{\bm{\mathbf{l}}}
is the angle between the vector
{\bm{\mathbf{l}}}
and the first coordinate axis on the sky. A similar expression can be written for the lensed but unrotated anisotropies.

We have assumed that all primary CMB anisotropies originate from the last scattering surface, neglecting the possibility that the effects of the ALP field may be different for polarizations produced at recombination and at reionization. Future CMB satellites such as LiteBIRD will be able to detect any difference in the birefringence angle between the two epochs to within
0.05^{\circ}

[

2.3
Hu-Okamoto estimator

Following the treatment of Hu & Okamoto for estimating weak lensing from the CMB anisotropies
[
18
]
, quadratic estimators can be constructed from the observed quantities

]
:

\hat{\alpha}_{XY}(\bm{\mathbf{L}})=\int\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{1}\,\mathrm{d}^{2}\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{2}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,\delta_{D}(\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{1}-\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{2}-\bm{\mathbf{L}})\,X_{o}(\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{1})\,Y_{o}^{*}(\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{2})\,F_{XY}(\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{1},\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{2}),

(2.9)

where
F_{XY}
is a yet undetermined real-valued function,

and we have assumed
\bm{\mathbf{L}}\neq 0
. The
\bm{\mathbf{L}}=0
case corresponds to a constant rotation of the polarization on the whole sky. See Refs.
[
24
,
25
,
26
,
27
,
28
,
29
,
11
]
for recent estimates. Henceforth, we will write
\bm{\mathbf{L}}=\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{1}-\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{2}
. Following Ref.
[
19
]
, we also introduce the shorthand notation:

\int\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{1}\,\mathrm{d}^{2}\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{2}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,\delta_{D}(\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{1}-\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{2}-\bm{\mathbf{L}})\to\int_{\bm{\mathbf{L}}=\mathbf{l}_{1}-\mathbf{l}_{2}}.

(2.10)

To find the coefficients
F_{XY}(\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{1},\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{2})
, three conditions need to be imposed on
\hat{\alpha}_{XY}(\bm{\mathbf{L}})
. First, the reality condition
\hat{\alpha}_{XY}^{*}(\bm{\mathbf{L}})=\hat{\alpha}_{XY}(-\bm{\mathbf{L}})
is needed because the rotation field to be estimated is real-valued. This requires
F_{XY}(-\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{1},-\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{2})=F_{XY}(\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{1},\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{2})
. Second, the estimator is required to be unbiased,
\expectationvalue{\hat\alpha_{XY}(\vect L)}=\alpha(\bm{\mathbf{L}})
, which leads to equation

1=\int_{\bm{\mathbf{L}}=\mathbf{l}_{1}-\mathbf{l}_{2}}f_{XY}(\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{1},\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{2})\,F_{XY}(\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{1},\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{2}).

(2.11)

Finally, subject to the above two conditions, we seek to minimize the variance of the estimator via the following condition:

\frac{\delta}{\delta F_{XY}}\left[\expectationvalue{\hat\alpha_{XY}(\vect L)\,\hat\alpha_{XY}^*(\vect L')}-\expectationvalue{\hat\alpha_{XY}(\vect L)}\expectationvalue{\hat\alpha_{XY}^*(\vect L')}\right]=0.

(2.12)

The solution to this minimization problem reads:

\displaystyle F_{XY}(\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{1},\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{2})

\displaystyle=\lambda_{XY}(\bm{\mathbf{L}})\,\frac{f_{XY}(\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{1},\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{2})}{(1+\delta_{XY})\,C_{l_{1}}^{XX}\,C_{l_{2}}^{YY}}\quad\text{for $XY\neq TE$},

(2.13)

\displaystyle F_{TE}(\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{1},\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{2})

\displaystyle=\lambda_{TE}(\bm{\mathbf{L}})\,\frac{C^{EE}_{l_{1}}\,C^{TT}_{l_{2}}\,f_{TE}(\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{1},\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{2})-C^{TE}_{l_{1}}\,C^{TE}_{l_{2}}\,f_{TE}(\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{2},\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{1})}{C^{TT}_{l_{1}}\,C^{EE}_{l_{2}}\,C^{EE}_{l_{1}}\,C^{TT}_{l_{2}}-(C^{TE}_{l_{1}}\,C^{TE}_{l_{2}})^{2}},

(2.14)

where we define the normalization constants

\displaystyle[\lambda_{XY}(\bm{\mathbf{L}})]^{-1}

\displaystyle=\int_{\bm{\mathbf{L}}=\mathbf{l}_{1}-\mathbf{l}_{2}}\frac{[f_{XY}(\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{1},\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{2})]^{2}}{(1+\delta_{XY})\,C^{XX}_{l_{1}}\,C^{YY}_{l_{2}}}\quad\text{for $XY\neq TE$},

(2.15)

\displaystyle[\lambda_{TE}(\bm{\mathbf{L}})]^{-1}

\displaystyle=\int_{\bm{\mathbf{L}}=\mathbf{l}_{1}-\mathbf{l}_{2}}f_{TE}(\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{1},\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{2})\,\frac{C^{EE}_{l_{1}}\,C^{TT}_{l_{2}}\,f_{TE}(\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{1},\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{2})-C^{TE}_{l_{1}}\,C^{TE}_{l_{2}}\,f_{TE}(\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{2},\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{1})}{C^{TT}_{l_{1}}\,C^{EE}_{l_{2}}\,C^{EE}_{l_{1}}\,C^{TT}_{l_{2}}-(C^{TE}_{l_{1}}\,C^{TE}_{l_{2}})^{2}}.

(2.16)

The covariance between two estimators is

\expectationvalue{\hat\alpha_{XY}(\vect L)\hat\alpha_{X'Y'}^*(\vect L')}-\expectationvalue{\hat\alpha_{XY}(\vect L)}\expectationvalue{\hat\alpha_{X'Y'}^*(\vect L')}=(2\pi)^{2}\delta(\bm{\mathbf{L}}-\bm{\mathbf{L}}^{\prime})N_{XYX^{\prime}Y^{\prime}}(\bm{\mathbf{L}}),

(2.17)

where

N_{XYX^{\prime}Y^{\prime}}(\bm{\mathbf{L}})=\int_{\bm{\mathbf{L}}=\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{1}-\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{2}}F_{XY}(\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{1},\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{2})\left[F_{X^{\prime}Y^{\prime}}(\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{1},\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{2})C^{XX^{\prime}}_{l_{1}}C^{YY^{\prime}}_{l_{2}}+F_{X^{\prime}Y^{\prime}}(\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{2},\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{1})C^{XY^{\prime}}_{l_{1}}C^{YX^{\prime}}_{l_{2}}\right].

(2.18)

The estimator constructed for any single pair
XY
has a variance

\expectationvalue{\hat\alpha_{XY}(\vect L)\,\hat\alpha_{XY}^*(\vect L')}-\expectationvalue{\hat\alpha_{XY}(\vect L)}\expectationvalue{\hat\alpha_{XY}^*(\vect L')}=(2\pi)^{2}\,\delta_{D}(\bm{\mathbf{L}}-\bm{\mathbf{L}}^{\prime})\,N_{XY}(\bm{\mathbf{L}}),

(2.19)

where it can be shown that
N_{XY}(\bm{\mathbf{L}})=N_{XYXY}=\lambda_{XY}(\bm{\mathbf{L}})
. We note that
N_{XY}(\bm{\mathbf{L}})
is only dependent on
L=|{\bm{\mathbf{L}}}|
due to statistical invariance under rotation of the sky. From here on, we will write
N_{XY}(L)
to reflect this fact.

The Hu-Okamoto (HO) estimator is constructed as the unique linear combination of the estimators
\hat{\alpha}_{XY}(\bm{\mathbf{L}})
corresponding to five pairs,
TE
,
TB
,
EE
,
EB
, and
BB
(the
TT

\hat{\alpha}_{HO}(\bm{\mathbf{L}})=\sum_{I}\,w_{I}(L)\,\hat{\alpha}_{I}(\bm{\mathbf{L}}),

(2.20)

where the index
I
ranges over unique pairs of observables
XY
. Defining the symmetric matrix
\bm{\mathbf{N}}(L)
with elements
[\bm{\mathbf{N}}]_{IJ}(L)=N_{IJ}(L)
as defined in Eq. (

w_{I}(L)=\frac{\sum_{J}[\bm{\mathbf{N}}^{-1}(L)]_{IJ}}{\sum_{JK}[\bm{\mathbf{N}}^{-1}(L)]_{JK}}.

(2.21)

Under the null hypothesis that there is no polarization rotation, the HO estimator has a variance

\expectationvalue{\hat\alpha_{HO}(\vect L)\,\hat\alpha_{HO}^*(\vect L')}-\expectationvalue{\hat\alpha_{HO}(\vect L)}\expectationvalue{\hat\alpha_{HO}^*(\vect L')}=(2\pi)^{2}\,\delta_{D}(\bm{\mathbf{L}}-\bm{\mathbf{L}}^{\prime})\,N_{HO}(\bm{\mathbf{L}}),

N_{HO}(\bm{\mathbf{L}})=N_{HO}(L)=\left(\sum_{IJ}[\bm{\mathbf{N}}^{-1}(L)]_{IJ}\right)^{-1}.

(2.23)

and
EE

2\times 2
block for
TB
and
BB

2.4
Global-minimum-variance estimator

Recently in the context of weak lensing reconstruction, Ref.
[
19
]
proposes another quadratic estimator called the global-minimum-variance (GMV) estimator, which is directly constructed from a minimum-variance linear combination of pairs of
all
observables. This idea can be straightforwardly applied to the case of cosmic birefringence, leading to the following quadratic estimator

\hat{\alpha}_{GMV}(\bm{\mathbf{L}})=\int_{\bm{\mathbf{L}}=\mathbf{l}_{1}-\mathbf{l}_{2}}X^{i}_{o}(\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{1})\,X^{j*}_{o}(\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{2})\,\Xi_{ij}(\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{1},\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{2}),

(2.24)

where Einstein summation is assumed over
i
and
j
, and
X^{i}=T
,
E
, or
B
. In contrast, the HO estimator, discussed in the previous subsection and historically widely used, is constructed as the minimum-variance linear combination of estimators which are in turn constructed from pairs of observables that correspond to different Fourier vectors. The work of Ref.
[
19
]
shows that the reconstruction noise (variance) of the HO estimator is strictly no less than that of the GMV estimator. Hence, the HO estimator is a sub-optimal quadratic estimator. The two-step process to construct the HO estimator compromises the optimality. In fact, the GMV estimator achieves the least variance among all unbiased quadratic estimators.

The derivation needed to specify the GMV estimator closely parallels that in the previous subsection. Here, we summarize the relevant results following the notation of Ref.
[
19
]
. To this end, we introduce bold face symbols,
\bm{\mathbf{f}}
,
\bm{\mathbf{C}}_{l}
, and
\bm{\mathbf{\Xi}}
, to denote matrices whose matrix elements are given by
f_{ij}
,
C_{l}^{ij}
, and
\Xi_{ij}
, respectively.

First of all, the GMV estimator can be made unbiased if we require

1=\int_{\bm{\mathbf{L}}=\mathbf{l}_{1}-\mathbf{l}_{2}}f_{ij}(\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{1},\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{2})\,\Xi_{ij}(\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{1},\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{2}).

(2.25)

The variance of
\hat{\alpha}_{GMV}
can be defined analogously to Eq. (
2.22
). Under the condition of unbiasedness, the weights that minimize this variance are

\bm{\mathbf{\Xi}}(\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{1},\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{2})=\frac{1}{2}\,N_{GMV}(L)\,\bm{\mathbf{C}}_{l_{1}}^{-1}\,\bm{\mathbf{f}}(\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{1},\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{2})\,\bm{\mathbf{C}}_{l_{2}}^{-1},

(2.26)

where the reconstruction noise spectrum
N_{GMV}(L)
is given by the integral of a matrix trace

[N_{GMV}(L)]^{-1}=\frac{1}{2}\,\int_{\bm{\mathbf{L}}=\mathbf{l}_{1}-\mathbf{l}_{2}}{\rm Tr}\left[\bm{\mathbf{C}}_{l_{1}}^{-1}\,\bm{\mathbf{f}}(\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{1},\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{2})\,\bm{\mathbf{C}}_{l_{2}}^{-1}\,\bm{\mathbf{f}}(\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{2},\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{1})\right].

(2.27)

Using the symmetry
f_{YX}(\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{1},\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{2})=f_{XY}(\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{2},\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{1})
, we can rewrite
\bm{\mathbf{f}}(\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{2},\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{1})=\bm{\mathbf{f}}(\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{1},\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{2})^{T}
.

The precise gap between the GMV estimator and the HO estimator is more manifest if we recast Eq. (
2.20
)) into the form

\hat{\alpha}_{HO}(\bm{\mathbf{L}})=\sum_{i,j}\,\int_{\bm{\mathbf{L}}=\mathbf{l}_{1}-\mathbf{l}_{2}}X^{i}(\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{1})\,X^{j*}(\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{2})\,w_{ij}(|\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{1}-\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{2}|)\,F_{ij}(\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{1},\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{2}).

(2.28)

A comparison with Eq. (
2.24
) suggests that
\hat{\alpha}_{HO}
would be made to match
\hat{\alpha}_{GMV}
precisely if we allowed
w_{ij}
to depend
arbitrarily
on
\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{1}
and
\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{2}
, i.e.
w_{ij}=w_{ij}(\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{1},\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{2})
, rather than on the combination
L=|\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{1}-\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{2}|
. In that case, we would have simply treated the product of
w_{ij}(\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{1},\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{2})
and
F_{ij}(\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{1},\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{2})
as a single coefficient
\Xi_{ij}(\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{1},\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{2})
, and hence followed the logic of constructing the GMV estimator by minimizing the variance.

Nevertheless, the conceptual distinction between the GMV and HO estimators does not lead to a drastic difference in the estimation error, as we demonstrate both analytically and numerically in Section
6
.

3
Maximum likelihood estimator

In this section, we sketch another approach to estimating birefringence rotation in the CMB observables from the perspective of maximizing the likelihood function. This approach is inspired by the work of Ref.
[
30
]
on constructing the maximum likelihood estimator for weak gravitational lensing. By modifying the maximum likelihood estimator, a quadratic estimator can be derived, which we show is precisely the GMV estimator. The connection between the maximum likelihood estimator and the quadratic estimator in terms of Fisher matrices in the context of weak gravitational lensing has been noted in Ref.
[
31
]
.

Let
\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{d}}}
denote a column vector that collectively contains all the available CMB observables,
\tilde{T}(\bm{\mathbf{l}}),\tilde{E}(\bm{\mathbf{l}}),\tilde{B}(\bm{\mathbf{l}})
, for all wave vectors
\bm{\mathbf{l}}
, free of polarization rotation or noise. The vector
\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{d}}}
has a covariance matrix:

\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{C}}}=\expectationvalue{\tilde{\vect d}\,\tilde{\vect d}^T},

(3.1)

where random realizations of CMB anisotropies are averaged over. The covariance matrix
\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{C}}}
in principle follows from the theoretical prediction that the CMB anisotropies of cosmological origin are Gaussian random variables.

While the Fourier components
\tilde{T}(\bm{\mathbf{l}})
,
\tilde{E}(\bm{\mathbf{l}})
and
\tilde{B}(\bm{\mathbf{l}})
are complex-valued, in principle a set of real-valued variables can be instead used to build the vector
\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{d}}}
. Correspondingly, the covariance matrix is real symmetric and positive definite. For this reason, we shall have in mind the machinery of linear algebra for real variables, and apply it to the following derivations.

We note that there are no primary
B
modes in the absence of primordial tensor perturbations;
[
32
,
33
,
34
]
the cosmic
B
-mode polarization detected in the CMB results from weak lensing by the large-scale structure (see Ref.
[
35
]
for a review), and for this reason the CMB anisotropies acquire mode couplings. Nevertheless, for our purpose it suffices to treat the
B
modes in the likelihood function as if they were genuinely Gaussian random primary anisotropies, as long as the corresponding (isotropic) power spectrum is used in constructing
\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{C}}}
.

The log likelihood of the vector
\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{d}}}
given the covariance matrix
\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{C}}}
is

\ln\mathcal{L}[\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{d}}}|\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{C}}}]=-\frac{1}{2}\,\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{d}}}^{T}\,\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{C}}}^{-1}\,\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{d}}}-\frac{1}{2}\,\ln\det\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{C}}}+\mathrm{const}.

(3.2)

In the presence of rotation
\alpha
and noise
\bm{\mathbf{n}}
, the measured CMB observables can be written as

\bm{\mathbf{d}}=\left[\bm{\mathbf{M}}(\alpha)\right]^{-1}\,\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{d}}}+\bm{\mathbf{n}},

(3.3)

where
\bm{\mathbf{M}}(\alpha)
is an orthogonal matrix (satisfying
[\bm{\mathbf{M}}(\alpha)]^{-1}=[\bm{\mathbf{M}}(\alpha)]^{T}
) that describes the action of pointwise polarization rotation on the anisotropies. Assuming that the noise
\bm{\mathbf{n}}
has a covariance matrix
\bm{\mathbf{C}}_{n}
, the joint log likelihood of
\bm{\mathbf{d}}
and
\bm{\mathbf{n}}
is now

\ln\mathcal{L}[\bm{\mathbf{d}},\bm{\mathbf{n}}|\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{C}}},\alpha,\bm{\mathbf{C}}_{n}]=-\frac{1}{2}\,\bm{\mathbf{n}}^{T}\,\bm{\mathbf{C}}_{n}^{-1}\,\bm{\mathbf{n}}-\frac{1}{2}\,(\bm{\mathbf{d}}-\bm{\mathbf{n}})^{T}\,[\bm{\mathbf{M}}(\alpha)]^{T}\,\bm{\mathbf{\tilde{C}}}^{-1}\,\bm{\mathbf{M}}(\alpha)\,(\bm{\mathbf{d}}-\bm{\mathbf{n}}),

(3.4)

where we have dropped irrelevant additive constant terms. For a fixed
\bm{\mathbf{d}}
, Eq. (
3.4
) is maximized at

\bm{\mathbf{n}}=\bm{\mathbf{n}}_{\mathrm{max}}=\left(\bm{\mathbf{C}}_{n}^{-1}+\bm{\mathbf{M}}^{T}\,\bm{\mathbf{\tilde{C}}}^{-1}\,\bm{\mathbf{M}}\right)^{-1}\,\bm{\mathbf{M}}^{T}\,\bm{\mathbf{\tilde{C}}}\,\bm{\mathbf{M}}\,\bm{\mathbf{d}},

(3.5)

and the maximum value of the likelihood is

\ln\mathcal{L}[\bm{\mathbf{d}}|\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{C}}},\alpha,\bm{\mathbf{n}}=\bm{\mathbf{n}}_{\mathrm{max}}]=-\frac{1}{2}\,\bm{\mathbf{d}}^{T}\,(\bm{\mathbf{C}}_{n}+\bm{\mathbf{M}}^{T}\,\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{C}}}\,\bm{\mathbf{M}})^{-1}\,\bm{\mathbf{d}},

(3.6)

which we shall simply call
\ln\mathcal{L}
. We can now estimate the rotation field by maximising this log likelihood with respect to
\alpha
. Anticipating
\alpha
to be numerically small in radians
[
17
]
, we consider an approximation of
\ln\mathcal{L}
as a second-order polynomial in
\alpha
:

\ln\mathcal{L}=\mathrm{const}+\bm{\mathbf{d}}^{T}\,\bm{\mathbf{K}}_{1}(\alpha)\,\bm{\mathbf{d}}-\frac{1}{2}\,\bm{\mathbf{d}}^{T}\,\bm{\mathbf{K}}_{2}(\alpha)\,\bm{\mathbf{d}}+O(\alpha^{3}).

(3.7)

The matrices
\bm{\mathbf{K}}_{1}(\alpha)
and
\bm{\mathbf{K}}_{2}(\alpha)
have linear and quadratic dependence on
\alpha
, respectively. To find these matrices, we first Taylor-expand
\bm{\mathbf{M}}(\alpha)
:

\bm{\mathbf{M}}(\alpha)=\bm{\mathbf{I}}-\bm{\mathbf{T}}(\alpha)+O(\alpha^{2}),

(3.8)

where
\bm{\mathbf{T}}(\alpha)
is an
antisymmetric
matrix with linear dependence on
\alpha
. Substituting this expansion into Eq. (
3.6
) and defining
\bm{\mathbf{C}}=\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{C}}}+\bm{\mathbf{C}}_{n}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\bm{\mathbf{K}}_{1}

\displaystyle=\bm{\mathbf{C}}^{-1}\,\bm{\mathbf{T}}\,\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{C}}}\,\bm{\mathbf{C}}^{-1},

(3.9)

\displaystyle\bm{\mathbf{K}}_{2}

\displaystyle=\bm{\mathbf{C}}^{-1}\,\bm{\mathbf{T}}^{T}\,\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{C}}}\,\bm{\mathbf{C}}^{-1}\,\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{C}}}\,\bm{\mathbf{T}}\,\bm{\mathbf{C}}^{-1}+\bm{\mathbf{C}}^{-1}\,\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{C}}}\,\bm{\mathbf{T}}^{T}\,\bm{\mathbf{C}}^{-1}\,\bm{\mathbf{T}}\,\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{C}}}\,\bm{\mathbf{C}}^{-1}-2\,\bm{\mathbf{C}}^{-1}\,\bm{\mathbf{T}}^{T}\,\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{C}}}\,\bm{\mathbf{C}}^{-1}\,\bm{\mathbf{T}}\,\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{C}}}\,\bm{\mathbf{C}}^{-1}

\displaystyle\quad+\bm{\mathbf{C}}^{-1}\,\bm{\mathbf{T}}^{T}\,\bm{\mathbf{T}}\,\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{C}}}\,\bm{\mathbf{C}}^{-1}-\bm{\mathbf{C}}^{-1}\,\bm{\mathbf{T}}^{T}\,\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{C}}}\,\bm{\mathbf{T}}\,\bm{\mathbf{C}}^{-1}.

(3.10)

We can make the dependence on
\alpha
explicit by writing

\displaystyle\bm{\mathbf{T}}(\alpha)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{\mathbf{L}}}\,\bm{\mathbf{T}}_{\bm{\mathbf{L}}}\,\alpha_{\bm{\mathbf{L}}},

(3.11)

\displaystyle\bm{\mathbf{d}}^{T}\,\bm{\mathbf{K}}_{1}(\alpha)\,\bm{\mathbf{d}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{\mathbf{L}}}\,\mathcal{K}_{1,\bm{\mathbf{L}}}\,\alpha_{\bm{\mathbf{L}}},

(3.12)

\displaystyle\bm{\mathbf{d}}^{T}\,\bm{\mathbf{K}}_{2}(\alpha)\,\bm{\mathbf{d}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{\mathbf{L}}}\,\sum_{\bm{\mathbf{L}}^{\prime}}\,\mathcal{K}_{2,\bm{\mathbf{L}}\bm{\mathbf{L}}^{\prime}}\,\alpha_{\bm{\mathbf{L}}}^{*}\,\alpha_{\bm{\mathbf{L}}^{\prime}},

(3.13)

where the indices
\bm{\mathbf{L}}
and
\bm{\mathbf{L}}^{\prime}
label the components of the anisotropic rotation field on the sky (i.e. its various Fourier modes
\alpha(\bm{\mathbf{L}})
). With the cubic and higher order terms neglected, Eq. (
3.6
) takes a quadratic form:

\ln\mathcal{L}=\mathrm{const}+\sum_{\bm{\mathbf{L}}}\,\mathcal{K}_{1,\bm{\mathbf{L}}}\,\alpha_{\bm{\mathbf{L}}}-\frac{1}{2}\,\sum_{\bm{\mathbf{L}}}\,\sum_{\bm{\mathbf{L}}^{\prime}}\,\mathcal{K}_{2,\bm{\mathbf{L}}\bm{\mathbf{L}}^{\prime}}\,\alpha_{\bm{\mathbf{L}}}^{*}\,\alpha_{\bm{\mathbf{L}}^{\prime}}.

(3.14)

This log likelihood is maximized for

\alpha_{\bm{\mathbf{L}}}=\sum_{\bm{\mathbf{L}}^{\prime}}\,[\mathcal{K}_{2}^{-1}]_{\bm{\mathbf{L}}\bm{\mathbf{L}}^{\prime}}\,\mathcal{K}_{1,\bm{\mathbf{L}}^{\prime}},

(3.15)

where the matrix inverse of
\mathcal{K}_{2}
is taken with respect to the indices
\bm{\mathbf{L}}
and
\bm{\mathbf{L}}^{\prime}
. Note that the matrices
\mathcal{K}_{1,\bm{\mathbf{L}}}
and
\mathcal{K}_{2,\bm{\mathbf{L}}\bm{\mathbf{L}}^{\prime}}
both depend quadratically on the specific realization of the measured CMB observables
\bm{\mathbf{d}}
.

4
Relation between various quadratic estimators

4.1
Equivalence to GMV estimator

To make contact with quadratic estimators, we consider replacing the matrix
\mathcal{K}_{2}
with
\bar{\mathcal{K}}_{2}=\expectationvalue{\mathcal K_2}
, which is the ensemble expectation after averaging over random realizations of
\bm{\mathbf{d}}
. The matrix
\mathcal{K}_{2}
that correspond to any specific realization of
\bm{\mathbf{d}}
fluctuates around
\bar{\mathcal{K}}_{2}
, but the fluctuation is usually small in the limit of a large number of Fourier modes. Without having any statistically preferred direction on the sky, the matrix
\bar{\mathcal{K}}_{2}
is shown below to be diagonal in
\bm{\mathbf{L}}
space. We therefore define the reconstructed rotation field as follows, by modifying Eq. (
3.15
):

\alpha_{\mathrm{rec},{\bm{\mathbf{L}}}}=\sum_{\bm{\mathbf{L}}^{\prime}}\,[\bar{\mathcal{K}}_{2}^{-1}]_{\bm{\mathbf{L}}\bm{\mathbf{L}}^{\prime}}\,\mathcal{K}_{1,\bm{\mathbf{L}}^{\prime}}.

(4.1)

Since the only dependence on the data
\bm{\mathbf{d}}
is in
\mathcal{K}_{1,\bm{\mathbf{L}}^{\prime}}
as a bilinear form in
\bm{\mathbf{d}}
,
\alpha_{\mathrm{rec},\bm{\mathbf{L}}}
is a quadratic estimator. After some algebra and using Eq. (
3.1
), we derive

\bar{\mathcal{K}}_{2,\bm{\mathbf{L}}\bm{\mathbf{L}}^{\prime}}={\rm Tr}\left[\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{C}}}\,\bm{\mathbf{C}}^{-1}\,\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{C}}}\,\bm{\mathbf{T}}^{T}_{-\bm{\mathbf{L}}}\,\bm{\mathbf{C}}^{-1}\,\bm{\mathbf{T}}_{\bm{\mathbf{L}}^{\prime}}\right]-{\rm Tr}\left[\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{C}}}\,\bm{\mathbf{C}}^{-1}\,\bm{\mathbf{T}}^{T}_{-\bm{\mathbf{L}}}\,\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{C}}}\,\bm{\mathbf{C}}^{-1}\,\bm{\mathbf{T}}_{\bm{\mathbf{L}}^{\prime}}\right].

(4.2)

Finally, we show that
\alpha_{\mathrm{rec},{\bm{\mathbf{L}}}}
coincides with the GMV estimator
\hat{\alpha}_{GMV}(L)
constructed in Section
2.4
. To this end, we first recast some of the equations in the preceding sections in terms of matrices. Eq. (
2.1
) becomes Eq. (
3.1
), while Eq. (
2.2
) is replaced by

\expectationvalue{[\vect M(\alpha)]^T\,\tilde{\vect d}\,\tilde{\vect d}^T\,\vect M(\alpha)}=\expectationvalue{\tilde{\vect d}\,\tilde{\vect d}^T}+\sum_{\bm{\mathbf{L}}}\,\bm{\mathbf{f}}_{\bm{\mathbf{L}}}\,\alpha_{\bm{\mathbf{L}}}+O(\alpha^{2}).

(4.3)

In other words, an arbitrary matrix element of
\bm{\mathbf{f}}_{\bm{\mathbf{L}}}
has the form
f_{ij}(\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{1},\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{2})\,(2\pi)^{2}\,\delta_{D}(\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{1}-\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{2}-\bm{\mathbf{L}})
. Using Eq. (
3.8
) and Eq. (
3.11
), we find

\bm{\mathbf{f}}_{\bm{\mathbf{L}}}=\bm{\mathbf{T}}_{\bm{\mathbf{L}}}\,\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{C}}}+\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{C}}}\,\bm{\mathbf{T}}^{T}_{\bm{\mathbf{L}}}.

(4.4)

On the other hand, an arbitrary element of
\bm{\mathbf{C}}
, denoted
C^{i\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{1},j\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{2}}
, is equal to
C^{ij}_{l_{1}}\,(2\pi)^{2}\,\delta_{D}(\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{1}-\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{2})
. With these in mind, we can rewrite Eq. (
2.27
) as

\displaystyle(2\pi)^{2}\delta_{D}(\bm{\mathbf{L}}-\bm{\mathbf{L}}^{\prime})N_{GMV}(L)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\,{\rm Tr}\left[\bm{\mathbf{C}}^{-1}\,\bm{\mathbf{f}}_{-\bm{\mathbf{L}}}\,\bm{\mathbf{C}}^{-1}\,\bm{\mathbf{f}}_{\bm{\mathbf{L}}^{\prime}}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\,{\rm Tr}\left[\bm{\mathbf{C}}^{-1}\,\bm{\mathbf{T}}_{-\bm{\mathbf{L}}}\,\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{C}}}\,\bm{\mathbf{C}}^{-1}\,\bm{\mathbf{T}}_{\bm{\mathbf{L}}^{\prime}}\,\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{C}}}+\bm{\mathbf{C}}^{-1}\,\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{C}}}\,\bm{\mathbf{T}}^{T}_{-\bm{\mathbf{L}}}\,\bm{\mathbf{C}}^{-1}\,\bm{\mathbf{T}}_{\bm{\mathbf{L}}^{\prime}}\,\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{C}}}\right.

\displaystyle\quad+\left.\bm{\mathbf{C}}^{-1}\,\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{C}}}\,\bm{\mathbf{T}}^{T}_{-\bm{\mathbf{L}}}\,\bm{\mathbf{C}}^{-1}\,\bm{\mathbf{T}}_{\bm{\mathbf{L}}^{\prime}}\,\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{C}}}+\bm{\mathbf{C}}^{-1}\,\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{C}}}\,\bm{\mathbf{T}}^{T}_{-\bm{\mathbf{L}}}\,\bm{\mathbf{C}}^{-1}\,\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{C}}}\,\bm{\mathbf{T}}^{T}_{\bm{\mathbf{L}}^{\prime}}\right]

\displaystyle=\bar{\mathcal{K}}_{2,\bm{\mathbf{L}}\bm{\mathbf{L}}^{\prime}},

(4.5)

where in the last line we have used the cyclic property of the trace and the antisymmetry of
\bm{\mathbf{T}}_{L}
to match Eq. (
4.2
). We note that
\bar{\mathcal{K}}_{2,\bm{\mathbf{L}}\bm{\mathbf{L}}^{\prime}}
is indeed diagonal in
\bm{\mathbf{L}}
space, as expected from statistical isotropy. Finally, Eq. (
2.24
) and Eq. (
2.26
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\hat{\alpha}_{GMV}(\bm{\mathbf{L}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\,N_{GMV}(L)^{-1}\,\bm{\mathbf{d}}^{T}\,\bm{\mathbf{C}}^{-1}\,\bm{\mathbf{f}}_{\bm{\mathbf{L}}}\,\bm{\mathbf{C}}^{-1}\,\bm{\mathbf{d}}

(4.6)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\,\sum_{\bm{\mathbf{L}}^{\prime}}\,\bar{\mathcal{K}}_{2,\bm{\mathbf{L}}\bm{\mathbf{L}}^{\prime}}\,\bm{\mathbf{d}}^{T}\,\bm{\mathbf{C}}^{-1}\,\left(\bm{\mathbf{T}}_{\bm{\mathbf{L}}^{\prime}}\,\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{C}}}+\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{C}}}\,\bm{\mathbf{T}}^{T}_{\bm{\mathbf{L}}^{\prime}}\right)\,\bm{\mathbf{C}}^{-1}\,\bm{\mathbf{d}}

(4.7)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{\mathbf{L}}^{\prime}}\,\bar{\mathcal{K}}_{2,\bm{\mathbf{L}}\bm{\mathbf{L}}^{\prime}}\,\bm{\mathbf{d}}^{T}\,\bm{\mathbf{C}}^{-1}\,\bm{\mathbf{T}}_{\bm{\mathbf{L}}^{\prime}}\,\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{C}}}\,\bm{\mathbf{C}}^{-1}\,\bm{\mathbf{d}}

(4.8)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{\mathbf{L}}^{\prime}}\,\bar{\mathcal{K}}_{2,\bm{\mathbf{L}}\bm{\mathbf{L}}^{\prime}}\,\mathcal{K}_{1\bm{\mathbf{L}}^{\prime}}=\hat{\alpha}_{\mathrm{rec},\bm{\mathbf{L}}}.

(4.9)

We note that the above derivation of the connection between the maximum likelihood estimator and GMV estimator assumes an antisymmetric
\bm{\mathbf{T}}
matrix (or more generally an anti-Hermitian matrix). In more familiar notation, if

\begin{pmatrix}\delta T(\bm{\mathbf{l}})\\
\delta E(\bm{\mathbf{l}})\\
\delta B(\bm{\mathbf{l}})\end{pmatrix}=\int\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}\bm{\mathbf{l}}^{\prime}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\bm{\mathbf{W}}(\bm{\mathbf{l}},\bm{\mathbf{l}}^{\prime})\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{T}(\bm{\mathbf{l}}^{\prime})\\
\tilde{E}(\bm{\mathbf{l}}^{\prime})\\
\tilde{B}(\bm{\mathbf{l}}^{\prime})\end{pmatrix},

(4.10)

for some
3\times 3
matrix
\bm{\mathbf{W}}
that depends on
\bm{\mathbf{l}}
and
\bm{\mathbf{l}}^{\prime}
, then we require
\bm{\mathbf{W}}^{T}(\bm{\mathbf{l}}^{\prime},\bm{\mathbf{l}})=-\bm{\mathbf{W}}(\bm{\mathbf{l}},\bm{\mathbf{l}}^{\prime})
[or more generally
\bm{\mathbf{W}}^{T}(\bm{\mathbf{l}}^{\prime},\bm{\mathbf{l}})=-\bm{\mathbf{W}}^{*}(\bm{\mathbf{l}},\bm{\mathbf{l}}^{\prime})
]. While this is true for polarization rotations, it is not true for gravitational lensing (see Eq. (4) of
[
18
]
).

4.2
Equivalence between HO and GMV estimators

Having summarized the quadratic estimators, we now quantify their performance using the power spectrum of reconstruction noise, namely the variance of
\hat{\alpha}(\bm{\mathbf{L}})
as a function of
L=|\bm{\mathbf{L}}|
. We will perform calculations under the null hypothesis as compelling evidence for anisotropic CMB birefringence has not yet been found. Since we have already shown in Section
3
that the simple modification of the maximum likelihood estimator is equivalent to the GMV estimator, it remains to compare the GMV and HO estimators. We show that, if the two are constructed using only the polarization but not the temperature anisotropies, then they have the same reconstruction noise power spectrum. When the temperature anisotropies are included in estimating rotation, we find numerically in Section
6
that the difference in the reconstruction noise spectra between the GMV and HO estimators is insignificant.

From Eq. (
2.14
) and Eq. (
2.16
), we can see that the presence of a non-vanishing cross-variable spectrum,
C^{TE}_{l}
, makes formulas more complicated (note that
C^{EB}_{l}
vanishes due to statistical invariance under parity). This complication is avoided if temperature anisotropies
T(\bm{\mathbf{l}})
are entirely excluded from the construction of the GMV and HO estimators. When only the
E
and
B
polarization anisotropies are considered, it is possible to show that the GMV and HO estimators are equivalent. In fact, this is a special case of the general statement that the GMV and HO estimators are identical whenever
C^{XY}_{l}=0
for any
X\neq Y
, which we now show.

Assuming
C^{XY}_{l}=0
for
X\neq Y
, Eq. (
2.18
) implies that
\bm{\mathbf{N}}
is a diagonal matrix,
i.e.
there is no covariance between different single-pair estimators. Using Eq. (
2.9
), Eq. (
2.13
), and Eq. (
2.20
), the HO estimator becomes

\hat{\alpha}_{HO}(\bm{\mathbf{L}})=N_{HO}(L)\sum_{(X,Y)}\int_{\bm{\mathbf{L}}=\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{1}-\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{2}}X_{o}(\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{1})\,Y^{*}_{o}(\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{2})\,\frac{f_{XY}(\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{1},\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{2})}{(1+\delta_{XY})\,C^{XX}_{l_{1}}\,C^{YY}_{l_{2}}},

(4.11)

where the sum is over unique pairs of observables
XY
, and

N_{HO}(L)=\left(\sum_{(X,Y)}N^{-1}_{XY}(L)\right)^{-1}.

(4.12)

On the other hand, Eq. (
2.24
) can be rewritten as

\hat{\alpha}_{GMV}=N_{GMV}(L)\sum_{(X,Y)}\int_{\bm{\mathbf{L}}=\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{1}-\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{2}}X_{o}(\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{1})\,Y^{*}_{o}(\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{2})\,\frac{f_{XY}(\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{1},\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{2})}{(1+\delta_{XY})\,C^{XX}_{l_{1}}\,C^{YY}_{l_{2}}}.

(4.13)

The equality of the reconstruction noise spectra
N_{HO}(L)=N_{GMV}(L)
can be straightforwardly derived from Eq. (
2.27
) and the symmetry property of
\bm{\mathbf{f}}(\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{1},\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{2})
. Therefore, we conclude that
\hat{\alpha}_{HO}(L)=\hat{\alpha}_{GMV}(L)
if
C^{XY}_{l}=0
for
X\neq Y
.

In the special case where only polarization anisotropies
E
and
B
are considered, the reconstruction noise spectrum has the form

N_{HO}(L)=N_{GMV}(L)=\left(\int_{\bm{\mathbf{L}}=\mathbf{l}_{1}-\mathbf{l}_{2}}\left[\frac{f_{EE}(\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{1},\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{2})^{2}}{2\,C_{l_{1}}^{EE}C_{l_{2}}^{EE}}+\frac{f_{EB}(\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{1},\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{2})^{2}}{C_{l_{1}}^{EE}C_{l_{2}}^{BB}}+\frac{f_{BB}(\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{1},\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{2})^{2}}{2\,C_{l_{1}}^{BB}C_{l_{2}}^{BB}}\right]\right)^{-1}.

(4.14)

5
Simultaneous estimation of lensing and rotation

We have carried out the analysis under the assumption that the power spectra of lensed (but unrotated) CMB observables are known. The estimator for the rotation
\hat{\alpha}
is constructed from these lensed power spectra through the weights
F_{XY}(\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{1},\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{2})
or
\Xi_{ij}(\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{1},\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{2})
. However, one might worry that the estimate for lensing is contaminated by the presence of anisotropic rotation, and conversely the estimate for rotation might be contaminated by lensing. We show in this section that any dependence of quadratic estimators
\expectationvalue{\hat\alpha_{XY}}
on the lensing potential
\phi
is of
\mathcal{O}(\phi^{2})
, and likewise any dependence of quadratic estimators for the lensing potential
\expectationvalue{\hat\phi_{XY}}
on the polarization rotation is of order
\mathcal{O}(\alpha^{2})
. In other words, at linear order
\alpha
and
\phi
can be simultaneously estimated from the CMB anisotropies without bias
[
14
]
. However, the second-order contamination of the rotation power spectrum due to lensing will eventually need to be corrected for in future experiments (see Fig. 2 in Ref.
[
36
]
).

We first rewrite Eq. (
2.2
) to include the linear response due to the lensing potential:

\expectationvalue{X(\vect l_1)Y^*(\vect l_2)}=\expectationvalue{\bar X(\vect l_1)\bar Y^*(\vect l_2)}+f_{XY}^{\phi}(\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{1},\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{2})\phi(\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{1}-\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{2})+f_{XY}^{\alpha}(\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{1},\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{2})\alpha(\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{1}-\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{2})+O(\alpha^{2},\phi^{2},\alpha\phi),

(5.1)

where
\bar{X},\bar{Y}
denote the unlensed
and
unrotated anisotropies. We now write down the “naive” minimum-variance estimator for
\alpha
and
\phi
, respectively, from the observed quantities (with subscript
o
), pretending that only one effect takes place at a time:

\displaystyle\hat{\alpha}_{XY}

\displaystyle=\int_{\bm{\mathbf{L}}=\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{1}-\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{2}}X_{o}(\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{1})Y_{o}^{*}(\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{2})F^{\alpha}_{XY}(\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{1},\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{2}),

(5.2)

\displaystyle\hat{\phi}_{XY}

\displaystyle=\int_{\bm{\mathbf{L}}=\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{1}-\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{2}}X_{o}(\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{1})Y_{o}^{*}(\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{2})F^{\phi}_{XY}(\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{1},\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{2}).

(5.3)

Here the weights
F^{\alpha}_{XY}
are the same as in Eq. (
2.13
) and Eq. (
2.14
). The weights
F^{\phi}_{XY}
are defined in Ref.
[
19
]
.

For
\bm{\mathbf{L}}\neq 0
, the rotation estimator has an expectation value

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{\hat\alpha_{XY}(\vect L)}

\displaystyle=\int_{\bm{\mathbf{L}}=\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{1}-\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{2}}\expectationvalue{\bar X(\vect l_1)\bar Y^*(\vect l_2)}\,F^{\alpha}_{XY}(\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{1},\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{2})+\int_{\bm{\mathbf{L}}=\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{1}-\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{2}}\expectationvalue{\bar X_n(\vect l_1)\bar Y_n^*(\vect l_2)}\,F^{\alpha}_{XY}(\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{1},\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{2})

\displaystyle\quad+\alpha(\bm{\mathbf{L}})\int_{\bm{\mathbf{L}}=\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{1}-\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{2}}f^{\alpha}_{XY}(\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{1},\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{2})\,F^{\alpha}_{XY}(\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{1},\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{2})+\phi(\bm{\mathbf{L}})\int_{\bm{\mathbf{L}}=\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{1}-\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{2}}f^{\phi}_{XY}(\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{1},\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{2})\,F^{\alpha}_{XY}(\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{1},\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{2}),

(5.4)

where
f^{\alpha}_{XY}(\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{1},\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{2})
are found in Table
1
, and
f^{\phi}_{XY}
are found in Table 1 of Ref.
[
19
]
. The first two terms vanish because
\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{1}\neq\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{2}
. The third term reduces to
\alpha(\bm{\mathbf{L}})
due to the unbiasedness of the estimator (Eq. (
2.11
)). It remains to see whether the last term vanishes, or in other words, whether the naive estimator for
\alpha
is contaminated by lensing.

Here, we show explicitly the proof for
XY=EB
, but the result holds analogously for all pairs
XY
.

\displaystyle\quad\int_{\bm{\mathbf{L}}=\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{1}-\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{2}}f^{\phi}_{EB}(\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{1},\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{2})\,F^{\alpha}_{EB}(\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{1},\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{2})

(5.5)

\displaystyle=\int_{\bm{\mathbf{L}}=\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{1}-\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{2}}\frac{f^{\phi}_{EB}(\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{1},\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{2})\,f^{\alpha}_{XY}(\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{1},\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{2})}{C^{EB}_{l_{1}}C^{EB}_{l_{2}}}

(5.6)

\displaystyle=\int\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{1}\,\mathrm{d}^{2}\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{2}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\delta_{D}(l_{1x}-l_{2x}-L_{x})\,\delta_{D}(l_{1y}-l_{2y}-L_{y})\,\left(C^{EB}_{l_{1}}\,C^{EB}_{l_{2}}\right)^{-1}

(5.7)

\displaystyle\quad\times\left(\tilde{C}^{E\nabla E}_{l_{1}}\,\bm{\mathbf{L}}\cdot\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{1}+\tilde{C}^{B\nabla B}_{l_{2}}\,\bm{\mathbf{L}}\cdot\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{2}\right)\,\sin 2\varphi_{12}\times 2\,\left(\tilde{C}^{BB}_{l_{1}}-\tilde{C}^{EE}_{l_{2}}\right)\,\cos 2\varphi_{12}.

(5.8)

The quantities
\tilde{C}^{X\nabla Y}_{l}
are the lensed gradient spectrum, defined in
[
37
,
38
]
. Under a change of variables:
l_{iy}\to-l_{iy}
for
i=1,2
, we have
\varphi_{12}\to-\varphi_{12}
and

\displaystyle\delta_{D}(l_{1y}-l_{2y}-L_{y})\,\bm{\mathbf{L}}\cdot\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{i}

\displaystyle\to\delta_{D}(-l_{1y}+l_{2y}-L_{y})\,(L_{x}l_{ix}-L_{y}l_{iy})

(5.9)

\displaystyle=\delta_{D}(-l_{1y}+l_{2y}-L_{y})\,[L_{x}l_{ix}+(l_{1y}-l_{2y})\,l_{iy}]

(5.10)

\displaystyle=\delta_{D}(l_{1y}-l_{2y}-L_{y})\,[L_{x}l_{ix}+(l_{1y}-l_{2y})\,l_{iy}]

(5.11)

\displaystyle=\delta_{D}(l_{1y}-l_{2y}-L_{y})\,\bm{\mathbf{L}}\cdot\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{i}.

(5.12)

The whole integrand is odd under this change of variables, and hence the integral vanishes.

By putting together Table
1
, and Table 1 of Ref.
[
19
]
, it is easy to see that the corresponding integrals for other pairs
XY
also vanish. We conclude that

\expectationvalue{\hat\alpha_{XY}(\vect L)}=\alpha(\bm{\mathbf{L}})+O(\alpha^{2},\phi^{2},\alpha\phi),

(5.13)

where
\hat{\alpha}_{XY}
here is constructed from unlensed and unrotated spectra. A similar statement can be made about
\hat{\phi}_{XY}(\bm{\mathbf{L}})
by the same parity argument. Ref.
[
14
]
reached the same conclusion based on the parity argument, for the full-sky version of these estimators.

The goal of estimating spatially dependent polarization rotation is to detect any new physics that might possibly cause this phenomenon, such as a pseudoscalar axion-like field that couples to the electromagnetic field. In this section, we study the constraining power of the quadratic estimators on the scenario that the rotation results from a network of cosmic strings. As explained in Ref.
[
8
]
, these are topological remnants reminiscent of a phase transition in the pseudoscalar field in the primordial Universe.

Since the cosmic strings that comprise the string network have random configurations, a key theoretical prediction is the power spectrum for the rotation field
\alpha
:

\expectationvalue{\alpha(\vect L)\alpha(\vect L')}=(2\pi)^{2}\,\delta_{D}(\bm{\mathbf{L}}-\bm{\mathbf{L}}^{\prime})\,K\,P(L),

(6.2)

where the notation for ensemble averaging
\langle\cdots\rangle
is over all string network realizations. Here
P(L)
is a fiducial functional form for the power spectrum, and
K
is a normalization constant that we would like to detect or constrain. In the presence of strings, a general quadratic estimator
\hat{\alpha}(\bm{\mathbf{L}})
has a covariance

\expectationvalue{\hat\alpha(\vect L)\,\hat\alpha(\vect L')}=(2\pi)^{2}\,\delta_{D}(\bm{\mathbf{L}}-\bm{\mathbf{L}}^{\prime})\,\left(N(L)+K\,P(L)\right),

(6.3)

where
N(L)
is the null-hypothesis reconstruction noise power spectrum as we have discussed in the previous sections. In practical analysis, one works with a discretized set of wave vectors
\bm{\mathbf{L}}
, and we may write

\expectationvalue{\hat\alpha_{\vect L}\hat\alpha_{\vect L'}}=A\,\delta_{\bm{\mathbf{L}},\bm{\mathbf{L}}^{\prime}}\,\left(N_{L}+K\,P_{L}\right),

(6.4)

where
A
is the total area of the sky that is surveyed.

An unbiased estimator for the normalization
K
may then be constructed from each
\hat{\alpha}(\bm{\mathbf{L}})
by

\hat{K}_{\bm{\mathbf{L}}}=P_{L}^{-1}(A^{-1}|\hat{\alpha}_{\bm{\mathbf{L}}}|^{2}-N_{L}).

(6.5)

To take full advantage of the information, these estimators can then be optimally combined to make a single estimator:

\hat{K}=\sum_{\bm{\mathbf{L}}}\,w_{\bm{\mathbf{L}}}\,\hat{K}_{\bm{\mathbf{L}}},

(6.6)

The weights
w_{\bm{\mathbf{L}}}
are chosen to minimize the variance of
\hat{K}
in the absence of polarization rotation (i.e.
K=0
):

w_{\bm{\mathbf{L}}}=\frac{\sum_{\bm{\mathbf{L}}^{\prime}}[\mathcal{C}_{K}^{-1}]_{\bm{\mathbf{L}},\bm{\mathbf{L}}^{\prime}}}{\sum_{\bm{\mathbf{L}}^{\prime},\bm{\mathbf{L}}^{\prime\prime}}[\mathcal{C}_{K}^{-1}]_{\bm{\mathbf{L}}^{\prime},\bm{\mathbf{L}}^{\prime\prime}}},

(6.7)

where
\mathcal{C}_{K}
is the covariance matrix of
K_{\bm{\mathbf{L}}}
’s for the various
\bm{\mathbf{L}}
’s. The variance of
\hat{K}
is then

\langle\hat{K}^{2}\rangle=\left[\sum_{\bm{\mathbf{L}},\bm{\mathbf{L}}^{\prime}}[\mathcal{C}_{K}^{-1}]_{\bm{\mathbf{L}},\bm{\mathbf{L}}^{\prime}}\right]^{-1}.

(6.8)

In fact, the covariance matrix is diagonal:

(\mathcal{C}_{K})_{\bm{\mathbf{L}},\bm{\mathbf{L}}^{\prime}}=\delta_{\bm{\mathbf{L}},\bm{\mathbf{L}}^{\prime}}\langle\hat{K}_{\bm{\mathbf{L}}}^{2}\rangle,

(6.9)

which is a consequence of statistical isotropy of the CMB anisotropies, namely that no direction on the sky has special statistics. While statistical isotropy holds for the primordial anisotropies, weak lensing by a specific realization of the foreground large-scale structure induces couplings between different Fourier modes
[
18
]
. For the purpose of estimating the signal-to-noise ratio of
\hat{K}
, we neglect the mode couplings due to lensing. This omission is justified, as we will show in Section
5
that any dependence of the rotation quadratic estimators on the lensing potential
\phi
is of order
\mathcal{O}(\phi^{2})
; when random realizations of foreground lens are averaged over, statistical isotropy is restored.

After some calculation, we have

\langle\hat{K}_{\bm{\mathbf{L}}}^{2}\rangle=\left(\frac{N_{L}}{P_{L}}\right)^{2}.

(6.10)

The variance of
\hat{K}
under the null hypothesis is given by

\langle\hat{K}^{2}\rangle^{-1}=\sum_{\bm{\mathbf{L}}}\left(\frac{P_{L}}{N_{L}}\right)^{2}.

(6.11)

The square of the signal-to-noise ratio is

\frac{K^{2}}{\langle\hat{K}^{2}\rangle}=\sum_{\bm{\mathbf{L}}}\left(\frac{KP_{L}}{N_{L}}\right)^{2}=\sum_{L}f_{\mathrm{sky}}(2L+1)\left(\frac{KP_{L}}{N_{L}}\right)^{2},

(6.12)

where
f_{\mathrm{sky}}
is the fraction of the full sky that is surveyed, and
2L+1
is the number of discrete
\bm{\mathbf{L}}
vectors at a fixed magnitude
L
.

7
String network models

As an example, we consider phenomenological models of string networks developed in Ref.
[
17
]
. The theoretical framework is as follows. The Universe is populated by many circular string loops oriented in random directions, arising from a pseudoscalar field with anomaly coefficient
\mathcal{A}
. At any given conformal time
\tau
, the number of string loops with radius between
r
and
r+\mathrm{d}r
is given by the distribution function
\nu(r,\tau)\,\mathrm{d}r
, specified by the model.

In order to compare models with different distributions
\nu
, a dimensionless quantity is defined:

\xi_{\infty}(\tau)=\frac{1}{a(\tau)^{2}H(\tau)^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}r\,2\pi r\,\nu(r,\tau).

(7.1)

Intuitively, if all the string loops at conformal time
\tau
were rearranged into a minimal number of Hubble-scale loops while conserving the energy density of the string network, then
\xi_{\infty}(\tau)
would be equal to the number of such Hubble-scale loops per Hubble volume. Equivalently,
\xi_{\infty}(\tau)
is the total length of strings in units of the Hubble scale per Hubble volume at conformal time
\tau
. Both models considered here are
in scaling
. That is to say, the string network’s energy density scales like the dominant energy density in the Universe, and
\xi_{\infty}(\tau)\equiv\xi_{0}
is independent of
\tau
.
1
1

1

Whether cosmic string networks exhibit this in-scaling property is a matter of debate, with some studies concluding the affirmative and others finding a logarithmic growth in
\xi_{\infty}
. See Ref.
[
17
]
for a brief discussion and references on this subject.
The model parameter
\xi_{0}
is also referred to as the effective number of strings per Hubble volume.

The differences and additional parameters of these models are briefly stated below.

•

Model I
: All string loops have the same radius
\zeta_{0}
(in units of the Hubble scale at each redshift).

•

\zeta_{\mathrm{max}}=1

For both models, the predicted power spectrum of the rotation field due to the string network is proportional to the product
\mathcal{A}^{2}\,\xi_{0}

\expectationvalue{\alpha_{\vect L}\,\alpha_{\vect L'}}=A\,\delta_{\bm{\mathbf{L}},\bm{\mathbf{L}}^{\prime}}\,\mathcal{A}^{2}\,\xi_{0}\,P_{L}(\cdots),

where the fiducial power spectrum shape

\mathcal{A}

\xi_{0}

9
Conclusion

We have summarized three different constructions of the quadratic estimator for direction dependent rotation of CMB polarization—the HO estimator
[
18
]
, the GMV estimator
[
19
]
, and one that is derived from the maximum likelihood estimator.
We have shown that the latter two are equivalent in the context of cosmic birefringence, and the first two are equivalent if temperature anisotropies are not used (a general sufficient condition for such equivalence is also stated). While the GMV estimator improves upon the HO estimator when both temperature and polarization anisotropies are taken into account, the improvement is numerically computed to be below 0.5% on all relevant angular scales.

Although we have not seen a substantial reduction in the reconstruction noise by replacing the HO estimator with the GMV estimator, it might still be preferable to adopt the former for two reasons. First, the GMV estimator (as well as its reconstruction noise spectrum) is computed from a single integral (see Eq. (
2.24
) and Eq. (
2.27
)), as opposed to one integral for every pair of anisotropy observables. Second, as pointed out in Ref.
[
19
]
, the weights
\bm{\mathbf{\Xi}}
used in the GMV estimator are separable into sums of products of functions of
\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{1}
and functions of
\bm{\mathbf{l}}_{2}
, allowing efficient computation via Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) without any further approximations (such as by setting
\tilde{C}_{l}^{TE}=0
in some formulae).

We present the reconstruction noise spectra of the HO estimator for the Planck mission, the Simons Observatory, as well as CMB-S4. This is compared to the reconstruction noise spectra of the single-pair
EB
and
TB
estimators
[
15
]
. See Figure
1
.

We have made SNR forecasts for phenomenological string network models (see Figure
2
and Figure
3
). It is found that contributions to the SNR are dominated by the reconstruction of modes on large angular scales:
L\lesssim 30
for Planck SMICA, and
L\lesssim 100
for the Simons Observatory and CMB-S4. When a continuous distribution of string loop sizes is accounted for, detectability does not strongly depend on the fraction of sub-horizon string loop sizes. In Figure
4
, it can be seen that existing Planck data should provide tight constraints in a significant portion of the theoretically plausible parameter space at
\mathcal{A}^{2}\,\xi_{0}\gtrsim 1
.

The birefringence power spectrum extracted from Planck 2015 data
[
20
]
has allowed us to place a constraint on
\mathcal{A}^{2}\,\xi_{0}
using either of the string network models. Model I, which postulates circular string loops of identical radii, leads to an upper bound of
\mathcal{A}^{2}\,\xi_{0}<8.0
at the 95% confidence level, while the more realistic Model II, which postulates circular string loops of a continuous distribution of radii, leads to a stronger upper bound of
\mathcal{A}^{2}\,\xi_{0}<0.93
at the 95% confidence level. Model I leads to a weaker constraint because much of the parameter space describes a Universe populated by sub-Hubble scale string loops (
\zeta_{0}<1
), and the signal in the power spectrum is suppressed by an overall factor
\zeta_{0}

[
17
]
. On the other hand, most of the parameter space in Model II describes a Universe with many Hubble-scale string loops, leading to the prediction of a stronger signal. Simulations have shown that less than 20% of the axion string loops are below the Hubble scale, while the remaining strings wrap the simulation box
[
46
]
, interpreted as string loops at the Hubble scale in Model II. It is for this reason that we view Model II, along with its bound on
\mathcal{A}^{2}\,\xi_{0}
, as a more accurate description of the phenomenology of cosmic string networks. In the future, more detailed numerical simulations of cosmic string network formation should provide more accurate models of the rotation field. The analysis presented in this work can also be performed for other models of cosmic birefringence, such as ALP domain wall scenarios presented in Ref.
[
47
]
.

Third- and fourth-generation CMB experiments from the ground are expected to improve upon the SNR of the Planck mission by another one and two orders of magnitude, respectively, and will be able to either discover or rule out such cosmic string networks for most of the theoretically favored region of the parameter space (see Figure
4
).

We have justified the simplifying assumption that for estimating polarization rotation the lensed temperature and polarization anisotropies may be taken to be Gaussian random fields, by showing that the lensing potential
\phi

\mathcal{O}(\phi)

While the quadratic estimators studied in this work can statistically detect a network of strings, it is sensitive to the combination
\mathcal{A}^{2}\,\xi_{0}

L\lesssim 50

\mathcal{A}

\xi_{0}

Moreover, linear stringy features imply phase correlations between Fourier modes of the rotation field, the confirmation of which will provide decisive evidence for topological defects as the origin of the ALP field. Extracting this information calls for further development of non-Gaussian statistical tests for polarization rotations in the CMB, or the employment of neural-network-based reconstruction techniques
