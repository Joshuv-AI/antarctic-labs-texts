Convergence Rate Analysis in Limit Theorems for Nonlinear Functionals of the Second Wiener Chaos
Gi-Ren Liu

Abstract
This paper analyzes the distribution distance between random vectors from the analytic wavelet transform of squared envelopes of Gaussian processes and their large-scale limits. For Gaussian processes with a long-memory parameter β \beta below 1/2, the limit combines the second and fourth Wiener chaos. Using a non-Stein approach, we determine the convergence rate in the Kolmogorov metric. When the long-memory parameter β \beta exceeds 1/2, the limit is a chi-distributed random process, and the convergence rate in the Wasserstein metric is determined using multidimensional Stein’s method. Notable differences in convergence rate upper bounds are observed for long-memory parameters within (1/2,3/4) and (3/4,1).

2.1
Analytic wavelet transform

In the field of signal segment classification, considering a real-valued process
\{Y(t)\}_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
and a set of real-valued testing functions
\{\psi_{s}\}_{s\in\Lambda}
, where
\Lambda
is an index set, for assessing the similarity between the pattern of the process
Y
during a time neighborhood of
t
and
\psi_{s}
for each
s\in\Lambda
, we consider the convolution

\displaystyle Y\star\psi_{s}(t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}Y(t-u)\psi_{s}(u)du,\ t\in\mathbb{R}.

(1)

In the field of wavelet theory,
\Lambda=(0,\infty)
and

\displaystyle\psi_{s}(u)=\frac{1}{s}\psi\left(\frac{u}{s}\right),

where
\psi
is an integrable function defined on
\mathbb{R}
satisfying
\int_{\mathbb{R}}\psi(u)du=0
.
For small values of
s
,
\psi_{s}
quantifies the small-scale fluctuations in
Y
through (
1
).
Subsequently, we refer to
s
as the scale variable.
To reduce the impact of small time shifting on the value of convolution (
1
), one prefers to use the envelope of

Y\star\psi_{s}
, which can be obtained from the magnitude of the following complex wavelet transform

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{s}Y(t)=Y\star\psi_{s}(t)+iY\star\mathcal{H}\psi_{s}(t),

(2)

where
\mathcal{H}
represents the Hilbert transform defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}\psi_{s}(t)=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\psi_{s}(u)}{t-u}du.

We denote the squared envelope of precess
Y\star\psi_{s}
as
U^{2}[s]Y
, i.e.,

\displaystyle U^{2}[s]Y(t):=\left(Y\star\psi_{s}(t)\right)^{2}+\left(Y\star\mathcal{H}\psi_{s}(t)\right)^{2},

(3)

which is known as the scalogram.
We denote the Fourier transform of
\psi
by
\widehat{\psi}
, which is defined as

\displaystyle\widehat{\psi}(\lambda)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-iu\lambda}\psi(u)du,\ \lambda\in\mathbb{R}.

As stated in
[
17
]
,
\widehat{\psi_{s}}(\lambda)=\widehat{\psi}(s\lambda)
and
\widehat{\mathcal{H}\psi_{s}}(\lambda)=-i\textup{sgn}(\lambda)\widehat{\psi}(s\lambda)
, where

\textup{sgn}(\lambda)=\lambda/|\lambda|
for
\lambda\neq 0
and
\textup{sgn}(0)=0
.
Because
\psi_{s}+i\mathcal{H}\psi_{s}
is analytic, meaning its Fourier transform is null for negative frequencies, (
2
) is referred to as the analytic wavelet transform
[
29
,
38
]
.
In the following content, we only require
\psi
and
\widehat{\psi}
to belong to
L^{1}
.
Commonly used wavelets such as the Morlet wavelet, the Daubechies wavelet, the Mexican Hat wavelet, and the Morse wavelet are suitable for our purposes, with the exception of the Haar wavelet.

2.2
Envelope of stationary Gaussian processes

Consider a real-valued, mean-square continuous, and stationary Gaussian random process

Definition 1

\displaystyle\underset{s\rightarrow\infty}{\lim}\frac{g(cs)}{g(s)}=c^{\tau}.

In accordance with the literature
[

Assumption 1

The spectral measure

is absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure with a density function

\displaystyle 8\left[\overset{4}{\underset{\ell=1}{\prod}}\sqrt{f_{X}(\lambda_{\ell})}\right]\widehat{\psi}(s(\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}))\widehat{\psi}(s(\lambda_{3}+\lambda_{4}))

\displaystyle\times 1_{\{\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}<0\}}1_{\{\lambda_{3}\lambda_{4}<0\}}1_{\{(\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2})(\lambda_{3}+\lambda_{4})<0\}}.

Remark 2
.

Let

denote the Laguerre polynomials, which form an orthogonal basis for the Hilbert space

,

, and
L_{2}(r)=2^{-1}r^{2}-2r+1

The first and second Laguerre chaos

and

derived from the chi-square random process
in Remark

can be expressed as linear combinations of the second and fourth Wiener chaos
[

as follows:

\displaystyle L_{1}(\chi^{2}_{2}(t))=-\frac{1}{2}\left(H_{2}(\zeta_{1}(t))+H_{2}(\zeta_{2}(t))\right)

\displaystyle L_{2}(\chi^{2}_{2}(t))=\frac{1}{8}\left[2H_{2}(\zeta_{1}(t))H_{2}(\zeta_{2}(t))-H_{4}(\zeta_{1}(t))-H_{4}(\zeta_{2}(t))\right],

(17)

Several limit theorems, including
[

,

[

, Theorem 2.4]
and
[

, Section 5]
, about additive functionals of the Laguerre chaos have been established based on (
16
), (
17
), and various filtering methods.

It is worth to mentioning that the transformation

is not an additive functional.
As shown in Figure

, the transformation

of

comprises two parallel convolution operators, namely

and

The order of the action of local and non-local operators on chi-square random processes is exactly opposite to that in the literature
[

The discrepancy makes the spectral representation of

difficult to express as Laguerre chaos.
Consequently, the main results presented below cannot be encompassed by existing limit theorems.

3

In the subsequent discussion, when comparing two functions,

4
Concluding remarks

For the nonlinear transformation depicted in Figure
1
, from Theorems
1
and
2
, we observed that the long-memory parameter of the initial input
X
at
1/2
acts as a dividing point.
Remarkably, this division directly corresponds to the long-memory parameter of
Y
, which represents the squared modulus of the analytic counterpart of
X
.
When the long-memory parameter of
X
either falls below or exceeds this threshold, it determines the nature of the process
Y
– whether it exhibits long-range dependence or short-range dependence.
This distinction leads to the convergence of the rescaled version of
U[s]Y
towards different limits.
The former (i.e.,
s^{2\beta}L^{-2}(s)U^{2}[s]Y(s\cdot)
) converges to an additive combination of the second and fourth Wiener chaos, while the latter (i.e.,
sU^{2}[s]Y(s\cdot)
) tends towards a chi-square random process.
Besides, we also conducted a systematic analysis of
the relationship between the convergence rate of the large scaling limits and the strength of long-range dependence in the initial input
X
.
About the upper bound of the distance between the distributions of the rescaled
U[s]Y
and its limit, our observations indicate that the speed of convergence to zero diminishes as the strength of long-range dependence in the initial input
X
increases.

. On the other hand, because
\beta\in(0,1/2)
under the hypothesis of Theorem
1
,
the Riesz composition formula shows that
g^{(4)}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{4})
with
\|\widetilde{g}^{(4)}\|_{2}\leq\|g^{(4)}\|_{2}.

Let
\{e_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
be an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
.
Then, there exist constants
\{c_{i_{1},i_{2}}\}_{i_{1},i_{2}\in\mathbb{N}}
and
\{d_{i_{1},i_{2},i_{3},i_{4}}\}_{i_{1},i_{2},i_{3},i_{4}\in\mathbb{N}}
such that

\displaystyle g^{(2)}=

\displaystyle\underset{i_{1},i_{2}\in\mathbb{N}}{\sum}c_{i_{1},i_{2}}e_{i_{1}}\otimes e_{i_{2}}

and

\displaystyle\widetilde{g}^{(4)}=

\displaystyle\underset{i_{1},i_{2},i_{3},i_{4}\in\mathbb{N}}{\sum}d_{i_{1},i_{2},i_{3},i_{4}}e_{i_{1}}\otimes e_{i_{2}}\otimes e_{i_{3}}\otimes e_{i_{4}}.

For any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, denote

\displaystyle g^{(2)}_{n}=

\displaystyle\underset{i_{1},i_{2}\in\{1,2,...,n\}}{\sum}c_{i_{1},i_{2}}e_{i_{1}}\otimes e_{i_{2}},

\displaystyle\widetilde{g}_{n}^{(4)}=

\displaystyle\underset{i_{1},i_{2},i_{3},i_{4}\in\{1,2,...,n\}}{\sum}d_{i_{1},i_{2},i_{3},i_{4}}e_{i_{1}}\otimes e_{i_{2}}\otimes e_{i_{3}}\otimes e_{i_{4}},

and

\displaystyle Z(t;n)=Z^{(0)}+I_{2}(g^{(2)}_{n})+I_{4}(\widetilde{g}_{n}^{(4)}).

Because
\|g^{(2)}_{n}-g^{(2)}\|_{2}
and
\|\widetilde{g}_{n}^{(4)}-\widetilde{g}^{(4)}\|_{2}
converge to zero
when
n\rightarrow\infty
, the isometry property of Wiener-It
\hat{\textup{o}}
integrals implies that

\mathbb{E}|Z(t;n)-Z(t)|^{2}\rightarrow 0
when
n\rightarrow\infty
.
Hence, there exists a subsequence
\{n_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
of
\{1,2,3,...\}
such that

Z(t;n_{j})\rightarrow Z(t)
almost surely
when
j\rightarrow\infty
.
It also follows that for any
\varepsilon>0
and
z\in\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(|Z(t)-z|\leq\varepsilon/2\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(\underset{j\rightarrow\infty}{\liminf}\ |Z(t;n_{j})-z|\leq\varepsilon/2\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\underset{j\rightarrow\infty}{\liminf}\ 1_{[0,\varepsilon]}\left(|Z(t;n_{j})-z|\right)\right]\leq\underset{j\rightarrow\infty}{\liminf}\ \mathbb{E}\left[1_{[0,\varepsilon]}\left(|Z(t;n_{j})-z|\right)\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\underset{j\rightarrow\infty}{\liminf}\ \mathbb{P}\left(|Z(t;n_{j})-z|\leq\varepsilon\right).

(105)

For estimating the right hand side of (
105
),
we note that

\displaystyle I_{2}(g^{(2)}_{n_{j}})=

\displaystyle I_{2}\left(\underset{i_{1},i_{2}\in\{1,2,...,n_{j}\}}{\sum}c_{i_{1},i_{2}}e_{i_{1}}\otimes e_{i_{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\underset{i_{1},i_{2}\in\{1,2,...,n_{j}\}}{\sum}c_{i_{1},i_{2}}I_{2}\left(e_{i_{1}}\otimes e_{i_{2}}\right),

where, by It
\hat{\textup{o}}
’s formula
[
37
, Theorem 4.3]
,

\displaystyle I_{2}\left(e_{i_{1}}\otimes e_{i_{2}}\right)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}H_{1}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}e_{i_{1}}(\lambda)W(d\lambda)\right)H_{1}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}e_{i_{2}}(\lambda)W(d\lambda)\right)&\textup{if}\ i_{1}\neq i_{2},\\
H_{2}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}e_{i_{1}}(\lambda)W(d\lambda)\right)&\textup{if}\ i_{1}=i_{2}.\end{array}\right.

Hence,

\displaystyle I_{2}(g^{(2)}_{n_{j}})=U_{2,n_{j}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}e_{1}(\lambda)W(d\lambda),...,\int_{\mathbb{R}}e_{n_{j}}(\lambda)W(d\lambda)\right),

(106)

for a certain polynomial function
U_{2,n_{j}}:\mathbb{R}^{n_{j}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
of degree two.
Similarly,

\displaystyle I_{4}(\widetilde{g}^{(4)}_{n_{j}})=U_{4,n_{j}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}e_{1}(\lambda)W(d\lambda),...,\int_{\mathbb{R}}e_{n_{j}}(\lambda)W(d\lambda)\right)

(107)

for a certain polynomial function
U_{4,n_{j}}:\mathbb{R}^{n_{j}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
of degree four.
For any fixed
z\in\mathbb{R}
, let
V_{4,n_{j}}=-z+Z^{(0)}+U_{2,n_{j}}+U_{4,n_{j}}
, which is a polynomial function of degree four.
By (
106
) and (
107
),

\displaystyle Z(t;n_{j})-z=V_{4,n_{j}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}e_{1}(\lambda)W(d\lambda),...,\int_{\mathbb{R}}e_{n_{j}}(\lambda)W(d\lambda)\right).

(108)

Because the inputs of the polynomial function
V_{4,n_{j}}
in (
108
) consist of
n_{j}

independent standard normal random variables, by the Carbery-Wright inequality
[
43
, Theorem 2.5]
,
there exists a constant
C
independent of
n_{j}
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(|Z(t;n_{j})-z|\leq\varepsilon\right)\leq C\left\{\mathbb{E}\left[\left|Z(t;n_{j})-z\right|^{2}\right]\right\}^{-\frac{1}{8}}\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{4}}.

(109)

Since
\mathbb{E}|Z(t;n_{j})-Z(t)|^{2}\rightarrow 0
when
j\rightarrow\infty
,

\displaystyle\underset{j\rightarrow\infty}{\liminf}\left\{\mathbb{E}\left[\left|Z(t;n_{j})-z\right|^{2}\right]\right\}^{-\frac{1}{8}}=\left\{\mathbb{E}\left[\left|Z(t)-z\right|^{2}\right]\right\}^{-\frac{1}{8}}.

(110)

Combining (
109
) and (
110
) yields

\displaystyle\underset{j\rightarrow\infty}{\liminf}\ \mathbb{P}\left(|Z(t;n_{j})-z|\leq\varepsilon\right)\leq C\left\{\mathbb{E}\left[\left|Z(t)-z\right|^{2}\right]\right\}^{-\frac{1}{8}}\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{4}}.

(111)

By substituting (
111
) into (
105
), we obtain the desired inequality

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(|Z(t)-z|\leq\varepsilon/2\right)\leq C\left\{\mathbb{E}\left[\left|Z(t)-z\right|^{2}\right]\right\}^{-\frac{1}{8}}\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{4}}.

∎
