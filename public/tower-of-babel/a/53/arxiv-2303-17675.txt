Data-Driven Covariance Steering Control Design
Joshua Pilipovsky Panagiotis Tsiotras

Abstract
This paper studies the problem of steering the distribution of a linear time-invariant system from an initial normal distribution to a terminal normal distribution under no knowledge of the system dynamics. This data-driven control framework uses data collected from the input and the state and utilizes the seminal work by Willems et al. to construct a data-based parametrization of the mean and the covariance control problems. These problems are then solved to optimality as convex programs using standard techniques from the covariance control literature. We also discuss the equivalence of indirect and direct data-driven covariance steering designs, as well as a regularized version of the problem that provides a balance between the two. We illustrate the proposed framework through a set of randomized trials on a double integrator system and show that the results match up almost exactly with the corresponding model-based method in the noiseless case. We then analyze the robustness properties of the data-free and data-driven covariance steering methods and demonstrate the trade-offs between performance and optimality among these methods in the presence of data corrupted with exogenous noise.



I
Introduction

Recently, there has been an emergence of an increasing reliance on data-driven methods for solving complex problems in science and engineering.
The field of artificial intelligence has demonstrated the ability to solve extremely difficult problems using input and output data using the machinery of neural networks and learning-based algorithms
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
.
One of the major fundamental flaws, however, of purely learning-based solutions is their lack of verifiability, that is, verifying that the networks will perform as expected given an input data stream.
Many works have begun looking at robustness properties to verify these neural networks both in the deterministic
[
4
,

,
6
,

8
]
and the probabilistic settings
[

,
10

.
The problem of analyzing the stability and robustness of a general learning-based solution is still intractable, however.

In the context of control theory, it is also often the case that we do not have prior knowledge of the system dynamics.
Ideally, we would like to use the data collected to perform control designs that have guaranteed performance and robustness properties, albeit in a learning/data-driven context.
To this end, another paradigm to perform control design is to use the input and output data streams to either estimate the model of the system, or directly perform controller synthesis using these data.
The former technique is referred to as an
indirect
design, as it performs system identification (sysID) first, followed by controller synthesis, while the latter is reffered to as a
direct
design, as it bypasses the sysID phase completely and directly generates control commands from input and output data.
Furthermore, each method may be classified into either a
certainty-equivalence
(CE) or
robust
approach, depending on whether uncertainties are taken into account.

The indirect or sysID approach has been long studied in the general setting
[
11
,
12
]
with methods such as subspace identification with broader applications to filtering and state estimation
[
13
]
.
For optimization-based control, such as the linear quadratic regular (LQR), there are many works that use the indirect approach
[
14
,
15
,
16
,
17
,
18
]
.
Similarly, the direct approach to data-driven LQR has been approached using behavioral methods
[
19
]
, gradient-based methods
[
20
,
21
]
, and Riccati-based methods
[
22
]
.
More recently, this problem has been solved using concepts from behavioral systems theory and subspace methods
[
23
]
using
Willems’ Fundamental Lemma
, which characterizes the trajectory of an LTI system through the range space of the input/output data matrix
[
24
]
.
This gives rise to a parametrization of the feedback gains as a linear combination of the collected data and allows to perform direct data-driven control design through semi-definite programming (SDP)
[

]
.
To this end, the authors in
[
26
,
27
]
were able to come up with a CE and regularized design, thus bridging the gap between the indirect and direct approaches.

All these optimization-based approaches to direct data-driven LQR design are done in the deterministic setting, assuming a single realization of the state trajectories from
x_{0}
to
x_{f}
.
To the best of our knowledge, this paper is the first work that looks at the problem where the state is a stochastic process instead of a deterministic trajectory.
Since it is still unclear how to perform data-driven designs in the context of process noise, we limit our analysis to uncertainties in the initial and final states, leaving the case of process noise for future investigation.
Specifically, we assume that the boundary values of the state must follow a normal distribution, and the objective is to steer the entire

This problem is referred to in the literature as

The CS problem in the unconstrained case has an elegant separation property between the mean and covariance dynamics, and both problems turn into convex programs

In this work, we present a data-driven covariance control design that steers the mean dynamics through an indirect sysID approach, and steers the covariance dynamics through a direct approach, using the techniques outlined in

We also analyze the regularized approach, which is a hybrid of the two.
We finally study the robustness of these approaches through a set of randomized simulations with noisy data and compare the various covariance steering frameworks.

II
Problem Statement

We consider the following discrete-time
deterministic
time-invariant system

~x_{k+1}=Ax_{k}+Bu_{k},

(1)

where
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},u\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, with time steps
k=0,\ldots,N-1
, where
N
representing the finite horizon.
The system matrices
A
and
B
are assumed to be unknown.
The uncertainty in the system resides in the initial state
x_{0}
, which is a random
n
-dimensional vector drawn from the normal distribution

~x_{0}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{i},\Sigma_{i}),

(2)

where
\mu_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the initial state mean and
\Sigma_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\succ 0
is the initial state covariance.
Thus, although the system dynamics (
1
) is deterministic, the uncertainty in the initial state makes the state evolution
\{x_{k}\}_{k=1}^{N}
a random process.
The objective is to steer the trajectories of (
1
) from the initial distribution (
2
) to the terminal distribution

~x_{N}=x_{f}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{f},\Sigma_{f}),

(3)

where
\mu_{f}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\Sigma_{f}\succ 0
are the desired state mean and covariance at time
N
, respectively.
The cost function to be minimized is

~J(u_{0},\ldots,u_{N-1})\coloneqq\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}x_{k}^{\intercal}Q_{k}x_{k}+u_{k}^{\intercal}R_{k}u_{k}\bigg],

(4)

where
Q_{k}\succeq 0
and
R_{k}\succ 0
for all
k=0,\ldots,N-1
.

Problem 1
.

Given the unknown linear system (
1
), find the optimal control sequence
\{u_{k}\}_{k=0}^{N-1}
that minimizes the objective function (
4
), subject to the initial state (
2
) and terminal state (
3
).

III
Problem Reformulation

Borrowing from the work in
[
32
]
, we adopt the control policy

~u_{k}=K_{k}(x_{k}-\mu_{k})+v_{k},

(5)

where
K_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
are the feedback gains that control the covariance of the state, and
v_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
is the feedforward term that controls the mean of the state.
Under this control law, it is possible to re-write Problem
1
as a convex program, which can be solved to optimality using off-the-shelf solvers.
Since the state distribution remains Gaussian at all time steps, and since a normal distribution is completely characterized by its first two moments, we decompose the system dynamics (
1
) into the mean dynamics and covariance dynamics.
Plugging in the control law (
5
) into the dynamics (
1
) yields the decoupled dynamics

\displaystyle\mu_{k+1}

\displaystyle=A\mu_{k}+Bv_{k},

(6a)

\displaystyle\Sigma_{k+1}

\displaystyle=(A+BK_{k})\Sigma_{k}(A+BK_{k})^{\intercal}.

(6b)

In the sequel, and similar to
[
38
]
, we treat the moments of the intermediate states
\{\Sigma_{k},\mu_{k}\}_{k=1}^{N-1}
in the steering horizon as decision variables in the resulting optimization problem.

Similar to the dynamics, the cost function can be decoupled and written in terms of the first two moments as follows

\displaystyle J

\displaystyle=J_{\mu}(\mu_{k},v_{k})+J_{\Sigma}(\Sigma_{k},K_{k}),

(7a)

\displaystyle J_{\mu}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\left(\mu_{k}^{\intercal}Q_{k}\mu_{k}+v_{k}^{\intercal}R_{k}v_{k}\right),

(7b)

\displaystyle J_{\Sigma}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\Big(\textrm{tr}(Q_{k}\Sigma_{k})+\textrm{tr}(R_{k}K_{k}\Sigma_{k}K_{k}^{\intercal})\Big).

(7c)

Lastly, the two boundary conditions are written as

\displaystyle\mu_{0}=\mu_{i},\quad\mu_{N}=\mu_{f},

(8a)

\displaystyle\Sigma_{0}=\Sigma_{i},\quad\Sigma_{N}=\Sigma_{f}.

(8b)

Problem
1
is now recast as the following two sub-problems.

Problem 2
.

Given the mean dynamics (
6a
), find the optimal mean trajectory
\{\mu_{k}\}_{k=1}^{N-1}
and feedforward control
\{v_{k}\}_{k=0}^{N-1}
that minimize the mean cost (
7b
) subject to the boundary conditions (
8a
).

Problem 3
.

Given the covariance dynamics (
6b
), find the optimal covariance trajectory
\{\Sigma_{k}\}_{k=1}^{N-1}
and feedback gains
\{K_{k}\}_{k=0}^{N-1}
that minimize the covariance cost (
7c
) subject to the boundary conditions (
8b
).

Remark 1
.

Both the mean and covariance steering problems rely on the system matrices
A
and
B
through the system dynamics (
6
).
Thus, the problems, as stated above, are not yet amenable to a data-driven solution.

Remark 2
.

Problem
2
is a standard quadratic program with linear constraints that can be solved analytically given knowledge of the system matrices
[
28
]
.
As such, we will perform an indirect design by first estimating the
A
and
B
matrices to solve this problem in a data-driven fashion.
Problem
3
, however, is a non-linear program due to the cost term
\mathrm{tr}(RK_{k}\Sigma_{k}K_{k}^{\intercal})
and the covariance dynamics.

In the following section, we review the main concepts from behavioral systems theory that will allow us to parametrize the decision variables in Problems
2
and
3
in terms of input and output data streams.

IV
Data-Driven Parameterization

In order to incorporate data into the problem formulation, we use the concept of persistence of excitation, along with Willems’ Fundamental Lemma
[
24
]
to parametrize the feedforward and feedback gains of the control policy.
First, recall the following definitions.

Definition 1
.

Given a signal
\{z_{k}\}_{k=0}^{T-1}
where
z\in\mathbb{R}^{\sigma}
, we denote its Hankel matrix by

Z_{i,\ell,j}:=\begin{bmatrix}z_{i}&z_{i+1}&\ldots&z_{i+j-1}\\
z_{i+1}&z_{i+2}&\ldots&z_{i+j}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
z_{i+\ell-1}&z_{i+\ell}&\ldots&z_{i+\ell+j-2}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{\sigma\ell\times j},

(9)

where
i\in\mathbb{Z}
and
\ell,j\in\mathbb{N}
.
For shorthand notation, if
\ell=1
, we denote the Hankel matrix by

Z_{i,1,j}\equiv Z_{i,j}:=[z_{i}\ z_{i+1}\ \ldots\ z_{i+j-1}].

(10)

Definition 2
.

The signal
\{z_{k}\}_{k=0}^{T-1}:[0,T-1]\cap\mathbb{Z}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{\sigma}
is persistently exciting of order
\ell
if the matrix
Z_{0,\ell,j}
with
j=T-\ell+1
has rank
\sigma\ell
.

Corollary 1
.

In order for a signal to be persistently exciting of order
\ell
, it must be sufficiently long, i.e., it must hold that
T\geq(\sigma+1)\ell-1
.

Suppose we carry out an experiment of duration
T\in\mathbb{N}
where we collect input and state data
\{u_{k}\}_{k=0}^{T-1}
and
\{x_{k}\}_{k=0}^{T}
, respectively.
Let the corresponding Hankel matrices for the input sequence, state sequence, and shifted state sequence (with
\ell=1
) be

\displaystyle U_{0,T}

\displaystyle:=[u_{0}\ u_{1}\ \ldots\ u_{T-1}],

(11a)

\displaystyle X_{0,T}

\displaystyle:=[x_{0}\ x_{1}\ \ldots\ x_{T-1}],

(11b)

\displaystyle X_{1,T}

\displaystyle:=[x_{1}\ x_{2}\ \ldots\ x_{T}].

(11c)

The next result characterizes the rank of the stacked Hankel matrices of the input and output data, and is central to the method used to formulate a tractable data-driven covariance steering problem.

Lemma 1

(Willems’ Fundamental Lemma
[
24
]
)
.

Suppose that system (
1
) is controllable.
If the input signal
\{u_{k}\}_{k=0}^{T-1}
is persistently exciting of order
n+1
, then

\mathrm{rank}\begin{bmatrix}U_{0,T}\\
X_{0,T}\end{bmatrix}=n+m.

(12)

Remark 3
.

In order to ensure that the input
u_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
is persistently exciting of order
n+1
to satisfy Willems’ Fundamental Lemma, it is sufficient to check that
T\geq(m+1)n+m
.
In practice, this can always be achieved in real-time during data collection.

Lemma
1
implies that
any
arbitrary input-state sequence can be expressed as a linear combination of the collected input-state data.
Furthermore, this can be extended
[
19
]
to parameterizing any arbitrary feedback interconnection as well.
In the following section, based on the work in
[
25
]
, we parameterize the feedback gains in terms of the input-state data and reformulate the covariance steering problem as a semi-definite program (SDP).

IV-A

Direct Data-Driven Covariance Steering

Assuming the signal
\{u_{k}\}_{k=0}^{T-1}
is persistently exciting of order
n+1
, we can express the feedback gains as follows

~\begin{bmatrix}K_{k}\\
I_{n}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}U_{0,T}\\
X_{0,T}\end{bmatrix}G_{k},

(13)

where
G_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times n}
are newly defined decision variables that provide the link between the feedback gains and the input-state data.
Furthermore, we can re-write the covariance dynamics (
6b
) as

\displaystyle\Sigma_{k+1}

\displaystyle=[B\ \ A]\begin{bmatrix}K_{k}\\
I_{n}\end{bmatrix}\Sigma_{k}\begin{bmatrix}K_{k}\\
I_{n}\end{bmatrix}^{\intercal}[B\ \ A]^{\intercal}

\displaystyle=X_{1,T}G_{k}\Sigma_{k}G_{k}^{\intercal}X_{1,T}^{\intercal},

(14)

where we use the fact that
X_{1,T}=AX_{0,T}+BU_{0,T}
.
Similarly, the covariance cost (
7c
) can be re-written as

~J_{\Sigma,k}=\mathrm{tr}(Q_{k}\Sigma_{k})+\mathrm{tr}(R_{k}U_{0,T}G_{k}\Sigma_{k}G_{k}^{\intercal}U_{0,T}^{\intercal}).

(15)

To remedy the nonlinearity
G_{k}\Sigma_{k}G_{k}^{\intercal}
in the covariance dynamics and the cost, define the new decision variables
S_{k}:=G_{k}\Sigma_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times n}
, which yields the covariance dynamics

~\Sigma_{k+1}=X_{1,T}S_{k}\Sigma_{k}^{-1}S_{k}X_{1,T}^{\intercal},

(16)

and the covariance cost

~J_{\Sigma,k}=\mathrm{tr}(Q_{k}\Sigma_{k})+\mathrm{tr}(R_{k}U_{0,T}S_{k}\Sigma_{k}^{-1}S_{k}^{\intercal}U_{0,T}^{\intercal}).

(17)

This problem is still non-convex due to the nonlinear term
S_{k}\Sigma_{k}^{-1}S_{k}^{\intercal}
.
To this end, let us relax the covariance dynamics by defining a new decision variable
Y_{k}\succeq S_{k}\Sigma_{k}^{-1}S_{k}^{\intercal}
, which yields the relaxed optimization problem

\displaystyle\hskip-11.38109pt\min_{\Sigma_{k},S_{k}Y_{k}}\bar{J}_{\Sigma}=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\left(\mathrm{tr}(Q_{k}\Sigma_{k})+\mathrm{tr}(R_{k}U_{0,T}Y_{k}U_{0,T}^{\intercal})\right),

(18a)

such that, for all
k=0,\ldots,N-1
,

\displaystyle C_{k}:=S_{k}\Sigma_{k}^{-1}S_{k}^{\intercal}-Y_{k}\preceq 0,

(18b)

\displaystyle G_{k}^{(1)}:=X_{1,T}Y_{k}X_{1,T}^{\intercal}-\Sigma_{k+1}=0,

(18c)

\displaystyle G_{k}^{(2)}:=\Sigma_{k}-X_{0,T}S_{k}=0,

(18d)

with the boundary conditions (
8b
).
The last equality constraint (
18d
) comes from the second block in (
13
) by multiplying
\Sigma_{k}
on the right.
The relaxed problem (
18
) is convex, since the constraint (
18b
) can be written using the Schur complement as the linear matrix inequaltiy (LMI)

\begin{bmatrix}\Sigma_{k}&S_{k}^{\intercal}\\
S_{k}&Y_{k}\end{bmatrix}\succeq 0.

(19)

The equality constraint (
18c
) and cost (
18a
), on the other hand, are simply linear in all the decision variables, and hence convex.

) in terms of the open-loop control

, Lemma
1
also provides a system identification type of result using the following theorem.

Theorem 1
.

Suppose the signal

is persistently exciting of order

.
Then, the system (
6a
) has the following equivalent representation

\mu_{k+1}=X_{1,T}\begin{bmatrix}U_{0,T}\\
X_{0,T}\end{bmatrix}^{\dagger}\begin{bmatrix}v_{k}\\

Remark 4
.

Theorem

gives a data-based open-loop representation of a (noise-less) linear system.
One may equivalently interpret equation (
20
)
as the solution to the least-squares problem

\min_{B,A}\left\|X_{1,T}-[B\ \ A]\begin{bmatrix}U_{0,T}\\

where
\|\cdot\|_{F}
is the Frobenius norm.
Thus, equation (

Using Theorem

, we can express the mean steering problem as the following convex problem

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\min_{\mu_{k},v_{k}}J_{\mu}=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}(\mu_{k}^{\intercal}Q_{k}\mu_{k}+v_{k}^{\intercal}R_{k}v_{k}),

(22a)

such that, for all
k=0,\ldots,N-1,

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad H_{k}^{(1)}:=F_{\mu}\mu_{k}+F_{v}v_{k}-\mu_{k+1}=0,

(22b)

with the boundary conditions (

), where
F_{\mu}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}

F_{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}

V
Certainty Equivalence and Regularized Data-Driven Methods

In this section, we establish the link between the direct CS design in
IV-A
and the indirect design, as well as briefly outline a regularized design based on
[
27
]
that trade-offs the two frameworks.

V-A

Certainty-Equivalence Design

For notational simplicity, let

W_{0}:=\begin{bmatrix}U_{0,T}\\
X_{0,T}\end{bmatrix}.

In the direct data-driven covariance control design, the set of optimal solutions
G_{k}^{*}
to (
18
) coincides with the set of solutions to (
13
), that is,

\{G_{k}^{*}:(\Sigma_{k}^{*},S_{k}^{*},Y_{k}^{*})\in\mathrm{argmin}\eqref{eq:convexProblem}\}=W_{0}^{\dagger}\begin{bmatrix}K_{k}^{*}\\
I_{n}\end{bmatrix}+G_{\mathrm{hom}},

(24)

where
G_{\mathrm{hom}}
is any matrix in the null space of
W_{0}
.
Let
\Pi_{W_{0}}:=I_{T}-W_{0}^{\dagger}W_{0}
be the orthogonal projection on the nullspace of
W_{0}
.
It has been shown in
[
26
]
that by introducing the extra
orthogonality
constraint
\Pi_{W_{0}}G_{k}=0
, for all
k=1,\ldots,N
, results in an optimization problem (
18
) that is
equivalent
to the corresponding indirect design, which amounts to first performing system identification then control design on the approximate system, as outlined in Section
IV-B
.
Since
S_{k}
is a decision variable in (
18
), this amount to adding the additional equality constraints
\Pi_{W_{0}}S_{k}=0
to (
18
).
In the context of covariance steering, the indirect design is equivalent to the
bi-level
program

\displaystyle\min_{\Sigma_{k},U_{k}Y_{k}}\bar{J}_{\Sigma}=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\mathrm{tr}(Q_{k}\Sigma_{k})+\mathrm{tr}(R_{k}Y_{k}),

(25a)

such that, for all
k=0,\ldots,N-1
,

\displaystyle C_{k}^{(1)}:=\begin{bmatrix}\Sigma_{k}&U_{k}^{\intercal}\\
U_{k}&Y_{k}\end{bmatrix}\succeq 0,

(25b)

\displaystyle C_{k}^{(2)}:=\begin{bmatrix}\Sigma_{k}&U_{k}^{\intercal}\hat{B}^{\intercal}\\
\hat{B}U_{k}&\hat{\Gamma}_{k}\end{bmatrix}\succeq 0,

(25c)

\displaystyle[\hat{B}\ \ \hat{A}]=\mathrm{argmin}_{B,A}\|X_{1}-[B\ \ A]W_{0}\|_{F},

(25d)

where
\Gamma_{k}:=\Sigma_{k+1}-\hat{A}\Sigma_{k}\hat{A}^{\intercal}-\hat{A}U_{k}^{\intercal}\hat{B}^{\intercal}-\hat{B}U_{k}\hat{A}^{\intercal}
.
See Appendix B for details on this derivation, which is based on the work in
[
32
]
.

V-B

Regularized Design

By adding the constraint
\Pi_{W_{0}}S_{k}=0
to the objective function, we arrive at a regularized direct data-driven covariance steering formulation.
Letting
\lambda\geq 0
be a tunable hyperparameter that balances indirect with direct designs, the regularized problem becomes

\displaystyle\min_{\Sigma_{k},S_{k}Y_{k}}\bar{J}_{\Sigma}=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\mathrm{tr}(Q_{k}\Sigma_{k})+\mathrm{tr}(R_{k}U_{0,T}Y_{k}U_{0,T}^{\intercal})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+\lambda\|\Pi_{W_{0}}S_{k}\|,

(26a)

such that, for all
k=0,\ldots,N-1
,

\displaystyle C_{k}:=S_{k}\Sigma_{k}^{-1}S_{k}^{\intercal}-Y_{k}\preceq 0,

(26b)

\displaystyle G_{k}^{(1)}:=X_{1,T}Y_{k}X_{1,T}^{\intercal}-\Sigma_{k+1}=0,

(26c)

\displaystyle G_{k}^{(2)}:=\Sigma_{k}-X_{0,T}S_{k}=0.

(26d)

It can be shown
[
27
]
that for
\lambda
sufficiently large, the regularized design (
26
) coincides with the certainty-equivalence design.

VII
Conclusion

In this work, we have presented a tractable data-driven solution to the Louisville problem of steering the distribution of a deterministic linear system from one normal distribution to another.
The problem was solved by decoupling the dynamics into mean and covariance dynamics and then performing an indirect data-driven design for the mean motion, and a direct data-driven design for the covariance motion through a convex relaxation.
The data-driven solution matches almost exactly with its model-based counterpart.
Further work in this direction will look at how to incorporate noise into the system dynamics.
This problem is much harder to solve, as the introduction of random noise (a) cannot be measured in a real-time data-driven scenario, and (b) yields input-state data that can have multiple realizations.
