Abstract

We study nonlocal nonlinear dynamical systems and uncover the gradient structure to investigate the convergence of solutions. Mainly but not exclusively, we use the Łojasiewicz inequality to prove convergence results in various spaces with continuous, or discrete temporal domain, and finite, or infinite dimensional spatial domain. To be more specific, we analyze Lotka-Volterra type dynamics and concentration-dispersion dynamics.

Lotka-Volterra equations describe the population dynamics of a group of species, in which individuals interact either competitively or cooperatively with each other. It is well-known that Lotka-Volterra equations form a gradient system with respect to the Shahshahani metric. The Shahshahani metric, unfortunately, becomes singular in the scenario where some species become extinct. This singular nature of the Shahshahani metric is an obstacle to the usual convergence analysis. Under the assumption that the interaction between species is symmetric, we present two different methods to derive the convergence result. One, the entropy trapping method, is to adapt the idea of Akin and Hofbauer (Math. Biosci. 61 (1982) 51–62) of using monotonicity of the energy to bound the entropy, which provides the proximal distance of the solution from the desired equilibrium. Another method, inspired by Jabin and Liu’s observation in (Nonlinearity 30 (2017) 4220) is to change variables to resolve the singular nature of gradient structure. We apply this idea to show the convergence result in generalized Lokta-Volterra systems, such as regularized Lotka-Volterra systems and nonlocal semi-linear heat equations, which can be seen as an infinite dimensional Lotka-Volterra equations with mutation.

Concentration-dispersion dynamics is a new type of equation that is inspired by fixed point formulations for solitary wave shapes. The equations are designed in a way that the solution evolves to match the shape of a concentrated and dispersed version of the solution, which is an outcome of power nonlinearity and convolution.
As a continuous time analogue of Petviashvili iteration, we aim to dynamically calculate the nontrivial solitary wave profile.
We show the well-posedness and compactness of the solutions. Moreover, we suggest specific initial data which stay away from the trivial equilibrium. Using the gradient structure we deduce the existence of nontrivial solitary and periodic wave profiles. Unfortunately, the convergence result remains open. Instead, we regularize the concentration dispersion equations by adding a nonlinear diffusive term, and prove the convergence of the solutions by using the Łojasiewicz convergence framework. In this way, we can dynamically approximate nontrivial wave profiles with arbitrarily small error.

Overview

Introduction

A gradient flow is a dynamical system, where the particle follows the steepest direction to decrease a given objective function, which is often called energy. Once the trajectory of the particle is bounded, it is commonly expected that the particle will stop at a critical point of the energy. Contrary to this popular belief, however, a bounded solution of gradient flow can fail to converge, even if the associated energy is smooth.

The idea for a such nonconvergence example was suggested by Haskell B. Curry (1944), who proposed an energy resembling an infinite spiral staircase with decreasing step size. In this way, the particle can move around a circle slowly and ceaselessly. A concrete example involving so called the “Mexican hat” function, was constructed later by Palis and DeMelo (1982)
[
50
]
and Absil, Mahony and Andrews (2005)
[
1
]
. This behavior is mainly enabled by lack of any relationship between the speed of the particle and the energy level where the particle is at.

In 1965, Łojasiewicz proved an inequality for analytic energy functions, that regulates the energy level with the speed of the particle, or the slope of the energy. Using the Łojasiewicz inequality, energy dissipation can be transformed to bound the length of the trajectory, and consequently any precompact solution of a gradient system with analytic energy converges. This convergence analysis can be, in fact, applied to gradient-“like” systems where the velocity of the particle loosely follow the gradient of the associated energy, satisfying a certain angle condition.

This convergence property comes in handy in optimization, but practically what we need is appropriate discrete-time version of gradient descent in order to calculate the local minimizer.
In 2005, Absil, Mahony and Andrews
[
1
]
, as an extension of Łojasiewicz convergence theorem, showed that a discrete iteration, which satisfies the angle condition with an analytic cost function, produces a convergent sequence as long as it is bounded.
The flexibility of the angle condition presents innumerable optimization algorithms so one can investigate how to design the efficient iteration method for a given cost function. The most basic and simple method, Euler’s method, performs well when the time step is small enough.

Generally in time-discrete schemes, extra treatment, such as choosing the optimal time step, is necessary to achieve the monotonicity of the energy. Considering it is the energy that drives the dynamics, it would be more reasonable to cook up a time-discrete equation so that the energy is monotonic along the sequence, i.e., energetically stable.
Pham Dinh Tao, in 1985, introduced an energetically stable optimizing algorithm and named it the Difference of Convex functions algorithm (DCA). It is an iterative algorithm for finding a fixed point of the gradient of two convex functions of which the difference is the objective function. In fact, the family of difference of convex functions is quite extensive that it has been applied to numerous nonconvex optimization problems. It was later in 2018
[
41
]
when the convergence of the DCA was proven using Łojasiewicz inequality.
Due to the flexibility in convex splitting, DCA can in fact be comprehensive, including the classical methods in convex analysis and proximal methods
[
57
]
.

The idea of using convex inequality to formulate energetically stable time-discrete equation was also developed independently in the area of partial differential equations.
In 1998, David J. Eyre came up with an idea of representing the energy as a difference of two convex functions, to form a time-discrete Cahn-Hilliard equation and showed the produced sequence well-approximates the continuous-time solution. By treating the convex part implicitly and the concave part explicitly, this semi-implicit Euler’s method becomes energetically stable. In fact the semi-implicit Euler’s method can be seen as the special case of DCA.

A few derivations of DCA can be discussed.
Since the implicit part of DCA is a convex optimization problem, the convex dual of DCA naturally arises. It is known that the dual DCA, or the DCA on the difference of dual of convex functions, is equivalent to the primal DCA. In order to boost the speed of convergence, we can group momentum methods into two types, whether the momentum is added externally or internally. The main and original examples of external, or internal addition of momentum on gradient descent are Polyak’s momentum method, or Nesterov’s acceleration method, respectively. As an extension of those, we present the DCA with momentum, which has slightly more flexibility on the definition of momentum. From this point of view, Polyak’s momentum method and Nesterov’s acceleration method can be regarded as the same algorithm on primal and dual energy, respectively.

Subject of the thesis

Of course, not every dynamical system is a gradient system. However, it is known that if a system has a strict Lyapunov function, there exists a metric to represent the system as a gradient system
[
8
]
. So finding an appropriate Lyapunov function and a metric often provides break-through for analyzing a dynamical system. One of the revolutionary examples is Jordan-Kinderlehrer-Otto’s characterization of the Fokker-Planck equation as Wasserstein gradient flow of KL-divergence.

In this thesis we study convergence problems in nonlocal nonlinear dynamical systems of two main types: Lotka-Volterra type dynamics and concentration-dispersion dynamics.

Lotka-Volterra type dynamics

Proposed, originally, as a model for population dynamics, Lotka-Volterra represents a fundamental model of nonlinear nonlocal system. It is well known that when the interaction matrix is symmetric, a Lotka-Voltera system is a gradient flow of a quadratic energy with respect to the so-called Shahshahani metric, which provides the inverse of population as weights on each species. Along the evolution it is possible that some species die out to make the Shahshahani metric to blow up. This singular structure is an obstacle for performing gradient analysis on Lotka-Volterra systems.

When the quadratic energy is convex, it is known that the Kullback–Leibler divergence (KL divergence), relative to the unique equilibrium, is decreasing. Since KL divergence provides proximal distance from the equilibrium, the monotonicity of the KL-divergence has been used to deduce global convergence result in Lotka-Voltera type systems
[
30
]

[
6
]

[
43
]

[
33
]
.

Our goal is, without assuming convexity of the energy, to prove convergence of bounded solutions.
We provide two different ways to prove convergence of solutions as
t\to\infty
. First approach is to adapt the entropy trapping method of Akin and Hofbauer when they proved the convergence of solutions in replicator dynamics
[
2
]
. Although the KL-divergence, or the entropy, is no longer monotonic without convexity assumption, it still plays a role of distance-like function from the equilibrium. The idea is to use the monotonicity of the energy to capture the possible oscillation of KL-divergence.

Another is to de-singularize the metric. This idea was suggested by Jabin and Liu for studying Lotka-Volterra equations with mutation
[
33
]
,

\partial_{t}u(t,x)=\Delta u(t,x)+\frac{1}{2}u(t,x)\left(a(x)-\int_{\Omega}b(x,y)u^{2}(t,y)dy\right).

They studied infinite dimensional Lotka-Volterra systems with a diffusion term, which represents mutation. Especially, the suggested equation has a quadratic competition term, rather than a linear, which makes the equation as a
L^{2}
gradient flow of quartic energy in weak sense. Furthermore, they showed that one can change variables to recover the original form of Lotka-Voltera equations with linear competition.

The method of de-singularizing Shahshahani metric can be used not only in the original Lotka-Volterra dynamics, but also in regularized Lotka-Volterra type systems,

f^{\prime}_{i}=-\frac{f_{i}}{\mu+\nu f_{i}}\nabla H(f)_{i}.

As a generalization of Lotka-Volterra equation, Attouch and Teboulle suggested regularized Lotka-Volterra dynamics, which is a gradient system of a smooth energy
H
with respect to Shahshahani-type metric
[
6
]
.

In both variations of Lotka-Volterra
[
33
]
,
[
6
]
, the authors proved convergence of the solution, provided the energy is convex, using suitable KL-divergences as Lyapunov functions. We assume only that the energy satisfies the Łojasiewicz inequality to show convergence of bounded solutions by applying Łojasiewicz convergence analysis on the de-singularized structure.

We also formulate energetically stable time-discrete Lotka-Volterra type equations using convex splitting and prove convergence of iterative algorithms by applying the entropy trapping method, and by de-singularizng the metric. This relates to practical uses of the Lotka-Volterra systems. For example, Hovsepian and Anslemo
[
32
]
suggested supervised learning algorithms based on Lotka-Volterra systems, where they postulate the convergence of the solution, or make rather strong assumptions on the interaction matrix such as diagonal dominance.

Concentration-dispersion dynamics

Solitary waves, physical phenomena of waves that travels at constant speed and shape, were inexplicable by the theories of hydrodynamics at the time when they were discovered by J. Scott Russell in 1834.
The first mathematical treatment of solitary waves, the KdV equation, was founded in 1877 by Joseph Valentin Boussinesq and actively rediscovered by Diederik Korteweg and Gustav de Vries in 1895. Since then the phenomena have been noticed in various nonlinear dispersive physical models, e.g., the sine-Gordon equation, the nonlinear Schrödinger equation etc.,
[
25
]
,
[
29
]
,
[
51
]
. Now it is understood that solitary waves is created when the nonlinear and the dispersive effects are balanced, but exact analytic solutions of solitary wave are known to be challenging to compute.

In 1976, Petviashvili successfully created an algorithm that is empirically known to converge, but the mathematical verification on global convergence is still unknown.
Motivated by fixed point formulations of several kinds of solitary wave equations, we study integro-differential equations of
u(x,t):t\mapsto u(x)\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
as follows,

(1)

\left\{\begin{aligned} \;\;\frac{d}{dt}u(t,x)&=K\ast u^{p}-c(t)u\\
c(t)&=\int u^{p}K\ast u^{p}dx.\end{aligned}\right.

where
*
indicates a convolution operator and
p>1
.
The equation (
1
) is designed in a way for
u
to chase after normalized
K\ast u^{p}
. The nonlinearity
u^{p}
has an effect of concentration at peaks and the convolution by
K
has an effect of dispersion. We can expect
u
to stop evolving when the dispersive effect cancels the nonlinear concentration, just as how a solitary wave is created.

The equation (
1
), in fact, can be seen as a gradient flow on an invariant manifold of
(p+1)
-th roots of probability densities with respect to the metric weighted by
u^{p-1}
, and the associated energy is a distance between
K\ast u^{p}
and
u
given by the metric.
On the other hand, if we initiate the evolution outside of the manifold, the associated energy can lose the monotonicity. It turns out we can stabilize the energy by multiplying it by an exponential factor related to the manifold. With the new energy, the dynamics is a gradient flow in the flat whole space, which makes analysis far easier.

Unlike usual dispersive effects in PDE does, the convolution does not provide smoothing in time. For example, if the initial data has a singularity or a spike, the solution at any time will have the same. However, we can show that the difference between the solution and scaled initial data is smoother than the initial data. So the solution is precompact in
H^{1}
on compact domains. On the real line, we show that the solution initiated from “bell-shaped” initial data is precompact in
L^{2}
.

Recall that we aim to approximate nontrivial wave profiles. Even if we prove the dynamics converges, it will be meaningless if every solution converges to trivial solutions, i.e., constant solutions. We show that the constant equilibria are unstable. Moreover, we can show that the peak of the solution stays strictly away from
0.

Thus, by LaSalle’s invariance principle type argument, we deduce the existence of nontrivial equilibria of (
1
).

Despite the
H^{1}
-analyticity of the energy, unfortunately, Łojasiewicz convergence framework cannot be applied to (
1
). To resolve the issue, we regularize the concentration-dispersion dynamics as follows.

(2)

\left\{\begin{aligned} \;\;\frac{d}{dt}u(t,x)&=\epsilon(u^{p})_{xx}+K\ast u^{p}-c(t)u\\
c(t)&=\int u^{p}K\ast u^{p}-\epsilon[(u^{p})_{x}]^{2}dx.\end{aligned}\right.

Indeed, the equation (
2
) also has nice
L^{2}
-gradient structure, and furthermore, we prove that every solution of (
2
) on compact domains converges. Also by showing the instability of the constant solution, we can approximate nontrivial periodic waves with arbitrarily small error
\epsilon>0
.

Plan of the thesis

This thesis comprises three parts. Part 1 is an overview for the Łojaisewicz convergence theorem on time-continuous (Chapter 1) and time-discrete (Chapter 2) gradient system. We discuss adaptation of DC algorithm, originated from optimization algorithm, to a time-discrete differential equation in Chapter 2.2. Additionally we provide novel perspectives on the Łojaisiewicz framework on infinite dimensional space (Chapter 1.2) and DC algorithms with momentum (Chapter 2.3).

Part 2 is dedicated to convergence analysis on Lotka-Volterra type systems. We provide two different approaches to prove convergence for the continuous time equations (Chapter 3) as well as the discrete time equations (Chapter 4). Entropy trapping method can be found in Chapter 3.1 and Chapter 4.1. The convergence analysis using de-singularized gradient structure can be found in Chapter 3.2 and Chapter 4.2. As derivations of Lotka-Volterra systems, Lotka-Volterra systems with mutation are treated in Chapter 3.3 and time-discrete regularized Lotka-Volterra equations are treated in Chapter 4.3.

In Part 3, we introduce a new type of integro-differential equations, namely concentration-dispersion dynamics. We discuss the motivation and the gradient structure of the concentration-dispersion equations in Chapter 5. In Chapter 6, we perform analysis to show well-posedness, compactness of solutions, and existence of nontrivial wave profiles. In Chapter 7, we study the regularized concentration-dispersion dynamics and prove convergence of the solutions to nontrivial wave profiles.

Chapter 1
Introduction to gradient flows

In this thesis, we reveal gradient structures for various types of nonlinear and nonlocal dynamics to establish convergence of solutions in continuous-time and discrete-time, without assuming convexity of the associated energy. Our main tool will be the Łojasiewicz inequality for analytic cost functions. In this chapter, together with a brief history, we will go over how the Łojasiewicz inequality is used to prove convergence of solutions of gradient-like systems on finite and infinite dimensional domains.

For brevity, we let the time differentiation

1.2.2.
Łojasiewicz convergence scheme for gradient-like systems

We present the Łojasiewicz convergence theorem in a generalized framework. More specifically, let
\mathcal{V}
,
\mathcal{H}
be two real Hilbert spaces such that

\mathcal{V}\subset\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{V}^{\prime}

with continuous dense embedding and
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
, the topological dual of
\mathcal{H}
is identified with
\mathcal{H}

Definition 1.3
.

Let

with the energy

(1.5)

\langle-\nabla{E}(u),u^{\prime}\rangle_{\mathcal{V}^{\prime}\times\mathcal{V}}\geq\sigma\|\nabla{E}(u)\|_{\mathcal{V}^{\prime}}\|u^{\prime}\|_{\mathcal{H}}.

Note that the angle condition (
1.5
) is, in fact, purely geometric, which is invariant under a change of positive time scale. So the angle condition is not sufficient to determine an explicit rate of convergence.

Definition 1.4
.

Let

. We say that

satisfies the
rate condition
with the energy

(1.6)

\|u^{\prime}\|_{\mathcal{H}}\geq\gamma\|\nabla{E}(u)\|_{\mathcal{V}^{\prime}}.

In usual case of gradient-like systems, we have the

(1.7)

-\frac{d}{dt}E(u(t))=\langle-\nabla{E}(u),u^{\prime}\rangle_{\mathcal{V}^{\prime}\times\mathcal{V}}\geq c\|u^{\prime}\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}.

Evidently, the rate condition (
1.6
) together with the dissipation inequality (
1.7
) provides the angle condition.

Remark 1.1
.

Note that Definition
1.3
is a generalization of the gradient system of
E\in C^{1}(\mathcal{V},\mathbb{R})

\frac{d}{dt}u(t)=-\nabla{E}(u(t)).

Assume that

is a solution. This means that for any

, the solution satisfies the following,

\langle u^{\prime},\phi\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=-\langle\nabla{E}(u),\phi\rangle_{\mathcal{V}^{\prime}\times\mathcal{V}}.

The rate condition is naturally satisfied and from that we can check the angle condition as well.

[Rate condition]

\|\nabla{E}(u)\|_{\mathcal{V}^{\prime}}=\sup_{\|\phi\|_{\mathcal{V}}=1}\langle\nabla{E}(u),\phi\rangle_{\mathcal{V}^{\prime}\times\mathcal{V}}=\sup_{\|\phi\|_{\mathcal{V}}=1}-\langle u^{\prime},\phi\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\leq\|u^{\prime}\|_{\mathcal{H}}.

\langle-\nabla{E}(u),u^{\prime}\rangle_{\mathcal{V}^{\prime}\times\mathcal{V}}=\|u^{\prime}\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}\geq\|\nabla{E}(u)\|_{\mathcal{V}^{\prime}}\|u^{\prime}\|_{\mathcal{H}}.

Chapter 2
Discrete-time gradient descent by convex splitting

Gradient descent is a widely used technique in optimization, since it steers the solution to optimize the objective function, or the energy.

In
[
1
]
, Absil, Mahony and Andrews successfully extended the Łojasewicz convergence theorem to sequences that satisfies
the strong descent condition
, which assures the produced sequence to behave as similar to a solution of gradient flow. Needless to say, the energy must be monotone along the sequence, however, in general, time discretization scheme does not provide the monotonicity of the energy for free without proper choice of time step, or localization, which might require expensive calculation.

Mainly, we discuss an optimization algorithm using the class of functions that have a representation as a
difference of convex functions
(DC). Widely used in non-convex optimization, DC algorithms, suggested by Tao
[
58
]
in 1986, treat the gradient of concave part explicitly and the gradient of convex part implicitly. Combining basic convex inequalities on each convex and concave part provides monotonicity of the energy. Independently, in the area of PDE, Eyre
[
20
]
used the same idea to formalize energetically stable time-discretization scheme for partial differential equations, such as Allen-Cahn equations, Cahn-Hilliard equations, etc. Indeed, Eyre’s semi-implicit Euler’s method can be regarded as a special case of DCA.
When the methods are applied to analytic cost functions, by Absil
et al
.’s convergence theorem, every bounded sequence converges
[
18
]
.

Lastly, we provide a simple, but novel, observation relating to methods of acceleration for DC algorithms. We describe a class of DC algorithms with momentum, which adds a (possibly non-convex) Bregman divergence centered at the previous point to the concave part of the DC algorithm. In this framework, Polyak’s heavy ball method and Nesterov’s acceleration method can be regarded as the same momentum method, but based on objective functions that are “dual” to each other.

2.1.
Convergence analysis of discrete schemes

In
[
1
]
, Absil
et al
. introduced the Łojasiewicz inequality to prove convergence of any bounded sequence driven by an analytic energy under the following

.

Definition 2.1
.

A sequence
\{u^{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}

if the following conditions are satisfied for

[Primary descent condition]

H(u^{k})-H(u^{k+1})\geq\sigma\|\nabla H(u^{k})\|\|u^{k+1}-u^{k}\|.

[Complementary descent condition]

[H(u^{k+1})=H(u^{k})]\Rightarrow[u^{k+1}=u^{k}].

The result is abstract in order to be applicable to various iterations, without specifying the connection between the sequence and the gradient. As a quantitative result, they showed the

-tail of the sequence is bounded by the decay of the energy.

Theorem 2.1

(Absil
et al

[
1
]
)

Let

\tilde{u}
.
Moreover,

- tail of
\{u^{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
can be estimated by the decay of

\sum_{k\geq n}\|u^{k+1}-u^{k}\|\leq\frac{1}{c\sigma\theta}H^{\theta}_{n}.

where

are Łojasiewicz constant and exponent at

, respectively.

Remark 2.1
.

Theorem
2.1
does not tell the explicit rate of convergence. Note that primary descent condition is a discrete-time analogue of the angle condition between the gradient and the velocity, i.e.,

\frac{d}{dt}H(u(t))=\langle\nabla H(u(t)),u^{\prime}(t)\rangle\geq\sigma\|\nabla H(u(t))\|\|u^{\prime}(t)\|.

The angle condition is, in fact, purely geometric, which is invariant under the choice of time scale. Therefore, the strong descent condition is not sufficient to produce explicit rate of convergence. For any
\{u^{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
satisfying strong descent condition, one can cook up super-, or subsequences that satisfy the same strong descent condition but have different rate of convergence.

In order to calculate quantitatively the rate of convergence, we add a condition which an ordinary “gradient-motivated” scheme likely satisfies.

Definition 2.2

A sequence

if for

\|u^{k+1}-u^{k}\|\geq\gamma\|\nabla H(u^{k})\|.

Note that the rate condition prevents the repetition of a sequence except at a critical point.

Corollary 2.1
.

In addition to the assumption in Theorem

\theta

(2.1)

(H_{0}^{2\theta-1}+(1-2\theta)an)^{\frac{1}{2\theta-1}},&\text{if}\;\;0<\theta<\frac{1}{2}.\end{array}\right.

Proof.

Note that the convergence rate of

depends on the decay rate of

. Combining primary descent condition and the ordinary descent condition gives estimate on the energy gap,

\displaystyle H_{n+1}-H_{n}

\displaystyle\leq-{\sigma}{\gamma}\|\nabla H_{n}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq-{\sigma}{\gamma}c^{2}H_{n}^{2-2\theta}.

Let

So,

\displaystyle{\sigma}{\gamma}c^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{H_{k+1}}^{H_{k}}H^{2\theta-2}_{k}dh

\displaystyle\leq\int_{H_{k+1}}^{H_{k}}h^{2\theta-2}dh.

If

\displaystyle H_{n}

\displaystyle\leq H_{0}e^{-{\sigma}{\gamma}c^{2}n}.

2.2.
Convergence of difference of convex functions algorithm

Our focus is to show convergence of gradient flows without assuming convexity of the energy. As the backbone of nonconvex programming and global optimization, the difference of convex algorithm, or DCA, uses the following class of functions.

Definition 2.3
.

We say
f
is a
difference of convex functions
(DC) if there are convex functions
g
and
h
such that
f=g-h
, and the pair
(g,h)
, is a
convex splitting
of
f
.

The family of DC functions is quite general. It is known that the set of DC functions is dense in the set of continuous functions on a compact convex domain in
\mathbb{R}^{N}.

Moreover, for a fixed
f
, choice of a convex splitting pair is flexible. The set of convex splitting is convex, since a convex combination of any two
(g_{1},h_{1})
and
(g_{2},h_{2})
convex splitting is again convex splitting for
f
. Also for any convex function
g^{\prime}
,
(g+g^{\prime},h+g^{\prime})
is again a convex splitting of
f
.

In this context, finding a critical point of
f
can be written as solving the following fixed point problem using convex splitting of
f
,

\nabla g(x)=\nabla h(x).

The analogous optimization scheme was introduced by Pham Dinh Tao
[
58
]
in 1986, and the iteration is named
DC algorithm
. The history of DCA is well reviewed in
[
39
]
.
Let
H=H_{+}-H_{-}
be a cost function with the convex splitting
H_{+}
and
H_{-}.

(2.2)

\nabla H_{+}(u^{n+1})=\nabla H_{-}(u^{n})

(
2.2
) is called DC Algorithm (Difference of Convex functions). Due to the implicit part of the algorithm, we need to make sure
\nabla H_{+}
is bijective.

Definition 2.4
.

Let
\kappa\in\mathbb{R}
, we say
f:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
is
\kappa

f(x)-\frac{\kappa}{2}\|x\|^{2}
is convex. Moreover, if
\kappa>0
we say

is strongly convex.

Definition 2.5
.

We say

is

f
has Lipschitz derivative with constant
L>0.

Throughout the thesis, we assume
H_{+}
is strongly convex, so that the iteration (
2.2
) is well-defined. Indeed, if
H_{+}
is strongly convex, there exists unique

for any

such that

\nabla H_{+}(u^{*})=v,

since
u\mapsto H_{+}(u)-v^{T}u
has a unique minimizer due to the strong convexity.

The main benefit of DCA is that DCA is energetically stable, in other words, the energy
H
is monotonic along the sequence. It is directly deduced from the following basic convex inequality.

Lemma 2.1

(Convex inequality)
.

For any convex
g\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R})
and for any
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
,

g^{\prime}(a)(b-a)\leq g(b)-g(a)\leq g^{\prime}(b)(b-a).

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
H=H_{+}-H_{-}
such that
H_{+}
is
\kappa
-convex and
H_{-}
is

\{u^{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
be a sequence from DCA (

\kappa+\mu>0

H(u^{n+1})\leq H(u^{n}).

Proof.

We apply Lemma
2.1
to

H_{+}(u)-\frac{\kappa}{2}\|u\|^{2}
and
H_{-}(u)-\frac{\mu}{2}\|u\|^{2}
so that

\displaystyle H_{+}(u^{n+1})-H_{+}(u^{n})

\displaystyle\leq\langle\nabla H_{+}(u^{n+1}),u^{n+1}-u^{n}\rangle-\frac{\kappa}{2}\|u^{n+1}-u^{n}\|^{2},

\displaystyle H_{-}(u^{n+1})-H_{-}(u^{n})

\displaystyle\geq\langle\nabla H_{-}(u^{n}),u^{n+1}-u^{n}\rangle+\frac{\mu}{2}\|u^{n+1}-u^{n}\|^{2}.

By subtracting those we check the monotonicity of
H
along the sequence.

(2.3)

H(u^{n+1})-H(u^{n})\leq-\frac{\kappa+\mu}{2}\|u^{n+1}-u^{n}\|^{2}.

We check the strong descent condition and the rate condition. The complementary descent condition follows directly from the monotonicity of

Remark 2.2
.

As in (

preconditioning. Considering the flexibility of convex splitting, it is important to choose proper convex splitting of the energy in order to make the algorithm efficient.
A question arises: how can we split

in order to achieve optimal convergence rate? For given convex splitting

and optimize

is well rounded in a sense that

, making

flatter might provide faster convergence.

Due to the flexibility of the convex splitting, (

Example 2.1

The simplest example is Euler’s method,

It is worth mentioning that the idea of convex splitting to stabilize the energy also arises independently in the area of partial differential equations.

2.3.
Duality of Polyak’s and Nesterov’s momentum method

In this section, we discuss a few variants of DC algorithms. Firstly,
due to the implicit part of the algorithm, each step of DC algorithm requires to solve a convex problem, inheriting the dual DC algorithm. In this section we will go over the equivalence of primal and dual DC algorithms.

In order to accelerate the rate of convergence, Polyak firstly developed the Heavy ball method (1964) which boosts iterates in the direction of the previous increment, like adding a “momentum”. Another successful and celebrated example, Nesterov’s acceleration method (1983), rather takes different approach. Comparing to external addition of momentum in the Heavy ball method, Nesterov’s acceleration applies momentum internally. Since then, numerous acceleration methods have been adapting either Polyak’s approach or Nesterov’s approach.
With the perspective of the primal and dual DC in mind, however, we can interpret Polyak’s Heavy ball method and Nesterov’s acceleration method as two examples of a general class of DC algorithms with momentum, applied to energies that are dual to each other. In this way, we provide a convergence proof that covers both types of method.

Dual DC algorithms

Inverting a gradient, when enough regularity is presumed, can be interpreted as a maximization problem,

\nabla f(x)=p\Longleftrightarrow\arg\!\max_{x}\{p^{T}x-f(x)\}\Longleftrightarrow\nabla f^{*}(p)=x,

where
f^{*}(p):=\sup_{x}\{p^{T}x-f(x)\}
is the
Legendre transform
of
f(x)
.
So by solving the implicit part of the DC algorithm, the dual DC algorithm naturally arise. Let
p^{n}:=\nabla H_{+}(u^{n})=\nabla H_{-}(u^{n-1})
, then we can write down the iteration of
p^{n}

\displaystyle p^{n+1}

\displaystyle=\nabla H_{-}(u^{n})

\displaystyle=\nabla H_{-}(\nabla H_{+}^{*}(p^{n})).

This leads us to the dual DC algorithm.

[Dual DC Algorithm]

\nabla H^{*}_{-}(p^{n+1})=\nabla H^{*}_{+}(p^{n}).

\nabla H_{+}
has to be easily invertible.

The dual DC algorithm aims to optimize the dual energy,
H_{-}^{*}-H_{+}^{*}
, not to be confused with the convex dual of the energy.
Indeed, we can check the equivalence between the primal and dual DC algorithms
[
57
]
,

\displaystyle\inf_{x}\{H_{+}(x)-H_{-}(x)\}

\displaystyle=\inf_{x}\{H_{+}(x)-\sup_{p}\{p^{T}x-H^{*}_{-}(p)\}\}

\displaystyle=\inf_{x,p}\{H_{+}(x)-p^{T}x+H^{*}_{-}(p)\}

\displaystyle=\inf_{p}\{H^{*}_{-}(p)-H^{*}_{+}(p)\}.

It is known that assuming
f
is strongly convex and analytic is enough to assure the Łojasiewicz inequality of
f^{*}
(Proposition 2.1
[
40
]
). So when
H_{+}
and
H_{-}
are strongly convex and analytic, we can apply Łojasiewicz convergence theorem to the dual DC. Later, we will use this to prove convergence of DC algorithm with momentum which includes Polyak’s momentum method and Nesterov’s acceleration algorithm.

Remark 2.3
.

A funny thing happens when
H_{+}=H_{-}^{*}
, i.e., if there exists
\Phi
such that
H=\Phi^{*}-\Phi
. Then the DC algorithm is given as follows,

\displaystyle\nabla\Phi^{*}(u^{n+1})=\nabla\Phi(u^{n}),

\displaystyle u^{n+1}=\nabla\Phi\circ\nabla\Phi(u^{n}).

In this particular case, minimizing
H
is equivalent to finding a fixed point of
\nabla\Phi^{(2)}
, in other word finding
x,y
such that
(\nabla\Phi(x)=y)\wedge(\nabla\Phi(y)=x)
.
Suppose that
H\in C^{2}
, then the Hessian of
\Phi
satisfies the following,

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}H

\displaystyle=\nabla^{2}\Phi^{*}-\nabla^{2}\Phi

\displaystyle=(\nabla^{2}\Phi)^{-1}-\nabla^{2}\Phi.

The solution is uniquely determined by the Hessian of
H
, i.e.,
\nabla^{2}\Phi=\frac{1}{2}(S-\nabla^{2}H\big)>0
, where
S
is a positive definite matrix satisfying
S^{2}=(\nabla^{2}H)^{2}+4I
.

DC algorithms with momentum

Now consider DC algorithms for the energy
H
modified by
\beta
-smooth “momentum”
b:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
centered at a point
v\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
,

(2.6)

~\tilde{H}(u):=H(u)-b(u)+b(v)+\langle\nabla b(v),u\rangle.

Here we do not necessarily assume
b
is convex. When
b
is convex, however, one can notice that (
2.6
) is exactly

\displaystyle\tilde{H}(u)=H(u)-D_{b}(u,v),

where
D_{b}(u,v)
is so-called Bregman divergence, which can be considered as adding repulsive potential pushing away from
v
. Treating the added momentum as a concave part of
H
, we have a convex splitting of
\tilde{H}
as follows,

\displaystyle\tilde{H}(u)

\displaystyle=H_{+}(u)-(H_{-}(u)+D_{b}(u,v))

\displaystyle=\tilde{H}_{+}(u)-\tilde{H}_{-}(u).

At each step, let’s fix
v
as the previous point,
u^{n-1}
. Then the corresponding DC algorithm on
\tilde{H}
motivates the following class of DC algorithms with momentum on
H
,

[DC algorithms with momentum]

(2.7)

\nabla H_{+}(u^{n+1})=\nabla H_{-}(u^{n})+\nabla b(u^{n})-\nabla b(u^{n-1}).

Due to the flexibility of convex splitting and choosing the momentum
b
, the DC algorithm with momentum can be quite comprehensive.

Example 2.3

(Polyak’s heavy ball method)
.

u^{n+1}=u^{n}-\tau\nabla H(u^{n})+\beta(u^{n}-u^{n-1}),

which is choosing
H_{+}(u)=\frac{1}{2}\|u\|^{2}
,
H_{-}(u)=\frac{1}{2}\|u\|^{2}-\tau H(u)
and
b(u)=\beta\|u\|^{2}/2
with
\tau<1/L
where
L
is Lipschtiz constant of
\nabla H
.

\nabla H_{+}(u^{n+1})=\nabla H_{-}(u^{n})+\beta(u^{n}-u^{n-1}).

Example 2.4

(Adaptive momentum algorithm from Bianchi & Barakat
[
7
]
)
.

\displaystyle x^{n+1}

\displaystyle=x^{n}-a_{n+1}p^{n+1}

\displaystyle p^{n+1}

\displaystyle=p^{n}+b(\nabla f(x^{n})-p^{n}).

Rearranging the equation with respect to
x
provides the following algorithm,

x^{n+1}=x^{n}-a_{n+1}b\nabla f(x^{n})+\frac{a_{n+1}(1-b)}{a_{n}}(x^{n}-x^{n-1}).

This is equivalent to letting
\tau
and
\beta
be adaptive in Polyak’s heavy ball method.

Example 2.5

(Nesterov’s acceleration method)
.

Note that for given convex splitting
(H_{+},H_{-})
of
H
, the dual energy of
H
is
H_{-}^{*}-H_{+}^{*}
. Let
H_{+}(u)=\frac{1}{2}\|u\|^{2}
,
H_{-}(u)=\frac{1}{2}\|u\|^{2}-\tau H(u)
with small enough
\tau<1/L
and consider adding
b(u)=\beta\|u\|^{2}/2
at the dual dc problem as follows,

\displaystyle\nabla H^{*}_{-}(p^{n+1})

\displaystyle=\nabla H^{*}_{+}(p^{n})+\beta(p^{n}-p^{n-1}).

Since
p^{n}=\nabla H_{+}(u^{n})=u^{n}
,

p^{n+1}=\nabla H_{-}\big(u^{n}+\beta(u^{n}-u^{n-1})\big).

This corresponds to the Nesterov’s acceleration method,

u^{n+1}=u^{n}+\beta(u^{n}-u^{n-1})-\tau\nabla H(u^{n}+\beta(u^{n}-u^{n-1})).

Thus, we can consider the Nesterov’s acceleration method, as the DC algorithm with momentum on the dual energy of
H
.

In

Example 2.7

(iPiano)

In

[

They came up with inertial proximal algorithm for non-convex optimization (iPiano) of the following form,

This can be considered as the DC algorithm with momentum with the convex splitting,

\displaystyle f(x)+g(x)

\displaystyle=(g(x)+\frac{1}{2\alpha}\|x\|^{2})-(\frac{1}{2\alpha}\|x\|^{2}-f(x))

Note that

Part II
Lotka-Volterra type dynamics

Chapter 3
Convergence of Lotka-Volterra dynamics with symmetric interaction matrix

Comprising a family of classic and prototypical models
in population ecology, Lotka-Volterra systems have nonlocal nonlinear structure in a fairly amenable way to mathematical analysis.
Let
\mathbb{R}^{N}_{+}:=\{f\in\mathbb{R}^{N}:f_{i}>0,\;\forall i\in[N]\}
, where
N\in\mathbb{N}
and
[N]:=\{1,2,...,N\}
. For
a,d\in\mathbb{R}^{N}_{+}
and
B\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
the differential equations of the
Lotka-Volterra system
are given by

(3.1)

f^{\prime}_{i}=d_{i}f_{i}(a_{i}-\sum_{j\in[N]}B_{ij}f_{j}),\quad\forall i\in[N].

In the ecosystem of
N
-many species, the population of the
i
-th species,
f_{i}
, is determined by its own growth rate
a_{i}
and the influence, whether inhibiting or cooperating, of the
j
-th species over the
i
-th species,
B_{ij}
.

There are two important functions, we name them energy and entropy, that are widely used to analyze Lotka-Voltera system.

Monotonic energy

When the interaction
B
is symmetric, the energy
E:\mathbb{R}^{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
,

E(f):=-H:=\sum_{i\in[N]}f_{i}a_{i}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j\in[N]}B_{ij}f_{i}f_{j},

is a Lyapunov functional of (
3.1
),

\frac{dE}{dt}=\sum_{i\in[N]}d_{i}f_{i}(a_{i}-\sum_{j\in[N]}B_{ij}f_{j})^{2}=\sum_{i\in[N]}\frac{f^{\prime}_{i}(t)^{2}}{d_{i}f_{i}}\geq 0\,.

Indeed, the Lotka-Volterra system is a gradient flow of
E
with respect to the metric which is named after Shahshahani,

\langle g,h\rangle_{f}:=\sum_{i\in[N]}g_{i}h_{i}f_{i}^{-1}.

To see this let’s take the variation of
E,

\displaystyle DE(f)(g)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in[N]}g_{i}a_{i}-g_{i}(Bf)_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in[N]}f_{i}(a_{i}-(Bf)_{i})g_{i}f^{-1}_{i}.

\displaystyle=:\langle\text{grad}E(f),g\rangle_{f}.

As we have seen in the previous chapter, Łojasiewicz convergence analysis requires an “angle condition” between
\nabla E(f)
and
f^{\prime}
, i.e., there is
\sigma>0
such that

\langle\nabla E(f),f^{\prime}\rangle\geq\sigma\|\nabla E(f)\|\|f^{\prime}\|.

In general, since there can be a species that is vanishing along the evolution, the Shahshahani metric can blow up. Due to this singular structure, it is difficult use the Shahshahani gradient structure to prove convergence of the solutions.

Proximal entropy

The entropy
F:\mathbb{R}^{N}_{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
relative to a given state
\tilde{f}\in\overline{\mathbb{R}^{N}_{+}}
with a weight
w_{i}>0
, given by

Proof.

\displaystyle\text{for all}\;\;j\in J,\qquad

\displaystyle\frac{\partial F}{\partial f_{j}}=w_{j}(1-\frac{\tilde{f}_{j}}{f_{j}}),\qquad\frac{\partial^{2}F}{\partial f_{j}^{2}}=w_{j}\frac{\tilde{f}_{j}}{f_{j}^{2}}>0,

\displaystyle\text{for all}\;\;j\notin J,\qquad

\displaystyle\frac{\partial F}{\partial f_{j}}=w_{j}.

Each term in

3.1.
Entropy trapping method

Our goal here is establishing the convergence of every trajectory of (

In particular, the key is to show that for some

Our main result on convergence is as follows.
Recall that the

Theorem 3.1
.

Assume

is a symmetric matrix. If

Proof.

Fix

and the weight on the entropy

. Define,

\displaystyle K

\displaystyle:=\{i\in[N]\;:\;a_{i}-\sum_{j}B_{ij}\tilde{f}_{j}\neq 0\},

\displaystyle Q(f)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{i\in K}f_{i}

\displaystyle:=\min_{i\in K}\{d_{i}(a_{i}-\sum_{j}B_{ij}f_{j})^{2}\}.

and

(3.2)

\frac{dE}{dt}=\sum_{i}d_{i}f_{i}(a_{i}-\sum_{j}B_{ij}f_{j})^{2}\geq Z(f)Q(f).

On the other hand,

\displaystyle\frac{dF}{dt}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}(f_{i}-\tilde{f}_{i})(a_{i}-\sum_{j}B_{ij}f_{j})

\displaystyle=2(\sum_{i}f_{i}a_{i}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j}f_{i}B_{ij}f_{j})-\sum_{i}f_{i}a_{i}-\sum_{i}\tilde{f}_{i}a_{i}+\sum_{i,j}\tilde{f}_{i}B_{ij}f_{j}

Now choose a sequence of times

approaching infinity so that

For

\displaystyle F(f(t_{N}))<\frac{\epsilon}{2}

Note that as long as a solution path stays inside the

So let

Then by integration and (

Chapter 4
Convergence of discrete-time Lotka-Volterra dynamics

In this chapter we form the time-discrete Lotka-Volterra equations inspired by DC algorithms, or convex splitting (Chapter
2
). We provide two different analysis, one follows the Shahshahani gradient and another follows regular Euclidean gradient constructed by change of variables. Accordingly, we prove convergence with different strategies for each method, without convexity of energy: the entropy trapping method and the Łojasiewicz theorem, respectively.

As we discussed in the continuous-time case, the singular nature of the Shahshahani metric makes convergence analysis challenging. So we prove the convergence by relating the energy with the entropy to show that the solution is trapped in the neighborhood of the equilibrium. Due to the time discrepancy caused by the discrete time steps, however, the KL-divergence that we used as the entropy, does not work in the same way. We add a quadratic term to resolve the issue. With the new entropy, we proceed with the entropy trapping method to prove convergence of every solution of competitive Lotka-Volterra systems. In fact, the time-discrete Lotka-Volterra equation with the Shahshahani metric can be seen as Euler’s method for regularized Lotka-Volterra equation of the form (
3.9
). This can be considered as an extension of the convergence theorem that is presented in
[
6
]
, where Attouch and Teboulle assume the convexity of the energy to prove convergence.

On the other hand, adapting the observation on changing variables presented by Jabin and Liu
[
33
]
, we, again, resolve the singularities of Shahshahani metric and apply the Łojasiewicz convergence theorem by Absil
et al
.
[
1
]
to the Lotka-Volterra system. We suggest a certain way to convex-split the energy, with which the implicit part of the algorithm is not too complex to invert, yet the solution stays in the positive orthant
\mathbb{R}^{N}_{+}
regardless of the time step
\tau>0.
Thus the convergence and the positivity of the solutions are independent of the time step
\tau
.

, where
A=\lambda-B
is a positive definite matrix. We split
H(f)
as follows,

\displaystyle H(f)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}f^{T}Bf-a^{T}f

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}f^{T}\lambda f-(\frac{1}{2}f^{T}Af+a^{T}f)

\displaystyle=:H_{+}(f)-H_{-}(f).

Now we evaluate the gradient of
H_{\pm}
with respect to Shahshahani metric at
f^{n}
, and apply semi-implicit Euler’s method as follows,

\displaystyle(f^{n+1}-f^{n})_{i}

\displaystyle=-\tau d_{i}(\text{grad}_{f^{n}}H_{+}(f^{n+1})-\text{grad}_{f^{n}}H_{-}(f^{n}))

(4.1)

\displaystyle=-\tau d_{i}f^{n}_{i}(\nabla H_{+}(f^{n+1})-\nabla H_{-}(f^{n}))_{i}

(4.2)

\displaystyle=\tau d_{i}f^{n}_{i}(a-Bf^{n}-\lambda(f^{n+1}-f^{n}))_{i}

Our goal here is to ensure the dynamics of (
4.2
) to be similar with its continuous version as much as possible. Firstly, we need to make sure the sequence
\{f^{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}_{+}
with appropriate choice of a time step
\tau
. It turns out when the interaction is competitive, i.e.,
B_{ij}>0
,
we will have the desired properties as follows.

Proposition 4.1
.

Assume that
\underline{B}:=\min_{i,j}B_{ij}>0
. For any
\epsilon>0
, define

\Omega_{\epsilon}:=\{f\in\mathbb{R}^{N}_{+}:\|f\|_{\infty}\leq(\lambda^{-1}+\underline{B}^{-1})\|a\|_{\infty}+\epsilon\},

then there exists
M_{\epsilon}>0
, such that for
0<\tau<\frac{1}{\|d\|_{\infty}M_{\epsilon}}
the sequence
\{f^{n}\}
generated by the discrete scheme (
4.2
) with initial condition
f^{0}\in\Omega_{\epsilon}
has properties as follows:

•

(Feasibility)
f^{n}\in\Omega_{\epsilon}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

•

(Ratio bound)

1-\tau\|d\|_{\infty}M_{\epsilon}\leq\frac{f^{n+1}}{f^{n}}\leq 1+\tau\|d\|_{\infty}M_{\epsilon}

•

(Monotonicity) E is non-decreasing along (
4.2
), i.e., for all
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}

E(f^{n+1})-E(f^{n})\geq 0,

and the equality holds if and only if
f^{n}_{i}=0
or
a_{i}=(Bf^{n})_{i}
for all
i
,

•

(
l^{2}
-bound)

\sum_{n=k}^{\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{\tau d_{i}f^{n}_{i}}(f^{n+1}-f^{n})^{2}_{i}\leq\lim_{n\to\infty}E(f^{n})-E(f^{k}).

Note that the restriction on
\tau
is to satisfy the natural constraint on Lotka-Volterra system to make the solution
\{f^{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
stay in the positive orthant
\mathbb{R}^{N}_{+}
. Meanwhile, the monotonicity and
l^{2}
-bound holds regardless of
\tau
, due to the convex splitting.

We use these properties to prove convergence of bounded solutions using the entropy trapping method. The proof follows by lemmas with more detail.

Lemma 4.1

(Feasibility)
.

For any
\epsilon>0
, define

M_{\epsilon}:=\max_{f\in\Omega_{\epsilon}}\|a-Bf\|_{\infty}.

Then
\Omega_{\epsilon}
is an invariant set of (
4.2
) when
0<\tau<\frac{1}{\|d\|_{\infty}M_{\epsilon}}
.

Proof.

Essentially, we need to show that the sequence
\{f^{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
stays positive and bounded.
Let’s rewrite (
4.2
) as follows

(4.3)

(1+\tau\lambda d_{i}f^{n}_{i})(f^{n+1}-f^{n})_{i}=\tau d_{i}f^{n}_{i}(a-Bf^{n})_{i},

(4.4)

f^{n+1}_{i}=f^{n}_{i}\Big(\frac{1+\tau\lambda d_{i}f^{n}_{i}+\tau d_{i}(a-Bf^{n})_{i}}{1+\tau\lambda d_{i}f^{n}_{i}}\Big).

[Positivity]
According to (
4.4
), given that
f^{n}>0
,
1+\tau d_{i}(a-Bf^{n})_{i}>0
will make
f^{n+1}>0
. This will allow us to control the numerator when
f^{n}_{i}
is negligible but
(a-Bf^{n})_{i}
is possibly negatively large.
Since we chose
\tau<\frac{1}{\|d\|_{\infty}M_{\epsilon}}
, for all
i
,

(4.5)

\displaystyle 1+\tau d_{i}(a-Bf^{n})_{i}

\displaystyle\geq 1-\tau\|d\|_{\infty}M_{\epsilon}>0.

[Boundedness]
For any
f\in\Upsilon:=\{f\in\mathbb{R}^{N}_{+}:\|f\|_{\infty}>\frac{\|a\|_{\infty}}{\underline{B}}\}
and for all
i\in[N]

\displaystyle(a-Bf)_{i}

\displaystyle\leq a_{i}-\underline{B}\sum_{j}f^{n}_{j}\leq a_{i}-\underline{B}\|f^{n}\|_{\infty}<0.

This estimate works because every component of
B
and
f
is positive and additionally
\sum_{j}f_{j}>\|f\|_{\infty}
. So from (
4.3
), we can tell that if
f^{n}\in\Omega_{\epsilon}\cap\Upsilon
,
f^{n+1}\in\Omega_{\epsilon}
.

On the other hand, for
f^{n}\in\Omega_{\epsilon}\setminus\Upsilon
we want to show that the jump is not too big, so that
f^{n+1}\in\Omega_{\epsilon}
. Indeed, from (
4.3
) and positivity

\displaystyle 0<f^{n+1}_{i}

\displaystyle=f^{n}_{i}+\frac{\tau d_{i}f^{n}_{i}}{1+\tau\lambda d_{i}f^{n}_{i}}(a-Bf^{n})_{i}

\displaystyle<f^{n}_{i}+\frac{1}{\lambda}\|a\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq(\frac{1}{\underline{B}}+\frac{1}{\lambda})\|a\|_{\infty}.

Therefore,
f^{n+1}\in\Omega_{\epsilon}
and we have the desired result.
∎

Unlike the continuous-time version, it is tricky to deal with the discrepancy between current and the next step. It turns out a small enough time step
\tau
provides a restriction on the ratio between two consecutive steps.

Lemma 4.2

(Ratio bound)
.

Under the same assumption from Lemma
4.1
,

0<1-\tau\|d\|_{\infty}M_{\epsilon}\leq\frac{f^{n+1}}{f^{n}}\leq 1+\tau\|d\|_{\infty}M_{\epsilon}.

Proof.

From (
4.3
) we have,

\Big|\frac{f^{n+1}}{f^{n}}-1\Big|=\Big|\frac{\tau d_{i}(a-Bf^{n})_{i}}{1+\tau\lambda d_{i}f^{n}_{i}}\Big|\leq\tau\|d\|_{\infty}M_{\epsilon}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle 0<1-\tau\|d\|_{\infty}M_{\epsilon}\leq\frac{f^{n+1}}{f^{n}}\leq 1+\tau\|d\|_{\infty}M_{\epsilon},

where
M_{\epsilon}
is from Lemma
4.1
.
∎

Lemma 4.3

(Monotonicity)
.

E is non-decreasing along the evolution of equation (
4.2
). In other words,

E(f^{n+1})-E(f^{n})\geq 0\qquad\forall n\in\mathbb{N},

and equality holds iff for all
i
,
f^{n}_{i}=0
or
a_{i}=(Bf^{n})_{i}.

Proof.

From Lemma
2.1

\displaystyle E(f^{n+1})-E(f^{n})\;\;

\displaystyle=-(H(f^{n+1})-H(f^{n}))

\displaystyle\geq\;\;-\langle\nabla H_{+}(f^{n+1})-\nabla H_{-}(f^{n}),f^{n+1}-f^{n}\rangle

\displaystyle=\;\;\tau\sum_{i}d_{i}f^{n}_{i}\big(\nabla H_{+}(f^{n+1})-\nabla H_{-}(f^{n})\big)^{2}_{i}\geq 0.

Suppose the equality holds. Then the middle term of the inequality vanishes. So for all
i
,
f^{n}_{i}=0
or
\big(\nabla H_{+}(f^{n+1})-\nabla H_{-}(f^{n})\big)_{i}=0
, which implies
f^{n+1}_{i}-f^{n}_{i}=0
by (
4.1
). Thus, by (
4.2
), for all
i
,
f^{n}_{i}=0
or
a_{i}=(Bf^{n})_{i}
.

Conversely, if for all
i
,
f^{n}_{i}=0
or
a_{i}=(Bf^{n})_{i}
, enough to show
f^{n+1}_{i}=f^{n}_{i}
for the latter case. For such i,

\displaystyle(f^{n+1}-f^{n})_{i}

\displaystyle=\tau d_{i}f^{n}_{i}(a-Bf^{n}-\lambda(f^{n+1}-f^{n}))_{i}

\displaystyle=-\tau d_{i}f^{n}_{i}(\lambda(f^{n+1}-f^{n}))_{i}

(1+\tau\lambda d_{i}f^{n}_{i})(f^{n+1}-f^{n})_{i}=0

implies
(f^{n+1}-f^{n})_{i}=0
. Since
f^{n+1}=f^{n}
, the equality holds.
∎

Lemma 4.4

(
l^{2}
bound)
.

\sum_{n=k}^{\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{\tau d_{i}f^{n}_{i}}(f^{n+1}-f^{n})^{2}_{i}\leq\liminf_{n}E(f^{n})-E(f^{k}).

Proof.

From (
4.1
),

\displaystyle E(f^{n+1})-E(f^{n})

\displaystyle\geq-\langle\nabla H_{+}(f^{n+1})-\nabla H_{-}(f^{n}),f^{n+1}-f^{n}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{\tau d_{i}f^{n}_{i}}(f^{n+1}-f^{n})^{2}_{i}.

For
m>k
using telescoping sum,

\displaystyle\sum_{n=k}^{m}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{\tau d_{i}f^{n}_{i}}(f^{n+1}-f^{n})^{2}_{i}

\displaystyle\leq E(f^{k})-E(f^{m+1}).

Thus

\sum_{n=k}^{\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{\tau d_{i}f^{n}_{i}}(f^{n+1}-f^{n})^{2}_{i}\leq E(f^{k})-\lim_{n\to\infty}E(f^{n}).\qed

4.1.2.
Convergence result

We tailored the discrete-time equation to make
E
increasing. In order to prove convergence, we need a suitable entropy function too. We modify the previous entropy
F
by adding quadratic terms.

Definition 4.1
.

For fixed
\tilde{f}
we define

F(f^{n}):=\sum_{i}w_{i}(\tilde{f}_{i}\log\frac{{\tilde{f}_{i}}}{f^{n}_{i}}+f^{n}_{i}-\tilde{f}_{i})+\sum_{i}\frac{\lambda\tau}{2}(f^{n}_{i}-\tilde{f}_{i})^{2}

The modified entropy still has nice properties, convexity and having a unique minimizer. In fact the entropy is a sum of two positive convex functions because the first term can be represented as

\sum_{i}w_{i}\tilde{f}_{i}(\frac{f^{n}_{i}}{\tilde{f}_{i}}-\log\frac{f^{n}_{i}}{\tilde{f}_{i}}-1)

with knowing that
x-\log x-1
is a convex function having minimum value 0 at 1. So we have
F\geq 0
, moreover
F(f)=0
if and only if
f=\tilde{f}
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
\{f^{n}\}
be a sequence generated from (
4.2
). For any
\epsilon>0
, there exists
M_{\epsilon}
such that if
0<\tau<\frac{1}{\|d\|_{\infty}M_{\epsilon}}
then
\lim_{n\to\infty}f^{n}
exists.

Proof.

The argument is similar to the proof of Theorem
3.1
but a tad more technical. Let
\{f^{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
be a sequence generated from the difference equation (
4.2
). Thanks to Lemma
4.1
,
\{f^{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
is bounded, so there is a convergent subsequence
\{f^{n_{k}}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
and
\tilde{f}\in\overline{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}}
such that
f^{n_{k}}\rightarrow\tilde{f}
as
k\rightarrow\infty
.

Let
K:=\{i\in[N]\;:\;a_{i}-\sum_{j}B_{ij}\tilde{f}_{j}\neq 0\}
and

\displaystyle Q(f)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{i\in K}f_{i},

\displaystyle Z(f)

\displaystyle:=\min_{i\in K}\{(a_{i}-\sum_{j}B_{ij}f_{j})^{2}\}.

If
K
is empty, define
Q=0
and
Z=1
. Then, as we have seen in the proof of Lemma
4.4

\displaystyle E(f^{n+1})-E(f^{n})

\displaystyle\geq\langle\frac{1}{\tau df^{n}}(f^{n+1}-f^{n}),f^{n+1}-f^{n}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in[N]}\frac{\tau d_{i}f^{n}_{i}}{(1+\lambda\tau d_{i}f^{n}_{i})^{2}}(a-Bf^{n})^{2}_{i}

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{i\in K}\frac{\tau d_{i}f^{n}_{i}}{(1+\lambda\tau d_{i}f^{n}_{i})^{2}}(a-Bf^{n})^{2}_{i}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{C_{0}}Z(f^{n})Q(f^{n}).

Also note that
Z(\tilde{f})=z>0
, so
\{f\;:\;Z(f)>\frac{z}{2}\}
is a neighborhood of
\tilde{f}
. Since
\tilde{f}=\bigcap_{\epsilon>0}\{f\;:\;F(f)\leq\epsilon\}
, we can find
\epsilon^{*}>0
such that,

\displaystyle F(f)\leq\epsilon^{*}\;\;

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow\;\;Z(f)>\frac{z}{2}\text{ and}\max_{i\in\textbf{supp}\tilde{f}}\frac{1}{f^{n}_{i}}<L,

for some
L>0.

Let’s analyze the difference of the entropy with
w_{i}=1/d_{i}
. By Lemma
2.1

\displaystyle F(f^{n+1})-F(f^{n})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i}\frac{1}{d_{i}f^{n+1}_{i}}(f^{n+1}_{i}-\tilde{f}_{i})(f^{n+1}_{i}-f^{n}_{i})+\sum_{i}\lambda\tau(f^{n+1}_{i}-\tilde{f}_{i})(f^{n+1}_{i}-f^{n}_{i})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\frac{1}{d_{i}f^{n+1}_{i}}(f^{n+1}_{i}-f^{n}_{i}+f^{n}_{i}-\tilde{f}_{i})(f^{n+1}_{i}-f^{n}_{i})

\displaystyle\;\;+\sum_{i}\lambda\tau(f^{n+1}_{i}-f^{n}_{i}+f^{n}_{i}-\tilde{f}_{i})(f^{n+1}_{i}-f^{n}_{i})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}(\frac{1}{d_{i}f^{n+1}_{i}}+\lambda\tau)(f^{n+1}_{i}-f^{n}_{i})^{2}+\sum_{i}\frac{1}{d_{i}f^{n+1}_{i}}(f^{n}_{i}-\tilde{f}_{i})(f^{n+1}_{i}-f^{n}_{i})

\displaystyle\;\;+\sum_{i}\lambda\tau(f^{n}_{i}-\tilde{f}_{i})(f^{n+1}_{i}-f^{n})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}(\frac{f^{n}_{i}}{f^{n+1}_{i}}+\lambda\tau d_{i}f^{n}_{i})\frac{1}{d_{i}f^{n}_{i}}(f^{n+1}_{i}-f^{n}_{i})^{2}

\displaystyle\;\;+\sum_{i}d_{i}^{-1}(\frac{1}{f^{n+1}_{i}}-\frac{1}{f^{n}_{i}})(f^{n}_{i}-\tilde{f}_{i})(f^{n+1}_{i}-f^{n}_{i})

\displaystyle\;\;+\sum_{i}\frac{1+\lambda\tau d_{i}f^{n}_{i}}{d_{i}f^{n}_{i}}(f^{n}_{i}-\tilde{f}_{i})(f^{n+1}_{i}-f^{n}_{i})

\displaystyle=\text{I}+\text{II}+\text{III}

Let’s analyze each term seperately.

I

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}\sum_{i}\frac{1}{d_{i}f^{n}_{i}}(f^{n+1}_{i}-f^{n}_{i})^{2}.

II

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\frac{f^{n}_{i}-f^{n+1}_{i}}{d_{i}f^{n}_{i}f^{n+1}_{i}}(f^{n}_{i}-\tilde{f}_{i})(f^{n+1}_{i}-f^{n}_{i})

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i\in\{\tilde{f}_{i}=0\}}\frac{1}{d_{i}f^{n+1}_{i}}(f^{n+1}_{i}-f^{n}_{i})^{2}-\sum_{i\in\{\tilde{f}_{i}\neq 0\}}\frac{f^{n}_{i}}{d_{i}f^{n+1}_{i}}(\frac{1}{f^{n}_{i}})^{2}(f^{n}_{i}-\tilde{f}_{i})(f^{n+1}_{i}-f^{n}_{i})^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C_{2}\sum_{i\in\{\tilde{f}_{i}\neq 0\}}d_{i}^{-1}(\frac{1}{f^{n}_{i}})^{2}(f^{n+1}_{i}-f^{n}_{i})^{2}.

C_{1}
and
C_{2}
are due to the bound on the domain and the ratio bound.
To analyze III recall the equation (
4.3
)

(1+\tau\lambda d_{i}f^{n}_{i})(f^{n+1}_{i}-f^{n}_{i})=\tau d_{i}f^{n}_{i}(a-Bf^{n})_{i}

III

\displaystyle=\tau\sum_{i}(f^{n}_{i}-\tilde{f}_{i})(a-Bf^{n})_{i}

\displaystyle=\tau(a\cdot f^{n}-f^{n}\cdot Bf^{n}-a\cdot\tilde{f}+\tilde{f}\cdot Bf^{n})

\displaystyle\leq\tau(a\cdot f^{n}-2a\cdot f^{n}+2a\cdot\tilde{f}-\tilde{f}\cdot B\tilde{f}-a\cdot\tilde{f}+\tilde{f}\cdot Bf^{n})

\displaystyle=\tau(-a\cdot f^{n}+\tilde{f}\cdot Bf^{n})

because
E(f^{n})=a\cdot f^{n}-\frac{1}{2}f^{n}Bf^{n}
is increasing and
\tilde{f}\cdot(a-B\tilde{f})=\sum_{i}\tilde{f}_{i}(a_{i}-B\tilde{f}_{i})=0
. Thus

\text{III}\leq\tau\sum_{i\in K}f^{n}_{i}(B\tilde{f}-a)_{i}.

All together, we have

(4.6)

F(f^{n+1})-F(f^{n})\leq(C_{1}+C_{2}\max_{i\in\textbf{supp}\tilde{f}}\frac{1}{f^{n}_{i}})\sum_{i}\frac{1}{d_{i}f^{n}_{i}}(f^{n+1}_{i}-f^{n}_{i})^{2}+\tau Q(f^{n})\max_{i\in K}|a_{i}-B\tilde{f}_{i}|.

Now let
M=\max_{i\in K}|a_{i}-B\tilde{f}_{i}|
and for any
\epsilon\in(0,\epsilon^{*})
, choose
m>0
such that
\forall n_{k}\geq m

\displaystyle F(f^{n_{k}})<\frac{\epsilon}{2},

\displaystyle\sum_{n=m}^{\infty}\sum_{i}\frac{1}{d_{i}f^{n}_{i}}(f^{n+1}_{i}-f^{n}_{i})^{2}<\frac{\epsilon}{4C(1+L)},

\displaystyle\tau\frac{C_{0}M}{z}[E(\tilde{f})-E(f^{n_{k}})]<\frac{\epsilon}{8},

where
C=\max\{C_{1},C_{2}\}
.

By combining (
4.6
) and the conditions above,

\displaystyle F(f^{n_{k}+1})<\epsilon<\epsilon^{*}.

Note that having predecessor in
\epsilon^{*}
neighborhood of
\tilde{f}
allows us to have an entropy difference inequality of form (
4.6
). We continue trapping the sequence by induction. Assume, for any fixed
l\in\mathbb{N}
, every predecessor of
f^{n_{k}+l}
, originated from
f^{n_{k}}
is in
\epsilon^{*}
neighborhood of
\tilde{f}
. Then we will have series of the entropy difference inequalities, (
4.6
), and the telescopic sum of those gives us

\displaystyle F(f^{n_{k}+l})

\displaystyle\leq F(f^{n_{k}})+C(1+L)\sum_{n=n_{k}}^{\infty}\sum_{i}\frac{1}{d_{i}f^{n}_{i}}(f^{n+1}_{i}-f^{n}_{i})^{2}+\tau M\sum_{n=n_{k}}^{\infty}Q(f^{n})

\displaystyle<\epsilon

Thus we show
F(f_{n})<\epsilon\quad\forall n>m.

∎

Remark 4.1
.

The main difficulty of this proof comes from the discreteness of the time step,
f^{n+1}-f^{n}
, which vanishes in continuous case, coarsening the logarithmic entropy difference with some redundancy. The key to balancing the error from the logarithmic term is adding extra quadratic terms.

Interestingly, the modified entropy
F
agrees with the relative entropy,
\mathcal{E}
, suggested by Attouch and Teboulle
[
6
]
,

\mathcal{E}(x,y):=\frac{\nu}{2}\|x-y\|^{2}+\mu\sum_{j}x_{j}\log(x_{j}/y_{j})+y_{j}-x_{j},

As we see in (
4.3
)

\frac{1}{\tau}(f^{n+1}-f^{n})_{i}=\frac{f^{n}_{i}}{1/d_{i}+\tau\lambda f^{n}_{i}}(a-Bf^{n})_{i}.

The equation (
4.2
) can be regarded as an application of Euler’s method to the regularized Lotka-Volterra equation with
\mu=1/d_{i}
and
\nu=\tau\lambda
, in which case
\mathcal{E}(\tilde{f},f)=F(f).

In the next section, we discuss another way to time-discretize regularized Lotka-Volterra type system with more general energies.

4.2.
Convex splitting for de-singularized systems

Technically, implicit iterations provided in DCA (
2.2
) demand to solve a convex optimization problem given by
H_{+}
in each steps. Therefore, in practice we need to find a nice convex splitting representation of
H=H_{+}-H_{-}
so that
H_{+}
is easy to invert.
In this section, we will apply the implicit discrete iterations to Lotka-Volterra equations. The first task is to to find a convex splitting representation of
H=H_{+}-H_{-}
in a way that
\nabla H_{+}
is simple enough to invert. It turns out we can not only makes the calculation simpler but also make the sequence stay positive.

Let
[u]^{p}_{i}:=u_{i}^{p}
denote the component-wise power on a vector
u\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
and
B=B^{+}-B^{-}
so that

\displaystyle H(u):=-\frac{1}{4}E([u]^{2})

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{4}\sum_{i\in[N]}u_{i}^{2}a_{i}+\frac{1}{8}\sum_{i,j\in[N]}B_{ij}u^{2}_{i}u^{2}_{j}

\displaystyle=-(\frac{1}{4}\sum_{i\in[N]}u_{i}^{2}a_{i}+\frac{1}{8}\sum_{i,j\in[N]}B^{-}_{ij}u^{2}_{i}u^{2}_{j})+\frac{1}{8}\sum_{i\in[N]}B^{+}_{ij}u^{2}_{i}u^{2}_{j}

\displaystyle=:-H_{-}(u)+H_{+}(u)

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
B_{+}
and
B_{-}
be positive definite with nonnegative entries. Then
H_{+}
and
H_{-}
are convex. Therefore we have convex splitting of
H
, i.e.,
H=H_{+}-H_{-}.

Proof.

It is enough to check, for
A
a positive definite matrix with nonnegative entries, that
h(u)=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{i,j\in[N]}A_{ij}u^{2}_{i}u^{2}_{j}
is convex.

\displaystyle Dh(u)(v)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j\in[N]}A_{ij}v_{i}u_{i}u^{2}_{j}

\displaystyle D^{2}h(u)(v,w)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j\in[N]}A_{ij}v_{i}w_{i}u^{2}_{j}+2v_{i}u_{i}A_{ij}u_{j}w_{j},

\displaystyle=\langle v,\nabla^{2}h(u)w\rangle.

By denoting
\backslash\cdot\backslash:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
a map that sends a vector to the corresponding diagonal matrix, we can represent the Hessian of
h
,

\nabla^{2}h(u)=\backslash A[u]^{2}\backslash+2\backslash u\backslash A\backslash u\backslash.

Since every component of
u
and
A
are positive
\nabla^{2}h(u)
is positive definite.
∎

The previous lemma leaves infinite possibility of DCA algorithms. We suggest the following splitting of
B
, which can further simplify the iteration.

Lemma 4.6
.

Given a matrix
B\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\mathbb{R}^{N}
, let
B^{+}:=\lambda I+\beta\mathds{1}\mathds{1}^{T}
where
\lambda>0
is the spectral radius of
B
,
\beta
is a maximum nonnegative off-diagonal element, or zero, and
\mathds{1}_{i}=1
for all
i\in[N]
. Then
B^{+}
and
B^{-}:=B^{+}-B
are positive definite matrices with nonnegative entries.

Proof.

It is clear that all entries of
B^{+}
and
B^{-}
are nonnegative.
B^{+}
is positive definite since
\mathds{1}\mathds{1}^{T}
is positive semidefinite. Note that for any
v\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
,

v^{T}B^{-}v=v^{T}(\lambda I-B)v+\beta v^{T}\mathds{1}\mathds{1}^{T}v\geq 0.\qed

Iteration scheme

With the suggested convex splitting, we have the following difference equations.

[DC algorithm]

\displaystyle\nabla H_{+}(u^{n+1})

\displaystyle=\nabla H_{-}(u^{n}),

(4.7)

\displaystyle u^{n+1}_{i}(B^{+}[u^{n+1}]^{2})_{i}

\displaystyle=u^{n}_{i},(a+B^{-}[u^{n}]^{2})_{i},

[Semi-implicit Euler’s]

\displaystyle u^{n+1}_{i}-u^{n}_{i}

\displaystyle=-\tau(\nabla H_{+}(u^{n+1})-\nabla H_{-}(u^{n}))_{i},

(4.8)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\tau}{2}u^{n+1}_{i}(B^{+}[u^{n+1}]^{2}_{i})+\frac{\tau}{2}u^{n}_{i}(a+B_{-}[u^{n}]^{2})_{i}.

Recall that the semi-implicit Euler’s method is the DCA with extra quadratic potential. However, for the semi-implict Euler’s method, instead of calculating the spectral radius
\lambda
in advance, we can change
\tau
smaller and smaller until the algorithm works empirically.
The suggested convex splitting of
H
from Lemma
4.6
reduces the complexity. Let
S:=\sum_{i}u_{i}^{2}
, then

\displaystyle 2\nabla H_{+}(u)_{i}

\displaystyle=u_{i}\sum_{j}B^{+}_{ij}u_{j}^{2}

\displaystyle=(\lambda+\beta)u_{i}^{3}+\beta u_{i}\sum_{j\not=i}u_{j}^{2}

\displaystyle=(\lambda+\beta)u_{i}^{3}+\beta(S-u_{i}^{2})u_{i}

\displaystyle=\lambda u_{i}^{3}+\beta Su_{i}.

So we can simplify (
4.7
) and (
4.8
) to the following
(N+1)
many
1
-dimensional problems respectively,

DC algorithm

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\lambda u_{i}^{3}+\beta Su_{i}=v_{i}\\
\sum_{i}u_{i}^{2}=S,\end{array}\right.

Semi-implicit Euler

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\tau\lambda u_{i}^{3}+(2+\tau\beta S)u_{i}=v_{i}\\
\sum_{i}u_{i}^{2}=S.\end{array}\right.

Remark 4.2

(Lotka-Volterra system with cooperative interaction)
.

If
B
does not have positive off diagonal element, i.e.,
B
is cooperative, we can choose
\beta=0
. In this case
S
does not play any role so the calculation will be much simpler. In other words, (
4.7
) and (
4.8
), become
N
-many 1-dimensional cubic equations.

Positivity of the sequence

Prior to proving convergence, we would like to point out that solutions of Lotka-Volterra stay in
\mathbb{R}^{N}_{+}
. In general, this provides another restriction on choosing time steps. Due to those specific splitting, however, the sequence stays in
\mathbb{R}^{N}_{+}
independent of the choice of the time step.

Proposition 4.2
.

\{u^{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
from (
4.7
) or (
4.8
) with initial
u_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}_{+}
stays in
\mathbb{R}^{N}_{+}.

Proof.

Since (
4.8
) is a special case of (
4.7
), it suffices show the proof for (
4.7
). Let’s take a look at the
i
-th component in the explicit formula of (
4.7
),

\displaystyle u^{n+1}_{i}\sum_{j\not=i}B^{+}_{ij}[u^{n+1}_{j}]^{2}+B^{+}_{ii}[u^{n+1}_{i}]^{3}

\displaystyle=u^{n}_{i}(a+B^{-}[u^{n}]^{2})_{i}.

Note that the strong convexity of
B^{+}
assures
B^{+}_{ii}>0
.
Since the iteration is well defined, we can consider the left hand side as a function of
u^{n+1}_{i}
which is strictly increasing and vanishes at
0
. On the other hand, the right hand side is nonnegative because
u^{n}\geq 0
. Therefore
u^{n+1}\geq 0.

∎

Therefore by Theorem
2.2
we can conclude the following.

Theorem 4.2
.

Any bounded sequence
\{u^{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
of discrete-time Lotka-Volterra equation stays in
\mathbb{R}^{N}_{+}
and if bounded converges.

4.3.
Time-discretization of regularized Lotka-Volterra systems

Preconditioning and Riemannian metric

Let
g
be a strongly convex function on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
and consider the Riemannian metric induced by
g
,
\langle v,w\rangle_{g(u)}:=v^{T}\nabla^{2}g(u)w
.

\displaystyle DH(u)(v)

\displaystyle=\nabla H(u)^{T}v

\displaystyle=(\nabla^{2}g(u)^{-1}\nabla H(u))^{T}\nabla^{2}g(u)v

\displaystyle=:\langle\text{grad}H(u),v\rangle_{g(u)}.

So the gradient flow of
H
with respect to the
g
-metric is as follows,

\displaystyle u^{\prime}(t)\;\;

\displaystyle=-\text{grad}_{g}H(u)

\displaystyle=-\nabla^{2}g(u)^{-1}\nabla H(u).

Or equivalently,

\frac{d}{dt}\nabla g(u(t))=-\nabla H(u),

in which
u
is following the gradient of
H
in the scope of
\nabla g
. The analogous time-discretization method is called the mirror descent or the preconditioning method.

\nabla g(u^{n+1})-\nabla g(u^{n})=-\tau\nabla H(u^{n}).

Note that we can use this idea to Euler’s semi-implicit method,

(4.9)

\nabla g(u^{n+1})-\nabla g(u^{n})=-\tau(\nabla\tilde{H}_{+}(u^{n+1})-\nabla\tilde{H}_{-}(u^{n})),

which is merely adding additional convexity,
g
, to the convex splitting
(\tilde{H}_{+},\tilde{H}_{-})
of
H
,

H(u)=\big(\tilde{H}_{+}(u)+\frac{1}{\tau}g(u)\big)-\big(\tilde{H}_{-}(u)+\frac{1}{\tau}g(u)\big).

As we discussed, the Regularized Lotka-Volterra equation can be seen as a convex preconditioning. So we suggest the following iteration schemes.

(4.10)

\nabla g(u^{n+1})-\nabla g(u^{n})=-\gamma\nabla H(u^{n}),

(4.11)

\nabla g(u^{n+1})-\nabla g(u^{n})=-\tau(\nabla H_{+}(u^{n+1})-\nabla H_{-}(u^{n})),

where

g(u)=\frac{\mu}{2}\mu\|u\|^{2}+\frac{\nu}{12}\sum_{i}u_{i}^{4}.

Note that both schemes fall into the category of DC algorithms.
The first scheme is preconditioned Euler’s method. So we need to choose
\tau>0
small enough to make
g-\tau H
convex. On the other hand, the second scheme does not have a restriction on
\tau
.

To conclude, by Theorem
2.2
we have the following.

Theorem 4.3
.

Assume that
\tau>0
and that
\gamma>0
is sufficiently small so that
\frac{1}{\gamma}g-H
is convex. Then the sequence
\{u^{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
generated from the time-discretized regularized Lotka-Volterra systems (
4.10
), or (
4.11
) converges.

Part III
Concentration-dispersion dynamics

Chapter 5
Gradient structure of concentration-dispersion models

Despite its gaining in importance in nonlinear analysis, exact solutions of solitary waves are challenging to compute. In 1976, Petviashvili proposed a powerful numerical method for computing solitary
wave solutions without analysis or proof. Due to the efficacy of the algorithm, Petviashili’s method was applied to numerous nonlinear problems in modern mathematical physics (
[
54
]
,
[
53
]
,
[
36
]
).

We study a new type of nonlinear dispersive integro-differential equations, inspired by the one dimensional nonlinear wave equation with power nonlinearity. Using the gradient structure and Łojasiewicz convergence theorem, we seek to prove global convergence of the solutions to the equilibrium which can be considered as a solitary wave.

Motivation

Consider a nonlinear scalar wave equation with power nonlinearity in one dimension:

u_{t}-(\mathcal{L}u)_{x}+pu^{p-1}u_{x}=0

where
u(t,x):\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
,
p>1
, and
\mathcal{L}
is a linear self-adjoint nonnegative pseudo-differential operator of order
m
, in
x
with constant coefficients in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
.
The ansatz
u(t,x)=\Phi(x-ct)

c\Phi+\mathcal{L}\Phi=\Phi^{p}

and Fourier transform,

(c+\widehat{\mathcal{L}}(k))\widehat{\Phi}(k)=\widehat{\Phi^{p}}(k),

inspires the following fixed point formula for a solitary wave profile:

\Phi=\int_{\mathbb{R}}K(x-y)\Phi^{p}(y)dy,

where the Fourier transform of
K
satisfies the following,

\widehat{K}(k)=\frac{1}{c+\widehat{\mathcal{L}}(k)}

Petviashvili iteration

In order to calculate nontrivial solitary wave solutions in a space of two dimensions, Petviashvili
[
54
]
devised the fixed point algorithm with a scaling factor
M_{n}
with power
\gamma>0
, when it is applied to a space of one dimension, as follows

(5.1)

\left\{\begin{aligned} \widehat{u}_{n+1}(k)&=M_{n}^{\gamma}\widehat{K}(k){\widehat{u^{p}_{n}}}(k)\\
M_{n}(\widehat{u}_{n})&=\frac{\int_{\mathbb{R}}[c+\widehat{\mathcal{L}}(k)][\widehat{u}_{n}(k)]^{2}dk}{\int_{\mathbb{R}}\widehat{u_{n}}(k)\widehat{u_{n}^{p}}(k)dk}.\end{aligned}\right.

It is known that without the scaling factor
M_{n}
, or when
\gamma=0
, the iteration (
5.1
) usually diverges.
When
p=2
Petviashvili empirically found that the iteration (
5.1
) method converges when
1<\gamma<3
, and at
\gamma=2
the fastest rate of convergence occurs. Indeed, Pelinovsky and Stepanyants in 2004
[
53
]
proved that, under spectral stability assumptions on the linearized operator at an unknown equilibrium (Assumption 2.1
[
53
]
), given
p>1
, the iteration (
5.1
) converges when
1<\gamma<\frac{p+1}{p-1}
, and at
\gamma=\frac{p}{p-1}
the solution converges at the fastest rate.

Concentration-dispersion equation

For
L\in(0,\infty]
, let
\mathds{T}_{L}:=\mathbb{R}/L=[-\frac{L}{2},\frac{L}{2}]
denote the torus of length
L
.
We consider a time continuous evolution of
u(t,x):\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathds{T}_{L}\to\mathbb{R}
, which is analogous to Petviashvili iteration. We define
concentration
-
dispersion

equation
as follows,

(5.2)

\left\{\begin{aligned} \;\;\frac{d}{dt}u(t,x)&=K\ast u^{p}-c(t)u,\\
c(t)&=\int u^{p}K\ast u^{p}dx.\end{aligned}\right.

In what follows, we assume that
K(x)>0
for all
x\in\mathds{T}_{L}
and
K(x)\in W^{1,1}\cap W^{1,p+1}(\mathds{T}_{L})
.

Power nonlinearity creates concentration effect and convolution gives dispersion effect. The equation is designed for
u
to chase after a scalar multiple of
K\ast u^{p}
.
Just for the comparison between (
5.1
) and (
5.2
), the scaling factors can be related as follows,

\displaystyle M_{n}(\widehat{u}_{n})

\displaystyle=\frac{\int_{\mathbb{R}}[c+\widehat{\mathcal{L}}(k)][\widehat{u}_{n}(k)]^{2}dk}{\int_{\mathbb{R}}\widehat{u_{n}}(k)\widehat{u_{n}^{p}}(k)dk}

\displaystyle=M_{n-1}^{2\gamma}\frac{\int_{\mathbb{R}}\widehat{u_{n-1}^{p}}(k)\widehat{K}(k)\widehat{u_{n-1}^{p}}(k)}{\int_{\mathbb{R}}\widehat{u_{n}}(k)\widehat{u_{n}^{p}}(k)dk}

\displaystyle=M_{n-1}^{2\gamma}\frac{\int_{\mathbb{R}}u^{p}_{n-1}K*u^{p}_{n-1}dx}{\int_{\mathbb{R}}u^{p+1}_{n}dx}

Prior to discussing the gradient structure of the concentration-dispersion equation (
5.2
), we study the asymptotic properties of a few important functionals.

Important functionals

Here we aim to study a dynamical way to calculate the solution, asymptotic behavior, and stability of non-local and nonlinear differential equations.
We introduce two functionals,
E
and
F
, which provides different gradient structure to describe concentration-dispersion dynamics (
5.2
). Let

\displaystyle E(u)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2p}\int u^{p}K\ast u^{p}dx,

\displaystyle F(u)

\displaystyle:=e^{-\frac{2p}{p+1}\int u^{p+1}dx}E(u).

Note that
c(t)=2pE(u)
.

Gradient structure on the sphere in
L^{p+1}

Let

\mathcal{M}:=\{u>0:\int u(x)^{p+1}dx=1\}\subset H^{1}(\mathds{T}_{L})=:\mathcal{V}.

\mathcal{M}
is an invariant manifold of (
5.2
), and, moreover,
\mathcal{M}
attracts every solution: for any
L\in(0,\infty]
,

\frac{1}{p+1}(\int u^{p+1}dx)^{\prime}=\int u^{p}K\ast u^{p}dx\big(1-\int u^{p+1}dx\big).

We will discuss the detailed proof later in Proposition
5.1
.
In this case, (
5.2
) is a gradient flow of
E
with respect to the Riemannian structure given as follows,

\langle v,w\rangle_{u}:=\int vwu^{p-1}dx,\quad\forall u\in\mathcal{M},\quad v,w\in T_{u}\mathcal{M},

where the tangent manifold at
u\in\mathcal{M}
is given as follows,

T_{u}\mathcal{M}:=\{v\in\mathcal{V}:\int vu^{p}dx=0\}.

It is interesting to see that with respect to the given metric
T_{u}\mathcal{M}
is “orthogonal” to
u
, i.e.,
\langle u,v\rangle_{u}=0
, as if
\mathcal{M}
is spherical. It is easy to check that (
5.2
) on
\mathcal{M}
is equivalent to the Gram-Schmidt projection from
X
onto the manifold, i.e.,

(5.3)

u^{\prime}=K*u^{p}-\frac{\langle K*u^{p},u\rangle_{u}}{\langle u,u\rangle_{u}}u.

Indeed, (
5.2
) is a projected gradient flow onto
\mathcal{M}
. If we perturb
u
with
v\in T_{u}\mathcal{M}

(5.4)

\displaystyle DE(u)(v)

\displaystyle=\int u^{p-1}(K\ast u^{p})vdx

\displaystyle=:\int\nabla E(u)vdx

\displaystyle=\langle\nabla E(u),v\rangle_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle=\int(K\ast u^{p}-c(t)u)vu^{p-1}dx

\displaystyle=\langle\text{grad}E(u),v\rangle_{u}

so that

\displaystyle u^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\text{grad}E(u),

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}E(u(t))

\displaystyle=\|\text{grad}E(u)\|^{2}_{u}.

L^{2}
-gradient structure

For a fixed
L\in(0,\infty]
, let
\mathcal{H}:=L^{2}(\mathds{T}_{L})
and
\mathcal{V}:=H^{1}(\mathds{T}_{L})
, so that

\mathcal{V}\subset\mathcal{H}\subset\mathcal{V}^{\prime}.

As long as
E
is well defined, we can extend the gradient structure on
\mathcal{M}
to
L^{2}
by the proper scaling of the energy
E
.
Let the scaling factor be

\alpha(t):=e^{\frac{2p}{p+1}\int u^{p+1}dx},

so that

F(u):=E(u)/\alpha(t).

Then we can check the
L^{2}
gradient-like structure given by
F
,

\displaystyle\alpha(t)DF(u)(v)

\displaystyle=DE(u)(v)-E(u)\frac{2p}{p+1}D(\int u^{p+1}dx)(v)

\displaystyle=\int u^{p-1}K*u^{p}vdx-2pE(u)\int u^{p}vdx

\displaystyle=\int u^{p-1}(K*u^{p}-2pE(u)u)vdx.

\displaystyle=\int u^{p-1}(K*u^{p}-c(t)u)vdx.

\displaystyle DF(u)(v)

\displaystyle=e^{-\frac{2p}{p+1}\int u^{p+1}dx}\int u^{p-1}(K*u^{p}-c(t)u)vdx,

\displaystyle=:\langle\nabla F(u),v\rangle_{L^{2}}.

Therefore, (
5.2
) takes the form

u^{\prime}=e^{\frac{2p}{p+1}\int u^{p+1}dx}u^{1-p}\nabla F(u).

We finish the chapter by proving some convergence properties of the functionals. It is easy to see that
u(t,\cdot)
of (
5.2
) stays positive when
u(0,\cdot)>0
. The concrete proof along with well-posedness can be found in Theorem
6.1
.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
u(x,t)\in L^{p+1}(\mathds{T}_{L})
be a solution of (
5.2
) such that
u(x,0)>0
for all
x\in\mathds{T}_{L}
, then the following functionals have convergent properties as
t\to\infty:

(1)

F(u)=E(u)/\alpha(t)
is nondecreasing,

(2)

\|u\|_{L^{p+1}}
converges monotonically to 1,

(3)

E(u)=\frac{1}{2p}\int u^{p}K\ast u^{p}dx
converges to a positive constant.

Additionally, if
\|u(0)\|_{L^{p+1}}\leq 1
then
E(u)
is nondecreasing as well.

Proof.

F
is nondecreasing because the time derivative of
F
is nonnegative, i.e.,

\displaystyle F(u(t))^{\prime}e^{\frac{2p}{p+1}\int u^{p+1}dx}

\displaystyle=E(u)^{\prime}-E(u)\frac{2p}{p+1}(\int u^{p+1}dx)^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\int u^{p-1}K*u^{p}u^{\prime}dx-2pE(u)\int u^{p}u^{\prime}dx

\displaystyle=\int u^{p-1}(K*u^{p}-2pE(u)u)u^{\prime}dx.

\displaystyle=\int u^{p-1}(K*u^{p}-c(t)u)^{2}dx\geq 0.

Since

F(u)=E(u)/\alpha(t)\leq E(u)=2pc(t),

we have
\inf_{t\geq 0}c(t)>0
. This yields the monotonic convergence of
L^{p+1}
norm together with taking time-differentiation,

(5.5)

\frac{1}{p+1}(\int u^{p+1}dx)^{\prime}=c(t)\big(1-\int u^{p+1}dx\big).

Therefore,

\lim_{t\to\infty}\|u(t)\|_{L^{p+1}}\to 1.

Additionally, if
\|u(0)\|_{L^{p+1}}\leq 1
,
E
is nondecreasing as well because
\|u(0)\|_{L^{p+1}}
is nondecreasing.

Finally, we show that
E(u)
is bounded above. From (
5.5
) we have

\|u\|_{L^{p+1}}\leq 1+\|u_{0}\|_{L^{p+1}}.

So we use Hölder’s inequality and Young’s convolution inequality to bound
E
,

\displaystyle 2pE(u)

\displaystyle=\int u^{p}K\ast u^{p}dx

\displaystyle\leq\|u\|^{p}_{L^{p+1}}\|K*u^{p}\|_{L^{p+1}}

\displaystyle\leq\|K\|_{L^{\frac{p+1}{2}}}\|u\|^{2p}_{L^{p+1}}

(5.6)

\displaystyle\leq(1+\|u_{0}\|_{L^{p+1}})^{2p}\|K\|_{L^{\frac{p+1}{2}}}<\infty.

This implies
F
is bounded. Therefore
F>0
converges monotonically to a positive constant, and thus
E=\alpha(t)F>0
also converges to a positive constant.
∎

Chapter 6
Well-posedness, compactness and nontrivial wave profiles

In this chapter, we explore properties of the concentration-dispersion equation (
5.2
) on the torus
\mathds{T}_{L}=\mathbb{R}/L=[-\frac{L}{2},\frac{L}{2}]
of length
L\in(0,\infty]
.

Recall that the equation (
5.2
) is motivated by solitary waves and the solution
u
evolves in a way to balance the concentration effect and the dispersion effect. We use Picard iteration to show that the solution is well-posed in
\mathcal{V}:=H^{1}(\mathds{T}_{L})
. Simultaneously, we show that if the initial data
u_{0}
is “bell-shaped”, then the solution stays bell-shaped along the evolution.

We also show that the solution is bounded above, and below away from
0
. Directly, by Arzela-Ascoli’s theorem, the solution is precompact in the space of continuous functions.
Actually, we can say much more. Recall that dispersive effect on the semi-linear heat equations provides the smoothing effect, which makes the solution smoother than it initially was, so we have compactness (Section
3.3
of Chapter
3
).
Even if the convolution operator cannot make the solution smoother than the initial data, but we can show that the solution is a combination of the scalar multiple of the initial data and the smoother part from the dispersion effect by
K\in W^{1,1}
. So the solution is precompact in
H^{1}(\mathds{T}_{L})
.

Note that we aim to approximate nontrivial solitary wave solutions. We show the constant equilibrium is unstable and, moreover, use the gradient structure to prove that nontrivial equilibria exist.
The convergence result, however, cannot be proven using the Łojasiewicz framework. We will discuss the issue at the end of this chapter.

6.1.
Well-posedness of solutions and consistency of bell-shape

We use the Banach fixed point theorem to show well-posedness of the concentration-dispersion equation. Later in the section, we continue the argument to prove that the solution with bell-shaped initial value stays bell-shaped.
At a first glance at (
5.2
), it is not immediate to believe this to be true, since
u^{\prime}
is a subtraction of two bell-shaped function which, in general, might not be bell-shaped. However, using simple ODE trick we show that the bell-shape of
u
persists

Theorem 6.1

(Well-posedness)
.

For any initial
u_{0}\in\mathcal{V}
, there exists unique solution
u(t,x)\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{+};\mathcal{V})
of (
5.2
). Additionally, if
u_{0}(\cdot)>0
then
u(t,\cdot)>0
for all
t\geq 0
.

Proof.

To use Picard iteration, we modify (
5.2
) as follows,

\displaystyle u^{\prime}+c(t)u

\displaystyle=K\ast u^{p}

\displaystyle u(t)

\displaystyle=e^{-\int_{0}^{t}c(s)ds}u(0)+\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\int_{s}^{t}c(r)dr}K\ast u^{p}ds.

For fixed
u_{0}\in\mathcal{V}
we define
I:C\big([0,T],\overline{B_{+}(R)}\big)\to C\big([0,T],\overline{B_{+}(R)}\big)
, as follows

I(u)(x,t):=e^{-\int_{0}^{t}c(s)ds}u_{0}(x)+\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\int_{s}^{t}c(r)dr}K\ast u^{p}ds,

where
B_{+}(R)=\{u\in\mathcal{V}:\|u\|_{\mathcal{V}}<R\text{ and }u(x)>0,\forall x\in\mathds{T}_{L}\}.

Claim that if
\|u_{0}(x)\|_{H^{1}}\leq\frac{R}{2}
for some
R
then there exists
T>0
such that,

I:C\big([0,T],\overline{B_{+}(R)}\big)\to C\big([0,T],\overline{B_{+}(R)}\big)

with the induced uniform norm

\|u\|_{\infty}:=\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|u(x,t)\|_{H^{1}}.

Let
u(x,t)\in C([0,T],\overline{B_{+}(R)})
,

\displaystyle\|I(u)\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\|e^{-\int_{o}^{T}c(t)dt}u_{0}\|_{\infty}+\int_{0}^{T}\|K\ast u^{p}\|_{\infty}dt

\displaystyle\leq\|u_{0}\|_{H^{1}}+T\|K\ast u^{p}\|_{\infty}.

If
1<p<2
, we use Young’s convolution inequality to analyze
K\ast u^{p}

\displaystyle\|K\ast u^{p}\|_{H^{1}}

\displaystyle\leq\|K\ast u^{p}\|_{L^{2}}+\|K_{x}\ast u^{p}\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq(\|K\|_{L^{q}}+\|K_{x}\|_{L^{q}})\|u\|_{L^{2}}^{p}

\displaystyle=\|K\|_{W^{q,1}}R^{p},

where
q
satisfies,
\frac{p}{2}+\frac{1}{q}=1+\frac{1}{2}
, i.e.
q=2/(3-p)
.

If
p\geq 2
,
\|K\ast u^{p}\|_{H^{1}}=\|K*u^{2}u^{p-2}\|_{L^{2}}+\|K_{x}*u^{2}u^{p-2}\|_{L^{2}}
so we can use Morrey’s embedding theorem and Young’s convolution inequality to get the bound.

\displaystyle\|K\ast u^{p}\|_{H^{1}}

\displaystyle=\|K*(u^{2}u^{p-2})\|_{L^{2}}+p\|K\ast(u_{x}uu^{p-2})\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq R^{p-2}(\|K\|_{L^{2}}\|u\|_{2}^{2}+p\|K\|_{L^{2}}\|u\|_{L^{2}}\|u_{x}\|_{L^{2}})

\displaystyle\leq pC\|K\|_{L^{2}}R^{p}

Therefore,

\displaystyle\|I(u)\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{R}{2}+C(K,p)R^{p}T

\displaystyle\leq R

by letting
T\leq\frac{1}{2CR^{p-1}}
.

Also we can check that
I
is Lipschitz on
C\big([0,T],\overline{B(0,R)}\big)
, since
x\mapsto e^{-x}
and
y\mapsto y^{p}
are Lipschitz when
x\geq 0
and when
y
is bounded, respectively. We use Picard iteration, or Banach fixed point theorem to conclude the well-posedness for short-time. To extend this to long-time, we build time independent
H^{1}
bound (Proposition
6.2
) later in this chapter, and use the Picard iteration again.
∎

Bell-shape consistency

In this section, in addition to prove well-posedness of the equation (
5.2
), we show that the solution with bell-shaped initial value stays bell-shaped.
We consider the equation (
5.2
) on compact domain
\mathds{T}_{L}
with the same kernel
K
for
L\in(0,\infty]
.
We start with making an observation on the shape of the periodization of
K
and
K*u
.

Definition 6.1
.

A real-valued function
u
on
\mathds{T}_{L}
is
bell-shaped
if
u
is even and monotone decreasing on
(0,L/2)
.

Definition 6.2
.

We say
K_{L}
is the
L
-periodization of a function
K
on
\mathbb{R}
when

\displaystyle K_{L}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}K(x-kL).

Also the convolution of
K
with a
L
-periodic function

is as follows,

\displaystyle(K\ast u)(x)

\displaystyle:=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}u(y)K(x-y)dy

\displaystyle=\int_{x_{0}}^{x_{0}+L}u(y)K_{L}(x-y)dy,

for any
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
K
be a bell-shaped function. If
K
is convex in
[0,\infty)
then
K_{L}
is bell-shaped on
\mathds{T}_{L}
.

Proof.

Enough to show that
K_{L}
is monotone non-increasing in
[0,\frac{L}{2}]
.

\displaystyle K_{L}(x)=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}K(x-nL)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}K(x+nL)+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}K(x-nL)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}K(x+nL)+K((n+1)L-x)

Let
h>0
,
x\in[0,\frac{L}{2})
.

\displaystyle K_{L}(x+h)-K_{L}(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}

\displaystyle[K(x+h+nL+K((n+1)L-x-h]

\displaystyle\quad-[K(x+hL)+K((n+1)L-x)]

Claim that
\forall n\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
,

K(x+h+nL)+K((n+1)L-x-h)-K(x+hL)+K((n+1)L-x)\leq 0.

In the interval
[x+nL,(n+1)L-x]
,
let
s=\frac{h}{L-2x}
and use convexity.

\displaystyle K(x+h+nL)+K((n+1)L-x-h)

\displaystyle\leq(1-s)K(x+nL)+sK((n+1)L-x)

\displaystyle+sK(x+nL)+(1-s)K((n+1)L-x)

\displaystyle\leq K(x+nL)+K((n+1)L-x)

as claimed.
∎

Remark 6.1
.

Note as long as
K
does not resemble “stairs”, i.e.
K_{x}
does not vanish except at
0
, we can generalize the previous result as follows. If
K
is a bell-shaped function which is strictly concave inside a compact interval and convex outside, then
K_{L}
is bell-shaped for big enough
L
. For example suppose that
K\approx o(x^{-m})
for
m>1
then

K_{L}(x)=K(x)+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(nL+x)^{m}}+\frac{1}{(nL-x)^{m}}\approx K(x)+\frac{1}{L^{m}}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(n+x/L)^{m}}+\frac{1}{(n-x/L)^{m}},

so that for big enough
L
and near
0
,
K_{L}
resembles the shape of
K
.

Lemma 6.2
.

For such
K
, where
K_{L}
is bell-shaped, if
u
is a
L
-periodic bell-shaped function, then
K\ast u
is
L
-periodic bell-shaped.

Proof.

\displaystyle K\ast u(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}K(y)u(y-x)dx=\int_{-\frac{L}{2}}^{\frac{L}{2}}K_{L}(y)u(y-x)dy.

On the inverval of half period such that
u(x)
is monotone,
u_{x}(x)
exists for almost every
x\in[0,\frac{L}{2}]
. Note that
u_{x}
is odd and
u_{x}(x)\leq 0
when
x\geq 0
. Claim that
\forall x\in[0,\frac{L}{2}]\quad(K\ast u)_{x}(x)\leq 0
. We start with partitioning
\mathds{T}_{L}
with the intervals of length
x
,
x
,
(\frac{L}{2}-x)
and
(\frac{L}{2}-x)
.

\displaystyle(K\ast u)_{x}(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\frac{L}{2}}^{\frac{L}{2}}K_{L}(y)\frac{d}{dx}u(y-x)dy

\displaystyle=\int_{-\frac{L}{2}}^{\frac{L}{2}}-K_{L}(y)u_{x}(y-x)dy

\displaystyle=(\int_{-\frac{L}{2}}^{-\frac{L}{2}+x}+\int_{-\frac{L}{2}+x}^{\frac{L}{2}+2x})+(\int_{-\frac{L}{2}+2x}^{x}+\int_{x}^{\frac{L}{2}})(-K_{L}(y)u_{x}(y-x))dy

\displaystyle=I+II

Now on each part, we reflect to another side.

\displaystyle I

\displaystyle=\int_{-\frac{L}{2}}^{-\frac{L}{2}+x}+\int_{-\frac{L}{2}+x}^{\frac{L}{2}+2x}(-K_{L}(y)u_{x}(y-x))dy

\displaystyle=\int_{-\frac{L}{2}}^{-\frac{L}{2}+x}(-K_{L}(y)u_{x}(y-x))-K_{L}(-L+2x-y)u_{x}(-L+x-y)dy

\displaystyle=\int_{-\frac{L}{2}}^{-\frac{L}{2}+x}(-K_{L}(y)u_{x}(y-x))-K_{L}(y^{\prime})u_{x}(x-y)dy

\displaystyle=\int_{-\frac{L}{2}}^{-\frac{L}{2}+x}-(K_{L}(y)-(K_{L}(y^{\prime}))(u_{x}(y-x))dy<0

Same for II.
where
\frac{y^{\prime}+y}{2}=-\frac{L}{2}+x
.
∎

Remark 6.2
.

With similar observations, we can prove the same for
L=\infty
, i.e. if
u
and
K
are bell-shaped on

then

is bell-shaped.

Theorem 6.2

(Bell-shape consistency)
.

If
u_{0}\in\mathcal{V}
is bell-shaped,
u(t)
is bell-shaped for all

Proof.

We continue from the proof of Theorem
6.1
.
Let

\mathcal{K}:=\{u\in H^{1}(\mathds{T}_{L}):\text{u is bell-shaped}\}

and suppose
u_{0}\in\mathcal{K}.
Due to Lemma
6.2
if
u\in\mathcal{K}
then
I(u)\in\mathcal{K}
.
Again, by Picard iteration,
u(t)
stays bell-shaped for

if

. Furthermore by the similar argument from the proof of Theorem

we can let

∎

6.2.
Compactness of the solutions

Asymptotic bound on
u
and
u_{x}

We investigate asymptotic pointwise bounds on the solution. It turns out the solution stays bounded not only above but also below, as long as
K
stays away from 0. We can expect that the lower bound will diminish as
L\to\infty
.

Proposition 6.1
.

Assume that
K(x)\in W^{1,1}\cap W^{1,p+1}(\mathds{T}_{L})
. Let
u\in\mathcal{V}
be a solution of (
5.2
). Then there exists
M(K,u_{0})>0
such that
\sup_{t\geq 0}\|u(x,t)\|_{\infty}<M
. Additionally, there exists
m>0
, for any
x
such that
\frac{\partial}{\partial x}u_{0}
exists,
\lim\sup_{t\to\infty}\|u_{x}(x,t)\|_{\infty}<m
.

Proof.

We want to find a pointwise threshold where
u
and
u_{x}
stop growing. Start from (
5.2
),

(6.1)

\displaystyle u^{\prime}

\displaystyle=K\ast u^{p}-c(t)u

(6.2)

\displaystyle=\int K(x-y)u(y)^{p}dy-c(t)u

(6.3)

\displaystyle\leq(\int K(x-y)^{p+1}dy)^{1/p+1}(\int u^{p+1}dy)^{p/(p+1)}-\underline{c}u,

where
\underline{c}=\inf_{t\geq 0}c(t)
. Note that
\underline{c}>0
because
u>0
and
F(u)=\frac{1}{2p}e^{-\frac{2p}{p+1}\int u^{p+1}dx}c(t)>0
is increasing according to the Proposition
5.1
.
This means

u^{\prime}(x,t)<0\quad\text{if }u(x,t)>\frac{1}{\underline{c}}\|K\|_{L^{p+1}}\|u\|^{p}_{L^{p+1}}.

By Morrey’s embedding theorem,
u\in L^{\infty}
.
Due to monotonic convergence of
\|u\|_{L^{p+1}}\to 1
as
t\to\infty
, we can conclude that

\max_{t\geq 0}\|u(x,t)\|_{\infty}\leq M=\|u_{0}\|_{\infty}+\frac{1}{\underline{c}}(1+\|u_{0}\|^{p}_{L^{p+1}})\|K\|_{L^{p+1}}.

Similarly, for any point where
u_{x}
exists and is positive,

\displaystyle u_{x}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=(K\ast u^{p})_{x}-c(t)u_{x}

\displaystyle=\int K_{x}(x-y)u(y)^{p}dy-c(t)u_{x}

\displaystyle\leq\|K_{x}\|_{L^{p+1}}\|u\|^{p}_{L^{p+1}}-\underline{c}u_{x},

and a similar argument holds for any point where
u_{x}
exists and is negative.
∎

Remark 6.3
.

In the case when
\|u(0)\|_{L^{p+1}}\leq 1
,
c(t)
is increasing. So we can obtain the explicit upper bound,

\|u(t)\|_{\infty}\leq M=\max\{\|u(x,0)\|_{\infty},\|K\|_{L^{p+1}}/c(0)\}.

In the compact spatial domain, we can use the upper bound to calculate the lower bound. This will be used to control the Riemannian metric. Naturally, the lower bound vanishes as
L\to\infty.

Corollary 6.1
.

For fixed
L<\infty
, let
u\in\mathcal{V}
be a solution of (
5.2
). Assume that
K_{L}(x)\in W^{1,1}\cap W^{1,p+1}(\mathds{T}_{L})
and there is
k>0
such that
K_{L}(x)\geq k,

\forall x\in\mathds{T}_{L}
. Then there exist
m(K,L,u_{0})>0
such that
\min_{x}u(x,t)\geq m
.

Proof.

From Proposition
6.1
, there exists
M(u_{0},K)>0
such that
u(x,t)<M
for all
t\geq 0
. Now for any
\epsilon>0
we can choose large enough
T>0
so that

1-\epsilon\leq\int u^{p+1}dx\leq M\int u^{p}dx.

Note that
\overline{c}:=\sup_{t\geq 0}c(t)
is well defined since it converges. Then,

\displaystyle u^{\prime}

\displaystyle=K\ast u^{p}-c(t)u

\displaystyle\geq k\int u^{p}dx-\overline{c}u

\displaystyle\geq\frac{(1-\epsilon)k}{M}-\overline{c}u>0,

when
u<(1-\epsilon)k/M\overline{c}
. So the result follows.
∎

Remark 6.4
.

If
\|u(0)\|_{L^{p+1}}\leq 1
, we can obtain a more explicit lower bound using Remark
6.3
and (
5.6
).
For any
\epsilon
there exists
T>0
such that for all
t>T
, we have

\min_{x}u(t,x)\geq m=\frac{(1-\epsilon)k}{M\|K\|_{L^{\frac{p+1}{2}}}}.

L^{2}
-compactness of the solutions

Since the Rellich–Kondrachov theorem is only valid on compact domain, we need more careful analysis to achieve compactness on the real line. We will take advantage of the bell-shaped solutions to show that the solution is tight in
L^{p}
-spaces for any
p>1
.

Proposition 6.2

(uniform
H^{1}
-norm)
.

Let
u\in H^{1}(\mathds{T}_{L})
be a (maximal) solution of (
5.2
). Then there exists time independent
C>0
such that
\|u(t,\cdot)\|_{H^{1}}<C.

Proof.

We used the fixed point formula (
6.5
),

(6.4)

\displaystyle u(x,t)

\displaystyle=e^{-\int_{0}^{t}c(\tau)d\tau}u_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\int_{s}^{t}c(\tau)d\tau}K*u^{p}ds.

Since we have uniform
L^{p+1}
and
L^{\infty}
norm (Proposition
5.1
, Proposition
6.1
), we have uniform
L^{2}
bound on the integrand as follows,

\displaystyle\|K*u^{p}\|_{H^{1}}\leq\|K\|_{W^{1,1}}\|u^{p}\|_{L^{2}}\leq\|K\|_{W^{1,1}}\|u\|_{L^{p+1}}^{\frac{p+1}{2}}\|u\|_{L^{\infty}}^{\frac{p-1}{2}}<\infty.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\|u\|_{H^{1}}

\displaystyle\leq\|u_{0}\|_{H^{1}}+\|\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\int_{s}^{t}c(\tau)d\tau}K*u^{p}ds\|_{H^{1}}

\displaystyle\leq\|u_{0}\|_{H^{1}}+\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\underline{c}(t-s)}\|K*u^{p}\|_{H^{1}}ds

\displaystyle=\|u_{0}\|_{H^{1}}+\underline{c}^{-1}(1-e^{-\underline{c}t})\|K*u^{p}\|_{H^{1}}<\infty,

where
\underline{c}=\inf_{t>0}c(t)>0
.
∎

When
L<\infty
, we can use the Rellich-Kondrachov theorem to directly infer that the solution is precompact in
L^{2}
. Indeed, we can show the solution is precompact in
H^{1}
. We will discuss in the next subsection with detail.
On the other hands, when
L=\infty
, we need further restriction on
u
to derive the compactness result. The following lemma will be useful to show the tightness of the bell-shaped solution.

Lemma 6.3

(Uniform
L^{1}
-norm)
.

Suppose
u_{0}\in H^{1}\cap L^{1}
then there exists
C>0
such that

\|u(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{1}}<C.

Proof.

The argument can be much simpler when
p\geq 2
. We claim that
u(t,x)
is uniformly bounded in
L^{p}
using the uniform
L^{2}
and
L^{\infty}
bound,

\int u^{p}dx\leq\|u\|_{\infty}^{p-2}\int u^{2}dx<\infty.

Now we use Grönwall type estimate on
L^{1}
norm of
u
as follows,

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\int udx=\int K*u^{p}dx-c(t)\int udx\leq\|K\|_{L^{1}}\|u(t,x)\|_{L^{p}}^{p}-\underline{c}\int udx.

The general proof starts from (
6.5
),

\displaystyle u(x,t)

\displaystyle=e^{-\int_{0}^{t}c(\tau)d\tau}u_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\int_{s}^{t}c(\tau)d\tau}K*u^{p}ds.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
be such that
p^{n}\geq 2
so that we have uniform control in
L^{p^{n}}
, then

\displaystyle\|K*u^{p}\|_{L^{p^{n-1}}}

\displaystyle\leq\|K\|_{L^{1}}\|u^{p}\|_{L^{p^{n-1}}}

\displaystyle\leq\|K\|_{L^{1}}\|u\|_{L^{p^{n}}}^{p}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\|u\|_{L^{p^{n-1}}}\leq\|e^{-\int_{0}^{t}c(\tau)d\tau}u_{0}\|_{L^{p^{n-1}}}+\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\int_{s}^{t}c(\tau)d\tau}\|K*u^{p}\|_{L^{p^{n-1}}}ds

\displaystyle\leq e^{-\underline{c}t}\|u_{0}\|_{L^{p^{n-1}}}+\underline{c}^{-1}(1-e^{-\underline{c}t})\|K*u^{p}\|_{L^{p^{n-1}}},

where
\underline{c}:=\inf_{t\geq 0}c(t)>0
, constructed by Proposition
5.1
. So we have uniform control in
L^{p^{n-1}}
.
We recur the argument until we reach
n=0
to get
L^{1}
control.
∎

Using the previous lemma, we will show that the solution is precompact in
L^{2}
. Prior to that, we show some interesting consequences of the lemma. Directly, we can show that the solution cannot converge uniformly to
0
.

Corollary 6.2
.

Suppose
u_{0}\in H^{1}\cap L^{1}
then

\lim\inf_{t\to\infty}\|u(t,\cdot)\|_{\infty}>0
.

Proof.

For the sake of contradiction, let’s assume that
\lim\inf_{t\to\infty}\|u(t,\cdot)\|_{\infty}=0
.

\displaystyle c(t)=\int u^{p}K*u^{p}dx\leq\|u(t,\cdot)\|_{\infty}^{p}\int K*u^{p}dx\leq\|u(t,\cdot)\|_{\infty}^{p}\|K\|_{L^{1}}\|u(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{p}}^{p}.

Since we achieve the uniform control on
L^{p}
norm and
c(t)
converges to a positive constant this leads to the contradiction.
∎

The next corollary will be useful to show
L^{2}
-compactness.

Corollary 6.3

(
L^{q}
tightness)
.

Let
q>1
and
u(t,x)
be a solution of (
5.2
) such that
u_{0}\in H^{1}\cap L^{1}
is bell shaped. Then for any
\epsilon>0
there exists
R>0
such that

\int_{|x|>R}u^{q}dx<\epsilon.

Proof.

Note that
u(t,x)
is bell-shaped. Using the previous lemma and the Chebyshev’s inequality, for
x>0

\displaystyle 2xu(t,x)\leq\|u(t,x)\|_{L^{1}}<C.

Therefore,

u(t,x)\leq C/2x

\int_{|x|>R}u^{q}dx<(\frac{C}{2})^{q}\int_{|x|>R}1/|x|^{q}dx=\tilde{C}R^{-q+1}<\epsilon.

∎

Now we show that any bell-shaped solutions are precompact in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
.

Theorem 6.3

(Compactness on
\mathbb{R}
)
.

Let
u(t,x)
be a solution of (
5.2
). If
u_{0}\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
is bell-shaped then
\{u(t,x)\}_{t\geq 0}
is precompact in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
.

Proof.

Since
u(t,\cdot)
is bell-shaped for all
t\geq 0
by Theorem
6.2
, we can use Helly’s selection theorem to have a (further) subsequence
\{u(t_{k})\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
and the pointwise limit
\tilde{u}
. Recall that we have uniform bound on
u
and
u_{x}
by Proposition
6.1
. This means, by Arzela-Ascoli theorem, on any compact interval, the (subsequential) convergence is uniform and
\tilde{u}
is continuous on
\mathbb{R}
.

Now we claim that
\|u(t_{k})-\tilde{u}\|_{L^{2}}\to 0
as
k\to\infty
. For given
\epsilon>0
, we use Corollary
6.3
to find
R>0
to have small
L^{2}
-tail bound and we choose
k\in\mathbb{N}
large enough to have small
L^{2}
-bound, i.e.,

\|u(t_{k})-\tilde{u}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq\int_{|x|\leq R}(u(t_{k})-\tilde{u})^{2}dx+\int_{|x|>R}(u(t_{k})-\tilde{u})^{2}dx<\epsilon.\qed

H^{1}
-compactness of the solutions (
L<\infty
)

In the nice case, when
L<\infty
and
u_{x}
is uniformly bounded, we can use Arzela-Ascoli’s theorem to directly infer the compactness of solutions.

Corollary 6.4
.

Assume
L<\infty
and let
u\in\mathcal{V}
be a solution of (
5.2
) such that
u_{x}\in L^{\infty}
. Then
\{u(x,t)\}_{t\geq 0}
is precompact in
C_{0}(\mathds{T}_{L})
.

Note that we can relax the assumption of uniform bound on
u_{x}
, using usual diagonalization trick. In fact, we can choose another approach to prove compactness of the solution, even with
L^{2}
-initial data.

Recall that in semi-linear heat equation, the Laplacian operator provides smoothing action resulting the solution trajectory to be precompact. Even though not as powerful as the Laplacian operator, convolution with a nice function is renowned for regularization. From the proof of Theorem
6.1
we have

(6.5)

\displaystyle u(x,t)

\displaystyle=e^{-\int_{0}^{t}c(\tau)d\tau}u_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\int_{s}^{t}c(\tau)d\tau}K*u^{p}ds.

Although we cannot claim that
u(x,t)
is smoother than the initial data
u_{0}
, we can see that
u(x,t)
has a smoother part.
Indeed, the fixed point formula (
6.5
) suggests that
u
consists of
u_{0}
with a scaling factor and a smoother trajectory created by the convolution with
K
. It is immediate to see that the first part of (
6.5
) converges to
0
as
t\to\infty
, since
c(t)
converges to a positive constant as
t\to\infty
. So the first part of (
6.5
) is precompact. Therefore once we prove the second part of (
6.5
) is precompact, we prove that
\{u(t,x)\}_{t\geq 0}
is precompact.

Theorem 6.4
.

Let
L<\infty
and
u(t,x)
be a solution of (
5.2
). If
u_{0}\in H^{1}(\mathds{T}_{L})
then
\{u(t,x)\}_{t\geq 0}
is precompact in
H^{1}(\mathds{T}_{L})
.

Proof.

from (
6.5
) and the previous discussion, it is enough to show that the trajectory
t\mapsto\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\int_{s}^{t}2pE(u)d\tau}K*u^{p}ds
is compact in
H^{1}(\mathds{T}_{L})
.
From Proposition
5.1
we know that
c(t)
stays positive and converges, i.e., there exists
\underline{c}:=\inf_{t\geq 0}c(t)>0
. Without specifying the norm, we have

\displaystyle\|\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\int_{s}^{t}c(\tau)d\tau}K*u^{p}ds\|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\underline{c}(t-s)}\|K*u^{p}(s,\cdot)\|ds

\displaystyle=\underline{c}^{-1}(1-e^{-\underline{c}t})\sup_{\tau\geq 0}\|K*u^{p}(\tau,\cdot)\|.

So once we achieve time independent control on the integrand, we can control the integral term likewise.

Firstly we use Proposition
5.1
and Proposition
6.1
to achieve uniform
H^{1}
bound,

\displaystyle\|K*u^{p}\|_{H^{1}}

\displaystyle=\|K*u^{p}\|_{L^{2}}+\|K_{x}*u^{p}\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\|K\|_{W^{1,1}}\|u^{p}\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\|K\|_{W^{1,1}}\|u\|_{L^{p+1}}^{\frac{p+1}{2}}\|u\|_{\infty}^{\frac{p-1}{2}}<\infty.

Since
u_{0}\in\mathcal{V}
, by (
6.5
) we have uniform
H^{1}
bound on
u
.

Finally, we claim that
\|K*u^{p}\|_{H^{2}}<C
for a time independent
C>0
. It is enough to check
\|K_{x}*(u^{p-1}u_{x})\|_{L^{2}}.

\displaystyle\|K_{x}*(u^{p-1}u_{x})\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle=\|\int K_{x}(x-y)u^{p-1}(y)u_{x}(y)dy\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\|K\|_{W^{1,1}}\|\|u\|_{\infty}^{p-1}\|u\|_{H^{1}}<\infty.

So the trajectory
t\mapsto\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\int_{s}^{t}2pE(u)d\tau}K*u^{p}ds
is uniformly bounded in
H^{2}
, and compact in
H
. Therefore by the Rellich–Kondrachov theorem we have the result.
∎

6.3.
Nontrivial wave profiles

The famous LaSalle’s invariance principle states that any precompact of gradient flow of
C^{1}
energy
E
approaches to the set of critical points of
E
. Since we achieve the compactness, one can expect that the subsequential limit point to be a critical point. Recall that the uniform lower bound holds only on the compact domain, so LaSalle’s principle cannot be directly applied. So we will perform careful analysis to show the limit point on
\mathbb{R}
satisfies the fixed point, which shows the existence of the solitary wave profile. Additionally, we show the instability of the constant solution, so that we have nontrivial limit point with the choice of the initial data.

Instability of constant solutions

Note that
\bar{u}
is a constant satisfying
L\bar{u}^{p+1}=1
,
\bar{u}
is a fixed point of (
5.2
). Considering that we aim to calculate nontrivial equilibria of (
5.2
), we need to investigate the stability of
\bar{u}.

Since (
5.2
) is linear in
K
, without loss of generality we assume that
\int K(y)dy=1.

In this case,

2pE(\bar{u})=\int_{\mathds{T}_{L}}\bar{u}^{p}K*\bar{u}^{p}=L\bar{u}^{2p}=\bar{u}^{p-1},

Recall that

\displaystyle DF(u)(v)=e^{-\frac{2p}{p+1}\int u^{p+1}dx}\int u^{p-1}(K*u^{p}-c(t)u)vdx.

The Hessian at an equilibrium
\tilde{u}
is the following,

\displaystyle D^{2}F(\tilde{u})(v,w)

\displaystyle=D(e^{-\frac{2p}{p+1}\int\tilde{u}^{p+1}dx})(w)\int\tilde{u}^{p-1}\cancelto{0}{(K*\tilde{u}^{p}-c(t)u)}vdx

\displaystyle+e^{-\frac{2p}{p+1}\int\tilde{u}^{p+1}dx}D(\int\tilde{u}^{p-1}(K*\tilde{u}^{p}-c(t)\tilde{u})vdx)(w)

\displaystyle=e^{-\frac{2p}{p+1}\int\tilde{u}^{p+1}dx}\int(p-1)\tilde{u}^{p-2}w\cancelto{0}{(K*\tilde{u}^{p}-c(t)\tilde{u})}vdx

\displaystyle+e^{-\frac{2p}{p+1}\int\tilde{u}^{p+1}dx}\int\tilde{u}^{p-1}\Big(pK*(\tilde{u}^{p-1}w)

\displaystyle-2p(\int\tilde{u}^{p-1}(K*\tilde{u}^{p})wdx)\tilde{u}-2pE(\tilde{u})w\Big)vdx

\displaystyle=\langle v,D^{2}F(\tilde{u})w\rangle_{L^{2}}.

(6.6)

\displaystyle D^{2}F(\bar{u})(v)=e^{-\frac{2p}{p+1}}\bar{u}^{2p-2}(pK*v-\frac{2p}{L}\int_{\mathds{T}_{L}}vdy-v).

Note that, the constant function
\mathds{1}
is an eigenvector with negative eigenvalue,

D^{2}F(\bar{u})(\mathds{1})=-e^{-\frac{2p}{p+1}}\bar{u}^{2p-2}(p+1)\mathds{1}.

In fact, other possible eigenvectors pointing outside of the invariant manifold
\mathcal{M}
have the same negative eigenvalue. Let’s take a look at the following eigenvalue problem,

\displaystyle K*v-\frac{2}{L}\int_{\mathds{T}_{L}}vdy

\displaystyle=\lambda v

\displaystyle\int_{\mathds{T}_{L}}(K*v-\frac{2}{L}\int_{\mathds{T}_{L}}vdy)dx

\displaystyle=\lambda\int_{\mathds{T}_{L}}vdx

\displaystyle-\int_{\mathds{T}_{L}}vdx

\displaystyle=\lambda\int_{\mathds{T}_{L}}vdx.

So we can conclude that, either

\lambda=-1\text{ or }\int_{\mathds{T}_{L}}vdx=0.

Note that the set of mean zero functions corresponds to the tangent manifold at the constant equilibrium
\bar{u}
, i.e.,
T_{\bar{u}}\mathcal{M}=\{v\in\mathcal{V}:\int_{\mathds{T}_{L}}vdx=0\}
.
So for such
v\in T_{\bar{u}}\mathcal{M}
,

(6.7)

\displaystyle D^{2}F(\bar{u})(v)=e^{-\frac{2p}{p+1}}\bar{u}^{2p-2}(pK*v-v).

We claim that
D^{2}F(\bar{u})
has positive eigenvalues, which implies the instability of the constant solution
\bar{u}.

Spectrum of convolution operator

Note that for
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
,
e^{i\lambda x}
is an eigenvector of convolution operators,

\displaystyle K*e^{i\lambda x}

\displaystyle=\int e^{i\lambda(x-y)}K(y)dy

\displaystyle=e^{i\lambda x}\int e^{-i\lambda y}K(y)dy

\displaystyle=\hat{K}(\lambda)e^{i\lambda x}.

Recall that the perturbation
\phi
we chose has mean zero, i.e.
\int\phi dx=0
. In order to satisfy
\int_{\mathds{T}_{L}}e^{i\lambda y}dy=0
, or
e^{i\lambda x}\in H^{1}(\mathds{T}_{L})
,
\lambda
must be resonant with
L
, i.e.,
\lambda=\frac{2n\pi}{L}
for
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Since
K
is even, we can check that the corresponding eigenvalue
\hat{K}(\lambda)
of the eigenvector
e^{i\lambda x}
is positive,

\displaystyle\hat{K}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\int(\cos(\lambda y)-i\sin(\lambda y))K(y)dy

\displaystyle=\int\cos(\lambda y)K(y)dy>0.

Therefore, the eigenvalues of
D^{2}F(\bar{u})
on
\mathcal{M}
is
e^{-\frac{2p}{p+1}}\bar{u}^{2p-2}(p\hat{K}(\frac{2n\pi}{L})-1)
.
Because
\hat{K}(\frac{2n\pi}{L})\to 1
as
L\to\infty
, for any
p>1
there exists large
L>0
such that
\bar{u}
is a saddle point.
Moreover, every eigenvalues of
D^{2}E(\bar{u})
becomes positive as
L\to\infty
, which corresponds to the fact that
0
is a source when
L=\infty
.

Existence of Nontrivial Wave Profiles

One of the benefits of a gradient system is that by LaSalle’s invariance principle for any bounded solution
u(t)
of gradient flow of
C^{1}
energy, we have
\lim_{t\to\infty}\|u^{\prime}(t)\|_{\mathcal{H}}=0
. So by showing the compactness of trajectory, we infer the existence of periodic and solitary wave profiles. Furthermore, we can show that there is a nontrivial wave profile by starting with
u_{0}=\bar{u}+\delta\cos(\frac{2\pi}{L}x)
for a small enough
\delta>0
, we can conclude that there is a nontrivial equilibrium of (
5.2

Theorem 6.5

(Existence of nontrivial wave profiles)
.

For large enough

\tilde{u}\in\mathcal{M}
of (

K\ast\tilde{u}^{p}=\tilde{c}\tilde{u}

\tilde{c}>0

Proof.

1.
L<\infty
:
Let
\bar{u}
be the constant equilibrium such that

\nabla F(\bar{u})=0

We continue the argument from the discussion about the instability of

Let

Using Taylor’s theorem for small enough

\displaystyle F(\bar{u}+\delta\phi)=F(\bar{u})+\frac{1}{2}\delta^{2}\nabla^{2}F(\bar{u})(\phi,\phi)+o(\delta^{2})>F(\bar{u}).

Now we initiate the evolution starting with

Note that when

We use LaSalle’s invariance principle (Theorem 8.4.6 of

Note that since

2.

Here we provide more careful analysis for

(1)

(2)

(3)

When

.

(4)

For any

,
\|\tilde{u}^{p}(K*\tilde{u}^{p}-\tilde{c}\tilde{u})\|_{L^{2}}=0
.

(5)

.

\tilde{u}
is nontrivial.

Step (1): Claim that for any

Note that by the dissipation of

\alpha(t)\frac{d}{dt}F(u(t))=\int_{\mathbb{R}}u^{p-1}(K*u^{p}-c(t)u)^{2}dx=\frac{4}{(p+1)^{2}}\|\frac{d}{dt}u^{\frac{p+1}{2}}(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\|u^{\frac{p+1}{2}}(t)-u^{\frac{p+1}{2}}(s)\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{s}^{t}\|\frac{d}{d\tau}u^{\frac{p+1}{2}}(\tau)\|_{L^{2}}d\tau

\displaystyle=|t-s|^{1/2}\int_{s}^{t}\|\frac{d}{dt}u^{\frac{p+1}{2}}(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}d\tau

Note that since

So for any

Step (2): Claim that

\displaystyle c(t)-c(s)

\displaystyle=\int(u^{p}(t)-u^{p}(s))K*u^{p}(t)dx+\int(u^{p}(t)-u^{p}(s))K*u^{p}(s)dx

\displaystyle\leq 2\|u^{p}(t)-u^{p}(s)\|_{L^{2}}\|K*u^{p}\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq 2\|u^{p}(t)-u^{p}(s)\|_{L^{2}}\|K\|_{L^{1}}\|u\|_{L^{p+1}}^{\frac{p+1}{2}}\|u\|_{\infty}^{\frac{p-1}{2}}.

By Proposition

Step (3): Let

Therefore, by averaging

Discussion

In fact, there is an issue with applying the Łojasiewicz convergence criterion to (
5.2
).
So at present we are unable to conclude convergence of solutions as
t\to\infty.

Let
H^{1}_{+}(\mathds{T}_{L})
denote the positive cone in
H^{1}(\mathds{T}_{L})
, i.e.,

H^{1}_{+}(\mathds{T}_{L}):=\{u\in H^{1}(\mathds{T}_{L}):u(x)>0,\;\;\forall x\in H^{1}(\mathds{T}_{L})\}.

Note that
H^{1}(\mathds{T}_{L})
is a Banach algebra. So the Lyapunov functional,

F(u)=\frac{1}{2p}e^{-\frac{2p}{p+1}\int u^{p+1}dx}\int u^{p}K*u^{p}dx,

is analytic in the neighborhood of
H^{1}_{+}(\mathds{T}_{L}).

The issue, however, arise from failure of the Hessian of
F

D^{2}F(\tilde{u}):w\mapsto e^{-\frac{2p}{p+1}}\tilde{u}^{p-1}\left({pK*(\tilde{u}^{p-1}w)}-{4p^{2}E(\tilde{u})(\int\tilde{u}^{p}wdx)\tilde{u}}-2pE(\tilde{u})w\right)

being semi-Fredholm.

Firstly, on
\mathbb{R}
there is no chance for
D^{2}E(\tilde{u})
to have closed range since
\tilde{u}(x)\to 0
as
x\to\pm\infty
. For example, for any
u\in\mathcal{V}
,
\big\{u^{p-1}\mathds{1}_{\{-L,L\}}\big\}_{L>0}
is Cauchy in
\mathcal{V}^{\prime}
but
\mathds{1}\not\in\mathcal{V}
.
So let’s assume that
L<\infty
. Now since
\tilde{u}
is bounded above and below from
0
, the multiplication with
\tilde{u}
,
v\mapsto\tilde{u}v
is an isomorphism from
\mathcal{V}
to itself.
By Theorem
1.4
one can check that
is semi-Fredholm from
\mathcal{V}
to itself, but not from
\mathcal{V}
to
\mathcal{V}^{\prime}
.

On the contrary, the key to expand the Łojasiewicz inequality to infinite dimensional domain is to construct the finite dimensional copy of the energy, which can be approximated by
\|\nabla E(u)\|_{\mathcal{H}}
, like in Proposition
1.5
. When
\Pi+A:\mathcal{V}\to\mathcal{V}^{\prime}
is an isomorphism, we represent
u\sim(\Pi u,\nabla E(u))
using the isomorphism
\mathcal{N}:\mathcal{V}\to\mathcal{V}^{\prime}
such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}u=\Pi u+\nabla E(u).

The issue with (
5.2
) for applying the infinite dimensional framework in Section
1.2
of Chapter
1
is
R(\Pi+A)
is too regular to be closed in
\mathcal{V}^{\prime}
. Moreover, even if we identify
\Pi+A
as an isomorphism from
\mathcal{V}
to itself, we will end up having
\|\nabla{E}(u)\|_{\mathcal{V}}
, not in control of
\mathcal{H}.

Chapter 7
Regularized concentration-dispersion dynamics

In this chapter, we propose and analyze a modification of concentration-dispersion dynamics with small
\epsilon>0
, as follows.

Regularized concentration-dispersion equation

(7.1)

\left\{\begin{aligned} \;\;\frac{d}{dt}u(t,x)&=\epsilon(u^{p})_{xx}+K\ast u^{p}-c(t)u\\
c(t)&=\int u^{p}K\ast u^{p}-\epsilon[(u^{p})_{x}]^{2}dx,\end{aligned}\right.

where
K(x)>0
for all
x\in\mathds{T}_{L}
and
K(x)\in W^{1,1}\cap W^{1,p+1}(\mathds{T}_{L})
.
We will use the gradient structure of the regularized concentration-dispersion equation (
7.1
) to prove convergence of solutions to a nontrivial equilibrium. From now on we consider only
L<\infty.

Well-posedness

For local well-posendess with positive initial data, we refer Chapter 8 of
[
46
]
, and set
X=C(\mathds{T}_{L})
and
D=C^{2}(\mathds{T}_{L})
. Considering singular aspect of solutions of porous medium equations, global well-posedness is not trivial. By combining the local well-posedness, regularity (Theorem 8.1.1, Theorem 8.3.4 of
[
46
]
) and the
C^{2}
-compactness result in the section 2, we deduce regularity up to
C^{5}(\mathds{T}_{L})
. Thus for any
u_{0}\in C^{5}(\mathds{T}_{L})
initial data, we can choose sufficiently small
\epsilon>0
to achieve global well-posedness in
C^{2}(\mathds{T}_{L})
by pointwise maximum principle and maximal solution argument (Proposition 8.2.1 of
[
46
]
).

7.1.
Gradient structure

Based on those regularity properties, we show the asymptotic bound of the solution
u(t,x)
.
Prior to show that the solution stay above from
0
uniformly, we show the solution
u(t,x)
stays positive. In this way, (
7.1
) is well-defined.

Proposition 7.1

(Positivity)
.

Let
u(t,x)\in C^{2}(\mathds{T}_{L})
be a solution of (
7.1
). If
u(0,x)>0
for all
x\in\mathds{T}_{L}
then
u(t,x)>0
for all
x\in\mathds{T}_{L}
and
t>0.

Proof.

Let
\beta(t):=\exp{(\int_{0}^{t}c(s)ds)}
and
v(t,x):=\beta(t)u(t,x)
. We claim that for all
t\geq 0
,

v(t,x)\geq\min_{x}v(0,x).

Suppose at
t_{0}>0
,
x_{0}=\arg\min_{x}v(t_{0},x)
, then

\displaystyle\frac{dv}{dt}(t_{0},x_{0})=\beta^{1-p}(t)\left(\epsilon(v^{p})_{xx}+K\ast v^{p}\right)\geq\beta^{1-p}(t)K\ast v^{p}>0.

In particular,
\{v\in C^{2}(\mathds{T}_{L}):v>0\}
is an invariant set of (
7.1
). So for all
t>0
,

u(t,x)=\beta^{-1}(t)v(t,x)>0.\qed

Important functionals

We will show that (
7.1
) has a nice
L^{2}
-gradient structure. We use the following functionals.

(7.2)

\displaystyle E(u)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2p}\int u^{p}K*u^{p}-\epsilon[(u^{p})_{x}]^{2}dx,

(7.3)

\displaystyle F(u)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2p}e^{-\frac{2p}{p+1}\int u^{p+1}dx}\int u^{p}K*u^{p}-\epsilon[(u^{p})_{x}]^{2}dx,

(7.4)

\displaystyle=:E(u)/\alpha(t)

The derivative of
F
shows that (
7.1
) is a gradient-like system of the energy
F.

\displaystyle\alpha(t)DF(u)(v)

\displaystyle=DE(u)(v)-E(u)\frac{2p}{p+1}D(\int u^{p+1}dx)(v)

\displaystyle=\int u^{p-1}vK*u^{p}-\epsilon(u^{p-1}v)_{x}(u^{p})_{x}dx-2pE(u)\int u^{p}u^{\prime}dx

(7.5)

\displaystyle=\int u^{p-1}(\epsilon(u^{p})_{xx}+K*u^{p}-c(t)u)vdx.

We show the convergence properties of the functionals.

Proposition 7.2
.

Let
u(t,x)\in L^{p+1}(\mathds{T}_{L})
be a solution of (
5.2
) such that
u_{0}(x)>0
for all
x\in\mathds{T}_{L}
. Assume that
E(u_{0})>0
, then the following functionals have convergent properties as
t\to\infty:

(1)

F(u)=E(u)/\alpha(t)
is nondecreasing,

(2)

\|u\|_{L^{p+1}}
converges monotonically to 1,

(3)

E(u)
stays positive and converges to a positive constant.

Additionally, if
\|u_{0}\|_{L^{p+1}}\leq 1
then
E(u)
is nondecreasing as well.

Proof.

Firstly, we prove monotonic convergence of
F
.

F
is nondecreasing because the time derivative of
F
is nonnegative, i.e.,

\displaystyle F^{\prime}(u(t))e^{\frac{2p}{p+1}\int u^{p+1}dx}

\displaystyle=E^{\prime}(u)-E(u)\frac{2p}{p+1}(\int u^{p+1}dx)^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\int u^{p-1}(K*u^{p}+\epsilon(u^{p})_{xx}u^{\prime}dx-2pE(u)\int u^{p}u^{\prime}dx

\displaystyle=\int u^{p-1}(\epsilon(u^{p})_{xx}+K*u^{p}-c(t)u)^{2}dx\geq 0.

Since
E(u_{0})>0
implies
F(u_{0})>0
and

(7.6)

F(u)=e^{\frac{-2p}{p+1}\int u^{p+1}dx}E(u)\leq E(u)=2pc(t),

we have
\inf_{t\geq 0}c(t)>0
. So the monotonic convergence of
L^{p+1}
norm can be shown by taking time-differentiation,

(7.7)

\frac{1}{p+1}(\int u^{p+1}dx)^{\prime}=c(t)\big(1-\int u^{p+1}dx\big).

We can conclude

\lim_{t\to\infty}\|u(t)\|_{L^{p+1}}\to 1.

Now we claim that
E(u)
is bounded above.

\displaystyle 2pE(u)

\displaystyle\leq\int u^{p}K\ast u^{p}dx

\displaystyle\leq\|u\|^{p}_{L^{p+1}}\|K*u^{p}\|_{L^{p+1}}

\displaystyle\leq\|K\|_{L^{\frac{p+1}{2}}}\|u\|^{2p}_{L^{p+1}}

(7.8)

\displaystyle\leq(1+\|u_{0}\|_{L^{p+1}})^{2p}\|K\|_{L^{\frac{p+1}{2}}}<\infty.

Therefore
E(u(t))
converges to a positive constant as
t\to\infty
and
F
converges monotonically.
∎

7.2.
Asymptotic bound on
u
and
C^{2}
-compactness

We open this section with doing the same pointwise estimate as in the original concentration-dispersion dynamics.

Proposition 7.3
.

Let
u\in\mathcal{V}
be a solution of (

). Then there exists
M(K,u_{0})>0
such that
\sup_{t\geq 0}\|u(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}}<M

Proof.

We use similar argument in the proof of Proposition

. Since
(u^{p})_{xx}\leq 0
at the maximizer,

(7.9)

\displaystyle u^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\epsilon(u^{p})_{xx}+K\ast u^{p}-c(t)u

(7.10)

\displaystyle\leq\int K(x-y)u(y)^{p}dy-c(t)u

(7.11)

\displaystyle\leq(\int K(x-y)^{p+1}dy)^{1/p+1}(\int u^{p+1}dy)^{p/(p+1)}-\underline{c}u.

where
\underline{c}=\inf_{t\geq 0}c(t)

This means

Due to monotonic convergence of

as

\max_{t\geq 0}\|u(t,x)\|_{\infty}\leq\|u_{0}\|_{\infty}+\frac{1}{\underline{c}}(1+\|u_{0}\|^{p}_{L^{p+1}})\|K\|_{L^{p+1}}.

∎

In the compact spatial domain, we can use the upper bound to calculate the lower bound. This will be used to control the Riemannian metric. Naturally, the lower bound vanishes as

Corollary 7.1
.

For fixed
L<\infty
, let
u
be a solution of (
5.2
) with

such that
K(x)\geq k,

\forall x\in\mathds{T}_{L}

such that
\min_{x}u(x,t)\geq m
.

Proof.

From Proposition
7.3

M(u_{0},K)>0
such that

for all
t\geq 0
. Now for any
\delta>0
we can choose large enough

1-\delta\leq\int u^{p+1}dx\leq M\int u^{p}dx.

Note that
\overline{c}:=\sup_{t\geq 0}c(t)
is well-defined since it converges. Then at the minimizer,

\displaystyle u^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\epsilon(u^{p})_{xx}+K\ast u^{p}-c(t)u

\displaystyle\geq k\int u^{p}dx-\overline{c}u

\displaystyle\geq\frac{(1-\delta)k}{M}-\overline{c}u>0,

when
u<(1-\delta)k/M\overline{c}
. So the result follows.
∎

C^{2}
-compactness

Recall that the original concentration dispersion equations (
5.2
), provide precompact solution even without smoothing effect.
For the regularized equations,
we can derive the following: for any regular enough initial data, we can make
\epsilon
small enough to make the trajectory precompact in
C^{2}
. Recall that we need
C^{2}
-compactness to achieve global well-posedness (cf.,
[
46
]
). We assume
L<\infty
and analyze asymptotic pointwise bound of
u_{x}
,
u_{xx}
and
u_{xxx}
.

Theorem 7.1
.

Let
L<\infty
. For any
u_{0}\in C^{5}(\mathds{T}_{L})
with
u_{0}(t,\cdot)>0
and
\|u_{0}\|_{L^{p+1}}\leq 1
, there exists
\epsilon_{0}>0
such that for any
\epsilon\in(0,\epsilon_{0}]
the solution
\{u(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
of (
7.1
) with
u(0)=u_{0}
has uniformly bounded
C^{3}
-norm. In particular,
\{u(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
precompact in
C^{2}(\mathds{T}_{L}).

Proof.

We will firstly calculate pointwise time evolution of
u_{x}
and
u_{xx}
and later argue how to deduce the conclusion.

The derivatives of each term on (
7.1
) are as follows,

\displaystyle\partial_{x}(u^{p})

\displaystyle=pu^{p-1}u_{x}

\displaystyle\partial^{2}_{x}(u^{p})

\displaystyle=p(p-1)u^{p-2}u_{x}^{2}+pu^{p-1}u_{xx}

\displaystyle\partial^{3}_{x}(u^{p})

\displaystyle=p(p-1)(p-2)u^{p-3}u_{x}^{3}+3p(p-1)u^{p-2}u_{x}u_{xx}+pu^{p-1}u_{xxx}

\displaystyle\partial^{4}_{x}(u^{p})

\displaystyle=p(p-1)(p-2)(p-3)u^{p-4}u_{x}^{4}+6p(p-1)(p-2)u^{p-3}u_{x}^{2}u_{xx}

\displaystyle\quad+3p(p-1)u^{p-2}u_{xx}^{2}+4p(p-1)u^{p-2}u_{x}u_{xxx}+pu^{p-1}u_{xxxx}.

\displaystyle\partial^{5}_{x}(u^{p})

\displaystyle=p(p-1)(p-2)(p-3)(p-4)u^{p-5}u_{x}^{5}+10p(p-1)(p-2)u^{p-4}u_{x}^{3}u_{xx}

\displaystyle\quad+15p(p-1)(p-2)u^{p-3}u_{x}u_{xx}^{2}+10p(p-1)(p-2)u^{p-3}u_{x}^{2}u_{xxx}

\displaystyle\quad+10p(p-1)u^{p-2}u_{xx}u_{xxx}+5p(p-1)u^{p-2}u_{x}u_{xxxx}+pu^{p-1}u_{xxxxx}.

\displaystyle|\partial_{x}(K*u^{p})|

\displaystyle=|K_{x}*u^{p}|\leq\|K\|_{W^{1}}\|u^{p}\|_{L^{\infty}}

\displaystyle|\partial^{2}_{x}(K*u^{p})|

\displaystyle=|K_{x}*(u^{p-1}u_{x})|\leq\|K\|_{W^{1}}\|u^{p-1}\|_{L^{\infty}}\|u_{x}\|_{L^{\infty}}.

Since
\|u_{0}\|_{L^{p+1}}\leq 1
,
c(t)
is increasing, so

c(t)=\int u^{p}K\ast u^{p}-\epsilon[(u^{p})_{x}]^{2}dx\geq c(0).

Let
c(0)=\alpha-\epsilon\beta
where,

\alpha=\int u_{0}^{p}K\ast u_{0}^{p}dx,\qquad\beta=\int[(u_{0}^{p})_{x}]^{2}dx.

We use Proposition
7.3
and Proposition
7.1
to control the powers of
u
.

1.
u_{x}
control: Suppose for a fixed
t>0
, there is a point
x_{0}\in\mathds{T}_{L}
,
u_{x}(t,x_{0})
is positive and reached maximum so that
u_{xx}=(u_{x})_{x}=0
and
u_{xxx}=(u_{x})_{xx}\leq 0
. Then from (
7.1
) there exist constants
A,B\in\mathbb{R}
such that

\partial_{t}u_{x}\leq\epsilon Au_{x}^{3}+B-(\alpha-\epsilon\beta)u_{x}.

Note that as
\epsilon
gets smaller, pointwise invariant interval for
u_{x}
gets larger.
Thus we can find small enough
\epsilon_{0}>0
so that
\limsup_{t}u_{x}(t,\cdot)
is bounded. We can use the similar argument when
u_{x}
is negative and reached minimum. Therefore, for
\epsilon\in(0,\epsilon_{0}]
we can say
u_{x}(t)\leq\max\{\|u_{x}(0,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}},1+B/\alpha\}
.

2.
u_{xx}
control: Suppose
u_{xx}
is positive and reached maximum so that
u_{xxx}=(u_{xx})_{x}=0
and
u_{xxxx}=(u_{xx})_{xx}\leq 0
. Then from (
7.1
) there exist constants, possibly depend on
\|u_{x}\|_{L^{\infty}}
,
A^{\prime},B^{\prime},D\in\mathbb{R}
such that

\partial_{t}u_{xx}\leq\epsilon A^{\prime}u_{xx}^{2}-(\alpha-\epsilon B^{\prime})u_{xx}+D.

Since we already achieved
\epsilon
and
t
independent control on
\|u_{x}\|_{L^{\infty}}
we continue the same argument. As
\epsilon
gets smaller, pointwise invariant interval for
u_{xx}
gets larger.
Thus we can find, possibly smaller,
\epsilon_{0}>0
so that
\limsup_{t}u_{xx}(t,\cdot)
is bounded. We can use the similar argument when
u_{xx}
is negative and reached minimum. In particular, since the domain is compact, we have uniform
H^{2}
-norm and by Rellich–Kondrachov theorem.

3.
u_{xxx}
control: Suppose
u_{xxx}
is positive and reached maximum so that
u_{xxxx}=(u_{xxx})_{x}=0
and
u_{xxxxx}=(u_{xxx})_{xx}\leq 0
. Then from (
7.1
) there exist constants, depend on
\|u_{x}\|_{L^{\infty}}
and
\|u_{xx}\|_{L^{\infty}}
,
A^{\prime\prime},B^{\prime\prime}\in\mathbb{R}
such that

\partial_{t}u_{xxx}\leq A^{\prime\prime}-(\alpha-\epsilon B^{\prime\prime})u_{xxx}.

We continue with the similar argument as before to make conclusion.
∎

7.3.
Convergence analysis

Let
L<\infty
and
\mathcal{V}=H^{1}(\mathds{T}_{L})
,
\mathcal{H}=L^{2}(\mathds{T}_{L})
so that

\mathcal{V}\subset\mathcal{H}\subset\mathcal{V}^{\prime}.

We follow the framework presented in Section
1.2
of Chapter
1
.

Analyticity

We prove convergence in compact spatial domain where
L<\infty
. Let
H^{1}_{+}(\mathds{T}_{L})
denote the positive cone in
H^{1}(\mathds{T}_{L})
, i.e.,

H^{1}_{+}(\mathds{T}_{L}):=\{u\in H^{1}(\mathds{T}_{L}):u(x)>0,\;\;\forall x\in H^{1}(\mathds{T}_{L})\}.

Note that
H^{1}(\mathds{T}_{L})
is a Banach algebra. So the Lyapunov functional,

\displaystyle F(u)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2p}e^{-\frac{2p}{p+1}\int u^{p+1}dx}\int u^{p}K*u^{p}-\epsilon[(u^{p})_{x}]^{2}dx,

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2p}e^{-\frac{2p}{p+1}\int u^{p+1}dx}\int u^{p}K*u^{p}-\epsilon p^{2}u^{2p-2}u_{x}^{2}dx.

is analytic in the neighborhood of
H^{1}_{+}(\mathds{T}_{L}).
In order to show that
F
satisfies the Łojaisewicz inequality, let’s analyze the derivatives of
F.

Hessian of F

Recall that

\displaystyle DF(u)(v)=e^{-\frac{2p}{p+1}\int u^{p+1}dx}\int u^{p-1}\Big[\epsilon(u^{p})_{xx}+K*u^{p}-c(t)u\Big]vdx.

The Hessian at an equilibrium
\tilde{u}
is the following,

\displaystyle D^{2}F(\tilde{u})(v,w)

\displaystyle=D(e^{-\frac{2p}{p+1}\int\tilde{u}^{p+1}dx})(w)\int\tilde{u}^{p-1}\cancelto{0}{\Big[\epsilon(\tilde{u}^{p})_{xx}+K*\tilde{u}^{p}-c(t)u\Big]}vdx

\displaystyle+e^{-\frac{2p}{p+1}\int\tilde{u}^{p+1}dx}D(\int\tilde{u}^{p-1}\Big[\epsilon(\tilde{u}^{p})_{xx}+K*\tilde{u}^{p}-c(t)\tilde{u}\Big]vdx)(w)

\displaystyle=e^{-\frac{2p}{p+1}\int\tilde{u}^{p+1}dx}\int(p-1)\tilde{u}^{p-2}w\cancelto{0}{\Big[\epsilon(\tilde{u}^{p})_{xx}+K*\tilde{u}^{p}-c(t)\tilde{u}\Big]}vdx

\displaystyle\quad+e^{-\frac{2p}{p+1}\int\tilde{u}^{p+1}dx}\int\tilde{u}^{p-1}\bigg[\epsilon p(\tilde{u}^{p-1}w)_{xx}+pK*(\tilde{u}^{p-1}w)

\displaystyle\quad-2p\Big\{\int\tilde{u}^{p-1}(\epsilon(\tilde{u}^{p})_{xx}+K*\tilde{u}^{p})wdx\Big\}\tilde{u}-2pE(\tilde{u})w\bigg]vdx

\displaystyle=\langle v,D^{2}F(\tilde{u})w\rangle_{L^{2}}.

Note that
\tilde{c}=\lim_{t\to\infty}c(t)=2pE(\tilde{u})
so that,

\epsilon(\tilde{u}^{p})_{xx}+K*\tilde{u}^{p}=\tilde{c}\tilde{u}.

We can identify
D^{2}F(\tilde{u}):\mathcal{V}\longrightarrow\mathcal{V}^{\prime}
as follows,

D^{2}F(\tilde{u}):w\mapsto e^{-\frac{2p}{p+1}}\tilde{u}^{p-1}\left(\epsilon p(\tilde{u}^{p-1}w)_{xx}+{pK*(\tilde{u}^{p-1}w)}-{2p\tilde{c}(\int\tilde{u}^{p}wdx)\tilde{u}}-\tilde{c}w\right).

Since
\tilde{u}\in C^{\infty}
is bounded above, and below away from
0
, the multiplication with
\tilde{u}
,
v\mapsto\tilde{u}v
is an isomorphism from
\mathcal{V}
to
\mathcal{V}
, and from
\mathcal{V}^{\prime}
to
\mathcal{V}^{\prime}
. As we discussed in Proposition
3.1
,
\Delta:\mathcal{V}\to\mathcal{V}^{\prime}
with periodic boundary condition is semi-Fredholm. Therefore,

w\mapsto\tilde{u}^{p-1}(\tilde{u}^{p-1}w)_{xx}

is semi-Fredholm from
\mathcal{V}
to
\mathcal{V}^{\prime}
.
So by Theorem
1.4

D^{2}F(\tilde{u})\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{V};\mathcal{V}^{\prime})
is semi-Fredholm.

Convergence result

We use Theorem
1.6
and Theorem
1.7
to deduce the convergence result as follows.

Theorem 7.2

(Convergence result)
.

Let
u(t)\in\mathcal{V}
a solution of (
5.2
). If
\{u(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
is precompact in
\mathcal{V}
, e.g., as in Theorem
7.1
, then there exists
\tilde{u}\in\mathcal{E}:=\{u\in\mathcal{V}:\nabla F(u)=0\}
such that

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\|u(t)-\tilde{u}\|_{\mathcal{V}}=0

Moreover, let
\theta
be any Łojasiewicz exponent of
F
at
\tilde{u}
. Then we have

\|u(t)-\tilde{u}\|_{\mathcal{H}}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}O(e^{-\delta t})&\text{if}\;\;\theta=\frac{1}{2},\;\;\;\;\text{for some}\;\;\delta>0\\
O(t^{-\theta/(1-2\theta)})&\text{if}\;\;0<\theta<\frac{1}{2}\end{array}\right.

Proof.

We use the uniform bound on
u
(Proposition
7.3
and Corollary
7.1
) to check the angle condition

\displaystyle\langle\nabla{F(u)},u^{\prime}\rangle

\displaystyle=\alpha(t)^{-1}\int u^{p-1}\Big[\epsilon(u^{p})_{xx}+K*u^{p}-c(t)u\Big]^{2}dx

\displaystyle\geq\sigma\|\nabla F(u)\|_{\mathcal{H}}\|u^{\prime}\|_{\mathcal{H}},

and the rate condition

\|u^{\prime}\|_{\mathcal{H}}\geq\gamma\|\nabla F(u)\|_{\mathcal{H}}.

Suppose
\tilde{u}\in\omega(u)
and let
\bar{F}=\lim_{t\to\infty}F(u(t))=F(\tilde{u})
and
\theta
be the Łojasiewicz exponent at
\tilde{u}
. Since the argument is the same with the proof of Theorem
1.7
, we conclude the proof by showing the following calculation,

\displaystyle-\frac{d}{dt}(\bar{F}-F(u(t)))^{\theta}

\displaystyle=(\bar{F}-F(u(t)))^{1-\theta}\langle\nabla{F(u)},u^{\prime}\rangle

\displaystyle\geq\sigma(\bar{F}-F(u(t)))^{1-\theta}\|\nabla F(u)\|_{\mathcal{H}}\|u^{\prime}\|_{\mathcal{H}}

\displaystyle\geq\sigma C\|u^{\prime}\|_{\mathcal{H}}.\qed

Instability of constant solutions

Note that
\bar{u}
is a constant satisfying
L\bar{u}^{p+1}=1
,
\bar{u}
is a fixed point of (
7.1
) as well as (
5.2
). So as we did in Theorem
6.5
, we can find a perturbation
\phi
at
\bar{u}
such that

F(\bar{u}+\phi)>F(\bar{u}).

Indeed, at
\bar{u}

(7.12)

\displaystyle D^{2}F(\bar{u})(v)=e^{-\frac{2p}{p+1}}\bar{u}^{2p-2}(\epsilon pv_{xx}+pK*v-\frac{2p}{L}\int_{\mathds{T}_{L}}vdy-v).

Note that, the constant function
\mathds{1}
is an eigenvector with negative eigenvalue,

D^{2}F(\bar{u})(\mathds{1})=-e^{-\frac{2p}{p+1}}\bar{u}^{2p-2}(p+1)\mathds{1}.

It is easy to see that the invariant manifold
\mathcal{M}
attracts the solutions.

For the perturbations in the tangent manifold at
\bar{u}
, i.e.,
T_{\bar{u}}\mathcal{M}=\{\phi\in\mathcal{V}:\int_{\mathds{T}_{L}}\phi dx=0\}
,

(7.13)

\displaystyle D^{2}F(\bar{u})(\phi)=e^{-\frac{2p}{p+1}}\bar{u}^{2p-2}(\epsilon p\phi_{xx}+pK*\phi-\phi).

Let
\phi_{n}(x)=\cos(\lambda_{n}x)
where
\lambda_{n}=\frac{2n\pi}{L}
then

D^{2}F(\bar{u})(\phi_{n})=e^{-\frac{2p}{p+1}}\bar{u}^{2p-2}(-\epsilon p\lambda_{n}^{2}+p\widehat{K}(\lambda_{n})-1)\phi_{n}.

So for small enough
\epsilon
,
D^{2}F(\bar{u})
have positive eigenvalues, which shows the instability of the constant solution
\bar{u}.

In conclusion, we have the following convergence result to a nontrivial equilibrium.

Theorem 7.3
.

For large enough
L>0
there exist small enough
\epsilon>0
and
\delta>0
such that the solution
u(t)
of (
7.1
) with the initial
u_{0}=\bar{u}+\delta\cos(\frac{2\pi}{L}x)
, with
L^{p+1}
-normalization if needed, converges to a nontrivial equilibrium.
