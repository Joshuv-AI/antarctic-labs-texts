Network Decoding
Allison Beemer, Altan B. Kılıç, Alberto Ravagnani

Abstract
We consider the problem of error control in a coded, multicast network, focusing on the scenario where the errors can occur only on a proper subset of the network edges. We model this problem via an adversarial noise, presenting a formal framework and a series of techniques to obtain upper and lower bounds on the network’s (1-shot) capacity, improving on the best currently known results. In particular, we show that traditional cut-set bounds are not tight in general in the presence of a restricted adversary, and that the non-tightness of these is caused precisely by the restrictions imposed on the noise (and not, as one may expect, by the alphabet size). We also show that, in sharp contrast with the typical situation within network coding, capacity cannot be achieved in general by combining linear network coding with end-to-end channel coding, not even when the underlying network has a single source and a single terminal. We finally illustrate how network decoding techniques are necessary to achieve capacity in the scenarios we examine, exhibiting capacity-achieving schemes and lower bounds for various classes of networks.



Introduction

Global propagation of interconnected devices and the ubiquity of communication demands in unsecured settings signify the importance of a unified understanding of the limits of communications in networks.
The correction of errors modeled by an adversarial noise
has been studied in a number of previous works, with results ranging from those concerning network capacity to specific code design (see
[
30
,
3
,
28
,
11
,
17
,
26
,
29
,
5
,
20
,
12
,
31
,
19
]
among many others). In this paper, we focus on the effects of a small, and yet crucial,
modification of previous models, where a malicious actor can access and alter transmissions across a
proper
subset of edges within a network. We show that not only does this modification disrupt the sharpness of known results on network capacity, but that more specialized network coding (in fact, network
decoding
) becomes necessary to achieve the capacity.

We adopt the setting of a communication network whose inputs are drawn from a finite alphabet and whose internal (hereafter referred to as intermediate) nodes may process incoming information before forwarding (this is known as network
coding
; see e.g.
[
1
,
15
,
14
]
).
We phrase our work in terms of adversarial noise, but we note that our treatment truly addresses
worst-case
errors, also providing guarantees in networks where there may be random noise, or a combination of random and adversarial noise. We assume that the adversary is omniscient in the sense that they may design their attacks given full knowledge of the network topology, of the symbols sent along all its edges,
and of the operations performed at the intermediate nodes.

Again, in contrast to most previous work in the area, we concentrate on networks with noise occurring only on a proper subset of the network edges: for example, an adversary who may see all transmitted symbols, but has limited access to edges in the network when actively corrupting symbols. We examine the 1-shot capacity of adversarial networks with these restricted adversaries, which roughly measures the number of alphabet symbols that can be sent with zero error during a single transmission round. The case of multi-shot capacity, where more than one transmission round is considered, is also interesting, but will involve a separate treatment and different techniques from our work here. Compellingly, the simple act of restricting possible error locations fundamentally alters the problem of computing the 1-shot capacity, as well as the manner in which this capacity may be achieved. This is discussed in further detail below.

This paper expands upon (and disrupts) the groundwork laid in
[
20
]
, where a combinatorial framework for adversarial networks and a generalized method for porting point-to-point coding-theoretic results to the network setting are established. The work in
[
20
]
makes a start on addressing the case of restricted adversaries, also unifying the best-known upper bounds on 1-shot capacity for such networks. These upper bounds fall under the category of cut-set bounds, where an edge-cut is taken between network source(s) and terminal(s) and a bound on capacity is derived from an induced channel that takes only the cut-set edges into account. We note that Cai and Yeung previously gave generalizations of the Hamming, Singleton, and Gibert-Varshamov bounds to the network setting using edge-cuts in
[
30
,
3
]
. The work in
[
20
]
allows for any point-to-point bound to be ported via an edge-cut.

In the case of random noise in a single-source, single-terminal network, it is well-known that the cut-set upper bound on capacity given by the Max-Flow Min-Cut Theorem may be achieved simply by forwarding information at the intermediate network nodes; see e.g.
[
6
]
. It has also been shown that in the scenarios of multiple terminals or where a malicious adversary may choose among
all
network edges, cut-set bounds may be achieved via
linear
operations (over the relevant finite field) at intermediate nodes (combined with rank-metric codes in the presence of a malicious adversary); see
[
22
,
5
,
18
]
. In prior, preliminary work in this area
[
2
]
, we demonstrated a minimal example of a network,
which we called the Diamond Network,
with a restricted adversary, where both (1) the Singleton cut-set bound (the best cut-set bound for this network) cannot be achieved, and (2) the actual 1-shot capacity cannot be achieved using linear operations at intermediate nodes. In fact, this example of network requires network
decoding
in order to achieve capacity. Via an extension of the Diamond Network, termed the Mirrored Diamond Network, we found that it is possible with restricted adversaries for the 1-shot capacity to meet the Singleton cut-set bound, but still be forced to use non-linear network codes to achieve this rate.

Generally speaking, the act of limiting the location of adversarial action is enough to put an end to the tightness of the best currently known cut-set bounds, and also to destroy the ability to achieve capacity with linear network codes in combination with end-to-end coding/decoding. This paper is the first stepping stone aimed at understanding the effects of restricting an adversary to a proper subset of the networks’ edges, and how to compute (1-shot) capacities of such adversarial networks.

The paper is organized as follows. Sections
1
and
2
introduce our problem set-up and notation. Section
3
is devoted to the Diamond Network mentioned above, which gives the smallest example illustrating our results. In Subsection
3.2
we present an information-theoretic view of our problem for the case of random rather than adversarial noise, in order to generate further intuition.
To address the more general problem at hand, we will later in the paper (in Section
7
) present a technique showing that the capacity of any network is upper bounded by the capacity of an induced, much less complicated network. The induced network utilized borrows from the original idea of cut-sets, but instead of studying the information flow through a single edge-cut, it considers the information flow between
two
edge-cuts. We call the result we obtain as an application of this idea the Double-Cut-Set Bound.
The less-complicated networks induced by the bound’s statement are introduced in Section
4
and the collection of families we study throughout the remainder of the paper are introduced in Subsection
4.3
. In Section
5
, we propose new combinatorial techniques to obtain upper bounds for the 1-shot capacities of these families, and in Section
6
, we establish lower bounds. Section
8
shows that there is a strong separation between linear and non-linear capacities, solidifying the necessity of network decoding in this setting. Finally, Section
9
is devoted to conclusions, a discussion of open problems, and possible future works.

2
Channels and Networks

In this section we include some preliminary definitions and results that will be needed throughout the entire paper. This will also allow us to establish the notation and to state the problems we will investigate in rigorous mathematical language.
This section is divided into two subsections. The first is devoted to arbitrary (adversarial) channels, while in the second we focus our attention on communication networks and their capacities.

2.1
Adversarial Channels

In our treatment, we will use the definition of (adversarial) channels proposed in
[
20
]
, based on the notion of
fan-out sets
.
This concept essentially dates back to Shannon’s fundamental paper on the zero-error capacity of a channel
[
21
]
.
Under this approach, a channel is fully specified by the collection of symbols
y
that can be received when a given symbol
x
is transmitted. Considering transition probabilities does not make sense in this model since the noise is assumed to be of an “adversarial” nature. The latter assumption conveniently models all those scenarios where
any
error pattern of a given type must be corrected
and no positive probability of unsuccessful decoding is tolerated.
This is the scenario considered (often implicitly) in standard network coding references such as
[
22
,
30
,
3
,
13
]
among many others.
We will briefly consider a probabilistic regime in Subsection
3.2
with the goal of forming intuition about certain information theory phenomena we will observe.

Definition 2.1
.

A (
discrete
,
adversarial
)
channel
is a map

\Omega:\mathcal{X}\to 2^{\mathcal{Y}}\setminus\{\emptyset\}
, where
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
are finite non-empty sets called the
input
and
output alphabets
respectively.
The notation for such a channel is
\Omega:\mathcal{X}\dashrightarrow\mathcal{Y}
. We say that
\Omega
is
deterministic
if
|\Omega(x)|=1
for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
.
We call
\Omega(x)
the
fan-out set
of
x
.

Note that a deterministic channel
\Omega:\mathcal{X}\dashrightarrow\mathcal{Y}
can be naturally identified with a function
\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}
, which we denote by
\Omega
as well.

Definition 2.2
.

A
(outer) code
for a channel
\Omega:\mathcal{X}\dashrightarrow\mathcal{Y}
is a non-empty subset
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
. We say that
\mathcal{C}
is

unambiguous
if
\Omega(x)\cap\Omega(x^{\prime})=\emptyset

for all
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}
with
x\neq x^{\prime}
.

The base-two logarithm of the largest size of an unambiguous code for a given channel is its
1-shot capacity
. In the graph theory representation of channels proposed by Shannon in
[
21
]
, the 1-shot capacity coincides with the base-two logarithm of the largest cardinality of an independent set in the corresponding graph. We refer to
[
20
, Section II]

for a more detailed discussion.

Definition 2.3
.

The (
1
-shot
)
capacity
of a channel
\Omega:\mathcal{X}\dashrightarrow\mathcal{Y}
is the real number

\textnormal{C}_{1}(\Omega)=\max\left\{\log_{2}|\mathcal{C}|\;:\;\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{X}\mbox{ is an unambiguous code for $\Omega$}\right\}.

We give an example to illustrate the previous definitions.

Example 2.4
.

Let
\mathcal{X}=\mathcal{Y}=\{0,1,2,3,4,5,6,7\}
. Define a channel
\Omega:\mathcal{X}\dashrightarrow\mathcal{Y}
by setting

\Omega(x)=\begin{cases}\{0,2\}&\text{if}\ \ x=0,\\
\{0,1,4,6\}&\text{if}\ \ x=1,\\
\{2,3,5\}&\text{if}\ \ x=2,\\
\{2,3,4,7\}&\text{if}\ \ x=3,\end{cases}\qquad\qquad\Omega(x)=\begin{cases}\{2,3,4,6\}&\text{if}\ \ x=4,\\
\{0,1,5\}&\text{if}\ \ x=5,\\
\{6\}&\text{if}\ \ x=6,\\
\{0,1,5,7\}&\text{if}\ \ x=7.\\
\end{cases}

Clearly,
\Omega
is not deterministic. It can be checked that the only unambigious code for
\Omega
of size 3 is
\mathcal{C}=\{3,5,6\}
, and that there are no unambiguous codes of size 4. Therefore we have
\textnormal{C}_{1}(\Omega)=\log_{2}3
.

In this paper we focus solely on the
1-shot capacity of
channels.
While other capacity notions can be considered as well (e.g., the
zero-error capacity
), in the networking context these are significantly more technical to treat
than the 1-shot capacity, especially when focusing on restricted noise. We therefore omit them
in this first paper on network decoding and leave them as a future research direction; see Section
9
.

We next describe how channels can be compared and combined with each other, referring to
[
20
]
for a more complete treatment.

Definition 2.5
.

Let
\Omega_{1},\Omega_{2}:\mathcal{X}\dashrightarrow\mathcal{Y}
be channels.
We say that
\Omega_{1}
is
finer

than
\Omega_{2}
(or that
\Omega_{2}
is
coarser
than
\Omega_{1}
) if
\Omega_{1}(x)\subseteq\Omega_{2}(x)
for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
.
The notation is
\Omega_{1}\leq\Omega_{2}
.

Finer channels have larger capacity, as the following simple result states.

Proposition 2.6
.

Let
\Omega_{1},\Omega_{2}:\mathcal{X}\dashrightarrow\mathcal{Y}
be channels with
\Omega_{1}\leq\Omega_{2}
. We have
\textnormal{C}_{1}(\Omega_{1})\geq\textnormal{C}_{1}(\Omega_{2})
.

Channels with compatible output/input alphabets can be concatenated with each other via the following construction.

Definition 2.7
.

Let
\Omega_{1}:\mathcal{X}_{1}\dashrightarrow\mathcal{Y}_{1}
and
\Omega_{2}:\mathcal{X}_{2}\dashrightarrow\mathcal{Y}_{2}

be channels, with
\mathcal{Y}_{1}\subseteq\mathcal{X}_{2}
.
The
concatenation
of

\Omega_{1}
and
\Omega_{2}
is the channel

\Omega_{1}\blacktriangleright\Omega_{2}:\mathcal{X}_{1}\dashrightarrow\mathcal{Y}_{2}
defined by

(\Omega_{1}\blacktriangleright\Omega_{2})(x):=\bigcup_{y\in\Omega_{1}(x)}\Omega_{2}(y).

The concatenation of channels is associative in the following precise sense.

Proposition 2.8
.

Let
\Omega_{i}:\mathcal{X}_{i}\dashrightarrow\mathcal{Y}_{i}
be channels, for
i\in\{1,2,3\}
,
with
\mathcal{Y}_{i}\subseteq\mathcal{X}_{i+1}
for

i\in\{1,2\}
. We have

(\Omega_{1}\blacktriangleright\Omega_{2})\blacktriangleright\Omega_{3}=\Omega_{1}\blacktriangleright(\Omega_{2}\blacktriangleright\Omega_{3})
.

The previous result allows us to
write expressions such as
\Omega_{1}\blacktriangleright\Omega_{2}\blacktriangleright\Omega_{3}

without parentheses, when all concatenations are defined.

We conclude this subsection with a
discrete
version
of the
data processing inequality
from classical information theory; see e.g.
[
4
, Section 2.8]
.

Proposition 2.9
.

Let
\Omega_{1}:\mathcal{X}_{1}\dashrightarrow\mathcal{Y}_{1}
and
\Omega_{2}:\mathcal{X}_{2}\dashrightarrow\mathcal{Y}_{2}

be channels, with
\mathcal{Y}_{1}\subseteq\mathcal{X}_{2}
.
We have
\textnormal{C}_{1}(\Omega_{1}\blacktriangleright\Omega_{2})\leq\min\{\textnormal{C}_{1}(\Omega_{1}),\,\textnormal{C}_{1}(\Omega_{2})\}
.

2.2
Networks and Their Capacities

In this subsection we formally define
communication networks, network codes, and the channels they induce.
Our approach is inspired by
[
20
]
, even though the notation used in this paper differs slightly. We omit some of the details and refer the interested reader directly to
[
20
]
.

Definition 2.10
.

A (
single-source
)
network
is a 4-tuple
\mathcal{N}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E},S,\bf T)
where:

(A)

(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E})
is a finite, directed, acyclic multigraph,

(B)

S\in\mathcal{V}
is the
source
,

(C)

{\bf T}\subseteq\mathcal{V}
is the set of
terminals
or
sinks
,

Note that we allow multiple parallel directed edges. We also assume that the following hold.

(D)

|{\bf T}|\geq 1
,
S\notin{\bf T}
.

(E)

For any
T\in{\bf T}
, there exists a directed path from
S
to
T
.

(F)

The source does not have incoming edges, and terminals do not have outgoing edges.

(G)

For every vertex
V\in\mathcal{V}\setminus(\{S\}\cup\bf T)
, there exists a directed path from

S
to
V
and a directed path from
V
to
T
for some
T\in{\bf T}
.

The elements of
\mathcal{V}
are called
vertices
or
nodes
. The elements of

\mathcal{V}\setminus(\{S\}\cup{\bf T})
are called
intermediate
nodes.
A (
network
)
alphabet
is a finite set
\mathcal{A}
with
|\mathcal{A}|\geq 2
.
The elements of
\mathcal{A}
are called
symbols
or
packets
. We say that
\mathcal{N}
is a
single-terminal
network if
|\bf T|=1
.

The network alphabet is interpreted as the set of symbols that can be sent over the edges of the network.

Notation 2.11
.

Throughout the paper,
\mathcal{N}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E},S,{\bf T})
will always denote a network and
\mathcal{A}
an alphabet, as in Definition
2.10
, unless otherwise stated. We let

\textnormal{min-cut}_{\mathcal{N}}(V,V^{\prime})

be the minimum cardinality of an edge-cut (a set of edges that cuts the connection) between vertices
V,V^{\prime}\in\mathcal{V}
. We denote the
set of incoming and outgoing edges of
V\in\mathcal{V}
by
\textnormal{in}(V)
and
\textnormal{out}(V)
, respectively, and their cardinalities by
\partial^{-}(V)
and
\partial^{+}(V)
.
These cardinalities are called the
in-degree
and
out-degree
of the vertex
V
, respectively.

The following concepts will be crucial in our approach.

Definition 2.12
.

The edges of a network
\mathcal{N}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E},S,\bf T)
can be partially ordered as follows. For
e,e^{\prime}\in\mathcal{E}
, we say that
e

precedes

e^{\prime}
if
there exists a directed path in
\mathcal{N}
that starts with
e
and ends with
e^{\prime}
. The notation is

e\preccurlyeq e^{\prime}
.

Notation 2.13
.

Following the notation of Definition
2.12
, it is well known in graph theory that
the partial order on
\mathcal{E}
can be extended to a (not necessarily unique) total order. By definition, such an extension
\leq
satisfies the following property:

e\preccurlyeq e^{\prime}
implies
e\leq e^{\prime}
.
Throughout the paper we will assume that such an order extension has been fixed in the various networks and we denote it by
\leq
(none of the results of this paper depend on the particular choice of the total order).
Moreover, we illustrate the chosen total order via the labeling of the edges; see, for example, Figure
1
.

In our model, the intermediate nodes of a network process incoming packets according to prescribed functions. We do not assume any restrictions on these functions. In particular, even when
\mathcal{A}
is a linear space over a given finite field, we do
not
require the functions to be linear over the underlying field.
This is in strong contrast with the most common approach taken in the context of network coding; see, for instance,

[
26
,
31
,
8
,
23
,
7
,
15
]
. In fact, as we will argue in Section
8
,
using non-linear network codes (e.g.
decoding
at intermediate nodes) is often
needed
to achieve capacity in the scenarios studied in this paper.

Definition 2.14
.

Let
\mathcal{N}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E},S,\bf T)
be a network and
\mathcal{A}
an alphabet. A
network code
for
(\mathcal{N},\mathcal{A})
is a family
\mathcal{F}=\{\mathcal{F}_{V}\,:\,V\in\mathcal{V}\setminus(\{S\}\cup{\bf T})\}
of functions,
where

\mathcal{F}_{V}:\mathcal{A}^{\partial^{-}(V)}\to\mathcal{A}^{\partial^{+}(V)}\quad\mbox{for all $V\in\mathcal{V}\setminus(\{S\}\cup{\bf T})$}.

A network code
\mathcal{F}
fully specifies how the intermediate nodes of a network process information packets. Note that the interpretation of each function
\mathcal{F}_{V}
is unique precisely thanks to the choice of the total order
\leq
; see Notation
2.13
.

This paper focuses on networks
\mathcal{N}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E},S,\bf T)
affected by

As already mentioned,
the notion of error probability does not make sense in this context, since the noise is adversarial in nature.
We can make the described problem

.

Let

4.3
Some Families of Simple 2-Level Networks

In this section, we introduce five families of simple 2-level networks. Thanks to Theorem
4.8
,
any upper bound for the capacities of these translates into an upper bound for the capacities of a 3-level networks associated with them; see Definition
4.6
.
The five families of networks introduced in this subsection should be regarded as the “building blocks” of the theory developed in this paper,
since in Section
7

we will argue how to use them to obtain upper bounds for the capacities of larger networks.

We focus our attention on the scenario where the adversary acts on the edges directly connected to the source
S
, which we denote by
\mathcal{U}_{S}
throughout this section.
The families we introduce will be studied in detail in later sections, but are collected here for preparation and ease of reference. Each family is parametrized by a positive integer (denoted by
t
or

Family A
.

Define the simple 2-level networks

\mathfrak{A}_{t}=([t,2t],[t,t]),\quad t\geq 1.

5
Simple 2-level Networks: Upper Bounds

In this section we present upper bounds on the capacity of simple 2-level networks based on a variety of techniques.
We then apply these bounds to the
families introduced in Subsection
4.3
. We start by establishing the notation that we will follow in the sequel.

Notation 5.1
.

Throughout this section,
n\geq 2
is an integer and

\mathcal{N}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E},S,\{T\})=([a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}],[b_{1},\ldots,b_{n}])

is a simple 2-level network; see Definition
4.2
.
We denote by
\mathcal{U}_{S}\subseteq\mathcal{E}
the set of edges directly connected to the source
S
and let
\mathcal{A}
be a network alphabet.
We denote the intermediate nodes of
\mathcal{N}
by
V_{1},\ldots,V_{n}
, which correspond to the structural parameters
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}
and

b_{1},\ldots,b_{n}
.
If
\mathcal{F}
is a network code for
(\mathcal{N},\mathcal{A})
,
then we simply write
\mathcal{F}_{i}
for
\smash{\mathcal{F}_{V_{i}}}
.
Observe moreover that the network code
\mathcal{F}
can be “globally” interpreted as a function

\mathcal{F}:\mathcal{A}^{a_{1}+\ldots+a_{n}}\to\mathcal{A}^{b_{1}+\ldots+b_{n}},

although of course allowed functions
\mathcal{F}
are restricted by the topology of the underlying 2-level network.

We start by spelling out the Generalized Network Singleton Bound of Theorem
2.21
specifically for simple 2-level networks.

Corollary 5.2

(Generalized Network Singleton Bound for simple 2-level networks)
.

Following Notation
5.1
, for all
t\geq 0
we have

\textnormal{C}_{1}(\mathcal{N},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{U}_{S},t)\leq\min_{P_{1}\sqcup P_{2}=\{1,\ldots,n\}}\left(\sum_{i\in P_{1}}b_{i}+\max\left\{0,\sum_{i\in P_{2}}a_{i}-2t\right\}\right),

where the minimum is taken over all 2-partitions
P_{1},P_{2}
of the set
\{1,\ldots,n\}
.

The upper bounds we derive in this section use a “mix” of projection and packing arguments. We therefore continue by reminding the reader of the notions of the Hamming metric
ball
and
shell
.

Notation 5.3
.

Given an outer code
\smash{\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{A}^{a_{1}+a_{2}+\ldots+a_{n}}}
, we let
\smash{\pi_{i}(\mathcal{C})}
be the projection of
\mathcal{C}
onto the
a_{i}
coordinates corresponding to the edges to intermediate node
V_{i}
of the simple 2-level network
\mathcal{N}
. For example,
\smash{\pi_{1}(\mathcal{C})}
is the projection onto the first
a_{1}
coordinates of the codeword. Moreover, for a given
x\in\mathcal{C}
, we denote by
B^{\textnormal{H}}_{t}(x)
the
Hamming ball
of radius
t
with center
x
, and by
S^{\textnormal{H}}_{t}(x)
the
shell
of that ball. In symbols,

B^{\textnormal{H}}_{t}(x)=\{y\in\mathcal{A}^{a_{1}+a_{2}+\ldots+a_{n}}\,:\,d^{\textnormal{H}}(x,y)\leq t\},\qquad S^{\textnormal{H}}_{t}(x)=\{y\in B^{\textnormal{H}}_{t}(x)\,:\,d^{\textnormal{H}}(x,y)=t\},

where
d^{\textnormal{H}}
denotes the usual Hamming distance.

The next observation focuses on the case
n=2
to illustrate an idea that will be generalized immediately after in Remark
5.6
below.

Remark 5.4
.

If
n=2
, then for all
t\geq 0

an outer code
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{A}^{a_{1}+a_{2}}
is unambiguous for the channel
\Omega[\mathcal{N},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{F},S\to T,\mathcal{U}_{S},t]
if and only if

\mathcal{F}(x+e)=(\mathcal{F}_{1}(\pi_{1}(x+e)),\mathcal{F}_{2}(\pi_{2}(x+e)))\neq(\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{1}(\pi_{1}(x^{\prime}+e^{\prime})),\mathcal{F}_{2}(\pi_{2}(x^{\prime}+e^{\prime})))=\mathcal{F}(x^{\prime}+e^{\prime})

for all

x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}
with
x\neq x^{\prime}
and for all
e,e^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}^{a_{1}+a_{2}}
of Hamming weight at most
t
.
Therefore via a packing argument
we obtain

\sum_{x\in\mathcal{C}}|\mathcal{F}\left(B^{\textnormal{H}}_{t}(x)\right)|\leq{|\mathcal{A}|}^{b_{1}+b_{2}}.

(2)

We will work towards extending the packing bound idea outlined in Remark
5.4
to higher numbers of intermediate nodes using the properties of simple 2-level networks. We start with
the following result.

Lemma 5.5
.

Following Notation
5.1
, suppose
n=2
and
b_{1}\geq a_{1}
.
Let
t\geq 0
and suppose that
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{A}^{a_{1}+a_{2}}
is unambiguous for the channel
\Omega[\mathcal{N},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{F},S\to T,\mathcal{U}_{S},t]
. Let
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{1}:\mathcal{A}^{a_{1}}\to\mathcal{A}^{b_{1}}
be any injective map. Then
\mathcal{C}
is unambiguous for the channel
\Omega[\mathcal{N},\mathcal{A},\{\mathcal{F}_{1}^{\prime},\mathcal{F}_{2}\},S\to T,\mathcal{U}_{S},t]
as well.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{F}=\{\mathcal{F}_{1},\mathcal{F}_{2}\}
and
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}=\{\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{1},\mathcal{F}_{2}\}
. Towards a contradiction and using Remark
5.4
, suppose that
there are
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}
with
x\neq x^{\prime}
and
e,e^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}^{a_{1}+a_{2}}
of Hamming weight at most
t
with
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}(x+e)=\mathcal{F}^{\prime}(x^{\prime}+e^{\prime})
. This implies

(\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{1}(\pi_{1}(x+e)),\mathcal{F}_{2}(\pi_{2}(x+e)))=(\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{1}(\pi_{1}(x^{\prime}+e^{\prime})),\mathcal{F}_{2}(\pi_{2}(x^{\prime}+e^{\prime}))).

Since
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{1}
is injective, we have

\pi_{1}(x+e)=\pi_{1}(x^{\prime}+e^{\prime})
and thus

(\mathcal{F}_{1}(\pi_{1}(x+e)),\mathcal{F}_{2}(\pi_{2}(x+e)))=(\mathcal{F}_{1}(\pi_{1}(x^{\prime}+e^{\prime})),\mathcal{F}_{2}(\pi_{2}(x^{\prime}+e^{\prime}))).

That is,

\mathcal{F}(x+e)=\mathcal{F}(x^{\prime}+e^{\prime})
, a contradiction.
∎

Remark 5.6
.

Lemma
5.5
extends easily to an arbitrary number of intermediate nodes, showing that whenever
b_{i}\geq a_{i}
for some
i
, without loss of generality we can assume
b_{i}=a_{i}
and take the corresponding function
\mathcal{F}_{i}
to be the identity (ignoring extraneous outgoing edges). In other words, the capacity obtained by maximizing over all possible choices of
\mathcal{F}
is the same as the capacity obtained by maximizing over the
\mathcal{F}
’s where
\mathcal{F}_{i}
is equal to the identity whenever
b_{i}\geq a_{i}
(again, where some edges can be disregarded). This will simplify the analysis of the network families we study. In particular,
we can reduce the study of a
simple 2-level network
\mathcal{N}=([a_{1},...,a_{n}],[b_{1},...,b_{n}])

as in Notation
5.1
to the study of

\mathcal{N}^{\prime}=([a_{1},\ldots,a_{r},a_{r+1},\ldots,a_{n}],[a_{1},\ldots,a_{r},b_{r+1},\ldots,b_{n}]),

where, up to a permutation of the vertices and edges,

r=\max\{i\,:\,a_{i}\leq b_{i}\mbox{ for all }1\leq i\leq r\}.

The next result combines the observation
of Remark
5.6
with a packing argument to derive an upper bound on the capacity of certain simple 2-level networks.

Theorem 5.7

(First Packing Bound)
.

Following Notation
5.1
,
suppose that
a_{i}\leq b_{i}
for all
1\leq i\leq r
. Let
t\geq 0
and let
\mathcal{C}
be an unambigious code for
\Omega[\mathcal{N},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{F},S\to T,\mathcal{U}_{S},t]
. Then

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}t_{1},\ldots,t_{r}\geq 0\\
t_{1}+\ldots+t_{r}\leq t\end{subarray}}\,\prod_{i=1}^{r}\binom{a_{i}}{t_{i}}(|\mathcal{A}|-1)^{t_{i}}\,\sum_{x\in\mathcal{C}}\,\prod_{j=r+1}^{n}\left|\mathcal{F}_{j}\left(B^{\textnormal{H}}_{t-(t_{1}+\ldots+t_{r})}(\pi_{j}(x))\right)\right|\leq|\mathcal{A}|^{b_{1}+b_{2}+\ldots+b_{n}}.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{F}=\{\mathcal{F}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{F}_{r},\ldots,\mathcal{F}_{n}\}
be a network code for
(\mathcal{N},\mathcal{A})
, where the first
r
functions correspond to the pairs
(a_{1},b_{1}),\ldots,(a_{r},b_{r})
. By Lemma
5.5
, we shall assume without loss of generality that
\mathcal{F}_{i}

is an injective map for
1\leq i\leq r.
By Remark
5.6
, we can assume them to be identity by ignoring the extraneous outgoing edges. For
x\in\mathcal{C}
, we have

B^{\textnormal{H}}_{t}(x)=\bigsqcup_{t_{1}+\ldots+t_{n}\leq t}\left[S^{\textnormal{H}}_{t_{1}}(\pi_{1}(x))\times\cdots\times S^{\textnormal{H}}_{t_{n}}(\pi_{n}(x))\right],

where
\sqcup
emphasizes that the union is disjoint. Then,

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(B^{\textnormal{H}}_{t}(x))

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{t_{1}+\ldots+t_{n}\leq t}\mathcal{F}\left[S^{\textnormal{H}}_{t_{1}}(\pi_{1}(x))\times\cdots\times S^{\textnormal{H}}_{t_{n}}(\pi_{n}(x))\right]

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{t_{1}+\ldots+t_{n}\leq t}\left[S^{\textnormal{H}}_{t_{1}}(\pi_{1}(x))\times\cdots\times S^{\textnormal{H}}_{t_{r}}(\pi_{r}(x))\times\mathcal{F}_{r+1}(S^{\textnormal{H}}_{t_{r+1}}(\pi_{r+1}(x))\times\cdots\times\mathcal{F}_{n}(S^{\textnormal{H}}_{t_{n}}(\pi_{n}(x))\right]

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{t_{1}+\ldots+t_{r}\leq t}\ \bigcup_{t_{r+1}+\ldots+t_{n}\leq t-(t_{1}+\ldots+t_{r})}\Bigl[S^{\textnormal{H}}_{t_{1}}(\pi_{1}(x))\times\cdots\times S^{\textnormal{H}}_{t_{r}}(\pi_{r}(x))\,\times

\displaystyle\hskip 18.49988pt\hskip 18.49988pt\hskip 18.49988pt\times\mathcal{F}_{r+1}(S^{\textnormal{H}}_{t_{r+1}}(\pi_{r+1}(x))\times\cdots\times\mathcal{F}_{n}(S^{\textnormal{H}}_{t_{n}}(\pi_{n}(x))\Bigr].

The first union in the last equality is disjoint due to
\mathcal{F}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{F}_{r}
being the identity and the fact that we are considering the shells.
So,

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(B^{\textnormal{H}}_{t}(x))

\displaystyle=\bigsqcup_{t_{1}+\ldots+t_{r}\leq t}\Bigl[S^{\textnormal{H}}_{t_{1}}(\pi_{1}(x))\times\cdots\times S^{\textnormal{H}}_{t_{r}}(\pi_{r}(x))\times

\displaystyle\hskip 18.49988pt\hskip 18.49988pt\hskip 18.49988pt\bigcup_{t_{r+1}+\ldots+t_{n}\leq t-(t_{1}+\ldots+t_{r})}\biggl(\mathcal{F}_{r+1}(S^{\textnormal{H}}_{t_{r+1}}(\pi_{r+1}(x))\times\cdots\times\mathcal{F}_{n}(S^{\textnormal{H}}_{t_{n}}(\pi_{n}(x))\biggr)\Bigr].

By taking cardinalities in the previous identity
we obtain

|\mathcal{F}(B^{\textnormal{H}}_{t}(x))|=\sum_{t_{1}+\ldots+t_{r}\leq t}\,\prod_{i=1}^{r}\binom{a_{i}}{t_{i}}(|\mathcal{A}|-1)^{t_{i}}\prod_{j=r+1}^{n}|\mathcal{F}_{j}(B^{\textnormal{H}}_{t-(t_{1}+\ldots+t_{r})}(\pi_{j}(x)))|,

where the terms in the second product come from considering that all shells up to the shell of radius
t-(t_{1}+\ldots+t_{r})
will be included for each projection. Summing over
x\in\mathcal{C}
, exchanging summations, and using the same argument as in (
2
), we obtain the desired result.
∎

are mainly interested in the case
(n,r)=(2,1)
of the previous result.
For convenience of the reader, we state this as a corollary of Theorem
5.7
.

Corollary 5.8
.

Following Notation
5.1
, suppose
n=2
and
a_{1}\leq b_{1}
.
Let
t\geq 1
and let

\mathcal{C}
be an unambiguous code for the channel
\Omega[\mathcal{N},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{F},S\to T,\mathcal{U}_{S},t]

\sum_{t_{1}=0}^{t}\binom{a_{1}}{t_{1}}(|\mathcal{A}|-1)^{t_{1}}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{C}}\left|\mathcal{F}_{2}\left(B^{\textnormal{H}}_{t-t_{1}}(\pi_{2}(x)))\right)\right|\leq|\mathcal{A}|^{b_{1}+b_{2}}.

We next apply the bound of Theorem
5.7
(in the form of Corollary
5.8
) to some of the network families
introduced in Subsection

.
With some additional information, Theorem
5.7
gives us that the Generalized Network Singleton Bound of Corollary
5.2
is in general not achievable, no matter what the network alphabet
\mathcal{A}
is. This is in strong contrast with what is commonly observed in classical coding theory, where the Singleton bound is always the sharpest (and in fact sharp) when the alphabet is sufficiently large.

Theorem 5.9
.

Let
\mathfrak{B}_{s}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E},S,\{T\})
be a member of
Family
B
. Let
\mathcal{A}
be any network alphabet and let
\mathcal{U}_{S}
be the set of edges of
\mathfrak{B}_{s}
directly connected to
S
.
We have

\textnormal{C}_{1}(\mathfrak{B}_{s},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{U}_{S},1)<s.

In particular, the Generalized Network Singleton Bound of Corollary
5.2
is not met.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a network code for the pair
(\mathfrak{B}_{s},\mathcal{A})
and let

q:=|\mathcal{A}|
to simplify the notation.
Suppose that
\mathcal{C}
is an unambiguous code for the channel

\Omega[\mathfrak{B}_{s},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{F},S\to T,\mathcal{U}_{S},1]
. We want to show that
|\mathcal{C}|<q^{s}
.
Corollary
5.8
gives

(q-1)\cdot|\mathcal{C}|+\sum_{x\in\mathcal{C}}\left|\mathcal{F}_{2}\left(B^{\textnormal{H}}_{1}(\pi_{2}(x))\right)\right|\leq q^{s+1}.

(3)

Suppose towards a contradiction that
|\mathcal{C}|=q^{s}
.
We claim that there exists a codeword
x\in\mathcal{C}
for which the cardinality of

\left\{\mathcal{F}_{2}(\pi_{2}(z))\,:\,d^{\textnormal{H}}(\pi_{2}(x),\pi_{2}(z))\leq 1,\ z\in\mathcal{A}^{s+2}\right\}

is at least 2. To see this, we start by observing that
\mathcal{F}_{2}
restricted to
{\pi_{2}(\mathcal{C})}
must be injective, as otherwise the fan-out sets of at least two codewords would intersect non-trivially, making
\mathcal{C}
ambiguous for the channel
\Omega[\mathfrak{B}_{s},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{F},S\to T,\mathcal{U}_{S},1]
.
Therefore it is now enough to show that
B^{\textnormal{H}}_{1}(\pi_{2}(x))\cap B^{\textnormal{H}}_{1}(\pi_{2}(y))\neq\emptyset
for some distinct
x,y\in\mathcal{C}
,
which would imply that the cardinality of one among

\left\{\mathcal{F}_{2}(\pi_{2}(z))\,:\,d^{\textnormal{H}}(\pi_{2}(x),\pi_{2}(z))\leq 1,\ z\in\mathcal{A}^{s+2}\right\},\ \left\{\mathcal{F}_{2}(\pi_{2}(z))\,:\,d^{\textnormal{H}}(\pi_{2}(y),\pi_{2}(z))\leq 1,\ z\in\mathcal{A}^{s+2}\right\}

is at least 2, since
\mathcal{F}_{2}(\pi_{2}(x))\neq\mathcal{F}_{2}(\pi_{2}(y))
.
Observe that
|B^{\textnormal{H}}_{1}(\pi_{2}(x))|=(s+1)(q-1)+1
for any
x\in\mathcal{C},
and that

\sum_{x\in\mathcal{C}}|B^{\textnormal{H}}_{1}(\pi_{2}(x))|=q^{s}(s+1)(q-1)+q^{s}=q^{s+1}(s+1)-sq^{s}>q^{s+1}=|\mathcal{A}^{a_{2}}|,

where we use the fact that
q>1
. If
B^{\textnormal{H}}_{1}(\pi_{2}(x))\cap B^{\textnormal{H}}_{1}(\pi_{2}(y))=\emptyset
for all distinct
x,y\in\mathcal{C}
, then
\sum_{x\in\mathcal{C}}|B^{\textnormal{H}}_{1}(\pi_{2}(x))|\leq|\mathcal{A}^{a_{2}}|
, a contradiction. Therefore

B^{\textnormal{H}}_{1}(\pi_{2}(x))\cap B^{\textnormal{H}}_{1}(\pi_{2}(y))\neq\emptyset,

proving our claim.
We finally combine the said claim with the inequality in (
3
), obtaining

q|\mathcal{C}|+1=(q-1)|\mathcal{C}|+2+(|\mathcal{C}|-1)\leq(q-1)\,|\mathcal{C}|+\sum_{x\in\mathcal{C}}|\mathcal{F}_{2}(B^{\textnormal{H}}_{1}(\pi_{2}(x)))|\leq q^{s+1}.

In particular,
|\mathcal{C}|<q^{s}
, establishing the theorem.
∎

Remark 5.10
.

While we cannot compute the exact capacity of the networks of Family

, preliminary experimental results and case-by-case analyses seem to indicate that

\textnormal{C}_{1}(\mathfrak{B}_{s},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{U}_{S},1)=\log_{|\mathcal{A}|}\left(\frac{|\mathcal{A}|^{s}+|\mathcal{A}|}{2}-1\right),

(4)

which would be consistent
with Corollary

At the time of writing this paper
proving (or disproving) the equality in (

) remains an open problem.

Notice that Theorem
5.7
uses a packing argument “downstream” of the intermediate nodes in a simple 2-level network. Next, we work toward an upper bound on capacity that utilizes a packing argument “upstream” of the intermediate nodes.
Later we will show that the packing argument “upstream” acts similarly to the Hamming Bound from classical coding theory and can sometimes give better results in the networking context.

We start with the following lemma, which will be the starting point for the Second Packing Bound below.

Lemma 5.11
.

We follow Notation
5.1

and let
t\geq 0
be an integer.
Let
\mathcal{C}
be an outer code for
(\mathcal{N},\mathcal{A})
. Then
\mathcal{C}
is unambiguous
for the channel
\Omega[\mathcal{N},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{F},S\to T,\mathcal{U}_{S},t]

if and only if
\mathcal{F}^{-1}(\mathcal{F}(B^{\textnormal{H}}_{t}(x)))\cap\mathcal{F}^{-1}(\mathcal{F}(B^{\textnormal{H}}_{t}(x^{\prime})))=\emptyset
for all distinct
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}
.

Proof.

Suppose
\mathcal{F}^{-1}(\mathcal{F}(B^{\textnormal{H}}_{t}(x)))\cap\mathcal{F}^{-1}(\mathcal{F}(B^{\textnormal{H}}_{t}(x^{\prime})))\neq\emptyset
for some distinct
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}
, and let
y
lie in that intersection. This implies

\mathcal{F}(y)\in\mathcal{F}(B^{\textnormal{H}}_{t}(x))\cap\mathcal{F}(B^{\textnormal{H}}_{t}(x^{\prime}))=\Omega(x)\cap\Omega(x^{\prime}).

In other words,
\mathcal{C}
is not unambiguous for the channel
\Omega[\mathcal{N},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{F},S\to T,\mathcal{U}_{S},t]
. For the other direction, it is straightforward to see that disjointness of the preimages implies disjointness of the fan-out sets.
∎

Following the notation of

Theorem 5.15
.

Let
\mathfrak{E}_{t}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E},S,\{T\})
be a member of

\mathcal{A}
be any network alphabet and let
\mathcal{U}_{S}

\textnormal{C}_{1}(\mathfrak{E}_{t},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{U}_{S},t)<1.

is not met.

Proof.

Let
q:=|\mathcal{A}|
.
Suppose by way of contradiction that there exists an unambiguous code
\mathcal{C}
of size
q
for
\Omega[\mathfrak{E}_{t},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{F},S\to T,\mathcal{U}_{S},t]

\mathcal{F}=\{\mathcal{F}_{1},\mathcal{F}_{2}\}

\mathcal{C}

\mathcal{C}

2t+1

\pi_{1}(\mathcal{C})

q

\mathcal{F}_{1}

\pi_{1}(\mathcal{C})

\mathcal{C}

\mathcal{F}_{1}(\pi_{1}(\mathcal{C}))=\mathcal{A}

x,y\in\mathcal{C}

\displaystyle x^{\prime}

\displaystyle:=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{t},x_{t+1},y_{t+2},y_{t+3},\ldots,y_{2t+1})\in B^{\textnormal{H}}_{t}(x),

\displaystyle y^{\prime}

Theorem 5.16
.

Let
\mathfrak{A}_{t}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E},S,\{T\})
be a member of
Family
A
. Let
\mathcal{A}
be any network alphabet and let
\mathcal{U}_{S}
be the set of edges of
\mathfrak{A}_{t}
directly connected to
S
.
We have

\textnormal{C}_{1}(\mathfrak{A}_{t},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{U}_{S},t)<t.

In particular, the Generalized Network Singleton Bound of Corollary
5.2
is not met.

Proof.

Let
q:=|\mathcal{A}|
. Towards a contradiction, assume that
the rate
t
is achievable. That is, there exists an unambiguous code
\mathcal{C}
of size
q^{t}
for
\Omega[\mathfrak{A}_{t},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{F},S\to T,\mathcal{U}_{S},t]
, where
\mathcal{F}=\{\mathcal{F}_{1},\mathcal{F}_{2}\}
is a network code for
(\mathfrak{A}_{t},\mathcal{A})
. Note that
|\pi_{2}(\mathcal{C})|=|\mathcal{C}|
and that the restriction of

\mathcal{F}_{2}
to
\pi_{2}(\mathcal{C})

is injective, as otherwise
the intersection of fan-out sets would be nonempty for some pair of codewords in
\mathcal{C}
, as one can easily check.

We claim that there must exist two distinct codewords
x,y\in\mathcal{C}
such that
B^{\textnormal{H}}_{t}(\pi_{2}(x))\cap B^{\textnormal{H}}_{t}(\pi_{2}(y))\neq\emptyset
.
Observe that

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in\mathcal{C}}|B^{\textnormal{H}}_{t}(\pi_{2}(x))|

\displaystyle=q^{t}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{t}\binom{2t}{k}(q-1)^{k}\right)

\displaystyle>q^{t}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{t}\binom{t}{k}(q-1)^{k}\right)

\displaystyle=q^{2t},

(6)

where Equation (
6
) follows from the Binomial Theorem. If
B^{\textnormal{H}}_{t}(\pi_{2}(x))\cap B^{\textnormal{H}}_{t}(\pi_{2}(y))=\emptyset
for all distinct
x,y\in\mathcal{C}
, then we would have

\sum_{x\in\mathcal{C}}|B^{\textnormal{H}}_{t}(\pi_{2}(x))|=\left|\bigcup_{x\in\mathcal{C}}B_{t}^{\textnormal{H}}(\pi_{2}(x))\right|\leq q^{2t}.

Therefore it must be the case that some such intersection is nonempty.
In other words, there exist some distinct
x,y\in\mathcal{C}
and
e\in\mathcal{A}^{2t}
such that
e\in B^{\textnormal{H}}_{t}(\pi_{2}(x))\cap B^{\textnormal{H}}_{t}(\pi_{2}(y))
.

By the fact that the restriction of

\mathcal{F}_{2}
to
\pi_{2}(\mathcal{C})

is injective and its codomain has size
q^{t}=|\pi_{2}(\mathcal{C})|
,
the restriction of

\mathcal{F}_{2}
to
\pi_{2}(\mathcal{C})

must be surjective as well.
Thus

\mathcal{F}_{2}(e)=\mathcal{F}_{2}(\pi_{2}(a))
for some
a\in\mathcal{C}
.
If
a=x
, then
\mathcal{F}_{2}(e)=\mathcal{F}_{2}(\pi_{2}(x))
and we have
(\mathcal{F}_{1}(\pi_{1}(y)),\mathcal{F}_{2}(e))=(\mathcal{F}_{1}(\pi_{1}(y)),\mathcal{F}_{2}(\pi_{2}(x)))\in\Omega(x)\cap\Omega(y)
, the intersection of the fan-out sets, which is a contradiction to
\mathcal{C}
being unambiguous. A similar argument holds if
a=y
. Otherwise, we still have
(\mathcal{F}_{1}(\pi_{1}(x)),\mathcal{F}_{2}(\pi_{2}(a)))\in\Omega(x)\cap\Omega(a),
a contradiction. We conclude that there cannot be an unambiguous code of size
q^{t}
.
∎

Simple 2-Level Networks: Lower Bounds

We devote this section to
deriving lower bounds
on the capacity of simple
2-level networks.
In connection with Theorem
4.8
,
we note that a rate may be achievable for a simple 2-level network and yet not achievable for every corresponding simple 3-level network.
We start by establishing the notation for this section.

Notation 6.1
.

Throughout this section we follow Notation
5.1
.
In particular, we work with simple 2-level networks

\mathcal{N}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E},S,\{T\})=([a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{n}],[b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{n}])
, denoting by
\mathcal{U}_{S}
the set of edges directly connected to the source
S
.
For any
t\geq 1
, we partition the vertices of such a network into the following (disjoint and possibly empty) sets:

\displaystyle I_{1}(\mathcal{N},t)

\displaystyle=\{i\,:\,a_{i}\geq b_{i}+2t\},

\displaystyle I_{2}(\mathcal{N},t)

\displaystyle=\{i\,:\,a_{i}\leq b_{i}\},

\displaystyle I_{3}(\mathcal{N},t)

\displaystyle=\{i\,:\,b_{i}+1\leq a_{i}\leq b_{i}+2t-1\}.

Theorem 6.2
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{A}
is a sufficiently large finite field.
Let
t\geq 1
and define the set
\tilde{I}_{3}(\mathcal{N},t)=\{i\in I_{3}(\mathcal{N},t)\,:\,a_{i}>2t\}
. Then,

\textnormal{C}_{1}(\mathcal{N},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{U}_{S},t)\geq\sum_{i\in I_{1}(\mathcal{N},t)}b_{i}+\max\left\{X,Y\right\},

where

\displaystyle X

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in\tilde{I}_{3}(\mathcal{N},t)}(a_{i}-2t)+\max\left\{0,\left(\sum_{i\in I_{2}(\mathcal{N},t)}a_{i}\right)-2t\right\},

\displaystyle Y

\displaystyle=\max\left\{0,\left(\sum_{i\in I_{2}(\mathcal{N},t)}a_{i}+\sum_{i\in I_{3}(\mathcal{N},t)}b_{i}\right)-2t\right\}.

Proof.

We will construct a network code
\mathcal{F}=\{\mathcal{F}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{F}_{n}\}
for
(\mathcal{N},\mathcal{A})
and an unambiguous outer code in various steps, indicating how each intermediate node operates. The alphabet
\mathcal{A}
should be
a large enough finite field
that admits MDS codes with parameters as described below.

For each
i\in I_{1}(\mathcal{N},t)
, let
\mathcal{F}_{i}
be a minimum distance decoder for an MDS code with parameters
\left[b_{i}+2t,\ b_{i},\ 2t+1\right]
that is sent along the first
b_{i}+2t
edges incoming to
V_{i}
. The source sends arbitrary symbols along the extraneous
a_{i}-(b_{i}+2t)
edges and these will be disregarded by
V_{i}
. Via the intermediate nodes indexed by
I_{1}(\mathcal{N},t)
, we have thus achieved
\sum_{i\in I_{1}(\mathcal{N},t)}b_{i}
information symbols decodable by the terminal.

In the remainder of the proof we will show that either an extra
X
symbols or an extra
Y
symbols (i.e., an extra
\max\{X,Y\}
symbols in general) can be successfully transmitted. We show how the two quantities
X
and
Y
can be achieved separately, so that we may choose the better option of the two and achieve the result.

1.

For each
i\in\tilde{I}_{3}(\mathcal{N},t)
, let
\mathcal{F}_{i}
be a minimum distance decoder for an MDS code with parameters
\left[a_{i},\ a_{i}-2t,\ 2t+1\right]
that sends its output across the first
a_{i}-2t
outgoing edges from
V_{i}
. Arbitrary symbols are sent along the remaining outgoing edges and they will be ignored at the destination. We disregard all intermediate nodes with indices from the set
I_{3}(\mathcal{N},t)\setminus\tilde{I}_{3}(\mathcal{N},t)
as they will not contribute to the lower bound in this scheme.
The symbols sent through the vertices indexed by
{I_{2}}(\mathcal{N},t)
will be globally encoded via an
MDS code with parameters

\left[\sum_{i\in I_{2}(\mathcal{N},t)}a_{i},\ \left(\sum_{i\in I_{2}(\mathcal{N},t)}a_{i}\right)-2t,\ 2t+1\right].

The intermediate nodes indexed by
{I_{2}}(\mathcal{N},t)

will simply forward the incoming symbols along the first
a_{i}
outgoing edges, sending arbitrary symbols along the other
b_{i}-a_{i}
outgoing edges. Symbols sent on these outgoing edges will be disregarded at the destination. If
\sum_{i\in I_{2}(\mathcal{N},t)}a_{i}\leq 2t
, we instead ignore the vertices indexed by
I_{2}(\mathcal{N},t)
in this scheme. In conclusion, via the intermediate nodes indexed by
I_{2}(\mathcal{N},t)\cup I_{3}(\mathcal{N},t)
, we have added
X
information symbols decodable by the terminal.

2.

For
i\in I_{2}(\mathcal{N},t)\cup I_{3}(\mathcal{N},t)
, let each
\mathcal{F}_{i}
simply forward up to
b_{i}
received symbols as follows. If
i\in I_{2}(\mathcal{N},t)
, then
a_{i}
symbols will be forwarded along the first
a_{i}
outgoing edges and arbitrary symbols will be sent along the other
b_{i}-a_{i}
outgoing edges. These edges will be disregarded at the destination. If
i\in I_{3}(\mathcal{N},t)
, then
b_{i}
symbols sent over the first
b_{i}
edges incoming to
V_{i}
will be forwarded. The source
S
will send arbitrary symbols along the other
a_{i}-b_{i}
incoming edges, which will however be ignored. At the concatenation of all (non-arbitrary) coordinates to an intermediate node with index in
I_{2}(\mathcal{N},t)\cup I_{3}(\mathcal{N},t)
, the outer code will be an MDS code with parameters

\left[\sum_{i\in I_{2}(\mathcal{N},t)}a_{i}+\sum_{i\in I_{3}(\mathcal{N},t)}b_{i},\ \left(\sum_{i\in I_{2}(\mathcal{N},t)}a_{i}+\sum_{i\in I_{3}(\mathcal{N},t)}b_{i}\right)-2t,\ 2t+1\right].

The MDS code is then decoded at the terminal. If
\sum_{i\in I_{2}(\mathcal{N},t)}a_{i}+\sum_{i\in I_{3}(\mathcal{N},t)}b_{i}\leq 2t
, we ignore the coordinates corresponding to
I_{2}(\mathcal{N},t)\cup I_{3}(\mathcal{N},t)
in this scheme.
In conclusion, via the intermediate nodes in
I_{2}(\mathcal{N},t)\cup I_{3}(\mathcal{N},t)
we have added
Y
information symbols decodable at the terminal.

This concludes the proof.

Proposition 6.3
.

Let
\mathfrak{A}_{t}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E},S,\{T\})
be a member of
Family

. Let
\mathcal{A}
be any network alphabet and let
\mathcal{U}_{S}
be the set of edges of
\mathfrak{A}_{t}
directly connected to
S
.
We have

\textnormal{C}_{1}(\mathfrak{A}_{2},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{U}_{S},2)\geq 1.

Proof.

Fix two distinct alphabet symbols
*,*^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}
.
The source
S
encodes each element of
\mathcal{A}
using a six-times repetition code. Vertex
V_{1}

simply forwards the received symbols, while vertex
V_{2}
proceeds as follows.
If, on the four incoming edges, an alphabet symbol appears at least three times,
V_{2}
forwards that symbol on both outgoing edges.
Otherwise
V_{2}
outputs
*
on one edge, and
*^{\prime}
on the other. At destination, if the incoming symbols from

match, then the terminal

decodes to that symbol. Otherwise, it decodes to the alphabet symbol sent from

.

We also give the following less sophisticated lower bound on the capacity of simple 2-level networks. The idea behind the proof is to construct a subnetwork of the original one where a lower bound for the capacity can be easily established.

Proposition 6.4
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{A}
is a sufficiently large finite field.
For all
t\geq 0
we have

\textnormal{C}_{1}(\mathcal{N},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{U}_{S},t)\geq\max\left\{0,\,\sum_{i=1}^{n}\min\{a_{i},b_{i}\}-2t\right\}.

(7)

Proof.

We will construct a network
\mathcal{N}^{\prime}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E}^{\prime},S,\{T\})
such that

\textnormal{C}_{1}(\mathcal{N},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{U}_{S},t)\geq\textnormal{C}_{1}(\mathcal{N}^{\prime},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{U}^{\prime}_{S},t),

where
\mathcal{U}^{\prime}_{S}
is the set of edges directly connected to
S
in the new network
\mathcal{N}^{\prime}
.
For
i\in I_{2}(\mathcal{N},t)
, remove the first
b_{i}-a_{i}
outgoing edges from
V_{i}.
Similarly, for each
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}\setminus I_{2}(\mathcal{N},t)
, remove the first
a_{i}-b_{i}

incoming edges to
V_{i}
. Denote the new network obtained in this way by
\mathcal{N}^{\prime}
and observe that all intermediate nodes
V_{i}^{\prime}
in
\mathcal{N}^{\prime}
have
\partial^{-}(V_{i}^{\prime})=\partial^{+}(V_{i}^{\prime})
. This implies that
I_{2}(\mathcal{N}^{\prime},t)=\{1,\ldots,n\}.
Since removing edges cannot increase the capacity (technically, this is a consequence of Proposition
2.6
), we have
\textnormal{C}_{1}(\mathcal{N},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{U}_{S},t)\geq\textnormal{C}_{1}(\mathcal{N}^{\prime},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{U}_{S},t).
We will give a coding scheme for
\mathcal{N}^{\prime}
that achieves the right-hand side of (
7
) to conclude the proof.
Consider the edge-cut

\mathcal{E}^{\prime}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\textnormal{in}(V_{i}^{\prime}).

By the definition of
\mathcal{N}^{\prime}
, we have
|\mathcal{E}^{\prime}|=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\min\{a_{i},b_{i}\}
.
Since the left-hand side of (
7
) is always non-negative, we shall assume

|\mathcal{E}^{\prime}|>2t
without loss of generality.
Under this assumption, we will prove that the rate

|\mathcal{E}^{\prime}|-2t

is achievable.

We choose the outer code
\mathcal{C}
to be an MDS code with parameters
[|\mathcal{E}^{\prime}|,|\mathcal{E}^{\prime}|-2t,2t+1]
over the finite field
\mathcal{A}
. All of the intermediate nodes forward incoming packets
(the number of outgoing edges will now allow this). The terminal receives a codeword of length
|\mathcal{E}^{\prime}|
and decodes it according to the chosen MDS code. ∎

.

Let

In particular, the Generalized Network Singleton Bound of Corollary

Proof.

We let
q:=|\mathcal{A}|
for ease of notation (but note that

does not need to be a prime power). We will construct
a network code
\mathcal{F}=\{\mathcal{F}_{1},\mathcal{F}_{2}\}

for

Recall that

by the very definition of Family

.

Case 1
:

. Let
\mathcal{A}=\{a,b\}.
Encode each element of
\mathcal{A}

achieves the desired capacity, where
\mathcal{C}=\{(a,a,a,a,a),(b,b,b,b,b)\}
and

\mathcal{F}_{1}(v)=v

(a,b)&\text{if}\ \ w\in S^{\textnormal{H}}_{1}(a,a,a),\\
(b,a)&\text{if}\ \ w\in S^{\textnormal{H}}_{1}(b,b,b).\\
\end{cases}

:
\max\{q,t\}\geq 3
. Encode each element of
\mathcal{A}
using a

-times repetition code, so that the codewords of

are given by

We will next define the function
\mathcal{F}_{2}

is defined in Notation
5.3
.

is the set of words that do not belong to any ball of radius

centered at the projection of a codeword, so that the union of

with the collection of the shells described above is all of

. Let

. Since
\max\{q,t\}\geq 3
,

q^{\prime}<q^{t}.

\mathcal{F}_{2}
to be such that the sets

and

element of
\mathcal{A}^{t}
.

Decoding at the terminal is accomplished as follows. Suppose that the terminal receives the pair
(x,y)\in\mathcal{A}^{t}\times\mathcal{A}^{t}
. First, the set
\mathcal{F}_{2}^{-1}(y)
is computed. If
\mathcal{F}_{2}^{-1}(y)=D
, then the terminal decodes to the majority of the coordinates in

,

was defined. If

.

(and up to

.

Theorem 7.6

(Double-Cut-Set Bound)
.

Let
\mathcal{N}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E},S,\bf T)
be a network,
\mathcal{A}
a network alphabet,
\mathcal{U}\subseteq\mathcal{E}
a set of edges and
t\geq 0
. Let
T\in{\bf T}
and let
\mathcal{E}_{1}
and
\mathcal{E}_{2}
be edge-cuts between
S
and
T
with the property that
\mathcal{E}_{1}
precedes
\mathcal{E}_{2}
. We have

\textnormal{C}_{1}(\mathcal{N},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{U},t)\leq\max_{\mathcal{F}}\,\textnormal{C}_{1}(\Omega[\mathcal{N},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{F},\mathcal{E}_{1}\to\mathcal{E}_{2},\mathcal{U}\cap\mathcal{E}_{1},t]),

where the maximum is taken over all
the network codes
\mathcal{F}
for
(\mathcal{N},\mathcal{A})
.

Proof.

Fix a network code
\mathcal{F}
for

(\mathcal{N},\mathcal{A})
. We consider the (fictitious) scenario where up to
t
errors can occur only on the edges from
\mathcal{U}\cap\mathcal{E}_{1}
. This scenario is modeled by the concatenation of channels

\Omega[\mathcal{N},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{F},\textnormal{out}(S)\to\mathcal{E}_{1},\mathcal{U}\cap\textnormal{out}(S),0]\,\blacktriangleright\,\Omega[\mathcal{N},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{F},\mathcal{E}_{1}\to\mathcal{E}_{2},\mathcal{U}\cap\mathcal{E}_{1},t]\\
\blacktriangleright\,\Omega[\mathcal{N},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{F},\mathcal{E}_{2}\to\textnormal{in}(T),\mathcal{U}\cap\mathcal{E}_{2},0],

(10)

where the three channels
in (
10
) are of the type introduced in Notation
7.3
.
Note moreover that the former and the latter channels in (
10
) are deterministic( see Definition
2.1
) as we consider an adversarial power of 0. They describe the transfer from the source to
\mathcal{E}_{1}
and from
\mathcal{E}_{2}
to
T
, respectively.
We set
\hat{\Omega}:=\Omega[\mathcal{N},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{F},\textnormal{out}(S)\to\mathcal{E}_{1},\mathcal{U},0]
and

\overline{\Omega}:=\Omega[\mathcal{N},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{F},\mathcal{E}_{2}\to\textnormal{in}(T),\mathcal{U},0]
to simplify the notation throughout the proof.

The channel
\Omega[\mathcal{N},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{F},S\to T,\mathcal{U},t]
is coarser (Definition
2.5
) than the channel in (
10
), since in the latter the errors can only occur on a subset of
\mathcal{U}
. In symbols, using Proposition
2.8
we have

\Omega[\mathcal{N},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{F},S\to T,\mathcal{U},t]\,\geq\,\hat{\Omega}\,\blacktriangleright\,\Omega[\mathcal{N},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{F},\mathcal{E}_{1}\to\mathcal{E}_{2},\mathcal{U}\cap\mathcal{E}_{1},t]\,\blacktriangleright\,\overline{\Omega}.

By Propositions
2.6
and
2.9
,
this implies
that

\textnormal{C}_{1}(\Omega[\mathcal{N},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{F},S\to T,\mathcal{U},t])\leq\textnormal{C}_{1}(\Omega[\mathcal{N},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{F},\mathcal{E}_{1}\to\mathcal{E}_{2},\mathcal{U}\cap\mathcal{E}_{1},t]).

(11)

Since (
11
) holds for any
\mathcal{F}
,
Proposition
2.19
finally gives

\textnormal{C}_{1}(\mathcal{N},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{U},t)\leq\max_{\mathcal{F}}\,\textnormal{C}_{1}(\Omega[\mathcal{N},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{F},\mathcal{E}_{1}\to\mathcal{E}_{2},\mathcal{U}\cap\mathcal{E}_{1},t]),

concluding the proof.
∎

8
Linear Capacity

As mentioned in Sections
1
and
2
,
in the presence of an “unrestricted” adversarial noise
the (1-shot) capacity of a network can be achieved by combining a rank-metric (outer) code with a
linear
network code;
see
[
22
,
5
,
20
,
13
]
. In words, this means that the intermediate nodes of the network focus on spreading information, while decoding is performed in an end-to-end fashion.

In this section, we show that
the strategy outlined above is far from being optimal when the adversary is restricted to operate
on a proper subset of the network edges.
In fact, we establish
some strong separation results
between the capacity (as defined in Section
2
) and the “linear” capacity of a network,
which we define by imposing that the intermediate nodes combine packets linearly. This indicates that implementing
network
decoding
becomes indeed necessary to achieve capacity in the scenario where the adversary is restricted.
The following definitions make the concept of linear capacity rigorous.

Definition 8.1
.

Let
\mathcal{N}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E},S,\bf T)
be a network and
\mathcal{A}
an alphabet. Consider a
network code

\mathcal{F}
for
(\mathcal{N},\mathcal{A})
as in Definition
2.14
.
We say that
\mathcal{F}
is a

linear
network code if
\mathcal{A}
is a finite field
and each function
\mathcal{F}_{V}
is
\mathcal{A}
-linear.

We can now define the linear version of the 1-shot capacity of an adversarial network, i.e., the analogue of Definition
2.18
.

Definition 8.2
.

Let
\mathcal{N}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E},S,\bf T)
be a network,
\mathcal{A}
a finite field,

\mathcal{U}\subseteq\mathcal{E}
an edge set,
and
t\geq 0
an integer.
The (
1-shot
)
linear capacity

of
(\mathcal{N},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{U},t)

is the largest
real number
\kappa
for which there exists an
outer code

\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{A}^{\partial^{+}(S)}

and a linear network code
\mathcal{F}
for
(\mathcal{N},\mathcal{A})

with
\kappa=\log_{|\mathcal{A}|}(|\mathcal{C}|)
such that

\mathcal{C}
is unambiguous for each channel

\Omega[\mathcal{N},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{F},S\to T,\mathcal{U},t]
,
T\in{\bf T}
. The notation for such largest
\kappa
is

\textnormal{C}^{\textnormal{lin}}_{1}(\mathcal{N},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{U},t).

Note that in the definition of linear capacity we do not require
\mathcal{C}
to be a linear code, but only that the network code
\mathcal{F}
is linear.

The first result of this section shows that the linear capacity of any member of Family
D
is zero. This is in sharp contrast with Theorem
6.11
.

Theorem 8.3
.

Let
\mathfrak{D}_{t}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E},S,\{T\})
be a member of
Family
D
. Let
\mathcal{A}
be any finite field
and let
\mathcal{U}_{S}
be the set of edges of
\mathfrak{D}_{t}
directly connected to
S
. We have

\textnormal{C}^{\textnormal{lin}}_{1}(\mathfrak{D}_{t},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{U}_{S},t)=0.

In particular, the linear capacity of the Mirrored Diamond Network of Figure
7
is zero.

Proof.

Let
q:=|\mathcal{A}|
. Fix any linear network code
\mathcal{F}=\{\mathcal{F}_{1},\mathcal{F}_{2}\}

for
(\mathfrak{D}_{t},\mathcal{A})
and
let
\mathcal{C}
be an unambigious code for the channel

\Omega[\mathfrak{D}_{t},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{F},S\to T,\mathcal{U}_{S},t]
.
Suppose that
|\mathcal{C}|\geq 2
and let
x,a\in\mathcal{C}
with
x\neq a
such that

x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{2t},x_{2t+1},\ldots,x_{4t}),\quad a=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{2t},a_{2t+1},\ldots,a_{4t}),

and

\mathcal{F}_{1}(u_{1},\ldots,u_{2t})=\sum_{i=1}^{2t}\lambda_{i}u_{i},\quad\mathcal{F}_{2}(u_{2t+1},\ldots,u_{4t})=\sum_{i=2t+1}^{4t}\lambda_{i}u_{i},

where
\lambda_{r}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
for
1\leq r\leq 4t
and
u\in\mathcal{A}^{4t}
.
We let
\Omega:=\Omega[\mathfrak{D}_{t},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{F},S\to T,\mathcal{U}_{S},t]
to simplify the notation throughout the remainder of the proof.

We start by observing that
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{2t}
cannot all be 0. Similarly,
\lambda_{2t+1},\ldots,\lambda_{4t}
cannot all be 0 (it is easy to see that the adversary can
cause ambiguity otherwise). Therefore we shall assume
\lambda_{1}\neq 0
and
\lambda_{4t}\neq 0
without loss of generality.
We will now construct vectors
y,b\in\mathcal{A}^{4t}

such that
d^{\textnormal{H}}(x,y)=d^{\textnormal{H}}(a,b)=1
.
Concretely, let

•

y_{i}=x_{i}\mbox{ for }1\leq i\leq 4t-1
,

•

y_{4t}=a_{4t}+\sum_{i=2t+1}^{4t-1}\lambda_{4t}^{-1}\lambda_{i}(a_{i}-x_{i})
,

•

b_{1}=x_{1}+\sum_{i=2}^{2t}\lambda_{1}^{-1}\lambda_{i}(x_{i}-a_{i})
,

•

b_{i}=a_{i}\mbox{ for }2\leq i\leq 4t
.

It follows from the definitions that
d^{\textnormal{H}}(x,y)=d^{\textnormal{H}}(a,b)=1
and that

z_{x}:=\left(\sum_{r=1}^{2t}\lambda_{r}y_{r},\ \sum_{r=2t+1}^{4t}\lambda_{r}y_{r}\right)\in\Omega(x),\qquad z_{a}:=\left(\sum_{r=1}^{2t}\lambda_{r}b_{r},\ \sum_{r=2t+1}^{4t}\lambda_{r}b_{r}\right)\in\Omega(a).

However, one easily checks that

z_{x}=z_{a}
, which in turn implies that

\Omega(x)\cap\Omega(a)\neq\emptyset
. Since
x
and
a
were arbitrary elements of
\mathcal{C}
, this
establishes the theorem.
∎

By proceeding as in the proof of Theorem
8.3
, one can check that the linear capacity
of any member of Family
E
is zero as well. This can also be established by observing that
\mathfrak{E}_{t}
is a “subnetwork” of
\mathfrak{D}_{t}
for all
t
.

Theorem 8.4
.

Let
\mathfrak{E}_{t}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E},S,\{T\})
be a member of
Family
E
. Let
\mathcal{A}
be any finite field and let
\mathcal{U}_{S}
be the set of edges of
\mathfrak{E}_{t}
directly connected to
S
. We have

\textnormal{C}^{\textnormal{lin}}_{1}(\mathfrak{E}_{t},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{U}_{S},t)=0.

In particular, the linear capacity of the Diamond Network of Section
3
is zero.

We conclude this section by observing
that the proof of
Proposition
6.4

actually uses a linear network code.
In particular, the following holds.

Proposition 8.5
.

Let
\mathcal{N}=([a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}],[b_{1},\ldots,b_{n}])=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E},S,\{T\})
be a simple 2-level network,
\mathcal{A}
a sufficiently large finite field,
t\geq 0
.
Let
\mathcal{U}_{S}
denote the set of edges directly connected to
S
. Then

\textnormal{C}^{\textnormal{lin}}_{1}(\mathcal{N},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{U}_{S},t)\geq\max\left\{0,\sum_{i=1}^{n}\min\{a_{i},b_{i}\}-2t\right\}.

Finally, by combining
Proposition
8.5
and Theorem
5.9
, we obtain the following result on the capacities of the members of Family

.

Let
\mathfrak{B}_{s}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E},S,\{T\})
be a member of
Family

. Let

9
Conclusions and Future Research Directions

In this paper, we considered the
1-shot capacity of multicast networks
affected by adversarial noise
restricted to a proper subset of vulnerable edges.
We introduced a formal framework to study
these networks based on the notions of adversarial channels and fan-out sets.
We defined five families
of 2-level networks that
play the role of fundamental
stepping stones in our developed theory,
and derived upper and lower bounds for the capacities of these families.
We also showed that upper
bounds for 2-level and 3-level networks can be ported to arbitrarily large networks via a Double-Cut-Set Bound.
Finally, we analyzed the capacity of certain partially vulnerable networks under the assumption that the intermediate nodes combine packets linearly.

The results presented in this paper show that
classical approaches to estimate or achieve capacity in multicast communication networks affected by an unrestricted adversary (cut-set bounds, linear network coding, rank-metric codes) are far from being optimal when the adversary is restricted to operate on a proper subset of the network edges. Moreover, the non-optimality
comes precisely from limiting the adversary to operate on a certain region of the network, which in turn forces the intermediate nodes to partially
decode
information before forwarding it towards the terminal. This is in strong contrast with the typical scenario within network coding, where capacity can be achieved in an
end-to-end
fashion using (random) linear network coding combined with a rank-metric code.

We conclude this paper by mentioning three research directions that naturally originate from our work.

1.

It remains an open problem to compute the capacities of three of the five fundamental families of networks we introduced in Subsection
4.3
for arbitrary values of the parameters.
We believe that this problem is challenging and requires developing new coding theory methods of combinatorial flavor that extend traditional packing arguments.

2.

A very natural continuation of this paper lies in the study of the scenario where a network can be used multiple times for communication, which we excluded from this first treatment.
The multi-shot scenario is modeled by the
power channel
construction; see
[
20
,
21
]
.

3.

Most of our results and techniques extend to networks having multiple sources. This is another research direction that arises from this paper very naturally.
