A dynamic p p -Laplacian
Alvaro de Diego Unanue, Gary Froyland, Oliver Junge, Péter Koltai

Abstract
We generalise the dynamic Laplacian introduced in (Froyland, 2015) to a dynamic p p -Laplacian, in analogy to the generalisation of the standard 2 2 -Laplacian to the standard p p -Laplacian for p > 1 p>1 . Spectral properties of the dynamic Laplacian are connected to the geometric problem of finding “coherent” sets with persistently small boundaries under dynamical evolution, and we show that the dynamic p p -Laplacian shares similar geometric connections. In particular, we prove that the first eigenvalue of the dynamic p p -Laplacian with Dirichlet boundary conditions exists and converges to a dynamic version of the Cheeger constant introduced in (Froyland, 2015) as p → 1 p\rightarrow 1 . We develop a numerical scheme to estimate the leading eigenfunctions of the (nonlinear) dynamic p p -Laplacian, and through a series of examples we investigate the behaviour of the level sets of these eigenfunctions. These level sets define the boundaries of sets in the domain of the dynamics that remain coherent under the dynamical evolution.

We now aim to generalise the

Recall that we consider a volume-preserving diffeomorphism

Equipped with this we define a dynamic version

\displaystyle{F}^{D}(u)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2}\left(\|\nabla u\|_{p}^{p}+\|\nabla T_{*}u\|_{p}^{p}\right)

or equivalently

{F}^{D}=\frac{1}{2}(F+F\circ T_{*}).

It now follows directly from the differentiability of

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\bigl(F^{\prime}(u)v+F^{\prime}(T_{*}u)T_{*}v\bigr)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}p\bigl((\Delta_{p}u)v+(T^{*}\Delta_{p}T_{*}u)v\bigr).

To get from (

as the purely linear

as a map on

\displaystyle{\Delta}^{D}_{p}u

\displaystyle:=-\frac{1}{p}({F}^{D})^{\prime}(u)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\Delta_{p}u+T^{*}\Delta_{p}T_{*}u\right).

, we have:

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
d\geq 2
,
1<p<2
and
M\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}

a compact
d
-dimensional manifold with
nonempty Lipschitz boundary.
Define
{J}^{D}:W_{0}^{1,p}(M)\backslash\{0\}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
by

{J}^{D}(u):=\frac{{F}^{D}(u)}{G(u)}.

The infimum

{\lambda}^{D}_{p}(M,T):=\inf_{u\in W_{0}^{1,p}(M)\backslash\{0\}}{J}^{D}(u)

(20)

is positive and achieved by a function
{u}^{D}_{p}\in W_{0}^{1,p}(M)
satisfying

-{\Delta}^{D}_{p}{u}^{D}_{p}={\lambda}^{D}_{p}{u}^{D}_{p}|{u}^{D}_{p}|^{p-2}

(21)

weakly.
We call
{\lambda}^{D}_{p}
the
first eigenvalue
of

-{\Delta}^{D}_{p}
and

{u}^{D}_{p}
a
first eigenfunction
of
-{\Delta}^{D}_{p}
.

Remark 3.1
.

We do not prove a uniqueness result as in
2.3
and thus do not talk about
the
first eigenfunction of
-{\Delta}^{D}_{p}
in
3.1
. The eigenfunction
u^{D}_{p}
is also not proven to be positive, in analogy to the positivity
result in
2.2
.

Proof of
3.1
.

We use the standard direct method that is
also used for the (static)
p
-Laplacian. The
crucial part is weak lower semicontinuity of the
numerator of
{J}^{D}
.
It is well known that
u\mapsto\|\nabla u\|_{p}^{p}

is weakly lower semicontinuous, as it is continuous
and convex (see e.g.
[
6
, Theorem 1.5.3]
).
To establish weak lower semicontinuity of

u\mapsto\|\nabla T_{*}u\|_{p}^{p}
, we first show
that for every weakly convergent series

u_{k}\rightharpoonup u
we also have
T_{*}u_{k}\rightharpoonup T_{*}u
. This is a simple
consequence of linearity and boundedness of
T_{*}
:
For any
f\in W^{1,p}_{0}(M)^{*}
we can define

\tilde{f}:=f\circ T_{*}\in W^{1,p}_{0}(M)^{*}
,
and hence
u_{k}\rightharpoonup u
implies

f(T_{*}u_{k})=\tilde{f}(u_{k})\rightarrow\tilde{f}(u)=f(T_{*}u).

This proves
T_{*}u_{k}\rightharpoonup T_{*}u
and implies that
for a weakly lower semicontinuous map
H
, the map

H\circ T_{*}
is also weakly lower semicontinuous.
In particular the functional
u\mapsto\|\nabla T_{*}u\|_{p}^{p}

and hence also the functional

{F}^{D}(u):=\frac{1}{2}\left(\|\nabla u\|_{p}^{p}+\|\nabla T_{*}u\|_{p}^{p}\right)

are weakly lower semicontinuous.
Now we can proceed with a standard argument,
as seen e.g. in

[
6
, Theorem 2.6.11]
:
Clearly,
{J}^{D}
is bounded from below by
0
and
thus has a non-negative finite infimum
{\lambda}^{D}_{p}
.
The Poincaré inequality establishes that
for some positive
C
,

G(u)=\|u\|_{p}^{p}\leq C\|\nabla u\|_{p}^{p}\leq 2C{F}^{D}(u)

and thus
{\lambda}^{D}_{p}\geq\frac{1}{2C}
is positive.
Let
(u_{k})_{k}
be a minimising sequence for
{J}^{D}
.
We can assume without loss that
\|u_{k}\|_{p}=1
for all
k
, as
{J}^{D}
is homogeneous. The sequence is
therefore bounded in
W_{0}^{1,p}(M)
and we
can pass to a weakly convergent
subsequence and assume
u_{k}\rightharpoonup u
for
some
u\in W_{0}^{1,p}(M)
.
Now by the Rellich–Kondrachov theorem (see e.g.
[
2
, Theorem 6.3]
) the inclusion mapping

W_{0}^{1,p}(M)\xhookrightarrow{}L^{p}(M)
is compact,
and as compact operators map weakly convergent
sequences to strongly convergent ones, we have

u_{k}\rightarrow u
in
L^{p}
and hence
G(u):=\|u\|_{p}^{p}=1
.
Finally, by weak lower semicontinuity of
{F}^{D}
we have

{\lambda}^{D}_{p}\leq{J}^{D}(u)={F}^{D}(u)\leq\liminf_{k}{F}^{D}(u_{k})=\liminf_{k}{J}^{D}(u_{k})={\lambda}^{D}_{p}

and hence
{\lambda}^{D}_{p}
is achieved by some
{u}^{D}_{p}:=u
.
To show that
{u}^{D}_{p}
satisfies (
21
), note that since
{u}^{D}_{p}
is a global minimum of
{J}^{D}={F}^{D}/G
we have
({J}^{D})^{\prime}({u}^{D}_{p})=0
, so for all
v\in W_{0}^{1,p}(M)
:

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=({J}^{D})^{\prime}({u}^{D}_{p})(v)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{G({u}^{D}_{p})^{2}}(G({u}^{D}_{p})({F}^{D})^{\prime}({u}^{D}_{p})(v)-{F}^{D}({u}^{D}_{p})G^{\prime}({u}^{D}_{p})(v))

\displaystyle=({F}^{D})^{\prime}({u}^{D}_{p})(v)-{\lambda}^{D}_{p}G^{\prime}({u}^{D}_{p})(v).

Now the last expression is the weak form of

-p{\Delta}^{D}_{p}{u}^{D}_{p}=p{\lambda}^{D}_{p}{u}^{D}_{p}|{u}^{D}_{p}|^{p-2}
,
which shows the claim.
∎

In the proof, an inequality that plays a crucial role is

We prove this inequality for the

For the readers’ convenience, we state two classical results that we use in the proof.

Theorem 3.2

(Co-area formula)
.

Let

be a compact

. The formula is commonly stated with

The second theorem is a special case of Cavalieri’s Principle:

Theorem 3.3

(Cavalieri’s Principle)

Let

be a compact

-dimensional manifold with Lipschitz boundary and

Proof.

See
[
9
, Proposition I.3.3]

∎

Equipped with these results, we now proceed to the

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
M\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a compact
d
-dimensional
submanifold with Lipschitz boundary,

(22)

Proof.

We modify the proof that is given in
the appendix of
[

]
and

[

.

Step 1.
First we prove the inequality for

.
For this, assume

and define

.
By the coarea formula

\int_{M}|\nabla w|=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\ell_{d-1}(\partial A(t))\ dt,

(23)

where the boundary is relative to
M
.
If we apply the coarea formula to
T_{*}w
we get

\displaystyle\int_{M}|\nabla T_{*}w|

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\ell_{d-1}(\partial\{x\in M\mid T_{*}w(x)>t\})\ dt.

(24)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\ell_{d-1}(\partial\{x\in M\mid w(T^{-1}(x))>t\})\ dt.

(25)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\ell_{d-1}(\partial\{Ty\mid w(y)>t\})\ dt.

(26)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\ell_{d-1}(\partial\{Ty\mid y\in A(t)\})\ dt.

(27)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\ell_{d-1}(\partial(TA(t)))\ dt.

(28)

Now by
A.1
(c)
we can express the integrals on the right-hand side of
(
23
) and (
28
) by using the
sets
B(t):=\{x\in M\mid|w(x)|>t\}
to get

\displaystyle\int_{M}|\nabla w|

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\ell_{d-1}(\partial B(t))\ dt.

(29)

\displaystyle\int_{M}|\nabla T_{*}w|

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\ell_{d-1}(\partial(TB(t)))\ dt.

(30)

Combining (
29
) and (
30
) we can continue with

\displaystyle\int_{M}\frac{1}{2}(|\nabla w|+|\nabla T_{*}w|)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\ell_{d-1}(\partial B(t))+\ell_{d-1}(\partial TB(t))\ dt

(31)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\max|w|}\frac{\ell_{d-1}(\partial B(t))+\ell_{d-1}(\partial TB(t))}{\ell_{d}(B(t))}\ell_{d}(B(t))dt

(32)

\displaystyle\geq{h}^{D}(M,T)\int_{0}^{\max|w|}\ell_{d}(B(t))\ dt

(33)

\displaystyle={h}^{D}(M,T)\int_{M}|w|,

(34)

where in the last step we have applied Cavalieri’s principle to
|w|
.

For the step (
32
) to (
33
)
we also need to justify that

is in the admissible class
for the Cheeger problem for almost all

. First observe
that, as
|w|
is continuous, and vanishes on the

,
B(t)
does not touch
\partial M
if

.
As for the regularity of
\partial B(t)
, note that
by
A.1
(b) we have
\partial B(t)
is the disjoint union of
\partial A(t)
and
\partial A(-t)
for almost all
t
.
Sard’s
theorem tells us that
w^{-1}(t)
contains no

. Hence, by the implicit

has

boundary for
almost all

and so does

.
After applying the same argument
to

{h}^{D}(M,T)
.

The inequality (
34
) also holds for

as

is dense in

. This shows the claim for

.

Step 2.
Next we generalise to
p>1
, essentially by reducing the result to the case in Step 1.
For
p>1
and

define

\Phi(v):=|v|^{p-1}v
. As
(|x|^{p-1}x)^{\prime}=p|x|^{p-1}
, we
can use the chain rule to get that the

\Phi(v)
is

\nabla\Phi(v(x))=p|v(x)|^{p-1}\nabla v(x).

By Hölder’s inequality we can estimate

\displaystyle\int_{M}|\nabla\Phi(v)|=p\int_{M}|v|^{p-1}|\nabla v|\leq p\left(\int_{M}|v|^{(p-1)\frac{p}{p-1}}\right)^{\frac{p-1}{p}}\|\nabla v\|_{p}=p\|v\|_{p}^{p-1}\|\nabla v\|_{p},

(35)

implying that
w:=\Phi(v)\in W^{1,1}_{0}(M)

for

.

We repeat for

.
First note that

\Phi(T_{*}v(x))=\Phi(v(T^{-1}(x)))=|v(T^{-1}x)|^{p-1}v(T^{-1}x)=\left[T_{*}(|v|^{p-1}v)\right](x)=T_{*}(\Phi(v))(x)

and hence
\Phi(T_{*}v)=T_{*}(\Phi(v))
.
Thus, as above:

\int_{M}|\nabla T_{*}w|=\int_{M}|\nabla T_{*}\Phi(v)|=\int_{M}|\nabla\Phi(T_{*}(v))|\leq p\|T_{*}v\|^{p-1}_{p}\|\nabla T_{*}v\|_{p}=p\|v\|^{p-1}_{p}\|\nabla T_{*}v\|_{p}

(36)

using that

is volume-preserving in the last step.
Now on the right-hand side of (

), both norms are bounded by

and smoothness of

. Hence,
also

.
We can therefore apply inequality (

)
to get

\displaystyle{h}^{D}(M,T)

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{coareacavalieri}}{\leq}\frac{\int_{M}|\nabla w|+|\nabla T_{*}w|}{2\int_{M}|w|}

(37)

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{holder},\eqref{holder2}}{\leq}\frac{p\|v\|^{p-1}_{p}(\|\nabla v\|_{p}+\|\nabla T_{*}v\|_{p})}{2\int_{M}|v|^{p}}

\displaystyle=p\frac{\|\nabla v\|_{p}+\|\nabla T_{*}v\|_{p}}{2\|v\|_{p}}

Raising this to the power of

and using that convexity of

we arrive at

\displaystyle\left(\frac{{h}^{D}(M,T)}{p}\right)^{p}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{\|\nabla v\|_{p}+\|\nabla T_{*}v\|}{2\|v\|_{p}}\right)^{p}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\|\nabla v\|_{p}^{p}+\|\nabla T_{*}v\|_{p}^{p}}{2\|v\|^{p}_{p}}

which gives the desired inequality after passing to the infimum.

Finally, we can turn our attention to the behaviour of

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
M\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
and

be as in

. Then the first eigenvalue
{\lambda}^{D}_{p}(M,T)
converges to

as

Proof.

We use a mixture of arguments from
[
20
, Corollary 6]
,
and
[
14
, Lemma A.1 and Theorem 3.1]
.
By

we already have a lower
bound on
{\lambda}^{D}_{p}(M,T)
that converges to

{h}^{D}(M,T)
as
p\rightarrow 1
. To
construct an upper bound, consider a subdomain

A_{k}\subset M
with smooth boundary not touching

\partial M

and

\frac{\ell_{d-1}(\partial A_{k})+\ell_{d-1}(\partial TA_{k})}{2\ell_{d}(A_{k})}-{h}^{D}(M,T)<\frac{1}{k}\quad\text{for a $k\in\mathbb{N}$}.

Define

\displaystyle f_{\varepsilon}(x):=\begin{cases}1&x\in A_{k}\\
1-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}d(x,A_{k})&0<d(x,A_{k})<\varepsilon\\
0&d(x,A_{k})\geq\varepsilon\\
\end{cases}

(42)

with
d(x,A_{k}):=\inf_{y\in A_{k}}|x-y|
.
Then
f_{\varepsilon}
is in
W_{0}^{1,p}(M)
for
\varepsilon
small enough and

{\lambda_{p}}^{D}(M,T)\leq\frac{\|\nabla f_{\varepsilon}\|_{p}^{p}+\|\nabla T_{*}f_{\varepsilon}\|^{p}_{p}}{2\|f_{\varepsilon}\|_{p}^{p}}=:C_{p,\varepsilon,k}.

(43)

To remove the dependence on

, first
note that
|\nabla f_{\varepsilon}|\leq G_{\varepsilon}
for some

and

converges pointwise

for

,
hence by compactness of

and dominated convergence
\lim_{p\to 1}\|\nabla f_{\varepsilon}\|_{p}^{p}=\|\nabla f_{\varepsilon}\|_{1}
. Using that

is continuously
differentiable and applying the same argument to

and

yields

\lim_{p\rightarrow 1}C_{p,\varepsilon,k}=\frac{\|\nabla f_{\varepsilon}\|_{1}+\|\nabla T_{*}f_{\varepsilon}\|_{1}}{2\|f_{\varepsilon}\|_{1}}=:C_{\varepsilon,k}.

Now
\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\|f_{\varepsilon}\|_{1}=\ell_{d}(A_{k})
by dominated convergence and

\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\|\nabla f_{\varepsilon}\|_{1}=\ell_{d-1}(\partial A_{k})
, as

almost everywhere on
\Gamma_{\varepsilon}:=\{x\in M\backslash A\mid d(x,M)\leq\varepsilon\}
and zero elsewhere so
\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\int_{M}|\nabla f_{\varepsilon}|=\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\ell_{d}(\Gamma_{\varepsilon})/\varepsilon=\ell_{d-1}(\partial A)
(see also proof of
[
14
, Theorem 3.1]
).
Following the proof of
[
14

,
using
[

\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\|\nabla T_{*}f_{\varepsilon}\|_{1}=\ell_{d-1}(T\partial A_{k})=\ell_{d-1}(\partial TA_{k})

By (

Finding eigenfunctions of the

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{G(u)^{2}}(G(u)({F}^{D})^{\prime}(u)v-{F}^{D}(u)G^{\prime}(u)v)

(51)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{b^{2}}\left(\frac{1}{2}b(F(u)v+F(T_{*}u)T_{*}v)-{a}^{D}G^{\prime}(u)v\right)

(52)

\displaystyle=\frac{p}{b^{2}}\int_{M}\left(\frac{1}{2}\left(|\nabla u|^{p-2}\nabla u\cdot\nabla v+|\nabla(u\circ T)|^{p-2}\nabla(u\circ T)\cdot\nabla(v\circ T)\right)-{a}^{D}|u|^{p-2}uv\right),

(53)

where
{a}^{D}:=\frac{1}{p}{F}^{D}(u)
. Defining
A(x):=DT^{-T}(x)
and using volume-preservation of
T
we make the replacement

\int_{M}|\nabla(u\circ T)|^{p-2}\nabla(u\circ T)\cdot\nabla(v\circ T)=\int_{M}|A\nabla u|^{p-2}(A\nabla u)\cdot(A\nabla v).

This yields

({J}^{D})^{\prime}(u)v=\frac{p}{b^{2}}\int_{M}\left(\frac{1}{2}\left(|\nabla u|^{p-2}\nabla u\cdot\nabla v+|A\nabla u|^{p-2}(A\nabla u)\cdot(A\nabla v)\right)-{a}^{D}|u|^{p-2}uv\right).

The descent direction.

We found that in the case of the dynamic

-Laplacian, it is not advisable
to define

using (

with
({J}^{D})^{\prime}
, as the resulting

defined by

(54)

and denote this choice by
{\grad}^{D}{J}^{D}:={d}^{D}
. Note that
because

\Big\langle\ell,\frac{1}{2}(I+A(x)^{T}A(x))\ell\Big\rangle\geq\frac{1}{2}|\ell|^{2}

for all

and
\ell\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and the coefficients of
A
are bounded in
M
, the left-hand side of (
54
) defines a uniformly strongly elliptic regular Dirichlet bilinear form of order
1
, so
[

, Theorem 7.5]
is again applicable if

has

boundary.

In our experiments, this choice of

lead to higher regularity of the descent direction, resulting in considerably bigger step sizes
(see

4.3
The second eigenpair

One of our numerical examples in
section

5
is on the torus
\mathbb{T}^{2}
, which has no boundary. This has the consequence that the infimum of
{J}^{D}
is
0
, realised by any constant function and carrying no information about the dynamics. We thus need to compute the second eigenfunction and therefore briefly describe the algorithm of Yao and Zhou for this case and present our version for the dynamic
p
-Laplacian
\smash{{\Delta}^{D}_{p}}
.

Note that the first eigenvalue
\lambda_{p}=\lambda_{p}^{(1)}
of
\Delta_{p}
is isolated
[
24
, Theorem 9]
. As the spectrum of
\Delta_{p}
is closed
[
24
, Theorem 3]
,
we may speak of the second eigenvalue
\lambda^{(2)}_{p}
of
\Delta_{p}

as the infimum over all eigenvalues bigger than
\lambda_{p}^{(1)}
. We assume this to be true for the dynamic
p
-Laplacian
\smash{{\Delta}^{D}_{p}}
as well.
The second eigenvalue can be characterised by the min-max-principle
[
36
, Equation (2.1)]
(cf. Appendix
A.1
for a derivation in the linear case)

\lambda^{(2)}_{p}=\min_{v\in L^{\prime}}\max_{u\in[L,v]\cap S}J(u),

(55)

where
L
is the span of the first eigenfunction
u_{p}=u_{p}^{(1)}
,
L^{\prime}
is a complement of
L
(i.e. such that
W^{1,p}_{0}(M)=L\oplus L^{\prime}
) and
[L,v]:=\textrm{span}(L,v)
.
By
S:=\{u\in W^{1,p}_{0}(M)\mid\|u\|=1\}
, we denote the unit sphere in
W^{1,p}_{0}(M)
and define
[L,v]_{S}:=\{t_{1}u_{p}^{(1)}+t_{2}v\mid t_{1}^{2}+t_{2}^{2}=1\}
.
Since
J(tu)=u
for all
t\neq 0
, and
[L,v]_{S}\xrightarrow{{u\ \mapsto\ u/\|u\|}}[L,v]\cap S
is a bijection, we have

\max\{J(u)\mid u\in[L,v]_{S}\}=\max\{J(u)\mid u\in[L,v]\cap S\}.

(56)

We work directly with the left-hand side of (
56
) as the optimisation is now finite-dimensional over
t_{1},t_{2}
.
From a maximiser of the left-hand side of (
56
) we can immediately obtain a maximiser of the right-hand side.

A local optimum of the maximisation in (
55
) can then be found with a finite-dimensional optimisation over the
t_{i}
(see
section

5.4
for implementation details).
If
u^{+}:L^{\prime}\rightarrow W^{1,p}_{0}(M)
is a
peak selection
of
J
, meaning

u^{+}(v)\in\arg\max\{J(u)\mid u\in[L,v]_{S}\},

it is thus sufficient to find

\displaystyle v_{2}:=\arg\min\{J(u^{+}(v))\mid v\in L^{\prime}\}

(57)

to get a second eigenfunction
u_{p}^{(2)}:=u^{+}(v_{2})
, i.e.
J(u_{p}^{(2)})=\lambda_{p}^{(2)}
. The minimisation problem (
57
) can be solved with a similar descent scheme as
Algorithm

1
: The crucial observation is that

-d
can be used as a descent direction for
v\mapsto J(u^{+}(v))

[
36
, Lemma 2.5]
. Note that this is subtly different from the
obvious application of (
48
)
which would be that
{\frac{d}{dt}J(u^{+}(v)-td)|_{t=0}<0}
, as opposed to
{\frac{d}{dt}J(u^{+}(v-td))|_{t=0}<0}
.
The corresponding scheme is given in
Algorithm

2
.

1.

Compute the first eigenfunction

with

.

2.

, choose an initial guess

.

(b)

For

define

6
Conclusions and outlook

We introduced a dynamic version of the nonlinear
p
-Laplace operator – denoted
{\Delta}^{D}_{p}
– suitable for the spectral analysis of dynamical systems.
In
3.1
we proved the existence of a positive leading eigenvalue
{\lambda}^{D}_{p}
for
-{\Delta}^{D}_{p}
with homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions.
Associated to the dynamic spectral geometry of the dynamical system is the dynamic Cheeger constant
{h}^{D}(M,T)
defined in (
6
), which quantifies how well the boundary of a carefully chosen subset in
M
can resist growth under evolution of the dynamics.
In
Equation

22
we developed a Cheeger-type inequality, relating
{\lambda}^{D}_{p}

and the dynamic Cheeger constant for
p\geq 1
.

3.5
showed that this inequality becomes increasingly tight as
p
approaches 1, and in fact that
\lim_{p\to 1}\lambda^{D}_{p}=h^{D}(M,T)
.

We then turned our attention to the numerical approximation of the dynamic
p
-Laplacian and its eigenfunctions.
We proposed an algorithm – modifying a similar algorithm for the classical
p
-Laplacian due to Yao & Zhou
[
36
]
– to solve the nonlinear eigenproblems for
1<p<2
, and compared the algorithm performance and solution quality for
p
between 1 and 2 on a variety of examples.
We found that (i) the level sets of the leading eigenfunctions tended to increasingly concentrate around the boundary of the optimal coherent set as
p\to 1
and (ii) the optimal level set for the dynamic 2-Laplacian was often not very different from the optimal level set of the dynamic
p
-Laplacian for
p
close to 1.
This first finding suggests that the “sharper” eigenfunctions of the dynamic
p
-Laplacian may be easier to use to find coherent sets than those of the dynamic
2
-Laplacian, including using multiple eigenfunctions post-processed via feature-separation algorithms such as
[
17
]
.
The second finding suggests that not too much is lost in terms of solution quality (where coherent set identification is quantified via
h^{D}(M,T)
) by using eigenfunctions of the dynamic 2-Laplacian.

There are numerous aspects of this work that could be addressed in the future.
One practical motivation for the current work was that (first) eigenfunctions of
\Delta_{p}
converge to characteristic functions of the Cheeger set. Although our results deliver positive empirical evidence, such a result for the dynamic
p
-Laplacian
{\Delta}^{D}_{p}
has yet to be proven.
Also, the uniqueness and positivity of the first eigenfunction of
{\Delta}^{D}_{p}
is not yet established, as discussed in
3.1
.

Finally, we have considered homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions, which is consistent with looking for sets
A\subset M
in the isoperimetric problem (
1
) that have a compact closure in
M
.
Neumann boundary conditions, on the other hand, translate to problems where
\partial A
can touch
\partial M
.
Results concerning Neumann boundary conditions for
\Delta_{p}
are less well developed, but extensions of our results to the dynamic
p
-Laplacian under Neumann boundary conditions are natural to pursue.

Appendix

A.1
On the min-max theorem

Theorem A.1

(Courant–Fischer)
.

Let
A\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
be symmetric. The eigenvalues
\lambda^{(1)}\leq\lambda^{(2)}\leq\cdots\leq\lambda^{(d)}
of
A
are given by

\lambda^{(k)}=\min\left\{\max\left\{\langle u,Au\rangle\mid u\in V\cap S\right\}\mid V\in V_{k}\right\},

where
V_{k}
is the set of
k
-dimensional subspaces of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
S
is the unit sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Proposition A.1
.

In the setting of Theorem
A.1
, if
u_{k}
denotes the
\|\cdot\|_{2}
-normalised eigenvector at
\lambda^{(k)}
,
L_{k}=\textrm{span}\left(u_{1},\ldots,u_{k}\right)=:[u_{1},\ldots,u_{k}]
, and
L_{k}^{\prime}
some fixed complement of
L_{k}
, then

\lambda^{(k)}=\min\left\{\max\left\{\langle u,Au\rangle\mid u\in[L_{k-1},v]\cap S\right\}\mid v\in L_{k-1}^{\prime}\right\}.

Moreover, if
v_{k}
denotes a minimiser of the above expression, then

u_{k}\in\arg\max\big\{\langle u,Au\rangle\mid u\in[L_{k-1},v_{k}]\cap S\big\}.

Proof.

In the following computation, line by line, we make use of the following facts:

1.

The Courant–Fischer theorem.

2.

For
v\in L_{k-1}^{\prime}
one has
[L_{k-1},v]\in V_{k}
.

3.

Estimating the minimum from above by setting
v=v_{k}\in L_{k-1}^{\prime}
such that
[L_{k-1},v_{k}]=L_{k}
.

4.

Using that the eigenvectors
u_{i}
are mutually orthogonal and setting
u=\sum_{i=1}^{k}t_{i}u_{i}
with
\sum_{i=1}^{k}t_{i}^{2}=1
.

The computation

\displaystyle\lambda^{(k)}

\displaystyle=\min\left\{\max\left\{\langle u,Au\rangle\mid u\in V\cap S\right\}\mid V\in V_{k}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\min\left\{\max\left\{\langle u,Au\rangle\mid u\in[L_{k-1},v]\cap S\right\}\mid v\in L_{k-1}^{\prime}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\max\left\{\langle u,Au\rangle\mid u\in L_{k}\cap S\right\}
