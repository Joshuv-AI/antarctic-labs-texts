Structured Sparsity Inducing Adaptive Optimizers for Deep Learning
Tristan Deleu, Yoshua Bengio

Abstract
The parameters of a neural network are naturally organized in groups, some of which might not contribute to its overall performance. To prune out unimportant groups of parameters, we can include some non-differentiable penalty to the objective function, and minimize it using proximal gradient methods. In this paper, we derive the weighted proximal operator, which is a necessary component of these proximal methods, of two structured sparsity inducing penalties. Moreover, they can be approximated efficiently with a numerical solver, and despite this approximation, we prove that existing convergence guarantees are preserved when these operators are integrated as part of a generic adaptive proximal method. Finally, we show that this adaptive method, together with the weighted proximal operators derived here, is indeed capable of finding solutions with structure in their sparsity patterns, on representative examples from computer vision and natural language processing.

Introduction

In machine learning, a large majority of problems involve the minimization of a composite loss function of the form

\min_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}f({\bm{x}})+h({\bm{x}}),

(1)

where
f
is a differentiable loss function, and
h
is a penalty function (or
regularizer
). Some standard penalties used in the context of training neural networks include the squared
\ell_{2}
norm (also called
ridge regression
in statistics, or
weight decay
in machine learning) and elastic net
(
Zou & Hastie 2005
)
. In this work, we focus on non-differentiable, and possibly non-convex, penalties leading to sparse solutions of this optimization problem. In particular, we are interested in penalties which encourage structure in the sparsity patterns of these solutions
(
Kyrillidis et al. 2015
)
, such as the mixed
\ell_{1}/\ell_{2}
norm, also known as
group Lasso
when
f
is the squared loss
(
Yuan & Lin 2006
)
. These penalties tend to produce solutions where complete groups of variables are zeroed-out, as opposed to the more frequently used
\ell_{1}
norm which yields unstructured sparsity. This class of penalties is called
structured sparsity inducing
penalties
(
Bach et al. 2012a
;
Bach et al. 2012b
)
.

This family of regularizers is particularly appropriate for neural networks, where the parameters are naturally organized in layers and groups of filters
(
Krizhevsky et al. 2012
;
Ioannou et al. 2017
)
. If some of these groups of parameters were exactly equal to zero, this would effectively be equivalent to deactivating certain neurons in the network
(
Zhou et al. 2016
)
. As a consequence, it may have an impact on the size of the model, reducing its memory footprint and computational cost at inference time. However in these structured sparsity inducing penalties, the groups of variables can be arbitrary, and they do not necessarily have to follow the layered nature of the neural network if we have some prior knowledge about the structure of the problem, such as a causal structure
(
Germain et al. 2015
;
Lachapelle et al. 2020
;
Ke et al. 2019
)
, or for feature selection.

To solve an optimization problem like the one in
Eq.

1
, it is standard practice in Deep Learning to use first-order methods, and in particular adaptive methods such as Adagrad
(
Duchi et al. 2011
)
, RMSprop
(
Tieleman & Hinton 2012
)
, or Adam
(
Kingma & Ba 2015
)
. Even when the penalty function
h
is non-differentiable, as is the case for structured sparsity inducing penalties, it is still possible to optimize this composite loss function using stochastic subgradient methods
(
Wen et al. 2016
)
. But even if these penalties are meant to introduce sparsity, the solutions found by subgradient methods are typically not sparse
(
Bach et al. 2012a
)
, and they often require a post-processing step where the parameters with a small magnitude are pruned out. An alternative approach is to use
proximal gradient methods
, which are specifically designed for the optimization of composite loss functions. These methods avoid choosing an arbitrary subgradient of
h
at non-differentiable points by applying a
proximal operator

(
Moreau 1962
)
, which can often be efficiently computed, to a standard gradient descent update rule.

While proximal gradient methods are often presented as derived from vanilla gradient descent, it has been shown that they can also be applied to adaptive first-order methods
(
Yang et al. 2020
;
Yun et al. 2020
)
, using a weighted version of the proximal operator
(
Hiriart-Urruty & Lemaréchal 1993
)
. For many penalties, including the
\ell_{1}
penalty
(
Becker et al. 2019
)
, the weighted proximal operator is a straightforward extension of its unweighted counterpart, and can often be computed in closed-form. However for structured sparsity inducing penalties, computing their weighted proximal operator becomes more challenging than their unweighted equivalent, due to their grouping property.

In this paper, we derive the
weighted proximal operator
of two different structured sparsity inducing penalties: the mixed
\ell_{1}/\ell_{2}
norm, and the group Minimax Concave Penalty
(
Breheny & Huang 2009
, group MCP;)
. We show that unlike their (unweighted) proximal operator, no closed-form solution exists for the weighted proximal operator of these penalties, although they can be approximated efficiently using a root finding algorithm. When this approximation is integrated as part of a stochastic proximal gradient method
(
Yun et al. 2020
)
, we prove that the resulting inexact method retains the same convergence guarantees as if the exact weighted proximal operator was applied. We further support empirically that this algorithm can find solutions with structure in their sparsity patterns, with examples from computer vision and natural language processing.

2
Related work

Pruning

In addition to having a smaller memory footprint, pruning groups of variables that only have a small impact on the network’s performance, such as filters in a convolutional neural network, can also significantly speedup inference on standard architectures
(
Li et al. 2017
)
. Closely related to our work,
Wen et al. 2016
used the mixed
\ell_{1}/\ell_{2}
norm in order to identify these groups, and minimized the objective with subgradient methods. However this required a post-processing step at the end of training because subgradient methods may fail at finding truly sparse solutions
(
Bach et al. 2012a
)
. In contrast, proximal gradient methods tend to not suffer from this problem, and they can find solutions with exact zeros.
Lin et al. 2019
also proposed to use structured sparsity regularization for pruning, with an approach inspired by ADMM.

Proximal gradient methods

Proximal gradient methods (also known as
forward-backward splitting
methods;
Duchi & Singer 2009
) is a class of first-order methods that are particularly designed for the optimization of composite objectives of the form of
Eq.

1
, where the non-differentiable penalty
h
admits a proximal operator that can be efficiently computed. See
Parikh & Boyd 2014
for a general introduction to proximal algorithms, and
Bach et al. 2012a
for an overview of proximal gradient methods for sparsity-inducing penalties (including structured sparsity).

Proximal gradient methods have also been embedded into adaptive first-order methods, such as Adam or RMSProp
(
Lee & Lee 2019
)
.
Yang et al. 2020
introduced
Prox-SGD
, an algorithm based on a weighted proximal operator to correct for the rescaling introduced by these adaptive methods. This algorithm was further refined in
ProxGen

(
Yun et al. 2020
)
, to treat the update as an exact proximal gradient step. Both of these methods are generic, and they can be applied to any penalty, provided the weighted proximal operator can be computed efficiently. But so far, their applications have been limited to sparsity-inducing penalties such as
\ell_{q}^{q}
, with
q\in[0,1]
, whose weighted proximal operators can be obtained in closed-form thanks to the separability of these penalties
(
Becker et al. 2019
)
. Our work is complementary, since we give an efficient algorithm to compute the weighted proximal operator of some structured sparsity inducing penalties, for which there is no closed-form solution available.
Melchior et al. 2019
also propose an alternative algorithm where the weighted proximal operator is approximated by iterating over the (unweighted) proximal operator.
Chen et al. 2020
proposed a Half-Space step, in conjunction with projected gradient methods, to increase the level of group-sparsity.

Finally, although we are focusing our attention here on first-order methods, which are more appropriate for Deep Learning applications, it is important to note that there also exists proximal quasi-Newton methods
(
Becker et al. 2019
)
, also based on weighted proximal operators, which are adapted from limited-memory quasi-Newton methods such as SR1 and L-BFGS.

3
Structured sparsity inducing penalties

Throughout this paper, we assume that we have a collection
{\mathcal{G}}
of (disjoint) groups of variables; for example, these groups might correspond to individual filters in a convolutional neural network
(
Wen et al. 2016
)
. Structured sparsity inducing penalties are penalty functions that encourage whole groups of variables as defined by
{\mathcal{G}}
to be ignored (see
Fig.

1
for an illustration). In this section, we will first recall the definition of two of these penalties, namely the mixed
\ell_{1}/\ell_{2}
norm and group MCP, and how they have been used in the past in conjunction with proximal gradient descent.

3.1
Mixed
\ell_{1}/\ell_{2}
norm

Taking inspiration from the
\ell_{1}
penalty, which is widely adopted for unstructured sparsity, the mixed
\ell_{1}/\ell_{2}
norm can be thought of as the
\ell_{1}
norm of a vector consisting of the
\ell_{2}
norms over each group in
{\mathcal{G}}
. More precisely, this penalty is defined as

h({\bm{x}})\triangleq\sum_{g\in{\mathcal{G}}}\lambda_{g}\|{\bm{x}}_{g}\|_{2},

(2)

where
\|{\bm{x}}_{g}\|_{2}=\sqrt{\sum_{j\in g}x_{j}^{2}}
is the
\ell_{2}
norm of the vector
{\bm{x}}
, restricted to group
g
. To account for groups of varying sizes, the weighting
\lambda_{g}
is typically set to
\lambda_{g}=\lambda\sqrt{|g|}
, where
|g|
is the number of elements in group
g

(
Murphy 2012
)
.

3.2
Bias reduction with non-convex penalties

Although applying the
\ell_{1}
penalty may result in sparse solutions, this penalty is also known to suffer from a
shrinkage
effect
(
Copas 1983
)
, introducing bias in the model. The mixed
\ell_{1}/\ell_{2}
norm inherits from this bias, but this time at the group level: groups of variables get pushed invariably towards
0
, regardless of their
\ell_{2}
norm.

In order to reduce this bias,
Zhang 2010
introduced a non-convex penalty called the
Minimax Concave Penalty
(MCP). MCP operates under two regimes: similarly to the
\ell_{1}
penalty, small values are shrunk towards
0
, but contrary to
\ell_{1}
, sufficiently large values are not penalized as much anymore. Formally, this penalty function is defined as

\MCP(x;\beta,\lambda)\triangleq\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\lambda|x|-\dfrac{x^{2}}{2\beta}&\textrm{if $|x|\leq\beta\lambda$}\\
\dfrac{\beta\lambda^{2}}{2}&\textrm{otherwise,}\end{array}\right.

(3)

with
\beta>1
controlling the magnitude beyond which values are no longer penalized.
Fig.

1
shows a comparison of
\MCP
against the
\ell_{1}
penalty. Similar to the mixed
\ell_{1}/\ell_{2}
norm, it is also possible to apply MCP to the
\ell_{2}
norm of groups of variables, in order to encourage structured sparsity while retaining the benefits of MCP: this is called group MCP
(
Breheny & Huang 2009
)

h({\bm{x}})=\sum_{g\in{\mathcal{G}}}\MCP(\|{\bm{x}}_{g}\|_{2};\beta,\lambda_{g}).

(4)

3.3
Proximal gradient descent

In order to minimize the composite objective in
Eq.

1
, the proximal gradient descent algorithm updates an iterate
{\bm{x}}_{t}
with an update similar to standard gradient descent, based on the gradient of the differentiable part
f

{\bm{x}}_{t+1}\leftarrow\prox_{\alpha h}\!\big({\bm{x}}_{t}-\alpha\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}f({\bm{x}}_{t})\big),

where
\alpha
is the learning rate, and
\prox_{\alpha h}
is the
proximal operator

(
Moreau 1962
)
of the penalty
h
, defined as

\prox_{\alpha h}({\bm{x}})=\argmin_{{\bm{z}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\frac{1}{2}\|{\bm{z}}-{\bm{x}}\|_{2}^{2}+\alpha h({\bm{z}}).

(5)

Interestingly, proximal gradient descent generalizes both standard gradient descent (when
h({\bm{x}})\equiv 0
), and projected gradient descent (when
h({\bm{x}})={\bm{I}}_{C}(x)
is the indicator function, equal to
0
if
{\bm{x}}\in C
, and
+\infty
otherwise). For many penalty functions of interest, even non-differentiable and possibly non-convex ones, the proximal operator can be computed efficiently, often in closed-form. This is true in particular for the structured sparsity inducing penalties presented in this paper.

Let’s take the example of the mixed
\ell_{1}/\ell_{2}
norm. First note that since the penalty in
Eq.

2
decomposes along the (disjoint) groups
g\in{\mathcal{G}}
, the proximal operator in
Eq.

5
benefits from the same decomposition, involving only the proximal operators of the
\ell_{2}
norm evaluated on each
{\bm{x}}_{g}
. Therefore, it is sufficient to know the proximal operator of the
\ell_{2}
norm
h({\bm{x}})=\lambda\|{\bm{x}}\|_{2}
in order to construct the update for proximal gradient descent, which is given in closed-form
(
Combettes & Wajs 2005
)
by

\prox_{\alpha h}({\bm{x}})=\left[1-\frac{\alpha\lambda}{\|{\bm{x}}\|_{2}}\right]_{+}\!{\bm{x}},

(6)

where
[z]_{+}=\max(0,z)
. Similarly, the group MCP enjoys the same decomposition property along groups, and there exists a closed-form expression for the proximal operator of the MCP /
\ell_{2}
penalty (i.e.
h({\bm{x}})=\MCP(\|{\bm{x}}\|_{2})
;
Breheny & Huang 2009
).

4
Adaptive proximal optimizers for structured sparsity inducing penalties

In practice in Deep Learning, the minimization of the objective is typically performed using stochastic and adaptive first-order methods, such as Adagrad, RMSprop, or Adam, where the update direction is rescaled with a diagonal preconditioning matrix
{\bm{D}}_{t}

{\bm{x}}_{t+1}\leftarrow{\bm{x}}_{t}-\alpha{\bm{D}}_{t}^{-1}{\bm{m}}_{t},

where
{\bm{m}}_{t}
is an estimate of the mean of the gradients of
f
; see also
Table

3
in
Section

C.1
for examples of matrices
{\bm{D}}
. Although it appears to be similar to the standard gradient descent update, the preconditioning
{\bm{D}}
makes composing these methods with a proximal operator more challenging.
Yun et al. 2020
proposed a general proximal method that is capable of dealing with this preconditioning, with an update of the form

{\bm{x}}_{t+1}\leftarrow\prox_{\alpha h}^{{\bm{D}}_{t}}\!\big({\bm{x}}_{t}-\alpha{\bm{D}}_{t}^{-1}{\bm{m}}_{t}\big),

(7)

where
\prox_{\alpha h}^{{\bm{D}}}
is the
weighted proximal operator
of
h

(
Hiriart-Urruty & Lemaréchal 1993
;
Lee et al. 2014
)

\prox_{\alpha h}^{{\bm{D}}}({\bm{x}})=\argmin_{{\bm{z}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\frac{1}{2}\|{\bm{z}}-{\bm{x}}\|_{{\bm{D}}}^{2}+\alpha h({\bm{z}}),

(8)

and
\|{\bm{z}}\|_{{\bm{D}}}^{2}=\langle{\bm{z}},{\bm{D}}{\bm{z}}\rangle
. This naturally extends to the case where the update is stochastic, see
Appendix

C
, and
Section

5
for details and additional theoretical guarantees. Contrary to the proximal operator, for which proximity is computed using the Euclidean distance, the weighted proximal operator captures the reweighting by
{\bm{D}}
by changing the geometry of the space over which proximity is measured.

However unlike their proximal operators (see
Section

3.3
), the weighted proximal operators of the
\ell_{2}
norm and MCP /
\ell_{2}
cannot be evaluated in closed-form in general.
Becker et al. 2019
;
Yang et al. 2020
show that we can get a closed-form expression for the mixed
\ell_{1}/\ell_{2}
norm when the preconditioning matrix
{\bm{D}}
decomposes along the groups into spherical matrices
{\bm{D}}_{g}=m{\bm{I}}_{g}
, which is not practical for Deep Learning applications: while being diagonal, the preconditioning matrix usually does not have the same value for all the variables belonging to the same group (e.g. it might be based on aggregated statistics from past gradients). In
Theorem

1
, we give an expression of the weighted proximal operator for the
\ell_{2}
norm, based on an implicit expression. Recall from
Section

3.3
that due to group decomposition, this is sufficient to compute the weighted proximal operator of the mixed
\ell_{1}/\ell_{2}
norm.

Theorem 1

(Weighted proximal operator of
\ell_{2}
)
.

Let
{\bm{D}}=\diag(d_{1},\ldots,d_{n})

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[\frac{d_{i}x_{i}}{d_{i}\theta^{\star}+\alpha\lambda}\right]^{2}=1.

(10)

The proof of

(

One consequence of this approximation of

The proof of

5
Convergence analysis

In this section, we will make stochasticity more explicit, and we will consider the following composite objective function

\min_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}\big(F({\bm{x}})\triangleq\mathbb{E}_{\xi}[f({\bm{x}};\xi)]+h({\bm{x}})\big).

(14)

Prior work studied the non-asymptotic convergence properties of stochastic proximal gradient descent on this kind of objective
(
Xu et al. 2019
;
Yun et al. 2020
)
. However, their results depend on the fact that the (weighted) proximal operator of
h
can be computed exactly, which is unfortunately impossible in practice for structured sparsity inducing penalties (their weighted version at least). We will see that these results remain valid, despite the approximation of the weighted proximal operator. Note that related to our work, convergence guarantees of (deterministic) inexact proximal gradient methods also exist, although with stronger conditions on the convexity of
f
and
h

(
Schmidt et al. 2011
)
.

Because the overall objective function
F
can be non-differentiable and non-convex, convergence is proven in terms of the expected distance of the
Frechet subdifferential

\widehat{\partial}F({\bm{x}}_{t})
to zero
1
1

1

This reduces to
\|\nabla F({\bm{x}}_{t})\|_{2}
when
F
is differentiable; see
Eq.

24

{\bm{x}}^{\star}
, we have
{\bm{0}}\in\widehat{\partial}F({\bm{x}}^{\star})
.

)

Assumption 1

(
Yun et al. 2020
)
.

x

1.

L-smoothness

\forall{\bm{x}},{\bm{y}},\|\nabla f({\bm{x}})-\nabla f({\bm{y}})\|_{2}\leq L\|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}}\|_{2}

\mathbb{E}_{\xi}[{\bm{g}}_{t}]=\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{t})

3.

(i) The update and (ii) the stochastic gradient are bounded, and (iii) the momentum parameter is exponentially decaying: (i)

Sufficiently positive-definite

6
Experimental results

To validate that proximal gradient methods with structured sparsity inducing penalties are indeed capable of finding solutions with structure in their sparsity patterns, we experimented with two families of representative architectures: convolutional neural networks from computer vision, and transformers from natural language processing. In all experiments with proximal gradient methods, we used the
ProxGen
algorithm
(
Yun et al. 2020
)
presented in
Section

4
, and the weighted proximal operators given in
Theorems

1
and
2
; the adaptive optimizer (i.e. the form of the preconditioning matrix) is Adam
(
Kingma & Ba 2015
)
. In order to approximate the weighted proximal operators, we used the Newton-Raphson algorithm for its fast convergence properties; see
Algorithm

3
for details about this procedure. We will get back to the choice of this algorithm in
Section

6.4
.

6.1

(
Simonyan & Zisserman 2015
)
on CIFAR-10 using both the mixed
\ell_{1}/\ell_{2}

Wen et al. 2016

Fig.

6.3
Pruning with indirect sparsity

Interestingly, as observed by
Li et al. 2017
, enforcing channel-wise and row-wise structured sparsity at the level of one layer has an effect on the computational efficiency of that one layer, but it has consequences on the neighboring layers as well: if one channel sees its outgoing weights being zeroed-out, then it does not contribute to the output of the network anymore, and can therefore be ignored in upstream computations as well. This has a significant impact on the effective size of the network, beyond group sparsity, where connections can be further pruned
indirectly
thanks to these structured sparsity inducing penalties without affecting predictions. The details of this pruning procedure are given in
Section

C.3
.

Fig.

2
shows the proportion of parameters preserved after applying this procedure on two networks: VGG-19 trained with
\ell_{1}/\ell_{2}
(group sparsity
22.03\%)

61.33\%
). The effective sparsity (i.e. the size of the pruned network, divided by the size of the original network) is about
3\%
for VGG-16, and
48\%
for BERT, with significant gains on some layers (e.g. 3 orders of magnitude fewer parameters for Conv9-12 in VGG-16). For BERT, we are still below the high (unstructured) sparsity of
Sanh et al. 2020
, where the same level of performance is reached with about
3\%
of the size of the original network. To achieve better sparsity with structured sparsity inducing penalties, Transformers in general could benefit from better choices of groups, e.g. spanning over multiple weight matrices; see
Section

D.3
for further discussion.

6.4
Approximation of the weighted proximal operator

Although our formulation of the weighted proximal operators for the mixed
\ell_{1}/\ell_{2}
norm and group MCP in
Theorems

1
and
2
is independent of the choice of the numerical solver to find
\theta^{\star}
, in practice we chose the Newton-Raphson algorithm. In this section, we evaluate empirically how efficient this algorithm is over the course of training, compared to other approximations of the weighted proximal operator. This also helps us to estimate the overhead induced by this routine over standard first-order methods.

As a direct replacement of Newton’s algorithm, we consider a bisection-based method, similar to the one described in
Duchi et al. 2011
. We also compare it to AdaProx
(
Melchior et al. 2019
)
, a general method that approximates the weighted proximal operator by iterating over the (unweighted) proximal operator. Since all three methods are iterative methods, we evaluate their efficiency as the number of iterations per gradient update. Similar to
Section

6.1
, we trained a VGG-16 network on CIFAR-10 with the mixed
\ell_{1}/\ell_{2}
norm as our penalty. In order to control for variations during the learning process, at each gradient update we approximate the weighted proximal operator with all three methods, but we only apply the one found by Newton’s algorithm. We also use the same tolerance
\varepsilon=10^{-6}
for all algorithms.

Fig.

3
shows the evolution of the number of iterations per group, and per gradient update, with the number of training steps. Thanks to the increasing group sparsity, the cost induced by both Newton’s algorithm and bisection decreases over time: in the case of
\ell_{1}/\ell_{2}
, only non-zero
\prox({\bm{x}})
require an approximation of
\theta^{\star}
(i.e. if
\|{\bm{D}}{\bm{x}}\|_{2}>\alpha\lambda
; see
Theorem

1
). AdaProx, on the other hand, iterates over the proximal operator of
\ell_{2}
regardless of our knowledge of
\|{\bm{D}}{\bm{x}}\|_{2}
, and therefore has a non-decreasing cost over the course of training. This cost is also significantly higher than the other two methods, because AdaProx was often reaching the maximum number of iterations, set at
100
per gradient update. Finally, Newton’s algorithm is about 3 times as fast as the bisection-based method, showing a clear advantage in terms of efficiency, and supporting our choice of this algorithm as part of our inexact proximal gradient method.

7
Conclusion

The weighted proximal operator is a quantity that plays a fundamental role in adaptive proximal gradient methods, a class of methods derived from adaptive optimizers in Deep Learning, specifically adapted for optimizing composite objectives with non-differentiable penalties. In this work, we derived the weighted proximal operators of two structured sparsity inducing penalties: the mixed
\ell_{1}/\ell_{2}
norm, and group MCP. We saw that unlike their unweighted counterparts, there exists no closed-form expression for the weighted proximal operators of these penalties. Nevertheless, we found that they can be efficiently approximated using the Newton-Raphson algorithm, and they can be incorporated as part of a general proximal gradient method called ProxGen
(
Yun et al. 2020
)
. We also proved that the convergence guarantees of this algorithm were maintained, despite the resulting method being inexact. We showed experimentally that this proximal gradient method applied to structured sparsity inducing penalties was capable of finding sparse solutions, with structure in their sparsity patterns, on examples from computer vision with VGG-16, and natural language processing with BERT, with a moderate loss of performance. Finally, we leveraged this structure to prune these models even further, and obtain much smaller models while keeping them functionally identical.

Going beyond pruning neural networks, proximal gradient methods, together with the weighted proximal operators derived here, can be applied to any problem where structure is known or expected a priori. In future work, we would like to evaluate this method on other choices of groups, for example motivated by the causal structure of the problem.

Appendix B
Proof of the convergence analysis

In this section, we prove the main theorem of convergence from
Section

5
, extending the analysis from
Xu et al. 2019
;
Yun et al. 2020
to inexact proximal gradient methods, under
2
on the approximation of the weighted proximal operator. Before getting the the proof of
Theorem

3
, we first recall the definition of the Frechet subdifferential, which plays a central role in the statement of convergence for non-differentiable and non-convex functions.

Definition 1

(Frechet subdifferential;
Rockafellar & Wets 2009
)
.

Let
F:{\mathbb{R}}^{N}\rightarrow\overline{{\mathbb{R}}}
be a function (possibly non-convex), and a point
\overline{{\bm{x}}}
such that
F(\overline{{\bm{x}}})
is finite. The
Frechet subdifferential
is the set
\widehat{\partial}F
of regular subgradients, satisfying:

\widehat{\partial}F(\overline{{\bm{x}}})=\bigg\{{\bm{v}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N}\;\Big|\;\liminf_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\bm{x}}\rightarrow\overline{{\bm{x}}}\\
{\bm{x}}\neq\overline{{\bm{x}}}\end{subarray}}\frac{F({\bm{x}})-F(\overline{{\bm{x}}})-\langle{\bm{v}},{\bm{x}}-\overline{{\bm{x}}}\rangle}{{\bm{x}}-\overline{{\bm{x}}}}\geq 0\bigg\}.

(24)

Recall that
F({\bm{x}})\triangleq\mathbb{E}_{\xi}[f({\bm{x}};\xi)]+h({\bm{x}})
. We are now ready to prove
Theorem

3
:

Theorem
3
.

Suppose that
1
and
2
are satisfied. If we run
Algorithm

2
with a non-increasing step-size
\alpha_{t}
, such that
\alpha_{0}<\delta/2L
, then the output
{\bm{x}}_{R}
of
Algorithm

2

is sampled uniformly in

, satisfies

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{R}\big[\dist\!\big({\bm{0}},\widehat{\partial}F({\bm{x}}_{R})\big)^{2}\big]\leq\frac{C_{1}}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\|{\bm{g}}_{t}-\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{t})\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{C_{2}\Delta}{T}+\frac{C_{3}}{T},

with
C_{1}
,
C_{2}
, and

positive constants independent of

. Here

{\bm{z}}
.

Proof.

The proof follows the same steps as the proof of the non-asymptotic convergence analysis from

(
Yun et al. 2020

2

\displaystyle\sum_{t=0}^{+\infty}\varepsilon_{t+1}^{2}<+\infty\qquad

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\qquad\forall t\geq 0,\ \|{\bm{x}}_{t+1}-{\bm{x}}_{t+1}^{\star}\|_{2}\leq\varepsilon_{t+1}\leq D^{\prime}

(
1
)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\qquad\forall t\geq 0,\ \|{\bm{x}}_{t+1}^{\star}-{\bm{x}}_{t}\|_{2}\leq\|{\bm{x}}_{t+1}-{\bm{x}}_{t}\|_{2}+\|{\bm{x}}_{t+1}-{\bm{x}}_{t+1}^{\star}\|_{2}\leq D+D^{\prime}

As an aside, note that while this provides an inequality over
\|{\bm{x}}_{t+1}^{\star}-{\bm{x}}_{t}\|_{2}
, the bound we will eventually derive from
Eq.

will be more appropriate for proving convergence. Using the definition of
{\bm{m}}_{t}=(1-\rho_{t}){\bm{g}}_{t}+\rho_{t}{\bm{m}}_{t-1}
, together with Young’s inequality (
|\langle{\bm{a}},{\bm{b}}\rangle|\leq c^{2}/2\|{\bm{a}}\|_{2}^{2}+1/2c^{2}\|{\bm{b}}\|_{2}^{2}
, for any constant
c\neq 0
), we get

\displaystyle\big|\langle{\bm{m}}_{t}-\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{t}),{\bm{x}}_{t+1}^{\star}-{\bm{x}}_{t}\rangle\big|

\displaystyle=\big|\langle(1-\rho_{t}){\bm{g}}_{t}+\rho_{t}{\bm{m}}_{t-1}-\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{t}),{\bm{x}}_{t+1}^{\star}-{\bm{x}}_{t}\rangle\big|

(Triangular inequality)

\displaystyle\leq\big|\langle{\bm{g}}_{t}-\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{t}),{\bm{x}}_{t+1}^{\star}-{\bm{x}}_{t}\rangle\big|+\big|\langle\rho_{t}{\bm{g}}_{t},{\bm{x}}_{t+1}^{\star}-{\bm{x}}_{t}\rangle\big|+\big|\langle\rho_{t}{\bm{m}}_{t-1},{\bm{x}}_{t+1}^{\star}-{\bm{x}}_{t}\rangle\big|

(Young & CS inequalities)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2L}\|{\bm{g}}_{t}-\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{t})\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{L}{2}\|{\bm{x}}_{t+1}^{\star}-{\bm{x}}_{t}\|_{2}^{2}+\rho_{t}(\|{\bm{g}}_{t}\|_{2}+\|{\bm{m}}_{t-1}\|_{2})\|{\bm{x}}_{t+1}^{\star}-{\bm{x}}_{t}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2L}\|{\bm{g}}_{t}-\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{t})\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{L}{2}\|{\bm{x}}_{t+1}^{\star}-{\bm{x}}_{t}\|_{2}^{2}+2\rho_{t}(D+D^{\prime})G.

(30)

In particular, the last inequality (

) uses

, and Lemma 1 of
(

)
to bound

, both by

. We then use Young’s inequality again to bound the second term on the left-hand side of

:

\displaystyle\big|\langle\nabla F({\bm{x}}_{t+1}^{\star}),{\bm{x}}_{t+1}-{\bm{x}}_{t+1}^{\star}\rangle\big|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2C}\|\nabla F({\bm{x}}_{t+1}^{\star})\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{C}{2}\|{\bm{x}}_{t+1}-{\bm{x}}_{t+1}^{\star}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2C}\dist\!\big({\bm{0}},\widehat{\partial}F({\bm{x}}_{t+1}^{\star})\big)^{2}+\frac{C}{2}\|{\bm{x}}_{t+1}-{\bm{x}}_{t+1}^{\star}\|_{2}^{2},

where

is a positive constant defined as

. Finally, since we have assumed in

that

where the second inequality is due to the learning rate
\alpha_{t}
being non-increasing. Putting it all together:

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\delta}{2\alpha_{0}}-\frac{L}{2}\right)\|{\bm{x}}_{t+1}^{\star}-{\bm{x}}_{t}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2\alpha_{t}}\|{\bm{x}}_{t+1}^{\star}-{\bm{x}}_{t}\|_{{\bm{D}}_{t}}^{2}-\frac{L}{2}\|{\bm{x}}_{t+1}^{\star}-{\bm{x}}_{t}\|_{2}^{2}

(
Eq.

29
)

\displaystyle\leq F({\bm{x}}_{t})-F({\bm{x}}_{t+1})-\langle{\bm{m}}_{t}-\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{t}),{\bm{x}}_{t+1}^{\star}-{\bm{x}}_{t}\rangle+\langle\nabla F({\bm{x}}_{t+1}^{\star}),{\bm{x}}_{t+1}-{\bm{x}}_{t+1}^{\star}\rangle

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\frac{L+L^{\prime}}{2}\|{\bm{x}}_{t+1}-{\bm{x}}_{t+1}^{\star}\|_{2}^{2}

(

30
and
31
)

\displaystyle\leq F({\bm{x}}_{t})-F({\bm{x}}_{t+1})+\frac{1}{2L}\|{\bm{g}}_{t}-\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{t})\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{L}{2}\|{\bm{x}}_{t+1}^{\star}-{\bm{x}}_{t}\|_{2}^{2}+2\rho_{t}(D+D^{\prime})G

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\frac{1}{2C}\dist\!\big({\bm{0}},\widehat{\partial}F({\bm{x}}_{t+1}^{\star})\big)^{2}+\frac{C}{2}\|{\bm{x}}_{t+1}-{\bm{x}}_{t+1}^{\star}\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{L+L^{\prime}}{2}\|{\bm{x}}_{t+1}-{\bm{x}}_{t+1}^{\star}\|_{2}^{2}

Gathering all the terms involving

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\delta}{2\alpha_{0}}-L\right)\|{\bm{x}}_{t+1}^{\star}-{\bm{x}}_{t}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq F({\bm{x}}_{t})-F({\bm{x}}_{t+1})+\frac{1}{2L}\|{\bm{g}}_{t}-\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{t})\|_{2}^{2}+2\rho_{t}(D+D^{\prime})G

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\frac{1}{2C}\dist\!\big({\bm{0}},\widehat{\partial}F({\bm{x}}_{t+1}^{\star})\big)^{2}+\frac{L+L^{\prime}+C}{2}\varepsilon_{t+1}^{2}.

Note that while we derived the above bound in the case where

Bound on

Recall that by definition of the weighted proximal operator as a minimizer, and by the first-order condition of optimality, we have

\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{t+1}^{\star})-{\bm{m}}_{t}-\frac{1}{\alpha_{t}}{\bm{D}}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t+1}^{\star}-{\bm{x}}_{t})\in\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{t+1}^{\star})+\widehat{\partial}h({\bm{x}}_{t+1}^{\star})=\widehat{\partial}F({\bm{x}}_{t+1}^{\star}).

Since we know one element of
\widehat{\partial}F({\bm{x}}_{t+1}^{\star})

{\bm{0}}

\displaystyle\dist\!\big({\bm{0}},\widehat{\partial}F({\bm{x}}_{t+1}^{\star})\big)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\Big\|{\bm{m}}_{t}-\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{t+1}^{\star})+\frac{1}{\alpha_{t}}{\bm{D}}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t+1}^{\star}-{\bm{x}}_{t})\Big\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\Big\|{\bm{m}}_{t}-\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{t+1}^{\star})+({\bm{x}}_{t+1}^{\star}-{\bm{x}}_{t})+\frac{1}{\alpha_{t}}{\bm{D}}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t+1}^{\star}-{\bm{x}}_{t})-({\bm{x}}_{t+1}^{\star}-{\bm{x}}_{t})\Big\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 3\left[\|{\bm{m}}_{t}-\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{t+1}^{\star})+{\bm{x}}_{t+1}^{\star}-{\bm{x}}_{t}\|_{2}^{2}+\Big\|\frac{1}{\alpha_{t}}{\bm{D}}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t+1}^{\star}-{\bm{x}}_{t})\Big\|_{2}^{2}+\|{\bm{x}}_{t+1}^{\star}-{\bm{x}}_{t}\|_{2}^{2}\right]

(
1

\displaystyle\leq 3\Big[\|{\bm{m}}_{t}-\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{t+1}^{\star})+{\bm{x}}_{t+1}^{\star}-{\bm{x}}_{t}\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{1}{\gamma^{2}}\|{\bm{x}}_{t+1}^{\star}-{\bm{x}}_{t}\|_{2}^{2}+\|{\bm{x}}_{t+1}^{\star}-{\bm{x}}_{t}\|_{2}^{2}\Big]

\displaystyle=3\Big[\|{\bm{m}}_{t}-\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{t+1}^{\star})\|_{2}^{2}+2\langle{\bm{m}}_{t}-\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{t+1}^{\star}),{\bm{x}}_{t+1}^{\star}-{\bm{x}}_{t}\rangle+\Big(2+\frac{1}{\gamma^{2}}\Big)\|{\bm{x}}_{t+1}^{\star}-{\bm{x}}_{t}\|_{2}^{2}\Big]

Again, we would like to bound some of the remaining terms on the right-hand side of the above inequality. We will start with the first term; using the definition of

\displaystyle\|{\bm{m}}_{t}-\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{t+1}^{\star})\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|(1-\rho_{t}){\bm{g}}_{t}-\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{t})+\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{t})-\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{t+1}^{\star})+\rho_{t}{\bm{m}}_{t-1}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 4\Big[\|{\bm{g}}_{t}-\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{t})\|_{2}^{2}+L^{2}\|{\bm{x}}_{t+1}^{\star}-{\bm{x}}_{t}\|_{2}^{2}+2\rho_{t}^{2}G^{2}\Big],

where we used the same bounds on

and

as in

\displaystyle\big|\langle{\bm{m}}_{t}-\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{t+1}^{\star}),{\bm{x}}_{t+1}^{\star}-{\bm{x}}_{t}\rangle\big|

\displaystyle\leq\big|\langle{\bm{m}}_{t}-\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{t}),{\bm{x}}_{t+1}^{\star}-{\bm{x}}_{t}\rangle\big|+\big|\langle\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{t+1}^{\star})-\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{t}),{\bm{x}}_{t+1}^{\star}-{\bm{x}}_{t}\rangle\big|

\displaystyle\leq\big|\langle{\bm{m}}_{t}-\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{t}),{\bm{x}}_{t+1}^{\star}-{\bm{x}}_{t}\rangle\big|+\|\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{t+1}^{\star})-\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{t})\|_{2}\|{\bm{x}}_{t+1}^{\star}-{\bm{x}}_{t}\|_{2}

Putting it all together, and using the bound on

\displaystyle\dist\!\big({\bm{0}},\widehat{\partial}F({\bm{x}}_{t+1}^{\star})\big)^{2}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\dist\!\big({\bm{0}},\widehat{\partial}F({\bm{x}}_{t+1}^{\star})\big)^{2}

\dist\!\big({\bm{0}},\widehat{\partial}F({\bm{x}}_{t+1})\big)^{2}

Although we have derived a bound on

, we are eventually interested in bounding the distance of
\widehat{\partial}F({\bm{x}}_{t+1})
to

of

\displaystyle\leq 2\dist\!\big({\bm{0}},\widehat{\partial}F({\bm{x}}_{t+1}^{\star})\big)^{2}+2d_{H}\big(\widehat{\partial}F({\bm{x}}_{t+1}),\widehat{\partial}F({\bm{x}}_{t+1}^{\star})\big)^{2},

where

is the Pompeiu–Hausdorff distance between two sets. In the case of

(2), where

(and therefore

-ball

around
{\bm{x}}_{t+1}^{\star}

\displaystyle d_{H}\big(\widehat{\partial}F({\bm{x}}_{t+1}),\widehat{\partial}F({\bm{x}}_{t+1}^{\star})\big)

\displaystyle=d_{H}\big(\{\nabla F({\bm{x}}_{t+1})\},\{\nabla F({\bm{x}}_{t+1}^{\star})\}\big)

\displaystyle=\|\nabla F({\bm{x}}_{t+1})-\nabla F({\bm{x}}_{t+1}^{\star})\|_{2}

(

is
L
-smooth,

is

-smooth in
{\mathcal{B}}
)

\displaystyle\leq(L+L^{\prime})\|{\bm{x}}_{t+1}-{\bm{x}}_{t+1}^{\star}\|_{2}

(
2
)

\displaystyle\leq(L+L^{\prime})\varepsilon_{t+1}.

Note that while we derived the inequality above with
2
(2), it is also valid when
{\bm{x}}_{t+1}={\bm{x}}_{t+1}^{\star}
, albeit looser (since the Pompeiu-Hausdorff distance would vanish in the latter case); hence this inequality is valid for all

. Using the bound on

, we can finally get the following bound on

\displaystyle\dist\!\big({\bm{0}},\widehat{\partial}F({\bm{x}}_{t+1})\big)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 4C\big(F({\bm{x}}_{t})-F({\bm{x}}_{t+1})\big)+4\Big[12+\frac{3}{L}+\frac{C}{2L}\Big]\|{\bm{g}}_{t}-\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{t})\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+4(2C+4)\rho_{t}(D+D^{\prime})G+32\rho_{t}^{2}G^{2}+\big[2C(L+L^{\prime}+C)+2(L+L^{\prime})^{2}\big]\varepsilon_{t+1}^{2}

Convergence result

To prove our final convergence result, we can simply write the expectation over squared distances (where
R
is uniform) as an average of quantities we have been capable of bounding:

\mathbb{E}_{R}\big[\dist\!\big({\bm{0}},\widehat{\partial}F({\bm{x}}_{R})\big)^{2}\big]=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\dist\!\big({\bm{0}},\widehat{\partial}F({\bm{x}}_{t+1})\big)^{2}

(39)

Moreover, note that the right-hand side of the inequality in
Eq.

38
involves the difference of
F
at two consecutive iterates, and therefore involves a telescoping series once summed over. Using
1
, we get

\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\big(F({\bm{x}}_{t})-F({\bm{x}}_{t+1})\big)=F({\bm{x}}_{0})-F({\bm{x}}_{T})\leq F({\bm{x}}_{0})-F({\bm{x}}^{\star})\leq\Delta.

We can similarly bound some of the remaining sums. Using the definition of
\rho_{t}=\rho_{0}\mu^{t}
(
1
), we have

\displaystyle\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\rho_{t}

\displaystyle\leq\rho_{0}\sum_{t=0}^{+\infty}\mu^{t}=\frac{\rho_{0}}{1-\mu}

\displaystyle\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\rho_{t}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\rho_{0}^{2}\sum_{t=0}^{+\infty}\mu^{2t}=\frac{\rho_{0}^{2}}{1-\mu^{2}},

and using
2
,

\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\varepsilon_{t+1}^{2}\leq\sum_{t=0}^{+\infty}\varepsilon_{t+1}^{2}=K.

To conclude, using the bound from
Eq.

38
inside
Eq.

39
, and the various bounds on the sums above, we get the following bound on the expected squared distance:

\mathbb{E}_{R}\big[\dist\!\big({\bm{0}},\widehat{\partial}F({\bm{x}}_{R})\big)^{2}\big]\leq\frac{C_{1}}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\|{\bm{g}}_{t}-\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{t})\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{C_{2}\Delta}{T}+\frac{C_{3}}{T},

(40)

where
C_{1}
,
C_{2}
, and
C_{3}
are three constants independent of
T
, defined as

\displaystyle C_{1}

\displaystyle=4\Big[12+\frac{3}{L}+\frac{C}{2L}\Big]

\displaystyle C_{2}

\displaystyle=4C

\displaystyle C_{3}

\displaystyle=\frac{4(2C+4)\rho_{0}(D+D^{\prime})G}{1-\mu}+\frac{32\rho_{0}^{2}G^{2}}{1-\mu^{2}}+K\big[2C(L+L^{\prime}+C)+2(L+L^{\prime})^{2}\big].

∎

C.3
Pruning with indirect sparsity

In
Section

6.3
, we argued that structured sparsity had benefit not only on the layer where groups of variables were zeroed-out, but on neighboring layer as well.
Fig.

4
shows an illustration of this behaviour on a convolutional neural network, inspired by
Fig.

1
, where structured sparsity has been applied channel-wise.

Figure 4:
Pruning a convolutional neural network with indirect sparsity. This illustration shows the groups of filters set to zero thanks to the structured sparsity inducing penalty (green for the first layer, blue for the second). The filters that can be further pruned in the first layer thanks to indirect sparsity are shown in light-blue.

The algorithm to prune these filters operates from right to left. When a group of filters are set to zero thanks to the structured sparsity inducing penalty, the corresponding channel at the previous layer (e.g. second channel of Layer 2 in
Fig.

4
, shown in light-blue) does not contribute to the predictions of the network anymore; here, the representation at Layer 3 is independent of the channel in light-blue. Therefore the channel in light-blue can be ignored from prior computations as well, meaning that the filters in light-blue can be set to zero as well, without functionally affecting the neural network: these filters were pruned
indirectly
by the structure in the sparsity patterns in Layer 2.

On top of this, the structured sparsity inducing penalty applied to the first layer can also set some groups of variables to zero (here the group shown in green); this procedure can then be run from the output layer, all the way to the input layer. This leads to significantly fewer parameters: in
Fig.

4
, the first layer only has 50% of non-zero parameters left, while only 25% were pruned directly through the structured sparsity inducing penalty applied to that layer (i.e. 75% group sparsity). Finally, note that while we showed the effect of indirect sparsity on a convolutional neural network, this applies similarly to linear layers with row-wise groups.

Choice of groups

As mentioned in
Section

6.2
, in our experiments we used
row-wise
structured sparsity to encourage entire rows of every weight matrix in BERT to be set to zero; this represents 86k groups in total. This choice was motivated by finding a network, where intermediate representations are only influenced by a subset of dimensions from the previous layer. This structure also helps in pruning, as shown in
Section

6.3
and
Section

C.3
.

In the context of Transformers, we could use the structure of the network itself even more in order to better define our groups. For example, with our current definition of row-wise groups, the 3 matrices to define the keys, queries and values in a self-attention layer see their rows being penalized independently from one another (i.e. the groups are define as the rows for each of these 3 matrices). However we could also encourage the keys, queries, and values to use the same information from (a subset of) the previous layer. Concretely, this is made possible by grouping together the rows from these 3 matrices from the self-attention layer (effectively reducing by 3x the number of groups for this layer). This would have a more significant impact in terms of pruning from indirect sparsity, since the key, query, and value modules would share the same subset of input dimensions (as opposed to what we currently do, where we can only prune indirectly one layer based on the intersection of the groups set to zero in the 3 matrices of the self-attention layer).

We can further improve this by combining the effect of shared and individual groups for the weights of self-attention layers. Indeed, while we restricted our attention in this paper on disjoint groups, it is also possible to encourage structured sparsity based on a groups following a
tree-structured hierarchy

(
Bach et al. 2012a
)
. Here, this would mean that we can encourage a subset of input dimensions based on groups spanning the 3 weight matrices of the self-attention layer, as well as having “specialized” dimensions for the keys, queries, and values with groups corresponding to the rows of each individual weight matrix (which are disjoint subgroups from those spanning all 3 matrices). The application of structured sparsity inducing penalties on tree-structured groups with adaptive proximal gradient methods is left as future work.
