Rotating fermions inside a spherical boundary
Zheng Zhang, Chao Shi, Xiaofeng Luo, Hong-Shi Zong

Abstract
We apply the canonical quantization procedure to the Dirac field inside a spherical boundary with rotating coordinates. The rotating quantum states with two kinds of boundary conditions, namely, spectral and MIT boundary conditions, are defined. To avoid faster-than-light, we require the speed on the surface to be less than the speed of light. For this situation, the definition of vacuum is unique and identical with the Minkowski vacuum. Finally, we calculate the thermal expectation value of the fermion condensate in a thermal equilibrium rotating fermion field and find it depends on the boundary condition.



I
Introduction

The quantization of fields in rotating coordinates attracted some attention since 1980s’, which was partly motivated by Hawking’s work on black-hole evaporation
Hawking 1975
. Fulling showed that quantization in Rindler coordinates is not identical with that in ordinary Minkowski coordinates and an accelerated observer sees the Minkowski vacuum as a thermal bath
Fulling 1973
. Then Unruh elucidated the relationship between the quantization schemes in Rindler coordinates and the black-hole evaporation, and showed a model particle detector in an accelerated state of motion indeed observes particles in the Minkowski vacuum
Unruh 1976
. It’s natural to investigate whether similar effects will occur in other noninertia frames. Letaw and Pfautsch studied the scalar field theory in rotating coordinates
Letaw and Pfautsch 1980
. They found unlike the uniformly accelerating observer, there is no such fancy effect in a rotating frame. Then Iyer investigated the Dirac field theory in rotating coordinates
Iyer 1982
. He found for Dirac field, quantization scheme in rotating coordinates is inequivalent to the usual Minkowski quantization scheme. However, the systems discussed in
Letaw and Pfautsch 1980
and
Iyer 1982
are both unbounded, which means the region outside a radius
R
will have a speed larger than the speed of light. This unphysical property leads to some difficulties of quantization for scalar field
Letaw and Pfautsch 1980
, and non-unique quantization schemes for Dirac field
Ambruş and Winstanley 2016
. In addition, some problems when calculating the thermal expectations in unbounded rotating systems will occur
Duffy and Ottewill 2003
;
Ambruş and Winstanley 2014
;
Ambruş and Winstanley 2016
. These problems can be solved by constraining the rotating system inside a region whose speed is less than the speed of light. Rotating scalar field bound inside a cylinder surface was investigated in
Duffy and Ottewill 2003
, and rotating Dirac field bound inside a cylinder surface was investigated in
Ambruş and Winstanley 2016
. It is shown that for rotating systems bound inside a cylindrical boundary, the quantization scheme is identical to that in usual Minkowski coordinates. These results imply that a physically possible cylindrical rotating frame is certainly different from a uniformly accelerating frame where some fancy effects will occur.

To understand the problems occur in quantization of field in rotating coordinates, let us review the usual cannonical quantization procedures in Minkowski coordinates. First, one solves the field equation and finds a set of complete orthogonal modes. These modes are split into positive and negative frequency modes. Then one expands the field operator by the modes and promotes the expansion coefficients into operators. The coefficients of the positive frequency modes are promoted to annihilation operators and the coefficients of the negative frequency modes are promoted to creation operators. Next, one assumes the commutation (anti-commutation) relations of the annihilation operators and the creation operators, and defines the vacuum as the state annihilated by all the annihilation operators. For a field in Minkowski coordinates, the split of positive and negative modes are clear, the positive (negative) modes have positive (negative) Minkowski energy
E
. However, for field in rotating coordinates, the split of positive modes and negative modes is not as clear as that in Minkowski coordinates. This is because a rotating mode with energy
\widetilde{E}>0
may have Minkowski energy
E<0
, and vice versa. Should we regard
\widetilde{E}>0
or
E>0
modes as positive frequency modes ? For a scalar field, if we want the positive frequency modes have positive Klein-Gordon norm, we must define
{E}>0
modes as positive frequency modes because the Klein-Gordon norm is proportional to
E

Iyer 1982
. But for Dirac field, all modes have positive Dirac norm, so the split of positive and negative modes seems less constrained. If we define
E>0
modes as positive frequency modes, the vacuum is called nonrotating vacuum, which is identical to the Minkowski vacuum. If we define
\widetilde{E}>0
modes as positive frequency modes, the vacuum is called rotating vacuum. On unbounded Minkowsi space-time, there exists modes with
E\widetilde{E}<0
, thus making the two vacua inequivalent. If one encloses the field inside the speed of light surface (SOL), one can expect that the modes with
E\widetilde{E}<0
will not occur, thus the rotating and nonrotating vacuum are equivalent, and the problems are solved. For scalar field, it is shown there are no
E\widetilde{E}<0
modes when enclosing the field inside a cylindrical boundary with the Dirichlet kind
Vilenkin 1980
. For Dirac field, one can also prove the same result for spectral and MIT cylindrical boundary conditions
Ambruş and Winstanley 2016
. In this paper, we will prove this result for Dirac field enclosed in a spherical boundary with spectral and MIT kind. It seems there is a general proof for this result, regardless what the shape or kind of the boundary condition is. (Of course, the boundary condition should satisfy some basic requirements, such as keeping the Hamiltonian self-adjoint.) This general proof is not available now, but it’s reasonable to believe it exists.

From the point of view above, the quantization in rotating coordinates is trivial. But it does not mean other aspects of a rotating system are also trivial. For example, macroscopic parity-violating effects can occur in rotating systems
Vilenkin 1979
. When considering the chiral anomaly, there can be chiral vortical effect in noncentral high energy heavy-ion collisions
Son and Surówka 2009
. To study these effects, one may need thermodynamics and statistical mechanics for rotating systems, whose principles had been introduced by Landau and Lifshitz
Landau and Lifshitz 1969
, and elaborated by Vilenkin
Vilenkin 1980
. Here we should note that the thermodynamics for rotating systems is not the thermodynamics in noninertial rotating frames. The former deals with what a static observer sees for a rotating system, while the latter deals with things seen by a rotating observer.
Since the observer is static, one need not use rotating coordinates when discussing about the thermodynamics for rotating systems. But interestingly, problems will occur if the speed of the surface exceeds the speed of light. These problems are also relevant to the modes with
E\widetilde{E}<0
, because the distribution in rotating systems is relevant to
[e^{\beta\widetilde{E}}\pm 1]^{-1}

Ambruş and Winstanley 2016
. For unbounded rotating scalar field, the existence of particle modes with
\widetilde{E}=0
leads to the divergence of the thermal expectations
Ambruş and Winstanley 2014
. For unbounded Dirac field, if one treat
E>0
modes as particle modes (positive frequency modes), the thermal expectation values will have an unphysical term
Vilenkin 1980
;
Ambruş and Winstanley 2016
. These problems can also be cured by enclosing the field inside the SOL. There are some results of thermal expectation values for fermion field bounded by the cylindrical boundary
Ambruş and Winstanley 2016
.

In this paper, we study the Dirac field theory inside a sphere with rotating coordinates. The axis of rotation is selected as
z
-axis. The rigidly-rotating quantum states are constructed. To bound the field inside a sphere, we follow Ref.
Ambruş and Winstanley 2016
to impose two kinds of boundary conditions, the spectral
Hortaçsu et al. 1980
and MIT
Chodos et al. 1974
boundary conditions, and give the spectrum in each case. We proved that the rotating and nonrotating vacua are identical when the boundary of the sphere lies within the SOL. The second quantization procedures are performed. The thermal expectation value of fermion condensate is calculated for each boundary condition and possible applications are discussed.

The aim of this paper is of twofold. First, by showing the vacuum of a rotating fermionic field enclosed inside a sphere (whose surface does not exceed the speed of light) is identical to the Minkowski vacuum, together with Ref.
Ambruş and Winstanley 2016
which obtained the same result but for a different geometry boundary, this paper suggests that: any physically possible rotating observer does not see the Minkowski vacuum has strange effects such as Unruh effect. Second, the results of this paper can be used to calculate thermal expectation values for some spherical rotating systems, which may have practical applications. We will discuss the fermion condensate as an example. One advantage of this paper is that it deals with finite rotating systems, which are more close to the real rotating systems, especially when the finite volume has important effects. For example, the results of this paper have potential applications to realistic simulation of the small rotating quark-gluon systems created in heavy-ion collisions
Shuryak 1980
;
Gyulassy and McLerran 2005
;
Adamczyk et al. 2017
;
Palhares et al. 2011
.

The structure of this paper is organized as follows. In Sec.
II
, we construct the mode solutions of the Dirac equation in unbounded spherical coordinates and review the second quantization procedure. The remainder of the paper considers the bounded space-time with spectral and MIT boundary conditions. In Sec.
III
, we give the mode solutions satisfying the two kind of boundary conditions, show that there is no
E\widetilde{E}<0
modes if the field is enclosed in SOL, and perform the second quantization procedures. The thermal expectation values of fermion condensate with the two kinds of boundary conditions are calculated in Sec.
IV
. Finally, a summary is included in Sec.
V
.

II.3
Second quantization

As we discussed in the Introduction, the vacuum of Dirac field in rotating coordinates is not uniquely defined, which comes from the freedom to choose the "particle" and "anti-particle" modes. For nonrotating vacuum, particle modes have Minkowski energy
E>0
. For rotating vacuum, particle modes have corotating energy
\widetilde{E}>0
. The difference between rotating vacuum and nonrotating vacuum arises from the modes with
E\widetilde{E}<0
. By enclosing the system inside the SOL, the modes with
E\widetilde{E}<0
can be eliminated, which has been proved with cylindrical boundary
Ambruş and Winstanley 2016
. We will also prove it for spherical boundary in this paper. Thus, the rotating vacuum and the nonrotating vacuum are equivalent.

Assuming that there is no modes with
E\widetilde{E}<0
, second quantization can be performed by expanding the field operator
\psi(x)
as:

\psi(x)=\sum_{k}\theta\left(E_{k}\right)\left[U_{k}(x)\mathbf{b}_{k}+V_{k}(x)\mathbf{d}_{k}^{\dagger}\right],

(48)

where
\theta(E_{k})
is the step function which ensures the Minkowski energy
E_{k}
is positive and

\sum_{k}=\sum_{j=1/2}^{\infty}\ \ \sum_{m_{j}=-j}^{j}\ \ \sum_{\kappa=\pm(j+1/2)}\ \ \int_{|E|>M}dE.

(49)

The one-particle operators
\mathbf{b}_{k}
and
\mathbf{d}_{k}^{\dagger}
obey canonical anti-commutation relations:

\{\mathbf{b}_{k},\mathbf{b}_{k^{\prime}}^{\dagger}\}=\delta(k,k^{\prime}),\ \ \ \{\mathbf{d}_{k},\mathbf{d}_{k^{\prime}}^{\dagger}\}=\delta(k,k^{\prime}).

(50)

All other anti-commutation relations are zero. The vacuum state
|0\rangle
is defined by

\mathbf{b}_{k}|0\rangle=\mathbf{d}_{k}|0\rangle=0.

(51)

In the next section, we will investigate the Dirac field enclosed by two kinds of boundary conditions, namely, spectral and MIT boundary conditions.

III
Bounded space-time

This paper focus on the quantum fermion field with rotating coordinates, inside a sphere which has radius
R
. To avoid the exceeding of speed of light, we require
R\Omega<1
.

To enclose the field inside the sphere, we consider two kinds of boundary conditions: the spectral and MIT boundary conditions. In Sec.
III.1
and Sec.
III.2
, the spectral and MIT boundary conditions are introduced respectively. For each case, the spectrum is derived and the vacuum state is discussed. And for each case, we show the rotating vacuum and nonrotating vacuum coincide.

III.1
Spectral boundary conditions

Before introducing the spectral boundary condition, we first discuss the constraint on the behavior of the field on the boundary due to the requirement of the self-adjointness of the Hamiltonian. Here we follow the discussion in
Ambruş and Winstanley 2016
.

The Hamiltonian is a self-adjoint operator, that is :

\langle\psi,\widetilde{H}\chi\rangle=\langle\widetilde{H}\psi,\chi\rangle.

(52)

Since
\widetilde{H}=i\partial_{t}
, Eq. (
52
) is equivalent to

\partial_{t}\langle\psi,\chi\rangle=0.

(53)

In the special case
\chi=\psi
, Eq. (
53
) implies the conservation of particle numbers.
In the rotating frame, one has
Ambruş and Winstanley 2016

\partial_{t}\langle\psi,\chi\rangle=-\int_{\partial V}d\Sigma_{i}\sqrt{-g}\bar{\psi}\gamma^{\hat{i}}\chi,

(54)

where
\partial V
is the 2 dimensional boundary of the 3 dimensional volume
V
. Thus in the spherical coordinates, the self-adjointness of the Hamiltonian requires:

R^{2}\int_{0}^{\pi}\sin{\theta}d\theta\int_{0}^{2\pi}d\phi\ \bar{\psi}\gamma^{\hat{r}}\chi|_{r=R}=0,

(55)

where
\gamma^{\hat{r}}=\gamma^{\hat{1}}\sin{\theta}\cos{\phi}+\gamma^{\hat{2}}\sin{\theta}\sin{\phi}+\gamma^{\hat{3}}\cos{\theta}
.

To implement the spectral boundary condition, we write the solution
\psi
of the Dirac equation with
z
-angular momentum
m
as:

\psi(x)=e^{i\phi m}\times(e^{-\frac{i}{2}\phi}\psi^{1}_{m}\ \ e^{\frac{i}{2}\phi}\psi^{2}_{m}\ \ e^{-\frac{i}{2}\phi}\psi^{3}_{m}\ \ e^{\frac{i}{2}\phi}\psi^{4}_{m})^{T}.

(56)

The inner product of
\psi
and another solution
\chi
with
z
-angular momentum
m^{\prime}
is time-invariant if:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\pi}\ \sin{\theta}d\theta(\psi^{4*}_{m}(\mathrm{s}\chi^{1}_{m}-\mathrm{c}\chi^{2}_{m})+\psi^{3*}_{m}(\mathrm{s}\chi^{2}_{m}+\mathrm{c}\chi^{1}_{m})

(57)

\displaystyle+\psi^{2*}_{m}(\mathrm{s}\chi^{3}_{m}-\mathrm{c}\chi^{4}_{m})+\psi^{1*}_{m}(\mathrm{s}\chi^{4}_{m}+\mathrm{c}\chi^{3}_{m}))\delta_{mm^{\prime}}=0,

where
\mathrm{c},\mathrm{s}
is the short writing of
\mathrm{cos}\theta,\mathrm{sin}\theta
. The inner product of the charge conjugate
\psi_{c}=i\gamma^{\hat{2}}\psi^{*}
of
\psi
and
\chi
must also be time-invariant. That is:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\pi}\ \sin{\theta}d\theta(\psi^{1}_{-m}(\mathrm{s}\chi^{1}_{m}-\mathrm{c}\chi^{2}_{m})-\psi^{2}_{-m}(\mathrm{s}\chi^{2}_{m}+\mathrm{c}\chi^{1}_{m})

(58)

\displaystyle-\psi^{3}_{-m}(\mathrm{s}\chi^{3}_{m}-\mathrm{c}\chi^{4}_{m})+\psi^{4}_{-m}(\mathrm{s}\chi^{4}_{m}+\mathrm{c}\chi^{3}_{m}))\delta_{m,-m^{\prime}}=0.

To satisfy both Eq. (
57
) and Eq. (
58
), we can set

\begin{array}[]{ll}\psi_{m}^{3}|_{r=R}=\psi_{m}^{4}|_{r=R}=0,&\ \ m>0,\\
\psi_{m}^{1}|_{r=R}=\psi_{m}^{2}|_{r=R}=0,&\ \ m<0,\end{array}

(59)

which we call spectral boundary condition. One can also set the third and fourth components zero when
m<0
, and the first and second components zero when
m>0
. Here we only discuss the implementation in Eq. (
59
). We expect the other implementation gives similar results for expectation values.

III.1.1
Discretization of the momentum

Apply Eq. (
59
) to the mode solutions (
42
) requires the momentum
p
must be discretized by:

p_{jm\kappa,i}R=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\xi_{j+\frac{1}{2},i}&m\kappa>0\\
\xi_{j-\frac{1}{2},i}&m\kappa<0\end{array}\right.,

(60)

where
\xi_{n,i}
is the
i
th nonzero root of the spherical Bessel function
j_{n}(x)
. Thus, the mode solutions of Dirac equation with spectral boundary condition can be written as:

U_{k}^{\mathrm{sp}}(x)=C_{k}^{\mathrm{sp}}U_{k}(x),

(61)

where
k
includes a new index
i
:

k=(E,j,m_{j},\kappa,i),

(62)

and
E=\pm\sqrt{p^{2}+M^{2}}
is the Minkowski energy. The constants
C_{k}^{\mathrm{sp}}
will be calculated in Sec.
III.1.3
to make the modes have unit norm.

III.1.2
Energy spectrum

Here we will show there is no
E\widetilde{E}<0
modes in the particle spectrum, then the rotating and nonrotating vacua are equivalent. But before the proof, let us explain the physical meaning of this conclusion briefly.

In the picture of the Dirac sea, the Minkowski vacuum is the state with all negative modes
E<0
occupied. But the mode with Minkowski energy
E
has rotating energy
\widetilde{E}
as a rotating observer sees. If
\widetilde{E}>0
, this occupied mode will be identified as a particle by the rotating observer. That is, the rotating observer will see the Minkowski vacuum contains particles. However, if the condition
E\widetilde{E}>0
is satisfied for all modes, the rotating observer will see nothing in the Minkowski vacuum.

To demonstrate there is no
E\widetilde{E}<0
modes for the spectral boundary condition, we use the property of the first zero of the spherical Bessel function
Watson 1922
:

\xi_{n,1}>n+1.

(63)

Thus for
E>0
, we have:

ER\geq pR>\xi_{j-\frac{1}{2},1}>j+\frac{1}{2}>m_{j}.

(64)

So

\widetilde{E}R=ER-\Omega m_{j}R>(1-\Omega R)m_{j}.

(65)

If
\Omega R\leq 1
, then
E\widetilde{E}>0
for all
M,j,m_{j},\kappa,i
. Similarly, when
E<0
, we can also verify that
E\widetilde{E}>0
for all
M,j,m_{j},\kappa,i
. Thus, the rotating and nonrotating vacua are equivalent. Here we can see the key point in the proof is that, by enclosing the system inside the sphere, the momentum is discretized and thus has a nonzero minimum value.

Since
E\widetilde{E}<0
modes do not appear, we can perform the second quantization procedure as introduced in Sec.
II.3
.

III.1.3
Normalization

Before performing the second quantization, we have to calculate the normalization constant
C_{k}^{\mathrm{sp}}
. The inner product for two particle modes
U_{k}^{\mathrm{sp}}
and
U_{k^{\prime}}^{\mathrm{sp}}
is:

\displaystyle\langle U_{k}^{\mathrm{sp}},U_{k^{\prime}}^{\mathrm{sp}}\rangle

\displaystyle=C_{k}^{\mathrm{sp}*}C_{k^{\prime}}^{\mathrm{sp}}\delta(k,k^{\prime})\mathfrak{I}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}^{+},

(66)

where

\delta(k,k^{\prime})=\delta_{jj^{\prime}}\delta_{m_{j},m_{j}^{\prime}}\delta_{\kappa,\kappa^{\prime}}\delta_{ii^{\prime}}\theta(EE^{\prime}),

(67)

and

\displaystyle

\displaystyle\mathfrak{I}_{n+1}^{+}=\int_{0}^{R}drr^{2}\frac{1}{2}\left[j_{n}^{2}(pr)+j_{n+1}^{2}(pr)\right]=\frac{R^{3}}{2}\left[j_{n+1}^{2}(pR)-\frac{2(n+1)}{pR}j_{n}(pR)j_{n+1}(pR)+j_{n}^{2}(pR)\right],

(68)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{I}_{n+1}^{-}=\int_{0}^{R}drr^{2}\frac{1}{2}\left[j_{n}^{2}(pr)-j_{n+1}^{2}(pr)\right]=\frac{R^{2}}{2p}j_{n}(pR)j_{n+1}(pR).

When
m\kappa>0
,
\mathfrak{I}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}^{+}=\frac{R^{3}}{2}j^{2}_{j-\frac{1}{2}}(\xi_{j+\frac{1}{2},i})
,
we take

C_{Ejm\kappa i}^{\mathrm{sp}}=\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{R^{3}}|j_{j-\frac{1}{2}}(\xi_{j+\frac{1}{2},i})|},\ \ \ m\kappa>0.

(69)

When
m\kappa<0
,
\mathfrak{I}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}^{+}=\frac{R^{3}}{2}j^{2}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}(\xi_{j-\frac{1}{2},i})
,
we take

C_{Ejm\kappa i}^{\mathrm{sp}}=\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{R^{3}}|j_{j+\frac{1}{2}}(\xi_{j-\frac{1}{2},i})|},\ \ \ m\kappa<0.

(70)

The anti-particle modes are related to particle modes by Eq. (
46
):

V_{k}^{\mathrm{sp}}(t,r,\theta,\phi)=(-1)^{m_{j}+\frac{1}{2}}\frac{iE}{|E|}U_{\overline{k}}^{\mathrm{sp}}(t,r,\theta,\phi),

(71)

where

\overline{k}=(-E,j,-m,-\kappa,i).

(72)

Since the particle modes are normalized (the above calculation is valid for both
E>0
and
E<0
), so are the anti-particle modes. One can check
U_{k}^{\mathrm{sp}}
has the same normalization constant with its charge conjugate
V_{k}^{\mathrm{sp}}
.

III.1.4
Second quantization

To perform the second quantization procedures, we first expand the field in terms of the normalized modes:

\psi_{\mathrm{sp}}=\sum_{k}\theta\left(E\right)\left[U_{k}^{\mathrm{sp}}\mathbf{b}_{k}^{\mathrm{sp}}+V_{k}^{\mathrm{sp}}\mathbf{d}_{k}^{\mathrm{sp}\dagger}\right],

(73)

where
k
is defined in Eq. (
62
) and

\sum_{k}=\sum_{j=1/2}^{\infty}\ \sum_{m_{j}=-j}^{j}\ \ \sum_{\kappa=\pm({j}+1/2)}\ \sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\sum_{E=\pm\left|E\right|}.

(74)

The vacuum
|0^{\mathrm{sp}}\rangle
for the spectral boundary condition is defined by

\mathbf{b}_{k}^{\mathrm{sp}}|0^{\mathrm{sp}}\rangle=\mathbf{d}_{k}^{\mathrm{sp}}|0^{\mathrm{sp}}\rangle=0.

(75)

III.2
MIT boundary conditions

The MIT boundary condition was firstly introduced in
Chodos et al. 1974
. It satisfies Eq. (
55
) by setting

i\not{n}\psi(x_{b})=\varsigma\psi(x_{b}),

(76)

where
n_{\mu}
is the normal to the boundary and
\not{n}=\gamma^{\mu}n_{\mu}
. The coefficient
\varsigma
can take the general form
Lutken and Ravndal 1984
:

\varsigma=\exp\left(-i\gamma_{5}\Theta\right)=\cos\Theta-i\gamma_{5}\sin\Theta,

(77)

where
\Theta
is the chiral angle. Here we only consider the cases when
\Theta=0
(MIT) and
\Theta=\pi
(chiral), that is:

\varsigma=\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}1\ \ \ \ \ (\mathrm{MIT})\\
-1\ \ \ (\mathrm{chiral})\ .\end{array}\right.

(78)

One can check that the MIT boundary condition Eq. (
76
) makes the normal component of the fermionic current
j^{\mu}=\overline{\psi}\gamma^{\mu}\psi
to be zero on the surface, i.e.,

n_{\mu}j^{\mu}(x_{b})=0

(79)

In spherical coordinates, the boundary condition (
76
) can be written as:

-i\gamma^{\hat{r}}\psi(x_{b})=\varsigma\psi(x_{b}).

(80)

One can check if
\psi(x)
satisfies this boundary condition, so does its charge conjugation
i\gamma^{\hat{2}}\psi^{*}(x)
.

III.2.1
Discretization of the momentum

Substitute the solutions Eqs. (
42
) into the boundary condition Eq. (
80
), and use the identity
(\bm{\sigma}\cdot\hat{r})\chi_{mj}^{\pm}=\bm{\sigma}\cdot\frac{\bm{r}}{r}\chi_{mj}^{\pm}=\chi_{mj}^{\mp}
, we can get the equation for the allowed momentum:

j_{l_{\kappa}}(pR)=\operatorname{sgn}(\kappa)\frac{\varsigma p}{E+M}j_{\overline{l}_{\kappa}}(pR),

(81)

where

\displaystyle l_{\kappa}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}{\kappa-1}&{\text{ for }\kappa>0}\\
{-\kappa}&{\text{ for }\kappa<0}\end{array}\right.,

(82)

\displaystyle\overline{l}_{\kappa}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}{\kappa}&{\text{ for }\kappa>0}\\
{-\kappa-1}&{\text{ for }\kappa<0}\end{array}\right..

Here we note our equation (
81
) is different from that in
Greiner et al. 2007
by a minus sign, because our definition of
\kappa
is different from theirs by a minus sign. We label the
i
th nonzero root of Eq. (
81
) with
E,j,\kappa
as
p_{Ej\kappa,i}
. The mode solutions of Dirac equation with MIT boundary condition can be written as:

U_{k}^{\mathrm{MIT}}(x)=C_{k}^{\mathrm{MIT}}U_{k}(x),

(83)

where
k
represents:

k=(E,j,m_{j},\kappa,i).

(84)

The normalization constant will be calculated in Sec.
III.2.3
.

III.2.2
Spectrum energy

Now we show there is no
E\widetilde{E}<0
modes, thus the rotating and nonrotating vacua are equivalent for MIT boundary condition case. First, we consider the case when
M=0
. The eigen equation (
81
) becomes:

j_{j-\frac{1}{2}}(pR)=\pm j_{j+\frac{1}{2}}(pR),

(85)

where the plus or minus sign depends on the signs of
\kappa
and
\varsigma
.
The roots of Eq. (
85
) times
R
are the zeros of functions

J_{j}(x)\pm J_{j+1}(x),

(86)

where
J_{n}
is the
n
th Bessel function. According to the theorems in
Beneventano et al. 2016
, the first nonzero zero
\xi_{j,1}^{-}
of
J_{j}(x)-J_{j+1}(x)
satisfies
\xi_{j,1}^{-}>\xi^{\prime}_{j,1}
, and the first nonzero zero
\xi_{j,1}^{+}
of
J_{j}(x)+J_{j+1}(x)
satisfies
\xi_{j,1}^{+}>\xi^{\prime}_{j+1,1}
, where
\xi^{\prime}_{j,1}
is the first zero of
J^{\prime}_{j}(x)
. Using the property
Watson 1922
:

\xi^{\prime}_{j,1}>\sqrt{j(j+2)},

(87)

we get

\xi_{j,1}^{\pm}>j\geq m_{j}.

(88)

Thus

|E_{jm_{j}\kappa,i}|R\geq p_{Ej\kappa,i}R>m_{j}.

(89)

Combine Eq. (
89
) with Eq. (
65
) and we prove that there is no
E\widetilde{E}<0
modes for
M=0
case.

When
M\neq 0
, we assume
E>0
(
E<0
case can be proved in a similar way). Let us first consider the ordinary case (
\varsigma=1
), the eigen equation (
81
) becomes:

\displaystyle J_{j}(pR)-\frac{p}{E+M}J_{j+1}(pR)=0,\ \ \ \kappa>0,

(90)

\displaystyle J_{j}(pR)+\frac{E+M}{p}J_{j+1}(pR)=0,\ \ \ \kappa<0.

For the equation with
\kappa>0
, we set the first nonzero root
pR=\xi_{j,1}
. Since
0<\frac{p}{E+M}<1
, one has
J_{j}(\xi_{j,1})<J_{j+1}(\xi_{j,1})
. However, in the interval
0<x\leq\xi_{j,1}^{-}
,
J_{j}(x)>J_{j+1}(x)>0
, so
\xi_{j,1}>\xi_{j,1}^{-}>m_{j}
, thus Eq. (
89
) is satisfied.

For the equation with
\kappa<0
, use
\frac{p}{E+M}>0
and
J_{j}(x)>J_{j+1}(x)>0
when
0<x\leq\xi_{j,1}^{-}
, one can easily know the first nonzero root is larger than
\xi_{j,1}^{-}
, thus Eq. (
89
) is satisfied.

Now let’s turn to the chiral case (
\varsigma=-1
), the eigen equations (
81
) becomes:

\displaystyle J_{j}(pR)+\frac{p}{E+M}J_{j+1}(pR)=0,\ \ \ \kappa>0,

(91)

\displaystyle J_{j}(pR)-\frac{E+M}{p}J_{j+1}(pR)=0,\ \ \ \kappa<0.

For the equation with
\kappa>0
, use
\frac{p}{E+M}>0
and
J_{j}(x)>J_{j+1}(x)>0
when
0<x\leq\xi_{j,1}^{-}
, one can easily know the first nonzero root is larger than
\xi_{j,1}^{-}
, thus Eq. (
89
) is satisfied.

For the equation with
\kappa>0
, it can be written in the following form:

p\frac{J_{j}(pR)}{J_{j+1}(pR)}=M+E,

(92)

which is the formula (3.49) in Ref
Ambruş and Winstanley 2016
. Then one can follow the proof below (3.49) in
Ambruş and Winstanley 2016
and finally Eq. (
89
) is satisfied.

Yet, we have proved there is no
E\widetilde{E}<0
modes and thus the rotating and nonrotating vacua are equivalent for the MIT boundary condition case.

III.2.3
Normalization

To perform the second quantization for MIT boundary condition case, we have to calculate the normalization constants
C_{k}^{\mathrm{MIT}}
. The inner product for two particle modes
U_{k}^{\mathrm{MIT}}
and
U_{k^{\prime}}^{\mathrm{MIT}}
is:

\displaystyle\langle U_{k}^{\mathrm{MIT}},U_{k^{\prime}}^{\mathrm{MIT}}\rangle

\displaystyle=C_{k}^{\mathrm{MIT}*}C_{k^{\prime}}^{\mathrm{MIT}}\delta(k,k^{\prime})[\mathfrak{I}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}^{+}(p_{Ej\kappa,i}R)

(93)

\displaystyle+\mathrm{sgn}(\kappa)\frac{M}{E}\mathfrak{I}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}^{-}(p_{Ej\kappa,i}R)],

where
\delta(k,k^{\prime})=\delta_{jj^{\prime}}\delta_{m_{j},m_{j}^{\prime}}\delta_{\kappa,\kappa^{\prime}}\delta_{ii^{\prime}}\theta(EE^{\prime})
, and
\mathfrak{I}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}^{\pm}
are given by Eq. (
68
).

Combine the Eq. (
81
) with Eq. (
93
), one can get the normalized constants:

C_{k}^{\mathrm{MIT}}=\frac{\sqrt{2}}{R|j_{j+\frac{1}{2}}(p_{Ej\kappa,i}R)|}\sqrt{\frac{E+M}{2ER-\varsigma(2j+1)+\varsigma\frac{M}{E}}},\ \ \kappa>0,

(94)

C_{k}^{\mathrm{MIT}}=\frac{\sqrt{2}}{R|j_{j-\frac{1}{2}}(p_{Ej\kappa,i}R)|}\sqrt{\frac{E+M}{2ER+\varsigma(2j+1)+\varsigma\frac{M}{E}}},\ \ \kappa<0.

(95)

The anti-particle modes are also normalized since the particle modes are normalized, and one can check
U_{k}^{\mathrm{MIT}}
has the same normalization constant with its charge conjugate
V_{k}^{\mathrm{MIT}}
.

III.2.4
Second quantization

The second quantization can be performed as before. Expand the field by normalized modes:

\psi_{\mathrm{MIT}}=\sum_{k}\theta\left(E\right)\left[U_{k}^{\mathrm{MIT}}\mathbf{b}_{k}^{\mathrm{MIT}}+V_{k}^{\mathrm{MIT}}\mathbf{d}_{k}^{\mathrm{MIT}\dagger}\right],

(96)

where

\sum_{k}=\sum_{j=1/2}^{\infty}\ \sum_{m_{j}=-j}^{j}\ \ \sum_{\kappa=\pm({j}+1/2)}\ \sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\sum_{E=\pm\left|E\right|}.

(97)

The vacuum state
|0^{\mathrm{MIT}}\rangle
for MIT boundary condition case is defined by

\mathbf{b}_{k}^{\mathrm{MIT}}|0^{\mathrm{MIT}}\rangle=\mathbf{d}_{k}^{\mathrm{MIT}}|0^{\mathrm{MIT}}\rangle=0.

(98)

Thus, we have finished the field quantization for the Dirac field in rotating coordinates with two kinds of boundary conditions.

IV
Fermion condensate

In this section, we calculate the thermal expectation value of fermion condensate in an thermal equilibrium rigidly-rotating sphere. The spectral and MIT boundary conditions are considered separately.

We calculate the fermion condensate
\overline{\psi}\psi
in a straight forward way. The field operator
\psi(x)
and
\overline{\psi}(x)
can be expanded by creation and annihilation operators:

\displaystyle\psi(x)=\sum_{k}\theta(E_{k})C_{k}[b_{k}U_{k}(x)+d_{k}^{\dagger}V_{k}(x)],

(99)

\displaystyle\overline{\psi}(x)=\sum_{k}\theta(E_{k})C_{k}^{*}[d_{k}\overline{V}_{k}(x)+b_{k}^{\dagger}\overline{U}_{k}(x)],

where
C_{k}
are the normalization constants. We have

\displaystyle\langle\overline{\psi}\psi\rangle=\sum_{kk^{\prime}}\theta(E_{k})\theta(E_{k^{\prime}})

\displaystyle C_{k}^{*}C_{k^{\prime}}[\langle b_{k}^{\dagger}b_{k^{\prime}}\rangle\overline{U}_{k}U_{k^{\prime}}+\langle d_{k}d_{k^{\prime}}^{\dagger}\rangle\overline{V}_{k}V_{k^{\prime}}

(100)

\displaystyle+\langle b_{k}^{\dagger}d_{k^{\prime}}^{\dagger}\rangle\overline{U}_{k}V_{k^{\prime}}+\langle d_{k}b_{k^{\prime}}\rangle\overline{V}_{k}U_{k^{\prime}}],

where
\langle\cdot\rangle
means the ensemble average for a thermal equilibrium rotating system. According to
Vilenkin 1979
;
Landau and Lifshitz 1969
,

\displaystyle\langle b_{k}^{\dagger}b_{k^{\prime}}\rangle=\frac{1}{e^{\beta(\widetilde{E_{k}}-\mu)}+1}\delta(k,k^{\prime}),

(101)

\displaystyle\langle d_{k}d_{k^{\prime}}^{\dagger}\rangle=1-\langle d_{k^{\prime}}^{\dagger}d_{k}\rangle=(1-\frac{1}{e^{\beta(\widetilde{E_{k}}+\mu)}+1})\delta(k,k^{\prime}),

\displaystyle\langle b_{k}^{\dagger}d_{k^{\prime}}^{\dagger}\rangle=\langle d_{k}b_{k^{\prime}}\rangle=0.

Using
V_{k}(x)=i\gamma^{\hat{2}}U_{k}^{*}(x)
, one has
\overline{V}_{k}V_{k}=-\overline{U}_{k}U_{k}
. Let

\displaystyle w(\widetilde{E_{k}})

\displaystyle=(1-\langle d_{k}^{\dagger}d_{k}\rangle-\langle b_{k}^{\dagger}b_{k}\rangle)\theta(E_{k})

(102)

\displaystyle=\frac{\theta(E_{k})}{2}(\mathrm{tanh}\frac{\beta(\widetilde{E_{k}}-\mu)}{2}+\mathrm{tanh}\frac{\beta(\widetilde{E_{k}}+\mu)}{2}),

then the condensate can be written as:

\langle\overline{\psi}\psi\rangle=-\sum_{k}{|C_{k}|}^{2}w(\widetilde{E_{k}})\overline{U}_{k}U_{k}.

(103)

Set

\displaystyle A_{jm_{j}\kappa i}(r,\theta)=\mathrm{sgn}(\kappa)\frac{1}{2}[

\displaystyle j_{j-\frac{1}{2}}^{2}(p_{k}r)(\chi_{jm_{j}}^{+})^{\dagger}\chi_{jm_{j}}^{+}

(104)

\displaystyle-j_{j+\frac{1}{2}}^{2}(p_{k}r)(\chi_{jm_{j}}^{-})^{\dagger}\chi_{jm_{j}}^{-}],

and

\displaystyle B_{jm_{j}\kappa i}(r,\theta)=\frac{M}{2E}[

\displaystyle j_{j-\frac{1}{2}}^{2}(p_{k}r)(\chi_{jm_{j}}^{+})^{\dagger}\chi_{jm_{j}}^{+}

(105)

\displaystyle+j_{j+\frac{1}{2}}^{2}(p_{k}r)(\chi_{jm_{j}}^{-})^{\dagger}\chi_{jm_{j}}^{-}].

We have

\overline{U_{k}}U_{k}=A_{jm_{j}\kappa i}+B_{jm_{j}\kappa i}.

(106)

Finally the condensate can be expressed as:

\displaystyle\langle\overline{\psi}\psi\rangle=-\sum_{j=1/2}^{\infty}

\displaystyle\sum_{\kappa=\pm}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\ \sum_{m_{j}=-j}^{j}

(107)

\displaystyle{\displaystyle|C_{jm_{j}\kappa i}|}^{2}w(\widetilde{E}_{jm_{j}\kappa i})(A_{jm_{j}\kappa i}+B_{jm_{j}\kappa i}).

IV.1
spectral boundary condition

For the case of spectral boundary condition, one notices that
p_{j,m_{j},\kappa,i}=p_{j,-m_{j},-\kappa,i}
,
(\chi_{j,m_{j}}^{\pm})^{\dagger}\chi_{j,m_{j}}^{\pm}=(\chi_{j,-m_{j}}^{\pm})^{\dagger}\chi_{j,-m_{j}}
,
C_{j,m_{j},\kappa,i}=C_{j,-m_{j},-\kappa,i}
, so one has

A_{j,m_{j},\kappa,i}=-A_{j,-m_{j},-\kappa,i},\ \ \ \ B_{j,m_{j},\kappa,i}=B_{j,-m_{j},-\kappa,i}.

(108)

Thus the expression of the condensate can be simplified as:

\displaystyle\langle\overline{\psi}\psi\rangle=-\sum_{j=1/2}^{\infty}\ \sum_{\kappa=\pm}\ \sum_{i=1}^{\infty}{|C_{j\kappa i}|}^{2}\sum_{m_{j}=1/2}^{j}\ \{[w(\widetilde{E}_{jm_{j}\kappa i})-w(\overline{E}_{jm_{j}\kappa i})]A_{jm_{j}\kappa i}+[w(\widetilde{E}_{jm_{j}\kappa i})+w(\overline{E}_{jm_{j}\kappa i})]B_{jm_{j}\kappa i}\},

(109)

where
\overline{E}=E+\Omega m_{j}
and
C_{j\kappa i}
is the abbreviation of
C_{j\kappa i,m_{j}>0}
, which only depends on
j,\kappa,i
when
m_{j}>0
.
The condensate is a function of
\theta
and
r
in general. In the special case
\Omega=0
, i.e., nonrotating case, one can simplify the expression further. Using the additional formula:

\displaystyle\sum_{m}Y_{lm}(\theta,\phi)Y_{lm}^{*}(\theta^{\prime},\phi^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\sum_{m}Y_{lm}^{*}(\theta,\phi)Y_{lm}(\theta^{\prime},\phi^{\prime})

(110)

\displaystyle=\frac{2l+1}{4\pi}P_{l}(\mathrm{cos}\Theta),

where
\mathrm{cos}\Theta=\mathrm{cos}\theta\mathrm{cos}\theta^{\prime}+\mathrm{sin}\theta\mathrm{sin}\theta^{\prime}\mathrm{cos}(\phi-\phi^{\prime})
, we can get

\sum_{m_{j}=-j}^{j}(\chi_{jm_{j}}^{\pm})^{\dagger}\chi_{jm_{j}}^{\pm}=\frac{2j+1}{4\pi}P_{j\mp\frac{1}{2}}(\cos{\Theta}=1)=\frac{2j+1}{4\pi}.

(111)

Then Eq. (
109
) with
\Omega=0
can be simplified as:

\displaystyle\langle\overline{\psi}\psi\rangle=-\sum_{j=1/2}^{\infty}\ \sum_{\kappa=\pm}\ \sum_{i=1}^{\infty}{|C_{j\kappa i}|}^{2}w({E}_{j\kappa i})\mathfrak{B}_{j\kappa i,m>0},

(112)

where
E_{j\kappa i}
is the abbreviation of
E_{j\kappa i,m_{j}>0}
and

\displaystyle\mathfrak{B}_{j\kappa i,m>0}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m_{j}=1/2}^{j}2B_{jm_{j}\kappa i}(r,\theta)

(113)

\displaystyle=\frac{M}{2E}\frac{2j+1}{4\pi}[j_{j-\frac{1}{2}}^{2}(p_{j\kappa i,m>0}r)+j_{j+\frac{1}{2}}^{2}(p_{j\kappa i,m>0}r)],

which only depends on
r
. So the condensate inside a nonrotating sphere with spectral boundary condition only depends on coordinate
r
. This is easy to understand since a nonrotating spherical system has spherical symmetry.

The condensate we calculate above is divergent. We can subtract its divergent part which is independent of temperature to get the condensate
\langle:\overline{\psi}\psi:\rangle
which is finite. To do this, we just need to replace
\omega(\widetilde{E})
by

\omega^{\prime}(\widetilde{E})=-\frac{\theta(E)}{1+e^{\beta(\widetilde{E}-\mu)}}-\frac{\theta({E})}{1+e^{\beta(\widetilde{E}+\mu)}}.

(114)

V
Summary

In this paper, we studied a Dirac field enclosed inside a sphere in Minkowski space-time. The solutions to the Dirac equation in rotating spherical coordinates have the same form with the solutions to the Dirac equation in nonrotating spherical coordinates, but the spectrum in rotating spherical coordinates is different from that in nonrotating spherical coordinates by a term relative to the rotation speed. To constrain the system inside the speed of light surface, we considered two kinds of boundary conditions, namely, the spectral and MIT boundary conditions. The rotating quantum states of the system inside the sphere have been constructed for each boundary condition. And the equivalence of the rotating and nonrotating vacua when the boundary is placed inside the speed of light surface was proved. Combining our proof and the proof for cylindrical boundary case in
Ambruş and Winstanley 2016
, one expect that any possible physical field in rotating coordinates has a unique quantization scheme, and the rotating vaccum is identical to the Minkowski vacuum. Thus, a rotating observer will not see the Minkowski vacuum has strange effects like Unruh effect.

Finally, the thermal expectation value of the fermion condensate for a thermal equilibrium rotating field was calculated. We found it depends on the boundary condition, and of course, varies with coordinates and rotation speed. Calculating thermal expectation values has some practical meaning for the study of rapidly rotating matter. For example, in noncentral high energy heavy-ion collisions (HICs), the strong interacting matter can carry large angular momentum and reach very high angular velocity
Adamczyk et al. 2017
. The great vorticity can cause some interesting effects, one is the so called chiral vortical effect
Kharzeev and Zhitnitsky 2007
;
Son and Surówka 2009
;
Kharzeev and Son 2011
, which is analogy to the chiral magnetic effect
Fukushima et al. 2008
;
Stephanov and Yin 2012
;
Kharzeev et al. 2016
. This effect predicts a non-vanishing current along the rotating axis, whose thermal expectation can be calculated based on the spectrum of fermions in the rotating frame
Vilenkin 1979
;
Ambruş and Winstanley 2016
. Great vorticity can also influence the phase transition of the matter
Jiang and Liao 2016
. In HICs, it is shown that the phase conversion from
the hadron phase to the quark phase can be influenced by rotation
Zhang et al. 2020
. To characterize this phase conversion, the fermion condensate can be used as an order parameter
Klevansky 1992
. However, the quark-gluon systems created in HICs, also called as the "fireballs", are estimated to be of only 2-10 fm in diameter.
Palhares et al. 2011
. The finite size effects can influence the phase transition as well
Klein 2017
. To consider both the rotation and finite size effects for the phase transition in HICs, the fermion condensate calculated in this paper may have possible applications, which we will study in the future.
