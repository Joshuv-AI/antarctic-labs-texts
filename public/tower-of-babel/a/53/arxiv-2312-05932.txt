Approximating the distribution of the L q L_{q} -norm of a random point in a d d -dimensional cube
Zoe Shapcott

Abstract
In this note, we assess the accuracy of CLT-based approximations for the volume of intersection of the d d -dimensional cube [ − 1 , 1 ] d [-1,1]^{d} and an L q L_{q} -ball centred at the origin; this is clearly equivalent to approximating the distribution of the L q L_{q} -norm of a random point in a d d -dimensional cube centered at 0. The approximations are CLT-based where to improve the normal approximation we use the first term in the Edgeworth expansion. We have included a section analysing the information obtained from ChatGPT in response to prompts regarding this theory; in our case, ChatGPT answers were not very helpful. Illustrations of the approximation formulae, as the radius of the ball increases, for different values of d d and q q are also given, alongside lines showing a Monte Carlo simulation of the intersection volume.

Introduction

In this note, we explore two formulae for approximating the volume of intersection of a
d
-dimensional cube and an
L_{q}
-ball using the Normal distribution.
We start in Section
2
by detailing the necessary theory and developing the formulae. This includes highlighting some basic notations and key definitions, the Law of Large Numbers, the Central Limit Theorem (CLT), the first three cumulants and the first-order Edgeworth expansion.

Having constructed our two approximations, the next section (Section
3
) provides a discussion of what ChatGPT provides in response to prompts regarding this topic. This is completed by comparing the information obtained from different questions with the theory described in Section
2
and other existing literature.

Following this, in Section
4
, we present numerical experiments comparing the approximations developed in Section
2
with simulations of the intersection volume obtained via Monte Carlo methods. This is performed for different values of
q
for the
L_{q}
-ball and different dimensions
d
. The graphs are plotted over increasing values for the radius. We are thus able to examine the effects of changing
d
and
q

A
.

1.1
Notation

Throughout the report, let
d>0
and integer denote the dimension of the space we are working in. Additionally, let a
d
-dimensional vector
\mathbf{x}
be given by

\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\dotsc,x_{d}).

(1.1)

Then, for
q>0
, the
L_{q}
-norm of
\mathbf{x}
is

\|\mathbf{x}\|_{q}=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}|x_{i}|^{q}\right)^{1/q}.

(1.2)

Taking the
q
-th power of both sides of equation (
1.2
), we obtain

\|\mathbf{x}\|_{q}^{q}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}|x_{i}|^{q}.

(1.3)

This represents the
q
-th power of the distance between
\mathbf{x}
and the origin,
\mathbf{0}=(0,\dotsc,0)
, and will be an important quantity in the analysis of Section
2
.

2
Theory

In this section we aim to develop two approximations for the volume of intersection of a cube and an
L_{q}
-ball. These are a Normal approximation based on the Central Limit Theorem (Section
2.1
) and an improved Normal approximation using the first order Edgeworth Expansion (Section
2.2
). Specifically, we are interested in approximating the volume of intersection between the
d
-dimensional cube
[-1,1]^{d}
and the
L_{q}
-ball of radius
r>0
centred at the origin. We denote this ball by
\mathcal{B}_{d,q}(r)
and define it via

\mathcal{B}_{d,q}(r)=\left\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\|\mathbf{x}\|_{q}\leq r\right\},

(2.1)

where
\|\mathbf{x}\|_{q}
is as given in equation (
1.2
).

In order to estimate the volume of intersection of the cube and the
L_{q}
-ball, we look for a formula to approximate the fraction of the cube covered by the ball. This is completed in a similar way to the specific case of
q=2
considered in detail in
[
1
]
and

[
2
]
; see also Section
1.1.8
of
[
3
]
. We define this quantity as

C_{d,q,r}=\frac{\text{vol}([-1,1]^{d}\cap\mathcal{B}_{d,q}(r))}{2^{d}},

(2.2)

where
\text{vol}(A)
denotes the volume of
A
. The denominator is
2^{d}
since
\text{vol}([-1,1]^{d})=\left(1-(-1)\right)^{d}=2^{d}
. Note that
C_{d,q,r}
depends on the dimension
d
, the
L_{q}
-norm used to define the ball, and the radius
r
of the ball. We may also observe that
0\leq C_{d,q,r}\leq 1
.

Let
\mathbf{x}
be a random vector uniformly distributed on the cube
[-1,1]^{d}
. Then we can also define
C_{d,q,r}
as

C_{d,q,r}=\mathbb{P}\left\{\|\mathbf{x}\|_{q}\leq r\right\}=\mathbb{P}\left\{\|\mathbf{x}\|_{q}^{q}\leq r^{q}\right\}=\mathbb{P}\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{d}|x_{i}|^{q}\leq r^{q}\right\}.

(2.3)

Thus to estimate the fraction of the cube
[-1,1]^{d}
covered by the
L_{q}
-ball, we can try to approximate the cumulative distribution function (CDF) of
\sum_{i=1}^{d}|x_{i}|^{q}
.

Since
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\dotsc,x_{d})
is uniform random on
[-1,1]^{d}
, we have that the
x_{i}
(
i=1,\dotsc,d
) are all independent and identically distributed random variables. Specifically, each
x_{i}
is uniformly distributed on
[-1,1]
, where the probability density function (PDF) of each
x_{i}
is given by
f_{x}(t)=\frac{1}{2}
for
t\in[-1,1]
and
f_{x}(t)=0
otherwise. Consequently, we also have that
\sum_{i=1}^{d}|x_{i}^{q}|
is equal in distribution to
\sum_{i=1}^{d}z_{i}^{q}
, where the
z_{i}

(i=1,\dotsc,d)
are independent and identically distributed random variables, uniform on
[0,1]
. This gives us that the PDF of each
z_{i}
is
f_{z}(t)=1
for
t\in[0,1]
and zero otherwise. Using this and equation (
2.3
), then yields

C_{d,q,r}=\mathbb{P}\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{d}z_{i}^{q}\leq r^{q}\right\},

(2.4)

and to derive the desired formulae we search for methods of approximating the CDF of
\sum_{i=1}^{d}z_{i}^{q}
. We begin by looking for an approximation using the Normal distribution.

2.1
Normal Approximation for
C_{d,q,r}

We break down finding a Normal approximation to the distribution of
\sum_{i=1}^{d}z_{i}^{q}
, where the
z_{i}
are independent random variables with uniform distribution on
[0,1]
(as defined above in Section
2
), into a few steps. First, in Section
2.1.1
, we calculate the moments of order
\alpha
of the random variables
z_{i}^{q}
. Subsequently, in Section
2.1.2
, we apply the Law of Large Numbers to
\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}z_{i}^{q}
. Finally, in Section
2.1.3
, we derive the desired formula using the Central Limit Theorem.

2.1.1
Moments of
z_{i}^{q}

In order to find a Normal approximation for the distribution of
\sum_{i=1}^{d}z_{i}^{q}
we first need to calculate the raw moments of order
\alpha
of
z_{i}^{q}
.

Recall that for a continuous random variable
X
with PDF
f_{X}(x)
on
[a,b]
, the raw moments, or moments about the origin, of order
\alpha
are defined as

\mu_{\alpha}=\mathbb{E}(X^{\alpha})=\int_{a}^{b}x^{\alpha}f_{X}(x)\mathrm{d}x,

(2.5)

see Section 2.5 of
[
4
]
for more detail. Therefore, since the PDF of each
z_{i}

(i=1,\dotsc,d)
is given by
f_{z}(t)=1
for
t\in[0,1]
and zero otherwise (see Section
2
), we can calculate that the
z_{i}^{q}
raw moments of order
\alpha
are given by

\mu_{\alpha,q}=\frac{1}{\alpha q+1},

(2.6)

for
\alpha>0
and
q>0
. Note that equation (
2.6
) follows from direct computation using the definition in equation (
2.5
), as

\begin{split}\mu_{\alpha,q}&=\mathbb{E}\left[(z_{i}^{q})^{\alpha}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[z_{i}^{\alpha q}\right]\\
&=\int_{0}^{1}x^{\alpha q}\cdot 1\mathrm{d}x\\
&=\left[\frac{x^{\alpha q+1}}{\alpha q+1}\right]_{0}^{1}\\
&=\frac{1}{\alpha q+1}.\\
\end{split}

(2.7)

In particular, we can use equation (
2.6
) to find that the mean of
z_{i}^{q}
is given by

\mathbb{E}(z_{i}^{q})=\mu_{1,q}=\frac{1}{q+1}.

(2.8)

Additionally, we have

\mathbb{E}\left[(z_{i}^{q})^{2}\right]=\mu_{2,q}=\frac{1}{2q+1}.

(2.9)

The variance of
z_{i}^{q}
can then be calculated via

\begin{split}\mathbb{V}\text{ar}(z_{i}^{q})&=\mathbb{E}\left[(z_{i}^{q})^{2}\right]-\left[\mathbb{E}(z_{i}^{q})\right]^{2}\\
&=\mu_{2,q}-\mu_{1,q}^{2}\\
&=\frac{1}{2q+1}-\frac{1}{(q+1)^{2}}.\\
\end{split}

(2.10)

Simplifying the above, we thus obtain

\mathbb{V}\text{ar}(z_{i}^{q})=\frac{q^{2}}{(2q+1)(q+1)^{2}}.

(2.11)

Using equation (
2.8
) for the expectation and equation (
2.11
) for the variance of the random variables
z_{i}^{q}
, we next apply the Law of Large Numbers.

2.1.2
The Law of Large Numbers

In this section, We first recall the Strong Law of Large Numbers using Section
8.3
of
[
4
]
, and then apply it to our specific case.

The Law of Large Numbers states that, for a sequence of independent and identically distributed random variables
X_{1},X_{2},\dotsc\,X_{n}
, with finite mean
\mu
and finite variance
\sigma^{2}
, we have that

\overline{X}_{n}=\frac{1}{n}(X_{1}+X_{2}+\cdots+X_{n})\longrightarrow\mu,

(2.12)

with probability one as
n\longrightarrow\infty
.

Implementing this in our case, we see that

\frac{1}{d}(z_{1}^{q}+z_{2}^{q}+\cdots+z_{d}^{q})\longrightarrow\mathbb{E}(z_{i}^{q}),

(2.13)

with probability one as
d\longrightarrow\infty
. This is because the
z_{i}^{q}
are all independent and identically distributed random variables with finite mean and finite variance, as computed in equations (
2.8
) and (
2.11
). Hence by the Law of Large Numbers, we obtain that

\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}z_{i}^{q}\longrightarrow\frac{1}{q+1},

(2.14)

with probability one as
d\longrightarrow\infty
.

In the next section we use this with the Central Limit Theorem to derive the desired approximation formula.

2.1.3
The Central Limit Theorem

Within this section, we begin by recalling the Central Limit Theorem, before utilising this to derive our first approximation formula for
C_{d,q,r}
, the fraction of the cube covered by the
L_{q}
-ball, as defined in equation (
2.4
).

Let
X_{1},X_{2},\dotsc,X_{n}
be a sequence of independent and identically distributed random variables with finite mean
\mu
and finite nonzero variance
\sigma^{2}
. By the Law of Large Numbers (Section
2.1.2
) we have that
\overline{X}_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\longrightarrow\mu
as
n\longrightarrow\infty
with probability one. Now, consider

\frac{S_{n}-\mathbb{E}(S_{n})}{\sqrt{\mathbb{V}\text{ar}(S_{n})}},

(2.15)

where
S_{n}=n\overline{X}_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}
, see Section 8.3 of
[
5
]
. We have

\mathbb{E}(S_{n})=\mathbb{E}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}(X_{i})=n\mu,

(2.16)

and, since the
X_{i}
are all independent,

\mathbb{V}\text{ar}(S_{n})=\mathbb{V}\text{ar}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{V}\text{ar}(X_{i})=n\sigma^{2}.

(2.17)

Thus

\frac{S_{n}-\mathbb{E}(S_{n})}{\sqrt{\mathbb{V}\text{ar}(S_{n})}}=\frac{S_{n}-n\mu}{\sigma\sqrt{n}}.

(2.18)

The Central Limit Theorem then states that

\mathbb{P}\left\{\frac{S_{n}-n\mu}{\sigma\sqrt{n}}\leq t\right\}\longrightarrow\Phi(t),

(2.19)

as
n\longrightarrow\infty
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
, where

\Phi(t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{t}e^{-\frac{1}{2}u^{2}}\mathrm{d}u,

(2.20)

is the CDF of the Standard Normal distribution,
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
. See Section 8.3 of
[
5
]
for more details.

Rearranging the inequality in equation (
2.19
), we can see that it is equivalent to say that

\mathbb{P}\left\{S_{n}\leq t\sigma\sqrt{n}+n\mu\right\}\longrightarrow\Phi(t),

(2.21)

as
n\longrightarrow\infty
. Therefore, using some simple algebraic manipulations, we obtain that for a constant
c
,

\mathbb{P}\left\{S_{n}\leq c\right\}\longrightarrow\Phi\left(\frac{c-n\mu}{\sigma\sqrt{n}}\right),

(2.22)

as
n\longrightarrow\infty
.

We are now in a position to obtain our first approximation by substituting the relevant values into equation (
2.19
) or (
2.22
). Recall that we have a sequence of
d
independent and identically distributed random variables
z_{1}^{q},z_{2}^{q},\dotsc,z_{d}^{q}
, where each
z_{i}
is uniformly distributed on
[0,1]
. These random variables
z_{i}^{q}
have finite mean
\mu
and finite nonzero variance
\sigma^{2}
given respectively by

\mu=\frac{1}{q+1}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ and }\hskip 10.00002pt\sigma^{2}=\frac{q^{2}}{(2q+1)(q+1)^{2}},

(2.23)

as found in equations (
2.8
) and (
2.11
). Therefore, by the Central Limit Theorem as stated in equation (
2.19
), we obtain

\mathbb{P}\left\{\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{d}z_{i}^{q}-\frac{d}{q+1}}{\sqrt{d}\sqrt{\frac{q^{2}}{(2q+1)(q+1)^{2}}}}\leq t\right\}\longrightarrow\Phi(t),

(2.24)

as
d\longrightarrow\infty
. This can be alternatively stated using equation (
2.22
) as

\mathbb{P}\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{d}z_{i}^{q}\leq t\right\}\longrightarrow\Phi\left(\frac{t-\frac{d}{q+1}}{\sqrt{d}\sqrt{\frac{q^{2}}{(2q+1)(q+1)^{2}}}}\right),

(2.25)

for
d\longrightarrow\infty
.

We can simplify the argument of
\Phi
in equation (
2.25
) and let
t=r^{q}
to obtain our first approximation formula for
C_{d,q,r}
. We investigate the effectiveness of this approximation for different values of
d
and
q
in Section
4
.

Approximation 1: Normal Approximation

For
d
large enough, we can approximate
C_{d,q,r}
, by

C_{d,q,r}=\mathbb{P}\left\{\|\mathbf{x}\|_{q}^{q}\leq r^{q}\right\}=\mathbb{P}\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{d}z_{i}^{q}\leq r^{q}\right\}\approx\Phi\left(\frac{\sqrt{2q+1}\bigl((q+1)r^{q}-d\bigr)}{q\sqrt{d}}\right).

(2.26)

Having now established the first of our approximations for
C_{d,q,r}
using the normal distribution, in Section
2.2
we next look to find an improvement on this formula using the first order Edgeworth Expansion.

2.2
Improved Normal Approximation for
C_{d,q,r}

In this section, similarly to Section
2.1
, we split the process of deriving a second, more accurate approximation for the distribution of
\sum_{i=1}^{d}z_{i}^{q}
into a couple of stages. Note that the
z_{i}
are independent and identically distributed random variables with uniform distribution on
[0,1]
, as described in Section
2
. Firstly, in Section
2.2.1
, we define and calculate the first few cumulants of
z_{i}^{q}
(
i=1,\dotsc,d
). Following this, in Section
2.2.2
, we introduce the first order Edgeworth Expansion, which is an extension of the Central Limit Theorem. We then deduce a second formula approximating the fraction of the cube covered by the
L_{q}
-ball by adding this term in the expansion to the formula (
2.26
) found in Section
2.1.3
. The relative accuracy of the two formulas against a Monte Carlo simulation of the quantity will be explored in Section
4
.

2.2.1
Cumulants of
z_{i}^{q}

In order to apply the first order Edgeworth Expansion in Section
2.2.2
, we need the first three cumulants of the random variables
z_{i}^{q}
defined in Section
2
. In general, cumulants of order
n
can be found using a cumulant generating function (see
[
6
]
for more details) but the form of the cumulant can be quite complicated for general n. However, here we are only interested in the first three, which can be simply defined as follows.

For a random variable
\xi
, the first three cumulants,
\text{cum}_{k}(\xi)
,
k=1,2,3
, are given by

\displaystyle\text{cum}_{1}(\xi)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}(\xi)\hskip 20.00003pt\hskip 20.00003pt\hskip 20.00003pt(\text{the mean of }\xi),

(2.27)

\displaystyle\text{cum}_{2}(\xi)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{V}\text{ar}(\xi)\hskip 20.00003pt\hskip 20.00003pt\hskip 10.00002pt(\text{the variance of }\xi),\text{ and }

(2.28)

\displaystyle\text{cum}_{3}(\xi)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\Bigl(\xi-\mathbb{E}(\xi)\Bigr)^{3}\right]\;\;(\text{the third central moment of }\xi).

(2.29)

From the results of equations (
2.8
) and (
2.11
) in Section
2.1.1
, we already know that, for all
i=1,\dotsc,d
, the first cumulant of
z_{i}^{q}
is

\text{cum}_{1}(z_{i}^{q})=\mathbb{E}(z_{i}^{q})=\frac{1}{q+1},

(2.30)

and the second cumulant of
z_{i}^{q}
is

\text{cum}_{2}(z_{i}^{q})=\mathbb{V}\text{ar}(z_{i}^{q})=\frac{q^{2}}{(2q+1)(q+1)^{2}}.

(2.31)

Thus it remains to calculate the third cumulant of
z_{i}^{q}
. Using the definition, We have

\begin{split}\text{cum}_{3}(z_{i}^{q})&=\mathbb{E}\left[\bigl(z_{i}^{q}-\mathbb{E}(z_{i}^{q})\bigr)^{3}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(z_{i}^{q}-\frac{1}{q+1}\right)^{3}\right]\\
&=\int_{0}^{1}\left(t^{q}-\frac{1}{q+1}\right)^{3}\cdot 1\mathrm{d}t,\\
\end{split}

(2.32)

since the PDF of
z_{i}
is equal to one for all t in the interval
[0,1]
and zero otherwise. Expanding the right hand side of equation (
2.32
), we get

\begin{split}\text{cum}_{3}(z_{i}^{q})&=\int_{0}^{1}\left[t^{3q}-\frac{3t^{2q}}{q+1}+\frac{3t^{q}}{(q+1)^{2}}-\frac{1}{(q+1)^{3}}\right]\mathrm{d}t\\
&=\frac{1}{3q+1}-\frac{3}{(2q+1)(q+1)}+\frac{2}{(q+1)^{3}}.\\
\end{split}

(2.33)

Performing some basic algebraic manipulations on this expression yields

\text{cum}_{3}(z_{i}^{q})=\frac{2q^{3}(q-1)}{(3q+1)(2q+1)(q+1)^{3}},

(2.34)

and we are now ready to introduce the first order Edgeworth Expansion to approximate the distribution of
\|\mathbf{x}\|_{q}^{q}
.

2.2.2
The Edgeworth Expansion

To develop an improved version of our first formula estimating
C_{d,q,r}
, equation (
2.26
), we include the first term in the Edgeworth Expansion, which is made up of correction terms added to the Central Limit Theorem. For a general discussion of all terms in the expansion, see for example Theorem
7.9
in
[
7
]
. Here, however, we use Theorem 7.8 in
[
7
]
to define the first order Edgeworth Expansion. For
X_{1},X_{2},\dotsc,X_{n}
a sequence of
n
independent and identically distributed random variables with mean
\mu
and variance
\sigma^{2}
, the first order Edgeworth Expansion states that

\mathbb{P}\left\{\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}-n\mu}{\sigma\sqrt{n}}\leq t\right\}\longrightarrow\Phi(t)+\frac{(1-t^{2})\phi(t)}{6\sqrt{n}}\cdot\frac{\text{cum}_{3}(X_{i})}{\sigma^{3}},

(2.35)

as
n\longrightarrow\infty
. Here
\phi(t)
is the PDF of the standard Normal distribution,
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
, and it is defined via

\phi(t)=\frac{1}{2\pi}e^{-t^{2}/2}.

(2.36)

We now apply this formula to our situation. The
z_{i}^{q},\;(i=1,2,\dotsc,d),
as defined in Section
2
are all independent and identically distributed random variables. These have mean
\mu=1/(q+1)
from equation (
2.8
), variance
\sigma^{2}=q^{2}/(2q+1)(q+1)^{2}
from equation (
2.11
), and third cumulant
\text{cum}_{3}(z_{i}^{q})=2q^{3}(q-1)/(3q+1)(2q+1)(q+1)^{3}
from equation (
2.34
). We thus obtain that the first order Edgeworth Expansion implies

\mathbb{P}\left\{\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{d}z_{i}^{q}-\frac{d}{q+1}}{\sqrt{d}\sqrt{\frac{q^{2}}{(2q+1)(q+1)^{2}}}}\leq t\right\}\longrightarrow\Phi(t)+\frac{(1-t^{2})\phi(t)}{6\sqrt{d}}\cdot\frac{2q^{2}(q-1)}{(3q+1)(2q+1)(q+1)^{3}}\cdot\left(\sqrt{\frac{(2q+1)(q+1)^{2}}{q^{2}}}\right)^{3},

(2.37)

as
d\longrightarrow\infty
.

Rearranging and simplifying the inequality on the left of equation (
2.37
) and also simplifying the expression on the right yields that

\mathbb{P}\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{d}z_{i}^{q}\leq\frac{t\sqrt{d}q}{(q+1)\sqrt{2q+1}}+\frac{d}{q+1}\right\}\longrightarrow\Phi(t)+\frac{(1-t^{2})\phi(t)}{6\sqrt{d}}\cdot\frac{2(q-1)\sqrt{2q+1}}{3q+1},

(2.38)

as
d\longrightarrow\infty
.

Therefore, we can now state our second Normal distribution based approximation for
C_{d,q,r}
. We compare the results of using this formula to the one obtained in equation (
2.26
) in the graphs and discussions of Section
4
.

Approximation 2: Improved Normal Approximation

For
d
large enough, we can approximate
C_{d,q,r}
, by

C_{d,q,r}=\mathbb{P}\left\{\|\mathbf{x}\|_{q}^{q}\leq r^{q}\right\}=\mathbb{P}\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{d}z_{i}^{q}\leq r^{q}\right\}\approx\Phi(t_{r})+\frac{(1-t_{r}^{2})\phi(t_{r})}{6\sqrt{d}}\cdot\frac{2(q-1)\sqrt{2q+1}}{3q+1},

(2.39)

where

t_{r}=\frac{\sqrt{2q+1}\bigl((q+1)r^{q}-d\bigr)}{q\sqrt{d}}.

(2.40)

Now that we have developed two formulae to approximate
C_{d,q,r}
(the fraction of the cube covered by the
L_{q}
-ball) as a function of the radius
r
for different values of
d
and
q
, we next compare the results found here with information obtained from ChatGPT (Section
3
). Following this, in Section
4

we will graph both these formulae against Monte Carlo simulations of the quantity
C_{d,q,r}
as
r
increases for a variety of different values for
d
and
q
.
