A Heintze-Karcher type inequality in hyperbolic space
Yingxiang Hu, Yong Wei, Tailong Zhou

Abstract
. In this paper, we prove a new Heintze-Karcher type inequality for shifted mean convex hypersurfaces in hyperbolic space. As applications, we prove an Alexandrov type theorem for closed embedded hypersurfaces with constant shifted k k th mean curvature in hyperbolic space. Furthermore, a uniqueness result for h h -convex hypersurfaces satisfying certain curvature equations is obtained.



1.
Introduction

The classical Alexandrov’s theorem
[
1
]
states that any closed, embedded hypersurface in Euclidean space with constant mean curvature is a round sphere. Alexandrov’s method is based on the maximum principle for elliptic equations. This result essentially improves previous results due to Süss
[
24
]
and Hsiung
[
12
]
, which require the additional assumption of convexity or star-shapedness on the hypersurface. Later, Reilly
[
23
]
provided a new proof of the Alexandrov’s theorem by using his famous integral formula. Another proof of Alexandrov’s theorem was also provided by Montiel and Ros
[
20
]
, by combining the Minkowski formulae with the following Heintze-Karcher inequality.

Theorem A
.

Let
\Omega
be a bounded domain with smooth boundary
\Sigma=\partial\Omega
in Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
. Assume that the mean curvature
H
of
\Sigma
is positive, then

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma}\frac{1}{H}d\mu\geq\frac{n+1}{n}\operatorname{Vol}(\Omega).

(1.1)

Equality holds in (
1.1
) if and only if
\Sigma
is umbilic.

The proof of Theorem A is inspired by a direct integral method due to Heintze and Karcher
[
11
]
, using the unit normal flow in Euclidean space. Montiel and Ros
[
20
]
also proved an analogue of the Alexandrov’s theorem in hyperbolic space and in the open hemisphere. However, it remains unknown for a long time whether the Heintze-Karcher inequality (
1.1
) can be generalized to space forms. A breakthrough in this direction was made by Brendle
[
5
]
, who utilized the unit normal flow with respect to a conformal metric to establish the Heintze-Karcher type inequality in certain warped product manifolds. In particular, Brendle proved the following result.

Theorem B
.

[
5
]

Let
\Omega
be a bounded domain with smooth boundary
\Sigma=\partial\Omega
in hyperbolic space
\mathbb{H}^{n+1}
. Fix a point
o\in\mathbb{H}^{n+1}
and
V=\cosh r(x)
, where
r(x)=d(x,o)
is the distance to
o
. Assume that the mean curvature
H
of
\Sigma
is positive, then

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma}\frac{V}{H}d\mu\geq\frac{n+1}{n}\int_{\Omega}Vd\operatorname{vol}.

(1.2)

Equality holds in (
1.2
) if and only if
\Sigma
is umbilic.

This inequality (
1.2
) was also proved later using the generalized Reilly’s formula, see
[
21
,
17
]
.

The main purpose of this paper is to establish the following new Heintze-Karcher type inequality for hypersurfaces with mean curvature
H>n
in hyperbolic space.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\Omega
be a bounded domain with smooth boundary
\Sigma=\partial\Omega
in hyperbolic space
\mathbb{H}^{n+1}
. Fix a point
o\in\mathbb{H}^{n+1}
and
V(x)=\cosh r(x)
, where
r(x)=d(o,x)
is the distance to this point
o
. Assume that the mean curvature of
\Sigma=\partial\Omega
satisfies
H>n
, then

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma}\frac{V-V_{,\nu}}{H-n}d\mu\geq\frac{n+1}{n}\int_{\Omega}Vd\operatorname{vol},

(1.3)

where
V_{,\nu}=\langle\overline{\nabla}V,\nu\rangle=\langle\sinh r\partial_{r},\nu\rangle
is the support function of
\Sigma
and
\nu
denotes the unit outward normal of
\Sigma
. Equality holds in (
1.3
) if and only if
\Sigma
is umbilic.

For the curve case (i.e.,
n=1
), the inequality (
1.3
) was proved by Li and Xu
[
16
, Proposition 8.2]
. However, it seems difficult to generalize their proof to the hypersurfaces in
\mathbb{H}^{n+1}

(n\geq 2)
. Our proof of Theorem
1.1
relies on the unit normal flow, whose solution is a family of level sets
\{v=t\}
. Here
v(x)=d(x,\Sigma)
is the distance from
x\in\Omega
to the hypersurface
\Sigma
. Since the distance function
v
is merely Lipschitz continuous, we define the following geometric quantity

Q(t)=e^{(n+1)t}\left[\int_{\Sigma_{t}^{\ast}}\frac{V-V_{,\nu}}{H-n}d\mu-\frac{n+1}{n}\int_{\{v>t\}}Vd\operatorname{vol}\right].

where
\Sigma_{t}^{\ast}
is the smooth part of
\{v=t\}
and
\{v>t\}
is the super-level set. Combining with the Minkowski type formula on
\Sigma_{t}^{\ast}
for a.e.
t\in(0,T)
(Lemma
3.4
), we prove that
Q(0)\geq Q(t)
for all
t\in(0,T)
(Proposition
3.5
). Finally, Theorem
1.1
follows from the asymptotic estimate
\liminf_{t\rightarrow T}Q(t)\geq 0
.

Next, we will provide two applications of the Heintze-Karcher type inequality (
1.3
) in Theorem
1.1
. For this, we recall the definition of shifted
k
th mean curvature. Given a smooth hypersurface
\Sigma
in
\mathbb{H}^{n+1}
, we denote its principal curvatures as
\kappa=(\kappa_{1},\cdots,\kappa_{n})
. The shifted principal curvatures are defined as
\tilde{\kappa}=(\widetilde{\kappa}_{1},\cdots,\widetilde{\kappa}_{n})=(\kappa_{1}-1,\cdots,\kappa_{n}-1)
. Then the shifted
k
th mean curvature
E_{k}(\widetilde{\kappa})
of
\Sigma
is defined as the normalized
k
th elementary symmetric function of
\tilde{\kappa}
. These definitions arise naturally in the context of horospherically convex (
h
-convex) geometry of hypersurfaces in
\mathbb{H}^{n+1}
(see
[
2
,
7
]
).

The first application of Theorem
1.1
is an Alexandrov-type theorem for smooth hypersurface with constant shifted
k
th mean curvature in hyperbolic space.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\Sigma
be a smooth closed and embedded hypersurface in
\mathbb{H}^{n+1}
. Let
k\in\{2,\cdots,n\}
. If the shifted
k
th mean curvature
E_{k}(\widetilde{\kappa})
is constant, then it is a geodesic sphere.

For
k=1
, Theorem
1.2
reduces to the original Alexandrov theorem for smooth embedded hypersurface with constant mean curvature in hyperbolic space
[
20
]
.

For the second application of Theorem
1.1
, we recall the following uniqueness result for
h
-convex domains by Li and Xu
[
16
, Proposition 8.1]
.

Theorem C
.

[
16
]

Let
n\geq 2
and
k\in\{1,\cdots,n\}
. Assume that
\chi(s)
is a smooth, positive and monotone non-decreasing function defined on
\mathbb{R}^{+}
. Let
\Omega
be a smooth uniformly h-convex bounded domain in
\mathbb{H}^{n+1}
which contains the origin in its interior. If
\Sigma=\partial\Omega
satisfies the following equation

\displaystyle E_{k}(\widetilde{\kappa})=\chi(V-V_{,\nu}),

then it must be a geodesic sphere. Moreover, if
\chi
is strictly increasing, then it is a geodesic sphere centered at the origin.

Here we say that a smooth bounded domain in hyperbolic space is
h
-convex (resp. uniformly
h
-convex), if the principal curvatures of its boundary satisfy
\kappa_{i}\geq 1
(resp.
\kappa_{i}\geq 1+\varepsilon
, for some constant
\varepsilon>0
). The
h
-convex domains in hyperbolic space are very natural geometric objects and have received much attentions in recent years, see e.g.
[
4
,
6
,
25
,
3
,
2
,
26
,
14
,
13
]
.

We can use Theorem
1.1
to remove the condition “
the origin lies in the interior of
\Omega
” in the statement of Theorem C and obtain the following uniqueness result.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
n\geq 2
. Assume that
\chi(s)
is a smooth, positive and monotone non-decreasing function defined on
\mathbb{R}^{+}
. Let
\Omega
be a smooth bounded and uniformly h-convex domain in
\mathbb{H}^{n+1}
. For each
k=1,\cdots,n
, if
\Sigma=\partial\Omega
satisfies

\displaystyle E_{k}(\widetilde{\kappa})=\chi(V-V_{,\nu}),

(1.4)

then
\Sigma
is a geodesic sphere. Moreover, if
\chi
is strictly increasing, then it is a geodesic sphere centered at the origin.

We note that if the origin lies in the interior of
\Omega
, then the uniform
h
-convexity of
\Sigma=\partial\Omega
implies that
\Sigma
is star-shaped with respect to the origin. The star-shapedness property is crucial to establish the uniqueness result in Theorem C, see
[
16
, p.97]
.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we give some preliminaries on closed hypersurfaces in hyperbolic space. In Section
3
, we use the unit normal flow in hyperbolic space to prove Theorem
1.1
. In Section
4
, we give the proofs of Theorems
1.2
and
1.3
.

2.
Preliminaries

In this section, we collect some preliminaries on elementary symmetric functions and geometry of hypersurfaces in hyperbolic space.

2.1.
Elementary symmetric functions

We first review some properties of elementary symmetric functions. See
[
9
]
for more details.

Let
\sigma_{m}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be the
m
th elementary symmetric function defined by

\displaystyle\sigma_{m}(\lambda)=\sum_{i_{1}<\cdots<i_{m}}\lambda_{i_{1}}\cdots\lambda_{i_{m}},

where
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Similarly, the normalized
m
th elementary symmetric function is defined by

\displaystyle E_{m}(\lambda)=\frac{1}{\binom{n}{m}}\sigma_{m}(\lambda).

We also set
\sigma_{0}(\lambda)=E_{0}(\lambda)=1
and
\sigma_{m}(\lambda)=E_{m}(\lambda)=0
for
m>n
by convention. For a symmetric matrix
A=(A_{i}^{j})
, we set

\sigma_{m}(A)=\frac{1}{m!}\delta_{j_{1}\cdots j_{m}}^{i_{1}\cdots i_{m}}A_{i_{1}}^{j_{1}}\cdots A_{i_{m}}^{j_{m}},

where
\delta_{j_{1}\cdots j_{m}}^{i_{1}\cdots i_{m}}
is the generalized Kronecker symbol.

If
\lambda(A)=(\lambda_{1}(A),\cdots,\lambda_{n}(A))
are the eigenvalues of
A
, then
E_{m}(A)=E_{m}(\lambda(A))
. The Gårding cone is defined by

\Gamma_{m}^{+}=\{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:~\sigma_{i}(\lambda)>0,1\leq i\leq m\}.

A symmetric matrix
A
is said to belong to
\Gamma_{m}^{+}
if its eigenvalues
\lambda(A)\in\Gamma_{m}^{+}
. Denote by

\displaystyle(\dot{E}_{m})_{i}^{j}(A)=\frac{\partial E_{m}(A)}{\partial A_{j}^{i}}=\frac{m(n-m)!}{n!}\delta_{ii_{1}\cdots i_{m-1}}^{jj_{1}\cdots j_{m-1}}A_{j_{1}}^{i_{1}}\cdots A_{j_{m-1}}^{i_{m-1}}.

If
A
is a diagonal matrix with
\lambda(A)=(\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{n})
, then

\displaystyle(\dot{E}_{m})_{i}^{j}(A)=\frac{\partial E_{m}}{\partial\lambda_{i}}(\lambda)\delta_{i}^{j}.

The following formulae for the elementary symmetric functions are well-known.

Lemma 2.1
.

[
10
,
22
]

We have

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j}(\dot{E}_{m})_{i}^{j}(A)A_{j}^{i}=

\displaystyle mE_{m}(A),

(2.1)

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j}(\dot{E}_{m})_{i}^{j}(A)\delta_{j}^{i}=

\displaystyle mE_{m-1}(A),

(2.2)

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j}(\dot{E}_{m})_{i}^{j}(A)(A^{2})_{j}^{i}=

\displaystyle nE_{1}(A)E_{m}(A)-(n-m)E_{m+1}(A),

(2.3)

where
(A^{2})_{j}^{i}=\sum_{k}A_{j}^{k}A_{k}^{i}
.

Lemma 2.2
.

[
9
, Lemma 2.5]

If
\lambda\in\Gamma_{m}^{+}
, then the following Newton-MacLaurin inequality holds

\displaystyle E_{m}(\lambda)\leq E_{1}(\lambda)E_{m-1}(\lambda).

(2.4)

Equality holds if and only if
\lambda=cI
for some constant
c>0
, where
I=(1,\cdots,1)
.

2.2.
Hypersurfaces in hyperbolic space

Fix a point
o\in\mathbb{H}^{n+1}
, we take
r(x)=d(o,x)
. Using the geodesic polar coordinates with respect to
o
, the hyperbolic space
\mathbb{H}^{n+1}
can be expressed as a warped product
[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{S}^{n}
equipped with the metric

\overline{g}=dr^{2}+\lambda(r)^{2}g_{\mathbb{S}^{n}},

where
g_{\mathbb{S}^{n}}
is the round metric of
\mathbb{S}^{n}
and
\lambda(r)=\sinh r
. Then
\lambda^{\prime}(r)=\cosh r
.

Let
\Sigma
be a smooth hypersurface in
\mathbb{H}^{n+1}
with unit outward normal
\nu
. Let
e_{1},\cdots,e_{n}
be an orthonormal basis of the tangent space of
\Sigma
. Denote
\overline{\nabla}
as the Levi-Civita connection on
\mathbb{H}^{n+1}
. Then the induced metric
g
of
\Sigma
is
g_{ij}=\overline{g}(e_{i},e_{j})
, and the second fundamental form
h=(h_{ij})
of
\Sigma
in
\mathbb{H}^{n+1}
is given by

\displaystyle h_{ij}=h(e_{i},e_{j})=\bar{g}(\overline{\nabla}_{e_{i}}\nu,e_{j}).

The principal curvatures
\kappa=(\kappa_{1},\cdots,\kappa_{n})
of
\Sigma
are the eigenvalues of the Weingarten matrix
(h_{i}^{j})=(g^{jk}h_{ki})
, where
(g^{ij})
is the inverse matrix of
(g_{ij})
. The mean curvature of
\Sigma
is defined as

H=g^{ij}h_{ij}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\kappa_{i}.

Without loss of generality, we assume that
e_{1},\cdots,e_{n}
are the corresponding principal directions with respect to the principal curvatures
\kappa_{1},\cdots,\kappa_{n}
.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
\Sigma
be a smooth hypersurface in
\mathbb{H}^{n+1}
. Denote
V=\cosh r
and
V_{,\nu}=\langle\overline{\nabla}V,\nu\rangle=\langle\lambda\partial_{r},\nu\rangle
. Then we have

\displaystyle\nabla_{i}V=\langle\overline{\nabla}V,e_{i}\rangle=\langle\lambda(r)\partial_{r},e_{i}\rangle,\quad\nabla_{i}V_{,\nu}=\langle\lambda\partial_{r},h_{i}^{j}e_{j}\rangle,

(2.5)

and

\displaystyle\nabla_{i}\nabla_{j}V=Vg_{ij}-V_{,\nu}h_{ij},

(2.6)

\displaystyle\nabla_{i}\nabla_{j}V_{,\nu}=\langle\lambda\partial_{r},\nabla h_{ij}\rangle+Vh_{ij}-V_{,\nu}(h^{2})_{ij},

(2.7)

where
\{e_{1},\cdots,e_{n}\}
is a local orthonormal frame on
\Sigma
and
(h^{2})_{ij}=h_{i}^{k}h_{kj}
.
Furthermore, if the hypersurface
\Sigma
is closed, then for each
\varepsilon\in\mathbb{R}
, there holds

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma}(V-\varepsilon V_{,\nu})E_{k-1}(A)d\mu=\int_{\Sigma}V_{,\nu}E_{k}(A)d\mu,\quad k=1,\cdots,n,

(2.8)

where
A=(h_{j}^{i}-\varepsilon\delta_{j}^{i})
.

Proof.

(
2.5
) – (
2.7
) are well-known, see e.g.
[
8
]
. To prove (
2.8
), by (
2.1
) – (
2.2
) and (
2.6
), we have

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j}(\dot{E}_{k})_{i}^{j}(A)\nabla^{i}\nabla_{j}V=

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j}(\dot{E}_{k})_{i}^{j}(A)(V\delta_{j}^{i}-V_{,\nu}h_{j}^{i})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j}(\dot{E}_{k})_{i}^{j}(A)\left[(V-\varepsilon V_{,\nu})\delta_{j}^{i}-V_{,\nu}(h_{j}^{i}-\varepsilon\delta_{j}^{i})\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle k\Big((V-\varepsilon V_{,\nu})E_{k-1}(A)-V_{,\nu}E_{k}(A)\Big).

(2.9)

Note that
A_{ij}=h_{ij}-\varepsilon g_{ij}
is a Codazzi tensor, i.e.,
\nabla_{\ell}A_{ij}
is symmetric in
i,j,\ell
. It follows that
(\dot{E}_{k})_{i}^{j}(A)
is divergence free. The equation (
2.8
) follows from integration by parts and the divergence free property of
(\dot{E}_{k})_{i}^{j}(A)
.
∎

Along the general flow

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}X=\mathcal{F}\nu

(2.10)

for hypersurfaces in hyperbolic space
\mathbb{H}^{n+1}
, where
\nu
is the unit outward normal and
\mathcal{F}
is the speed function of the smooth part of the evolving hypersurface
\Sigma_{t}
, we have the following evolution equations (see e.g.
[
15
, Theorem 3.2]
).

Proposition 2.4
.

Along the flow (
2.10
) in
\mathbb{H}^{n+1}
, we have

\displaystyle\partial_{t}g_{ij}=

\displaystyle 2\mathcal{F}h_{ij},

(2.11)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}d\mu_{t}=

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}Hd\mu_{t},

(2.12)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\nu=

\displaystyle-\nabla\mathcal{F},

(2.13)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}h_{i}^{j}=

\displaystyle-\nabla^{j}\nabla_{i}\mathcal{F}-\mathcal{F}((h^{2})_{i}^{j}-\delta_{i}^{j}),

(2.14)

where
(h^{2})_{i}^{j}=h_{i}^{k}h_{k}^{j}
, and
d\mu_{t}
is the area form on
\Sigma_{t}^{\ast}
.

3.
Proof of Theorem
1.1

Let
\Omega
be a bounded domain in
(\mathbb{H}^{n+1},\overline{g})
with smooth boundary
\Sigma=\partial\Omega
. Let
\nu
denote the outward unit normal to
\Sigma
. Throughout this section, we assume that the mean curvature of
\Sigma
satisfies
H>n
with respect to this choice of unit normal.

Denote by
X:\Sigma\times[0,\infty)\rightarrow\overline{\Omega}
the normal exponential map with respect to
\overline{g}
. For each point
y\in\Sigma
, the curve
t\mapsto X(y,t)
is a geodesic which has unit speed, and

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} \left.\frac{\partial}{\partial t}X(y,t)\right|_{t=0}=&-\nu(y),\\
X(y,0)=&y.\end{aligned}\right.

(3.1)

For each point
p\in\overline{\Omega}
, we denote by
v(p)=d(p,\Sigma)
the geodesic distance to
\Sigma
and
\{v=0\}=\Sigma
. We define

\displaystyle A=

\displaystyle\left\{(x,t)\in\Sigma\times[0,\infty):v(X(x,t))=t\right\},

and

\displaystyle A^{\ast}=

\displaystyle\left\{(x,t)\in\Sigma\times[0,\infty):(x,t+\delta)\in A~\text{for some $\delta>0$}\right\}.

The following proposition follows from the definition of segment domain of a Riemannian manifold, see
[
5
, Proposition 3.1]
.

Proposition 3.1
.

[
5
]

The set
A
and
A^{\ast}
have the following properties:

(i)

If
(x,t_{0})\in A
, then
(x,t)\in A
for all
t\in[0,t_{0}]
.

(ii)

The set
A
is closed, and we have
X(A)=\overline{\Omega}
.

(iii)

The set
A^{\ast}
is an open subset of
\Sigma\times[0,\infty)
, and the restriction
X|_{A^{\ast}}
is a diffeomorphism.

The set
G=X(A^{\ast})
is the largest open subset of
\Omega
such that for every
x
in
G
, there is a unique closest point
y\in\Sigma
to
x
. Moreover, the boundary
\Sigma
is smooth, and thus the distance function
d(\cdot,\Sigma):\overline{\Omega}\rightarrow[0,\infty)
belongs to
C^{\infty}(G\cup\Sigma)\cap\operatorname{Lip}(\Omega)
. The set
C=\Omega\backslash G
is called the
cut locus
of
\Sigma
. A result of Li and Nirenberg
[
18
, Corollary 1.6]
states that the
n
-dimensional Hausdorff measure of the cut-locus
C
of the hypersurface
\Sigma
is finite, i.e.,
\mathcal{H}^{n}(C)<\infty
.

Consider a geodesic from a point
y\in\Sigma
going into
\Omega
in the normal direction until it first hits a point in
x=m(y)
in
C
. The point
m(y)
is called the
cut point of
y
on
\Sigma
, which means that if we go beyond the point
x
on the geodesic to any other point
x^{\prime}
, then
x^{\prime}
has a closer point
y^{\prime}\in\Sigma
such that
d(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})<d(x^{\prime},y)
. The cut locus of
\Sigma
is the collection of all these cut points
m(y)
for all
y\in\Sigma
. An equivalent way to define the cut point of
y\in\Sigma
is as follows: The set of
t>0
satisfying
d(X(y,t),\Sigma)=t
is either
(0,\infty)
or
(0,\widetilde{t}(y)]
for some
0<\widetilde{t}(y)<\infty
. In the latter case,
\widetilde{m}(y)=X(y,\widetilde{t}(y))
is the cut point of
y\in\Sigma
, and the collection of
\widetilde{m}(y)
for all
y\in\Sigma
, denoted as
\widetilde{C}
, is also called the cut locus of
\Sigma
. These two definitions are the same, i.e.,
\widetilde{m}(y)=m(y)
for all
y\in\Sigma
and
\widetilde{C}=C
, see
[
18
, Section 4]
.

According to
[
18
, Theorem 1.5]
, as
\Sigma
is compact, the function
d(y,m(y))
is Lipschitz continuous in
y\in\Sigma
, and hence
\widetilde{t}(y)=d(y,X(y,\widetilde{t}(y)))=d(y,\widetilde{m}(y))=d(y,m(y))
is also Lipschitz continuous in
y\in\Sigma
. Let
T=\max_{y\in\Sigma}\{\widetilde{t}(y)\}<\infty
. Then we have
0\leq v(x)\leq T<\infty
for all
x\in\overline{\Omega}
. For each
t\in[0,T]
, we define

\displaystyle\Sigma_{t}^{\ast}=X(A^{\ast}\cap(\Sigma\times\{t\})).

Note that
\Sigma_{t}^{\ast}
is a smooth hypersurface which is contained in the level set
\{v=t\}
. Then we have
\Sigma_{0}^{\ast}=\Sigma=\{v=0\}
, and
\Sigma_{T}^{\ast}=\emptyset
due to the definition of
A^{\ast}
. In particular,
\Sigma_{t}^{\ast}=\{v=t\}\backslash C
for all
0<t<T
and

\Omega\backslash C=X(A^{\ast})=\bigcup_{0<t<T}\Sigma_{t}^{\ast}.

Denote by
H
and
h
the mean curvature and the second fundamental form of the smooth hypersurface
\Sigma_{t}^{\ast}
in hyperbolic space.

Lemma 3.2
.

The mean curvature of
\Sigma_{t}^{\ast}
satisfies
H>n
and the following differential inequality

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\frac{V-V_{,\nu}}{H-n}\right)\leq-\frac{H}{n}\frac{V-V_{,\nu}}{H-n}.

(3.2)

Proof.

Using Proposition
2.4
with
\mathcal{F}=-1
, the mean curvature of
\Sigma_{t}^{\ast}
evolves by

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}H=|h|^{2}-n.

By the trace inequality
|h|^{2}\geq H^{2}/n
, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}(H-n)\geq\frac{1}{n}(H-n)(H+n)

(3.3)

at each point on
\Sigma_{t}^{\ast}
. Since the mean curvature of the initial hypersurface
\Sigma
satisfies
H>n
, applying the comparison principle to (
3.3
) we conclude that
H>n
on
\Sigma_{t}^{\ast}
for each
t\in[0,T)
.

To prove the differential inequality (
3.2
), we use the evolution equation (
2.13
) of the unit normal
\nu
to calculate that

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}V=

\displaystyle\langle\overline{\nabla}V,-\nu\rangle=-V_{,\nu},

(3.4)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}V_{,\nu}=

\displaystyle\langle\overline{\nabla}_{-\nu}(\lambda\partial_{r}),\nu\rangle+\langle\lambda\partial_{r},\partial_{t}\nu\rangle=-\lambda^{\prime}=-V,

(3.5)

where we used the fact that
\lambda\partial_{r}
is a conformal Killing vector field, that is,

\langle\overline{\nabla}_{X}(\lambda\partial_{r}),Y\rangle=\lambda^{\prime}\langle X,Y\rangle

(3.6)

for any tangent vector fields
X,Y
of
\mathbb{H}^{n+1}
(see
[
5
, Lemma 2.2]
). Since

\displaystyle V-V_{,\nu}=\cosh r-\sinh r\langle\partial_{r},\nu\rangle\geq\cosh r-\sinh r>0,

it follows from (
3.3
)–(
3.5
) that

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\frac{V-V_{,\nu}}{H-n}\right)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{H-n}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}(V-V_{,\nu})-\frac{V-V_{,\nu}}{(H-n)^{2}}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}H

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle-\frac{H}{n}\frac{V-V_{,\nu}}{H-n}

at each point on
\Sigma_{t}^{\ast}
. This completes the proof. ∎

Corollary 3.3
.

The function
t\mapsto\mu(\Sigma_{t}^{\ast})
is monotone decreasing.

Proof.

On
\Sigma_{t}^{\ast}
, the area form
d\mu_{t}
satisfies

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}d\mu_{t}=-Hd\mu_{t}\quad\text{on $\Sigma_{t}^{\ast}$}.

(3.7)

Since
H>n
on
\Sigma_{t}^{\ast}
, (
3.7
) implies that the area form of
\Sigma_{t}^{\ast}
is monotone decreasing in
t
. Moreover, the set
\{x\in\Sigma:(x,t)\in A^{\ast}\}
becomes smaller as
t
increases. Then the assertion follows.
∎

The following lemma is crucial in the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Lemma 3.4
.

For a.e.
t\in(0,T)
, there holds

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma_{t}^{\ast}}V_{,\nu}d\mu=(n+1)\int_{\{v>t\}}Vd\operatorname{vol}.

(3.8)

Proof.

Let
(\mathbb{B}^{n+1},\delta)
be the unit open ball centered at the origin in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
equipped with the Euclidean metric
\delta
. We use the Poincaré ball model to represent the hyperbolic space
\mathbb{H}^{n+1}
, that is,

\displaystyle(\mathbb{H}^{n+1},\overline{g})=\left(\mathbb{B}^{n+1},f^{2}\delta\right),\quad f^{2}=\dfrac{4}{(1-|x|_{\delta}^{2})^{2}},

where
|x|_{\delta}=\tanh\left(\frac{r}{2}\right)\in[0,1)
and
r=r(x)
is the hyperbolic distance to the origin
o
. It is easy to check that
f=\cosh r+1
.

Let
\Omega
be the bounded domain in
\mathbb{H}^{n+1}
with smooth boundary
\Sigma
, then it can be also viewed as a smooth bounded domain in
\mathbb{B}^{n+1}
. For each
0<t<T
, the level set
\{v=t\}
, which is the topological boundary of
\{v>t\}
, is a solution of the unit normal flow (
3.1
).

Claim
: For a.e.
t\in(0,T)
, there holds

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{n}(\{v=t\}\backslash\Sigma_{t}^{\ast})=0.

(3.9)

Proof of Claim.

It follows from
[
18
, Corollary 1.6]
that
\mathcal{H}^{n}(C)<\infty
. Suppose (
3.9
) does not hold, there exists uncountably many
\{t_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in\Lambda}\in(0,T)
such that

\mathcal{H}^{n}(\{v=t\}\backslash\Sigma_{t_{\alpha}}^{\ast})>0
. It follows that

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{n}(C)=

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{n}\left(\bigcup_{0<t<T}(\{v=t\}\backslash\Sigma_{t}^{\ast})\right)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha\in\Lambda}\mathcal{H}^{n}(\{v=t\}\backslash\Sigma_{t_{\alpha}}^{\ast})=\infty,

which contradicts with
\mathcal{H}^{n}(C)<\infty
.
∎

We continue the proof of Lemma
3.4
. Since
v
is Lipschitz continuous, by
[
19
, Theorem 13.1 and Example 13.3]
we conclude that for a.e.
t\in(0,T)
, the super-level set
\{v>t\}
is an open set of finite perimeter in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
. The reduced boundary
\partial^{\ast}\{v>t\}
of
\{v>t\}
exists and it is always a subset of the topological boundary
\{v=t\}
, see
[
19
, Remark 15.3]
. Moreover, by the coarea formula for Lipschitz functions, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{n}(\partial^{\ast}\{v>t\})=\mathcal{H}^{n}(\{v=t\}),\quad\text{for a.e. $t\in[0,T)$},

(3.10)

see
[
19
, Theorem 18.1 and Remark 18.2]
. Recall that the reduced boudary
\partial^{\ast}\{v>t\}
is the union of countably many compact subsets
K_{i}
of
C^{1}
-hypersurfaces such that
\partial^{\ast}\{v>t\}=N\cup\bigcup_{i\geq 1}K_{i}
, where
\mathcal{H}^{n}(N)=0
. It follows from (
3.9
) and (
3.10
) that for a.e.
t\in(0,T)
,

\{v=t\}=\Sigma_{t}^{\ast}\cup Z
with
\mathcal{H}^{n}(Z)=0
and hence
\{v=t\}
is an open set with almost
C^{1}
-boundary.

Let
\{E_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n+1}
be an orthonormal basis in
(\mathbb{B}^{n+1},\delta)
. Then
\{\overline{E}_{i}=f^{-1}E_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n+1}
is an orthonormal basis of
(\mathbb{B}^{n+1},f^{2}\delta)
. Denote by
\overline{\nabla}
(resp.
\nabla^{\delta}
) and
\overline{\Delta}
(resp.
\Delta_{\delta}
) the gradient and the Laplacian with respect to
\overline{g}=f^{2}\delta
(resp.
\delta
). For any function
F\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{B}^{n+1},f^{2}\delta)
, it can also be considered as a function
F\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{B}^{n+1},\delta)
. Then there holds

\displaystyle\nabla^{\delta}F=\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}E_{i}(F)E_{i}=f^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}\overline{E}_{i}(F)\overline{E}_{i}=f^{2}\overline{\nabla}F.

Using the transformation law of the Laplacian for
\overline{g}=f^{2}\delta
, one has

\displaystyle\overline{\Delta}V=f^{-2}\left(\Delta_{\delta}V+(n-1)d\ln f(\nabla^{\delta}V)\right).

(3.11)

We also have

\displaystyle d\operatorname{vol}=f^{n+1}d\operatorname{vol}_{\delta},\quad d\mu=f^{n}d\mu_{\delta},\quad\nu=f^{-1}\nu_{\delta},

(3.12)

where
d\operatorname{vol}_{\delta}
is Euclidean volume form,
d\mu_{\delta}
is the area form and
\nu_{\delta}
is the unit outward normal of
\Sigma
in
(\mathbb{B}^{n+1},\delta)
.

Using the Gauss-Green theorem on open sets with almost
C^{1}
-boundary in Euclidean space
[
19
, Theorem 9.6]
, there holds

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma_{t}^{\ast}}f^{n-1}dV(\nu_{\delta})d\mu_{\delta}=

\displaystyle\int_{\{v>t\}}\operatorname{div}^{\delta}(f^{n-1}\nabla^{\delta}V)d\operatorname{vol}_{\delta}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\{v>t\}}\left(f^{n-1}\Delta_{\delta}V+(n-1)f^{n-2}df(\nabla^{\delta}V)\right)d\operatorname{vol}_{\delta}.

(3.13)

Applying (
3.11
) and (
3.12
) to (
3
), we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\{v>t\}}\overline{\Delta}Vd\operatorname{vol}=

\displaystyle\int_{\{v>t\}}\left(f^{n-1}\Delta_{\delta}V+(n-1)f^{n-2}df(\nabla^{\delta}V)\right)d\operatorname{vol}_{\delta}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma_{t}^{\ast}}f^{n-1}dV(\nu_{\delta})d\mu_{\delta}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma_{t}^{\ast}}\langle\overline{\nabla}V,\nu\rangle d\mu.

Since
\overline{\Delta}V=(n+1)V
(which follows from the conformal property (
3.6
), see also
[
20
]
), we obtain the equation (
3.8
).
∎

Next, we introduce the quantity

\displaystyle Q(t)=e^{(n+1)t}\left[\int_{\Sigma_{t}^{\ast}}\frac{V-V_{,\nu}}{H-n}d\mu-\frac{n+1}{n}\int_{\{v>t\}}Vd\operatorname{vol}\right].

(3.14)

We have the following monotonicity property.

Proposition 3.5
.

For any
t\in[0,T)
, there holds

\displaystyle Q(0)\geq Q(t).

Proof.

By the coarea formula, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\{v>t\}}Vd\operatorname{vol}=\int_{X(A^{\ast}\cap(\Sigma\times(t,T)))}Vd\operatorname{vol}=\int_{t}^{T}\int_{\Sigma_{\tau}^{\ast}}Vd\mu d\tau,

and hence

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\{v>t\}}Vd\operatorname{vol}=-\int_{\Sigma_{t}^{\ast}}Vd\mu_{t}.

(3.15)

Using (
3.2
), (
3.7
) and (
3.15
), we obtain

\displaystyle\limsup_{h\searrow 0}\frac{1}{h}\left(Q(t)-Q(t-h)\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(n+1)Q(t)+e^{(n+1)t}\Bigg(\int_{\Sigma_{t}^{\ast}}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\frac{V-V_{,\nu}}{H-n}\right)d\mu_{t}

\displaystyle\qquad-\int_{\Sigma_{t}^{\ast}}\frac{V-V_{,\nu}}{H-n}Hd\mu_{t}+\frac{n+1}{n}\int_{\Sigma_{t}^{\ast}}Vd\mu_{t}\Bigg)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{n+1}{n}e^{(n+1)t}\left(\int_{\Sigma_{t}^{\ast}}V_{,\nu}d\mu_{t}-(n+1)\int_{\{v>t\}}Vd\operatorname{vol}\right).

(3.16)

By Lemma
3.4
, the last line of (
3
) vanishes for a.e.
t\in[0,T)
. It follows that for any
t\in[0,T)
,

\displaystyle Q(t)-Q(0)\leq

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\frac{n+1}{n}e^{(n+1)\tau}\left(\int_{\Sigma_{\tau}^{\ast}}V_{,\nu}d\mu_{t}-(n+1)\int_{\{v>\tau\}}Vd\operatorname{vol}\right)d\tau

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 0.

∎

Now we can complete the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

By Proposition
3.5
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma}\frac{V-V_{,\nu}}{H-n}d\mu-\frac{n+1}{n}\int_{\Omega}Vd\operatorname{vol}=Q(0)\geq\liminf_{t\rightarrow T}Q(t),

(3.17)

where we used the fact that
\Sigma=\Sigma_{0}^{\ast}
and
\Omega=\{v>0\}
. To prove the desired inequality (
1.3
), it suffices to show that
\liminf_{t\rightarrow T}Q(t)\geq 0
.

Since
V-V_{,\nu}>0
and
H>n
on
\Sigma_{t}^{\ast}
(by Lemma
3.2
), we deduce that for a.e.
t\in[0,T)
,

\displaystyle Q(t)\geq

\displaystyle-\frac{n+1}{n}e^{(n+1)T}\int_{\{v>t\}}Vd\operatorname{vol}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{n+1}{n}e^{(n+1)T}\int_{X(A^{\ast}\cap(\Sigma\times(t,T)))}Vd\operatorname{vol}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{n+1}{n}e^{(n+1)T}\int_{t}^{T}\int_{\Sigma_{\tau}^{\ast}}Vd\mu d\tau.

As
d\mu_{t}
is monotone decreasing by (
3.7
),
V=\cosh r
is uniformly bounded on
\Omega
and
T<\infty
, we conclude that

\liminf_{t\rightarrow T}Q(t)\geq 0.

If equality holds in (
1.3
), then the equality holds in the differential inequality (
3.2
), which implies that
\Sigma_{t}^{\ast}
is totally umbilical for all
t\in[0,T)
. Therefore, the initial hypersurface
\Sigma=\Sigma_{0}^{\ast}
is umbilical. This completes the proof of Theorem
1.1
.
∎

4.
Proof of Theorems
1.2
and
1.3

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Fix
k\in\{2,\cdots,n\}
. Since
\Sigma
is compact, there exists an elliptic point such that all principal curvatures
\kappa_{i}>1
for
i=1,\cdots,n
and hence the constant
E_{k}(\widetilde{\kappa})
is positive. By continuity of the principal curvatures, we have
\widetilde{\kappa}\in\Gamma_{k}^{+}
. Then we have

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma}(V-V_{,\nu})\frac{E_{k-1}(\widetilde{\kappa})}{E_{k}(\widetilde{\kappa})}d\mu=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{E_{k}(\widetilde{\kappa})}\int_{\Sigma}(V-V_{,\nu})E_{k-1}(\widetilde{\kappa})d\mu

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{E_{k}(\widetilde{\kappa})}\int_{\Sigma}V_{,\nu}E_{k}(\widetilde{\kappa})d\mu

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma}V_{,\nu}d\mu,

(4.1)

where in the second equality we used Minkowski identity (
2.8
).
By the Minkowski formula (see
[
20
]
), we also have

\int_{\Sigma}V_{,\nu}d\mu=(n+1)\int_{\Omega}Vd\mathrm{vol},

(4.2)

where
\Omega
is the domain enclosed by
\Sigma
. Then the Heintze-Karcher type inequality (
1.3
) and the Newton-MacLaurin inequality (
2.4
) imply

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma}(V-V_{,\nu})\frac{E_{k-1}(\widetilde{\kappa})}{E_{k}(\widetilde{\kappa})}d\mu\geq

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma}\frac{V-V_{,\nu}}{E_{1}(\widetilde{\kappa})}d\mu

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle(n+1)\int_{\Omega}Vd\mathrm{vol}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma}V_{,\nu}d\mu.

(4.3)

Combining (
4
) and (
4
), we conclude that the equality holds in the Newton-MacLaurin inequality (
2.4
), so
\Sigma
is totally umbilical and it is a geodesic sphere.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

As the hypersurface
\Sigma
is uniformly
h
-convex, we have
\widetilde{\kappa}_{i}>0
,
E_{k}(\widetilde{\kappa})>0
and
\frac{\partial E_{k}(\widetilde{\kappa})}{\partial\widetilde{\kappa}_{i}}>0
. We deduce from the Heintze-Karcher type inequality (
1.3
) and (
4.2
) that

\displaystyle 0\leq

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma}\left(\frac{V-V_{,\nu}}{E_{1}(\widetilde{\kappa})}-V_{,\nu}\right)d\mu

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma}\frac{(V-V_{,\nu})E_{k-1}(\widetilde{\kappa})-V_{,\nu}E_{k}(\widetilde{\kappa})}{E_{k}(\widetilde{\kappa})}d\mu

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma}\frac{1}{\chi}\Big((V-V_{,\nu})E_{k-1}(\widetilde{\kappa})-V_{,\nu}E_{k}(\widetilde{\kappa})\Big)d\mu,

(4.4)

where the second inequality follows from
V>V_{,\nu}
and the Newton-MacLaurin inequality (
2.4
), and the last equality follows from the curvature equation (
1.4
). Using (
2.2
) with
\varepsilon=1
, we can rewrite the last line of (
4
) as

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma}\frac{1}{\chi}\Big((V-V_{,\nu})E_{k-1}(\widetilde{\kappa})-V_{,\nu}E_{k}(\widetilde{\kappa})\Big)d\mu

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{k}\int_{\Sigma}\frac{1}{\chi}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\partial E_{k}(\widetilde{\kappa})}{\partial\widetilde{\kappa}_{i}}\nabla_{i}\nabla_{i}Vd\mu

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{k}\int_{\Sigma}\frac{\chi^{\prime}}{\chi^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\partial E_{k}(\widetilde{\kappa})}{\partial\widetilde{\kappa}_{i}}\nabla_{i}V\nabla_{i}(V-V_{,\nu})d\mu

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{k}\int_{\Sigma}\frac{\chi^{\prime}}{\chi^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\partial E_{k}(\widetilde{\kappa})}{\partial\widetilde{\kappa}_{i}}\widetilde{\kappa}_{i}|\nabla_{i}V|^{2}d\mu\leq 0,

(4.5)

where we used integration by parts and the divergence-free property of
E_{k}(\widetilde{\kappa})
in the second equality, and the third equality follows from (
2.5
). The last inequality is due to
\chi>0
and
\chi^{\prime}\geq 0
.

Combining (
4
) and (
4
), we see that both the Heintze-Karcher type inequality (
1.3
) and Newton-MacLaurin inequality (
2.4
) must achieve the equality. This implies that
\Sigma
is a geodesic sphere. Furthermore, if
\chi^{\prime}>0
, the last inequality of (
4
) implies that
\nabla_{i}V=0
for all
i=1,\cdots,n
. Then the unit normal
\nu
is proportional to the radial direction
\partial_{r}
, and hence
\Sigma
must be a geodesic sphere centered at the origin. ∎
