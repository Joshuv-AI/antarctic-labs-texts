Abstract

Consider a continuous, stationary, centered Gaussian field B = ( B x ) x ∈ ℝ d B=(B_{x})_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}} with unit variance. Finally, let φ : ℝ → ℝ \varphi:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R} be a measurable function such that 𝔼 ⁡ [ φ ​ ( N ) 2 ] < ∞ \mathbb{E}[\varphi(N)^{2}]<\infty for N ∼ N ⁡ ( 0 , 1 ) N\sim N(0,1) . In this paper, we investigate central and non-central limit theorems as t 1 , … , t p → ∞ t_{1},\ldots,t_{p}\to\infty for functionals of the form Y ⁡ ( t 1 , … , t p ) := ∫ t 1 ​ D 1 × ⋯ × t p ​ D p φ ⁡ ( B x ) ​ 𝑑 x . Introduction

Gaussian fields are widely used to model random quantities arising in different applications, see e.g.
[
16
,
8
,
11
,
7
]
. These random quantities may depend for instance on space
x_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}
and time
x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
(e.g.
[
11
]
) or more generally on several variables
x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},\dots
belonging to (possibly non-Euclidean) spaces of different dimensions (e.g.
[
3
,
20
,
21
]
).

Throughout this paper, we consider a continuous, stationary, real-valued, centered Gaussian field
B=(B_{x})_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}
with unit variance, where
d\geq 2
. We denote by
C:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
the
covariance function
of
B
, defined as

{\rm Cov}(B_{x},B_{y})=C(x-y),\quad\quad x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

We also consider a non constant measurable function
\varphi:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
satisfying
\mathbb{E}[\varphi^{2}(N)]<\infty
for
N\sim N(0,1)
.
As is well-known,
\varphi
admits an Hermite decomposition (see, e.g.
[
23
, Section 1.4]
) of the form

\varphi=\mathbb{E}[\varphi(N)]+\sum_{q=R}^{\infty}a_{q}H_{q},\quad\mbox{with $R\geq 1$ such that $a_{R}\neq 0$},

(1)

where
H_{q}
is the
q
th Hermite polynomial and
a_{q}=a_{q}(\varphi)=\frac{1}{q!}\mathbb{E}\left[\varphi(N)H_{q}(N)\right]
.
The integer
R\geq 1
is called the
Hermite rank
of
\varphi
.

Finally, we consider a family of compact subsets
D_{i}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d_{i}}
,
1\leq i\leq p
, satisfying
\vol(D_{i})>0
for each
i
. The number
d=d_{1}+\dots+d_{p}
is called the total dimension.

The main object of interest of our paper is the additive functional

Y(t_{1},\dots,t_{p}):=\int_{t_{1}D_{1}\times\dots\times t_{p}D_{p}}\varphi(B_{x})dx\quad\mbox{for $t_{1},\dots,t_{p}>0$}.

(2)

We remark that the integral in (
2
) is well defined thanks to the continuity assumption made on
B
as well as the square integrability of
\varphi
with respect to the standard Gaussian measure (see e.g.
[
18
, Proposition 3]
).

Under sufficient conditions ensuring that
\var(Y(t_{1},\ldots,t_{p}))>0
for every
t_{1},\ldots,t_{p}>0
large enough, we study the limit in distribution as
t_{1},\ldots,t_{p}\to\infty
of the normalized version of the functional
Y(t_{1},\ldots,t_{p})
defined as

\widetilde{Y}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{p}):=\frac{Y({t_{1},\ldots,t_{p}})-\mathbb{E}[Y({t_{1},\ldots,t_{p}})]}{\sqrt{{\rm Var}(Y(t_{1},\ldots,t_{p}))}}.

(3)

While the case
p=1
has been extensively studied since the eighties (see e.g. the seminal works
[
6
]
,
[
9
]
,
[
31
]
,
[
34
]
), not much literature can be found when the integration domain of (
2
) does not grow uniformly with respect to all its directions (that is, when
p\geq 2
and when
t_{1},\ldots,t_{p}
go to infinity at possibly different rates). In fact, the extended class of functionals (
2
) has aroused interest only in the past decade (see e.g.
[
30
]
,
[
28
]
,
[
4
]
,
[
16
]
,
[
2
]
,
[
29
]
), because of the more and more important role that
spatio-temporal
functionals of random fields or random fields with
separable covariance function
(e.g. the fractional Brownian sheet) play now in applications.

In our work, the number
p
of growing domains and their dimensions
d_{i}
can be arbitrary. This is why we refer to (
2
) as a
p
-domain
(rather than a
spatio-temporal
)
functional
. To give at least one explicit motivation for studying the asymptotic behavior of this type of functionals, let us consider the case where
\varphi=\mathbf{1}_{[a,\infty)}
. It corresponds to the excursion volume of
B
at level
a\in\mathbb{R}
in the observation window
t_{1}D_{1}\times\dots\times t_{p}D_{p}
, namely

Y(t_{1},\dots,t_{p})=\vol\left(\left\{\right(x_{1},\dots,x_{p})\in t_{1}D_{1}\times\ldots\times t_{p}D_{p}\,:\,B_{(x_{1},\dots,x_{p})}\geq a\}\right),

which is a (random) geometrical object that has been extensively studied in the literature, see e.g
[
1
,
16
]
.
Our approach offers more flexibility than the usual one (corresponding to
p=1
), as it gives the possibility to study the excursion sets of
B
when the parameters are in domains
t_{1}D_{1},\ldots,t_{p}D_{p}
that can grow at different rates.

In a broader setting, the aim of the present work is to enhance our understanding of the asymptotic behaviour of
p
-domain functionals associated with stationary Gaussian fields, when the covariance function has a specific form. In particular, we will show how, in some cases, this asymptotic behaviour can be simply obtained from that of
1
-domain functionals, explaining in a new light and in a more systematic way some results from the recent literature (e.g. those contained in
[
30
]
).

1.1
Separable case

In this section, we investigate the asymptotic behavior of (
3
) when we assume that the covariance function of
B
is separable.

A covariance function
C:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is said
separable
when it can be written as
C=C_{1}\otimes\ldots\otimes C_{p}
, that is, when

C(x_{1},\ldots,x_{p})=\prod_{i=1}^{p}C_{i}(x_{i}),\quad x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{i}},\quad 1\leq i\leq p,

(4)

for some functions
C_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{d_{i}}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfying
C_{i}(0)=1
for each
i
.
It is easy to check that
C
is non-negative definite if and only if
C_{i}
is non-negative definite for every
i
. Also, by stationarity of
B
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we have that
C_{i}
is the covariance function of the field
B^{(i)}:=(B_{(x_{1},\ldots,x_{i},\ldots,x_{p})})_{x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{i}}}
for any fixed values of
x_{j}
,
j\neq i
. Since we are only interested in distributions, we can define the
marginal functionals

Y_{i}(t_{i}):=\int_{t_{i}D_{i}}\varphi(B_{x_{i}}^{(i)})dx_{i},\quad i=1,\ldots p,

(5)

and their normalized versions

\widetilde{Y}_{i}(t_{i}):=\frac{Y_{i}(t_{i})-\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}(t_{i})]}{\sqrt{{\rm Var}(Y_{i}(t_{i}))}},\quad i=1,\ldots p,

(6)

with the convention that, for any given
i
, the values of
x_{j}
,
j\neq i
, in (
5
) are arbitrary but fixed.

Let us denote by
\overset{d}{\rightarrow}
the convergence in distribution. The main result of this section is the following.

Theorem 1
.

Let
\varphi:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a measurable function satisfying
\mathbb{E}[\varphi^{2}(N)]<\infty
for
N\sim N(0,1)
, with Hermite rank
R\geq 1
.
Let
B=(B_{x})_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}
be a real-valued, continuous, centered, stationary Gaussian field with unit-variance.
Let
C:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be the covariance function of
B
, assume it is separable in the sense of (
4
), and also that it satisfies, for each
i
:

C^{R}\geq 0\quad\mbox{and}\quad C_{i}\in\bigcup_{M=1}^{\infty}L^{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{i}}).

Let us consider
\widetilde{Y}
given by (
3
) and
\widetilde{Y}_{i}
given in (
6
).
Then, the following two assertions are equivalent:

(a)

\widetilde{Y}_{i}(t_{i})\overset{d}{\rightarrow}N(0,1)
as
t_{i}\to\infty
for at least one
i\in\{1,\ldots,p\}
;

(b)

\widetilde{Y}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{p})\overset{d}{\rightarrow}N(0,1)
as
t_{1},\ldots,t_{p}\to\infty
.

Remark 1
.

The integrability assumptions on
C_{i}
for Theorem
1
to hold may be removed when
\varphi=H_{q}
; moreover, in this case we also have
quantitative results
for the convergence in distribution (see Subsection
3.1
).

Remark 2
.

Given a
discrete
stationary centered Gaussian field
B=(B_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
with unit variance, the definition of separable covariance function
C:\mathbb{Z}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is similar, i.e.
C(z)=\prod_{i=1}^{p}C_{i}(z_{i})
with
C_{i}:\mathbb{Z}^{d_{i}}\to\mathbb{R}
for
i=1,\ldots,p
, whereas the functionals (
2
) and (
5
) are defined as follows:

Y(n_{1},\dots,n_{p})=\sum_{k\in(\prod_{i=1}^{p}[0,n_{i}]^{d_{i}})\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\varphi(B_{k}),\quad\quad Y_{i}(n_{i})=\sum_{k_{i}\in[0,n_{i}]^{d_{i}}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d_{i}}}\varphi(B_{k_{1},\dots,k_{p}}),

where
n_{i}\in\mathbb{N}
for every
i=1,\ldots,p
. In this setting, analogous results to Theorem
1
could be obtained, see e.g. Remark
6
where we highlight that Proposition
9
directly translates with straightforward modifications for a discrete Gaussian field.

Remark 3
.

The previous result provides a large class of fields
B
with long-range dependence, i.e. with covariance function
C\notin L^{R}
, such that the functional
Y_{t}:=Y(t,\ldots,t)
exhibits Gaussian fluctuations around its mean. See e.g. Example
3
. We refer to
[
18
]
and the references therein for a deeper analysis and further examples of this phenomenon.

Since central limit theorems for
1
-domain functionals have been extensively explored in the literature, it is not difficult to imagine how useful and powerful the implication
(a)\Rightarrow(b)
in Theorem
1
can be.
It is noteworthy that a specific instance of this implication had previously been observed in the papers
[
30
]
and
[
28
]
; however, it was restricted to a very specific context – rectangular increments of a fractional Brownian sheet – and was not part of a comprehensive systematic investigation, as we undertake in this work.

Since Theorem
1
establishes that (
2
) displays Gaussian fluctuations (if and only) if at least one of its marginal functionals does, a natural question arises: what happens when none of the
Y_{i}(t_{i})
’s exhibits Gaussian fluctuations? We investigate this problem in the classical framework of regularly varying covariance functions.

Given two functions
f
and
g
, we write
f(x)\sim g(x)
to indicate that
\lim_{\|x\|\to 0}f(x)g(x)^{-1}=1
. Recalling that
R
denotes the Hermite rank of
\varphi
(see (
1
)), we consider the following conditions:

•

C_{i}
is regularly varying with parameter
-\beta_{i}\in(-d_{i}/R,0)
, that is

C_{i}(z_{i})=\rho_{i}(\|z_{i}\|)=L_{i}(\|z_{i}\|)\|z_{i}\|^{-\beta_{i}}\quad\quad\beta_{i}\in(0,d_{i}/R),

(7)

where
L_{i}:(0,\infty)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is slowly varying, i.e.
L_{i}(rs)/L_{i}(s)\rightarrow 1
as
s\rightarrow\infty
,
\forall r>0
.

•

C_{i}
admits an absolutely continuous spectral measure
G_{i}(d\lambda_{i})=g_{i}(\lambda_{i})d\lambda_{i}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d_{i}}
, and for some constant
c_{i}>0
we have

g_{i}(\lambda_{i})\sim c_{i}L_{i}(1/\|\lambda_{i}\|)\|\lambda_{i}\|^{\beta_{i}-d_{i}}\quad\quad\text{as }\|\lambda_{i}\|\rightarrow 0.

(8)

If (
7
)-(
8
) hold and
R\geq 2
, then it is known that
\widetilde{Y}_{i}(t_{i})
converges in distribution to a non-Gaussian random variable (see Theorem
6
). In the following Theorem
2
, we give conditions so that
Y
has non-Gaussian fluctuations either.

Theorem 2
.

Let
\varphi:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a measurable function satisfying
\mathbb{E}[\varphi^{2}(N)]<\infty
for
N\sim N(0,1)
, with Hermite rank
R\geq 1
(in particular, we have
a_{R}\neq 0
, see (
1
)).
Let
B=(B_{x})_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}
be a real-valued, continuous, centered, stationary Gaussian field with unit-variance.
Let
C:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be the covariance function of
B
, assume it is separable in the sense of (
4
), and satisfies

C^{R}\geq 0\quad\quad\text{ and }\quad\quad\text{\eqref{equ:regvar}-\eqref{equ:regspec} hold $\quad\forall i\in\{1,\ldots,p\}$.}

Let us consider
\widetilde{Y}
given by (
3
).
Then, as

, we have

\widetilde{Y}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{p})\overset{d}{\rightarrow}\frac{{\rm sgn(a_{R})}I_{\nu_{1}\times\ldots\times\nu_{p},R}\left(f_{R,t_{1}D_{1}\times\ldots\times t_{p}D_{p}}\right)}{\sqrt{\var\left(I_{\nu_{1}\times\ldots\times\nu_{p},R}\left(f_{R,t_{1}D_{1}\times\ldots\times t_{p}D_{p}}\right)\right)}},

(9)

where
f_{R,t_{1}D_{1}\times\ldots\times t_{p}D_{p}}:(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{R}\to\mathbb{C}

f_{R,t_{1}D_{1}\times\ldots\times t_{p}D_{p}}(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{R}):=\int_{t_{1}D_{1}\times\ldots\times t_{p}D_{p}}e^{i\langle x,\lambda_{1}+\dots+\lambda_{R}\rangle}dx,

(10)

the product measure
\nu_{1}\times\ldots\times\nu_{p}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is defined by

\nu_{i}(dx_{i})=\|x_{i}\|^{\beta_{i}-d_{i}}dx_{i}

denotes the
R

\sigma

2
). In particular, the limit (
9

R\geq 2
.

Theorem
2
represents a generalization from
1
-domain to
p
-domain of the celebrated Dobrushin-Major-Taqqu (see Theorem
6
). We remark that depending on the choice of the domains, the function
f
can be computed explicitly (see e.g. Remark

).
In the particular case of the rectangular increments of a fractional Brownian sheet,
we note that Theorem

for

[
30
]
and
[

]
.

Remark 4
.

Analogous results (both central and non-central) hold if we fix some of the domains, i.e. considering

and

1.2
Non separable case

It is easy to construct examples illustrating that the normal convergence of functionals
\widetilde{Y}_{i}(t_{i})
is in general not enough to determine the behavior of
\widetilde{Y}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{p})
when the separability of the covariance function (
4
) is dropped.
See Example
4
for such a situation.

This is why we examine in Section
5
what can happen when we go beyond the separable case,
by investigating two different classes: Gneiting covariance functions (Section
5.1
) and additively separable covariance functions (Section
5.2
).

Although not separable, the covariance functions belonging to the Gneiting class are wedged between two separable functions.
It is therefore not surprising that a comparable phenomenon can still be proven in this context, see Theorem
17
.

In contrast, the situation in the additively separable case is much more complicated. We still manage to prove a kind of “reduction” theorem, see Theorem
18
, with however a big difference: the marginal functionals to be considered in the additively separable case are really different from the
Y_{i}
’s of Theorem
1
.

We refer to Section 5 for details.

1.3
Plan of the paper

The paper is organized as follows. Section
2
contain some needed preliminaries. In Section
3
we prove Theorem
1
and Theorem
2
. In Section
4
we provide several examples where our results apply, and we compare them with the existing literature. In Section
5
we go beyond the separability assumption by investigating two other frameworks. Finally, in the Appendix we prove some auxiliary results.

r
th contraction of
h^{\otimes q}
and
g^{\otimes q}
as the (generally non-symmetric) element of
\mathcal{H}^{\otimes 2q-2r}
given by

h^{\otimes q}\otimes_{r}g^{\otimes q}=\langle h,g\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}^{r}\,\,h^{\otimes q-r}\otimes g^{\otimes q-r}.

(20)

We then extend the definition of contraction to every pair of elements in
\mathcal{H}^{\odot q}
. We will denote the norm in this space by
\|\cdot\|_{q}
.

We are finally ready to state the celebrated Fourth Moment Theorem.

Theorem 3

(Fourth Moment Theorem,
[
25
]
)
.

Fix
q\geq 2
, consider
(h_{t})_{t>0}\subset\mathcal{H}^{\odot q}
and assume that
\mathbb{E}[I_{q}(h_{t})^{2}]\to 1
as
t\to\infty
. Then, the following three assertions are equivalent:

•

I_{q}(h_{t})
converges in distribution to a standard Gaussian random variable
N\sim N(0,1)
;

•

\mathbb{E}[I_{q}(h_{t})^{4}]\to 3=\mathbb{E}[N^{4}]
, where
N\sim N(0,1)
;

•

\|h_{t}\otimes_{r}h_{t}\|_{2q-2r}\to 0
as
t\to\infty
, for all
r=1,\ldots,q-1
.

We will also need a quantitative version of the Fourth Moment Theorem, which can be stated as follows (see
[
23
, Theorem 5.2.6 and (5.2.6)]
):

Theorem 4
.

Fix
q\geq 2
and consider
h\in\mathcal{H}^{\odot q}
.
Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[I_{q}(h)^{4}]-3\mathbb{E}[I_{q}(h)^{2}]^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{3}{q}\sum_{r=1}^{q-1}rr!^{2}\binom{q}{r}^{4}(2q-2r)!\|h\widetilde{\otimes}_{r}h\|^{2}_{2q-2r}

(21)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{r=1}^{q-1}q!^{2}\binom{q}{r}^{2}\left\{\|h\otimes_{r}h\|^{2}_{2q-2r}+\binom{2q-2r}{q-r}\|h\widetilde{\otimes}_{r}h\|^{2}_{2q-2r}\right\},

(22)

and, with
N\sim N(0,1)
,

d_{TV}(I_{q}(h),N)\leq\frac{2}{\sqrt{3}}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[I_{q}(h)^{4}]-3\mathbb{E}[I_{q}(h)^{2}]^{2}}.

(23)

2.2
Classical results for
1
-domain functionals

In this section, we state the two most popular results in the framework of limit theorems for
1
-domain
functionals.
The first result, the celebrated Breuer-Major theorem, was first proved in
[
6
]
in a discrete version, and then extended to several settings. Here we state the continuous version of the result. Its proof can be found e.g. in
[
26
]
.

Theorem 5

(Breuer-Major)
.

Let
B=(B_{x})_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}
be a real-valued, continuous, centered Gaussian field on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, assumed to be stationary and to have unit-variance. Let
C
denotes its covariance function. Let
\varphi:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a measurable function satisfying
\mathbb{E}[\varphi^{2}(N)]<\infty
,
N\sim N(0,1)
, with Hermite rank
R
. Let us consider

Y(t):=\int_{tD}\varphi(B_{x})dx,

(24)

where
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a compact set, and
t\geq 0
. If
C\in L^{R}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
then, as
t\to\infty
,

\frac{Y(t)-\mathbb{E}[Y(t)]}{t^{d/2}}\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}N(0,\sigma^{2}),

(25)

where
\sigma^{2}=\vol(D)\sum_{q=R}^{\infty}q!a_{q}^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}C(x)^{q}dx\geq 0
. In particular, if
\sigma^{2}>0
, then
\var(Y(t))\sim\sigma^{2}t^{d}
and a central limit theorem holds for
(Y(t)-\mathbb{E}[Y(t)])/\sqrt{\var(Y(t))}
.

The idea behind Breuer-Major theorem is that if the fields is not “too correlated” at infinity (precisely, if
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|C(x)|^{R}dx<\infty
), then the fluctuations of the functional
Y
are Gaussian.
Conversely, if this is not the case, then one can have non-Gaussian fluctuations (this does not mean that we necessarily have non-Gaussian fluctuations, see e.g.
[
18
]
).
The following Theorem
2
provides a non-central limit theorem for functionals of Gaussian fields having a regularly varying covariance function, see (
7
), satisfying (
8
). In the discrete case, a first proof for functionals with Hermite rank
R=1,2
, was given by Taqqu in
[
33
]
, and then generalized to any
R
by Dobrushin and Major in
[
9
]
. The following sticks to the continuous case and a proof can be found e.g. in
[
15
]
.

Theorem 6

(Dobrushin-Major-Taqqu)
.

Let
B=(B_{x})_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}
be a real-valued, continuous, centered, stationary Gaussian field with unit-variance. Let
\varphi:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a measurable function satisfying
\mathbb{E}[\varphi^{2}(N)]<\infty
,
N\sim N(0,1)
, with Hermite rank
R
and
R
th coefficient
a_{R}\neq 0
, see (
1
). Let us consider

Y(t):=\int_{tD}\varphi(B_{x})dx,

where
D
is a compact set with
\vol(D)>0
and
t\geq 0
.
Let
C:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be the covariance function of
B
and suppose that (
7
)-(
8
) hold for
C
with parameter
-\beta\in(-d/R,0)
. Then, as
t\rightarrow\infty
, we have

\widetilde{Y}(t)\overset{d}{\rightarrow}\frac{{\rm sgn(a_{R})}I_{\nu,R}\left(f_{R,D}\right)}{\sqrt{\var\left(I_{\nu,R}\left(f_{R,D}\right)\right)}},

(26)

where
\nu
is the measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
defined as
\nu(dx):=|x|^{\beta-d}dx
, and
f_{R,D}
is defined as

f_{R,D}(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{R}):=\int_{D}e^{i\langle x,\lambda_{1}+\dots+\lambda_{R}\rangle}dx.

(27)

In particular, the limit
I_{\nu,R}\left(f_{R,D}\right)
is not Gaussian as soon as
R\geq 2
.

Remark 5
.

The previous theorem shows that the limit
I_{\nu,R}\left(f_{R,D}\right)
depends on
R
,
d
,
\beta
and the domain
D
. In fact, the integrand is
f_{R,D}(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{R})=\mathcal{F}[\mathbf{1}_{D}](\lambda_{1}+\dots+\lambda_{R})
, where
\mathcal{F}[\mathbf{1}_{D}]
is the Fourier transform of the indicator function of the compact set
D
. The most common choices for
D
are the rectangles
D=\bigtimes_{i=1}^{d}[0,u_{i}]
,
u_{i}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
, with Fourier transform

\mathcal{F}[\mathbf{1}_{D}](\lambda)=\prod_{j=1}^{d}\int_{0}^{u_{j}}e^{i\lambda_{j}x_{j}}dx_{j}=\prod_{j=1}^{d}\frac{e^{i\lambda_{j}u_{j}}-1}{i\lambda_{j}},

and domains
D=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\|x\|\leq u\}
,
u\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
, with Fourier transform

\mathcal{F}[\mathbf{1}_{D}](\lambda)=c_{d}\,J_{d/2}(u\|\lambda\|)\left(\frac{u}{\|\lambda\|}\right)^{d/2},

where
J_{d/2}
is a Bessel function of the first kind and order
d/2
, and
c_{d}
is a positive constant, see e.g.
[
18
, Section 2.2]
.

Proof of Theorem

Therefore, see Section

, we may express it as follows

where

Let us recall the definition of the marginal functionals (
5
), and, accordingly, set

Y_{i}{(t_{i})}[q]=I_{q}\bigg(\int_{t_{i}D_{i}}(e_{x_{i}}^{(i)})^{\otimes q}dx_{i}\bigg)=I_{q}(f_{i}{(t_{i})}),\quad i=1,\ldots,p.

(31)

Proposition 7
.

Let
B=(B_{x})_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}
be a real-valued, continuous, centered, stationary Gaussian field with unit-variance. Let
C:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be the covariance function of
B
and assume it is separable in the sense of (
4
). Then

\var(Y(t_{1},\ldots,t_{p})[q])=(q!)^{1-p}\prod_{i=1}^{p}\var(Y_{i}{(t_{i})}[q]).

(32)

Moreover, the following holds: if there exists
i\in\{1,\ldots,p\}
such that

\widetilde{Y}_{i}(t_{i})[q]\overset{d}{\rightarrow}N(0,1)\quad\quad\text{ as }t_{i}\to\infty,

(33)

then

\widetilde{Y}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{p})[q]\overset{d}{\rightarrow}N(0,1)\quad\quad\text{ as }t_{1},\ldots,t_{p}\to\infty.

(34)

Proof.

Note that
\var(Y_{i}{(t_{i})}[q])=q!\|f_{i}(t_{i})\|^{2}_{q}
. As a result, (
32
) follows from the separability of
C
:

\displaystyle\var(Y(t_{1},\ldots,t_{p})[q])

\displaystyle=q!\int_{(t_{1}D_{1}\times\ldots\times t_{p}D_{p})^{2}}C(x_{1}-y_{1},\ldots,x_{p}-y_{p})^{q}dx_{1}\dots dx_{p}dy_{1}\dots dy_{p}

(35)

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{p}{\int_{(t_{i}D_{i})^{2}}C_{i}(x_{i}-y_{i})^{q}dx_{i}dy_{i}}=(q!)^{1-p}\prod_{i=1}^{p}\var(Y_{i}{(t_{i})}[q]).

(36)

If
q=1
everything is Gaussian and there is nothing more to show. Thus, let us assume that
q\geq 2
and let us compute the norm of the
r
th contraction of
f(t_{1},\ldots,t_{p})
with itself, for
r=1,\ldots,q-1
:

\displaystyle\|f{(t_{1},\dots,t_{p})}\otimes_{r}f{(t_{1},\dots,t_{p})}\|_{{2q-2r}}^{2}=

(37)

\displaystyle=\int_{(t_{1}D_{1}\times\dots\times t_{p}D_{p})^{4}}C(x-z)^{r}C(y-u)^{r}C(x-y)^{q-r}C(z-u)^{q-r}dxdydudz

(38)

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{p}\int_{(t_{i}D_{i})^{4}}C_{i}(x_{i}-z_{i})^{r}C_{i}(y_{i}-u_{i})^{r}C_{i}(x_{i}-y_{i})^{q-r}C_{i}(z_{i}-u_{i})^{q-r}dx_{i}dy_{i}du_{i}dz_{i},

(39)

where
x_{i}
denotes the projection of
x
onto the
i
th block of
\mathbb{R}^{d}=\prod_{i=1}^{p}\mathbb{R}^{d_{i}}
. Let us define
\tilde{f}:=\frac{f}{\|f\|}
. By linearity, it immediately follows that
\widetilde{Y}{(t_{1},\dots,t_{p})}[q]=I_{q}(\tilde{f}{(t_{1},\dots,t_{p})})
and
\widetilde{Y}_{i}{(t_{i})}[q]=I_{q}(\tilde{f}_{i}{(t_{i})})
. Then, combining (
37
) and (
32
), we obtain

\|\widetilde{f}{(t_{1},\ldots,t_{p})}\otimes_{r}\widetilde{f}{(t_{1},\ldots,t_{p})}\|_{2q-2r}^{2}=\prod_{i=1}^{p}\|\widetilde{f}_{i}{(t_{i})}\otimes_{r}\widetilde{f}_{i}(t_{i})\|_{2q-2r}^{2}.

(40)

Since for every
f\in\mathcal{H}^{\otimes q}
and
r=1,\ldots,q-1
, we have

\|f\otimes_{r}f\|_{2q-2r}^{2}\leq\|f\|_{q}^{4},

(41)

we obtain

\|\widetilde{f}{(t_{1},\dots,t_{p})}\otimes_{r}\widetilde{f}{(t_{1},\dots,t_{p})}\|_{2q-2r}^{2}\leq\min\{\|\widetilde{f}_{i}{(t_{i})}\otimes_{r}\widetilde{f}_{i}(t_{i})\|_{2q-2r}^{2}:i=1,\ldots,p\}.

(42)

It remains to apply Theorem
3
to conclude that, if
\widetilde{Y}_{i}(t_{i})\rightarrow N(0,1)
as
t_{i}\rightarrow\infty
, then
\|\widetilde{f}_{i}{(t_{i})}\otimes_{r}\widetilde{f}_{i}(t_{i})\|_{2q-2r}\to 0
for all
r\in\{1,\ldots,q-1\}
, implying that

\|\widetilde{f}{(t_{1},\dots,t_{p})}\otimes_{r}\widetilde{f}{(t_{1},\dots,t_{p})}\|_{2q-2r}\to 0
for all
r\in\{1,\ldots,q-1\}
, implying finally that
\widetilde{Y}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{p})\overset{d}{\rightarrow}N(0,1)
.
∎

The following result provides the converse implication of Proposition
7
, under additional assumptions.

Proposition 8
.

Let
B=(B_{x})_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}

(43)

then there exists at least one

\widetilde{Y}_{i}(t_{i})[q]\overset{d}{\rightarrow}N(0,1)\quad\quad\text{ as }t_{i}\to\infty.

(44)

Proof.

When
q=1
, we have that
\widetilde{Y}
and the
\widetilde{Y}_{i}

meaning that the statement is correct but empty.

So, let us assume from now on that
q\geq 2
and that
\widetilde{Y}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{p})[q]\overset{d}{\rightarrow}N(0,1)
. By the Fourth Moment Theorem
3
one has that

\|\widetilde{f}{(t_{1},\ldots,t_{p})}\otimes_{r}\widetilde{f}{(t_{1},\ldots,t_{p})}\|_{2q-2r}\to 0

as
t_{1},\ldots,t_{p}\rightarrow\infty
for any
r\in\{1,\dots,q-1\}
.

When
q=2
, there is only one contraction to consider. Looking at

40
), we deduce that at least one of the factors in (
40
) must go to
0
which, by Theorem
3
, implies that at least one among the

Consider now the case where
q=3
.
By Fubini, we have for every

\|\widetilde{f}_{i}(t_{i})\otimes_{1}\widetilde{f}_{i}(t_{i})\|_{4}^{2}=\|\widetilde{f}_{i}(t_{i})\otimes_{2}\widetilde{f}_{i}(t_{i})\|_{2}^{2},

(45)

and (
40
) allows again to conclude.

Finally, let us suppose that
q\geq 4
and

, that is either
q
even, or
q

.
Since the second contraction satisfies

\|\widetilde{f}({t_{1},\ldots,t_{p}})\otimes_{2}\widetilde{f}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{p})\|_{2q-4}\longrightarrow 0,\quad\text{as }t_{1},\ldots,t_{p}\rightarrow\infty,

(46)

we deduce from (

such that

\|\widetilde{f}_{i}{(t_{i})}\otimes_{2}\widetilde{f}_{i}(t_{i})\|_{2q-4}^{2}\rightarrow 0\text{ as }t_{i}\rightarrow\infty.

(47)

Let us show that this is sufficient to conclude about the asymptotic normality of
\widetilde{Y}_{i}(t_{i})[q]
when

. For this, let us consider a positive sequence
(a_{t_{i}})_{t_{i}>0}
with

will be made precise later) and define the subset

of

A_{t_{i}}=\left\{(x_{i},y_{i},u_{i},z_{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{4d_{i}}:|C_{i}(x_{i}-z_{i})C_{i}(y_{i}-u_{i})|\leq a_{t_{i}}|C_{i}(x_{i}-y_{i})C_{i}(z_{i}-u_{i})|\right\}.

We can then write, for every

:

\displaystyle\|f_{i}(t_{i})\otimes_{r}f_{i}(t_{i})\|_{2q-2r}^{2}=

(48)

\displaystyle=\int_{(t_{i}D_{i})^{4}}C_{i}(x_{i}-z_{i})^{r}C_{i}(y_{i}-u_{i})^{r}C_{i}(x_{i}-y_{i})^{q-r}C_{i}(z_{i}-u_{i})^{q-r}dx_{i}dy_{i}du_{i}dz_{i}

-domain functionals

In this subsection, we provide an extension of the celebrated Breuer-Major theorem from the classical setting of
1
-domain functionals (see Theorem
5
) to the setting of
p
-domain functionals.

Theorem 10
.

Let
B=(B_{x})_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}
be a real-valued, continuous, centered, stationary Gaussian field with unit-variance. Let
\varphi:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a measurable function satisfying
\mathbb{E}[\varphi^{2}(N)]<\infty
,
N\sim N(0,1)
, with Hermite rank
R\geq 1
. Let us consider
\widetilde{Y}
as in (
3
). Let
C:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be the covariance function of
B
and assume it is separable in the sense of (
4
). If
C_{i}\in L^{R}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{i}})
for any
i\in\{1,\ldots,p\}
, then, as
t_{1},\ldots,t_{p}\to\infty
,

\frac{Y(t_{1},\ldots,t_{p})-\mathbb{E}[Y(t_{1},\ldots,t_{p})]}{t_{1}^{d_{1}/2}\ldots t_{p}^{d_{p}/2}}\overset{d}{\rightarrow}N(0,\sigma^{2}),

(67)

where

\sigma^{2}=\sum_{q\geq R}a_{q}^{2}q!\prod_{i=1}^{p}\vol(D_{i})\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{i}}}C_{i}^{q}(z_{i})dz_{i}.

(68)

Moreover, if
\sigma^{2}>0
then

\var(Y(t_{1},\ldots,t_{p}))\sim\sigma^{2}\,t_{1}^{d_{1}}\ldots t_{p}^{d_{p}}

(69)

and we have a central limit theorem for (
3
), that is

\widetilde{Y}(t_{1},\dots,t_{p})\overset{d}{\rightarrow}N(0,1)\quad\text{ as }t_{1},\ldots,t_{p}\rightarrow\infty.

Remark 7
.

The previous theorem may be seen as a Breuer-Major theorem for
p
-domains. Its proof follows from similar arguments to the ones in
[
23
, Theorem 7.2.4]
. However, we observe that the separable assumption allows us to recover the rate of the variance as a function of the (possibly distinct) growth rates of the domains.

Proof of Theorem
10
.

We proceed as in the proof of
[
23
, Theorem 7.2.4]
, using
[
23
, Theorem 6.3.1]
.
First of all, we have

\displaystyle\frac{Y(t_{1},\dots,t_{p})-\mathbb{E}[Y(t_{1},\dots,t_{p})]}{t_{1}^{d_{1}/2}\ldots t_{p}^{d_{p}/2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{q=R}^{\infty}a_{q}\int_{t_{1}D_{1}\times\dots\times t_{p}D_{p}}H_{q}(B_{x})dx}{t_{1}^{d_{1}/2}\ldots t_{p}^{d_{p}/2}}

(70)

\displaystyle=\sum_{q=R}^{\infty}I_{q}(f_{q}(t_{1},\dots,t_{p})),

(71)

where

f_{q}(t_{1},\dots,t_{p}):=\frac{a_{q}\int_{t_{1}D_{1}\times\dots\times t_{p}D_{p}}e_{x}^{\otimes q}dx}{t_{1}^{d_{1}/2}\ldots t_{p}^{d_{p}/2}}.

To conclude the proof we only need to check the conditions (a)-(d) of
[
23
, Theorem 6.3.1]
.

Condition (a):
We need to check that

\sigma^{2}[q]:=\lim_{t_{1},\ldots,t_{p}\to\infty}q!\|f_{q}(t_{1},\dots,t_{p})\|^{2}

exists in
[0,\infty)
for each
q\geq R
.
Since
C_{i}\in L^{R}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{i}})
(implying
C_{i}\in L^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{i}})
) for every
i\in\{1,\ldots,p\}
, the change of variable
z_{i}=x_{i}-y_{i}
yields

\displaystyle\int_{(t_{i}D_{i})^{2}}C_{i}^{q}(x_{i}-y_{i})dx_{i}dy_{i}

\displaystyle=t_{i}^{d_{i}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{i}}}C_{i}^{q}(z_{i})\vol(D_{i}\cap(D_{i}+z_{i}/t_{i}))dz_{i}

(72)

\displaystyle\sim t_{i}^{d_{i}}\vol(D_{i})\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{i}}}C_{i}^{q}(z_{i})dz_{i},\quad\quad\text{as }t_{i}\rightarrow\infty.

(73)

Combining the previous equivalent with the separability of
C
, we obtain

\displaystyle q!\|f_{q}(t_{1},\dots,t_{p})\|^{2}=q!a_{q}^{2}\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{p}\int_{(t_{i}D_{i})^{2}}C_{i}^{q}(x_{i}-y_{i})dx_{i}dy_{i}}{t_{1}^{d_{1}/2}\ldots t_{p}^{d_{p}/2}}\rightarrow\sigma^{2}[q],

(74)

as
t_{1},\ldots,t_{p}\to\infty
, where

\sigma^{2}[q]:=q!a_{q}^{2}\prod_{i=1}^{p}\vol(D_{i})\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{i}}}C_{i}^{q}(z_{i})dz_{i}.

Condition (b):
We need to check that

\sum_{q=R}^{\infty}\sigma^{2}[q]<\infty.

Since
\|C_{i}\|_{\infty}\leq 1
for every
i=1,\dots,p
, we have

\sum_{q=R}^{\infty}\sigma^{2}[q]\leq\left(\sum_{q=R}^{\infty}q!a_{q}^{2}\right)\prod_{i=1}^{p}\left(\vol(D_{i})\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{i}}}|C_{i}(z_{i})|^{R}dz\right).

Thanks to

\var(\varphi(N))=\sum_{q=R}^{\infty}q!a_{q}^{2}<\infty
, the claim follows.

Condition (c):
We need to check that for every
q\geq R
and every
r=1,\dots,q-1

\|f_{q}(t_{1},\dots,t_{p})\otimes_{r}f_{q}(t_{1},\dots,t_{p})\|^{2}\rightarrow 0\quad\quad\text{ as }t_{1},\ldots,t_{p}\rightarrow\infty.

Since
C
is separable, we deduce from (
37
) that

\|f_{q}(t_{1},\dots,t_{p})\otimes_{r}f_{q}(t_{1},\dots,t_{p})\|^{2}=\prod_{i=1}^{p}\|f_{i,q}(t_{i})\otimes_{r}f_{i,q}(t_{i})\|^{2},

where
f_{i,q}(t_{i})
is given by

f_{i,q}(t_{i}):=\int_{t_{i}D_{i}}(e_{x_{i}}^{(i)})^{\otimes q}dx_{i}.

Therefore, it is enough to prove that

\|f_{i,q}(t_{i})\otimes_{r}f_{i,q}(t_{i})\|^{2}\rightarrow 0

(75)

for at least one
i\in\{1,\ldots,p\}
as
t_{i}\rightarrow\infty
.
But (
75
) is actually true for any
i\in\{1,\ldots,p\}
,
see indeed point (c) in the proof of
[
23
, Theorem 7.2.4]
(which uses that
C_{i}\in L^{R}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{i}})
).

Condition (d):
We need to check that

\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\sup_{t_{1},\ldots,t_{p}\geq 1}\sum_{q=N+1}^{\infty}q!\|f_{q}(t_{1},\dots,t_{p})\|^{2}=0.

By (
72
) and since
\|C_{i}\|_{\infty}\leq 1
for every
i=1,\dots,p
, we have

\displaystyle\sup_{t_{1},\ldots,t_{p}\geq 1}\sum_{q=N+1}^{\infty}q!\|f_{q}(t_{1},\dots,t_{p})\|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup_{t_{1},\ldots,t_{p}\geq 1}\sum_{q=N+1}^{\infty}q!a_{q}^{2}\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{p}\int_{(t_{i}D_{i})^{2}}C_{i}^{q}(x_{i}-y_{i})dx_{i}dy_{i}}{t_{1}^{d_{1}/2}\ldots t_{p}^{d_{p}/2}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{q=N+1}^{\infty}q!a_{q}^{2}\right)\prod_{i=1}^{p}\left(\vol(D_{i})\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{i}}}|C_{i}(z_{i})|^{R}dz_{i}\right)\rightarrow 0\quad\quad\text{as }N\rightarrow\infty,

where
\left(\sum_{q=N+1}^{\infty}q!a_{q}^{2}\right)\rightarrow 0
being the tail of a convergent series.
∎

Proposition 11

Let
B=(B_{x})_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}

Proof.

We divide the proof in three steps.

Step 1: upper and lower bounds for the variance.

By assumption, their exist

C_{j}\notin L^{R}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{j}})

C_{j}\in L^{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{j}})

by assumption, by doubling conditions for non-negative definite functions (see

]

[

In particular, this implies

t_{j}^{-d_{j}}\var(Y_{j}(t_{j})[R])\rightarrow\infty\quad\quad\text{as }t_{j}\rightarrow\infty.

(79)

Step 2: we prove that
{\rm Var}(Y(t_{1},\dots,t_{p}))\sim a^{2}_{R}{\rm Var}(Y(t_{1},\dots,t_{p})[R])
as

Since
|C|\leq 1
and by assumption

for

, we have that, for any

and
any

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm Var}(Y(t_{1},\dots,t_{p})[q])}{{\rm Var}(Y(t_{1},\dots,t_{p})[R])}

\displaystyle=\frac{q!}{R!}\prod_{i=1}^{p}\frac{\int_{(t_{i}D_{i})^{2}}C_{i}^{q}(x_{i}-y_{i})dx_{i}dy_{i}}{\int_{(t_{i}D_{i})^{2}}C_{i}^{R}(x_{i}-y_{i})dx_{i}dy_{i}}

(80)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{q!}{R!}\frac{\int_{(t_{j}D_{j})^{2}}C_{j}^{q}(x_{j}-y_{j})dx_{j}dy_{j}}{\int_{(t_{j}D_{j})^{2}}C_{j}^{R}(x_{j}-y_{j})dx_{j}dy_{j}}=\frac{{\rm Var}(Y(t_{j})[q])}{{\rm Var}(Y(t_{j})[R])}.

(81)

Now, by applying Cauchy-Schwarz
n
times, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\int_{(t_{j}D_{j})^{2}}C_{j}^{q}(x_{j}-y_{j})dx_{j}dy_{j}}{\int_{(t_{j}D_{j})^{2}}C_{j}^{R}(x_{j}-y_{j})dx_{j}dy_{j}}

\displaystyle\leq\dots

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{\int_{(t_{j}D_{j})^{2}}|C_{j}(x_{j}-y_{j})|^{R+2^{n}(q-R)}dx_{j}dy_{j}}{\int_{(t_{j}D_{j})^{2}}C_{j}^{R}(x_{j}-y_{j})dx_{j}dy_{j}}\right)^{1/2^{n}}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\vol(D_{j})t_{j}^{d_{j}}\frac{\int_{\{\|x_{j}\|\leq{\rm diam}(D_{j})t_{j}\}}|C_{j}(z_{j})|^{R+2^{n}}dz_{j}}{\int_{(t_{j}D_{j})^{2}}C_{j}^{R}(x_{j}-y_{j})dx_{j}dy_{j}}\right)^{1/2^{n}}

\displaystyle=\left(\vol(D_{j})t_{j}^{d_{j}}R!\frac{\int_{\{\|x_{j}\|\leq{\rm diam}(D_{j})t_{j}\}}|C_{j}(z_{j})|^{R+2^{n}}dz_{j}}{\var(Y_{j}(t_{j})[R])}\right)^{1/2^{n}}

where the last inequality follows by a change of variable
x_{j}-y_{j}=z_{j}
, and the fact that
q-R\geq 1
.
Since

sufficiently large, we deduce from (

\frac{{\rm Var}(Y(t_{1},\dots,t_{p})[q])}{{\rm Var}(Y(t_{1},\dots,t_{p})[R])}\leq cq!\left(t_{j}^{d_{j}}/\var(Y_{j}(t_{j})[R])\right)^{1/2^{n}},

\left|\frac{{\rm Var}(Y(t_{1},\dots,t_{p}))}{{\rm Var}(Y(t_{1},\dots,t_{p})[R])}-a^{2}_{R}\right|\leq c\,\left(\sum_{q=R+1}^{\infty}a_{q}^{2}q!\right)\left(t_{j}^{d_{j}}/\var(Y_{j}(t_{j})[R])\right)^{1/2^{n}}.

Step 3:

\displaystyle\widetilde{Y}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{p})-{\rm sgn}(a_{R})\widetilde{Y}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{p})[R]

\displaystyle=\frac{{\rm sgn}(a_{R})({Y}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{p})-\mathbb{E}[{Y}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{p})]-a_{R}Y(t_{1},\dots,t_{p})[R])}{a_{R}\sqrt{{\rm Var}(Y(t_{1},\dots,t_{p})[R])}}

\displaystyle\hskip 36.98866pt+\frac{Y(t_{1},\dots,t_{p})-\mathbb{E}[{Y}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{p})]}{\sqrt{{\rm Var}(Y(t_{1},\dots,t_{p}))}}\left\{1-\frac{1}{|a_{R}|}\sqrt{\frac{{\rm Var}(Y(t_{1},\dots,t_{p}))}{{\rm Var}(Y(t_{1},\dots,t_{p})[R])}}\right\},

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\widetilde{Y}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{p})-{\rm sgn}(a_{R})\widetilde{Y}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{p})[R]\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq 2\frac{\mathbb{E}[({Y}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{p})-\mathbb{E}[{Y}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{p})]-a_{R}Y(t_{1},\dots,t_{p})[R]))^{2}]}{a_{R}^{2}{\rm Var}(Y(t_{1},\dots,t_{p})[R])}

(86)

\displaystyle\hskip 36.98866pt+2\,\left(1-\frac{1}{|a_{R}|}\,\sqrt{\frac{{\rm Var}(Y(t_{1},\dots,t_{p}))}{{\rm Var}(Y(t_{1},\dots,t_{p})[R])}}\right)^{2}.

(87)

By the previous step, the second addend converges to

Regarding the first addend, since by (

\mathbb{E}\bigg[({Y}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{p})-\mathbb{E}[{Y}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{p})]-a_{R}Y(t_{1},\dots,t_{p})[R])^{2}\bigg]=\sum_{q=R+1}^{\infty}a^{2}_{q}\,{{\rm Var}(Y({t_{1},\ldots,t_{p}})[q])},

.

Let us recall that
B=(B_{x})_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}

The functionals

5
Going beyond the separability assumption

While the separability assumption holds true in numerous applications (see, e.g.,
[
7
, Chapter 5]
for examples in hydrology and fluid dynamics, or think of the many frameworks where the fractional Brownian sheet arises, see, e.g.,
[
27
,
32
]
), there also many instances where it does not.

In this section, we aim to examine what can happen when we go beyond the separable case.
First, let us give a counterexample that shows that we cannot always expect a result as simple as Theorem
1

in the non separable context.

Example 4

(Separability matters!)
.

Let us consider
p=2
,
d_{1}=d_{2}=1
, and thus
d=2
. Fix
R\geq 2
and consider
\alpha\in(1/R,2/R)
. Let
B=(B_{x})_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
be a centered stationary Gaussian field with unit-variance and covariance function
C:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
as

C(x)=\frac{L(\|x\|)}{\|x\|^{\alpha}},\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{0\},

(124)

with
L
a slowly varying function, such that
C
is continuous in
0
. Assuming that
C
satisfies (
8
), since
\alpha<\frac{2}{R}
, Theorem
6
applies to the functional

Y(t,t)[R]=\int_{t(D_{1}\times D_{2})}H_{R}(B_{x})dx

(125)

and we obtain that
\widetilde{Y}(t,t)[R]
is asymptotically not Gaussian. However, both
\widetilde{Y}_{1}(t)[R]
and
\widetilde{Y}_{2}(t)[R]
constructed as in (
6
) have Gaussian fluctuations as
t\to\infty
. Indeed, they both have covariance functions as follows:

C_{i}(x_{i})=\frac{L(|x_{i}|)}{|x_{i}|^{\alpha}},\quad i=1,2;

(126)

and since
\alpha>1/R
, they both satisfy Theorem
5
. This shows how the convergence of both functionals
Y_{i}(t_{i})
is in general not enough to determine the behavior of
Y(t_{1},\ldots,t_{p})
, when the separability of the covariance function (
4
) is not satisfied.

In what follows, we drop the separability assumption by examining
two different classes: Gneiting covariance functions (Section
5.1
) and additively separable covariance functions (Section
5.2
). We will explore whether, akin to the separable case, it is possible to simplify the asymptotic study of

Y(t_{1},\dots,t_{p})
given by (
2
) by reducing it to that of simpler functionals
Y_{i}(t_{i})
.

5.1
Gneiting covariance functions

The class of Gneiting covariance functions was first introduced by Gneiting in
[
11
]
. These functions are very popular in many applications, including geostatistics, environmental science, climatology and meteorology. A
Gneiting covariance function

C:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
has the form

C(x_{1},x_{2})=\psi\left(\|x_{2}\|^{2}\right)^{-d_{1}/2}\varphi\left(\frac{\|x_{1}\|^{2}}{\psi\left(\|x_{2}\|^{2}\right)}\right),\quad\quad x_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}},x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}},

(127)

where
\varphi:[0,\infty)\rightarrow(0,\infty)
is a
completely monotone function
, that is

(-1)^{n}\varphi^{(n)}(t)\geq 0\quad\quad\forall n\in\mathbb{N},\ t\geq 0,

and
\psi:[0,\infty)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
has
completely monotone derivative. The fact that (
127
) defines a non-negative definite function on
\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}+d_{2}}
if
d_{2}=1
was proved in
[
11
]
, and can be extended to
d_{2}\geq 1
using similar arguments. Assuming that
B
is a centered Gaussian random field with unit-variance and covariance function as in (
127
), we may infer that
C(0,0)=\psi(0)^{-d_{1}/2}\varphi(0)=1
, and suppose, without loss of generality, that
\varphi(0)=\psi(0)=1
.
As a consequence, the Gaussian fields
(B^{(1)}_{x_{1}})_{x_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}}:=(B_{x_{1},0})_{x_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}}
and
(B^{(2)}_{x_{2}})_{x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}}:=(B_{0,x_{2}})_{x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}}
have the following covariance functions

\displaystyle C_{1}(x_{1}):=C(x_{1},0)=\varphi\left(\|x_{1}\|^{2}\right);

(128)

\displaystyle C_{2}(x_{2}):=C(0,x_{2})=\psi\left(\|x_{2}\|^{2}\right)^{-d_{1}/2}.

(129)

For this reason, we rewrite (
127
) as

C(x_{1},x_{2})=C_{2}(x_{2})C_{1}\left(x_{1}C_{2}(x_{2})^{2/d_{1}}\right),\quad\quad x_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}},x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}.

(130)

From the expression (
130
), since
C_{1}(x_{1})
,
C_{2}(x_{2})
are positive and non-increasing in the norms
\|x_{1}\|
,
\|x_{2}\|
, we deduce that

C_{2}(x_{2})C_{1}(x_{1})\leq C(x_{1},x_{2})\leq C_{2}(x_{2})C_{1}\left(x_{1}\,\underline{C_{2}}(D_{2})^{2/d_{1}}\right),

(131)

where
D_{2}
denotes the domain of the variable
x_{2}
, and

\underline{C_{2}}(D_{2})=\inf_{x_{2}\in(D_{2}-D_{2})}C_{2}(x_{2})=\psi\left({\rm diam}(D_{2})^{2}\right)^{-d_{1}/2}.

(132)

Therefore,
C(x_{1},x_{2})
is wedged between two separable covariance functions,
C_{1}(x_{1})C_{2}(x_{2})
and
C_{1}(x_{1}\,\underline{C_{2}}(D_{2})^{2/d_{1}})C_{2}(x_{2})
. This suggests that the Gneiting case may be studied combining the bounds (
131
) and Theorem
1
. We will partially formalize this intuition in Theorem
17
, and we conjecture that the latter could be extended to more general functionals, growing domains and classes of non-separable covariance functions satisfying properties analogous to (
131
). These extensions are left for future research.

Analogously to the version of Theorem
1
explained in Remark
4
, Theorem
17
allows to reduce the study of

Y(t_{1},t_{2})[q]=\int_{t_{1}D_{1}\times t_{2}D_{2}}H_{q}(B_{x})dx,\quad\quad\text{ as }t_{i}\rightarrow\infty,

for only one index
i\in\{1,2\}
(i.e. only the

5.2
Additively separable covariance functions

In this subsection we consider
additively separable covariance functions
of the form

C(x_{1},x_{2})=K_{1}(x_{1})+K_{2}(x_{2}),\quad\quad x_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}},\ x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}},

(142)

where
K_{1}
and
K_{2}
are the covariance functions of two stationary, continuous, centered Gaussian fields
(B^{(1)})_{x_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}}
and
(B^{(2)})_{x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}}
, with
K_{1}(0),K_{2}(0)>0
. From the point of view of applications, the study of Gaussian fields with additively separable covariance function (
142
) is motivated by the fact that they can model the sum of two independent Gaussian fields.
Unlike the Gneiting class considered in the previous subsection, which is comparable to the separable case (see (
131
)), here reduction theorems have to be developed in a different way. In this case, we define the marginal functionals as

A_{i}(t_{i})[q]=\int_{t_{i}D_{i}}H_{q}\left(\frac{B^{(i)}_{x_{i}}}{K_{i}(0)}\right)dx_{i}.

(143)

We introduce new quantities to describe the interplay between the growth rates of the volumes and the variances, that are the quotients

\gamma^{i}_{t_{i}}[q]:=\frac{\int_{(t_{i}D_{i})^{2}}K_{i}^{q}(x_{i}-y_{i})dx_{i}dy_{i}}{\vol(t_{i}D_{i})^{2}}.

(144)

Theorem 18
.

Let
B=(B_{x})_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}
be a real-valued, continuous, centered, stationary Gaussian field with unit-variance. Let
C:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be the covariance function of
B
, and assume it is additively separable in the sense of (
142
), with
K_{1},K_{2}\geq 0
. Let
\widetilde{Y}
be as in (
3
). For
i=1,2
, let
A_{i}(t_{i})[q]
be as in (
143
),
\widetilde{A}_{i}
its normalized versions (see e.g. (
3
)), and recall the definition (
144
) of
\gamma^{i}_{t_{i}}[q]
. Finally, assume that

\frac{\gamma^{1}_{t_{1}}[q]}{\gamma^{2}_{t_{2}}[q]}\rightarrow 0\quad\text{as $t_{1},t_{2}\rightarrow\infty$}.

Then, the following holds:

\widetilde{A}_{2}(t_{2})[q]\overset{d}{\rightarrow}N(0,1),\quad\text{ as }t_{2}\rightarrow\infty,

if and only if

\widetilde{Y}_{1}(t_{1},t_{2})[q]\overset{d}{\rightarrow}N(0,1),\quad\text{ as }t_{1},t_{2}\rightarrow\infty.

Remark 8
.

Note that, by symmetry, the roles of
K_{1}
and
K_{2}
can be exchanged in the statement of Theorem
18
, obtaining an equivalence between
\widetilde{A}_{1}(t_{1})[q]\overset{d}{\rightarrow}N(0,1)
and
\widetilde{Y}(t_{1},t_{2})[q]\overset{d}{\rightarrow}N(0,1)
as
t_{1},t_{2}\rightarrow\infty
, if
\frac{\gamma^{2}_{t_{2}}[q]}{\gamma^{1}_{t_{1}}[q]}\rightarrow 0
.

Remark 9
.

To ease the computation of the quotients
\gamma^{i}_{t_{i}}[q]
,
i=1,2
, as noted in Equation (
185
), assuming that
K_{i}
is both non-negative and non-negative definite, one can observe that

\gamma_{t_{i}}^{i}[q]\asymp\frac{\int_{\{\|x\|\leq t_{i}\}}K_{i}^{q}(x_{i})dx_{i}}{{t_{i}}^{d_{i}}}.

(145)

Just like Theorem
1
and Theorem
17
, Theorem
18
should be interpreted as a reduction theorem, since it allows to reduce the asymptotic problem of a
2
-domain functional
{Y}(t_{1},t_{2})[q]
to that of a
1
-domain functional.
The difference here is that the marginal functionals to be considered in the additively separable case are not the same considered for the separable and Gneiting classes. Moreover, unlike the latter cases, here the growth rates of the integration domains come into play by means of the quotients
\gamma_{t_{i}}^{i}[q]
in (
144
) (see also Example

). In order to prove Theorem

, we state and prove the following lemma.

Lemma 19
.

Under the notations and assumptions of Theorem
18
, we have that

\var(Y({t_{1},t_{2}})[q])=\vol(t_{1}D_{1})^{2}\var(A_{2}({{t_{2}}})[q])+\var(A_{1}({t_{1}})[q])\vol({t_{2}}D_{2})^{2}\\
+\sum_{k=1}^{q-1}\binom{q}{k}^{2}\var(A_{1}(t_{1})[k])\var(A_{2}({t_{2}})[q-k]).

(146)

In addition, assuming

, we have that

\frac{\var(Y(t_{1},t_{2})[q])}{\vol(t_{1}D_{1})^{2}\var(A_{2}(t_{2})[q])}\longrightarrow 1\quad\quad\text{ as }t_{1},t_{2}\rightarrow\infty.

(147)

Proof.

Recall
q\geq 2
. Applying Newton’s binomial formula, we may write

\displaystyle\var(Y(t_{1},{t_{2}})[q])=q!\int_{({t_{1}}D_{1}\times{t_{2}}D_{2})^{2}}C(x-y)^{q}dxdy

(148)

\displaystyle\quad=q!\int_{(t_{1}D_{1}\times t_{2}D_{2})^{2}}(K_{1}(x_{1}-y_{1})+K_{2}(x_{2}-y_{2}))^{q}dx_{1}dx_{2}dy_{1}dy_{2}

(149)

\displaystyle\quad=\sum_{k=0}^{q}q!\binom{q}{k}\int_{(t_{1}D_{1})^{2}}K_{1}(x_{1}-y_{1})^{k}dx_{1}dy_{1}\cdot\int_{(t_{2}D_{2})^{2}}K_{2}(x_{2}-y_{2})^{q-k}dx_{2}dy_{2},

(150)

which is (
146

Now assume that
\gamma^{1}_{t_{1}}[q]/\gamma^{2}_{t_{2}}[q]\rightarrow 0
. Then, as

\frac{\var(Y(t_{1},t_{2})[q])}{\vol(t_{1}D_{1})^{2}\var(A_{2}(t_{2})[q])}\longrightarrow 1.

Indeed,

\frac{\var(A_{1}(t_{1})[q])\vol(t_{2}D_{2})^{2}}{\vol(t_{1}D_{1})^{2}\var(A_{2}(t_{2})[q])}=\frac{\gamma^{1}_{t_{1}}[q]}{\gamma^{2}_{t_{2}}[q]}\longrightarrow 0

by assumption.
By Jensen inequality in

and recalling that

, we have that

\var(A_{i}(t_{i})[k])=k!\int_{(t_{i}D_{i})^{2}}K_{i}^{k}(x_{i}-y_{i})dx_{i}dy_{i}\lesssim\vol(t_{i}D_{i})^{2(q-k)/q}\var(A_{i}({t_{i}})[q])^{k/q}.

(151)

Therefore, the proof is concluded by observing that, for

\frac{\var(A_{1}(t_{1})[k])\var(A_{2}(t_{2})[q-k])}{\vol(t_{1}D_{1})^{2}\var(A_{2}(t_{2})[q])}\lesssim\frac{\var(A_{1}(t_{1})[q])^{k/q}\vol(t_{2}D_{2})^{2k/q}}{\vol(t_{1}D_{1})^{2k/q}\var(A_{2}(t_{2})[q])^{k/q}}=\frac{\gamma^{1}_{t_{1}}[q]^{k/q}}{\gamma^{2}_{t_{2}}[q]^{k/q}}\longrightarrow 0.

(152)

(X(\underline{s}^{-1/2}e_{i}(\cdot/s_{1},\dots,\cdot/s_{d})))_{i=1}^{\infty}
share the same law, and, by extension, the following random variables are equally distributed:

I_{q}(h(\cdot))\overset{\rm law}{=}\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{q}=1}^{\infty}c_{i_{1},\dots,i_{q}}\,X(\underline{s}^{-1/2}e_{i_{1}}(\cdot/s_{1},\dots,\cdot/s_{d}))\dots\\
\dots X(\underline{s}^{-1/2}e_{i_{q}}(\cdot/s_{1},\dots,\cdot/s_{d})).

(178)

Moreover, by product formula (see e.g.
[
23
]
), we have that

\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{q}=1}^{\infty}c_{i_{1},\dots,i_{q}}\,X(\underline{s}^{-1/2}e_{i_{1}}(\cdot/s_{1},\dots,\cdot/s_{d}))\dots X(\underline{s}^{-1/2}e_{i_{q}}(\cdot/s_{1},\dots,\cdot/s_{d}))\overset{d}{=}I_{q}(\underline{s}^{-\frac{q}{2}}\tilde{h}(s_{1},\ldots,s_{d})).

(179)

By linearity,
I_{q}(\underline{s}^{-\frac{q}{2}}\tilde{h}(s_{1},\ldots,s_{d}))=\underline{s}^{-\frac{q}{2}}I_{q}(\tilde{h}(s_{1},\ldots,s_{d}))
, which concludes the proof.
∎

Lemma 20
.

Let
K:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a non-negative definite function such that
K\geq 0
. Suppose that
D\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
compact with
{\rm Vol}(D)>0
. Then, for every
k_{1},k_{2},k_{3},k_{4}\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists a positive constant
c
, depending on
K,D
and the exponents
k_{i}
,
i=1,2,3,4
, satisfying for all
t>0
the following inequality:

\int_{(tD)^{4}}K(x-y)^{k_{1}}K(z-u)^{k_{2}}K(x-z)^{k_{3}}K(y-u)^{k_{4}}dxdydzdu\\
\leq c\int_{(tD)^{2}}K(x-y)^{k_{1}+k_{3}}dxdy\int_{(tD)^{2}}K(z-u)^{k_{2}+k_{4}}dzdu.

(180)

Proof.

First, by using the inequality
x^{a}y^{b}\leq x^{a+b}+y^{a+b}
, the LHS of the inequality (
180
) can be bounded by

2\int_{(tD)^{4}}K(x-y)^{k_{1}+k_{3}}K(z-u)^{k_{2}+k_{4}}dxdydzdu+2\int_{(tD)^{4}}K(x-y)^{k_{1}+k_{3}}K(y-u)^{k_{2}+k_{4}}dxdydzdu.

(181)

Then, by the change of variable
a=x-y
and
b=y-u
and compactness of the domain
D
, we may also bound the second term in the previous equation, up to a positive constant, by

\vol(tD)\int_{(tD-tD)^{2}}K(a)^{k_{1}+k_{3}}K(b)^{k_{2}+k_{4}}\vol(tD\cap(tD-a)\cap(tD-b))dadb\\
\leq\vol(tD)^{2}\int_{tD-tD}K(a)^{k_{1}+k_{3}}da\int_{tD-tD}K(b)^{k_{2}+k_{4}}db.

(182)

To conclude, we need to show that

\vol(tD)\int_{tD-tD}K(a)^{k_{1}+k_{3}}da\lesssim\int_{(tD)^{2}}K(x-y)^{k_{1}+k_{3}}dxdy.

(183)

By positivity of the integrand and the doubling conditions for non-negative definite functions that are also non-negative proved in
[
12
]
, we deduce that

\int_{tD-tD}K(a)^{k_{1}+k_{3}}da\leq\int_{\{\|x\|\leq t\cdot{\rm diam}(D)\}}K(a)^{k_{1}+k_{3}}da\lesssim\int_{\{\|x\|\leq t\}}K(a)^{k_{1}+k_{3}}da.

(184)

Reasoning as in
[
18
, Proof of Proposition 9, Step 1]
, we have that

t^{d}\int_{\{\|x\|\leq t\}}K(a)^{k_{1}+k_{3}}da\asymp\int_{(tD)^{2}}K(x-y)^{k_{1}+k_{3}}dxdy,

(185)

which is enough to conclude.
∎
