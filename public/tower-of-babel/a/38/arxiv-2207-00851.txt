What Makes a Strong Monad?
Dylan McDermott, Tarmo Uustalu

Abstract
Strong monads are important for several applications, in particular, in the denotational semantics of effectful languages, where strength is needed to sequence computations that have free variables. Strength is non-trivial: it can be difficult to determine whether a monad has any strength at all, and monads can be strong in multiple ways. We therefore review some of the most important known facts about strength and prove some new ones. In particular, we present a number of equivalent characterizations of strong functor and strong monad, and give some conditions that guarantee existence or uniqueness of strengths. We look at strength from three different perspectives: actions of a monoidal category 𝐕 \mathbf{V} , enrichment over 𝐕 \mathbf{V} , and powering over 𝐕 \mathbf{V} . We are primarily motivated by semantics of effects, but the results are also useful in other contexts.

Introduction

Following Moggi
[
24
]
, effectful computations are often modelled using
strong monads.
Strength also appears in other applications; for example, strength
is crucial for the notion of commutative monad

[

It can be difficult in these contexts to determine whether a given functor or
monad admits a strength,
and various facts have been proved about strength to help with this.
Some appear in published work (often as a small lemma in a paper not
primarily about strength)
[
25
,
29
,
21
]
, while others are folklore.
These have some overlap, and levels of
generality vary.

We collect together a number of important results about strength.
There are two groups of results in particular that we focus on.
One is the equivalence of various definitions of strong functor and
strong monad.
These are useful in particular for reasoning about strong functors and
monads, and are also useful for constructing strengths for ordinary
functors and monads.
The other is results concerning existence and uniqueness of strengths
for functors and monads.
Several of these results are known, but a good number are, to the best
of our knowledge, new.

The difference between monads and strong monads is best seen by looking
at the
Kleisli extension
operator.
If
T
is the underlying endofunctor of a monad, then
every morphism
f:X\to TY
induces a morphism
f^{\dagger}:TX\to TY
, as
on the left below.
In the Cartesian case, if
T
forms a
strong
monad, then
the Kleisli extension has the more general form on the right.

\frac{f:X\to TY}{f^{\dagger}:TX\to TY}\qquad\frac{f:\Gamma\times X\to TY}{f^{\dagger}:\Gamma\times TX\to TY}

Our main interest is the semantics of effects (though the results we give
here can be applied more widely).
Strength in this case enables interpretation of terms with free
variables.
Consider the following typing rule:

\frac{\Gamma\vdash t:A\qquad\Gamma,x:A\vdash t^{\prime}:B}{\Gamma\vdash\mathsf{let}~x=t~\mathsf{in}~t^{\prime}:B}

In a monadic model of a call-by-value language, the terms
t
and
t^{\prime}
would be interpreted
as morphisms
\llbracket t\rrbracket:\llbracket\Gamma\rrbracket\to T\llbracket A\rrbracket
and

\llbracket t^{\prime}\rrbracket:\llbracket\Gamma\rrbracket\times\llbracket A\rrbracket\to T\llbracket B\rrbracket
, where
TX
is the
object of (possibly effectful) computations that return values in
X
.
Using the strong Kleisli extension of
\llbracket t^{\prime}\rrbracket
we can interpret the

\mathsf{let}
as

\llbracket\mathsf{let}~x=t~\mathsf{in}~t^{\prime}\rrbracket:\llbracket\Gamma\rrbracket\xrightarrow{\langle\mathrm{id}_{\llbracket\Gamma\rrbracket},\llbracket t\rrbracket\rangle}\llbracket\Gamma\rrbracket\times T\llbracket A\rrbracket\xrightarrow{\llbracket t^{\prime}\rrbracket^{\dagger}}T\llbracket B\rrbracket

The Kleisli extension of an ordinary monad suffices when
\Gamma
is
empty (because then
\llbracket\Gamma\rrbracket=\mathbf{1}
), but
we need the strong version in general.

Instead of assuming products, we work in the more general setting of
an
action
of a monoidal category on another category.
Strengths with respect to an action appear for example
in
[
3
,
5
,
23
,
10
,
31
]
.
Working with actions instead of a Cartesian, symmetric monoidal or

[
15
]
.
The same three-perspective approach can be found in the nLab article on

[
30

We discuss actions, strong functors, and strong monads in

Our novel contributions are sufficient criteria for unique existence

We also provide a number of examples. We consider enrichment
in

Strong functors

Throughout this section, we suppose a monoidal category

3.1
Uniqueness and existence of strengths

It is well-known that every endofunctor
F
on
\mathbf{Set}
has a unique
strength with respect to the Cartesian monoidal structure.
Various other results about uniqueness of strengths have been proved
(e.g.
[
24
,
29
,
21
]
).
Uniqueness results are useful for determining whether a given functor
admits a strength at all when for some reason there is only one candidate to check.
Conversely, existence results make it easier to construct strengths for
functors.
We supply uniqueness and existence results for strengths in this
section.

We first define a notion of
functional completeness
for an
action, which guarantees both uniqueness and existence of strengths.
2

2

This notion is similar in spirit to functional completeness in

In categorical logic,
\mathbf{V}

for any
\mathbf{V}[\Gamma]
-morphism

For each morphism

Definition 3.5

Proposition 3.6

If

Example 3.7
.

The category
\mathbf{Set}
with the Cartesian product is functionally

have unique strengths.
More generally, if
\mathbf{D}

\mathbf{Set}

\mathbf{D}

In this case, points

Functional completeness thus says equivalently that, for every

Example 3.8
.

In contrast, the Cartesian product of pointed sets is not functionally
complete as an action of
(\mathbf{Set}_{\star},\mathbf{1},\times)
on itself: every
\Gamma

has only one point, so the morphisms

The Cartesian product of posets also fails to be functionally complete as an action of
(\mathbf{Poset},\mathbf{1},\times)
on itself: the morphisms

f:\Gamma\times X\to Y

may fail to be
monotone.

There are few examples of functionally complete actions.
We break down the notion of functional completeness into

well-pointedness
, which guarantees uniqueness of strength, and
the existence of a
weak functional completeness structure
, which
guarantees existence of a canonical strength for each functor.
Both of these have more examples.

Definition 3.9
.

The action
\triangleright_{\mathbf{D}}

\llparenthesis{-}\rrparenthesis

If the action is a monoidal category acting on itself,
well-pointedness in our sense is equivalent to Abramsky and Heunen’s

(monoidal) well-pointedness

2
]

According to their
definition, a monoidal category

is well-pointed if two morphisms

f\circ(\xi\otimes\xi^{\prime})\circ\rho_{I}=g\circ(\xi\otimes\xi^{\prime})\circ\rho_{I}

for all
\xi:I\to X

\xi^{\prime}:I\to X^{\prime}

For a Cartesian monoidal category acting on itself, our

to a terminal object (two morphisms

A similar simplification is possible when each

is well-pointed.

Functional completeness is a strictly stronger property than
well-pointedness; the latter implies that strengths are unique if they
exist, but does not guarantee existence.

Suppose that

Example 3.11
.

The smash product
\otimes
of pointed sets is a well-pointed action of

(\mathbf{Set}_{\star},I,\otimes)
on
\mathbf{Set}_{\star}
.
In this case, the unit

is the two-element pointed set

to

are in bijection with elements of

\Gamma

\llparenthesis f\rrparenthesis=\zeta
.
In contrast, the Cartesian product of pointed sets is not

\Gamma
has only one point.

The Cartesian product of posets is a well-pointed action of

(\mathbf{Poset},\mathbf{1},\times)
on itself, again because points of
\Gamma
are
in bijection with elements of
\Gamma
.
If

is an endofunctor on

would

This property enables us to show that some functors have no strength.
For example, the functor

that

to the discrete poset

has no

is not a monotone function

For existence of strengths, we introduce the following.

Definition 3.12
.

A

for

is

-morphism

Proposition 3.13

Let

, there is a strong functor

such that

Weak functional completeness does not necessarily deliver the
canonical strengths for the identity functor and the composition of

Example 3.14
.

We construct a weak functional completeness structure for

(\mathbf{Set}_{\star},\mathbf{1},\times)
acting on itself.
Every
\Gamma
has exactly one point

, so to give a function

of pointed sets.
We can therefore define the morphism
\Phi_{\Gamma}\zeta:\Gamma\times X\to Y

by
\Phi_{\Gamma}\zeta(\gamma,x)=\zeta(\star)(x)
.
This is in fact the only possible

in this case, even though the action fails to be well-pointed.
By

on pointed sets forms a strong

\hat{F}^{(\Gamma)}f\,(\gamma,t)=F(\lambda x.\,f(\star,x))\,t

\mathrm{str}_{\Gamma,X}(\gamma,t)=F(\lambda x.\,(\star,x))\,t

There may in general be other strengths for

For example, the identity functor on

also has the canonical strength

\mathrm{id}_{\Gamma\times X}:\Gamma\times X\to\Gamma\times X
.

Example 3.15
.

We give an example of an action that has multiple weak functional
completeness structures.
Fix a set

, and let
\mathbf{D}
be the Kleisli category of the monad

{-}+E
on
\mathbf{Set}

This category is coCartesian, as is the Kleisli category of any monad on any coCartesian category.
It therefore forms a monoidal category

Every
\Gamma
has exactly one point

For each

We note that, if

Functional completeness is equivalent to the latter being an

We now turn to strong monads.
There is a richer collection of equivalent definitions of strong monad

Because of our focus on semantics, the primary definition we use asks

Again we work in the action-based setting, so we suppose a monoidal

4.2
Uniqueness and existence of strengths for monads

The situation for uniqueness of strengths carries over immediately from
functors (

Proposition 4.7

Suppose that
\mathsf{T}=(T,\eta,\mu)

Existence of strengths for monads is more problematic.

5
Enrichment

So far, we have considered strength only from the perspective of actions
of the monoidal category
\mathbf{V}
.
A well-known result of Kock
[
16
]
is that, in a certain situation,
strong functors are the same as enriched functors.
More precisely, Kock shows that if
\mathbf{V}
is a monoidal category
that is (left) closed in the sense that each
{-}\otimes X:\mathbf{V}\to\mathbf{V}

has a right adjoint
X\multimap{-}:\mathbf{V}\to\mathbf{V}

\Gamma\otimes FX\to F(\Gamma\otimes X)
for an endofunctor

F:\mathbf{V}\to\mathbf{V}
are in bijection with
suitable natural transformations
X\multimap Y\to FX\multimap FY
.
The latter make
F
into an enriched functor
\mathbf{V}\to\mathbf{V}
(in the sense
of enriched category theory
[
12
]
), where
\mathbf{V}
enriches
over itself using the closed structure.

It is less well-known that this connection between enrichment and
strength holds more generally.
If
\mathbf{C}
and
\mathbf{D}
are any categories that enrich over
\mathbf{V}
, and
suitable adjoints exist, then enriched functors
\mathbf{C}\to\mathbf{D}
are the same
as strong functors
\mathbf{C}\to\mathbf{D}
.
There are similar bijections for natural transformations, and for
monads.
Strength and enrichment are therefore just two perspectives on the same
structure.
In particular, facts from enriched category theory can be transferred
along these bijections to become facts about strength.

We give the precise connection between strength and enrichment in this
section, again working with an general monoidal category

\mathbf{V}

symmetry.

7
Conclusion

We have shown and commented on a number of different equivalent
definitions of strong functor and of strong monad, and explained how and
why they arise. These definitions differ significantly in the amount
of data and the equations they involve, and they serve different
applications. We presented some sufficient conditions for uniqueness
and existence of strengths for all functors, in particular the
condition of weak functional completeness, which is new as far as we
know, and some examples of absence and multiplicity of strengths,
which we crafted to demonstrate that these conditions are not
necessary.

There are some questions we could not settle; for example, we could
neither find a Cartesian category with multiple weak functional
completeness structures nor show that there is none. We would
like to identify interesting examples of unique existence, absence and
multiplicity of strengths for non-symmetric monoidal categories and
non-self-actions.

A finer analysis of strength could proceed from a generally
non-symmetric non-monoidal closed category
\mathbf{V}
, this being the
minimal structure needed for self-enrichment of
\mathbf{V}
. A further
possible direction of refinement would be to work with skew
monoidal/closed categories and actions,
cf.
[
31
]
. There are no immediate indications of
obstacles, but we would also like to find interesting applications of
this level of generality.

\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\alpha_{\Gamma,\Gamma^{\prime},X}\triangleleft\Delta}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\alpha_{\Gamma,\Gamma^{\prime},X\triangleleft\Delta}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\alpha_{\Gamma,\Gamma^{\prime}\triangleright X,\Delta}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Gamma\triangleright\alpha_{\Gamma^{\prime},X,\Delta}}

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces((\Gamma\triangleright X)\triangleleft\Delta)\triangleleft\Delta^{\prime}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces(\Gamma\triangleright(X\triangleleft\Delta))\triangleleft\Delta^{\prime}}

\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\alpha_{\Gamma,X,\Delta}\triangleleft\Delta^{\prime}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\alpha_{\Gamma\triangleright X,\Delta,\Delta^{\prime}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\alpha_{\Gamma,X\triangleleft\Delta,\Delta^{\prime}}}

An example is
\mathbf{C}=\mathbf{V}
and
\triangleright=\triangleleft=\otimes
.

In a biaction situation, if a functor has both a left strength and a
right strength, these can be required to cohere as follows.

Definition A.3
.

Suppose a biaction of a monoidal category
\mathbf{V}
on a category
\mathbf{D}
. A

bistrength
for a functor
F:\mathbf{C}\to\mathbf{D}
is a pair of a left
strength

\mathrm{str}_{\Gamma,X}:\Gamma\triangleright_{\mathbf{D}}FX\to F(\Gamma\triangleright_{\mathbf{C}}X)
and a
right strength

\mathrm{str}^{\mathrm{R}}_{X,\Delta}:FX\triangleleft_{\mathbf{D}}\Delta\to F(X\triangleleft_{\mathbf{C}}\Delta)
such
that

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{(\Gamma\triangleright_{\mathbf{D}}FX)\triangleleft_{\mathbf{D}}\Delta}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{F(\Gamma\triangleright_{\mathbf{C}}X)\triangleleft_{\mathbf{D}}\Delta}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{F((\Gamma\triangleright_{\mathbf{C}}X)\triangleleft_{\mathbf{C}}\Delta)}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\Gamma\triangleright_{\mathbf{D}}(FX\triangleleft_{\mathbf{D}}\Delta)}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\Gamma\triangleright_{\mathbf{D}}F(X\triangleleft_{\mathbf{C}}\Delta)}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{F(\Gamma\triangleright_{\mathbf{C}}(X\triangleleft_{\mathbf{C}}\Delta))}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Gamma\triangleright_{\mathbf{D}}\mathrm{str}^{\mathrm{R}}_{X,\Delta}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathrm{str}_{\Gamma,X\triangleleft_{\mathbf{C}}\Delta}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\alpha_{\Gamma,FX,\Delta}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathrm{str}_{\Gamma,X}\triangleleft_{\mathbf{D}}\Delta}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathrm{str}^{\mathrm{R}}_{\Gamma\triangleright_{\mathbf{C}}X,\Delta}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces F\alpha_{\Gamma,X,\Delta}}

A natural transformation between two bistrong functors is bistrong
if it is both left strong and right strong.

Consider the case
\mathbf{C}=\mathbf{D}=\mathbf{V}
and
\triangleright=\triangleleft=\otimes
. If

\mathbf{V}
is symmetric, with braiding
c_{X,Y}:X\otimes Y\to Y\otimes X
, then any left strength
\mathrm{str}
of a functor
F

induces a right strength
\mathrm{str}^{\mathrm{R}}
via

\mathrm{str}^{\mathrm{R}}_{X,\Delta}=Fc_{\Delta,X}\circ\mathrm{str}_{\Delta,X}\circ c_{FX,\Delta}
.
The two strengths together form a bistrength.
But the right strength does not have to be related to the left
strength like this, not even when
\mathbf{V}
is a Cartesian category. For
example, take
\mathbf{V}
to be the category of pointed sets with its
Cartesian structure. The identity functor is bistrong with

\mathrm{str}_{\Gamma,X}(\gamma,x)=(\gamma,x)
and

\mathrm{str}^{\mathrm{R}}_{X,\Delta}(x,\delta)=(x,\star)
.

A.2
Commutative monads

Kock
[
13
]
studied what he named commutative monads for the case of a
symmetric monoidal category. His commutative monads were left-strong
monads subject to an additional equational condition.

Symmetry is in fact not needed. The concept of commutative monad makes
sense for a general monoidal category
\mathbf{V}
; Kock’s condition can be
formulated for any bistrength for the tensor as a biaction (where the
right strength need not in general be defined in terms of the left
strength like we did above).

Definition A.4
.

Suppose a monoidal category
(\mathbf{V},I,\otimes)
. A
commutative
monad
is a monad
\mathsf{T}=(T,\eta,\mu)
with a bistrength

(\mathrm{str},\mathrm{str}^{\mathrm{R}})
of
T
(wrt.
\otimes
as a biaction of
\mathbf{V}
on
itself) such that
\eta
,
\mu
are bistrong and moreover the
following diagram commutes:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces TX\otimes TY}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces T(TX\otimes Y)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces T(T(X\otimes Y))}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces T(X\otimes TY)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces T(T(X\otimes Y))}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces T(X\otimes Y)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathrm{str}_{TX,Y}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathrm{str}^{\mathrm{R}}_{X,TY}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces T\mathrm{str}^{\mathrm{R}}_{X,Y}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mu_{X\otimes Y}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces T\mathrm{str}_{X,Y}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mu_{X\otimes Y}}

Commutative monads in this sense are exactly the same as lax
monoidal monads.
Even when
\mathbf{V}
is symmetric, the bistrength of a commutative monad does
not need to be defined by symmetry, so this notion of commutative monad
(i.e. lax monoidal monad) is strictly more general than
Kock’s.
For example, consider the writer monad
\mathsf{Wr}_{\mathsf{M}}
on
\mathbf{Act}\,\mathsf{M}
from

Example

4.4
, where
\mathsf{M}
is any commutative monoid.
From the two strengths given there, we can make a bistrength

\mathrm{str}_{\Gamma,X}(\gamma,(x,m))=((\gamma,x),m)\qquad\mathrm{str}^{\mathrm{R}}_{X,\Delta}((x,m),\delta)=((x,\delta*m),m)

and
\mathsf{Wr}_{\mathsf{M}}
equipped with this bistrength is a commutative monad.
Kock’s commutative monads are the same as symmetric lax monoidal monads.
