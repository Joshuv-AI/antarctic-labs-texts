We consider a closed Riemannian manifold
(M^{n},g)
,
n\geq 3
. Let
\Delta=\Delta_{g}=div_{g}\nabla

(non-positive)

The

Paneitz type equations appeared in Riemannian geometry in the study of the
Q
-curvature in dimensions greater than 4.
Important results on the critical case were obtained by Z. Djadli, E. Hebey and M. Ledoux in
[
10
]
.

The
Q
-curvature of a Riemannian manifold was introduced by S. Paneitz
[
32
]
and T. Branson
[
4
]

in their study of conformally invariant operators. It is defined for Riemannian manifolds
(\mathbf{M},\mathfrak{g})

of dimension
N\geq 3
by

(1.2)

Q_{\mathfrak{g}}:=-\frac{1}{2(N-1)}\Delta_{\mathfrak{g}}\operatorname{scal}_{\mathfrak{g}}-\frac{2}{(N-2)^{2}}\left|\mathrm{Ric}_{\mathfrak{g}}\right|^{2}+\frac{N^{3}-4N^{2}+16N-16}{8(N-1)^{2}(N-2)^{2}}\operatorname{scal}_{\mathfrak{g}}^{2}.

where
\operatorname{scal}_{\mathfrak{g}}
is
the scalar curvature and
\mathrm{Ric}_{\mathfrak{g}}
is the Ricci tensor.

The treatment in the case when the dimension is 3 or 4 is different, so we will assume in the rest of this discussion that the dimension
is
N\geq 5
.

Closely related to the
Q
-curvature is the Paneitz operator
P_{\mathfrak{g}}
, defined using a local
\mathfrak{g}
-orthonormal frame

\left(e_{i}\right)_{i=1}^{n}
, as

(1.3)

P_{\mathfrak{g}}\psi:=\Delta_{\mathfrak{g}}^{2}\psi+\frac{4}{N-2}\mathrm{div}_{\mathfrak{g}}\left(\mathrm{Ric}_{\mathfrak{g}}\left(\nabla\psi,e_{i}\right)e_{i}\right)-\frac{N^{2}-4N+8}{2(N-1)(N-2)}\mathrm{div}_{\mathfrak{g}}\left(\operatorname{scal}_{\mathfrak{g}}\nabla\psi\right)+\frac{N-4}{2}Q_{\mathfrak{g}}\psi,

where
\mathrm{div}_{\mathfrak{g}}
is the divergence.

We write a conformal metric
\mathfrak{g}\in\left[\mathfrak{g}_{0}\right]
as
\mathfrak{g}=u^{\frac{4}{N-4}}\mathfrak{g}_{0}
, for a positive function
u:\mathbf{M}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
.
The Paneitz operator is conformally invariant in the sense that for any
\psi:\mathbf{M}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
,

P_{\mathfrak{g}}\psi=u^{-\frac{N+4}{N-4}}P_{\mathfrak{g}_{0}}(u\psi).

It follows that we can express the
Q
-curvature of
\mathfrak{g}
in terms of
\mathfrak{g}_{0}
and
u
by:

Q_{\mathfrak{g}}=\frac{2}{N-4}P_{\mathfrak{g}}(1)=\frac{2}{N-4}u^{-\frac{N+4}{N-4}}P_{\mathfrak{g}_{0}}(u).

There has been great interest in studying the problem of finding metrics of constant
Q
-curvature in a given conformal class.
In the 4-dimensional case important results were obtained for instance by S-Y. A. Chang and P. C. Yang
[

by S. Brendle
[

[

Note that it follows from the previous comments that finding a conformal metric

The problem of the existence of metrics of constant

-curvature in dimension

[

]
, by E. Hebey and F. Robert in

]
,

. See also

Note that Einstein metrics have constant

-curvature.
Jérôme Vétois
[

proved that if

is an Einstein metric with positive scalar curvature,
different from the constant curvature metric on the sphere, then it is the only metric of constant

Conformal diffeomorphisms of the round sphere,
(\mathbb{S}^{N},\mathfrak{g}_{0}
), give a non-compact family of solutions to the Paneitz-Branson equation.
But all the conformal metrics are actually isometric. The compactness of the space of positive solutions of the Paneitz-Branson equation
was studied for instance in
[
20
,
24
,
25
,
28
]
. High dimensional examples for which the space of solution is not compact

[

There are a few other cases where one can show the non-uniqueness of conformal metrics of constant
Q
-curvature.
One case, is that of Riemannian products.
If
(M,g)
and
(X,h)
are closed Einstein manifolds with positive Einstein constant, of dimension at least
3
then
for any
\delta>0
, the Riemannian product
(M\times X,g+\delta h)
has constant positive
Q
-curvature
and constant positive scalar curvature.
It is easy to compute that as

or
\delta\rightarrow\infty
the

of the

goes to
\infty
.
We are in the conditions of a result proved in
[
19
]
, which says that in the conformal class of

, there must be at least one other metric of constant
Q
-curvature, with uniformly bounded energy.

R. Bettiol, P. Piccione and Y. Sire
[
3

considered appropriate Riemannian submersions and reduce the equation to basic functions (functions which are constant along the fibers of the submersion).
Using bifurcation theory for this reduced equation on the canonical variation obtained by varying the size of the fibers
(under certain conditions the total spaces of these variations have constant Q-curvature), they prove the existence of bifurcation instants, giving the existence of metrics in the family for which there are other conformal metrics of constant
Q
-curvature.

In the present article we will give multiplicity results for conformal constant

Let (

Recall that a function

Isoparametric functions on general Riemannian manifolds were first considered by Q-M. Wang in

We will recall now a few known results about isoparametric functions. We are interested in writing the
Paneitz-type Equation (

which amounts to compute the Laplacian on such functions. Explicit details were shown in
[
2

,
where the reader can find appropriate references.

Given an isoparametric function, with the corresponding functions
a
and

it is known that the only zeroes of the function

are

and

, which
means that the only critical values of

are its minimum and its

[
38

[

that the critical

and

of

be the dimension of

Let

It is easy to see (see

and, in particular,

We will consider functions which are constant on the level sets of
F

A function

The function

The classical and important case of the round sphere can be treated explicitly.
A Cartan-Münzner polynomial
P

of degree
k
on
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
is a polynomial, of degree
k
, which verifies

\langle\nabla P,\nabla P\rangle=k^{2}\|x\|^{2k-2}

\Delta P=\frac{1}{2}ck^{2}\|x\|^{k-2},

which are called the Cartan-Münzner equations.
Then the restriction to the sphere,
p=P_{|_{S^{n}}}
, verifies the isoparametric conditions

\|\nabla p\|^{2}=b\circ p
,
\Delta p=a\circ p
, with

b(t)=k^{2}(1-t^{2})
,
a(t)=-k(n+k-1)t+(1/2)ck^{2}
.
It follows that
p:S^{n}\rightarrow[0,1]
.
The constant
c
depends on the geometry,
the principal curvatures, of the level surfaces
p^{-1}(t)
,
t\in(0,1)
.

Every isoparametric function on the sphere
S^{n}
is
given (up to reparametrization) by the restriction of a Cartan-Münzner polynomial on
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
.

Note that in this case

\frac{-b^{\prime}+2a}{2\sqrt{b}}(t)=\frac{-2k(n-1)t+ck^{2}}{2k\sqrt{1-t^{2}}}.

We obtain that

{\bf d}(x)=\int_{-1}^{f(x)}\frac{1}{\sqrt{b(s)}}ds=\frac{1}{k}\left(\arcsin(f(x))+\frac{\pi}{2}\right),\ \ \ D=\frac{\pi}{k}

and then, with
t={\bf d}(x)
so
f(x)=\sin(kt-\pi/2)=-\cos(kt)
, we have

h(t)=\frac{-b^{\prime}+2a}{2\sqrt{b}}(-\cos(kt))=(n-1)\frac{\cos(kt)}{\sin(kt)}+\frac{ck}{2\sin(kt)}.

which is invariant around
D/2=\pi/2k
if and only if
c=0
.

The classification of isoparametric functions on spheres was only recently completed by Q-S Chi in
[
8
]
, concluding the work
started by E. Cartan and others around 1930.
It is known,
[
29
,
30
]
, that
k
can only take the values 1, 2, 3, 4 and 6.
The case
k=1
corresponds to linear functions, which satisfy the
Cartan-Münzner equations with
c=0
. For all Cartan-Münzner polynomials of degree 3 and 6 we also have
c=0
. The case

k=2
corresponds to polynomials invariant by an action of
O(l)\times O(j)
, with
l+j=n
: in this case

c=0
if and only if
j=l
. In the case
k=4
there are also examples with
c=0
, and examples with
c\neq 0
. Detailed
discussions on isoparametric functions on spheres can be found for instance in
[
21
]
.

We consider now the case of the 3-sphere,
S^{3}
. Recall that the eigenvalues of the positive Laplacian on

are

\mu_{i}=-i(i+2)
,

In this case there are 2 types of isoparametric functions. The first case are
the isoparametric functions invariant by an action of
O(3)
, fixing an axis in
\mathbb{R}^{4}
. These correspond to the
Cartan-Münzner polynomial of degree one, the linear functions. The eigenvalues of the Laplacian on functions
invariant in this case are the same as the eigenvalues of the Laplacian,
\lambda_{i}=\mu_{i}
. For the
Paneitz-type Equation
1.1
for invariant functions, the function

is

, and
the equation is defined on
[0,\pi]
.

The second case are functions invariant by an isometric action by
O(2)\times O(2)
on
\mathbb{R}^{4}
. In this case
k=2
, the
corresponding Cartan-Münzner polynomial is
P(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4})=x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}-x_{3}^{2}-x_{4}^{2}
. The eigenvalues
of the restricted Laplacian are
\lambda_{i}=\mu_{2i}
. The corresponding equation is defined in the interval
[0,\pi/2]
, with

h(t)=2\frac{\cos 2t}{\sin 2t}
.

3.

In this section we will study ordinary differential equation
1.5
, where we assume
that
\alpha,\beta>0
and
\alpha^{2}\geq 4\beta
,

h
will be defined in the open interval

We will assume that

. This implies in particular that also

. And in the limiting points

we assume that

\lim_{t\rightarrow 0}th(t)=j=-\lim_{t\rightarrow D}(D-t)h(t)

We begin with the following elementary observation:

Lemma 3.1
.

Under the previous hypothesis if
\varphi
solves Equation
1.5
then
\phi(t)=\varphi(D-t)
also solves Equation
1.5
.

We write
1.5
as a first order system:

v_{0}^{\prime}=v_{1}

v_{1}^{\prime}=v_{2}-hv_{1}

v_{2}^{\prime}=v_{3}+\alpha v_{1}

v_{3}^{\prime}=\beta(v^{q}_{0}-v_{0})-hv_{3}.

In this system
v_{0}
is the solution of Equation
1.5
.
Note that
v_{2}=v_{0}^{\prime\prime}+hv_{0}^{\prime}
, and
v_{3}=v_{1}^{\prime\prime}+h^{\prime}v_{1}+hv_{1}^{\prime}-\alpha v_{1}=v_{0}^{\prime\prime\prime}+hv_{0}^{\prime\prime}+h^{\prime}v_{0}^{\prime}-\alpha v_{0}^{\prime}
. The system is similar to
[
22
, (2.2)]
, but it is not quasimonotone.

Remark 3.2
.

We assume that
\alpha,\beta>0
and that
\alpha^{2}\geq 4\beta
. This implies that there exists
c,d>0
such that

c.d=\beta
and
c+d=\alpha
. Explicitly,
c=(1/2)(\alpha+\sqrt{\alpha^{2}-4\beta})
and
d=(1/2)(\alpha-\sqrt{\alpha^{2}-4\beta})
.

Remark 3.3
.

It follows easily from the system that a solution which is continuous and defined at
0
must verify that
v_{1}(0)=v_{3}(0)=0
. Also
by the conditions we are assuming on

we have that
\lim_{t\rightarrow 0}v_{1}(t)h(t)=jv_{1}^{\prime}(0)
. It follows that

v_{2}(0)=(1+j)v_{1}^{\prime}(0)
. Similarly
(1+j)v_{3}^{\prime}(0)=\beta(v^{q}_{0}-v_{0})(0)
. In the same way we have that
v_{2}(D)=(1+j)v_{1}^{\prime}(D)
and
(1+j)v_{3}^{\prime}(D)=\beta(v_{0}^{q}-v_{0})(D)
.

Remark 3.4
.

Given any pair
(a,b)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
there exists a unique solution of the system with initial conditions
v_{0}(0)=a
,

v_{2}(0)=b
,
v_{1}(0)=v_{3}(0)=0
. The solutions are
C^{1}
, and then it follows that

v_{i}
is
C^{5-i}
. In particular
v_{0}
is

, and a standard solution of Equation
1.5

depend

.

Definition 3.5

A solution

v_{1}(0)=v_{3}(0)=v_{1}(D)=v_{3}(D)=0

Then we have that if

\beta\int_{0}^{D}(v_{0}^{q}(t)-v_{0}(t)H(t)dt=\int_{0}^{D}(v_{3}^{\prime}(t)+h(t)v_{3}(t))H(t)dt=\int_{0}^{D}(Hv_{3})^{\prime}(t)dt=0.

We consider the Paneitz-type operator

In this section we shall study positive solutions of the Paneitz-type equation

The proof of the next lemma follows the argument in

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
u
be a positive function solving

P_{(\alpha,\beta)}u=u^{q}
. Assume that

(4.1)

q\|u\|^{q-1}_{\infty}<\mu_{1}(0),

where
\mu_{1}(0)
is the first positive eigenvalue of the Bilaplacian :

\mu_{1}(0)=\inf_{\{u\neq 0:\int u=0\}}\dfrac{\int(\Delta u)^{2}}{\int u^{2}}.

Then
u
is constant.

Proof.

We have that

\mu_{1}(0)=\inf_{\int u=0}\dfrac{\int(\Delta u)^{2}}{\int u^{2}}\leq\mu_{1}(\alpha)=\inf_{\int u=0}\dfrac{\int(\Delta u)^{2}+\alpha\int|\nabla u|^{2}}{\int u^{2}}.

Now consider the average
\overline{u}
of the positive solution
u
, defined by

\overline{u}:=\dfrac{\int_{M}u}{vol(M,g)}.

We get

\displaystyle\mu_{1}(0)\int(u-\overline{u})^{2}\leq

\displaystyle\int(\Delta(u-\overline{u}))^{2}+\alpha\int|\nabla(u-\overline{u})|^{2}

\displaystyle=\int(\Delta^{2}(u-\overline{u})-\alpha\Delta(u-\overline{u}))\ (u-\overline{u})

\displaystyle=\int(\Delta^{2}u-\alpha\Delta u)(u-\overline{u})

\displaystyle=\int-\beta u(u-\overline{u})+\int u^{q}(u-\overline{u})

\displaystyle=-\beta\int(u-\overline{u})(u-\overline{u})+\int(u^{q}-\overline{u}^{q})(u-\overline{u}).

In last equality we use that

\int\overline{u}(u-\overline{u})=\int\overline{u}^{q}(u-\overline{u})=0.

On the other hand, applying the mean value theorem to the function
T(x)=x^{q}
we obtain that

(u^{q}-\overline{u}^{q})(u-\overline{u})\leq q\|u\|_{\infty}^{q-1}(u-\overline{u})(u-\overline{u})
. Then

\int(u^{q}-\overline{u}^{q})(u-\overline{u})\leq q\|u_{i}\|_{\infty}^{q-1}\int(u-\overline{u})^{2}.

Combining the previous inequalities, we have

\mu_{1}(0)\int(u-\overline{u})^{2}\leq-\beta\int(u-\overline{u})^{2}+q\|u\|_{\infty}^{q-1}\int(u-\overline{u})^{2}.

By condition
4.1

\int(u-\overline{u})^{2}=0.

Therefore

is constant.

5.
Bifurcation

We consider a closed Riemannian manifold
(M,g)
with a proper isoparametric function

F:M\rightarrow[t_{0},t_{1}]
. As in Section 2, we consider the focal submanifolds
M_{0}=F^{-1}(t_{0})

and
M_{1}=F^{-1}(t_{1})
.
We let
{\bf d}:M\rightarrow[0,D]
be the distance function to
M_{0}
,
D=d_{g}(M_{0},M_{1})
.
We let
\mathcal{B}_{4}=\{\phi\in C^{4,\alpha}([0,D]):\phi^{\prime}(0)=\phi^{\prime\prime\prime}(0)=0=\phi^{\prime}(D)=\phi^{\prime\prime\prime}(D)\}
,
which is identified with the
C^{4,\alpha}
functions on
M
which are
F
-invariant. We let
h(t)
be the mean curvature of

{\bf d}^{-1}(t)
.

Consider smooth, positive, increasing functions
{\bf{a}},{\bf b}:(0,\infty)\rightarrow(0,\infty)
. We will assume that

{\bf a}^{2}(t)>4{\bf b}(t)
for all
t\in(0,\infty)
,

\lim_{t\rightarrow 0}{\bf a}(t)=\lim_{t\rightarrow 0}{\bf b}(t)=0
and

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}{\bf a}(t)=\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}{\bf b}(t)=\infty
.

Then we define the map
H:\mathcal{B}_{4}\times(0,\infty)\rightarrow C^{0,\alpha}([0,D]
, by

H(u,t)=u^{\prime\prime\prime\prime}+2hu^{\prime\prime\prime}+(2h^{\prime}+h^{2}-{\bf a}(t))u^{\prime\prime}+(h^{\prime\prime}+hh^{\prime}-{\bf a}(t)h)u^{\prime}+{\bf b}(t)(u-u^{q}).

We look for solutions of the equation
H(u,t)=0
with
u>0
. We call
(1,t)
,
t\in(0,\infty)
, the
family of
trivial solutions
. A solution
(u,t)
with
u\neq 1
is called
nontrivial
.

The linearization of the equation
H(u,t)=0
at the constant solution
(1,t)
is given by

(5.1)

D_{u}H(1,t)[v]=v^{\prime\prime\prime\prime}+2hv^{\prime\prime\prime}+(2h^{\prime}+h^{2}-{\bf a}(t))v^{\prime\prime}+(h^{\prime\prime}+hh^{\prime}-{\bf a}(t)h)v^{\prime}+{\bf b}(t)(1-q)v=0.

Let
L:\mathcal{B}_{2}\rightarrow C^{0,\alpha}([0,D]
be given by

L(u)=u^{\prime\prime}+hu^{\prime}
. The linearized equation at
(1,t)
is
L^{2}(v)-{\bf a}(t)L(v)+{\bf b}(t)(1-q)v=0
, which is
the eigenvalue equation for
L^{2}-{\bf a}(t)L
.

The space of solutions of the eigenvalue problem
L(v)=\lambda v
has at most dimension 1, since
it is a second order equation, and solutions must verify
v^{\prime}(0)=0
. Sturm Liouville theory says that
L
has
infinite eigenvalues

0=\lambda_{0}>\lambda_{1}>\lambda_{2}...\rightarrow-\infty
. Moreover, if
\varphi_{0}=1,\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2},...
are corresponding
nonzero eigenfunctions,
L(\varphi_{i})=\lambda_{i}\varphi_{i}
, then
\varphi_{i}
has exactly
i
zeroes in
(0,D)
.
Then we have that
\{\varphi_{i}\}
is a complete set of eigenfunctions for
L^{2}-{\bf a}(t)L
, with corresponding
eigenvalues
\mu_{i}=\lambda_{i}^{2}-{\bf a}(t)\lambda_{i}
.

Therefore the linearized equation (at
(1,t)
) has a solution if and only if for some
i
we have that

\lambda_{i}^{2}-{\bf a}(t)\lambda_{i}={\bf b}(t)(q-1)>0
. We must have

\lambda_{i}=(1/2)({\bf a}(t)\pm\sqrt{{\bf a}^{2}(t)+4{\bf b}(t)(q-1)}
.
And since
\lambda_{i}
must be nonpositive we must
have
\lambda_{i}=(1/2)({\bf a}(t)-\sqrt{{\bf a}^{2}(t)+4{\bf b}(t)(q-1)}=\phi(t)
.

To apply bicurcation theory we would like that

\phi
is a decreasing function,
\lim_{t\rightarrow 0}\phi(t)=0
, and
\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\phi(t)=-\infty
.
Under these conditions for each
i\geq 1
there exists a unique
t_{i}=t_{i}({\bf a},{\bf b})>0
such that
\lambda_{i}=\phi(t_{i})
.

We will study bifurcation from the family of trivial solutions
t\mapsto(1,t)
. The point
(1,t^{*})
is called a

bifurcation point
for the family if it is in the closure of the space of nontrivial solutions.

Note that

D^{2}_{u,t}H(1,t_{i})[\varphi_{i}]=-{\bf a}^{\prime}(t_{i})L(\varphi_{i})+{\bf b}^{\prime}(t_{i})(1-q)\varphi_{i}.

Then

\int D^{2}_{u,t}H(1,t_{i})[\varphi_{i}]\ \varphi_{i}=\left(-{\bf a}^{\prime}(t_{i})\lambda_{i}+{\bf b}^{\prime}(t_{i})(1-q)\right)\int\varphi_{i}^{2}.

Since
T:=D_{u}H(1,t_{i})[\cdot]
is self-adjoint and
ker(T)=span(\varphi_{i})
the last computation implies that
if
-{\bf a}^{\prime}(t_{i})\lambda_{i}+{\bf b}^{\prime}(t_{i})(1-q)\neq 0
then

D^{2}_{u,t}H(1,t_{i})[\varphi_{i}]\notin Range(T).

Then the following result follows from the previous discussion and the classical Local Bifurcation Theorem for simple eigenvalues of M. G. Crandall and P. Rabinowitz

[
9
]
:

Proposition 5.1
.

Assume that
{\bf a}^{2}(t)>4{\bf b}(t)
for all

,

\lim_{t\rightarrow 0}{\bf a}(t)=\lim_{t\rightarrow 0}{\bf b}(t)=0
and

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}{\bf a}(t)=\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}{\bf b}(t)=\infty

\phi(t)=(1/2)({\bf a}(t)-\sqrt{{\bf a}^{2}(t)+4{\bf b}(t)(q-1)}

\lim_{t\rightarrow 0}\phi(t)=0
, and
\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\phi(t)=-\infty

be the values such that

.
Assume that for each positive integer

-{\bf a}^{\prime}(t_{i})\lambda_{i}+{\bf b}^{\prime}(t_{i})(1-q)\neq 0
.
Then
\{(1,t_{i})\}
is the set of

H(u,t)=0
at the family of trivial solutions
\{(1,t)\}
. These bifurcation points are simple,
\varphi_{i}
is a solution of the
linearized equation at
(1,t_{i})
. The set of nontrivial solutions around this bifurcation point is given by a path

with

for

The proof is a direct application of the classical theorem of Crandall and Rabinowitz.

We have now all the elements needed to prove our main global bifurcation result:

Theorem 5.2

Assume the conditions of the previous proposition.

be the subset of solutions which have exactly
i+1
critical points. Note that non-constant solutions close to the bifurcation
point
(1,t_{i})
are in
S_{i}^{0}
. It follows from Lemma
3.15
that all the critical points of solutions are
nondegenerate and this implies that
S_{i}^{0}
is open in
S_{i}
.
It also follows from Proposition
5.1
that if
j\neq i
then
(1,t_{j})\notin\overline{S_{i}^{0}}
.
It follows from Theorem
3.17
that

S_{i}^{0}\cup\{(1,t_{i})\}
is closed in
S_{i}
and therefore
S_{i}=S_{i}^{0}\cup\{(1,t_{i})\}
. Then all solutions on
S_{i}

have exactly
i+1
critical points. This implies that if
i\neq j
we have that
S_{i}\cap S_{j}=\emptyset
.
Then we know from the Global Bifurcation Theorem of P. Rabinowitz
[
34
]
that
S_{i}
is not compact.
It follows from Theorem
4.2
, that there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that
S_{i}\subset C^{4,\alpha}[0,D]\times[\varepsilon,\infty)
. If there exists

4.3
that
S_{i}
is compact. Therefore

S_{i}
intersects

for each

and the theorem follows.
