Apollonian carpets and the boundary of
the Kähler cone of a hyperkähler manifold

Abstract




Abstract

The ample cone of a compact Kähler
n
-manifold
M
is the
intersection of its Kähler cone and the
real subspace generated by integer (1,1)-classes.
Its isotropic boundary is the set of all points

\eta
on its boundary such that

\int_{M}\eta^{n}=0
. We are interested in the
relation between the shape of the isotropic
boundary of the ample cone of a hyperkähler
manifold and the dynamics of its holomorphic
automorphism group
G
. In this
case, the projectivization of the ample cone is
realized as an open, locally polyhedral subset
in a hyperbolic space
{\mathbb{H}}
.
The isotropic boundary
S
is realized as a subset of the hyperbolic
boundary (the absolute)
A
of
{\mathbb{H}}
, which is naturally identified
with a Euclidean sphere. It is clear that
the isotropic boundary
S
contains the
limit set of
G
acting on its ample cone.
We prove that, conversely, all irrational
points on
S
belong to the limit set.
Using a result of N. Shah about limiting
distributions of curves under geodesic
flow on hyperbolic manifolds, we prove that every real analytic curve in
S
is contained in a geodesic sphere in
S
,
and in presence of such curves the limit set is the closure of the union of these geodesic spheres. We study
the geometry of such fractal sets, called Apollonian
carpets, and establish a link
between the geometry of the Apollonian carpet and the
structure of the automorphism group.

1.1
Boundary of the ample cone of a hyperkähler
manifold

Throughout this paper, we
treat a hyperkähler manifold
as a complex manifold of Kähler type, admitting
a holomorphic symplectic form. Let
M
be a compact
hyperkähler manifold of maximal
holonomy (
2.1
; see
Subsection
2.1

for more details and references).
Then the second cohomology of
M
is equipped
with a non-degenerate, rational, bilinear
symmetric form
q
of signature
(3,b_{2}-3)
,
called
the Bogomolov–Beauville–Fujiki
form,
or the
BBF form
(Subsection
2.1
).
Complex automorphisms of
M
act on
H^{2}(M)

preserving
q
and the Hodge decomposition.
Denote by
\mathcal{K}_{M}
the Kähler cone, i.e. the set of all Kähler classes in
H^{1,1}(M)
,
and by
\mathcal{A}_{M}
the set of all ample classes in
\operatorname{\rm NS}_{\mathbb{R}}(M)=(H^{1,1}(M)\cap H^{2}(M,{\mathbb{Q}}))\otimes_{\mathbb{Q}}{\mathbb{R}}
.
This set is called
the ample cone of

M
; it is obtained as the intersection of
\operatorname{\rm NS}_{\mathbb{R}}

and the Kähler cone of
M
.

This paper grew out from an attempt to understand
the geometry of the boundary of
\mathcal{K}_{M}
and
\mathcal{A}_{M}
.
These cones were described as follows in

[
AV1
]
. Let
\mathcal{C}_{M}\subset H^{1,1}(M,{\mathbb{R}})

be the one of two connected components of the set of all vectors
with positive square that contains the Kähler cone,
and consider the set of so-called “MBM
classes”
H_{2}(M,{\mathbb{Z}})
(Subsection
2.2
). Denote by
R_{I}

the set of all effective MBM classes of type (1,1)
(that is, MBM classes represented by a linear
combination of complex curves with positive coefficients).
Then

\mathcal{K}_{M}=\{\eta\in\mathcal{C}_{M}\ \ |\ \ \ \forall u\in R_{I},\ \ q(\eta,u)>0\}

(1.1)

In other words, the Kähler cone is a subset of
\mathcal{C}_{M}

given by a system of linear inequalities. Its boundary
inside
\mathcal{C}_{M}
is locally polyhedral
(
[
AV2
]
). In this paper, we are
interested in the
isotropic boundary
of

\mathcal{K}_{M}
and
\mathcal{A}_{M}
, that is, the part of the boundary
contained in the set
\{\eta\in H^{1,1}(M,{\mathbb{R}})\ \ |\ \ q(\eta,\eta)=0\}.

We denote the isotropic boundary by
\partial\mathcal{K}_{M}
and
\partial\mathcal{A}_{M}
.

When the Picard rank of
M
is not maximal,
that is,
\operatorname{rk}(\operatorname{\rm NS})<b_{2}-2
, the isotropic boundary
contains an open subset in the cone of isotropic elements
\eta\in H^{1,1}(M)
,
as follows from

[
V2
, Proposition 3.4]
.
However,
\partial\mathcal{A}_{M}=\partial\mathcal{K}_{M}\cap\operatorname{\rm NS}_{\mathbb{R}}
is a complicated
fractal set, if
R_{I}
is non-empty and the Picard number of
M
is at least 3.
Further in this paper we relate the
geometry of this fractal with the geometric properties
of the manifold
M
and its automorphism group.

1.2
The limit set of the automorphism group

Dynamical properties
of the group
\operatorname{Aut}(M)
of holomorphic automorphisms
of a hyperkähler manifold
M
is closely related to
the dynamics of the group of isometries of the hyperbolic
space. We always use one of the standard models of the hyperbolic
space, identifying it with the projectivisation of
the cone of positive vectors in a quadratic vector space of signature

(1,n)
. We are mostly interested in the case when
the hyperbolic space is
{\mathbb{H}}:={\mathbb{P}}(\mathcal{C}_{M}\cap\operatorname{\rm NS}_{\mathbb{R}})
, when
M
is projective of Picard number at least 3.

The action of
\operatorname{Aut}(M)
on
H^{1,1}(M,{\mathbb{R}})

preserves the BBF form, giving a homomorphism

\operatorname{Aut}(M){\>\longrightarrow\>}O(1,b_{2}-3)
.

Any holomorphic automorphism of
M

which preserves a Kähler class
[\omega]\in\mathcal{K}_{M}
, acts on
M

by isometries of the corresponding Calabi-Yau metric.
Since the automorphism group of
M
is discrete,
the group of automorphisms preserving
[\omega]
is
finite, and
\operatorname{Aut}(M)
is mapped to
O(1,b_{2}-3)

with finite kernel. We are interested in the dynamics of
its image acting on the corresponding hyperbolic
space
{\mathbb{P}}\mathcal{C}_{M}
, and, even more, on a smaller hyperbolic
space,
{\mathbb{P}}(\mathcal{C}_{M}\cap NS_{\mathbb{R}})
.

Consider a quadratic vector space
(V,q)
of signature
(1,n)
,
and let
{\mathbb{H}}:={\mathbb{P}}(V^{+})
be the corresponding
hyperbolic space.
The
absolute
of
{\mathbb{H}}
is the set

\operatorname{Abs}:={\mathbb{P}}(\{\eta\in V\ \ |\ \ q(\eta,\eta)=0\})
,
identified with the Euclidean sphere.
Given a group
\Gamma\subset SO(1,n)
acting on

{\mathbb{H}}
, its
limit set
is the set of all
x\in\operatorname{Abs}

obtained as limit points of an orbit of
\Gamma
.

It turns out that
\partial\mathcal{A}_{M}
is closely related to
the limit set of the automorphism group.
We call a cohomology class

\eta\in\operatorname{\rm NS}_{\mathbb{R}}

irrational

if it is not proportional to
a rational cohomology class.

Theorem 1.1:
Let
M
be a hyperkähler manifold,
and
\operatorname{Aut}(M)
the group of its holomorphic
automorphisms.
Then all irrational points on the
isotropic boundary
\partial({\mathbb{P}}\operatorname{Amp})

belong to the limit set of

\operatorname{Aut}(M)
acting on
{\mathbb{P}}\operatorname{Amp}
.
Conversely, any point in the limit
set of
\operatorname{Aut}(M)
-action belongs to

\partial({\mathbb{P}}\operatorname{Amp})
.

Proof:
5.4
.

By
[
CNS
, Theorem 1.2.24]
,
all points of the limit set
\Lambda(\Gamma)

have dense orbits in
\Lambda(\Gamma)
, hence this
set is fractal. Its fractal geometry is the main subject
of this paper.

1.3
CHOPBs and Apollonian carpets

The group of automorphisms of a hyperkähler
manifold can be described explicitly in terms of
its MBM classes and Hodge structure, as shown in
[
Ma
]
,

[
AV2
]
and
[
AV4
]
.
Let
\operatorname{\sf Mon}\subset O(H^{2}(M,{\mathbb{Z}}),q)

be the group generated by the monodromy
of the Gauss-Manin local systems for
all complex deformations of
M
.
This group is called
the monodromy group

(Section
4
).
It has finite index in
O(H^{2}(M,{\mathbb{Z}}),q)

(
[
V1
]
).
The
Hodge monodromy group

\operatorname{\sf Mon}_{I}
is its subgroup which
preserves the Hodge decomposition
associated to a complex structure
I

of hyperkähler type. It is mapped
to
O(H^{2}(M,{\mathbb{Z}})\cap H^{1,1}(M))
with
finite kernel and finite index
(
4.1
).

Let
\Gamma
be the image of
\operatorname{\sf Mon}_{I}
in

O(H^{2}(M,{\mathbb{Z}})\cap H^{1,1}(M))
, and
\Gamma_{0}
a subgroup of
\Gamma

which preserves the Kähler cone.
As shown by Markman
[
Ma
]
,
the group
\operatorname{Aut}(M)
of automorphisms of a hyperkähler
manifold is a subgroup of
\operatorname{\sf Mon}_{I}

which preserves the Kähler cone.
Then the natural map
\operatorname{Aut}(M){\>\longrightarrow\>}\Gamma_{0}

has finite kernel and finite index.

Let
{\mathbb{H}}
be the hyperbolic space obtained
as the projectivisation of the positive cone
\operatorname{Pos}
in
\operatorname{\rm NS}_{\mathbb{R}}
.
Since
\Gamma
has finite index in
O(H^{2}(M,{\mathbb{Z}})\cap H^{1,1}(M))
,
the quotient
{\cal M}:={\mathbb{H}}/\Gamma
is a hyperbolic orbifold
of finite volume. The ample cone can be described as

\mathcal{A}_{M}=\{\eta\in\operatorname{Pos}\ \ |\ \ \ q(\eta,u)>0\ \ \forall u\in R_{I}\}

as follows from (
1.1
).
In fact,
\mathcal{A}_{M}
is a connected component of the complement in
\operatorname{Pos}
to the union of hyperplanes
z^{\bot}
, where
z
belongs to the
set
R
of MBM classes of type
(1,1)
.
The group
\Gamma
preserves the set
R
and
acts on it with finitely many orbits (
[
AV2
]
).
Therefore, the image of the union of the orthogonal complements to
R

is a finite union of closed hyperbolic orbifolds

{\cal M}_{1},...,{\cal M}_{k}
immersed
in
{\cal M}={\mathbb{H}}/\Gamma
(
4.2
).
The complement
{\cal M}\backslash\bigcup_{i}{\cal M}_{i}

is a union of finitely many locally convex orbifold
polyhedral spaces. Each of these orbifolds is obtained
as the quotient of a connected component
U
of

{\mathbb{H}}\backslash\bigcup_{z\in R}z^{\bot}
,
with
U
being a convex, open subset of
{\mathbb{H}}

called
an ample chamber
. Every ample chamber

P
is the ample cone of an appropriate birational
model
(M,I_{P})
of
M
(
[
Ma
]
,
[
AV4
]
).
The automorphism group of
(M,I_{P})

is mapped to the stabilizer of
P
in
\operatorname{\sf Mon}_{I}

with finite kernel and finite index (
4.1
).

This way, we reduce the study of the automorphism
group of a hyperkähler manifold to the study of the orbifold fundamental group of
a certain type of a polyhedral space, called CHOPB.

Definition 1.2:
Let
{\cal H}={\mathbb{H}}^{n}/\Gamma

be a hyperbolic orbifold, and
S_{1},...,S_{n}\subset{\cal H}

a collection of closed locally geodesic immersed hypersurfaces.
A
convex hyperbolic orbifold with
polyhedral boundary
(CHOPB) is
a connected component of the complement

{\cal H}\backslash\bigcup_{i}S_{i}
.

Let
{\cal P}
be a CHOPB, and
P\subset{\mathbb{H}}^{n}

a connected component of its preimage in
{\mathbb{H}}^{n}
.

The isotropic boundary
of the CHOPB
is the set
\overline{P}\cap\operatorname{Abs}
, where

\operatorname{Abs}=\partial{\mathbb{H}}^{n}
. It turns out that the
isotropic boundary of a CHOPB is either
a countable set or a fractal (
5.4
); in particular,
its interior is empty, unless
P={\mathbb{H}}^{n}

As mentioned in Subsection

1.2
,
the limit set
\Lambda
of
\Gamma_{0}=\pi_{1}({\cal P})

is in the isotropic boundary of
P
, and,
conversely, all non-cusp points of the
isotropic boundary belong to
\Lambda

(
5.4
).

The fractal nature of this set is explored in
some detail in Section
7
.
We define
the Apollonian carpet
of
the isotropic boundary of a CHOPB as
the union of all positive dimensional irreducible
real analytic subvarieties in its isotropic boundary,
and prove that each component of the
Apollonian carpet is a geodesic sphere
S
in
\operatorname{Abs}

(
6.1
). Moreover, the
convex hull of each
S
is a hyperbolic subspace
in the closure of
P
, and its image in the quotient

{\mathbb{H}}^{n}/\Gamma
is a closed
hyperbolic immersed submanifold
{\cal M}
in the
closure of
{\cal P}
.
This implies, in particular, that the lattice

\pi_{1}({\cal M})
is a subgroup of
\pi_{1}({\cal P})
.
(
7.1
; here
this result is stated for CHOPBs associated with the
hyperkähler manifolds, but the
proof works generally).

1.4
Indra’s pearls, Apollonian gaskets and
geometry of K3 surfaces (after A. Baragar et al)

The “Apollonian gasket” is a classical concept,
known since Apollonius of Perga, c. 240 BC - c. 190 BC.
(
[
MSW
]
).
Apollonian gasket is obtained by taking
three kissing circles, inscribing a fourth circle
kissing these three, then inscribing
a circle inside a triangle formed by the
segments of three circles, and so on,
ad infinitum:

This construction can (and should) be performed on a 2-sphere.
Since the Moebius group acts transitively on triples of
pairwise kissing circles, the Apollonian gasket on
{\mathbb{C}}P^{1}

is unique up to a conformal equivalence (
[
MSW
, p. 201]
).

Interestingly enough, the Apollonian gasket is a limit
set of a discrete subgroup of the Moebius group
PSL(2,{\mathbb{C}})

acting on
{\mathbb{C}}P^{1}
. This is why the Apollonian gasket is one
of the central subjects of “Indra’s Pearls”
[
MSW
]
, by
D. Mumford, C. Series and D. Wright. In
[
MSW
]
this was
shown by an explicit argument with matrices. Using

6.1
together with

5.4
, we obtain a
conceptual argument which implies that the Apollonian
gasket is a limit set (Subsection
7.4.1
).

The Apollonian gasket is ubiquitous in physics
(
[
Sa
]
) and even in art: the largest
artwork in history, “Earth Drawings” by Jim Denevan,
drawn in the sand of Black Rock Desert (Nevada),
depicts the Apollonian gasket.

This fractal can be obtained as the isotropic boundary
of a universal covering of a CHOPB.
Consider a circle
S
in

{\mathbb{C}}P^{1}=\operatorname{Abs}({\mathbb{H}}^{3})
. The convex hull of
S
in
{\mathbb{H}}^{3}

is a hyperbolic 2-plane, denoted by
W
, and
S
is its isotropic
boundary. The 2-plane
W
can be obtained as a projectivization
of the positive cone in a subspace
\widetilde{W}={\mathbb{R}}^{1,2}
in
R^{1,3}
.
Choose an integer lattice in
R^{1,3}
, and
consider the corresponding lattice group
\Gamma:=SO_{\mathbb{Z}}(1,3)
.
If
\widetilde{W}
was chosen rational,
the stabilizer
\operatorname{St}_{\Gamma}(S)
of
S
in
\Gamma:=SO_{\mathbb{Z}}(1,3)
is
a group which is commensurable with
SO_{\mathbb{Z}}(1,2)
.
We can choose
\widetilde{W}
in such a way that
its images under
\Gamma
can be tangent, but
never intersect transversally (Subsection

7.4.1
).

was obtained earlier in
[
Ba1

.

Then its image in the hyperbolic manifold
{\cal M}={\mathbb{H}}^{3}/\Gamma

is
{\mathbb{H}}^{2}/\operatorname{St}_{\Gamma}(S)
, a hyperbolic manifold of finite volume. The
universal cover of the corresponding CHOPB
{\cal P}
is a locally finite
hyperbolic polyhedron, which looks as follows:

From this picture, it is visually clear that
the isotropic boundary of
{\cal P}
is
the Apollonian gasket. A formal proof of this
observation can be obtained by application of

7.1
,
which describes the Apollonian carpet
of a CHOPB for an arithmetic hyperbolic orbifold
(the statement is made for CHOPBs of hyperkähler
origin, but the proof works for this general
situation).

This construction was realized in an ample
cone of a K3 surface by A. Baragar in
[
Ba3
]
.
In a serie of papers (
[
Ba1
,
Ba2
,
Ba3
,
Ba4
,
Ba5
]
), Baragar constructed many examples
illustrating the fractal nature of the isotropic
boundary of the ample cone of a K3 surface.

For K3 surfaces, an MBM class is an integer
class of square -2, usually abbreviated as “(-2)-class”.
The BBF form
q
on a K3 surface is the intersection form.
The corresponding hyperbolic manifold is

{\mathbb{P}}(\operatorname{Pos}\cap\operatorname{\rm NS}_{\mathbb{R}})/\Gamma
, where
\Gamma
is a sublattice
of finite rank in
SO(\operatorname{\rm NS}_{\mathbb{Z}},q)
, and the corresponding
hyperbolic polyhedron
P\subset{\mathbb{P}}(\operatorname{Pos}\cap\operatorname{\rm NS}_{\mathbb{R}})

is one of the connected components of

{\mathbb{P}}(\operatorname{Pos}\cap\operatorname{\rm NS}_{\mathbb{R}})\backslash\bigcup_{i}S_{i}^{\bot}
,
where
\{S_{i}\}\subset\operatorname{\rm NS}_{\mathbb{Z}}
is the set of all MBM classes.

In
[
Ba1
]
, Baragar constructed an arithmetic
lattice
\Lambda
of rank 4, such that its isometry group acts
on the set of (-2)-classes transitively. This means that

O(\Lambda)
acts on the set of faces of
P
transitively.
Moreover, for any pair of (-2)-classes
a,b\in\Lambda
,
the lattice
\langle a,b\rangle
generated by
a
and
b

has signature
(1,1)
(this means that their orthogonal
complements don’t intersect in the absolute) or
(0,1)
(and this means
that the orthogonal circles are tangent). This gives a
fractal formed by kissing circles, which is conformally
equivalent to the Apollonian gasket by

[
MSW
, p. 201]
, as we indicated earlier.

In his earliest work on the subject
(
[
Ba1
]
), Baragar established the
fractal nature of the isotropic boundary of the ample
cone of a K3 surface, and computed the Hausdorff dimension
of this fractal, identified with the limit set of the
automorphism group. This computation is based on
a result of D. Sullivan, relating the Hausdorff
dimension of the limit set with the growth rate
of the number of orbit points in a ball of radius
r
.

This observation was later used by I. Dolgachev

[
Do
]
, who used the bounds on the Hausdorff dimension
to estimate the growth rate of the number of curves on a K3 surface
in a given orbit of the automorphism group.
Note that the framework built in the present paper can be used
to extend these estimates to an arbitrary hyperkähler
manifold. Indeed, the Hausdorff dimension of the isotropic boundary of an ample
cone is bounded from below by the dimension of the
components of its Apollonian carpet.

1.5
The structure of this paper

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
we
recall some basic definitions and facts about the
hyperkähler manifolds. We also introduce the convex
cones in
H^{1,1}(M,{\mathbb{R}})
which are central for our
further investigation. In Section

3
we prove that whenever ample, movable or
effective cone does not coincide with the
positve cone in the real Neron-Severi group, its isotropic
boundary is nowhere dense (the cone has no “round
part”); on the contrary, when the Picard number is not
maximal, the Kähler cone always has a “round
part”, which is open in the boundary of the positive
cone. The statement for the ample cone is mentioned in

[
Mat
]
, with a partial proof, and for the effective
cone it is proved in
[
De
]
. Our starting point was an
earlier version of
[
De
]
where the proof for the
effective cone was given for the known families of IHS
manifolds only, by adapting Kovacs’ argument
[
Ko1
]

on K3 surfaces. It seems that the simple general proof of this result
relying only on hyperbolic geometry was not widely known. We
have therefore decided to give a self-contained and
complete account here.

In Sections 4 and 5, we study the boundary of the ample
cone using hyperbolic geometry. In Section 6, we recall a
theorem of N. Shah which permits us to prove that the
“analytic” part of the absolute of the ample cone is a
union of spheres. In Section 7, we describe these spheres
more explicitly and give a number of examples of their possible
behavior. We also make a connection to the structure of
the automorphism group of the hyperkähler manifold.

Hyperkähler manifolds

We briefly recall the basic definitions of hyperkähler geometry.
We refer to
[
Bea
]
,
[

]
and
[

]
for a more detailed

2.1
The basic definitions

Let
M
be a complex manifold with a Riemannian metric
g
. Denote by
I
the complex structure on
M
,
and recall that
g
is Hermitian if for any pair of tangent vectors
u
and
v
we
have
g(u,v)=g(Iu,Iv)
. In this case one defines
\omega(u,v)=g(Iu,v)
. Then

\omega
is a two-form on
M
, and
M
is called Kähler if
d\omega=0
.
If
M
is Kähler then
\omega
is a symplectic form on
M
.

Definition 2.1:
A
hyperkähler structure
on a manifold
M
is a Riemannian metric
g

and a triple of complex structures
I
,
J
,
K
, satisfying quaternionic relations

IJ=-JI=K
, such that
g
is Kähler for
I
,
J
and
K
.
A
hyperkähler manifold
is a manifold with a hyperkähler structure.

A hyperkähler manifold carries symplectic forms
\omega_{I}
,
\omega_{J}
and
\omega_{K}

associated to
g
and the complex structures
I
,
J
and
K
, as above.
Hyperkähler manifolds are holomorphically symplectic. Indeed,
\Omega_{I}=\omega_{J}+\sqrt{-1}\>\omega_{K}

is a holomorphic symplectic form on
(M,I)
. The holomorphic symplectic
forms on
(M,J)
and
(M,K)
are be defined analogously.

Recall that for a Riemannian metric
g
and a point
x\in M

the subgroup of
GL(T_{x}M)
generated by the operators of parallel
transport (along all loops with endpoints at
x
, with respect to the
Levi–Civita connection) is called the holonomy group of
g
.
A hyperkähler manifold can be equivalently defined as a Riemannian manifold
whose holonomy is contained in
Sp(n)
(the group of all orthogonal isometries
of a quaternionic vector space preserving
I,J,K
).

In this paper we will consider only one special class of hyperkähler
manifolds.

Definition 2.2:

A compact hyperkähler manifold
M
is
of maximal holonomy
, or
IHS
,
if
\pi_{1}(M)=1
and the holonomy group of the Levi–Civita connection
equals
Sp(n)
, where
2n=\dim_{\mathbb{C}}(M)
.

For the rest of this paper, we abuse the language
slightly, and “a hyperkähler manifold” for us always
means a
compact hyperkähler manifold of maximal holonomy.

On any hyperkähler manifold of maximal holonomy, the space
of holomorphic two-forms is one-dimensional, spanned by the holomorphic
symplectic form described above.

Fix a hyperkähler manifold
M
and consider its singular cohomology
group
H^{2}(X,\mathbb{Q})
. One of the main properties of hyperkähler manifolds
of maximal holonomy is the existence of a canonical quadratic form

q\in\mathrm{Sym}^{2}H^{2}(X,\mathbb{Q})^{*}
, called the
Bogomolov–Beauville–Fujiki
form
or the
BBF form
. This form can be characterized by the following
property (see
[
So
]
for a more detailed discussion):

Theorem 2.3:
(Fujiki,
[
F
]
)
For any hyperkähler manifold
M
of complex dimension
2n
there exists
a constant
c_{M}\in\mathbb{Q}
and a quadratic form
q
on
H^{2}(X,\mathbb{Q})
such that for any

a\in H^{2}(M,\mathbb{Q})
we have

\int_{M}a^{2n}=c_{M}q(a)^{n}.

We may assume, after rescaling, that the BBF form
q
is integral and primitive on
H^{2}(M,{\mathbb{Z}})
.
In the above theorem the integral on the left hand side denotes the pairing
of the cohomology class
a^{2n}\in H^{4n}(M,\mathbb{Q})
with the fundamental
class of
M
determined by the canonical orientation of
M
, see
[
So
]
for details.

The form
q
has signature
(3,b_{2}(M)-3)
. If
I
is one of the complex structures that appears
as part of a hyperkähler structure on
M
, then
q
is negative definite on the primitive forms
of
I
-type
(1,1)
and positive definite on the subspace of
H^{2}(M,\mathbb{R})

spanned by
\omega_{I}
,
\mathrm{Re}(\Omega_{I})
and
\mathrm{Im}(\Omega_{I})
,
where
\omega_{I}
is the Kähler form and
\Omega_{I}
is the holomorphic symplectic form.

2.2
Cones in the cohomology of a hyperkähler manifold

Let us fix a hyperkähler manifold
M
of maximal holonomy with a
hyperkähler structure
(g,I,J,K)
. We will consider the Hodge decomposition
of the cohomology of
M
with respect to
I
if not stated otherwise.

The main goal of the present paper is to study the shape of several natural
convex cones in the real vector space
H^{1,1}(M,\mathbb{R})
. The latter
means the intersection of
H^{1,1}(M)
with
H^{2}(M,\mathbb{R})
inside
H^{2}(M,\mathbb{C})
.
As was explained above, the vector space
V
carries the quadratic form
q
,
the restriction of the BBF form to the
(1,1)
-part of the cohomology.
The form
q
has signature
(1,b_{2}(M)-3)
on
H^{1,1}(M,\mathbb{R})
.

Recall that the Néron–Severi group
\operatorname{\rm NS}(M)
is the image of the Picard group
\operatorname{Pic}(M)

in
H^{2}(M,\mathbb{Z})
under the map that computes the first Chern class of a line bundle.
By Hodge theory, the group
\operatorname{\rm NS}(M)
is contained in
H^{1,1}(M,\mathbb{R})
, and we will denote
by
\operatorname{\rm NS}_{\mathbb{R}}
the
\mathbb{R}
-subspace of
H^{1,1}(M,\mathbb{R})

spanned by
\operatorname{\rm NS}(M)
.
The condition that a hyperkähler manifold
M
is projective
is equivalent to the condition that
q|_{\operatorname{\rm NS}_{\mathbb{R}}}
has signature
(1,\rho(M)-1)
,
where
\rho(M)
is the Picard number of
M
, see
[
Hu1
]
.

Let us recall the definitions of the cones that we are going to study.

1.

The positive cone
\mathcal{C}_{M}\subset H^{1,1}(M,\mathbb{R})
is the connected component of the cone

\{v\in H^{1,1}(M,\mathbb{R})\enskip|\enskip q(v)>0\}
that contains the class of the Kähler form
\omega_{I}
.

2.

The Kähler cone
\mathcal{K}_{M}\subset\mathcal{C}_{M}
is the cone consisting of the cohomology classes of all Kähler forms on
M
.
The cone
\mathcal{K}_{M}
is open and its closure is called the nef cone.

3.

The birational Kähler cone
\mathcal{BK}_{M}\subset\mathcal{C}_{M}
is the cone consisting of the cohomology
classes of the form
f^{*}[\omega]
, where
f\colon M\dashrightarrow M^{\prime}
is an arbitrary bimeromorphic map
to a hyperkähler manifold
M^{\prime}
and
\omega
is an arbitrary Kähler form on
M^{\prime}
. Note that
the cone
\mathcal{BK}_{M}
is not convex (but its closure is, as we shall see from its description below).

4.

The pseudoeffective cone
\mathcal{P}_{M}\subset H^{1,1}(M,\mathbb{R})
consists of the cohomology classes
of all closed positive
(1,1)
-currents on
M
. It is known that
\mathcal{P}_{M}
is closed and contains
\mathcal{C}_{M}
.

5.

The ample cone
\mathcal{A}_{M}\subset\operatorname{\rm NS}_{\mathbb{R}}(M)
is spanned by the classes of ample line bundles.
The ample cone is open in
\operatorname{\rm NS}_{\mathbb{R}}(M)
.

6.

The movable cone
\mathcal{M}_{M}\subset\operatorname{\rm NS}_{\mathbb{R}}(M)
is spanned by the classes of movable line bundles, i.e.
the line bundles whose complete linear systems have base loci of codimension at least two.

We summarize the relations between the cones introduced above. We have the following inclusions:

\mathcal{K}_{M}\subset\mathcal{BK}_{M}\subset\mathcal{C}_{M}\subset\mathcal{P}_{M}.

Moreover, it is known that
\overline{\mathcal{BK}_{M}}
is dual to
\mathcal{P}_{M}
with respect to
q
, see
[
Hu2
, Corollary 4.6]
.

The ample and the movable cones are related to the Kähler cone and the birational Kähler cone as follows.
For the ample cone we have
\mathcal{A}_{M}=\mathcal{K}_{M}\cap\operatorname{\rm NS}_{\mathbb{R}}(M)
.
For the movable cone
\mathcal{M}_{M}^{in}=(\overline{\mathcal{BK}_{M}})^{in}\cap\operatorname{\rm NS}_{\mathbb{R}}(M)
,
where the superscript
{in}
means the interior of the cone, see
[
Ma
, Proposition 5.6 and Lemma 6.22]
.

The Kähler cone and the birational Kähler cone admit an important description
in terms of wall and chamber decomposition of the positive cone. To give this description
we need to recall the notions of an MBM class and of a prime exceptional divisor.

A cohomology class
z\in H^{2}(M,{\mathbb{Z}}),\ q(z)<0
on a
hyperkähler manifold
M
with a complex
structure
I
is called
an MBM class
if some
(equivalently, any) deformation
(M,I^{\prime})
of
(M,I)

with
\operatorname{\rm NS}_{\mathbb{R}}
generated by
z
contains a rational curve;
see
[
AV5
]
.
We will denote by
\mathrm{MBM}(M)\subset H^{2}(M,\mathbb{Z})
the set of primitive MBM classes. The set
\mathrm{MBM}(M)
is a deformation invariant of
a hyperkähler manifold
M
.

Example 2.4:
Recall that a
(-2)
-class on a K3 surface

M
is a cohomology class
\eta\in H^{2}(M,{\mathbb{Z}})
with
self-intersection
-2
. The MBM classes on a K3 surface
are exactly the
(-2)
-classes (
[
AV1
]
).

Recall from
[
AV2
]
that the set of MBM classes on a hyperkähler
manifold
M
is
q
-bounded: there exists a constant
\alpha>0
such that
for all
v\in\mathrm{MBM}(M)
we have
-\alpha\leq q(v)<0
.
Now consider the intersection
\mathcal{S}=\mathrm{MBM}(M)\cap\operatorname{\rm NS}(M)
. Then
the collection of hyperplanes
v^{\perp}
,
v\in\mathcal{S}
is locally
finite in
\mathcal{C}_{M}
(see e.g.
[
SSV
, Proposition 5.12]
) and
the complement
\mathcal{C}_{M}\setminus\cup_{v\in\mathcal{S}}v^{\perp}
is a disjoint
union of open chambers, the Kähler cone
\mathcal{K}_{M}
being one of
the chambers, see
[
AV1
]
.
In other words, the MBM classes are orthogonal to the
faces of the Kähler chambers, and all faces of the Kähler
cones of deformations and birational models of
M
are
realized in this way (
[
AV4
]
).

Now we recall an analogous description of the closure of the birational
Kähler cone
\overline{\mathcal{BK}(M)}
. Recall that a
prime exceptional
divisor
is a prime divisor
D
on
M
whose cohomology class is negative:

q([D])<0
. Prime exceptional divisors are uniruled, see
[
Bou
]
. We will denote by
\mathrm{PE}(M)\subset\operatorname{\rm NS}(M)

the set of cohomology classes of prime exceptional divisors on
M
.

It follows from the uniruledness (see
[
AV5
, Lemma 2.11]
)
that the cohomology class
[D]
of a prime exceptional divisor
D

is MBM. We will call an MBM class of type
(1,1)

divisorial

if its orbit under the action of the Hodge monodromy group (see
[
Ma
]
)
contains a class that is proportional to the class of a prime exceptional divisor.
Let us now denote by
\mathcal{S}
the set of divisorial primitive MBM classes on a
hyperkähler manifold
M
. The complement
\mathcal{C}_{M}\setminus\cup_{v\in\mathcal{S}}v^{\perp}

is again a disjoint union of open chambers and the closure of one
of these chambers is
\overline{\mathcal{BK}(M)}
.

Let us finally recall the following description of the pseudoeffective
cone given in
[
Bou
]
:

\mathcal{P}_{M}=\overline{\mathcal{C}_{M}+\sum_{v\in\mathrm{PE}(M)}\mathbb{R}_{+}\cdot v},

(2.1)

which means that the boundary of the cone
\mathcal{P}_{M}
is locally polyhedral away from
\mathcal{C}_{M}
,
with extremal rays spanned by the classes of the prime exceptional
divisors.

3
Hyperbolic spaces and arithmetic lattices

In this section we recall some standard notions of hyperbolic geometry
in the form convenient for applications to hyperkähler manifolds.
As a general reference on hyperbolic geometry one may use, for example,

[
Ka1
]
, where most of the facts that we recall
are proven in greater generality. In order to streamline the exposition,
we choose not to deduce what we need from those general theorems,
but to give self-contained elementary proofs, claiming no originality
whatsoever. We also immediately apply the statements about the hyperbolic
spaces to hyperkähler manifolds, reformulating them in the terminology
introduced in the previous section.

3.1
The hyperbolic space

Let
V
be a vector space over
\mathbb{R}
and
q\in\mathrm{Sym}^{2}V^{*}

a quadratic form of signature
(1,d)
.
Let
V^{+}=\{x\in V\enskip|\enskip q(x)>0\}\subset V
be the cone of positive vectors
and
\mathbb{P}(V^{+})
its image in the real projective space
\mathbb{P}(V)
.
Analogously, set
V^{-}=\{v\in V\enskip|\enskip q(v)<0\}
.
Denote by
Q=\{x\in\mathbb{P}(V)\enskip|\enskip q(x)=0\}\subset\mathbb{P}(V)
the
real projective quadric defined by the quadratic form
q
.
The quadric
Q
is the boundary sphere of the hyperbolic space
\mathbb{P}(V^{+})
.
For a non-zero vector
v\in V
we will denote by
[v]
its image in
\mathbb{P}(V)
.

The group
O(V,q)
has 4 connected components and
we denote the connected component of the identity by
SO^{+}(V,q)
.
It is clear that
\mathbb{P}(V^{+})\simeq SO^{+}(V,q)/SO(d)
.
The hyperbolic space
\mathbb{P}(V^{+})
carries an
SO^{+}(V,q)
-invariant
Riemannian metric
g
. It is easy to see that this metric
is unique up to a constant multiplier. One can also check
that
SO^{+}(V,q)
is the group of all oriented isometries of

\mathbb{H}^{d}=(\mathbb{P}(V^{+}),g)
. We recall the following classification
of isometries of a hyperbolic space.

Theorem-definition:

Let
\alpha\in SO^{+}(1,d)
be an isometry acting on
\mathbb{H}^{d}
.
Then one and only one of the following cases occurs:

1.

\alpha
has an eigenvector
x
with
q(x)>0
(then
\alpha
is called
elliptic
);

2.

\alpha
has an eigenvector
x
with
q(x)=0
and eigenvalue
\lambda
satisfying
|\lambda|>1

(then
\alpha
is called
hyperbolic
, or
loxodromic
);

3.

\alpha
has a unique eigenvector
x
with
q(x)=0
(then
\alpha
is called
parabolic
).

3.2
Wall and chamber decompositions of the hyperbolic space

For the applications to hyperkähler geometry, we need to study wall and chamber
decompositions of the hyperbolic space
\mathbb{P}(V^{+})
. Such decompositions are
determined by a subset
\mathcal{S}\subset V^{-}
of elements orthogonal to the walls.
We will always assume the following:

\mathcal{S}\neq\emptyset,\quad-\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{S},\quad\mathcal{S}\mbox{ is discrete in }V\mbox{ and $q$-bounded}.

(3.1)

The latter condition means that there exists some constant
\alpha>0
such that
-\alpha\leq q(v)<0
for any
v\in\mathcal{S}
.

For
v\in V^{-}
denote
H_{v}=v^{\perp}\subset V
and

V^{\Diamond}=V^{+}\setminus\cup_{v\in\mathcal{S}}H_{v}.

(3.2)

It is well-known that the collection of hyperplanes
H_{v}
,
v\in\mathcal{S}
is locally finite in
V^{+}
,
see e.g.
[
SSV
, Proposition 5.12]
. Therefore
V^{\Diamond}
is a union of disjoint open
connected components which we will call the
\mathcal{S}
-chambers
of
V^{+}
.

Given an
\mathcal{S}
-chamber
\mathcal{C}
we will denote by
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{C})
its image in
\mathbb{P}(V)
.
We are interested in the structure of the boundary of
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{C})
at infinity,
or, more precisely, in the structure of the set
\mathbb{P}(\overline{\mathcal{C}})\cap Q
.
The properties of this set depend on the geometry of
\mathcal{S}
, and there are
two substantially different cases to consider: either the set
\mathcal{S}
is
“big”, i.e. spans
V
and is invariant under the action of an arithmetic
lattice in
SO(V,q)
, or
\mathcal{S}
is contained in a proper subspace
N\subset V
.
In the applications, the first case arises when
V
is the space spanned by the
Néron–Severi lattice and
\mathcal{C}
is the ample or the movable cone, and the second case arises
when
V=H^{1,1}(X,\mathbb{R})
and
\mathcal{C}
is the Kähler cone (the subspace

N
is then spanned by the Néron-Severi lattice). We will treat these
two cases separately below.

3.2.1
The case when
\mathcal{S}
spans
V

In the above setting, assume moreover that
V=V_{\mathbb{Z}}\otimes\mathbb{R}
for some
lattice
V_{\mathbb{Z}}
. Denote
V_{\mathbb{Q}}=V_{\mathbb{Z}}\otimes\mathbb{Q}
and assume that
q

is rational, i.e.
q\in\mathrm{Sym}^{2}V_{\mathbb{Q}}^{*}
. Let
\Gamma\subset O(V_{\mathbb{Z}},q)
be
a subgroup of finite index and assume that
\mathcal{S}\subset V_{\mathbb{Z}}
and

\mathcal{S}
is
\Gamma
-invariant.
The following proposition is well known.
We sketch an elementary proof for the sake of completeness.

Proposition 3.1:

In the above setting assume that
d\geq 2
.
For any
x\in Q
the orbit
\Gamma x
is dense in
Q
in the analytic topology.

Proof:

Let
U\subset Q
be a non-empty open subset. We have to prove that
\Gamma x\cap U\neq\emptyset
.
We will consider separately two cases: when
Q
contains a rational point and when it doesn’t.
We will construct some
\gamma\in\Gamma
with
\gamma^{n}x\in U
for sufficiently big
n
. In the first case

\gamma
will be parabolic, and in the second case hyperbolic.

Case 1.
Assume that
Q
contains a rational point: let
u\in V_{\mathbb{Q}}
be a non-zero vector with
q(u)=0
.
In this case, intersecting
Q
with lines passing through
[u]\in Q
we see that rational points are dense in
Q
.
Therefore we may assume that
[u]\in U
.

Let
G\subset O(V,q)
be the stabilizer of
u
. The group
G
is unipotent
and its Lie algebra is
u\wedge u^{\perp}\subset\Lambda^{2}V
, where we identify the Lie algebra of the orthogonal group
O(V,q)

with
\Lambda^{2}V
via the form
q
. For any
v\in u^{\perp}
,
w\in V
and
t\in\mathbb{R}
the action
of
e^{tu\wedge v}\in G
on
w
is given by the following formula:

e^{tu\wedge v}w=w+tq(v,w)u-tq(u,w)v-\frac{t^{2}}{2}q(u,w)q(v,v)u.

(3.3)

Rescaling
u
, we may assume that
u\in V_{\mathbb{Z}}
. The assumption
d\geq 2
implies that we may find

v\in u^{\perp}\cap V_{\mathbb{Z}}
with
q(v,v)\neq 0
. Replacing
v
by its multiple, we may
assume that the operator
\gamma=e^{u\wedge v}
preserves
V_{\mathbb{Z}}
and, furthermore, that
\gamma\in\Gamma
.
Choose
w\in V
so that
x=[w]
. Then
q(u,w)\neq 0
unless
u
and
w
are proportional.
In any case, we see from the equation (
3.3
) that
\gamma^{n}x\to[u]
for
n\to+\infty
, therefore
\gamma^{n}x\in U
for
n

big enough.

Case 2.
Assume that
Q
does not contain rational points. We may still find a two-dimensional rational
subspace
W_{\mathbb{Q}}\subset V_{\mathbb{Q}}
such that
q|_{W_{\mathbb{Q}}}
is non-degenerate,
x\notin\mathbb{P}(W)
and
\mathbb{P}(W)\cap U\neq\emptyset
,
where
W=W_{\mathbb{Q}}\otimes\mathbb{R}\subset V
.
Then
Q\cap\mathbb{P}(W)
consists of two points
[u]
and
[u^{\prime}]
, where
u,u^{\prime}\in W
and we
may assume that
[u]\in U
.

By our assumption, the quadratic form
q|_{W}
does not admit non-zero rational isotropic vectors.
After possibly rescaling
q
by a rational factor, we may represent it in an appropriate basis by a diagonal matrix with entries
1
and
-d
,
where
d>1
is a square-free integer. We may identify the field
K=\mathbb{Q}[\sqrt{d}]
with
W

as a rational vector space, then
q
is identified with the norm of an algebraic number.
It is well known that the group of units in the ring of algebraic integers
\mathcal{O}_{K}
is infinite.
Choose an element
a\in\mathcal{O}_{K}^{*}
of infinite order. Multiplication by
a
preserves the
lattice
\mathcal{O}_{K}\subset W
. Replacing
a
by its power we may assume that there exists
an element
\gamma\in\Gamma
such that
\gamma|_{W^{\perp}}=\mathrm{Id}
and
\gamma|_{W}
acts
as multiplication by
a
. Then
u
and
u^{\prime}
are eigenvectors of
\gamma|_{W}
with eigenvalues

\lambda
and
\lambda^{-1}
, and possibly replacing
\gamma
by its inverse we may assume that
\lambda>1
.
Recall that
x\notin\mathbb{P}(W)
by construction and note that
x\notin\mathbb{P}(W^{\perp})
,
since
q|_{W^{\perp}}
is negative-definite. Since
\lambda
is the
unique eigenvalue of
\gamma
with absolute value greater than one, it is clear that

\gamma^{n}x\to[u]
as
n\to+\infty
and so
\gamma^{n}x\in U
for
n
big enough.

Recall our notation:
Q=\{x\in\mathbb{P}(V)\enskip|\enskip q(x)=0\}
is the boundary of
\mathbb{P}(V^{+})

and
\Gamma\subset O(V_{\mathbb{Z}},q)
is a subgroup of finite index.

Proposition 3.2:

Assume that
d\geq 2
. For any
x\in\mathbb{P}(V)\setminus Q
we have

Q\subset\overline{\Gamma x}
, where the overline denotes the closure in
\mathbb{P}(V)
in the analytic topology.

Proof:

The closed subset
\overline{\Gamma x}\subset\mathbb{P}(V)
is clearly
\Gamma
-invariant, so by
3.2.1
it
is enough to show that
\overline{\Gamma x}
contains at least one point of
Q
.

Fix an arbitrary Euclidean norm
\|\cdot\|
on
V
and some
v\in V
with
[v]=x
.
Since
\Gamma
is an arithmetic lattice in a non-compact orthogonal group and the
orbit of
v
under the action of this group is unbounded in
V
,
we can find a sequence
\gamma_{i}\in\Gamma
, such that for
y_{i}=\gamma_{i}v

we have
\|y_{i}\|\to+\infty
for
i\to+\infty
. Since the unit
\|\cdot\|
-sphere in
V
is compact,
we may also assume that the unit length vectors
y_{i}/\|y_{i}\|
converge to a point
z\in V
.
Since
q(y_{i})=q(x)
, we see that
q(z)=\lim q(y_{i})/\|y_{i}\|=0
, and the image of
z
in
\mathbb{P}(V)

lies in
Q
.

Theorem 3.3:

In the above setting let
\mathcal{C}
be one of the
\mathcal{S}
-chambers for some
\mathcal{S}

satisfying (
3.1
) and
\overline{\mathcal{C}}\subset V
be the closure
of
\mathcal{C}
in
V
. Then the set
\mathbb{P}(\overline{\mathcal{C}})\cap Q
is nowhere dense in
Q
.

Proof:

Let
U\subset Q
be an open subset. The set of positive vectors
V^{+}
consists of two connected components and
\mathcal{C}
lies
in one of them. We can therefore find
u\in V
such that its image
[u]\in\mathbb{P}(V)
lies in
U
and
q(w,u)>0
for any
w\in\mathcal{C}
.

By
3.2.1

for any
v\in\mathcal{S}
there exists a sequence
\gamma_{i}\in\Gamma
such that
[\gamma_{i}v]\to[u]
for
i\to+\infty
.
We may choose a sequence of vectors
v_{i}\in V^{-}
such that
v_{i}
is proportional to
\gamma_{i}v
and

v_{i}\to u
for
i\to+\infty
. Let
H_{i}=v_{i}^{\perp}
,
H_{i}^{-}=\{w\in V\enskip|\enskip q(w,v_{i})<0\}

and
H_{i}^{+}=\{w\in V\enskip|\enskip q(w,v_{i})>0\}
. Since the vectors
v_{i}
converge to
u
, the
hyperplanes
H_{i}
converge to
u^{\perp}
. Since
\mathbb{P}(u^{\perp})
is tangent to
Q
at
the point
[u]\in U
, it follows that infinitely many of the hyperplanes

\mathbb{P}(H_{i})
have non-empty intersection with
U
. Therefore, passing to a subsequence,
we may assume that
\mathbb{P}(H_{i}^{-})\cap U\neq\emptyset
for all
i
.

Observe that for any
w\in\cap_{i}H_{i}^{-}
we have
q(w,v_{i})<0
, so
q(w,u)\leq 0
, because
v_{i}
converge to
u
.
It follows that there must exist some
i_{0}
such that
\mathcal{C}\nsubseteq H_{i_{0}}^{-}
: otherwise for
w\in\mathcal{C}

we would have
q(w,u)\leq 0
which contradicts the choice of
u
.
Since
\mathcal{S}
is
\Gamma
-invariant,
\gamma_{i}v\in\mathcal{S}
for every
i
, so the chamber
\mathcal{C}
is either
contained in
H_{i}^{-}
or in
H_{i}^{+}
. Therefore
\overline{\mathcal{C}}\cap H_{i_{0}}^{-}=\emptyset
and
we conclude that the non-empty open subset
U\cap\mathbb{P}(H_{i_{0}}^{-})\subset U
does not intersect
\mathbb{P}(\overline{\mathcal{C}})
.

Recall that by our assumptions (
3.1
) we have
-\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{S}
.
Define

\mathcal{S}^{\prime}=\{v\in\mathcal{S}\enskip|\enskip q(v,w)>0\,\,\forall w\in\mathcal{C}\}

(3.4)

and note that
\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{S}^{\prime}\coprod(-\mathcal{S}^{\prime})
. For
v\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}

let
H_{v}^{+}=\{w\in V\enskip|\enskip q(v,w)>0\}
.

Corollary 3.4:

In the above setting we have
\overline{\mathcal{C}}=\overline{\cap_{v\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}}H_{v}^{+}}=\cap_{v\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}}\overline{H_{v}^{+}}
.

Proof:

Denote
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}=\cap_{v\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}}H_{v}^{+}
.
Note that the set
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}\cap V^{+}
is convex and
open, because the collection of hyperplanes
H_{v}
,
v\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
is locally finite in
V^{+}

by
[
SSV
, Proposition 5.12]
.
It follows that
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}^{\prime}\cap V^{+}
, because
\mathcal{C}
is a connected component of the complement
to this collection of hyperplanes, and clearly
\mathcal{C}\subset\mathcal{C}^{\prime}\cap V^{+}
.

Let us show that
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}\cap V^{-}=\emptyset
.
Assume that
v\in\mathcal{C}^{\prime}\cap V^{-}
. Since
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
is convex, it contains the segments connecting
v

with the points of
\mathcal{C}
, and the intersections of these segments with the boundary
of
V^{+}
form a subset of the boundary of
\mathcal{C}
with non-empty interior.
This implies that the boundary of
\mathcal{C}
contains an open subset of the boundary of
V^{+}
,
contradicting
3.2.1
. It follows that
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}\cap V^{-}=\emptyset
.

We see that
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}\subset\overline{V^{+}}
. The convexity of
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
now implies that
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}\subset\overline{\mathcal{C}}
.
Since
\mathcal{C}\subset\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
, we also have
\overline{\mathcal{C}}\subset\overline{\mathcal{C}^{\prime}}
and the first
equality in the statement of the corollary follows.

To prove the second equality, note that any closed convex cone with non-empty interior
is the closure of its interior. Hence it suffices to prove that the cones

\overline{\mathcal{C}^{\prime}}
and
\cap_{v\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}}\overline{H_{v}^{+}}
have the same non-empty interior.
It is clear that the interior of
\overline{\mathcal{C}^{\prime}}
is
\mathcal{C}
, which is non-empty and is contained
in
\cap_{v\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}}\overline{H_{v}^{+}}
. To prove the inclusion in the opposite direction,
note that a point in the interior of
\cap_{v\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}}\overline{H_{v}^{+}}
has an
open neighbourhood that lies in
H_{v}^{+}
for all
v\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
, therefore lies in the
interior of
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
. This completes the proof.

Applying the above statements to the ample cone of a projective hyperkähler manifold we recover the following theorem proved by

[

]

[

]
(see also

]

Denote

under the natural representation, where

By

is of finite index in

.
Recall the definitions of the cones in the cohomology of

Theorem 3.5:

Assume that

If

Otherwise the boundary of
\overline{\mathcal{M}_{M}}

Proof:

Part 1.
Let
\mathcal{S}=\mathrm{MBM}(M)\cap\operatorname{\rm NS}(M)

\mathcal{A}_{M}=\mathcal{K}_{M}\cap\operatorname{\rm NS}_{\mathbb{R}}(M)
,
we deduce the following from the description of
\mathcal{K}_{M}

\mathcal{A}_{M}
is a connected component of

is
\Gamma

Part 2.
Analogously to part 1, but with

We recall that
\mathrm{PE}(M)\subset\operatorname{\rm NS}(M)
denotes the set of classes of prime exceptional divisors.

Theorem 3.6:

Assume that
M
is a projective hyperkähler manifold with
\rho(M)>2
.
If
\mathrm{PE}(M)
is empty, then
\mathcal{P}_{M}=\overline{\mathcal{C}_{M}}
, otherwise

\mathcal{P}_{M}\cap\operatorname{\rm NS}_{\mathbb{R}}(M)=\overline{\sum_{v\in\mathrm{PE}(M)}\mathbb{R}_{+}\cdot v}.

Proof:

We use the description of
\mathcal{P}_{M}
given in (
2.1
).
The case when
\mathrm{PE}(M)=\emptyset
is clear, so we may assume the contrary.

Denote by
\mathcal{S}
the set of divisorial MBM classes and for
v\in\mathcal{S}

denote
H_{v}^{+}=\{u\in\operatorname{\rm NS}_{\mathbb{R}}(M)\enskip|\enskip q(u,v)>0\}
. Then
\mathcal{S}
defines

\mathcal{C}_{M}\cap\operatorname{\rm NS}_{\mathbb{R}}(M)
and
we denote by
\mathcal{C}

\overline{\mathcal{M}_{M}}

By

The latter inclusion must be equality, because the set of faces of

inside the positive cone is given by the orthogonal

We have shown that
\overline{\mathcal{M}_{M}}=\overline{\mathcal{C}}=\cap_{v\in\mathrm{PE}(M)}\overline{H_{v}^{+}}

The claim now follows from duality between

3.2.2
The case when
\mathcal{S}
is contained in a proper subspace of
V

We go back to the setting introduced in the beginning of section
3.1
.
We additionally assume that there exists a proper subspace
N\subset V
such that
\mathcal{S}\subset N
.
Then
Q^{\circ}=Q\setminus\mathbb{P}(N)
is an open subset of
Q
.
For
v\in\mathcal{S}
let
Q_{v}=Q\cap\mathbb{P}(H_{v})
, where as above

H_{v}=v^{\perp}
.

Lemma 3.7:

The collection of subvarieties
Q_{v}
,
v\in\mathcal{S}
is locally finite in
Q^{\circ}
:
for any
x\in Q^{\circ}
there exists an open subset
U\subset\mathbb{P}(V)

such that
x\in U
and
U
intersects
Q_{v}
only for finitely many
v\in\mathcal{S}
.

Proof:

Let
u\in V
be such that
[u]=x
. The idea is to translate the vector
u

in the direction given by some
w\in N^{\perp}
, so that the translated
vector is contained in the positive cone. Local finiteness of the hyperplanes

H_{v}
,
v\in\mathcal{S}
inside the positive cone is known, so we may find a ball
around the translated vector
that intersects only finite number of the hyperplanes. Translating this ball back,
we get a neighbourhood of
u
that we need. Note that
N^{\perp}\subset H_{v}

for all
v\in\mathcal{S}
, so that all hyperplanes are invariant under translation
in the direction of any
w\in N^{\perp}
.

More precisely, we note that since
x\in Q^{\circ}
,
we have
u\notin N
. The latter implies that there exists
w\in N^{\perp}

with
q(u,w)\neq 0
. It follows that
u^{\prime}=u+t_{0}w\in V^{+}
for some
t_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
.
By
[
SSV
, Proposition 5.12]
the collection of hyperplanes
H_{v}
,
v\in\mathcal{S}

is locally finite in
V^{+}
, so there exists an open ball
B^{\prime}\subset V^{+}
containing
u^{\prime}

that intersects only finitely many hyperplanes
H_{v}
. Set
B=B^{\prime}-t_{0}w
and
note that since
w\in N^{\perp}
the ball
B
also intersects only finitely
many hyperplanes
H_{v}
,
v\in\mathcal{S}
. Defining
U
to be the image of
B
in
\mathbb{P}(V)

we conclude the proof of the claim.

Assume that
\mathcal{C}\subset V^{+}
is one of the
\mathcal{S}
-chambers and
let
\mathscr{B}=\mathbb{P}(\overline{\mathcal{C}})\cap Q
be the isotropic part of the boundary
of
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{C})
. Denote by
\mathscr{B}^{{in}}
the interior of
\mathscr{B}
as a subset
of
Q
.

Proposition 3.8:

In the above setting the set
\mathscr{B}
has the following properties.

(1)

The intersection
\mathscr{B}^{{in}}\cap Q^{\circ}
is non-empty and dense in
\mathscr{B}\cap Q^{\circ}
;

(2)

If there exists
w\in N^{\perp}
with
q(w)<0
then
\mathscr{B}^{{in}}
is dense in
\mathscr{B}
;

(3)

If there exists a subspace
W\subset N^{\perp}
such that
\dim(W)\geq 2
and
q|_{W}
is negative definite,
then
\mathscr{B}^{{in}}
and
\mathscr{B}
are arcwise connected.

Proof:

Proof of (1).
Note that when
q|_{N}
is negative definite, then
\mathcal{S}
is finite, because it is

q
-bounded and discrete. In this case the collection of hyperplanes
H_{v}
,
v\in\mathcal{S}

is finite and this easily implies part (1). It remains to prove (1) when
q|_{N}
is either
degenerate or has mixed signature. Note that in both cases
q|_{N^{\perp}}
is negative semidefinite.

Since
\mathcal{C}\subset V
is open and
N\subset V
is a proper subspace,
there exists
u\in\mathcal{C}\setminus N
. Since
u\notin N
, there exists
w\in N^{\perp}

with
q(u,w)\neq 0
. By the remark above, we may assume that

q(w)\leq 0
. It follows that there exists
t_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
such that
u^{\prime}=u+t_{0}w
is isotropic.
Since for any
v\in\mathcal{S}
and
t\in\mathbb{R}
we have
q(u+tw,v)=q(u,v)\neq 0
, the affine line

u+\mathbb{R}w
does not intersect any of the hyperplanes
H_{v}
,
v\in\mathcal{S}
.
In follows that
\overline{\mathcal{C}}
contains the segment joining
u
and
u^{\prime}
, so
[u^{\prime}]\in\mathscr{B}\cap Q^{\circ}
.
Since
u
lies in the interior of
\overline{\mathcal{C}}
, we have
[u^{\prime}]\in\mathscr{B}^{{in}}\cap Q^{\circ}
,
so
\mathscr{B}^{{in}}\cap Q^{\circ}
is non-empty. The fact that it is dense in
D\cap Q^{\circ}

follows from
3.2.2
: any point
x\in\mathscr{B}\cap Q^{\circ}
has an open
neighbourhood
U\subset\mathbb{P}(V)
that intersects only finitely many hyperplanes
H_{v}
,
v\in\mathcal{S}
.
This finite collection of hyperplanes and the quadric
Q
cut
U
into finitely many
connected components, and
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{C})\cap U
is one of these components. The claim of part (1)
follows easily from this.

Proof of (2).
Let us fix a subspace
W
such that
q|_{W^{\perp}}

is negative definite. By assumption we may find such a
W
with
W^{\perp}
at least
one-dimensional. Then
N\subset W
and therefore
\mathcal{S}\subset W
.

Let
W^{+}=W\cap V^{+}
and
\mathcal{C}_{W}=\mathcal{C}\cap W.
Note that the
linear projection of
V
onto
W
along
W^{\perp}
induces a fibration of
p\colon\mathbb{P}(\overline{V^{+}})\to\mathbb{P}(\overline{W^{+}})
.
The fibre
F_{p}=p^{-1}[v]
over a point
[v]\in\mathbb{P}(\overline{W^{+}})
is isomorphic to the projectivization
of the cone

\{av+w\enskip|\enskip a\in\mathbb{R},\,w\in W^{\perp},\,q(av+w)\geq 0\}\subset W^{\perp}+\mathbb{R}v.

(3.5)

This shows that
F_{p}
is either a closed ball of dimension
\dim(V)-\dim(W)

or a single point.
Since
\mathcal{S}\subset W,
each fibre
F_{p}
is either contained in
\mathbb{P}(H_{v})
or does
not intersect it, for any given
v\in\mathcal{S}
. It follows that
p
induces a fibration

\pi\colon\mathbb{P}(\overline{\mathcal{C}})\to\mathbb{P}(\overline{\mathcal{C}_{W}})
with the same fibres
as
p
. Note the following property of the fibration

: for any
x\in\mathbb{P}(W)\cap Q

it is clear from (

) that
p^{-1}(x)=\{x\}
. Moreover, if

is an arbitrary sequence
of points such that the sequence
\{p(y_{i})\}
converges to
x
, then the sequence
\{y_{i}\}
also converges to
x
.
Analogous statements also hold true for the fibration
\pi
.

If for a point
x\in\mathscr{B}\setminus\mathbb{P}(N)
, we see from part (1) that
x
lies
in the closure of
\mathscr{B}^{{in}}
.
To conclude the proof of part (2), take a point
x\in\mathscr{B}\cap\mathbb{P}(N)
and a sequence of points

y_{i}\in\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{C})
converging to
x
. Since
x\in\mathbb{P}(W)
, we have
\pi(x)=x

and we may choose arbitrary
z_{i}\in\mathscr{B}^{{in}}
such that
\pi(z_{i})=\pi(y_{i})\in\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{C})
.
Then
\{z_{i}\}
converges to
x
by the remark in the end of the previous paragraph.

Proof of (3).
We may now additionally assume that
\dim(W^{\perp})\geq 2
. We use the
fibration
\pi
constructed above. Let
x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathscr{B}^{{in}}
. Then

y_{1}=\pi(x_{1})
and
y_{2}=\pi(x_{2})
lie in
\mathbb{P}(W^{+})
. Connect
y_{1}
to
y_{2}

by a segment and lift this segment to
\mathscr{B}^{{in}}
by taking a section of
\pi
.
This gives an arc connecting
x_{1}
to some point

, where

\pi(x_{2}^{\prime})=\pi(x_{2})
. The condition on the dimension of

implies
that the boundary of the fibre

is a sphere of positive dimension,
in particular it is connected. Therefore we may connect
x_{2}
and

by an
arc lying in that sphere. This proves the claim for

, and the

is analogous.

We now apply the above proposition to the Kähler cone of a hyperkähler manifold.

4
The monodromy group and automorphisms of hyperkähler manifolds

Here we present some basic results about the rational
curves, automorphisms and the Kähler cone of a
hyperkähler manifold. We follow

[
V1
,
AV1
,
AV3
,
AV4
]
.

4.1
The monodromy orbifold of a hyperkähler manifold

We recall that an
orbifold
is a
topological space with a maximal atlas consisting
of quotients of open subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
by finite groups,
with smooth transition functions respecting the group
actions, see
[
MP
]
for a precise definition
and
[
ALR
]
for a detailed discussion. It is sometimes
useful to think of orbifolds in terms of topological
groupoids, see loc. cit. for this point of view.
The basic example (and the only one that we will need)
is the
action groupoid
: if
M
is a smooth manifold
and
\Gamma
is a discrete group acting on
M
properly discontinuously,
then the orbifold groupoid is given by the two maps
s,t\colon\Gamma\times M\to M
,
where
s
is the projection to
M
and
t
is the map defining the action,
see
[
ALR
, section 1]
for details. The orbifold represented by
the action groupoid is the quotient orbifold
M/\Gamma
.

A
covering
of an orbifold is a
map
\pi\colon X_{1}\to X_{2}
which can be locally
represented as
B/\Gamma_{1}{\>\longrightarrow\>}B/\Gamma_{2}
,
where
B\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is an open ball and
\Gamma_{1}\subset\Gamma_{2}
,
and such that any point in
X_{2}
has a neighbourhood

U_{2}
such that its preimage is a union of
open subsets in
X_{1}
mapping to
U_{2}
in this
fashion. An orbifold
X
is called
simply connected

if any connected covering of
X
is isomorphic to
X
.
A
universal covering
of
X
is a connected
covering which is simply connected. One can show
that any orbifold admits an universal covering which is unique up to an isomorphism.
The
fundamental group
of a connected
orbifold
X
is the group of automorphisms
of its universal covering compatible with
the projection to
X
. See
[
ALR
, section 2.2]

for the definition of the orbifold fundamental group
in terms of groupoids.

Going back to the example of the quotient orbifold,
let
\Gamma
be a discrete group acting properly
discontinuously on a contractible manifold
Z
.
Then
Z
is the universal covering of the
orbifold
Z/\Gamma
, and
\pi_{1}(Z/\Gamma)=\Gamma
.

Let
\Gamma\subset SO^{+}(1,n)

be a lattice, i.e. a discrete subgroup of finite covolume.
A
hyperbolic orbifold
is the quotient
{\mathbb{H}}^{m}/\Gamma
of the
hyperbolic space.
It is called a
hyperbolic manifold
if
\Gamma
has
no elements of finite order (then, by the classification of automorphisms of the hyperbolic space,
\Gamma
acts freely on
\mathbb{H}
).

Let
G
be a semisimple algebraic group defined over
{\mathbb{Q}}
.
An
arithmetic lattice
is a subgroup
\Gamma\subset G

which is commensurable with
G_{\mathbb{Z}}
. Recall that by
a theorem of Borel and Harish–Chandra
[
Mo
]
,
for an algebraic Lie group
G
defined over
{\mathbb{Q}}

and without non-trivial rational characters
(such as a semisimple group),
G_{\mathbb{Z}}
has finite covolume.
This theorem implies
that for an arithmetic lattice
\Gamma\subset SO^{+}(1,n)

the quotient
{\mathbb{H}}^{m}/\Gamma
is a hyperbolic orbifold.

We will consider hyperbolic orbifolds that arise from the
action of the automorphism group of a hyperkähler manifold
on its cohomology. For a hyperkähler manifold
M

we will denote by
\operatorname{\sf Diff}_{0}(M)
the connected component of
its diffeomorphism group containing the identity (the group of isotopies).
We will denote by
\operatorname{\sf Comp}(M)
the (infinite-dimensional) space
of all complex structures of hyperkähler type on
M
, and we define
the
Teichmüller space
as
\operatorname{\sf Teich}(M)=\operatorname{\sf Comp}(M)/\operatorname{\sf Diff}_{0}(M)
.
It is known that
\operatorname{\sf Teich}(M)
is a finite-dimensional non-Hausdorff
complex analytic space (see e.g.
[
C
]
,
[
V1
]
).

Let
\Gamma_{0}:=\operatorname{\sf Diff}(M)/\operatorname{\sf Diff}_{0}(M)
denote the mapping class group of
M
.
For a complex structure
I\in\operatorname{\sf Comp}(M)
let

\Gamma^{I}
be the subgroup of
\Gamma_{0}
preserving the
connected component
\operatorname{\sf Teich}^{I}\subset\operatorname{\sf Teich}
. The
image of
\Gamma^{I}
in
O(H^{2}(M,{\mathbb{Z}}))
is
called
the monodromy group
of
(M,I)
,
denoted by
\operatorname{\sf Mon}(M,I)
or
\operatorname{\sf Mon}(M)
when
I
is clear from the context.

Remark 4.1:
This notion is due to E. Markman (
[
Ma
]
).
Monodromy group can be alternatively defined
as the subgroup of
O(H^{2}(M,{\mathbb{Z}}))
generated by
the monodromy operators for all Gauss–Manin local systems
obtained from deformations of
(M,I)
.

Let
I\in\operatorname{\sf Comp}(M)
be a complex structure on
M
.
The
Hodge monodromy group
is the group
\operatorname{\sf Mon}_{I}

of all
s\in\operatorname{\sf Mon}(M,I)\subset O(H^{2}(M,{\mathbb{Z}}))
that preserve the Hodge
decomposition
H^{2}(M,{\mathbb{C}})=H^{2,0}(M)\oplus H^{1,1}(M)\oplus H^{0,2}(M)
.

We recall (see
[
Ma
]
) that
the automorphism group of
(M,I)
is discrete, the restriction map

\operatorname{Aut}(M,I)\to\operatorname{Aut}(M,I)|_{H^{2}(M,{\mathbb{Z}})}

has finite kernel, and its image is the set of all

\tau\in\operatorname{\sf Mon}_{I}
that preserve the Kähler cone. Slightly more generally, the following claim is true.

Claim 4.2:

The orthogonal hyperplanes to the MBM classes partition the positive cone
\mathcal{C}_{M}
in chambers, one of which is the Kähler cone

\mathcal{K}_{M}
. The group
\operatorname{\sf Mon}_{I}
acts on the set of the chambers (called
Kähler chambers
), and its elements which lift to automorphisms of

M
are exactly the ones which preserve
\mathcal{K}_{M}
. The Kähler chambers are the Kähler cones of the birational models of
M
and their monodromy images.

We sometimes denote by
H^{1,1}(M,{\mathbb{Z}})
the group
H^{2}(M,{\mathbb{Z}})\cap H^{1,1}(M)=\operatorname{\rm NS}(M)
.
Clearly,
\operatorname{\sf Mon}_{I}
acts on
H^{1,1}(M,{\mathbb{Z}})
.

Proposition 4.3:

Let
M
be a projective hyperkähler manifold with complex
structure
I
. Then
the natural homomorphism
\operatorname{\sf Mon}_{I}\to O(H^{1,1}(M,{\mathbb{Z}}))

has finite kernel, and it image is of finite index
in
O(H^{1,1}(M,{\mathbb{Z}}))
.

Proof. Step 1:
As shown in
[
V1
]
,
the group
\operatorname{\sf Mon}
has finite index in
O(H^{2}(M,{\mathbb{Z}}))
.
Therefore,
\operatorname{\sf Mon}_{I}
has finite index in the group
O_{I}(H^{2}(M,{\mathbb{Z}}))

of all
s\in O(H^{2}(M,{\mathbb{Z}}))
preserving the Hodge
decomposition. Let
\Lambda\subset H^{2}(M,\mathbb{Z})
be the
orthogonal complement of
H^{1,1}(M,{\mathbb{Z}})
in
H^{2}(M,{\mathbb{Z}})
.
Since we assume that
M
is projective, the lattice

H^{1,1}(M,{\mathbb{Z}})\oplus\Lambda
has finite index in
H^{2}(M,{\mathbb{Z}})
.
It follows that the image of
O_{I}(H^{2}(M,{\mathbb{Z}}))

in
O(H^{1,1}(M,{\mathbb{Z}}))
has finite index.
This implies that the image of

\operatorname{\sf Mon}_{I}
in
O(H^{1,1}(M,{\mathbb{Z}}))

has finite index.

Step 2:

It remains to show that the kernel
of the natural map
\operatorname{\sf Mon}_{I}\to O(H^{1,1}(M,{\mathbb{Z}}))

is finite. This would follow if we prove that
the kernel
K
of the natural map
O_{I}(H^{2}(M,{\mathbb{Z}})){\>\longrightarrow\>}O(H^{1,1}(M,{\mathbb{Z}}))

is finite. Since
M
is projective and
K
acts trivially
on
H^{1,1}(M,\mathbb{Z})
, the group
K
is contained in the group
of automorphisms of the integral polarized Hodge structure
H^{2}(M,\mathbb{Z})
.
The latter group is finite.

The intersection

\operatorname{Pos}_{M}=\mathcal{C}_{M}\cap\big(H^{1,1}(M,{\mathbb{Z}})\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}{\mathbb{R}}\big)

will be called called
the rational positive cone
.
Recall that the intersection
\operatorname{Pos}_{M}\cap\,\mathcal{K}_{M}
is the
ample cone
\mathcal{A}_{M}
. Clearly, we have an exact analogue of
4.1
in this setting. The resulting chambers,
one of which is
\mathcal{A}_{M}
, are called
ample chambers
.
The projectivization
{\mathbb{P}}\operatorname{Pos}_{M}

is a hyperbolic space; assuming that
M
is projective,
it follows from
4.1
that
the group
\operatorname{\sf Mon}_{I}
acts on
{\mathbb{P}}\operatorname{Pos}_{M}
as an arithmetic lattice.

Definition 4.4:

The quotient orbifold
{\mathbb{P}}\operatorname{Pos}_{M}/\operatorname{\sf Mon}_{I}

will be called
the monodromy orbifold
of the hyperkähler
manifold
(M,I)
. Define
the hyperbolic ample polyhedron

as the image of
{\mathbb{P}}\mathcal{A}_{M}
in the monodromy orbifold.

Remark 4.5:
The monodromy orbifold has a finite covering which is a manifold, indeed by Selberg lemma the image of
Mon_{I}
in
O(\operatorname{\rm NS}(M))
has a torsion-free subgroup of finite index, and elements with fixed points on
{\mathbb{P}}\operatorname{Pos}_{M}
are torsion.

4.2
Shape of the Kähler cone

Consider the Kähler cone
\mathcal{K}_{M}
of a hyperkähler manifold
M
.
The Kähler cone is open and convex in
H^{1,1}(M,\mathbb{R})
.
A
wall
of
\mathcal{K}_{M}
is the intersection
of the boundary
\partial\mathcal{K}_{M}
and a hyperplane
W\subset H^{1,1}(M,{\mathbb{R}})

that has a non-empty interior (in both). A
face
of
\mathcal{K}_{M}
is a nontrivial intersection of walls.
A hyperplane
W
supporting a wall of
\mathcal{K}_{M}
is the orthogonal hyperplane to an MBM class. So a face of
\mathcal{K}_{M}
is obtained by intersecting the boundary of
\mathcal{K}_{M}
and a finite number of orthogonal hyperplanes to MBM classes.

The walls and faces are defined in the same way for the ample cone
\mathcal{A}_{M}
of a projective hyperkähler manifold
M
, by considering

\operatorname{\rm NS}_{\mathbb{R}}(M)
instead of
H^{1,1}(M,\mathbb{R})
.

In

[
AV2
]
, it has been proved that the Hodge monodromy group acts with
finitely many orbits on the set of MBM classes of type
(1,1). As a consequence, the following version of the
Kawamata–Morrison conjecture has been obtained.

Theorem 4.6:
[
AV4
]

Let
(M,I)
be a hyperkähler manifold. Then
the set
F
of walls of the Kähler cone
\mathcal{K}_{M}
is
locally finite outside of the boundary of the positive cone,
and the group of complex automorphisms

\operatorname{Aut}(M,I)
acts on
F
with finite number of orbits. If
M
is projective, the same holds for the ample cone.

Remark 4.7:
It follows from the above theorem that the boundary
\partial\mathcal{K}_{M}

of the Kähler cone is the union of the faces
and “the parabolic part”, i.e. the set

\{\eta\in\partial\mathcal{K}_{M}\enskip|\enskip q(\eta)=0\}
.

Definition 4.8:
Let
V
be a quadratic vector space of signature
(1,n)
,
and
{\mathbb{H}}^{n}={\mathbb{P}}V^{+}
the corresponding hyperbolic space.

A hyperbolic hyperplane

in the hyperbolic space
{\mathbb{H}}^{n}={\mathbb{P}}V^{+}
is
the image of
{\mathbb{P}}W^{+}
, where
W\subset V

is a hyperplane of signature
(1,n-1)
.
Note that the set of hyperbolic hyperplanes
is in bijective correspondence with

{\mathbb{P}}V^{-}
, where
V^{-}
is the
set of negative vectors.
A locally geodesic hypersurface

in a hyperbolic orbifold
{\mathbb{H}}^{n}/\Gamma
is
the image of a hyperplane
{\mathbb{P}}W^{+}\subset{\mathbb{H}}^{n}
.
Note that a locally geodesic hypersurface, if it is closed,
is also a hyperbolic orbifold.

Remark 4.9:
A locally geodesic hypersurface is closed if and only if
\Gamma\cap SO(1,n-1)
is a lattice in
SO(1,n-1)
(the stabilizer of
W
).
This is a consequence of Ratner theory when
n>2
, and elementary when
n=2
. In presence of an integral structure, that is, when

{\mathbb{H}}^{n}={\mathbb{P}}V^{+}
with

, and
\Gamma
is arithmetic, this amounts to saying that
W
is rational. Of course the same applies to the case when
W\subset V
is a subspace of higher codimension
k
(but still of signature
(1,n-k)
). One calls
{\mathbb{P}}W^{+}

a hyperbolic subspace
, and its image in

{\mathbb{H}}^{n}/\Gamma

a locally geodesic (immersed) submanifold
.

Definition 4.10:

Let
{\cal H}={\mathbb{H}}^{n}/\Gamma

be a hyperbolic orbifold, and
S_{1},...,S_{n}\subset{\cal H}

be a collection of closed locally geodesic hypersurfaces.
A
convex hyperbolic orbifold with
polyhedral boundary
(CHOPB)
is a connected component of the complement

{\cal H}\backslash\bigcup_{i}S_{i}
.

Theorem 4.11:

Let
(M,I)
be a projective hyperkähler manifold,
and

be its hyperbolic ample polyhedron.
Then
{\cal P}
is a convex hyperbolic orbifold with

and

\pi_{1}({\cal P})
is equal to the image of

\operatorname{Aut}(M,I)
in

. Moreover,
the natural surjection
\operatorname{Aut}(M,I)\to\pi_{1}({\cal P})

Proof:
The orthogonal hyperplanes to the MBM classes of type
(1,1)
partition
{\mathbb{P}}\operatorname{Pos}_{M}
in chambers, one of which is
{\mathbb{P}}\mathcal{A}_{M}
.
Let
{\cal M}={\mathbb{P}}\operatorname{Pos}_{M}/\operatorname{\sf Mon}_{I}

.
The preimage of
{\cal P}\subset{\cal M}

in the universal cover
{\mathbb{P}}\operatorname{Pos}_{M}
of

is the union of
{\mathbb{P}}\mathcal{A}_{M}
and some other chambers which are its
\operatorname{\sf Mon}_{I}
-translates, and
{\mathbb{P}}\mathcal{A}_{M}
is clearly
the universal covering of
{\cal P}
.
Let

be the closed hypersurfaces obtained

. Then

is a connected component

.
As follows from the

]

5
Limit points of the automorphism group

5.1
The absolute and the limit
set of a group acting by hyperbolic isometries

Definition 5.1:
Let
V
be a real vector space equipped with
a scalar product
q
of signature
(1,n)
,
and
V^{+}
the set of all vectors with positive square.
Then
{\mathbb{H}}^{n}={\mathbb{P}}V^{+}
is the hyperbolic space
of constant negative curvature.

The absolute

\operatorname{Abs}
is
the projectivization of the set of all vectors
with square 0; it is identified with the boundary
of
{\mathbb{H}}^{n}
. Clearly,
\operatorname{Abs}
is diffeomorphic to
the sphere
S^{n-1}
, and the union
{\mathbb{H}}^{n}\cup\operatorname{Abs}

Remark 5.2:
Let
W\subset V
be a 2-dimensional subspace
of signature (1,1). We set
W^{+}=V^{+}\cap W
.
Then
{\mathbb{P}}W^{+}\subset{\mathbb{H}}

is a geodesic in the hyperbolic space, and all
geodesics are obtained this way.

Definition 5.3: The endpoints

of a geodesic
l={\mathbb{P}}W^{+}

are two points
\operatorname{Abs}\cap{\mathbb{P}}(W)
.

Remark 5.4:
Clearly, any geodesic is uniquely determined
by its two endpoints. This defines a bijection
between the set of geodesic lines in
{\mathbb{H}}^{n}

and the set of pairs of distinct points in
\operatorname{Abs}
.

Claim 5.5:

Let
l_{1}
,
l_{2}
be two geodesics
on a hyperbolic space, and
\delta:\;l_{1}{\>\longrightarrow\>}{\mathbb{R}}

the distance from a point of
l_{1}
to
l_{2}
.
Let
\infty_{+}
,
\infty_{-}
be the endpoints of
l_{1}
,
considered as points in
\operatorname{Abs}
.
Then
\lim_{x{\>\longrightarrow\>}\infty_{+}}\delta(x)=\infty

if
\infty_{+}
is not an endpoint of
l_{2}
, and

\lim_{x{\>\longrightarrow\>}\infty_{+}}\delta(x)=0
otherwise.

Proof:
Left as an exercise.

Remark 5.6:
5.1
is equivalent to the following
geometric observation. Given a constant
R>0
, denote by
l(R)

the
R
-neighbourhood of
l
in
{\mathbb{H}}
.
Then the ends of
l
are the only accumulation points
of
l(R)
on
\operatorname{Abs}
.

Claim 5.7:
Let
\Gamma\subset SO(1,n)

be a group of hyperbolic isometries
acting on
{\mathbb{H}}^{n}
, and

a,b\in{\mathbb{H}}^{n}

any two points. Let
\Lambda_{a},\Lambda_{b}\subset\operatorname{Abs}

be the set of all points in
\operatorname{Abs}

obtained as accumulation points
for
\Gamma\cdot a,\Gamma\cdot b
.
Then
\Lambda_{a}=\Lambda_{b}
.

Proof:
Let
x\in\Lambda_{a}
, and
\{y_{i}=\gamma_{i}a\}\in\Gamma\cdot a

be a sequence converging to
x
. Since
\gamma_{i}
are isometries,

, and
the sequence
\gamma_{i}b
converges to
x
as well.
Indeed, since
{\mathbb{H}}^{n}\cup\operatorname{Abs}
is compact,
otherwise we would have a point
y\neq x
which is
a limit of a subsequence
\gamma_{i}b
.
However, for any two sequences of points

\{x_{i}\}
converging to
x
and
\{y_{i}\}

converging to

, we have
\lim_{i}d(x_{i},y_{i})=\infty
,
giving a contradiction.

Definition 5.8:
In these assumptions,

the limit set
of the action of
\Gamma
on
{\mathbb{H}}
is

\Lambda_{a}
, for some (and, therefore, any)

.

Remark 5.9:
Let
\Lambda
be the limit set of
\Gamma
. Clearly,

\Gamma
acts on
\operatorname{Abs}\backslash\Lambda
properly
discontinuously.

5.2
Convex hyperbolic orbifolds with

Definition 5.10:
Let
V_{\mathbb{Z}}
be a lattice

(1,n)
,

, and
\Gamma\subset SO^{+}(V)

.
Then

is called

,
or
of first type
(for an explanation of this terminology,

[

]

We sometimes use the notation

for

.

Remark 5.11:
Let
\Gamma\subset SO^{+}(1,n)
be a group of isometries
of finite covolume (that is, a lattice),
and
{\mathbb{P}}W^{+}\subset{\mathbb{P}}V^{+})

a hyperbolic subspace. Clearly,
the image of
{\mathbb{P}}W^{+}
in

{\mathbb{P}}V^{+}/\Gamma

as
{\mathbb{P}}W^{+}/\Gamma_{W}
, where
\Gamma_{W}

is
\{\gamma\in\Gamma\ \ |\ \ \gamma(W)=W\}

Any integer lattice in

By Ratner’s theorem, any geodesic immersed submanifold
of

{\mathbb{P}}W^{+}/\Gamma_{W}
,
for some rational subspace

.
This implies the following description of
a convex hyperbolic orbifold with
polyhedral boundary (CHOPB) in arithmetic
hyperbolic manifolfds.

Claim 5.12:
Let
V_{\mathbb{Z}}
be a lattice
equipped with a scalar product of signature

(1,n)
, and
\Gamma\subset SO^{+}(V)

a subgroup commensurable with
SO(V_{\mathbb{Z}})
.
Consider a finite collection of
rational hyperplanes
W_{i}\subset V
of
signature
(1,n-1)
, let
{\mathfrak{S}}

be
\bigcup_{i}\Gamma W_{i}
, and

P
a connected component
of
{\mathbb{P}}V^{+}\backslash{\mathbb{P}}{\mathfrak{S}}
.
Denote by
\Gamma_{P}
the group
\{\gamma\in\Gamma\ \ |\ \ \gamma(P)=P\}
.
Then

is a convex hyperbolic orbifold with

polyhedral boundary in
{\mathbb{P}}V^{+}/\Gamma
are obtained this way.

The following question is crucial for this paper.

Question 5.13:
What is the limit set of
\Gamma_{P}
acting on
P
?

The reason is the following theorem, true for all
hyperbolic manifolds, but it is much easier
to state and prove it when
{\cal H}
is
compact. In Subsection
5.4

we will explain how to generalize it
to all hyperbolic
{\cal H}
.

Theorem 5.14:

Let
{\cal H}:={\mathbb{P}}V^{+}/\Gamma

be a hyperbolic orbifold, and
{\cal P}\subset{\cal H}

a convex hyperbolic orbifold with
polyhedral boundary. Let
P
be a connected
component of the preimage of
{\cal P}

in
{\mathbb{P}}V^{+}={\mathbb{H}}
; clearly,

P
is a convex polyhedron in
{\mathbb{H}}

with hyperbolic faces. Denote by
\operatorname{Abs}P

the set
\operatorname{Abs}\cap\overline{P}
.
Assume that
{\cal H}
is compact.
Then
\operatorname{Abs}P
is the limit set of

\Gamma_{P}
acting on
P
.

Proof. Step 1:
Clearly, for any
x\in P
, its orbit
belongs to
P
, hence its limit set
belongs to
\operatorname{Abs}P
.

Step 2:

Conversely,
let
x\in\operatorname{Abs}P
,
and let
l
be a geodesic
with an end in
x
. We may choose
l
so that contains a point in
P
.
Since
P
is convex, a ray
l_{+}\subset l
converging to
x

also belongs to
P
. Choose a fundamental domain

D
of
\Gamma_{P}
-action on
P
.
Since
\overline{P}/\Gamma_{P}
is a closed subset of
{\cal H}
,
this space is compact, hence

can be chosen relatively compact.
Let

.

Step 3:

Choose
z\in D

{\operatorname{\sf diam}}D=R

R
-neighbourhood
of any point

contains
\gamma z
for
some
\gamma\in\Gamma_{P}
. Choose a sequence
of points

converging to

,
and let
\gamma_{i}z
be points which satisfy

Then

is a point in an

.

5.3
The thick-thin decomposition

Here we give a brief introduction to cusp
points on hyperbolic manifolds. For
more details, see
[
Th
,
Ka3
]
.

Definition 5.15: A horosphere
on a hyperbolic space is
a sphere which is everywhere orthogonal to the pencil
of geodesics passing through a given point at infinity, and

a horoball
is a ball bounded by a horosphere.

A cusp point
for an

n
-dimensional hyperbolic manifold
{\mathbb{H}}/\Gamma

is a point on the boundary
\partial{\mathbb{H}}

such that its stabilizer in
\Gamma

contains a free abelian group of rank
n-1
.
Such subgroups are called
maximal parabolic
.
Horospheres are orbits of maximal parabolic
subgroups, and all orbits of maximal parabolic
subgroups are horospheres.

Claim 5.16:
Let
V
be a quadratic vector space of signature

(1,n)
, and
\Gamma\subset SO^{+}(V)
a discrete
subroup of finite covolume.
Consider a point
p\in\partial{\mathbb{H}}
, and let

\Gamma_{p}\subset\Gamma
be its stabilizer. Clearly,

\Gamma_{p}
acts on any horosphere
S
tangent to the
boundary in
p
by isometries.
In such a situation,
p
is a cusp point
if and only if
(S\backslash p)/\Gamma_{p}

is compact.

Remark 5.17:
A cusp point
p
yields a
cusp

in the quotient
{\mathbb{H}}/\Gamma
, that is,
a geometric end of
{\mathbb{H}}/\Gamma

of the form
B/{\mathbb{Z}}^{n-1}
, where

B\subset{\mathbb{H}}
is a horoball tangent
to the boundary at
p
.

(Thick-thin decomposition)

a union of a “thick part”, which is a compact manifold

, where
B
is a horoball
tangent to the boundary at a cusp point
p
, and

\Gamma_{p}
its stabilizer in
\Gamma
. Moreover,

\Gamma_{p}
is isomorphic to
{\mathbb{Z}}^{n-1}
, and

B/\Gamma_{p}
is diffeomorphic to a product of a torus

and an interval.

Proof:
See

[
Th

or

[
Ka3

.

{\cal H}

Clearly,
{\cal P}
is diffeomorphic to a cylinder, hence

\pi_{1}({\cal P})={\mathbb{Z}}
. The corresponding free geodesic
\gamma
in
{\cal P}

has finite length, hence it corresponds to a hyperbolic automorphism
of
{\mathbb{H}}^{2}
preserving
P
. This implies that
\pi_{1}({\cal P})

has only two limit points, which are the ends of

The group
\pi_{1}({\cal P})
freely acts on the set of
cusp points on

6
The Apollonian carpet

6.1
Real analytic intervals in the limit set

Definition 6.1:
Let
V_{\mathbb{Z}}
be a lattice
equipped with a scalar product of signature

(1,n)
, and
\Gamma\subset SO^{+}(V)

a subgroup commensurable with
SO(V_{\mathbb{Z}})
.
Consider a convex hyperbolic orbifold

{\cal P}\subset{\cal H}
with
polyhedral boundary. Let
P
be a connected
component of the preimage of
{\cal P}

in
{\mathbb{P}}V^{+}={\mathbb{H}}
, and

\operatorname{Abs}P
the set of accumulation points
of
P
on
\operatorname{Abs}
.
The Apollonian carpet
associated to
{\cal P}

is the union of all positive-dimensional
real analytic subvarieties in
\operatorname{Abs}P
.

We start by proving that the
Apollonian carpet is a union of geodesic spheres
in
\operatorname{Abs}
; this result follows from a theorem of N. Shah.

Theorem 6.2:

Let
{\cal H}
be an arithmetic hyperbolic
manifold,
{\cal H}={\mathbb{P}}V^{+}/\Gamma
, where

\Gamma
is commensurable to

SO(V_{\mathbb{Z}})
,
and
{\cal P}\subset{\cal H}
a convex hyperbolic
orbifold with polyhedral boundary. Denote by
P\subset\mathbb{H}
a connected component of its preimage, and
let
\Gamma_{P}\subset\Gamma
be its stabilizer.
Then the Apollonian carpet is a union of spheres

\operatorname{Abs}\cap{\mathbb{P}}W_{i}
, where

W_{i}\subset V
is a rational
subspace of signature
(1,k)
.
Moreover, the group
\Gamma_{P}

acts on the set
\{W_{i}\}
with finitely many orbits.

Proof:
See Subsection
6.3
.

Definition 6.3:
A
component
of an Apollonian carpet
A

is a sphere
\operatorname{Abs}\cap{\mathbb{P}}W_{1}\subset A

which is not contained in another sphere

\operatorname{Abs}\cap{\mathbb{P}}W_{2}\subset A
of greater dimension.

Remark 6.4:
From
6.1
it follows that
an Apollonian carpet is a union of its components.

Remark 6.5:
Note that
the components of an Apollonian carpet
can be disjoint, tangent or intersect each other;
see examples in
Subsection
7.3
.

6.2
Shah’s theorem on ergodic properties of the geodesic flow

Let
ST{\mathbb{H}}^{n}
be the manifold
of unit tangent vectors to the hyperbolic space. We use

\operatorname{Vis}:\;ST{\mathbb{H}}^{n}{\>\longrightarrow\>}\operatorname{Abs}
to denote
the map taking a tangent vector to the limit
point of the corresponding geodesic. If
{\cal H}={\mathbb{H}}^{n}/\Gamma
is a hyperbolic manifold and
(x,v)\in ST{\cal H}
, we
define
\operatorname{Vis}(x,v)
by fixing a point in the preimage of
x
in
{\mathbb{H}}^{n}
and lifting the geodesic to
{\mathbb{H}}^{n}
, so that
\operatorname{Vis}
is defined up to
\Gamma
-action. The definition also makes sense for orbifolds for a suitable definition of
ST
at the singular points. In our situation every orbifold has a finite cover which is a manifold (
4.1
), and for our purposes we may assume it is already a manifold.

Definition 6.6:
Let
(x,v)\in ST{\mathbb{H}}^{n}
,
with
v\in T_{x}{\mathbb{H}}^{n}
being a unit tangent vector.
Denote by
l_{x,v}(t)
the geodesic starting
to
x
and tangent to
v
. The

geodesic flow
is a measure-preserving
flow of diffeomorphisms
G_{t}:\;ST{\mathbb{H}}^{n}\times{\mathbb{R}}{\>\longrightarrow\>}ST{\mathbb{H}}^{n}

taking
((x,v),t)
to the tangent vector
l_{x,v}^{\prime}(t)
. Similarly, one defines the geodesic flow
g_{t}
on
{\cal H}
.

Remark 6.7:
By E. Hopf theorem, the geodesic flow on a
hyperbolic manifold is ergodic. The closure of
a given geodesic on a hyperbolic manifold or orbifold might be very pathological, such as
a product of a Cantor set and an interval.

By Ratner theorem on homogeneous flow, this pathology does not happen when instead of a geodesic, one considers the image in

{\cal H}
of a hyperbolic subspace of dimension
k>1
in
{\mathbb{H}}^{n}
: the closure is a hyperbolic submanifold, that is, the image of a rational subspace in
{\mathbb{H}}^{n}
. The following theorem by N. Shah goes further in this direction.

Theorem 6.8:
[
Sh
, Theorem 1.2]

Let
{\cal H}={\mathbb{H}}^{n}/\Gamma
be a hyperbolic manifold, and

C\subset ST{\cal H}
a real analytic interval, such that
\operatorname{Vis}(C)

is not a singleton. Then there is a hyperbolic subspace
{\mathbb{H}}^{m}\subset{\mathbb{H}}^{n}
with
\operatorname{Vis}(C)\subset\partial{\mathbb{H}}^{m}

such that the image
{\cal H}_{1}
of
{\mathbb{H}}^{m}
in
{\cal H}
is a closed submanifold, and for any function
f\in C_{c}(ST{\cal H})

\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{|I|}\int_{I}f(g_{t}\psi(s))ds=\int_{ST{\cal H}_{1}}f|_{ST{\cal H}_{1}},

where
\psi:I\to C
is a parametrization of
C
by the unit interval and the integration is taken with respect to the normalized volume form.

In particular, taking
f
which is zero outside of a small neighbourhood of some point
x\in ST{\cal H}_{1}
, one obtains that
x
is a limit point of geodesic rays starting at points of
C
, cf.
[
Sh
, Remark 1.1]
. In the same remark, it is explained how to obtain
{\cal H}_{1}
: choose the smallest subsphere of
\partial{\mathbb{H}}^{n}
containing
\operatorname{Vis}(C)
, it is the absolute of a hyperbolic subspace
{\mathbb{H}}^{k}
, and
{\cal H}_{1}
is the closure of the image of
{\mathbb{H}}^{k}
in
{\cal H}
.

6.3
Apollonian carpet is a union of geodesic spheres

We are now in a position to prove
6.1
.

Proof. Step 1:
Let
C\subset\operatorname{Abs}P
be a real analytic curve.
Connecting a point in
P
with
C
by a real analytic
family of geodesics, we obtain a real analytic path

C_{1}\subset ST{\cal P}
satisfying the assumptions of Shah’s theorem.
By Shah’s theorem,
the closure of the image of a general geodesic on closure of the CHOPB
{\cal P}
contains a closed hyperbolic submanifold
{\cal H}_{1}
. It follows that a component
{\mathbb{H}}^{m}
of the preimage of
{\cal H}_{1}
in
{\mathbb{H}}^{n}
is contained in the closure of
P
, and its boundary is a sphere
S
,
C\subset S\subset\operatorname{Abs}P
.

Step 2:

Consider a vector subspace
W\subset V
, such that
S={\mathbb{P}}W\cap\operatorname{Abs}
. The convex hull of
S
is
the hyperbolic subspace
{\mathbb{P}}W^{+}\subset{\mathbb{P}}V^{+}
.
By Ratner theorem, its image in
{\cal H}={\mathbb{P}}V^{+}/\Gamma

is closed if and only if

\Gamma_{W}:=\{\gamma\in\Gamma\ \ |\ \ \gamma(W)=W\}
is a
lattice in
O(W)
, which happens if and only if
W
is rational. So
W
must be rational.

Step 3:

For a component
S={\mathbb{P}}W\cap\operatorname{Abs}

of the Apollonian carpet, denote by
H_{S}\subset{\cal H}

the image of
{\mathbb{P}}W^{+}
in
{\cal H}
. We claim that there is only a finite number of
H_{S}
.
By Mozes-Shah and Dani-Margulis theorems
(
[
MS
]
, Corollaries 1.1, 1.3, 1.4),
the closure of the union of countably many such subsets
is a finite union of subsets of the same shape
(i.e. images of
{\mathbb{P}}U^{+}
for possibly larger rational

U
). If there is an infinite number of
H_{S}
, we thus
obtain a closed hyperbolic submanifold
{\cal H}^{\prime}
of
greater dimension as the closure of some union of them. By
maximality of the
H_{S}
,
{\cal H}^{\prime}
cannot coincide with
any of them. But the
H_{S}
are contained in the
the CHOPB
{\cal P}
, hence

{\cal H}^{\prime}
belongs to the closure

{\cal P}
. It follows that there is an extra sphere

\operatorname{Vis}(ST{\cal H}^{\prime})
in
\operatorname{Abs}P
, a contradiction. Hence, up to the

\Gamma_{P}
-action, the number of components of the
Apollonian carpet is finite.

6.4
Closure of the Apollonian carpet

Recall that a group
\Gamma\subset SO^{+}(1,n)

is called

is its limit set has at most 2
points.

Theorem 6.9:
Let

be a non-elementary

Then the closure of an orbit

.

[

, Theorem 1.2.24]
.

This result immediately brings the following corollary.

Corollary 6.10:

Let
{\cal H}
be a hyperbolic manifold,
and
{\cal P}\subset{\cal H}
a CHOPB.
Denote by

7
Apollonian carpet of a hyperkähler manifold

7.1
Apollonian carpet and MBM classes

Definition 7.1: The Apollonian carpet of a hyperkähler manifold

is the union of all geodesic spheres of positive dimension which
belong to the absolute of its ample cone.

Remark 7.2:
Clearly, the Apollonian carpet of a hyperkähler manifold
is the Apollonian carpet of the corresponding
convex hyperbolic orbifold with
polyhedral boundary
\overline{{\cal P}}\subset{\cal H}
,
obtained as the image of
\overline{{\mathbb{P}}\operatorname{Amp}}
in

{\cal H}={\mathbb{P}}\operatorname{Pos}_{M}/\operatorname{\sf Mon}_{I}

(
4.2
).

The Apollonian carpet of a hyperkähler manifold
can be described explicitly in terms of its BBF form
and MBM classes, as follows.

Theorem 7.3:

Let
M
be a projective hyperkähler manifold, and

NS(M)=H^{1,1}(M,{\mathbb{R}})\cap H^{2}(M,{\mathbb{Z}})
its
Néron–Severi lattice. Consider the set
of all maximal rational subspaces
W_{i}\subset NS_{\mathbb{R}}

of signature
(1,k)
,
k\geqslant 2
such that for any
MBM class
\eta\in NS(M)
with
\eta\not\!\!\bot W_{i}
,
we have that
W_{i}^{\bot}+\eta
is not negative definite. Then
the sphere
S_{i}=\operatorname{Abs}\cap{\mathbb{P}}W_{i}

lies in the closure of some ample chamber (which can be seen as the ample cone of
(M,I_{i})
for a complex structure
I_{i}
).
Moreover
S_{i}
belongs to the Apollonian carpet of

(M,I_{i})
, and all components of the
Apollonian carpet of
(M,I_{i})
are obtained this way. Finally, the group of automorphisms of
(M,I_{i})
contains a lattice in

SO(1,k)=\operatorname{Aut}W_{i}
, which is commensurable to
SO(W_{i},{\mathbb{Z}})
.

Remark 7.4:
The subspace
W_{i}
as above can lie in
\eta^{\bot}
for some MBM class
\eta
. The space generated by all such
\eta
for a given
W_{i}
is negative definite. If the addition of an extra MBM class
\eta^{\prime}
destroys negativity, this means that
W_{i}
supports a face of an ample chamber which does not contain other faces, in other words,
W_{i}^{+}
itself is a minimal face of the ample chamber. Changing the complex structure if necessary, we may assume that the chamber is the ample cone.

Proof of the theorem, step 1:

When
W_{i}^{+}
is a face of the ample cone as in
7.1
, clearly
\operatorname{Abs}{\mathbb{P}}W_{i}^{+}
is a sphere on the boundary of the ample cone and a component of Apollonian carpet.

In general, let
U_{1},U_{2}\subset V
be spaces of negative signature
of a space of signature
(1,n)
. Then
{\mathbb{P}}(U_{1}^{+})^{\bot}

intersects
{\mathbb{P}}(U_{2}^{+})^{\bot}
if and only if

U_{1}+U_{2}
is negative definite. If
U_{1}+U_{2}

is degenerate, the spheres
\operatorname{Abs}{\mathbb{P}}(U_{1}^{+})^{\bot}

and
\operatorname{Abs}{\mathbb{P}}(U_{2}^{+})^{\bot}
are tangent.

Take a
W_{i}
which is not contained in
\eta^{\bot}
with
\eta
MBM. The condition of the theorem:
W_{i}^{\bot}+\eta
is not negative definite means that
S_{i}
does
not meet any orthogonal hyperpane to an MBM class except maybe tangentially. This means that
S_{i}
is contained in a chamber which, as before, we assume to be the ample cone. Therefore
S_{i}
is again a component of the Apollonian carpet.

Step 2:

Let
S_{i}
be a component of the Apollonian
carpet of
(M,I)
. Then
S_{i}=\operatorname{Abs}\cap{\mathbb{P}}W_{i}
,
where
W_{i}
is a rational subspace. Since
W_{i}
intersects the positive cone, it must have signature
(1,k)
.
Also,
S_{i}
is contained in the closure of the ample cone. The ample cone is cut inside the positive cone by the orthogonal
hyperplanes to MBM classes. We derive that for
\eta
MBM,
S_{i}
cannot intersect
\eta^{\bot}
transversally (it is disjoint, tangent to or contained in
\eta^{\bot}
). This amounts to saying that
W_{i}^{\bot}+\eta
is not negative definite when
W_{i}
is not orthogonal to
\eta
.

Step 3:

Denote by
\Gamma_{I}

the group of all
\nu\in O^{+}(H^{2}(M,{\mathbb{Z}}))

preserving the Hodge decomposition. Then

{\cal H}:={\mathbb{P}}\operatorname{Pos}_{M}/\Gamma_{I}

is a hyperbolic manifold, containing the closure of the
image of the projectivization of the ample cone
as a convex hyperbolic orbifold with
polyhedral boundary. Denote by
\Gamma\subset\Gamma_{I}

its fundamental group; by

4.2
,

\Gamma
is commensurable with
\operatorname{Aut}(M,I)
. Recall that the elements of
\Gamma_{I}
permute the chambers of the decomposition given by MBM classes, and
\Gamma
is the subgroup of those elements in
\Gamma_{I}
which preserve one of the chambers, namely the ample cone.

Let
S_{i}
be a component of the Apollonian carpet and
W_{i}
the associated linear subspace as above. Set
\Gamma_{W_{i}}:=\{\gamma\in\Gamma_{I}\ \ |\ \ \Gamma(W_{i})=W_{i}\}
.
Then
\Gamma_{W_{i}}
is a lattice in
O(W_{i})
. If
S_{i}
is not contained in the orthogonal to an MBM class, then
S_{i}
intersects all

\eta^{\bot}
, where
\eta
is MBM, at most tangentially, and therefore an element
\gamma
of
\Gamma_{W_{i}}
, preserving
S_{i}
, must also
preserve its chamber, i.e. the ample cone. Thus
\gamma
lifts to an automorphism of
(M,I)
. If
S_{i}
is contained in the orthogonal
to an MBM class, then
{\mathbb{P}}Pos(W_{i})
is a face of the ample cone, and
\gamma\in\Gamma_{W_{i}}
can also take the ample cone to another chamber adjacent to
{\mathbb{P}}Pos(W_{i})
, but these are finitely many. So a finite index subgroup of
\Gamma_{W_{i}}
lifts to a subgroup in

\operatorname{Aut}(M,I)
, q.e.d..

7.2
The Baragar gasket

In the proof of
7.1

we have seen that the Apollonian carpet has two types of components: the ones which come from the faces of the ample cone, and the ones which do not.

More precisely, let
F
,
\dim F>2
, be a
face of the ample cone which is minimal with respect to inclusion, that is,
L
is the intersection of orthogonals to MBM classes which does not meet other orthogonals to MBM classes except possibly tangentially. Then
F=L^{+}
for a vector subspace
L
, and

{\mathbb{P}}L^{+}\cap\operatorname{Abs}
is
a component of the Apollonian carpet.

In the case of K3 surfaces, the union of such components has been studied by A. Baragar, see e.g.
[
Ba1
]
. Hence the following definition.

Definition 7.5:

The union of all components of the Apollonian carpet
obtained this way is called
the Baragar
gasket
.

Remark 7.6:
The Baragar components do not intersect transversally, but
they might be tangential. Other components of the
Apollonian gasket can intersect (
7.3
), but they cannot
intersect the Baragar components transversally. In the
CHOPB setting, the Baragar components correspond
to the hyperspheres which are obtained as isotropic
boundaries of faces of the CHOPB which don’t contain
faces of smaller dimension, and hence are given
by a hyperbolic subspace which lies fully on
a boundary of the CHOPB.

Remark 7.7:
This gives an effective and easy way to
determine the Apollonian carpet for particular manifolds
when we have good control over the BBF form, the
Néron–Severi lattice and the MBM classes. Note that
by Nikulin’s theorem (
[
Ni
]
), any even quadratic
lattice of signature
(1,n)
,
n\leqslant 10

can be realized as the Néron–Severi lattice for an appropriate K3
surface. Note also that for a K3 surface, MBM classes are integer
classes with square -2.

7.3
Examples

We give several examples illustrating the shape of the Apollonian carpet and Baragar gasket. For simplicity, we restrict ourselves to K3 surfaces.
As we have already observed, a sphere on the boundary of the projectivized positive cone is the intersection of this boundary with a linear subspace
W
, and the BBF form
q
(the intersection
form in this case) is negative definite on
W^{\bot}
. This sphere belongs to an ample chamber if it is either
disjoint from the walls, or intersects them tangentially. This condition means that adding a
(-2)
-class
z
to
W^{\bot}
destroys negativity:

z^{\bot}
is disjoint from the sphere iff
q
is of signature
(1,\dim W^{\bot})
on
W^{\bot}+z
, and tangent to the sphere iff
q
is
negative semi-definite on
W^{\bot}+z
. Finally, such a sphere is a Baragar component when
W^{\bot}
is generated by integral classes of square
-2
, and not a Baragar component otherwise.

To calculate the signature of
q
on the relevant subspaces of the real Neron-Severi group, we prove some auxiliary results.

Lemma 7.8:

Assume that
L\subset\Lambda
is a primitive sublattice, and denote by
A
the
quotient
\Lambda/L
. Then we have the following exact triples:

\displaystyle 0\to A\to\Lambda^{\vee}/L\to D(\Lambda)\to 0

(7.1)

\displaystyle 0\to A^{\vee}\to\Lambda^{\vee}/L\to D(L)\to 0

(7.2)

Proof:

By the definition of
D(\Lambda)
we have an exact triple

0\to\Lambda\to\Lambda^{\vee}\to D(\Lambda)\to 0.

Taking the quotient of the first two terms by
L
, we obtain (
7.1
).

Since the sublattice
L
is primitive, the quotient
A=\Lambda/L
is free, so we have an exact triple

0\to A^{\vee}\to\Lambda^{\vee}\to L^{\vee}\to 0.

Taking the quotient of the last two terms by
L
, we obtain (
7.2
).

Proposition 7.9:

Let
(\Lambda,q)
be a quadratic lattice of rank
r
for some
r\geq 3
.
Assume that the
\mathbb{F}_{p}
-vector
space
D(\Lambda)\otimes\mathbb{F}_{p}
has dimension
r-k
. Then for any primitive sublattice
L
of rank
l>k
,
p
divides
|D(L)|
.

Proof:

Applying
7.3
and using our assumption
on
D(\Lambda)
, we deduce from the sequence (
7.1
) by tensoring it with
\mathbb{F}_{p}

that the dimension of
(\Lambda^{\vee}/L)\otimes\mathbb{F}_{p}
is at least
r-k
. On the other
hand,
A^{\vee}
is a free abelian group of rank
r-l
, and we deduce from the sequence (
7.2
)
by tensoring it with
\mathbb{F}_{p}
that
D(L)\otimes\mathbb{F}_{p}
is at least one-dimensional.
It follows that the group
D(L)
must contain
p^{k}
-torsion for some
k
,
in particular
p
divides
|D(L)|
.

The proof of the following two corollaries is an easy exercise, left to the reader.

Corollary 7.10:
Let
(\Lambda,q)
be a quadratic lattice of signature
(1,r-1)
for some
r\geq 3
, with

D(\Lambda)\otimes\mathbb{F}_{p}
of dimension
r-1
. Let
L
be a rank-two
sublattice generated by
q
-negative vectors
v_{1}
,
v_{2}
with
q(v_{1})q(v_{2})<p
. Then
L
is either degenerate and negative semi-definite,
or has signature
(1,1)
.

Corollary 7.11:
Let
(\Lambda,q)
be a quadratic lattice of signature
(1,r-1)
for some
r\geq 3
, with

D(\Lambda)\otimes\mathbb{F}_{p}
of dimension
r-2
, where
p\geqslant 5
. Let
L
be a rank-three sublattice generated by three elements of square
-2

is of signature
(1,2)
or
(0,2)
(i.e. degenerate negative semi-definite).

Example 7.12:
Consider the lattice
\Lambda
of rank four with the quadratic form
q(x,y,z,w)=2px^{2}-2y^{2}-2pz^{2}-2pw^{2}

for some prime number
p\geqslant 5
. This is an even lattice of signature
(1,3)
. By the classical results
about lattice embeddings (
[
Ni
]
, see also
[

, section 3.3]
),
\Lambda
can be primitively embedded into the K3 lattice
\Lambda_{K3}
,
therefore

can be realized as the Néron–Severi lattice of an algebraic K3 surface. Set

and consider
{\mathbb{H}}^{3}={\mathbb{P}}V^{+}
. Then
\operatorname{Abs}=\partial{\mathbb{H}}^{3}=S^{2}
.
It is clear that the set of classes of square
-2
is non-empty. By

, the circles on

cut out by the orthogonal hyperplanes to
(-2)
-classes

and

Therefore we obtain a circle packing on the boundary of the positive cone of the K3 surface
and a non-trivial Baragar gasket (see

) in the closure of its ample cone.

Example 7.13:
Note that in the example above, some spheres of the Baragar gasket may be tangent.
Let us construct an example where all of the spheres are disjoint. Note that the spheres
defined by two vectors
v_{1},v_{2}\in\Lambda
(as

) are tangent if and only if the restriction
of

to
\langle v_{1},v_{2}\rangle
is degenerate. In particular, this implies that
\Lambda

contains non-trivial isotropic vectors (they correspond to the points of tangency of the spheres).
Therefore to construct our example it is sufficient to produce an even lattice without non-zero
isotropic elements such that it contains classes of square
-2
and satisfies the conditions
of
7.3
for some prime
p\geq 5
.

Start from the rank four lattice
\Lambda_{0}
with the quadratic form
q_{0}(x,y,z,w)=3x^{2}-y^{2}-7z^{2}-7w^{2}
.
Let us observe that
\Lambda_{0}
does not contain non-zero isotropic elements. To see this, assume
that

are coprime integers satisfying

that

and since

. Writing

and

21x_{0}^{2}-7y_{0}^{2}-z^{2}-w^{2}=0

and since
-1

arriving at a contradiction.

Now let
\Lambda

for
d=2
. By the same argument
as above, this lattice can be realized as the Néron–Severi lattice of an algebraic K3 surface,
giving an example of the Baragar gasket with all spheres disjoint.

Example 7.14:
Let us now give an example of an Apollonian carpet which also has non-Baragar components. Let

\Lambda
be the rank four lattice with the quadratic form
q(x,y,z,w)=46x^{2}-2y^{2}-46z^{2}-46w^{2}
(i.e. the form from the first example
with
p=23
). This form obviously represents both
-2
and
-4
. Moreover, by
7.3
, if
q(v_{1})=-2
and
q(v_{2})=-4
, the circles cut out on the absolute by
v_{1}^{\bot}
and
v_{2}^{\bot}
do not intersect except maybe tangentially. This means that the latter circle lies entirely on the
isotropic boundary of the projectivization of the ample cone, i.e. is a component of the Apollonian carpet. Notice that this is not a Baragar component, because
q(v_{2})=-4
and all MBM classes are of square
-2
.

Example 7.15:
The components of the Apollonian carpet can be of codimension more than one. Consider a K3 surface with Picard lattice
\Lambda
of rank 5, with

q(x,y,z,w,u)=2px^{2}-y^{2}-z^{2}-2pw^{2}-2pu^{2}
, then by
7.3
the orthogonal to
\langle e_{2},e_{3}\rangle
does not intersect the other walls of the Kähler chambers except possibly tangentially, therefore gives a codimension-two Baragar component of the Apollonian carpet.

Example 7.16:
The non-Baragar components can also intersect transversally. As an explicit example, we can take a rank-four lattice

\Lambda
with the quadratic form
2q
, where
q=5x^{2}-y^{2}-5z^{2}-5w^{2}
. The orthogonal circles to vectors
v_{1}=(1,2,1,0)
and
v_{2}=(1,2,0,1)
(here
q(v_{1})=q(v_{2})=-4
) do intersect, since the restriction of
q
to the plane they generate is negative. We deduce from
7.3
applied to
q
that
both circles are components of the Apollonian carpet. See Figure
5
in the next subsection for the picture.

Remark 7.17:
The reason why we have only considered K3 surfaces is that the description of MBM classes is particularly simple in this case:
indeed these are precisely the elements of square
-2
in the lattice. But it is clear from the discussion above that the same method should yield analogous examples for a hyperkähler manifold as soon as one has a description of its MBM classes. Indeed it is known (
[
AV2
]
) that MBM classes have bounded Beauville-Bogomolov square, so setting the prime
p
as above large enough allows to produce lattices with desired properties. Strictly speaking, there is an additional difficulty to prove that a given lattice embeds into the second cohomology lattice of a
hyperkähler manifold, as Nikulin’s theorem requires unimodularity of this larger lattice. But there exist some ways to get around this difficulty, see e.g.
[
AV3
]
.

7.4
Illustrations

In this section we provide some examples of computer
generated images of the sphere packings on the boundary of the hyperbolic
3-space. All of the presented configurations of spheres may appear
in the Néron–Severi groups of algebraic K3 surfaces (some of the lattices considered below
are odd; one has to multiply them by two before embedding into the K3 lattice). We hope that out illustrations will
give an idea of what the isotropic boundary of the ample cone of a hyperkäher manifold
may look like, more vividly than the mere formal description.

We start by describing the procedure that was used to produce all the figures below. We fix
a quadratic lattice
(\Lambda,q)
of rank
4
and signature
(1,3)
. We fix
an integer
d<0
and let
\mathcal{S}=\{v\in\Lambda\enskip|\enskip q(v)=d\}
. The collection
\mathcal{S}

defines an arrangement of hyperplanes
H_{v}=v^{\perp}\subset V=\Lambda\otimes\mathbb{R}
, where
v\in\mathcal{S}
.

We fix an isomorphism
\Lambda\simeq\mathbb{Z}^{4}
, identifying
q
with a symmetric matrix
A
.
We denote by
\nu_{0}
,
\nu_{1}
,
\nu_{3}
and
\nu_{3}
the eigenvectors of
A
with eigenvalues

\lambda_{0}>0>\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\lambda_{3}
, normalized by the condition
|q(\nu_{i})|=1
.
We identify
V
with
\mathbb{R}^{1,3}
using the basis
\nu_{0},\ldots,\nu_{3}
,
so that for
x=(x_{0},x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})\in\mathbb{R}^{1,3}
we have
q(x)=x_{0}^{2}-x_{1}^{2}-x_{2}^{2}-x_{3}^{2}
.

The hyperbolic 3-space
\mathbb{H}^{3}=\mathbb{P}(V^{+})
is identified with the open unit ball in
\mathbb{R}^{3}

via the stereographic projection from the point
(-1,0,0,0)
. The inverse of this projection is
given by

(y_{1},y_{2},y_{3})\mapsto(1+|y|^{2}:2y_{1}:2y_{2}:2y_{3}),

where
|y|
denotes the standard Euclidan norm in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Under the stereographic projection the boundary
Q

of
\mathbb{H}^{3}
is identified with the unit sphere
S^{2}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
and we have

Q\cap H_{v}=\left\{y\in S^{2}\enskip|\enskip v_{0}=\sum_{i=1}^{3}v_{i}y_{i}\right\},

where
v=(v_{0},v_{1},v_{2},v_{3})\in\mathbb{R}^{1,3}
.

As we have discussed above, the collection of hyperplanes
H_{v}
,
v\in\mathcal{S}
is locally finite in
\mathbb{H}^{3}
,
and it splits
\mathbb{H}^{3}
into a countable set of open chambers.
We will study the boundary of the
\mathcal{S}
-chamber in
\mathbb{H}^{3}
that contains the origin, which corresponds to the point
(1:0:0:0)\in\mathbb{P}(V^{+}
).
More precisely, since the origin might lie on one of the walls
H_{v}
, we will study the boundary
of the union of all
\mathcal{S}
-chambers that contain the origin in their closure. The number of such chambers
is finite and we denote their union by
\mathcal{C}_{0}\subset\mathbb{H}^{3}
. We also define
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}=\{v\in\mathcal{S}\enskip|\enskip v_{0}>0\}
,
so that
\overline{\mathcal{C}_{0}}=\cap_{v\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}}H_{v}^{+}
. We let
Q_{v}=H_{v}^{-}\cap Q
for
v\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
.
Then the isotropic part of the boundary of
\mathcal{C}_{0}

is

\overline{\mathcal{C}_{0}}\cap Q=S^{2}\setminus\cup_{v\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}}Q_{v}.

We further consider the stereographic projection from the point
(1,0,0)\in S^{2}
onto
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
The inverse of that projection identifies
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with the complement of
(1,0,0)\in S^{2}
:

(z_{1},z_{2})\mapsto(y_{1},y_{2},y_{3})=\left(\frac{|z|^{2}-1}{|z|^{2}+1},\frac{2z_{1}}{|z|^{2}+1},\frac{2z_{2}}{|z|^{2}+1}\right).

Under this projection
Q_{v}
is mapped to the subset

D_{v}=\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\enskip|\enskip|z|^{2}(v_{1}-v_{0})+2v_{2}z_{1}+2v_{3}z_{2}>v_{0}+v_{1}\},

which is either the interior or the exterior of the circle with centre at the
point
(v_{2}/(v_{0}-v_{1}),v_{3}/(v_{0}-v_{1}))
of radius
|q(v)|^{1/2}|v_{1}-v_{0}|^{-1}
,
or a half-plane, depending on the non-vanishing and the sign of
v_{1}-v_{0}
. Slightly abusing the terminology,
we will call
D_{v}
a
v
-disc (although it might not be really a disc, but a “hole” in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
or a half-plane).
The isotropic boundary of
\mathcal{C}_{0}
under our projection is identified with
the complement
\mathcal{B}_{0}
to the union of
v
-discs for all
v\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
.

The
v
-discs are partially ordered by inclusion as subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, and this defines a partial order
on
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
. It is clear that any increasing chain with respect to this partial order stabilizes (because
of the local finiteness of the hyperplanes
H_{v}
in
\mathbb{H}^{3}
).
Hence to describe the isotropic boundary
\mathcal{B}_{0}
it is sufficient to consider only the maximal
v
-discs.
The maximal
v
-discs correspond to the faces of
\overline{\mathcal{C}_{0}}
.

Listing all elements of the set
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
, we obtain increasing finite collections of maximal
v
-discs,
and the complements to such finite collections give approximations to
\mathcal{B}_{0}
. In the pictures
below, we show such finite collections of
v
-discs inside the unit disc in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
(which
corresponds to the hemisphere in
S^{2}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
given by the inequality
y_{1}<0
). The complement
to the
v
-discs (black points in the pictures) is the approximation of the isotropic boundary of
\mathcal{C}_{0}
.

The classical Apollonian gasket is a configuration of circles in the
plane that is obtained iteratively by the following procedure. One starts from a triple of
pairwise tangent circles having three distinct points of tangency.

in the previous step. One can obtain the Apollonian configuration starting from the

1&-1&1&1\\
