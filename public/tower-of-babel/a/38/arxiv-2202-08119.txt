The Parameterized Complexity of Quantum Verification
Srinivasan Arunachalam, Sergey Bravyi, Chinmay Nirkhe, Bryan O’Gorman

Abstract
We initiate the study of parameterized complexity of 𝖰𝖬𝖠 {\mathsf{QMA}} problems in terms of the number of non-Clifford gates in the problem description. We show that for the problem of parameterized quantum circuit satisfiability, there exists a classical algorithm solving the problem with a runtime scaling exponentially in the number of non-Clifford gates but only polynomially with the system size. This result follows from our main result, that for any Clifford + t t T T -gate quantum circuit satisfiability problem, the search space of optimal witnesses can be reduced to a stabilizer subspace isomorphic to at most t t qubits (independent of the system size). Furthermore, we derive new lower bounds on the T T -count of circuit satisfiability instances and the T T -count of the W W -state assuming the classical exponential time hypothesis ( 𝖤𝖳𝖧 \mathsf{ETH} ). Lastly, we explore the parameterized complexity of the quantum non-identity check problem.

Introduction

The solutions to many important computational problems require resources that seem to scale exponentially in the system size in the worst case.
Parameterized complexity refines this phenomenon by identifying one or more parameters along with algorithms that scale exponentially only in the identified parameters (but polynomially in system size).
In the worst case, these parameters typically scale with system size, but often interesting instances have an intermediate value of the parameter.
In quantum computing, parameterized complexity has been applied to the classical simulation of quantum systems

By definition, solving

\rho\mathrel{\overset{\makebox[0.0pt]{\mbox{\tiny def}}}{=}}\bra{0^{m}}U^{\dagger}\outerproduct*{1^k}{1^k}U\ket{0^{m}}

(2) applying a unitary Clifford gate, and (3) post-selectively measuring a qubit

they always produce a (mixed) stabilizer-type state
[
gottesman-knill-theorem
]

Accordingly, the largest eigenvalue of

]

Suppose now that

t

T
-gates. It is well-known
[

|A\rangle\propto|0\rangle+e^{i\pi/4}|1\rangle

Replacing each

-gate in

\rho=\mathrm{Tr}_{\{[t]\}}((\outerproduct*{A^t}{A^t}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{n})\rho^{\prime})

is a bipartite stabilizer state

[

]

\rho^{\prime}=(C_{1}^{\dagger}\otimes C_{2}^{\dagger})\rho^{\prime\prime}(C_{1}\otimes C_{2})
, where

are unitary Clifford operators and
\rho^{\prime\prime}
is a tensor product of
local single-qubit stabilizer states and at most
t

Using this decomposition we are able to show that

Thus,

of the
t

To prove Theorem

1.2
Implied lower bounds from the exponential time hypothesis

Theorem
2
provides an upper bound on the runtime of a classical algorithm for
\mathsf{QCSAT}
with respect to the number of
T
-gates in the verifier.
In conjunction with other complexity-theoretic assumptions, this also implies a

Informally,

-time algorithm for

\mathsf{ETH}

\displaystyle\ket{W_{m}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\big(\ket{100\cdots 0}+\ket{010\cdots 0}+\cdots+\ket{000\cdots 1}\big).

1.3
The complexity of the non-identity check problem

The second
{\mathsf{QMA}}
-complete problem we consider in this work is the Non-Identity Check (
\mathsf{NIC}
) problem: given a classical description of an
n
-qubit quantum circuit
U
, decide if
U
is close to the identity operation, i.e., is
\min_{\phi}\norm{U - e^{i\phi}\cdot\II}

\geq c
or
\leq s
for
c-s\geq 1/{\mathsf{poly}}(n)
, promised one of them is the case.
4
4

4

We remark that the operator norm is important here; the problem is in
{\mathsf{BQP}}
if the goal is to decide if
\|U-\mathbb{I}\|_{2}
is small or large, where
\|\cdot\|_{2}
is the normalized-
2
norm
[
DBLP:journals/toc/MontanaroW16
]
.

\mathsf{NIC}
was first considered by Janzing et al.
[
nic-def
]
who showed that this problem is
{\mathsf{QMA}}
-complete, by reducing it to the
\mathsf{QCSAT}
problem. Subsequently, Ji and Wu
[
nic-ji-wu
]
showed that the
\mathsf{NIC}
problem remains
{\mathsf{QMA}}
-complete for even depth-
2
circuits with
arbitrary
gates. This motivates a natural question: what is the parameterized complexity of
\mathsf{NIC}
? In particular, how does the complexity of
\mathsf{NIC}
scale with the number of non-Clifford gates? Below we show that
\mathsf{NIC}
can be solved in polynomial-time for Clifford circuits.

Theorem 5

\mathsf{NIC}
for Clifford circuits is contained in
{\mathsf{P}}
.

We sketch the proof of this theorem here while a complete proof is provided in Section
.
By definition, solving
\mathsf{NIC}
for a Clifford circuit
U
on
n
qubits is equivalent to estimating the
maximum eigenvalue of a Hamiltonian

H=(\mathbb{I}-U)^{\dagger}(\mathbb{I}-U)
, that is, checking if
\|H\|\geq c^{2}
or
\leq s^{2}
. In the former case, observe that
\Tr(H^p)\geq c^{2p}
and in the latter case
\Tr(H^p)\leq 2^{n}\cdot s^{2p}
. Furthermore, since
c-s\geq 1/{\mathsf{poly}}(n)
, we can pick
p={\mathsf{poly}}(n)
to make
c^{2p}\gg 2^{n}\cdot s^{2p}
.
Thus it suffices to estimate
\Tr(H^p)
with
p={\mathsf{poly}}(n)
.
To this end, observe that
H^{p}
can be written as a weighted sum of Clifford powers
U^{i}
with
i\in\{-p,\ldots,p\}
.
Thus
\Tr(H^p)=\sum_{i=-p}^{p}\alpha_{i}\Tr(U^i)
and
the coefficients
\alpha_{i}
can be efficiently computed
using a simple recursive formula. Furthermore, we can efficiently compute
\Tr(U^i)
for every
i

using the identity
\Tr(U^i)=2^{n}\bra{\Phi^{\otimes n}}U^{i}\otimes\mathbb{I}\ket{\Phi^{\otimes n}}
,
where
|\Phi\rangle
is the EPR state.
The right-hand side is the inner product between two stabilizer states of
2n
qubits. Such inner products can be computed exactly in time
O(n^{3})
,
see
[
garcia2012efficient
,
bravyi2016improved
]
.
Putting all this together, we can compute
\Tr(H^p)
exactly in time
{\mathsf{poly}}(n)
, which determines if this is a “yes" or “no" instance of the
\mathsf{NIC}
problem.

In addition, we also prove that the
\mathsf{NIC}
problem for constant-depth circuits using gates from a constant-sized gate set is solvable for vanishing completeness parameters. This is in contrast to
[
nic-ji-wu
]
which shows constant-depth circuits using gates from a general gate set is
{\mathsf{QMA}}
-complete.

Since the algorithm for
