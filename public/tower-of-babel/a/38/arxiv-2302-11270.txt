Perturbations of non-autonomous second-order abstract Cauchy problems Thanks: Declarations of interest: none.
Christian Budde, Christian Seifert

Abstract
. In this paper we present time-dependent perturbations of second-order non-autonomous abstract Cauchy problems associated to a family of operators with constant domain. We make use of the equivalence to a first-order non-autonomous abstract Cauchy problem in a product space, which we elaborate in full detail. As an application we provide a perturbed non-autonomous wave equation.

1.
Introduction

Autonomous second-order abstract Cauchy problems, which are of the form

(ACP
2
)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\ddot{u}(t)=Au(t),&\quad t>0,\\
u(0)=x,\\
\dot{u}(0)=y,\end{cases}

for some (unbounded) operator
(A,\mathrm{D}(A))
, which often occur in the context of wave equations, have been studied intensively by several authors in the past, e.g., Sova
[
39
]
, Da Prato and Giusti
[
5
]
, Fattorini
[
8
]
, Neubrander
[
27
]
, Xio and Jin
[
49
]
as well as Xiao and Liang
[
48
]
. One can also find more information in the monographs by Arendt et al.
[
3
, Sect. 3.14 & 3.15]
, Melnikova and Filinkov
[
24
, Sect. 1.7]
or Vasil’ev and Piskarev
[
44
]
. In contrast to the first-order problem, where (classical) solutions are given by
C_{0}
-semigroups, one needs another solution concept for (
ACP2
), the so-called cosine and sine families. Similar to the Hille–Yosida generation theorem for strongly continuous semigroups, one can also characterize generators of cosine families, cf.
[
3
, Thm. 3.15.3]
,
[
24
, Thm. 1.7.2]
or
[
49
, Thm. A]
. The classical operator theoretical approach to (
ACP2
) is to reduce these to first-order ones, where one can apply the theory of
C_{0}
-semigroups. For a detailed overview on
C_{0}
-semigroups, we refer for example to the monographs by Engel and Nagel
[
6
]
, Goldstein
[
9
]
or Pazy
[
30
]
. In
[
17
]
, Kisyński gives an explicit correspondence between generators of cosine families and strongly continuous semigroups, see also
[
3
, Thm. 3.14.11]
.

In contrast to the autonomous second-order problems, one has the non-autonomous second-order abstract Cauchy problems of the form

(nACP
2
)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\ddot{u}(t)=A(t)u(t),&\quad t\in\left(0,T\right],\\
u(0)=x,\\
\dot{u}(0)=y,\end{cases}

for some fixed
T>0
, where
(A(t),\mathrm{D}(A(t)))_{t\in\left[0,T\right]}
is a family of operators. These non-autonomous second-order abstract Cauchy problems have been studied first by Kozak
[
18
,
19
,
20
]
and later on by Bochneck
[
4
]
, Winiarska
[
47
,
46
]
and Lan
[
21
]
, just to mention a few. The same idea as for (
ACP2
) helps to reduce (
nACP2
) again to a first-order problem. Solutions of non-autonomous first-order abstract Cauchy problems have been studied exhaustively by means of evolution families for example by Acquistapace and Terreni
[
1
]
and Kato and Tanabe
[
41
,
15
,
42
]
. A semigroup approach by so-called evolution semigroups was firstly introduced by Howland
[
13
]
and later on studied by several authors, e.g., Evans
[
7
]
, Nagel
[
25
]
, Nickel
[
28
]
, Rhandi
[
26
]
and Schnaubelt
[
34
]
.

The goal of this paper is to establish a perturbation result for (
nACP2
). Perturbation theorems for cosine families associated to (
ACP2
) have been developed for example by Piskarev and Shaw
[
31
,
32
]
, Miyadera
[
38
]
, Takenaka and Okazawa
[
40
]
as well as Travis and Webb
[
43
]
. Also time-dependent perturbations have been studied, cf.
[
37
,
22
,
23
]
. We want to perturb (
nACP2
) in a time-dependent way as it has been done for first-order non-autonomous problems by Räbinger et al.
[
33
,
35
]
. Especially, we want to cover time-dependent perturbations of bounded type.

The paper is structured as follows. Section
2
consists of preliminary definitions regarding solutions of non-autonomous abstract Cauchy problems.
The following Section
3
provides a relation between existence of fundamental solutions to first- and second-order non-autonomous Cauchy problems.
This can be viewed as a non-autonomous version of the generation theorem of Kisyński in
[
17
]
, cf. Theorem
3.4
. Section
4
provides our main result regarding perturbations of second-order non-autonomous Cauchy problems, cf. Theorem
4.2
. The final Section
5
provides an example of a non-autonomous wave equation.

2.
Preliminaries on Non-Autonomous Abstract Cauchy Problems

Let
X
be a Banach space,
T>0
and
(A(t),\mathrm{D}(A(t)))_{t\in\left[0,T\right]}
be a family of closed operators on
X
.

We make the following crucial assumption throughout this article.

Assumption 2.1
.

The domains
\mathrm{D}(A(t))
do not depend on time, i.e., there exists
D\subseteq X
such that
\mathrm{D}(A(t))=D
for all
t\in[0,T]
.

We write
\Delta:=\Delta_{T}:=\{(t,s)\in\left[0,T\right]^{2}:\ t\geq s\}
.

2.1.
Non-Autonomous First-Order Cauchy Problems

Non-autonomous first-order abstract Cauchy problems are of the form

(nACP)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\dot{v}(t)=A(t)v(t),&\quad t\in\left(0,T\right],\\
v(0)=x,\end{cases}

where
x\in X
.

Definition 2.2
.

A function
v\colon\left[0,T\right]\to X
is called a
(classical) solution
to (
nACP
) if
v
is continuously differentiable,
v(t)\in D
for all
t\in\left(0,T\right]
and
v
satisfies (
nACP
).

The following solution concept is important for non-autonomous first-order problems.

Definition 2.3
.

A
fundamental solution
to (
nACP
) associated with
(A(t),\mathrm{D}(A(t)))_{t\in\left[0,T\right]}
satisfying Assumption
2.1
is a family of bounded linear operators
(U(t,s))_{(t,s)\in\Delta}
on a Banach space
X
satisfying the following conditions:

(U1)

U(t,t)=I
and
U(t,s)U(s,r)=U(t,r)
for all
(t,s),(s,r)\in\Delta
.

(U2)

The mapping
\Delta\ni(t,s)\mapsto U(t,s)
is strongly continuous on
X
.

(U3)

U(t,s)D\subseteq D

(U4)

For all
(t,s)\in\Delta
and
x\in D
one has that
\frac{\partial}{\partial{t}}U(t,s)x
and
\frac{\partial}{\partial{s}}U(t,s)x
exist and

(2.1)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial{t}}U(t,s)x=A(t)U(t,s)x\text{ and }\frac{\partial}{\partial{s}}U(t,s)x=-U(t,s)A(s)x.

Note that
(U4)
requires
(U3)
.

Remark 2.4
.

(a)

Observe that a fundamental solution to (
nACP
) is bounded in
\mathcal{L}(X)
, which follows from
(U2)
, compactness of
\Delta
and the uniform boundedness principle.

(b)

Fundamental solutions of (
nACP
) as defined in Definition
2.3
are also often called evolution families.

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
(U(t,s))_{(t,s)\in\Delta}
be a fundamental solution of (
nACP
). If
x\in D
then
v(t):=U(t,0)x
defines a classical solution of (
nACP
).

Proof.

The statement follows easily from the properties
(U1)
–
(U4)
.
∎

2.2.
Non-Autonomous Second-Order Cauchy Problems

Non-autonomous second-order abstract Cauchy problems are of the form

(nACP
2
)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\ddot{u}(t)=A(t)u(t),&\quad t\in\left(0,T\right],\\
u(0)=x,\\
\dot{u}(0)=y,\end{cases}

where
x,y\in X
.

Definition 2.6
.

A function
u\colon\left[0,T\right]\to X
is called a
(classical) solution
to (
nACP2
) if
u
is twice continuously differentiable,
u(t)\in D
for all
t\in\left(0,T\right]
and
u
satisfies (
nACP2
).

The solution concept of (
nACP2
) is vastly more involved than this of (
nACP
). In fact, it goes back to Kozak
[
20
, Def. 2.1, Def. 3.1]
. Note that our definition is slightly different from the one of Kozak, see also Remark
2.8
below.

Definition 2.7
.

A
fundamental solution
to (
nACP2
) associated with
(A(t),\mathrm{D}(A(t)))_{t\in\left[0,T\right]}
satisfying Assumption
2.1
is a family of bounded linear operators
(S(t,s))_{(t,s)\in\Delta}
on
X
satisfying the following conditions:

(S1)

(a)

S(t,t)=0
for all
t\in\left[0,T\right]
.

(b)

The mapping
\Delta\ni(t,s)\mapsto S(t,s)
in strongly continuous on
X
.

(c)

For all
x\in X
and
s\in[0,T]
the mapping
[s,T]\ni t\mapsto S(t,s)x
is continuously differentiable, and
(t,s)\mapsto\mathop{\frac{\partial}{\partial{t}}}S(t,s)x
is continuous with

\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial{t}}S(t,s)x\right|_{t=s}=x.

(d)

For all
x\in D
and
t\in[0,T]
the mapping
[0,t]\ni s\mapsto S(t,s)x
is continuously differentiable, and
(t,s)\mapsto\mathop{\frac{\partial}{\partial{s}}}S(t,s)x
is continuous with

\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial{s}}S(t,s)x\right|_{t=s}=-x.

(S2)

S(t,s)D\subseteq D
for all
(t,s)\in\Delta
, for
x\in D
the mapping
\Delta\ni(t,s)\mapsto S(t,s)x
is twice continuously differentiable and

(a)

\displaystyle{\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial{t^{2}}}S(t,s)x=A(t)S(t,s)x.}

(b)

\displaystyle{\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial{s^{2}}}S(t,s)x=S(t,s)A(s)x.}

(c)

\displaystyle{\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial{t}}\frac{\partial}{\partial{s}}S(t,s)x\right|_{t=s}=0}

(S3)

For all
(t,s)\in\Delta
, if
x\in D
then
\frac{\partial}{\partial s}S(t,s)x\in D
, there exist
\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial{t^{2}}}\frac{\partial}{\partial{s}}S(t,s)x
and
\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial{s^{2}}}\frac{\partial}{\partial{t}}S(t,s)x
and the following properties hold

(a)

\displaystyle{\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial{t^{2}}}\frac{\partial}{\partial{s}}S(t,s)x=A(t)\frac{\partial}{\partial{s}}S(t,s)x.}

(b)

\displaystyle{\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial{s^{2}}}\frac{\partial}{\partial{t}}S(t,s)x=\frac{\partial}{\partial{t}}S(t,s)A(s)x.}

(c)

The mapping
\Delta\ni(t,s)\mapsto A(t)\frac{\partial}{\partial{s}}S(t,s)x
is continuous.

Moreover, we call a fundamental solution
(S(t,s))_{(t,s)\in\Delta}

evolutionary
if additionally

(S4)

For all
(t,s),(s,r)\in\Delta
and
x\in D
one has

\displaystyle\left(-\frac{\partial}{\partial{s}}S(t,s)\right)S(s,r)x+S(t,s)\frac{\partial}{\partial{s}}S(s,r)x=S(t,r)x.

Remark 2.8
.

(a)

Note that our Definition of fundamental solutions is slightly different from
[
20
, Def. 2.1, Def. 3.1]
in the sense that we do not assume
(t,s)\mapsto S(t,s)x
to be continuously differentiable for all
x\in X
in
(S1)
, but allow for the partial derivative
\mathop{\frac{\partial}{\partial{s}}}S(t,s)x
only for
x\in D
in
(S1)
(d). Thus, our definition is also different from the ones used in
[
4
,
10
,
11
,
12
]
. The reason we adjusted the definition stems from
[
19
, Theorem 4.1, 5)]
which seems to be only possible for
x\in\mathrm{D}(B)
instead of
x\in X
there. In fact, the reasoning in
[
19
, Remark 4.2]
does not apply for
\mathop{\frac{\partial}{\partial{s}}}S(t,s)
in the topology of
X
, but only in the topology of
\mathrm{D}(B)
there.

(b)

Moreover, in contrast to
[
19
,
20
,
4
,
10
,
11
,
12
]
, we only define the fundamental solutions on
\Delta
instead of
[0,T]^{2}
, see also
[
18
]
.

Lemma 2.9
.

Let
(S(t,s))_{(t,s)\in\Delta}
be a fundamental solution of (
nACP2
) in
X
. Then
(S(t,s))_{(t,s)\in\Delta}
and
(\frac{\partial}{\partial t}S(t,s))_{(t,s)\in\Delta}
are bounded in
\mathcal{L}(X)
.

Proof.

Since
(S1)
(b)–(c) yield that
(t,s)\mapsto S(t,s)x
and
(t,s)\mapsto\mathop{\frac{\partial}{\partial{t}}}S(t,s)x
are continuous on the compact set
\Delta
for all
x\in X
, we observe that
(S(t,s))_{(t,s)\in\Delta}
and
(\frac{\partial}{\partial t}S(t,s))_{(t,s)\in\Delta}

are pointwise bounded. The uniform boundedness principle then yields boundedness in
\mathcal{L}(X)
.
∎

Proposition 2.10
.

Let
(S(t,s))_{(t,s)\in\Delta}
be a fundamental solution of (
nACP2
). If
x,y\in D
, then
u(t):=-\mathop{\frac{\partial}{\partial{s}}}S(t,0)x+S(t,0)y
defines a classical solution of (
nACP2
).

Proof.

The statement follows easily from the properties
(S1)
–
(S3)
.
∎

3.
Existence of Fundamental Solutions: A Generation Type Result

Let
X
be a Banach space,
T>0
,
(A(t),\mathrm{D}(A(t)))_{t\in\left[0,T\right]}
a family of closed linear operators in
X
satisfying Assumption
2.1
with
D\subseteq X
dense. We will further assume the following.

Assumption 3.1
.

There exists
C>0
such that

\frac{1}{C}\|\cdot\|_{A(s)}\leq\|\cdot\|_{A(t)}\leq C\|\cdot\|_{A(s)}\quad(s,t\in[0,T]).

We equip
D
with the graph norm of
A(0)
(equivalently, with the graph norm of any
A(t)
) such that
D
is a Banach space.

It is worth to mention, that the equivalence of the graph norms is an assumption made regularly throughout the literature, see for example
[
36
, Rem. 4.5]
,
[
45
, Rem. 4.2]
and
[
2
, Sect. 7]
, just to mention a few.

Moreover, the following regularity assumption on
A(\cdot)
will be made.

Assumption 3.2
.

Assume
A(\cdot)x\in\mathrm{C}^{1}([0,T];X)
for all
x\in D
.

Note that the first-order non-autonomous abstract Cauchy problem (
nACP
) is well-posed according to
[
6
, Chapter VI, Def. 9.1]
if Assumption
2.1
and Assumption
3.2
are satisfied and every operator generates a contractive
C_{0}
-semigroup, cf.
[
14
,
16
]
.

The following lemma will be useful in what follows.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
Z,X
be Banach spaces,
Z\subseteq X
,
B\in\mathcal{L}(X)
.

(a)

Assume
BX\subseteq Z
. Then
B\in\mathcal{L}(X,Z)
.

(b)

Assume
BZ\subseteq Z
. Then
B\in\mathcal{L}(Z)
.

Proof.

(a)

Let
(x_{n})
in
X
,
x\in X
,
y\in Z
such that
x_{n}\to x
in
X
and
Bx_{n}\to y
in
Z
. Since
Z\subseteq X
we also have
Bx_{n}\to y
in
X
. Hence, by continuity of
B
on
X
, we have
Bx=y
, so
B\colon X\to Z
is closed and hence bounded by the closed graph theorem.

(b)

Let
(x_{n})
in
Z
,
x\in Z
,
y\in Z
such that
x_{n}\to x
in
Z
and
Bx_{n}\to y
in
Z
. Since
Z\subseteq X
we also have
x_{n}\to x
in
X
and
Bx_{n}\to y
in
X
. Hence, by continuity of
B
on
X
, we have
Bx=y
, so
B\colon Z\to Z
is closed and hence bounded by the closed graph theorem.

∎

Let
Z
be a Banach space such that
D\subseteq Z
dense and
Z\subseteq X
dense.
Let

(\mathcal{A}(t),\mathrm{D}(\mathcal{A}(t)))_{t\in\left[0,T\right]}
in
\mathcal{Z}:=Z\times X
be defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(t):=\begin{pmatrix}0&I\\
A(t)&0\end{pmatrix},\quad\mathrm{D}(\mathcal{A}(t)):=\mathcal{D}:=D\times Z.

Theorem 3.4
.

The following assertions are equivalent:

(a)

There exists an evolutionary fundamental solution
(S(t,s))_{(t,s)\in\Delta}
on
X
of (
nACP2
) associated to
(A(t),D)_{t\in\left[0,T\right]}
such that
for all
(t,s)\in\Delta
we have

•

S(t,s)X\subseteq Z
,
S(t,s)Z\subseteq D
,
(t,s)\mapsto S(t,s)x\in Z
is continuous for all
x\in X
,

•

\mathop{\frac{\partial}{\partial{t}}}S(t,s)Z\subseteq Z
,
\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t^{2}}S(t,s)x
exists for all
x\in Z
and
\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t^{2}}S(t,s)x=A(t)S(t,s)x
,

•

\mathop{\frac{\partial}{\partial{s}}}S(t,s)x
exists for all
x\in Z
,
\mathop{\frac{\partial}{\partial{s}}}S(t,s)Z\subseteq Z
and
(t,s)\mapsto\mathop{\frac{\partial}{\partial{s}}}S(t,s)x\in Z
is continuous for all
x\in Z
,

•

\mathop{\frac{\partial}{\partial{t}}}\mathop{\frac{\partial}{\partial{s}}}S(t,s)D\subseteq Z
,
\mathop{\frac{\partial}{\partial{t}}}\mathop{\frac{\partial}{\partial{s}}}S(t,s)x
exists for all
x\in Z
and there exists
C\geq 0
such that
\|\mathop{\frac{\partial}{\partial{t}}}\mathop{\frac{\partial}{\partial{s}}}S(t,s)x\|_{X}\leq C\|x\|_{Z}
for all
x\in Z
and
(t,s)\in\Delta
.

(b)

There exists a fundamental solution
(\mathcal{U}(t,s))_{(t,s)\in\Delta}
on
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{Z})
of (
nACP
) associated to
(\mathcal{A}(t),\mathcal{D})_{t\in\left[0,T\right]}
.

Proof.

We first prove the implication (
b
)
\Longrightarrow
(
a
).

Define
(S(t,s))_{(t,s)\in\Delta}
on
X
by

(3.1)

\displaystyle S(t,s)x:=\pi_{1}\mathcal{U}(t,s)\begin{pmatrix}0\\
x\end{pmatrix},\quad x\in X,

where
\pi_{1}\colon Z\times X\to Z
denotes the projection on the first component. Hence,
S(t,s)\in\mathcal{L}(X,Z)
and since
Z\subseteq X
we also have
S(t,s)\in\mathcal{L}(X)
for all
(t,s)\in\Delta
. Since
(\mathcal{U}(t,s))_{(t,s)\in\Delta}
is strongly continuous on
\mathcal{Z}
, also
(t,s)\mapsto S(t,s)\in\mathcal{L}(X,Z)
strongly continuous.

Let us provide some useful formulas. First, we note that
\pi_{1}\mathcal{A}(t)=\pi_{2}|_{\mathcal{D}}
, where
\pi_{2}\colon Z\times X\to X
is the projection on the second component. Second, for
x\in Z
we have that
\mathcal{A}(s)\begin{pmatrix}0\\
x\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}x\\
0\end{pmatrix}
for all
s\in[0,T]
. In particular, for
x\in D
we have
\mathcal{A}(s)\begin{pmatrix}0\\
x\end{pmatrix}\in\mathcal{D}
for all
s\in[0,T]
.

For
x\in Z
we have
\begin{pmatrix}0\\
x\end{pmatrix}\in\mathcal{D}
, so by
(U3)
we obtain
S(t,s)x\in D
for all
(t,s)\in\Delta
. Moreover,
t\mapsto S(t,s)x
is differentiable and

\mathop{\frac{\partial}{\partial{t}}}S(t,s)x=\pi_{1}\mathop{\frac{\partial}{\partial{t}}}\mathcal{U}(t,s)\begin{pmatrix}0\\
x\end{pmatrix}=\pi_{1}\mathcal{A}(t)\mathcal{U}(t,s)\begin{pmatrix}0\\
x\end{pmatrix}=\pi_{2}\mathcal{U}(t,s)\begin{pmatrix}0\\
x\end{pmatrix}.

By
(U3)
we observe
\mathop{\frac{\partial}{\partial{t}}}S(t,s)x\in Z
.

Let
x\in Z
. Then, by
(U4)
, we have that
\mathcal{U}(t,s)\begin{pmatrix}0\\
x\end{pmatrix}
is differentiable with respect to
s
and we obtain

\mathop{\frac{\partial}{\partial{s}}}\mathcal{U}(t,s)\begin{pmatrix}0\\
x\end{pmatrix}=-\mathcal{U}(t,s)\mathcal{A}(s)\begin{pmatrix}0\\
x\end{pmatrix}=-\mathcal{U}(t,s)\begin{pmatrix}x\\
0\end{pmatrix}.

Thus,
\mathop{\frac{\partial}{\partial{s}}}S(t,s)x
exists,
\mathop{\frac{\partial}{\partial{s}}}S(t,s)x\in Z
and

\mathop{\frac{\partial}{\partial{s}}}S(t,s)x=\pi_{1}\mathop{\frac{\partial}{\partial{s}}}\mathcal{U}(t,s)\begin{pmatrix}0\\
x\end{pmatrix}=-\pi_{1}\mathcal{U}(t,s)\begin{pmatrix}x\\
0\end{pmatrix}

for all
(t,s)\in\Delta
. As
(\mathcal{U}(t,s))_{(t,s)\in\Delta}
is strongly continuous, also
(t,s)\mapsto\mathop{\frac{\partial}{\partial{s}}}S(t,s)x\in Z
is continuous for all
x\in Z
.

We now show existence of the mixed derivative of
(S(t,s))_{(t,s)\in\Delta}
. First, let
x\in D
.
Then, by
(U4)
and the above, we have that
\mathop{\frac{\partial}{\partial{s}}}\mathcal{U}(t,s)\begin{pmatrix}0\\
x\end{pmatrix}
is differentiable with respect to
t
and

\mathop{\frac{\partial}{\partial{t}}}\mathop{\frac{\partial}{\partial{s}}}\mathcal{U}(t,s)\begin{pmatrix}0\\
x\end{pmatrix}=-\mathop{\frac{\partial}{\partial{t}}}\mathcal{U}(t,s)\begin{pmatrix}x\\
0\end{pmatrix}=-\mathcal{A}(t)\mathcal{U}(t,s)\begin{pmatrix}x\\
0\end{pmatrix}.

Thus,
\mathop{\frac{\partial}{\partial{t}}}\mathop{\frac{\partial}{\partial{s}}}S(t,s)x
exists and

\mathop{\frac{\partial}{\partial{t}}}\mathop{\frac{\partial}{\partial{s}}}S(t,s)x=-\pi_{1}\mathcal{A}(t)\mathcal{U}(t,s)\begin{pmatrix}x\\
0\end{pmatrix}=\pi_{2}\mathcal{U}(t,s)\begin{pmatrix}x\\
0\end{pmatrix}.

By
(U3)
this shows that
\mathop{\frac{\partial}{\partial{t}}}\mathop{\frac{\partial}{\partial{s}}}S(t,s)x\in Z
.

Let
x\in Z
. Then
\begin{pmatrix}0\\
x\end{pmatrix}\in\mathcal{D}
, so as above

\mathop{\frac{\partial}{\partial{t}}}S(t,s)x=\pi_{2}\mathcal{U}(t,s)\begin{pmatrix}0\\
x\end{pmatrix}.

As the right-hand side is differentiable with respect to

,
\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t^{2}}S(t,s)x
exists and

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t^{2}}S(t,s)x

\displaystyle=\pi_{2}\mathcal{A}(t)\mathcal{U}(t,s)\begin{pmatrix}0\\
x\end{pmatrix}=\pi_{2}\mathcal{A}(t)\begin{pmatrix}\pi_{1}\\
\pi_{2}\end{pmatrix}\mathcal{U}(t,s)\begin{pmatrix}0\\
x\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=A(t)\pi_{1}\mathcal{U}(t,s)\begin{pmatrix}0\\
x\end{pmatrix}=A(t)S(t,s)x.

Now, since

, there exists

(t,s)\in\Delta
.
Thus,

\|\mathop{\frac{\partial}{\partial{t}}}\mathop{\frac{\partial}{\partial{s}}}S(t,s)x\|_{X}\leq C\|x\|_{Z}

for all
(t,s)\in\Delta
and
x\in Z
.
Let
x\in Z
and choose
(x_{n})
in
D
such that
x_{n}\to x
in
Z
. Then
t\mapsto\mathop{\frac{\partial}{\partial{s}}}S(t,s)x_{n}
is continuously differentiable for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and these functions converge uniformly to
t\mapsto\mathop{\frac{\partial}{\partial{s}}}S(t,s)x
, by boundedness of
(\mathcal{U}(t,s))_{(t,s)\in\Delta}
. Moreover,
t\mapsto\mathop{\frac{\partial}{\partial{t}}}\mathop{\frac{\partial}{\partial{s}}}S(t,s)x_{n}
is continuous for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and these functions converge uniformly by the estimate above. Thus,
t\mapsto\mathop{\frac{\partial}{\partial{s}}}S(t,s)x
is continuously differentiable and (
3.2
) holds for all
x\in Z
.

(S1)

(d)

Since the right-hand side is continuous in

0\end{pmatrix}.

Since the right-hand side is continuous in

Moreover,

(S2)
: We have already established

Let

x\end{pmatrix}

(b)

0\end{pmatrix}

(c)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial{s}}\frac{\partial}{\partial{t}}S(t,s)x

0\end{pmatrix}

(U1)
.

(S3)
:
Let
(t,s)\in\Delta
and

. Then by

(b)

.

t\mapsto\mathop{\frac{\partial}{\partial{s}}}\mathcal{U}(t,s)\begin{pmatrix}x\\
y_{n}\end{pmatrix}=\mathcal{U}(t,s)\mathcal{A}(s)\begin{pmatrix}x\\
y_{n}\end{pmatrix}=\mathcal{U}(t,s)\begin{pmatrix}y_{n}\\
A(s)x\end{pmatrix}
converges uniformly to
s\mapsto\mathcal{U}(t,s)\mathcal{A}(s)\begin{pmatrix}x\\
y\end{pmatrix}
, since
(\mathcal{U}(t,s))_{(t,s)\in\Delta}
is bounded and
s\mapsto A(s)x
is continuous.
Thus,

\mathop{\frac{\partial}{\partial{s}}}\mathcal{U}(t,s)\begin{pmatrix}x\\
y\end{pmatrix}
exists and

\mathop{\frac{\partial}{\partial{s}}}\mathcal{U}(t,s)\begin{pmatrix}x\\
y\end{pmatrix}=\mathcal{U}(t,s)\mathcal{A}(s)\begin{pmatrix}x\\
y\end{pmatrix}.

∎

(

2.10
can be extended to

4.
A Bounded Perturbation Type Result

Let
(B(t))_{t\in\left[0,T\right]}
be a family of bounded operators on
X
. We first prove a first-order non-autonomous bounded perturbation type result similar to
[
6
, Chapter VI, Cor. 9.20]
which fits into our framework of fundamental solutions.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
(A(t),\mathrm{D}(A(t)))_{t\in\left[0,T\right]}
be a family of densely defined closed operators on a Banach space
X
satisfying Assumption
2.1
, Assumption
3.1
and Assumption
3.2
. Let
(S(t,s))_{(t,s)\in\Delta}
be an evolutionary fundamental solution of the corresponding (
nACP
) on
X
and let
Z
be the space occuring in Theorem
3.4

such that
for all
(t,s)\in\Delta
we have

•

S(t,s)X\subseteq Z
,
S(t,s)Z\subseteq D
,
(t,s)\mapsto S(t,s)x\in Z
is continuous for all
x\in X
,

•

\mathop{\frac{\partial}{\partial{t}}}S(t,s)Z\subseteq Z
,
\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t^{2}}S(t,s)x
exists for all
x\in Z
and
\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t^{2}}S(t,s)x=A(t)S(t,s)x
,

•

\mathop{\frac{\partial}{\partial{s}}}S(t,s)x
exists for all
x\in Z
,
\mathop{\frac{\partial}{\partial{s}}}S(t,s)Z\subseteq Z
and
(t,s)\mapsto\mathop{\frac{\partial}{\partial{s}}}S(t,s)x\in Z
is continuous for all
x\in Z
,

•

\mathop{\frac{\partial}{\partial{t}}}\mathop{\frac{\partial}{\partial{s}}}S(t,s)D\subseteq Z
,
\mathop{\frac{\partial}{\partial{t}}}\mathop{\frac{\partial}{\partial{s}}}S(t,s)x
exists for all
x\in Z
and there exists
C\geq 0
such that
\|\mathop{\frac{\partial}{\partial{t}}}\mathop{\frac{\partial}{\partial{s}}}S(t,s)x\|_{X}\leq C\|x\|_{Z}
for all
x\in Z
and
(t,s)\in\Delta
.

Let
(\mathcal{U}(t,s))_{(t,s)\in\Delta}
be a fundamental solution of (
nACP
) on
\mathcal{Z}:=Z\times X
corresponding to
(\mathcal{A}(t),\mathrm{D}(\mathcal{A}(t)))_{t\in\left[0,T\right]}
. Let
B(\cdot)\in\mathrm{C}\left(\left[0,T\right];\mathscr{L}_{\mathrm{s}}(Z)\right)
. Then there exists a fundamental solution
(\mathcal{V}(t,s))_{(t,s)\in\Delta}
of (
nACP
) on
\mathcal{Z}
corresponding to the family of operators
(\mathcal{A}(t)+\mathcal{B}(t),\mathrm{D}(\mathcal{A}(t)))_{t\in\left[0,T\right]}
, where

\mathcal{B}(t):=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
B(t)&0\end{pmatrix},\quad t\in\left[0,T\right].

Proof.

Firstly, by Theorem
3.4
we know that
(\mathcal{U}(t,s))_{(t,s)\in\Delta}
is indeed a fundamental solution of (
nACP
) on
\mathcal{Z}=Z\times X
. By
[
6
, Chapter VI, Cor. 9.20]
there exists a family of operators
(\mathcal{V}(t,s))_{(t,s)\in\Delta}
satisfying
(U1)
and
(U2)
. Moreover, we know that the variation of constants formula holds, i.e., one has that

(4.1)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{V}(t,s)\binom{z}{x}&=\mathcal{U}(t,s)\binom{z}{x}+\int_{s}^{t}{\mathcal{U}(t,r)\mathcal{B}(r)\mathcal{V}(r,s)\binom{z}{x}\ \mathrm{d}{r}}\\
&=\mathcal{U}(t,s)\binom{z}{x}+\int_{s}^{t}{\mathcal{V}(t,r)\mathcal{B}(r)\mathcal{U}(r,s)\binom{z}{x}\ \mathrm{d}{r}},\end{split}

for all
z\in Z
and
x\in X
. In order to show that
(\mathcal{V}(t,s))_{(t,s)\in\Delta}
is a fundamental solution according to Definition
2.3
we have to show that also
(U3)
and
(U4)
hold. To do so, let
\binom{x}{y}\in\mathcal{D}:=D\times Z
be arbitrary. Then
\mathcal{U}(t,s)\binom{x}{y}\in\mathcal{D}
by the assumption that
(\mathcal{U}(t,s))_{(t,s)\in\Delta}
is a fundamental solution, cf. Definition
2.3
. As
(\mathcal{V}(t,s))_{(t,s)\in\Delta}
is a family of operators in
Z\times X
we obviously have
\mathcal{V}(t,s)(D\times X)\subseteq Z\times X
. Now, we observe that by the assumption that
B(\cdot)\in\mathrm{C}\left(\left[0,T\right];\mathscr{L}_{\mathrm{s}}(Z)\right)
and the explicit representation of the operators we have
\mathcal{B}(t)(Z\times X)\subseteq\left\{0\right\}\times Z\subseteq D\times Z
so that the integral term appearing in the variation of constant formula (
4.1
) is in
D\times Z
as well. This shows that
(U3)
holds. For
(U4)
, let
\binom{x}{y}\in\mathcal{D}
. Then we have

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial{t}}\mathcal{V}(t,s)\binom{x}{y}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial{t}}\mathcal{U}(t,s)\binom{x}{y}+\frac{\partial}{\partial{t}}\int_{s}^{t}{\mathcal{U}(t,r)\mathcal{B}(r)\mathcal{V}(r,s)\binom{x}{y}\ \mathrm{d}{r}}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{A}(t)\mathcal{U}(t,s)\binom{x}{y}+\mathcal{U}(t,t)\mathcal{B}(t)\mathcal{V}(t,s)\binom{x}{y}+\int_{s}^{t}{\frac{\partial}{\partial{t}}\mathcal{U}(t,r)\mathcal{B}(r)\mathcal{V}(r,s)\binom{x}{y}\ \mathrm{d}{r}}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{A}(t)\mathcal{U}(t,s)\binom{x}{y}+\mathcal{B}(t)\mathcal{V}(t,s)\binom{x}{y}+\int_{s}^{t}{\mathcal{A}(t)\mathcal{U}(t,r)\mathcal{B}(r)\mathcal{V}(r,s)\binom{x}{y}\ \mathrm{d}{r}}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{A}(t)\mathcal{U}(t,s)\binom{x}{y}+\mathcal{B}(t)\mathcal{V}(t,s)\binom{x}{y}+\mathcal{A}(t)\int_{s}^{t}{\mathcal{U}(t,r)\mathcal{B}(r)\mathcal{V}(r,s)\binom{x}{y}\ \mathrm{d}{r}}

\displaystyle=(\mathcal{A}(t)+\mathcal{B}(t))\mathcal{V}(t,s)\binom{x}{y},

where we have used Hille’s theorem. Moreover,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial{s}}\mathcal{V}(t,s)\binom{x}{y}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial{s}}\mathcal{U}(t,s)\binom{x}{y}+\frac{\partial}{\partial{s}}\int_{s}^{t}{\mathcal{V}(t,r)\mathcal{B}(r)\mathcal{U}(r,s)\binom{x}{y}\ \mathrm{d}{r}}

\displaystyle=-\mathcal{U}(t,s)\mathcal{A}(s)\binom{x}{y}-\mathcal{V}(t,s)\mathcal{B}(s)\mathcal{U}(s,s)\binom{x}{y}+\int_{s}^{t}{\frac{\partial}{\partial{s}}\mathcal{V}(t,r)\mathcal{B}(r)\mathcal{U}(r,s)\binom{x}{y}\ \mathrm{d}{r}}

\displaystyle=-\mathcal{U}(t,s)\mathcal{A}(s)\binom{x}{y}-\mathcal{V}(t,s)\mathcal{B}(s)\binom{x}{y}+\int_{s}^{t}{\mathcal{V}(t,r)\mathcal{B}(r)\frac{\partial}{\partial{t}}\mathcal{U}(r,s)\binom{x}{y}\ \mathrm{d}{r}}

\displaystyle=-\mathcal{U}(t,s)\mathcal{A}(s)\binom{x}{y}-\mathcal{V}(t,s)\mathcal{B}(s)\binom{x}{y}-\int_{s}^{t}{\mathcal{V}(t,r)\mathcal{B}(r)\mathcal{U}(r,s)\mathcal{A}(s)\binom{x}{y}\ \mathrm{d}{r}}

\displaystyle=-\mathcal{V}(t,s)(\mathcal{A}(s)+\mathcal{B}(s))\binom{x}{y}.

Hence, we conclude that there exists a fundamental solution of (
nACP
) corresponding to the family of operators
(\mathcal{A}(t)+\mathcal{B}(t),\mathrm{D}(\mathcal{A}(t)))_{t\in\left[0,T\right]}
according to Definition
2.3
.
∎

•

,

5.
Example: Non-Autonomous Wave Equation

Motivated by
[
12
, Sec. 5]
, we consider the following perturbed non-autonomous wave equation on
\mathrm{L}^{2}\left(0,\pi\right)
given by

(5.1)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t^{2}}w(t,\xi)=\alpha(t)\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\xi^{2}}w(t,\xi)+\beta(t,\xi)w(t,\xi),&\quad t\in\left(0,T\right],\ \xi\in\left(0,\pi\right),\\
w(t,0)=w(t,\pi)=0,&\quad t\in\left(0,T\right],\\
w(0,\xi)=\varphi(\xi),&\\
\frac{\partial}{\partial t}w(0,\xi)=\psi(\xi),&\quad\xi\in\left[0,\pi\right],\end{cases}

where
T>0
,
\varphi,\psi\in\mathrm{L}^{2}\left(0,\pi\right)
and
\alpha\colon\left[0,T\right]\to\mathbb{R}
is continuously differentiable such that
\alpha(t)\geq 1
for all
t\in\left[0,T\right]
. Moreover, we assume that
\beta\in\mathrm{C}^{2}([0,T]\times\left[0,\pi\right])
. For
t\in\left[0,T\right]
, we define a family of operators
(A(t),\mathrm{D}(A(t)))_{t\in\left[0,T\right]}
on
X:=\mathrm{L}^{2}\left(0,\pi\right)
by
A(t)=\alpha(t)A_{0}
with dense domain
\mathrm{D}(A(t))=\mathrm{D}(A_{0})=:D
,
t\in\left[0,T\right]
, where

A_{0}f:=f^{\prime\prime},\quad\mathrm{D}(A_{0}):=\left\{f\in\mathrm{H}^{2}\left(0,\pi\right):\ f(0)=f(\pi)=0\right\}

is the Dirichlet Laplacian. In particular, Assumption
2.1
is satisfied.
Since
\alpha
is continuous, there exists
C>0
such that
\alpha(t)\leq C
for all
t\in[0,T]
, and therefore by the assumption
\alpha\geq 1
, we observe

\|\cdot\|_{A_{0}}\leq\|\cdot\|_{A(t)}\leq C\|\cdot\|_{A_{0}}

for all
t\in[0,T]
, which implies that Assumption
3.1
holds.
Moreover, as
\alpha
is continuously differentiable, Assumption
3.2
is satisfied as well.

Furthermore, we introduce a family of bounded operators
(B(t))_{t\in\left[0,T\right]}
by

B(t)f:=\beta(t,\cdot)f,\quad t\in\left[0,T\right]

Then (
5.1
) has an abstract form of a non-autonomous second-order abstract Cauchy problem

(5.2)

\begin{cases}\ddot{u}(t)=(A(t)+B(t))u(t),&\quad t\in\left(0,T\right],\\
u(0)=\varphi,&\\
\dot{u}(0)=\psi.&\end{cases}

As elaborated by Henríquez and Pozo in
[
12
]
, for
n\in\mathbb{N}
let
z_{n}\colon[0,\pi]\to\mathbb{R}
,
z_{n}(\xi):=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\sin(n\xi)
. Then
(z_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is an orthonormal basis of
\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,\pi)
of eigenfunctions of
A_{0}
corresponding to the sequence of eigenvalues
(-n^{2})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and the family of bounded linear operators
(S(t,s))_{(t,s)\in\Delta}
on
\mathrm{L}^{2}\left(0,\pi\right)
defined by

S(t,s)x:=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}{r_{n}(t,s)\left\langle x,z_{n}\right\rangle z_{n}},

provides a fundamental solution to the second-order non-autonomous abstract Cauchy problem associated to
(A(t),\mathrm{D}(A(t)))_{t\in\left[0,T\right]}
, where the functions
r_{n}
denote the solution of the initial value problem

(5.3)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}r^{\prime\prime}(t)+n^{2}\alpha(t)r(t)=0,&\quad 0\leq s\leq t\leq T,\\
r(s)=0,&\\
r^{\prime}(s)=1.&\end{cases}

Note that we have
|r_{n}(t,s)|\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha(s)}n}\leq\frac{1}{n}
,
|\mathop{\frac{\partial}{\partial{t}}}r_{n}(t,s)|\leq 1
,
|\mathop{\frac{\partial}{\partial{s}}}r_{n}(t,s)|\leq 1
and
|\mathop{\frac{\partial}{\partial{t}}}\mathop{\frac{\partial}{\partial{s}}}r_{n}(t,s)|\leq n
for all
(t,s)\in\Delta
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
; cf.
[
12
, (5.12)]
as well as
[
29
]
. By spectral theory,

D=\{x\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,\pi):\;\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}n^{4}\langle x,z_{n}\rangle^{2}<\infty\}.

Let

Z:=\mathrm{H}_{0}^{1}\left(0,\pi\right)=\left\{f\in\mathrm{H}^{1}\left(0,\pi\right):\ f(0)=f(\pi)=0\right\}=\{x\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,\pi):\ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}n^{2}\langle x,z_{n}\rangle^{2}<\infty\}.

The estimates on the
r_{n}
imply that the hypotheses on
(S(t,s))_{(t,s)\in\Delta}
in Theorem
4.2
are satisfied, due to uniform convergence of the series representations. Note that these hypotheses are the same as those in Theorem
3.4
(
a
).

Since
\beta\in\mathrm{C}^{2}([0,T]\times[0,\pi])
we easily obtain
B(\cdot)\in\mathrm{C}([0,T];\mathscr{L}_{\mathrm{s}}(X))\cap\mathrm{C}([0,T];\mathscr{L}_{\mathrm{s}}(Z))
. Thus, Theorem
4.2
yields a fundamental solution to (
5.2
).
