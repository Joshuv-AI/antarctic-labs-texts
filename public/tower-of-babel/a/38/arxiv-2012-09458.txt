Theoretical proposals to measure resonator-induced modifications of the electronic ground-state in doped quantum wells
Yuan Wang, Simone De Liberato

Abstract
Recent interest in the physics of non-perturbative light-matter coupling led to the development of solid-state cavity quantum electrodynamics setups in which the interaction energies are comparable with the bare ones. In such a regime the ground state of the coupled system becomes interaction-dependent and is predicted to contain a population of virtual excitations which, notwithstanding having been object of many investigations, remain still unobserved. In this paper we investigate how virtual electronic excitations in quantum wells modify the ground-state charge distribution, and propose two methods to measure such a cavity-induced perturbation. The first approach we consider is based on spectroscopic mapping of the electronic population at a specific location in the quantum well using localised defect states. The second approach exploits instead the photonic equivalent of a Kelvin probe to measure the average change distribution across the quantum well. We find both effects observable with present-day or near-future technology. Our results thus provide a route toward a demonstration of cavity-induced modulation of ground-state electronic properties.



I
Introduction

The many advances in the fabrication of nanophotonic resonators and nanostructured materials have made solid-state cavity quantum electrodynamics (CQED) an interdisciplinary research domain, with applications ranging from chemistry
feist_polaritonic_2018
to machine learning
ballarini_polaritonics_2019
. One of the figures of merit of CQED setups which has seen sustained improvements is the coupling strength between light and matter. When their mutual interaction energy becomes comparable with the excitation energy, higher order effects become observable, a regime called ultrastrong coupling
de_bernardis_cavity_2018
;
frisk_kockum_ultrastrong_2019
;
forn-diaz_ultrastrong_2019
. In 2009 the impact of these higher-order perturbative effects was observed for the first time in the anomalous shift of intersubband polariton resonances: transitions between conduction subbands in doped semiconductor quantum wells (QWs) strongly coupled to photonic resonators
anappara_signatures_2009
.
Large couplings with the photonic vacuum is expected to modify not only the system’s optical response, but also the underlying matter degrees of freedom.
Interest in the impact of CQED effects on electronic and molecular degrees of freedom
dates back to Khurgin’s idea of very strong coupling
khurgin_excitonic_2001
;
yang_verification_2015
;
brodbeck_experimental_2017
;
khurgin_pliable_2018
, but only in more recent years a broader interest followed
galego_cavity-induced_2015
;
cwik_excitonic_2016
;
cortese_collective_2017
;
schafer_ab_2018
;
levinsen_microscopic_2019
;
ebbesen_hybrid_2016
;
herrera_cavity-controlled_2016
;
galego_many-molecule_2017
;
ruggenthaler_quantum-electrodynamical_2018
;
ribeiro_polariton_2018
;
thomas_tilting_2019
;
cortese_excitons_2020
.

Crucially for this work, the ground state of an ultrastrongly coupled CQED system is predicted to host a cloud of virtual excitations, both photonic and matter ones
ciuti_quantum_2005
. These excitations are predicted to be stable also in realistic dissipative environments
de_liberato_virtual_2017
but, notwithstanding many theoretical works proposing different approaches to observe them, a direct measurement is still missing.
Except a proposal to observe the virtual excitations via electro-optical sampling
benea-chelmus_electric_2019
, whose efficacy has been questioned
de_liberato_electro-optical_2019
, and one to use the Lamb shift of an ancilla qubit

lolli_ancillary_2015
, all the other proposals we are aware of deal with variants of one basic idea: to non-adiabatically modulate the system in order to make some of these excitations real
de_liberato_quantum_2007
;
carusotto_back-reaction_2012
;
stassi_spontaneous_2013
;
garziano_switching_2013
;
huang_photon_2014
;
cirio_multielectron_2019
;
falci_ultrastrong_2019
. One problem with this idea is that we need to consider a modulated, time-dependent system, in analogy with the dynamical Casimir effect
dodonov_current_2010
;
wilson_observation_2011
;
nation_colloquium_2012
, and similarly sensitive to the density of dressed states, not to the presence of vacuum excitations.
A second problem is that, in order to achieve non-vanishing emission, perturbation frequencies of the order of the bare optical frequency are necessary
gunter_sub-cycle_2009
;
halbhuber_non-adiabatic_2020
, a requirement which has until now thwarted any attempt to observe vacuum excitations.

This paper explores a novel approach to these problems, by noticing that
cavity-induced virtual electronic excitations do modify the ground-state charge distribution, and they can thus be measured exploiting already well-tested methods used to map charge distributions in nanoscopic systems without requiring any non-adiabatic modulation.
Our results thus provide both a route to a first direct observation of virtual excitations in the ultrastrong-coupling vacuum, and a test bench for theoretical and numerical approaches studying CQED-induced modifications of the electronic ground state.

\displaystyle\left[\hat{b}_{n\mathbf{q}},\hat{b}_{n^{\prime}\mathbf{q}^{\prime}}^{\dagger}\right]

. As shown in Appendix A, such an Hamiltonian can be diagonalised in the bosonic regime using a Hopfield-Bogoliubov rotation. The coupled theory is then described by free bosonic polaritonic operators, linear superpositions of the bare ones

\displaystyle\hat{p}_{s\mathbf{q}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m}\left(x_{smq}\hat{a}_{m\mathbf{q}}+z_{smq}\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{m\mathbf{-q}}\right)

\displaystyle+\sum_{n}\left(y_{snq}\hat{b}_{n\mathbf{q}}+w_{snq}\hat{b}_{n\mathbf{-q}}^{\dagger}\right).

The linear transformation in Eq. (
11
) can then
be inverted to yield

\displaystyle\hat{b}_{n\mathbf{q}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{s}\left(\bar{y}_{snq}\hat{p}_{s\mathbf{q}}-\bar{w}_{snq}\hat{p}^{\dagger}_{s-\mathbf{q}}\right).

(12)

From Eq. (
12
) we can calculate the total density of matter excitations in the
n
th QW in the coupled ground state defined by
\hat{p}_{s\mathbf{q}}\mathinner{|{G}\rangle}=0

\displaystyle\sigma_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{S}\sum_{\mathbf{q}}\mathinner{\langle{G}|}\hat{b}_{n\mathbf{q}}^{\dagger}\hat{b}_{n\mathbf{q}}\mathinner{|{G}\rangle}=\frac{1}{S}\sum_{s\mathbf{q}}\lvert w_{snq}\rvert^{2}.

(13)

Intersubband transitions modify the electronic density because, as clearly shown in the example in Fig.
1
, the electronic charge density in each subband is different. In Appendix B we extend the approach from Ref.

\displaystyle=\rho_{F}(z)+\sum_{n}\Xi(z-z_{n})\sigma_{n},

\displaystyle\Xi(z)

\displaystyle=\left[\lvert\psi_{2}(z)\rvert^{2}-\lvert\psi_{1}(z)\rvert^{2}\right].

In the following we will discuss two different experimental procedures to measure the quantity in Eq. (

), exploiting two different observables linked to the modifications it induces on the charge distribution. On the one hand, electronic excitations modify the charge distribution at a specific position

, leading to an average charge displacement when an electron jumps between the first two subbands.
In the two-subband approximation we are employing the level of asymmetry can be quantified by a single parameter: the average electron displacement induced by an intersubband transition

\displaystyle\Delta z

\displaystyle=\mathinner{\langle{\psi_{2}}|}\hat{z}\mathinner{|{\psi_{2}}\rangle}-\mathinner{\langle{\psi_{1}}|}\hat{z}\mathinner{|{\psi_{1}}\rangle}.

A graphical illustration of both

and

\displaystyle\approx\mathinner{|{F}\rangle}-\sum_{mn\mathbf{q}}\frac{\omega_{P}K_{mnq}}{\omega_{12}+\omega_{mq}}\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{m-\mathbf{q}}\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{n\mathbf{q}}\mathinner{|{F}\rangle},

, defined in Appendix A, embeds all the other microscopic details of the CQED setup.
From Eq. (
13
), to the lowest non-trivial order, we thus obtain the following expression for the electronic excitation density in the

th QW

\displaystyle{\sigma}_{n}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{S}\sum_{m\mathbf{q}}\frac{\omega_{P}^{2}K_{mnq}^{2}}{(\omega_{12}+\omega_{mq})^{2}}.

Still, for

It will thus be useful to partition the sum in Eq. (

\displaystyle{\sigma}_{n}\approx{\sigma}_{n}^{<}+\sigma_{n}^{>}.

(19)

The first component of Eq. (

\displaystyle{\sigma}_{n}^{<}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{S}\sum_{m\mathbf{q}}\frac{\omega_{P}^{2}K_{mnq}^{2}}{(\omega_{12}+\omega_{mq})^{2}}\Theta(\omega_{C}-\omega_{mq}),

We will develop all our theory assuming zero temperature, which is a good approximation even at room temperature for intersubband energies

In the previous Section we investigated the ground-state excited electronic population through a spectroscopic measurement of different charge distribution between the two subbands at a specific position
z_{d}
, quantified by the quantity
\Xi(z_{d})
. Here instead we will discuss how to electrostatically measure the average charge displacement

from Eq. (

The scheme here proposed is based on the fact that resonator-induced charge perturbation described by the second term in Eq. (
14
) creates a built-in electrostatic potential in each QW. Considering a sample surface
S\gg L^{2}
large enough to neglect border effects, such a potential can be determined solving the Poisson equation

\displaystyle\partial^{2}_{z}V(z)

\displaystyle=-\frac{e\sigma\Xi(z)}{\epsilon_{0}\epsilon_{r}}.

(30)

Writing a formal solution of Eq. (
30
)

\displaystyle V(z)=-\int_{-\infty}^{z}dz^{\prime}\int_{-\infty}^{z^{\prime}}dz^{\prime\prime}\;\frac{e\sigma\Xi(z^{\prime\prime})}{\epsilon_{0}\epsilon_{r}},

(31)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Xi(z)dz=0,

(32)

we can calculate the potential drop across the QW

\displaystyle\Delta V

\displaystyle=\frac{e\sigma}{\epsilon_{0}\epsilon_{r}}\int dzz\Xi(z).

(33)

Using Eq. (
14

\displaystyle\Delta V

\displaystyle=\frac{e\sigma\Delta z}{\epsilon_{0}\epsilon_{r}}.

From Eq. (

\sigma

\Delta z

The induced potential difference can not be measured with a standard voltmeter because it is not a difference in the Fermi level but an in-built equilibrium potential. In can nevertheless be measured using a modified Kelvin probe

;
Hutchison2013

Kelvin probes are usually employed to measure the difference in work function between a known reference material and a sample, by using an AFM tip as the top plate of a capacitor whose lower plate is the sample. When the distance between the two is modulated by the tip oscillation, the capacitance also varies. Being the potential difference fixed by the work functions, a change in the capacitance leads to a change in the charge on the capacitor’s plates, and thus to a measurable current.
The apparatus we will consider, schematised in Fig.
3
(a), is thus similar to the one considered in the previous Section, with the top mirror now doubling as capacitor top plate. Moreover, the top and bottom mirrors are now electrically connected and the time-dependent current flowing between them is measured as the tip oscillates.

Applying the Kirchoff’s law to the circuit equivalent of the apparatus shown in Fig.
3

Deriving over time the electron density on the mirror and using Eq. (

\displaystyle I_{K}

\displaystyle=eS\dot{\sigma}_{M}=eS\dot{L}\partial_{L}\frac{\Delta zn_{QW}\sigma+D}{L}

\displaystyle=eS\dot{L}\left[\Delta zn_{QW}\partial_{L}\frac{\sigma^{<}}{L}-\frac{\tilde{D}}{L^{2}}\right].

lead to time-dependent signals. This is to be expected given that a standard Kelvin probe is meant to measure the static in-built potentials

In order to filter out the background polarization we can introduce the two functions
g_{p}
with
p=\{2,3\}

\left[0,2\pi\right]

\displaystyle g_{p}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sin(x)}{\left[1+a\cos(x)\right]^{p}},

(37)

their

\displaystyle l_{p}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\int_{0}^{2\pi}g_{p}(x)^{2}dx},

(38)

and their normalised versions
\tilde{g}_{p}(x)=g_{p}(x)/l_{p}
.
Substituting Eq. (
22
) and Eq. (
23

\displaystyle I_{K}(t)

\displaystyle=I^{<}\tilde{g}_{3}(\omega_{m}t)+\frac{eSa\omega_{m}\tilde{D}l_{2}}{L_{0}}\tilde{g}_{2}(\omega_{m}t),

(39)

with

\displaystyle I^{<}

\displaystyle=\frac{Sa\omega_{C}\omega_{m}z_{12}^{2}\Delta ze^{3}n_{QW}\sigma_{e}l_{3}}{2\pi\epsilon_{0}c^{2}\hbar L_{0}^{2}}.

(40)

By projecting Eq. (

\tilde{f}_{p}

\displaystyle\eta

\displaystyle=1-\lvert\int_{0}^{2\pi}dx\tilde{g}_{2}(x)\tilde{g}_{3}(x)\rvert^{2},

(41)

VI
Conclusions

In this paper we have explored the possibility of detecting resonator-induced effects on the electronic ground state of a CQED system. In particular we discussed two schemes which can achieve such an objective by providing a first direct measurement of virtual electronic excitations in the ground state of a CQED system. Instead of focussing, as most proposals do, on the detection of photonic excitations through non-adiabatic modulation, we aim at detecting material excitations using an adiabatic modulation. Our estimates show that detection of electronic CQED-induced modifications of the electronic ground state could be within technological reach. Although highly not trivial, the experiments we propose would not incur in the many hurdles linked with sub-cycle optical modulation and detection, which have until now thwarted multiple efforts to observe ground-state virtual photons through the non-adiabatic route.

We conclude by pointing out that the two schemes we proposed are not intended to represent an exhaustive list. The general theory developed in Sec.
II
shows how the ground-state charge distribution in doped quantum wells is modified by the coupling with the electromagentic field. The results of many experiments which can be performed on QWs depend on different details of the ground-state charge distribution and we thus expect many different experiments could be used to highlight its CQED-induced modifications. Other possible examples which are not investigated in this paper include nonlinear emission in asymmetric QWs
Khurgin1989
or lifting of selection rules is symmetric ones.

We hope this work will stimulate novel interest toward the physics of virtual excitations and more broadly on the study of CQED effects on electronic degrees of freedom.

Appendix A
Diagonalization of the PZW Hamiltonian

Following Ref.
todorov_dipolar_2015
, the PZW Hamiltonian describing the system can be written in the bosonic approximation

\displaystyle\hat{H}=\sum_{\mathbf{q}}\hbar\left(\hat{W}_{0\mathbf{q}}+\hat{W}_{I\mathbf{q}}+\hat{W}_{D\mathbf{q}}\right),

(42)

with the first part describing the bare fields

\displaystyle\hat{W}_{0\mathbf{q}}=

\displaystyle\sum_{n}\omega_{12}\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{n\mathbf{q}}\hat{b}_{n\mathbf{q}}+\sum_{m}\omega_{mq}\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{m\mathbf{q}}\hat{a}_{m\mathbf{q}},

(43)

the second their interaction

\displaystyle\hat{W}_{I\mathbf{q}}=

\displaystyle\sum_{mn}\omega_{P}K_{mnq}(\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{m\mathbf{-q}}+\hat{a}_{m\mathbf{q}})(\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{n\mathbf{q}}+\hat{b}_{n\mathbf{-q}}),

(44)

and the third the self-interaction of the electronic polarization

\displaystyle\hat{W}_{D\mathbf{q}}=

\displaystyle\sum_{n}\frac{\omega_{P}^{2}}{4\omega_{12}}(\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{n\mathbf{q}}+\hat{b}_{n\mathbf{-q}})(\hat{b}_{n\mathbf{-q}}^{\dagger}+\hat{b}_{n\mathbf{q}}).

(45)

In Eq. (
44
) we have the plasma frequency for a single QW

\displaystyle\omega_{P}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{I_{12}\hbar e^{2}\sigma_{e}}{2m_{e}^{*2}\epsilon_{0}\epsilon_{r}\omega_{12}},

(46)

with
m_{e}^{*}
the effective mass of conduction electrons and the dimensionless coupling coefficient

\displaystyle K_{mnq}^{2}=\omega_{mq}\omega_{12}\frac{z_{12}^{2}m_{e}^{*2}}{\hbar^{2}LI_{12}}\frac{2\cos^{2}\theta_{mq}}{(1+\delta_{m0})}\cos^{2}(\frac{\pi mz_{n}}{L}),

(47)

parametrising the coupling between the bright matter mode in the
n
th QW and the TM
m

\theta_{mq}
the angle of propagation relative to the
z
-axis

\displaystyle\cos\theta_{mq}=\frac{cq}{\sqrt{\epsilon_{r}}\omega_{mq}}.

(48)

Note that the term in Eq. (
45
), quadratic in the plasma frequency
\omega_{P}
, is responsible for the phenomenon of polarization shift

;
de_liberato_quantum_2012
.
This can be seen by considering only the terms of the full Hamiltonian describing the bare field and the electron-electron interaction

\displaystyle\hat{W}_{0\mathbf{q}}^{\prime}=\hat{W}_{0\mathbf{q}}+\hat{W}_{D\mathbf{q}},

(49)

and performing a Bogoliubov rotation over the
\hat{b}_{n\mathbf{q}}
operators, leading to a new Hamiltonian describing modified intersubband transitions operators
\hat{d}_{n\mathbf{q}}
whose frequency is renormalised by the interaction

\displaystyle\hat{W}_{0\mathbf{q}}^{\prime}=

\displaystyle\sum_{n}\sqrt{\omega_{12}^{2}+\omega_{P}^{2}}\,\hat{d}^{\dagger}_{n\mathbf{q}}\hat{d}_{n\mathbf{q}}+\sum_{m}\omega_{mq}\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{m\mathbf{q}}\hat{a}_{m\mathbf{q}}.

(50)

The electronic wavefunctions in the two subbands enter in play through the definition of the

\displaystyle z_{12}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\hbar}{2m_{e}^{*}\omega_{12}}\int\left[\bar{\psi}_{1}(z)\partial_{z}\psi_{2}(z)-\psi_{2}(z)\partial_{z}\bar{\psi}_{1}(z)\right]dz,

and of the normalization factor

\displaystyle I_{12}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\left[\bar{\psi}_{1}(z)\partial_{z}\psi_{2}(z)-\psi_{2}(z)\partial_{z}\bar{\psi}_{1}(z)\right]^{2}dz.

(52)

In the bosonic regime we can then use the Hopfield-Bogoliubov approach to diagonalise
\hat{H}
in Eq. (
42

\displaystyle\hat{p}_{s\mathbf{q}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m}\left(x_{smq}\hat{a}_{m\mathbf{q}}+z_{smq}\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{m\mathbf{-q}}\right)

(53)

\displaystyle+\sum_{n}\left(y_{snq}\hat{b}_{n\mathbf{q}}+w_{snq}\hat{b}_{n\mathbf{-q}}^{\dagger}\right),

is defined by

.
The linear transformation in Eq. (

Appendix B
Derivation of the bosonic expression for the electron density

Here we will provide a derivation of the equation

\displaystyle\rho_{G}(z)-\rho_{F}(z)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{S}\sum_{n\mathbf{q}}\left[\lvert\psi_{2}(z-z_{n})\rvert^{2}-\lvert\psi_{1}(z-z_{n})\rvert^{2}\right]

(55)

\displaystyle\times\mathinner{\langle{G}|}\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{n\mathbf{q}}\hat{b}_{n\mathbf{q}}\mathinner{|{G}\rangle},

describing the change of the electronic density in the ground state created by the vacuum excitations. While physically intuitive its derivation requires some care as it links fermionic to bosonised quantities. We will thus expand the approach originally developed in Ref.
cortese_strong_2019
to calculate the electronic wavefunctions of photon-bound excitons.

We start by introducing the electron field operator projected on the first two subbands

\displaystyle\hat{\Psi}(\mathbf{r})=\sum_{n\mathbf{k}}\left[\phi_{1n\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r})\hat{c}_{1n\mathbf{k}}+\phi_{2n\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r})\hat{c}_{2n\mathbf{k}}\right].

(56)

Using as reference the electronic distribution in the absence of the resonator, we can then write the induced electron density as

\displaystyle\rho_{G}(z)-\rho_{F}(z)

\displaystyle=\mathinner{\langle{G}|}\hat{\Psi}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{r})\hat{\Psi}(\mathbf{r})\mathinner{|{G}\rangle}-\mathinner{\langle{F}|}\hat{\Psi}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{r})\hat{\Psi}(\mathbf{r})\mathinner{|{F}\rangle}.

(57)

The expression in Eq. (
57
) can be simplified by noticing that both the free and coupled Hamiltonians, with ground states
\mathinner{|{F}\rangle}
and
\mathinner{|{G}\rangle}
respectively, commute with the parity of the total excitation number operator

\displaystyle\hat{T}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m\mathbf{q}}\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{m\mathbf{q}}\hat{a}_{m\mathbf{q}}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n\mathbf{k}}\left(\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{2n\mathbf{k}}\hat{c}_{2n\mathbf{k}}-\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{1n\mathbf{k}}\hat{c}_{1n\mathbf{k}}\right).

(58)

Both the coupled and free ground states have thus a well defined excitation number parity.
This implies that all the ground states expectation values
involving terms which increase
\hat{T}
by one, like
\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{2n\mathbf{k}}\hat{c}_{1n^{\prime}\mathbf{k}}
, have to vanish. Exploiting the fact that the number of electrons in each QW is fixed

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\left(\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{1n\mathbf{k}}\hat{c}_{1n\mathbf{k}}+\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{2n\mathbf{k}}\hat{c}_{2n\mathbf{k}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sigma_{e}S,

(59)

we can then put Eq. (
57
) in the form

\displaystyle\rho_{G}(z)-\rho_{F}(z)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{S}\sum_{n\mathbf{k}}\left[\lvert\psi_{2}(z-z_{n})\rvert^{2}-\lvert\psi_{1}(z-z_{n})\rvert^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\times\mathinner{\langle{G}|}\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{2n\mathbf{k}}\hat{c}_{2n\mathbf{k}}\mathinner{|{G}\rangle}.

(60)

In order to prove Eq. (
55
) we have thus to demonstrate that

\displaystyle\mathinner{\langle{G}|}\hat{N}_{Fn}\mathinner{|{G}\rangle}

\displaystyle=\mathinner{\langle{G}|}\hat{N}_{Bn}\mathinner{|{G}\rangle},

(61)

that is that the total number of electrons in the second subband of any QW

\displaystyle\hat{N}_{Fn}=\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{2n\mathbf{k}}\hat{c}_{2n\mathbf{k}},

(62)

and the total number of matter excitations in the same QW

\displaystyle\hat{N}_{Bn}=\sum_{\mathbf{q}}\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{n\mathbf{q}}\hat{b}_{n\mathbf{q}},

(63)

have the same expectation value in the coupled ground state. Note that by construction

\displaystyle\hat{N}_{Fn}\mathinner{|{F}\rangle}

\displaystyle=\hat{N}_{Bn}\mathinner{|{F}\rangle}=0,

(64)

and they thus also trivially coincide in the uncoupled ground state.
Using the definition of the intersubband transition operator in terms of electron operators

\displaystyle\hat{b}_{n\mathbf{q}}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{S\sigma_{e}}}\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{2n\mathbf{k+q}}\hat{c}_{1n\mathbf{k}},

(65)

we can verify that the fermionic commutator of
\hat{N}_{Fn}
with the right-hand-side of Eq. (
65
)

\displaystyle\left[\hat{N}_{Fn},\hat{b}_{n\mathbf{q}}^{\dagger}\right]

\displaystyle=\hat{b}_{n\mathbf{q}}^{\dagger},

(66)

generates the same algebra as the commutator of
\hat{N}_{Bn}
with the left-hand-side of the same equation, when considering the
\hat{b}_{n\mathbf{q}}
as perfect bosons,

\displaystyle\left[\hat{N}_{Bn},\hat{b}_{n\mathbf{q}}^{\dagger}\right]

\displaystyle=\hat{b}_{n\mathbf{q}}^{\dagger}.

(67)

Using the same bosonic assumption the coupled ground state
\mathinner{|{G}\rangle}
can be easily written in a perturbative expansion as a sum over orthogonal vectors
\mathinner{|{G}\rangle}=\sum_{\zeta}\mathinner{|{\zeta}\rangle}
, with

\displaystyle\mathinner{|{\zeta}\rangle}

\displaystyle=\chi_{\zeta}\prod_{j=1}^{j_{\zeta}}\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{q}_{\zeta j}}\prod_{h=1}^{h_{\zeta}}\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{m_{\zeta h}\mathbf{q}_{\zeta h}}\mathinner{|{F}\rangle}.

(68)

Exploiting Eq. (
64
) and the fact that
N_{Fn}
and
N_{Bn}
have the same commutation relations with all the operators appearing in the
\mathinner{|{\zeta}\rangle}
states, their expectation value in the ground state is necessarily the same, proving Eq. (
61
) and thus concluding our proof of Eq. (
55
).

Appendix C
Casimir-induced polarization

In the main body of the paper we considered two sources of induced polarization: the virtual excitations due to the transverse electromagnetic mode confinement, leading to
\sigma_{n}\propto L^{-1}
, and in-built potentials independent from
L
.
At small scales there are nevertheless other interactions between the mirror and the electron gas which can lead to induced polarization and, should those forces be comparable or larger than the ones due to the transverse field, they would need to be taken into account when performing data analysis.
In this Appendix we will derive the general form for the polarization induced on the
n
th QW from an arbitrary mirror-induced pressure
P(d_{n})
depending upon the distance between the mirror and the quantum well
d_{n}=L-z_{n}
.
We will also provide an upper estimate of such an effect for the Casimir force, showing that, although potentially measurable, such a force is not large enough to cover the electrostatic signal whose measurement we describe in Sec.
IV
.

A pressure
P(d_{n})
translates in a force per single electron
F(d_{n})=P(d_{n})/\sigma_{e}
and its impact on one electron in the nth QW can thus be described by the Hamiltonian

\displaystyle H_{n}

\displaystyle=F(d_{n})\hat{z},

(69)

with
\hat{z}
the electron coordinate.
To the lowest order the modified wavefunction in the first subband can thus be written as

\displaystyle\mathinner{|{\psi_{1}^{\prime}}\rangle}

\displaystyle=\mathinner{|{\psi_{1}}\rangle}-\frac{F(d_{n})}{\hbar\omega_{12}}\mathinner{\langle{\psi_{2}}|}\hat{z}\mathinner{|{\psi_{1}}\rangle}\mathinner{|{\psi_{2}}\rangle},

(70)

and the corresponding shift in electron position is thus, to the lowest order

\displaystyle\Delta z^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\mathinner{\langle{\psi_{1}^{\prime}}|}\hat{z}\mathinner{|{\psi_{1}^{\prime}}\rangle}-\mathinner{\langle{\psi_{1}}|}\hat{z}\mathinner{|{\psi_{1}}\rangle}\approx\frac{2F(d_{n})z_{12}^{2}}{\hbar\omega_{12}}.

(71)

The total dipole density induced in the QW is thus

\displaystyle P^{\prime}_{n}=\sigma_{e}\Delta z^{\prime},

(72)

which needs to be added to Eq. (
35
).

While precise calculations of Casimir force in planar systems are possible
Klimchitskaya2000
they are beyond the scope of the present paper. We can instead obtain an upper bound of such an effect by assuming that the electrons gas in a single QW is dense enough to act as a perfect mirror. Note that this would only be true for unphysical densities leading to coupling frequencies larger than
\omega_{12}
as shown in Ref.
DeLiberato2014
. In the sample we are considering the effect of a single QW is instead largely perturbative and the result we will obtain only represents a very loose overestimate of the actual effect.
We can then consider each QW as a perfect mirror and use the analytical expression for the Casimir pressure between two planar metallic mirrors

\displaystyle P(d_{n})

\displaystyle=-\frac{\hbar c\pi^{2}}{240\sqrt{\epsilon_{r}}d_{n}^{4}}.

(73)

Plugging Eq. (
73
) into Eq. (
71
) we obtain from Eq. (
72
) the induced dipole, and using the parameters from Sec.
V
we conclude that even at the point of the oscillation in which the mirror is the closest to the QW stack, and considering a QW on the very top of the stack, the induced dipole is only one-half of the CQED-induced one, dropping to
10^{-4}
times smaller for a QW on the bottom of the stack when the mirror is at its furthest point. These estimates, as rough as they are, demonstrate that the Casimir effect, if not always completely negligible, does not represent a critical obstacle to the measurement scheme described in Sec.
IV
and
a fortiori
for the one from Sec.
III
.
