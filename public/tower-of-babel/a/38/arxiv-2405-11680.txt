Contrasting chaotic and stochastic forcing: tipping windows and attractor crises
Peter Ashwin, Julian Newman, Raphael Römer

Abstract
Nonlinear dynamical systems subjected to a combination of noise and time-varying forcing can exhibit sudden changes, critical transitions or tipping points where large or rapid dynamic effects arise from changes in a parameter that are small or slow. Noise-induced tipping can occur where extremes of the forcing causes the system to leave one attractor and transition to another. If this noise corresponds to unresolved chaotic forcing, there is a limit such that this can be approximated by a stochastic differential equation (SDE) and the statistics of large deviations determine the transitions. Away from this limit it makes sense to consider tipping in the presence of chaotic rather than stochastic forcing. In general we argue that close to a parameter value where there is a bifurcation of the unforced system, there will be a chaotic tipping window outside of which tipping cannot happen, in the limit of asymptotically slow change of that parameter. This window is trivial for a stochastically forced system. Entry into the chaotic tipping window can be seen as a boundary crisis/non-autonomous saddle-node bifurcation and corresponds to an exceptional case of the forcing, typically by an unstable periodic orbit. We discuss an illustrative example of a chaotically forced bistable map that highlight the richness of the geometry and bifurcation structure of the dynamics in this case. If a parameter is changing slowly we note there is a dynamic tipping window that can also be determined in terms of unstable periodic orbits.



1
Introduction

For complex nonlinear systems subjected to a time-varying input (that we call forcing in general), there may be critical transitions (or tipping points) where a small change in input leads to a large change in system state. In many contexts both intrinsic noisy variability and slow drift variability in parameters have a strong deterministic component and either of these may give rise to abrupt changes via noise-induced or bifurcation-induced tipping
[
21
,
22
]
. Moreover, if the slow drift is not slow enough, rate-induced tipping may also appear
[
4
,
3
,
34
]
.

Indeed, such effects may already appear within a subsystem of an autonomous (unforced) nonlinear multiscale system because of other timescales present within the system. As an example, noise in climate models is often seen to be result of fast forcing by turbulent fluid dynamics of ocean and atmosphere, while variation appears due to slow forcing such as changes in sea level or land ice. Each of these fast noise and slow drift processes has a distinct effect on the dynamics of the climate. This relates to Hasselmann’s programme to understand weather as “noise” added to climate: see for example
[
27
]
for a recent review, and is particularly relevant in case where critical transitions/tipping points and chaotic variability are both present - see for example
[
26
,
8
,
2
]
. In many problems of practical importance there are no clear timescale separations, meaning that we are not at the limit where stochastic forcing is appropriate.

The paper is organized as follows. In Sec.
2
we briefly review relations between stochastic and chaotic forced models with tipping, and describe the chaotic tipping window that appears in the chaotically forced case. Sec.
3
discusses a discrete-time chaotically forced bistable system where we analyse the structure of the dynamics and geometry within phase and parameter space. Implications of slow variation of parameters in this model are discussed in Sec.
4
, where we introduce the dynamic tipping window. In Sec.
5
discuss possible implications and lessons for more general cases.

Tipping points and the chaotic tipping window

Systems of the form (

3.3
Geometry and dimension of the chaotic saddle

In cases of bistability, the basin boundary contains a chaotic saddle
\mathcal{C}_{\beta,\sigma}
(actually a repeller) that occupies all of the boundary. As observed in Figure
2
, this invariant set is a repeller of the skew product system whose geometry we remark on here in relation to the literature of repellers in skew affine systems; see for example
[
33
,
32
]
. In this section we give some numerical estimates of the dimension of
\mathcal{C}_{\beta,\sigma}
both via a box counting algorithm
[
10
]
and using the Lyapunov exponents and escape time from
\mathcal{C}_{\beta,\sigma}
to give a Lyapunov dimension
[
18
]
, as estimates of the Hausdorff dimension of
\mathcal{C}_{\beta,\sigma}
.

Note that for the affine skew product

x_{n+1}=[\lambda(\theta_{n})]^{-1}x_{n}+\sigma\cos(\theta_{n})

with
\lambda(\theta)>1
there will be a repelling invariant graph
[
6
]
whose box dimension is given in terms of the thermodynamic formalism by
[
6
]
as a
t
that is a zero of a pressure function that in our case is

\mathcal{P}\left((1-t)\ln|\partial_{\theta}g|-\ln|\partial_{x}f|\right)=0

also called Bowen’s formula
[
6
,
33
]
. In the case that the expansion in the
\theta
direction is uniform at rate
B>1
and in the
x
direction is uniform at rate
A>1
this reduces to

\ln B+(1-t)\ln B-\ln A=0

and so it is possible to show that the invariant graph has Hausdorff dimension
2-\ln(A)/\ln(B)
. These results do not obviously extend to nonlinear skew products so we explore the dimensions of the chaotic saddle numerically.

3.3.1
Box-counting dimension

Making use of the skew product structure of the system we approximate the shape of the saddle via forward and backward iterations as follows: We choose a box
B_{\beta,\sigma}=[x_{l},x_{u}]\times[\theta_{l},\theta_{u}]
that contains a part of
\mathcal{C}_{\beta,\sigma}
, and randomly choose
k
values of
\theta\in[\theta_{l},\theta_{u}]
. Iterating the base map (i.e.
\theta_{n+1}=3\theta_{n}\mod 2
) forward for
N
iterations and storing the trajectories, allows evaluation of the inverted fibre map under
N
iterations backward in time. Note that under time inversion, the fibre map becomes attracting towards
\mathcal{C}_{\beta,\sigma}
.
This procedure results in an approximation of the saddle with
k
points.

Then, we compute the saddle’s box-counting dimension. This is done by considering the number
T(\kappa)
of non-intersecting boxes with edge-length
\kappa
that is needed to cover
\mathcal{C}_{\beta,\sigma}
.
The dimension is then given by:

\displaystyle d_{box}(\mathcal{C}_{\beta,\sigma})

\displaystyle=\lim_{\kappa\to 0}\frac{\ln T(\kappa)}{\ln(1/\kappa)}

(15)

Rearranging this formula for a finite value
\kappa
gives

\displaystyle\ln T(\kappa)

\displaystyle\propto d_{box}(\mathcal{C})\cdot\ln T(\kappa).

(16)

Thus, plotting
\ln T(\kappa)
vs.
\ln T(\kappa)
, and assuming that this relation correctly describes the behaviour for small
\kappa
allows an estimation of the box counting dimension via a linear fit
[
31
]
.
The results are presented in Table

3.3.2

\displaystyle D_{L}

5
Discussion

In this paper we have explored the effect of bounded
chaotic forcing
as a model for noise that we argue may in many applications be closer to the truth than the
stochastic forcing
, i.e. unbounded Wiener noise that can be obtained in a limit of fast chaos.

By analysing an explicit example of a discrete time bistable map with chaotic forcing we are able to understand the interaction between multiple attractors and bounded noise forcing and the appearance of a chaotic tipping window. The start of this window can be seen as a boundary crisis
[
24
]
of an attractor of the skew product system or as a non-autonomous saddle-node bifurcation of the forced system. This becomes a dynamic tipping window if there is a slow variation of parameters in addition to chaotic forcing.

The problem of precisely which situations of forcing give rise to tipping and at what time is likely to be quite complicated in the presence of non-uniform parameter variation, especially when the timescale of change of
\beta
is faster than the timescales associated with attraction to and mixing on the chaotic attractor. The chaos-forced transitions discussed can be related to bifurcations in set-valued dynamical systems such as investigated in
[
25
]
as a model for bounded noise forced systems.

In this paper, although we initially discuss continuous time models with chaotic forcing, we do not consider any explicit examples for continuous time models with chaos. These have been considered elsewhere, for example
[
2
,
5
]
consider a bistable (Stommel) system forced by a chaotic (Lorenz) system as a conceptual model for tipping of the Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation (AMOC) in response to fast chaotic variability, looking at cases where the system limits to an autonomous system in the past and future limits.

Some of the results here can presumably be generalized to more general cases of non-autonomous parameter variation. For example, considering
\beta_{n}=\max(0,\min(n\epsilon,\beta^{\max}))
can be investigated using examples as in Section
4
. Such a case is analogous to a discrete “parameter shift” between
\beta=0
and
\beta=\beta^{\max}>0
at a rate
\epsilon

[
4
,
3
]
. By picking
\theta_{0}
and
x_{0}
on one of the attractors for
\beta=0
and then fixing
\beta^{\max}
we expect to find different behaviours depending on where the chaotic tipping window lies in relation to
[0,\beta^{\max}]
.

The tipping thresholds in general will involve considering extreme measures of the forcing map that may not be so simple as in this example: see
[
19
]
for a recent review. For the example considered, one can include chaotic forcing of arbitrary amplitude by varying the parameter
\sigma
. Nonetheless, the large positive Lyapunov exponent
\ln(3)
of the forcing chaos is always fast with respect to the dynamics of forced map unless
|\beta|
is large. There are likely to be other effects present when the chaotic forcing becomes comparable or weaker than the attraction for the forced map, and it may be interesting to investigate such cases. We do not discuss here the problem of finding early warnings of an impending tipping point in this context. However, the observation that the fibre LE does not need to be zero at the crisis suggests that methods based purely on LEs are likely to miss prediction of an impending tipping point. See also for example
[
21
,
23
]
. Conversely, as suggested in
[
30
]
, it may be possible to use the detailed dynamics of the forced system to control the location of the tipping point or the behaviour after tipping. This will effectively require coupling from the forced system back to the forcing, and we do not consider this here.

A.7

Note that

A.8

Lemma 2

Proof.

For notational convenience, given

Since
\mathcal{A}^{+}_{\beta,\sigma}
is
G_{\beta,\sigma}
-invariant and
\varphi^{n}(\infty)\to 0
as

, this gives the desired result.

Fix
(x,\theta)\in[x^{*},\infty)\times[0,2\pi)
. Pick a point
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\Theta
whose
0
-coordinate is equal to
\theta
, and set

. We have that both

and
\tilde{x}
belong to

; and so for all
n\geq 0
, since the diameter of
f_{\beta,\sigma,\theta,n}([x^{*},\infty])
is at most

Now if

is strictly greater than

A.9
Construction and properties of
\mu_{\beta,\sigma}^{-}

Let us first verify that the involution

serves as a conjugacy between

and

:

\displaystyle\big(G_{\beta,\sigma}\circ\iota\big)(x,\theta)

\displaystyle=G_{\beta,\sigma}(-x,\theta+\pi)

\displaystyle=(\alpha\atan(-x)+\beta+\sigma\cos(\theta+\pi)\,,\,3(\theta+\pi))

\displaystyle=(-\alpha\atan(x)+\beta-\sigma\cos(\theta)\,,\,3\theta+3\pi)

\displaystyle=\big(-(\alpha\atan(x)-\beta+\sigma\cos(\theta))\,,\,3\theta+\pi\big)

\displaystyle=\iota\big(\alpha\atan(x)-\beta+\sigma\cos(\theta)\,,\,3\theta\big)

\displaystyle=\big(\iota\circ G_{-\beta,\sigma}\big)(x,\theta).

Since

for every

we can define

and

A.10
Construction of

Rather than seeking to define
\mathcal{C}_{\beta,\sigma}
directly in terms of the basins of attraction of
\mathcal{A}^{\pm}_{\beta,\sigma}
, we will first present a construction of the function

, and we will define
\mathcal{C}_{\beta,\sigma}
to be the graph of

; with these definitions, we will prove all the required properties, including that when both
\mathcal{A}^{-}_{\beta,\sigma}
and
\mathcal{A}^{+}_{\beta,\sigma}
exist,

is the basin boundary of both
\mathcal{A}^{-}_{\beta,\sigma}
and
\mathcal{A}^{+}_{\beta,\sigma}
.

Recall that the parameter region
\mathrm{A}
is defined to be

. For all
(\beta,\sigma,\theta)\in\mathrm{A}\times[0,2\pi)
, since
\beta-\sigma\geq-\beta^{*}
we have as in Sec.

that

Combining these two facts, we have that

Therefore, noting that for all

f_{\beta,\sigma,\theta,n+1}^{-1}=f_{\beta,\sigma,\theta,n}^{-1}\circ f_{\beta,\sigma,g^{n}(\theta)}^{-1}

on the range of

is strictly less than

is strictly less than
x^{*}-a_{-\beta^{*}}^{-}
and so

as

and therefore

Z=\left\{(x,\theta)\in\mathbb{R}\times[0,2\pi)\,:\,\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\#\left\{n\in\{1,\ldots,N\}:G_{\beta,\sigma}^{n}(x,\theta)\in Y\right\}=\mu_{\beta,\sigma}(Y)\right\}.

Since
\mu_{\beta,\sigma}
is a
G_{\beta,\sigma}
-ergodic probability measure, we have that
\mu_{\beta,\sigma}(Z)=1
. Now since
X_{\beta,\sigma,n}
converges in probability to
0
as
n\to\infty
, by Lemma
11
(applied to
\mathbbm{1}_{\{X_{\beta,\sigma,n}>\delta_{1}\}}
) there is a
\mathbb{P}
-full measure set
E_{0}\subset[0,2\pi)
such that for all
\theta\in E_{0}
,

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\#\left\{n\in\{1,\ldots,N\}:X_{\beta,\sigma,n}(\theta)\leq\delta_{1}\right\}=1.

Let
E
be the intersection of
E_{0}
and the
\theta
-projection of
Z
. So
E
is a
\mathbb{P}
-full measure set. We will show that for all
\theta\in E
,
X_{\beta,\sigma,n}(\theta)\to 0
exponentially as
n\to\infty
. Fix
\theta\in E
, and fix
x
such that
(x,\theta)\in Z
. Since

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\#\left\{n\in\{1,\ldots,N\}:X_{\beta,\sigma,n}(\theta)\leq\delta_{1}\right\}

\displaystyle=1\quad\textrm{and}

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\#\left\{n\in\{1,\ldots,N\}:G_{\beta,\sigma}^{n}(x,\theta)\in Y\right\}

\displaystyle>0,

there must exist
n_{0}\geq 1
with the properties that
X_{\beta,\sigma,n_{0}}(\theta)\leq\delta_{1}
and
G_{\beta,\sigma}^{n_{0}}(x,\theta)\in Y
. But

•

the fact that
X_{\beta,\sigma,n_{0}}(\theta)\leq\delta_{1}
implies that

f_{\beta,\sigma,\theta,n_{0}}(\overline{\mathbb{R}})\subset[f_{\beta,\sigma,\theta,n_{0}}(x)-\delta_{1},f_{\beta,\sigma,\theta,n_{0}}(x)+\delta_{1}]=:I_{n_{0}}

and therefore

f_{\beta,\sigma,\theta,n_{0}+n}(\overline{\mathbb{R}})\subset f_{\beta,\sigma,g^{n_{0}}(\theta),n}(I_{n_{0}})

(28)

for all
n\geq 0
;

•

the fact that
G_{\beta,\sigma}^{n_{0}}(x,\theta)\in Y
implies that

\mathrm{diam}(f_{\beta,\sigma,g^{n_{0}}(\theta),n}(I_{n_{0}}))\to 0\textrm{ exponentially as }n\to\infty.

(29)

Combining Eqs.
28
and (
29
) gives that
X_{\beta,\sigma,n}(\theta)=\mathrm{diam}(f_{\beta,\sigma,\theta,n}(\overline{\mathbb{R}}))\to 0
exponentially as
n\to\infty
.
∎

Proposition 14
.

For all
\sigma\geq 0
, there is no
\mathbb{P}
-full measure subset of
[0,2\pi)
on which

uniformly as

.

Proof.

The set
\{\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{3\pi}{2}\}
is a
2
-periodic orbit of
g
on which
\cos=0
, and hence

f_{0,\sigma,\frac{\pi}{2},n}=f_{0}^{n}

. Hence

\lim_{n\to\infty}X_{0,\sigma,n}(\tfrac{\pi}{2})=\left(\lim_{n\to\infty}f_{0}^{n}(\infty)\right)-\left(\lim_{n\to\infty}f_{0}^{n}(-\infty)\right)=2a_{0}>0.

Since

is continuous for each

∎
