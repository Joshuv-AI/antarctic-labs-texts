Concentration and mean field approximation results for Markov processes on large networks
Dániel Keliger, Balázs Ráth

Abstract
We study Markov processes on weighted directed hypergraphs where the state of at most one vertex can change at a time. Our setting is general enough to include simplicial epidemic processes, processes on multilayered networks or even the dynamics of the edges of a graph. Our results are twofold. Firstly, we prove concentration bounds for the number of vertices in a certain state under mild assumptions. Our results imply that even the empirical averages of subpopulations of diverging but possibly sublinear size are well concentrated around their mean. In the case of undirected weighted graphs, we completely characterize when said averages concentrate around their expected value. Secondly, we prove (under assumptions which are tight in some significant cases) upper bounds for the error of the N-Intertwined Mean Field Approximation (NIMFA). In particular, for undirected unweighted graphs, the error has the same order of magnitude as the reciprocal of the average degree, improving the previously known state of the art bound which had the same order of magnitude as the reciprocal of the square root of the average degree. Keywords: interacting particle systems on networks, dynamics with higher order interactions, N-Intertwined Mean Field Approximation, graphical construction, error bounds, epidemic modelling AMS MSC 2020: 82C22, 60K35, 34A30

1
Introduction

Interacting particle systems on graphs have been studied intensely as a flexible modeling tool. A wide range of applications include epidemic processes
[
9
]
, opinion dynamics
[
2
]
, computer protocols
[
13
]
, financial models
[
24
]
and statistical physics
[
23
]
.

The prime difficulty emerging while facing such processes is the rapid increase in complexity as the size of the system grows. The exact calculation of occupation probabilities involves the numerical solution of an exponential amount of ordinary differential equations, a task infeasible for modern computers even for graphs of moderate size.

The two main approaches to circumvent said issue are stochastic simulations and mean field approximations. The advantage of the former is its versatility and relative ease in implementation. However, Monte-Carlo methods require a sizable set of generated samples and assessing the effect of many parameters might be computationally demanding. As for mean field approximations, they offer a lower number of differential equations which need to be numerically integrated only once - as opposed to the stochastic simulations - and in same cases they even provide analytical tractability for certain quantities such as the basic reproduction number in epidemic models. On the other hand, their derivation usually relies on heuristics, making it hard to judge under what circumstances are they reliable.

Our paper focuses on the latter approach regarding the so called N-Intertwined Mean Field Approximation (NIMFA), also known as Quenched Mean Field Approximation
[
26
]
. NIMFA is an individual-based method approximating the occupation probabilities of vertices.

Our contributions are twofold.

Firstly, we show that, under some conditions on the interaction rates, (i) the maximal and (ii) the average error of NIMFA are of order
O\left(\frac{1}{d}\right)
, where
d
is the average degree of the graph (note that (i) is a stronger result, but we derive (ii) under weaker conditions).
This is a major improvement compared to the previously known
O\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\right)
bounds. We also show that both of the error bounds (i) and (ii) are in some sense tight. In other words, we rigorously clarify the precision (and thus the limitations) of NIMFA for a wide class of cases.

Secondly, we study whether the empirical averages of a subpopulation
\mathcal{M}
(say, the ratio of infected among people above the age of 65) concentrate around their expectation. We give an upper bound for the variance of empirical averages which is shown to be
O\left(\frac{1}{\left|\mathcal{M}\right|}\right)
under mild conditions for systems with pair interactions. Note that this bound is also tight since the variance of the empirical average of

\left|\mathcal{M}\right|
i.i.d. Bernoulli variables is also comparable to
\frac{1}{\left|\mathcal{M}\right|}
.

Lastly, when restricted to symmetric pair interactions, we give a useful equivalent characterization for the concentration of empirical averages, namely, we show that concentration happens if and only if an associated SI infection process cannot blow up if we start it from a single infected vertex that is uniformly chosen from
\mathcal{M}
.

We work under a quite general setup with Markovian transitions allowing higher order interactions - that is, two or more vertices together affecting the rate at which another vertex changes its state - nonetheless, we only allow vertices to change their states one at the time. Said setup enables modeling interacting particle systems on hypergraphs, multilayered networks, the evolution the graphs themselves or the co-evolution of the states of vertices and the underlying graph at the same time. (Although, the NIMFA is only shown to be accurate in the case when the evolution of the states of vertices does not affect the evolution of the edges of the graph.)

The outline of the article is as follows: In Section
2
we give a detailed discussion of the literature. In Section
3
we introduce the necessary notation and concepts to state the theorems precisely in Section
4
. In Section
5
we give a variety of examples of models for which the stated results are applicable. In Section
6
we introduce the graphical construction of the original interacting particle system and an auxiliary branching structure that used in the proofs. Lastly, the proofs are presented in Section
7
.

Related works

It is impossible to talk about interacting particle systems without addressing the works of Thomas G. Kurtz. His foundational works show that the Homogenous Mean Field Approximation (HMFA) arises as the limit object of the state densities of Markov processes on the complete graph with fluctuations of order
\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}
(where
N
stands for the number of particles)
[
20
,
21
]
.

Gast et al. proved many results related to the so-called refined mean field approximation which provides higher order approximations for occupation probabilities
[
12
]
. Their result for NIMFA in
[
1
]
on dense weighted (hyper)graphs show an error bound of order
\frac{1}{N}
which we generalize to the sparse case. Their method is based on power series expansion and it does not seem to generalize to the sparse case.

In the works of Shidhar and Kar
[
30
]
and in our previous work
[
16
]
Markov processes on weighted networks were studied. In particular in the case of unweighted graphs we obtained an error bound of order
\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}
for the accuracy of NIMFA, where
d
stands for the average degree. In the current paper we present an upper bound of order
\frac{1}{d}
and show that (under some conditions on the norm of the matrix of rates) this bound is tight.

There is an intense research about the connection of Markov processes and graph limits both for jump processes
[
10
,
17
]
and diffusion processes
[
22
,
19
]
.

We refer to the work of Garbe et al.
[
11
]
on Markov processes
of
graphs which we discuss in detail in Section
5.2
.

We prove our results using the notion of graphical construction of the interacting particle system in terms of a family of Poisson point processes called instructions. In particular, we make use of the so-called backward construction by recursively tracing the instructions that could potentially affect the
the state of a vertex at time
t
backwards in time. We refer the reader to the introduction of
Section
6
for brief survey of the literature of the backward construction.

3.1
Transition rates

Most of the notations in this section are inspired by
[
1
]
.

The set of vertices is denoted by
V
. We denote the cardinality of
V
by
|V|=N
.
V
can usually be identified with
[N]=\{1,\dots,N\}
. At each time
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
each vertex is in a state from the set
\mathcal{S}
of possible states. We assume that
\mathcal{S}
is finite.

\xi_{i,s}(t)
denotes the indicator of the event that vertex
i\in V
is in state
s\in\mathcal{S}
at time
t
. For the corresponding vector we use the convention
\xi_{i}(t):=\left(\xi_{i,s}(t)\right)_{s\in\mathcal{S}}.

We always assume that the initial conditions
\left(\xi_{i}(0)\right)_{i\in V}
are independent, but not necessarily identically distributed.

Vertices can change their states via self-, pair- or higher order interactions. An order
m
interaction involves
m+1
vertices:
\underline{j}=(j_{1},\dots,j_{m})\in V^{m}
and
i\in V
. If the vertices
\underline{j}
are in state
\underline{\tilde{s}}=(\tilde{s}_{1},\dots,\tilde{s}_{m})\in\mathcal{S}^{m}
and
i
is in state
s^{\prime}
then
\underline{j}
changes the state of
i
to
s
at rate

r^{(m)}_{\underline{j}i;(\underline{\tilde{s}},s^{\prime})\to(\underline{\tilde{s}},s)}.

For example, the rate of an order
0
(self) interaction has the form
r_{i,s^{\prime}\to s}^{(0)}
and it corresponds to the rate at which vertex
i
changes its state from
s^{\prime}
to
s
by itself. Similarly, the rate of an order
1
(pair) interaction is of form
r^{(1)}_{ji;(\tilde{s},s^{\prime})\to(\tilde{s},s)}
and it corresponds to the rate at which vertex
j
in state
\tilde{s}
changes the state of vertex
i
from
s^{\prime}
to
s
.

The upper index
(m)
is omitted when the order is clear from the context.

Let us stress that this setup only allows transitions where exactly one vertex changes its state at a time.

Without loss of generality we can assume that
r^{(m)}_{\underline{j}i;(\underline{\tilde{s}},s^{\prime})\to(\underline{\tilde{s}},s)}=0
whenever two or more indices from the list
j_{1},\dots,j_{m},i
have the same value, as these are either contradictory descriptions (like
j_{1}=j_{2}
while
\tilde{s}_{1}\neq\tilde{s}_{2}
) or they can be described via a lower order interaction.

Furthermore, for any permutation
\sigma
of
m
elements we may assume

\displaystyle r^{(m)}_{\sigma(\underline{j})i;\left(\sigma(\tilde{\underline{s}}),s^{\prime}\right)\to\left(\sigma(\tilde{\underline{s}}),s^{\prime}\right)}=r^{(m)}_{\underline{j}i;(\underline{\tilde{s}},s^{\prime})\to(\underline{\tilde{s}},s)}

(1)

as they describe the same event.

We assume

0\leq m\leq M

for some fixed
M\in\mathbb{N}
which can be interpreted as the maximal order of interactions.

We make use of the convention

\displaystyle r_{\underline{j}i;(\underline{\tilde{s}},s)\to(\underline{\tilde{s}},s)}^{(m)}:=

\displaystyle-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}\\
s^{\prime}\neq s\end{subarray}}r_{\underline{j}i;(\underline{\tilde{s}},s)\to(\underline{\tilde{s}},s^{\prime})}^{(m)}

(2)

representing the transition rates of vertex
i\in V
leaving state
s\in\mathcal{S}
with a minus sign.
Additionally, we denote by

\displaystyle\bar{r}_{\underline{j}i}^{(m)}:=\sum_{\underline{\tilde{s}}\in\mathcal{S}^{m}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}s,s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}\\
s^{\prime}\neq s\end{subarray}}r_{\underline{j}i;(\underline{\tilde{s}},s)\to(\underline{\tilde{s}},s^{\prime})}^{(m)}

(3)

the total rate at which the
m-
tuple of vertices
\underline{j}=(j_{1},\dots,j_{m})
can potentially influence vertex
i
via an order
m
interaction, including “phantom” interactions that do not actualize.
The total incoming rate of vertex
i
is denoted by

\displaystyle\delta_{i}^{(m)}:=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m!}\sum_{\underline{j}\in V^{m}}\bar{r}_{\underline{j}i}^{(m)}.

(4)

where the factor
m!
takes into account the over counting of the permutations of the vertices
j_{1},\dots,j_{m}
. Due to property (
1
) in these type of sums one can replace
\frac{1}{m!}\sum_{\underline{j}\in V^{m}}
with
\sum_{j_{1}<\dots<j_{m}}
.

The concentration and error bounds that we will prove, in general, do not depend on self-interactions as they do not create correlations between vertices, thus,
\delta_{i}^{(m)}
only interests us for
1\leq m\leq M
(excluding
m=0
), thus we define the maximal incoming rate as follows:

\displaystyle\delta_{max}:=\max_{1\leq m\leq M}\max_{i\in V}\delta_{i}^{(m)}.

(5)

Let
i\neq j\in V
. The
influence
of vertex
j
on vertex
i
, denoted by
\tilde{r}_{ji}
, is defined as the total rate of interactions in which
j
could participate in that could potentially have an effect on the state of vertex
i
. With formulas:

\displaystyle\tilde{r}_{ji}:=\sum_{m=1}^{M}\frac{1}{m!}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\underline{k}\in V^{m}\\
j\in\underline{k}\end{subarray}}\bar{r}^{(m)}_{\underline{k}i},

(6)

where
j\in\underline{k}
means that there is an index
1\leq l\leq m
such that
j=k_{l}.

Note that if
M=1
then
\tilde{r}_{ji}=\bar{r}_{ji}
.

The maximal influence is denoted by

\displaystyle\tilde{r}_{max}:=\max_{i,j\in V}\tilde{r}_{ij}.

(7)

We pay special attention to the case when we only have pair interactions (
M=1
).
In this case the total rates form a matrix

R=\left(\bar{r}_{ji}\right)_{j,i\in V}.

(8)

It follows from our assumptions that the diagonal elements of
R
are zeros:
\bar{r}_{ii}=0
for all
i\in V
.
We will call the rates
symmetric
if the corresponding matrix is symmetric, i.e., if
R^{\intercal}=R
.

\|R\|_{2}
refers to the induced
L^{2}
operator norm of
R
.

3.2
Weighted and unweighted hypergraphs

An important special case of the general setting described in Section
3.1
is when we have dynamics on a hypergraph with weights

(w_{\underline{j}i}^{(m)})_{\begin{subarray}{c}\underline{j}\in V^{m}\\
i\in V\\
m\in\{1,\dots,M\}\end{subarray}}.

The setup of Section
3.1
also includes other possible models such as dynamics on multilayered networks or edge dynamics, cf. Section
5
.

In the special case of weighted hypergraphs the transition rates can be factorized into

\displaystyle r_{\underline{j}i;(\tilde{\underline{s}},s^{\prime})\to(\underline{\tilde{s}},s)}^{(m)}=w_{\underline{j}i}^{(m)}q_{(\tilde{\underline{s}},s^{\prime})\to(\underline{\tilde{s}},s)}^{(m)},

(9)

separating the effect of the connectivity between vertices
j_{1},\dots,j_{m},i
and the effect of their state on the transition rates.
We have

\displaystyle\bar{r}_{\underline{j}i}^{(m)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:r_bar}}}{{=}}w_{\underline{j}i}^{(m)}\underbrace{\sum_{\underline{\tilde{s}}\in\mathcal{S}^{m}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}s,s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}\\
s^{\prime}\neq s\end{subarray}}q_{(\tilde{\underline{s}},s^{\prime})\to(\underline{\tilde{s}},s)}^{(m)}}_{=:\bar{q}^{(m)}}

(10)

making
\bar{r}_{\underline{j}i}^{(m)}
proportional to
w_{\underline{j}i}^{(m)}
for fixed
m
.

For unweighted hypergraphs, let
a_{\underline{j}i}^{(m)}
denote the indicator of
j_{1},\dots,j_{m},i
forming a hyperedge. The
m
-in-degree of vertex
i\in V
and the average
m
-in-degree are defined as

d^{(m)}_{i}:=\frac{1}{m!}\sum_{\underline{j}\in V^{m}}a_{\underline{j}i}^{(m)},\qquad\bar{d}^{(m)}:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in V}d^{(m)}_{i}.

Without further normalization imposed, the total potential incoming weights through
m
-interactions would be proportional to the in-degrees
d^{(m)}_{i}
. Thus, when we have a graph sequence with diverging average degree then the dynamics would eventually become too fast. Therefore, we will apply the normalization

\displaystyle w^{(m)}_{\underline{j}i}=\frac{1}{\bar{d}^{(m)}}a^{(m)}_{\underline{j}i}.

(11)

For instance in the
M=1
case (i.e., if we only have self-interactions and pairwise interactions) this leads to

R\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{def_R_matrix}}}{{=}}\frac{\bar{q}}{\bar{d}}A

(12)

where
A
is the adjacency matrix of the graph and we use the shorthand
\bar{d}=\bar{d}^{(1)}
.

Furthermore,

\displaystyle\delta_{i}^{(m)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:delta^m}}}{{=}}\frac{\bar{q}^{(m)}}{\bar{d}^{(m)}}d_{i}^{(m)}.

Assuming
\delta_{max}\leq K
(cf. (
5
)) in the setting of weighted hypergraphs is the same as requiring that

\displaystyle\forall i\in V,1\leq m\leq M\ d^{(m)}_{i}\leq\frac{K}{\bar{q}^{(m)}}\bar{d}^{(m)},

(13)

or in other words, requiring that the hypergraph is upper regular.

Note that for a (possibly oriented) unweighted graph (
M=1
) the maximal influence (cf. (
7
)) satisfies

\tilde{r}_{max}=\frac{1}{\bar{d}}.

3.3
The N-intertwined Mean Field Approximation

The models described in Section
3.1
are Markov chains with state space
\mathcal{S}^{V}.
One could theoretically calculate the occupation probabilities via Kolmogorov’s forward equation. However, that would require the numerical integration of a system of
\mathcal{S}^{N}
ODEs, an infeasible task even for moderate sized graphs.

To mitigate the problem, we follow an individual-based approach where we aim to approximate the marginal probabilities

\displaystyle y_{i,s}(t):=\mathbb{P}\left(\xi_{i,s}(t)=1\right)=\mathbb{E}\left(\xi_{i,s}(t)\right)

(14)

instead of the joint probabilities
\mathbb{P}\left(\xi_{i_{1},s_{1}}(t)=1,\dots,\xi_{i_{N},s_{N}}(t)=1\right)

as it only requires tracking
\left|\mathcal{S}\right|\cdot N
variables, a drastic reduction of complexity. In plain words,
y_{i,s}(t)
is the probability that vertex
i
is in state
s
at time
t
.

In order to write down the dynamics for
y_{i,s}(t)
, one should apply the infinitesimal generator
\mathcal{A}.

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}\xi_{i,s}(t)=

\displaystyle\lim_{\Delta t\to 0_{+}}\frac{1}{\Delta t}\mathbb{E}\left(\left.\xi_{i,s}(t+\Delta t)-\xi_{i,s}(t)\right|\mathcal{F}_{t}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{m=0}^{M}\frac{1}{m!}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\underline{j}\in V^{m}\\
\underline{\tilde{s}}\in\mathcal{S}^{m}\\
s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}\end{subarray}}r^{(m)}_{\underline{j}i;(\underline{\tilde{s}},s^{\prime})\to(\underline{\tilde{s}},s)}\xi_{i,s^{\prime}}(t)\prod_{l=1}^{m}\xi_{j_{l},\tilde{s}_{l}}(t)

where
\mathcal{F}_{t}
is the filtration of the process. Note that the negative terms corresponding to vertex
i
leaving state
s
are included via the convention (
2
).

After taking the expectation of both sides, we get

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}y_{i,s}(t)=\sum_{m=0}^{M}\frac{1}{m!}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\underline{j}\in V^{m}\\
\underline{\tilde{s}}\in\mathcal{S}^{m}\\
s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}\end{subarray}}r^{(m)}_{\underline{j}i;(\underline{\tilde{s}},s^{\prime})\to(\underline{\tilde{s}},s)}\mathbb{E}\left(\xi_{i,s^{\prime}}(t)\prod_{l=1}^{m}\xi_{j_{l},\tilde{s}_{l}}(t)\right).

(15)

The problem with (
15
) is that it does not form a closed system of ODEs, it is not enough for determining the values of
y_{i,s}(t)
. One way to achieve closure of (
15
) is to assume that
\xi_{i,s^{\prime}}(t),\xi_{j_{1},\tilde{s}_{1}},\dots,\xi_{j_{m},\tilde{s}_{m}}
are approximately independent from each other:

\mathbb{E}\left(\xi_{i,s^{\prime}}(t)\prod_{l=1}^{m}\xi_{j_{l},\tilde{s}_{l}}(t)\right)\approx\mathbb{E}\left(\xi_{i,s^{\prime}}(t)\right)\prod_{l=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}\left(\xi_{j_{l},\tilde{s}_{l}}(t)\right)=y_{i,s^{\prime}}(t)\prod_{l=1}^{m}y_{j_{l},\tilde{s}_{l}}(t).

(16)

Inserting this approximate identity into (
15
)
gives the approximate, but closed ODE system called quenched mean field approximation or N-Intertwined Mean Field Approximation (NIMFA)
[
26
]
:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}z_{i,s}(t)=

\displaystyle\sum_{m=0}^{M}\frac{1}{m!}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\underline{j}\in V^{m}\\
\underline{\tilde{s}}\in\mathcal{S}^{m}\\
s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}\end{subarray}}r^{(m)}_{\underline{j}i;(\underline{\tilde{s}},s^{\prime})\to(\underline{\tilde{s}},s)}z_{i,s^{\prime}}(t)\prod_{l=1}^{m}z_{j_{l},\tilde{s}_{l}}(t)

(17)

\displaystyle z_{i,s}(0)=

\displaystyle y_{i,s}(0).

The heuristic suggests that if the states of the vertices are approximately independent then we must have
y_{i,s}(t)\approx z_{i,s}(t)
.

Since the right hand side of (
17
) is locally Lipschitz, it exhibits a unique maximal solution. Note that for each
i\in V

y_{i}(t):=\left(y_{i,s}(t)\right)_{s\in\mathcal{S}}
is a probability vector. This property is inherited by
z_{i}(t):=\left(z_{i,s}(t)\right)_{s\in\mathcal{S}}
for all
t\geq 0
when the initial conditions are
z_{i}(0)=y_{i}(0).
This is due to the fact that (
17
) also exhibits a stochastic representation. In Section
6
we construct an an auxiliary branching structure which produces an
\mathcal{S}
-valued random variable
\widetilde{\sigma}_{i}(t)
such that
z_{i,s}(t)=\mathbb{P}(\widetilde{\sigma}_{i}(t)=s)
. This also means that,
z_{i,s}(t)
cannot blow up for
t\geq 0
, making the solutions well-defined on
\mathbb{R}^{+}.

There are several advantages of (
17
).

Firstly, it is only composed of
|\mathcal{S}|N
ODEs out of which lne only has to treat
(|\mathcal{S}|-1)N
of them due to the conservation of probabilities giving
\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}z_{i,s}(t)=1.
In contrast, the exact Kolmogorov forward equations corresponding to our Markov process form a system of
|S|^{N}-1
ordinary differential equations which are numerically infeasible even for moderate sized
N
.

Secondly, for concrete models (see (
19
) for the SIS process) (
17
) might have a relatively simple form enabling even closed form analytical results (such as the critical value of the infection parameter
[
26
]
).

Lastly, one can use (
17
) as a stepping stone for deriving a limit object which usually takes the form of an integro-differential equation, cf. (
20
) for the SIS process.

3.4
The simplicial SIS process

The most simple example of a complex contagion model is the simplicial SIS model
[
7
]
. Vertices can be in two states: susceptible (
S
) and infected (
I
), making the state space
\mathcal{S}=\{S,I\}
. For
1\leq m\leq M
if vertices
\underline{j}=(j_{1},\dots,j_{m})\in V^{m}
are infected and
i\in V
is susceptible, then
i
becomes infected at rate

r^{(m)}_{\underline{j}i;((I,\dots,I),S)\to((I,\dots,I),I)}=\bar{r}_{\underline{j}i}^{(m)}.

Recovery (i.e., curing) happens via the following self-interaction: an infected vertex
i\in V
recovers at rate
r_{i,I\to S}=\bar{r}_{i}.

When it comes to (
17
), it is enough to consider the variables
z_{i,I}(t),i\in V
since we have
z_{i,S}(t)=1-z_{i,I}(t)
, resulting in

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}z_{i,I}(t)=-\bar{r}_{i}z_{i,I}(t)+(1-z_{i,I}(t))\sum_{m=1}^{M}\frac{1}{m!}\sum_{\underline{j}\in V^{m}}\bar{r}_{\underline{j}i}^{(m)}\prod_{l=1}^{m}z_{j_{l},I}(t).

(18)

In the
M=1
case we get back the classical version of the SIS model
[
26
]
on the weighted graph
R
with recovery rates
(\bar{r}_{i})_{i\in V}
. Here, (
18
) reduces to

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}z_{i,I}(t)=-\bar{r}_{i}z_{i,I}(t)+(1-z_{i,I}(t))\sum_{j\in V}\bar{r}_{ji}z_{j,I}(t).

(19)

When the recovery rates
\bar{r}_{i}
are zero, we will refer to the model as the simplicial SI process.

Remark 3.1

(Integro-differential equation limit of SIS process as
N\to\infty
)
.

When there are some functions
\gamma,W
such that
\bar{r}_{i}\approx\gamma\left(\frac{i}{N}\right)
and
\bar{r}_{ji}\approx\frac{1}{N}W\left(\frac{j}{N},\frac{i}{N}\right)
, then it is possible to approximate
z_{i}(t)
as
z_{i}(t)\approx u\left(t,\frac{i}{N}\right)
where
u
satisfies

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u(t,x)=-\gamma(x)u(t,x)+(1-u(t,x))\int_{0}^{1}W(y,x)u(t,y)\mathrm{d}y.

(20)

For the precise statement and conditions, see
[
19
]
.

4
Results

In Section
4.1
we state our results about the concentration
of empirical averages of form
\frac{1}{\left|\mathcal{M}\right|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{M}}\xi_{i,s}(t)
, where
\mathcal{M}\subseteq V
.
In Section
4.2
we give upper and lower bounds on the individual error

\left|y_{i,s}(t)-z_{i,s}(t)\right|
in terms of
\tilde{r}_{max}
(cf. (
7
)).
In Section
4.3
we give upper and lower bounds on the average error
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in V}\left|y_{i,s}(t)-z_{i,s}(t)\right|
in the
M=1
case in terms of
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i,j\in V}\bar{r}_{ij}^{2}
.

Remark 4.1

(Average degree assumptions)
.

Note that in the case of unweighted graphs (cf. (
12
)) we will need the average degree
\bar{d}
to be big for the error of the approximation (
16
) (and therefore the error of the NIMFA approximation results of Sections

4.2
and
4.3
) to be small, but the concentration results of Section
4.1
do not require
\bar{d}
to be big.
More specifically, the average error of the NIMFA approximation does not vanish as
N\to\infty
if the underlying graph is
d
-regular with fixed
d
(cf. Theorem
4.25
), but the concentration bound (
24
) is essentially optimal in this case, cf. Remark
4.5
.

4.1
Concentration results

Here we present our results concerning the concentration of macroscopic or mesoscopic averages around their expectation. We show that under mild conditions, the statistics of large subpopulations (say, a city or an age group) will have small fluctuations around a deterministic value.

Some of our results are formulated in terms of a random set
\mathcal{H}_{i}(t)
corresponding to those vertices which could potentially influence the state of vertex
i
at time
t
. We call
\mathcal{H}_{i}(t)
the
information set
of vertex
i
at time
t
. In our proofs the set

\mathcal{H}_{i}(t)
naturally appears in the graphical construction of the interacting particle systems that we study (cf. Section
6
), but in order
to state our theorems, we merely need to define the evolution of the set
\mathcal{H}_{i}(t)
:

Definition 4.2

(Information set)
.

Let
\mathcal{H}_{i}(t)
be a time-dependent random subset of
V
with initial condition
\mathcal{H}_{i}(0)=\{i\}
which evolves according to the following Markovian dynamics:
If the set is in state
\mathcal{H}_{i}(t)=H
then for every
k\in H
, every
m\in\{1,\dots,M\}
and every
\underline{j}=(j_{1},\dots,j_{m})\in V^{m}
that satisfies
j_{1}<j_{2}<\dots<j_{m}
it jumps to state
H\cup\{j_{1},\dots,j_{m}\}
at rate
\bar{r}_{\underline{j}k}^{(m)}
(cf. (
3
)).

Observe that information sets increase, i.e., if
t^{\prime}\leq t
then
\mathcal{H}_{i}(t^{\prime})\subseteq\mathcal{H}_{i}(t)
.

Our first lemma quantifies the error of the approximation (
16
) using information sets. Loosely speaking, if the information sets don’t overlap then independence is guaranteed.

Lemma 4.3

(Propagation of chaos)
.

Let
i_{1},\dots,i_{m}\in V
be distinct vertices and
s_{1},\dots,s_{m}\in S
arbitrary. Then we have

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}\left(\prod_{l=1}^{m}\xi_{i_{l},s_{l}}(t)\right)-\prod_{l=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}\left(\xi_{i_{l},s_{l}}(t)\right)\right|\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j\in\{i_{1},\dots,i_{m}\}\\
i\neq j\end{subarray}}\{\mathcal{H}_{i}(t)\cap\mathcal{H}_{j}(t)\neq\emptyset\}\right)

(21)

We will prove Lemma
4.3
in Section
7.1
.

We are ready to state the first main result of Section
4.1
.

Theorem 4.4

(Concentration bounds)
.

Let
\emptyset\neq\mathcal{M}\subseteq V
be a set of vertices. Let us denote the fraction of vertices from
\mathcal{M}
being in state
s
at time
t
by

\bar{\xi}^{\mathcal{M}}_{s}(t):=\frac{1}{\left|\mathcal{M}\right|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{M}}\xi_{i,s}(t).

(22)

Then for any
s\in\mathcal{S}
we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}\left(\bar{\xi}^{\mathcal{M}}_{s}(t)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\left|\mathcal{M}\right|^{2}}\sum_{i,j\in\mathcal{M}}\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{H}_{i}(t)\cap\mathcal{H}_{j}(t)\neq\emptyset\right).

(23)

Recall (
8
). In the
M=1
case we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}\left(\bar{\xi}^{\mathcal{M}}_{s}(t)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\left|\mathcal{M}\right|}e^{2\|R\|_{2}t}.

(24)

We will prove Theorem
4.4
in Section
7.1
.

Remark 4.5

(Sharpness)
.

The bound (
23
) is tight in the following sense: since
\mathcal{H}_{i}(0)=\{i\}
, at
t=0
the r.h.s. of (
23
) is equal to
\frac{1}{\left|\mathcal{M}\right|}
.
Now if we consider the SI process with i.i.d. initial conditions
\mathbb{P}\left(\xi_{i,I}(0)=1\right)=\frac{1}{2}
then at
t=0
the l.h.s. of (
23
) is equal to

\frac{1}{4\left|\mathcal{M}\right|}
.

Corollary 4.6
.

It is natural to assume
\|R\|_{2}=O(1)
since this assumption holds e.g. in the case of upper regular graphs (cf. (
13
)) or
W
-random graphs with density
\gg\frac{\log N}{N}
, cf.
[
6
]
. In this case the r.h.s. of (
24
) simply reduces to
O\left(\frac{1}{\left|\mathcal{M}\right|}\right)
where the implied constant on
O(\cdot)
depends on
t
.

Corollary 4.7
.

Assume
\|R\|_{2}=o\left(\log\left|\mathcal{M}\right|\right)
and
|\mathcal{M}|=\Omega\left(N^{\alpha}\right)
for some
0<\alpha\leq 1
. Under this assumption, for any
0<\varepsilon<1
, the r.h.s. of (
24
) is

O\left(\left|\mathcal{M}\right|^{-(1-\varepsilon)}\right)
, where the implied constant
in
O(\cdot)
depends on
t
and
\varepsilon
.

The assumption
\|R\|_{2}=o\left(\log(N)\right)
holds with high probability for the Erdős-Rényi graph
\mathcal{G}\left(N,\frac{\lambda}{N}\right)
, cf.
[
4
]
, thus Corollary
4.7
can be applied.
However, as we will see in Proposition
4.8
, the tight
O\left(\frac{1}{\left|\mathcal{M}\right|}\right)
bound can also be achieved, despite
\|R\|_{2}\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
.

The scaling that we employ in
(
25
) below is formally different from our usual convention (
11
), but the two normalizations have the same order of magnitude since in
\mathcal{G}\left(N,\frac{\lambda}{N}\right)
the average degree satisfies
\bar{d}\approx\lambda
and in Proposition
4.8
we assume that
\lambda
is a fixed positive constant that does not depend on
N
.

Proposition 4.8

(Erdős-Rényi concentration bound)
.

Take a process with self interactions and pair interactions with rates

r_{ji;(\tilde{s},s^{\prime})\to(\tilde{s},s)}=a_{ji}q_{(\tilde{s},s^{\prime})\to(\tilde{s},s)},

(25)

similarly to the convention in (
9
), where
\left(a_{ji}\right)_{j,i\in V}
is the adjacency matrix of an Erdős-Rényi graph
\mathcal{G}\left(N,\frac{\lambda}{N}\right)
. Take any (deterministic) subset
\emptyset\neq\mathcal{M}\subseteq V
. Then using the notation
\bar{q}:=\bar{q}^{(1)}
from (
10
) we have

\mathbb{P}\left[\mathrm{Var}\left(\bar{\xi}^{\mathcal{M}}_{s}(t)\,\left|\,\mathcal{G}\left(N,\frac{\lambda}{N}\right)\right.\right)\leq\frac{K}{|\mathcal{M}|}e^{2\lambda\bar{q}t}\right]\geq 1-\frac{1}{K}.

(26)

We will prove Proposition
4.8
in Section
7.1
.

Recall the notion of the maximal incoming rate
\delta_{max}
from (
5
).

Remark 4.9

(Symmetry matters)
.

Note that by (
24
) it is enough to assume

\delta_{max}=O(1)
for the variance to be
O\left(\frac{1}{\left|\mathcal{M}\right|}\right)
if
R
is symmetric (since
\|R\|_{2}\leq\|R\|_{\infty}=\delta_{max}
holds if
R^{\intercal}=R
), but
\delta_{max}=O(1)
does not imply the
O\left(\frac{1}{\left|\mathcal{M}\right|}\right)
variance bound in the non-symmetric case, as we now explain.
Take an SI process on the directed star graph where the edges are pointing outwards the center. That is,
r_{1j;(I,S)\to(I,I)}=1
for
j>1
and
\bar{r}_{ij}=0
otherwise (i.e., vertex
1
can infect others but not the other way around). If initially vertex
1
is infected with probability
\frac{1}{2}
while the others are initially susceptible then the ratio of infected vertices at
t=1
fluctuates a lot based on whether the center is infected or not, despite the fact that the maximal in-degree
\delta_{max}
is equal to
1
.

To take care of the problem mentioned in Remark
4.9
, we provide an upper bound that works for directed hypergraphs as well. Recall the notion of the maximal influence
\tilde{r}_{max}
from (
7
).

Theorem 4.10

(Concentration bounds using
\delta_{max}
and
\tilde{r}_{max}
)
.

For any
i\neq j\in V
we have

\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{H}_{i}(t)\cap\mathcal{H}_{j}(t)\neq\emptyset\right)\leq t\cdot e^{\delta_{max}Mt}(1+e^{\delta_{max}Mt})\cdot\tilde{r}_{max}.

(27)

We prove Theorem
4.10
in Section
7.1
.
To put it more simply, the r.h.s. of (
27
) is
O(\tilde{r}_{max})
,
where the implied constant in
O(\cdot)
depends on
t
,
\delta_{max}
and
M
.

So far we only provided an upper bound for the variance of
\bar{\xi}^{\mathcal{M}}_{s}(t)
. In the case of
M=1
and with symmetric rates, however, more can be said. Namely, it is possible to characterize a sequence of rate matrices
R
and subsets of vertices
\mathcal{M}\subseteq V
for which the variance of empirical averages goes to zero, regardless the choice of (independent) initial conditions or the underlying process.

Theorem 4.11

(Characterization of concentration using information sets)
.

Let
M=1
. Given a sequence of symmetric rate matrices
R(n),n\in\mathbb{N}
and sample sets
\emptyset\neq\mathcal{M}(n)\subseteq V(n)
,
the following two statements are equivalent:

•

For all Markov processes satisfying (
3
) and for all sequences of independent initial conditions
\left(\xi_{i}(0)\right)_{i\in V(n)}
we have

\forall\,t\geq 0,\ s\in\mathcal{S}\ \lim_{n\to\infty}\mathrm{Var}\left(\bar{\xi}^{\mathcal{M}(n)}_{s}(t)\right)=0.

•

\forall\,t\geq 0\ \lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{\left|\mathcal{M}(n)\right|^{2}}\sum_{i,j\in\mathcal{M}(n)}\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{H}_{i}(t)\cap\mathcal{H}_{i}(t)\neq\emptyset\right)=0.

(28)

We will prove Theorem
4.11
in Section
7.1
.

Conjecture 4.12
.

We believe that Theorem
4.11
is true even without assuming symmetry or
M=1
.

Heuristically, if
\frac{1}{\left|\mathcal{M}(n)\right|^{2}}\sum_{i,j\in\mathcal{M}(n)}\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{H}_{i}(t)\cap\mathcal{H}_{i}(t)\neq\emptyset\right)\geq c>0
then
\mathcal{H}_{i}(t)
and
\mathcal{H}_{j}(t)
has a non-empty intersection for two independently and uniformly chosen
i,j\in\mathcal{M}(n)
with non-vanishing probability, making it possible for information to flow to both
i
and
j
from the same source, hence making them correlated (cf. Section
6
for the interpretation of information sets in terms of the graphical construction of the interacting particle system).

Remark 4.13
.

We will later see in Claim
6.1
that in the case of symmetric pair interactions the information set
\mathcal{H}_{i}(t)
evolves as the infected set of an SI process on the weighted graph
R
started from a single infected individual at vertex
i
.
Moreover, by (
72
), if
R(n)=R^{\intercal}(n)
then
the condition (
28
) is equivalent to

\forall\,t\geq 0\ \lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{\left|\mathcal{M}(n)\right|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{M}(n)}\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{\left|\mathcal{H}_{i}(2t)\cap\mathcal{M}(n)\right|}{\left|\mathcal{M}(n)\right|}\right)=0,

which simply means that the SI process on the weighted graph
R
started from a uniform vertex of
\mathcal{M}(n)
cannot blow up in finite time.

4.2
Mean field approximation -
\ell^{\infty}
error

In this chapter we discuss the maximal error between the occupation probabilities
y_{i,s}(t)
(cf. (
14
)) and their NIMFA approximation
z_{i,s}(t)
(cf. (
17
)).

The main heuristic is that if any vertex has a small influence on any other vertex
then the
states of vertices will be almost independent, making the error of NIMFA small.

Firstly, we show that if the total incoming interaction rates are uniformly bounded in the set of vertices - i.e., there are no fast changing nodes - then the error of NIMFA is
O(\tilde{r}_{max})
(where the maximal influence
\tilde{r}_{max}
is defined in (
6
))
uniformly
in
i\in V
. Therefore, it is possible to given an accurate approximation at the individual level.

Theorem 4.14

(NIMFA
\ell^{\infty}
upper bound in terms of
\delta_{max}
and
\tilde{r}_{max}
)
.

We have

\displaystyle\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in V\\
s\in\mathcal{S}\end{subarray}}\left|y_{i,s}(t)-z_{i,s}(t)\right|\leq 2Mt\cdot e^{2M\delta_{max}t}\cdot\tilde{r}_{max}

(29)

We prove Theorem
4.14
in Section
7.2
.
To put it more simply, the r.h.s. of (
29
) is

O\left(\tilde{r}_{max}\right)
,
where the implied constant in
O(\cdot)
depends on
\delta_{max}
,
M
and
t
.

The following theorem contains lower bounds that complement the upper bounds of Theorem
4.14
.

Theorem 4.15

(NIMFA
\ell^{\infty}
lower bound in terms of
\tilde{r}_{max}
)
.

(I)

For any
\left(\bar{r}_{\underline{j}i}^{(m)}\right)_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq m\leq M\\
j_{1},\dots,j_{m},i\in V\end{subarray}}
there is a process satisfying (
3
) and an appropriately chosen set of initial conditions such that

\displaystyle\exists\,i\in V,\ s\in\mathcal{S}\ \left|y_{i,s}(1)-z_{i,I}(1)\right|=\frac{1}{2}\left(1-e^{-\frac{1}{2}\tilde{r}_{max}}\right)^{2}.

(30)

(II)

Assume
\bar{r}_{ij}=\frac{a_{ij}}{\bar{d}}
for some unweighted graph with (symmetric) adjacency matrix
\left(a_{ij}\right)_{i,j\in V}
with average degree
\bar{d}\geq 1.
Pick any vertex
i
with degree
d_{i}\geq\bar{d}
. Take the SI process with initial condition
\mathbb{P}\left(\xi_{i,I}(0)=1\right)=\frac{1}{2}
and
\xi_{j,S}(0)=1
(
j\neq i
). Then

\displaystyle\left|y_{i,I}(1)-z_{i,I}(1)\right|\geq\frac{1}{32}\frac{1}{\bar{d}}=\frac{1}{32}\tilde{r}_{max}.

(31)

We prove Theorem
4.15
in Section
7.2
.

The identity (
30
) together with Theorem
4.14
implies that
if we consider a sequence of models indexed by
n
and
assume
\delta_{max}(n)=O(1)
then
\tilde{r}_{max}(n)\to 0
characterizes whether the
\ell^{\infty}
error vanishes or not as
n\to\infty
.

The lower bound (
31
) shows that the bound (
29
) is tight in the
M=1
unweighted (not necessarily undirected) graph case with average in-degree
\bar{d}\geq 1
.

4.3
Mean field approximation -
\ell^{1}
error

In Section
4.2
we focused on error bounds at the individual level, i.e.,
\ell^{\infty}
bounds. However, as we have already seen in (
30
), it is a necessary condition for
\tilde{r}_{max}
to vanish, otherwise the
\ell^{\infty}
bound becomes separated from
0
. For this reason we study the average error (also known as
\ell^{1}
error), which we can control better. However, we restrict our focus to symmetric pair interactions.

Theorem 4.16

(NIMFA
\ell^{1}
upper bounds using Frobenius norm of
R
)
.

Assume
M=1
and
R^{\intercal}=R
. Then the following bounds hold:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in V}\max_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\left|y_{i,s}(t)-z_{i,s}(t)\right|\leq

\displaystyle 4t^{2}e^{3\delta_{max}t}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{m\in V}\left(R^{2}\right)_{mm},

(32)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in V}\max_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\left|y_{i,s}(t)-z_{i,s}(t)\right|\leq

\displaystyle 4t^{2}e^{3\|R\|_{2}t}\sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{m\in V}\left(R^{2}\right)_{mm}^{2}}.

(33)

We will prove Theorem
4.16
in Section
7.3
.

Remark 4.17
.

Note that due to symmetry we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{m\in V}\left(R^{2}\right)_{mm}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{l,m\in V}\bar{r}_{ml}\bar{r}_{lm}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{m,l\in V}\bar{r}_{ml}^{2}=\frac{1}{N}\|R\|_{F}^{2},

(34)

where
\|\cdot\|_{F}
stands for the Frobenius norm.

In the previous works
[
30
,
16
]
only the weaker
\ell^{1}
upper bound
O\left(\sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\|R\|_{F}^{2}}\right)
was proved under the assumption
\delta_{max}=O(1)
, albeit without the need of the symmetry assumption.
Our bound (
32
) improves this to
O\left(\frac{1}{N}\|R\|_{F}^{2}\right)
where the implied constant in
O(\cdot)
depends on
\delta_{max}
and
t
.

The bound (
32
) is only useful if
\delta_{max}=O(1)
while the bound (
33
) is only useful if
\|R\|_{2}=O(1)
. If
R=R^{\intercal}
then we have

\|R\|_{2}\leq\|R\|_{\infty}=\delta_{max},

(35)

and in general
\|R\|_{2}=O(1)
is a much weaker assumption than
\delta_{max}=O(1)
as one can see from the example in Proposition
4.18
bellow.

Proposition 4.18

(Chung-Lu: an inhom. random graph with
\|R\|_{2}=O(1)
)
.

Take the rates
\bar{r}_{ji}=\frac{1}{N^{\alpha}}a_{ji}
where
\left(a_{ji}\right)_{j,i\in V}=A=A(N)
is the adjacency matrix of a Chung-Lu graph with connection probabilities

\mathbb{P}\left(a_{ij}=1\right)=N^{\alpha}\frac{\left(\frac{N}{i}\right)^{\gamma}\left(\frac{N}{j}\right)^{\gamma}}{\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left(\frac{N}{k}\right)^{\gamma}},\qquad 1\leq i<j\leq N,

(36)

where the exponents
\alpha
and
\gamma
satisfy
0<\gamma<\frac{1}{3},\ \gamma<\alpha<1-2\gamma
.

Then the sequence
R=R(N)
satisfies
\delta_{max}=\Theta\left(N^{\gamma}\right)
and
\|R\|_{2}=O(1)
with high probability.

We will prove Proposition
4.18
in Section
7.3
.

Since
\delta_{max}=\Theta\left(N^{\gamma}\right)
, the bound (
32
) is useless for a Markov process with rate matrix
R(N)
introduced in Proposition
4.18
if
N
is large. On the other hand, (
33
) is very much useful: in fact it gives an error bound which has the optimal order of magnitude in this case, as explained in Remark
4.23
below.

Lemma 4.19

(Frobenius norm and average degree of simple graphs)
.

Assume
\bar{r}_{ij}=\frac{a_{ij}}{\bar{d}}
for a an unweigthed undirected graph. Then we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{m\in V}\left(R^{2}\right)_{mm}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\bar{d}},

(37)

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{m\in V}\left(R^{2}\right)_{mm}^{2}}\leq

\displaystyle\|R\|_{2}\frac{1}{\bar{d}}.

(38)

We will prove Lemma
4.19
in in Section
7.3
.

Lemma
4.19
and (
35
)
imply that (
32
) and (
33
) give error bounds of the same order of magnitude for unweighted undirected graphs satisfying
\delta_{max}=O(1).

Now we state our lower bounds on the
\ell^{1}
error of NIMFA that complement the upper bounds obtained in Theorem

4.16
.

Theorem 4.20

(NIMFA
\ell^{1}
lower bound using (almost) the Frobenius norm)
.

Let us define an SI process on the (not necessarily symmetric) weighted graph
R
where initially every vertex is infected with probability
\frac{1}{2}
. Then

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in V}\left|y_{i,I}(1)-z_{i,I}(1)\right|\geq\frac{1}{16}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i,j\in V}e^{-(\delta_{i}+\delta_{j})}\bar{r}_{ji}^{2}.

(39)

We will prove Theorem
4.20
in Section
7.3
.

Corollary 4.21
.

If
\delta_{max}=O(1)
and
R^{\intercal}=R
then

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i,j\in V}e^{-(\delta_{i}+\delta_{j})}\bar{r}_{ij}^{2}=\Omega\left(\frac{1}{N}\|R\|_{F}^{2}\right)
,
which (together with (
32
)) gives that the tight bound for the average error is
\Theta\left(\frac{1}{N}\|R\|_{F}^{2}\right).

A consequence of (
37
) and Corollary
4.21
is that for undirected, unweighted graphs the
\ell^{1}
error is
\Theta\left(\frac{1}{\bar{d}}\right)
once we assume
\delta_{max}=O(1)
. However, under said assumption even the
\ell^{\infty}
error is
\Theta\left(\frac{1}{\bar{d}}\right)
according to Theorems
4.14
and
4.15
, making this statement trivial. As we will see in the next theorem, the condition
\delta_{max}=O(1)
can be relaxed to
\|R\|_{2}=O(1)
in the case of the
\ell^{1}
error.

Theorem 4.22

(NIMFA
\ell^{1}
lower bound on simple graphs)
.

For an unweighted undirected graph with
\bar{r}_{ij}=\frac{a_{ij}}{\bar{d}}
, if we take an SI process with i.i.d.
\operatorname{Ber}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)
initial infections then the following lower bound holds:

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in V}\left|y_{i,I}(1)-z_{i,I}(1)\right|\geq\frac{1}{32}e^{-8\|R\|_{2}^{2}}\frac{1}{\bar{d}}.

(40)

We will prove Theorem
4.22
in Section
7.3
.

Putting together (
33
), (
38
) and (
40
), we obtain that for unweighted undirected graphs it is enough to assume
\|R\|_{2}=O(1)
to obtain that the average error of NIMFA is
\Theta\left(\frac{1}{\bar{d}}\right)
.

Remark 4.23

(
\ell^{1}
error bounds (
33
) are sharp for Chung-Lu example)
.

One can check that the average degree of the random graph with adjacency matrix
A(N)
from Proposition
4.18
is well concentrated around
\frac{N^{\alpha}}{1-\gamma}
, thus the normalization
R(N)=\frac{1}{N^{\alpha}}A(N)

used in Proposition
4.18
only differs from the normalization
R=\frac{1}{\bar{d}}A
used in Lemma
4.19
by a random, but well-concentrated multiplier. Thus
(
33
) and (
38
) together give an upper bound of order
O(\frac{1}{\bar{d}})
on the
\ell^{1}
error of NIMFA in the case of the Chung-Lu graphs of Proposition
4.18
. The order of magnitude of this bound is optimal
by Theorem
4.22
.

Lastly, we return to the case of general weighted rate matrices
R
(as defined in (
8
)) and
characterize when the average error vanishes using
\frac{1}{N}\|R\|_{F}^{2}
, assuming only
R^{\intercal}=R
and
\|R\|_{2}=O(1)
. The first step is to show that the key term on the r.h.s. of (
33
)
is small if and only if
\frac{1}{N}\|R\|_{F}^{2}
is small.

Claim 4.24

(Frobenius bounds for weighted graphs)
.

Let
e_{i}
denote the
i
th unit vector. Then for all
i
we have

\left(R^{2}\right)_{ii}=e_{i}^{\intercal}R^{2}e_{i}\leq\|R^{2}\|_{2}\|e_{i}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\|R\|_{2}^{2},

(41)

therefore,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{m\in V}\left(R^{2}\right)_{mm}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{CSB}}}{{\leq}}\sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{m\in V}\left(R^{2}\right)_{mm}^{2}}\leq\|R\|_{2}\sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{m\in V}\left(R^{2}\right)_{mm}},

hence,

5.1
Multilayered networks

In Section
3.2
we discussed how the transition rates factorizes to (
9
) when studying Markov processes on a single hypergraph. However, some models require more than one network to operate on, such as in the case of epidemic processes on multilayered graphs where infection spreads on physical contact networks while information diffuses on a virtual social network.

An example of this is the so called SAIS model
[
8
]
where, besides susceptible (S) and infected (I), vertices can be in the alert (A) state as well. There are two (potentially directed) weighted networks
\left(w_{1;ij}\right)_{i,j\in V},\left(w_{2;ij}\right)_{i,j\in V}.

An infected vertex
i
recovers at rate
r_{i;I\to S}=\gamma_{i}
. An infected vertex can infect another susceptible
vertex
j
at rate
r_{ij;(I,S)\to(I,I)}=\beta_{S}w_{1;ij}
. An infected vertex can infect an
alert vertex
j
at rate
r_{ij;(I,A)\to(I,I)}=\beta_{A}w_{1;ij}
. We usually set
\beta_{A}<\beta_{S}
to ensure that alert vertices are less likely to get infected. Lastly, on the second network, an infected vertex
i
can make a susceptible vertex
j
alert at rate
r_{ij;(I,S)\to(I,A)}=\kappa w_{2;ij}
.

It is straightforward to generalize these sort of models to the case when we have a hypergraph with
L
layers. In this case the total potential interaction rate could be upper bounded by

\displaystyle\bar{r}_{\underline{j}i}^{(m)}\leq C\sum_{l=1}^{L}w_{l,\underline{j}i}^{(m)},

therefore all the results stated in Section
4
hold.

5.2
Dynamics of networks

So far we only discussed the dynamics of the states of vertices. Now we wish to extend the approach to include edge dynamics as well. This can be achieved by thinking of the edges of an evolving graph as vertices of a larger hypergraph. To avoid confusion, in this and the next section (Section
5.3
) we will call the elements of
V
as
agents
, where agents can be either vertices or edges of an evolving graph.

In this section we wish to describe a model of graph processes resembling the one in proposed in
[
11
]
. The discrete-time graph processes in
[
11
]
are described as follows: at each time step we sample
n
i.i.d. uniformly distributed vertices which induce a subgraph
H
of the current graph. This subgraph is then changed to
H^{\prime}
with probability
R_{H,H^{\prime}}
given by a stochastic matrix
R
(not related to the matrix
\left(\bar{r}_{ij}\right)_{i,j\in V}
in our notation). The authors of
[
11
]
show that if the sequence
G_{N}(0)
of initial graphs converges to a graphon as
N\to\infty
then for any

the graphs
G_{N}(\lfloor N^{2}t\rfloor)
also converge to a graphon and the dynamics on the space of graphons is described by a differential equation. Note that, by the definition of convergence of dense graph sequences, these results can be recast as laws of large numbers for hompomorphism densities.

The main differences between the setting of
[
11
]
and ours are the following:
(i) in our work, the graph evolves in continuous time (although, this difference does not matter when it comes to the law of large numbers); (ii) our main results are formulated in a way that we fix the number of vertices (instead of studying graph sequences with
N\to\infty
); (iii) we only allow
one
edge to change her state at a time, while in
[
11
]
more edges can change their states in one step; (iv)
the error bounds that we prove in Section
4.2
pertain to the NIMFA approximation of the probability that an individual agent (in this case, an edge) is in a certain state (in this case, present or absent), and the law of large numbers for the homomorphism densities can be derived from these results.

Informally, we look at random subgraphs with a fixed number of
n\geq 2
vertices and change one of the edges based on the state of the other edges. This means there are

edges that take part in an interaction.

The set of agents corresponds to the set of edges
V=\binom{[N]}{2}
. The state space is

\displaystyle r_{\underline{f}e;(\underline{\tilde{s}},s)\to(\underline{\tilde{s}},s^{\prime})}^{(M)}=\frac{1}{\binom{N-2}{n-2}}q_{(\underline{\tilde{s}},s)\to(\underline{\tilde{s}},s^{\prime})}^{(M)}{\mathds{1}}_{\left\{\textit{$f_{1},\dots,f_{M},e$ forms a $K_{n}$}\right\}}.

Recalling (

\displaystyle\bar{r}^{(M)}_{\underline{f}e}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\binom{N-2}{n-2}}\bar{q}^{(M)}{\mathds{1}}_{\left\{\textit{$f_{1},\dots,f_{M},e$ forms a $K_{n}$}\right\}}

\displaystyle\delta_{e}^{(M)}=

\displaystyle\bar{q}^{(M)}

where we used the fact that an agent is a member of exactly

hyper edges. When it comes to influences, there are two cases. When the edges

\displaystyle\tilde{r}_{fe}=

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\frac{\binom{N-3}{n-3}}{\binom{N-2}{n-2}}\bar{q}^{(M)}\text{ if $f,e$ share a common vertex}\\
\frac{\binom{N-4}{n-4}}{\binom{N-2}{n-2}}\bar{q}^{(M)}\text{ otherwise},\end{cases}

The main example in
[

is the triangle removal process where we independently and uniformly pick three vertices of the graph and delete their edges in case they form a triangle. Our setting does not allow this process as it requires changing the state of three edges at a time. Hence, we propose an modified model where we only remove one edge of the triangle (

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}z_{ij}(t)=-z_{ij}(t)\frac{1}{N-2}\sum_{k\in V\setminus\{i,j\}}z_{ik}(t)z_{kj}(t)

Let the evolving graph be denoted by

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(t(H,\mathcal{G}(t))\right)\overset{\textit{Lemma.\ \ref{t:chaos}, Thm.\ \ref{t:concentration_delta_max}}}{=}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N^{k}}\sum_{e_{1},\dots,e_{k}\in E(H)}\prod_{l=1}^{k}y_{e_{k},1}(t)+O\left(\frac{k^{2}}{N}\right)

What we would like to emphasise is that our setup is general enough to cover dynamics of graphs for which propagation of chaos and mean field approximation results holds. Given these results, the only thing left to study is the integro-differential equation that arises as the limit object of NIMFA.

5.3
Simultaneous vertex and edge dynamics

Previously we discussed how to treat vertex and edge dynamics separately. Here we show how can they be combined in certain cases in a way that fits our general setup. For further works on processes on dynamic graphs, see
[
27
,
14
,
28
]
.

The two main restrictions are:
(I) as before, we only allow one agent to change her state at a time;
(II) the edge dynamics can affect the state of the vertices but not the other way around.
The reason behind condition (II) will be explained later.

Instead of giving a general framework, we give an example as a proof of concept which is easy to generalize. More specifically, we study an SI process on a graph governed by the (modified) triangle removal process.

The set of agents are made of two disjoint sets
V=[N]\sqcup\binom{[N]}{2}
representing vertex and edge type agents. The state space is
\mathcal{S}=\{S,I,1,0\}
where vertex agents can only be at state
S
or
I
and edge agents can only be in state
0
or
1
.

The transition rates are given by:

\displaystyle\bar{r}_{j(ji)i}=

\displaystyle r_{j(ji)i;(I,1;S)\to(I,1,I)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N-1}\ \ (\textit{vertex $j$ infects vertex $i$ through edge $ji$}),

\displaystyle\bar{r}_{(ik)(jk)(ij)}=

\displaystyle r_{(ik)(jk)(ij);(1,1,1)\to(1,1,0)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N-2}(\textit{edge $ik$ and $jk$ deletes edge $ij$}).

Using the simplified notation

\displaystyle w_{ij}(t):=z_{ij,1}(t),\qquad u_{i}(t):=z_{i,I}(t),

NIMFA takes the form

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}u_{i}(t)=

\displaystyle(1-u_{i}(t))\frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{k\in V}w_{ik}(t)u_{k}(t),

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}w_{ij}(t)=

\displaystyle-w_{ij}(t)\frac{1}{N-2}\sum_{k\in V\setminus\{i,j\}}w_{ik}(t)w_{kj}(t).

Now let us check the conditions for concentration and mean field accuracy. One can check that

\displaystyle\delta_{i}^{(2)}=1\;\text{ and }\;\delta_{(ij)}^{(2)}=1,\;\text{ therefore }\;\delta_{max}=1,

and the non-zero terms of the influences are

\displaystyle\tilde{r}_{ji}=\tilde{r}_{(ji)i}=\frac{1}{N-1}\;\text{ and }\;\tilde{r}_{(ik)(ij)}=\frac{1}{N-2},\;\text{ therefore }\;\tilde{r}_{max}=\frac{1}{N-2}.

This means that the
\ell^{\infty}
error for NIMFA is
O\left(\frac{1}{N}\right)
and the law of large numbers results hold.

In general under assumption (II) vertex agents do not influence edge agents, hence,
\tilde{r}_{fe}
remains the same as in Section
5.2
. The influence on a vertex is virtually the same as in the classical case of vertex dynamics described in Section
3.2
with the only caveat that we have to “activate” any potential edge to calculate the influence. In general, a vertex
j
will have the same influence on vertex
i
as the edge
ji
.

However,
\bar{r}_{max}
may not vanish if we do not assume (II). E.g., with an SI dynamic on a graph where susceptible vertices can delete their edge to an infected neighbor at rate
\alpha
(to prevent infection). This, however would mean that the edge
(ij)
is strongly influenced by both vertex
i
and
j
as
\tilde{r}_{i(ij)}=\tilde{r}_{j(ij)}=\alpha,
hence the usual heuristic that the direct effect between two individual agents is low fails.

In Section

In Section

Now let us give a brief survey of the literature of the methods that we use in Section
6
.
The idea of looking at the graphical construction backward in time has been widely used in various concrete cases. In general, two processes
are called pathwise dual if they can be coupled using the same source of randomness in a way that one process is running forward, the other is running backward in time, see
[
15
]
for a survey on the notion of duality.
In particular, methods similar to ours using the backward graphical construction and the notion of so-called ghost vertices (cf. e.g. (

[

18

The idea of the stochastic representation of the solution of mean field differential equations like NIMFA in terms of a recursive tree structure already appeared in
[
25
, Section 3.3]
, moreover the idea of using a coupling of the interacting particle system with an auxiliary recursive tree structure to control the error of mean field approximations already appeared in

[

Proof of Theorem

,
thus
p_{ij}<1
holds if
N
is large enough.

The elements of the adjacency matrix
A
satisfy
a_{ij}=a_{ji}
for all
i,j\in[N]
and
\mathbb{P}\left(a_{ij}=1\right)=p_{ij}
if
i\neq j
, while
a_{ii}=0
.

We first prove that
\delta_{max}=\Theta\left(N^{\gamma}\right)
holds with high probability.

Let us define
X_{ij}:=a_{ij}-\mathbb{P}\left(a_{ij}=1\right)
for all
i,j\in V
(noting that
X_{ii}=0
). The random variables
X_{i,j},1\leq i<j\leq N
are independent with zero mean that satisfy
|X_{ij}|\leq 1
hence, Bernstein’s inequality yields

\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\sum_{j=1}^{N}X_{ij}\right|\geq t\right)\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{\frac{1}{2}t^{2}}{\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\left(X_{ij}^{2}\right)+\frac{1}{3}t}\right).

(100)

Set
t_{i}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{N}p_{ij}=\frac{1}{2}N^{\alpha}\left(\frac{N}{i}\right)^{\gamma}
. We have

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\left(X_{ij}^{2}\right)=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\operatorname{Var}\left(a_{ij}\right)\leq\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathbb{P}\left(a_{ij}=1\right)\leq 2t_{i},

(101)

\displaystyle\frac{\frac{1}{2}t_{i}^{2}}{\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\left(X_{ij}^{2}\right)+\frac{1}{3}t_{i}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{matra1}}}{{\geq}}\frac{\frac{1}{2}t_{i}^{2}}{2t_{i}+\frac{1}{3}t_{i}}=\frac{3}{14}t_{i}=\frac{3}{28}N^{\alpha}\left(\frac{N}{i}\right)^{\gamma}\geq\frac{3}{28}N^{\alpha}.

(102)

Noting that the degrees of our graph satisfy
\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}(d_{i})=t_{i}-\frac{1}{2}p_{ii}\leq t_{i}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{N}\left\{\left|d_{i}-\mathbb{E}\left(d_{i}\right)\right|\geq\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\left(d_{i}\right)\right\}\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{bernstejn},\eqref{matra2}}}{{\leq}}2Ne^{-\frac{3}{28}N^{\alpha}}.

(103)

We have
R=\frac{1}{N^{\alpha}}A
, thus
\delta_{i}=\frac{1}{N^{\alpha}}d_{i}
.
We have
\mathbb{E}(\delta_{i})=\frac{1}{N^{\alpha}}\mathbb{E}(d_{i})=\left(\frac{N}{i}\right)^{\gamma}-\frac{p_{ii}}{N^{\alpha}}
.
It follows from this and (
103
)
that with high probability for all
i\in V
we have
\ \frac{1}{4}\left(\frac{N}{i}\right)^{\gamma}\leq\delta_{i}\leq 2\left(\frac{N}{i}\right)^{\gamma}
, thus
\delta_{max}=\Theta\left(N^{\gamma}\right)
holds with high probability.

Now we prove that
\|R\|_{2}=O(1)
holds with high probability.

Let
\Delta:=\mathbb{E}\left(d_{1}\right)=\theta(N^{\alpha+\gamma})
denote the largest expected degree and let us define
\varepsilon=\frac{1}{N}
, say. We have
\Delta>\frac{4}{9}\log\left(\frac{2N}{\varepsilon}\right)
for large enough
N
, thus by the results of
[
6
]
and our assumption
\gamma<\alpha
we have

\displaystyle\|R\|_{2}\leq\|\mathbb{E}\left(R\right)\|_{2}+\frac{1}{N^{\alpha}}\sqrt{4\Delta\log\left(\frac{2N}{\varepsilon}\right)}=\|\mathbb{E}\left(R\right)\|_{2}+\underbrace{O\left(\sqrt{N^{\gamma-\alpha}\log N}\right)}_{\to 0},

with probability at least
1-\varepsilon
. We still need to show that
\|\mathbb{E}\left(R\right)\|_{2}=O(1)
.

Let us define
R^{\prime}=(\frac{p_{ij}}{N^{\alpha}})_{i,j=1}^{N}
. Note that we have
\mathbb{E}(\bar{r}_{ji})=\frac{p_{ij}}{N^{\alpha}}
if
i\neq j
, while
0=\bar{r}_{ii}\leq\frac{p_{ii}}{N^{\alpha}}
, thus the inequalities
0\leq\mathbb{E}\left(R\right)\leq R^{\prime}
hold coordinate-wise, thus we have
\|\mathbb{E}\left(R\right)\|_{2}\leq\|R^{\prime}\|_{2}
. Observe that the rank of
R^{\prime}
is one. More specifically, if we define

v_{i}:=\frac{\left(\frac{N}{i}\right)^{\gamma}}{\sqrt{\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left(\frac{N}{k}\right)^{2\gamma}}}
then
\|v\|_{2}=1
and
R^{\prime}=\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left(\frac{N}{k}\right)^{2\gamma}}{\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left(\frac{N}{k}\right)^{\gamma}}vv^{\intercal}
, thus
\|R^{\prime}\|_{2}=O(1)
by (
99
).
∎

Proof of Lemma
4.19
.

Using
\bar{r}_{ij}=\frac{a_{ij}}{\bar{d}}
we have

\left(R^{2}\right)_{mm}=\sum_{l\in V}\left(\frac{a_{ml}}{\bar{d}}\right)^{2}=\frac{d_{m}}{\bar{d}^{2}},\quad\frac{1}{N}\sum_{m\in V}\left(R^{2}\right)_{mm}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{m\in V}\frac{d_{m}}{\bar{d}^{2}}=\frac{1}{\bar{d}},

\sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{m\in V}\left(R^{2}\right)_{mm}^{2}}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{m\in V}\left(\frac{d_{m}}{\bar{d}^{2}}\right)^{2}}=\frac{1}{\bar{d}}\sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{m\in V}\left(\frac{d_{m}}{\bar{d}}\right)^{2}}\leq\|R\|_{2}\frac{1}{\bar{d}},

where the last inequality follows from

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{m\in V}\left(\frac{d_{m}}{\bar{d}}\right)^{2}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{m\in V}\left(R\mathds{1}\right)_{m}^{2}=\frac{1}{N}\|R\mathds{1}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\|R\|_{2}^{2},

(104)

where
\mathds{1}
denotes the all
1
column vector in
\mathbb{R}^{V}
.
∎

Our next goal is to prove the lower bound stated in Theorem

Lemma 7.15

(

dominates
\sigma_{i}(t)
in the case of the SI model)

Recall the notation from Section

\sigma_{i}(t)

Take an SI process on the weighted graph

Proof.

In the original process vertex

if and only if there is a vertex in
\mathcal{H}_{i}(t)
which is infected at time

. Similarly, we have
\widetilde{\sigma}_{i}(t)=I
in our auxiliary branching structure if and only if there is at least one initially infected augmented vertex in
\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{i,t}(t)

Recalling that we identified

Proof of Theorem
4.20
.

Recall the notation in Section

.

\mathcal{A}_{ij}

\mathcal{A}_{ij}

(j,1)
does not receive any infection signals is lower bounded by

Proof of Theorem
4.22
.

For some

(to be chosen later), let

denote the set of vertices

\geq\frac{e^{-2K}}{16}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i,j\in V_{K}}\bar{r}_{ji}^{2}=\frac{e^{-2K}}{16}\left(\frac{1}{N\bar{d}}\sum_{i,j\in V_{K}}a_{ij}\right)\frac{1}{\bar{d}}.

(105)

\iota
