Local exotic tori
Joé Brendel

Abstract
. For a broad class of symplectic manifolds of dimension at least six, we find the following new phenomenon: there exist local exotic Lagrangian tori. More specifically, let X X be a geometrically bounded symplectic manifold of dimension at least six. We show that every open subset of X X contains infinitely many Lagrangian tori which are distinct up to symplectomorphisms of X X while being Lagrangian isotopic and having the same classical invariants. The proof relies on a locality property of the displacement energy germ, which allows us to compute it in a Darboux chart. Since these tori are not monotone, bubbling may occur and the count of Maslov index two J J -holomorphic disks does not yield an invariant.



1.
Introduction

This paper is motivated by the classification question of Lagrangian submanifolds up to symplectomorphisms of the ambient symplectic manifold. The full classification is open in most cases, for example for Lagrangian tori in
\mathbb{R}^{4}
. In this paper we show that infinitely many Lagrangian tori, which cannot be mapped to one another by an ambient symplectomorphism, occur in every open set of every geometrically bounded symplectic manifold of dimension at least six.

1.1.
Lagrangian tori in symplectic vector spaces

Equip
\mathbb{R}^{2}=\mathbb{C}
with the standard area form
\omega_{0}=d\lambda_{0}
. In
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, the only closed Lagrangians are closed embedded curves and Hamiltonian isotopies are just smooth area-preserving isotopies with compact support. Every closed embedded curve
L
is isotopic to a circle
S^{1}(a)=\{\pi|z|^{2}=a\}
by an area-preserving isotopy which has compact support, where
a=\int_{L}\lambda_{0}
is the area enclosed by
L
. This settles the classification question of Lagrangian tori in
(\mathbb{R}^{2},\omega_{0})
.

Let
\mathbb{R}^{2n}=\mathbb{C}^{n}
be equipped with the product symplectic form
\omega_{0}\oplus\ldots\oplus\omega_{0}=d\lambda
. Then we obtain a family of Lagrangian tori, called
product tori
(or
split tori
), by setting

T(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})=S^{1}(a_{1})\times\ldots\times S^{1}(a_{n}),\quad a_{i}>0.

(1)

Inspired by the case
n=1
, it is natural to ask if every Lagrangian torus in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
is Hamiltonian isotopic to a product torus.

In
\mathbb{C}^{2}
this question was answered negatively by Chekanov
[
13
]
, who constructed the so-called
Chekanov torus
, which cannot be mapped by a symplectomorphism to a product torus. A Lagrangian torus in a symplectic vector space
\mathbb{R}^{2n}=\mathbb{C}^{n}
which cannot be mapped by a symplectomorphism to a product torus is called
exotic
. An alternate construction of the Chekanov torus, which heavily influenced the present paper, was given by Eliashberg–Polterovich
[
20
]
. See also
[
3
,
7
,
15
]
for different points of view on the same construction. Despite significant progress made by Dimitroglou–Rizell
[
18
]
towards a classification, it is unknown as of yet if there are other tori in
\mathbb{R}^{4}
besides product and Chekanov tori.

In
\mathbb{C}^{3}
, on the other hand, it is known that there are infinitely many Lagrangian tori which are exotic and pairwise distinct up to symplectomorphisms. This was proved by Auroux
[
5
]
. The Chekanov torus as well as Auroux’s examples are
monotone
(meaning their area and Maslov classes are positively proportional) and appear in one-parameter families. For every
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 2}
, set

U_{k}=\left\{(a_{1},a_{2})\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{2}\,\left|\,\frac{a_{1}}{k+1}\leqslant a_{2}<\frac{a_{1}}{k}\right.\right\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}.

(2)

In §§
4.1
-
4.2
, we construct Lagrangian tori,

\Upsilon_{k}(a)\subset\mathbb{C}^{3},\quad a\in U_{k}.

(3)

The parameter
a=(a_{1},a_{2})
has the following geometric significance. There is a basis
e_{1},e_{2},e_{3}\in H_{1}(\Upsilon_{k}(a))
of Maslov two classes with

\int_{e_{1}}\lambda|_{\Upsilon_{k}(a)}=a_{1}-ka_{2},\quad\int_{e_{2}}\lambda|_{\Upsilon_{k}(a)}=\int_{e_{3}}\lambda|_{\Upsilon_{k}(a)}=a_{2}.

(4)

This means that
a
determines the area class as defined in (
5
). In particular, the torus
\Upsilon_{k}(a)
is monotone if and only if
a_{1}=(k+1)a_{2}
. In §
4.3
, we prove the following.

Theorem A
.

For every
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 2}
, the torus
\Upsilon_{k}(a)
is exotic. Let
k\neq k^{\prime}
and
a\in U_{k},a^{\prime}\in U_{k^{\prime}}
, then
\Upsilon_{k}(a)
cannot be mapped to
\Upsilon_{k^{\prime}}(a^{\prime})
by a symplectomorphism of
\mathbb{C}^{3}
.

Our construction, as well as our method of distinguishing Lagrangians differs from
[
5
]
. Due to the occurence of non-monotone exotic tori, Theorem
A
strenghtens Auroux’s result.

Remark 1.1
.

We do not know for which
a,a^{\prime}\in U_{k}
, the tori
\Upsilon_{k}(a)
and
\Upsilon_{k}(a^{\prime})
are equivalent up to a symplectomorphism of
\mathbb{C}^{3}
. Note that even the classification of product tori up to ambient symplectomorphisms is a subtle question, see
[
13
, Theorem A]
.

Remark 1.2
.

The monotone members of (
3
) were independently constructed and studied in unpublished work by Dmitry Tonkonog and Renato Vianna; Jeff Hicks was also aware of the construction. Furthermore, R. Vianna informed the author that it is likely, based on computing the superpotential, that the monotone subfamily of the
\Upsilon_{k}
corresponds to Auroux’s examples
[
5
]
.

1.2.
Lagrangian tori in general symplectic manifolds

We consider the question of exotic tori in more general ambient spaces. In sharp contrast to what is known in the case of
\mathbb{C}^{2}
, Vianna
[
53
,
52
]
proved that there are infinitely many monotone Lagrangian tori in
\mathbb{C}P^{2}
and other Del Pezzo surfaces. We will discuss this in detail in §
3.3
. This result was conjectured earlier by Galkin–Usnich
[
32
]
and worked out independently by Galkin–Mikhalkin
[
31
]
. Earlier work producing finitely many exotic Lagrangian tori in different ambient spaces includes
[
2
,
6
,
15
,
22
,
29
,
30
,
44
,
54
]
. We refer to the introduction of
[
44
]
for an overview.

The main aim of this paper is to construct
exotic tori
in general (geometrically bounded) symplectic manifolds of dimension greater or equal to six. The notion of
exoticity
only makes sense in spaces in which there is a natural family of Lagrangian tori, from which the
exotic
examples are distinguished. For example, in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
, the standard tori are product tori; in more general toric manifolds, standard tori are toric fibres.

For a general symplectic manifold
(X,\omega)
, let us clarify the question we are interested in.

Definition 1.3
.

We say that two Lagrangians
L,L^{\prime}\subset(X,\omega)
are
equivalent
if there is
\phi\in\operatorname{Ham}(X,\omega)
with
\phi(L)=L^{\prime}
. This is denoted by
L\cong L^{\prime}
. We say that
L,L^{\prime}
are
inequivalent
or
distinct
if there is no
\phi\in\operatorname{Symp}(X,\omega)
with
\phi(L)=L^{\prime}
and denote this by
L\ncong L^{\prime}
.

The mix of Hamiltonian diffeomorphisms and symplectomorphisms in the definition is justified by the fact that in all the examples we treat, Lagrangians are distinguished in the strongest sense (up to symplectomorphisms) or shown to be equivalent in the strongest sense (up to Hamiltonian diffeomorphisms). We are thus looking for sets of
distinct
Lagrangian tori.

Lagrangian tori may be distinct because they have different
soft invariants
. By soft invariants, we mean Maslov and area class,

m_{L}\colon H_{2}(X,L)\rightarrow\mathbb{Z},\quad\sigma_{L}\colon H_{2}(X,L)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}.

(5)

We have
\phi^{*}m_{\phi(L)}=m_{L}
and
\phi^{*}\sigma_{\phi(L)}=\sigma_{L}
for all
\phi\in\operatorname{Symp}(X,\omega)
. For example if
\operatorname{im}\sigma_{L}\neq\operatorname{im}\sigma_{L^{\prime}}\subset\mathbb{R}
, then
L\ncong L^{\prime}
. In light of this, let us make the following definition.

Definition 1.4
.

We say that
L,L^{\prime}\subset X
are
area-equivalent (in
X
)
if there is a Lagrangian isotopy with endpoints
L
and
L^{\prime}
preserving the area class in the sense that
\Phi^{*}\sigma_{L^{\prime}}=\sigma_{L}
, where
\Phi\colon H_{2}(X,L)\rightarrow H_{2}(X,L^{\prime})
is the isomorphism induced by the Lagrangian isotopy. We write
L\sim L^{\prime}
in that case.

The Maslov class is preserved by Lagrangian isotopies, meaning that both soft invariants of
L
and
L^{\prime}
agree if
L
and
L^{\prime}
are area-equivalent. Both the area and the Maslov class are preserved by Hamiltonian isotopies (and symplectomorphisms more generally), meaning that
L\cong L^{\prime}
implies
L\sim L^{\prime}
. The converse gives rise to the main question addressed in this paper.

Question 1.5
.

For a given symplectic manifold
X
, are there Lagrangians
L,L^{\prime}\subset X
such that
L\sim L^{\prime}
, but
L\ncong L^{\prime}
?

All of the exotic tori from
[
5
,
13
,
15
,
53
,
52
]
discussed so far (and all other examples of exotic tori known to the author) yield examples answering Question
1.5
positively. For example the Chekanov torus in
\mathbb{C}^{2}
is area-equivalent to a monotone product torus
T(a,a)
for a suitable
a>0
, depending on the size of the chosen Chekanov torus. Similarly, the tori
\Upsilon_{k}(a)
appearing in Theorem
A
are area-equivalent for different
k
. More precisely, we prove that
\Upsilon_{k}(a_{1},a_{2})\sim T(a_{1}-ka_{2},a_{2},a_{2})
, where
T(\cdot)
denotes a product torus as in (
1
). See also Remark
4.7
.

We prove the following Theorem in §
4.4
. See
[
39
, Chapter X, Definition 2.2.1]
for a definition of geometrically bounded symplectic manifolds.

Theorem B
.

Let
(X^{6},\omega)
be a geometrically bounded symplectic manifold and
\psi\colon B^{6}(R)\rightarrow X
a Darboux chart. Then there is
\varepsilon>0
such that

\psi(\Upsilon_{k}(a))\ncong\psi(\Upsilon_{k^{\prime}}(a^{\prime}))

(6)

for all
k\neq k^{\prime}
and
a=(a_{1},a_{2})\in U_{k},a^{\prime}=(a_{1}^{\prime},a_{2}^{\prime})\in U_{k^{\prime}}
with
a_{1},a_{1}^{\prime}<\varepsilon
.

Again, we have
\psi(\Upsilon_{k}(a))\sim\psi(T(a_{1}-ka_{2},a_{2},a_{2}))
in
X
, meaning that we find tori answering Question
1.5
in the affirmative.

The condition
a_{1}<\varepsilon
means that the tori to which Theorem
B
applies have a sufficiently small area class. The threshold
\varepsilon>0
is discussed in Remark
1.16
. A significant drawback of this construction is the following. For fixed
\varepsilon>0
, the area class of tori to which the statement of Theorem
B
applies become smaller as
k
increases. Indeed, this follows from (
4
), which yields the area class of
\Upsilon_{k}(a)
in terms of
a\in U_{k}
, togther with (
2
). This means that in a fixed area class, Theorem
B
produces only finitely many inequivalent Lagrangian tori in
(X,\omega)
.

To remedy this drawback, we construct another family of exotic tori in
\mathbb{C}^{3}
by lifting Vianna’s tori in
\mathbb{C}P^{2}
to tori in
\mathbb{C}^{3}
via the Hopf reduction map. For every Markov triple
{\mathfrak{m}}
, meaning every triple
{\mathfrak{m}}=(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
of natural numbers satisfying the Diophantine equation

\alpha^{2}+\beta^{2}+\gamma^{2}=3\alpha\beta\gamma,

(7)

there is a monotone Lagrangian torus
T_{{\mathfrak{m}}}\subset\mathbb{C}P^{2}
appearing as the monotone fibre of a certain almost toric fibration of
\mathbb{C}P^{2}
with
T_{{\mathfrak{m}}}\cong T_{{\mathfrak{m}}^{\prime}}
if and only if
{\mathfrak{m}}={\mathfrak{m}}^{\prime}
, see §§
3.2
-
3.3
or
[
31
,
52
]
. In §
4.5
, we show that the lifted tori
\Theta_{{\mathfrak{m}}}(a)=p_{a}^{-1}(T_{{\mathfrak{m}}})\subset\mathbb{C}^{3}
by the reduction map
p_{a}\colon\mathbb{C}^{3}\supset S^{5}(a)\rightarrow(\mathbb{C}P^{2},a\omega_{\mathbb{C}P^{2}})
have the same property. Note that these tori are monotone, as well.

Proposition 1.6
.

Let
{\mathfrak{m}},{\mathfrak{m}}^{\prime}
be Markov triples, then
\Theta_{{\mathfrak{m}}}(a)\cong\Theta_{{\mathfrak{m}}^{\prime}}(a)
if and only if
{\mathfrak{m}}={\mathfrak{m}}^{\prime}
.

This result was expected and may have been known to experts. What is interesting in the context of the present paper is that this property persists under Darboux embeddings into geometrically bounded symplectic manifolds, provided
a>0
is small enough.

Theorem C
.

Let
(X^{6},\omega)
be a geometrically bounded symplectic manifold and
\psi\colon B^{6}(R)\rightarrow X
a Darboux chart. Then there is
\varepsilon>0
such that

\psi(\Theta_{{\mathfrak{m}}}(a))\ncong\psi(\Theta_{{\mathfrak{m}}^{\prime}}(a)),\quad\psi(\Theta_{{\mathfrak{m}}}(a))\sim\psi(\Theta_{{\mathfrak{m}}^{\prime}}(a))

(8)

for all
a<\varepsilon
and
{\mathfrak{m}}\neq{\mathfrak{m}}^{\prime}
.

See §
4.6
for a proof. Since the set of Markov triples is infinite, Theorem
C
shows that
(X,\omega)
contains infinitely many distinct Lagrangian tori in a (small enough) fixed area class. Thus Theorem
C
remedies the drawback of Theorem
B
pointed out above. The idea used to upgrade Proposition
1.6
to Theorem
C
is the same as the one used to upgrade Theorem
A
to Theorem
B
, and will be discussed in detail in §
1.6
. Again, we refer to Remark
1.16
for a discussion of the size
\varepsilon>0
of tori to which Theorem
C
applies.

Remark 1.7
.

Note that, although we will not consider these in detail, the lifted Vianna tori
\Theta_{{\mathfrak{m}}}
can be extended to natural two-parameter families, by lifting non-monotone Vianna tori from
\mathbb{C}P^{2}
to

Remark 1.8
.

The families
\Upsilon_{k}
and
\Theta_{{\mathfrak{m}}}
are disjoint up to one exception in the sense that
\Upsilon_{k}\ncong\Theta_{{\mathfrak{m}}}

1.3.
Higher dimensions

For simplicity, we have restricted our attention to dimension six in the above discussion. Since our methods behave well when taking products, one obtains the statements of Theorems
A
,
B
and
C

L\times T(c_{1},\ldots,c_{m})\subset\mathbb{C}^{m+3}

\Upsilon_{k}

\Upsilon_{k}

\Theta_{{\mathfrak{m}}}

Theorem D

(Local exotic tori)
.

Let
X
be a geometrically bounded symplectic manifold of dimension at least six and
U\subset X
an open subset. Then
U
contains infinitely many area-equivalent tori which are distinct up to symplectomorphisms of
X
.

These local exotic tori naturally appear in

\dim X=2n
, which seems to be a general feature of exotic tori, see Remark

and Question

. Beyond Theorem

1.5.
The invariant

The invariant we use to distinguish Lagrangian tori is the so-called
displacement energy germ
. It is a strengthened symplectic invariant of Lagrangian submanifolds derived from displacement energy via
versal deformations
. Versal deformations of Lagrangian submanifolds were first introduced by Chekanov
[
13
]
. See §
2.4
for a detailed introduction to the displacement energy germ and
[
9
,
7
,
15
,
16
]
for further applications. Here we give a brief discussion.

The
displacement energy germ
of a closed Lagrangian
L\subset(X,\omega)
is the function

{\mathcal{E}}_{L}\colon H^{1}(L;\mathbb{R})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0}\cup\{+\infty\},

(10)

defined as a germ at
0\in H^{1}(L;\mathbb{R})
of the displacement energy
e(\cdot)\in\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
viewed as a function on the space of Lagrangians. By Weinstein’s neighbourhood theorem, a neighbourhood of
L
in the space of Lagrangians is parametrized by
C^{1}
-small closed differential one-forms on
L
. Furthermore, the graphs of two such forms are Hamiltonian isotopic in the Weinstein neighbourhood if and only if the corresponding forms are cohomologuous. The germ of Lagrangian neighbours of
L
up to the germ of Hamiltonian isotopies is thus in natural bijection with
H^{1}(L;\mathbb{R})
. Since
e(\cdot)
is invariant under symplectomorphisms
{\mathcal{E}}_{L}
is well defined. The displacement energy germ is a symplectic invariant in the sense that

{\mathcal{E}}_{\phi(L)}={\mathcal{E}}_{L}\circ(\phi|_{L})^{*},\quad\phi\in\operatorname{Symp}(X,\omega).

(11)

In practice, one picks a
versal deformation
of
L
, meaning a continuous family of Lagrangians
b\mapsto v_{L}(b)
with
b\in U\subset H^{1}(L;\mathbb{R})
which are parametrized by their Lagrangian flux, see Definition
2.12
. Versal deformations induce the bijection discussed above and thus, to compute
{\mathcal{E}}_{L}
, one can take the germ at the origin
0\in H^{1}(L;\mathbb{R})
of the function
b\mapsto e(v_{L}(b))
. This is independent of the choice of versal deformation.

In our case,
L
is diffeomorphic to a torus and thus
H^{1}(L;\mathbb{R})\cong\mathbb{R}^{n}
and we obtain an invariant up to transformations
(\phi|_{L})^{*}\in\operatorname{GL}(n;\mathbb{Z})
. Furthermore, in applications, we usually determine
b\mapsto e(v_{L}(b))
on an open dense subset of a neighbourhood of the origin
0\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. This yields
{\mathcal{E}}_{L}
on an open dense subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, which is sufficient to distinguish Lagrangians. We make the following definition.

Definition 1.10
.

Let
L,L^{\prime}\subset X
be Lagrangian tori.
We say that
{\mathcal{E}}_{L},{\mathcal{E}}_{L^{\prime}}\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
are
equivalent
if there is
\Phi\in\operatorname{GL}(n;\mathbb{Z})
such that
{\mathcal{E}}_{L^{\prime}}={\mathcal{E}}_{L}\circ\Phi
on an open dense subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. We write
{\mathcal{E}}_{L}\sim{\mathcal{E}}_{L^{\prime}}
.

1.6.
Key ideas: distinguishing tori

Let us first discuss how to distinguish tori in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
. Let
L=\Upsilon_{k}\subset\mathbb{C}^{3}

e(T(a))=\underline{a},\quad a=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{n},

(12)

Remark 1.11

The idea of using the instability of exotic tori to compute invariants coming from versal deformations is due to Chekanov

To distinguish embeddings of

Definition 1.12
.

Let

be a Lagrangian. We say that an embedding
\psi(L)
under a Darboux chart
\psi

if (

The main idea to prove the locality property is to combine the

with the fact that, under certain conditions,

. This goes back to Chekanov–Schlenk

. Let us make some definitions first. Let
J\in{\mathcal{J}}_{\omega}
be an
\omega
-tame almost complex structure. Let

be the infimum over areas of non-constant

-holomorphic spheres in

-holomorphic sphere. Let

\omega
-tame almost complex structures which agree with the push-forward of the standard complex structure
J_{0}
on

\lambda_{S}(X,\psi)=\sup\{\lambda_{S}(X,J)\,|\,J\in{\mathcal{J}}_{\omega},J|_{\operatorname{im}\psi}=\psi_{*}J_{0}\}.

(15)

Definition 1.13
.

We say that a Lagrangian torus

has

if there is a Darboux chart
\psi\colon B(R)\rightarrow(X,\omega)
with

a_{1}+\ldots+a_{n}+\underline{a}<R,

and

\underline{a}<\lambda_{S}(X,\psi).

(17)

In the aspherical case, the following proposition goes back to

(Chekanov–Schlenk)

Let

Lemma 1.15
.

Let

be a Lagrangian torus and

defined on

Proof.
We identify

and

via

induces a natural versal deformation

of

1.7.
Discussion and further questions

In geometrically bounded symplectic manifolds of dimension at least six, the existence of an infinite collection of tori which are area-equivalent but pairwise distinct is a
local
phenomenon, see Theorem
D
. Relative to the current state of knowledge, this is in contrast to the situation in dimension four. As of now - meaning as long as no other tori besides product and Chekanov are found in
\mathbb{C}^{2}
- the existence of infinitely many exotic tori in four-dimensional symplectic manifolds is a
global
phenomenon in the sense that it is highly dependent on the geometry of the ambient space. As illustrated in Example
5.7
, even embedding the Chekanov torus into a manifold by a Darboux chart does not yield a torus which is exotic in a robust way. However, in dimension four there is a semi-local analogue of the constructions discussed in this paper. Instead of using Darboux charts, i.e. ball embeddings, one can use symplectic embeddings of more complicated domains into
X^{4}
in order to construct exotic Lagrangian tori. This is ongoing work joint with Johannes Hauber and Joel Schmitz. See §
5.2
for more details.

The second main point of this paper is that it sheds some light on the (un-)importance of monotonicity when it comes to the study of exotic tori. Most exotic tori which have been studied are monotone, see
[
5
,
9
,
13
,
15
,
31
,
53
,
52
]
, with the notable exceptions of
[
29
]
and
[
49
]
. However, we make the case that this circumstance is mainly an artifact of the methods used to distinguish Lagrangians. In contrast to the superpotential counting Maslov-two
J
-holomorphic disks, the displacement energy germ does not require any adaptations in order to yield a well-defined invariant in the case of non-monotone Lagrangians. In some sense, the tori studied in Theorems
B
,
C
and
D
are the very opposite of monotone, since they bound very small Maslov two disks inside the Darboux chart as opposed to potentially very large disks coming from the ambient space. The fact that the embedded tori in Theorems
B
,
C
and
D
are chosen small relative to the size of the ball embedding contrasts with the phenomenon of exotic tori
untwisting
when they are given enough space, see for example
[
15
, §4]
.

An interesting direction of inquiry raised in §
5.3
is that of interpreting the
\Upsilon_{k}
-tori as fibres of Lagrangian torus fibrations on
\mathbb{C}^{3}
arising from mutations of its toric structure, just as the Vianna tori in
\mathbb{C}P^{2}
appear as fibres of almost toric mutations of the toric structure on
\mathbb{C}P^{2}
. This seems to lead to an analogue of almost toric fibrations in higher dimensions which would provide a framework to carry out higher mutations in arbitrary dimensions to produce more exotic tori. Taking fibres of such mutated Lagrangian torus fibrations should provide a general framework in which both the
\Upsilon_{k}
- and the
\Theta_{{\mathfrak{m}}}
-tori appear as special cases.

1.8.
Structure of the paper

Section
2
provides background on symplectic reduction, integral geometry, displacement energy and the displacement energy germ which is used in later sections. Section
3
gives an overview of
almost toric fibrations
, mutations of ATFs on
\mathbb{C}P^{2}
and a computation of the displacement energy germ of certain almost toric fibres. The approach we follow in §
3.3
was suggested to us by Felix Schlenk based on discussions with Yuri Chekanov. The displacement energy germ of almost toric fibres in
\mathbb{C}P^{2}
is used to distiguish the tori
\Theta_{{\mathfrak{m}}}(a)\subset\mathbb{C}^{3}
and their embeddings, see Proposition
1.6
and Theorems
C
,
D
. The heart of the paper can be found in Section
4
, which contains the construction of the
\Upsilon_{k}
-tori, as well as all proofs. In Section
5

2.1.
Symplectic reduction

In this subsection we introduce some notions surrounding symplectic reduction which will be used in subsequent sections. Although most properties hold in the more general context of Hamiltonian actions of compact Lie groups, we restrict our attention to Hamiltonian torus actions. We refer to
[
12
,
35
,
42
,
43
]
for more details on this classical topic. Let
(X,\omega)
be a symplectic manifold equipped with a Hamiltonian
T^{k}
-action, generated by the moment map
\mu\colon X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{k}
. If the restriction of the
T^{k}
-action to a level set
\mu^{-1}(c)
is free, then the corresponding level set is smooth and its quotient
X_{c}=\mu^{-1}(c)/T^{k}
is a smooth manifold equipped with a naturally induced symplectic form
\omega_{c}
satisfying (
21
). This procedure is called
symplectic reduction
and the symplectic manifold
(X_{c},\omega_{c})
is called
symplectic quotient
. We will use a slightly generalized version of symplectic reduction, where we allow for group actions having finite stabilizers. In that case, the reduced space is a symplectic orbifold, see for example
[
40
]
for details. Symplectic reduction is best summarized by a diagram,

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mu^{-1}(c)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces(X,\omega)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces´(X_{c},\omega_{c}),}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi}

(20)

with

\pi^{*}\omega_{c}=\omega|_{\mu^{-1}(c)}.

(21)

A crucial feature of symplectic reduction we will use repeatedly in this paper is the fact that one can lift Lagrangian submanifolds and Hamiltonian isotopies in a natural way from the reduced space
X_{c}
to
X
, see for example
[
1
,
9
,
8
]
.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
L\subset X_{c}
be a Lagrangian, then
\pi^{-1}(L)\subset\mu^{-1}(c)\subset X
is a
T^{k}
-invariant Lagrangian. Let
{\varphi_{t}}
be a Hamiltonian isotopy of
X_{c}
, then there is a
T^{k}
-equivariant Hamiltonian isotopy
\{\phi_{t}\}
satisfying
\pi\circ\phi_{t}=\varphi_{t}\circ\pi
.

Similarly, invariant Lagrangians and equivariant Hamiltonian isotopies in
X
project to the symplectic quotient
X_{c}
. For Hamiltonian group actions we have the following.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
\nu\colon X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{l}
be another Hamiltonian
T^{l}
-action which commutes with the
T^{k}
-action, i.e.
\{\mu_{i},\nu_{j}\}=0
for all
i,j
. Then there is an induced Hamiltonian
T^{l}
-action on the symplectic quotient
X_{c}
generated by
\nu_{c}\colon X_{c}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{l}
with
\pi^{*}\nu_{c}=\nu|_{\mu^{-1}(c)}
for which
\pi
is
T^{l}
-equivariant.

A special case of this is
toric reduction
. A toric manifold is a symplectic
2n
-manifold
(X,\omega)
equipped with an effective Hamiltonian
T^{n}
-aciton. The image of the moment map
\Delta=\mu(X)\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a polytope with special properties, a so-called
Delzant polytope
, whose integral affine geometry classifies
(X,\omega,\mu)
up to equivariant symplectomorphisms by the Delzant Theorem
[
17
]
. We refer to
[
23
,
34
]
for more details. Performing symplectic reduction with respect to a subtorus
K\subset T^{n}
of the toric action, we are in the context of Proposition
2.2
, since the actions of
K
and
T^{n}
commute. The quotient
X_{c}
carries an effective
T^{n}/K
-action and is thus a toric manifold in its own right. On the level of moment polytopes, toric reduction is encoded by an integral affine embedding of the Delzant polytope of
X_{c}
into
\Delta
. The Delzant construction of toric manifolds
[
34
, Chapter 1]
and McDuff’s probes
[
41
]
are special cases of toric reduction. The symplectic reductions in §
4.1
are of this type, too, and can be seen as performing toric reduction on a family of segments in the moment polytope
\mathbb{R}^{3}_{\geqslant 0}
of
\mathbb{C}^{3}
. We refer to
[
8
, §2.2]
for more details.

Regular fibres
\mu^{-1}(x)
of toric moment maps are Lagrangian tori, see also Example
2.17
. As a special case of Proposition
2.1
, recall from
[
8
, Proposition 2.8]
that toric fibres lift to toric fibres under the toric reduction.

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
(X_{c},\omega_{c},\mu_{c})
be the toric reduction of a toric
(X,\omega,\mu)
with
\iota\colon\Delta_{c}\hookrightarrow\Delta
the corresponding integral affine embedding of Delzant polytopes. Then

\mu^{-1}(\iota(x))=\pi^{-1}(\mu_{c}^{-1}(x)),\quad x\in\operatorname{int}\Delta_{c}.

(22)

2.2.
Some integral geometry

Integral geometry is the geometry associated to transformations preserving a lattice of maximal rank in a finite-dimensional vector space. We restrict our attention to the lattice
\mathbb{Z}^{n}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
which has symmetry group
\operatorname{GL}(n;\mathbb{Z})
. Our interest in integral geometry comes from the fact that, as discussed in §
1.5
and §
2.4
, the displacement energy germ of Lagrangian tori is an invariant up to
\operatorname{GL}(n;\mathbb{Z})
. For related reasons, the regular locus of the base of any Lagrangian torus fibration has an
integral affine structure
. This means that the Lagrangian torus fibration induces a manifold atlas with transition charts in
\operatorname{GL}(n;\mathbb{Z})\ltimes\mathbb{R}^{n}
, which essentially comes from action coordinates and the Arnold–Liouville theorem, see
[
23
, §2.3]
. The integral affine structure of Lagrangian torus fibrations is intimately related to the versal deformations of its regular fibres, see Proposition
2.14
.

A vector
v\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
is called
primitive
if its entries are coprime. Let
w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a vector with a rational direction. Then there is a unique
\alpha>0
such that
w=\alpha v
for a primitive
v\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
and
\alpha
is called
integral affine length of
w
and denoted by
\ell_{\rm IA}(w)=\alpha
. Note that this corresponds to the Euclidean length of the image of
w
under a map in
\operatorname{GL}(n;\mathbb{Z})
mapping
w
into a coordinate axis of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Let
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a rational affine hyperplane. Then the
integral affine distance

d_{\operatorname{IA}}(x,P)
of a point
x
to
P
is defined as the Euclidean distance of
\phi(x)
to
\{x_{n}=0\}
, where
\phi\in\operatorname{GL}(n,\mathbb{Z})\ltimes\mathbb{R}^{n}
is an integral affine transformation with
\phi(P)=\{x_{n}=0\}
. This is independent of the choice of
\phi
. Write
P=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\,|\,\langle y,v\rangle+\lambda=0\}
for a primitive
v\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, then
d_{\rm IA}(x,P)=|\langle x,v\rangle+\lambda|
. Now let

\Delta=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\;|\;\ell_{i}(x)\geqslant 0\},\quad\ell_{i}(x)=\langle x,v_{i}\rangle+\lambda_{i}

(23)

be a convex polytopes defined by the intersection of half-spaces delimited by rational affine hyperplanes
P_{i}=\{\ell_{i}(y)=0\}
for
i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
. Again, the
v_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
are chosen to be primitive. They can be thought of as the inwards pointing normal vectors to the facets of
\Delta
. Then the
integral affine distance

d_{\rm IA}(x,\partial\Delta)
of
x\in\Delta
to the boundary
\partial\Delta
is defined as

d_{\rm IA}(x,\partial\Delta)=\min_{1\leqslant i\leqslant N}\{d_{\rm IA}(x,P_{i})\}=\min\{\ell_{1}(x),\ldots,\ell_{N}(x)\}.

(24)

Definition 2.4
.

Let
v_{1},\ldots,v_{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
for
k\leqslant n
. The
integral index

D_{\rm IA}=D_{\rm IA}(v_{1},\ldots,v_{k})
is defined as the greatest common divisor of

\left\{\det(v_{1}|\ldots|v_{k}|w_{k+1}|\ldots|w_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}\,|\,w_{k+1},\ldots,w_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\right\}.

(25)

This is an integral invariant of the unordered
k
-tuple
v_{1},\ldots,v_{k}
. Its geometric interpretation is that it is equal to the index of the sublattice
\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{Z}}\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{k}\}
of the lattice
\mathbb{Z}^{n}\cap\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{R}}\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{k}\}
. For computations, the following characterization is the most convenient, see
[
38
, Chapter 18]
for a proof and more details.

Proposition 2.5
.

The integral index
D_{\rm IA}(v_{1},\ldots,v_{k})\in\mathbb{Z}
is equal to the greatest common divisor of all
k
-minors (meaning determinants of
k\times k
submatrices) of the matrix
(v_{1}|\ldots|v_{k})
.

2.3.
Displacement energy

Let
A
be a compact subset of a symplectic manifold
(X,\omega)
. Recall that the
displacement energy of
A
is defined as

e(A)=\inf\{\|H\|\,|\,\phi^{H}_{1}(A)\cap A=\varnothing\},

(26)

where
\phi^{H}_{1}
denotes the time-one flow of a compactly supported, time-dependent Hamiltonian
H
. We set
e(A)=\infty
if the infimum in (
26
) is taken over the empty set and by
\|\cdot\|
we denote the Hofer norm,

\|H\|=\int_{0}^{1}\left(\max_{x\in X}H_{t}(x)-\min_{x\in X}H_{t}(x)\right)dt.

(27)

We refer to the classic
[
47
]
for details on Hofer geometry. The quantity
e(A)
is a symplectic invariant of the set
A
, in the sense that if there is a symplectomorphism of
(X,\omega)
mapping
A
to another subset
B\subset X
, then the displacement energies of
A
and
B
agree. In this article, we are exclusively interested in the displacement energy of Lagrangian tori. Chekanov
[
14
]
(see also
[
45
]
) gave a lower bound for displacement energy of compact Lagrangian submanifolds in geometrically bounded symplectic manifolds in terms of
J
-holomorphic spheres in
X
and
J
-holomorphic disks with boundary on the Lagrangian in question. Denote by
{\mathcal{J}}={\mathcal{J}}(X,\omega)
the set of
\omega
-tame almost complex structures. Let
\lambda_{D}(X,L,J)
denote the infimum of symplectic areas realized by non-trivial
J
-disks with boundary on
L
. If such disks exist, then the infimum is positive and is attained by at least one disk by Gromov compactness. If not, then set
\lambda_{D}(X,L,J)=\infty
. Similarly, denote by
\lambda_{S}(X,J)
the infimum of areas realized by non-trivial
J
-spheres.

Theorem 2.6

(Chekanov)
.

Let
L\subset X
be a compact Lagrangian in a geometrically bounded symplectic manifold and
J
an
\omega
-tame almost complex structure. Then

\min\{\lambda_{D}(X,L,J),\lambda_{S}(X,J)\}\leqslant e(L).

(28)

Example 2.7

(Product tori in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
)
.

Let
\mathbb{C}
be equipped with the standard symplectic form and let
S^{1}(x)\subset\mathbb{C}
be the circle enclosing symplectic area
x>0
. Then
e(S^{1}(x))=x
. The upper bound comes from an explicit construction and the lower bound comes from the obvious holomorphic disk with boundary on
S^{1}(x)
together with Theorem
2.6
. Taking products of this example, we obtain the
product tori
in
\mathbb{C}^{n}

2.4.
Versal deformations and the displacement energy germ

We give a brief overview of the method of versal deformations here. When applied to displacement energy, it yields the
displacement energy germ
, which is the invariant used in this paper to distinguish Lagrangian submanifolds. See also
[
13
,
16
]
and
[
9
,
7
,
15
]
for further applications.

Let
L\subset X
be a compact Lagrangian and let
{\mathcal{L}}={\mathcal{L}}_{L}
be the space of Lagrangians which are Lagrangian isotopic to
L
, equipped with the
C^{1}
-topology, see for example
[
46
]
for details. In this topology, a small enough neighbourhood
{\mathcal{U}}
of
L\in{\mathcal{L}}
is in bijection with a neighbourhood of the zero
1
-form in the set of closed differential one-forms on
L
. This follows from Weinstein’s neighbourhood theorem applied to
L
together with the fact that a graph
\Gamma_{\alpha}\subset(T^{*}L,d\lambda)
of a differential
1
-form
\alpha\in\Omega^{1}(L)
is Lagrangian if and only if
\alpha
is closed. Furthermore, the graphs
\Gamma_{\alpha},\Gamma_{\beta}
are Hamiltonian isotopic in
T^{*}L
if and only if the corresponding forms are cohomologous,
[\alpha]=[\beta]\in H_{\rm dR}^{1}(L;\mathbb{R})
. In other words, there is a continuous bijection between
C^{1}
-small Lagrangian perturbations of
L
up to locally supported Hamiltonian isotopies and small elements in
H^{1}(L;\mathbb{R})
. In terms of a Weinstein chart
\varphi\colon T^{*}L\supset V\rightarrow X
, this bijection can be realized by taking the class up to Hamiltonian isotopies supported in
\operatorname{im}\varphi
of
v_{L}^{\varphi}(b)=\varphi(\Gamma_{\beta(b)})
. Here
b\mapsto\beta(b)
is a continuous family of closed
1
-forms satisfying
[\beta(b)]=b
.

Example 2.9
.

Let
L\subset X
be a Lagrangian torus and
\varphi\colon T^{*}T^{n}\supset V\rightarrow X
be a Weinstein chart. Identifying
T^{n}=(\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z})^{n}
, we obtain induced identifications
T^{*}T^{n}\cong(\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z})^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}=\{(\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{n},p_{1},\ldots,p_{n})\}
and
H^{1}(T^{n};\mathbb{R})\cong\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Set
\beta(b)=\sum_{i}b_{i}d\theta_{i}
to obtain

v^{\varphi}_{L}(b)=\varphi(T^{n}\times\{b\}).

(33)

The above discussion is closely related to the Lagrangian flux of Lagrangian isotopies contained in the image of
\varphi
. We recall the definition of Lagrangian flux for the reader’s convenience, see also
[
46
]
. Let
\{i_{t}\}_{t\in[0,1]}
be a Lagrangian isotopy, meaning that there is a smooth map
i\colon[0,1]\times L\rightarrow X
such that
i_{t}=i(t,\cdot)
is a Lagrangian embedding for every
t\in[0,1]
. The embeddings being Lagrangian is equivalent to the forms
i_{t}^{*}\iota(X_{t})\omega\in\Omega^{1}(L)
being closed for all
t\in[0,1]
, where
X_{t}\in\Gamma(i_{t}^{*}TX)
is the vector field generating
i_{t}
. The
Lagrangian flux of
\{i_{t}\}
is defined as the average cohomology class of this family of forms,

\operatorname{Flux}\{i_{t}\}=\int_{0}^{1}[i_{t}^{*}\iota(X_{t})\omega]\in H^{1}(L;\mathbb{R}).

(34)

As in the case of the flux of symplectic isotopies, see
[
47
, Chapter 14]
or
[
42
, §10.2]
, the flux of Lagrangian isotopies can be geometrically interpreted as a measure of the symplectic area of cylinders swept out by one-cycles. Let
c
be a one-cycle on
L
with homology class
\xi=[c]\in H_{1}(L)
. We obtain a two-chain
C_{\xi}=\cup_{t\in[0,1]}i_{t}(c)
in
X
, defining a relative homology class
[C_{\xi}]\in H_{2}(X,\iota_{0}(L)\cup\iota_{1}(L))
. The assignment
\xi\mapsto[C_{\xi}]
yields a well-defined, linear map
H_{1}(L)\rightarrow H_{2}(X,\iota_{0}(L)\cup\iota_{1}(L))
. Since
\iota_{0},\iota_{1}
are Lagrangian embeddings, the symplectic form yields a well-defined functional
[\omega]\colon H_{2}(X,\iota_{0}(L)\cup\iota_{1}(L))\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. From this point of view, Lagrangian flux can be viewed as

\langle\operatorname{Flux}\{i_{t}\},\xi\rangle=\langle[\omega],[C_{\xi}]\rangle,\quad\xi\in H_{1}(L).

(35)

In this paper, we work with unparametrized Lagrangian isotopies, i.e. families
\{L_{t}\}_{t\in[0,1]}
of Lagrangian submanifolds. One can always choose a parametrization
i_{t}(L)=L_{t}
and prove that the above discussion is independent of this choice up to picking an identification
L=L_{0}
. Furthermore, we sometimes consider
continuous
families of Lagrangians, i.e. continuous maps
[0,1]\rightarrow{\mathcal{L}}
. By its homological interpretation (
35
), the Lagrangian flux is well-defined. Furthermore since
\omega
is closed, Stokes’ theorem and (
35
) show that the Lagrangian flux is invariant under homotopies of Lagrangian isotopies with fixed endpoints.

Remark 2.10
.

A Lagrangian isotopy
\{i_{t}\}
is induced by an ambient Hamiltonian isotopy if and only if the forms
i_{t}^{*}\iota(X_{t})\omega
are exact, i.e. the Lagrangian flux
\operatorname{Flux}\{i_{t}\}_{t\in[0,t_{0}]}
is zero for all
t_{0}\in[0,1]
.

Remark 2.11
.

The Lagrangian flux measures the difference between the area classes
\sigma_{L_{0}},\sigma_{L_{1}}
(see (
5
)) of endpoints of a Lagrangian isotopy
\{L_{t}\}
, in the sense that

\langle\Phi^{*}\sigma_{L_{1}}-\sigma_{L_{0}},D\rangle=\langle\operatorname{Flux}\{L_{t}\},\partial D\rangle,\quad D\in H_{2}(X,L_{0}),

(36)

where
\Phi\colon H_{2}(X,L_{0})\rightarrow H_{2}(X,L_{1})
is the isomorphism induced by
\{L_{t}\}
and
\partial\colon H_{2}(X,L_{0})\rightarrow H_{1}(L_{0})
is the boundary map of the long exact sequence of the pair
(X,L_{0})
. In particular, a Lagrangian isotopy with vanishing flux and endpoints on non-equivalent Lagrangians yields an example answering Question
1.5
positively. The existence of such Lagrangian isotopies (see for example Remark
4.7
) is in contrast with the case of symplectic flux, where every isotopy with vanishing symplectic flux is homotopic to a Hamiltonian isotopy, see
[
42
, Theorem 10.2.5]
.

Let us return to the map
v_{L}^{\varphi}
defined above. Since
\varphi^{*}\omega=d\lambda
on
\operatorname{im}\varphi
, Stokes’ theorem shows that the flux of a Lagrangian isotopy
\{L_{t}\}
contained in
\operatorname{im}\varphi
depends only on its endpoints. Furthermore, if
L_{1}=\varphi(\Gamma_{\beta})
, then
\operatorname{Flux}\{L_{t}\}=[\beta]\in H^{1}(L;\mathbb{R})
. This means that the Lagrangian flux induces an inverse map to the bijection
v_{L}^{\varphi}
. The map
v_{L}^{\varphi}
is an example of a versal deformation. However, we sometimes work with versal deformations without considering an underlying Weinstein chart
\varphi
. In general, a versal deformation is defined as a right-inverse to the Lagrangian flux map.

Definition 2.12
.

A continuous map
v_{L}\colon H^{1}(L;\mathbb{R})\supset U\rightarrow{\mathcal{L}}_{L}
defined on a simply-connected neighbourhood
U
of the origin is called a
versal deformation
of
L
if
v_{L}(0)=L
and if the Lagrangians in its image are parametrized by their flux. By this we mean that

\operatorname{Flux}\{v_{L}(\gamma(t))\}_{t\in[0,1]}=b,

(37)

for any
b\in U
and any continuous curve
\gamma\colon[0,1]\rightarrow U
with
\gamma(0)=0
and
\gamma(1)=b
.

Although versal deformations are not unique, they are locally unique up to Hamiltonian isotopy. More precisely, let
v_{L}\colon U\rightarrow{\mathcal{L}}_{L}
and
v_{L}^{\prime}\colon U^{\prime}\rightarrow{\mathcal{L}}_{L}
be two versal deformations of
L
. Then there is a neighbourhood of the origin
V\subset U\cap U^{\prime}
such that

v_{L}(b)\cong v_{L}^{\prime}(b),\quad b\in V.

(38)

This follows from the fact that, given a Weinstein chart
\varphi
of
L
, there is a small enough neighbourhood
V
of the origin
0\in H^{1}(L;\mathbb{R})
such that every Lagrangian in the image of
v_{L}|_{V}
and
v_{L}^{\prime}|_{V}
is the image of a graph of a closed
1
-form under
\varphi
, and closed
1
-forms are classified up to Hamiltonian isotopy by their cohomology class. Furthermore, let
\phi\in\operatorname{Symp}(X,\omega)
and
v_{\phi(L)}
be a versal deformation of
\phi(L)
, then there is a neighbourhood of the origin
V^{\prime}\subset H^{1}(\phi(L);\mathbb{R})
such that

v_{\phi(L)}(b)\cong(\phi\circ v_{L}\circ\phi|_{L}^{*})(b),\quad b\in V^{\prime}.

(39)

Chekanov’s idea is to evaluate a symplectic invariant of Lagrangian submanifolds on the image of a versal deformation and then take its germ to extract an invariant. In our case, we take displacement energy as symplectic invariant and consider the function

H^{1}(L;\mathbb{R})\supset U\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\},\quad b\mapsto e(v_{L}(b)),

(40)

where
v_{L}
is a versal deformation of
L
.

Definition 2.13
.

Let
L\subset(X,\omega)
be a compact Lagrangian.
The germ
{\mathcal{E}}_{L}\colon H^{1}(L;\mathbb{R})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
at
0\in H^{1}(L;\mathbb{R})
of the function (
40

.

By (
38
) and the invariance of

under symplectomorphisms, the displacement energy germ is independent of the choice of versal deformation. The symplectic invariance property (
11
) of the displacement energy germ similarly follows from (
39

is an orbit of a Hamiltonian

-equivariant Weinstein chart of

defined by

Proposition 2.14
.

Let

be a Lagrangian torus and

the moment map of a (locally defined) Hamiltonian

In particular, a versal deformation of a regular fibre of a Lagrangian torus fibration (or integrable system) can be computed by computing local action-coordinates on the basis. Recall that this is equivalent to computing the flux map, see
[
23

3.
Almost toric fibrations

3.1.
Overview

Almost toric fibrations (ATFs) are a type of Lagrangian torus fibrations on four-dimensional symplectic manifolds introduced by Symington
[
51
]
, which are slightly more general than toric fibrations. We give a brief outline of ATFs here with an emphasis on the properties used in the context of this paper. For more details on almost toric fibrations, we recommend
[
23
]
.

Toric fibrations lie at the intersection of Hamiltonian group actions and Lagrangian torus fibrations (or integrable systems, the two can be used interchangeably for the present purposes). Indeed the moment map
\mu\colon X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
of a toric action on a symplectic manifold
(X,\omega)
can be viewed as a special type of Lagrangian torus fibration. It is special in the sense that the moment map yields
global
action coordinates, i.e. action coordinates on the regular locus
\mu^{-1}(\operatorname{int}\Delta)
of
\mu
. In the vicinity of a compact regular fibre, every Lagrangian torus fibration can be equipped with
local
action-angle coordinates compatible with the fibration; this is the classical Arnold–Liouville Theorem
[
23
, Theorem 1.42]
. However, in general, action-angle coordinates do not extend globally. See Duistermaat
[
19
]
for a study of obstructions to this extension. One obstruction that we encounter when discussing ATFs is the topological monodromy of the torus bundle over the regular locus. If the regular locus is simply-connected, as is the case for toric fibrations, then the topological monodromy is trivial.

To move from toric to almost toric fibrations, we lose the Hamiltonian group action. In terms of Lagrangian torus fibrations/integrable systems, four-dimensional toric fibrations have a very restricted set of allowable singularities, namely only elliptic-regular and elliptic-elliptic singularities appear. We refer to
[
55
,
57
]
for details. In the almost toric case, the set of permitted singularities is extended by adding the focus-focus case, see
[
56
]
. An almost toric fibration is thus a (proper) Lagrangian torus fibration
F\colon X^{4}\rightarrow B^{2}
with singularities of the three types discussed above. In the context of this paper, we have
B^{2}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Note that the focus-focus singularity does not have
T^{2}
-symmetry (it only has
S^{1}
-symmetry), meaning that we lose the globally defined Hamiltonian
T^{2}
-action present in the toric case. Furthermore, the focus-focus singularities map to singular values (called
nodes
or
nodal points
) in the interior of
B
, meaning that the regular locus
B_{\rm reg}\subset B
is not simply connected (as opposed to the toric case) and the torus bundle
F|_{F^{-1}(B_{\rm reg})}\colon F^{-1}(B_{\rm reg})\rightarrow B_{\rm reg}
has non-trivial topological monodromy. This means that whenever
F
has at least one focus-focus singularity, there are no global action-angle coordinates on
F^{-1}(B_{\rm reg})
, meaning that a priori it is not obvious what the analogue of the moment map
\mu
and the moment polytope
\Delta
should be in the almost toric case. However, one can find curves
C_{i}
in
B
, such that their complement
B_{\rm reg}\setminus\cup_{i}C_{i}\subset B_{\rm reg}
is simply-connected. In the cases relevant to this paper, every
C_{i}
connects a node to the toric boundary of

. For simplicity, we assume the set of curves

to be mutually disjoint. On

, there are global action coordinates. Thus one gets a reasonable analogue of the moment map as follows. First, post-compose
F|_{F^{-1}(B_{\rm reg}\setminus\cup_{i}C_{i})}
with the diffeomorphism yielding action coordinates. Denote the resulting map by
\pi_{0}
. Second, as it turns out, one can choose the curves
C_{i}
in such a way that there is a continuous extension
\pi
of
\pi_{0}
mapping

to the closure
\Delta_{\pi}=\overline{\operatorname{im}\pi_{0}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
such that
\Delta_{\pi}
is a polytope and such that the image of every
F^{-1}(C_{i})
in
\Delta_{\pi}
is a line segment, which we call a

. Decorating
\Delta_{\pi}
with branch cuts (represented by dashed lines in the conventions of
[

]
) indicating the image of the curves
C_{i}
and with nodes (represented by crosses) indicating which fibres contain focus-focus singularities, we obtain a so-called
ATF base diagram
of the Lagrangian torus fibration
F\colon X\rightarrow B
we started with. Abusing notation, we denote by
\Delta_{\pi}
both the polytope
\Delta_{\pi}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
obtained as the image of
\pi
and the base diagram, i.e. the polytope decorated with branch cuts and nodes. A crucial point in the ATF-framework is that there is a Delzant-type theorem

4

The classical Delzant Theorem
[

]
states that a toric manifold is determined, up to equivariant symplectomorphism, by the integral affine equivalence class of its moment polytope.
for ATF base diagrams. More precisely, the ATF base diagram
\Delta_{\pi}
determines the symplectic manifold

equipped with an almost toric fibration that is unique up to symplectomorphisms which are fibre-preserving up to a union of arbitrarily small neighbourhoods of the nodal points in

[

.

The almost toric base diagram
\Delta_{\pi}
should be thought of as a geometric-combinatorial object encoding an almost toric fibration
F\colon X\rightarrow B
having
\Delta_{\pi}
as its ATF base diagram in the sense described above, i.e. by removing suitable curves
C_{i}\subset B
and computing action coordinates on their complement. In practice, these subtleties are often glossed over, and, by abuse of terminology, the map
\pi\colon X\rightarrow\Delta_{\pi}
is called
almost toric fibration
. When working with the map
\pi
explicitly, we keep in mind that it is not smooth over the branch cuts, but that it yields honest action coordinates when restricted to the complement of branch cuts.

Note that, since it involves a choice of curves
C_{i}
, the ATF base diagram is
not
uniquely defined up to integral affine transformations. However, the choice of curves
C_{i}
for which the outcome is as described above is very restricted. In fact, there are only two possible choices of
C_{i}
(pointing in opposite directions) for the
i
-th node. Switching at the
i
-th node from a given choice of
C_{i}
to the one in the opposite direction is called

. Note that changing a branch cut does not change the underlying almost toric fibration
F\colon X\rightarrow B
, merely the way of representing it by a base diagram. Let
l_{i}
be the line containing the branch cut emanating from the
i
-th node in
\Delta_{\pi}
. In terms of base diagrams, changing the branch cut at the
i
-th node corresponds to passing from
\Delta_{\pi}
to
\Delta_{\pi^{\prime}}
by the branch cut switching sides in
l_{i}
at the cross representing the
i
-th node. A computation of action coordinates on the so-obtained simply-connected domain yields that passing from
\Delta_{\pi}
to
\Delta_{\pi^{\prime}}
corresponds to applying a piece-wise linear transformation
\tau\colon\Delta_{\pi}\rightarrow\Delta_{\pi^{\prime}}
. The map
\tau
is continuous, has eigenline
l_{i}
and acts by integral affine transformations on the two halves of
\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus l_{i}
. By convention, since everything is up to integral affine transformations, one chooses
\tau
to be the identity on one of the halves. Then it is explicitly given by

\tau(x)=\begin{cases}\sigma_{w}(x)=x-\det(w|x)w,&\det(w|x)\geqslant 0\\
x,&\det(w|x)<0,\end{cases}

(45)

where
w\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
is the primitive vector contained in
l_{i}
pointing in the direction of the new branch cut. We call

a
mutation of ATF base diagrams
.

Another crucial feature of almost toric fibrations is that, as opposed to toric fibrations, one can apply certain operations to them which produce new ATFs from given ones. These operations are called
nodal trades
and
nodal slides
. Nodal trades exchange an elliptic-elliptic singularity for a focus-focus singularity, merging the two one-parameter families of elliptic-regular singularities emanating from the original elliptic-elliptic singularity. Nodal slides change the position of the node in the ATF base diagram
\Delta_{\pi}
by sliding along the line
l_{i}
containing the corresponding node and its branch cut. See
[
23
, Sections 8.2/8.3]
for details. Thus, given a toric fibration, one can first apply nodal trades to obtain an ATF and then nodal slides to obtain new ATFs on a fixed symplectic manifold. Usually, one changes the branch cut along the way in order for the different branch cuts not to intersect. This process is called
mutation
of ATFs, see
[
23
, Section 8.4]
. As we shall see, mutations are an effective method, pioneered by Vianna
[
53
,
52
]
, to come up with exotic Lagrangian tori.

For the reader’s convenience and to set up notation, let us briefly recall the almost toric fibrations obtained on

The tori

3.3.
Almost toric fibres

In this subsection, we compute the displacement energy of Lagrangian tori appearing as regular fibres of certain almost toric fibrations, which allows us to determine their displacement energy germ. The idea to use the displacement energy germ to distinguish almost toric fibres to obtain results similar to

3.3

.
The
T^{3}
-action on
\mathbb{C}^{3}
by coordinate-wise rotation is Hamiltonian and generated by the standard moment map,

\mu\colon\mathbb{C}^{3}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0}^{3},\quad\mu(z_{1},z_{2},z_{3})=(\pi|z_{1}|^{2},\pi|z_{2}|^{2},\pi|z_{3}|^{2})

(60)

Let
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 2}
and define the moment map

\nu_{k}\colon\mathbb{C}^{3}\rightarrow\Delta_{k},\quad\nu_{k}=(\mu_{1}+k\mu_{3},\mu_{2}-\mu_{3}).

(61)

This moment map generates a Hamiltonian
T^{2}
-action induced by the standard
T^{3}
-action via the inclusion

T^{2}\hookrightarrow T^{3},\quad\exp(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})\mapsto\exp(\theta_{1},\theta_{2},k\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}).

(62)

Here,
\Delta_{k}=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\,|\,x\geqslant 0,x+ky\geqslant 0\}
denotes the image of
\nu_{k}
. The set of critical points is given by
\operatorname{Crit}\nu_{k}=\cup_{i\neq j}\{z_{i}=z_{j}=0\}
and the critical values are given by
\partial\Delta_{k}\cup(\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0}\times\{0\})
. Denote the subtorus obtained as image of the inclusion (
62
) by
T_{k}\subset T^{3}
.

We discuss symplectic reduction with respect to
\nu_{k}
for

, paying special attention to the critical values of the form

. Every level set
\nu_{k}^{-1}(c)
contains one orbit with stabilizer isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}/k\mathbb{Z}
, namely the orbit obtained by intersecting
\nu_{k}^{-1}(c)
with
\{z_{1}=0\}
. This orbit maps to an orbifold point of order
k
in the reduced space
\Sigma_{k,c}
. For
c_{2}\neq 0
, the group
T_{k}
acts freely on the complement in
\nu_{k}^{-1}(c)
of that orbit. For
c=(c_{1},0)
, the level set
\nu_{k}^{-1}(c)
intersects
\{z_{2}=z_{3}=0\}
in the orbit
\mu^{-1}(c_{1},0,0)
and thus for
c_{1}>0
, the stabilizer of that orbit is isomorphic to
S^{1}
. Removing this exceptional orbit, we can perform symplectic reduction to obtain an orbifold reduced space with one puncture. To summarize, for
c\in\operatorname{int}\Delta_{k}
we have the following reduction diagram,

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Z_{k,c}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{C}^{3}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces´(\Sigma_{k,c},\omega_{k,c}),}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi_{k,c}}

where
Z_{k,c}=\nu_{k}^{-1}(c)
in case

c_{2}=0

Proposition 4.1
.

For
c_{2}\neq 0
, the reduced space
\Sigma_{k,c}
is a symplectic orbifold sphere with one orbifold point of order
k
. For
c_{2}=0
, the reduced space is an orbifold sphere with one orbifold point of order
k
and one puncture. The quotient space carries an induced effective Hamiltonian
S^{1}
-action generated by a moment map
H_{k,c}\colon\Sigma_{k,c}\rightarrow[0,\alpha_{k}(c)]
, where

\alpha_{k}(c)=\int_{\Sigma_{k,c}}\omega_{k,c}=\begin{cases}\frac{c_{1}}{k},&c_{2}\geqslant 0\\
\frac{c_{1}}{k}+c_{2},&c_{2}<0\end{cases}

(64)

is the symplectic area of the reduced space. Furthermore, product tori in
\mathbb{C}^{3}
map to orbits of the
H_{k,c}
-action on
\Sigma_{k,c}
. More precisely, we have

\pi_{k,c}^{-1}(H_{k,c}^{-1}(c_{3}))=\begin{cases}T(c_{1}-kc_{3},c_{3}+c_{2},c_{3}),&c_{2}\geqslant 0\\
T(c_{1}-kc_{3}+kc_{2},c_{3},c_{3}-c_{2}),&c_{2}<0.\end{cases}

(65)

Proof.
The topology of the reduced spaces has been discussed above. Away from the orbifold point and the puncture in the case
c_{2}=0
, the reduction (
63
) is a special case of toric reduction (see the discussion in §
2.1
), meaning that there is an induced Hamiltonian circle action on

which is toric. This can be used to prove (
64
) and (
65
), see Proposition
2.3
.

For the reader’s convenience, let us nonetheless discuss the details. The Hamiltonian
T^{3}
-action on
\mathbb{C}^{3}
descends to the quotients
\Sigma_{k,c}
since
\nu_{k}
(generating a subaction of the standard

-action) commutes with
\mu
, see Proposition

. The induced

-action on
\Sigma_{k,c}
has stabilizer
T_{k}
, meaning that it yields an effective Hamiltonian action by

on the quotient. A moment map for this induced action can be defined by picking a circle which is complementary to

, whose action is generated by
\mu_{3}
. For

, the moment map

descends to the Hamiltonian

. In case
c_{2}<0
, we take
\mu_{3}|_{Z_{k,c}}+c_{2}
, instead. By computing the image

takes values in
[0,\alpha_{k}(c)]
(respectively
(0,\alpha_{k}(c)]
for

) where
\alpha_{k}(c)
is defined as in (

). Since this Hamiltonian circle action is effective, it is toric and thus (as an action coordinate) its range computes the area of
\Sigma_{k,c}
, proving (
64
).

To prove (
65
), note that

is an orbit of the residual action induced by the
T^{3}
-action on
\mathbb{C}^{3}
. By
T^{3}
-equivariance of
\pi_{k,c}
, see Proposition
2.2
, this orbit lifts to an orbit of the

-action on
\mathbb{C}^{3}

. To determine

and

by definition of

is equal to the area of the sublevel set

, since

generates a Hamiltonian circle action and by its normalization, we find that

is the integral affine distance of the point
(a_{1},a_{2},a_{3})
to the union of facets
\{x_{1}=0\}\cup\{x_{2}=0\}

Remark 4.3

The same construction can also be carried out, with small modifications at various points, for

Let
a_{1},a_{2}>0

-equivariant Weinstein chart,

T^{*}T^{3}=(\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z})^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}=\{(\theta,p)=(\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\theta_{3},p_{1},p_{2},p_{3})\}

on

. As in (

v_{\Upsilon_{k}}^{\varphi}\colon H^{1}(T^{3};\mathbb{R})\cong\mathbb{R}^{3}\supset U\rightarrow{\mathcal{L}}_{\Upsilon_{k}},\quad v_{\Upsilon_{k}}^{\varphi}(b)=\varphi(T^{3}\times\{b\}),

(67)

where we have identified

Let

bounds a disk of area

(69)

be the standard basis of the first homology of

y
is given by (
105
). Furthermore (
108
) and (
109
) imply that the versal deformations of
\Theta_{{\mathfrak{m}}}^{n}
have property (CS). This means that we can apply Lemma
1.15
to conclude that
\psi(\Theta_{{\mathfrak{m}}}^{n})
has the locality property. Together with (
103
), this allows us to deduce that if
{\mathfrak{m}}\neq{\mathfrak{m}}^{\prime}
, then

\psi(\Theta_{{\mathfrak{m}}}^{n}(a,a_{4},\ldots,a_{n}))\ncong\psi(\Theta_{{\mathfrak{m}}^{\prime}}^{n}(a,a_{4},\ldots,a_{n})),\quad\psi(\Theta_{{\mathfrak{m}}}^{n}(a,a_{4},\ldots,a_{n}))\sim\psi(\Theta_{{\mathfrak{m}}^{\prime}}^{n}(a,a_{4},\ldots,a_{n})),

(110)

which proves Theorem
D
.

\Box

5.
Questions and outlook

5.1.
Questions

The following question came up in discussions with L. Polterovich.

Question 5.1
.

When are exotic tori unstable?

As mentioned in §
1.6
, we say that an exotic torus
L\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
is
unstable
if an open dense subset of tori in its versal deformation are not exotic, meaning they are equivalent to product tori. In a general symplectic manifold
X
, we can ask if there is a pair of Lagrangians
L,L^{\prime}\subset X
which are
stably distinct
. By this we mean that
L\ncong L^{\prime}
and
L\sim L^{\prime}
such that their versal deformations
v_{L}\colon U\rightarrow{\mathcal{L}}
and
v_{L^{\prime}}\colon U^{\prime}\rightarrow{\mathcal{L}}
are distinct in the sense that
v_{L}(b)\ncong v_{L^{\prime}}(b^{\prime})
for all
b\in V,b^{\prime}\in V^{\prime}
, where
V\subset U,V^{\prime}\subset U^{\prime}
are neighbourhoods of the origin.

Remark 5.2
.

In
[
37
]
, Hind and Zhang have shown that there are stably exotic tori in balls, ellipsoids and in polydisks in
\mathbb{C}^{2}
, see
[
37
, Theorems 2.5-2.7]
. Note however that the authors conjecture that these tori lose their exoticity when considered in all of
\mathbb{C}^{2}
.

All exotic tori in compact ambient spaces known to the author are unstable. We heavily use the instability of the
\Upsilon_{k}
- and
\Theta_{{\mathfrak{m}}}
-tori to compute their displacement energy germs. Every exotic torus known to the author extends to a family of exotic tori of at least codimension one in its deformation space, see for example Remarks
1.7
and
4.12
and §
1.3
.

Question 5.3
.

Does every exotic torus come at least in a codimension one family of exotic tori?

Note that the Vianna tori (and their lifts) lie at the intersection of three codimension one families of exotic tori. This suggests a stratified structure of the set of exotic tori in the space of all tori.

Question 5.4
.

Is the displacement energy germ of every Lagrangian torus of the form (
43
)?

Again, this is the case in all examples known to the author. One way of answering Question
5.4
may be to link the displacement energy germ to an invariant coming from Maslov two
J
-holomorphic disks of minimal area as suggested in Remark
2.18
. Let
L\subset X
be a (not necessarily monotone) Lagrangian torus.

Question 5.5
.

Is there a symplectic invariant of
L
based on counting Maslov two
J
-holomorphic disks (of minimal area) which has the locality property? Can
{\mathcal{E}}_{L}
be recovered from this invariant?

In case
X
is monotone and (almost) toric, the displacement energy germ
{\mathcal{E}}_{L}
of the monotone fibre
L
is of the form (
43
) and it recovers the geometry of the toric base or of the ATF base diagram, respectively, in the sense that the base polytope is given by

\Delta_{{\mathcal{E}}_{L}}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\,|\,\langle x,v_{i}\rangle+a\geqslant 0\},

(111)

where
v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
and
a>0
are given by (
43
).

Question 5.6
.

Let
L\subset X
be a monotone Lagrangian torus with displacement energy germ
{\mathcal{E}}_{L}
given by (
43
). Is there a Lagrangian torus fibration with base
\Delta_{{\mathcal{E}}_{L}}
as defined in (
111
)?

Note that in that case
{\mathcal{E}}_{L}
is the germ of the function
d_{\rm IA}(\cdot,\partial\Delta_{{\mathcal{E}}_{L}})
. In that sense, one might expect a correspondence between the set of monotone Lagrangian tori in
X
and certain Lagrangian torus fibrations (up to a reasonable equivalence relation, e.g. nodal slides in the almost toric case) on
X
. In Remark
4.10
and §
5.3
we discuss some evidence for a positive answer to Question
5.6
in the case of the
\Theta_{{\mathfrak{m}}}
- and
\Upsilon_{k}
-tori constructed in this paper. In a second step, one can ask which singularities appear in the Lagrangian torus fibration and how these are related to properties of
L\subset X
.

5.2.
Dimension four

In dimension four there is, as of now, no analogue of the local exotic tori constructed in this paper. It is still open whether every Lagrangian torus
L\subset\mathbb{C}^{2}
is equivalent to either a product or a Chekanov torus, see
[
18
]
for evidence in that direction. In case this classification holds, there can be no such analogue due to a lack of exotic tori, even in
\mathbb{C}^{2}
. Furthermore, embeddings of the Chekanov torus by Darboux charts are not always exotic in a robust sense. The following example makes use of symmetric probes, see
[
8
]
, and will appear as an application of the classification of toric fibres in non-monotone
S^{2}\times S^{2}
in
[
11
]
.

Example 5.7
.

Let
\lambda>2
and let
X_{\lambda}
be the symplectic manifold obtained by equipping
S^{2}\times S^{2}
with the symplectic form
(\lambda\omega_{S^{2}})\oplus\omega_{S^{2}}
. Recall that
T_{\rm Ch}(a)\subset\mathbb{C}^{2}
is defined as the lift of a curve enclosing area
a>0
in the reduced space fibering over the diagonal in the toric moment map image of
\mathbb{C}^{2}
, see §
1.4
. Hence, whenever
a<r
, we find a copy
T_{\rm Ch}(a)\subset B^{4}(r)
. In
[
11
]
, it is shown that for every ball embedding
\varphi\colon B^{4}(r)\hookrightarrow X_{\lambda}
, the image
\varphi(T_{\rm Ch}(a))
is Hamiltonian isotopic to a
split torus
in
S^{2}\times S^{2}
, meaning a torus splitting as a product of circles. Thus Chekanov tori in non-monotone
S^{2}\times S^{2}
are not exotic. This stands in contrast with the results of this paper, see Corollary
2.20
. Furthermore, again in
[
11
]
, it is shown that for most (but not all)
a
, the embedded torus
\varphi(T_{\rm Ch}(a))
is Hamiltonian isotopic to
\varphi(T(a,b))
for some non-monotone product torus
T(a,b)\subset\mathbb{C}^{2}
. By choosing
a>0
small enough, one can arrange for
\varphi(T_{\rm Ch}(a))
to have property
(CS)
. Again, this example stands in contrast to the local exotic tori in dimension six and higher constructed in this paper, see Remark
1.20
.

However, in a recent work joint with Johannes Hauber and Joel Schmitz
[
10
]
, we treat a
semi-local
analogue of the ideas developed in this paper yielding tori which are exotic in a more robust way. Instead of embedding Lagrangian tori contained in symplectic balls, we treat Lagrangian tori contained in more complicated symplectic domains. For example, let
L\subset X^{4}
be a Lagrangian sphere in a symplectic four-manifold. Then a neighbourhood of
L
is symplectomorphic to a neighbourhood of the zero-section in
T^{*}S^{2}
which serves as our domain. Any neighbourhood of the zero-section in
T^{*}S^{2}
contains three types of tori, namely product, Chekanov and Polterovich tori. For a discussion of the Polterovich torus, we refer to
[
2
]
, see also
[
44
]
for a detailed discussion in higher dimensions. Under similar smallness assumptions on the tori as in Theorems
B
and
C
, see also Remark
1.16
, one can prove that these types of tori are not equivalent in
X^{4}
. More generally, we consider embeddings of certain Liouville domains
B_{d,p,q}
into
X^{4}
, yielding
d+1
distinct tori. For example
B_{2,1,1}\cong T^{*}S^{2}
and
B_{1,2,1}\cong T^{*}\mathbb{R}P^{2}
, see
[
24
]
for a discussion of the spaces
B_{d,p,q}
. These spaces appear as Milnor fibres of cyclic quotient singularities, or equivalently, neighbourhoods of
Lagrangian pinwheels
, see
[
25
]
for more details. The space
B_{d,p,q}
admits an almost toric fibration with
d
nodes lying on one branch cut line and the tori studied in
[
10
]
appear as regular fibres over the branch cut line of these ATFs. For a fixed area class
a>0
, the corresponding torus changes equivalence class whenever a node is moved across it. As in the present paper, in
[
10
]
we prove a locality property which implies that the tori remain distinct after embedding them into a geometrically bounded
X^{4}
.

The main difference with the purely local case of Darboux embeddings is that there are constraints from symplectic topology for symplectic embeddings of the type
B_{d,p,q}\supset U\rightarrow X^{4}
to exist. A full discussion of this can be found in
[
25
]
for the case
d=1
for
X=\mathbb{C}P^{2}
.

5.3.
Almost toric fibrations in higher dimensions

In the same vein as Question
5.6
, one can try to construct a Lagrangian torus fibration on
\mathbb{C}^{3}
having the
\Upsilon_{k}
-tori as fibres. Note that for the Chekanov torus
T_{\rm Ch}\subset\mathbb{C}^{2}
, this works and yields an almost toric fibration on
\mathbb{C}^{2}
. We suspect that this leads to a notion of almost toric fibrations in arbitrary dimension. Let us give a rough outline of this.

One can construct a Lagrangian torus fibration by hand, using a global version of the arguments we have used to deal with the versal deformation of the
\Upsilon_{k}
. As projection map, consider

\pi_{k,G}\colon\mathbb{C}^{3}\rightarrow\Delta_{k}\times\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0},\quad\pi_{k,G}(z)=(\nu_{k}(z),\pi_{k,\nu_{k}(z)}^{*}G_{k,\nu_{k}(z)}(z)).

(112)

Since
\nu_{k}
generates the
T_{k}
-action considered in §
4.1
, we can interpret
\pi_{k,G}
as a
T_{k}
-equivariant perturbation of the toric system on
\mathbb{C}^{3}
. Indeed, the last component of
\pi_{k,G}
is a lift of a Hamiltonian
G_{k,c}\colon\Sigma_{k,c}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
from the reduced space
\Sigma_{k,c}
of the
T_{k}
-action. Note that if we lifted
H_{k,c}
instead, then we would end up with the standard toric system on
\mathbb{C}^{3}
, since the fibres of
\pi_{k,H}
are product tori by Proposition
4.1
. However, by lifting perturbations of
H_{k,c}
having the circles
C(\cdot)\subset\Sigma_{k,c}
appearing in the definition of the
\Upsilon_{k}
-tori as level sets, we end up with an Lagrangian torus fibration having
\Upsilon_{k}
-tori as fibres. A good candidate for a family
\{G_{k,c}\}_{c\in\Delta_{k}}
of functions to define
\pi_{k,G}
seems to be: start with
H_{k,c}
and perturb it on a small neighbourhood of the ray
\{(c_{1},0)\}\subset\Delta_{k}
. Just as a similar
S^{1}
-equivariant perturbation of the toric system on
\mathbb{C}^{2}
produces an almost toric nodal trade at the vertex of
\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0}^{2}
, this
T_{k}
-equivariant procedure produces an analoguous
nodal trade at the edge

\{x_{2}=x_{3}=0\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}_{\geqslant 0}
. Singular fibres appear where the level sets of
G_{k,(c_{1},0)}
intersect the punctures of
\Sigma_{k,(c_{1},0)}
and they are of focus-focus-regular type, since locally the nodal trade at the vertex is equivalent to starting with the standard toric system on
\mathbb{C}^{2}\times T^{*}S^{1}
and performing a nodal trade in the
\mathbb{C}^{2}
-component.
Nodal slides correspond, in this picture, to varying the level of
G_{k,(c_{1},0)}
at which the puncture is intersected.
The corresponding ATF base diagrams are computed by removing two-dimensional branch sets and computing action coordinates on their complement.

In
\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0}^{3}
, there are three edges at which one can perform nodal trades and for each of those, one can choose
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}
. Therefore, for each triple
(k_{1},k_{2},k_{3})\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 2}^{3}
, we obtain a series of ATFs and mutations of
\mathbb{C}^{3}
. We call
(k_{1},k_{2},k_{3})
the
nodal trade vector
. Note that for the nodal trade vector
(k_{1},k_{2},k_{3})=(2,2,2)
, the
T^{2}
-actions at chosen at each edge intersect in a common circle, namely the diagonal circle
S^{1}\subset T^{3}
. Therefore, the corresponding mutation in
\mathbb{C}^{3}
are compatible with symplectic reduction by the diagonal circle and project to mutations of
\mathbb{C}P^{2}
. This reproduces the Markov mutations in
\mathbb{C}P^{2}
, see §
3.2
. In other words, our construction for
k_{i}=2
reproduces the cone construction discussed in Remark
4.10
and thus fibres of the higher mutations correspond, in this case, to the tori
\Theta_{{\mathfrak{m}}}
. On the other hand, simple mutations at only one edge correspond to the tori
\Upsilon_{k}
as we have seen. Therefore these conjectural ATF-mutations in higher dimensions have fibres which generalize both the
\Upsilon_{k}
- and
\Theta_{{\mathfrak{m}}}
-tori, with
\Theta_{{\mathfrak{m}}}
-tori appearing as fibres of ATFs obtained by
higher mutations for the fixed nodal trade vector

(k_{1},k_{2},k_{3})=(2,2,2)
and
\Upsilon_{k}
-tori appearing as fibres of ATFs obtained by
first order mutations for the nodal trade vectors

(k_{1},k_{2},k_{3})=(k,\varnothing,\varnothing)
, where
\varnothing
stands for not making a nodal trade. For every
(k_{1},k_{2},k_{3})\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 2}^{3}
, we obtain a Markov-like tree of Lagrangian tori. It would be highly interesting to give a proper construction of these families tori and distinguish them. As in the four-dimensional analogue in §
3.3
, this should be possible by the use of versal deformations. In particular, it should be possible to find out how the displacement energy germ behaves under mutations and that it can be read off from the base of the corresponding ATF.

Remark 5.8
.

Note that this could lead to a theory of ATFs for compact toric manifolds of arbitrary dimension. Indeed, every compact toric manifold
X
is, via the Delzant construction, see
[
34
]
, an equivariant symplectic quotient of some
\mathbb{C}^{N}
by a subtorus
K\subset T^{N}
acting on
\mathbb{C}^{N}
. We can restrict our attention to nodal trades at codimension
(k\geqslant 2)
-faces of
\mathbb{R}^{N}_{\geqslant 0}
such that their corresponding tori contain
K
. Just as the
(2,2,2)
-ATFs on
\mathbb{C}^{3}
induce ATFs on
\mathbb{C}P^{2}
, this general recipe should induce ATFs on
X
.

Remark 5.9
.

Note that the Lagrangian torus fibrations in (
112
) are perturbations of the toric moment map which are somewhat
mild
in the sense that, locally, a
T^{2}
-action is preserved. What happens if one decides to only preserve an
S^{1}\subset T^{3}
? This line of inquiry should be started by analzing what perturbations of the toric system on
\mathbb{C}^{2}
make sense in this context, since the reduced spaces are two dimensional in this case.
