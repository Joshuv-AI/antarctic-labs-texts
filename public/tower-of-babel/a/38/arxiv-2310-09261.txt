Global Positioning: the Uniqueness Question and a New Solution Method
Mireille Boutin, Gregor Kemper

Abstract
. We provide a new algebraic solution procedure for the global positioning problem in n n dimensions using m m satellites. We also give a geometric characterization of the situations in which the problem does not have a unique solution. This characterization shows that such cases can happen in any dimension and with any number of satellites, leading to counterexamples to some open conjectures. We fill a gap in the literature by giving a proof for the long-held belief that when m ≥ n + 2 m\geq n+2 , the solution is unique for almost all user positions. Even better, when m ≥ 2 ​ n + 2 m\geq 2n+2 , almost all satellite configurations will guarantee a unique solution for all user positions. Some of our results are obtained using tools from algebraic geometry.

Introduction

Positioning systems play an important role in many applications. A commonly-used positioning system is the GPS (originally Navstar GPS), a satellite-based system owned by the United States, which allows users to determine their location. It essentially works as follows. The satellites are equipped with a precise clock and know their position at all times. They broadcast this information (position and time) continuously using radio waves. A user equipped with a radio receiver is assumed to receive the data from at least four satellites. Using the information received, the user computes their own position in 3D. The clocks of the satellites are assumed to be precisely synchronized together, but not with the clock of the receiver. So the difference between the time-of-arrival on the local clock of the receiver
and the time of emission as recorded by the clock on a satellite is equal to the time taken by the signal to travel from the satellite to the receiver (time-of-flight) plus an unknown offset which is the same for all satellites. Since radio waves travel at a known constant speed (the speed of light), the time-of-flight determines the distance between the satellite position and the user position (the range). Therefore the receiver obtains the
pseudo-range
values, which are the values of the ranges shifted by the unknown offset.

In general, the global positioning problem consists in determining the location of a receiver given the location of several sources and the distance to each source, up to an unknown offset. This is equivalent to determining the position of the source of a signal emitted at an unknown time and received by several receivers at known positions equipped with a synchronized clock. For example, one may be interested in determining the location of a gunshot heard by several microphones at known locations. There are many other scenarios in which this problem arises. For example, the bio-acoustic community is interested in using microphones to track the path of animals in nature in a non-intrusive way [
?
rhinehart2020acoustic]. In particular, recent work used this technique to identify patterns in the flight of some male birds [
?
dutilleux2023chasing]. The same principles are also used in a newly proposed muon-based navigation system for indoor and underground environments [
?
tanaka2023first].

The solution method

Consider the following situation: A source at an unknown
position
\mathbf{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
emits a signal at an unknown time
t
.
There are
m
sensors at known positions

\mathbf{a}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{a}_{m}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. They receive the signal at
times
t_{1},\ldots,t_{m}
. We choose the unit of time such that the signal
propagation speed becomes
1
. So we have the fundamental equations

(1.1)

\lVert\mathbf{a}_{i}-\mathbf{x}\rVert=t_{i}-t\qquad(i=1,\ldots,m)

The task is to work out the position
\mathbf{x}
and the emission
time
t
. The very same equations arise if there are
m
sources
(e.g. satellites) at known positions
\mathbf{a}_{i}
emitting signals at
known times, which are then received by a device at an unknown
position
\mathbf{x}
(referred to as the user position). In
this case the
t_{i}
are the differences between the reception times

according to the clock on the device
and the emission times
according to the (near-perfect) clocks on the sources, and
t
is the
unknown bias between the clock on the receiver and the clocks on the
sources. This is the
global positioning problem
. Since among the problems that involve solving (
1.1
) for the unknowns
\mathbf{x}
and
t
, the global positioning problem is the most familiar one, we will from now on think of the
\mathbf{a}_{i}
as the
satellite positions
, and of
\mathbf{x}
as the
user position
.

In the following derivation we will use the equations

(1.2)

\lVert\mathbf{a}_{i}-\mathbf{x}\rVert=|t_{i}-t|,\qquad(i=1,\ldots,m),\\

which become equivalent to (
1.1
) when combined with the
inequalities

(1.3)

t_{i}\geq t,\qquad(i=1,\ldots,m).

Writing
L:=\operatorname{diag}(-1,1,\ldots,1)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{(n+1)\times(n+1)}
,

\widetilde{\mathbf{a}_{i}}:=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}t_{i}\\
\mathbf{a}_{i}\end{smallmatrix}\right)
and

\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}:=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}t\\
\mathbf{x}\end{smallmatrix}\right)
, we have

\lVert\mathbf{a}_{i}-\mathbf{x}\rVert^{2}-(t_{i}-t)^{2}=(\widetilde{\mathbf{a}_{i}}-\widetilde{\mathbf{x}})^{T}\cdot L\cdot(\widetilde{\mathbf{a}_{i}}-\widetilde{\mathbf{x}})=\widetilde{\mathbf{a}_{i}}^{T}L\widetilde{\mathbf{a}_{i}}-2\widetilde{\mathbf{a}_{i}}^{T}L\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}+\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}^{T}L\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}=\\
\lVert\mathbf{a}_{i}\rVert^{2}-t_{i}^{2}+2t_{i}t-2\mathbf{a}_{i}^{T}\mathbf{x}+\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert^{2}-t^{2},

so (
1.2
) is equivalent to

(1.4)

-2t_{i}t+2\mathbf{a}_{i}^{T}\mathbf{x}-\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert^{2}+t^{2}=\lVert\mathbf{a}_{i}\rVert^{2}-t_{i}^{2}\qquad(i=1,\ldots,m).

We form the matrix

(1.5)

A:=\begin{pmatrix}-2t_{1}&2\mathbf{a}_{1}^{T}&-1\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
-2t_{m}&2\mathbf{a}_{m}^{T}&-1\end{pmatrix}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times(n+2)},

which contains only known quantities. With this, (
1.4
) can
be expressed as a system of linear equations for the unknown
quantities:

(1.6)

A\cdot\begin{pmatrix}t\\
\mathbf{x}\\
\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert^{2}-t^{2}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\lVert\mathbf{a}_{1}\rVert^{2}-t_{1}^{2}\\
\vdots\\
\lVert\mathbf{a}_{m}\rVert^{2}-t_{m}^{2}\end{pmatrix}.

Now we make the assumption that
A
has rank
n+2
,
so (
1.6
) has a unique solution. (The existence of at least
one solution follows from the fact that the point
\mathbf{x}
and number
t
exist.) In the case
m=n+2
we can then simply
invert
A
. More generally, for
m\geq n+2
, we use
the Moore-Penrose inverse

A^{+}=(A^{T}A)^{-1}A^{T}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{(n+2)\times m}
, which is a left
inverse of
A
. So multiplying both sides of (
1.6
) with

A^{+}
and then with the matrix that deletes the last entry of the
vector
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1\\
\mathbf{x}\\
\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert^{2}-t^{2}\end{smallmatrix}\right)
yields

(1.7)

\begin{pmatrix}t\\
\mathbf{x}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1&\cdots&0&0\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&\cdots&1&0\end{pmatrix}\cdot A^{+}\cdot\begin{pmatrix}\lVert\mathbf{a}_{1}\rVert^{2}-t_{1}^{2}\\
\vdots\\
\lVert\mathbf{a}_{m}\rVert^{2}-t_{m}^{2}\end{pmatrix}.

So we have obtained a unique solution of (
1.2
). This works if

A
has rank
n+2
. In
Section

5
we will show that this is “very
likely” if
m\geq n+2
.

Now we consider the case that
A
has rank
\leq n+1
, which is certain
to occur if
m\leq n+1
, with the aim of solving (
1.2
) in that case as well. We assume that the
\mathbf{a}_{i}
do not lie on a common affine
hyperplane. (So for

n=3
this means that they are not coplanar, for
n=2
that they
are not collinear, and for
n=1
that they are not all equal.) This
assumption makes sense, since if the
\mathbf{a}_{i}
all lay in the same
hyperplane, then (
1.1
) would almost never have a unique
solution (see
Remark

1.5
(a)
below).
This assumption amounts to saying that the matrix

(1.8)

B:=\begin{pmatrix}2\mathbf{a}_{1}^{T}&-1\\
\vdots&\vdots\\
2\mathbf{a}_{m}^{T}&-1\end{pmatrix}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times(n+1)}

has rank
n+1
, so in particular we need
m\geq n+1
. Since
B
is a submatrix of

A
, we obtain
\operatorname{rank}(A)=n+1
. Now (
1.4
) can be restated
as

(1.9)

B\cdot\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{x}\\
\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert^{2}-t^{2}\end{pmatrix}=2t\begin{pmatrix}t_{1}\\
\vdots\\
t_{m}\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}\lVert\mathbf{a}_{1}\rVert^{2}-t_{1}^{2}\\
\vdots\\
\lVert\mathbf{a}_{m}\rVert^{2}-t_{m}^{2}\end{pmatrix},

and multiplying by the Moore-Penrose inverse

B^{+}=(B^{T}B)^{-1}B^{T}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{(n+1)\times m}
yields

(1.10)

\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{x}\\
\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert^{2}-t^{2}\end{pmatrix}=t\cdot\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{u}\\
2\alpha\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{v}\\
\beta\end{pmatrix},

where

(1.11)

\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{u}\\
2\alpha\end{pmatrix}:=2B^{+}\begin{pmatrix}t_{1}\\
\vdots\\
t_{m}\end{pmatrix}\ \text{and}\ \begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{v}\\
\beta\end{pmatrix}:=B^{+}\begin{pmatrix}\lVert\mathbf{a}_{1}\rVert^{2}-t_{1}^{2}\\
\vdots\\
\lVert\mathbf{a}_{m}\rVert^{2}-t_{m}^{2}\end{pmatrix}.

Extracting components from (
1.10
), we obtain the equations

(1.12)

\mathbf{x}=t\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}\quad\text{and}\quad\bigl(\lVert\mathbf{u}\rVert^{2}-1\bigr)t^{2}+2\bigl(\mathbf{u}^{T}\mathbf{v}-\alpha\bigr)t+\lVert\mathbf{v}\rVert^{2}-\beta=0.

Observe that
\mathbf{u}
,
\mathbf{v}
,
\alpha
, and
\beta
are all derived
from known quantities, so (
1.12
) can be resolved. This is how we can solve our problem in the case
\operatorname{rank}(A)=n+1
. Since we derived (
1.12
) from the
equations (
1.2
), some of its solutions may not satisfy
the original equations (
1.1
). But we can easily test the
inequalities (
1.3
) for any solution of (
1.12
), so
there is hope that we can whittle down multiple solutions in this way
to just one. So we arrive at the following solution procedure, whose correctness will be formally proved in
Theorem

1.3
.

Procedure 1.1

(Solution of the global positioning problem)
.

Input:

Points
\mathbf{a}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{a}_{m}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, assumed not to lie on a common affine hyperplane, and
t_{1},\ldots,t_{m}\in{\mathbb{R}}
. It is also assumed that (
1.1
) is solvable.

Output:

The set of solutions
(\mathbf{x},t)
of (
1.1
).

(1)

Compute the matrix
A
according to (
1.5
) and determine its rank.

(2)

If
\operatorname{rank}(A)=n+2
then compute
A^{+}:=(A^{T}A)^{-1}A^{T}
and determine
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}t\\
\mathbf{x}\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}
according to (
1.7
). Output
(\mathbf{x},t)
as the unique solution of (
1.1
). The solution procedure finishes here.

(3)

If, on the other hand,
\operatorname{rank}(A)\leq n+1
then compute the matrix
B
according to (
1.8
), and work out the vectors
\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}
and the scalars
\alpha
and
\beta
by using (
1.11
).

(4)

For every
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
satisfying the equation

\bigl(\lVert\mathbf{u}\rVert^{2}-1\bigr)t^{2}+2\bigl(\mathbf{u}^{T}\mathbf{v}-\alpha\bigr)t+\lVert\mathbf{v}\rVert^{2}-\beta=0,

perform step
(4)(5)
.

(5)

If
t\leq t_{i}
for all
i=1,\ldots,m
then compute
\mathbf{v}:=t\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}
and output
(\mathbf{x},t)
as a solution of (
1.1
).

(6)

The solution procedure finishes here, having output
1
or
2
solutions.

Notice that our solution procedure was directly derived from the equations (
1.1
) that we want to solve. This means that every solution of (
1.1
) will be found by
1.1
. However, the converse is another matter. Is it also true that every pair
(\mathbf{x},t)
spit out by the procedure is really a solution of (
1.1
)? On a finer scale, is it true that in the case
\operatorname{rank}(A)=n+1
, unique
solvability of the quadratic equation in the procedure is equivalent to unique solvability
of (

)? These questions will be answered in the affirmative by

1.3
.

2.
Interlude: quadrics with a focus

Our next goal is to provide a geometric characterization of
when our fundamental equations (
1.1
) have a unique solution. For this purpose, we first need to deal with some facts related to quadrics. Although this is just elementary
geometry, we were unable to find a suitable reference, except for dimension three (see
?
zwillinger2018crc [
?
zwillinger2018crc]). We start by recalling the focus-directrix property.

A point set
Q\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, with
n\geq 2
, is said to satisfy
the
focus-directrix property
if it is given as

(2.1)

Q=\bigl\{P\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\ \bigr|\ \lVert P-F\rVert=e\cdot d(H,P)\bigr\},

where
F\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is a point (the
focus
),

H\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is an affine hyperplane (the
directrix
),

e\in{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}
is a number (the
eccentricity
), and

d(H,P)
denotes the distance between
H
and
P
. We make the
additional hypothesis that
Q
contains non-collinear
points. Extending the concept, we also regard the
(n-1)
-sphere with
any positive radius around
F
as satisfying the focus-directrix
property with
e=0
, thinking of a directrix at infinity. (For
shortness, we will from now on just say
sphere
for the

(n-1)
-sphere.)

If
e>0
, it is convenient to choose a normal vector
\mathbf{u}
on
H

with length
\lVert\mathbf{u}\rVert=e
. Then
H
is given as

H=\bigl\{P\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\mid\langle\mathbf{u},P\rangle=\alpha\bigr\}

for some
\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}
. With this, we obtain

(2.2)

Q=\bigl\{P\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\ \bigr|\ \lVert P-F\rVert=|\langle\mathbf{u},P\rangle-\alpha|\bigr\}.

It is helpful that for
\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{0}
this defines the sphere with
radius
|\alpha|
around
F
, so by including the possibility

\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{0}
we need not consider the sphere as a separate case any
longer.
Q
splits into two subsets
Q_{+}
and
Q_{-}
with

(2.3)

Q_{\pm}:=\bigl\{P\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\ \bigr|\ \lVert P-F\rVert=\pm\bigl(\langle\mathbf{u},P\rangle-\alpha\bigr)\bigr\},

the intersections of
Q
with the two closed half-spaces defined by
the hyperplane
H
. However,
Q_{+}
and
Q_{-}
can only be both nonempty
if
Q
is disconnected or if
F\in Q
. Each of
Q_{+}
and
Q_{-}
, if
nonempty, is called a
sheet
of
Q
. If
F\in Q
it is contained
in both sheets. There is nothing intrinsically positive or negative
about the sheets, since they can be swapped by substituting
\mathbf{u}

by
-\mathbf{u}
and
\alpha
by
-\alpha
. Comparing the definition of

Q_{+}
with (
1.15
) already shows how intimately the
focus-directrix property is connected to the topic of this paper.

It is also convenient to choose a unit vector

\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
with
\mathbf{u}=e\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}
, so

\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}=e^{-1}\mathbf{u}
if
e>0
, and
\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}

arbitrary if
e=0
. Apart from the eccentricity,
Q
has another
parameter, which is obtained by intersecting
Q
with the affine plane
through
F
with normal vector
\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}
. Using (
2.2
), we
find the intersection to be

\bigl\{F+\mathbf{v}\ \bigr|\ \mathbf{v}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n},\langle\widetilde{\mathbf{u}},\mathbf{v}\rangle=0,\lVert\mathbf{v}\rVert=|\langle\mathbf{u},F\rangle-\alpha|\bigr\},

so it is an
(n-2)
-sphere around
F
with radius
|l|
, where we have
set

(2.4)

l:=\langle\mathbf{u},F\rangle-\alpha.

The radius
|l|
is called the
semilatus rectum
, and is the other
numerical parameter of
Q
, complementing the
eccentricity.

instances of the focus-directrix property that occur in dimension two.

What do point sets

Setting

, the defining equation (

) for

,

This shows that

is a quadric. From now on we will speak of a

To find out which types of quadrics have a focus, write

for the part of

that is perpendicular to

. With

(1-e^{2})\langle\widetilde{\mathbf{u}},P^{\prime}\rangle^{2}+\lVert P^{\prime}_{\perp}\rVert^{2}-2el\langle\widetilde{\mathbf{u}},P^{\prime}\rangle-l^{2}=0.

First assume

, so

Since

, multiplying (

) by

,

(2.7)

Extending

by vectors

Before we go on, let us consider the remaining case

. Setting

P^{\prime\prime}:=\operatorname{sgn}(l)\cdot\bigl(P^{\prime}+\frac{l}{2}\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}\bigr)

gives

, so (

(2.9)

\lVert P^{\prime\prime}_{\perp}\rVert^{2}-2|l|\cdot\langle\widetilde{\mathbf{u}},P^{\prime\prime}\rangle=0,\quad\text{or}\quad-2|l|z+\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}x_{i}^{2}=0,

as the

,

Case 2:

\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}u_{i}^{2}-1\right)\alpha^{2}+2\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}u_{i}h_{i}(u_{1},\ldots,u_{n})\right)\alpha+\sum_{i=1}^{n}h_{i}(u_{1},\ldots,u_{n})^{2}-h(u_{1},\ldots,u_{n})=0.

We can write this as

g_{1}(u_{1},\ldots,u_{n})\alpha^{2}+g_{2}(u_{1},\ldots,u_{n})\alpha+g_{3}(u_{1},\ldots,u_{n})=0

with

, and specifically

g_{1}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}U_{i}^{2}-1.

Let us summarize our findings so far. For every

\mathbf{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
that makes at least one of the determinants
in (

) vanish, there exist
\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}
and

\mathbf{u}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
satisfying (
5.4
), (
5.5
),
and (
5.6
). The polynomials

,
and

occurring in these equations only
depend on the given points
\mathbf{a}_{i}
and not on
\mathbf{x}
.

We now take an additional indeterminate

and consider the ideal

I:=\bigl(g,g_{1}V^{2}+g_{2}V+g3\bigr)\subseteq{\mathbb{C}}[U_{1},\ldots,U_{n},V]

and the affine variety
X\subseteq{\mathbb{C}}^{n+1}
given by

. With

Y\subseteq{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
given by the polynomial
g
, the projection to
the first
n

\pi\mbox{:}\ X\rightarrow Y
. For a

is isomorphic to the

with

. Let

be a closed, irreducible subset. Then the Zariski closure

of the image is also
irreducible. We distinguish two cases.

Case 1:

there exists

such that
g_{1}(y)\neq 0
. Then the fiber
\pi^{-1}(\{y\})
is finite and therefore

0
-dimensional. By Chevalley’s theorem on fiber dimension (see

?
Kemper.Comalg [
?
Kemper.Comalg, Corollary 10.6] or
?
hart [
?
hart, Chapter II,
Exercise 3.22(b)]), we obtain

0\geq\dim(X^{\prime})-\dim(Y^{\prime}).

But
\dim(Y^{\prime})\leq\dim(Y)=n-1

, so

\dim(X^{\prime})\leq n-1
.

Case 2:

g_{1}(y)=0
for every

. Then

vanishes on
\pi(X^{\prime})
and therefore also on the closure
Y^{\prime}
,
which means that both
g
and
g_{1}
lie in the vanishing ideal of

. Now by looking at its definition (
5.7
), we see that

g_{1}\in{\mathbb{C}}[U_{1},\ldots,U_{n}]
is irreducible (since
n\geq 2
). Moreover (
5.5
) implies that
g_{1}
does not divide
g

(again since
n\geq 2
). So
(g_{1})\subset{\mathbb{C}}[U_{1},\ldots,U_{n}]
is
a nonzero prime ideal strictly contained in
(g_{1},g)
, and it
follows that the Krull dimension of the quotient ring

{\mathbb{C}}[U_{1},\ldots,U_{n}]/(g_{1},g)
is at most
n-2
. Therefore

\dim(Y^{\prime})\leq n-2.

Take

then the fiber
\pi^{-1}(\{y\})
has
dimension
0
or
1
(the latter only occurs if

g_{2}(y)=g_{3}(y)=0
, but this cannot be ruled out). Using
Chevalley’s theorem again, we now obtain

\dim(X^{\prime})\leq\dim(Y^{\prime})+1\leq n-1
.

Since in both cases
\dim(X^{\prime})\leq n-1
, we conclude that also
X

has dimension
\leq n-1
, and therefore the quotient ring

{\mathbb{C}}[U_{1},\ldots,U_{n},V]/I
has Krull dimension
\leq n-1

?
Kemper.Comalg, Theorem 5.9]
or [
?
hart, Chapter I, Theorem1.8A]), so
n

from this algebra are always algebraically dependent. In particular,
this applies to the classes modulo

of the polynomials

-h_{i}-VU_{i}
(

). So there exists a nonzero
polynomial
f\in{\mathbb{C}}[T_{1},\ldots,T_{n}]
(with

new

f\bigl(-h_{1}-VU_{1},\ldots,-h_{n}-VU_{n}\bigr)\in I
, so we can
write

(5.8)

f\bigl(-h_{1}-VU_{1},\ldots,-h_{n}-VU_{n}\bigr)=f_{1}\cdot g+f_{2}\cdot\bigl(g_{1}V^{2}+g_{2}V+g_{3}\bigr)

with

. We claim that

for every point

that makes at least

) vanish. Indeed, as we have
seen, for such an

and
\mathbf{u}=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{n})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}

f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\underset{\eqref{eqXU}}{=}f\bigl(-h_{1}(\mathbf{u})-\alpha u_{1},\ldots,-h_{n}(\mathbf{u})-\alpha u_{n}\bigr)\underset{\eqref{eqFHVU}}{=}\\
f_{1}(\mathbf{u},\alpha)\cdot g(\mathbf{u})+f_{2}(\mathbf{u},\alpha)\cdot\bigl(g_{1}(\mathbf{u})\alpha^{2}+g_{2}(\mathbf{u})\alpha+g_{3}(\mathbf{u})\bigr)\underset{\eqref{eqG},\eqref{eqGUA}}{=}0.

With this

is almost proved. The one thing that is missing

has complex coefficients, whereas the

and

∎

The following lemma was used in the proof.

Lemma 5.3
.

In the situation of
5.2
, let
t_{1},\ldots,t_{k}
, with

k=\binom{n+2}{2}
, be all the monomials in variables
y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}

of degree
\leq 2
. Then the matrix

M(\mathbf{a}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{a}_{n+2}):=\Bigl(t_{j}(\mathbf{a}_{i})\Bigr)_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1,\ldots,n+2\\
j=1,\ldots,k\end{subarray}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{(n+2)\times k}

has rank
n+2
.

Proof.

By hypothesis the
\mathbf{a}_{i}
do not lie on an affine hyperplane,
i.e., the matrix

\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\mathbf{a}_{1}&\cdots&\mathbf{a}_{n+2}\\
1&\cdots&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{(n+1)\times(n+2)}

has rank
n+1
. By renumbering the
\mathbf{a}_{i}
we may assume that the
first
n+1
columns are linearly independent, which means that

\mathbf{a}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{a}_{n+1}
do not lie on an affine
hyperplane. Write
\mathbf{a}_{n+2}=(c_{1},\ldots,c_{n})^{T}
, and for a
monomial
t=y_{1}^{e_{1}}\cdots y_{n}^{e_{n}}
write

\widetilde{t}:=\prod_{i=1}^{n}(y_{i}-c_{i})^{e_{i}}
. This is equal to
t

plus a linear combination of lower-degree monomials, so we have a
matrix
C\in{\mathbb{R}}^{k\times k}
such that

\bigl(\widetilde{t}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{t}_{k}\bigr)=\bigl(t_{1},\ldots,t_{k})\cdot C.

If we order the
t_{i}
by degrees,
C
is a triangular matrix with

1
’s on the diagonal, so
C\in\operatorname{GL}_{k}({\mathbb{R}})
. By setting

\widetilde{\mathbf{a}}_{i}:=\mathbf{a}_{i}-\mathbf{a}_{n+2}
we obtain

\widetilde{t}_{j}(\mathbf{a}_{i})=t_{j}(\widetilde{\mathbf{a}}_{i})
, so

M(\widetilde{\mathbf{a}}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{\mathbf{a}}_{n+2})=M(\mathbf{a}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{a}_{n+2})\cdot C
and therefore

\operatorname{rank}\bigl(M(\mathbf{a}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{a}_{n+2})\bigr)=\operatorname{rank}\bigl(M(\widetilde{\mathbf{a}}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{\mathbf{a}}_{n+2})\bigr).

Since
\widetilde{\mathbf{a}}_{n+2}=\mathbf{0}
and
t_{1}=1
we have

M(\widetilde{\mathbf{a}}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{\mathbf{a}}_{n+2})=\left(\begin{array}[]{c|ccc}1&&&\\
\vdots&&M^{+}(\widetilde{\mathbf{a}}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{\mathbf{a}}_{n+1})&\\
1&&&\\
\hline\cr 1&0&\cdots&0\end{array}\right),

where

M^{+}(\widetilde{\mathbf{a}}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{\mathbf{a}}_{n+1}):=\Bigl(t_{j}(\widetilde{\mathbf{a}}_{i})\Bigr)_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1,\ldots,n+1\\
j=2,\ldots,k\end{subarray}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{(n+1)\times(k-1)}
is formed by evaluating
the monomials of positive degree. So we need to show that

\operatorname{rank}\bigl(M^{+}(\widetilde{\mathbf{a}}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{\mathbf{a}}_{n+1})\bigr)=n+1
. Observe that
\widetilde{\mathbf{a}}_{i}\neq\mathbf{0}
(
1\leq i\leq n+1
)
since the
\mathbf{a}_{i}
are pairwise distinct by hypothesis, and that

\widetilde{\mathbf{a}}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{\mathbf{a}}_{n+1}
do not lie on an affine
hyperplane. Therefore the following claim implies the desired
rank-equation.

Claim
.

Let
\mathbf{b}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{b}_{m}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}
be
affinely independent (i.e., the matrix

\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\mathbf{b}_{1}&\cdots&\mathbf{b}_{m}\\
1&\cdots&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{(n+1)\times m}

has rank
m
). Then

M^{+}(\mathbf{b}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{b}_{m})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times(\binom{n+2}{2}-1)}

also has rank
m
.

We prove the claim by induction on
n
. Let
d_{1},\ldots,d_{m}
be the
first coordinates of the vectors
\mathbf{b}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{b}_{m}
, and
renumber the
\mathbf{b}_{i}
such that
d_{i}\neq 0
for
1\leq i\leq r
and

d_{r+1}=\cdots=d_{m}=0
. We have
0\leq r\leq m
, and
r=m

if
n=1
. At this point, it is helpful to reorder the monomials

t_{2},\ldots,t_{k}
(which are precisely the monomials of degree
1

or
2
) such that the monomials

y_{1},y_{1}^{2},y_{1}y_{2},y_{1}y_{3},\ldots,y_{1}y_{n}
come first, and are
followed by the monomials of degree
1
or
2
in the
variables
y_{2},\ldots,y_{n}
. Then

(5.9)

M^{+}(\mathbf{b}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{b}_{m})=\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c}D&\Asterisk\\
\hline\cr 0&E\end{array}\right),

where

D=\begin{pmatrix}d_{1}\\
&\ddots\\
&&d_{r}\end{pmatrix}\cdot\begin{pmatrix}1&\mathbf{b}_{1}^{T}\\
\vdots&\vdots\\
1&\mathbf{b}_{r}^{T}\end{pmatrix}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{r\times(n+1)},

and where
E
is formed by evaluating all monomials of degree
1

or
2
in the variables
y_{2},\ldots,y_{n}
at

\mathbf{b}_{r+1},\ldots,\mathbf{b}_{m}
. It follows from the affine independence
of
\mathbf{b}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{b}_{r}
that
\operatorname{rank}(D)=r
, so the rows of
D

are linearly independent. If

, which is guaranteed in the

, this finishes the proof. On the other hand, if

, then with
\pi\mbox{:}\ {\mathbb{R}}^{n}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{(n-1)}
the projection on

Since
\mathbf{b}_{r+1},\ldots,\mathbf{b}_{m}
are affinely independent and their

are also affinely

\pi(\mathbf{b}_{i})
are also nonzero for

\mathbf{b}_{i}\neq\mathbf{0}
. So by induction we get
\operatorname{rank}(E)=m-r
, so
the rows of

are linearly independent. Now from (

is as claimed.

Remark 5.4
.

The proof of
5.2
is not constructive. But, intriguingly, the
proposition gives rise to its own constructive version, and with
that to a constructive version of
Theorem

5.1
. In fact, we claim that

(5.10)

\widetilde{f}(\mathbf{x}):=\prod_{\varepsilon_{2},\ldots,\varepsilon_{m}\in\{\pm 1\}}\det\begin{pmatrix}\lVert\mathbf{a}_{1}-\mathbf{x}\rVert&\mathbf{a}_{1}^{T}&1\\
\varepsilon_{2}\cdot\lVert\mathbf{a}_{2}-\mathbf{x}\rVert&\mathbf{a}_{2}^{T}&1\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
\varepsilon_{m}\cdot\lVert\mathbf{a}_{m}-\mathbf{x}\rVert&\mathbf{a}_{m}^{T}&1\end{pmatrix}

(with
m:=n+2
) is a polynomial in the coordinates
x_{i}

of
\mathbf{x}
, and can be taken as the polynomial
f
whose existence
is asserted in
5.2
. (One can also include an
\varepsilon_{1}

as a sign before
\lVert\mathbf{a}_{1}-\mathbf{x}\rVert
in the
multiplication, but that makes
\widetilde{f}
unnecessarily large.)

In fact, let
d(y_{1},\ldots,y_{m})
be the
determinant of a given
m\times m
matrix with indeterminates

y_{1},\ldots,y_{m}
in the first column. Then

g:=\prod_{\varepsilon_{2},\ldots,\varepsilon_{m}\in\{\pm 1\}}d(y_{1},\varepsilon_{2}y_{2},\ldots,\varepsilon_{m}y_{m})
is invariant under
the automorphisms sending
y_{i}
to
-y_{i}
(
i=2,\ldots,m
). But
also

g(-y_{1},y_{2},\ldots,y_{m})=\prod_{\varepsilon_{2},\ldots,\varepsilon_{m}\in\{\pm 1\}}d(-y_{1},-\varepsilon_{2}y_{2},\ldots,-\varepsilon_{m}y_{m})=\\
\prod_{\varepsilon_{2},\ldots,\varepsilon_{m}\in\{\pm 1\}}\bigl(-d(y_{1},\varepsilon_{2}y_{2},\ldots,\varepsilon_{m}y_{m})\bigr)=g(y_{1},y_{2},\ldots,y_{m}).

So only the squares of the

occur in

. Hence by specializing
the

to
\lVert\mathbf{a}_{i}-\mathbf{x}\rVert
, we see
that
\widetilde{f}(\mathbf{x})
is indeed a polynomial in the

. Now

5.2
tells us that there exists an
\mathbf{x}
for which none of
the determinants in (

) vanishes. This implies

\widetilde{f}\neq 0
. Moreover, if
\widetilde{f}(\mathbf{x})\neq 0
, then the
determinant in (
5.1
) does not vanish
for

, and by the above calculation also
for
\varepsilon_{1}=-1
.

\triangleleft

6.
Uniqueness for all positions
\mathbf{x}

In this section, as in the previous one, we consider given
positions
\mathbf{a}_{i}
of satellites. The question then is for which positions
\mathbf{x}
of the
GPS-user the basic system of
equations (
1.1
) has a unique solution. In the previous
section the answer was that if
m\geq n+2
, we have uniqueness for

almost
all
\mathbf{x}
. It would, of course be better to have
uniqueness for all
\mathbf{x}
, without exceptions. This is not only of theoretical interest, since in the
vicinity of points where a problem is ill-posed it tends behave badly
in the presence of inexact measurements and other errors; so if it is
well-posed everywhere, this problem does not arise.

Theorem 6.1

(Uniqueness for all
\mathbf{x}
)
.

Assume that in the situation of
1.2
we have

m\geq 2n+2
. Then for almost all
\mathbf{a}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{a}_{m}
we have
that (
1.2
), and therefore also (
1.1
), has a
unique solution for all
\mathbf{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.

More precisely, there exists a nonzero polynomial
f
in
mn

variables such that if
f(\mathbf{a}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{a}_{m})\neq 0
, then for
all
\mathbf{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
the matrix
A\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times(n+2)}
,
defined by (
1.5
), has rank
n+2
. (Recall that
A
depends
on
\mathbf{x}
by (
1.13
).) So (
1.7
) gives the unique
solution of (
1.2
), and therefore of (
1.1
).

Again, the proof uses some algebraic geometry.

Proof.

Consider the affine variety
X\subseteq{\mathbb{C}}^{mn+2n+1}
given
by the equations

(6.1)

\sum_{j=1}^{n}(U_{j}^{2}-1)A_{i,j}+2\!\!\!\sum_{1\leq j<k\leq n}\!\!U_{j}U_{k}A_{i,j}A_{i,k}+2\sum_{j=1}^{n}(X_{j}-VU_{j})A_{i,j}+V^{2}-\sum_{j=1}^{n}X_{j}^{2}\quad(i=1,\ldots,m)

with indeterminates
A_{i,j}
,
U_{j}
,
X_{j}
, and
V
. Mapping a
point of
X
to the coordinates corresponding to the indeterminates

U_{j}
,
X_{j}
, and
V
gives a morphism

\pi\mbox{:}\ X\rightarrow{\mathbb{C}}^{2n+1}
. Every fiber of
\pi
is isomorphic to
the (cartesian) product of
m
copies of a hypersurface in
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
,
so its dimension is
m(n-1)
. Hence as in the proof of
5.2

(but without any case distinction) we see that

\dim(X^{\prime})-\dim(Y^{\prime})\leq m(n-1)
for every closed, irreducible
subset
X^{\prime}\subseteq X
, with
Y^{\prime}:=\overline{\pi(X^{\prime})}
. Since

\dim(Y^{\prime})\leq 2n+1
, we conclude that

\dim(X)\leq m(n-1)+2n+1
. Since
m>2n+1
, this implies

(6.2)

\dim(X)<mn.

Now consider the morphism
\psi\mbox{:}\ X\rightarrow{\mathbb{C}}^{mn}
given by
mapping a point of
X
to the coordinates corresponding to the
indeterminates
A_{i,j}
. It follows from (
6.2
) that the
Zariski closure
Y:=\overline{\psi(X)}
of the image has dimension

<mn
, so there is a nonzero
f\in{\mathbb{C}}[A_{1,1},\ldots,A_{m,n}]

that vanishes on
Y
.

To finish the proof, assume that for

\mathbf{a}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{a}_{m}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
there exists
\mathbf{x}
such that the
matrix
A
has rank
<r+2
. We have seen that this rank does not
change if we subtract
t
from the
t_{i}
, i.e., if we
assume
t=0
. Then
Lemma

1.4
tells us that

\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{a}_{i}\rangle-\alpha=\lVert\mathbf{a}_{i}-\mathbf{x}\rVert

for all
i
, and squaring both sides yields

(6.3)

\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{a}_{i}\rangle^{2}-\langle\mathbf{a}_{i},\mathbf{a}_{i}\rangle+2\langle\mathbf{x}-\alpha\mathbf{u},\mathbf{a}_{i}\rangle+\alpha^{2}-\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}\rangle=0\quad(i=1,\ldots,m).

This means that
\alpha
and the coordinates of
\mathbf{u}
,
\mathbf{x}
and
the
\mathbf{a}_{i}
satisfy the equations (
6.1
) defining
X
. So
the point
(\mathbf{a}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{a}_{m})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{mn}
lies in the image

\psi(X)
and therefore in
Y
. We obtain

f(\mathbf{a}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{a}_{m})=0
, and this also holds if we
replace
f
by its coefficient-wise real or imaginary part. So

\operatorname{Re}(f)
or
\operatorname{Im}(f)
(whichever is
nonzero) provides the desired polynomial for the theorem.
∎

Theorem 6.3

(An explicit condition guaranteeing uniqueness)

Assume that in the situation of

7.
Conclusion

We have presented a comprehensive analysis of the global positioning problem in any dimension
n
and for any number
m
of satellites assuming that the data is exact (no noise) and the satellites are not in a common affine hyperplane. This is equivalent to the multilateration problem in which a source at an unknown location emits a signal received by
m
sensors in known locations equipped with a synchronized clock.
Our analysis is based on a simple algebraic formulation of the problem that consists in either one linear equation (
1.6
) or one quadratic equation in one variable together with one linear equation (
1.12
). This formulation allowed us to fully characterize the cases where the solution of the problem is not unique and describe these cases in simple geometric terms. Our geometric description is based on quadrics of revolution “with a focus” (see section [ref]): the satellites lie on the quadric, and the foci of the quadric are the possible solutions for the user position. This allows us to quantify the number of cases where the solution is not unique for different numbers of satellites.

Theorem

and
Theorem

6.1
) required the use of Chevalley’s theorem on fiber dimension, which is a staple from algebraic geometry, in a crucial step of their proofs.

The case where the satellites are coplanar is still open and would be an interesting one to study. We also did not focus on the case where the user is located on the surface of the Earth and the satellites are in orbit around it. This would of be another compelling question to investigate. It would also be worthwhile to explore if our new problem formulation could be used to formulate better iterative solution methods for the case where the time measurements are corrupted by noise and possibly include outliers or extraneous data points.
