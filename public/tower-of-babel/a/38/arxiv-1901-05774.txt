Darboux and Calapso transforms of meromorphically isothermic surfaces
Andreas Fuchs

Abstract
We consider those simply connected isothermic surfaces for which their Hopf differential factorizes into a real function and a meromorphic quadratic differential that has a zero or pole at some point, but is nowhere zero and holomorphic otherwise. Upon restriction to a simply connected patch that does not contain the zero or pole, the Darboux and Calapso transformations yield new isothermic surfaces. We determine the limiting behaviour of these transformed patches as the zero or pole of the meromorphic quadratic differential is approached and investigate whether they are continuous around that point.

Introduction

In differential geometry transformations of surfaces have contributed considerably in revealing the structures of various surface classes and relations between them. Already in the nineteenth century geometers discovered methods to construct new surfaces from a given one while preserving some geometrical properties, such as the Combescure transformation, which preserves tangent planes up to translations, and the Ribaucour transformation, which preserves curvature lines and an enveloped sphere congruence, see
[
Bia22
]
and
[
Eis62
]
, or
[
DT03
]
for a more modern treatment. While these transformations exist for arbitrary smooth surfaces in Euclidean space, others are defined only on subclasses of smooth surfaces, such as the Bäcklund and Lie transformations of pseudospherical surfaces (see
[
Eis60
]
) in Euclidean geometry or the Christoffel, Darboux and Calapso transformations of isothermic surfaces in Möbius geometry (see
[
Bur06
,
HJ03
]
) and Lie applicable surfaces in Lie sphere geometry (see
[
Pem16
]
). During the last three decades, many of these surfaces have been shown to constitute integrable systems by relating their transformations to a pencil of flat connections, as summarized in
[
Bur17
]
, cf.
[
TU00
,
BC10
,
Bur06
,
BFPP93
,
FP96
]
. Thereby, their rich transformation theory is placed into common ground and the highly developed tools of integrable systems theory are made available. A discrete version of integrability serves as the guideline for how to define discrete analogues of certain smooth surface classes
[
BS07
]
.

Transformations of surfaces can also be used to shed a different light on representation formulas. For example, the Weierstrass representation of a minimal surface can be interpreted as the Goursat transform of its Weierstrass data, as explained in
[
HJ03
,
HJH17
]
, and Bryant’s representation
[
Bry87
]
of CMC-1 surfaces in hyperbolic space can be related to the Darboux transformation of isothermic surfaces, see
[
HJMN01
]
. Recently, these and further representation formulas have been linked to the transformation theory of
\Omega
-surfaces in
[
Pem16
]
.

We are interested in the class of smooth isothermic surfaces, classically characterized by the local existence of conformal curvature line coordinates, away from umbilics. For these surfaces, the transformation theory is defined only locally and away from problematic umbilics. In other words, it is well defined only on the subclass that contains those simply connected isothermic surfaces which can be covered by conformal curvature line coordinate charts. These surfaces, which we call simple isothermic, can equivalently be characterized by the existence of a factorization of the Hopf differential of the surface into a real factor and a nowhere zero holomorphic quadratic differential (cf.
[
Bur06
,
HJ03
,
Smy04
]
). In particular, a simple isothermic surface cannot contain an umbilic of nonzero index and hence cannot have the topology of the sphere by the Poincaré-Hopf theorem (cf.
[
Hop89
]
).

Arguably, this limitation of the transformation theory is not quite satisfactory though as many classical examples of isothermic surfaces are not simple isothermic. For instance, a triaxial ellipsoid, surfaces of revolution which smoothly intersect the axis of revolution, and minimal surfaces with appropriate Weierstrass data all contain umbilics with nonzero index. However, all these examples fall into the class of meromorphically isothermic surfaces, that is, their Hopf differential factorizes into a real factor and a globally defined meromorphic quadratic differential. The globally existing factorization makes this class of surfaces a promising candidate for an extension of the simple isothermic transformation theory.

For a given meromorphically isothermic surface which is not simple isothermic, the transformation theory is still not defined globally. But after removing the zeros and poles of the meromorphic quadratic differential from the domain of the surface and then passing to the universal cover, one obtains a simple isothermic surface as a branched covering of the original surface. One may then ask

(31)

where
\Xi^{\Re}=V_{0}\wedge W
with

.

Proof.

The bounds (

) and (

) follow from (

), the bound (

) and
[
Fuc18b
, Prop 3.1]
. The limits (
27
) and (

) follow from (

),
[
Fuc18b
, Prop 1]
, (
21
) and again the bound (

). Finally, if the restriction of
\Gamma_{p}(\boldsymbol{\xi})
to
\mathcal{C}
had a limit at

, then also its restriction to some segment of some

-parameter line contained in

and ending in

would have a limit at

, Prop 3.1]
.

∎

We note that the functions
\left|\frac{\boldsymbol{z}(q)}{\boldsymbol{z}(p)}\right|^{\zeta}

are independent of the chosen coordinate adapted to

3.3
Primitives of pole forms

We now study the behaviour of primitives of those
\mathfrak{p}\mathfrak{o}(\mathbb{R}^{n+2}_{1})
-valued
1-forms

\boldsymbol{\psi}
for which
\psi
is a sum of a pure pole form and a bounded
\mathfrak{p}\mathfrak{o}(\mathbb{R}^{n+2}_{1})
-valued 1-form.

Definition 3.4
.

A
\mathfrak{p}\mathfrak{o}(\mathbb{R}^{n+2}_{1})
-valued 1-form
\psi
on
\Delta\backslash\{s\}
is a

if
\dd+\psi
is flat and
\psi-\xi
is bounded for a pure pole form
\xi
on
\Delta\backslash\{s\}
.

Let
(r,\phi)
be polar coordinates associated to a coordinate
z
adapted to
\xi
. Then the pullback of
\xi
to any
r
-parameter line is a pure pole form in the sense of
[
Fuc18b
, Sect 3.1]
such that the pullback of
\psi
is a pole form in the sense of
[
Fuc18b
, Def 3]
.

Thus, we know from Cor 1 and Prop 2 of
[
Fuc18b
]
how the primitives of
\psi
behave as one approaches
s
along an
r
-parameter line. We now investigate how the primitives of the pullbacks of
\psi
via the family
(\gamma_{T})_{T\in(0,r_{0}]}
of
\phi
-parameter circles behave as
T
tends to zero. It follows directly from (
19
) that there is a constant
B\in\mathbb{R}
such that

\forall T\in(0,r_{0}]:~~~~~|\gamma_{T}^{*}\psi-\xi^{\Im}\dd\varphi|<T\,B\,|\dd\varphi|.

(32)

We now use this bound to get a corresponding bound for the primitives of
\gamma^{*}_{T}\psi
.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
\psi
be a pole form on
\Delta\backslash\{s\}
with
\psi-\xi
bounded for the pure pole form
\xi
. Choose a coordinate
z
adapted to
\xi
with associated polar coordinates
(r,\phi)
and denote by
r_{0}
the radius of the largest disc with centre
z(s)
in
z(\Delta)
. Let
(\gamma_{T})_{T\in(0,r_{0}]}
be the family of
\phi
-parameter circles in
\Delta\backslash\{s\}
. Then for every compact subset
\mathcal{C}\subset\mathbb{R}
, there is a constant
B\in\mathbb{R}
such that

\forall T\in(0,r_{0}]~~\forall\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2}\in\mathcal{C}:~~~~~~|\Gamma_{\varphi_{1}}^{~\varphi_{2}}(\gamma_{T}^{*}\Psi)|<B,

(33)

and

\forall T\in(0,r_{0}]~~\forall\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2}\in\mathcal{C}:~~~~~~|\Gamma_{\varphi_{1}}^{~\varphi_{2}}(\gamma_{T}^{*}\Psi)-\Gamma_{\varphi_{1}}^{~\varphi_{2}}(\Xi^{\Im}\dd\varphi)|<B~T.

(34)

Proof.

We first show (
33
). As in
[
FR17
, Prop A.1]
, we have the bound

|\Gamma_{\varphi_{1}}^{~\varphi_{2}}(\gamma_{T}^{*}\Psi)|\leq e^{\left|\int_{\varphi_{1}}^{\varphi_{2}}|\gamma_{T}^{*}\Psi|\right|}\leq e^{\left|\int_{\varphi_{1}}^{\varphi_{2}}|\gamma_{T}^{*}\Psi-\Xi^{\Im}\dd\varphi|\right|}e^{\left|\int_{\varphi_{1}}^{\varphi_{2}}|\Xi^{\Im}|\dd\varphi\right|}.

This, the boundedness of
\Psi-\Xi
and (
25
) show that indeed there is a
B\in\mathbb{R}
such that (
33
) holds. To show (
34
), we note that by differentiation and setting
\varphi_{1}=\varphi_{2}
one readily verifies that

\Gamma_{\varphi_{1}}^{~\varphi_{2}}(\gamma_{T}^{*}\Psi)-\Gamma_{\varphi_{1}}^{~\varphi_{2}}(\Xi^{\Im}\dd\varphi)=\int_{\varphi_{1}}^{\varphi_{2}}\Gamma_{\varphi_{1}}^{~\tilde{\varphi}}(\gamma^{*}_{T}\Psi)\left((\gamma_{T}^{*}\Psi)_{\tilde{\varphi}}-\Xi^{\Im}\right)\Gamma_{\tilde{\varphi}}^{~\varphi_{2}}(\Xi^{\Im}\dd\varphi)\dd\tilde{\varphi}

(35)

holds for all
T\in(0,r_{0}]
and all
\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
. Now take the norm
|\cdot|
of this and use the bounds (
32
), (
33
) and (
25
) to find that indeed there is a constant
B
such that (
34
) holds.

∎

We now come to the main result of this section for pole forms
\psi
with
\psi-\xi
bounded for a pure pole form

of the first kind.

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
\psi
be a pole form on
\Delta\backslash\{s\}
with

bounded for a pure pole form

of the first kind. For every

\boldsymbol{\psi}
using the map

by

\boldsymbol{\xi}\ltimes_{\!p}\boldsymbol{\psi}:=\Gamma_{p}(\boldsymbol{\xi})\boldsymbol{\psi}\Gamma^{~p}(\boldsymbol{\xi})-\boldsymbol{\xi},

(36)

such that

\forall p\in\boldsymbol{\Delta}\backslash\{s\}:~~\Gamma_{p}(\boldsymbol{\psi})=\Gamma_{p}(\boldsymbol{\xi}\ltimes_{\!p}\boldsymbol{\psi})\Gamma_{p}(\boldsymbol{\xi})

(37)

and

\forall p,\tilde{p}\in\boldsymbol{\Delta}\backslash\{s\}:~~~\Gamma_{\tilde{p}}(\boldsymbol{\xi}\ltimes_{\!\tilde{p}}\boldsymbol{\psi})=\Gamma_{\tilde{p}}^{~p}(\boldsymbol{\psi})\Gamma_{p}(\boldsymbol{\xi}\ltimes_{\!p}\boldsymbol{\psi})\Gamma_{p}^{~\tilde{p}}(\boldsymbol{\xi}).

(38)

1.

There is a limit map
p\mapsto\Gamma_{p}^{~s}(\boldsymbol{\xi}\ltimes_{\!p}\boldsymbol{\psi})
defined on all of
\boldsymbol{\Delta}\backslash\{s\}
such that for all
\mathcal{C}\subset\boldsymbol{\Delta}\backslash\{s\}

\mathcal{C}\cup\{s\}\subset\boldsymbol{\Delta}
is compact with limit point

we have

\forall p\in\boldsymbol{\Delta}\backslash\{s\}:~~~~~\lim_{\mathcal{C}\ni q\rightarrow s}\Gamma_{p}^{~q}(\boldsymbol{\xi}\ltimes_{\!p}\boldsymbol{\psi})=\Gamma_{p}^{~s}(\boldsymbol{\xi}\ltimes_{\!p}\boldsymbol{\psi}).

(39)

Moreover, if
\xi
is degenerate or spacelike, that limit map satisfies

\lim_{p\rightarrow s}\Gamma_{p}^{~s}(\boldsymbol{\xi}\ltimes_{\!p}\boldsymbol{\psi})=\id.

(40)

2.

The monodromy of
\psi
with base point
P\in\Delta\backslash\{s\}
is

\mathcal{M}_{P}(\psi)=\Gamma_{p}^{~s}(\boldsymbol{\xi}\ltimes_{\!p}\boldsymbol{\psi})\,\mathcal{M}(\xi)\,\Gamma_{s}^{~p}(\boldsymbol{\xi}\ltimes_{\!p}\boldsymbol{\psi}),

(41)

where

is an arbitrary point in
\boldsymbol{\Delta}\backslash\{s\}
such that
\mathfrak{p}(p)=P
and
\Gamma_{p}^{~s}(\boldsymbol{\xi}\ltimes_{\!p}\boldsymbol{\psi})
is the above limit map.

Proof.

Again, due to (
9
), we may replace
\Delta
by any closed, simply connected neighbourhood of
s
. By doing so, we can achieve that there is a coordinate
z
adapted to
\xi
that is defined on all of
\Delta
and such that

is a closed disc with some radius

, we denote polar coordinates associated to

The relations (
37
) and (
38
) follow from (
9
) and (
10
).

1.

It is sufficient to prove the proposition for all
p
and
\mathcal{C}
such that
p\in\mathcal{C}
. Namely, if (
39
) holds for some
\mathcal{C}_{1}\subset\boldsymbol{\Delta}\backslash\{s\}
such that
\mathcal{C}_{1}\cup\{s\}
is compact with limit point
s
, it also holds for all
\mathcal{C}_{2}\subseteq\mathcal{C}_{1}
with limit point
s
. Therefore, the cases in which
p\notin\mathcal{C}
follow from the cases in which
p\in\mathcal{C}
. So fix
\mathcal{C}\subset\boldsymbol{\Delta}\backslash\{s\}
such that
\mathcal{C}\cup\{s\}\subset\boldsymbol{\Delta}
is compact with limit point
s
and a point
p\in\mathcal{C}
. Denote by
\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{R}}\in\mathbb{R}
any compact subset of
\mathbb{R}
such that
\phi(\mathcal{C})\subseteq\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{R}}
, where
(r,\phi)
are polar coordinates associated to
z
. Since we assumed

to be a disc, we can connect
p
to a point

by first moving radially along the
r
-parameter line
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\phi(p)}
from

to

and then moving along the
\phi
-parameter curve
\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{r(q)}
from
\phi(p)
to
\phi(q)

\Gamma_{p}^{~q}(\boldsymbol{\xi}\ltimes_{\!p}\boldsymbol{\psi})=\Gamma_{r(p)}^{~r(q)}(\boldsymbol{\mu}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\xi}\ltimes_{\!p}\boldsymbol{\psi}))\Gamma_{\phi(p)}^{~\phi(q)}(\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*}_{r(q)}(\boldsymbol{\xi}\ltimes_{\!p}\boldsymbol{\psi})).

(42)

To prove that the left hand side has a limit at

, we show that both factors on the right hand side have a limit at

.

To see that the first factor has a limit at

, we note that, since

, we have

\boldsymbol{\mu}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\xi}\ltimes_{\!p}\boldsymbol{\psi})=\Gamma_{r(p)}(\mu^{*}\xi)(\mu^{*}\psi)\Gamma^{~r(p)}(\mu^{*}\xi)-\mu^{*}\xi.

This is precisely the gauge transform of the pole form

in the sense of
[
Fuc18b
, Def 3]
on

by the map
\Gamma^{~r(p)}(\mu^{*}\xi)

[
Fuc18b
, Cor 1]
that the first factor on the right hand side of (

.

We now prove that the second factor in (
42

q
approaches
s
in
\mathcal{C}
. For

we have

\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*}_{T}(\boldsymbol{\xi}\ltimes_{\!p}\boldsymbol{\psi})=\Gamma_{r(p)}^{~T}(\mu^{*}\xi)~\Gamma_{\phi(p)}(\xi^{\Im}\dd\varphi)\gamma^{*}_{T}(\psi-\xi)\Gamma^{~\phi(p)}(\xi^{\Im}\dd\varphi)~\Gamma^{~r(p)}_{T}(\mu^{*}\xi),

where we used

\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*}_{T}\Gamma_{p}(\boldsymbol{\xi})=\Gamma_{r(p)}^{~T}(\mu^{*}\xi)~\Gamma_{\phi(p)}(\xi^{\Im}\dd\varphi).

By (
25

\Gamma_{\phi(p)}^{~\phi(q)}(\xi^{\Im}\dd\varphi)

. Since
\psi
is a pole form,

\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*}_{T}(\boldsymbol{\xi}\ltimes_{\!p}\boldsymbol{\psi})=T~\Gamma_{r(p)}^{~T}(\mu^{*}\xi)\,\chi(T,\cdot)\,\Gamma^{~r(p)}_{T}(\mu^{*}\xi)\,\dd\varphi,\\

(44)

to

Fuc18b

|\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*}_{T}(\boldsymbol{\xi}\ltimes_{\!p}\boldsymbol{\psi})|\leq T\,|\Gamma_{r(p)}^{~T}(\mu^{*}\xi)\chi(T,\cdot)\Gamma^{~r(p)}_{T}(\mu^{*}\xi)|\leq T\,\mathcal{B}_{r(p)}(T).

Now use
[
FR17
, A.6]
to conclude that the second factor in (

\left|\Gamma_{\phi(p)}^{~\varphi}(\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*}_{T}(\boldsymbol{\xi}\ltimes_{\!p}\boldsymbol{\psi}))-\id\right|\leq e^{\left|\int_{\phi(p)}^{\varphi}TB_{r(p)}(T)\dd\tilde{\varphi}\right|}-1=e^{\left|(\varphi-\phi(p))TB_{r(p)}(T)\right|}-1

(46)

for all
\varphi\in\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{R}}
. Taking the limit
T\rightarrow 0
of this using (

\forall\varphi\in\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{R}}:~~~~\lim_{T\rightarrow 0}\Gamma_{\phi(p)}^{~\varphi}(\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*}_{T}(\boldsymbol{\xi}\ltimes_{\!p}\boldsymbol{\psi}))=\id.

Thus, the second factor in (

in
\mathcal{C}
.

Taking the limit of (

\lim_{q\rightarrow s}\Gamma_{p}^{~q}(\boldsymbol{\xi}\ltimes_{\!p}\boldsymbol{\psi})=\Gamma_{r(p)}^{~0}(\boldsymbol{\mu}^{*}\boldsymbol{\xi}\ltimes_{\!p}\boldsymbol{\psi})=:\Gamma_{p}^{~s}(\boldsymbol{\xi}\ltimes_{\!p}\boldsymbol{\psi}),

which defines the limit map

. Clearly, that map is independent of the chosen subset
\mathcal{C}
. (

Fuc18b

.

2.

We now compute the monodromy of
\psi

). We may thus conveniently choose

and

. Then

\mathcal{M}_{P}(\psi)=\Gamma_{0}^{~2\pi}(\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*}_{r(p)}\boldsymbol{\psi})=\Gamma_{0}^{~2\pi}(\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*}_{r(p)}\boldsymbol{\xi}\ltimes_{\!p}\boldsymbol{\psi})\mathcal{M}(\xi).

(48)

The 1-form

\boldsymbol{\psi}
and hence

to some

(49)

for arbitrary

. We want to take the limit

into (

We now turn to primitives of pole forms
\psi

\psi-\xi

Proposition 3.7
.

Let
\psi

\mathcal{C}

, we have
\Gamma_{\phi(p)}^{~\phi(q)}(\gamma^{*}_{r(q)}\Xi)V_{-}=V_{-}
and so the third summand equals
K(p)V_{-}^{*}
. Therefore, indeed

\lim_{\mathcal{C}\ni q\rightarrow s}\left(\frac{r(q)}{r(p)}\right)^{\zeta}\Gamma_{p}^{~q}(\boldsymbol{\Psi})=K(p)V_{-}^{*},

(56)

which proves (
52
).

To show (
53
), we note that the map
~{}^{*}
which sends an endomorphism of
\mathbb{R}^{n+2}_{1}
to its adjoint with respect to the Minkowski inner product is continuous. Since
\Gamma_{p}^{~q}(\Psi)^{*}=\Gamma_{q}^{~p}(\Psi)
, the limit (
53
) follows from (
52
).

Finally, the first relation in (
51
) is a consequence of

K(p)V_{-}^{*}=\lim_{q\rightarrow s}\Gamma_{p}^{~q}(\Psi)=\Gamma_{p}^{~\tilde{p}}(\Psi)\lim_{q\rightarrow s}\Gamma_{\tilde{p}}^{~q}(\Psi)=\Gamma_{p}^{~\tilde{p}}(\Psi)K(\tilde{p})V_{-}^{*}.

Smoothness of
K
now follows from (
51
) and the smoothness of
\boldsymbol{\psi}
.
∎

The expression (
41
) for the monodromy of a pole form of the first kind is very useful. It not only shows that its Jordan normal form is the same as that of
\mathcal{M}(\xi)
, which can be computed explicitly. When
\psi
is the 1-form associated to a meromorphically isothermic surface, (
41
) also gives a geometric meaning to the (generalized) eigenvectors of
\mathcal{M}_{P}(\lambda\omega)
, as we will see in Cor
4.3
, Cor
5.4
and Cor
5.10
. The results that we have found for the monodromy of pole forms of the second kind are less explicit and incomplete.

Proposition 3.8
.

Let
\psi
be a pole form on
\Delta\backslash\{s\}
with
\psi-\xi
bounded for a pure pole form
\xi
of the second kind. Then, for all
P\in\Delta\backslash\{s\}
the monodromies
\mathcal{M}_{P}(\Psi)
and
\mathcal{M}(\Xi)
have the same eigenvalues. Moreover, with
\langle K\rangle
as in Prop
3.7
and
p\in\boldsymbol{\Delta}\backslash\{s\}
such that
\mathfrak{p}(p)=P
, the line
\langle K(p)\rangle
is an eigendirection of
\mathcal{M}_{P}(\Psi)
with eigenvalue
1
.

Proof.

Since
\mathcal{M}_{P}(\Psi)
and
\mathcal{M}_{\tilde{P}}(\Psi)
are similar for all
P,\tilde{P}\in\Delta\backslash\{s\}
, it is sufficient to prove that
\mathcal{M}_{P}(\psi)
and
\mathcal{M}(\Xi)
have the same eigenvalues for one point
P\in\Delta\backslash\{s\}
. Let
z
be a coordinate adapted to
\xi
and choose
P\in\Delta\backslash\{s\}
such that
P
can be joined to
s
along an
r
-parameter line
\mu
entirely contained in
\Delta
. For
T\in[|z(P)|,0)
, define

\mathcal{M}(T):=\mathcal{M}_{\mu(T)}(\psi)~~~\text{and}~~~\mathcal{M}(0):=\mathcal{M}(\xi),

such that
\mathcal{M}(|z(P)|)=\mathcal{M}_{P}(\Psi)
. Then
\mathcal{M}:[|z(P)|,0]\rightarrow\mathbb{P}O(\mathbb{R}^{n+2}_{1})
is continuous. Moreover,
\mathcal{M}(T_{1})
and
\mathcal{M}(T_{2})
are similar for all
T_{1},T_{2}\in[|z(P)|,0)
. Thus, the eigenvalues of
\mathcal{M}(T)
are the same for all
T\in[|z(P)|,0)
. But since
\mathcal{M}
is continuous and eigenvalues of a matrix depend continuously on its entries, we can conclude that the eigenvalues of
\mathcal{M}(T)
are the same for all
T\in[T,0]
. In particular, the eigenvalues of
\mathcal{M}_{P}(\Psi)
coincide with those of
\mathcal{M}(\Xi)
.

To see that
\langle K(p)\rangle
is an eigendirection of
\mathcal{M}_{P}(\Psi)
, write
\Xi=\Re(-(\Xi^{\Re}+i\Xi^{\Im})\dd z/z)
and let
V_{\pm}
be eigenvectors of
\Xi^{\Re}
to the eigenvalues
\pm\zeta
with
\zeta>0
. Denote by
K
the lift of
\langle K\rangle
from Prop
3.7
. Then, by (
51
), we get

\forall q\in\Delta\backslash\{s\}:~~~~\mathcal{M}_{P}(\Psi)K(p)=\Gamma_{p}^{~q}(\Psi)\mathcal{M}_{\mathfrak{p}(q)}(\Psi)\Gamma_{q}^{~p}(\Psi)K(p)=\left|\frac{\boldsymbol{z}(q)}{\boldsymbol{z}(p)}\right|^{\zeta}\Gamma_{p}^{~q}(\Psi)\mathcal{M}_{\mathfrak{p}(q)}(\Psi)K(q).

Now choose
\mathcal{C}\subset\boldsymbol{\Delta}\backslash\{s\}
such that
\mathcal{C}\cup\{s\}
is compact with limit point
s
and take the limit
\mathcal{C}\ni q\rightarrow s
to find

\mathcal{M}_{P}(\Psi)K(p)=\lim_{\mathcal{C}\ni q\rightarrow s}\left|\frac{\boldsymbol{z}(q)}{\boldsymbol{z}(p)}\right|^{\zeta}\Gamma_{p}^{~q}(\Psi)\mathcal{M}_{\mathfrak{p}(q)}(\Psi)K(q)=K(p)V_{-}^{*}\,\mathcal{M}(\Xi)\frac{V_{+}}{\left\llangle V_{+},V_{-}\right\rrangle}=K(p),

where we used (
52
), (
34
) and (
51
).
∎

In particular, this shows that the pushforward of
\langle K\rangle
from
\boldsymbol{\Delta}
to
\Delta
is well defined. However, we cannot conclude from Prop
3.8
that
\mathcal{M}_{P}(\Psi)
is diagonalizable (over
\mathbb{C}
). Namely, although
\mathcal{M}(\Xi)
is a rotation and thus diagonalizable over
\mathbb{C}
, it could be that
\mathcal{M}_{P}(\Psi)
is similar to a product
e^{V_{0}\wedge\tilde{W}}\mathcal{M}(\Xi)
with
V_{0}\in\mathcal{L}^{n+1}
, spacelike
\tilde{W}
and
\mathcal{M}(\Xi)V_{0}=V_{0}
,
\mathcal{M}(\Xi)\tilde{W}=\tilde{W}
. In other words,
\mathcal{M}(\Psi)
could be the product of a rotation and a commuting translation. Thus, either there is an
(n-2)
-dimensional sphere of points invariant under

or

is the only invariant point.

4
Pole of first order

We now consider the case of a meromorphically isothermic surface
(\langle f\rangle,Q)
with domain
\Delta
diffeomorphic to a closed disc and such that
Q
has a pole of first order at an interior point
s\in\Delta
, but is nowhere zero and holomorphic otherwise. For simplicity, we say that
(\langle f\rangle,Q)
has a pole of first order at
s
. Using (
7
), we may write

\omega=\omega^{(1,0)}+\omega^{(0,1)}=2\Re\left(\omega^{(1,0)}\right)=\Re\left(f\wedge\bar{\partial}f\frac{Q}{\left\llangle\bar{\partial}f,\partial f\right\rrangle}\right).

Now define

\xi=\Re\left(f(s)\wedge\bar{\partial}_{s}f(\nu)\frac{Q}{\left\llangle\bar{\partial}_{s}f(\nu),\partial f\right\rrangle}\right)~~~~\text{where}~~~\nu\in T_{s}\Delta\backslash\{0\}\subset T_{s}\Delta_{\mathbb{C}}.

(57)

Then
\lambda\xi
is a degenerate pure pole form for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{\times}
. Since
\langle f\rangle
is smooth and
Q
has a pole of first order at
s
, the difference
\omega-\xi
is bounded and hence
\lambda\omega
is a pole form for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{\times}
.
Now choose
\nu\in T_{s}\Delta
to be such that

\Res_{s}\left(\frac{Q}{\left\llangle\bar{\partial}_{s}f(\nu),\partial f\right\rrangle}\right)=\frac{2i}{\|\dd_{s}f(\nu)\|^{2}}.

(58)

This choice of
\nu
is independent of the choice of lift
f
of
\langle f\rangle
. Then, from Def
3.2
we find

\xi^{\Im}=-\Im\left(f(s)\wedge\bar{\partial}_{s}f(\nu)\Res_{s}\left(\frac{Q}{\left\llangle\bar{\partial}_{s}f(\nu),\partial f\right\rrangle}\right)\right)=-f(s)\wedge\frac{2\Re(\bar{\partial}_{s}f(\nu))}{\|\dd_{s}f(\nu)\|^{2}}=-f(s)\wedge\frac{\dd_{s}f(\nu)}{\|\dd_{s}f(\nu)\|^{2}}.

Using (
24
), the monodromy of
\lambda\xi
thus reads

\mathcal{M}(\lambda\xi)=\left\langle\id+2\pi\lambda f(s)\wedge\frac{\dd_{s}f(\nu)}{\|\dd_{s}f(\nu)\|^{2}}-\frac{(2\pi)^{2}\lambda^{2}}{2}\,\frac{f(s)f(s)^{*}}{\|\dd_{s}f(\nu)\|^{2}}\right\rangle.

(59)

We remark that there are precisely two curvature lines that end in the umbilic
s
. The tangent directions of these two lines at
s
coincide and are orthogonal to
\langle\nu\rangle
.

4.1

We can now apply Prop
3.6
to the pole forms
\lambda\omega

is bounded for the degenerate pure pole form
\lambda\xi

\lambda
-Calapso transforms of

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
(\langle f\rangle,Q)
have a pole of first order at
s
and
(\langle f_{\lambda,p}\rangle,\boldsymbol{Q})
be the
\lambda
-Calapso transform normalized at

f
of
\langle f\rangle

f_{\lambda,p}
be given by

f_{\lambda,p}=\Gamma_{p}(\lambda\boldsymbol{\Omega})\boldsymbol{f}

(60)

and choose
\mathcal{C}\subset\boldsymbol{\Delta}\backslash\{s\}
such that
\mathcal{C}\cup\{s\}\subset\boldsymbol{\Delta}
is compact with limit point
s
.

1.

The lift

at

2.

Denote by
\sigma

\Delta

\boldsymbol{\sigma}
its pullback to
\boldsymbol{\Delta}\backslash\{s\}
. Then
\|\dd f_{\lambda,p}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})\|^{2}

.

3.

With
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
as above,

\lim_{\mathcal{C}\ni q\rightarrow s}f_{\lambda,p}(q)\wedge\dd_{q}f_{\lambda,p}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})=\Gamma_{p}^{~s}(\lambda\boldsymbol{\Xi}\ltimes_{\!p}\lambda\boldsymbol{\Omega})~\big(f(s)\wedge\dd_{s}f(\sigma)\big)~\Gamma_{s}^{~p}(\lambda\boldsymbol{\Xi}\ltimes_{\!p}\lambda\boldsymbol{\Omega}).

(61)

In particular, the limit of the tangent congruence of
\langle f_{\lambda,p}\rangle

\lim_{\mathcal{C}\ni q\rightarrow s}\left\langle f_{\lambda,p}(q),\dd_{q}f_{\lambda,p}\right\rangle=\Gamma_{p}^{~s}(\lambda\boldsymbol{\xi}\ltimes_{\!p}\lambda\boldsymbol{\omega})\left\langle f(s),\dd_{s}f\right\rangle.

Proof.

1.

Let
z
be a coordinate adapted to
\xi
. Then, by Lemma
3.3
and the smoothness of
f
, there is a constant
B\in\mathbb{R}
such that

\forall q\in\mathcal{C}:~~~~~|\Gamma_{p}^{~q}(\lambda\boldsymbol{\Xi})(\boldsymbol{f}(q)-f(s))|\leq B\,\ell(p,q)\,|\boldsymbol{z}(q)|.

Since
\lim_{\mathcal{C}\ni q\rightarrow s}\ell(p,q)|\boldsymbol{z}(q)|=0

\Xi(f(s))=0
, we find

\lim_{\mathcal{C}\ni q\rightarrow s}\Gamma_{p}^{~q}(\lambda\boldsymbol{\Xi})\boldsymbol{f}(q)=\lim_{\mathcal{C}\ni q\rightarrow s}\Gamma_{p}^{~q}(\lambda\boldsymbol{\Xi})f(s)=f(s).

(62)

Therefore, using the factorization (
37
), we find

\lim_{\mathcal{C}\ni q\rightarrow s}f_{\lambda,p}(q)=\lim_{\mathcal{C}\ni q\rightarrow s}\Gamma_{p}^{~q}(\lambda\boldsymbol{\Xi}\ltimes_{\!p}\lambda\boldsymbol{\Omega})\Gamma_{p}^{~q}(\lambda\boldsymbol{\Xi})\boldsymbol{f}(q)=\Gamma_{p}^{~s}(\lambda\boldsymbol{\Xi}\ltimes_{\!p}\lambda\boldsymbol{\Omega})f(s).

2.

With
f_{\lambda,p}
given by (
60
), since
\Omega(f)=0
and
\Gamma_{p}^{~q}(\lambda\boldsymbol{\Omega})\in O(\mathbb{R}^{n+2}_{1})

\|\dd f_{\lambda,p}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})\|^{2}=\|\dd\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})\|^{2}=\|\dd f(\sigma)\|^{2},

which has a finite limit at
s
.

3.

To see (
61
), we write

f_{\lambda,p}\wedge\dd f_{\lambda,p}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})=\Gamma_{p}(\lambda\boldsymbol{\Xi}\ltimes_{\!p}\lambda\boldsymbol{\Omega})\Gamma_{p}(\lambda\boldsymbol{\Xi})~\big(\boldsymbol{f}\wedge\dd\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})\big)~\Gamma^{~p}(\lambda\boldsymbol{\Xi})\Gamma^{~p}(\lambda\boldsymbol{\Xi}\ltimes_{\!p}\lambda\boldsymbol{\Omega}).

(63)

Similarly as in (

\displaystyle\lim_{\mathcal{C}\ni q\rightarrow s}\Gamma_{p}^{~q}(\lambda\boldsymbol{\Xi})~\big(\boldsymbol{f}(q)\wedge\dd_{q}\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})\big)~\Gamma_{q}^{~p}(\lambda\boldsymbol{\Xi})

5
Pole of second order

We now come to the case of a meromorphically isothermic surface
(\langle f\rangle,Q)
with simply connected domain
\Delta
and such that
Q
has a pole of second order at an interior point
s\in\Delta
, but is nowhere zero and holomorphic otherwise. For simplicity, we say that
(\langle f\rangle,Q)
has a pole of second order at
s
. In this case, the 1-form
\omega
associated to
(\langle f\rangle,Q)
has a pole of second order at
s
. But we can gauge transform every
\lambda\omega
using a singular map
{g}
to a 1-form
{g}\!\ltimes\!\lambda\omega
that has only a pole of first order.

As explained below Defs
2.1
and
2.4
, we may replace
Q
by any nonzero, real multiple of it and thereby achieve that the holomorphic 1-form
\sqrt{Q}
has residue
1
at
s
. Denote by
z
a coordinate adapted to
\sqrt{Q}
, such that
Q=\dd z^{2}/z^{2}
. As before, we assume
z
to be defined on all of
\Delta
. Polar coordinates
(r,\phi)
associated to
z
are curvature line coordinates for
\langle\boldsymbol{f}\rangle=\mathfrak{p}^{*}\langle f\rangle
because

\boldsymbol{Q}=\frac{\left(\dd r\,e^{i\phi}+r\,i\dd\phi\,e^{i\phi}\right)^{2}}{r^{2}e^{2i\phi}}=\frac{\dd r^{2}}{r^{2}}-\dd\phi^{2}+\frac{2i}{r}\dd r\dd\phi,

such that
\boldsymbol{Q}
and thus also the Hopf differential
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}
restricted to
\phi=const.
or
r=const.
are real (cf.
[
Hop89
, Ch VI, Sect 1.2]
or
[
Fuc18a
, Lemma 1.1.5]
). Conformal curvature line coordinates on
\boldsymbol{\Delta}\backslash\{s\}
are thus given by
(\rho,\phi)
with
\rho=\ln(r)
, that is,
\boldsymbol{z}=e^{\rho+i\phi}
.

The map
{g}
that we use for the gauge transformation has the form of a product
{g}=\langle F\rangle\langle R\rangle
. The first factor
\langle F\rangle:\Delta\backslash\{s\}\rightarrow\mathbb{P}O(\mathbb{R}^{n+2}_{1})
is constructed with a smooth frame
F
for the flat lift
f
of
\langle f\rangle
with respect to
z
. We choose
F
such that

\forall p\in\Delta\backslash\{s\}:~~~~F(p)o=f(p),~~~F(p)\frac{1}{2}(\mathfrak{t}_{u}-i\mathfrak{t}_{v})=\frac{\partial f}{\partial z}(p),~~~F(p)\boldsymbol{\iota}\in\left\langle\frac{\partial f}{\partial u}(p),\frac{\partial f}{\partial v}(p)\right\rangle^{\perp},

(66)

where
o,\boldsymbol{\iota}\in\mathcal{L}^{n+1}
with
\left\llangle o,\boldsymbol{\iota}\right\rrangle=-1
and
\mathfrak{t}_{u}
,
\mathfrak{t}_{v}
are two orthonormal vectors in
\langle o,\boldsymbol{\iota}\rangle^{\perp}
. We define the second factor
\langle R\rangle:\Delta\backslash\{s\}\rightarrow\mathbb{P}O(\mathbb{R}^{n+2}_{1})
by

R\,(\mathfrak{t}_{u}-i\mathfrak{t}_{v})=\frac{z}{|z|}(\mathfrak{t}_{u}-i\mathfrak{t}_{v}),~~~~R\,o=\frac{1}{|z|}\,o,~~~~R\,\boldsymbol{\iota}=|z|\,\boldsymbol{\iota},~~~~R\big|_{\langle o,\boldsymbol{\iota},\mathfrak{t}_{u},\mathfrak{t}_{v}\rangle^{\perp}}=\id.

It is singular at
s
and so the product
g=\langle F\rangle\langle R\rangle=\langle FR\rangle
is a singular frame for
\langle f\rangle
with limit

\lim_{q\rightarrow s}{g}(q)=\lim_{q\rightarrow s}\langle F(q)R(q)\rangle=\lim_{q\rightarrow s}\langle|z(q)|F(q)R(q)\rangle=\langle f(s)\boldsymbol{\iota}^{*}\rangle.

(67)

The
(1,0)
-component of the gauge transformed 1-form
{g}\!\ltimes\!\lambda\omega
then computes to

({g}\!\ltimes\!\lambda\omega)^{(1,0)}=R^{-1}F^{-1}\lambda\omega^{(1,0)}FR+R^{-1}F^{-1}\partial FR+R^{-1}\partial R

with

\displaystyle R^{-1}F^{-1}\lambda\omega^{(1,0)}FR=

\displaystyle R^{-1}\frac{\lambda}{2}o\wedge\left(\mathfrak{t}_{u}+i\mathfrak{t}_{v}\right)\frac{\dd z}{z^{2}}\,R+\mathbb{R}\,\id

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda}{2}\,o\wedge\left(\mathfrak{t}_{u}+i\mathfrak{t}_{v}\right)\frac{\dd z}{z}+\mathbb{R}\,\id,

\displaystyle R^{-1}F^{-1}\partial FR=

\displaystyle R^{-1}\left(\mathcal{F}^{\perp\perp}-\boldsymbol{\iota}\wedge(\mathfrak{t}_{u}-i\mathfrak{t}_{v})\frac{\dd z}{2}+o\wedge\mathcal{F}^{\perp}\right)R

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle R^{-1}\mathcal{F}^{\perp\perp}R-\boldsymbol{\iota}\wedge(\mathfrak{t}_{u}-i\mathfrak{t}_{v})\frac{\dd z}{2z}+|z|\,o\wedge R^{-1}\mathcal{F}^{\perp},

\displaystyle R^{-1}\partial R=

\displaystyle\frac{\dd z}{2z}\,\left(\boldsymbol{\iota}\wedge o-i\,\mathfrak{t}_{u}\wedge\mathfrak{t}_{v}\right),

where
\mathcal{F}^{\perp\perp}
is the projection of
F^{-1}\partial F
onto
\Lambda^{2}\langle o,\boldsymbol{\iota}\rangle_{\mathbb{C}}^{\perp}
and the 1-form
\mathcal{F}^{\perp}

\forall q\in\mathcal{C}~\text{with}~|\boldsymbol{z}(q)|\leq\tilde{r}:~~~\langle u(q)\rangle\notin U.

(80)

To prove this, we note that as one approaches
s
in
\mathcal{C}
, the map
\langle u\rangle
approaches
\langle\tilde{u}\rangle
defined by

\langle\tilde{u}\rangle:=\Gamma^{~p}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\lambda})\Gamma_{s}^{~p}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\lambda}\ltimes_{\!p}\boldsymbol{{g}}\!\ltimes\!\lambda\boldsymbol{\omega})\langle{\hat{F}}_{p}\rangle

because, by (
40
),
\lim_{\mathcal{C}\ni q\rightarrow s}\left\llangle u(q),\tilde{u}(q)\right\rrangle=0
for the lifts

u=\Gamma^{~p}(\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{\lambda})\Gamma^{~p}(\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{\lambda}\ltimes_{\!p}\boldsymbol{G}\!\ltimes\!\lambda\boldsymbol{\Omega}){\hat{F}}_{p},~~~\tilde{u}=\Gamma^{~p}(\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{\lambda})\Gamma_{s}^{~p}(\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{\lambda}\ltimes_{\!p}\boldsymbol{G}\!\ltimes\!\lambda\boldsymbol{\Omega}){\hat{F}}_{p},

which are bounded from below with respect to
|\cdot|
. Under our assumption on
\langle{\hat{f}}(p)\rangle
, this limiting map
\langle\tilde{u}\rangle
does not contain
\langle\boldsymbol{\iota}\rangle
in its image. Namely, we have the equivalence

\langle\tilde{u}(q)\rangle=\langle\boldsymbol{\iota}\rangle~~~\Leftrightarrow~~~\langle{\hat{f}}(p)\rangle=\boldsymbol{{g}}(p)\Gamma_{p}^{~s}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\lambda}\ltimes_{\!p}\boldsymbol{{g}}\!\ltimes\!\lambda\boldsymbol{\omega})\Gamma_{p}^{~q}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\lambda})\langle\boldsymbol{\iota}\rangle

(81)

for all
q\in\boldsymbol{\Delta}\backslash\{s\}
. From (
78
) we see that
\boldsymbol{{g}}(p)\Gamma_{p}^{~s}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\lambda}\ltimes_{\!p}\boldsymbol{{g}}\!\ltimes\!\lambda\boldsymbol{\omega})\Gamma_{p}^{~q}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\lambda})\langle\boldsymbol{\iota}\rangle
is a point on
\pounds[\langle f_{\lambda,p}\rangle]
while, by assumption,
\langle{\hat{f}}(p)\rangle\notin\pounds[\langle f_{\lambda,p}\rangle]
. Thus, we can conclude from (
81
) that indeed
\langle\boldsymbol{\iota}\rangle
does not lie in the image of
\langle\tilde{u}\rangle
. Finally, since
\langle u\rangle
approaches
\langle\tilde{u}\rangle
as one approaches
s
in
\mathcal{C}
, the limit set of
\langle u\rangle
does not contain
\langle\boldsymbol{\iota}\rangle
and so there is an
\tilde{r}\in(0,r_{0}]
such that (
80
) holds. We can thus apply
[
Fuc18b
, Lemma 4]
to complete the proof.
∎

The following theorem treats the case of those
\lambda
-Darboux transforms for which
\langle{\hat{f}}(p)\rangle
lies on the limit set of the
\lambda
-Calapso transform normalized at
p
. Since it can be proved in complete analogy to the first two parts of
[
Fuc18b
, Thm 6]
, we only sketch its proof very briefly.

Theorem 5.9
.

Let
(\langle f\rangle,Q)
have a pole of second order at
s
and
(\langle{\hat{f}}\rangle,\boldsymbol{Q})
be a
\lambda
-Darboux transform of
(\langle\boldsymbol{f}\rangle,\boldsymbol{Q})
with
1-2\lambda<0
and
\langle{\hat{f}}(p)\rangle\in\pounds[\langle f_{\lambda,p}\rangle]
. For any
\mathcal{C}\subset\boldsymbol{\Delta}\backslash\{s\}
such that
\mathcal{C}\cup\{s\}
is compact with limit point
s
, as one approaches
s
in
\mathcal{C}
, the surface
\langle{\hat{f}}\rangle
approaches the curvature sphere of
\langle f\rangle
at
s
, but does not have a limit.

Proof.

Using the frame and lifts of normal fields
N_{i}
of Lemma
5.1
, one can show that
\left\llangle{\hat{f}},N_{i}\circ\mathfrak{p}\right\rrangle
converges to zero as one approaches
s
inside
\mathcal{C}
for a lift
{\hat{f}}
that is bounded from below with respect to
|\cdot|
. Thus,
\langle{\hat{f}}\rangle
approaches the curvature sphere of
\langle f\rangle
at the umbilic
s
. To show that both
\langle f(s)\rangle=\langle F(s)o\rangle
and
\langle F(s)\boldsymbol{\iota}\rangle
are limit points of
\langle{\hat{f}}\rangle
, one may use the form (
78
) of the limit set
\pounds[\langle f_{\lambda,p}\rangle]\ni\langle{\hat{f}}(p)\rangle
to conclude that there are sequences
(q_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(\tilde{q}_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
in
\mathcal{C}
with limit
s
such that

\forall i\in\mathbb{N}:~~~\Gamma_{p}^{~q_{i}}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\lambda})\Gamma_{s}^{~p}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\lambda}\ltimes_{\!p}\boldsymbol{{g}}\!\ltimes\!\lambda\boldsymbol{\omega})\boldsymbol{{g}}(p)^{-1}\langle{\hat{f}}(p)\rangle=\langle o\rangle,~~~\Gamma_{p}^{~\tilde{q}_{i}}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\lambda})\Gamma_{s}^{~p}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\lambda}\ltimes_{\!p}\boldsymbol{{g}}\!\ltimes\!\lambda\boldsymbol{\omega})\boldsymbol{{g}}(p)^{-1}\langle{\hat{f}}(p)\rangle=\langle\boldsymbol{\iota}\rangle.

As in the second part of the proof of
[
Fuc18b
, Thm 6]
one can then show that
\langle{\hat{f}}(q_{i})\rangle
converges to
\langle f(s)\rangle
and
\langle{\hat{f}}(\tilde{q}_{i})\rangle
converges to
\langle F(s)\boldsymbol{\iota}\rangle
as
i
tends to infinity.
∎

5.2.2
Monodromy of transforms

For
1-2\lambda<0
, the pole form
{g}\!\ltimes\!\lambda\omega
is of the first kind. An expression for its monodromy is thus obtained from Prop
3.6
.

Corollary 5.10
.

Let
(\langle f\rangle,Q)
have a pole of second order at
s
. Denote by
\omega
the 1-form associated to
(\langle f\rangle,Q)
. For
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
with
1-2\lambda<0
and points
P\in\Delta\backslash\{s\}
and
p\in\boldsymbol{\Delta}\backslash\{s\}
with
\mathfrak{p}(p)=P
, the monodromy of
\lambda\omega
with base point
P
is

\mathcal{M}_{P}(\lambda\omega)=\left\langle\Pi_{\langle W_{+}(\lambda,p),W_{-}(\lambda,p)\rangle^{\perp}}+e^{-2\pi\sqrt{2\lambda-1}}\Pi_{\langle W_{+}(\lambda,p)\rangle}+e^{2\pi\sqrt{2\lambda-1}}\Pi_{\langle W_{-}(\lambda,p)\rangle}\right\rangle,

(82)

where

\langle W_{\pm}(\lambda,p)\rangle=\boldsymbol{{g}}(p)\Gamma_{p}^{~s}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\lambda}\ltimes_{\!p}\boldsymbol{{g}}\!\ltimes\!\lambda\boldsymbol{\omega})\langle W_{\pm}(\lambda)\rangle,~~~~W_{\pm}(\lambda)=\sqrt{2\lambda-1}\,\mathfrak{t}_{v}\pm(\lambda o+\boldsymbol{\iota}-\mathfrak{t}_{u}),

(83)

are two points on the limit sphere of the
\lambda
-Calapso transform normalized at
p
.

In particular,
\langle W_{\pm}(\lambda,p)\rangle
are the only null eigenspaces of
\mathcal{M}_{P}(\lambda\omega)^{j}
for all
j\in\mathbb{N}
.

We remark that, by (
78
), the points
\langle W_{\pm}(\lambda,p)\rangle
lie on the limit sphere, but not on the limit set of the
\lambda
-Calapso transform normalized at
p
.

Corollary 5.11
.

Let
(\langle f\rangle,Q)
have a pole of second order at
s
. No
\lambda
-Calapso transform of
(\langle\boldsymbol{f}\rangle,\boldsymbol{Q})
with
1-2\lambda<0
can be pushed forward to the
j
-fold cover of
\Delta\backslash\{s\}
for any
j\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof.

This follows readily from Prop
2.5
: no
\lambda
-Calapso transform is constant. Therefore, the image of any
\lambda
-Calapso transform contains infinitely many points. But for any
j\in\mathbb{N}
there are only two points,
\langle W_{\pm}(\lambda,p)\rangle
, which are invariant under
(\mathcal{M}_{\mathfrak{p}(p)}(\lambda\omega))^{j}
, by Cor
5.10
.

∎

Corollary 5.12
.

Let
(\langle f\rangle,Q)
have a pole of second order at
s
. The pushforward of a
\lambda
-Darboux transform
(\langle{\hat{f}}\rangle,\boldsymbol{Q})
of
(\langle\boldsymbol{f}\rangle,\boldsymbol{Q})
with
1-2\lambda<0
to the
j
-fold cover of
\Delta\backslash\{s\}
is well defined if and only if for any
p\in\boldsymbol{\Delta}\backslash\{s\}

\langle{\hat{f}}(p)\rangle\in\{\langle W_{+}(\lambda,p)\rangle,\langle W_{-}(\lambda,p)\rangle\}.

Proof.

Again, this follows from Prop
2.5
and Cor
5.10
.
∎
