Quantitative stability for overdetermined nonlocal problems with parallel surfaces and investigation of the stability exponents
Serena Dipierro, Giorgio Poggesi, Jack Thompson, Enrico Valdinoci

Abstract
. In this article, we analyze the stability of the parallel surface problem for semilinear equations driven by the fractional Laplacian. We prove a quantitative stability result that goes beyond that previously obtained in [ Cir+23 ] . Moreover, we discuss in detail several techniques and challenges in obtaining the optimal exponent in this stability result. In particular, this includes an upper bound on the exponent via an explicit computation involving a family of ellipsoids. We also sharply investigate a technique that was proposed in [ Cir+18 ] to obtain the optimal stability exponent in the quantitative estimate for the nonlocal Alexandrov’s soap bubble theorem, obtaining accurate estimates to be compared with a new, explicit example.

35R11, 47G20, 35N25, 35B50

1.
Introduction and main results

1.1.
The long-standing tradition of overdetermined problems

Overdetermined problems are a broad class of partial differential equations (PDEs) where ‘too many conditions’ are imposed on the solution. Since not every region will admit a solution which satisfies all the conditions, the objective in the study of overdetermined problems is to classify those regions which do admit solutions.

Often, overdetermined problems arise naturally in applications as a combination of a well-posed partial differential equation (
PDE
) which describes the dynamics of a given physical system, as well as an extra condition, often referred to as the overdetermined condition, which describes a property or an optimal quality you would like the solution to possess. As such, overdetermined problems have a close relationship with optimization, free boundary problems and calculus of variations, particularly shape optimization, as well as many other applications including fluid mechanics, solid mechanics, thermodynamics, electrostatics, see
[
Ser71
,
FG08
,
HP18
,
DPV21
]
.

The study of overdetermined problems began in the early 1970’s with the celebrated paper of Serrin
[
Ser71
]
. In this influential paper, Serrin proved that, given a bounded domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
C^{2}
boundary and
f\in C^{0,1}_{\text{loc}}(\mathbb{R})
, if there exists a positive solution
u\in C^{2}(\overline{\Omega})
that satisfies the Dirichlet boundary value problem

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{r@{ \ }l @{\quad\: \;} l}-\Delta u&=f(u)\hfil\quad\>&\text{in }\Omega,\\
u&=0\hfil\quad\>&\text{on }\partial\Omega,\end{array}\right.

as well as the overdetermined condition

(1.3)

\displaystyle\partial_{\nu}u

\displaystyle=c\qquad\text{on }\partial\Omega

for some constant
c\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
, then
\Omega
must be a ball. Here
\nu
is the unit outward pointing normal to
\partial\Omega
and
\partial_{\nu}u=\nabla u\cdot\nu
.
The proof relies on a powerful technique, now known as the
method of moving planes
, a refinement of a reflection principle conceived by Alexandrov in
[
Ale62
]
to prove the so-called
soap bubble theorem
, which states that the only connected closed hypersurfaces embedded in a space form with everywhere constant mean curvature are spheres.

Since the work of Alexandrov and Serrin, the analysis of overdetermined problems and the method of moving planes has seen intense research activity. Some of the literature on the method of moving planes, overdetermined problems, and symmetry in
PDE
include:

•

Alternate/incomplete overdetermined conditions:
One can consider elliptic equations such (
1.1
) or otherwise with an alternate overdetermined condition to (
1.3
),
see
[
HP98
,
FG08
,
BHS14
,
DPV21
]
. The parallel surface problem fits into this category, see below for references.

•

Integral identities:
Integral identities such as the Pohozaev identity are often employed in the analysis of overdetermined problems, providing an alternative approach to the method of moving planes. Such an alternative approach was pioneered in
[
Wei71
]
and then further developed in, e.g.,
[
PS89
,
GL89
,
Bra+08
,
CS09
,
QX17
,
CV19
,
MP20
,
MP20a
]
.

•

Nonlinear elliptic equations:
The original paper of Serrin
[
Ser71
]
dealt with uniformly elliptic quasilinear equations including equations of mean curvature type. Other (possibly degenerate) quasilinear and fully nonlinear equations have been analyzed
[
GL89
,
DS07
,
FK08
,
Bra+08
,
BK11
,
BD13
,
SS15
]
.

•

Symmetry:
The method of moving planes was famously used to prove symmetry results for solutions to semilinear
PDE
in domains with symmetry in
[
GNN79
,
GNN81
]
. For this type of results, an alternative approach which combines Pohozaev identity and isoperimetric-type inequalities was pioneered in
[
Lio81
]
and then further developed in
[
KP94
,
Ser13
,
DPV22
]
.

•

Nonlocal operators:
For overdetermined problems and symmetry for nonlocal equations, see
[
FJ15
,
Cab+18
,
Cir+18
,
SV19
,
Dip+22
,
Cir+23
]
.

1.2.
The nonlocal parallel surface problem and the
main results of this paper

In this paper, we are concerned with the nonlocal parallel surface problem. The context of this problem is as follows: Let
n
be a positive integer and
s\in(0,1)
. Suppose that
G
is an open bounded subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
let
\Omega=G+B_{R}
for some
R>0
, where
A+B
is the Minkowski sum of sets defined by

\displaystyle A+B=\{a+b\text{ s.t. }a\in A,b\in B\}.

Moreover, let
(-\Delta)^{s}
denote the fractional Laplacian defined by

\displaystyle(-\Delta)^{s}u(x)

\displaystyle=c_{n,s}\mathrm{P.V.}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{u(x)-u(y)}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\mathop{}\!dy

where
c_{n,s}
is a positive normalization constant and
\mathrm{P.V.}
denotes the Cauchy principle value. Also, assume that
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is locally Lipschitz and satisfies
f(0)\geqslant 0
. Then, the nonlocal parallel surface problem asks: if there exists a function
u
that satisfies (in an appropriate sense) the equation

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{r@{ \ }l @{\quad\: \;} l}(-\Delta)^{s}u&=f(u)\hfil\quad\>&\text{in }\Omega,\\
u&=0\hfil\quad\>&\text{in }\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega,\\
u&\geqslant 0\hfil\quad\>&\text{in }\mathbb{R}^{n},\end{array}\right.

as well as the overdetermined condition

(1.7)

\displaystyle u=\text{constant}\qquad\text{on }\partial G,

then is
\Omega
necessarily a ball?

This question is referred to as the rigidity problem for the parallel surface problem. Furthermore, one can ask about the stability of this problem, that is, heuristically, if
u
satisfies (
1.2
) and ‘almost’ satisfies (
1.7
) then is
\Omega
‘almost’ a ball? This is the subject of the current article. Of course, one must be precise by what one means by ‘almost’—this will be made clear in the proceeding paragraphs as we describe the literature and our main results.

The local analogue of the parallel surface problem (i.e. the case
s=1
) was introduced in
[
Sha12
]
as a discrete analogue of the original Serrin’s problem. Moreover, it also appears in
[
MS10
,
CMS15
]
in the context of invariant isothermic surfaces of a nonlinear non-degenerate fast diffusion equation.

To see why the parallel surface problem can be
viewed as a discrete analogue of Serrin’s problem, consider that
u\in C^{2}(\overline{\Omega})
satisfies (
1.1
) as well as
u=c_{k}>0
on an countably infinite family of parallel surfaces
\Gamma_{k}
that are a distance
1/k
from the boundary of
\Omega
. Then
kc_{k}
necessarily converges to some
c
by regularity assumptions on
u
and
u
satisfies (
1.3
). Consequently,
\Omega
must be a ball.

To reiterate, in the parallel surface problem there is only a single parallel surface (not a family as just described); regardless, this was enough to prove in
[
Sha12
,
CMS15
]
the rigidity result: if there exists a solution to (
1.1
) satisfying (
1.7
) then
\Omega
must be a ball.

Subsequently, in
[
CMS16
]
, the stability of (
1.2
) for the local problem was addressed. In that article, the authors used the shape functional

\displaystyle\rho(\Omega)=\inf\big\{R-r\text{ s.t. }B_{r}(x)\subset\Omega\subset B_{R}(x)\text{ for some }x\in\Omega\big\}

to quantify how close
\Omega
is to being a ball and the semi-norm

\displaystyle[u]_{\partial G}=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y\in\partial G\\
x\neq y\end{subarray}}\bigg\{\frac{|u(x)-u(y)|}{|x-y|}\bigg\}

to quantify how close
u
is to being constant on
\partial\Omega
. They showed, under reasonable assumptions on
\Omega
, that

(1.8)

\displaystyle\rho(\Omega)\leqslant C[u]_{\partial G}.

Moreover, for the nonlocal parallel surface problem, the rigidity problem was answered affirmatively in
[
Cir+23
]
for the case
f=1
and
[
Dip+22
]
for general
f
. Moreover,
[
Cir+23
]
also addressed the stability problem (still for the case
f=1
) and showed that

(1.9)

\displaystyle\rho(\Omega)\leqslant C[u]_{\partial G}^{\frac{1}{s+2}}.

It is interesting to observe that (
1.9
) is sub-optimal in the sense that it does not recover the estimate (
1.8
) when
s=1
. This is due to the nonlocality of the fractional Laplacian which caused contributions from mass ‘far away’ to have a significant effect on the analysis.

This leads us to our main result.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
G
be an open bounded subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\Omega=G+B_{R}
for some
R>0
. Furthermore, let
\Omega
and
G
have
C^{1}
boundary,
and let
f\in C^{0,1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R})
be such that
f(0)\geqslant 0
. Suppose that
u
satisfies (
1.2
) in the weak sense.

Then,

(1.10)

\displaystyle\rho(\Omega)

\displaystyle\leqslant C[u]_{\partial G}^{\frac{1}{s+2}}

with

C:=C(n,s)\left[\frac{(\max\{1,\operatorname{diam}\Omega\})^{n+2s}\big(1+(\operatorname{diam}\Omega)^{2s}[f]_{C^{0,1}([0,\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}])}\big)}{R^{s}\big(f(0)+\|u\|_{L_{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\big)}+(\operatorname{diam}\Omega)^{n-1}+\frac{|\Omega|}{R}\right]\\
\times\frac{(\operatorname{diam}\Omega)^{n+2s+3}}{R^{2s}|\Omega|}\left(1+R^{2s}[f]_{C^{0,1}([0,\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}])}\right).

For the precise definition of a solution satisfying (
1.2
) in the weak sense, see Section
2
.

?THM?
is a direct extension of
[
Cir+23
]
to the case
f\neq 1
. Moreover, ?THM?
relaxes some of the regularity assumptions on
\Omega
appearing in
[
Cir+23
]
.
It is clear that the dependence on the volume
|\Omega|
in the constant of Theorem
can be
removed by means of the following bounds

|B_{1}|R^{n}\leqslant|\Omega|\leqslant|B_{1}|(\operatorname{diam}\Omega)^{n},\text{ where }|B_{1}|\text{ is the volume of the unit ball in }\mathbb{R}^{n},

which hold true in light of the monotonicity of the volume with respect to inclusion.

Currently, an important open problem for the nonlocal parallel surface problem is to understand the optimality of ?THM?
. Indeed, let
\overline{\beta}(s)
be the optimal exponent in (
1.10
) defined as the supremum over
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
such that if
u=u_{f}
is a weak solution of (
1.2
) for some
f
satisfying the assumptions of ?THM?
then
\rho(\Omega)\leqslant C[u]_{\partial G}^{\beta}
. In this framework, an explicit expression for
\overline{\beta}
as a function of
s
is still unknown. ?THM?
implies that
\overline{\beta}(s)\geqslant\frac{1}{s+2}
and
[
Sha12
,
CMS15
]
establish that
\overline{\beta}(1)=1
,
therefore we believe it is an interesting problem to detect optimal stability exponents in the nonlocal setting, also to recover, whenever possible, the classical exponent of the local cases in the limit.

In Section
7
, we explicitly construct a family of domains
G_{\varepsilon}
that are small perturbations of a ball and corresponding solutions
u_{\varepsilon}
to (
1.2
) when
f=1
which satisfy
[u_{\varepsilon}]_{\partial G_{\varepsilon}}\simeq C\rho(\Omega_{\varepsilon})
as
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
. This entails the following result:

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
G
be an open bounded subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\Omega=G+B_{R}
for some
R>0
. Furthermore, let
\Omega

and
G
have
C^{1}
boundary.

Then, we have that
\overline{\beta}(s)\leqslant 1
.

As far as the authors are aware, these are the only known estimates for
\overline{\beta}(s)
. Furthermore, by considering the case in which
\Omega=\Omega_{\varepsilon}
is a small perturbation of a ball and exploting
interior regularity for the fractional Laplacian, one would expect that
\rho(\Omega_{\varepsilon})\simeq[u_{\varepsilon}]_{\partial G_{\varepsilon}}\simeq\varepsilon
(up to constant) as
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
which suggests that
\overline{\beta}(s)=1
for all
s\in(0,1]
. In Section
5
, we give a broad discussion on some of the challenges that
the nonlocality of the fractional Laplacian presents in obtaining this result. In particular, by way of an example via the Poisson representation formula, we show that estimates for a singular integral involving the reciprocal of the distance to the boundary function play a key role in obtaining the anticipated optimal result. This suggests, surprisingly, that fine geometric estimates for the distance function close to the boundary are required to obtain the optimal exponent.

1.3.
Scrutiny of the stability exponent

In the recent literature,
some inventive methods have been introduced to improve
the stability results obtained via the moving plane method.
A remarkable one was put forth in
[
Cir+18
]
.
Roughly speaking, the setting considered in
[
Cir+18
, Proposition 3.1(b)]
focused
on the critical hyperplane
\pi_{\lambda}=\{x_{n}=\lambda\}
for the moving
plane technique for a set
\Omega
which contains the ball
B_{r}
and is contained in the ball
B_{R}
, looked at the symmetric difference between
\Omega
and its reflection
\Omega^{\prime}
and obtained a bound on the measure of the set

(1.11)

\big\{x\in\Omega\triangle\Omega^{\prime}\text{ s.t. }\operatorname{dist}(x,\pi_{\lambda})\leqslant\gamma\big\}

which was linear in both
\gamma
and
R-r
.

This constituted a fundamental ingredient in
[
Cir+18
]
to achieve an optimal stability exponent.

Unfortunately, we believe that, in the very broad generality in which the result is stated in
[
Cir+18
]
, this statement may not be true, and we present an explicit counter-example.

Nevertheless, in our opinion, a weaker version of
[
Cir+18
, Proposition 3.1(b)]
does hold true. We state and prove this new result and check its optimality against the counter-example mentioned above. This construction plays a decisive role in improving the stability exponent.

Though we refer the reader to Section
6
for full details
of this strategy, let us anticipate here some important details.

More specifically, in the forthcoming Theorem
, under the additional assumption that the set
\Omega
is of class
C^{\alpha}
, with
\alpha>1
, we will bound the measure of the set in (
1.11
), up to constants,
by

(1.12)

\gamma(R-r)^{1-\frac{1}{\alpha}}.

We stress that this bound formally recovers exactly the one stated in
[
Cir+18
, Proposition 3.1(b)]
when
\alpha=+\infty
.

However, we believe that the bound in (
1.12
) is optimal and cannot be improved (in particular, while the dependence in
\gamma
of the estimate above is linear, the dependence in
R-r
, surprisingly, is not!). This will be shown by an explicit counter-example put forth in Theorem
6.6
—while the analytical details
of this counter-example are very delicate, the foundational idea behind
it is sketched in Figure
2
(roughly speaking, the example
is obtained by a very small and localized modification of a ball to induce
a critical situation at the maximal location allowed by the regularity of the set; the construction is technically demanding since the constraint for the set of “being between two balls” induces two different scales, in the horizontal and in the vertical directions, which in principle could provide different contributions).

As a consequence of the bound (
1.12
) for the measure of the set in (
1.11
), as given in ?THM?
, the stability exponent
1/(s+2)
of ?THM?
can be improved to
\alpha/(1+\alpha(s+1))
provided that
\Omega
is of class
C^{\alpha}
for
\alpha>1
: we refer to Section
6.2
and ?THM?
for details.

2.
Preliminaries and notation

In this section we fix the notation that
we will use throughout the article and give some relevant definitions. Let
n\geqslant 1
and
s\in(0,1)
. The fractional Sobolev space
H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is defined as

\displaystyle H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})=\big\{u\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\text{ such that }[u]_{H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}<+\infty\big\}

where
[\cdot]_{H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}
is the Gagliardo semi-norm given by

\displaystyle[u]_{H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}=c_{n,s}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{|u(x)-u(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\mathop{}\!dy\mathop{}\!dx

where
c_{n,s}
is the same constant appearing in the definition of the fractional Laplacian. Functions in
H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
that are equal almost everywhere are identified. Moreover, the bilinear form associated with
[\cdot]_{H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}
is denoted by
\mathcal{E}:H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\times H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\to\mathbb{R}
and is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(u,v)=\frac{c_{n,s}}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{(u(x)-u(y))(v(x)-y(y)}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\mathop{}\!dy\mathop{}\!dx.

We also define the following weighted
L^{1}
norm via

\displaystyle\|u\|_{L_{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{|u(x)|}{1+|x|^{n+2s}}\mathop{}\!dx

and the space
L_{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
as

\displaystyle L_{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})=\big\{L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\text{ such that }\|u\|_{L_{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}<+\infty\big\}.

Now, suppose that
\Omega
is an open, bounded subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and define the space
\mathcal{H}^{s}_{0}(\Omega)
by

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{s}_{0}(\Omega)

\displaystyle=\big\{u\in H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\text{ such that }u=0\text{ in }\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega\big\}.

Let
c:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
be a measurable function such that
c\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
and
g\in L^{2}(\Omega)
. We say that
a function
u\in H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\cap L_{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
satisfies
(-\Delta)^{s}u+cu\geqslant g
(respectively,
\leqslant
) in the
weak sense
if

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(u,v)+\int_{\Omega}cuv\mathop{}\!dx\geqslant\int_{\Omega}gv\mathop{}\!dx\qquad(\text{respectively, }\leqslant)

for all
v\in\mathcal{H}^{s}_{0}(\Omega)
with
v\geqslant 0
. In this case, we also say that
u
is a supersolution (respectively,
subsolution) of
(-\Delta)^{s}u+cu=g
in the weak sense. Moreover, we say a function
u\in H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\cap L_{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
satisfies
(-\Delta)^{s}u+cu=g
in the
weak sense
if

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(u,v)+\int_{\Omega}cuv\mathop{}\!dx=\int_{\Omega}gv\mathop{}\!dx

for all
v\in\mathcal{H}^{s}_{0}(\Omega)
. This is equivalent to
u
being both a weak supersolution and a weak subsolution. Note that we are assuming
a priori
that weak solutions are in
L_{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

Next, we describe some notation regarding antisymmetric functions. This is closely related to the notation of the method of moving planes; however, we will defer our explanation of the method of moving planes until Section
4
in the interests of simplicity.

A function
v:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is said to be
antisymmetric
with respect to a plane
T
if

\displaystyle v(Q_{T}(x))=-v(x)\qquad\text{for all }x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

where
Q_{T}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the function that reflects
x
across
T
. Often it will suffice to consider the case
T=\{x_{1}=0\}
, in which case
Q_{T}(x)=x-2x_{1}e_{1}
.

For simplicity, we will refer to
v
as
antisymmetric
if it is antisymmetric with respect to the plane
\{x_{1}=0\}
. Moreover, we sometimes write
x^{\prime}
to denote
Q_{T}(x)
when it is clear from context what
T
is.

Finally, some other notation we will employ through the article is:
given a set
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the function
\chi_{A}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
denotes the characteristic function of
A
, given by

\displaystyle\chi_{A}(x)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }x\in A,\\
0,&\text{if }x\not\in A,\end{cases}

and
\delta_{A}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to[0,+\infty]
denotes the distance function to
A
, given by

\displaystyle\delta_{A}(x)=\inf_{y\in A}|x-y|.

We will denote by
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}:=\{x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\;{\mbox{s.t.}}\;x_{1}>0\}
and, given
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we will denote by
A^{+}:=A\cap\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
.

Moreover, if
A
is open and bounded with sufficiently regular boundary (we only use the case in which
A
has smooth boundary), we will denote by
\psi_{A}
the (unique) function in
C^{\infty}(A)\cap C^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
such that
\psi_{A}
satisfies

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{r@{ \ }l @{\quad\: \;} l}(-\Delta)^{s}\psi_{A}&=1\hfil\quad\>&\text{in }A,\\
\psi_{A}&=0\hfil\quad\>&\text{in }\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus A.\end{array}\right.

Also, we will use
\lambda_{1}(A)
to denote the first Dirichlet eigenvalue of the fractional Laplacian.

3.
Quantitative maximum principles up to the boundary

The basic idea of the proof of ?THM?
is to apply the method of moving planes, as in the proof of the analogous rigidity result
[
Dip+22
, Theorem 1.4]
, but replace ‘qualitative’ maximum principles, such as the strong maximum principles, with ‘quantitative’ maximum principles, such as the Harnack inequality.

The purpose of this section is to prove several such quantitative maximum principles both in the non-antisymmetric and the antisymmetric setting. In particular, we require that these maximum principles hold up to the boundary of regions with very little boundary regularity. Moreover, we are careful to keep track of precisely how constants depend on relevant quantities since we believe that this may be useful in future analyses of the nonlocal parallel surface problem.

The section is split into two parts: maximum principles for equations without any antisymmetry assumptions and maximum principles for equations with antisymmetry assumptions.

3.1.
Equations without antisymmetry

In this subsection, we will give several maximum principles for linear equations with zero-th order terms without any antisymmetry assumptions. This will culminate in a quantitative analogue of the Hopf lemma for non-negative supersolutions of general semilinear equations (see ?THM?
below).

Our first result is as follows:

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
\Omega
be an bounded open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
c\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
, and
g\in L^{2}(\Omega)
with
g\geqslant 0
. Suppose that
u
satisfies

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{r@{ \ }l @{\quad\: \;} l}(-\Delta)^{s}u+cu&\geqslant g\hfil\quad\>&\text{in }\Omega,\\
u&\geqslant 0\hfil\quad\>&\text{in }\mathbb{R}^{n}\end{array}\right.

in the weak sense.

Then,

\displaystyle u(x)

\displaystyle\geqslant C\Big(\essinf_{\Omega}g+\|u\|_{L_{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\Big)\delta_{\partial\Omega}^{2s}(x)\qquad\text{for a.e. }x\in\Omega

with
C:=C(n,s)(\max\{1,\operatorname{diam}\Omega\})^{-n-2s}\big(1+(\operatorname{diam}\Omega)^{2s}\|c^{+}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\big)^{-1}
.

The proof of ?THM?
essentially follows by, at each point
x\in\Omega
, ‘touching’ the solution
u
from below by
\psi_{B}
(recall the notation in (
2
)) where
B
is the largest ball contained in
\Omega
and centred at
x
. This is similar to the proof of
[
RS19
, Theorem 2.2]
where a nonlinear interior analogue of ?THM?
was proven. To prove ?THM?
, however, some care must be taken since the touching point may occur on the boundary of
\Omega
(unlike in
the interior result of
[
RS19
, Theorem 2.2]
) where the PDE does not necessarily hold. Moreover, ?THM?
is stated in the context of weak solutions where pointwise techniques no longer make sense, so a simple mollification argument needs to be made. We require the following lemma.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\rho>0
,
c\in L^{\infty}(B_{\rho})
, and
g\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
be such that
g\geqslant 0
in
B_{\rho}
. Suppose that
u\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
satisfies

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{r@{ \ }l @{\quad\: \;} l}(-\Delta)^{s}u+cu&\geqslant g\hfil\quad\>&\text{in }B_{\rho},\\
u&\geqslant 0\hfil\quad\>&\text{in }\mathbb{R}^{n}.\end{array}\right.

Then,

\displaystyle u(x)\geqslant C\Big(\inf_{B_{\rho}}g+\|u\|_{L_{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\Big)\psi_{B_{\rho}}(x)\qquad\text{for all }x\in B_{\rho}

with
C:=C(n,s)(\max\{1,\rho\})^{-n-2s}\big(1+\rho^{2s}\|c^{+}\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{\rho})}\big)^{-1}
.

We observe that Lemma
can be seen as a quantitative version of a strong maximum principle (which can be proved directly):
in particular, it entails that if
u(x_{0})=0
for some
x_{0}\in\Omega
, then
u
vanishes identically.

Proof of Lemma
.

We will begin by proving the case
\rho=1
. Fix
\varepsilon>0
small and let
\tau\geqslant 0
be the largest value such that
u\geqslant\tau\psi_{B_{1-\varepsilon}}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Now, by the definition of
\tau
, there exists
x_{0}\in B_{1-\varepsilon}
such that
u(x_{0})=\tau\psi_{B_{1-\varepsilon}}(x_{0})
. On one hand,

\displaystyle(-\Delta)^{s}(u-\tau\psi_{B_{1-\varepsilon}})(x_{0})+c(x_{0})(u-\tau\psi_{B_{1-\varepsilon}})(x_{0})

\displaystyle\geqslant\inf_{B_{1}}g-\tau\big(1+c(x_{0})\psi_{B_{1-\varepsilon}}(x_{0})\big)

\displaystyle\geqslant\inf_{B_{1}}g-C(n,s)\big(1+\|c^{+}\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1})}\big)\tau.

On the other hand, since
|x_{0}-y|^{-n-2s}\geqslant C(n,s)(1+|y|^{n+2s})^{-1}
, we have that

\displaystyle(-\Delta)^{s}(u-\tau\psi_{B_{1-\varepsilon}})(x_{0})+c(x_{0})(u-\tau\psi_{B_{1-\varepsilon}})(x_{0})

\displaystyle=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{(u-\tau\psi_{B_{1-\varepsilon}})(y)}{|x_{0}-y|^{n+2s}}\mathop{}\!dy

\displaystyle\leqslant-C(n,s)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{(u-\tau\psi_{B_{1-\varepsilon}})(y)}{1+|y|^{n+2s}}\mathop{}\!dy

\displaystyle\leqslant-C(n,s)\big(\|u\|_{L_{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}-\tau\big).

Rearranging gives
\tau\geqslant C(n,s)\big(1+\|c^{+}\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1})}\big)^{-1}\big(\inf_{B_{1}}g+\|u\|_{L_{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\big)
, which implies that

\displaystyle u(x)

\displaystyle\geqslant C(n,s)\big(1+\|c^{+}\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1})}\big)^{-1}\Big(\inf_{B_{1}}g+\|u\|_{L_{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\Big)\psi_{B_{1-\varepsilon}}.

Sending
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
gives the result when
\rho=1
.

Now, for the case that
\rho
is not necessarily 1, let
u_{\rho}(x):=u(\rho x)
. Then

\displaystyle(-\Delta)^{s}u_{\rho}+\rho^{2s}c_{\rho}

\displaystyle\geqslant\rho^{2s}g_{\rho}\qquad\text{in }B_{1}

where
c_{\rho}(x):=c(\rho x)
and
g_{\rho}(x):=g(\rho x)
, so, for all
x\in B_{\rho}
, we have that

\displaystyle u(x)

\displaystyle=u_{\rho}(x/\rho)

\displaystyle\geqslant C(n,s)\big(1+\rho^{2s}\|c^{+}_{\rho}\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1})}\big)^{-1}\Big(\rho^{2s}\inf_{B_{1}}g_{\rho}+\|u_{\rho}\|_{L_{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\Big)\psi_{B_{1}}(x/\rho)

\displaystyle=C(n,s)\rho^{-2s}\big(1+\rho^{2s}\|c^{+}\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{\rho})}\big)^{-1}\Big(\rho^{2s}\inf_{B_{\rho}}g+\|u_{\rho}\|_{L_{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\Big)\psi_{B_{\rho}}(x).

Finally,

\displaystyle\|u_{\rho}\|_{L_{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{|u(\rho x)|}{1+|x|^{n+2s}}\mathop{}\!dx=\rho^{2s}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{|u(x)|}{\rho^{n+2s}+|x|^{n+2s}}\mathop{}\!dx\geqslant\rho^{2s}(\max\{1,\rho\})^{-n-2s}\|u\|_{L_{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}

which completes the proof.
∎

We can now give the proof of ?THM?
.

Proof of ?THM?
.

If
u,g\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
then the proof follows immediately from ?THM?
. Indeed, let
x\in\Omega
be arbitrary. Then, applying ?THM?
in the ball
B_{\rho}(x)
with
\rho:=\delta_{\partial\Omega}(x)
, we obtain that

\displaystyle u(y)

\displaystyle\geqslant C\Big(\essinf_{\Omega}g+\|u\|_{L_{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\Big)\psi_{B_{\rho}}(y)\qquad\text{for all }y\in B_{\rho}(x)

with

. Substituting

completes the proof.

In the general case that
u\in H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, if
u_{\varepsilon}
and
g_{\varepsilon}
are mollifications of

and

respectively then

in

. Then, applying the result for smooth compactly supported functions, we have that

with

as in the statement of Proposition
(and, in particular, uniformly bounded in
\varepsilon
). Moreover, given
x\in\Omega_{\varepsilon}
, if
y\in\partial\Omega_{\varepsilon}
is the closest point to
x
on
\partial\Omega_{\varepsilon}
and

is the closest point to
y
on
\partial\Omega
then

\displaystyle\delta_{\partial\Omega}(x)\leqslant|x-z|\leqslant|x-y|+|y-z|\leqslant\delta_{\partial\Omega_{\varepsilon}}(x)+\varepsilon,

so

in

a.e., so sending

∎

Next, we obtain a refined version of ?THM?
when
\Omega
satisfies the uniform interior ball condition.
As customary, we say that a bounded domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
satisfies the
uniform interior ball condition
with radius

if for every point

there exists a ball

of radius

such that its closure intersects
\partial\Omega
only at

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
\Omega
be an open bounded subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
C^{1}
boundary and satisfying the uniform interior ball condition with radius
r_{\Omega}>0
,
c\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
, and
g\in L^{2}(\Omega)
with
g\geqslant 0
. Suppose that
u
satisfies

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{r@{ \ }l @{\quad\: \;} l}(-\Delta)^{s}u+cu&\geqslant g\hfil\quad\>&\text{in }\Omega,\\
u&\geqslant 0\hfil\quad\>&\text{in }\mathbb{R}^{n}\end{array}\right.

in the weak sense.

Then,

\displaystyle u(x)

\displaystyle\geqslant C\Big(\essinf_{\Omega}g+\|u\|_{L_{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\Big)\delta_{\partial\Omega}^{s}(x)\qquad\text{for a.e. }x\in\Omega

with
C:=C(n,s)r_{\Omega}^{s}(\max\{1,\operatorname{diam}\Omega\})^{-n-2s}\big(1+(\operatorname{diam}\Omega)^{2s}\|c^{+}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\big)^{-1}
.

Proof.

By an analogous argument to the one in the proof of ?THM?
, we may assume that
u,g\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Let
x\in\Omega
be arbitrary. If
\delta_{\partial\Omega}(x)\geqslant r_{\Omega}
then we are done by ?THM?
, so let
\delta_{\partial\Omega}(x)<r_{\Omega}
. If
\bar{x}
denotes the closest point to
x
on
\partial\Omega
then there exists a ball
B=B_{r_{\Omega}}(x_{0})
such that
x\in B
,
B\subset\Omega
, and
B
touches
\partial\Omega
at
\bar{x}
. Then, applying ?THM?
in
B
, we have that
u(y)\geqslant C\big(\essinf_{\Omega}g+\|u\|_{L_{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\big)\psi_{B_{r_{\Omega}}}(y)

with
C=C(n,s)(\max\{1,\operatorname{diam}\Omega\})^{-n-2s}\big(1+(\operatorname{diam}\Omega)^{2s}\|c^{+}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\big)^{-1}
. Substituting
y=x
, we obtain

\displaystyle u(x)

\displaystyle\geqslant C\Big(\essinf_{\Omega}g+\|u\|_{L_{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\Big)(r_{\Omega}^{2}-|x-x_{0}|^{2})^{s}

\displaystyle=C\Big(\essinf_{\Omega}g+\|u\|_{L_{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\Big)(r_{\Omega}+|x-x_{0}|)^{s}(r_{\Omega}-|x-x_{0}|)^{s}

\displaystyle\geqslant Cr_{\Omega}^{s}\Big(\essinf_{\Omega}g+\|u\|_{L_{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\Big)(r_{\Omega}-|x-x_{0}|)^{s}.

Observing that
r_{\Omega}-|x-x_{0}|=\delta_{\partial\Omega}(x)
completes the proof.
∎

From ?THM?
, we immediately obtain the following corollary.

Corollary 3.4
.

Let
\Omega
be an open bounded subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
C^{1}
boundary and satisfying the uniform interior ball condition with radius
r_{\Omega}>0
. Let
f\in C^{0,1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\overline{\Omega}\times\mathbb{R})
satisfy
f_{0}:=\inf_{x\in\Omega}f(x,0)\geqslant 0
. Suppose that
u
satisfies

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{r@{ \ }l @{\quad\: \;} l}(-\Delta)^{s}u&\geqslant f(x,u)\hfil\quad\>&\text{in }\Omega,\\
u&\geqslant 0\hfil\quad\>&\text{in }\mathbb{R}^{n}\end{array}\right.

in the weak sense.

Then,

\displaystyle u(x)

\displaystyle\geqslant C_{\ast}\big(f_{0}+\|u\|_{L_{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\big)\delta_{\partial\Omega}^{s}(x)\qquad\text{for a.e. }x\in\Omega

with
C_{\ast}:=C(n,s)r_{\Omega}^{s}(\max\{1,\operatorname{diam}\Omega\})^{-n-2s}\big(1+(\operatorname{diam}\Omega)^{2s}[f]_{C^{0,1}(\overline{\Omega}\times[0,\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}])}\big)^{-1}
.

Proof.

Since
f=f(x,z)
is locally lipschitz,
\partial_{z}f
exists a.e., so we may define

\displaystyle c(x):=-\int_{0}^{1}\partial_{z}f(x,tu(x))\mathop{}\!dt.

Observe that
\|c\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leqslant[f]_{C^{0,1}(\overline{\Omega}\times[0,\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}])}
. Then,
(-\Delta)^{s}u+cu\geqslant f(\cdot,0)
in
\Omega
in the weak sense, so the result follows by ?THM?
.
∎

Remark 3.5
.

One could also obtain an analogous result to ?THM?

without assuming that
\Omega
has
C^{1}
boundary and satisfies the uniform interior ball condition; this can be achieved by applying ?THM?
instead of ?THM?
.
∎

3.2.
Equations with antisymmetry

The purpose of this subsection is to prove the following proposition.

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
H\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a halfspace,
U
be an open subset of
H
, and
a\in H
and
\rho>0
such that
B_{\rho}(a)\cap H\subset U
. Moreover, let
c\in L^{\infty}(U)
be such that

(3.1)

\|c^{+}\|_{L^{\infty}(U)}<\lambda_{1}(B_{\rho}(a)\cap H).

Suppose that
v
is antisymmetric with respect to
\partial H
and satisfies

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{r@{ \ }l @{\quad\: \;} l}(-\Delta)^{s}v+cv&\geqslant 0\hfil\quad\>&\text{in }U,\\
v&\geqslant 0\hfil\quad\>&\text{in }H\end{array}\right.

in the weak sense.

If
K\subset H
is a non-empty open set that is disjoint from
B_{\rho}(a)
and

\inf_{{x\in K}\atop{y\in B_{\rho}(a)\cap H}}|Q_{\partial H}(x)-y|^{-1}\geqslant M\geqslant 0

then

(3.2)

\displaystyle v(x)

\displaystyle\geqslant C\|\delta_{\partial H}v\|_{L^{1}(K)}\delta_{\partial H}(x)\qquad\text{for a.e. }x\in B_{\rho/2}(a)\cap H

with

\displaystyle C:=C(n,s)\rho^{2s}M^{n+2s+2}\Big(1+\rho^{2s}\|c^{+}\|_{L^{\infty}(U)}\Big)^{-1}.

?THM?
can be viewed as a quantitative version of Proposition 3.3 in
[
FJ15
]
. A similar result was also obtained in
[
Cir+23
]
in the case
c=0
. One advantage that
?THM?
has over both of the results of
[
FJ15
]
and
[
Cir+23
]
is that it allows the ball
B_{\rho}(a)
to
go right up to and, indeed, overlap the plane of symmetry
\partial H
. To allow for this possibility required the construction of an antisymmetric barrier given in ?THM?
below.

However, a disadvantage of ?THM?
is that it is not a boundary estimate, in the sense that (
3.2
) holds in
B_{\rho/2}(a)\cap H
and not
B_{\rho}(a)\cap H
, so it does not give any information up to
\partial U\setminus\partial H
. This is also a by-product of the barrier given in ?THM?
. In theory, one should be able to fix this issue by adjusting the barrier to behave like distance to the power
s
close to the boundary of its support, but this was unnecessary for the purposes of our results.

To prove ?THM?
, we require two lemmata.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
v\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
be antisymmetric with respect to
\{x_{1}=0\}
.

Then,
(-\Delta)^{s}v
is a smooth function in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
that is antisymmetric with respect to
\{x_{1}=0\}
. Furthermore, if
w(x):=v(x)/x_{1}
then

(3.3)

\displaystyle|(-\Delta)^{s}v(x)|

\displaystyle\leqslant C\Big(\|w\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}+\|x_{1}^{-1}\partial_{1}w\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}+\|D^{2}w\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\Big)x_{1}

for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
. The constant
C
depends only on
n
and
s
.

Proof.

The proof that
(-\Delta)^{s}v
is a smooth function and antisymmetric follows from standard properties of the fractional Laplacian, so we will omit it and focus on the proof of (
3.3
). For all
\tilde{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n+2}
, define
\tilde{w}(\tilde{x}):=w\big(\sqrt{\tilde{x}_{1}^{2}+\tilde{x}_{2}^{2}+\tilde{x}_{3}^{2}},\tilde{x}_{4},\dots,\tilde{x}_{n+2}\big)
. By Bochner’s relation, we have that

(3.4)

\displaystyle(-\Delta)^{s}v(x)

\displaystyle=x_{1}(-\Delta)^{s}_{\mathbb{R}^{n+2}}\tilde{w}(x_{1},0,0,x_{2},\dots,x_{n})

where
(-\Delta)^{s}_{\mathbb{R}^{n+2}}
is the fractional Laplacian in
\mathbb{R}^{n+2}
(and
(-\Delta)^{s}
still refers to the fractional Laplacian in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
). For more details regarding Bochner’s relation and a proof of (
3.4
), we refer the interested reader to the upcoming note
[
Dip+23
]
.

Thus, applying standard estimates for the fractional Laplacian, we have that,

\displaystyle|(-\Delta)^{s}v(x)|

\displaystyle\leqslant C\Big(\|D^{2}\tilde{w}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n+2})}+\|\tilde{w}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n+2})}\Big)x_{1}

for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
and
C>0
depending on
n
and
s
. Clearly,
\|\tilde{w}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n+2})}\leqslant\|w\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}
. Moreover, by a direct computation, one can check that

\displaystyle\partial_{ij}\tilde{w}(\tilde{x})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\partial_{11}w\Big(\sqrt{\tilde{x}_{1}^{2}+\tilde{x}_{2}^{2}+\tilde{x}_{3}^{2}},\tilde{x}_{4},\dots,\tilde{x}_{n+2}\Big)\frac{\tilde{x}_{i}\tilde{x}_{j}}{\tilde{x}_{1}^{2}+\tilde{x}_{2}^{2}+\tilde{x}_{3}^{2}}

\displaystyle+\partial_{1}w\Big(\sqrt{\tilde{x}_{1}^{2}+\tilde{x}_{2}^{2}+\tilde{x}_{3}^{2}},\tilde{x}_{4},\dots,\tilde{x}_{n+2}\Big)\left[\frac{\delta_{ij}}{\sqrt{\tilde{x}_{1}^{2}+\tilde{x}_{2}^{2}+\tilde{x}_{3}^{2}}}-\frac{\tilde{x}_{i}\tilde{x}_{j}}{(\tilde{x}_{1}^{2}+\tilde{x}_{2}^{2}+\tilde{x}_{3}^{2})^{3/2}}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad{\mbox{ if }}i,j\in\{1,2,3\},

\displaystyle\partial_{ij}\tilde{w}(\tilde{x})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\partial_{1j-2}w\Big(\sqrt{\tilde{x}_{1}^{2}+\tilde{x}_{2}^{2}+\tilde{x}_{3}^{2}},\tilde{x}_{4},\dots,\tilde{x}_{n+2}\Big)\frac{\tilde{x}_{i}}{\sqrt{\tilde{x}_{1}^{2}+\tilde{x}_{2}^{2}+\tilde{x}_{3}^{2}}}

\displaystyle\qquad{\mbox{ if }}i\in\{1,2,3\}\;{\mbox{ and }}\;j\in\{4,\dots,n+2\},

\displaystyle\partial_{ij}\tilde{w}(\tilde{x})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\partial_{i-2j-2}w\Big(\sqrt{\tilde{x}_{1}^{2}+\tilde{x}_{2}^{2}+\tilde{x}_{3}^{2}},\tilde{x}_{4},\dots,\tilde{x}_{n+2}\Big)\qquad{\mbox{ if }}i,j\in\{4,\dots,n+2\},

and therefore

\displaystyle\|D^{2}\tilde{w}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n+2})}

\displaystyle\leqslant C\Big(\|x_{1}^{-1}\partial_{1}w\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}+\|D^{2}w\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\Big)

for some universal constant
C>0
, which implies the desired result.
∎

We now construct the barrier that will be essential to allow

in ?THM?

Lemma 3.8

Let

Now, we will give the proof of ?THM?

Proof of ?THM?

Without loss of generality, we may assume that

, we immediately obtain the proof of the first part of ?THM?
:

Corollary 7.3
.

Suppose that
n\geqslant 2
is an integer,
s\in(0,1)
,
\varepsilon>0
,
and
\Omega_{\varepsilon}
be as in (
7.1
).

Then, for
0<\varepsilon<1/4
, there exists a 1-parameter family
G_{\varepsilon}\subset\Omega_{\varepsilon}
with smooth boundary such that

\displaystyle G_{\varepsilon}+B_{1/2}=\Omega_{\varepsilon}.

Proof.

It is easy to check that
\Omega_{\varepsilon}
satisfies the uniform interior ball condition with uniform radius given by
r_{\Omega_{\varepsilon}}=(1+\varepsilon)^{-1}
. Hence, since
\varepsilon<1/4
, we have that
r_{\Omega_{\varepsilon}}>1/2
, so if
G_{\varepsilon}:=\Omega_{\varepsilon}^{\rho}
with
\rho=1/2
(i.e.
G_{\varepsilon}:=\{x\in\Omega_{\varepsilon}\text{ s.t. }\operatorname{dist}(x,\partial\Omega_{\varepsilon})>1/2\}
) then ?THM?
implies that
G_{\varepsilon}
satisfies all the required properties.
∎

The remainder of this section will be spent proving the second part of ?THM?
. In theory, this is relatively simple: since
\Omega_{\varepsilon}
is an ellipsoid, the solution to the torsion problem (
7.1
) is known explicitly, see
[
AJS21
]
, and is given by

\displaystyle u_{\varepsilon}(x):=\gamma_{n,s,\varepsilon}\bigg(1-\frac{x_{1}^{2}}{(1+\varepsilon)^{2}}-|x^{\prime}|^{2}\bigg)^{s}_{+}

where

\displaystyle\gamma_{n,s,\varepsilon}:=\frac{\gamma_{n,s}}{(1+\varepsilon){}_{2}F_{1}\big(\frac{n+2s}{2},\frac{1}{2};\frac{n}{2};1-(1+\varepsilon)^{2}\big)}

and
\gamma_{n,s}
is given by (
7.5
).

Here above
{}_{2}F_{1}
is the Hypergeometric function, see
[
AS92
]
for more details. Note that

{}_{2}F_{1}(a,b;c;0)=1
, so
\gamma_{n,s,0}=\gamma_{n,s}
, and
\gamma_{n,s}
is precisely the constant for which
u_{0}(x):=\gamma_{n,s}(1-|x|^{2})^{s}_{+}
satisfies
(-\Delta)^{s}u_{0}=1
in
B_{1}
.

Now, it is easy to check that
\rho(\Omega_{\varepsilon})=\varepsilon
, so to prove the second part of ?THM?
, one simply has to find the first order expansion of
[u_{\varepsilon}]_{\partial G_{\varepsilon}}
in
\varepsilon
. However, in practice, this is not trivial for a few reasons: one, parametrizations of
G_{\varepsilon}
are algebraically complicated; two,
[u_{\varepsilon}]_{\partial G_{\varepsilon}}
is defined in terms of a supremum, which, in general, does not commute well with limits; and three, the supremum is over a quotient whose numerator and denominator both depend on
\varepsilon
and whose denominator is not bounded away from zero.

We will now state a technical result that will allow us to postpone addressing these difficulties and to proceed directly to the proof of ?THM?
.

Lemma 7.4
.

Let
\varepsilon\in[0,1)
and define
a_{\varepsilon},b_{\varepsilon}:[0,+\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
and
\phi_{\varepsilon}:B_{1}^{n-1}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
as follows

(7.6)

\begin{split}a_{\varepsilon}(\tau):=\;&1+\varepsilon-\frac{1}{2\sqrt{1+\big((1+\varepsilon)^{2}-1\big)\tau^{2}}},\\
b_{\varepsilon}(\tau):=\;&1-\frac{1+\varepsilon}{2\sqrt{1+\big((1+\varepsilon)^{2}-1\big)\tau^{2}}},\\
{\mbox{and}}\qquad\phi_{\varepsilon}(r):=\;&\Big(a_{\varepsilon}(|r|)\sqrt{1-|r|^{2}},b_{\varepsilon}(|r|)r\Big).\end{split}

Then,

\displaystyle\bigg|

\displaystyle\frac{|(u_{\varepsilon}\circ\phi_{\varepsilon})(r)-(u_{\varepsilon}\circ\phi_{\varepsilon})(\tilde{r})|}{\varepsilon|\phi_{\varepsilon}(r)-\phi_{\varepsilon}(\tilde{r})|}-\frac{1}{2}s\gamma_{n,s}\left(\frac{3}{4}\right)^{s-1}\frac{\big||r|^{2}-|\tilde{r}|^{2}\big|}{|\phi_{0}(r)-\phi_{0}(\tilde{r})|}\bigg|\leqslant C\varepsilon.

The constant
C>0
depends only on
n
and
s
.

In the statement of ?THM?
and in what follows, we use the notation
B^{n-1}_{1}
for the unit ball in
\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
.

We will withhold the proof of ?THM?
until the end of the section. Given ?THM?
, we can now complete the proof of ?THM?
.

Proof ?THM?
.

The proof of the first statement regarding the existence of
G_{\varepsilon}
is the subject of ?THM?
, so we will focus on proving (
7.4
). Since the ellipsoid is symmetric with respect to reflections across the plane
\{x_{1}=0\}
, it follows that
G_{\varepsilon}
is too. Hence, we have that

\displaystyle[u_{\varepsilon}]_{\partial G_{\varepsilon}}

\displaystyle=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y\in\partial G_{\varepsilon}^{+}\\
x\neq y\end{subarray}}\bigg\{\frac{|u_{\varepsilon}(x)-u_{\varepsilon}(y)|}{|x-y|}\bigg\}.

Here, we are using the notation that, given
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
A^{+}:=A\cap\{x_{1}>0\}
.

Next, the upper half ellipsoid
\Omega_{\varepsilon}^{+}
can be parametrized by
\bar{\phi}_{\varepsilon}:B_{1}^{n-1}\to\Omega_{\varepsilon}^{+}
defined
by
\bar{\phi}_{\varepsilon}(r):=((1+\varepsilon)\sqrt{1-|r|^{2}},r)
. Moreover, the outward pointing normal to
B_{1}
is given by

\displaystyle\nu_{\varepsilon}(x)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\frac{x_{1}^{2}}{(1+\varepsilon)^{4}}+|x^{\prime}|^{2}}}\bigg(\frac{x_{1}}{(1+\varepsilon)^{2}},x^{\prime}\bigg),

so a parametrization of
\partial G_{\varepsilon}^{+}
is
\phi_{\varepsilon}:B_{1}^{n-1}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
defined by
\phi_{\varepsilon}:=\bar{\phi}_{\varepsilon}-\frac{1}{2}(\nu_{\varepsilon}\circ\bar{\phi}_{\varepsilon})
, that is,

\displaystyle\phi_{\varepsilon}(r)

\displaystyle=\big((1+\varepsilon)\sqrt{1-|r|^{2}},r\big)-\frac{1+\varepsilon}{2\sqrt{1+((1+\varepsilon)^{2}-1)|r|^{2}}}\bigg(\frac{\sqrt{1-|r|^{2}}}{1+\varepsilon},r\bigg)

\displaystyle=\big(a_{\varepsilon}(|r|)\sqrt{1-|r|^{2}},b_{\varepsilon}(|r|)r\big)

in the notation of ?THM?
.

Hence,

\displaystyle[u_{\varepsilon}]_{\partial G_{\varepsilon}}

\displaystyle=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}r,\tilde{r}\in B_{1}^{n-1}\\
r\neq\tilde{r}\end{subarray}}\left\{\frac{|(u_{\varepsilon}\circ\phi_{\varepsilon})(r)-(u_{\varepsilon}\circ\phi_{\varepsilon})(\tilde{r})|}{|\phi_{\varepsilon}(r)-\phi_{\varepsilon}(\tilde{r})|}\right\}.

Next, using ?THM?
and the second triangle inequality in the
\sup
norm (over
r,\tilde{r}\in B_{1}^{n-1}
), we obtain that

\displaystyle\bigg|\frac{[u_{\varepsilon}]_{\partial G_{\varepsilon}}}{\varepsilon}-

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}s\gamma_{n,s}\left(\frac{3}{4}\right)^{s-1}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}r,\tilde{r}\in B_{1}^{n-1}\\
r\neq\tilde{r}\end{subarray}}\bigg\{\frac{\big||r|^{2}-|\tilde{r}|^{2}\big|}{|\phi_{0}(r)-\phi_{0}(\tilde{r})|}\bigg\}\bigg|

\displaystyle\leqslant\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}r,\tilde{r}\in B_{1}^{n-1}\\
r\neq\tilde{r}\end{subarray}}\bigg|\frac{|(u_{\varepsilon}\circ\phi_{\varepsilon})(r)-(u_{\varepsilon}\circ\phi_{\varepsilon})(\tilde{r})|}{\varepsilon|\phi_{\varepsilon}(r)-\phi_{\varepsilon}(\tilde{r})|}-\frac{1}{2}s\gamma_{n,s}\left(\frac{3}{4}\right)^{s-1}\frac{\big||r|^{2}-|\tilde{r}|^{2}\big|}{|\phi_{0}(r)-\phi_{0}(\tilde{r})|}\bigg|

\displaystyle\leqslant C\varepsilon,

so, by sending
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
, we find that

(7.7)

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\frac{[u_{\varepsilon}]_{\partial G_{\varepsilon}}}{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}s\gamma_{n,s}\left(\frac{3}{4}\right)^{s-1}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}r,\tilde{r}\in B_{1}^{n-1}\\
r\neq\tilde{r}\end{subarray}}\bigg\{\frac{\big||r|^{2}-|\tilde{r}|^{2}\big|}{|\phi_{0}(r)-\phi_{0}(\tilde{r})|}\bigg\}.

Finally, we claim that

(7.8)

\displaystyle\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}r,\tilde{r}\in B_{1}^{n-1}\\
r\neq\tilde{r}\end{subarray}}\bigg\{\frac{\big||r|^{2}-|\tilde{r}|^{2}\big|}{|\phi_{0}(r)-\phi_{0}(\tilde{r})|}\bigg\}=2.

Indeed, if

\displaystyle Q:=\frac{\big(|r|^{2}-|\tilde{r}|^{2}\big)^{2}}{|\phi_{0}(r)-\phi_{0}(\tilde{r})|^{2}}

then

\displaystyle Q=\frac{4\big(|r|^{2}-|\tilde{r}|^{2}\big)^{2}}{\big(\sqrt{1-|r|^{2}}-\sqrt{1-|\tilde{r}|^{2}}\big)^{2}+|r-\tilde{r}|^{2}}\leqslant\frac{4\big(|r|^{2}-|\tilde{r}|^{2}\big)^{2}}{(\sqrt{1-|r|^{2}}-\sqrt{1-|\tilde{r}|^{2}})^{2}+\big(|r|-|\tilde{r}|\big)^{2}}.

Next, let
t,\tilde{t}\in(0,\pi/2)
be such that
|r|=\cos t
and
|\tilde{r}|=\cos\tilde{t}
. Then

\displaystyle Q

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{4(\cos^{2}t-\cos^{2}\tilde{t})^{2}}{(\sin t-\sin\tilde{t})^{2}+(\cos t-\cos\tilde{t})^{2}}.

Then, via elementary trigonometric identities,

\displaystyle(\cos^{2}t-\cos^{2}\tilde{t})^{2}=4\sin^{2}(t+\tilde{t})\sin^{2}\bigg(\frac{t-\tilde{t}}{2}\bigg)\cos^{2}\bigg(\frac{t-\tilde{t}}{2}\bigg)

and

\displaystyle(\sin t-\sin\tilde{t})^{2}+(\cos t-\cos\tilde{t})^{2}=4\sin^{2}\bigg(\frac{t-\tilde{t}}{2}\bigg),

so

\displaystyle Q\leqslant 4\sin^{2}(t+\tilde{t})\cos^{2}\bigg(\frac{t-\tilde{t}}{2}\bigg)\leqslant 4,

which shows that the left-hand side of (
7.8
) is less than or equal to the right-hand side of (
7.8
). To show that the reverse inequality is also true, consider
r:=e_{1}/\sqrt{2}
and
\tilde{r}:=(1/\sqrt{2}+h)e_{1}
. Then

\displaystyle\frac{\big||r|^{2}-|\tilde{r}|^{2}\big|}{|\phi_{0}(r)-\phi_{0}(\tilde{r})|}

\displaystyle=\frac{2|h|\left(h+\sqrt{2}\right)}{\sqrt{1-h\sqrt{2}-\sqrt{1-2\sqrt{2}h-2h^{2}}}}\to 2

as
h\to 0
which completes the proof of (
7.8
). Thus, recalling that
\rho(\Omega_{\varepsilon})=\varepsilon
, (
7.4
) follows from (
7.7
) and (
7.8
).
∎

We will now give the proof of ?THM?
. We will do this over two lemmata.

Lemma 7.5
.

Let
\varepsilon\in[0,1)
and
a_{\varepsilon}
,
b_{\varepsilon}
, and
\phi_{\varepsilon}
be as in ?THM?
.

Then, for all
r,\tilde{r}\in B_{1}^{n-1}
with
r\neq\tilde{r}
,

(7.9)

\displaystyle\bigg|\frac{|\phi_{0}(r)-\phi_{0}(\tilde{r})|}{|\phi_{\varepsilon}(r)-\phi_{\varepsilon}(\tilde{r})|}-1\bigg|\leqslant C\varepsilon.

The constant
C>0
depends only on
n
.

Proof.

Observe that

(7.10)

\begin{split}\bigg|\frac{|\phi_{0}(r)-\phi_{0}(\tilde{r})|}{|\phi_{\varepsilon}(r)-\phi_{\varepsilon}(\tilde{r})|}-1\bigg|&=\bigg|\frac{|\phi_{0}(r)-\phi_{0}(\tilde{r})|-|\phi_{\varepsilon}(r)-\phi_{\varepsilon}(\tilde{r})|}{|\phi_{\varepsilon}(r)-\phi_{\varepsilon}(\tilde{r})|}\bigg|\\
&\leqslant\frac{|(\phi_{0}-\phi_{\varepsilon})(r)-(\phi_{0}-\phi_{\varepsilon})(\tilde{r})|}{|\phi_{\varepsilon}(r)-\phi_{\varepsilon}(\tilde{r})|}.\end{split}

Next, observe that

\displaystyle\phi_{0}(r)

\displaystyle=A_{\varepsilon}(|r|)\phi_{\varepsilon}(r)

where, for each
\tau\in\mathbb{R}
,
A_{\varepsilon}(\tau)
is the
n\times n
matrix given by

\displaystyle A_{\varepsilon}(\tau)

\displaystyle:=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{2a_{\varepsilon}(\tau)}&0\\
0&\frac{1}{2b_{\varepsilon}(\tau)}I_{n-1}\end{pmatrix}.

Here
I_{k}
denotes the
k\times k
identity matrix and we are thinking of
\phi_{\varepsilon}
and
\phi_{0}
as column vectors.

It will be useful in the future to note that
a_{\varepsilon}
and
b_{\varepsilon}
are strictly increasing for
\tau\geqslant 0
(see (
7.16
) below for
a_{\varepsilon}
, the computation for
b_{\varepsilon}
is analogous), so, for all
\tau\in[0,1)
,

(7.11)

\begin{split}&\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon\leqslant a_{\varepsilon}(\tau)\leqslant 1+\varepsilon-\frac{1}{2(1+\varepsilon)}\leqslant\frac{1}{2}+C\varepsilon\\
{\mbox{and }}\quad&\frac{1}{2}(1-\varepsilon)\leqslant b_{\varepsilon}(\tau)\leqslant\frac{1}{2}.\end{split}

In particular, when
\varepsilon
is small,
A_{\varepsilon}(\tau)
is well-defined since
a_{\varepsilon},b_{\varepsilon}>0
. Hence, it follow from (
7.10
) that

(7.12)

\begin{split}&\bigg|\frac{|\phi_{0}(r)-\phi_{0}(\tilde{r})|}{|\phi_{\varepsilon}(r)-\phi_{\varepsilon}(\tilde{r})|}-1\bigg|\\
\leqslant\;&\frac{|(I_{n}-A_{\varepsilon}(|r|))\phi_{\varepsilon}(r)-(I_{n}-A_{\varepsilon}(|\tilde{r}|))\phi_{\varepsilon}(\tilde{r})|}{|\phi_{\varepsilon}(r)-\phi_{\varepsilon}(\tilde{r})|}\\
\leqslant\;&\frac{|(I_{n}-A_{\varepsilon}(|r|))\phi_{\varepsilon}(r)-(I_{n}-A_{\varepsilon}(|r|))\phi_{\varepsilon}(\tilde{r})|}{|\phi_{\varepsilon}(r)-\phi_{\varepsilon}(\tilde{r})|}+\frac{|(A_{\varepsilon}(|\tilde{r}|)-A_{\varepsilon}(|r|))\phi_{\varepsilon}(\tilde{r})|}{|\phi_{\varepsilon}(r)-\phi_{\varepsilon}(\tilde{r})|}\\
\leqslant\;&\|I_{n}-A_{\varepsilon}(|r|)\|_{\textrm{OP}}+\frac{\|A_{\varepsilon}(|\tilde{r}|)-A_{\varepsilon}(|r|)\|_{\mathrm{OP}}}{|\phi_{\varepsilon}(r)-\phi_{\varepsilon}(\tilde{r})|}|\phi_{\varepsilon}(\tilde{r})|,\end{split}

where
\|\cdot\|_{\mathrm{OP}}
denotes the matrix operator norm.

It follows from (
7.11
) that

(7.13)

\displaystyle\|I_{n}-A_{\varepsilon}(\tau)\|_{\textrm{OP}}

\displaystyle=\max\bigg\{\bigg|1-\frac{1}{2a_{\varepsilon}(\tau)}\bigg|,\bigg|1-\frac{1}{2b_{\varepsilon}(\tau)}\bigg|\bigg\}\leqslant C\varepsilon.

Moreover, if
\|\cdot\|_{\mathrm{F}}
denotes the Frobenius norm, then

\displaystyle\|A_{\varepsilon}(|\tilde{r}|)-A_{\varepsilon}(|r|)\|_{\mathrm{OP}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant C\|A_{\varepsilon}(|\tilde{r}|)-A_{\varepsilon}(|r|)\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}

\displaystyle=C\left[\left(\frac{1}{a_{\varepsilon}(|r|)}-\frac{1}{a_{\varepsilon}(|\tilde{r}|)}\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{1}{b_{\varepsilon}(|r|)}-\frac{1}{b_{\varepsilon}(|\tilde{r}|)}\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=C\left(\frac{\big(a_{\varepsilon}(|r|)-a_{\varepsilon}(|\tilde{r}|)\big)^{2}}{a_{\varepsilon}(|r|)^{2}a_{\varepsilon}(|\tilde{r}|)^{2}}+\frac{\big(b_{\varepsilon}(|r|)-b_{\varepsilon}(|\tilde{r}|)\big)^{2}}{b_{\varepsilon}(|r|)^{2}b_{\varepsilon}(|\tilde{r}|)^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant C\Big(\big(a_{\varepsilon}(|r|)-a_{\varepsilon}(|\tilde{r}|)\big)^{2}+\big(b_{\varepsilon}(|r|)-b_{\varepsilon}(|\tilde{r}|)\big)^{2}\Big),

where we used (
7.11
) to obtain the final inequality.

We also have that

\displaystyle|\phi_{\varepsilon}(r)-\phi_{\varepsilon}(\tilde{r})|^{2}

\displaystyle=\Big(a_{\varepsilon}(|r|)\sqrt{1-|r|^{2}}-a_{\varepsilon}(|\tilde{r}|)\sqrt{1-|\tilde{r}|^{2}}\Big)^{2}+|b_{\varepsilon}(|r|)r-b_{\varepsilon}(|\tilde{r}|)\tilde{r}|^{2}

\displaystyle\geqslant\Big(a_{\varepsilon}(|r|)\sqrt{1-|r|^{2}}-a_{\varepsilon}(|\tilde{r}|)\sqrt{1-|\tilde{r}|^{2}}\Big)^{2}+\big(b_{\varepsilon}(|r|)|r|-b_{\varepsilon}(|\tilde{r}|)|\tilde{r}|\big)^{2}.

We claim that

(7.14)

\begin{split}&\big|a_{\varepsilon}(|r|)-a_{\varepsilon}(|\tilde{r}|)\big|\leqslant C\varepsilon\big|a_{\varepsilon}(|r|)\sqrt{1-|r|^{2}}-a_{\varepsilon}(|\tilde{r}|)\sqrt{1-|\tilde{r}|^{2}}\big|\\
\text{and}\qquad&\big|b_{\varepsilon}(|r|)-b_{\varepsilon}(|\tilde{r}|)\big|\leqslant C\varepsilon\big|b_{\varepsilon}(|r|)|r|-b_{\varepsilon}(|\tilde{r}|)|\tilde{r}|\big|.\end{split}

We will prove the inequality in (
7.14
) involving
a_{\varepsilon}
, the proof of the inequality involving
b_{\varepsilon}
is entirely analogous. Let
\bar{a}_{\varepsilon}(\tau):=a_{\varepsilon}(\tau)\sqrt{1-\tau^{2}}
. Then, by Cauchy’s Mean Value Theorem,

(7.15)

\displaystyle\bigg|\frac{a_{\varepsilon}(|r|)-a_{\varepsilon}(|\tilde{r}|)}{\bar{a}_{\varepsilon}(|r|)-\bar{a}_{\varepsilon}(|\tilde{r}|)}\bigg|

\displaystyle\leqslant\bigg\|\frac{a_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}}{\bar{a}_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}}\bigg\|_{L^{\infty}((0,1))}.

On one hand,

\displaystyle a^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}(\tau)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon(\varepsilon+2)\tau\big(1+\big((1+\varepsilon)^{2}-1\big)\tau^{2}\big)^{-3/2},

so, for all
\tau\in[0,1)
,

(7.16)

\displaystyle C^{-1}\varepsilon\tau\leqslant a^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}(\tau)\leqslant C\varepsilon\tau.

On the other hand, using (
7.11
) and (
7.16
), we have that

\displaystyle\bar{a}_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(\tau)

\displaystyle=a_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(\tau)\sqrt{1-\tau^{2}}-\frac{\tau a_{\varepsilon}(\tau)}{\sqrt{1-\tau^{2}}}\leqslant C(\varepsilon-1)\tau<0.

Hence,
|\bar{a}_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(\tau)|=-a_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(\tau)\geqslant C\tau
, so

\displaystyle\bigg|\frac{a_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(\tau)}{\bar{a}_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(\tau)}\bigg|

\displaystyle=\frac{a_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(\tau)}{|\bar{a}_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(\tau)|}\leqslant C\varepsilon,\qquad\text{for all }\tau\in(0,1),

which, along with (
7.15
), implies the inequality for
a_{\varepsilon}
in (
7.14
).

Gathering these pieces of information, we obtain that

(7.17)

\begin{split}\frac{\|A_{\varepsilon}(|\tilde{r}|)-A_{\varepsilon}(|r|)\|_{\mathrm{OP}}}{|\phi_{\varepsilon}(r)-\phi_{\varepsilon}(\tilde{r})|}\leqslant\;&\frac{C\sqrt{\big((a_{\varepsilon}(|r|)-a_{\varepsilon}(|\tilde{r}|)\big)^{2}+\big(b_{\varepsilon}(|r|)-b_{\varepsilon}(|\tilde{r}|)\big)^{2}}}{\sqrt{\Big(a_{\varepsilon}(|r|)\sqrt{1-|r|^{2}}-a_{\varepsilon}(|\tilde{r}|)\sqrt{1-|\tilde{r}|^{2}}\Big)^{2}+\big(b_{\varepsilon}(|r|)|r|-b_{\varepsilon}(|\tilde{r}|)|\tilde{r}|\big)^{2}}}\\
\leqslant\;&C\varepsilon.\end{split}

Finally, since
G_{\varepsilon}\subset\Omega_{\varepsilon}\subset B_{1+\varepsilon}
, we have that

(7.18)

\displaystyle|\phi_{\varepsilon}(\tilde{r})|

\displaystyle\leqslant 1+\varepsilon\leqslant C.

Thus, (
7.9
) follows from (
7.12
), (
7.13
), (
7.17
), and (
7.18
).
∎

\varepsilon\in[0,1)
and
a_{\varepsilon}
,

, and

Then, for all

depends only on

.

Proof.

Let

\displaystyle v_{\varepsilon}(x)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{\gamma_{n,s,\varepsilon}}u_{\varepsilon}(x)=\left(1-\frac{x_{1}^{2}}{(1+\varepsilon)^{2}}-|x^{\prime}|^{2}\right)^{s}

Exploiting the expressions of
a_{\varepsilon}
and
b_{\varepsilon}
in (
7.6

with respect to

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\tau}\psi_{\varepsilon}(\tau)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle s\left[-\frac{\tau^{3}(1+\varepsilon)\left(2\varepsilon+\varepsilon^{2}\right)\left(1-\frac{1+\varepsilon}{2\sqrt{1+\tau^{2}\left(2\varepsilon+\varepsilon^{2}\right)}}\right)}{\left(1+\tau^{2}\left(2\varepsilon+\varepsilon^{2}\right)\right)^{3/2}}+\frac{2\tau\left(1+\varepsilon-\frac{1}{2\sqrt{1+\tau^{2}\left(2\varepsilon+\varepsilon^{2}\right)}}\right)^{2}}{(1+\varepsilon)^{2}}\right.

\left\|\frac{d}{d\tau}\psi_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{L^{\infty}((0,1))}\leqslant C\varepsilon,

for some

. Not to interrupt the flow of the argument, we defer the proof of (

)

Now, we notice that
