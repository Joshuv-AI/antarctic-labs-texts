Universal properties of locally generated terahertz waveforms from polarization-controlled two- and multi-color ionizing fields
H. Alirezaee, S. Skupin, V. Vaicaitis, A. Demircan, I. Babushkin, Luc Bergé, U. Morgner

Abstract
The polarization states of terahertz (THz) radiation generated in a photo-ionized gas driven by strong two- or multi-frequency fields with locally controlled polarization are studied. We reveal a universal property of the resulting THz waveforms: the ellipticity of their polarization state increases linearly with the frequency. This “linear chirp of ellipticity” makes plasma-based THz generation unique among other THz sources. However, it also puts some constraints on the polarization properties of the generated THz radiation. We derive a general expression for the THz ellipticity and demonstrate how the polarization states of the generated THz waveforms can be manipulated and controlled by the polarization of the pump pulses.

[

,

,
24
,

(see also below). Although in general incommensurate combinations of frequencies can be used

\omega_{0}

,

, here we focus on the response of an infinitely small emitting volume of plasma. This corresponds to analyzing the elementary source term one would have to introduce into, e.g., Maxwell equations to address later a more complex propagation geometry.

The electric field of the pump pulse in the multi-color case, consisting of the fundamental harmonic at frequency
\omega_{0}
and their harmonics
m\omega_{0}
,
m=2,3,\ldots
is given by:

\begin{split}\mathrm{\mathbf{E}}(t)=\sum_{m\geq 1}f_{m\omega_{0}}(t)e_{m\omega_{0}}\Big[&{\cos{\left(m\omega_{0}t+\phi_{x,m\omega_{0}}\right)}\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}}\\
+{\epsilon}_{m\omega_{0}}&{\sin{\left(m\omega_{0}t+\phi_{y,m\omega_{0}}\right)}\mathrm{\mathbf{y}}}\Big].\end{split}

(1)

Here we assume laser field components polarized in
x
and
y
directions,
\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}
and
\mathrm{\mathbf{y}}
being the corresponding unit vectors,
e_{m\omega_{0}}
define the amplitudes of the
m
th harmonics,

define the slow envelopes of the pulses,
{\epsilon}_{m\omega_{0}}
determine their relative amplitudes and
\phi_{x,m\omega_{0}}

\phi_{y,m\omega_{0}}
the carrier envelope phases of
x
and
y
components.

The generation of the free electron density is described by the rate equation

{\partial}_{t}{\rho}_{e}\left(t\right)=W\left(t\right)\left[{\rho}_{\rm at}-{\rho}_{e}\left(t\right)\right],

(2)

with
{\rho}_{\rm at}
being the neutral density. For simplicity, the ionization rate
W\left(t\right)
can be evaluated from the quasi-static tunneling model
[
57
]
,

W(t)=\frac{\alpha}{|E(t)|}e^{-\frac{\beta}{|E(t)|}},

(3)

with constants
\alpha
and
\beta
characterizing the ionization potential. In the numerical simulations below we assume
argon (ionization energy of 15.6 eV).

The electrons released acquire a drift velocity
\mathrm{\mathbf{v}}(t,t^{\prime})=(q/m_{e})\int_{t^{\prime}}^{t}\vec{E}(t^{\prime\prime})dt^{\prime\prime}
, where
t^{\prime}
denotes the time when they were born and
q
denotes the electron charge. For simplicity, we assume that electrons are born with zero drift velocity, and collisions are neglected in our analytical approach. The macroscopic current density
{\mathrm{\mathbf{J}}}
is then given by

{\mathrm{\mathbf{J}}}(t)=q\int^{t}_{-\infty}\mathrm{\mathbf{v}}(t,t^{\prime})\;\left[\partial_{t^{\prime}}\rho_{e}(t^{\prime})\right]dt^{\prime}.

(4)

This net current density is responsible for the emission of the so-called Brunel radiation
{\mathrm{\mathbf{E}}}_{\mathrm{Br}}
, which can be estimated as

{{\mathrm{\mathbf{E}}}}_{\mathrm{Br}}(t)\propto{\partial}_{t}\mathrm{\mathbf{J}}=\frac{q^{2}}{m_{e}}{\rho_{e}{\mathrm{\mathbf{E}}}}.

(5)

This key equation is often referred to as the local current (LC) approximation. Note that this model does not take into account any propagation effects of the pump. However, it allows for a simple and vivid analysis of the locally generated THz waveforms. Propagation effects in more complex geometries can then be explained by superimposing these local contributions
[
27
,
26
,
32
,
54
,
55
]
,
provided that charge separation effects in the plasma can be neglected
[
58
]
. We mention that the LC model does not take into account any effects related to the electron deflection by the tail of the atomic Coulomb potential
[
8
]
.

II.2
Polarization analysis

As we shall see later, the polarization states of the emitted THz pulses are quite unusual, since they exhibit an “ellipticity chirp” (see Fig.
1
b). To describe this property, instead of considering the pump field
\mathrm{\mathbf{E}}(t)
and the Brunel radiation
\mathrm{\mathbf{E}}_{\mathrm{Br}}
in time, we operate with their Fourier components
\hat{\mathrm{\mathbf{E}}}(\omega)\propto\int\mathrm{\mathbf{E}}(t)\exp{-i\omega t)}\mathrm{d}t
, and analogous to this
\hat{\mathrm{\mathbf{E}}}_{\mathrm{Br}}(\omega)
.

Furthermore, we shall use the Jones formalism to describe our THz waveforms. To any (real-valued) monochromatic field
\mathrm{\mathbf{E}}(t)
oscillating at frequency
\omega
, we assign a complex vector

\mathrm{\mathbf{j}}
, describing its polarization state. For example, for the field
\mathrm{\mathbf{E}}(t)=(\cos{\omega t},\epsilon\sin{\omega t})
we consider its complex representation
\propto(1e^{-i\omega t},i\epsilon e^{-i\omega t})
, and
\mathrm{\mathbf{j}}
can be defined as the
pre-factors before the oscillating term, that is, for this example,
\mathrm{\mathbf{j}}=(1,i\epsilon)
. This complex vector is commonly represented as an “oriented ellipse” [see Fig.
2
and also Fig.
1
(a)] whose principal axis is generally rotated with respect to the
x
-axis of the laboratory coordinate system by some angle
\Psi
. The ellipticity of the polarization state
\epsilon
is defined as the ratio between the lengths of the minor and major axes, that is,
|\epsilon|=\beta/\alpha
for
\beta\leq\alpha
. The sign of
\epsilon
specifies the direction of rotation of the electric field. This direction is given by assigning a positive (clockwise) or negative (anti-clockwise) sign to
\epsilon
. In the example above, we chose anti-clockwise polarization. In this way, we can assign a polarization state to
\hat{\mathrm{\mathbf{E}}}(\omega)
and
\hat{\mathrm{\mathbf{E}}}_{\mathrm{Br}}(\omega)
for each frequency
\omega
, thus obtaining a frequency-dependent ellipticity
\epsilon(\omega)
and polarization angle
\Psi(\omega)
.

Figure 2:
Polarization ellipse in the laboratory coordinate system (
x,y
) and the principal axes coordinate system (
\xi,\eta
). The angle between
x
-axis of the laboratory corrdinate system and the major axis of the ellipse is denoted by
\Psi
. The ellipticity of the polarization state
\epsilon
is defined as the ratio between the lengths of the minor and major axes. The sign of
\epsilon
specifies the direction of rotation of the electric field (see text for details).

To assign some well-defined ellipticity to a THz pulse as a whole, we will also use the frequency-averaged ellipticity

\langle\epsilon(\omega)\rangle=\frac{\int\limits_{0}^{\omega_{\mathrm{co}}}\epsilon(\omega)I(\omega)\mathrm{d}\omega}{\int\limits_{0}^{\omega_{\mathrm{co}}}I(\omega)\mathrm{d}\omega},

(6)

where
\epsilon(\omega)
denotes the frequency-dependent ellipticity and
I(\omega)\propto|\hat{\mathrm{\mathbf{E}}}_{\mathrm{Br}}(\omega)|^{2}
is the THz spectral intensity, later extracted from the LC model. The cutoff frequency
\omega_{\mathrm{co}}
(in the upcoming simulations we took
\omega_{\mathrm{co}}=\omega_{0}/4
) selects the frequency range of interest and thus gives a physical meaning to the above quantity, assuming that
\epsilon(\omega)
does not change sign. The frequency-averaged ellipticity is what one would expect from a typical measurement setup, for instance, a conventional scheme employing a linear rotating polarizer and frequency-nonresolving THz detection.

IV
THz waveshapes in the zero frequency limit

Assuming ionization far below saturation (that is,
\rho_{e}\ll{\rho}_{\rm at}
), and neglecting the effects of the field envelope, we can expand the pump electric field into harmonics of the fundamental frequency
\omega_{0}
:

\mathrm{\mathbf{E}}(t)=\sum_{m}{\mathrm{\mathbf{E}}_{m}e^{-im{\omega}_{0}t}},

(10)

where we assume
\mathrm{\mathbf{E}}_{0}=0
in what follows (no DC component in the pump). Note that because
\mathrm{\mathbf{E}}(t)
is real-valued,
\mathrm{\mathbf{E}}_{m}=\mathrm{\mathbf{E}}_{-m}^{*}
holds, where
∗
denotes the complex conjugate. Similarly, the ionization rate
W(t)
can be written as a sum over harmonics
l\omega_{0}
,

W(t)=\sum_{l}{W_{l}e^{-il{\omega}_{0}t}}.

(11)

Then, according to Eq. (
2
), which simplifies to

{\partial}_{t}\rho_{e}\approx{\rho}_{\rm at}W,

(12)

the time derivative of the electron density
{\partial}_{t}\rho_{e}
is periodic and can also be written as a Fourier series. Thus, the electron density
\rho_{e}(t)
reads

\rho_{e}(t)\approx\sum_{l}{{\rho}_{l}e^{-il{\omega_{0}}t}}+{\rho}_{\rm at}W_{0}t,

(13)

and the coefficients
{\rho}_{l}
are given by

{\rho}_{l}=i\frac{{\rho}_{\rm at}}{\omega_{0}l}W_{l},\quad l\neq 0.

(14)

The coefficient
\rho_{0}
can be formally computed as
\rho_{0}=\rho_{e}(t=0)-\sum_{l\neq 0}{\rho}_{l}
. We note that the non-periodic term in Eq. (
13
) conflicts with our initial assumption
\rho_{e}\ll\rho_{\rm at}
. This inconsistency comes from neglecting the finite pulse duration and using Fourier series expansion. However, the non-periodic term and the coefficient
\rho_{0}
do not play any role in the following considerations.

With Eqs. (
5
), (
10
) and (
13
) we have

\mathrm{\mathbf{E}}_{\mathrm{Br}}\propto\rho_{e}\mathrm{\mathbf{E}}=\sum_{m,l}{{\rho}_{l}e^{-il{\omega_{0}}t}}{\mathrm{\mathbf{E}}_{m}e^{-im{\omega}_{0}t}}+{\rho}_{\rm at}W_{0}t\mathrm{\mathbf{E}}.

(15)

In this section, we are interested in the zero frequency limit. Therefore, only summands with
m=-l
contribute:

\mathrm{\mathbf{E}}_{\mathrm{Br},0}\propto\sum_{m\neq 0}{\mathrm{\mathbf{E}}_{m}{\rho}_{-m}}.

(16)

Here, we can exclude
m=0
from the summation because
\mathrm{\mathbf{E}}_{0}=0
, and therefore the coefficient
\rho_{0}
does not contribute to
\mathrm{\mathbf{E}}_{\mathrm{Br},0}
. Only the harmonics of the fundamental frequency
\omega_{0}
contribute to
\mathrm{\mathbf{E}}_{\mathrm{Br},0}
, which is why we call Eq. (
16
) ”frequency representation” in what follows.

Since ionization takes place on the subcycle scale of the optical driver near the maxima of the pump electric field, the free electron density growths in the form of ”sharp steps,”
[
20
]
:

\rho_{e}(t)\approx\sum_{n}\delta\rho_{n}\Theta(t-t_{n}),

(17)

where
\Theta(t)
is the Heaviside step-function,
t_{n}
are the positions of the ionization events, and
\delta\rho_{n}
are the step sizes. The time derivative of the electron density then reads

\partial_{t}\rho_{e}(t)\approx\sum_{n}\delta\rho_{n}\delta(t-t_{n}),

(18)

where
\delta(t)
denotes the Dirac
\delta
function. For the assumed pump electric field configuration, the sequence of ionization steps
t_{n}
is periodic with period
2\pi/\omega_{0}
. Assuming
N_{c}
ionization events per optical cycle, we can write

\partial_{t}\rho_{e}(t)\approx\sum_{n=1}^{N_{c}}\delta\rho_{n}\sum_{j}\delta(t-t_{n}+2\pi j/\omega_{0})

(19)

as a sum of
{N_{c}}
Dirac combs with period
2\pi/\omega_{0}
. The Fourier representation of one Dirac comb reads

\sum_{j}\delta(t-t_{n}+2\pi j/\omega_{0})=\frac{\omega_{0}}{2\pi}\sum_{l}e^{-il\omega_{0}(t-t_{n})}\,.

(20)

Therefore, we find the Fourier series representation for the time derivative of the electron density as

\partial_{t}\rho_{e}(t)=\sum_{l}\left(\frac{\omega_{0}}{2\pi}\sum_{n=1}^{N_{c}}\delta\rho_{n}e^{il\omega_{0}t_{n}}\right)e^{-il\omega_{0}t}\,.

(21)

With Eq. (
11
) and Eq. (
12
) we can conclude that

W_{l}=\frac{\omega_{0}}{2\pi{\rho}_{\rm at}}\sum_{n=1}^{N_{c}}\delta\rho_{n}e^{il\omega_{0}t_{n}},

(22)

and Eq. (
14
) yields

{\rho}_{l}=\frac{i}{2\pi l}\sum_{n=1}^{N_{c}}\delta\rho_{n}{e^{il\omega_{0}t_{n}}},\quad l\neq 0.

(23)

Thus, with Eq. (
16
) we arrive to:

\mathrm{\mathbf{E}}_{\mathrm{Br},0}\propto\sum_{m\neq 0}\sum_{n=1}^{N_{c}}\frac{\delta\rho_{n}\mathrm{\mathbf{E}}_{m}}{m}e^{-im\omega_{0}t_{n}}.

(24)

This expression can be simplified using the vector potential

\partial_{t}\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}(t)=-\mathrm{\mathbf{E}}(t).

With

\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}(t)=\sum_{m}\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}_{m}e^{-im\omega_{0}t}

(25)

we find

\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}_{m}=-\frac{i}{m\omega_{0}}\mathrm{\mathbf{E}}_{m},\quad m\neq 0

(26)

for the
m
th harmonic. If we further set
\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}_{0}=0
, we get

\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}(t)=-\int_{-\infty}^{t}\mathrm{\mathbf{E}}(t^{\prime})dt^{\prime},

(27)

and Eq. (
24
) can be expressed as

\mathrm{\mathbf{E}}_{\mathrm{Br},0}\propto\sum_{m}\sum_{n=1}^{N_{c}}\delta\rho_{n}\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}_{m}e^{-im\omega_{0}t_{n}}.

(28)

Eq. (
28
) as well as Eq. (
24
) represent the ”mixed representation”: they contain the harmonics of the pump,
\mathrm{\mathbf{E}}_{m}
or
\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}_{m}
, respectively, and, at the same time, the step sizes of ionization
\delta\rho_{n}
in time; see Fig.
8

in the time domain, together with the phase information on the ionization event
\exp\!\left(imt_{n}\omega_{0}\right)

To further understand the physical meaning of
\mathrm{\mathbf{E}}_{\mathrm{Br},0}

\mathrm{\mathbf{E}}_{\mathrm{Br},0}\propto\sum_{n=1}^{N_{c}}\delta\rho_{n}\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}(t_{n}).

(29)

This equation allows to predict which pump waveshapes generate THz radiation effectively
[

\delta\rho_{n}
and
\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}(t_{n})
simultaneously, which is not trivial: Since the
t_{n}
are located at the extrema of
\mathrm{\mathbf{E}}(t)

|\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}(t_{n})|\approx 0
, rendering
\delta\rho_{n}\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}(t_{n})
negligible. Examples of two-color pump waveshapes with
\delta\rho_{n}\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}(t_{n})

is larger in case (a).2 due to the larger amplitude of

\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}(t_{n})
are pointing in different directions. As a result, the maximum THz yield is achieved when one ionization event dominates. For a circularly polarized two-color pump pulse with corotating SH and FH [see Fig.

(a)
[

The physics behind Eq. (

d\mathrm{\mathbf{v}}/dt\propto\mathrm{\mathbf{E}}
. Integrating this expression from the electron birth time
t_{n}
to
t=\infty
, we obtain the electron velocity after the pulse,

\mathrm{\mathbf{v}}(t\to\infty,t_{n})\propto\int_{t_{n}}^{\infty}\mathrm{\mathbf{E}}(t^{\prime})dt^{\prime}.

(30)

Because

0=\mathrm{\mathbf{E}}_{0}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{\mathbf{E}}(t^{\prime})dt^{\prime}=\int_{-\infty}^{t_{n}}\mathrm{\mathbf{E}}(t^{\prime})dt^{\prime}+\int_{t_{n}}^{\infty}\mathrm{\mathbf{E}}(t^{\prime})dt^{\prime}

we find with Eq. (
27

\mathrm{\mathbf{v}}(t\to\infty,t_{n})\propto\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}(t_{n}).

(31)

The fact that the electron velocity is proportional to the vector potential at the time of ionization is well known
[
60
]
. It is the heart of the so-called atto-streaking technique
[
61
,
62
]
, which allows one to measure the waveform of a strong pulse in a rather direct way – in contrast to other pulse-characterization techniques.

The net current density produced by the pulse reads

\Delta\mathrm{\mathbf{J}}=\sum_{n=1}^{N_{c}}\delta\rho_{n}\mathrm{\mathbf{v}}(t\to\infty,t_{n})\propto\sum_{n=1}^{N_{c}}\delta\rho_{n}\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}(t_{n}),

(32)

which was already exploited in the context of THz generation
[

,

,
40
,
64
]

The relevant quantity in

is
v_{f}(t)=\int_{-\infty}^{t}E(t^{\prime})e^{\gamma(t-t^{\prime})}dt^{\prime}

. Since we assume that there is no net current density before the pulse arrives,
\mathrm{\mathbf{J}}(t\to-\infty)=0

Because the current density
\mathrm{\mathbf{J}}
is a real-valued vector, the 0th harmonic
\mathrm{\mathbf{E}}_{\mathrm{Br},0}

Thus, the 0th Brunel harmonic is linearly polarized in the direction in which the electrons fly after the pulse has passed. This property is valid for arbitrary pump shapes.

For the particular case of a two-color pump, the polarization of the THz field is determined by the harmonics

\begin{split}\mathrm{\mathbf{E}}_{\mathrm{Br},0}\propto\sum_{n=1}^{N_{c}}\delta\rho_{n}&\Big(\mathrm{\mathbf{E}}_{1}e^{-i\omega_{0}t_{n}}-\mathrm{\mathbf{E}}_{-1}e^{i\omega_{0}t_{n}}\\

(33)

To see how this ”mixed representation” can be used, let us consider the particular waveshape Eqs. (

)-(

\epsilon_{\omega_{0}}=\epsilon_{2\omega_{0}}=1

\phi=0

\displaystyle\mathrm{\mathbf{E}}_{1}=\frac{e_{\omega_{0}}}{2}\left(1,+i\right),\,\mathrm{\mathbf{E}}_{-1}=\frac{e_{\omega_{0}}}{2}\left(1,-i\right),

V
THz waveshapes at non-zero frequencies

As shown in the previous section, the THz polarization at
\omega\to 0
is always linear. This is of course not surprising, since no temporal dynamics is possible at zero frequency. The orientation of the polarization depends on the final direction of the net current density, which in turn depends on the pump waveshape. For non-zero frequencies, the situation is more involved. In Fig.
10
, we present the spectrum, ellipticity, and polarization angle of the THz radiation computed for an exemplary pump pulse of finite duration using the LC model Eq. (
5
). The remarkable feature is that, while the polarization angle remains constant with frequency in first-order approximation (
\omega/\omega_{0}<0.2
), the THz ellipticity increases linearly, revealing a “linear chirp of ellipticity”. As we show later, this is a generic feature of ionization-based THz generation.

In the next subsections, we derive the general expressions for
the polarization state for arbitrary pump waveshape for (low) non-zero frequencies, and present closed analytical expressions for the linear ellipticity chirp for some selected pump waveshapes.

V.1
THz polarization state for generic multi-color pump
pulses

For simplicity, we assume
\rho_{e}\ll\rho_{\rm at}
as in the previous section, but now we have to consider the finite duration of the pump pulse. In this situation, the pump waveshape is no longer periodic. Therefore, in this section,
N
denotes the (finite)
total
number of ionization events in the pulse and must not be confused with the number of ionization events per optical cycle
N_{c}
used before. Since
\mathrm{\mathbf{E}}_{\mathrm{Br}}(t)\propto\rho_{e}(t)\mathrm{\mathbf{E}}(t)
, we find a convolution integral in the Fourier domain,

\hat{\mathrm{\mathbf{E}}}_{\mathrm{Br}}(\omega)\propto\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\hat{\rho}_{e}(\omega-\Omega)\hat{\mathrm{\mathbf{E}}}(\Omega)d\Omega,

(39)

where
\,\widehat{}\,
symbol refers to the Fourier transform in time. From Eq. (
18
) we immediately get

-i\omega\hat{\rho}_{e}(\omega)\approx\sum_{n=1}^{N}\delta\rho_{n}e^{i\omega t_{n}},

(40)

which implies that
\hat{\rho}_{e}(\omega)
is rather broad in frequency space. In contrast, we assume that the multi-color pump field
\hat{\mathrm{\mathbf{E}}}(\omega)
is given by narrow peaks at the harmonic frequencies. These narrow peaks will be approximated by Dirac
\delta
functions in the following, and we write

\hat{\mathrm{\mathbf{E}}}(\omega)\approx\sum_{m}\mathrm{\mathbf{E}}_{m}\delta(\omega-m\omega_{0}),

(41)

setting
\mathrm{\mathbf{E}}_{0}=0
. We note that the approximation Eq. (
41
) renders the pump field similar to Eq. (
10
) used in the previous section. However, we must remember that Eq. (
41
) is only an approximation to facilitate further computations. In this section, we will not use any discrete Fourier series expansion of periodic waveforms but only continuous Fourier transforms of finite pulses.

Figure 10:
(a) Spectra of THz radiation, (b) THz spectral ellipticity, and (c) orientation of the THz polarization ellipse versus frequency considering right-handed circular polarization for SH and linear or right-handed circular polarization for FH (see legend), numerically computed from the LC model Eq. (
5
). The amplitudes of the two pump harmonics are equal, their durations are 50 fs, and
\phi=0
.

With Eqs. (
39
)-(
41
), the Brunel radiation in the THz range can be expressed as

\begin{split}\hat{\mathrm{\mathbf{E}}}_{\mathrm{Br}}(\omega)&\propto\sum_{n=1}^{N}\delta\rho_{n}\int\hat{\mathrm{\mathbf{E}}}(\Omega)\frac{e^{i(\omega-\Omega)t_{n}}}{\omega-\Omega}d\Omega\\
&=\sum_{m}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\delta\rho_{n}\mathrm{\mathbf{E}}_{m}\frac{e^{i(\omega-m\omega_{0})t_{n}}}{\omega-m\omega_{0}}.\end{split}

(42)

This is, in general, a complex function of
\omega
. However, for small
\omega
one can approximate it as

\hat{\mathrm{\mathbf{E}}}_{\mathrm{Br}}(\omega)\propto\boldsymbol{\mu}_{0}+\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1}\frac{\omega}{\omega_{0}}+\ldots,

(43)

where the vector-valued coefficients
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{0}
and
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1}
are

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mu}_{0}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{m}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\delta\rho_{n}\mathrm{\mathbf{E}}_{m}\frac{e^{-im\omega_{0}t_{n}}}{m\omega_{0}},

(44)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{m}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\delta\rho_{n}\mathrm{\mathbf{E}}_{m}\frac{e^{-im\omega_{0}t_{n}}\left(1+im\omega_{0}t_{n}\right)}{m^{2}\omega_{0}}.

(45)

Alternatively, in terms of
\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}
we can write, cf. Eq. (
26
):

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mu}_{0}

\displaystyle=-i\sum_{m}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\delta\rho_{n}\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}_{m}e^{-im\omega_{0}t_{n}},

(46)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1}

\displaystyle=-i\sum_{m}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\delta\rho_{n}\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}_{m}\frac{e^{-im\omega_{0}t_{n}}\left(1+im\omega_{0}t_{n}\right)}{m}.

(47)

With these expressions, it is possible to compute the ellipticity
\epsilon(\omega)
of the THz polarization ellipse at each frequency. We note that the zero-order term Eq. (
46
) coincides with Eq. (
28
), except that here we sum over all ionization events of the pulse. Thus, for
\omega=0
, we obtain a linearly polarized THz field, consistent with the previous section.

V.2
THz ellipticity
\epsilon(\omega)
for the multi-color case

When taking into account zero ellipticity at
\omega=0
and neglecting higher order terms in
\omega
, Eq. (
43
) predicts a linearly increasing ellipticity with
\omega
:

\epsilon(\omega)\approx\mathcal{B}\frac{\omega}{\omega_{0}},\quad\omega\ll\omega_{0},

(48)

that is, the ellipticity is linearly chirped in frequency, and we call
\mathcal{B}
the “ellipticity chirp”. To obtain an analytical expression for
\mathcal{B}
, let us first recall that since the electric field satisfies
\mathrm{\mathbf{E}}_{m}=\mathrm{\mathbf{E}}_{-m}^{*}
and
\mathrm{\mathbf{E}}_{0}=0
, we can rewrite Eqs. (
44
) and (
45
) as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mu}_{0}

\displaystyle=-2i\sum_{n=1}^{N}\delta\rho_{n}\sum_{m>0}\Im\left[\frac{\mathrm{\mathbf{E}}_{m}}{m\omega_{0}}e^{-im\omega_{0}t_{n}}\right],

(49)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1}

\displaystyle=-2\sum_{n=1}^{N}\delta\rho_{n}\sum_{m>0}\Re\left[\frac{\mathrm{\mathbf{E}}_{m}(1+im\omega_{0}t_{n})e^{-im\omega_{0}t_{n}}}{m^{2}\omega_{0}}\right].

(50)

Therefore,
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{0}
is pure imaginary, while
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1}
always takes real values.

To compute the ellipticity
\epsilon(\omega)
, we have to evaluate the orthogonal component of
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1}
with respect to
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{0}
. This amounts to computing the cross-product of both vectors normalized to the reference vector
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{0}
, namely,

{\cal B}=\frac{{|\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{0}\times{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{1}|}{|{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{0}|^{2}},

(51)

or equivalently:

{\cal B}=\frac{|\mu_{1y}\mu_{0x}-\mu_{1x}\mu_{0y}|}{|\mu_{0x}|^{2}+|\mu_{0y}|^{2}}.

(52)

For elliptically polarized multi-color pump fields,
|\mathcal{B}|>0
and therefore non-zero frequency components of the generated THz pulse are generally elliptically polarized.

V.3
THz ellipticity
\epsilon(\omega)
for the two-color case

Here we consider a vectorial electric field for the incident two-color pump pulse given by Eqs. (
7
)–(
9
). We simplify the SH component to circular polarization by setting
\epsilon_{2\omega_{0}}=1
. Still, the FH component may have an arbitrary ellipticity
\epsilon_{\omega_{0}}
, and we allow for an arbitrary phase and ratio of amplitudes between SH and FH. Furthermore, we ignore the contributions of the Gaussian envelope. As illustrative examples, we will address two polarization geometries: A CP-S polarization state (co-rotating FH and SH) where
\epsilon_{\omega_{0}}=1
, and a
\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}
linearly polarized FH coupled to a circularly polarized SH where
\epsilon_{\omega_{0}}=0
. Examples of pump and numerically calculated THz waveshapes for different values of the phase
\phi
between FH and FH are shown in Fig.
9
(a-c) for the former (CP-S) and Fig.
9
(d-f) for the latter case.

In the Jones representation, such an input field is expressed as

\displaystyle\mathrm{\mathbf{E}}_{1}=\frac{e_{\omega_{0}}}{2}\left(1,+i\epsilon_{\omega_{0}}\right),\,\mathrm{\mathbf{E}}_{-1}=\frac{e_{\omega_{0}}}{2}\left(1,-i\epsilon_{\omega_{0}}\right),

(53)

\displaystyle\mathrm{\mathbf{E}}_{2}=\frac{e_{2\omega_{0}}}{2}\left(1,+i\right)\mbox{e}^{-i\phi},\,\mathrm{\mathbf{E}}_{-2}=\frac{e_{2\omega_{0}}}{2}\left(1,-i\right)\mbox{e}^{+i\phi}.

(54)

Evaluating Eqs. (
44
) and (
45
) yields

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mu}_{0}

\displaystyle=-i\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{\delta\rho_{n}}{\omega_{0}}\left[e_{\omega_{0}}\begin{pmatrix}-\sin{(\omega_{0}t_{n})}\\
\epsilon_{\omega_{0}}\cos{(\omega_{0}t_{n})}\end{pmatrix}\right.

\displaystyle+\left.\frac{e_{2\omega_{0}}}{2}\begin{pmatrix}-\sin{(2\omega_{0}t_{n}+\phi)}\\
\cos{(2\omega_{0}t_{n}+\phi)}\end{pmatrix}\right],

(55)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{\delta\rho_{n}}{\omega_{0}}\left[{e_{\omega_{0}}}\begin{pmatrix}\cos{(\omega_{0}t_{n})}+\omega_{0}t_{n}\sin{(\omega_{0}t_{n})}\\
\epsilon_{\omega_{0}}\left[\sin{(\omega_{0}t_{n})}-\omega_{0}t_{n}\cos{(\omega_{0}t_{n})}\right]\end{pmatrix}\right.

\displaystyle+\left.\frac{e_{2\omega_{0}}}{4}\begin{pmatrix}\cos{(2\omega_{0}t_{n}+\phi)}+2\omega_{0}t_{n}\sin{(2\omega_{0}t_{n}+\phi)}\\
\sin{(2\omega_{0}t_{n}+\phi)}-2\omega_{0}t_{n}\cos{(2\omega_{0}t_{n}+\phi)}\end{pmatrix}\right].

(56)

The instants of ionization
t_{n}
depend on the polarization geometry of the input field. They correspond to maxima of the absolute value of the pump electric field
|\mathrm{\mathbf{E}}|
. For our two-color pulse, one has

\displaystyle|\mathrm{\mathbf{E}}(t)|^{2}=e_{\omega_{0}}^{2}\left(\frac{1+\epsilon_{\omega_{0}}^{2}}{2}+r^{2}+r(1+\epsilon_{\omega_{0}})\cos{(\omega_{0}t+\phi)}\right.

\displaystyle+\left.\frac{1-\epsilon_{\omega_{0}}^{2}}{2}\cos{(2\omega_{0}t)}+r(1-\epsilon_{\omega_{0}})\cos{(3\omega_{0}t+\phi)}\right),

(57)

where
r\equiv e_{2\omega_{0}}/e_{\omega_{0}}
. The ionization instants
t_{n}
are determined by the roots of
\partial_{t}|\mathrm{\mathbf{E}}(t)|^{2}=0
, from which we have to select those that satisfy
\partial^{2}_{t}|\mathrm{\mathbf{E}}(t)|^{2}<0
.

In CP-S geometry
(\epsilon_{\omega_{0}}=1)
one has to solve
\sin{(\omega_{0}t+\phi)}=0
. The ionization instants are given by

\omega_{0}t_{n}=2n\pi-\phi.

(58)

For a
\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}
linearly polarized FH
(\epsilon_{\omega_{0}}=0)
, the ionization instants proceed from the roots of

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left\{\cos{(\omega_{0}t)}[\cos{(\omega_{0}t)}+2r\cos{(2\omega_{0}t+\phi)}]\right)\}=0.

(59)

Solving this equation is difficult for an arbitrary
\phi
, and in this paper only the limit
r\rightarrow 1
is considered. Yet, there exist exact solutions for particular values of
\phi
, namely,

\displaystyle\phi=0

\displaystyle\quad\rightarrow\quad\omega_{0}t_{n}=2n\pi,

(60)

\displaystyle\phi=\frac{\pi}{4}

\displaystyle\quad\rightarrow\quad\omega_{0}t_{n}=\arctan\left(\frac{\sqrt{2}-\sqrt{6}}{\sqrt{2}+\sqrt{6}}\right)+2n\pi,

(61)

\displaystyle\phi=\frac{\pi}{2}

\displaystyle\quad\rightarrow\quad\omega_{0}t_{n}=n\pi-(-1)^{n}\frac{\pi}{6}.

(62)

Whereas for
\phi=0
and
\phi=\pi/4
there are only one strong ionization event over the cycle, for the case
\phi=\pi/2
there are two events with equal amplitudes, given by
n=2\pi n
and
n=(2n+1)\pi
(see insets in Fig.
11
). We note that the influence of the phase angle
\phi
on the laser-to-THz conversion efficiency was studied extensively in Refs.
[
16
,
20
,
65
,
19
,
44
]
. Here, we complement these findings by its impact on the THz waveforms and ellipticities. In the following, we consider two distinct situations: zero or non-zero relative phase
\phi
.

Zero relative phase

For
\phi=0
, Eqs. (

) and (

) show that ionization events occur at
t_{n}=0
modulo

. In the CP-S geometry (

and the slope

For FH and SH with equal amplitudes this yields

(65)

which slightly corrects the result obtained in

In the hybrid polarization geometry (FH is LP; SH is CP –

\begin{split}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{0}&=-\frac{1}{\omega_{0}}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\delta\rho_{n}\frac{e_{\omega_{0}}}{2}\begin{pmatrix}0\\
i\end{pmatrix},\\
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1}&=-\frac{1}{\omega_{0}}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\delta\rho_{n}\begin{pmatrix}{e_{\omega_{0}}}+\frac{e_{2\omega_{0}}}{4}\\
-n\pi e_{2\omega_{0}}\end{pmatrix}\end{split}

(66)

and

{\cal B}=\left|\frac{\mu_{1x}}{\mu_{0y}}\right|=\left|\frac{e_{\omega_{0}}+e_{2\omega_{0}}/4}{e_{\omega_{0}}/2}\right|.

(67)

For FH and SH with equal amplitudes, we easily obtain
{\cal B}=5/2
,
that is,

\epsilon(\omega)=\frac{5}{2}\frac{\omega}{\omega_{0}}\quad\textrm{for}\quad e_{\omega_{0}}=e_{2\omega_{0}}.

V.3.2
Non-zero relative phase

When
\phi
is non-zero, Eq. (
58
) shows that for the CP-S geometry (
\epsilon_{\omega_{0}}=1
) the maximum of the electric field shifts from

to
-\phi/\omega_{0}

. Repeating the previous calculations and applying Eq. (

{\cal B}=\frac{|\mu_{1y}(\mu_{0x})-\mu_{1x}(\mu_{0y})|}{|\boldsymbol{\mu}_{0}|^{2}}=\left|\frac{e_{\omega_{0}}+e_{2\omega_{0}}/4}{e_{\omega_{0}}+e_{2\omega_{0}}/2}\right|.

Thus, the ellipticity does not change with varying
\phi

(a-c).

By contrast, for a linearly polarized FH and a circularly polarized SH (
\epsilon_{\omega_{0}}=0
), the THz ellipticity becomes dependent on
\phi
. For certain configurations, cf. Eq. (
61
) and Eq. (
62

) can be found and Eq. (

\displaystyle\phi=\frac{\pi}{4}

\displaystyle\quad\rightarrow\quad{\cal B}=1+\frac{3\sqrt{3}}{4},

V.4
Frequency-averaged THz ellipticity

The previous analysis suggests a straightforward physical explanation for the non-zero ellipticity of the THz pulses produced by pump fields with finite duration. We can expect that the larger the THz pulse bandwidth
\Delta
, the larger the frequency-averaged ellipticity, provided that
\omega_{\mathrm{co}}>\Delta
. The dependence of the ellipticity of THz pulses on the duration of the pump pulse was already reported in
[
20
,
66
,
37
]
. Using the equations derived above, we can provide a rough estimate of this dependency. The bandwidth
\Delta
can be estimated as
\Delta\approx 2/\tau
(which is a suitable approximation for unchirped Gaussian pulses
[
38
]
). We may also assume that the THz spectrum spans from
0
to
\Delta
and has a roughly constant spectral intensity in this range. Under these assumptions, we obtain

\langle\epsilon(\omega)\rangle\approx\frac{1}{\Delta}\int\limits_{0}^{\Delta}\epsilon(\omega)d\omega=\frac{\mathcal{B}}{\omega_{0}\tau}.

(75)

This estimate is in good agreement with Fig.
7
, where the dependencies
\langle\epsilon(\omega)\rangle\propto\mathcal{B}/\tau
predicted by the analytics is clearly visible.

VI
Conclusions and Discussion

In conclusion, we analyzed the THz waveshapes generated via the ionization-based Brunel mechanism by two- and multi-color pump pulses with nontrivial polarization. By numerically solving the local current model and analytical evaluations, we have shown that photo-ionization-driven THz waveshapes have in general a frequency-dependent ellipticity. In the limit of zero frequency, the generated radiation is linearly polarized, and the polarization vector points in the direction of the free current formed by the ionized electrons moving away from the parent ions after the pump pulse has passed. The ellipticity is generally linearly dependent on the frequency, i.e. the THz waveshapes possess a “linear ellipticity chirp”, whose magnitude
\mathcal{B}
depends on the particular driving pulse. This behavior sets ionization-based THz emission appart from other schemes, which tend to produce, at least in the first approximation, pulses with constant ellipticity across the frequency bandwidth.

The ellipticity chirp
\mathcal{B}
tends to strongly peak for the pump waveshapes, where free electron currents from several ionization events cancel each other. Since the net THz energy in such situations is low, as a rule of thumb, the ellipticity chirp is high for low THz energy and vise versa (for comparable free electron densities). Pulses with non-zero ellipticity chirp
\mathcal{B}
do not have a well-defined polarization state. Yet, one can introduce a frequency-averaged THz ellipticity
\langle\epsilon\rangle
, which is proportional to the spectral width of the pump pulse. Decreasing, for instance, the pump pulse duration
\tau
increases the THz bandwidth, and thereby increases its average ellipticity, so in general we have
\langle\epsilon\rangle\sim 1/\tau
. In contrast, the polarization direction is in first-order approximation independent of the frequency and coincides with the net free electron current which, in turn, is determined by the values of the vector potential
\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}(t_{n})
at the ionization instants
t_{n}
(
n=1,2,\ldots
).

Our results demonstrate the unique properties of THz waveforms produced in gas plasmas by polarization controled multi-color pulses. If the characteristics of the pump (polarization states and relative phases of its components) are similar in the whole plasma volume, these properties should be directly observable in the generated THz radiation. If the pump characteristics change over the pump pulse duration, or in space, such as in the case of filaments, our results can be considered as the first building block for more complex THz waveshapes by superimposing different locally generated waveforms
[
54
,
26
]
, giving rise to a even larger diversity of THz waveshapes.
