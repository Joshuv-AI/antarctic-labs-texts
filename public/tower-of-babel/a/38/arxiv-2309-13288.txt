On the residual Monge-Ampère mass of plurisubharmonic functions with symmetry, II
Weiyong He, Long Li, Xiaowei Xu

Abstract
. The aim of this article is to study the residual Monge-Ampère mass of a plurisubharmonic function with an isolated singularity, provided with the circular symmetry. With the aid of Sasakian geometry, we obtain an estimate on the residual mass of this function with respect to its Lelong number and maximal directional Lelong number. This result partially answers the zero mass conjecture raised by Guedj and Rashkovskii.

1.
Introduction

The zero mass conjecture for plurisubharmonic functions,
raised by Guedj and Rashkovskii,
is a fundamental but difficult problem in pluripotential theory, see
[
16
]
,
[
29
]
.
It states that the Lelong number of a plurisubharmonic function (with an isolated singularity)
must be positive, if its complex Monge-Ampère measure has a Dirac mass at the singularity.
Equivalently, this says that the residual Monge-Ampère mass at the singularity of the plurisubharmonic function
must be zero, if it has zero Lelong number at this point.

In literature, there have been many important contributions towards this conjecture,
including works like
[
9
]
,
[
27
]
,
[
26
]
,
[
28
]
,
[
21
]
,
[
6
]
,
[
1
]
and
[
20
]
.
In particular, Rashkovskii
[
27
]
confirms it,
provide that the plurisubharmonic function has the
toric symmetry
.
As a generalization of the toric symmetry,
the so called
circular symmetry

has been recently studied,
and the zero mass conjecture is confirmed for
all circular symmetric plurisubharmonic functions in
{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
, see
[
4
]
,
[
23
]
and
[
22
]
.

In this paper, we study the zero mass conjecture for plurisubharmonic functions with circular symmetry in higher dimensions.
Consider the family
{\mathcal{F}}(B_{1})

consisting of all circular symmetric (or
S^{1}
-invariant)
plurisubharmonic functions on the unit ball
B_{1}
in
{\mathbb{C}}^{n+1}

that are locally bounded outside the origin, see Definition
2.1
.
Moveover,
we denote
{\mathcal{F}}^{\infty}(B_{1})
by the sub-collection of
{\mathcal{F}}(B_{1})

that requires
C^{2}
-continuity of the functions outside the origin, see Definition
2.2
.
In the case of complex dimension two, the second-named author proved the following estimate.

Theorem 1.1

(
[
22
]
)
.

For a function
u\in{\mathcal{F}}(B_{1})
in
{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
, we have

(1.1)

[\nu_{u}(0)]^{2}\leq\tau_{u}(0)\leq 2\lambda_{u}(0)\cdot\nu_{u}(0)+[\nu_{u}(0)]^{2}.

Here
\nu_{u}(0)
and
\tau_{u}(0)
denote
the Lelong number and the residual Monge-Ampère mass
of
u
at the origin, respectively.
Moreover, the constant
\lambda_{u}(0)
is
the
maximal directional Lelong number
of
u
at the origin.
Let
{\ell}_{\zeta}:={\mathbb{C}}\cdot{\zeta}
be the complex line through the origin
in the complex direction
{\zeta}\in({\mathbb{C}}^{n+1})^{*}
.
This terminology stems from taking the supreme of
the Lelong number at the origin
of the restriction
u|_{{\ell}_{\zeta}}

among all complex directions.
For more details, see Definition
2.6
and
2.7
.

However, taking supremes does not guarantee the finiteness in general.
Fortunately, it turns out that

\lambda_{u}(0)

is indeed a non-negative real number for any function
u\in{\mathcal{F}}(B_{1})
, see Proposition
2.8
.
Then the zero mass conjecture in
{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
directly follows from the estimate in equation (
1.1
).

More precisely, this estimate is due to a decomposition formula
of the complex Monge-Ampère measure, see Theorem 4.4,
[
22
]
.
To obtain this formula,
we have utilized a particular local coordinate system (called the complex Hopf-coordinate) on
{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
,
and it is naturally induced from the famous Hopf-fiberation:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ S^{1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces S^{3}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathbb{C}}{\mathbb{P}}^{1}.}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces p}

In higher dimesions, the new observation is that there is a natural

Kähler cone structure
on
({\mathbb{C}}^{n+1})^{*}=S^{2n+1}\times{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
.
That is to say,
we can consider the
standard Sasakian structure
of the unit sphere
S^{2n+1}

with the base manifold
{\mathbb{C}}{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
.
It is nothing but the Hopf-fiberation
as a principle circle bundle over the complex projective space:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ S^{1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces S^{2n+1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathbb{C}}{\mathbb{P}}^{n}.}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces p}

Sasakian geometry is served as a bridge to connect with
the two Kähler structures respectively of the cone and the base manifold.
It enables us to achieve the decomposition formula (Theorem
4.4
) in any dimension.
Comparing to the domain case,
this formula
can be viewed as the push forward of the complex Monge-Ampère measure of a
u\in{\mathcal{F}}^{\infty}(B_{1})

to the base manifold, under the Sasakian structure of
S^{2n+1}
, see Section
5.3
.
For more discussion on Sasakian manifolds, the readers are referred to

[
30
]
,
[
25
]
,
[
7
]
,
[
33
]
and
[
32
]
.

Finally,
we come up with the following estimate on the residual Monge-Ampère mass.

Theorem 1.2

(Theorem
5.11
)
.

For a function
u\in{\mathcal{F}}(B_{1})
,
there exists a dimensional constant
C_{n}\geq n+1
such that we have

(1.2)

[\nu_{u}(0)]^{n+1}\leq\tau_{u}(0)\leq 2C_{n}[\lambda_{u}(0)]^{n}\cdot\nu_{u}(0).

The lefthand side of the inequality in equation (
1.2
)
was indicated by Cegrell
[
10
]
,
and the zero mass conjecture follows from the righthand side.

Theorem 1.3

(Theorem
5.12
)
.

For a function
u\in{\mathcal{F}}(B_{1})
, we have

(1.3)

\nu_{u}(0)=0\Rightarrow\tau_{u}(0)=0.

The estimate in equation (
1.2
) is stronger than the case in complex dimension two.
This is because of a better understanding of the positivity conditions, see Lemma
4.5
.
However, this inequality is not sharp.
Moreover,
this estimate fails if the plurisubharmonic function is no longer circular symmetric, see Example 6.13,
[
22
]
.

In the last section,
we provide a different proof of the decomposition formula
via Cartan’s method of moving frames, see
[
13
]
,
[
12
]
.
This should be useful in the future work when there is no symmetry conditions.

2.
Plurisubharmonic functions with circular symmetry

In this section, we recall a few basic facts about

S^{1}
-invariant plurisubharmonic functions.
Denote by
z:=(z^{0},\cdots,z^{n})
the complex Euclidean coordinate on
{\mathbb{C}}^{n+1}
.
There is a natural
S^{1}
-action that sends

z\rightarrow e^{i\theta}z:=(e^{i\theta}z^{0},\cdots,e^{i\theta}z^{n}),

for all angles
\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}
.
A domain
D
is called balanced if it is invariant under this
S^{1}
-action,
and a function
u
on a balanced domain is said to be circular symmetric
or
S^{1}
-invariant if it satisfies

u(z)=u(e^{i\theta}z).

Assume that
D
contains the origin.
We say that a plurisubharmonic function
u
on
D

has an isolated singularity at the origin,
if it is locally bounded on the punctured domain
D^{*}:=D-\{0\}
and
u(0)=-\infty
.
Then we can consider the following two families of plurisubharmonic functions.

Definition 2.1
.

An
S^{1}
-invariant plurisubharmoinc function belongs to the family
{\mathcal{F}}(D)
,
if it is
L^{\infty}_{loc}
on
D^{*}
.

Definition 2.2
.

An
S^{1}
-invariant plurisubharmoinc function belongs to the family
{\mathcal{F}}^{\infty}(D)
,
if it is
C^{2}
-continuous on
D^{*}
.

We adopt the following normalization condition,
\sup_{D}u=-1,

possibly after shrinking
D
to a smaller balanced domain.

In the previous work
[
22
]
,
we have discovered several useful properties of a function
u\in{\mathcal{F}}(D)
.
Although these properties were stated in complex dimension two,
they are adapted to all dimensions. We recall these facts.

2.1.
The residual mass

Let
B_{R}\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{n+1}
be the open ball centered at the origin with radius
R>0
,
and
B_{R}^{*}
be the corresponding punctured ball.
Denote
S_{R}
by the boundary of the ball, satisfying the equation

(2.1)

\sum_{A=0}^{n}|z^{A}|^{2}=R^{2}.

In this paper, we always take
D:=B_{1}
, and focus on the local behaviors
of a function near the origin.
Consider a function
u\in{\mathcal{F}}(B_{1})
,
and then its complex Monge-Ampère measure is a closed positive
(n+1,n+1)
-current:

(2.2)

\mbox{MA}(u):=dd^{c}u\wedge\cdots\wedge dd^{c}u=(dd^{c}u)^{n+1},

where the wedge is taken in the sense of Demailly and Bedford-Talyor.
For more details, see
[
15
]
,
[
3
]
,
[
2
]
,
[
10
]
and
[
8
]
.
Here we have used the notation

d:=\partial+\bar{\partial};\ \ \ \ d^{c}:=\frac{i}{2}(\bar{\partial}-\partial).

Fixing an
R\in(0,1)
, we take this measure on the ball as

\mbox{MA}(u)(B_{R}):=\int_{B_{R}}(dd^{c}u)^{n+1}.

The residual Monge-Ampère mass of
u
at the origin is equal to the limit:

(2.3)

\displaystyle\tau_{u}(0)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\pi^{n+1}}\mbox{MA}(u)(\{0\})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\pi^{n+1}}\lim_{R\rightarrow 0}\mbox{MA}(u)(B_{R}).

Next we introduce the standard regularization of a plurisubharmonic function.
Let
\rho(z):=\rho(|z|)
be a non-negative smooth mollifier in
{\mathbb{C}}^{n+1}

satisfying
\rho(r)=0
for all
r\geq 1
, and

\int_{{\mathbb{C}}^{n+1}}\rho(z)\ d\lambda(z)=1,

where
d\lambda
is the Lebesgue measure.
Take its rescaling for each
\varepsilon>0
small as

\rho_{\varepsilon}(z):=\varepsilon^{-2n-2}\rho(z/\varepsilon).

For a function
u\in{\mathcal{F}}(B_{1})
,
we can define its regularization
as a sequence of smooth plurisubharmonic functions that decreases to
u
pointwise:

(2.4)

\displaystyle u_{\varepsilon}(z):

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(u*\rho_{\varepsilon})(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{|z-y|\leq\varepsilon}\rho_{\varepsilon}(z-y)u(y)d\lambda(y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{|w|\leq 1}u(z-\varepsilon w)\rho(w)d\lambda(w).

This sequence converges to
u
in
C^{2}
-norm on any compact subset of
B^{*}_{1}

if
u\in{\mathcal{F}}^{\infty}(B_{1})
. Fixing a small
\delta>0
,
the regularization
u_{\varepsilon}
is in
{\mathcal{F}}^{\infty}(B_{1-\delta})
for all
\varepsilon
small enough.
Then we have the following convergence of the Monge-Ampère masses.

Lemma 2.3

(
[
22
]
)
.

For a function
u\in{\mathcal{F}}(B_{1})
,
the complex Monge-Ampère measure of its regularization
u_{\varepsilon}
converges on a ball as

(2.5)

\emph{MA}(u)(B_{R})=\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}\emph{MA}(u_{\varepsilon})(B_{R}),

for almost all
R\in(0,1)
.

This convergence follows the Portemanteau type inequalities below and the fact that the Monge-Ampère measure
\mbox{MA}(u)

vanishes on the boundary hypersphere
S_{R}
for almost all
R\in(0,1)
. In fact we have

(2.6)

\begin{split}&\mbox{MA}(u)(B_{R})\leq\liminf_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}\mbox{MA}(u_{\varepsilon})(B_{R}),\text{on any open ball}\;B_{R}\subsetneq B_{1}\\
&\mbox{MA}(u)(\overline{B}_{R})\geq\limsup_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}\mbox{MA}(u_{\varepsilon})(\overline{B}_{R}),\text{on any closed ball}\;\overline{B_{R}}\subset B_{1}.\end{split}

We note that the convergence in Lemma
2.3
holds for every
R\in(0,1)
, if we assume
u\in{\mathcal{F}}^{\infty}(B_{1})
.
Then by the Stokes Theorem we have

Lemma 2.4

(
[
22
]
)
.

For
u\in{\mathcal{F}}^{\infty}(B_{1})
and for all
R\in(0,1)
,

(2.7)

\int_{B_{R}}(dd^{c}u)^{n+1}=\int_{S_{R}}d^{c}u\wedge(dd^{c}u)^{n},

2.2.
Maximal directional Lelong numbers

Denote
S_{u}(0,r)
to be the average of
u
on the sphere
S_{r}
,

(2.8)

S_{u}(0,r):=\frac{1}{a_{2n+1}}\int_{|\xi|=1}u(r\xi)d\sigma(\xi),

where
d\sigma
is the area form of the unit sphere
S^{2n+1}
,
and
a_{2n+1}
is its total area.
The Lelong number of a plurisubharmonic function
u

at the origin is defined to be the following limit

\nu_{u}(0)=\lim_{r\rightarrow 0^{+}}\nu_{u}(0,r),\;\text{with}\;\nu_{u}(0,r):=r\partial_{r}^{-}S_{u}(0,r).

In fact,
S_{u}(0,r)
is a convex and non-decreasing function of
t:=\log r
,
and hence the limit
\nu_{u}(0)
always exits.
Assume the function
u
is in the family
{\mathcal{F}}(B_{1})
from now on.
Denote
{\ell}_{\zeta}
by the complex line through the origin
in the complex direction
{\zeta}\in{\mathbb{C}}{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
as

{\ell}_{{\zeta}}:={\mathbb{C}}\cdot[{\zeta}],

where
[{\zeta}]
means a homogeneous coordinate of
{\zeta}
in
({\mathbb{C}}^{n+1})^{*}
.
Thanks to the plurisubharmonicity and
S^{1}
-symmetry,
the restriction
u|_{{\ell}_{\zeta}}

is a convex and non-decreasing function of the variable
t\in(-\infty,0)
.

It is more convenient to use a parametrization
(r,\theta,{\zeta},\bar{\zeta})

of the space
({\mathbb{R}}^{2n+2})^{*}\cong({\mathbb{C}}^{n+1})^{*}

induced by the fiber maps of the Hopf-fiberation

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ S^{1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces S^{2n+1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathbb{C}}{\mathbb{P}}^{n}.}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces p}

In this parametrization,
the
r
-variable denotes the radius function, and

\theta
stands for the direction induced by the
S^{1}
-action.
Then we can re-write the function
u
under this parametrization as

u_{t}({\zeta}):=\hat{u}(t,{\zeta},\bar{\zeta})=u(e^{t},{\zeta},\bar{\zeta}),

and denote its derivative with respect to
t
by

\dot{u}_{t}({\zeta}):=\partial_{t}\hat{u}(t,{\zeta},\bar{\zeta})=\frac{d}{dt}u|_{{\ell}_{{\zeta}}}.

Then it follows for each
{\zeta}\in{\mathbb{C}}{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
fixed

(2.9)

\dot{u}_{t}\geq 0;\ \ \ \ \ddot{u}_{t}\geq 0,

for almost all
t\in(-\infty,0)
.
Moreover, the Lelong number at zero of the restriction
u|_{{\ell}_{{\zeta}}}

is equal to

\nu_{u|_{{\ell}_{{\zeta}}}}(0)=\lim_{t\rightarrow-\infty}\dot{u}_{t}({\zeta})

It is a well-known fact that
the Lelong number of a plurisubharmonic function is invariant
under the restriction to almost all complex directions in
{\mathbb{C}}{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
.
This means that we have

\nu_{u}(0)=\nu_{u|_{{\ell}_{{\zeta}}}}(0),

for almost all
{\zeta}\in{\mathbb{C}}{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
.
In particular, the Lelong number
\nu_{u}(0)
is the infimum of all such restrictions.
Then the following result follows from the dominated convergence theorem.

Lemma 2.5

(
[
22
]
)
.

For any
u\in{\mathcal{F}}^{\infty}(B_{1})
, we have

(2.10)

[\nu_{u}(0)]^{p}=\lim_{t\rightarrow-\infty}\frac{1}{\pi^{n}}\int_{{\mathbb{C}}{\mathbb{P}}^{n}}(\dot{u}_{t})^{p}\omega_{FS}^{n},

for all
p=1,\cdots,n+1
.
Here
\omega_{FS}
stands for the Fubini-Study metric on
{\mathbb{C}}{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
with the normalization

\int_{{\mathbb{C}}{\mathbb{P}}^{n}}\omega_{FS}^{n}=\pi^{n}.

In order to apply the dominated convergence theorem to a general
u\in{\mathcal{F}}(B_{1})
,
we actually need an upper bound of
\dot{u}_{t}({\zeta})
for all
{\zeta}\in{\mathbb{C}}{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
.
This leads us to consider the supremum of all such restrictions.

Definition 2.6
.

The maximal directional Lelong number of a function
u\in{\mathcal{F}}(B_{1})
at a distance
A>0
is defined to be the following

(2.11)

M_{A}(u):=\sup_{{\zeta}\in{\mathbb{C}}{\mathbb{P}}^{n}}\partial_{t}^{+}u_{t}({\zeta})|_{t=-A}\in[0,+\infty].

Thanks to the log-convexity of each restriction
u|_{{\ell}_{{\zeta}}}
,
it is apparent that the number

M_{A}(u)
is well-defined, and is non-negative and non-increasing in
A
.
Then we can take its limit as
A\rightarrow+\infty
.

Definition 2.7
.

The maximal directional Lelong number of a function
u\in{\mathcal{F}}(B_{1})
at the origin is defined to be the following

(2.12)

\lambda_{u}(0):=\lim_{A\rightarrow+\infty}M_{A}(u).

A fundamental fact about
\lambda_{u}(0)
is that for a
u\in{\mathcal{F}}^{\infty}(B_{1})
,

\lambda_{u}(0)
is always finite, which
is crucial in our estimate of the residual Monge-Ampère mass .
For the convenience of readers,
we will recall the proof as follows.

Proposition 2.8

(
[
22
]
)
.

For a function
u\in{\mathcal{F}}(B_{1})
,
its maximal directional Lelong number
M_{A}(u)
is finite for all
A>0
.
In particular, we have

(2.13)

0\leq\lambda_{u}(0)<+\infty.

Proof.

For each hypersphere
S_{R}
with
R\in(0,1)
,
there is a constant
C_{R}>0
such that we have

u|_{S_{R}}>-C_{R}.

This is because
u
is an
L^{\infty}_{loc}
-function defined everywhere in
B^{*}_{1}
,
and hence its restriction to the hypersphere is
in the space
L^{\infty}(S_{R})
.

Suppose on the contrary, we have
M_{A}(u)=+\infty

for some
A>0
. Then there exists a subsequence of points
{\zeta}_{j}\in{\mathbb{C}}{\mathbb{P}}^{n}

such that the slope

(2.14)

\partial_{t}^{+}u_{t}({\zeta}_{j})|_{t=-A}=\frac{d}{dt}|_{t=(-A)^{+}}\left(u|_{{\ell}_{{\zeta}_{j}}}\right)

diverges to
+\infty
as
j\rightarrow+\infty
.
In particular, we can pick up a point
\xi
among this subsequence satisfying

(2.15)

\partial_{t}^{+}u_{t}(\xi)|_{t=-A}>\frac{2C_{R}}{A},

for
R=e^{-A}
.
However, as a convex function of
t
, the graph of
u_{t}(\xi)

is above the following straight line

y(x)=\frac{2C_{R}}{A}(x+A)-C_{R},

for all
t\in[-A,-A/2]
.
Hence we conclude

u_{-A/2}(\xi)\geq y(-A/2)\geq 0.

This contradicts to the fact that
u
is a negative function in
B_{1}
,
and then our result follows.
∎

The maximal directional Lelong numbers
provide a way to measure the difference of the infimums of
u
on the hyperspheres.

Lemma 2.9
.

For a function
u\in{\mathcal{F}}(B_{1})
and two constants
1<A_{1}<A_{2}
,
we have the estimate

(2.16)

-\inf_{S_{R_{2}}}u\leq\int_{A_{1}}^{A_{2}}M_{T}(u)dT-\inf_{S_{R_{1}}}u,

where
R_{i}=e^{-A_{i}}
for
i=1,2
.

Proof.

For a complex direction
{\zeta}\in{\mathbb{C}}{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
,
the function
u_{t}({\zeta})
is convex, and hence it is Lipschitz continuous.
Then it follows from the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus

(2.17)

\displaystyle u_{-A_{1}}({\zeta})-u_{-A_{2}}({\zeta})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{-A_{2}}^{-A_{1}}\dot{u}_{t}({\zeta})dt

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{A_{1}}^{A_{2}}M_{T}(u)dT.

Then we have

(2.18)

\inf_{S_{R_{1}}}u-u_{-A_{2}}({\zeta})\leq\int_{A_{1}}^{A_{2}}M_{T}(u)dT.

Take a sequence of points
{\zeta}_{k}\in{\mathbb{C}}{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
satisfying

u_{-A_{2}}({\zeta}_{k})\rightarrow\inf_{S_{R_{2}}}u,

as
k\rightarrow+\infty
. Then our result follows.
∎

In particular,
we have
M_{A}(u)>0
for all
A>0
,
if
u
has an isolated singularity at the origin.

2.3.
Regularization and convergence

As a direct consequence of Proposition
2.8
,

\dot{u}_{t}=\partial_{t}\hat{u}=r\partial_{r}u

is an
L^{\infty}_{loc}
-function in
B_{1}^{*}
.
Thanks to the slicing theory, we first conclude that Lemma
2.5
holds for all functions in
{\mathcal{F}}(B_{1})
.
Then it is legal to introduce the following functionals for almost all
t\in(-\infty,0)
,

{\mathcal{I}}(u_{t}):=\int_{{\mathbb{C}}{\mathbb{P}}^{n}}u_{t}\omega_{FS}^{n};\ \ \ \ \ I(u_{t}):=\int_{{\mathbb{C}}{\mathbb{P}}^{n}}(\dot{u}_{t})\omega_{FS}^{n},u\in{\mathcal{F}}(B_{1}).

We recall a few basic facts about these functionals.

(i)

the functional
{\mathcal{I}}(u_{t})
is a convex and non-decreasing function of
t\in(-\infty,-1)
,
and it is a primitive of the functional
I(u_{t})
along
t
;

(ii)

the functional
I(u_{t})
is a non-negative and non-decreasing
L^{\infty}
-function of
t\in(-\infty,-1)
;

(iii)

the functional
I(u_{t})
converges as
t\rightarrow-\infty

I(u_{t})\rightarrow\pi^{n}\nu_{u}(0).

Next the standard regularization
u_{\varepsilon}
will be utilized to perform the approximation of these functionals.
Write the
t
-derivatives of the regularization as

\dot{u}_{\varepsilon,t}:=r\partial_{r}u_{\varepsilon}=r\partial_{r}(u*\rho_{\varepsilon}),

and then we have the following Friedrichs’ type estimate.

Lemma 2.10

(
[
22
]
)
.

For a function
u\in{\mathcal{F}}(B_{1})
, a small number
\delta>0

and a point
z\in B^{*}_{1-2\delta}
, we have, for all
\varepsilon<\min\{|z|,\delta\}
,

(2.19)

|r\partial_{r}(u*\rho_{\varepsilon})(z)-r(\partial_{r}u*\rho_{\varepsilon})(z)|\leq 2\varepsilon||\nabla u||_{L^{1}(B_{1-\delta})}.

It is a standard fact that a function
u\in{\mathcal{F}}(B_{1})
is in the Sobolev space

W^{1,p}_{loc}(B_{1})
for any
1\leq p<2
.
Hence by Lemma
2.10
we can compare with the following two convolutions as

(2.20)

r(\partial_{r}u)_{\varepsilon}(z)=\dot{u}_{\varepsilon,t}+O(\varepsilon),

on any relatively compact domain
\Omega\subset\subset B_{1}^{*}
.
Therefore, Proposition
2.8
implies that
the regularization converges on
\Omega
as

\dot{u}_{\varepsilon,t}\rightarrow\partial_{t}u

strongly in
L^{p}
-norm for any
p\geq 1
.
Thanks to the slicing theory again,
we can infer the following convergence results.

Proposition 2.11

(
[
22
]
)
.

For a function
u\in{\mathcal{F}}(B_{1})
, and any constants
1<A<B
,
there exists a subsequence
\{u_{\varepsilon_{k}}\}
of its standard regularization
satisfying

I(u_{\varepsilon_{k},t})\rightarrow I(u_{t}),

as
k\rightarrow+\infty
for almost all
t\in[-B,-A]
.

We end up this section with another application of Proposition
2.8

and Lemma
2.10
,
and it will provide the a priori estimate on the maximal directional Lelong numbers of the regularization.

Lemma 2.12

(
[
22
]
)
.

Fixing any two constants
1<A<B
,
there exists a uniform constant
C>0
such that we have

(2.21)

M_{B}(u_{\varepsilon})\leq 2M_{A}(u)+C\varepsilon,

for all
\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{0}:=\frac{1}{2}\min\{(e^{-A}-e^{-B}),e^{-B}\}.

3.
The Kähler cone structure

In this section, we are going to use a different point
of view to look at the complex hessian of a function
u\in{\mathcal{F}}^{\infty}(B_{1})
.
In fact, there is a natural Kähler cone structure on the space

({\mathbb{C}}^{n+1})^{*}\cong({\mathbb{R}}^{2n+2})^{*}
,
that induces the standard Sasakian structure on the unit sphere
S^{2n+1}
.
In the following, we will decompose the usual complex structure on
{\mathbb{C}}^{n+1}

with respect to this Kähler cone structure.

Let
{\mathbb{R}}^{2n+2}
be the
(2n+2)
-dimensional Euclidean space
with rectangular coordinates

(x^{0},\cdots,x^{n},y^{0},\cdots,y^{n}),

and we briefly write it as
(x^{A},y^{A})
for
A=0,1,\cdots,n
.
Then the
(2n+1)
-dimensional hypersphere
S_{r}
with radius
r
is defined by

\sum_{A}\left\{(x^{A})^{2}+(y^{A})^{2}\right\}=r^{2},

and denote
S^{2n+1}
by the unit sphere with
r=1
.
Put

z^{A}:=x^{A}+iy^{A},

and then
z^{A}
’s define a complex structure in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2n+2}
.
Its standard almost complex structure is given by the
(1,1)
-tensor field

I:=\sum_{A}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial{y^{A}}}\otimes dx^{A}-\frac{\partial}{\partial{x^{A}}}\otimes dy^{A}\right)=\sum_{A}i\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial z^{A}}\otimes dz^{A}-\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar{z}^{A}}\otimes d\bar{z}^{A}\right).

Denote
g
by the flat metric on
{\mathbb{R}}^{2n+2}
,
and then its associated Kähler form on
{\mathbb{C}}^{n+1}
is defined as

\omega_{e}:=\frac{i}{2}\partial\bar{\partial}r^{2}=\frac{i}{2}\sum_{A}dz^{A}\wedge d\bar{z}^{A}.

3.1.
Sasakian manifolds

To talk about
a
Sasakian structure
on
S^{2n+1}
,
it is equivalent to describe a
Kähler cone structure

on the product space
({\mathbb{R}}^{2n+2})^{*}\cong S^{2n+1}\times{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
, see Chapter 6,
[
7
]
.
First we note that the flat metric
g
splits as a metric cone as

g=dr^{2}+r^{2}g_{0},

for any radius
r>0
.
Here
g_{0}
is the canonical metric on
S^{2n+1}
with constant sectional curvature
1
,
and it is the restriction of
g
to the sphere.
Denote
\eta_{0}
by the contact
1
-form:

(3.1)

\displaystyle\eta_{0}:

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle I(r^{-1}dr)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{r^{2}}\sum_{A}\left(y^{A}dx^{A}-x^{A}dy^{A}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{i}{2r^{2}}\sum_{A}\left(z^{A}d\bar{z}^{A}-\bar{z}^{A}dz^{A}\right).

Then it is clear that we have

\omega_{e}=-d(r^{2}\eta_{0})/2.

Based on this Kähler structure on the metric cone,
we say that the quadruple

(3.2)

\left(S^{2n+1}\times{\mathbb{R}}_{+},dr^{2}+r^{2}g_{0},-d(r^{2}\eta_{0})/2,I\right)

defines a Kähler cone structure on the manifold
({\mathbb{R}}^{2n+2})^{*}
.
Moreover,
there is another important ingredient,
the
Reeb vector field

\xi_{0}
, that is defined as

(3.3)

\displaystyle\xi_{0}:

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-I(r\partial_{r})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{A}\left(y^{A}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{A}}-x^{A}\frac{\partial}{\partial y^{A}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(-i)\sum_{A}\left(z^{A}\frac{\partial}{\partial z^{A}}-\bar{z}^{A}\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar{z}^{A}}\right).

It is a holomorphic Killing field on
({\mathbb{R}}^{2n+2})^{*}
,
and
\eta_{0}
is its dual
1
-form.
Moreover, the metric
g
has homothetic degree two, and
the almost complex structure
I
has homothetic degree zero in the following sense:

\mathcal{L}_{r\partial_{r}}g=2g;\ \ \ \ \mathcal{L}_{r\partial_{r}}I=0.

Denote
(\eta_{0},\xi_{0})
also by their restrictions to the manifold
S^{2n+1}
,
and then we have the following facts with the metric
g_{0}
:

(i)

\eta_{0}
is a contact
1
-form, and
\xi_{0}
is a Killing vector field on
S^{2n+1}
;

(ii)

\eta_{0}(\xi_{0})=1
and
\ \iota_{\xi_{0}}d\eta_{0}(\cdot)=d\eta_{0}(\xi_{0},\cdot)=0
;

(iii)

the integral curves of
\xi_{0}
are exactly the great circles on
S^{2n+1}
.

It follows that the Reeb vector field
\xi_{0}
defines a
regular foliation

{\mathcal{F}}_{\xi_{0}}

of
S^{2n+1}
by the great circles,
and it is nothing but the Hopf-fiberation:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ S^{1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces S^{2n+1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathbb{C}}{\mathbb{P}}^{n}.}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces p}

Let
L_{\xi_{0}}
be the trivial line bundle generated by
\xi_{0}
,
and then we have a splitting of the tangent space of the sphere as

TS^{2n+1}=L_{\xi_{0}}\oplus{\mathcal{D}},

where the contact sub-bundle
{\mathcal{D}}:=\ker(\eta_{0})
is the kernel of the contact
1
-form.
Moreover, it can be identified with the the normal bundle
\nu({\mathcal{F}}_{\xi_{0}})

of the foliation via an isomorphism induced from the metric
g_{0}
.

Next we define an endomorphism of
TS^{2n+1}

by restricting the almost complex structure
I
to
{\mathcal{D}}
,
and extending it trivially to
L_{\xi_{0}}
.
Explicitly, it is a
(1,1)
-tensor field as

(3.4)

\displaystyle\Phi_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{A,B}\left\{(x^{A}x^{B}-\delta^{AB})\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{A}}\otimes dy^{B}+(\delta^{AB}-y^{A}y^{B})\frac{\partial}{\partial y^{A}}\otimes dx^{B}\right\}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\sum_{A,B}\left\{y^{A}x^{B}\frac{\partial}{\partial y^{A}}\otimes dy^{B}-x^{A}y^{B}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{A}}\otimes dx^{B}\right\}.

Observe that
\eta_{0}\circ\Phi_{0}=0
,
and then we can infer the following equation

(3.5)

\Phi_{0}^{2}=-\mathbb{I}+\xi_{0}\otimes\eta_{0}.

That is to say,
the restriction
\Phi|_{{\mathcal{D}}}
defines
an almost complex structure on
{\mathcal{D}}
,
and it is compatible with the symplectic form
d\eta_{0}

in the following sense:

(3.6)

\begin{split}&d\eta_{0}(\Phi_{0}X,\Phi_{0}Y)=d\eta_{0}(X,Y)\ \text{for all $X,Y\in\Gamma({\mathcal{D}})$};\\
&d\eta_{0}(\Phi_{0}X,X)>0\ \text{for all $X\neq 0$}.\end{split}

It follows that
the pair
({\mathcal{D}},\Phi_{0}|_{{\mathcal{D}}})

defines an almost
CR
-structure,
and
its Levi form
L_{\eta_{0}}:=d\eta_{0}\circ(\Phi_{0}\otimes\mathbb{I})

is strictly pseudo-convex.
Then it
induces a Riemannian metric on the distribution transversal to
\xi_{0}
, i.e.
we define the transversal metric as

(3.7)

g^{T}(X,\Phi_{0}Y):=d\eta_{0}(X,Y)\ \ \ \text{for all}\ X,Y\in\Gamma({\mathcal{D}}).

The upshot is that the metric
g_{0}
on the sphere
is compatible with the
almost contact structure

(\xi_{0},\eta_{0},\Phi_{0})

in the following sense:

(3.8)

g_{0}=g^{T}+\eta_{0}\otimes\eta_{0}.

Together with the Killing condition of
\xi_{0}
,
the quadruple

(\xi_{0},\eta_{0},\Phi_{0},g_{0})

is called a
Sasakian structure
on
S^{2n+1}
.
This means that
the almost
CR
-structure
({\mathcal{D}},\Phi_{0}|_{{\mathcal{D}}})
is in fact integrable,
and
\Phi_{0}
is invariant under
\xi_{0}
.
Then it follows a splitting

(3.9)

{\mathcal{D}}\otimes{\mathbb{C}}={\mathcal{D}}^{1,0}\oplus{\mathcal{D}}^{0,1}\ \ \ \text{with}\ \ \overline{{\mathcal{D}}^{1,0}}={\mathcal{D}}^{0,1},

where
{\mathcal{D}}^{1,0}
and
{\mathcal{D}}^{0,1}

are eigenspaces of
\Phi_{0}
with eigenvalues
i
and
-i
, respectively.
In particular, the eigenspace with eigenvalue
0
is exactly
L_{\xi_{0}}\otimes{\mathbb{C}}
.

Furthermore, this splitting induces a complex structure

\bar{J}
of the normal bundle via the isomorphism

({\mathcal{D}},\Phi_{0}|_{{\mathcal{D}}})\cong(\nu({\mathcal{F}}_{\xi_{0}}),\bar{J})
,
and then it gives a transversal holomorphic structure on the foliation.

It is clear from the construction (equation (
3.7
) and (
3.8
))
that the triple
(g^{T},\omega^{T},\bar{J})
defines a transversal Kähler structure
on the local leaf space of the foliation, where the transversal Kähler metric is

\omega^{T}:=-d\eta_{0}.

Since the foliation is regular,
the push forward of this transversal Kähler structure
to the base manifold (via the fiber map of the Hopf-fiberation)
is exactly the Kähler structure on
{\mathbb{C}}{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
with the usual Fubini-Study metric.
For further discussion, the reader is referred to
[
30
]
,
[
7
]
,
[
33
]
and
[
18
]
.

3.2.
The complex Hopf-coordinate

In order to illustrate the Sasakian structure on the sphere in an explicit way,
we will invoke a particular local coordinate system on the cone

({\mathbb{R}}^{2n+2})^{*}\cong({\mathbb{C}}^{n+1})^{*}
.
In fact, it is a generalization of the
complex Hopf-coordinate in
{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
, see
[
22
]
.

First consider the following holomorphic functions on the set
\{z^{0}\neq 0\}
:

{\zeta}^{{\alpha}}:=\frac{z^{{\alpha}}}{z^{0}}=|{\zeta}^{{\alpha}}|e^{i\varphi_{{\alpha}}}

for all
{\alpha}:=1,\cdots,n
,
and
\varphi_{{\alpha}}
is an argument of
{\zeta}^{{\alpha}}
as a complex number.
Then the complex Hopf-coordinate on
({\mathbb{R}}^{2n+2})^{*}
is introduced as

(r,\theta,{\zeta},\bar{\zeta}):=(r,\theta,{\zeta}^{1},\cdots,{\zeta}^{n},\bar{{\zeta}}^{1},\cdots,\bar{{\zeta}}^{n}),

for all
r\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
,
\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}
and
{\zeta}^{{\alpha}}\in{\mathbb{C}}
with the following change of variables:

(3.10)

z^{0}=re^{\frac{i}{2}\theta}\frac{\varrho({\zeta},\bar{{\zeta}})}{\left(1+\sum_{\beta}|{\zeta}^{\beta}|^{2}\right)^{1/2}};\ \ \ z^{{\alpha}}=re^{\frac{i}{2}\theta}\frac{{\zeta}^{{\alpha}}\cdot\varrho({\zeta},\bar{{\zeta}})}{\left(1+\sum_{\beta}|{\zeta}^{\beta}|^{2}\right)^{1/2}},

where the factor
\varrho
is defined as

\varrho({\zeta},\bar{\zeta}):=\prod_{{\alpha}=1}^{n}\left(\frac{\bar{{\zeta}}^{{\alpha}}}{|{\zeta}^{\alpha}|}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

This factor is introduced for the purpose to
gain more symmetry while taking change of variables.
For example, it enables us to write

(3.11)

z^{0}=\frac{re^{\frac{i}{2}\left(\theta-\sum_{{\alpha}}\varphi_{{\alpha}}\right)}}{\left(1+\sum_{\beta}|{\zeta}^{\beta}|^{2}\right)^{1/2}};\ \ \ \ z^{{\alpha}}=\frac{re^{\frac{i}{2}\left(\theta+\varphi_{{\alpha}}-\sum_{\beta}\widehat{\varphi_{{\alpha}}}\right)}}{\left(1+\sum_{\beta}|{\zeta}^{\beta}|^{2}\right)^{1/2}},

where
the notation
\sum_{\gamma}\widehat{\varphi_{{\alpha}}}
means taking the summation of all
\varphi_{\gamma}
’s without the angle
\varphi_{\alpha}
.

In fact,
the complex Hopf-coordinate
is induced from a local trivialization of
({\mathbb{C}}^{n+1})^{*}

as a principle
{\mathbb{C}}^{*}
-bundle over
{\mathbb{C}}{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
.
That is to say, there is a homeomorphism of the fiber map
p
as

\Psi:{\mathbb{C}}^{*}\times U\rightarrow p^{-1}(U),

that sends

(re^{i\theta/2},{\zeta}^{1},\cdots,{\zeta}^{n})\rightarrow(z^{0},\cdots,z^{n})

via the map defined in equation (
3.10
).
Here
U\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
is an open set on which one branch of the factor
\varrho
is well-defined,
and it can be identified with an open subset in
{\mathbb{C}}{\mathbb{P}}^{n}-[0:z^{1}:\cdots:z^{n}]
.

With the aid of this complex Hopf-coordinate,
we can compute the contact
1
-form in the cone
({\mathbb{R}}^{2n+2})^{*}
.
However, it is convenient to introduce the following notations first:

(3.12)

J:=-I/2\ \ \ \text{and}\ \ \ \eta:=J(r^{-1}dr)=-\eta_{0}/2.

This is because of the normalization in the
d^{c}
-operator, since we have

d^{c}=\frac{i}{2}(\bar{\partial}-\partial)=Jd.

Writing the complex variable
z^{A}
in the polar coordinate as

z^{A}:=r^{A}e^{i\theta_{A}}
,
it follows from equation (
3.11
)

(3.13)

\theta_{0}=\theta-\sum_{{\alpha}=1}^{n}\varphi_{{\alpha}};\ \ \ \ \theta_{{\alpha}}=\theta+\varphi_{{\alpha}}-\sum_{\gamma=1}^{n}\widehat{\varphi_{{\alpha}}}.

Hence it follows

(3.14)

\eta=\frac{1}{2r^{2}}\sum_{A}\frac{x^{A}dy^{A}-y^{A}dx^{A}}{(x^{A})^{2}+(y^{A})^{2}}\cdot(r^{A})^{2}=\frac{1}{2r^{2}}\sum_{A}(r^{A})^{2}d\theta_{A},

and we have

\displaystyle 2\sum_{A}(r^{A})^{2}d\theta_{A}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{r^{2}}{1+\sum_{\beta}|{\zeta}^{\beta}|^{2}}\left\{d\theta-\sum_{{\alpha}}d\varphi_{{\alpha}}+\sum_{{\alpha}}|{\zeta}^{{\alpha}}|^{2}\left(d\theta+d\varphi_{{\alpha}}-\sum_{\gamma}\widehat{d\varphi_{{\alpha}}}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle r^{2}\left\{d\theta-\sum_{{\alpha}}\left(1-\frac{2|{\zeta}^{{\alpha}}|^{2}}{1+\sum_{\beta}|{\zeta}^{\beta}|^{2}}\right)d\varphi_{{\alpha}}\right\}.

Observe that we can write

d\varphi_{{\alpha}}=\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits\left(\frac{d{\zeta}^{{\alpha}}}{{\zeta}^{{\alpha}}}\right).

Therefore, if we put

\cos{\kappa}_{{\alpha}}:=1-\frac{2|{\zeta}^{{\alpha}}|^{2}}{1+\sum_{\beta}|{\zeta}^{\beta}|^{2}}\in[-1,1],

then the formula in equation (
3.2
)
can be reduced to the following form, cf. Lemma 4.1,
[
22
]
.

Lemma 3.1
.

The normalized contact
1
-form can be written under the complex Hopf-coordinate as

(3.16)

\eta=\frac{1}{4}\left\{d\theta-\sum_{{\alpha}}\cos{\kappa}_{{\alpha}}\cdot\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits\left(\frac{d{\zeta}^{{\alpha}}}{{\zeta}^{{\alpha}}}\right)\right\}.

Moreover, one can also directly check the following useful identities:

(3.17)

\frac{\partial\bar{z}^{0}}{\partial{\zeta}^{\beta}}=\frac{\bar{z}^{0}}{4{\zeta}^{\beta}}\cos{\kappa}_{\beta};\ \ \ \ \frac{\partial\bar{z}^{{\alpha}}}{\partial{\zeta}^{\beta}}=\frac{\bar{z}^{{\alpha}}}{4{\zeta}^{\beta}}\cos{\kappa}_{\beta},

for each
{\alpha},\beta=1,\cdots,n
.
Next we can introduce a
local basic function

h

with respect to the complex Hopf-coordinate as

h({\zeta},\bar{\zeta}):=\log\left(1+\sum_{{\alpha}}|{\zeta}^{{\alpha}}|^{2}\right)-\sum_{{\alpha}}\log|{\zeta}^{{\alpha}}|,

and then obtain a
local defining equation
of the contact
1
-form as

(3.18)

\eta=\frac{1}{4}\left\{d\theta-i\left(\partial_{{\zeta}}h-\bar{\partial}_{{\zeta}}h\right)\right\},

where the operators are defined by

\partial_{{\zeta}}h:=\sum_{{\alpha}}\frac{\partial h}{\partial{\zeta}^{{\alpha}}}d{\zeta}^{{\alpha}};\ \ \ \ \bar{\partial}_{{\zeta}}h:=\sum_{{\alpha}}\frac{\partial h}{\partial\bar{\zeta}^{{\alpha}}}d\bar{\zeta}^{{\alpha}}.

Finally it follows

(3.19)

\displaystyle d\eta

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}dd^{c}_{{\zeta}}h

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}dd^{c}_{{\zeta}}\log\left(1+\sum_{{\alpha}}|{\zeta}^{{\alpha}}|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\omega_{FS}.

As we have expected,
the transversal Kähler metric
d\eta
is exactly the Fubini-Study metric
\omega_{FS}

on the quotient space
{\mathbb{C}}{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
, and we have its total volume

\int_{{\mathbb{C}}{\mathbb{P}}^{n}}\omega^{n}_{FS}=\pi^{n}.

Although everything is computed under the complex Hopf-coordinate,
we emphasis that the local defining equation of
\eta
(equation (
3.18
)), and hence equation (
3.19
),
actually follow from the Sasakian structure.
This means that they are in fact independent of the chosen
holomorphic coordinate on
{\mathbb{C}}{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
(induced from a local trivialization),
possibly with a different basic function
h
and a re-normalized angle
\theta
, see
[
18
]
,
[
19
]
.

Remark 3.2
.

There is another local trivialization of the fiber map
p
as

\Psi^{\prime}:{\mathbb{C}}^{*}\times{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\rightarrow p^{-1}({\mathbb{C}}^{n}),

that sends
(r,\theta^{\prime},{\zeta},\bar{\zeta})
to

z^{0}:=\frac{re^{i\theta^{\prime}}}{\left(1+\sum_{\beta}|{\zeta}^{\beta}|^{2}\right)^{1/2}};\ \ \ z^{{\alpha}}=\frac{re^{i\theta^{\prime}}\cdot{\zeta}^{\alpha}}{\left(1+\sum_{\beta}|{\zeta}^{\beta}|^{2}\right)^{1/2}}.

Thus we have a different holomorphic coordinate

{\zeta}\in{\mathbb{C}}{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
that is induced from the trivialization
\Psi^{\prime}
.
Moreover, the local basic function
h^{\prime}
corresponding to
\Psi^{\prime}
is defined as

h^{\prime}({\zeta},\bar{\zeta}):=\log\left(1+\sum_{{\alpha}}|{\zeta}^{{\alpha}}|^{2}\right).

For an
S^{1}
-invariant function,
these two trivializations make no difference on its value
since they only differ by an angle factor, see Remark 3.1,
[
22
]
.
Therefore, we also refer
\Psi^{\prime}
as a complex Hopf-coordinate.

3.3.
The complex structure splits

As we have seen in equation (
3.9
),
the complex structure on the cone
({\mathbb{R}}^{2n+2})^{*}

splits with respect to the Sasakian structure on the sphere.
Moreover, this splitting can be explicitly written down
locally through the complex Hopf-coordinate as follows.

It turns out that
it is easier to first describe it on the cotangent bundle,
namely,
we consider the
(1,0)
-part of the complexified cotangent bundle of the cone as

(T^{*})^{1,0}({\mathbb{R}}^{2n+2})^{*}\subset T^{*}({\mathbb{R}}^{2n+2})^{*}\otimes{\mathbb{C}}.

Then there is a well-defined
1
-form on the cone as

(3.20)

\lambda^{0}:=dr-ir\eta_{0};\ \ \ \ \bar{\lambda}^{0}:=dr+ir\eta_{0},

and it satisfies

I(\lambda^{0})=i\lambda^{0};\ \ \ \ I(\bar{\lambda}^{0})=(-i)\bar{\lambda}^{0}.

Locally we introduce the following

1
-forms
under the complex Hopf-coordinate:

(3.21)

\lambda^{{\alpha}}:=d{\zeta}^{{\alpha}};\ \ \ \ \bar{\lambda}^{{\alpha}}:=d\bar{\zeta}^{{\alpha}},

for all
{\alpha}=1,\cdots,n
.
Since
{\zeta}^{{\alpha}}
’s are holomorphic functions,
it follows

I(\lambda^{{\alpha}})=i\lambda^{{\alpha}};\ \ \ \ I(\bar{\lambda}^{{\alpha}})=(-i)\bar{\lambda}^{{\alpha}}.

Then it is clear that the
n
-tuples

\{\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{n}\}

is a local coframe field of the bundle
(T^{*})^{1,0}({\mathbb{C}}{\mathbb{P}}^{n})
over the base manifold.
Thanks to the local defining equation of
\eta
(equation (
3.18
)),
we can further infer that the
(n+1)
-tuples

\{\lambda_{0},\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{n}\}

builds a local coframe field of the bundle
(T^{*})^{1,0}({\mathbb{R}}^{2n+2})^{*}
over the cone,
and its complex conjugate is a local coframe of the bundle
(T^{*})^{0,1}({\mathbb{R}}^{2n+2})^{*}
.
Clearly, these coframes give a decomposition of the complex structure
with respect to the Kähler cone structure.
Moreover, this construction is due to the Sasakian structure,
i.e. it is independent of the chosen holomorphic coordinate on
{\mathbb{C}}{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
,
because the local defining equation of
\eta
is.

On the other hand, we can also describe the splitting in the dual space:

(3.22)

T({\mathbb{R}}^{2n+2})^{*}\otimes{\mathbb{C}}=\left(L_{e_{0}}\oplus L_{\bar{e}_{0}}\right)\oplus{\mathcal{D}}^{1,0}\oplus{\mathcal{D}}^{0,1},

where the vector fields
e_{0}
and
\bar{e}_{0}
are defined as

e_{0}:=\frac{1}{2}\left\{\partial_{r}+ir^{-1}\xi_{0}\right\};\ \ \ \ \bar{e}_{0}:=\frac{1}{2}\left\{\partial_{r}-ir^{-1}\xi_{0}\right\}.

In fact, the Reeb vector field can written as

\xi_{0}=-I(r\partial_{r})=-2\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta},

and then it is clear that we have

I(e_{0})=ie_{0};\ \ \ \ I(\bar{e}_{0})=(-i)\bar{e}_{0}.

Moreover, we define the following vector fields
under the complex Hopf-coordinate:

e_{{\alpha}}:=\partial_{{\zeta}^{{\alpha}}}+\frac{i}{4{\zeta}^{{\alpha}}}\cos{\kappa}_{{\alpha}}\ \xi_{0};\ \ \ \ \bar{e}_{{\alpha}}:=\partial_{\bar{\zeta}^{{\alpha}}}-\frac{i}{4\bar{\zeta}^{{\alpha}}}\cos{\kappa}_{{\alpha}}\ \xi_{0}.

Then one can check

\lambda^{A}(e_{B})=\delta^{A}_{B},

for all
A,B=0,\cdots,n
.
In particular, the
e_{{\alpha}}
’s, and hence
\bar{e}_{{\alpha}}
’s, belong to
the complexified contact sub-bundle
{\mathcal{D}}\otimes{\mathbb{C}}
.
Moreover, it follows from equation (
3.17
) that we have

(3.23)

I(e_{{\alpha}})=ie_{{\alpha}};\ \ \ \ I(\bar{e}_{{\alpha}})=(-i)\bar{e}_{{\alpha}}.

It follows that the almost complex structure
\Phi_{0}
can be written locally as

\Phi_{0}=i\sum_{{\alpha}}e_{{\alpha}}\otimes d{\zeta}^{{\alpha}}-i\sum_{{\alpha}}\bar{e}_{{\alpha}}\otimes d\bar{\zeta}^{{\alpha}},

and then we have

(3.24)

\Phi_{0}(e_{{\alpha}})=ie_{{\alpha}};\ \ \ \ \Phi_{0}(\bar{e}_{{\alpha}})=(-i)\bar{e}_{{\alpha}}.

Therefore, the
n
-tuples
\{e_{1},\cdots,e_{n}\}

is a local frame of the bundle
{\mathcal{D}}^{1,0}
.
It follows that the
(n+1)
-tuples
\{e_{0},e_{1},\cdots,e_{n}\}

builds a local frame of the bundle
T^{1,0}({\mathbb{R}}^{2n+2})^{*}
.
Together with its complex conjugate, they describe the splitting (equation (
3.22
))
of the complex structure on the tangent space of the cone.

4.
The decomposition formula

In this section, we will compute the
following integral, for a function
u\in{\mathcal{F}}^{\infty}(B_{1})
:

\int_{S_{r}}d^{c}u\wedge(dd^{c}u)^{n},

on the hypersphere
S_{r}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{2n+2}
with radius
r\in(0,1)
.
Consider the space
({\mathbb{R}}^{2n+2})^{*}\cong({\mathbb{C}}^{n+1})^{*}

as a Kähler cone over the Sasakian manifold
S^{2n+1}
,
and denote
(\xi_{0},\eta_{0},\Phi_{0},g_{0})
by its standard Sasakian structure.
We recall the concept of basic forms.

Definition 4.1
.

A
k
-form
\vartheta
on
S^{2n+1}
is called basic if it satisfies

\iota_{\xi_{0}}\vartheta=0;\ \ \ \ \mathcal{L}_{\xi_{0}}\vartheta=0.

The exterior differential preserves basic forms.
There is a natural splitting of the complexification of the bundle of the basic
k
-forms
{\bigwedge}_{B}^{k}(S^{2n+1})
as

{\bigwedge}^{k}_{B}(S^{2n+1})\otimes{\mathbb{C}}=\bigoplus_{i+j=k}{\bigwedge}_{B}^{i,j}(S^{2n+1}),

where
{\bigwedge}_{B}^{i,j}(S^{2n+1})

denotes the bundle of basic forms of type
(i,j)
.
Then one can define the operators
\partial_{B}
and
\bar{\partial}_{B}
.
Put

d_{B}:=d\ |_{{\bigwedge}^{k}_{B}};\ \ \ \ d^{c}_{B}:=\frac{i}{2}(\bar{\partial}_{B}-\partial_{B}),

and it follows as usual

d_{B}=\partial_{B}+\bar{\partial}_{B};\ \ \ d_{B}d^{c}_{B}=i\partial_{B}\bar{\partial}_{B};\ \ \ (d_{B})^{2}=(d^{c}_{B})^{2}=0.

In other words,
the operators
\partial_{B}
and
\bar{\partial}_{B}
are exactly (the pull back of)
the
\partial
and
\bar{\partial}
operators on
{\mathbb{C}}{\mathbb{P}}^{n}

under the isomorphism with the transversal holomorphic structures:
(\nu({\mathcal{F}}_{\xi_{0}}),\bar{J})\cong({\mathcal{D}},\Phi_{0}|_{{\mathcal{D}}})
.

4.1.
Computations

It is clear that
u
is basic,
if it is in the family
{\mathcal{F}}^{\infty}(B_{1})
.
Then we can decompose its exterior derivative as

du=(u_{r})dr+d_{B}u,

and hence it follows

(4.1)

\displaystyle d^{c}u

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(u_{r})Jdr+Jd_{B}u

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(ru_{r})\eta+d^{c}_{B}u,

where
\eta=-\eta_{0}/2
is the normalized contact
1
-form. We compute

(4.2)

\displaystyle dd^{c}u

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d\left\{(ru_{r})\eta+d^{c}_{B}u\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(ru_{r})d\eta+(ru_{r})_{,r}dr\wedge\eta+d_{B}(ru_{r})\wedge\eta+dr\wedge\partial_{r}(d^{c}_{B}u)+d_{B}d^{c}_{B}u

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Theta_{1}+\Theta_{2},

where the
2
-forms are defined as

(4.3)

\displaystyle\Theta_{1}:=(ru_{r})_{,r}dr\wedge\eta+dr\wedge\partial_{r}(d^{c}_{B}u)+d_{B}(ru_{r})\wedge\eta;

(4.4)

\displaystyle\Theta_{2}:=(ru_{r})d\eta+d_{B}d^{c}_{B}u.

Then we first come up with
the following decomposition formula of the complex hessian of
u
restricted to the sphere.

Lemma 4.2
.

For a function
u\in{\mathcal{F}}^{\infty}(B_{1})
, we have

(4.5)

\displaystyle dd^{c}u|_{S_{r}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(ru_{r})d\eta+d_{B}(ru_{r})\wedge\eta+d_{B}d^{c}_{B}u

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Theta_{2}+d_{B}(ru_{r})\wedge\eta,

on each hypersphere
S_{r}
with
r\in(0,1)
.

Next we can compute the
(n,n)
-form on the hypersphere as

(4.6)

\displaystyle(dd^{c}u|_{S_{r}})^{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\Theta_{2}+d_{B}(ru_{r})\wedge\eta\right)^{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Theta_{2}^{n}+n\Theta_{2}^{n-1}\wedge d_{B}(ru_{r})\wedge\eta.

Since the two terms in
\Theta_{2}
are both of type
(1,1)
in the transversal Kähler structure,
we expand its
n
th power as

\displaystyle\Theta_{2}^{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(ru_{r})^{n}(d\eta)^{n}+(d_{B}d^{c}_{B}u)^{n}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}n\\
k\end{array}\right)(ru_{r})^{n-k}(d\eta)^{n-k}\wedge(d_{B}d^{c}_{B}u)^{k}.

Observe that we have for each
k=0,\cdots,n

(4.10)

d^{c}_{B}u\wedge(d\eta)^{n-k}\wedge(d_{B}d^{c}_{B}u)^{k}=0.

It follows

(4.11)

\displaystyle d^{c}u\wedge(dd^{c}u|_{S_{r}})^{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{(ru_{r})\eta+d^{c}_{B}u\right\}\wedge\left\{\Theta_{2}^{n}+n\Theta_{2}^{n-1}\wedge d_{B}(ru_{r})\wedge\eta\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(ru_{r})\eta\wedge\Theta_{2}^{n}+n\ d^{c}_{B}u\wedge\Theta_{2}^{n-1}\wedge d_{B}(ru_{r})\wedge\eta.

The first term on the R.H.S. of (
4.11
) can be computed as

\displaystyle(ru_{r})\eta\wedge\Theta_{2}^{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(ru_{r})^{n+1}\eta\wedge(d\eta)^{n}+(ru_{r})\eta\wedge(d_{B}d^{c}_{B}u)^{n}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}n\\
k\end{array}\right)(ru_{r})^{n-k+1}\eta\wedge(d\eta)^{n-k}\wedge(d_{B}d^{c}_{B}u)^{k},

and the second term is

\displaystyle(-n)\ \eta\wedge d_{B}(ru_{r})\wedge d^{c}_{B}u\wedge\Theta_{2}^{n-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(-n)\ \eta\wedge d_{B}(ru_{r})\wedge d^{c}_{B}u\wedge\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}n-1\\
j-1\end{array}\right)(ru_{r})^{n-j}(d\eta)^{n-j}\wedge(d_{B}d^{c}_{B}u)^{j-1}

In conclusion, we have the following formula.

Lemma 4.3
.

For a function
u\in{\mathcal{F}}^{\infty}(B_{1})
, we have the expansion formula

\displaystyle d^{c}u\wedge(dd^{c}u|_{S_{r}})^{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\eta\wedge\left\{\ \sum_{k=0}^{n}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}n\\
k\end{array}\right)(ru_{r})^{n-k+1}(d\eta)^{n-k}\wedge(d_{B}d^{c}_{B}u)^{k}\right.

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\left.n\ d_{B}(ru_{r})\wedge d^{c}_{B}u\wedge\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}n-1\\
k-1\end{array}\right)(ru_{r})^{n-k}(d\eta)^{n-k}\wedge(d_{B}d^{c}_{B}u)^{k-1}\right\},

Observe that all the terms in the bracket on the R.H.S. of equation (
4.3
)
has transversal degree
2n
.
Then their wedge product with the contact
1
-form
\eta

is equivalent to taking the wedge with
d\theta/4
.
Thanks to equation (
3.16
) and (
3.18
),
we can write down this
(2n+1)
-form under the complex Hopf-coordinate as

\displaystyle d^{c}u\wedge(dd^{c}u|_{S_{r}})^{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}d\theta\wedge\left\{\ \sum_{k=0}^{n}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}n\\
k\end{array}\right)(ru_{r})^{n-k+1}\omega_{FS}^{n-k}\wedge(d_{B}d^{c}_{B}u)^{k}\right.

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\left.n\ d_{B}(ru_{r})\wedge d^{c}_{B}u\wedge\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}n-1\\
k-1\end{array}\right)(ru_{r})^{n-k}\omega_{FS}^{n-k}\wedge(d_{B}d^{c}_{B}u)^{k-1}\right\}.

Here the operators
d_{B}
and
d^{c}_{B}
should be interpreted as
the
d
and
d^{c}
operators on the complex projective space
{\mathbb{C}}{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
.

Recall that we have taken a change of variables
t:=\log r
,
and denote the function
u
under this new variable as

u_{t}({\zeta}):=\hat{u}(t,{\zeta},\bar{\zeta})=u(e^{t},{\zeta},\bar{\zeta}),

and then it follows

\dot{u}_{t}({\zeta}):=\partial_{t}\hat{u}(t,{\zeta},\bar{\zeta})=r\partial_{r}u(r,{\zeta},\bar{\zeta}).

Finally, we obtain the following decomposition formula.

Theorem 4.4
.

For a function
u\in{\mathcal{F}}^{\infty}(B_{1})
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{S_{r}}d^{c}u\wedge(dd^{c}u)^{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\begin{pmatrix}n+1\\
k\end{pmatrix}\int_{{\mathbb{C}}{\mathbb{P}}^{n}}(\dot{u}_{t})^{n+1-k}\omega_{FS}^{n-k}\wedge(d_{B}d^{c}_{B}u)^{k},

on each hypersphere
S_{r}
with radius
r=e^{t}\in(0,1)
.

Proof.

Take integration on both sides of equation (
4.1
),
and we can first integrate out the
d\theta
-direction over
[0,4\pi]

due to Fubini’s Theorem.
Then perform the integration by parts on the second term
in the bracket on the R.H.S. of this equation as

\displaystyle(-n)\int_{{\mathbb{C}}{\mathbb{P}}^{n}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}n-1\\
k-1\end{array}\right)(\dot{u}_{t})^{n-k}d_{B}\dot{u}_{t}\wedge d^{c}_{B}u\wedge\omega_{FS}^{n-k}\wedge(d_{B}d^{c}_{B}u)^{k-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{C}}{\mathbb{P}}^{n}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{n}{n-k+1}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}n-1\\
k-1\end{array}\right)(\dot{u}_{t})^{n-k+1}\omega_{FS}^{n-k}\wedge(d_{B}d^{c}_{B}u)^{k}.

Plug in the first term in the bracket, and we obtain

\displaystyle\left\{\ \sum_{k=0}^{n}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}n\\
k\end{array}\right)+\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{n}{n-k+1}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}n-1\\
k-1\end{array}\right)\right\}(\dot{u}_{t})^{n-k+1}\omega_{FS}^{n-k}\wedge(d_{B}d^{c}_{B}u)^{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}n+1\\
k\end{array}\right)(\dot{u}_{t})^{n+1-k}\omega_{FS}^{n-k}\wedge(d_{B}d^{c}_{B}u)^{k}+(\dot{u}_{t})^{n+1}\omega_{FS}^{n}.

Here we have used the combinatorial identity:

(4.50)

\left(\begin{array}[]{c}n\\
k\end{array}\right)+\frac{n}{n-k+1}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}n-1\\
k-1\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}n+1\\
k\end{array}\right).

Finally, combine with the two terms on the R.H.S. of equation (
4.1
),
and then our result follows.

∎

The above formula (equation (
4.4
))
boils down to the decomposition formula in the two dimensional case
if we take
n=1
, see Theorem 4.4,
[
22
]
.

4.2.
The positivity

The plurisubharmonicity of the function
u

induces the positivity of its complex hessian on
({\mathbb{C}}^{n+1})^{*}
.
As we have seen,
there is a natural splitting of the complex structure of the cone
({\mathbb{C}}^{n+1})^{*}

under the standard Sasakian structure of
S^{2n+1}
.
Therefore, we can study the positivity of the complex hessian
under this splitting.

Recall that decompose the complex hessian of
a function
u\in{\mathcal{F}}^{\infty}(B_{1})
in equation (
4.2
) as

dd^{c}u=\Theta_{1}+\Theta_{2}.

Here the
2
-form
\Theta_{1}
can be written as

(4.51)

\displaystyle\Theta_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(ru_{r})_{,r}dr\wedge\eta+dr\wedge d^{c}_{B}u_{r}+d_{B}(ru_{r})\wedge\eta

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle r^{-1}(ru_{r})_{,r}(i\lambda^{0}\wedge\bar{\lambda}^{0})+\frac{1}{2}\left(\lambda^{0}\wedge d^{c}_{B}u_{r}+\bar{\lambda}^{0}\wedge d^{c}_{B}u_{r}\right)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{i}{2r}\left\{\bar{\lambda}_{0}\wedge d_{B}(ru_{r})-\lambda_{0}\wedge d_{B}(ru_{r})\right\}.

Moreover, the
2
-form
\Theta_{2}
is of degree
(1,1)

under the transversal holomorphic structure.
In particular, it descends to a
(1,1)
-form with continuous coefficients on
{\mathbb{C}}{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
as

\Theta_{2}:=(\dot{u}_{t})\omega_{FS}+d_{B}d^{c}_{B}u.

Then we are going to prove that this
(1,1)
-form is positive for each
t<0
.

Lemma 4.5
.

The
2
-form
\Theta_{2}
is a positive
(1,1)
-current on
{\mathbb{C}}{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
, i.e. we have

(4.52)

(\dot{u}_{t})\omega_{FS}+d_{B}d^{c}_{B}u\geq 0,

for each
t<0
fixed.

Proof.

Let
\gamma^{{\alpha}}
be an arbitrary local
(1,0)
-form on
{\mathbb{C}}{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
for
{\alpha}=2,\cdots,n
.
Then we need to prove the following
(n,n)
-form

T:=\Theta_{2}\wedge(i\gamma^{2}\wedge\bar{\gamma}^{2})\wedge\cdots\wedge(i\gamma^{n}\wedge\bar{\gamma}^{n})

is a positive measure.
Thanks to the splitting of the complexified cotangent bundle,
each of
\gamma^{{\alpha}}
’s can be written as a linear combination of the coframe
\lambda^{\beta}

for
\beta=1,\cdots,n
,
see Section
3.3
.
Then we can write
T
with respect to the local coframe, and obtain

(4.53)

T=f(r,{\zeta},\bar{\zeta})(i\lambda^{1}\wedge\bar{\lambda}^{1})\wedge\cdots\wedge(i\lambda^{n}\wedge\bar{\lambda}^{n}).

Then everything boils down to prove
that the continuous function
f
is always non-negative.
Take
T
as an
(n,n)
-form on the cone
({\mathbb{C}}^{n+1})^{*}
,
and then we can wedge it with the positive
(1,1)
-current
i\lambda^{0}\wedge\bar{\lambda}^{0}
as

(4.54)

\displaystyle(i\lambda^{0}\wedge\bar{\lambda}^{0})\wedge T

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{(i\lambda^{0}\wedge\bar{\lambda}^{0})\wedge\Theta_{2}\right\}\wedge(i\gamma^{2}\wedge\bar{\gamma}^{2})\wedge\cdots\wedge(i\gamma^{n}\wedge\bar{\gamma}^{n})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{(i\lambda^{0}\wedge\bar{\lambda}^{0})\wedge(\Theta_{1}+\Theta_{2})\right\}\wedge(i\gamma^{2}\wedge\bar{\gamma}^{2})\wedge\cdots\wedge(i\gamma^{n}\wedge\bar{\gamma}^{n})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle dd^{c}u\wedge(i\lambda^{0}\wedge\bar{\lambda}^{0})\wedge(i\gamma^{2}\wedge\bar{\gamma}^{2})\wedge\cdots\wedge(i\gamma^{n}\wedge\bar{\gamma}^{n})

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle 0.

Here we have used equation (
4.51
) on the second line
on the R.H.S. of equation (
4.54
).
Hence we can infer
f(r,{\zeta},\bar{\zeta})\geq 0
,
and then our result follows.

∎

5.
The general results

The goal of this section is to provide a useful estimate on the
complex Monge-Ampère mass

\int_{B_{r}}(dd^{c}u)^{n+1}

on each ball
B_{r}
inside the unit ball.
It means that we need to find an upper bound
of the decomposition formula (Theorem
4.4
).
This will be finished via an induction argument.

Recall that the maximal directional Lelong number

M_{A}(u)
at a distance
A>0
is defined as
the maximum of
\dot{u}_{t}({\zeta})
over all
{\zeta}\in{\mathbb{C}}{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
with fixed
t=-A
.
It is always a non-negative and finite real number, and non-increasing in
A
as
A\rightarrow+\infty
.

5.1.
A priori estimates

In the following context, the positivity condition (Lemma
4.5
) will be used repeatedly.
Therefore, it is convenient to introduce the following brief notations for the
(1,1)
-forms:

{\mathbf{a}}:=\omega_{FS};\ \ \ \ \ {\mathbf{b}}:=d_{B}d^{c}_{B}u,

and for the functions

{\mathbf{c}}:=\dot{u}_{t};\ \ \ \ \ M:=M_{A}(u),

for any
t<-A
.
Moreover, we also denote

{\mathbf{e}}:={\mathbf{c}}{\mathbf{a}}+{\mathbf{b}}=\Theta_{2},

and then it follows from the positivity conditions

(5.1)

\displaystyle{\mathbf{a}}>0;\ \ \ \ {\mathbf{e}}\geq 0;\ \ \ \ M\geq{\mathbf{c}}\geq 0.

During the combinatorial computation,
we will delete the symbol
\wedge

between the
2
-forms
{\mathbf{a}}
,
{\mathbf{b}}
and
{\mathbf{e}}
.
In fact,
there is no harm to pretend the wedge product between them
as an ordinary multiplication between polynomials,
since
2
-forms are commutative with respect to the wedge.
Then we first have a rough estimate as follows.

Lemma 5.1
.

For a function
u\in{\mathcal{F}}^{\infty}(B_{1})
, we have the estimate

(5.2)

\int_{B_{r}}(dd^{c}u)^{n+1}\leq(n+1)\pi^{n+1}M_{A}^{n+1}(u),

for all
r<e^{-A}
.

Proof.

A direct computation shows that we have the following equation

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}n+1\\
k\end{array}\right)M_{A}^{n+1-k}\omega_{FS}^{n-k}\wedge(d_{B}d^{c}_{B}u)^{k}

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}n+1\\
k\end{array}\right)(\dot{u}_{t})^{n+1-k}\omega_{FS}^{n-k}\wedge(d_{B}d^{c}_{B}u)^{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(M_{A}-\dot{u}_{t})\left\{\sum_{k=0}^{n}(M_{A}\omega_{FS}+d_{B}d^{c}_{B}u)^{n-k}\wedge\Theta_{2}^{k}\right\}.

In fact, we can infer it from the combinatorial identity

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}n+1\\
k\end{array}\right)M^{n+1-k}{\mathbf{a}}^{n-k}{\mathbf{b}}^{k}-\sum_{k=0}^{n}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}n+1\\
k\end{array}\right){\mathbf{c}}^{n+1-k}{\mathbf{a}}^{n-k}{\mathbf{b}}^{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(M-{\mathbf{c}})\left\{\sum_{k=0}^{n}(M{\mathbf{a}}+{\mathbf{b}})^{n-k}\cdot({\mathbf{c}}{\mathbf{a}}+{\mathbf{b}})^{k}\right\}\geq 0.

Thanks to the Stokes Theorem, each term like

\omega^{n-k}_{FS}\wedge(d_{B}d^{c}_{B}u)^{k}

for
k=1,\cdots,n
vanishes if we take its integration on
{\mathbb{C}}{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
.
Therefore, the R.H.S. of the decomposition formula (Theorem
4.4
)
can be estimated from the above as

(5.16)

\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{S_{r}}d^{c}u\wedge(dd^{c}u)^{n}\leq(n+1)M_{A}^{n+1}\int_{{\mathbb{C}}{\mathbb{P}}^{n}}\omega_{FS}^{n},

and then our result follows.

∎

First take the radius
r\rightarrow 0^{+}
on the L.H.S. of equation (
5.2
),
and then it converges to the residual Monge-Ampère mass of
u
at the origin.
Next let the distance
A
converges to
+\infty
on the R.H.S. of this equation,
and then we obtain the following rough estimate.

Theorem 5.2
.

For a function
u\in{\mathcal{F}}^{\infty}(B_{1})
, we can control its residual mass
by the maximal directional Lelong number at the origin as

(5.17)

\tau_{u}(0)\leq(n+1)\lambda^{n+1}_{u}(0).

However, this rough estimate in Lemma
5.1

is not enough for our purpose to prove the zero mass conjecture,
since there is no Lelong number that could possibly appear
from the R.H.S. of equation (
5.2
).
The difficulty is that we should keep at least one
{\mathbf{c}}
,
but no combination terms like
{\mathbf{c}}\cdot{\mathbf{b}}

in the end of the estimate.

In order to overcome this difficulty,
we will utilize the positivity of

{\mathbf{a}}
,
{\mathbf{c}}
and
{\mathbf{e}}
, and rewrite the decomposition formula as follows.

Corollary 5.3
.

For a function
u\in{\mathcal{F}}^{\infty}(B_{1})
,
the decomposition formula can be written as

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{S_{r}}d^{c}u\wedge(dd^{c}u)^{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}n+1\\
k+1\end{array}\right)(-1)^{k}\int_{{\mathbb{C}}{\mathbb{P}}^{n}}(\dot{u}_{t})^{k+1}\omega_{FS}^{k}\wedge\Theta_{2}^{n-k}.

on each hypersphere
S_{r}
with radius
r\in(0,1)
.

Proof.

It directly follows from the following combinatorial identity

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}n+1\\
k\end{array}\right){\mathbf{c}}^{n-k+1}{\mathbf{a}}^{n-k}{\mathbf{b}}^{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n+1}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}n+1\\
k\end{array}\right)(-1)^{k-1}({\mathbf{c}}{\mathbf{a}}+{\mathbf{b}})^{n+1-k}{\mathbf{c}}^{k}{\mathbf{a}}^{k-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{n}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}n+1\\
j+1\end{array}\right)(-1)^{j}{\mathbf{e}}^{n-j}{\mathbf{c}}^{j+1}{\mathbf{a}}^{j}.

∎

The formula in Corollary
5.3
gives a new representation of
the decomposition formula of the complex Monge-Ampère mass,
such that each monomial in it like
{\mathbf{e}}^{n-j}{\mathbf{c}}^{j+1}{\mathbf{a}}^{j}
is positive.
Then we can infer the following crucial estimate on the upper bound of such
monomials on the R.H.S. of equation (
5.3
).
Before moving on, we introduce the notation

\{{\mathbf{a}},{\mathbf{b}}\}^{n}_{1}.

It stands for a polynomial consisting of
monomials like
{\mathbf{a}}^{n-k}\cdot{\mathbf{b}}^{k}
for
k=1,\cdots,n
,
and here we permit the constant
M
appearing as coefficients of the monomials.
We note that the integral of each monomial inside the polynomial
\{{\mathbf{a}},{\mathbf{b}}\}^{n}_{1}

vanishes, since we have

\int_{{\mathbb{C}}{\mathbb{P}}^{n}}\omega_{FS}^{n-k}\wedge(d_{B}d^{c}_{B}u)^{k}=0,

for each
k=1,\cdots,n
by the Stokes Theorem.
Moreover, the part in a polynomial without the factor

\{{\mathbf{a}},{\mathbf{b}}\}_{1}^{n}
will be called as
the principal part
of this polynomial.

Lemma 5.4
.

For each integer
0\leq k\leq n
,
there is a positive constant
B_{k}:=B(k,n)
such that we have

(5.30)

\int_{{\mathbb{C}}{\mathbb{P}}^{n}}(\dot{u}_{t})^{n+1-k}\omega_{FS}^{n-k}\wedge\Theta_{2}^{k}\leq B_{k}M_{A}^{n}(u)\int_{{\mathbb{C}}{\mathbb{P}}^{n}}(\dot{u}_{t})\omega_{FS}^{n},

for all
r=e^{t}<e^{-A}
.
Moreover, the constants can be constructed inductively by setting
B_{0}=1
, and satisfying

B_{k+1}=\sum_{j=0}^{k}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}k\\
j\end{array}\right)B_{j},

for each
k=0,\cdots,n-1
.

Proof.

First we claim that the following estimate holds

(5.31)

{\mathbf{c}}{\mathbf{a}}^{n-k}{\mathbf{e}}^{k}\leq B_{k}M^{k}{\mathbf{c}}{\mathbf{a}}^{n}+\{{\mathbf{a}},{\mathbf{b}}\}^{n}_{1},

for each
k=0,\cdots,n
. Then we have

(5.32)

{\mathbf{c}}^{n-k+1}{\mathbf{a}}^{n-k}{\mathbf{e}}^{k}\leq B_{k}M^{n}{\mathbf{c}}{\mathbf{a}}^{n}+\{{\mathbf{a}},{\mathbf{b}}\}^{n}_{1},

for each
k
, and our result follows from the Stokes Theorem.

In order to prove the claim,
we will invoke an induction argument on equation (
5.31
).
First check for
k=0
,
and hence we have
B_{0}=1
.
Then we have for each
k=1,\cdots,n

(5.33)

\displaystyle{\mathbf{a}}^{n-k}{\mathbf{e}}^{k}{\mathbf{c}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle M{\mathbf{a}}^{n-k}({\mathbf{b}}+{\mathbf{a}}{\mathbf{c}})({\mathbf{b}}+M{\mathbf{a}})^{k-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{{\mathbf{a}},{\mathbf{b}}\}^{n}_{1}+M{\mathbf{a}}^{n-k+1}{\mathbf{c}}({\mathbf{b}}+M{\mathbf{a}})^{k-1}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\{{\mathbf{a}},{\mathbf{b}}\}^{n}_{1}+M{\mathbf{a}}^{n-k+1}{\mathbf{c}}({\mathbf{e}}+M{\mathbf{a}})^{k-1},

and then it follows from our induction hypothesis

\displaystyle M{\mathbf{a}}^{n-k+1}{\mathbf{c}}({\mathbf{e}}+M{\mathbf{a}})^{k-1}=\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}k-1\\
j\end{array}\right)M^{k-j}{\mathbf{a}}^{n-j}{\mathbf{e}}^{j}{\mathbf{c}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle M^{k}\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}k-1\\
j\end{array}\right)B_{j}{\mathbf{a}}^{n}{\mathbf{c}}+\{{\mathbf{a}},{\mathbf{b}}\}^{n}_{1}.

Here the constant
B_{k}
is defined inductively by

B_{k}=\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}k-1\\
j\end{array}\right)B_{j}.

Combine with equation (
5.33
) and (
5.1
),
and then our claim follows.

∎

Then we are ready to find a useful upper bound of the
complex Monge-Ampère mass via the decomposition formula
and estimate in Lemma
5.4
.

Proposition 5.5
.

For a function
u\in{\mathcal{F}}^{\infty}(B_{1})
,
there exists a dimensional constant
C_{n}\geq(n+1)
satisfying

(5.40)

\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{B_{r}}(dd^{c}u)^{n+1}\leq C_{n}M^{n}_{A}(u)\int_{{\mathbb{C}}{\mathbb{P}}^{n}}(\dot{u}_{t})\omega^{n}_{FS},

for each
r=e^{t}<e^{-A}
.

Proof.

Thanks to Theorem
4.4
and Corollary
5.3
,
it is equivalent to estimate
the following polynomial from the above

(5.41)

\sum_{j=0}^{n}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}n+1\\
j+1\end{array}\right)(-1)^{j}{\mathbf{e}}^{n-j}{\mathbf{c}}^{j+1}{\mathbf{a}}^{j},

and we note that only the principle part will matter in the end.

Step 1
.
Drop off all the negative terms.
More precisely, there are two cases for even and odd dimensions.
For instance, the polynomial in equation (
5.41
)
can be estimated from the above, if
n=2m
is even

(5.42)

\sum_{k=0}^{m}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}2m+1\\
2k+1\end{array}\right){\mathbf{e}}^{2m-2k}{\mathbf{c}}^{2k+1}{\mathbf{a}}^{2k}.

On the other hand, if
n=2m+1
is odd, we have

(5.43)

\sum_{k=0}^{m}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}2m+2\\
2k+1\end{array}\right){\mathbf{e}}^{2m-2k+1}{\mathbf{c}}^{2k+1}{\mathbf{a}}^{2k}.

Then everything boils down to estimate the upper bounds
of the polynomials in equation (
5.42
) and (
5.43
).
In the following, we will deal with the even case first.

Step 2
.
For an even
n=2m
,
we apply Lemma
5.4
to equation (
5.42
) as

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{m}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}2m+1\\
2k+1\end{array}\right){\mathbf{c}}^{2k+1}{\mathbf{a}}^{2k}{\mathbf{e}}^{2m-2k}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle M^{n}\sum_{k=0}^{m}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}2m+1\\
2k+1\end{array}\right)B_{2m-2k}{\mathbf{c}}{\mathbf{a}}^{2m}+\{{\mathbf{a}},{\mathbf{b}}\}^{2m}_{1}.

Thanks to the Stokes Theorem,
our result follows with the dimensional constant

C_{2m}:=\sum_{k=0}^{m}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}2m+1\\
2k+1\end{array}\right)B_{2m-2k}.

Step 3
.
For an odd
n=2m+1
,
we apply Lemma
5.4
to equation (
5.43
) as

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{m}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}2m+2\\
2k+1\end{array}\right){\mathbf{c}}^{2k+1}{\mathbf{a}}^{2k}{\mathbf{e}}^{2m-2k+1}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle M^{n}\sum_{k=0}^{m}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}2m+2\\
2k+1\end{array}\right)B_{2m-2k+1}{\mathbf{c}}{\mathbf{a}}^{2m+1}+\{{\mathbf{a}},{\mathbf{b}}\}^{2m+1}_{1}.

Then
our result follows from the Stokes Theorem again,
with the dimensional constant

C_{2m+1}:=\sum_{k=0}^{m}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}2m+2\\
2k+1\end{array}\right)B_{2m-2k+1}.

Finally, it is straightforward to check that the constant
C_{n}
is no smaller than
(n+1)
in both cases.

∎

The inequality in Proposition
5.5
is not sharp,
since all the negative terms have been dropped.
In the following examples,
we calculate the dimensional constant
C_{n}

for small
n
’s.

Example 5.6
.

For
n=1
odd and
m=0
, we can first compute

(5.56)

{\mathbf{c}}^{2}{\mathbf{a}}+2{\mathbf{c}}{\mathbf{b}}=2{\mathbf{e}}{\mathbf{c}}-{\mathbf{a}}{\mathbf{c}}^{2}\leq 2{\mathbf{e}}{\mathbf{c}},

and then it follows

(5.57)

\displaystyle 2{\mathbf{e}}{\mathbf{c}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2M({\mathbf{b}}+{\mathbf{a}}{\mathbf{c}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2M{\mathbf{b}}+2M{\mathbf{a}}{\mathbf{c}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{{\mathbf{a}},{\mathbf{b}}\}^{1}_{1}+2M{\mathbf{a}}{\mathbf{c}}.

Then we have the dimensional constant
C_{1}=2
.
Comparing with the upper bound that we obtained in the previous work
[
22
]
,
there is no surprise that the estimate has been improved,
since a more accurate
positivity condition (Lemma
4.5
) has been used.

Moreover, we note that the above estimate is not sharp,
since we have removed all the negative terms. In fact,
we can obtain a better estimate as

(5.58)

{\mathbf{c}}^{2}{\mathbf{a}}+2{\mathbf{c}}{\mathbf{b}}\leq\{{\mathbf{a}},{\mathbf{b}}\}^{1}_{1}+2M{\mathbf{a}}{\mathbf{c}}-{\mathbf{a}}{\mathbf{c}}^{2}.

Example 5.7
.

For
n=2
even and
m=1
, we compute

(5.59)

\displaystyle{\mathbf{c}}^{3}{\mathbf{a}}^{2}+3{\mathbf{c}}^{2}{\mathbf{a}}{\mathbf{b}}+3{\mathbf{c}}{\mathbf{b}}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathbf{c}}^{3}{\mathbf{a}}^{2}-3{\mathbf{c}}^{2}{\mathbf{a}}{\mathbf{e}}+3{\mathbf{c}}{\mathbf{e}}^{2}\leq M^{2}{\mathbf{a}}^{2}{\mathbf{c}}+3{\mathbf{c}}{\mathbf{e}}^{2},

and then it follows

(5.60)

\displaystyle 3{\mathbf{c}}{\mathbf{e}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 3M({\mathbf{b}}+{\mathbf{a}}{\mathbf{c}})({\mathbf{b}}+M{\mathbf{a}})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\{{\mathbf{a}},{\mathbf{b}}\}^{2}_{1}+3M{\mathbf{a}}{\mathbf{c}}({\mathbf{e}}+M{\mathbf{a}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{{\mathbf{a}},{\mathbf{b}}\}^{2}_{1}+3M{\mathbf{a}}{\mathbf{c}}{\mathbf{e}}+3M^{2}{\mathbf{a}}^{2}{\mathbf{c}}.

Plug equation (
5.57
) into the last line on the R.H.S. of the above equation,
and we obtain

(5.61)

3{\mathbf{c}}{\mathbf{e}}^{2}\leq\{{\mathbf{a}},{\mathbf{b}}\}^{2}_{1}+6M^{2}{\mathbf{a}}^{2}{\mathbf{c}}.

Then we have the dimensional constant
C_{2}=7
.

Example 5.8
.

For
n=3
odd and
m=1
, we compute

(5.62)

\displaystyle{\mathbf{c}}^{4}{\mathbf{a}}^{3}+4{\mathbf{c}}^{3}{\mathbf{a}}^{2}{\mathbf{b}}+6{\mathbf{c}}^{2}{\mathbf{a}}{\mathbf{b}}^{2}+4{\mathbf{c}}{\mathbf{b}}^{3}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 4M^{2}{\mathbf{a}}^{2}{\mathbf{e}}{\mathbf{c}}+4{\mathbf{e}}^{3}{\mathbf{c}}.

and then it follows

(5.63)

\displaystyle{\mathbf{c}}{\mathbf{e}}^{3}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle M({\mathbf{b}}+{\mathbf{a}}{\mathbf{c}})({\mathbf{b}}+M{\mathbf{a}})^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\{{\mathbf{a}},{\mathbf{b}}\}^{3}_{1}+M{\mathbf{a}}{\mathbf{c}}({\mathbf{e}}+M{\mathbf{a}})^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{{\mathbf{a}},{\mathbf{b}}\}^{3}_{1}+M{\mathbf{a}}{\mathbf{c}}{\mathbf{e}}^{2}+2M^{2}{\mathbf{a}}^{2}{\mathbf{e}}{\mathbf{c}}+M^{3}{\mathbf{a}}^{3}{\mathbf{c}}.

Plug equation (
5.57
) and (
5.61
) into the last line on the R.H.S. of the above equation,
and we obtain

(5.64)

{\mathbf{c}}{\mathbf{e}}^{3}\leq\{{\mathbf{a}},{\mathbf{b}}\}^{3}_{1}+5M^{3}{\mathbf{a}}^{3}{\mathbf{c}}.

Then we have the dimensional constant
C_{3}=24
.

5.2.
The results

For a function
u\in{\mathcal{F}}^{\infty}(B_{1})
,
recall that we have defined the non-negative functional

I(u_{t}):=\int_{{\mathbb{C}}{\mathbb{P}}^{n}}(\dot{u}_{t})\omega_{FS}^{n},

for all
t<0
.
Then we can rewrite the estimate
in Proposition
5.5
as

(5.65)

\pi^{-1}\mbox{MA}(u)(B_{r})\leq C_{n}M_{A}^{n}(u)\cdot I(u_{t}),

for all
r=e^{t}<e^{-A}
.
As a direct consequence,
our main result follows for an
S^{1}
-invariant plurisubharmonic function
that is also
C^{2}
-continuous outside the origin.

Theorem 5.9
.

For a function
u\in{\mathcal{F}}^{\infty}(B_{1})
,
there is a dimensional constant
C_{n}\geq(n+1)
satisfying

(5.66)

\tau_{u}(0)\leq C_{n}[\lambda_{u}(0)]^{n}\cdot\nu_{u}(0).

Proof.

First take
r\rightarrow 0^{+}
, i.e.
t\rightarrow-\infty
,
on both sides of equation (
5.40
)
and we obtain

(5.67)

\tau_{u}(0)\leq C_{n}M^{n}_{A}(u)\nu_{u}(0).

Then the result follows by taking
A\rightarrow+\infty
.

∎

As a corollary, we confirm the zero mass conjecture in this special case.

Corollary 5.10
.

For a function
u\in{\mathcal{F}}^{\infty}(B_{1})
,
we have

\nu_{u}(0)=0\Rightarrow\tau_{u}(0)=0.

For a general function
u\in{\mathcal{F}}(B_{1})
, we can use the approximation of the functional
by its standard regularization

u_{\varepsilon}:=u*\rho_{\varepsilon}
.
Then we are ready to prove the main theorem.

Theorem 5.11
.

For a function
u\in{\mathcal{F}}(B_{1})
,
there is a dimensional constant
C_{n}\geq(n+1)
satisfying

(5.68)

\tau_{u}(0)\leq 2C_{n}[\lambda_{u}(0)]^{n}\cdot\nu_{u}(0).

Proof.

It is enough to prove the following estimate

(5.69)

\tau_{u}(0)\leq C_{n}\left(2M_{A}(u)+{\kappa}\right)^{n}\cdot\nu_{u}(0),

for each
A>1
large and
{\kappa}>0
small.
Take two large constants as

2<A<2A<B.

Thanks to Lemma
2.3
and
Proposition
2.11
,
we can extract
a subsequence
u_{\varepsilon_{k}}
from the regularization of
u

satisfying

(5.70)

\mbox{MA}(u_{\varepsilon_{k}})(B_{r})\rightarrow\mbox{MA}(u)(B_{r})

and

(5.71)

I(u_{\varepsilon_{k},t})\rightarrow I(u_{t}),

as
k\rightarrow+\infty
for almost all
t=\log r\in[-B,-A]
.
Meanwhile, Lemma
2.12
implies that we have
a uniform control of the maximal directional Lelong numbers of the regularization as

(5.72)

M_{A^{\prime}}(u_{\varepsilon})\leq 2M_{A}(u)+C\varepsilon,

for all
2A<A^{\prime}<B
and
\varepsilon<e^{-2A}/2
.
Apply Proposition
5.5
to the subsequence,
and then we obtain the estimate

(5.73)

\displaystyle\pi^{-1}\mbox{MA}(u_{\varepsilon_{k}})(B_{r})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C_{n}M_{A^{\prime}}^{n}(u_{\varepsilon_{k}})I(u_{\varepsilon_{k},t})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C_{n}\left(2M_{A}(u)+Ce^{-2A}/2\right)^{n}I(u_{\varepsilon_{k},t}),

for all
-2A<t<-B
and
k
large enough.
Take
k\rightarrow+\infty

on both sides of equation (
5.73
),
and combine with equation (
5.70
) and (
5.71
).
Hence we have the following inequality

(5.74)

\pi^{n}\tau_{u}(0)\leq C_{n}\left(2M_{A}(u)+Ce^{-2A}/2\right)^{n}I(u_{t}),

for almost all
t=\log r\in[-B,-2A]
.
Fix any
A>\log(C/{\kappa})
, and then
equation (
5.69
) follows by taking
B\rightarrow+\infty
.
Hence our main result follows.

∎

Finally, the zero mass conjecture directly follows from
Proposition
2.8
and the above estimate
for an
S^{1}
-invariant plurisubharmoinc function.

Theorem 5.12
.

For a function
u\in{\mathcal{F}}(B_{1})
,
we have

\nu_{u}(0)=0\Rightarrow\tau_{u}(0)=0.

We remark that the estimate in Theorem
5.11
can be improved.
With a stronger apriori estimate on the maximal directional Lelong numbers (Remark 6.7,
[
22
]
),
we can further obtain

(5.75)

\tau_{u}(0)\leq C_{n}[\lambda_{u}(0)]^{n}\cdot\nu_{u}(0),

for any function
u\in{\mathcal{F}}(B_{1})
.
In
{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
with constant
C_{1}=2
,
one can directly check that the inequality in
equation (
5.75
) is satisfied for all

S^{1}
-invariant examples provided in Section 6,
[
22
]
.

However, this estimate is still not sharp,
since all negative terms have been removed during the estimates.
Finally, we note that
this control of the residual Monge-Ampère mass
(by its Lelong number and maximal directional Lelong number) fails if the function is no longer
S^{1}
-invariant, see Example 6.13,
[
22
]
and
[
14
]
.

5.3.
Energy functionals

Through a variational approach,
we have another point of view to describe
the decomposition formula (Theorem
4.4
)
and the result of the zero mass conjecture.

In fact,
we can think this formula to be
the push forward
of the complex Monge-Ampère measure
of a
u\in{\mathcal{F}}^{\infty}(B_{1})

from the Kähler cone
S^{2n+1}\times{\mathbb{R}}_{+}

to its base manifold
{\mathbb{C}}{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
.
To see this, re-write equation (
4.4
) as follows:

(5.76)

\pi^{-1}\mbox{MA}(u)(B_{r})=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\begin{pmatrix}n+1\\
k\end{pmatrix}E_{n,k}(u_{t}),

where we define the functionals as

E_{n,k}(u_{t}):=\int_{{\mathbb{C}}{\mathbb{P}}^{n}}(\dot{u}_{t})^{n+1-k}\omega_{FS}^{n-k}\wedge(i\partial\bar{\partial}_{{\zeta}}u_{t})^{k},

where
\partial_{{\zeta}}
,
\bar{\partial}_{\zeta}
and
\partial\bar{\partial}_{\zeta}
mean
operators on
{\mathbb{C}}{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
.
In particular,
the last term
E_{n,n}

is related to the
pluri-complex energy

{\mathcal{E}}
(see
[
5
]
,
[
4
]
) in the following way:

(n+1)E_{n,n}(u_{t})=-\frac{d}{dt}{\mathcal{E}}(u_{t}),\ \ \ \text{where}\ \ \ {\mathcal{E}}(u):=\int_{{\mathbb{C}}{\mathbb{P}}^{n}}(-u)(i\partial\bar{\partial}_{{\zeta}}u)^{n}.

In the domain case,
it is a well-known fact that
the second variation of
-{\mathcal{E}}
is
the push forward of the complex Monge-Ampère operator.
Hence the energy
{\mathcal{E}}
is a concave functional along the so called
sub-geodesic ray
.
For more details about geodesics and sub-geodesics in the space of Kähler potentials,
see
[
17
]
,
[
31
]
,
[
11
]
and
[
24
]
.

In our case, it is plausible to take
u_{t}
for a function
u\in{\mathcal{F}}^{\infty}(B_{1})
as a sub-geodesic ray
with
C^{2}
-regularity,
and it is a
geodesic ray
if the complex Monge-Ampère mass of
u
vanishes, i.e. we have

(dd^{c}u)^{n+1}=0.

Then we can take the primitives of the above functionals,
and define the following energies (up to a constant) along a sub-geodesic ray:

\frac{d}{dt}{\mathcal{E}}_{n,k}(u_{t}):=-\begin{pmatrix}n+1\\
k\end{pmatrix}E_{n,k}(u_{t}).

In particular, we take
{\mathcal{E}}_{n,n}={\mathcal{E}}
.
Then equation (
5.76
) can be rewritten as

(5.77)

{\mathcal{M}}=-\sum_{k=0}^{n}{\mathcal{E}}_{n,k},

along a sub-geodesic ray up to a constant, where
{\mathcal{M}}
is a
t
-primitive of the measure
\pi^{-1}\mbox{MA}(u)(B_{r})
.
It is clear now that the decomposition formula
induces a push forward of the complex Monge-Ampère operator, under the Sasakian structure of the sphere.
That is to say,
the non-triviality of the Kähler cone structure
is reflected by the terms like

{\mathcal{E}}_{n,k}
for
k=0,\cdots,n-1
in the formula.
Furthermore,
it implies the following concavity of the energy.

Corollary 5.13
.

Along a sub-geodesic ray
u_{t}
with
C^{2}
-regularity,
the energy functional

\sum_{k=0}^{n}{\mathcal{E}}_{n,k}

is concave. Moreover,
it is affine if and only if
u_{t}
is a geodesic ray.

The two dimensional version of the above Corollary has been
proved in Section 7,
[
22
]
,
and this is its higher dimensional analogue.

Finally, we would like to point out that the first term
E_{n,0}
in equation (
5.76
)
also has a meaning.
It is in fact a kind of
L^{p}
-Lelong number for
p=n+1
,
since we have the following convergence due to Lemma
2.5
:

(5.78)

\lim_{t\rightarrow-\infty}E_{n,0}(u_{t})=[\nu_{u}(0)]^{n+1}.

Recall that we introduce a primitive of the functional
I
as

{\mathcal{I}}(u_{t}):=\int_{{\mathbb{C}}{\mathbb{P}}^{n}}u_{t}\omega_{FS}^{n}.

Thanks to equation (
5.77
) and (
5.78
), we can rephrase the zero mass conjecture
in terms of the energies.

Corollary 5.14
.

Along a sub-geodesic ray
u_{t}
with
C^{2}
-regularity,
assume that
the asymptote of
the functional
{\mathcal{I}}
at
-\infty
is zero.
Then we have

(5.79)

\lim_{t\rightarrow-\infty}\frac{d}{dt}{\mathcal{M}}(u_{t})=\lim_{t\rightarrow-\infty}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{d}{dt}{\mathcal{E}}_{n,k}(u_{t})=0.

It will be interesting to see if Corollary
5.14

also holds for a function
u
in the family
{\mathcal{F}}(B_{1})
,
i.e. along a bounded sub-geodesic ray.
However,
there is no a priori reason that these energies
{\mathcal{E}}_{n,k}
are well defined in that case.

6.
The method of moving frames

In this section, we are going to provide
an alternative proof of the decomposition formula (Theorem
4.4
)
via the method of moving frames, see
[
13
]
,
[
12
]
.
Moreover, the complex hessian equation of a function without symmetry will be presented.

In the language of moving frames,
a real coordinate
(x^{A},y^{A})
of a point
p\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2n+2}

should be interpreted as a vector

x^{A}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{A}}+y^{A}\frac{\partial}{\partial y^{A}}\in T_{p}{\mathbb{R}}^{2n+2},

Then a complex coordinate
(z^{A})
corresponds to a vector in
T_{p}^{1,0}({\mathbb{C}}^{n+1})
,
and its complex conjugate
(\bar{z}^{A})
is a vector in
T_{p}^{0,1}({\mathbb{C}}^{n+1})
.
In the following, we are going to use the Einstein summation convention.

6.1.
The structure equations

Let
\{e_{0},\cdots,e_{n}\}
be a unitary field of
{\mathbb{C}}^{n+1}
,
which means that we have for
A,B=0,\cdots,n

(6.1)

(e_{A}\ ,\ e_{B})=\delta_{AB},

where the hermitian inner product is taken on
{\mathbb{C}}^{n+1}
.
Taking the exterior derivative of the position vector
z
, we can write

(6.2)

dz=\omega^{A}e_{A};\ \ \ \ \omega^{A}=a^{A}_{B}\cdot dz^{B},

where
(\omega^{A})
is a vector-valued
(1,0)
-form,
and
(a^{A}_{B})
is a unitary matrix with coefficients as smooth functions in
{\mathbb{C}}^{n+1}
.
Then the Euclidean metric of
{\mathbb{C}}^{n+1}
is given as

ds^{2}_{e}=\omega^{A}\overline{\omega^{A}}.

Hence it follow from equation (
6.1
) and (
6.2
) as

(6.3)

de_{A}=\omega^{B}_{A}e_{B};\ \ \ \ \omega^{B}_{C}=a^{B}_{A}\cdot d\left(\overline{a^{C}_{A}}\right),

where
(\omega^{A}_{B})
is a matrix-valued
1
-form on
{\mathbb{C}}^{n+1}
,
and then we have

(6.4)

\omega^{A}_{B}+\overline{\omega^{B}_{A}}=0.

Taking exterior derivatives on both sides of equation (
6.3
) and (
6.2
),
we obtain the following structure equations

(6.5)

\displaystyle d\omega^{A}+\omega^{A}_{B}\wedge\omega^{B}=0;

(6.6)

\displaystyle d\omega^{A}_{B}+\omega^{A}_{C}\wedge\omega^{C}_{B}=0.

The above equations reflect the fact that the Euclidean space is flat.
Moreover, we can decompose the differential of a smooth function
u
as

du=\partial u+\bar{\partial}u,

where

\partial u=u_{A}\omega^{A};\ \ \ \bar{\partial}u=u_{\bar{A}}\overline{\omega^{A}};\ \ \ u_{\bar{A}}=\overline{(u_{A})}.

6.2.
The Fubini-Study metric

Next we set the first element in the frame as the
unit vector towards the point:

e_{0}:=e^{-t}z,

where the variable is
t:=\log|z|
.
Take exterior derivatives, and we obtain

(6.7)

dz=e^{t}(dt+\omega^{0}_{0})\cdot e_{0}+e^{t}\omega_{0}^{{\alpha}}\cdot e_{{\alpha}},

for all
{\alpha}=1,\cdots,n
.
Then it follows

(6.8)

\omega^{0}=e^{t}(dt+\omega^{0}_{0});\ \ \ \ \ \omega^{{\alpha}}=e^{t}\omega^{{\alpha}}_{0}.

Fixing such an
e_{0}
,
a local unitary frame field

(e_{0},e_{1},\cdots,e_{n})
is in fact a local section
of the principle bundle
U(n+1)
over
{\mathbb{C}}{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
.
That is to say, we have the projection map

\pi:U(n+1)\rightarrow{\mathbb{C}}{\mathbb{P}}^{n},

defined as

(e_{0},e_{1},\cdots,e_{n})\rightarrow[e_{0}],

where
[e_{0}]
is the equivalent class of
z
in
{\mathbb{C}}{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
.
It is clear that each fiber of this projection map
is isomorphic to
U(1)\times U(n)
,
and hence a local section of this principle bundle
also gives such a frame field.

Moreover, the Fubini-Study metric on
{\mathbb{C}}{\mathbb{P}}^{n}

can be given by a direct computation as

(6.9)

ds^{2}_{FS}=\theta^{{\alpha}}\overline{\theta^{{\alpha}}},\ \ \ \ \ \theta^{{\alpha}}=\omega_{0}^{{\alpha}}.

Then it follows

(6.10)

\theta^{{\alpha}}=e^{-t}\omega^{{\alpha}},

and the
(1,0)
-forms
\{\theta^{{\alpha}},1\leq{\alpha}\leq n\}
builds a local coframe field on
{\mathbb{C}}{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
.
In fact, the
1
-form
\omega^{0}_{0}
exactly corresponds to the contact
1
-form in Sasakian geometry,
and the coframe field gives the decomposition of the complex structure
under the Kähler cone structure of
({\mathbb{C}}^{n+1})^{*}
.

Take exterior derivatives on equation (
6.10
),
and then we obtain the structure equation for this coframe field:

(6.11)

\displaystyle d\theta^{{\alpha}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\omega_{0}^{0}\wedge\omega_{0}^{{\alpha}}+\omega_{0}^{\beta}\wedge\omega^{{\alpha}}_{\beta}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-(\omega^{{\alpha}}_{\beta}-\omega_{0}^{0}\delta^{{\alpha}}_{\beta})\wedge\omega_{0}^{\beta}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\theta^{{\alpha}}_{\beta}\wedge\theta^{\beta},

where

\theta^{{\alpha}}_{\beta}:=\omega^{{\alpha}}_{\beta}-\omega_{0}^{0}\delta^{{\alpha}}_{\beta}
satisfies

\theta^{{\alpha}}_{\beta}+\overline{\theta^{\beta}_{\alpha}}=0.

This is the connection
1
-forms with respect to the coframe field
\{\theta^{{\alpha}},1\leq{\alpha}\leq n\}
.
For a smooth function
v
on
{\mathbb{C}}{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
, we can write as follows:

\partial v=v_{{\alpha}}\theta^{{\alpha}};\ \ \ \bar{\partial}v=v_{\bar{\alpha}}\overline{\theta^{{\alpha}}};\ \ \ \overline{(v_{{\alpha}})}=v_{\bar{\alpha}}.

Then we infer

(6.12)

\displaystyle\partial\bar{\partial}v

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d(v_{\bar{\alpha}}\overline{\theta^{{\alpha}}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\ dv_{\bar{\alpha}}-v_{\bar{\beta}}\overline{\theta^{\beta}_{{\alpha}}}\ \right)\wedge\overline{\theta^{{\alpha}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\nabla v_{\bar{\alpha}}\wedge\overline{\theta^{{\alpha}}},

where we put

(6.13)

\displaystyle\nabla v_{\bar{\alpha}}:

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle dv_{\bar{\alpha}}-v_{\bar{\beta}}\overline{\theta^{\beta}_{{\alpha}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle v_{\gamma\bar{\alpha}}\theta^{\gamma}+v_{\bar{\gamma}\bar{\alpha}}\overline{\theta^{\gamma}}.

Thanks to the symmetry of
v_{\bar{\gamma}\bar{\alpha}}
,
we obtain the complex hessian of
v
as

(6.14)

i\partial\bar{\partial}v=iv_{\gamma\bar{\alpha}}\theta^{\gamma}\wedge\overline{\theta^{{\alpha}}}.

6.3.
The complex hessian

Take a unitary frame field
\{e_{A},0\leq A\leq n\}

on
{\mathbb{C}}^{n+1}
satisfying

e^{t}e_{0}=z.

Then we can compute for a function
u\in{\mathcal{F}}^{\infty}(B_{1})
as follows:

(6.15)

\displaystyle\bar{\partial}u

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle u_{\bar{0}}\overline{\omega^{0}}+u_{\bar{\alpha}}\overline{\omega^{{\alpha}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{t}\left\{u_{\bar{0}}(dt-\omega^{0}_{0})+u_{\bar{\alpha}}\overline{\theta^{{\alpha}}}\right\}.

Thanks to equation (
6.10
),
we can decompose the exterior derivative on
{\mathbb{C}}^{n+1}
locally as

(6.16)

\displaystyle du_{\bar{0}}=u_{0,\bar{0}}\omega^{0}e^{-t}+u_{\bar{0},\bar{0}}\overline{\omega^{0}}e^{-t}+d^{T}u_{\bar{0}};

(6.17)

\displaystyle du_{\bar{\alpha}}=u_{0,\bar{\alpha}}\omega^{0}e^{-t}+u_{\bar{0},\bar{\alpha}}\overline{\omega^{0}}e^{-t}+d^{T}u_{\bar{\alpha}},

where
d^{T}
denotes the exterior derivative in the transversal direction
{\mathbb{C}}{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
.
Hence the complex hessian of
u
can be calculated as

(6.18)

\displaystyle\partial\bar{\partial}u

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d(\bar{\partial}u)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{t}dt\wedge\left(-u_{\bar{0}}\omega^{0}_{0}+u_{\bar{\alpha}}\overline{\theta^{{\alpha}}}\right)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle e^{t}\left\{du_{\bar{0}}\wedge(dt-\omega^{0}_{0})+u_{\bar{0}}\theta^{\beta}\wedge\overline{\theta^{\beta}}\right\}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle e^{t}d(u_{\bar{\alpha}}\overline{\theta^{{\alpha}}}).

Here we note that
u_{\bar{0}}=u_{0}
is real since the function
u
is
S^{1}
-invariant,
and then it follows on the hypersphere
S_{r}

(6.19)

du_{\bar{0}}|_{S_{r}}=d^{T}u_{\bar{0}};\ \ \ \ \ du_{\bar{\alpha}}|_{S_{r}}=d^{T}u_{\bar{\alpha}}.

Hence we have the restriction

(6.20)

i\partial\bar{\partial}u|_{S_{r}}=ie^{t}\left\{(u_{\bar{0}}\delta_{{\alpha}\beta}+u_{{\alpha}\bar{\beta}})\theta^{{\alpha}}\wedge\overline{\theta^{\beta}}+d^{T}u_{\bar{0}}\wedge\overline{\omega^{0}_{0}}\right\},

and this is the same formula in our Lemma
4.2
.
Moreover, it is clear to have

(6.21)

d^{c}u=ie^{t}\left\{u_{\bar{0}}\overline{\omega^{0}_{0}}+\frac{1}{2}\left(u_{\bar{\alpha}}\overline{\theta^{{\alpha}}}-u_{{\alpha}}\theta^{{\alpha}}\right)\right\}

Then it is the same way to compute the
(2n+1)
-form

d^{c}u\wedge(dd^{c}u|_{S_{r}})^{n},

and the decomposition formula follows by
taking the integral on
{\mathbb{C}}{\mathbb{P}}^{n}\times\{t\}
and perform the integration by parts.

6.4.
No symmetry

When there is no symmetry of the function, the situation becomes more interesting.
Then equation (
6.19
) fails,
and we need to directly compute from equation (
6.18
).
First we have

(6.22)

\displaystyle dt=\frac{1}{2}e^{-t}(\omega^{0}+\overline{\omega^{0}});

(6.23)

\displaystyle\omega^{0}_{0}=\frac{1}{2}e^{-t}(\omega^{0}-\overline{\omega^{0}}),

and then it follows

(6.24)

dt\wedge\omega^{0}_{0}=-\frac{1}{2}e^{-2t}\omega^{0}\wedge\overline{\omega^{0}}.

Hence the first term on the R.H.S. of equation (
6.18
)
is equal to the following:

(6.25)

\frac{1}{2}e^{-t}u_{\bar{0}}\omega^{0}\wedge\overline{\omega^{0}}+\frac{1}{2}u_{\bar{\alpha}}\omega^{0}\wedge\overline{\theta^{{\alpha}}}+\frac{1}{2}u_{\bar{\alpha}}\overline{\omega^{0}}\wedge\overline{\theta^{{\alpha}}}.

The second term can be computed as

(6.26)

\displaystyle du_{\bar{0}}\wedge(dt-\omega^{0}_{0})e^{t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(u_{0,\bar{0}}\omega^{0}e^{-t}+u_{\bar{0},\bar{0}}\overline{\omega^{0}}e^{-t}+d^{T}u_{\bar{0}})\wedge\overline{\omega^{0}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{-t}u_{0,\bar{0}}\omega^{0}\wedge\overline{\omega^{0}}+u_{{\alpha},\bar{0}}\theta^{{\alpha}}\wedge\overline{\omega^{0}}+u_{\bar{\alpha},\bar{0}}\overline{\theta^{{\alpha}}}\wedge\overline{\omega^{0}},

where we put

(6.27)

d^{T}u_{\bar{0}}=u_{{\alpha},\bar{0}}\theta^{{\alpha}}+u_{\bar{\alpha},\bar{0}}\overline{\theta^{{\alpha}}}.

Moreover, we can compute the third term as

(6.28)

\displaystyle e^{t}d(u_{\bar{\alpha}}\overline{\theta^{{\alpha}}})=e^{t}\left(du_{\bar{\alpha}}\wedge\overline{\theta^{{\alpha}}}+u_{\bar{\alpha}}d\overline{\theta^{{\alpha}}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(u_{0,\bar{\alpha}}\omega^{0}+u_{\bar{0},\bar{\alpha}}\overline{\omega^{0}})\wedge\overline{\theta^{{\alpha}}}+e^{t}(d^{T}u_{\bar{\alpha}}+u_{\bar{\beta}}\overline{\theta^{\beta}_{{\alpha}}})\wedge\overline{\theta^{{\alpha}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle u_{0,\bar{\alpha}}\omega^{0}\wedge\overline{\theta^{{\alpha}}}+u_{\bar{0},\bar{\alpha}}\overline{\omega^{0}}\wedge\overline{\theta^{{\alpha}}}+e^{t}u_{\gamma\bar{\alpha}}\theta^{\gamma}\wedge\overline{\theta^{{\alpha}}}.

Combining with equation (
6.25
), (
6.26
) and (
6.28
),
we obtain the complex hessian as

(6.29)

\displaystyle\partial\bar{\partial}u

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{-t}\left(u_{0,\bar{0}}+\frac{1}{2}u_{\bar{0}}\right)\omega^{0}\wedge\overline{\omega^{0}}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\left(u_{0,\bar{\alpha}}+\frac{1}{2}u_{\bar{\alpha}}\right)\omega^{0}\wedge\overline{\theta^{{\alpha}}}+u_{{\alpha},\bar{0}}{\theta^{{\alpha}}}\wedge\overline{\omega^{0}}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\left(u_{\bar{\alpha},\bar{0}}-u_{\bar{0},\bar{\alpha}}-\frac{1}{2}u_{\bar{\alpha}}\right)\overline{\theta^{{\alpha}}}\wedge\overline{\omega^{0}}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle e^{t}(u_{\bar{0}}\delta_{{\alpha}\beta}+u_{{\alpha}\bar{\beta}})\theta^{{\alpha}}\wedge\overline{\theta^{\beta}}.

Since the complex hessian is a
(1,1)
-form, it follows

(6.30)

u_{\bar{\alpha},\bar{0}}=u_{\bar{0},\bar{\alpha}}+\frac{1}{2}u_{\bar{\alpha}}.

Moreover, we can use the identity
-\partial\bar{\partial}u=\bar{\partial}\partial u
to obtain
the following commutation relations:

(6.31)

\displaystyle u_{\bar{0}}\delta_{{\alpha}\beta}+u_{{\alpha}\bar{\beta}}=u_{0}\delta_{{\alpha}\beta}+u_{\bar{\beta}{\alpha}};

(6.32)

\displaystyle u_{0,\bar{0}}+\frac{1}{2}u_{\bar{0}}=u_{\bar{0},0}+\frac{1}{2}u_{0};

(6.33)

\displaystyle u_{{\alpha},0}=u_{0,{\alpha}}+\frac{1}{2}u_{{\alpha}},

(6.34)

\displaystyle u_{\bar{\alpha},0}=u_{0,\bar{\alpha}}+\frac{1}{2}u_{\bar{\alpha}};\ \ \ \ u_{{\alpha},\bar{0}}=u_{\bar{0},{\alpha}}+\frac{1}{2}u_{{\alpha}}.

Finally, we end up with the following complex hessian equation for a general function.

Theorem 6.1
.

For a smooth function
u
on
({\mathbb{C}}^{n+1})^{*}
,
its complex hessian can be decomposed as

(6.35)

\displaystyle\partial\bar{\partial}u

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{-t}\left(u_{0,\bar{0}}+\frac{1}{2}u_{\bar{0}}\right)\omega^{0}\wedge\overline{\omega^{0}}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle u_{\bar{\alpha},0}\omega^{0}\wedge\overline{\theta^{{\alpha}}}+u_{{\alpha},\bar{0}}{\theta^{{\alpha}}}\wedge\overline{\omega^{0}}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle e^{t}(u_{\bar{0}}\delta_{{\alpha}\beta}+u_{{\alpha}\bar{\beta}})\theta^{{\alpha}}\wedge\overline{\theta^{\beta}}.

In particular, we have

(6.36)

u_{0,\bar{0}}+\frac{1}{2}u_{\bar{0}}\geq 0\ \ \ \text{and}\ \ \ (u_{\bar{0}}\delta_{{\alpha}\beta}+u_{{\alpha}\bar{\beta}})(i\theta^{{\alpha}}\wedge\overline{\theta^{\beta}})\geq 0,

if
u
is further plurisubharmonic.
