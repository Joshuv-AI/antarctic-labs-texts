1
Introduction

Gauge is an important concept in electromagnetic theory
[
1
]
. In fact, it was first used by Maxwell in his original work, because he used the vector and scalar potential formulation
[
2
]
.
Recently, the vector-scalar potential formulation has been resuscited in computational electromagneics because it has a number of advantages
[
3
,
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
,
8
,
9
]
. First, it is immune to low-frequency breakdown. Namely, when such a method is applied, the system equation is solvable even when the frequency is low or the mesh density is high. Therefore, this can help with the convergence and robustness of the solution when iterative methods are applied. Also, the vector-scalar potential formulation merges well with the quantum formulation of electromagnetic theory

[
10
]
.

In this paper, we shall show that the vector-scalar potential (
{{\bf A}}
-
\Phi
) formulation, for many problems, can be further simplified by ignoring the scalar potential contribution and setting it to zero. While this is not possible for all problems, it is possible for a large class of problems.
When the scalar potential is ignored by setting it to zero, the Lorenz gauge is the same as the Coulomb gauge. (It also removes some unpleasant physics of the simple Coulomb gauge where the scalar potential is not zero, resulting in a formulation that is not relativistically invariant as discussed in
[
1
]
.)
Such a gauge is sometimes called the radiation gauge, the
\Phi=0
gauge, or the Weyl gauge (the radiation gauge is a gauge often used in general relativity)
[
11
]
.

Because of the absence of low-frequency breakdown,
{{\bf A}}
-
\Phi
formulation can be used to compute the solutions of multi-scale structures more easily

[
6
,
12
,
13
,
14
,
15
]
. Moreover, in many applications such as quantum electromagnetics
[
10
]
, the vector and scalar potentials,
{{\bf A}}
and
\Phi
are more fundamental than the electric and magnetic fields,
{{\bf E}}
and
{{\bf H}}
.

2
Free Space Case

If the medium is free space which is homogeneous, linear, and dispersionless, (assuming
e^{-i\omega t}
time convention) Maxwell’s equations in the frequency domain become

\displaystyle\nabla\times{\bf E}=

\displaystyle i\omega\mu_{0}{\bf H}

(1)

\displaystyle\nabla\times{\bf H}=

\displaystyle-i\omega\varepsilon_{0}{\bf E}+{{\bf J}}_{\text{imp}}

(2)

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\mu_{0}{\bf H}=

\displaystyle 0

(3)

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot{\varepsilon_{0}}{\bf E}=

\displaystyle\varrho_{\text{imp}}

(4)

where
{{\bf J}}_{\text{imp}}
and
\varrho_{\text{imp}}
are impressed sources: They are immutable in the presence of their environment, and generate the incident field in many scattering problems.

1

This trick is used to decouple two systems that are otherwise coupled together. This is similar to the use of alternating direction method to decouple two dynamic systems
[
16
]
.

The above equations are solvable by using the vector-scalar potential approach by letting

\displaystyle{{\bf B}}

\displaystyle=\mu_{0}{{\bf H}}=\nabla\times{{\bf A}},\qquad

(5)

\displaystyle{{\bf E}}

\displaystyle=i\omega{{\bf A}}-\nabla\Phi

(6)

In this case, as shown in many textbooks, e.g.
[
17
,
1
]
, one way to solve the above equations uniquely is by invoking the Lorenz gauge.

2.1
Lorenz Gauge

The Lorenz gauge is the preferred gauge because it treats space and time on equal footing: Hence, it is relativistically invariant
[
1
]
.
Starting with the four Maxwell’s equations,
with the use of Lorenz gauge where

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot{{\bf A}}=i\omega\mu_{0}\varepsilon_{0}\Phi

(7)

we arrive at two second order equations
[
3
]
,

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\nabla\Phi({{\bf r}},\omega)

\displaystyle+\omega^{2}\mu_{0}\varepsilon_{0}\Phi({{\bf r}},\omega)=-\varrho_{\text{imp}}({{\bf r}},\omega)/\varepsilon_{0}

(8)

\displaystyle-\nabla\times\nabla\times{\bf{A}({{\bf r}},\omega)}

\displaystyle+\nabla(\nabla\cdot{\bf{A}({{\bf r}},\omega)})+\omega^{2}\mu_{0}\varepsilon_{0}{\bf{A}({{\bf r}},\omega)}=-\mu_{0}\bf{J}_{\text{imp}}({{\bf r}},\omega),

(9)

where
{{\bf A}}({{\bf r}},\omega)
and
\Phi({{\bf r}},\omega)
are the vector and scalar potentials, respectively, in the frequency domain.
By using
-\nabla\times\nabla\times{\bf{A}({{\bf r}},\omega)}+\nabla(\nabla\cdot{\bf{A}({{\bf r}},\omega)})=\nabla\cdot\nabla{{\bf A}}({{\bf r}},\omega)
, the above can be rewritten as

\nabla\cdot\nabla{{\bf A}}({{\bf r}},\omega)+\omega^{2}\mu_{0}\varepsilon_{0}{\bf{A}({{\bf r}},\omega)}=-\mu_{0}\bf{J}_{\text{imp}}({{\bf r}},\omega)

(10)

In
(
9
),
when converted back to the time domain where
\omega^{2}\rightarrow-\partial_{t}^{2}
, the above equations put space and time on the same footing. Hence, they are relativistically invariant
[
1
]
. Furthermore, it is a common practice to regard the quadruplet,
\left\{{{\bf A}},\Phi\right\}
to be a four-vector in special relavitiy
[
1
]
.
In addition, in (
8
) and (
9
),

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot{{\bf J}}_{\text{imp}}({{\bf r}},\omega)=i\omega\varrho_{\text{imp}}({{\bf r}},\omega)

(11)

is the current continuity equation
which is a statement of charge conservation. In other words, the current
{{\bf J}}_{\text{imp}}
and the charge
\varrho_{\text{imp}}
are not independent of each other.
And, (
8
) and (
9
) are not independent of each other: (
8
) can be derived from (
9
) by taking its divergence, and using the Lorenz gauge (
7
). When
\omega\neq 0
, it suffices to solve only (
9
) and obtain (
8
) by invoking the Lorenz gauge and the current continuity equation. But
\Phi
cannot be retrieved from
{{\bf A}}
by using (
7
) when
\omega=0
, or when
\omega\rightarrow 0
. Hence, both equations (
8
) and (
9
) have to be solved together when
\omega=0
. This is similar to the notion that all four Maxwell’s equations have to be solved when
\omega=0

[
18
]
.

The Laplacian operator
\nabla\cdot\nabla=\nabla^{2}
is a negative definite operator, and hence, it has no low-frequency breakdown, implying that the above equations have solutions no matter how small
\omega
is or how low the frequency is.
2
2

2

The Laplacian operator does have an issue with fine-mesh ill-conditioning, which can be remedied with the multi-grid method
[
19
,
20
]
.
To derive the above two equations (
8
) and (
9
), all four of Maxwell’s equations are used, which is the reason for their robustness.

3
Field-Source Relations in Lorenz Gauge

Before we proceed, it is useful to examine the field-source relation of an electromagnetics system.
Using the Green’s function technique
[
17
,
21
]
, the solutions to the above equations are found to be

\displaystyle\Phi({{\bf r}},\omega)=\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{0}}\int_{V}g({{\bf r}},{{\bf r}}^{\prime})\varrho_{\text{imp}}({{\bf r}}^{\prime},\omega)d{{\bf r}}^{\prime}

(12)

\displaystyle{{\bf A}}({{\bf r}},\omega)=\mu_{0}\int_{V}g({{\bf r}},{{\bf r}}^{\prime}){{\bf J}}_{\text{imp}}({{\bf r}}^{\prime},\omega)d{{\bf r}}^{\prime}

(13)

where
g({{\bf r}},{{\bf r}}^{\prime})=\exp(ik_{0}|{{\bf r}}-{{\bf r}}^{\prime}|)/(4\pi|{{\bf r}}-{{\bf r}}^{\prime}|)
is the Green’s function with
k_{0}=\omega\sqrt{\mu_{0}\varepsilon_{0}}=\omega/c_{0}
. It can be shown that the above (
12
) and (
13
) satisfy
(
8
) and (
9
) by back substitution
[
17
,
22
,
18
]
. It is to be noted that in free-space, the above is the convolution between the Green’s function and the source. Since the Green’s function has infinite support, the resultant field also has infinite support, implying that the fields are, in general, nonzero outside the source region.

Figure 1:
A source bounded with
S_{\text{source}}
but radiating to its outside in free space.

Using (
6
), we get that
{{\bf E}}({{\bf r}},\omega)=i\omega{{\bf A}}({{\bf r}},\omega)-\nabla\Phi({{\bf r}},\omega)
. Thus the total electric field
{{\bf E}}({{\bf r}},\omega)
is given by

\displaystyle{{\bf E}}({{\bf r}},\omega)=i\omega\mu_{0}\int_{V}d{{\bf r}}^{\prime}g({{\bf r}},{{\bf r}}^{\prime}){{\bf J}}_{\text{imp}}({{\bf r}}^{\prime},\omega)-\frac{\nabla}{\varepsilon_{0}}\int_{V}d{{\bf r}}^{\prime}g({{\bf r}},{{\bf r}}^{\prime})\varrho_{\text{imp}}({{\bf r}}^{\prime},\omega)

(14)

At this point, we can give physical interpretation to the above: In the vector potential term or the first term on the right-hand side, the current generates the vector potential
{{\bf A}}
via (
13
). Then the time derivative of the vector potential gives the electric field by induction, which in the frequency domain,
{{\bf E}}_{A}=i\omega{{\bf A}}
. On the other hand, in the second term, the charge generates the scalar potential
\Phi
directly, and the negative gradient of it gives the electric field.

In general, by using Helmholtz decomposition, the vector current
{{\bf J}}_{\text{imp}}={{\bf J}}_{\perp,\text{imp}}+{{\bf J}}_{\parallel,\text{imp}}
, where
\nabla\cdot{{\bf J}}_{\perp,\text{imp}}=0
and
\nabla\times{{\bf J}}_{\parallel,\text{imp}}=0
. Then it can be shown that by Helmholtz decomposition, and letting
{{\bf A}}={{\bf A}}_{\perp}+{{\bf A}}_{\parallel}
, where
\nabla\cdot{{\bf A}}_{\perp}=0
and
\nabla\times{{\bf A}}_{\parallel}=0
, that

\displaystyle{{\bf A}}_{\perp}({{\bf r}},\omega)=\mu_{0}\int_{V}g({{\bf r}},{{\bf r}}^{\prime}){{\bf J}}_{\perp,{\text{imp}}}({{\bf r}}^{\prime},\omega)d{{\bf r}}^{\prime}

(15)

\displaystyle{{\bf A}}_{\parallel}({{\bf r}},\omega)=\mu_{0}\int_{V}g({{\bf r}},{{\bf r}}^{\prime}){{\bf J}}_{\parallel,{\text{imp}}}({{\bf r}}^{\prime},\omega)d{{\bf r}}^{\prime}

(16)

It can be shown that the above equations are divergence-free and curl-free, respectively, by back-substitution.

3.1
Redundancy of the Scalar Potential

First, we will show that in a homogeneous source-free medium (or outside the source region), the scalar potential is redundant and hence, can be omitted for simplicity. From the previous equations (
8
) and (
9
), we have
Outside the source region or
V_{\text{source}}
,
{\bf{J}}_{\textrm{inc}}=\varrho_{\textrm{inc}}=0
, and we have
\nabla\cdot{\bf{E}}=0
. Hence, by using (
5
) and taking its divergence, we have

\nabla\cdot{\bf{E}}=i\omega\nabla\cdot{\bf{A}}-\nabla\cdot\nabla\Phi=0.

(17)

In other words, the non-divergence-free part of
{{\bf A}}
is cancelled by the non-divergence-free part of
\nabla\Phi
.
To see the physics more clearly, first we decompose
{{\bf A}}
into a divergence-free and curl-free parts according to Helmholtz (or Hodge) decomposition
[
23
,
24
]
as before or
{{\bf A}}={{\bf A}}_{\perp}+{{\bf A}}_{\parallel}
.
Then (
17
) becomes

\nabla\cdot{\bf E}=i\omega\nabla\cdot{\bf A}_{\parallel}-\nabla^{2}\Phi=0

(18)

The above follows because
\nabla\cdot{{\bf A}}_{\perp}=0
. Also, when
\omega\rightarrow 0
, (
18
) goes to zero smoothly without
{\bf A}_{\parallel}
or
\Phi
being divergent. Or
i\omega\nabla\cdot{{\bf A}}_{\parallel}=\nabla^{2}\Phi=-\omega^{2}\mu_{0}\varepsilon_{0}\Phi\rightarrow 0
.
Using the Lorenz gauge given in (
7
), the frequency dependence of the above can be easily verified.

In other words, outside the source region, it suffices to have the vector potential
{{\bf A}}_{\perp}\neq 0
outside the source region, or

\displaystyle{{\bf E}}=i\omega{{\bf A}}_{\perp}

(19)

The above field is clearly divergence-free corresponding to a field in a source-free region.

4
Physical Interpretation

It is often said that the radiation gauge is similar to the
\Phi=0
gauge. This can be a misunderstanding, as
\Phi\neq 0
for most problems. In fact, in the near field, the electric field associate with
\Phi
can be much larger than that associated with
{{\bf A}}
as
{{\bf E}}=i\omega{{\bf A}}-\nabla\Phi
. One can surmise the
i\omega{{\bf A}}
can be much smaller than
\nabla\Phi
when
\omega
is small. This is synonymous with being in the near field, or in the low frequency limit.

However, in the far-field approximations of both (
12
) and (
13
), the fields then look like spherical waves or plane waves. In this case,
i\omega{{\bf A}}_{\parallel}
and
\nabla\Phi
are of the same order, and they almost cancel each other numerically. Hence, the fields are redundant and we can set
\Phi={{\bf A}}_{\parallel}=0
with abandon.

4.1
Homomorphism with Electric Field

When we let
{{\bf E}}=i\omega{{\bf A}}_{\perp}
, and ignore
\Phi
, the integral equation of scattering using the
{{\bf A}}
-
\Phi
formulation is mathematically similar to the electric field integral equation (EFIE) of scattering. When
\omega\neq 0
, and
{{\bf E}}
and
{{\bf A}}
are of the same order, and they differ by a phase difference. Therefore, setting
\Phi=0
in the
{{\bf A}}
-
\Phi
formulation is similar to solving the scattering problem with EFIE. However, one has to do this with caution when
\omega\rightarrow 0
since
{{\bf E}}=i\omega{{\bf A}}-\nabla\Phi
. When
\Phi=0
, the first term
i\omega{{\bf A}}
will have to bear the burden of modeling a static electric field whenever
\omega\rightarrow 0
, since the electric field is non-zero for an electrostatic problem. This makes
{{\bf A}}
diverges as
1/\omega
when
\omega\rightarrow 0
.

5
Inhomogeneous Dispersionless Media

Again, we can expand
{{\bf B}}
and
{{\bf E}}
as in
(
5
) and (
6
) where
{{\bf B}}=\mu({{\bf r}}){{\bf H}}
now.
To satisfy the other two Maxwell’s equations,
it can be shown that
the equations to be satisfied are
[
3
]

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\varepsilon({{\bf r}})\nabla\Phi

\displaystyle+\omega^{2}\varepsilon^{2}({{\bf r}})\mu({{\bf r}})\Phi=-\varrho_{\text{imp}}

(20)

\displaystyle-\nabla\times\mu^{-1}({{\bf r}})\nabla\times{\bf A}

\displaystyle+\varepsilon({{\bf r}})\nabla\left(\frac{1}{\mu({{\bf r}})\varepsilon^{2}({{\bf r}})}\nabla\cdot\varepsilon({{\bf r}}){\bf A}\right)+\omega^{2}\varepsilon({{\bf r}}){\bf A}=-\bf J_{\text{imp}}

(21)

In arriving at the above, we have used the generalized Lorenz gauge for inhomogeneous media
[
3
]

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\varepsilon({{\bf r}})\mathbf{A}=i\omega\varepsilon^{2}({{\bf r}})\mu({{\bf r}}){\Phi}

(22)

Also, it is to be noted that the fields above are implied functions of
{{\bf r}}
and
\omega
as well.

It can be shown that a generalized Helmholtz (or Hodge) decomposition can be used to divide the Hilbert space that
{{\bf A}}
lives in, into
two orthogonal subspaces
[
25
]
. Then the vector potential can be decomposed into
[
25
]

\displaystyle{{\bf A}}={{\bf A}}_{\varepsilon\perp}+{{\bf A}}_{\parallel}

(23)

where
\nabla\cdot\varepsilon{{\bf A}}_{\varepsilon\perp}=0
and
\nabla\times{{\bf A}}_{\parallel}=0
. In a word,
{{\bf A}}_{\varepsilon\perp}
lives in the null space of the divergence operator, while
{{\bf A}}_{\parallel}
lives in the null space of the curl operator.
Moreover, it can be shown
[
25
]
that vectors in these two spaces are
\varepsilon
-orthogonal to each other, or that

\displaystyle\langle{{\bf A}}^{*}_{\varepsilon,\perp},\varepsilon{{\bf A}}_{\parallel}\rangle=\int_{V}d{{\bf r}}A^{*}_{\varepsilon,\perp}({{\bf r}},\omega)\cdot\varepsilon{{\bf A}}_{\parallel}({{\bf r}},\omega)=0

(24)

In the above, we have used the reaction inner product that is popular in the electromagnetics literature
[
26
,
27
,
28
]
where the complex conjugation is explicitly shown.
It is to be noted that this decomposition is independent of frequency since
\varepsilon
is frequency independent.
Again, for the inhomogeneous, dispersionless, and source-free media, we can show that the scalar potential is redundant.
In this case, using the generalized Helmholtz decomposition, letting
{{\bf A}}={{\bf A}}_{\varepsilon,\perp}+{{\bf A}}_{\parallel}
. Then requiring
\nabla\cdot\varepsilon{{\bf E}}=0
, we arrive at

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\varepsilon{{\bf E}}=0

\displaystyle=i\omega\nabla\cdot\varepsilon{{\bf A}}-\nabla\cdot\varepsilon\nabla\Phi

\displaystyle=i\omega\nabla\cdot\varepsilon{{\bf A}}_{\parallel}-\nabla\cdot\varepsilon\nabla\Phi

(25)

By using the generalized Lorenz gauge (
22
), and (
20
) outside the source region, it can be shown that the above is zero, satisfying the requirement of a source-free
{{\bf E}}
field. Hence, outside the source region,
\Phi
produces a component of
{{\bf E}}
field that together with the contribution of from
{{\bf A}}_{\parallel}
makes
\varepsilon{{\bf E}}
divergence free. To make
\varepsilon{{\bf E}}
divergence free outside the source, it suffices to let

\displaystyle{{\bf E}}=i\omega{{\bf A}}_{\varepsilon,\perp}

(26)

In other words, both
\Phi
and
{{\bf A}}_{\parallel}
are redundant outside the source. They can be set to zero for simplicity, but they are not individually zero.
However, since
\Phi
and
{{\bf A}}_{\parallel}
are produced by finite sources in accordance with (
12
) and (
13
), just as in the free-space case, we do not expect them to be zero outside their source region.

6
Conclusion

We have gained a better understanding of the radiation gauge and summarized it in this article.
While this gauge has been called by various names such as Weyl gauge
[
11
]
,
\Phi=0
gauge
[
29
]
, and radiation gauge
[
1
]
, there has not been a proper documentation as to when this gauge is valid. In general, the radiation gauge is safe to use for electrodynamic problems where the scalar potential and vector potential are of equal importance and one is outside the source region.

But in the near field or the low frequency regime, one has to use this gauge with caution. The electrostatic field can be quite strong, and if we let
{{\bf E}}=i\omega{{\bf A}}
, because
{{\bf E}}
is finite when
\omega\rightarrow 0
,
{{\bf A}}\rightarrow 1/\omega
. Thus
{{\bf A}}
will become bigger as
\omega\rightarrow 0
to bear the burden of modeling the electrostatic field. This is unphysical because it diverges. Similar statement can be made when the medium is inhomogeneous and dispersionless.
