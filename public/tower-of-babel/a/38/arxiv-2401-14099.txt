Abstract
. Using the method of q q -exponential differential operator we give an extension of the Sears ϕ 3 4 {}_{4}\phi_{3} transformation formula. Based on this extended formula and a q q -series expansion formula for an analytic function around the origin, we present a transformation formula for triple q q -series, which includes several interesting special cases, especially a double q q -series summation formula. Some application of this transformation formula to Rogers–Hecke type series are discussed. More than 100 Rogers–Hecke type identities including Andrews’s identities for the sums of three squares and the sums of three triangular numbers are obtained.



1.
Introduction and preliminaries

In this paper we will prove a transformation formula for triple
q
-hypergeometric series, which includes several interesting special cases. One special case of this transformation allows us to derive several new identities for Rogers–Hecke type series. Rogers–Hecke type series play an important role in the study of Ramanujan’s mock-theta functions.

As usual, we use
\mathbb{C}
to denote the set of all complex numbers. Throughout the paper, we shall use the standard
q
-notations. Unless stated otherwise, it is assumed that
0<q<1
.
Let
n
be an arbitrary non-negative integer and
a\in\mathbb{C}
.
We define the
q
-shifted factorial by

(a;q)_{n}=\frac{(a;q)_{\infty}}{(aq^{n};q)_{\infty}},~\text{where}~~(a;q)_{\infty}=\prod_{k=0}^{\infty}(1-aq^{k}).

It is obvious that
(a;q)_{0}=1
, and by a simple calculation we find that for any positive integer
n
,

(a;q)_{n}=(1-a)(1-aq)\cdots(1-aq^{n-1}),\quad(a;q)_{-n}=(-a)^{-n}q^{n(n+1)/2}(q/a;q)_{n}^{-1}.

(1.1)

We sometimes also adopt the multiple

q
-shifted factorial which are defined by

(a_{1},a_{2},...,a_{m};q)_{n}=(a_{1};q)_{n}(a_{2};q)_{n}\cdots(a_{m};q)_{n},

where
n
is an integer or
\infty

The basic hypergeometric series or the
q
-hypergeometric series
{{}_{r}\phi_{s}}(\cdot)
are defined as

{}_{r}\phi_{s}\left({{a_{1},\ldots,a_{r}}\atop{b_{1},\ldots,b_{s}}};q,z\right)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(a_{1},\ldots,a_{r};q)_{n}}{(q,b_{1},\ldots,b_{s};q)_{n}}\left((-1)^{n}q^{n(n-1)/2}\right)^{1+s-r}z^{n}.

One of the most important results in the theory of

[

.

Setting

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(bx;q)_{\infty}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(bx)^{n}}{(q;q)_{n}},\quad|bx|<1,

(1.2)

\displaystyle(ax;q)_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-ax)^{n}q^{n(n-1)/2}}{(q;q)_{n}},\quad|ax|<\infty.

(1.3)

By a simple calculation it is found that the
q
-Pfaff–Saalschütz formula

The Rogers

2.1.
A

-series expansion formula

In order to prove Theorem

, we need to introduce some basic knowledge of

The
q
-derivative was introduced by L. Schendel
[
27
]
in 1878 and F. H. Jackson
[
11
]
in 1908, which is a
q
-analog of the ordinary derivative.

Definition 2.1
.

If
q
is a complex number, then, for any function
f(x)
of one variable, the
q
-derivative of
f(x)

with respect to
x,
is defined as

{D}_{q}f(x)=\frac{f(x)-f(qx)}{x},

and we further define
{D}_{q}^{0}f=f,
and for
n\geq 1
,
{D}_{q}^{n}f={D}_{q}\{{D}_{q}^{n-1}f\}.

Using mathematical induction, one can easily derive the following formula of Jackson
[
11
]
, which writes

{D}^{n}_{q}f(x)
in terms of
f(q^{k}x)
for
k=0,1,\ldots,n
.

Lemma 2.2
.

For any nonnegative integer
n,
we have

{D}^{n}_{q}f(x)=x^{-n}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{(q^{-n};q)_{k}}{(q;q)_{k}}q^{k}f(q^{k}x).

The proof of Theorem
1.7
.

Proof.

If
f(x)
is a formal power series in
x
over the field of complex numbers
\mathbb{C}
, then, we have the following expansion formula
[
13
, Theorem 2]
:

f(\alpha a)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(1-\alpha q^{2n})(q/a;q)_{n}(\alpha a)^{n}}{(q,\alpha a;q)_{n}}\left[{D}^{n}_{q}\{f(x)(x;q)_{n-1}\}\right]_{x=\alpha q}.

(2.1)

Using the Jackson formula in Lemma
2.2
and the definition of
q
-shifted factorial, we have the following evaluation:

\displaystyle{D}^{n}_{q}\{f(x)(x;q)_{n-1}\}

\displaystyle=x^{-n}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{(q^{-n};q)_{k}}{(q;q)_{k}}q^{k}f(q^{k}x)(xq^{k};q)_{n-1}

\displaystyle=x^{-n}(x;q)_{n-1}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{(q^{-n},xq^{n-1};q)_{k}}{(q,x;q)_{k}}q^{k}f(q^{k}x),

which gives

\left[{D}^{n}_{q}\{f(x)(x;q)_{n-1}\}\right]_{x=\alpha q}=\frac{(q\alpha;q)_{n}}{(1-\alpha)(q\alpha)^{n}}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{(q^{-n},\alpha q^{n};q)_{k}q^{k}}{(q,q\alpha;q)_{k}}f(\alpha q^{k+1}).

Combining this equation with (
2.1
), we complete the proof of Theorem
1.7
.

Theorem
1.7
is a convenient tool for deriving
q

in Theorem
1.7

f(x):=\frac{(bx/q,cx/q;q)_{\infty}}{(x,bcx/q^{2};q)}

in Theorem
1.7

f(\alpha a)=\frac{(\alpha ab/q,\alpha ac/q;q)_{\infty}}{(\alpha a,bc/q^{2};q)},

and

f(\alpha q^{k+1})=\frac{(\alpha b,\alpha c;q)_{\infty}(q\alpha,\alpha bc/q;)_{k}}{(q\alpha,\alpha bc/q)_{\infty}(\alpha b,\alpha c;q)_{k}}.

Hence by Theorem
1.7
we conclude that

\displaystyle\frac{(q\alpha,\alpha ab/q,\alpha ac/q,\alpha bc/q;q)_{\infty}}{(\alpha a,\alpha b,\alpha c,\alpha abc/q^{2};q)_{\infty}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(1-\alpha q^{2n})(\alpha,q/a;q)_{n}(a/q)^{n}}{(1-\alpha)(\alpha a;q)_{n}}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{(q^{-n},\alpha q^{n},\alpha bc/q;q)_{n}q^{k}}{(q,\alpha b,\alpha c;q)_{n}}.

Using the

\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{(q^{-n},\alpha q^{n},\alpha bc/q;q)_{n}q^{k}}{(q,\alpha b,\alpha c;q)_{n}}=\frac{(q/b,q/c;q)_{n}}{(\alpha b,\alpha c;q)_{n}}\left(\frac{\alpha bc}{q}\right)^{n}.

(2.2)

Combining the above two equations we immediately at the Rogers
{}_{6}\phi_{5}
summation in Proposition

Watson’s

3.1.
Some basic properties of

-exponential differential operator

Now we give the definitions of the
q
-partial derivative and the
q
-partial differential equations
[
21
]
.

Definition 3.1
.

A
q
-partial derivative of a function of several variables is its
q
-derivative with respect to one of those variables, regarding other variables as constants. The
q
-partial derivative of a function
f
with respect to the variable
x
is denoted by
\partial_{q,x}\{f\}
.

Definition 3.2
.

A
q
-partial differential equation is a equation that contains unknown multivariable functions and their
q
-partial derivatives.

The concept of
q
-partial differential equations is quite useful in
q
-analysis
[
8
,
19
,
21
,
22
]
.

Based on
\partial_{q,x}
, we can construct the
q
-exponential differential operator
T(y\partial_{q,x})
as follows:

\displaystyle T(y\partial_{q,x})=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(y\partial_{q,x})^{n}}{(q;q)_{n}}.

(3.1)

One of the most important result of the
q
-exponential operational operator is the following operator identity
[
15
, Eq.(3.1)]
.

Proposition 3.3
.

For
\max\{|as|,|at|,|au|,|bs|,|bt|,|bu|,|abstu/v|\}<1,
we have

\displaystyle T(b\partial_{q,a})\left\{\frac{(av;q)_{\infty}}{(as,at,au;q)_{\infty}}\right\}

\displaystyle=\frac{(av,bv,abstu/v;q)_{\infty}}{(as,at,au,bs,bt,bu;q)_{\infty}}

\displaystyle\quad\times{{}_{3}\phi_{2}}\left({{v/s,v/t,v/u}\atop{av,bv}};q,\frac{abstu}{v}\right).

The following proposition can be found in
[
19
, Lemma 13.2]
and its proof is very short. For completeness, we will repeat the proof here.

Proposition 3.4
.

Suppose that
f(x,y)
is a two-variable analytic function near
(x,y)=(0,0).

Then

f(x,y)=T(y\partial_{q,x})f(x,0),
if and only if
\partial_{q,x}f=\partial_{q,y}f
.

Proof.

If
f(x,y)=T(y\partial_{q,x})f(x,0),
then, by a direct computation, we find that

\partial_{q,x}f=\partial_{q,y}f=T(y\partial_{q,x})\partial_{q,x}f(x,0).

Conversely, if
f
is a two-variable analytic function near
(x,y)=(0,0),
satisfying

\partial_{q,x}f=\partial_{q,y}f,
then, we may assume that

f(x,y)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}A_{n}(x)\frac{y^{n}}{(q;q)_{n}}.

Substituting the equation into the
q
-partial differential equation,

\partial_{q,x}f=\partial_{q,y}f
, we easily find that

A_{n}(x)=\partial_{q,x}A_{n-1}(x)=\cdots=\partial^{n}_{q,x}A_{0}(x).

It is easily seen that
A_{0}(x)=f(x,0).
Thus, we conclude that

f(x,y)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\partial^{n}_{q,x}f(x,0)\frac{y^{n}}{(q;q)_{n}}=T(y\partial_{q,x})f(x,0).

This completes the proof of Proposition
3.4
.

It is proved that
[
19
, Proposition 13.4]
if
f(x)
is analytic near
x=0,
then,
T(y\partial_{q,x})f(x)
is analytic near
(x,y)=(0,0).

Using Proposition
3.4
, we
[
19
, Proposition 13.9]
proved the following proposition, which allows us to act an infinite series term by term with
T(y\partial_{q,x})
.

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
\{f_{n}(x)\}
be a sequence of analytic functions near
x=0
,
such that the series

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}f_{n}(x)

converges uniformly to an analytic function
f(x)
near
x=0
, and the series

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}T(y\partial_{q,x})f_{n}(x)

converges uniformly to an analytic function
f(x,y)
near
(x,y)=(0,0)
. Then we have

f(x,y)=T(y\partial_{q,x})f(x)
, or

T(y\partial_{q,x})\left\{\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}f_{n}(x)\right\}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}T(y\partial_{q,x})f_{n}(x).

For completeness, we repeat the proof here.

Proof.

If we use
f_{n}(x,y)
to denote
T(y\partial_{q,x})f_{n}(x),
then, by Proposition
3.4
, we find that
\partial_{q,x}f_{n}(x,y)=\partial_{q,y}f_{n}(x,y)
. It follows that

\partial_{q,x}f(x,y)=\partial_{q,y}f(x,y).
Thus, using Proposition
3.4
again,
we have

f(x,y)=T(y\partial_{q,x})f(x,0)=T(y\partial_{q,x})f(x).

A multivariable form of Theorem
3.5
is proved in
[
26
]
.
