The asymptotic spectrum distance, graph limits, and the Shannon capacity
David de Boer, Pjotr Buys, Jeroen Zuiddam

Abstract




Abstract

Determining the Shannon capacity of graphs (Shannon 1956) is a long-standing open problem in information theory, graph theory and combinatorial optimization. Over decades, a wide range of upper and lower bound methods (of combinatorial, algebraic, analytic, and computational nature) have been developed to analyze this problem (e.g., Lovász 1979, Alon 1998, Bohman–Holzman 2003, Bukh–Cox 2019, Polak–Schrijver 2019, Guruswami–Riazanov 2021). However, despite tremendous effort, even small instances of the problem have remained open (e.g., the Shannon capacity of any odd cycle of length at least seven), and central computational and structural questions unanswered.

In recent years, a new dual characterization of the Shannon capacity of graphs, asymptotic spectrum duality, has unified and extended known upper bound methods and structural theorems. This duality originated from the work of Strassen in algebraic complexity theory (Strassen, J. Reine Angew. Math. 1987, 1988, 1991) and has recently seen much activity (Christandl–Vrana–Zuiddam, J. Amer. Math. Soc. 2023, Wigderson–Zuiddam, Bull. Amer. Math. Soc. 2026).

In this paper, building on asymptotic spectrum duality, we develop a new theory of graph distance, that we call
asymptotic spectrum distance
, and corresponding limits (reminiscent of, but different from, the celebrated theory of cut-norm, graphons and flag algebras). We propose a graph limit approach to the Shannon capacity problem: to determine the Shannon capacity of a graph, construct a sequence of easier to analyse graphs converging to it.

(1) We give a very general construction of non-trivial converging sequences of graphs (in the asymptotic spectrum distance). As a consequence, we prove new continuity properties of the Shannon capacity, the Lovász theta function and other graph parameters, answering questions of Schrijver and Polak.

(2) We construct Cauchy sequences of finite graphs that do not converge to any finite graph, but do converge to an infinite graph (in the asymptotic spectrum distance). We establish strong connections between convergence questions of finite graphs and the asymptotic properties of Borsuk-like infinite graphs on the circle, and use ideas from the theory of dynamical systems to study these infinite graphs.

(3) We observe that all best-known lower bound constructions for Shannon capacity of small odd cycles can be obtained from a “finite” version of the graph limit approach, in which we approximate the target graph by another graph with a large independent set that is an “orbit” under a natural group action. We develop computational and theoretical aspects of this approach and use these to obtain a new Shannon capacity lower bound for the fifteen-cycle.

The graph limit point-of-view brings many of the constructions
that have appeared over time in a unified picture, makes new connections to ideas in topology and dynamical systems, and offers new paths forward. The theory of asymptotic spectrum distance applies not only to Shannon capacity of graphs, but indeed we will develop it for a general class of mathematical objects and their asymptotic properties.

1.1
Shannon capacity and asymptotic spectrum distance

We discuss some basic notions (a full discussion is in

) and then elaborate on our results.
The Shannon capacity of a graph
G
is defined as the rate of growth of the independence number under taking large powers of the graph (under the “strong product”), that is,
\Theta(G)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\alpha(G^{\boxtimes n})^{1/n}

[

]

By Fekete’s lemma this equals the supremum
\sup_{n}\alpha(G^{\boxtimes n})^{1/n}

Asymptotic spectrum duality
[
80
]
characterizes the Shannon capacity as the minimization

\Theta(G)=\min_{F\in\mathcal{X}}F(G),

where
\mathcal{X}

Section 2.2
).
The asymptotic spectrum

contains the Lovász theta function, fractional Haemers bound, fractional clique covering number, and other well-known upper bounds on Shannon capacity. While we do not have an explicit description of all elements of
\mathcal{X}
, several structural properties are known
[
72
]
.

We define the asymptotic spectrum distance by

d(G,H)=\sup_{F\in\mathcal{X}}|F(G)-F(H)|
for any two graphs
G,H
.
2
2

2

Strictly speaking, this is not a distance on graphs, but it is a distance if we identify graphs under “asymptotic equivalence”,
Section 2
.

From asymptotic spectrum duality it can be seen that converging graphs have converging Shannon capacity (
2.7
).
As we will see (
2.8
), asymptotic spectrum distance can be phrased in an operational form using asymptotic spectrum duality. Namely,

is small if and only if a “polynomial blow up” of

can be mapped (cohomomorphically) to a slightly larger polynomial blow up of
H
, and similarly for
G
and
H
reversed.
3
3

3

Write
G\leq H
if there is a cohomomorphism

(a map
V(G)\to V(H)
that maps non-edges to non-edges). Then for any

we have

if and only if
(E_{b}\boxtimes G)^{\boxtimes n}\leq((E_{b}\boxtimes H)\sqcup E_{a})^{\boxtimes(n+o(n))}

1.3
Infinite graphs as limit points

We prove that certain infinite graphs (on the circle) are limit points for non-converging Cauchy sequences of finite graphs, and investigate the properties of these graphs, and strong connections to the Shannon capacity of finite graphs.

1.5
Asymptotic spectrum distance of tensors, matrix multiplication

Let us take a step back and note again that the theory of asymptotic spectrum distance applies much more broadly than just to graphs. Namely, it applies to any semiring with a preorder for which the asymptotic spectrum duality holds; the exact conditions for this we discuss in
Appendix A
.

An interesting and much studied setting where asymptotic spectrum duality exists (and thus asymptotic spectrum distance) is the semiring of tensors (under direct sum and tensor product) with the “restriction preorder” (
Appendix A
). This was in fact the original application of asymptotic spectrum duality in the seminal work
[
68
,
69
,
70
]
, motivated by the problem of constructing fast matrix multiplication algorithms.

Remarkably, recent works of Draisma, Blatter and Rupniewski
[
16
]
and Briët, Christandl, Leigh, Shpilka and Zuiddam
[
19
]
show that (contrary to the graph setting) many asymptotic tensor parameters are discrete. In particular, it can be shown that over any finite field, with the asymptotic spectrum distance, any Cauchy sequence of tensors must be eventually constant, and thus the space of tensors with asymptotic spectrum distance is complete.

The discreteness in the tensor setting is in stark contrast to what we see in the graph setting of this paper:
as we have discussed (
Section 1.2
), in the space of graphs with the asymptotic spectrum distance we can construct non-trivial converging sequences of graphs, and also non-converging Cauchy sequences. It is this surprisingly “continuous” behaviour of graphs that we aim to capitalize on in this work.

2
Shannon capacity and asymptotic spectrum distance

The goal of this section is to define a distance function on graphs that is geared towards attacking the Shannon capacity problem—and indeed arises naturally from it—and to discuss its basic properties.

We begin with a brief discussion of Shannon capacity and related basic notions. We then discuss asymptotic spectrum duality, which provides a dual description for the Shannon capacity.
Asymptotic spectrum duality naturally leads to a distance on graphs, the asymptotic spectrum distance. Finally, we discuss a method for constructing pairs of graphs that have small distance in the asymptotic spectrum distance.

In this section we only work with finite graphs, for simplicity and since this suffices for
Section 3
. Most ideas, in particular asymptotic spectrum duality and distance, extend directly to infinite graphs. Later, in
Section 4
and
Section 5
, we will indeed use infinite graphs, and we will discuss the needed material for infinite graphs there (
Section 4.2
). In fact, most material in this section holds much more generally, which we discuss in
Appendix A
(and see the general survey
[
73
]
).

2.1
Shannon capacity and basic notions

Let
G
and
H
be (finite, simple) graphs. We denote by
G\boxtimes H
the strong product of
G
and
H
.
8
8

8

The vertex set
V(G\boxtimes H)
is the cartesian product
V(G)\times V(H)
and the edge set is given by
E(G\boxtimes H)=\{\{(a,x),(b,y)\}:(a=b\vee\{a,b\}\in E(G))\wedge(x=y\vee\{x,y\}\in E(H))\}
. This product is also called the AND product. It can alternatively be described in terms of adjacency matrices: Let
A(G)
be the adjacency matrix of
G
in which we put ones on the main diagonal. Then
A(G\boxtimes H)=A(G)\otimes A(H)
, where
\otimes
is the Kronecker product of matrices.

We denote by
\alpha(G)
the independence number of
G
(which is defined as the size of the largest independent set in
G
) and by
\Theta(G)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\alpha(G^{\boxtimes n})^{1/n}
the Shannon capacity of
G

[
66
]
. It is known by Fekete’s lemma that the limit exists and can be replaced by a supremum. Determining the Shannon capacity of a graph is a hard problem, even for small graphs. Various techniques are known to upper bound the Shannon capacity, including the (fractional) clique covering number
[
66
]
, (fractional) orthogonal rank (projective rank)
[
53
,
38
]
, Lovász theta function
[
48
]
, and (fractional) Haemers bound
[
33
,
15
,
20
]
.

We use the following basic terminology throughout the paper.
We denote by
G\sqcup H
the disjoint union of
G
and
H
.
We denote by
\overline{G}
the complement graph of
G
. We denote the complete graph on
n
vertices by
K_{n}
, and its complement by
E_{n}=\overline{K}_{n}
, so that
E_{n}
is the graph with
n
vertices and no edges.
With the addition
\sqcup
and multiplication
\boxtimes
the set of (isomorphism classes of) graphs becomes a commutative semiring. In this semiring, the graphs
E_{0}
and
E_{1}
are the additive and multiplicative unit, respectively.

We say that a map
f:V(G)\to V(H)
is a cohomomorphism
G\to H
if for every distinct
u,v\in V(G)
if
\{u,v\}\not\in E(G)
, then
f(u)\neq f(v)
and
\{f(u),f(v)\}\not\in E(H)
. We write
G\leq H
if there is a cohomomorphism
G\to H
. We call
\leq
the cohomomorphism preorder. Cohomomorphisms are precisely the maps that preserve independent sets, and in particular
G\leq H
implies
\alpha(G)\leq\alpha(H)
.
We say that
G
and
H
are equivalent if
G\leq H
and
H\leq G
.

The independence number can be written in terms of cohomomorphisms and the graphs
E_{n}
by
\alpha(G)=\max\{n\in\mathbb{N}:E_{n}\leq G\}
. The “opposite” graph parameter
\overline{\chi}(G)=\min\{n\in\mathbb{N}:G\leq E_{n}\}
is known as the clique covering number, and equals the smallest number of cliques in the graph
G
that are needed to cover all its vertices.
9
9

9

The (standard) notation
\overline{\chi}
for the clique covering number originates from the relation of this parameter to the chromatic number, which is denoted by
\chi
. Namely,
\overline{\chi}(G)=\chi(\overline{G})
.

2.2
Asymptotic spectrum duality

Understanding the Shannon capacity naturally asks for understanding how graphs behave when taking large powers (under the strong product
\boxtimes
). For this we will use ideas developed in the theory of asymptotic spectrum duality
[
69
,
79
,
80
,
29
,
73
]
, which (for convenience of the reader) we will explain here in the setting of graphs, following
[
80
]
.

For any graphs
G,H
, we write
G\lesssim H
if there is a function
f:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
with
f(n)=o(n)
such that for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
we have
G^{\boxtimes n}\leq H^{\boxtimes(n+f(n))}
. We call
{\lesssim}
the asymptotic preorder. We say that
G
and
H
are asymptotically equivalent if
G\lesssim H
and
H\lesssim G
. Note that in particular, Shannon capacity is monotone under
\lesssim
.

The asymptotic spectrum of graphs
\mathcal{X}
is defined as the set of all maps
F
from graphs to non-negative reals, such that for any graphs
G,H
we have:
F(G\sqcup H)=F(G)+F(H)
(additivity),
F(G\boxtimes H)=F(G)F(H)
(multiplicativity),
F(E_{n})=n
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
(normalisation), and if
G\leq H
, then
F(G)\leq F(H)
(monotonicity).

It is an open problem to fully determine
\mathcal{X}
.
Known elements in
\mathcal{X}
are the Lovász theta function
\vartheta

[
48
]
, the fractional Haemers bound (over different fields)
[
20
]
, the fractional orthogonal rank (projective rank), and the fractional clique covering number
\overline{\chi}_{f}
(see
[
80
]
). Continuously many elements in
\mathcal{X}
can be constructed using the interpolation method of Vrana
[
72
]
. All of these are upper bounds on Shannon capacity, and it is not hard to prove that indeed every element of
\mathcal{X}
is an upper bound on Shannon capacity.

The asymptotic spectrum duality theorem says, in particular, that the upper bounds in
\mathcal{X}
are sufficiently strong to determine the Shannon capacity. In fact, it says that the functions in
\mathcal{X}
are strong enough to completely characterize the asymptotic preorder
\lesssim
.

Theorem 2.1

(Asymptotic spectrum duality)
.

Let
G,H
be graphs.

(a)

\Theta(G)=\min_{F\in\mathcal{X}}F(G)
.

(b)

\overline{\chi}_{f}(G)=\max_{F\in\mathcal{X}}F(G)
.

(c)

G\lesssim H
if and only if for every
F\in\mathcal{X}
,
F(G)\leq F(H)
.

Remark 2.2
.

The Shannon capacity
\Theta
itself is not in
\mathcal{X}
, which follows from results of Haemers
[
33
]
and Alon
[
5
]
. Thus, in
Theorem 2.1
, the element
F
minimizing
\min_{F\in\mathcal{X}}F(G)
depends on
G
. On the other hand, the fractional clique covering number
\overline{\chi}_{f}
is in
\mathcal{X}
, and it is the pointwise largest function in there. This is closely related to the nontrivial fact that
\overline{\chi}_{f}(G)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\overline{\chi}(G^{\boxtimes n})^{1/n}

[
64
, Theorem 67.17]
.

Remark 2.3
.

Theorem 2.1
characterizes
\Theta
as a minimization, rather than an infimum. This is a consequence of compactness of
\mathcal{X}
, which intuitively follows from the fact that the defining conditions of
\mathcal{X}
are closed conditions and the fact that for every graph
G
, the values that any
F\in X
can take on
G
are bounded (by
\alpha(G)
and
\overline{\chi}(G)
, say). We discuss compactness further in
Section 2.4
.

2.3
Distance and convergence

The functions in the asymptotic spectrum
\mathcal{X}
provide, in the precise sense of
Theorem 2.1
, all asymptotic information of a graph. They also naturally allow us to measure distance between graphs in a way that is appropriate in the context of Shannon capacity. We discuss this distance and basic properties.

Definition 2.4
.

For any graphs
G,H
, we define the
asymptotic spectrum distance
by

d(G,H)=\sup_{F\in\mathcal{X}}|F(G)-F(H)|.

Remark 2.5
.

The asymptotic spectrum distance is not quite a distance on graphs, because for distinct graphs
G,H
we may have
d(G,H)=0
. However, if we let
\mathcal{G}
denote the set of all graphs in which we identify graphs that are asymptotically equivalent (recall that
G,H
are asymptotically equivalent if
G\lesssim H
and
H\lesssim G
,
Section 2.2
), then the asymptotic spectrum distance is indeed a distance on
\mathcal{G}
(this follows from
Theorem 2.1
) and the set
\mathcal{G}
is a metric space.

Remark 2.6
.

The supremum in
2.4
is in fact attained. This follows from compactness of
\mathcal{X}
, as we will discuss in
Section 2.4
.

Lemma 2.7
.

If a sequence of graphs
(G_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
converges to a graph
H
in the asymptotic spectrum distance, then
(\Theta(G_{i}))_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
converges to
\Theta(H)
.

Proof.

Let
\varepsilon>0
. There is an
N
such that for every
i>N
and every
F\in\mathcal{X}
we have
|F(G_{i})-F(H)|<\varepsilon
.
Let
F_{G_{i}},F_{H}\in\mathcal{X}
such that
F_{G_{i}}(G_{i})=\Theta(G_{i})
and
F_{H}(H)=\Theta(H)
(
Theorem 2.1
).
Then we have

\Theta(H)=F_{H}(H)>F_{H}(G_{i})-\varepsilon\geq\Theta(G_{i})-\varepsilon

and

\Theta(G_{i})=F_{G_{i}}(G_{i})>F_{G_{i}}(H)-\varepsilon\geq\Theta(H)-\varepsilon
.
Thus
|\Theta(G_{i})-\Theta(H)|<\varepsilon
.
∎

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
G,H
be graphs and
a,b\in\mathbb{N}
,
b\geq 1
. The following are equivalent.

(i)

d(G,H)\leq a/b

(ii)

E_{b}\boxtimes G\lesssim(E_{b}\boxtimes H)\sqcup E_{a}
and
E_{b}\boxtimes H\lesssim(E_{b}\boxtimes G)\sqcup E_{a}

(iii)

(E_{b}\boxtimes G)^{\boxtimes n}\leq((E_{b}\boxtimes H)\sqcup E_{a})^{\boxtimes(n+o(n))}
and
(E_{b}\boxtimes H)^{\boxtimes n}\leq((E_{b}\boxtimes G)\sqcup E_{a})^{\boxtimes(n+o(n))}

Proof.

We give the proof (which is a simple application of
Theorem 2.1
) in a more general setting in
A.4
.
∎

2.4
Graphs as continuous functions on a compact space

So far we have treated the asymptotic spectrum
\mathcal{X}
simply as a set of functions on the semiring of graphs, and we made the semiring of (asymptotic equivalence classes of) graphs into a metric space using the distance induced by these functions. We will now take a different perspective that will allow us to apply ideas from analysis and that will in particular provide a natural (albeit abstract) embedding into a complete space.

In this different perspective we will turn
\mathcal{X}
into a topological space and we will turn graphs into continuous functions on this space. This works as follows.
We associate to every graph
G
its evaluation function

\widehat{G}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}:F\mapsto F(G).

We may think of
\widehat{G}
as the (infinitely long) vector of evaluations of the elements in the asymptotic spectrum on
G
.
10
10

10

The object
\widehat{G}
acts as an “asymptotic profile” of
G
in a way that is reminiscent of (but different from) the homomorphism profile of Lovász
[
49
, Section 5.4.1]
. The latter counts the number of homomorphisms to (or from) a given graph, and uniquely determines graphs up to isomorphism.

The basic properties of these objects are:

Lemma 2.9
.

Let
G
and
H
be graphs.

(i)

\widehat{G\boxtimes H}=\widehat{G}\widehat{H}

(ii)

\widehat{G\sqcup H}=\widehat{G}+\widehat{H}

(iii)

G\lesssim H
if and only if
\widehat{G}\leq\widehat{H}
pointwise

(iv)

\min\widehat{G}=\Theta(G)

(v)

\max\widehat{G}=\overline{\chi}_{f}(G)

Proof.

These follow directly from
Theorem 2.1
and the definition of
\mathcal{X}
.
∎

We claim that
\mathcal{X}
can be given a topology that is compact, Hausdorff and such that every function
\widehat{G}
is continuous on
\mathcal{X}
.
Indeed, we get this by giving
\mathcal{X}
the topology generated by the functions
\widehat{G}
, that is, the coarsest (i.e., weakest) topology for which every
\widehat{G}
is continuous.
11
11

11

This is also called the weak or initial topology on
\mathcal{X}
induced by the functions
\widehat{G}
. This is the topology on
\mathcal{X}
that is generated (under unions and finite intersections) by the inverse images
\widehat{G}^{-1}(U)
where
G
goes over all graphs and
U
over all open subsets of the nonnegative reals (with the Euclidean topology).
Then
\mathcal{X}
is easily seen to be Hausdorff (but we will not use this). Importantly, it is compact:

Lemma 2.10
.

The set
\mathcal{X}
is compact in the coarsest topology such that for every graph
G
the function
\widehat{G}
is continuous.

Proof.

This is a “standard” fact
[
13
,
69
,
73
]
, but we give here a proof for the convenience of the reader.
We consider
\mathcal{X}
as a subset of
\prod_{G\in\mathcal{G}}\mathbb{R}
with inclusion map
i:F\mapsto(F(G))_{G\in\mathcal{G}}
.
Endowing
\prod_{G\in\mathcal{G}}\mathbb{R}
with the product topology, the map
i
is continuous if and only if all evaluation functions
\widehat{G}
are continuous
[
52
, Theorem 19.6]
. The coarsest topology on
\mathcal{X}
that makes
i
continuous is by definition the subspace topology.
For every graph
G
, the set of values
\{F(G):F\in\mathcal{X}\}
is a subset of
[0,\overline{\chi}(G)]
, where
\overline{\chi}
denotes the clique covering number. Thus
\mathcal{X}
is a subset of
\prod_{G\in\mathcal{G}}[0,\overline{\chi}(G)]
.
Since
\prod_{G\in\mathcal{G}}[0,\overline{\chi}(G)]
is compact by Tychonoff’s theorem
[
52
, Theorem 37.3]
, it is now sufficient to show that
\mathcal{X}
is closed.

For every graph
H
, denote by
\pi_{H}:\prod_{G\in\mathcal{G}}\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
the projection on the coordinate indexed by
H
. These are continuous by definition of the product topology and so is any polynomial combination of them. We can write
\mathcal{X}=Z_{\text{add}}\cap Z_{\text{mul}}\cap Z_{\text{norm}}\cap Z_{\text{mon}}
, where

\displaystyle Z_{\text{add}}

\displaystyle=\bigcap_{G,H\in\mathcal{G}}(\pi_{G\sqcup H}-(\pi_{G}+\pi_{H}))^{-1}(\{0\}),\quad

\displaystyle Z_{\text{mul}}

\displaystyle=\bigcap_{G,H\in\mathcal{G}}(\pi_{G\boxtimes H}-\pi_{G}\pi_{H})^{-1}(\{0\}),

\displaystyle Z_{\text{norm}}

\displaystyle=\bigcap_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\pi_{E_{n}}^{-1}(\{n\}),

\displaystyle Z_{\text{mon}}

\displaystyle=\bigcap_{\begin{subarray}{c}G,H\in\mathcal{G}\\
G\leq H\end{subarray}}(\pi_{H}-\pi_{G})^{-1}([0,\infty)).

All sets are written as the intersections of pre-images of closed sets under continuous maps and are thus closed. We conclude that
\mathcal{X}
is closed.
∎

Remark 2.11
.

Since the image of a compact space under a continuous map is compact, we see that
\{F(G):F\in\mathcal{X}\}=\widehat{G}(\mathcal{X})
is a compact subset of the reals, that is, closed and bounded. In particular, infimum and supremum over this set are attained (cf.
Theorem 2.1
(ii) and (iii), and
2.9
(iv) and (v)).

Denote by
C(\mathcal{X})
the set of continuous functions
\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
. Equip
C(\mathcal{X})
with the supremum-norm, which is given by
||f||_{\infty}=\sup_{F\in\mathcal{X}}|f(F)|
for
f\in C(\mathcal{X})
. The distance of any
f,g\in C(\mathcal{X})
is given by
||f-g||_{\infty}
. Then
C(\mathcal{X})
is a complete metric space
[
63
, Theorem 7.15]
.

The set of continuous functions
C(\mathcal{X})
is a semiring (and in fact ring) under pointwise addition and pointwise multiplication.
The map
G\mapsto\widehat{G}
is a semiring homomorphism from the semiring of graphs to
C(\mathcal{X})
.

Lemma 2.12
.

For any graphs
G,H
, we have

d(G,H)=||\widehat{G}-\widehat{H}||_{\infty}
.

Proof.

||\widehat{G}-\widehat{H}||_{\infty}=\sup_{F\in\mathcal{X}}|\widehat{G}(F)-\widehat{H}(F)|=\sup_{F\in\mathcal{X}}|F(G)-F(H)|=d(G,H)
. ∎

Through the above construction, graphs correspond to elements of
C(\mathcal{X})
. Since
C(\mathcal{X})
is complete, any Cauchy sequence of graphs will indeed have a limit point in
C(\mathcal{X})
, but as we will see (
3.16
), this limit point may not correspond to a graph! (This answers a question from
[
55
, Section 9.3]
.)

To finish this part, we mention that the continuous function point of view naturally lets us use Dini’s theorem from analysis to prove the following sufficient condition for convergence of graphs.

Lemma 2.13
.

Let
(G_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of graphs and let
H
be a graph. Suppose that
F(G_{1}),F(G_{2}),F(G_{3}),\ldots
converges to
F(H)
for every
F\in\mathcal{X}
. If
F(G_{1}),F(G_{2})
,
F(G_{3}),\ldots
is nondecreasing for every
F\in\mathcal{X}
, or nonincreasing for every
F\in\mathcal{X}
, then
G_{i}
converges to
H
.

Proof.

The sequence of continuous functions
\widehat{G}_{i}\in C(\mathcal{X})
converges pointwise to the continuous function
\widehat{H}\in C(\mathcal{X})
. Moreover,
\widehat{G}_{i}(F)
is monotone in
i
for every
F\in\mathcal{X}
(either all nondecreasing or all nonincreasing). Thus
\widehat{G}_{i}
converges uniformly to
\widehat{H}
by Dini’s theorem
[
63
, Theorem 7.13]
.
∎

2.5
Distance of induced subgraphs of vertex-transitive graphs

We give a general method for constructing pairs of graphs that are close in the asymptotic spectrum metric (which we defined in
Section 2.3
). This method makes use of induced subgraphs of vertex-transitive graphs.

Let
G
be a graph, let
S\subseteq V(G)
, and let
G[S]
denote the induced subgraph of
G
to
S
. Then we have the inequality
G[S]\leq G
in the cohomomorphism preorder.
In this section we will quantify how close
G[S]
is to
G
.

As a starting point, we state a basic relation between the independence numbers
\alpha(G[S])
and
\alpha(G)
, which follows from the well-known No-Homomorphism Lemma of Albertson and Collins
[
1
, Theorem 2]
(see also
[
37
, Corollary 1.23]
).

Lemma 2.14
.

Let
G
be a vertex-transitive graph and
S\subseteq V(G)
. Then

\alpha(G[S])\leq\alpha(G)\leq\frac{|V(G)|}{|S|}\alpha(G[S]).

We will now extend

2.14
to elements in the asymptotic spectrum
\mathcal{X}
, that is, we will replace the independence number
\alpha
by any function
F\in\mathcal{X}
.
12
12

12

We note that the independence number
\alpha
is not in
\mathcal{X}
.
The proof is based on an inequality for vertex-transitive graphs
G
of
[
72
]
, which says
G\leq E_{N}\boxtimes G[S]
for
N=\lceil|V(G)||S|^{-1}\ln|V(G)|\rceil
. (Recall that
E_{N}=\overline{K}_{N}
denotes the graph with
N
vertices and no edges.)

Lemma 2.15
.

Let
G
be a vertex-transitive graph and
S\subseteq V(G)
. Let
F\in\mathcal{X}
. Then

F(G[S])\leq F(G)\leq\frac{|V(G)|}{|S|}F(G[S]).

Proof.

The first inequality follows directly from
F
being monotone under cohomomorphism.
For any vertex-transitive graph
G
and
S\subseteq V(G)
we have
G\leq E_{N}\boxtimes G[S]

for
N=\lceil|V(G)||S|^{-1}\ln|V(G)|\rceil
. Let
m\in\mathbb{N}
. Then
G[S]^{\boxtimes m}
is an induced subgraph of
G^{\boxtimes m}
. Note that
G^{\boxtimes m}
is vertex-transitive. Then
G^{\boxtimes m}\leq E_{N_{m}}\boxtimes G[S]^{\boxtimes m}
with
N_{m}=\lceil|V(G)|^{m}|S|^{-m}m\ln|V(G)|\rceil
.
Apply
F
to both sides of the inequality, take the
m
-th root on both sides, and let
m
go to infinity to get the claim.
∎

Finally, we mention that,
from either of
2.14
or
2.15
follows the analogous inequality for the Shannon capacity:
13
13

13

The Shannon capacity
\Theta
is not in
\mathcal{X}

[
33
,
5
]
, so this requires a short proof.
Let
G
be a vertex-transitive graph and
S\subseteq V(G)
. Then

\Theta(G[S])\leq\Theta(G)\leq|V(G)|\cdot|S|^{-1}\cdot\Theta(G[S]).

Indeed, this follows from
2.14
applied to powers of
G
. Alternatively, there is an
F\in\mathcal{X}
such that
\Theta(G[S])=F(G[S])
by
Theorem 2.1
, and then applying
2.15
gives the result.
For ease of reference, we combine the results of this section into the following lemma.

Lemma 2.16
.

Let
G
be a vertex-transitive graph and
S\subseteq V(G)
. Let
F\in\mathcal{X}\cup\{\alpha,\Theta\}
. Then

F(G[S])\leq F(G)\leq\frac{|V(G)|}{|S|}F(G[S]).

2.15
lets us construct graphs
G
and
G[S]
that are close to each other in the asymptotic spectrum distance, as long as we have a handle on the ratio
|V(G)|/|S|
. The next goal will be to obtain Cauchy sequences using this method. For this we need to do more, as we will discuss in the next sections.

3
Converging sequences of finite graphs

In
Section 2
we have defined the asymptotic spectrum distance on graphs, and discussed its basic properties, including characterizations of convergence and the motivating connection to the Shannon capacity. A central question is how to construct (non-trivial) converging sequences, or even just Cauchy sequences.

In this section we will give several kinds of constructions of non-trivial converging sequences of graphs. For this we will use a natural family of vertex-transitive graphs which we call fraction graphs (and which are also called circular graphs).

First we will discuss the definition and basic properties of fraction graphs. An important component is a vertex removal strategy for constructing fraction graphs that are close in the asymptotic spectrum distance.

Next we use the vertex removal strategy to construct non-trivial converging sequences of graphs. In particular, for any fraction graph we construct a non-trivial sequence of fraction graphs that converges to it.
As a consequence of these constructions, we obtain a right-continuity property of all elements in the asymptotic spectrum (e.g., the Lovász theta function) on fraction graphs.

Finally, we construct Cauchy sequences of fraction graphs that have no limit in the set of finite graphs. Thus the set of finite graphs is not complete in the asymptotic spectrum distance. In
Section 4
we will study infinite graphs on the circle as the missing limit points.

3.1
Fraction graph definition and basic properties

Fraction graphs have been studied since the work of Vince
[
71
]
, who used them to study variations on the chromatic number. These graphs have been studied under many names, including circular graphs
[
55
,
54
]
, cycle-powers
[
9
]
, and (the complement of) rational complete graphs
[
37
]
and circular complete graphs
[
78
]
.

Definition 3.1
.

For any
p,q\in\mathbb{N}
with
p/q\geq 2
we let
E_{p/q}
be the graph with vertex set
V=\{0,\dots,p-1\}
and with distinct
u,v\in V
forming an edge if
u-v\pmod{p}<q
or
v-u\pmod{p}<q
, that is, if their distance modulo
p
is strictly less than
q
. The graphs
E_{p/q}
we call
fraction graphs
.
It will sometimes be convenient to allow also
p,q\in\mathbb{N}
with
p/q<2
, in which case we will use the (natural) convention that
E_{p/q}
is the complete graph on
p
vertices.
Also, it will sometimes be convenient to identify the vertex set
V=\{0,\dots,p-1\}
with
\mathbb{Z}_{p}=\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
, the integers modulo
p

A fundamental property of the fraction graphs is that they are ordered under cohomomorphism as the rational numbers.

Lemma 3.2

(
[
37
, Theorem 6.3]
)
.

For
p,q,p^{\prime},q^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}

if and only if
E_{p/q}\leq E_{p^{\prime}/q^{\prime}}
.

In fact, a cohomomorphism
E_{p/q}\to E_{p^{\prime}/q^{\prime}}
is simply
x\mapsto\lfloor xp^{\prime}/p\rfloor
(assuming that
p/q\leq p^{\prime}/q^{\prime}
).

In particular, it follows from

p/q=p^{\prime}/q^{\prime}

and

are equivalent under cohomomorphism, that is,
E_{p/q}\geq E_{p^{\prime}/q^{\prime}}
and
E_{p/q}\leq E_{p^{\prime}/q^{\prime}}
. Also, we see that for any two inequivalent fraction graphs, there exists a fraction graph that is strictly in between them in the cohomomorphism order, making the fraction graphs a natural family for constructing interesting converging sequences of graphs.

The “label” of a fraction graph can be computed using the fractional clique covering number
\overline{\chi}_{f}
.

Lemma 3.3

(
[
37
, Cor. 6.20, 6.24]
)

For
p,q\in\mathbb{N}
,
p/q\geq 2

\overline{\chi}_{f}(E_{p/q})=p/q
.

Since
\overline{\chi}_{f}
is an element of the asymptotic spectrum

E_{p/q}\leq E_{p^{\prime}/q^{\prime}}
if and only if
E_{p/q}\lesssim E_{p^{\prime}/q^{\prime}}
, that is, cohomomorphism and asymptotic cohomomorphism coincide on fraction graphs.

Remark 3.4
.

We warn the reader that, while the fraction graphs are ordered (under cohomomorphism) as the rational numbers (
3.2
), in many other ways they do not behave as the rational numbers, in particular when it comes to how they behave under the strong product or the disjoint union (the arithmetic operations of our semiring). Indeed, it is not true in general that

is equivalent to
E_{(ps)/(qt)}
. For example, suppose

is equivalent to
E_{p/q}
, then

. However, this contradicts the well-known fact that
\alpha(E_{5/2}^{\boxtimes 2})=5<6
, which follows, for instance, from
\vartheta(E_{5/2}^{\boxtimes 2})=5

[
48
]
, where

is the Lovász theta function.

3.2
Convergence to any fraction graph from above

In this section we will use
the vertex removal strategy of
Section 3.1
to construct non-trivial converging sequences of fraction graphs.
More precisely, for any fraction graph
E_{a/b}
we will construct a non-trivial sequence of fraction graphs
E_{p_{n}/q_{n}}
converging to
E_{a/b}
. The convergence will be “from above” in the sense that the numbers
p_{n}/q_{n}
will satisfy
a/b\leq p_{n}/q_{n}
and will be decreasing in
n
, and thus
E_{p_{n}/q_{n}}
approaches
E_{a/b}
from above in the cohomomorphism order. It will naturally follow that for any sequence
p_{n}/q_{n}
converging to
a/b
from above, we have that
E_{p_{n}/q_{n}}
converges to
E_{a/b}
.

In terms of the asymptotic spectrum
\mathcal{X}
, our result says that for any
F\in\mathcal{X}
(and in fact for any
F\in\mathcal{X}\cup\{\alpha,\Theta\}
), the function
\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 2}\to\mathbb{R}:p/q\mapsto F(E_{p/q})
is right-continuous. So for instance, the Lovász theta function is right-continuous on fraction graphs.

Our result solves a problem of Schrijver and Polak
[
55
, Chapter 9]
.
They constructed for every integer
m\in\mathbb{N}
a sequence of fraction graphs
E_{p_{n}/q_{n}}
that converges to
E_{m}
from below, which implies that for any sequence
p_{n}/q_{n}
converging to
m
from below we have that
E_{p_{n}/q_{n}}
converges to
E_{m}
. In terms of the asymptotic spectrum
\mathcal{X}
, their result says that for any
F\in\mathcal{X}
the function
p/q\mapsto F(E_{p/q})
is left-continuous at every integer.

Theorem 3.7
.

(a)

Let
F\in\mathcal{X}\cup\{\alpha,\Theta\}
.
The function
\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 2}\to\mathbb{R}:p/q\mapsto F(E_{p/q})
is right-continuous.

(b)

The right-continuity in
(
a
) is uniform in
\mathcal{X}
: for each
p^{\prime}/q^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 2}
and each
\varepsilon>0
there is a
\delta>0
such that

F(E_{p^{\prime}/q^{\prime}})\leq F(E_{p/q})<F(E_{p^{\prime}/q^{\prime}})+\varepsilon

for all
F\in\mathcal{X}
and all
p/q\in\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 2}
with
p^{\prime}/q^{\prime}\leq p/q<p^{\prime}/q^{\prime}+\delta
.

(c)

If
p_{n}/q_{n}
converges to
a/b
from above, then
E_{p_{n}/q_{n}}
converges to
E_{a/b}
.

Proof.

(a) Choose a fixed coprime pair
p^{\prime},q^{\prime}
, we will show that
F
is right-continuous at
p^{\prime}/q^{\prime}
.
The function
p/q\to F(E_{p/q})
is monotone and thus it is enough to find an explicit sequence
(p_{n}/q_{n})_{n\geq 1}
approaching
p^{\prime}/q^{\prime}
from above for which
F(E_{p_{n}/q_{n}})
converges to
F(E_{p^{\prime}/q^{\prime}})
.

Because
p^{\prime}
and
q^{\prime}
are coprime, there exist positive integers
a,b
such that

aq^{\prime}-bp^{\prime}=1
. Define the sequences
p_{n}=a+p^{\prime}\cdot n
and
q_{n}=b+q^{\prime}\cdot n
. For every
n\geq 1
we find that
0<p^{\prime}<p_{n}
,
0<q^{\prime}<q_{n}
and
p_{n}q^{\prime}-q_{n}p^{\prime}=1
, therefore we can apply

2.16

to obtain

F(E_{p^{\prime}/q^{\prime}})\leq F(E_{p_{n}/q_{n}})\leq\frac{p_{n}}{p_{n}-1}F(E_{p^{\prime}/q^{\prime}}).

Since
p_{n}/(p_{n}-1)
converges to
1
when
n
goes to infinity, we find that
F(E_{p_{n}/q_{n}})
converges to
F(E_{p^{\prime}/q^{\prime}})
.

(b) The uniform right-continuity follows from applying Dini’s theorem,
2.13
. We give a direct proof for convenience. Let
\varepsilon>0
. Choose an
n
such that
\tfrac{p^{\prime}}{p_{n}-1}<\varepsilon
. Now let
\delta=p_{n}/q_{n}-p^{\prime}/q^{\prime}
.
If
0\leq p/q-p^{\prime}/q^{\prime}<\delta
, then
p^{\prime}/q^{\prime}\leq p/q\leq p_{n}/q_{n}
, and thus
F(E_{p/q})-F(E_{p^{\prime}/q^{\prime}})\leq\tfrac{1}{p_{n}-1}F(E_{p^{\prime}/q^{\prime}})\leq\tfrac{1}{p_{n}-1}\overline{\chi}(E_{p^{\prime}/q^{\prime}})=\tfrac{p^{\prime}}{p_{n}-1}<\varepsilon
.

(c) This follows directly from (b).
∎

Remark 3.8
.

It follows from

that for every graph

.

Remark 3.9
.

Theorem 3.7
in particular implies that

Remark 3.10
.

In the context of

, we note that if
p_{n}/q_{n}
is a strictly decreasing sequence converging to

E_{p_{n}/q_{n}}
will also be strictly decreasing in the cohomomorphism order, and in fact in the asymptotic cohomomorphism order, since the asymptotic clique covering number is strictly decreasing. However, we do not know whether every

(or the Shannon capacity

Remark 3.11
.

Theorem 3.7
leaves open whether
\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 2}\to\mathbb{R}:p/q\mapsto F(E_{p/q})
is left-continuous at the non-integer points of

(left-continuity at integer points being proven in
[

converges to a non-integer

from below, then we do not know whether

converges to

(and we even do not know convergence for concrete elements in the asymptotic spectrum like the Lovász theta function). This question turns out to be equivalent to a question about infinite graphs on the circle, which we will discuss in

.

3.3
Intermezzo: Continued fraction expansion of irrational numbers

To prepare for our next construction of converging sequences of fraction graphs in
Section 3.4
, and for the convenience of the reader, we recall here basic facts about the continued fraction expansion of irrational numbers. We will use these facts again later in
Section 5
.

Given an irrational number
r
define the sequence
(r_{n})_{n\geq 0}
recursively by
r_{0}=r
and
r_{n+1}=(r_{n}-\lfloor r_{n}\rfloor)^{-1}
. The sequence
(a_{n})_{n\geq 0}
defined by
a_{n}=\lfloor r_{n}\rfloor
describes the coefficients of the continued fraction expansion of
r
. The sequence of
convergents

(p_{n}/q_{n})_{n\geq 0}
is defined by
(p_{0},q_{0})=(a_{0},1)
,
(p_{1},q_{1})=(a_{0}a_{1}+1,a_{1})
, and

(p_{n},q_{n})=(a_{n}p_{n-1}+p_{n-2},a_{n}q_{n-1}+q_{n-2})

for
n\geq 2
. We call
p_{n}/q_{n}
the
n
th-order convergent
of
r
. We recall the following facts concerning convergents.

Lemma 3.12
.

Let
r\in\mathbb{R}
be irrational and let
(p_{n}/q_{n})_{n\geq 0}
be its sequence of convergents.

(a)

We have

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{p_{n}}{q_{n}}=r.

(b)

For all
n\geq 1
,

q_{n}p_{n-1}-p_{n}q_{n-1}=(-1)^{n}.

(c)

The even-order convergents form an increasing sequence, while the odd-order convergents form a decreasing sequence, that is,

\frac{p_{0}}{q_{0}}<\frac{p_{2}}{q_{2}}<\frac{p_{4}}{q_{4}}<\cdots<r<\cdots<\frac{p_{5}}{q_{5}}<\frac{p_{3}}{q_{3}}<\frac{p_{1}}{q_{1}}.

(d)

The convergents are best possible approximations to
r
in the following sense: for every
n\geq 0
and integers
a,b
with
|a|\leq|p_{n}|
,

\left|r-\frac{p_{n}}{q_{n}}\right|\leq\left|r-\frac{a}{b}\right|.

Proof.

Proofs for these statements can be found in any elementary text on continued fractions. For example, these four items are respectively proved in Section 5, Theorem 2, Theorem 4, and Theorem 17 of
[
42
]
.
∎

Corollary 3.13
.

Let
r\in\mathbb{R}_{>1}
be irrational and let
(p_{n}/q_{n})_{n\geq 0}
be its sequence of convergents. Then for all
n\geq 0
we have

\lceil p_{2n}/r\rceil=q_{2n}\text{ and }\lfloor p_{2n+1}/r\rfloor=q_{2n+1}.

Proof.

Because
r>1
we have that
p_{m}
and
q_{m}
are strictly positive for all
m
.
Because
p_{2n}/q_{2n}
is the best approximation to
r
among fractions with equal or smaller numerator we see that
q_{2n}
is the unique positive integer
q
for which
(q-1)\cdot p_{2n}<r<q\cdot p_{2n}
. This equation is satisfied for
q=\lceil p_{2n}/r\rceil
and thus
\lceil p_{2n}/r\rceil=q_{2n}
. The proof of the other equality goes analogously.
∎

3.4
Cauchy sequences with no finite limit graph

In
Section 3.2
we used the vertex-removal strategy to prove that if
p_{n}/q_{n}
converges to
a/b
from above, then
E_{p_{n}/q_{n}}
converges to
E_{a/b}
.
In this section we give another construction of interesting converging sequences, or rather of Cauchy sequences.

We will prove that if
p_{n}/q_{n}
converges to any irrational number
r\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 2}
(without requiring this convergence to be “from above” or “from below”), then
E_{p_{n}/q_{n}}
is a Cauchy sequence.
For this result we will make use of similar ideas as in
Section 3.2
and of the continued fraction expansion of
r
(
Section 3.3
).
It will follow from a simple argument (rationality of the fractional clique covering number of finite graphs) that this sequence cannot have a finite graph as a limit point. Thus we find that the set of finite graphs is not complete.
In
Section 4
we will discuss how the limit points of these Cauchy sequences of fraction graphs can be described by infinite graphs on the circle.

In terms of the asymptotic spectrum
\mathcal{X}
, our result says that for any element
F\in\mathcal{X}
(and in fact any
F\in\mathcal{X}\cup\{\alpha,\Theta\}
), the map
p/q\mapsto F(E_{p/q})
has an extension to
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 2}
that is continuous at every irrational number.

Theorem 3.14
.

Let
r\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 2}
be irrational.

(a)

For every
F\in\mathcal{X}\cup\{\alpha,\Theta\}
,

\sup_{a/b<r}F(E_{a/b})=\inf_{a/b>r}F(E_{a/b}).

(1)

In particular,
\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 2}\to\mathbb{R}:p/q\mapsto F(E_{p/q})
has an extension
\tilde{F}
to
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 2}
that is
continuous at every irrational number, namely by letting
\tilde{F}(r)=\sup_{a/b<r}F(E_{a/b})
.

(b)

The continuity in
(
a
)
is uniform over all
F\in\mathcal{X}
in the following sense. Given any
\varepsilon>0
, there is a
\delta>0
such that if
|r-p/q|<\delta
, then
|\sup_{a/b<r}F(E_{a/b})-F(E_{p/q})|<\varepsilon
for every
F\in\mathcal{X}
.

(c)

If
a_{n}/b_{n}
converges to
r
, then
E_{a_{n}/b_{n}}
is Cauchy.

Proof.

(
a
) Let
(p_{n}/q_{n})_{n\geq 1}
and
(p_{n}^{\prime}/q_{n}^{\prime})_{n\geq 1}
be the odd-order and even-order convergents of
r
respectively. It follows from
3.12
that (i)
p_{n}/q_{n}
and
p_{n}^{\prime}/q_{n}^{\prime}
converge to
r
from above and below, respectively, (ii) for all
n
we have
p_{n}q_{n}^{\prime}-p_{n}^{\prime}q_{n}=1
, and (iii)
p_{n}\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
. By
2.16
and the fact that
a/b\mapsto F(E_{a/b})
is nondecreasing we have

F(E_{p_{n}^{\prime}/q_{n}^{\prime}})\leq\sup_{a/b<r}F(E_{a/b})\leq\inf_{a/b>r}F(E_{a/b})\leq F(E_{p_{n}/q_{n}})\leq\frac{p_{n}}{p_{n}-1}F(E_{p_{n}^{\prime}/q_{n}^{\prime}}).

Thus both the left-hand side and the right-hand side of (
1
) are contained in an interval of length
\frac{1}{p_{n}-1}F(E_{p_{n}^{\prime}/q_{n}^{\prime}})
which is upper bounded by
\frac{1}{p_{n}-1}\overline{\chi}_{f}(E_{p_{n}^{\prime}/q_{n}^{\prime}})<\frac{r}{p_{n}-1}
. This bound does not depend on
F
and goes to
0
as
n
goes to infinity. This proves

Equation 1
and thus
\tilde{F}
as defined in (
a
) is continuous at
r
.

(
b
) To prove uniform continuity, let
\varepsilon>0
. Choose an
n
such that
\frac{r}{p_{n}-1}<\varepsilon
. Now take any
\delta>0
such that

\delta<\min\{p_{n}/q_{n}-r,r-p_{n}^{\prime}/q_{n}^{\prime}\}.

Then if
|r-x|<\delta
we have
x\in[p_{n}^{\prime}/q_{n}^{\prime},p_{n}/q_{n}]
and thus
|\tilde{F}(r)-\tilde{F}(x)|\leq\frac{r}{p_{n}-1}<\varepsilon
.

(
c
) Let
a_{n}/b_{n}
be a sequence of rational numbers converging to
r
. Then we have

\displaystyle d(E_{a_{n}/b_{n}},E_{a_{m}/b_{m}})

\displaystyle=\sup_{F\in\mathcal{X}}|F(E_{a_{n}/b_{n}})-F(E_{a_{m}/b_{m}})|

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{F\in\mathcal{X}}|\tilde{F}(a_{n}/b_{n})-\tilde{F}(r)|+\sup_{F\in\mathcal{X}}|\tilde{F}(a_{m}/b_{m})-\tilde{F}(r)|,

where
\tilde{F}
is defined as above. Since both summands converge to
0
we see that
E_{a_{n}/b_{n}}
is indeed Cauchy.
∎

Remark 3.15
.

Unlike for
Theorem 3.7
(b),
the uniform continuity of
Theorem 3.14
(b) does not directly follow using Dini’s theorem (
2.13
). However, if we first extend the semiring of graphs to
infinite
graphs (as we will do in the next section), then there is such a natural proof.

As a consequence of
Theorem 3.14
we find that the set of finite graphs with the asymptotic spectrum distance is not complete.

Corollary 3.16
.

There is a Cauchy sequence of fraction graphs
E_{a_{n}/b_{n}}
that does not converge to any finite graph.

Proof.

Let
r>2
be an irrational number and let
a_{n}/b_{n}
be a sequence of rational numbers converging to
r
.
Then
E_{a_{n}/b_{n}}
is a Cauchy sequence (
Theorem 3.14
(c)).
Note that the fractional clique covering number of
E_{a_{n}/b_{n}}
is given by
\overline{\chi}_{f}(E_{a_{n}/b_{n}})=a_{n}/b_{n}
. Thus any limit graph
G
of the sequence
E_{a_{n}/b_{n}}
should satisfy
\overline{\chi}_{f}(G)=r
. This contradicts the fact that
\overline{\chi}_{f}(G)
is rational for any finite graph
G
.
∎

4
Infinite graphs as limit points

In
Section 3
we constructed Cauchy sequences of finite graphs in two ways: non-trivial converging sequences of fraction graphs to any fraction graph, and Cauchy sequences of fraction graphs with no finite graph as limit point. What are these limit points? In an abstract sense we know that we can describe them as elements of the space
C(\mathcal{X})
of continuous functions on the asymptotic spectrum
\mathcal{X}
, since this space is complete (
Section 2.3
). In this section we will see how infinite graphs give a concrete model for these limit points.

The protagonists of this section are a family of infinite graphs on the circle, which we may think of as “infinite versions” of the fraction graphs of
Section 3
. These infinite graphs are labeled by real numbers
r\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 2}
instead of rational numbers
p/q\in\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 2}
.
We present two versions of these graphs: an “open” version and a “closed” version, depending on whether a strict or non-strict inequality is used in their definition (which we will state in a moment). Understanding this seemingly minor difference will turn out to play an important role in the theory (in this section, and in
Section 5
).

We will first define the two versions of circle graphs and prove their basic properties. Next, we prove that for any irrational number
r\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 2}
, if
p_{n}/q_{n}
converges to
r
, then the Cauchy sequence
E_{p_{n}/q_{n}}
converges to a circle graph. Finally, we prove that the problem of convergence of fraction graphs “from below”, or left-continuity of the asymptotic spectrum, is equivalent to the asymptotic equivalence of open and closed circle graphs.

In
Section 5
we will study the circle graphs further to prove that the open and closed version are not equivalent under cohomomorphism, characterizing their self-cohomomorphisms in the process.

4.1
Circle graph definition and basic properties

Circle graphs are a natural family of infinite graphs. They have been studied in the context of circular colorings by Zhu
[
76
]
(implicitly) and Bauslaugh–Zhu
[
12
]
. See also, e.g.,
[
44
, Section 17.2.3]
. They are related to Borsuk graphs
[
27
,
47
]
. The definition and notation we use is as follows.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
C\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
denote a circle with unit circumference.
Let
r\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 2}
. Let
E_{r}^{\mathrm{o}}
be the infinite graph with vertex set
C
for which two vertices are adjacent if and only if they have distance strictly less than
1/r
on
C
.
Let
E_{r}^{\mathrm{c}}
be the infinite graph with vertex set
C
for which two vertices are adjacent if and only if they have distance at most
1/r
on
C
.

For illustration, we draw in
Figure 3
the neighbourhood of a vertex in the circle graphs
E^{\mathrm{o}}_{3}
,
E^{\mathrm{c}}_{3}
and
E^{\mathrm{c}}_{5/2}
. The neighbourhood in
E^{\mathrm{o}}_{3}
is open, while the neighbourhood in
E^{\mathrm{c}}_{3}
and
E^{\mathrm{c}}_{5/2}
is closed.

Figure 3:
The neighbourhood of a vertex (in red) in the circle graphs
E^{\mathrm{o}}_{3}
,
E^{\mathrm{c}}_{3}
and
E^{\mathrm{c}}_{5/2}
(from left to right). We take the circles to have unit circumference.

It is not hard to see that, just like the fraction graphs, the graphs
E_{r}^{\mathrm{o}}
are cohomomorphism-monotone with respect to
r
, and the same for the
E_{r}^{\mathrm{c}}
. Moreover, for every fixed
r
the closed version
E_{r}^{\mathrm{c}}
is always at most the open version
E_{r}^{\mathrm{o}}
. Conversely, if
r<s
, then the open version
E^{\mathrm{o}}_{r}
is at most the closed version
E^{\mathrm{c}}_{s}
.
We summarize these properties in the following lemma.

Lemma 4.2
.

If
r,s\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 2}
with
r<s
we have
E_{r}^{\mathrm{c}}\leq E_{r}^{\mathrm{o}}\leq E_{s}^{\mathrm{c}}\leq E_{s}^{\mathrm{o}}
.

Proof.

Each graph in this sequence of inequalities is a spanning subgraph of the previous graph and thus the identity map
f:C\to C
is a cohomomorphism.
∎

The next basic property, which we will use several times, is about finite induced subgraphs of the closed circle graphs. It is a priori clear that for any subset
S\subseteq V(E_{r}^{\mathrm{c}})
we have
E_{r}^{\mathrm{c}}[S]\leq E_{r}^{\mathrm{c}}
. For finite
S
, the following lemma bounds
E_{r}^{\mathrm{c}}[S]
further away from
E_{r}^{\mathrm{c}}
by means of an open circle graph that is strictly below
E_{r}^{\mathrm{c}}
.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
r\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 2}
and let
S\subseteq V(E_{r}^{\mathrm{c}})
be finite. Then there is an
a/b<r
such that
E_{r}^{\mathrm{c}}[S]\leq E^{\mathrm{o}}_{a/b}
.

Proof.

There is an
s\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 2}
such that the smallest distance over all distinct and non-adjacent
\alpha,\beta\in S
equals
1/s
. Then
E^{\mathrm{c}}_{r}[S]\leq E^{\mathrm{o}}_{s}
. Since
s<r
, any
a/b
such that
s<a/b<r
satisfies the claim by
4.2
.
∎

We call two (possibly infinite) graphs
G
and
H

equivalent
if
G\leq H
and
H\leq G
.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
p/q\in\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 2}
. Then
E^{\mathrm{o}}_{p/q}
and
E_{p/q}
are equivalent.

Proof.

To prove
E_{p/q}\leq E_{p/q}^{\mathrm{o}}
, consider
C
as
[0,1]
where
0
and
1
are identified, and let
f:[p]\to C
be defined by
v\mapsto v/p
. If
v,w\in[p]
are not adjacent, then they have distance at least
q
modulo
p
. Then
v/p
and
w/p
have distance at least
q/p
on
C
. Thus
f
is a cohomomorphism
E_{p/q}\to E_{p/q}^{\mathrm{o}}
.

To prove
E_{p/q}^{\mathrm{o}}\leq E_{p/q}
, let
f:C\to[p]
be defined by
\alpha\mapsto\lfloor p\alpha\rfloor
. One verifies that if
\alpha
and
\beta
have distance at least
q/p
on
C
, then
\lfloor p\alpha\rfloor
and
\lfloor p\beta\rfloor
have distance at least
q
modulo
p
. Thus

is a cohomomorphism

.
∎

We will see a generalization of

in

.

4.2
Asymptotic spectrum distance of infinite graphs

In
Section 2
we discussed asymptotic spectrum duality and distance for finite graphs. The theory extends naturally to infinite graphs, as was first noted by Fritz
[
28
]
, and which we will need in this section. We discuss the extension to infinite graphs briefly here, and refer to the appendix for a more detailed discussion of the general theory of which finite and infinite graphs are a special case (
Appendix A
).

Let
\mathcal{G}_{\infty}
be the set of all (possibly) infinite graphs with finite clique covering number.
16
16

16

To avoid any issues with
\mathcal{G}_{\infty}
not being a set, we will make the technical assumption that all our graphs have bounded cardinality. For concreteness, we may take this to be the cardinality of
\mathbb{R}
, since this suffices for our examples.
Strong product
\boxtimes
, Shannon capacity
\Theta
, disjoint union
\sqcup
, cohomomorphism
\rightarrow
, cohomomorphism preorder
\leq
, asymptotic preorder
\lesssim
, and asymptotic spectrum are defined for infinite graphs in the same way as for finite graphs. We will denote the asymptotic spectrum of infinite graphs by
\mathcal{X}_{\infty}
and keep using
\mathcal{X}
for the asymptotic spectrum of finite graphs. (This will allow us to compare the two, in a moment.) In
\mathcal{G}_{\infty}
we identify any two graphs that are asymptotically equivalent.

The asymptotic spectrum duality for infinite graphs is as follows:

Theorem 4.5

(Asymptotic spectrum duality)
.

Let
G,H\in\mathcal{G}_{\infty}
.

(a)

G\lesssim H
if and only if for every
F\in\mathcal{X}_{\infty}
,
F(G)\leq F(H)
.

(b)

\Theta(G)=\min_{F\in\mathcal{X}_{\infty}}F(G)
.

(c)

\mathcal{X}_{\infty}
is compact in the topology induced by the evaluation maps
\widehat{G}:F\mapsto F(G)
.

Remark 4.6
.

Note that, unlike for finite graphs (
Theorem 2.1
), for infinite graphs we do not know whether
\max_{F\in\mathcal{X}_{\infty}}F(G)
is an element of
\mathcal{X}_{\infty}
. One can prove, with general arguments (see
Theorem A.1
), that this maximization equals the asymptotic clique covering number
\lim_{n\to\infty}\overline{\chi}(G^{\boxtimes n})^{1/n}
.

The other theory we discussed for finite graphs remains true for infinite graphs as well. We define asymptotic spectrum distance on
\mathcal{G}_{\infty}
by
d(G,H)=\sup_{F\in\mathcal{X}_{\infty}}|F(G)-F(H)|
, and the supremum is attained. We may equivalently think of elements of
\mathcal{G}_{\infty}
as elements
\widehat{G}
of
C(\mathcal{X}_{\infty})
, real-valued continuous functions on
\mathcal{X}_{\infty}
, equipped with the sup-norm. Shannon capacity is continuous. And finally, Dini’s theorem implies that if
(G_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a sequence in
\mathcal{G}_{\infty}
and
H\in\mathcal{G}_{\infty}
, and if for every
F\in\mathcal{X}_{\infty}
we have that
(F(G_{n}))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is monotone in
i\in\mathbb{N}
and converges to
F(H)
, then
G_{n}
converges to
H
. (See
Appendix A
.)

It is natural to ask how
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{X}_{\infty}
are related. Indeed, there is a strong connection; to prove it we use the following basic lemma.

Lemma 4.7
.

For any two finite graphs
G,H
, we have
G\leq H
in the cohomomorphism preorder on finite graphs if and only if
G\leq H
in the cohomomorphism preorder on infinite graphs.

Proof.

This follows directly from the definition of a cohomomorphism
G\to H
(of graphs or infinite graphs) as a map from
V(G)
to
V(H)
that maps any pair of distinct non-adjacent vertices of
G
to distinct non-adjacent vertices of
H
.
∎

Thus,
4.7
says that the natural embedding from finite graphs to infinite graphs,
i:\mathcal{G}\to\mathcal{G}_{\infty}
is, first of all, well-defined (under cohomomorphism equivalence), and second, is an order-embedding: for every
G,H\in\mathcal{G}
we have
G\leq H
if and only if
i(G)\leq i(H)
.
In other words, infinite graphs do not introduce any new inequalities between finite graphs in the cohomomorphism preorder.
This simple observation implies (as a special case of a general result,
Theorem A.2
) the following relation between the asymptotic spectra
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{X}_{\infty}
. We consider the restriction map
i^{*}:\mathcal{X}_{\infty}\to\mathcal{X}:F\mapsto F\circ i
, which simply restricts the domain of any element
F\in\mathcal{X}_{\infty}
to finite graphs.

Theorem 4.8
.

The restriction map
i^{*}:\mathcal{X}_{\infty}\to\mathcal{X}
is surjective.

Remark 4.9
.

In other words,
Theorem 4.8
says that any element
F\in\mathcal{X}
can be extended to an element in
\mathcal{X}_{\infty}
. So, for instance, the Lovász theta function (on finite graphs) can be extended to infinite graphs (perhaps in multiple ways) in such a way that it remains
\boxtimes
-multiplicative,
\sqcup
-additive,
\leq
-monotone and
E_{n}
-normalised.

Remark 4.10
.

As a consequence of
Theorem 4.8
,
\sup_{F\in\mathcal{X}_{\infty}}|F(G)-F(H)|
and
\sup_{F\in\mathcal{X}}|F(G)-F(H)|
coincide on any finite graphs
G,H
.

Theorem 4.8
will play an important role in several arguments that involve both infinite and finite graphs.

4.3
Circle graphs as limit points of fraction graphs

In
Section 3.4
we proved that if
p_{n}/q_{n}
converges to an irrational
r\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 2}
, then
E_{p_{n}/q_{n}}
is Cauchy.
We noted that no finite graph can be the limit point of these Cauchy sequences (
3.16
). We will prove here that the limit points are circle graphs.

Theorem 4.11
.

Let
r\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 2}
be irrational.

(a)

For every
F\in\mathcal{X}_{\infty}
,

F(E_{r}^{\mathrm{c}})=F(E_{r}^{\mathrm{o}})=\sup_{a/b<r}F(E_{a/b})=\inf_{r<c/d}F(E_{c/d}).

In particular,
E_{r}^{\mathrm{c}}
and
E_{r}^{\mathrm{o}}
are asymptotically equivalent.

(b)

If
p_{n}/q_{n}
converges to
r
, then
E_{p_{n}/q_{n}}
converges to
E^{\mathrm{o}}_{r}
.

Proof.

For any
a/b,c/d\in\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 2}
with
a/b<r<c/d
we have

E_{a/b}\leq E^{\mathrm{c}}_{r}\leq E^{\mathrm{o}}_{r}\leq E_{c/d}.

(2)

From
Theorem 3.14
we know that for every
F\in\mathcal{X}
,

\sup_{a/b<r}F(E_{a/b})=\inf_{r<c/d}F(E_{c/d})
.
Thus applying any
F\in\mathcal{X}_{\infty}
to
Equation 2
, and taking the supremum over
a/b<r
and the infimum over
r<c/d
, gives
F(E^{\mathrm{c}}_{r})=F(E^{\mathrm{o}}_{r})
. Thus
E^{\mathrm{c}}_{r}
and
E^{\mathrm{o}}_{r}
are asymptotically equivalent.
∎

4.4
Characterizing convergence to fraction graphs from below

In
Section 3.2
we proved that if
p_{n}/q_{n}
converges to
a/b
from above, then
E_{p_{n}/q_{n}}
converges to
E_{a/b}
. We left open whether the same is true if
p_{n}/q_{n}
converges to
a/b
from below. Here we will prove that this question is equivalent to the question whether the “closed” and “open” version of the circle graphs are asymptotically equivalent.

Theorem 4.12
.

Let
r=p/q\in\mathbb{Q}_{>2}
be rational. The following are equivalent:

(i)

E_{r}^{\mathrm{c}}
and
E_{r}^{\mathrm{o}}
are asymptotically equivalent

(ii)

There is a sequence
m_{n}=n+o(n)
such that for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
there is a rational number
a_{n}/b_{n}<p/q
such that
E_{p/q}^{\boxtimes n}\leq E_{a_{n}/b_{n}}^{\boxtimes m_{n}}

(iii)

For every
\varepsilon>0
, there is a
\delta>0
, such that for any
a/b\in\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 2}
, if

, then for every

F(E_{p/q})-F(E_{a/b})<\varepsilon

(iv)

If
a_{n}/b_{n}\to p/q
from below, then
E_{a_{n}/b_{n}}\to E_{p/q}

(v)

For every
F\in\mathcal{X}
,
\sup_{a/b<p/q}F(E_{a/b})=F(E_{p/q})

Proof.

(i)
\Rightarrow
(ii). Suppose

and

are asymptotically equivalent. Then there is a sequence
m_{n}=n+o(n)

Let

(applied to every factor) there are some
a_{n}/b_{n}<r
such that
E_{p/q}^{\boxtimes n}\leq E_{a_{n}/b_{n}}^{\boxtimes m_{n}}
.

(ii)
\Rightarrow
(iii).
Write

m_{n}=n(1+f(n))

.
Applying any

and taking

F(E_{a_{n}/b_{n}})\leq F(E_{p/q})\leq F(E_{a_{n}/b_{n}})^{1+f(n)}

Letting
L_{n}=F(E_{a_{n}/b_{n}})

\displaystyle|L_{n}-R_{n}|

\displaystyle\leq F(E_{a_{n}/b_{n}})^{1+f(n)}-F(E_{a_{n}/b_{n}})

\displaystyle=F(E_{a_{n}/b_{n}})\bigl(F(E_{a_{n}/b_{n}})^{f(n)}-1\bigr)

Let

. Let

.

(iii)
\Rightarrow

(iv)

(v)
\Rightarrow
(i).
Suppose that

. For any

5
Non-equivalence of open and closed circle graphs

In
Section 4
we proved that for irrational
r
the circle graphs
E_{r}^{\mathrm{o}}
and
E_{r}^{\mathrm{c}}
are asymptotically equivalent (
Theorem 4.11
). We also proved that for rational
r
the circle graphs
E_{r}^{\mathrm{o}}
and
E_{r}^{\mathrm{c}}
are asymptotically equivalent if and only if the fraction graphs are “left-continuous” at
r
(
Theorem 4.12
). Whether
E_{r}^{\mathrm{o}}
and
E_{r}^{\mathrm{c}}
are indeed asymptotically equivalent for rational
r
remains an open problem.

The above raises the natural question whether the open and closed circle graphs are equivalent. Equivalence, in particular, would imply asymptotic equivalence.
We will prove that the circle graphs
E_{r}^{\mathrm{o}}
and
E_{r}^{\mathrm{c}}
are not equivalent for any
r
(rational or irrational). Thus, any resolution of the asymptotic equivalence problem of rational open and closed circle graphs must be asymptotic in nature (or at least make use of powers of the graphs).

Our proof of non-equivalence relies on characterizing all cohomomorphisms
E_{r}^{\mathrm{c}}\to E_{r}^{\mathrm{c}}
and
E_{r}^{\mathrm{o}}\to E_{r}^{\mathrm{o}}
. We prove that these self-cohomomorphisms are precisely rotations composed with reflections. Before we develop these ideas for the circle graphs, we first prove the analogous statement for the (finite) fraction graphs
E_{p/q}
, which we will then use as a proof ingredient to deal with the circle graphs.

5.1
Self-cohomomorphisms of fraction graphs

We call any cohomomorphism
G\to G
a
self-cohomomorphism
.
We will prove that for the fraction graphs, these are precisely rotations composed with reflections. We begin by showing that the self-cohomomorphisms of fraction graphs, for reduced fractions, are isomorphisms. (In the language of “cores”
[
37
, Section 1.6]
, this says that the complement of a fraction graph, for reduced fractions, is a core.)

Lemma 5.1

(
[
10
, Lemma 2.5]
)
.

Let
p,q\in\mathbb{N},p/q\geq 2
such that
\gcd(p,q)=1
. Then any cohomomorphism from
E_{p/q}
to itself is an isomorphism.

Proof.

We give a brief proof for the convenience of the reader.
This is obvious when
p/q=2
. Suppose
p/q>2
. Then there are (unique) integers
1\leq p^{\prime}\leq p,1\leq q^{\prime}\leq q
with
p^{\prime}/q^{\prime}\geq 2
satisfying
pq^{\prime}-qp^{\prime}=1
. A cohomomorphism from
E_{p/q}
to a proper induced subgraph would induce a cohomomorphism from
E_{p/q}
to
E_{p^{\prime}/q^{\prime}}
by
3.5
which is precluded by comparing
\overline{\chi}_{f}
(
3.2
,
3.3
). Alternatively, the result can be proven using the No-Homomorphism Lemma; see
[
10
, Lemma 2.5]
.
∎

Having
5.1
, it remains to show that these isomorphisms are rotations composed with reflections.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
p/q\in\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 2}
,
q\geq 2
such that
\gcd(p,q)=1
. Let
f:\mathbb{Z}_{p}\to\mathbb{Z}_{p}
be a cohomomorphism from
E_{p/q}
to itself. Then
f
is an isomorphism of the form
f(x)=a-x
or
f(x)=a+x
for some
a\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}
.

Proof.

By
5.1
,
f
is an isomorphism.
The maximum cliques of
E_{p/q}
are of the form
C_{x}=\{x,x+1,\dots,x+(q-1)\}
for
x\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}
and thus there is a bijection
g:\mathbb{Z}_{p}\to\mathbb{Z}_{p}
satisfying
f(C_{x})=C_{g(x)}
for all
x
. The map
g
must be an automorphism of the auxiliary graph
H
on vertex set
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
connecting
x
and
y
if and only if the symmetric difference of
C_{x}
and
C_{y}
has size two. Observe that the fact that
q\geq 2
implies that the graph
H
is isomorphic to a
p
-cycle. It follows that
g(x+1)-g(x)\in\{\pm 1\}
for all
x
. Moreover, because
g
is a bijection,
g(x+1)-g(x)
must actually constantly be either
1
or
-1

for all

for all

∎

5.2
Self-cohomomorphisms of circle graphs

In this section we characterise the self-cohomomorphisms of irrational circle graphs. As an ingredient we use the following basic property about finite induced subgraphs of circle graphs.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
r\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 2}
. Every finite induced subgraph of either
E_{r}^{\mathrm{c}}
or
E_{r}^{\mathrm{o}}
is equivalent to a fraction graph.

Proof.

In
[
10
, Theorem 3.1]
it is shown that convex-round graphs are homomorphically equivalent to the complement of a fraction graph. A convex-round graph on
n
vertices is a graph with vertex set
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
, where the neighborhood of any vertex is an interval, i.e. of the form
\{u,u+1,\dots,u_{d_{v}-1}\}
. Every finite induced subgraph of either
E_{r}^{\mathrm{c}}
or
E_{r}^{\mathrm{o}}
is the complement of a convex-round graph and thus cohomomorphically equivalent to a fraction graph.
∎

When the finite set of vertices that we induce to is
equidistant
on the circle
C
(the vertex set of the circle graphs), we can say more. Namely, we can say precisely which fraction graph we are equivalent to, and the equivalence becomes an isomorphism. Moreover, we describe how the natural “rounding map” on a circle graph to finitely many equidistant points gives a cohomomorphism to a fraction graph.

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
r\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 2}
,
N\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 2}
, and let
S
be a set of
N
equidistant points on
C
.

(a)

The induced subgraphs
E_{r}^{\mathrm{o}}[S]
and
E_{r}^{\mathrm{c}}[S]
are isomorphic to
E_{N/\lceil N/r\rceil}
and
E_{N/(\lfloor N/r\rfloor+1)}
, respectively.

(b)

Identify
S
with
\mathbb{Z}_{N}
(in an order-preserving way) and define the map
f:C\to\mathbb{Z}_{N}
by rounding any
x\in C
to the clockwise next element of
S
. Then
f
is a cohomomorphism from both
E_{r}^{\mathrm{o}}
and
E_{r}^{\mathrm{c}}
to
E_{N/\lfloor N/r\rfloor}
.

Proof.

(
a
) Both
E_{r}^{\mathrm{o}}[S]
and
E_{r}^{\mathrm{c}}[S]
are vertex-transitive. Let
q_{\mathrm{o}}
be the unique positive integer for which
(q_{\mathrm{o}}-1)/N<1/r\leq q_{\mathrm{o}}/N
, that is,
q_{\mathrm{o}}=\lceil N/r\rceil
and let
q_{\mathrm{c}}
be the unique positive integer for which
(q_{\mathrm{c}}-1)/N\leq 1/r<q_{\mathrm{c}}/N
, that is,
q_{\mathrm{c}}=\lfloor N/r\rfloor+1
. We will prove that
E_{r}^{\mathrm{o}}[S]
is isomorphic to
E_{N/q_{\mathrm{o}}}
. The fact that
E^{\mathrm{c}}[S]
is isomorphic to
E_{N/q_{\mathrm{c}}}
can be proved completely analogously.

Any vertex in
E_{r}^{\mathrm{o}}[S]
is adjacent to its
q_{\mathrm{o}}-1
nearest clockwise
C
-neighbors and
q_{\mathrm{o}}-1
nearest anti-clockwise
C
-neighbors. If this describes all vertices in
S
then
E_{r}^{\mathrm{o}}[S]
is a complete graph on
N
vertices where
N\leq 2(q_{\mathrm{o}}-1)-1=2q_{\mathrm{o}}-1
. In this case
N/q_{\mathrm{o}}<2
and thus
E_{r}^{\mathrm{o}}[S]
is indeed isomorphic to
E_{N/q_{\mathrm{o}}}
because by definition
E_{p/q}
denotes a complete graph on
p
vertices whenever
p/q<2
. Otherwise we observe that in both the clockwise and anti-clockwise direction any vertex in
E_{r}^{\mathrm{o}}
is adjacent to its
q_{\mathrm{o}}-1
nearest
C
-neighbors but not to its
q_{\mathrm{o}}
nearest
C
-neighbor, that is,
E_{r}^{\mathrm{o}}[S]
is isomorphic to
E_{N/q_{\mathrm{o}}}
.

(
b
) Take distinct
x,y\in C
and suppose that
f(x)
is adjacent or equal to
f(y)
. We may assume that
f(y)=f(x)+m
for some
0\leq m<\lfloor N/r\rfloor
. Let
d_{x}
and

denote the distance from

and
y
to the nearest clockwise element of

respectively. If
f(x)=f(y)
we assume that
d_{x}\geq d_{y}
. The clockwise distance from

to

is

m/N+d_{x}-d_{y}<(m+1)/N\leq\lfloor N/r\rfloor/N\leq 1/r.

Therefore,
x
and

are adjacent in both
E_{r}^{\mathrm{o}}
and
E_{r}^{\mathrm{c}}
and thus

is indeed a cohomomorphism.
∎

For context, recall that for
p/q\in\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 2}
the open circle graph

and the fraction graph

are equivalent (
4.4
). We note that this is a special case of the above

. Indeed, let

(

and thus

to

.

Now we will work towards the characterization of self-cohomomorphisms of irrational circle graphs. We first show that these are continuous.

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
r\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 2}
be irrational and let

be a cohomomorphism from either
E_{r}^{\mathrm{o}}
or
E_{r}^{\mathrm{c}}
to itself. Then

is continuous.

Proof.

Let
(p_{n}/q_{n})_{n\geq 0}
be the sequence of convergents of the continued fraction representation of

and the fact that

is irrational, that for every

\lceil p_{2n}/r\rceil=\lfloor p_{2n}/r\rfloor+1=q_{2n}\quad\text{ and }\quad\lfloor p_{2n+1}/r\rfloor=q_{2n+1}.

We will show that
f
is continuous at an arbitrary
x\in C
. Let
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
and let
S
be the set of
p_{2n}
equidistant points on

containing

. Label the elements of

as
(x,s_{1},\dots,s_{p_{2n}-1})
ordered (say clockwise) around

. By
5.4

S
induces

in both

and
E_{r}^{\mathrm{c}}
. In fact, because

is irrational, there is some

such that if

(this also implies that the sequence

is still ordered around

It follows from

that

with

is at most

.

We can thus conclude that for all

and

and has distance at most

from

Theorem 5.6
.

Let
r\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 2}
irrational and let
f
be a cohomomorphism from either
E_{r}^{\mathrm{o}}
or
E_{r}^{\mathrm{c}}
to itself. Then
f
is a rotation possibly composed with a reflection.

Proof.

From
5.5
we know that
f
is continuous.
By identifying
C
with
\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
the map

can be lifted to a continuous map

under the canonical projection
\pi:\mathbb{R}\to C
, that is,
\pi\circ g=f\circ\pi
. The lift

is unique up to an additive integer. There is an integer

, called the degree of
f
, such that
g(x+1)=g(x)+d
for all

. (See, e.g.,
[

[

]

By possibly composing with a reflection we may assume that

is nonnegative.

Because

is a cohomomorphism we find that for every
(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}\times(1/r,1-1/r)
there is an integer

such that

. Because
\mathbb{R}\times(1/r,1-1/r)
is connected and

is continuous,

does not depend on

. Furthermore, the inclusion extends to the boundary, that is,

. It follows that the value of the following integral must be contained in the interval

for all

,

\int_{0}^{1}g(x+y)-g(x)dx=\int_{y}^{1}g(x)dx+\int_{0}^{y}g(x+1)dx-\int_{0}^{1}g(x)dx=d\cdot y,

using
g(x+1)=g(x)+d
.
It follows that

and

. Because

and integrates to exactly

we have that

for all

and thus for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
. It follows that the function
h(x)=g(x)-x
has period
1
and period

is irrational,

is constant and thus

. We conclude that

is a rotation.
∎

5.3
Non-equivalence of open and closed circle graphs

We will now prove the non-equivalence of open and closed circle graphs. We will first give the proof for the rational case, which is simpler, and then prove the irrational case using the self-cohomorphism characterization of
Theorem 5.6
.

Theorem 5.7
.

Let
p/q\in\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 2}
be rational. Then
E_{p/q}^{\mathrm{c}}
and
E_{p/q}^{\mathrm{o}}
are not equivalent.

Proof.

Suppose
E_{p/q}^{\mathrm{o}}\leq E_{p/q}^{\mathrm{c}}
. We know that
E_{p/q}
is equivalent to
E_{p/q}^{\mathrm{o}}
. Thus there is a cohomomorphism
f:E_{p/q}\to E_{p/q}^{\mathrm{c}}
. The image of
f
is a finite induced subgraph of
E_{p/q}^{\mathrm{c}}
. Then by
4.3
, there is an
a/b<p/q
such that
E_{p/q}\leq E_{a/b}
. This is a contradiction.
∎

Theorem 5.8
.

Let
r\in\mathbb{R}_{>2}
be irrational. Then
E_{r}^{\mathrm{c}}
and
E_{r}^{\mathrm{o}}
are not equivalent.

Proof.

Any supposed cohomomorphism
f
from
E_{r}^{\mathrm{o}}
to
E_{r}^{\mathrm{c}}
is also a cohomomorphism from
E_{r}^{\mathrm{o}}
to itself because the identity is a cohomomorphism from
E_{r}^{\mathrm{c}}
to
E_{r}^{\mathrm{o}}
. Therefore, by
Theorem 5.6
,

is a rotation possibly composed with a reflection. However, such a map is distance preserving and in particular sends pairs of vertices with distance exactly
1/r
to pairs with distance exactly
1/r
, which contradicts

being a cohomomorphism from
E_{r}^{\mathrm{o}}
to
E_{r}^{\mathrm{c}}
.
∎

6
Independent sets in products of fraction graphs

In the previous sections we developed—ultimately motivated by the Shannon capacity problem—a theory of convergence of graphs in the asymptotic spectrum distance.

In this section we return to the Shannon capacity problem, and use ideas from the previous sections (e.g., approximation by fraction graphs, and vertex removal,
3.5
) to obtain explicit independent sets and new bounds.

First, we will give a brief overview of the best-known lower bounds on the Shannon capacity of small odd-cycle graphs, and observe that a simple, high-level framework encompasses all of these bounds.
Then, in the context of this framework, we introduce a “nondeterministic rounding” technique, which we use to find a new lower bound on the Shannon capacity of the fifteen-cycle,
C_{15}
. Finally, we determine the independence number of products of fraction graphs, in several regimes.

6.1

We observe (and explicitly describe in

. We will now improve this bound by constructing a large independent set in the fourth power. For this we will introduce a new nondeterministic rounding technique, which we will apply to an orbit construction in a product of fraction graphs.

Theorem 6.2
.

\alpha(C_{15}^{\boxtimes 4})\geq 2842
.

Theorem 6.2
gives
\Theta(C_{15})\geq\alpha(C_{15}^{\boxtimes 4})^{1/4}\geq 7.30139
. The best-known upper bound is
\Theta(C_{15})\leq\vartheta(C_{15})=7.4171482
, where
\vartheta
is the Lovász theta function. We provide an explicit independent set in
Appendix B
.

The method we use to obtain
Theorem 6.2
is inspired by the method used by Polak and Schrijver
[
54
]
to give a lower bound for
\alpha(C_{7}^{\boxtimes 5})
. They considered the following independent set in the graph
E_{382/108}^{\boxtimes 5}

S=\{t\cdot(1,7,7^{2},7^{3},7^{4}):t\in\mathbb{Z}_{382}\}.

Because
382/108\approx 3.537>7/2
this does not immediately give an independent set in
C_{7}^{\boxtimes 5}
. However, roughly described, they use a rounded down image of
S
into
C_{7}^{\boxtimes 5}
as a starting point to optimize for an independent set of size
367
.

Our construction also starts with a similarly shaped independent set
S
, that is, the orbit of a single generator in a product of fraction graphs slightly above
C_{15}^{\boxtimes 4}
. Subsequently we use a different method of rounding down
S
that is based on the following heuristic. Whenever
p/q\leq 15/2
, the map
x\mapsto\lfloor 15x/p\rfloor
is a cohomomorphism from
E_{p/q}
to
E_{15/2}
. This is not the case when
p/q>15/2
but heuristically the map is still close to a cohomomorphism whenever
p/q
is close to
15/2
. Therefore we still use this map, however if a vertex is close to being mapped to a different element, that is, when
15x/p
is close to an integer, we add both choices to the image
S
. The following contains an exact description of our construction.

Proof of
Theorem 6.2
.

We describe the construction here and give the explicit independent set in
Appendix B
.
We start with the set

S=\{t\cdot(1,15,1073,1125):t\in\mathbb{Z}_{2873}\}.

This is an independent set in
E_{2873/383}\boxtimes E_{2873/382}\boxtimes E_{2873/381}\boxtimes E_{2873/381}
of size
2873
. For any
x\in\{0,1,\ldots,2872\}
we let
r(x)=15x/2873
. For any
\varepsilon\in[0,1/2)
we define the set
F_{\varepsilon}(x)\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{15}
by

F_{\varepsilon}(x)=\begin{cases}\{\lfloor r(x)\rfloor,\lfloor r(x)\rfloor+1\}&\text{if }r(x)-\lfloor r(x)\rfloor>1-\varepsilon\\
\{\lfloor r(x)\rfloor,\lfloor r(x)\rfloor-1\}&\text{if }r(x)-\lfloor r(x)\rfloor<\varepsilon\\
\{\lfloor r(x)\rfloor\}&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

For any tuple
\vec{\varepsilon}=(\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2},\varepsilon_{3},\varepsilon_{4})
, we define the set

T_{\vec{\varepsilon}}=\hskip-15.0pt\bigcup_{(v_{1},\ldots,v_{4})\in S}\hskip-15.0ptF_{\varepsilon_{1}}(v_{1})\times\cdots\times F_{\varepsilon_{4}}(v_{4}).

Then
T_{\vec{\varepsilon}}
is a subset of
V(C_{15}^{\boxtimes 4})
.
For small
\vec{\varepsilon}
the set
T_{\vec{\varepsilon}}
is sufficiently small for optimization software (in our case Gurobi
[
31
]
) to quickly calculate close to optimal lower bounds for
\alpha(C_{15}^{\boxtimes 4}[T_{\vec{\varepsilon}}])
.
For
\vec{\varepsilon}=(0.12,0.22,0.32,0.32)
, we have
|T_{\vec{\varepsilon}}|=13718
, and we find
\alpha(C_{15}^{\boxtimes 4}[T_{\vec{\varepsilon}}])\geq 2842
.
∎

Remark 6.3
.

The lower bound
\alpha(C_{7}^{\boxtimes 5})\geq 367
of
[
54
]
can similarly be obtained using nondeterministic rounding (with
\varepsilon=0.125
) applied to the orbit independent set
S=\{t\cdot(1,7,7^{2},7^{3},7^{4}):t\in\mathbb{Z}_{382}\}

6.3
Characterization of discontinuities of the independence number

In this subsection we determine the independence number of products of fraction graphs in certain regimes. We will do this by determining where (and by how much) this value “jumps” (i.e., the discontinuity points) when we vary the fraction graphs.

Nested floor bound

We start with a well-known upper bound on the independence number
\alpha
of the product of any two graphs. Recall that
\overline{\chi}_{f}
denotes the fractional clique covering number.
Hales
[
35
]
, using ideas of Rosenfeld
[
62
]
(see also
[
64
, Equation 67.101]
), proved for any two graphs
G,H
that

\alpha(G\boxtimes H)\leq\lfloor\overline{\chi}_{f}(G)\alpha(H)\rfloor.

(3)

As a direct consequence we have the following “nested floor bound” on the independence number of any product of fraction graphs.

Theorem 6.4
.

Let
p_{1}/q_{1},\ldots,p_{k}/q_{k}\in\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 2}
. Then

\alpha(E_{p_{1}/q_{1}}\boxtimes E_{p_{2}/q_{2}}\boxtimes\cdots\boxtimes E_{p_{k}/q_{k}})\leq\Bigl\lfloor\cdots\Bigl\lfloor\Bigl\lfloor\frac{p_{1}}{q_{1}}\Bigr\rfloor\frac{p_{2}}{q_{2}}\Bigr\rfloor\cdots\frac{p_{k}}{q_{k}}\Bigr\rfloor.

(4)

Proof.

This follows from
\overline{\chi}_{f}(E_{p/q})=p/q
and repeated application of
Equation 3
.
∎

We note that the left-hand side of
Equation 4
is invariant under permuting the numbers
p_{i}/q_{i}
, while the right-hand side is not. Thus when applying
Theorem 6.4
we may minimize over these permutations to get the best bound. Moreover, we note that the minimizing permutation is not obtained by simply sorting the
p_{i}/q_{i}
. For instance, note that
\lfloor\lfloor\lfloor 5/2\rfloor 2\rfloor 3\rfloor=\lfloor\lfloor\lfloor 5/2\rfloor 3\rfloor 2\rfloor=12
while all other permutations of
5/2
,
2
and
3
lead to a larger value.

Discontinuities for one or two fraction graphs

For a single factor, the bound of
Theorem 6.4
is easily seen to be optimal, that is,

\alpha(E_{p/q})=\Bigl\lfloor\frac{p}{q}\Bigr\rfloor.

Hales
[
35
, Theorem 7.1]
proved that the bound of
Theorem 6.4
is optimal for the product of any two odd cycles. Badalyan and Markosyan
[
9
]
proved that the bound of
Theorem 6.4
is optimal for the product of any two fraction graphs, that is:

Theorem 6.5
.

Let
p_{1}/q_{1},p_{2}/q_{2}\in\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 2}
. Then

\alpha(E_{p_{1}/q_{1}}\boxtimes E_{p_{2}/q_{2}})=\min\Bigl\{\Bigl\lfloor\Bigl\lfloor\frac{p_{1}}{q_{1}}\Bigr\rfloor\frac{p_{2}}{q_{2}}\Bigr\rfloor,\Bigl\lfloor\Bigl\lfloor\frac{p_{2}}{q_{2}}\Bigr\rfloor\frac{p_{1}}{q_{1}}\Bigr\rfloor\Bigr\}.

We give a short proof of
Theorem 6.5
. For this we use the following construction of an independent set in certain products of fraction graphs, which extends the well-known construction of an independent set of size five in
C_{5}^{\boxtimes 2}
.

Lemma 6.6
.

Let
p/q\in\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 2}
. Then

\alpha(E_{p/q}\boxtimes E_{p/\lfloor p/q\rfloor})=p
.

Proof.

The upper bound follows from
Theorem 6.4
. For the lower bound, define the set
S=\{t\cdot(1,\lfloor p/q\rfloor):t\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}\}
. One verifies that
S
is an independent set in
E_{p/q}\boxtimes E_{p/\lfloor p/q\rfloor}
.
∎

Proof of
Theorem 6.5
.

Let
n_{i}=\lfloor p_{i}/q_{i}\rfloor
. We can write
p_{1}/q_{1}=n_{1}+(m_{1}+\ell_{1})/n_{2}
and
p_{2}/q_{2}=n_{2}+(m_{2}+\ell_{2})/n_{1}
for some
m_{i}\in\mathbb{N}
and
\ell_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
such that
0\leq m_{1}<n_{2}
,
0\leq m_{2}<n_{1}
and
0\leq\ell_{i}<1
. Then

\Bigl\lfloor\Bigl\lfloor\frac{p_{1}}{q_{1}}\Bigr\rfloor\frac{p_{2}}{q_{2}}\Bigr\rfloor=n_{1}n_{2}+m_{2}

and

\Bigl\lfloor\Bigl\lfloor\frac{p_{2}}{q_{2}}\Bigr\rfloor\frac{p_{1}}{q_{1}}\Bigr\rfloor=n_{1}n_{2}+m_{1}.

We may assume that
m_{1}\leq m_{2}
. Apply
6.6
with
p=n_{1}n_{2}+m_{1}
and
q=n_{2}
to get

\alpha(E_{(n_{1}n_{2}+m_{1})/n_{2}}\boxtimes E_{(n_{1}n_{2}+m_{1})/n_{1}})=n_{1}n_{2}+m_{1}.

Because
(n_{1}n_{2}+m_{1})/n_{2}\leq p_{1}/q_{1}
and
(n_{1}n_{2}+m_{1})/n_{1}\leq p_{2}/q_{2}
we find that
\alpha(E_{p_{1}/q_{1}}\boxtimes E_{p_{2}/q_{2}})\geq\alpha(E_{(n_{1}n_{2}+m_{1})/n_{2}}\boxtimes E_{(n_{1}n_{2}+m_{1})/n_{1}})=n_{1}n_{2}+m_{1}
.
∎

Discontinuities for three (or more) fraction graphs

Theorem 6.5
says that
Theorem 6.4
is tight for two factors. For three factors, however, the bound in
Theorem 6.4
is no longer always optimal and the independence number
\alpha(E_{p_{1}/q_{1}}\boxtimes E_{p_{2}/q_{2}}\boxtimes E_{p_{3}/q_{3}})
shows more intricate behaviour. We fully determine this behaviour for all triples of fractions in the interval
[2,3]
. Since the function
\alpha(E_{p_{1}/q_{1}}\boxtimes E_{p_{2}/q_{2}}\boxtimes E_{p_{3}/q_{3}})
is monotone in each
p_{i}/q_{i}
and integral-valued, it has only finitely many discontinuities, and to determine the function it suffices to determine the value at every discontinuity.

To discuss the discontinuities of the independence number of products of fraction graphs, we define for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
the function

\alpha_{k}:\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 2}^{k}\to\mathbb{N}:(p_{1}/q_{1},\ldots,p_{k}/q_{k})\mapsto\alpha(E_{p_{1}/q_{1}}\boxtimes\cdots\boxtimes E_{p_{k}/q_{k}}).

For
u,v\in\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 2}^{k}
we write
u\leq v
if there is a permutation of
u
that is pointwise at most
v
. Then
u\leq v
implies
\alpha_{k}(u)\leq\alpha_{k}(v)
. We write
u<v
if
u\leq v
and
u\neq v
.
We call
v\in\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 2}^{k}
an (extremal) discontinuity of
\alpha_{k}
if for every
u\in\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 2}^{k}
if
u<v
then
\alpha_{k}(u)<\alpha_{k}(v)
.

Lemma 6.7
.

For every
n,k\in\mathbb{N}
,
(n,p_{1}/q_{1},\ldots,p_{k}/q_{k})
is a discontinuity of
\alpha_{k+1}
if and only if
(p_{1}/q_{1},\ldots,p_{k}/q_{k})
is a discontinuity of
\alpha_{k}
.

Proof.

We use the fact that the independence number is additive under disjoint union, so that
\alpha(E_{n}\boxtimes G)=n\alpha(G)
for any graph
G
and any
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Suppose
(n,p_{1}/q_{1},\ldots,p_{k}/q_{k})
is a discontinuity. Let
r/s<p_{1}/q_{1}
, say. Then

\displaystyle\alpha(E_{r/s}\boxtimes E_{p_{2}/q_{2}}\boxtimes\cdots)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{n}\alpha(E_{n}\boxtimes E_{r/s}\boxtimes E_{p_{2}/q_{2}}\boxtimes\cdots)

\displaystyle<\tfrac{1}{n}\alpha(E_{n}\boxtimes E_{p_{1}/q_{1}}\boxtimes E_{p_{2}/q_{2}}\boxtimes\cdots)

\displaystyle=\alpha(E_{p_{1}/q_{1}}\boxtimes E_{p_{2}/q_{2}}\boxtimes\cdots),

so
(p_{1}/q_{1},\ldots,p_{k}/q_{k})
is a discontinuity.

Suppose
(p_{1}/q_{1},\ldots,p_{k}/q_{k})
is a discontinuity. Suppose
r/s<p_{1}/q_{1}
, say. Then

\displaystyle\alpha(E_{n}\boxtimes E_{r/s}\boxtimes E_{p_{2}/q_{2}}\boxtimes\cdots)

\displaystyle=n\alpha(E_{r/s}\boxtimes E_{p_{2}/q_{2}}\boxtimes\cdots)

\displaystyle<n\alpha(E_{p_{1}/q_{1}}\boxtimes E_{p_{2}/q_{2}}\boxtimes\cdots)

\displaystyle=\alpha(E_{n}\boxtimes E_{p_{1}/q_{1}}\boxtimes E_{p_{2}/q_{2}}\boxtimes\cdots).

Suppose
r/s<n
. Then
Theorem 6.4
gives
\alpha(E_{r/s}\boxtimes E_{p_{1}/q_{1}}\boxtimes\cdots)<\alpha(E_{n}\boxtimes E_{p_{1}/q_{1}}\boxtimes\cdots)
. We conclude that
(n,p_{1}/q_{1},\ldots,p_{k}/q_{k})
is a discontinuity. ∎

Remark 6.12
.

We have seen in the proof of

that all discontinuity points of

\alpha_{3}(8/3,8/3,8/3)=12
cannot be realized by a subgroup in a very general sense.

Let
H_{1},H_{2},H_{3}
be finite groups with symmetric subsets
S_{i}
and let
G_{i}=\text{Cayley}(H_{i},S_{i})
be the corresponding Cayley graphs.
Suppose that
G_{i}\leq E_{8/3}
and suppose that there is a subgroup
I\subseteq H_{1}\times H_{2}\times H_{3}
of size
12
that is an independent set in
G_{1}\boxtimes G_{2}\boxtimes G_{3}
. We will show that this gives a contradiction.
Consider the auxiliary graphs
L_{i}
with vertex set
I
where
x\not\sim y
if and only if
x_{i}\not\sim y_{i}
in
G_{i}
. Now
L_{i}\leq G_{i}\leq E_{8/3}
and thus
\overline{\chi}_{f}(L_{i})\leq 8/3
. Moreover,
L_{i}
is vertex-transitive so
\overline{\chi}_{f}(L_{i})=|L_{i}|/\omega(L_{i})=12/\omega(L_{i})
, where
\omega
denotes the clique number. Because
\omega(L_{i})
is an integer we can conclude that
\overline{\chi}_{f}(L_{i})\leq 12/5
. Observe that

is an independent set in
L_{1}\boxtimes L_{2}\boxtimes L_{3}
implying
\alpha(L_{1}\boxtimes L_{2}\boxtimes L_{3})\geq 12
. However, using the nested floor bound (
Theorem 6.4
), we observe that

\alpha(L_{1}\boxtimes L_{2}\boxtimes L_{3})\leq\left\lfloor\overline{\chi}_{f}(L_{1})\left\lfloor\overline{\chi}_{f}(L_{2})\left\lfloor\overline{\chi}_{f}(L_{3})\right\rfloor\right\rfloor\right\rfloor\leq\Bigl\lfloor\frac{12}{5}\Bigl\lfloor\frac{12}{5}\Bigl\lfloor\frac{12}{5}\Bigr\rfloor\Bigr\rfloor\Bigr\rfloor=9,

which gives a contradiction.

The proof of
Theorem 6.9
consists of a computer-assisted component together with several reductions to the problem, for which we use
6.7
and the following lemma. (We note that all the ideas we present here work also outside the interval
[2,3]
. However, the computer-assisted component becomes more costly.)

Lemma 6.13
.

If
(p_{1}/q_{1},\ldots,p_{k}/q_{k})
is a discontinuity of
\alpha_{k}
, then

\max\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{k}\}\leq\alpha_{k}(p_{1}/q_{1},\ldots,p_{k}/q_{k}).

Proof.

Let
m=\alpha_{k}(p_{1}/q_{1},\ldots,p_{k}/q_{k})
. Suppose
\max_{i}p_{i}>m
. Let
S
be any independent set of
E_{p_{1}/q_{1}}\boxtimes\cdots\boxtimes E_{p_{k}/q_{k}}
of size
m
. Then there is an
i\in[k]
and a vertex
v
of
E_{p_{i}/q_{i}}
such that for every
s\in S
,
s_{i}\neq v
. Then
S\subseteq E_{p_{1}/q_{1}}\boxtimes\cdots\boxtimes(E_{p_{i}/q_{i}}-\{v\})\boxtimes\cdots\boxtimes E_{p_{k}/q_{k}}
. We know that
E_{p_{i}/q_{i}}-\{v\}
is equivalent to a fraction graph
E_{a/b}
for some
a/b\in\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 2}
that is strictly smaller than
p_{i}/q_{i}
(
3.5
). Then
\alpha_{k}(p_{1}/q_{1},\ldots,a/b,\ldots,p_{k}/q_{k})=m
, so
(p_{1}/q_{1},\ldots,p_{k}/q_{k})
is not a discontinuity.
∎

Proof of
Theorem 6.9

7
Discussion and open problems

Motivated by the Shannon capacity problem, and with the approach in mind of determining the Shannon capacity of “hard” graphs (e.g., the prototypical odd cycles) by approximating them with “easier” graphs (e.g., very structured graphs), we have developed the theory of the asymptotic spectrum distance on graphs and the corresponding notion of convergence. In particular, in
Section 3
we have developed several methods for understanding convergence of fraction graphs, including effective distance bounds and topological incompleteness results.

A central problem that we leave open regarding convergence is left-continuity on fraction graphs:

Problem 7.1
.

Does
p_{n}/q_{n}\to a/b
from below, imply that
E_{p_{n}/q_{n}}\to E_{a/b}
?

In
Theorem 4.12
we proved that
7.1
is equivalent to determining whether the (infinite) open and closed circle graphs
E^{\mathrm{o}}_{a/b}
and
E^{\mathrm{c}}_{a/b}
are asymptotically equivalent.

Generally, we feel that the study of natural infinite graphs like the circle graphs of
Section 4
will shed new light on the theory of Shannon capacity. This angle and the use of asymptotic spectrum distance in general hint at the further potential of analytic methods for this otherwise combinatorial and algebraic problem.
A central open problem regarding infinite graphs is about their completeness:

Problem 7.2
.

Are the infinite graphs
\mathcal{G}_{\infty}
complete?
19
19

19

Recall (
Section 4.2
) that we defined
\mathcal{G}_{\infty}
as the set of infinite graphs with cardinality at most the cardinality of the reals and with finite clique cover number.

Another open problem is about density of the finite graphs in the infinite graphs:

Problem 7.3
.

Are the finite graphs
\mathcal{G}
dense in the infinite graphs
\mathcal{G}_{\infty}
?

In fact, the following is also open:

Problem 7.4
.

Is there, for every finite graph
G
, a non-trivial sequence of finite graphs
H_{i}
converging to it,
H_{i}\to G
?

Specifically,
7.4
is open for graphs that are not of the form
G=E_{p/q}\boxtimes G^{\prime}
with
p/q\in\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 2}
, since those are covered by
Theorem 3.7
.

In
Section 6
we took a computational approach and used some of the developed methods to determine precisely several relevant independence numbers. In particular, we obtained a new lower bound on the Shannon capacity of
C_{15}
, using what could be considered a “finite” version of the converging sequence approach (following Polak–Schrijver
[
54
]
), by using a fraction graph that sits
“just above” it. We expect the development and further integration of these ideas to lead to deeper, structural understanding of the Shannon capacity.

Appendix A
General theory of asymptotic spectrum distance

In this section we discuss the general theory of asymptotic spectrum distance on preordered semirings (with the appropriate conditions), which has the theory for graphs and for infinite graphs (as discussed in
Section 2
) as special cases. The goal of this section is to put our ideas in a broader perspective and to clarify which parts of the theory discussed in the previous sections are general. We begin by reviewing the theory of asymptotic spectrum duality, as can be found in
[
69
,
79
,
80
,
73
,
29
]
(this is closely related to the real representation theorem of Kadison and Dubois
[
13
,
58
,
50
,
57
]
), and some examples. Then we discuss the general notion of asymptotic spectrum distance and basic properties.

Semiring and order

Let
\mathcal{R}
be a commutative semiring with additive unit 0 and multiplicative unit 1. For every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
n\in\mathcal{R}
denote the sum of
n
copies of the element 1. Let
\leq
be a partial order on
\mathcal{R}
that satisfies the following properties:

(i)

For every
a,b,c\in\mathcal{R}
, if
a\leq b
, then
a+c\leq b+c
and
ac\leq bc
.

(ii)

For every
n,m\in\mathbb{N}
we have
n\leq m
in
\mathbb{N}
if and only if
n\leq m
in
\mathcal{R}
.

(iii)

For every nonzero element
a\in\mathcal{R}
there is an element
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
1\leq a\leq n
.

Let
\lesssim
be the preorder defined on
\mathcal{R}
by
a\lesssim b
if and only if
a^{n}\leq b^{n}2^{o(n)}
. (In all the examples that we consider we will have that for every
a,b\in\mathcal{R}
,
a^{n}\leq b^{n}2^{o(n)}
if and only if
a^{n}\leq b^{n+o(n)}
.)
We may without loss of generality assume that
\lesssim
is a partial order, by identifying any two elements
a,b\in\mathcal{R}
for which
a\lesssim b
and
b\lesssim a
.
For every
a\in\mathcal{R}
, let
\rank(a)=\min\{n\in\mathbb{N}:a\leq n\}
and
\subrank(a)=\max\{n\in\mathbb{N}:n\leq a\}
. These functions are called rank and subrank, respectively.
Let
\asymprank(a)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\rank(a^{n})^{1/n}=\inf_{n}\rank(a^{n})^{1/n}
and
\asympsubrank(a)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\subrank(a^{n})^{1/n}=\sup_{n}\subrank(a^{n})^{1/n}
. These functions are called asymptotic rank and asymptotic subrank, respectively.

Asymptotic spectrum duality

Let
\mathcal{X}
be the set of
\leq
-monotone semiring-homomorphisms
\mathcal{R}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
. We call
\mathcal{X}
the asymptotic spectrum. For every
a\in\mathcal{R}
, let
\widehat{a}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}:F\mapsto F(a)
. We equip
\mathcal{X}
with the coarsest topology such that for every
a\in\mathcal{R}
the map
\widehat{a}
is continuous.

We call an element
a\in\mathcal{R}

gapped
if there is an element
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
a^{k}\geq 2
or there is an element
\phi\in\mathcal{X}
such that
\phi(a)\leq 1
.

Theorem A.1

(e.g.,
[
73
, Section 3]
)
.

(i)

\mathcal{X}
is a non-empty compact Hausdorff space.

(ii)

Let
a,b\in\mathcal{R}
. Then
a\lesssim b
if and only if for every
F\in\mathcal{X}
,
F(a)\leq F(b)
.

(iii)

Let
a\in\mathcal{R}
. Then
\asymprank(a)=\max_{F\in\mathcal{X}}F(a)
.

(iv)

Let
a\in\mathcal{R}
be gapped. Then
\asympsubrank(a)=\min_{F\in\mathcal{X}}F(a)
.

Theorem A.2

(e.g.,
[
73
, Section 4.3]
)
.

Let
(\mathcal{R}_{1},\leq_{1})
and
(\mathcal{R}_{2},\leq_{2})
be a pair of semirings and preorders as above. Let
i:\mathcal{R}_{1}\to\mathcal{R}_{2}
be a semiring homomorphism such that for every
a,b\in\mathcal{R}_{1}
we have
a\leq_{1}b
if and only if
i(a)\leq_{2}i(b)
. Let
i^{*}:\mathcal{X}_{2}\to\mathcal{X}_{1}:F\to F\circ i
. Then
i^{*}
is surjective.

We discuss some examples that fit in the above framework. We refer to
[

]
for a more detailed discussion of examples.

Finite graphs.
This is the setting of
Section 2
. Let
\mathcal{R}
be the set of graphs with addition given by disjoint union, multiplication by strong product, the graph with no vertices as 0 and the graph with one vertex as
1

\leq
be the cohomomorphism preorder on
\mathcal{R}
defined by
G\leq H
if there is a cohomomorphism
G\to H

\mathcal{R}
is a commutative semiring, and
\mathcal{R}

we discussed known elements of
\mathcal{X}

Infinite graphs.
This is the setting of

\mathcal{R}
be the set of infinite graphs with finite clique covering number. (Technically, for this to be a set we restrict ourselves to infinite graphs with bounded cardinality. \mathcal{R}
with the same operations and preorder as above, and identify graphs that are equivalent under cohomomorphism. Then
\mathcal{R}
is a commutative semiring, and
\mathcal{R}

can be extended to an element of

and

satisfy the properties outlined above. A known family of elements in the asymptotic spectrum are the quantum functionals

Asymptotic spectrum distance

Leading up to the introduction of the asymptotic spectrum distance, a first natural step is to rephrase
Theorem A.1
in terms of the elements
\widehat{a}
for
a\in\mathcal{R}
. By construction of the topology on
\mathcal{X}
each
\widehat{a}
is a continuous map
\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
.
Let
C(\mathcal{X})
denote the ring of all continuous functions
\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
under pointwise addition and multiplication, and for
\phi,\psi\in C(\mathcal{X})
write
\phi\leq\psi
if
\phi
is at most
\psi
pointwise on
\mathcal{X}
. Let
\max\phi
denote the maximum of
\phi
on
\mathcal{X}
, and let
\min\phi
denote the minimum of
\phi
on
\mathcal{X}
. The minimum and maximum are indeed attained since
\mathcal{X}
is compact.

Theorem A.1
(ii), (iii) and (iv) can be rephrased as saying that

a\lesssim b
if and only if
\widehat{a}\leq\widehat{b}
;

\asymprank(a)=\max\,\widehat{a}
; and

\asympsubrank(a)=\min\,\widehat{a}
.

We equip
C(\mathcal{X})
with the supremum-norm
||\phi||=\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}|\phi(x)|
for
\phi\in C(\mathcal{X})
and the corresponding distance function

for
\phi,\psi\in C(\mathcal{X})
. Then

is complete

.
The map
\Phi:\mathcal{R}\to C(\mathcal{X}):a\mapsto\widehat{a}
induces a distance on

as follows.

Definition A.3
.

For every

, we define

.
We call

.

It follows directly from the definitions that

Identifying in

From the asymptotic spectrum duality (

Let

(ii)

For every

(iii)

ma\lesssim mb+n

(iv)

(iii)

(iii)
\Leftrightarrow

Convergence

We have the standard notions of Cauchy and convergence in
\mathcal{R}
:
A sequence
a_{1},a_{2},\ldots
in
\mathcal{R}
is Cauchy if for every
n/m>0

(n,m\in\mathbb{N})
there is an
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that for every
i,j>N
, we have
d(a_{i},a_{j})\leq n/m
.
The sequence
a_{1},a_{2},\ldots
in
\mathcal{R}
converges to
b\in\mathcal{R}
if for every
n/m>0

(n,m\in\mathbb{N})
there is an
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that for every
i>N
, we have
d(a_{i},b)\leq n/m
.

Lemma A.5
.

If the sequence
a_{1},a_{2},\ldots
in
\mathcal{R}
converges to
b\in\mathcal{R}
, then
\asympsubrank(a_{i})
converges to
\asympsubrank(b)
, and
\asymprank(a_{i})
converges to
\asymprank(b)
.

Proof.

This follows from the general fact that if a sequence of functions
f_{1},f_{2},\ldots
on a compact space converges uniformly to
f
, then the sequence
\min f_{1},\min f_{2},\ldots
converges to
\min f
, and the same for the maximum. See also the proof of
2.7
which directly generalizes to this setting.
∎

Lemma A.6
.

Let
a_{1},a_{2},\ldots
be a sequence in
\mathcal{R}
and let
b\in\mathcal{R}
. If for every
F\in\mathcal{X}
we have that
F(a_{1}),F(a_{2}),\ldots
is monotone (i.e., non-increasing for every
F\in\mathcal{X}
or non-decreasing for every
F\in\mathcal{X}
) and converges to
F(b)
, then
a_{1},a_{2},\ldots
converges to
b
.

Proof.

This follows from Dini’s theorem
[
63
, Theorem 7.13]
, which says that for any sequence of continuous functions
f_{1},f_{2},\ldots
on a compact space
\mathcal{X}
, if the sequence
f_{1},f_{2},\ldots
converges pointwise to a continuous function, and for every
x\in\mathcal{X}
we have that
i\mapsto f_{i}(x)
is monotone in
i
, then the sequence
f_{1},f_{2},\ldots
converges uniformly to
f
.
Apply this theorem to the sequence of continuous functions
\widehat{a}_{1},\widehat{a}_{2},\ldots
to get the claim. ∎
