Existence of density for the solution of stochastic delay differential equations with reflection driven by a fractional Brownian motion Thanks: D. Márquez-Carreras and C. Rovira are supported by the grant PID2021-123733NB-I00 from SEIDI, Ministerio de Economia y Competividad.
M. Besalú, D. Márquez-Carreras, C. Rovira

Abstract
. In this note we prove the existence of a density for the law of the solution for 1-dimensional stochastic delay differential equations with normal reflection. The equations are driven by a fractional Brownian motion with Hurst parameter H > 1 / 2 H>1/2 . The stochastic integral with respect to the fractional Brownian motion is a pathwise Riemann-Stieltjes integral.



1.
Introduction

There are some models affected by some type of noise where the dynamics are related to propagation delay and some of them are naturally non-negative quantities. For instance, applications as rated and prices in internet model, concentrations of ions or proportions of a population that are infected (see the references in
[
13
]
). It is then natural to consider stochastic differential equations with delay and non-negativity constraints. In our paper
[
1
]
, we initiate their study when the noise is a fractional Brownian motion, obtaining the existence and uniqueness of solution.

More precisely, consider a stochastic delay differential equation with positivity constraints of the form:

\displaystyle X_{t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\eta_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}b(X_{s})ds+\int_{0}^{t}\sigma(X_{s-r})dW_{s}^{H}+Y_{t},\quad t\in(0,T_{0}],

\displaystyle X_{t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\eta_{t},\qquad t\in[-r,0].

(1.1)

Here
r
denotes a strictly positive time delay;
W^{H}
is a fractional Brownian motion with Hurst parameter
H>\frac{1}{2}
defined in a complete probability space
(\Omega,{\mathcal{F}},{\mathbb{P}})
;
\eta:[-r,0]\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
is a non negative smooth function, with
{\mathbb{R}}_{+}=[0,+\infty)
and
Y
is a vector-valued non-decreasing process which ensures that the non-negativity constraints on X are enforced.

Set

Z_{t}=\eta_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}b(X_{s})ds+\int_{0}^{t}\sigma(X_{s-r})dW_{s}^{H},\qquad t\in[0,T_{0}].

(1.2)

It is known that we have an explicit formula for the regulator term
Y
in terms of
Z
:

Y_{t}=\max_{s\in[0,t]}\left(Z_{s}\right)^{-},\qquad t\in[0,T_{0}],

where
x^{-}=\max(0,-x)
. Then, using the called Skorohod’s mapping, the solution of (
1.1
) satisfies

X_{t}=\qquad\begin{cases}Z_{t}+Y_{t},\qquad t\in[0,T_{0}],\\
\eta_{t},\qquad\quad t\in[-r,0].\end{cases}

As
H>\frac{1}{2}
, the integral with respect to
W^{H}
can be defined as a pathwise Riemann-Stieltjes integral using the results given by Young
[
24
]
. Moreover, Zälhe
[
25
]
introduced a
generalized Stieltjes integral using the techniques of fractional calculus. In particular, she
obtained a formula for the Riemann-Stieltjes integral using fractional derivatives (see (2.2)
below). Using this formula, Nualart and Răşcanu
[
19
]
proved a general result on existence,
uniqueness and finite moments of the solution to a class of general differential equations. In
[
1
]
we extended these results to equation (
1.1
).

Nualart and Sausserau
[
21
]
obtained the Malliavin differentiability and the existence of density for the solutions to equations considered in
[
19
]
. Our aim is to extend these results to our equations and to study the existence of density of the solution to (
1
).

As in all the papers following the methods presented in
[
19
]
, we will obtain first deterministic results and then we will apply pathwise to the stochastic equations.

Our main result states as follow:

Theorem 1.1
.

Assume
b,\sigma\in{\mathcal{C}}^{2}_{b}
and
\eta\in{\mathcal{C}}^{1-\alpha}(-r,0)
. Set
X
the solution of (
1
) and assume that
\sigma>0
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{+}
. Then, for any

t_{0}\in(0,T_{0}]
, the law of
X_{t_{0}}
restricted on
(0,+\infty)
is absolutely continuous.

Notice that we obtain the regularity restricted on
(0,+\infty)
since we are not able to prove that
P(X_{t_{0}}=0)=0
. It is the same situation that appears in the study of SPDEs
[
6
]
.
In our paper, the main difficult of the problem lies in the lack of properties of the Skorohod map with respect to the topologies used working with fractional Brownian motion.

Stochastic systems with delay have been studied deeply (see, as a basic reference,
[
17
]
), but the literature about stochastic differential equations with delay driven by a fractional Brownian
motion is scarce. There are references dealing with existence and uniqueness of solution
[
7
,
16
,
18
,
23
]
,
existence and regularity of the density
[
16
]
and convergence when the delay goes to zero
[
8
]
.
There has been little work on stochastic differential equations with delay and non-negativity constraints driven by standard Brownian motion.
We can only refer the reader to
[
12
]
, dedicated to study numerical methods, and
[
13
]

where the authors obtain sufficient conditions for existence and uniqueness of stationary solutions.
The study of equations with fractional Brownian motion and non negative constraints is also weak. The existence of solution is given in
[
9
]
and the existence and uniqueness in
[
10
]
. In
[
4
]
the authors deal with existence and uniqueness using rough path techniques.

On the other hand, the study of the regularity of the solution to stochastic differential equations with reflection is also scarce. We can refer the reader to the initial papers dealing with diffusions with reflection
[
14
]
and
[
15
]
and the work dedicated to stochastic partial differential equations
[
6
]
and
[
20
]
.

The structure of the paper is as follows. In the next Section, we give some preliminaries about the Skorohod problem and fractional calculus. In Sections
3
,
4
,
5
and
6
we deal with the deterministic case. Section 3 is devoted to get some comparison, existence and uniqueness theorems for deterministic equations. In Section
4
we obtain the convergence results to solve the deterministic Skorohod problem. The Fréchet differentiability of our deterministic equations is considered in Section
5
and in Section
6
we put together the deterministic results of the previous sections to obtain the approximations that we will consider pathwise in our stochastic case. Finally, Section
7
is devoted to apply all the deterministic results to the stochastic case proving our main theorem.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Skorohod Problem

Let

{\mathcal{C}}_{+}({\mathbb{R}}_{+},{\mathbb{R}}):=\left\{x\in{\mathcal{C}}({\mathbb{R}}_{+},{\mathbb{R}}):x(0)\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\right\}.

We can recall now the Skorokhod problem.

Definition 2.1
.

Given a path
z\in{\mathcal{C}}_{+}({\mathbb{R}}_{+},{\mathbb{R}})
, we say that a pair
(x,y)
of functions in
{\mathcal{C}}_{+}({\mathbb{R}}_{+},{\mathbb{R}})
solves the Skorokhod problem for
z
with reflection if

(1)

x_{t}=z_{t}+y_{t}
for all
t\geq 0
and
x_{t}\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
for each
t\geq 0
,

(2)

y(0)=0
and
y
is non-decreasing,

(3)

{\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}x_{s}dy_{s}=0}
for all
t\geq 0
, so
y
can increase only when
x
is at zero.

It is known that we have an explicit formula for
y
in terms of
z
:

y_{t}=\max_{s\in[0,t]}\left(z_{s}\right)^{-}.

The path
z
is called the reflector of
x
and the path
y
is called the regulator of
x
. We use the Skorokhod mapping for constraining a continuous real-valued function to be non-negative by means of reflection at the origin. We will apply it to each path of
z
defined by (
1.2
).

2.2.
Fractional calculus

Let
\alpha\in\left(0,\frac{1}{2}\right)
and
(a,b)\subseteq[-r,T]
. We denote by
W_{1}^{\alpha}(a,b)
the space of measurable functions
f:[a,b]\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
such that

\left\|f\right\|_{\alpha,1(a,b)}:=\sup_{u\in[a,b]}\Delta_{t}^{\alpha}(f)=\sup_{u\in[a,b]}\left(\left|f(u)\right|+\int_{a}^{u}\frac{\left|f(u)-f(v)\right|}{(u-v)^{\alpha+1}}dv\right)<\infty.

We also denote by
W_{2}^{1-\alpha}(0,T)
the space of measurable functions
g:[0,T]\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
such that

\left\|g\right\|_{1-\alpha,2}:=\sup_{0\leq u<v\leq T}\left(\frac{\left|g(v)-g(u)\right|}{|v-u|^{1-\alpha}}+\int_{u}^{v}\frac{\left|g(y)-g(u)\right|}{(y-u)^{2-\alpha}}dy\right)<\infty.

Finally, we define the supremum norm for functions
f:[a,b]\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
as

\|f\|_{\infty(a,b)}=\sup_{u\in[a,b]}|f(u)|,

the space of
\lambda
-Hölder continuous functions
f:[a,b]\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
denoted by
{\mathcal{C}}^{\lambda}(a,b)
for any
0<\lambda\leq 1
with the norm

\left\|f\right\|_{\lambda(a,b)}:=\left\|f\right\|_{\infty(a,b)}+\sup_{a\leq u<v\leq b}\frac{\left|f(v)-f(u)\right|}{(v-u)^{\lambda}}<\infty,

and for any integer
k\geq 1
we denote by
{\mathcal{C}}^{k}_{b}
the class of real-valued functions on
{\mathbb{R}}
which are
k
times continuously differentiable with bounded partial derivatives up to the
k
-th order.

Clearly, for any
\varepsilon>0
,

C^{1-\alpha+\varepsilon}(0,T)\subset W^{1-\alpha}_{2}(0,T)\subset C^{1-\alpha}(0,T).

(2.1)

Moreover, as
\alpha\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
,

C^{1-\alpha}(0,T)\subset W^{\alpha}_{1}(0,T).

If
f\in C^{\lambda}(a,b)
and
g\in C^{\mu}(a,b)
with
\lambda+\mu>1
, it is proved in
[
25
]
that the Riemman-Stieltjes integral
\int_{a}^{b}fdg
exists and it can be expressed as

\int_{a}^{b}fdg=(-1)^{\alpha}\int_{a}^{b}D_{a+}^{\alpha}f(t)D_{b-}^{1-\alpha}g_{b-}(t)dt,

(2.2)

where
g_{b-}(t)=g(t)-g(b),

1-\mu<\alpha<\lambda
, and the fractional derivatives are defined as

\displaystyle D_{a+}^{\alpha}f(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\left(\frac{f(t)}{(t-a)^{\alpha}}+\alpha\int_{a}^{t}\frac{f(t)-f(s)}{(t-s)^{\alpha+1}}ds\right),

\displaystyle D_{b-}^{\alpha}f(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{(-1)^{\alpha}}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\left(\frac{f(t)}{(b-t)^{\alpha}}+\alpha\int_{t}^{b}\frac{f(t)-f(s)}{(s-t)^{\alpha+1}}ds\right).

We refer to
[
19
]
and
[
25
]
and the references therein for a detailed account about this generalized integral and the fractional calculus.

Let
\Omega=C_{0}([0,T];{\mathbb{R}})
be the Banach space of continuous functions, null at time 0, equipped with the supremum norm.
Let
{\rm P}
be the unique probability measure on
\Omega
such that the canonical process
\{W^{H}_{t},t\in[0,T]\}
is an
1
-dimensional fractional Brownian motion with Hurst parameter
H>\frac{1}{2}
.

We denote by
\mathcal{E}
the space of step functions on
[0,T]
with values in
{\mathbb{R}}
. Let
\mathcal{H}
be the Hilbert space defined as the closure of
\mathcal{E}
with respect to the scalar product

\langle{\bf 1}_{[0,t]},{\bf 1}_{[0,s]}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=R_{H}(t,s),

where

R_{H}(t,s)=\int_{0}^{t\wedge s}K_{H}(t,r)K_{H}(s,r)dr,

and
K_{H}(t,s)
is the square integrable kernel defined by

K_{H}(t,s)=c_{H}s^{1/2-H}\int_{s}^{t}(u-s)^{H-3/2}u^{H-1/2}du,\qquad\mbox{for}\;t>s,

(2.3)

where
c_{H}=\sqrt{\frac{H(2H-1)}{\beta(2-2H,H-1/2)}}
and
\beta
denotes the Beta function. And for
t\leq s
, we set
K_{H}(t,s)=0
.

The mapping
{\bf 1}_{[0,t]}\rightarrow W^{H}_{t}
can be extended to an isometry between
\mathcal{H}
and the Gaussian space
\mathcal{H}_{1}
associated to
W^{H}
. We denote this isometry by
\varphi\rightarrow W^{H}(\varphi)
.

Consider the operator
K^{\ast}_{H}
from
\mathcal{E}
to
L^{2}(0,T;{\mathbb{R}})

defined by

(K^{\ast}_{H}\varphi)(s)=\int_{s}^{T}\varphi(t)\partial_{t}K_{H}(t,s)dt.

From (
2.3
), we get

\partial_{t}K_{H}(t,s)=c_{H}\left(\frac{t}{s}\right)^{H-1/2}(t-s)^{H-3/2}.

Notice that

K^{\ast}_{H}({\bf 1}_{[0,t_{1}]},\ldots,{\bf 1}_{[0,t_{m}]})=(K_{H}(t_{1},\cdot),\ldots,K_{H}(t_{m},\cdot)).

For any
\varphi,\psi\in\mathcal{E}
,

\langle\varphi,\psi\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=\langle K^{\ast}_{H}\varphi,K^{\ast}_{H}\psi\rangle_{L^{2}(0,T;{\mathbb{R}}^{m})}={\rm E}(W^{H}(\varphi)W^{H}(\psi))

and
K^{\ast}_{H}
provides an isometry between the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
and a closed subspace of
L^{2}(0,T;{\mathbb{R}})
.

Following
[
21
]
, we consider the fractional version of the Cameron-Martin space
\mathcal{H}_{H}:=\mathcal{K}_{H}(L^{2}(0,T;{\mathbb{R}}))
, where for
h\in L^{2}(0,T;{\mathbb{R}})
,

(\mathcal{K}_{H}h)(t):=\int_{0}^{t}K_{H}(t,s)h_{s}ds.

We finally denote by
\mathcal{R}_{H}=\mathcal{K}_{H}\circ\mathcal{K}_{H}^{\ast}:\mathcal{H}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}_{H}
the operator

\mathcal{R}_{H}\varphi=\int_{0}^{\cdot}K_{H}(\cdot,s)(\mathcal{K}^{\ast}_{H}h)(s)ds.

We remark that for any
\varphi\in\mathcal{H}
,
\mathcal{R}_{H}\varphi
is Hölder continuous of order
H
. Therefore, for any
1-H<\alpha<1/2
,

\mathcal{H}_{H}\subset C^{H}(0,T;{\mathbb{R}})\subset W^{1-\alpha}_{2}(0,T;{\mathbb{R}}).

Notice that
\mathcal{R}_{H}{\bf 1}_{[0,t]}=R_{H}(t,\cdot)
, and, as a consequence,
\mathcal{H}_{H}
is the Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Space associated with the Gaussian process
W^{H}
. The injection

\mathcal{R}_{H}:\mathcal{H}\rightarrow\Omega
embeds
\mathcal{H}
densely into
\Omega
and for any
\varphi\in\Omega^{\ast}\subset\mathcal{H}
,

{\rm E}\left(e^{iW^{H}(\varphi)}\right)=\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\|\varphi\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}}\right).

As a consequence,
(\Omega,\mathcal{H},{\rm P})
is an abstract Wiener space in the sense of Gross.

3.
Comparison and existence and uniqueness theorems

Let
0<\alpha<\frac{1}{2}
. Consider now the following equation

w_{t}=\eta_{0}+\zeta_{t}+\int_{0}^{t}b(w_{s})ds,\quad t\in(0,T],

(3.1)

where
\zeta\in{\mathcal{C}}^{1-\alpha}(0,T)
and
\eta_{0}>0
.

In this section we will prove the existence and uniqueness of solution to equation (
3.1
) and a comparison result.

Lemma 3.1
.

Assume that
b
is a Lipschitz function and that
\zeta\in{\mathcal{C}}^{1-\alpha}(0,T)
. Then (
3.1
) has a unique solution
w\in{\mathcal{C}}^{1-\alpha}(0,T)
.

Proof.

The proof is a simple case of the proof of Theorem 5.1 in
[
21
]
.
∎

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
b,\,\tilde{b}
Lipschitz functions, such that
b\leq\tilde{b}
,
\zeta\in{\mathcal{C}}^{1-\alpha}(0,T)
and
t\in[0,T]
, then if

\displaystyle w_{t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\eta_{0}+\zeta_{t}+\int_{0}^{t}b(w_{s})ds,

\displaystyle\tilde{w}_{t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\eta_{0}+\zeta_{t}+\int_{0}^{t}\tilde{b}(\tilde{w}_{s})ds,

we have that

w_{t}\leq\tilde{w}_{t}
, for any
t\in[0,T]
.

Proof.

For
k>0
, consider the function

\varphi_{k}(x)={\bf 1}_{\{x\geq 0\}}\int_{0}^{x}\int_{0}^{y}\rho_{k}(z)dzdy,

where

\rho_{k}(z)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}2kz,&\textrm{if}\;\;z\in\left[0,\frac{1}{k}\right],\\[5.0pt]
2\times{\bf 1}_{\{z\geq\frac{1}{k}\}},&\textrm{otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

From
[
11
, Theorem 5.1]
, we can check that
\varphi_{k}\in{\mathcal{C}}^{2}_{b}
and that their derivatives are bounded:

0\leq\varphi^{\prime}_{k}(x)\leq 2x^{+},\;\textrm{and}\;0\leq\varphi^{\prime\prime}_{k}(x)\leq 2\times{\bf 1}_{\{x\geq 0\}},

where
x^{+}=\max(0,x)
. We also have that
\varphi_{k}(x)\uparrow(x^{+})^{2}
when
k\rightarrow 0
.

Then using this properties on
\varphi_{k}
we have

\displaystyle\varphi_{k}(w_{t}-\tilde{w}_{t})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\varphi^{\prime}_{k}(w_{s}-\tilde{w}_{s})(b(w_{s})-\tilde{b}(\tilde{w}_{s}))ds

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\varphi^{\prime}_{k}(w_{s}-\tilde{w}_{s})(\tilde{b}(w_{s})-\tilde{b}(\tilde{w}_{s}))ds

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\int_{0}^{t}(w_{s}-\tilde{w}_{s})^{+}|w_{s}-\tilde{w}_{s}|ds

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\int_{0}^{t}\left((w_{s}-\tilde{w}_{s})^{+}\right)^{2}ds,

where
C
does not depend on
k
.
Now, letting
k\rightarrow 0
the last inequality becomes

\left((w_{t}-\tilde{w}_{t})^{+}\right)^{2}\leq C\int_{0}^{t}\left((w_{s}-\tilde{w}_{s})^{+}\right)^{2}ds,

and using the Gronwall’s inequality we obtain that
\left((w_{t}-\tilde{w}_{t})^{+}\right)^{2}=0
and from here the desired result.
∎

4.
Convergence results

In this section, we consider the following equations

\displaystyle x_{t}^{(\zeta)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\eta_{0}+\zeta_{t}+\int_{0}^{t}b(x_{s}^{(\zeta)})ds+y_{t}^{(\zeta)},\,\qquad t\in[0,T],

(4.1)

where
y^{(\zeta)}
is the regulator term of
x^{(\zeta)}
,
and

\displaystyle x_{t}^{(\zeta),\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\eta_{0}+\zeta_{t}+\int_{0}^{t}\left[b(x_{s}^{(\zeta),\varepsilon})+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}f(x_{s}^{{(\zeta),\varepsilon}})\right]ds,\,\qquad t\in[0,T].

(4.2)

We assume that
f
is a
{\mathcal{C}}^{2}_{b}
not increasing function such that

\begin{cases}f(x)=0,&\mbox{if}\;x\geq 0,\\
0<f(x)\leq x^{-},&\mbox{if}\;x<0,\end{cases}

with
x^{-}=\max(0,-x)
.

We will assume this definition of
f
throughout the paper.

Following the same computations in
[
1
]
, we can easily check that equation (
4.1
) has a unique solution.
The purpose of this section is to prove that equation (
4.2
) has a unique solution and moreover, these solutions converge to the solution of equation (
4.1
) when
\varepsilon
goes to zero. The result states as follows:

Theorem 4.1
.

Assume that
b
is a Lipschitz function,
\zeta\in{\mathcal{C}}^{1-\alpha}(0,T)
. Then the solutions
x^{(\zeta),\varepsilon}
of (
4.2
) converge uniformly, as
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
, to
x^{(\zeta)}
, the unique solution of the equation (
4.1
). Moreover,
x^{(\zeta)}\in{\mathcal{C}}^{1-\alpha}(0,T).

The proof of this theorem follows easily from the following three propositions in this section.

Proposition 4.2
.

Under the hypothesis of Theorem
4.1
, equation (
4.2
) has a unique solution
x_{t}^{(\zeta),\varepsilon}\in{\mathcal{C}}^{1-\alpha}(0,T)
.

Proof.

If we consider
\tilde{b}(x)=b(x)+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}f(x)
, since
f
is Lipschitz we can apply Lemma
3.1
and we obtain the desired result.
∎

Proposition 4.3
.

Under the hypothesis of Theorem
4.1
, the solutions
x^{(\zeta),\varepsilon}
of (
4.2
) converge uniformly to a continuous function
G
on
[0,T]
as
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
.

Proof.

Following the method used in
[
5
]
, we will organize the proof in several steps.

Step 1.
We consider the following equation for
t\in[0,T]
,

v_{t}=\eta_{0}+\zeta_{t}+\int_{0}^{t}b(v_{s})ds.

(4.3)

We want to prove that
v_{t}\leq x_{t}^{(\zeta),\varepsilon}
for all
\varepsilon>0
and
t\in[0,T]
.

Using Lemma
3.1
, equation (
4.3
) has a unique solution. Moreover, since
b\leq\tilde{b}
(
\tilde{b}(x)=b(x)+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}f(x)
where
\frac{1}{\varepsilon}f(x)\geq 0
), we can apply Lemma
3.2
and we obtain the desired result.

Step 2.
Now we consider

u_{t}=\eta_{0}+\zeta_{t}+\int_{0}^{t}b\left(u_{s}+\sup_{s^{\prime}\leq s}u_{s^{\prime}}^{-}\right)ds.

(4.4)

We also define
\tilde{u}_{t}=u_{t}+\sup_{s^{\prime}\leq t}u_{s^{\prime}}^{-}
and
\phi_{t}=\sup_{s^{\prime}\leq t}u_{s^{\prime}}^{-}
so we can write
\tilde{u}_{t}=u_{t}+\phi_{t}
.

We observe that
\tilde{u}_{t}\geq 0
and
\phi_{t}
is an increasing function. Following the same ideas in the proof of Lemma
3.1
we can prove that equation (
4.4
) has a unique solution.

We want to prove that
x_{t}^{(\zeta),\varepsilon}\leq\tilde{u}_{t}
for all
\varepsilon>0
. We write
\tilde{\theta}_{t}^{\varepsilon}=x_{t}^{(\zeta),\varepsilon}-\tilde{u}_{t}
.

We can use again the function
\varphi_{k}
defined in the proof of Lemma
3.2
and we have

\displaystyle\varphi_{k}(\tilde{\theta}_{t}^{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\varphi^{\prime}_{k}(\tilde{\theta}_{s}^{\varepsilon})\left(b(x_{s}^{(\zeta),\varepsilon})+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}f(x_{s}^{(\zeta),\varepsilon})-b(\tilde{u}_{s})\right)ds-\int_{0}^{t}\varphi^{\prime}_{k}(\tilde{\theta}_{s}^{\varepsilon})d\phi_{s}

(4.5)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\varphi^{\prime}_{k}(\tilde{\theta}_{s}^{\varepsilon})\left(b(x_{s}^{(\zeta),\varepsilon})-b(\tilde{u}_{s})\right)ds+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{0}^{t}\varphi^{\prime}_{k}(\tilde{\theta}_{s}^{\varepsilon})f(x_{s}^{(\zeta),\varepsilon})ds-\int_{0}^{t}\varphi^{\prime}_{k}(\tilde{\theta}_{s}^{\varepsilon})d\phi_{s}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle T_{1}^{\varepsilon}(k)+T_{2}^{\varepsilon}(k)+T_{3}^{\varepsilon}(k).

Now we observe that,

0\leq T_{2}^{\varepsilon}(k)\leq\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{0}^{t}2(\tilde{\theta}_{s}^{\varepsilon})^{+}(x_{s}^{(\zeta),\varepsilon})^{-}ds=0.

In fact, if
(\tilde{\theta}_{s}^{\varepsilon})^{+}>0
, then
\tilde{\theta}_{s}^{\varepsilon}=x_{s}^{(\zeta),\varepsilon}-\tilde{u}_{s}>0
. So, since we have proved that
\tilde{u}_{t}\geq 0
, it is necessary that
x_{s}^{(\zeta),\varepsilon}>0
and then
(x_{s}^{(\zeta),\varepsilon})^{-}=0
.

On the other hand,
T_{3}^{\varepsilon}(k)\leq 0
, since
\varphi^{\prime}_{k}(\tilde{\theta}_{s}^{\varepsilon})\geq 0
and
\phi_{t}
is an increasing process.
Then,

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle T_{1}^{\varepsilon}(k)\leq\int_{0}^{t}2(\tilde{\theta}_{s}^{\varepsilon})^{+}|b(x_{s}^{(\zeta),\varepsilon})-b(\tilde{u}_{s})|ds

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\int_{0}^{t}(\tilde{\theta}_{s}^{\varepsilon})^{+}|x_{s}^{(\zeta),\varepsilon}-\tilde{u}_{s}|ds=C\int_{0}^{t}(\tilde{\theta}_{s}^{\varepsilon})^{+}|\tilde{\theta}_{s}^{\varepsilon}|ds=C\int_{0}^{t}\left((\tilde{\theta}_{s}^{\varepsilon})^{+}\right)^{2}ds.

Using the previous computations of
T_{1}^{\varepsilon}(k)
,
T_{2}^{\varepsilon}(k)
and
T_{3}^{\varepsilon}(k)
on (
4.5
) and for
k\rightarrow 0
we have

\left((\tilde{\theta}_{t}^{\varepsilon})^{+}\right)^{2}\leq C\int_{0}^{t}\left((\tilde{\theta}_{s}^{\varepsilon})^{+}\right)^{2}ds.

Now we have just to apply Gronwall’s lemma and we obtain that
(\tilde{\theta}_{t}^{\varepsilon})^{+}=0
. So
x_{t}^{(\zeta),\varepsilon}\leq\tilde{u}_{t}
for all
\varepsilon
.

Step 3.
Since we have seen that
v_{t}\leq x_{t}^{(\zeta),\varepsilon}\leq\tilde{u}_{t}
and
|\tilde{u}_{t}|\leq 2\sup_{s\leq t}|u_{s}|
we can state

|x_{t}^{(\zeta),\varepsilon}|\leq|v_{t}|+2\sup_{s\leq t}|u_{s}|.

So,

\sup_{\varepsilon}|x_{t}^{(\zeta),\varepsilon}|\leq|v_{t}|+2\sup_{s\leq t}|u_{s}|.

Step 4.
We can use Lemma
3.2
to see that
x_{t}^{(\zeta),\varepsilon}
is an increasing sequence when
\varepsilon
decreases. Using that we have proved that it is bounded for all
\varepsilon>0
we can define
G_{t}:=\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}x_{t}^{(\zeta),\varepsilon}
. Since the sequence
\{x^{(\zeta),\varepsilon}\}_{\varepsilon}
is increasing, the converge is uniform in
[0,T]
(see
[
20
]
). That is,
\sup_{t}|x_{t}^{(\zeta),\varepsilon}-G_{t}|\rightarrow 0
when
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
, and so

is a continuous function.
∎

Proposition 4.4
.

If
G
is the function appearing in Proposition
4.3
, then
G_{t}
is the solution of the equation (
4.1
) and
G\in{\mathcal{C}}^{1-\alpha}(0,T).

Proof.

We will prove that
G
is a solution of our equation. So, by uniqueness we will obtain the desired result.
Then, we have to check that we have a pair
(G_{t},y_{t}^{(\zeta)})\in{\mathcal{C}}_{+}({\mathbb{R}}_{+},{\mathbb{R}})
that solves the Skorohod problem with reflection for
z^{(\zeta)}
a path in
{\mathcal{C}}_{+}({\mathbb{R}}_{+},{\mathbb{R}})
. By Definition
2.1
we have to prove the following facts

(i)

G_{t}=z_{t}^{(\zeta)}+y_{t}^{(\zeta)}
and
G_{t}\geq 0
, for each
t\geq 0
.

(ii)

y_{0}^{(\zeta)}=0
and
y^{(\zeta)}
is non-decreasing.

(iii)

For all
t\in[0,T]
,
\int_{0}^{t}G_{s}dy^{(\zeta)}_{s}=0
.

Step 1
: Define, using monotone convergence theorem,

\displaystyle y_{t}^{(\zeta)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{0}^{t}f\left(x_{s}^{(\zeta),\varepsilon}\right)ds,

\displaystyle z_{t}^{(\zeta)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\eta_{0}+\zeta_{t}+\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\int_{0}^{t}b(x_{s}^{(\zeta),\varepsilon})ds=\eta_{0}+\zeta_{t}+\int_{0}^{t}b(G_{s})ds.

(4.6)

So, it only remains to check that
G_{t}\geq 0
,
\forall t\in[0,T]
.

Since

x_{t}^{(\zeta),\varepsilon}-\eta_{0}-\zeta_{t}-\int_{0}^{t}b(x_{s}^{(\zeta),\varepsilon})ds=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{0}^{t}f\left(x_{s}^{(\zeta),\varepsilon}\right)ds

\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\left[\varepsilon x_{t}^{(\zeta),\varepsilon}-\varepsilon\eta_{0}-\varepsilon\zeta_{t}-\varepsilon\int_{0}^{t}b(x_{s}^{(\zeta),\varepsilon})ds\right]=0,\quad\forall\,t\in[0,T],

we have

\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\int_{0}^{t}f\left(x_{s}^{(\zeta),\varepsilon}\right)ds=0,\quad\forall\,t\in[0,T].

The monotone convergence theorem implies that

\int_{0}^{t}f\left(G_{s}\right)ds=0,

\forall\,t\in[0,T]
. Then,
G_{s}^{-}=0
and
G_{s}\geq 0
.

Step 2
:
Obviously
y_{0}^{(\zeta)}=0
. Moreover,
y^{(\zeta)}
is non-decreasing since

Step 3
:
With the definition of
y_{t}^{(\zeta)}
in mind, we define the following two measures on
[0,T]

\mu_{\varepsilon}(A)=\int_{A}\frac{1}{\varepsilon}f\left(x_{s}^{(\zeta),\varepsilon}\right)ds,\qquad\qquad\mu(A)=\int_{A}dy_{s}^{(\zeta)},\qquad\quad A\in\mathcal{B}([0,T]).

We have that

converges weakly to

\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\mu_{\varepsilon}([0,t])=\mu([0,t]),\qquad\forall\,t\geq 0.

Observe that if

, then

for all

, and
f(x^{(\zeta),\varepsilon}_{t})\leq f(x^{(\zeta),\varepsilon^{\prime}}_{t}),
for all

.
It implies that

, and so,

, for any

.

So, if

and it satisfies that

. So, we get, for any

\int_{0}^{t}x^{(\zeta),\varepsilon}_{s}dy_{s}^{(\zeta)}=\int_{0}^{t}x^{(\zeta),\varepsilon}_{s}d\mu(s)\leq 0.

Finally, by the monotone convergence theorem

\int_{0}^{t}G_{s}dy_{s}^{(\zeta)}\leq 0.

The condition

\int_{0}^{t}G_{s}dy_{s}^{(\zeta)}=0.

Step 4
: To finish the proof let us check that

From expression (

∎

The aim of this section is to study the Fréchet differentiability in the direction

\displaystyle x_{t}^{g}=\eta_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}b(x_{s}^{g})ds+\int_{0}^{t}\sigma(\bar{x}^{g}_{s-r})dg_{s},\qquad t\in[0,T],

\displaystyle x_{t}^{g}=\eta_{t},\qquad t\in[-r,0]

(5.1)

where
\bar{x}^{g}

[

]
. The main result of this section is the following:

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
\bar{x}
be a map
g\mapsto\bar{x}^{g}
from
W_{2}^{1-\alpha}(0,T)
to

Proof.

It follows easily combining Propositions
5.2
and
5.4
. ∎

We begin studying the differentiability of the integral.

Proposition 5.2
.

Let
x
be a map
g\mapsto\bar{x}^{g}
from
W_{2}^{1-\alpha}(-r,T-r)
to
W_{1}^{\alpha}(-r,T),
continuously Fréchet differentiable in the direction
h\in W_{2}^{1-\alpha}(0,T)
. Let
\sigma\in{\mathcal{C}}^{1}_{b}
. Then the mapping

F:W_{2}^{1-\alpha}(0,T)\mapsto W_{1}^{\alpha}(0,T),

defined by

F(g)_{\cdot}:=\int_{0}^{\cdot}\sigma(\bar{x}_{s-r}^{g})dg_{s}

is Fréchet differentiable in the direction
h\in W_{2}^{1-\alpha}(0,T)
with directional derivative

D_{h}F(g)_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}\sigma(\bar{x}^{g}_{s-r})dh_{s}+\int_{0}^{t}\sigma^{\prime}(\bar{x}^{g}_{s-r})D_{h}\bar{x}^{g}_{s-r}dg_{s}.

Proof.

Let us consider the mapping

H:W_{2}^{1-\alpha}(0,T)\times W_{1}^{\alpha}(-r,T-r)\mapsto W_{1}^{\alpha}(0,T),

defined by

H(g,x):=\int_{0}^{\cdot}\sigma(x_{s-r})dg_{s}.

Following the same computations as in Lemma 3 in
[
21
]
we can check that
H
is
Fréchet differentiable with directional derivatives:

•

for any
h\in W_{2}^{1-\alpha}(0,T)

D_{1,h}H(g,x)_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}\sigma(x_{s-r})dh_{s},

•

for any
w\in W_{1}^{\alpha}(-r,T-r)

D_{2,w}H(g,x)_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}\sigma^{\prime}(x_{s-r})w_{s-r}dg_{s}.

Then, since
F(g)=H(g,\bar{x}^{g})
, using the chain rule we get that, for
h\in W_{2}^{1-\alpha}(0,T)

\displaystyle D_{h}F(g)_{t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle D_{1,h}H(g,\bar{x}^{g})_{t}+D_{2,D_{h}\bar{x}^{g}}H(g,\bar{x}^{g})_{t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\sigma(\bar{x}^{g}_{s-r})dh_{s}+\int_{0}^{t}\sigma^{\prime}(\bar{x}^{g}_{s-r})D_{h}\bar{x}^{g}_{s-r}dg_{s}.

∎

We need a technical lemma before the last proposition.

Lemma 5.3
.

Assume that
\eta_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}
and that
b
belongs to
C^{2}_{b}
and that the mapping

\zeta:W_{2}^{1-\alpha}(0,T)\mapsto W_{1}^{\alpha}(0,T)

is Fréchet differentiable in the direction
h\in W_{2}^{1-\alpha}(0,T)
.
Then the mapping

F:W_{2}^{1-\alpha}(0,T)\times W_{1}^{\alpha}(0,T)\rightarrow W_{1}^{\alpha}(0,T)

defined by

(g,x)\mapsto F(g,x):=x-\eta_{0}-\int_{0}^{\cdot}b(x_{s})ds-\zeta^{g}_{\cdot}

is Fréchet differentiable in the direction
h\in W_{2}^{1-\alpha}(0,T)
. Moreover, for
h\in W_{2}^{1-\alpha}(0,T)
,
v\in W_{1}^{\alpha}(0,T)
and
(g,x)\in W_{2}^{1-\alpha}(0,T)\times W_{1}^{\alpha}(0,T)
, the Fréchet derivatives with respect to
h
and
v
are given respectively by

\displaystyle D_{1,h}F(g,x)_{t}

\displaystyle=-D_{h}\zeta_{t}^{g},

(5.2)

\displaystyle D_{2,v}F(g,x)_{t}

\displaystyle=v_{t}-\int_{0}^{t}b^{\prime}(x_{s})v_{s}ds.

(5.3)

Proof.

For
(h,x)
and
(\tilde{h},\tilde{x})
in
W_{2}^{1-\alpha}(0,T)\times W_{1}^{\alpha}(0,T)
we have

F(h,x)_{t}-F(\tilde{h},\tilde{x})_{t}=x_{t}-\tilde{x}_{t}-\int_{0}^{t}(b(x_{s})-b(\tilde{x}_{s}))ds-(\zeta_{t}^{h}-\zeta_{t}^{\tilde{h}}).

Using
[
2
, Proposition 2.2]
, we get that

\begin{split}\bigg\|x-\tilde{x}-\int_{0}^{\cdot}(b(x_{s})-b(\tilde{x}_{s}))ds\bigg\|_{\alpha,1}&\leq c_{\alpha,T}\|x-\tilde{x}\|_{\alpha,1}.\end{split}

On the other hand, since
\zeta
is differentiable, it will be continuous and

|\zeta^{h}-\zeta^{\tilde{h}}|
can be controlled.
Therefore,
F
is continuous in both variables

.

We next show the Fréchet differentiability. Let
v,w\in W_{1}^{\alpha}(0,T)
. By

[
2
, Proposition 2.2]
, we have that

\|D_{2,v}F(h,x)-D_{2,w}F(h,x)\|_{\alpha,1}\leq c_{\alpha,T}\|v-w\|_{\alpha,1}.

Thus,
D_{2,\cdot}F(h,x)
is a bounded linear operator.
Moreover,

F(h,x+v)_{t}-F(h,x)_{t}-D_{2,v}F(h,x)_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}(b(x_{s})-b(x_{s}+v_{s})+b^{\prime}(x_{s})v_{s})ds.

By the mean value theorem and
[
2

,

\bigg\|\int_{0}^{\cdot}(b(x_{s})-b(x_{s}+v_{s})+b^{\prime}(x_{s})v_{s})ds\bigg\|_{\alpha,1}\leq c_{\alpha,T}\|v\|_{\alpha,1}^{2}.

This shows that (
5.3
) is the Fréchet derivative of

with respect to
x
. The Fréchet differentiability with respect to
h
and the derivative is given easily by (
5.2
) from our hypothesis.
∎

Proposition 5.4

Assume the hypotheses of Lemma

7.
Stochastic case

In this section we apply the results obtained in the previous sections to the stochastic delay differential equation with positivity constraints (
1.1
). We can assume without loss of generality that
T_{0}=mr
.

Recall that
W^{H}=\{W_{t}^{H},\,t\in[0,T_{0}]\}

by the first inclusion in (

), we can apply the framework of the previous sections. In particular, under the assumptions of Theorem
1.1
, there exist the solutions of equation (
1.1
) and all the other stochastic equations that appear in this section.

We next proceed with the study of the Malliavin differentiability of the solution.
We begin applying the deterministic results of Theorem
5.1
to our class of stochastic equations.

Theorem 7.1
.

Let
\{\bar{X}_{t},t\in[-r,kr]\}
be a stochastic process in

such that

is almost surely differentiable in the directions of the Cameron-Martin space.
Assume

\tilde{X}
the solution to equation

\displaystyle\tilde{X}_{t}=\eta_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}b(\tilde{X}_{s})ds+\int_{0}^{t}\sigma(\bar{X}_{s-r})dW^{H}_{s},\qquad t\in[0,r(k+1)],

\displaystyle\tilde{X}_{t}=\eta_{t},\qquad t\in[-r,0].

Then, the solution
\tilde{X}
is almost surely differentiable in the directions of the Cameron-Martin space. Moreover, for any
t>0
, the derivative satisfies

D_{s}\tilde{X}_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}b^{\prime}(\tilde{X}_{u})D_{s}\tilde{X}_{u}du+\sigma(\bar{X}_{s-r})+\int_{0}^{t}\sigma^{\prime}(\bar{X}_{u-r})D_{s}\bar{X}_{u-r}dW^{H}_{u}

(7.1)

if
s\leq t
and
D_{s}\tilde{X}_{t}=0
if
s>t
.

Proof.

By Proposition
5.5
, the solution is Fréchet differentiable and for all
\varphi\in\mathcal{H}
the Fréchet derivative

D_{\mathcal{R}_{H}\varphi}\tilde{X}_{t}^{i}=\frac{d}{d\epsilon}\tilde{X}_{t}^{i}(\omega+\epsilon\mathcal{R}_{H}\varphi)|_{\epsilon=0}

exists, which proves the first statement of the theorem.

The derivative
D_{\mathcal{R}_{H}\varphi}\tilde{X}_{t}
coincides with

\langle D\tilde{X}_{t},\varphi\rangle_{\mathcal{H}},

where
D
is the usual Malliavin derivative. Furthermore, for any
\varphi\in\mathcal{H}
, since we can write

D_{\mathcal{R}_{H}\varphi}\bar{X}_{t}=\langle D\bar{X}_{t},\varphi\rangle_{\mathcal{H}},

by Proposition
5.5
, we get,

\begin{split}D_{\mathcal{R}_{H}\varphi}\tilde{X}_{t}&=\int_{0}^{t}\Phi_{t}^{W^{H}}(s)d(\mathcal{R}_{H}\varphi)(s)\\
&=\int_{0}^{t}\Phi_{t}^{W^{H}}(s)\left(\int_{0}^{s}\partial_{s}K_{H}(s,u)(\mathcal{K}_{H}^{\ast}\varphi)(u)du\right)ds\\
&=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\int_{0}^{T}(\mathcal{K}_{H}^{\ast}\Phi_{t}^{W^{H}})(s)(\mathcal{K}_{H}^{\ast}\varphi)(s)ds\\
&=\langle\Phi_{t}^{W^{H}},\varphi\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\end{split}

and equation (
7.1
) follows from (
5.4
). This concludes the proof.
∎

Now, we can deal with the Malliavin derivability of the solution to the equation (

Lemma 7.2
.

Let
\{F_{n},\,n\geq 1\}
be a sequence of random variables in

that converges to F in

and such that

Then,

belongs to
{\mathbb{D}}^{1,p}
, and the sequence of derivatives

converges to

in the weak topology of

.

Using the lemma above we can state the following result:

Proposition 7.3
.

For any
t\in(0,T_{0}]
,
X_{t}\in{\mathbb{D}}^{1,2}
.

Proof.

Let us recall that we have for the deterministic case that

in

We begin studying

Using Theorem

, since the initial condition

and its derivative satisfies, for

The solution of this equation can be written

\displaystyle D_{s}X_{t}^{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sigma(\eta_{s-r})\exp\left(\int_{s}^{t}b^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}(X_{u}^{\varepsilon})du\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sigma(\eta_{s-r})\exp\left(\int_{s}^{t}b^{\prime}(X_{u}^{\varepsilon})du\right)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{s}^{t}f^{\prime}(X_{u}^{\varepsilon})du\right).

Using that

\mathbf{E}\left[\|DX_{t}^{\varepsilon}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}}\right]\leq C_{H}\int_{0}^{t}\mathbf{E}\left(|D_{u}X_{t}^{\varepsilon}|^{2}\right)du,

(7.2)

\displaystyle D_{s}X_{t}^{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\left(\sigma(X_{s-r})+\int_{s}^{t}\sigma^{\prime}(X_{u-r})D_{s}X_{u-r}dW_{u}\right)

Using again (

\displaystyle\sup_{\varepsilon}\mathbf{E}\left[\|DX_{t}^{\varepsilon}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}}\right]\leq 2C_{H}\int_{0}^{t}\mathbf{E}\left(\sigma^{2}(X_{s-r})+\left|\int_{s}^{t}\sigma^{\prime}(X_{u-r})D_{s}X_{u-r}dW_{u}\right|^{2}\right)

Remark 7.4
.

Notice that in the previous proof we have seen that

\sup_{\varepsilon}\int_{0}^{t_{0}}\mathbf{E}\left(|D_{s}X_{t_{0}}^{\varepsilon}|^{2}\right)ds<\infty.

So, there will exist a convergent sub-sequence that will converge in the weak topology of
L^{2}(\Omega;L^{2}([0,T]))
. Moreover, by uniqueness, the limit will be
DX_{t_{0}}.

Now we can give the proof of the main result of this paper.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

We will prove that for any
a>0
, the restriction on
[a,+\infty)
of the law of
X_{t_{0}}
is absolutely continuous.
We have seen in Proposition
7.3
that
X_{t_{0}}
belongs to
{\mathbb{D}}^{1,2}
. Then, using the classical result due to Bouleau and Hirsch (see
[
3
]
or
[
22

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t_{0}}(D_{s}X_{t_{0}})^{2}ds>0,\qquad\mbox{ a.s on }\Omega_{a}.

Since

\int_{0}^{t_{0}}|D_{s}X_{t_{0}}|ds<t_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\int_{0}^{t_{0}}(D_{s}X_{t_{0}})^{2}ds\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

it suffices to check that

\int_{0}^{t_{0}}|D_{s}X_{t_{0}}|ds>0,\qquad\mbox{ a.s on }\Omega_{a}.

On the other hand, since
\sigma>0
, we have that
\sigma(X_{t_{0}-r})>0
and we can find
t_{1}(\omega)<t_{0}
such that for

\sigma(X_{s-r})+\int_{s}^{t_{0}}\sigma^{\prime}(X_{u-r})D_{s}X_{u-r}dW_{u}>\frac{1}{2}\sigma(X_{t_{0}-r})>0.

From expression (
7.3
), we get that for any
s,\,t_{1}(\omega)<s<t_{0}
and for all
\varepsilon>0

it holds that
D_{s}X_{t_{0}}^{\varepsilon}>0
.

Passing to the limit, we obtain that for any
s,\,t_{1}(\omega)<s<t_{0}
it holds that
D_{s}X_{t_{0}}\geq 0
and so, it will be enough to prove that

\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{0}}D_{s}X_{t_{0}}ds>0,\qquad\mbox{ a.s on }\Omega_{a}.

Fixed
a>0
set
B_{s}=\{\omega,X_{s}(\omega)>\frac{a}{2}\}
and
\tau_{s}=\inf\{t\geq s,X_{t}=\frac{a}{2}\}
. We will assume that our path

Then

\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{0}}D_{s}X_{t_{0}}ds\geq\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{0}}D_{s}X_{t_{0}}{\bf 1}_{\{\tau_{s}>t_{0}\}}ds.

(7.4)

From Remark
7.4
it yields that there exists a sub-sequence that will converge in the weak topology, such that,

\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{0}}D_{s}X_{t_{0}}{\bf 1}_{\{\tau_{s}>t_{0}\}}ds=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{0}}D_{s}X_{t_{0}}^{\varepsilon}{\bf 1}_{\{\tau_{s}>t_{0}\}}ds,

(7.5)

in the weak topology of

Set
F_{s,t}^{\varepsilon}
the solution to the integral equation, for
s<t

F_{s,t}^{\varepsilon}=\sigma(X_{s-r})+\int_{s}^{t}\sigma^{\prime}(X_{u-r})D_{s}X_{u-r}dW_{u}+\int_{s}^{t}b^{\prime}(X_{u}^{\varepsilon})F_{s,u}^{\varepsilon}du.

Consider a

with
\tau_{s}>t_{0}
. For all

By the uniform convergence of

to
