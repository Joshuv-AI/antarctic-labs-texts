Differential Privacy for Clustering Under Continual Observation
Max Dupré la Tour, Monika Henzinger, David Saulpic

Abstract
We consider the problem of clustering privately a dataset in ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} that undergoes insertion and deletion of points. Specifically, we give an ε \varepsilon -differentially private clustering mechanism for the k k -means objective under continual observation. This is the first approximation algorithm for that problem with an additive error that depends only logarithmically in the number T T of updates. The multiplicative error is almost the same as non privately. To do so we show how to perform dimension reduction under continual observation and combine it with a differentially private greedy approximation algorithm for k k -means.

Introduction

The massive, continuous and automatic collection of personal data of public as well as private organisations has raised privacy concerns, in legal terms
[
Uni18
]
but also in terms of citizens’ demands
[
oNYT20
,
Dem21
]
.
To answer those, formal privacy standards for algorithms were defined and developed: the most prominent one is Differential Privacy
[
DMNS06
]
. It allows to have a formal definition of
privacy
, and therefore to give algorithms with provable privacy guarantees.
Differentially private algorithms are now deployed quite massively. For instance, the U.S. Census Bureau uses it to release information from a private Census
[
Abo18
]
, Apple collects data from its phone’s users with an algorithm that respects differential privacy
[
20116a
,
20116b
]
, and Google develops an extensive library of private algorithms ready to be used
[
Gue19
]
.
Differential privacy ensures that those algorithms “behave roughly the same way” on two databases that differ by a single element.
The idea behind it is that one cannot infer from the result whether a specific element is present in the database, i.e. protecting against membership-inference attacks.

However, this guarantee is only valid for a
static
database. As real-life data often evolves over time – as in Apple’s example, where personal data is collected and transferred every day – a definition of privacy that accounts for such changes is necessary.
To address that issue, an extension of differential privacy was proposed by Dwork, Naor, Pitassi, and Rothblum
[
DNPR10
]
: an algorithm that respects privacy
under continual observation
is given a sequence of updates to its data set, one per time step, and computes an output at each time step, such that differential privacy is preserved for all time steps.
A sequence of publications has studied maintaining a mere counter under that model
[
DNPR10
,
CSS11
,
JRSS21
,
GKMN22
,
HUU23
,
FHU22
]
, and other basic tools such as frequency estimation
[
EMM+23
]
, histograms
[
CR22
]
, heavy hitters
[
LT23
]
, and various graph properties
[
FHO21
]
.

In this article, we consider a more complex problem, namely clustering.
This is one of the most central problems in unsupervised machine learning, with many applications ranging from community detection to duplicate detection.
One example of an application is to report clusters of people infected by a disease, based on the web search in geographic regions. The input data for that application is inherently evolving – new web searches are continuously performed – and highly sensitive.
The question we ask is therefore:

Is it possible to maintain a provably good clustering that is private under continual observation?

1.1
Our Results.

In order to answer this question,

The

Our Techniques.

We focus from now on the ”pure”
\varepsilon
-differential privacy setting, and defer the results and discussion of
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP to
Appendix

.

In this work, we present an algorithm designed to overcome those difficulties under continual observation. First, to address
(2)
, we simplify a non-private
O(1)
-approximation algorithm from Mettu and Plaxton
[
MP00
]
so that it works with a fixed, hierarchical decomposition of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and merely requires counting how many input points are in each cell of the decomposition.
Combining this with any continual,
\varepsilon
-DP histogram algorithm allows to solve
k^{\prime}
-means under continual observation, for any
k^{\prime}

d
and
O(1)
-multiplicative error. The resulting set of centers has been computed in a DP manner and thus, for some large enough value of
k^{\prime}
, we can treat it as a

, i.e. a set of points that “represents” the input points with regards to
k
-means cost (see
[
Ngu20
]
). We then run the best-known non-DP
k
-means algorithm on this coreset at every time step.
This results in the following theorem.

Theorem 3
.

Consider an input stream of additions and deletions of points from
B(0,\Lambda)
in the metric space
(\mathbb{R}^{d},\ell_{2})
.
For any
\alpha>0
, there exists an algorithm that is
\varepsilon
-differentially private under continual observation and computes, for each time step
t
, a solution to
k
-means that has cost at most

(1+\alpha)w^{*}\cdot\textsc{opt}_{t}+\frac{k2^{O(d)}\log(n)^{4}}{\varepsilon}\cdot\log(T)^{3/2}\cdot\Lambda^{2},

with probability

, where
w^{*}
is the best approximation ratio for non-private and static

-means.

To show a similar result without an exponential dependency in
d
, we overcome Challenge
(1)

Showing how to make dimension reduction work under continual observation with small additive error is the main contribution of our work.

This works as follows: we use standard dimension reduction (which conveniently is independent of the input data), run the above algorithm in the resulting low-dimensional metric space, and show how to lift a low-dimensional clustering back into the original space.
The centers in the low-dimensional space define a clustering, and we use the means of all such clusters in the original
\mathbb{R}^{d}
as our solution.
To compute these means in a DP-manner, we use the counters for each cell from the
\varepsilon
-DP histogram.
As the additive error in each mean computation for each high-dimensional center is the sum of the additive error of the counters of all cells of its cluster, we need to make sure that each cluster consists of only
k^{O(1)}
cells of our decomposition.
We had carefully designed our decomposition so that this is true.
Additionally, to make this work, we need to keep multiple counters per cell, namely the number of points as well as the sum of the points by coordinate.

Unfortunately, the resulting algorithm is only correct with
O(1)
probability
per time step
and want to get a constant success probability
over all time steps
.
The standard technique of simply decreasing the failure probability in each time step does not work as the additive error would be polynomial in
T
.
Thus, instead
we boost the success probability of each time step by running
\log(T)
many low-dimensional clustering algorithms, leading only to an increase of the additive error by
\log(T)^{1/2}
. However, to then pick the best solution out of them is not easy as it requires to evaluate the cost of each solution in a DP-manner. We show how to do this based on additional counters we maintain in our histogram.

Preliminaries

In a metric space
(X,\dist)
, we define the following

. For any
P,\mathcal{S}\subset X

\cost(P,\mathcal{S}):=\sum_{p\in P}\dist(p,\mathcal{S})^{2}
.
With a slight abuse of notation, when

The

is the following. We are given a set
P\subseteq X

k

that minimizes
\cost(P,\mathcal{S})
. We call any set of

An algorithm is a

, where

is the optimal

-means cost on the data set. As mentioned in the introduction, in continual observation, the optimal solution at time
t
is noted
\textsc{opt}_{t}
,
n
is the maximum size of the data set across time, and
T
is the total number of time steps.

Assumption.

As usual in studying clustering under differential privacy, we will assume all points come from the ball
B_{d}(0,\Lambda)=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\|x\|_{2}\leq\Lambda\right\}

]

2.2
Differential Privacy

We start with the formal definition of differential privacy under continual observation.
Let
\sigma
be a stream of updates to a dataset:
\sigma(t)
corresponds to either the addition of a new item to the dataset, the removal of one, or a “no operation” time (where nothing happens to the dataset).
Two streams are
neighboring
if they differ by the addition of a single item at a single time step (and possibly its subsequent removal).
This is the so-called
event-level
privacy
[
DNPR10
]
.
Let
\mathcal{M}
be a randomized mechanism that associates a stream
\sigma
to an output
\mathcal{M}(\sigma)\in\mathcal{X}
.
For an
\varepsilon\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
,
\mathcal{M}
is
\varepsilon
-differentially private under continual observation if, for any pair of neighboring streams
\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}
, and any possible set of outcomes

\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
, it holds that

\Pr\left[\mathcal{M}(\sigma)\in\mathcal{S}\right]\leq\exp(\varepsilon)\Pr\left[\mathcal{M}(\sigma^{\prime})\in\mathcal{S}\right].

The main tool we will use for the design of private algorithm is a histogram mechanism, that allows to count how many points are in sets.
Due to space constraint, we define formally this mechanism in
appendix

A
. Essentially, given a stream of update and ground sets
G_{1},...,G_{m}
, such that each input element is part of at most
b
sets, one can maintain the count of how many elements are in each
G_{i}
, privately under continual observation, with additive error
O(b)\cdot\frac{\log(T)}{\varepsilon}\cdot\left(\sqrt{\log(T)}+\log(m/\beta)\right)
.

We use crucially this histogram construction to prove the following lemma. It allows to count over
clusters
of solutions to
k
-means that evolve over time.

Lemma 5
.

Let
1>\alpha>0
, and
\sigma
be a stream of addition or deletion of points from a ground set
G\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
of length
T
, and let

be the set of points at time

with
1\leq t\leq T
. Let
f:G\rightarrow[-1,1]
be a function, and for each time
t

\mathcal{C}^{t}
be a set of
k

There is an
\varepsilon
-DP algorithm that releases a value
\Sigma_{i}^{t}
for each
i=1,...,k
and time
t
, with the following guarantee:

•

for any time
t
, there is a partition
C^{t}_{1},...,C^{t}_{k}
of

such that assigning points of
C^{i}_{t}
to the center

•

\left|\Sigma_{i}^{t}-\sum_{p\in C^{t}_{i}}f(p)\right|\leq k\cdot\alpha^{-O(d)}\cdot\log(n)^{3}\cdot\frac{\log(T)}{\varepsilon}\cdot\left(\sqrt{\log(T)}+\log(1/\beta)\right)

We also note that the clusters

A Well-Structured Static Clustering Algorithm

In this section, we present a non-private algorithm that is
well structured
(in the sense that it can be easily turned into a private algorithm, as we will see), and works in low dimensional space.
Our algorithm is highly inspired by the static greedy algorithm for
k
-means proposed by Mettu and Plaxton
[
MP00
]
. This essentially reduces the problem to computing a histogram, for which there are known algorithms working privately under continual observation.

More precisely, the algorithm computes a solution with a constant multiplicative approximation factor, taking as an input a mere summary of the data: given the recursive net decomposition described in
section

2.1
, the algorithm merely requires a function
\nval
that assigns a weight to every net point. This function is an approximation to the value of net points, as formalized in
definition

6
.

The algorithm works in
k
rounds: at each round, a new center is placed greedily as follows. Initially, all net points are
available

At the beginning of a round, the algorithm selects the available net point with maximal

To reduce the additive error to polynomial in

we use the following result, that states that the cost of any clustering is roughly preserved when projecting onto

For a set

Lemma 9

We now sketch how to prove the following theorem.
See
2

For this, we follows the same path as for our

We follow the same path for

First, we note that the algorithm for low dimensions from

7
extends directly to
k

[

]
works for both
k
-means and

[
Ngu20
]
extends directly as well: this yields an algorithm with multiplicative approximation

and additive error

.

The dimension-reduction theorem of
lemma

9
also works for
k
-median. However, given a clustering in the projected space, computing the centers in the original space is not as easy as for
k
-means. For this, we show that the mean is actually a
2
-approximation of the
1
-median:

Lemma 15
.

Let
P
be a set of point in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, with optimal
1
-median
m
and optimal
1
-mean
\mu
. Then,

\sum_{p\in P}\|p-\mu\|\leq 2\sum_{p\in P}\|p-m\|.

Proof.

Our goal is to bound the distance between
m
and
\mu
.
Assume for simplicity that
m
is the origin, and consider the line
\ell
going through the origin and
\mu
.
Let
P_{\ell}
be the set
P
projected onto
\ell
.
Since the mean is linear,
\mu
is also the mean of
P_{\ell}
, i.e.,
\mu=\frac{\sum_{p\in P_{\ell}}p}{|P|}
.
Therefore, we have
|P|\|\mu\|\leq\sum_{p\in P_{\ell}}\|p\|
: since projection only decrease the norm, this is at most
\sum_{p\in P}\|p\|
.

Rephrasing, this means that
\|\mu-m\|\leq\frac{\sum_{p\in P}\|p-m\|}{|P|}
: the distance between the mean and the
1
-median is at most the
1
-median cost divided by
|P|
. Therefore,

\sum_{p\in P}\|p-\mu\|\leq\sum_{p\in P}\|p-m\|+|P|\|\mu-m\|\leq 2\sum_{p\in P}\|p-m\|
. This concludes the lemma.
∎

Therefore, given a clustering in low-dimensional space, using the mean of each cluster in high-dimensional space yields a mere factor

The third step of our

How to extend to

We additionally note that the simple

D.1

To boost the probabilities so that the algorithm outputs solutions that are good at all steps simultaneously, we will run several copies and at each time output the solution with best cost. For that, it is necessary to measure the cost of a cluster.
Our first lemma formalizes how to do this without having access to its center – which will be helpful to not lose additional privacy.

Lemma 26

For any set

\cost(X,\mu(X))=\sum_{x\in X}\|x\|_{2}^{2}-\frac{\left\|\sum_{y\in X}x\right\|_{2}^{2}}{|X|}
.

Proof.

Using properties of squared distances, we have the following:

\displaystyle\cost(X,\mu(X))

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in X}\|x-\mu(X)\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{x\in X}\sum_{i=1}^{d}|x_{i}-\mu(X)_{i}|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in X}\sum_{i=1}^{d}x_{i}^{2}+\mu(X)_{i}^{2}-2x_{i}\mu(X)_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in X}\|x\|_{2}^{2}+\|\mu(X)\|_{2}^{2}-2\sum_{i=1}^{d}x_{i}\mu(X)_{i}.

Here, we note that
\mu(X)=\frac{1}{|X|}\sum_{y\in X}y
: therefore,
\sum_{x\in X}\|\mu(X)\|_{2}^{2}=|X|\cdot\|\mu(X)\|_{2}^{2}=\frac{\left\|\sum_{y\in X}y\right\|_{2}^{2}}{|X|}

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in X}\sum_{i=1}^{d}x_{i}\mu(X)_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left(\frac{1}{|X|}\sum_{y\in X}y_{i}\right)\left(\sum_{x\in X}x_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|X|}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left(\sum_{x\in X}x_{i}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\left\|\sum_{x\in X}x\right\|_{2}^{2}}{|X|}.

Combining those equations concludes the lemma:

also contains one.
First, we argue that the cells in
\mathcal{A}
cover the whole unit ball. For a point
p
in the ball, consider the sequence of parts containing it. There are two cases: Either the smallest part at level
\lceil\log(n)\rceil
contains a center in its
\ell
-neighborhood, and, thus, it is added to
\mathcal{A}
; or it does not, and then the largest part of the sequence that does not contain a center in its
\ell
-neighborhood is added to
\mathcal{A}
. Note that such a largest part must exist, as the cell of level
0
contains all centers.

Next we argue that all cells
in
\mathcal{A}
are disjoint. Consider two intersecting cells
A
and
B
in
\mathcal{A}
.
By property 2 of the decomposition, it must be that one is included in the other: assume wlog that
A\subset B
(therefore
\level(A)>\level(B)
).
Note that if
A
is on level
\log(n)
and has a center in its
\ell
-neighborhood, then trivially
N^{\ell}(\textsc{Parent}(A))
contains a center.
Thus, for all
A\in\cal A
it holds that
N^{\ell}(\textsc{Parent}(A))
contains a center.
But
\textsc{Parent}(A)\subseteq B
: therefore,
N^{\ell}(B)
contains a center, which implies that
B\notin\mathcal{A}
.
Thus,
A
and
B
cannot be both in
\mathcal{A}
, which concludes the proof that
\mathcal{A}
is a partition of
B_{d}(0,1)
.

Assigning cells to center.
We can now define
c
as follows: for each cell
A\in\mathcal{A}
, we define
a(A)=\argmin_{i}\dist(v_{A},c_{i})
to be the closest point from
\mathcal{C}
to
v_{A}
.

We first define an assignment for a cell
A\in\mathcal{A}
at level
\log(n)
. In this case, triangle inequality ensures that paying the diameter of
A
for each point allows to serve all points in
A
with the same center. Formally, let
a(A)
be the closest center to
v_{A}
. For any
p\in A
, we have using
16
:

\displaystyle\dist(p,a(A))^{2}

\displaystyle\leq(\dist(p,v_{A})+\dist(v_{A},a(A)))^{2}\leq(2\dist(p,v_{A})+\dist(p,\mathcal{C}))^{2}

\displaystyle\leq(1+\alpha/2)\dist(p,\mathcal{C})^{2}+(1+\frac{2}{\alpha})\cdot 4\dist(p,v_{A})^{2}

\displaystyle\leq(1+\alpha/2)\dist(p,\mathcal{C})^{2}+\frac{9}{\alpha}\cdot\frac{1}{n}.

Therefore, summing all points in cells at level
\log(n)
gives an additive error at most
\frac{9}{\alpha}
.

Now, fix a cell
A\in\mathcal{A}
at level
>\log(n)
, and a point
p\in A
. Since, by construction of
\mathcal{A}
, the
\ell
-neighborhood of
A
contains at most one center from
\mathcal{C}
, it holds that:

•

either
p
is already assigned to
a(A)
, and then
\dist(p,a(A))=\dist(p,\mathcal{C})
,

•

or the center serving
p
in
\mathcal{C}
is outside of the
\ell
-neighborhood of
A
: in particular,
\dist(p,\mathcal{C})\geq\ell\cdot\dist(p,v_{A})
. In that case, we use the modified triangle inequality from
16
. This yields similarly as above:

\displaystyle\dist(p,a(A))^{2}

\displaystyle\leq(\dist(p,v_{A})+\dist(v_{A},a(A)))^{2}\leq(2\dist(p,v_{A})+\dist(p,\mathcal{C}))^{2}

\displaystyle\leq(1+\alpha/2)\dist(p,\mathcal{C})^{2}+(1+\frac{2}{\alpha})\cdot 4\dist(p,v_{A})^{2}

\displaystyle\leq(1+\alpha/2)\dist(p,\mathcal{C})^{2}+(1+\frac{2}{\alpha})\cdot 4\cdot\frac{1}{\ell^{2}}\dist(p,\mathcal{C})^{2}

\displaystyle=(1+\alpha/2)\dist(p,\mathcal{C})^{2}+(4+\frac{8}{\alpha})\cdot\frac{\alpha^{2}}{100}\cdot\dist(p,\mathcal{C})^{2}

\displaystyle\leq(1+\alpha)\dist(p,\mathcal{C})^{2}.

Summing over all
p\in P
and combining with the cells at level
\log(n)
, we conclude that:

\displaystyle\sum_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\sum_{p\in P\cap A}\dist(p,a(A))^{2}\leq(1+\alpha)\cost(P,\mathcal{C})+\frac{9}{\alpha}.

The other direction is straightforward: since
a(A)\in\mathcal{C}
, we have by definition for any
p\in A

\dist(p,\mathcal{C})\leq\dist(p,a(A))
, and therefore
\cost(P,\mathcal{C})\leq\sum_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\sum_{p\in P\cap A}\dist(p,a(A))^{2}
.
∎

Appendix F
Running Time Analysis and Handling Unknown
n

Running Time Analysis.

The running time of
Algorithm

1
, given the values
v
, is
2^{O(d)}\cdot k\log(n)
. Therefore, in dimension
O(\log(k))
, the running time is
k^{O(1)}\log(n)
.
Computing the values for all cell is the most expensive part of the algorithm: it is both slow and takes lot of memory, as there are
n^{O(d)}
many cells.

However, the algorithm
Algorithm

1
does not consider more than
2^{O(d)}\cdot k\log(n)
values. In particular, it does never use the value of a cell whose parents have
\nval
less than
\Theta
– where the parents of a net point
z
are all net point at previous level with
z
in their 1-neighborhood.
Therefore, it is not necessary to maintain any information for those, which leaves simply
2^{O(d)}\cdot k\log(n)
many counters – or
k^{O(1)}\log(n)
for the high-dimensional case.

The only detail to deal with is to add new counters in memory, when the count of net point
z
goes from below
\Theta
to above: then, one needs to incorporate the counts of its children. For this, it is merely enough to start a new counter from scratch, and add to it all points currently present in the dataset (at most
n
).
For two neighboring datasets, those sets of points are neighboring as well, and therefore the privacy proof follows.

Knowledge of
n
.

Knowing
n
(the maximum number of points at any given step) is necessary
to apply
Lemma

17
with the right privacy parameters – as we scale
\varepsilon
by
\log(n)
.

If
n
is not known, we can proceed exactly as presented for the time
T
, by guessing a value of
n
(initially some constant, say 100), and restarting the algorithm from scratch every time the maximum number of points doubles, and scaling the privacy budget by
1/i^{1+\kappa}
for the
i
-th algorithm.
As in the proof of
Lemma

12
, one can show that this increases the additive error only by a factor
O\left(\frac{\log(n)^{1+\kappa}}{\kappa}\right)
.

Appendix G
Extension to
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-Differential Privacy

Instead of using
\varepsilon
-differentially private algorithm, one could improve slightly the additive error by tolerating an
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-differentially private one.
The definition is the following:
For an
\varepsilon,\delta\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
, a mechanism
\mathcal{M}
is
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-differentially private under continual observation if, for any pair of neighboring streams
\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}
, and any possible set of outcomes

\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
, it holds that

\Pr\left[\mathcal{M}(\sigma)\in\mathcal{S}\right]\leq\exp(\varepsilon)\Pr\left[\mathcal{M}(\sigma^{\prime})\in\mathcal{S}\right]+\delta.

The first improvement we can get using
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-differential privacy is due to the advanced composition theorem of
[
KOV17
]
: for any
\delta>0
, composing
k
algorithms that are
(\varepsilon_{1},\delta_{1}),...,(\varepsilon_{k},\delta_{k})
-DP results in an
(\varepsilon^{\prime},\delta^{\prime}
-DP algorithm, with
\varepsilon=\sqrt{2\log(1/\delta)\sum\varepsilon_{i}^{2}}+\sum\varepsilon_{i}\frac{\exp(\varepsilon_{i})-1}{\exp(\varepsilon_{i})+1}
, and
\delta^{\prime}=\delta+\sum\delta_{i}
.
Note that, when all
\varepsilon_{i}\leq 1
,
\exp(\varepsilon_{i})-1\leq 2\varepsilon_{i}
, and therefore
\varepsilon^{\prime}\leq\sqrt{2\log(1/\delta)\sum\varepsilon_{i}}+2\sum\varepsilon_{i}^{2}

Therefore, when running the different copies of the algorithm in
Algorithm

5
, with parameters
\varepsilon_{i}=\frac{\varepsilon}{\sqrt{s(\kappa)i^{1+\kappa}\cdot 8\log(1/\delta)}},\delta_{i}=\frac{\delta}{s(\kappa)i^{1+\kappa}}
one get an algorithm that is
(\varepsilon^{\prime},\delta^{\prime})
-DP, with

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2\log(1/\delta)\sum_{i=1}^{\log T}\varepsilon_{i}^{2}}+2\sum\varepsilon_{i}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon/2+\varepsilon/2\leq\varepsilon

\displaystyle\delta^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{\log T}\frac{\delta}{s(\kappa)i^{1+\kappa}}

\displaystyle\leq\delta.

Therefore, the algorithm is
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP; and most importantly the quality loss due to the rescaling of
\varepsilon
is now essentially
\sqrt{\log(T)^{1+\kappa}}
, instead of
\log(T)^{1+\kappa}
.

In addition, we can use an
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP algorithm to maintain histogram with a better dependency in
\log T
as well:

Lemma 28

(Corollary 2 in
[
FHU23
]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{U}
be a universe of size
u
, and
b
be a given integer. Consider a stream of
T
vectors
\sigma(t)\in[-1,1]^{u}
such that at most
b
entries of
\sigma(t)
are non-zero, and such that for all time
t
all entries of
\sum_{i=1}^{t}\sigma(i)
are non-negative. Then there is an
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP algorithm which output a vector
h_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{u}
for each round
t
, such that, with probability
2/3
, it holds that

\forall t,\left\|h_{t}-\sum_{i=1}^{t}\sigma(i)\right\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{c\sqrt{\log(1/\delta)}}{\varepsilon}\log(T)\sqrt{b\log(6uT)},

where
c
is some constant.

Compared to
Lemma

17
, this works directly for all time steps simultaneously. Therefore, the proof of
Lemma

13
is now almost immediate, as it avoids the union-bound over
T
steps, and using the lemma above allows to save another factor
\sqrt{\log T}
.

Combined, those two improvements yield an algorithm with additive error
k^{O(1)}\frac{\log(n)^{4}\log(T)^{2+\kappa}\log(1/\delta)}{\varepsilon}
.
