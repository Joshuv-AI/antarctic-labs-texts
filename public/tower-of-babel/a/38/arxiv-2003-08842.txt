The higher structure of unstable homotopy groups
Samik Basu, David Blanc, and Debasis Sen

Abstract
. We construct certain unstable higher order homotopy operations indexed by the simplex categories of 𝚫 n \mathbf{\Delta}^{n} for n ≥ 2 n\geq 2 , and prove that all elements in the homotopy groups of a wedge of spheres are generated under such operations by Whitehead products and the group structure. This provides a stronger unstable analogue of Cohen’s theorem on the decomposition of stable homotopy.

Primary: 55Q35; secondary: 55Q40, 55T99

0.
Introduction

In
[
Co
]
, Joel Cohen showed how all elements in the stable homotopy groups
of the sphere spectrum can be generated under composition and higher order homotopy
operations from certain atomic elements: the three Hopf classes and their odd-primary
analogues. The main importance of this result is conceptual, as one of the few known
global facts about the stable homotopy groups as a whole
(see
[
Lin
,
N
,
Sc1
,
Sc2
,
BSS1
,
BSS2
]
).
Our goal here is to show that a similar but stronger result holds for the unstable
homotopy groups of wedges of spheres.

There are a number of competing definitions of higher homotopy operations, particularly
in the unstable setting (see
[
Sp
,
BMa
,
Sh
,
Sa
,
CF
,
BJT2
]
),
all of which ultimately involve obstructions to lifting appropriate diagrams from the
homotopy category. For our purposes, the relevant indexing categories turn out to be
finite subcategories of
\Delta
(see §
0.5
), yielding the notion of a

higher order simplicial operation
, defined in Section
1
.
This construction makes sense in any
(\infty,1)
-category, is in fact independent of
the specific model of
\infty
-categories we use, and includes as a special case
the long Toda brackets of
[
Wal
,
BJT3
,
BBS1
]
. Formally, it also includes
the (iterated) suspension as a special case (see §
5.1
below).

We are mainly concerned here with decomposability for the homotopy groups of
spheres, since the decomposition for wedges of spheres follows from
Hilton’s Theorem. This can be thought of as a higher order “ringoid” version of
identifying the indecomposables for a graded algebra. However,
following
[
Co
, Theorem 4.5]
, we first address the “module” version: that is,
the question of higher order decomposability for
\pi_{\ast}{\mathbf{X}}
as a
\Pi
-algebra (a graded group equipped with an action of the primary homotopy operations – see
[
St
, §1]
), in the following sense:

0.1 Definition
.

Denote by
\Pi_{>1}
the graded set of all classes in
\pi_{\ast}{\mathbf{V}}
,
for all finite wedges
{\mathbf{V}}
of simply-connected spheres.
For a simply-connected space
{\mathbf{X}}\in\mbox{\sf Top}_{\ast}
, we say that
\pi_{\ast}{\mathbf{X}}
is

generated of higher order over

\Pi_{>1}
by a graded subset

if any
\gamma\in\pi_{N}{\mathbf{X}}
is a linear combination
over
\mathbb{Z}
of elements obtained from
A
recursively

(i)

By the action of a non-trivial primary operation – that is, a Whitehead product
or precomposition with an element
\alpha\in\pi_{\geq N+1}{\mathbf{S}}^{N}
for some
N\geq 2

(see
[
Wh
, Ch. XI]
); or

(ii)

As the value of the higher order simplicial operation associated to an augmented
restricted simplicial space
{\mathbf{W}}\!_{\bullet}\to{\mathbf{X}}
as in Section
2
, with each

{\mathbf{W}}\!_{n}
a wedge of simply-connected spheres.

Note that both types of constructions raise degrees.

0.2 Definition
.

The set
A
of
atomic
classes in
\pi_{\ast}{\mathbf{X}}

consists of those

which, for some prime

,
induce a non-zero map

(where
\mathbb{F}_{0}:=\mathbb{Q}
by convention). These classes are determined up to
multiplication by

when

.

Theorem A
.

If
{\mathbf{X}}
is simply-connected, the elements of
\pi_{\ast}{\mathbf{X}}
are generated of
higher order over
\Pi_{>1}
by the atomic maps.

See Theorem
3.1
below. Our main technical result is then:

Theorem B
.

If
{\mathbf{X}}
is the
k
-connected cover of the
k
-sphere for
k\geq 2
, the atomic maps in
\pi_{\ast}{\mathbf{X}}
consist of (the lifts of) the Hopf maps

\eta_{k}\in\pi_{k+1}{\mathbf{S}}^{k}
,

\nu_{k}\in\pi_{k+3}{\mathbf{S}}^{k}
, and

\sigma_{k}\in\pi_{k+7}{\mathbf{S}}^{k}
, and the first
p
-torsion
classes
\alpha_{1}(p)\in\pi_{k+2p-3}{\mathbf{S}}^{k}
, for odd primes

.

See Theorem
4.1
below. The “spheres of birth” for the various
classes differ, of course:
k=2
for
\eta_{k}
,
k=4
for
\nu_{k}
,

k=8
for
\sigma_{k}
, and
k=3
for
\alpha_{1}(p)
.

In this paper we are mainly concerned with the “ringoid”
\Pi_{>1}
itself,
whose elements correspond to the primary operations on homotopy groups (as noted above).

0.3 Definition
.

We say that
\Pi_{>1}
is
generated of higher order
by a (graded)
subset
\mathcal{F}_{0}
if there is an exhaustive increasing filtration

\mathcal{F}_{0}\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{1}\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{2}\subseteq\dotsc
of

\Pi_{>1}
, such that
\mathcal{F}_{n}
is obtained from
classes in lower filtration by group operations, composition (of elements in

\mathcal{F}_{<n}
), or as the value of a higher order simplicial operation associated to a
simplicial space
{\mathbf{W}}\!_{\bullet}
with
\|{\mathbf{W}}\!_{\bullet}\|\simeq{\mathbf{V}}
, where that
each
{\mathbf{W}}\!_{n}
is a finite wedge of spheres and each face map of
{\mathbf{W}}\!_{\bullet}

is in
\mathcal{F}_{<n}
.

Theorem B then implies that
\Pi_{>1}
is generated of higher order by the atomic
maps listed there, yielding an unstable version of
[
Co
, Theorem 4.2]
(see
Corollary
4.13
). A further analysis yields:

Theorem C
.

The homotopy groups of a finite wedge of simply-connected spheres are generated
of higher order by the fundamental classes (i.e., inclusions of wedge summands)
and their Whitehead products.

See Theorem
5.16
below

.

In addition to the sample calculations of Section
5
, used in the proof of
Theorem C, in Section
6
we show what form it takes in practice
by providing a rational higher operation description of the Hopf fibrations

{\mathbf{S}}^{2n+1}\to\mathbb{C}\mathbf{P}^{n}
for all complex projective spaces.

0.4 Remark
.

Cohen actually provides an algorithm for finding decompositions of stable
classes, using spectral sequences (and conversely, see
[
Ada
,
WX
]
for examples
of the use of such decompositions to calculate differentials in the
Adams spectral sequence).

It seems unlikely that such an algorithm could be obtained unstably with the
present state of our knowledge. However, in
[
BBS3
]
we provide a more detailed
description of how the value of a previously defined higher order operation may be
inserted in the diagram defining another such operation, with a view to defining
an explicit notion of the “algebra of unstable higher homotopy operations”. This
is perhaps more meaningful than Theorem C itself, since the abstract fact that a
particular set
\mathcal{F}_{0}
suffices to generate
\Pi_{>1}
is not very useful on
its own.

for the value of

, with

the constant

for the constant augmented

induces

.

The category of topological spaces will be denoted by

,

, and that of pointed connected

(see

is one

for

1.
Simplicial higher order operations

As noted in the introduction, many versions of higher order operations have
appeared in the literature, starting with Adem’s secondary cohomology operations
(see
[
Ade
]
), Massey’s triple products (see
[
Mas
]
), and the Toda
brackets of
[
T1
]
.
The values of such operations usually appear as obstructions to lifting a
homotopy-commutative diagram to a model category
\mathcal{C}
,
and are characterized by the following properties:

(i)

The operations are
indeterminate
, in the sense that more than
one value may be obtained, depending on choices made in lifting;

(ii)

An
n
-th order operation is defined only when all lower order operations
(associated to partial diagrams) vanish, for some consistent set of choices.

(iii)

The final values are expressible in
\operatorname{ho}\mathcal{C}
.

See
[
BMa
,
BJT2
]
.

1.1
.

Models of
\infty
-categories.

Although in this paper we are only concerned with topological spaces, the notion of
higher operation we use here makes sense in a more general setting – namely, any model of
\infty
-category theory consisting of:

(a)

A category
\mathcal{C}
, with a distinguished full subcategory
\mathcal{C}_{0}
of

\infty
-
categories
.

(b)

A
homotopy category
functor
\mathcal{P}\colon\mathcal{C}_{0}\to\mbox{\sf Cat}
,
with a right adjoint
B:\mbox{\sf Cat}\to\mathcal{C}_{0}
called the
nerve
functor.

(c)

A
set of objects
functor

, such that for
each
X\in\mathcal{C}_{0}
and
x,y\in\operatorname{Obj}(X)
, we have a Kan complex

{\mathcal{M}ap}_{X}(x,y)
with homotopy associative and unital composition, and

\mathcal{P}(x,y)=\pi_{0}{\mathcal{M}ap}_{X}(x,y)
. Morphisms

in
\mathcal{C}
induce

{\mathcal{M}ap}_{X}(x,y)\rightarrow{\mathcal{M}ap}_{Y}(F(x),F(y))
,
which respects the composition operation up to homotopy.

(d)

When dealing with any specific
X\in\mathcal{C}_{0}
, we shall assume that all
necessary limits and colimits exist in
X
, and that it is
pointed
(that is,
the initial and final objects coincide).

Compare
[
RV
]
and
[
BMe
, §2]
.

In Toën’s axiomatization (see
[
T
, §4]
), and in all examples of interest,

\mathcal{C}
is a model category and
\mathcal{C}_{0}
consists of the fibrant objects in
\mathcal{C}
.
For example, if
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{S}
with the Joyal model category,
\mathcal{C}_{0}
consists of
the quasi-categories, while if
\mathcal{C}
is simplicial categories,
\mathcal{C}_{0}
consists of
those enriched in Kan complexes.
One then has a Quillen equivalence of
\mathcal{C}
to the complete Segal model structure of

[

]
, and therefore to quasi-categories (
[
Lu
]
),
simplicial categories (
[
B
]
), and the other standard models of

(\infty,1)
-categories.

4.13 Corollary
.

All homotopy classes of maps between wedges of simply-connected spheres are
generated of higher order by the fundamental classes, the Hopf maps

\eta_{k}\in\pi_{k+1}{\mathbf{S}}^{k}
,

\nu_{k}\in\pi_{k+3}{\mathbf{S}}^{k}
, and

\sigma_{k}\in\pi_{k+7}{\mathbf{S}}^{k}

Proof.

This follows from the stable analysis in
[

]
and
[

]
.
Note that we may post-compose the augmentation obtained from a resolution

of

with the covering map

to obtain the augmentation

in Definition

{\mathbf{Y}}
can be decomposed. However, our methods also allow us to describe
the elements generated by suspending
{\mathbf{Y}}
.

5.1
.

The suspension spectral sequence.

Given a connected pointed space
{\mathbf{Y}}
, we denote by
{\mathbf{Y}}\otimes S^{n}

the
n
-skeletal simplicial space with a single non-degenerate copy of
{\mathbf{Y}}

in simplicial dimension
n
, so that its geometric realization is weakly equivalent
to
\Sigma^{n}{\mathbf{Y}}
.

In particular, the homotopy spectral sequence for
{\mathbf{W}}\!_{\bullet}:={\mathbf{Y}}\otimes S^{1}
will
be called the
suspension spectral sequence
for
{\mathbf{Y}}
. This simplicial space
may be described combinatorially in terms of the degeneracies of
{\mathbf{Y}}
:

(5.2)

\scriptstyle{d_{0}}
\scriptstyle{d_{1}}
\scriptstyle{d_{2}}
\scriptstyle{d_{3}}
\textstyle{s_{0}{\mathbf{Y}}\vee s_{1}{\mathbf{Y}}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{d_{0}}
\scriptstyle{d_{1}}
\scriptstyle{d_{2}}
\textstyle{{\mathbf{Y}}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}

\textstyle{\ast}

with the degeneracies as indicated, and all face maps determined by the

Since
\pi_{\ast}{\mathbf{W}}\!_{0}=0
,
\pi_{\ast}\Sigma{\mathbf{Y}}
has no elements in filtration

in
this spectral sequence, so all its elements are formally exhibited as values
of higher simplicial homotopy operations.

5.3 Remark
.

The elements of
\pi_{\ast}\Sigma{\mathbf{Y}}
in filtration
1
are evidently
just the image of the suspension homomorphism

E:\pi_{\ast}{\mathbf{Y}}\to\pi_{\ast+1}\Sigma{\mathbf{Y}}
. Note that it is natural to think of
suspensions as values of a secondary homotopy operation, since any map

f:{\mathbf{S}}^{n}\to{\mathbf{Y}}
is null homotopic in
\Sigma{\mathbf{Y}}
for two different reasons – namely, the two cones on the “equatorial” copy of
{\mathbf{Y}}
.

5.4 Example
.

In the spectral sequence for
{\mathbf{W}}\!_{\bullet}:={\mathbf{S}}^{2}\otimes S^{1}
,
the
1
-cycles
\iota_{2}\in\pi_{2}{\mathbf{W}}\!_{1}
,

\eta_{2}\in\pi_{3}{\mathbf{W}}\!_{1}
, and

\eta_{2}\eta_{3}\in\pi_{4}{\mathbf{W}}\!_{1}
represent the suspension classes

\iota_{3}\in\pi_{3}{\mathbf{S}}^{3}
,

\eta_{3}\in\pi_{4}{\mathbf{S}}^{3}
, and

\eta_{3}\eta_{4}\in\pi_{5}{\mathbf{S}}^{3}
, respectively.

The
2
-chain
[s_{0}\iota_{2},s_{1}\iota_{2}]\in\pi_{3}{\mathbf{W}}\!_{2}

is not a cycle, since
[\iota_{2},\iota_{2}]\neq 0
in
\pi_{3}{\mathbf{W}}\!_{1}
.
However,
[s_{0}\iota_{2},s_{1}\iota_{2}]\circ\eta_{3}\in\pi_{4}{\mathbf{W}}\!_{2}

is a cycle, since

[\iota_{2},\iota_{2}]\circ\eta_{3}=(2\eta_{2})\circ\eta_{3}=0
in

\pi_{4}{\mathbf{W}}\!_{1}
. It has order
2
, and is not a boundary, so it must represent

\pm\nu^{\prime}\in\pi_{6}{\mathbf{S}}^{3}
, with
2\nu^{\prime}=\eta_{3}\eta_{4}\eta_{5}

(a suspension, and thus in filtration
1
).

5.5 Remark
.

More generally, we could start with any simplicial resolution
{\mathbf{W}}\!_{\bullet}
of
{\mathbf{Y}}
, and by
letting
{\mathbf{V}}_{\bullet}:={\mathbf{W}}\!_{\bullet}\otimes S^{k}
we obtain a resolution of
\Sigma^{k}{\mathbf{Y}}
.

5.6
.

Whitehead products.

To identify certain terms in higher filtration in the suspension spectral sequence,
we consider the special case where
{\mathbf{Y}}={\mathbf{S}}^{p}\vee{\mathbf{S}}^{q}
. Note that we have
two split inclusions
i_{p}:{\mathbf{S}}^{p}\hookrightarrow{\mathbf{Y}}
and
i_{q}:{\mathbf{S}}^{q}\hookrightarrow{\mathbf{Y}}
,
so the spectral sequences for each summand split off from that for
{\mathbf{Y}}
; we are only
interested in the remaining cross-term part.

First assume that
p,q\geq 2
: by Hilton’s Theorem, we know
that the lowest dimensional non-trivial cross-term cycle in the simplicial abelian
group
\pi_{i}{\mathbf{W}}\!_{\bullet}
in filtration (=simplicial dimension)
2
necessarily
has the form:

(5.7)

{}w_{2}~:=~[s_{0}i_{p},\,s_{1}i_{q}]~-~[s_{1}i_{p},\,s_{0}i_{q}]~\in~\pi_{p+q-1}{\mathbf{W}}\!_{2}

(see (
5.2
)). For dimensional reasons this cycle cannot be hit
by any differentials, and the class it represents in

\pi_{p+q+1}\|{\mathbf{W}}\!_{\bullet}\|=\pi_{p+q+1}({\mathbf{S}}^{p+1}\vee{\mathbf{S}}^{q+1})
is therefore the
generator
[i_{p+1},\,i_{q+1}]
(up to sign) – which thus can be expressed as
the value of a third order homotopy operation.

Likewise, the lowest dimensional cycles in filtration
3
will have the form

(5.8)

{}\begin{split}&~[s_{1}s_{0}i_{p},[s_{2}s_{0}i_{p},\,s_{2}s_{1}i_{q}]]-[s_{1}s_{0}i_{p},[s_{2}s_{1}i_{p},\,s_{2}s_{0}i_{q}]]+[s_{2}s_{0}i_{p},[s_{2}s_{1}i_{p},\,s_{1}s_{0}i_{q}]]\\
&-[s_{2}s_{0}i_{p},[s_{1}s_{0}i_{p},\,s_{2}s_{1}i_{q}]]+[s_{2}s_{1}i_{p},[s_{1}s_{0}i_{p},\,s_{2}s_{0}i_{q}]]-[s_{2}s_{1}i_{p},[s_{2}s_{0}i_{p},\,s_{1}s_{0}i_{q}]]\end{split}

representing
[i_{p+1},[i_{p+1},i_{q+1}]]
in

\pi_{2p+q+1}\in{\mathbf{S}}^{p+1}\vee{\mathbf{S}}^{q+1}
(and similarly for the other Hall basis
elements). Note that the
2
-cycle

[s_{0}i_{p},[s_{0}i_{p},\,s_{1}i_{q}]]-[s_{1}i_{p},[s_{0}i_{p},\,s_{1}i_{q}]]
, in the cross-term of

\pi_{2p+q-2}{\mathbf{W}}\!_{2}
, bounds

[s_{2}s_{0}i_{p},[s_{1}s_{0}i_{p},\,s_{2}s_{1}i_{q}]]
,
and so on.

When
p=q=1
,
\pi_{\ast}{\mathbf{W}}\!_{\bullet}
is concentrated in dimension
1
, and the
Whitehead products are commutators, so we cannot use a dimension argument to
identify the cycles. In this case it is convenient to work with a simplicial group
model for
{\mathbf{S}}^{1}\vee{\mathbf{S}}^{1}
, namely,
F(S^{0}\vee S^{0})
– a constant
(free) simplicial group. Thus
{\mathbf{W}}\!_{\bullet}
is a bisimplicial group which is equivalent to its
own diagonal, which is thus a simplicial group model
F(S^{1}\vee S^{1})
for

S^{2}\vee S^{2}
, and the spectral sequence collapses at the

E^{2}
-term.

In this case we see that the
1
-dimensional analogue of (
5.7
) is the
product of two commutators:

(5.9)

{}\omega~:=~\left(s_{0}\alpha s_{1}\beta s_{0}\alpha^{-1}s_{1}\beta^{-1}\right)\cdot\left(s_{1}\alpha s_{0}\beta s_{1}\alpha^{-1}s_{0}\beta^{-1}\right)~,

where
\alpha\in\pi_{1}{\mathbf{S}}^{1}_{(1)}
and

\beta\in\pi_{1}{\mathbf{S}}^{1}_{(2)}
are the fundamental classes of the two wedge
summands in
{\mathbf{Y}}={\mathbf{S}}^{1}_{(1)}\vee{\mathbf{S}}^{1}_{(2)}
. This is known to represent the
Whitehead product in
S^{2}\vee S^{2}
(see
[
Cu
, §11.11]
).
A similar statement holds for the case
p=1<q
.

Since
{\mathbf{S}}^{k}\vee{\mathbf{S}}^{k}
is the universal space for Whitehead products of

k
-dimensional classes, we see that whenever
{\mathbf{Y}}
is
(k-1)
-connected
and
\alpha,\beta\in\pi_{k}{\mathbf{Y}}
, the Whitehead product
[E\alpha,E\beta]

in
\pi_{2k+1}\Sigma{\mathbf{Y}}
is represented in the spectral sequence by

(5.10)

{}\gamma~:=~[s_{0}\alpha,\,s_{1}\beta]-[s_{1}\alpha,\,s_{0}\beta]~,

modulo suspensions.

We can use the suspension spectral sequence to provide an elementary proof of the
following well-known facts (cf.
[
HW
, (3.5)]
):

5.11 Lemma
.

Let
{\mathbf{Y}}
be
(k-1)
-connected (
k\geq 1
), and
\alpha,\beta\in\pi_{k}{\mathbf{Y}}
:

(a)

If
[\alpha,\beta]=0
in
\pi_{2k-1}{\mathbf{Y}}
,
then
[E\alpha,E\beta]
is divisible by
2
in

\pi_{2k+1}\Sigma{\mathbf{Y}}
, modulo elements in the image of the suspension

E:\pi_{\ast}{\mathbf{Y}}\to\pi_{\ast+1}\Sigma{\mathbf{Y}}
.

(b)

If
\alpha=\beta
and
k
is even, then
[E\alpha,E\beta]
is a suspension.

Proof.

By the universal example (
5.7
), the class

\gamma\in\pi_{2}\pi_{2k-1}{\mathbf{W}}\!_{\bullet}
of (

) is a cycle representing

[E\alpha,E\beta]
(modulo lower filtration, which is
\operatorname{Im}(E)
).

(a)

If
[\alpha,\beta]=0
, then
\delta:=[s_{0}\alpha,\,s_{1}\beta]

is another cycle which survives to
\pi_{\ast}\Sigma{\mathbf{Y}}
whose double is
\gamma
.

(b)

When
k
is even,
\gamma=0
, so
[E\alpha,E\beta]
vanishes modulo
suspensions.

∎

5.15 Remark
.

As noted above, in the spectral sequence for
{\mathbf{W}}\!_{\bullet}:={\mathbf{S}}^{4}\otimes S^{1}

the
1

5.16 Theorem
.

All elements in the homotopy groups of a finite wedge of
simply-connected spheres are generated of higher order by the
fundamental classes and their Whitehead products.

Proof.

Since any suspension is the value of a secondary homotopy operation (see §
5.3
),
by Corollary
4.13
, it suffices to show this for

\eta_{2}\in\pi_{3}{\mathbf{S}}^{2}
,
\nu_{4}\in\pi_{7}{\mathbf{S}}^{4}
, and

\sigma_{8}\in\pi_{15}{\mathbf{S}}^{8}
, and the maps

\alpha_{1}(p)\in\pi_{2p}{\mathbf{S}}^{3}
for odd primes
p
.

Indeed, from (
5.9
) we see that by Lemma
5.11
(a):

(a)

\eta_{2}
is represented by either half of

\omega
– e.g.,

h:=s_{0}\iota_{1}\cdot s_{1}\iota_{1}\cdot s_{0}\iota_{1}\cdot^{-1}s_{1}\iota_{1}^{-1}
in
\pi_{1}{\mathbf{W}}\!_{2}

for
{\mathbf{W}}\!_{\bullet}={\mathbf{S}}^{1}\otimes S^{1}
;

(b)

\nu_{4}
is represented by

[s_{0}i_{3},\,s_{1}i_{3}]\in\pi_{5}{\mathbf{W}}\!_{2}
for

{\mathbf{W}}\!_{\bullet}={\mathbf{S}}^{3}\otimes S^{1}
;

(c)

\sigma_{8}
is represented by

for

{\mathbf{W}}\!_{\bullet}={\mathbf{S}}^{7}\otimes S^{1}

Now for any odd prime

(5.17)

{}\pi_{i}{\mathbf{S}}^{2}_{(p)}~:=~\begin{cases}\mathbb{Z}_{(p)}\langle{\iota_{2}}\rangle&\text{for}\ \ i=2\\

Since

6.10

Some combinatorics

.

In order to describe the general case, we require some combinatorial notions:

6.11 Definition
.

Given a
k
-tuple
I=(i_{1}<\dotsc<i_{k})
of non-negative integers (in ascending
order), let
\underline{I}
denote the underlying unordered set (and conversely),
and
s_{I}=s_{i_{k}}s_{i_{k-1}}\dotsc s_{i_{2}}s_{i_{1}}
the
corresponding iterated degeneracy map, so that

(6.12)

{}\forall~x~\exists~y\ s_{I}x=s_{j}y\hskip 14.22636pt\text{if and only if}\hskip 14.22636ptj\in I~.

If
\underline{I}
and
\underline{J}
are disjoint sets of natural numbers,
I\sqcup J=J\sqcup I

will denote the disjoint union
\underline{I}\sqcup\underline{J}
, in ascending order.

Given two finite sets
I
and
J
of non-negative integers, each arranged in
ascending order, their
sign

\operatorname{sgn}(I,J)
is defined as follows:

(i)

If
\underline{I}
and
\underline{J}
form a partition of
\{1,2,\dotsc,n\}
, we write

\sigma_{(I,J)}\in S_{n}
for the permutation obtained by concatenating

I
with
J
, and let
\operatorname{sgn}(I,J):=\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma_{(I,J)})
.

(ii)

If
\underline{I}
and
\underline{J}
are disjoint subsets of
\{0,1,2,\dotsc,N\}
, let

\pi:I\sqcup J\to\{1,2,\dotsc,n\}
be an order-preserving
isomorphism, with
\operatorname{sgn}(I,J):=\operatorname{sgn}(\pi[I],\pi[J])
.

(iii)

If
\underline{I}
and
\underline{J}
are any two finite sets
I
and
J
of non-negative integers,
let
I^{\prime}:=\underline{I}\setminus\underline{J}
and
J^{\prime}:=\underline{J}\setminus\underline{I}
(each in ascending order),
and set
\operatorname{sgn}(I,J):=\operatorname{sgn}(I^{\prime},J^{\prime})
.

6.13 Example
.

For
\underline{I}=\{2,4\}
and
\underline{J}=\{1,3,5\}
, we have

\sigma_{(I,J)}=\binom{1\ 2\ 3\ 4\ 5}{2\ 4\ 1\ 3\ 5}
, so
\operatorname{sgn}(I,J)=-1
.

For
\underline{I}=\{1,5\}
and
\underline{J}=\{0,2,7\}
, the map
\pi
is given
by
\binom{0\ 1\ 2\ 5\ 7}{1\ 2\ 3\ 4\ 5}
and so

\pi[I]=\{2,4\}
and
\pi[J]=\{1,3,5\}
, and thus
\operatorname{sgn}(I,J)=-1
.

For
\underline{I}=\{1,3,5,6\}
and
\underline{J}=\{0,2,3,6,7\}
, we have

I^{\prime}=\{1,5\}
and
J^{\prime}=\{0,2,7\}
, so again
\operatorname{sgn}(I,J)=-1
.

6.14 Notation
.

Given
n\geq 2
, for each
0\leq k<n-1
, let
\mathcal{I}_{k}^{n}

denote the collection of all
(k,n-k-1)
-partitions
(I,J)
of
\{0,\dotsc,n-2\}
.
When
2k=n-1
, we include in
\mathcal{I}_{k}^{n}
only sets with

0\in I
(to avoid double counting).

6.15 Remark
.

If
(I^{\prime},J^{\prime})
is obtained from
(I,J)
by switching a pair of
elements between
I
and
J
(while maintaining ascending order),
then
\operatorname{sgn}(I^{\prime},J^{\prime})=-\operatorname{sgn}(I,J)
. As a result, if

\underline{I}\setminus\underline{J}
has cardinality
k
and
\underline{J}\setminus\underline{I}

has cardinality

, then

(6.16)

{}\operatorname{sgn}(I,J)~=~(-1)^{k\cdot\ell}\operatorname{sgn}(J,I).

Finally, if
M=(m_{1},\dotsc,m_{k})
is an ordered
k
-tuple of natural numbers (in
ascending order), denote its underlying set by
\underline{M}
. Conversely,
For any decomposition
\underline{M}\sqcup\underline{N}\sqcup\underline{P}=\{0,\dotsc,n-1\}
, the
sign of corresponding three-fold shuffle
(M,N,P)
satisfies:

(6.17)

{}\operatorname{sgn}(M,N\sqcup P)\cdot\operatorname{sgn}(N,P)~=~\operatorname{sgn}(M,N,P)~=~\operatorname{sgn}(M\sqcup N,P)\cdot\operatorname{sgn}(M,N)~.
