Inverse Source Problem for Acoustically- Modulated Electromagnetic Waves
Wei Li, John C. Schotland, Yang Yang, Yimin Zhong

Abstract
. We propose a method to reconstruct the electrical current density from acoustically-modulated boundary measurements of time-harmonic electromagnetic fields. We show that the current can be uniquely reconstructed with Lipschitz stability. We also report numerical simulations to illustrate the analytical results.



1.
Introduction

The inverse source problem for the Maxwell equations is of fundamental interest and considerable practical importance, with applications ranging from geophysics to biomedical imaging
[

It is well known that this problem is underdetermined and does not admit a unique solution, due to the existence of so-called nonradiating sources
[
16
]
.
However, if the source is spatially localized or some other a priori information is available, it is often possible to characterize the source to some extent. Such a method is applied to the localization of low-frequency electric and magnetic signals originating from current sources in the brain or heart
[
30
]
.

In this paper, we propose an alternative approach to the electromagnetic inverse source problem. In this approach, which is an extension of the authors’ previous work on the acousto-electric inverse source problem for static fields
[
26
]
, a wavefield is used to control the material properties of a medium of interest, which is then probed by a second wavefield. Also see related work on hybrid imaging

. Here the electric current density as well as the conductivity, electric permittivity and magnetic permeability are spatially modulated by an acoustic wave. In this manner, we find that it is possible to uniquely recover the current density from boundary measurements of the fields with Lipschitz stability.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. In Section 2 we introduce a model for the acoustic modulation of the current density and the material parameters.
In Sections 3 and 4 this model is used to formulate the inverse source problem and thereby derive an internal functional from which the source may be recovered.
Numerically simulated reconstructions are given in Section 5. Finally, our conclusions are presented in Section 6.

2.
Model

We begin by developing a simple model for acoustic modulation of the electrical current density and material parameters, following the approach of
[
26
]
.
We begin by considering
the time-harmonic Maxwell equations in a bounded domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
:

(1)

\displaystyle i\omega\varepsilon\,\mathbf{E}+\nabla\times\mathbf{H}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{J}+\sigma\mathbf{E}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\text{in }\quad\Omega,

\displaystyle-i\omega\mu\mathbf{H}+\nabla\times\mathbf{E}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\quad\quad\text{in }\quad\Omega\ .

We also impose the impedance boundary condition

(2)

\mathbf{H}\times\hat{\mathbf{n}}-\lambda(\hat{\mathbf{n}}\times\mathbf{E})\times\hat{\mathbf{n}}=0\quad\quad\text{on }\quad\partial\Omega\ ,

which arises since
\Omega
is taken to be enclosed by a good conductor.
Here the vector functions
\mathbf{J}
,
\mathbf{E}
and
\mathbf{H}
are the current density, the electric field, and the magnetic field, respectively. The scalar functions
\varepsilon
,
\mu
,
\sigma
and
\lambda
are the electric permittivity, magnetic permeability, conductivity, and surface impedance, respectively. The vector
\hat{\mathbf{n}}
is the outward unit outward normal to
\Omega
and
\omega
is a fixed frequency. Note that in the above, we do not write the equations governing the divergence of
\mathbf{E}
and
\mathbf{H}
which are not needed in what follows.

The inverse source problem is to reconstruct the source
\mathbf{J}
from boundary measurements, assuming that the coefficients
\mu
,
\varepsilon
,
\sigma
,
\lambda
are known. A typical measurement is the tangential electric field on the boundary:

(3)

\mathbf{g}:=(\hat{\mathbf{n}}\times\mathbf{E})\times\hat{\mathbf{n}}|_{\partial\Omega}.

This problem does not have a unique solution
[
16
]
. That is, distinct sources may give rise to the same boundary measurements.

Remark 2.1
.

An alternative measurement is
\mathbf{h}:=\hat{\mathbf{n}}\times\mathbf{H}|_{\partial\Omega}
. Knowledge of
\mathbf{g}
is equivalent to knowledge of
\mathbf{h}
when the impedance boundary condition (
2
) is taken into account, since

\mathbf{h}=-\lambda\mathbf{g}
on
\partial\Omega
.

We now examine the effect of acoustic modulation. Following
[
26
,
6
]
, we consider a system of charge carriers in a fluid, in which a small-amplitude acoustic plane wave propagates. It follows that the current density
\mathbf{J}_{\delta}
is modulated according to

(4)

\displaystyle\mathbf{J}_{\delta}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{J}(1+\delta\cos(\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}+\varphi)),

where
\mathbf{J}
is the conductivity in the absence of the acoustic wave,
\delta\ll 1
is a small parameter that is proportional to the acoustic pressure,
\mathbf{k}
is the wave vector of the acoustic wave and
\varphi
is its phase. Likewise, the conductivity
\sigma_{\delta}
and permittivity
\varepsilon_{\delta}
are also modulated:

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{\delta}

\displaystyle=\varepsilon(1+\delta\gamma_{\varepsilon}\cos(\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}+\varphi)),

\displaystyle\sigma_{\delta}

\displaystyle=\sigma(1+\delta\gamma_{\sigma}\cos(\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}+\varphi)),

where
\sigma
and
\varepsilon
are the unmodulated conductivity and permittivity, and the constants
\gamma_{\varepsilon},\gamma_{\sigma}
are known as the elasto-electric constants.
For simplicity we assume that the impedance
\lambda
is not affected by the acoustic modulation. It follows that the modulated electric and magnetic fields

\mathbf{E}_{\delta}
and
\mathbf{H}_{\delta}
satisfy the modified Maxwell equations

(5)

\displaystyle i\omega\varepsilon_{\delta}\,\mathbf{E}_{\delta}+\nabla\times\mathbf{H}_{\delta}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{J}_{\delta}+\sigma_{\delta}\mathbf{E}_{\delta}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\text{in }\quad\Omega,

\displaystyle-i\omega\mu\mathbf{H}_{\delta}+\nabla\times\mathbf{E}_{\delta}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\quad\quad\text{in }\quad\Omega,

together with the boundary condition

(6)

\mathbf{H}_{\delta}\times\hat{\mathbf{n}}-\lambda(\hat{\mathbf{n}}\times\mathbf{E}_{\delta})\times\hat{\mathbf{n}}=0\quad\quad\text{on }\quad\partial\Omega.

The corresponding boundary measurement becomes

(7)

\mathbf{g}_{\delta}:=(\hat{\mathbf{n}}\times\mathbf{E}_{\delta})\times\hat{\mathbf{n}}|_{\partial\Omega}.

3.
Internal Functional

In this section, we derive the internal functional from boundary measurements of the electric field. We also introduce the necessary function spaces and specify certain technical requirements on the conductivity and permittivity.

3.1.
Function Spaces

We will use the following standard spaces to discuss the wellposedness of the Maxwell’s equations
[
11
]
. Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
be an open bounded set with a
C^{1,1}
boundary, and

H(\mathrm{curl},\Omega)=\left\{\mathbf{u}\in(L^{2}(\Omega))^{3}:\nabla\times\mathbf{u}\in(L^{2}(\Omega))^{3}\right\}.

The norm on
H(\mathrm{curl},\Omega)
is given by

\|u\|_{H(\mathrm{curl},\Omega)}=\big(\|\mathbf{u}\|^{2}_{(L^{2}(\Omega))^{3}}+\|\nabla\times\mathbf{u}\|^{2}_{(L^{2}(\Omega))^{3}}\big)^{1/2}.

The two tangential trace maps
\Gamma_{\tau}
and
\Pi_{\tau}
have the following definitions

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\tau}:H(\mathrm{curl},\Omega)

\displaystyle\rightarrow H^{-1/2}(\text{div},\partial\Omega),

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}

\displaystyle\mapsto\hat{\mathbf{n}}\times\mathbf{u}|_{\partial\Omega}

and

\displaystyle\Pi_{\tau}:H(\mathrm{curl},\Omega)

\displaystyle\rightarrow H^{-1/2}(\text{curl},\partial\Omega),

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}

\displaystyle\mapsto(\hat{\mathbf{n}}\times\mathbf{u})\times\hat{\mathbf{n}}|_{\partial\Omega},

where

H^{-1/2}(\text{div},\partial\Omega)=\left\{\mathbf{u}\in(H^{-1/2}(\partial\Omega))^{3}:\text{div}_{\partial\Omega}\mathbf{u}\in H^{-1/2}(\partial\Omega)\right\},

and

H^{-1/2}(\text{curl},\partial\Omega)=\left\{\mathbf{u}\in(H^{-1/2}(\partial\Omega))^{3}:\text{curl}_{\partial\Omega}\mathbf{u}\in H^{-1/2}(\partial\Omega)\right\}.

Here
\text{div}_{\partial\Omega}
is the surface divergence and
\text{curl}_{\partial\Omega}
is the surface curl. The two spaces
H^{-1/2}(\text{div},\partial\Omega)
and
H^{-1/2}(\text{curl},\partial\Omega)
are dual to each other.
To handle the impedance boundary condition, we define the tangential trace of a vector field

(8)

\bm{\phi}_{T}:=\Pi_{\tau}(\bm{\phi})=(\hat{\mathbf{n}}\times\bm{\phi})\times\hat{\mathbf{n}}|_{\partial\Omega},

and the space

X=\left\{\mathbf{u}\in H(\mathrm{curl},\Omega):u_{T}\in(L^{2}(\partial\Omega))^{3}\right\}.

The norm on
X
is

\|\mathbf{u}\|_{X}^{2}=\|\mathbf{u}\|_{H(\mathrm{curl},\Omega)}^{2}+\|\mathbf{u}_{T}\|_{(L^{2}(\partial\Omega))^{3}}^{2}.

We denote the
(L^{2}(\Omega))^{3}
-inner product by

(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})_{(L^{2}(\Omega))^{3}}:=\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}^{*}d{\bf x},\quad\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\in(L^{2}(\Omega))^{3},

and the
(L^{2}(\partial\Omega))^{3}
inner product by

\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\rangle_{(L^{2}(\partial\Omega))^{3}}:=\int_{\partial\Omega}\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}^{*}d{\bf x},\quad\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\in(L^{2}(\partial\Omega))^{3},

where
*
denotes the complex conjugate.
We denote the dual paring of
\mathbf{u}\in H^{-1/2}(\text{div},\partial\Omega)
and
\mathbf{v}\in H^{-1/2}(\text{curl},\partial\Omega)
by

\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\rangle
.

3.2.
Assumptions and Weak Formulation

We will make the following assumptions throughout this paper.

A-1.

The domain
\Omega
is an open bounded connected domain in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
with
C^{1,1}
boundary.

A-2.

The medium is nonmagnetic with
\mu=\mu_{0}
in
\Omega
, where
\mu_{0}
is the magnetic permeability in vacuum. The coefficients
\varepsilon
and
\sigma
are real piecewise
H^{3}(\Omega)
functions.

A-3.

There exists positive constants
K_{1}
and
K_{2}
, such that

(9)

K_{1}>\varepsilon,\lambda>K_{2}>0,\quad K_{1}>\sigma\geq 0,

and the conductivity
\sigma
is nonzero.

A-4.

The source
\mathbf{J}
is an
(L^{2}(\Omega))^{3}
vector field and is compactly supported in
\Omega
.

Remark:
We conclude from A-2 that
\varepsilon
and
\sigma
are piecewise
C^{1}
by the Sobolev embedding theorem. We conclude from A-3 that
K_{1}>\varepsilon_{\delta}>K_{2}>0
and
\sigma_{\delta}\geq 0
, so long as
\delta
is sufficiently small.

The modulated Maxwell equations (
5
) and the impedance boundary condition (
6
) can be written in terms of only the electric field:

(10)

\displaystyle\nabla\times\nabla\times\mathbf{E}_{\delta}-\mu(\omega^{2}\varepsilon_{\delta}+i\omega\sigma_{\delta})\mathbf{E}_{\delta}=i\omega\mu\mathbf{J}_{\delta}\quad\text{in }\quad\Omega,

which is subject to the impedance boundary condition

(11)

\left(\frac{1}{\mu}\nabla\times\mathbf{E}_{\delta}\right)\times\hat{\mathbf{n}}-i\omega\lambda(\hat{\mathbf{n}}\times\mathbf{E}_{\delta})\times\hat{\mathbf{n}}=0\quad\text{on }\quad\partial\Omega.

We say
\mathbf{E}_{\delta}\in X
is a weak solution of (
10
) obeying the impedance boundary condition (
11
) if for all
\bm{\phi}\in X
,

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{\mu}\nabla\times\mathbf{E}_{\delta},\nabla\times\bm{\phi}\right)_{(L^{2}(\Omega))^{3}}-\left((\omega^{2}\varepsilon+i\omega\sigma)\mathbf{E}_{\delta},\bm{\phi}\right)_{(L^{2}(\Omega))^{3}}-i\omega\langle\lambda\mathbf{E}_{\delta T},\bm{\phi}_{T}\rangle

(12)

\displaystyle=i\omega\left(\mathbf{J},\bm{\phi}\right)_{(L^{2}(\Omega))^{3}}.

It follows from Assumptions A1-A4, that the weak solution
\mathbf{E}_{\delta}\in X
exists and is unique
[
27
]
.

3.3.
Internal Functional

We now derive the internal functional for both classical and weak solutions.
To proceed, we consider the fields
\mathbf{F}
and
\mathbf{G}
which obey the Maxwell equations without sources:

(13)

\displaystyle i\omega\varepsilon\,\mathbf{F}^{*}+\nabla\times\mathbf{G}^{*}

\displaystyle=\sigma\mathbf{F}^{*}\quad\text{in }\quad\Omega,

\displaystyle-i\omega\mu\mathbf{G}^{*}+\nabla\times\mathbf{F}^{*}

\displaystyle=0\quad\text{in }\quad\Omega,

along with the impedance boundary condition

\mathbf{G}^{*}\times\hat{\mathbf{n}}-\lambda(\hat{\mathbf{n}}\times\mathbf{F}^{*})\times\hat{\mathbf{n}}=\mathfrak{g}\quad\text{on }\quad\partial\Omega,

where
\mathfrak{g}\in(L^{2}(\partial\Omega))^{3}
.
Equivalently,

(14)

\displaystyle\nabla\times\nabla\times\mathbf{F}^{*}-\mu(\omega^{2}\varepsilon+i\omega\sigma)\mathbf{F}^{*}=0\quad\text{in }\quad\Omega

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{i{\omega}\mu}\nabla\times\mathbf{F}^{*}\right)\times\hat{\mathbf{n}}-\lambda(\hat{\mathbf{n}}\times\mathbf{F}^{*})\times\hat{\mathbf{n}}=\mathfrak{g}\quad\text{on }\quad\partial\Omega.

Note that (
13
) are explicitly solvable, since the required coefficients are known.
Next, we take the inner product of (
5
) with
\mathbf{F}^{*}
, the inner product of (
14
) with
\mathbf{E}_{\delta}
, and then subtract to obtain

\displaystyle\nabla\times\nabla\times\mathbf{E}_{\delta}\cdot\mathbf{F}^{*}-\nabla\times\nabla\times\mathbf{F}^{*}\cdot\mathbf{E}_{\delta}

\displaystyle=\mu[\omega^{2}(\varepsilon_{\delta}-\varepsilon)+i\omega(\sigma_{\delta}-\sigma)]\mathbf{F}^{*}\cdot\mathbf{E}_{\delta}+i\mu\omega\mathbf{J}_{\delta}\cdot\mathbf{F}^{*}.

Integrating the above result over
\Omega
and using the vector identity
(\nabla\times\mathbf{A})\cdot\mathbf{B}=\nabla\cdot\left(\mathbf{A}\times\mathbf{B}\right)+(\nabla\times\mathbf{B})\cdot\mathbf{A}
, we find that

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\left[\nabla\cdot\left(\frac{1}{\mu}\nabla\times\mathbf{E}_{\delta}\times\mathbf{F}^{*}\right)+\left(\nabla\times\mathbf{F}^{*}\right)\cdot\left(\frac{1}{\mu}\nabla\times\mathbf{E}_{\delta}\right)\right]d{\bf x}

\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega}\left[\nabla\cdot\left(\frac{1}{\mu}\nabla\times\mathbf{F}^{*}\times\mathbf{E}_{\delta}\right)+\left(\nabla\times\mathbf{E}_{\delta}\right)\cdot\left(\frac{1}{\mu}\nabla\times\mathbf{F}^{*}\right)\right]d{\bf x}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}[\omega^{2}(\varepsilon_{\delta}-\varepsilon)+i\omega(\sigma_{\delta}-\sigma)]\mathbf{F}^{*}\cdot\mathbf{E}_{\delta}d{\bf x}+i\omega\mathbf{J}_{\delta}\cdot\mathbf{F}^{*}

We now integrate by parts the divergence terms, which using the relations
\frac{1}{\mu}\nabla\times\mathbf{E}_{\delta}=i\omega\mathbf{H}_{\delta}
and
\frac{1}{\mu}\nabla\times\mathbf{F}^{*}=i\omega\mathbf{G}^{*}
yields

\displaystyle i\omega\int_{\partial\Omega}\hat{\mathbf{n}}\cdot(\mathbf{H}_{\delta}\times\mathbf{F}^{*})d{\bf x}-i\omega\int_{\partial\Omega}\hat{\mathbf{n}}\cdot(\mathbf{G}^{*}\times\mathbf{E}_{\delta})d{\bf x}

(15)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}[\omega^{2}(\varepsilon_{\delta}-\varepsilon)+i\omega(\sigma_{\delta}-\sigma)]\mathbf{F}^{*}\cdot\mathbf{E}_{\delta}d{\bf x}+i\omega\mathbf{J}_{\delta}\cdot\mathbf{F}^{*}.

Note that the boundary integral only depends on the tangential components of the fields
\mathbf{H}_{\delta}
,
\mathbf{E}_{\delta}
,
\mathbf{F}
and
\mathbf{G}
, which are known from the boundary measurements (
7
). Therefore, the left-hand side can be determined from experiment. For the right-hand side, we consider the asymptotic expansion in the small quantity
\delta
. The
O(1)
term is

i\omega\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{J}_{\delta}\cdot\mathbf{F}^{*}.

The
O(\delta
) term is of the form

(16)

\int_{\Omega}\left[\left(\omega^{2}\varepsilon\gamma_{\varepsilon}+i\omega\sigma\gamma_{\sigma}\right)\mathbf{F}^{*}\cdot\mathbf{E}+i\omega\gamma_{J}\mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{F}^{*}\right]\cos(\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}+\varphi))d{\bf x}.

Varying
\mathbf{k}
and
\varphi
in (
16
), and performing the inverse Fourier transform, we obtain the internal functional

(17)

Q:=\left(\omega^{2}\varepsilon\gamma_{\varepsilon}+i\omega\sigma\gamma_{\sigma}\right)\mathbf{F}^{*}\cdot\mathbf{E}+i\omega\gamma_{J}\mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{F}^{*},

which is known at every point in
\Omega
.

We make the following hypothesis to extract more information from the internal function (
17
):

Hypothesis 3.1
.

There exists a finite open cover
\left\{\Omega_{\alpha}\right\}_{\alpha\in\Lambda}
of
\Omega
, such that for each
\alpha\in\Lambda
, there exist three solutions to (
14
) in
\Omega
, denoted
\mathbf{F}_{1\alpha}^{*}
,
\mathbf{F}_{2\alpha}^{*}
and
\mathbf{F}_{3\alpha}^{*}
, that are linearly independent on
X_{\alpha}
.

The hypothesis means that, in each
\Omega_{\alpha}
, we can form the non-singular matrix
[\mathbf{F}^{*}_{1\alpha},\mathbf{F}^{*}_{2\alpha},\mathbf{F}^{*}_{3\alpha}]
, where
\mathbf{F}^{*}_{j\alpha}
is the
j
th column,
j=1,2,3
. Let
Q_{j\alpha}
be the internal functional defined as in (
17
), with
\mathbf{F}^{*}
replaced by
\mathbf{F}_{j\alpha}^{*}
. Given the row vector
[Q_{1\alpha},Q_{2\alpha},Q_{3\alpha}]
, we have

[Q_{1\alpha},Q_{2\alpha},Q_{3\alpha}]^{T}=[\mathbf{F}_{1\alpha}^{*},\mathbf{F}_{2\alpha}^{*},\mathbf{F}_{3\alpha}^{*}]^{T}\left[\left(\omega^{2}\varepsilon\gamma_{\varepsilon}+i\omega\sigma\gamma_{\sigma}\right)\mathbf{E}+i\omega\gamma_{J}\mathbf{J}\right],\quad\text{ in }\Omega_{\alpha}

where we view
\mathbf{E}
and
\mathbf{J}
as column vectors, and
T
denotes the transpose.
Therefore, if we define
\mathbf{Q}\in(L^{2}(\Omega))^{3}
by specifying its restrictions according to

\mathbf{Q}|_{\Omega_{\alpha}}:=[\mathbf{F}_{1\alpha}^{*},\mathbf{F}_{2\alpha}^{*},\mathbf{F}_{3\alpha}^{*}]^{-T}[Q_{1\alpha},Q_{2\alpha},Q_{3\alpha}]^{T},

then
\mathbf{Q}
is well-defined since both
\mathbf{E}
and
\mathbf{J}
are global vector fields over
\Omega
, and we have

(18)

\mathbf{Q}=i\omega\gamma_{J}\mathbf{J}+\left(\omega^{2}\varepsilon\gamma_{\varepsilon}+i\omega\sigma\gamma_{\sigma}\right)\mathbf{E}.

Note that we view
\mathbf{Q}
as a vector-valued internal functional.

4.
Inverse Problem and Internal Functional

It follows from the above discussion that the inverse problem consists of recovering the source current
\mathbf{J}
from the internal functional
\mathbf{Q}
. In this section we will derive a reconstruction procedure that uniquely recovers
\mathbf{J}
with Lipschitz stability. The analysis depends critically on whether the constant
\gamma_{J}
vanishes.

4.1.
Case I:
\gamma_{J}=0
.

In this situation, the equality (
18
) does not involve
\mathbf{J}
directly.

Proposition 4.1
.

Suppose the assumptions A1-A4 and the hypothesis (
3.1
) hold. If
\gamma_{J}=0
, then we have the following two subcases:

(I.1)

If
\Omega\subseteq\rm{supp}\,(\omega^{2}\varepsilon\gamma_{\varepsilon}+i\omega\sigma\gamma_{\sigma})
, then the source
\mathbf{J}
is uniquely determined with the stability estimate

\|\mathbf{J}-\tilde{\mathbf{J}}\|_{X^{*}}\leq C\Big\|\frac{\mathbf{Q}-\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}}{\omega^{2}\varepsilon\gamma_{\varepsilon}+i\omega\sigma\gamma_{\sigma}}\Big\|_{X},

for some constant
C>0
independent of
\mathbf{J},\tilde{\mathbf{J}}
.

(I.2)

If
\Omega\not\subseteq\rm{supp}\,(\omega^{2}\varepsilon\gamma_{\varepsilon}+i\omega\sigma\gamma_{\sigma})
, then the source
\mathbf{J}
cannot be uniquely determined.

Moreover, whenever
\mathbf{J}
is uniquely determined, there are explicit reconstruction procedures.

Proof.

If
\Omega\subseteq\rm{supp}\,(\omega^{2}\varepsilon\gamma_{\varepsilon}+i\omega\sigma\gamma_{\sigma})
,
then (
18
) implies

\mathbf{E}=\frac{\mathbf{Q}}{\omega^{2}\varepsilon\gamma_{\varepsilon}+i\omega\sigma\gamma_{\sigma}}.

This uniquely determines the weak solution
\mathbf{E}\in X
everywhere in
\Omega
. Consequently,
\mathbf{H}
and
\mathbf{J}
are also uniquely determined via the Maxwell’s equations (
1
). Note that all these procedures are constructive: given
\mathbf{Q}
, we compute
\mathbf{E}
from the above equality, and then
\mathbf{J}
from (
1
).

The stability can be derived as follows. If there is another source
\tilde{\mathbf{J}}
with corresponding electric field
\tilde{\mathbf{E}}
, and vector internal functional
\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}
defined as in (
18
), then
\mathbf{E}-\tilde{\mathbf{E}}
is a weak solution of the Maxwell equations. That is,

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{\mu}\nabla\times(\mathbf{E}-\tilde{\mathbf{E}}),\nabla\times\bm{\phi}\right)_{(L^{2}(\Omega))^{3}}-\left((\omega^{2}\varepsilon+i\omega\sigma)(\mathbf{E}-\tilde{\mathbf{E}}),\bm{\phi}\right)_{(L^{2}(\Omega))^{3}}

\displaystyle-i\omega\langle\lambda(\mathbf{E}-\tilde{\mathbf{E}})_{T},\bm{\phi}_{T}\rangle=i\omega\left(\mathbf{J}-\tilde{\mathbf{J}},\bm{\phi}\right)_{(L^{2}(\Omega))^{3}}

for all
\bm{\phi}\in X
. As the coefficients in this weak formulation are all bounded, there exists a constant
C>0
such that

\left|\omega\left(\mathbf{J}-\tilde{\mathbf{J}},\bm{\phi}\right)\right|\leq C\|\mathbf{E}-\tilde{\mathbf{E}}\|_{X}\;\|\phi\|_{X}.

We deduce that

\|\mathbf{J}-\tilde{\mathbf{J}}\|_{X^{*}}\leq C\|\mathbf{E}-\tilde{\mathbf{E}}\|_{X}=C\left\|\frac{\mathbf{Q}-\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}}{\omega^{2}\varepsilon\gamma_{\varepsilon}+i\omega\sigma\gamma_{\sigma}}\right\|_{X}.

If
\Omega\nsubseteq\rm{supp}\,(\omega^{2}\varepsilon\gamma_{\varepsilon}+i\omega\sigma\gamma_{\sigma})
, there exists an open set
D\subseteq\Omega\backslash\rm{supp}\,(\omega^{2}\varepsilon\gamma_{\varepsilon}+i\omega\sigma\gamma_{\sigma})
. For any compactly supported smooth function

\phi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(D)
, if
(\mathbf{E},\mathbf{H})
solves (
1
), then
(\mathbf{E}+\nabla\phi,\mathbf{H})
solves (
1
) with
\mathbf{J}
replaced by
\mathbf{J}+(i\omega\varepsilon-\sigma)\nabla\phi
. Moreover, since
(\mathbf{E}+\nabla\phi,\mathbf{H})|_{\partial\Omega}=(\mathbf{E},\mathbf{H})
, these two pairs both satisfy the boundary condition (
2
) and produce identical measurement (
3
). This means that sources of the form

\mathbf{J}_{\phi}:=(i\omega\varepsilon-\sigma)\nabla\phi
are non-radiating. Thus the source
\mathbf{J}
cannot be uniquely determined from the boundary measurement (
3
).
∎

4.1.1.
Increased Regularity

The stability estimate for the subcase
\gamma_{J}=0
and
\Omega\subseteq\rm{supp}\,(\omega^{2}\varepsilon\gamma_{\varepsilon}+i\omega\sigma\gamma_{\sigma})

is in terms of the
X^{*}
norm, which follows because
\mathbf{J}
was obtained from
\mathbf{E}
using a weak formulation. When the reconstructed
\mathbf{E}
and
\mathbf{H}
are smooth enough, for example, when
\mathbf{E}\in(H^{2}(\Omega))^{3}
, we can utilize the strong formulation to control
\mathbf{J}
in
(L^{2}(\Omega))^{3}
in terms of the higher order derivatives of the internal data.

Proposition 4.2
.

Suppose the assumptions A1-A4 and the hypothesis (
3.1
) hold. Suppose, in addition, that
\varepsilon,\sigma\in C^{1,1}(\Omega)
.
If
\gamma_{J}=0
and
\Omega\subseteq\rm{supp}\,(\omega^{2}\varepsilon\gamma_{\varepsilon}+i\omega\sigma\gamma_{\sigma})
, then the following stability estimate holds for any two compactly supported sources
\mathbf{J},\tilde{\mathbf{J}}\in(H^{2}(\Omega))^{3}
:

\|\mathbf{J}-\tilde{\mathbf{J}}\|_{(L^{2}(\Omega))^{3}}\leq C\left\|\frac{\mathbf{Q}-\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}}{\omega^{2}\varepsilon\gamma_{\varepsilon}+i\omega\sigma\gamma_{\sigma}}\right\|_{(H^{2}(\Omega_{1}))^{3}}.

Here
\Omega_{1}
is an open set compactly contained in
\Omega
such that
\rm{supp}\,{\mathbf{J}}\subset\Omega_{1}
and
\rm{supp}\,{\tilde{\mathbf{J}}}\subset\Omega_{1}
, and the constant
C>0
is independent of
\mathbf{J},\tilde{\mathbf{J}}
.

Proof.

Define

\mathbf{u}:=\mathbf{E}-\tilde{\mathbf{E}}=\frac{\mathbf{Q}-\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}}{\omega^{2}\varepsilon\gamma_{\varepsilon}+i\omega\sigma\gamma_{\sigma}}.

Then
\mathbf{u}
solves

(19)

\nabla\times\frac{1}{\mu}\nabla\times\mathbf{u}-(\omega^{2}\varepsilon+i\omega\sigma)\mathbf{u}=i\omega(\mathbf{J}-\tilde{\mathbf{J}}).

The following stability estimate is immediate:

\|\mathbf{J}-\tilde{\mathbf{J}}\|_{(L^{2}(\Omega))^{3})}=\|\mathbf{J}-\tilde{\mathbf{J}}\|_{(L^{2}(\Omega_{1}))^{3}}\leq C\|\mathbf{u}\|_{(H^{2}(\Omega_{1}))^{3}}=C\left|\frac{\mathbf{Q}-\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}}{\omega^{2}\varepsilon\gamma_{\varepsilon}+i\omega\sigma\gamma_{\sigma}}\right\|_{(H^{2}(\Omega_{1}))^{3}}.

It remains to show that the quantity

\|\mathbf{u}\|_{(H^{2}(\Omega_{1}))^{3}}=\left\|\frac{\mathbf{Q}-\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}}{\omega^{2}\varepsilon\gamma_{\varepsilon}+i\omega\sigma\gamma_{\sigma}}\right\|_{H^{2}(\Omega_{1})}

is finite. To proceed we will employ an interior regularity estimate for elliptic equations.
Denote by
a:=\omega^{2}\varepsilon>0
,
b:=i\omega\sigma\geq 0
,
\mathbf{f}:=i\omega(\mathbf{J}-\tilde{\mathbf{J}})
. Then take the divergence of (
19
) to obtain

(20)

\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}=-\frac{\nabla(a+ib)\cdot\mathbf{u}+\nabla\cdot\mathbf{f}}{a+ib}.

Using the identity
\nabla\times\nabla\times\mathbf{u}=\nabla(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u})-\Delta\mathbf{u}
, we obtain the following elliptic system

\Delta\mathbf{u}=-\nabla\left[\frac{\nabla(a+ib)\cdot\mathbf{u}+\nabla\cdot\mathbf{f}}{a+ib}\right]-\mu(a+ib)\mathbf{u}-\mu\mathbf{f}.

For each component of
\mathbf{u}
, the left hand side of the above defines a second order elliptic operator with constant coefficients, so we can apply an interior regularity estimate
[
17
, Lemma 6.32]
. Since
a
is bounded away from zero and
a,b
are piecewise
C^{1,1}(\Omega)
, the following quantities are all bounded in
\overline{\Omega}
:

|a+ib|,\ \left|\nabla\cdot\frac{\nabla(a+ib)}{a+ib}\right|,\ \left|\frac{\nabla(a+ib)}{a+ib}\right|,\ \left|\nabla\frac{1}{a+ib}\right|,\ \left|\frac{1}{a+ib}\right|.

Thus

\displaystyle\nabla\left(\frac{\nabla(a+ib)\cdot\mathbf{u}+\nabla\cdot\mathbf{f}}{a+ib}\right)+\mu(a+ib)\mathbf{u}+\mu\mathbf{f}\in(H_{\text{loc}}^{-1}(\Omega))^{3}.

Combing this with the fact that
\mathbf{u}\in(L^{2}(\Omega))^{3}
, we obtain from
[
17
, Lemma 6.32]
that
\mathbf{u}\in(H_{\text{loc}}^{1}(\Omega))^{3}
.
This increased regularity implies that

\displaystyle\nabla\left[\frac{\nabla(a+ib)\cdot\mathbf{u}+\nabla\cdot\mathbf{f}}{a+ib}\right]+

\displaystyle\mu(a+ib)\mathbf{u}+\mu\mathbf{f}\in{(L_{\text{loc}}^{2}(\Omega))^{3}}.

Applying
[
17
, Lemma 6.32]
again, we obtain that
\mathbf{u}\in(H_{\text{loc}}^{2}(\Omega))^{3}
.
Next, choose a smooth cutoff function
\chi
that is compactly supported in
\Omega
and equal to one on
\Omega_{1}
. We find that

\|\mathbf{u}\|_{(H^{2}(\Omega_{1}))^{3}}\leq\|\chi\mathbf{u}\|_{(H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3}))^{3}}<\infty.

Thus we obtain that
\mathbf{u}\in H^{2}(\Omega_{1})
.
∎

This subcase corresponds to

We now take the inner product of (

\mathbf{u}^{*}
and use the vector identity
(\nabla\times\mathbf{A})\cdot\mathbf{B}=\nabla\cdot(\mathbf{A}\times\mathbf{B})+(\nabla\times\mathbf{B})\cdot\mathbf{A}
to integrate by parts:

\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Omega}\left[\frac{1}{\mu}(\nabla\times\mathbf{u})\times\mathbf{u}^{*}\right]\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}\,d{\bf x}+\int_{\Omega}\left[\frac{1}{\mu}|\nabla\times\mathbf{u}|^{2}-(a+ib)|\mathbf{u}|^{2}\right]\,d{\bf x}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\gamma_{J}}\int_{\Omega}(\mathbf{Q}-\tilde{\mathbf{Q}})\cdot\mathbf{u}^{*}\,d{\bf x}.

For the boundary integral, we apply the vector triple product identity

to obtain

\left[\frac{1}{\mu}(\nabla\times\mathbf{u})\times\mathbf{u}^{*}\right]\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}=\mathbf{u}_{T}^{*}\cdot\left[\hat{\mathbf{n}}\times\left(\frac{1}{\mu}\nabla\times\mathbf{u}\right)\right]\quad\text{ on }\partial\Omega.

Here
\mathbf{u}_{T}=(\hat{\mathbf{n}}\times\mathbf{u})\times\hat{\mathbf{n}}|_{\partial\Omega},

as defined in (

From (

we obtain
\hat{\mathbf{n}}\times(\frac{1}{\mu}\nabla\times\mathbf{u})=-i\omega\lambda\mathbf{u}_{T}
.
Thus the boundary integrand becomes

\left[(\frac{1}{\mu}\nabla\times\mathbf{u})\times\mathbf{u}^{*}\right]\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}=\mathbf{u}_{T}^{*}\cdot\left[\hat{\mathbf{n}}\times\left(\frac{1}{\mu}\nabla\times\mathbf{u}\right)\right]=-i\omega\lambda|\mathbf{u}_{T}|^{2}=-i\omega\lambda|\mathbf{g}-\tilde{\mathbf{g}}|^{2}.

Therefore, separating the real and imaginary parts of (
4.2.2

(27)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\mathrm{Re}\,\left[\frac{(\mathbf{Q}-\tilde{\mathbf{Q}})\cdot\mathbf{u}^{*}}{\gamma_{J}}\right]\,d{\bf x},

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Omega}\mathrm{Im}\,\left[\frac{(\mathbf{Q}-\tilde{\mathbf{Q}})\cdot\mathbf{u}^{*}}{\gamma_{J}}\right]\,d{\bf x}.

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{u}\|^{2}_{H(curl,\Omega)}\leq

\displaystyle\leq
