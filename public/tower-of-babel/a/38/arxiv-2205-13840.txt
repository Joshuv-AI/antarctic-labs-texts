Adapting to general quadratic loss via singular value shrinkage
Takeru Matsuda

Abstract
The Gaussian sequence model is a canonical model in nonparametric estimation. In this study, we introduce a multivariate version of the Gaussian sequence model and investigate adaptive estimation over the multivariate Sobolev ellipsoids, where adaptation is not only to unknown smoothness but also to arbitrary quadratic loss. First, we derive an oracle inequality for the singular value shrinkage estimator by Efron and Morris, which is a matrix generalization of the James–Stein estimator. Next, we develop an asymptotically minimax estimator on the multivariate Sobolev ellipsoid for each quadratic loss, which can be viewed as a generalization of Pinsker’s theorem. Then, we show that the blockwise Efron–Morris estimator is exactly adaptive minimax over the multivariate Sobolev ellipsoids under the corresponding quadratic loss. It attains sharp adaptive estimation of any linear combination of the mean sequences simultaneously.

Introduction

Suppose that we observe

\displaystyle y_{i}=\theta_{i}+\varepsilon\xi_{i},\quad i=1,2,\cdots,

where
\varepsilon>0
and
\xi_{i}\sim{\rm N}_{p}(0,I_{p})
are independent
p
-dimensional Gaussian random vectors.
This is a multivariate version of the Gaussian sequence model, which is a canonical model in nonparametric estimation
[
28
,
42
,
43
]
.
We consider estimation of
\theta=(\theta_{i})
under the quadratic loss specified by a
p\times p
positive definite symmetric matrix
Q
:

\displaystyle L_{Q}(\theta,\hat{\theta})=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}(\hat{\theta}_{i}-\theta_{i})^{\top}Q(\hat{\theta}_{i}-\theta_{i}).

Let
R_{Q}(\theta,\hat{\theta})={\rm E}_{\theta}[L_{Q}(\theta,\hat{\theta})]
be the risk function.
For a parameter space
\Theta
, an estimator
\hat{\theta}_{*}
is said to be asymptotically minimax on
\Theta
if

\displaystyle\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}R_{Q}(\theta,\hat{\theta}_{*})\sim\inf_{\hat{\theta}}\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}R_{Q}(\theta,\hat{\theta})

as
\varepsilon\to 0
, where
a\sim b
denotes
a/b\to 1
and
\inf
is taken over all estimators.
For a class of parameter spaces
\mathcal{C}=\{\Theta\}
, an estimator
\hat{\theta}_{*}
is said to be adaptive minimax over
\mathcal{C}
if it is asymptotically minimax on every
\Theta
in
\mathcal{C}
.
Note that we focus on exact minimaxity in this study, which is stronger than minimaxity in terms of the rate of convergence.

The above problem has been well studied in the case of
p=1

[
28
,
6
]
, where we can fix
Q=1
without loss of generality.
It is asymptotically equivalent to various problems such as nonparametric regression
[
4
]
and density estimation
[
34
]
.
In these settings, each
\theta_{i}
corresponds to the coefficient in basis expansion of an unknown function
f
, and smoothness constraints on
f
are translated to domain constraints on
\theta=(\theta_{i})
.
Among them, the Sobolev class is represented by infinite-dimensional ellipsoids under the Fourier expansion.
For such ellipsoids, Pinsker
[
36
]
showed that a linear estimator attains asymptotic minimaxity and derived its concrete form.
Pinsker’s estimator for the Sobolev ellipsoid depends on its smoothness and scale parameters, which are generally unknown.
Thus, estimators that are adaptive minimax over the Sobolev ellipsoids have been developed.
Among them, the blockwise Jame–Stein estimator
[
15
]
partitions the observations into blocks and apply the James–Stein shrinkage to each block.
Adaptive minimaxity of this estimator is proved by using an oracle inequality for the James–Stein estimator, which indicates that the James–Stein estimator attains almost the same risk with the best linear estimator.
More details will be given in Section
2
.

In this study, we generalize the above results to
p\geq 2
, which corresponds to multivariate versions of nonparametic problems such as nonparametric regression with vector response.
Unlike the case of
p=1
, there is an arbitrariness in the choice of the positive definite matrix
Q
in the quadratic loss.
We introduce a multivariate version of the Sobolev ellipsoid, and develop an estimator that attains adaptive minimaxity over the multivariate Sobolev ellipsoids under any choice of
Q
simultaneously.
In this sense, our estimator is adaptive not only to smoothness but also to arbitrary quadratic loss.
Note that, while minimax estimation under arbitrary quadratic loss has been studied in the finite-dimensional (parametric) setting as well
[
2
,
39
]
, these previous studies proposed minimax estimators that depend on
Q
.
In contrast, we develop an estimator that attains adaptive minimaxity under any quadratic loss simultaneously.
Note that applying the blockwise James–Stein estimator for each of the
p
components only attains adaptive minimaxity for diagonal
Q
.

Our estimator is based on the Efron–Morris estimator
[
16
]
, which was originally developed as an empirical Bayes estimator of a normal mean matrix.
Efron and Morris
[
16
]
showed that this estimator is minimax and dominates the maximum likelihood estimator under the Frobenius loss.
The Efron–Morris estimator shrinks the singular values towards zero and can be viewed as a matrix generalization of the James–Stein estimator.
It attains large risk reduction when the truth is close to low rank.
Recently, the Efron–Morris estimator has been shown to dominate the maximum likelihood estimator even under the matrix quadratic loss, which is a matrix-valued loss function suitable for matrix estimation
[
33
]
.
This result implies that the Efron–Morris estimator attains improved estimation of any linear combination of the column vectors simultaneously.
In other words, it is adaptive to arbitrary quadratic loss.
More details will be given in Section
3
.

This paper is organized as follows.
In Section
2
, we briefly review existing results on adaptive estimation in the Gaussian sequence model.
In Section
3
, we introduce the Efron–Morris estimator and derive its oracle inequality.
In Section
4
, we define the multivariate Gaussian sequence model and multivariate Sobolev ellipsoid.
In Section
5
, we derive an asymptotically minimax estimator on the multivariate Sobolev ellipsoid.
In Section
6
, we prove the adaptive minimaxity of the blockwise Efron–Morris estimator over the multivariate Sobolev ellipsoids.
In Section
7
, we present simulation results.
In Section
8
, we give concluding remarks.

Our primary contribution is threefold: (1) We introduce a multivariate version of the Gaussian sequence model and Soboloev ellipsoid, (2) We derive an oracle inequality for the Efron–Morris estimator, (3) We show that the blockwise Efron–Morris estimator attains adaptive minimaxity over the multivariate Sobolev ellipsoids.
To the best of our knowledge, adaptive estimation in the multivariate Gaussian sequence model has not been investigated so far.

Throughout the paper, we write
a\sim b
when
a/b\to 1
.
For a real number
a
, we define
(a)_{+}=\max(0,a)
.
For a symmetric matrix
S\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
that has a spectral decomposition
S=U^{\top}\Lambda U
with
U\in O(p)
and
\Lambda={\rm diag}(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{p})
, we write its projection onto the positive semidefinite cone by
S_{+}=U^{\top}\Lambda_{+}U\succeq O
, where
\Lambda_{+}={\rm diag}((\lambda_{1})_{+},\dots,(\lambda_{p})_{+})
.
For two symmetric matrices
A\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
and
B\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
, we write
A\preceq B
when
B-A
is positive semidefinite (Löwner order).
For a vector-valued function
f:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{p}
, we write each component of
f
as
f_{j}:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
for
j=1,\dots,p
.
For
\beta\in\{1,2,\cdots\}
, we define
f^{(\beta)}:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{p}
by
f^{(\beta)}(x)=(f_{1}^{(\beta)}(x),\dots,f_{p}^{(\beta)}(x))^{\top}
where
f_{j}^{(\beta)}
is the
\beta
-th derivative of
f_{j}
.

2
Preliminaries

2.1
Gaussian sequence model

The Gaussian sequence model
[
28
,
42
]
is defined as

\displaystyle y_{i}=\theta_{i}+\varepsilon\xi_{i},\quad i=1,2,\cdots,

(1)

where
\varepsilon>0
and
\xi_{i}\sim{\rm N}(0,1)
are independent standard Gaussian random variables.
This model is equivalent to the Gaussian white noise model, which is defined by the stochastic differential equation

\displaystyle{\rm d}Y(t)=f(t){\rm d}t+\varepsilon{\rm d}W(t),\quad t\in[0,1],

where
f(t)
is a drift function to be estimated and
W(t)
is the standard Wiener process.
By using an orthonormal basis
\phi=(\phi_{i})
of
L_{2}[0,1]
such as the Fourier basis, let

\displaystyle y_{i}=\int_{0}^{1}\phi_{i}(t){\rm d}Y(t),\quad\theta_{i}=\int_{0}^{1}\phi_{i}(t)f(t){\rm d}t,\quad\xi_{i}=\int_{0}^{1}\phi_{i}(t){\rm d}W(t).

Then,
y=(y_{i})
follows the Gaussian sequence model (
1
).
In addition, the Gaussian nonparametric regression model

\displaystyle y_{i}=f(t_{i})+\xi_{i},\quad i=1,\dots,n,

where
f:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
,
t_{i}=i/n
and
\xi_{i}\sim{\rm N}(0,1)
are independent, is asymptotically equivalent to the Gaussian sequence model (
1
) with
\varepsilon=n^{-1/2}
as
n\to\infty

[
4
]
in the sense of Le Cam’s limits of experiments
[
21
]
.
Asymptotic equivalence to other problems such as density estimation and Poisson processes has been also established
[
34
,
5
]
.
Thus, the Gaussian sequence model (
1
) is a canonical model in nonparametric statistics and many studies have investigated the problem of estimating
\theta=(\theta_{i})
from
y=(y_{i})

[
6
,
28
,
42
]
.

2.2
Sobolev ellipsoid and Pinsker’s theorem

In nonparametric statistics, unknown functions are often assumed to belong to some smoothness class.
Among them, the periodic Sobolev class on
[0,1]
is defined as

\displaystyle W(\beta,L):=\left\{f\in L_{2}[0,1]\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}\right.

\displaystyle f^{(k)}(0)=f^{(k)}(1),\ k=0,\cdots,\beta-1,

\displaystyle\left.f^{(\beta-1)}:{\rm absolutely\ continuous},\ \int_{0}^{1}(f^{(\beta)}(x))^{2}{\rm d}x\leq L^{2}\right\},

where
\beta\in\{1,2,\cdots\}
,
L>0
,
L_{2}[0,1]
is the space of real-valued square-integrable functions on
[0,1]
and
f^{(k)}
denotes the
k
-th order derivative of
f
.
It has a useful representation using the Fourier coefficients as follows
[
28
,
42
]
.

Lemma 2.1
.

For
\theta=(\theta_{i})
, let

\displaystyle f(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\theta_{i}\phi_{i}(x),

where
\phi=(\phi_{i})
is the Fourier basis of
L_{2}[0,1]
given by

\displaystyle\phi_{1}(x)\equiv 1,\ \phi_{2k}(x)=\sqrt{2}\cos(2\pi kx),\ \phi_{2k+1}(x)=\sqrt{2}\sin(2\pi kx),\ k=1,2,\dots.

(2)

Then,
f\in W(\beta,L)
if and only if

\displaystyle\theta\in\Theta(\beta,R):=\left\{\theta=(\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\dots)\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}a_{\beta,i}^{2}\theta_{i}^{2}\leq R\right\},

(3)

where
R:=L^{2}/\pi^{2\beta}
and

\displaystyle a_{\beta,i}:=\begin{cases}i^{\beta},&i:{\rm even},\\
(i-1)^{\beta},&i:{\rm odd}.\end{cases}

(4)

From Lemma
2.1
, the infinite-dimensional ellipsoid
\Theta(\beta,R)
in (
3
) is called the Sobolev ellipsoid for
\beta>0
and
R>0
.

Now, consider the problem of estimating
\theta=(\theta_{i})
from
y=(y_{i})
in the Gaussian sequence model (
1
) under the quadratic loss

\displaystyle L(\theta,\hat{\theta})=\|\hat{\theta}-\theta\|^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}(\hat{\theta}_{i}-\theta_{i})^{2},

(5)

and assume that
\theta
is restricted to the Sobolev ellipsoid
\Theta(\beta,R)
in (
3
).
Pinsker
[
36
]
studied this problem under the asymptotics
\varepsilon\to 0
and derived an asymptotically minimax estimator as follows.

Proposition 2.1
.

(Pinsker’s theorem for Sobolev ellipsoid)
Let
C_{\mathrm{P},i}=(1-a_{\beta,i}\kappa R)_{+}
for
i=1,2,\dots
, where
(a)_{+}=\max(0,a)
and
\kappa>0
is the unique solution of

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{2}\kappa^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}a_{\beta,i}(1-a_{\beta,i}\kappa R^{-1})_{+}=1.

Then, the linear estimator
\hat{\theta}_{\rm P}=(\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{P},i})
with
\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{P},i}=C_{\mathrm{P},i}y_{i}
is asymptotically minimax on the Sobolev ellipsoid
\Theta(\beta,R)
:

\displaystyle\sup_{\theta\in\Theta(\beta,R)}{\rm E}_{\theta}[\|\hat{\theta}_{\rm P}-\theta\|^{2}]\sim\inf_{\hat{\theta}}\sup_{\theta\in\Theta(\beta,R)}{\rm E}_{\theta}[\|\hat{\theta}-\theta\|^{2}]\sim P(\beta,R)\varepsilon^{{4\beta}/{(2\beta+1)}}

as
\varepsilon\to 0
, where
\inf
is taken over all estimators and

\displaystyle P(\beta,R):=(R(2\beta+1))^{1/(2\beta+1)}\left(\frac{\beta}{\beta+1}\right)^{2\beta/(2\beta+1)}.

2.3
Blockwise James–Stein estimator

Pinsker’s estimator in Proposition
2.1
depends on the smoothness parameter
\beta
and scale parameter
R
of the Sobolev ellipsoid
\Theta(\beta,R)
in (
3
), which are difficult to specify in general.
Thus, adaptive minimax estimators over the Sobolev ellipsoids have been developed, which are asymptotically minimax on
\Theta(\beta,R)
for every
\beta
and
R
simultaneously.
Among them, here we focus on the blockwise James–Stein estimator
[
15
,
42
]
.

In estimation of
\mu
from
X\sim{\rm N}_{n}(\mu,I_{n})
under the quadratic loss, the maximum likelihood estimator
\hat{\mu}_{{\rm ML}}=x
is minimax
[
31
]
.
However, Stein
[
38
]
showed that it is inadmissible when
n\geq 3
and James and Stein
[
27
]
proved that the shrinkage estimator

\displaystyle\hat{\mu}_{\rm JS}(x)=\left(1-\frac{n-2}{\|x\|^{2}}\right)x

is minimax and dominates the maximum likelihood estimator:

\displaystyle{\rm E}_{\mu}[\|\hat{\mu}_{{\rm JS}}-\mu\|^{2}]\leq{\rm E}_{\mu}[\|\hat{\mu}_{{\rm ML}}-\mu\|^{2}]=n.

The James–Stein estimator also satisfies the following oracle inequality
[
28
,
42
]
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
\hat{\mu}_{C}=Cx
be the linear estimator of
\mu
for
C\in\mathbb{R}
.
Then,

\displaystyle\min_{C}{\rm E}_{\mu}[\|\hat{\mu}_{C}-\mu\|^{2}]\leq{\rm E}_{\mu}[\|\hat{\mu}_{{\rm JS}}-\mu\|^{2}]\leq\min_{C}{\rm E}_{\mu}[\|\hat{\mu}_{C}-\mu\|^{2}]+2,

(6)

for every
\mu
.

The oracle inequality (
6
) indicates that the James–Stein estimator attains almost the same risk with the best linear estimator.
Then, since Pinsker’s estimator in Proposition
2.1
is a linear estimator, it is expected that an estimator that is asymptotically minimax on every Sobolev ellipsoid can be constructed by using the James–Stein shrinkage.
This idea leads to the blockwise James–Stein estimator for the Gaussian sequence model (
1
) as follows.

Consider a partition of
\{1,2,\dots,N\}
with blocks

\displaystyle B_{j}:=\{l_{j-1}+1,l_{j-1}+2,\dots,l_{j}\},\quad j=1,\dots,J,

where
0=l_{0}<l_{1}<\dots<l_{J}=N
.
The blockwise James–Stein estimator is defined as

\displaystyle(\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{BJS}}(y))_{i}=\begin{cases}y_{i},&i\in B_{j},\ |B_{j}|<3,\\
\left(1-\frac{(|B_{j}|-2)\varepsilon^{2}}{\|y_{(j)}\|^{2}}\right)y_{i},&i\in B_{j},\ |B_{j}|\geq 3,\\
0,&i>N,\end{cases}

(7)

where
\|y_{(j)}\|^{2}:=\sum_{i\in B_{j}}y_{i}^{2}
.
This estimator applies the James–Stein shrinkage to each block
B_{j}
.
In this study, we focus on the weakly geometric blocks defined by
N:=\lfloor\varepsilon^{-2}\rfloor
and

\displaystyle l_{j}:=\lfloor\rho_{\varepsilon}^{-1}(1+\rho_{\varepsilon})^{j}\rfloor,\quad j=1,\dots,J-1,

where
\rho_{\varepsilon}:=(\log(1/\varepsilon))^{-1}
and
J:=\min\{j\mid\rho_{\varepsilon}^{-1}(1+\rho_{\varepsilon})^{j}\geq\varepsilon^{-2}\}
.
The blockwise James–Stein estimator with the weakly geometric blocks attains adaptive minimaxity as follows
[
15
,
42
]
.

Proposition 2.2
.

For every
\beta
and
R
, the blockwise James–Stein estimator
\hat{\theta}_{\rm BJS}
with the weakly geometric blocks is asymptotically minimax on the Sobolev ellipsoid
\Theta(\beta,R)
:

\displaystyle\sup_{\theta\in\Theta(\beta,R)}{\rm E}_{\theta}[\|\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{BJS}}-\theta\|^{2}]\sim\inf_{\hat{\theta}}\sup_{\theta\in\Theta(\beta,R)}{\rm E}_{\theta}[\|\hat{\theta}-\theta\|^{2}]\sim P(\beta,R)\varepsilon^{{4\beta}/{(2\beta+1)}}

as
\varepsilon\to 0
, where
\inf
is taken over all estimators.

3
Efron–Morris estimator and its oracle inequality

3.1
Efron–Morris estimator

Suppose that we have a matrix observation
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
whose entries are independent Gaussian random variables
X_{ij}\sim{\rm N}(M_{ij},1)
, where
n-p-1>0
and
M\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
is an unknown mean matrix. In this setting, Efron and Morris
[
16
]
considered estimation of
M
under the Frobenius loss

\displaystyle l(M,\hat{M})=\|\hat{M}-M\|_{{\rm F}}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{p}(\hat{M}_{ij}-M_{ij})^{2},

and proposed an estimator

\displaystyle\hat{M}_{{\rm EM}}=X(I_{p}-(n-p-1)(X^{\top}X)^{-1}).

(8)

It coincides with the James–Stein estimator when
p=1
.
They showed that
\hat{M}_{{\rm EM}}
is minimax and dominates the maximum likelihood estimator
\hat{M}_{\rm ML}=X
under the Frobenius loss:

\displaystyle{\rm E}_{M}[\|\hat{M}_{\rm EM}-M\|_{{\rm F}}^{2}]\leq{\rm E}_{M}[\|\hat{M}_{\rm ML}-M\|_{{\rm F}}^{2}]=np.

The Efron–Morris estimator
\hat{M}_{{\rm EM}}
can be interpreted as an empirical Bayes estimator, in which an independent prior
{\rm N}_{p}(0,\Sigma)
is put on each row of
M
and the hyperparameter
\Sigma
is estimated from
X
.

Stein
[
38
]
pointed out that
\hat{M}_{{\rm EM}}
does not change the singular vectors but shrinks the singular values of
X
towards zero.
Namely, let
X=U\Sigma V^{\top}
be the singular value decomposition of
X
, where
U^{\top}U=V^{\top}V=I_{p}
and
\Sigma={\rm diag}(\sigma_{1}(X),\dots,\sigma_{p}(X))
.
Then, the singular value decomposition of
\hat{M}_{{\rm EM}}
is given by
\hat{M}_{{\rm EM}}=U\widetilde{\Sigma}V^{\top}
, where
\widetilde{\Sigma}={\rm diag}(\sigma_{1}(\hat{M}_{{\rm EM}}),\dots,\sigma_{p}(\hat{M}_{{\rm EM}}))
and

\displaystyle\sigma_{j}(\hat{M}_{{\rm EM}})=\left(1-\frac{n-p-1}{\sigma_{j}(X)^{2}}\right)\sigma_{j}(X),\quad j=1,\dots,p.

From this property,
\hat{M}_{{\rm EM}}
attains large risk reduction when
M
has small singular values.
In particular,
\hat{M}_{{\rm EM}}
works well when
M
is close to low rank.

Figure
1
compares the Frobenius risk of
\hat{M}_{{\rm EM}}
with that of
\hat{M}_{{\rm JS}}(X)=(1-(np-2)/\|X\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2})X
, which applies the James–Stein shrinkage to the vectorization of
X
.
It indicates that
\hat{M}_{{\rm EM}}
attains constant risk reduction when some singular values of
M
are small, regardless of the magnitude of the other singular values.
Thus,
\hat{M}_{{\rm EM}}
works well for low rank matrices and this advantage is more pronounced in higher dimensions
[
33
]
.
On the other hand,
\hat{M}_{{\rm JS}}
works well only when
\|M\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{p}\sigma_{j}(M)^{2}
is small.

3.2
Matrix quadratic loss

Instead of the Frobenius loss, it is insightful to investigate estimation of
M
under the matrix quadratic loss

\displaystyle L(M,\hat{M})=(\hat{M}-M)^{\top}(\hat{M}-M),

(9)

which takes a value in the set of
p\times p
positive semidefinite matrices
[
33
]
.
Under this loss, an estimator
\hat{M}_{1}
is said to dominate another estimator
\hat{M}_{2}
if

\displaystyle{\rm E}_{M}[(\hat{M}_{1}-M)^{\top}(\hat{M}_{1}-M)]\preceq{\rm E}_{M}[(\hat{M}_{2}-M)^{\top}(\hat{M}_{2}-M)]

for every
M
, where
\preceq
is the Löwner order:
A\preceq B
means that
B-A
is positive semidefinite.
Thus, if
\hat{M}_{1}
dominates
\hat{M}_{2}
under the matrix quadratic loss, then

\displaystyle{\rm E}_{M}[\|(\hat{M}_{1}-M)u\|^{2}]\leq{\rm E}_{M}[\|(\hat{M}_{2}-M)u\|^{2}]

for every
M
and
u\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
.
In other words,
\hat{M}_{1}
dominates
\hat{M}_{2}
in estimating any linear combination of the columns of
M
under the quadratic loss.
In particular, each column of
\hat{M}_{1}
dominates that of
\hat{M}_{2}
as an estimator of the corresponding column of
M
under the quadratic loss.
Domination under the matrix quadratic loss is stronger than domination under the Frobenius loss, because the Frobenius loss is equal to the trace of the matrix quadratic loss:
l(M,\hat{M})={\rm tr}(L(M,\hat{M}))
.

The Efron–Morris estimator dominates the maximum likelihood estimator even under the matrix quadratic loss as follows
[
33
]
.

Lemma 3.1
.

When
n-p-1>0
, the matrix quadratic risk of the Efron–Morris estimator
\hat{M}_{{\rm EM}}
in (
8
) is

\displaystyle{\rm E}_{M}[(\hat{M}_{{\rm EM}}-M)^{\top}(\hat{M}_{{\rm EM}}-M)]=nI_{p}-(n-p-1)^{2}{\rm E}_{M}[(X^{\top}X)^{-1}]\preceq nI_{p}.

(10)

Thus, the Efron–Morris estimator dominates the maximum likelihood estimator under the matrix quadratic loss.

The matrix quadratic risk (
10
) of
\hat{M}_{{\rm EM}}
at
M=O
is
(p+1)I_{p}

[
33
]
.
Here, we extend this result by using the recent result by
[
25
]
on the expectation of the inverse of a noncentral Wishart matrix.

Proposition 3.1
.

The matrix quadratic risk of the Efron–Morris estimator
\hat{M}_{{\rm EM}}
in (
8
) has the same eigenvectors with
M^{\top}M
.
If
M^{\top}M
has a zero eigenvalue, then the corresponding eigenvalue of the matrix quadratic risk is
p+1
.

Proof.

The matrix
X^{\top}X
follows the noncentral Wishart distribution
W_{p}(n,I_{p},M^{\top}M)

[
23
]
.
Let
M^{\top}M=U^{\top}\Lambda U
be a spectral decomposition of
M^{\top}M
, where
U\in O(p)
and
\Lambda={\rm diag}(\sigma_{1}(M)^{2},\dots,\sigma_{p}(M)^{2})
.
Then, from Theorem 1 of
[
25
]
,
{\rm E}_{M}[(X^{\top}X)^{-1}]=U^{\top}\Psi U
,where
\Psi={\rm diag}(\psi_{1}(M),\dots,\psi_{p}(M))
and each
\psi_{j}(M)
is given by an infinite sum involving matrix-variate hypergeometric functions.
Therefore, the matrix quadratic risk (
10
) of
\hat{M}_{{\rm EM}}
is given by

\displaystyle{\rm E}_{M}[(\hat{M}_{{\rm EM}}-M)^{\top}(\hat{M}_{{\rm EM}}-M)]=U^{\top}(nI_{p}-(n-p-1)^{2}\Psi)U,

(11)

which has the same eigenvectors with
M^{\top}M=U^{\top}\Lambda U
.
If the
j
-th eigenvalue of
M^{\top}M
is zero, then
\psi_{j}(M)=(n-p-1)^{-1}
from Corollary 3 of
[
25
]
, which means that the corresponding eigenvalue of (
11
) is
n-(n-p-1)=p+1
.
∎

Therefore, the Efron–Morris estimator works well when
M
is close to low-rank.
Specifically, if
M^{\top}M
has a zero eigenvalue, then the ratio of the corresponding eigenvalues of the matrix quadratic risks of the Efron–Morris estimator and the maximum likelihood estimator is
(p+1)/n
, which goes to zero as
n
increases.
Thus, the advantage of the Efron–Morris estimator over the maximum likelihood estimator for low-rank matrices is pronounced when the number of row is much larger than the number of columns.

3.3
Oracle inequality

Now, we derive an oracle inequality for the Efron–Morris estimator, which will be used to show the adaptive minimaxity of the blockwise Efron–Morris estimator.

The matrix quadratic risk of the linear estimator
\hat{M}_{C}=XC
is

\displaystyle{\rm E}_{M}[(\hat{M}_{C}-M)^{\top}(\hat{M}_{C}-M)]=C^{\top}(M^{\top}M+nI_{p})C-2M^{\top}MC+M^{\top}M.

For a fixed
M
, it is uniquely minimized by
C_{*}=C_{*}(M)=(M^{\top}M+nI_{p})^{-1}M^{\top}M
:

\displaystyle{\rm E}_{M}[(\hat{M}_{C_{*}}-M)^{\top}(\hat{M}_{C_{*}}-M)]

\displaystyle=nI_{p}-n^{2}(M^{\top}M+nI_{p})^{-1}

\displaystyle\preceq{\rm E}_{M}[(\hat{M}_{C}-M)^{\top}(\hat{M}_{C}-M)]

for every
C
.
Thus,
\hat{M}_{C_{*}(M)}
can be viewed as the linear oracle estimator.
The Efron–Morris estimator
\hat{M}_{{\rm EM}}
attains almost the same matrix quadratic risk with this oracle as follows.

Theorem 3.1
.

The matrix quadratic risk of the Efron–Morris estimator
\hat{M}_{{\rm EM}}
satisfies

\displaystyle{\rm E}_{M}[(\hat{M}_{{\rm EM}}-M)^{\top}(\hat{M}_{{\rm EM}}-M)]\preceq{\rm E}_{M}[(\hat{M}_{C_{*}}-M)^{\top}(\hat{M}_{C_{*}}-M)]+2(p+1)I_{p}

(12)

for every
M
, where
C_{*}=C_{*}(M)=(M^{\top}M+nI_{p})^{-1}M^{\top}M
.

Proof.

From (
10
),

\displaystyle{\rm E}_{M}[(\hat{M}_{{\rm EM}}-M)^{\top}(\hat{M}_{{\rm EM}}-M)]

\displaystyle=nI_{p}-(n-p-1)^{2}{\rm E}_{M}[(X^{\top}X)^{-1}]

\displaystyle\preceq nI_{p}-(n-p-1)^{2}{\rm E}_{M}[X^{\top}X]^{-1}

\displaystyle=nI_{p}-(n-p-1)^{2}(M^{\top}M+nI_{p})^{-1},

where we used the operator convexity of the inverse function
[
22
,
24
]
.
Then, from
(n-p-1)^{2}\geq n^{2}-2n(p+1)
and
(M^{\top}M+nI_{p})^{-1}\preceq n^{-1}I_{p}
, we obtain (
12
).
∎

Consider the quadratic loss specified by a
p\times p
positive definite matrix
Q
:
l_{Q}(M,\hat{M})={\rm tr}((\hat{M}-M)Q(\hat{M}-M)^{\top})
.
Since this quadratic loss is related to the matrix quadratic loss by
l_{Q}(M,\hat{M})={\rm tr}(L(M,\hat{M})Q)
, Theorem
3.1
is translated to an oracle inequality under this loss as follows.

Corollary 3.1
.

The
Q
-quadratic risk of the Efron–Morris estimator
\hat{M}_{{\rm EM}}
satisfies

\displaystyle{\rm E}_{M}[{\rm tr}((\hat{M}_{{\rm EM}}-M)Q(\hat{M}_{{\rm EM}}-M)^{\top})]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle{\rm E}_{M}[{\rm tr}((\hat{M}_{C_{*}}-M)Q(\hat{M}_{C_{*}}-M)^{\top})]+2(p+1){\rm tr}(Q).

(13)

We conjecture that the constant term
2(p+1)I_{p}
in (
12
) can be improved to
(p+1)I_{p}
, although the current form is enough for the present purpose.
When
p=1
, this conjecture is true from (
6
), which is proved by using the Poisson mixture representation of the noncentral chi-square distribution
[
28
, Proposition 2.8]
.
The latter half of Proposition
3.1
also supports this conjecture when
M
has low rank.
Figure

) and its conjectured modification.
It implies that the improved oracle inequality holds tightly when
n
is large, which is consistent with the finding in
[
28
, Figure 2.6]
for
p=1
.
It is an interesting future work to prove this conjecture.

4
Multivariate Gaussian sequence model and Sobolev ellipsoid

4.1
Multivariate Gaussian sequence model

For
p>1
, we consider the multivariate version of the Gaussian sequence model defined by

\displaystyle y_{i}=\theta_{i}+\varepsilon\xi_{i},\quad i=1,2,\cdots,

(14)

where
\varepsilon>0
and
\xi_{i}\sim{\rm N}_{p}(0,I_{p})
are independent Gaussian random vectors.
This model is equivalent to the multivariate version of the Gaussian white noise model

\displaystyle{\rm d}Y_{j}(t)=f_{j}(t){\rm d}t+\varepsilon{\rm d}W_{j}(t),\quad j=1,\cdots,p,

where
t\in[0,1]
,
f_{1}(t),\cdots,f_{p}(t)
are drift functions to be estimated and
W_{1}(t),\cdots,W_{p}(t)
are independent standard Wiener processes.
Specifically, for an orthonormal basis
\phi=(\phi_{i})
of
L_{2}[0,1]
, let

\displaystyle y_{ij}=\int\phi_{i}(t){\rm d}Y_{j}(t),\quad\theta_{ij}=\int\phi_{i}(t)f_{j}(t){\rm d}t,\quad\xi_{ij}=\int\phi_{i}(t){\rm d}W_{j}(t).

Then, by putting
y_{i}=(y_{i1},\dots,y_{ip})
,
\theta_{i}=(\theta_{i1},\dots,\theta_{ip})
and
\xi_{i}=(\xi_{i1},\dots,\xi_{ip})
, the multivariate Gaussian sequence model (
14
) is obtained.
Also, from a similar argument to
[
4
]
, nonparametric regression with vector response

\displaystyle y_{i}=f(t_{i})+\xi_{i},\quad i=1,\dots,n,

where
f:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{p}
,
t_{i}=i/n
and
\xi_{i}\sim{\rm N}_{p}(0,I_{p})
are independent, is asymptotically equivalent to the multivariate Gaussian sequence model (
14
) with
\varepsilon=n^{-1/2}
as
n\to\infty
.

In the following, we consider estimation of
\theta=(\theta_{i})
from
y=(y_{i})
under the quadratic loss specified by a
p\times p
positive definite matrix
Q
:

\displaystyle L_{Q}(\theta,\hat{\theta})=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}(\hat{\theta}_{i}-\theta_{i})^{\top}Q(\hat{\theta}_{i}-\theta_{i}).

(15)

In the multivariate Gaussian white noise model setting, this quadratic loss corresponds to the weighted
L^{2}
loss given by

\displaystyle L_{Q}(\theta,\hat{\theta})=\int_{0}^{1}(\hat{Y}(t)-Y(t))^{\top}Q(\hat{Y}(t)-Y(t)){\rm d}t.

The current setting is motivated from practical applications of nonparameteric estimation.
For example, consider brain time series data such as EEG, which is a multichannel recording of brain activity.
Due to the multidimensionality, denoising of such data corresponds to nonparametric regression with vector response.
In brain-machine interface, some spatial filters are commonly applied to EEG in order to obtain relevant features for decoding brain activity
[
3
]
.
Namely, a linear transformation
g(t)=Af(t)
of EEG
f(t)
with some matrix
A
is considered for later use.
Since
\|\hat{g}(t)-g(t)\|^{2}=(\hat{f}(t)-f(t))^{\top}Q(\hat{f}(t)-f(t))
with
Q=A^{\top}A
, mean squared error of an estimate of
g(t)
corresponds to the quadratic loss with
Q
of an estimate of
f(t)
.

The multivariate Gaussian sequence model is closely related to the matrix denoising model, in which a low-rank matrix is observed with noise.
For the matrix denoising model, several methods for estimating the signal matrix have been developed and their theoretical properties have been investigated.
Among them, Gavish and Donoho
[
17
]
and Donoho and Gavish
[
13
]
studied singular value thresholding estimators in a high-dimensional asymptotic framework.
They derived the optimal thresholds in closed form, by which the estimator can adapt to unknown rank and unknown noise level optimally in terms of mean squared error.
Later, Gavish and Donoho
[
18
]
also derived the optimal singular value shrinkage rules (nonlinearities) for various loss functions.
Recently, these results have been extended to correlated noise
[
14
,
19
]
.
Other studies on the matrix denoising model include data-driven rank selection in the context of PCA
[
10
]
and nuclear norm penalization
[
9
,
30
]
.
The multivariate Gaussian sequence model can be viewed as an infinite-dimensional version of the matrix denosing model.
Thus, some generalization of the above results for the matrix denoising model may be possible, which is an interesting future problem.

4.2
Multivariate Sobolev ellipsoid

For
\beta>0
and a
p\times p
positive definite matrix
R
, we define the multivariate Sobolev ellipsoid as

\displaystyle\Theta(\beta,R):=\left\{\theta=(\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\cdots)\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}a_{\beta,i}^{2}\theta_{i}^{\top}R^{-1}\theta_{i}\leq 1\right\},

(16)

where
a_{\beta,i}
is given by (
4
).
When
p=1
, it reduces to the original Sobolev ellipsoid (
3
).

The multivariate Sobolev ellipsoid is related to a multivariate version of the periodic Sobolev class.
For a vector-valued function
f:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{p}
, we write each component of
f
as
f_{j}:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
for
j=1,\dots,p
.
For
\beta\in\{1,2,\cdots\}
, we define
f^{(\beta)}:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{p}
by
f^{(\beta)}(x)=(f_{1}^{(\beta)}(x),\dots,f_{p}^{(\beta)}(x))^{\top}
where
f_{j}^{(\beta)}
is the
\beta
-th derivative of
f_{j}
.
For
\beta\in\{1,2,\cdots\}
and a
p\times p
positive semidefinite matrix
L
, we define the multivariate periodic Sobolev class as

\displaystyle W(\beta,L)

\displaystyle:=\left\{f:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{p}\mid f_{j}\in L_{2}[0,1],\ f_{j}^{(l)}(0)=f_{j}^{(l)}(1),\ j=1,\dots,p,\ l=0,1,\cdots,\beta-1,\right.

\displaystyle\left.f_{1}^{(\beta-1)},\cdots,f_{p}^{(\beta-1)}:{\rm absolutely\ continuous},\ \int_{0}^{1}f^{(\beta)}(x)^{\top}L^{-2}f^{(\beta)}(x){\rm d}x\leq 1\right\}.

Then, Lemma
2.1
is extended as follows.
Its proof is deferred to the Appendix.

Proposition 4.1
.

For
\theta=(\theta_{ij})
, let

\displaystyle f_{j}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\theta_{ij}\phi_{i}(x),\quad j=1,\dots,p,

(17)

where
\phi=(\phi_{i})
is the Fourier basis of
L_{2}[0,1]
in (
2
).
Then,
f\in W(\beta,L)
if and only if
\theta\in\Theta\left(\beta,R\right)
in (
16
) where
R=L^{2}/\pi^{2\beta}
.

Figure
3
plots a function in
W(\beta,L)
with
p=2
for several settings of
\beta
and
L
.
Like the usual Sobolev space, the parameter
\beta
represents the smoothness of
f
.
On the other hand, the matrix parameter
L
specifies not only the scale but also the correlations between
f_{1},\dots,f_{p}
.
The functions in Figure
3
were obtained by using a similar method to Figure 6.3 of
[
28
]
.
Specifically, we sampled
\theta_{i}\sim{\rm N}_{p}(0,12\pi^{-2}p^{-1}b_{\beta,i}^{2}R)
independently for
i=1,\dots,2N+1
and substituted them into (
17
), where
N=10^{4}
,
R=L^{2}/\pi^{2\beta}
,
b_{\beta,1}=0
and

\displaystyle b_{\beta,i}=\begin{cases}i^{-\beta-1},&i:{\rm even},\\
(i-1)^{-\beta-1},&i:{\rm odd},\end{cases}

for
i\geq 2
.
From Lemma
.3
,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{2N+1}a_{\beta,i}^{2}\theta_{i}^{\top}R^{-1}\theta_{i}\approx\sum_{i=1}^{2N+1}a_{\beta,i}^{2}{\rm tr}(R^{-1}\cdot 12\pi^{-2}p^{-1}b_{\beta,i}^{2}R)=\frac{12}{\pi^{2}}\sum_{m=1}^{N}\frac{2}{(2m)^{2}}\approx 1

as
N\to\infty
.
Thus, the sampled
\theta
is considered to lie around the boundary of
\Theta(\beta,R)
.

5
Asymptotically minimax estimator on multivariate Sobolev ellipsoid

Now, we derive an asymptotically minimax estimator on the multivariate Sobolev ellipsoid (
16
), which can be viewed as an extension of Pinsker’s theorem (Proposition
2.1
).
Several technical lemmas are given in the Appendix.
For completeness, we also extended the finite-dimensional version of Pinsker’s theorem
[
35
,
43
]
to multivariate setting.
Its detail is given in the Appendix.

For a symmetric matrix
S\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
that has a spectral decomposition
S=U^{\top}\Lambda U
with
U\in O(p)
and
\Lambda={\rm diag}(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{p})
, let
S_{+}
be its projection onto the positive semidefinite cone defined by
S_{+}=U^{\top}\Lambda_{+}U\succeq O
, where
\Lambda_{+}={\rm diag}((\lambda_{1})_{+},\dots,(\lambda_{p})_{+})
.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
a=(a_{i})
be a non-decreasing sequence such that
a_{i}\geq 0
and
a_{i}\to\infty
as
i\to\infty
, and
Q
be a
p\times p
positive definite matrix with eigenvalues
\lambda_{1}\geq\dots\geq\lambda_{p}>0
.
Then, the equation

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{2}\kappa^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}a_{i}{\rm tr}(I_{p}-a_{i}\kappa Q^{-1})_{+}=1

(18)

has a unique solution of
\kappa>0
for every
\varepsilon>0
, which satisfies

\displaystyle\kappa=\varepsilon^{2}\left(1+\varepsilon^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\lambda_{j}^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{j}(\kappa)}a_{i}^{2}\right)^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{j}(\kappa)}a_{i},

(19)

where
N_{j}(\kappa)=\max\{i\mid 1-a_{i}\kappa\lambda_{j}^{-1}>0\}
for
j=1,\dots,p
.

Proof.

Let

\displaystyle g(\kappa):=\varepsilon^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}a_{i}{\rm tr}(I_{p}-a_{i}\kappa Q^{-1})_{+}=\varepsilon^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{j}(\kappa)}a_{i}(1-a_{i}\kappa\lambda_{j}^{-1}).

Clearly,
g
is continuous and piecewise linear.
For
\kappa_{1}<\kappa_{2}
, we have
1-a_{i}\kappa_{1}\lambda_{j}^{-1}>1-a_{i}\kappa_{2}\lambda_{j}^{-1}
and
N_{j}(\kappa_{1})\geq N_{j}(\kappa_{2})
for every
j
, which leads to
g(\kappa_{1})>g(\kappa_{2})
.
Thus,
g(\kappa)
is strictly monotone decreasing.
Also,
g(\kappa)\to\infty
as
\kappa\to 0
and
g(\kappa)\to 0
as
\kappa\to\infty
.
Therefore,
\kappa^{-1}g(\kappa)
is also strictly monotone decreasing,
\kappa^{-1}g(\kappa)\to\infty
as
\kappa\to 0
and
\kappa^{-1}g(\kappa)\to 0
as
\kappa\to\infty
.
Hence, the equation (
18
), which is equivalent to
\kappa^{-1}g(\kappa)=1
, has a unique solution of
\kappa>0
.
From

\displaystyle\kappa^{-1}g(\kappa)=\varepsilon^{2}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{p}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{j}(\kappa)}a_{i}\right)\kappa^{-1}-\varepsilon^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\lambda_{j}^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{j}(\kappa)}a_{i}^{2},

the solution of
\kappa^{-1}g(\kappa)=1
satisfies (
19
).
∎

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
a_{i}=a_{\beta,i}
be the coefficients of the multivariate Sobolev ellipsoid (
4
).
Then, the equation (
18
) has a unique solution that satisfies
\kappa=\kappa_{*}(1+o(1))
as
\varepsilon\to 0
, where

\displaystyle\kappa_{*}:=\left(\frac{\beta{\rm tr}(Q^{(\beta+1)/\beta})}{(\beta+1)(2\beta+1)}\right)^{{\beta}/{(2\beta+1)}}\varepsilon^{{2\beta}/{(2\beta+1)}}.

Proof.

Since
a_{\beta,i}\geq 0
is non-decreasing and
a_{\beta,i}\to\infty
as
i\to\infty
, the equation (
18
) has a unique solution from Lemma
5.1
.
By putting
M_{j}=\max\{i\mid 1-a_{\beta,i}\kappa\lambda_{j}^{-1}>0\}=\lfloor(\kappa\lambda_{j}^{-1})^{-1/\beta}/2\rfloor
, the left hand side of (
18
) is rewritten as

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{2}\kappa^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}a_{\beta,i}(1-a_{\beta,i}\kappa\lambda_{j}^{-1})_{+}=2\varepsilon^{2}\kappa^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\sum_{m=1}^{M_{j}}(2m)^{\beta}(1-(2m)^{\beta}\kappa\lambda_{j}^{-1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\varepsilon^{2}\kappa^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\left(\frac{2^{\beta}M_{j}^{\beta+1}}{\beta+1}-\frac{4^{\beta}M_{j}^{2\beta+1}}{2\beta+1}\kappa\lambda_{j}^{-1}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\beta\varepsilon^{2}}{(\beta+1)(2\beta+1)}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{p}\lambda_{j}^{-1}(\kappa\lambda_{j}^{-1})^{-(2\beta+1)/{\beta}}\right)(1+o(1))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\beta\varepsilon^{2}}{(\beta+1)(2\beta+1)}{\rm tr}(Q^{(\beta+1)/{\beta}})\kappa^{-(2\beta+1)/{\beta}}(1+o(1))=\left(\frac{\kappa_{*}}{\kappa}\right)^{(2\beta+1)/{\beta}}(1+o(1))

as
\varepsilon\to 0
, where we used

\displaystyle\sum_{m=1}^{M}m^{a}=\frac{M^{a+1}}{a+1}(1+o(1))

(20)

as
M\to\infty
.
Therefore,
\kappa=\kappa_{*}(1+o(1))
as
\varepsilon\to 0
.
∎

Consider estimation of
\theta=(\theta_{i})
in the multivariate Gaussian sequence model (
14
) under the quadratic loss
L_{Q}
in (
15
).
The risk function of an estimator
\hat{\theta}
is

\displaystyle R_{Q}(\theta,\hat{\theta})={\rm E}_{\theta}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}(\hat{\theta}_{i}-\theta_{i})^{\top}Q(\hat{\theta}_{i}-\theta_{i})\right].

Suppose that
\theta
is restricted to the multivariate Sobolev ellipsoid
\Theta(\beta,Q)
in (
16
).
From Lemma
5.2
, the equation (
18
) with
a_{i}=a_{\beta,i}
has a unique solution of
\kappa>0
.
By using this
\kappa
, we define a linear estimator
\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}=(\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{P},i})
by

\displaystyle\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{P},i}=C_{\mathrm{P},i}y_{i},\quad C_{\mathrm{P},i}=(I_{p}-a_{\beta,i}\kappa Q^{-1})_{+},

(21)

where
(\cdot)_{+}
denotes the projection onto the positive semidefinite cone introduced above.
This estimator can be viewed as a generalization of Pinsker’s estimator (Proposition
2.1
).

Lemma 5.3
.

For
C_{\mathrm{P}}=(C_{\mathrm{P},i})
in (
21
),

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}{\rm tr}(C_{\mathrm{P},i}Q)=P(\beta,Q)\varepsilon^{{4\beta}/(2\beta+1)}(1+o(1))

(22)

as
\varepsilon\to 0
, where

\displaystyle P(\beta,Q):=(2\beta+1)^{1/(2\beta+1)}\left(\frac{\beta{\rm tr}(Q^{(\beta+1)/{\beta}})}{\beta+1}\right)^{{2\beta}/(2\beta+1)}.

(23)

Proof.

Let
\lambda_{1}\geq\dots\geq\lambda_{p}>0
be the eigenvalues of
Q
and
M_{j}=\max\{i\mid 1-a_{\beta,i}\kappa\lambda_{j}^{-1}>0\}=\lfloor(\kappa\lambda_{j})^{-1/\beta}/2\rfloor
for
j=1,\dots,p
.
Then,

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}{\rm tr}(C_{\mathrm{P},i}Q)

\displaystyle=2\varepsilon^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\sum_{m=1}^{M_{j}}(1-(2m)^{\beta}\kappa\lambda_{j}^{-1})\lambda_{j}

\displaystyle=2\varepsilon^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\left(M_{j}-2^{\beta}\frac{M_{j}^{\beta+1}}{\beta+1}\kappa\lambda_{j}^{-1}\right)\lambda_{j}(1+o(1))

\displaystyle=\varepsilon^{2}\frac{\beta}{\beta+1}\kappa^{-1/\beta}{\rm tr}(Q^{(\beta+1)/{\beta}})(1+o(1))

as
\varepsilon\to 0
, where we used (
20
).
Therefore, from Lemma
5.2
, we obtain (
22
).
∎

Theorem 5.1
.

The estimator
\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}
in (
21
) is asymptotically minimax under
L_{Q}
in (
15
) on the multivariate Sobolev ellipsoid
\Theta(\beta,Q)
in (
16
):

\displaystyle\sup_{\theta\in\Theta(\beta,Q)}R_{Q}(\theta,\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}})

\displaystyle\sim\inf_{\hat{\theta}}\sup_{\theta\in\Theta(\beta,Q)}R_{Q}(\theta,\hat{\theta})\sim P(\beta,Q)\varepsilon^{{4\beta}/{(2\beta+1)}}

as
\varepsilon\to 0
, where
\inf
is taken over all estimators and
P(\beta,Q)
is given by (
23
).

Proof.

We write
a_{i}=a_{\beta,i}
for convenience.
Let
Q=U^{\top}\Lambda U
be a spectral decomposition of
Q
with
\Lambda={\rm diag}(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{p})
.
Then,
C_{\mathrm{P},i}=U^{\top}(I_{p}-a_{i}\kappa\Lambda^{-1})_{+}U
.
Therefore, by using
(1-(1-l)_{+})^{2}\leq l^{2}
,

\displaystyle(I_{p}-C_{\mathrm{P},i})Q(I_{p}-C_{\mathrm{P},i})

\displaystyle=U^{\top}(I_{p}-(I_{p}-a_{i}\kappa\Lambda^{-1})_{+})^{2}\Lambda U

\displaystyle\preceq U^{\top}\cdot a_{i}^{2}\kappa^{2}\Lambda^{-2}\cdot\Lambda\cdot U=a_{i}^{2}\kappa^{2}Q^{-1}.

(24)

Also, by using
a_{i}\kappa\lambda_{j}^{-1}+(1-a_{i}\kappa\lambda_{j}^{-1})_{+}=1
when
(1-a_{i}\kappa\lambda_{j}^{-1})_{+}>0
,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}{\rm tr}(C_{\mathrm{P},i}Q(a_{i}\kappa Q^{-1}+C_{\mathrm{P},i}))

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{p}(1-a_{i}\kappa\lambda_{j}^{-1})_{+}\lambda_{j}(a_{i}\kappa\lambda_{j}^{-1}+(1-a_{i}\kappa\lambda_{j}^{-1})_{+})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{p}(1-a_{i}\kappa\lambda_{j}^{-1})_{+}\lambda_{j}=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}{\rm tr}(C_{\mathrm{P},i}Q).

(25)

Similarly,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}{\rm tr}(C_{\mathrm{P},i}Q(a_{i}\kappa Q^{-1}+C_{\mathrm{P},i})^{-1})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}{\rm tr}(C_{\mathrm{P},i}Q).

(26)

Now, from

\displaystyle{\rm E}_{\theta}\left[(\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{P},i}-\theta_{i})(\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{P},i}-\theta_{i})^{\top}\right]=(I_{p}-C_{\mathrm{P},i})\theta_{i}\theta_{i}^{\top}(I_{p}-C_{\mathrm{P},i})+\varepsilon^{2}C_{\mathrm{P},i}^{2}

and (
24
), we obtain

\displaystyle R_{Q}(\theta,\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\theta_{i}^{\top}(I_{p}-C_{\mathrm{P},i})Q(I_{p}-C_{\mathrm{P},i})\theta_{i}+\varepsilon^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}{\rm tr}(C_{\mathrm{P},i}^{2}Q)

\displaystyle\leq\kappa^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}a_{i}^{2}\theta_{i}^{\top}Q^{-1}\theta_{i}+\varepsilon^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}{\rm tr}(C_{\mathrm{P},i}^{2}Q)

\displaystyle\leq\kappa^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}{\rm tr}(C_{\mathrm{P},i}^{2}Q)

for
\theta\in\Theta(\beta,Q)
.
By using (
18
) and (
5
),

\displaystyle\kappa^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}{\rm tr}(C_{\mathrm{P},i}^{2}Q)

\displaystyle=\varepsilon^{2}\kappa\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}a_{i}{\rm tr}(I_{p}-a_{i}\kappa Q^{-1})_{+}+\varepsilon^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}{\rm tr}(C_{\mathrm{P},i}^{2}Q)

\displaystyle=\varepsilon^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}{\rm tr}(C_{\mathrm{P},i}Q(a_{i}\kappa Q^{-1}+C_{\mathrm{P},i}))

\displaystyle=\varepsilon^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}{\rm tr}(C_{\mathrm{P},i}Q).

Therefore,

\displaystyle\inf_{\hat{\theta}}\sup_{\theta\in\Theta(\beta,Q)}R_{Q}(\theta,\hat{\theta})

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\theta\in\Theta(\beta,Q)}R_{Q}(\theta,\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}})\leq\varepsilon^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}{\rm tr}(C_{\mathrm{P},i}Q).

(27)

Now, we consider the minimax lower bound. Let
N=N_{1}(\kappa)=\max\{i\mid 1-a_{i}\kappa\lambda_{1}^{-1}>0\}
and

\displaystyle\Theta_{N}(\beta,Q):=\left\{\theta=(\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\dots)\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}\theta_{1}=0;\ \sum_{i=2}^{N}a_{i}^{2}\theta_{i}^{\top}Q^{-1}\theta_{i}\leq 1;\ \theta_{i}=0,i>N\right\},

where
\theta_{1}=0
comes from
a_{1}=0
.
Then, from
\Theta_{N}(\beta,Q)\subset\Theta(\beta,Q)
,

\displaystyle\inf_{\hat{\theta}}\sup_{\theta\in\Theta(\beta,Q)}R_{Q}(\theta,\hat{\theta})

\displaystyle\geq\inf_{\hat{\theta}}\sup_{\theta\in\Theta_{N}(\beta,Q)}R_{Q}(\theta,\hat{\theta})

\displaystyle=\inf_{\hat{\theta}}\sup_{\theta\in\Theta_{N}(\beta,Q)}{\rm E}_{\theta}\left[\sum_{i=2}^{N}(\hat{\theta}_{i}-\theta_{i})^{\top}Q(\hat{\theta}_{i}-\theta_{i})\right],

(28)

where the equality follows because the infimum is attained by an estimator such that
\hat{\theta}_{i}=0
for
i=1
and
i>N
.
Thus, we restrict our attention to estimation of
\theta^{N}=(\theta_{2},\dots,\theta_{N})
in the following.
Let
\delta\in(0,1)
and
\pi
be the prior on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
such that
\theta_{i}\sim{\rm N}_{p}(0,\delta^{2}V_{i})
independently with
V_{i}=\varepsilon^{2}a_{i}^{-1}\kappa^{-1}Q(I_{p}-a_{i}\kappa Q^{-1})_{+}
for
i=2,\dots,N
.
From Lemma
.1
, the Bayes estimator with respect to this prior (posterior mean) is

\displaystyle\hat{\theta}^{\pi}_{i}=C^{\pi}_{i}x_{i},\quad C^{\pi}_{i}=\delta^{2}V_{i}(\delta^{2}V_{i}+\varepsilon^{2}I_{p})^{-1},

for
i=2,\dots,N
, and its Bayes risk is

\displaystyle r(\pi)=\delta^{2}\varepsilon^{2}\sum_{i=2}^{N}{\rm tr}(QV_{i}(\delta^{2}V_{i}+\varepsilon^{2}I_{p})^{-1})

\displaystyle\geq\delta^{2}\varepsilon^{2}\sum_{i=2}^{N}{\rm tr}(QV_{i}(V_{i}+\varepsilon^{2}I_{p})^{-1})

\displaystyle=\delta^{2}\varepsilon^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}{\rm tr}(C_{\mathrm{P},i}Q(a_{i}\kappa Q^{-1}+C_{\mathrm{P},i})^{-1})

\displaystyle=\delta^{2}\varepsilon^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}{\rm tr}(C_{\mathrm{P},i}Q),

(29)

where we used (
26
).
Let

\displaystyle\Phi:=\left\{\theta^{N}=(\theta_{2},\dots,\theta_{N})\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}\sum_{i=2}^{N}a_{i}^{2}\theta_{i}^{\top}Q^{-1}\theta_{i}\leq 1\right\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{p(N-1)}

be the truncation of
\Theta_{N}(a,Q)
and
\mathbb{E}
be the class of estimators
\hat{\theta}^{N}
of
{\theta}^{N}=({\theta}_{2},\dots,{\theta}_{N})
that take values in
\Phi
.
Since

is closed and convex, we can restrict our attention to estimators in

in considering the lower bound for the minimax risk over

Let

\displaystyle R_{N,Q}(\theta^{N},\hat{\theta}^{N}):={\rm E}_{\theta^{N}}\left[\sum_{i=2}^{N}(\hat{\theta}_{i}-\theta_{i})^{\top}Q(\hat{\theta}_{i}-\theta_{i})\right].

Then, for any estimator
\hat{\theta}^{N}\in\mathbb{E}
, from the definition of the Bayes risk,

\displaystyle r(\pi)

\displaystyle\leq\int R_{N,Q}(\theta^{N},\hat{\theta}^{N})\pi(\theta^{N}){\rm d}\theta^{N}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\theta^{N}\in\Phi}R_{N,Q}(\theta^{N},\hat{\theta}^{N})+\sup_{\hat{\theta}^{N}\in\mathbb{E}}\int_{\Phi^{c}}R_{N,Q}(\theta^{N},\hat{\theta}^{N})\pi(\theta^{N}){\rm d}\theta^{N},

where
\Phi^{c}=\mathbb{R}^{p(N-1)}\setminus\Phi
.
Taking the infimum over all estimators in
\mathbb{E}
,

\displaystyle r(\pi)

\displaystyle\leq\inf_{\hat{\theta}^{N}\in\mathbb{E}}\sup_{\theta^{N}\in\Phi}R_{N,Q}(\theta^{N},\hat{\theta}^{N})+\sup_{\hat{\theta}^{N}\in\mathbb{E}}\int_{\Phi^{c}}R_{N,Q}(\theta^{N},\hat{\theta}^{N})\pi(\theta^{N}){\rm d}\theta^{N},

which yields

\displaystyle\inf_{\hat{\theta}^{N}\in\mathbb{E}}\sup_{\theta^{N}\in\Phi}R_{N,Q}(\theta^{N},\hat{\theta}^{N})\geq r(\pi)-\sup_{\hat{\theta}^{N}\in\mathbb{E}}\int_{\Phi^{c}}R_{N,Q}(\theta^{N},\hat{\theta}^{N})\pi(\theta^{N}){\rm d}\theta^{N}.

(30)

Now, from
(a+b)^{\top}Q(a+b)\leq 2(a^{\top}Qa+b^{\top}Qb)
and the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality,

\displaystyle\sup_{\hat{\theta}^{N}\in\mathbb{E}}\int_{\Phi^{c}}R_{N,Q}(\theta^{N},\hat{\theta}^{N})\pi(\theta^{N}){\rm d}\theta^{N}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\int_{\Phi^{c}}\left(\sum_{i=2}^{N}\theta_{i}^{\top}Q\theta_{i}\right)\pi(\theta^{N}){\rm d}\theta^{N}+2\sup_{\hat{\theta}^{N}\in\mathbb{E}}\int_{\Phi^{c}}{\rm E}_{\theta^{N}}\left[\sum_{i=2}^{N}\hat{\theta}_{i}^{\top}Q\hat{\theta}_{i}\right]\pi(\theta^{N}){\rm d}\theta^{N}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2(\pi(\Phi^{c}))^{1/2}{\rm E}_{\pi}\left[\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\theta_{i}^{\top}Q\theta_{i}\right)^{2}\right]^{1/2}+2M\pi(\Phi^{c}),

(31)

where

\displaystyle M:=\max_{\theta^{N}\in\Phi}\sum_{i=2}^{N}{\theta}_{i}^{\top}Q{\theta}_{i}={\lambda_{1}^{2}}{a_{2}^{-2}}=O(1).

Let
\xi_{i}=\delta^{-1}V_{i}^{-1/2}\theta_{i}\sim{\rm N}_{p}(0,I_{p})
and
V_{i}^{1/2}Q^{-1}V_{i}^{1/2}=U^{\top}\Lambda_{i}U
with
\Lambda_{i}={\rm diag}(\lambda_{i1},\dots,\lambda_{ip})
be a spectral decomposition of

.
Then,

\displaystyle\theta_{i}^{\top}Q^{-1}\theta_{i}=\delta^{2}\xi_{i}^{\top}V_{i}^{1/2}Q^{-1}V_{i}^{1/2}\xi_{i}=\delta^{2}(U\xi_{i})^{\top}\Lambda_{i}(U\xi_{i})=\delta^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\lambda_{ij}(U{\xi}_{i})_{j}^{2}.

Therefore, by putting
b_{ij}^{2}=\delta^{2}a_{i}^{2}\lambda_{ij}=\delta^{2}\varepsilon^{2}\kappa^{-1}a_{i}(1-a_{i}\kappa\lambda_{j}^{-1})_{+}
,

\displaystyle\pi(\Phi^{c})

\displaystyle={\rm Pr}\left[\sum_{i=2}^{N}a_{i}^{2}\theta_{i}^{\top}Q^{-1}\theta_{i}>1\right]

\displaystyle={\rm Pr}\left[\sum_{i=2}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{p}b_{ij}^{2}((U\xi_{i})_{j}^{2}-1)>\frac{1-\delta^{2}}{\delta^{2}}\sum_{i=2}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{p}b_{ij}^{2}\right],

where we used
\sum_{i,j}b_{ij}^{2}=\delta^{2}
.
Since
U\xi_{i}\sim{\rm N}_{p}(0,I_{p})
independently for
i=1,\dots,N
, from Lemma
.2
,

\displaystyle\pi(\Phi^{c})

\displaystyle=O\left(\exp\left(-\frac{(1-\delta^{2})^{2}}{8\delta^{4}}\frac{\sum_{i,j}b_{ij}^{2}}{\max_{i,j}\{b_{ij}^{2}\mid b_{ij}^{2}>0\}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=O\left(\exp\left(-C\varepsilon^{-2/(2\beta+1)}\right)\right)

(32)

for
\delta\in(1/\sqrt{2},1)
with some constant
C>0
, where we used
b_{ij}^{2}\leq\delta^{2}\varepsilon^{2}\kappa^{-1}a_{N_{j}(\kappa)}\leq\delta^{2}\varepsilon^{2}\kappa^{-2}\lambda_{j}=O(\varepsilon^{2/(2\beta+1)})
for

and
b_{ij}^{2}=0
for

.
Also, by putting
V_{i}^{1/2}QV_{i}^{1/2}=U^{\top}DU
with

,

\displaystyle\theta_{i}^{\top}Q\theta_{i}=\delta^{2}\xi_{i}^{\top}V_{i}^{1/2}QV_{i}^{1/2}\xi_{i}=\delta^{2}(U\xi_{i})^{\top}D(U\xi_{i})=\delta^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\mu_{ij}(U\xi_{i})_{j}^{2}.

Thus, from Lemma

,

\displaystyle{\rm E}_{\pi}\left[\left(\sum_{i=2}^{N}\theta_{i}^{\top}Q\theta_{i}\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq 3\delta^{4}\left(\sum_{i=2}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\mu_{ij}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=3\delta^{4}\left(\sum_{i=2}^{N}{\rm tr}(QV_{i})\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 3\delta^{4}a_{2}^{-4}\left(\sum_{i=2}^{N}a_{i}^{2}{\rm tr}(QV_{i})\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=3\delta^{4}a_{2}^{-4}\left(\varepsilon^{2}\kappa^{-1}\sum_{i=2}^{N}a_{i}{\rm tr}(C_{\mathrm{P},i}Q^{2})\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 3\delta^{4}\lambda_{1}^{4}a_{2}^{-4}\left(\varepsilon^{2}\kappa^{-1}\sum_{i=2}^{N}a_{i}{\rm tr}(C_{\mathrm{P},i})\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=3\delta^{4}\lambda_{1}^{4}a_{2}^{-4}

\displaystyle=O(1).

(33)

Substituting (
32
) and (

) into (

\displaystyle\sup_{\hat{\theta}^{N}\in\mathbb{E}}\int_{\Phi^{c}}R(\theta^{N},\hat{\theta}^{N})\pi(\theta^{N}){\rm d}\theta^{N}=O\left(\exp\left(-C\varepsilon^{-2/(2\beta+1)}\right)\right).

Combining it with (

), (

) and (

\displaystyle\inf_{\hat{\theta}}\sup_{\theta\in\Theta(\beta,Q)}R_{Q}(\theta,\hat{\theta})\geq\delta^{2}\varepsilon^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}{\rm tr}(C_{\mathrm{P},i}Q)(1+o(1)).

By taking

\displaystyle\inf_{\hat{\theta}}\sup_{\theta\in\Theta(\beta,Q)}R_{Q}(\theta,\hat{\theta})

\displaystyle\geq\varepsilon^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}{\rm tr}(C_{\mathrm{P},i}Q)(1+o(1)).

(34)

From (

), (

) and Lemma

Adaptive minimaxity of blockwise Efron–Morris estimator

6.1
Monotone and blockwise constant oracles

For the multivariate Gaussian sequence model (
14
), the linear estimator with coefficient matrices
C=(C_{i})
where
C_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
is defined by

\displaystyle\hat{\theta}_{C}=(\hat{\theta}_{C,i}),\quad\hat{\theta}_{C,i}=C_{i}y_{i}.

Here, we consider two classes of coefficient matrices that depend on the choice of the quadratic loss
L_{Q}
.
Let
Q=U^{\top}\Lambda U
be a spectral decomposition of
Q
and
N
be a positive integer, which will be set to
\lfloor\varepsilon^{-2}\rfloor
in the next subsection.

The first one is the class of monotone coefficient matrices commutative with
Q
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{W}_{\mathrm{mon},Q}:=\{C=(C_{i})\mid C_{i}

\displaystyle=U^{\top}\Lambda_{i}U,\ \Lambda_{i}:\mathrm{diagonal},\ i=1,\dots,N;

\displaystyle I_{p}\succeq\Lambda_{1}\succeq\Lambda_{2}\succeq\dots\succeq\Lambda_{N}\succeq O;\ C_{i}=O,\ i>N\}.

The second one is the class of blockwise constant coefficient matrices commutative with
Q
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{W}_{B,Q}=\{C=({C}_{i})\mid{C}_{i}

\displaystyle={C}_{(j)},\ i\in B_{j};\ C_{i}=O,\ i>N;

\displaystyle C_{(j)}=U^{\top}\Lambda_{(j)}U,\ \Lambda_{(j)}:\mathrm{diagonal},\ O\preceq\Lambda_{(j)}\preceq I_{p}\},

where
B=(B_{1},\dots,B_{J})
is a partition of
\{1,2,\dots,N\}
that satisfies
\max B_{j-1}<\min B_{j}
for
j=2,\dots,J
, similar to the weakly geometric blocks introduced in Section
2.3
.

For a fixed
\theta
, we can consider two oracle estimators that attain the smallest risk among
\mathbb{W}_{\mathrm{mon},Q}
and
\mathbb{W}_{B,Q}
, respectively.
The risks of these oracle estimators are related as follows.

Lemma 6.1
.

If
{|B_{j+1}|}\leq(1+\eta)|B_{j}|
for
j=1,\dots,J-1
with
\eta>0
, then

\displaystyle\min_{\bar{C}\in\mathbb{W}_{B,Q}}R_{Q}(\theta,\hat{\theta}_{\bar{C}})\leq(1+\eta)\min_{C\in\mathbb{W}_{\mathrm{mon},Q}}R_{Q}(\theta,\hat{\theta}_{C})+\varepsilon^{2}|B_{1}|{\rm tr}(Q).

(35)

Proof.

For
C=(C_{i})\in\mathbb{W}_{\mathrm{mon},Q}
, define
\bar{C}=(\bar{C}_{1},\bar{C}_{2},\dots)\in\mathbb{W}_{B,Q}
by

\displaystyle\bar{C}_{i}:=\begin{cases}\bar{C}_{(j)},&i\in B_{j},\\
O,&i>N,\end{cases}

where
\bar{C}_{(j)}=C_{\min B_{j}}
.
For convenience, we put
\Lambda_{(j)}=\Lambda_{\min B_{j}}
so that
\bar{C}_{(j)}=U^{\top}\Lambda_{(j)}U
.
Then,

\displaystyle(I_{p}-\bar{C}_{i})Q(I_{p}-\bar{C}_{i})

\displaystyle=U^{\top}(I_{p}-{\Lambda}_{(j)})\Lambda(I_{p}-{\Lambda}_{(j)})U

\displaystyle\preceq U^{\top}(I_{p}-{\Lambda}_{i})\Lambda(I_{p}-{\Lambda}_{i})U

\displaystyle=(I_{p}-{C}_{i})Q(I_{p}-{C}_{i})

for
i\in B_{j}
, since
\Lambda,\Lambda_{i},\Lambda_{(j)}
are all diagonal and
O\preceq\Lambda_{i}\preceq\Lambda_{(j)}\preceq I_{p}
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle R_{Q}(\theta,\hat{\theta}_{\bar{C}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}(\theta_{i}^{\top}(I_{p}-\bar{C}_{i})Q(I_{p}-\bar{C}_{i})\theta_{i}+\varepsilon^{2}{\rm tr}(\bar{C}_{i}Q\bar{C}_{i}))

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\theta_{i}^{\top}(I_{p}-C_{i})Q(I_{p}-C_{i})\theta_{i}+\varepsilon^{2}{\rm tr}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\bar{C}_{i}Q\bar{C}_{i}\right).

By definition of
\bar{C}
,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\bar{C}_{i}Q\bar{C}_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{J}|B_{j}|\bar{C}_{(j)}Q\bar{C}_{(j)}\preceq|B_{1}|Q+\sum_{j=2}^{J}|B_{j}|\bar{C}_{(j)}Q\bar{C}_{(j)},

where we used
\bar{C}_{(1)}Q\bar{C}_{(1)}=U^{\top}\Lambda_{(1)}^{2}\Lambda U\preceq U^{\top}\Lambda U=Q
.
From

\displaystyle\bar{C}_{(j)}Q\bar{C}_{(j)}=U^{\top}\Lambda_{(j)}^{2}\Lambda U\preceq U^{\top}\Lambda_{(j-1)}^{2}\Lambda U=\bar{C}_{(j-1)}Q\bar{C}_{(j-1)}

and
|B_{j}|\leq(1+\eta)|B_{j-1}|
for
j\geq 2
,

\displaystyle\sum_{j=2}^{J}|B_{j}|\bar{C}_{(j)}Q\bar{C}_{(j)}

\displaystyle\preceq(1+\eta)\sum_{j=2}^{J}|B_{j-1}|\bar{C}_{(j-1)}Q\bar{C}_{(j-1)}

\displaystyle\preceq(1+\eta)\sum_{j=1}^{J-1}\sum_{i\in B_{j}}{C}_{i}QC_{i}\preceq(1+\eta)\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}{C}_{i}QC_{i}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle R_{Q}(\theta,\hat{\theta}_{\bar{C}})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\theta_{i}^{\top}(I_{p}-C_{i})Q(I_{p}-C_{i})\theta_{i}+\varepsilon^{2}{\rm tr}\left(|B_{1}|Q+(1+\eta)\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}{C}_{i}Q{C}_{i}\right)

\displaystyle\leq(1+\eta)\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}(\theta_{i}^{\top}(I_{p}-C_{i})Q(I_{p}-C_{i})\theta_{i}+\varepsilon^{2}{\rm tr}({C}_{i}Q{C}_{i}))+\varepsilon^{2}|B_{1}|{\rm tr}(Q)

\displaystyle=(1+\eta)R_{Q}(\theta,\hat{\theta}_{{C}})+\varepsilon^{2}|B_{1}|{\rm tr}(Q).

Since
C\in\mathbb{W}_{\mathrm{mon},Q}
is arbitrary, (
35
) is obtained.
∎

6.2
Blockwise Efron–Morris estimator

Let
N=\lfloor\varepsilon^{-2}\rfloor

B=(B_{1},\dots,B_{J})

\{1,\dots,N\}

For the multivariate Gaussian sequence model (
14

\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{BEM}}=(\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{BEM},i})
by

\displaystyle\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{BEM},i}=\begin{cases}y_{i},&i\in B_{j},\ |B_{j}|<p+2,\\
(I_{p}-\varepsilon^{2}(|B_{j}|-p-1)(\sum_{i\in B_{j}}y_{i}y_{i}^{\top})^{-1})y_{i},&i\in B_{j},\ |B_{j}|\geq p+2,\\
0,&i>N.\end{cases}

(36)

This estimator applies the Efron–Morris type singular value shrinkage to each block
B_{j}
.
For the weakly geometric blocks introduced in Section

, the blockwise Efron–Morris estimator attains the adaptive minimaxity over the multivariate Sobolev ellipsoids as follows.

For every

From the definition of the weakly geometric blocks and Lemma 3.12 of

\displaystyle|B_{1}|=\log^{2}(1/\varepsilon),\quad J\leq C\log^{2}(1/\varepsilon),\quad\max_{1\leq j\leq J-1}\frac{|B_{j+1}|}{|B_{j}|}=1+3\rho_{\varepsilon},

(37)

for sufficiently small
\varepsilon

is some constant and

We take
\varepsilon
to be sufficiently small so that

for every

Theorem

indicates that the blockwise Efron–Morris estimator is adaptive not only to smoothness of the unknown function but also to arbitrary quadratic loss.

8
Conclusion

In this study, we introduced a multivariate version of the Gaussian sequence model and showed that the blockwise Efron–Morris estimator attains adaptive minimaxity over the multivariate Sobolev ellipsoids.
Notably, it adapts to arbitrary quadratic loss in addition to smoothness of the unknown function.
This adaptivity comes from the singular value shrinkage in the Efron–Morris estimator, which is different from the scalar shrinkage in the James–Stein estimator and thresholding-type shrinkage commonly used in sparse estimation.
By shrinking the singular values towards zero separately, the Efron–Morris estimator successfully captures the correlations between multiple sequences and utilize them for adaptive estimation.
We expect that the singular value shrinkage is useful in other estimation problems as well.

In addition to adaptive estimation over the Sobolev ellipsoids, many results have been obtained for estimation in the Gaussian sequence model, which corresponds to
p=1
in our setting
[
28
]
.
It is an interesting future work to further investigate generalization of these results to multivariate Gausian sequence models.
For example, since a penalized version of the blockwise James–Stein estimator attains adaptive minimaxity over general ellipsoids and hyperrectangles
[
7
]
, such a modification may be possible for the blockwise Efron–Morris estimator as well.
Generalization to the linear inverse problem
[
8
]
, which corresponds to the Gaussian sequence model where the observation variance increases with indices, is another direction for future research.
Also, it is an interesting problem whether existing methods for adaptation to inhomogenous smoothness in the Besov spaces, such as wavelet thresholding
[
11
,
12
]
and deep neural networks
[
40
]
, can be extended to attain adaptation to arbitrary quadratic loss.
There are several extensions of the Efron–Morris estimator
[
41
]
and they may be also useful.

Empirical Bayes methods have been found to be effective in adaptive estimation
[
29
,
44
]
.
The blockwise Efron–Morris estimator provides another example of this, since the Efron–Morris estimator is naturally interpreted as an empirical Bayes estimator
[
16
]
.
Recently, a superharmonic prior for the normal mean matrix parameter has been developed
[
32
]
, which shrinks the singular values towards zero and can be viewed as a generalization of Stein’s prior
[
38
]
.
The generalized Bayes estimator with respect to this prior is minimax under the matrix quadratic loss and has qualitatively the same behavior with the Efron–Morris estimator
[
33
]
.
It is an interesting future work to investigate Bayesian inference in multivariate Gaussian sequence models with respect to such singular value shrinkage priors, including uncertainty quantification.

\displaystyle L_{Q}(\theta,\hat{\theta})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\hat{\theta}_{i}-\theta_{i})^{\top}Q(\hat{\theta}_{i}-\theta_{i}).

\displaystyle R_{Q}(\theta,\hat{\theta})={\rm E}_{\theta}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\hat{\theta}_{i}-\theta_{i})^{\top}Q(\hat{\theta}_{i}-\theta_{i})\right].

We consider asymptotically minimax estimation on the ellipsoid

\displaystyle\Theta_{n}(Q)=\left\{\theta=(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{n})\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\theta_{i}^{\top}Q^{-1}\theta_{i}\leq 1\right\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{np},

for
\kappa>0

g

g(\kappa)\to\infty

\kappa\to 0

g(\kappa)\to 0

\kappa\to\infty

\kappa

g(\kappa)=1
.

\kappa

\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}=(\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{P},i})

\displaystyle\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{P},i}=C_{\mathrm{P}}y_{i},\quad C_{\mathrm{P}}=(I_{p}-\kappa Q^{-1})_{+}.

Theorem .1
.

The estimator
\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}=(\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{P},i})

\displaystyle\sup_{\theta\in\Theta_{n}(Q)}R_{Q}(\theta,\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}})

\displaystyle\sim\inf_{\hat{\theta}}\sup_{\theta\in\Theta_{n}(Q)}R_{Q}(\theta,\hat{\theta})\sim\varepsilon^{2}{\rm tr}(C_{\mathrm{P}}Q)(=O(1)),

(43)

as

Proof.

Let

\displaystyle(I_{p}-C_{\mathrm{P}})Q(I_{p}-C_{\mathrm{P}})

\displaystyle=U^{\top}(I_{p}-(I_{p}-\kappa\Lambda^{-1})_{+})^{2}\Lambda U\preceq U^{\top}\cdot\kappa^{2}\Lambda^{-2}\cdot\Lambda U=\kappa^{2}Q^{-1}.

(44)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{p}(1-\kappa\lambda_{j}^{-1})_{+}\lambda_{j}(\kappa\lambda_{j}^{-1}+(1-\kappa\lambda_{j}^{-1})_{+})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{p}(1-\kappa\lambda_{j}^{-1})_{+}\lambda_{j}={\rm tr}(C_{\mathrm{P}}Q).

From

\displaystyle{\rm E}_{\theta}\left[(\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{P},i}-\theta_{i})(\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{P},i}-\theta_{i})^{\top}\right]=(I_{p}-C_{\mathrm{P}})\theta_{i}\theta_{i}^{\top}(I_{p}-C_{\mathrm{P}})+n^{-1}\varepsilon^{2}C_{\mathrm{P}}^{2},

\displaystyle\leq\kappa^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\theta_{i}^{\top}Q^{-1}\theta_{i}+\varepsilon^{2}{\rm tr}(C_{\mathrm{P}}^{2}Q)

\displaystyle\leq\kappa^{2}\cdot\varepsilon^{2}\kappa^{-1}{\rm tr}(C_{\mathrm{P}})+\varepsilon^{2}{\rm tr}(C_{\mathrm{P}}^{2}Q)

\displaystyle=\varepsilon^{2}{\rm tr}(C_{\mathrm{P}}Q(\kappa Q^{-1}+C_{\mathrm{P}}))=\varepsilon^{2}{\rm tr}(C_{\mathrm{P}}Q)

for
\theta\in\Theta_{n}(Q)
, where we used (

),

Thus,

\displaystyle\inf_{\hat{\theta}}\sup_{\theta\in\Theta_{n}(Q)}{\rm E}_{\theta}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{P},i}-\theta_{i})^{\top}Q(\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{P},i}-\theta_{i})\right]\leq\varepsilon^{2}{\rm tr}(C_{\mathrm{P}}Q).

(46)

Let
\delta\in(0,1)
and
\pi
be the prior
\theta_{i}\sim{\rm N}(0,\delta^{2}V)
independently with
V=(\varepsilon^{2}/n)\kappa^{-1}Q(I_{p}-\kappa Q^{-1})_{+}
for
i=1,\dots,n
.
Then, the Bayes estimator with respect to this prior (posterior mean) is

\displaystyle\hat{\theta}^{\pi}_{i}=C^{\pi}x_{i},\quad C^{\pi}=\delta^{2}V(\delta^{2}V+(\varepsilon^{2}/n)I_{p})^{-1},

for
i=1,\dots,n
and its Bayes risk is

\displaystyle r(\pi)

\displaystyle=n\cdot\delta^{2}(\varepsilon^{2}/n){\rm tr}(QV(\delta^{2}V+(\varepsilon^{2}/n)I_{p})^{-1})

\displaystyle\geq\delta^{2}\varepsilon^{2}{\rm tr}(QV(V+(\varepsilon^{2}/n)I_{p})^{-1})=\delta^{2}\varepsilon^{2}{\rm tr}(C_{\mathrm{P}}Q).

Let
\mathbb{E}
be the class of estimators
\hat{\theta}
of
{\theta}
that take values in
\Theta_{n}(Q)
.
Since
\Theta_{n}(Q)
is closed and convex, we can restrict our attention to estimators in
\mathbb{E}
in considering the lower bound for minimax risk over
\Theta_{n}(Q)
.
For any estimator
\hat{\theta}\in\mathbb{E}
,

\displaystyle r(\pi)

\displaystyle\leq\int R_{Q}(\theta,\hat{\theta})\pi(\theta){\rm d}\theta\leq\sup_{\theta\in\Theta_{n}(Q)}R_{Q}(\theta,\hat{\theta})+\sup_{\hat{\theta}\in\mathbb{E}}\int_{\Theta_{n}(Q)^{c}}R_{Q}(\theta,\hat{\theta})\pi(\theta){\rm d}\theta,

where
\Theta_{n}(Q)^{c}=\mathbb{R}^{np}\setminus\Theta_{n}(Q)
.
Taking the infimum over all estimators in
\mathbb{E}
,

\displaystyle r(\pi)

\displaystyle\leq\inf_{\hat{\theta}}\sup_{\theta\in\Theta_{n}(Q)}R_{Q}(\theta,\hat{\theta})+\sup_{\hat{\theta}\in\mathbb{E}}\int_{\Theta_{n}(Q)^{c}}R_{Q}(\theta,\hat{\theta})\pi(\theta){\rm d}\theta,

which yields

\displaystyle\inf_{\hat{\theta}}\sup_{\theta\in\Theta_{n}(Q)}R_{Q}(\theta,\hat{\theta})\geq r(\pi)-\sup_{\hat{\theta}\in\mathbb{E}}\int_{\Theta_{n}(Q)^{c}}R_{Q}(\theta,\hat{\theta})\pi(\theta){\rm d}\theta.

Now, from
(a+b)^{\top}Q(a+b)\leq 2(a^{\top}Qa+b^{\top}Qb)
and the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality,

\displaystyle\sup_{\hat{\theta}\in\mathbb{E}}\int_{\Theta_{n}(Q)^{c}}R_{Q}(\theta,\hat{\theta})\pi(\theta){\rm d}\theta

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\int_{\Theta_{n}(Q)^{c}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\theta_{i}^{\top}Q\theta_{i}\right)\pi(\theta){\rm d}\theta+2\sup_{\hat{\theta}\in\mathbb{E}}\int_{\Theta_{n}(Q)^{c}}{\rm E}_{\theta}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\hat{\theta}_{i}^{\top}Q\hat{\theta}_{i}\right]\pi(\theta){\rm d}\theta

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2(\pi(\Theta_{n}(Q)^{c}))^{1/2}{\rm E}_{\pi}\left[\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\theta_{i}^{\top}Q\theta_{i}\right)^{2}\right]^{1/2}+2M\pi(\Theta_{n}(Q)^{c}),

where

\displaystyle M=\max_{\theta\in\Theta_{n}(Q)}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\theta_{i}^{\top}Q\theta_{i}=\lambda_{1}^{2}=O(1).

Let
\xi_{i}=\delta^{-1}V^{-1/2}\theta_{i}\sim{\rm N}_{p}(0,I_{p})
and
V^{1/2}Q^{-1}V^{1/2}=U^{\top}\Psi U
with
\Psi={\rm diag}(\psi_{1},\dots,\psi_{p})
be a spectral decomposition of
V^{1/2}Q^{-1}V^{1/2}
.
Then,

\displaystyle\theta_{i}^{\top}Q^{-1}\theta_{i}=\delta^{2}\xi_{i}^{\top}V^{1/2}Q^{-1}V^{1/2}\xi_{i}=\delta^{2}(U\xi_{i})^{\top}\Psi(U\xi_{i})=\delta^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\psi_{j}(U{\xi}_{i})_{j}^{2}.

Therefore, by putting
b_{j}^{2}=\delta^{2}\psi_{j}
,

\displaystyle\pi(\Theta_{n}(Q)^{c})

\displaystyle={\rm Pr}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\theta_{i}^{\top}Q^{-1}\theta_{i}>1\right]={\rm Pr}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{p}b_{j}^{2}((U\xi_{i})_{j}^{2}-1)>\frac{1-\delta^{2}}{\delta^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{p}b_{j}^{2}\right],

where we used
\sum_{i,j}b_{j}^{2}=n\delta^{2}{\rm tr}(V^{1/2}Q^{-1}V^{1/2})=\delta^{2}g(\kappa)=\delta^{2}
.
Since
U\xi_{i}\sim{\rm N}_{p}(0,I_{p})
independently for
i=1,\dots,n
, from Lemma A.2,

\displaystyle\pi(\Theta_{n}(Q)^{c})=O\left(\exp\left(-\frac{(1-\delta^{2})^{2}}{8\delta^{4}}\frac{\sum_{i,j}b_{j}^{2}}{\max_{j}\{b_{j}^{2}\mid b_{j}^{2}>0\}}\right)\right)=O\left(\exp\left(-Cn\right)\right)

for
\delta\in(1/\sqrt{2},1)
with some constant
C>0
.
Also, by putting
V^{1/2}QV^{1/2}=U^{\top}DU
with
D={\rm diag}(\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{p})
,

\displaystyle\theta_{i}^{\top}Q\theta_{i}=\delta^{2}\xi_{i}^{\top}V^{1/2}QV^{1/2}\xi_{i}=\delta^{2}(U\xi_{i})^{\top}D(U\xi_{i})=\delta^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\mu_{j}(U\xi_{i})_{j}^{2}.

Thus, from Lemma A.3,

\displaystyle{\rm E}_{\pi}\left[\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\theta_{i}^{\top}Q\theta_{i}\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq 3\delta^{4}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\mu_{j}\right)^{2}=3\delta^{4}n^{2}{\rm tr}(QV)^{2}=3\delta^{4}\varepsilon^{4}\kappa^{-1}{\rm tr}(C_{\mathrm{P}}Q^{2})=O(1).

Therefore, from
{\rm tr}(C_{\mathrm{P}}Q)=O(1)
,

\displaystyle\inf_{\hat{\theta}}\sup_{\theta\in\Theta_{n}(Q)}R_{Q}(\theta,\hat{\theta})\geq\delta^{2}\varepsilon^{2}{\rm tr}(C_{\mathrm{P}}Q)(1+o(1)).

By taking
\delta\to 1
,

\displaystyle\inf_{\hat{\theta}}\sup_{\theta\in\Theta_{n}(Q)}R_{Q}(\theta,\hat{\theta})\geq\varepsilon^{2}{\rm tr}(C_{\mathrm{P}}Q)(1+o(1)).
