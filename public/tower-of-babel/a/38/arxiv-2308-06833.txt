A Van Kampen type obstruction for string graphs
Moshe J. White

Abstract
In this paper we prove that a graph is a string graph (the intersection graph of curves in the plane) if and only if it admits a drawing in the plane with certain properties. This also allows us to define an algebraic obstruction, similar to the Van Kampen obstruction to embeddability, which must vanish for every string graph. However, unlike in the case of graph planarity this obstruction is incomplete.



1
Introduction

A graph
\mathsf{G}=(V,E)
is a
string graph
, if it is the intersection graph of a collection of curves in the plane: we may find
\left\{\gamma_{v}\ :\ v\in V\right\}
such that every
\gamma_{v}
is the image of a continuous map
[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
, and for any two vertices
v\neq w
we have
\gamma_{v}\cap\gamma_{w}\neq\emptyset
if and only if
\left\{v,w\right\}\in E
. The collection of curves
\left\{\gamma_{v}\right\}
is said to be a representation of
\mathsf{G}
. For a recent brief exposition of string graphs, see
[
5
, §1]
.

In some sense, we consider string graphs as a generalization of planar graphs. In one of the earliest papers dealing with string graphs, Sinden
[
9
]
already determined that every planar graph is a string graph, while some non-planar graphs are string graphs (such as any complete graph) and some are not (such as a barycentric subdivision of any non-planar graph). The approach presented in this paper arose from previous work of the author
[
11
]
, dealing with simplicial complexes representable by convex sets in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where representability was described in terms of the existence of certain linear maps. Furthermore, necessary conditions (but usually not sufficient) for representability were given. The lowest dimensions in which representability is not fully classified is for graphs (
1
-dimensional simplicial complexes) representable in dimension
2
. The topological restrictions on graphs are in fact satisfied by all string graphs (a class strictly containing graphs representable in dimension
2
, see
[
11
, Example 4.13]
).

In this paper we further explore the necessary topological restrictions on string graphs. Keeping in mind the analogy between string graphs and planar graphs, a drawing of
\mathsf{G}
in the plane where
none
of the edges intersect indicates that
\mathsf{G}
is planar. If we instead allow
some
intersections among the edges of
\mathsf{G}
, the drawing indicates that our graph is a string graph. The precise conditions classifying string graphs (in terms of edge intersections) is given in Theorem
3.6
. Continuing our analogy, we consider the (strong) Hanani-Tutte Theorem (see
[
7
]
for more details):

Theorem 1.1

(Hanani-Tutte)
.

A graph
\mathsf{G}
can be drawn in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
such that every pair of disjoint edges cross an even number of times, if and only if
\mathsf{G}
is planar.

The “if” part is clear, as an embedding of
\mathsf{G}
admits no crossings among edges. The converse may be deduced from Kuratowski’s theorem
[
10
]
, or even proved directly
[
6
]
. The even crossing condition is equivalent to the existence of a solution to a system of linear equations over
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
, described in cohomological terms as the vanishing of the (mod 2) Van Kampen obstruction. We introduce a modified Van Kampen obstruction that must vanish for every string graph
\mathsf{G}
(Theorem
3.2
). This theorem essentially states that if
\mathsf{G}
is a string graph, then there exists a drawing of
\mathsf{G}
in the plane such that certain pairs of edges cross an even number of times. Unlike with graph planarity, the converse fails, as seen in Example
3.4
.

1.1
Notation for graphs

A graph
\mathsf{G}=(V,E)
is an abstract collection of vertices
V=V(\mathsf{G})
and edges
E=E(\mathsf{G})
, where each edge is a set containing exactly two vertices.

Abusing notation, when we use a graph as the domain or range of a function, such as
f:\mathsf{G}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
, this
\mathsf{G}
is to be understood as the topological space which is the geometric realization of the abstract graph
\mathsf{G}
(a point for each vertex and a line segment for every edge, attached at the endpoints to the points corresponding to its vertices). We sometimes identify an edge
\alpha\in E
with the corresponding line segment, so
f(\alpha)
denotes the image of the line segment corresponding to
\alpha
. We also use the abbreviated notation
vw
in place of
\left\{v,w\right\}
(often an edge).

We define
a subdivision
of
\mathsf{G}
to be a graph
\mathsf{H}
together with a homeomorphism
\mathsf{G}\to\mathsf{H}
taking vertices of
\mathsf{G}
to vertices of
\mathsf{H}
. If no edge of
\mathsf{G}
is mapped to a single edge of
\mathsf{H}
, we say that
\mathsf{H}
is a
proper
subdivision. We let
\mathsf{G}^{*}
denote the barycentric subdivision of
\mathsf{G}=(V,E)
:

V(\mathsf{G}^{*})=V\cup E\quad,\quad E(\mathsf{G}^{*})=\left\{v\alpha\ :\ v\in\alpha\in E\right\}.

We usually require that maps
f:\mathsf{G}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
are piecewise linear and in general position: there exists
\mathsf{H}
which is a subdivision of
\mathsf{G}
, such that the composition of
f
with the (inverse) homeomorphism
\mathsf{H}\to\mathsf{G}
is linear (every edge is mapped to a line segment), and takes no three vertices of
\mathsf{H}
to the same line.

2
Modified Van Kampen obstructions

We adopt the geometric definition of the (mod
2
) Van Kampen obstruction for graph planarity from
[
6
]
, and also borrow notation from
[
4
, Appendix D]
. For a graph
\mathsf{G}=(V,E)
, we let

P_{\Delta}(\mathsf{G})=\left\{(\alpha,\beta)\in E^{2}\ :\ \alpha\cap\beta=\emptyset\right\}

denote the collection of disjoint pairs of edges in
\mathsf{G}
. The Van Kampen obstruction can be defined as an equivariant cohomology class (with coefficients in
\mathbb{Z}/2
) of the deleted product

\mathsf{G}^{2}_{P}:=\bigcup_{(\alpha,\beta)\in P}F(\alpha)\times F(\beta),

where
P=P_{\Delta}(\mathsf{G})
and
F:\mathsf{G}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
is some embedding. The only change we introduce to the ordinary mod 2 Van Kampen obstruction, is that we allow for
P\subseteq P_{\Delta}
to be some symmetric subset. We do not assume familiarity with cohomology or the Van Kampen obstruction, readers familiar with the argument (using finger moves) that the obstruction must vanish for planar graphs may skip to Proposition
2.4
, which is proved analogously to the more familiar case of the deleted product.

The set
P
should be thought of as a collection of edge pairs whose intersections are forbidden: our goal will be to find some map
f:\mathsf{G}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
such that
f(\alpha)\cap f(\beta)=\emptyset
for every
(\alpha,\beta)\in P
(or prove that no such
f
exists).

Since any pair
(\alpha,\beta)\in P
involves disjoint edges, the intersection
f(\alpha)\cap f(\beta)
is either empty or consists of finitely many points, all in the interior of both edges. Assuming
f
is piecewise linear and in general position, we define a vector
o_{f}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{P}
by

o_{f}(\alpha,\beta)=\left|f(\alpha)\cap f(\beta)\right|\text{ mod }2.

If
f
is not piecewise linear and in general position, we say that
o_{f}
is undefined. Our next step is to define vectors corresponding to the Van Kampen “finger moves”. For every vertex
u\in V
and edge
\omega\in E
such that
u\notin\omega
, define
\varphi_{\omega,u}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{P}
by

\varphi_{\omega,u}\left(\alpha,\beta\right)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if $\alpha=\omega$ and $u\in\beta$,}\\
1&\text{if $\beta=\omega$ and $u\in\alpha$,}\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

The collection of all finger move vectors
\Phi=\left\{\varphi_{\omega,u}:u\in V,\ \omega\in E,\ u\notin\omega\right\}
defines an equivalence relation on
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{P}
, where two vectors
\varphi,\psi
are equivalent if
\varphi-\psi\in\textnormal{span}_{\mathbb{Z}_{2}}(\Phi)
.

Note that both
o_{f}
and
\varphi_{\omega,u}
in fact depend on the set of pairs
P
, and we will denote them by
o_{f}^{P}
and
\varphi_{\omega,u}^{P}
respectively if
P
isn’t clear from the context. We can now choose some function
f:\mathsf{G}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
, and define:

Definition 2.1
.

The Van Kampen obstruction of a graph
\mathsf{G}
is defined as the equivalence class
{\mathfrak{o}_{\mathsf{G}}=\left[o_{f}^{P_{\Delta}(\mathsf{G})}\right]}
.

And more generally:

Definition 2.2
.

Let
\mathsf{G}=(V,E)
be a graph, and let
P\subseteq E^{2}
be a symmetric collection of disjoint edges: any
(\alpha,\beta)\in P
satisfies
(\beta,\alpha)\in P
and
\alpha\cap\beta=\emptyset
.

The Van Kampen obstruction of
\mathsf{G}
(modified for
P
) is the equivalence class

\mathfrak{o}_{\mathsf{G}}(P)=\left[o_{f}^{P}\right].

We say that the obstruction
vanishes
if
\mathfrak{o}_{\mathsf{G}}(P)=\left[0\right]
.

The definition implies that
\mathfrak{o}_{\mathsf{G}}(P)
doesn’t depend on our choice of
f
. Indeed, the vectors equivalent to
any

o_{f}
are precisely
all
vectors of the form
o_{g}
. Explicitly:

Lemma 2.3
.

For
\mathsf{G},P
as above and
f:\mathsf{G}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
, we have:

1.

For every
\varphi_{\omega,u}\in\Phi
, there exists
g:\mathsf{G}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
, such that
o_{g}=o_{f}+\varphi_{\omega,u}
,

2.

For every
g:\mathsf{G}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
, we have
o_{f}-o_{g}\in\textnormal{span}_{\mathbb{Z}_{2}}(\Phi)
.

Proof.

We achieve (1) by applying a finger move to
f
: take a thin tube around a path connecting an interior point of
f(\omega)
to the vertex
u
, such that
u
is the only vertex within the tube. Then we only modify the image of
\omega
to go around the boundary of the tube. The resulting modified function
g
has
o_{g}=o_{f}+\varphi_{\omega,u}
(see Figure

).

This clearly implies that
\mathfrak{o}_{\mathsf{G}}
vanishes if
\mathsf{G}
is planar. For modified obstructions, we deduce from the lemma:

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
\mathsf{G}=(V,E)
be a graph,
P\subseteq E^{2}
a symmetric collection of disjoint edges. Then
\mathfrak{o}_{\mathsf{G}}(P)
vanishes if and only if there exists
f:\mathsf{G}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
, such that
\left|f(\alpha)\cap f(\beta)\right|
is even for every
(\alpha,\beta)\in P
.

3
The string graph obstruction

We are now ready to define the string obstruction of a graph:

Definition 3.1
.

For a graph
\mathsf{G}=(V,E)
, denote

P=P_{s}(\mathsf{G})=\left\{(\alpha,\beta)\in E^{2}\ :\ vw\notin E\text{ for every }v\in\alpha,w\in\beta\right\}

and define the
string obstruction
of
\mathsf{G}
as

\mathfrak{o}^{s}_{\mathsf{G}}=\mathfrak{o}_{\mathsf{G}}(P).

We claim:

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
\mathsf{G}
be a string graph. Then
\mathfrak{o}^{s}_{\mathsf{G}}
vanishes.

The proof will be postponed until after establishing Lemma
3.7

\mathsf{H}

is non-zero.

3.1
The obstruction via barycentric subdivisions

Definition 3.5
.

Let
\mathsf{G}=(V,E)
be a graph, and recall that
\mathsf{G}^{*}
denotes the barycentric subdivision of
\mathsf{G}
: the edges of
\mathsf{G}^{*}
are
v\alpha
where
v\in\alpha\in E
. Denote

P=P_{\textnormal{sd}}(\mathsf{G})=\left\{(v\alpha,w\beta)\in E\left(\mathsf{G}^{*}\right)^{2}\ :\ v\neq w\text{ and }vw\notin E\right\},

and define the
subdivided string obstruction
of
\mathsf{G}
as

\mathfrak{o}_{\mathsf{G}}^{\textnormal{sd}}=\mathfrak{o}_{\mathsf{G}^{*}}(P).

Note that
\mathfrak{o}_{\mathsf{G}}^{\textnormal{sd}}
is in fact a Van Kampen obstruction of
\mathsf{G}^{*}
modified for
P
(in the sense of Definition
2.2
). We associate it with
\mathsf{G}
, however, because it vanishes when
\mathsf{G}
is a string graph. In fact,
P_{\textnormal{sd}}(\mathsf{G})
contains precisely the pairs of edges whose intersections are forbidden if
\mathsf{G}
is a string graph, in the following sense:

Theorem 3.6
.

Let
\mathsf{G}=(V,E)
be a graph. Then
\mathsf{G}
is a string graph if and only if there exists a drawing of the barycentric subdivision
g:\mathsf{G}^{*}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
, such that

g(v\alpha)\cap g(w\beta)=\emptyset

for every
(v\alpha,w\beta)\in P_{\textnormal{sd}}(\mathsf{G})
.

Proof.

Suppose
g:\mathsf{G}^{*}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
is as above. For every
v\in E
let
\mathsf{T}_{v}
denote the subgraph of
\mathsf{G}^{*}
induced on the collection (of
\mathsf{G}^{*}
-vertices)
\left\{v\right\}\cup\left\{\alpha\in E\ :\ v\in\alpha\right\}
. From our assumption on the properties of
g
, it is clear that for every
v,w\in V
with
v\neq w
we have
g(\mathsf{T}_{v})\cap g(\mathsf{T}_{w})\neq\emptyset
if and only if
vw\in E
. For every
v\in V
we know that
g(\mathsf{T}_{v})
is connected (in fact
\mathsf{T}_{v}
is a tree), so let
\gamma_{v}=g(\mathsf{T}_{v})
be a path tracing along the line segments
\bigcup_{\alpha\ni v}g(v\alpha)
. This gives us a representation of
\mathsf{G}
as a string graph.

For the converse, suppose that
\mathsf{G}
is a string graph represented by a collection of paths
\gamma_{v}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
for every
v\in V
. So for every
v\neq w
in
V
, the intersection
\gamma_{v}\cap\gamma_{w}
is non-empty if and only if
vw\in E
. For every
v\in V
define
g(v)
as an arbitrary point in
\gamma_{v}
, and for every
\alpha=vw\in E
define
g(\alpha)
as an arbitrary point in
\gamma_{v}\cap\gamma_{w}
. For every
v\in\alpha\in E
, note that both
g(v)
and
g(\alpha)
are in
\gamma_{v}
. We may therefore connect them within this image, and obtain
g:\mathsf{G}^{*}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
such that
g(v\alpha)\subseteq\gamma_{v}
for every
v\in\alpha\in E
. For every
(v\alpha,w\beta)\in P_{\textnormal{sd}}(\mathsf{G})
, we clearly have

, therefore
g(v\alpha)\cap g(w\beta)\subseteq\gamma_{v}\cap\gamma_{w}=\emptyset
completing our proof.
∎

It might seem that we have described a second obstruction for string graphs. However, we will now prove in the following lemma that they vanish together, therefore once we know
\mathfrak{o}_{\mathsf{G}}^{s}
, computing
\mathfrak{o}_{\mathsf{G}}^{\textnormal{sd}}
tells us nothing new about whether
\mathsf{G}
is a string graph or not. Half of the statement is also our final piece for the proof of Theorem

.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
\mathsf{G}=(V,E)
be a graph. Then
\mathfrak{o}_{\mathsf{G}}^{s}
vanishes if and only if
\mathfrak{o}_{\mathsf{G}}^{\textnormal{sd}}
vanishes.

Proof.

We will prove that there exists a function
f:\mathsf{G}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
such that
\left|f(\alpha)\cap f(\beta)\right|
is even for every
(\alpha,\beta)\in P_{s}(\mathsf{G})
, if and only if there exists a function
g:\mathsf{G}^{*}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
such that
\left|g(v\alpha)\cap g(w\beta)\right|
is even for every
(v\alpha,w\beta)\in P_{\textnormal{sd}}(\mathsf{G})
. This statement is equivalent to our lemma, due to Proposition
2.4
(applied for both obstructions).

Suppose such
g:\mathsf{G}^{*}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
exists. Let
f:\mathsf{G}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
denote the composition of the homeomorphism
\mathsf{G}\to\mathsf{G}^{*}

. Let
(\alpha,\beta)\in P_{s}(\mathsf{G})
, where
\alpha=v_{1}v_{2}
and
\beta=w_{1}w_{2}
. As line segments, we have
\alpha=v_{1}\alpha\cup v_{2}\alpha
and
\beta=w_{1}\beta\cup w_{2}\beta
. Clearly we have
(v_{i}\alpha,w_{j}\beta)\in P_{\textnormal{sd}}(\mathsf{G})
for every

. We deduce that

\left|f(\alpha)\cap f(\beta)\right|=\sum_{i,j=1}^{2}\left|g(v_{i}\alpha)\cap g(w_{j}\beta)\right|

(3.1)

is even as a sum of even terms.

Conversely, if
f:\mathsf{G}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}

\mathsf{G}^{*}

\left|g(v\alpha)\cap g(w\beta)\right|

is
problematic
, and denote
\alpha=v_{1}v_{2}
and
\beta=w_{1}w_{2}
. For every such problematic pair, we will modify
g
using the finger moves (for
\mathsf{G}^{*}
):

\varphi_{v_{1}\alpha,\beta}\ ,\ \varphi_{v_{2}\alpha,\beta}\ ,\ \varphi_{w_{1}\beta,\alpha}\ ,\ \varphi_{w_{2}\beta,\alpha}

to obtain some modified
g^{\prime}:\mathsf{G}^{*}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Note that these finger moves only effect the parity of
\left|g(v_{i}\alpha)\cap g(w_{j}\beta)\right|
for
i,j=1,2
, and applying some or all of these finger moves allows us to freely move any number of intersection points between the four sets
g(v_{i}\alpha)\cap g(w_{j}\beta)
(
i,j=1,2
). We proceed as follows: if
v_{k}w_{l}\in E
for some
k,l\in\left\{1,2\right\}
, then
(v_{k}\alpha,w_{l}\beta)\notin P_{\textnormal{sd}}(\mathsf{G})
, and we may apply the finger moves to guarantee that
\left|g(v_{i}\alpha)\cap g(w_{j}\beta)\right|
is even for every
(i,j)\neq(k,l)
. If
v_{i}w_{j}\notin E
for every
i,j=1,2
then
(\alpha,\beta)\in P_{s}(\mathsf{G})
. Clearly (
3.1
) still holds, and the sum is even by our assumption on
f
. We may therefore apply the finger moves to guarantee that
\left|g(v_{i}\alpha)\cap g(w_{j}\beta)\right|
is even for every
i,j=1,2
. Proceeding similarly for every problematic pair
(\alpha,\beta)
, we obtain our modified
g^{\prime}
as required.
∎

Proof of Theorem
3.2
.

Let
\mathsf{G}
be a string graph. By Theorem
3.6
, and Proposition
2.4
,
\mathfrak{o}_{\mathsf{G}}^{\textnormal{sd}}
vanishes. Our proof now follows from Lemma
3.7
.
∎

4
Final remarks

In an attempt to strengthen Theorem
3.2
, we may consider an
integer valued
modified Van Kampen obstruction for string graphs. This can be achieved using cohomology with integer coefficients, or as a variation of the geometric definitions we used. Given
f:\mathsf{G}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
, every intersection point in
f(\alpha)\cap f(\beta)
(where
\alpha,\beta\in E(\mathsf{G})
) has either a positive or negative sign. Defining
\widetilde{o}_{f}(\alpha,\beta)
as the sum of these contributions gives us an element
\widetilde{o}_{f}\in\mathbb{Z}^{P}
such that
o_{f}
is the modulo
2
reduction of
\widetilde{o}_{f}
. This was already considered (in a different form) by Tutte
[
10
]
, see
[
4
, Appendix D]
for the integer Van Kampen obstruction. Clearly there are functions
f
such that
\widetilde{o}_{f}
is non-zero while
o_{f}=0\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{P}
. However, the author is currently unaware of any example
\mathsf{G}
for which
\mathfrak{o}_{\mathsf{G}}^{s}
vanishes but the integer valued obstruction does not (
\widetilde{o}_{f}\neq 0\in\mathbb{Z}^{P_{s}(\mathsf{G})}
for every
f
).

Our final remark relates to the computational complexity of recognizing string graphs. Determining if
\mathfrak{o}_{\mathsf{G}}^{s}
vanishes can be done in polynomial time (even if we consider the integer valued obstruction), however Kratochvíl
[
2
]
proved that recognizing string graphs is NP-hard (later determined to be NP-complete
[
8
]
). Proof of NP-hardness is done by reducing the planar 3-satisfiability problem to determining whether certain graphs are string graphs. Avoiding almost all details, a formula
\varphi
(in conjunctive normal form) with certain properties is satisfiable if and only if some graph
\mathsf{G}
derived from
\varphi
is a string graph. String representations of
\mathsf{G}
are in one-to-one correspondence with variable assignments that satisfy
\varphi
.

Obtaining a string graph representation for
\mathsf{G}
from an assignment which doesn’t satisfy
\varphi
would be possible if
C*\overline{C}_{6}
(see Figure
3.4
) were a string graph: representing each subgraph of
\mathsf{G}
corresponding to a false clause in
\varphi
in a manner consistent with the assignment, is equivalent to obtaining a string representation of
C*\overline{C}_{6}
. Since we have
f:C*\overline{C}_{6}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
for which
o_{f}=0
, any arbitrary assignment to the variables of
\varphi
indicates that
\mathfrak{o}_{\mathsf{G}}^{s}
vanishes, whether
\varphi
is satisfiable or not. We thus deduce, perhaps unsurprisingly, that the computationally easy task of determining whether the string obstruction vanishes is of no use to the NP-hard problem described by Kratochvíl.
