Constructive exceptional bundles on ℙ 3 \mathbb{P}^{3}
Benjamin Gould

Abstract
. We give a complete classification of the Chern characters of constructive exceptional vector bundles on ℙ 3 \mathbb{P}^{3} analogous to the work of Drézet and Le Potier on ℙ 2 \mathbb{P}^{2} , and using this classification prove that a constructive exceptional bundle E E on ℙ 3 \mathbb{P}^{3} with μ ⁡ ( E ) ≥ 0 \mu(E)\geq 0 is globally generated.



1.
Introduction

In this paper, we give a complete classification of the Chern characters of constructive exceptional bundles on
\mathbb{P}^{3}
. As an application of the classification, we show that when
E
is a constructive exceptional bundle on
\mathbb{P}^{3}
with
\mu(E)\geq 0
, then
E
is globally generated. These results directly generalize analogous results obtained on
\mathbb{P}^{2}
respectively by Drézet and Le Potier
[
DLP
]
and Coskun-Huizenga-Kopper
[
CHK
]
.

A sheaf
E
is called exceptional if
\text{Hom}(E,E)=\mathbb{C}
, and
\text{Ext}^{i}(E,E)=0
for
i>0
. Exceptional sheaves have proved to be significant and complex in lower dimensions and on other varieties. On
\mathbb{P}^{2}
, exceptional sheaves are stable vector bundles which have a fractal structure, and they govern the classification of stable bundles
[
DLP
]
. On other Fano varieties, exceptional sheaves span the most easily accessible part of the derived category of coherent sheaves (see, e.g.,
[
Kuz
]
). As such, the structure of the exceptional bundles on a variety determines a significant amount of its geometry and homological algebra.

On projective spaces, the simplest examples of exceptional bundles are the line bundles
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{n}}(d)
, and there is a process for forming new exceptional bundles from others, called mutation. We call an exceptional bundle on
\mathbb{P}^{n}

constructive
if it is obtained from line bundles through mutation. Every exceptional bundle on
\mathbb{P}^{2}
is constructive
[
Dré
]
. In higher dimensional projective spaces there are no known examples of non-constructive exceptional sheaves. In this paper, we focus on constructive exceptional bundles on
\mathbb{P}^{3}
.

On
\mathbb{P}^{3}
, exceptional bundles are slope-stable
[
Zub
]
. By definition, they are rigid, so their moduli spaces are zero-dimensional and reduced. Moduli spaces of stable sheaves on
\mathbb{P}^{3}
are central objects of study in algebraic geometry. Despite the fact that they underlie many of the richest and most well-studied problems appearing in the literature and provide some of its most important examples, they are often ungovernably complex
[
Vak
]
and difficult to study. Constructive exceptional bundles provide interesting examples of stable bundles and moduli spaces on
\mathbb{P}^{3}
. In this paper, we classify these moduli spaces using the structure of mutation on
\mathbb{P}^{3}
and prove when the associated bundles are globally generated.

1.1.
Exceptional bundles on
\mathbb{P}^{3}

Our first main result is the classification of Chern characters of constructive exceptional bundles on
\mathbb{P}^{3}
, along the lines of the classification of exceptional slopes on
\mathbb{P}^{2}
by Drézet and Le Potier
[
DLP
]
.

Constructive exceptional bundles on
\mathbb{P}^{n}
appear in infinite ordered collections called
helices
, any
n+1
consecutive elements of which form a full exceptional collection. The simplest example is the standard helix
\sigma_{0}=(\mathcal{O}(i))_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
of line bundles. Other constructive helices are obtained from
\sigma_{0}
via a process called mutation.

Theorem 1.1

(= Theorem
4.3
)
.

Let
E
be a constructive exceptional bundle on
\mathbb{P}^{3}
. There is a well-defined way to choose a distinguished constructive helix
\sigma=\sigma(E)
containing
E
and a full exceptional collection
(E_{1},E,E_{2},E_{3})
of sheaves in
\sigma
containing
E
. For such a collection, we write
\text{ch}(E)=perp(E_{1},E_{2},E_{3})
. This association produces a bijection

\epsilon_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}:\{\text{3-adic rationals}\}\rightarrow\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}\text{Chern characters of constructive}\\
\text{exceptional bundles on }\mathbb{P}^{3}\end{array}\right\}

defined inductively as

\epsilon_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}(n)=n\quad n\in\mathbb{Z},

and for
q\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\epsilon_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}\left(\frac{3p+1}{3^{q+1}}\right)

\displaystyle=\text{perp}\left(\epsilon_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}\left(\frac{p}{3^{q}}\right),E_{2}\left(\epsilon_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}\left(\frac{p}{3^{q}}\right)\right),E_{3}\left(\epsilon_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}\left(\frac{p}{3^{q}}\right)\right)\right)

\displaystyle\epsilon_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}\left(\frac{3p+2}{3^{q+1}}\right)

\displaystyle=\text{perp}\left(E_{1}\left(\epsilon_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}\left(\frac{p+1}{3^{q}}\right)\right),\epsilon_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}\left(\frac{p+1}{3^{q}}\right),E_{3}\left(\epsilon_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}\left(\frac{p+1}{3^{q}}\right)\right)\right).

The proof of Theorem
1.1
involves a close examination of the helix theory on
\mathbb{P}^{3}
, which we organize into a graph
\text{H}_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}
whose vertices are the constructive helices, and whose edges correspond to mutation. The graph
\text{H}_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}
carries a distinguished subgraph
\Gamma_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}
, which is a tree, and every constructive exceptional bundle appears in a helix in
\Gamma_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}
(Proposition
3.16
). The proof of Theorem
1.1
is an identification of this tree with the numerical data of exceptional bundles appearing in these helices.

Once the structure of the mutations forming constructive exceptional bundles is described, their study is much more accessible. Our primary application is the following theorem.

Theorem 1.2

(= Theorem
5.1
)
.

Let
E
be a constructive exceptional bundle on
\mathbb{P}^{3}
with
\mu(E)\geq 0
. Then
E
is globally generated.

The paper is organized as follows. Section
2
reviews the theory of exceptional bundles and helices on projective space. In Section
3
we analyze the structure of mutations of helices on
\mathbb{P}^{3}
and obtain the necessary results for the classification, which we carry out in Section
4
. In Section
4.1
we include explicit computations of the low-order exceptional bundles on
\mathbb{P}^{3}
, and in Section
5
we prove global generation.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Exceptional bundles and helix theory on
\mathbb{P}^{n}

Exceptional bundles were first introduced by Drézet and Le Potier in
[
DLP
]
in order to classify the Chern characters of stable bundles on
\mathbb{P}^{2}
(see Section
2.2
for further details). The theory was expanded to any projective space
\mathbb{P}^{n}
by Gorodentsev-Rudakov
[
GR
]
and studied much more generally by the Rudakov seminar
[
Rud
]
. Exceptional bundles ordinarily fit together in exceptional collections, whose transformations are known as mutations, which naturally occur in helices. We define all of these notions now. Note that the theory works just as well on any Fano variety, but we only form our definitions on
\mathbb{P}^{n}
.

Throughout, we will let
\hom(A,B)=\dim\text{Hom}(A,B)
and
\text{ext}^{i}(A,B)=\dim\text{Ext}^{i}(A,B)
for sheaves
A,B
.

Definition 2.1
.

A sheaf
E
on
\mathbb{P}^{n}
is
exceptional
if
\hom(E,E)=1
and
\text{ext}^{i}(E,E)=0
for
i>0
. An ordered collection
(E_{1},...,E_{k})
of sheaves is an
exceptional collection
if each
E_{i}
is exceptional and in addition we have
\text{Ext}^{m}(E_{i},E_{j})=0
for
m\geq 0
when
i>j
. When the extension-closure of an exceptional collection generates the derived category
D^{b}(\mathbb{P}^{n})
, we say it is
full
. A full exceptional collection on
\mathbb{P}^{n}
has length
n+1
.

The simplest examples of exceptional sheaves on
\mathbb{P}^{n}
are the line bundles
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{n}}(d)
. Every exceptional sheaf on
\mathbb{P}^{n}
is a vector bundle
[
Pos
]
. One can also form these definitions for objects in the derived category; all exceptional objects on
\mathbb{P}^{n}
are shifts of vector bundles.

Given an ordered pair of sheaves
(E,F)
, we form the evaluation and coevaluation maps

\text{ev}:E\otimes\text{Hom}(E,F)\rightarrow F,\quad\text{coev}:E\rightarrow F\otimes\text{Hom}(E,F)^{\vee}

each of which is associated to the identity element of the space
\text{Hom}(E,F)\otimes\text{Hom}(E,F)^{\vee}
. If the evaluation map is surjective it determines an exact sequence

0\rightarrow L_{E}F\rightarrow E\otimes\text{Hom}(E,F)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{ev}}}{{\rightarrow}}F\rightarrow 0

and if the coevaluation map is injective it determines an exact sequence

0\rightarrow E\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{coev}}}{{\rightarrow}}F\otimes\text{Hom}(E,F)^{\vee}\rightarrow R_{F}E\rightarrow 0.

Definition 2.2
.

The sheaf
L_{E}F
is the
left mutation
of
F
across
E
, and the sheaf
R_{F}E
is the
right mutation
of
E
across
F
. We denote mutations by arrows
(E,F)\mapsto(F,R_{F}E),(L_{E}F,E)
.

If
(E,F)
is an ordered pair of exceptional bundles, whenever the left and right mutation sheaves
L_{E}F
and
R_{F}E
are defined, they are exceptional as well
[
GR
, Proposition 1.5]
. Thus mutation provides a way to form new exceptional bundles from old ones.

Example 2.3
.

The Euler sequence

0\rightarrow\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{n}}\rightarrow\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{n}}(1)\otimes\text{Hom}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{n}},\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{n}}(1))^{\vee}\rightarrow T_{\mathbb{P}^{n}}\rightarrow 0

realizes
T_{\mathbb{P}^{n}}
as the right mutation
R_{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{n}}(1)}\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{n}}
. It follows that
T_{\mathbb{P}^{n}}
is also exceptional.

We call an infinite ordered sequence of sheaves
(E_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
on
\mathbb{P}^{n}

periodic
of period
m
if for each
i
and
k
, we have
E_{i-mk}\simeq E_{i}\otimes\omega_{\mathbb{P}^{n}}^{\otimes k}\simeq E_{i}(k(-n-1))
. If
m=n+1
, we will simply call the collection periodic.

From a full exceptional collection
(E_{1},...,E_{n+1})
on
\mathbb{P}^{n}
one can form an infinite periodic collection
(E_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
by setting
E_{i-(n+1)k}=E_{i}\otimes\omega^{k}_{\mathbb{P}^{n}}=E_{i}(k(-n-1))
for
0\leq i\leq n+1
and
k\in\mathbb{Z}
. Clearly such a collection on
\mathbb{P}^{n}
is determined by any
n+1
consecutive sheaves, which we call a
foundation
.

Mutations can also be formed in these infinite periodic collections: if
(E_{i},E_{i+1})
has a surjective evaluation map (resp. injective coevaluation map), then one chooses a foundation
(E_{i},E_{i+1},...,E_{i+n+1})
including
(E_{i},E_{i+1})
; forms the mutation pair
(L_{E_{i}}E_{i+1},E_{i})
; mutates the foundation to obtain a new collection
(L_{E_{i}}E_{i+1},E_{i},...,E_{i+n+1})
and then extends this collection to an infinite periodic collection. Entirely similar statements hold for right mutations.

When the operations are defined, one can iterate mutation. We write
L_{j}(E_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
for the left mutation
L_{E_{j}}
(so
L_{j}
shifts/replaces the
j
th bundle in the sequence). We include the following example for clarity.

Example 2.4
.

Let
\sigma=(\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}(-1),\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{3}},\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}(1),\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}(2))
be the standard exceptional collection on
\mathbb{P}^{3}
. We extend
\sigma
to an infinite collection
(E_{i})=(E_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
of line bundles by setting
E_{i}=\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}(i)
; this collection is clearly periodic.

We form the mutation
L_{0}(E_{i})
to demonstrate the operations. The mutation
L_{0}(E_{i})
is the infinite collection obtained from
(E_{i})
by taking the left mutation of the pair
(E_{0},E_{1})=(\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{3}},\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}(1))
, and then extending this mutation to the rest of the collection. Observe that
L_{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}}\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}(1)=T_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}^{\vee}(1)
, as follows from Example
2.3
. Then to perform the mutation on the collection
(E_{i})
, we first mutate
\sigma
as follows:

(\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}(-1),\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{3}},\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}(1),\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}(2))\mapsto(\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}(-1),T_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}^{\vee}(1),\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{3}},\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}(2)).

Then we extend this exceptional collection to an infinite periodic collection
(F_{i})=(F_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
. We set
F_{0}=\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}(-1)
,
F_{1}=T_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}^{\vee}(1)
,
F_{2}=\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}
and
F_{3}=\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}(2)
. Then we extend to all integers by setting
F_{i-4k}=F_{i}(-4k)
for each
k\in\mathbb{Z}
. E.g., we have
F_{5}=F_{1+4}=T_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}^{\vee}(5)
.

Definition 2.5
.

An ordered infinite periodic collection
\sigma=(E_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
of exceptional bundles on
\mathbb{P}^{n}
is a
helix
if

(1)

each evaluation map
E_{i}\otimes\text{Hom}(E_{i},E_{i+1})\rightarrow E_{i+1}
is surjective,

(2)

each coevaluation map
E_{i}\rightarrow E_{i+1}\otimes\text{Hom}(E_{i},E_{i+1})^{\vee}
is injective, and

(3)

We have
(L_{j-n}\cdots L_{j-1}L_{j})\sigma=\sigma
and
(R_{j+n}\cdots R_{j+1}R_{j})\sigma=\sigma
.

The last condition is known as the “helix condition.” It is equivalent to the following condition, which will be more useful for our applications:

(
3^{\prime}
)

(L_{j-(n-k)}\cdots L_{j-1}L_{j})\sigma=(R_{j-(n-k)-1}\cdots R_{j-n})\sigma
for
0\leq k\leq n
.

The most important example of a helix is the collection of line bundles
\sigma_{0}=(\mathcal{O}(i))_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
; for a proof that this is indeed a helix, see
[
GR
, Proposition 1.11]
. The most important property of helices is that they are preserved under mutation: a mutation of a helix is a helix
[
GR
, Theorem 2.1]
.

Definition 2.6
.

A helix
\sigma
(or bundle
E
) on
\mathbb{P}^{n}
is called
constructive
if it can be obtained from the standard helix
\sigma_{0}
by a sequence of mutations.

Beilinson showed that line bundles form
full
exceptional collections, i.e., that they generate the derived category
[
Bei
]
, and Bondal-Gorodentsev generalized this result to constructive helices
[
BG
]
. There are no known examples of helices or exceptional bundles on any
\mathbb{P}^{n}
which are not constructive.

2.2.
Exceptional bundles on
\mathbb{P}^{2}

Exceptional bundles on
\mathbb{P}^{2}
were intensively studied by Drézet and Le Potier
[
DLP
,
Dré
,
LP
]
. Every exceptional bundle on
\mathbb{P}^{2}
is constructive and slope-stable
[
Dré
,
LP
]
. Their moduli spaces consist of a single reduced point
[
LP
]
.

Vector bundles on
\mathbb{P}^{2}
are often described by their rank
r=\text{ch}_{0}
, slope
\mu=\frac{\text{ch}_{1}}{\text{ch}_{0}}
, and discriminant
\Delta=\frac{1}{2}\mu^{2}-\frac{\text{ch}_{2}}{\text{ch}_{0}}
. The Riemann-Roch theorem implies that
\Delta=\frac{1}{2}(1-\frac{1}{r^{2}})
. The rank and degree of an exceptional bundle on
\mathbb{P}^{2}
are coprime, and the discriminant is a function of the rank, so the slope determines the rank and discriminant, hence the entire Chern character.

The slopes of exceptional bundles have been classified, by the following theorem of Drézet and Le Potier. (See also
[
LP
, 16.3]
.) Set
\mathcal{E}
to be the set of slopes of exceptional bundles on
\mathbb{P}^{2}
. For any rational number
\alpha
we define
r(\alpha)
to be the smallest integer such that
r(\alpha)\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}
, and

\Delta(\alpha)=\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{1}{r(\alpha)^{2}}\right),\quad\chi(\alpha)=r(\alpha)(P(\alpha)-\Delta(\alpha)),

where
P(x)=\frac{1}{2}x^{2}+\frac{3}{2}x+1
is the Hilbert polynomial of
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{2}}
.

The Riemann-Roch theorem implies that when
E
is exceptional on
\mathbb{P}^{2}
with
\mu(E)=\alpha
, we have
\Delta(E)=\Delta(\alpha)
and
\chi(E)=\chi(\alpha)
. We say a pair of rational numbers
\alpha,\beta
is
admissible
if are slope-close, cohomologically orthogonal, and have integral Euler characteristics. (See
[
LP
, Definition 16.3.2]
for a precise definition.)

For a pair of admissible rational numbers, one then introduces the “dot” operator:

\alpha.\beta=\frac{\alpha+\beta}{2}+\frac{\Delta(\beta)-\Delta(\alpha)}{3+\alpha-\beta}.

From this set-up, Drézet and Le Potier define a map
\epsilon_{\mathbb{P}^{2}}:\{\text{dyadic rationals}\}\rightarrow\mathcal{E}
inductively as follows:
\epsilon_{\mathbb{P}^{2}}(n)=n
, and for
q\geq 0
,

\epsilon_{\mathbb{P}^{2}}\left(\frac{2p+1}{2^{q+1}}\right)=\epsilon_{\mathbb{P}^{2}}\left(\frac{p}{2^{q}}\right).\epsilon_{\mathbb{P}^{2}}\left(\frac{p+1}{2^{q}}\right).

Theorem 2.7
.

[
LP
, Theorem 16.3.4]

The map
\epsilon_{\mathbb{P}^{2}}
is a bijection.

The conditions of admissibility encode the cohomological orthogonality of exceptional collections as in the previous section. The third condition is vacuous when
\alpha,\beta
are the slopes of exceptional bundles. The second specifies that
\chi(E_{\beta},E_{\alpha})=0
, where
E_{\mu}
is the exceptional bundle of slope
\mu
. And one can readily check that
\epsilon(\alpha.\beta)=\mu(R_{E_{\gamma}}E_{\alpha})
where
(E_{\gamma},E_{\alpha},E_{\beta})
is a full exceptional collection. Note that on
\mathbb{P}^{2}
, the helix condition reads
R_{E_{\gamma}}E_{\alpha}=L_{E_{\alpha}}E_{\beta}
; left mutations are also right mutations.

In Section
4
, we will see how these properties generalize to
\mathbb{P}^{3}
.

2.3.
Exceptional bundles and stability on
\mathbb{P}^{2}

Exceptional bundles on
\mathbb{P}^{2}
are significant in the first place because of their appearance in the classification of the Chern characters of stable bundles, due to Drézet and Le Potier. Since exceptional bundles on
\mathbb{P}^{2}
are stable, given an exceptional bundle
E_{\alpha}
and a stable bundle
E
of slope
\mu
with
0\leq\mu-\alpha<3
, one sees from stability and Serre duality that

\hom(E,E_{\alpha})=0\quad\text{and}\quad\text{ext}^{2}(E,E_{\alpha})=\hom(E_{\alpha},E(-3))=0

so by Riemann-Roch

P(\alpha-\mu)-\Delta_{\alpha}<\Delta(E).

Drézet and Le Potier’s classification shows that this numerical constraint on
\Delta(E)
is the only constraint for stability.

Theorem 2.8
.

[
DLP
]

Set

\delta(\mu)=\sup_{\alpha\in\mathcal{E},|\alpha-\mu|<3}\{P(-|\alpha-\mu|)-\Delta(\alpha)\}.

Then
(r,\mu,\Delta)
is the Chern character of a stable bundle if and only if it is exceptional, or its Euler characteristic and Chern classes are integers with

\delta(\mu)\leq\Delta.

2.4.
Exceptional bundles on
\mathbb{P}^{3}

Exceptional bundles on
\mathbb{P}^{3}
are not as well-understood as those on
\mathbb{P}^{2}
, however, some important results have been obtained. Zube showed that each exceptional bundle on
\mathbb{P}^{3}
is slope-stable by restricting to an anticanonical K3 surface, by showing the restriction to the K3 surface is spherical and slope-stable
[
Zub
]
. Nogin classified the values of full exceptional collections in the Grothendieck group (i.e. Chern characters) and showed that each is equal to the value in the Grothendieck group of a constructive exceptional collection
[
Nog
]
.

Polishchuk built on these results. It follows from the vanishing result
[
Pol
, Theorem 1.2]
that any exceptional bundle on
\mathbb{P}^{3}
with the Chern character equal to that of a constructive exceptional bundle is itself constructive. It follows that the moduli space of a constructive exceptional bundle is a single reduced point. In fact it is proved that the action of the braid group on the set of
simple
helices on some Fano threefolds, including
\mathbb{P}^{3}
, is transitive
[
Pol
, Theorem 1.1]
, using Nogin’s results. This does not quite accomplish the classification of exceptional bundles on
\mathbb{P}^{3}
, as it is not clear that an exceptional bundle can be included into a full exceptional collection.

Each full exceptional collection on
\mathbb{P}^{3}
has length 4, and we often work with helices via the choice of a foundation, which forms a full exceptional collection. Let
\sigma
denote a constructive helix on
\mathbb{P}^{3}
, and choose a foundation
(E,F,G,H)
for
\sigma
.

Lemma 2.9
.

(1)

The slopes of the elements of a foundation are ordered:

\mu(E)<\mu(F)<\mu(G)<\mu(H).

(2)

The helix
\sigma
has exactly 8 distinct mutations, which are given on this foundation by the mutations of the pairs

(E,F),\quad(F,G),\quad(G,H),

the right mutation of the pair
(H(-4),E)
, and the left mutation of the pair
(H,E(4))
.

Proof.

For each pair
(A,B)
of bundles appearing consecutively in a helix we can form the evaluation map
\text{ev}:A\otimes\text{Hom}(A,B)\rightarrow B
, which is surjective. Since exceptional bundles on
\mathbb{P}^{3}
are slope-stable
[
Zub
]
, it follows that
\mu(A)=\mu(A\otimes\text{Hom}(A,B))<\text{image}(\text{ev})<\mu(B)
. Item (1) follows.

There are 8 possible mutations of a given helix on
\mathbb{P}^{3}
, defined on a foundation by the statement of the Lemma; we need to show that each is distinct. Applying each possible mutation to the foundation
(E,F,G,H)
and applying (1), we get inequalities

\displaystyle\mu(E)

\displaystyle<\mu(R_{E}(H(-4))),\mu(L_{F}G)<\mu(F),

\displaystyle\mu(F)

\displaystyle<\mu(R_{F}E),\mu(L_{G}H)<\mu(G),

\displaystyle\mu(G)

\displaystyle<\mu(R_{G}F),\mu(L_{H}F(4))<\mu(H).

It remains to show that the mutations whose slopes lie between the slopes of bundles in the original foundation are similarly ordered. These mutations all commute with one another, so we can form double mutation foundations, e.g.,
(F,R_{F}E,L_{G}H,G)
. The remaining inequalities follow again from Item (1).
∎

2.5.
The helix condition on
\mathbb{P}^{3}

We will repeatedly exploit the helix condition to produce new helices containing a given bundle. If
\sigma
is a helix on
\mathbb{P}^{3}
with foundation
(E,F,G,H)
, we usually apply the helix condition in the forms

R(E,F)=L(G,L_{H}(E(4)))\circ L(H,E(4))\quad\text{and}\quad L(G,H)=R(R_{E(4)}H,F(4))\circ R(H,E(4)).

On foundations, these are:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{(E,F,G,H)}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{(F,G,L_{H}(E(4)),H)}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{(E,F,G,H)}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{(E,R_{E}(H(-4)),F,G)}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{(F,R_{F}E,G,H)}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{(F,L_{G}(L_{H}(E(4))),G,H)}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{(E,F,L_{G}H,G)}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{(E,F,R_{F}(R_{E}(H(-4))),G)}}

When we have an instance of the helix relation of the form
\gamma_{1}=\gamma_{2}\circ\gamma_{3}
, we will often refer to these as a
helix triangle
. See Figure
1(b)
for an illustration.

The braid group naturally acts on the set of full exceptional collections via mutations. This action gives a way to identify new sets of mutations which produce the same helix, but we will not use it in the sequel. Instead, we will use the helix relation to produce new mutations.

2.6.
Exceptional bundles and stability on
\mathbb{P}^{3}

There are several natural ways to try to extend the classification of stable Chern characters on
\mathbb{P}^{2}
in terms of exceptional bundles to
\mathbb{P}^{3}
. On
\mathbb{P}^{2}
, when
F
is a stable bundle of slope
\mu
and
E_{\alpha}
is an exceptional bundle of slope
\alpha
with
0<\alpha-\mu<3
, then by stability
\text{Hom}(F,E_{\alpha})=0
and by Serre duality
\text{Ext}^{2}(F,E_{\alpha})=0
, so
\chi(F,E_{\alpha})<0
; applying this bound, and the entirely similar bound obtained from exceptional slopes
\alpha
with
0<\mu-\alpha<3
, for each exceptional bundle produces precisely the condition that
\Delta(F)\geq\delta(\mu)
from Theorem
2.8
.

If
F
is a stable bundle on
\mathbb{P}^{3}
of slope
\mu
and
E_{\alpha}
is a constructive exceptional bundle of slope
\alpha
with
0<\mu-\alpha<4
, then, again by stability and Serre duality,

\hom(F,E_{\alpha})=0\quad\text{and}\quad\text{ext}^{3}(F,E_{\alpha})=\hom(E_{\alpha},F(-4))=0.

If
\text{Ext}^{2}(F,E_{\alpha})=0
, then we would deduce an inequality
\chi(F,E_{\alpha})<0.

If similarly we have
0<\alpha-\mu<4
and a vanishing
\text{Ext}^{2}(E_{\alpha},F)=0
, then we would also conclude that
\chi(E_{\alpha},F)<0
, and these inequalityies together would produce a lower bound on
\Delta(F)
analogous to that on
\mathbb{P}^{2}
. Unfortunately, the following example, which applies recent work of Coskun-Huizenga-Smith
[
CHS
]
, shows that this
\text{Ext}^{2}
vanishing property does not hold in general, so this procedure does not produce the classification of stable Chern characters on
\mathbb{P}^{3}
.

Example 2.10
.

Consider a bundle
V
on
\mathbb{P}^{3}
which is defined via an exact sequence

0\rightarrow\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}(-4)^{8}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle M}}{{\rightarrow}}\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}(-3)^{11}\rightarrow V\rightarrow 0,

where
M
is a general matrix of linear forms. Then
[
CHS
, Theorem 5.1]
implies that
V
is slope-stable. In particular, since
h^{i}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}(-3))=0
for all

and
h^{3}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}(-4))=1
with
h^{i}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}(-4))=0
otherwise, we have
H^{2}(V)=\text{Ext}^{2}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{3}},V)=8
, which contradicts the above vanishing statement. The discriminant
\Delta(V)
does not lie above the lower bounds predicted above.

In other words, exceptional bundles on
\mathbb{P}^{3}
do not determine the sharp Bogomolov-Gieseker inequality, unlike
\mathbb{P}^{2}
. The sharp Bogomolov-Gieseker inequality on
\mathbb{P}^{3}
remains an interesting and difficult area of research (see, e.g.,
[
Sch
]
).

, with the property that each mutation
\sigma_{k-1}^{\prime}\mapsto\sigma_{k}^{\prime}
is admissible with respect to the prior mutation in the new sequence. The parallel mutations to the mutations
\sigma_{k-1}\mapsto\sigma_{k}
will be the central tool for forming these replacements.

See Example
3.13
for an example of a parallel sequence.

Because parallel mutations form squares in which the original mutation sits, we can iterate the process of forming parallel mutations.

Definition 3.11
.

If
\gamma:\sigma\mapsto\tau
is a mutation,
\gamma^{\prime}:\sigma^{\prime}\mapsto\tau^{\prime}
is a parallel mutation, and
\tau\mapsto\tau_{1}\mapsto\cdots\mapsto\tau_{k}
is a sequence of mutations, then the sequence
\tau^{\prime}\mapsto\tau_{1}^{\prime}\mapsto\cdots\mapsto\tau^{\prime}_{k}
obtained by taking parallel mutations at each step is called the
parallel sequence
to the given sequence.

We will use the notion of parallel mutations to replace arbitrary paths on the graph
\text{H}_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}
with admissible paths.

Proof.

The second statement obviously implies the first. We can reduce the proof of the lemma to two cases, as follows. We will consider the case where

is a right mutation, the left mutation case being entirely similar. Note that each of the descendants of

have slope less than each of the descendants of

have slope greater than the slope of

and

is obtained through successive

is obtained through successive

Consider the limit
\overline{\mu}
of the slopes
\mu(v_{1,k})
as

\overline{\mu}^{\prime}
of the slopes

We can compute the limits
\overline{\mu}
and
\overline{\mu}^{\prime}
explicitly, as follows. For each

to be the unique bundle with slope between

by the mutation

in the right branch off of

. Note that there are canonical isomorphisms

\text{Hom}(R_{k},R_{k+1})\simeq\text{Hom}(R_{k+1},R_{k+1})^{\vee}

so in particular their dimensions agree. Set
h_{R}
for this value on the right branch off of
\sigma_{i}
and
h_{L}
for this value along the left branch off of
\sigma_{j}
. Then along the right branch, we have

r(R_{k+1})=h_{R}r(R_{k})-r(R_{k-1}),\quad d(R_{k+1})=h_{R}d(R_{k})-d(R_{k-1}).

The rank
r(R_{n})
and degree
d(R_{n})
can be written explicitly by solving these generalized Fibonacci relations.

To do so, let us focus first on the right branch
\{R_{k}\}
. Set
r_{i}=r(R_{i})
for each

and
d_{i}=d(R_{i})
. Then we set

R(z)=\sum_{n\geq 0}r_{n}z^{n},\quad D(z)=\sum_{n\geq 0}d_{n}z^{n}

to be the associated generating functions. We have

\displaystyle R(z)

\displaystyle=r_{0}+r_{1}z+\sum_{n\geq 0}(h_{R}r(R_{n+1})-r(R_{n}))z^{n+2}

\displaystyle=r_{0}+r_{1}z+h_{R}z\sum_{n\geq 0}r(R_{n+1})z^{n+1}-z^{2}\sum_{n\geq 0}r(R_{n})z^{n}

\displaystyle=r_{0}+r_{1}z+h_{R}z(R(z)-r_{0})-z^{2}R(z)

Therefore

R(z)=\frac{r_{0}+(r_{1}-h_{R}r_{0})z}{z^{2}-h_{R}z+1}.

Let
\varphi_{R}
and
\psi_{R}
denote the roots of the polynomial
z^{2}-h_{R}z+1
. Both
\varphi_{R}
and
\psi_{R}
are positive, and without loss of generality we have
0<\varphi_{R}<1<\psi_{R}
.

We solve the partial fraction decomposition

R(z)=\frac{A_{R}}{z-\varphi_{R}}+\frac{B_{R}}{z-\psi_{R}}

with

A_{R}=\frac{r_{0}+(r_{1}-h_{R}r_{0})\varphi_{R}}{\varphi_{R}-\psi_{R}},\quad B_{R}=\frac{r_{0}+(r_{1}-h_{R}r_{0})\psi_{R}}{\psi_{R}-\varphi_{R}}.

Entirely similarly, we have

D(z)=\frac{C_{R}}{z-\varphi_{R}}+\frac{D_{R}}{z-\psi_{R}}

with

C_{R}=\frac{d_{0}+(d_{1}-h_{R}d_{0})\varphi_{R}}{\varphi_{R}-\psi_{R}},\quad D_{R}=\frac{d_{0}+(d_{1}-h_{R}d_{0})\psi_{R}}{\psi_{R}-\varphi_{R}}.

Now we can solve, using the fact that
\varphi_{R}\psi_{R}=1
,

\displaystyle R(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{A_{R}}{z-\varphi_{R}}+\frac{B_{R}}{z-\psi_{R}}

\displaystyle=A_{R}\frac{1}{z-\varphi_{R}}+B_{R}\frac{1}{z-\psi_{R}}

\displaystyle=-A_{R}\frac{1/\varphi_{R}}{1-\psi_{R}z}-B_{R}\frac{1/\psi_{R}}{1-\varphi_{R}z}

\displaystyle=-\frac{A_{R}}{\varphi_{R}}\sum_{n\geq 0}(\psi_{R}z)^{n}-\frac{B_{R}}{\psi_{R}}\sum_{n\geq 0}(\varphi_{R}z)^{n}.

Thus we have

r_{n}=-\frac{A_{R}}{\varphi_{R}}\psi_{R}^{n}-\frac{B_{R}}{\psi_{R}}\varphi_{R}^{n}

and similarly

d_{n}=-\frac{C_{R}}{\varphi_{R}}\psi_{R}^{n}-\frac{D_{R}}{\psi_{R}}\varphi_{R}^{n}.

In particular, we have

\mu(R_{n})=\frac{d_{n}}{r_{n}}=\frac{\frac{C_{R}}{\varphi_{R}}\psi_{R}^{n}+\frac{D_{R}}{\psi_{R}}\varphi_{R}^{n}}{\frac{A_{R}}{\varphi_{R}}\psi_{R}^{n}+\frac{B_{R}}{\psi_{R}}\varphi_{R}^{n}}.

Multiplying top and bottom by
\varphi^{n}
and taking the limit as
n\rightarrow\infty
, we obtain

\overline{\mu}=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\frac{C_{R}}{\varphi_{R}}+\frac{D_{R}}{\psi_{R}}\varphi_{R}^{2n}}{\frac{A_{R}}{\varphi_{R}}+\frac{B_{R}}{\psi_{R}}\varphi_{R}^{2n}}=\frac{C_{R}}{A_{R}}

as
0<\varphi_{R}<1
so
\varphi_{R}^{n}\rightarrow 0
.

Explicitly, this is

\overline{\mu}=\frac{d_{0}+(d_{1}-h_{R}d_{0})\varphi_{R}}{r_{0}+(r_{1}-h_{R}r_{0})\varphi_{R}}=:\frac{\overline{d}}{\overline{r}}.

Recall that we want to show that
\overline{\mu}<\overline{\mu}^{\prime}
. The same process as above gives us

\overline{\mu}^{\prime}=\frac{d^{\prime}_{0}+(d^{\prime}_{1}-h_{L}d^{\prime}_{0})\varphi_{L}}{r^{\prime}_{0}+(r^{\prime}_{1}-h_{L}r^{\prime}_{0})\varphi_{L}}=:\frac{\overline{d}^{\prime}}{\overline{r}^{\prime}}.

It is sufficient to show that
\overline{d}^{\prime}\overline{r}-\overline{d}\overline{r}^{\prime}>0
. We have

\displaystyle\overline{d}^{\prime}\overline{r}-\overline{d}\overline{r}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=(d^{\prime}_{0}+(d^{\prime}_{1}-h_{L}d^{\prime}_{0})\varphi_{L})(r_{0}+(r_{1}-h_{R}r_{0})\varphi_{R})

\displaystyle\qquad-(d_{0}+(d_{1}-h_{R}d_{0})\varphi_{R})(r^{\prime}_{0}+(r^{\prime}_{1}-h_{L}r^{\prime}_{0})\varphi_{L})

\displaystyle=((d^{\prime}_{1}-h_{L}d^{\prime}_{0})(r_{1}-h_{R}r_{0})-(d_{1}-h_{R}d_{0})(r^{\prime}_{1}-h_{L}r^{\prime}_{0}))\varphi_{R}\varphi_{L}

(1)

\displaystyle\quad+(d_{0}^{\prime}(r_{1}-h_{R}r_{0})-(d_{1}-h_{R}d_{0})r^{\prime}_{0})\varphi_{R}+((d^{\prime}_{1}-h_{L}d^{\prime}_{0})r_{0}-d_{0}(r^{\prime}_{1}-h_{L}r^{\prime}_{0}))\varphi_{L}

\displaystyle\qquad+(d^{\prime}_{0}r_{0}-d_{0}r^{\prime}_{0}).

We will show that each coefficient on
\varphi_{L}\varphi_{R},\varphi_{R},\varphi_{L},1>0
is positive. Note that in the mutation forming
R_{1}
from
R_{0}
, we have an exact sequence

0\rightarrow Z\rightarrow R_{0}^{\oplus h_{R}}\rightarrow R_{1}\rightarrow 0

so that
d(Z)=h_{R}d_{0}-d_{1}
and
r(Z)=h_{R}r_{0}-r_{1}
. Similarly, we have an exact sequence

0\rightarrow L_{1}\rightarrow L_{0}^{\oplus h_{L}}\rightarrow Z^{\prime}\rightarrow 0

with
d(Z^{\prime})=h_{L}d_{0}^{\prime}-d_{1}^{\prime}
and
r(Z)=h_{L}r_{0}^{\prime}-r_{1}^{\prime}
. We have
\mu(Z)<\mu(E_{0})<\mu(E_{1})<\mu(E_{1}^{\prime})<\mu(E_{0}^{\prime})
by Lemmas
2.9
and
3.14
.

Then the constant term of (
3.2
) is positive since
\mu(E_{0}^{\prime})>\mu(E_{0})
. The coefficient on
\varphi_{R}
is positive since
\mu(E_{0}^{\prime})>\mu(Z)
, the coefficient on
\varphi_{L}
is positive since
\mu(E_{0})<\mu(Z^{\prime})
, and the coefficient on
\varphi_{L}\varphi_{R}
is positive since
\mu(Z)<\mu(Z^{\prime})
. Thus
\overline{\mu}<\overline{\mu}^{\prime}
, and the lemma is proved.
∎

The following proposition, which shows that the inductive structure of
\Gamma_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}^{\prime}
is especially simple, is a corollary of Lemma
3.15
. It will be fundamental to establishing our main theorems.

Proposition 3.16
.

The graph
\Gamma^{\prime}_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}
is a connected tree; every vertex except for that corresponding to
\sigma_{0}
has degree 4, and
\sigma_{0}
has degree 2. Every constructive exceptional bundle
E
of slope
0<\mu(E)<1
occurs in a helix in
\Gamma_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}^{\prime}
.

Proof.

The fact that
\Gamma_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}^{\prime}
is a tree follows from Lemma
3.15
. Specifically, the branches off of a single vertex are disjoint from the lemma. The proof of the lemma shows that to check disjointness of two branches, all that is needed is that the first two leaves in the first branch are bounded by the first two leaves in the other branch. This is clearly the case for branches off of distinct vertices as well.

The proof of Lemma
3.15
also shows that the branches of this tree are ordered by the slope of the bundle produced. In particular, it follows that no admissible mutation can produce a bundle of slope between 0 and 1 from a bundle with slope less than 0 or larger than 1. It follows from this and Proposition
3.12
that each constructive exceptional bundle of slope between 0 and 1 appears in
\Gamma_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}^{\prime}
, and connectedness also follows from this.
∎

4.
Classifying constructive exceptional bundles

In this section we construct a bijection
\epsilon_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}
between the 3-adic rationals and the Chern characters of constructive exceptional bundles on
\mathbb{P}^{3}
, analogous to Drézet and Le Potier’s construction of
\epsilon_{\mathbb{P}^{2}}
on
\mathbb{P}^{2}
. We begin with some notational set-up.

Notation
.

The rational Grothendieck group
K(\mathbb{P}^{3})_{\mathbb{Q}}=K(D^{b}(\mathbb{P}^{3}))_{\mathbb{Q}}
of
\mathbb{P}^{3}
is isomorphic to the rational Chow ring
A^{*}(\mathbb{P}^{3})\otimes\mathbb{Q}
(or singular cohomology
H^{*}(\mathbb{P}^{3},\mathbb{Q})
) via the Chern character map

\text{ch}:K(\mathbb{P}^{3})\otimes\mathbb{Q}\rightarrow A^{*}(\mathbb{P}^{3})\otimes\mathbb{Q}\simeq\mathbb{Q}^{4},\quad v\mapsto(\text{ch}_{0}(v).H^{3},\text{ch}_{1}(v).H^{2},\text{ch}_{2}(v).H,\text{ch}_{3}(v))

where
H
is the hyperplane class. The Chern characters
\text{ch}_{i}
can be regarded as
\mathbb{Q}
-valued functions
\text{ch}_{i}=\text{ch}_{i}.H^{3-i}
on
K(\mathbb{P}^{3})
. These form an integral basis, and we use them as coordinates.

Hirzebruch-Riemann-Roch (which we will often shorten to simply Riemann-Roch) expresses the bilinear form
\chi(-,-)
on
K(\mathbb{P}^{3})
in terms of these coordinates as follows:

\chi(\mathcal{O},E)=\chi(E)=\text{ch}_{0}(E)+\frac{11}{6}\text{ch}_{1}(E)+2\text{ch}_{2}(E)+\text{ch}_{3}(E)

(where the coefficients can be read off as the Todd class of
\mathbb{P}^{3}
backwards), and

\displaystyle\chi(E,F)=\chi(F\otimes E^{\vee})

\displaystyle=\text{ch}_{0}(F)\text{ch}_{0}(E)

\displaystyle+\frac{11}{6}(\text{ch}_{1}(F)\text{ch}_{0}(E)-\text{ch}_{1}(E)\text{ch}_{0}(F))

\displaystyle+2(\text{ch}_{2}(F)\text{ch}_{0}(E)-\text{ch}_{1}(F)\text{ch}_{1}(E)+\text{ch}_{2}(E)\text{ch}_{0}(F))

\displaystyle+\text{ch}_{3}(F)\text{ch}_{0}(E)-\text{ch}_{2}(F)\text{ch}_{1}(E)+\text{ch}_{2}(E)\text{ch}_{1}(F)-\text{ch}_{3}(E)\text{ch}_{0}(F).

It follows that for a nonzero class
v\in K(\mathbb{P}^{3})
, the conditions
\chi(-,v)=0
and
\chi(v,-)=0
are linear conditions in the Chern character basis for
K(\mathbb{P}^{3})
.

In particular, we will also consider the affine space patch on the projectivization
\mathbb{P}(K(\mathbb{P}^{3})_{\mathbb{Q}})
defined by setting
\text{ch}_{0}\neq 0
. It is a three-dimensional rational vector space with basis
(\frac{\text{ch}_{1}}{\text{ch}_{0}},\frac{\text{ch}_{2}}{\text{ch}_{0}},\frac{\text{ch}_{3}}{\text{ch}_{0}})
. Any sheaf
E
on
\mathbb{P}^{3}
with nonzero rank defines a unique point in this space.

The following definition encodes the data of cohomological orthogonality among exceptional collections.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
E
,
G
, and
H
be sheaves on
\mathbb{P}^{3}
(or classes in
K(\mathbb{P}^{3})
). Then, by Hirzebruch-Riemann-Roch, the conditions

\chi(-,E)=\chi(G,-)=\chi(H,-)=0

are linear conditions on
K(\mathbb{P}^{3})\simeq\mathbb{Z}^{4}
. When
E
,
G
, and
H
are independent in
K(\mathbb{P}^{3})
, they determine a 1-dimensional subspace
L\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{4}
; if
L
is not contained in the locus
(\text{ch}_{0}=0)\subseteq K(\mathbb{P}^{3})
, then it determines a unique point
\ell
in the
\frac{\text{ch}_{i}}{\text{ch}_{0}}
-space defined above, and we define

\text{perp}(E,G,H):=\ell\in\left\{\frac{\text{ch}_{1}}{\text{ch}_{0}},\frac{\text{ch}_{2}}{\text{ch}_{0}},\frac{\text{ch}_{3}}{\text{ch}_{0}}\right\}.

Explicit formulas for
\text{perp}(E,G,H)
in terms of the Chern characters of
E
,
G
, and
H
can be easily given, but are rather complicated and we will not use them, so we do not list them.

When
(Z,E,G,H)
are the foundation of a constructive helix on
\mathbb{P}^{3}
, they form a full exceptional collection for
D^{b}(\mathbb{P}^{3})
. The right mutation bundle
F=R_{E}Z
fits into a new exceptional collection
(E,F,G,H)
, and
F
satisfies the linear conditions above. Similar conditions are satisfied by left mutations. The point
\ell
above corresponding to
F
is thus well-defined, and since
\text{ch}_{1}
and
\text{ch}_{0}
are coprime for exceptional bundles, determines the full Chern character of
F
in terms of the Chern characters of
E
,
G
, and
H
.

Example 4.2
.

We compute perp for the exceptional collection
(\mathcal{O},T(-1),\mathcal{O}(1),\mathcal{O}(2))
, to recompute
\text{ch}(T(-1))
. This can easily be done by using the defining exact sequence, but is more convenient to do with the perp operator in Theorem
4.3
.

The Chern character
\text{ch}(T(-1))
satisfies the orthogonality relations

\chi(T(-1),\mathcal{O})=\chi(\mathcal{O}(1),T(-1))=\chi(\mathcal{O}(2),T(-1))=0.

Set
\text{ch}_{i}=\text{ch}_{i}(T(-1))
. Via Riemann-Roch, these equations can be written

\displaystyle\chi(T(-1),\mathcal{O})

\displaystyle=\text{ch}_{0}-\frac{11}{6}\text{ch}_{1}+2\text{ch}_{2}-\text{ch}_{3}=0

\displaystyle\chi(\mathcal{O}(1),T(-1))

\displaystyle=\text{ch}_{0}+\frac{11}{6}(\text{ch}_{1}-\text{ch}_{0})+2(\text{ch}_{2}-\text{ch}_{1}+\frac{1}{2}\text{ch}_{0})

\displaystyle\qquad+\text{ch}_{3}-\text{ch}_{2}+\frac{1}{2}\text{ch}_{1}-\frac{1}{6}\text{ch}_{0}=0

\displaystyle\chi(\mathcal{O}(2),T(-1))

\displaystyle=\text{ch}_{0}+\frac{11}{6}(\text{ch}_{1}-2\text{ch}_{0})+2(\text{ch}_{2}-2\text{ch}_{1}+2\text{ch}_{0})

\displaystyle\qquad+\text{ch}_{3}-2\text{ch}_{2}+2\text{ch}_{1}-\frac{4}{3}\text{ch}_{0}=0

Dividing by
\text{ch}_{0}\neq 0
, we obtain three linear equations in the three variables
\frac{\text{ch}_{1}}{\text{ch}_{0}}
,
\frac{\text{ch}_{2}}{\text{ch}_{0}}
, and
\frac{\text{ch}_{3}}{\text{ch}_{0}}
, which is easily solved by hand or by computer. Then Riemann-Roch implies that
\text{ch}_{1}
and
\text{ch}_{0}
are coprime, so one can determine
\text{ch}_{0}
and the rest of the Chern character from these. One can then easily check that we get

Theorem 4.3
.

Let

be a constructive exceptional bundle on
\mathbb{P}^{3}

. This association produces a bijection

\epsilon_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}:\{\text{3-adic rationals}\}\rightarrow\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}\text{Chern characters of constructive}\\
\text{exceptional bundles on }\mathbb{P}^{3}\end{array}\right\}

defined inductively as

\epsilon_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}(n)=\mathrm{ch}(\mathcal{O}(n))=\left(1,n,\frac{n^{2}}{2},\frac{n^{3}}{6}\right)\quad n\in\mathbb{Z},

and for

\displaystyle\epsilon_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}\left(\frac{3p+1}{3^{q+1}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{perp}\left(\epsilon_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}\left(\frac{p}{3^{q}}\right),E_{2}\left(\epsilon_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}\left(\frac{p}{3^{q}}\right)\right),E_{3}\left(\epsilon_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}\left(\frac{p}{3^{q}}\right)\right)\right)

\displaystyle\epsilon_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}\left(\frac{3p+2}{3^{q+1}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{perp}\left(E_{1}\left(\epsilon_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}\left(\frac{p+1}{3^{q}}\right)\right),\epsilon_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}\left(\frac{p+1}{3^{q}}\right),E_{3}\left(\epsilon_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}\left(\frac{p+1}{3^{q}}\right)\right)\right).

As in the statement of the theorem, we will sometimes abuse notation and denote by

Definition 4.4

When

Conjecture 4.5

(Weak Brill-Noether for exceptional bundles)
.

Let
E
be a constructive exceptional bundle on
\mathbb{P}^{3}
. Then
h^{i}(E)\neq 0
for at most one
i
, and

\chi(E)=\begin{cases}h^{0}(E)&\text{if }\mu(E)\geq 0,\\
-h^{1}(E)&\text{if }-4<\mu(E)<0\text{ and }\chi(E)<0,\\

Conjecture

5.
Global generation

Recall that given a mutation
\gamma:\sigma\mapsto\tau
, there are three mutations of
\tau
that are
\gamma
-admissible; see Example
3.4
. To prove global generation, we will only need to consider these mutations; see Figure
9
for a drawing of the these as a graph.

Theorem 5.1
.

If
E
is a constructive exceptional bundle of slope
\mu\geq 0
, then
E
is globally generated.

Proof.

First, recall in the proof of Theorem
4.3
that to each constructive exceptional bundle
E
we have associated a distinguished helix
\sigma=\sigma(E)
containing
E
. We say
E
is a right mutation if the final mutation forming
E
in the sequence described in the classification is a right mutation, and similary for left mutations. If
E
is a right mutation we include it into a foundation for
\sigma
of the form
(A,E,B,C)
, and the Chern character of
E
is associated to a rational number of the form
\frac{3p+1}{3^{q}}
. If
E
is a left mutation we include it into a foundation for
\sigma
of the form
(A,B,E,C)
, and the Chern character of
E
is associated to a rational number of the form
\frac{3p+2}{3^{q}}
. In each case,
q=\text{ord}(E)
.

To prove the theorem we induct on the order of
E
. If the order of
E
is zero then
E
is a line bundle, and global generation when
\mu(E)\geq 0
is clear. Otherwise, we may write
E
as a left or right mutation

0\rightarrow E\rightarrow C\otimes\text{Hom}(C,F)\rightarrow F\rightarrow 0,\quad 0\rightarrow F\rightarrow A\otimes\text{Hom}(F,A)^{\vee}\rightarrow E\rightarrow 0

where
C
(resp.
A
) has order one less than
E
.

We address the right mutation case first. In this case, by construction we have
\text{ch}(A)=\epsilon_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}(\frac{p}{3^{q-1}})
. Whenever
\mu(\epsilon_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}(x))>0
, we have
x>0
. Thus we have
3p+1>0
, hence
p\geq 0
. So since
\epsilon_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}
is an increasing function,
\mu(A)\geq 0
. By induction on the order we can assume
A
is globally generated, so
E
is a quotient of a globally generated bundle. It follows that
E
is globally generated.

In the left mutation case, the first step is to reduce to the case of a right mutation using the helix relation. Recall that in this case,
\sigma
has a foundation
(A,B,E,C)
. Writing
E=L_{C}F
, the helix from which
E
is formed by mutation has foundation
(A,B,C,F)
. The helix relation takes the form
L_{C}=R_{B}R_{F(-4)}
. These two right mutations are defined via exact sequences

0\rightarrow F(-4)\rightarrow A\otimes\text{Hom}(F(-4),A)^{\vee}\rightarrow R_{A}F(-4)=E^{\prime}\rightarrow 0

and

0\rightarrow E^{\prime}\rightarrow B\otimes\text{Hom}(E^{\prime},B)^{\vee}\rightarrow R_{B}E^{\prime}=E\rightarrow 0.

The next step is to show that
\mu(B)\geq 0
, in which case we can again use induction on the order to deduce that
B
is globally generated, from which it will again follow from the second exact sequence that
E
is globally generated as well.

We first introduce some notation and terminology. Given a constructive exceptional bundle
M
on
\mathbb{P}^{3}
, we can include it as in Theorem
4.3
into a helix
\sigma(M)
of order
\text{ord}(M)
. We set
\text{par}_{L}(M)
and
\text{par}_{R}(M)
, respectively, to be the “parent” bundles directly to the left and right of
M
in
\sigma
, and writing
\epsilon_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}(\eta)=\text{ch}(M)
, we write
\text{par}_{L}(\eta)
and
\text{par}_{R}(\eta)
for the rationals
\epsilon_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}^{-1}(\text{par}_{L}(M))
and
\epsilon_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}^{-1}(\text{par}_{R}(M))
, respectively. Write
\epsilon_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}(\eta)=E
, for the bundle
E
appearing in this proof. Because
E
is a quotient of a direct sum of copies of the bundle
B=\text{par}_{L}(E)
, we need to show that
\mu(\text{par}_{L}(E))\geq 0
. The projection
\eta\mapsto\epsilon_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}(\eta)\mapsto\mu(\epsilon_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}(\eta))
is increasing by slope-stability, so it is enough to check that
\text{par}_{L}(\eta)\geq 0
when
\eta>0
.

Using Theorem
4.3
we can isolate the left mutations producing exceptional bundles and prove this by induction. As in Figure
9
, there are only three left mutations to consider:
L0
,
L1
, and
L2
. We will describe these mutations explicitly. We set
\eta=(3p+2)/3^{q+1}
. On foundations, we have:

\displaystyle L0:(B,\underline{C},F,A(4))

\displaystyle\mapsto(B,L_{C}F,C,A(4))

\displaystyle L1:(B,\underline{C},F,A(4))

\displaystyle\mapsto(B,L_{C}F,C,A(4))

\displaystyle L2:(A,\underline{B},C,F)

\displaystyle\mapsto(A,B,L_{F}C,C).

where underlined bundles are new. In the first two mutations,
\text{par}_{L}(C)=B
and
C
has order less than
E=L_{C}F
, so we conclude by induction. In the third mutation (
L2
) we argue as follows. We will show the contrapositive: it is enough to show that if the numerator of
\epsilon_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}^{-1}(B)
is negative, then the numerator of
\epsilon_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}^{-1}(L_{C}F)
is nonpositive. In this case,
B
is a left mutation, and we write
\epsilon_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}^{-1}(B)=(3p+2)/3^{q}
, and we have
\epsilon_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}^{-1}(C)=(p+1)/3^{q}
and
\epsilon_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}^{-1}(L_{C}F)=(3(p+1)-1)/3^{q+1}
. The numerator of
\epsilon_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}^{-1}(B)
is negative if and only if
p<-2/3
, if and only if the numerator of
\epsilon_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}^{-1}(L_{C}F)
is
3p+2<0
, as required. This concludes the proof.
∎

Remark 5.2
.

The same statement is true for
\mathbb{P}^{2}
; see
[
CHK
, Proposition 4.4]
. The proof is substantially simpler on
\mathbb{P}^{2}
, for two reasons. First, it is possible to explicitly identify the “parent” bundles of an exceptional bundle on
\mathbb{P}^{2}
, and the case of a left mutation is indistinguishable from the case of a right mutation.
