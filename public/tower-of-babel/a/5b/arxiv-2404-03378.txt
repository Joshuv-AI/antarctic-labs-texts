Abstract
. In this paper, we introduce the spectral projection operators ℙ m \mathbb{P}_{m} on non-degenerate nilpotent Lie groups 𝒩 \mathcal{N} of step two, associated to the joint spectrum of sub-Laplacian and derivatives in step two. We construct their kernels P m ​ ( 𝐲 , 𝐭 ) P_{m}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t}) by using Laguerre calculus and find a simple integral representation formula for 𝐲 ≠ 0 \mathbf{y}\neq 0 . Then we show the kernels are Lipschitzian homogeneous functions on 𝒩 ∖ { 𝟎 } \mathcal{N}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\} by analytic continuation. Moreover, they are shown to be Calderón-Zygmund kernels, so that the spectral projection operator ℙ m \mathbb{P}_{m} can be extended to a bounded operator from L p ​ ( 𝒩 ) L^{p}(\mathcal{N}) to itself. We also prove a convergence theorem of the Abel sum lim R → 1 − ∑ m = 0 ∞ R m ​ ℙ m ​ ϕ = ϕ \lim_{R\rightarrow 1^{-}}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}R^{m}\mathbb{P}_{m}\phi=\phi by estimating the L p ​ ( 𝒩 ) L^{p}(\mathcal{N}) -norms of ℙ m \mathbb{P}_{m} . 1.
Introduction

The Heisenberg groups
\mathcal{H}_{n}
are non-commutative nilpotent Lie groups, with underlying manifold
\mathbb{R}^{2n+1}
and the group law

(\mathbf{x},t)\cdot(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},s)=\bigg(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{x}^{\prime},t+s-2\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}(x_{j}x_{n+j}^{\prime}-x_{n+j}x_{j}^{\prime})\bigg),

where
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{2n}),\mathbf{x}^{\prime}=(x^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,x^{\prime}_{2n})\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}
,
a_{j}\in\mathbb{R}_{+},j=1,2,\ldots,n
. Many aspects of harmonic analysis on the Euclidean space have been successfully generalized to the Heisenberg group (cf.
[
24
]
). But the theory of Fourier transform on the Heisenberg group has not been completed so far (cf.
[
10
]
[
12
]
[
13
]
[
14
]
[
18
]
and reference therein).
The spectral theory of differential operators on the Heisenberg group is also more difficult and complicated than its Euclidean counterpart.

Strichartz
[
26
]
investigated the joint spectral
theory of the sub-Laplacian operators
\Delta_{b}
and
iT
on the Heisenberg group, where
T=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}
.
Since
\Delta_{b}
and
iT
are essentially self-adjoint strongly commutative operators, there is a well defined joint spectrum, which is the complement of the set of
(\lambda,\mu)\in\mathbb{C}^{2}
where neither
\lambda I-\Delta_{b}
or
\mu I-iT
is invertible. It is the closed subset of the plane called
Heisenberg fan
, consisting of the union of rays

R_{k,\pm 1}=\left\{(\lambda,\mu):\mu=\frac{\pm\lambda}{n+2k}\right\}

for
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and the limit ray
R_{\infty}=\{(\lambda,\mu):\mu=0,\lambda\geq 0\}.

The associated spectral projection operators are convolution operators with kernel that can be described explicitly. These kernels are homogeneous functions of degree
-2n-2
on
\mathcal{H}_{n}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}
and are Calderón-Zygmund kernels. Strichartz also gave the spectral decomposition for all
\phi\in L^{p}(\mathcal{H}_{n})
,
1<p<\infty
.
Chang-Tie
[
8
]
used a different approach to this problem from the point of view of Laguerre calculus and derived the explicit kernels for the projection operators. They deduced the regularity properties for spectral projection operators in other function spaces, e.g. Hardy space as well as Morry space.

Recently, there are many works for harmonic analysis on nilpotent Lie group of step two and its applications to other parts of analysis (cf.
[
9
]
[
21
]
[
22
]
[
23
]
[
28
]
[
29
]
[
30
]
and references therein). For example, as the first spectral projection operator on the Heisenberg group is the distinguished Szegö projection, the first spectral projection operator on some nondegenerate nilpotent Lie group of step two is associated to the Szegö projection in quaternionic analysis(cf.
[
23
]
[
28
]
[
29
]
). It is necessary to develop the joint spectral theory on this kind of groups for further applications. A nilpotent Lie group
\mathcal{N}
of step two is a vector space
\mathbb{R}^{{2n}}\times\mathbb{R}^{r}
with the group multiplication given by

(1.1)

(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{t})\cdot(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{s})=\left(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t}+\mathbf{s}+2B(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\right),

where
B:\mathbb{R}^{2n}\times\mathbb{R}^{2n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{r}
is a skew-symmetric mapping. The sub-Laplacian on
\mathcal{N}
is the differential operator

(1.2)

\Delta_{b}:=-\frac{1}{4}\sum_{k=1}^{{2n}}\mathbf{Y}_{k}\mathbf{Y}_{k},

where
\mathbf{Y}_{k}
’s are standard left vector fields on
\mathcal{N}
(cf. (
2.1
)). Let

\mathbf{T}=(\mathbf{T}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{T}_{r}):=\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial t_{1}},\ldots,\frac{\partial}{\partial t_{r}}\right).

The spectrum of
\Delta_{b}
alone is the set of nonnegative numbers

\{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}:\lambda\geq 0\}
while the spectrum of
i\mathbf{T}
is the set
\mathbb{R}^{r}
. These operators are commutative, then their joint spectrum of
(\Delta_{b},i\mathbf{T})
is well defined and contains

(1.3)

\sigma(\Delta_{b},i\mathbf{T})=\bigcup_{\mathbf{k}=(k_{1},\ldots,k_{n})\in\left(\mathbb{Z}_{+}\right)^{n}}\left\{(\lambda,-\tau)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{r}:\lambda=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mu_{j}(\tau)(2k_{j}+1)\right\},

where
\mu_{j}(\tau)
’s are defined by Proposition
2.1
.

Laguerre calculus on non-degenerate nilpotent Lie group of step two was developed in
[
7
]
. It is more complicated compared to the Heisenberg group case, since we need to use
\tau
-coordinates for each
\tau
in the second step
\mathbb{R}^{r}
to define Laguerre distribution. Let
\widetilde{\mathscr{L}}_{\mathbf{k}}^{(\mathbf{0})}(\mathbf{y},\tau)
be the partial Fourier transform of Laguerre distribution on
\mathcal{N}
. Then the function

(1.4)

\Psi_{\mathbf{k},\tau}^{(\lambda)}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t}):=e^{i\mathbf{t}\cdot\tau}\widetilde{\mathscr{L}}_{\mathbf{k}}^{(\mathbf{0})}(\mathbf{y},\tau)

is smooth in
(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t})\in\mathbb{R}^{2n+r}
for fixed
\tau\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
and is the eigenfunction corresponding to the joint spectrum
(\lambda,-\tau)\in\sigma(\Delta_{b},i\mathbf{T})
, that is,

\displaystyle(\lambda\mathbf{I}-\Delta_{b})\Psi_{\mathbf{k},\tau}^{(\lambda)}=\mathbf{0},

\displaystyle(-\tau_{\beta}\mathbf{I}-i\mathbf{T}_{\beta})\Psi_{\mathbf{k},\tau}^{(\lambda)}=\mathbf{0},\,\,\,\,\beta=1,\ldots,r,

(cf. Proposition
2.4
).
For fixed
m
, the spectral projection operator on
\mathcal{N}
is defined formally as

(\mathbb{P}_{m}\phi)(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t}):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{r}}\phi\ast\sum_{|\mathbf{k}|=m}\Psi_{\mathbf{k},\tau}^{(\lambda)}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t})d\tau,

where
(\lambda,-\tau)\in\sigma(\Delta_{b},i\mathbf{T})
.

To define the operator
\mathbb{P}_{m}
explicitly, we introduce a kernel in terms of Laguerre functions

(1.5)

\displaystyle Q_{m}(\mathbf{y},\tau):=\sum_{|\mathbf{k}|=m}\widetilde{\mathscr{L}}_{\mathbf{k}}^{(\mathbf{0})}(\mathbf{y},\tau),

which is proved to be integrable in
\tau
for
\mathbf{y}\neq\mathbf{0}
. So we can define its inverse partial Fourier transform as

(1.6)

P_{m}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t}):=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{r}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{r}}e^{i\mathbf{t}\cdot\tau}Q_{m}(\mathbf{y},\tau)d\tau,\,\,\,\,\text{for}\,\,\mathbf{y}\neq\mathbf{0}.

On the other hand, we introduce a distribution
\mathscr{P}_{m}
on
\mathcal{N}
defined by the pair

(1.7)

\left\langle\mathscr{P}_{m},\phi\right\rangle:=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{r}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n+r}}Q_{m}(\mathbf{y},\tau)\widetilde{\phi}(\mathbf{y},-\tau)d\mathbf{y}d\tau,

for
\phi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{N})
, the space of Schwartz functions on
\mathcal{N}
, where

\widetilde{\phi}
is the partial Fourier transformation of function
\phi
.
It is proved to be a homogeneous distribution of degree
-Q
, where
Q:=2n+2r
(cf. Proposition
4.1
).
Then the
spectral projection operator
is defined as

(\mathbb{P}_{m}\phi)(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t}):=\phi*\mathscr{P}_{m}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t}),

for
\phi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{N})
. We can give the integral representation of
P_{m}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t})
for
\mathbf{y}\neq\mathbf{0}
.

Proposition 1.1
.

On a non-degenerate nilpotent Lie group of step two
\mathcal{N}
,
P_{m}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t})
has the integral representation:

(1.8)

\displaystyle P_{m}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t})=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{r}C_{m,j}\mathcal{I}_{m,j}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t}),\,\,\text{for}\,\,\mathbf{y}\neq\mathbf{0},

where

(1.9)

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{m,j}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t})=\int_{S^{r-1}}\frac{(\det\mathcal{B}^{\tau})^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\langle\mathcal{B}^{\tau}\mathbf{y},\mathbf{y}\rangle+i\mathbf{t}\cdot\tau\right)^{m-j}}{\left(\langle\mathcal{B}^{\tau}\mathbf{y},\mathbf{y}\rangle-i\mathbf{t}\cdot\tau\right)^{m+n+r-j}}d\tau,

and

\mathcal{B}^{\tau}:=[(B^{\tau})^{t}B^{\tau}]^{\frac{1}{2}}

is a
2n\times 2n
symmetric matrix, and constants
C_{m,j}
given by (
3.5
) depend on
m,j,n,r
.

This is a very simple formula since Laguerre distributions are defined in terms of
\tau
-coordinates
\mathbf{y}^{\tau}
, while (
1.8
)-(
1.9
) only depend on
\mathbf{y}
, not
\mathbf{y}^{\tau}
.

P_{m}
can be further continuated to a Lipschitzian function on
\mathcal{N}\backslash\{\mathbf{0}\}
by analytic continuation (cf. Theorem
3.1
).

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\mathcal{N}
be a non-degenerate nilpotent Lie group of step two.
The distribution
\mathscr{P}_{m}
and
p.v.P_{m}
on
\mathcal{N}
satisfy

(1.10)

\mathscr{P}_{m}=p.v.P_{m}+C\delta_{\mathbf{0}},

for some constant
C
, where
\delta_{\mathbf{0}}
is the delta distribution at
\mathbf{0}
and for
\phi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{N})
,

(1.11)

\langle p.v.P_{m},\phi\rangle=\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n+r}\backslash B(\mathbf{0},\varepsilon)}\phi(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t})P_{m}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t})d\mathbf{y}d\mathbf{t},

where
B(\mathbf{0},\varepsilon):=\{(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t})\in\mathcal{N}:(|\mathbf{y}|^{4}+|\mathbf{t}|^{2})^{\frac{1}{4}}\leq\varepsilon\}
.
P_{m}
has mean value zero, that is,

(1.12)

\int_{\partial B(\mathbf{0},1)}P_{m}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t})d\mathbf{y}d\mathbf{t}=0.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\mathcal{N}
be a non-degenerate nilpotent Lie group of step two. Then
\mathbb{P}_{m}
is a Calderón-Zygmund operator and then can be extended to a bounded operator from the
L^{p}(\mathcal{N})
space to itself. Moreover, they are mutually orthogonal project operators in
L^{2}(\mathcal{N})
, that is,

\mathbb{P}_{m_{1}}\circ\mathbb{P}_{m_{2}}=\delta_{m_{1}}^{m_{2}}\mathbb{P}_{m_{1}}.

Chang-Markina-Wang
[
7
, Theorem 4.1]
showed that for
\phi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{N})
,

\lim_{R\rightarrow 1^{-}}\sum_{|\mathbf{k}|=0}^{\infty}\phi\ast\mathscr{L}_{\mathbf{k}}^{(\mathbf{0})}R^{|\mathbf{k}|}=\phi.

We generalize this convergence theorem to
L^{p}(\mathcal{N})
functions.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
\mathcal{N}
be a non-degenerate nilpotent Lie group of step two. Then for
\phi\in L^{p}(\mathcal{N}),1<p<\infty
,
we have
\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}R^{m}\mathbb{P}_{m}\phi\in L^{p}(\mathcal{N})
and

\lim_{R\rightarrow 1^{-}}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}R^{m}\mathbb{P}_{m}\phi=\phi,

exists in
L^{p}(\mathcal{N})
.

On
L^{2}(\mathcal{N})
, we have the following decomposition of functions without using Abel sum.

Corollary 1.1
.

Let
\mathcal{N}
be a non-degenerate nilpotent Lie group of step two. Then for
\phi\in L^{2}(\mathcal{N})
, we have

(1.13)

\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}_{m}\phi=\phi,

in
L^{2}(\mathcal{N})
.

As we almost accomplish the proofs of main theorems, we noted the reference
[
20
]
, where the authors studied the spectral projection operators on H-type groups, a special kind of nilpotent Lie groups of step two. For this kind of groups, eigenfunctions of joint spectrum can be written down directly as

\displaystyle\Delta_{b}u=(2k+n)|\tau|u,

with

u(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t}):=e^{-i\tau\cdot\mathbf{t}}L_{k}^{n-1}\left(\frac{|\tau||\mathbf{y}|^{2}}{2}\right)e^{-\frac{1}{4}|\tau||\mathbf{y}|^{2}}

by using properties of the Laguerre functions.
That means
u
is an eigenfunction of the sub-Laplacian operator
\Delta_{b}
with the eigenvalue
2k+n
and by using explicit integrals, they can also write down the kernel of spectrum projection operator in a very concrete way. But for general non-degenerate nilpotent Lie group of step two, we have

\displaystyle\Delta_{b}\left(e^{i\mathbf{t}\cdot\tau}\widetilde{\mathscr{L}}_{\mathbf{k}}^{(\mathbf{0})}(\mathbf{y},\tau)\right)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mu_{j}(\tau)(2k_{j}+1)e^{i\mathbf{t}\cdot\tau}\widetilde{\mathscr{L}}_{\mathbf{k}}^{(\mathbf{0})}(\mathbf{y},\tau).

It is worth mentioning that eigenvalues depend on
\tau
, while for H-type groups, we always have
\mu_{j}(\tau)\equiv 1
. We have to construct eigenfunctions by using Laguerre calculus, which is more complicated in the general step two case. The difficulty comes from the fact that the Laguerre functions
\mathscr{L}_{\mathbf{k}}^{(\mathbf{0})}(\mathbf{y},\tau)
’s are only distrubutions and their definition depend on
\tau
-coordinates, i.e. depend on directions in
\mathbb{R}^{r}
. Their behavior is so bad that it is difficult to construct the kernel
P_{m}
of the spectral projection operator
\mathbb{P}_{m}
and prove it to be Lipschitzian on
\mathcal{N}\backslash\{\mathbf{0}\}
. But for
\mathbf{y}\neq 0
, we still have a simple integral representation formula for
P_{m}
, and have to use analytic continuation for
\mathbf{y}=0
. For general nondegenerate nilpotent Lie groups of step two, the substantial work to do is to construct the spectral projection operators and their kernels.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2.1
, we recall the Laguerre calculus on non-degenerate nilpotent Lie group of step two. In Section
2.2
, we give the joint spectrum of
(\Delta_{b},i\mathbf{T})
and eigenfunctions
\Psi_{\mathbf{k},\tau}^{(\lambda)}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t})
corresponding to
(\lambda,-\tau)\in\sigma(\Delta_{b},i\mathbf{T})
and the relation between function
\Psi_{\mathbf{k},\tau}^{(\lambda)}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t})
and
P_{m}
. In Section
3
, we give the integral representation of
P_{m}
for
\mathbf{y}\neq\mathbf{0}
and further continuate it to a Lipschitzian function on
\mathcal{N}\backslash\{\mathbf{0}\}
by analytic continuation. In Section
4
, we prove that the distribution
\mathscr{P}_{m}
is homogeneous of degree
-Q
and is a homogeneous extension of
P_{m}
, and
\mathbb{P}_{m}
is a spectral projection operator and a Calderón-Zygmund operator. So it can be extended to a bounded operator from the
L^{p}(\mathcal{N})
to itself. In Section
5
, another kind of principal value
\widehat{p.v.}P_{m}
is introduced in order to estimating the boundedness of the operator associated with the Abel sum
\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}R^{m}\mathbb{P}_{m}
. We give the explicit bound of the operator norm of
\widehat{p.v.}P_{m}
on
L^{p}(\mathcal{N})
and
\widehat{p.v.}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}R^{m}P_{m}
on
L^{p}
respectively. Then we give the proof of Theorem
1.3
. For
\phi\in L^{2}(\mathcal{N})
, we prove the decomposition
\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}_{m}\phi=\phi
without Abel sum.

2.
preliminary

2.1.
Laguerre distribution on non-degenerate nilpotent Lie group of step two

Laguerre calculus is the symbolic tensor calculus originally induced in terms of the Laguerre functions on the Heisenberg group
\mathcal{H}_{n}
. It was first introduced on
\mathcal{H}_{1}
by Greiner
[
15
]
and later extended to

\mathcal{H}_{n}
and
\mathcal{H}_{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}
by Beals, Gaveau, Greiner and Vauthier
[
1
]
[
2
]
.
The Laguerre functions have been used in the study of the twisted convolution, or equivalently, the Heisenberg convolution for several decades.
For example, Geller
[
12
]
found a formula that expressed the group Fourier transform of radial functions on the isotropic Heisenberg group in terms of Laguerre transform, while Peetre
[
21
]
derived the relation between the Weyl transform and Laguerre calculus.
See Chang-Chang-Tie
[
4
]
, Chang-Greiner-Tie
[
5
]
, Tie
[
27
]
for the application to find the inversion of differential operators, and Chang-Tie
[
8
]
for the study of the associated spectral projection operators. The Laguerre calculus was developed for non-degenerate nilpotent Lie groups of step two in Chang-Markina-Wang
[
7
]
. It was used in
[
6
]
to find the explicit formulas for the heat kernel of sub-Laplacian operator and the fundamental solution of power of sub-Laplacian operator on this kind of groups.

The skew-symmetric mapping
B:\mathbb{R}^{2n}\times\mathbb{R}^{2n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{r}
in (
1.1
) can be written as

B(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}):=\left(B^{1}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}),\ldots,B^{r}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\right),\qquad B^{\beta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}):=\sum_{k,l=1}^{2n}B^{\beta}_{kl}x_{k}y_{l},

for
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{2n}),\mathbf{y}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{2n})\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}
.
For any
\tau=(\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{r})\in\mathbb{R}^{r}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}
, denote a skew-symmetric
2n\times 2n
matrix

B^{\tau}:=\sum_{\beta=1}^{r}\tau_{\beta}B^{\beta}.

For simplicity, we assume that
B^{\tau}
is non-degenerate for all
\tau\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
in this paper and the corresponding nilpotent Lie group of step two is called
non-degenerate
. Vector fields

(2.1)

\mathbf{Y}_{k}:=\partial_{y_{k}}+2\sum_{\beta=1}^{r}\sum_{l=1}^{2n}B^{\beta}_{lk}y_{l}\partial_{t_{\beta}},\qquad k=1,\ldots,2n,

are left invariant vector fields on
\mathcal{N}
.

Chang-Markina-Wang
[
7
]
showed that for
\tau\in S^{r-1}\setminus E
, where
E\subset S^{r-1}
is of Hausdorff dimension at most
r-2
, there exists an orthonormal basis locally normalizing
B^{\tau}
.

Proposition 2.1
.

[
7
, Proposition 2.3]

There exists a subset
E\subseteq S^{r-1}
of Hausdorff dimension at most
r-2
, such that for any
\tau_{0}\in S^{r-1}\setminus E
, we can find a neighborhood
U
of
\tau_{0}\in S^{r-1}
and an orthonormal basis

\{v^{\tau}_{1},

\ldots,v^{\tau}_{{2n}}\}
of
\mathbb{R}^{{2n}}
, smoothly depending on
\tau\in U
, such that the matrix

O(\tau)=(v^{\tau}_{1},\ldots,v^{\tau}_{{2n}})
normalizes
B^{\tau}
, i.e.

(2.2)

O(\tau)^{t}B^{\tau}O(\tau)=J(\tau):=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccccc}0&{-\mu_{1}(\tau)}&0&0&\cdots\\
\mu_{1}(\tau)&0&0&0&\cdots\\
0&0&0&-\mu_{2}(\tau)&\cdots\\
0&0&\mu_{2}(\tau)&0&\cdots\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots\end{array}\right),

where
\mu_{1}(\tau)\geq\mu_{2}(\tau)\geq\cdots\geq\mu_{n}(\tau)\geq 0
also smoothly depend on
\tau
in this neighborhood, and
i\mu_{1}(\tau),-i\mu_{1}(\tau),\ldots,i\mu_{n}(\tau),-i\mu_{n}(\tau)
represent repeated pure imaginary eigenvalues of
B^{\tau}
.

We can write a point
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}
in terms of the basis
\{v^{\tau}_{k}\}
as

(2.3)

\mathbf{y}=\sum_{k=1}^{2n}y_{k}^{\tau}v^{\tau}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}

for some
y_{1}^{\tau},\ldots,y_{2n}^{\tau}\in\mathbb{R}
. We call
(y_{1}^{\tau},\ldots,y_{2n}^{\tau})
the
\tau
-coordinates
of the point
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}
.

Chang-Markina-Wang
[
7
]
defined Laguerre distributions by using
\tau
-coordinates.
Let
L_{k}^{(p)}
be the generalized Laguerre polynomials.
It is well known
[
3
]
that

l_{k}^{(p)}(\sigma):=\left[\frac{\Gamma(k+1)}{\Gamma(k+p+1)}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}L_{k}^{(p)}(\sigma)\sigma^{\frac{p}{2}}e^{-\frac{\sigma}{2}},\quad

where
\sigma\in[0,\infty),k,p\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, constitute an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}([0,\infty),d\sigma)
for fixed
p
.
Let
\mathscr{L}_{k}^{(p)}
on
\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{r}
be the distribution defined via their partial Fourier transformations

(2.4)

\widetilde{\mathscr{L}}_{k}^{(p)}(y,\tau):=\frac{2|\tau|}{\pi}({\rm sgn}\,p)^{p}l_{k}^{(|p|)}(2|\tau||y|^{2})e^{ip\theta},

which is smooth on
(\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{r})\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}
, where
p\in\mathbb{Z}
,
y=(y_{1},y_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2},y=y_{1}+iy_{2}=|y|e^{i\theta},\tau\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
. Here the
partial Fourier transformation
of a function
\phi
is defined as

\widetilde{\phi}(\mathbf{y},\tau):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{r}}e^{-i\tau\cdot\mathbf{t}}\phi(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t})d\mathbf{t},\qquad{\rm for}\quad\tau\in\mathbb{R}^{r}.

The
partial Fourier transformation of a distribution

\mathscr{K}
is defined as

\langle\widetilde{\mathscr{K}},\phi\rangle:=\langle\mathscr{K},\widetilde{\phi}\rangle,

for
\phi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{N})
.
Define the
exponential Laguerre distributions

{\mathscr{L}}_{\mathbf{k}}^{(\mathbf{p})}(y,s)
on
\mathbb{R}^{2n+r}
via their partial Fourier transformations

(2.5)

\widetilde{\mathscr{L}}_{\mathbf{k}}^{(\mathbf{p})}(\mathbf{y},\tau):=\prod_{j=1}^{n}\mu_{j}(\dot{\tau})\widetilde{\mathscr{L}}_{k_{j}}^{(p_{j})}\left(\sqrt{\mu_{j}(\dot{\tau})}{\mathbf{y}^{\tau}_{j}},\tau\right),

where
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n},\tau\in\mathbb{R}^{r},\mathbf{p}=(p_{1},\ldots p_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}^{n},

\mathbf{k}=(k_{1},\ldots k_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}_{\geq 0}
, and

\dot{\tau}=\frac{\tau}{|\tau|}\in S^{r-1},\qquad\mu_{j}(\tau)=|\tau|\mu_{j}(\dot{\tau}),

{\mathbf{y}^{\tau}_{j}}=\left(y_{2j-1}^{\tau},y_{2j}^{\tau}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{2},\qquad{j}=1,\ldots,n.

The definition of
\widetilde{\mathscr{L}}_{\mathbf{k}}^{(\mathbf{p})}(\mathbf{y},\tau)
above depends on the choice of local orthonormal basis normalizing
B^{\tau}
, and in that local neighborhood, it smoothly depends on
\mathbf{y}
and
\tau
. Then
\widetilde{\mathscr{L}}_{\mathbf{k}}^{(\mathbf{p})}(\mathbf{y},\tau)

\mathscr{P}_{m}

4.1.
The distribution
\mathscr{P}_{m}

Recall that a distribution
\mathscr{K}
on
\mathcal{N}
is called
homogeneous of degree
a
if

(4.1)

\displaystyle\left\langle\mathscr{K},\phi_{\lambda}\right\rangle=

\displaystyle\lambda^{a}\left\langle\mathscr{K},\phi\right\rangle,\,\,\,\,\,\,\text{for any}\,\,\phi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{N}),

where
\phi_{\lambda}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t})=\lambda^{-Q}\phi\left(\lambda^{-1}\mathbf{y},\lambda^{-2}\mathbf{t}\right)
. Let
\delta_{\lambda}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t})=(\lambda\mathbf{y},\lambda^{2}\mathbf{t})
. Then for
a homogeneous distribution
\mathscr{K}
of degree
-Q
, we have

(4.2)

\left\langle\mathscr{K},\phi\circ\delta_{\lambda}\right\rangle=\left\langle\mathscr{K},\phi\right\rangle.

Proposition 4.1
.

\mathscr{P}_{m}
defined by (
1.7
) is a distribution. Moreover, it is homogeneous of degree
-Q
.

Proof.

Since
\|\widetilde{\phi}\|_{(N,0)}\leq C_{N}\|\phi\|_{(N,2N)}
, for some constant
C_{N}
depending on
N
, by definition

\|\phi\|_{(k,l)}:=\sup_{(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t})\in\mathbb{R}^{2n+r},|\mathbf{I}|=l}\left(1+|\mathbf{y}|^{4}+|\mathbf{t}|^{2}\right)^{k}|\partial^{\mathbf{I}}_{(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t})}\phi(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t})|,

where
\partial^{\mathbf{I}}_{(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t})}=\partial^{I_{1}}_{y_{1}}\cdots\partial^{I_{2n}}_{y_{2n}}\partial^{I_{2n+1}}_{t_{1}}\cdots\partial^{I_{2n+r}}_{y_{r}}
and
|\mathbf{I}|=I_{1}+\cdots+I_{2n+r}
, it follows from the definition (
1.7
) of
\mathscr{P}_{m}
and the estimate (
3.3
) that

\displaystyle\left|\left\langle\mathscr{P}_{m},\phi\right\rangle\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{r}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n+r}}|Q_{m}(\mathbf{y},\tau)||\widetilde{\phi}(\mathbf{y},\tau)|d\mathbf{y}d\tau

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{r}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n+r}}|Q_{m}(\mathbf{y},\tau)|\frac{\|\widetilde{\phi}\|_{(N,0)}}{(1+|\mathbf{y}|^{4}+|\tau|^{2})^{N}}d\mathbf{y}d\tau

\displaystyle\leq C_{m}\|\widetilde{\phi}\|_{(N,0)}

\displaystyle\leq C_{m}C_{N}\|\phi\|_{(N,2N)},

for
\phi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{N})
and sufficiently large
N
, where
C_{m}
is a constant depending on
m
. Hence,

\mathscr{P}_{m}
is a distribution.

Since
\phi_{\lambda}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t})=\lambda^{-Q}\phi\left(\lambda^{-1}\mathbf{y},\lambda^{-2}\mathbf{t}\right)
, then

\displaystyle\widetilde{\phi}_{\lambda}(\mathbf{y},-\tau)=\lambda^{-Q}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{r}}\phi\left(\lambda^{-1}\mathbf{y},\lambda^{-2}\mathbf{t}\right)e^{i\mathbf{t}\cdot\tau}d\mathbf{t}=\lambda^{-2n}\widetilde{\phi}\left(\lambda^{-1}\mathbf{y},-\lambda^{2}\tau\right).

Hence,

\displaystyle\left\langle\mathscr{P}_{m},\phi_{\lambda}\right\rangle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{r}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n+r}}Q_{m}(\mathbf{y},\tau)\widetilde{\phi}_{\lambda}(\mathbf{y},-\tau)d\mathbf{y}d\tau

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{r}}\cdot\frac{2^{n}}{\pi^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n+r}}(\det\mathcal{B}^{\tau})^{\frac{1}{2}}e^{-\langle\mathcal{B}^{\tau}\mathbf{y},\mathbf{y}\rangle}L_{m}^{(n-1)}(2\langle\mathcal{B}^{\tau}\mathbf{y},\mathbf{y}\rangle)\lambda^{-2n}\widetilde{\phi}\left(\lambda^{-1}\mathbf{y},-\lambda^{2}\tau\right)d\mathbf{y}d\tau

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda^{-Q}}{(2\pi)^{r}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n+r}}Q_{m}(\mathbf{y}^{\prime},\tau^{\prime})\widetilde{\phi}(\mathbf{y}^{\prime},-\tau^{\prime})d\mathbf{y}^{\prime}d\tau^{\prime}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda^{-Q}\left\langle\mathscr{P}_{m},\phi\right\rangle,

by taking the coordinates transformation
\mathbf{y}^{\prime}=\lambda^{-1}\mathbf{y},\tau^{\prime}=\lambda^{2}\tau
in the third identity. Here
\langle\mathcal{B}^{\tau}\mathbf{y},\mathbf{y}\rangle
is invariant under this transformation and
(\det\mathcal{B}^{\tau})^{\frac{1}{2}}=\lambda^{-2n}(\det\mathcal{B}^{\tau^{\prime}})^{\frac{1}{2}}
.
So
\mathscr{P}_{m}
is homogeneous of degree
-Q
by (
4.1
).
∎

Proposition 4.2
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{N}
is a non-degenerate nilpotent Lie group of step two. Then the distribution
\mathscr{P}_{m}
defined by (
1.7
) coincides with the Lipschitzian function
P_{m}
on
\mathcal{N}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}
.

Proof.

For
\phi\in S(\mathcal{N}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\})
, there exists
\varepsilon_{0}>0
such that
supp\phi\subseteq\{(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t}):|(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t})|>\epsilon_{0}\}
.
Since
\phi
is a Schwartz function on variable
(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t})
,
\widetilde{\phi}
also decays fast.
L^{(n-1)}_{m}
is a polynomial of degree
m
, then it follows from the expression (
3.2
) and Lemma
3.1
that for any given
N\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists a constant
C_{N}>0
, such that

(4.3)

\displaystyle|Q_{m}(\mathbf{y},\tau)\widetilde{\phi}(\mathbf{y},-\tau)|\leq\frac{C_{N}|\tau|^{n}(|\mathbf{\tau}||\mathbf{y}|^{2}+1)^{m}}{1+(|\mathbf{y}|^{4}+|\mathbf{\tau}|^{2})^{N}},

almost everywhere.

If
|\mathbf{y}|\neq 0
, since
B^{\tau}
is non degenerate, it follows from Lemma
3.1
that there exists a constant
C>0
, such that

\displaystyle\left|Q_{m}(\mathbf{y},\tau)\phi(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t})e^{i\mathbf{t}\cdot\tau}\right|\lesssim e^{-C^{-1}|\tau||\mathbf{y}|^{2}}\frac{|\tau|^{n}(|\mathbf{\tau}||\mathbf{y}|^{2}+1)^{m}}{1+(|\mathbf{y}|^{4}+|\mathbf{t}|^{2})^{N}},

for sufficient large

almost everywhere on

. By using Fubini’s theorem, we get

\displaystyle\int_{B_{\varepsilon}^{c}\times\mathbb{R}^{r}}\phi(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t})P_{m}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t})d\mathbf{y}d\mathbf{t}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{r}}\int_{B_{\varepsilon}^{c}\times\mathbb{R}^{r}}\phi(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t})d\mathbf{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{r}}Q_{m}(\mathbf{y},\tau)e^{i\mathbf{t}\cdot\tau}d\mathbf{y}d\tau

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{r}}\int_{B_{\varepsilon}^{c}\times\mathbb{R}^{r}}Q_{m}(\mathbf{y},\tau)d\tau\int_{\mathbb{R}^{r}}\phi(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t})e^{i\mathbf{t}\cdot\tau}d\mathbf{y}d\mathbf{t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{r}}\int_{B_{\varepsilon}^{c}\times\mathbb{R}^{r}}Q_{m}(\mathbf{y},\tau)\widetilde{\phi}(\mathbf{y},-\tau)d\mathbf{y}d\tau,

by using (

is Lipschitzian on

by its expression (
3.6
)-(
3.7
) in Theorem

, we see that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n+r}}\phi(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t})P_{m}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t})d\mathbf{y}d\mathbf{t}=

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\int_{B_{\varepsilon}^{c}\times\mathbb{R}^{r}}\phi(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t})P_{m}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t})d\mathbf{y}d\mathbf{t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{r}}\int_{B_{\varepsilon}^{c}\times\mathbb{R}^{r}}Q_{m}(\mathbf{y},\tau)\widetilde{\phi}(\mathbf{y},-\tau)d\mathbf{y}d\tau

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{r}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n+r}}Q_{m}(\mathbf{y},\tau)\widetilde{\phi}(\mathbf{y},-\tau)d\mathbf{y}d\tau

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\langle\mathscr{P}_{m},\phi\right\rangle.

The third identity follows from

) and

4.2.
P_{m}
is a Calderón-Zygmund kernel

For a fixed point
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{t})\in\mathcal{N}
, the left multiplication by
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{t})
is an affine transformation of
\mathbb{R}^{2n+r}
:

\mathbf{y}\mapsto\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{x},\quad\mathbf{s}\mapsto\mathbf{s}+\mathbf{t}+2B(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}),

which preserves the Lebegues measure
d\mathbf{y}d\mathbf{s}
of
\mathbb{R}^{2n+r}
. The measure
d\mathbf{y}d\mathbf{s}
is also right invariant, and so it is a Haar measure on the nilpotent Lie group
\mathcal{N}
of step two. The convolution on
\mathcal{N}
is defined as

\varphi*\psi(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{s}):=\int_{\mathcal{N}}\varphi(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{t})\psi\left((\mathbf{x},\mathbf{t})^{-1}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{s})\right)d\mathbf{x}d\mathbf{t}=\int_{\mathcal{N}}\varphi\left((\mathbf{y},\mathbf{s})(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{t})^{-1}\right)\psi(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{t})d\mathbf{x}d\mathbf{t},

for
\phi,\psi\in L^{1}(\mathcal{N})
.
For
\phi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{N})
, the convolution of
\phi
and the distribution
\mathscr{P}_{m}
is defined as

\phi\ast\mathscr{P}_{m}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t}):=\left\langle\mathscr{P}_{m},\phi_{(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t})}\right\rangle,

where
\phi_{(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t})}(\mathbf{y^{\prime}},\mathbf{t^{\prime}})=\phi((\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t})(\mathbf{y^{\prime}},\mathbf{t^{\prime}})^{-1})
.
Let
\phi_{1}(\mathbf{y^{\prime}},\mathbf{t^{\prime}})=\phi_{(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t})}(\mathbf{y^{\prime}},-\mathbf{t^{\prime}})
. By the definition of the distribution
\mathscr{P}_{m}
, we have

\displaystyle\phi\ast\mathscr{P}_{m}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t})=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{r}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n+r}}Q_{m}(\mathbf{y^{\prime}},\tau)\widetilde{\phi}_{(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t})}(\mathbf{y^{\prime}},-\tau)d\mathbf{y^{\prime}}d\tau

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{r}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n+r}}Q_{m}(\mathbf{y^{\prime}},\tau)\widetilde{\phi}_{1}(\mathbf{y^{\prime}},\tau)d\mathbf{y^{\prime}}d\tau,

since

\phi_{1}(\mathbf{y^{\prime}},\mathbf{t^{\prime}})=\phi((\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t})(\mathbf{y^{\prime}},-\mathbf{t^{\prime}})^{-1})=\phi((\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t})(-\mathbf{y^{\prime}},\mathbf{t^{\prime}})),

then

\displaystyle\widetilde{\phi}_{1}(\mathbf{y^{\prime}},\tau)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{r}}\phi(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{y^{\prime}},\mathbf{t}+\mathbf{t}^{\prime}-2B(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{y^{\prime}}))e^{-i\mathbf{t}^{\prime}\cdot\tau}d\mathbf{t}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{r}}\phi(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{y^{\prime}},\mathbf{u})e^{-i(\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{t}+2B(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{y^{\prime}}))\cdot\tau}d\mathbf{u}

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\phi}(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{y^{\prime}},\tau)e^{i\mathbf{t}\cdot\tau-2iB(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{y^{\prime}})\cdot\tau},

by taking transformation
\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{t}+\mathbf{t}^{\prime}-2B(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{y^{\prime}})
in the second identity and

(4.5)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\phi}_{(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t})}(\mathbf{y^{\prime}},-\tau)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{r}}\phi_{(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t})}(\mathbf{y^{\prime}},\mathbf{t^{\prime}})e^{i\mathbf{t}^{\prime}\cdot\tau}d\mathbf{t}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{r}}\phi(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{y^{\prime}},\mathbf{t}-\mathbf{t}^{\prime}-2B(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{y^{\prime}}))e^{i\mathbf{t}^{\prime}\cdot\tau}d\mathbf{t}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{r}}\phi(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{y^{\prime}},\mathbf{u})e^{i(\mathbf{t}-\mathbf{u}-2B(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{y^{\prime}}))\cdot\tau}d\mathbf{u}

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\phi}(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{y^{\prime}},\tau)e^{i\mathbf{t}\cdot\tau-2iB(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{y^{\prime}})\cdot\tau}=\widetilde{\phi}_{1}(\mathbf{y^{\prime}},\tau),

where we take transformation
\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{t}-\mathbf{t}^{\prime}-2B(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{y^{\prime}})
in the third identity.

Recall that an absolutely continuous curve
\gamma:[0,1]\rightarrow\mathcal{N}
is horizontal if its tangent vectors
\dot{\gamma}(s),s\in[0,1]
, lie
in the horizontal tangent space
H_{\gamma(s)}
. Any given two points
\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q}\in\mathcal{N}
can be connected by a horizontal curve.
The Carnot-Carathéodory metric on
\mathcal{N}
is defined as follows:

(4.6)

d_{cc}(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h}):=\inf_{\gamma}\int_{0}^{1}\langle\dot{\gamma}(s),\dot{\gamma}(s)\rangle_{H}^{1/2}\mathrm{~d}s\quad\text{ for }\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h}\in\mathcal{N},

where
\gamma:[0,1]\rightarrow\mathcal{N}
is a horizontal Lipschitzian curve with
\gamma(0)=\mathbf{g},\gamma(1)=\mathbf{h}
and
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{H}
is the standard Hermitian inner product on
\mathbb{C}^{2n}
.
In order to proving Theorem
1.2
, we need a mean value theorem on stratified
groups. Note that there is a version
[
11
, (1.41)]
for
C^{1}
functions, but the kernel
P_{m}
of the spectrum projection operator
\mathbb{P}_{m}
is only Lipschitzian and it has singularity at the origin.
We now need the following mean value theorem on
\mathcal{N}
adapted to our kernel
P_{m}
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
K
be a Lipschitzian function on
\mathcal{N}\backslash\{\mathbf{0}\}
and
Y_{j}
’s are left invariant vector fields on
\mathcal{N}
defined by (
2.1
). There are
C>0
and
c_{0}\in(0,1)
such that for
\mathbf{g},\mathbf{g}_{0}\in\mathcal{N}\backslash\{\mathbf{0}\}
with
d_{cc}\left(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{g}_{0}\right)<c_{0}d_{cc}\left(\mathbf{g}_{0},\mathbf{0}\right)
, we have

\left|K(\mathbf{g})-K\left(\mathbf{g}_{0}\right)\right|\leq Cd_{cc}\left(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{g}_{0}\right)\times\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq j\leq 2n\\
\mathbf{u}:\,d_{cc}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{0})\leq d_{cc}\left(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{g}_{0}\right)\end{subarray}}\left|Y_{j}K\left(\mathbf{g}_{0}\cdot\mathbf{u}\right)\right|.

For any
\mathbf{g}=(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t})\in\mathcal{N}
, the homogeneous norm of
\mathbf{g}
is defined by

\|\mathbf{g}\|=\left(|\mathbf{y}|^{4}+|\mathbf{t}|^{2}\right)^{1/4}.

Obviously,
\left\|\mathbf{g}^{-1}\right\|=\|\mathbf{g}\|
and
\left\|\delta_{\lambda}(\mathbf{g})\right\|=\lambda\|\mathbf{g}\|
, where
\delta_{\lambda},\lambda>0
, is the dilation on
\mathcal{N}
, which is defined as
\delta_{\lambda}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t})=\left(\lambda\mathbf{y},\lambda^{2}\mathbf{t}\right)
.
On
\mathcal{N}
, we define the quasi-distance

\rho(\mathbf{h},\mathbf{g})=\left\|\mathbf{g}^{-1}\cdot\mathbf{h}\right\|.

It is clear that
\rho
is symmetric and satisfies the generalized triangle inequality

(4.7)

\rho(\mathbf{h},\mathbf{g})\leq C_{\rho}(\rho(\mathbf{h},\mathbf{w})+\rho(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{g})),

for any
\mathbf{h},\mathbf{g},\mathbf{w}\in\mathcal{N}
and some
C_{\rho}>0
.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.
Recall that the kernel
P_{m}
is Lipschitzian on
\mathcal{N}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}
, and is homogeneous of degree
-Q
by Theorem
3.1
.
In order to proving
P_{m}
is a Calderón-Zygmund kernel, we need to prove kernel
P_{m}(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h})
on
\mathcal{N}
for
\mathbf{g}\neq\mathbf{h},\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h}\in\mathcal{N}
satisfies the following conditions:

(i)
\quad|P_{m}(\mathbf{h}^{-1}\cdot\mathbf{g})|\lesssim\frac{1}{\rho(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h})^{Q}}
;

(ii)
\quad\left|P_{m}(\mathbf{h}^{-1}\cdot\mathbf{g})-P_{m}\left(\mathbf{h}^{-1}\cdot\mathbf{g}_{0}\right)\right|\lesssim\frac{\rho\left(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{g}_{0}\right)}{\rho\left(\mathbf{g}_{0},\mathbf{h}\right)^{Q+1}},\quad
if
\rho\left(\mathbf{g}_{0},\mathbf{h}\right)\geq c\rho\left(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{g}_{0}\right)
;

(iii)
\quad\left|P_{m}(\mathbf{h}^{-1}\cdot\mathbf{g})-P_{m}\left(\mathbf{h}^{-1}_{0}\cdot\mathbf{g}\right)\right|\lesssim\frac{\rho\left(\mathbf{h},\mathbf{h}_{0}\right)}{\rho\left(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h}_{0}\right)^{Q+1}},\quad
if
\rho\left(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h}_{0}\right)\geq c\rho\left(\mathbf{h},\mathbf{h}_{0}\right)

for some constant
c>0
, where
Q=2n+2r
is the homogeneous dimension of
\mathcal{N}
.

Note that

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{m,j}(-\mathbf{y},-\mathbf{t})=

\displaystyle\int_{S^{r-1}}\frac{(\det\mathcal{B}^{\tau})^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\langle\mathcal{B}^{\tau}\mathbf{y},\mathbf{y}\rangle+i(-\mathbf{t})\cdot\tau\right)^{m-j}}{\left(\langle\mathcal{B}^{\tau}\mathbf{y},\mathbf{y}\rangle-i(-\mathbf{t})\cdot\tau\right)^{m+n+r-j}}d\tau=\overline{\mathcal{I}_{m,j}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t})}.

It follows from the integral representation formula (
1.8
)-(
1.9
) that

(4.8)

P_{m}(-\mathbf{y},-\mathbf{t})=\overline{P_{m}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t})}.

So we only need to prove (i) and (ii). Denote

\mathscr{B}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t},\tau)=\langle\mathcal{B}^{\tau}\mathbf{y},\mathbf{y}\rangle-i\mathbf{t}\cdot\tau.

We begin with proving the size estimate (i). For
|\mathbf{y}|^{2}\geq\varepsilon^{2}|\mathbf{t}|
with
\varepsilon
as in the proof of Theorem
3.1
, there exists a constant
C>1
such that

\left|\overline{\mathscr{B}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t},\tau)}\right|^{2}\leq C|\mathbf{y}|^{4}+|\mathbf{t}|^{2}\leq C|\mathbf{y}|^{4}+C|\mathbf{t}|^{2}=C\|\mathbf{g}\|^{4},

by Lemma
3.1
and

\left|\mathscr{B}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t},\tau)\right|^{2}\geq C^{-1}|\mathbf{y}|^{4}\gtrsim|\mathbf{y}|^{4}+|\mathbf{t}|^{2}=\|\mathbf{g}\|^{4},\qquad\text{for}\,\,\tau\in S^{r-1}.

Therefore

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\overline{\mathscr{B}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t},\tau)}^{a}}{\mathscr{B}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t},\tau)^{b}}\right|\leq\frac{C}{\|\mathbf{g}\|^{2b-2a}},

for
a,b\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Similarly, for
|\mathbf{y}|^{2}<\varepsilon^{2}|\mathbf{t}|
,

\left|\langle\mathcal{B}^{O_{\mathbf{t}}\chi_{\omega^{\prime}}(z)}\mathbf{y},\mathbf{y}\rangle+i|\mathbf{t}|z\right|^{2}\leq C|\mathbf{y}|^{4}+|\mathbf{t}|^{2}\leq C|\mathbf{y}|^{4}+C|\mathbf{t}|^{2}=C\|\mathbf{g}\|^{4},

and

\left|\langle\mathcal{B}^{O_{\mathbf{t}}\chi_{\omega^{\prime}}(z)}\mathbf{y},\mathbf{y}\rangle-i|\mathbf{t}|z\right|\geq\varepsilon|\mathbf{t}|-C\varepsilon^{2}|\mathbf{t}|\gtrsim\|\mathbf{g}\|^{2},

since
\varepsilon\leq|z|\leq 1
for
z\in L_{\varepsilon}
. Here we take sufficiently small
\varepsilon
such that
\frac{1}{\varepsilon}>C
.
Therefore

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\left(\langle\mathcal{B}^{O_{\mathbf{t}}\chi_{\omega^{\prime}}(z)}\mathbf{y},\mathbf{y}\rangle+i|\mathbf{t}|z\right)^{a}}{\left(\left|\langle\mathcal{B}^{O_{\mathbf{t}}\chi_{\omega^{\prime}}(z)}\mathbf{y},\mathbf{y}\rangle-i|\mathbf{t}|z\right|\right)^{b}}\right|\leq\frac{C}{\|\mathbf{g}\|^{2b-2a}},

for
a,b\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.
By Proposition
1.1
and Theorem
3.1
, we have

\displaystyle\left|P_{m}(\mathbf{g})\right|\leq\frac{C}{\|\mathbf{g}\|^{Q}},

where the implicit constant depends on
m,n,r
, which shows that (i) holds.

We now prove the regularity estimate (ii).
For
\mathbf{y}\neq\mathbf{0}
,

\displaystyle Y_{j}P_{m}(\mathbf{g})=\sum_{j=0}^{r}C_{m,j}\int_{S^{r-1}}(\det\mathcal{B}^{\tau})^{\frac{1}{2}}\cdot Y_{j}\frac{\overline{\mathscr{B}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t},\tau)}^{m-j}}{\mathscr{B}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t},\tau)^{m+n+r-j}}d\tau,

by Proposition
1.1
. Note that

(4.9)

\displaystyle Y_{j}\frac{\overline{\mathscr{B}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t},\tau)}^{a}}{\mathscr{B}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t},\tau)^{b}}=

\displaystyle\frac{aM_{j}\overline{\mathscr{B}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t},\tau)}^{a-1}\mathscr{B}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t},\tau)^{b}}{\mathscr{B}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t},\tau)^{2b}}-\frac{b\overline{M}_{j}\overline{\mathscr{B}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t},\tau)}^{a}\mathscr{B}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t},\tau)^{b-1}}{\mathscr{B}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t},\tau)^{2b}},

where
M_{j}:=Y_{j}\overline{\mathscr{B}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t},\tau)}.

Since

\left|\frac{\partial}{\partial y_{j}}\mathscr{B}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t},\tau)\right|\leq C\left(|\mathbf{y}|^{4}+|\mathbf{t}|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}\quad\text{and}\,\,\left|\frac{\partial}{\partial t_{j}}\mathscr{B}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t},\tau)\right|=|\tau_{j}|\leq|\tau|=1,

we have

(4.10)

\left|M_{j}\right|\leq C\left(|\mathbf{y}|^{4}+|\mathbf{t}|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}.

Hence,

\displaystyle\left|Y_{j}\frac{\overline{\mathscr{B}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t},\tau)}^{a}}{\mathscr{B}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t},\tau)^{b}}\right|\leq C\left(|\mathbf{y}|^{4}+|\mathbf{t}|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}\frac{\left|\overline{\mathscr{B}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t},\tau)}\right|^{a}}{\left|\mathscr{B}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t},\tau)\right|^{b+1}}\leq\frac{C}{\|\mathbf{g}\|^{2b-2a+1}},

by (
4.9
)-(
4.10
).
Therefore,

\left|Y_{j}P_{m}(\mathbf{g})\right|\leq\frac{C}{\|\mathbf{g}\|^{Q+1}},\quad\mathbf{g}\in\mathcal{N}\backslash\{\mathbf{0}\}.

Note that
Y_{j}=\frac{\partial}{\partial y_{j}}
for
\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{0}
.
It follows from Theorem
3.1
that

\displaystyle Y_{j}P_{m}(\mathbf{g})=\sum_{j=0}^{r}C_{m,j}\int_{L_{\varepsilon}}\int_{S^{r-2}}(1-z^{2})^{\frac{r-2}{2}}I_{1}d\omega^{\prime}dz,

with

\displaystyle I_{1}

\displaystyle:=\frac{\partial}{\partial y_{j}}\left[\frac{(\det\mathcal{B}^{O_{\mathbf{t}}\chi_{\omega^{\prime}}(z)})^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\langle\mathcal{B}^{O_{\mathbf{t}}\chi_{\omega^{\prime}}(z)}\mathbf{y},\mathbf{y}\rangle+i|\mathbf{t}|z\right)^{m-j}}{\left(\langle\mathcal{B}^{O_{\mathbf{t}}\chi_{\omega^{\prime}}(z)}\mathbf{y},\mathbf{y}\rangle-i|\mathbf{t}|z\right)^{m+n+r-j}}\right].

Note that

\displaystyle I_{1}|_{\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{0}}=0.

Therefore,

(4.11)

\left|Y_{j}P_{m}(\mathbf{g})\right|\leq\frac{C}{\|\mathbf{g}\|^{Q+1}},\quad\mathbf{g}\in\mathcal{N}\backslash\{\mathbf{0}\}.

It is known that the Carnot-Carathéodory metric
d_{cc}
defined by (
4.6
) is left-invariant, and it is equivalent to the homogeneous metric
\rho
in the sense that there exist
C_{d},\widetilde{C}_{d}>0
such that, for any
\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h}\in\mathcal{N}
(see
[
16
, (1.21)]
),

(4.12)

C_{d}\rho(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h})\leq d_{cc}(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h})\leq\widetilde{C}_{d}\rho(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h}).

For any
\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h},\mathbf{g}_{0}
with
\rho\left(\mathbf{g}_{0},\mathbf{h}\right)\geq c\rho\left(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{g}_{0}\right)
, we set
\mathbf{q}=\mathbf{h}^{-1}\cdot\mathbf{g}_{0}
, then by Lemma
4.1
and (
4.12
), we have

(4.13)

\displaystyle\left|P_{m}\left(\mathbf{h}^{-1}\cdot\mathbf{g}\right)-P_{m}\left(\mathbf{h}^{-1}\cdot\mathbf{g}_{0}\right)\right|

\displaystyle=\left|P_{m}\left(\mathbf{q}\cdot\left(\mathbf{g}_{0}^{-1}\cdot\mathbf{g}\right)\right)-P_{m}(\mathbf{q})\right|

\displaystyle\leq Cd_{cc}\left(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{g}_{0}\right)\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq j\leq 2n\\
\mathbf{u}:d_{cc}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{0})\leq d_{cc}\left(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{g}_{0}\right)\end{subarray}}\left|Y_{j}P_{m}(\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{u})\right|

\displaystyle\leq C\widetilde{C}_{d}\rho\left(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{g}_{0}\right)\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq j\leq 2n\\
\mathbf{u}:d_{cc}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{0})\leq d_{cc}\left(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{g}_{0}\right)\end{subarray}}\left|Y_{j}P_{m}(\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{u})\right|.

Since
\rho
is a quasi-distance, by (
4.7
), (
4.12
) and the fact that
\rho\left(\mathbf{g}_{0},\mathbf{h}\right)\geq

c\rho\left(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{g}_{0}\right)
, we have

\displaystyle\rho\left(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{q}^{-1}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{C_{\rho}}\rho\left(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{q}^{-1}\right)-\rho(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{u})\geq\frac{1}{C_{\rho}}\rho\left(\mathbf{g}_{0},\mathbf{h}\right)-\frac{\widetilde{C}_{d}}{C_{d}}\rho\left(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{g}_{0}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{C_{\rho}}\rho\left(\mathbf{g}_{0},\mathbf{h}\right)-\frac{\widetilde{C}_{d}}{cC_{d}}\rho\left(\mathbf{g}_{0},\mathbf{h}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{C_{\rho}}-\frac{\widetilde{C}_{d}}{cC_{d}}\right)\rho\left(\mathbf{g}_{0},\mathbf{h}\right).

Consequently,

\left|Y_{j}P_{m}(\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{u})\right|\leq\frac{C}{\|\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{u}\|^{Q+1}}=\frac{C}{\rho(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{q}^{-1})^{Q+1}}\lesssim\frac{1}{\rho\left(\mathbf{g}_{0},\mathbf{h}\right)^{Q+1}},

by using (
4.11
). Together with (
4.13
), we have

\left|P_{m}(\mathbf{h}^{-1}\cdot\mathbf{g})-P_{m}\left(\mathbf{h}^{-1}\cdot\mathbf{g}_{0}\right)\right|\lesssim\frac{\rho\left(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{g}_{0}\right)}{\rho\left(\mathbf{g}_{0},\mathbf{h}\right)^{Q+1}}.

As a consequence, we see that the spectral operator
\mathbb{P}_{m}
is a standard Calderón-Zygmund operator. Since
\mathbb{P}_{m}1=0
by (
1.12
),
\mathbb{P}_{m}
is
L^{2}
bounded by the well known
T1
Theorem. Therefore,
\mathbb{P}_{m}
is
L^{p}
bounded,
1<p<\infty
.
To prove the last statement, note that

\displaystyle(\mathbb{P}_{m}\phi)(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t})

\displaystyle=\langle\mathscr{P}_{m},\phi_{(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t})}\rangle=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{r}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n+r}}Q_{m}(\mathbf{y^{\prime}},\tau)\widetilde{\phi}(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{y^{\prime}},\tau)e^{i\mathbf{t}\cdot\tau-2iB(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{y^{\prime}})\cdot\tau}d\mathbf{y^{\prime}}d\tau

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{r}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{r}}\bigg[\widetilde{\phi}(\cdot,\tau)\ast_{\tau}Q_{m}(\cdot,\tau)\bigg](\mathbf{y})e^{i\mathbf{t}\cdot\tau}d\tau,

by using Fubini’s Theorem and (
4.5
), where we use (
2.6
) in the last identity. The integral is convergent since
|Q_{m}(\cdot,\cdot)\widetilde{\phi}(\cdot,\cdot)|
is in
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2n+r})
by estimate (
4.3
). Since
\mathbb{P}_{m}
is
L^{2}
bounded,
\mathbb{P}_{m}\phi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2n+r})
. Then for almost all
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}
,
\mathbb{P}_{m}\phi(\mathbf{y},\cdot)\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{r})
. Consequently,

(4.14)

\widetilde{(\mathbb{P}_{m}\phi)}(\mathbf{y},\tau)=\left(\widetilde{\phi}(\cdot,\tau)\ast_{\tau}Q_{m}(\cdot,\tau)\right)(\mathbf{y}),

by the Fourier inverse formula for
L^{2}
functions on
\mathbb{R}^{r}
.

For
\phi\in L^{2}(\mathcal{N})
, there exists a sequence
\phi_{n}\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{N})
such that
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\phi_{n}=\phi
in the
L^{2}
sense. We can define

\mathbb{P}_{m}:L^{2}(\mathcal{N})\longmapsto L^{2}(\mathcal{N})

by letting

\mathbb{P}_{m}\phi:=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{P}_{m}\phi_{n},

in
L^{2}
, which is obviously independent of the choice of the sequence
\phi_{n}
, and consequently,

\widetilde{\mathbb{P}_{m}\phi}=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\widetilde{\mathbb{P}_{m}\phi_{n}},

in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2n+r})
by Plancherel identity. Since

\left\|\bigg(\widetilde{\phi}_{n}(\cdot,\tau)-\widetilde{\phi}(\cdot,\tau)\bigg)\ast_{\tau}Q_{m}(\cdot,\tau)\right\|_{L^{2}}\leq\left\|Q_{m}(\cdot,\tau)\right\|_{L^{1}}\left\|\widetilde{\phi}_{n}(\cdot,\tau)-\widetilde{\phi}(\cdot,\tau)\right\|_{L^{2}},

by Proposition
2.2
, where
\left\|Q_{m}(\cdot,\tau)\right\|_{L^{1}}
is uniformly bounded by Lemma
2.1
, we see that

\widetilde{(\mathbb{P}_{m}\phi_{n})}(\mathbf{y},\tau)=\bigg(\widetilde{\phi}_{n}(\cdot,\tau)\ast_{\tau}Q_{m}(\cdot,\tau)\bigg)(\mathbf{y})\longrightarrow\left(\widetilde{\phi}(\cdot,\tau)\ast_{\tau}Q_{m}(\cdot,\tau)\right)(\mathbf{y}),

in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2n+r})
. Hence, (
4.14
) holds for
\phi\in L^{2}(\mathcal{N}).

Now we can use (
4.14
) repeatedly to get

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathbb{P}_{m}(\mathbb{P}_{m}\phi)}(\mathbf{y},\tau)=

\displaystyle\bigg[\bigg(\widetilde{\phi}(\cdot,\tau)\ast_{\tau}Q_{m}(\cdot,\tau)\bigg)\ast_{\tau}Q_{m}(\cdot,\tau)\bigg](\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\widetilde{\phi}(\cdot,\tau)\ast_{\tau}Q_{m}(\cdot,\tau)\right)(\mathbf{y})=\widetilde{(\mathbb{P}_{m}\phi)}(\mathbf{y},\tau),

since

\displaystyle Q_{m_{1}}\ast_{\tau}Q_{m_{2}}(\mathbf{y},\tau)=

\displaystyle\sum_{|\mathbf{k}|=m_{1}}\sum_{|\mathbf{q}|=m_{2}}\widetilde{\mathscr{L}}_{\mathbf{k}}^{(\mathbf{0})}\ast_{\tau}\widetilde{\mathscr{L}}_{\mathbf{q}}^{(\mathbf{0})}(\mathbf{y},\tau)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{|\mathbf{k}|=m_{1}}\sum_{|\mathbf{q}|=m_{2}}\delta_{\mathbf{k}}^{(\mathbf{q})}\widetilde{\mathscr{L}}_{\mathbf{k}}^{(\mathbf{0})}(\mathbf{y},\tau)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\delta_{m_{1}}^{m_{2}}\sum_{|\mathbf{k}|=m_{1}}\widetilde{\mathscr{L}}_{\mathbf{k}}^{(\mathbf{0})}(\mathbf{y},\tau)=\delta_{m_{1}}^{m_{2}}Q_{m_{1}}(\mathbf{y},\tau),

by Proposition
2.3
. Hence,

\mathbb{P}_{m_{1}}\circ\mathbb{P}_{m_{2}}=\delta_{m_{1}}^{m_{2}}\mathbb{P}_{m_{1}},

and so
\mathbb{P}_{m}
is a projection operator and mutually orthogonal in
L^{2}
. The theorem is proved. ∎

5.
The spectral projection decomposition

Strichartz
[
26
]
investigated the joint spectral
theory of the sub-Laplacian operators
\Delta_{b}
and
iT
on the Heisenberg group
\mathcal{H}_{n}
, where
T=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}
.
The associated spectral projection operators are convolution operators with kernel
K
that can be described explicitly. These kernels are homogeneous functions of degree
-2n-2
on
\mathcal{H}_{n}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}
and are Calderón-Zygmund kernels. Strichartz showed that the convolution operator is
L^{p}
bounded by introducing another principal value associated the continuous function
K
on
\mathcal{N}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}
defined by

(5.1)

\langle\widehat{p.v.}K,\phi\rangle:=\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}\int_{|\mathbf{t}|>\varepsilon}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\phi(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t})K(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t})d\mathbf{y}d\mathbf{t}.

In order to estimating the boundedness of the operator associated with the Abel sum
\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}R^{m}\mathbb{P}_{m}
,
we need the explicit bound of each operator
\mathbb{P}_{m}
. Hence, here we need to choose the principal value defined by (
5.1
) which help us to get the explicit bound of each operator
\mathbb{P}_{m}
.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
\mathcal{N}
be a nilpotent Lie group of step two and
K
be a continuous function on
\mathcal{N}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}
which is homogeneous of degree
-Q
and satisfies

(5.2)

\mu_{K}:=\int_{S^{r-1}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}K(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t})d\mathbf{y}d\mathbf{t}=0.

Then the formula (
5.1
)
for any
\phi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{N})
, defines a tempered distribution
\widehat{p.v.}K
which is homogeneous of degree
-Q
. Conversely, suppose
\mathscr{K}
is a tempered distribution which is homogeneous of degree
-Q
and whose restriction on
\mathcal{N}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}
is a continuous function
K
. Then (
5.2
) holds and

\mathscr{K}=\widehat{p.v.}K+C\delta_{\mathbf{0}},

for some constant
C
.

Proof.

Note that

(5.3)

\displaystyle

\displaystyle\int_{|\mathbf{t}|>\varepsilon}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}K(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t})\phi(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t})d\mathbf{y}d\mathbf{t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{U_{1,\varepsilon}}K(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t})\bigg(\phi(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t})-\phi(\mathbf{0})\bigg)d\mathbf{y}d\mathbf{t}+\int_{U_{2,\varepsilon}}K(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t})\bigg(\phi(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t})-\phi(\mathbf{0})\bigg)d\mathbf{y}d\mathbf{t}

\displaystyle+\int_{|\mathbf{t}|\geq 1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}K(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t})\phi(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t})d\mathbf{y}d\mathbf{t}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle I_{1,\varepsilon}+I_{2,\varepsilon}+I_{2},

by (
5.2
), where
U_{1,\varepsilon}:=\{(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t}):|\mathbf{y}|\leq 1,\epsilon<|\mathbf{t}|\leq 1\}
,
U_{2,\varepsilon}:=\{(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t}):|\mathbf{y}|\geq 1,\varepsilon<|\mathbf{t}|\leq 1\}
with
\epsilon>0
. Since
K
is a continuous function on
\mathcal{N}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}
which is homogeneous of degree
-Q
, then there exists a constant
C
such that

|K(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t})|\leq\frac{C}{(|\mathbf{y}|^{4}+|\mathbf{t}|^{2})^{\frac{Q}{4}}}.

Since
\phi
is a Schwartz function on
\mathbb{R}^{2n+r}
, then
I_{2}
is convergent. Similarly,
I_{2,\epsilon}
is convergent. For Schwartz function
\phi
, there exists constants
C
such that

|\phi(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t})-\phi(\mathbf{0})|\leq C(|\mathbf{y}|+|\mathbf{t}|),

then

\left|K(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t})\big(\phi(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t})-\phi(\mathbf{0})\big)\right|\leq\frac{C(|\mathbf{y}|+|\mathbf{t}|)}{(|\mathbf{y}|^{4}+|\mathbf{t}|^{2})^{\frac{Q}{4}}},

which is integrable on
U_{1,\varepsilon}
, i.e.,
I_{1,\epsilon}
is convergent. Take limit on both sides of (
5.3
) to get

(5.4)

\displaystyle\langle\widehat{p.v.}K,\phi\rangle=

\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\int_{|\mathbf{t}|>\varepsilon}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}K(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t})\phi(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t})d\mathbf{y}d\mathbf{t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{|\mathbf{t}|<1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}K(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t})\bigg(\phi(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t})-\phi(\mathbf{0})\bigg)d\mathbf{y}d\mathbf{t}+\int_{|\mathbf{t}|\geq 1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}K(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t})\phi(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t})d\mathbf{y}d\mathbf{t}.

Moreover,

\displaystyle\langle\widehat{p.v.}K,\phi\circ\delta_{\lambda}\rangle=

\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\int_{|\mathbf{t}|\geq\varepsilon}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}K(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t})\phi(\lambda\mathbf{y},\lambda^{2}\mathbf{t})d\mathbf{y}d\mathbf{t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\int_{|\widetilde{\mathbf{t}}|\geq\lambda^{2}\varepsilon}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}K(\widetilde{\mathbf{y}},\widetilde{\mathbf{t}})\phi(\widetilde{\mathbf{y}},\widetilde{\mathbf{t}})d\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}d\widetilde{\mathbf{t}}=\langle\widehat{p.v.}K,\phi\rangle,

for any
\lambda>0
by taking coordinates transformation
\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}=\lambda\mathbf{y},\widetilde{\mathbf{t}}=\lambda^{2}\mathbf{t}
and using the fact that
K
is homogeneous of degree
-Q
. So that
\widehat{p.v.}K
is homogeneous of degree
-Q
as a distribution by (
4.2
).

Conversely, if
\mathscr{K}
is a homogeneous distribution which agrees with
K
away from
\mathbf{0}
, then
\mathscr{K}-\widehat{p.v.}K
is supported at
\mathbf{0}
since
\widehat{p.v.}K
agrees with
K
on
\mathcal{N}\backslash\{\mathbf{0}\}
by definition (
5.1
). Hence it is a linear combination of
\delta_{\mathbf{0}}
and its derivatives. Let
\mathbf{x}=(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t})
. But
\bigg(\frac{\partial}{\partial\mathbf{x}}\bigg)^{\mathbf{I}}\delta_{\mathbf{0}}
is homogeneous of degree
-Q-d(\mathbf{I})
, where

with
\mathbf{I}=(I_{1},\ldots,I_{2n+r})
. So by homogeneity we must have
\mathscr{K}-\widehat{p.v.}K=C\delta_{\mathbf{0}}
.

Note that for
\lambda>0
,

(5.5)

\displaystyle\int_{S^{r-1}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}K(\mathbf{y},\lambda\dot{\mathbf{t}})d\mathbf{y}d\mathbf{\dot{t}}=

\displaystyle\int_{S^{r-1}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}K(\sqrt{\lambda}\frac{\mathbf{y}}{\sqrt{\lambda}},\lambda\dot{\mathbf{t}})d\mathbf{y}d\mathbf{\dot{t}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda^{-n-r}\int_{S^{r-1}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}K(\frac{\mathbf{y}}{\sqrt{\lambda}},\dot{\mathbf{t}})d\mathbf{y}d\mathbf{\dot{t}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda^{-r}\int_{S^{r-1}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}K(\widetilde{\mathbf{y}},\dot{\mathbf{t}})d\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}d\mathbf{\dot{t}},

where the second identity holds since
K
is homogeneous of degree
-Q
and we take a coordinate transformation
\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}=\frac{\mathbf{y}}{\sqrt{\lambda}}
in the third identity.

Consider the distribution
\mathscr{F}
defined by

\displaystyle\langle\mathscr{F},\phi\rangle=

\displaystyle\int_{|\mathbf{t}|<1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}K(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t})[\phi(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t})-\phi(\mathbf{0})]d\mathbf{y}d\mathbf{t}+\int_{|\mathbf{t}|\geq 1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}K(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t})\phi(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t})d\mathbf{y}d\mathbf{t},

is homogeneous of degree
-Q
for any

, then it follows from (

\displaystyle\langle\mathscr{F},\phi\circ\delta_{\lambda}\rangle-\langle\mathscr{F},\phi\rangle=\langle\mathscr{F}-\mathscr{K},\phi\circ\delta_{\lambda}\rangle-\langle\mathscr{F}-\mathscr{K},\phi\rangle=\sum_{\mathbf{I}}a_{\mathbf{I}}(\lambda^{d(\mathbf{I})}-1)\bigg(\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial\mathbf{x}}\bigg)^{\mathbf{I}}(\mathbf{0}),

which is bounded as

\displaystyle\int_{|\mathbf{t}|<\lambda^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}[\phi(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t})-\phi(\mathbf{0})]K(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t})d\mathbf{y}d\mathbf{t}+\int_{|\mathbf{t}|\geq\lambda^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}K(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t})\phi(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t})d\mathbf{y}d\mathbf{t}

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\int_{|\mathbf{t}|<1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}[\phi(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t})-\phi(\mathbf{0})]K(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t})d\mathbf{y}d\mathbf{t}-\int_{|\mathbf{t}|\geq 1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}K(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t})\phi(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t})d\mathbf{y}d\mathbf{t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\phi(\mathbf{0})\int_{\lambda^{2}\leq|\mathbf{t}|<1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}K(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t})d\mathbf{y}d\mathbf{t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\phi(\mathbf{0})\int_{\lambda^{2}}^{1}|\mathbf{t}|^{r-1}d|\mathbf{t}|\int_{S^{r-1}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}K(\mathbf{y},|\mathbf{t}|\dot{\mathbf{t}})d\mathbf{y}d\mathbf{\dot{t}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\phi(\mathbf{0})\mu_{K}\int_{\lambda^{2}}^{1}|\mathbf{t}|^{-1}d|\mathbf{t}|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\phi(\mathbf{0})\mu_{K}\log\lambda^{2}\longrightarrow\infty,\,\,\,\,\,\,\text{as}\,\lambda\rightarrow 0,

where the forth identity holds by using (
5.5
).
If
\mu_{K}\neq 0
, then there is an contraction when
\phi(\mathbf{0})\neq 0
. Hence, (
5.2
) holds. The proposition is proved.

Proposition 5.3
.

On a non-degenerate nilpotent Lie group of step two
\mathcal{N}
, the operator norm of
\widehat{p.v.}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}R^{m}P_{m}
on
L^{p}
is bounded by
\frac{c_{p}}{(R+1)^{n}},1<p<\infty
,
0<R<1
, where
c_{p}
is a constant only depending on
p
.

Proof.

Since
\sum_{m=0}^{+\infty}R^{m}P_{m}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t})
is also homogeneous of degree
-Q
and satisfies (
5.2
) with
K=\sum_{m=0}^{+\infty}R^{m}P_{m}
, then the operator norm of
\widehat{p.v.}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}R^{m}P_{m}
on
L^{p}
is bounded by

c_{p}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\int_{S^{r-1}}\bigg|\sum_{m=0}^{+\infty}R^{m}P_{m}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t})\bigg|d\mathbf{y}d\mathbf{t},

by Lemma
5.2
.
Let
M:=\frac{1-R}{R+1}
. Note that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\int_{S^{r-1}}\bigg|\int_{S^{r-1}}\frac{(\det\mathcal{B}^{\tau})^{\frac{1}{2}}}{\bigg[\langle\mathcal{B}^{\tau}\mathbf{y},\mathbf{y}\rangle-iM\mathbf{t}\cdot\tau\bigg]^{n+r}}d\tau\bigg|d\mathbf{y}d\mathbf{t}\leq\int_{S^{r-1}}\int_{S^{r-1}}(\det\mathcal{B}^{\tau})^{\frac{1}{2}}I_{1}d\tau d\mathbf{t},

where

\displaystyle I_{1}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\frac{1}{\bigg|\langle\mathcal{B}^{\tau}\mathbf{y},\mathbf{y}\rangle-iM\mathbf{t}\cdot\tau\bigg|^{n+r}}d\mathbf{y}.

By Lemma
3.1
, there exists a constant
C>0
such that

\displaystyle I_{1}\lesssim\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\rho^{2n-1}d\rho}{(C\rho^{4}+M^{2})^{\frac{n+r}{2}}}=C^{\prime}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{u^{n-1}du}{(u^{2}+M^{2})^{\frac{n+r}{2}}}=\frac{C^{\prime}}{M^{r}},

where we take the coordinate transform
\sqrt{C}\rho^{2}=u
in the first identity. It follows from Proposition
5.2
that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\int_{S^{r-1}}\bigg|\sum_{m=0}^{+\infty}R^{m}P_{m}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t})\bigg|d\mathbf{y}d\mathbf{t}\leq

\displaystyle\frac{C^{\prime}}{(R+1)^{n}}.

The proposition is proved.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

In
[
7
, Theorem 4.1]
, for
\phi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{N})
, it is known that

\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}R^{m}\mathbb{P}_{m}\phi\rightarrow\phi\qquad{\rm as}\quad R\rightarrow 1^{-}.

By Theorem
1.2
,

\mathbb{P}_{m}\phi\in L^{p}(\mathcal{N})
for any
\phi\in L^{p}(\mathcal{N})
.
On the other hand, the operator norm of convolution operator
\widehat{p.v.}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}R^{m}P_{m}
is uniformly bounded for
0<R<1
by Proposition
5.3
. Therefore,

\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}R^{m}\mathbb{P}_{m}\phi
is also uniformly bounded for
0<R<1
by using identity (
5.6
). Consequently,

\lim_{R\rightarrow 1^{-}}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}R^{m}\mathbb{P}_{m}\phi=\phi

holds for all
\phi\in L^{p}(\mathcal{N})
. ∎

Proof of Corollary
1.1
.

It follows from Theorem
1.1
that
\mathbb{P}_{m}
is self adjoint since

\displaystyle\langle\psi,\,\,\phi\ast p.v.P_{m}\rangle=

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\int_{\|(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t})^{-1}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{s})\|\geq\varepsilon}\psi(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{s})\overline{P_{m}((\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t})^{-1}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{s}))\phi(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t})}d\mathbf{y}d\mathbf{t}d\mathbf{x}d\mathbf{s}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\int_{\|(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t})^{-1}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{s})\|\geq\varepsilon}P_{m}((\mathbf{x},\mathbf{s})^{-1}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t}))\psi(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{s})\overline{\phi(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{t})}d\mathbf{y}d\mathbf{t}d\mathbf{x}d\mathbf{s}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\psi\ast p.v.P_{m},\phi\rangle,

for
\phi,\psi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathcal{N})
, where the second identity holds since the kernel
P_{m}
satisfies (
4.8
), and the constant
C
in (
1.10
) is a real number, which can be calculated as
[
26
, P. 364-365]
in the case of the Heisenberg group.
Therefore,

(5.11)

\langle\mathbb{P}_{m}\phi,\,\,\mathbb{P}_{m^{\prime}}\phi\rangle=\langle\mathbb{P}_{m^{\prime}}\mathbb{P}_{m}\phi,\,\,\phi\rangle=\langle\delta_{m}^{m^{\prime}}\mathbb{P}_{m^{\prime}}\phi,\,\,\phi\rangle=\delta_{m}^{m^{\prime}}\|\mathbb{P}_{m^{\prime}}\phi\|_{L^{2}}^{2},

by Theorem
1.2
. Hence,

\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}R^{2m}\left\|\mathbb{P}_{m}\phi\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=\left\|\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}R^{m}\mathbb{P}_{m}\phi\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\longrightarrow\left\|\phi\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2},

by Theorem
1.3
. Consequently, we get

\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\left\|\mathbb{P}_{m}\phi\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\geq\left\|\phi\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

On the other hand,

\sum_{m=0}^{N}\left\|\mathbb{P}_{m}\phi\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=\left\|\sum_{m=0}^{N}\mathbb{P}_{m}\phi\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq\left\|\phi\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2},

by (
5.11
), since
\sum_{m=0}^{N}\mathbb{P}_{m}
is also a projection operator. Letting
N\rightarrow\infty
, we get

\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\left\|\mathbb{P}_{m}\phi\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq\left\|\phi\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

Therefore we must have

\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\left\|\mathbb{P}_{m}\phi\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=\left\|\phi\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

Hence, (
1.13
) holds in
L^{2}
. The corollary is proved.
∎
