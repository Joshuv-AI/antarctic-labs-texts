Over-the-Air Federated Learning with Retransmissions (Extended Version)
Henrik Hellström, Viktoria Fodor, and Carlo Fischione

Abstract
Motivated by increasing computational capabilities of wireless devices, as well as unprecedented levels of user- and device-generated data, new distributed machine learning (ML) methods have emerged. In the wireless community, Federated Learning (FL) is of particular interest due to its communication efficiency and its ability to deal with the problem of non-IID data. FL training can be accelerated by a wireless communication method called Over-the-Air Computation (AirComp) which harnesses the interference of simultaneous uplink transmissions to efficiently aggregate model updates. However, since AirComp utilizes analog communication, it introduces inevitable estimation errors. In this paper, we study the impact of such estimation errors on the convergence of FL and propose retransmissions as a method to improve FL convergence over resource-constrained wireless networks. First, we derive the optimal AirComp power control scheme with retransmissions over static channels. Then, we investigate the performance of Over-the-Air FL with retransmissions and find two upper bounds on the FL loss function. Finally, we propose a heuristic for selecting the optimal number of retransmissions, which can be calculated before training the ML model. Numerical results demonstrate that the introduction of retransmissions can lead to improved ML performance, without incurring extra costs in terms of communication or computation. Additionally, we provide simulation results on our heuristic which indicate that it can correctly identify the optimal number of retransmissions for different wireless network setups and machine learning problems.

I
Introduction

The data collection rate in wireless devices is growing at an exceptional speed due to the increasing adoption of smartphones, tablets, and
IoT
(
IoT
) devices
[
1
,
2
]
. These devices are expected to provide a broad range of
AI
(
AI
) services in
6G
(
6G
) networks, such as predictive healthcare
[
3
]
, search-and-rescue drones
[
4
]
, and environmental monitoring
[
5
]
. As a consequence, new distributed machine learning methods, such as
FL
(
FL
), have become essential to enable privacy-preserving and communication-efficient model training
[
6
]
. A recent survey on open problems of
FL
, argues that communication is often a primary bottleneck for
FL
because wireless links operate at low rates that can be both expensive and unreliable
[
7
]
. Communication-efficient
FL
is investigated thoroughly in
[
8
]
, where various compression techniques such as quantization, random rotation, and subsampling are evaluated. In
[
9
,
10
,
11
,
12
]
it is established that new wireless methods can greatly improve the communication efficiency of edge
AI
.

A relatively unexplored approach to wireless communication, called
AirComp
(
AirComp
) (also known as computation over multiple-access channels), has recently been adapted to support
ML
(
ML
) services
[
13
,
14
]
.
AirComp
is an analog communication scheme that orders its users to communicate simultaneously over the same frequency band, thereby promoting interference. This interference is leveraged to compute a function of the transmitted messages by utilizing the superposition property of the wireless channel
[
15
]
. By appropriately precoding the transmitted signals, many functions can be calculated over the air, for instance, the arithmetic mean, product, and min functions
[
16
]
. In
FL
, the central server is interested in collecting the arithmetic mean of model updates from the participating devices, and therefore
AirComp
is a suitable communication solution
[
10
]
.

Compared to conventional point-to-point digital communications,
AirComp
is very attractive from a communication-efficiency standpoint, with throughput gains approximately proportional to the number of users
[
13
]
. The reason for this drastic improvement is that the entire wireless spectrum can be utilized concurrently by all devices, rather than dividing it and allocating a smaller resource block to each device. Additionally,
AirComp
obfuscates the participating users since the central server directly receives the arithmetic mean rather than the individual model updates, thereby enhancing privacy
[
17
]
.

Currently,
AirComp
is reliant upon specialized hardware and fine synchronization that might be difficult to achieve in practice
[
18
]
. Additionally,
AirComp
is unable to guarantee perfect reconstruction of the transmitted messages at the receiver. Shannon’s ”fundamental theorem for a discrete channel with noise” establishes that for any degree of noise contamination, it is possible to communicate discrete data with an arbitrarily small frequency of errors
[
19
]
. However, to achieve a non-zero communication rate, redundant information must be transmitted in the form of a code
[
20
]
. Since the information transmitted in
AirComp
is not discrete, existing codes do not appear to be applicable. Instead,
AirComp
settles for estimating the desired function as closely as possible, while retaining some non-zero estimation error
[
21
]
. In
[
22
]
, it is proved that these errors reduce the convergence rate of
FL
, thereby requiring additional communication rounds to reach an optimum.

In the current
AirComp
literature, the main way of reducing the estimation error is to optimize the transmission powers. In
[
21
]
and
[
23
]
, the authors propose a closed-form power control scheme that minimizes the
MSE
(
MSE
) between the received signal and the true sum of the sources’ messages. They consider single-antenna devices with a peak transmission power constraint. A similar problem is solved in
[
24
]
, where a sum-power constraint is used to preserve the long-term energy consumption of the participating devices. For the case of multiple antennas, no closed-form power control scheme has been found, but
[
14
]
develops a strong heuristic by using a difference-of-convex-functions representation of the problem. In
[
25
]
, the multi-antenna problem is coupled with wireless power transfer to improve the battery life of participating
IoT
devices. To further improve the power control,
[
26
]
proposes a gradient-statistics aware scheme that learns statistical properties of the model updates to improve the
AirComp
estimation error. Similarly,
[
27
]
learns the temporal structure of gradient sparsity to develop a Bayesian prior that improves the estimation. Finally, several works have incorporated intelligent reflective surfaces with
AirComp
to reach substantially lower estimation errors
[
28
,
29
,
30
]
.

As a general pattern, none of these works offer avenues to trade-off communication resources for improved estimation. In digital communications, such communication-estimation trade-offs are the main way to reduce errors. For instance, it is standard to adaptively control the modulation order and coding rate to compensate for poor channels
[
31
]
. Unfortunately, none of these approaches are directly compatible with
AirComp
since the communication is analog. In this paper, we take a first step towards enabling this communication-estimation tradeoff for Over-the-Air federated learning with a system we call AirReComp. The contributions of this paper are summarized as follows.

•

This is the first work that allows communication resources to be traded for improved estimation in Over-the-Air
FL
;

•

A power control scheme for AirReComp is proposed. The proposed scheme is proven to be globally optimal in terms of
MSE
between the estimated and desired function.

•

Upper bounds on the
FL
loss function are derived for single-epoch Lipschitz-smooth functions, both for the strongly-convex and convex case.

•

Using the bound for convex functions, we develop a heuristic for selecting the number of retransmissions that minimize the
FL
loss given a limited energy budget;

•

To further support the feasibility of AirReComp under non-convex functions, we provide numerical results with
DNN
. These results suggest that AirReComp can outperform state-of-the-art Over-the-Air
FL
in terms of classification accuracy, without increasing the total cost of training.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section
II
introduces the system model of Federated Learning, Over-the-Air Computation, and the retransmissions. Section
III
presents and solves the power control problem to minimize the
MSE
between the desired and received sum. Section
IV
provides worst-case analysis on the performance of AirReComp in terms of two upper bounds on the
FL
loss function. These bounds are then used in Section
V
to develop a heuristic for selecting the number of retransmissions that minimizes the
FL
loss without exceeding communication and computation cost constraints. In Section
VI
, the performances of AirReComp and the retransmission selection heuristic are numerically evaluated for non-convex loss functions. Finally, section
VII
concludes the work and discusses future work.

Communication round

starts when the
PS
broadcasts the global model
\mathbf{w}_{n}
to all user devices in the downlink
[
12
]
. Upon receiving the model, user device

\mathbf{w}_{n,k}^{*}=\argmin_{\mathbf{w}}F_{k}(\mathbf{w})=\argmin_{\mathbf{w}}\sum_{\mathbf{u}_{i}\in\mathcal{D}_{k}}l(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{u}_{i})

(3)

where
\mathbf{u}_{i}
denotes one training sample and

is the sample-wise loss function. Generally, (
3
) can not be solved exactly. Instead, each device runs

) as follows

\mathbf{w}_{n,k}(i)\leftarrow\mathbf{w}_{n,k}(i-1)-\beta\nabla F_{k}(\mathbf{w}_{n,k}(i-1)),\forall i=1,..,E,

(4)

where the first iteration is based on the global model, i.e.
\mathbf{w}_{n,k}(0)=\mathbf{w}_{n}
, and

is the step size. After executing

\Delta\mathbf{w}_{n,k}=(\mathbf{w}_{n}-\mathbf{w}_{n,k}(E))/\beta
. After all local model updates have been computed, they are transmitted in the uplink to the
PS
.

At the
PS
, the local model updates are aggregated to form a global model update, written as

\Delta\mathbf{w}_{n}=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\Delta\mathbf{w}_{n,k}.

(5)

Finally, the

concludes the communication round by generating the next iteration of the model parameters

\mathbf{w}_{n+1}=\mathbf{w}_{n}-\beta\Delta\mathbf{w}_{n}.

(6)

The algorithm repeats until
\mathbf{w}_{N}
is generated as the final model. After all
N
communication rounds have completed, the
PS
broadcasts
\mathbf{w}_{N}
to the user devices, so that every device has the ability to perform inference with the model.

In the uplink aggregation step of

To start, the devices normalize the model updates according to

\Delta\tilde{\mathbf{w}}_{n,k}=\frac{\Delta\mathbf{w}_{n,k}-\mu_{n,k}}{\sigma_{n,k}},

\mu_{n,k}

\begin{split}\mu_{n,k}=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\Delta w_{n,k}^{(i)}\\
\sigma_{n,k}^{2}=\frac{1}{d-1}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left|\Delta w_{n,k}^{(i)}-\mu_{n,k}\right|^{2},\end{split}

(8)

where
\Delta w_{n,k}^{(i)}
is element

\Delta\mathbf{w}_{n,k}

are transmitted to the
PS
over a separate control channel. Note that the overhead of transmitting 2 scalars per device is negligible for most
FL
problems. After normalizing, the model updates are communicated over the
MAC
defined in (
2
) as

\mathbf{x}_{k}=\Delta\tilde{\mathbf{w}}_{n,k}\frac{\bar{h}_{k}}{|h_{k}|}\sqrt{p_{k}}.

(9)

By combining (
2
) and (
9
) we get the received value at the
PS

\mathbf{y}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}|h_{k}|\sqrt{p_{k}}\Delta\tilde{\mathbf{w}}_{n,k}+\mathbf{z}.

(10)

Ideally, the transmission powers would be chosen as
p_{k}=1/|h_{k}|^{2}
, which would completely compensate for the fading effect. However, with a natural constraint on the maximum transmission power,
p_{k}=1/|h_{k}|^{2}
might be impossible to achieve. Because of this limitation and due to the additive noise, the
PS
can never perfectly reconstruct
\Delta\tilde{\mathbf{w}}_{n}
. Instead, it estimates
\Delta\tilde{\mathbf{w}}_{n}
by dividing the received signal by a post-transmission scalar
\sqrt{\eta}
and the number of devices
K

\overline{\mathbf{y}}=\frac{\mathbf{y}}{\sqrt{\eta}K}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{|h_{k}|\Delta\tilde{\mathbf{w}}_{n,k}\sqrt{p_{k}}}{\sqrt{\eta}K}+\frac{\mathbf{z}}{\sqrt{\eta}K}.

(11)

Coupled with the transmission powers,
\sqrt{\eta}
has an important role. We see that the ideal choice of the transmission powers is now
\sqrt{p_{k}}=\sqrt{\eta}/|h_{k}|
. As such, the selection of a small
\eta
will reduce the amount of energy required to invert a channel and thereby reduce the fading error. However, lowering
\eta
will also increase the relative power of the noise. Therefore, the post-transmission scalar
\sqrt{\eta}
will play the role of a tradeoff parameter between the fading error and the noise-induced error
[
21
]
.

In this work we propose AirReComp, which considers retransmissions in the uplink aggregation step. Specifically, the devices transmit the same values in the uplink
M
times. This way, the signal part of (
11
) will combine constructively, while the additive noise is different for each transmission. After receiving
M
values, the
PS
forms its estimate by calculating their arithmetic mean

\overline{\mathbf{y}}=\frac{\sum_{m=1}^{M}\mathbf{y}_{m}}{M\sqrt{\eta}K}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{|h_{k}|\Delta\tilde{\mathbf{w}}_{n,k}\sqrt{p_{k}}}{\sqrt{\eta}K}+\sum_{m=1}^{M}\frac{\mathbf{z}_{m}}{M\sqrt{\eta}K}.

(12)

Here, we have assumed static fading coefficients
h_{k}
for the duration of the
M
transmissions. To finalize the global update, the
PS
takes the real part of
\overline{\mathbf{y}}
and denormalizes the result according to

\Delta\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{n}=\operatorname{Re}(\overline{\mathbf{y}})\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sigma_{n,k}}{K}+\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mu_{n,k}}{K}.

(13)

This final estimate is then used in the global update step to generate the next iteration of the model as

\mathbf{w}_{n+1}=\mathbf{w}_{n}-\beta\Delta\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{n}.

(14)

The whole AirReComp process is summarized in Algorithm
1
.

Remark 1
.

Since
\Delta\tilde{\mathbf{w}}_{n,k}
has unit variance,
p_{k}
denotes the expected transmission power for each element of the model update, as follows

\mathbb{E}[||\mathbf{x}_{k}||^{2}]=\mathbb{E}[\Delta\tilde{\mathbf{w}}_{n,k}^{T}\Delta\tilde{\mathbf{w}}_{n,k}]p_{k}=dp_{k}.

(15)

Remark 2
.

If the
PS
only has access to the sum of the normalized model updates
\sum_{k=1}^{K}\Delta\tilde{\mathbf{w}}_{n,k}
, it is not possible to perfectly reconstruct
\Delta\mathbf{w}_{n}
in general. However, the distortion caused by transmitting raw model updates with
AirComp
is generally much greater than the distortion caused by the denormalization scheme we opt for here. In the specific case of
\sigma_{1}=\sigma_{2}=...=\sigma_{K}
, this scheme offers perfect reconstruction.

III
Power Control

In this section, we consider a power control problem to minimize the mean-squared estimation error defined as

\mathbb{E}[(\Delta\mathbf{w}_{n}-\Delta\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{n})^{2}],

(16)

where the expectation is taken over the
AWGN
, and
\Delta\mathbf{w}_{n}
and
\Delta\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{n}
are defined in (
5
) and (
13
) respectively. For mathematical tractability, we assume that these gradient elements are
IID
(
IID
)
[
21
,
23
,
24
]
. Additionally, we ignore the effect of normalization by considering
\mu_{n,k}=0
and
\sigma_{n,k}=1
. To perform the minimization, we seek the optimal choice of the transmission powers
\sqrt{p_{k}}
and the post-transmission scalar
\sqrt{\eta}
. Since we consider static fading coefficients, the power control problem only has to be solved once per communication round. To model the limited transmission power of the devices, we consider a peak power constraint

\mathbb{E}[|x_{k}^{(i)}|^{2}]=\mathbb{E}\left[|\Delta\tilde{w}_{n,k}^{(i)}\frac{\bar{h}_{k}}{|h_{k}|}\sqrt{p_{k}}|^{2}\right]=p_{k}\leq P_{\text{max}}\ \forall k,

(17)

where the expectation is taken over the model update
\Delta\tilde{w}_{n,k}^{(i)}
and
P_{\text{max}}
is the maximum transmission power. The minimization of (
16
) is formulated as

\begin{split}\begin{aligned} &\min_{\mathbf{p},\eta}\\
&\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{|h_{k}|\Delta\mathbf{w}_{n,k}\sqrt{p_{k}}}{\sqrt{\eta}K}+\sum_{m=1}^{M}\frac{\operatorname{Re}(\mathbf{z}_{m})}{M\sqrt{\eta}K}-\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{\Delta\mathbf{w}_{n}}{K}\right)^{2}\right]\\
&\textrm{s.t.}\quad p_{k}\leq P_{\text{max}},\ \forall k\in\{1<k<K\}.\end{aligned}\end{split}

(18)

Note that the number of transmissions
M
is given as an input parameter and is selected before the power control problem is solved.

Proposition 1
.

Problem (
18
) has a unique solution. The optimal post-transmission scalar is given by the solution to the
K
subproblems

\eta^{*}=\min_{k}\tilde{\eta}_{k},

(19)

where

\tilde{\eta}_{k}=\left(\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{k}|h_{j}|^{2}\overline{P}+\sigma_{z}^{2}/M}{\sum_{j=1}^{k}|h_{j}|\sqrt{\overline{P}}}\right)^{2}.

(20)

The optimal transmission powers are

p_{k}^{*}=\operatorname{min}\left(\overline{P},\frac{\eta^{*}}{|h_{k}|^{2}}\right).

(21)

The proof of Proposition
1
follows the proof in
[
21
]
and is omitted from this paper.

Remark 3
.

From (
20
), we see that the post-transmission scalar
\sqrt{\eta}
assumes a lower value when more retransmissions are used. As we increase the number of retransmissions, the
SNR
(
SNR
) increases and consequently, the noise-induced error reduces. Therefore, the fading error becomes dominant and the optimal post-transmission scalar
\eta^{*}
is lowered to improve it.

IV
Convergence Analysis

In this section, we analyze the learning performance of Algorithm
1
. As before, we assume that the updates are zero-mean and unit variance, i.e.
\mu_{n,k}=0
,
\sigma_{n,k}=1
. Additionally, we assume that there is only one epoch of local training in each communication round (
E=1
). The performance is measured as the gap between the
FL
loss gap at iteration
n
, defined as

\mathbb{E}[F(\mathbf{w}_{n})]-F(\mathbf{w}^{*}).

(22)

We derive two upper bounds on this loss gap, one for strongly-convex functions and one for convex functions. For both bounds, we use the following well-known lemma
[
22
,
34
]
.

Lemma 1
.

Let
F(\mathbf{x}):\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a convex function with L-Lipschitz gradient. Then, the following inequality holds:

F(\mathbf{y})-F(\mathbf{x})-\nabla F(\mathbf{x})^{T}(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x})\leq\frac{L}{2}||\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}||^{2}.

(23)

Additionally, we make an assumption on the similarity of the local model updates
\Delta\mathbf{w}_{n,k}
and the global model update
\Delta\mathbf{w}_{n}

[
35
,
26
]
.

Assumption 1
.

The local model updates
\Delta\mathbf{w}_{n,k}
are assumed to be independent and unbiased estimates of the global model update
\Delta\mathbf{w}_{n}
.

\mathbb{E}[\Delta\mathbf{w}_{n,k}]=\Delta\mathbf{w}_{n},\ \forall k\in\{1,2,\dots,K\}.

(24)

The local model updates and the global gradient update are in general different. The difference has coordinate bounded variance:

\mathbb{E}[(\Delta w_{n,k}^{(i)}-\Delta w_{n}^{(i)})^{2}]\leq(\sigma^{(i)})^{2},

(25)

where
\Delta w_{n,k}^{(i)}
is the
i
-th element of
\Delta\mathbf{w}_{n,k}
, and
(\sigma^{(i)})^{2}
are the element-wise upper bounds. We will also use
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
to denote the vector of variance bounds.

IV-A

Strongly-convex loss

In this subsection, we assume that the
FL
loss is
\mu
-strongly convex. For such a loss, we use the following lemma
[
22
,
34
]
:

Lemma 2
.

Let
F(\mathbf{x}):\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a
\mu
-strongly convex function with
L
-Lipschitz gradient. Then, the following inequality holds:

\begin{split}\left(\nabla F(\mathbf{x})-\nabla F(\mathbf{y})\right)^{T}\left(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\right)\geq\\
\frac{\mu L}{\mu+L}||\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}||^{2}+\frac{1}{\mu+L}||\nabla F(\mathbf{x})-\nabla F(\mathbf{y})||^{2}.\end{split}

(26)

Before we are ready to state the upper bound, we must also assert that the fixed step size
\beta
has been selected to be sufficiently small for convergence. For AirReComp, the norm of the gradient is dependent on the power with which the signal is received, as such, the step size must be upper bounded as a function of the power control.

Assumption 2
.

Let the fixed step size
\beta
be:

\beta<\operatorname{min}\left(\frac{K\sqrt{\eta}(\mu+L)}{2\mu L\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sqrt{p_{k}}|h_{k}|},\frac{2\sqrt{\eta}}{\mu+L}\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sqrt{p_{k}}|h_{k}|}{\sum_{k=1}^{K}p_{k}|h_{k}|^{2}}\right).

(27)

We are now ready to give the first upper bound on the
FL
loss function (
22
) given the update described in (
13
) and (
14
).

Proposition 2
.

Let

c_{1}\coloneqq\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sqrt{p_{k}}|h_{k}|}{\sqrt{\eta}},

(28)

c_{2}\coloneqq 1-\frac{2\beta}{K}\frac{\mu L}{\mu+L}c_{1},

(29)

and

c_{3}\coloneqq||\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}||^{2}\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{K}p_{k}|h_{k}|^{2}}{K\eta}+\frac{d\sigma_{z}^{2}}{MK^{2}\eta}.

(30)

Then the
FL
loss is upper bounded by

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}[F(\mathbf{w}_{n})]-F(\mathbf{w}^{*})\leq\frac{L}{2}c_{2}^{n}\mathbb{E}[r_{0}^{2}]+\frac{\beta^{2}L}{2(1-c_{2})}c_{3},\end{split}

(31)

where
r_{0}=||\mathbf{w}_{0}-\mathbf{w}^{*}||
is the distance between the initial weight vector and the optimal one,
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}
is a vector of the coordinate bounded variances from (
25
), and
d
is the number of model parameters.

Proof.

The proof is provided in Appendix
A
.
∎

We refer to the first term of the RHS of (
31
) as the
diminishing term
, because it approaches zero if
n\rightarrow\infty
. Along the same line, we refer to the other terms as the
post-convergence terms
because they remain even if
n\rightarrow\infty
. The implications of Proposition
2
are given in Section
IV-C
.

IV-B

Convex loss

In this subsection, we relax the assumption on strong convexity and develop a bound for Lipschitz smooth and convex loss functions. For this bound, we need a different guarantee on the fixed step size than for the strongly convex case.

Assumption 3
.

The fixed step size
\beta
is selected to satisfy:

0<\beta<\frac{\sqrt{\eta}}{L}\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sqrt{p_{k}}|h_{k}|}{\sum_{k=1}^{K}p_{k}|h_{k}|^{2}}.

(32)

Proposition 3
.

Consider Assumption
1
and
3
. Then the
FL
loss is upper bounded by

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}[F(\mathbf{w}_{n})]-F(\mathbf{w}^{*})\leq\frac{K}{2n\beta c_{1}}\mathbb{E}[r_{0}^{2}]+\frac{\beta}{2}(\frac{K}{c_{1}}+L\beta)c_{3},\end{split}

(33)

where
c_{1}
and
c_{3}
are defined in (
28
) and (
30
) respectively.

Proof.

The proof is provided in Appendix
B
.
∎

Just as for Proposition
2
, we refer to the first term of the RHS of (
31
) as the
diminishing term
, and the other terms as the
post-convergence terms
. The implications of Proposition
3
are given in Section
IV-C
.

IV-C

Discussion on Proposition
2
and
3

In this section, we discuss Proposition
2
and
3
. Since the propositions are upper bounds, we are discussing the worst-case properties of the
FL
loss using AirReComp. We are specifically interested in the impact of the number of retransmissions.

IV-C
1
Convergence Rate

As a shorthand, let
x_{n}
equal the diminishing term of (
31
) or (
33
) depending on which bound we are considering. Then, suppose the following holds

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{|x_{n+1}-L|}{|x_{n}-L|}=\alpha.

(34)

If
\alpha=1
, we say that the worst-case convergence rate of AirReComp is sublinear, and if
0<\alpha<1
, we say that the worst-case convergence rate is linear.

For the strongly convex bound, we substitute
x_{n}
with the right-hand-side of (
31
) and
L
with the post-convergence terms. Basic algebra then tells us that
\alpha=c_{2}
. Since
c_{2}<1
, AirReComp has linear convergence rate for strongly-convex loss functions. Additionally, the rate of convergence is increasing with decreasing
c_{2}
. Since
c_{2}
is decreasing in
c_{1}
, we can also say that the rate of convergence is increasing with
c_{1}
.

For the convex bound, the same procedure yields
\alpha=1
. Therefore, AirReComp has sublinear convergence rate for convex loss functions. Since
\alpha
is constant with respect to
M
, this indicates that the asymptotic rate of convergence is independent of the number of retransmissions. However, if we consider a finite
n
, the diminishing term of (
33
) is decreasing in
c_{1}
. Therefore, we say that the non-asymptotic convergence rate of AirReComp with convex loss functions is increasing in
c_{1}
. With these clarifications, we can state the following corollary.

Corollary 1
.

The worst-case convergence rate of AirReComp is increasing in
M
.

Proof.

As clarified previously, the convergence rate is increasing in
c_{1}
for both bounds. Therefore, we need to establish that
c_{1}
is increasing in
M
. From (
28
), we see that
c_{1}
depends on the power control policy via the post-transmission scalar
\eta
and the transmission powers
p_{k}
. We already know that
\eta
is decreasing in
M
from Remark
3
. For the transmission powers, we see in (
21
) that the transmission powers take one of two values:

1.

The device inverts its channel with
p_{k}=\eta/|h_{k}|^{2}
. These devices contribute
1
to the sum in
c_{1}
regardless of
M
;

2.

The device transmits with maximum power
p_{k}=\overline{P}
. These devices contribute
\sqrt{\overline{P}}|h_{k}|/\sqrt{\eta}
to the sum in
c_{1}
. Since
\eta
is decreasing in
M
, these devices contribute more to
c_{1}
for higher
M
.

With these two statements we can readily conclude that
c_{1}
is strictly increasing in
M
, given the condition that at least one device transmits with maximum power. In
[
21
]
, they prove that this condition always holds with optimal power control. Thus, we can conclude that
c_{1}
is strictly increasing in
M
.
∎

IV-C
2
Final error

Since both bounds have post-convergence terms, the algorithm does not converge to a local optimum. Instead, the algorithm converges to a region around the optimum, where the expected remaining loss gap is given by the post-convergence terms. There are two reasons why AirReComp does not converge exactly. Firstly, the channel noise (characterized by
\sigma_{z}
) causes unavoidable errors which prevents exact convergence. Secondly, the difference between local and global model updates (characterized by
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}
) causes a global model update that differs from what is achieved in centralized gradient descent. This result aligns with what was found in
[
35
]
.

Unfortunately, the post-convergence terms are not strictly decreasing in
M
1
1

1

We made a mistake in
[
36
]
claiming the opposite.
. Depending on the parameters, the diminishing terms can both increase and decrease with
M
. As a rule of thumb, if
||\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}||^{2}>>\sigma_{z}^{2}
, the final error increases with
M
. However, due to resource constraints, training must generally be stopped long before convergence is reached. As such, the diminishing term tends to be dominant
[
37
]
and thus the convergence rate tends to be more relevant than the final error. It is worth to note that the gap could possibly be made to approach zero as
n\rightarrow\infty
by considering learning rate scheduling, but in this paper we restrict ourselves to static
\beta
.

V
Heuristic for selecting
M

As we have demonstrated in Section
IV
, the inclusion of retransmissions in Over-the-Air
FL
speeds up the worst-case convergence speed of
FL
training. Simultaneously, the uplink communication cost is proportional to
M
. Therefore,
M
acts as a tradeoff parameter between communication cost and
FL
convergence speed. As such, the selection of
M
is an important factor in model training on resource-constrained devices.

To characterize this tradeoff more precisely, we consider a cost budget
\overline{C}
for the whole training process from communication round
n=0
to the final round
n=N
. For the purposes of this paper, the cost budget
\overline{C}
corresponds to an abstract cost, but could be replaced with, e.g., energy or time depending on the application. As introduced in Section
II
, each communication round is associated with a computational cost
C_{t}
and an uplink communication cost of
C_{u}
, which leads to the following constraint

(C_{t}+MC_{u})N\leq\overline{C}.

(35)

Given that the system satisfies constraint (
35
), we want to find the
M
that minimizes the
FL
loss after the final communication round
N
. We can formulate this as the following integer programming problem

\begin{split}\begin{aligned} &\min_{M,N}\mathbb{E}[F(\mathbf{w}_{N})]\\
&\textrm{s.t.}\quad(C_{t}+MC_{u})N\leq\overline{C}\\
&M,N\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}.\end{aligned}\end{split}

(36)

Since
\mathbb{E}[F(\mathbf{w}_{N})]
is decreasing in N, the highest possible integer
N
that does not break constraint (
35
) can be selected without loss of optimality. Thus we can transform (
36
) to

\begin{split}\begin{aligned} &\min_{M}\mathbb{E}[F(\mathbf{w}_{N})]\\
&\textrm{s.t.}\quad N=\left\lfloor\frac{\overline{C}}{C_{t}+MC_{u}}\right\rfloor\\
&M\in\mathbb{Z}^{+},\end{aligned}\end{split}

(37)

which is easier than (
36
). However, in order to solve this problem, we require an exact expression of
\mathbb{E}[F(\mathbf{w}_{N})]
. In the current
ML
literature, no such expression is known. Instead, we propose to replace the objective function of (
37
) with a proxy function that resembles
\mathbb{E}[F(\mathbf{w}_{N})]
.

V-A

Full bound proxy

Two possible proxy functions for
\mathbb{E}[F(\mathbf{w}_{N})]
are the upper bounds on
\mathbb{E}[F(\mathbf{w}_{N})]-F(\mathbf{w}^{*})
in (
31
) (Proposition
2
) and (
33
) (Proposition
3
). There are two things to note about this choice.

•

Initially, it might seem wrong to use
\mathbb{E}[F(\mathbf{w}_{N})]-F(\mathbf{w}^{*})
as a proxy function for
\mathbb{E}[F(\mathbf{w}_{N})]
, because the desired objective function is the expected loss, while our bounds are on the difference between the expected loss and the optimal loss. However, the optimal loss
F(\mathbf{w}^{*})
is constant with respect to
M
, so including it in the objective function has no impact on the optimal decision variable
M^{*}
.

•

The upper bounds represent a worst-case analysis of the actual loss and we have no proof of tightness for these bounds. Therefore, this proxy function can at best be considered a heuristic for finding the optimal
M
and the efficacy of the choice should be evaluated numerically, which we do in Section
VI
.

V-B

Diminishing term proxy

The full bound proxy described above is the best approximation of
F(\mathbf{w}_{N})
we have available. However, to use it in optimization problem (
37
), rich knowledge about the dataset and loss function is required. Specifically, the Lipschitz smoothness constant
L
and the strong convexity parameter
\mu
must be known to calculate the full bound. The calculation of these constants requires access to the global dataset, whereas the devices are only carrying smaller local datasets. Additionally, for more complicated models such as
DNN
, the calculation of
L
is difficult and one can at best hope to estimate it
[
38
]
. As such, it is desirable to have a proxy function that can be calculated without the knowledge of
\mu
and
L
.

In the strongly convex bound from Proposition
2
(
31
), the
\mu
and
L
constants are ubiquitous, which makes the bound difficult to calculate in practice. However, in (
33
) from Proposition
3
there is of course no
\mu
and the Lipschitz smoothness constant
L
only appears in the post-convergence term. As such, the diminishing term of (
33
) can be calculated using quite limited information. Additionally, we expect the diminishing term to be dominant since training over resource-constrained networks is generally stopped long before the algorithm has fully converged
[
37
]
. With this choice of proxy function, the optimization problem for finding
M
becomes

\begin{split}\begin{aligned} &\min_{M}\frac{K\sqrt{\eta}}{2N\beta\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sqrt{p_{k}}|h_{k}|}\mathbb{E}[r_{0}^{2}]\\
&\textrm{s.t.}\quad N=\left\lfloor\frac{\overline{C}}{C_{t}+MC_{u}}\right\rfloor\\
&M\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}.\end{aligned}\end{split}

(38)

Here, every variable except
\mathbb{E}[r_{0}^{2}]
is reasonable to know in practice. However, since
\mathbb{E}[r_{0}^{2}]
is constant with respect to
M
it can be dropped without changing the optimal decision variable
M^{*}
. We then have the problem

\begin{split}\begin{aligned} &\min_{M}\frac{K\sqrt{\eta}}{2N\beta\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sqrt{p_{k}}|h_{k}|}\\
&\textrm{s.t.}\quad N=\left\lfloor\frac{\overline{C}}{C_{t}+MC_{u}}\right\rfloor\\
&M\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}.\end{aligned}\end{split}

(39)

This final optimization problem is practical and computationally cheap to solve, however it is based on rough approximations of the desired problem (
36
). Therefore, the efficacy of the heuristic is not clear without experimental results, which we provide in Section
VI
.

VII
Conclusion

In this paper, we propose retransmissions for Over-the-Air
FL
, in a system we call AirReComp. Arguably, this is the first work to enable a trade-off between communication resources and convergence speed for Over-the-Air
FL
. To improve the estimation error of AirReComp, we find a closed-form solution for optimal power control in the uplink. This power control solution shows that the number of retransmissions must be known by the transmitters to realize the MMSE estimator. We also prove two upper bounds on the
FL
loss for the AirReComp system, both for strongly-convex and convex loss functions. These bounds show that the convergence rate of
FL
is strictly increasing in the number of retransmissions. Finally, we provide a heuristic for selecting the optimal number of retransmissions based on the upper bounds. All of these results are verified numerically for non-convex loss functions by training
DNN
with AirReComp, both in classification and regression tasks. The simulations also demonstrate that AirReComp can significantly outperform single uplink transmissions. There are conditions for which retransmissions are not desirable, we demonstrate that the heuristic can detect such conditions before training starts, which avoids introducing harmful retransmissions.

There are still interesting open problems on the reduction of estimation errors for Over-the-Air
FL
, including:

•

The consideration of fast-fading channels for AirReComp.
In this work, we consider a network with static channels, which restricts the applicability of the results to application areas with static sensors. In a fast-fading scenario, the problem changes substantially, especially the power control problem described in Section
III
.

•

Other methods of controlling the estimation error for Over-the-Air
FL
.
For instance, one could consider the possibility of a distributed channel code. With analog communication, this appears to be inapplicable, but with the ideas of one-bit digital Over-the-Air Computation, there might be possibilities to explore in this direction
[
42
]
.

•

Tradeoff between transmission power and retransmissions.
Instead of focusing on adding retransmissions to improve the estimation error, one could consider changing the transmission power. Similar to the tradeoff explored in this work, there is a tradeoff between transmission power and convergence rate. Especially for low-powered IoT-devices, it would be interesting to analyze how much the transmission power could be reduced without significantly harming the
FL
performance.

Appendix A
Proof of Proposition
2

In Section
IV
we assumed that the model updates
\Delta\mathbf{w}_{n}
are already of zero mean and with unit variance. Therefore, no normalization is required. This can equivalently be expressed by saying that
\mu_{n,k}=0
and
\sigma_{n,k}=1
. As a consequence of this assumption, we have

\Delta\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{n}=\operatorname{Re}(\overline{\mathbf{y}})=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{|h_{k}|\Delta\mathbf{w}_{n,k}\sqrt{p_{k}}}{\sqrt{\eta}K}+\sum_{m=1}^{M}\frac{\operatorname{Re}(\mathbf{z}_{m})}{M\sqrt{\eta}K}.

(41)

We start the proof by expressing the distance between the optimal global model
\mathbf{w}^{*}
and the current global model
\mathbf{w}_{n}
at communication round
n
as

r_{n}^{2}\coloneqq||\mathbf{w}_{n}-\mathbf{w}^{*}||^{2}.

(42)

This distance can be related to the
FL
loss function via Lemma
1
and Lemma
2
. The plan for the proof is to utilize this relationship to form the upper bound. But before we get to that stage, we need to introduce the impact of AirReComp on the model update. To do so, we use (
14
) with (
42
) to express

\begin{split}r_{n+1}^{2}\coloneqq||\mathbf{w}_{n}-\mathbf{w}^{*}-\beta\Delta\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{n}||^{2}&\\
=r_{n}^{2}-2\beta\Delta\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{n}^{T}(\mathbf{w}_{n}-\mathbf{w}^{*})+\beta^{2}||\Delta\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{n}||^{2}&,\end{split}

(43)

where
\Delta\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{n}
is the model update from (
41
). Next, we take the expectation of (
43
) with respect to
\Delta\mathbf{w}_{n,k}
and
\mathbf{z}_{m}
. To do that, we first need to determine
\mathbb{E}\left[\Delta\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{n}\right]
and
\mathbb{E}\left[||\Delta\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{n}||^{2}\right]
. Beginning with
\mathbb{E}\left[\Delta\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{n}\right]
, we use (
41
) to get

\mathbb{E}\left[\Delta\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{n}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{|h_{k}|\Delta\mathbf{w}_{n,k}\sqrt{p_{k}}}{\sqrt{\eta}K}+\sum_{m=1}^{M}\frac{\operatorname{Re}(\mathbf{z}_{m})}{M\sqrt{\eta}K}\right].

(44)

Because of Assumption
1
and since the noise
\mathbf{z}_{m}
is zero-mean, this simplifies to:

\mathbb{E}\left[\Delta\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{n}\right]=\mathbb{E}[\Delta\mathbf{w}_{n}]\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{|h_{k}|\sqrt{p_{k}}}{\sqrt{\eta}K}.

(45)

Since we assume there is only one epoch (
E=1
), the model update is the gradient of the global loss function, i.e.,
\mathbb{E}[\Delta\mathbf{w}_{n}]=\mathbb{E}[\nabla F(\mathbf{w}_{n})]
, which gives us

\mathbb{E}\left[\Delta\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{n}\right]=\mathbb{E}[\nabla F(\mathbf{w}_{n})]\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{|h_{k}|\sqrt{p_{k}}}{\sqrt{\eta}K}.

(46)

Next, we find
\mathbb{E}\left[||\Delta\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{n}||^{2}\right]
, once again using (
41
)

\begin{split}&\mathbb{E}\left[||\Delta\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{n}||^{2}\right]=\\
&\frac{1}{K^{2}\eta}\mathbb{E}\left[||\sum_{k=1}^{K}|h_{k}|\Delta\mathbf{w}_{n,k}\sqrt{p_{k}}+\sum_{m=1}^{M}\frac{\operatorname{Re}(\mathbf{z}_{m})}{M}||^{2}\right].\end{split}

(47)

The cross-multiplication term disappears because the noise is zero mean and independent from the model update

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\left[||\Delta\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{n}||^{2}\right]=\frac{1}{K^{2}\eta}\mathbb{E}\left[||\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sqrt{p_{k}}|h_{k}|\Delta\mathbf{w}_{n,k}||^{2}\right]\\
+\frac{1}{M^{2}K^{2}\eta}\mathbb{E}\left[||\sum_{m=1}^{M}\operatorname{Re}(\mathbf{z}_{m})||^{2}\right].\end{split}

(48)

The first term of (
48
) can be upper-bounded by the Cauchy-Schwartz inequality as follows

||\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sqrt{p_{k}}|h_{k}|\Delta\mathbf{w}_{n,k}||^{2}\leq K\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}p_{k}|h_{k}|^{2}(\Delta w_{n,k}^{(i)})^{2}\right).

(49)

Then, we apply our assumption on the local model updates from (
25
) to (
49
) to get

||\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sqrt{p_{k}}|h_{k}|\Delta\mathbf{w}_{n,k}||^{2}\leq K\sum_{k=1}^{K}p_{k}|h_{k}|^{2}(||\Delta\mathbf{w}_{n}||^{2}+||\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}||^{2}).

(50)

Combining (
48
) and (
50
) gives us

\begin{split}&\mathbb{E}\left[||\Delta\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{n}||^{2}\right]\leq\\
&\frac{1}{K\eta}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{k=1}^{K}p_{k}|h_{k}|^{2}(||\Delta\mathbf{w}_{n}||^{2}+||\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}||^{2})\right]+\frac{d\sigma_{z}^{2}}{MK^{2}\eta},\end{split}

(51)

and since we assume
E=1
, we get

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\left[||\Delta\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{n}||^{2}\right]\leq\\
\frac{1}{K\eta}\sum_{k=1}^{K}p_{k}|h_{k}|^{2}(||\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}||^{2}+\mathbb{E}\left[||\nabla F(\mathbf{w}_{n})||^{2}\right])+\frac{d\sigma_{z}^{2}}{MK^{2}\eta}.\end{split}

(52)

With
\mathbb{E}\left[\Delta\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{n}\right]
and
\mathbb{E}\left[||\Delta\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{n}||^{2}\right]
evaluated in (
46
) and (
52
), we go back to the model distance. Taking the expectation on both sides of (
43
) yields

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}[r_{n+1}^{2}]\leq\mathbb{E}[r_{n}^{2}]-\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{2\beta\sqrt{p_{k}}|h_{k}|\mathbb{E}[\nabla F(\mathbf{w}_{n})^{T}(\mathbf{w}_{n}-\mathbf{w}^{*})]}{K\sqrt{\eta}}\\
+\frac{\beta^{2}}{K\eta}\sum_{k=1}^{K}p_{k}|h_{k}|^{2}(||\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}||^{2}+\mathbb{E}[||\nabla F(\mathbf{w}_{n})||^{2}])+\frac{d\sigma_{z}^{2}\beta^{2}}{MK^{2}\eta}.\end{split}

(53)

Now we are ready to introduce the
FL
loss by utilizing strong convexity and Lipschitz smoothness. We do this by rewriting Lemma
2
to

\begin{split}&\mathbb{E}\left[\nabla F(\mathbf{w}_{n})^{T}(\mathbf{w}_{n}-\mathbf{w}^{*})\right]\geq\\
&\frac{\mu L}{\mu+L}\mathbb{E}[r_{n}^{2}]+\frac{1}{\mu+L}\mathbb{E}\left[||\nabla F(\mathbf{w}_{n})||^{2}\right],\end{split}

(54)

where we have utilized
\nabla F(\mathbf{w}^{*})=0
for the final term on the RHS. Combining (
53
) and (
54
) yields

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}[r_{n+1}^{2}]\leq\mathbb{E}[r_{n}^{2}]-\frac{2\beta}{K\sqrt{\eta}}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sqrt{p_{k}}|h_{k}|\bigg(\frac{\mu L}{\mu+L}\mathbb{E}[r_{n}^{2}]\\
+\frac{1}{\mu+L}\mathbb{E}\left[||\nabla F(\mathbf{w}_{n})||^{2}\right]\bigg)\\
+\frac{\beta^{2}}{K\eta}\sum_{k=1}^{K}p_{k}|h_{k}|^{2}(||\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}||^{2}+\mathbb{E}[||\nabla F(\mathbf{w}_{n})||^{2}])+\frac{d\sigma_{z}^{2}\beta^{2}}{MK^{2}\eta}.\end{split}

(55)

Since this expression is getting long, we use three constants
c_{2}
,
c_{3}
, and
c_{4}

\mathbb{E}[r_{n+1}^{2}]\leq c_{2}\mathbb{E}[r_{n}^{2}]+c_{3}+c_{4}\mathbb{E}[||\nabla F(\mathbf{w}_{n})||^{2}],

(56)

where

and

were defined in (
29
) and (
30
) respectively. Because of our choice of learning rate in Assumption

(57)

Since
c_{4}

\mathbb{E}[r_{n+1}^{2}]\leq c_{2}\mathbb{E}[r_{n}^{2}]+\beta^{2}c_{3}.

(58)

\mathbb{E}[r_{n}^{2}]
and
\mathbb{E}[r_{0}^{2}]

\mathbb{E}[r_{n}^{2}]\leq c_{2}\mathbb{E}[r_{n-1}^{2}]+\beta^{2}c_{3}.

(59)

We then combine (

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}[r_{n+1}^{2}]\leq c_{2}^{2}\mathbb{E}[r_{n-1}^{2}]+(c_{2}+1)\beta^{2}c_{3}.\end{split}

\mathbb{E}[r_{n}^{2}]\leq c_{2}^{n}\mathbb{E}[r_{0}^{2}]+\beta^{2}c_{3}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}c_{2}^{i}.

(61)

Then we apply

\mathbb{E}[r_{n}^{2}]\leq c_{2}^{n}\mathbb{E}[r_{0}^{2}]+\frac{\beta^{2}}{(1-c_{2})}c_{3}.

(63)

which is the bound from Proposition
2
. ∎

to bound the second term on the RHS

\begin{split}&\mathbb{E}\left[F(\mathbf{w}_{n+1})\right]\leq\\
&\mathbb{E}\left[F(\mathbf{w}_{n})\right]-\frac{\beta c_{1}}{2K}\mathbb{E}\left[||\nabla F(\mathbf{w}_{n})||^{2}\right]+\frac{L\beta^{2}}{2}c_{3}.\end{split}

(71)

Next, we are going to upper bound the first term on the RHS of (
71
). We use the following standard property of convexity (see equation 3.2 from
[
43
]
):

\mathbb{E}[F(\mathbf{w}_{n})]\leq F(\mathbf{w}^{*})+\mathbb{E}[\nabla F(\mathbf{w}_{n})^{T}(\mathbf{w}_{n}-\mathbf{w}^{*})].

(72)

Plug this into (
71
)

\begin{split}&\mathbb{E}\left[F(\mathbf{w}_{n+1})\right]\leq F(\mathbf{w}^{*})+\mathbb{E}[\nabla F(\mathbf{w}_{n})^{T}(\mathbf{w}_{n}-\mathbf{w}^{*})]\\
&-\frac{\beta c_{1}}{2K}\mathbb{E}\left[||\nabla F(\mathbf{w}_{n})||^{2}\right]+\frac{L\beta^{2}}{2}c_{3}.\end{split}

(73)

Some tedious algebraic manipulation transforms (
73
) to

\begin{split}&\mathbb{E}\left[F(\mathbf{w}_{n+1})\right]\leq F(\mathbf{w}^{*})\\
&+\frac{K}{2\beta c_{1}}\mathbb{E}\bigg[||\mathbf{w}_{n}-\mathbf{w}^{*}||^{2}-||(\mathbf{w}_{n}-\mathbf{w}^{*})-\frac{\beta c_{1}}{K}\nabla F(\mathbf{w}_{n})||^{2}\bigg]\\
&+\frac{L\beta^{2}}{2}c_{3}.\end{split}

(74)

Let
r_{n}^{2}\coloneqq||\mathbf{w}_{n}-\mathbf{w}^{*}||^{2}
(same as (
42
)) and

\tilde{r}_{n+1}^{2}\coloneqq||\mathbf{w}_{n}-\mathbf{w}^{*}-\frac{\beta c_{1}}{K}\nabla F(\mathbf{w}_{n})||^{2}.

(75)

Then (
74
) becomes

\begin{split}&\mathbb{E}\left[F(\mathbf{w}_{n+1})\right]\leq F(\mathbf{w}^{*})\\
&+\frac{K}{2\beta c_{1}}\mathbb{E}\left[r_{n}^{2}-\tilde{r}_{n+1}^{2}\right]+\frac{L\beta^{2}}{2}c_{3}.\end{split}

(76)

Now, just like in the previous proof, we want to form a bound with respect to
r_{0}^{2}
. However, instead of using induction we use a telescoping sum. To set it up, we start by taking a sum of (
76
) over
n
iterations to get

\begin{split}&\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[F(\mathbf{w}_{i})\right]-nF(\mathbf{w}^{*})\leq\\
&+\frac{K}{2\beta c_{1}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[r_{i-1}^{2}-\tilde{r}_{i}^{2}\right]+\frac{nL\beta^{2}}{2}c_{3}.\end{split}

(77)

The sum
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[r_{i-1}^{2}-\tilde{r}_{i}^{2}\right]
can be rewritten as

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[r_{i-1}^{2}-\tilde{r}_{i}^{2}\right]=\mathbb{E}[r_{0}^{2}]-\mathbb{E}[\tilde{r}_{n}^{2}]+\sum_{i=0}^{n-2}\mathbb{E}[r_{i+1}^{2}-\tilde{r}_{i+1}^{2}]

(78)

and the middle terms
\sum_{i=0}^{n-2}\mathbb{E}[r_{i+1}^{2}-\tilde{r}_{i+1}^{2}]
will be upper bounded to a constant. We develop this bound next. To start, we plug in the definition of
r_{i+1}^{2}
and
\tilde{r}_{i+1}^{2}
into
\mathbb{E}[r_{i+1}^{2}-\tilde{r}_{i+1}^{2}]
:

\begin{split}&\mathbb{E}\left[r_{i+1}^{2}-\tilde{r}_{i+1}^{2}\right]=\\
&\mathbb{E}\bigg[||\mathbf{w}_{i+1}-\mathbf{w}^{*}||^{2}-||\mathbf{w}_{i}-\mathbf{w}^{*}-\frac{\beta c_{1}}{K}\nabla F(\mathbf{w}_{i})||^{2}\bigg].\end{split}

(79)

Applying (
14
) and doing some algebra yields

\begin{split}&\mathbb{E}\left[r_{i+1}^{2}-\tilde{r}_{i+1}^{2}\right]=\beta\mathbb{E}\left[\beta||\Delta\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{i}||^{2}-2(\mathbf{w}_{i}-\mathbf{w}^{*})^{T}\Delta\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{i}\right]\\
&+\beta\mathbb{E}\bigg[\frac{2c_{1}}{K}(\mathbf{w}_{i}-\mathbf{w}^{*})^{T}\nabla F(\mathbf{w}_{i})-\frac{\beta c_{1}^{2}}{K^{2}}||\nabla F(\mathbf{w}_{i})||^{2}\bigg].\end{split}

(80)

Then we apply (
41
) and after some algebra we have

\begin{split}&\mathbb{E}\left[r_{i+1}^{2}-\tilde{r}_{i+1}^{2}\right]\\
&=\beta^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[||\Delta\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{i}||^{2}-\frac{c_{1}^{2}}{K^{2}}||\nabla F(\mathbf{w}_{i})||^{2}\right].\end{split}

(81)

Next, we insert
||\Delta\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{i}||^{2}
from (
52
) and do some algebra which yields

\begin{split}&\mathbb{E}\left[r_{i+1}^{2}-\tilde{r}_{i+1}^{2}\right]=\beta^{2}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\frac{1}{K^{2}\eta}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sqrt{p_{k}}|h_{k}|\right)^{2}||\Delta\mathbf{w}_{n,k}||^{2}\\
&+\frac{d\sigma_{z}^{2}}{MK^{2}\eta}-\frac{c_{1}^{2}}{K^{2}}||\nabla F(\mathbf{w}_{i})||^{2}\bigg].\end{split}

(82)

Assumption
1
together with some algebra gives us

\begin{split}&\mathbb{E}\left[r_{i+1}^{2}-\tilde{r}_{i+1}^{2}\right]\leq\beta^{2}\frac{c_{1}^{2}}{K^{2}}||\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}||^{2}+\beta^{2}\frac{d\sigma_{z}^{2}}{MK^{2}\eta}.\end{split}

(83)

Finally, we apply the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality to get

\begin{split}&\mathbb{E}\left[r_{i+1}^{2}-\tilde{r}_{i+1}^{2}\right]\leq\\
&\beta^{2}||\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}||^{2}\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{K}p_{k}|h_{k}|^{2}}{K\eta}+\beta^{2}\frac{d\sigma_{z}^{2}}{MK^{2}\eta}=\beta^{2}c_{3}\end{split}

(84)

That concludes the upper bound on
\mathbb{E}\left[r_{i+1}^{2}-\tilde{r}_{i+1}^{2}\right]
so we plug it back into (
77
) to get

\begin{split}&\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[F(\mathbf{w}_{i})\right]-nF(\mathbf{w}^{*})\leq\\
&\frac{K}{2\beta c_{1}}\mathbb{E}\left[r_{0}^{2}-\tilde{r}_{n}^{2}+(n-1)\beta^{2}c_{3}\right]+\frac{nL\beta^{2}}{2}c_{3}.\end{split}

(85)

Since
\tilde{r}_{n}^{2}
and
c_{3}
are positive, we can add one of each to the expectation in the RHS of (
85
) without breaking the inequality, which yields

\begin{split}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[F(\mathbf{w}_{i})\right]-nF(\mathbf{w}^{*})\leq\frac{K}{2\beta c_{1}}\mathbb{E}\left[r_{0}^{2}\right]+\frac{n\beta}{2}(\frac{K}{c_{1}}+L\beta)c_{3}.\end{split}

(86)

Finally, we note that
\mathbb{E}\left[F(\mathbf{w}_{n})\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[F(\mathbf{w}_{i})\right]
for all
i\leq n
. Therefore

\begin{split}&\mathbb{E}\left[F(\mathbf{w}_{n})\right]-F(\mathbf{w}^{*})\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[F(\mathbf{w}_{i})\right]-F(\mathbf{w}^{*})\leq\\
&\frac{K}{2n\beta c_{1}}\mathbb{E}\left[r_{0}^{2}\right]+\frac{\beta}{2}\left(\frac{K}{c_{1}}+L\beta\right)c_{3},\end{split}

(87)

which is the bound from Proposition
3
. ∎
