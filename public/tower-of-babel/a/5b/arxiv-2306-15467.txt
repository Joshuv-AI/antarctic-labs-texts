Second Hankel Determinant for Logarithmic Inverse Coefficients of Convex and Starlike Functions
Vasudevarao Allu, Amal Shaji

Abstract
. In this paper, we obtain the sharp bounds of the second Hankel determinant of logarithmic inverse coefficients for the starlike and convex functions.



2010 Mathematics Subject Classification
30C45, 30C50, 30C55.

1.
Introduction

Let
\mathcal{H}
denote the class of analytic functions in the unit disk
\mathbb{D}:=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\,|z|<1\}
. Here
\mathcal{H}
is
a locally convex topological vector space endowed with the topology of uniform convergence over compact subsets of
\mathbb{D}
. Let
\mathcal{A}
denote the class of functions
f\in\mathcal{H}
such that
f(0)=0
and
f^{\prime}(0)=1
. Let
\mathcal{S}

denote the subclass of
\mathcal{A}
consisting of functions which are univalent (
i.e., one-to-one
) in
\mathbb{D}
.
If
f\in\mathcal{S}
then it has the following series representation

(1.1)

f(z)=z+\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}a_{n}z^{n},\quad z\in\mathbb{D}.

For
q,n\in\mathbb{N}
, the Hankel determinant
H_{q,n}(f)
of Taylor’s coefficients of function
f\in\mathcal{A}
of the form (
1.1
) is defined by

H_{q,n}(f)=\begin{vmatrix}a_{n}&a_{n+1}&\cdots&a_{n+q-1}\\
a_{n+1}&a_{n+2}&\cdots&a_{n+q}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
a_{n+q-1}&a_{n+q}&\cdots&a_{n+2(q-1)}\end{vmatrix}.

The Hankel determinant for various order is also studied recently by several authors in different contexts;
for instance see
[
21
,
22
,
3
]
.
One can easily observe that the Fekete-Szegö functional is the second Hankel determinant
H_{2,1}(f)
. Fekete-Szeg
\ddot{o}
then
further generalized the estimate
|a_{3}-\mu a_{2}^{2}|
with
\mu
real for
f
given by (
1.1
) (see
[
10
, Theorem 3.8]
).

Let
g
be the inverse function of
f\in\mathcal{S}
defined in a neighborhood of the
origin with the Taylor series expansion

(1.2)

g(w)=f^{-1}(w)=w+\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}A_{n}w^{n},

where we may choose
|w|<1/4
, as we know from Koebe’s
1/4
-theorem. Using
variational method, Löwner
[
18
]
obtained the sharp estimate:

|A_{n}|\leq K_{n}\quad\text{for each}\,\,\,n\in\mathbb{N}

where
K_{n}=(2n)!/(n!(n+1)!)
and
K(w)=w+K_{2}w_{2}+K_{3}w_{3}+\cdots
is the
inverse of the Koebe function. There has been a good deal of interest in determining the behavior of the inverse coefficients of
f
given in (
1.1
) when the
corresponding function
f
is restricted to some proper geometric subclasses
of
\mathcal{S}
.

Let
f(z)=z+\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}a_{n}z^{n}
be a function in class
\mathcal{S}
. Since
f(f^{-1})(w)=w
and using (
1.2
), it follows that

(1.3)

\displaystyle A_{2}=-a_{2},

\displaystyle A_{3}=-a_{3}+2a_{2}^{2},

\displaystyle A_{4}=-a_{4}+5a_{2}a_{3}-5a_{2}^{3}.

The
Logarithmic coefficients

\gamma_{n}
of
f\in\mathcal{S}
are defined by,

(1.4)

F_{f}(z):=\log\frac{f(z)}{z}=2\sum\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}\gamma_{n}z^{n},\quad z\in\mathbb{D}.

The logarithmic coefficients
\gamma_{n}
play a central role in the theory of univalent functions. A very few exact upper bounds for
\gamma_{n}
seem to have been established. The significance of this problem in the context of Bieberbach conjecture was pointed by Milin
[
19
]
in his conjecture. Milin
[
19
]
has conjectured that for
f\in\mathcal{S}
and
n\geq 2
,

\sum\limits_{m=1}^{n}\sum\limits_{k=1}^{m}\left(k|\gamma_{k}|^{2}-\frac{1}{k}\right)\leq 0,

which led De Branges, by proving this conjecture, to the proof of Bieberbach conjecture
[
6
]
. For the Koebe function
k(z)=z/(1-z)^{2}
, the logarithmic coefficients are
\gamma_{n}=1/n
. Since the Koebe function
k
plays the role of extremal function for most of the extremal problems in the class
\mathcal{S}
, it is expected that
|\gamma_{n}|\leq 1/n
holds for functions in
\mathcal{S}
. But this is not true in general, even in order of magnitude. Indeed, there exists a bounded function
f
in the class
\mathcal{S}
with logarithmic coefficients
\gamma_{n}\neq O(n^{-0.83})
(see
[
10
, Theorem 8.4]
). By differentiating (
1.4
) and the equating coefficients we obtain

(1.5)

\displaystyle\gamma_{1}=\frac{1}{2}a_{2},

\displaystyle\gamma_{2}=\frac{1}{2}(a_{3}-\frac{1}{2}a_{2}^{2}),

\displaystyle\gamma_{3}=\frac{1}{2}(a_{4}-a_{2}a_{3}+\frac{1}{3}a_{2}^{3}).

If
f\in\mathcal{S}
, it is easy to see that
|\gamma_{1}|\leq 1
, because
|a_{2}|\leq 2
. Using the Fekete-Szeg
\ddot{o}
inequality
[
10
, Theorem 3.8]
for functions in
\mathcal{S}
in (1.4), we obtain the sharp estimate

|\gamma_{2}|\leq\frac{1}{2}\left(1+2e^{-2}\right)=0.635\ldots.

For
n\geq 3
, the problem seems much harder, and no significant bound for
|\gamma_{n}|
when
f\in\mathcal{S}
appear to be known. In 2017, Ali and Allu
[
1
]
obtained the initial logarithmic coefficients bounds for close-to-convex functions. The problem of computing the bound of the logarithmic coefficients is also considered in
[
8
,
25
,
26
,
27
]
for several subclasses of close-to-convex functions.

The notion of logarithmic inverse coefficients,
i.e.
, logartithmic coefficients of inverse of
f
, was proposed by ponnusamy
et al.

[
24
]
. The
logarithmic
inverse coefficients

\Gamma_{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, of
f
are defined by the equation

(1.6)

F_{f^{-1}}(w):=\log\frac{f^{-1}(w)}{w}=2\sum\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}\Gamma_{n}w^{n},\quad|w|<1/4.

In
[
24
]
Ponnusamy
et al.
found the sharp upper bound for the logarithmic inverse coefficients for the class
\mathcal{S}
. In fact ponnusamy
et al.

[
24
]
proved that when
f\in\mathcal{S}
,

|\Gamma_{n}|\leq\frac{1}{2n}\left(\begin{matrix}2n\\
n\end{matrix}\right),\quad n\in\mathbb{N}

and equality holds only for the Koebe function or one of its rotations. Further, ponnusamy
et al.

[
24
]
obtained sharp bound for the initial logarithmic inverse coefficients for some of the important geometric subclasses of
\mathcal{S}
.

Recently, Kowalczyk and Lecko
[
15
]
together have proposed the study of the Hankel determinant whose entries are logarithmic coefficients of
f\in\mathcal{S}
, which is given by

H_{q,n}(F_{f}/2)=\begin{vmatrix}\gamma_{n}&\gamma_{n+1}&\cdots&\gamma_{n+q-1}\\
\gamma_{n+1}&\gamma_{n+2}&\cdots&\gamma_{n+q}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\gamma_{n+q-1}&\gamma_{n+q}&\cdots&\gamma_{n+2(q-1)}\end{vmatrix}.

Kowalczyk and Lecko
[
15
]
have obtained the sharp bound of the second Hankel determinant of
F_{f}/2
,
i.e.,

H_{2,1}(F_{f}/2)
for starlike and convex functions. The problem of computing the sharp bounds of
H_{2,1}(F_{f}/2)
has been considered by many authors for various subclasses of
\mathcal{S}
(See
[
5
,
4
,
14
,
20
]
).

In this paper, we consider the notion of the second Hankel determinant for logarithmic inverse coefficients.
Let
f\in\mathcal{S}
given by (
1.1
), then the second Hankel determinant of
F_{f^{-1}}/2
by using (
1.5
), is given by

(1.7)

\displaystyle H_{2,1}(F_{f^{-1}}/2)

\displaystyle=\Gamma_{1}\Gamma_{3}-\Gamma_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\left(A_{2}A_{4}-A_{3}^{2}+\frac{1}{4}A_{2}^{4}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{48}\left(13a_{2}^{4}-12a_{2}^{2}a3-12a_{3}^{2}+12a_{2}a_{4}\right).

It is now appropriate to remark that
H_{2,1}(F_{f^{-1}}/2)
is invariant under rotation, since for
f_{\theta}(z):=e^{-i\theta}f\left(e^{i\theta}z\right),\theta\in\mathbb{R}
when
f\in\mathcal{S}
we have

H_{2,1}(F_{f_{\theta}^{-1}}/2)=\frac{e^{4i\theta}}{48}\left(13a_{2}^{4}-12a_{2}^{2}a3-12a_{3}^{2}+12a_{2}a_{4}\right)=e^{4i\theta}H_{2,1}(F_{f^{-1}}/2).

The main aim of this paper is to find sharp upperbound for
|H_{2,1}(F_{f^{-1}}/2)|
when
f
belongs to the class of convex or starlike functions. A domain
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{C}
is said to be starlike domain with respect to a point
z_{0}\in\Omega
if the line segment joining
z_{0}
to any point in
\Omega
lies entirely in
\Omega
. If
z_{0}
is the origin then we say that
\Omega
is a starlike domain. A function
f\in\mathcal{A}
is said to be starlike function if
f(\mathbb{D})
is a starlike domain. We denote by
\mathcal{S}^{*}
the class of starlike functions
f
in
\mathcal{S}
. It is well-known that a function
f\in\mathcal{A}
is in
\mathcal{S}^{*}
if, and only if,

(1.8)

{\rm Re\,}\left(\cfrac{zf^{\prime}(z)}{f(z)}\right)>0\quad\text{for}\,\,z\in\mathbb{D}.

Further, a domain
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{C}
is called convex if the line segment joining any two points of
\Omega
lies entirely in
\Omega
. A function
f\in\mathcal{A}
is called convex if
f(\mathbb{D})
is a convex domain. We denote
\mathcal{C}
the class of convex functions in
\mathcal{S}
. A function
f\in\mathcal{A}
is in
\mathcal{C}
if, and only if,

(1.9)

{\rm Re\,}\left(1+\cfrac{zf^{\prime\prime}(z)}{f^{\prime}(z)}\right)>0\quad\text{for}\,\,z\in\mathbb{D}.

2.
Preliminary Results

In this section, we present the key lemmas which will be used to prove the main results of this paper. Let
\mathcal{P}
denote the class of all analytic functions
p
having positive real part in
\mathbb{D}
, with the form

(2.1)

p(z)=1+c_{1}z+c_{2}z^{2}+c_{3}z^{3}+\cdots.

A member of
\mathcal{P}
is called a Carathéodory function. It is known that
\left|c_{n}\right|\leq 2,n\geq 1
for a function
p\in\mathcal{P}
. By using (
1.8
) and (
1.9
), functions in the classes
\mathcal{S}^{*}
and
\mathcal{C}
can be represented interms of functions in Carathéodory class
\mathcal{P}
.

Parametric representations of the coefficients are often useful. In Lemma
2.1
, the formula (
2.2
) is due to Carathéodary
[
10
]
. The formula (
2.3
) can be found in
[
23
]
. In 1982, Libera and Zlotkiewicz
[
16
,
17
]
derived the formula (
2.4
) with the assumption that
c_{1}\geq 0
. Later, Cho
et al.

[
7
]
have derived the formula (
2.4
) in general case and they have also given the explicit form of extremal function.

Lemma 2.1
.

If
p\in\mathcal{P}
is of the form (
2.1
), then

(2.2)

c_{1}=2p_{1}

(2.3)

c_{2}=2p_{1}^{2}+2\left(1-p_{1}^{2}\right)p_{2}

and

(2.4)

c_{3}=2p_{1}^{3}+4\left(1-p_{1}^{2}\right)p_{1}p_{2}-2\left(1-p_{1}^{2}\right)p_{1}p_{2}^{2}+2\left(1-p_{1}^{2}\right)\left(1-\left|p_{2}\right|^{2}\right)p_{3}

for some
p_{1},p_{2},p_{3}\in\overline{\mathbb{D}}:=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|\leq 1\}
.

For
p_{1}\in\mathbb{T}:=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|=1\}
, there is a unique function
p\in\mathcal{P}
with
c_{1}
as in (
2.2
), namely

p(z)=\frac{1+p_{1}z}{1-p_{1}z},\quad z\in\mathbb{D}.

For
p_{1}\in\mathbb{D}
and
p_{2}\in\mathbb{T}
, there is a unique function
p\in\mathcal{P}
with
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
as in (
2.2
) and (
2.3
), namely

(2.5)

p(z)=\frac{1+\left(p_{1}+\overline{p_{1}}p_{2}\right)z+p_{2}z^{2}}{1-\left(p_{1}-\overline{p_{1}}p_{2}\right)z-p_{2}z^{2}}.

For
p_{1},p_{2}\in\mathbb{D}
and
p_{3}\in\mathbb{T}
, there is unique function
p\in\mathcal{P}
with
c_{1},c_{2}
, and
c_{3}
as in (
2.2
)-(
2.3
), namely

p(z)=\frac{1+\left(\overline{p_{2}}p_{3}+\overline{p_{1}}p_{2}+p_{1}\right)z+\left(\overline{p_{1}}p_{3}+p_{1}\overline{p_{2}}p_{3}+p_{2}\right)z^{2}+p_{3}z^{3}}{1+\left(\overline{p_{2}}p_{3}+\overline{p_{1}}p_{2}-p_{1}\right)z+\left(\overline{p_{1}}p_{3}-p_{1}\overline{p_{2}}p_{3}-p_{2}\right)z^{2}-p_{3}z^{3}},\quad z\in\mathbb{D}.

3.
Main Results

Now we will prove the first main result of this paper. We obtain the following sharp bound for
H_{2,1}(F_{f^{-1}}/2)
for functions in the class
\mathcal{C}
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{C}
given by (
1.1
) then

(3.1)

|H_{2,1}(F_{f^{-1}}/2)|\leq\frac{1}{33}.

The inequality is sharp.

Proof.

Let
f\in\mathcal{C}
be of the form (
1.1
). Then by (
1.9
),

(3.2)

1+\cfrac{zf^{\prime\prime}(z)}{f^{\prime}(z)}=p(z)

for some
p\in\mathcal{P}
of the form (
2.1
).
Since the class
\mathcal{C}
is invaraint under rotation and the function is also rotationally invariant, we can assume that
c_{1}\in[0,2]
.
By comparing the coefficients on both sides of (
3.2
) yields

(3.3)

\displaystyle a_{2}=\frac{1}{2}c_{1},

\displaystyle a_{3}=\frac{1}{6}(c_{2}+c_{1}^{2}),

\displaystyle a_{4}=\frac{1}{24}\left(2c_{3}+3c_{1}c_{2}+c_{1}^{3}\right).

Hence by (
1.7
),

H_{2,1}(F_{f^{-1}}/2)=\cfrac{1}{2304}\left(11c_{1}^{4}-20c_{1}^{2}c_{2}-16c_{2}^{2}+24c_{1}c_{3}\right).

Now using (
2.2
)-(
2.4
) and by simplification, we obtain

(3.4)

\displaystyle H_{2,1}(F_{f^{-1}}/2)

\displaystyle=\frac{p_{1}^{4}}{48}-\frac{1}{24}(1-p_{1}^{2})p_{1}^{2}p_{2}-\frac{1}{72}(1-p_{1}^{2})(2+p_{1}^{2})p_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{24}(1-p_{1}^{2})(1-|p_{1}^{2}|)p_{1}p_{3}.

Hereby we have the following cases on
p_{1}
.

Case 1:
Let
p_{1}=1
, then by (
3.4
), we get

|H_{2,1}(F_{f^{-1}}/2)|=\frac{1}{48}.

Case 2:
Let
p_{1}=0
, then by (
3.4
), we get

|H_{2,1}(F_{f^{-1}}/2)|=\frac{1}{36}|p_{2}^{2}|\leq\frac{1}{36}.

Case 3:
Let
p_{1}\in(0,1)
. Applying the triangle inequality in (
3.4
) and by using the fact
that
|p_{3}|\leq 1
, we obtain

(3.5)

\displaystyle H_{2,1}(F_{f^{-1}}/2)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{24}p_{1}(1-p_{1}^{2})\left(\left|\frac{p_{1}^{3}}{2(1-p_{1}^{2}}-p_{1}p_{2}-\frac{2+p_{1}^{2}}{3p_{1}}p_{2}^{2}\right|+1-|p_{2}^{2}|\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{24}p_{1}(1-p_{1}^{2})\left(\left|A+Bp_{2}+Cp_{2}^{2}\right|+1-|p_{2}^{2}|\right)

where

A:=\cfrac{p_{1}^{3}}{2(1-p_{1}^{2})},\quad B:=-p_{1},\quad C:=-\cfrac{2+p_{1}^{2}}{3p_{1}}.

Since
AC<0
, so we can apply case (ii) of Lemma
2.2
.

3(a).
Note that for
p_{1}\in(0,1)
, we have

-4AC\left(\frac{1}{C^{2}}-1\right)-B^{2}=-\cfrac{p_{1}^{2}(14+p_{1}^{2})}{3(2+p_{1}^{2})}\leq 0.

Moreover, the inequality
|B|<2(1-|C|)
is equivalent to
p_{1}(4-6p_{1}+5p_{1}^{2})<0
which is not true for
p_{1}\in(0,1)
.

3(b).
It is easy to check that

\min\left\{4(1+|C|)^{2},-4AC\left(\frac{1}{C^{2}}-1\right)\right\}=-4AC\left(\frac{1}{C^{2}}-1\right),

and from
3(a),
we know that

-4AC\left(\frac{1}{C^{2}}-1\right)\leq B^{2}.

Therefore the inequality
B^{2}<\min\left\{4(1+|C|)^{2},-4AC\left(\frac{1}{C^{2}}-1\right)\right\}
does not holds for
0<p_{1}<1
.

3(c).
Note that the inequality
|C|(|B|+4|A|)-|AB|\leq 0
is equivalent to
4+6p_{1}^{2}-p_{1}^{4}\leq 0
, which is false for
p_{1}\in(0,1)
.

3(d).

Observe that the inequality

|AB|-|C|(|B|-4|A|)=\cfrac{9p_{1}^{4}+10p_{1}^{2}-4}{1-p1^{2}}\leq 0

is equivalent to
9p_{1}^{4}+10p_{1}^{2}-4\leq 0
, which is true for

0<p_{1}\leq p_{1}^{\prime}=\frac{1}{3}\sqrt{\sqrt{61}-5}\approx 0.5588.

It follows from Lemma
2.2
and the inequality (
3.5
) that,

(3.6)

\displaystyle H_{2,1}(F_{f^{-1}}/2)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{24}p_{1}(1-p_{1}^{2})(-|A|+|B|+|C|)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{144}(4+4p_{1}^{2}-11p_{1}^{4})=h(p_{1})

where
h(x)=4+4x^{2}-11x^{4},\quad 0<x\leq p_{1}^{\prime}
.
By simple calculation we can show that maximum of the function
g(x)
exists at the point
x_{0}=\sqrt{2/11}
.

Therefore we can conclude that, for
0<p_{1}\leq p_{1}^{\prime},
we have

|H_{2,1}(F_{f^{-1}}/2)|\leq h\left(\sqrt{\frac{2}{11}}\right)=\frac{1}{33}.

3(e).
For
p_{1}^{\prime}<p_{1}<1
, we use the last case of Lemma
2.2
together with (
3.5
) to obtain

(3.7)

\displaystyle H_{2,1}(F_{f^{-1}}/2)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{24}p_{1}(1-p_{1}^{2})(|C|+|A|)\sqrt{1-\frac{B^{2}}{4AC}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{144}(p_{1}^{4}-2p_{1}^{2}+4)\sqrt{\cfrac{7-p_{1}^{2}}{4+2p_{1}^{2}}}=k(p_{1})

where
k(x)=\cfrac{1}{144}\left(x^{4}-2x^{2}+4\right)\sqrt{\cfrac{7-x^{2}}{4+2x^{2}}},\quad p_{1}^{\prime}<x<1
.

Now we want to find the maximum of
k(x)
over the interval
p_{1}^{\prime}<x<1
. We observe that

k^{\prime}(x)=\cfrac{x}{144}\sqrt{\cfrac{7-x^{2}}{4+2x^{2}}}\left(\frac{92-54x^{2}-15x^{4}+4x^{6}}{(-7+x^{2})(2+x^{2})}\right)=0,

if, and only, if
92-54x^{2}-15x^{4}+4x^{6}=0
. However, all the real roots of the last equation lies outside the interval
p_{1}^{\prime}<x<1
and
k^{\prime}(x)<0
for
p_{1}^{\prime}<x<1
. So
k
is decreasing and hence
k(x)\leq k(p_{1}^{\prime})
for
p_{1}^{\prime}<x<1
.

Therefore we can conclude that for
p_{1}^{\prime}<x<1,

we have

|H_{2,1}(F_{f^{-1}}/2)|\leq k(p_{1}^{\prime})\approx 0.0290035.

Summarizing parts from Case 1-3, it follows the desired inequality (
3.1
).

By tracking back the above proof, we see that the equality in (
3.1
) holds when it is satisfied that

(3.8)

p_{1}=\sqrt{\frac{2}{11}},\quad p_{3}=1,

and

(3.9)

|A+Bp_{2}+Cp_{2}^{2}|+1-|p_{2}^{2}|=-|A|+|B|+|C|,

where

A=\cfrac{\sqrt{\frac{2}{11}}}{9},\,\,B=-\sqrt{\frac{2}{11}},\,\,C=4\sqrt{\frac{2}{11}}.

Indeed we can easily verify that one of the solutions of equation (
3.9
) is

p_{2}=1.

In view of Lemma
2.2
, we conclude that equality holds for the function
f\in\mathcal{A}
given by (
1.9
), where the function
p\in\mathcal{P}
of the form (
2.5
) with
p_{1}=\sqrt{2/11},p_{2}=1
and
p_{3}=1
, that is

p(z)=\cfrac{1+2\sqrt{2/11}z+z^{2}}{1-z^{2}}.

This complete the proof.
∎

Next, we obtained the following sharp bound for
H_{2,1}(F_{f^{-1}}/2)
for functions in the class
\mathcal{S}^{*}
.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{S}
given by (
1.1
) then

(3.10)

|H_{2,1}(F_{f^{-1}}/2)|\leq\frac{13}{12}.

The inequality is sharp.

Proof.

Let
f\in\mathcal{C}
be of the form (
1.1
). Then by (
1.8
),

(3.11)

\cfrac{zf^{\prime}(z)}{f(z)}=p(z)

for some
p\in\mathcal{P}
of the form (
2.1
). By comparing the coefficients on both the sides of (
3.11
), we obtain

(3.12)

\displaystyle a_{2}=c_{1},

\displaystyle a_{3}=\frac{1}{2}(c_{2}+c_{1}^{2}),

\displaystyle a_{4}=\frac{1}{6}\left(2c_{3}+3c_{1}c_{2}+c_{1}^{3}\right).

Hence by (
1.7
), we have

H_{2,1}(F_{f^{-1}}/2)=\cfrac{1}{48}\left(6c_{1}^{4}-6c_{1}^{2}c_{2}-3c_{2}^{2}+4c_{1}c_{3}\right).

Now using (
2.2
)-(
2.4
) and by straightforward computation,

(3.13)

\displaystyle H_{2,1}(F_{f^{-1}}/2)

\displaystyle=\frac{13}{12}p_{1}^{4}-\frac{5}{2}(1-p_{1}^{2})p_{2}-\frac{1}{12}(1-p_{1}^{2})(3+p_{1}^{2})p_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{3}(1-p_{1}^{2})(1-|p_{1}^{2}|)p_{1}p_{3}.

Now we have the following cases on
p_{1}
.

Case 1:
Let
p_{1}=1
, then by (
3.13
), we obtain

|H_{2,1}(F_{f^{-1}}/2)|=\frac{13}{12}.

Case 2:
Let
p_{1}=0
, then by (
3.13
), we obtain

|H_{2,1}(F_{f^{-1}}/2)|=\frac{1}{4}|p_{2}^{2}|\leq\frac{1}{4}.

Case 3:
Let
p_{1}\in(0,1)
. Applying the triangle inequality in (
3.13
) and by using the fact
that
|p_{3}|\leq 1
, we obtain

(3.14)

\displaystyle H_{2,1}(F_{f^{-1}}/2)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3}p_{1}(1-p_{1}^{2})\left(\left|\frac{13p_{1}^{3}}{4(1-p_{1}^{2}}-\frac{5}{2}p_{1}p_{2}-\frac{3+p_{1}^{2}}{4p_{1}}p_{2}^{2}\right|+1-|p_{2}^{2}|\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{24}p_{1}(1-p_{1}^{2})\left(\left|A+Bp_{2}+Cp_{2}^{2}\right|+1-|p_{2}^{2}|\right),

where

A:=\frac{13p_{1}^{3}}{4(1-p_{1}^{2})},\quad B:=-\frac{5}{2}p_{1},\quad C:=-\frac{3+p_{1}^{2}}{4p_{1}}.

Since
AC<0
, so we can apply case (ii) of Lemma
2.2
.

3(a).
Note that for
p_{1}\in(0,1)
we have

-4AC\left(\frac{1}{C^{2}}-1\right)-B^{2}=-\cfrac{3p_{1}^{2}(16+p_{1}^{2})}{(3+p_{1}^{2}}\leq 0.

Moreover, the inequality
|B|<2(1-|C|)
is equivalent to
3-4p_{1}+2p_{1}^{2}<0
which is not true for
p_{1}\in(0,1)
.

3(b).
It is easy to see that

\min\left\{4(1+|C|)^{2},-4AC\left(\frac{1}{C^{2}}-1\right)\right\}=-4AC\left(\frac{1}{C^{2}}-1\right),

and from
3(a),
we know that

-4AC\left(\frac{1}{C^{2}}-1\right)\leq B^{2}.

Therefore, the inequality
B^{2}<\min\left\{4(1+|C|)^{2},-4AC\left(\frac{1}{C^{2}}-1\right)\right\}
does not holds for
0<p_{1}<1
.

3(c).
Note that the inequality
|C|(|B|+4|A|)-|AB|\leq 0
is equivalent to
44p_{1}^{4}-68p_{1}^{2}-16-p_{1}^{4}\geq 0
, which is false for
p_{1}\in(0,1)
.

3(d).

Observe that the inequality

|AB|-|C|(|B|-4|A|)=\cfrac{96p_{1}^{4}+88p_{1}^{2}-15}{1-p1^{2}}\leq 0

is equivalent to
96p_{1}^{4}+88p_{1}^{2}-15\leq 0
, which is true for

0<p_{1}\leq p_{1}^{\prime\prime}=\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\cfrac{\sqrt{211}-11}{6}}\approx 0.38328.

From (
3.11
) and Lemma
2.2
, we obtain

(3.15)

\displaystyle H_{2,1}(F_{f^{-1}}/2)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{3}p_{1}(1-p_{1}^{2})(-|A|+|B|+|C|)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{12}(3+8p_{1}^{2}-24p_{1}^{4})=k(p_{1})

where
k(x)=3+8x^{2}-24x^{4}
,
0<x\leq p_{1}^{\prime\prime}
. Since the function
k^{\prime}(x)>0
in
0<x\leq p_{1}^{\prime\prime}
, we get
k(x)\leq k(p_{1}^{\prime\prime})
for
0<x\leq p_{1}^{\prime\prime}
.
Therefore, we have

|H_{2,1}(F_{f^{-1}}/2)|\leq\cfrac{1}{48}(-58+5\sqrt{211)}\approx 0.304775.

3(e).
Furthermore, for
p_{1}^{\prime\prime}<p1<1
, from (
3.13
) and Lemma
2.2
, it follows that

(3.16)

\displaystyle H_{2,1}(F_{f^{-1}}/2)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{24}p_{1}(1-p_{1}^{2})(|C|+|A|)\sqrt{1-\frac{B^{2}}{4AC}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{6}(12p_{1}^{4}-2p_{1}^{2}+3)\sqrt{\cfrac{16-3p_{1}^{2}}{39+13p_{1}^{2}}}=k(p_{1})

where

k(x)=\cfrac{1}{6}\sqrt{\cfrac{16-3x^{2}}{39+13x^{2}}}\left(12x^{4}-2x^{2}+3\right)\text{for}p_{1}^{\prime\prime}<x<1.

As
k^{\prime}(x)=0
has no solution in
(p_{1}^{\prime\prime},1)
and
k^{\prime}(x)>0
, the maximum exits at
x=1
.
Therefore we conclude that

|H_{2,1}(F_{f^{-1}}/2)|\leq k(1)=\cfrac{13}{12}\quad\text{for}\quad p_{1}^{\prime\prime}<x<1.

Summarizing the cases 1-3 it follows that the inequality (
3.10
) holds. We now proceed to prove the equality part. Consider the Koebe function

k(z)=\frac{z}{(1-z)^{2}}.

Clearly
k\in\mathcal{S}^{*}
and it is easy to show that

|H_{2,1}(F_{k^{-1}}/2)|=\cfrac{13}{12}.

This completes the proof.
