Abstract
We propose four-field and five-field Hu–Washizu-type mixed formulations for nonlinear poroelasticity – a coupled fluid diffusion and solid deformation process – considering that the permeability depends on a linear combination between fluid pressure and dilation. As the determination of the physical strains is necessary, the first formulation is written in terms of the primal unknowns of solid displacement and pore fluid pressure as well as the poroelastic stress and the infinitesimal strain, and it considers strongly symmetric Cauchy stresses. The second formulation imposes stress symmetry in a weak sense and it requires the additional unknown of solid rotation tensor. We study the unique solvability of the problem using the Banach fixed-point theory, properties of twofold saddle-point problems, and the Banach–Nečas–Babuška theory. We propose monolithic Galerkin discretisations based on conforming Arnold–Winther for poroelastic stress and displacement, and either PEERS or Arnold–Falk–Winther finite element families for the stress-displacement-rotation field variables. The wellposedness of the discrete problem is established as well, and we show a priori error estimates in the natural norms. Some numerical examples are provided to confirm the rates of convergence predicted by the theory, and we also illustrate the use of the formulation in some typical tests in Biot poroelasticity.



Keywords:

Mixed finite element methods,
Hu–Washizu formulation, nonlinear poroelasticity, twofold saddle-point problems, fixed-point operators.

Introduction

1.1
Scope

The coupling of interstitial fluid flow and solid mechanics in a porous medium has an important role in a number of socially relevant applications
[
11
]
. In particular, nonlinear poroelasticity equations arise, for example, in models of geomechanics and in the study of deformable soft tissues (such as filtration of aqueous humor through cartilage-like structures in the eye and with application in glaucoma formation). In the present work we focus on the case of fully saturated deformable porous media (the solid and fluid constituents of the mixture occupy a complementary fraction of volume in the macroscopic body) and in instances where the permeability coefficient depends on the porosity (which is in turn related to the total amount of fluid in the poroelastic mixture)
[
21
]
. In the classical form for this class of problems the momentum and mass balance equations for a solid-fluid mixture are written in terms of the solid displacement of the porous matrix and the averaged interstitial pressure. Examples of analysis of existence and uniqueness of solution can be found in
[
8
,
10
,
16
,
28
,
47
,
48
,
50
]
. These works expand the theory available for linear Biot consolidation problems using, for example, constructive Galerkin approximations together with Brouwer’s fixed-point arguments with compactness and passage to the limit, the theory of monotone operators in Banach spaces and semigroups, abstract results on doubly nonlinear evolution equations, and the modification of the arguments to the case of pseudo-monotone nonlinear couplings using Brézis’ theory. One of the goals of this paper is to extend the previous analysis to the case of mixed formulations for the solid phase. Rewriting the governing equations in mixed form using the Hellinger–Reissner principle and writing the total poroelastic stress as a new unknown, is an approach employed already in the analysis of a number of mixed models for linear poroelasticity
[
2
,
7
,
24
,
39
,
53
]
and in poroelasticity/free-fluid couplings
[
1
,
19
,
41
]
. These formulations may incorporate also the tensor of rotations to impose in a weak manner the symmetry of the poroelastic stress tensor. There, in deriving the weak forms, one tests the constitutive equation for stress against a test function for stress. In contrast, in the present treatment we also require the tensor of infinitesimal strains as an unknown since it is an important field acting in the coupling with the fluid phase mechanics via the nonlinear permeability (regarded as a function of the interstitial fluid pressure and the trace of the strain tensor). In the context of elasticity problems, the popular Hu–Washizu formulation
[
36
,
52
]
has displacement, stress and strain tensors as three unknowns, and we note that the Hellinger–Reissner formulation mentioned above is a special case of the Hu–Washizu formulation (it is obtained after applying the Fenchel–Legendre transformation eliminating the strain from the latter formulation
[
12
]
).

Apart from the application of Hu–Washizu formulations in many works for linear elasticity (see, e.g.,
[
22
,
23
,
37
,
38
,
51
]
and the references therein), the solvability analysis of the continuous and discrete twofold saddle-point mixed problems (including also error estimates) has been carried out in
[
30
,
32
]
for Hencky-strain nonlinear elasticity, as well as for more recent models for stress-assisted diffusion coupled with poroelasticity
[
33
]
. There, one tests the constitutive equation for stress against the test function associated with the space of infinitesimal strains. In that setting, a key ingredient in the analysis is the assumption that the nonlinearity in the weak forms induces a Lipschitz continuous and strongly monotone operator (this last condition being required in a suitable kernel).

In the present scenario the analysis requires to define a nonlinear operator
\mbox{\boldmath{$A$}}:\mathbf{X}\to\mathbf{X}^{\prime}
where
\mathbf{X}
consists of square integrable and symmetric tensors and scalar functions in
\mathrm{H}^{1}
. The nonlinearity is inherited from the nonlinear dependence of permeability on fluid pressure and on skeleton strains, and for some constitutive forms, it does not necessarily imply that
A
is monotone. In this work we present two formulations, in the first we impose the symmetry of the stress tensor in a strong way, while in the second we impose the symmetry in a weak sense, using the rotation tensor as a further unknown. Then, similarly to
[
18
]
(see also
[
31
]
) we have the nonlinear term inside the saddle-point structure, unlike
[
15
,
20
]
where the nonlinear term is associated with a perturbation of the saddle-point problem. Therefore we proceed by a fixed-point argument and consider a linear twofold saddle-point formulation that suggests the structure of a fixed-point operator. This map is shown to be well-defined (for this we use an appropriate adaptation of the theory from, e.g.,
[
35
]
), to map a conveniently chosen ball into itself, and to be Lipschitz continuous. Then, by establishing a contracting property the unique solvability will be a consequence of Banach fixed-point theorem. Such an analysis hinges on data smallness assumptions, which involve boundary data, source terms, and permeability bounds.

For the associated Galerkin schemes we employ, on the one hand, for the strong symmetry formulation, Arnold–Winther finite elements of degree
k\geq 1

[
5
]
to approximate the strain tensor, poroelastic stress tensor and displacement, and continuous piecewise polynomials of degree
k+1
for the pore pressure; and we note that for this finite element family, one can also employ piecewise polynomials of degree
k+2
for the symmetric strain tensor to maintain inf-sup stability. On the other hand, for the weak symmetry formulation, we use the classical PEERS elements
[
3
]
to approximate the strain tensor, poroelastic stress tensor, displacement and the rotation tensor, and continuous piecewise polynomials of degree
k+1
for the interstitial pressure (we also use a family based on Arnold–Falk–Winther elements
[
4
]
). Next we apply the same arguments utilised for the continuous problem to prove unique solvability. In addition, using standard tools and techniques for the error decomposition, and approximation properties of mentioned finite element spaces, we obtain the corresponding Céa estimate and rates of convergence.

Let

\Omega\subset\mbox{$\mathbb{R}$}^{d}

\mathbb{L}_{\mathrm{sym}}^{2}(\Omega):=\{\boldsymbol{\tau}\in\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega):\,\boldsymbol{\tau}=\boldsymbol{\tau}^{\tt t}\},\quad\mathbb{L}_{\mathrm{skew}}^{2}(\Omega):=\{\boldsymbol{\tau}\in\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega):\,\boldsymbol{\tau}=-\boldsymbol{\tau}^{\tt t}\},

to represent the symmetric and skew-symmetric tensors in
\Omega
with each component being square-integrable. Standard notation will be employed for Sobolev spaces
\mathrm{H}^{m}(\Omega)
with
m\geq 0
(and we note that
\mathrm{H}^{0}(\Omega)=L^{2}(\Omega)
). Their norms and seminorms are denoted as
\|\cdot\|_{m,\Omega}
and
|\cdot|_{m,\Omega}
, respectively (as well as for their vector and tensor-valued counterparts
\mathbf{H}^{m}(\Omega)
,
\mathbb{H}^{m}(\Omega)
) see, e.g.,
[
13
]
.

As usual
\mathbb{I}
stands for the identity tensor in
\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
,
and
|\cdot|
denotes the Euclidean norm in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Also, for any vector fields
\boldsymbol{v}=(v_{i})_{i=1,d}
we set the gradient and divergence operators as

\nabla\boldsymbol{v}\,:=\,\left(\frac{\partial v_{i}}{\partial x_{j}}\right)_{i,j=1,d}\,{\quad\hbox{and}\quad}\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits\,\boldsymbol{v}\,:=\,\sum_{j=1}^{d}\frac{\partial v_{j}}{\partial x_{j}}\,.

In addition, for any tensor fields
\boldsymbol{\tau}=(\tau_{ij})_{i,j=1,d}

and
{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}=(\zeta_{ij})_{i,j=1,d}
, we let
\mathbf{div}\,\boldsymbol{\tau}
be the divergence operator
\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits
acting along the rows of
\boldsymbol{\tau}
, and define the transpose, the trace and the tensor inner product, respectively, as

\boldsymbol{\tau}^{\mathrm{t}}:=(\tau_{ji})_{i,j=1,d},\quad\mathop{\rm tr}(\boldsymbol{\tau}):=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\tau_{ii},{\quad\hbox{and}\quad}\boldsymbol{\tau}:{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}:=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\tau_{ij}\zeta_{ij}.

We also recall the Hilbert space

\mathbf{H}(\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits;\Omega)\,:=\,\big\{\mbox{\boldmath{$z$}}\in\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega):\,\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits\,\mbox{\boldmath{$z$}}\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)\big\},

with norm
\|\mbox{\boldmath{$z$}}\|_{\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits;\Omega}^{2}:=\|\mbox{\boldmath{$z$}}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}+\|\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits\,\mbox{\boldmath{$z$}}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}
,
and introduce the tensor version of

given by

\mathbb{H}(\mathbf{div};\Omega)\,:=\,\big\{\boldsymbol{\tau}\in\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega):\quad\mathbf{div}\,\boldsymbol{\tau}\in\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega)\big\}\,,

whose norm will be denoted by
\|\cdot\|_{\mathbf{div};\Omega}
.

1.4
Governing equations

Let us consider a fully-saturated poroelastic medium (consisting of a mechanically isotropic and homogeneous fluid-solid mixture) occupying the open and bounded domain
\Omega
in
\mbox{$\mathbb{R}$}^{d}
, with Lipschitz boundary
\Gamma
. The symbol
n
will stand for the unit outward normal vector on the boundary. Let
\boldsymbol{f}\in\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega)
be a prescribed body force per unit of volume (acting on the fluid-structure mixture) and let
g\in L^{2}(\Omega)
be a net volumetric fluid production rate.

Under the assumption of negligible gravitational effects as well as material deformations being sufficiently small, and varying sufficiently slowly so that inertial effects are considered negligible (see further details in
[
46
]
), we have that
the balance of linear momentum for the solid-fluid mixture is written as

-\mathbf{div}\,\boldsymbol{\sigma}=\boldsymbol{f}\qquad\text{in $\Omega$},

(1.1)

with
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
being the total Cauchy stress tensor of the mixture (conformed by the effective solid stress and effective fluid stress), whose dependence on strain and on fluid pressure is given by the constitutive assumption (or effective stress principle)

\boldsymbol{\sigma}=\mathcal{C}\boldsymbol{d}-\alpha p\mathbb{I}\qquad\text{in $\Omega$}.

(1.2)

Here the skeleton displacement vector
\boldsymbol{u}
from the position
\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}\in\Omega
is an unknown, the tensor
\boldsymbol{d}=\mbox{\boldmath{$\varepsilon$}}(\boldsymbol{u}):=\frac{1}{2}(\nabla\boldsymbol{u}+[\nabla\boldsymbol{u}]^{\tt t})
is the infinitesimal strain, by
\mathcal{C}
we denote the fourth-order elasticity tensor, also known as Hooke’s tensor (symmetric and positive definite and characterised by
\mathcal{C}\boldsymbol{d}:=\lambda(\mathop{\rm tr}\boldsymbol{d})\mathbb{I}+2\mu\,\boldsymbol{d}
),
\mathbb{I}
is the identity second-order tensor,
\lambda
and
\mu
are the Lamé parameters (assumed constant and positive),
0\leq\alpha\leq 1
is the Biot–Willis parameter, and
p
denotes the Darcy fluid pressure (positive in compression), which is an unknown in the system.

We also consider the balance of angular momentum, which in this context states that the total poroelastic stress is a symmetric tensor

\boldsymbol{\sigma}=\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\tt t}.

(1.3)

The fluid content (due to both fluid saturation and local volume dilation) is given by

\zeta=c_{0}p+\alpha\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits\boldsymbol{u},

where
c_{0}
is the constrained specific storage (or storativity) coefficient. Using Darcy’s law to describe the discharge velocity in terms of the fluid pressure gradient, we can write the balance of mass for the total amount of fluid in the mixture as
\partial_{t}\zeta-\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits(\kappa(\phi)\nabla p)=g
in
\Omega\times(0,t_{\mathrm{end}})
, where
\kappa
is the intrinsic permeability (divided by the fluid viscosity) of the laminar flow in the medium, a nonlinear function of porosity
\phi
. In turn, in the small strains limit the porosity can be approximated by a linear function of the fluid content (see for example
[
50
, Section 2.1]
), and so – making abuse of notation – we can simply write
\kappa(\zeta)
. Furthermore, after a backward Euler semi-discretisation in time with a constant time step and rescaling appropriately, we only consider the type of equations needed to solve at each time step and therefore we will concentrate on the form

c_{0}p+\alpha\mathop{\rm tr}\boldsymbol{d}-\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits(\kappa(\boldsymbol{d},p)\nabla p)=g\qquad\text{in $\Omega$}.

(1.4)

Typical constitutive relations for permeability are, for example, of exponential or Kozeny–Carman type (see, e.g.,
[
6
]
)

\kappa(\zeta)=\frac{k_{0}}{\mu_{f}}\mathbb{I}+\frac{k_{1}}{\mu_{f}}\exp(k_{2}\zeta)\mathbb{I},\quad\kappa(\zeta)=\frac{k_{0}}{\mu_{f}}\mathbb{I}+\frac{k_{1}\zeta^{3}}{\mu_{f}(1-\zeta)^{2}}\mathbb{I},

(1.5)

where
\mu_{f}
denotes the viscosity of the interstitial fluid and
k_{0},k_{1},k_{2}
are model constants. We note that in the case of incompressible constituents one has
c_{0}=0
and
\alpha=1
, indicating that permeability depends only on the dilation
\mathop{\rm tr}\boldsymbol{d}=\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits\boldsymbol{u}
(see, e.g.,
[
8
]
). We also note that even in such a scenario (of incompressible phases) the overall mixture is not necessarily incompressible itself.
More precise assumptions on the behaviour of the permeability are postponed to Section
3
.

To close the system of equations, we consider non-homogeneous displacement boundary conditions for the momentum balance and non-homogeneous flux boundary conditions on the mass balance equation. For prescribed
\boldsymbol{u}_{\Gamma}\in\mathbf{H}^{1/2}(\Gamma)
and
r_{\Gamma}\in\mathrm{H}^{-1/2}(\Gamma)
we set

\boldsymbol{u}=\boldsymbol{u}_{\Gamma}\quad\text{and}\quad\kappa(\boldsymbol{d},p)\nabla p\cdot\mbox{\boldmath{$n$}}=r_{\Gamma}\qquad\mbox{on }\Gamma.

(1.6)

Two weak formulations and preliminary properties

2.1
Derivation of weak forms

We proceed to test equation (
1.1
) against
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega)

1.6

\begin{array}[]{rlll}\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\mathbf{div}\,\boldsymbol{\sigma}&=&\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{f}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}&\displaystyle\forall\,\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega),\\[8.61108pt]
\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\tau}:\boldsymbol{d}-\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\mathbf{div}\,\boldsymbol{\tau}&=&\displaystyle-\langle\boldsymbol{\tau}\mbox{\boldmath{$n$}},\boldsymbol{u}_{\Gamma}\rangle_{\Gamma}&\displaystyle\forall\,\boldsymbol{\tau}\in\mathbb{H}_{\mathrm{sym}}(\mathbf{div};\Omega),\\[8.61108pt]

where

\underline{\boldsymbol{d}}:=(\boldsymbol{d},p)\in\mathbf{X},\quad\boldsymbol{\sigma}\in\mathbf{Y},\quad\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbf{Z},

\underline{\boldsymbol{e}}:=(\boldsymbol{e},q)\in\mathbf{X},\quad\boldsymbol{\tau}\in\mathbf{Y},\quad\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbf{Z},

where
\mathbf{X}
,
\mathbf{Y}
,

and
\mathbf{Z}

Introducing the nonlinear and bilinear weak forms

and

\displaystyle b_{2}((\underline{\boldsymbol{e}},\boldsymbol{\tau}),\boldsymbol{v})&\displaystyle:=-\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\mathbf{div}\,\boldsymbol{\tau},\end{array}

(2.2)

respectively; and the linear functionals

are endowed with the norms

\|\underline{\boldsymbol{e}}\|^{2}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}}:=\|\boldsymbol{e}\|^{2}_{0,\Omega}+\|q\|^{2}_{1,\Omega},\quad\|\boldsymbol{\tau}\|_{\widetilde{\mathbf{Y}}}:=\|\boldsymbol{\tau}\|_{\mathbf{div},\Omega},\quad\|(\underline{\boldsymbol{e}},\boldsymbol{\tau})\|^{2}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}\times\widetilde{\mathbf{Y}}}:=\|\underline{\boldsymbol{e}}\|^{2}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}}+\|\boldsymbol{\tau}\|^{2}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{Y}}},

\|\underline{\boldsymbol{v}}\|^{2}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{Z}}}:=\|\boldsymbol{v}\|^{2}_{0,\Omega}+\|{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\|^{2}_{0,\Omega},\quad\|((\underline{\boldsymbol{e}},\boldsymbol{\tau}),\underline{\boldsymbol{v}})\|^{2}:=\|(\underline{\boldsymbol{e}},\boldsymbol{\tau})\|^{2}_{\tilde{\mathbf{X}}\times\tilde{\mathbf{Y}}}+\|\underline{\boldsymbol{v}}\|^{2}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{Z}}}.

Next, we define the weak forms
\tilde{a}:\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}\times\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}\to\mathbb{R}
,
\tilde{b}_{1}:\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}\times\widetilde{\mathbf{Y}}\to\mathbb{R}
and
\tilde{b}_{2}:(\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}\times\widetilde{\mathbf{Y}})\times\widetilde{\mathbf{Z}}\to\mathbb{R}
from the expressions

\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle\tilde{a}(\underline{\boldsymbol{d}},\underline{\boldsymbol{e}})&:=\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{C}\boldsymbol{d}:\boldsymbol{e}+\int_{\Omega}\kappa(\underline{\boldsymbol{d}})\nabla p\cdot\nabla q+c_{0}\int_{\Omega}p\,q+\alpha\int_{\Omega}q\,\mathop{\rm tr}\boldsymbol{d}-\alpha\int_{\Omega}p\,\mathop{\rm tr}\boldsymbol{e},\\
\tilde{b}_{1}(\underline{\boldsymbol{e}},\boldsymbol{\tau})&\displaystyle:=-\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\tau}:\boldsymbol{e},\\
\tilde{b}_{2}((\underline{\boldsymbol{e}},\boldsymbol{\tau}),\underline{\boldsymbol{v}})&\displaystyle:=-\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\mathbf{div}\,\boldsymbol{\tau}-\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\tau}:{\boldsymbol{\eta}},\end{array}

(2.6)

respectively, and the linear functionals
\widetilde{G}\in\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}^{\prime}
,
\widetilde{H}\in\widetilde{\mathbf{Y}}^{\prime}
and
\widetilde{F}\in\widetilde{\mathbf{Z}}^{\prime}
as

\widetilde{G}(\underline{\boldsymbol{e}}):=\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}g\,q+\langle r_{\Gamma},q\rangle_{\Gamma},\quad\widetilde{H}(\boldsymbol{\tau}):=-\langle\boldsymbol{\tau}\mbox{\boldmath{$n$}},\boldsymbol{u}_{\Gamma}\rangle_{\Gamma},\quad\widetilde{F}(\underline{\boldsymbol{v}}):=\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{f}\cdot\boldsymbol{v},

respectively, so that the weak formulation of the nonlinear coupled system (
2.5
) reads:
Find
((\underline{\boldsymbol{d}},\boldsymbol{\sigma}),\underline{\boldsymbol{u}})\in(\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}\times\widetilde{\mathbf{Y}})\times\widetilde{\mathbf{Z}}
such that

\begin{array}[]{rlll}\displaystyle\tilde{a}(\underline{\boldsymbol{d}},\underline{\boldsymbol{e}})&+\quad\tilde{b}_{1}(\underline{\boldsymbol{e}},\boldsymbol{\sigma})&&=\widetilde{G}(\underline{\boldsymbol{e}}),\\[4.30554pt]
\displaystyle\tilde{b}_{1}(\underline{\boldsymbol{d}},\boldsymbol{\tau})&&+\quad\tilde{b}_{2}(\boldsymbol{\tau},\underline{\boldsymbol{u}})&=\widetilde{H}(\boldsymbol{\tau}),\\[4.30554pt]
&\qquad\tilde{b}_{2}(\boldsymbol{\sigma},\underline{\boldsymbol{v}})&&=\widetilde{F}(\underline{\boldsymbol{v}}),\end{array}

(2.7)

for all
((\underline{\boldsymbol{e}},\boldsymbol{\tau}),\underline{\boldsymbol{v}})\in(\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}\times\widetilde{\mathbf{Y}})\times\widetilde{\mathbf{Z}}.

Remark 2.1
.

Note that when
c_{0}
approaches zero, our control over the
L^{2}
-part of the fluid pressure norm diminishes. Consequently, the uniqueness of fluid pressure cannot be guaranteed unless we search for it within a space such as
\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)\cap L^{2}_{0}(\Omega)
, due to the pure flux boundary conditions imposed on the mass balance equation. Without this consideration, the lack of uniqueness would also extend to stress, as implied by (
1.2
). In such cases, it becomes necessary to restrict tensors in the spaces
\mathbf{Y}
and
\widetilde{\mathbf{Y}}
to those with a zero mean value. A similar scenario arises when
\alpha
tends to zero: the poroelastic stress loses its unique definition (even though the fluid pressure retains it), requiring the adoption of the zero mean condition within the stress space.

2.2
Stability properties and suitable inf-sup conditions

We start by establishing the boundedness of the bilinear forms
b_{1}
,
b_{2}
,
\tilde{b}_{1}
and
\tilde{b}_{2}
:

\displaystyle\big|b_{1}(\underline{\boldsymbol{e}},\boldsymbol{\tau})\big|\leq\|\underline{\boldsymbol{e}}\|_{\mathbf{X}}\|\boldsymbol{\tau}\|_{\mathbf{Y}},\qquad\big|b_{2}((\underline{\boldsymbol{e}},\boldsymbol{\tau}),\boldsymbol{v})\big|\leq\|(\underline{\boldsymbol{e}},\boldsymbol{\tau})\|_{\mathbf{X}\times\mathbf{Y}}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{\mathbf{Z}},

(2.8a)

\displaystyle\big|\tilde{b}_{1}(\underline{\boldsymbol{e}},\boldsymbol{\tau})\big|\leq\|\underline{\boldsymbol{e}}\|_{\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}}\|\boldsymbol{\tau}\|_{\widetilde{\mathbf{Y}}},\qquad\big|\tilde{b}_{2}((\underline{\boldsymbol{e}},\boldsymbol{\tau}),\underline{\boldsymbol{v}})\big|\leq\|(\underline{\boldsymbol{e}},\boldsymbol{\tau})\|_{\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}\times\widetilde{\mathbf{Y}}}\|\underline{\boldsymbol{v}}\|_{\widetilde{\mathbf{Z}}}.

(2.8b)

On the other hand, using Hölder and trace inequalities we can readily observe that the right-hand side functionals are all bounded

\displaystyle\big|G(\underline{\boldsymbol{e}})\big|\lesssim(\|g\|_{0,\Omega}+\|r_{\Gamma}\|_{-1/2,\Gamma})\|\underline{\boldsymbol{e}}\|_{\mathbf{X}}\quad\forall\underline{\boldsymbol{e}}\in\mathbf{X},\qquad\big|\widetilde{G}(\underline{\boldsymbol{e}})\big|\lesssim(\|g\|_{0,\Omega}+\|r_{\Gamma}\|_{-1/2,\Gamma})\|\underline{\boldsymbol{e}}\|_{\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}}\quad\forall\underline{\boldsymbol{e}}\in\widetilde{\mathbf{X}},

\displaystyle\big|H(\boldsymbol{\tau})\big|\lesssim\|\boldsymbol{u}_{\Gamma}\|_{1/2,\Gamma}\|\boldsymbol{\tau}\|_{\mathbf{Y}}\quad\forall\boldsymbol{\tau}\in\mathbf{Y},\qquad\big|\widetilde{H}(\boldsymbol{\tau})\big|\lesssim\|\boldsymbol{u}_{\Gamma}\|_{1/2,\Gamma}\|\boldsymbol{\tau}\|_{\widetilde{\mathbf{Y}}}\qquad\forall\boldsymbol{\tau}\in\widetilde{\mathbf{Y}},

(2.9)

\displaystyle\big|F(\boldsymbol{v})\big|\leq\|\boldsymbol{f}\|_{0,\Omega}\,\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{\mathbf{Z}}\quad\forall\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbf{Z},\qquad\big|\widetilde{F}(\underline{\boldsymbol{v}})\big|\leq\|\boldsymbol{f}\|_{0,\Omega}\,\|\underline{\boldsymbol{v}}\|_{\widetilde{\mathbf{Z}}}\quad\forall\underline{\boldsymbol{v}}\in\widetilde{\mathbf{Z}}.

Finally, it is straightforward to see that the kernels of the bilinear forms
b_{2}
and
\tilde{b}_{2}
are closed subspaces of
\mathbf{X}\times\mathbf{Y}
and
\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}\times\widetilde{\mathbf{Y}}
, respectively. They are denoted as

\begin{array}[]{cc}\mathbf{X}\times\mathbf{Y}_{0}{\quad\hbox{and}\quad}\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}\times\mathbf{Y}_{0},\end{array}

(2.10)

and the second component admits the characterisation

\mathbf{Y}_{0}:=\{\boldsymbol{\tau}\in\mathbf{Y}:\quad\mathbf{div}\,\boldsymbol{\tau}=\boldsymbol{0}\}=\{\boldsymbol{\tau}\in\widetilde{\mathbf{Y}}:\quad\mathbf{div}\,\boldsymbol{\tau}=\boldsymbol{0}{\quad\hbox{and}\quad}\boldsymbol{\tau}=\boldsymbol{\tau}^{\tt t}\}.

(2.11)

On the other hand, we note that
b_{2}
satisfies the inf-sup condition

\sup_{\boldsymbol{0}\neq(\underline{\boldsymbol{e}},\boldsymbol{\tau})\in\mathbf{X}\times\mathbf{Y}}\frac{b_{2}((\underline{\boldsymbol{e}},\boldsymbol{\tau}),\boldsymbol{v})}{\|(\underline{\boldsymbol{e}},\boldsymbol{\tau})\|_{\mathbf{X}\times\mathbf{Y}}}\geq\beta_{b_{2}}\,\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{\mathbf{Z}}\quad\forall\,\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbf{Z}.

(2.12)

This is a well-known result, proven by means of the wellposed auxiliary problem of finding, for a given
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbf{Z}
, the unique
\mbox{\boldmath{$y$}}\in\mathbf{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
such that

-\mathbf{div}[\boldsymbol{\epsilon}(\mbox{\boldmath{$y$}})]=\boldsymbol{v}\quad\text{in }\Omega;\qquad\mbox{\boldmath{$y$}}=\boldsymbol{0}\quad\text{on $\Gamma$},

and then constructing
\hat{\boldsymbol{\tau}}=\boldsymbol{\epsilon}(\mbox{\boldmath{$y$}})
which clearly belongs to
\mathbf{Y}
and, moreover, it satisfies
\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\|_{\mathbf{Y}}\leq C\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{\mathbf{Z}}
.
Similarly,
from
[
29
, Section 3.4.3.1]
we have that there exists
\beta_{\tilde{b}_{2}}>0
such that

\sup_{\boldsymbol{0}\neq(\underline{\boldsymbol{e}},\boldsymbol{\tau})\in\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}\times\widetilde{\mathbf{Y}}}\frac{\tilde{b}_{2}((\underline{\boldsymbol{e}},\boldsymbol{\tau}),\underline{\boldsymbol{v}})}{\|(\underline{\boldsymbol{e}},\boldsymbol{\tau})\|_{\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}\times\widetilde{\mathbf{Y}}}}\geq\beta_{\tilde{b}_{2}}\,\|\underline{\boldsymbol{v}}\|_{\widetilde{\mathbf{Z}}}\quad\forall\,\underline{\boldsymbol{v}}\in\widetilde{\mathbf{Z}}.

(2.13)

In addition, we note that for all
\boldsymbol{\tau}\in\mathbf{Y}_{0}
, it suffices to take
\boldsymbol{e}=\boldsymbol{\tau}
to easily arrive at

\displaystyle\sup_{\boldsymbol{0}\neq\underline{\boldsymbol{e}}\in\mathbf{X}}\frac{b_{1}(\underline{\boldsymbol{e}},\boldsymbol{\tau})}{\|\underline{\boldsymbol{e}}\|_{\mathbf{X}}}\geq\|\boldsymbol{\tau}\|_{\mathbf{Y}}\quad\forall\,\boldsymbol{\tau}\in\mathbf{Y}_{0}{\quad\hbox{and}\quad}\sup_{\boldsymbol{0}\neq\underline{\boldsymbol{e}}\in\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}}\frac{\tilde{b}_{1}(\underline{\boldsymbol{e}},\boldsymbol{\tau})}{\|\underline{\boldsymbol{e}}\|_{\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}}}\geq\|\boldsymbol{\tau}\|_{\widetilde{\mathbf{Y}}}\quad\forall\,\boldsymbol{\tau}\in\mathbf{Y}_{0}.

(2.14)

3
Existence and uniqueness of weak solution

3.1
Preliminaries

We stress that if the permeability
\kappa
is a positive constant
\kappa=\kappa_{0}
or a space-dependent uniformly bounded scalar field
\kappa(\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}})
in
L^{\infty}(\Omega)
, or a positive definite matrix
\kappa=\mathbb{K}(\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}})
, then the variational forms
a(\cdot,\cdot)
and
\tilde{a}(\cdot,\cdot)
are bilinear forms bounded and coercive in
\mathbf{X}
and
\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}
, respectively. In this case the systems (
2.3
) and (
2.7
) are linear twofold saddle-point problems which are uniquely solvable, thanks to the properties of the bilinear forms
\tilde{b}_{i}(\cdot,\cdot),b_{i}(\cdot,\cdot)
and owing to, e.g.,
[
35
, Theorem 3.1]
.

On the other hand, if the permeability
in the variational forms
a(\cdot,\cdot)
,
\tilde{a}(\cdot,\cdot)
induces monotone and Lipschitz-continuous nonlinear operators, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath{$A$}}:\mathbf{X}\to\mathbf{X}^{\prime},\quad\underline{\boldsymbol{d}}\mapsto\mbox{\boldmath{$A$}}(\underline{\boldsymbol{d}}),\quad\langle\mbox{\boldmath{$A$}}(\underline{\boldsymbol{d}}),\underline{\boldsymbol{e}}\rangle:=a(\underline{\boldsymbol{d}},\underline{\boldsymbol{e}}),

\displaystyle\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath{$A$}}}:\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}\to\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}^{\prime},\quad\underline{\boldsymbol{d}}\mapsto\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath{$A$}}}(\underline{\boldsymbol{d}}),\quad\langle\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath{$A$}}}(\underline{\boldsymbol{d}}),\underline{\boldsymbol{e}}\rangle:=\tilde{a}(\underline{\boldsymbol{d}},\underline{\boldsymbol{e}}),

with

\displaystyle|\langle\mbox{\boldmath{$A$}}(\underline{\boldsymbol{d}}_{1})-\mbox{\boldmath{$A$}}(\underline{\boldsymbol{d}}_{2}),\underline{\boldsymbol{d}}_{1}-\underline{\boldsymbol{d}}_{2}\rangle|

\displaystyle\gtrsim\|\underline{\boldsymbol{d}}_{1}-\underline{\boldsymbol{d}}_{2}\|^{2}_{\mathbf{X}},\qquad\|\mbox{\boldmath{$A$}}(\underline{\boldsymbol{d}}_{1})-\mbox{\boldmath{$A$}}(\underline{\boldsymbol{d}}_{2})\|_{\mathbf{X}^{\prime}}\lesssim\|\underline{\boldsymbol{d}}_{1}-\underline{\boldsymbol{d}}_{2}\|_{\mathbf{X}},

\displaystyle|\langle\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath{$A$}}}(\underline{\boldsymbol{d}}_{1})-\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath{$A$}}}(\underline{\boldsymbol{d}}_{2}),\underline{\boldsymbol{d}}_{1}-\underline{\boldsymbol{d}}_{2}\rangle|

\displaystyle\gtrsim\|\underline{\boldsymbol{d}}_{1}-\underline{\boldsymbol{d}}_{2}\|^{2}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}},\qquad\|\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath{$A$}}}(\underline{\boldsymbol{d}}_{1})-\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath{$A$}}}(\underline{\boldsymbol{d}}_{2})\|_{\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}^{\prime}}\lesssim\|\underline{\boldsymbol{d}}_{1}-\underline{\boldsymbol{d}}_{2}\|_{\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}},

then the systems (
2.3
) and (
2.7
) are nonlinear twofold saddle-point problems, which are uniquely solvable thanks to the properties of the bilinear forms
\tilde{b}_{i}(\cdot,\cdot),b_{i}(\cdot,\cdot)
and a direct application of
[
31
, Lemma 2.1]
.

However, and as discussed in
[
9
,
10
,
50
]
, some of the typical nonlinearities assumed by
\kappa
(
1.5
) do not guarantee monotonicity of the nonlinear operators
\mbox{\boldmath{$A$}},\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath{$A$}}}
.

Note, for example, that in
[
50
]
the authors ask that
\kappa
(they only consider it a function of the dilation
\mathop{\rm tr}\boldsymbol{d}
) is such that

\kappa\in C^{1}(\Omega),\quad\kappa(0)>0,\quad\kappa^{\prime}>0,

in
[
28
]
the permeability
\kappa
depends only on the fluid pressure
p
and it is assumed that

0<k_{0}\leq\kappa(s)\leq k_{1}\qquad\forall s\in\mathbb{R}^{+},

and in
[
27
]
a similar uniform boundedness is assumed even if the permeability depends on both pore pressure and the symmetric strain.
In our case, for sake of the analysis in this section, we allow the permeability
\kappa(\underline{\boldsymbol{d}})=\kappa(\boldsymbol{d},p)
to be anisotropic but still require that it is a uniformly positive definite second-order tensor in
\mathbb{L}^{\infty}(\Omega)
, and Lipschitz continuous in
p\in\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)
. That is, there exist positive constants
\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2}
such that

\kappa_{1}|\boldsymbol{v}|^{2}\leq\boldsymbol{v}^{\tt t}\kappa(\cdot,\cdot)\boldsymbol{v},\qquad\|\kappa(\cdot,p_{1})-\kappa(\cdot,p_{2})\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leq\kappa_{2}\|p_{1}-p_{2}\|_{1,\Omega},

(3.1)

for all
\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{\boldsymbol{0}\}
, and for all
p_{1},p_{2}\in\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)
.

3.2
Definition of a fixed-point operator

In view of the discussion
in Section
3.1
, if
A
or
\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath{$A$}}}
(the operators induced by
the nonlinear weak forms
a(\cdot,\cdot)
or
\tilde{a}(\cdot,\cdot)
, respectively) are not monotone, then we proceed to define, for a given
r>0
, the following sets

\mathbf{W}:=\Big\{\underline{\boldsymbol{w}}:=(\boldsymbol{w},s)\in\mathbf{X}\,:\quad\|\underline{\boldsymbol{w}}\|_{\mathbf{X}}\leq r\Big\}{\quad\hbox{and}\quad}\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}:=\Big\{\underline{\boldsymbol{w}}:=(\boldsymbol{w},s)\in\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}\,:\quad\|\underline{\boldsymbol{w}}\|_{\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}}\leq r\Big\},

(3.2)

which are closed balls of
\mathbf{X}
and
\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}
, respectively, with centre at the origin and radius
r
. Next, for a fixed
\underline{\boldsymbol{w}}:=(\boldsymbol{w},s)
in
\mathbf{W}
or
\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}
, we define the bilinear forms
a_{\underline{\boldsymbol{w}}}:\mathbf{X}\times\mathbf{X}\to\mathbb{R}
and
\tilde{a}_{\underline{\boldsymbol{w}}}:\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}\times\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}\to\mathbb{R}
as follows

\begin{array}[]{cc}\displaystyle a_{\underline{\boldsymbol{w}}}(\underline{\boldsymbol{d}},\underline{\boldsymbol{e}}):=\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{C}\boldsymbol{d}:\boldsymbol{e}+\int_{\Omega}\kappa(\underline{\boldsymbol{w}})\nabla p\cdot\nabla q+c_{0}\int_{\Omega}p\,q+\alpha\int_{\Omega}q\,\mathop{\rm tr}\boldsymbol{d}-\alpha\int_{\Omega}p\,\mathop{\rm tr}\boldsymbol{e},\quad\forall\,\underline{\boldsymbol{d}},\,\underline{\boldsymbol{e}}\in\mathbf{X},\\[12.91663pt]
\displaystyle\tilde{a}_{\underline{\boldsymbol{w}}}(\underline{\boldsymbol{d}},\underline{\boldsymbol{e}}):=\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{C}\boldsymbol{d}:\boldsymbol{e}+\int_{\Omega}\kappa(\underline{\boldsymbol{w}})\nabla p\cdot\nabla q+c_{0}\int_{\Omega}p\,q+\alpha\int_{\Omega}q\,\mathop{\rm tr}\boldsymbol{d}-\alpha\int_{\Omega}p\,\mathop{\rm tr}\boldsymbol{e},\quad\forall\,\underline{\boldsymbol{d}},\,\underline{\boldsymbol{e}}\in\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}.\end{array}

(3.3)

Thanks to the assumptions on the nonlinear permeability, we can infer that these forms are continuous

\begin{array}[]{cc}\big|a_{\underline{\boldsymbol{w}}}(\underline{\boldsymbol{d}},\underline{\boldsymbol{e}})\big|\leq C_{a}\|\underline{\boldsymbol{d}}\|_{\mathbf{X}}\|\underline{\boldsymbol{e}}\|_{\mathbf{X}}\qquad\forall\,\underline{\boldsymbol{d}},\,\underline{\boldsymbol{e}}\in\mathbf{X},\\
\big|\tilde{a}_{\underline{\boldsymbol{w}}}(\underline{\boldsymbol{d}},\underline{\boldsymbol{e}})\big|\leq C_{a}\|\underline{\boldsymbol{d}}\|_{\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}}\|\underline{\boldsymbol{e}}\|_{\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}}\qquad\forall\,\underline{\boldsymbol{d}},\,\underline{\boldsymbol{e}}\in\widetilde{\mathbf{X}},\end{array}

(3.4)

with
C_{a}:=\mathop{\rm max}\limits\{2\mu+d\lambda,\,c_{0},\,d\,\alpha,\,\kappa_{2}\,r\}>0
, as well as coercive over all of
\mathbf{X}
and
\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}
, respectively

\begin{array}[]{cc}a_{\underline{\boldsymbol{w}}}(\underline{\boldsymbol{e}},\underline{\boldsymbol{e}})\geq c_{a}\|\underline{\boldsymbol{e}}\|^{2}_{\mathbf{X}}\qquad\forall\,\underline{\boldsymbol{e}}\in\mathbf{X},\\
\tilde{a}_{\underline{\boldsymbol{w}}}(\underline{\boldsymbol{e}},\underline{\boldsymbol{e}})\geq c_{a}\|\underline{\boldsymbol{e}}\|^{2}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}}\qquad\forall\,\underline{\boldsymbol{e}}\in\widetilde{\mathbf{X}},\end{array}

(3.5)

with
c_{a}:=\min\{\kappa_{1},\,2\mu,\,c_{0}\}>0
.

Then we define the following fixed-point operators

\begin{array}[]{cc}\mathcal{J}:\mathbf{W}\subseteq\mathbf{X}\to\mathbf{X},\quad\underline{\boldsymbol{w}}\mapsto\mathcal{J}(\underline{\boldsymbol{w}}):=\underline{\boldsymbol{d}},\\[8.61108pt]
\tilde{\mathcal{J}}:\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}\subseteq\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}\to\widetilde{\mathbf{X}},\quad\underline{\boldsymbol{w}}\mapsto\tilde{\mathcal{J}}(\underline{\boldsymbol{w}}):=\underline{\boldsymbol{d}},\end{array}

(3.6)

where given
\underline{\boldsymbol{w}}=(\boldsymbol{w},s)\in\mathbf{W}
,
\mathcal{J}(\underline{\boldsymbol{w}})=\underline{\boldsymbol{d}}=(\boldsymbol{d},p)\in\mathbf{X}
is the first component of the solution of the linearised version of problem (
2.3
): Find
((\underline{\boldsymbol{d}},\boldsymbol{\sigma}),\boldsymbol{u})\in\mathbf{X}\times\mathbf{Y}\times\mathbf{Z}
such that

\begin{array}[]{rlll}\displaystyle a_{\underline{\boldsymbol{w}}}(\underline{\boldsymbol{d}},\underline{\boldsymbol{e}})&+\quad b_{1}(\underline{\boldsymbol{e}},\boldsymbol{\sigma})&&=G(\underline{\boldsymbol{e}}),\\[4.30554pt]
\displaystyle b_{1}(\underline{\boldsymbol{d}},\boldsymbol{\tau})&&+\quad b_{2}(\boldsymbol{\tau},\boldsymbol{u})&=H(\boldsymbol{\tau}),\\[4.30554pt]
&\qquad b_{2}(\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{v})&&=F(\boldsymbol{v}),\end{array}

(3.7)

for all
((\underline{\boldsymbol{e}},\boldsymbol{\tau}),\boldsymbol{v})\in(\mathbf{X}\times\mathbf{Y})\times\mathbf{Z}
. On the other hand, given
\underline{\boldsymbol{w}}=(\boldsymbol{w},s)\in\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}
,
\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}(\underline{\boldsymbol{w}})=\underline{\boldsymbol{d}}=(\boldsymbol{d},p)\in\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}
is the first component of the solution of the linearised version of problem (
2.7
): Find
((\underline{\boldsymbol{d}},\boldsymbol{\sigma}),\underline{\boldsymbol{u}})\in\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}\times\widetilde{\mathbf{Y}}\times\widetilde{\mathbf{Z}}
such that

\begin{array}[]{rlll}\displaystyle\tilde{a}_{\underline{\boldsymbol{w}}}(\underline{\boldsymbol{d}},\underline{\boldsymbol{e}})&+\quad\tilde{b}_{1}(\underline{\boldsymbol{e}},\boldsymbol{\sigma})&&=\widetilde{G}(\underline{\boldsymbol{e}}),\\[4.30554pt]
\displaystyle\tilde{b}_{1}(\underline{\boldsymbol{d}},\boldsymbol{\tau})&&+\quad\tilde{b}_{2}(\boldsymbol{\tau},\underline{\boldsymbol{u}})&=\widetilde{H}(\boldsymbol{\tau}),\\[4.30554pt]
&\qquad\tilde{b}_{2}(\boldsymbol{\sigma},\underline{\boldsymbol{v}})&&=\widetilde{F}(\underline{\boldsymbol{v}}),\end{array}

(3.8)

for all
((\underline{\boldsymbol{e}},\boldsymbol{\tau}),\underline{\boldsymbol{v}})\in(\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}\times\widetilde{\mathbf{Y}})\times\widetilde{\mathbf{Z}}
.

It is clear that
((\underline{\boldsymbol{d}},\boldsymbol{\sigma}),\boldsymbol{u})
is a solution to (
2.3
) if and only if
\underline{\boldsymbol{d}}
satisfies
\mathcal{J}(\underline{\boldsymbol{d}})=\underline{\boldsymbol{d}}
, and consequently, the wellposedness of (
2.3
) is equivalent to the unique solvability of the fixed-point problem: Find
\underline{\boldsymbol{d}}\in\mathbf{W}
such that

\mathcal{J}(\underline{\boldsymbol{d}})=\underline{\boldsymbol{d}}.

(3.9)

Similarly, the tuple
((\underline{\boldsymbol{d}},\boldsymbol{\sigma}),\underline{\boldsymbol{u}})
is a solution to (
2.7
) if and only if
\underline{\boldsymbol{d}}
satisfies
\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}(\underline{\boldsymbol{d}})=\underline{\boldsymbol{d}}
, and consequently, the wellposedness of (
2.7
) is equivalent to the unique solvability of the fixed-point problem: Find
\underline{\boldsymbol{d}}\in\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}
such that

\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}(\underline{\boldsymbol{d}})=\underline{\boldsymbol{d}}.

(3.10)

In this way, in what follows we focus on proving the unique solvability of (
3.9
) and (
3.10
).
According to the definition of
\mathcal{J}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}
(cf. (
3.6
)), it is clear that proving that these operators are well-defined amounts to prove that problems (
3.7
) and (
3.8

With that in mind, let us define the bilinear forms
A_{\underline{\boldsymbol{w}}}:(\mathbf{X}\times\mathbf{Y}_{0})\times(\mathbf{X}\times\mathbf{Y}_{0})\to\mathbb{R}
and
\tilde{A}_{\underline{\boldsymbol{w}}}:(\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}\times\mathbf{Y}_{0})\times(\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}\times\mathbf{Y}_{0})\to\mathbb{R}

A_{\underline{\boldsymbol{w}}}((\underline{\boldsymbol{d}},\boldsymbol{\sigma}),(\underline{\boldsymbol{e}},\boldsymbol{\tau})):=a_{\underline{\boldsymbol{w}}}(\underline{\boldsymbol{d}},\underline{\boldsymbol{e}})+b_{1}(\underline{\boldsymbol{e}},\boldsymbol{\sigma})+b_{1}(\underline{\boldsymbol{d}},\boldsymbol{\tau}),

(3.11a)

and

\tilde{A}_{\underline{\boldsymbol{w}}}((\underline{\boldsymbol{d}},\boldsymbol{\sigma}),(\underline{\boldsymbol{e}},\boldsymbol{\tau})):=\tilde{a}_{\underline{\boldsymbol{w}}}(\underline{\boldsymbol{d}},\underline{\boldsymbol{e}})+\tilde{b}_{1}(\underline{\boldsymbol{e}},\boldsymbol{\sigma})+\tilde{b}_{1}(\underline{\boldsymbol{d}},\boldsymbol{\tau}),

(3.11b)

respectively, and we state the unique solvability of the linearised problems (
3.7
) and (
3.8
), depending on a smallness of data assumption, as follows.

Lemma 3.1

Given

\dfrac{\gamma_{1}}{r}\big(\|g\|_{0,\Omega}+\|r_{\Gamma}\|_{-1/2,\Gamma}+\|\boldsymbol{u}_{\Gamma}\|_{1/2,\Gamma}+\|\boldsymbol{f}\|_{0,\Omega}\big)\leq 1,

. From the properties of

), (

), (

) and (

,
\mathbf{X}\times\mathbf{Y}_{0}
(cf. (

2.12

((\underline{\boldsymbol{d}},\boldsymbol{\sigma}),\boldsymbol{u})\in(\mathbf{X}\times\mathbf{Y})\times\mathbf{Z}
solution to (

), or equivalently, the existence of a unique
\underline{\boldsymbol{d}}\in\mathbf{X}
such that

. Finally, from

\begin{array}[]{ll}\|(\underline{\boldsymbol{d}},\boldsymbol{\sigma}),\boldsymbol{u}\|_{(\mathbf{X}\times\mathbf{Y})\times\mathbf{Z}}\\[8.61108pt]
\qquad\displaystyle\leq\gamma_{1}\,\sup_{\boldsymbol{0}\neq((\underline{\boldsymbol{e}},\boldsymbol{\tau}),\boldsymbol{v})\in(\mathbf{X}\times\mathbf{Y})\times\mathbf{Z}}\frac{A_{\underline{\boldsymbol{w}}}((\underline{\boldsymbol{d}},\boldsymbol{\sigma}),(\underline{\boldsymbol{e}},\boldsymbol{\tau}))+b_{2}((\underline{\boldsymbol{d}},\boldsymbol{\sigma}),\boldsymbol{v})+b_{2}((\underline{\boldsymbol{e}},\boldsymbol{\tau}),\boldsymbol{u})}{\|(\underline{\boldsymbol{e}},\boldsymbol{\tau}),\boldsymbol{v}\|}\\[17.22217pt]

(3.14)

and after invoking assumption (

Lemma 3.2
.

Given

6.4
Reproducing the Mandel effect

To conclude this section, we utilise the proposed formulation, specifically focusing on the scenario of weakly symmetric stress, to simulate Mandel’s effect (see, e.g.,
[
42
]
or also
[
14
,
17
,
21
,
26
,
34
,
49
]
). Such a problem involves a specimen made of isotropic poroelastic material, which is positioned between two rigid frictionless impervious plates at the top and bottom. The slab is infinitely long with a cross section measuring
2L\times 2H
. The lateral sides of the specimen are free and permeable. In this simulation, a compressive force is exerted on the horizontal plates. As a result, the pore pressure at the centre of the specimen surpasses its starting value during the early stages of the process and subsequently diminishes until it reaches zero. This behavior can be attributed to the drainage of fluid from the specimen through the side edges. Consequently, a greater portion of the applied load is transferred towards the comparatively stiffer central region of the specimen.

To simplify the analysis, as usual, taking into account the symmetry of the geometry and problem set up, we only consider a quarter of the entire domain:
\Omega=(0,L)\times(0,H)

\Omega
are as follows: on the boundary
x=L
the pore pressure is fixed at zero and the normal poroelastic stress is also zero (both imposed as essential boundary conditions). At the boundaries
x=0
and

we impose a sliding condition
