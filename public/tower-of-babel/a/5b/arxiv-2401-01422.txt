Linear-Quadratic Problems in Systems and Controls via Covariance Representations and Linear-Conic Duality: Finite-Horizon Case
Bassam Bamieh

Abstract
Linear-Quadratic (LQ) problems that arise in systems and controls include the classical optimal control problems of the Linear Quadratic Regulator (LQR) in both its deterministic and stochastic forms, as well as 𝖧 ∞ {\sf H}^{\infty} -analysis (the Bounded Real Lemma), the Positive Real Lemma, and general Integral Quadratic Constraints (IQCs) tests. We present a unified treatment of all of these problems using an approach which converts linear-quadratic problems to matrix-valued linear-linear problems with a positivity constraint. This is done through a system representation where the joint state/input covariance (the outer product in the deterministic case) matrix is the fundamental object. LQ problems then become infinite-dimensional semidefinite programs, and the key tool used is that of linear-conic duality. Linear Matrix Inequalities (LMIs) emerge naturally as conal constraints on dual problems. Riccati equations characterize extrema of these special LMIs, and therefore provide solutions to the dual problems. The state-feedback structure of all optimal signals in these problems emerge out of alignment (complementary slackness) conditions between primal and dual problems. Perhaps the new insight gained from this approach is that first LMIs, and then second, Riccati equations arise naturally in dual, rather than primal problems. Furthermore, while traditional LQ problems are set up in 𝖫 2 {\sf L}^{2} spaces of signals, their equivalent covariance-representation problems are most naturally set up in 𝖫 1 {\sf L}^{1} spaces of matrix-valued signals.

Introduction and Motivation

Linear Quadratic (LQ) control problems in systems and controls first arose through the original Linear Quadratic Regulator (LQR)
[
1
]
, which is an optimal control problem, as well as the celebrated Kalman-Yacubovic-Popov (KYP) Lemma
[
2
,
3
,
4
]
. The KYP Lemma can be considered as a test for an Integral Quadratic Constraint (IQC), which can be phrased as whether an LQ optimal control problem has finite or infinite infima as advocated in the influential paper of Willems
[
5
]
.
Other IQC tests can be used to characterize robust stability of feedback systems subject to uncertainties that can be characterized by IQCs
[
6
]
. Those include the Bounded Real Lemma for testing a system’s

{\sf H}^{\infty}
(
{\sf L}^{2}
-induced) norm, as well as the Positive Real Lemma for testing a system’s passivity.
In the same manner as
[
5
]
, by LQ problems we mean something more general than the LQR problem,
namely any problem involving linear dynamics with inputs, and a quadratic form defined jointly on the state and input. The goal is to characterize the extrema of the quadratic form subject to the dynamics as a constraint.
The literature on these problems is
vast, and will not be summarized here. Notably, Linear Matrix Inequalities (LMIs) and Riccati equations appear frequently as
central characters in these intertwined stories.

Connections between LQ problems and LMIs were pointed out by Willems
[
5
]
. The books
[
7
,
8
]
(see also
[
9
]
) have since popularized the many uses of LMIs in systems and controls, as well as more general optimization problems. Another theme in
[
7
,
8
]
is that once a controls problem is formulated as an LMI, then its solution via semidefinite programming is readily achieved. The literature on LMIs is also vast, including a great variety of analysis and synthesis methods, and cannot be summarized here. It is however hard to escape the impression that many arguments with LMIs involve what might be called “algebraic acrobatics”; an LMI for a particular problem is proposed by a skillful acrobat, after which the verification of whether this LMI characterizes the problem at hand is given. One goal of this paper is to step back to try to see a more natural way in which LMIs arise in LQ problems.

It is well known that quadratic programs can be converted to semidefinite programs using Schur complements
[
8
]
. Alternatively, for problems with purely quadratic objectives, a reparameterization in terms of the covariance matrix of the variables (tensor product of vectors) renders the objective linear. This idea appears in optimal control problems in
[
10
,
11
]
, where a discrete-time stochastic LQ problem is reformulated such that the joint covariance of the state and control is the new state. The dynamics of covariances are linear but underdetermined, the objective becomes linear, and there are additional positivity constraints on the state expressing that covariance matrices must be non-negative definite. Such a finite-horizon problem then becomes a finite semidefinite program, and it is then shown that the dual problem naturally leads to an LMI. With a similar approach
[
12
,
13
,
14
]
the covariance representation has been used for
{\sf H}^{\infty}
analysis. More recently
[
15
]
, a joint “empirical covariance” of the state and control vectors is used as the “state” of a data-driven controller, indicating perhaps that covariance representations are suitable for data-driven control methods as well.

In this paper, a variation of the covariance representation method of
[
10
]
is used with a slightly different treatment of the dual problem. Both stochastic and deterministic problems are considered, where the “deterministic covariance” is the rank-one matrix of the outer product of the joint state/input vector. We address continuous-time finite-horizon LQ problems, and view this covariance representation together with its conal constraints as an infinite-dimensional semidefinite program. A natural approach to such problems is to use linear-conic duality and investigate the dual problem. This is where Differential Linear Matrix Inequalities (DLMIs) show up naturally as dual constraints. Since the dual objective is also linear, the dual problem is then solved by extremal solutions (in the Loewner ordering on matrices) of the DLMIs, which in turn are given by Differential Riccati Equations (DREs). We use a Banach space version of weak duality that has a simple proof. Rather than give technical conditions for strong duality, we instead use a complementary slackness (alignment) condition between primal and dual problems. Alignment actually gives additional insight into the solutions, as it shows that all optimal input signals for LQ problems are of the form of static state feedback.

The use of duality in optimal control has some history. In our present context, an LQ problem in covariance representation is most naturally set up in a matrix-valued
{\sf L}^{1}[0,{\scriptstyle T})
signal space. Other examples of
{\sf L}^{1}
optimization in control include
[
16
,
17
]
where duality was used to characterize optimal closed loops as “sparse” impulse responses. Later on
[
18
,
19
]
finite-dimensional approximations to infinite-dimensional dual problems were used to obtain convergent confidence intervals for numerical solutions to mixed-norm robust control problems in a similar spirit to primal-dual algorithms in semidefinite programming
[
8
,
20
]
.

In a general setting, Vinter
[
21
, Theorem 2.1]
developed a duality framework for nonlinear optimal control problems in which the dual constraint is a Partial Differential Inequality (PDI). The number of independent variables in the PDI is one (for time) plus the number of states. In our present context, the dual constraint of an LQ problem is a DLMI, i.e. a ordinary differential inequality. The relation between the PDI of
[
21
]
and the DLMI presented here appears similar to that between the HJB equation of dynamic programming (a PDE) whose solutions for LQ problems are quadratic functionals parameterized by solutions to matrix-valued differential Riccati equations. It appears that when restricted to LQ problems, the PDI of
[
21
]
collapses to the DLMIs presented here, although those exact connections remain to be explored.

In
[
22
]
, Rantzer introduced a criterion for stability from almost all initial conditions in terms of a PDI (without time dependence). Density functionals that satisfy this PDI are duals of classic Lyapunov functions. The interpretation of a Lyapunov functions as a cost-to-go in an optimal control problem gives a similar interpretation of a density functional in a dual optimal control problem. More recently
[
23
]
, duality between this type of analysis and the Koopman representation of dynamical systems have been explored. We again point out that in the present context of LQ problems, DLMIs are parameterized by matrices, which are finite dimensional objects in contrast to the infinite-dimensional objects which solve the PDIs of the more general criteria.

Positive dynamical systems are those whose states have positive components, or more generally evolve in positive cones
[
24
,
25
]
.
For optimal control of positive systems with linear objectives, it is natural to use a dual formulation of the problem as was recently done to provide explicit solutions to the associated HJB equations
[
26
]
. For any linear dynamical system, the covariance representation is a linear positive system (in the Loewner order of positive semi-definite matrices), and thus ideas from positive systems appear more generally applicable to not-necessarily-positive systems. A similar theme is used in
[
27
]
for analysis of arbitrary linear systems with multiplicative stochastic uncertainty, where an equivalent deterministic system acting on matrix-valued covariance signals is a monotone system.

We finally mention that some of the statements with DLMIs in this paper resemble those in
[
28
]
where finite time-horizon IQC problems are investigated, though the techniques we use are somewhat different. In fact, essentially all the results in this paper have appeared elsewhere, and most are a reworking of the ideas of
[
5
]
using duality. Thus the novelty is not in the set of ideas presented, but perhaps in the
sequencing
of these ideas. Whether this is compelling or not is probably a matter of taste. The next subsections summarize the problem formulation and the sequence of steps for using duality to arrive at solutions in terms of DLMIs and DREs. The remainder of the paper is devoted to the details and the necessary background. The paper is written in a tutorial style with an attempt at self containment. Thus many facts that appear in the literature are repeated here for that purpose.

all LQ Problems in four main steps.

1.

The differential equation (
10

\Sigma
can be converted to a linear constraint in function
space by integrating it forward in time

\left.\begin{array}[]{rcl}{\mathcal{E}}\big(\dot{\Sigma}\big)&=&\mathcal{A}\big(\Sigma\big)\\
{\mathcal{E}}(\Sigma(0))&=&{\sf X}_{\rm i}\end{array}\right\}\hskip 20.00003pt\Leftrightarrow\hskip 20.00003pt\left({\mathcal{E}}-{\sf{\bm{I}}}_{\sf f}\mathcal{A}\right)(\Sigma)~=~\mathfrak{h}{\sf X}_{\rm i},

(13)

where
{\sf{\bm{I}}}_{\sf f}
is the forward integration operator, and
\mathfrak{h}
is the unit-step (Heaviside)
function (thus
\mathfrak{h}{\sf X}_{\rm i}

is a constant, matrix-valued function with value
{\sf X}_{\rm i}
).
\left({\mathcal{E}}-{\sf{\bm{I}}}_{\sf f}\mathcal{A}\right)
is a bounded
operator on (matrix-valued)
{\sf L}^{1}[0,{\scriptstyle T}]
, and
equation (
13
) is a linear equality constraint on
\Sigma
.

2.

The “weak duality” statement
for the covariance problem (
12
) is

{\begin{array}[]{rcl}\displaystyle\inf_{\Sigma\in{\sf L}^{1}(0,{\scriptscriptstyle T})}&&\displaystyle\mathchoice{\scalebox{.8}{$\displaystyle\int_{{0}}^{{{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}$}}{\scalebox{.8}{$\textstyle\int_{{0}}^{{{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}$}}{\scalebox{.8}{$\scriptstyle\int_{{0}}^{{{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}$}}{\scalebox{.8}{$\scriptscriptstyle\int_{{0}}^{{{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}$}}\left<{\bm{Q}}\bm{,}\Sigma\right>~dt\\
\big({\mathcal{E}}-{\sf{\bm{I}}}_{\sf f}\mathcal{A}\big)(\Sigma)&=&\mathfrak{h}{\sf X}_{\rm i}\rule{0.0pt}{15.00002pt}\\
{\rm\bf rk}\left(\Sigma\right)&=&1,~\Sigma\geq 0\end{array}}\hskip 20.00003pt\hskip 20.00003pt\geq\hskip 20.00003pt{\begin{array}[]{rcl}\displaystyle\sup_{Y\in{\sf L}^{\infty}(0,{\scriptscriptstyle T})}&&\displaystyle\mathchoice{\scalebox{.8}{$\displaystyle\int_{{0}}^{{T}}$}}{\scalebox{.8}{$\textstyle\int_{{0}}^{{T}}$}}{\scalebox{.8}{$\scriptstyle\int_{{0}}^{{T}}$}}{\scalebox{.8}{$\scriptscriptstyle\int_{{0}}^{{T}}$}}\left<Y\bm{,}{\sf X}_{\rm i}\right>~dt\\

(14)

where the dual variable
Y
is a matrix-valued function over
[0,{\scriptstyle T}]
.
Note that since
(x,v)
are in
{\sf L}^{2}(0,{\scriptstyle T})
, then their outer product

\Sigma\in{\sf L}^{1}_{{\mathbb{S}}_{n}}(0,{\scriptstyle T})
, the
{\sf L}^{1}

space of functions taking values in
{\mathbb{S}}_{n}
, the space of
n\times n
symmetric matrices.

The key to the dual problem is the dual inequality constraint
{\bm{Q}}-\big({\mathcal{E}}-{\sf{\bm{I}}}_{\sf f}\mathcal{A}\big)^{{\bm{*}}}(Y)\geq 0
,
whose structure depends on the adjoint operator
\big({\mathcal{E}}-{\sf{\bm{I}}}_{\sf f}\mathcal{A}\big)^{{\bm{*}}}
. A simple adjoint calculation
(Section
2.2
)
shows that

\displaystyle\big({\mathcal{E}}-{\sf{\bm{I}}}_{\sf f}\mathcal{A}\big)^{{\bm{*}}}(Y)~=~{\mathcal{E}}^{{\bm{*}}}(Y)-\mathcal{A}^{{\bm{*}}}({\sf{\bm{I}}}_{\sf\bm{b}}Y)~

\displaystyle=~\begin{bmatrix}I\\
0\end{bmatrix}Y\begin{bmatrix}I&0\end{bmatrix}-\begin{bmatrix}A^{*}\\
B^{*}\end{bmatrix}\big({\sf{\bm{I}}}_{\sf\bm{b}}Y\big)\begin{bmatrix}I&0\end{bmatrix}-\begin{bmatrix}I\\
0\end{bmatrix}\big({\sf{\bm{I}}}_{\sf\bm{b}}Y\big)\begin{bmatrix}A&B\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\hskip 20.00003pt{\bm{Q}}-({\mathcal{E}}-{\sf{\bm{I}}}_{\sf f}\mathcal{A})^{*}(Y)~

\displaystyle=~{\bm{Q}}-\left({\mathcal{E}}\left({\dot{\Lambda}}\right)-\mathcal{A}^{*}(\Lambda)\right)~=~{\bm{Q}}+\begin{bmatrix}{\dot{\Lambda}}+A^{*}\Lambda+\Lambda A&\Lambda B\\
B^{*}\Lambda&0\end{bmatrix},

where the dual variable
Y
is replaced with

\Lambda:={\sf{\bm{I}}}_{\sf\bm{b}}Y
, the backwards integral of
Y
(which implies
Y=-{\dot{\Lambda}}
).

Thus a Differential Linear Matrix Inequality (DLMI) arises out of the dual cone constraint

\big({\mathcal{E}}-{\sf{\bm{I}}}_{\sf f}\mathcal{A}\big)^{{\bm{*}}}(Y)\geq 0
when expressed in terms of the backwards integral of
Y
.
Note how the structure of the DLMI is really a corollary of the structure of the operators
{\mathcal{E}}
,

\mathcal{A}
, and their adjoints!

Conic duality thus shows that LMIs arise naturally from problems dual to
covariance

optimization problems. A similar statement is much less transparent if one only considers
the original problem statement (
4
) without a covariance reformulation.

3.

Differential
Riccati equations give extremal solutions to DLMIs, which are then shown to be the solutions
to the dual problem since the objective is a linear functional in the dual variables
Y
or

\Lambda
. Thus, Riccati
equations also arise naturally from conic duality. However, In this framework
the DLMIs
come first
, and Riccati
equations arise second as extremal solutions to the DLMIs. Riccati equations can be thought of
in this context as a technique to solve these special DLMIs (and LMIs) rather than using general-purpose convex
optimization methods.

4.

The duality gap will be shown to be zero by constructing optimal primal and dual solutions
together with an
alignment
(complementary slackness) condition between them.
Specifically, linear-conic duality states that if there exists primal and dual variables
{\bar{\Sigma}}
and
{\bar{\Lambda}}

such that

0~=~\left<{\bm{Q}}-{\mathcal{E}}^{*}(\dot{{\bar{\Lambda}}})-\mathcal{A}^{*}({\bar{\Lambda}})\bm{,}{\bar{\Sigma}}\right>,

(15)

then
{\bar{\Sigma}}
and
{\bar{\Lambda}}
are optimal for the primal and dual respectively, and the duality gap is zero.
The optimal solution
{\bar{\Lambda}}
to the dual problem is the solution of a Riccati equation. A classic argument
implies that this solution gives a minimal-rank factorization of the LMI matrix as
follows
2
2

2

This expression is shown here for the notationally simpler case with
N=0

{\bm{Q}}-{\mathcal{E}}^{*}(\dot{{\bar{\Lambda}}})-\mathcal{A}^{*}({\bar{\Lambda}})={\bm{Q}}+\begin{bmatrix}\dot{{\bar{\Lambda}}}+A^{*}{\bar{\Lambda}}+{\bar{\Lambda}}A&{\bar{\Lambda}}B\\
B^{*}{\bar{\Lambda}}&0\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}{\bar{\Lambda}}BR^{\text{-}\frac{1}{2}}\\
R^{\frac{1}{2}}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}R^{\text{-}\frac{1}{2}}B^{*}{\bar{\Lambda}}B&R^{\frac{1}{2}}\end{bmatrix}.

Since any possible
{\bar{\Sigma}}
must be of the form (

condition (
15
) then reads

\displaystyle 0=\left<\begin{bmatrix}{\bar{\Lambda}}BR^{\text{-}\frac{1}{2}}\\
R^{\frac{1}{2}}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}R^{\text{-}\frac{1}{2}}B^{*}{\bar{\Lambda}}&R^{\frac{1}{2}}\end{bmatrix}\bm{,}\begin{bmatrix}{\bar{x}}\\
{\bar{v}}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}{\bar{x}}^{*}&{\bar{v}}^{*}\end{bmatrix}\right>\hskip 20.00003pt

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\hskip 20.00003pt\begin{bmatrix}R^{\text{-}\frac{1}{2}}B^{*}{\bar{\Lambda}}&R^{\frac{1}{2}}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}{\bar{x}}\\
{\bar{v}}\end{bmatrix}=0

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\hskip 20.00003pt{\bar{v}}~=~\left(R^{\text{-}1}B^{*}{\bar{\Lambda}}\right){\bar{x}},

where the first implication is a property of symmetric factorizations of any two positive matrices

Thus the state-feedback nature of optimal solutions of any LQ problem is a consequence of the alignment
condition (

2.1
Matrix Signals and their Norms

We will be concerned with the vector space
{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n}
of
n\times n
matrices, and in particular its subspace

{\mathbb{S}}_{n}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n}
of symmetric matrices.
The vector space
{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n}
has a natural inner product given by

\displaystyle\left<H\bm{,}M\right>~:=~{\rm tr}\!\left(H^{*}M\right).

(16)

This is simply the total sum of the element-by-element product of two matrices
H
and
M
. Equivalently, if each matrix
H
and
M
is expanded as a single vector by stacking its columns over each other, the above sum would simply be the dot product of the two vectors. We note that this inner product generates the Frobenius norm
\|M\|_{F}^{2}={\rm tr}\!\left(M^{*}M\right)=\left<M\bm{,}M\right>
on matrices.

If we restrict attention to the subspace
{\mathbb{S}}_{n}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n}
of
n\times n
symmetric matrices,
the algebraic dual of
{\mathbb{S}}_{n}
is itself since
the action of any matrix
H
on a symmetric matrix
M
is

\left<H\bm{,}M\right>~=~{\rm tr}\!\left(H^{*}M\right)~=~\left({\rm tr}\!\left(H^{*}M\right)+{\rm tr}\!\left(MH\right)\rule{0.0pt}{9.10004pt}\right)/2~=~\left({\rm tr}\!\left(H^{*}M\right)+{\rm tr}\!\left(HM\right)\rule{0.0pt}{9.10004pt}\right)/2~=~{\rm tr}\!\left({\scriptstyle\frac{1}{2}}(H^{*}+H)~M\right),

and therefore the action of
\left<H\bm{,}.\right>
on
{\mathbb{S}}_{n}
is the same as the action of its symmetrization
\left<(H^{*}+H)/2\bm{,}.\right>
.

We will need norms other than the Frobenius norm on matrices, and therefore a different interpretation of (
16
) as a linear functional action (of
H
on
M
). When
M
is a symmetric positive matrix (like
\Sigma
in (
6
)), its trace is the quantity of interest. The trace on positive matrices is a special case of the “nuclear norm” on
n\times n
matrices defined as

\|M\|_{1}~:=~\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{i}(M),

(17)

where
\left\{\sigma_{i}(M)\right\}
are the singular values of
M
. When
M
is symmetric and positive,
{\rm tr}\!\left(M\right)=\|M\|_{1}
, but the definition (
17
) applies to any matrix.
We denote the space of symmetric
n\times n
matrices with the
\|.\|_{1}
norm by
\left({\mathbb{S}}_{n},\|.\|_{1}\right)
.
The norm dual to
\|.\|_{1}
is the maximum singular value norm

\|H\|_{\infty}~:=~\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\sigma_{i}(H).

The fact that this norm is dual to
\|.\|_{1}
follows from the following tight “trace duality” inequality whose proof is given in Appendix
A.1

\left<H\bm{,}M\right>~:=~{\rm tr}\!\left(HM\right)~\leq~\|H\|_{\infty}\|M\|_{1},\hskip 20.00003pt\hskip 20.00003pt\sup_{\|M\|_{1}\leq 1}{\rm tr}\!\left(HM\right)=\|H\|_{\infty}.

Thus the space dual to
\left({\mathbb{S}}_{n},\|.\|_{1}\right)
is
\left({\mathbb{S}}_{n},\|.\|_{\infty}\right)
.
More generally, we can define the space
\left({\mathbb{S}}_{n},\|.\|_{p}\right)
for
p\in(1,\infty)
with the norm

\|M\|_{p}^{p}~:=~\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\sigma_{i}(M)\right)^{p},

and the space dual to it is
\left({\mathbb{S}}_{n},\|.\|_{q}\right)
with
1/p+1/q=1
. However, this level of generality is not needed
here
3
3

3

The
\|.\|_{p}
norms defined above are the so-called Schatten norms
.

We will also need to consider norms and linear functional actions on matrix-valued functions of time.
To motivate the definition we will shortly introduce, consider again the covariance matrix (
6
) and observe that

\int_{0}^{T}{\rm tr}\!\left(\Sigma(t)\rule{0.0pt}{10.00002pt}\right)~dt~=~\int_{0}^{T}{\rm tr}\!\left(\begin{bmatrix}x(t)\\
v(t)\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}x^{*}(t)&v^{*}(t)\end{bmatrix}\right)~dt~=~\int_{0}^{T}\left(x^{*}(t)x(t)+v^{*}(t)v(t)\rule{0.0pt}{10.00002pt}\right)~dt,

where
{\scriptstyle T}=\infty
is a possible limit of this integral. If all signals
(v,x)

need to have finite
{{\sf L}}^{\!2}
norms, then we should require
\Sigma
itself to have finite trace norm integrated over the time interval. We therefore require our matrix-valued signals to be in the following Banach space

{{\sf L}}^{\!1}_{{\mathbb{S}}_{n}}(0,{\scriptstyle T})~:=~\left\{M:(0,{\scriptstyle T})\rightarrow{\mathbb{S}}_{n};~\|M\|_{1}:=\mathchoice{\scalebox{.8}{$\displaystyle\int_{{0}}^{{T}}$}}{\scalebox{.8}{$\textstyle\int_{{0}}^{{T}}$}}{\scalebox{.8}{$\scriptstyle\int_{{0}}^{{T}}$}}{\scalebox{.8}{$\scriptscriptstyle\int_{{0}}^{{T}}$}}\|M(t)\|_{1}~dt~<\infty\right\},\hskip 20.00003pt{\scriptstyle T}<\infty,~\mbox{or}~{\scriptstyle T}=\infty.

(18)

Note that the matrix norm

The space dual to (

(19)

The linear functional action is defined as follows

\left<H\bm{,}M\right>~:=~\mathchoice{\scalebox{.8}{$\displaystyle\int_{{0}}^{{T}}$}}{\scalebox{.8}{$\textstyle\int_{{0}}^{{T}}$}}{\scalebox{.8}{$\scriptstyle\int_{{0}}^{{T}}$}}{\scalebox{.8}{$\scriptscriptstyle\int_{{0}}^{{T}}$}}\left<H(t)\bm{,}M(t)\right>~dt~:=~\mathchoice{\scalebox{.8}{$\displaystyle\int_{{0}}^{{T}}$}}{\scalebox{.8}{$\textstyle\int_{{0}}^{{T}}$}}{\scalebox{.8}{$\scriptstyle\int_{{0}}^{{T}}$}}{\scalebox{.8}{$\scriptscriptstyle\int_{{0}}^{{T}}$}}{\rm tr}\!\left(\rule{0.0pt}{10.00002pt}H(t)~M(t)\right)~dt,\hskip 20.00003ptM\in{{\sf L}}^{\!1}(0,{\scriptstyle T}),\hskip 20.00003ptH\in{{\sf L}}^{\!\!\infty}(0,{\scriptstyle T}),

(20)

The fact that

Definition 1

Definition 5

Let

be a (not necessarily convex)

Example 6
.

Any positive rank-one matrix can be written as

Let

Schur Complements

Given any Hermitian matrix
M
, it defines a quadratic form

. The matrix is positive
definite (semi-definite) if the quadratic form is positive (semi-definite) for all non-zero vectors. If the underlying
vector space is transformed by a linear transformation

{\sf q}(v)~=~\left<v\bm{,}Mv\right>~=~\left<Tw\bm{,}MTw\right>~=~\left<w\bm{,}T^{*}MTw\right>.

The transformation
M\mapsto T^{*}MT
is a
congruence transformation
on the matrix
M
.
Thus under a change of basis, matrix representations of bilinear forms undergo congruence
transformations
9
9

9

This is in contrast to matrix representations of linear transformations, which undergo
similarity transformations.
.
Consequently,
congruence transformations preserve the sign definiteness of a Hermitian matrix. They also preserve its “inertia”,
namely the triple of numbers indicating the number of negative, zero, and positive eigenvalues
respectively.

The well-known Schur complement can be understood as block-diagonalizatoin
by a congruence transformation on a
2\times 2
-block
partitioned matrix. Consider a Hermitian matrix

partitioned as

If

is invertible, then
M
can be block-diagonalized by the following congruence transformation

\begin{bmatrix}M_{11}&M_{\rm o}\\
M_{\rm o}^{*}&M_{22}\end{bmatrix}~=~\begin{bmatrix}I&M_{\rm o}M_{22}^{-1}\\
0&I\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}M_{11}-M_{\rm o}M_{22}^{-1}M_{\rm o}^{*}&0\\
0&M_{22}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}I&0\\
M_{22}^{-1}M_{\rm o}^{*}&I\end{bmatrix}.

(40)

We can therefore immediately conclude that if
M_{22}>0
, then
M\geq 0
iff the Schur complement

M_{11}-M_{\rm o}M_{22}^{-1}M_{\rm o}^{*}\geq 0
. This is particularly useful if for example
M_{22}
is fixed,
and the other blocks of
M
contain a matrix variable such as might occur in a Linear Matrix Inequality (LMI).
In this case, positivity of the Schur complement gives another matrix inequality (a “Riccati inequality”,
not an LMI) which can be more useful as we will
see in the next section.

3
DLMIs, DRIs and DREs

In this section we explore the relationships between Differential Linear Matrix Inequalities (DLMIs),
Differential Riccati Inequalities (DRIs) and Differential Riccati Equations (DREs). To begin with, we recall some fundamental facts about the simplest of DLMIs, namely
Differential Lyapunov Inequalities, and then apply those to compare solutions of Riccati differential equations
in terms of their coefficients’ differences.
The exposition here is intended to be self contained.

Differential Lyapunov Inequalities

Consider a differential Lyapunov inequality
of the form

\dot{X}+F^{*}X+XF~\leq~0,\hskip 20.00003ptt\in[0,{\scriptstyle T}],\hskip 20.00003pt\begin{array}[]{rcl}X(0)&=&0,\\

\displaystyle H(t)\geq 0,

Differential Riccati Inequalities and Riccati Comparisons

By adding a quadratic and a constant term to the differential Lyapunov inequality, we obtain a
Differential Riccati Inequality (DRI) which is of the form

\dot{\Lambda}+A^{*}\Lambda+\Lambda A-\Lambda~M~\Lambda+Q~\geq~0,\hskip 20.00003pt\Lambda({\scriptstyle T})=0.

(46)

For simplicity of exponsition, the

\geq

The DRI (

In a manner similar to what was done for Lyapunov inequalities, the DRI (

\displaystyle\dot{\Lambda}+A^{*}\Lambda+\Lambda A-\Lambda~M~\Lambda+Q~

Differential Linear Matrix Inequalities (DLMIs)

We are concerned with
Differential Linear Matrix Inequalities (DLMIs) of a very special form where a matrix-valued function
\Lambda(.)

is required to satisfy

\displaystyle{\cal M}({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\Lambda}})~:=

\displaystyle~{\bm{Q}}+{\mathcal{E}}^{{\bm{*}}}(\dot{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\Lambda}}})+\mathcal{A}^{{\bm{*}}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\Lambda}})

\displaystyle~:=

\displaystyle~\begin{bmatrix}Q&N\\
N^{*}&R\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}I\\
0\end{bmatrix}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\dot{\Lambda}}}\begin{bmatrix}I&0\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}A^{*}\\
B^{*}\end{bmatrix}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\Lambda}}\begin{bmatrix}I&0\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}I\\
0\end{bmatrix}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\Lambda}}\begin{bmatrix}A&B\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\begin{bmatrix}Q+\dot{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\Lambda}}}+A^{*}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\Lambda}}+{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\Lambda}}A&N+{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\Lambda}}B\\
(N+{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\Lambda}}B)^{*}&R\end{bmatrix}~\geq~0,\hskip 20.00003pt\hskip 20.00003ptt\in[0,{\scriptstyle T}].

(52)

Even when boundary conditions for
\Lambda
are specified, such DLMIs typically have non-unique
solutions. In other words, the inequality above does not provide sufficient constraints to uniquely
determined
{\dot{\Lambda}}(t)
given
\Lambda(t)
, thus the non-uniqueness of solutions.
However, as in the case of Riccati inequalities, we are able to find maximal solutions of the DLMI by
relating it to a DRI via the Schur complement.
For notational simplicity, the statements in this section are presented for the case
N=0
. The general case
is given in Theorem
14
of Appendix
A.5
.

Since the matrix
{\cal M}(\Lambda)
is in a
2\times 2
-block partition, it is natural
to use the Schur complement to characterize its positivity.
First note that
R\geq 0
is a necessary condition for (
52
). If we further assume
that
R>0
, then the Schur complement (
40
) gives the congruence

\begin{bmatrix}Q+\dot{\Lambda}+A^{*}\Lambda+\Lambda A&\Lambda B\\
B^{*}\Lambda&R\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}I&\Lambda BR^{-1}\\
0&I\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}Q+\dot{\Lambda}+A^{*}\Lambda+\Lambda A-\Lambda~BR^{\text{-}1}B^{*}~\Lambda&0\\
0&R\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}I&0\\
R^{-1}B^{*}\Lambda^{*}&I\end{bmatrix}

(53)

Thus the DLMI is equivalent to the DRI

Q+\dot{\Lambda}+A^{*}\Lambda+\Lambda A-\Lambda~BR^{\text{-}1}B^{*}~\Lambda~\geq~0.

Furthermore, if
we have final conditions on the DLMI, then
the equivalent DRI is of the form (
46
).
Therefore the previously stated facts about maximal solutions apply.
In addition, the left-hand-side of (
53
) implies that the lowest rank possible for the DLMI matrix
is bounded from below by the rank of
R
. The right-hand-side of (
53
) shows that this minimum
ranks is achieved
by setting the inequality in the DRI to equality (which renders the
(1,1)
as zero). This gives a special
symmetric factorization of the DLMI matrix at the maximal solution as stated next.

Lemma 10
.

(Maximal Solutions of the DLMI)
Consider the final-value DLMI and its associated DRI

\begin{aligned} {\cal M}(\Lambda)&:={\bm{Q}}+{\mathcal{E}}^{{\bm{*}}}({\dot{\Lambda}})+\mathcal{A}^{{\bm{*}}}(\Lambda)=\begin{bmatrix}Q+\dot{\Lambda}+A^{*}\Lambda+\Lambda A&\Lambda B\\
B^{*}\Lambda&R\end{bmatrix}\geq~0,\\
\mathcal{R}(\Lambda)&:={\dot{\Lambda}}+A^{*}\Lambda+\Lambda A-\Lambda BR^{\text{-}1}B^{*}\Lambda+Q~\geq~0,\end{aligned}\hskip 20.00003ptt\in[0,{\scriptstyle T}],\hskip 20.00003pt\Lambda({\scriptstyle T})=\Lambda_{\rm f},

where
R>0
.
A maximal solution of either inequality is given by the
Differential Riccati Equation (DRE)

\dot{{\bar{\Lambda}}}+A^{*}{\bar{\Lambda}}+{\bar{\Lambda}}A-{\bar{\Lambda}}BR^{\text{-}1}B^{*}{\bar{\Lambda}}+Q~=~0,\hskip 20.00003pt\hskip 20.00003pt{\bar{\Lambda}}(T)=\Lambda_{\rm f},

(54)

i.e. for any other
\Lambda
satisfying the DLMI or equivalently the DRI,

{\bar{\Lambda}}(t)~\geq~\Lambda(t),~t\in[0,{\scriptstyle T}].

Furthermore, at the maximal solution, the matrix
{\cal M}({\bar{\Lambda}})
has the
full-rank symmetric factorization

{\cal M}({\bar{\Lambda}})~=~\begin{bmatrix}{\bar{\Lambda}}BR^{\text{-}\frac{1}{2}}\\
R^{\frac{1}{2}}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}R^{\text{-}\frac{1}{2}}B^{*}{\bar{\Lambda}}&R^{\frac{1}{2}}\end{bmatrix}.

(55)

Proof.

Note that the equivalences of the DREs and the DLMIs follow from the
Schur complements for
R>0
. Thus showing the maximality property for the DRIs (already shown in
Lemma
9
)
proves the maximality property for the DLMIs.

For the factorization (
55
), note that if
{\bar{\Lambda}}
which satisfies the DRE is
used in both sides of (
53
)

\displaystyle\hskip-10.00002pt\begin{bmatrix}Q+\dot{{\bar{\Lambda}}}+A^{*}{\bar{\Lambda}}+{\bar{\Lambda}}A&{\bar{\Lambda}}B\\
B^{*}{\bar{\Lambda}}&R\end{bmatrix}~

\displaystyle=~\begin{bmatrix}{\bar{\Lambda}}BR^{-1}\\
I\end{bmatrix}R\begin{bmatrix}R^{-1}B^{*}{\bar{\Lambda}}^{*}&I\end{bmatrix}~=~\begin{bmatrix}{\bar{\Lambda}}BR^{\text{-}\frac{1}{2}}\\
R^{\frac{1}{2}}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}R^{\text{-}\frac{1}{2}}B^{*}{\bar{\Lambda}}B&R^{\frac{1}{2}}\end{bmatrix},

which is a full-rank factorization since
R
(and therefore
R^{\frac{1}{2}}
) is assumed non-singular. ∎

A few remarks are needed to emphasize the implications of the above result.

•

The symmetric factorization (
55
)
implies that the rank of
{\cal M}(.)

at the extremum
{\bar{\Lambda}}
is exactly equal the rank of
R
.
Since we have assumed
R
is non-singular,
then this is the lowest rank
{\cal M}(.)
can be.
We therefore conclude that the DRE solution
also
minimizes the rank
of the DLMI matrix
{\cal M}(\Lambda)
.
This fact will be important for understanding the
structure of optimal signals later on, as it will imply the specific state feedback form of optimal inputs.

•

Another important point to reiterate here is that one needed to convert a DLMI to a DRI in order to find extremal
solutions by substituting equality for inequality in the DRIs.
It is not possible to find maximal solutions of the DLMIs directly by changing their inequalities to equalities.
The DLMI with equality is infeasible (it would imply for example that
R=0
). The
DLMI and the DRI are equivalent by the Schur complement, but changing the inequalities to equalities
does not maintain
that equivalence. Thus to find maximal solutions to a DLMI, one must convert it to the equivalent DRI,
and then set the latter
to equality.

Symmetric Factorizations and the Lur’e Equations

As an aside, we point out that the
factorization (
55
) indicates another route that can be taken to arrive at the DRE starting from the
DLMI
without using the Schur complement
.
Observe that a matrix
M
is positive iff it has a symmetric factorization of the form
M=UU^{*}
for some matrix
U
.
Consider any such factorization of
{\cal M}(\Lambda)
which is partitioned conformably as

{\cal M}(\Lambda)~:=~\begin{bmatrix}Q+\dot{\Lambda}+A^{*}\Lambda+\Lambda A&(N+\Lambda B)\\
(N+\Lambda B)^{*}&R\end{bmatrix}~=~\begin{bmatrix}U_{1}\\
U_{2}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}U_{1}^{*}&U_{2}^{*}\end{bmatrix}~=:~UU^{*}.

(56)

This gives the following constraints on the submatrices

U_{1}
and
U_{2}

U_{1}U_{1}^{*}=Q+\dot{\Lambda}+A^{*}\Lambda+\Lambda A,\hskip 20.00003ptU_{1}U_{2}^{*}=N+\Lambda B,\hskip 20.00003ptU_{2}U_{2}^{*}=R.

(57)

These equations are the time-varying version of what are
sometimes referred to as Lur’e equations, which are normally stated as follows. Given

A,B,Q,N,R
, find matrices
\Lambda
,
U_{1}
and
U_{2}
such that the equations (
57
) hold.
In our current language, this amounts to finding
\Lambda
such that
{\cal M}(\Lambda)\geq 0
, as well as a
symmetric factorization of
{\cal M}(\Lambda)
. Such symmetric factorizations always exist for any positive
matrix
{\cal M}(\Lambda)
.

If
R>0
, then the factorization
R=U_{2}U_{2}^{*}
implies that
U_{2}
has full row rank. The “tightest” such
factorization would have
U_{2}
to be square. This corresponds to the factorization (
56
) being
full rank, which is also the minimal rank that the factor
U
can have.
Thus, if
R>0
, and we adopt a full-rank factorization in (
56
), then

U_{2}
is square and invertible, which gives
U_{1}=\Lambda BU_{2}^{-*}
, and in turn

U_{1}U_{1}^{*}~=~\Lambda BU_{2}^{-*}U_{2}^{-1}B^{*}\Lambda~=~\Lambda B\left(U_{2}U_{2}^{*}\right)^{-1}B^{*}\Lambda~=~\Lambda BR^{\text{-}1}B^{*}\Lambda.

Substituting this in the first equation in (
57
) gives the DRE

\Lambda BR^{-1}B^{*}\Lambda=Q+\dot{\Lambda}+A^{*}\Lambda+\Lambda A.

Thus we see that
the DRE arises out of a full-rank factorization of the DLMI matrix
(
56
),
or equivalently, a minimal-rank (of
U
) solution to the Lur’e equations.
The Schur complement is not used in this argument.

.

Consider a linear (possibly time-varying) system of the form

and a quadratic form defined on

u\end{bmatrix}dt~=:\int_{0}^{{\scriptscriptstyle T}}\begin{bmatrix}x\\
u\end{bmatrix}^{*}\!{\begin{bmatrix}&&\\
&{\bm{Q}}&\\
&&\end{bmatrix}}\begin{bmatrix}x\\
u\end{bmatrix}dt~=:\int_{0}^{{\scriptscriptstyle T}}{\sf q}(x,u)~dt,

with
Q\geq 0
and
R>0
.

The infimum of the quadratic form
\mathbf{q}
subject to the constraints (
58
) is

\inf_{x,u}~{\mbox{\large$\mathbf{q}$}}(x,u)~=~{\rm x}_{\rm i}^{*}~{\bar{\Lambda}}(0)~{\rm x}_{\rm i},

(59)

where
{\bar{\Lambda}}
is the maximal solution of the Differential Linear Matrix Inequality (DLMI) over
[0,{\scriptstyle T}]

{\cal M}(\Lambda):={\bm{Q}}+\begin{bmatrix}{\dot{\Lambda}}+A^{*}\Lambda+\Lambda A&\Lambda B\\
B^{*}\Lambda&0\end{bmatrix}~\geq~0,\hskip 20.00003pt\Lambda({\scriptstyle T})=0.

(60)

The maximal solution of this DLMI is given by the solution of
the Differential Riccati Equation (DRE)

Q+\dot{{\bar{\Lambda}}}+A^{*}{\bar{\Lambda}}+{\bar{\Lambda}}A-{\bar{\Lambda}}BR^{\text{-}1}B^{*}{\bar{\Lambda}}=0,\hskip 20.00003pt\hskip 20.00003pt{\bar{\Lambda}}({\scriptstyle T})=0.

The optimal control
{\bar{u}}
is given by the state feedback law

{\bar{u}}(t)=\left(R^{\text{-}1}(t)B^{*}(t){\bar{\Lambda}}(t)\rule{0.0pt}{10.00002pt}\right)~{\bar{x}}(t),\hskip 20.00003ptt\in[0,{\scriptstyle T}].

As outlined in the introduction, we will give the proof of this Theorem in several steps.
Step (1) is to reformulate
the minimization problem in terms of covariance matrices, which yields a linear-linear optimization problem with a
cone constraint.
Step (2) is to use conic duality to reformulate it as a maximization problem
with a
Differential Linear Matrix Inequality (DLMI) constraint.
Step (3) is to use the maximal
solution of the DLMI (which is given by a DRE) to solve the dual problem. Finally, step (4) uses the alignment (complementary
slackness) conditions to give the optimal primal solution in the form of a state feedback and show that the duality gap is zero.

1.

The first step is to
define the joint (deterministic) covariance of
x
and
u

\Sigma(t)~:=~\begin{bmatrix}x(t)\\
u(t)\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}x^{*}(t)&u^{*}(t)\end{bmatrix},

(61)

which is always of rank one.
The differential equation for
\Sigma
is (
8
), which we recap for clarity and rewrite it in
the abstract integral form (
30
) developed in Section

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}\big(\Sigma\big):=\begin{bmatrix}I&0\end{bmatrix}{\Sigma}\begin{bmatrix}I\\
0\end{bmatrix},\hskip 20.00003pt

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}\big(\Sigma\big):=\begin{bmatrix}A&B\end{bmatrix}{\Sigma}\begin{bmatrix}I\\
0\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}I&0\end{bmatrix}{\Sigma}\begin{bmatrix}A^{*}\\
B^{*}\end{bmatrix},

\displaystyle\left.\begin{array}[]{rcl}{\mathcal{E}}\big(\dot{\Sigma}\big)&=&\mathcal{A}\big(\Sigma\big),\\
{\mathcal{E}}\big(\Sigma(0)\big)&=&{\rm x}_{\rm i}{\rm x}_{\rm i}^{*}=:{\sf X}_{\rm i}\end{array}\right\}\hskip 20.00003pt

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\hskip 20.00003pt\big({\mathcal{E}}-{\sf{\bm{I}}}_{\sf f}\mathcal{A}\big)(\Sigma)~=~{\sf X}_{\rm i}.

Now the LQR problem can be written in the covariance representation as

\boxed{\begin{aligned} \inf_{\Sigma\in{{\sf L}}^{\!1}(0,T)}&\int_{0}^{T}{\rm tr}\!\left({\bm{Q}}~\Sigma\right)~dt\hskip 100.00015pt&\framebox{\sf Primal: LQR}\\
\big({\mathcal{E}}-{\sf{\bm{I}}}_{\sf f}\mathcal{A}\big)(\Sigma)&={\sf X}_{\rm i}\\
\Sigma\geq 0,~{\rm\bf rk}\!\left(\Sigma\right)&=1.\end{aligned}~}

(64)

2.

This problem is in the form to which the linear-conic duality Theorem
8
can be applied.
The primal and dual problems are then

{\sf Primal:}\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}{\begin{array}[]{rcl}\displaystyle\inf_{\Sigma\in{{\sf L}}^{\!1}(0,{\scriptscriptstyle T})}&&\left<{\bm{Q}}\bm{,}\Sigma\right>\\
\big({\mathcal{E}}-{\sf{\bm{I}}}_{\sf f}\mathcal{A}\big)(\Sigma)&=&{\sf X}_{\rm i}\rule{0.0pt}{15.00002pt}\\
\Sigma\geq 0,~{\rm\bf rk}\!\left(\Sigma\right)&=&1\end{array}}&\geq&{\begin{array}[]{rcl}\displaystyle\sup_{Y\in{{\sf L}}^{\!\!\infty}(0,{\scriptscriptstyle T})}&&\left<Y\bm{,}{\sf X}_{\rm i}\right>\\
{\bm{Q}}-\big({\mathcal{E}}^{{\bm{*}}}-\mathcal{A}^{{\bm{*}}}{\sf{\bm{I}}}_{\sf\bm{b}}\big)(Y)&\geq&0\rule{0.0pt}{15.00002pt}\end{array}}\end{array}\right\}{\sf:Dual},

(65)

where we recall the calculation of the adjoint of the linear operator in the equality constraint

\big({\mathcal{E}}-{\sf{\bm{I}}}_{\sf f}\mathcal{A}\big)^{{\bm{*}}}~=~{\mathcal{E}}^{{\bm{*}}}-\mathcal{A}^{{\bm{*}}}{\sf{\bm{I}}}_{\sf f}^{{\bm{*}}}~=~{\mathcal{E}}^{{\bm{*}}}-\mathcal{A}^{{\bm{*}}}{\sf{\bm{I}}}_{\sf\bm{b}}.

(66)

Note that the rank condition does not have any effect on the dual problem.
This is of course because the cone dual to positive rank-one matrices
\mathbb{P}_{n}^{1}
is the convex cone

\mathbb{P}_{n}
of positive matrices, i.e.
the dual problem is always
convex even if the primal problem is not. Furthermore, since the cost objective is linear, the argument in (
35
)
implies that the extrema are achieved at the “vertices” of the convex cone.

Examining the dual objective
\left<Y\bm{,}{\sf X}_{\rm i}\right>
, we see that since
{\sf X}_{\rm i}
is a constant function over

[0,{\scriptstyle T}]
, the objective can be simplified by introducing the backwards integral of

Y
as follows

\left<Y\bm{,}{\sf X}_{\rm i}\right>~=~\mathchoice{\scalebox{.8}{$\displaystyle\int_{{0}}^{{T}}$}}{\scalebox{.8}{$\textstyle\int_{{0}}^{{T}}$}}{\scalebox{.8}{$\scriptstyle\int_{{0}}^{{T}}$}}{\scalebox{.8}{$\scriptscriptstyle\int_{{0}}^{{T}}$}}{\rm tr}\!\left(Y(t)~{\sf X}_{\rm i}\rule{0.0pt}{10.00002pt}\right)~dt~=~{\rm tr}\!\left(\mathchoice{\scalebox{.8}{$\displaystyle\int_{{0}}^{{T}}$}}{\scalebox{.8}{$\textstyle\int_{{0}}^{{T}}$}}{\scalebox{.8}{$\scriptstyle\int_{{0}}^{{T}}$}}{\scalebox{.8}{$\scriptscriptstyle\int_{{0}}^{{T}}$}}Y(t)dt~{\sf X}_{\rm i}\right)~=:~{\rm tr}\!\left(\Lambda(0)~{\rm x}_{\rm i}{\rm x}_{\rm i}^{*}\rule{0.0pt}{10.00002pt}\right)~=~{\rm x}_{\rm i}^{*}\Lambda(0)~\!{\rm x}_{\rm i},

where we defined
\Lambda
as the backwards integral of
Y

\Lambda:={\sf{\bm{I}}}_{\sf\bm{b}}Y\hskip 20.00003pt\Leftrightarrow\hskip 20.00003pt\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl}\Lambda(t)&:=&\int_{t}^{T}Y(\tau)~d\tau\\
-\dot{\Lambda}(t)&:=&Y(t)\end{array}\right\}\hskip 20.00003pt\Leftrightarrow\hskip 20.00003pt\Lambda(0)=\int_{0}^{T}Y(t)dt,\hskip 10.00002pt\Lambda({\scriptstyle T})=0.

(67)

The dual constraint in terms of
\Lambda={\sf{\bm{I}}}_{\sf\bm{b}}Y
becomes

{\bm{Q}}-\big({\mathcal{E}}^{{\bm{*}}}-\mathcal{A}^{{\bm{*}}}{\sf{\bm{I}}}_{\sf\bm{b}}\big)(Y)~=~{\bm{Q}}-{\mathcal{E}}^{{\bm{*}}}(Y)+\mathcal{A}^{{\bm{*}}}({\sf{\bm{I}}}_{\sf\bm{b}}Y)~=~{\bm{Q}}+{\mathcal{E}}^{{\bm{*}}}({\dot{\Lambda}})+\mathcal{A}^{{\bm{*}}}(\Lambda),\hskip 20.00003pt\Lambda({\scriptstyle T})=0.

This is actually a DLMI for
\Lambda

\displaystyle{\bm{Q}}+{\mathcal{E}}^{{\bm{*}}}\big({\dot{\Lambda}}\big)+\mathcal{A}^{{\bm{*}}}\big(\Lambda\big)~

\displaystyle=~\begin{bmatrix}Q&0\\
0&R\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}I\\
0\end{bmatrix}\dot{\Lambda}\begin{bmatrix}I&0\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}A^{*}\\
B^{*}\end{bmatrix}\Lambda\begin{bmatrix}I&0\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}I\\
0\end{bmatrix}\Lambda\begin{bmatrix}A&B\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=~\begin{bmatrix}Q+\dot{\Lambda}+A^{*}\Lambda+\Lambda A&\Lambda B\\
B^{*}\Lambda&R\end{bmatrix}~=:~{\cal M}(\Lambda).

(68)

The abstract dual problem (
65
) can now be written concretely in terms of
\Lambda
as

\boxed{~\begin{aligned} \sup_{\Lambda\in{{\sf L}}^{\!\!\infty}(0,T)}~{\rm x}_{\rm i}^{*}\Lambda(0)~\!{\rm x}_{\rm i}&\hskip 70.0001pt&\framebox{\sf Dual: LQR}\\
{\cal M}(\Lambda):=\begin{bmatrix}Q+\dot{\Lambda}+A^{*}\Lambda+\Lambda A&\Lambda B\\
B^{*}\Lambda&R\end{bmatrix}~&\geq~0,\hskip 20.00003pt&t\in[0,{\scriptstyle T}],~~\Lambda({\scriptstyle T})=0,\end{aligned}~}

(69)

Note that while the objective
{\rm x}_{\rm i}^{*}\Lambda(0)~\!{\rm x}_{\rm i}
is quadratic in
{\rm x}_{\rm i}
, it is
linear

in the optimization variable
\Lambda
.

3.

The dual problem is a maximization problem with a final condition on a DLMI constraint. We can therefore
use the maximal solution
{\bar{\Lambda}}
of the DLMI (Lemma
10
) to obtain the function

{\bar{\Lambda}}(.)
that achieves the supremum.

{\bar{\Lambda}}
satisfies the DRE

-\dot{{\bar{\Lambda}}}~=~A^{*}{\bar{\Lambda}}+{\bar{\Lambda}}A-\Lambda BR^{-1}B^{*}\Lambda+Q,\hskip 20.00003pt{\bar{\Lambda}}({\scriptstyle T})=0.

The maximality
property implies that for any other
\Lambda
satisfying the DLMI constraint

{\bar{\Lambda}}(t)~\geq~\Lambda(t)\hskip 20.00003pt\Rightarrow\hskip 20.00003pt{\bar{\Lambda}}(0)~\geq~\Lambda(0)\hskip 20.00003pt\Rightarrow\hskip 20.00003pt{\rm x}_{\rm i}^{*}{\bar{\Lambda}}(0)~\!{\rm x}_{\rm i}~\geq~{\rm x}_{\rm i}^{*}\Lambda(0)~\!{\rm x}_{\rm i}.

Therefor
{\bar{\Lambda}}
solves the dual problem (
69
).

4.

It remains to check the alignment condition. The optimal
{\bar{\Sigma}}
, if it exists must satisfy the alignment
condition (
37
), which in this case is

0~=~\left<{\bm{Q}}-\big({\mathcal{E}}^{{\bm{*}}}-\mathcal{A}^{{\bm{*}}}{\sf{\bm{I}}}_{\sf\bm{b}}\big)(\bar{Y})\bm{,}\rule{0.0pt}{10.00002pt}{\bar{\Sigma}}\right>~=~\left<{\cal M}({\bar{\Lambda}})\bm{,}\rule{0.0pt}{10.00002pt}{\bar{\Sigma}}\right>

Furthermore,
{\bar{\Sigma}}
must be rank one, which means

0=\left<{\cal M}({\bar{\Lambda}})\rule{0.0pt}{10.00002pt}\bm{,}{\bar{\Sigma}}\right>=\left<\begin{bmatrix}{\bar{\Lambda}}BR^{\text{-}\frac{1}{2}}\\
R^{\frac{1}{2}}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}R^{\text{-}\frac{1}{2}}B^{*}{\bar{\Lambda}}&R^{\frac{1}{2}}\end{bmatrix}\bm{,}\begin{bmatrix}{\bar{x}}\\
{\bar{u}}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}{\bar{x}}^{*}&{\bar{u}}^{*}\end{bmatrix}\right>,

(70)

where the last expression comes from the full-rank factorization property (
55
) of the DRE.
Now observe that (
70
) is a statement that two positive matrices are orthogonal. Symmetric
factorizations of the two orthogonal matrices are given, and therefore Lemma
4
implies
that if such a
{\bar{\Sigma}}
existed, then

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}R^{\text{-}\frac{1}{2}}B^{*}{\bar{\Lambda}}&R^{\frac{1}{2}}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}{\bar{x}}\\
{\bar{u}}\end{bmatrix}~=~0\hskip 20.00003pt

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\hskip 20.00003ptR^{\text{-}\frac{1}{2}}B^{*}{\bar{\Lambda}}~{\bar{x}}~+~R^{\frac{1}{2}}{\bar{u}}~=~0

(71)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\hskip 20.00003pt{\bar{u}}(t)~=~\left(-R^{-1}B^{*}{\bar{\Lambda}}\right)~{\bar{x}}(t).

(72)

The argument then goes as follows. If we choose
{\bar{u}}
according to (
72
), then (
71
) holds,
and in turn the joint covariance
{\bar{\Sigma}}
of
({\bar{x}},{\bar{u}})
is of rank one and
satisfies the alignment condition (
70
). This proves that the duality gap is zero.
The alignment condition therefore forces the optimal control signal
{\bar{u}}
to be
in the form of a state feedback on the optimal state trajectory
{\bar{x}}
.
This is the well-known classical solution to the LQR problem.

Finally, the optimal cost is given from
\Lambda(0)
as

{\mbox{\large$\mathbf{q}$}}_{\rm opt}({\rm x}_{\rm i})~=~{\rm x}^{*}_{\rm i}\Lambda(0){\rm x}_{\rm i}.

This is the so-called “value function” of this optimal control problem,
which is a quadratic form in the initial conditions of the LQR problem.
Note that while the optimal feedback gain
-R^{\text{-}1}B^{*}{\bar{\Lambda}}
is independent of the initial condition,
the optimal trajectory
{\bar{x}}
, control
{\bar{u}}
and cost
{\rm x}_{\rm i}^{*}\Lambda(0){\rm x}_{\rm i}
are functions of the initial condition.

5
The Stochastic LQR Problem

The stochastic version of the LQR is not to be confused with the Linear Quadratic Gaussian (LQG) problem,
in which measurements are partial and noisy. The Stochastic LQR (SLQR) problem assumes
additive stochastic disturbances in the state equation that play the role of perturbing the equilibrium state,
just like the non-zero initial condition in the deterministic
LQR problem perturbs the equilibrium.
As we will see, the solution to this problem is exactly the same as the deterministic one, which is the LQR
state feedback.

The problem can be stated as
follows. Consider a (possibly time-varying) system with a random initial condition and
driven by zero-mean white noise uncorrelated with past histories of
x
and
u

\dot{x}=Ax+Bu+w,\hskip 20.00003pt\begin{array}[]{rcl}{\mathbb{E}}\left[w(t)w^{*}(\tau)\rule{0.0pt}{9.10004pt}\right]&=&\delta(t-\tau)~{\sf W},\\
{\mathbb{E}}\left[w(t)x^{*}(\tau)\rule{0.0pt}{9.10004pt}\right]&=&0,~t\geq\tau,\\
{\mathbb{E}}\left[w(t)u^{*}(\tau)\rule{0.0pt}{9.10004pt}\right]&=&0,~t\geq\tau,\end{array}\hskip 20.00003pt\begin{array}[]{rcl}x(0)&=&{\rm x}_{\rm i},\\
{\mathbb{E}}\left[{\rm x}_{\rm i}{\rm x}_{\rm i}^{*}\rule{0.0pt}{9.10004pt}\right]&=&{\sf X}_{\rm i},\\
{\mathbb{E}}\left[{\rm x}_{\rm i}w^{*}(t)\right]&=&0,\end{array}

(73)

where
{\sf W}
and
{\sf X}_{\rm i}
are the covariance matrices of
w
and
x(0)
respectively.
The goal is to find the control input
u
that minimizes the
following quadratic performance objective

{\mbox{\large$\mathbf{q}$}}(x,u)~:=~\mathchoice{\scalebox{.8}{$\displaystyle\int_{{0}}^{{T}}$}}{\scalebox{.8}{$\textstyle\int_{{0}}^{{T}}$}}{\scalebox{.8}{$\scriptstyle\int_{{0}}^{{T}}$}}{\scalebox{.8}{$\scriptscriptstyle\int_{{0}}^{{T}}$}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\rule{0.0pt}{10.00002pt}x^{*}Qx+2x^{*}Nu+u^{*}Ru\right]dt.

(74)

The expectation is taken over the joint
probability distribution of
w
and
x(0)
, and therefore
\mathbf{q}
is an “average” over all
realizations of the process
w
and random variable
x(0)
.
That is why
w
and
x(0)
are not included as arguments of
\mathbf{q}
. After taking expectation,

\mathbf{q}
is a function of only
x
and
u
.

As before,
\mathbf{q}
can be rewritten as a linear functional on the joint covariance of
(x,w)
, which is now an actual covariance
matrix of stochastic processes

\mathbf{q}

\displaystyle:=\int_{0}^{T}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\begin{bmatrix}x^{*}&u^{*}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}Q&N\\
N^{*}&R\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}x\\
u\end{bmatrix}\right]dt=\int_{0}^{T}{\mathbb{E}}\left[{\rm tr}\!\left(\begin{bmatrix}Q&N\\
N^{*}&R\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}x\\
u\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}x^{*}&u^{*}\end{bmatrix}\right)\right]dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}{\rm tr}\!\left(\begin{bmatrix}Q&N\\
N^{*}&R\end{bmatrix}~{\mathbb{E}}\left[\begin{bmatrix}x\\
u\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}x^{*}&u^{*}\end{bmatrix}\right]\right)dt~=:\int_{0}^{T}{\rm tr}\!\left({\bm{Q}}\,\Sigma\rule{0.0pt}{10.00002pt}\right)dt~=\left<{\bm{Q}}\bm{,}\Sigma\rule{0.0pt}{10.00002pt}\right>,

(75)

where

\Sigma~:=~{\mathbb{E}}\left[\begin{bmatrix}x\\
u\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}x^{*}&u^{*}\end{bmatrix}\right]~=~\begin{bmatrix}{\mathbb{E}}\left[xx^{*}\right]&{\mathbb{E}}\left[xu^{*}\right]\\
{\mathbb{E}}\left[ux^{*}\right]&{\mathbb{E}}\left[uu^{*}\right]\end{bmatrix}~=:~\begin{bmatrix}\Sigma_{xx}&\Sigma_{xu}\\
\Sigma_{ux}&\Sigma_{uu}\end{bmatrix}.

Keep in mind that
\Sigma
is now a deterministic function which is to be chosen as a the solution of a (deterministic)
optimization problem.

The differential equation for
\Sigma_{xx}
is obtained from a standard
calculation

\dot{\Sigma}_{xx}~=~A~\Sigma_{xx}~+~B~\Sigma_{ux}~+~\Sigma_{xx}~A^{*}~+~\Sigma_{xu}~B^{*}~+~{\sf W}.

(76)

Using our earlier notation for the matrix operators
{\mathcal{E}}
and
\mathcal{A}
, this differential equation can be written
as follows together with its integral form

{\mathcal{E}}\big(\dot{\Sigma}\big)=\mathcal{A}\big(\Sigma\big)+{\sf W},\hskip 20.00003pt{\mathcal{E}}\big(\Sigma(0)\big)={\sf X}_{\rm i},\hskip 20.00003pt\Leftrightarrow\hskip 20.00003pt\big({\mathcal{E}}~-~{\sf{\bm{I}}}_{\sf f}\mathcal{A}\big)(\Sigma)~=~{\sf{\bm{I}}}_{\sf f}{\sf W}~+~{\sf X}_{\rm i}.

(77)

We can now state the primal stochastic LQR problem abstractly as

\boxed{~\begin{aligned} \inf_{\Sigma}&~\int_{0}^{T}{\rm tr}\!\left({\bm{Q}}~\Sigma\right)~dt\hskip 100.00015pt&\framebox{\sf Primal: Stochastic LQR}\\
\big({\mathcal{E}}-{\sf{\bm{I}}}_{\sf f}\mathcal{A}\big)(\Sigma)&={\sf{\bm{I}}}_{\sf f}{\sf W}+{\sf X}_{\rm i}\\
\Sigma&\geq 0\end{aligned}~}

(78)

Let’s compare this problem statement with the deterministic LQR version (
64
). If

{\sf W}=0
, then the two statements are identical except for the rank-1 constraint on
\Sigma
. Since this
constraint does not effect the dual problem, the duals of deterministic and stochastic LQR (with
{\sf W}=0
)
should be identical. The only difference would be in the alignment condition, which we will explore shortly.

The linear operator in the equality constraint is exactly the same as in the deterministic LQR problem, and its
adjoint has already been calculated in (
66
). Linear-conic duality thus gives the following
statement

{\sf Primal:}\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}{\begin{array}[]{rcl}\displaystyle\inf_{\Sigma}&&\left<{\bm{Q}}\bm{,}\Sigma\right>\\
\big({\mathcal{E}}-\mathcal{A}{\sf{\bm{I}}}_{\sf f}\big)(\Sigma)&=&{\sf{\bm{I}}}_{\sf f}{\sf W}+{\sf X}_{\rm i}\rule{0.0pt}{15.00002pt}\\
\Sigma&\geq&0\end{array}}&\geq&{\begin{array}[]{rcl}\displaystyle\sup_{Y}&&\left<Y\bm{,}{\sf{\bm{I}}}_{\sf f}{\sf W}+{\sf X}_{\rm i}\right>\\
{\bm{Q}}-\big({\mathcal{E}}^{{\bm{*}}}-\mathcal{A}^{{\bm{*}}}{\sf{\bm{I}}}_{\sf\bm{b}}\big)(Y)&\geq&0\rule{0.0pt}{15.00002pt}\end{array}}\end{array}\right\}{\sf:Dual},

(79)

The only difference between the dual here and the dual in the deterministic problem is the cost objective,

\Lambda

defined in (
67

\displaystyle\left<Y\bm{,}{\sf{\bm{I}}}_{\sf f}{\sf W}+{\sf X}_{\rm i}\rule{0.0pt}{9.10004pt}\right>~=~\left<{\sf{\bm{I}}}_{\sf\bm{b}}Y\bm{,}{\sf W}\right>+\left<Y\bm{,}{\sf X}_{\rm i}\right>~=~\left<\Lambda\bm{,}{\sf W}\right>+{\rm tr}\!\left(\mathchoice{\scalebox{.8}{$\displaystyle\int_{{0}}^{{T}}$}}{\scalebox{.8}{$\textstyle\int_{{0}}^{{T}}$}}{\scalebox{.8}{$\scriptstyle\int_{{0}}^{{T}}$}}{\scalebox{.8}{$\scriptscriptstyle\int_{{0}}^{{T}}$}}Y(t)~{\sf X}_{\rm i}dt\right)~

\displaystyle=~\left<\Lambda\bm{,}{\sf W}\right>+{\rm tr}\!\left(\mathchoice{\scalebox{.8}{$\displaystyle\int_{{0}}^{{T}}$}}{\scalebox{.8}{$\textstyle\int_{{0}}^{{T}}$}}{\scalebox{.8}{$\scriptstyle\int_{{0}}^{{T}}$}}{\scalebox{.8}{$\scriptscriptstyle\int_{{0}}^{{T}}$}}Y(t)dt~{\sf X}_{\rm i}\right)

\displaystyle=~\left<\Lambda\bm{,}{\sf W}\right>+\left<\Lambda(0)\bm{,}{\sf X}_{\rm i}\right>.

Note that the first functional
\left<\Lambda\bm{,}{\sf W}\right>
is between functions on
[0,{\scriptstyle T}]
, while the
second
\left<\Lambda(0)\bm{,}{\sf X}_{\rm i}\right>
is between matrices.

Recalling the expression (
68
) for the dual inequality constraint in terms of
\Lambda
,
the dual problem can now be stated as

\boxed{~\begin{aligned} \sup_{\Lambda}~~{\rm tr}\!\left(\rule{0.0pt}{10.00002pt}\Lambda(0){\sf X}_{\rm i}\right)+\mathchoice{\scalebox{.8}{$\displaystyle\int_{{0}}^{{T}}$}}{\scalebox{.8}{$\textstyle\int_{{0}}^{{T}}$}}{\scalebox{.8}{$\scriptstyle\int_{{0}}^{{T}}$}}{\scalebox{.8}{$\scriptscriptstyle\int_{{0}}^{{T}}$}}{\rm tr}\!\left(\rule{0.0pt}{10.00002pt}\Lambda{\sf W}\right)dt&&\framebox{\sf Dual: Stochastic LQR}\\
\begin{bmatrix}Q+\dot{\Lambda}+A^{*}\Lambda+\Lambda A&\Lambda B\\
B^{*}\Lambda&R\end{bmatrix}~&\geq~0,\hskip 20.00003pt\hskip 20.00003pt&t\in[0,{\scriptstyle T}],~~\Lambda(T)=0.\end{aligned}~}

(80)

Although the objective of this problem has two terms, each of those terms is maximized by the maximal
solution to the DLMI. Since the DLMI has a final condition, we know this maximal solution is the solution
of the DRE

A^{*}{\bar{\Lambda}}+{\bar{\Lambda}}A-{\bar{\Lambda}}BR^{-1}B^{*}{\bar{\Lambda}}+Q~=~0,\hskip 20.00003pt\hskip 20.00003pt{\bar{\Lambda}}(T)=0.

The alignment for this problem requires a little more care and interpretation than the deterministic LQR problem.
Let
{\bar{\Sigma}}
be a candidate joint covariance for the optimal
\Sigma
. In the stochastic setting, the rank one
constraint is not imposed, and
{\bar{\Sigma}}
may have higher rank. To investigate this, let

{\bar{\Sigma}}(t)~:=~{\mathbb{E}}\left[\begin{bmatrix}{\bar{x}}(t)\\
{\bar{u}}(t)\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}{\bar{x}}(t)^{*}&{\bar{u}}(t)^{*}\end{bmatrix}\right]~=~U(t)~U^{*}\!(t)~=~\begin{bmatrix}U_{1}(t)\\
U_{2}(t)\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}U_{1}^{*}(t)&U_{2}^{*}(t)\end{bmatrix},

is partitioned conformably with

to be full rank for the arguments that follow.
As described in Appendix
A.3
, this

{\bar{x}}
and
{\bar{u}}
as a linear transformation on
a random process
v
with uncorrelated components (i.e.
{\mathbb{E}}\left[v(t)v^{*}(t)\right]=I
)

\begin{bmatrix}{\bar{x}}(t)\\
{\bar{u}}(t)\end{bmatrix}~=~\begin{bmatrix}U_{1}(t)\\
U_{2}(t)\end{bmatrix}v(t)

(82)

Now recall the alignment condition (
37
), which in this case reads

0=\left<{\bar{\Sigma}}\bm{,}{\bm{Q}}+{\mathcal{E}}^{*}(\dot{{\bar{\Lambda}}})+\mathcal{A}^{*}({\bar{\Lambda}})\right>=\left<\begin{bmatrix}U_{1}\\
U_{2}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}U_{1}^{*}&U_{2}^{*}\end{bmatrix}\bm{,}\begin{bmatrix}{\bar{\Lambda}}BR^{\text{-}\frac{1}{2}}\\

(83)

where we again used the full-rank factorization property (

\displaystyle 0~=~\begin{bmatrix}R^{-\frac{1}{2}}B^{*}{\bar{\Lambda}}&R^{\frac{1}{2}}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}U_{1}\\
U_{2}\end{bmatrix}\hskip 20.00003pt

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\hskip 20.00003ptR^{-\frac{1}{2}}B^{*}{\bar{\Lambda}}~U_{1}~+~R^{\frac{1}{2}}~U_{2}~=~0

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\hskip 20.00003ptU_{2}~=~-R^{-1}B^{*}{\bar{\Lambda}}~~U_{1}.

This last equation, together with (

) gives a relation between the optimal
{\bar{x}}
and
{\bar{u}}

{\bar{u}}(t)~=~U_{2}(t)~v(t)~=~-R^{-1}B^{*}{\bar{\Lambda}}(t)~~U_{1}(t)~v(t)~=~\left(-R^{-1}B^{*}{\bar{\Lambda}}(t)\right)~{\bar{x}}(t).

(84)

Thus the optimal control
{\bar{u}}
is the same static state feedback as the deterministic LQR problem.
The difference in this case is that the optimal covariance
{\bar{\Sigma}}
is not necessarily of rank one, but possibly
higher. Equation (

) (together with the rank bound of Lemma

implies that the rank of
{\bar{\Sigma}}
can be at most

(the state dimension),
and may be lower depending on the rank of
{\sf W}
(this last statement is not a consequence of (

)).

Note again that the optimal feedback control law (

) does not depend on either the initial
condition
{\sf X}_{i}
or the disturbance
{\sf W}
covariance matrices. The optimal cost value however depends on those

{\mbox{\large$\mathbf{q}$}}_{\rm opt}~=~{\rm tr}\!\left(\rule{0.0pt}{10.00002pt}{\bar{\Lambda}}(0){\sf X}_{\rm i}\right)+\mathchoice{\scalebox{.8}{$\displaystyle\int_{{0}}^{{T}}$}}{\scalebox{.8}{$\textstyle\int_{{0}}^{{T}}$}}{\scalebox{.8}{$\scriptstyle\int_{{0}}^{{T}}$}}{\scalebox{.8}{$\scriptscriptstyle\int_{{0}}^{{T}}$}}{\rm tr}\!\left(\rule{0.0pt}{10.00002pt}{\bar{\Lambda}}(t)~{\sf W}(t)\right)dt

General Integral Quadratic Constraints (IQCs)

We now consider the case of general Integral Quadratic Constraint (IQCs).
In general, these problems concern a state space model with an input, and a
functional jointly quadratic in input and state. The problem is to characterize the
set of values of this quadratic form
evaluated over the set of all possible trajectories generated by all possible inputs.
Due to the linearity of the system’s equations, this set of values can only be of three types
[\gamma,\infty)
,

(-\infty,-\gamma]
or
(-\infty,\infty)
for some number
\gamma\geq 0
.
The problem is then reduced to computing the infimum of the quadratic form subject to the system’s equations,
then this infimum will be either
-\infty
or a finite number
\pm\gamma
. A geometric illustration of such LQP problems in general is given in Figure
3
.
For example, in
the
{{\sf H}^{\infty}}
-norm and passivity checking problems, the initial condition is typically assumed to be zero,
and the problems are thus reduced to checking whether the value set
of a quadratic form is
[0,\infty)
. This property holds for the system if the respective infimum is zero,
and does not hold if it is
-\infty
.

Figure 3
:
Depiction of a quadratic form on input-state pairs
(v,x)
. The blue line represents the linear-affine space
of all input-state pairs satisfying the dynamics
\dot{x}=Ax+Bv
,
x(0)=\rm{\rm x}_{\rm i}
. When the input
v=0
, the
state trajectory is the initial condition response
\bar{x}(t)=e^{At}\rm{\rm x}_{\rm i}
, depicted here as the intersection of
the linear-affine space with the “
x
-axis”. The quadratic form
{\mbox{\large$\mathbf{q}$}}(x,v)
over all signal pairs (not necessarily
constrained by the dynamics) is depicted as the grey surface.
\mathbf{q}
can have mixed signature as depicted
here. The LQ Problem is to determine the infimum of the values of the quadratic form over the linear-affine constraint
set (those values depicted here as the dashed red curve). Whether the infimum is finite or
-\infty
depends on both
the quadratic form and the system dynamics. In the case where the initial condition is zero (
{\bar{x}}=0
),
the constraint set is a
subspace, and the constrained infimum can only be either
0
or
-\infty
.

Theorem 12
.

(85)

and a quadratic form defined on

{\mbox{\large$\mathbf{q}$}}(x,w)~:=~\int_{0}^{{\scriptscriptstyle T}}\begin{bmatrix}x\\

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt+\Lambda_{1}M\Lambda_{1}-\Lambda_{1}M\Lambda_{1}+\Lambda_{1}M\Lambda_{2}-\Lambda_{1}M\Lambda_{2}+\Lambda_{2}M\Lambda_{1}-\Lambda_{2}M\Lambda_{1}

\displaystyle=~\big(A+M(\Lambda_{1}+\Lambda_{2})/2\big)^{*}~(\Lambda_{1}-\Lambda_{2})~+~(\Lambda_{1}-\Lambda_{2})~\big(A+M(\Lambda_{1}+\Lambda_{2})/2)\big)

(98)

A.5
DLMIs with initial or final conditions

Theorem 14

(Extremal Solutions)
.

Consider the linear matrix
{\cal M}
and the associated Riccati
\mathcal{R}
operators

\displaystyle{\cal M}(\Lambda)~

\displaystyle:=~{\bm{Q}}+{\mathcal{E}}^{{\bm{*}}}({\dot{\Lambda}})+\mathcal{A}^{{\bm{*}}}(\Lambda)~=\begin{bmatrix}Q+\dot{\Lambda}+A^{*}\Lambda+\Lambda A&N+\Lambda B\\
(N+\Lambda B)^{*}&R\end{bmatrix},

(102)

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\Lambda)~

\displaystyle:=~{\dot{\Lambda}}+A^{*}\Lambda+\Lambda A-\left(N+\Lambda B\right)R^{\text{-}1}\left(N+\Lambda B\right)^{*}+Q,

(103)

where
R>0
or
R<0
.
Consider also initial- and final-value Differential Linear Matrix Inequalities (DLMIs) and their equivalent
Differential Riccati Inequalities (DRIs) over
[t_{\rm i},t_{\rm f}]

\displaystyle\left.\begin{array}[]{rclcrcl}{\cal M}(\Lambda)&\geq&0&\hskip 20.00003pt\Leftrightarrow&\hskip 20.00003pt\mathcal{R}(\Lambda)&\geq&0\\
\mbox{or}~{\cal M}(\Lambda)&\leq&0&\hskip 20.00003pt\Leftrightarrow&\hskip 20.00003pt\mathcal{R}(\Lambda)&\leq&0\end{array}\right\}

\displaystyle\Lambda(t_{\rm i})

\displaystyle=\Lambda_{\rm i},\hskip 20.00003pt{\rm(DLMI_{i}/DRI_{i})}

(104)

\displaystyle\left.\begin{array}[]{rclcrcl}{\cal M}(\Lambda)&\geq&0&\hskip 20.00003pt\Leftrightarrow&\hskip 20.00003pt\mathcal{R}(\Lambda)&\geq&0\\
\mbox{or}~{\cal M}(\Lambda)&\leq&0&\hskip 20.00003pt\Leftrightarrow&\hskip 20.00003pt\mathcal{R}(\Lambda)&\leq&0\end{array}\right\}

\displaystyle\Lambda(t_{\rm f})

\displaystyle=\Lambda_{\rm f}.\hskip 20.00003pt{\rm(DLMI_{f}/DRI_{f})}

Minimal and maximal solutions of the initial- and final-value inequalities respectively are given by the
following Differential Riccati Equations (DREs)

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}({\underline{\Lambda}})~

\displaystyle=~0,

\displaystyle{\underline{\Lambda}}(t_{\rm i})=\Lambda_{\rm i},\hskip 20.00003pt{\rm(DRE_{i}):}~\mbox{\rm minimal-initial}

(105)

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}({\bar{\Lambda}})~

\displaystyle=~0,

\displaystyle{\bar{\Lambda}}(t_{\rm f})=\Lambda_{\rm f},\hskip 20.00003pt{\rm(DRE_{f}):}~\mbox{\rm maximal-final}

(106)

i.e. for any
\Lambda
satisfying

\rm DLMI_{i}/DRI_{i}
(resp.
\rm DLMI_{f}/DRI_{f}
)

{\underline{\Lambda}}(t)~\leq~\Lambda(t)\hskip 20.00003pt\left(\rule{0.0pt}{10.00002pt}\mbox{resp.}~\Lambda(t)~\leq~{\bar{\Lambda}}(t)\right),\hskip 20.00003pt\hskip 20.00003ptt\in[t_{\rm i},t_{\rm f}].

Furthermore, at the extremal solutions, the matrix
{\cal M}
has the
full-rank symmetric factorization

{\cal M}({\bar{\Lambda}})~=~\begin{bmatrix}(N+{\bar{\Lambda}}B)R^{\text{-}\frac{1}{2}}\\
R^{\frac{1}{2}}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}R^{\text{-}\frac{1}{2}}(N+{\bar{\Lambda}}B)^{*}&R^{\frac{1}{2}}\end{bmatrix},

(107)

and similarly for
{\cal M}({\underline{\Lambda}})
.

Proof.

We consider the case
{\cal M}(\Lambda)\geq 0
with
\Lambda(t_{\rm f})=\Lambda_{\rm f}
. The other three cases are
argued similarly. The Riccati operator (
103
) can be written in the more standard form

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\Lambda)~

\displaystyle:=~{\dot{\Lambda}}+A^{*}\Lambda+\Lambda A-\left(N+\Lambda B\right)R^{\text{-}1}\left(N+\Lambda B\right)^{*}+Q

\displaystyle=~{\dot{\Lambda}}+\big(A-BR^{\text{-}1}N^{*}\big)^{*}~\Lambda+\Lambda~\big(A-BR^{\text{-}1}N^{*}\big)-\Lambda~BR^{\text{-}1}B^{*}~\Lambda+\big(Q-NR^{\text{-}1}N^{*}\big)

\displaystyle=:~{\dot{\Lambda}}+\hat{A}^{*}\Lambda+\Lambda\hat{A}-\Lambda M\Lambda+\hat{Q}.

(108)

The
Riccati comparison result (
101
) now implies that the solution
{\bar{\Lambda}}
of
\mathcal{R}({\bar{\Lambda}})=0

is maximal over all
solutions of the
\rm DRE_{f}
, i.e. over all solutions of the
\rm DLMI_{f}

For the factorization (
55
), note that the
\rm DRE_{f}
for
{\bar{\Lambda}}
implies

Q+\dot{{\bar{\Lambda}}}+A^{*}{\bar{\Lambda}}+{\bar{\Lambda}}A~=~(N+{\bar{\Lambda}}B)R^{\text{-}1}(N+{\bar{\Lambda}}B)^{*}.

\displaystyle\hskip-10.00002pt\begin{bmatrix}Q+\dot{{\bar{\Lambda}}}+A^{*}{\bar{\Lambda}}+{\bar{\Lambda}}A&(N+{\bar{\Lambda}}B)\\
(N+{\bar{\Lambda}}B)^{*}&R\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}(N+{\bar{\Lambda}}B)R^{\text{-}1}(N+{\bar{\Lambda}}B)^{*}&N+{\bar{\Lambda}}B\\
(N+{\bar{\Lambda}}B)^{*}&R\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}(N+{\bar{\Lambda}}B)R^{\text{-}\frac{1}{2}}\\
