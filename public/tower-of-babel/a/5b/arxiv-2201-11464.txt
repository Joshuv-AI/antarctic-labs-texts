Foundations for Entailment Checking in Quantitative Separation Logic (extended version) Thanks: This work is partially supported by the ERC AdG project 787914 FRAPPANT.
Kevin Batz, Ira Fesefeldt, Marvin Jansen, Joost-Pieter Katoen, Florian Keßler, Christoph Matheja, Thomas Noll

Abstract
Quantitative separation logic ( QSL ) is an extension of separation logic ( SL ) for the verification of probabilistic pointer programs. In QSL , formulae evaluate to real numbers instead of truth values, e.g., the probability of memory-safe termination in a given symbolic heap. As with SL , one of the key problems when reasoning with QSL is entailment : does a formula f f entail another formula g g ? We give a generic reduction from entailment checking in QSL to entailment checking in SL . This allows to leverage the large body of SL research for the automated verification of probabilistic pointer programs. We analyze the complexity of our approach and demonstrate its applicability. In particular, we obtain the first decidability results for the verification of such programs by applying our reduction to a quantitative extension of the well-known symbolic-heap fragment of separation logic.

Separation logic

Probabilistic programs

Quantitative separation logic

We make the following technical contributions:

We present a generic construction that reduces the entailment problem for

We use our construction to derive the

\triangle

Finally, given

, we say that

\text{for all}~(s,h)\in\textsf{{States}}\colon\quad\left\llbracket{f}\right\rrbracket(s,h)~{}\leq{}~\left\llbracket{g}\right\rrbracket(s,h)~.

Quantitative entailments

Our problem statement now reads as follows: Reduce entailment checking in
\textsf{{QSL}}\left[\mathfrak{A}\right]
to checking finitely many entailments in

Recall from

4.1

We reduce entailment checking in

\displaystyle\mathfrak{A}~=~ \{\>\mathsf{true},~\textsf{{{emp}}},~x=y,~x\neq y,~x\mapsto y~\mid~x,y\in\mathsf{Vars}\>\}~.

Now, consider the following entailment
u_{1}\models u_{2}
as a running example:

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0.5,0.5,0.5}u_{1}~{}={}~}0.4\cdot(\underbrace{\left[{x\mapsto y}\right]\mathbin{{\star}}\left[{y\mapsto z}\right]}_{=g_{1}})+0.6\cdot\underbrace{\left[{x\mapsto y}\right]}_{=g_{2}}~{}\models{}~0.6\cdot(\left[{x\mapsto y}\right]\mathbin{{\star}}\left[{\mathsf{true}}\right]){\color[rgb]{0.5,0.5,0.5}~{}={}~u_{2}}~.

Intuitively speaking,
u_{1}
expresses that with probability
0.4
the heap consists of two cells where
x
points to
y
and separately
y
points to
z
, and that with probability
0.6
the heap consists of a single cell where
x
points to
y
. Formula
u_{2}
expresses that with probability
0.6
the heap contains a cell where
x
points to
y
. How can we reduce the problem of checking whether
u_{1}\models u_{2}
holds to checking finitely many entailments in
\textsf{{SL}}\left[\mathfrak{A}\right]
?
We rely on two key observations:

Observation 1.
For every
f\in\textsf{{QSL}}\left[\mathfrak{A}\right]
, the set

\textsf{{Eval}}\left(f\right)~{}={}~\left\{\left\llbracket{f}\right\rrbracket{(s,h)}~\mid~(s,h)\in\textsf{{States}}\right\}~{}\subset{}~\mathbb{P}

is
finite
. Moreover, there is an effectively constructible
finite
and
sound overapproximation

\textsf{{Val}}\left[f\right]
of
\textsf{{Eval}}\left(f\right)
, i.e.,
\textsf{{Eval}}\left(f\right)\subseteq\textsf{{Val}}\left[f\right]
.

Example 3

Consider the expectation
u_{1}
from our running example: We have
\textsf{{Eval}}\left(u_{1}\right)=\{0,0.4,0.6\}
. We construct a finite overapproximation of
\textsf{{Eval}}\left(u_{1}\right)
as follows: First, we observe that both subformulae
g_{1}
and
g_{2}
evaluate to a value in
\{0,1\}
, i.e,
\textsf{{Val}}\left[g_{1}\right]=\textsf{{Val}}\left[g_{2}\right]=\{0,1\}
. From
\textsf{{Val}}\left[g_{1}\right]
and
\textsf{{Val}}\left[g_{2}\right]
, we obtain a finite overapproximation
\textsf{{Val}}\left[u_{1}\right]
of
\textsf{{Eval}}\left(u_{1}\right)
given by

\textsf{{Val}}\left[u_{1}\right]~{}={}~\left\{0.4\cdot\alpha+0.6\cdot\beta~\mid\alpha\in\textsf{{Val}}\left[g_{1}\right],~\beta\in\textsf{{Val}}\left[g_{2}\right]\right\}~{}={}~\{0,0.4,0.6,1\}~.

Notice that
\textsf{{Val}}\left[u_{1}\right]
is a
proper
superset of
\textsf{{Eval}}\left(u_{1}\right)
since
1\not\in\textsf{{Eval}}\left(u_{1}\right)
.
\triangle

We consider the construction of
\textsf{{Val}}\left[f\right]
for arbitrary
f\in\textsf{{QSL}}\left[\mathfrak{A}\right]
in
Section

4.2
.

Observation 2.
Given
f\in\textsf{{QSL}}\left[\mathfrak{A}\right]
and a probability
\alpha\in\mathbb{P}
, there is an effectively constructible
\textsf{{SL}}\left[\mathfrak{A}\right]
formula, which we denote by
\lceil\alpha\preceq f\rceil
, such that
(s,h)
is a model of
\lceil\alpha\preceq f\rceil
if and only if
f
evaluates at least to
\alpha
on state
(s,h)
, i.e.,

\underbrace{(s,h)~{}\models{}~\lceil\alpha\preceq f\rceil}_{\text{in}~\textsf{{SL}}\left[\mathfrak{A}\right]}\qquad\text{iff}\qquad\underbrace{\alpha~{}\leq{}~\left\llbracket{f}\right\rrbracket(s,h)}_{\text{in}~\textsf{{QSL}}\left[\mathfrak{A}\right]}~.

We can thus lower bound
\textsf{{QSL}}\left[\mathfrak{A}\right]
formulae in terms of
\textsf{{SL}}\left[\mathfrak{A}\right]
formulae.

Example 4

Continuing our running example, we construct
\lceil 0.5\preceq u_{1}\rceil
, i.e., an
\textsf{{SL}}\left[\mathfrak{A}\right]
formula evaluating to
\mathsf{true}
on state
(s,h)
if and only if
u_{1}
evaluates at least to
0.5
. We start by considering the subformulae of
u_{1}
. Since both
g_{1}
and
g_{2}
embed
\textsf{{SL}}\left[\mathfrak{A}\right]
predicates, we have for every
\alpha\in\mathbb{P}

\displaystyle\lceil\alpha\preceq g_{1}\rceil~{}={}~\mathsf{true}~\text{if $\alpha=0$ else}~x\mapsto y\mathbin{{\star}}y\mapsto z

and

\displaystyle\lceil\alpha\preceq g_{2}\rceil~{}={}~\mathsf{true}~\text{if $\alpha=0$ else}~x\mapsto y~.

The intuition is as follows:
\alpha=0
lower bounds every probability. Conversely, if
\alpha>0
then
\alpha
lower bounds
g_{1}
(resp.
g_{2}
) on state
(s,h)
if and only if
(s,h)
satisfies the predicate
g_{1}
(resp.
g_{2}
).
Now, when does
u_{1}
evaluate at least to
0.5
? Given
\textsf{{Val}}\left[g_{1}\right]
and
\textsf{{Val}}\left[g_{2}\right]
and the fact that the valuation of
u_{1}
is a convex combination of the valuations of
g_{1}
and
g_{2}
, there are (at most) two cases: Either
both

g_{1}

and

g_{2}
evaluate to (at least)
1
, or
g_{2}
(but not necessarily
g_{1}
) evaluates to (at least)
1
. Given
\lceil 1\preceq g_{1}\rceil
and
\lceil 1\preceq g_{2}\rceil
, the aforementioned informal disjunction translates to a formal
disjunction in
\textsf{{SL}}\left[\mathfrak{A}\right]
:

\displaystyle\lceil 0.5\preceq u_{1}\rceil~{}={}~

\displaystyle\big(\lceil 1\preceq g_{1}\rceil\wedge\lceil 1\preceq g_{2}\rceil\big)\vee\lceil 1\preceq g_{2}\rceil

\displaystyle~{}={}~

\displaystyle\big((x\mapsto y\mathbin{{\star}}y\mapsto z)\wedge x\mapsto y\big)\vee x\mapsto y~.

Notice that—as it is the case for
\textsf{{Val}}\left[u_{1}\right]
—we construct
\lceil 0.5\preceq u_{1}\rceil

syntactically
. In particular, we disregard that the disjunct
(x\mapsto y\mathbin{{\star}}y\mapsto z)\wedge x\mapsto y
is unsatisfiable and therefore equivalent to
\mathsf{false}
.
\triangle

We provide the construction of
\lceil\alpha\preceq f\rceil
for
arbitrary

\textsf{{QSL}}\left[\mathfrak{A}\right]
formulae
f
—including quantitative quantifiers and the magic wand—in
Section

4.3
.

Finally, Observations
1
and
2
together yield our reduction from
f\models g
to finitely many entailments in
\textsf{{SL}}\left[\mathfrak{A}\right]
. Intuitively speaking, we formalize that

whenever
f
evaluates at least to
\alpha
, then
g
too evaluates at least to
\alpha

equivalently in terms of finitely many
\textsf{{SL}}\left[\mathfrak{A}\right]
entailments. Put more formally, since
\textsf{{Val}}\left[f\right]
is finite, we have

\displaystyle f\models g

iff

\displaystyle\text{for all $(s,h)$}\colon\left\llbracket{f}\right\rrbracket(s,h)\leq\left\llbracket{g}\right\rrbracket(s,h)

(by definition)

iff

\displaystyle\text{for all $(s,h)$ and all $\alpha\in\textsf{{Val}}\left[f\right]$}\colon\alpha\leq\left\llbracket{f}\right\rrbracket(s,h)~\text{implies}~\alpha\leq\left\llbracket{g}\right\rrbracket(s,h)

(by Observation
1
)

iff

\displaystyle\text{for all $(s,h)$ and all $\alpha\in\textsf{{Val}}\left[f\right]$}\colon(s,h)\models\lceil\alpha\preceq f\rceil~\text{implies}~(s,h)\models\lceil\alpha\preceq g\rceil

(by Observation
2
)

iff

\displaystyle\text{for all $\alpha\in\textsf{{Val}}\left[f\right]$}\colon\lceil\alpha\preceq f\rceil\models\lceil\alpha\preceq g\rceil~.

(by definition)

Example 5

Reconsider our running example. Since
|\textsf{{Val}}\left[u_{1}\right]|=4
, the
\textsf{{QSL}}\left[\mathfrak{A}\right]
entailment
u_{1}\models u_{2}
is equivalent to the four entailments

\lceil\alpha\preceq u_{1}\rceil\models\lceil\alpha\preceq u_{2}\rceil\quad\text{for $\alpha\in\{0,0.4,0.6,1\}$}

in
\textsf{{SL}}\left[\mathfrak{A}\right]
, each of which actually holds.
\triangle

Theorem 4.2

For every
f\in\textsf{{QSL}}\left[\mathfrak{A}\right]
and all
\alpha\in\mathbb{P}

\textsf{{SL}}\left[\mathfrak{A}\right]

\lceil\alpha\preceq f\rceil
such that for all
(s,h)\in\textsf{{States}}
, we have

(s,h)~{}\models{}~\lceil\alpha\preceq f\rceil\qquad\text{iff}\qquad\alpha~{}\leq{}~\left\llbracket{f}\right\rrbracket(s,h)~.

Proof

By induction on
f
.
See

0.C
for details.

Theorem 4.3

Entailment checking in
\textsf{{QSL}}\left[\mathfrak{A}\right]
reduces to entailment checking in
\textsf{{SL}}\left[\mathfrak{A}\right]
, i.e,
for all
f,g\in\textsf{{QSL}}\left[\mathfrak{A}\right]
, we have

f~{}\models{}~g\qquad\text{iff}\qquad\text{for all $\alpha\in\textsf{{Val}}\left[f\right]$}\colon~\lceil\alpha\preceq f\rceil~{}\models{}~\lceil\alpha\preceq g\rceil~.

Proof

Follows from Theorems
4.1
and
4.2
and the reasoning
at the end of
Section

4.1
.

Remark 1
(Avoiding
\mathsf{true}
in
\textsf{{SL}}\left[\mathfrak{A}\right]
entailments)

Formulae of the form
\lceil\alpha\preceq f\rceil\in\textsf{{SL}}\left[\mathfrak{A}\right]
may introduce the atom
\mathsf{true}
, which is not admitted by some decidable separation logic fragments,
such as
[
26
]
. Fortunately, we can avoid
\mathsf{true}
in
\lceil\alpha\preceq f\rceil
formulae.
\mathsf{true}
is only required in formulae of the form
\lceil 0\preceq f\rceil
, which arise in two situations when applying
Theorem

4.3

\lceil 0\preceq f\rceil\models\lceil 0\preceq g\rceil

. In the latter case, if we have
\alpha\neq 0
in

\lceil\alpha\preceq f\rceil~{}={}~\bigvee_{\beta\in\textsf{{Val}}\left[g\right],\gamma\in\textsf{{Val}}\left[u\right],p\cdot\beta+(1-p)\cdot\gamma\geq\alpha}\quad\lceil\beta\preceq g\rceil\wedge\lceil\gamma\preceq u\rceil~,

then either
\beta\neq 0
or
\gamma\neq 0
holds for every disjunct. Hence, subformulae of the form
\lceil 0\preceq g\rceil
or
\lceil 0\preceq u\rceil
can be omitted, as well.
\triangle

We now analyze the complexity of our approach. Recall that

Weakest preexpectations.

Weakest precondition reasoning was established in a classical setting by Dijkstra

Logics for probabilistic pointer programs.

Although many algorithms rely on randomized dynamic data structures, formal reasoning about programs that are both probabilistic and heap manipulating has received scarce attention.
A notable exception is the work by Tassarotti and Harper
[

However, no other decision procedures targeting entailments for

Discussion and Conclusion

We studied entailment checking in
QSL
by means of a reduction to entailment checking in
SL
. We analyzed the complexity of our approach and demonstrated its applicability by means of several examples. In particular, our reduction yields the first decidability result for probabilistic pointer program verification.

Our primary goal was to investigate the entailment problem for QSL to pave the way for automated verification of probabilistic pointer programs.

Theorem

6.1
provides a generic result that enables building upon the large body of work dealing with classical SL entailments to obtain both theoretical and practical insights.
Theoretically,
Theorem

6.1
gives sufficient criteria to derive QSL fragments with a decidable entailment problem from a classical SL fragment.
We derived a QSL fragment such that reasoning about a simple probabilistic heap-manipulating language becomes decidable.
More practically,
Theorem

6.1
allows reusing existing (possibly incomplete) SL solvers to solve the entailments derived by our construction—an empirical evaluation of how well existing solvers can deal with these entailments is an interesting direction for future work.

We believe that our fine-grained complexity analysis demonstrates that our approach can be practically feasible: the exponential blow-up in
Theorem

5.2
stems from the number of probabilistic constructs in the given QSL formulae. We expect the number of such constructs to be small for many randomized algorithms.
We remark that existing approaches on checking quantitative entailments between heap-independent expectations encounter similar exponential blow-ups (cf.,
[
35
,
6
]
).
There is thus some
evidence that such exponential blow-ups do not prohibit one from automatically verifying non-trivial properties. We are not aware of work on checking quantitative entailments between expectations that avoids such exponential blow-ups.

Future work includes considering richer classes of
QSL
and applications of entailment checking such as
k
-induction
[
6
]
. Another interesting direction is the applicability of our reduction to other approaches that aim for local reasoning about the resources employed by probabilistic programs, such as
[
50
,
3
,
5
]
.

Theorem
2.1

The semantics of
\textsf{{QSL}}\left[\mathfrak{A}\right]
formulae is well-defined, i.e., for all
f\in\textsf{{QSL}}\left[\mathfrak{A}\right]
, we have

\left\llbracket{f}\right\rrbracket\in\mathbb{E}_{\leq 1}.

Proof

By induction on
f
. For the base case
f=\left[{\psi}\right]
, we have
\left\llbracket{\left[{\psi}\right]}\right\rrbracket(s,h)\in\{0,1\}
. For all other cases, it is straightforward to prove that
\left\llbracket{f}\right\rrbracket(s,h)\in[0,1]
, since
[0,1]
is closed under all operations used in
Table

3
if they are defined. It is only left to proof that
\left\llbracket{\reflectbox{{{S}}}\hskip 0.86108ptx\colon g}\right\rrbracket
,
\left\llbracket{\raisebox{0.6pt}{\rotatebox{-30.0}{{{\reflectbox{J}}}}\hskip-0.43057pt}x\colon g}\right\rrbracket
and
\left\llbracket{g\mathbin{{\star}}u}\right\rrbracket
are well-defined. For that the
\max
and
\min
need to be defined on the given sets, i.e. that the sets

\displaystyle\big\{\left\llbracket{g}\right\rrbracket(s\left[{x}\texttt{:=}{v}\right],h)~\mid~v\in\mathsf{Vals}\big\}

\displaystyle\left\{\left\llbracket{g}\right\rrbracket(s,h_{1})\cdot\left\llbracket{u}\right\rrbracket(s,h_{2})~\mid~h=h_{1}\mathbin{{\star}}h_{2}\right\}

are non-empty and finite. Since
\mathsf{Vals}
is non-empty and
h=h\mathbin{{\star}}h_{\emptyset}
where
h_{\emptyset}
is the heap with
\textsf{{dom}}\left({h_{\emptyset}}\right)=\emptyset
, both sets are non-empty. Lastly, from
Theorem

4.1
it follows directly, that both sets are also finite.

Lemma 4

For all formulae
f\in\textsf{{QSL}}\left[\mathfrak{A}\right]
we have
0,1\in\textsf{{Val}}\left[f\right]
.

Proof

By induction on
f
.

For the base case
f=\left[{\psi}\right]
,
we have
0,1\in\{0,1\}=\textsf{{Val}}\left[f\right]
.

For all other composite cases we assume for some fixed, but arbitrary
g,u\in\textsf{{QSL}}\left[\mathfrak{A}\right]
that
0,1\in\textsf{{Val}}\left[g\right]
and
0,1\in\textsf{{Val}}\left[u\right]
.

For the induction step
f=\left[{\pi}\right]\cdot g+\left[{\neg\pi}\right]\cdot u
,
we have by the induction hypothesis
0,1\in\textsf{{Val}}\left[g\right]\subseteq\textsf{{Val}}\left[f\right]
.

For the induction step
f=p\cdot g+(1-p)\cdot u
,
we have by the induction hypothesis
0,1\in\textsf{{Val}}\left[g\right]
and
0,1\in\textsf{{Val}}\left[u\right]
. Thus we also have
0,p\in p\cdot\textsf{{Val}}\left[g\right]
and
0,(1-p)\in(1-p)\cdot\textsf{{Val}}\left[u\right]
, thus with
0=0+0
and
1=p+(1-p)
also
0,1\in\textsf{{Val}}\left[f\right]
.

For the induction step
f=g\cdot u
,
we have by the induction hypothesis
0,1\in\textsf{{Val}}\left[g\right]
and
0,1\in\textsf{{Val}}\left[u\right]
. Thus we also have with
0=0\cdot 0
and
1=1\cdot 1
that
0,1\in\textsf{{Val}}\left[f\right]
.

For the induction step
f=1-g
,
we have by the induction hypothesis
0,1\in\textsf{{Val}}\left[g\right]
, thus with
0=1-1
and
1=1-0
we also have
0,1\in\textsf{{Val}}\left[f\right]
.

For the induction step
f=g\max u
,
we have by the induction hypothesis
0,1\in\textsf{{Val}}\left[g\right]
and
0,1\in\textsf{{Val}}\left[u\right]
thus with
0=\max(0,0)
and
1=\max(1,1)
we also have
0,1\in\textsf{{Val}}\left[f\right]
.

The induction step
f=g\min u
is analogous to the previous case.

For the induction steps
f=\raisebox{0.6pt}{\rotatebox{-30.0}{{{\reflectbox{J}}}}\hskip-0.43057pt}x\colon g
,
we have by the induction hypothesis
0,1\in\textsf{{Val}}\left[g\right]=\textsf{{Val}}\left[f\right]
.

The induction step
f=\reflectbox{{{S}}}\hskip 0.86108ptx\colon g
is analogous to the previous case.

The induction step
f=g\mathbin{{\star}}u
is analogous to the case
g\cdot u
.

f=\left[{\psi}\right]\mathbin{\text{\raisebox{-0.43057pt}{$\boldsymbol{{-}\hskip-2.44444pt{-}}$}}\hskip-4.30554pt\text{\raisebox{0.55974pt}{\rotatebox{-17.0}{$\star$}}}}g
,

.

. Notice that
\vartheta_{2}
does not contain
\mathbin{\text{\raisebox{-0.43057pt}{$\boldsymbol{{-}\hskip-2.44444pt{-}}$}}\hskip-4.30554pt\text{\raisebox{0.55974pt}{\rotatebox{-17.0}{$\star$}}}}
. We may without loss of generality assume that
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
do not occur in
\vartheta_{2}
and that
y_{1},\ldots,y_{m}
do not occur in
\vartheta_{1}
. Hence, we get

\displaystyle\varphi~{}\models{}~\psi

iff

\displaystyle\varphi\wedge\neg\psi~\text{is unsatisfiable}

iff

\displaystyle\exists x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\colon\vartheta_{1}\wedge\neg(\exists y_{1},\ldots,y_{m}\colon\vartheta_{2})~\text{is unsatisfiable}

(by above reasoning)

iff

\displaystyle(\exists x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\colon\vartheta_{1})\wedge(\forall y_{1},\ldots,y_{m}\colon\neg\vartheta_{2})~\text{is unsatisfiable}

iff

\displaystyle\exists x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\colon\forall y_{1},\ldots,y_{m}\colon\vartheta_{1}\wedge\neg\vartheta_{2}~\text{is unsatisfiable}~,

(standard prenexing)

since
\vartheta_{1}\wedge\neg\vartheta_{2}
is quantifier free and since none of
y_{1},\ldots,y_{m}
occur in an instance of
\mathbin{\text{\raisebox{-0.43057pt}{$\boldsymbol{{-}\hskip-2.44444pt{-}}$}}\hskip-4.30554pt\text{\raisebox{0.55974pt}{\rotatebox{-17.0}{$\star$}}}}
occurring in
\vartheta_{1}\wedge\neg\vartheta_{2}
, the claim follows.
