Quantum Zeno Dynamics from General Quantum Operations
Daniel Burgarth, Paolo Facchi, Hiromichi Nakazato, Saverio Pascazio, Kazuya Yuasa

Abstract
We consider the evolution of an arbitrary quantum dynamical semigroup of a finite-dimensional quantum system under frequent kicks, where each kick is a generic quantum operation. We develop a generalization of the Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff formula allowing to reformulate such pulsed dynamics as a continuous one. This reveals an adiabatic evolution. We obtain a general type of quantum Zeno dynamics, which unifies all known manifestations in the literature as well as describing new types.



1
Introduction

Physics is a science that is often based on approximations. From high-energy physics to the quantum world, from relativity to thermodynamics, approximations not only help us to solve equations of motion, but also to reduce the model complexity and focus on important effects. Among the largest success stories of such approximations are the effective generators of dynamics (Hamiltonians, Lindbladians), which can be derived in quantum mechanics and condensed-matter physics. The key element in the techniques employed for their derivation is the separation of different time scales or energy scales.

Recently, in quantum technology, a more active approach to condensed-matter physics and quantum mechanics has been taken. Generators of dynamics are
reversely engineered
by tuning system parameters and device design. This allows the creation of effective generators useful for many information-theoretic tasks, such as adiabatic quantum computing
[
1
]
, reservoir engineering
[
2
]
, quantum gates
[
3
]
, to name a few.

A key player for such approximations has been the adiabatic theorem
[
4
,
5
]
. It exploits a clear separation of slow and fast time scales and has fascinated generations of physicists due to its simplicity, its beauty, and its intriguing geometric interpretations.
In its original formulation, the adiabatic theorem deals with generators of dynamics. On the other hand, in quantum technology, we often deal with
discrete
dynamics such as fixed gates and quantum maps. It is not always straightforward, and sometimes seemingly impossible, to translate between continuous and discrete descriptions. This difficulty can be seen more clearly in the case of non-Markovian quantum channels: these are physical operations [completely positive and trace-preserving (CPTP) maps
[
6
]
] for which there are no physical (e.g. Lindbladian) generators [a non-Markovian quantum channel cannot be realized by the time-ordered integral of an infinitesimal CPTP generator of the Gorini-Kossakowski-Lindblad-Sudarshan (GKLS) form
[
7
,
8
]
]. Such maps typically arise from unitary dynamics on larger (but finite-dimensional) spaces, such as two-level fluctuators forming the environment of solid-state qubits. This makes them rather common in experiments
[
9
]
.

The key question we pose in this article is if discrete dynamics can give rise to a limit evolution? We provide a positive answer to this question by providing a general mapping from pulsed to continuous dynamics. Such connections had been noted before only for a specific unitary case. For the generic situation, we need to develop a more powerful framework. This is because in the nonunitary case one has to take into account both nondiagonalizability and noninvertibility of the maps.

We focus on finite-dimensional systems and provide a route to connecting strong-coupling limits and frequent pulsed dynamics using three key ingredients. The first is a slight generalization of the Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff (BCH) theorem
[
10
]
(Lemma
1
in Sec.
5
). We employ operator logarithms to describe discrete maps by (potentially unphysical) generators and unify the product of exponentials as a new exponential up to the first order in a sense explained later. This only works for invertible maps (there is no logarithm of a noninvertible map since the logarithm of the vanishing eigenvalue does not exist), so the second ingredient is a delicate error estimate allowing us to take logarithms of only the invertible part of a generic map: the remaining part decays anyway (Lemma
3
in Sec.
5
). We believe that these key lemmas might find applications in other areas of quantum technology. The third and ultimate ingredient is a strong-coupling theorem developed by the present authors recently in Ref.
[
11
]
(see also Ref.
[
12
]
), which can be applied to unphysical generators (Theorem 1 of Ref.
[
11
]
). Even though the logarithm of a physical operation is not of the GKLS form in general, one can deal with it by the adiabatic theorem proved in Ref.
[
11
]
. Through the adiabatic theorem, low-energy components are eliminated and the physicality of the generator is restored. Our approach therefore works for arbitrary quantum maps without unnecessary structural assumptions.

Our generalization goes in two main directions: 1) the unitary dynamics
\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}tH}
is generalized to an arbitrary quantum semigroup
\mathrm{e}^{t\mathcal{L}}
; 2) the projective measurement
P
is generalized to an arbitrary quantum operation
\mathcal{E}
. Moreover, we also generalize to 3) kicked dynamics of cycles of quantum operations
\{\mathcal{E}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{E}_{m}\}
.
This unifies many applications, such as the quantum Zeno effect (QZE) and dynamical decoupling,
and provides a deeper relationship through adiabaticity.
The main result of the present work is Theorem
1
in Sec.
4
.
It reveals that in quantum technology one has more freedom than previously thought to achieve effective generators (see the next section for a brief summary of the previous results).
In addition, we derive explicit bounds on matrix functions and the BCH formula for nondiagonalizable matrices (Lemma
9
and Proposition
2
in Appendix
B
), which may be of interest independently.

\mathcal{H}_{Z}=[H_{Z},{}\,\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-3.0)\circle*{2.0}\end{picture}\ \,\,{}]

Some Preliminaries on Quantum Operations

Let us recall that every linear operator
A
on a finite-dimensional space can be expressed (essentially) uniquely in terms of its Jordan normal form (
canonical form
or
spectral representation
)
[
44
]
:

A=\sum_{k}(\lambda_{k}P_{k}+N_{k}),

(3.1)

where
\{\lambda_{k}\}
, the
spectrum
of
A
, is the set of distinct eigenvalues of
A
(
\lambda_{k}\neq\lambda_{\ell}
for
k\neq\ell
),
\{P_{k}\}
, the
spectral projections
of
A
, are the corresponding eigenprojections, satisfying

P_{k}P_{\ell}=\delta_{k\ell}P_{k},\qquad\sum_{k}P_{k}=I,

(3.2)

for all
k
and
\ell
, and
\{N_{k}\}
are the corresponding
nilpotents
of
A
, satisfying for all
k
and
\ell

P_{k}N_{\ell}=N_{\ell}P_{k}=\delta_{k\ell}N_{k},\qquad N_{k}^{n_{k}}=0,

(3.3)

for some integer
1\leq n_{k}\leq\mathop{\mathrm{rank}}\nolimits P_{k}
.

Notice that the spectral projections, which determine a partition of the space through the
resolution of identity
(
3.2
), are not Hermitian in general,
P_{k}\neq P_{k}^{\dagger}
. An eigenvalue
\lambda_{k}
of
A
is called
semisimple
or
diagonalizable
if the corresponding nilpotent
N_{k}
is zero (equivalently,
n_{k}=1
). The operator
A
is diagonalizable if and only if all its eigenvalues are semisimple.

The main actors in our investigation are the
quantum operations

[
6
]
, that is, maps
\mathcal{E}
that are completely positive (CP) and trace-nonincreasing,
\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits[\mathcal{E}{(\rho)}]\leq\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits\rho
. We recall that a map
\mathcal{E}
on a
d
-dimensional quantum system is a quantum operation iff it has an operator-sum (Kraus) representation of the form

\mathcal{E}(X)=\sum_{j=1}^{m}K_{j}XK_{j}^{\dagger}\quad\text{with}\quad\sum_{j=1}^{m}K_{j}^{\dagger}K_{j}\leq I,

(3.4)

where
m\leq d^{2}
. When
\sum_{j}K_{j}^{\dagger}K_{j}=I
, the map is trace-preserving (TP) and one gets a completely positive trace-preserving (CPTP) map, also known as a
quantum channel
.

In the following, it will be convenient to endow the space of operators on a
d
-dimensional Hilbert space with the Hilbert-Schmidt inner product
\langle A|B\rangle_{2}=\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits(A^{\dagger}B)
, which makes the space of operators a
d^{2}
-dimensional Hilbert space
\mathcal{T}_{2}
.
We get that the adjoint
\mathcal{E}^{\dagger}
[with respect to the Hilbert-Schmidt inner product, defined through
\langle A|\mathcal{E}(B)\rangle_{2}=\langle\mathcal{E}^{\dagger}(A)|B\rangle_{2}
for all
A,B\in\mathcal{T}_{2}
] of the quantum operation
\mathcal{E}
in Eq. (
3.4
) has the operator sum

\mathcal{E}^{\dagger}(X)=\sum_{j}K_{j}^{\dagger}XK_{j},

(3.5)

and thus is subunital
\mathcal{E}^{\dagger}(I)\leq I
.
It is unital iff
\mathcal{E}
is CPTP.
Moreover, given a Hermitian operator
H=H^{\dagger}
, then the corresponding superoperator
\mathcal{H}=[H,{}\,\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-3.0)\circle*{2.0}\end{picture}\ \,\,{}]
is also Hermitian (with respect to the Hilbert-Schmidt inner product),

\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}^{\dagger}
, namely
\langle A|\mathcal{H}(B)\rangle_{2}=\langle\mathcal{H}(A)|B\rangle_{2}
for all
A,B\in\mathcal{T}_{2}
. As a consequence, the unitary group
t\mapsto\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}tH}
is lifted to a unitary group
t\mapsto\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}t\mathcal{H}}=\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}tH}\,\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-3.0)\circle*{2.0}\end{picture}\ \,\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}tH}
.
Finally, a (Hermitian) projection
P=P^{2}\,(=P^{\dagger})
is lifted to a (Hermitian) projection
\mathcal{P}=P\,\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-3.0)\circle*{2.0}\end{picture}\ \,\,P
, satisfying
\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{P}^{2}\,(=\mathcal{P}^{\dagger})
.

We mainly use the operator norm (2–2 norm), when we need to specify the norm of a map
\mathcal{A}:\mathcal{T}_{2}\to\mathcal{T}_{2}
,

\|\mathcal{A}\|=\sup_{\|X\|_{2}=1}\|\mathcal{A}(X)\|_{2},

(3.6)

where
\|X\|_{2}=(\langle X|X\rangle_{2})^{1/2}
for
X\in\mathcal{T}_{2}
.
It coincides with the largest singular value of
\mathcal{A}
,

\|\mathcal{A}\|=\sup_{\|X\|_{2}=1}(\langle\mathcal{A}(X)|\mathcal{A}(X)\rangle_{2})^{1/2}=\sup_{\|X\|_{2}=1}(\langle X|(\mathcal{A}^{\dagger}\mathcal{A})(X)\rangle_{2})^{1/2}=r(\mathcal{A}^{\dagger}\mathcal{A})^{1/2},

(3.7)

with
r(\mathcal{A}^{\dagger}\mathcal{A})
being the spectral radius of
\mathcal{A}^{\dagger}\mathcal{A}
.

We now state without proofs some useful spectral properties of the quantum operations. For further details and proofs see e.g. Refs.
[
45
,
46
]
, and in particular Propositions 6.1–6.3 and Theorem 6.1 of Ref.
[
45
]
.

Proposition 1

(Spectral properties of quantum operations)
.

Let
\mathcal{E}
be a quantum operation on a finite-dimensional space. Then, the following properties hold:

(i)

The spectrum
\{\lambda_{k}\}
of
\mathcal{E}
is confined in the closed unit disc
\mathbb{D}=\{\lambda\in\mathbb{C},|\lambda|\leq 1\}
. Moreover, all the “peripheral” eigenvalues, belonging to the boundary of
\mathbb{D}
, i.e. on the unit circle
\partial\mathbb{D}=\{\lambda\in\mathbb{C},|\lambda|=1\}
, are semisimple. If
\mathcal{E}
is TP, then
\lambda=1
is an eigenvalue of
\mathcal{E}
.

(ii)

The canonical form of
\mathcal{E}
reads

\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{E}_{\varphi}+\sum_{|\lambda_{k}|<1}(\lambda_{k}\mathcal{P}_{k}+\mathcal{N}_{k}),

(3.8)

where
\{\mathcal{P}_{k}\}
and
\{\mathcal{N}_{k}\}
are the spectral projections and the nilpotents of
\mathcal{E}
, respectively, and

\mathcal{E}_{\varphi}=\sum_{|\lambda_{k}|=1}\lambda_{k}\mathcal{P}_{k}

(3.9)

is the “peripheral” part of
\mathcal{E}
, i.e. its component belonging to the peripheral spectrum on the unit circle
\partial\mathbb{D}
.

(iii)

The peripheral part
\mathcal{E}_{\varphi}
and the projection onto the peripheral spectrum of
\mathcal{E}
,

\mathcal{P}_{\varphi}=\sum_{|\lambda_{k}|=1}\mathcal{P}_{k},

(3.10)

are both quantum operations, and
\mathcal{E}_{\varphi}=\mathcal{E}\mathcal{P}_{\varphi}=\mathcal{P}_{\varphi}\mathcal{E}
. The maps
\mathcal{E}_{\varphi}
and
\mathcal{P}_{\varphi}
are TP iff
\mathcal{E}
is TP.

(iv)

The inverse of
\mathcal{E}_{\varphi}
on the range of
\mathcal{P}_{\varphi}
,

\mathcal{E}_{\varphi}^{-1}=\sum_{|\lambda_{k}|=1}\lambda_{k}^{-1}\mathcal{P}_{k},

(3.11)

satisfying

\mathcal{E}_{\varphi}^{-1}\mathcal{E}_{\varphi}=\mathcal{E}_{\varphi}\mathcal{E}_{\varphi}^{-1}=\mathcal{P}_{\varphi}
,
is also a quantum operation, and it is TP if
\mathcal{E}
is TP.

Similar properties hold for GKLS generators
\mathcal{L}
, whose exponential
\mathrm{e}^{t\mathcal{L}}
is CPTP for all
t\geq 0
. See Proposition 2 of Ref.
[
11
]
.

Remark 1
.

Note that if the peripheral spectrum is empty then all peripheral maps are null,
\mathcal{P}_{\varphi}=\mathcal{E}_{\varphi}=\mathcal{E}_{\varphi}^{-1}=0
. By property (i), this cannot happen if
\mathcal{E}
is TP.

4
Main Theorem

The main result of this paper is the unification of the pulsed QZDs, via (i) frequent projective measurements and via (ii) frequent unitary kicks, which at the same time allows us to generalize the pulses to arbitrary quantum operations.
We further generalize the bang-bang decoupling/dynamical decoupling to cycles of generic kicks.
These are all summarized in the following theorem, which will be proved in Sec.
7
:

Theorem 1

(QZD by cycles of generic kicks)
.

Let
\{\mathcal{E}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{E}_{m}\}
be a finite set of quantum operations and
\mathcal{L}
be a GKLS generator acting on a finite-dimensional quantum system. Then, we have

\left(\mathcal{E}_{m}\mathrm{e}^{\frac{t}{mn}\mathcal{L}}\cdots\mathcal{E}_{1}\mathrm{e}^{\frac{t}{mn}\mathcal{L}}\right)^{n}=\mathcal{E}_{\varphi}^{n}\mathrm{e}^{t\mathcal{L}_{Z}}+\mathcal{O}(1/n)\quad\text{as}\quad n\to+\infty,

(4.1)

uniformly in
t
on compact intervals of
[0,+\infty)
,
with

\mathcal{L}_{Z}=\sum_{|\lambda_{k}|=1}\mathcal{P}_{k}\overline{\mathcal{L}}\mathcal{P}_{k},\qquad\overline{\mathcal{L}}=\frac{1}{m}\,\biggl(\mathcal{L}+\mathcal{E}_{\varphi}^{-1}\sum_{j=2}^{m}\mathcal{E}_{m}\cdots\mathcal{E}_{j}\mathcal{L}\mathcal{E}_{j-1}\cdots\mathcal{E}_{1}\biggr),

(4.2)

where
\mathcal{P}_{k}
is the spectral projection
of
\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{E}_{m}\cdots\mathcal{E}_{1}
belonging to the eigenvalue
\lambda_{k}
,
and
\mathcal{E}_{\varphi}
and
\mathcal{E}_{\varphi}^{-1}
are the peripheral part of
\mathcal{E}
and its peripheral inverse, respectively.

In particular, for
m=1
, we have the following corollary, which covers both QZDs via (i) projective measurements and via (ii) unitary kicks, and generalizes them to generic kicks:

Corollary 1

(QZD by generic kicks)
.

Let
\mathcal{E}
be a quantum operation and
\mathcal{L}
be a GKLS generator of a finite-dimensional quantum system. Then, we have

\left(\mathcal{E}\mathrm{e}^{\frac{t}{n}\mathcal{L}}\right)^{n}=\mathcal{E}_{\varphi}^{n}\mathrm{e}^{t\mathcal{L}_{Z}}+\mathcal{O}(1/n)\quad\text{as}\quad n\to+\infty,

(4.3)

uniformly in
t
on compact intervals of
[0,+\infty)
,
with

\mathcal{L}_{Z}=\sum_{|\lambda_{k}|=1}\mathcal{P}_{k}\mathcal{L}\mathcal{P}_{k},

(4.4)

where
\mathcal{P}_{k}
is the spectral projection
of
\mathcal{E}
belonging to the eigenvalue
\lambda_{k}
, and
\mathcal{E}_{\varphi}
is the peripheral part of
\mathcal{E}
.

If in the above statements the maps
\mathcal{E}
and
\mathcal{E}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{E}_{m}
are assumed to be CPTP and describe measurement processes, they are nonselective measurements.
An interesting corollary of Theorem
1
is available for selective measurements.
In particular, we provide a corollary for the QZD via cycles of
multiple selective projective measurements. This is a generalization of the standard QZD via (i) frequent selective projective measurements.

Corollary 2

(QZD by cycles of projective measurements)
.

Let
\{\mathcal{P}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{P}_{m}\}
be a finite set of CP Hermitian projections on
the Hilbert-Schmidt space
\mathcal{T}_{2}
of operators on a finite-dimensional Hilbert space, and

\mathcal{L}
be a GKLS generator.
The projections are not assumed to be pairwise orthogonal, i.e.
\mathcal{P}_{i}\mathcal{P}_{j}\neq 0
for
i\neq j
, in general.
Then, we have

\left(\mathcal{P}_{m}\mathrm{e}^{\frac{t}{mn}\mathcal{L}}\cdots\mathcal{P}_{1}\mathrm{e}^{\frac{t}{mn}\mathcal{L}}\right)^{n}=\mathcal{P}_{\varphi}\mathrm{e}^{t\mathcal{P}_{\varphi}\mathcal{L}\mathcal{P}_{\varphi}}+\mathcal{O}(1/n)\quad\text{as}\quad n\to+\infty,

(4.5)

uniformly in
t
on compact intervals of
\mathbb{R}
,
where
\mathcal{P}_{\varphi}=\mathcal{P}_{1}\wedge\mathcal{P}_{2}\wedge\cdots\wedge\mathcal{P}_{m}
is the Hermitian projection onto the intersection of the ranges of the projections
\mathcal{P}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{P}_{m}
.
If such intersection is trivial, then
\mathcal{P}_{\varphi}=0
, and the sequence in Eq. (
4.5
) just decays to zero.

Proof.

The proof makes use of the crucial fact that the peripheral part of the product of the Hermitian projections

\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{P}_{m}\cdots\mathcal{P}_{1}

reads

\mathcal{E}_{\varphi}=\mathcal{E}^{-1}_{\varphi}=\mathcal{P}_{\varphi}

(4.6)

(
\lambda=1
is the only peripheral eigenvalue of
\mathcal{E}
), and

\mathcal{P}_{\varphi}\mathcal{P}_{j}=\mathcal{P}_{j}\mathcal{P}_{\varphi}=\mathcal{P}_{\varphi}
for all
j=1,\dots,m
.
This will be proved in Lemma
8
in Appendix
A
.
Then, Eq. (
4.6
) implies that
\mathcal{E}_{\varphi}^{n}=\mathcal{P}_{\varphi}^{n}=\mathcal{P}_{\varphi}
, and
\overline{\mathcal{L}}
in Eq. (
4.2
) of Theorem
1
is simplified to

\overline{\mathcal{L}}=\frac{1}{m}\,\biggl(\mathcal{L}+\mathcal{P}_{\varphi}\mathcal{L}\sum_{j=2}^{m}\mathcal{P}_{j-1}\cdots\mathcal{P}_{1}\biggr).

(4.7)

Therefore,
\mathcal{L}_{Z}
in Eq. (
4.2
) of Theorem
1
reads

\mathcal{L}_{Z}=\mathcal{P}_{\varphi}\overline{\mathcal{L}}\mathcal{P}_{\varphi}=\mathcal{P}_{\varphi}\mathcal{L}\mathcal{P}_{\varphi},

(4.8)

and Eq. (
4.1
) of Theorem
1
becomes Eq. (
4.5
).
∎

Remark 2
.

Let us consider a unitary evolution,
\mathcal{L}=-\mathrm{i}[H,{}\,\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-3.0)\circle*{2.0}\end{picture}\ \,\,{}]
with
H=H^{\dagger}
.
For
\mathcal{P}_{j}=P_{j}\,\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-3.0)\circle*{2.0}\end{picture}\ \,\,P_{j}
, with
P_{1},\ldots,P_{m}
being Hermitian projections, Eq. (
4.5
) particularizes to

\left(P_{m}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\frac{t}{mn}H}\cdots P_{1}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\frac{t}{mn}H}\right)^{n}=P_{\varphi}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}tP_{\varphi}HP_{\varphi}}+\mathcal{O}(1/n)\quad\text{as}\quad n\to+\infty,

(4.9)

where
P_{\varphi}=P_{1}\wedge P_{2}\wedge\cdots\wedge P_{m}
is the Hermitian projection onto the intersection of the ranges of
P_{1},\ldots,P_{m}
, and one gets a QZD by cycles of (nonorthogonal) selective measurements.
More generally, if

\mathcal{P}_{j}=\sum_{k=1}^{n_{j}}P_{k}^{(j)}\,\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-3.0)\circle*{2.0}\end{picture}\ \,\,P_{k}^{(j)},

(4.10)

where
\{P_{1}^{(j)},\ldots,P_{n_{j}}^{(j)}\}_{j=1,\ldots,m}
are sets of Hermitian projections with

P^{(j)}_{k}P^{(j)}_{\ell}=\delta_{k\ell}P_{k}^{(j)}
,
then one gets a QZD by cycles of (nonorthogonal) partially selective measurements.
A particular case is when
\sum_{k=1}^{n_{j}}P_{k}^{(j)}=I
for all
j=1,\dots,m
, and one has a cycle of nonselective (i.e. CPTP) measurements.
There exist more general CP Hermitian projections, that cannot be cast in the form (
4.10
).
For instance,
\mathcal{P}=\frac{I}{d}\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits({}\,\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-3.0)\circle*{2.0}\end{picture}\ \,\,{})
is a CPTP Hermitian projection for a
d
-dimensional system.
Corollary
2
works for general CP Hermitian projections including such a projection.

5
Key Lemmas

The key idea is to bridge from the pulsed strategies to the continuous strategies via the BCH formula
[
10
]
, and then prove the Zeno limit by the generalized adiabatic theorem, which was proved and used to unify the continuous strategies in Ref.
[
11
]
(Theorems 1 and 2 therein).
In this way, the pulsed strategies are unified with the continuous strategies.
The key lemma for the bridge is the following generalized BCH formula:

Lemma 1

(Pulsed vs continuous for invertible kicks)
.

Let
E
and
L
be linear operators on a finite-dimensional Banach space, with
E
invertible.
Let
A=\log E
be a primary logarithm of
E
so that
\mathrm{e}^{A}=E
.
Then, we have

\left(E\mathrm{e}^{\frac{t}{n}L}\right)^{n}=\mathrm{e}^{nA+t\tilde{L}+\mathcal{O}(1/n)}\quad\text{as}\quad n\to+\infty,

(5.1)

uniformly in
t
on compact intervals of
\mathbb{R}
,
with

\tilde{L}=g(\mathop{\mathrm{ad}}\nolimits_{A})(L),

(5.2)

where
g
is the meromorphic function on
\mathbb{C}
defined by

g(z)=\begin{cases}\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt\displaystyle{\frac{z}{1-\mathrm{e}^{-z}}}&(z\notin 2\pi\mathrm{i}\mathbb{Z}),\\
1&(z=0),\end{cases}

(5.3)

and
\mathop{\mathrm{ad}}\nolimits_{A}=[A,{}\,\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-3.0)\circle*{2.0}\end{picture}\ \,\,{}]
.

Proof.

We will prove it in Sec.
6
.
∎

Remark 3
.

The assumption that
A
be a
primary
logarithm of
E

[
47
,
48
]
is necessary to get Eq. (
5.1
), as the following example shows.
Take
E=I
and
L=X
, the identity and the first Pauli matrix on
\mathbb{C}^{2}
, respectively. Then, we have

\left(E\mathrm{e}^{\frac{t}{n}L}\right)^{n}=\left(I\mathrm{e}^{\frac{t}{n}X}\right)^{n}=\mathrm{e}^{tX}.

(5.4)

On the other hand, consider
A=2\pi\mathrm{i}Z
, where
Z
is the third Pauli matrix. Then,
\mathrm{e}^{A}=\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}Z}=I=E
, but
A
is
not
a primary logarithm of
E
. It is apparent that there exists no matrix
\tilde{L}
such that

\mathrm{e}^{tX}=\mathrm{e}^{2n\pi\mathrm{i}Z+t\tilde{L}}+\mathcal{O}(1/n),

(5.5)

since
\mathrm{e}^{2n\pi\mathrm{i}Z+t\tilde{L}}=\mathrm{e}^{t\tilde{L}_{Z}}+\mathcal{O}(1/n)
as a strong-coupling limit
[
11
]
, with
\tilde{L}_{Z}
a diagonal matrix. Thus, Eq. (
5.1
) does not hold for
A=2\pi\mathrm{i}Z
.

We can apply this lemma to physical situations where
E
are invertible quantum operations and
L
are GKLS generators.
This lemma is however useful only for invertible
E
, and cannot accommodate e.g. the standard QZD via projective measurements. To circumvent this problem, we consider instead the primary logarithm of
E+Q
, with
Q
a projection onto the kernel of
E
, and by projecting
L
on a complementary space:

Lemma 2

(Pulsed vs continuous for noninvertible kicks)
.

Let
E
and
L
be linear operators on a finite-dimensional Banach space. Let
Q
be a projection onto
\ker E
and set
P=1-Q
. Let
A=\log(E+Q)
be a primary logarithm of the invertible operator
E+Q
,
so that
\mathrm{e}^{A}=E+Q
.
Then, we have

\left(E\mathrm{e}^{\frac{t}{n}PLP}\right)^{n}=\mathrm{e}^{nA+t\tilde{L}+\mathcal{O}(1/n)}P\quad\text{as}\quad n\to+\infty,

(5.6)

uniformly in
t
on compact intervals of
\mathbb{R}
,
with

\tilde{L}=Pg(\mathop{\mathrm{ad}}\nolimits_{A})(L)P,

(5.7)

where
g
is the meromorphic function on
\mathbb{C}
defined in Eq. (
5.3
).

Proof.

Notice first that, even when
E
is not invertible,
F=E+Q
is invertible, and we can consider one of its primary logarithms, say
A=\log F
.
Then, we can apply Lemma
1
as

\left(E\mathrm{e}^{\frac{t}{n}PLP}\right)^{n}=\left(F\mathrm{e}^{\frac{t}{n}PLP}\right)^{n}P=\left(\mathrm{e}^{A}\mathrm{e}^{\frac{t}{n}PLP}\right)^{n}P=\mathrm{e}^{nA+tg(\mathop{\mathrm{ad}}\nolimits_{A})(PLP)+\mathcal{O}(1/n)}P

(5.8)

for large
n
.
Since
[P,F]=0
and
A=\log F
is primary, it implies
[P,A]=0
, and we have

g(\mathop{\mathrm{ad}}\nolimits_{A})(PLP)=Pg(\mathop{\mathrm{ad}}\nolimits_{A})(L)P=\tilde{L}.

(5.9)

The statement of the lemma thus holds.
∎

Remark 4
.

If
E
is invertible, i.e.
\ker E=\{0\}
, then
Q=0
and
P=1
,
and Lemma
2
is reduced to Lemma
1
.

Remark 5
.

If
L
in the exponent on the left-hand side of Eq. (
5.6
) is not projected by
P
as
PLP
, we are not allowed to promote
E
to
E+Q
to define
A=\log(E+Q)
, since
\mathrm{e}^{\frac{t}{n}L}
is in general not commutative with
P
.

The second ingredient to bridge a pulsed dynamics to a continuous one is the following approximation lemma:

Lemma 3

(Asymptotic projection of a sequence of operators)
.

Let
(E_{n})
be a sequence of linear operators on a finite-dimensional Banach space and
P\,(=P^{2})
be a projection.
Assume that the following conditions hold:

1.

The operators
E_{n}
asymptotically commute with
P
as

PE_{n}=E_{n}P+\mathcal{O}(1/n)\quad\text{as}\quad n\to+\infty.

(5.10)

2.

There exist
M\geq 0
and
n_{0}>0
such that, for all
n\geq n_{0}
,

\|(PE_{n}P)^{k}\|\leq M,\quad\forall k\in\mathbb{N}.

(5.11)

3.

There exist
K\geq 0
,
\mu\in[0,1)
, and
n_{0}>0
such that, for all
n\geq n_{0}
,

\|(E_{n}-PE_{n}P)^{k}\|\leq K\mu^{k},\quad\forall k\in\mathbb{N}.

(5.12)

Then, we have

E_{n}^{n}=(PE_{n}P)^{n}+\mathcal{O}(1/n)\quad\text{as}\quad n\to+\infty.

(5.13)

Proof.

The proof is given in Appendix
C
.
∎

Remark 6
.

For a sequence of quantum operations
(\mathcal{E}_{n})
converging to a quantum operation
\mathcal{E}
as
\mathcal{E}_{n}=\mathcal{E}+\mathcal{O}(1/n)
as
n\to+\infty
, all the conditions 1–3 of Lemma
3
are automatically fulfilled with the peripheral projection
\mathcal{P}_{\varphi}
of
\mathcal{E}
taken as
P
.
See Lemmas
4
and
5
in Appendix
A
, which guarantee conditions 2 and 3 for the sequence of quantum operations
(\mathcal{E}_{n})
.
Then, according to Lemma
3
, we have

\mathcal{E}_{n}^{n}=(\mathcal{P}_{\varphi}\mathcal{E}_{n}\mathcal{P}_{\varphi})^{n}+\mathcal{O}(1/n)\quad\text{as}\quad n\to+\infty.

(5.14)

We will use these lemmas to prove Theorem
1
.

6
Proof of Lemma
1

Let us prove Lemma
1
, which is the key to the proof of Theorem
1
.

Proof of Lemma
1
.

First, let us recall some properties of functions of operators on a finite-dimensional Banach space (see e.g. Refs.
[
44
]
,

[
47
, Chap. 1]
, and
[
48
, Sec. 6.2]
).
Given a function
h(z)
on the complex plane, we wish to define a function
h(X)
of operators
X
. Notice that, in general, an operator function
h(X)
does not have a series expansion, unless the spectrum of
X
lies within the convergence radius of a power series of the function
h(z)
. Neverthless, by making use of the resolvent
(zI-X)^{-1}
of the operator
X
, we can define functions
h(X)
of
X
for a large class of functions
h
.

Suppose that
h(z)
is holomorphic in a domain
\Delta
of the complex plane containing the spectrum of
X
, and let
\Gamma\subset\Delta
be a smooth curve with positive direction enclosing all the eigenvalues in its interior. Then, a
primary function

h(X)
is defined by the Dunford-Taylor integral

h(X)=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\oint_{\Gamma}\mathrm{d}z\,h(z)\frac{1}{zI-X}.

(6.1)

More generally,
\Gamma
may consist of several simple closed curves, such that the union of their interiors contains all the eigenvalues of
X
.
Note that Eq. (
6.1
) does not depend on
\Gamma
as long as the latter satisfies these conditions, that is
h(z)
is holomorphic in
\Delta
and
\Gamma\subset\Delta
.

Now, let us start the proof of Lemma
1
.

Step 1.

We wish to define a logarithm of
E
.
Since
E
is invertible, its spectrum
\sigma(E)
does not contain
0
. Choose a half-line
\mathrm{c}=\{r\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\varphi}\in\mathbb{C}\,|\,r\geq 0\}
such that
\mathrm{c}\cap\sigma(E)=\emptyset
, and let
h(z)=\log z
denote a branch of the logarithm function.
Take a contour
\Gamma
enclosing all the eigenvalues of
E
and contained in
\Delta=\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathrm{c}
.
See Fig.
3
.
Since
h(z)
is analytic in
\Delta
, it can be taken as a stem function to define a primary logarithm function
A=\log E
by Eq. (
6.1
), and
\mathrm{e}^{A}=E
is inherited from the functional properties of the function
h(z)
. Note that there is a neighborhood of
E
on which this logarithm function is well-defined
[
44
,
49
]
.

Figure 3:
An example of contour
\Gamma
to define a primary logarithm function of an operator
E
. The crosses represent the eigenvalues of
E
, the cut
\mathrm{c}
does not intersect any eigenvalue,
and the contour
\Gamma
runs in the domain of analiticity
\Delta=\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathrm{c}
of a branch
h(z)=\log z
of the logarithm function, and encloses all the eigenvalues.

Step 2.

Given the operator
A=\log E
, we follow the proof of the BCH formula given in Sec. 5.5 of Ref.
[
10
]
.
For
n
large enough,
\mathrm{e}^{A}\mathrm{e}^{\frac{s}{n}L}=E\mathrm{e}^{\frac{s}{n}L}

is invertible and lies in the neighborhood of
E
for all
0\leq s\leq t
, whence its logarithm is defined by the integral (
6.1
) along the same contour
\Gamma
. Let

Z(s)=\log(\mathrm{e}^{A}\mathrm{e}^{\frac{s}{n}L})

(6.2)

for
0\leq s\leq t
.

Z(s)
is an analytic operator-valued function
and

\mathrm{e}^{-Z(s)}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}\mathrm{e}^{Z(s)}=(\mathrm{e}^{A}\mathrm{e}^{\frac{s}{n}L})^{-1}\mathrm{e}^{A}\mathrm{e}^{\frac{s}{n}L}\frac{1}{n}L=\frac{1}{n}L.

(6.3)

On the other hand, by Theorem 5.4 of Ref.
[
10
]
, we have

\mathrm{e}^{-Z(s)}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}\mathrm{e}^{Z(s)}=f(\mathop{\mathrm{ad}}\nolimits_{Z(s)})\!\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}Z(s)}{\mathrm{d}s}\right),

(6.4)

where

f(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n}}{(n+1)!}z^{n}=\begin{cases}\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt\displaystyle\frac{1-\mathrm{e}^{-z}}{z}&(z\neq 0),\\
1&(z=0)\end{cases}

(6.5)

is an entire analytic function, and the superoperator
f(\mathop{\mathrm{ad}}\nolimits_{Z(s)})
is defined by Eq. (
6.1
) for a given curve
\Gamma
enclosing the spectrum of
\mathop{\mathrm{ad}}\nolimits_{Z(s)}
for all
0\leq s\leq t
. Notice that
f(\mathop{\mathrm{ad}}\nolimits_{Z(0)})=f(\mathop{\mathrm{ad}}\nolimits_{A})
, and that
f(z)=0
only at the imaginary points
z_{k}=2\pi\mathrm{i}k
with
k\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
.

Step 3.

Now, we wish to invert Eq. (
6.4
) in order to obtain an explicit expression for the derivative
\mathrm{d}Z(s)/\mathrm{d}s
. We claim that
f(\mathop{\mathrm{ad}}\nolimits_{A})
is invertible. That is to say that all eigenvalues of
\mathop{\mathrm{ad}}\nolimits_{A}
are not zeros of
f(z)
, namely that
\ker(\mathop{\mathrm{ad}}\nolimits_{A}-z_{k})=\{0\}
for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
. Indeed, let the operator
X_{*}
belong to
\ker(\mathop{\mathrm{ad}}\nolimits_{A}-z_{k})
for some
k\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
.
Then,

\mathop{\mathrm{ad}}\nolimits_{A}(X_{*})=[A,X_{*}]=z_{k}X_{*}.

(6.6)

By exponentiating it, we get

\mathrm{e}^{t\mathop{\mathrm{ad}}\nolimits_{A}}(X_{*})=\mathrm{e}^{tA}X_{*}\mathrm{e}^{-tA}=\mathrm{e}^{tz_{k}}X_{*}
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
. In particular, at
t=1
,

\mathrm{e}^{A}X_{*}\mathrm{e}^{-A}=\mathrm{e}^{z_{k}}X_{*},

(6.7)

that is
EX_{*}E^{-1}=X_{*}
, whence

[E,X_{*}]=0
. But this implies that also
A=\log E
, as a function of
E
, commutes with
X_{*}
, namely

\mathop{\mathrm{ad}}\nolimits_{A}(X_{*})=[A,X_{*}]=0,

(6.8)

which, together with Eq. (
6.6
), implies that
X_{*}=0
, since
z_{k}\neq 0
.

Hence the superoperator
f(\mathop{\mathrm{ad}}\nolimits_{A})
is invertible. Furthermore, this implies that the inverse of
f(\mathop{\mathrm{ad}}\nolimits_{Z(s)})
does
exist for all
0\leq s\leq t
, if
n
is large enough.
It is given by
g(\mathop{\mathrm{ad}}\nolimits_{Z(s)})
, defined by the stem function
g(z)=1/f(z)
, that is the meromorphic function given in Eq. (
5.3
).

We can then combine Eqs. (
6.3
) and (
6.4
) to obtain

\frac{\mathrm{d}Z(s)}{\mathrm{d}s}=\frac{1}{n}g(\mathop{\mathrm{ad}}\nolimits_{Z(s)})(L).

(6.9)

Noting that
Z(0)=A
, we integrate Eq. (
6.9
) to get

Z(t)=\log(\mathrm{e}^{A}\mathrm{e}^{\frac{t}{n}L})=A+\frac{1}{n}\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}s\,g(\mathop{\mathrm{ad}}\nolimits_{Z(s)})(L).

(6.10)

Step 4.

We are only interested in terms up to
\mathcal{O}(1/n)
as
n\to+\infty
.
In general, by the integral representation (
6.1
) for
h(z)
analytic on a domain
\Delta
and for the spectrum of
X+\frac{1}{n}Y
enclosed by
\Gamma\subset\Delta
, where
X
and
Y
are operators of order
\mathcal{O}(1)
on a finite-dimensional Banach space, we have

\displaystyle h\!\left(X+\frac{1}{n}Y\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\oint_{\Gamma}\mathrm{d}z\,h(z)\frac{1}{zI-X-\frac{1}{n}Y}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\oint_{\Gamma}\mathrm{d}z\,h(z)\left(\frac{1}{zI-X}+\frac{1}{n}\frac{1}{zI-X}Y\frac{1}{zI-X-\frac{1}{n}Y}\right)

\displaystyle=h(X)+\mathcal{O}(1/n),\vphantom{\oint}

(6.11)

for all
n>n_{0}
for some
n_{0}>0
.
Therefore, for
h(z)=\log z
we get
Z(s)=\log(\mathrm{e}^{A}\mathrm{e}^{\frac{s}{n}L})=A+\mathcal{O}(1/n)
by choosing
X=\mathrm{e}^{A}
and
Y=n\mathrm{e}^{A}(\mathrm{e}^{\frac{s}{n}L}-I)=\mathcal{O}(1)
, which implies
\mathop{\mathrm{ad}}\nolimits_{Z(s)}=\mathop{\mathrm{ad}}\nolimits_{A}+\mathcal{O}(1/n)
. Then, expanding

g(\mathop{\mathrm{ad}}\nolimits_{Z(s)})
in Eq. (
6.10
),
we get

\displaystyle\log(\mathrm{e}^{A}\mathrm{e}^{\frac{t}{n}L})

\displaystyle=A+\frac{1}{n}\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}s\,g(\mathop{\mathrm{ad}}\nolimits_{A})(L)+\mathcal{O}(1/n^{2})

8.5
Efficiency in Time by Pulsed Weak Measurements

In real experiments, it takes time to perform strong (projective) measurements (it takes time to project a system).
While the convergence to QZD would be faster with a stronger measurement (requiring less number of measurements; see Fig.
4
), it would consume more experimental time.
Since Theorem
1
tells us that QZD can be induced even via weak measurements, it could be better to proceed to the next measurement without waiting for a system being projected by a measurement, to save experimental time.
It is actually the case.
Let us see the efficiency in inducing QZD in terms of experimental time.

Consider, for instance, the model analyzed in Sec.
8.1
.

\tau

\mathcal{E}

Here, we assume that the unitary evolution of the system is turned off during the measurement process, or that the coupling to the measurement apparatus is strong enough so that the unitary evolution during the measurement process is negligible.
The total time spent for the
n
measurements is given by
n\tau
and the total experimental time is
n\tau+t
.

.

Conclusions

Our unification and generalization of QZDs has revealed an adiabatic evolution as the key ingredient. It is remarkable that such a variety of limits can be reduced to adiabaticity. We left for future studies a discussion of the tightness of our error bounds, and how they scale with the dimensionality of the Hilbert space. We also did not consider infinite-dimensional systems. Since the adiabatic theorem has itself many generalizations to infinite-dimensional systems and unbounded operators, this connection might pave the way to QZDs with unbounded operators, where there remain many open problems
[
14
,
12
,
38
,
50
,
51
,
52
]
. However, our proof via a generalized Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff formula does not easily generalize to infinite dimensions, and finding a more direct bridge between kicked dynamics and the adiabatic theorem would be desirable.

Lemma 4

(Norm of quantum operation
[
55
]
)
.

Consider the
d^{2}
-dimensional Hilbert space
\mathcal{T}_{2}
of operators on a
d
-dimensional Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
, with the Hilbert-Schmidt inner product
\langle X|Y\rangle_{2}=\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits(X^{\dagger}Y)
for
X,Y\in\mathcal{T}_{2}
.
The operator norm of any quantum operation
\mathcal{E}:\mathcal{T}_{2}\to\mathcal{T}_{2}
is bounded by

\|\mathcal{E}\|\leq\sqrt{d}.

(A.1)

Proof.

Recall the definition of the operator norm of
\mathcal{A}:\mathcal{T}_{2}\to\mathcal{T}_{2}
in Eq. (
3.6
):

\|\mathcal{A}\|=\sup_{\|X\|_{2}=1}\|\mathcal{A}(X)\|_{2},

(A.2)

where
\|X\|_{2}=[\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits(X^{\dagger}X)]^{1/2}
.
We have
\|\mathcal{A}^{\dagger}\|=\|\mathcal{A}\|
. Moreover, for any operator
X
on the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
, we have that

\|X\|_{\infty}\leq\|X\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{d}\,\|X\|_{\infty},

(A.3)

where
\|X\|_{\infty}=\sup_{\|v\|=1}\|Xv\|
with
v\in\mathcal{H}
.

Now, we have that
\|\mathcal{E}^{\dagger}(X)\|_{\infty}\leq\|\mathcal{E}^{\dagger}(I)\|_{\infty}\|X\|_{\infty}
by a theorem of Russo and Dye
[
56
, Corollary 2.9]
. But the adjoint of a quantum operation
\mathcal{E}
is subunital,
\mathcal{E}^{\dagger}(I)\leq I
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{E}^{\dagger}(X)\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{d}\,\|\mathcal{E}^{\dagger}(X)\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{d}\,\|\mathcal{E}^{\dagger}(I)\|_{\infty}\|X\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{d}\,\|I\|_{\infty}\|X\|_{\infty}=\sqrt{d}\,\|X\|_{\infty}\leq\sqrt{d}\,\|X\|_{2},

(A.4)

whence

\|\mathcal{E}\|=\|\mathcal{E}^{\dagger}\|\leq\sqrt{d}.

(A.5)

∎

We note that a more natural choice of norm for CP maps would be the trace norm, in which they are contractive.
However, to prove the next Lemma
5
, the operator norm is more useful since we need to deal with spectral radius.
A universal bound on quantum operation in the operator norm is required in Lemma
5
, and therefore, we have derived it in Lemma
4
.
Using the trace norm does not simplify matters and we stick to the operator norm.

The next lemma is a variant of Refs.
[
45
, Lemma 8.5 and Theorem 8.24]
and
[
53
]
.

Lemma 5
.

Let
(\mathcal{E}_{n})
be a convergent sequence of quantum operations on
a
d
-dimensional quantum system
with

\mathcal{E}_{n}\to\mathcal{E}\quad\text{as}\quad n\to+\infty.

(A.6)

Let
\mathcal{P}_{\varphi}
be the peripheral spectral projection of the quantum operation
\mathcal{E}
, and
\mu_{0}=r(\mathcal{E}-\mathcal{E}_{\varphi})<1
the spectral radius of its nonperipheral part.
Then, for any
\mu\in(\mu_{0},1)
there exists an integer
n_{0}>0
and a positive number
K>0
such that

\displaystyle\|(\mathcal{E}_{n}-\mathcal{P}_{\varphi}\mathcal{E}_{n}\mathcal{P}_{\varphi})^{k}\|\leq K\mu^{k},

(A.7)

for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
and for all
n>n_{0}
.

Proof.

Given
\mu\in(\mu_{0},1)
, fix a
\mu_{1}\in(\mu_{0},\mu)
and define
\mathcal{E}_{n}^{\prime}=\mathcal{E}_{n}-\mathcal{P}_{\varphi}\mathcal{E}_{n}\mathcal{P}_{\varphi}
.
Since

r(\mathcal{E}_{n}^{\prime})\to r(\mathcal{E}-\mathcal{P}_{\varphi}\mathcal{E}\mathcal{P}_{\varphi})=\mu_{0}<1,

(A.8)

as
n\to+\infty
, there exists an integer
n_{0}>0
such that

r(\mathcal{E}_{n}^{\prime})<\mu_{1}<\mu<1,\quad\forall n>n_{0}.

(A.9)

Recall now that
\mathcal{E}_{n}^{\prime}
can be transformed into an upper-triangular matrix by a unitary transformation
\mathcal{U}_{n}
(Schur triangulation),

\mathcal{E}_{n}^{\prime}=\mathcal{U}_{n}^{\dagger}(\Lambda_{n}+\mathcal{N}_{n})\mathcal{U}_{n},

(A.10)

where
\Lambda_{n}
is diagonal while
\mathcal{N}_{n}
is a strictly upper-triangular matrix with vanishing diagonal elements, which is nilpotent,

\mathcal{N}_{n}^{d^{2}}=0.

(A.11)

Since
\mathcal{P}_{\varphi}
is a quantum operation by Proposition
1
(iii),

\mathcal{P}_{\varphi}\mathcal{E}_{n}\mathcal{P}_{\varphi}
is also a quantum operation.
Then, the operator norm
of the nilpotent part
\mathcal{N}_{n}
is bounded as

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{N}_{n}\|

\displaystyle=\|\mathcal{U}_{n}\mathcal{E}_{n}^{\prime}\mathcal{U}_{n}^{\dagger}-\Lambda_{n}\|

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathcal{E}_{n}^{\prime}\|+\|\Lambda_{n}\|

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathcal{E}_{n}\|+\|\mathcal{P}_{\varphi}\mathcal{E}_{n}\mathcal{P}_{\varphi}\|+\|\Lambda_{n}\|

\displaystyle\leq 2\sqrt{d}+\mu_{1},

(A.12)

where we have used the fact that
\|\mathcal{E}_{n}\|,\|\mathcal{P}_{\varphi}\mathcal{E}_{n}\mathcal{P}_{\varphi}\|\leq\sqrt{d}
by Lemma
4
, and the fact that
\|\Lambda_{n}\|=r(\mathcal{E}_{n}^{\prime})\leq\mu_{1}
.

Now, we estimate the norm
\|\mathcal{E}_{n}^{\prime k}\|=\|(\Lambda_{n}+\mathcal{N}_{n})^{k}\|
.
To this end, notice that, in the binomial expansion of
(\Lambda_{n}+\mathcal{N}_{n})^{k}
, the terms in which
\mathcal{N}_{n}
appears more than
d^{2}-1
times vanish, irrespective of the fact that
\Lambda_{n}
and
\mathcal{N}_{n}
do not commute in general.
Then, we can bound the norm as

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{E}_{n}^{\prime k}\|

\displaystyle=\|(\Lambda_{n}+\mathcal{N}_{n})^{k}\|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=0}^{\min(k,d^{2}-1)}\begin{pmatrix}k\\
j\end{pmatrix}\|\Lambda_{n}\|^{k-j}\|\mathcal{N}_{n}\|^{j}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=0}^{\min(k,d^{2}-1)}\begin{pmatrix}k\\
j\end{pmatrix}\mu_{1}^{k-j}(2\sqrt{d}+\mu_{1})^{j}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=0}^{d^{2}-1}\frac{k^{j}}{j!}\mu_{1}^{k-j}(2\sqrt{d}+\mu_{1})^{j}

\displaystyle\leq k^{d^{2}-1}\mu_{1}^{k}\sum_{j=0}^{+\infty}\frac{1}{j!}\left(\frac{2\sqrt{d}}{\mu_{1}}+1\right)^{j}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}^{2\sqrt{d}/\mu_{1}+1}k^{d^{2}-1}\mu_{1}^{k},\qquad\forall k\in\mathbb{N},\quad\forall n>n_{0},

(A.13)

that is

\|(\mathcal{E}_{n}-\mathcal{P}_{\varphi}\mathcal{E}_{n}\mathcal{P}_{\varphi})^{k}\|\leq K_{1}k^{d^{2}-1}\mu_{1}^{k},\qquad\forall k\in\mathbb{N},\quad\forall n>n_{0},

(A.14)

with
K_{1}=\mathrm{e}^{2\sqrt{d}/\mu_{1}+1}
.

Finally, since
\mu>\mu_{1}
, we can always find a
K\geq K_{1}
such that

K_{1}k^{D}\mu_{1}^{k}\leq K\mu^{k},\qquad\forall k\in\mathbb{N},

(A.15)

where
D=d^{2}-1
.
Indeed, we have

\displaystyle\log\frac{K\mu^{k}}{K_{1}k^{D}\mu_{1}^{k}}

\displaystyle=\log\frac{K}{K_{1}}+k\log\frac{\mu}{\mu_{1}}-D\log k

\displaystyle\geq\log\frac{K}{K_{1}}+D-D\log\frac{D}{\log(\mu/\mu_{1})}.

(A.16)

We can make it nonnegative by choosing

K\geq K_{1}\left(\frac{D}{\mathrm{e}\log(\mu/\mu_{1})}\right)^{D}=\mathrm{e}^{2\sqrt{d}/\mu_{1}+1}\left(\frac{d^{2}-1}{\mathrm{e}\log(\mu/\mu_{1})}\right)^{d^{2}-1}.

(A.17)

Together with Eq. (
A.14
) this gives the bound (
A.7
) of the lemma.
∎

Lemma 6
.

Let
\{\mathcal{E}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{E}_{m}\}
be a finite set of quantum operations and
\mathcal{L}
be a GKLS generator of a
d

\|\mathcal{E}_{m}\mathrm{e}^{\frac{t}{mn}\mathcal{L}}\cdots\mathcal{E}_{1}\mathrm{e}^{\frac{t}{mn}\mathcal{L}}-\mathcal{E}\|\leq d^{m/2}\frac{t}{n}\|\mathcal{L}\|\mathrm{e}^{\frac{t}{n}\|\mathcal{L}\|},\quad\forall n\in\mathbb{N},

(A.20)

uniformly in
t
on compact intervals of
[0,+\infty)
, where
\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{E}_{m}\cdots\mathcal{E}_{1}
, and we have chosen the operator norm.

Proof.

We split each piece of the evolution as
\mathrm{e}^{\frac{t}{mn}\mathcal{L}}=1+(\mathrm{e}^{\frac{t}{mn}\mathcal{L}}-1)
.
The deviation from the identity map is bounded by

\|\mathrm{e}^{\frac{t}{mn}\mathcal{L}}-1\|\leq\mathrm{e}^{\frac{t}{mn}\|\mathcal{L}\|}-1.

(A.21)

Then, the distance is bounded by

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{E}_{m}\mathrm{e}^{\frac{t}{mn}\mathcal{L}}\cdots\mathcal{E}_{1}\mathrm{e}^{\frac{t}{mn}\mathcal{L}}-\mathcal{E}\|

\displaystyle\leq d^{m/2}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\begin{pmatrix}m\\
j\end{pmatrix}\|\mathrm{e}^{\frac{t}{mn}\mathcal{L}}-1\|^{j}

\displaystyle=d^{m/2}\left[\left(1+\|\mathrm{e}^{\frac{t}{mn}\mathcal{L}}-1\|\right)^{m}-1\right]

\displaystyle\leq d^{m/2}\left(\mathrm{e}^{\frac{t}{n}\|\mathcal{L}\|}-1\right)

\displaystyle=d^{m/2}\frac{t}{n}\|\mathcal{L}\|\mathrm{e}^{\frac{t_{*}}{n}\|\mathcal{L}\|}

\displaystyle\leq d^{m/2}\frac{t}{n}\|\mathcal{L}\|\mathrm{e}^{\frac{t}{n}\|\mathcal{L}\|},

(A.22)

where we have used
\|\mathcal{E}_{j}\|\leq\sqrt{d}
(
j=1,\ldots,m
) for the operator norm (Lemma
4
) and the mean-value theorem,
[F(t)-F(0)]/t=F^{\prime}(t_{*})
for some
t_{*}\in[0,t]
, for
F(t)=\mathrm{e}^{\frac{t}{n}\|\mathcal{L}\|}
, with
F^{\prime}(t)
denoting the derivative of
F(t)
with respect to
t
.
∎

Lemma
5
states that for any
\mu\in(\mu_{0},1)

, for the bang-bang sequence
\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{n}=\mathcal{E}_{m}\mathrm{e}^{\frac{t}{mn}\mathcal{L}}\cdots\mathcal{E}_{1}\mathrm{e}^{\frac{t}{mn}\mathcal{L}}

Lemma 7
.

Let
\{\mathcal{E}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{E}_{m}\}
be a finite set of quantum operations and
\mathcal{L}
be a GKLS generator of a
d
-dimensional quantum system.
We consider the sequence of quantum operations
\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{n}=\mathcal{E}_{m}\mathrm{e}^{\frac{t}{mn}\mathcal{L}}\cdots\mathcal{E}_{1}\mathrm{e}^{\frac{t}{mn}\mathcal{L}}
, which converges to
\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{E}_{m}\cdots\mathcal{E}_{1}
in the limit

, and let
\mathcal{P}_{\varphi}
the peripheral spectral projection of
\mathcal{E}
.
Then, to bound
\|(\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{n}-\mathcal{P}_{\varphi}\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{n}\mathcal{P}_{\varphi})^{k}\|

, we can choose

satisfying

\mu_{0}+\left((1+d)d^{m/2+2}\frac{t}{n_{0}}\|\mathcal{L}\|\mathrm{e}^{\frac{t}{n_{0}}\|\mathcal{L}\|}\right)^{1/d^{2}}\Bigl(1+\|\mathcal{N}_{n_{0}}\|\Bigr)\leq\mu<1,

(A.23)

where
\mathcal{N}_{n}
is the nilpotent part of
\Theta_{n}=(\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{n}-\mathcal{E})-\mathcal{P}_{\varphi}(\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{n}-\mathcal{E})\mathcal{P}_{\varphi}

Proof.

The constant
\mu>\mu_{0}=r(\mathcal{E}-\mathcal{P}_{\varphi}\mathcal{E}\mathcal{P}_{\varphi})
is to bound the spectral radius
r(\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{n}^{\prime})
of
\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{n}^{\prime}=\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{n}-\mathcal{P}_{\varphi}\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{n}\mathcal{P}_{\varphi}
for all

We wish to know how much
\mu
should be larger than
\mu_{0}

To this end, we use Theorem 7.2.3 of Ref.

Let

\|\Theta_{n}\|\leq(1+d)\|\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{n}-\mathcal{E}\|\leq(1+d)d^{m/2}\frac{t}{n}\|\mathcal{L}\|\mathrm{e}^{\frac{t}{n}\|\mathcal{L}\|},

\displaystyle r(\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{n}^{\prime})

Lemma 8

(Peripheral part of the product of Hermitian projections)
.

Let
\{P_{1},\ldots,P_{m}\}
be a set of Hermitian projection operators,
P_{j}^{2}=P_{j}=P_{j}^{\dagger}
for
j=1,\ldots,m
, on a Hilbert space.
Then, the peripheral part of the product
P_{m}\cdots P_{1}
is given by the Hermitian projection

P_{\varphi}=P_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge P_{m}

(A.30)

onto the intersection of the ranges
\mathop{\mathrm{ran}}\nolimits P_{1}\cap\cdots\cap\mathop{\mathrm{ran}}\nolimits P_{m}
.
We get
P_{\varphi}P_{j}=P_{j}P_{\varphi}=P_{\varphi}
for all
j=1,\ldots,m
.

Proof.

Suppose that
P_{2}P_{1}
admits a peripheral eigenvalue
\lambda
, and let
u\neq 0
be the corresponding eigenvector,

P_{2}P_{1}u=\lambda u\quad\text{with}\quad|\lambda|=1.

(A.31)

By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we have

\|u\|^{2}=\|P_{2}P_{1}u\|^{2}=\langle P_{1}u|P_{2}P_{1}u\rangle\leq\|P_{1}u\|\|P_{2}P_{1}u\|\leq\|u\|^{2},

(A.32)

where we have used
\|P_{j}\|\leq 1
and Eq. (
A.31
).
The two inequalities are actually saturated, implying

P_{1}u=u.

(A.33)

This simplifies Eq. (
A.31
) to

P_{2}u=\lambda u,

(A.34)

and hence one gets that
\lambda=1
.
In this way, the peripheral eigenvalue of
P_{2}P_{1}
, if any, is
1
, and the corresponding eigenvector
u
is a simultaneous eigenvector of
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
belonging to their nonvanishing eigenvalue
1
.
Moreover, since we have

(P_{2}P_{1})^{\dagger}u=P_{1}P_{2}u=u,

(A.35)

the conjugate of
u
is a left-eigenvector of
P_{2}P_{1}
belonging to the same eigenvalue
1
.
Therefore, the peripheral part of
P_{2}P_{1}
is diagonalizable and is the Hermitian projection

P_{\varphi}=P_{1}\wedge P_{2}

(A.36)

onto
\mathop{\mathrm{ran}}\nolimits P_{1}\cap\mathop{\mathrm{ran}}\nolimits P_{2}
, the intersection of the ranges of
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
.

By induction we get that the peripheral part of
P_{m}\cdots P_{1}
is given by the Hermitian projection

P_{\varphi}=P_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge P_{m}

(A.37)

onto the intersection of the ranges
\mathop{\mathrm{ran}}\nolimits P_{1}\cap\cdots\cap\mathop{\mathrm{ran}}\nolimits P_{m}
.
∎

Appendix B
Bounding the BCH Formula for Lemma
1

It is known that in general the BCH series converges only for small operators
[
58
]
.
This is related to the issue of the convergence of the Magnus expansion
[
59
]
.
For our purpose, however, this is not a problem.
We just need contributions up to the first order in
1/n
in the BCH formula
Z(t)=\log(\mathrm{e}^{A}\mathrm{e}^{\frac{t}{n}L})
, and the higher-order corrections are under control, as proved in Lemma
1
.
Here we provide a concise and explicit bound on the corrections.

We first need to know how to bound
\|h(X+Y)-h(X)\|
for a primary operator function
h(X)
.
If a series expansion exists for the stem function
h(z)
, e.g.

h(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_{n}z^{n},

(B.1)

it can be easily bounded as
[
48
, Theorem 6.2.30]

\|h(X+Y)-h(X)\|\leq h_{\mathrm{abs}}^{\prime}(\|X\|+\|Y\|)\|Y\|,

(B.2)

where

h_{\mathrm{abs}}(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}|c_{n}|z^{n},

(B.3)

and
h_{\mathrm{abs}}^{\prime}(z)
is its derivative.
It is, however, not always the case that a good series expansion exists for
h(z)
.
For instance, suppose that the spectrum of
X
is distributed on the complex plane as in Fig.
3
and
h(X)=\log X
.

Lemma 9

Proof.

(C.6)

where
k=(k_{1},\dots,k_{j})
and
k^{\prime}=(k_{1}^{\prime},\dots,k_{j}^{\prime})
are multi-indices of integers with
j=\ell
or
\ell+1
,
|k|=k_{1}+\cdots+k_{j}
denotes the order of
k
, and
\lfloor x\rfloor
denotes the greatest integer less than or equal to
x
.
We are going to show that the corrections to the first contribution
(PE_{n}P)^{n}
in Eq. (
C.2
) accumulate only up to
\mathcal{O}(1/n)
.

We first note that, since
PE_{n}^{\prime}P=0
by construction, the contributions with
k_{i}^{\prime}=1
for some
i
between two
(PE_{n}P)
’s vanish and do not contribute to
R_{m}
or
R_{m}^{\prime}
.
Therefore,
k_{i}^{\prime}\geq 2
for any
i
sandwiched by
(PE_{n}P)
’s.
Second,
PE_{n}=E_{n}P+\mathcal{O}(1/n)
[Eq. (
5.10
) in condition 1] implies

PE_{n}P=PE_{n}+\mathcal{O}(1/n)=E_{n}P+\mathcal{O}(1/n),

(C.7)

and hence, since
E^{\prime}_{n}=E_{n}-PE_{n}P
,

PE_{n}^{\prime}=\mathcal{O}(1/n),\qquad E_{n}^{\prime}P=\mathcal{O}(1/n).

(C.8)

Therefore, each contact between
PE_{n}P
and
E_{n}^{\prime}
yields
\mathcal{O}(1/n)
, and we are advised to count the number of the contacts in the corrections to see the orders of the contributions:

R_{2\ell}
and
R_{2\ell}^{\prime}
have
(2\ell-1)
contacts, while
R_{2\ell+1}
and
R_{2\ell+1}^{\prime}
have
2\ell
contacts.
Third, since
\|PE_{n}P\|
and
\|E_{n}^{\prime}\|
can be greater than
1
, it is not helpful to bound the corrections like
\|(PE_{n}P)^{k_{1}}E_{n}^{\prime k_{1}^{\prime}}\cdots\|\leq\|PE_{n}P\|^{k_{1}}\|E_{n}^{\prime}\|^{k_{1}^{\prime}}\cdots
.
On the other hand, we have the bounds (
5.11
) and (
5.12
) (conditions 2 and 3), which facilitate bounding the corrections.

Let us start estimating the corrections.
Thanks to Eq. (
5.12
) (condition 3), the last correction in Eq. (
C.2
) is bounded by

\|E_{n}^{\prime n}\|\leq K\mu^{n},

(C.9)

which is
o(1/n)
as
n\to+\infty
.

Let us set

C_{n}=\max\{\|E_{n}^{\prime}P\|,\|PE_{n}^{\prime}\|\}=\mathcal{O}(1/n),

(C.10)

where we have used Eq. (
C.8
). By the bounds (
5.11
) and (
5.12
) (conditions 3 and 4), the correction
R_{n,2\ell}
is bounded as

\displaystyle\|R_{n,2\ell}\|

\displaystyle\leq\mathop{\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}^{\ell},\,k^{\prime}\geq(2,\ldots,2,1)}}_{|k|+|k^{\prime}|=n}\|(PE_{n}P)^{k_{1}}\|\|PE_{n}^{\prime}\|\|E_{n}^{\prime k_{1}^{\prime}-2}\|\|E_{n}^{\prime}P\|\|(PE_{n}P)^{k_{2}}\|\cdots{}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad{}\cdots\|E_{n}^{\prime k_{\ell-1}^{\prime}-2}\|\|E_{n}^{\prime}P\|\|(PE_{n}P)^{k_{\ell}}\|\|PE_{n}^{\prime}\|\|E_{n}^{\prime k_{\ell}^{\prime}-1}\|\vphantom{\mathop{\sum\cdots\sum}_{k_{1}+\cdots+k_{m+1}=n}}

\displaystyle\leq M^{\ell}K^{\ell}C_{n}^{2\ell-1}\mathop{\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}^{\ell},\,k^{\prime}\geq(2,2,\dots,1)}}_{|k|+|k^{\prime}|=n}\mu^{|k^{\prime}|-2\ell+1}

\displaystyle=M^{\ell}K^{\ell}C_{n}^{2\ell-1}\mathop{\sum_{k^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}^{\ell}}}_{|k^{\prime}|\leq n-2\ell+1}\mu^{|k^{\prime}|-\ell}\mathop{\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}^{\ell}}}_{|k|=n-|k^{\prime}|-\ell+1}1

\displaystyle=M^{\ell}K^{\ell}C_{n}^{2\ell-1}\mathop{\sum_{k^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}^{\ell}}}_{|k^{\prime}|\leq n-2\ell+1}\begin{pmatrix}\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0ptn-|k^{\prime}|-\ell\\
\ell-1\end{pmatrix}\mu^{|k^{\prime}|-\ell}

\displaystyle\leq M^{\ell}K^{\ell}C_{n}^{2\ell-1}\frac{n^{\ell-1}}{(\ell-1)!}\mathop{\sum_{k^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}^{\ell}}}_{|k^{\prime}|\leq n-2\ell+1}\mu^{|k^{\prime}|-\ell}

\displaystyle\leq M^{\ell}K^{\ell}C_{n}^{2\ell-1}\frac{n^{\ell-1}}{(\ell-1)!}\frac{1}{(1-\mu)^{\ell}}

\displaystyle\equiv a_{n,\ell},

(C.11)

where we have used the identities

\mathop{\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}^{\ell}}}_{|k|=m}1=\begin{pmatrix}m-1\\
\ell-1\end{pmatrix},\qquad\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}^{\ell}}\mu^{|k|-\ell}=\biggl(\sum_{j\geq 0}\mu^{j}\biggr)^{\ell}=\frac{1}{(1-\mu)^{\ell}},

(C.12)

and the inequality

\begin{pmatrix}n-m\\
j\end{pmatrix}\leq\frac{n^{j}}{j!}.

(C.13)

The correction
R_{n,2\ell}^{\prime}
is bounded as

\displaystyle\|R_{n,2\ell}^{\prime}\|

\displaystyle\leq\mathop{\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}^{\ell},\,k^{\prime}\geq(1,2,\dots,2)}}_{|k|+|k^{\prime}|=n}\|E_{n}^{\prime k_{1}^{\prime}-1}\|\|E_{n}^{\prime}P\|\|(PE_{n}P)^{k_{1}}\|\|PE_{n}^{\prime}\|\|E_{n}^{\prime k_{2}^{\prime}-2}\|\cdots{}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad{}\cdots\|(PE_{n}P)^{k_{\ell-1}}\|\|PE_{n}^{\prime}\|\|E_{n}^{\prime k_{\ell}^{\prime}-2}\|\|E_{n}^{\prime}P\|\|(PE_{n}P)^{k_{\ell}}\|\vphantom{\mathop{\sum\cdots\sum}_{k_{1}+\cdots+k_{m+1}=n}}

\displaystyle\leq M^{\ell}K^{\ell}C_{n}^{2\ell-1}\mathop{\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}^{\ell},\,k^{\prime}\geq(1,2,\dots,2)}}_{|k|+|k^{\prime}|=n}\mu^{|k^{\prime}|-2\ell+1}

\displaystyle=M^{\ell}K^{\ell}C_{n}^{2\ell-1}\mathop{\sum_{k^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}^{\ell}}}_{|k^{\prime}|\leq n-2\ell+1}\mu^{|k^{\prime}|-\ell}\mathop{\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}^{\ell}}}_{|k|=n-|k^{\prime}|-\ell+1}1

\displaystyle\leq a_{n,\ell},

(C.14)

as for
\|R_{n,2\ell}\|
.

The correction
R_{n,2\ell+1}
is bounded as

\displaystyle\|R_{n,2\ell+1}\|

\displaystyle\leq\mathop{\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}^{\ell+1},\,k^{\prime}\geq(2,\ldots,2)}}_{|k|+|k^{\prime}|=n}\|(PE_{n}P)^{k_{1}}\|\|PE_{n}^{\prime}\|\|E_{n}^{\prime k_{1}^{\prime}-2}\|\|E_{n}^{\prime}P\|\|(PE_{n}P)^{k_{2}}\|\cdots{}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad{}\cdots\|(PE_{n}P)^{k_{\ell}}\|\|PE_{n}^{\prime}\|\|E_{n}^{\prime k_{\ell}^{\prime}-2}\|\|E_{n}^{\prime}P\|\|(PE_{n}P)^{k_{\ell+1}}\|\vphantom{\mathop{\sum\cdots\sum}_{k_{1}+\cdots+k_{m+1}=n}}

\displaystyle\leq M^{\ell+1}K^{\ell}C_{n}^{2\ell}\mathop{\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}^{\ell+1},\,k^{\prime}\geq(2,\dots,2)}}_{|k|+|k^{\prime}|=n}\mu^{|k^{\prime}|-2\ell}

\displaystyle=M^{\ell+1}K^{\ell}C_{n}^{2\ell}\mathop{\sum_{k^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}^{\ell}}}_{|k^{\prime}|\leq n-2\ell-1}\mu^{|k^{\prime}|-\ell}\mathop{\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}^{\ell+1}}}_{|k|=n-|k^{\prime}|-\ell}1

\displaystyle=M^{\ell+1}K^{\ell}C_{n}^{2\ell}\mathop{\sum_{k^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}^{\ell}}}_{|k^{\prime}|\leq n-2\ell-1}\begin{pmatrix}\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0ptn-|k^{\prime}|-\ell-1\\
\ell\end{pmatrix}\mu^{|k^{\prime}|-\ell}

\displaystyle\leq M^{\ell+1}K^{\ell}C_{n}^{2\ell}\frac{n^{\ell}}{\ell!}\frac{1}{(1-\mu)^{\ell}}

\displaystyle\equiv b_{n,\ell}.

(C.15)

Finally, the correction
R_{n,2\ell+1}^{\prime}
is bounded as

\displaystyle\|R_{n,2\ell+1}^{\prime}\|

\displaystyle\leq\mathop{\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}^{\ell},\,k^{\prime}\geq(1,2,\dots,2,1)}}_{|k|+|k^{\prime}|=n}\|E_{n}^{\prime k_{1}^{\prime}-1}\|\|E_{n}^{\prime}P\|\|(PE_{n}P)^{k_{1}}\|\|PE_{n}^{\prime}\|\|E_{n}^{\prime k_{2}^{\prime}-2}\|\cdots

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad{}\cdots\|E_{n}^{\prime k_{\ell}^{\prime}-2}\|\|E_{n}^{\prime}P\|\|(PE_{n}P)^{k_{\ell}}\|\|PE_{n}^{\prime}\|\|E_{n}^{\prime k_{\ell+1}^{\prime}-1}\|\vphantom{\mathop{\sum\cdots\sum}_{k_{1}+\cdots+k_{m+1}=n}}

\displaystyle\leq M^{\ell}K^{\ell+1}C_{n}^{2\ell}\mathop{\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}^{\ell},\,k^{\prime}\geq(1,2,\dots,2,1)}}_{|k|+|k^{\prime}|=n}\mu^{|k^{\prime}|-2\ell}

\displaystyle=M^{\ell}K^{\ell+1}C_{n}^{2\ell}\sum_{k^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}^{\ell+1}}\mu^{|k^{\prime}|-\ell-1}\mathop{\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}^{\ell}}}_{|k|=n-|k^{\prime}|-\ell+1}1

\displaystyle=M^{\ell}K^{\ell+1}C_{n}^{2\ell}\sum_{k^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}^{\ell+1}}\begin{pmatrix}\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0ptn-|k^{\prime}|-\ell\\
\ell-1\end{pmatrix}\mu^{|k^{\prime}|-\ell-1}

\displaystyle\leq M^{\ell}K^{\ell+1}C_{n}^{2\ell}\frac{n^{\ell-1}}{(\ell-1)!}\frac{1}{(1-\mu)^{\ell+1}}

\displaystyle\equiv c_{n,\ell}.

(C.16)

Collecting all these bounds, the correction to the leading contribution
(PE_{n}P)^{n}
in Eq. (
C.2
) is bounded by

\displaystyle\Biggl\|\sum_{\ell=1}^{\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor}R_{n,2\ell}+\sum_{\ell=1}^{\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor}R_{n,2\ell}^{\prime}+\sum_{\ell=1}^{\lfloor\frac{n-1}{2}\rfloor}R_{n,2\ell+1}+\sum_{\ell=1}^{\lfloor\frac{n-1}{2}\rfloor}R_{n,2\ell+1}^{\prime}+E_{n}^{\prime n}\Biggr\|

\displaystyle\qquad\leq 2\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}a_{n,\ell}+\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}b_{n,\ell}+\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}c_{n,\ell}+K\mu^{n}

\displaystyle\qquad=M\left[\left(1+\frac{K}{1-\mu}C_{n}\right)^{2}\mathrm{e}^{\frac{MK}{1-\mu}nC_{n}^{2}}-1\right]+K\mu^{n}

\displaystyle\qquad=\mathcal{O}(1/n),\vphantom{\Bigl(}

(C.17)

since
C_{n}=\mathcal{O}(1/n)
by Eq. (
C.10
).
This proves the lemma.
∎
