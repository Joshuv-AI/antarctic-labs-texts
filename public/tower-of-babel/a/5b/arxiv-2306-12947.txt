Complex Weyl symbols of metaplectic operators: An elementary approach
Benjamin Cahen

Abstract
. We give explicit formulas for the Berezin symbols and the complex Weyl symbols of the metaplectic representation operators by using the holomorphic representations of the Jacobi group. Then we recover some known formulas for the symbols of the metaplectic operators in the classical Weyl calculus, in particular for the classical Weyl symbol of the exponential of an operator whose Weyl symbol is a quadratic form.



2000 Mathematics Subject Classification
22E45; 22E70; 81R05; 81S10; 81R30.

1.
Introduction

The metaplectic representation (also called oscillator representation or Weil representation) is
a projective unitary representation of the symplectic group
Sp(n,{\mathbb{R}})
which was first investigated by I. E. Segal,
D. Shale and A. Weil, see for instance
[
21
]
and its references. The metaplectic representation plays an important role in very different aeras of mathematics such as number theory (automorphic forms) and mathematical physics (quantum mechanics).

For the computations, it is sometimes convenient to realize
Sp(n,{\mathbb{R}})
as a subgroup
S
of
SU(n,n)
, see
[
21
]
, p. 175. The group
S
acts naturally
on the
(2n+1)
-dimensional
(real) Heisenberg group
H_{n}
and then on the generic representations (the non-degenerated unitary irreducible representations) of
H_{n}
on the Fock space
\mathcal{F}
.
Let
k\cdot\rho
denote the action of

k\in S
on the generic representation
\rho
of
H_{n}
.
Then
k\cdot\rho

and
\rho
are unitarily equivalent representations and there exists a unitary operator
\sigma(k)
on
\mathcal{F}
(defined up to a unit scalar) such that

(1.1)

(k\cdot\rho)(h)\sigma(k)=\sigma(k)\rho(h)

for each
h\in H_{n}
. The map
\sigma
is thus the metaplectic representation of
S
.

One can find in the literature various methods to construct the metaplectic representation of
S
, that is, to obtain explicit formulas for
\sigma(k)
,
k\in S
,
see for instance
[
21
]
and
[
28
]
. However, as mentioned in
[
30
]
, p. 533, the most direct method is to use the holomorphic representations of the (multi-dimensional) Jacobi group
G:=H_{n}\rtimes S
. Here we apply this method in full details in order to get explicit expressions for the kernels of the metaplectic operators
\sigma(k)
and then for the Berezin symbols of
\sigma(k)
. Note that this method also works for the harmonic representation of

SU(p,q)
, see
[
15
]
.

The complex Weyl calculus
W_{0}
is the correspondence between operators on
\mathcal{F}
and functions on
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
obtained by translating the usual Weyl correspondence
(see
[
21
]
,
[
26
]
) by means of the Bargmann transform. It is known that
W_{0}

is the unitary component in the polar decomposition of the Berezin correspondence on
\mathcal{F}
, see
[
29
]
,
[
11
]
.

In the present paper, we give explicit formulas for the complex Weyl symbols of the metaplectic operators. More precisely, we compute
W_{0}(\sigma(k))
for
k\in S
and
W_{0}(d\sigma(X))

for
X
in the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{s}
of
S
. Our method is quite elementary, let us describe it briefly. For any operator
A
on
\mathcal{F}
,
W_{0}(A)
can be expressed by an integral formula involving the kernel of
A
. This allows us to reduce the computation of
W_{0}(d\sigma(X))
to that of some Gaussian integral.

As an immediate consequence, we recover some known formulas for
W_{1}(\sigma^{\prime}(g))
,
g\in Sp(n,{\mathbb{R}})
and

W_{1}(d\sigma^{\prime}(X))
,
X\in sp(n,{\mathbb{R}})
where
\sigma^{\prime}
denotes the metaplectic representation of
Sp(n,{\mathbb{R}})
and
W_{1}
the inverse map of the classical Weyl correspondence,
[
21
]
,
[
16
]
. In particular, since
W_{1}(d\sigma^{\prime}(X))
is a quadratic form on
{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
for each
X\in sp(n,{\mathbb{R}})
, we recover a formula of
[
27
]
for the Weyl symbol of the exponential of an operator having a quadratic form as Weyl symbol. A similar formula for the star exponential
of a quadratic form for the Moyal star product can be found in
[
6
]
. Note that these formulas were then established in
[
27
]
and
[
6
]
by solving some differential systems, see also
[
17
]
. Note also that the study of the Weyl symbol of the function of an operator whose Weyl symbol is quadratic is still a subject of active research, see for instance

[
19
]
and references therein.

The plan of this paper is as follows. In Section
2
, we review some generalities
about the non-degenerated unitary irreducible representations of
H_{n}
on the Fock space
and about the Berezin correspondence. In Section
3
, we introduce the complex Weyl correspondence and we emphasize its connection with the Berezin correspondence and also with
the classical Weyl correspondence. In Section
4
, we consider the metaplectic representation
\sigma
and we give a functional equation satisfied by the kernel of
\sigma(k)
for
k\in S
.
Section
5
is devoted to a short presentation of the (multi-dimensional) Jacobi group

G=H_{n}\rtimes S
and its holomorphic representations
[
9
]
. We deduce from the
formula for the Berezin symbol of a holomorphic representation operator
\pi(h,k)
given in

[
12
]
a functional equation for the kernel of
\pi(id,k)
which is used in Section
6
to find formula for the kernel of
\sigma(k)
. From this, we derive in Section
7
a formula for
W_{0}(\sigma(k))
(
k\in S
). Finally, we compute
W_{0}(d\sigma(X))
(
X\in{\mathfrak{s}}
) in Section
8
and we relate the results to those of
[
27
]
and
[
6
]
.

2.
Heisenberg group: Berezin quantization

In this section, we first review some general facts on the
the Bargmann-Fock
model for the unitary irreducible (non-degenerated)
representations of the Heisenberg group, see
[
21
]
. We follow the presentation
of
[
13
]
, see also
[
14
]
.

For each
z,\,w\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
, we denote
zw:=\sum_{k=1}^{n}z_{k}w_{k}
. For each
z,z^{\prime},w,w^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
, let

\omega((z,w),(z^{\prime},w^{\prime}))=\tfrac{i}{2}(zw^{\prime}-z^{\prime}w).

Then the
(2n+1)
-dimensional real Heisenberg group is

H:=\{((z,{\bar{z}}),c)\,:\,z\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n},c\in{\mathbb{R}}\}

endowed with the
multiplication law

((z,{\bar{z}}),c)\cdot((z^{\prime},{\bar{z}^{\prime}}),c^{\prime})=((z+z^{\prime},{\bar{z}}+{\bar{z}^{\prime}}),c+c^{\prime}+\tfrac{1}{2}\omega((z,{\bar{z}}),(z^{\prime},{\bar{z}^{\prime}}))).

Let
\lambda>0
. By the Stone-von Neumann
theorem, there exists a unique (up to unitary equivalence) unitary
irreducible representation
\rho_{\lambda}
of
H_{n}
whose restriction to the center
of
H_{n}
is the character
(0,c)\rightarrow e^{i\lambda c}

[
32
]
.
The Bargmann-Fock realization of
\rho_{\lambda}
is defined as follows
[
3
]
.

Let
{\mathcal{F}}_{\lambda}
be the Hilbert space of all holomorphic functions
f

on
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
such that

\|f\|^{2}_{{\mathcal{F}}_{\lambda}}:=\int_{{\mathbb{C}}^{n}}|f(z)|^{2}\,e^{-\lambda|z|^{2}/2}\,d\mu_{\lambda}(z)<+\infty

where

d\mu_{\lambda}(z):=(2\pi)^{-n}{\lambda}^{n}\,dm(z)
. Here
z=x+iy
with
x
and
y
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
and
dm(z):=dx\,dy
is the standard Lebesgue measure on
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
.

Then

({\rho}_{\lambda}(h)f)(z)=\exp\left(i\lambda c_{0}+\tfrac{\lambda}{2}{\bar{z}_{0}}z-\tfrac{\lambda}{4}|z_{0}|^{2}\right)\,f(z-z_{0})

for each
h=(z_{0},c_{0})\in H_{n}
and
z\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
.

For each
z\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
, consider the
coherent state

e_{z}(w)=\exp(\lambda{\bar{z}}w/2)
. Then we have the reproducing property

f(z)=\langle f,e_{z}\rangle_{{\mathcal{F}}_{\lambda}}
for each
f\in{\mathcal{F}}_{\lambda}
.

We can introduce the Berezin
calculus on
{\mathcal{F}}_{\lambda}

[
7
]
,
[
8
]
,
[
11
]
.
The Berezin (covariant) symbol of an operator
A
on
{\mathcal{F}}_{\lambda}
is the
function
S_{\lambda}(A)
defined on
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
by

S_{\lambda}(A)(z):=\frac{\langle A\,e_{z}\,,\,e_{z}\rangle_{{\mathcal{F}}_{\lambda}}}{\langle e_{z}\,,\,e_{z}\rangle_{{\mathcal{F}}_{\lambda}}}

and the double Berezin symbol
s_{\lambda}
is defined by

s_{\lambda}(A)(z,w):=\frac{\langle A\,e_{w}\,,\,e_{z}\rangle_{{\mathcal{F}}_{\lambda}}}{\langle e_{w}\,,\,e_{z}\rangle_{{\mathcal{F}}_{\lambda}}}

for each
(z,w)\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\times{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
such that

\langle e_{w}\,,\,e_{z}\rangle_{{\mathcal{F}}_{\lambda}}\not=0
.

Note that
s_{\lambda}(A)(z,w)
is holomorphic in the variable
z
and anti-holomorphic in the variable
w
, then
s_{\lambda}(A)
is determined by its restriction to the diagonal of
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\times{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
, that is, by
S_{\lambda}(A)
. Moreover, the operator
A
can be recovered from
s_{\lambda}(A)
as follows. We have

\displaystyle A\,f(z)

\displaystyle=\langle A\,f\,,\,e_{z}\rangle_{{\mathcal{F}}_{\lambda}}=\langle f\,,\,A^{\ast}\,e_{z}\rangle_{{\mathcal{F}}_{\lambda}}

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{C}}^{n}}\,f(w)\overline{A^{\ast}\,e_{z}(w)}\,e^{-\lambda|w|^{2}/2}\,d\mu_{\lambda}(w)

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{C}}^{n}}\,f(w)\overline{\langle A^{\ast}\,e_{z},e_{w}\rangle}\,e^{-\lambda|w|^{2}/2}\,d\mu_{\lambda}(w)

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{C}}^{n}}\,f(w)\,s_{\lambda}(A)(z,w)\langle e_{w},e_{z}\rangle\,e^{-\lambda|w|^{2}/2}\,d\mu_{\lambda}(w).

In particular, we see that the map
A\rightarrow S_{\lambda}(A)
is injective and that the
kernel of
A
is the function

(2.1)

k_{A}(z,w)=\langle Ae_{w},e_{z}\rangle_{{\mathcal{F}}_{\lambda}}=s_{\lambda}(A)(z,w)\langle e_{w},e_{z}\rangle_{{\mathcal{F}}_{\lambda}}.

The map
S_{\lambda}
is a bounded operator from the
space
{\mathcal{L}}_{2}({\mathcal{F}}_{\lambda})
of all Hilbert-Schmidt operators
on
{\mathcal{F}}_{\lambda}
(endowed with the
Hilbert-Schmidt norm) to
L^{2}({\mathbb{C}}^{n},\mu_{\lambda})
which
is one-to-one and has dense range
[
33
]
. Let us introduce the Berezin transform which will be needed later. Let
S_{\lambda}^{\ast}
be the adjoint operator of
S_{\lambda}
.
Then
the Berezin transform is the operator
B_{\lambda}

on
L^{2}({\mathbb{C}}^{n},\mu_{\lambda})
defined by
B_{\lambda}:=S_{\lambda}S_{\lambda}^{\ast}
.
We have the integral formula

(B_{\lambda}f)(z)=\int_{{\mathbb{C}}^{n}}\,f(w)\,e^{-\lambda|z-w|^{2}/2}\,d\mu_{\lambda}(w),

see
[
7
]
,
[
8
]
,
[
33
]
.
Note also that we have
B_{\lambda}=\exp(\Delta/2\lambda)
where

\Delta=4\sum_{k=1}^{n}\partial^{2}/\partial z_{k}\partial{\bar{z}}_{k}
,
see
[
33
]
,
[
29
]
.

3.
Complex Weyl correspondence for Heisenberg group

The complex Weyl correspondence can be constructed from a
Stratonovich-Weyl quantizer

see
[
31
]
,
[
23
]
,
[
13
]
and
[
1
]
, Example 2.2 and Example 4.2.

Let
R_{0}
be the parity operator on
{\mathcal{F}}_{\lambda}
defined by

(R_{0}f)(z)=2^{n}f(-z).

Then we define

\Omega_{0}(z):=\rho_{\lambda}((z,{\bar{z}}),0)R_{0}\rho_{\lambda}((z,{\bar{z}}),0)^{-1}

for each
z\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
.
By an easy computation we get

(3.1)

(\Omega_{0}(z)f)(w)=2^{n}\exp\left({\lambda}(w{\bar{z}}-|z|^{2})\right)f(2z-w)

for each
z,w\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
and
f\in{\mathcal{F}}_{\lambda}
.
The map
\Omega_{0}
is called a
Stratonovich-Weyl quantizer
.
For each trace-class operator
A
on
{\mathcal{F}}_{\lambda}
, we define

W_{0}(A)(z):=\Tr(A\Omega_{0}(z))

for each
z\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
.

Recall that we denote by
k_{A}
the kernel
of the trace-class (or more generally Hilbert-Schmidt) operator
A
on

{\mathcal{F}}_{\lambda}
, see Section
2
.
We have the following result, see
[
14
]
,
[
13
]
,
[
1
]
.

Proposition 3.1
.

For each trace-class operator
A
on
{\mathcal{F}}_{\lambda}

and each
z\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
, we have

(3.2)

W_{0}(A)(z)=2^{n}\int_{{\mathbb{C}}^{n}}k_{A}(w,2z-w)\exp\left({\lambda}\left(-z{\bar{z}}+z{\bar{w}}-\tfrac{1}{2}w{\bar{w}}\right)\right)d\mu_{\lambda}(w).

and, equivalently, on a more symmetric form

(3.3)

W_{0}(A)(z)=2^{n}\int_{{\mathbb{C}}^{n}}k_{A}(z+w,z-w)\exp\left(\tfrac{\lambda}{2}\left(-z{\bar{z}}-w{\bar{w}}+z{\bar{w}}-{\bar{z}}w\right)\right)d\mu_{\lambda}(w).

These integral formulas allow us to extend
W_{0}
to operators
on
{\mathcal{F}}_{\lambda}
which are not necessarily trace-class, for instance Hilbert-Schmidt operators. It is known that
W_{0}:{\mathcal{L}}_{2}({\mathcal{F}}_{\lambda})\rightarrow L^{2}({\mathbb{C}}^{n},\mu_{\lambda})
is the unitary part in the polar decomposition of
S_{\lambda}
, that is we have
S_{\lambda}=B_{\lambda}^{1/2}W_{0}
, see
[
29
]
, Theorem 6,
[
11
]
,
[
14
]
.

Now, with the aim of linking
W_{0}
to the classical Weyl correspondence, we consider
another realization of the unitary irreducible representation of
H_{n}
with central character

(0,c)\rightarrow e^{i\lambda c}
, namely the Schrödinger representation
\rho^{\prime}_{\lambda}

defined on
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
by

(\rho^{\prime}_{\lambda}((a+ib,a-ib),c)\phi)(x)=\exp\left(i\lambda(c-bx+\tfrac{1}{2}ab)\right)\,\phi(x-a)

for each
a,b,x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.

An (unitary) intertwining operator between
\rho_{\lambda}
and
\rho^{\prime}_{\lambda}
is the Bargmann
transform
\mathcal{B}:L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})\rightarrow{\mathcal{F}}_{\lambda}
defined by

({\mathcal{B}}f)(z)=\left(\tfrac{\lambda}{\pi}\right)^{n/4}\,\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\,\exp\left(-\tfrac{\lambda}{4}z^{2}+\lambda zx-\tfrac{\lambda}{2}x^{2}\right)\,\phi(x)\,dx,

see
[
21
]
,
[
11
]
.

Starting from the parity operator
R_{1}
on
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
defined by

(R_{1}\phi)(x)=2^{n}\phi(-x),

we can define the Stratonovich-Weyl quantizer
\Omega_{1}
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
by

\Omega_{1}(a,b):=\rho^{\prime}_{\lambda}((a+ib,a-ib),0)R_{1}\rho^{\prime}_{\lambda}((a+ib,a-ib),0)^{-1}

or, equivalently, by

(3.4)

(\Omega_{1}(a,b)\phi)(x)=2^{n}\exp\left(2i{\lambda}b(a-x)\right)\phi(2a-x)

for each
\phi\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
.
Then, for each trace-class operator
A
on
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
, we define the function

W_{1}(A)
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
by

W_{1}(A)(x,y):=\Tr(A\Omega_{1}(x,y))

for each
x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.

On the other hand, recall that the classical Weyl correspondence on
{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
is defined as follows
[
21
]
,
[
26
]
.
For each function
f
in the Schwartz space
{\mathcal{S}}({\mathbb{R}}^{2n})
, we define the operator
{\mathcal{W}}(f)
acting on the Hilbert
space
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
by

(3.5)

({\mathcal{W}}(f)\phi)(x)={(2\pi)}^{-n}\,\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}}\,e^{iyt}f(x+\tfrac{1}{2}y,t)\,\phi(x+y)\,dy\,dt.

Consider the Fourier transform
{\mathcal{F}}_{2}f
of
f\in{\mathcal{S}}({\mathbb{R}}^{2n})
with respect to the second variable

({\mathcal{F}}_{2}f)(x,y)={(2\pi)}^{-n/2}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}e^{-iyt}f(x,t)\,dt.

Then we can write

({\mathcal{W}}(f)\phi)(x)={(2\pi)}^{-n/2}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}({\mathcal{F}}_{2}f)(\tfrac{1}{2}(x+y),x-y)\phi(y)dy

and we can see that the kernel of
{\mathcal{W}}(f)
is

k_{{\mathcal{W}}(f)}(x,y)={(2\pi)}^{-n/2}({\mathcal{F}}_{2}f)(\tfrac{1}{2}(x+y),x-y).

Lemma 3.2
.

For each
f\in{\mathcal{S}}({\mathbb{R}}^{2n})
such that
{\mathcal{W}}(f)
is trace-class we have

\Tr(\Omega_{1}(a,b){\mathcal{W}}(f))=f(a,\lambda b)

for each
a,b\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.

Proof.

Let
f\in{\mathcal{S}}({\mathbb{R}}^{2n})
and
a,b\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. Then we have

\displaystyle(\Omega_{1}(a,b){\mathcal{W}}(f)

\displaystyle\phi)(x)=2^{n}\exp\left(2i{\lambda}b(a-x)\right)\,({\mathcal{W}}(f)\phi)(2a-x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2^{n}\exp\left(2i{\lambda}b(a-x)\right)\,\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}k_{{\mathcal{W}}(f)}(2a-x,y)\phi(y)\,dy.

Thus the kernel of
\Omega_{1}(a,b){\mathcal{W}}(f)
is

k^{\prime}(x,y):=2^{n}\exp\left(2i{\lambda}b(a-x)\right)k_{{\mathcal{W}}(f)}(2a-x,y)

and by Mercer’s theorem we have

\displaystyle\Tr(\Omega_{1}(a,b)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{W}}(f))=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}k^{\prime}(x,x)\,dx

\displaystyle=2^{n}\,\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\exp\left(2i{\lambda}b(a-x)\right)k_{{\mathcal{W}}(f)}(2a-x,x)\,dx

\displaystyle={(2\pi)}^{-n/2}2^{n}\,\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\exp\left(2i{\lambda}b(a-x)\right)({\mathcal{F}}_{2}f)(a,2a-2x)\,dx

\displaystyle={(2\pi)}^{-n/2}\,\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\exp\left(i{\lambda}bx\right)({\mathcal{F}}_{2}f)(a,x)\,dx

\displaystyle=f(a,\lambda b),

by the Fourier inversion theorem.
∎

As an immediate consequence of this lemma, we get the following proposition.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
f\in{\mathcal{S}}({\mathbb{R}}^{2n})
such that
{\mathcal{W}}(f)

is trace-class. Then, for each
x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, we have

W_{1}({\mathcal{W}}(f))(x,y)=f(x,\lambda y).

If, in particular, we take
\lambda=1
, we see that
W_{1}
and
\mathcal{W}
are inverse
to each other.

We can now specify the connection between
W_{0}
and
W_{1}
, hence between
W_{0}
and
\mathcal{W}
.

Proposition 3.4
.

For each trace-class operator
A
on
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
and each

a,b\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, we have

W_{1}(A)(a,b)=W_{0}({\mathcal{B}}A{\mathcal{B}}^{-1})(a+ib).

Proof.

We can easily verify that
{\mathcal{B}}R_{1}=R_{0}{\mathcal{B}}
. Let

A
be a trace-class operator on
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
and let

a,b\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. Recall that
\mathcal{B}
intertwines
\rho_{\lambda}
and

\rho^{\prime}_{\lambda}
. Then we have

\displaystyle W_{1}(A)(a,b)=

\displaystyle\Tr(A\Omega_{1}(a,b))=\Tr(A\rho^{\prime}_{\lambda}((a+ib,a-ib),0)R_{1}\rho^{\prime}_{\lambda}((a+ib,a-ib),0)^{-1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Tr(A\rho^{\prime}_{\lambda}((a+ib,a-ib),0){\mathcal{B}}^{-1}R_{0}{\mathcal{B}}\rho^{\prime}_{\lambda}((a+ib,a-ib),0)^{-1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Tr(A{\mathcal{B}}^{-1}\rho_{\lambda}((a+ib,a-ib),0)R_{0}\rho_{\lambda}((a+ib,a-ib),0)^{-1}{\mathcal{B}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Tr({\mathcal{B}}A{\mathcal{B}}^{-1}\Omega_{0}(a+ib))=W_{0}({\mathcal{B}}A{\mathcal{B}}^{-1})(a+ib).

∎

Of course, Proposition
3.4
can be extended to operators which are not
necessarily of trace-class.

4.
The metaplectic representation

Here we consider the group
S:=Sp(n,{\mathbb{C}})\cap SU(n,n)

which is isomorphic to
Sp(n,{\mathbb{R}})
via the map
M\rightarrow UMU^{-1}

where
U:=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}I_{n}&iI_{n}\\
I_{n}&-iI_{n}\end{smallmatrix}\right)
, see
[
21
]
, p. 175.
Then
S

consists of all matrices

k=\begin{pmatrix}P&Q\\
{\bar{Q}}&{\bar{P}}\end{pmatrix},\quad P,Q\in M_{n}({\mathbb{C}}),\quad PP^{\ast}-QQ^{\ast}=I_{n},\quad PQ^{t}=QP^{t}.

Note that we also have

P^{\ast}P-Q^{t}{\bar{Q}}=I_{n},\quad\quad P^{\ast}Q=Q^{t}{\bar{P}}.

This implies, in particular, that
P^{-1}Q
and
{\bar{Q}}P^{-1}
are symmetric.

The group
S
acts on
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
by

k(z,{\bar{z}})=(Pz+Q{\bar{z}},{\bar{Q}}z+{\bar{P}}{\bar{z}})

where
k=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}P&Q\\
{\bar{Q}}&{\bar{P}}\end{smallmatrix}\right)
. Then
\omega
is invariant for the action of
S
,
that is, we have

\omega(k(z,{\bar{z}}),k(z^{\prime},{\bar{z}^{\prime}}))=\omega((z,{\bar{z}}),(z^{\prime},{\bar{z}^{\prime}}))

for each
z,z^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
. We will denote

kz=Pz+Q{\bar{z}}
.

Thus
S
also acts on
H_{n}
by
k\cdot((z,{\bar{z}}),c)=(k(z,{\bar{z}}),c)
.

Let
\lambda>0
and
\rho:=\rho_{\lambda}
. For each
k\in S
, we define

\rho_{k}
by
\rho_{k}(h):=\rho(k\cdot h)
for each
h\in H_{n}
. Since
k\in S
acts on
H_{n}

as a group isomorphism, we see that
\rho_{k}
is also a generic representation of
H_{n}
.
Moreover, for each
h=((0,0),c)

in the center of
H_{n}
, we have
\rho_{k}(h)=\rho(h)
. Hence, by the Stone-von Neumann
theorem,
\rho_{k}
and
\rho
are unitarily equivalent, that is, there exists a unitary operator

A_{k}
of
{\mathcal{F}}_{\lambda}
(defined up to a unit complex number) such that

(4.1)

\rho_{k}(h)=A_{k}\rho(h)A_{k}^{-1}

for each
h\in H_{n}
.

Now we express Eq.
4.1
in terms of kernels.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
k\in S
. Then the operator
A_{k}

on
{\mathcal{F}}_{\lambda}
satisfies Eq.
4.1
if and only if
its kernel
b_{k}(z,w):=k_{A_{k}}(z,w)
satisfies the relation

(4.2)

\exp\left(-\tfrac{\lambda}{4}|kz_{0}|^{2}+\tfrac{\lambda}{2}\overline{kz_{0}}z\right)b_{k}(z-kz_{0},w)=\exp\left(-\tfrac{\lambda}{4}|z_{0}|^{2}-\tfrac{\lambda}{2}\bar{w}z_{0}\right)b_{k}(z,w+z_{0})

and

Proof.

Let
k\in S
and

. We have

(A_{k}f)(z)=\int_{{\mathbb{C}}^{n}}b_{k}(z,w)f(w)\,e^{-\lambda|w|^{2}/2}\,d\mu_{\lambda}(w).

Then, on the one hand, we get

\displaystyle\rho_{\lambda}(k\cdot h)(A_{k}f)(z)=

\displaystyle\exp\left(-\tfrac{\lambda}{4}|kz_{0}|^{2}+\tfrac{\lambda}{2}\overline{kz_{0}}z\right)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{C}}^{n}}b_{k}(z-kz_{0},w)f(w)\,e^{-\lambda|w|^{2}/2}\,d\mu_{\lambda}(w).

On the other hand, we have

that the kernels of

The

under the integral sign,
we get

\frac{\partial f}{\partial z_{j}}=\tfrac{\lambda}{2}\int_{{\mathbb{C}}^{n}}{\bar{w}_{j}}e^{\lambda z{\bar{w}}/2}f(w)e^{-\lambda|w|^{2}/2}\,d\mu_{\lambda}(w)

for each

and,
by differentiating again, we also obtain

\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial z_{j}\partial z_{k}}=\left(\tfrac{\lambda}{2}\right)^{2}\int_{{\mathbb{C}}^{n}}{\bar{w}_{j}}{\bar{w}_{k}}e^{{\lambda}z{\bar{w}}/2}f(w)e^{-\lambda|w|^{2}/2}\,d\mu_{\lambda}(w)

for each

This allows us to compute

and to get the desired result.
∎

Lemma 7.1

Write

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\exp(-xAx+zx)\,dx=(\Det A)^{-1/2}\pi^{n/2}\exp\left(\tfrac{1}{4}z(A^{-1}z)\right)

Lemma 7.2
.

(1)

Let

\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}-a&I_{n}+p^{t}\\

\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}\alpha&\beta\\
\gamma&\delta\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)

\begin{pmatrix}a&I_{n}-p^{t}\\

\gamma&\delta\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}a&p^{t}-I_{n}\\
I_{n}-p&d\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}4\delta-a&3I_{n}-4\gamma-p^{t}\\
3I_{n}-4\beta-p&4\alpha+d\end{pmatrix}.

(2)

Take
a={\bar{Q}}P^{-1}
,
d=P^{-1}Q
and
p=P^{-1}
with

{\bar{Q}}&{\bar{P}}\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\in S

\tfrac{1}{2}J(k-I_{2n})(k+I_{2n})^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}\delta&\tfrac{1}{2}I_{n}-\gamma\\
\tfrac{1}{2}I_{n}-\beta&\alpha\end{pmatrix}.

(3)

Let
k=\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}P&Q\\
{\bar{Q}}&{\bar{P}}\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\in S

\Det\begin{pmatrix}-{\bar{Q}}P^{-1}&I_{n}+(P^{t})^{-1}\\
I_{n}+P^{-1}&P^{-1}Q\end{pmatrix}=(-1)^{n}(\Det P)^{-1}\Det(k+I_{2n}).

Proof.

(1) By writing

\begin{pmatrix}\alpha&\beta\\
\gamma&\delta\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}-a&I_{n}+p^{t}\\
I_{n}+p&d\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}-a&I_{n}+p^{t}\\
I_{n}+p&d\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\alpha&\beta\\
\gamma&\delta\end{pmatrix}=I_{2n}

we obtain the series of equations

\displaystyle\alpha a=

\displaystyle\beta(I_{n}+p)-I_{n}\quad;\quad\gamma a=\delta(I_{n}+p);

\displaystyle\beta d=

\displaystyle-\alpha(I_{n}+p^{t})\quad;\quad\delta d=I_{n}-\gamma(I_{n}+p^{t});

\displaystyle a\alpha=

\displaystyle(I_{n}+p^{t})\gamma-I_{n}\quad;\quad a\beta=(I_{n}+p^{t})\delta;

\displaystyle d\gamma=

\displaystyle-(I_{n}+p)\alpha\quad;\quad d\delta=I_{n}-(I_{n}+p)\beta.

By using these equations, we obtain firstly

\begin{pmatrix}\alpha&\beta\\
\gamma&\delta\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}a&-I_{n}+p^{t}\\
I_{n}-p&d\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}2\beta-I_{n}&-2\alpha\\
2\delta&I_{n}-2\gamma\end{pmatrix}

and, secondly,

\begin{pmatrix}a&I_{n}-p^{t}\\
-I_{n}+p&d\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}2\beta-I_{n}&-2\alpha\\
2\delta&I_{n}-2\gamma\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}4\delta-a&3I_{n}-4\gamma-p^{t}\\
3I_{n}-4\beta-p&4\alpha+d\end{pmatrix}.

(2)-(3) First we have

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}-{\bar{Q}}P^{-1}&I_{n}+(P^{t})^{-1}\\
I_{n}+P^{-1}&P^{-1}Q\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}-P&-Q\\
0&I_{n}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}{\bar{Q}}&{\bar{Q}}P^{-1}Q+I_{n}+(P^{t})^{-1}\\
-I_{n}-P&-Q\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}{\bar{Q}}&I_{n}+{\bar{P}}\\
-I_{n}-P&-Q\end{pmatrix}=J(k+I_{2n})

since

{\bar{Q}}P^{-1}Q+(P^{t})^{-1}=({\bar{Q}}P^{-1}QP^{t}+I_{n})(P^{t})^{-1}=({\bar{Q}}Q^{t}+I_{n})(P^{t})^{-1}={\bar{P}}.

On the one hand, passing to the determinant, we obtain (3) and, on the other hand, we
deduce that

\begin{pmatrix}-P&-Q\\
0&I_{n}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha&\beta\\
\gamma&\delta\end{pmatrix}\,J(k+I_{2n}).

This implies that

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\delta&\tfrac{1}{2}I_{n}-\gamma\\
\tfrac{1}{2}I_{n}-\beta&\alpha\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}0&I_{n}\\
I_{n}&0\end{pmatrix}-J\begin{pmatrix}\alpha&\beta\\
\gamma&\delta\end{pmatrix}J

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}0&I_{n}\\
I_{n}&0\end{pmatrix}-J\begin{pmatrix}-P&-Q\\
0&I_{n}\end{pmatrix}(k+I_{2n})^{-1}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}0&I_{n}\\
I_{n}&0\end{pmatrix}(k+I_{2n})-J\begin{pmatrix}-P&-Q\\

∎

about the Weyl symbol of the exponential of an operator whose Weyl symbol is a quadratic form
q_{M}(x,y)
.

8.2.
Star exponentials

The preceding results can be reformulated in terms of star exponentials for the Moyal star product. Let us recall that the notion of star product was introduced in
[
4
]
in order to interpret quantum mechanics as deformation of classical mechanics. Roughly speaking, a star product on a Poisson manifold
M
is a formal deformation

(f,g)\rightarrow f\ast g=f.g+t\{f,g\}+\sum_{l\geq 2}t^{l}C_{l}(f,g)
of the pointwise multiplication of

C^{\infty}(M)
. An important problem is then the computation of the star exponential

\exp_{\ast}(f)=\sum_{l\geq 0}\frac{1}{l!}f^{\ast,l}
for some functions
f
, see
[
2
]
,

[
22
]
. Note that such computations are usually done by solving some differential systems,
see
[
5
]
,
[
6
]
. Here we only consider the Moyal star product on
{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
defined as follows.

Take coordinates

(p,q)
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}\cong{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
and let

x=(p,q)
. Then one has
x_{i}=p_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq n
and
x_{i}=q_{i-n}
for
n+1\leq i\leq 2n
. For
u,v\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{2n})
, define
P^{0}(u,v):=uv
,

P^{1}(u,v):=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left(\frac{\partial u}{\partial p_{k}}\frac{\partial v}{\partial q_{k}}-\frac{\partial u}{\partial q_{k}}\frac{\partial v}{\partial p_{k}}\right)=\sum_{1\leq i,j\leq n}\Lambda^{ij}{\partial_{x_{i}}}u{\partial_{x_{j}}}v

(the Poisson brackets) and, more generally, for
l\geq 2
,

P^{l}(u,v):=\sum_{1\leq i_{1},\ldots,i_{l},j_{1},\ldots,j_{l}\leq n}\Lambda^{i_{1}j_{1}}\Lambda^{i_{2}j_{2}}\cdots\Lambda^{i_{l}j_{l}}\partial^{l}_{x_{i_{1}}\ldots x_{i_{l}}}u\,\partial^{l}_{x_{j_{1}}\ldots x_{j_{l}}}v.

Then the Moyal product
\ast
is the following formal deformation of the pointwise multiplication of
C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{2n})

u\ast v:=\sum_{l\geq 0}\frac{t^{l}}{l!}P^{l}(u,v)

where
t
is a formal parameter.

Let us restrict
\ast
to polynomials on
{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
(this is sufficient for our purpose) and take
t=-i/2
.
Then
\ast
induces an associative product on the polynomials also denoted by
\ast
.

On the other hand, the classical Weyl correspondence
{\mathcal{W}}
(see Section
3
)
can be extended to polynomials
[
26
]
. More precisely, if
f(p,q)=u(p)q^{\alpha}
where
u
is a polynomial on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
then we have

({\mathcal{W}}(f)\varphi)(p)=\left(i\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\right)^{\alpha}\left(u(p+\tfrac{1}{2}s)\,\varphi(p+s)\right)\Bigl|_{s=0},

see
[
34
]
.
Hence if
f
is a polynomial then
{\mathcal{W}}(f)
is a differential operator with polynomial coefficients. Moreover, we can show that
\ast
corresponds to the composition of operators in the Weyl quantization, that is, for each polynomials
f_{1},f_{2}
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
,
we have
{\mathcal{W}}(f_{1}\ast f_{2})={\mathcal{W}}(f_{1}){\mathcal{W}}(f_{2})
. Equivalently,
we also have
W_{1}(A_{1})\ast W_{1}(A_{2})=W_{1}(A_{1}A_{2})
for each differential operators
A_{1},A_{2}
with polynomial coefficients.

Let
M
be a real, symmetric
(2n)\times(2n)
matrix. Let
q_{M}
be the quadratic form on

{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
associated with
M
. Let
X=-2JM\in sp(n,{\mathbb{R}})
. Then by Proposition
8.3
we have
W_{1}(d\sigma^{\prime}(X))=-iq_{M}
, hence

\exp_{\ast}(-iq_{M})=\exp_{\ast}(W_{1}(d\sigma^{\prime}(X)))=W_{1}(\exp(d\sigma^{\prime}(X)))=W_{1}(\sigma^{\prime}(\exp(X)))

and

\exp_{\ast}(-iq_{M})(x,y)=(\Det(\cosh(JM))^{-1/2}\exp\left(i\begin{pmatrix}x&y\end{pmatrix}J\tanh(JM)\begin{pmatrix}x\\
y\end{pmatrix}\right).

This is precisely the equation given in
[
6
]
, Theorem 1. In particular, for
q_{M}(x,y)=t(x^{2}+y^{2})
,
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
, we have
JM=tJ
,
\cosh(JM)=\cos(t)I_{2n}
,

\tanh(JM)=\tan(t)J
and the preceding formula becomes

\exp_{\ast}(-it(x^{2}+y^{2}))=(\cos(t))^{-1/2}\exp(-i\tan(t)(x^{2}+y^{2})).

Up to mormalization, we recover the formula of
[
5
]
, Proposition 1.

Note that, in general, computations of star exponentials involve special functions, see

[
5
]
,
[
24
]
,
[
22
]
.

Strangely enough, it seems that the connection between the computation of the star exponential
of a quadratic form (for the Moyal product) and the computation of the Weyl symbol of the exponential of a differential operator whose Weyl symbol is a quadratic form
has not been mentioned in the literature.
