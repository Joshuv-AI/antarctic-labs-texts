Non-uniqueness of weak solutions for a logarithmically supercritical hyperdissipative Navier-Stokes system
Marco Romito, Francesco Triggiano

Abstract
. The existence of non-unique solutions of finite kinetic energy for the three dimensional Navier-Stokes equations is proved in the slightly supercritical hyper-dissipative setting introduced by Tao [ Tao10 ] . The result is based on the convex integration techniques of Buckmaster and Vicol [ BV19 ] , and extends Luo and Titi [ LT20 ] in the slightly supercritical setting. To be able to be closer to the threshold identified by Tao, we introduce the impulsed Beltrami flows, a variant of the intermittent Beltrami flows of Buckmaster and Vicol.



2020 Mathematics Subject Classification
Primary 35Q30; secondary 76D03, 76D05

1.
Introduction

In this work we consider the following hyperdissipative Navier-Stokes system on the 3-dimensional torus

(1.1)

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}u+\Div(u\otimes u)+\nu(-\Delta)^{\theta,\beta}u+\nabla p=0,\\
\Div(u)=0,\end{cases}

with periodic boundary conditions and zero spatial mean, where
\beta
and
\theta
are non-negative parameters, and the dissipative term is defined via Fourier transform
\mathcal{F}
by

\mathcal{F}[(-\Delta)^{\theta,\beta}u](k)=\mathbf{1}_{\{k\neq 0\}}(k)\frac{|k|^{2\theta}}{\log^{\beta}(10+|k|_{1})}\mathcal{F}(u)(k),\qquad\text{for all }k\in\mathbb{Z}^{3},

where
|k|
and
|k|_{1}
are the 2-norm and the 1-norm of
k
, respectively.

Well-posedness of a similar system has been widely studied in the last century. In particular, it is well known that global existence and uniqueness of sufficiently smooth solutions holds when
\beta=0
and
\theta\geq\frac{5}{4}

[
Lio69
,
KP02
]
.

On the critical threshold
\theta=\frac{5}{4}
, the same result has been proved by Tao
[
Tao10
]
and Barbato et al.
[
BMR15
]
when the dissipative term is slightly weakened by a Fourier multiplier that mimics a logarithmic behaviour raised to a sufficiently small power
\beta
.

When
\theta<\frac{5}{4}
it is well known that there are various open problems, such as uniqueness of Leray-Hopf solutions for classical NSE, i.e.
\theta=1
and
\beta=0
.

Furthermore, various negative results appeared during the last decade.
In particular, Luo et al.
[
LT20
]
proved non-uniqueness of global distributional solutions, less regular than Leray weak solutions
[
Ler34
]
, by means of the convex integration theory
[
BV19
,
BV20
,
DLS14
,
Ise18
,
MS18
]
.
The convex integration machinery has been used by Li et al.
[
LQZZ22
]
in order to prove non-uniqueness in suitable functional spaces also above the Lions exponent, i.e.
\theta\geq\frac{5}{4}
and
\beta=0
, but non-uniqueness is proved in the space
C_{t}L^{p}
for all
1\leq p<2
, thus in larger spaces than energy (in general below the Ladyzenskaja-Prodi-Serrin threshold).

In this paper we work at the critical threshold
\theta=\frac{5}{4}
, whose relevance can be also highlighted by making some scaling considerations. See
[
Kla10
]
for a more complete discussion on the topic.
Under the assumption
\beta=0
,
we recall that a functional space
\mathbf{X}
is said to be a critical space if
\|u\|_{\mathbf{X}}
is invariant under the following scaling transformation:

u(t,x)\to\lambda^{2\theta-1}u(\lambda^{2\theta}t,\lambda x),\qquad p(t,x)\to\lambda^{4\theta-2}p(\lambda^{2\theta}t,\lambda x).

Since its norm represents the kinetic energy, one important functional space is
\mathbf{X}=C_{t}L^{2}_{x}
, which is a critical space only if
\theta=\frac{5}{4}
.

In this work we show that the choice
\theta=\frac{5}{4}
and
\beta
large enough, that is weakening the dissipation by a logarithmic corrector, allows to prove a non-uniqueness result for distributional solutions to
Eq.

1.1
that are in the relevant functional space
C_{t}L^{2}_{x}
.
This non-uniqueness result in a logarithmic supercritical case is obtained by stretching as much as possible the convex integration scheme proposed in
[
BV19
,
LT20
]
.
In particular from a technical point of view, the proof is mainly based on combining new estimates for various Fourier multipliers, which allow to exploit the logarithmic corrector, and classical harmonic analysis results in some Orlicz spaces. Moreover, we also propose a new modification of the classical Beltrami flows to achieve the result by assuming a weaker condition on
\beta
. For more details see the discussion in
Remark

2.4
.

The paper is organized as follows. In
Section

2
we state and prove the main result by means of the iteration lemma. In
Section

3
we introduce and study various Fourier multipliers.
Section

4
is devoted to the proof of the iteration lemma, namely the inductive step which solves
Eq.

1.1
in the limit.

1.1.
Notations

To ease the notations, for any
\alpha\geq 0
and
p\in[1,\infty]
we denote

\displaystyle C_{t}L^{p}_{x}

\displaystyle=C([0,\infty),L^{p}(\mathbf{T}^{3},\mathbb{R}^{3})),

\displaystyle C_{t}\mathrm{L}(\mathrm{logL})^{\alpha}_{x}

\displaystyle=C([0,\infty),\mathrm{L}(\mathrm{logL})^{\alpha}(\mathbf{T}^{3},\mathbb{R}^{3})),

\displaystyle L^{\infty}_{t}L^{p}_{x}

\displaystyle=L^{\infty}([0,\infty),L^{p}(\mathbf{T}^{3},\mathbb{R}^{3})),

\displaystyle L^{\infty}_{t}\mathrm{L}(\mathrm{logL})^{\alpha}_{x}

\displaystyle=L^{\infty}([0,\infty),\mathrm{L}(\mathrm{logL})^{\alpha}(\mathbf{T}^{3},\mathbb{R}^{3})),

where
\mathbf{T}^{3}
denotes the 3-dimensional torus and
\mathrm{L}(\mathrm{logL})^{\alpha}
is the Orlicz space associated with the Young function
A(s)=s\log^{\alpha}(2+s)
(see
Definition

3.1
for the detailed definition).
We denote by
C_{w}(\mathbb{R},L^{2}(\mathbf{T}^{3},\mathbb{R}^{3}))
the space of weakly continuous functions with values within the Banach space
L^{2}(\mathbf{T}^{3},\mathbb{R}^{3})
, while
C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R},C^{\infty}(\mathbf{T}^{3},\mathbb{R}^{3}))
is the space of smooth compactly supported and spatially periodic functions. In addition, if
v\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R},C^{\infty}(\mathbf{T}^{3},\mathbb{R}^{3}))
we denote its time support by
\supp_{t}(v)
.
For any sub-interval
I\subset\mathbb{R}_{+}
, let
\mathbf{P}_{I}
be the operator defined by

\mathbf{P}_{I}g(x)=\sum_{|k|_{1}\in I}\mathcal{F}(g)(k)e^{ik\cdot x}.

We denote by
P_{H}
the Leray-Helmholtz projector, namely the projector onto the space of divergence-free vector fields.
For any
A\subset\mathbb{R}
and
\delta>0
, let
N_{\delta}(A)
be the
\delta
-neighborhood of A, namely

N_{\delta}(A)=\{z\in\mathbb{R}:\text{ there is }y\in A\text{ s.t. }|z-y|<\delta\}.

If
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
, then
x\times y
will denote the standard vector cross-product between
x
and
y
. Given
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
, we use the notations
a\lesssim b
and
a\approx b
if there exist
C,C_{1},C_{2}>0
such that
a\leq Cb
and
C_{1}a\leq b\leq C_{2}a
, respectively.

2.
Main result

We first recall the definition of weak solution to
Eq.

1.1
.

Definition 2.1
.

A vector field
v\in C_{w}(\mathbb{R},L^{2}(\mathbf{T}^{3},\mathbb{R}^{3}))
is a weak solution to
Eq.

1.1
if it is satisfied in a distributional sense, namely,

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbf{T}^{3}}(\partial_{t}\phi)\cdot v\,dx\,dt=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbf{T}^{3}}v\cdot(v\cdot\nabla)\phi-\big(\nu(-\Delta)^{\theta,\beta}\phi\big)\cdot v\,dx\,dt,

for all
\phi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R},C^{\infty}(\mathbf{T}^{3},\mathbb{R}^{3}))
such that
\Div(\phi(t,\cdot))=0
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
.

Theorem 2.2
.

For
\theta=\frac{5}{4}
and
\beta>\frac{29}{2}
, consider a smooth divergence-free vector field
u:[0,\infty)\times\mathbf{T}^{3}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
, with compact support in time and zero spatial-mean,

\int_{\mathbf{T}^{3}}u(t,x)dx=0.

Then, for all
\epsilon>0
, there exists a weak solution
v\in C(\mathbb{R},L^{2}(\mathbf{T}^{3},\mathbb{R}^{3}))
of
Eq.

1.1
with compact support in time and such that

\||\nabla|^{\frac{3}{2}}T_{M}(v-u)\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}L^{1}_{x}}\leq\epsilon,

where
T_{M}
and
|\nabla|^{\frac{3}{2}}
are the operators corresponding to the Fourier multipliers
M(k)=\mathbf{1}_{\{k\neq 0\}}(k)\log^{-\beta}(|k|_{1}+10)
and
m^{\prime}(k)=|k|^{\frac{3}{2}}
(see
Definition

3.3
).
In particular, there are infinitely many weak solutions with zero initial condition.

The result is shown by using the classical iterative procedure of the convex integration theory, i.e. following the groundbreaking work of Buckmaster and Vicol
[
BV19
]
.
Therefore, the proof is strongly based on the following lemma.

Lemma 2.3

(Iteration Lemma)
.

Fix
\theta=\frac{5}{4}
and
\beta>\frac{29}{2}
. let
(v_{q},p_{q},R_{q})
be a smooth solution to the approximate system

(2.1)

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}u+\Div(u\otimes u)+\nu(-\Delta)^{\frac{5}{4},\beta}u+\nabla p=\Div(R),\\
\Div(u)=0,\\
\Tr(R(t,x))=0,\qquad R(t,x)=R(t,x)^{T}\quad\text{for all }t,x,\end{cases}

such that

\|R_{q}\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}L^{1}_{x}}\leq\delta_{q+1}.

Then for every
\delta_{q+2}>0
, there exists a smooth solution
(v_{q+1},p_{q+1},R_{q+1})
of
Eq.

2.1
such that

(2.2)

\begin{gathered}\|R_{q+1}\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}L^{1}_{x}}\leq\delta_{q+2},\\
\supp_{t}(v_{q+1})\cup\supp_{t}(R_{q+1})\subset N_{\delta_{q+1}}(\supp_{t}(v_{q})\cup\supp_{t}(R_{q})).\end{gathered}

Moreover, the following estimates hold

(2.3)

\displaystyle\|v_{q+1}-v_{q}\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}L^{2}_{x}}

\displaystyle\leq C\delta_{q+1}^{\frac{1}{2}},

\displaystyle\||\nabla|^{\frac{3}{2}}T_{M}(v_{q+1}-v_{q})\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}L^{1}_{x}}

\displaystyle\leq\delta_{q+2}.

Remark 2.4

(On the parameter
\beta
)
.

The requirement on the parameter
\beta
will be clear in the proof of
Lemma

2.3
. In particular, it is required that
\beta>\frac{29}{2}
. This specific value is due to various harmonic analysis estimates (in negative, forcing a larger value of
\beta
), which are mainly shown in
Section

3
, and to a suitable construction of the building blocks of the convex integration scheme (in positive, lowering the minimal threshold we impose on
\beta
), which differs from the intermittent Beltrami flows
[
BV19
]
by a logarithmic factor (see
Remark

4.3
).

As already mentioned in the introduction, Tao
[
Tao10
]
and Barbato et al.
[
BMR15
]
proved the existence and uniqueness of smooth solutions to an equation similar to
Eq.

1.1
, where the dissipative term
D(u)
is defined via Fourier transform as

\mathcal{F}[D(u)](k)=\mathbf{1}_{\{k\neq 0\}}(k)\frac{|k|^{2\theta}}{g(|k|)}\mathcal{F}(u)(k),\qquad\text{for all }k\in\mathbb{Z}^{3},

for any positive, non-decreasing function
g
such that
\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{sg(s)}ds=\infty
. This operator differs from our dissipative term in two aspects.
Firstly, it presents a corrector computed on
|k|
, while our works on
|k|_{1}
.
This difference is due to the fact that we need to estimate the
\mathcal{L}(L^{1},L^{1})
-norm of the operator associated with the Fourier multiplier
log^{-\beta}(10+|k|_{1})\mathbf{P}_{\geq N}
(see
Proposition

3.4
and
Remark

3.5
) in order to exploit completely the Fourier properties of our building blocks.
The second difference is the presence of the function
g
. Even if their result covers a larger family of dissipative terms, the most interesting one is clearly given by
g(s)=log^{\beta}(10+|k|)
with
\beta\leq 1
.

Therefore, apart from the structural differences between our dissipative term and the one adopted in
[
Tao10
,
BMR15
]
, it can be interesting to study why their constraint on
\beta
is adimensional while ours depends on the dimension (see
Proposition

3.4
and
Remark

3.5
) and to understand if additional technical novelties could allow to weaken our assumption on the
\beta
parameter in order to get closer to the threshold identified by their results.

Assuming the previous Lemma, the proof of
Theorem

2.2
basically follows the lines of Luo and Titi
[
LT20
]
. We report it for completeness.

Proof of
Theorem

2.2
.

Consider the triple

\displaystyle v_{0}=u,\qquad p_{0}=-\frac{1}{3}|v_{0}|^{2},

\displaystyle R_{0}=\mathcal{R}(\partial_{t}v_{0}+\nu(-\Delta)^{\frac{5}{4},\beta}v_{0})+v_{0}\otimes v_{0}+p_{0}I,

where
\mathcal{R}
is the anti-divergence operator, defined below in
Definition

4.10
.

Given
\delta_{1}=\|R_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}L^{1}_{x}}
and
\delta_{q}=2^{-q}\epsilon
, consider the sequence
(v_{q},p_{q},R_{q})_{q\in\mathbb{N}}
coming from
Lemma

2.3
.
Then,

\displaystyle\sum_{q}\|v_{q+1}-v_{q}\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}L^{2}_{x}}\lesssim\sum_{q}\delta_{q+1}^{\frac{1}{2}}<\infty,

\displaystyle\|R_{q}\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}L^{1}_{x}}\to 0\qquad\text{as }q\to\infty.

Therefore,
(v_{q})_{q}
converges strongly to a weak solution
v\in C_{T}L^{2}_{x}
. Moreover,

\displaystyle\||\nabla|^{\frac{3}{2}}T_{M}(v-u)\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}L^{1}_{x}}\leq\sum\||\nabla|^{\frac{3}{2}}T_{M}(v_{q+1}-v_{q})\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}L^{1}_{x}}\leq\sum_{q}\delta_{q+1}\leq\epsilon,

\displaystyle\supp_{t}(v)\subset\cup_{q}\supp_{t}(v_{q})\subset N_{\sum_{q}\delta_{q+1}}(\supp_{t}(u))\subset N_{\delta_{1}+\epsilon}\supp_{t}(u).

Finally, infinitely many weak solutions with zero initial condition can be obtained by considering
u(t,x)=\phi(t)\sum_{|k|\leq N}a_{k}e^{ik\cdot x}
with
a_{0}=0
,
a_{k}^{*}=a_{-k}
,
a_{k}\cdot k=0
and
\phi\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
, with
\phi(0)=0
.
∎

3.
Preliminaries on Fourier multipliers

In this section we mainly prove that the operators associated with various Fourier multipliers are bounded from
\mathrm{L}(\mathrm{logL})^{\beta}
to
\mathrm{L}(\mathrm{logL})^{\alpha}
for suitable
\alpha,\beta\geq 0
.
Let us start by defining the functional space
\mathrm{L}(\mathrm{logL})^{\alpha}
and by proving an elementary inequality.

Definition 3.1
.

Given
0\leq\alpha<\infty
and
d\in\mathbb{N}
, consider the space

\mathrm{L}(\mathrm{logL})^{\alpha}=\Bigl\{f:\mathbf{T}^{3}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}\text{ measurable }:\int_{\mathbf{T}^{3}}|f(x)|\log^{\alpha}(2+|f(x)|)\,dx<\infty\Bigr\}.

This is a Banach space when equipped with the so-called
Luxemburg norm

(3.1)

\|f\|_{\mathrm{L}(\mathrm{logL})^{\alpha}}=\inf\Bigl\{\lambda>0:\int_{\mathbf{T}^{3}}A(|f(x)|/\lambda)dx\leq 1\Bigr\},

where
A(s)=s\log^{\alpha}(2+s)
for all
s\geq 0
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Consider a scalar function
f\in L^{\infty}(\mathbf{T}^{3})
and
g\in\mathrm{L}(\mathrm{logL})^{\alpha}(\mathbf{T}^{3},\mathbb{R}^{d})
for
\alpha\geq 0
. Then

\|fg\|_{\mathrm{L}(\mathrm{logL})^{\alpha}}\leq\|f\|_{L^{\infty}}\|g\|_{\mathrm{L}(\mathrm{logL})^{\alpha}}.

Proof.

If
A(s)=s\log^{\alpha}(2+s)
, then

\int_{\mathbf{T}^{3}}A\Big(\frac{|fg|}{\|f\|_{L^{\infty}}\|g\|_{\mathrm{L}(\mathrm{logL})^{\alpha}}}\Big)dx\leq\int_{\mathbf{T}^{3}}A\Big(\frac{|g|}{\|g\|_{\mathrm{L}(\mathrm{logL})^{\alpha}}}\Big)dx\leq 1,

where the first inequality follows since
A
is non-decreasing, and the second follows from the definition of the Luxemburg norm.
∎

Now, we first define the operator corresponding to a Fourier multiplier. Then we will focus on specific multipliers.

Definition 3.3
.

Given a family
m=(m(k))_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}}
, its corresponding operator
T_{m}
is defined by

T_{m}(f)(x)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}}m(k)\mathcal{F}(f)(k)e^{ik\cdot x},\qquad\text{ for }f\in C^{\infty}(\mathbf{T}^{3}),

where
\mathcal{F}(f)(k)
is the
k^{\text{th}}
Fourier coefficient of
f
.

Proposition 3.4
.

Consider
m(k)=\mathbf{1}_{\{k\neq 0\}}(k)\frac{1}{|k|_{1}}
, then
T_{m}
is bounded from
L^{1}(\mathbf{T}^{3})
to itself.

Moreover, for all
N\in\mathbb{N}
,

(3.2)

\|\mathbf{P}_{|k|_{1}>N}T_{m}\|_{\mathcal{L}(L^{1},L^{1})}\lesssim\frac{1}{N}\log^{3}(N),

where
\mathcal{L}(L^{1},L^{1})
is the space of bounded and linear operators from
L^{1}(\mathbf{T}^{3})
to itself.

Proof.

Consider
f_{N}(x)=\sum_{|k|_{1}\leq N}\frac{1}{|k|_{1}}e^{ik\cdot x},
then the following formula holds,

f_{N}(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}D^{1}_{j}(x)\Bigl(\frac{1}{j}-\frac{1}{j+1}\Bigr)+D^{1}_{N}(x)\frac{1}{N},

where
D^{1}_{j}(x)=\sum_{|k|_{1}\leq j}e^{ik\cdot x}
.

Therefore, for
N<M
,

\|f_{N}-f_{M}\|_{L^{1}}\leq\frac{1}{N}\|D_{N}^{1}\|_{L^{1}}+\frac{1}{M}\|D_{M}^{1}\|_{L^{1}}+\sum_{j=N}^{M-1}\|D^{1}_{j}\|_{L^{1}}\big(\frac{1}{j}-\frac{1}{j+1}\big).

It is known that
\|D^{1}_{j}\|_{L^{1}}\lesssim\log^{3}(j)
(see
[
KL21
]
), so

\|f_{N}-f_{M}\|_{L^{1}}\leq\frac{1}{N}\log^{3}(N)+\frac{1}{M}\log^{3}(M)+\sum_{j=N}^{M-1}\frac{\log^{3}(j)}{j(j+1)}\lesssim\frac{1}{N}\log^{3}(N).

The sequence
(f_{N})_{N}
is a Cauchy sequence in
L^{1}
and converges to
f
such that
\nu(A)=\int_{A}f(x)\,dx
is a finite Borel measure on the torus which has
m(k)
as Fourier coefficients. The existence of the measure
\nu
implies that
m
is a
(1,1)
-multiplier and that
\|T_{m}\|_{\mathcal{L}(L^{1},L^{1})}\leq|\nu|=\|f\|_{L^{1}}
, where
|\nu|
is the total variation of the measure
\nu
(see
[
G+08
]
).
At last, by the same argument, the estimate on
\|\mathbf{P}_{|k|_{1}>N}T_{m}\|_{\mathcal{L}(L^{1},L^{1})}
follows from

\|f-f_{N}\|_{L^{1}}=\lim_{M\to\infty}\|f_{M}-f_{N}\|_{L^{1}}\leq\frac{1}{N}\log^{3}(N).\qed

Remark 3.5
.

By repeating the same argument above, the conclusions of
Proposition

3.4
hold for
M(k)=\mathbf{1}_{k\neq 0}(k)\frac{1}{\log^{\beta}(|k|_{1}+10)}
, with
\beta>3
, and

(3.3)

\|\mathbf{P}_{|k|_{1}>N}T_{M}\|_{\mathcal{L}(L^{1},L^{1})}\lesssim\frac{\log^{3}(N)}{\log^{\beta}(10+N)}.

We notice though that the same machinery works for
m(k)=\frac{1}{|k|_{\infty}}
, but not for
m(k)=\frac{1}{|k|}
because of
\|D_{j}^{2}\|_{L^{1}}\approx j^{\frac{3-1}{2}}
where
D^{2}_{j}(x)=\sum_{|k|\leq j}e^{ik\cdot x}
, see for instance
[
Sha75
]
.

These results clearly hold in any dimension
d
and make use of a multidimensional version of Abel summation formula. In addition, the appearance of a factor
\log^{d}(N)
is coherent with various results obtained for
L^{1}
-convergence of double Fourier series
[
Mor94
]
.

Proposition 3.6
.

Given
j\in\{1,2,3\}
and the multiplier
m(k)=\mathbf{1}_{\{k\neq 0\}}(k)i[\sign(k_{j})]
, then
T_{m}
is bounded from
\mathrm{L}(\mathrm{logL})^{\alpha+1}
to
\mathrm{L}(\mathrm{logL})^{\alpha}
for all
\alpha\geq 0
.

Proof.

It is known that if an operator
T
is bounded from
L^{2}
to itself and from
L^{1}
to the weak
L^{1}
space
L^{1,\infty}
, then it is bounded from
\mathrm{L}(\mathrm{logL})^{\alpha+1}
to
\mathrm{L}(\mathrm{logL})^{\alpha}
for all
\alpha\geq 0
(see Theorem 4.4.11 and Theorem 4.6.16 in
[
BS88
]
).

Since
m
is bounded, then
T_{m}
is bounded from
L^{2}(\mathbf{T}^{3})
to itself. Now, we will conclude the proof by proving that
T_{m}
is bounded from
L^{1}
to
L^{1,\infty}
.

In the remaining part of the proof
|A|
denotes the Lebesgue measure of any Borel set
A
. It can be easily proved that

T_{m}(f)(t)=\frac{1}{4\pi}\,\text{p.v.}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f(t-\theta e_{j})\arctan\Big(\frac{\theta}{2}\Big)\,d\theta\\
:=\frac{1}{4\pi}\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\int_{\epsilon<|\theta|<\pi}f(t-\theta e_{j})\arctan\Big(\frac{\theta}{2}\Big)\,d\theta,

where
e_{j}
denotes the
j^{\text{th}}
element of the canonical basis.

By a density argument, we can assume that
f
is a finite linear combination of characteristic functions of disjoint dyadic intervals in
[-\pi,\pi]^{3}
. Given
\alpha>0
, then by the Calderon-Zygmund decomposition (see
[
G+08
]
), there exist
g,b
such that
\hat{f}=g+b
, where
\hat{f}
is an extension of
f
at
[-\pi,\pi]^{3}\cup([-\pi,\pi]^{3}+2\pi e_{j})\cup([-\pi,\pi]^{3}-2\pi e_{j})
by periodicity.
In addition, the following properties hold,

(1)

\|g\|_{L^{1}}\lesssim\|f\|_{L^{1}}
,
\|g\|_{L^{\infty}}\lesssim\alpha
,

(2)

b=\sum_{j}b_{j}
, where the
b_{j}
functions are supported on pairwise disjoint dyadic cubes
Q_{j}
,

(3)

\int_{Q_{j}}b_{j}(x)dx=0,

\|b_{j}\|_{L^{1}}\lesssim\alpha|Q_{j}|
and
\sum_{j}|Q_{j}|\lesssim\frac{1}{\alpha}\|f\|_{L^{1}}
,

(4)

The particular structure of
f
implies that there is a finite number of
b_{j}
’s functions.

For all
j
, define

\overline{Q_{j}}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:\text{there is }\epsilon\in[-l(Q_{j}),l(Q_{j})]\text{ s.t. }x+\epsilon e_{j}\in Q_{j}\},

where
l(Q_{j})=|Q_{j}|^{\frac{1}{3}}
is the length of the side of
Q_{j}
. Now,

\displaystyle\lx@add@cssclass{ltx_eqn_lefteqn}$\displaystyle|\{x\in[-\pi,\pi]^{3}:|T_{m}(f)(x)|>\alpha\}|\leq$\mbox{}

\displaystyle\leq|\{x\in[-\pi,\pi]^{3}:|T_{m}(g)(x)|>\tfrac{1}{2}\alpha\}|+|\cup_{j}\overline{Q_{j}}|

\displaystyle+|\{x\in[-\pi,\pi]^{3}\cap(\cup_{j}\overline{Q_{j}})^{c}:|T_{m}(b)(x)|>\tfrac{1}{2}\alpha\}|

\displaystyle\lesssim\alpha^{-1}\|f\|_{L^{1}}+|\{x\in[-\pi,\pi]^{3}\cap(\cup_{j}\overline{Q_{j}})^{c}:|T_{m}(b)(x)|>\tfrac{1}{2}\alpha\}|,

where the second inequality follows from Markov’s inequality and the properties satisfied by
b
and
g
.

The last term on the right-hand side can be estimated by Markov’s inequality and

\int_{[-\pi,\pi]^{3}\cap(\cup_{j}\overline{Q_{j}})^{c}}|T_{m}(b)(x)|\,dx\leq\sum_{j}\int_{[-\pi,\pi]^{3}\cap(\overline{Q_{j}})^{c}}|T_{m}(b_{j})(x)|\,dx\\
\leq\sum_{j}\int_{[-\pi,\pi]^{3}\cap(\overline{Q_{j}})^{c}}\int_{l(Q_{j})<|\theta|<\pi}\big|\arctan(\tfrac{\theta}{2})b_{j}(x-\theta e_{j})\big|\,d\theta\,dx.

Now if

, then

\int_{[-\pi,\pi]^{3}\cap(\overline{Q_{j}})^{c}}\int_{l(Q_{j})<|\theta|<\pi}\big|\arctan(\tfrac{\theta}{2})b_{j}(x-\theta e_{j})\big|\,d\theta\,dx\lesssim\|b_{j}\|_{L^{1}}.

Otherwise,

\begin{split}\lx@add@cssclass{ltx_eqn_lefteqn}$\displaystyle\int_{[-\pi,\pi]^{3}\cap(\overline{Q_{j}})^{c}}\int_{l(Q_{j})<|\theta|<\pi}|\arctan(\tfrac{\theta}{2})b_{j}(x-\theta e_{j})|\,d\theta\,dx\leq$\mbox{}\hfil\qquad&\\
&=\int_{[-\pi,\pi]^{3}\cap(\overline{Q_{j}})^{c}}\Bigl(\int_{l(Q_{j})<|\theta|<\frac{\pi}{4}+l(Q_{j})}|\arctan(\tfrac{\theta}{2})b_{j}(x-\theta e_{j})|\,d\theta\\
&\quad+\int_{\frac{\pi}{4}+l(Q_{j})<|\theta|<\pi}|\arctan(\tfrac{\theta}{2})b_{j}(x-\theta e_{j})|\,d\theta\Bigr)\,dx\\
&\lesssim\|b_{j}\|_{L^{1}}\Big(\int_{l(Q_{j})}^{\frac{\pi}{4}+l(Q_{j})}\arctan(\tfrac{\theta}{2})\,d\theta+\pi\arctan(\tfrac{\pi}{8})\Big)\\
&\leq\|b_{j}\|_{L^{1}}\log(\frac{\sin(\frac{1}{2}(\frac{\pi}{4}+l(Q_{j})))}{\sin(\frac{1}{2}l(Q_{j}))})\\
&\leq C\|b_{j}\|_{L^{1}};\end{split}

since

is bounded on
[0,\frac{\pi}{4}]
.
In conclusion, by the properties of the

∎

Proposition 4.1
.

(

BV20

Let

(2)

The impulsed Beltrami flows are obtained by modifying the classical Beltrami flows with suitable spatial and temporal intermittency. In particular, given

Let us summarise some properties of the

-Fejer kernels, which can be easily extended to

-Fejer kernels using Fubini-Tonelli theorem. We point out the crucial fact that, unlike the Dirichlet kernels, the
L^{1}
estimate does not contain logarithmic corrections.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let r
\in 2\mathbb{N}^{*}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
, then

\displaystyle\|F\|_{L^{1}}

\displaystyle=1,

\displaystyle\|F\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\approx r^{1/2},

\displaystyle\|F\|_{L^{\infty}}

\displaystyle\lesssim r,

\displaystyle\|\tfrac{d^{n}}{dy^{n}}F\|_{L^{1}}

\displaystyle\lesssim r^{n},

\displaystyle\|\tfrac{d^{n}}{dy^{n}}F\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim r^{n+\frac{1}{2}},

\displaystyle\|\tfrac{d^{n}}{dy^{n}}F\|_{L^{\infty}}

\displaystyle\lesssim r^{n+1}.

Proof.

The proof of the first three inequalities can be found in
[
G+08
]
.
Consider then the case
n>0
. The
L^{2}
and
L^{\infty}
norms can be easily estimated, respectively, by Fourier coefficients and by the formula

\sup_{[-\pi,\pi]}\Big|\frac{d^{n}}{dy^{n}}F(y)\Big|\lesssim\sum_{j=1}^{r}(1-\frac{j}{r+1})j^{n}.

Instead, the estimate of the

norm follows from the equalities:

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dy}F(y)=\frac{2}{r+1}\frac{\sin((r+1)\frac{y}{2})}{\sin\frac{y}{2}}\frac{d}{dy}\bigg[\frac{\sin((r+1)\frac{y}{2})}{\sin\frac{y}{2}}\bigg]=\frac{2}{r+1}D_{k}(y)\frac{d}{dy}D_{k}(y),

\displaystyle\frac{d^{n}}{dy^{n}}F(y)=\frac{1}{r+1}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}C(n,j)\Bigl(\frac{d^{j}}{dy^{j}}D_{k}(y)\Bigr)\Bigl(\frac{d^{n-j}}{dy^{n-j}}D_{k}(y)\Bigr),

where

are constants depending on

and

Indeed, the

\|\frac{d^{n}}{dy^{n}}D_{k}(y)\|_{L^{2}}\approx k^{n}k^{1/2}

Remark 4.12
.

Eq.

4.20
shows that
|\nabla|\mathcal{R}(u)
is obtained by applying three or one Riesz transforms to the components of the vector field
u
.

Since
(v_{q},p_{q},R_{q})
solves
Eq.

2.1
, then

\displaystyle\lx@add@cssclass{ltx_eqn_lefteqn}$\displaystyle\partial_{t}v_{q+1}+\Div(v_{q+1}\otimes v_{q+1})+(-\Delta)^{\frac{5}{4},\beta}v_{q+1}=$\mbox{}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\Div(R_{q})-\nabla p_{q}+\Div(w^{(p)}\otimes w^{(p)})+\partial_{t}w^{(t)}}_{\text{oscillation error}}+{}

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\Div(v_{q}\otimes w+w\otimes v_{q})+(-\Delta)^{\frac{5}{4},\beta}w+\partial_{t}(w^{(c)}+w^{(p)})}_{\text{linear error}}+{}

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\Div(w^{(p)}\otimes(w^{(c)}+w^{(t)})+(w^{(c)}+w^{(t)})\otimes w)}_{\text{corrector error}}.

Now, we show that the terms on the RHS can be expressed as the gradient of a scalar function
p_{q+1}
and as the divergence of a matrix-valued function
R_{q+1}
. In addition, we shall prove that
\|R_{q+1}\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}L^{1}_{x}}\leq\delta_{q+2}
.

Before estimating

, let us prove a useful lemma.

Lemma 4.13
.

Consider
a,b\in C^{\infty}(\mathbf{T}^{3})
such that
\mathbf{P}_{\geq N}b=b
.

(1)

Let
\beta>3
and
N\in 2\mathbb{N}^{*}
. Then,

(4.21)

\|\mathcal{R}|\nabla|T_{M}(ab)\|_{L^{1}}\leq\log^{3-\beta}(N)\|a\|_{H^{3}}\|b\|_{\mathrm{L}(\mathrm{logL})^{1}},

where
|\nabla|
is the operator associated with the multiplier
m(k)=|k|
.

(2)

Moreover,

(4.22)

\|\mathcal{R}\mathbf{P}_{\neq 0}(ab)\|_{L^{1}}\lesssim\frac{1}{N}\log^{3}(N)\|a\|_{H^{4}}\|b\|_{\mathrm{L}(\mathrm{logL})^{2}},

where the space
\mathrm{L}(\mathrm{logL})^{\alpha}
is defined in
Definition

3.1
.

Proof.

Define
a_{1}=\mathbf{P}_{<\frac{N}{2}}(a)
and
a_{2}=\mathbf{P}_{\geq\frac{N}{2}}(a)
. The assumption on
b
implies that

ab=\mathbf{P}_{\geq\frac{N}{2}}(a_{1}b)+(a_{2}b).

Notice that
Eq.

4.20
implies that the operator
\mathcal{R}|\nabla|T_{M}
corresponds to the multiplier

\frac{m_{1}(k)}{|k|}M(k)|k|,

where
m_{1}
is a suitable linear combination of iterated Riesz multipliers (see
Proposition

3.7
). Now,

\|\mathcal{R}|\nabla|T_{M}(ab)\|_{L^{1}}\lesssim\log^{3-\beta}(N)\|a_{1}b\|_{\mathrm{L}(\mathrm{logL})^{1}}+\|a_{2}b\|_{\mathrm{L}(\mathrm{logL})^{1}}\leq\\
\leq\log^{3-\beta}(N)\|a_{1}\|_{L^{\infty}}\|b\|_{\mathrm{L}(\mathrm{logL})^{1}}+\|a_{2}\|_{L^{\infty}}\|b\|_{\mathrm{L}(\mathrm{logL})^{1}}\leq\\
\leq\bigl(\log^{3-\beta}(N)\|a\|_{H^{2}}+\tfrac{1}{N}\|a\|_{H^{3}}\bigr)\|b\|_{\mathrm{L}(\mathrm{logL})^{1}},

where the first inequality is due to
Proposition

3.7
and
Eq.

3.3
, the second one follows from
Lemma

3.2
and the third one can be justified by Sobolev embeddings.

The second inequality,
Eq.

4.22
, follows from a slight modification of the previous proof. In particular, recall that
\mathcal{R}\mathbf{P}_{\neq 0}
can be seen as corresponding to the Fourier multiplier given by
Eq.

4.20
, which is equal to

\frac{m_{1}(k)}{|k|_{1}}\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{3}|k_{j}|}{|k|}=\frac{m_{1}(k)}{|k|_{1}}\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{3}\sign(k_{j})k_{j}}{|k|}=\frac{1}{|k|_{1}}\sum_{j}m_{2,j}(k)\sign(k_{j}),

where
m_{1}
and
m_{2,j}
are linear combinations of iterated Riesz transforms.
Therefore,
\mathcal{R}\mathbf{P}_{\neq 0}
is a finite linear combination of operators given by the composition of
T_{m}
with
m(k)=\mathbf{1}_{\{k\neq 0\}}(k)\frac{1}{|k|_{1}}
, iterated Riesz transforms and directional Hilbert transforms. Then, the result is easily achieved by using
Proposition

3.4
,
Proposition

3.7
and
Proposition

3.6
.
∎

4.3.1.
Corrector error

Given its formulation, it produces two addends of
R_{q+1}
whom
L^{\infty}_{t}L^{1}_{x}
norm can be estimated thanks to
Lemma

4.9
. Indeed,

\displaystyle\|w^{(p)}\otimes(w^{(c)}+w^{(t)})+(w^{(c)}+w^{(t)})\otimes w\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}L^{1}_{x}}\leq

\displaystyle\leq\|w+w^{(p)}\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}L^{2}_{x}}+\|w^{(c)}+w^{(t)}\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}L^{2}_{x}}

\displaystyle\leq C_{a}(1)\Bigl(\frac{1}{\lambda}+\sigma r+\frac{r^{\frac{3}{2}}}{\mu}\Bigr)\Bigl(\frac{1}{\lambda}+\sigma r+\frac{r^{\frac{3}{2}}}{\mu}+\delta_{q+1}^{\frac{1}{2}}+(\lambda\sigma)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\Bigr).

4.3.2.
Linear error

Since
\mathcal{R}
is the right inverse to the divergence operator (
Eq.

4.19
), the terms
v_{q}\otimes w+w\otimes v_{q}
and
\mathcal{R}((-\Delta)^{\frac{5}{4},\beta}w+\partial_{t}(w^{(c)}+w^{(p)}))
are part of
R_{q+1}
. In addition,
Lemma

4.9
implies that

\|v_{q}\otimes w\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}L^{1}_{x}}\leq\|v_{q}\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}L^{\infty}_{x}}\|w\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}L^{s}_{x}}\lesssim C_{a}(0)\|v_{q}\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}L^{\infty}_{x}}r^{\frac{3}{2}-\frac{3}{s}},

for
s\in(1,2)
.

The remaining terms are estimated through
Lemma

4.13
.

Proposition 4.14
.

The following estimates hold:

(1)

\|\mathcal{R}(\partial_{t}(w^{(c)}+w^{(p)}))\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}L^{1}_{x}}\lesssim C_{a}(1)\sigma\mu r^{-\frac{1}{2}}\log(\lambda)
,

(2)

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{R}(-\Delta)^{\frac{5}{4},\beta}(w^{(p)}+w^{(c)})\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}L^{1}_{x}}\lesssim C_{a}(2)\Bigl(\frac{\lambda}{r}\Bigr)^{\frac{3}{2}}\log^{4-\beta}(\lambda)
;

(3)

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{R}(-\Delta)^{\frac{5}{4},\beta}(w^{(t)})\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}L^{1}_{x}}\lesssim C_{a}(2)\frac{(\lambda\sigma r)^{\frac{3}{2}}}{\mu}\log(\lambda)
.

Proof.

To prove the first inequality, notice that
w^{(p)}+w^{(c)}=\frac{1}{\lambda}\nabla\times w^{(p)}
, so

\|\mathcal{R}(\partial_{t}(w^{(c)}+w^{(p)}))\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}L^{1}_{x}}=\frac{1}{\lambda}\|\mathcal{R}(\nabla\times(\partial_{t}w^{(p)}))\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}L^{1}_{x}}\lesssim\\
\lesssim\frac{1}{\lambda}\|\partial_{t}w^{(p)}\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}\mathrm{L}(\mathrm{logL})^{1}_{x}}\leq\frac{1}{\lambda}(C_{a}(1)\|\eta_{\xi}\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}\mathrm{L}(\mathrm{logL})^{1}_{x}}+C_{a}(0)\|\partial_{t}\eta_{\xi}\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}\mathrm{L}(\mathrm{logL})^{1}_{x}}),

where the first inequality is due to
Eq.

4.20
and
Proposition

3.7
and the second one follows from
Lemma

3.2
.
Now,
Eq.

4.11
implies the required inequality.

We turn to the proof of the second inequality. Recall that
T_{M}
is the operator associated with the multiplier

M(k)=\mathbf{1}_{\{k\neq 0\}}(k)\log^{-\beta}(|k|_{1}+10).

Then by a classical interpolation argument, we have

\|\mathcal{R}(-\Delta)^{\frac{5}{4},\beta}(w^{(p)}+w^{(c)})\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}L^{1}_{x}}=\frac{1}{\lambda}\||\nabla|^{\frac{3}{2}}(\mathcal{R}|\nabla|T_{M}(\nabla\times w^{(p)}))\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}L^{1}_{x}}\leq\\
\leq\frac{1}{\lambda}\|\mathcal{R}|\nabla|T_{M}\nabla(\nabla\times w^{(p)}))\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}L^{1}_{x}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\mathcal{R}|\nabla|T_{M}\nabla^{2}(\nabla\times w^{(p)}))\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}L^{1}_{x}}^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Now we estimate the first factor
\|\mathcal{R}|\nabla|T_{M}\nabla(\nabla\times w^{(p)}))\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}L^{1}_{x}}
, the second one can be estimated by a similar procedure.

First of all,

\nabla(\nabla\times(a_{\xi}\mathbf{W}_{\xi}))=\nabla(a_{\xi}(\nabla\times\mathbf{W}_{\xi})+\nabla a_{\xi}\times\mathbf{W}_{\xi})=\\
=(\nabla a_{\xi})(\nabla\times\mathbf{W}_{\xi})+a_{\xi}\nabla(\nabla\times\mathbf{W}_{\xi})+\nabla^{2}a_{\xi}\times\mathbf{W}_{\xi}+\nabla a_{\xi}\times\nabla\mathbf{W}_{\xi}.

Notice that
Eq.

4.6
implies that all the terms are composed by a suitable derivative of
a_{\xi}
and a
(\frac{\mathbf{T}}{\lambda/5})^{3}
-periodic function. Therefore,

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{R}|\nabla|T_{M}\nabla(\nabla\times w^{(p)}))\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}L^{1}_{x}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\log^{3-\beta}(\lambda)C_{a}(5)\|\nabla^{2}\mathbf{W}_{\xi}\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}\mathrm{L}(\mathrm{logL})^{1}_{x}}

\displaystyle\lesssim C_{a}(5)\lambda^{2}r^{-\frac{3}{2}}\log^{4-\beta}(\lambda),

where the first inequality is due to
Eq.

4.21
, while
Eq.

4.12
implies the second one.

Finally, we prove the third inequality. Following the same strategy used above, we have that

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{R}(-\Delta)^{\frac{5}{4},\beta}(w^{(t)})\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}L^{1}_{x}}

\displaystyle=\||\nabla|^{\frac{3}{2}}(\mathcal{R}|\nabla|T_{M}(w^{(t)}))\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}L^{1}_{x}}

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathcal{R}|\nabla|T_{M}\nabla(w^{(t)}))\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}L^{1}_{x}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\mathcal{R}|\nabla|T_{M}\nabla^{2}(w^{(t)}))\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}L^{1}_{x}}^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Let us show how we can estimate the first factor. Recall that the Leray-Helmholtz projector
P_{H}
corresponds to the multiplier
I-\frac{k\otimes k}{|k|^{2}}
, therefore
\mathcal{R}|\nabla|P_{H}
is associated with a linear combination of iterated Riesz transforms. So, by
Proposition

3.7
and
Remark

3.5
, we have that
\mathcal{R}|\nabla|P_{H}T_{M}
maps
\mathrm{L}(\mathrm{logL})
into
L^{1}
. Hence,

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{R}|\nabla|T_{M}\nabla(w^{(t)}))\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}L^{1}_{x}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{\mu}\|\mathcal{R}|\nabla|P_{H}T_{M}(\nabla(a_{\xi}^{2})\eta_{\xi}^{2}+a^{2}_{\xi}\nabla(\eta_{\xi}^{2}))\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}L^{1}_{x}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{\mu}\|\nabla(a_{\xi}^{2})\eta_{\xi}^{2}+a^{2}_{\xi}\nabla(\eta_{\xi}^{2})\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}\mathrm{L}(\mathrm{logL})^{1}_{x}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{\mu}C_{a}(1)\bigl(\|\eta_{\xi}^{2}\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}\mathrm{L}(\mathrm{logL})^{1}_{x}}+\|\nabla\eta_{\xi}^{2}\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}\mathrm{L}(\mathrm{logL})^{1}_{x}}\bigr)

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{\mu}C_{a}(1)\lambda\sigma r\log(\lambda),

where the third inequality follows from
Lemma

3.2
and the last one follows from
Eq.

4.11
.
∎

4.3.3.

Finally, the oscillation error is treated as in
[
BV19
]
. So,

\displaystyle\Div(R_{q}-w^{(p)}\otimes w^{(p)})=

\displaystyle=\Div\bigl(R_{q}-\sum_{\xi,\xi^{\prime}}a_{\xi}a_{\xi^{\prime}}\fint_{\mathbf{T}^{3}}\mathbf{W}_{\xi}\otimes\mathbf{W}_{\xi^{\prime}}dx+\sum_{\xi,\xi^{\prime}}a_{\xi}a_{\xi^{\prime}}\mathbf{P}_{\neq 0}\mathbf{W}_{\xi}\otimes\mathbf{W}_{\xi^{\prime}}\bigr)

\displaystyle=\Div\bigl(R_{q}-\sum_{\xi}a_{\xi}^{2}\fint_{\mathbf{T}^{3}}\mathbf{W}_{\xi}\otimes\mathbf{W}_{-\xi}dx+\sum_{\xi,\xi^{\prime}}a_{\xi}a_{\xi^{\prime}}\mathbf{P}_{\neq 0}\mathbf{W}_{\xi}\otimes\mathbf{W}_{\xi^{\prime}}\bigr)

\displaystyle=\Div\bigl(\rho I_{3}+\sum_{\xi,\xi^{\prime}}a_{\xi}a_{\xi^{\prime}}\mathbf{P}_{\neq 0}\mathbf{W}_{\xi}\otimes\mathbf{W}_{\xi^{\prime}}\bigr),

where the second equality follows from
Eq.

4.8
and the third one from
Eq.

4.16
. Since
\Div(\rho I_{3})=\nabla\rho
, then
\rho
goes into
p_{q+1}
as well as
p_{q}
.

Now, we can treat
\Div(\sum_{\xi,\xi^{\prime}}a_{\xi}a_{\xi^{\prime}}P_{\neq 0}\mathbf{W}_{\xi}\otimes\mathbf{W}_{\xi^{\prime}})=:\sum_{\xi,\xi^{\prime}}E_{\xi,\xi^{\prime}}
by taking into account each term
E_{\xi,\xi^{\prime}}+E_{\xi^{\prime},\xi}
. In particular, since
E_{\xi,\xi^{\prime}}+E_{\xi^{\prime},\xi}
has zero mean, then

\displaystyle E_{\xi,\xi^{\prime}}+E_{\xi^{\prime},\xi}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{P}_{\neq 0}\bigl(\nabla(a_{\xi}a_{\xi^{\prime}})\cdot\mathbf{P}_{\geq\lambda\sigma/2}(\mathbf{W}_{\xi}\otimes\mathbf{W}_{\xi^{\prime}}+\mathbf{W}_{\xi^{\prime}}\otimes\mathbf{W}_{\xi})\bigr)

\displaystyle\quad+\mathbf{P}_{\neq 0}\big(a_{\xi}a_{\xi^{\prime}}\Div(\mathbf{W}_{\xi}\otimes\mathbf{W}_{\xi^{\prime}}+\mathbf{W}_{\xi^{\prime}}\otimes\mathbf{W}_{\xi})\big)

\displaystyle=:E_{\xi,\xi^{\prime},1}+E_{\xi,\xi^{\prime},2},

The term
\mathcal{R}E_{\xi,\xi^{\prime},1}
goes into
R_{q+1}
,
Eq.

4.22
and
Lemma

3.2
imply that

\|\mathcal{R}E_{\xi,\xi^{\prime},1}\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}L^{1}_{x}}\lesssim\frac{\log^{3}(\lambda\sigma)}{\lambda\sigma}C_{a}(5)\|\mathbf{W}_{\xi}\otimes\mathbf{W}_{\xi^{\prime}}\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}\mathrm{L}(\mathrm{logL})^{2}_{x}}\lesssim\\
\lesssim\frac{\log^{3}(\lambda\sigma)}{\lambda\sigma}C_{a}(5)\|\eta_{\xi}\eta_{\xi^{\prime}}\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}\mathrm{L}(\mathrm{logL})^{2}_{x}}\lesssim C_{a}(5)\frac{\log^{3}(\lambda\sigma)}{\lambda\sigma}\log^{2}(\lambda),

where
\|\eta_{\xi}\eta_{\xi^{\prime}}\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}\mathrm{L}(\mathrm{logL})^{2}_{x}}
can be estimated arguing as in
Eq.

4.11
.

Instead, we need to distinguish two cases in order to deal with
E_{\xi,\xi^{\prime},2}
.

First case:
\xi\neq-\xi^{\prime}
.
If
\xi\neq-\xi^{\prime}
, then
\mathbf{P}_{>\lambda/10}(\mathbf{W}_{\xi}\otimes\mathbf{W}_{\xi^{\prime}})=\mathbf{W}_{\xi}\otimes\mathbf{W}_{\xi^{\prime}}
. Therefore,

\begin{split}\lx@add@cssclass{ltx_eqn_lefteqn}$\displaystyle a_{\xi}a_{\xi^{\prime}}\Div((\mathbf{W}_{\xi}\otimes\mathbf{W}_{\xi^{\prime}}+\mathbf{W}_{\xi^{\prime}}\otimes\mathbf{W}_{\xi})=$\mbox{}\hfil\qquad&\\
&=a_{\xi}a_{\xi^{\prime}}\Div(\mathbf{P}_{\geq\lambda/10}\big(\eta_{\xi}\eta_{\xi^{\prime}}(W_{\xi}\otimes W_{\xi^{\prime}}+W_{\xi^{\prime}}\otimes W_{\xi})\big)\\
&=a_{\xi}a_{\xi^{\prime}}\mathbf{P}_{\geq\lambda/10}\big(\nabla(\eta_{\xi}\eta_{\xi^{\prime}})\cdot(W_{\xi}\otimes W_{\xi^{\prime}}+W_{\xi^{\prime}}\otimes W_{\xi})\big)+{}\\
&\quad+a_{\xi}a_{\xi^{\prime}}\mathbf{P}_{\geq\lambda/10}\big(\eta_{\xi}\eta_{\xi^{\prime}}\nabla(W_{\xi}\cdot W_{\xi^{\prime}})\big)\\
&=a_{\xi}a_{\xi^{\prime}}\mathbf{P}_{\geq\lambda/10}\big(\nabla(\eta_{\xi}\eta_{\xi^{\prime}})\cdot(W_{\xi}\otimes W_{\xi^{\prime}}+W_{\xi^{\prime}}\otimes W_{\xi})\big)+\nabla(a_{\xi}a_{\xi^{\prime}}\mathbf{W}_{\xi}\cdot\mathbf{W}_{\xi^{\prime}})+{}\\
&\quad-\nabla(a_{\xi}a_{\xi^{\prime}})\mathbf{P}_{\geq\lambda/10}(\mathbf{W}_{\xi}\cdot\mathbf{W}_{\xi^{\prime}})-a_{\xi}a_{\xi^{\prime}}\mathbf{P}_{\geq\lambda/10}\big(W_{\xi}\cdot W_{\xi^{\prime}}\nabla(\eta_{\xi}\eta_{\xi^{\prime}})\big),\end{split}

where the second term produces a pressure, the third one can be estimated as
E_{\xi,\xi^{\prime},1}
, the first and the fourth produce two new terms of
R_{q+1}
whose
L^{\infty}_{t}L^{1}_{x}
norms can be estimated in the same way. Namely,

\|\mathcal{R}\mathbf{P}_{\neq 0}\Big(a_{\xi}a_{\xi^{\prime}}\mathbf{P}_{\geq\lambda/10}\big(W_{\xi}\cdot W_{\xi^{\prime}}\nabla(\eta_{\xi}\eta_{\xi^{\prime}})\big)\Big)\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}L^{1}_{x}}\lesssim\\
\lesssim C_{a}(5)\frac{\log^{3}(\lambda)}{\lambda}\|\nabla(\eta_{\xi}\eta_{\xi^{\prime}})\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}\mathrm{L}(\mathrm{logL})^{2}_{x}}\lesssim C_{a}(5)\frac{\log^{3}(\lambda)}{\lambda}\|\nabla(\eta_{\xi})\eta_{\xi^{\prime}}\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}\mathrm{L}(\mathrm{logL})^{2}_{x}}\lesssim\\
\lesssim C_{a}(5)\frac{\log^{3}(\lambda)}{\lambda}\log^{2}(\lambda)\lambda\sigma r,

where we have used
Eq.

4.22
.

Second case:
\xi=-\xi^{\prime}
.
If
\xi^{\prime}=-\xi
, then

E_{\xi,-\xi,2}=\mathbf{P}_{\neq 0}\big(a_{\xi}^{2}(\nabla\mathbf{P}_{\geq\lambda\sigma/2}(\eta_{\xi}^{2})-\frac{\xi}{\mu}\partial_{t}\eta_{\xi}^{2})\big)
. Now, let us recall that
\partial_{t}w^{(t)}
is part of the oscillation term, i.e. we need to deal with
\sum_{\xi\in\Lambda^{+}}[\mathbf{P}_{\neq 0}\big(a_{\xi}^{2}(\nabla\mathbf{P}_{\geq\lambda\sigma/2}(\eta_{\xi}^{2})-\frac{\xi}{\mu}\partial_{t}\eta_{\xi}^{2})\big)+\frac{1}{\mu}\partial_{t}P_{H}\mathbf{P}_{\neq 0}(a_{\xi}^{2}\eta_{\xi}^{2}\xi)]
.

Now,

\mathbf{P}_{\neq 0}\big(a_{\xi}^{2}(\nabla\mathbf{P}_{\geq\lambda\sigma/2}(\eta_{\xi}^{2}))\big)=\nabla(a_{\xi}^{2}\mathbf{P}_{\geq\lambda\sigma/2}(\eta_{\xi}^{2}))-\mathbf{P}_{\neq 0}(\nabla a_{\xi}^{2}\mathbf{P}_{\geq\lambda\sigma/2}(\eta_{\xi}^{2})),

where the first term goes into the pressure
p_{q+1}
, while the second produces a term of
R_{q+1}
with

estimated using
Eq.

4.22
. Indeed,

\|\mathcal{R}\mathbf{P}_{\neq 0}(\nabla a_{\xi}^{2}\mathbf{P}_{\geq\lambda\sigma/2}(\eta_{\xi}^{2}))\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}L^{1}_{x}}\lesssim C_{a}(5)\frac{\log^{3}(\lambda\sigma)}{\lambda\sigma}\log^{2}(\lambda).

Instead,

-\mathbf{P}_{\neq 0}\Bigl(a_{\xi}^{2}\partial_{t}\eta_{\xi}^{2}\frac{\xi}{\mu}\Bigr)+P_{H}\mathbf{P}_{\neq 0}\Bigl(a_{\xi}^{2}\eta_{\xi}^{2}\frac{\xi}{\mu}\Bigr)=-(I-P_{H})\Bigl(\mathbf{P}_{\neq 0}\partial_{t}(a_{\xi}^{2}\eta_{\xi}^{2})\frac{\xi}{\mu}\Bigr)+\mathbf{P}_{\neq 0}\Bigl(\frac{\xi}{\mu}\partial_{t}(a_{\xi}^{2})\eta_{\xi}^{2}\Bigr),

where the first term goes into the pressure thanks to

, while the second one can be seen as part of

, and

\bigl\|\mathcal{R}\mathbf{P}_{\neq 0}\bigl(\tfrac{\xi}{\mu}\partial_{t}(a_{\xi}^{2})\eta_{\xi}^{2}\bigr)\bigr\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}L^{1}_{x}}\lesssim C_{a}(5)\frac{1}{\mu}\log^{2}(\lambda).

4.3.4.
Conclusion of the proof

Summing up all the contributions to
R_{q+1}
, we end up with

(4.23)

\displaystyle|R_{q+1}|_{L^{\infty}_{t}L^{1}_{x}}\lesssim

\displaystyle C_{a}(1)\big(\frac{1}{\lambda}+\sigma r+\frac{r^{\frac{3}{2}}}{\mu}\big)\big(\frac{1}{\lambda}+\sigma r+\frac{r^{\frac{3}{2}}}{\mu}+\delta_{q+1}^{\frac{1}{2}}+(\lambda\sigma)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\big)

\displaystyle+C_{a}(0)r^{\frac{3}{2}-\frac{3}{s}}+C_{a}(1)\sigma\mu r^{-\frac{1}{2}}\log(\lambda)+C_{a}(2)\big(\frac{\lambda}{r}\big)^{\frac{3}{2}}\log^{4-\beta}(\lambda)

\displaystyle+C_{a}(2)\frac{(\lambda\sigma r)^{\frac{3}{2}}}{\mu}\log(\lambda)+C_{a}(5)\frac{\log^{3}(\lambda\sigma)}{\lambda\sigma}\log^{2}(\lambda)

\displaystyle+C_{a}(5)\log^{5}(\lambda)\sigma r+C_{a}(5)\frac{\log^{2}(\lambda)}{\mu}.

Since the values of
C_{a}(N)
do not depend on the parameter
\lambda
(see
Lemma

4.7
), then we can choose the parameters
s
,
\sigma
,
\mu
and
r
such that
Eq.

4.5
hold and
\|R_{q+1}\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}L^{1}_{x}}
is smaller than
\delta_{q+2}
as long as
\lambda
is large enough.
Therefore, consider

s=\frac{3}{2},\quad\sigma=\frac{1}{\lambda}\log^{2}(\lambda)\log^{4}(\log(\lambda)),\quad r=\frac{\lambda}{\log^{y}(\lambda)},\quad\mu=\lambda^{\frac{3}{2}}\log^{z}(\lambda),

where
y>2
and
z>-\frac{3}{2}y
in order to satisfy
Eq.

4.5
.
In particular, by this choice, the RHS of
Eq.

4.23
tends to zero as long as
\lambda
diverges to
\infty
if the following inequalities hold

\displaystyle z>-\frac{3}{2}y,\qquad z+\frac{y}{2}<-3,\qquad y>7,

\displaystyle\frac{3}{2}y+4-\beta<0,\qquad\frac{3}{2}y+z>4.

It is possible to find a pair
(y,z)
that satisfies the previous inequality if and only if
\beta>\frac{29}{2}
.
In order to conclude the proof of
Lemma

2.3
, we need to show that
Eq.

2.2
and
Eq.

2.3
are fully satisfied by our construction and our choice of the parameters. In particular, the control on the temporal support follows directly from
Eq.

4.18
, while the estimates on
\|v_{q+1}-v_{q}\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}L^{2}_{x}}
and
\||\nabla|^{\frac{3}{2}}T_{m}(v_{q+1}-v_{q})\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}L^{1}_{x}}
are due to

4.9
and

4.14
.

This concludes the proof of the
Iteration Lemma
(
Lemma

2.3
).
