Lagrange’s planetary equations with time-dependent secular perturbations
Barnabás Deme

Abstract
The long-term evolution of astrophysical systems is driven by a Hamiltonian that is independent of the fast angle. As this Hamiltonian may contain explicitly time-dependent parameters, the conservation of mechanical energy is not guaranteed in such systems. We derive how the semi-major axis evolves in these cases. We analyze two astrophysically interesting examples, those of the harmonic and quadrupole perturbations.

we conclude.

Perturbation as effective mass

The Hamiltonian in Eq. (
3
) consists of an unperturbed and a perturbing part:

\mathcal{H}=\frac{\dot{x}^{2}+\dot{y}^{2}}{2}-\frac{\mu}{r}+\epsilon(t)V(r),

(4)

where
r=\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}
,
\epsilon(t)\ll 1
is a small parameter
4
4

4

In fact, we demand it to be small initially:
\epsilon(0)\ll 1
. In Sec.
5

and

\ddot{x}=-\frac{\mu x}{r^{3}}-V^{\prime}\frac{x}{r}

(5)

and similarly for

\ddot{x}=-\frac{\left(\mu+V^{\prime}r^{2}\right)x}{r^{3}}

(6)

\mu+V^{\prime}r^{2}

\mu_{\mathrm{eff}}=\langle\mu+V^{\prime}r^{2}\rangle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\left(\mu+V^{\prime}r^{2}\right)\mbox{ d}M.

(7)

Equations (
1

3

5

5

We ignore the equation of the eccentricity, because it does not evolve in our approximation.

\dot{a}=-\frac{a}{\mu_{\mathrm{eff}}}\dot{\mu}_{\mathrm{eff}},

(8)

and

\dot{\omega}=-\frac{\sqrt{1-e^{2}}}{e\sqrt{\mu a}}\frac{\partial\langle\mathcal{H}\rangle}{\partial e},

(9)

which drive the secular evolution of the orbital elements in the presence of any central potential field perturbations. Note that
\mu
is not to be changed to
\mu_{\mathrm{eff}}
in the equation of
\dot{\omega}

5
Discussion

Actions are well-known as adiabatic invariants: they remain constant if the physical parameter in the Hamiltonian (e.g. the mass parameter
\mu
) changes slowly. Here we showed how the
unperturbed
Hamiltonian’s action (or more precisely, the semi-major axis) evolves when the parameter in the
perturbation
(e.g. the coupling strength
\epsilon
) varies in time. The validity of the theory, similarly to that of the standard adiabatic invariants, does not depend on how the parameter evolves in time. The only requirement is that it must be adiabatic. Indeed, Figs.
1

-
2
show linear, while Fig.
3
shows exponential time evolution.

Figure 3:
Same as the left panel of Fig.
2
, but with a perturbation evolving as
\epsilon=1-\mathrm{e}^{-0.001t}
. The amplitude of the oscillations grows because of the significant change in the semi-major axis.

Secular problems, i.e. those that occur on long timescales compared to the orbital period, are usually treated via von Zeipel’s transformation
[
27
]
. In Appendix
B
we show that in systems with secular time-dependent perturbations von Zeipel’s transformation is inconvenient compared to the method we suggest. Its main disadvantage is that one needs to perform several consecutive transformations to increase the accuracy, while the method described above does not need it.
7
7

7

The main reason of this is that the actions can be adiabatic invariants up to high orders in the ”slowness” parameter
\dot{\epsilon}/(\mu^{1/2}a^{-3/2})
. For a similar discussion about the harmonic oscillator, see Ref.
[
14
]
.

The biggest caveat of our work is that it works only for central potential perturbations, i.e. those that depend only on
r
. The reason for this is that in anisotropic cases each direction may have its own
\mu_{\mathrm{eff}}
and it is not obvious which of them one should use in Eq. (
8
). However, Eqs. (
8
)-(
9
) may remain qualitatively correct even if the perturbation is anisotropic, as demonstrated in Fig.
4
. The blue curve in that figure is the solution of the (non-conservative) system

\ddot{x}=-\frac{\mu x}{r^{3}}-2\epsilon k_{1}x

(18)

and

\ddot{y}=-\frac{\mu y}{r^{3}}-\epsilon k_{1}y.

(19)

The modulation of the amplitude of the blue curve is the consequence of the angular momentum not being conserved anymore.

Another important point is the strength of the perturbation,
\epsilon(t)
. It must be small initially (
|\epsilon(0)|\lesssim 0.01
) for the orbit to be (quasi-) Keplerian. If it decreases, it hardly has any effect on the dynamics of the semi-major axis, because
|\Delta a|\sim|\Delta\epsilon|\sim 0.01
(see, e.g., Eq.
12
). However, if
|\epsilon|
increases (which is not unphysical, e.g. dark matter halos gaining mass via accretion in the example of Sec.
4.1
), the change in the semi-major axis can be more significant:
|\Delta a|\sim|\Delta\epsilon|\gg 0.01
(see Fig.
3
, where
\Delta a/a\sim 1
, which is also indicated by the
r
-amplitude becoming larger). Importantly,
\epsilon(t)
itself does not need to remain small, only the ratio of the perturbing and unperturbed (Keplerian) energies. This latter statement holds, as proved heuristically in Appendix
C
.

Finally, we emphasize that the machinery in Sec.
3
does not rely on the form of the unperturbed Hamiltonian. It can be applied to systems other than the Kepler problem.

Appendix A
Derivation of Lagrange’s planetary equations

Here we follow the derivation in Ref.
[
26
]
. However, we constrain ourselves to planar motion.
8
8

8

Normally,
\omega
is measured from the ascending node. In this 2D case, we arbitrarily define a reference direction.
We start with Delaunay’s canonical variables:

\displaystyle l=M,\quad

\displaystyle L=\sqrt{\mu a},

(20)

\displaystyle g=\omega,\quad

\displaystyle G=\sqrt{\mu a(1-e^{2})},

(21)

where
\mu=\mathcal{G}m
with
\mathcal{G}
and
m
being the gravitational constant and the mass of the one-center problem, respectively, and
M
is the mean anomaly. These variables obey Hamilton’s equations of motion:

\displaystyle\dot{l}=\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}}{\partial L},\quad

\displaystyle\dot{L}=-\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}}{\partial L},

(22)

\displaystyle\dot{g}=\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}}{\partial G},\quad

\displaystyle\dot{G}=-\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}}{\partial g},

(23)

where the
\cdot
denotes time derivative and
\mathcal{H}
is the system’s Hamiltonian. The defining relations above can be inverted to give

a=\frac{L^{2}}{\mu},

(24)

e=\sqrt{1-\frac{G^{2}}{L^{2}}},

(25)

and

\omega=g.

(26)

Lagrange’s planetary equations are obtained by taking the time derivative of these expressions. In particular, using Hamilton’s equations (
22
)-(
23
),

\dot{a}=\frac{\partial a}{\partial\mu}\dot{\mu}+\frac{\partial a}{\partial L}\dot{L}=\frac{\partial a}{\partial\mu}\dot{\mu}-\frac{\partial a}{\partial L}\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}}{\partial l},

(27)

\dot{e}=\frac{\partial e}{\partial L}\dot{L}+\frac{\partial e}{\partial G}\dot{G}=-\frac{\partial e}{\partial L}\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}}{\partial l}-\frac{\partial e}{\partial G}\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}}{\partial g}

(28)

and

\dot{\omega}=\dot{g}=\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}}{\partial G}.

(29)

With the help of the conversion formulae (
24
)-(
26
), we arrive at

\dot{a}=-\frac{a}{\mu}\dot{\mu}-2\sqrt{\frac{a}{\mu}}\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}}{\partial M},

(30)

\dot{e}=-\frac{1-e^{2}}{e\sqrt{\mu a}}\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}}{\partial M}+\frac{\sqrt{1-e^{2}}}{e\sqrt{\mu a}}\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}}{\partial\omega},

(31)

and

\dot{\omega}=-\frac{\sqrt{1-e^{2}}}{e\sqrt{\mu a}}\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}}{\partial e}.

(32)

Appendix B
Elimination of the harmonic perturbation by von Zeipel’s method

We investigate the problem of Sec.
4.1
with von Zeipel’s method. We rewrite Eq. (
10
) as

\mathcal{H}=-\frac{\mu^{2}}{2L^{2}}+J+\epsilon(\theta)k_{1}r^{2}(l),

(33)

where Delaunay’s elements are used and we introduced an extra degree of freedom (
\theta
,
J
), such that the new angle plays the role of time:

\dot{\theta}=\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}}{\partial J}=1\to\theta=\theta_{0}+t.

(34)

The generating function of von Zeipel’s transformation is

F=lL^{\prime}+\theta J^{\prime}-\frac{\epsilon(\theta)k_{1}}{\mu^{1/2}a^{-3/2}}\int_{0}^{l}r^{2}(l^{*})\mbox{ d}l^{*},

(35)

where the primed quanities are the ”new” (transformed) ones.
9
9

9

With the introduction of
\theta
and
J
, the total energy of the system is formally conserved. Also, the ”new” and ”old” Hamiltonians are the same, since
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}=\mathcal{H}+\frac{\partial F}{\partial t}=\mathcal{H}
.
The action
L
up to
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)
accuracy reads

L=\frac{\partial F}{\partial l}=L^{\prime}-\frac{\epsilon(\theta)k_{1}}{\mu^{1/2}a^{-3/2}}r^{2}(l),

(36)

from which

a=\frac{1}{\mu}\left(L_{1}-\frac{\epsilon(\theta)k_{1}}{\mu^{1/2}}a^{1/2}\left(1-e\cos E\right)^{2}\right),

(37)

where
r=a(1-e\cos E)
is used, with
E
being the eccentric anomaly. Note that
L^{\prime}
is constant up to the prescribed accuracy.

This solution, plotted in orange in Fig.
5
, is inconvenient for several reasons: (i) it is only first-order in
\epsilon
, although higher-order solutions can be obtained with more work via Deprit’s method
[
6
]
; (ii) it is not secular in the sense that it contains oscillatory terms of orbital period; (iii) the function
r(t)
cannot be given explicitly in closed form.

Appendix C
The smallness of the perturbation

We take a Hamiltonian with a power-law perturbation,

\mathcal{H}=\frac{\dot{x}^{2}+\dot{y}^{2}}{2}-\frac{\mu}{r}+\epsilon kr^{n},

(38)

where
k
is a dimensional constant. The unperturbed (Keplerian) and perturbing energies, respectively, are

E_{0}=-\frac{\mu}{2a}

(39)

and

E_{1}\approx\epsilon ka^{n},

(40)

where orbit-averaging is executed and we use
\approx
sign for an order-of-magnitude equality (dropping a factor of eccentricities, etc.). The effective mass parameter is

\mu_{\mathrm{eff}}\approx\epsilon ka^{n+1}.

(41)

Their time evolution, via Eq. (
1
), is driven by

\dot{E_{0}}=\frac{\mu}{2a^{2}}\dot{a}\approx-\frac{\mu a\dot{\mu}_{\mathrm{eff}}}{2a^{2}\mu_{\mathrm{eff}}},

(42)

\dot{E_{1}}\approx\dot{\epsilon}ka^{n}

(43)

and

\dot{\mu}_{\mathrm{eff}}\approx\dot{\epsilon}a^{n+1}.

(44)

Note that we omit terms like
\epsilon\dot{a}\approx(\epsilon(0)+\dot{\epsilon}t)\dot{\epsilon}
, because they are of second order in small parameters like
\epsilon(0)
(see Sec.
5
) and
\dot{\epsilon}/\epsilon
(see Sec.
4.1
). Substituting Eq. (
44
) back to Eq. (
42
), we see that
|\dot{E_{0}}|\approx|\dot{E_{1}}|\approx\dot{\epsilon}a^{n}
, i.e., they evolve with the same rate. Consequently, if their initial ratio was small, it remains so on timescales longer than
\epsilon(0)/\dot{\epsilon}
.
