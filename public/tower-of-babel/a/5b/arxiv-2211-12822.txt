Non-symmetric intrinsic Hopf-Lax semigroup vs. intrinsic Lagrangian
Daniela Di Donato

Abstract
. In this paper, we analyze the ’symmetrized’ of the intrinsic Hopf-Lax semigroup introduced by the author in the context of the intrinsically Lipschitz sections in the setting of metric spaces. Indeed, in the usual case, we have that d ⁡ ( x , y ) = d ⁡ ( y , x ) d(x,y)=d(y,x) for any point x x and y y belong to the metric space X ; X; on the other hand, in our intrinsic context, we have that d ⁡ ( f ⁡ ( x ) , π − 1 ​ ( y ) ) ≠ d ⁡ ( f ⁡ ( y ) , π − 1 ​ ( x ) ) , d(f(x),\pi^{-1}(y))\neq d(f(y),\pi^{-1}(x)), for every x , y ∈ X . x,y\in X. Therefore, it is not trivial that we get the same result obtained for the "classical" intrinsic Hopf-Lax semigroup, i.e., the ’symmetrized’ Hopf-Lax semigroup is a subsolution of Hamilton-Jacobi type equation. Here, an important observation is that f f is just a continuous section of a quotient map π \pi and it can not intrinsic Lipschitz. However, following Evans, the main result of this note is to show that the "new" intrinsic Hopf-Lax semigroup satisfies a suitable variational problem where the functional contained an intrinsic Lagrangian. Hence, we also define and prove some basic properties of the intrinsic Fenchel-Legendre transform of this intrinsic Lagrangian that depends on a continuous section of π \pi .

The simple idea of intrinsic graph in the FSSC sense is the following one: let
\mathbb{V}
and
\mathbb{W}
be complementary homogeneous subgroups of a Carnot group
\mathbb{G}
, i.e.,
\mathbb{W}\cap\mathbb{V}=\{0\}
and
\mathbb{G}=\mathbb{W}\cdot\mathbb{V}
(where
\cdot
is the group operation given by the Campbell-Hausdorff formula), then the intrinsic left graph of
\varphi:\mathbb{W}\to\mathbb{V}
is the set

\mbox{graph}{(\varphi)}:=\{x\cdot\varphi(x)\,|\,x\in\mathbb{W}\}.

A function
\varphi
is said to be intrinsic Lipschitz if it is possible to put, at each point
p\in\mbox{graph}{(\varphi)}
, an intrinsic cone with vertex
p
, axis
\mathbb{V}
and fixed opening, intersecting
\mbox{graph}{(\varphi)}
only at
p
.

Moreover, in
[
FSSC01
,
FSSC03a
]
, the authors introduce the notion of intrinsic
C^{1}
surface adapting to groups De Giorgi’s classical technique valid in Euclidean spaces to show that the boundary of a finite perimeter set can be seen as a countable union of
C^{1}
regular surfaces. A set
S
is a
d
-codimensional intrinsic
C^{1}
surface if there exists a continuous function
f:\mathbb{G}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that, locally,

S=\{p\in\mathbb{G}:f(p)=0\},

and the horizontal jacobian of
f
has maximum rank, locally (see also
[
DD20
]
).

These two approaches are natural counterparts of the notions of rectifiability in Euclidean spaces, where their equivalence follows from well-known theorems as, for instance, Rademacher Theorem. Hence, it is surprising that the connection between these two notions of rectifiability is poorly understood already in Carnot groups of step 2.

Starting from the seminal papers by Franchi, Serapioni and Serra Cassano, in metric spaces, Le Donne and the author
[
DDLD22
]
introduced and studied the so-called intrinsically Lipschitz sections (see Section
2
) which generalize the intrinsically Lipschitz maps in the FSSC sense. The difference between the two approaches is that Franchi, Serapioni and Serra Cassano study the properties of intrinsically Lipschitz maps; while we study the ”sections” or rather the properties of the graphs that are intrinsic Lipschitz. Roughly speaking, in our approach, a section
\psi
is such that
\mbox{graph}(\varphi)=\psi(Y)\subset X
where
X
is a metric space and
Y
is a topological space. In
[

]
, we prove the Ascoli-Arzelá theorem, the Ahlfors-David regularity, the link between intrinsic Lipschitz sections and level sets of biLipschitz maps on fibers, as well as the Extension theorem. In
[

or
\mathbb{C}
for a suitable class of these sections; we give an equivalence relation for these sections.

In our context a section
\varphi:Y\to X
of
\pi:X\to Y
is such that
\pi
produces a foliation for
X,
i.e.,
X=\coprod\pi^{-1}(y)
and the Lipschitz property of

consists to ask that the distance between two points
\varphi(y_{1}),\varphi(y_{2})
is not comparable with the distance between

and

but between
\varphi(y_{1})
and the fiber of
y_{2}
. Following this idea and recall that the Hopf-Lax formula

F(y,t)=\inf_{z\in M}\left\{f(z)+\frac{d^{2}(y,z)}{2t}\right\},

is the viscosity solution to the Hamilton-Jacobi equation on a compact Riemannian manifold
M,
i.e.,

\frac{\partial F}{\partial t}+\frac{|\nabla F|^{2}}{2}=0.

(where
f:M\to\mathbb{R}
is the initial continuous condition,

is the geodesic distance on

and

it is natural to define the intrinsic Hopf-Lax semigroup as follows. Let
X=\mathbb{R}^{\kappa}
,

be bounded and
\pi:X\to Y
a quotient map.
The intrinsic Hopf-Lax semigroup is the family of operators
(iQ_{\cdot})_{t>0}
defined as

(1)

f\,\,\mapsto\,\,iQ_{t}f(y):=\inf_{z\in Y}\left\{\max_{j=1,\dots,\kappa}f_{j}(z)+\frac{1}{2t}d^{2}(f(z),\pi^{-1}(y))\right\}.

for any continuous section
f:Y\to X
of
\pi.
We also consider the case
\kappa>1
because being intrinsically Lipschitz is equivalent to Lipschitz in the classical sense when we consider the basic case
X=Y=\mathbb{R}.
Moreover, I underline that, in most cases,
f
is just a continuous section of
\pi
and hence we do not ask for its intrinsic Lipschitz condition.

This is the topic of
[
DD22b
]
where, following
[
AGS14a
,
LV07
]
, we prove the link between the intrinsic Hopf-Lax semigroup and the intrinsic slope and we also show that the intrinsic Hopf-Lax semigroup is a subsolution of Hamilton-Jacobi type equality. Yet, in the usual case, the first result is one step in order to analyze when a Dirichlet form is regular (i.e., when it coincides with the Cheeger energy) and thus a natural question may be whether the results known in
[

However, in this paper, we analyze a sort of ’symmetrized’ of (
1

d(x,y)=d(y,x)
for any point

on the other hand, in our intrinsic context, it is easy to see that

d(f(x),\pi^{-1}(y))\neq d(f(y),\pi^{-1}(x)),

for every

In particular, it holds

(2)

\displaystyle d(f(y),\pi^{-1}(x))-d(f(z),\pi^{-1}(x))

\displaystyle\leq d(f(y),f(z)),\quad\forall x,y,z\in Y

\displaystyle d(f(x),\pi^{-1}(y))-d(f(x),\pi^{-1}(z))

\displaystyle\nleq d(f(y),f(z)),\quad\mbox{for some }x,y,z\in Y.

Indeed, for any fixed

if we choose

we get that

\displaystyle d(f(y),\pi^{-1}(x))-d(f(z),\pi^{-1}(x))

\displaystyle\leq d(f(y),a)-d(f(z),\pi^{-1}(x))

\displaystyle\leq d(f(y),f(z))+d(f(z),a)-d(f(z),\pi^{-1}(x))

\displaystyle=d(f(y),f(z)),

i.e., the first inequality of (
2
) holds.
On the other hand, for the second inequality in (
2
), we give the following example. let
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
the set given by the two lines with vertex
(0,8),(8,8)
and
(0,3),(8,7)
and the subset

of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
defined as the line with vertex

If we consider a continuous section

and

it is easy to see that

\displaystyle d(f(x),\pi^{-1}(y))-d(f(x),\pi^{-1}(z))

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{5}{4}},

\displaystyle d(f(y),f(z))

\displaystyle=1,

and so

\displaystyle d(f(x),\pi^{-1}(y))-d(f(x),\pi^{-1}(z))

\displaystyle\nleq d(f(y),f(z)).

Then, it is not trivial to define

(4)

u(y,t)=\inf_{z\in Y}\left\{\frac{d^{2}(f(y),\pi^{-1}(z))}{2t}+g(z)\right\}.

and to prove that it is a subsolution of Hamilton-Jacobi type equation (see Corollary

However, the main object of this paper is defined in (

The main results of this paper are Theorem

2.
Intrinsically Lipschitz sections

2.1.
Intrinsically Lipschitz sections

The notion of intrinsically Lipschitz maps was introduced by Franchi, Serapioni and Serra Cassano
[
FSSC01
,
FSSC03b
,
FSSC03a
]
(see also
[
SC16
,
FS16
]
) in the context of subRiemannian Carnot groups after a negative result given by Ambrosio and Kirchheim
[
AK00
]
. Their aim is to establish a good definition of rectifiability in Carnot groups.

Here we present a generalization of this concept introduced in
[
DDLD22
]
. Our setting is the following. We have a metric space
X
, a topological space
Y
, and a
quotient map
\pi:X\to Y
, meaning
continuous, open, and surjective.
The standard example for us is when
X
is a metric Lie group
G
(meaning that the Lie group
G
is equipped with a left-invariant distance that induces the manifold topology), for example a subRiemannian Carnot group, and
Y
if the space of left cosets
G/H
, where

H<G
is a closed subgroup and
\pi:G\to G/H
is the projection modulo
H
,
g\mapsto gH
.

Definition 2.1
.

We say that a map
\varphi:Y\to X
is a section of
\pi
if

(6)

\pi\circ\varphi=\mbox{id}_{Y}.

Moreover, we say that a map
\varphi:Y\to X
is an
intrinsically Lipschitz section of
\pi
with constant
L
, with
L\in[1,\infty)
, if in addition

(7)

d(\varphi(y_{1}),\varphi(y_{2}))\leq Ld(\varphi(y_{1}),\pi^{-1}(y_{2})),\quad\mbox{for all }y_{1},y_{2}\in Y.

Here
d
denotes the distance on
X
, and, as usual, for a subset
A\subset X
and a point
x\in X
, we have

d(x,A):=\inf\{d(x,a):a\in A\}
.

A first observation is that this class is contained in the class of continuous maps (see
[
DDLD22
, Section 2.4]
) but cannot be uniformly continuous (see Example 1.2 in
[
DD22c
]
).
In the case
\pi
is a Lipschitz quotient or submetry
[
BJL+99
,
VN88
]
, being intrinsically Lipschitz is equivalent to biLipschitz embedding, see Proposition 2.4 in
[
DDLD22
]
. Moreover, since
\varphi
is injective by (
6
), the class of Lipschitz sections not include the constant maps.

Remark 2.2
.

If
Y
is bounded, we get that

(8)

K:=\sup_{y_{1},y_{2}\in Y}d(\varphi(y_{1}),\pi^{-1}(y_{2}))<+\infty.

This follows because, on the contrary, if
K=+\infty,
then we get the contradiction
+\infty=d(\varphi(y_{1}),\pi^{-1}(y_{2}))\leq d(\varphi(y_{1}),\varphi(y_{2})).

2.2.
Intrinsically Lipschitz constants

We recall the definition of the intrinsically Lipschitz constants as in
[
DD22a
,
DD22e
]
, where we have adapted the theory of
[
Che99
,
DM14
]
in our intrinsic case.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
\varphi:Y\to X
be a section of
\pi
. Then we define

ILS(\varphi):=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}y_{1},y_{2}\in Y\\
y_{1}\neq y_{2}\end{subarray}}\frac{d(\varphi(y_{1}),\varphi(y_{2}))}{d(\varphi(y_{1}),\pi^{-1}(y_{2}))}\in[0,+\infty]

and

\displaystyle ILS(Y,X,\pi)

\displaystyle:=\{\varphi:Y\to X\,:\,\varphi\mbox{ is an intrinsically Lipschitz section of $\pi$ and }ILS(\varphi)<+\infty\},

\displaystyle ILS_{b}(Y,X,\pi)

\displaystyle:=\{\varphi\in ILS(Y,X,\pi)\,:\,\mbox{spt}(\varphi)\mbox{ is bounded}\}.

For simplicity, we will write
ILS(Y,\mathbb{R}^{\kappa})
instead of
ILS(Y,\mathbb{R}^{\kappa},\pi).

Definition 2.4
.

Let
\varphi:Y\to X
be a section of
\pi
. Then we define the local intrinsically Lipschitz constant (also called intrinsic slope) of
\varphi
the map
Ils(\varphi):Y\to[0,+\infty)
defined as

Ils(\varphi)(z):=\limsup_{y\to z}\frac{d(\varphi(y),\varphi(z))}{d(\varphi(y),\pi^{-1}(z))},

if
z\in Y
is an accumulation point; and
Ils(\varphi)(z):=0
otherwise.

Definition 2.5
.

Let
\varphi:Y\to X
be a section of
\pi
. Then we define the asymptotic intrinsically Lipschitz constant of
\varphi
the map
Ils_{a}(\varphi):Y\to[0,+\infty)
given by

Ils_{a}(\varphi)(z):=\limsup_{y_{1},y_{2}\to z}\frac{d(\varphi(y_{1}),\varphi(y_{2}))}{d(\varphi(y_{1}),\pi^{-1}(y_{2}))}

if
z\in Y
is an accumulation point and
Ils(\varphi)(z):=0
otherwise.

Remark 2.6
.

Notice that by
\varphi(y_{2})\in\pi^{-1}(y_{2}),
it is trivial that
d(\varphi(y_{1}),\pi^{-1}(y_{2}))\leq d(\varphi(y_{1}),\varphi(y_{2}))
and so
Ils(\varphi)\geq 1.
Moreover, it holds

Ils(\varphi)\leq Ils_{a}(\varphi)\leq ILS(\varphi).

3.
The intrinsic Lagrangian
L

3.1.
Properties of L

We underline that our standard
L
has the following form:

tL\left(\frac{d(f(x),\pi^{-1}(z))}{t}\right)=\frac{d^{2}(f(x),\pi^{-1}(z))}{2t},

for any
x,z\in Y.
Hence, we want that
L
satisfies the following three properties:

•

As in the classical case, we ask that
L
is convex map.

•

It holds

(9)

tL\left(\frac{d(f(y),\pi^{-1}(z))}{t}\right)-tL\left(\frac{d(f(x),\pi^{-1}(z))}{t}\right)\leq 2K\sqrt{L}\left(\frac{d(f(y),f(x))}{t}\right),

for any
x,y,z\in Y
and
t>0.

This is because the following observation: for any
x,y,z\in Y,
by (
3
), we know that
d(f(y),\pi^{-1}(x))-d(f(z),\pi^{-1}(x))\leq d(f(y),f(z)),\quad\forall x,y,z\in Y
and so if
Y
is bounded, then we have that

d^{2}(f(y),\pi^{-1}(z))-d^{2}(f(x),\pi^{-1}(z))\leq 2Kd(f(y),f(x)),

for any
x,y,z\in Y,
where
K>0
is given by (
8
).

•

As in the usual case, by the simply fact that if
0<s<t,
then
1/s>1/t
we ask

(10)

tL\left(\frac{d(f(x),\pi^{-1}(z))}{t}\right)\leq sL\left(\frac{d(f(x),\pi^{-1}(z))}{s}\right),

for any
x,z\in Y.

3.2.
Properties of the solution
u

The purpose of this section is to follows the theory in
[
DB09
,
Eva10
]
where the authors investigate the sense in which function
\ell
defined as

\ell(y,t):=\inf\left\{\int_{0}^{t}L(\dot{w}(s))\,ds+g(w(0))\,|\,w(t)=y\right\},

(see
[
Eva10
, Section 3.3]
) actually solves the initial-valued problem for the Hamilton-Jacobi PDE:

(11)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\ell_{t}+H(D\ell)=0\quad\mbox{in }\mathbb{R}^{\kappa}\times(0,\infty),\\
\ell=g\quad\mbox{on }\mathbb{R}^{\kappa}\times\{t=0\},\\
\end{array}\right.

where
H
is a smooth map such that

(1)

H
is convex;

(2)

\lim_{|p|\to\infty}\frac{H(p)}{|p|}=+\infty;

and
g:\mathbb{R}^{\kappa}\to\mathbb{R}
is a Lipschitz map in the usual sense.

The main aims of this paper is to adapt this theory in our intrinsic context. More precisely, we prove that a suitable map
u
defined as (
15
) is a subsolution of Hamilton-Jacobi type equation and that this map is equal to
g
when
t=0
(see Corollary
4.3
and Proposition
3.2
(3)). Moreover, in Theorem
3.1
we show that this map
u
is also a solution of a suitable variational problem (see (
14
)).

Our setting is as follows. Let
\pi:\mathbb{R}^{\kappa}\to Y
be a quotient map with
Y
a bounded subset of
\mathbb{R}^{\kappa}.
We consider a continuous section
f:Y\to\mathbb{R}^{\kappa}
such that

g(y):=\max_{j=1,\dots,\kappa}f_{j}(y),

where if
f
is an intrinsically Lipschitz section of
\pi,
then
g
is so too with the same Lipschitz constant (see
[
DD22e
, Proposition 4.1]
). However, we present some result where
f
is just a continuous section of
\pi.

Given
y\in Y
and
t>0,
we propose to minimize among curves
w:\mathbb{R}^{+}\cup\{0\}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfying

(12)

w(t)=w(0)+d(f(y),\pi^{-1}(w(0))),

the expression

(13)

\int_{0}^{t}L(\dot{w}(s))\,ds+g(w(0)),

which is the action augmented with the value of the initial data. We accordingly now define

(14)

v(y,t):=\inf\left\{\int_{0}^{t}L(\dot{w}(s))\,ds+g(w(0))\,|\,w(t)=w(0)+d(f(y),\pi^{-1}(w(0)))\right\},

the infimum taken over all
C^{1}
maps
w(.).

Theorem 3.1
.

If
y\in Y
and
t>0,
then the solution
u=u(y,t)
of the minimization problem (
14
) is

(15)

u(y,t)=\inf_{z\in Y}\left\{tL\left(\frac{d(f(y),\pi^{-1}(z))}{t}\right)+g(z)\right\}.

Proof.

Fix
z\in Y
and define
\tilde{w}(s):=z+\frac{s}{t}d(f(y),\pi^{-1}(z))
for
s\in[0,t].
By definition of
v
we deduce that

v(y,t)\leq\int_{0}^{t}L(\dot{\tilde{w}}(s))\,ds+g(z)=tL\left(\frac{d(f(y),\pi^{-1}(z))}{t}\right)+g(z),

and so
v(y,t)\leq u(y,t).

On the other hand, if
w(.)
is any
C^{1}
map satisfying
w(t)=w(0)+d(f(y),\pi^{-1}(w(0)))
, using the convexity of
L,
we have

L\left(\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}\dot{w}(s)\,ds\right)\leq\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}L(\dot{w}(s))\,ds,

by Jensen’s inequality. Thus if we write
z=w(0)
we find

tL\left(\frac{d(f(y),\pi^{-1}(z))}{t}\right)+g(z)\leq\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}L(\dot{w}(s))\,ds+g(z).

Consequently
v(y,t)\geq u(y,t)
and the proof is complete.
∎

When
f
is a bounded map, we get the following observation.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
f\in C_{b}(Y,\mathbb{R}^{\kappa})
be a section of
\pi:\mathbb{R}^{\kappa}\to Y
. Then we have the following basic properties for
u(y,t):

i:

\inf_{z\in Y}\min_{j=1,\dots,\kappa}f_{j}\leq u(\cdot,t)\leq\max_{j=1,\dots,\kappa}f_{j}\leq\sup_{z\in Y}\max_{j=1,\dots,\kappa}f_{j}<+\infty.

ii:

Any quasi-minimizing sequence
(y_{n})_{n}
for
u(y,t_{n})
converges to
y
as
t_{n}\to 0
and

(16)

\pi^{-1}(y_{n})\to\pi^{-1}(y),

as
n\to\infty.

Proof.

i)
It is a trivial consequence of the fact that
\frac{1}{2t}d^{2}(f(z),\pi^{-1}(z))\geq 0
and that we can use
z=y
as a competitor in the infimum of (
15
).

ii)
Fix
y\in Y
and take a sequence
t_{n}\to 0
; consider a quasi-minimizing sequence
(y_{n})_{n}
for
u(y,t_{n})
, in the sense that:

u(y,t_{n})+\frac{1}{n}\geq g(y_{n})+\frac{1}{2t_{n}}d^{2}(f(y),\pi^{-1}(y_{n})),\quad\forall n\in\mathbb{N}.

Indeed,

\displaystyle d^{2}(\pi^{-1}(y),\pi^{-1}(y_{n}))

\displaystyle\leq d^{2}(f(y),\pi^{-1}(y_{n}))

\displaystyle\leq 2t_{n}\left(u(y,t_{n})+\frac{1}{n}-g(y_{n})\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2t_{n}\left(2\|f\|_{\infty}+\frac{1}{n}\right)\,\,\longrightarrow_{n\to\infty}0.

Consequently, (
16
) holds by continuity of
f
.

∎

Proposition 3.3
.

The function

(2).

3.3.
The intrinsic Fenchel-Legendre transform

It is interesting to notice that in the proof or Proposition
3.2
(3) we have a sort of the Fenchel-Legendre transform in our intrinsic context.

Following
[
DB09
,
Eva10
]
, we define

Definition 3.5
.

Let
y\in Y,t>0
and
f:Y\to\mathbb{R}^{\kappa}
be a continuous section of
\pi.
The intrinsic Fenchel-Legendre transform of
L
is a map
L^{*}:[0,Ils(f)]\to\mathbb{R}
given by

(19)

L^{*}(\xi)=L^{*}_{f,y,t}(\xi):=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}z\in Y\,\\
w=\frac{d(f(y),\pi^{-1}(z))}{t}\,\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\end{subarray}}(\xi w-L\left(w\right)).

As in classical way, we define the intrinsic Hamiltonian
H
associated by
L
as the intrinsic Fenchel-Legendre transform of
L
and so

H(\xi):=L^{*}(\xi),

for any
\xi\in[0,Ils(f)].
Notice that since
\xi\in[0,Ils(f)],
it makes no sense to consider properties of
H
when
|\xi|\to\infty,
as in the usual case. However, inspired by
[
Eva10
, Theorem 3]
, we give the following properties of this notion.

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
y\in Y,t>0
and
f:Y\to\mathbb{R}^{\kappa}
be a continuous section of
\pi.
Then,

(1)

If
Y
is bounded set, then
L^{*}(\xi)=\xi\frac{K}{t}-min_{\begin{subarray}{c}w=\frac{d(f(y),\pi^{-1}(z))}{t}\end{subarray}}L(w).

(2)

H
is a convex map
;

(3)

L=H^{*}.

Thus
H
is the intrinsic Fenchel-Legendre transform of
L
and vice versa.

Proof.

(1). This follows recall the definition of
K
(see (
8
)).

(2). For each fixed
v,
the function
p\mapsto p\cdot v-L(v)
is linear; and consequently, the mapping

p\mapsto H(p)=L^{*}(p)=\sup_{v\in\mathbb{R}^{+}}pv-L(v),

is convex. Indeed, if
\tau\in[0,1]
and
p,q\in\mathbb{R},
it holds

\displaystyle H(\tau p+(1-\tau)q)

\displaystyle=\sup_{v}\left((\tau p+(1-\tau)q)\right)v-L(v)

\displaystyle\leq\tau\sup_{v}(pv-L(v))+(1-\tau)\sup_{v}(qv-L(v))

\displaystyle=\tau H(p)+(1-\tau)H(q),

as desired.

(3). We prove the inequality
(\geq)
. By
H(p)=L^{*}(p)=\sup_{v}(pv-L(v)),
we get that

H(p)+L(v)\geq pv,\quad\forall p,v\in\mathbb{R}^{+}

and consequently

L(v)\geq\sup_{p}(pv-H(p))=H^{*}(p).

On the other hand, the second inequality
(\leq)
follows noting

(20)

\displaystyle H^{*}(v)

\displaystyle=\sup_{p}(pv-\sup_{r}(pr-L(r)))

\displaystyle=\sup_{p}\inf_{r}(p(v-r)+L(r)).

Now since
v\mapsto L(v)
is convex, according with the Section B.1 in
[
Eva10
]
we know that there is
\ell\in\mathbb{R}
such that

\displaystyle L(r)\geq L(v)+\ell(r-v),\quad\mbox{with }r\in\mathbb{R}.

Choosing
p=\ell
in (
20
) and using the last inequality, we deduce that

H^{*}(v)\geq\inf_{r}(\ell(v-r)+L(r))\geq L(v),

and this complete the proof of the statement.

∎

4.
Hamilton-Jacobi type equation

In this section we show that the ’symmetrized’ Hopf-Lax semigroup is a subsolution of Hamilton-Jacobi type equation. Here we consider the model case, i.e., when
L(v)=v^{2}
and so

u(y,t)=\inf_{z\in Y}\left\{\frac{d^{2}(f(y),\pi^{-1}(z))}{2t}+g(z)\right\}.

4.1.
Semicontinuity of
i{\mathfrak{D}}^{\pm}

Given a continuous section
f:Y\to\mathbb{R}^{\kappa}
of
\pi
,
u(y,t)
is then defined by the minimum problem (
15
).
We also define:

(21)

\displaystyle i{\mathfrak{D}}^{+}f(y,t)

\displaystyle:=\sup\left\{\limsup_{n\to\infty}d(f(y),\pi^{-1}(y_{n}))\,:\,(y_{n})_{n}\mbox{ is a minimizing sequence in }\eqref{equationu21}\right\}

\displaystyle i{\mathfrak{D}}^{-}f(y,t)

\displaystyle:=\inf\left\{\liminf_{n\to\infty}d(f(y),\pi^{-1}(y_{n}))\,:\,(y_{n})_{n}\mbox{ is a minimizing sequence in }\eqref{equationu21}\right\}

The map
(y,t)\mapsto u(y,t)
,
Y\times(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{\pm\infty\}
is obviously upper semicontinuous. The behavior of
u(\cdot,t)
is not trivial only in the set

\{y\in Y\,:\,d(f(y),\pi^{-1}(z))<\infty\mbox{ for some $z\in Y$ with }g(z)<\infty\},

and so we shall restrict our analysis in this set. In particular, it is sufficient to ask that
f
is a bounded section and
Y
is a bounded subset of
\mathbb{R}^{\kappa}
(see (
8
)). Moreover, in this set,
u(y,t)\in\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty\}
and so we also define

t_{*}(y):=\sup\{t\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\,:\,u(y,t)>-\infty\},

with the convention
t_{*}(y)=0
if
u(y,t)=-\infty
for all
t>0.

Notice that by (
8
), if
Y
is bounded, then
i{\mathfrak{D}}^{+}f<+\infty.
Moreover, in general we have that

i{\mathfrak{D}}^{+}f(y,t)\geq i{\mathfrak{D}}^{-}f(y,t),\quad\forall y\in Y,t\in\mathbb{R}^{+}.

Regarding the other inequality we have the following result.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
f\in C_{b}(Y,\mathbb{R}^{\kappa})
be a section of
\pi:\mathbb{R}^{\kappa}\to Y
. Then for every
y\in Y
and
0<t<s<t_{*}(y),
it holds:
i{\mathfrak{D}}^{+}f(y,t)\leq i{\mathfrak{D}}^{-}f(y,s).

Proof.

Fix
0<t<s
and
y\in Y.
Let’s make this proof under the additional condition that the infimum in
u(y,t)
and in
u(y,s)
are both attained and so they are minima (if not one should arrange a bit the proof but it is mainly the same idea). Hence take
y_{t},y_{s}
minima related respectively to
u(y,t)
and to
u(y,s)
and so, we deduce that

\displaystyle g(y_{t})+\frac{1}{2t}d^{2}(f(y),\pi^{-1}(y_{t}))

\displaystyle\leq g(y_{s})+\frac{1}{2t}d^{2}(f(y),\pi^{-1}(y_{s})),

\displaystyle g(y_{s})+\frac{1}{2s}d^{2}(f(y),\pi^{-1}(y_{s}))

\displaystyle\leq g(y_{t})+\frac{1}{2s}d^{2}(f(y),\pi^{-1}(y_{t})).

Summing up the previous equations, it holds

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{2t}-\frac{1}{2s}\right)d^{2}(f(y),\pi^{-1}(y_{t}))

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{1}{2t}-\frac{1}{2s}\right)d^{2}(f(y),\pi^{-1}(y_{s})),

and so, recall that
s>t
and
1/s<1/t,
we obtain

d^{2}(f(y),\pi^{-1}(y_{t}))\leq d^{2}(f(y),\pi^{-1}(y_{s})).

Now let the square root in the last inequality,
d(f(y),\pi^{-1}(y_{t}))\leq d(f(y),\pi^{-1}(y_{s}))
holds. More precisely,

d(f(y),\pi^{-1}(y_{t}))\leq d(f(y),\pi^{-1}(y_{s}))

is true for every choice
(y_{s},y_{t})
into the class of minimizers of
u(y,s)
and
u(y,t),
respectively. This gives us the sought inequality and the proof of the statement is complete.

∎

Proposition 4.2

(Semicontinuity of
i{\mathfrak{D}}^{\pm}
)
.

Let
y_{n}\to y
and
t_{n}\to t\in(0,t_{*}(y)).
Then,

\displaystyle i{\mathfrak{D}}^{-}f(y,t)

\displaystyle\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}i{\mathfrak{D}}^{-}f(y_{n},t_{n}),

\displaystyle i{\mathfrak{D}}^{+}f(y,t)

\displaystyle\geq\limsup_{n\to\infty}i{\mathfrak{D}}^{+}f(y_{n},t_{n}).

In particular, for every
y\in Y
the map
t\mapsto i{\mathfrak{D}}^{-}f(y,t)
is left continuous in
(0,t_{*}(y))
and the map
t\mapsto i{\mathfrak{D}}^{+}f(y,t)
is right continuous in
(0,t_{*}(y)).

Proof.

We adapt the proof as in
[
AGS14a
, Proposition 3.2]
. For every
n\in\mathbb{N},
let
(y_{n}^{\ell})_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}}
be a minimizing sequence for
u(y_{n},t_{n})
for which the limit of
d(f(y_{n}),\pi^{-1}(y^{\ell}_{n}))
as
\ell\to\infty
equals
i{\mathfrak{D}}^{-}(y_{n},t_{n}).
By Remark
8
,
\sup_{\ell,n}d(f(y_{n}),\pi^{-1}(y_{n}^{\ell}))<+\infty
and for any
n
we have that

\lim_{\ell\to\infty}f(y^{\ell}_{n})+\frac{1}{2t_{n}}d^{2}(f(y_{n}),\pi^{-1}(y^{\ell}_{n}))=u(y_{n},t_{n}).

Moreover, the upper semicontinuity of
(y,t)\mapsto u(y,t)
gives that
\limsup_{n}u(x_{n},t_{n})\leq u(y,t).
Since
d(f(y_{n}),\pi^{-1}(y^{\ell}_{n}))
is bounded, it follows that

\sup_{\ell}|d^{2}(f(y_{n}),\pi^{-1}(y^{\ell}_{n}))-d^{2}(f(y),\pi^{-1}(y_{n}^{\ell}))|

is infinitesimal and so by a diagonal argument we can find a sequence
n\mapsto\ell(n)
such that

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}f(y_{n}^{\ell(n)})+\frac{1}{2t}d^{2}(f(y),\pi^{-1}(y_{n}^{\ell(n)}))\leq u(y,t),

\displaystyle|d(f(y_{n})),\pi^{-1}(y^{\ell(n)}_{n}))-i{\mathfrak{D}}^{-}(y_{n},t_{n})|\leq\frac{1}{n}.

This implies that
y\mapsto y^{\ell(n)}_{n}
is a minimizing sequence for
u(y,t),
therefore

{\mathfrak{D}}^{-}f(y,t)\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}d(f(y),\pi^{-1}(y_{n}^{\ell(n)}))=\liminf_{n\to\infty}d(f(y_{n}),\pi^{-1}(y_{n}^{\ell(n)}))=\liminf_{n\to\infty}{\mathfrak{D}}^{-}f(y_{n},t_{n}).

Notice that in the equality we used that fact that
y_{n}\to y
which we will prove in Proposition
4.1
(ii).
In a similar way, if we choose instead sequence
(y^{\ell}_{n})_{\ell}
on which the supremum in the definition of
i{\mathfrak{D}}^{+}f(y_{n},t_{n})
is attained, we obtain the upper semicontinuity property of
i{\mathfrak{D}}^{+}f
.

∎

We conclude this section with an easy result when
f
is an intrinsically Lipschitz section. The proof is similar to
[
DD22b
, Proposition 3.4]
.

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
f\in ILS(Y,\mathbb{R}^{\kappa})
and let
L\geq 1
be its Lipschitz constant. Then,

2tL\geq i{\mathfrak{D}}^{+}f(y,t)\geq i{\mathfrak{D}}^{-}f(y,t),

for every
y\in Y
and
t\in\mathbb{R}^{+}.

Proof.

We can suppose
u(y,t)<g(y);
indeed, if not, it must be

u(y,t)=g(y)\quad\Rightarrow\quad i{\mathfrak{D}}^{+}f(y,t)=0.

Hence we can take a minimizing sequence
(y_{n})_{n}
for
u(y,t)
so that definitively

g(y_{n})+\frac{1}{2t}d^{2}(f(y),\pi^{-1}(y_{n}))\leq g(y).

Then,

d^{2}(f(y),\pi^{-1}(y_{n}))\leq 2td(f(y_{n}),f(y))\leq 2tLd(f(y),\pi^{-1}(y_{n})),

where in the second inequality we used the Lipschitz property of
f.
Finally, dividing for
d(f(y),\pi^{-1}(y_{n}))
and taking the limsup in
n
, the thesis follows.
∎

4.2.
The time derivative of
u(\cdot,t)

We find a precise estimate of the time derivative of the Hopf-Lax semigroup in terms of
i{\mathfrak{D}}^{\pm}f(y,t);
in order to do this fact, we give an alternative proof of Lipschitz property of
u(\cdot,t).
Moreover, we recall that semiconcave map
g
on an open interval are local quadratic perturbations of concave maps; they inherit from concave functions all pointwise differentiability properties, as existence of right and left derivatives
\frac{d^{-}}{dt}g\geq\frac{d^{+}}{dt}g
which is important for the next result.

Proposition 4.4

(Time derivative of
u(\cdot,t)
)
.

The map
(0,t_{*}(y))\ni t\mapsto u(\cdot,t)
is locally Lipschitz and locally semiconcave. For all
t\in(0,t_{*}(y))
it satisfies

(22)

\displaystyle\frac{d^{-}}{dt}u(y,t)

\displaystyle=-\frac{(i{\mathfrak{D}}^{-}f(y,t))^{2}}{2t^{2}},

\displaystyle\frac{d^{+}}{dt}u(y,t)

\displaystyle=-\frac{(i{\mathfrak{D}}^{+}f(y,t))^{2}}{2t^{2}},

In particular,
t\mapsto u(\cdot,t)
is differentiable at
t\in(0,t_{*}(y))
if and only if
i{\mathfrak{D}}^{+}f(y,t)=i{\mathfrak{D}}^{-}f(y,t).

Proof.

We follows
[
AGS14a
, Proposition 3.3]
(see also
[
DD22b
, Proposition 3.9]
). Let
(y_{t}^{n})_{n},(y_{s}^{n})_{n}
be minimizing sequences for
u(y,t)
and
u(y,s)
. We have

\displaystyle u(y,s)-u(y,t)

\displaystyle\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{d^{2}(f(y)),\pi^{-1}(y_{t}^{n}))}{2}\left(\frac{1}{s}-\frac{1}{t}\right),

\displaystyle u(y,s)-u(y,t)

\displaystyle\geq\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{d^{2}(f(y)),\pi^{-1}(y_{s}^{n}))}{2}\left(\frac{1}{s}-\frac{1}{t}\right).

Now we have two cases:
s>t
or vice versa. In the first case, we get

(23)

\displaystyle\frac{(i{\mathfrak{D}}^{-}f(y,s))^{2}}{2}\left(\frac{1}{s}-\frac{1}{t}\right)\leq u(y,t)-u(y,s)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(i{\mathfrak{D}}^{+}f(y,t))^{2}}{2}\left(\frac{1}{s}-\frac{1}{t}\right),

recalling that
\lim_{s\to t}i{\mathfrak{D}}^{-}f(y,s)=i{\mathfrak{D}}^{+}f(y,t),
a division by
s-t
(noting that
1/s-1/t=-(s-t)/st
) and a limit as
s\to t
gives the identity for the right derivative in (
22
). In a similar way, we can obtain the left derivative.

Moreover, the local Lipschitz continuity follows by (
23
) recalling that
i{\mathfrak{D}}^{\pm}f(y,\cdot)
are locally bounded functions; we easily get the quantitative bound

\left\|\frac{d}{dt}u(y,t)\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})}\leq\frac{1}{2\tau_{1}^{2}}\|i{\mathfrak{D}}^{+}f(y,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})},

for every
0<\tau_{1}<\tau_{2}<t_{*}(y).
Finally, since the distributional derivative of the map
t\mapsto(i{\mathfrak{D}}^{+}f(y,t))^{2}/(2t^{2})
is locally bounded from below, we also deduce that
t\mapsto u(y,\cdot)
is locally semiconcave, as desired. Hence, the proof is complete.
∎

Remark 4.5
.

We want to underline that since

(0,t_{*}(y))\ni t\mapsto u(y,t)
is
globally
Lipschitz.

Proposition 4.6
.

Let
f:Y\to\mathbb{R}^{\kappa}
be a continuous section of
\pi.
Then, for
t\in(0,t_{*}(y))
it holds

(24)

\displaystyle\limsup_{y\to z}\frac{u(z,t)-u(y,t)}{d(f(y),f(z))}\leq\frac{i{\mathfrak{D}}^{+}f(z,t)}{2t}

Proof.

We use a similar technique as in
[
AGS14a
, Proposition 3.4]
(see also
[
DD22b
, Proposition 3.11]
).

Let
y,z\in Y
such that

We want to show that

u(z,t)-u(y,t)\leq\frac{d(f(z),f(y))}{2t}\left(i{\mathfrak{D}}^{-}f(y,t)+d(f(z),\pi^{-1}(y))\right).

Let
(y_{n})_{n}
be a minimizing sequence for

on which the infimum in the definition of

\displaystyle\leq\frac{d(f(y),f(z))}{2t}(d(f(z),\pi^{-1}(y))+i{\mathfrak{D}}^{-}f(y,t)),

where in the last inequality we used the fact that

(see (

)). Hence (

) holds. Now dividing both sides of (

) by

and taking the

), since Proposition
