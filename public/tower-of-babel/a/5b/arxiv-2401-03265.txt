Finite Hilbert systems for Weak Kleene logics
Vitor Greati, Sérgio Marcelino, Umberto Rivieccio

Abstract
Multiple-conclusion Hilbert-style systems allow us to finitely axiomatize every logic defined by a finite matrix. Having obtained such axiomatizations for Paraconsistent Weak Kleene and Bochvar-Kleene logics, we modify them by replacing the multiple-conclusion rules with carefully selected single-conclusion ones. In this way we manage to introduce the first finite Hilbert-style single-conclusion axiomatizations for these logics.

Introduction

In his classic book
Kleene 1952
,
S.C. Kleene employs two different sets of three-valued truth tables
to introduce the logical systems
known, in today’s parlance, as
Strong Kleene
and
Weak Kleene
logics.
The latter,
independently considered in 1937 by Bochvar
Bochvar 1938
;
Bochvar and Bergmann 1981
,
is also called
Bochvar-Kleene logic
(henceforth
\mathbf{BK}
).

From a formal point of view, the main difference between the strong and the weak Kleene tables
is that in the latter the third truth value (
\mathtt{u}
)
exhibits an
infectious

behaviour: any interaction
between
\mathtt{u}
and either of the classical values (
\mathtt{t}
and
\mathtt{f}
)
delivers

\mathtt{u}
itself. This feature, as we shall see, makes the resulting
logics somewhat less tractable than most well-known three-valued logics, both from an algebraic and a proof-theoretic point of view.

From the Bochvar-Kleene tables two logics naturally arise. One (
\mathbf{BK}
)
is obtained by choosing the single truth value
\mathtt{t}
as designated; the other,
which we call

Paraconsistent Weak Kleene
(
\mathbf{PWK}
),
results from designating both
\mathtt{t}
and
\mathtt{u}
.
Concerning both these systems, a positive and a negative result are particularly
worth mentioning in the present context.

The good news is that
both logics are closely related, from a formal point of view, to the classical: more precisely,

\mathbf{PWK}
and
\mathbf{BK}
are, respectively, the
left
and
right variable inclusion companions
of classical logic (more on this below). The bad news, on the other hand,
is that
for neither of these logics a
finite
Hilbert-style axiomatization currently exists (this observation was made in
Greati et al.
, to which we also refer the reader for further background
and examples of axiomatizations of three-valued logics).
In other words, we do not know
whether these logics admit a
finite basis

Wójcicki 1988
.
This is precisely the gap we wish to bridge in the present paper, thus solving a fundamental
open problem concerning these logics.

According to Bochvar’s original paper
Bochvar 1938
;
Bochvar and Bergmann 1981
,
the intended applications
of the
Bochvar-Kleene logic are in the formalization of paradoxes, future contingent statements
and presuppositions (see e.g.
Ferguson 2014

for a more recent computational interpretation of
\mathbf{BK}
).
The third value is therefore meant to
represent nonsensical statements,
or corrupted data in the interpretation given by Kleene.
This explains the infectious behaviour of the third value,
because any complex formula having a nonsensical or paradoxical subformula
should be regarded as nonsensical/paradoxical too.

Paraconsistent Weak Kleene (
\mathbf{PWK}
) seems to have been considered already by S. Halldén
in his 1949 monograph
Halldén 1949
, and two decades later by A. Prior
Prior 1967
, but has only recently been studied in more depth (see e.g.
Bonzio et al. 2017

and
Da Ré et al. 2018
, the latter of which
explores applications to the theory of truth).

The proof theory of
\mathbf{BK}
and
\mathbf{PWK}

has been intensively developed in the
last years employing different
formalisms and approaches, like
sequent calculi
Paoli and Pra Baldi 2020
;
Bonzio et al. 2022b
, natural deduction
Petrukhin 2017
;
Belikov 2021

and tableaux
Paoli and Pra Baldi 2020
;
Bonzio et al. 2022b
.
In addition, a number of Hilbert-like systems for these logics exist in the literature
Baaz et al. 1996
;
Bonzio et al. 2021
;
Bonzio and Baldi 2021
.
However,
as explained in
Section
3
,
none of them are finite Hilbert-style systems in the usual sense (we shall call these
Set-Fmla
H-systems
).

For

\mathbf{BK}
, a finite but non-standard axiomatization may be obtained
by taking
any complete
Set-Fmla
H-system
for classical logic (with
modus ponens
as its only rule) and, while keeping all the axioms, replacing
modus ponens
by a restricted version that satisfies the
containment condition

(
Bonzio et al. 2017
, Prop. 4)
,
Bonzio et al. 2021
.
The finite
Hilbert-style system for
\mathbf{BK}

we introduce here will instead be standard, i.e. consisting of a finite number of axioms and unrestricted rule schemas.

For both
\mathbf{BK}
and
\mathbf{PWK}
,
infinite
Hilbert-style systems
may be found in
Bonzio et al. 2021
;
Bonzio and Baldi 2021
; we note that the completeness proofs
found
in these papers are essentially algebraic, and rely on
the above-mentioned observation that
\mathbf{BK}
and

\mathbf{PWK}
are, respectively,
the right and the left variable inclusion companion
of classical logic
(
Urquhart 2001
, Thm. 4, p. 258)
,
Bonzio et al. 2021
;
Caleiro et al. 2020
.

In the present paper we follow a two-step strategy.
Relying on the general observation
that every finite logical matrix can be finitely axiomatized by means of a Hibert-style
multiple-conclusion system (here called a
Set-Set
H-system
),
we first introduce finite
Set-Set
H-systems for
\mathbf{BK}
and
\mathbf{PWK}
,
then show how from these

Set-Fmla
axiomatizations may be obtained preserving finiteness.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
we formally introduce
the language and semantics of
\mathbf{BK}
and
\mathbf{PWK}
. Section
3

contains as much theory of
Set-Set
and
Set-Fmla
H-systems as we shall need in order to introduce our
axiomatic systems for
\mathbf{PWK}
and
\mathbf{BK}
. The former is then presented and shown to be complete
in Section
4
(
\mathbf{PWK}
),
the latter in Section
5
(
\mathbf{BK}
).
The final Section
6
contains concluding remarks and suggestions for
future research.

3
Basics of Hilbert-style axiomatizations

Logical matrices are a semantical way to
define
Set-Fmla
and

Set-Set

logics. Another popular way are
proof systems
,
which
manipulate
syntactical objects
envisaging the construction of derivations
that bear witnesses to consecutions.
Proof systems can be classified
with respect to the
proof formalism
they belong to,
based mainly on the objects they manipulate
and the shape of their rules of inference and derivations.
Each proof system induces a logic
based on the derivations one may
build via its rules of inference.

In this work, we are interested in

Hilbert-style proof systems
,
or
H-systems
for short.
As main characteristics,
these
have (a) rules of inference with the same shape of the
consecutions of the induced logic;
(b) derivations as trees labelled
with sets of formulas;
and (c) the fact that they represent
a logical basis for the logics they induce, meaning that the latter is the
least logic containing the rules of
inference of the system
Wójcicki 1988
.

Before the work of Shoesmith and
Smiley
Shoesmith and Smiley 1978
, rules of inference in
H-systems were constrained to
be
Set-Fmla
statements, that is, pairs
(\Gamma,\delta)\in\mathsf{Pow}(L_{\Sigma}(P))\times L_{\Sigma}(P)
,
usually denoted by
\frac{\Gamma}{\delta}{\scriptstyle}
,
where
\Gamma
is called
the
antecedent
and
\delta
,
the
succedent
of the rule.
For this reason, we call them
Set-Fmla

rules of inference

and sets thereof constitute
Set-Fmla
or
traditional H-systems
.
They are also referred to
as
single-conclusion
H-systems.
In the above-mentioned work, H-systems were
generalized to allow for multiple formulas
in the succedent of rules of inference.
In other words, rules of inference
became
Set-Set
statements, that is, pairs
of the form

(\Gamma,\Delta)\in\mathsf{Pow}(L_{\Sigma}(P))\times\mathsf{Pow}(L_{\Sigma}(P))
, which we usually denote by

\frac{\Gamma}{\Delta}{\scriptstyle}
.
Collections of these so-called

Set-Set
rules of inference

constitute what we refer to as
Set-Set
or
multiple-conclusion

H-systems
.

In both formalisms, rules of inference are
usually presented
schematically
, that is,
as being induced by applying

\Sigma
-substitutions over
representative rules called
rule schemas
.
An H-system is
finite
when it
is presented via a finite number of rule schemas.

Users of traditional H-systems are
accustomed to derivations that are
sequences of formulas, where each
member is either a premise or results
from the application of a rule of
inference of the H-system on previous formulas in
the sequence. A proof in a traditional H-system
\mathcal{H}
of a
statement
(\Phi,\psi)

is then a derivation
where the set of premises is
\Phi

and the last formula is
\psi
. Equivalently, we could
see these derivations as rooted labelled linear
trees whose nodes are labelled with sets of formulas,
where the root node is labelled with
the set of premises
and the child of each non-leaf node
\mathsf{n}

is labelled with the label
\Gamma
of
\mathsf{n}
plus the succedent of a rule of inference of
\mathcal{H}

whose antecedent is contained in
\Gamma
.
A proof of
(\Phi,\psi)
,
then, is just a linear tree
whose root node is labelled with

\Phi
(or a subset thereof)
and whose leaf node contains
\psi
.

Every
Set-Fmla
H-system
\mathcal{H}

induces a
Set-Fmla
logic

\vdash_{\mathcal{H}}

such that

\Phi\vdash_{\mathcal{H}}\psi

if and only if there is a proof of

(\Phi,\psi)
in
\mathcal{H}
.
Given a
Set-Fmla
logic

\vdash

and a
Set-Fmla
H-system
\mathcal{H}
,
we say that
\mathcal{H}
is
sound
for
\vdash
when
\vdash_{\mathcal{H}}\,\subseteq\,\vdash
;
that
\mathcal{H}
is
complete for
\vdash
when

\vdash\,\subseteq\,\vdash_{\mathcal{H}}
;
and that
\mathcal{H}

axiomatizes
\vdash
(or is an
axiomatization of
)
\vdash
when it is both sound
and complete for
\vdash
, that is, when
\vdash\;=\;\vdash_{\mathcal{H}}
.

The following is a well-known

\displaystyle\frac{\varnothing}{(\neg r\to\neg q)\to((\neg r\to q)\to r)}{\scriptstyle{\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{CL}}}_{3}}\quad\frac{p,p\to q}{q}{\scriptstyle{\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{CL}}}_{4}}

Here is a proof in

.
If
\Phi,\varphi\lor\psi,\delta\vdash_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{BK}}}\neg\delta
, then

\Phi,\varphi\lor\psi\vdash_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{BK}}}\neg\delta
.

Proof.

We begin with the case
\delta=\varphi
.
Let
\mathsf{t}=\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{n}
be a proof witnessing that

\Phi,\varphi\lor\psi,\varphi\vdash_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{BK}}}\neg\varphi
.
In case no application of
{\mathbf{BK}}_{1\star}
is used in
\mathsf{t}
,
we have

\Phi,\varphi\lor\psi\vdash_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{BK}}}\varphi\to\neg\varphi\vdash_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{BK}}}\neg\varphi\lor\neg\varphi\vdash_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{BK}}}\neg\varphi
by Proposition
18
and
{\mathbf{BK}}_{22}
, as desired.
On the other hand, suppose that
\gamma_{k}
was the formula produced by the first application
of
{\mathbf{BK}}_{1\star}
. Then
k>2
and there are

\gamma_{m_{1}}
and
\gamma_{m_{2}}=\neg\gamma_{m_{1}}
, with
m_{1},m_{2}<k
, such that

\Phi,\varphi\lor\psi,\varphi\vdash_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{BK}}}\gamma_{m_{1}}
and

\Phi,\varphi\lor\psi,\varphi\vdash_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{BK}}}\neg\gamma_{m_{1}}
.
By Proposition
18
, then, we have
(a):
\Phi,\varphi\lor\psi\vdash_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{BK}}}\varphi\to\gamma_{m_{1}}
,
and

\Phi,\varphi\lor\psi\vdash_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{BK}}}\varphi\to\neg\gamma_{m_{1}}
,
and so, by Corollary
16
,

\Phi,\varphi\lor\psi\vdash_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{BK}}}\varphi\to(\gamma_{m_{1}}\land\neg\gamma_{m_{1}})
,
that is,

\Phi,\varphi\lor\psi\vdash_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{BK}}}\neg\varphi\lor(\gamma_{m_{1}}\land\neg\gamma_{m_{1}})\vdash_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{BK}}}(\gamma_{m_{1}}\land\neg\gamma_{m_{1}})\lor\neg\varphi
.
But
\Phi,\varphi\lor\psi\vdash_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{BK}}}\neg(\gamma_{m_{1}}\land\neg\gamma_{m_{1}})

by (a) and
{\mathbf{BK}}_{30}
,
and thus

\Phi,\varphi\lor\psi\vdash_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{BK}}}\neg\varphi

by
{\mathbf{BK}}_{20}
.
Now, for
\delta=\psi
,
we have that from

\Phi,\varphi\lor\psi,\psi\vdash_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{BK}}}\neg\psi

and the rule
{\mathbf{BK}}_{23}
, we get

\Phi,\psi\lor\varphi,\psi\vdash_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{BK}}}\neg\psi
.
By Proposition
20
, we get

\Phi,\psi\lor\varphi\vdash_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{BK}}}\neg\psi

and, again by
{\mathbf{BK}}_{23}
, we have

\Phi,\varphi\lor\psi\vdash_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{BK}}}\neg\psi
.
∎

A consequence of the previous result
is the following.

Proposition 21
.

Let
\delta_{1},\delta_{2}\in\left\{{\varphi,\psi}\right\}\subseteq L_{{\Sigma_{\land\lor\neg}}}(P)

with
\delta_{1}\neq\delta_{2}
,
and
\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{n}
be a proof in
\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{BK}}
witnessing
that
\Phi,\varphi\lor\psi,\delta_{1}\vdash_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{BK}}}\gamma
.
If the rule
{\mathbf{BK}}_{1\star}
was applied
in such proof, then
\Phi,\varphi\lor\psi\vdash_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{BK}}}\delta_{2}
.

Proof.

We will prove the case
\delta_{1}=\varphi

and the other will be analogous.
Suppose that
\gamma_{k}
was the formula produced by the first application
of
{\mathbf{BK}}_{1\star}
. Then
k>2
and there are

\gamma_{m_{1}}
and
\gamma_{m_{2}}=\neg\gamma_{m_{1}}
, with
m_{1},m_{2}<k
, such that

\Phi,\varphi\lor\psi,\varphi\vdash_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{BK}}}\gamma_{m_{1}}
and

\Phi,\varphi\lor\psi,\varphi\vdash_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{BK}}}\neg\gamma_{m_{1}}
.
But then, by
{\mathbf{BK}}_{1\star}
,
we have

\Phi,\varphi\lor\psi,\varphi\vdash_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{BK}}}\neg\varphi
.
By Proposition
20
, then, we have

\Phi,\varphi\lor\psi\vdash_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{BK}}}\neg\varphi
,
and then

\Phi,\varphi\lor\psi\vdash_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{BK}}}\psi

by
{\mathbf{BK}}_{20}
.
∎

In Proposition
11
, we proved that

\mathbf{BK}
does not allow to express
a connective satisfying (
disj
).
Nevertheless, we now show that having

\varphi\lor\psi
in the context is also enough
to recover this result.

Lemma 22
.

For all
\Phi,\left\{{\varphi,\psi,\gamma}\right\}\subseteq L_{{\Sigma_{\land\lor\neg}}}(P)
, we have

\Phi,\varphi\lor\psi,\varphi\vdash_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{BK}}}\gamma

and

\Phi,\varphi\lor\psi,\psi\vdash_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{BK}}}\gamma
,
if, and only if,

\Phi,\varphi\lor\psi\vdash_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{BK}}}\gamma
.

Proof.

The right-to-left direction is obvious by
reflexivity of the consequence relation.
For the left-to-right direction,
suppose that
\mathsf{t}_{1}
and
\mathsf{t}_{2}
are witnesses of

\Phi,\varphi\lor\psi,\varphi\vdash_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{BK}}}\gamma

and

\Phi,\varphi\lor\psi,\psi\vdash_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{BK}}}\gamma
,
respectively.
Consider the following cases:

1.

In both there are no applications of
{\mathbf{BK}}_{1\star}
:
then, by Proposition
18
,
we have

\Phi,\varphi\lor\psi\vdash_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{BK}}}\varphi\to\gamma

and

\Phi,\varphi\lor\psi\vdash_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{BK}}}\psi\to\gamma
.
Thus

\Phi,\varphi\lor\psi\vdash_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{BK}}}(\varphi\lor\psi)\to\gamma
, by
{\mathbf{BK}}_{28}
,
and

\Phi,\varphi\lor\psi\vdash_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{BK}}}\gamma
, by
{\mathbf{BK}}_{26}
.

2.

If there is an application of
{\mathbf{BK}}_{1\star}
in
\mathsf{t}_{1}
:
then, by Proposition
21
,
we have

\Phi,\varphi\lor\psi\vdash_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{BK}}}\psi
.
Then, by transitivity
considering

\Phi,\varphi\lor\psi,\psi\vdash_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{BK}}}\gamma
,
we obtain

\Phi,\varphi\lor\psi\vdash_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{BK}}}\gamma
.

3.

If there is an application of
{\mathbf{BK}}_{1\star}
in
\mathsf{t}_{2}
: similar to the previous case.

∎

Finally we get to the desired axiomatization result.

Theorem 23
.

\vdash_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{BK}}}\;=\;\vdash_{\mathbf{BK}}
.

Proof.

We will show that

\Phi\vdash_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{BK}}}\psi
if, and only if,

\Phi\rhd_{\mathsf{R}_{\mathbf{BK}\star}}{\left\{{\psi}\right\}}
.
The left-to-right direction easily follows,
since every rule of
\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{BK}}

is sound with respect to the matrix of
\mathbf{BK}
by Lemma
13
,
and thus derivable in
\mathsf{R}_{\mathbf{BK}\star}
.
From the right to the left,
we will show by induction on the structure of derivations in
\mathsf{R}_{\mathbf{BK}\star}

that
P(\mathsf{t})
: if
\mathsf{t}
witnesses that
\Phi\rhd_{\mathsf{R}_{\mathbf{BK}\star}}{\left\{{\psi}\right\}}
,
then there is a proof in
\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{BK}}
bearing witness to
\Phi\vdash_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{BK}}}\psi
.
In the base case,
\mathsf{t}
has a single node, meaning that
\psi\in\Phi
, and we are done by
reflexivity of
\vdash_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{BK}}}
.
In the inductive step, we assume
P(\mathsf{t}^{\prime})
for each subtree
\mathsf{t}^{\prime}
of
\mathsf{t}

and consider
\mathsf{t}
resulting from an application of the rules of
\mathsf{R}_{\mathbf{BK}\star}
.
Let us consider three cases:

1.

\mathsf{t}
results from a rule that is derivable in
\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{BK}}
: here, there is
nothing to do, as the same rule may be applied to produce the desired derivation.

2.

\mathsf{t}
results from an application of
{\mathbf{BK}}_{1}^{\rhd}
: use
{\mathbf{BK}}_{1\star}
instead.

3.

\mathsf{t}
results from an application of
{\mathbf{BK}}_{20}^{\rhd}
:
if the root of
\mathsf{t}
is labelled with
\Gamma
, then

\gamma\lor\delta\in\Gamma
,
and we have, by the induction hypothesis,
(a):
\Gamma,\gamma\lor\delta,\gamma\vdash_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{BK}}}\psi
and
(b):
\Gamma,\gamma\lor\delta,\delta\vdash_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{BK}}}\psi
.
By Lemma
22
, then,
we obtain the desired result.

∎

6
Concluding remarks

Taking stock of what we achieved in
the previous sections,
we highlight that we have settled fundamental questions
regarding
\mathbf{BK}
and
\mathbf{PWK}
, two logics that are among the main subjects of this Special Issue.
We also wish to mention an interesting corollary of our results, namely
that
some finite subset of the axioms employed in the papers
Bonzio et al. 2021
;
Bonzio and Baldi 2021

must already suffice to axiomatize each of the two logics. We leave this observation as a suggestion for
future developments.

Besides the intrinsic interest in the results established above,
the present paper may also be seen as another illustration of
the differences in expressive power among the various
available proof-theoretic formalisms in logic, and in particular between

Set-Set
over
Set-Fmla
H-systems. The latter are obviously
less expressive than the former – even weaker if compared to sequent systems –
even though they afford more fine-grained tools for comparing and also for combining logics (in particular when one wishes to introduce
the least possible interactions),
as recent results amply demonstrate
Marcelino and Caleiro 2017
;
Caleiro and Marcelino 2023
.

Another direction for future research worth mentioning is the study of these and other logics associated to the algebra
\mathbf{B}_{\mathtt{u}}
(and other three-valued algebras)
in the setting of different kinds of H-systems. In particular,
a two-dimensional version of
Set-Set
H-systems
Greati et al. 2021
;
Greati and Marcos 2022
, whose induced logics are the so-called
B
-consequence relations

C. Blasio 2017
,
may be employed as a uniform setting for investigating

pure consequence relations
(like
\mathbf{BK}
and
\mathbf{PWK}
), their intersection (
order-theoretic consequence relations
) and
mixed consequence relations
(we use here the terminology of
Chemla et al. 2017
),
the latter being non-Tarskian consequence relations
(lacking either reflexivity
Malinowski 1994
or transitivity
Frankowski 2004
).

Not only can
a
two-dimensional logic express all of these very different notions of logics in the same logical environment: we also
have that it has a neat analytic two-dimensional axiomatization.
That is, this two-dimensional logic has not only great theoretical value due to its expressiveness, but also constitutes an important tool for using the above-mentioned logics and studying their properties.
