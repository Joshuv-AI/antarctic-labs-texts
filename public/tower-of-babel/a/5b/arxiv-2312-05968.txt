Jumpstarting Surgical Computer Vision
Deepak Alapatt, Aditya Murali, Vinkle Srivastav, Pietro Mascagni, AI4SafeChole Consortium, Nicolas Padoy

Abstract
Purpose: General consensus amongst researchers and industry points to a lack of large, representative annotated datasets as the biggest obstacle to progress in the field of surgical data science. Self-supervised learning represents a solution to part of this problem, removing the reliance on annotations. However, the robustness of current self-supervised learning methods to domain shifts remains unclear, limiting our understanding of its utility for leveraging diverse sources of surgical data. Methods: In this work, we employ self-supervised learning to flexibly leverage diverse surgical datasets, thereby learning task-agnostic representations that can be used for various surgical downstream tasks. Based on this approach, to elucidate the impact of pre-training on downstream task performance, we explore 22 different pre-training dataset combinations by modulating three variables: source hospital, type of surgical procedure, and pre-training scale (number of videos). We then finetune the resulting model initializations on three diverse downstream tasks: namely, phase recognition and critical view of safety in laparoscopic cholecystectomy and phase recognition in laparoscopic hysterectomy. Results: Controlled experimentation highlights sizable boosts in performance across various tasks, datasets, and labeling budgets. However, this performance is intricately linked to the composition of the pre-training dataset, robustly proven through several study stages. Conclusion: The composition of pre-training datasets can severely affect the effectiveness of SSL methods for various downstream tasks and should critically inform future data collection efforts to scale the application of SSL methodologies. Keywords: Self-Supervised Learning, Transfer Learning, Surgical Computer Vision, Endoscopic Videos, Critical View of Safety, Phase Recognition

Introduction

Over the last decade, developments in data science have had a sweeping effect spanning almost all areas of healthcare. While this has catalyzed the development of numerous clinically impactful applications in fields such as pathology and radiology, the same cannot be said for surgery
[
14
]
. In 2019, a large survey, conducted by the Surgical Data Science Initiative
[
13
]
, primarily attributed this lack of success stories to a lack of representative annotated data
[
14
]
. On the other hand, it is also unclear whether available datasets are being well-utilized when not directly specific to the task at hand. For example, a recently conducted survey
[
6
]
on participant practices across 80 biomedical image analysis competitions, revealed that only 9% of all respondents that used deep learning utilized data outside what was provided for the competition (excluding pre-trained models). A natural explanation may be that available public datasets are often small, specific, and mono-centric
[
14
]
, making it unclear whether they could be leveraged to improve model performance.

In the broader computer vision domain, one of the most effective strategies for leveraging related datasets is transfer learning. For deep learning models, a neural network is pre-trained on a first dataset and task, then the resulting learned features are used as an initialization, or a “jumpstart”, for learning the task of interest on a smaller labeled dataset. This simple approach for visual representation learning has proven almost unreasonably effective, contributing significantly to pushing the state of the art for a wide range of tasks including segmentation
[
11
]
, object detection
[
12
]
, and activity recognition
[
2
]
. Since then, this paradigm of pre-training and then fine-tuning has become the de facto standard, with pre-trained initializations for large-scale general computer vision datasets, such as ImageNet
[
19
]
, now widely available for integration into other work. We take inspiration from these developments, hypothesizing that similar access to more appropriate (see Fig.
1
) domain-specific initializations can effectively consolidate disparate datasets in the field and channel them toward widely enabling early prototyping for various clinical applications and, eventually, developing more success stories for surgical data science.

SSL methodologies in the broader computer vision domain have seen significant developments in design, moving from early heuristic-based pre-training tasks, such as predicting image augmentations, to more elegant strategies that directly learn to predict latent representations specific to the content of an image. Until recently, these shifts in design were slow to be adopted into the surgical domain with most work focusing on hand-crafted pre-text tasks, such as colorization

However, in the same study, the authors demonstrate through extensive experimentation that when applied correctly, these methods achieve state-of-the-art results on a range of tasks, benchmarks, and labeled-data regimes. Since then, a number of works have explored the value of pre-training similar methods on a massive scale by aggregating public datasets

1.

3.

We validate our findings at scale (

Methodology

In this section, we begin by enumerating the various datasets we use and the downstream tasks we evaluate. Then, we describe our training procedure for generating each model initialization and finetuning for each downstream task and finally we describe our study stages.

2.1
Datasets

We use 9 different surgical computer vision datasets of minimally invasive abdominal procedures including: AutoLaparo
[

]
, Cholec80
[

]
,
Endoscapes
[

, HeiChole

, Laparo425
[

, and MultiChole2022
[

. We also use three unlabeled cholecystectomy datasets: MultiChole2023, StrasChole200, and StrasChole400, containing 205, 200, and 400 randomly selected cholecystectomy videos, respectively. StrasChole200 and StrasChole400 contain videos only from Nouvel Hôpital Civil, Strasbourg, France while MultiChole2023, an extension of MultiChole2022, contains 205 videos, with
\sim
50 videos from each of the 4 following centers through the AI4SafeChole Consortium: Policlinico Universitario Agostino Gemelli, Rome; Azienda Ospedaliero-Universitaria Sant’Andrea, Rome; Fondazione IRCCS Ca’ Granda Ospedale Maggiore Policlinico, Milan; and Monaldi Hospital, Naples.

These datasets span 10 different surgical procedures (several cholecystectomy datasets, 8 others in Laparo420, 1 other in AutoLaparo), and 7 centers (several Strasbourg datasets, 4 others in MultiCholec2023, 2 aggregated in HeiChole).
Lastly, for fair experimentation, we remove 5 videos from Laparo425 that are found in the test sets for the downstream tasks, yielding Laparo420.
Using these datasets, we create our various pre-training data combinations.

2.2
Downstream Tasks

We consider 3 downstream tasks, each using a different dataset: phase recognition (on both Cholec80 and AutoLaparo) and critical view of safety (CVS) prediction (Endoscapes), thus spanning 2 procedures (cholecystectomy, hysterectomy) and 2 centers (Strasbourg, Hong Kong). Our selected downstream tasks are also highly diverse: CVS criteria assessment (3-class binary classification) is one of several tasks proposed for the prevention of bile duct injuries, all of which require fine-grained analysis.
Meanwhile, phase recognition is a widely studied and benchmarked task in surgical computer vision, that, unlike CVS classification, requires long-range temporal reasoning.

For each of the 2 phase recognition datasets, we consider one high-label data regime using 100% of the available training labels (40 videos for Cholec80, 10 videos for Autolaparo) and two low-label data regimes using 5 videos and 3 videos, respectively. For the CVS task, we similarly consider 3 label settings using 120 videos (100% of the train set), 40 videos (25% of the train set), and 20 videos (12.5% of the train set). For every low-label setting on each downstream task, we present results using the mean and std over 3 randomly sub-selected splits to mitigate the effects of selection bias.

2.3
Training Procedure

For all experiments, we use Momentum Contrast (MoCo v2)
[
3
]
to pre-train a ResNet-50 feature extractor.
MoCo v2 is a contrastive learning method that learns powerful image representations by minimizing differences between embeddings of augmented views of the same image to be similar and maximizing distance between embeddings of different images.
We use MoCo v2 for all pre-training motivated by the results in
[
17
]
, where it is shown to outperform several competing methods for surgical video analysis tasks.

This pre-training procedure results in a trained ResNet-50 backbone parametrized by model weights
\Theta_{c}
for each pre-training data configuration
c
.
We use each
\Theta_{c}
to initialize a ResNet-50 classifier that we finetune separately for each downstream task while varying the number of labeled videos.
Finally, for the phase recognition downstream tasks (Cholec80, AutoLaparo), we additionally train TeCNO
[
5
]
on top of the finetuned ResNet-50 features to enable temporal reasoning as in
[
17
]
.

2.4
Study Stages

Stage 1.

The purpose of this stage is to establish performance baselines for how robust SSL pre-training is to extreme domain shifts. Through these experiments, we aim to better understand how related datasets (e.g. laparoscopic abdominal procedure videos of a different surgery type) could be leveraged for various tasks. To do this we perform 4 cross-over experiments examining the added value over ImageNet initializations: (1) where the downstream task is from the same dataset as the pre-training data (matched finetuning), and (2) where the downstream task and dataset are different from the pre-training dataset (unmatched finetuning). Note that both downstream tasks are conceptually similar (surgical phase recognition); however, the two datasets span two geographic regions (Hong Kong and France), two anatomical targets (Gallbladder and Uterus), and likely stylistic and functional differences in workflow and instrumentation.

We adopt this process for two datasets, Cholec80 and AutoLaparo, reporting both matched and unmatched finetuning performance for both downstream phase recognition tasks. We only utilized public datasets in this stage to additionally illustrate the potential of aggregating already accessible datasets.

3
Results and Discussion

3.1
Stage 1: Cross-over Experiments

In this subsection, we examine if and how two public datasets (Cholec80 and AutoLaparo) for surgical phase recognition could be leveraged using SSL-based pre-training initializations. In Table
2
, we see that for the matched pre-trainings, i.e. performing SSL pre-training on the same dataset as the downstream task, there are sizable boosts in performance; particularly so when many labeled examples are not available for supervised finetuning. Quantitatively, when using as few as 3 labeled videos, we see an 11.3% and 8.7% boost over the standard ImageNet initialization when performing matched pre-training on Cholec80 and AutoLaparo, respectively. On the other hand, except for the lowest labeled setting (3 labeled videos), the unmatched pre-training experiments perform on par or worse than using just an ImageNet initialization despite both being laparoscopic video datasets for abdominal procedures. We hypothesize that SSL can help integrate critical representational attributes in lower data settings that are otherwise easily learned when more data is available. Both pairs of matched and unmatched experiments appear largely consistent over all labeled data settings. These results underscore the need to carefully consider available labeling and data resources when applying SSL methodologies to leverage related datasets for surgical computer vision.

3.2
Stage 2: Cross-Procedure Experiments

In Fig.
2
, in line with the results of Stage 1, we see that pre-training on the procedure relevant to the downstream tasks (cholecystectomy) brings boosts in performance over ImageNet initializations across the board for all labeled data settings and for both downstream tasks/datasets. Interestingly, using the 7 initializations other than those pre-trained on the same procedure as the downstream task delivers performance markedly worse, even lower than ImageNet initializations at times. In Stage 4, we further investigate whether merging subsets of data generated from procedures that are not directly relevant to the downstream task could bring additional value.

3.3
Stage 3: Cross-Center Experiments

The stage 3 experiments (Fig.
3
), present a number of interesting findings. Firstly, as in the the previous stage, we see large performance boosts over ImageNet initializations across all data settings and both downstream tasks: for phase recognition and CVS assessment respectively, we obtain improvements of up to 10.7
\%

and 6.3
\%
with Same Center SSL and up to 8.8
\%
and 7.2
\%
with Different Center SSL. Notably, when pre-training, domain shifts introduced by sourcing videos from different centers appear to be much less impactful than those introduced by varying procedure types. This effect is resoundingly confirmed through the 7 centers (2 aggregated in HeiChole) included in this stage. Unlike the cross-procedure experiments, each initialization, irrespective of which center’s data it was generated using, delivers boosts over the ImageNet initialization, at times even higher than initializations generated using data from the same center. However, across the considered downstream tasks and pre-training datasets, using SSL-based initializations generated through datasets from the same center still generally appears to be superior to those generated from other centers. These results may suggest that access to large-scale procedure-specific initializations, even if sourced from different centers, could enable rapid prototyping for various downstream tasks.

3.4
Stage 4: Approaches to Scale

The previous 3 sections present strong evidence that pre-training dataset composition can severely impact downstream performance and must be carefully considered. In this final stage, we aim to validate those findings at scale, to better understand the interplay between the different effects, and to provide indications for how to optimally scale SSL pre-training for surgical computer vision. In Table
3
, when using initializations pre-trained on large (
\sim
400 videos) cholecystectomy datasets (StrasChole400 and StrasChole200 + MultiChole2023), we see performance largely on par with the cholecystectomy-only datasets included in previous study stages despite being an order of magnitude larger. An important caveat to this finding, though consistent with previous works
[
17
]
, is that higher capacity models may be better suited, if not required, to effectively leverage this scale of data for self-supervised pre-training
[
8
]
. We also note that the two
\sim
200-video subsets, StrasCholec200 and MultiChole2023, deliver fairly comparable boosts across downstream tasks, splits, and labeled-data percentages, despite strictly including or excluding videos sourced from the center relevant to the downstream tasks. These findings echo those discussed in Stage 3 regarding the impact of domain shifts introduced by multi-centric pre-training data on downstream task performance. Finally, we aim to provide indications about the impact of curating multi-procedure datasets for SSL pre-training using two
\sim
400-video scale datasets, Laparo420 (with LC) and Laparo420 (w/o LC), containing 45/420 and 0/375 cholecystectomy procedures, respectively. Both of these datasets, despite being of comparable size (# videos) with StrasChole400 and StrasChole200 + MultiCholec2023, perform markedly worse, further reinforcing the findings of Stage 2 that learning procedure-agnostic features may require dedicated methodological design. To the best of our knowledge, each of the 3 previous works utilizing massive scale pre-training
[
1
,
8
,
25
]
only validated their pre-trained initializations on downstream tasks well-represented in the pre-training dataset. These findings further emphasize the need for validation to better understand how generalizable those initializations may be for downstream tasks conducted on other surgical procedures.

4
Conclusion

Recent developments in self-supervised learning for surgery allow us to learn generic representational features, useful for a range of downstream tasks in the domain. In this work, we build on these developments, shifting the focus from models to data, and exploring whether pre-trained networks could be an effective means to consolidate disparate datasets in the field of Surgical Data Science. We illustrate that simple model initializations can indeed help leverage these disparate datasets, helping ’jumpstart’ work on new problems in new procedures at new clinical centers with fewer annotations.
