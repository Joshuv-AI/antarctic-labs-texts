Eigenvalue estimates for the fractional Laplacian on lattice subgraphs
Jiaxuan Wang

Abstract
. We introduce the the fractional Laplacian on a subgraph of a graph with Dirichlet boundary condition. For a lattice graph, we prove the upper and lower estimates for the sum of the first k k Dirichlet eigenvalues of the fractional Laplacian, extending the classical results by Li-Yau and Kröger.



Mathematics Subject Classification 2010: 05C63, 35J91, 35P15.

1.
Introduction

The fractional Laplacian has significant applications across various research fields in mathematics. The properties and applications of this operator in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
are increasingly discussed in literature, e.g.
[
Fra16
,
CRS+15
,
Kwa15
,
Poz16
,
SV15
,
CKS10
]
. In this paper, we introduce the fractional Laplacian on a locally finite connected graph and define the fractional Laplacian operator with Dirichlet boundary condition on a finite subgraph. For an integer lattice graph, we give the well-defined fractional Laplacian, and derive the upper and lower estimates for the Dirichlet eigenvalues of the fractional Laplacian.

In a bounded domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we consider the Dirichlet eigenvalue problem as follows:

\left\{\begin{aligned} &Lu+\lambda u=0\quad\quad in\ \Omega,\\
&u|_{\partial\Omega}=0.\\
\end{aligned}\right.

Let
L
be the Laplacian, and we can order the eigenvalues as

0<\lambda_{1}(\Omega)<\lambda_{2}(\Omega)\leqslant\ldots\leqslant\lambda_{k}(\Omega)\leqslant\ldots.

Let
V(\Omega)
be the volume of the domain
\Omega
and
V_{d}
be the volume of the unit ball in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. In 1912, Weyl
[
Wey12
]
proved that as
k\rightarrow\infty
,

\lambda_{k}(\Omega)\sim C_{d}(\frac{k}{V(\Omega)})^{\frac{2}{d}},

where
C_{d}=(2\pi)^{2}V_{d}^{-\frac{2}{d}}
. For any planar domain
\Omega
that tiles
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, Pólya
[
P6́1
]
provided the lower bound estimate:

\lambda_{k}(\Omega)\geqslant C_{d}(\frac{k}{V(\Omega)})^{\frac{2}{d}}.

His proof is applicable in any dimension
d\geqslant 2
, and he conjectured that this estimate holds for all bounded domains in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. To address this conjecture, Lieb
[
Lie79
]
obtained the result:

\lambda_{k}(\Omega)\geqslant D_{d}(\frac{k}{V(\Omega)})^{\frac{2}{d}}.

Here, the constant
D_{d}<C_{d}
is proportional to
C_{d}
. Later in 1983, Li-Yau
[
LY83
]
derived the sharp estimate known as the Berezin-Li-Yau inequality:

\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}(\Omega)\geqslant\frac{d}{d+2}C_{d}(\frac{k}{V(\Omega)})^{\frac{2}{d}}.

The upper bound estimate was proved by Kröger
[
Kro94
]
for large
k
in 1994, which includes an extra term depending on the geometry of
\Omega
.

For the fractional Laplacian
(-\Delta)^{s}
,
0<s<1
, we denote by
\lambda_{s,i}(\Omega)
the
i
-th eigenvalue of
(-\Delta)^{s}
. The Berezin-Li-Yau type inequality was generalized in
[
YY13
]
as:

\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{s,i}(\Omega)\geqslant\frac{d}{d+2s}C_{d}^{s}(\frac{k}{V(\Omega)})^{\frac{2s}{d}}.

In recent years, there has been increasing attention on the analysis of graphs. The graph Laplacian has been extensively studied in the literature, e.g.
[
GMS90
,
GM00
,
HK13
,
BL21
]
. Some results also exist for the fractional graph Laplacian, such as
[
GGWY22
,
YL22
,
BBDS20
,
Sch21
]
. However, in these works, the fractional Laplacian is typically defined on the entire graph, without reference to the Dirichlet eigenvalues of this operator. In this paper, for a locally finite, simple, undirected, connected graph
G
, we first introduce the definition of the fractional Laplacian on a subgraph with Dirichlet boundary condition, and study the Dirichlet eigenvalues.

We recall the setting of graphs. A locally finite, simple, undirected and connected graph
G=(V,E)
consists of the set of vertices
V
and the set of edges
E
. We write
x\in G
instead of
x\in V
and denote by
|G|
the number of vertices in
G
.
Two vertices
x,y
are called neighbors, denoted by
x\sim y
, if there exists
e\in E
connecting
x
and
y
. The graph distance on
G
is defined by

d(x,y):=\inf\{n|x=z_{0}\sim\dots\sim z_{n}=y\},

and we define balls
B(x,r)=\{y\in G:d(x,y)\leqslant r\}
on
G
. We denote by
V(x,r)=V(B(x,r))
the number of vertices in
B(x,r)
.
A subgraph
G_{1}=(V_{1},E_{1})
of
G
means
V_{1}\subset V
and
E_{1}\subset E
. In this paper, we always consider locally finite, simple, undirected and connected graphs.

Recall that the graph Laplacian is defined as, for any
f:G\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
,

\Delta f(x)=\sum_{y\sim x}(f(y)-f(x)).

Let
(-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}
be the fractional Laplacian operator,
0<\alpha<2
. Here we use the equivalent definition (Bochner’s definition) in
[
Kwa15
]
to describe the fractional Laplacian on
G
:

(-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}u(x)=\frac{1}{|\Gamma(-\frac{\alpha}{2})|}\int^{\infty}_{0}(u(x)-e^{t\Delta}u(x))t^{-1-\frac{\alpha}{2}}dt,

where
u:G\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
, and
e^{t\Delta}
denotes the heat semigroup, see Definition
2.2
.

Given a finite subgraph
G_{1}\subsetneq G
, we introduce the fractional Laplacian operator with Dirichlet boundary condition, denoted by
L^{\alpha}_{G_{1}}
, which is defined as:

L^{\alpha}_{G_{1}}u(x)=(-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}u^{*}(x)|_{G_{1}}.

Here
u:G_{1}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
, and
u^{*}:G\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
is the zero extension of
u
. One readily sees that
L^{\alpha}_{G_{1}}
is a real symmetric operator on
G_{1}
. The Dirichlet eigenvalue problem is given by

L^{\alpha}_{G_{1}}u(x)=\lambda u(x).

There are
|G_{1}|
solutions and corresponding real eigenvalues:

0<\lambda_{1}(G_{1})<\lambda_{2}(G_{1})\leqslant\ldots\leqslant\lambda_{|G_{1}|}(G_{1}).

See Section
2.2
for more properties about this operator. It is important to note that on the graph
G
, the eigenvalues of
(-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}
are actually
\lambda^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}
, where
\lambda
is an eigenvalue of
-\Delta
on
G
. However, the Dirichlet eigenvalues of the fractional Laplacian and the graph Laplacian don’t follow this relationship. Thus, it is meaningful to study the estimates for the Dirichlet eigenvalues of the fractional Laplacian.

Consider the infinite d-dimensional integer lattice
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
as a graph. We study the fractional Laplacian on a finite subgraph
\Omega
of
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
using the Fourier transform. In 2021, Bauer and Lippner
[
BL21
]
studied the graph Laplacian eigenvalues with Dirichlet boundary condition on
\Omega
. For the average of the first k Dirichlet eigenvalues, they derived the upper estimate in the spirit of Kröger
[
Kro94
]
and the lower estimate in the spirit of Li-Yau
[
LY83
]
. In this paper, we follow the proof strategies in
[
Kro94
,
LY83
,
BL21
]
to derive the upper and lower bounds for the Dirichlet eigenvalues of the fractional Laplacian.

For the fractional Laplacian on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, we prove that it is well-defined if
u
satisfies

\sum_{y\neq x}\frac{|u(x)-u(y)|}{d(x,y)^{d+\alpha}}<+\infty

for any vertex
x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. This result is similar to the continuous case. For a finite subgraph
\Omega\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, we define a boundary term

|\partial^{\alpha}\Omega|=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\Omega^{c}\\
y\in\Omega\end{subarray}}Q_{\alpha}(x,y)<+\infty.

This could be regarded as the size of the boundary associated with
L^{\alpha}_{\Omega}
, where

Q_{\alpha}(x,y)=\frac{1}{|\Gamma(-\frac{\alpha}{2})|}\int^{\infty}_{0}t^{-1-\frac{\alpha}{2}}p(t,x,y)dt,

and
p(t,x,y)
is the heat kernel on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. Moreover, we prove the order estimates of
Q_{\alpha}(x,y)
, see Definition
2.2
and Theorem
2.4
. The following are the main results of this paper.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\Omega
be a finite subgraph of
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, and the operator
L^{\alpha}_{\Omega}
be the fractional Laplacian
(-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}
with Dirichlet boundary condition,
0<\alpha<2
. Then

(a):
for all
1\leqslant k\leqslant\min\{1,\frac{V_{d}}{2^{d}}\}|\Omega|
, we have the upper bound estimate

\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}(\Omega)\leqslant(2\pi)^{\alpha}\frac{d}{d+\alpha}(\frac{k}{V_{d}|\Omega|})^{\frac{\alpha}{d}}+\frac{|\partial^{\alpha}\Omega|}{|\Omega|}.

For
1\leqslant k\leqslant\min\{1,\frac{V_{d}}{2^{d+1}}\}|\Omega|
, there holds

\lambda_{k+1}(\Omega)\leqslant(2\pi)^{\alpha}\frac{d\cdot 2^{\frac{d+\alpha}{d}}}{d+\alpha}(\frac{k}{V_{d}|\Omega|})^{\frac{\alpha}{d}}+2\frac{|\partial^{\alpha}\Omega|}{|\Omega|}.

(b):
For all
1\leqslant k\leqslant\min\{1,(\frac{2^{1-\frac{1}{\alpha}}\sqrt{3}}{2\pi})^{d}V_{d}\}|\Omega|
, the lower bound estimate is given as

\displaystyle\lambda_{k}(\Omega)\geqslant\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}(\Omega)\geqslant(2\pi)^{\alpha}\frac{d}{d+\alpha}(\frac{k}{V_{d}|\Omega|})^{\frac{\alpha}{d}}-(2\pi)^{2\alpha}(\frac{1}{12})^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\frac{d}{d+2\alpha}(\frac{k}{V_{d}|\Omega|})^{\frac{2\alpha}{d}}.

The proof strategy is as follows. The upper bound estimate follows from the Rayleigh-Ritz formula. Let
\{\phi_{j}\}_{1\leqslant j\leqslant|\Omega|}
be the corresponding eigenfunctions,
V_{k}
be the subspace of
\mathbb{C}^{\Omega}
spanned by
\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{k}
and
P_{k}
be the orthogonal projection to
V_{k}
. First by the Rayleigh quotient, we write
\lambda_{k+1}=\inf_{0\neq w\perp V_{k}}\frac{\langle w,L^{\alpha}_{\Omega}w\rangle_{\Omega}}{\langle w,w\rangle_{\Omega}}
. For any
g:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
, set
g=P_{k}g+g-P_{k}g
and we have Lemma
3.1
. With the Fourier transform, we choose
g(x)=h_{z}(x)=e^{i\langle x,z\rangle}
and integrate in a measurable subset
B
of
[-\pi,\pi]^{d}
to prove Lemma
3.2
. Finally, we prove
(a)
by choosing proper set
B
.

For the lower bound estimate, the key is to prove an integral inequality (Lemma
3.5
) which is an adaption of Li and Yau’s method. To prove this lemma, we construct a radially symmetric function
\varphi(z):[-\pi,\pi]^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
which satisfies
0\leqslant\varphi(z)\leqslant(\Phi(z))^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}
and

\int_{[-\pi,\pi]^{d}}(\Phi(z))^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}F(z)dz\geqslant\int_{[-\pi,\pi]^{d}}\varphi(z)F(z)dz.

One easily sees that
\widetilde{F}=M1_{B_{R}}
(see Lemma
3.5
) minimizes the integral
\int_{[-\pi,\pi]^{d}}\varphi(z)F(z)dz
, which gives the lower bound of
\int_{[-\pi,\pi]^{d}}(\Phi(z))^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}F(z)dz
. By choosing
F(z)=\sum_{j=1}^{k}|\langle\phi_{j},h_{z}\rangle_{\Omega}|^{2}
,
(b)
is derived from this lemma.

Note that on
\Omega
, compared to the Laplacian, the fractional Laplacian is a global operator, whereas the Laplacian is a local operator. Recall that

\overline{\Omega}=\Omega\cup\{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}|\exists y\in\Omega:y\sim x\},\quad\partial\Omega=\overline{\Omega}\setminus\Omega.

Here we define a new boundary term
|\partial^{\alpha}\Omega|
, which depends on all vertices outside
\Omega
, different from the finite sum term
|\partial\Omega|
. In addition, the leading term of our estimates is

(2\pi)^{\alpha}\frac{d}{d+\alpha}(\frac{k}{V_{d}|\Omega|})^{\frac{\alpha}{d}},

which is the same as
[
YY13
]
. Letting
\alpha\rightarrow 2
, we know the operator tends to the Laplacian with Dirichlet boundary condition. The leading term is consistent with
[
LY83
,
Kro94
]
, and our results actually are generalizations of
[
BL21
]
.

The paper is organized as follows:
In next section, we introduce the fractional Laplacian on a locally finite connected graph
G
, including a well-definition on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, and define the Dirichlet eigenvalue problem on a finite subgraph. In Section
3
, we prove Theorem
1.1
.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
The fractional Laplacian on graphs

Let
G=(V,E)
be a locally finite, simple, undirected and connected graph. The Laplacian on
G
is defined as

\Delta u(x)=\sum_{y\sim x}(u(y)-u(x)),

where
u\in C(G):=\{f:V(G)\rightarrow\mathbb{C}\}
, and clearly
\Delta
is a local operator. We know the heat equation on
G
is given as

(1)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\frac{\partial}{\partial t}v(t,x)=\Delta v(t,x)\quad\quad in\ (0,+\infty)\times G,\\
&v(0,x)=u(x)\quad\quad on\ G.\\
\end{aligned}\right.

We denote the solution
v(t,x)
of (1) by
e^{t\Delta}u(x)
, and the heat kernel by
p(t,x,y)
. We have

e^{t\Delta}u(x)=\sum_{y\in G}p(t,x,y)u(y).

Here we recall some fundamental properties as follows.

Proposition 2.1

(
[
HLLY17
,
Woj08
]
)
.

For
t>0
, and any
x,y\in G
, we have

(a)

p(t,x,y)=p(t,y,x)
.

(b)

p(t,x,y)>0
.

(c)

\sum_{y\in G}p(t,x,y)\leqslant 1
. The graph
G
is stochastically complete if
\sum_{y\in G}p(t,x,y)=1
.

(d)

\partial_{t}p(t,x,y)=\Delta_{x}p(t,x,y)=\Delta_{y}p(t,x,y)
.

(e)

\lim_{t\rightarrow 0}p(t,x,y)=1_{\{x=y\}}
.

We always consider stochastically complete graphs. With these properties, we provide the definition of the fractional Laplacian.

Definition 2.2
.

For a graph
G=(V,E)
,
0<\alpha<2
, the fractional Laplacian is defined as:

(2)

\displaystyle(-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}u(x)=\frac{1}{|\Gamma(-\frac{\alpha}{2})|}\int^{\infty}_{0}(u(x)-e^{t\Delta}u(x))t^{-1-\frac{\alpha}{2}}dt.

If the function
u
satisfies the conditions that

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in G\\
y\neq x\end{subarray}}p(t,x,y)(u(x)-u(y))t^{-1-\frac{\alpha}{2}}

uniformly converges and

|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in G\\
y\neq x\end{subarray}}p(t,x,y)(u(x)-u(y))t^{-1-\frac{\alpha}{2}}|\leqslant F(t,x)\in L^{1}_{t},

the fractional Laplacian is well-defined and can be written as:

(-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}u(x)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in G\\
y\neq x\end{subarray}}Q_{\alpha}(x,y)(u(x)-u(y)),

where for
x\neq y
,

0<Q_{\alpha}(x,y)=\frac{1}{|\Gamma(-\frac{\alpha}{2})|}\int^{\infty}_{0}t^{-1-\frac{\alpha}{2}}p(t,x,y)dt<+\infty,

and
\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in G\\
y\neq x\end{subarray}}Q_{\alpha}(x,y)<+\infty
.

Proof.

Using Lebesgue’s dominated convergence theorem, we have

\displaystyle(-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}u(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\Gamma(-\frac{\alpha}{2})|}\int^{\infty}_{0}(u(x)-e^{t\Delta}u(x))t^{-1-\frac{\alpha}{2}}dt

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\Gamma(-\frac{\alpha}{2})|}\int^{\infty}_{0}(u(x)-\sum_{y\in G}p(t,x,y)u(y))t^{-1-\frac{\alpha}{2}}dt

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\Gamma(-\frac{\alpha}{2})|}\int^{\infty}_{0}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in G\\
y\neq x\end{subarray}}p(t,x,y)(u(x)-u(y))t^{-1-\frac{\alpha}{2}}dt

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in G\\
y\neq x\end{subarray}}Q_{\alpha}(x,y)(u(x)-u(y)),

which converges by the conditions.

By Proposition
2.1
, it is easy to see
Q_{\alpha}(x,y)>0
. Using the fact that
0<\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in G\\
y\neq x\end{subarray}}p(t,x,y)\leqslant 1
, and

\displaystyle|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in G\\
y\neq x\end{subarray}}p(t,x,y)|=|1-p(t,x,x)|=|p(0,x,x)-p(t,x,x)|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|t\cdot\partial_{t}p(\xi,x,x)|=|t\cdot\Delta p(\xi,x,x)|\leqslant ct,

we know
\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in G\\
y\neq x\end{subarray}}Q_{\alpha}(x,y)<+\infty
.
∎

Remark 2.3
.

At least, for
u\in l^{\infty}(G)
, the fractional Laplacian is well-defined.

On the lattice graph
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, we can derive the order estimates of
Q_{\alpha}(x,y)
. Furthermore, we prove a corollary which gives a suitable domain of definition of the fractional Laplacian.

Theorem 2.4
.

For any
x\neq y
on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, we have the following order estimates:

C_{1}d(x,y)^{-d-\alpha}\leqslant Q_{\alpha}(x,y)\leqslant C_{2}d(x,y)^{-d-\alpha},

where
C_{1},C_{2}
are constants only depending on
\alpha
and the dimension
d
.

To prove this theorem, we need two lemmas which provide the heat kernel estimates. The first lemma in
[
Dav93
,
BHY17
]
gives a upper bound estimate for the heat kernel
p(t,x,y)
for any
t>0
. The second lemma comes from
[
BHL+15
,
HLLY19
]
, which proves that
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
satisfies the Gaussian heat kernel property for
t\geqslant d(x,y)
, and this result is crucial for us to calculate the order.

Lemma 2.5

(
[
Dav93
,
BHY17
]
)
.

On
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, we have

p(t,x,y)\leqslant e^{-\zeta_{1}(t,d(x,y))},

where
t>0
and

\zeta_{1}(t,r)=r\arcsinh\frac{r}{t}-\sqrt{r^{2}+t^{2}}+t.

Lemma 2.6

(
[
BHL+15
,
HLLY19
]
)
.

The lattice graph
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
satisfies the Gaussian heat kernel property if
t\geqslant d(x,y)
implies

\frac{c_{1}}{V(x,\sqrt{t})}e^{-c_{2}\frac{d(x,y)^{2}}{t}}\leqslant p(t,x,y)\leqslant\frac{c_{3}}{V(x,\sqrt{t})}e^{-c_{4}\frac{d(x,y)^{2}}{t}},

where
c_{1},c_{2},c_{3},c_{4}
are constants only depending on the dimension
d
.

Proof of Theorem
2.4
.

For convenience, we write
D=d(x,y)
for calculation, and we use uniform constants
C,C^{\prime}
only depending on
\alpha
and the dimension
d
. For

Q_{\alpha}(x,y)=\frac{1}{|\Gamma(-\frac{\alpha}{2})|}\int^{\infty}_{0}t^{-1-\frac{\alpha}{2}}p(t,x,y)dt,

we divide the integral into three parts to prove the theorem.

For
0<\frac{t}{D}\leqslant\frac{1}{e}
,
using Lemma
2.5
, we get the following estimates:

\displaystyle p(t,x,y)

\displaystyle\leqslant\exp\left\{-D\arcsinh\frac{D}{t}+\sqrt{D^{2}+t^{2}}-t\right\}

\displaystyle=\exp\left\{-D\ln\left(\frac{D}{t}+\sqrt{1+\left(\frac{D}{t}\right)^{2}}\right)+\frac{D^{2}}{t+\sqrt{t^{2}+D^{2}}}\right\}

\displaystyle\leqslant\exp\left\{-D\ln\frac{2D}{t}+D\right\}=e^{D}\left(\frac{t}{2D}\right)^{D}.

Then we have

\displaystyle\int^{\frac{D}{e}}_{0}t^{-1-\frac{\alpha}{2}}p(t,x,y)dt\leqslant\left(\frac{e}{2D}\right)^{D}\int^{\frac{D}{e}}_{0}t^{D-1-\frac{\alpha}{2}}dt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{e}{2D}\right)^{D}\cdot\frac{1}{D-\frac{\alpha}{2}}\cdot\left(\frac{D}{e}\right)^{D-\frac{\alpha}{2}}\leqslant e^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{D}\frac{1}{D-\frac{\alpha}{2}}\leqslant CD^{-d-\alpha}.

For
\frac{1}{e}\leqslant\frac{t}{D}\leqslant 2
, we define a function

h(a)=\frac{\ln\left(a+\sqrt{1+a^{2}}\right)}{a}-\frac{1}{1+\sqrt{1+a^{2}}}

for
a>0
, and it is not difficult to see that
\zeta_{1}(t,D)=\frac{D^{2}}{t}h\left(\frac{D}{t}\right)
. Let
b=a+\sqrt{1+a^{2}}>1
, and
\frac{1}{b}=\sqrt{a^{2}+1}-a
. We have

\displaystyle ah(a)=\ln b-\frac{b-1}{b+1}>0.

This implies
h>0
, and

c_{5}=\inf_{[\frac{1}{2},e]}h(a)>0.

Thus we derive that
\zeta_{1}(t,D)\geqslant c_{5}\frac{D^{2}}{t}
, and

\displaystyle\int^{2D}_{\frac{D}{e}}t^{-1-\frac{\alpha}{2}}p(t,x,y)dt\leqslant\int^{2D}_{\frac{D}{e}}e^{-c_{5}\frac{D^{2}}{t}}t^{-1-\frac{\alpha}{2}}dt

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\left(2-\frac{1}{e}\right)De^{-\frac{c_{5}D}{2}}\left(\frac{D}{e}\right)^{-1-\frac{\alpha}{2}}\leqslant CD^{-d-\alpha}.

For
\frac{t}{D}\geqslant 2
, by Lemma
2.6
we know the Gaussian heat kernel property holds. Since on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, the volume of a ball
B(x,r)
has the order
r^{d}
, we derive that

\displaystyle\int_{2D}^{\infty}t^{-1-\frac{\alpha}{2}}p(t,x,y)dt\leqslant c_{6}\int_{2D}^{\infty}e^{-c_{4}\frac{D^{2}}{t}}t^{-1-\frac{\alpha}{2}-\frac{d}{2}}dt

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle c_{6}D^{-d-\alpha}\int_{0}^{\frac{D}{2}}e^{-c_{4}z}z^{\frac{\alpha}{2}+\frac{d}{2}-1}dz\leqslant CD^{-d-\alpha},

where
c_{6}
is a constant depending on
c_{3}
and the dimension
d
. Similarly, we also have that

\int_{2D}^{\infty}t^{-1-\frac{\alpha}{2}}p(t,x,y)dt\geqslant C^{\prime}D^{-d-\alpha}.

Combining the above calculations, it is not difficult to deduce that the theorem holds.
∎

Corollary 2.7
.

The fractional Laplacian on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
is well-defined if
u
satisfies

u\in H_{\alpha}(\mathbb{Z}^{d}):=\{u\in C(\mathbb{Z}^{d}):\ \forall x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d},\ \sum_{y\neq x}\frac{|u(x)-u(y)|}{d(x,y)^{d+\alpha}}<+\infty\}.

Proof.

For convenience, we write
D=d(x,y)
. From
u\in H_{\alpha}
, we can assume the condition

|u(x)-u(y)|\leqslant C_{3}D^{d+\alpha}.

Let

F_{k}(t,x)=\sum_{D\leqslant k}p(t,x,y)|u(x)-u(y)|t^{-1-\frac{\alpha}{2}}.

We know
0\leqslant F_{k}\leqslant F_{k+1}
and

F(t,x)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\\
y\neq x\end{subarray}}p(t,x,y)|u(x)-u(y)|t^{-1-\frac{\alpha}{2}}=\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}F_{k}(t,x).

For any fixed
t>0
, by the proof of Theorem
2.4
we note that

\displaystyle F(t,x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{D\leqslant et}p(t,x,y)|u(x)-u(y)|t^{-1-\frac{\alpha}{2}}+\sum_{D>et}p(t,x,y)|u(x)-u(y)|t^{-1-\frac{\alpha}{2}}

\displaystyle\leqslant C(t,u)+C_{3}\sum_{D>et}e^{D}\left(\frac{t}{2D}\right)^{D}D^{d+\alpha}t^{-1-\frac{\alpha}{2}}

\displaystyle\leqslant C(t,u)+C_{3}\sum_{D>et}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{D}D^{d+\alpha}t^{-1-\frac{\alpha}{2}}<+\infty.

Thus by the monotone convergence theorem, we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}F(t,x)dt=\lim_{k\rightarrow+\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}F_{k}(t,x)dt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{k\rightarrow+\infty}\sum_{D\leqslant k}Q_{\alpha}(x,y)|u(x)-u(y)|

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle C_{2}\sum_{y\neq x}\frac{|u(x)-u(y)|}{d(x,y)^{d+\alpha}}<+\infty,

which implies that
F\in L^{1}_{t}
. By Lebesgue’s dominated convergence theorem, we prove that the fractional Laplacian is well-defined.
∎

2.2.
The Dirichlet eigenvalue problem and some properties

Let
G_{1}\subsetneq G
be a finite subgraph. By Definition
2.2
, we know that
(-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}
is well-defined on
G
. Suppose
u\in C(G_{1})
, and we define the Dirichlet eigenvalue problem on
G_{1}
as follows:

(3)

\displaystyle L^{\alpha}_{G_{1}}u(x)=(-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}u^{*}(x)|_{G_{1}}=\lambda u(x).

Here
L^{\alpha}_{G_{1}}
denotes the fractional Laplacian with Dirichlet boundary condition on
G_{1}
, and
u^{*}(x)\in C(G)
obtained by extending functions to be 0 on
G_{1}^{c}
.

L^{\alpha}_{G_{1}}
becomes a finite dimensional operator, and thus has
|G_{1}|
eigenvalues. Note that for any
u\in C(G_{1})
, we have
u^{*}\in l^{\infty}(G)\subset H_{\alpha}(G)
. Furthermore, the quadratic form
\langle u,L^{\alpha}_{G_{1}}u\rangle_{G_{1}}=\langle u^{*},(-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}u^{*}(x)\rangle_{G}
, with the natural Hermitian inner product is well-defined. We give some properties as follows.

Proposition 2.8
.

\forall u,v\in C(G_{1})
,
0<\alpha<2
, we have that

(a)

\langle L^{\alpha}_{G_{1}}u,v\rangle_{G_{1}}=\langle u,L^{\alpha}_{G_{1}}v\rangle_{G_{1}}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y\in G\\
x\neq y\end{subarray}}Q_{\alpha}(x,y)(u^{*}(x)-u^{*}(y))\overline{(v^{*}(x)-v^{*}(y))}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y\in G\\
x\neq y\end{subarray}}Q_{\alpha}(x,y)(u(x)-u(y))\overline{(v(x)-v(y))}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in G_{1}\\
y\in G_{1}^{c}\end{subarray}}Q_{\alpha}(x,y)u(x)\overline{v(x)}
.

(b)

The eigenvalues are positive real numbers, labeled as

0<\lambda_{1}(G_{1})<\lambda_{2}(G_{1})\leqslant\ldots\leqslant\lambda_{|G_{1}|}(G_{1}).

(c)

\phi_{1}>0
, where
\{\phi_{i}\}_{1\leqslant i\leqslant|\Omega|}
denote the corresponding real eigenfunctions.

(d)

For any
f\in C(G_{1})
, the Poisson’s equation
L^{\alpha}_{G_{1}}u(x)=f
has a unique solution in
C(G_{1})
.

Proof.

(a)
: From (3), Proposition
2.1
and Definition
2.2
, we have
Q_{\alpha}(x,y)=Q_{\alpha}(y,x)
, and

\displaystyle\langle L^{\alpha}_{G_{1}}u,v\rangle_{G_{1}}=\langle(-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}u^{*}(x),v^{*}(x)\rangle_{G}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y\in G\\
x\neq y\end{subarray}}Q_{\alpha}(x,y)(u^{*}(x)-u^{*}(y))\overline{v^{*}(x)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y\in G\\
x\neq y\end{subarray}}Q_{\alpha}(x,y)(u^{*}(x)-u^{*}(y))\overline{(v^{*}(x)-v^{*}(y))}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y\in G\\
x\neq y\end{subarray}}Q_{\alpha}(x,y)(u(x)-u(y))\overline{(v(x)-v(y))}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in G_{1}\\
y\in G_{1}^{c}\end{subarray}}Q_{\alpha}(x,y)u(x)\overline{v(x)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle u,L^{\alpha}_{G_{1}}v\rangle_{G_{1}}.

(b)
: The property (a) implies that the operator
L^{\alpha}_{G_{1}}
is self-adjoint and positive definite, which derives that the eigenvalues are positive and real numbers. To prove
\lambda_{1}
is single, suppose that there exist real eigenfunctions
\phi_{1}
and
\varphi_{1}
corresponding to
\lambda_{1}
, which satisfy
\phi_{1}\neq c\varphi_{1}
. Select a constant
k
to make
\phi_{1}-k\varphi_{1}
a sign changing eigenfunction, which contradicts the property (c).

(c)
: Suppose
\langle\phi_{1},\phi_{1}\rangle_{G_{1}}=1
. If
\phi_{1}
changes sign, we choose
\phi_{1}(x_{1})<0
and
\phi_{1}(x_{2})>0
. Define
\widetilde{\phi_{1}}=|\phi_{1}|
, and there holds

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}=\langle\phi_{1},L^{\alpha}_{G_{1}}\phi_{1}\rangle_{G_{1}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y\in G_{1}\\
x\neq y\end{subarray}}Q_{\alpha}(x,y)(\phi_{1}(x)-\phi_{1}(y))^{2}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in G_{1}\\
y\in G_{1}^{c}\end{subarray}}Q_{\alpha}(x,y)\phi_{1}^{2}(x)

\displaystyle>

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y\in G_{1}\\
x\neq y\end{subarray}}Q_{\alpha}(x,y)(\widetilde{\phi_{1}}(x)-\widetilde{\phi_{1}}(y))^{2}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in G_{1}\\
y\in G_{1}^{c}\end{subarray}}Q_{\alpha}(x,y)\widetilde{\phi_{1}}^{2}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\widetilde{\phi_{1}},L^{\alpha}_{G_{1}}\widetilde{\phi_{1}}\rangle_{G_{1}},

causing a contradiction.

The above argument shows that
\phi_{1}\geqslant 0
. Assume
G_{1}\supset M=\{x\in G_{1}:\phi_{1}(x)=0\}\neq\emptyset
. Let

a=\min_{x\in G_{1}}\{\phi_{1}(x)\neq 0\}>0,\ a_{1}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in M\\
y\in G_{1}^{c}\end{subarray}}Q_{\alpha}(x,y)>0,\ a_{2}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in M\\
y\in G_{1}\backslash M\end{subarray}}Q_{\alpha}(x,y)>0,

and choose
0<\epsilon<\frac{2aa_{2}}{a_{1}+a_{2}}<2a
. Then we consider a new function

u(x)=\left\{\begin{aligned} &\phi_{1}(x)\quad,x\notin M,\\
&\epsilon\quad\quad\quad,x\in M.\\
\end{aligned}\right.

It is clear that
\langle u,u\rangle_{G_{1}}=1+|M|\epsilon^{2}
, and
\frac{2a-\epsilon}{\epsilon}>\frac{a_{1}}{a_{2}}
. By calculation, we have

\displaystyle\langle u,L^{\alpha}_{G_{1}}u\rangle_{G_{1}}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y\in G_{1}\\
x\neq y\end{subarray}}Q_{\alpha}(x,y)(u(x)-u(y))^{2}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in G_{1}\\
y\in G_{1}^{c}\end{subarray}}Q_{\alpha}(x,y)u^{2}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\phi_{1},L^{\alpha}_{G_{1}}\phi_{1}\rangle_{G_{1}}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in M\\
y\in G_{1}^{c}\end{subarray}}Q_{\alpha}(x,y)\epsilon^{2}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in M\\
y\in G_{1}\setminus M\end{subarray}}Q_{\alpha}(x,y)[(\epsilon-u(y))^{2}-(0-u(y))^{2}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}+a_{1}\epsilon^{2}-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in M\\
y\in G_{1}\setminus M\end{subarray}}Q_{\alpha}(x,y)\epsilon(2u(y)-\epsilon)

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}+a_{1}\epsilon^{2}-a_{2}\epsilon(2a-\epsilon)=\lambda_{1}+(a_{1}-\frac{2a-\epsilon}{\epsilon}a_{2})\epsilon^{2}<\lambda_{1},

which is a contradiction.

(d)
: The unique solution is given by
(L^{\alpha}_{G_{1}})^{-1}f
.
∎

2.3.
The Fourier transform on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}

Let
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
be the infinite d-dimensional integer lattice, and we choose
S=\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{d},-e_{1},\ldots,-e_{d}\}
as the generating set. Here
e_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
is the vector whose i-th component is
1
and the rest are
0
. Thus we can consider
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
as the Cayley graph generated by
S
. From Definition
2.2
and (3), we are clear about the definitions of the fractional Laplacian and the Dirichlet eigenvalue problem on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. In this subsection, we introduce the Fourier transform on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, and derive some critical equalities and inequalities on a finite subgraph
\Omega
of
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
.

For
z\in[-\pi,\pi]^{d}
, let us define
h_{z}(x):\mathbb{Z}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
by
h_{z}(x)=e^{i\langle x,z\rangle}
, where
\langle x,z\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{d}x_{i}z_{i}
. Thus, the Fourier transform on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
is defined by

\displaystyle\mathscr{F}:\quad\mathbb{C}^{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\ \mathbb{C}^{[-\pi,\pi]^{d}}

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle\mapsto\ \ \widehat{u},

where
u\in l^{1}(\mathbb{Z}^{d})
and

\widehat{u}(z)=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}e^{-i\langle x,z\rangle}u(x)=\langle u,h_{z}\rangle_{\mathbb{Z}^{d}},

u(x)=(2\pi)^{d}\int_{[-\pi,\pi]^{d}}e^{i\langle x,\xi\rangle}\widehat{u}(\xi)d\xi.

Let
\Omega
be a finite subgraph of
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. By (3), if
u\in C(\Omega)
, it is easy to see
u^{*}\in l^{1}(\mathbb{Z}^{d})
and
\widehat{L^{\alpha}_{\Omega}u}
is well-defined.

To prove Theorem
1.1
, we introduce the function
\Phi(z)=\sum_{i=1}^{d}(2-2\cos z_{i})
for
z\in[-\pi,\pi]^{d}
, and prove some relevant lemmas as follows.

Lemma 2.9
.

For any
u\in l^{1}(\mathbb{Z}^{d})
,
0<\alpha<2
,
\xi\in[-\pi,\pi]^{d}
, we have

\widehat{(-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}u}(\xi)=(\Phi(\xi))^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\widehat{u}(\xi).

Proof.

By Definition
2.2
, we apply the Fourier transform operator to both sides of equation (2) and get

\widehat{(-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}u}(\xi)=\frac{1}{|\Gamma(-\frac{\alpha}{2})|}\int^{\infty}_{0}(\widehat{u}(\xi)-\widehat{e^{t\Delta}u}(\xi))t^{-1-\frac{\alpha}{2}}dt.

For the heat equation (1), we also apply the the Fourier transform operator to both sides, and the equations are transformed into

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\frac{d}{dt}\widehat{v}(t,\xi)=-\sum_{j=1}^{d}(2-e^{i\langle e_{j},\xi\rangle}-e^{-i\langle e_{j},\xi\rangle})\widehat{v}(t,\xi)\\
&\quad\quad\quad\ \ =-\Phi(\xi)\widehat{v}(t,\xi)\quad\quad in\ (0,+\infty)\times[-\pi,\pi]^{d},\\
&\widehat{v}(0,\xi)=\widehat{u}(\xi)\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\ in\ [-\pi,\pi]^{d}.\\
\end{aligned}\right.

The solution is given as
\widehat{e^{t\Delta}u}(\xi)=\widehat{v}(t,\xi)=e^{-\Phi(\xi)t}\widehat{u}(\xi)
. Hence, by the identity

\lambda^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}=\frac{1}{|\Gamma(-\frac{\alpha}{2})|}\int^{\infty}_{0}(1-e^{-\lambda t})t^{-1-\frac{\alpha}{2}}dt,

we derive that

\widehat{(-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}u}(\xi)=(\Phi(\xi))^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\widehat{u}(\xi).

∎

With Lemma
2.9
and the Plancherel formula, we observe that for any
u,v\in C(\Omega)
,

(4)

\displaystyle\langle v,L^{\alpha}_{\Omega}u\rangle_{\Omega}=\langle v^{*},(-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}u^{*}\rangle_{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\langle\widehat{v^{*}},\widehat{(-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}u^{*}}\rangle_{[-\pi,\pi]^{d}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{[-\pi,\pi]^{d}}(\Phi(\xi))^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\widehat{v^{*}}(\xi)\widehat{\overline{u^{*}}}(\xi)d\xi,

and this integral is bounded.

Lemma 2.10
.

Let
\Omega
be a finite subgraph of
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. For any
u\in C(\Omega)
,
0<\alpha<2
, the following holds:

(a)

\langle u,u\rangle_{\Omega}=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{[-\pi,\pi]^{d}}|\langle u,h_{z}\rangle_{\Omega}|^{2}dz.

(b)

\langle u,L^{\alpha}_{\Omega}u\rangle_{\Omega}=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{[-\pi,\pi]^{d}}(\Phi(z))^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}|\langle u,h_{z}\rangle_{\Omega}|^{2}dz.

Proof.

(a)
: The result comes from the Plancherel formula.

(b)
: From equation (4), let us replace v with u, then we have

\displaystyle\langle u,L^{\alpha}_{\Omega}u\rangle_{\Omega}=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{[-\pi,\pi]^{d}}(\Phi(z))^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}|\widehat{u^{*}}(z)|^{2}dz.

Since
\widehat{u^{*}}(z)=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}e^{-i\langle x,z\rangle}u^{*}(x)=\sum_{x\in\Omega}u(x)\overline{h_{z}}(x)=\langle u,h_{z}\rangle_{\Omega}
, the proof is completed.

∎

Lemma 2.11
.

Let
\Omega
be a finite subgraph of
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. For
0<\alpha<2
, the following holds:

(a)

\langle h_{z},h_{z}\rangle_{\Omega}=|\Omega|.

(b)

|\langle h_{z},L^{\alpha}_{\Omega}h_{z}\rangle_{\Omega}|\leqslant(\Phi(z))^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}|\Omega|+|\partial^{\alpha}\Omega|,

where

|\partial^{\alpha}\Omega|=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\Omega^{c}\\
y\in\Omega\end{subarray}}Q_{\alpha}(x,y)<+\infty.

Proof.

(a)
:

\langle h_{z},h_{z}\rangle_{\Omega}=\sum_{x\in\Omega}h_{z}(x)\overline{h_{z}}(x)=\sum_{x\in\Omega}1=|\Omega|.

(b)
: Since

\displaystyle|\langle h_{z},L^{\alpha}_{\Omega}h_{z}\rangle_{\Omega}|=|\langle h_{z}^{*},(-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}h_{z}^{*}\rangle_{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}|=|\sum_{x\in\Omega}e^{-i\langle x,z\rangle}\cdot(-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}h_{z}^{*}(x)|

and

\displaystyle|\langle h_{z},(-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}h_{z}^{*}\rangle_{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}|=|\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}e^{-i\langle x,z\rangle}\cdot(-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}h_{z}^{*}(x)|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|(2\pi)^{\frac{d}{2}}\widehat{(-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}h_{z}^{*}}(z)|=|(2\pi)^{\frac{d}{2}}(\Phi(z))^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\widehat{h_{z}^{*}}(z)|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|(\Phi(z))^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\sum_{x\in\Omega}h_{z}(x)\cdot e^{-i\langle x,z\rangle}|=(\Phi(z))^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}|\Omega|,

we can estimate the left-hand side of this inequality,

\displaystyle|\langle h_{z},L^{\alpha}_{\Omega}h_{z}\rangle_{\Omega}|\leqslant

\displaystyle(\Phi(z))^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}|\Omega|+\sum_{x\in\Omega^{c}}|e^{-i\langle x,z\rangle}\cdot(-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}h_{z}^{*}(x)|

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle(\Phi(z))^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}|\Omega|+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\Omega^{c}\\
y\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\end{subarray}}|Q_{\alpha}(x,y)h_{z}^{*}(y)|

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle(\Phi(z))^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}|\Omega|+|\partial^{\alpha}\Omega|.

∎

Remark 2.12
.

As
\alpha\rightarrow 0
on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, it is known that
(-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}
tends to the identity operator. Indeed, we have

(-\Delta)^{s}u(x)=u(x)+sL_{\Delta}u(x)+o(s)\quad as\ s\rightarrow 0^{+},

where
L_{\Delta}
is the logarithmic Laplacian. By the Fourier transform we define

\langle v,L_{\Delta}u\rangle=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{[-\pi,\pi]^{d}}\ln\Phi(\xi)\widehat{v}(\xi)\widehat{\overline{u}}(\xi)d\xi,

which is well-defined for
u,v\in l^{1}(\mathbb{Z}^{d})
. There have been a lot of researches about the logarithmic Laplacian in the continuous case, and the above properties of the fractional Laplacian may provide insights into the logarithmic Laplacian in the discrete case..

3.
Proof of Theorem
1.1

Let
\Omega
be a finite subgraph of
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. The eigenvalues of the operator
L^{\alpha}_{\Omega}
are denoted by

0<\lambda_{1}<\ldots\leqslant\lambda_{|\Omega|},

and
\{\phi_{j}\}_{1\leqslant j\leqslant|\Omega|}
are the corresponding standardized eigenfunctions. In this section, we follow the methods in
[
LY83
,
BL21
,
Kro94
]
to derive the upper and lower bounds of the Dirichlet eigenvalues of the fractional Laplacian, and prove Theorem
1.1
.

3.1.
Upper bound

We begin with a general lemma (proved in
[
BL21
]
) on eigenvalues.

Lemma 3.1

(Lemma 3.1 in
[
BL21
]
)
.

Let L be a self-adjoint, positive semidefinite operator on a finite dimensional, Hermitian, complex vector space W with Hermitian inner product
\langle,\rangle
. Let
0\leqslant\gamma_{1}\leqslant\ldots\leqslant\gamma_{s}
denote its eigenvalues, and let us choose an orthonormal basis of eigenfunctions
f_{i}:i=1,\ldots,s
, where
f_{i}
corresponds to
\gamma_{i}
. Then for any
1\leqslant k\leqslant s
and any vector
g\in W
one has

(5)

\displaystyle\gamma_{k+1}\langle g,g\rangle\leqslant\langle g,Lg\rangle+\sum_{j=1}^{k}(\gamma_{k+1}-\gamma_{j})|\langle g,f_{j}\rangle|^{2}.

In this lemma, we choose
g=h_{z},f_{j}=\phi_{j}
, and we can derive an inequality between
\lambda_{k+1}
and
\sum_{j=1}^{k}\lambda_{j}
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\Omega
be a finite subgraph of
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. For any
1\leqslant k\leqslant|\Omega|
,
0<\alpha<2
, and measurable set
B\subset[-\pi,\pi]^{d}
, we have

\displaystyle\lambda_{k+1}(|\Omega||B|-(2\pi)^{d}k)\leqslant|\Omega|\cdot\int_{z\in B}(\Phi(z))^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}dz-(2\pi)^{d}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\lambda_{j}+|B|\cdot|\partial^{\alpha}\Omega|,

where
\Phi(z)=\sum_{i=1}^{d}(2-2\cos z_{i})
, and
\lambda_{k+1}=0
if
k=|\Omega|
.

Proof.

In Lemma
3.1
, we choose

W=\mathbb{C}^{\Omega},\quad L=L^{\alpha}_{\Omega},\quad g=h_{z},\quad\gamma_{j}=\lambda_{j},\quad f_{j}=\phi_{j}.

From Proposition
2.8
, we know the above choice satisfies the conditions of Lemma
3.1
. Integrating both sides of the inequality (5) yields

\displaystyle\lambda_{k+1}\int_{z\in B}\langle h_{z},h_{z}\rangle_{\Omega}\leqslant\int_{z\in B}\langle h_{z},L^{\alpha}_{\Omega}h_{z}\rangle_{\Omega}+\sum_{j=1}^{k}(\lambda_{k+1}-\lambda_{j})\int_{z\in B}|\langle h_{z},\phi_{j}\rangle_{\Omega}|^{2}.

By Lemma
2.10
and Lemma
2.11
, the left-hand side is equal to
\lambda_{k+1}|B||\Omega|
, while

\displaystyle\text{the right-side hand}

\displaystyle\leqslant\int_{z\in B}((\Phi(z))^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}|\Omega|+|\partial^{\alpha}\Omega|)dz+(2\pi)^{d}\sum_{j=1}^{k}(\lambda_{k+1}-\lambda_{j})\langle\phi_{j},\phi_{j}\rangle_{\Omega}

\displaystyle=|\Omega|\cdot\int_{z\in B}(\Phi(z))^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}dz+|B|\cdot|\partial^{\alpha}\Omega|+(2\pi)^{d}\sum_{j=1}^{k}(\lambda_{k+1}-\lambda_{j}),

which completes the proof of this lemma.
∎

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
\Omega
be a finite subgraph of
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. For
1\leqslant k\leqslant\min\{1,\frac{V_{d}}{2^{d}}\}|\Omega|
and
0<\alpha<2
, we obtain the upper bound estimate:

\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\lambda_{j}\leqslant(2\pi)^{\alpha}\frac{d}{d+\alpha}(\frac{k}{V_{d}|\Omega|})^{\frac{\alpha}{d}}+\frac{|\partial^{\alpha}\Omega|}{|\Omega|}.

For
1\leqslant k\leqslant\min\{1,\frac{V_{d}}{2^{d+1}}\}|\Omega|
, there holds

\lambda_{k+1}\leqslant(2\pi)^{\alpha}\frac{d\cdot 2^{\frac{d+\alpha}{d}}}{d+\alpha}(\frac{k}{V_{d}|\Omega|})^{\frac{\alpha}{d}}+2\frac{|\partial^{\alpha}\Omega|}{|\Omega|},

where
\lambda_{k+1}=0
if
k=|\Omega|
.

Proof.

In Lemma
3.2
, for
1\leqslant k\leqslant\min\{1,\frac{V_{d}}{2^{d}}\}|\Omega|
, we choose the measurable set B as a ball of radius
2\pi(\frac{k}{V_{d}|\Omega|})^{\frac{1}{d}}
centered at the origin in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, so that the radius
R=2\pi(\frac{k}{V_{d}|\Omega|})^{\frac{1}{d}}\leqslant\pi
and
|B|=\frac{k(2\pi)^{d}}{|\Omega|}
. Thus we derive an inequality:

(6)

\displaystyle(2\pi)^{d}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\lambda_{j}\leqslant|\Omega|\cdot\int_{z\in B}(\Phi(z))^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}dz+|B|\cdot|\partial^{\alpha}\Omega|.

In the rest of the proof, we just need to estimate
\int_{z\in B}(\Phi(z))^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}dz
.

We know by calculation,

\displaystyle(\Phi(z))^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}=(\sum_{i=1}^{d}(2-2\cos z_{i}))^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}=(\sum_{i=1}^{d}4\sin^{2}\frac{z_{i}}{2})^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\leqslant(\sum_{i=1}^{d}z^{2}_{i})^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}=|z|^{\alpha}.

This yields that

\displaystyle\int_{z\in B}(\Phi(z))^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}dz\leqslant\int_{z\in B}|z|^{\alpha}=dV_{d}\int^{R}_{0}r^{\alpha}\cdot r^{d-1}dr=\frac{dV_{d}}{d+\alpha}R^{d+\alpha}.

Applying the above inequality into (6), we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\lambda_{j}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{dV_{d}}{d+\alpha}\frac{|\Omega|R^{d+\alpha}}{k(2\pi)^{d}}+\frac{|\partial^{\alpha}\Omega|}{|\Omega|}

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{\alpha}\frac{d}{d+\alpha}(\frac{k}{V_{d}|\Omega|})^{\frac{\alpha}{d}}+\frac{|\partial^{\alpha}\Omega|}{|\Omega|}.

Similarly, if
1\leqslant k\leqslant\min\{1,\frac{V_{d}}{2^{d+1}}\}|\Omega|
, we choose
R^{\prime}=2^{\frac{1}{d}}R
, which implies
R^{\prime}=2\pi(\frac{2k}{V_{d}|\Omega|})^{\frac{1}{d}}\leqslant\pi
and
|B|=\frac{2k(2\pi)^{d}}{|\Omega|}
. Then we repeat the above proof and ignore the effect of
\sum_{j=1}^{k}\lambda_{j}
, obtaining that

\displaystyle k(2\pi)^{d}\lambda_{k+1}

\displaystyle\leqslant|\Omega|\cdot\int_{z\in B}(\Phi(z))^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}dz+|B|\cdot|\partial^{\alpha}\Omega|

\displaystyle\leqslant|\Omega|\cdot\frac{dV_{d}}{d+\alpha}R^{d+\alpha}+\frac{2k(2\pi)^{d}|\partial^{\alpha}\Omega|}{|\Omega|},

which implies the result

\lambda_{k+1}\leqslant(2\pi)^{\alpha}\frac{d\cdot 2^{\frac{d+\alpha}{d}}}{d+\alpha}(\frac{k}{V_{d}|\Omega|})^{\frac{\alpha}{d}}+2\frac{|\partial^{\alpha}\Omega|}{|\Omega|}.

∎

Remark 3.4
.

Note that as
\alpha\rightarrow 2
, the fractional Laplacian tends to the Laplacian, and the upper bound estimate in Theorem
3.3
also holds, which is the result in
[
BL21
]
.

3.2.
Lower bound

To prove the lower bound estimate (
(b)
of Theorem
1.1
), we follow the method by Li and Yau
[
LY83
]
. In the following part we prove a modification of
Lemma 1 from
[
LY83
]
, and with Lemma
2.10
we complete the proof.

Lemma 3.5

(Modification of
Lemma 1 from
[
LY83
]
)
.

Let
F
be a real-valued function in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, satisfying
0\leqslant F\leqslant M
and

\int_{[-\pi,\pi]^{d}}F(z)dz\geqslant K.

Assume
0<\alpha<2
, and

(\frac{K}{MV_{d}})^{\frac{1}{d}}\leqslant a=2^{1-\frac{1}{\alpha}}\sqrt{3}<\pi.

Then we have the integral inequality

\int_{[-\pi,\pi]^{d}}(\Phi(z))^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}F(z)dz\geqslant\frac{dK}{d+\alpha}(\frac{K}{MV_{d}})^{\frac{\alpha}{d}}-(\frac{1}{12})^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\cdot\frac{dK}{d+2\alpha}(\frac{K}{MV_{d}})^{\frac{2\alpha}{d}},

where
\Phi(z)=\sum_{i=1}^{d}(2-2\cos z_{i})
.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we assume that
\int_{[-\pi,\pi]^{d}}F(z)dz=K
. Let us prove the lemma in two steps.

Step 1: At first we are going to construct a radially symmetric function
\varphi(z):[-\pi,\pi]^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that

0\leqslant\varphi(z)\leqslant(\Phi(z))^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}
. By the Taylor expansion, we observe that if
|z|\leqslant a=2^{1-\frac{1}{\alpha}}\sqrt{3}<\pi
, there holds

\displaystyle(\Phi(z))^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}=(\sum_{i=1}^{d}(2-2\cos z_{i}))^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}

\displaystyle\geqslant

\displaystyle(\sum_{i=1}^{d}(z_{i}^{2}-\frac{1}{12}z_{i}^{4}))^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\geqslant((|z|^{2}-\frac{1}{12}|z|^{4}))^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|z|^{\alpha}(1-\frac{1}{12}|z|^{2})^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\geqslant|z|^{\alpha}(1-(\frac{1}{12})^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}|z|^{\alpha}),

where the last step follows from
(1-x)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\geqslant 1-x^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}
for any
0\leqslant x\leqslant 1
. For
|z|\leqslant a
, one easily sees that

|z|^{\alpha}(1-(\frac{1}{12})^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}|z|^{\alpha})
is increasing with respect to
|z|
. Thus we define

\varphi(z)=\left\{\begin{aligned} &|z|^{\alpha}-(\frac{1}{12})^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}|z|^{2\alpha}\quad&,\ |z|\leqslant a,\\
&\frac{1}{4}(12)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}&,\ |z|>a.\\
\end{aligned}\right.

It is clear that
\varphi(z)
is increasing radially and
0\leqslant\varphi(z)\leqslant(\Phi(z))^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}
when
|z|\leqslant a
. If
|z|>a
and
z\in[-\pi,\pi]^{d}
, we reduce some
|z_{i}|
and choose
|z^{\prime}|=a<|z|
which satisfies
|z^{\prime}_{i}|\leqslant|z_{i}|
. Hence, from the expression of
(\Phi(z^{\prime}))^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}
, we know that

(\Phi(z))^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\geqslant(\Phi(z^{\prime}))^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\geqslant\varphi(z^{\prime})=\frac{1}{4}(12)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}=\varphi(z).

Step 2: With the function
\varphi(z)
, we can estimate the lower bound of
\int_{[-\pi,\pi]^{d}}(\Phi(z))^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}F(z)dz
. Since
F\geqslant 0
, we have

(7)

\displaystyle\int_{[-\pi,\pi]^{d}}(\Phi(z))^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}F(z)dz\geqslant\int_{[-\pi,\pi]^{d}}\varphi(z)F(z)dz.

Choose the constant
R=(\frac{K}{MV_{d}})^{\frac{1}{d}}\leqslant a=2^{1-\frac{1}{\alpha}}\sqrt{3}<\pi
to satisfy that
MR^{d}V_{d}=K
, and we define

\widetilde{F}(z)=\left\{\begin{aligned} &M\ &,\ |z|\leqslant R,\\
&\ 0&,\ |z|>R.\\
\end{aligned}\right.

Thus,

\int_{[-\pi,\pi]^{d}}\widetilde{F}(z)dz=\int_{[-\pi,\pi]^{d}}F(z)dz=K.

For any function
F
which satisfies the conditions
0\leqslant F\leqslant M
and
\int_{[-\pi,\pi]^{d}}F(z)dz=K
, we claim that
\widetilde{F}
minimizes the integral

\int_{[-\pi,\pi]^{d}}\varphi(z)F(z)dz
since
\varphi(z)
is monotonic and radially symmetric. The strict proof is as follows:

\displaystyle\int_{[-\pi,\pi]^{d}}\varphi(z)(F(z)-\widetilde{F}(z))dz

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{[-\pi,\pi]^{d}\setminus B_{R}}\varphi(z)F(z)dz-\int_{B_{R}}\varphi(z)(M-F(z))dz

\displaystyle\geqslant

\displaystyle\varphi(R)\int_{[-\pi,\pi]^{d}\setminus B_{R}}F(z)dz-\varphi(R)\int_{B_{R}}(M-F(z))dz

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\varphi(R)(\int_{[-\pi,\pi]^{d}}F(z)dz-\int_{B_{R}}Mdz)=0.

Thus we have the estimate

\displaystyle\int_{[-\pi,\pi]^{d}}(\Phi(z))^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}F(z)dz\geqslant\int_{[-\pi,\pi]^{d}}\varphi(z)F(z)dz\geqslant M\int_{B_{R}}\varphi(z)dz

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle M\int_{B_{R}}(|z|^{\alpha}-(\frac{1}{12})^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}|z|^{2\alpha})dz=MdV_{d}\int_{0}^{R}(r^{\alpha}-(\frac{1}{12})^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}r^{2\alpha})r^{d-1}dr

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle MdV_{d}(\frac{R^{d+\alpha}}{d+\alpha}-(\frac{1}{12})^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\frac{R^{d+2\alpha}}{d+2\alpha})=\frac{dK}{d+\alpha}(\frac{K}{MV_{d}})^{\frac{\alpha}{d}}-(\frac{1}{12})^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\cdot\frac{dK}{d+2\alpha}(\frac{K}{MV_{d}})^{\frac{2\alpha}{d}}.

∎

Theorem 3.6
.

Let
\Omega
be a finite subgraph of
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. For
1\leqslant k\leqslant\min\{1,(\frac{2^{1-\frac{1}{\alpha}}\sqrt{3}}{2\pi})^{d}V_{d}\}|\Omega|
and
0<\alpha<2
, we obtain the lower bound estimate

\lambda_{k}(\Omega)\geqslant\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\lambda_{j}(\Omega)\geqslant(2\pi)^{\alpha}\frac{d}{d+\alpha}(\frac{k}{V_{d}|\Omega|})^{\frac{\alpha}{d}}-(2\pi)^{2\alpha}(\frac{1}{12})^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\frac{d}{d+2\alpha}(\frac{k}{V_{d}|\Omega|})^{\frac{2\alpha}{d}}.

Proof.

The standardized eigenfunctions
\{\phi_{j}\}_{j=1}^{k}
form an orthogonal basis in
l^{2}(\Omega)
. We define
P_{j}
the projection operator on the space spanned by
\phi_{j}
, and
P
the projection operator on the space spanned by
\{\phi_{j}\}_{j=1}^{k}
. Let
\|\cdot\|
be the
l^{2}
norm on
\Omega
and
h_{z}(x)=e^{i\langle z,x\rangle}
as before. To apply Lemma
3.5
, let

F_{j}(z)=\langle P_{j}h_{z},P_{j}h_{z}\rangle_{\Omega}=\|P_{j}h_{z}\|^{2},

F(z)=\sum_{j=1}^{k}F_{j}(z)=\|Ph_{z}\|^{2}.

By Lemma
2.10
, we have

F(z)=\|Ph_{z}\|^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}|\langle\phi_{j},h_{z}\rangle_{\Omega}|^{2}\leqslant\|h_{z}\|^{2}=|\Omega|,

\int_{[-\pi,\pi]^{d}}F(z)dz=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\int_{[-\pi,\pi]^{d}}|\langle\phi_{j},h_{z}\rangle_{\Omega}|^{2}dz=(2\pi)^{d}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\langle\phi_{j},\phi_{j}\rangle_{\Omega}=k(2\pi)^{d},

\displaystyle\int_{[-\pi,\pi]^{d}}(\Phi(z))^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}F(z)dz

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\int_{[-\pi,\pi]^{d}}(\Phi(z))^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}|\langle\phi_{j},h_{z}\rangle_{\Omega}|^{2}dz

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{d}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\langle\phi_{j},L^{\alpha}_{\Omega}\phi_{j}\rangle_{\Omega}=(2\pi)^{d}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\lambda_{j}.

Using Lemma
3.5
for
M=|\Omega|,K=k(2\pi)^{d}
, we get the result

\lambda_{k}(\Omega)\geqslant\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\lambda_{j}(\Omega)\geqslant(2\pi)^{\alpha}\frac{d}{d+\alpha}(\frac{k}{V_{d}|\Omega|})^{\frac{\alpha}{d}}-(2\pi)^{2\alpha}(\frac{1}{12})^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\frac{d}{d+2\alpha}(\frac{k}{V_{d}|\Omega|})^{\frac{2\alpha}{d}}.

∎

Remark 3.7
.

As
\alpha\rightarrow 2
, the fractional Laplacian tends to the Laplacian, and the above lower bound estimate also holds, which is the result proved in
[
BL21
]
.
