Conformal quantum mechanics of causal diamonds: Time evolution, thermality, and instability via path integral functionals
H. E. Camblong, A. Chakraborty, P. Lopez-Duque, C. R. Ordóñez

Abstract
An observer with a finite lifetime 𝒯 \mathcal{T} perceives the Minkowski vacuum as a thermal state at temperature T D = 2 ​ ℏ / ( π ​ 𝒯 ) T_{D}=2\hbar/(\pi\mathcal{T}) , as a result of being constrained to a double-coned-shaped region known as a causal diamond. In this paper, we explore the emergence of thermality in causal diamonds due to the role played by the symmetries of conformal quantum mechanics (CQM) as a (0+1)-dimensional conformal field theory, within the de Alfaro-Fubini-Furlan model and generalizations. In this context, the hyperbolic operator S S of the SO(2,1) symmetry of CQM: (i) is the generator of the time evolution of a diamond observer; (ii) its dynamical behavior leads to the predicted thermal nature; and (iii) its associated quantum instability has a Lyapunov exponent λ L = π ​ T D / ℏ \lambda_{L}=\pi T_{D}/\hbar , which is half the upper saturation bound of the information scrambling rate. Our approach is based on a comprehensive framework of path-integral representations of the CQM generators in canonical and microcanonical forms, supplemented by semiclassical arguments. The properties of the operator S S are studied with emphasis on an operator duality with the corresponding elliptic operator R R , using a representation in terms of an effective scale-invariant inverse square potential combined with inverted and ordinary harmonic oscillator potentials.



I
Introduction

This paper addresses the thermal nature of causal diamonds in flat spacetime.
In principle, the thermality of the Minkowski vacuum corresponds to the detection of particles
with a thermal distribution by a finite-lifetime observer.
The theory is defined in terms of the action of a conformal quantum mechanics (CQM) model
as a
(0+1)
-dimensional field theory, with focus on a single-component field. The implementation
of the formalism is performed via the properties of the SO(2,1) conformal symmetry generators.
Our main results, which are fully quantum-mechanical and based on the propagator and Green’s function functionals of CQM, corroborate and complement similar findings from other methods.

The term

45

In what follows, we will summarize the symmetry framework introduced
in Ref.
[
74
]
,
and more generally analyzed within a path-integral approach in Ref.
[
75
]

The dAFF model, as defined by the action (
3

\displaystyle t^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\frac{at+b}{ct+d}\;,

(4)

\displaystyle Q^{\prime}(t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=(ct+d)^{-1}Q(t)\;,

(5)

where

are real numbers subject to the constraint
ad-bc=1

The transformation of the time parameter can be represented by a matrix

M=\Bigg(\;\begin{matrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{matrix}\;\Bigg)\;,

(6)

which belongs to the SL(2,
\mathbb{R}
) group and enforces the group properties of

•

Time-translation generator:

H=\frac{1}{2}\,P^{2}+\frac{g}{2Q^{2}}\;.

(7)

•

Dilatation generator:

(8)

•

\displaystyle K=t^{2}H-\frac{1}{2}t(QP+PQ)+\frac{1}{2}Q^{2}\;.

(9)

These generators are elements of the
sl(2,\mathbb{R})
Lie algebra with commutators

[D,H]=-iH\;,\qquad\qquad[D,K]=iK\;,\qquad\qquad[H,K]=2iD\;.

(10)

In addition, in the Cartan-Weyl basis
[

,
the generators

are replaced by the linear combinations

\displaystyle\hat{R}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\alpha H+\frac{1}{\alpha}\,K\right)\;,

(11)

\displaystyle S^{\prime}

\displaystyle=-\hat{S}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\alpha H-\frac{1}{\alpha}\,K\right)\;,

(12)

where

[
74

The dissimilar physical dimensions of

\alpha

The physical meaning of this parameter
\alpha

Then, the operators

, and

[D,\hat{R}]=i\hat{S}\;,\qquad\qquad[\hat{R},\hat{S}]=iD\;,\qquad\qquad[D,\hat{S}]=i\hat{R}\;,

(13)

which is homomorphic to the

sl(2,\mathbb{R})
algebra (

The

involves the compact operator
\hat{R}

\hat{S}

This implies that the time-evolution orbits of

are closed and periodic, whereas orbits of

and

75
]

Most importantly, the algebra of Eq. (

It should be noted that the operator

was introduced as an alternative to the original

to get a more transparent physical interpretation of the corresponding effective potential (see Refs.

In this article, we will rescale the Cartan-Weyl generators

with a factor

\displaystyle R=\frac{2}{\alpha}\hat{R}=H+\frac{1}{\alpha^{2}}\,K\;,

(14)

\displaystyle S=

\displaystyle\frac{2}{\alpha}{S^{\prime}}=-\frac{2}{\alpha}\hat{S}=H-\frac{1}{\alpha^{2}}\,K\;,

II.2
CQM generalized Hamiltonians and dynamics

The properties of CQM, including the uniqueness of the symmetry generators,
is based on enforcing the condition of conformal symmetry under arbitrary time transformations,
at the level of the action (
3
).
Once the symmetry algebra is established, all the operators
R,S,D
are on equal footing, and can be used
as generators of effective-time translations.
This is a key concept introduced in the original dAFF model
[
74
]
, leading to the
generalized symmetry generators

G=uH+vD+wK\;,

(16)

which can be interpreted as effective Hamiltonians of a redefined theory in terms of a
corresponding dynamical time
\tau
. Moreover, the operators (
16
)
can be completely characterized in terms of the discriminant

\Delta=v^{2}-4uw\;

(17)

that determines its nature:
rotations (elliptic type), for
\Delta<0
, which include
R
;
boosts (hyperbolic type), for
\Delta>0
,
which include both
S
and
D
;
and parabolic (“lightlike”) operators, for
\Delta=0
,
which include the original
H
and
K
generators.

Then, the resultant generalized dynamics governed by the effective Hamiltonian (
16
) as the
time-translation generator involves an effective time
\tau
given by

d\tau=\frac{dt}{u+vt+wt^{2}}\;,

(18)

where
t
is the standard time corresponding to the original Hamiltonian
H
.
In addition, the associated field or coordinate variable
q(\tau)
is given by

q(\tau)=\frac{Q(t)}{|u+vt+wt^{2}|^{1/2}}\;,

(19)

with a corresponding canonical momentum

{p}=\sigma\left|f_{G}\right|^{1/2}\biggl({P}-\frac{\dot{f_{G}}}{2f_{G}}\,{Q}\biggr)\;,

(20)

where
[
75
]

f_{G}(t)=u+vt+wt^{2}=\sigma\,\left|u+vt+wt^{2}\right|\;,

(21)

and the sign
\sigma\equiv\sigma_{G}=\mathrm{sgn}[f_{G}(t)]
allows for
arbitrary linear combinations of the generalized generator (
16
).
This analysis, pioneered in Ref.
[
74
]
, and further developed (for any number of dimensions)
in Ref.
[
75
]
, shows that
G
acts as a time-translation operator
in the generalized Schrödinger picture

G\ket{\psi(\tau)}=i\frac{d}{d\tau}\ket{\psi(\tau)}\;,

(22)

with
\ket{\psi(\tau)}=e^{-iG(\tau-\tau_{0})}\ket{\psi(\tau_{0})}
.

The CQM generalized dynamics admits a Hamiltonian representation for each generalized generator
G
.
The conjugate generalized momentum, after applying the transformations of
Eqs. (
18
)–(
19
), is given by Eq. (
20

p=\dot{q}
, where
\dot{q}(\tau)
denotes the derivative with respect to its associated time
\tau

The corresponding effective Hamiltonian is

\tilde{H}_{G}=\frac{1}{2}\,p^{2}+\frac{g}{2q^{2}}+\frac{1}{2}\left(-\frac{\Delta}{4}\right)q^{2}\;,

(23)

(after removing the extra sign
\sigma=\mathrm{sgn}[f_{G}(t)]
and rescaling)
[

]

With such Hamiltonian formulations, the

flowing according to the
effective times
\tau

18
).
In addition,
as a convenient effective-physics interpretation of the generators,

gives an exact analog of a quantum-mechanical problem with potential

\tilde{V}(q)=\frac{1}{2}\,\frac{g}{q^{2}}+\frac{1}{2}\omega^{2}q^{2}\;,

(24)

yielding the original inverse square potential superimposed with a harmonic oscillator of squared “frequency”

\displaystyle\omega^{2}=-\frac{\Delta}{4}

For the study of causal diamonds, we are especially interested in the physics generated by the rescaled

Specifically, the role of

Now, from Eq. (

the associated effective time for

[with the initial condition

The corresponding transformed field variables

Then, Eq. (

III.1
Time-Evolution dynamics of causal diamonds with radial conformal Killing fields

The relationship between causal diamonds and the symmetry generators of CQM has been explored in
Refs.
[
72
,
73
]

in the context of radial conformal Killing fields (RCKF)
[
82
]
in Minkowski spacetime.

The RCKFs are radial vector fields
\xi
such that the Lie derivative
\mathcal{L}_{\xi}
of the Minkowski metric
satisfies
\mathcal{L}_{\xi}\eta_{\mu\nu}\propto\eta_{\mu\nu}

The most general RCKF is a linear combination of the following three relativistic Killing
generators in Minkowski spacetime, as shown in Ref.
[

(i) the time-translation Killing vector
P_{0}=\partial_{t}

(ii) the dilatation generator
D_{0}=r\partial_{r}+t\partial_{t}

and (iii) the special conformal transformation generator

K_{0}=2tr\partial_{r}+(t^{2}+r^{2})\partial_{t}
(with respect to the time direction).
As a cautionary remark, in this section, the time variable

is the inertial Minkowski time, which is conceptually
different from (but analogous to) the CQM time
t
of the other sections of this paper.
Then,
\partial_{t}
is the timelike Minkowski Killing vector; and also,

is the radial distance from the origin.
The Killing generators satisfy an
sl(2,\mathbb{R})\approx so(2,1)
algebra

[D_{0},P_{0}]=-P_{0}\;,\qquad\qquad[D_{0},K_{0}]=K_{0}\;,\qquad\qquad[P_{0},K_{0}]=2D_{0}\;,

(28)

as a subset of the spacetime conformal algebra.
The algebra (

(with the extra imaginary unit

in the conversion from Killing fields to self-adjoint Lie generators),
is in one-to-one correspondence with the CQM

algebra of Eq. (

Moreover, the statement that an RCKF is of the form

\xi=aK_{0}+bD_{0}+cP_{0}

b
, and
c

is also in one-to-one correspondence with the CQM statement (

Therefore, via this

CQM-RCKF operator correspondence
:

CQM can be regarded as an effective theory
whose symmetry algebra and generator flows are isomorphic to those of the spacetime RCKFs.

From this isomorphism, one concludes
[
72
,
73
]
that:
(i) the flows of Killing vectors in Minkowski spacetime
can be faithfully represented with the corresponding Hamiltonian evolutions of CQM;
(ii) the CQM classification of (Hamiltonian) generators correspondingly applies to the RCKFs.
These properties are further outlined in the next paragraph.

Given the CQM-RCKF operator correspondence, one can define the analogs of the
R
and
S
operators in the
form of Eq. (

\displaystyle R_{K}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\alpha P_{0}+\frac{1}{\alpha}\,K_{0}\right)=\frac{1}{2\alpha}\left[(\alpha^{2}+t^{2}+r^{2})\partial_{t}+2tr\partial_{r}\right]\;,

(29)

\displaystyle S_{K}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\alpha P_{0}-\frac{1}{\alpha}\,K_{0}\right)=\frac{1}{2\alpha}\left[(\alpha^{2}-t^{2}-r^{2})\partial_{t}-2tr\partial_{r}\right]\;,

\displaystyle D_{K}\equiv D_{0}=r\partial_{r}+t\partial_{t}\;.

III.2
Time-evolution dynamics of causal diamonds: The physics of diamond observers

In this article, using the explicit Hamiltonian form of the

IV
Quantum analysis of thermality via path integrals

This section presents the most important result of our paper:

the thermal nature of causal diamonds, driven by the properties of the
hyperbolic operator
S
as the generator of its time evolution.

Our complete analysis involves path integral and Green’s function techniques. In addition to addressing
the relevant quantum features of the thermality of causal diamonds, we will examine some ingredients of its semiclassical regime, which has the seeds of the full-fledged quantum behavior.

It is noteworthy that the operator
S
is noncompact, with a spectrum unbounded from below. As a result,
it exhibits a dynamical instability, which can be formally discussed both in terms
of a semiclassical method and using an out-of-time-order correlation (OTOC) function
[
64
,
66
]
;
with these methods, a growth scrambling rate (quantum Lyapunov exponent)

\lambda_{L}
can be extracted, as elaborated upon in
Sec.
V
.
Moreover, as in the case of black holes, this appears to be related to their thermal nature
[
52
]
.

In this section, and for the remainder of the paper, we restore the reduced
Planck’s constant
\hbar
in all the relevant equations.

(30)

The frequency parameter

IV.2
Path-integral amplitude functionals: Canonical and microcanonical

Another important set of tools in our analysis is the use of path-integral expressions for the probability
amplitudes
[
84
,
85
]
.
These are defined in terms of exponentials of action functionals summed over configuration paths.
In particular, there are two important action functionals at the classical level: the ordinary
Lagrangian action associated with Hamilton’s principle and the Jabobi action associated with the Maupertuis principle.
These lead to two distinct but related path-integral expressions at the quantum level.
In addition, the path-integral results can be derived either in full-fledged quantum mechanical form or via
asymptotic semiclassical approximations.
In what follows, we will use appropriate ingredients of these miscellaneous approaches in the arguments supporting the thermality of causal diamonds.

Hamilton’s formulation of the action functional
S[q(t)]=\int L(q,\dot{q},t)dt
,
associated with the variational Hamilton’s principle,
gives a value of
S[q(t)]
that depends on fixed configuration end points

q^{\prime}
and
q^{\prime\prime}
with given endpoint times
t^{\prime}
and
t^{\prime\prime}
;
in particular, this value only depends on the time difference
T=t^{\prime\prime}-t^{\prime}
for conservative systems.
This feature can be emphasized by writing the action functional (or Hamilton’s principal function)
as
S[q(t)](q^{\prime\prime},q^{\prime};T)
, for which we will use the shorthand
S[q;T]
,
highlighting that
T
is fixed but
E
is not.
(In this section, we will consider
q
to be a one-dimensional coordinate, though it could represent
a system with an arbitrary number of coordinates, and this is relevant for CQM;
see Appendix
A
).
Moreover, from the variational conditions satisfied in classical dynamics
[
86
,
87
,
88
]
, the following time derivative gives one of the fundamental equations:

-\partial S[q;T]/\partial T=E
; this statement is applicable to the Hamiltonian taking values
H=E
.

Alternatively, Jacobi’s action formulation for conservative systems
[
86
,
87
,
88
]

involves the combination

W[q(t)](q^{\prime\prime},q^{\prime};E)\equiv W[q;E]=S[q;T]+ET\;,

(32)

which is a Legendre transform that exchanges the variables
E
and
T
.
Thus, this functional
W[q;E]
, called the Jacobi action,
Hamilton’s characteristic function, or abbreviated action,
has a fixed energy
E
for all paths, but the corresponding physical time interval
T
between initial and final configurations is path dependent
[
86
,
87
,
88
]
.
The Jacobi action so defined can be rewritten,
from the definition of the Hamiltonian,
as the momentum
integral with respect to the configuration-space path,

W[q;E]=\int pdq\;,

(33)

and it is associated with the variational Maupertuis principle.
Even though the Jacobi formulation of the action principle does not involve the time explicitly, the
conjugate
classical time interval
can be determined from the ensuing classical equations of motion,
and is given from the Legendre transform by

T=\frac{\partial W[q;E]}{\partial E}\;.

(34)

This formulation
has important applications in gravitational theory vis-a-vis the general-relativistic definition of time
[
89
,
90
,
91
,
92
]
,
and is central to semiclassical descriptions of quantum mechanics and path integrals, as in the context of quantum chaos
[
93
,
94
,
95
,
96
]
.

For the quantum-theory path integrals, these two frameworks (Hamilton’s versus Jacobi’s action) correspond to the configuration-space representations of the transition amplitude
K(q^{\prime\prime},q^{\prime};T)
and
the energy Green’s function
G(q^{\prime\prime},q^{\prime};E)
respectively.
The transition amplitude is given by the standard path integral

K(q^{\prime\prime},q^{\prime};T)=\int_{{q}(t^{\prime})={q}^{\prime}}^{{q}(t^{\prime\prime})={q}^{\prime\prime}}\;{\cal D}{q}(t)\,\exp\left\{\frac{i}{\hbar}S\left[{q}(t)\right]({q}^{\prime\prime},{q}^{\prime};T)\right\}\;;

(35)

and the (retarded) energy Green’s function is defined
by

G(q^{\prime\prime},q^{\prime};E)=\frac{1}{i\hbar}\int_{0}^{\infty}dT\,\int_{{q}(t^{\prime})={q}^{\prime}}^{{q}(t^{\prime\prime})={q}^{\prime\prime}}\;{\cal D}{q}(t)\,\exp\left\{\frac{i}{\hbar}W\left[{q}(t)\right]({q}^{\prime\prime},{q}^{\prime};E)\right\}\;.

(36)

It should be noted that the energy Green’s functions involve a path integral with a measure that includes an extra
T
integration, and unlike
the ordinary path integral of Eq. (
35
),
they are effectively computed with the Jacobi action—this can be further developed using the theory of constraints to characterize all the energy functionals in a compact
formulation
[
90
,
91
,
92
]
.
In the thermal interpretation of quantum physics with imaginary time, these two frameworks are the

canonical and microcanonical actions and path integrals.

We will use these frameworks below to derive the basic thermal properties of causal diamonds,
with supporting details and generalizations in Appendix
A
.

In addition, by considering periodic boundary conditions where
q(t^{\prime\prime})=q(t^{\prime})
, one can define formal
traces of the above operators, which have been called quantum-mechanical
partition functions
[
84
]
—and
indeed, they correspond to the statistical-mechanical partition functions when the analytic extension
to imaginary time defines thermal functionals. This is used in the discussion of thermal properties below.
The canonical and microcanonical trace functions are defined
as the operator traces (associated with a Hamiltonian
\hat{H}
)

\displaystyle\tilde{Z}^{(H)}(T)\equiv{\rm Tr}[K^{(H)}(T)]\equiv{\rm Tr}\left[e^{-i\hat{H}T/\hbar}\right]=\int dq^{\prime}K^{(H)}(q^{\prime},q^{\prime};T)\;,

(37)

\displaystyle{\rm Tr}\left[\hat{G}^{(H)}(E)\right]=\frac{1}{i\hbar}\int_{0}^{\infty}dT\,e^{iET/\hbar}\;\tilde{Z}^{(H)}(T)=\int dq^{\prime}G^{(H)}(q^{\prime},q^{\prime};E)\;.

(38)

We can refer to these quantities (
37
) and (
38
)
as the “traces of the corresponding amplitude functions,” i.e.,

{\rm Tr}[K(T)]
and
{\rm Tr}[G(E)]
.
As a byproduct of these definitions,
one can also define various derived statistical quantities, including
the density of states (see Appendix
B
).

IV.4
Thermality of causal diamonds: Canonical-ensemble characterization
and conclusions

The physical meaning of the imaginary time period, which was found in the previous section,

In this framework, the Wick rotation to Euclidean time is defined in the

The expression for the period

Then, this shows that the partition function or trace of the propagator indeed corresponds to the canonical-ensemble

V
Semiclassical Aspects of Instability and Thermality in Causal Diamonds

In this section, we develop another key result of our paper:

the phase-space dynamics of the hyperbolic operator
S
shows an instability behavior

that appears to be related to the thermal properties of causal diamonds.

We obtain this behavior by first studying the

S
-driven Hamiltonian dynamics, leading to the determination of the Lyapunov exponent.
In addition, we fully characterize the closed phase-space orbits and associated Jacobi action, rederiving some
of the thermality results of the previous section.
This analysis concludes with basic results on information scrambling.

Additional semiclassical aspects of causal diamonds are explored
in Appendix
C
via the density of states of the operator
S
,
along with the corresponding fully quantum-mechanical results.

V.1
Hamiltonian dynamics of the
S
operator

The phase-space analysis is based on the identification of the operator
S
as the Hamiltonian for
the time evolution governed by the natural time parameter

\tau_{\!{}_{S}}
,
as was formulated in the previous section.
The system’s Hamiltonian dynamics can then be fully characterized using Hamilton’s equation of motion,

\dot{q}=\frac{\partial S}{\partial p}\;,\qquad\qquad\dot{p}=-\frac{\partial S}{\partial q}\;,

(52)

where
p
is the canonical conjugate momentum.
Therefore, with the explicit form of the operator
S
in Eq. (
31
), Hamilton’s equations read

\dot{q}=p\;,\qquad\qquad\dot{p}=\frac{g}{q^{3}}+\frac{q}{\alpha^{2}}\;.

(53)

These equations are straightforward, but the behavior of the term
{q}/\alpha^{2}
in the second one has
dramatic consequences for the stability analysis of the theory, as we show next.

V.2
Phase-space analysis of the
S
operator: Lyapunov exponent

The phase-space dynamics of the hyperbolic operator
S

can be understood by its representation with phase-space trajectories.
These direction-field orbits are shown in Fig.
4
, with the asymptotes drawn in red.

Figure 4:
Phase-space direction field for the trajectories for the
S
operator (with
g=1,\alpha=2
). The red line
p=q/\alpha
shows the asymptotes of the trajectories.

An important property for our purposes is the nature of the stability of the phase-space orbits.
This question can be answered by analyzing the evolution of a perturbation
\delta q(\tau)

away from a given orbit
q(\tau)
.
In particular, if the perturbation grows exponentially, then the orbit is unstable, with a
Lyapunov exponent or instability factor

\lambda_{L}=\lim_{\tau\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{\tau}\ln\left[\frac{\delta q(\tau)}{\delta q(0)}\right]\;,

(54)

which is completely determined by the long-term behavior of the perturbation,

\delta(q)(\tau\rightarrow\infty)
.
This asymptotic behavior can be determined via an effective-potential
analysis, considering
that
S
has a purely repulsive behavior, with an infinite
barrier (
\propto 1/q^{2}
) near the origin (for
q\approx 0
)
and an infinite well (
\propto-q^{2}
) near infinity
(for
q\approx\infty
);
thus, we expect that
q
will monotonically approach
q\approx\infty

for sufficiently large values of
\tau
.
This asymptotic behavior sets in when
\tau\gg\alpha
and
q^{4}\gg g\alpha^{2}
,
and reduces Eq. (
53
) self-consistently to

\dot{q}=p\;,\qquad\qquad\dot{p}\approx\frac{q}{\alpha^{2}}\;,

(55)

which leads to the late-time perturbation

\bigl.\delta q(\tau\gg\alpha)\bigr|_{q^{4}\gg g\alpha^{2}}\approx\delta q(0)\,e^{\tau/\alpha}\;.

(56)

As a result, the Lyapunov exponent is given by

\lambda_{L}=\frac{1}{\alpha}>0\;.

(57)

In conclusion, the positive instability factor of Eq. (
57
)
characterizes a perturbation that grows exponentially in the
q
direction, due to a repulsive interaction, as in
Eq. (
55
), for any late-time orbit.
This shows that the system is unstable, supporting the expected intuition for a potential dominated at late
times by an inverted oscillator.
This dynamical instability is closely related to the thermal behavior of causal diamonds, as we will see next.

V.3
Semiclassical analysis: Jacobi action of
R

We can now reexamine the microcanonical analysis of Sec.
IV.3

via the explicit evaluation of the Jacobi action,
by considering closed periodic orbits
\gamma
of
R
in classical phase space.
As shown in Sec.
IV.2
,
these periodic orbits, despite being classical, are the ones used in
the quantum-mechanical traces (
37
) and (
38
)
in the path-integral formulation of the theory. As such, they encode the full-fledged quantum and thermal behavior, but the origin of this property can be probed at the semiclassical level.
Moreover, they are the basic building blocks in the study of both classical and quantum chaos.
In dealing with conservative systems, the existence of these orbits is guaranteed, leading to their definition via the Hamiltonian
R\equiv\tilde{H}_{R}

(denoted with the symbol of the corresponding generator),
given as in Eq. (
40
).
A typical trajectory of constant energy
E_{\gamma}
is shown in Fig.
5
.

Figure 5:
Example of a closed trajectory for
R
(with
g=1,\alpha=2
, and
E_{\gamma}=2
).

For the description of the orbits of the operator
R
defined by Eq. (
40
), a fixed-energy condition is required, and this converts the usual action
S[q;T]
into the Legendre-transformed Jacobi action (
32
).
For the periodic orbits, the Jacobi action integral
W(E_{\gamma})

admits the following geometrical interpretation:
it gives the area enclosed by the closed path in Fig.
5
, which can
be expressed in closed form as

\displaystyle W(E_{\gamma})=\oint p\,dq

\displaystyle=\oint\,dq\,\sqrt{2E_{\gamma}-\frac{g}{q^{2}}-\frac{1}{\alpha_{\!{}_{R}}^{2}}q^{2}}

(58)

\displaystyle=\pi\alpha_{\!{}_{R}}E_{\gamma}-\pi\sqrt{g}\;

(e.g.,
2.267.1
in Ref
[
102
]
).
The result of Eq. (
58
) shows that the Jacobi action is an increasing
function with respect to
\alpha_{R}
and decreasing with respect to
g
.

Finally, as Eq. (
58
) has been obtained via a classical argument,
its correct semiclassical interpretation requires the
Langer correction
[
103
]
:

\sqrt{g}\rightarrow\sqrt{g+1/4}=\mu
,
when used as part of the phase of a WKB or quasiclassical expansion
in the presence of inverse square potential terms
[
40
,
41
]
.
Thus, the correct semiclassical result (to be used in the next section) is

\left.W(E_{\gamma})\right|_{\rm Langer-corr}=\pi\alpha_{\!{}_{R}}E_{\gamma}-\pi\mu\;.

(59)

V.4
Semiclassical analysis: Microcanonical approach and information scrambling

The Langer-corrected Eq. (
59
)
allows the computation of the period
T_{\gamma}

via the general equation for the Legendre-transformed conjugate classical transit
time (
34
).
Thus, the time period
T_{\gamma}
of these closed orbits is

T_{\gamma}=\frac{\partial W(E_{\gamma})}{\partial E_{\gamma}}=\pi\alpha_{\!{}_{R}}\;,

(60)

which agrees with Eq. (
42
),
confirming our conclusions on thermality of Sec.
IV
.
In particular, this yields the diamond temperature (
1
).

A final point is in order regarding the crucially important relationship of the diamond temperature (
1
)
with the instability analysis of Sec.
V.2
, along
with the related quantum information scrambling.
The information scrambling rate
\lambda_{L}
for causal diamonds, according to
Eq. (
57
), satisfies the upper-bound condition,

\lambda_{L}=\frac{1}{\alpha}<\frac{2\pi T_{D}}{\hbar}=\frac{2}{\alpha}\;,

(61)

with a strict inequality.
This upper bound is determined by the observer’s lifetime or diamond size, just as the
upper bound for black holes is determined by the surface gravity.
However,
unlike the case of black holes, the upper-bound is not saturated.

Most importantly, the main lesson from this analysis is that an instability is instrumental for nontrivial thermal
and quantum information scrambling effects in spacetime,
just as it was earlier found in Ref.
[
52
]
for black holes.

VI
Discussion and further work

We have shown that a finite-lifetime observer in Minkowski spacetime, described within
a causal diamond, detects a thermal state with temperature
T_{D}
, Eq. (
1
).
This prediction is solely inferred
within a path-integral framework for CQM, from the properties of the time-evolution generator, which is the
hyperbolic
S
operator of the dAFF model, confirming properties known from a variety of alternative approaches. Moreover, these path-integral findings further corroborate the role of the effective potentials
including an inverted harmonic oscillator
[
104
]

to generate thermal behavior linked to an instability,
extending them to the remarkable physics of causal diamonds in flat spacetime.

Even though our findings concerning the relation between the
instability and thermality

have been partially motivated via a semiclassical limit in a manner related to the work of Refs.
[
51
,
52
,
53
,
54
,
55
,
56
]
,
we have
extended them to a more general, exact quantum-mechanical argument with path integrals,
via analytic continuation with the canonical partition function of the operator
R
.

We are currently investigating methods to establish a more direct connection of quantum mechanical chaos with the
S
operator using fully quantum mechanical tools such as the finite-temperature out-of-time-order correlation (OTOC) function
[
64
,
66
]
, which can be applied to the single particle operator
S
,
e.g., following the footsteps of Ref.
[
105
]
.

Moreover, the CQM model is also related to the Sachdev-Ye-Kitaev (SYK) model in the large-
N
limit
[
68
,
69
]
,
with connections to black holes in AdS spacetime
[
70
]
and applications to a broad range
of areas of physics regarding information scrambling
[
71
]
.
It seems plausible that further connections of our line of work with the SYK model may provide additional insights into the links between CQM, chaos, and black hole thermodynamics.

In closing, the importance of finding experimental realizations of the causal-diamond physics of finite-lifetime observers
should be highlighted. This could potentially lead to critical tests of
relativistic quantum information. In fact, the possible use of time-dependent Stark or Zeeman effects has been considered
[
17
]
, but
additional phenomenological analysis is needed for its implementation.

K_{l+\nu}^{(R)}(r^{\prime\prime},r^{\prime};T)=\frac{M\omega}{i\hbar\sin\omega T}\,\sqrt{r^{\prime}r^{\prime\prime}}\,\exp\left[\frac{iM\omega}{2\hbar}\left(r^{\prime 2}+r^{\prime\prime 2}\right)\cot\omega T\right]I_{\mu}\left(\frac{M\omega r^{\prime}r^{\prime\prime}}{i\hbar\sin\omega T}\right)\;,

(71)

where
I_{\mu}(z)
is the modified Bessel function of the first kind and order
\mu
.
This expression is completely general for complex values of the parameter
\omega
; thus, it can be used to relate
the elliptic and hyperbolic sectors of the theory, using

\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}R\longrightarrow S\\
S\longrightarrow R\end{array}\right\}=\begin{pmatrix}\omega\longrightarrow\mp i\omega\end{pmatrix}\;;

(72)

this is the same as the duality of Eq. (
30
).
Thus,
with the replacement
\omega\rightarrow-i\omega
, Eq. (
71
)
gives the propagator for hyperbolic operators,

K_{l+\nu}^{(S)}(r^{\prime\prime},r^{\prime};T)=\frac{M\omega}{i\hbar\sinh\omega T}\sqrt{r^{\prime}r^{\prime\prime}}\,\exp\left[\frac{iM\omega}{2\hbar}\left({r^{\prime}}^{2}+{r^{\prime\prime}}^{2}\right)\coth\omega T\right]\,I_{\mu}\left(\frac{M\omega r^{\prime}r^{\prime\prime}}{i\hbar\sinh\omega T}\right)\;.

(73)

Moreover, as mentioned in the main text,
the choice
\omega\rightarrow-i\omega
(instead of the opposite sign)
has the operational advantage that it preserves the boundary condition at infinity
corresponding to retarded Green’s functions
[
75
]
, and it is also
consistent with the standard extension to Euclidean time (see below).
In addition, Eq. (
72
) can also be applied to the Green’s functions, e.g.,

\left.G_{l+\nu}^{(R)}(r^{\prime\prime},r^{\prime};E)\right|_{\omega\rightarrow-i\omega}=G_{l+\nu}^{(S)}(r^{\prime\prime},r^{\prime};E)
.
Thus, it suffices to specify, as shown in Ref.
[
75
]
,

G^{(\pm)(R)}_{l+\nu}(r^{\prime\prime},r^{\prime};E)=\mp(\hbar\omega)^{-1}\,\frac{\Gamma\left((1+\mu)/2\mp\kappa\right)}{\Gamma(1+\mu)}\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{r^{\prime}r^{\prime\prime}}}W_{\pm\kappa,\mu/2}(\pm\check{r}_{>}^{2})\,{M}_{\pm\kappa,\mu/2}(\pm\check{r}_{<}^{2})\;,

(74)

where

{M}_{\lambda,\mu/2}(z)

and

W_{\lambda,\mu/2}(z)
are Whittaker functions
[
102
]
,

\kappa=\tilde{E}/2\hbar\omega
[for the eigenvalues of the operator (
69
)],

\displaystyle\check{r}^{2}=\frac{M\omega}{\hbar}\,{r}^{2}
,
and
r_{>}
and
r_{<}

are the greater and lesser of the set
\{r^{\prime},r^{\prime\prime}\}
.

Fourth, the simplest realization of this
d
-dimensional theory is the
one-dimensional case (
d=1
, i.e.,
\nu=-1/2
), as introduced in the original dAFF model and used in our current paper.
Due to the presence of a nonzero repulsive inverse square potential, with
g>0
,
the one-dimensional problem is restricted to a half-line axis (
r\geq 0
, with
r
labeled as
q
in the main body of this work.)
This choice corresponds to the following set of radial propagators and indices:

K^{(R)}_{l+\nu}(r^{\prime\prime},r^{\prime};T)=K^{(R)}_{\mp 1/2}(r^{\prime\prime},r^{\prime};T)\;,\;\;\text{ with }\;l+\nu=\mp 1/2\;\;\;\text{ and }\;\mu=\sqrt{g+1/4}\;,

(75)

which includes the two angular momentum channels
l=0,1
with equal contributions.
This procedure is enforced via the Bessel functions with the common positive square root for the conformal index

\mu=\sqrt{g+1/4}>0
, as opposed to the distinct indices
\mu=\pm 1/2
that would be used for a pure oscillator with
g=0
.
As discussed in Appendix
A
, Sec.
A.1
,
with the equality of the propagators in Eq. (
75
),
the partial-wave expansion gives the same function

K=K_{-1/2}=K_{1/2}

for all the propagators, and similarly, for all the Green’s functions,

G=G_{-1/2}=G_{1/2}
.
In hindsight, the restriction
q>0
could be handled
bypassing some of these details, by choosing
K^{(R)}_{1/2}(r^{\prime\prime},r^{\prime};T)

(odd-channel,

l=1
, solutions)
with
\mu=\sqrt{g+1/4}

as the main propagator from scratch,
according to Eq. (
75
).

Finally, another analytic continuation, central to our derivations, is the extension to imaginary time
via the Wick rotation
[
98
]

\begin{pmatrix}\text{Real-time functional}\\
\text{with}\;T\longrightarrow-iT\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\text{Euclidean}\\
\text{functional}\end{pmatrix}\;.

(76)

Thus, as derived in Ref.
[
75
]
, from Eq. (
71
), the corresponding Euclidean-time path integral is

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!K^{(R_{\rm Eucl})}_{l+\nu}(r^{\prime\prime},r^{\prime};T)

\displaystyle\equiv K^{(R)}_{l+\nu}(r^{\prime\prime},r^{\prime};-iT)

(77)

\displaystyle=\frac{M\omega}{\hbar\sinh\omega T}\,\sqrt{r^{\prime}r^{\prime\prime}}\,\exp\left[-\frac{M\omega}{2\hbar}\left(r^{\prime 2}+r^{\prime\prime 2}\right)\coth\omega T\right]I_{\mu}\left(\frac{M\omega r^{\prime}r^{\prime\prime}}{\hbar\sinh\omega T}\right)\,.

B.2
Relationships between elliptic and hyperbolic path integrals of CQM

The propagators (
71
)–(
77
),
their corresponding traces, and the associated Green’s functions satisfy simple equivalence relations that can be derived from first principles and/or the explicit form of these functional integrals.
These relations provide an important generic tool in our analysis of CQM:
the ability to relate the two nontrivial sectors of the theory, elliptic and hyperbolic
[beyond the original Hamiltonian
H
corresponding to Eq. (
3
)].
This is based on the reasonable ansatz that the Euclidean-time version of a theory with a potential generates a
problem in imaginary time with the inverted potential. In the case of CQM, when the inverse square potential is
treated separately as part of the functional measure (or absorbed in a nontrivial angular momentum), the inversion is only enforced for the harmonic oscillator, which amounts to the analytic continuation

\omega\rightarrow-i\omega
,
i.e., the conversion of
R
into
S
.
Thus, barring minor adjustments, there should exist relations of the form

\begin{pmatrix}\text{Functional}\\
\text{for}\;R_{\rm Eucl}\end{pmatrix}\sim\begin{pmatrix}\text{Functional}\\
\text{for}\;S\end{pmatrix}\;.

(78)

In short, the
network of analytic-continuation relations among the functionals
consists of
Eqs. (
72
),
(
76
),
and
(
78
).
However, we have to specify what is meant by the subset of relations (
78
).
These include the following statements, which we prove below.

•

Propagators:

\left.K^{(R_{\rm Eucl})}_{l+\nu}(r^{\prime\prime},r^{\prime};T)\right|_{M\rightarrow-iM}=K^{(S)}_{l+\nu}(r^{\prime\prime},r^{\prime};T)\;.

(79)

•

Energy Green’s functions:

\left.G^{(R_{\rm Eucl})}_{l+\nu}(r^{\prime\prime},r^{\prime};E)\right|_{\omega\rightarrow-i\omega}=G^{(S)}_{l+\nu}(r^{\prime\prime},r^{\prime};E)\;.

(80)

•

Propagator traces:

\displaystyle\tilde{Z}_{l+\nu}^{(R_{\rm Eucl})}(T)

\displaystyle=\tilde{Z}_{l+\nu}^{(S)}(T)

(81)

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{-\mu\omega T}}{2\sinh\omega T}\;.

(82)

•

Energy Green’s function traces:

\left.{\rm Tr}\left[\hat{G}^{(R_{\rm Eucl})}(E)\right]\right|_{\omega\rightarrow-i\omega}={\rm Tr}\left[\hat{G}^{(S)}(E)\right]\;.

(83)

The first basic relation (
79
)
is the direct formal connection between the propagators.
Its validity is due to that the full-fledged real-time evolution of
S
can be restored with an imaginary mass,
which multiplies all the terms of the Hamiltonian
in Eq. (
69
). This is also
explicitly verified from a direct comparison of the resulting propagators (
73
) and
(
77
).

The second relation (
80
)
is a similar formal connection between the energy Green’s functions.
This is due to the replacement of the time variable by the energy
E
; and by definition,
the same energy Green’s function is introduced in either framework
in such a way that the Green’s operators take the form (
67
).
Thus, the analytic continuation simply reduces back to Eq. (
72
).
This is also explicitly verified with the form of the energy Green’s
functions (
74
)—with additional details in Ref.
[
75
]
.

The fourth relation (
83
)
is a consequence of the identity of energy Green’s functions, Eq. (
80
).
This directly leads to the formal identity of the corresponding density of states,
but their values need to be adjusted as follows.
The formal connection between both relations for the energy Green’s functions:
Eqs. (
80
) and (
83
),
involve the subtle replacement of the frequency parameter required by the analytic continuation;
in particular, this rotation in the frequency complex plane changes the nature of the functions, making the density
of states (
83
) divergent, in a way that requires regularization
(see Appendix
C
).

Finally, the third relation,
Eqs. (
81
)–(
82
)
requires a more detailed analysis.
This central identity is the key to the proof of the characterization of thermality,
Eq. (
44
).
This is the statement of identity of the traces, when defined via Eq. (
37
),
and can be proved as follows.
First, without finding its specific value,
the equality

\tilde{Z}_{l+\nu}^{(R_{\rm Eucl})}(T)=\tilde{Z}_{l+\nu}^{(S)}(T)
is based on the scaling
of the original Hamiltonian with the variable
Mr^{2}
, as can be seen from
Eq. (
69
). This scaling corresponds to the analytic extension
M\longrightarrow-iM
, which
relates the two propagators; then, when computing the corresponding traces, which are radial integrals, the final outcome only depends on the combination
Mr^{2}/\hbar
, such that the extension
Mr^{2}\longrightarrow-iMr^{2}

converts
\tilde{Z}_{l+\nu}^{(R_{\rm Eucl})}(T)
exactly into
\tilde{Z}_{l+\nu}^{(S)}(T)
.
This common value, computed as
\tilde{Z}_{l+\nu}(T)
with the operator
S
, can be rewritten with
the substitution
x={M\omega r^{2}}/{\hbar}
, yielding

\tilde{Z}^{(S)}(T)={\rm Tr}\left[K_{l+\nu}^{(S)}\right]=\int_{0}^{\infty}dr\;K_{l+\nu}^{(S)}(r,r;T)=\frac{1}{2i\sinh{\omega T}}\int_{0}^{\infty}dx\;e^{ix\coth\omega T}I_{\mu}\left(\frac{x}{i\sinh{\omega T}}\right)\;.

(84)

Then, in Eq. (
84
), with
z=\omega T
,
the integral

\mathcal{I}(z,\mu)=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{ix\coth z}\;I_{\mu}\left(\frac{x}{i\sinh z}\right)\;dx=i\,\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-y\coth z}\;I_{\mu}\left(\frac{y}{\sinh z}\right)\;dy=i\;e^{-\mu z}

(85)

is evaluated using either one of the equations
6.611.1

or
6.622.3
in Ref
[
102
]
;
for the latter, with the replacements
\zeta=y/\sinh z
and
a=z
,
the more general integral in Ref
[
102
]
,

\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\zeta\cosh a}I_{\mu}(\zeta)\,\zeta^{\nu-1}\,d\zeta=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\,e^{-(\nu-1/2)\pi i}\;\frac{Q_{\mu-\frac{1}{2}}^{\nu-\frac{1}{2}}\bigl(\cosh a\bigr)}{\sinh^{\nu-\frac{1}{2}}a}\;,

involves the associated Legendre function of the second kind
Q_{\mu}^{\nu}
,
with
Q^{1/2}_{\mu-1/2}(\cosh a)=i\,e^{-\mu a}\,\sqrt{\pi/2\sinh a}

(Ref
[
102
]
, Secs. 8.7–8.8).
[The integrals are quoted in terms of
y
with the real exponential in standard references,
as in Ref.
[
102
]
; the substitution
x=iy
for the integral with respect to
x

in Eq. (
85
) is valid, as it amounts to a contour integral in the lower-right quadrant
of the complex plane
y
, where the integrand is exponentially small at infinity.]
Thus, with the auxiliary equation (
85
),
the integral in Eq. (
84
) gives the final form of the trace,

\tilde{Z}_{l+\nu}^{(S)}(T)=\frac{e^{-\mu\omega T}}{2\sinh\omega T}\;,

(86)

in agreement with Eq. (
82
).
Similarly, for the Euclideanized
R
operator,
setting
x=M\omega r^{2}/\hbar
,

\tilde{Z}_{l+\nu}^{(R_{\rm Eucl})}(T)=\frac{1}{2\sinh{\omega T}}\;\int_{0}^{\infty}dx\;\exp\left({-x\coth{\omega T}}\right)\;I_{\mu}\left(\frac{x}{\sinh{\omega T}}\right)\;,

(87)

where, using again the auxiliary equation (
85
),
the partition function becomes

\tilde{Z}_{l+\nu}^{(R_{\rm Eucl})}(T)=\frac{e^{-\mu\omega{T}}}{2\sinh{\omega{T}}}\;,

(88)

which gives the same value as

\tilde{Z}_{l+\nu}^{(S)}(T)
.
This completes the proof of
Eqs. (
81
)–(

).

APPENDIX C
DENSITY OF STATES ASSOCIATED WITH THE OPERATOR

This appendix develops all the basic results on the density of states
\rho^{(S)}(E)

corresponding to the operator
S
.
In addition to deriving exact, fully quantum-mechanical expressions for
\rho^{(S)}(E)
,
we highlight various results involving regularization of the resulting

These miscellaneous results provide additional insight into the main properties
of thermality and instability of causal diamonds derived in the main text, and can be used to reexamine other
treatments of the problem.

C.1
Density of states from the exact energy Green’s functions

For the energy Green’s functions, defined to satisfy the resolvent operator
equation (
67
), their trace is

\displaystyle{\rm Tr}\left[\hat{G}_{l+\nu}^{(S)}(E)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{i\hbar}\int_{0}^{\infty}dT\,e^{iET/\hbar}\;\tilde{Z}_{l+\nu}^{(S)}(T)=\frac{1}{i\hbar}\int_{0}^{\infty}\,dT\;e^{iET/\hbar}\;\frac{e^{-\mu\omega T}}{2\sinh\omega T}

(89)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{i\hbar\omega}\int_{0}^{\infty}\,d{z}\;e^{iE{z}/\hbar\omega}\;\frac{e^{-\mu{z}}}{2\sinh{ z }}\;

(90)

(assuming the retarded operators/functions with the plus sign),
where
{z}=\omega T
and
Eqs. (
37
)–(
38
) and (
82
) were used.

In what follows, we will consider only the density of states expressions for the original dAFF model, which corresponds to an effective one-dimensional problem (
d=1
).
In that case, from Eq. (
75
),
the conformal index is
\mu=\sqrt{g+1/4}
.
Also, as discussed in Appendix
A
, Sec.
A.1
,
the net path-integral functionals for an effective one-dimensional
problem restricted to the positive half-line by an inverse square potential are the same as their individual angular momentum
components. In particular,

\hat{G}(E)=\hat{G}_{l+\nu}(E)
, so that
the density of states is obtained from the retarded Green’s functions
via Eq. (
68
).
Then, with Eq. (
90
), the density of states for the operator
S
has the form

\!\!\rho^{(S)}(E)\!=-\frac{1}{2\pi\hbar}{\rm Im}\!\left[\frac{1}{i\omega}\int_{0}^{\infty}d{z}\;e^{iE{z}/\hbar\omega}\;\frac{e^{-\mu{z}}}{\sinh{{ z }}}\right]\!=\!\frac{1}{2\pi\hbar}{\rm Re}\!\left(\frac{1}{\omega}\int_{0}^{\infty}d{z}\;\frac{\exp\left[i(\eta+i\mu){z}\right]}{\sinh{{ z }}}\right)\,,

(91)

where
\eta=E/(\hbar\omega)
.
In addition, according to the relation (
83
),
this density function
can also be computed with the Euclideanized version of the
R
operator,
giving exactly the same result, but it requires
taking the real (or imaginary) part
in Eq. (
91
) after the analytic continuation is performed
.

Several useful properties of Eq. (
91
) can be highlighted with the following procedure.
First, the integral

I=\int dzF(z)
, where
F(z)=e^{i\zeta z}\,(\sinh z)^{-1}
, with
\zeta=\eta+i\mu
, can be evaluated as follows.
Expanding the denominator via a geometric series of exponentials,

F(z)=2\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}e^{-z\left[(1+2n)-i\zeta\right]}
, and integrating,
Eq. (
91
) yields

\rho^{(S)}(E)=\frac{1}{\pi\hbar}\,{\rm Re}\left(\frac{1}{\omega}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\left[\left(2n+\mu+1\right)-i\eta\right]}\right)\;.

(92)

The series (
92
) explicitly displays the poles in the denominator,
with respect to the energy parameter
\eta
, which occur at

\eta=-i(2n+\mu+1)
; with a rotation in the omega plane,

\omega\longrightarrow i\omega
, these become the discrete eigenvalues of the
R

operator, Eq. (
48
).
These poles, which arise from the Fourier transform of the partition
function, Eqs. (
46
) and (
82
),
effectively generate the density of states.
Equation (
92
)
is a restricted form (via the real part) of the analytic continuation of the
microcanonical counterpart of the familiar canonical energy
expansion of Eq. (
47
).

C.2
Regularization and semiclassical limit of the density of states

Another important property of Eq. (
92
) for our discussion below
is that it is divergent, thus requiring regularization.
This is a serious technical problem of the density of states of the operator
S
, unlike the original amplitude functions
(propagator and Green’s functions), which results from the trace operation applied to the energy Green’s function.
In effect, in Eq. (
92
), taking
\omega
to be real in this parametrization, the series
is divergent, in a manner similar to a harmonic series.
This should not be surprising because the operator
S
is not of trace class
[
111
]
.
Thus, all the expressions at the level of
\rho^{(S)}(E)
are purely formal, and they require
some form of regularization via a careful redefinition of the model.

Some hints of how regularization works for this problem can be revealed from the semiclassical approximation,
related to various aspects of the analysis of Sec.
IV.3
.
As a first step, by using the corresponding expression for the operator
R
, i.e., via
\hat{G}^{(R_{\rm Eucl})}(E)
,
the density of states is well-defined (as for a radial harmonic oscillator in the quantum-mechanical interpretation); this is easily verified by replacing
\omega\longrightarrow i\omega

in Eq. (
92
) and then taking the real part.
Now, the known classical limit of the density of states can be derived from integration in classical
phase space, and is

\displaystyle\overline{\rho}(E)=\frac{1}{\pi\hbar}\,\int_{q_{-}}^{q_{+}}\,\frac{dq}{\displaystyle\bigl|p(q;E)\!/M\bigr|}\;,

(93)

where
p(q;E)
is defined via Eq. (
40
),
and the integral is between the turning points
q_{\mp}
.
This is known as the Thomas-Fermi approximation, average, or Weyl term
in the density of states
[
84
,
85
]
;
and it is completely general within the semiclassical approximation.

In addition, for an operator
with classical periodic orbits of period
T(E)
, one can write

T(E)=2\int_{q_{-}}^{q_{+}}\,\frac{dq}{\displaystyle\bigl|p(q;E)\!/M\bigr|}\;,\;\;\text{so that}\;\;\;\overline{\rho}(E)=\frac{1}{2\pi\hbar}\,T(E)\;.

(94)

The final results for the operator
R
are thus obtained by straightforward integration, which gives

\displaystyle\overline{\rho}(E)=1/(2\hbar\omega)

and
T(E)=\pi/\omega
.
It is noteworthy that these expressions are identical to Eqs. (
41
)–(
42
),
which are central to our characterization of the thermal nature of causal diamonds.

On the other hand, for the operator
S
, which has a potential with an inverted harmonic oscillator term,
there is only one finite turning point (and the other limit is at infinity), and this causes a divergence
in the density of states and transit time.
Using these classical integral expressions,
an obvious regularization procedure is
real-space cutoff regularization
, i.e., for a particle confined in a box of length
L
.
Then, by direct integration in the classical limit of the operator
S
,
one gets a logarithmic leading order

\displaystyle\overline{\rho}^{(S)}(E)\approx\frac{1}{\pi\hbar\omega}\ln\left(\sqrt{\frac{2M}{|E|}}\,\omega L\right)\;,

(95)

which is the leading high-energy approximation, when
E\gg\hbar\omega
.
This regularization technique has been used for the pure inverted harmonic oscillator
[
104
]
.

Another powerful approach available for this problem, given the form of the series (
92
),
is a
digamma-function regularization
technique, which is usually stated as
the replacement via the formal identity
\sigma(z)\equiv\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}1/(n+z)=-\psi(z)
,
where
\psi(z)\equiv\psi^{(0)}(z)
is the
digamma (psi) function
[
102
,
112
]
. This procedure is based on the
more basic formal identity (Eq.
5.7.6
in Ref
[
112
]
):
\sigma(z)\equiv\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}1/(n+z)=-\psi(z)+C
,
where
C=-\gamma+\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}1/(n+1)
is an infinite constant
(including the finite Euler-Mascheroni constant
\gamma
), which can either be assumed to be removed by
regularization subtraction or interpreted via a physical regularization technique such as the cutoff procedure mentioned above.
Thus, Eq. (
92
) becomes

\displaystyle{\rho}^{(S)}(E)=-\frac{1}{2\pi\hbar}\,{\rm Re}\biggl(\frac{1}{\omega}\,\bigl[\psi\bigl((\mu+1-i\eta)/2\bigr)-C\bigr]\biggr)\;,

(96)

defined up to an energy-independent constant
C
.
While the details are beyond the scope of this work, these considerations give an idea of how to deal with the divergence
problem, and Eq. (
96
), properly interpreted, describes the physical density of states.
In a more detailed treatment, one can use asymptotic methods,
with a Dirichlet boundary condition at
q=L
, to further interpret and determine the leading behavior
as
L\rightarrow\infty
, for all energies
E
, and correspondingly adjust the path-integral propagators and Green’s functions
derived in Ref.
[
75
]
.
One simple check of the correctness of this procedure is to extract the asymptotic limit

\eta\gg 1
(i.e.
E\gg\hbar\omega
) using Stirling’s series for the digamma function
(Eq.
5.11.2
in Ref
[
112
]
):
\psi(z)\sim\ln z-1/(2z)
,
with
z=(1-i\zeta)/2\sim-i\eta/2
, whose real part in
Eq. (
96
)
yields

\displaystyle{\rho}^{(S)}(E)\sim\left[1/(2\pi\hbar\omega)\right]\ln(2/|\eta|)
,
which agrees with Eq. (
95
) and
fixes the ad-hoc constant via the cutoff-length
L
,
with
C=-\ln[\hbar/(M\omega L^{2})]
.

C.3
Density of states: Contribution from poles and semiclassical regime

Even though Eq. (
91
) is a formal expression that requires regularization,
additional information can also be gathered from examination of the integral
in the complex time
T
plane.
In effect, in terms of
z=\omega T
,
the integrand
F({z})
has poles on the imaginary axis,
located at
{z}_{k}=i\pi k
, where
k
is an integer,
with residues

R_{k}=(-1)^{k}e^{i\zeta{z}_{k}}=(-e^{-\pi\zeta})^{k}=(-e^{-i\pi\mu}e^{-\pi\eta})^{k}
.
These poles give a sort of resonant contribution to the density in the form of a series of terms,
with each one due to a conjugate point of the action in the path integral (in the stationary-phase evaluation
within the semiclassical method; see Sec.
C.4
).
For
E>0
, these terms can be extracted by performing the integral in the complex plane
with a counterclockwise contour comprised of the half real axis
L_{1}
(
{\rm Re}({z})\in[0,\infty)
),
the upper-half imaginary axis
{L}_{2}
(
{\rm Im}({z})\in[0,\infty)
)
traversed downward circumventing the poles,
and a circular path
C_{R}
of large radius
R\rightarrow\infty
connecting them.
As the integrand has no singularities in the first quadrant (excluding the boundary poles on the imaginary line),
and as
\int_{C_{R}}F({z})d{z}=0
in the limit
R\rightarrow\infty
, it follows that
the original integral (
91
) along the half real axis
L_{1}

can be computed with the integral
\int_{L_{2}}F({z})d{z}

(traversed backward up and including the poles along the path itself).
Thus, with
\alpha=1/\omega
, Eq. (
91
) gives

\rho^{(S)}(E)=\frac{\alpha}{2\pi\hbar}\;{\rm Re}\;\left[\pi i\left(\frac{R_{0}}{2}+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}R_{k}\right)\right]+f(E)=\frac{\alpha}{2\hbar}\;{\rm Im}\;\left(e^{\pi\zeta}+1\right)^{-1}+f(E)\;,

(97)

where each pole
{z}_{k}=i\pi k
,
with
k>0
, yields half the usual residue amount
(due to a circumventing half-circle), while
k=0
gives only a quarter of the usual amount.
It is noteworthy that, for
E<0
, a similar procedure involves a clockwise contour closed
with an infinite circular path in the lower-half plane; and

{L}_{2}
along the lower-half imaginary axis going through similar poles

{z}_{k}=i\pi k
, with

k=0,-1,-2,\ldots
:
the outcome is a reversal in the sign of the energy, i.e.,
this conforms to the even nature of the density of states that can be deduced from
Eq. (
91
).
The resulting value of the density of states, Eq. (
97
), for
E>0

C.4
Semiclassical framework and Gutzwiller trace formula

A general framework for the computation of the density of states—and more generally of all the functional integrals—is
available within the semiclassical approximation.
In the Gutzwiller approach
[

94
]

which applies to the class of Hamiltonians of the form
