Yosida duality
Bas Westerbaan

Abstract
In this note we prove Yosida duality — that is: the category of compact Hausdorff spaces with continuous maps is dually equivalent to the category of uniformly complete Archimedean Riesz spaces with distinguished units and unit-preserving Riesz homomorphisms between them.



For a compact Hausdorff space
X
, write
C(X)

for the vector space of real-valued continuous functions on it.
The space
C(X)
contains sufficient additional structure to reconstruct

X
from it. In fact, there are several ways to do it.

One particularly well-known method is using Gel’fand-duality:
it states that any commutative unital C
∗
-algebra
is isomorphic to the algebra of

complex valued
continuous functions on
some up-to-isomorphism unique compact Hausdorff spaces.
The categorical corollary is that the category
of unital C
∗
-algebras with unital
*
-homomorphisms
is dually equivalent to that of compact Hausdorff spaces.

The proof of Gel’fand’s Theorem is rather involved —
in this note we will present a lesser known method
due to Yosida
[
Yos42
]
which will lead to a different duality.
Gel’fand uses the product on
C(X)

to reconstruct
X
. Yosida instead uses the lattice order.
At the end of this note,
we will have shown that the category
of compact Hausdorff spaces
is dually equivalent
to the category of uniformly complete Archimedean unitary Riesz spaces.

The present proof is based
on a lecture series given by A. van Rooij in 2011
of which notes can be found here
[
vR11
]
.
The present text takes a short path to Yosida duality;
for a broad treatment of Riesz spaces,
see e.g.
[
vR11
,
DJR77
,
Zaa12
]
.

1
Riesz spaces

We will first define Riesz spaces and derive their elementary theory.

Definition 1
.

1.

A real
ordered vector space

E
is
a real vector space with a partial order
\leq
such
that for all
x,y\in E
with
x\leq y
we have

(a)

x+a\leq y+a
for any
a\in E
and

(b)

\lambda\cdot x\leq\lambda\cdot y
for all scalars
\lambda\geq 0
.

2.

A
Riesz space
is a real ordered vector space that is a lattice
(i.e. all finite infima and suprema exist.)

3.

A linear map
\varphi\colon E\to E^{\prime}
between Riesz spaces
is called a
Riesz homomorphism

if
\varphi
preserves finite infima and suprema.

Example 2
.

1.

For any topological space
X
,
the vector space
C(X)
of continuous real-valued functions on
X
is
a Riesz space.

2.

Let
\leq_{\mathrm{lex}}
be the lexicographic order on
\mathbbm{R}^{2}
—
that is

(x,y)\leq_{\mathrm{lex}}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\quad\iff\quad x<x^{\prime}\text{ or }(x=x^{\prime}\text{ and }y\leq y^{\prime}).

Then
\mathbbm{R}^{2}
ordered by
\leq_{\mathrm{lex}}

is a Riesz space.

In a Riesz space
the lattice operations interact nicely with
other structure:

Lemma 3
.

For a Riesz space
E
with elements
x,y,a\in E
it holds

1.

\lambda(x\vee y)=(\lambda x)\vee(\lambda y)

for scalars
\lambda\geq 0

2.

-(x\vee y)=(-x)\wedge(-y)

3.

(x+a)\vee(y+a)=(x\vee y)+a

4.

x\vee y+x\wedge y=x+y

5.

(x\wedge y)\vee a=(x\vee a)\wedge(y\vee a)

Proof.

The maps
x\mapsto\lambda x
and
x\mapsto x+a
are order isomorphisms
(if
\lambda>0
)
and these preserve suprema, hence 1 and 3.
Negation is an order anti-isomorphism and so 2.
Point 4 follows from the previous two:

(x\vee y)-x-y\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 3}}{{=}}(x-x-y)\vee(y-x-y)=(-y)\vee(-x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 2}}{{=}}-(x\wedge y).

In every lattice
(x\wedge y)\vee a\leq(x\vee a)\wedge(y\vee a)
.
For the other inequality, note

(x\vee a)\wedge(y\vee a)\leq x\vee a\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 4}}{{=}}x+a-x\wedge a\leq x+a-x\wedge y\wedge a

and so
(x\vee a)\wedge(y\vee a)+x\wedge y\wedge a-a\leq x
.
Similarly
(x\vee a)\wedge(y\vee a)+x\wedge y\wedge a-a\leq y
.
And so
(x\vee a)\wedge(y\vee a)+x\wedge y\wedge a-a\leq x\wedge y
.

(x\vee a)\wedge(y\vee a)\leq x\wedge y+a-x\wedge y\wedge a\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 4}}{{=}}(x\wedge y)\vee a.\qed

The lattice-order on a Riesz space gives quite some structure.

Definition 4
.

For any Riesz space
E
, define

1.

the
absolute value

|x|:=x\vee(-x)
for
x,y\in E
;

2.

the
positive cone

E^{+}:=\{e\in E;e\geq 0\}
;

3.

orthogonality

x\perp y:\Leftrightarrow|x|\wedge|y|=0

for
x,y\in E

and

4.

the
positive

x^{+}:=x\vee 0
and
negative parts

x^{-}:=(-x)\vee 0
.

Note that in
C(X)
the absolute value is point-wise;

C(X)^{+}
are the positive valued functions

and

f\perp g
iff
f
and
g
have disjoint support.

Lemma 5
.

For any Riesz space
E
with
x,y\in E
we have

1.

x=x^{+}-x^{-}

2.

|x|=x^{+}+x^{-}

3.

|x|\geq 0

4.

|x|=x^{+}\vee x^{-}

5.

x^{+}\perp x^{-}

6.

|\lambda x|=|\lambda||x|

7.

|x|=0\implies x=0

8.

(x+y)^{+}\leq x^{+}+y^{+}

9.

|x+y|\leq|x|+|y|

10.

x\vee y=\frac{x+y}{2}+\left|\frac{x-y}{2}\right|

Proof.

As
x=x+0=x\vee 0+x\wedge 0=x\vee 0-(-x)\vee 0=x^{+}-x^{-}
we have 1. So

x^{+}+x^{-}=2x^{+}-x=2(x\vee 0)-x=(2x)\vee 0-x=(2x-x)\vee(-x)=|x|

hence 2.
Clearly
0\leq 0\vee x=x^{+}
and similarly
0\leq x^{-}
.
So by the previous point
0\leq x^{+}+x^{-}=|x|

hence 3.
So
|x|=|x|\vee 0=x\vee(-x)\vee 0=(x\vee 0)\vee((-x)\vee 0)=x^{+}\vee x^{-}
, which is point 4.
Also
x^{+}+x^{-}=x^{+}\vee x^{-}+x^{+}\wedge x^{-}

thus we must have
x^{+}\wedge x^{-}=0
viz point 5.
For
\lambda\geq 0

we have
|\lambda||x|=\lambda(x\vee(-x))=(\lambda x)\vee(-\lambda x)=|\lambda x|

and as
|x|=|-x|

we establish 6.
Note
-|x|\leq x\leq|x|
and so
if
|x|=0
we have
x=0
i.e. point 7.
Always
x\leq x^{+}
.
So
x+y\leq x^{+}+y^{+}
.
Hence
(x+y)^{+}\leq(x^{+}+y^{+})^{+}=x^{+}+y^{+}
so 8.
Similarly
(x+y)^{-}\leq x^{-}+x^{-}
.
So 9 follows:
|x+y|\leq|x|+|y|
.
From Lemma
3
point 3 it follows

\begin{split}x+y+\left|x-y\right|&=x+y+(x-y)\vee(y-x)\\
&=(x+y+x-y)\vee(x+y+y-x)=2(x\vee y),\end{split}

which (together with pt. 2) shows pt. 10 and completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 6

(Riesz Decomposition Lemma)
.

Let
E
be any Riesz space.
For any positive
x,a,b\in E^{+}
with
x\leq a+b

there is a decomposition
x=a^{\prime}+b^{\prime}

with
0\leq a^{\prime}\leq a
and
0\leq b^{\prime}\leq b
.

Proof.

Define
a^{\prime}:=x\wedge a
and
b^{\prime}:=x-x\wedge a
.
Clearly
x=a^{\prime}+b^{\prime}
and
a^{\prime}\leq a
.
We have to show
b^{\prime}\leq b
.
Note
b^{\prime}=x-x\wedge a=x\vee a-a
.
By assumption
x\leq a+b

and so
x\vee a\leq(a+b)\vee a=a+b

hence
b^{\prime}\equiv x\vee a-a\leq b
.
∎

2
Riesz subspaces and ideals

To construct Riesz homomorphisms into
\mathbbm{R}

we study Riesz ideals.

Definition 7
.

Let
E
be a Riesz space. A subset
D\subseteq E
is called
a
Riesz ideal
if it is the kernel of some Riesz homomorphism.
The ideal
D
is said to be
proper

when
D\neq E
.
The set
D\subseteq E
is called a
Riesz subspace

if
x\vee y,x\wedge y\in D
whenever
x,y\in D
.

Lemma 8
.

A linear subspace
D\subseteq E
is a Riesz subspace
if and only if
x^{+}\in D
whenever
x\in D
.

Proof.

If
D
is a Riesz subspace, then clearly
x^{+}=x\vee 0\in D

whenever
x\in D
.
The converse follows
from
x\vee y=\frac{x+y}{2}+\left|\frac{x-y}{2}\right|

and
|x|=x^{+}+(-x)^{+}
.
∎

Lemma 9
.

Given a Riesz space
E
and a
D\subseteq E
, then
TFAE:

1.

D
is a Riesz ideal.

2.

D
is a linear subspace and for all
a\in D
and
x\in E
:

if
|x|\leq|a|
, then
x\in D
.

3.

D
is a Riesz subspace and for all
a\in D^{+}
and
x\in E^{+}
:

if
x\leq a
, then
x\in D
.

Proof.

1
\Rightarrow
2. Assume
D
is a Riesz ideal,
say it is the kernel of a Riesz homomorphism
f
.
Clearly
D
is a linear subspace.
Assume
|x|\leq|a|
for some
x\in E
and
a\in D
.
Note Riesz homomorphisms preserve absolute
values as
|x|\equiv x\vee(-x)
and
so
|f(x)|=f(|x|)\leq f(|a|)=|f(a)|=0

hence
f(x)=0
. Thus
x\in D
, as desired.

2
\Rightarrow
3. Assume
D
is a linear subspace
for which
x\in D
whenever
|x|\leq|a|
for some
a\in D
.
We only need to show
D
is a Riesz subspace.
Assume
a\in D
. We have to show
a^{+}\in D
.
Recall
a^{+}\leq a^{+}+a^{-}=|a|

and so
a^{+}\in D
, as desired.

3
\Rightarrow
1. Consider the linear quotient
q\colon E\to E/_{D}
.
We will turn
E/_{D}
into a Riesz space.
We want to use
q(E^{+})
as positive cone for a partial order on
E/_{D}
;
that is we define
q(x)\leq q(y)\iff q(y)-q(x)\in q(E^{+})
.
This turns
E/_{D}
into a ordered vector space
if
q(E^{+})
is closed under addition, scalar multiplication
by positive reals and if we
have
q(a),-q(a)\in q(E^{+})\Rightarrow q(a)=q(0)
.
Clearly
q(E^{+})
is closed under addition and scalar multiplication
by positive reals.
Note
q(a)\in q(E^{+})
iff there is
d\in D
with
d\leq a
.
To show the last condition, assume
q(a),-q(a)\in q(E^{+})
.
Then
d\leq a
and
d^{\prime}\leq-a
for some
d,d^{\prime}\in D
.
Hence
0\leq a-d\leq-d^{\prime}-d
.
By assumption on
D
we find
a-d\in D
.
Thus
d\in D
and
q(a)=q(0)
.
Indeed
q(E^{+})
is a positive cone and we can
order
E/_{D}
using it.
Automatically
q
is order preserving.

To show
E/_{D}
is a Riesz space
assume
q(x),q(y)\in E/_{D}
.
Clearly
q(x\wedge y)\leq q(x)

and
q(x\wedge y)\leq q(y)
.
Assume there is a
q(a)\in E/_{D}

with
q(a)\leq q(x)
and
q(a)\leq q(y)
.
Then
x-c\geq d

and
y-c\geq d^{\prime}
for some
d,d^{\prime}\in D
.
Consequently
x\wedge y-c=(x-c)\wedge(y-c)\geq d\wedge d^{\prime}
.
As
d\wedge d^{\prime}\in D
we see
q(c)\leq q(x\wedge y)
.
We have shown
E/_{D}
has pairwise infima.
As
x\mapsto-x
is an order anti-isomorpism
we see
E/_{D}
has pairwise suprema as well.
We also saw
q
is a Riesz homomorphism
and by construction
D
is its kernel.
∎

Lemma 10
.

For any
a\in E
the set
a^{\perp}:=\{x;\ x\in E;\ x\perp a\}

is a Riesz ideal.

Proof.

We will first prove that
a^{\perp}
is a linear subspace.
Let
x\in a^{\perp}

and
\lambda\in\mathbbm{R}
.
Assume
|\lambda|\leq 1
.
Then
0\leq|\lambda x|\wedge|a|=|\lambda||x|\wedge|a|\leq|x|\wedge|a|=0

and so
\lambda x\perp a
.
In the other case
|\lambda|\geq n
for some
n\in\mathbbm{N}
with
n\neq 0

and so
|\frac{\lambda}{n}|\leq 1
.
By the previous
|\frac{\lambda}{n}|\wedge|a|=0
.
Hence
0=|\lambda|\wedge(n|a|)\geq|\lambda|\wedge|a|
, as desired.

Let
x,y\in a^{\perp}
.
By distributivity
(|x|\vee|y|)\wedge|a|=(|x|\wedge|a|)\vee(|y|\wedge|a|)=0

so
|x|\vee|y|\perp a
.
By the previous
2(|x|\vee|y|)\perp a
.
Thus

|x+y|\wedge|a|\leq(|x|+|y|)\wedge|a|\leq(2(|x|\vee|y|))\wedge|a|=0.

Thus
a^{\perp}
is a linear subspace.
To show
a^{\perp}
is a Riesz ideal,
we will demonstrate condition 3 from Lemma
9
.
Assume
0\leq x\leq y\in a^{\perp}
.
Then
0\leq x\wedge|a|\leq y\wedge|a|=0

and so
x\in a^{\perp}
— it remains to be
shown that
a^{\perp}
is a Riesz subspace.
It is sufficient to show
x^{+}\in a^{\perp}
whenever
x\in a^{\perp}
.
Indeed from

0=|x|\wedge|a|=(x^{+}\vee x^{-})\wedge|a|=(x^{+}\wedge|a|)\vee(x^{-}\wedge|a|)

it follows
x^{+}\perp a
as desired.
∎

3
Units and their norms

Definition 11
.

1.

An element
u\in E^{+}
is called an (order)
unit

if for every
x\in E
there
exists a
n\in\mathbbm{N}
such that
|x|<n\cdot u
. A Riesz space is called

unitary
if it has a unit.

2.

An element
\varepsilon\in E
is called
infinitesimal
if there is
a
b\in E
such that
n\cdot\varepsilon\leq b
for all
n\in\mathbbm{Z}
.
A Riesz space is called
Archimedean
if its only infinitesimal is
0
.

Definition 12
.

Given an Riesz space
E
with unit
u\in E

write

\|x\|_{u}:=\inf\{\lambda;\ \lambda\in\mathbbm{R}^{+};\ |x|\leq\lambda u\}.

Lemma 13
.

Let
E
be a Riesz space with units
e,u
.

1.

\|\ \|_{u}
is a seminorm.

2.

If
\|x\|_{u}=0
, then
x
is infinitesimal.

3.

If
E
is Archimedean,
then
\|\ \|_{u}
is a norm
and
|x|\leq\|x\|_{u}u
.

4.

\|\ \|_{e}
and
\|\ \|_{u}
are equivalent.

Proof.

First we’ll show
\|\ \|_{u}
is a seminorm.
Let
\varepsilon>0
be given.
For any
x\in E
we have
|x|\leq(\|x\|_{u}+\varepsilon)u
.
So

|a+b|\leq|a|+|b|\leq(\|a\|_{u}+\|b\|_{u}+2\varepsilon)u
.
Hence
\|a+b\|_{u}\leq\|a\|_{u}+\|b\|_{u}+2\varepsilon
.
Thus, as
\varepsilon>0
was arbitrary, the triangle inequality
holds for
\|\ \|_{u}
.

Now we’ll show the absolute homogeneity.
Let
\mu>0
be given.
With
|\mu a|=|\mu||a|

and similar reasoning as before
we see
\|\mu a\|_{u}\leq|\mu|\|a\|_{u}
.
To prove the other inequality
first note that for any
\|a\|_{u}\geq\varepsilon>0

we have
|a|\nleq(\|a\|_{u}-\varepsilon)u
.
Thus
|\mu a|=|\mu||a|\nleq(|\mu|\|a\|_{u}-|\mu|\varepsilon)u
.
As we can make
|\mu|\varepsilon
arbitrarily small
we see
\|\mu a\|_{u}\geq|\mu|\|a\|_{u}
.
This gives absolute homogeneity except for the case
\mu=0
,
which is trivially true.
Finally, by definition
\|\ \|_{u}
is positive,
so it’s a seminorm.

Assume
\|x\|_{u}=0
. Then
|x|\leq\frac{1}{n}u
for any
n\in\mathbbm{N}

with
n>0
.
Hence
n|x|\leq u

Now, for any
m\in\mathbbm{Z}

we have
mx\leq|mx|=|m||x|\leq u

and so
x
is infinitesimal.

If
E
is Archimedean then directly by the previous
we see
\|\ \|_{u}
is a norm.
Now we show
|a|\leq\|a\|_{u}u
.
Suppose
n\in\mathbbm{N}
with
n\neq 0
.
Then
|a|\leq(\|a\|_{u}+\frac{1}{n})u=\|a\|_{u}u+\frac{1}{n}u
.
By taking the infimum over
n\in\mathbbm{N}_{>0}
,
we see it suffices to
show
\inf\{\frac{1}{n}u;\ n\in\mathbbm{N}_{>0}\}=0
.
Clearly
0
is a lower-bound.
Assume
b\in E
is another lower-bound,
i.e.
b\leq\frac{1}{n}u
for all
n\in\mathbbm{N}_{>0}
.
Then
b^{+}\leq(\frac{1}{n}u)^{+}=\frac{1}{n}u

so
nb^{+}\leq\frac{1}{n}u

and
-nb^{+}\leq 0\leq\frac{1}{n}u

for all
n\in\mathbbm{N}
.
Thus
b^{+}
is infinitesimal: so
b^{+}=0

and
b\leq 0
, as desired.

Finally we show point 4.
As for any
\varepsilon>0
we have
|x|\leq(\|x\|_{u}+\varepsilon)u

we get
\|x\|_{e}\leq(\|x\|_{u}+\varepsilon)\|u\|_{e}

and so
\|x\|_{e}\leq\|x\|_{u}\|u\|_{e}
.
Similarly
\|x\|_{u}\leq\|x\|_{e}\|e\|_{u}

and so
\frac{1}{\|e\|_{u}}\|x\|_{u}\leq\|x\|_{e}\leq\|u\|_{e}\|x\|_{u}
,
which shows
\|\ \|_{u}
and
\|\ \|_{e}
are equivalent norms.
∎

Lemma 14
.

Any non-zero Riesz homomorphism
f\colon E\to E^{\prime}

between unital Archimedean Riesz spaces
is continuous with respect to the norms
induced by any unit.

Proof.

Pick any unit
u\in E
and
u^{\prime}\in E^{\prime}
.
As all unit norms are equivalent
it is sufficient to show
f
is continuous
with respect to
\|\ \|_{u}
and
\|\ \|_{u^{\prime}}
.
If
\|f(u)\|_{u^{\prime}}=0
, then
f(u)=0

and so
f=0
as
-\|x\|_{u}u\leq x\leq\|x\|_{u}u

for any
x\in E
. Thus
\|f(u)\|_{u^{\prime}}>0
.
Let
\varepsilon>0
be given.
If
\|x-y\|_{u}\leq\delta

for some
\delta>0

then
|x-y|\leq\delta u

and so
|f(x)-f(y)|=f(|x-y|)\leq\delta f(u)=\delta|f(u)|\leq\delta\|f(u)\|_{u^{\prime}}u^{\prime}
.
Thus it is sufficient to
choose
\delta\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{\|f(u)\|_{u^{\prime}}}
.
∎

Definition 15
.

An Archimedean unitary Riesz space is called
uniformly complete

if
\|\ \|_{u}
is complete for some unit
u\in E
.

As order-unit norms are equivalent we know that
if
E
is complete with respect to one unit, then it is complete
with respect to all units.

Definition 16
.

We write
\mathsf{CAURiesz}
for the category of
uniformly complete
Archimedean Riesz spaces with distinguished unit as objects
and Riesz homomorphisms that preserve the selected unit as arrows between.

4
Maximal Riesz ideals

The real numbers form a totally ordered
Archimedean Riesz space and is, in fact, the only one.
This Proposition will play the same rôle
as the Banach–Mazur Theorem in the development
of Gel’fand Duality.

Proposition 17
.

If
E
is a Riesz space with more than one element, then
TFAE.

1.

E\cong\mathbbm{R}

2.

The only ideals of
E
are
\{0\}
and
E
.

3.

E
is totally ordered and Archimedean.

Proof.

1
\Rightarrow
2. Direct.

2
\Rightarrow
3. First we will prove
E
is totally ordered.
Assume
x,y\in E
. Then
J=\{v\in E;\ v\perp(x-y)^{+}\}
is
a Riesz ideal by Lemma
10
.
If
J=\{0\}
then since
(x-y)^{-}\perp(x-y)^{+}
, we know

(x-y)^{-}=0
and thus
0\leq x-y
. Hence
x\geq y
.
In the other case: if
J=E
, then
(x-y)^{+}\perp(x-y)^{+}
and thus
(x-y)^{+}=0
.
Hence
x-y=-(x-y)^{-}\leq 0
and thus
x\leq y
.

Now we will prove
E
is Archimedean.
Assume
\varepsilon
is an infinitesimal of
E
.
We will show
\varepsilon=0
.
Let
b\in E
such that
n|\varepsilon|\leq b
for all
n\in\mathbbm{N}
.
Note
J:=\{x\in E;\ n|x|\leq b\text{ for all }n\in\mathbbm{N}\}

is a Riesz ideal.
If
J=\{0\}
we are done.
In the other case
J=E
.
Then
b\in J
and
n|b|\leq b\leq n|b|
for all
n\neq 0
.
Thus
2|b|=b=|b|
so
|\varepsilon|\leq b=0
and we are done.

3
\Rightarrow
1. Pick any
a>0
.
The map
\lambda\mapsto\lambda a

is a linear order isomorphism of
\mathbbm{R}

onto a
1
-dimensional subspace of
E
.
We are done if this subspace is
E
itself.
Let
b\in E^{+}
.
Define
r:=\sup\{r;\ r\in\mathbbm{R}^{+};\ rb\leq a\}
.
As
E
is Archimedean,
there is a
n
with
nb\nleq a
.
Thus
nb\geq a
and so
r<\infty
.
For any
\varepsilon>0

we must have
(r+\varepsilon)b>a
as
E
is totally ordered.
Taking infimum over
\varepsilon>0
we see
a=rb

thus indeed
E
is 1-dimensional.
∎

An important corollary is the following:

Proposition 18
.

Given an Archimedean Riesz
space
E
with unit
e
.
For every
a\in E
,
a\neq 0
, there is a
Riesz homomorphism
\varphi\colon E\to\mathbbm{R}
such that
\varphi(e)=1

and
\varphi(a)\neq 0
.

Proof.

It is sufficient to find such
\varphi
for
|a|

as
0\neq\varphi(|a|)=|\varphi(a)|
implies
\varphi(a)\neq 0
.
Thus assume
a>0
.
As
E
is Archimedean, there is an
n\in\mathbbm{N}

with
na\nleq e
.
That is
na-e\nleq 0
. But
na-e\leq(na-e)^{+}
,
so
(na-e)^{+}\neq 0
.

Consider the proper Riesz ideal
J:=\{x;\ x\perp(na-e)^{+}\}
.
Find using Zorn’s Lemma a maximal proper Riesz ideal
J_{0}\supseteq J
.
Then
E/_{J_{0}}
contains precisely two ideals and must be isomorphic to
\mathbbm{R}
.
Define
\varphi_{0}
as the composition of this isomorphism with
the quotient-map
E\to E/_{J_{0}}\cong\mathbbm{R}
.
Note
\varphi_{0}(e)>0

as otherwise
\varphi_{0}=0
.
Define
\varphi:=\frac{\varphi_{0}}{\varphi_{0}(e)}
.
Clearly
\varphi(e)=1
.

Note
(na-e)^{-}\in J
and so
0=\varphi((na-e)^{-})=(n\varphi(a)-\varphi(e))^{-}

which implies
n\varphi(a)-\varphi(e)=(n\varphi(a)-\varphi(a))^{+}\geq 0
.
Thus
n\varphi(a)\geq\varphi(e)>0

and so
\varphi(a)\neq 0
.
∎

5
Function spaces

Example 19
.

Let
X
be a compact Hausdorff space. Then
C(X)
with the
obvious pointwise operations and order, is a Riesz space. Since
X
is
compact, all these functions are bounded,

\mathbbm{1}(x):=1
is a unit and

\|\ \|_{\mathbbm{1}}
is a supnorm for which
C(X)
is complete.
It is also obvious that the zero function is the only
infinitesimal. Thus
C(X)
is Archimedean.
Thus
C(X)
with unit
\mathbbm{1}
is an object of
\mathsf{CAURiesz}
.

Let
X
be a compact Hausdorff space
and
x\in X
.
Then
point evaluation

\delta_{x}\colon C(X)\to\mathbbm{R}
defined by
\delta_{x}(f)=f(x)

is a Riesz homomorphism into
\mathbbm{R}

with
\delta_{x}(\mathbbm{1})=1
.
Every unit-preserving Riesz homomorphism
C(X)\to\mathbbm{R}

is of this form:

Proposition 20
.

Let
X
be a compact Hausdorff space.
For any Riesz homomorphism
\varphi\colon C(X)\to\mathbbm{R}

with
\varphi(\mathbbm{1})=1

there is a point
x\in X

with
\varphi=\delta_{x}
.

Proof.

For any
f\in C(X)

define
\tilde{f}:=|f-\varphi(f)\mathbbm{1}|
.
If we can find an
x_{0}\in X

with
x_{0}\notin\mathop{\mathrm{supp}}\tilde{f}:=\{x;\ \tilde{f}(x)\neq 0\}

for all
f\in C(X)
,
then we are done
as
\tilde{f}(x_{0})=0

implies
\varphi(f)=f(x_{0})=\delta_{x_{0}}(f)
.
Note that by definition
\tilde{f}\geq 0

and
\varphi(\tilde{f})=0
.
For a single
f\in C(X)
there must be an
x\in X

with
x\notin\mathop{\mathrm{supp}}\tilde{f}
(i.e.
\tilde{f}(x)=0
)
for otherwise
\tilde{f}>0
and
so by compactness
\tilde{f}>\varepsilon\mathbbm{1}

for some
\varepsilon>0

which gives
0=\varphi(\tilde{f})>\varepsilon
quod non.
Now, let
f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}\in C(X)
be given.
Write
g:=\tilde{f}_{1}\vee\ldots\vee\tilde{f}_{n}
.
Note
\varphi(g)=0

and so there must also be an
x\in X
with
g(x)=0
.
As
\mathop{\mathrm{supp}}g=\mathop{\mathrm{supp}}\tilde{f}_{1}\cup\ldots\cup\mathop{\mathrm{supp}}\tilde{f}_{n}

we see
x\notin\mathop{\mathrm{supp}}\tilde{f}_{i}
for every
1\leq i\leq n
.
Thus no finite subset of the
family of open sets
\mathcal{A}:=\{\mathop{\mathrm{supp}}\tilde{f};\ f\in C(X)\}
can
cover
X
. Thus by contraposition of compactness we
see that
\cup\mathcal{A}
cannot cover
X
.
Hence there is an
x_{0}\in X
with
x_{0}\notin\mathop{\mathrm{supp}}\tilde{f}
for
any
f\in C(X)
as desired.
∎

This Proposition is a cornerstone of the duality. Before
we will look into that, we will proof a variant
of the Stone-Weierstrass theorem in the language of Riesz spaces.

Theorem 21

(Stone-Weierstrass)
.

For any compact Hausdorff space
X
and Riesz subspace
D

of
C(X)
such that:

1.

D
is unital — that is:
\mathbbm{1}\in D

2.

D
separates the points — that is:
for all
x,y\in X
with
x\neq y

there is an
f\in D
with
f(x)\neq f(y)
.

Then:
D
is
\|\ \|_{\mathbbm{1}}
-dense in
C(X)
.

Proof.

Let
g\in C(X)
and
\varepsilon>0
be given.
We will find a
g_{0}\in D

with
\|g-g_{0}\|_{\mathbbm{1}}\leq\varepsilon
.
By approximating
g^{+}
and
-g^{-}
separately
we see we may assume without loss of generality
g\geq 0
.
We will approximate
g
progressively in three steps.

Let
y,z\in X
be any points with
y\neq z
.
By assumption there is an
f\in D
with
f(z)\neq f(y)
.
Define
f_{y}:=\frac{g(z)}{f(z)-f(y)}(f-f(y)\mathbbm{1})
.
Note
f_{y}(y)=0
,
f_{y}(z)=g(z)
and still
f_{y}\in D
.

Write
U_{f_{y}}:=\{x;\ x\in X;\ f_{y}(x)<g(x)+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\}
.
Clearly both
z,y\in U_{f_{y}}
.
Thus the
U_{f_{y}}
are an open cover of
X
.
By compactness we can find
n\in\mathbbm{N}
and
y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}

such that
U_{y_{1}}\cup\cdots\cup U_{y_{n}}=X
.
Define
f^{\prime}_{z}:=f_{y_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge f_{y_{n}}
.
The map
f^{\prime}_{z}\in D
is a better approximation:

f^{\prime}_{z}(z)=g(z)

and
f^{\prime}_{z}\leq g+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\mathbbm{1}
.

Write
V_{f^{\prime}_{z}}:=\{x;\ g(x)-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}<f^{\prime}_{z}(x)\}
,
which are also open sets with
z\in V_{f^{\prime}_{z}}
.
By compactness we can find
m\in\mathbbm{N}

and
z_{1},\ldots,z_{m}

with
V_{f^{\prime}_{z_{1}}}\cup\cdots\cup V_{f^{\prime}_{z_{m}}}=X
.
Now define
g_{0}:=f^{\prime}_{z_{1}}\vee\cdots\vee f^{\prime}_{z_{m}}
.
Note
g-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\mathbbm{1}\leq g_{0}\leq g+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\mathbbm{1}

and so
\|g_{0}-g\|_{\mathbbm{1}}\leq\varepsilon
.
∎

6
The spectrum

We can turn a compact Hausdorff space
X

into an Archimedean uniformly complete
Riesz space
C(X)
— how do we go the other way?

Definition 22
.

Let
E
be an Archimedean Riesz space with a unit
u
.
The
spectrum
of
E
is defined to be
the set

\Phi(E):=\{\varphi;\ \varphi\colon E\to\mathbbm{R}\ \text{Riesz homomorphism with }\varphi(u)=1\}.

which is a topological space with induced
topology of the inclusion
\Phi(E)\subseteq\mathbbm{R}^{E}

where we take the product topology on the latter.

At first glance, the spectrum seems to depend on the choice of unit. However:

Claim 23
.

Let
E
be an Archimedean Riesz space with units
e,u\in E
.
We will write
\Phi_{e}(E)
and
\Phi_{u}(E)
for the spectra
respective to
e
and
u
.

Then:
\Phi_{e}(E)\cong\Phi_{u}(E)

via
\varphi\mapsto(x\mapsto\frac{\varphi(x)}{\varphi(u)})
.

Lemma 24
.

The spectrum
\Phi(E)
is a compact Hausdorff space.

Proof.

As for any
x\in E

it holds
-\|x\|_{u}\leq\varphi(x)\leq\|x\|_{u}

we have the following inclusions
of topological spaces

\Phi(E)\ \subseteq\ \prod_{x\in E}[-\|x\|_{u},\|x\|_{u}]\ \subseteq\ \mathbbm{R}^{E}.

The middle one is compact by Tychonoff’s Theorem theorem.
Thus if we can show
\Phi(E)

is closed in
\mathbbm{R}^{E}
, we know
\Phi(E)
is compact and Hausdorff.

Let
(\varphi_{\alpha})_{\alpha}
in
\Phi(E)
be a net
converging to some
\varphi
in
\mathbbm{R}^{E}
.
As addition, infimum, supremum and scalar multiplication
are continuous the map
\varphi
must be a Riesz homorphism as well.
Trivially
\varphi(u)=\lim_{\alpha}\varphi_{\alpha}(u)=1
.
Thus indeed
\Phi(E)
is closed in
\mathbbm{R}^{E}
as desired.
∎

7
Yosida duality

We are almost ready to state and prove Yosida duality.
First a notational matter:
recall the objects of
\mathsf{CAURiesz}

are pairs
(E,u)
of a uniformly complete Archimedean Riesz space
E

together with a distinguished unit
u\in E
.
For brevity we’ll simply write
E
and denote the implicit
distinguished unit by
u_{E}
.

Theorem 25

(Yosida)
.

C
and
\Phi
are categorically dual:

1.

The map
X\mapsto C(X)

extends to a functor
\mathsf{CHsdf}\to\mathsf{CAURiesz}^{\partial}

taking
\mathbbm{1}
as unit on
C(X)

and sending a continuous map
f\colon X\to Y

to the Riesz homomorphism
C(f)\colon C(Y)\to C(X)

given by
C(f)(g)=g\mathbin{\circ}f
.

2.

The map
E\mapsto\Phi(E)
extends to a functor
\mathsf{CAURiesz}\to\mathsf{CHsdf}^{\partial}

by mapping a Riesz homomorphism
f\colon E\to E^{\prime}

to
\Phi(f)\colon\Phi(E^{\prime})\to\Phi(E)

given by
\Phi(f)(\varphi)=\varphi\mathbin{\circ}f
.

3.

We have
C\Phi\cong\mathrm{id}
(every uniformly complete Archimedean Riesz
space is naturally isomorphic to a continuous
function space) and
\Phi C\cong\mathrm{id}

(every compact Hausdorff space is naturally isomorphic to
the spectrum of an Archimedean Riesz space)
and so
\mathsf{CHsdf}
and
\mathsf{CAURiesz}

are dually equivalent.

Proof.

We first show 1 and 2.

1.

We already saw
C(X)
is in
\mathsf{CAURiesz}

(Example
19

and that
\Phi(E)
is in
\mathsf{CHsdf}

(Lemma
24
).
Maps on arrows defined by
pre-composition (such as
C
and
\Phi
)
are automatically functorial.

2.

Clearly
C(f)(g)\equiv g\mathbin{\circ}f
is continuous
as it is the composition of two continuous maps.
To finish 1, we still have to check
C(f)
is a Riesz
homomorphism.
It is additive:
C(f)(g+g^{\prime})(x)=(g+g^{\prime})\mathbin{\circ}f(x)=g(f(x))+g^{\prime}(f(x))=C(f)(g)(x)+C(f)(g)(x)
.
In a similar fashion one checks
C(f)

preserves scalar multiplication,
\vee
and
\wedge
.
Unit-preservation is different, but
also simple:
C(f)(\mathbbm{1})(x)=\mathbbm{1}\mathbin{\circ}f(x)=1=\mathbbm{1}(x)
.

3.

To wrap up 2 we need to check
\Phi(f)
is a continuous map
for all
f\colon E\to E^{\prime}
.
Let
(\varphi_{\alpha})_{\alpha}
be a net in
\Phi(E^{\prime})

converging to
\varphi
.
In particular
we have for any
x\in E
that
\varphi_{\alpha}(f(x))\to\varphi(f(x))
.
Hence
\Phi(f)(\varphi_{\alpha})\equiv\varphi_{\alpha}\mathbin{\circ}f\to\varphi\mathbin{\circ}f\equiv\Phi(f)(\varphi)

in
\Phi(E)
, as desired.

Now we turn to the proof of
C\Phi\cong\mathrm{id}
.
Let
E
be in
CAURiesz
.

4.

First we define what will turn out to be an isomorphism.
For
x\in E
, define
\hat{x}
(initially in
\mathbbm{R}^{\Phi(E)}
)
by
\hat{x}(\varphi):=\varphi(x)
.
To prove
\hat{x}
is continuous,
assume
\varphi_{\alpha}\to\varphi
is a converging net
in
\Phi(E)
. Then
\hat{x}(\varphi_{\alpha})=\varphi_{\alpha}(x)\rightarrow\varphi(x)=\hat{x}(\varphi)
, thus
\hat{x}\in C\Phi(E)
.
For the moment, write
\hat{E}:=\{\hat{x};\ x\in E\}
.

5.

Note that
(\hat{x}+\hat{y})(\varphi)=\varphi(x+y)=\varphi(x)+\varphi(y)=\hat{x}(\varphi)+\hat{y}(\varphi)

and thus
\hat{x}+\hat{y}=\widehat{x+y}\in\hat{E}
.
The same reasoning applies to
\cdot
,
\wedge
and
\vee
.
Thus
x\mapsto\hat{x}
is a Riesz-homomorphism
and
\hat{E}
, being its image, is
a Riesz subspace of
C\Phi(E)
.

6.

Suppose
x\in E
and
x\neq 0
.
Then by Proposition
18
, there is a
\varphi\in\Phi(E)

such that
\varphi(x)\neq 0
. Thus
\hat{x}\neq 0
.
Thus
x\mapsto\hat{x}

is injective. Consequently
E\cong\hat{E}
.

7.

Given
\varphi,\psi\in\Phi(E)
with
\varphi\neq\psi
.
By definition there is an
x\in E

such that
\varphi(x)\neq\psi(x)
.
Together with
\hat{u}_{E}(\varphi)=\varphi(u_{E})=\mathbbm{1}
,
we conclude with the theorem of Stone-Weierstrass
(see Thm.
21
) that
\hat{E}

is
\|\ \|_{\mathbbm{1}}
-dense in
C\Phi(E)
. Since
E\cong\hat{E}
is
uniformly complete (i.e.
\|\ \|_{\mathbbm{1}}
-complete)
we find
C\Phi(E)\cong E
.

8.

We finish with naturality.
Assume
f\colon E\to E^{\prime}
in
\mathsf{CAURiesz}
,
x\in E

and
\varphi\in\Phi(E^{\prime})
.
Expanding definitions we find
C\Phi(f)(\hat{x})(\varphi)=\hat{x}(\Phi(f)(\varphi))=\hat{x}(\varphi\circ f)=\varphi\circ f(x)=\widehat{f(x)}(\varphi)
.
Thus
C\Phi\cong\mathrm{id}
.

Now we prove the other direction:
\Phi C\cong\mathrm{id}
.
Let
X\in\textsf{CHsdf}
be given.

9.

Recall
\delta_{x}\in\Phi C(X)
defined by
\delta_{x}(f)\equiv f(x)
.
Write
\Delta(X):=\{\delta_{x};\ x\in X\}
.

10.

Suppose
x_{\alpha}\rightarrow x
in
X
.
Then for
f\in C(X)
we have
\delta_{x_{\alpha}}(f)=f(x_{\alpha})\rightarrow f(x)=\delta_{x}(f)
. Thus
x\mapsto\delta_{x}
is continuous.

11.

Let
x,y\in X
with
x\neq y
be given.
By Urysohn’s Lemma there is an
f\in C(X)
such
that
f(x)\neq f(y)
. Hence
\delta_{x}\neq\delta_{y}
.
Thus
x\mapsto\delta_{x}
is injective.

12.

Assume
\varphi\in\Phi C(X)
. By Proposition
20

there is an
x\in X
such that
\varphi=\delta_{x}
.
Thus the map
x\mapsto\delta_{x}
is
surjective.
Together with the previous two points
we see
x\mapsto\delta_{x}
is a continuous bijection
between compact Hausdorff spaces and thus a homeomorphism.

13.

The demonstration of naturality is the same as in the previous Proposition.
Thus
\Phi C\cong\mathrm{id}
as desired.
∎
