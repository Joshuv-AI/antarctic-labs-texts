Abstract
. Field-Programmable Gate Arrays (FPGAs) have asserted themselves as vital assets in contemporary computing by offering adaptable, reconfigurable hardware platforms. FPGA-based accelerators incubate opportunities for breakthroughs in areas, such as real-time data processing, machine learning or cryptography—to mention just a few. The procedure of placement—determining the optimal spatial arrangement of functional blocks on an FPGA to minimize communication delays and enhance performance—is an NP-hard problem, notably requiring sophisticated algorithms for proficient solutions. Clearly, improving the placement leads to a decreased resource utilization during the implementation phase. Adiabatic quantum computing (AQC), with its capability to traverse expansive solution spaces, has potential for addressing such combinatorial problems. In this paper, we re-formulate the placement problem as a series of so called quadratic unconstrained binary optimization ( Qubo ) problems which are subsequently solved via AQC. Our novel formulation facilitates a straight-forward integration of design constraints. Moreover, the size of the sub-problems can be conveniently adapted to the available hardware capabilities. Beside the sole proposal of a novel method, we ask whether contemporary quantum hardware is resilient enough to find placements for real-world-sized FPGAs. A numerical evaluation on a D-Wave Advantage 5.4 quantum annealer suggests that the answer is in the affirmative.

1.
Introduction

Logic optimization, placing and routing are fundamental and the most time-consuming steps in the field of chip design for both ASICs and FPGAs
(
Rajavel and Akoglu 2011
)
.
The number of transistors and logic gates on a single chip is increasing more and more, leading to the mentioned processes consuming more and more time.
This limits the speed of development cycles, which is an issue of productivity but can also be a security issue,
since faster development cycles for cryptography related algorithms allow for improved security analysis.

Here, we focus on the placement step, in which we aim to find an ideal placement of functional blocks on the chip.
The advantages of a good placement are two-fold:
On the one hand, minimizing the physical distance between connected elements leads to shorter wire lengths and therefore a higher maximum clock rate.
On the other hand, a good placement can lead to a faster routing process, i.e., the routing algorithm of the connections between elements finding a good solution in fewer iterations and less time.
Since the placement itself is an increasingly time-consuming step, decreasing the runtime of the placement algorithm while maintaining a high solution quality is of great interest.

Taking a closer look at the math behind the placement in the floor-planning case,
we find that it is equivalent to the quadratic assignment problem (
QAP
)
(
Anjos and Liers 2012
;
Feld 2017a
)
.
The goal of the
QAP
is to assign each given element to a unique location, minimizing a given cost function.
In chip design, that cost function can be the total wire length between connected units given by a placement on the chip.
Minimizing that function leads to a shorter maximum wire length and with that the possibility for a higher maximum clock rate.
The
QAP
is an NP-hard combinatorial optimization problem, and moreover, one of the hardest in this class, since there is no approximation algorithm for producing a sub-optimal solution with guarantees in polynomial time
(
Sahni and Gonzalez 1976
)
.
Quantum computing (QC), especially adiabatic quantum computing (AQC)
(
Albash and Lidar 2018
;
Farhi et al. 2000
)
and its physical implementation of quantum annealing (QA), is very promising in solving such problems better than classical

“classical”

“not quantum”
.

This can be done by reformulating the given

to a quadratic unconstrained binary optimization (

While real-world quantum devices suffer from a series of technical limitations, there is theoretical evidence that hard combinatorial problems can be solved exactly via AQC.
We leverage these theoretical insights and describe the first algorithm for placement of functional blocks (e.g., Lookup Tables (LUT), BlockRAMs (BRAM), Digital Signal Processing (DSP)) on an FPGA, based on solving a

Our contributions can be summarized as follows:

The QAP is a traditional combinatorial optimization problem
(
Koopmans and Beckmann 1957
)
,
(

It is NP-hard and no classical algorithms are known which can approximate a solution with quality guarantees in polynomial time.
The current state-of-the-art methods for solving the floor planning problem/QAP in

The analytic approach compromises this quality-speed trade-off, by being very fast and showing similar performances to the SA approach.

3.2.
Practical Quantum Computing

Let us quickly introduce the basic notion of what can be considered as quantum computation
(
Nielsen and Chuang 2010
)
.
Today, practical QC consists of two dominant paradigms: AQC and gate-based QC. In both scenarios, a quantum state
\ket{\psi}
for a system with
n
qubits is a
2^{n}
dimensional complex
vector. In the gate-based framework, a quantum computation is defined as a matrix multiplication
\ket{\bm{\psi}_{\text{out}}}=C\ket{\bm{\psi}_{\text{in}}}
, where the
C
is a
(2^{n}\times 2^{n})
-dimensional unitary matrix (the circuit), given via a
series of inner and outer products of low-dimensional unitary matrices (the gates). In AQC—the
framework that we consider in the paper at hand—the result of computation is defined to be the eigenvector
\ket{\phi_{\min}}
that corresponds to the smallest eigenvalue of some
(2^{n}\times 2^{n})
-dimensional Hermitian matrix
\bm{H}
. In
practical AQC,
\bm{H}
is further restricted to be a real diagonal matrix whose entries can be
written as
\bm{H}_{i,i}=\bm{H}(\bm{Q})_{i,i}=\bm{\bm{x}^{i\top}\bm{Q}\bm{x}^{i}}
where
\bm{x}^{i}=\operatorname{binary}(i)\in\{0,1\}^{n}
is some (arbitrary but
fixed)
n
-bit binary expansion of the unsigned integer
i
. Here,
\bm{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is the so-called
Qubo
matrix. By construction, computing
\ket{\bm{\psi}_{\text{out}}}
is equivalent to solving
\min_{\bm{x}}\bm{x}^{\top}\bm{Q}\bm{x}
. Adiabatic quantum algorithms rely on this construction by encoding (sub-)problems as QUBO matrices.

In both paradigms, the output vector is
2^{n}
-dimensional and can thus not be read-out efficiently for non-small
n
. Instead, the output of a practical quantum computation is a random integer
i
between 1 and
2^{n}
, which is drawn from the probability mass function
\operatorname{Prob}(i)=|\braket{i|\bm{\psi}_{\text{out}}}|^{2}=|\ket{\bm{\psi}_{\text{out}}}_{i}|^{2}
. This sampling step is also known as collapsing the quantum state
\ket{\bm{\psi}_{\text{out}}}
to a classical binary state
\operatorname{binary}(i)
.

AQC has been applied to numerous hard combinatorial optimization problems
(
Lucas 2014
)
, ranging over satisfiability
(
Kochenberger et al. 2005
)
, routing problems
(
Neukart et al. 2017
)
to machine learning
(
Bauckhage et al. 2019
)
.

3.3.
FPGA-Placement

It is well known that the
Placement
problem can be formulated as an unbalanced
QAP
. We now recap this formulation, since our construction in Sec.
4
relies on it to transform the
Placement
problem into a series of
Qubo
problems.

Definition 0
.

Given are a set of facilities
\mathcal{F}=\{p_{1},\dots,p_{n}\}
and a set of locations
\mathcal{L}=\{l_{1},\dots,l_{n}\}
,
along with a flow function
f:\mathcal{F}\times\mathcal{F}\to\mathbb{R}
between facilities and a distance function
d:\mathcal{L}\times\mathcal{L}\to\mathbb{R}
between locations.
We define the flow and distance matrices as

\bm{F}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\left(f(p_{i},p_{j})\right)_{i,j=1}^{n},\quad\bm{D}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\left(d(l_{i},l_{j})\right)_{i,j=1}^{n}\;.

We formulate the quadratic assignment problem (
QAP
) as

\argmin_{\pi}\ \sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\bm{F}_{i,j}\bm{D}_{\pi(i),\pi(j)}\;,

where
\pi:[n]\to[n]
is a permutation on
[n]\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\{1,\dots,n\}
.
An equivalent formulation is given by the corresponding permutation matrices

\argmin_{\bm{P}\in\mathbb{P}_{n}}\ \tr\left(\bm{F}\bm{P}\bm{D}\bm{P}^{\top}\right)\;.

For given flow and distance matrices
\bm{F}
and
\bm{D}
, we define the cost function as

c\left(\bm{A},\bm{B}\right)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\tr\left(\bm{F}\bm{A}\bm{D}\bm{B}^{\top}\right),\quad c\left(\bm{A}\right)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=c\left(\bm{A},\bm{A}\right)\;.

In
FPGA
-
Placement
the goal is to place
m
functional blocks into
n
physical slots on the
FPGA
chip grid such that the total wire length is minimized, with
m\leq n
.
The
\bm{F}
indicates how two functional blocks are connected in the given net list and the distance matrix
\bm{D}
indicates the distances between different locations on the chip.
However, in the
QAP
framework we need the two matrices to have the same dimensionality.

Definition 0
.

Given two sets of indices
\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J}\subset[n]
with
\left|\mathcal{I}\right|=k
,
\left|\mathcal{J}\right|=l
, a matrix
\bm{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
and a vector
\bm{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
We denote the sub-matrix consisting only of the rows indexed by
\mathcal{I}
and the columns indexed by
\mathcal{J}
as
\bm{A}_{\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J}}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times l}
.
Similarly we denote the sub-vector of
\bm{a}
consisting only of the entries index by
\mathcal{I}
as
\bm{a}_{\mathcal{I}}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
.

Using this definition, we can formalize the
Placement
problem as a
QAP
by introducing a new matrix
\bm{F}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
,
which is
0
everywhere except for its
m\times m
upper block matrix, i.e.,
{\bm{F}^{\prime}_{[m],[m]}=\bm{F}}
.
Descriptively, we introduce
n-m
“dummy” functional blocks which are not connected to any other unit.
The placement objective can now be written as

(1)

\argmin_{\bm{P}\in\mathbb{P}_{n}}\ \tr\left(\bm{F}^{\prime}\bm{P}\bm{D}\bm{P}^{\top}\right)\;.

Even though it is a common way for obtaining a
QAP
formulation, inserting
n-m
dummy elements leads to a large amount of redundancy and high dimensionality, especially if
m\ll n
.
We can overcome this issue by considering sub-permutations.

Definition 0
.

With
m,n\in\mathbb{N},m\leq n

we define a map
\pi_{m,n}:[m]\to[n]
to be a sub-permutation if it is injective.

\pi_{m,n}
can also be described by a binary sub-permutation matrix, whose rows sum to
1
and whose columns contain at most a single
1
.
The space of sub-permutation matrices of dimension
m\times n
can hence be defined as

\mathbb{P}_{m,n}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\{\bm{X}\in\{0,1\}^{m\times n}\mid\bm{X}\bm{1}_{n}=\bm{1}_{m},~\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{1}_{m}\leq\bm{1}_{n}\}\;,

where the relation
\leq
is to be understood component wise.

With this definition we can rewrite the objective in
Eq.

1

(2)

\argmin_{\bm{P}\in\mathbb{P}_{m,n}}\ \tr\left(\bm{F}\bm{P}\bm{D}\bm{P}^{\top}\right)\;.

For
m<n
, the formulation in
Eq.

2
is also called unbalanced
QAP
.

4.
Method

We describe a first quantum-compatible problem formulation in

4.1.
QUBO Formulation

To obtain a
Qubo
formulation, we can leverage the optimization over all sub-permutation matrices in
Eq.

2
.

Lemma 0
.

The
Qubo
formulation

(3)

\argmin_{\bm{x}\in\{0,1\}^{mn},\bm{s}\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\ \ \bm{x}^{\top}\bm{Q}\bm{x}+\lambda\left\|\bm{A}\bm{x}-\bm{1}_{m}\right\|^{2}+\mu\left\|\bm{B}\bm{x}-\bm{s}\right\|^{2}\;,

where
\bm{Q}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\bm{F}\otimes\bm{D}
,
\bm{A}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\bm{I}_{m}\otimes\bm{1}_{n}^{\top}
and
\bm{B}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\bm{1}_{m}^{\top}\otimes\bm{I}_{n}
,
is equivalent to
Eq.

2
for sufficiently large penalty parameters
\lambda,\mu\in\mathbb{R}_{+}

Proof.

The objective in (
2
) can be written as

\displaystyle\argmin_{\bm{X}\in\{0,1\}^{m\times n}}\ \

\displaystyle c\left(\bm{X}\right)

subject to

\displaystyle\bm{X}\bm{1}_{n}=\bm{1}_{m},~\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{1}_{m}\leq\bm{1}_{n}\;.

Using
\bm{x}=\vect\left(\bm{X}\right)
this can be reformulated to

\displaystyle\argmin_{\bm{x}\in\{0,1\}^{n^{2}}}\ \

\displaystyle\bm{x}^{\top}\bm{Q}\bm{x}

subject to

\displaystyle\bm{A}\bm{x}=\bm{1}_{m},~\bm{B}\bm{x}=\bm{s},\ \bm{s}\in\{0,1\}^{n}\;.

With

\bm{A}\bm{x}=\bm{1}_{m},~\bm{B}\bm{x}=\bm{s}\Leftrightarrow\left\|\bm{A}\bm{x}-\bm{1}_{m}\right\|^{2}=0,~\left\|\bm{B}\bm{x}-\bm{s}\right\|^{2}=0\;,

we can incorporate the constraints into our objective with using penalty parameters, ending up with
Eq.

3
.
∎

Even though we have a quantum-compatible problem formulation for the
QAP
at hand, we remark that our problem dimension is
mn+n=n(m+1)
.
That is, for solving an
FPGA
-placement problem with
m
functional blocks and
n
grid locations, we need
n(m+1)
qubits, which is beyond capabilities of current (and upcoming) quantum hardware.
Furthermore, choosing the penalty parameters
\lambda,\mu
maintaining the equivalence between
Eq.

1
and
Eq.

3
while also having preferable conditioning for quantum hardware is tedious and error prone.
In
(
Benkner et al. 2020
)
, coarse upper bounds are provided for these parameters.
Furthermore, constraints on the permutation space can not easily be integrated into such a formulation.
However, this is of great importance in
FPGA
-
Placement
since we have to take into account that the types of every functional block and the corresponding placement location have to match.
For example, it is impossible to place a LUT onto an IO location (cf.
Fig.

4.2.
Cyclic Expansion

The above issues can be overcome by not considering a single
Qubo
formulation but a series of
Qubo
s.
We follow the approach from
(
Benkner et al. 2021
)
and use a variant of the
\alpha
-expansion algorithm
(
Boykov et al. 2001
)
, which we will henceforth denote as
CyclicExpansion
.
This reduces the dimensionality of the
Qubo
problem and removes the penalty terms in
Eq.

3
.
Furthermore, constraints on the allowed sub-permutations can be incorporated easily into this algorithm.
The idea is that instead of optimizing over all permutation matrices at once, an iterative optimization over cyclic permutations is carried out which converges towards the original optimization.
For the upcoming sections we assume that
m=n
, the case
m<n
follows analogously.

Informally, the cyclic expansion algorithm works as follows:

(1)

Initialize permutation matrix
\bm{P}\in\mathbb{P}_{n}
,

(2)

Choose a set of simpler permutation matrices
\mathbb{C}\subset\mathbb{P}_{n}
with
{|\mathbb{C}|=k<n}
,

(3)

Solve a QUBO of size
k
to decide which permutation in
\mathbb{C}
to apply to
\bm{P}
,

(4)

Update
\bm{P}
with the chosen permutation,

(5)

Repeat steps 2-4 until convergence of cost
c(\bm{P})
.

In what follows, we define all necessary terms and provide a detailed description of
Algorithm

1
, also depicted in
Fig.

1
.

Definition 0
.

Define a
2
-cycle
\bm{C}
as
\bm{C}\in\mathbb{P}_{n}

such that

\displaystyle\exists i,j\in[n],i\neq j:C_{i,j}=C_{j,i}=1

and

\displaystyle\forall l\in[n],l\notin\{i,j\}:C_{l,l}=1\;,

and denote the set of
2
-cycles with
\mathbb{P}_{n}^{(2)}
.

We remark that any permutation matrix
\bm{P}\in\mathbb{P}_{n}
can be written as a product of
2
-cycles,

(4)

\forall\bm{P}\in\mathbb{P}_{n}:\exists\{C_{1},\dots,C_{s}\}\subset\mathbb{P}_{n}^{(2)}:\bm{P}=\prod_{i=1}^{s}\bm{C}_{i}=\bm{C}_{1}\cdots\bm{C}_{s}\;.

Instead of optimizing over all
2
-cycles the idea of
CyclicExpansion
is to iteratively consider fixed subsets of
\mathbb{P}_{n}^{(2)}
.

Definition 0
.

Let
\mathbb{C}=\{C_{1},\dots,C_{s}\}\subset\mathbb{P}_{n}^{(2)}
be a set of
2
-cycles and let
\alpha\in\{0,1\}^{s}
.
For
\bm{P}\in\mathbb{P}_{n}
we define

(5)

g\left(\bm{P},\bm{\alpha},\mathbb{C}\right)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\left(\prod_{i=1}^{s}\bm{C}_{i}^{\alpha_{i}}\right)\bm{P}=\bm{C}_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots\bm{C}_{s}^{\alpha_{s}}\bm{P}\;,

with
\bm{C}_{i}^{0}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\bm{I}_{n}
,
\bm{C}_{i}^{1}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\bm{\bm{C}_{i}}
.
In words, the vector
\bm{\alpha}
indicates which cycle in
\mathbb{C}
should be applied to
\bm{P}
.

For a given
\bm{P}\in\mathbb{P}_{n}
the following objective is optimized in each iteration

(6)

\argmin_{\bm{\alpha}\in\{0,1\}^{s}}\ c\left(g\left(\bm{P},\bm{\alpha},\mathbb{C}\right)\right)\;.

However,
Eq.

6
is not in
Qubo
form and can thus not be directly solved on actual quantum hardware.
To overcome this issue, we only consider disjoint
2
-cycles.

Definition 0
.

Let
\bm{C},\bm{C}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{P}_{n}^{(2)}
.

\bm{C}
and
\bm{C}^{\prime}
are disjoint if

\left(C_{i,i}=0\Rightarrow C^{\prime}_{i,i}=1\right)\wedge\left(C^{\prime}_{i,i}=0\Rightarrow C_{i,i}=1\right)\;,

which leads to commutativity, i.e.,
\bm{C}\bm{C}^{\prime}=\bm{C}^{\prime}\bm{C}
.
We call a set
\mathbb{C}\subset\mathbb{P}_{n}^{(2)}
disjoint if all elements are pairwise disjoint.

Sets of disjoint
2
-cycles have a large expressive power in terms of covering the whole permutation space.

Lemma 0
.

Given a permutation matrix
\bm{P}
, there exist two sets
\mathbb{C}
,
\mathbb{C}^{\prime}\subset\mathbb{P}_{n}^{(2)}
of disjoint
2
-cycles such that

\bm{P}=\bm{L}\bm{R},\quad\bm{L}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\prod_{\bm{C}\in\mathbb{C}}\bm{C},\quad\bm{R}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\prod_{\bm{C}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{C}^{\prime}}\bm{C}^{\prime}\;.

Proof.

See
(
Benkner et al. 2021
)
.
∎

With assuming disjoint
2
-cycles we obtain the following result.

Lemma 0
.

Assume that
\mathbb{C}=\{\bm{C}_{1},\dots,\bm{C}_{s}\}\subset\mathbb{P}_{n}^{(2)}
is a disjoint set of
2
-cycles and let
\bm{P}\in\mathbb{P}_{n}
be a permutation matrix.
Then, the following identity holds

(7)

g\left(\bm{P},\bm{\alpha},\mathbb{C}\right)=\bm{P}+\sum_{i=1}^{s}\alpha_{i}\tilde{\bm{C}}_{i},\quad\tilde{\bm{C}}_{i}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\left(\bm{C}_{i}-\bm{I}_{n}\right)\bm{P}\;.

Proof.

We proof the statement by induction.
For
s=1

Eq.

5
reduces to

\bm{C}^{\alpha}\bm{P}=\left(1-\alpha\right)\bm{P}+\alpha\bm{C}\bm{P}=\bm{P}+\alpha\left(\bm{C}-\bm{I}_{n}\right)\bm{P}\;,

leading to
Eq.

7
.
Multiplying the inverse of
\bm{P}
to the right, we obtain

\bm{C}^{\alpha}=\bm{I}_{n}+\alpha\left(\bm{C}-\bm{I}_{n}\right)\;.

Now, consider
s
and assume that

\displaystyle\left(\prod_{i=1}^{s}\bm{C}_{i}^{\alpha_{i}}\right)=\left(\prod_{i=1}^{s-1}\bm{C}_{i}^{\alpha_{i}}\right)\bm{C}_{s}^{\alpha_{s}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\bm{I}_{n}+\sum_{i=1}^{s-1}\alpha_{i}\left(\bm{C}_{i}-\bm{I}_{n}\right)\right)\left(\bm{I}_{n}+\alpha_{s}\left(\bm{C}_{s}-\bm{I}_{n}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\bm{I}_{n}+\sum_{i=1}^{s}\alpha_{i}\left(\bm{C}_{i}-\bm{I}_{n}\right)\right)\left(\sum_{i=1}^{s}\alpha_{i}\alpha_{s}\left(\bm{C}_{i}-\bm{I}_{n}\right)\left(\bm{C}_{s}-\bm{I}_{n}\right)\right)\;,

and it remains to show that

Lemma 0
.

Assume that

\bm{P}\in\mathbb{P}_{n}

\argmin_{\bm{\alpha}\in\{0,1\}^{s}}\ \bm{\alpha}^{\top}\tilde{\bm{Q}}\bm{\alpha}\;,

with

(9)

\tilde{Q}_{ij}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\begin{cases}c\left(\tilde{\bm{C}}_{i},\tilde{\bm{C}}_{j}\right),&\text{if }i\neq j\;,\\
c\left(\tilde{\bm{C}}_{i}\right)+c\left(\tilde{\bm{C}}_{i},\bm{P}\right)+c\left(\bm{P},\tilde{\bm{C}}_{i}\right),&\text{else}\;.\end{cases}

Proof.

Since tr and matrix multiplication are linear functions,

is bilinear and we obtain

\displaystyle\argmin_{\bm{\alpha}\in\{0,1\}^{s}}

\displaystyle\quad c\left(g\left(\bm{P},\bm{\alpha},\mathbb{C}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\argmin_{\bm{\alpha}\in\{0,1\}^{s}}

\displaystyle\quad c\left(\bm{P}+\sum_{i=1}^{s}\alpha_{i}\tilde{\bm{C}}_{i},\bm{P}+\sum_{j=1}^{s}\alpha_{j}\tilde{\bm{C}}_{j}\right)

\displaystyle=\argmin_{\bm{\alpha}\in\{0,1\}^{s}}

\displaystyle\quad c\left(\bm{P}\right)+\sum_{i,j=1}^{s}\alpha_{i}\alpha_{j}c\left(\tilde{\bm{C}}_{i},\tilde{\bm{C}}_{j}\right)

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=1}^{s}\alpha_{i}c\left(\tilde{\bm{C}}_{i},\bm{P}\right)+\sum_{j=1}^{s}\alpha_{j}c\left(\bm{P},\tilde{\bm{C}}_{j}\right)=\argmin_{\bm{\alpha}\in\{0,1\}^{s}}\bm{\alpha}^{\top}\tilde{\bm{Q}}\bm{\alpha}\;,

where

is defined as in

.
∎

4.2.1.
Choosing Indices

Instead of optimizing over the whole index set
[m]
in each iteration we can reduce the problem size by considering an index set
\mathcal{I}\subset[m]
of size
k
.
These indices can be chosen randomly but having a deep understanding of the underlying problem setting one could use a more informative approach.
For example, we could choose the indices depending on the impact of the overall cost, i.e.,

(10)

\argmax_{\mathcal{I}\subset[m],\left|\mathcal{I}\right|=k}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}F_{i,j}D_{\pi(i),\pi(j)}\;.

Intuitively, it makes sense to greedily permute the currently worst performing indices. However, one might get stuck in a local optimum too early.
Both methods (random and greedy) are investigated later on in
Section

5
.

Even though we now have a problem dimension only dependent on
k
and not the number of locations
n
, this approach is not yet guaranteed to converge towards an optimal solution.
If we only consider index sets
\mathcal{I}\subset[m]
we stick to permute the initial sub-permutation and thus concentrate on a fixed set of
m
locations.
To prevent this, in each iteration, we also sample a set
\mathcal{J}\subset[n]
of
k_{u}\leq k
unbound locations, i.e., locations which are not assigned to a functional block.
We sample each unbound location with a probability proportional to the distance to the nearest neighbor in the set of bound locations.
With this method, we also explore the set of unbound locations and can place the given functional blocks on the whole chip grid.

4.2.2.
Choosing Cycles

For fixed numbers of indices
k,k_{u}\leq m
, we iteratively sample a set of disjoint
2
-cycles and optimize the current permutation until every
2
-cycle has occurred in the sampling process (Lines
7
-
12
).
The question arises on which indices these cycles should be sampled.
Considering an index set
\mathcal{I}=\{i_{1},\dots,i_{k}\}\subset[m]
we can compute the sub-permuted index set under the sub-permutation
\pi
as
\mathcal{I}_{\pi}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\{\pi(i_{1}),\dots,\pi(i_{k})\}\subset[n]
.
We first sample
k_{u}
disjoint
2
-cycles which map
\{i_{1},\dots,i_{k_{u}}\}
to
\mathcal{J}
.
Secondly, we sample
\left\lfloor(k-k_{u})/2\right\rfloor
disjoint
2
-cycles which map
\{i_{k_{u}+1},\dots,i_{k}\}
to
\{\pi(i_{k_{u}+1}),\dots,\pi(i_{k})\}
.
Since there are restrictions on which functional block can be mapped to which chip location (e.g. a LUT cannot be placed on an IO cell), one cannot simply sample these cycles arbitrarily between chosen indices (see
Fig.

2
).

Figure 2
.
Illustration of allowed 2-cycles. Current placement (left) with corresponding cell types (cyan and red). Exemplary legal and illegal 2-cycles (right).

However, this does not pose a problem for this framework, because these constraints can be integrated into the sampling process.
The total number of
2
-cycles is thus
s=k_{u}+\left\lfloor(k-k_{u})/2\right\rfloor
, which is only dependent on the freely selectable index set sizes
k
and
k_{u}
.
Since the
Qubo
dimension corresponds exactly to the number of considered disjoint cycles, we can conveniently adapt the problem to the available hardware size, either for real quantum annealers or classical digital annealing
Qubo
solvers.
However, there is a trade-off between problem size and performance, which will be investigated later on in
Section

5
.

4.2.3.
Constructing
\bm{Q}\left(\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J}\right)

It remains to clarify how the matrix
\bm{Q}\left(\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J}\right)
from
9
is constructed.
One way would be to precompute the cost matrix
\bm{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{mn\times mn}
and then using standard methods for reducing the
Qubo
size with fixed variables.
Since real-world algorithms to be implemented on an
FPGA
-chip can contain up to millions of functional blocks and chip locations, the size of the cost matrix
\bm{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{mn\times mn}
(
Eq.

3
) can get infeasible to hold the precomputed matrix in memory.
We resolve this issue by exploiting the tensor product-like structure of
\bm{Q}=\bm{F}\otimes\bm{D}
.

Definition 0
.

For an index set
\mathcal{I}\subset[m]
and a sub-permutation matrix
\bm{P}\in\mathbb{P}_{m,n}
, define
\mathcal{I}^{c}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=[m]\setminus\mathcal{I}
and let
\mathcal{I}_{\pi},\mathcal{I}^{c}_{\pi}\subset[n]
be the sets created by applying the underlying sub-permutation
\pi
of
\bm{P}
to
\mathcal{I},\mathcal{I}^{c}
.

Lemma 0
.

Given
\bm{F}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
,
\bm{D}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
,
\bm{P}\in\mathbb{P}_{m,n}
,
\mathcal{I}\subset[m]
with
\left|\mathcal{I}\right|=k
and
\mathcal{J}\subset[n]
with
\left|\mathcal{I}\right|=k_{u}
it holds

\displaystyle\argmin_{\bm{x}\left(\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J}\right)\in\{0,1\}^{k(k+k_{u})}}\bm{x}^{\top}\bm{Q}\bm{x}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\argmin_{\bm{x}\left(\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J}\right)\in\{0,1\}^{k(k+k_{u})}}\left(\bm{x}\left(\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J}\right)\right)^{\top}\bm{Q}\left(\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J}\right)\bm{x}\left(\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J}\right)\;,

with
\bm{x}\left(\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J}\right)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\vect(\bm{P}_{\mathcal{I},\mathcal{I}_{\pi}^{\prime}})
,
\mathcal{I}_{\pi}^{\prime}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\mathcal{I}_{\pi}\cup\mathcal{J}
,
\bm{x}=\vect\left(\bm{P}\right)
and

(11)

\bm{Q}\left(\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J}\right)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\bm{F}_{\mathcal{I}}\otimes\bm{D}_{\mathcal{I}_{\pi}^{\prime}}+2\ \diag\left(\vect\left(\bm{F}_{\mathcal{I},\mathcal{I}^{c}}\bm{D}_{\mathcal{I}_{\pi}^{c},\mathcal{I}_{\pi}^{\prime}}\right)\right)\;.

Proof.

With the following equality

\displaystyle\ \tr\left(\bm{F}\bm{P}\bm{D}\bm{P}^{\top}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \tr\left(\bm{F}_{\mathcal{I}}\bm{P}_{\mathcal{I},\mathcal{I}_{\pi}^{\prime}}\bm{D}_{\mathcal{I}}\bm{P}_{\mathcal{I},\mathcal{I}_{\pi}^{\prime}}^{\top}\right)+\tr\left(\bm{F}_{\mathcal{I}^{c}}\bm{P}_{\mathcal{I}^{c},\mathcal{I}^{c}_{\pi}}\bm{D}_{\mathcal{I}^{c}}\bm{P}_{\mathcal{I}^{c},\mathcal{I}^{c}_{\pi}}^{\top}\right)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle 2\ \tr\left(\bm{F}_{\mathcal{I},\mathcal{I}^{c}}\bm{P}_{\mathcal{I}^{c},\mathcal{I}^{c}_{\pi}}\bm{D}_{\mathcal{I}^{c},\mathcal{I}}\bm{P}_{\mathcal{I},\mathcal{I}_{\pi}^{\prime}}^{\top}\right)\;,

we can deduce

\displaystyle\argmin_{\bm{x}\left(\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J}\right)\in\{0,1\}^{k(k+k_{u})}}\bm{x}^{\top}\bm{Q}\bm{x}\Leftrightarrow\argmin_{\bm{P}_{\mathcal{I},\mathcal{I}_{\pi}^{\prime}}\in\mathbb{P}_{k,k+k_{u}}}c\left(\bm{P}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\argmin_{\bm{P}_{\mathcal{I},\mathcal{I}_{\pi}^{\prime}}\in\mathbb{P}_{k,k+k_{u}}}\tr\left(\bm{F}_{\mathcal{I}}\bm{P}_{\mathcal{I},\mathcal{I}_{\pi}^{\prime}}\bm{D}_{\mathcal{I}}\bm{P}_{\mathcal{I},\mathcal{I}_{\pi}^{\prime}}^{\top}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+2\ \tr\left(\bm{F}_{\mathcal{I},\mathcal{I}^{c}}\bm{P}_{\mathcal{I}^{c},\mathcal{I}^{c}_{\pi}}\bm{D}_{\mathcal{I}^{c},\mathcal{I}}\bm{P}_{\mathcal{I},\mathcal{I}_{\pi}^{\prime}}^{\top}\right)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle\argmin_{\bm{x}\left(\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J}\right)\in\{0,1\}^{k(k+k_{u})}}\left(\bm{x}\left(\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J}\right)\right)^{\top}\left(\bm{F}_{\mathcal{I}}\otimes\bm{D}_{\mathcal{I}_{\pi}^{\prime}}\right)\bm{x}\left(\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+2\ \left(\vect\left(\bm{F}_{\mathcal{I},\mathcal{I}^{c}}\bm{D}_{\mathcal{I}_{\pi}^{c},\mathcal{I}_{\pi}^{\prime}}\right)\right)^{\top}\bm{x}\left(\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\argmin_{\bm{x}\left(\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J}\right)\in\{0,1\}^{k(k+k_{u})}}\left(\bm{x}\left(\mathcal{I}\right)\right)^{\top}\bm{Q}\left(\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J}\right)\bm{x}\left(\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J}\right)\;,

with
\bm{Q}\left(\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J}\right)
as defined in
Eq.

11
.
∎

6.
Conclusion

In this paper, we showed that the
FPGA
-
Placement
problem can be solved with quantum computing.
For this, we first formulated the problem in an unbalanced
QAP
framework, and then proceeded by working out
Qubo
formulations, which can be conveniently solved with quantum annealing and classical hardware solvers.
The
QAP
belongs to the hardest problems in NP, meaning there does not exist an approximate algorithm solving this problem in polynomial time.
With the notion of sub-permutations, we find a new
Qubo
formulation for the unbalanced
QAP
without introducing dummy facilities, leading to a lower dimensionality.
The problem of incorporating constraints for the set of allowed sub-permutations is overcome by considering the iterative
CyclicExpansion
algorithm.
It works by optimizing over sets of disjoint
2
-cycles, where the size of these sets can be conveniently adapted to the available hardware size.
Moreover, initial placements can easily be incorporated into this algorithm.

Experiments on a fictional
FPGA
architecture leads to supporting the theoretical guarantees and show good results.
In this work, we consider binary flow matrices and Manhattan distances between locations on the
FPGA
chip.
However, real architectures can easily be integrated into our framework, by adapting the distance matrix and flow matrix correspondingly.
These experiments are conducted on randomly generated problems as well as a small real-world circuit (CRC-32).
The
Qubo
problems are solved using software solvers, classical hardware solvers, and real-world quantum annealers.
We defer the investigation of large-scale use-cases as well as additional performance metrics to follow-up work.
Here, we were more interested in theoretical properties. To this end, we investigated the effect of varying the sub-problem size
k
and the number of unbound variables
k_{u}
, as well as the impact of different methods for choosing the sub-problems on the
QAP
cost.
We find that our method allows us to trade-off the solution quality against the running time,
which makes the
CyclicExpansion
algorithm a very promising candidate for both, real-world quantum hardware and real-world placement problems.
