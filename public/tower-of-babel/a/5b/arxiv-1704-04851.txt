Rational Solutions of the Painlevé-II Equation Revisited This paper is a contribution to the Special Issue on Symmetries and Integrability of Difference Equations. The full collection is available at http://www.emis.de/journals/SIGMA/SIDE12.html
Peter D. MILLER and Yue SHENG

Abstract
The rational solutions of the Painlevé-II equation appear in several applications and are known to have many remarkable algebraic and analytic properties. They also have several different representations, useful in different ways for establishing these properties. In particular, Riemann–Hilbert representations have proven to be useful for extracting the asymptotic behavior of the rational solutions in the limit of large degree (equivalently the large-parameter limit). We review the elementary properties of the rational Painlevé-II functions, and then we describe three different Riemann–Hilbert representations of them that have appeared in the literature: a representation by means of the isomonodromy theory of the Flaschka–Newell Lax pair, a second representation by means of the isomonodromy theory of the Jimbo–Miwa Lax pair, and a third representation found by Bertola and Bothner related to pseudo-orthogonal polynomials. We prove that the Flaschka–Newell and Bertola–Bothner Riemann–Hilbert representations of the rational Painlevé-II functions are explicitly connected to each other. Finally, we review recent results describing the asymptotic behavior of the rational Painlevé-II functions obtained from these Riemann–Hilbert representations by means of the steepest descent method.

Introduction

Rational solutions of the second Painlevé equation are important in several applied problems. It was discovered by Bass
[
3
]
that a certain Nernst–Planck model of steady electrolysis with two ions reduces to the Painlevé-II equation, and in
[
36
]
the special role of rational solutions was highlighted in the context of this model (see also
[
4
]
). Johnson
[
24
]
notes that rational solutions of the Painlevé-II equation parametrize certain string theoretic models in high-energy physics. Clarkson
[
11
]
reviews the application of rational solutions of Painlevé-II to the classification of certain equilibrium vortex configurations in ideal planar fluid flow (the poles of opposite residue determine the location of vortices of opposite circulation). More recently Buckingham and one of the authors
[
7
]
found that the collection of rational solutions of the Painlevé-II equation universally describes the space-time location of kinks in the semiclassical sine-Gordon equation near a transversal crossing of the separatrix for the simple pendulum. Also, Shapiro and Tater
[
37
]
have observed a connection between the rational Painlevé-II solutions of large degree and the characteristic polynomials for the quasi-exactly solvable part of the discrete spectrum in a boundary-value problem for the stationary Schrödinger operator with a quartic potential exhibiting PT-symmetry.

In this paper, we review some of the well-known elementary properties of the rational solutions of the Painlevé-II equation, including three ways to represent them in terms of the solution of a Riemann–Hilbert problem. We make a new contribution by showing that two of the three representations, until now thought to be unrelated, are in fact connected with each other via a simple and explicit transformation. Then we review results on the asymptotic behavior of the rational Painlevé-II solutions in the large-degree limit that have been recently obtained by analyzing these Riemann–Hilbert representations. Here our aim is to simplify the results and correct them where necessary as well as to report them in a unified context.

1.1
Basic properties of rational Painlevé-II solutions

In this paper, we consider the Painlevé-II equation with parameter
m
written in the form

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}p}{\mathrm{d}y^{2}}=2p^{3}+\frac{2}{3}yp-\frac{2}{3}m,\qquad p=p(y),\qquad m\in\mathbb{C}.

(1.1)

1.1.1
Necessary conditions for existence of rational solutions

Obviously if
m=0
, one has
p(y)=0
as a particular solution. Moreover this is the only rational solution. Indeed, any nonzero rational solution
p(y)
would necessarily admit, for some
p_{0}\neq 0
and
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, a series expansion

\displaystyle p(y)=y^{n}\big(p_{0}+p_{1}y^{-1}+\cdots\big)

(1.2)

convergent for sufficiently large
|y|
, and the a dominant balance in (
1.1
) with
m=0
would require

.

Conversely, if
m\in\mathbb{C}
is nonzero, (
1.1
) does not admit the zero solution. In this case, every rational solution
p(y)
again has the form (
1.2
) for sufficiently large
|y|
and
p_{0}\neq 0
with
n\in\mathbb{Z}
; it then follows from (
1.1
) that a dominant balance is achieved between the terms
\tfrac{2}{3}yp
and
-\tfrac{2}{3}m
yielding

and

. Therefore if

is a counterclockwise-oriented circle of sufficiently large radius,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\oint\nolimits_{C}p(y)\,\mathrm{d}y=m.

(1.3)

Likewise if
y_{0}\in\mathbb{C}
is a finite pole of order

of the rational solution
p(y)
, then the substitution of the Laurent series

\displaystyle p(y)=(y-y_{0})^{-n}(p_{0}+p_{1}(y-y_{0})+\cdots)

into (
1.1
) results in a dominant balance between
p^{\prime\prime}
and
2p^{3}
yielding

and
p_{0}=\pm 1
, so every pole of

is simple and has residue
\pm 1
. If

denotes the number of poles of
p
of residue
\pm 1
, then

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\oint\nolimits_{C}p(y)\,\mathrm{d}y=N_{+}(p)-N_{-}(p)\in\mathbb{Z},

so comparing with (
1.3
), we see that (
1.1
) admits a rational solution
p(y)
only if
m\in\mathbb{Z}
.

The Bäcklund transformation (

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\widecheck{p}(y):=\frac{-3p^{\prime\prime}(y)-3p(y)p^{\prime}(y)+3p(y)^{3}+yp(y)-1}{3p^{\prime}(y)+3p(y)^{2}+y}=-p(y)+\frac{2m-1}{3p^{\prime}(y)+3p(y)^{2}+y}\\

and
\widetilde{p}(y)

17

for further information about Bäcklund transformations for Painlevé equations.

Explicitly, the first several rational Painlevé-II solutions are

\displaystyle p_{0}(y)=0,\qquad p_{\pm 1}(y)=\pm\frac{1}{y},\qquad p_{\pm 2}(y)=\pm\frac{2y^{3}-6}{y(y^{3}+6)},

\displaystyle p_{\pm 3}(y)=\pm\frac{3y^{2}(y^{6}+12y^{3}+360)}{(y^{3}+6)(y^{6}+30y^{3}-180)},

(1.6)

where the subscript indicates the value of

in (
1.1
).

1.1.3
Representation in terms of special polynomials

It was first observed by Yablonskii
[
39
]
and Vorob’ev
[
38
]
that rational solutions of the
Painlevé-II
equation (
1.1
) can be represented in terms of special polynomials having an explicit recurrence relation. The
Yablonskii–Vorob’ev polynomials
are defined recursively by
Q_{0}(z):=1
,
Q_{1}(z):=z
, and then

\displaystyle Q_{n+1}(z):=\frac{zQ_{n}(z)^{2}-4\big(Q_{n}^{\prime\prime}(z)Q_{n}(z)-Q_{n}^{\prime}(z)^{2}\big)}{Q_{n-1}(z)},\qquad n=1,2,3,\dots.

(1.7)

Then the rational solution of (
1.1
) can be expressed as

\displaystyle p(y)=p_{m}(y)=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}y}\ln\left(\frac{Q_{m}(z)}{Q_{m-1}(z)}\right),\qquad z=\left(\frac{2}{3}\right)^{1/3}y,\qquad m=1,2,3,\dots.

(1.8)

Of course the first surprise regarding the recursion formula (
1.7
) is that
\{Q_{n}(z)\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
is indeed a sequence of polynomials in
z
. Indeed this is the case, as can be seen by the alternative formula due to Kajiwara and Ohta
[
26
]
expressing
Q_{n}(z)
as a constant multiple of the Wronskian of
2n-1
polynomials in
z
(see also
[
10
, Section 2.4]
), and the first several iterates of (
1.7
) produce:

\displaystyle Q_{2}(z)=z^{3}+4,\qquad Q_{3}(z)=z^{6}+20z^{3}-80,\qquad Q_{4}(z)=z^{10}+60z^{7}+11200z.

It has been shown that the polynomials
\{Q_{n}(z)\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
all have simple roots and that
Q_{m}
and
Q_{m-1}
can have no roots in common
[
20
]
, facts that are consistent via (
1.8
) with the fact that all poles of
p(y)
are simple with residues
\pm 1
. Real roots of the Yablonskii–Vorob’ev polynomials correspond to real poles of
p(y)
, and these have been studied extensively by Roffelsen who has shown that all nonzero real roots are all irrational
[
34
]
and that there are precisely
\lfloor(n+1)/3\rfloor
negative roots of
Q_{n}
and
\lfloor(n+1)/2\rfloor
total real roots of
Q_{n}
, and
Q_{n}(0)=0
if and only if
n=1\pmod{3}

[
35
]
. Also, the real roots of
Q_{n+1}
and
Q_{n-1}
interlace, as was proven by Clarkson
[
10
]
.

1.2
Outline of the paper

The fact that all rational solutions of the Painlevé-II equation (
1.1
) can be iteratively constructed, either via the direct Bäcklund transformations (
1.4
) and (
1.5
) or via the recurrence relation for the Yablonskii–Vorob’ev polynomials (
1.7
), is quite remarkable and indicative of deeper integrable structure underlying the Painlevé-II equation. However, it must also be pointed out that the use of these iterative constructions is limited in practice, because the formulae generated become increasingly complicated as
|m|
increases. The situation is similar to that encountered when studying orthogonal polynomials, which in general can be constructed systematically by a Gram–Schmidt orthogonalization algorithm, but the number of steps of this algorithm increases with the degree of the polynomial desired, making it difficult to appeal to this approach to deduce properties of the general polynomial in the family.

Therefore, if our interest is to understand the analytic properties of the rational Painlevé-II functions, it is necessary to have an alternative representation that admits the possibility of asymptotic analysis for large
|m|
. In Section
2
we describe three such representations of the rational Painlevé-II solutions, two coming directly from the isomonodromic integrable structure underlying the Painlevé-II equation, and one related to a recently discovered representation of the squares of the Yablonskii–Vorob’ev polynomials in terms of the integrable structure behind orthogonal polynomials (which provides a work-around for the Gram–Schmidt procedure allowing large-degree asymptotics of general orthogonal polynomials to be computed). One of the contributions of our paper is then to establish a new identity relating the orthogonal polynomial approach to one of the isomonodromic approaches; see Section
2.4
.

These representations of the rational Painlevé-II solutions have indeed proven to be useful in characterizing the rational functions
p_{m}(y)
in the limit of large
|m|
. In Section
3
we review some of the results that have been proven with their help, outlining some of the methods of proof.

Below we will make frequent use of the Pauli spin matrices defined by

\displaystyle\sigma_{1}:=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{bmatrix},\qquad\sigma_{2}:=\begin{bmatrix}0&-\mathrm{i}\\
\mathrm{i}&0\end{bmatrix},\qquad\sigma_{3}:=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{bmatrix}.

2
Riemann–Hilbert problem representations

of the rational Painlevé-II solutions

2.1
Flaschka–Newell representation

In 1980, Flaschka and Newell
[
16
]
showed how a self-similar reduction of the Lax pair representation of the modified Korteweg–de Vries equation reveals the Painlevé-II equation in the form (
1.1
) to be an isomonodromic deformation of the linear equation

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathbf{v}}{\partial\lambda}=\mathbf{A}^{\mathrm{FN}}(\lambda,y)\mathbf{v},\qquad\mathbf{A}^{\mathrm{FN}}(\lambda,y):=\begin{bmatrix}-6\mathrm{i}\lambda^{2}-3\mathrm{i}p^{2}-\mathrm{i}y&6p\lambda+3\mathrm{i}p^{\prime}+m\lambda^{-1}\\
6p\lambda-3\mathrm{i}p^{\prime}+m\lambda^{-1}&6\mathrm{i}\lambda^{2}+3\mathrm{i}p^{2}+\mathrm{i}y\end{bmatrix}

(2.1)

in which
p
,
p^{\prime}
,
y
, and
m
are regarded as numerical parameters. Indeed, (
2.1
) is compatible with the auxiliary linear equation

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathbf{v}}{\partial y}=\mathbf{B}^{\mathrm{FN}}(\lambda,y)\mathbf{v},\qquad\mathbf{B}^{\mathrm{FN}}(\lambda,y):=\begin{bmatrix}-\mathrm{i}\lambda&p\\
p&\mathrm{i}\lambda\end{bmatrix}

(2.2)

only if the
compatibility condition

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathbf{A}}{\partial y}-\frac{\partial\mathbf{B}}{\partial\lambda}+[\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}]=\mathbf{0}

(2.3)

holds with
\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{A}^{\mathrm{FN}}
and
\mathbf{B}=\mathbf{B}^{\mathrm{FN}}
. This forces
p
to depend on
y
by the Painlevé-II equation in the form (
1.1
) and forces
p^{\prime}=p^{\prime}(y)
. The equation (
2.2
) then implies that the monodromy data associated with solutions of (
2.1
) depends trivially on
y
.

Let us describe the monodromy data associated with rational solutions
p=p_{m}(y)
of (
1.1
) for
m\in\mathbb{Z}
. It is pointed out in
[
16
]
that whenever
(p,p^{\prime})=(p_{m}(y),p^{\prime}_{m}(y))
in (
2.1
) for the rational solution
p_{m}(y)
, the irregular singular point at
\lambda=\infty
for (
2.1
) exhibits only trivial Stokes phenomenon. This implies the existence of a
fundamental solution matrix of (
2.1
) of the form

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}_{\infty}(\lambda,y)=\left[\mathbb{I}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathbf{K}^{n}(y)\lambda^{-n}\right]\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\theta(\lambda,y)\sigma_{3}}

(2.4)

for some matrix coefficients
\mathbf{K}^{1}(y)
,
\mathbf{K}^{2}(y)
, and so on, where

\displaystyle\theta(\lambda,y):=2\lambda^{3}+y\lambda,

and where the infinite series in (
2.4
) is convergent for
|\lambda|
sufficiently large, which in view of
\lambda=0
being the only finite singular point actually means for
\lambda\neq 0
. Assuming compatibility, i.e., that
p=p(y)
solves (
1.1
) with
p^{\prime}=p^{\prime}(y)
, it can be shown that
\mathbf{V}_{\infty}(\lambda,y)
is also a fundamental solution matrix for (
2.2
), and then it follows by substitution into the latter system that
p(y)
is recovered from the subleading term of the expansion (
2.4
) by the formula

\displaystyle p(y)=2\mathrm{i}K^{1}_{12}(y)=-2\mathrm{i}K^{1}_{21}(y).

(2.5)

On the other hand,
\lambda=0
is a regular singular point for (
2.1
).
Applying the method of Frobenius, there exists a fundamental solution matrix of (
2.1
) defined in a neighborhood of

\lambda=0
having the form

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}_{0}(\lambda,y)=\left[\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix}1&-1\\
1&1\end{bmatrix}h(y)^{\sigma_{3}}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathbf{H}^{n}(y)\lambda^{n}\right]\lambda^{m\sigma_{3}}

(2.6)

for some scalar function
h(y)\neq 0
and matrix coefficients
\mathbf{H}^{1}(y)
,
\mathbf{H}^{2}(y)
, and so on. The absence of logarithms in spite of the fact that the Frobenius exponents
\pm m
differ by an integer follows from the fact that, due to the triviality of the Stokes phenomenon at
\lambda=\infty
, the monodromy matrix for (
2.1
) corresponding to any loop about the origin is the identity, hence diagonalizable. However the same fact implies an ambiguity in the formula (
2.6
) in which the dominant column in the limit
\lambda\to 0
is only determined up to addition of a multiple of the subdominant column. Flaschka and Newell
[
16
]
resolve this ambiguity as follows. They first observe that the subdominant column is well-defined after the choice of the scalar
h(y)
, and from the recurrence relations determining the higher-order terms from the preceding terms a predictable pattern emerges in which consecutive terms are alternating scalar multiples of the vectors
(1,1)^{\top}
and
(-1,1)^{\top}
. A similar well-defined alternating pattern holds for the dominant column, but only through the terms with
n\leq 2|m|-1
, with the term for
n=2|m|
satisfying an equation that is consistent but indeterminate. Here a choice is made: the term for
n=2|m|
is taken to continue the alternating pattern of vectors
(1,1)^{\top}
and
(-1,1)^{\top}
. Once this choice has been made, the alternating pattern again continues to all orders of the dominant column. In other words, Flaschka and Newell take
\mathbf{V}_{0}(\lambda,y)
in the more specific form

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}_{0}(\lambda,y)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix}1&-1\\
1&1\end{bmatrix}\left(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sigma_{1}^{n}\begin{bmatrix}h^{n}_{11}(y)&0\\
0&h^{n}_{22}(y)\end{bmatrix}\lambda^{n}\right)\lambda^{m\sigma_{3}},

\displaystyle h^{0}_{11}(y)=h(y)=h^{0}_{22}(y)^{-1}.

(2.7)

There is then exactly one matrix solution of (
2.1
) of this form for a given scalar
h(y)
, and moreover, assuming compatibility,
h(y)
can be chosen up to a constant scalar multiple so that
\mathbf{V}_{0}(\lambda,y)
simultaneously solves (
2.2
). Again, the infinite series appearing in (
2.7
) is convergent near
\lambda=0
, and since there are no other finite singular points it is actually convergent for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
. By taking the limits
\lambda\to 0
and
\lambda\to\infty
respectively, Abel’s theorem implies the identities
\det(\mathbf{V}_{0}(\lambda,y))=1
and
\det(\mathbf{V}_{\infty}(\lambda,y))=1
because the coefficient matrix
\mathbf{A}^{\mathrm{FN}}(\lambda,y)
in (
2.1
) has zero trace. Therefore, as both
\mathbf{V}_{\infty}(\lambda,y)
and (for a suitable choice of
h(y)
)
\mathbf{V}_{0}(\lambda,y)
are simultaneous fundamental solution matrices for (
2.1
) and (
2.2
) defined in a common domain
0<|\lambda|<\infty
, there exists a constant unimodular matrix
\mathbf{G}_{m}
such that

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}_{\infty}(\lambda,y)=\mathbf{V}_{0}(\lambda,y)\mathbf{G}_{m},\qquad 0<|\lambda|<\infty.

(2.8)

The
connection matrix

\mathbf{G}_{m}
is the monodromy data for the linear problem (
2.1
) in the case that
p=p_{m}(y)
is a rational solution of (
1.1
). For more general solutions given
m\in\mathbb{Z}
, or for non-integral values of
m
, the monodromy data becomes augmented with six
Stokes matrices
of alternating triangularity connecting solutions each having the form (
2.4
) (but only as an asymptotic series, with no convergence properties implied) in six overlapping sectors of the irregular singular point at
\lambda=\infty
.

It is easy to see that
\overline{\mathbf{V}}(\lambda,y):=\sigma_{1}\mathbf{V}(-\lambda,y)\sigma_{1}
is a fundamental solution matrix for the system (
2.1
) whenever
\mathbf{V}(\lambda,y)
is. This substitution also leaves (
2.2
) invariant. Since
\mathbf{V}_{\infty}(\lambda,y)
is uniquely determined from (
2.1
) and the leading term of its large-
\lambda

p=p_{m}(y)
),
we deduce the identity

\displaystyle\overline{\mathbf{V}}_{\infty}(\lambda,y)=\mathbf{V}_{\infty}(\lambda,y).

(2.9)

Similarly, given the scalar
h(y)
, it follows from (
2.7
) that

\displaystyle\overline{\mathbf{V}}_{0}(\lambda,y)=\mathbf{V}_{0}(\lambda,y)\begin{bmatrix}0&(-1)^{m}\\
(-1)^{m+1}&0\end{bmatrix}.

(2.10)

Therefore, conjugating by
\sigma_{1}
and replacing
\lambda\mapsto-\lambda
in (
2.8
), the use of the identities (
2.9
)–(
2.10
) shows that also

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}_{\infty}(\lambda,y)=\mathbf{V}_{0}(\lambda,y)(-1)^{m}\sigma_{3}\mathbf{G}_{m}\sigma_{1},\qquad 0<|\lambda|<\infty,

and hence comparing again with (
2.8
) one sees that
\mathbf{G}_{m}=(-1)^{m}\sigma_{3}\mathbf{G}_{m}\sigma_{1}
. This matrix identity along with the condition that
\det(\mathbf{G}_{m})=1
implies that
\mathbf{G}_{m}
necessarily has the form

\displaystyle\mathbf{G}_{m}=\begin{bmatrix}\alpha&(-1)^{m}\alpha\\
(-1)^{m+1}(2\alpha)^{-1}&(2\alpha)^{-1}\end{bmatrix},

(2.11)

where only the nonzero constant
\alpha
is undetermined by symmetry.

We may now formulate a Riemann–Hilbert problem to recover
\mathbf{V}_{\infty}(\lambda,y)
and
\mathbf{V}_{0}(\lambda,y)
, and hence also the rational Painlevé-II function
p_{m}(y)
, from the monodromy data, i.e., from the connection matrix
\mathbf{G}_{m}
. To this end, we define a matrix
\mathbf{M}^{m}(\lambda,y)
by

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}^{m}(\lambda,y)=\begin{cases}\mathbf{V}_{\infty}(\lambda,y)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta(\lambda,y))\sigma_{3}}\lambda^{-m\sigma_{3}},&|\lambda|>1,\\
\mathbf{V}_{0}(\lambda,y)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta(\lambda,y)\sigma_{3}}\lambda^{-m\sigma_{3}},&|\lambda|<1.\end{cases}

It is then clear that

solves the following Riemann–Hilbert problem.

Riemann–Hilbert Problem 2.1

(Flaschka–Newell representation)
.

Let

and

Jump condition.

where the limit may be taken in any direction.

The solution of this Riemann–Hilbert problem exists precisely for those values of

that are not poles of

from the limit (cf. (

\displaystyle p_{m}(y)=2\mathrm{i}\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}\lambda^{1+m}M^{m}_{12}(\lambda,y)=-2\mathrm{i}\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}\lambda^{1-m}M^{m}_{21}(\lambda,y).

(2.12)

Note also that without loss of generality one may take the constant
\alpha

) to be

, simply by re-defining

within the unit circle by multiplication on the right by

. Such a re-definition clearly does not affect

Flaschka and Newell observe that Riemann–Hilbert Problem
2.1
can be solved by reduction to finite-dimensional linear algebra, resulting in determinantal formulae for

equivalent to iterated Bäcklund transformations studied by Airault
[
1
]
. To see this, note that uniqueness of solutions of Riemann–Hilbert Problem
2.1
is an elementary consequence of Liouville’s theorem, so it is sufficient to construct a solution by any means. Now,
\mathbf{M}^{m}(\lambda,y)
necessarily has a convergent Laurent expansion about
\lambda=\infty

\mathbf{M}^{m}(\lambda,y)
as a suitable Laurent polynomial. In fact, assuming without loss of generality that
m\geq 0
, we may suppose that in the domain
|\lambda|>1
the first row of
\mathbf{M}^{m}(\lambda,y)
has the form

\displaystyle M^{m}_{11}(\lambda,y)=\lambda^{-m}+a_{1}(y)\lambda^{-m-1}+\cdots+a_{m-1}(y)\lambda^{1-2m}+a_{m}(y)\lambda^{-2m},

\displaystyle M^{m}_{12}(\lambda,y)=b_{1}(y)\lambda^{m-1}+b_{2}(y)\lambda^{m-2}+\cdots+b_{m-1}(y)\lambda+b_{m}(y).

(2.13)

This ansatz clearly satisfies the necessary analyticity condition for
|\lambda|>1
as well as the normalization condition at
\lambda=\infty
. The jump condition can then be reinterpreted as requiring that the linear combinations

\displaystyle M^{m}_{11+}(\lambda,y):=\frac{1}{2\alpha}\big[M^{m}_{11-}(\lambda,y)+(-1)^{m}\mathrm{e}^{2\mathrm{i}\theta(\lambda,y)}\lambda^{-2m}M^{m}_{12-}(\lambda,y)\big],

\displaystyle M^{m}_{12+}(\lambda,y):=\alpha\big[(-1)^{m+1}\mathrm{e}^{-2\mathrm{i}\theta(\lambda,y)}\lambda^{2m}M^{m}_{11-}(\lambda,y)+M^{m}_{12-}(\lambda,y)\big],

where the boundary values

and

are given by the ansatz (

), both be analytic functions within the unit disk, where the only potential singularity is

. The form of the ansatz automatically guarantees that this is the case for
M_{12+}^{m}(\lambda,y)
, but

has precisely
2m
negative powers of
\lambda
whose coefficients are required to vanish. It is easily seen that this amounts to a square inhomogeneous linear system of equations, explicit in terms of the Taylor coefficients of

and

in the form

. For example, in the case

we require that

\displaystyle M^{2}_{11+}(\lambda,y)=\lambda^{-2}+a_{1}(y)\lambda^{-3}+a_{2}(y)\lambda^{-4}+\mathrm{e}^{2\mathrm{i}\theta(\lambda,y)}\big(b_{1}(y)\lambda^{-3}+b_{2}(y)\lambda^{-4}\big)

\displaystyle\hphantom{M^2_{11+}(\lambda,y)}{}=(a_{2}(y)+b_{2}(y))\lambda^{-4}+(a_{1}(y)+b_{1}(y)+2\mathrm{i}yb_{2}(y))\lambda^{-3}

\displaystyle\hphantom{M^2_{11+}(\lambda,y)=}{}+\big(1+2\mathrm{i}yb_{1}(y)-2y^{2}b_{2}(y)\big)\lambda^{-2}\!+\big({-}2y^{2}b_{1}(y)+4\mathrm{i}\big(1\!-\!\tfrac{1}{3}y^{3}\big)b_{2}(y)\big)\lambda^{-1}\!+\mathcal{O}(1),

where the last term represents a function analytic at
\lambda=0
, be analytic at
\lambda=0

as expected (cf. (

2.2
Jimbo–Miwa representation

In 1981, Jimbo and Miwa
[
23
]
found a representation of the Painlevé-II equation as the compatibility condition for a Lax pair different from that found by Flaschka and Newell. We take Jimbo and Miwa’s linear equations in the form

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathbf{v}}{\partial\zeta}=\mathbf{A}^{\mathrm{JM}}(\zeta,y)\mathbf{v},\qquad\mathbf{A}^{\mathrm{JM}}(\zeta,y):=\begin{bmatrix}-\tfrac{3}{2}\zeta^{2}-3\mathcal{U}\mathcal{V}-\tfrac{1}{2}y&3\mathcal{U}\zeta+\mathcal{W}\\
-3\mathcal{V}\zeta-\mathcal{Z}&\tfrac{3}{2}\zeta^{2}+3\mathcal{U}\mathcal{V}+\tfrac{1}{2}y\end{bmatrix}

(2.14)

and

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathbf{v}}{\partial y}=\mathbf{B}^{\mathrm{JM}}(\zeta,y)\mathbf{v},\qquad\mathbf{B}^{\mathrm{JM}}(\zeta,y):=\begin{bmatrix}-\tfrac{1}{2}\zeta&\mathcal{U}\\
-\mathcal{V}&\tfrac{1}{2}\zeta\end{bmatrix}

(2.15)

For this system, the compatibility condition (
2.3
) with
\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{A}^{\mathrm{JM}}
and
\mathbf{B}=\mathbf{B}^{\mathrm{JM}}
is equivalent to the following first-order system of equations:

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}^{\prime}(y)=-\tfrac{1}{3}\mathcal{W}(y),

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}^{\prime}(y)=\tfrac{1}{3}\mathcal{Z}(y),

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}^{\prime}(y)=6\mathcal{U}(y)^{2}\mathcal{V}(y)+y\mathcal{U}(y),

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}(y)=-6\mathcal{U}(y)\mathcal{V}(y)^{2}-y\mathcal{V}(y).

(2.16)

This system admits a first integral

\displaystyle m:=\mathcal{U}(y)\mathcal{Z}(y)+\mathcal{V}(y)\mathcal{W}(y)+\tfrac{1}{2}=\text{const},

(2.17)

and then with

the system (
2.16
) yields the Painlevé-II equation for
p(y)
in the form (
1.1
).

As with the Flaschka–Newell approach, it is the problem (

determines the monodromy data, which is then independent of

for simultaneous solutions of (
2.14
)–(

). However, the direct monodromy problem (

) has a different character than in the Flaschka–Newell approach because (

) has only one singular point, an irregular singular point at infinity, while (
2.1
) has in addition a regular singular point at the origin if

. Thus, all solutions of (
2.14
) are entire functions of
\zeta
, and all monodromy data is generated only from the Stokes phenemonon about the singular point at infinity. In particular, it is the case that for the rational solution
p=p_{m}(y)
for
m\in\mathbb{Z}
, solutions of (
2.14
) exhibit nontrivial Stokes phenomenon in contrast to the situation in Flaschka–Newell theory.

The Stokes multipliers for (
2.14
) when
p=p_{m}(y)
is the rational solution of (
1.1
) for

can be inferred from the following Riemann–Hilbert problem, which arises naturally in the study of solutions of the sine-Gordon equation

2.3
Bertola–Bothner representation

In
[
5
]
, Bertola and Bothner derived a new Hankel determinant representation of the squares of the Yablonskii–Vorob’ev polynomials
\{Q_{n}(z)\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
defined by the recurrence relation (
1.7
) with initial conditions
Q_{0}(z)=1
and
Q_{1}(z)=z
. This new identity leads to a formula expressing the rational Painlevé-II function
p_{m}(y)
in terms of pseudo-orthogonal polynomials (i.e., polynomials orthogonal with respect to an indefinite inner product involving contour integration with a complex-valued weight), and this in turn leads to a Riemann–Hilbert representation.

The main theorem reported and proved in
[
5
]
is the following.

Theorem 2.3

(Bertola and Bothner,
[
5
]
)
.

Given
z\in\mathbb{C}
, let
\{\mu_{k}(z)\}_{k=0}^{\infty}
denote the Taylor coefficients of the generating function
f(t):=\mathrm{e}^{tz-\tfrac{1}{3}t^{3}}
:

\displaystyle\mathrm{e}^{tz-\tfrac{1}{3}t^{3}}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\mu_{k}(z)t^{k},\qquad(z,t)\in\mathbb{C}^{2}.

Then, for any
n\geq 1
,

\displaystyle Q_{n-1}(z)^{2}=(-1)^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\frac{{D}_{n}(z)}{2^{n-1}}\prod_{k=1}^{n-1}\left[\frac{(2k)!}{k!}\right]^{2},

where
\lfloor u\rfloor
denotes the greatest integer less than or equal to
u
and
{D}_{n}(z)
is the Hankel determinant

\displaystyle{D}_{n}(z):=\det[\mu_{l+j-2}(z)]_{l,j=1}^{n}.

The coefficients
\mu_{k}(z)
are polynomials with numerous special properties, some of which are enumerated in
[
5
]
. Similar determinantal representations of the Yablonskii–Vorob’ev polynomials themselves (not the squares) had been previously known
[
26
]
, including one representing
Q_{n}(z)
via a non-Hankel determinant involving the scaled functions
\mu_{k}\big(4^{1/3}z\big)
and one representing
Q_{n}(z)
as a Hankel determinant built from functions that can be extracted from a generating function via a non-convergent asymptotic series
[
21
]
. However, it is the combination of the Hankel structure of the determinant with the convergent nature of the generating function expansion that leads to a Riemann–Hilbert representation of
p_{m}(y)
as we will now explain.

When combined with Theorem
2.3
, the representation (
1.8
) of
p_{m}(y)
in terms of the Yablonskii–Vorob’ev polynomials gives

\displaystyle p_{m}(y)=\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}y}\ln(\eta_{m}((\tfrac{2}{3})^{1/3}y)),\qquad\eta_{m}(z):=\frac{{D}_{m+1}(z)}{{D}_{m}(z)},\qquad m=1,2,3,\dots.

(2.20)

Now, since the polynomials
\{\mu_{k}(z)\}_{k=0}^{\infty}
are Taylor coefficients of the entire function
f(t)=\mathrm{e}^{tz-\tfrac{1}{3}t^{3}}
, they may be written as contour integrals using the Cauchy integral formula:

\displaystyle\mu_{k}(z)=\left.\frac{1}{k!}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{k}}{\mathrm{d}t^{k}}\mathrm{e}^{tz-\tfrac{1}{3}t^{3}}\right|_{t=0}=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\oint\nolimits_{C}t^{-k-1}\mathrm{e}^{tz-\tfrac{1}{3}t^{3}}\,\mathrm{d}t,\qquad k=0,1,2,3,\dots.

Here
C
is a simple contour encircling the origin in the counterclockwise direction; without loss of generality we will take it to coincide with the unit circle. Setting
t=\xi^{-1}
in the integrand puts the formula in the equivalent form

\displaystyle\mu_{k}(z)=\oint\nolimits_{C}\xi^{k}\,\mathrm{d}\nu(\xi;z),\qquad k=0,1,2,3,\dots,

where
C
may be taken to be the same contour, and where

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\nu(\xi;z):=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\tfrac{1}{3}\xi^{-3}+\xi^{-1}z}}{2\pi\mathrm{i}\xi}\,\mathrm{d}\xi.

Thus,
\{\mu_{k}(z)\}_{k=0}^{\infty}
are revealed as the monomial moments of a complex-valued weight parametrized by
z\in\mathbb{C}
and defined on the unit circle. This fact immediately gives an interpretation to the ratio
\eta_{m}(z)
of consecutive Hankel determinants (cf. (
2.20
)); it is the
norming constant
of the monic
pseudo-orthogonal polynomial

\psi_{m}(\xi;z)=\xi^{m}+c_{m,m-1}(z)\xi^{m-1}+\cdots+c_{m,1}(z)\xi+c_{m,0}(z)
defined given
z\in\mathbb{C}
by the pseudo-orthogonality conditions

\displaystyle\oint\nolimits_{C}\psi_{m}(\xi;z)\xi^{j}\,\mathrm{d}\nu(\xi;z)=0,\qquad j=0,1,2,\dots,m-1.

(2.21)

Indeed, if
\psi_{m}(\xi;z)
exists
2
2

2

Existence is not guaranteed for every
z\in\mathbb{C}
because integration against
\mathrm{d}\nu(\xi;z)
does not define a definite inner product, nor does (
2.21
) represent Hermitian orthogonality which would require replacing
\xi^{j}
with its complex conjugate. Hence the terminology of “pseudo-orthogonality”.
for the given value of
z\in\mathbb{C}
then it follows that

\displaystyle\eta_{m}(z)=\oint\nolimits_{C}\psi_{m}(\xi;z)\xi^{m}\,\mathrm{d}\nu(\xi;z).

(2.22)

The points
y\in\mathbb{C}
where either
\psi_{m}\big(\xi;\big(\tfrac{2}{3}\big)^{1/3}y\big)
fails to exist or
\eta_{m}\big(\big(\tfrac{2}{3}\big)^{1/3}y\big)=0
(but possibly not both, should cancellation occur) are precisely the poles of

.

Now, it is well-known that given any complex measure on a suitable contour, the corresponding pseudo-orthogonal polynomial of degree

can be characterized via the solution of a matrix Riemann–Hilbert problem of Fokas–Its–Kitaev type

Riemann–Hilbert Problem 2.4

(Bertola–Bothner representation)
.

Let
m\geq 0
be an integer, and let

be given. Seek a
2\times 2

\mathbf{Y}^{m}(\xi,z)
defined for
\xi\in\mathbb{C}
,

•

Analyticity.

\mathbf{Y}^{m}(\xi,z)
is analytic for
|\xi|\neq 1
, taking continuous boundary values
\mathbf{Y}^{m}_{+}(\xi,z)
and
\mathbf{Y}^{m}_{-}(\xi,z)
for

from the interior and exterior respectively of the unit circle.

•

Jump condition.
The boundary values are related by

\displaystyle\mathbf{Y}^{m}_{+}(\xi,z)=\mathbf{Y}^{m}_{-}(\xi,z)\begin{bmatrix}1&\nu^{\prime}(\xi;z)\\
0&1\end{bmatrix},\qquad|\xi|=1,

\displaystyle\nu^{\prime}(\xi;z):=\frac{\mathrm{d}\nu(\xi;z)}{\mathrm{d}\xi}=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\tfrac{1}{3}\xi^{-3}+z\xi^{-1}}}{2\pi\mathrm{i}\xi}.

(2.23)

•

The matrix
\mathbf{Y}^{m}(\xi,z)
is normalized at

\displaystyle\lim_{\xi\to\infty}\mathbf{Y}^{m}(\xi,z)\xi^{-m\sigma_{3}}=\mathbb{I},

where the limit may be taken in any direction.

Indeed, all of the relevant quantities associated with the pseudo-orthogonal polynomials for the weight

are encoded in the solution of this problem. In particular,

\displaystyle Y^{m}_{11}(\xi,z)=\psi_{m}(\xi;z)\qquad\text{and}\qquad Y^{m}_{12}(\xi,z)=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\oint\nolimits_{C}\frac{\psi_{m}(w;z)\,\mathrm{d}\nu(w;z)}{w-\xi},

from which it follows (cf. (
2.21

2.22

\displaystyle\eta_{m}(z)=-2\pi\mathrm{i}\lim_{\xi\to\infty}\xi^{m+1}Y_{12}^{m}(\xi,z).

Asymptotic analysis of the pseudo-orthogonal polynomials
\psi_{m}(\xi;z)
in the limit of large

2.4

[
14
]
and in the case of true orthogonality on the unit circle in
[
2
]
. However, noting that the expression (
2.20

\mathbf{Y}^{m}(\xi,z)
of Riemann–Hilbert Problem
2.4
. The essence of the argument is as follows. The related matrix
\mathbf{N}^{m}(\xi,z):=\mathbf{Y}^{m}(\xi,z)\mathrm{e}^{z\xi^{-1}\sigma_{3}/2}
must be analytic for
\xi\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
and satisfies jump condition across the unit circle of exactly the form (
2.23
) in which
z
has been replaced by
z=0
. As the parameter
z
no longer appears in the jump matrix for
\mathbf{N}^{m}(\xi,z)
, it follows that the partial derivative
\mathbf{N}_{z}^{m}(\xi,z)
also satisfies exactly the same jump condition, and therefore the matrix ratio
\mathbf{N}_{z}^{m}(\xi,z)\mathbf{N}^{m}(\xi,z)^{-1}
has no jump and so extends to an analytic function on the punctured complex plane
\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
. The asymptotic behavior of
\mathbf{N}_{z}^{m}(\xi,z)\mathbf{N}^{m}(\xi,z)^{-1}
for large and small
\xi
is easily expressed in terms of
\mathbf{Y}^{m}(\xi,z)
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{N}_{z}^{m}(\xi,z)\mathbf{N}^{m}(\xi,z)^{-1}=\begin{cases}\big(\mathbf{Y}^{m\prime}_{1}(z)+\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{3}\big)\xi^{-1}+\mathcal{O}\big(\xi^{-2}\big),&\xi\to\infty,\\
\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{Y}^{m}(0,z)\sigma_{3}\mathbf{Y}^{m}(0,z)^{-1}\xi^{-1}+\mathcal{O}(1),&\xi\to 0,\end{cases}

where
\mathbf{Y}^{m}(\xi,z)\xi^{-m\sigma_{3}}=\mathbb{I}+\mathbf{Y}_{1}^{m}(z)\xi^{-1}+\mathcal{O}(\xi^{-2})
as
\xi\to\infty
. Therefore
\mathbf{N}_{z}^{m}(\xi,z)\mathbf{N}^{m}(\xi,z)^{-1}
is a
z
-dependent multiple of
\xi^{-1}
given by two equivalent formulae:

\displaystyle\mathbf{N}_{z}^{m}(\xi,z)\mathbf{N}^{m}(\xi,z)^{-1}=\big(\mathbf{Y}^{m\prime}_{1}(z)+\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma_{3}\big)\xi^{-1}=\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{Y}^{m}(0,z)\sigma_{3}\mathbf{Y}^{m}(0,z)^{-1}\xi^{-1}.

From the
(1,2)
-entry in this matrix identity one obtains

\displaystyle\eta_{m}^{\prime}(z)=-2\pi\mathrm{i}Y^{m\prime}_{1,12}(z)=2\pi\mathrm{i}Y_{11}^{m}(0,z)Y_{21}^{m}(0,z),\qquad m=0,1,2,\dots,

where we have used the fact that the necessarily unique solution of Riemann–Hilbert Problem
2.4
has unit determinant. Therefore, from the solution of Riemann–Hilbert Problem
2.4
the rational Painlevé-II function
p_{m}(y)
can be expressed without differentiation with respect to
z
as

\displaystyle p_{m}(y)=-\frac{Y_{11}^{m}(0,z)Y_{12}^{m}(0,z)}{12^{1/3}Y_{1,12}^{m}(z)},\qquad z=\left(\frac{2}{3}\right)^{1/3}y,

\displaystyle\mathbf{Y}^{m}_{1}(z):=\lim_{\xi\to\infty}\xi\big(\mathbf{Y}^{m}(\xi,z)\xi^{-m\sigma_{3}}-\mathbb{I}\big),

(2.24)

for
m=0,1,2,\dots
.

2.4
Explicit relation between the Flaschka–Newell

and Bertola–Bothner representations

The Riemann–Hilbert representations of the rational Painlevé-II functions appearing in the isomonodromy approaches of Flaschka–Newell (cf. Section
2.1
) and Jimbo–Miwa (cf. Section
2.2
) are known to be related. Indeed, Joshi, Kitaev, and Treharne found an explicit integral transform relating simultaneous solutions of the corresponding Lax pairs
[
25
, Corollary 3.2]
. This integral transform provides another explanation for the fact that the solution of Riemann–Hilbert Problem
2.1
is rational in
\lambda
while that of Riemann–Hilbert Problem
2.2
is transcendental in
\zeta
, being built from Airy functions
[
7
]
. The approach of Bertola–Bothner also leads to a Riemann–Hilbert representation of the rational Painlevé-II functions, but the approach is not motivated by isomonodromy theory for any Lax pair, so it seems more mysterious from this point of view. In this section we show that the Riemann–Hilbert problem appearing in the Bertola–Bothner approach is in fact explicitly connected to that arising in the Flaschka–Newell isomonodromy theory:

Theorem 2.5
.

Let
m\geq 0
be an integer, suppose that
y\in\mathbb{C}
is not a pole of the rational
Painlevé-II
function
p_{m}(y)
, and let
z=\big(\tfrac{2}{3}\big)^{1/3}y
. Then the unique solution
\mathbf{M}^{m}(\lambda,y)
of Riemann–Hilbert Problem
2.1
arising from Flaschka–Newell theory is related to the unique solution
\mathbf{Y}^{m}(\xi,z)
of Riemann–Hilbert Problem
2.4
arising from the Bertola–Bothner approach by an explicit elementary transformation with an explicit elementary inverse
(
cf. equations (
2.25
)–(
2.27
), (
2.29
), (
2.30
), (
2.34
), and (
2.36
) in the proof below
)
.

Proof.

We start with the Flaschka–Newell approach and Riemann–Hilbert Problem
2.1
. Suppose without loss of generality that
m=1,2,3,\dots
. We begin by noting that the matrix
\mathbf{G}_{m}^{-1}
defined by (
2.11
) has the lower-upper factorization

\displaystyle\mathbf{G}_{m}^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}(2\alpha)^{-1}&(-1)^{m+1}\alpha\\
(-1)^{m}(2\alpha)^{-1}&\alpha\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
(-1)^{m}&1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}(2\alpha)^{-1}&(-1)^{m+1}\alpha\\
0&2\alpha\end{bmatrix},

and therefore the jump matrix in Riemann–Hilbert Problem
2.1
is

\displaystyle\lambda^{m\sigma_{3}}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\theta(\lambda,y)\sigma_{3}}\mathbf{G}_{m}^{-1}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta(\lambda,y)\sigma_{3}}\lambda^{-m\sigma_{3}}

\displaystyle\qquad{}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
(-1)^{m}\lambda^{-2m}\mathrm{e}^{2\mathrm{i}\theta(\lambda,y)}&1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}(2\alpha)^{-1}&(-1)^{m+1}\alpha\lambda^{2m}\mathrm{e}^{-2\mathrm{i}\theta(\lambda,y)}\\
0&2\alpha\end{bmatrix},

and the right-hand factor is obviously analytic within the unit disk and has unit determinant. Therefore, defining a new matrix
\mathbf{P}^{m}(\lambda,y)
in terms of the unknown
\mathbf{M}^{m}(\lambda,y)
by

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}^{m}(\lambda,y):=\begin{cases}\mathbf{M}^{m}(\lambda,y),&|\lambda|>1,\\
\mathbf{M}^{m}(\lambda,y)\begin{bmatrix}(2\alpha)^{-1}&(-1)^{m+1}\alpha\lambda^{2m}\mathrm{e}^{-2\mathrm{i}\theta(\lambda,y)}\\
0&2\alpha\end{bmatrix}^{-1},&|\lambda|<1,\end{cases}

(2.25)

we see that
\mathbf{P}^{m}(\lambda,y)
satisfies exactly the same conditions as specified in Riemann–Hilbert Problem
2.1
except that the jump condition across the unit circle becomes instead

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}^{m}_{+}(\lambda,y)=\mathbf{P}^{m}_{-}(\lambda,y)\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
(-1)^{m}\lambda^{-2m}\mathrm{e}^{2\mathrm{i}\theta(\lambda,y)}&1\end{bmatrix},\qquad|\lambda|=1.

(2.26)

This triangular jump matrix already suggests the Fokas–Its–Kitaev form that appears in the approach of Bertola and Bothner, but we require two more steps to complete the identification. Firstly, we make the simple substitution

\displaystyle\mathbf{Q}^{m}(\xi,z):=k^{\sigma_{3}}\sigma_{1}\mathbf{P}^{m}\big(0,\big(\tfrac{3}{2}\big)^{1/3}z\big)^{-1}\mathbf{P}^{m}\big(c\xi^{-1},\big(\tfrac{3}{2}\big)^{1/3}z\big)\xi^{-m\sigma_{3}}\sigma_{1}k^{-\sigma_{3}},

(2.27)

where

\displaystyle c:=-\mathrm{i}\cdot 12^{-1/3}\qquad\text{and}\qquad k:=\frac{\mathrm{i}^{m+1}c^{m}}{\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\pi/4}\sqrt{2\pi}}.

Now observe that the following Riemann–Hilbert problem captures at the same time the matrix
\mathbf{Q}^{m}(\xi,z)
and the matrix
\mathbf{Y}^{m}(\xi,z)
appearing in the Bertola–Bothner approach, for different values of the auxiliary parameter
j\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Riemann–Hilbert Problem 2.6
.

Let
m\in\mathbb{Z}
,
j\in\mathbb{Z}
, and
z\in\mathbb{C}
be given. Seek a
2\times 2
matrix-valued function
\mathbf{C}^{m,j}(\xi,z)
defined for
\xi\in\mathbb{C}
,
|\xi|\neq 1
, with the following properties:

•

Analyticity.

\mathbf{C}^{m,j}(\xi,z)
is analytic for
|\xi|\neq 1
, taking continuous boundary values
\mathbf{C}^{m,j}_{+}(\xi,z)
and
\mathbf{C}^{m,j}_{-}(\xi,z)
for
|\xi|=1
from the interior and exterior respectively of the unit circle.

•

Jump condition.
The boundary values are related by

\displaystyle\mathbf{C}^{m,j}_{+}(\xi,z)=\mathbf{C}^{m,j}_{-}(\xi,z)\begin{bmatrix}1&\xi^{j}\nu^{\prime}(\xi;z)\\
0&1\end{bmatrix},\qquad|\xi|=1,

\displaystyle\nu^{\prime}(\xi;z)=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\tfrac{1}{3}\xi^{-3}+z\xi^{-1}}}{2\pi\mathrm{i}\xi}.

(2.28)

•

Normalization.
The matrix
\mathbf{C}^{m,j}(\xi,z)
is normalized at
\xi=\infty
as follows:

\displaystyle\lim_{\xi\to\infty}\mathbf{C}^{m,j}(\xi,z)\xi^{-m\sigma_{3}}=\mathbb{I},

where the limit may be taken in any direction.

Indeed, it is easy to check that

\displaystyle\mathbf{Q}^{m}(\xi,z)=\mathbf{C}^{m,1}(\xi,z)\qquad\text{and, for $m\geq 0$,}\qquad\mathbf{Y}^{m}(\xi,z)=\mathbf{C}^{m,0}(\xi,z)

(2.29)

by comparison with the conditions of Riemann–Hilbert Problems
2.1
and
2.4
. We complete the connection between the Flaschka–Newell and Bertola–Bothner approaches by next establishing the relation between solutions
\mathbf{C}^{m,j}(\xi,z)
for consecutive values of
j\in\mathbb{Z}
.

The solution
\mathbf{C}^{m,j}(\xi,z)
of Riemann–Hilbert Problem
2.6
has a convergent Laurent expansion for large
|\xi|
of the form

\displaystyle\mathbf{C}^{m,j}(\xi,z)=\big(\mathbb{I}+\mathbf{R}^{m,j}(z)\xi^{-1}+\mathcal{O}\big(\xi^{-2}\big)\big)\xi^{m\sigma_{3}},\qquad\xi\to\infty

(2.30)

for some residue matrix
\mathbf{R}^{m,j}(z)
. Noting that if it exists for a given
z\in\mathbb{C}
, the unique solution of Riemann–Hilbert Problem
2.6
has unit determinant, consider the matrix
\widecheck{\mathbf{E}}(\xi,z)
defined by

\displaystyle\widecheck{\mathbf{E}}(\xi,z):=\mathbf{C}^{m,j}(\xi,z)\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&\xi\end{bmatrix}\mathbf{C}^{m,j+1}(\xi,z)^{-1},\qquad|\xi|\neq 1.

(2.31)

It is straightforward to check from (
2.28
) that the boundary values taken by
\widecheck{\mathbf{E}}(\xi,z)
on the unit circle satisfy the trivial jump condition
\widecheck{\mathbf{E}}_{+}(\xi,z)=\widecheck{\mathbf{E}}_{-}(\xi,z)
for
|\xi|=1
; hence
\widecheck{\mathbf{E}}(\xi,z)
extends to the whole complex plane as an entire function of
\xi
. Moreover, using (
2.30
) it follows that
\widecheck{\mathbf{E}}(\xi,z)
has the following asymptotic expansion for large
\xi
:

\displaystyle\widecheck{\mathbf{E}}(\xi,z)=\big(\mathbb{I}+\mathbf{R}^{m,j}(z)\xi^{-1}+\mathcal{O}\big(\xi^{-2}\big)\big)\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&\xi\end{bmatrix}\big(\mathbb{I}-\mathbf{R}^{m,j+1}(z)\xi^{-1}+\mathcal{O}\big(\xi^{-2}\big)\big)

\displaystyle\hphantom{\widecheck{\mathbf{E}}(\xi,z)}{}=\begin{bmatrix}1&R_{12}^{m,j}(z)\\
-R_{21}^{m,j+1}(z)&\xi+R_{22}^{m,j}(z)-R_{22}^{m,j+1}(z)\end{bmatrix}+\mathcal{O}\big(\xi^{-1}\big),\qquad\xi\to\infty.

(2.32)

It then follows by Liouville’s theorem that all negative power terms in the Laurent expansion of
\widecheck{\mathbf{E}}(\xi,z)
vanish, i.e.,
\widecheck{\mathbf{E}}(\xi,z)
is the linear function of
\xi
given by the explicit matrix on the second line of (
2.32
). Returning to (
2.31
), we have established the identity

\displaystyle\mathbf{C}^{m,j}(\xi,z)\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&\xi\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}1&R_{12}^{m,j}(z)\\
-R_{21}^{m,j+1}(z)&\xi+R_{22}^{m,j}(z)-R_{22}^{m,j+1}(z)\end{bmatrix}\mathbf{C}^{m,j+1}(\xi,z),

\displaystyle|\xi|\neq 1.

(2.33)

If we can express the second column of
\mathbf{R}^{m,j}(z)
in terms of
\mathbf{C}^{m,j+1}(\xi,z)
, then this becomes an explicit formula for
\mathbf{C}^{m,j}(\xi,z)
in terms of the latter.

To this end, consider the second column of (
2.33
) evaluated at
\xi=0
, which reads

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}0\\
0\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}C^{m,j+1}_{12}(0,z)+R_{12}^{m,j}(z)C^{m,j+1}_{22}(0,z)\\
-R_{21}^{m,j+1}(z)C^{m,j+1}_{12}(0,z)+(R_{22}^{m,j}(z)-R_{22}^{m,j+1}(z))C^{m,j+1}_{22}(0,z)\end{bmatrix}

because
\mathbf{C}^{m,j}(\xi,z)
and
\mathbf{C}^{m,j+1}(\xi,z)
are analytic at
z=0
. Therefore,

\displaystyle R_{12}^{m,j}(z)=-\frac{C_{12}^{m,j+1}(0,z)}{C_{22}^{m,j+1}(0,z)}\qquad\text{and}\qquad R_{22}^{m,j}(z)=R_{22}^{m,j+1}(z)+\frac{C_{12}^{m,j+1}(0,z)}{C_{22}^{m,j+1}(0,z)}R_{21}^{m,j+1}(z),

so substituting into (
2.33
) we recover the explicit formula for decrementing the value of
j
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{C}^{m,j}(\xi,z)=\widecheck{\mathbf{E}}(\xi,z)\mathbf{C}^{m,j+1}(\xi,z)\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&\xi^{-1}\end{bmatrix},\qquad\text{where}

\displaystyle\widecheck{\mathbf{E}}(\xi,z)=\begin{bmatrix}1&-C_{12}^{m,j+1}(0,z)C_{22}^{m,j+1}(0,z)^{-1}\\
-R_{21}^{m,j+1}(z)&\xi+R_{21}^{m,j+1}(z)C_{12}^{m,j+1}(0,z)C_{22}^{m,j+1}(0,z)^{-1}\end{bmatrix}.

(2.34)

In a similar way, the matrix

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbf{E}}(\xi,z):=\mathbf{C}^{m,j+1}(\xi,z)\begin{bmatrix}\xi&0\\
0&1\end{bmatrix}\mathbf{C}^{m,j}(\xi,z)^{-1}

is an entire function that equals the polynomial part of its Laurent expansion for large
\xi
, and hence

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbf{E}}(\xi,z)=\begin{bmatrix}\xi+R_{11}^{m,j+1}(z)-R_{11}^{m,j}(z)&-R_{12}^{m,j}(z)\\
R_{12}^{m,j+1}(z)&1\end{bmatrix},

leading to the following analogue of (
2.33
):

\displaystyle\mathbf{C}^{m,j+1}(\xi,z)\begin{bmatrix}\xi&0\\
0&1\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\xi+R_{11}^{m,j+1}(z)-R_{11}^{m,j}(z)&-R_{12}^{m,j}(z)\\
R_{12}^{m,j+1}(z)&1\end{bmatrix}\mathbf{C}^{m,j}(\xi,z).

(2.35)

From the first column of (
2.35
) evaluated at
\xi=0
we get

\displaystyle R_{12}^{m,j+1}(z)=-\frac{C_{21}^{m,j}(0,z)}{C_{11}^{m,j}(0,z)}\qquad\text{and}\qquad R_{11}^{m,j+1}(z)=R_{11}^{m,j}(z)+\frac{C_{21}^{m,j}(0,z)}{C_{11}^{m,j}(0,z)}R_{12}^{m,j}(z),

so substituting into (
2.35
) we recover the explicit formula for incrementing the value of
j
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{C}^{m,j+1}(\xi,z)=\widehat{\mathbf{E}}(\xi,z)\mathbf{C}^{m,j}(\xi,z)\begin{bmatrix}\xi^{-1}&0\\
0&1\end{bmatrix},\qquad\text{where}

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbf{E}}(\xi,z)=\begin{bmatrix}\xi+R_{12}^{m,j}(z)C_{21}^{m,j}(0,z)C_{11}^{m,j}(0,z)^{-1}&-R_{12}^{m,j}(z)\\
-C_{21}^{m,j}(0,z)C_{11}^{m,j}(0,z)^{-1}&1\end{bmatrix}.

(2.36)

Note that equations (
2.34
) and (
2.36
) can be interpreted as discrete Schlesinger/Darboux transformations (see
[
6
, Section 2]
and
[
23
]
) for Riemann–Hilbert Problem
2.6
.

Taking into account the explicit and obviously invertible transformations (
2.25
)–(
2.27
) relating
\mathbf{M}^{m}(\lambda,y)
solving Riemann–Hilbert Problem
2.1
to
\mathbf{Q}^{m}(\xi,z)=\mathbf{C}^{m,1}(\xi,z)
via
\mathbf{P}^{m}(\lambda,y)
, the formulae (
2.34
) and (
2.36
) establish the connection with Riemann–Hilbert Problem
2.4
having solution
\mathbf{Y}^{m}(\xi,z)=\mathbf{C}^{m,0}(\xi,z)
.
∎

We remark that although Theorem
2.5
provides an explicit relation between the solutions of Riemann–Hilbert Problems
2.1
and
2.4
, it can happen that for given
z\in\mathbb{C}
one of these problems is solvable and the other is not. This occurs precisely when one of the denominators
C_{22}^{m,1}(0,z)
in (
2.34
) or
C_{11}^{m,0}(0,z)
in (
2.36
) vanishes. Indeed, we have mentioned before (and it actually follows from the formula (
2.12
)) that the points
z
where Riemann–Hilbert Problem
2.1
fails to be solvable correspond precisely to the poles of
p_{m}
. On the other hand, the formula (
2.24
) shows that it is possible that some poles of
p_{m}
can arise from the well-defined function
Y^{m}_{1,12}
vanishing at a point
z
where Riemann–Hilbert Problem
2.4
has a solution; hence Riemann–Hilbert Problem
2.4
is solvable while Riemann–Hilbert Problem
2.1
is not. It can also happen that Riemann–Hilbert Problem
2.4
fails to be solvable at a point
z
corresponding to a regular point of
p_{m}
and hence a point of solvability of Riemann–Hilbert Problem
2.1
, in which case the formula (
2.24
) retains sense locally via a limit process (i.e., l’Hôpital’s rule).

3
Asymptotic behavior of the rational Painlevé-II functions

3.1
Numerical observations and heuristic analysis

In this section, we assume without loss of generality that
m\geq 0
.
There have been several studies of the rational solutions
p_{m}(y)
of the Painlevé-II equation from the numerical point of view, mostly concerned with looking for patterns in the distribution of poles of
p_{m}(y)
in the complex
y
-plane as
m
varies. The earliest work in this direction that we are aware of is the 1986 paper of Kametaka et al.
[
27
]
in which numerical methods were brought to bear on the problem of finding roots of the Yablonskii–Vorob’ev polynomials for
m
as large as
m=37
; the figures in
[
27
]
for the largest values of
m
display features suggesting the breakdown of the numerical method. A figure such as those from
[
27
]
also appears in the 1991 monograph
[
22
]
. These studies show the poles of
p_{m}(y)
being contained for reasonably large
m
within a roughly triangular-shaped region of size increasing with
m
and therein organized in an apparently regular, crystalline pattern. Plots of poles of
p_{m}(y)
obtained by similar methods also appear in
[
12
]
, a paper that includes in addition a study of corresponding phenomena in higher-order equations in the Painlevé-II hierarchy. More recently, general numerical methods for the study of solutions with many poles in differential equations have been advanced based on such techniques as Padé approximation, and these methods have been shown to be capable of accurately reproducing the pole pattern of
p_{m}(y)
, treating the Painlevé-II equation (
1.1
) as an initial-value problem to be solved numerically taking as initial conditions the exact values of
p_{m}(0)
and
p_{m}^{\prime}(0)

[
19
,
33
]
. In Fig.
2
we give our own plots of poles of
p_{m}(y)
for
m=15
,
m=30
, and
m=60
, which we made by symbolically constructing the relevant Yablonskii–Vorob’ev polynomials in
Mathematica
and using
NSolve
with the option
WorkingPrecision->50
to find the roots.

Figure 2:
The poles of residue
1
(blue) and
-1
(red) of

These numerical observations suggest structure that should be explained, and yet the
large-
m
limit in which the structural features of interest appear to become clear in the numerics is fundamentally out of reach of exact methods like iterated Bäcklund transformations or explicit determinantal formulae, the study of which becomes combinatorially prohibitive in this limit. Therefore one may consider instead methods of asymptotic analysis. A formal approach may be based upon the observation that the modulus of the poles or zeros of
p_{m}(y)
most distant from the origin scales roughly like
m^{2/3}

[
22
]
, which suggests examining
p_{m}(y)
in a small neighborhood of a point
y=m^{2/3}x
; dominant balance arguments suggest that the size of the neighborhood should then be proportional to
m^{-1/3}
. So, letting
x\in\mathbb{C}
be fixed, consider the change of independent variable
y\mapsto w
in (
1.1
) given by (the relatively small shifts by
1/2
are convenient for later but at this point are inconsequential)

\displaystyle y=\big(m-\tfrac{1}{2}\big)^{2/3}x+\big(m-\tfrac{1}{2}\big)^{-1/3}w.

Substituting this into (
1.1
) along with the scaling of the independent variable by
p=\big(m-\tfrac{1}{2}\big)^{1/3}\mathcal{P}
, one arrives at the equivalent equation

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathcal{P}}{\mathrm{d}w^{2}}=2\mathcal{P}^{3}+\frac{2x}{3}\mathcal{P}-\frac{2}{3}+\frac{2w\mathcal{P}-1}{3\big(m+\tfrac{1}{2}\big)},

which for large
m
appears to be a perturbation of an autonomous equation for an approximating function
{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}(w)
:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}}{\mathrm{d}w^{2}}=2{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}^{3}+\frac{2x}{3}{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}-\frac{2}{3}.

(3.1)

Multiplying by
\mathrm{d}{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}/\mathrm{d}w
and integrating gives

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}}{\mathrm{d}w}\right)^{2}={\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}^{4}+\frac{2x}{3}{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}^{2}-\frac{4}{3}{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}+\Pi,

(3.2)

where
\Pi
is an integration constant. If
\Pi
and
x
are related in such a way that the quartic polynomial on the right-hand side of (
3.2
) has a double root
{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}_{0}
, then
{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}(w)={\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}_{0}
is an equilibrium solution of (
3.1
). Double roots
{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}_{0}
are necessarily related to
x
via the cubic equation

\displaystyle 3{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}_{0}^{3}+x{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}_{0}-1=0

(3.3)

and then the relation between
\Pi
and
x
guaranteeing the existence of the double root can be expressed in terms of a solution
{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}_{0}={\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}_{0}(x)
of (
3.3
) by

\displaystyle\Pi=\Pi_{0}(x):=2{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}_{0}(x)-\frac{2x}{3}{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}_{0}(x)^{2}.

(3.4)

It turns out (see Section
3.3.1
below) that this approximation of
\mathcal{P}(w)
by the equilibrium solution
{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}_{0}(x)
accurately describes the rational Painlevé-II function
p_{m}(y)
in the pole-free region, provided that one selects the (unique) solution
{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}_{0}(x)
of (
3.3
) with the asymptotic behavior
{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}}_{0}(x)=x^{-1}+\mathcal{O}\big(x^{-2}\big)
as

. This solution has branch points at

and

as functions of the variable

3.3
Asymptotic description of
\boldsymbol{p_{m}(y)}
by steepest descent

We now present several results on the asymptotic behavior of the rational Painlevé-II function
p_{m}(y)
, all of which have been obtained by the application of variants of the Deift–Zhou steepest descent method
[
15
]
to either Riemann–Hilbert Problem
2.2
(see
[
8
,
9
]
) or Riemann–Hilbert Problem
2.4
(see
[
5
]
). Regardless of which Riemann–Hilbert problem is the starting point, the basic steps of the method are the same:

1.

Introduce a diagonal matrix multiplier built from exponentials of a scalar function frequently called a “
g
-function” with the aim of simultaneously obtaining normalization to the identity matrix at infinity and stabilizing the jump matrices of the problem so that they are alternately exponentially small perturbations of either constant matrices or purely oscillatory matrices along different contour arcs. Frequently this step also requires some deformation of the contour of the original Riemann–Hilbert problem by means of analytic continuation of the jump matrices.

2.

Use explicit matrix factorizations to algebraically separate oscillatory factors in the jump matrices having phase derivatives of opposite signs. Splitting the jump contour into separate arcs for each factor, a subsequent deformation to either side of the original jump contour ensures that the oscillatory factors now become exponentially small in the limit
m\to\infty
.

3.

Construct an explicit model of the solution called a “parametrix” by considering only those remaining jump matrices that are not exponentially small perturbations of the identity matrix.

4.

By comparing the unknown matrix obtained after the second step with the parametrix, obtain an equivalent Riemann–Hilbert problem for the matrix quotient. The aim of the method is to ensure that the resulting Riemann–Hilbert problem is of “small-norm” type, meaning that it can be solved by a convergent iterative procedure that also allows for the rigorous estimation of the solution. This analysis proves the accuracy of approximate formulae for the unknowns of interest, such as
p_{m}(y)
, which are extracted from the explicit parametrix.

The steepest descent method gets its name from the second step in the procedure, which resembles the type of contour deformations that one carries out in implementing the steepest descent method for the asymptotic expansion of exponential integrals.

The form of the parametrix that one obtains is determined in most of the complex plane by the number of contour arcs on which the
g
-function induces oscillations. This number is related to the genus of a hyperelliptic Riemann surface whose function theory is exploited to construct the parametrix. As the original Riemann–Hilbert problem depends on a complex parameter
y
, it is to be expected that the genus may be different for different values of
y\in\mathbb{C}
, leading to the phenomenon of phase transitions. Indeed, the boundary of the elliptic region turns out to be exactly such a phase transition. In particular the hyperelliptic curve that characterizes the rational Painlevé-II function
p_{m}(y)
for large
m
when
y
lies outside of the elliptic region has genus zero. An interesting difference between the application of the steepest descent method to the Jimbo–Miwa problem
[
8
,
9
]
and its application to the Bertola–Bothner problem
[
5
]
is that in the former case the curve corresponding to the elliptic region has genus
1
(hence the terminology “elliptic”) while in the latter case it instead has genus
2
(with some symmetries that allow its function theory to be reducible to elliptic functions after all, see
[
5
, Section 4.6]
).

We give no further details of the proofs of the following results, leading the reader to the original references
[
5
,
8
,
9
]
for complete information. We also note that some of the results below have also been captured by the isomonodromy method, a WKB-ansatz based asymptotic approach to Riemann–Hilbert problems
[
28

.

3.3.4
Asymptotic description of
\boldsymbol{p_{m}}
near corners

The Painlevé-I equation
Y^{\prime\prime}(t)=6Y(t)^{2}+t
has a unique
tritronquée
solution with the property that

\displaystyle Y(t)=-\left(\frac{t}{6}\right)^{1/2}+\mathcal{O}\big(t^{-2}\big),\qquad t\to\infty,\qquad|\arg(-t)|\leq\frac{4}{5}\pi-\delta

(3.8)

for every
\delta>0
; see Kapaev
[
29
]
. Thus the tritronquée solution
Y(t)
is asymptotically pole-free in a sector of opening angle
4\pi/5
. It has recently been proven
[
13
]
that in fact
Y(t)
is exactly pole-free for
|\arg(-t)|\leq 4\pi/5
without any condition on
|t|
. This is the particular solution of the Painlevé-I equation appearing in the formal analysis described in Section
3.1
that is needed to describe the rational Painlevé-II functions near corner points of
T
as the following result shows. Recall that
x_{\mathrm{c}}:=-(9/2)^{2/3}
is the corner point of
T
on the negative real axis.

Theorem 3.6

(Buckingham & Miller
[
9
, Theorem 3]
)
.

Let
Y(t)
be the tritronquée solution of the Painlevé-I equation determined by the asymptotic expansion (
3.8
). If
\mathcal{K}
is any compact set in the complex
t
-plane that does not contain any poles of
Y(t)
, then

\displaystyle m^{-1/3}p_{m}\big(\big(m-\tfrac{1}{2}\big)^{2/3}x\big)=-6^{-1/3}-\frac{1}{m^{2/5}}\left(\frac{128}{243}\right)^{1/15}Y(t)+\mathcal{O}\big(m^{-3/5}\big)

holds as
m\to\infty
uniformly for

\displaystyle t:=\left(\frac{2}{243}\right)^{1/15}m^{4/5}(x-x_{\mathrm{c}})\in\mathcal{K}.

This result is interesting in part because
p_{m}(y)
is a function with simple poles only, and the approximating function

is known to have double poles only. What actually happens in the limit

merge into the “holes” excluded from
\mathcal{K}
located near the double poles of

[

]
