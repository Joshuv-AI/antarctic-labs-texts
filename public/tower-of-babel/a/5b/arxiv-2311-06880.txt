Deadbeat Robust Model Predictive Control: Robustness without Computing Robust Invariant Sets
Georg Schildbach

Abstract
Deadbeat Robust Model Predictive Control (DRMPC) is introduced as a new approach of Robust Model Predictive Control (RMPC) for linear systems with additive disturbances. Its main idea is to completely extinguish the effect of the disturbances in the predictions within a small number of time steps, called the deadbeat horizon. To this end, explicit deadbeat input sequences are calculated for the vertices of the disturbance set. They generalize to a nonlinear disturbance feedback policy for all disturbances by means of a barycentric function. Similar to previous approaches, this disturbance feedback policy can be either part of the online optimization (Online DRMPC) or pre-calculated during the design phase of the controller (Offline DRMPC). The main advantage over all other RMPC approaches is that no Robust Positive Invariant (RPI) set has to be calculated, which is often intractable for systems with higher dimensions. Nonetheless, for Online DRMPC and Offline DRMPC recursive feasibility and input-to-state stability can be guaranteed. A small numerical example compares the two versions of DRMPC and demonstrates that the performance of DRMPC is competitive with other state-of-the-art RMPC approaches. Its main advantage is its easy extension to linear time-varying (LTV) and linear parameter-varying (LPV) systems.



I
Introduction

Over the past decade, Model Predictive Control (MPC) has developed into one of the most important methods of advanced control. Practical applications have revealed imperfections of nominal MPC regarding different types of uncertainties, such as disturbances, unknown system parameters, and general model mismatch
[
13
]
. In many cases, these uncertainties are not negligible for control design. Even though nominal MPC is known to possess a natural level of robustness
[
5
,
10
]
, an important focus has developed on Robust MPC (RMPC). RMPC is often able to successfully robustify the controller against these types of uncertainty.

Numerous Robust MPC (RMPC) formulations have therefore been proposed. The most common approach is reformulate the actual system as a linear control system with persistent, additive disturbances inside a bounded set
[
2
,
4
]
. The disturbance set is constructed by worst-case approximations of the actual uncertainty. The main control task is then to keep the states and the inputs of the linear system inside their respective constraint sets, at all times and for all possible realizations of the uncertainty, which also constitutes the basis for this paper.

Literature Review

Several approaches for solving the RMPC problem have been proposed in the literature
[
17
]
. Parts of them deal, often quite successfully, with uncertainty in an ad-hoc manner. However, the focus here is only on those with system-theoretic guarantees of recursive feasibility and stability.

One of the most popular approaches fall under Tube-based RMPC (Tube-RMPC)
[
14
,
16
,
17
]
. The main idea is to find a linear feedback gain that stabilizes the system and a corresponding minimal robust positive invariant (mRPI) set for the state
[
15
]
. The control input is split into two parts: one that maintains system state within the mRPI set and one that drives the center of these sets to the origin. The main problems of Tube-RMPC lie in the (usually sub-optimal) selection of the linear feedback gain and the computation (or rather approximation) of the mRPI set.

A second category of RMPC approaches involve a maximal robust positive invariant (MRPI) set. These approaches typically show a slightly lower performance than Tube-RMPC, in terms of the size of the region of attraction. However, the MRPI set has practical advantages over the mRPI set, because exact algorithms are available for its computation and it is tractable even for higher state dimensions
[
9
]
. A reasonable performance requires closed-loop predictions
[
1
]
. For this to be computationally tractable, piecewise-affine feedback policies are generally used
[
11
]
, and they should be based on disturbances rather than states (Affine Disturbance Feedback, ADF)
[
7
]
.

A third category of approaches is Minimax RMPC
[
19
]
, which is concerned with minimizing the maximal cost over all possible disturbances. Its finite scenario tree is based on the extremal realizations of the disturbances over the prediction horizon. However, this approach suffers from exponential complexity with growing prediction horizon
[
11
]
.

Contributions

Many refinements of the above RMPC approaches have been proposed in the last decade. In this paper, a completely new approach is explored, called Deadbeat Robust Model Predictive Control (DRMPC). It bears some similarities, but also some differences with each of the three categories. The rationale is that DRMPC offers benefits for some particular applications, and may serve as a useful basis for control problems that remain challenging today, like linear time-varying (LTV) and linear parameter-varying (LPV), but also nonlinear and large-scale systems.

Coming from Minimax MPC
[
19
,
11
]
, DRMPC introduces a second horizon, significantly smaller than the prediction horizon, over which the effect of the disturbances is completely extinguished (see Section
II
). For a controllable linear system, this so-called
deadbeat horizon
can be in the order of the number of states, so that the combinatorial growth of the scenario tree remains manageable. In contrast to Minimax MPC approaches, an exact solution may hence be possible.

Compared to ADF, DRMPC is not restricted to affine feedback policies in the predictions. Instead, it features a full nonlinear feedback policy based on a barycentric function. In terms of the feasibility of linear MPC with convex constraints, this is the most general class of nonlinear policies that one can wish for. Similar to ADF, DRMPC allows to solve for the optimal feedback policies online (‘Online DRMPC’), while retaining convexity of the optimal control problem (see Section
III
). Yet ADF requires even a Robust Control Invariant (RCI) set for this purpose
[
7
]
. Alternatively, the feedback policies in DRMPC can also be pre-computed offline (‘Offline DRMPC’). Then the solution is sub-optimal, but it reduces the computational complexity of DRMPC to approximately the same level as nominal MPC. The Online DRMPC and Offline DRMPC approaches both come with full guarantees of recursive feasibility and stability (see Section
IV
).

Similar to Tube-RMPC, DRMPC also splits the control input into two parts: one that compensates the disturbances and one that drives the state to the origin on a bigger scale. Unlike Tube-RMPC, however, DRMPC does not require the a priori selection of a stabilizing feedback gain. In fact, a linear feedback gain is not required at all. Instead, the feedback policy and hence the exact split of the control input is computed optimally. Furthermore, DRMPC does not require the a priori computation of an mRPI set for the tube section. For the terminal set, a (non-robust) positive invariant (PI) set suffices, e.g., simply the origin. This is of practical relevance because the origin is a popular choice especially for large scale systems, where (robust) positive invariant sets are too complex or computationally intractable. Even more importantly, this setup makes the extension of DRMPC to the robust control of LTV and LPV systems straightforward.

II-B

Control System

Consider the discrete-time (DT), linear-time invariant (LTI) control system with additive disturbances

x_{k+1}=Ax_{k}+Bu_{k}+d_{k}\;.

(1)

Here
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{0+}
denotes the time step,
x_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
u_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
are the state and the input and

is the disturbance at time step
k
, respectively. System (
8
) comes with a given initial condition
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Assumption 1
(control system)

(a) The state of the system
x_{k}
can be measured in each step
k
. (b) The system matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
and input matrix
B\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
form a controllable pair.

The control objective is to regulate the state of system (
8
) to the origin, while respecting state and input constraints:

x_{k}\in\mathbb{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}\;,\kern 5.0ptu_{k}\in\mathbb{U}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}\quad\forall\;k\in\mathbb{Z}_{0+}\;.

(2)

The additive disturbance is also contained in a given disturbance set:

d_{k}\in\mathbb{D}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}\quad\forall\;k\in\mathbb{Z}_{0+}\;.

(3)

Assumption 2
(constraint sets)

(a) The input and state constraint sets
\mathbb{U}
and
\mathbb{X}
are convex and contain the origin. (b) The disturbance set
\mathbb{D}
is a convex polytope that contains the origin.

II-C

Deadbeat Input Sequence

This section gradually introduces the concept of an
deadbeat input sequence
, which is the main idea behind DRMPC.

For a start, consider the control problem without constraints. Suppose that the state of the system in some time step
k-1\in\mathbb{Z}_{0+}
is at the origin,
x_{k-1}=0
, whence
u_{k-1}=0
. Consider only the effect of a single disturbance in step
k-1
,
d_{k-1}\neq 0
. According to (
8
), the state of the system at step
k
is given by
x_{k}=d_{k-1}
.

By Assumption
1
(b), the system state can be steered from
x_{k}
back to the origin in at most
n
steps, assuming there no further disturbances during these steps. So pick any
M\geq n
, and denote with
z_{k|k},\ldots,z_{k+M|k}
the
predicted states
under the assumption of
d_{k}=\ldots=d_{k+M-1}=0
. There hence exists a sequence of inputs
w_{k|k},\ldots,w_{k+M-1|k}
such that

z_{k+j+1|k}=Az_{k+j|k}+Bw_{k+j|k}\quad\forall\;j=0,\ldots,M-1\;,

with
z_{k|k}=x_{k}=d_{k-1}
and
z_{k+M|k}=0
.

Definition 3
(deadbeat input sequence)

(a) The input sequence
w_{k|k},\ldots,w_{k+M-1|k}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
is called an
deadbeat input sequence
for the disturbance
d_{k-1}
. (b) The sequence
z_{k|k},\ldots,z_{k+M|k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is called the corresponding
state predictions
. (c)
M\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
is called the
deadbeat horizon
(
M\geq n
).

Next, suppose there is another disturbance in step
k
,
d_{k-1}\neq 0
and
d_{k}\neq 0
. Let
w_{k+1|k+1},\ldots,w_{k+M|k+1}
be a deadbeat input sequence for
d_{k}
. By the superposition principle for linear systems, the following selection of inputs

\displaystyle u_{k}

\displaystyle=w_{k|k}\;,

\displaystyle u_{k+1}

\displaystyle=w_{k+1|k}+w_{k+1|k+1}\;,

\displaystyle\;\;\vdots

\displaystyle u_{k+M-1}

\displaystyle=w_{k+M-1|k}+w_{k+M-1|k+1}\;,

\displaystyle u_{k+M}

\displaystyle=w_{k+M|k+1}

annihilates both disturbances
d_{k-1}
and
d_{k}
, in the sense that the predicted state
z_{k+M+1|k+1}
is zero.

This procedure can be generalized to the case where all disturbances are non-zero, i.e.,
d_{k}\neq 0
for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{0+}
. By the considerations above, the required input to be applied at step
k
is given by

u_{k}=\sum_{j=0}^{\max\{k-1,M-1\}}w_{k|k-j}\;,

(4)

where
w_{k|k-j}
is the element of the deadbeat input sequence for the disturbance
d_{k-j-1}
to be applied at step
k
.

II-D

Vertex Representations

Assumption
2
(b) implies that the disturbance set can be represented as the
convex hull
of a finite number
i=1,\ldots,p
of vertices
d^{(i)}
,

\mathbb{D}=\text{conv}\left(d^{(1)},d^{(2)},\ldots,d^{(p)}\right)\;.

(5)

For any
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{0+}
, the disturbance vector
d_{k}\in\mathbb{D}
can hence be expressed as

d_{k}=\sum_{i=1}^{p}\lambda_{k}^{(i)}d^{(i)}\;,

(6)

for appropriate scalars
\lambda_{k}^{(i)}\geq 0
with
\sum_{i=1}^{p}\lambda_{k}^{(i)}=1
.

Denote with
w_{0}^{(i)},\ldots,w_{M-1}^{(i)}
the deadbeat input sequence for the case where
x_{0}
coincides with the vertex point
d^{(i)}
. Due to linearity of the system, the deadbeat input sequence for
any

d_{k-1}\in\mathbb{D}
can be expressed as

w_{k+j|k}=\sum_{i=1}^{p}\lambda_{k-1}^{(i)}w^{(i)}_{j}\quad\forall\;j=0,\ldots,M-1\;,

(7)

where
\lambda_{k-1}^{(i)}
are the scalars in (
6
) pertaining to
d_{k-1}
.

Moreover, denote with
z_{0}^{(i)},\ldots,z_{M}^{(i)}
the corresponding state predictions for the vertex points
x_{0}=d^{(i)}
:

z_{j+1}^{(i)}=Az_{j}^{(i)}+Bw_{j}^{(i)}\;,\quad\forall\;j=0,\ldots,M-1\;.

Note that
z_{0}^{(i)}=d^{(i)}
and
z_{M}^{(i)}=0
for all
i=1,\ldots,p
due to the deadbeat property of the input sequences. By the superposition principle, the state predictions
z_{k|k},\ldots,z_{k+M|k}
corresponding to (
7
) can be expressed as

z_{k+j|k}=\sum_{i=1}^{p}\lambda_{k-1}^{(i)}z^{(i)}_{j}\quad\forall\;j=0,\ldots,M\;,

(8)

where
\lambda_{k-1}^{(i)}
are the scalars in (
6
) pertaining to
d_{k-1}
. Observe that
z_{k|k}=d_{k-1}
and
z_{k+M|k}=0
by construction, because
z_{M}^{(i)}=0
for all
i=1,\ldots,p
.

Remark 4

The inputs
w_{k|k},\dots,w_{k+M-1|k}
in (
7
) are given as nonlinear functions of
d_{k-1}
(‘
disturbance feedback policy
’), defined through the barycentric coordinates
\lambda_{k-1}^{(1)},\dots,\lambda_{k-1}^{(p)}

III
Deadbeat Robust MPC

III-A

Constraint Tightening

For simplicity, consider the DRMPC problem setup only in step
k=0
. The generalization to an arbitrary step
k=1,2,\dots
is straightforward. Let
x_{0}\in\mathbb{X}
be the initial condition of the system and
N
be the selected
prediction horizon
, where
n\leq M\leq N
. By Assumption
1
(b), there exist control inputs
u_{0|0},\ldots,u_{N-1|0}
that drive the system state to the origin; i.e., the predicted states
x_{0|0},\ldots,x_{N|0}
, calculated recursively via

x_{k+1|0}=Ax_{k|0}+Bu_{k|0}\;,\quad x_{0|0}=x_{0}\;,

satisfy
x_{N|0}=0
. Thus the terminal set for the DRMPC is chosen as the origin.

The notation
u_{k+j|k}
refers to the input to be applied to the system at step
k+j
, according to the plan made in step
k
. Correspondingly,
x_{k+j|k}
refers to the state prediction for step
k+j
made in step
k
.

According to (
4
), for any disturbance
d_{k-1}
that is observed at any step
k=1,\ldots,N-1
, the plan is to superimpose a deadbeat input sequence
w_{k|k},\ldots,w_{k+M-1|k}
over the subsequent
M
inputs, in order to eliminate the effects of the disturbance. For instance,

\displaystyle u_{0}

\displaystyle=u_{0|0}\;,

(9a)

\displaystyle u_{1}

\displaystyle=u_{1|0}\,\,+\,\,w_{1|1}\;,

(9b)

\displaystyle u_{2}

\displaystyle=u_{2|0}\,\,+\,\,w_{2|1}\,\,+\,\,w_{2|2}\;,

(9c)

\displaystyle\;\;\vdots

\displaystyle u_{M}

\displaystyle=u_{M|0}+w_{M|1}+\ldots+w_{M|M}\;,\hskip 25.6073pt\phantom{a}

(9d)

up to the deadbeat horizon, and

u_{k}=u_{k|0}+\sum_{j=0}^{M-1}w_{k|k-j}\quad\forall\;M\leq k<N\;.

(9e)

Since the disturbances
d_{0},\ldots d_{N-1}
over the prediction horizon are unknown at step
0
, the disturbance compensation terms
w_{k|k-j}
in (
12
) are not fixed when the inputs
u_{0|0},\ldots,u_{N-1|0}
have to be computed. It is thus necessary to tighten the input constraints accordingly. With
w^{(1)}_{j},\ldots,w^{(p)}_{j}
from (
7
), the tightening can be based on the corner cases

w_{k|k-j}\in\text{conv}\left(w^{(1)}_{j},\ldots,w^{(p)}_{j}\right)\\
\forall\;j=0,\ldots,M-1\;.

(10)

The corner case inputs are included as decision variables into the DRMPC optimization problem. The tightened input constraints can hence be written as

\displaystyle u_{0|0}\in\mathbb{U}\;,

(11a)

\displaystyle u_{1|0}+w^{(i_{0})}_{0}\in\mathbb{U}\;,

(11b)

\displaystyle u_{2|0}+w^{(i_{0})}_{0}+w^{(i_{1})}_{1}\in\mathbb{U}\;,

(11c)

\displaystyle\;\;\vdots

\displaystyle u_{M|0}+w^{(i_{0})}_{0}+\ldots+w^{(i_{M-1})}_{M-1}\in\mathbb{U}\hskip 58.32823pt\phantom{a}

(11d)

up to the deadbeat horizon, and beyond that

u_{k|0}+w^{(i_{0})}_{0}+\ldots+w^{(i_{M-1})}_{M-1}\in\mathbb{U}\quad\forall\;M\leq k<N\;.

(11e)

The tightening is such that (
11
) must hold for all combinations of
i_{0},\ldots,i_{M-1}\in\{1,\ldots,p\}
. Thus (
11
b) represents
p
input constraints, (
11
c) represents
p^{2}
input constraints, and (
11

p^{M}
input constraints. This sums up to a total of
1+p+p^{2}+\ldots+p^{(M-1)}+(N-M)p^{M}
input constraints.

The tightening of the corresponding state constraints is analogous. Based on the inputs (
12
) and the predicted states
x_{0|0},\ldots,x_{N|0}
, the true states satisfy

\displaystyle x_{1}

\displaystyle=x_{1|0}+z_{1|1}\;,

(12a)

\displaystyle x_{2}

\displaystyle=x_{2|0}+z_{2|1}+z_{2|2}\;,

(12b)

\displaystyle\;\;\vdots

\displaystyle x_{M}

\displaystyle=x_{M|0}+z_{M|1}+\ldots+z_{M|M}\;,\hskip 28.45274pt\phantom{a}

(12c)

up to the deadbeat horizon, and beyond that

x_{k}=x_{k|0}+\sum_{j=0}^{M-1}z_{k|k-j}\quad\forall\;M\leq k<N\;.

(12e)

Again, the tightening of the state constraints can be based on the corner cases of the deadbeat state predictions (
8
),

z_{k|k-j}\in\text{conv}\left(z^{(1)}_{j},\ldots,z^{(p)}_{j}\right)\\
\forall\;j=0,\ldots,M-1\;.

(13)

The corner case states are also decision variables of the DRMPC optimization problem. The tightened state constraints can hence be written as

\displaystyle x_{1|0}+z^{(i_{0})}_{0}\in\mathbb{X}\;,

(14a)

\displaystyle x_{2|0}+z^{(i_{0})}_{0}+z^{(i_{1})}_{1}\in\mathbb{X}\;,

(14b)

\displaystyle\;\;\vdots

\displaystyle x_{M|0}+z^{(i_{0})}_{0}+\ldots+z^{(i_{M-1})}_{M-1}\in\mathbb{X}\hskip 58.32823pt\phantom{a}

(14c)

up to the deadbeat horizon, and beyond that

x_{k|0}+z^{(i_{0})}_{0}+\ldots+z^{(i_{M-1})}_{M-1}\in\mathbb{X}\quad\forall\;M\leq k\leq N\;.

(14e)

The tightening is such that (
14
) must hold for all combinations of
i_{0},\ldots,i_{M-1}\in\{1,\ldots,p\}
. Thus, in total, there are
p+p^{2}+\ldots+p^{(M-1)}+(N-M+1)p^{M}
state constraints, not counting the additional terminal constraint.

III-B

DRMPC Optimization Problem

All required constraints have already been derived. The standard cost function consists of a sum of
stage costs

\ell:\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{U}\to\mathbb{R}
over the prediction horizon. Thus the
DRMPC (optimization) problem
reads as follows:

\displaystyle\min\kern 5.0pt

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\ell\left(u_{k|0},x_{k|0}\right)

(15a)

s.t.

\displaystyle\text{for all}\kern 5.0ptj\in\{0,\ldots,M-1\}\;,\;\;i\in\{1,\ldots,p\}:

\displaystyle\quad z^{(i)}_{j+1}=Az^{(i)}_{j}+Bw^{(i)}_{j}\;,\kern 5.0ptz^{(i)}_{0}=d^{(i)}\;,

(15b)

\displaystyle\quad z^{(i)}_{M}=0\;,

(15c)

\displaystyle\text{for all}\kern 5.0ptk\in\{0,\ldots,N-1\}:

\displaystyle\quad x_{k+1|0}=Ax_{k|0}+Bu_{k|0}\;,\kern 5.0ptx_{0|0}=\bar{x}_{0}

(15d)

\displaystyle\quad x_{N|0}=0

(15e)

\displaystyle\quad u_{k|0}\in\mathbb{U}

(15f)

\displaystyle\quad x_{k+1|0}\in\mathbb{X}

(15g)

\displaystyle\quad\text{for all}\kern 5.0pti_{0},\ldots,i_{M-1}\in\{1,\ldots,p\}:

\displaystyle\qquad u_{k|0}+\sum_{l=0}^{\max\{k-1,M-1\}}w^{(i_{l})}_{l}\in\mathbb{U}\;,

(15h)

\displaystyle\qquad x_{k+1|0}+\sum_{l=0}^{\max\{k,M-1\}}z^{(i_{l})}_{l}\in\mathbb{X}\;.

(15i)

Recall that the sums in (
15
h,i) refer to all possible combinations of the indices
i_{l}
.

Remark 5
(terminal condition)

For the DRMPC formulation (
15
), the
terminal set
is the origin and there is no
terminal cost
.

It is possible, however, to choose a different terminal condition for a DRMPC in the case with offline pre-computations, which is the subject of Section

.

III-C

Computational Complexity

If the stage cost is a convex function and
\mathbb{U}
,
\mathbb{X}
are convex sets, then (
15
) is a convex optimization problem. In this case, it can be solved quickly and efficiently, even for larger problem instances
[
3
,
20
]
. There are many excellent software tools available, some of which also support code generation for embedded systems
[
12
,
6
,
21
,
18
]
.

The decision variables of the DRMPC problem comprise

•

Nm
selected inputs
u_{0|0},\ldots,u_{N-1|0}
,

•

Nn
predicted states
x_{1|0},\ldots,x_{N|0}
,

•

Mmp
deadbeat inputs
w_{0}^{(i)},\ldots,w_{M-1}^{(i)}
,

•

Mnp
corresponding states
z_{1}^{(i)},\ldots,z_{M}^{(i)}
.

The total of
(Mp+N)(m+n)
decision variables can be reduced to
(Mp+N)m
, if all state variables are eliminated by substitution of the equality constraints (
15
b,c) and (
15
d,e). This is sometimes called the
sequential approach
.

The total number of inequality constraints in (
15
) is

\hskip-11.38092pt\left(p+\ldots+p^{(M-1)}+(N-M)p^{M}\right)(n+m)+m+np^{M}\\
=\mathcal{O}\left((n+m)Np^{M}\right)\;.

(16)

This formula aligns with the case of nominal MPC, where
p^{M}=1
since
p=1
and
M=0
. The number of equality constrains in (
15
) is
(M+1)pn+n
, which can be reduced to
(p+1)n
when using the sequential approach.

Clearly, the most significant factor for the computational complexity of DRMPC is the exponential term
p^{M}
in (
16
). It is due to the combination of all the corner cases in the tightening of the input and state constraints (
11
) and (
14
). To put this into perspective, recall that the deadbeat horizon
M
is selected as any number
M\geq n
. The number of states
n
is typically small for Robust MPC applications (usually,
n\leq 4
). Furthermore,
p
is the number of vertices of the disturbance set
\mathbb{D}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, which may again be small, especially if
n
is small and
\mathbb{D}
is not fully dimensional. For example, consider a system with
n=3
states, where the disturbance is a hypercube affecting only 2 of the states. Then the DRMPC contains about
p^{M}=4^{3}=64
times the number of constraints as the corresponding nominal MPC problem. In conclusion, DRMPC scales poorly with the dimension of the system state and the vertices of the disturbance set. It may be tractable for small systems. To circumvent the complexity problem, the deadbeat input sequences can be computed offline, as discussed next.

III-D

Offline DRMPC

The case where the deadbeat input sequences are computed online (Online DRMPC) is the most general, but mainly of theoretical interest, due to its computational complexity. Presumably of greater practical interest is the case where they are pre-computed offline (Offline DRMPC). This significantly reduces the computational complexity and also allows for a less restrictive terminal condition. However, it may lead to sub-optimality of the solution to (
15
), and hence to a possible decrease in closed-loop performance. The offline pre-computations work as described below.

Remark 6
(offline pre-computations)

(a) For each vertex
d^{(i)}
, the deadbeat control inputs
w_{0}^{(i)},\ldots,w_{M-1}^{(i)}
and corresponding states
z_{0}^{(i)},\ldots,z_{M-1}^{(i)}
are pre-computed and substituted as fixed values into the DRMPC problem (
15
). This eliminates them as decision variables, together with the contraints (
15
b,c). (b) To this end, a finite-horizon optimal control problem over the deadbeat horizon
M
is solved for each vertex
d^{(i)}
. The vertex represents the initial condition, the nominal input and state constraints are imposed, and an analogous cost function to (
15
a) can be used.

The pre-computed deadbeat control inputs and states are used to define the convex hulls

\displaystyle\mathbb{W}_{k}

\displaystyle:=\text{conv}\left(0,w^{(1)}_{k},w^{(2)}_{k},\dots,w^{(p)}_{k}\right)\;,

(17a)

\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}_{k}

\displaystyle:=\text{conv}\left(0,z^{(1)}_{k},z^{(2)}_{k},\dots,z^{(p)}_{k}\right)

(17b)

for all
k=0,1,\dots,M-1
. Based on this, the tightened input constraints (
15
f,h) can be expressed as

\displaystyle u_{0|0}\in\mathbb{U}\;,

(18a)

\displaystyle u_{1|0}\in\mathbb{U}\ominus\mathbb{W}_{0}\;,

(18b)

\displaystyle\;\;\vdots

\displaystyle u_{k|0}\in\mathbb{U}\ominus\mathbb{W}_{0}\ominus\mathbb{W}_{1}\ominus\dots\ominus\mathbb{W}_{\max\{k-1,M-1\}}

(18c)

for all
k=0,1,\dots,N-1
. Analogously, the tightened state constraints (
15
g,i) can be expressed as

\displaystyle x_{1|0}\in\mathbb{X}\ominus\mathbb{Z}_{0}\;,

(19a)

\displaystyle\;\;\vdots

\displaystyle x_{k|0}\in\mathbb{X}\ominus\mathbb{Z}_{0}\ominus\mathbb{Z}_{1}\ominus\dots\ominus\mathbb{Z}_{\max\{k-1,M-1\}}

(19b)

for all
k=1,2,\dots,N
.

With these offline pre-computations, the complexity of Offline DRMPC will be similar to Nominal MPC. In particular, it will be independent of the dimensions
n
and
p
, and of the choice for the deadbeat horizon
M
. All system-theoretic guarantees of Online DRMPC, which are discussed in Section
IV
, will be preserved.

Remark 7
(alternative terminal conditions)

For Offline DRMPC, where the deadbeat input sequences are pre-computed offline, any terminal condition of a nominal MPC can be used, such as a positive invariant (PI) set.

For example, if the stage cost function in (
15
a) is quadratic,

\ell\left(u_{k},x_{k}\right)=u_{k}^{\scriptsize{\mathrm{T}}}Ru_{k}+x_{k}^{\scriptsize{\mathrm{T}}}Qx_{k}

for some
R\succ 0
and
Q\succeq 0
, the Linear Quadratic Regulator (LQR)
u_{k}=K_{\text{LQR}}x_{k}
can be selected as the terminal controller. Then the terminal set for
x_{N|0}
can be selected as any PI set for the LQR and

\displaystyle u_{k}\in\mathbb{U}\ominus\mathbb{W}_{0}\ominus\mathbb{W}_{1}\ominus\dots\ominus\mathbb{W}_{M-1}\;,

\displaystyle x_{k}\in\mathbb{X}\ominus\mathbb{Z}_{0}\ominus\mathbb{Z}_{1}\ominus\dots\ominus\mathbb{Z}_{M-1}\;.

The terminal cost is then selected as
x_{N|0}^{\scriptsize{\mathrm{T}}}Px_{N|0}
, where
P\succeq 0
is the solution to the Algebraic Riccati Equation of the LQR.

Note that using an alternative terminal condition according to Remark
7
will increase the region of attraction. However, a major point of this paper that no invariant set has to be calculated, even though in the case of DRMPC it may be a simpler (non-robust) PI set.

IV
System-theoretic Guarantees

It is shown in this section that DRMPC comes with all relevant system-theoretic guarantees, including recursive feasibility and stability. The line of argument is closely related to those of other RMPC approaches
[
10
,
17
]
.

IV-A

Recursive Feasibility

Under the DRMPC regime, in each time step
k=0,1,\ldots
the first element of the optimal input sequence from (
15
),
u_{k}=u_{k|k}^{\star}
, is applied to the system.

Theorem 8
(recursive feasibility)

If the DRMPC problem (
15
) is feasible at
k=0
for
\bar{x}_{0}
, it remains feasible for all future states
x_{1},x_{2},\ldots
of system under the DRMPC regime.

Proof:
Consider the solution to (
15
) in step
k=0
. Let
u_{0|0}^{\star},\ldots,u_{N-1|0}^{\star}
be the optimal inputs and
x_{0|0}^{\star},\ldots,x_{N|0}^{\star}
be the optimal states. They satisfy the input constraints (
15
h) and state constraints (
15
i), respectively. By the terminal constraint (
15
e),
x_{N|0}^{\star}=0
. Moreover, let
w_{0}^{(i)\star},\ldots,w_{M-1}^{(i)\star}
be the optimal deadbeat inputs and
z_{0}^{(i)\star},\ldots,z_{M}^{(i)\star}
be the corresponding states, with
z_{M}^{(i)\star}=0
, for any
i=1,\ldots,p
.

For recursive feasibility, it must be shown that in time step
k=1
there exists a candidate input sequence
u_{1|1},\ldots,u_{N|1}
and a corresponding state sequence
x_{1|1},\ldots,x_{N+1|1}
that satisfy the input, state, and terminal constraints, for any admissible disturbance
d_{0}
. For the sake of this proof, it is assumed that
w_{0}^{(i)\star},\ldots,w_{M-1}^{(i)\star}
and
z_{0}^{(i)\star},\ldots,z_{M}^{(i)\star}
remain unchanged from step 0 to step 1, which is a feasible choice.

By the presence of a disturbance,

x_{1|1}=x_{1|0}^{\star}+d_{0}\;,\quad\text{for some}\;d_{0}\in\mathbb{D}\;.

Thus
d_{0}
is known in step 1, and according to (
6
) it can be expressed as

d_{0}=\sum_{i=1}^{p}\lambda_{0}^{(i)}d^{(i)}\;,

for some
\lambda_{0}^{(i)}\geq 0
with
\sum_{i=1}^{p}\lambda_{0}^{(i)}=1
. Consider the candidate input sequence

\displaystyle u_{1|1}

\displaystyle=u_{1|0}^{\star}+\sum_{i=1}^{p}\lambda_{0}^{(i)}w_{0}^{(i)\star}\;,

\displaystyle\;\;\vdots

\displaystyle u_{M|1}

\displaystyle=u_{M|0}^{\star}+\sum_{i=1}^{p}\lambda_{0}^{(i)}w_{M-1}^{(i)\star}\;,

\displaystyle u_{M+1|1}

\displaystyle=u_{M+1|0}^{\star}\;,

\displaystyle\;\;\vdots

\displaystyle u_{N-1|1}

\displaystyle=u_{N-1|0}^{\star}\;,

\displaystyle u_{N|1}

\displaystyle=0\;.

By construction, this input sequence satisfies all the input constraints (
15
h). Moreover, the corresponding state sequence

\displaystyle x_{1|1}

\displaystyle=x_{1|0}^{\star}+\sum_{i=1}^{p}\lambda_{0}^{(i)}z_{0}^{(i)\star}\;,

\displaystyle\;\;\vdots

\displaystyle x_{M+1|1}

\displaystyle=x_{M+1|0}^{\star}+\sum_{i=1}^{p}\lambda_{0}^{(i)}z_{M}^{(i)\star}\;,

\displaystyle x_{M+2|1}

\displaystyle=x_{M+2|0}^{\star}\;,

\displaystyle\;\;\vdots

\displaystyle x_{N|1}

\displaystyle=x_{N|0}^{\star}\;,

\displaystyle x_{N+1|1}

\displaystyle=0\;.

satisfies all the state constraints (
15
i) and the terminal constraint. The last step follows from
x_{N|0}^{\star}=0
and the choice of
u_{N|1}=0
.
\square

IV-B

Input-to-State Stability

Let
\mathcal{X}_{N}\subseteq\mathbb{X}
be the
feasible set
, i.e., the set of initial conditions
x_{0}
for which problem (
15
) is feasible. The
state feedback control law

\kappa_{N}:\mathcal{X}_{N}\to\mathbb{U}
of the DRMPC regime is defined as
\kappa_{N}(x_{k})=u_{k|k}^{\star}
. From Theorem
8
, this map is well-defined.

Definition 9
(robust positive invariant set)

Consider system (
8
) with any state feedback control law
\kappa:\mathbb{X}\to\mathbb{U}
. A set
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathbb{X}
is called a
robust positive invariant (RPI) set
if for all
x\in\mathcal{P}
it holds that

\kappa(x)\in\mathbb{U}\quad\text{and}\quad Ax+B\kappa(x)\oplus\mathbb{D}\subseteq\mathcal{P}\;.

Corollary 10
(invariance of the feasible set)

The feasible set
\mathcal{X}_{N}
is a RPI set for system (
8
) under the DRMPC regime.

Proof:
This is immediate from Theorem
8
.
\square

Computing the feasible set of the DRMPC problem (
15
) hence leads to an RPI set. Unlike most other RPI sets, this one is based on a
nonlinear
disturbance feedback policy, following Remark
4
.

On top of this invariance property, it can be shown that the DRMPC stabilizes system (
8
). Due to the presence of disturbances, the appropriate stability concept to be used is
input-to-state stability (ISS)

[
8
,
10
,
17
]
. It requires the following standard assumption on the stage cost function.

Assumption 11
(stage cost)

For the stage cost function
\ell:\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{U}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{0+}
there exist two
K_{\infty}
-functions
\varepsilon_{1}
and
\epsilon_{2}
with

\varepsilon_{1}(\|x\|)\leq\ell\left(x,u\right)\leq\varepsilon_{2}(\|x\|)\quad\forall\;x\in\mathbb{X}\;,\;u\in\mathbb{U}\;.

(20)

Definition 12
(input-to-state stability)

System (
8
) under some state feedback law
\kappa:\mathcal{P}\to\mathbb{U}
is
input-to-state stable (ISS)
on some RPI set
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathbb{X}
if there exist a
KL
-function
\beta
and a
K
-function
\gamma
such that

\|x_{k}\|\leq\beta\bigl(\|x_{0}\|,k\bigr)+\gamma\bigl(\max_{j<k}\|d_{j}\|\bigr)\quad\forall\;k\in\mathbb{Z}_{0+}\;,

for any
x_{0}\in\mathcal{P}
and any disturbances
d_{0},d_{1},\ldots\in\mathbb{D}
.

Definition
12
reduces to
asymptotic stability
if all disturbances vanish beyond some step
k
, i.e.,
d_{j}=0
for all
j\geq k
. If the disturbances are non-zero, the states
x_{k}
are bounded by norm balls, whose size increases with the maximum value of
\|d_{j}\|
observed in the past
j<k
.

Definition 13
(ISS Lyapunov function)

V:\mathcal{P}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{0+}
is called an
ISS Lyapunov function
for system (
8
) under the state feedback law
\kappa:\mathcal{P}\to\mathbb{U}
, if the following conditions are satisfied:

(a) There exist two
K_{\infty}
-functions
\alpha_{1}
and
\alpha_{2}
such that

\alpha_{1}(\|x\|)\leq V\left(x\right)\leq\alpha_{2}(\|x\|)\quad\forall\;x\in\mathcal{P}\;.

(21)

(b) There exists a
K_{\infty}
-function
\alpha_{3}
and
K
-function
\sigma
with

V\left(x_{k+1}\right)\leq V\left(x_{k}\right)-\alpha_{3}(\|x_{k}\|)+\sigma(\|d_{k}\|)

(22)

for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{0+}
, any
x_{0}\in\mathcal{P}
, and any
d_{k}\in\mathbb{D}
.

Definition
13
reduces to a
Lyapunov function
if the term
\sigma(\|d_{k}\|)
is removed in (
22
).

Lemma 14
(Lyapunov stability)

If there exists a ISS Lyapunov function
V:\mathcal{P}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{0+}
for system (
8
) under some feedback law
\kappa:\mathbb{X}\to\mathbb{U}
and on some RPI set
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathbb{X}
, then the closed-loop system is ISS.

Proof:
See
[
8
]
.

Theorem 15
(input-to-state stability)

The closed-loop system (
8
) under the DRMPC regime
\kappa_{N}:\mathcal{X}_{N}\to\mathbb{U}
is ISS on
\mathcal{X}_{N}
.

Proof:
By Lemma
14
, it suffices to show that the optimal value function of the DRMPC problem
V^{\star}_{N}:\mathcal{X}_{N}\to\mathbb{R}_{0+}
is an ISS Lyapunov function in the sense of Definition
13
.

Definition
13
(a) is satisfied by virtue of Assumption
11
. Definition
13
(b) is verified as follows.

First, for the case of
d_{k}=0
, the shifted optimal input sequence from the previous step

\displaystyle u_{k+1|k+1}

\displaystyle=u^{\star}_{k+1|k}\;,

\displaystyle\dots\;,

\displaystyle u_{k+N|k+1}

\displaystyle=u^{\star}_{k+N|k}\;,

\displaystyle u_{k+N+1|k+1}

\displaystyle=0

is feasible, and so is the corresponding state sequence. Note that this is due to the terminal constraint (
15
e) as well as the constraints (
15
f,g) and the zeros in (
17
a,b), which are for this reason essential. By the standard argument in MPC stability proofs, the optimal value function hence decreases by more than the first stage cost,

V^{\star}_{N}\left(x_{k+1}\right)\leq V^{\star}_{N}\left(x_{k}\right)-\ell\left(x_{k},u_{k}\right)\;.

Together with Assumption
11
this verifies the term
-\alpha_{3}(\|x_{k}\|)
in (
22
), for the case of
d_{k}=0
.

Second, for the case of
d_{k}\neq 0
, observe that the optimal value function
V^{\star}_{N}(x_{k+1|k}^{\star}+d_{k})
is continuous in
d_{k}
, for all
d_{k}\in\mathbb{D}
. Together with the compactness of
\mathbb{D}
, this verifies the term
+\sigma(\|d_{k}\|)
in (
22
), for the case of
d_{k}\neq 0
.
\square

VI
Conclusion

This paper has proposed Deadbeat Robust Model Predictive Control (DRMPC) as a new approach of Robust Model Predictive Control (RMPC) for linear systems with additive disturbances. The main idea is to completely extinguish the disturbances within a small number of time steps, called the deadbeat horizon. In two variations, the deadbeat inputs are either part of the online optimization (Online DRMPC) or pre-calculated during the design phase of the controller (Offline DRMPC). The first of more of theoretical interest, due to its computational complexity. The latter is presumably more relevant for practical applications.

Full system-theoretic guarantees have been established for both versions. The relationships of DRMPC to existing approaches, including Tube-based RMPC, RMPC with Affine Disturbance Feedback, and Minimax MPC, have been discussed and demonstrated. The performance of DRMPC turns out to be competitive with other state-of-the-art RMPC approaches.

The main advantage of DRMPC, however, is the fact that no calculation of any Positive Invariant (PI) or Robust Positive Invariant (RPI) set is required. This implies that it is straightforward to extend the DRMPC approach to linear time-varying (LTV) and linear parameter-varying (LPV) systems. The details will be explored in future research.
