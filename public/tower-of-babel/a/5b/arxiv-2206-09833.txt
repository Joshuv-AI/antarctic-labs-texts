Abstract
. A basic version of the Pólya-Szegő inequality states that if Φ \Phi is a Young function, the Φ \Phi -Dirichlet energy—the integral of Φ ⁡ ( ‖ ∇ f ‖ ) \Phi(\|\nabla f\|) —of a suitable function f ∈ 𝒱 ⁡ ( ℝ n ) f\in{\mathcal{V}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n}) , the class of nonnegative measurable functions on ℝ n {\mathbb{R}}^{n} that vanish at infinity, does not increase under symmetric decreasing rearrangement. This fact, along with variants that apply to polarizations and to Steiner and certain other rearrangements, has numerous applications. Very general versions of the inequality are proved that hold for all smoothing rearrangements, those that do not increase the modulus of continuity of functions. The results cover all the main classes of functions previously considered: Lipschitz functions f ∈ 𝒱 ⁡ ( ℝ n ) f\in{\mathcal{V}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n}) , functions f ∈ W 1 , p ​ ( ℝ n ) ∩ 𝒱 ⁡ ( ℝ n ) f\in W^{1,p}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})\cap{\mathcal{V}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n}) (when 1 ≤ p < ∞ 1\leq p<\infty and Φ ⁡ ( t ) = t p \Phi(t)=t^{p} ), and functions f ∈ W l ​ o ​ c 1 , 1 ​ ( ℝ n ) ∩ 𝒱 ⁡ ( ℝ n ) f\in W^{1,1}_{loc}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})\cap{\mathcal{V}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n}) . In addition, anisotropic versions of these results, in which the role of the unit ball is played by a convex body containing the origin in its interior, are established. Taken together, the results bring together all the basic versions of the Pólya-Szegő inequality previously available under a common and very general framework.

Introduction

A familiar version of the Pólya-Szegő inequality states that if

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\|\nabla f^{\#}(x)\|^{p}\,dx\leq\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\|\nabla f(x)\|^{p}\;dx.

is the primary example of a rearrangement on

Known proofs of (

The first, adopted in the present paper, proceeds via isoperimetric inequalities applied to (super-) level sets, while the second uses approximation by special rearrangements, principally polarizations. \min\{f(x),f(x^{\dagger})\},&{\text{if $x\in H^{-}$}},\end{cases}

[

and refined by Van Schaftingen

Lemma 2.1
.

Let

.

(i)

, essentially, then

.

(ii)
If
A=B
, essentially, then
A^{*}=B^{*}
.

(iii)

(A^{*})^{*}=A^{*}
.

(iv)
If

is open.

Proof.

(i) If
A\subset B
, essentially, then
1_{A}\leq 1_{B}
, essentially. Therefore, if

, then

\displaystyle 1=1_{A}^{*}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{r\to 0^{+}}\frac{1}{{\mathcal{H}}^{n}(B(x,r))}\int_{B(x,r)}1_{A}(y)\,dy\leq\liminf_{r\to 0^{+}}\frac{1}{{\mathcal{H}}^{n}(B(x,r))}\int_{B(x,r)}1_{B}(y)\,dy

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\limsup_{r\to 0^{+}}\frac{1}{{\mathcal{H}}^{n}(B(x,r))}\int_{B(x,r)}1_{B}(y)\,dy\leq 1

and hence
1_{B}^{*}(x)=1
. Therefore
A^{*}\subset B^{*}
.

Parts (ii) and (iii) follow easily.

(iv) Let

and choose
y\in A^{*}
such that
x\in y+K
. Choose
r>0
and an open cone
C
with vertex at

such that

. Note that if

, then
x\in y-w+{\mathrm{int}}\,K
. Since
{{\mathcal{H}}}^{n}(-C\cap rD^{n})>0
,

, and

, essentially, there is a

such that

. Hence

and it follows from the definition of

that

is open since

is open.

Lemma 3.1
.

If
\diamondsuit:{\mathcal{E}}\subset{\mathcal{L}}^{n}\to{\mathcal{L}}^{n}
is monotonic and measure preserving, then
\diamondsuit
is continuous from the inside and from the outside.

Proof.

Let
(A_{m})
be an increasing sequence of sets in
{\mathcal{E}}
such that

. Since
\diamondsuit
is monotonic, we have

, essentially, and hence
\cup_{m\in{\mathbb{N}}}\,\diamondsuit A_{m}\subset\diamondsuit(\cup_{m\in{\mathbb{N}}}A_{m})

It follows that

The proof of the continuity from the outside is similar.

4.
Smoothing rearrangements and reduction of the modulus of continuity

Before embarking on the main goal of this section, we prove the inequalities (
4.1
) below for smoothing rearrangements. These will not be needed for the sequel, but seem interesting and follow fairly easily from what we know so far.

Lemma 4.1
.

Suppose that
{\mathcal{K}}_{n}^{n}\subset{\mathcal{E}}\subset{\mathcal{L}}^{n}
and that
\diamondsuit:{\mathcal{E}}\to{\mathcal{L}}^{n}
is measure preserving and smoothing. Then
S(\diamondsuit K)\leq S(K)
for
K\in{\mathcal{K}}_{n}^{n}
and hence
\diamondsuit
maps balls to balls.

Proof.

Let
K\in{\mathcal{K}}_{n}^{n}
. For
\varepsilon>0
, the assumed properties of
\diamondsuit
imply that

{\mathcal{H}}^{n}((\diamondsuit^{*}K)+\varepsilon B^{n})\leq{\mathcal{H}}^{n}(\diamondsuit(K+\varepsilon B^{n}))={\mathcal{H}}^{n}(K+\varepsilon B^{n})

and
{\mathcal{H}}^{n}(\diamondsuit^{*}K)={\mathcal{H}}^{n}(K)
. It follows, using (
2.12
) with
A=\diamondsuit K
and the relation between the (lower) outer Minkowski content (defined by (
2.8
)) and perimeter
[
9
, Theorem 14.2.1]
, that

\displaystyle S(\diamondsuit K)=S(\diamondsuit^{*}K)\leq\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{*}(\diamondsuit^{*}K)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0+}\frac{{\mathcal{H}}^{n}((\diamondsuit^{*}K)+\varepsilon B^{n})-{\mathcal{H}}^{n}(\diamondsuit^{*}K)}{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0+}\frac{{\mathcal{H}}^{n}(K+\varepsilon B^{n})-{\mathcal{H}}^{n}(K)}{\varepsilon}=S(K).

Consequently,
\diamondsuit K
has finite perimeter. The fact that
\diamondsuit
maps balls to balls is now a direct consequence of the isoperimetric inequality for sets in
\mathcal{L}^{n}
of finite perimeter and its equality condition (see
[
26
, p. 165]
).
∎

Recall that
\overline{\mathcal{M}}^{*}(A)
and
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{*}(A)
are the upper and lower outer Minkowski content of
A
, defined by (
2.8
).

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
X={\mathcal{M}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
,
{\mathcal{M}}_{+}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
,
{\mathcal{S}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
, or
{\mathcal{V}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
and suppose that
T:X\to X
is a rearrangement. If
T
is smoothing, then

(4.1)

\overline{\mathcal{M}}^{*}(\diamondsuit^{*}_{T}A)\leq\overline{\mathcal{M}}^{*}(A^{*})\quad{\text{and}}\quad\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{*}(\diamondsuit^{*}_{T}A)\leq\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{*}(A^{*})

for bounded
A\in\mathcal{M}^{n}
. Moreover,
S(\diamondsuit_{T}K)\leq S(K)
for
K\in{\mathcal{K}}_{n}^{n}
and hence
\diamondsuit_{T}
maps balls to balls.

Proof.

Let
A\in\mathcal{M}^{n}
be bounded and let
\varepsilon>0
. Then the fact that
\diamondsuit^{*}_{T}A=\diamondsuit^{*}_{T}A^{*}
by Lemma
3.9
(iii), (
3.5
) with
A
replaced by

, Lemma
3.9
(iv) with
A
replaced by

, and the equimeasurability of

imply that

(4.2)

{\mathcal{H}}^{n}((\diamondsuit_{T}^{*}A)+\varepsilon B^{n})\leq{\mathcal{H}}^{n}(\diamondsuit_{T}^{*}(A^{*}+\varepsilon B^{n}))={\mathcal{H}}^{n}(\diamondsuit_{T}(A^{*}+\varepsilon B^{n}))={\mathcal{H}}^{n}(A^{*}+\varepsilon B^{n})

and

(4.3)

{\mathcal{H}}^{n}(\diamondsuit^{*}_{T}A)={\mathcal{H}}^{n}(\diamondsuit_{T}A)={\mathcal{H}}^{n}(A)={\mathcal{H}}^{n}(A^{*}).

We obtain the inequalities (
4.1
) directly from (
2.8
), (
4.2
), and (

).

The second statement in the lemma follows directly from Lemma
4.1
with
\diamondsuit
replaced by
\diamondsuit_{T}
, which is valid by Lemma
3.8
.
∎

Example 4.3
.

(i) Let

and define

cannot be dropped.

(ii) Let

and define
\diamondsuit:\mathcal{L}^{n}\rightarrow\mathcal{K}^{n}_{n}
by
\diamondsuit A=r_{A}K
, where

. This map, which corresponds to the convex symmetrization in

maps balls to balls if and only if

is a ball, and we claim that it is smoothing if and only if

is an

{{\mathcal{H}}^{n}}(A)^{1/n}K+d{{\mathcal{H}}^{n}}(K)^{1/n}B^{n}\subset{{\mathcal{H}}^{n}}(A+dB^{n})^{1/n}K

{{\mathcal{H}}^{n}}(K)^{1/n}(K+dB^{n})\subset{{\mathcal{H}}^{n}}(K+dB^{n})^{1/n}K.

Since the sets on both sides of this inclusion have the same volume, the inclusion must be an equality and hence

{{\mathcal{H}}^{n}}(K)^{1/n}K+{{\mathcal{H}}^{n}}(K)^{1/n}dB^{n}={{\mathcal{H}}^{n}}(K)^{1/n}K+\left({{\mathcal{H}}^{n}}(K+dB^{n})^{1/n}-{{\mathcal{H}}^{n}}(K)^{1/n}\right)K.

The cancelation law
[

, p. 139]
for Minkowski addition yields

{{\mathcal{H}}^{n}}(K)^{1/n}dB^{n}=\left({{\mathcal{H}}^{n}}(K+dB^{n})^{1/n}-{{\mathcal{H}}^{n}}(K)^{1/n}\right)K,

which holds if and only if

for some

.

Now suppose that

. Then the superlevel sets of

See Example

∎

Lemma 4.5

Let

Let

\omega_{K,d}(f)=\esssup_{\|x-y\|_{K}\leq d;~x,y\in C}|f(x)-f(y)|.

\esssup_{\|x-y\|_{K}\leq d;~x,y\in C}|f(x)-f(y)|\leq\sup_{\|x-y\|_{K}\leq d;~x,y\in C}|f(x)-f(y)|=s,

-{\mathcal{H}}^{2n}(B(\bar{x},r)\times B(\bar{y},r))+{\mathcal{H}}^{2n}\left((B(\bar{x},r)\times B(\bar{y},r))\cap E_{d}\right).

such that

. Then define

f,&\text{ if $f$ is of type II}.\\

Clearly,

is equimeasurable. If

, then either

and

are of the same type, or

is of type I. It follows that

is a rearrangement. The associated mapping

Lemma 5.4
.

Let

be Lipschitz. If

Proof.

Proof.

Define

\Psi(t)=\begin{cases}\Phi(t),&\text{ if $0\leq t\leq M$},\\
mt+q,&\text{ if $t\geq M$},\\
\end{cases}

where
m>0
and
q\leq 0
are such that
\Psi:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
is convex. Then, for
y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
and
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
, define

(5.18)

\displaystyle h(y,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{cases}|t|\left(1+b\,\Psi(h_{K}(y)/|t|\right),&\text{ if $t\neq 0$},\\
b\,m\,h_{K}(y),&\text{ if $t=0$},\\
\end{cases}

(5.19)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{cases}|t|\left(1+b\,\Phi(h_{K}(y)/|t|\right),&\text{ if $|t|\geq h_{K}(y)/M$},\\
b\,m\,h_{K}(y)+(1+b\,q)|t|,&\text{ if $|t|\leq h_{K}(y)/M$},\\
\end{cases}

where
b>0
. We show that
b
can be chosen so that
h=h_{C}
is the support function of a convex body
C
. To this end, note that from (
5.18
), the positive homogeneity of
h
follows immediately and the subadditivity of
h
for
t>0
or for
t<0
is a routine exercise using the triangle inequality and the convexity of
\Psi
. It is then enough to observe that if
b
is small enough to ensure that
1+b\,q>0
, then the function
b\,m\,h_{K}(y)+(1+b\,q)|t|
in (
5.19
) coincides with the support function of the cylinder
b\,m\,K\times[-(1+b\,q),1+b\,q]\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times{\mathbb{R}}
. This proves (
5.17
).

It remains to prove that all sections
C\cap\{x_{n+1}=t\}
,
t\in[-1,1]
, are dilates of
K
. Clearly
o\in{\mathrm{int}}\,C
, since
h_{C}>0
. From (
2.3
) and (
5.18
), it follows easily that the sublevel sets of
h_{C}(y,1)
, considered as a function of
y
, are dilates of the polar body
K^{\circ}
of
K
. If
t\in(0,1]
, and
\rho_{C^{\circ}}
denotes the radial function of the polar body
C^{\circ}
of
C
(cf. (
2.4
)), then

\displaystyle C^{\circ}\cap\{x_{n+1}=t\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{(z,t):\rho_{C^{\circ}}(z,t)\geq 1\}=\{t(z/t,1):\rho_{C^{\circ}}(z/t,1)\geq t\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{t(y,1):\rho_{C^{\circ}}(y,1)\geq t\}=t\{(y,1):h_{C}(y,1)\leq 1/t\}

is also a dilate of
K^{\circ}
. This argument can be repeated for the sections of
C^{\circ}
corresponding to
t\in[-1,0)
and, by continuity, for
C^{\circ}\cap\{x_{n+1}=0\}
. Thus there exists a concave function
f
on
[-1,1]
such that

C^{\circ}=\{(f(t)z,t)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times{\mathbb{R}}:t\in[-1,1],z\in K^{\circ}\}.

Let
s\in[-1,1]
. A point
(y,s)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times{\mathbb{R}}
belongs to
C
if and only if

f(t)(y\cdot z)+s\,t\leq 1\quad\forall z\in K^{\circ},\quad\forall t\in[-1,1],

that is, if and only if

y\cdot z\leq\min_{t\in-[1,1]}\frac{1-s\,t}{f(t)}\quad\forall z\in K^{\circ}.

The last formula shows that
C\cap\{x_{n+1}=s\}
equals
K
dilated by the factor
\min_{t\in-[1,1]}(1-s\,t)/f(t)
.
∎

The special case
r=R
of the following lemma will be needed for the main results of this section, while the general case is applied in Section
7
.

Lemma 5.7
.

Let
X={\mathcal{S}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
or
{\mathcal{V}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
, let
K\in\mathcal{K}^{n}
satisfy
rB^{n}\subset K\subset RB^{n}
for
0<r\leq R
, and let
T:X\to X
be a
K
-smoothing rearrangement. If
f\in X
is Lipschitz with Lipschitz constant
L
, then there is a Lipschitz function
F:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}
, with Lipschitz constant at most
LR/r
, such that
F(x)=Tf(x)
for
{\mathcal{H}}^{n}
-almost all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.

Proof.

Let
f\in X
be Lipschitz with Lipschitz constant
L
and let
A
be the set of points of approximate continuity of
Tf
. Since
f
is continuous, Lemma
4.5
and Theorem
4.11
(ii) yield

\sup_{\|x-y\|\leq dr;~x,y\in A}|Tf(x)-Tf(y)|\leq\sup_{\|x-y\|\leq dR}|f(x)-f(y)|.

Let
x,y\in A
,
x\neq y
, and choose
d>0
so that
\|x-y\|=dr
. Then, by the previous inequality,

\displaystyle|Tf(x)-Tf(y)|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sup_{\|w-z\|\leq dr;~w,z\in A}|Tf(w)-Tf(z)|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sup_{\|w-z\|\leq dR}|f(w)-f(z)|\leq LdR=(LR/r)\|x-y\|.

Therefore
Tf
is Lipschitz on
A
with Lipschitz constant at most
LR/r
. By the McShane-Whitney extension theorem (see, e.g.,
[
16
, p. 202]
) there is a function
F:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}
with the same Lipschitz properties as
Tf
on the entire space
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, such that
F=Tf
on
A
. Since
{\mathcal{H}}^{n}({\mathbb{R}}^{n}\setminus A)=0
, the proof is complete.
∎

Recall that a Young function is a left-continuous and convex function
\Phi:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty]
with
\Phi(0)=0
.

Theorem 5.8
.

Let
X={\mathcal{S}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
or
{\mathcal{V}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
, let
T:X\to X
be a rearrangement, and let
\Phi
be a Young function. If
T
is smoothing and
f\in X
is Lipschitz, then
Tf
coincides with a Lipschitz function
{\mathcal{H}}^{n}
-almost everywhere on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, and

(5.20)

\int_{\{x:~Tf(x)\geq a\}}\Phi\left(\|\nabla Tf(x)\|\right)\,dx\leq\int_{\{x:~f(x)\geq a\}}\Phi\left(\|\nabla f(x)\|\right)\,dx

for each
a>\essinf f
.
Hence

(5.21)

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\Phi\left(\|\nabla Tf(x)\|\right)\,dx\leq\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\Phi\left(\|\nabla f(x)\|\right)\,dx,

where the integrals may be infinite.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we may assume that
\Phi
is a nontrivial real-valued function. Indeed, the result is obvious if
\Phi\equiv 0
, and if
\Phi
attains the value
\infty
, it does so on some maximal interval
(t_{0},\infty)
,
t_{0}\geq 0
. Suppose first that
t_{0}>0
. For
0<t<t_{0}
, the right derivative
\Phi^{\prime+}(t)
of
\Phi
at
t
is increasing, so we may define
c=\lim_{t\to t_{0}-}\Phi^{\prime+}(t)
. If
c=\infty
, let
(t_{k})
be a strictly increasing sequence in
(0,t_{0})
converging to
t_{0}
, and define the real-valued Young functions

\Phi_{k}(t)=\begin{cases}\Phi(t),&\text{if }0\leq t<t_{k},\\
\Phi^{\prime+}(t_{k})(t-t_{k})+\Phi(t_{k}),&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

If
c<\infty
, we must have
\Phi(t_{0})<\infty
by left continuity, and may define

\Phi_{k}(t)=\begin{cases}\Phi(t),&\text{if }0\leq t\leq t_{0},\\
(c+k)(t-t_{0})+\Phi(t_{0}),&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

When
t_{0}=0
, we put
\Phi_{k}(t)=kt
. In each case, we have
\Phi_{k}\leq\Phi
and
(\Phi_{k})
is an increasing sequence of real-valued Young functions converging pointwise to
\Phi
. Thus, if (
5.20
) holds for real-valued Young functions, it holds with
\Phi
replaced by
\Phi_{k}
, and hence, by the monotone convergence theorem, for
\Phi
itself.

The set of values
t
such that
{\mathcal{H}}^{n}(\{x:f(x)=t\})=0
is dense in
(\essinf f,\infty)
, so there is an increasing sequence
\{a_{m}\}
contained in
(\essinf f,a)
and converging to
a
such that
{\mathcal{H}}^{n}(\{x:f(x)=a_{m}\})=0
for each
m
. Fix
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
. By Lemma
5.4
,
\{x:f(x)\geq a_{m}\}
is bounded, and the equimeasurability of
T
implies that

{\mathcal{H}}^{n}(\{x:Tf(x)=a_{m}\})={\mathcal{H}}^{n}(\{x:f(x)=a_{m}\})=0.

Assume that
L
is the Lipschitz constant for
f
. As
T
reduces the modulus of continuity by Corollary
4.12
, Lemma
5.7
with
r=R
implies that there is a Lipschitz function
F
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
with Lipschitz constant at most
L
such that
F(x)=Tf(x)
for
{\mathcal{H}}^{n}
-almost all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. Weak derivatives and the remainder of this proof are unaffected by changing
Tf
on a set of measure zero, so we may assume that
Tf
itself is Lipschitz on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
with Lipschitz constant at most
L
. Then, by Lemmas
5.3
and
5.5
(the latter applied to both
f
and
Tf
) with
a
replaced by
a_{m}
, and the equimeasurability of
T
, we obtain

\int_{G_{Tf}\cap\{x_{n+1}>a_{m}\}}h_{C}(\nu_{Tf}(x))\,d{\mathcal{H}}^{n}(x)\leq\int_{G_{f}\cap\{x_{n+1}>a_{m}\}}h_{C}(\nu_{f}(x))\,d{\mathcal{H}}^{n}(x),

where
C
is any convex body as in Lemma
5.2
, and
\nu_{g}(x)
is the outer unit normal of
K_{g}
at
x
for a Lipschitz function
g
. The integral on the right can be written as

\int_{\{y:f(y)>a_{m}\}}h_{C}\left(\frac{(-\nabla f(y),1)}{\sqrt{1+\|\nabla f(y)\|^{2}}}\right){\sqrt{1+\|\nabla f(y)\|^{2}}}\,dy=\int_{\{y:f(y)>a_{m}\}}h_{C}(-\nabla f(y),1)\,dy,

where we used the
1
-homogeneity of
h_{C}
. Similarly, the integral on the left can be rewritten in the same form, with
f
replaced by
Tf
. Consequently,

\int_{\{y:Tf(y)>a_{m}\}}h_{C}(-\nabla Tf(y),1)\,dy\leq\int_{\{y:f(y)>a_{m}\}}h_{C}(-\nabla f(y),1)\,dy,

which also yields

(5.22)

\int_{\{y:Tf(y)>a_{m}\}}h_{C}(-\nabla Tf(y),1)-1\,dy\leq\int_{\{y:f(y)>a_{m}\}}h_{C}(-\nabla f(y),1)-1\,dy,

since
{\mathcal{H}}^{n}(\{y:Tf(y)>a_{m}\})={\mathcal{H}}^{n}(\{y:f(y)>a_{m}\})
. If
\ell
is the Lipschitz constant for some Lipschitz function
g
, we have
\|\nabla g(x)\|\leq\ell
for
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
where the derivative of
g
exists. Applying this to
g=f
and
g=Tf
implies that

(5.23)

\max\left\{\|\nabla f(x)\|,\|\nabla Tf(x)\|\right\}\leq L

for
{\mathcal{H}}^{n}
-almost all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. As
\Phi
is real-valued and not identically
0
, we may define the convex body
C
as in Lemma
5.6
corresponding to
K=B^{n}
,
M=L
, and
\Phi
. Then
C
satisfies the conditions stated in Lemma
5.2
and by (
5.17
),

h_{C}(y,1)-1=b\,\Phi(\|y\|),

for
y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
with
\|y\|\leq M
and some
b>0
. Substituting in (
5.22
) and taking limits as
m\to\infty
, we obtain (
5.20
).

By Proposition
3.2
, we have
\essinf Tf=\essinf f
. Letting
a\to\essinf f
in (
5.20
), we arrive at (
5.21
).
∎

Theorem
5.8
yields Pólya-Szegő inequalities with
\Phi(t)=t^{p}
,
1\leq p<\infty
, and it is possible to extend these to functions in
W^{1,p}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})\cap{\mathcal{V}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
by means of standard techniques, utilized for example in the proof of (
1.1
) in
[
3
, Theorem 3.20]
(though the case
p=1
needs extra work). We shall not state this extension here, however, but instead derive it from a still more general result, Theorem
6.3
, proved in Section
6
. See Corollary
6.5
. We also postpone to Section
6
a proof that the assumption in Theorem
5.8
that

is smoothing cannot be dropped in general; see Example

. This example also shows that when
\Phi(t)=t
in Theorem

, the smoothing property is not necessary for the stated inequalities to hold.

Corollary 5.9
.

Let
X={\mathcal{S}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
or
{\mathcal{V}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
, and let
T:X\to X
be a rearrangement. If
T
is smoothing,
f\in X
is Lipschitz, and
a>\essinf f
, then

{\mathcal{H}}^{n}(G_{Tf}\cap\{x_{n+1}\geq a\})\leq{\mathcal{H}}^{n}(G_{f}\cap\{x_{n+1}\geq a\}).

Proof.

Since
f
is Lipschitz, we have the familiar formula (see, e.g.,
[
15
, p. 101]
)

{{\mathcal{H}}}^{n}(G_{f}\cap\{x_{n+1}\geq a\})=\int_{\{x:f(x)\geq a\}}\left(1+\|\nabla f(x)\|^{2}\right)^{1/2}\,dx.

Noting that
T
reduces the modulus of continuity, by Corollary
4.12
, we may, as in the proof of Theorem
5.8
, assume that
Tf
is also Lipschitz, so the same formula holds with
f
replaced by
Tf
. The result now follows from (
5.20
) on setting
\Phi(t)=\sqrt{1+t^{2}}-1
for
t\geq 0
, and recalling that
{\mathcal{H}}^{n}(\{x:Tf(x)\geq a\})={\mathcal{H}}^{n}(\{x:f(x)\geq a\})
.
∎

6.
Extension of Theorem
5.8
to
W^{1,1}_{loc}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})

We begin by recalling some notions from the theory of Orlicz spaces. Much of the literature is based on N-functions (nice Young functions), that is, functions
\Phi:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
that are continuous, convex, and such that
\Phi(t)=0
if and only if
t=0
,
\lim_{t\to 0}\Phi(t)/t=0
, and
\lim_{t\to\infty}\Phi(t)/t=\infty
. However, this restriction is sometimes unnecessary, so we prefer to follow
[
14
]
and
[
44
]
by working with nontrivial Young functions
\Phi
, those such that
\Phi\not\equiv 0
and
\Phi\not\equiv\infty
on
(0,\infty)
.

Let
\Phi:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty]
be a nontrivial Young function. The
Orlicz space

{L^{\Phi}(\Omega)}
, where
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is an open set, is the set of all real-valued measurable functions
f
on
\Omega
such that

(6.1)

\|f\|_{{L^{\Phi}(\Omega)}}=\inf\left\{\lambda>0:\int_{\Omega}\Phi\left(\frac{|f(x)|}{\lambda}\right)\,dx\leq 1\right\}<\infty.

The norm defined by (
6.1
) is called the
Luxemburg norm
.

Note that
f\in{L^{\Phi}(\Omega)}
if and only if there is a
c>0
such that
\int_{\Omega}\Phi(c|f(x)|)\,dx
is finite. Indeed, if the latter condition holds, we can choose
\lambda>0
large enough that
1/\lambda<c
and
\int_{\Omega}\Phi(c|f(x)|)\,dx\leq c\lambda
. Then, since
\Phi(t)/t
is increasing, we have
\lambda\Phi(|f(x)|/\lambda)\leq\Phi(c|f(x)|)/c
and hence

\int_{\Omega}\Phi\left(\frac{|f(x)|}{\lambda}\right)\,dx\leq\frac{1}{c\lambda}\int_{\Omega}\Phi(c|f(x)|)\,dx\leq 1.

If
f,f_{k}\in{L^{\Phi}(\Omega)}
,
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, then

(6.2)

\lim_{k\to\infty}\|f_{k}-f\|_{{L^{\Phi}(\Omega)}}=0\iff\lim_{k\to\infty}\int_{\Omega}\Phi\left(c|f_{k}(x)-f(x)|\right)\,dx=0,\text{ for all }c>0,

by
[
14
, Proposition 2.1.10(5)]
.

The
complementary function
\Psi:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty]
to
\Phi
, defined by
\Psi(t)=\sup_{s\geq 0}(st-\Phi(s))
, is also a nontrivial Young function, sometimes called the conjugate function of
\Phi
. Another norm on
{L^{\Phi}(\Omega)}
is the
Orlicz norm

\|g\|^{\prime}_{{L^{\Phi}(\Omega)}}=\sup\left\{\int_{\Omega}|g(x)h(x)|\,dx:\int_{\Omega}\Psi(|h(x)|)\,dx\leq 1\right\};

by
[
44
, Theorem 132.2]
, this norm is equivalent to the Luxemburg norm.

The set

(6.3)

H^{\Phi}(\Omega)=\left\{f\in L^{\Phi}(\Omega):\int_{\Omega}\Phi(c|f(x)|)\,dx<\infty\text{ for all }c>0\right\}

is called the
heart
of
{L^{\Phi}(\Omega)}
.

We say that the Young function
\Phi

satisfies a
\Delta_{2}
condition at infinity
if there exist
c,t_{0}>0
such that

\Phi(2t)\leq c\,\Phi(t)

for
t\geq t_{0}
. If
\Phi
is in addition nontrivial and real-valued, and if
{\mathcal{H}}^{n}(\Omega)<\infty
, then
H^{\Phi}(\Omega)={L^{\Phi}(\Omega)}
; see
[
14
, Theorem 2.1.17(2)]
.

The
Orlicz-Sobolev space

W^{1,\Phi}(\Omega)
is defined as

W^{1,\Phi}(\Omega)=\{f:f\in{L^{\Phi}(\Omega)},f\text{ is weakly differentiable in $\Omega$, and }\|\nabla f\|\in{L^{\Phi}(\Omega)}\}.

The norm of
f\in W^{1,\Phi}(\Omega)
is defined by

(6.4)

\|f\|_{W^{1,\Phi}(\Omega)}=\|f\|_{L^{\Phi}(\Omega)}+\big\|\|\nabla f\|\big\|_{L^{\Phi}(\Omega)}.

Let
\Phi_{1}
and
\Phi_{2}
be nontrivial Young functions. We say that
\Phi_{1}

dominates
\Phi_{2}
globally
and write
\Phi_{1}\succ\Phi_{2}
if there are constants
a,b>0
such that

(6.5)

b\,\Phi_{1}(at)\geq\Phi_{2}(t)

for all
t\geq 0
. If
\Phi_{1}\succ\Phi_{2}
, then
L^{\Phi_{1}}(\Omega)\subset L^{\Phi_{2}}(\Omega)
by
[
14
, Theorem 2.2.3(1)]
. If both
\Phi_{1}\succ\Phi_{2}
and
\Phi_{2}\succ\Phi_{1}
, we say that
\Phi_{1}
and
\Phi_{2}
are
equivalent
. In this case,
L^{\Phi_{1}}(\Omega)=L^{\Phi_{2}}(\Omega)
and by
[
14
, Proposition 2.2.1]
, the norms
\|\cdot\|_{L^{\Phi_{1}}(\Omega)}
and
\|\cdot\|_{L^{\Phi_{2}}(\Omega)}
are equivalent.

Consider the complementary pair of nontrivial Young functions

\Phi_{\text{min}}(t)=\begin{cases}0,&\text{ if }0\leq t\leq 1,\\
t-1,&\text{ if }1<t<\infty,\end{cases}\qquad\Phi_{\text{max}}(t)=\begin{cases}t,&\text{ if }0\leq t\leq 1,\\
\infty,&\text{ if }1<t<\infty;\end{cases}

see
[
14
, p. 54]
. The following facts are gathered in
[
14
, Proposition 2.2.4]
and the remarks that follow it. We have

L^{\Phi_{\text{min}}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})=\{f_{1}+f_{\infty}:f_{1}\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{n}),f_{\infty}\in L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})\},

with Luxemburg norm

(6.6)

\|f\|_{L^{\Phi_{\text{min}}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}=\inf\{\max\{\|f_{1}\|_{L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})},\|f_{\infty}\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}\}:f=f_{1}+f_{\infty}\}

for
f\in L^{\Phi_{\text{min}}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
, and
L^{\Phi_{\text{max}}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})=L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})\cap L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
, with Luxemburg norm

(6.7)

\|f\|_{L^{\Phi_{\text{max}}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}=\max\{\|f\|_{L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})},\|f\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}\}

for
f\in L^{\Phi_{\text{max}}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
. We will often write
L^{\Phi_{\text{min}}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})=L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})+L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
. Any nontrivial Young function
\Phi
satisfies
\Phi_{\text{max}}\succ\Phi\succ\Phi_{\text{min}}
and hence all Orlicz spaces with nontrivial Young functions contain
L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})\cap L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
and are contained in
L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})+L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
.

The proof of the following lemma is a variant of arguments in
[
30
, p. 77]
.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
Z
be the closure of

(6.8)

\left\{\sum_{j=1}^{k}a_{j}1_{A_{j}}:a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}\in{\mathbb{R}},A_{1},\ldots,A_{k}\in{\mathcal{L}}^{n},k\in{\mathbb{N}}\right\}

in
L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})+L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
. Then
Z=L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})+L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
.

Proof.

The step functions in the set (
6.8
) are clearly bounded and integrable, so we only have to show that
L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})+L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})\subset Z
.

Let
f\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})+L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
and suppose that
|f(x)|<M
for
{\mathcal{H}}^{n}
-almost all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. For
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
define
I_{j}=[jM/k,(j+1)M/k)
,
A_{j}=f^{-1}(I_{j})
, and

a_{j}=\begin{cases}\min I_{j},&\text{ if }j\geq 0,\\
\sup I_{j},&\text{ otherwise,}\end{cases}

for
j=-k,\ldots,k-1
. For
j\in\{-1,0\}
we have
a_{j}=0
, and for all other
j
,

|a_{j}|{\mathcal{H}}^{n}(A_{j})\leq\int_{A_{j}}|f(x)|dx<\infty,

as
f\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
. Therefore

f_{k}=\sum_{j=-k}^{k-1}a_{j}1_{A_{j}}\in Z.

By construction,
|f_{k}(x)-f(x)|\leq M/k
for
{\mathcal{H}}^{n}
-almost all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, so
\|f_{k}-f\|_{\infty}\to 0
as
k\to\infty
. Since
|f_{k}(x)|\leq|f(x)|
for
{\mathcal{H}}^{n}
-almost all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, the dominated convergence theorem yields
\|f_{k}-f\|_{1}\to 0
as
k\to\infty
. It follows that
f_{k}\to f
as
k\to\infty
in the norm (
6.7
), proving the lemma.
∎

By definition, a subset
S
of
L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})+L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
is relatively sequentially compact in the weak topology
\sigma\left(L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})+L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{n}),L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})\cap L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})\right)
if any sequence
(f_{j})
of functions in
S
has a subsequence
(f_{j_{k}})
converging to some
f\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})+L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
, in the sense that

\lim_{k\to\infty}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}(f_{j_{k}}(x)-f(x))h(x)\,dx=0

for all
h\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})\cap L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
. As
\sigma\left(L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})+L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{n}),L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})\cap L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})\right)
is the only weak topology used in this section, henceforth weak convergence and compactness will always refer to this topology. If
S
is bounded in the norm (
6.6
), it is relatively weakly sequentially compact if and only if there is a real-valued Young function
\widetilde{\Phi}
with
\lim_{t\to\infty}\widetilde{\Phi}(t)/t=\infty
and

(6.9)

\sup_{f\in S}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\widetilde{\Phi}(f(x))\,dx<\infty.

This is essentially the criterion of de La Vallée Poussin
[
14
, Theorem 2.3.5]
combined with
[
14
, Proposition 2.3.16]
, where the latter uses the
\sigma
-finiteness of the underlying measure in the proof.

For the reader’s convenience we provide a proof of the following approximation lemma, mainly to stress that
\Phi
need not be an N-function.

Lemma 6.2
.

If
\Phi:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
is a real-valued Young function and
f\in H^{\Phi}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
, there is a sequence
(f_{j})
of
C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
functions with compact supports that converges to
f
in
L^{\Phi}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
.

If, in addition,
f
is nonnegative, or has bounded support, or
\|\nabla f\|\in H^{\Phi}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
, then
f_{j}
,
j\in{\mathbb{N}}
, can be chosen to be nonnegative, or have uniformly bounded supports, or such that
\lim_{j\to 0}\|f_{j}-f\|_{W^{1,\Phi}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}=0
, respectively.

Proof.

We may assume that
f\in H^{\Phi}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
has compact support. Indeed, let
0\leq\phi\leq 1
be a
C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
function with support in
2B^{n}
and
\phi(x)=1
for all
x\in B^{n}
, and let
\phi_{m}(x)=\phi(x/m)
for
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. Then
\phi_{m}f\in H^{\Phi}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
has compact support, and using (
6.2
) and (
6.3
), it is easy to see that
\phi_{m}f
converges to
f
as
m\to\infty
in
{L^{\Phi_{r}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}
. If
\|\nabla f\|\in H^{\Phi}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
is assumed in addition, this convergence even holds in
W^{1,\Phi}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
, in view of (
6.2
) and (
6.4
). Indeed, since
\Phi
is convex, we have, for
s,t\geq 0
,

\Phi(s+t)\leq(1/2)\left(\Phi(2s)+\Phi(2t)\right).

Therefore

\Phi\left(c\|\nabla(f(1-\phi_{m})\right)\|)\leq(1/2)\left(\Phi(2c\|\nabla f\|)+\Phi\left((2c/m)|f|\sup_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\|\nabla\phi\|\right)\right).

Since
\|\nabla f\|\in H^{\Phi}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
, the integrals of both terms on the right-hand side are finite, and the desired conclusion follows from the dominated convergence theorem. Whenever
f
is nonnegative, we also have
\phi_{m}f\geq 0
. Thus if the result holds for functions with compact support, a standard diagonal-type argument shows that it holds generally.

Suppose that
f\in H^{\Phi}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
has compact support. Let
\rho:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to[0,\infty)
be a
C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
function with support in
B^{n}
and integrating to
1
, and define
\rho_{j}(x)=j^{n}\rho(jx)
for
j\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. The convolution
f_{j}=f*\rho_{j}
is
C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
, satisfies
f_{j}\geq 0
when
f\geq 0
, and has support in
M+B^{n}
when the support of
f
is
M\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. We claim that
\|f-f_{j}\|_{L^{\Phi}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}\to 0
as
j\to\infty
. To see this, note that since
\Phi
is real-valued, there is a sequence of integrable simple functions (i.e., finite weighted sums of integrable characteristic functions) converging to
f
in the
L^{\Phi}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
norm, by
[
14
, Theorem 2.1.14(b)]
. Using monotone convergence, the proof of the latter theorem can easily be modified to show that these simple functions can be assumed to have bounded supports. Thus, for each
\varepsilon>0
there is a simple function
h
with
|h(x)|\leq b1_{RB^{n}}(x)
for some
b,R>0
and all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, such that
\|f-h\|_{L^{\Phi}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}\leq\varepsilon/4
. If
h_{j}=h*\rho_{j}
, Jensen’s inequality (see, e.g.,
[
30
, p. 62, Proposition 5]
) implies that

\|f_{j}-h_{j}\|_{L^{\Phi}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}=\|(f-h)*\rho_{j}\|_{L^{\Phi}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}\leq\|f-h\|_{L^{\Phi}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}\leq\varepsilon/4,

so

\displaystyle\|f-f_{j}\|_{L^{\Phi}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}

\displaystyle\leq\|f-h\|_{L^{\Phi}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}+\|h-h_{j}\|_{L^{\Phi}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}+\|h_{j}-f_{j}\|_{L^{\Phi}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}

(6.10)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}+\|h-h_{j}\|_{L^{\Phi}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}.

We have

|h(x)-h_{j}(x)|\leq\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}|h(x)-h(y)|\rho_{j}(x-y)dy\leq\|\rho_{j}\|_{\infty}\int_{B(x,1/j)}|h(x)-h(y)|\,dy.

As
h
is integrable, the Lebesgue differentiation theorem (see, e.g.,
[
23
, Proposition 3.5.4]
) implies that
h_{j}(x)\to h(x)
for
{\mathcal{H}}^{n}
-almost all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. Since
|h_{j}(x)|\leq b1_{(R+1)B^{n}}(x)
for all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
and
\Phi
is real-valued, the dominated convergence theorem gives
\lim_{j\to\infty}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\Phi(c|h(x)-h_{j}(x)|)\,dx=0
for all
c>0
. Therefore, by (
6.2
), there is a
j_{0}\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that
\|h-h_{j}\|_{L^{\Phi}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}\leq\varepsilon/2
for all
j\geq j_{0}
, and inserting this into (
6.10
) proves the claim.

Since
f_{j}\geq 0
when
f\geq 0
, it only remains to deal with the statement involving the extra assumption that
\|\nabla f\|\in H^{\Phi}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
. For this, we may proceed exactly as in the proof of
[
13
, Theorem 2.1]
. If
f
and
\|\nabla f\|
are functions in
H^{\Phi}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
with compact support, we have
\partial f_{j}/\partial x_{i}=(\partial f/\partial x_{i})*\rho_{j}
(where the weak derivative is used on the right-hand side). Applying the above, both to
f
and with
f
replaced by
\partial f/\partial x_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,n
, yields
\lim_{j\to 0}\|f-f_{j}\|_{W^{1,\Phi}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}=0
, as required.
∎

Theorem 6.3
.

Let
T:{\mathcal{V}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})\to{\mathcal{V}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
be a smoothing rearrangement and let
\Phi
be a Young function. If
f\in W^{1,1}_{loc}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})\cap{\mathcal{V}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
and
\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\Phi(\|\nabla f(x)\|)\,dx<\infty
, then
Tf\in W^{1,1}_{loc}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
and

(6.11)

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\Phi(\|\nabla Tf(x)\|)\,dx\leq\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\Phi(\|\nabla f(x)\|)\,dx.

Proof.

As at the beginning of the proof of Theorem
5.8
, we may assume that
\Phi
is a nontrivial real-valued function. We first aim to prove (
6.11
) when
{\mathcal{H}}^{n}({\mathrm{supp}}\,{f})<\infty
. Let
\Omega
be an open set such that
{\mathrm{supp}}\,f\subset\Omega
and
{\mathcal{H}}^{n}(\Omega)<\infty
. The proof will proceed via a succession of claims.

For
r>\sup\{t\geq 0:\Phi(t)=0\}
, define

\Lambda_{r}(t)=\begin{cases}\Phi(t),&\text{if }0\leq t<r,\\
\Phi^{\prime+}(r)(t-r)+\Phi(r),&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

where
\Phi^{\prime+}(r)
is the right derivative of
\Phi
at
r
.
Then
\Phi_{r}=\max\{0,\Lambda_{r}-1/r\}
is a nontrivial real-valued (and hence continuous) Young function with
\Phi_{r}\leq\Phi
. By construction, there is a
\delta>0
such that

\delta\leq\Phi_{r}^{\prime+}(t)\leq 1/\delta
for all
t>t_{0}=\sup\{t\geq 0:\Phi_{r}(t)=0\}>0
. It follows that for
t\geq t_{0}
, we have

\delta(t-t_{0})=\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\delta\,ds\leq\Phi_{r}(t)\leq\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\frac{1}{\delta}\,ds=\frac{1}{\delta}(t-t_{0}).

Comparing (
6.5
), we see that
\Phi_{r}
is equivalent to
\Phi_{\text{min}}
. Therefore, as we remarked before Lemma
6.2
, the
L^{\Phi_{r}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
norm is equivalent to the norm (
6.6
) and hence
L^{\Phi_{r}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})=L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})+L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
. If
\Psi_{r}
denotes the complementary Young function to
\Phi_{r}
, then

L^{\Psi_{r}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})=L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})\cap L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
and the
L^{\Psi_{r}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
norm is equivalent to (
6.7
).

Our first claim is that
f
can be approximated in the
W^{1,\Phi_{r}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
norm by a sequence
(f_{j})
of nonnegative
C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
functions with compact support. To see this, note that since
0\leq\Phi_{r}\leq\Phi
, our assumptions give
\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\Phi_{r}(\|\nabla f(x)\|)\,dx<\infty
. By
[
36
, Lemma 3]
, this yields the existence of a
c>0
such that
\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\Phi_{r}(c|f(x)|)\,dx
is finite, and hence
f\in L^{\Phi_{r}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
, as was explained in the remarks at the beginning of this section. Therefore
f\in L^{\Phi_{r}}(\Omega)
and our assumptions and
0\leq\Phi_{r}\leq\Phi
imply that
\|\nabla f\|\in L^{\Phi_{r}}(\Omega)
.
As was mentioned before Lemma
6.1
,
L^{\Phi_{r}}(\Omega)=H^{\Phi_{r}}(\Omega)
because
\Phi_{r}
satisfies a
\Delta_{2}
condition at infinity and
{\mathcal{H}}^{n}(\Omega)<\infty
. Since
f
vanishes on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\setminus\Omega
, we have
f,\|\nabla f\|\in H^{\Phi_{r}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
. By Lemma
6.2
, there exists a sequence
(f_{j})
of nonnegative
C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
functions with compact support that converges to
f
in the
W^{1,\Phi_{r}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
norm. This completes the proof of the first claim.

Our second claim is that
(Tf_{j})
converges to
Tf
in
{L^{\Phi_{r}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}
,
Tf\in W^{1,1}_{loc}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
, and a subsequence of
(\nabla Tf_{j})
converges weakly to the weak gradient of
Tf
. Indeed, Proposition
3.7
implies that
\|Tf_{j}-Tf\|_{L^{\Phi_{r}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}\leq\|f_{j}-f\|_{L^{\Phi_{r}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}
. Thus
Tf_{j}\to Tf
in
{L^{\Phi_{r}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}
and, in particular,
Tf\in{L^{\Phi_{r}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}
and
Tf\in L^{1}_{loc}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
. We also have

(6.12)

\lim_{j\to\infty}\big\|\|\nabla f_{j}\|\big\|_{L^{\Phi_{r}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}=\big\|\|\nabla f\|\big\|_{L^{\Phi_{r}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}.

The map
T
and each
f_{j}
satisfy the hypotheses of Theorem
5.8
, so for
j\geq 1
, the latter theorem implies that the nonnegative function
Tf_{j}
agrees with a Lipschitz function
{\mathcal{H}}^{n}
-almost everywhere, and

(6.13)

\big\|\|\nabla Tf_{j}\|\big\|_{L^{\Phi_{r}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}\leq\big\|\|\nabla f_{j}\|\big\|_{L^{\Phi_{r}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}.

Due to (
6.12
) and (
6.13
), the set
\{\|\nabla Tf_{j}\|,j\geq 1\}
is bounded in
{L^{\Phi_{r}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}
and therefore also in
{L^{\Phi_{\text{min}}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}
by norm equivalence.
Since
\|\nabla f_{j}\|
converges in
{L^{\Phi_{r}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}
, it also converges in
{L^{\Phi_{\text{min}}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}
, and the criterion of de La Vallée Poussin stated before Lemma
6.2
yields the existence of a real-valued Young function
\widetilde{\Phi}
with

\lim_{t\to\infty}\widetilde{\Phi}(t)/t=\infty
such that (
6.9
) holds with
S=\{\|\nabla f_{j}\|:j\geq 1\}
. Theorem
5.8
, applied with
\widetilde{\Phi}
instead of
\Phi
, shows that (
6.9
) also holds for

S=\{\|\nabla Tf_{j}\|:j\geq 1\}
with the same
\widetilde{\Phi}
. De La Vallée Poussin’s criterion now shows the relative weak compactness of
\{\|\nabla Tf_{j}\|:j\geq 1\}
. Hence, there is a subsequence of
(\nabla Tf_{j})
, also denoted
(\nabla Tf_{j})
, and a vector field
g\in\left(L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})+L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})\right)^{n}=\left({L^{\Phi_{r}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}\right)^{n}
, such that
\nabla Tf_{j}
converges weakly to
g
, i.e., for each
h\in\left(L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})\cap L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})\right)^{n}=\left(L^{{\Psi_{r}}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})\right)^{n}
,

\lim_{j\to\infty}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}(\nabla Tf_{j})(x)h(x)\,dx=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}g(x)h(x)\,dx.

If
h\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
has compact support, then
h,\partial h/\partial x_{i}\in L^{{\Psi_{r}}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
and

(6.14)

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}g_{i}(x)h(x)\,dx

\displaystyle=\lim_{j\to\infty}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\frac{\partial Tf_{j}}{\partial x_{i}}(x)h(x)\,dx=-\lim_{j\to\infty}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}Tf_{j}(x)\frac{\partial h}{\partial x_{i}}(x)\,dx

\displaystyle=-\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}Tf(x)\frac{\partial h}{\partial x_{i}}(x)\,dx,

where we have used the fact that the convergence of
Tf_{j}
to
Tf
in
L^{\Phi_{r}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
also implies weak convergence. From (
6.14
) we see that
g
is the weak gradient of
Tf
, and the fact that
\partial Tf/\partial x_{i}
is locally integrable is a direct consequence of the fact that it belongs to
L^{{\Psi_{r}}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
. The second claim is proved.

Next, we claim that

(6.15)

\big\|\|\nabla Tf\|\big\|_{L^{\Phi_{r}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}\leq\liminf_{j\to\infty}\big\|\|\nabla Tf_{j}\|\big\|_{L^{\Phi_{r}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}.

With this goal in mind, we first prove that if
u\in(L^{\Phi_{r}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n}))^{n}
, then

(6.16)

\big\|\|u\|\big\|_{L^{\Phi_{r}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}=\sup\left\{\left|\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}u(x)\cdot h(x)\,dx\right|:h\in(L^{{\Psi_{r}}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n}))^{n},\big\|\|h\|\big\|^{\prime}_{L^{{\Psi_{r}}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}\leq 1\right\}.

A similar formula in the scalar case is proved in
[
30
, (10), Proposition 10, Section 3.4]
; when applied with
f
and
\Phi
there replaced by
\|u\|
and
\Phi_{r}
, respectively, it becomes

(6.17)

\big\|\|u\|\big\|_{L^{\Phi_{r}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}=\sup\left\{\left|\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\|u(x)\|\,v(x)\,dx\right|:v\in{\mathcal{M}}^{{\Psi_{r}}},\|v\|^{\prime}_{L^{{\Psi_{r}}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}\leq 1\right\},

where
{\mathcal{M}}^{{\Psi_{r}}}
is the closure of the span of all step functions in
L^{{\Psi_{r}}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
. (We warn the reader that in
[
30
]
,
\|u\|_{L^{\Phi}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}
and
\|u\|^{\prime}_{{L^{\Phi}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}}
are denoted by
N_{\phi}(u)
and
\|u\|_{\Phi}
, respectively.) We have already seen that
L^{\Psi_{r}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})=L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})\cap L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
and that the two spaces have equivalent norms, so
{\mathcal{M}}^{{\Psi_{r}}}=L^{{\Psi_{r}}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
due to Lemma
6.1
. Thus, (
6.17
) becomes

(6.18)

\big\|\|u\|\big\|_{L^{\Phi_{r}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}=\sup\left\{\left|\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\|u(x)\|\,v(x)\,dx\right|:v\in L^{{\Psi_{r}}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n}),\|v\|^{\prime}_{L^{{\Psi_{r}}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}\leq 1\right\}.

Let
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
denote the right-hand sides of (
6.16
) and (
6.18
), respectively. The Cauchy-Schwartz inequality yields
S_{1}\leq S_{2}
. We can restrict the supremum in (
6.18
) to nonnegative
v
. If
v\in L^{{\Psi_{r}}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
,
v\geq 0
,
\|v\|^{\prime}_{L^{{\Psi_{r}}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}\leq 1
, and

h(x)=\begin{cases}v(x)\frac{u(x)}{\|u(x)\|},&\text{if $u(x)\neq 0$},\\
0,&\text{if $u(x)=0$},\end{cases}

then
h\in(L^{{\Psi_{r}}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n}))^{n}
,
\big\|\|h\|\big\|^{\prime}_{L^{{\Psi_{r}}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}\leq 1
, and

S_{1}\geq\left|\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}u(x)\cdot h(x)\,dx\right|=\left|\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\|u(x)\|v(x)\,dx\right|.

This proves that
S_{1}\geq S_{2}
and concludes the proof of (
6.16
). Now (
6.16
) with
u
replaced by
\nabla Tf
and
\nabla Tf_{j}
gives

\displaystyle\big\|\|\nabla Tf\|\big\|_{L^{\Phi_{r}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}

\displaystyle=\sup\left\{\left|\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\nabla Tf(x)\cdot h(x)\,dx\right|:h\in(L^{{\Psi_{r}}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n}))^{n},\big\|\|h\|\big\|^{\prime}_{L^{{\Psi_{r}}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}\leq 1\right\}

\displaystyle=\sup\left\{\lim_{j\to\infty}\left|\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\nabla Tf_{j}(x)\cdot h(x)\,dx\right|:h\in(L^{{\Psi_{r}}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n}))^{n},\big\|\|h\|\big\|^{\prime}_{L^{{\Psi_{r}}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}\leq 1\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\liminf_{j\to\infty}\sup\left\{\left|\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\nabla Tf_{j}(x)\cdot h(x)\,dx\right|:h\in(L^{{\Psi_{r}}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n}))^{n},\big\|\|h\|\big\|^{\prime}_{L^{{\Psi_{r}}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}\leq 1\right\}

\displaystyle=\liminf_{j\to\infty}\big\|\|\nabla Tf_{j}\|\big\|_{L^{\Phi_{r}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}.

This proves (
6.15
).

From (
6.12
), (
6.13
), and (
6.15
), we conclude that

(6.19)

\big\|\|\nabla Tf\|\big\|_{L^{\Phi_{r}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}\leq\big\|\|\nabla f\|\big\|_{L^{\Phi_{r}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}.

Our fourth claim is that (
6.11
) holds when
\Phi
is replaced by
\Phi_{r}
. To see this, note that (
6.19
) holds if
\Phi_{r}
is replaced by
a\,\Phi_{r}
for any
a>0
, because all the preceding arguments are valid with this replacement, due to
\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}a\,\Phi_{r}(\|\nabla f(x)\|)\,dx<\infty
. If we choose
a
so that
\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}a\,\Phi_{r}(\|\nabla f(x)\|)\,dx=1
, then
\big\|\|\nabla f\|\big\|_{L^{a\,\Phi_{r}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}=1
, by
[
14
, Proposition 2.1.10(4)]
and the fact that
\|\nabla f\|\in H^{a\Phi_{r}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
. Thus (
6.19
) becomes

\big\|\|\nabla Tf\|\big\|_{L^{a\Phi_{r}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}\leq 1.

By
[
14
, Proposition 2.1.10(2)]
, the previous inequality holds if and only if

(6.20)

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}a\,\Phi_{r}(\|\nabla Tf(x)\|)\,dx\leq 1=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}a\,\Phi_{r}(\|\nabla f(x)\|)\,dx.

This proves the fourth claim.

Since the nonnegative function
\Phi_{r}
increases to
\Phi
as
r\to\infty
, one may apply the monotone convergence theorem to both sides of (
6.20
) and obtain (
6.11
) when
{\mathcal{H}}^{n}({\mathrm{supp}}\,{f})<\infty
.
To remove the latter restriction, let
f\in W^{1,1}_{loc}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})\cap{\mathcal{V}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
and let
f_{c}=\max\{f-c,0\}
for
c>0
. Then
f_{c}
satisfies the hypotheses of the theorem and
{\mathcal{H}}^{n}({\mathrm{supp}}\,{f_{c}})<\infty
. Moreover,
\nabla f_{c}=\nabla f
on
\{x:f(x)>c\}
and
Tf_{c}=\max\{Tf-c,0\}
, by Proposition
3.6
with
\varphi(t)=(t-c)^{+}
, so
\nabla Tf_{c}=\nabla Tf
on
\{x:Tf(x)>c\}
. By (
6.11
) with
f
replaced by
f_{c}
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\{x:Tf(x)>c\}}\Phi(\|\nabla Tf(x)\|)\,dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\Phi(\|\nabla Tf_{c}(x)\|)\,dx

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\Phi(\|\nabla f_{c}(x)\|)\,dx=\int_{\{x:f(x)>c\}}\Phi(\|\nabla f(x)\|)\,dx.

Letting
c\to 0
and applying the monotone convergence theorem, we obtain (
6.11
).
∎

Lemma 6.4
.

Let
X=\mathcal{S}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
or
{\mathcal{V}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
and let
T:X\to X
be a smoothing rearrangement. If
f\in W^{1,\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})\cap X
, then
Tf\in W^{1,\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
and

(6.21)

\esssup_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\|\nabla Tf(x)\|\leq\esssup_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\|\nabla f(x)\|.

Proof.

If
f\in W^{1,\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})\cap X
, then by
[
3
, Proposition 3.17]
,
f
coincides
{\mathcal{H}}^{n}
-almost everywhere with a Lipschitz function in
X
. We may therefore assume that
f
is Lipschitz, and then, as in the proof of Theorem
5.8
, also assume that
Tf
is Lipschitz. By
[
3
, Corollary 3.4]
, (
6.21
) is equivalent to
L_{1}\leq L_{2}
, where
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
are the Lipschitz constants of
Tf
and
f
, respectively. Since

L_{1}=\sup_{d>0}\omega_{d}(Tf)/d\leq\sup_{d>0}\omega_{d}(f)/d=L_{2},

the proof is complete.
∎

Corollary 6.5
.

Let
T:{\mathcal{V}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})\to{\mathcal{V}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
be a smoothing rearrangement and let
1\leq p\leq\infty
. If
f\in W^{1,p}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})\cap{\mathcal{V}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
, then
Tf\in W^{1,p}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
and

(6.22)

\big\|\|\nabla Tf(x)\|\big\|_{p}\leq\big\|\|\nabla f(x)\|\big\|_{p},

where
\|\cdot\|_{p}
denotes the

Lemma 7.2

The purpose of this appendix is to provide a proof of Proposition

below, first proved by Crowe, Zweibel, and Rosenbloom
[

for Schwarz rearrangement without the assumption that

decrease with

and

, respectively. Variants of Proposition

are stated for general rearrangements by Brock and Solynin
[

and by Van Schaftingen and Willem
[

, whose approaches to rearrangements differ from ours; see
[

, Appendix]
for a comparison. Brock and Solynin refer to
[

]
for a proof, but do not explain why it should apply to general rearrangements, while
[

, Corollary 1]
is stated with the extra assumption that the function

is even. The proof of
[

is based on that of
[

, which does not assume that

is even, or that it is nonnegative, but which requires a considerable amount of preliminary observations and terminology. For this reason, we prefer to follow the argument in
[

]
.

Lemma 8.1
.

Let
F:{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be continuous with
F(0,0)=0
and such that
F(s,0)
and
F(0,t)
decrease with
s\geq 0
and
t\geq 0
, respectively. Suppose that for all coordinate rectangles
R=[a,b]\times[c,d]
, where
a\leq b
,
c\leq d
,

(8.1)

G(R)=F(b,d)+F(a,c)-F(b,c)-F(a,d)\geq 0.

Let
T:{\mathcal{V}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})\to{\mathcal{V}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
be a rearrangement. If
f,g\in{\mathcal{V}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
, then

(8.2)

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}F(f(x),g(x))\,dx\leq\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}F(Tf(x),Tg(x))\,dx.

Proof.

The function
G
is additive on coordinate rectangles, that is, if
R
,
S
, and
R\cup S
are non-overlapping coordinate rectangles, then
G(R\cup S)=G(R)+G(S)
. This allows
G
to be extended to a measure
\nu
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
such that each coordinate rectangle
R
is
\nu
-measurable and
\nu(R)=G(R)
; see
[
31
, pp. 64–68]
(where the union of sets is denoted by
+
).

Let
H
denote the Heaviside function, i.e.,
H(x)=1
if
x\geq 0
and
H(x)=0
if
x<0
. Then for
b,d,s,t\geq 0
,

1_{[0,b]\times[0,d]}(s,t)=H(b-s)H(d-t).

It follows that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}H(b-s)H(d-t)\,d\nu(s,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}1_{[0,b]\times[0,d]}(s,t)\,d\nu(s,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\nu([0,b]\times[0,d])=G([0,b]\times[0,d])

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle F(b,d)-F(b,0)-F(0,d).

From this we obtain

F(b,d)=F(b,0)+F(0,d)+\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}H(b-s)H(d-t)\,d\nu(s,t).

On setting
b=f(x)
and
d=g(x)
and integrating, this gives

(8.3)
