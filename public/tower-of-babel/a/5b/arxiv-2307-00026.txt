Classical Fisher information for differentiable dynamical systems Preprint: AIP/123-QED
Mohamed Sahbani, Swetamber Das, Jason R. Green

Abstract
Fisher information is a lower bound on the uncertainty in the statistical estimation of classical and quantum mechanical parameters. While some deterministic dynamical systems are not subject to random fluctuations, they do still have a form of uncertainty: Infinitesimal perturbations to the initial conditions can grow exponentially in time, a signature of deterministic chaos. As a measure of this uncertainty, we introduce another classical information, specifically for the deterministic dynamics of isolated, closed, or open classical systems not subject to noise. This classical measure of information is defined with Lyapunov vectors in tangent space, making it less akin to the classical Fisher information and more akin to the quantum Fisher information defined with wavevectors in Hilbert space. Our analysis of the local state space structure and linear stability lead to upper and lower bounds on this information, giving it an interpretation as the net stretching action of the flow. Numerical calculations of this information for illustrative mechanical examples show that it depends directly on the phase space curvature and speed of the flow.

Introduction

Across science and engineering
Watanabe 2014
;
Fox and Munsky 2019
, Fisher information is widely used in the design of statistical experiments on both classical and quantum mechanical systems.
Loosely speaking, it is a measure of the amount of information that a data set contains about a statistical parameter.
In classical statistics, it was introduced by Fisher

for experiment design and predicting the minimum achievable error in estimating physical quantities. It has since become an important metric in information geometry
ichi Amari 2016
and a part of physical theories
Machta et al. 2013
.
For example, the classical Fisher information has been used to investigate phase transitions

, the emergence of criticality in living systems
Hidalgo et al. 2014
and collective synchronization
Kalloniatis, Zuparic, and Prokopenko 2018
, and as a complexity measure in neural networks
Liang et al. 2020
.
For quantum mechanical systems, the quantum Fisher information
Helstrom 1967
;
Nielsen and Chuang 2010
also guides measurements, characterizes the sensitivity of quantum states to system parameters, and sets limits on optimal estimation
Paris 2009
.
It is a key element in quantum speed limits
Deffner and Campbell 2017
;
García-Pintos et al. 2022
that has been applied to quantum criticality
Yin et al. 2019
, quantum phase transitions
Wang et al. 2014
;
Marzolino and Prosen 2017
, coherence
Luis 2012
;
Ye et al. 2020
, entanglement
Obada and Abdel-Khalek 2010
;
Li and Luo 2013
, and metrology
Nielsen and Chuang 2010
.

While classical and quantum theories are distinct,
their respective
Fisher information sets bounds on the estimation of parameters.

Fisher information is
also an integral part of the geometric structure of the theories, defining a metric on the appropriate statistical manifold and, so, being related to measures of curvature

;
Sidhu and Kok 2020
.
Consider a classical probability distribution

and the unbiased estimation of the parameter
\theta

The classical Fisher information
I^{C}_{F}
is the expectation value
\langle(d_{\theta}\ln p)^{2}\rangle
of the square of the derivative of the log of the probability distribution with respect to

in the unbiased estimation of

, cannot be smaller than
1/I^{C}_{F}
.
If the probability distribution is Gaussian with variance
\sigma^{2}
, the bound saturates and
I^{C}_{F}=\sigma^{-2}
.

Sharply peaked Gaussian distributions will then be better estimates of the mean
\theta
than broad distributions, and a given data set will have more information about the parameter.

A more sharply peaked probability distribution indicates that the manifold has a higher local curvature
near the maximum
.
More precisely,
I^{C}_{F}\propto-\kappa_{\text{max}}/(1+(d_{\theta}\ln p_{\text{max}})^{2})
, where
\kappa_{\text{max}}
is the curvature of the distribution at its maximum.
From this intuition, Fisher information is
important to
the geometrical structure of statistical manifolds

;
Kim 2021
.

Like its classical counterpart, the quantum Fisher information is the minimum possible error in the unbiased estimation of an unknown parameter. Here, though, the measurement is on a quantum state

\hat{{\bm{\varrho}}}
that evolves unitarily under a Hamiltonian
\hat{H}
.
If
\hat{e}^{2}
represents the variance of the quantum parameter being measured

\hat{e}^{2}\mathcal{I}_{F}^{Q}\geq 1
.
For pure states, the quantum Fisher information
\mathcal{I}^{Q}_{F}=4\Delta\hat{H}^{2}
is directly related to the variance of the Hamiltonian
\Delta\hat{H}^{2}

Among geometric formulations of quantum mechanics

;
Ashtekar and Schilling 1999
;
Brody and Hughston 2001
;
Bengtsson and Zyczkowski 2006
;
Anza and Crutchfield 2021
;

;
Anza and Crutchfield 2022b

Cafaro and Alsing 2018
.
The quantum statistical manifold consisting of a collection of quantum states is endowed with a metric related to
\mathcal{I}_{F}^{Q}
, which defines the curvature of the manifold
Sidhu and Kok 2020
.
On a manifold of pure density matrices, the quantum Fisher information sets the upper limit on the instantaneous rate of change of the absolute statistical distance

;
Kim 2018
between two pure quantum states.
This geometric perspective leads to quantum estimation and the comparison of quantum sensing protocols with
\mathcal{I}_{F}^{Q}

In both classical and quantum theories, the Fisher information is also a part of some stochastic and quantum speed limits, upper bounds on how quickly observables evolve in time.
Both classical and quantum speed limits are rapidly developing fields with interesting parallels that include bounds set by the Fisher information
Poggi, Campbell, and Deffner 2021
;
García-Pintos et al. 2022
;
Aghion and Green 2023
;
Pati et al. 2023
.
Stochastic thermodynamic speed limits on observables set by the classical Fisher information, for example, apply to the fluxes of energy and entropy exchange

.
While in isolated quantum systems, the Fisher information bounds the speed of the average energy, purity, and entropy
Deffner and Campbell 2017
.
Recently, two of us

derived a classical speed limit on the growth rates of perturbations and rates of dissipation for classical deterministic systems.
One of these speed limits is set by a quantity that we can identify as a classical Fisher information.

However,
this Fisher information
\mathcal{I}^{C}_{F}
is a classical quantity, but it is distinct from the classical Fisher information based on probability distributions,

.

For non-Hamiltonian dynamics, it is defined in the tangent space of the state space.
For many-particles systems in the position-momentum phase space, it is defined in the tangent space of a classical phase space point, making it a purely mechanical quantity.

Here, we analyze this Fisher information for deterministic, differentiable dynamics to relate it to both local instability and state space curvature for classical, many-body systems.
For many-body Hamiltonian systems, there is evidence that the curvature of the potential energy landscape can affect the stability of dynamical trajectories and the fluctuations in the chaotic properties of fluids

;
Das and Green 2019
.
Phase space curvature can also regularize the dynamics of barrier crossing, including the isomerization of atomic clusters

;

;
Green et al. 2012

The magnitude of (finite-time) Lyapunov exponents

reflect this behavior and can even be directly related to the reactive flux over the barrier
Revuelta et al. 2017
.
These examples suggest the need for a better understanding of the relationship between phase space curvature and the speed of dynamical processes.

To address this need, we examine this Fisher information
\mathcal{I}^{C}_{F}
for deterministic dynamical systems, showing that a classical density matrix theory

in Sec.
II
leads naturally to its definition, Sec.
III
, and a relationship to the speed through state space.
Given the relationship of other forms of Fisher information to curvature, we corroborate the hypothesis that this new Fisher information is a measure of state space curvature with larger (smaller) values of
\mathcal{I}^{C}_{F}
at a given state space point correspond to higher (lower) curvature, Sec.
III.2
.
In addition, we derive upper and lower bounds on this Fisher information.
To gain physical insight, we compute these bounds for analytically tractable model oscillators, Sec.
IV
, one conservative and one dissipative
.

II
Classical density matrix theory

We recently established a classical density matrix theory to describe the time evolution of infinitesimal perturbations in deterministic systems
Das and Green 2022
.
This theory is based on the normalized time evolution of the perturbation vectors in the tangent space of classical systems and analogous to the density matrix formulation of quantum mechanics based on wavevectors in Hilbert space.
In this classical framework, the classical density matrix is mathematically similar to the metric tensor associated with the underlying phase space, which led to extensions of Liouville’s theorem and equation for non-Hamiltonian systems
Das and Green 2022
.
Local measures of dynamical instability and chaos also appear, but in classical (anti)commutators; for Hamiltonian dynamics, they are directly related to Poisson brackets.
We first summarize the parts of this theory that we will need.

Consider a differentiable dynamical system,
\dot{{\bm{x}}}=\bm{F}({\bm{x}})
, in which a point
{\bm{x}}(t):=[x^{1}(t),x^{2}(t),\ldots,x^{n}(t)]^{\top}
evolves in the
n
-dimensional state space. A small perturbation to that point,
\ket{\delta\ex}
, also evolves in time but according to the linearized dynamics:

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\ket{\delta\ex(t)}={\bm{A}}[x(t)]\ket{\delta{\ex(t)}}.

(1)

The stability matrix,
{\bm{A}}:={\bm{A}}[x(t)]=\nabla{\bm{F}}
has entries
\left({\bm{A}}\right)^{i}_{j}=\partial{\dot{x}^{i}(t)}/\partial{x^{j}(t)}
and governs the time evolution of a perturbation vector
\ket{\delta\ex(t)}
.
Here, we use Dirac’s notation
Dirac 1982
to represent a finite-dimensional column (row) vector with the ket (bra):
\ket{\delta{x^i(t)}}:=[\delta{x^{1}(t)},\delta{x^{2}}(t),\ldots,\delta{x^{n}(t)}]^{\top}
in a real tangent space.

To define the classical density matrix and associated Fisher information, we use the norm-preserving dynamics of the unit perturbation vector
\ket{\delta{\yu(t)}}=\ket{\delta{\ex(t)}}/\lVert\ket{\delta\ex(t)}\rVert
.
Here,
\|.\|
represents the
\ell^{2}
norm:
\lVert\ket{\delta\ex(t)}\rVert:=\langle\delta{\bm{x}}|\delta{\bm{x}}\rangle^{1/2}
.
The time evolution of this vector,

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\ket{\delta\yu(t)}=\bar{\bm{A}}\ket{\delta\yu(t)},

(2)

preserves the unit norm through the generator of the evolution
\bar{\bm{A}}={\bm{A}}-\bra{\delta\yu(t)}{\bm{A}}\ket{\delta\yu(t)}\mathbb{I}
.
The matrix
\mathbb{I}
is the
n\times n
identity matrix.

The source/sink term
\bra{\delta\yu(t)}{\bm{A}}\ket{\delta\yu(t)}
is the instantaneous Lyapunov exponent
, which here counteracts any stretching or contraction and ensures the unit vector is normalized at all times.

Overall, Eq.
2

plays a role that
is similar to the Schrödinger equation from quantum mechanics with
\bar{{\bm{A}}}
playing the role of the Hamiltonian operator.
However, unlike the Hamiltonian, which has a Hermitian matrix representation, the real matrix
\bar{{\bm{A}}}
is generally not symmetric and depends on the unit perturbation vector at time
t
.

The classical density matrix associated with a unit perturbation vector
\ket{\delta\yu}
is the dyadic product,
{\bm{\varrho}}(t)=\outerproduct{\delta\yu(t)}{\delta\yu(t)}
.
Keeping in mind this symmetric matrix is composed of classical variables, we borrow the nomenclature of quantum mechanics and call this density matrix a
pure
perturbation state.
It has the usual properties of a projection operator: it is positive semi-definite,
{\bm{\varrho}}\succeq 0
, with
\Tr{\bm{\varrho}}=1
and
{\bm{\varrho}}={\bm{\varrho}}^{2}
.
Partitioning the matrix
{\bm{A}}
into its symmetric
{\bm{A}}_{+}=({\bm{A}}+{\bm{A}}^{\top})/2
and anti-symmetric
{\bm{A}}_{-}=({\bm{A}}-{\bm{A}}^{\top})/2
parts, the time evolution of the density matrix
{\bm{\varrho}}
is:

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}{\bm{\varrho}}=\{{\bm{A}}_{+},{\bm{\varrho}}\}+[{\bm{A}}_{-},{\bm{\varrho}}]-2\langle{\bm{A}}\rangle{\bm{\varrho}}.

(3)

The notation
\{\cdot\}
and
[\cdot]
indicate the anti-commutator and commutator, respectively, while
\langle\bm{X}\rangle:=\Tr(\boldsymbol{X}\brho)
is the expectation value of
\bm{X}
with respect to the classical density matrix
{\bm{\varrho}}
.
Equation
3
is a classical analogue of the von-Neumann equation. Unlike the quantum version, this equation contains an anti-commutator along with a source/sink term
\langle{\bm{A}}\rangle
and is valid for differentiable deterministic systems,
not only those that are Hamiltonian
.
From this equation of motion, we construct a classical Fisher information and consider its geometric interpretation in terms of phase space curvature and speed.

III
Fisher information for classical pure states

To define this Fisher information on the first variations of the state variables (e.g., position and momentum), we start with Eq.
3
in the form
Das and Green 2023a
:

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}{\bm{\varrho}}=\bar{{\bm{A}}}{\bm{\varrho}}+{\bm{\varrho}}\bar{{\bm{A}}}^{\top}.

(4)

Comparing to
d_{t}{\bm{\varrho}}:=\frac{1}{2}\left({\bm{L}}{\bm{\varrho}}+{\bm{\varrho}}{\bm{L}}^{\top}\right)
, this equation of motion implicitly defines the logarithmic derivative
{\bm{L}}
of the density matrix,
{\bm{L}}=2\bar{\bm{A}}
.
From the logarithmic derivative, we can then show the Fisher information
\mathcal{I}^{C}_{F}
for a pure state
{\bm{\varrho}}
is the variance of
{\bm{L}}
(App.
A
):

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{F}^{C}=\Delta{{\bm{L}}^{2}}=4\Delta{{\bm{A}}^{2}}.

(5)

We define the variance for a matrix
\bm{X}
as
\Delta\bm{X}^{2}=\langle\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{X}\rangle-\langle\bm{X}^{\top}\rangle\langle\bm{X}\rangle
,
and use the fact that the expectation value of
{\bm{L}}
vanishes:
\langle{\bm{L}}\rangle=2\langle\bar{{\bm{A}}}\rangle=0
.
The same expression of
\mathcal{I}^{C}_{F}
follows from the variance of the symmetric logarithmic derivative (App.
B
).

The mathematical form of this Fisher information resembles the quantum Fisher information for pure quantum states.
For a pure quantum state evolving under unitary dynamics, the quantum Fisher information is
\mathcal{I}_{F}^{Q}=\Delta\hat{{\bm{L}}}^{2}=4\Delta\hat{\bm{H}}^{2}/\hbar^{2}
directly related to
\Delta\hat{\bm{H}}^{2}
, the variance of the Hamiltonian
\hat{\bm{H}}
with respect to the pure state
\hat{{\bm{\varrho}}}
.
It derives from the symmetric logarithmic derivative
\hat{{\bm{L}}}
defined implicitly in
d_{t}\hat{{\bm{\varrho}}}=(\hat{{\bm{L}}}\hat{{\bm{\varrho}}}+\hat{{\bm{\varrho}}}\hat{{\bm{L}}})/2
for a pure quantum state
\hat{{\bm{\varrho}}}

Sidhu and Kok 2020
.
The expectation value
\langle\hat{{\bm{L}}}\rangle
also vanishes.

III.1
Lower and upper bounds for pure states

Because
\mathcal{I}^{C}_{F}
for pure states is a variance, it is nonnegative,
\mathcal{I}_{F}^{C}\geq 0
, despite being computed with a perturbation vector in the tangent space of a point in state space.

However, there is also an upper bound on this Fisher information:
At a given point in state space
, the spectrum of the stability matrix
{\bm{A}}
constrains the maximum value.
To derive this upper bound, we consider the definition of
\mathcal{I}^{C}_{F}
in Eq.
5
and write
\Delta{\bm{A}}^{2}
explicitly as:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}\mathcal{I}_{F}^{C}=\langle{\bm{A}}^{\top}{\bm{A}}\rangle-\langle{\bm{A}}\rangle^{2},

(6)

using the equality
\langle{\bm{A}}\rangle=\langle{\bm{A}}^{\top}\rangle
.
We use two secondary results to upper bound
\mathcal{I}_{F}^{C}/4
.

First, we recognize
\langle{\bm{A}}^{\top}{\bm{A}}\rangle
as the Rayleigh quotient of
\ket{\delta\yu}
with respect to the matrix
{\bm{A}}^{\top}{\bm{A}}
.
The symmetric matrix
{\bm{A}}^{\top}{\bm{A}}
is diagonalizable and
{\bm{\varrho}}
is positive-semidefinite, so we can apply the min-max theorem
Horn and Johnson 1985
to get:

\displaystyle\lambda_{\text{min}}({\bm{A}}^{\top}{\bm{A}})\leq\frac{\Tr(\stability^\top\stability\brho)}{\Tr{\bm{\varrho}}}\leq\lambda_{\text{max}}({\bm{A}}^{\top}{\bm{A}}).

(7)

The minimum,
\lambda_{\text{min}}({\bm{A}}^{\top}{\bm{A}})
, and maximum,
\lambda_{\text{max}}({\bm{A}}^{\top}{\bm{A}})
, eigenvalues of
{\bm{A}}^{\top}{\bm{A}}
set bounds on
\langle{\bm{A}}^{\top}{\bm{A}}\rangle=\Tr(\stability^\top\stability\brho)
.
These inequalities have been rigorously proven
Wang, Kuo, and Hsu 1986
for any two real matrices
\bm{X}
and
\bm{Y}
of order
n\times n
, provided
\bm{X}
is symmetric and
\bm{Y}
is positive semidefinite,
\lambda_{\text{min}}^{\bm{X}}\Tr(\boldsymbol{Y})\leq\Tr(\boldsymbol{X}\boldsymbol{Y})\leq\lambda_{\text{max}}^{\bm{X}}\Tr(\boldsymbol{Y})
.
Recognizing the dynamics of
{\bm{\varrho}}
is trace-preserving,
\Tr{\bm{\varrho}}=1
for all times, the result here simplifies to (App.
C
):

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}_{\text{min}}({\bm{A}})\leq\langle{\bm{A}}^{\top}{\bm{A}}\rangle\leq\sigma^{2}_{\text{max}}({\bm{A}}),

(8)

with bounds set by the singular values
\sigma
of
{\bm{A}}
.
The upper (lower) bound in Eq.
8
saturates when
{\bm{\varrho}}
is composed of the eigenvector of
{\bm{A}}^{\top}{\bm{A}}
corresponding to the maximum (minimum) eigenvalue.

Second, we recognize that the second term on the right hand side of Eq.
6
is nonnegative,
\langle{\bm{A}}\rangle^{2}\geq 0
,
and appears with a negative sign.
Putting this fact together with the Rayleigh quotient in Eq.
7
, the bounds on
\mathcal{I}^{C}_{F}
are:

\displaystyle 0\leq\frac{\mathcal{I}_{F}^{C}}{4}\leq\sigma^{2}_{\text{max}}({\bm{A}}).

(9)

This upper bound has a couple of useful features. Because the largest singular value of
{\bm{A}}
sets the maximum value, the bound is independent of the basis chosen for
{\bm{\varrho}}
.
The upper bound also gives a mathematical interpretation of this Fisher information.

Loosely speaking, s
ingular values are a measure of how much the matrix scales the space it acts on.
So, we can interpret
\mathcal{I}^{C}_{F}
as a measure of the extent to which we can estimate the stretching action of the linearized dynamics in the neighborhood of a point in state space.

When does the Fisher information here saturate the upper and lower bounds?
If
{\bm{A}}
is a symmetric matrix, then
\langle{\bm{A}}^{2}\rangle=\langle{\bm{A}}\rangle^{2}
in the direction of its eigenvectors.
Therefore, the Fisher information
\mathcal{I}_{F}^{C}
vanishes.
In this case, the lower bound on
\mathcal{I}_{F}^{C}
saturates.
For the upper bound to saturate, a perturbation vector
\ket{\delta\yu}
needs to fulfill two conditions: (a)
\langle{\bm{A}}\rangle=0
, and (b)
\ket{\delta\yu}
must be that singular vector of
{\bm{A}}
that corresponds to its maximum singular value.
When these conditions are met, we can see from Eqs.
6
and
8
that
\mathcal{I}_{F}^{C}=\sigma^{2}_{\text{max}}({\bm{A}})
.
This happens, for example, in the simple harmonic oscillator, Sec.
IV.2
.

A tighter, and nontrivial, lower bound is possible for basis sets where
\langle{\bm{A}}\rangle
is nonzero.
To see how, we first recognize that
\langle{\bm{A}}\rangle=\langle{\bm{A}}_{+}\rangle=\Tr(\stability_+\brho)
is an instantaneous Lyapunov exponent associated with a pure perturbation state
{\bm{\varrho}}
.
From Eq.
7
, this exponent is bounded by the extremal eigenvalues
Das and Green 2023b
of
{\bm{A}}_{+}
.
Then, the possible values of
\langle{\bm{A}}_{+}\rangle^{2}
belong to the interval
[0,\sigma_{\text{max}}^{2}({\bm{A}}_{+})]
, where
\sigma_{\text{max}}({\bm{A}}_{+})
is the maximum singular value of the symmetric matrix
{\bm{A}}_{+}
.
Now, using interval arithmetic, we obtain a potentially tighter lower bound on
\mathcal{I}^{C}_{F}
:

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}_{\text{min}}({\bm{A}})-\sigma^{2}_{\text{max}}({\bm{A}}_{+})\leq\frac{\mathcal{I}_{F}^{C}}{4}\leq\sigma^{2}_{\text{max}}({\bm{A}}).

(10)

The bounds are independent of the basis chosen for
{\bm{\varrho}}=\outerproduct{\delta\yu}{\delta\yu}
.
This lower bound will be tighter than zero for arbitrary differentiable dynamical systems with nonvanishing local Lyapunov exponents: specifically, when
\sigma^{2}_{\text{min}}({\bm{A}})>\sigma^{2}_{\text{max}}({\bm{A}}_{+})

III.2
Decomposition into phase space curvature and speed

For a geometric interpretation of
\mathcal{I}^{C}_{F}
, we use Eq.
2
to find (App.
A
):

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}^{C}_{F}=4\|\ket{\delta\dot{\yu}}\|^{2}=\|{\bm{L}}\ket{\delta\yu}\|^{2}=\bra{\delta\yu}{\bm{L}}^{\top}{\bm{L}}\ket{\delta\yu}.

(11)

We consider an infinitesimal perturbation vector
\ket{\delta\yu}
in the tangent space at a point
{\bm{x}}(t)
on the state space trajectory
\Gamma(t)
.
The logarithmic derivative
{\bm{L}}
is a linear transformation on
\ket{\delta{\yu}}
producing another vector
\ket{\boldsymbol{f}}={\bm{L}}\ket{\delta{\yu}}
.
The vector
\ket{\boldsymbol{f}}
is the
Fisher vector
and the matrix
{\bm{L}}^{\top}{\bm{L}}
is the Fisher matrix.
Its
\ell^{2}
norm gives the tangent space Fisher information at
{\bm{x}}(t)
for the pure state of perturbation
{\bm{\varrho}}=\outerproduct{\delta\yu(t)}{\delta\yu(t)}
.
Because the projection of
\ket{\delta{\yu}}
on
\ket{\boldsymbol{f}}
given by
\langle{\bm{L}}\rangle=\bra{\delta{\yu}}{\bm{L}}\ket{\delta{\yu}}
vanishes, the angle between an arbitrary tangent vector and Fisher vector
\ket{\boldsymbol{f}}
is
\pi/2
at all points along a trajectory. For a system with Hamiltonian
H
, the Fisher vector aligns with
\nabla H
.

A natural choice for a perturbation vector is in the flow direction
\dot{\bm{x}}
at a state-space point, Fig.
1
.
For an infinitesimal time
\delta t
, the perturbation in the flow direction is
\ket{\delta\ex}=\ket{\dot\ex}\delta t
.
Once normalized,
\ket{\delta\yu}=\bra{\delta\ex}\ket{\delta\ex}^{-1/2}\ket{\delta\ex}
, we write it explicitly for the flow in the
n
-dimensional phase space:

\displaystyle\ket{\delta\yu_{\dot\ex}}=\frac{[{\dot{x}_{1}(t)},{\dot{x}_{2}}(t),\ldots,{\dot{x}_{n}(t)}]^{\top}}{\sqrt{{\dot{x}^{2}_{1}(t)}+{\dot{x}^{2}_{2}(t)}+\ldots+{\dot{x}^{2}_{n}(t)}}}=\begin{pmatrix}u_{1}\\
\vdots\\
u_{n}\end{pmatrix},

(12)

where
u_{i}=\dot{x}_{i}/\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\dot{x}^{2}_{i}}
so that the normalization condition
\sum_{i=1}^{n}u^{2}_{i}=1
is satisfied at all times.
The subscript
\dot{{\bm{x}}}
indicates the flow direction.
We will use this vector to relate the Fisher information here to the state space curvature.

A classical trajectory experiences the local curvature as it evolves through state space.
Geometrically, the curvature at a point on the trajectory is the rate of change of the unit perturbation vector
\ket{\delta\yu_{\dot\ex}}
along the tangential direction to the flow
\dot{{\bm{x}}}
.
The curvature of a trajectory segment of length
s
is (App.
D
):

\displaystyle\kappa(t)=\norm{\frac{d\ket{\delta\yu_{\dot\ex}}}{ds}},

(13)

where
s(t)=\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\norm{\ket{\dot\ex}}dt^{\prime}
for a trajectory given by integrating the flow
\dot{\bm{x}}
.
Applying the chain rule, the curvature becomes:

\displaystyle\kappa(t)=\dot{s}^{-1}\norm{\ket{\delta\dot\yu_{\dot\ex}}}=\norm{\ket{\dot\ex}}^{-1}\norm{ \ket{\delta\dot\yu_{\dot\ex}}},

(14)

with
\dot{s}=\norm{\ket{\dot\ex}}
by the fundamental theorem of calculus. From Eq.

, the Fisher information in the flow direction is

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}^{C}_{F}(\dot{{\bm{x}}})=4\kappa^{2}\norm{\ket{\dot\ex}}^{2}

(15)

is determined by the local curvature and the phase speed along a trajectory.

This form of the Fisher information is specific to the direction of the flow and independent of whether the dynamics are conservative or dissipative.

The upper and lower bounds in the previous section immediately apply to this form of the Fisher information.
To evaluate the bounds and to elucidate the connection between
\mathcal{I}^{C}_{F}(\dot{{\bm{x}}})
with phase space curvature, we consider paradigmatic model mechanical systems.

IV
Model systems

IV.1
Free particle

The classical free particle has a nonnegative kinetic energy and no potential energy.
It is linear system with Hamiltonian
H(q,p)=p^{2}/2m
and equations of motions
\dot{q}=p/m
and
\dot{p}=0
.
The particle thus moves in a straight line with a constant momentum
p
.
A perturbation in the direction of the flow is time independent:

\displaystyle\ket{\delta{\yu}_{\dot\ex}}=\frac{[{\dot{q}(t)},{\dot{p}(t)}]]^{\top}}{\sqrt{{\dot{q}^{2}(t)}+{\dot{p}^{2}(t)}}}=\pm\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix},\qquad

where
\pm
indicates the direction of the phase space velocity vector
[{\dot{q}(t)},{\dot{p}(t)}]]^{\top}
.
The time derivative of
\ket{\delta{\yu}_{\dot\ex}}
vanishes.
As a consequence, the Fisher information,
\mathcal{I}^{C}_{T}=0
, from Eq.
11
also vanishes, reflecting the curvature-less geometry of the free particle phase space.

IV.2
Linear harmonic oscillator

As an example with a controllable phase space structure, we consider the one-dimensional linear harmonic oscillator
Goldstein, Poole, and Safko 2002
with mass
m
, frequency
\omega
, and Hamiltonian
H(q,p)=p^{2}/2m+m\omega^{2}q^{2}/2
.
Its equations of motion are:

\displaystyle\dot{q}=p/m,\quad\text{and}\quad\dot{p}=-m\omega^{2}q.

(16)

For a given total energy, it is well known that the harmonic oscillator has an elliptical phase space.
The ellipses are a convenient feature for illustrating the dependence of the Fisher information on phase space curvature.
In fact, in suitable dimensionless coordinates
Q
and
P
, we can introduce a single parameter to control the orientation and major axes of the ellipses, and, so, the variations in curvature an oscillator will sample along a dynamical trajectory.
We define the generalized coordinate
Q
and the conjugate momentum
P
through:

\displaystyle q={q}_{m}Q,\qquad\text{and}\qquad p={p}_{m}P.

(17)

The coordinates
Q
and
P
are made dimensionless by the constant factors
q_{m}
and
p_{m}
with units of length
[L]
and momentum
[MLT^{-1}]
.
This coordinate transformation requires that the Poisson brackets be
\{Q(q,p),P(q,p)\}=1
and
\{Q(q,p),Q(q,p)\}=\{P(q,p),P(q,p)=0\}
.
Together, these lead to the condition
{q_{m}}{p_{m}}=1
. We will use this relation to define a de-dimensionalization parameter.

To write the transformed Hamiltonian, we introduce a parameter
a:=q_{m}/p_{m}
, with dimensions
[TM^{-1}]
.
This parameter controls the orientation of the phase space ellipses through the relative contributions of the kinetic and potential energies to the Hamiltonian.
Larger (smaller) values of
a
will amplify the potential (kinetic) energy term and the curvature of phase space trajectories around
q=0
(
p=0
).
Using
a
, the Hamiltonian is:

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}(Q,P,a)={q_{m}}{p_{m}}\omega\left(\frac{P^{2}}{2ma\omega}+\frac{ma\omega{Q}^{2}}{2}\right).

(18)

The quantity
{q_{m}}p_{m}{\omega}
has the dimensions of energy
[ML^{2}T^{-2}]
and the terms inside the parenthesis in Eq.
18
are dimensionless.
The non-dimensionalized equations of motion become

\displaystyle\dot{Q}=\frac{P}{ma},\qquad\text{and}\qquad\dot{P}=-ma{\omega^{2}}Q

(19)

with the solution:

\displaystyle Q(t)=B\cos(\omega{t} + \phi),\quad P(t)=-ma\omega B\sin(\omega{t} + \phi).

(20)

The amplitude
B
is set by the initial condition. The stability matrix for the system in Eq.
19
is:

Upper and lower bounds on
\mathcal{I}^{C}_{F}(\dot{{\bm{x}}})
and their saturation

As we have seen, Fisher information in the direction of the flow is directly related to the curvature of the trajectory. This information is readily computed for the simple harmonic oscillator, including the lower and upper bounds.
The upper bound saturates at the turning points on the trajectory where the potential energy is at its highest point. The lower bound saturates at the minimum potential energy, when kinetic energy is at its highest.

In the flow direction
\dot{\bm{x}}
, we start with the expression for
\mathcal{I}^{C}_{F}
, which takes the form (App.
F
):

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}^{C}_{F}(\dot{{\bm{x}}})=4\omega^{2}\left(\frac{ma\omega}{1+[(ma\omega)^{2}-1]\frac{V}{E}}\right)^{2},

(26)

where
0\leq V/E\leq 1
. From this expression, the Fisher information depends on the potential
V
for fixed energy
E
,
a
,
m
, and
\omega
. Since
ma\omega
and
V/E
are dimensionless, it has dimensions of
\omega^{2}
.

Bounds on
\mathcal{I}^{C}_{F}(\dot{{\bm{x}}})
follow from:

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}^{C}_{F}(\dot{{\bm{x}}})=4\omega^{2}\left(\frac{ma\omega}{1+\left(\left(ma\omega\right)^{2}-1\right)\cos^{2}(\omega{t}+\phi)}\right)^{2},

(27)

which we derive in App.
G
.
The extremal values of the cosine function lead to two cases:

\displaystyle\frac{4}{m^{2}a^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq{\mathcal{I}^{C}_{F}(\dot{{\bm{x}}})}\leq 4m^{2}a^{2}{\omega^{4}},

\displaystyle\text{for}\quad a\leq\frac{1}{m\omega}

(28)

\displaystyle 4m^{2}a^{2}{\omega^{4}}

\displaystyle\leq{\mathcal{I}^{C}_{F}(\dot{{\bm{x}}})}\leq\frac{4}{m^{2}a^{2}},

\displaystyle\text{for}\quad a\geq\frac{1}{m\omega}.

(29)

For
a<1/m\omega
, the minimum and maximum bounds saturate at phase angles
\omega t+\phi=(l+1)\pi/2
and
\omega t+\phi=l\pi
, respectively, for
l\in\{0,1,2,\cdots\}
.
At these phase angles, for
a>1/m\omega
, it is the maximum and minimum bounds that are saturated, respectively.
The bounds saturate because at these angles
\langle{\bm{A}}\rangle=0
and thus
\langle{\bm{A}}\rangle
does not contribute to
\mathcal{I}^{C}_{F}(\dot{{\bm{x}}})
.

To illustrate these bounds, we choose three representative values of
a\in\{1,2,3\}
that determine the orientation of the phase space ellipse.
We choose
m=1
and
\omega=0.5
such that the exceptional point is located at
a=2
.
Panels (a), (b), and (c) in Fig.

show the phase space trajectories for each

.
The geometry of the phase space is an oblate ellipse at

a=2

a=3

As shown in panels (d), (e), and (f) in Fig.

and the potential energy

For
a=1
, the oscillations of
\mathcal{I}^{C}_{F}(\dot{{\bm{x}}})
are in phase with those in
V
, Fig.
3
(d).
The periodic variation in
\mathcal{I}^{C}_{F}(\dot{{\bm{x}}})
saturates the upper and lower bounds at its maximum and minimum values, respectively.
In addition, we can see that
\mathcal{I}^{C}_{F}(\dot{{\bm{x}}})
correlates with the local curvature of the trajectory.
The points of largest local curvature occur at the turning points in Fig.
3
(a) where the potential energy
V
is maximum. Figure
3
(d) shows that at these points,
\mathcal{I}^{C}_{F}(\dot{\bm{x}})

As shown in Figure

a=2

R=\sqrt{2mE}
and constant curvature
R^{-1}

In this case,
\mathcal{I}^{C}_{F}(\dot{{\bm{x}}})
is constant and both bounds saturate.
The potential energy does not share this signature of the constant curvature.
The case
a=3
shown in Fig.
3
(f) is complementary to
a=1

The potential energy is out of phase with
\mathcal{I}^{C}_{F}(\dot{{\bm{x}}})
.
The phase space structure is instead a prolate ellipse as can be seen in panel (c), with a maximum local curvature at
Q=0
and a minimum at the turning points.
Once again,
\mathcal{I}^{C}_{F}(\dot{{\bm{x}}})
correlates with the local curvature at all points along the trajectory and its maximum (minimum) saturates the upper (lower) bound.

Relation between
\mathcal{I}^{C}_{F}(\dot{{\bm{x}}})
and the energy and period

In the case of the simple harmonic oscillator, the Fisher information can also be expressed as a function of the Hamiltonian. Combining the Hamiltonian in Eq.
15
, Eq.
18
, and Eq.
24
, we get (App.
H
):

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}^{C}_{F}(\dot{{\bm{x}}})=\left(16\omega^{2}\frac{\mathcal{H}}{{q_{m}}{p_{m}}}\right)^{\frac{2}{3}}\kappa^{\frac{4}{3}},

(30)

where
\mathcal{H}/q_{m}p_{m}
has the dimensions of frequency
\omega
and
\kappa
is the local curvature.
From this equation, we conclude that for a constant energy given by
\mathcal{H}
, the Fisher information
\mathcal{I}^{C}_{F}(\dot{{\bm{x}}})\propto\kappa^{\frac{4}{3}}
.

One interpretation of the square root of the Fisher information (parameterized by time) is as a speed of the evolution across the statistical manifold defined by
{\bm{\varrho}}

Deffner and Campbell 2017
;
Sidhu and Kok 2020
.
Because the Fisher information has dimensions of inverse time squared, we can also relate it to the natural frequency of the oscillator.
Leaning on our interpretation, we derive an expression for the period of the harmonic oscillator (App.
I
):

\displaystyle T=2\int_{s_{0}}^{s}\frac{\kappa(s^{\prime})}{\sqrt{\mathcal{I}^{C}_{F}(\dot{{\bm{x}}})}}ds^{\prime}

(31)

When
a=2
, the phase space is a circle, Fig.
3
(b), with radius
\kappa=R^{-1}
.
The Fisher information is constant
\mathcal{I}^{C}_{F}(\dot{{\bm{x}}})=4\omega^{2}
.
The arc length of the circle
s
can then be expressed in terms of the angle such that
s=R\theta
.
We then get

\displaystyle T=2\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{1}{R}\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\omega^{2}}}Rd{\theta}=\frac{2\pi}{\omega},

(32)

recovering the well known period of the simple harmonic motion.

IV.3
Damped harmonic oscillator

For comparison to the conservative dynamics of the simple harmonic oscillator, we analyzed the dissipative dynamics of the damped harmonic oscillator.
In dissipation dynamics, the phase space contracts over time, which we expect to reduce the curvature and speed along a trajectory and, so, the Fisher information.

For the damped harmonic oscillator, the equations of motion are:

\displaystyle\dot{q}=\frac{p}{m}\qquad\dot{p}=-m{\omega^{2}}q-\frac{\gamma}{m}p,

(33)

where
\gamma
is the positive friction coefficient
Goldstein, Poole, and Safko 2002
. Again using the parameter
a
to non-dimensionalize, we get:

\displaystyle\dot{Q}=\frac{P}{ma},\qquad\text{and}\qquad\dot{P}=-ma{\omega^{2}}Q-\frac{\gamma}{m}P.

(34)

In these coordinates, the stability matrix is now,

\displaystyle{\bm{A}}=\omega\begin{pmatrix}0&(ma\omega)^{-1}\\
-ma\omega&-\gamma(m\omega)^{-1}\end{pmatrix}.

(35)

As for the harmonic oscillator, we can use the symmetric matrix,

\displaystyle{\bm{A}}^{\top}{\bm{A}}={\omega}^{2}\begin{pmatrix}(ma\omega)^{2}&\gamma{a}\\
\gamma{a}&(1+\gamma^{2}a^{2})(ma\omega)^{-2}\end{pmatrix},

(36)

to construct the Fisher information.

The analytical form of Fisher information
\mathcal{I}^{C}_{F}=4\Delta{\bm{A}}^{2}
for a general perturbation vector
\ket{\delta{\yu}}=[u,v]^{\top}
for the damped harmonic oscillator is (App.
J
):

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}^{C}_{F}=4\omega^{2}\left(ma\omega{u}^{2}+\frac{\gamma{a}}{ma\omega}uv+\frac{v^{2}}{ma\omega}\right)^{2}.

(37)

For the perturbation vector in the flow direction
\dot{{\bm{x}}}
, it becomes (App.
J
):

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}^{C}_{F}(\dot{{\bm{x}}})=4\frac{\omega^{6}}{\norm{\dot{\boldsymbol{X}}}^{4}}\left(ma\omega{Q}^{2}+\frac{\gamma{a}}{ma\omega}QP+\frac{P^{2}}{ma\omega}\right)^{2},

(38)

where
\norm{\dot{\boldsymbol{X}}}=\sqrt{\dot{P}^{2}+\dot{Q}^{2}}
uses the equations of motion in Eq.
34
.
For
\gamma=0
, we recover the expressions of Fisher information for the harmonic oscillator.

As is well known, depending on the damping constant
\gamma
, frequency
\omega
, and mass
m
, there are three distinct cases – underdamped, critically damped, and overdamped.
We will consider these cases next, setting
m=1
and
\omega=0.5
.

The angular frequency for the damped harmonic motion is
\omega_{d}=\sqrt{\omega^{2}-(\gamma/2m)^{2}}
.

For small damping
\gamma

\gamma/2m<\omega

\omega_{d}<\omega

The solutions to the equations of motion in Eq.
34
are

\displaystyle Q(t)

\displaystyle=C\exp(-\frac{\gamma}{2m}t)\cos(\omega_d{t} + \phi),

\displaystyle P(t)

\displaystyle=-Ce^{-\frac{\gamma}{2m}t}\left(\frac{\gamma{a}}{2}\cos(\omega_d{t} + \phi)+ma\omega_{d}\sin(\omega_d{t} + \phi)\right),

where
\phi=-\arctan[(\frac{\gamma{a}}{2}+\frac{P(t_{0})}{Q(t_{0})})/(ma\omega_{d})]
,
C
is a constant,
Q(t_{0})
and
P(t_{0})
are the initial conditions.

):

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}^{C}_{F}(\dot{{\bm{x}}})=4\omega^{2}\left(\frac{ma{\omega}{{{\omega^{2}_{d}}}}{C^{2}}e^{-\frac{\gamma}{m}t}}{\norm{\dot{\boldsymbol{X}}}^{2}}\right)^{2}.

(39)

shows the phase space trajectories, Fisher information, and potential energy for the three representative values of
a
.
Unlike the harmonic oscillator, the point
a=2
is no longer an exceptional point. As a consequence of damping, phase trajectories spiral inwards to the phase space origin; see panels (a), (b), and (c) in Fig.
4
. The local curvature of these trajectories varies in all three values of
a
as indicated by changing shapes of the spirals and their orientation about the
(Q,P)
axes.

As in the simple harmonic oscillator, the Fisher information
\mathcal{I}^{C}_{F}(\dot{{\bm{x}}})
exhibits oscillatory behavior in response to changes in local curvature along the spiraling trajectories.
To illustrate this behavior, we chose a representative trajectory for each of the three values of
a
.
These trajectories are red in Fig.
4
(a)-(c).
For these trajectories, the potential energy
V
and the Fisher information
\mathcal{I}^{C}_{F}(\dot{{\bm{x}}})
are shown for (d)
a=1
, (e)
a=2
, and (f)
a=3
.
However,
\mathcal{I}^{C}_{F}(\dot{{\bm{x}}})
does not decay over time.
In this case, the effect damping is neutralized because the vector
\ket{\delta\yu_{\dot\ex}}
is
normalized
at all times (as long as the dynamics persist in the system).
The potential energy
V
decays exponentially due to damping, as expected.
As a consequence, the largest (smallest) local curvature of the trajectory does not exactly correspond to local maxima (minima) in
V
.
The oscillations in
\mathcal{I}^{C}_{F}(\dot{{\bm{x}}})
and
V
are no longer in phase because the spiral geometry of the trajectories is not symmetric about the
(Q,P)
axes.

The numerical calculations confirm the bounds on
\mathcal{I}^{C}_{F}(\dot{{\bm{x}}})
in Fig.
4
(d)-(f).
The upper bound is still given by the largest eigenvalue of
{\bm{A}}^{\top}{\bm{A}}
which does not saturate as
\langle{\bm{A}}\rangle
never vanishes.
To compute the lower bound, we use the singular value of
{\bm{A}}
and
{\bm{A}}_{+}
according to Eq.

.
In panel (d), the lower bound computed from Eq.

is not useful as, in this case,

at all phase space points.

Overdamped and critically damped oscillator.—

Our results for the overdamped and critically damped dynamics are qualitatively similar, so we present our numerical results for the latter.
The oscillator exhibits critical damping when
\gamma/(2m)=\omega
, i.e.,
\omega_{d}=0
.
In this case, the equations of motion have the solution:

\displaystyle Q(t)

\displaystyle=\left(B+Ct\right)e^{-\frac{\gamma}{2m}t},

\displaystyle P(t)

\displaystyle=\left(maC-\frac{\gamma{a}}{2}B+\frac{\gamma{a}}{2}Ct\right)e^{-\frac{\gamma}{2m}t},

where
B
is a dimensionless constant and
C
is a constant with dimensions of frequency
[T^{-1}]
.
Both are set by initial conditions
Q(t_{0})
and
P(t_{0})
. Their ratio is:

\displaystyle\frac{B}{C}=\frac{ma}{\frac{\gamma{a}}{2}+\frac{P_{0}}{Q_{0}}},

(40)

where
B=Q_{0}
,
C=\omega{Q_{0}}+\frac{P_{0}}{ma}
, and
\omega=\gamma/2m
.
In this case, the Fisher information takes the form (App.
J
):

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}^{C}_{F}(\dot{{\bm{x}}})=4\omega^{2}\left(\frac{\sqrt{ma{\omega}}{\bm{C}}e^{-\frac{\gamma}{2m}t}}{\norm{\dot{\boldsymbol{X}}}}\right)^{4},

(41)

where
\norm{\dot{\boldsymbol{X}}}=\sqrt{\dot{Q}^{2}+\dot{P}^{2}}
.

When overdamped or critically damped, the oscillator does not show oscillations.
Its phase trajectories dampen exponentially until motion ceases.
The oscillator’s phase space for
a\in\{1,2,3\}
are shown in panels (a), (b), and (c), respectively, of Fig.
5
.
To compute trajectories, we set
m=1
,
\gamma=1
, and
\omega=0.5
.
As before, we chose one representative trajectory for each of the three values of
a
and show the corresponding potential energy and Fisher information in the panels (d), (e) and (f) of Fig.
5
for
a=1
, 2, and 3, respectively.

In each of these cases, the phase space trajectory is a curve with one local maximum before reaching the fixed point.
The corresponding Fisher information has a single peak and vanishes when the trajectory reaches the origin.
Intuitively, there is no Fisher information when the system is static and at a fixed point.
A higher peak in
\mathcal{I}^{C}_{F}(\dot{{\bm{x}}})
indicates a higher curvature in the corresponding trajectory.
The potential energy decays, as expected.
The bounds on
\mathcal{I}^{C}_{F}(\dot{{\bm{x}}})
are indicated by horizontal dashed line in gray.
The upper bounds are the largest eigenvalue of
{\bm{A}}^{\top}{\bm{A}}
for the given
a
.
The lower bound is zero as
\sigma^{2}_{\text{min}}({\bm{A}})
never exceeds
\sigma^{2}_{\text{max}}({\bm{A}}_{+})
.

IV.4
Fisher information
\mathcal{I}^{C}_{F}
in higher dimensional systems

In the preceding subsection, we obtained closed form expressions for
\mathcal{I}^{C}_{F}
and demonstrated its relation with phase space curvature for low-dimensional conservative and dissipative systems.
For higher dimensional systems that are not amenable to analytical treatment,
\mathcal{I}^{C}_{F}
is numerically computable.
For instance, the Fisher information is computable from molecular dynamics simulations.
The stability matrix has been computed for atomic clusters
Hinde and Berry 1993
;
Green, Jellinek, and Berry 2009
, small molecules
Green et al. 2011
;
Green et al. 2012
, simple liquids
Posch and Hoover 1988
;
Das and Green 2017
, and proteins
Chekmarev 2019
.
The results here are also applicable to classic dynamical systems, such as the Lorenz-Fetter model, for which one can compute the numerical trajectory, the stability matrix
{\bm{A}}
, and the unit perturbation vector in the flow direction,
\ket{\delta\yu_{\dot\ex}}
(from Eq.
12
).
With these ingredients,
\ket{\delta\yu_{\dot\ex}}
and
{\bm{A}}
, one can compute the Fisher information in the flow direction
\dot{{\bm{x}}}
using Eq.
5
:
\mathcal{I}_{F}^{C}(\dot{{\bm{x}}})=4\Delta{{\bm{A}}^{2}}=4\bra{\delta\yu_{\dot\ex}}{\bm{A}}^{\top}{\bm{A}}\ket{\delta\yu_{\dot\ex}}-4\bra{\delta\yu_{\dot\ex}}{\bm{A}}\ket{\delta\yu_{\dot\ex}}^{2}
.
This algorithm can also be used to calculate
\mathcal{I}^{C}_{F}
for arbitrary perturbation vector
\ket{\delta\yu}
at any phase space point
Das and Green 2023a
.

V
Conclusions

We introduced a Fisher information for the differentiable dynamics of classical systems.
While classical, it is distinct from the well-known classical Fisher information associated with probability distributions that is used in the estimation of statistical parameters.

Its mathematical form is more akin to the quantum Fisher information, and, for many-particle systems, it is a purely mechanical quantity that depends on positions and momenta.
Analyzing this Fisher information
\mathcal{I}^{C}_{F}
led to several interpretations.
As a part of a recent classical density matrix theory
Das and Green 2022
, this information measure is directly related to the fluctuations in local dynamical stability for pure states.
For these pure states, it is also a measure of the speed at which the system progresses through the space of classical density matrices.
The square of the singular value of the stability matrix sets an upper bound and gives an interpretation as the net stretching action of the flow.
Considering the
direction of the flow, the Fisher information depends on the curvature of phase space; A higher (lower) value of
\mathcal{I}^{C}_{F}
indicates greater (smaller) curvature.
To illustrate this interpretation, we analyzed the conservative and damped harmonic oscillator to obtain analytical expressions of
\mathcal{I}^{C}_{F}
and its bounds.
Overall, this classical information measure is computable for deterministic, differentiable systems, is a mechanical counterpart to the classical Fisher information in statistics, and offers a new perspective on how the time-evolution of classical systems depends on the geometry of state space.

Appendix

A
Derivation of Fisher information

The equation of motion of a pure state defined by the perturbation vector
\ket{\delta u}
is:

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\ket{\delta\yu}=\bar{\bm{A}}\ket{\delta\yu}.

(A1)

A pure state of perturbation defined as
{\bm{\varrho}}=\outerproduct{\delta\yu}{\delta\yu}
evolves in time as

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}{\bm{\varrho}}=\bar{\bm{A}}{\bm{\varrho}}+{\bm{\varrho}}{\bm{A}}^{\top}.

(A2)

Using the logarithmic derivative
{\bm{L}}:=2\bar{\bm{A}}
, the Fisher information is:

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}^{C}_{F}

\displaystyle:=\Delta{\bm{L}}^{2}=\langle{\bm{L}}^{\top}{\bm{L}}\rangle

\displaystyle=4\langle\bar{\bm{A}}^{\top}\bar{\bm{A}}\rangle=4\left(\langle{\bm{A}}^{\top}{\bm{A}}\rangle-\langle{\bm{A}}\rangle^{2}\right)=4\Delta{\bm{A}}^{2}.

(A3)

Here, the average
\langle\cdot\rangle
is with respect to
{\bm{\varrho}}
:
\langle\bm{X}\rangle=\Tr(\boldsymbol{X}\brho)
.
Alternatively, we can use
d_{t}\ket{\delta\yu}
to express
\mathcal{I}^{M}_{F}
as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}^{C}_{F}

\displaystyle=4\langle\bar{\bm{A}}^{\top}\bar{\bm{A}}\rangle=4(\bra{\delta\yu}\bar{\bm{A}}^{\top})\left(\bar{\bm{A}}\ket{\delta\yu}\right)

\displaystyle=4\norm{\frac{d}{dt}\ket{\delta\yu}}^{2}.

(A4)

B
Symmetric logarithmic derivative and Fisher information

The equation of motion of a pure state is:

\displaystyle\frac{d{\bm{\varrho}}}{dt}=\frac{d{\bm{\varrho}}^{2}}{dt}

\displaystyle=\frac{d{\bm{\varrho}}}{dt}{\bm{\varrho}}+{\bm{\varrho}}\frac{d{\bm{\varrho}}}{dt}=\frac{\left(2d_{t}{\bm{\varrho}}\right){\bm{\varrho}}+{\bm{\varrho}}\left(2d_{t}{\bm{\varrho}}\right)}{2}.

(B1)

Because
{\bm{\varrho}}
is symmetric, its time derivative
d_{t}{\bm{\varrho}}
is also symmetric.
We then obtain the symmetric logarithmic derivative
{\bm{L}}_{S}=2d_{t}{\bm{\varrho}}
.
The Fisher information is the expectation value of
{\bm{L}}_{S}
for
{\bm{\varrho}}
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}^{C}_{F}

\displaystyle=\langle{{\bm{L}}_{S}}^{2}\rangle=\langle\left(2d_{t}{\bm{\varrho}}\right)^{2}\rangle=4\langle\left(d_{t}{\bm{\varrho}}\right)^{2}\rangle.

(B2)

From Eq.
A2
,

\displaystyle\left(\frac{d{\bm{\varrho}}}{dt}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\left(\bar{{\bm{A}}}{\bm{\varrho}}+{\bm{\varrho}}\bar{{\bm{A}}}^{\top}\right)\left(\bar{{\bm{A}}}{\bm{\varrho}}+{\bm{\varrho}}\bar{{\bm{A}}}^{\top}\right)

(B3)

\displaystyle=\bar{{\bm{A}}}{\bm{\varrho}}\bar{{\bm{A}}}{\bm{\varrho}}+\bar{{\bm{A}}}{\bm{\varrho}}{\bm{\varrho}}\bar{{\bm{A}}}^{\top}+{\bm{\varrho}}\bar{{\bm{A}}}^{\top}\bar{{\bm{A}}}{\bm{\varrho}}+{\bm{\varrho}}\bar{{\bm{A}}}^{\top}{\bm{\varrho}}\bar{{\bm{A}}}^{\top}.

Using
{\bm{\varrho}}^{2}={\bm{\varrho}}
and
\langle\bar{\bm{A}}\rangle=0
, the expectation value with respect to
{\bm{\varrho}}
,

\displaystyle\left\langle\left(\frac{d{\bm{\varrho}}}{dt}\right)^{2}\right\rangle=\langle\bar{\bm{A}}^{\top}\bar{\bm{A}}\rangle=\Delta{\bm{A}}^{2},

(B4)

which gives the Fisher information for a pure state:

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}^{C}_{F}=4\Delta{\bm{A}}^{2}.

(B5)

C
Fisher information inequality of singular values

We partition the stability matrix
{\bm{A}}
into its symmetric and anti-symmetric parts,
{\bm{A}}={\bm{A}}_{+}+{\bm{A}}_{-}
, and notice that:

\displaystyle\langle{\bm{A}}\rangle=\langle{\bm{A}}_{+}\rangle,

(C1)

since
\langle{\bm{A}}_{-}\rangle=\Tr(\stability_-\brho)=0
, i.e., the trace of the product of a symmetric matrix and an anti-symmetric matrix vanishes.
As explained in the main text, according to the min-max theorem and norm preservation
\Tr{\bm{\varrho}}=1
, the minimum and maximum eigenvalues of
{\bm{A}}_{+}
bound
\langle{\bm{A}}_{+}\rangle
:

\displaystyle\lambda_{\text{min}}\left({\bm{A}}_{+}\right)\leq\langle{{\bm{A}}_{+}}\rangle\leq\lambda_{\text{max}}\left({\bm{A}}_{+}\right),

(C2)

where
\lambda_{\text{min}}
and
\lambda_{\text{max}}
are minimum and maximum eigenvalues of
{\bm{A}}_{+}
respectively. The square of
\langle{\bm{A}}_{+}\rangle
is bounded from below by zero and above by the square of its maximum singular values:

\displaystyle 0\leq\langle{\bm{A}}_{+}\rangle^{2}\leq\sigma^{2}_{\text{max}}({\bm{A}}_{+}).

(C3)

Similarly,
\langle{\bm{A}}^{\top}{\bm{A}}\rangle
is bounded by,

\displaystyle\lambda_{\text{min}}\left({\bm{A}}^{\top}{\bm{A}}\right)\leq\langle{{\bm{A}}^{\top}{\bm{A}}}\rangle\leq\lambda_{\text{max}}\left({\bm{A}}^{\top}{\bm{A}}\right),

(C4)

the minimum and maximum eigenvalues of
{\bm{A}}^{\top}{\bm{A}}
:
\lambda_{\text{min}}\left({\bm{A}}^{\top}{\bm{A}}\right)
and
\lambda_{\text{max}}\left({\bm{A}}^{\top}{\bm{A}}\right)
, respectively.
These eigenvalues are the square of minimum and maximum singular values
\sigma_{\text{min}}
and
\sigma_{\text{max}}
of
{\bm{A}}
.

These bounds extend to the Fisher information
\mathcal{I}^{C}_{F}
.
Using the bounds on the terms on the right hand side of
\tfrac{1}{4}\mathcal{I}^{C}_{F}=\langle{\bm{A}}^{\top}{\bm{A}}\rangle-\langle{\bm{A}}_{+}\rangle^{2}
, we obtain:

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}_{\text{min}}({\bm{A}})-\sigma^{2}_{\text{max}}\left({\bm{A}}_{+}\right)\leq\tfrac{1}{4}\mathcal{I}^{C}_{F}\leq\sigma^{2}_{\text{max}}({\bm{A}}).

(C5)

D
The connection between curvature and Fisher information

The curvature
\kappa
of a state space trajectory of length
s
is related to the perturbation vector
\ket{\delta\yu_{\dot{\ex}}}
in the flow direction
\dot{\bm{x}}
:

\displaystyle\kappa(t)=\norm{\frac{d}{d s}\ket{\delta\yu_{\dot{\ex}}}}.

(D1)

From the chain rule, we get

\displaystyle\kappa(t)

\displaystyle=\norm{\frac{d}{dt}\ket{\delta{\yu}_{\dot{\ex}}}}\norm{\frac{ds}{dt}}^{-1}

(D2)

and recognize
ds/dt=\norm{d{\ket{\ex}}/dt}=\norm{\ket{\dot{{\ex}}}}
.
Using this result in Eq.
11
, the Fisher information becomes:

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}^{C}_{F}(\dot{{\bm{x}}})=4\norm{\frac{d}{dt}\ket{\delta{{\yu}_{\dot{\ex}}}}}^{2}=4\kappa^{2}\norm{\ket{\dot{{\ex}}}}^{2}.

(D3)

E
Fisher information for the damped harmonic oscillator

The stability matrix of the harmonic oscillator,

\displaystyle{\bm{A}}=\omega\begin{pmatrix}0&1/ma\omega\\
-ma\omega&-\gamma/m\omega\end{pmatrix},

(E1)

has an expectation value of
{\bm{A}}
with respect to a pure state
\ket{\delta{\yu}}=(u,v)^{\top}
:

\displaystyle\langle{\bm{A}}\rangle

\displaystyle=\bra{\delta u}{\bm{A}}\ket{\delta u}=\frac{v}{ma}\left(u-\left(\left(ma\omega\right)^{2}u+\gamma{a}v\right)\right).

(E2)

Using
u^{2}+v^{2}=1
and some algebra, we get

\displaystyle\langle{\bm{A}}\rangle^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{v^{2}}{\left(ma\right)^{2}}\left(u-\left(\left(ma\omega\right)^{2}u+\gamma{a}v\right)\right)^{2}.

(E3)

Similarly, we obtain:

\displaystyle\langle{\bm{A}}^{\top}{\bm{A}}\rangle

\displaystyle=\omega^{2}\left((ma\omega)^{2}u^{2}+2\gamma{a}uv+\frac{1+\left(\gamma{a}\right)^{2}}{(ma\omega)^{2}}v^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\left(ma\right)^{2}}\left(\left((ma\omega)^{2}u+\gamma{a}v\right)^{2}+v^{2}\right).

(E4)

Now, we can calculate the variance of the stability matrix
{\bm{A}}
from the equation
\Delta{\bm{A}}^{2}=\langle{\bm{A}}{\bm{A}}^{\top}\rangle-\langle{\bm{A}}^{\top}\rangle\langle{\bm{A}}\rangle
:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Delta{\bm{A}}^{2}&=\omega^{2}\left(ma\omega{u^{2}}+\frac{\gamma{a}}{ma\omega}uv+\frac{v^{2}}{ma\omega}\right)^{2}.\end{split}

(E5)

Finally, the Fisher information for the damped harmonic oscillator is:

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}^{C}_{F}=4\omega^{2}\left(ma\omega{u^{2}}+\frac{\gamma{a}}{ma\omega}uv+\frac{v^{2}}{ma\omega}\right)^{2}.

(E6)

When the damping coefficient vanishes,
\gamma=0
, this expression simplifies to:

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}^{C}_{F}=4\omega^{2}\left(ma\omega{u^{2}}+\frac{v^{2}}{ma\omega}\right)^{2},

(E7)

the Fisher information for the simple harmonic oscillator.

F
The relation between Fisher information and potential energy for the simple harmonic oscillator

For the simple harmonic oscillator, the solutions to the equations of motion are:

\displaystyle Q(t)

\displaystyle=B\cos(\omega{t} + \phi)

(F1)

\displaystyle P(t)

\displaystyle=-ma\omega{B}\sin(\omega{t} + \phi).

The time-derivatives are:

\displaystyle\dot{Q}(t)=-B\omega\sin(\omega{t} + \phi)

(F2)

\displaystyle\dot{P}(t)=-ma\omega^{2}{B}\cos(\omega{t} + \phi).

Then, the coordinates of the pure state
\ket{\delta{\yu}_{\dot{\ex}}}
in the flow direction take the form

\displaystyle\ket{\delta{\yu}_{\dot{\ex} }}=\begin{pmatrix}u\\
v\end{pmatrix}=\frac{[\dot{Q},\dot{P}]^{\top}}{\sqrt{\dot{Q}^{2}+\dot{P}^{2}}}=\frac{[\dot{Q},\dot{P}]^{\top}}{\norm{\dot{\boldsymbol{X}}}}

(F3)

where
\norm{\dot{\boldsymbol{X}}}=\sqrt{\dot{Q}^{2}+\dot{P}^{2}}
.
Now, we substitute the expressions of
u
and
v
into the Fisher information:

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}^{C}_{F}(\dot{{{\bm{x}}}})

\displaystyle=4\omega^{2}\left(ma\omega{u}^{2}+\frac{v^{2}}{ma\omega}\right)^{2}

(F4)

\displaystyle=4\omega^{2}\left(\frac{ma\omega}{1+\left((ma\omega)^{2}-1\right)\cos^{2}(\omega{t}+\phi)}\right)^{2}.

The total energy
E
of the simple harmonic oscillator, the sum of the kinetic energy
K
and the potential energy
V
, is conserved:

\displaystyle E=V_{\text{max}}=q_{m}{p_{m}}ma\omega^{2}{Q^{2}_{max}}/2=q_{m}{p_{m}}ma\omega^{2}B^{2}/2

(F5)

Thus,

\displaystyle\frac{V}{E}=\frac{q_{m}{p_{m}}ma\omega^{2}B^{2}\cos^{2}(\omega{t}+\phi)/2}{q_{m}{p_{m}}ma\omega^{2}B^{2}/2}=\cos^{2}(\omega{t}+\phi).

(F6)

Substituting this result into the last expression of Fisher information, we get:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{I}^{C}_{F}(\dot{{{\bm{x}}}})&=4\omega^{2}\left(\frac{ma\omega}{1+\left(\left(ma\omega\right)^{2}-1\right)\frac{V}{E}}\right)^{2}.\end{split}

G
Fisher information bounds for the simple harmonic oscillator

One way to find the bounds of
\mathcal{I}^{C}_{F}
is using the purity of the state
\ket{\delta{u}}
,
u^{2}+v^{2}=1
, with
-1\leq{u}\leq{1}
and
-1\leq{v}\leq{1}
. The maximum and the minimum of the Fisher information,

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}^{C}_{F}

\displaystyle=4\omega^{2}\left(ma\omega+\left(\frac{1}{ma\omega}-ma\omega\right)v^{2}\right)^{2}

(G1)

\displaystyle=4\omega^{2}\left(\frac{1}{ma\omega}+\left(ma\omega-\frac{1}{ma\omega}\right)u^{2}\right)^{2},

depend on the nondimensionalization parameter
a
:

\displaystyle 4(ma{\omega^{2})^{2}}\leq{\mathcal{I}^{M}_{T}}\leq\frac{4}{(ma)^{2}}\quad\quad a\leq{\frac{1}{m\omega}},

(G2)

\displaystyle\frac{4}{(ma)^{2}}\leq{\mathcal{I}^{M}_{T}}\leq 4(ma{\omega^{2})^{2}}\quad\quad a\geq{\frac{1}{m\omega}}.

(G3)

Another way to find the bounds is by taking the first derivative of
\mathcal{I}^{C}_{F}
with respect to time:

\displaystyle\frac{d\mathcal{I}^{C}_{F}}{dt}

\displaystyle=4\frac{d}{dt}\norm{\ket{\delta{\dot{\yu}}}}^{2}

(G4)

\displaystyle=4\left(\bra{\delta{\ddot{\yu}}}\ket{\delta{\dot{\yu}}}+\bra{\delta{\dot{\yu}}}\ket{\delta{\ddot{\yu}}}\right)

\displaystyle=8\bra{\delta{\dot{\yu}}}\ket{\delta{\ddot{\yu}}}.

Because
\langle{\bm{A}}^{2}\rangle=-\omega^{2}I
for the simple harmonic oscillator, where
I
is the
2\times 2
identity matrix, we get

\displaystyle\frac{d\mathcal{I}^{C}_{F}}{dt}=-4\langle{\bm{A}}\rangle\mathcal{I}^{C}_{F}.

(G5)

The maxima and the minima of Fisher information occur when

\displaystyle\langle{\bm{A}}\rangle=\left(\frac{1}{ma\omega}-ma\omega\right)uv=0.

(G6)

This condition is satisfied when the potential energy is maximal, which is when there is no momentum
p=P=0
, and
u=0
and
v=1
in Eq.
24
.
Thus,

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}^{C}_{F}{\left(p=0\right)}=\frac{4}{(ma)^{2}}=4\lambda_{\text{2}}.

(G7)

The condition is also satisfied when the kinetic energy is maximal, which is when
q=Q=0
and
u=1
and
v=0
.
In this case, the potential energy
V={q_{m}{p_{m}}}ma\omega^{2}Q^{2}/2=0
. Thus,

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}^{C}_{F}{\left(q=0\right)}=4(ma\omega^{2})^{2}=4\lambda_{\text{1}}.

(G8)

Since the Fisher information has the form

\mathcal{I}^{C}_{F}=4\left<{\bm{A}}^{\top}{\bm{A}}\right>=4\Tr(\stability^\top\stability\brho)
, we can use Eq.
7
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle 4\lambda_{\text{min}}({\bm{A}}^{\top}{\bm{A}})\leq\ \mathcal{I}^{C}_{F}\leq 4\lambda_{\text{max}}({\bm{A}}^{\top}{\bm{A}})

(G9)

the Fisher information is in the interval
[4\lambda_{\text{min}},4\lambda_{\text{max}}]
.

H
Fisher information in terms of the curvature

From Eq.
24
, using the coordinates of the pure state in Eq.
F3
, the equations of motion in Eq.
19
and the definition of speed
\norm{\dot{\boldsymbol X}}=\sqrt{\dot{Q}^{2}+\dot{P}^{2}}
, we get

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}^{C}_{F}(\dot{{{\bm{x}}}})

\displaystyle=4\omega^{2}\left(ma\omega{u}^{2}+\frac{v^{2}}{ma\omega}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=4\frac{\omega^{2}}{\norm{\dot{\boldsymbol X}}^{4}}\left(ma\omega\dot{Q}^{2}+\frac{\dot{P}^{2}}{ma\omega}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=4\frac{\omega^{6}}{\norm{\dot{\boldsymbol X}}^{4}}\left(\frac{P^{2}}{ma\omega}+ma\omega{Q^{2}}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=16\frac{\omega^{6}}{\norm{\dot{\boldsymbol X}}^{4}}\left(\frac{\mathcal{H}}{{q_{m}}{p_{m}}\omega}\right)^{2}

(H1)

The last equality uses the Hamiltonian in Eq.
18
.
Because arc length,
s=s\left(Q,P\right)
, is dimensionless here, the derivative
\dot{s}=\norm{\ket{\dot\ex}}=\norm{\dot{\boldsymbol X}}
has the units
[T^{-1}]
, which makes the curvature
\kappa
dimensionless.
Rearranging Eq.
15
, we get
\norm{\dot{\boldsymbol X}}^{2}=\mathcal{I}^{C}_{F}(\dot{{{\bm{x}}}})/{4\kappa^{2}}
,
and substituting into Eq.
H
gives:

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}^{C}_{F}(\dot{{{\bm{x}}}})=\left(16\omega^{2}\frac{\mathcal{H}}{{q_{m}}{p_{m}}}\right)^{\frac{2}{3}}\kappa^{\frac{4}{3}}.

(H2)

I
Relation between state-space curvature and period

From Eq.
15
and the derivative of the arc length
\dot{s}=\norm{\dot\ex}
, we get

\displaystyle\dot{s}=\frac{ds}{dt}=\norm{\dot\ex}=\sqrt{\frac{\mathcal{I}^{C}_{F}(\dot{{{\bm{x}}}})}{4\kappa^{2}}}

(I1)

The period is:

\displaystyle T

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}dt=\int_{s_{0}}^{s}\frac{ds^{\prime}}{\sqrt{\frac{\mathcal{I}^{M}_{T}}{4\kappa^{2}}}}

\displaystyle=2\int_{s_{0}}^{s}\kappa(s^{\prime})\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mathcal{I}^{C}_{F}(\dot{{\bm{x}}})}}ds^{\prime}.

(I2)

For the simple harmonic oscillator when
a=2
, the Fisher information
\mathcal{I}^{C}_{F}(\dot{{\bm{x}}})=4\omega^{2}
, curvature, and radius
\kappa=R^{-1}
are all constant. The arc length of the circle can be expressed in terms of the angle such that
s=R\theta
and
ds=Rd{\theta}
. So, the period evaluates to

\displaystyle T=2\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{1}{R}\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\omega^{2}}}Rd{\theta}=\frac{2\pi}{\omega},

(I3)

the well-known result.

Expressions of mechanical Fisher information in the flow direction for the underdamped and critically damped harmonic oscillator

into Eq.
