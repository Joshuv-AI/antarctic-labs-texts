Dialectical language model evaluation: An initial appraisal of the commonsense spatial reasoning abilities of LLMs
Anthony G Cohn, José Hernández-Orallo \affiliations University of Leeds & The Alan Turing Institute & Tongji University

Abstract
Language models have become very popular recently and many claims have been made about their abilities, including for commonsense reasoning. Given the increasingly better results of current language models on previous static benchmarks for commonsense reasoning, we explore an alternative dialectical evaluation. The goal of this kind of evaluation is not to obtain an aggregate performance value but to find failures and map the boundaries of the system. Dialoguing with the system gives the opportunity to check for consistency and get more reassurance of these boundaries beyond anecdotal evidence. In this paper we conduct some qualitative investigations of this kind of evaluation for the particular case of spatial reasoning (which is a fundamental aspect of commonsense reasoning). We conclude with some suggestions for future work both to improve the capabilities of language models and to systematise this kind of dialectical evaluation.

2.2
Size, shape and location

Circle size

Reasoning about relative size is another important aspect of spatial common sense.

Prompt-5.1:
If a circle b is larger than a circle c, is it possible to move b so that it is entirely contained by c?

C3.5-Response:

Yes, it is possible to move a larger circle (b) so that it is entirely contained by a smaller circle (c).
To do this, you would need to move the center of circle b towards the center of circle c until the edge of circle b touches the edge of circle c. As long as the radius of circle b is smaller than the distance between the centers of the two circles, it is possible to move circle b in this way so that it is entirely contained by circle c.
It’s important to note that in order for circle b to be entirely contained by circle c, the radius of circle b must be less than or equal to the difference between the radii of circle c and circle b.

C3.5 is wrong in its answer and also, in the last sentence, in its specification of what it would take for it to be possible.

This will provide the necessary support to the plant without causing any unnecessary movement or creating any hazards in the garden.
In some cases, using a longer string may be necessary if the plant is particularly tall or the stake needs to be placed far away from the plant for some reason. However, it’s important to use the right length of string for each specific situation to ensure that the plant is properly supported and that the garden remains safe and tidy.

Prompt-9.4:
Could I twist the ends of the string together instead of making a knot?

C3.5-Response:

Yes, twisting the ends of the string together can be an effective way to tie a plant to a stake. This is known as a “twist tie” and it can be a convenient and secure method for attaching the string to the stake.
[…]
Twist ties can be particularly useful for delicate plants with soft stems, as they can be tied tightly without damaging the plant. They can also be easily adjusted or removed as needed, which makes them a convenient option for plants that may require frequent staking.
Overall, whether you choose to tie the string in a knot or use a twist tie, the most important thing is to ensure that the plant is properly supported and that the tie is not too tight or too loose. This will help prevent damage to the plant and promote healthy growth.
Here C3.5 doesn’t show any understanding of what twisting the ends of the string means and also implies that it might damage the plant which would not be the case. The end of the response concerning the knot not being too tight does not show real understanding of how the string should be used (i.e. to keep the plant close to the stake rather than tying it to it).

The Handle Problem

This problem concerns trying to formalise what a handle is and what could be used as a handle.
Prompt-10.1:
Give a general purpose characterisation of what constitutes a handle, in the ordinary sense of door-handle or drawer-handle, which is sufficient to enable one to infer from a qualitative description of the shape of a part of an object whether or not it can be a handle for that object.

C3.5-Response:

In the ordinary sense, a handle is a structural component of an object that is intended to be grasped by the user to facilitate movement or manipulation of the object. A handle is typically a protruding part that provides a surface for the user to grip onto, and it is designed to be ergonomic, easy to grasp, and durable.
Based on this general purpose characterization of a handle, one can infer whether or not a part of an object can be a handle by considering the following factors:
Protrusion: A handle typically protrudes from the surface of the object to provide a surface for the user to grip onto. […]
Ergonomics: A handle should be designed to fit comfortably in the user’s hand, with a surface that is easy to grip onto. […]
Durability: A handle should be durable enough to withstand repeated use without breaking or wearing down. […]

The response is a good characterisation, though not really surprising given the large numbers of definitions of handles likely to be in its training data.

Prompt-10.2:

Could I use a blunt conical projection as a handle for a door or drawer?

C3.5-Response:

Yes, a blunt conical projection can be used as a handle for a door or drawer, depending on its design and size. A conical projection can provide a comfortable grip for the user and can be easy to operate.
The size and shape of the conical projection should be designed with ergonomics in mind, ensuring that it fits comfortably in the user’s hand and is easy to grasp. Additionally, the conical projection should be durable enough to withstand repeated use without breaking or wearing down.
It’s worth noting that a blunt conical projection may not be the most common type of handle for doors or drawers, as other shapes such as cylindrical or rectangular handles are more commonly used. […]
This is clearly a failure of commonsense reasoning, although it does note it’s not a common shape – the response fails to realise that this really would not be very effective as a handle. So we checked which orientation C3.5 thought the handle was in, and responded that it is indeed tapering away from the drawer:

Prompt-10.3:
Is the base of this conical handle next to or away from the drawer?

C3.5-Response:

The orientation of the conical handle base in relation to the drawer will depend on the specific design of the handle. However, in most cases, the base of the conical handle would be attached to the surface of the drawer or door, with the tapering section of the handle extending away from the drawer or door.
This orientation allows the user to grasp the tapered section of the handle and pull it outward, which in turn opens the drawer or door. The base of the handle provides a stable attachment point for the handle, ensuring that it remains securely in place when in use.
[…]
So C3.5 does believe that the base is next to the door/drawer. One strong point of this response is the comment that attaching the handle this way round provides a secure attachment point (not literally a point of course!) which displays some spatio-mechanical knowledge. A negative point is that this orientation does not allow a good grasp.
So we challenged C3.5 about the orientation:
Prompt-10.4:
Wouldn’t it be more ergonomic to have the base away from the drawer?

C3.5-Response:

Having the base of the conical handle away from the drawer or door could provide a different grip orientation and could be more ergonomic for certain users or situations. This design could allow the user to wrap their fingers around the base of the handle, which could provide a more comfortable and secure grip, especially if the handle is being used to lift a heavy object.
However, this design could also make it more difficult for the user to operate the drawer or door, as they would need to push the handle inward instead of pulling it outward. […] A handle with the base attached to the surface of the drawer or door and the tapering section extending outward is a common and effective design, but there may be situations where an alternative design, such as one with the base away from the drawer, could be more appropriate.
The first part is a good response, but the middle section is bizarre and clearly fails a lack of understanding about how opening a drawer works. It also displays a lack of common knowledge in the final part where it claims that this orientation is common.

Reasoning about linked chains

Another spatial problem in the CPP concerns chains
9
9

9

The formulation of the initial prompt is changed to try to make it clear what kind of chain is envisaged (open loop rather than a bicycle type chain for instance) – in a prior conversation C3.5 claimed that chains had pins, rivets and hinges.
:

Prompt-11.1:
Formally characterize the structure of a metallic linked chain. What will happen if one picks up one end of such a chain and walks away? By a linked chain I mean a chain which is constructed as a series of interconnected loops of metal and no other parts, such as is used as an anchor chain in a ship.

C3.5-Response:

A metallic linked chain is a structure composed of interconnected metal loops that form a continuous chain. Each loop in the chain is linked to the adjacent loop through a mechanical joint, which provides flexibility and allows the chain to bend and conform to different shapes.
When one end of the linked chain is picked up and pulled away, the rest of the chain will follow due to the interlinked nature of the loops. As the chain is pulled, the links will stretch and become elongated, causing the chain to become thinner and longer. The chain will also experience tension forces along its length, which will increase as the pulling force is applied. If the force applied is too great, the chain may break at its weakest point, or one of the links may become dislodged from the rest of the chain.
[…]

This response starts off well (predicting bending and confirming a following behaviour) but then becomes flawed: one wouldn’t expect such a metal chain to stretch (except by virtue of the links always being tight up against its neighbours) and it would not become thinner (as a string of Plasticine would). The conversation continued:

Prompt-11.2:
If the loops of this chain are large, is it possible to move the last link in the chain at all without any other link moving?

C3.5-Response:

If the loops of the chain are large, it may be possible to move the last link in the chain without any other link moving, but it depends on the specific geometry and construction of the chain.
[…]
If the last link in the chain is located near the end of the chain and there is enough slack or play between adjacent links, it may be possible to move the last link without any other link moving. However, if the last link is located closer to the middle of the chain, […]

This is mostly a good response but C3.5 is clearly confused about where the end of chain is, saying it might be in the middle! We then probed its understanding of what it means to be the last link in a chain.

Prompt-11.3:
How many other links is the last link in a chain connected to?

C3.5-Response:

The number of other links that the last link in a metallic linked chain is connected to will depend on the specific design and construction of the chain.
In general, a metallic linked chain is designed to be a continuous loop, with each link connected to the adjacent link through a mechanical joint. The last link in the chain will be connected to the second-to-last link, which in turn will be connected to the third-to-last link, and so on, until all of the links in the chain are connected in a continuous loop.
[…]
Therefore, while the last link in a metallic linked chain is typically connected to one or more adjacent links, the number of other links it is connected to may vary depending on the specific application and use case of the chain.

There are several odd things here: C3.5 seems to think a chain is necessarily a loop rather than being just
made
of loops. But in that case there would not be a last link! And it explicitly suggests that the last link is connected to more than one other link. So we pressed C3.5 further.

Prompt-11.4:
Earlier you said that the last link could be in the middle of the chain. Can you explain further?

C3.5-Response:

I apologize for the confusion in my previous response. In a continuous loop metallic linked chain, the last link will always be connected to the second-to-last link, and the second-to-last link will be connected to the third-to-last link, and so on. Therefore, it is not possible for the last link to be in the middle of the chain.
However, it is possible for the chain to have a break in the loop, in which case the last link may not be connected to any other links. For example, if a section of the chain is removed or cut, the last link on one side of the break will no longer be connected to the second-to-last link on the other side of the break.
If a break occurs in the chain, the links on either side of the break will no longer be connected in a continuous loop, and the chain will no longer be able to function as intended. […]

This is good except for C3.5’s insistence that chains must be a circular and cannot function as intended if not a loop.

Prompt-11.5:
If a chain is used a ship’s anchor chain, is the chain a continuous loop?

C3.5-Response:

Yes, a chain used as a ship’s anchor chain is typically a continuous loop metallic linked chain. The chain is connected to the ship at one end and to the anchor at the other end, forming a continuous loop that allows the anchor to be lowered and raised as needed.
[…]

This is not only partly wrong but also self contradictory as a loop does not have ends!
We then pressed C3.5 specifically about the nature of the chain.

Prompt-11.6:

When you talk of a “continuous loop metallic linked chain”, do you mean that the components of the chain are continuous loops, or that the chain itself forms a continuous loop?

C3.5-Response:

When I refer to a “continuous loop metallic linked chain,” I mean that the chain itself forms a continuous loop, not necessarily that the components of the chain are continuous loops.
A continuous loop metallic linked chain is a chain in which each link is connected to the adjacent link to form a continuous loop. The chain can be made up of individual links that are not continuous loops themselves, but when connected, they form a loop that is continuous and unbroken.
[…]
In general, a continuous loop metallic linked chain is designed to be flexible and able to bend in multiple directions, which allows it to conform to different shapes and absorb shock loads without breaking. This flexibility also allows the chain to be wound up and stored in a compact space when not in use.
[…]

C3.5 is not able to consistently explain the nature of a chain and contains several inaccuracies.

2.4
Object permanence

In this section we present some other problems specifically invented in this paper for the purposes of probing the systems’ understanding of object permanence.

Where are the car keys?

This problem refers to object permanence when
an agent is moving (egocentric object permanence).
Prompt-12.1:
John and Mary are having breakfast together and Mary puts the car keys on the table. Mary then goes out for a walk. Whilst she is out, John puts the keys in his pocket. Mary then returns. Where does Mary expect to find the keys?

C3.5-Response:

Mary likely expects to find the keys where she left them, which is on the table. Since she did not give any indication to John that he could take the keys or move them, she would assume that they are still in the same place where she left them.
If she doesn’t find the keys on the table, she may ask John about it, but it’s also possible that John may return the keys to the table before she realizes they are missing. In any case, it would be important for John to communicate with Mary if he moved the keys, so as to avoid any confusion or inconvenience.
This answer is good – just what one would hope for. However if we then ask:
Prompt-12.2:

If the car wasn’t in the drive when Mary returned from the walk, and John was out, then where would she expect the keys to be?
then we run into problems:

C3.5-Response:

If the car wasn’t in the drive when Mary returned from the walk and John was not there, Mary would likely check the usual places where they keep the keys, such as a key holder, a drawer, a hook, or a purse. If Mary could not find the keys in any of the places, she might have to wait for John to return or call or message him to ask where the car is and where the keys are.
If John had taken the keys with him and was not reachable, Mary might have to find alternative transportation or wait for John to return. If Mary had a spare set of keys, she might be able to use those to drive the car.
[…]
C3.5 has completely failed to connect the change of spatial position of the car with the change of spatial position of the keys. Worse, it goes on to suggest that she might use some spare keys to drive the car which is not in the drive any more!

Cars behind the sofa

Now let us try an example where it’s the object moving (allocentric object permanence).

Prompt-13.1:
You are the only person in a room and see a green toy car on the left, a sofa in front of you and a door on the right. A car starts moving from left to right and you can see how it approaches the sofa. Then you don’t see the car for a while. Finally a car appears again approaching the door. What happened while you couldn’t see any car?

C3.5-Response:

[…]based on the information you’ve provided, it seems that the green toy car passed behind the sofa while you couldn’t see it. This suggests that there may have been an obstruction blocking your view of the car for a period of time, such as the sofa.

This is very good. It seems C3.5 understands object permanence with occlusion. But let us probe a bit further.

Prompt-13.2:
The car that appeared on the right was red. Can you explain what happened?

C3.5-Response:

Certainly! Based on the information you’ve provided, it seems that there were two toy cars in the room - a green one and a red one. The green car initially started moving from the left side of the room towards the sofa in front of you, and then passed behind the sofa while you couldn’t see it. After a while, the red car appeared, moving towards the door on the right side of the room.
It’s possible that the red car was already present in the room, but not initially visible to you due to your line of sight being blocked by the green car or another object in the room. Alternatively, the red car may have entered the room at some point while you were not observing it. Without more information, it’s difficult to say for sure what exactly happened.

It starts well, but the final part shows important problems, such as thinking that the red car was occluded by the green car, or the comment that the red car could have come from the door, when we said it was approaching the door.

3
Discussion

As we have seen above, the systems are never stuck for an answer for any of the problems posed (though they may occasionally incorrectly says there is not enough information to answer), but frequently their answers are wrong, or display fundamental misunderstandings. Table
1

summarises the findings from the earlier sections. This granular table is more insightful and informative about failures than any performance metric aggregation, and maps the contour of capabilities. The effort in the dialogues is put to find a variation or continuation where a system fails, to properly map this contour of spatial common sense.

It is worth noting that “ChatGPT has limited knowledge of world and events after 2021 and may also occasionally produce harmful instructions or biased content” (ChatGPT FAQ). The problems here are not related to knowledge of events post 2021, and certainly in the tests performed here, most answers are incorrect. Notably most of the questions here require reasoning rather than something more akin to factual look-up, and this has already been widely noted as something that LLMs are not good at. Indeed “all” that LLMs are really doing is next word prediction, albeit with clever harnessing. This has been discussed at length
[
2021
]
, where in particular it is noted that the seeming coherence of ChatGPT text is just that “seeming” – i.e., in their words “Coherence in the Eye of the Beholder”. Ever since the days of the earliest AI chatbot, Eliza
[
1976
]
, humans have been taken in by machine output, attributing too much intelligence to the mechanism which produced and assuming more understanding than actually exists. Bender et al (
?
) suggest that “contrary to how it may seem when we observe its output, a language model is a system
for haphazardly stitching together sequences of linguistic forms
it has observed in its vast training data, according to probabilistic
information about how they combine, but without any reference to
meaning: a stochastic parrot”. Whether actual intelligence and real reasoning abilities can
emerge
from such a mechanism trained on huge amounts of data is still an open question.

3.1
Limitations

This paper is far from a comprehensive evaluation of the spatial reasoning abilities of LLMs, and less so of the state of the art of the fast-changing landscape of foundation models
10
10

10

Liang et al (
?
) are leading a major effort towards evaluating FMs in general, and there is much other work too.
. But it indicates that they frequently fail to display relatively elementary spatial reasoning abilities
11
11

11

A community generated list other failures of LLMs can be found here:
https://tinyurl.com/yap5p9pz
.
. It could be argued that the
interactive approach followed here is a weakness, but we believe it is also a strength since the dialogues presented show not only when the system is right or wrong, but also its explanations of its answers, and these explanations can be right or wrong independently of the correctness of the answer – e.g. the right answer may be given along with a fallacious reason, and vice versa.
The differences between models as shown in Table
1

is very notable, and looking at the correct answers for more advanced models indicates certain progress in commonsense reasoning, but the persistent failures suggest that our dialectical approach could be pursued in order to try to find the new boundaries of more powerful systems.

At present it is not possible to finetune ChatGPT, GPT, Bart or their underlying base models so
this evaluation “out of the box”.
For those models for which finetuning becomes available, we could investigate to what extent the problems revealed here can be mitigated. However, fine-tuning is perhaps against some of the principles of common sense, according to Davis (
?
), because we want to determine if a generic system has common sense, not building or fine tuning a system for common sense.
Also note that so far we have not given any of the systems any feedback on their responses. An alternative to fine-tuning could be
to use
human feedback (through reinforcement learning or other mechanisms such as rule learning, as used in
[
2023
]
).

The LLMs are stochastic, giving different answers for the same prompt for different sessions. This is an advantage when we are looking for a case that shows no good understanding, as we are driven by achieving refutation. However, it makes automation difficult as each dialogue must be based on the answer to previous prompts.

3.2
Future Work

One aspect of spatial reasoning not investigated here is geographical spatial reasoning, for example reasoning about cardinal directions
12
12

12

A companion paper is under way in which the performance of the systems is evaluated – similar problems as noted here emerge.
.
Another area for future work is a more automated approach to evaluation. We have already discussed above some issues with existing benchmarks, as particularly highlighted by Davis (
?
). The advantage of the dialectical
approach taken in this paper is that following an initial prompt, the response can be analysed and further prompts issued to test understanding. This kind of conversation provides an arguably more robust and reliable indicator of the capabilities of the AI system, at the expense of the human being intimately involved – this can thus be seen as both an advantage and a disadvantage because of the cost of the human time. It may be that some middle ground is possible, whereby a pre-stored tree of questions/prompts is created to facilitate an automated approach. Creating such trees would not be easy though, and the difficulty of anticipating possible responses very challenging. Using a multi-choice evaluation removes many of these problems, but there are well known issues with the design and use multiple-choice tests. A challenge for the future would be the automated generation of such trees. This would be a form of adaptive testing.
A combination of both automated and manual evaluation methods is probably still required for a thorough evaluation.

Another aspect to be investigated further, which we started to address in a small way here, is to test robustness of the responses by posing not just a single query, but with small variations, both linguistic and semantic.
The sensitivity of LLMs to such perturbations has already been noted in a Theory-of-Mind setting
[
2023
]
.
