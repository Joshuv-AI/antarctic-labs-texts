Persistence properties of a Camassa-Holm type equation with ( n + 1 ) − (n+1)- order non-linearities
Igor Leite Freire 1 Centro de Matemática, Computação e Cognição Universidade Federal do ABC Santo André, Brazil

Abstract
Lower order conservation laws and symmetries of a family of hyperbolic equations having the Camassa-Holm equation as a particular member are obtained. We show that the equation has two conservation laws with zeroth order characteristics and that its symmetries are generated by translations in the independent variables and a certain scaling, as well as some invariant solutions are studied. Next, we consider persistence and asymptotic properties for the solutions of the equation considered. In particular, we analyse the behaviour of the solutions of the equation for large values of the spatial variable. We show that if the initial data has a certain asymptotic exponential decaying, then such property persists for any time as long as the solution exists. Moreover, depending on the behaviour of the initial data for large values of the spatial variable and if for some further time the solution shares the same behaviour, then it necessarily vanishes identically. Finally, we prove unique continuation results for the solutions of the equation.

. If

(2.1)

We say that

, but the converse may not be true.

Finally, we recall the Grönwall inequality, e.g., see

is non-negative and

We recall that equation (

2.2
Main results and outline of the paper

Our first result is concerned with conserved currents, which are vector fields
(C^{0},C^{1})
whose divergence
\partial_{t}C^{0}+\partial_{x}C^{1}
vanishes on the solutions of the equation (for further details, see
[
22
, Sec. 4.3]
). A characteristic of order

(
m=0,\,1,\,2,\cdots
) of a conservation law of an equation
E=0
is a function

of

,
u
and derivatives of
u
up to order
m
such that
\partial_{t}C^{0}+\partial_{x}C^{1}=QE
. Therefore, as long as
u
is a solution of
E=0
, we then have
\partial_{t}C^{0}+\partial_{x}C^{1}=0
.

Theorem 2.1
.

Equation (
1.1
) admits only two conserved currents with zeroth characteristic
Q_{1}=1
and
Q_{2}=u
, for any positive integer
n
.

Corollary 2.1
.

Assume that
u
is a solution of (
1.1
) vanishing as
|x|\rightarrow\infty
, whose derivatives up to second order are bounded. Then the quantities

{\cal H}_{1}(t):=\int_{\mathbb{R}}u(t,x)dx

(2.3)

and

{\cal H}(t):=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{u(t,x)^{2}+u_{x}(t,x)^{2}}{2}dx=\frac{1}{2}\|u(t,\cdot)\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}

(2.4)

are constant. In particular, if
u
is either non-negative or non-positive, then
\|u(t,\cdot)\|_{1}
is constant.

Note that solutions belonging to
C^{0}([0,T);H^{s}(\mathbb{R}))
, with
s>3/2
, will have both (
2.3
) and (
2.4
) as conserved quantities, meaning that
{\cal H}_{1}(t)={\cal H}_{1}(0)
and
{\cal H}(t)={\cal H}(0)
, for any
t\in(0,T)
.

Our next result gives the Lie point symmetries of (
1.1
). We recall that a Lie point symmetry of a given differential equation is a local diffeomorphism that maps a (strong or smooth) solution of the equation into another solution in a smooth way. As such, the symmetries are fluxes of certain linear operators, their generators, and therefore, once we know the generators we know the symmetries and vice-versa. Geometrically, or even physically, the Lie symmetries can be seen as smooth motions or deformations of the solutions of the equation.

Theorem 2.2
.

The Lie point symmetries of the equation (
1.1
) are

{\bf v}_{1}=\partial_{t},\,\,{\bf v}_{2}=\partial_{x},\,\,{\bf v}_{3}=nt\partial_{t}-u\partial_{u}.

(2.5)

The proof of Theorem
2.2
is straightforward, but long. It consists in applying the algorithm for finding the coefficients of the generator of symmetries
[
22
, Chap. 2]
. This will lead us to obtain an over-determined system of linear differential equations to the unknown coefficients whose solution, substituted into the generator of symmetries, will correspond to a linear combination of (
2.5
). Since this process is very long, and can be carried out by several computational packages available, e.g., see
[
10
,
11
]
, we omit its demonstration.

The fluxes of the generators (
2.5
) correspond to translations in time
(t,x,u)\mapsto(t+\epsilon,x,u)
, space
(t,x,u)\mapsto(t,x+\epsilon,u)
and the scaling
(t,x,u)\mapsto(e^{n\epsilon}t,x,e^{-\epsilon}u)
, where
\epsilon\in\mathbb{R}
. The invariance under the scaling leads us to solutions of the type

u(t,x)=\frac{f(x)}{t^{1/n}}.

Substituting this
u
into (
1.1
) we obtain the following ODE to

:

-\frac{1}{n}(f-f^{\prime\prime})+\frac{(n+1)(n+2)}{2}f^{n}f^{\prime}=\frac{n(n-1)}{2}f^{n-2}(f^{\prime})^{3}+2nf^{n-1}f^{\prime}f^{\prime\prime}+f^{n}f^{\prime\prime\prime},

where we omitted the dependence with respect to
x
and the prime
′
denotes derivative with respect the independent variable. Two straightforward solutions to this ODE are
f_{+}(x)=e^{x}
and
f_{-}(x)=e^{-x}
and, therefore, it is easy to check that they provide solutions of (
1.1
) that does not conserve (
2.3
) nor (
2.4
). On the other hand, the invariance under translations in
t
and
x
naturally lead us to the investigation of travelling waves admitted by (
1.1
).

Let
z=x-ct
and
u(t,x)=\phi(z)
. Substituting this

into (
1.1
) and rearranging the result, we have:

\frac{d}{dz}\left(-c\phi+c\phi^{\prime\prime}+\frac{n+2}{2}\phi^{n+2}\right)=\frac{d}{dz}\left(\frac{n}{2}\phi^{n-1}(\phi^{\prime})^{2}+\phi^{n}\phi^{\prime\prime}\right),

(2.6)

where the dependence is again omitted and the prime denotes usual derivative. Integrating the equation above and denoting the constant of integration by
k_{1}/2
, we have

-c\phi+c\phi^{\prime\prime}+\frac{n+2}{2}\phi^{n+1}=\frac{n}{2}\phi^{n-2}(\phi^{\prime})^{2}+\phi^{n}\phi^{\prime\prime}+\frac{k_{1}}{2}.

Multiplying the latter equation by
\phi^{\prime}
, integrating once more, denoting the new constant of integration by
k_{2}/2
and rearranging the terms, we obtain

(\phi^{\prime})^{2}=\frac{c\phi^{2}-\phi^{n+2}+k_{1}\phi+k_{2}}{c-\phi^{n}}.

(2.7)

Equation (
2.7
) shows that, in principle, we should expect problems at the derivatives of
\phi
if there exists a point

such that
\phi(z)\rightarrow c^{1/n}
as

. However, if such a pole is removable, we can hope for the emergence of a solution with some issues regarding differentiability, meaning that we may expect that (
1.1
) has solutions in the distributional sense. In particular, if the constants of integration

and

are taken as

, then

, and as a consequence we have the solution

, implying that

(2.8)

solves (

In

3
Preliminaries and technical results

In this section we present a couple of essential steps needed for the proof of the our main results.

Given a solution
u
of (
1.1
), for each
t\in[0,T)
we define

f_{t}(x):=\frac{n}{2}u^{n-1}u_{x}^{2}+\frac{n(n+3)}{2(n+1)}u^{n+1},

(3.1)

and

F_{t}(x):=\partial_{x}\Lambda^{-2}\ast f_{t}(x).

(3.2)

Let us denote the families

{\cal F}_{1}=(f_{t})_{t\in[0,T)},\quad{\cal F}_{2}=(F_{t})_{t\in[0,T)}

(3.3)

and their corresponding sub-families

{\cal F}^{\ast}_{1}=(f_{t})_{t\in(0,T)},\quad{\cal F}_{2}^{\ast}=(F_{t})_{t\in(0,T)}.

(3.4)

Note that
{\cal F}_{1}\subset C^{0}(\mathbb{R})
whereas
{\cal F}_{2}\subset C^{1}(\mathbb{R})
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
u
be a solution of (
1.1
) and
f_{t}(\cdot)
be the function given by (
3.1
). Assume that
n
is odd. Then we have the equivalences
f_{t}(\cdot)=0\Leftrightarrow\Lambda^{-2}f_{t}(x)=0\Leftrightarrow{\cal H}(t)=0
, where
{\cal H}(\cdot)
is the conserved quantity (
2.4
).

Proof.

The proof is straightforward once we note that the function (
2.1
) is positive and
\Lambda^{-2}f_{t}(x)=(g\ast f_{t})(x)
. Therefore,
\Lambda^{-2}f_{t}(x)=0
if and only if
f_{t}(x)\equiv 0
. Moreover, we also have
u\equiv 0
if and only if
{\cal H}(\cdot)=\|u(t,\cdot)\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}=0
. By (
3.1
) we realise that if
n
is odd, then
f_{t}
vanishes only when
u
vanishes.
∎

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
u
be a solution of (
1.1
) and assume that
n
is odd. If there exist numbers
t^{\ast}
,
x_{0}
and
x_{1}
such that
\{t^{\ast}\}\times[x_{0},x_{1}]\subset(0,T)\times\mathbb{R}
,
f_{t^{\ast}}\big|_{(x_{0},x_{1})}\equiv 0
,
F_{t^{\ast}}(x_{0})=F_{t^{\ast}}(x_{1})
, then
{\cal F}_{1}={\cal F}_{2}=\{0\}
. In particular,
u\equiv 0
.

Proof.

Since
\partial_{x}^{2}\Lambda^{-2}=\Lambda^{-2}-1
, we have

F_{t^{\ast}}^{\prime}(x)=\partial_{x}F_{t^{\ast}}(x)=(\partial_{x}^{2}\Lambda^{-2})f_{t^{\ast}}(x)=\Lambda^{-2}f_{t^{\ast}}(x)-f_{t^{\ast}}(x).

Therefore, as long as
x\in(x_{0},x_{1})
, we conclude that
F_{t^{\ast}}^{\prime}(x)=\Lambda^{-2}f_{t^{\ast}}(x)
. The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus jointly the condition
F_{t^{\ast}}(x_{0})=F_{t^{\ast}}(x_{1})
yield

0=F_{t^{\ast}}(x_{1})-F_{t^{\ast}}(x_{0})=\int_{x_{0}}^{x_{1}}\Lambda^{-2}f_{t^{\ast}}(x)dx,

which forces
\Lambda^{-2}f_{t^{\ast}}(x)=0
. The result follows from Proposition
3.1
.
∎

Note that Proposition
3.2
has a very strong and beautiful meaning: if we can find a member of
{\cal F}_{1}^{\ast}
that vanishes identically, then
{\cal H}(t)=0
(see (
2.4
)) for all

, in view of the conservation of the

norm. Moreover, this implies that
{\cal F}_{1}=\{0\}
, as well as
{\cal F}_{2}=\{0\}
. On the contrary, if it is possible to find a member of
{\cal F}_{1}^{\ast}
that does not vanish, then any other member of this family will not be identically zero.

Proof of Theorem

A straightforward calculation shows the following formal identities for any

\\

\begin{array}[]{lcl}\displaystyle{\partial_{t}\left(\frac{v^{2}+v_{x}^{2}}{2}\right)+\partial_{x}\left(\frac{v^{n+2}}{n+2}+\frac{1-n}{2}v^{n}v_{x}^{2}-v^{n+1}v_{xx}-vv_{tx}\right)=}\\
\\
\displaystyle{v\left(v_{t}-v_{txx}+\frac{(n+1)(n+2)}{2}v^{n}v_{x}-\frac{n(n-1)}{2}v^{n-2}v_{x}^{3}+2nv^{n-1}v_{x}v_{xx}+v^{n}v_{xxx}\right)}.\end{array}

(4.2)

Therefore, if

Proof of Theorem
2.4
:
Integrating (

) with respect to

-\int_{0}^{t_{1}}F_{\tau}(x)d\tau=u(t_{1},x)-u_{0}(x)+\int_{0}^{t_{1}}(uu^{n})(\tau,x)d\tau=:J.

By Theorem

, we conclude that
(uu^{n})(\tau,x)\sim O(e^{-\alpha(n+1)x})
. Moreover, since

, then

. Thus,

\lim_{x\rightarrow\infty}\frac{|(uu^{n})(t,x)|}{e^{-x}}=\lim_{x\rightarrow\infty}\Big(\underbrace{\frac{|(uu)^{n}(t,x)|}{e^{-\alpha(n+1)x}}}_{bounded}\,\,\underbrace{\frac{e^{-\alpha(n+1)x}}{e^{-x}}}_{\rightarrow 0}\Big)=0,

meaning that
(uu^{n})(\tau,x)\sim o(e^{-x})
. Therefore, it is immediate that
J\sim o(e^{-x})
.

We will prove the result by contradiction: we assume that
u
is not identically zero. Then we show that if this is true, we can find a positive constant

such that

-\int_{0}^{t_{1}}F_{\tau}(x)d\tau\geq Ke^{-x}

which contradicts

.

We begin by recalling that
F_{t}(x)=(\partial_{x}g)\ast f_{t}(x)
, where

Let us define

Then we have the following identity:

-\int_{0}^{t_{1}}F_{\tau}(x)d\tau=(\partial_{x}g)\ast\rho(x).

(4.11)

We also observe that

(\partial_{x}g)\ast\rho(x)=-\frac{1}{2}e^{-x}\int_{-\infty}^{x}e^{y}\rho(y)dy+\frac{1}{2}e^{x}\int_{x}^{\infty}e^{-y}\rho(y)dy,

\square

5
Discussion

To the best of the author’s knowledge, the Lie point symmetries of he equation (
1.1
) has not been previously investigated, as well as a classification of its lower order conservation laws. The Lie algebra of the Lie symmetries of (
1.1
) is just the same as the CH equation (compare
[
1
, Proposition 1.1]
with Theorem
2.2
), which is somewhat expected in view of the structure of the equation. In regard to conservation laws, we showed that the zeroth order characteristics are
\phi_{1}=1
and
\phi_{2}=u
, that correspond to conserved currents of zero and first orders, respectively. We note that these characteristics are just the same admitted by the CH equation, see
[
1
, Proposition 2.1]
and
[
1
, Theorem 2.1]
, respectively.

We observe that from the symmetries we found some solutions of (
1.1
). More precisely, from the scaling
(t,x,u)\mapsto(e^{n\epsilon}t,x,e^{-\epsilon}u)
we obtained two solutions for (
1.1
):
u(t,x)=e^{x}/t^{1/n}
and
u(t,x)=e^{-x}/t^{1/n}
. These solutions, of course, do not conserve both (
2.3
) and (
2.4
). Another sort of solution coming from the Lie symmetries are the traveling waves. In particular, these solutions satisfy the relation (
2.7
). Although (
2.7
) was obtained, in principle, for classical solutions, the fact that it may have a singularity lead us naturally to consider solutions in the week sense and, in particular, we show that the equation has peakon solutions for each
n
. Moreover, as shown in
[
16
]
, these peakons are orbitally stable solutions for (
1.1
) for each positive integer
n
.

As far as the author knows, the first (and unique) work considering asymptotic properties of (
1.1
) with
n\neq 1
is
[
26
]
, in which the ideas introduced by Brandolese
[

for proving theorems

We note that our results are mostly concerned when
