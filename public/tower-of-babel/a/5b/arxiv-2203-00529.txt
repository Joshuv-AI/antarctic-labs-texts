The Duflo–Serganova functor, vingt ans après
Maria Gorelik, Crystal Hoyt, Vera Serganova, Alexander Sherman

Abstract
. We review old and new results concerning the D ​ S DS functor and associated varieties for Lie superalgebras. These notions were introduced in the unpublished manuscript [ DS ] by Michel Duflo and the third author. This paper includes the results and proofs of the original manuscript, as well as a survey of more recent results.

1.
Introduction

The
DS
functor was introduced by Michel Duflo and the third author approximately 20 years ago, but the original manuscript

[
DS
]
was never published. Since then much progress has been made in the study of the
DS
functor. This paper includes the results of the original manuscript, as well as a survey of more recent results obtained by different authors.

The
DS
functor has a large and growing list of applications throughout the literature.
It was used in
[
S2
]

to prove the Kac–Wakimoto conjecture (see Section
7
); in
[
IRS
]
to describe the supercharacter ring for
\mathfrak{p}(n)
(see Section
8
); in
[
HPS
]
to study important
\mathfrak{sl}(\infty)
-modules (see Section
9
); in
[
ES2
]
to give a formula for the superdimension of
\mathfrak{p}(n)
-modules; in
[
HsW
]
to give a new proof of the superdimension formula for
GL(m|n)
-modules; in
[
Hs
]
to obtain reductive envelopes of certain supergroups; in
[
BKN2
]
to compute complexity of certain modules over
\mathfrak{gl}(m|n)
; in
[
CH
]
to classify the indecomposable summands of tensor powers of the standard representation of
OSP(m|2n)
; and in
[
EHS
]
to construct universal tensor categories.
The
DS
functor has been applied to study Deligne categories in numerous papers (see e.g.,
[
CH
,
EHS
,
ES1
]
).

The associated variety of a module over a Lie superalgebra
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{g}_{0}\oplus\mathfrak{g}_{1}
is a subvariety of the cone
X\subset\mathfrak{g}_{1}
of self-commuting odd elements. The cone
X
was studied in
[
Gr1
,
Gr2
,
Gr3
]
, where geometric properties of
X
were used to obtain important results about the cohomology of Lie superalgebras.

Now if
x\in X
and
M
is a
\mathfrak{g}
-module, then
x^{2}(M)=0
and hence we can take the cohomology
M_{x}=\operatorname{Ker}x_{M}/\operatorname{Im}x_{M}
.
The assignment
M\mapsto M_{x}
defines the Duflo–Serganova functor
DS_{x}:\operatorname{mod}(\mathfrak{g})\to\operatorname{mod}(\mathfrak{g}_{x})
, where
\mathfrak{g}_{x}=\operatorname{Ker}\,\operatorname{ad}x/\operatorname{Im}\,\operatorname{ad}x
is a Lie algebra. It is easy to see that this functor is symmetric monoidal. This obvious but remarkable fact does not have an analogue in the theory of Harish-Chandra modules or in the theory of restricted Lie algebras.

For the basic classical Lie superalgebras,
DS_{x}(L)
has been computed for every simple
finite-dimensional module
L
. These computations show that
DS_{x}(L)

is semisimple and “pure” in the following sense: for every simple
\mathfrak{g}_{x}
-module
L^{\prime}

one has
[DS_{x}(L):L^{\prime}]\cdot[DS_{x}(L):\Pi L^{\prime}]=0
. It would be interesting to find a conceptual
proof of these facts, see Section
12
for details.

The associated variety
X_{M}
for a
\mathfrak{g}
-module
M
is the closure in
X
of the subset consisting of all elements
x\in X
for which
M_{x}
is nonzero.
The associated variety for a module over a Lie superalgebra can be seen as an analogue of the associated varieties for Harish-Chandra modules, if we think about a Lie
superalgebra
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{g}_{0}\oplus\mathfrak{g}_{1}
as a symmetric pair. Associated varieties for Harish-Chandra modules have many interesting
applications in the classical representation theory (see, for example,

[
V
,
KO
,
NOT
]
).
While the associated variety in the theory of Harish-Chandra modules is
trivial if a module is finite-dimensional, finite-dimensional modules over
Lie superalgebras have interesting associated varieties. Some applications of these associated varieties are given in Sections
7
and
11
.

On the other hand, the associated variety for a module over a Lie superalgebra is also an analogue of the rank variety for restricted Lie algebras in positive characteristic,
see
[
FP
]
. For example, in many cases these associated varieties for Lie superalgebras detect projectivity in the category of finite-dimensional
\mathfrak{g}
-modules.
This is proven in Section
10
of the present paper; the original proof in the preprint
[
DS
]
had a mistake.

In the category of finite-dimensional
\mathfrak{g}
-modules, associated varieties are closely related to blocks and central characters, see Theorem
6.3
and
Theorem
6.4
. In the original preprint
[
DS
]
, Theorem
6.4
was proven for
\mathfrak{gl}(m|n)
, now it is known for all basic classical superalgebras (
[
S2
,
M
]
).
It also seems that associated varieties can be used to study category
\mathcal{O}
for Kac–Moody superalgebras. Some results in this direction are
obtained in
[
CS
]
and
[
GS
]
.

Finally, let us mention that in contrast with restricted Lie algebras,
[
FP
]
, the cohomological support varieties defined and
studied in
[
BKN1
]
and
[
BKN2
]
are
quite different from the varieties studied in this paper. This may indicate existence of a third definition which interpolates these two constructions.

The Duflo-Serganova functor has also appeared under different guises in theoretical physics. Odd operators
Q
in a supersymmetric field theory that satisfy
Q^{2}=0
are examples of BRST operators, and allow one to employ the so-called BRST formalism, which includes taking cohomology in
Q
. Such a situation arises in several places in the literature. In
[
W
]
, it was used as a key part in topological and holomorphic twists of supersymmetric field theories. Twisting a supersymmetric field theory gives rise to simpler field theories that can either be topological (giving a TQFT), holomorphic, or something in between, depending on properties of the chosen
Q
. A mathematically rigorous approach to the twisting of supersymmetric field theories is explained in
[
Cos
]
. In
[
CCMV
]
, the authors apply
DS
functors (referred to as cohomological reductions in their paper) to the algebra of smooth functions as well as certain vector bundles on the target spaces of sigma models, which are homogeneous superspaces. Like in the mathematical setting, this reduction produces simpler theories which then in turn can give information about the original theory.

2.
Definitions and basic properties

Our ground field is
\mathbb{C}
, and by
\overline{Y}
we denote the Zariski closure of a subset
Y
of an affine space.
By
\Pi
we denote the change of parity functor in the category of vector superspaces.

Throughout this paper we assume that the Lie superalgebra
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{g}_{0}\oplus\mathfrak{g}_{1}
is finite dimensional. Let
G_{0}

denote a simply-connected connected algebraic group with Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}_{0}
. We will write
\operatorname{{\mathcal{F}}in}(\mathfrak{g})
for the category of finite-dimensional
\mathfrak{g}
-modules, and
\mathcal{F}(\mathfrak{g})
for the full subcategory of
\operatorname{{\mathcal{F}}in}(\mathfrak{g})
consisting of modules which are semisimple over
\mathfrak{g}_{0}
. The category
\mathcal{F}(\mathfrak{g})
will be the main object of study after Section
2
.

2.1.
The associated variety
X_{M}

Let

X=\left\{x\in\mathfrak{g}_{1}\mid\left[x,x\right]=0\right\}.

It is clear that
X
is a
G_{0}
-invariant Zariski closed cone in
\mathfrak{g}_{1}
.

Let
M
be a

\mathfrak{g}
-module. For every
x\in X
, the corresponding
element
x_{M}\in\operatorname{End}_{\mathbb{C}}(M)
satisfies

x_{M}^{2}=0
. Set

M_{x}:=\operatorname{Ker}x_{M}/xM

and define

X_{M}:=\left\{x\in X\mid M_{x}\not=0\right\}.

We call
X_{M}
the
associated variety
of
M
.

Lemma 2.1
.

If
M
is a finite-dimensional
(\mathfrak{g},G_{0})
-module, then
X_{M}
is Zariski
closed
G_{0}
-invariant subvariety.

Proof.

For a finite-dimensional
M
,

X\setminus X_{M}=\{x\in X\,|\,\operatorname{rank}\,x_{M}=\operatorname{dim}M_{0}=\operatorname{dim}M_{1}\}.

Hence
X_{M}
is Zariski closed.
Now
M
is a
G_{0}
-module. For each

g\in G_{0}
and
x\in M
one has

M_{\operatorname{Ad}_{g}\left(x\right)}=gM_{x},

which implies the lemma.∎

2.2.
The Lie superalgebra
\mathfrak{g}_{x}

For
x\in X
, we
define

\mathfrak{g}_{x}:=\mathfrak{g}^{x}/[x,\mathfrak{g}],

where
\mathfrak{g}^{x}:=\operatorname{Ker}\operatorname{ad}_{x}
and
[x,\mathfrak{g}]:=\operatorname{Im}\,\operatorname{ad}_{x}
.

The next lemma follows from the definitions.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a finite-dimensional Lie superalgebra and
x\in X
.

(1)

Then
[x,\mathfrak{g}]
is an ideal in
\mathfrak{g}^{x}
, and hence
\mathfrak{g}_{x}
has the natural structure of a Lie superalgebra.

(2)

If
M
is a
\mathfrak{g}
-module, then
M_{x}
is a
\mathfrak{g}_{x}
-module.

Now we observe that for each
x\in X
, the correspondence
M\mapsto M_{x}
is functorial. Let
\operatorname{mod}(\mathfrak{g})
(respectively,
\operatorname{mod}(\mathfrak{g}_{x})
) denote the category of all
\mathfrak{g}
-modules (respectively,
\mathfrak{g}_{x}
-modules).

Definition 2.3
.

The
Duflo–Serganova functor

DS_{x}:\operatorname{mod}(\mathfrak{g})\to\operatorname{mod}(\mathfrak{g}_{x})
is defined by
DS_{x}(M):=M_{x}
.

The functor
DS_{x}
has many nice properties. The following lemma shows that
DS_{x}
is a symmetric monoidal tensor functor.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a finite-dimensional Lie superalgebra, let
x\in X
, and let
M,N
be
\mathfrak{g}_{x}
-modules.

(1)

We have a canonical isomorphism
(M\otimes N)_{x}\simeq M_{x}\otimes N_{x}
of
\mathfrak{g}_{x}
-modules.

(2)

For any
\mathfrak{g}
-module
M
we have a canonical isomorphism
(M^{*})_{x}\to(M_{x})^{*}
of
\mathfrak{g}_{x}
-modules.

Hence,
DS_{x}:\operatorname{mod}(\mathfrak{g})\to\operatorname{mod}(\mathfrak{g}_{x})
is a symmetric monoidal tensor functor.

Proof.

For (1), we have the natural morphism of
\mathfrak{g}_{x}
-modules

M_{x}\otimes N_{x}\to(M\otimes N)_{x}
. We have to check that this is an
isomorphism. This follows from the fact that
over the
(0|1)
-dimensional superalgebra
\mathbb{C}x
, we have

M=M_{x}\oplus F
,
N=N_{x}\oplus F^{\prime}
for some free
\mathbb{C}x
-modules
F
and
F^{\prime}
, and we have

(M\otimes N)=M_{x}\otimes N_{x}\oplus(F\otimes N\oplus M\otimes F^{\prime}),

where
F\otimes N\oplus M\otimes F^{\prime}
is a free
\mathbb{C}x
-module.

For (2), we have a natural map
(M^{*})_{x}\to(M_{x})^{*}
given by
\varphi\mapsto\varphi|_{\operatorname{Ker}x}
, using the fact that
\varphi(\operatorname{im}x)=0
. In the other direction: given
\varphi:M_{x}\to\mathbb{C}
, write
\tilde{\varphi}
for the lift of
\varphi
to
\operatorname{Ker}x
and choose a splitting
M=\operatorname{Ker}x\oplus V
. Then
\phi=\tilde{\varphi}\oplus 0
is annihilated by
x
, and this defines a morphism
(M_{x})^{*}\to(M^{*})_{x}
inverse to our previous map.
∎

The next lemma shows that the functor
DS_{x}
preserves the superdimension of a finite-dimensional module
M
, where the superdimension of
M=M_{0}\oplus M_{1}
is given by
\operatorname{sdim}M:=\operatorname{dim}M_{0}-\operatorname{dim}M_{1}
.

Lemma 2.5
.

For any finite-dimensional
\mathfrak{g}
-module
M
and
x\in X
,
\operatorname{sdim}M=\operatorname{sdim}M_{x}
.

Proof.

Let
\Pi\left(N\right)
stand for the superspace
isomorphic to
N
with switched parity. Since
M/\operatorname{Ker}x_{M}
is isomorphic to
\Pi\left(xM\right)
,
we have

\operatorname{sdim}M=\operatorname{sdim}(\operatorname{Ker}x_{M})-\operatorname{sdim}\left(xM\right)=\operatorname{sdim}\left(\operatorname{Ker}x_{M}/xM\right)=\operatorname{sdim}M_{x}.

∎

In fact, Lemma
2.5
has a natural generalization, as we will see in the next section.

2.3.
Reduced Grothendieck groups and
ds_{x}

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a full abelian subcategory of
\operatorname{mod}(\mathfrak{g})
such that:

(*)\ \ \ \ \Pi M\text{ is an object of }\mathcal{C}\text{ whenever }M\text{ is.}

We define the Grothendieck group
{K}(\mathcal{C})
in the usual way as the quotient of the free
\mathbb{Z}
-module with basis
M_{gr}
, for each object
M
in
\mathcal{C}
, with relations
M_{gr}=M_{gr}^{\prime}+M_{gr}^{\prime\prime}
for every short exact sequence
0\to M^{\prime}\to M\to M^{\prime\prime}\to 0
in
\mathcal{C}
.

The
reduced Grothendieck group

{K}_{-}(\mathcal{C})
of the category
\mathcal{C}
is the quotient
{K}(\mathcal{C})
by the relation
M_{gr}=-(\Pi M)_{gr}
for all objects
M\in\mathcal{C}
. We define the
character group

{K}_{+}(\mathcal{C})
to be the quotient of
{K}(\mathcal{C})
by
M_{gr}=(\Pi M)_{gr}
. Write
(-)_{\mathbb{Q}}
for the extension of scalars from
\mathbb{Z}
to
\mathbb{Q}
. Then by the Chinese Remainder Theorem we have

(2.1)

{K}(\mathcal{C})_{\mathbb{Q}}\cong{K}_{-}(\mathcal{C})_{\mathbb{Q}}\times{K}_{+}(\mathcal{C})_{\mathbb{Q}}.

If
\mathcal{C}
is closed under
\otimes
, then its tensor structure provides
{K}(\mathcal{C})
with a ring structure such that
{K}_{-}(\mathcal{C})
and
{K}_{+}(\mathcal{C})
are quotient rings, and (
2.1
) becomes an isomorphism of rings.

Remark 2.6
.

Since we work over the integers, observe that if
M
is any module in
\mathcal{C}
with
M\cong\Pi M
, its image in
{K}_{-}(\mathcal{C})
will be 2-torsion (although it need not be 0).

Lemma 2.7
.

[V. Hinich] If

0\to N\xrightarrow{\psi}M\xrightarrow{\varphi}L\to 0

is an exact sequence of
\mathfrak{g}
-modules, then there exists an exact sequence

0\to E\to N_{x}\to M_{x}\to L_{x}\to\Pi E\to 0

for some
\mathfrak{g}_{x}
-module
E
.

Proof.

Set
E
be the kernel of the induced map
\psi:N_{x}\to M_{x}
and
E^{\prime}
be the quotient
L_{x}/\varphi(M_{x})
. Then we have the exact sequence

0\to E\to N_{x}\to M_{x}\to L_{x}\to E^{\prime}\to 0.

The odd map
\psi^{-1}x\varphi^{-1}:L_{x}\to N_{x}
induces an isomorphism
E^{\prime}\to\Pi E
.
∎

Lemma
2.4
and Lemma
2.7
imply the following.

Corollary 2.8
.

The functor
DS_{x}
is a middle exact tensor functor and satisfies
DS_{x}(\Pi M)=\Pi DS_{x}(M)
.

Corollary 2.9
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
, (resp.
\mathcal{C}_{x}
) be full abelian subcategories of
\operatorname{mod}(\mathfrak{g})
(resp.
\operatorname{mod}(\mathfrak{g}_{x})
) satisfying (*). Suppose that
DS_{x}(M)
lies in
\mathcal{C}_{x}
whenever
\mathcal{C}
lies in
\mathcal{C}
. Then the functor
DS_{x}:\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{C}_{x}
induces a homomorphism on the corresponding reduced Grothendieck groups

ds_{x}:{K}_{-}(\mathcal{C})\to{K}_{-}(\mathcal{C}_{x}).

We now focus in particular on the case when
\mathcal{C}=\operatorname{{\mathcal{F}}in}(\mathfrak{g})
. Then Lemma
2.7
in particular implies that the following diagram commutes:

(2.2)

\textstyle{{K}_{-}(\operatorname{{\mathcal{F}}in}(\mathfrak{g}))\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{ds_{x}}
\textstyle{{K}_{-}(\operatorname{{\mathcal{F}}in}(\mathfrak{g}^{x}))}
\textstyle{{K}_{-}(\operatorname{{\mathcal{F}}in}(\mathfrak{g}_{x}))\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}

where the horizontal arrow is induced by the restriction functor
\operatorname{Res}^{\mathfrak{g}}_{\mathfrak{g}^{x}}
, and the up arrow is induced by

, where

Remark 2.10
.

The map
ds_{x}:{K}_{-}(\operatorname{{\mathcal{F}}in}(\mathfrak{g}))\to{K}_{-}(\operatorname{{\mathcal{F}}in}(\mathfrak{g}_{x}))
is a ring homomorphism compatible with duality. This follows from the fact that
\operatorname{{\mathcal{F}}in}(\mathfrak{g})
and
\operatorname{{\mathcal{F}}in}(\mathfrak{g}_{x})
are tensor categories, since

DS_{x}
is a tensor functor that preserves the duality.

Remark 2.11
.

The existence of a homomorphism between reduced Grothendieck rings was first observed when
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{gl}(m|n)
in
[
HsW
]
. In
[
HR
]
, this homomorphism was introduced in the language of supercharacters for the category of finite-dimensional modules of finite-dimensional Kac-Moody superalgebras, and its kernel and image were described.

2.4.
ds_{x}
as restriction

For a module
M
, we write
[M]
for its image in the reduced Grothendieck ring.

Lemma 2.12
.

Suppose that we have a splitting
\mathfrak{g}_{x}\subseteq\mathfrak{g}^{x}
so that
\mathfrak{g}^{x}=\mathfrak{g}_{x}\ltimes[x,\mathfrak{g}]
. Then for a finite-dimensional
\mathfrak{g}
-module
M
we have

ds_{x}[M]=[\operatorname{Res}_{\mathfrak{g}_{x}}^{\mathfrak{g}}M].

Proof.

This follows immediately by applying the restriction
{K}_{-}(\operatorname{{\mathcal{F}}in}(\mathfrak{g}^{x}))\to{K}_{-}(\operatorname{{\mathcal{F}}in}(\mathfrak{g}_{x}))
to the equality
[DS_{x}M]=[\operatorname{Res}_{\mathfrak{g}^{x}}^{\mathfrak{g}}M]
coming from Lemma
2.7
.
∎

Lemma 2.13
.

Let
x,y\in X
such that
[x,y]=0
, and suppose that we have splittings

\mathfrak{g}^{y}=\mathfrak{g}_{y}\ltimes[y,\mathfrak{g}],\ \ \ \mathfrak{g}^{x+y}=\mathfrak{g}_{x+y}\ltimes[x+y,\mathfrak{g}],

Furthermore suppose that under these splittings,
x\in\mathfrak{g}_{y}
and

(\mathfrak{g}_{y})^{x}=\mathfrak{g}_{x+y}\ltimes[x,\mathfrak{g}_{y}].

Then we have

ds_{x+y}=ds_{x}\circ ds_{y}:{K}_{-}(\mathfrak{g})\to{K}_{-}(\mathfrak{g}_{x+y})

Proof.

This follows immediately from Lemma
2.12
and the corresponding statement for restriction.
∎

Proof.

2.5.
Supermultiplicity

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a finite-dimensional Lie superalgebra, and let
\mathfrak{a}
be any subalgebra of
\mathfrak{g}
.
We will view

\mathfrak{a}_{x}:=\mathfrak{a}^{x}/([\mathfrak{g},x]\cap\mathfrak{a})

as a subalgebra of
\mathfrak{g}_{x}
.

In addition to preserving superdimension, the

\mathfrak{g}^{x}
-modules, when this notion is well-defined. We continue to work just with finite-dimensional modules.
The

of a simple module

of

(2.3)

\text{smult}(M;L):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\ [M:L]-[M:\Pi L]&\text{ if }L\not\cong\Pi L\\
\ [M:L]\text{ mod }2&\text{ if }L\cong\Pi L.\end{array}\right.

The following lemma is immediate.

Lemma 2.15
.

Let
L
be simple, finite-dimensional
\mathfrak{g}
-module. Then
\text{smult}(-;L)
defines a homomorphism

{K}_{-}(\operatorname{{\mathcal{F}}in}(\mathfrak{g}))\to\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathbb{Z}&\text{ if }L\not\cong\Pi L\\
\mathbb{Z}_{2}&\text{ if }L\cong\Pi L\end{array}\right.

The following proposition is from
[

]
.

Let

be in

-module. Then one has

is viewed as a

-module.

Proof.

This follows immediately from (

2.6.
Properties of associated varieties

Here we list a few basic properties of associated varieties for a finite-dimensional Lie superalgebra
\mathfrak{g}
. Let
\mathcal{U}(\mathfrak{g})
denote the universal enveloping algebra of
\mathfrak{g}
.

We have the following.

Lemma 2.20
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a finite-dimensional Lie superalgebra.

(1)

If
M=\mathcal{U}\left(\mathfrak{g}\right)\otimes_{\mathcal{U}\left(\mathfrak{g}_{0}\right)}M^{\prime}
for some
\mathfrak{g}_{0}
-module
M^{\prime}
, then
X_{M}=\left\{0\right\}
;

(2)

If
M={\mathbb{C}}
is trivial, then
X_{M}=X
;

(3)

For any
\mathfrak{g}
-modules
M
and
N
, one has
X_{M\oplus N}=X_{M}\cup X_{N}
;

(4)

For any
\mathfrak{g}
-modules
M
and
N
, one has
X_{M\otimes N}=X_{M}\cap X_{N}
;

(5)

For any
\mathfrak{g}
-module
M
,
X_{M^{*}}=X_{M}
;

Proof.

(2) and (3) follow directly from the definition, while (4) and (5) follow from Lemma
2.4
.

To prove (1), let
x\in X
and
x\not=0
. Let
\left\{v_{j}\right\}_{j\in J}
be a basis of
M^{\prime}
and
x_{1},\dots,x_{m}
be a basis of
\mathfrak{g}_{1}

such that

. Then by the PBW Theorem for Lie superalgebras, the elements
x_{i_{1}}x_{i_{2}}\dots x_{i_{k}}\otimes v_{j}
for all

1\leq i_{1}<i_{2}<\dots<i_{k}\leq m
,
j\in J
form a basis of
M
. The action of

x=x_{1}
in this basis is
easy to write, and it is clear that
\operatorname{Ker}x=xM
is spanned by the vectors

x_{1}x_{i_{2}}\dots x_{i_{k}}\otimes v_{j}
.
∎

The following lemma is the premise of Section
10
, where the relationship between projectivity of module and its associated variety will be studied more in depth.

Lemma 2.21
.

Suppose that
\mathfrak{g}_{0}
is reductive (i.e.,
\mathfrak{g}
is quasireductive). If
M
is projective in
\mathcal{F}(\mathfrak{g})
then we have

.

Proof.

This follows from (1) of Lemma
2.20
using that

will be a direct summand of
\operatorname{Ind}_{\mathfrak{g}_{0}}^{\mathfrak{g}}\operatorname{Res}_{\mathfrak{g}_{0}}^{\mathfrak{g}}M
.
∎

Remark 2.22
.

There is a natural action of

on the associated variety

of

, where the one-dimensional torus

3.
The universal enveloping algebra and central characters

In this section,
\mathfrak{g}
denotes a finite-dimensional Lie superalgebra.
Let
Z(\mathfrak{g})
(respectively,
Z(\mathfrak{g}_{x})
) denote the center of the universal enveloping algebra
\mathcal{U}(\mathfrak{g})
(respectively,
\mathcal{U}(\mathfrak{g}_{x})
).

For each central character
\chi:Z(\mathfrak{g})\to\mathbb{C}
, we denote by
\operatorname{mod}_{\chi}^{r}(\mathfrak{g})

the full subcategory of
\operatorname{mod}(\mathfrak{g})
consisting of the modules
that are annihilated by
(z-\chi(z))^{r}
for every
z\in Z(\mathfrak{g})
. We set

\operatorname{mod}_{\chi}(\mathfrak{g})=\bigcup\limits_{r=1}^{\infty}\operatorname{mod}_{\chi}^{r}(\mathfrak{g}),

and we say that a
\mathfrak{g}
-module
M
admits central character
\chi
if
M
lies in
\operatorname{mod}_{\chi}(\mathfrak{g})
.
By Dixmier’s generalization of Schur’s Lemma (see
[
Dix
]
), each simple module lies in
\operatorname{mod}_{\chi}^{1}(\mathfrak{g})
for a suitable central character
\chi
.

Symmetrization gives an isomorphism
\mathcal{U}(\mathfrak{g})\simeq S(\mathfrak{g})
as
\operatorname{ad}_{\mathfrak{g}}
-modules. Then since
M\mapsto M_{x}
is a tensor functor we have

\mathcal{U}(\mathfrak{g})_{x}\simeq\mathcal{U}(\mathfrak{g}_{x}).

Observe that
\operatorname{ad}_{x}(\mathcal{U}(\mathfrak{g}))
is an ideal in
\mathcal{U}(\mathfrak{g})^{\operatorname{ad}_{x}}
and consider the canonical homomorphism of algebras
\pi:\mathcal{U}(\mathfrak{g})^{\operatorname{ad}_{x}}\to\mathcal{U}(\mathfrak{g}_{x})
.
Then we have a homomorphism

Z(\mathfrak{g})\hookrightarrow\mathcal{U}(\mathfrak{g})^{\operatorname{ad}_{x}}\xrightarrow{\pi}\mathcal{U}(\mathfrak{g}_{x})

and since
Z(\mathfrak{g}_{x})=\mathcal{U}(\mathfrak{g}_{x})^{ad\mathfrak{g}_{x}}
we have a well defined homomorphism

\eta_{x}:Z(\mathfrak{g})\to Z(\mathfrak{g}_{x}).

The dual map of central characters

(3.1)

\eta_{x}^{*}\colon\operatorname{Hom}\left(Z\left(\mathfrak{g}_{x}\right),{\mathbb{C}}\right)\to\operatorname{Hom}\left(Z(\mathfrak{g}),{\mathbb{C}}\right)

is very important due to the following statement.

Proposition 3.1
.

Take
M\in\operatorname{mod}_{\chi}^{r}(\mathfrak{g})
.

(1)

If
\eta_{x}
is surjective, then
DS_{x}(M)
lies in
\operatorname{mod}_{(\eta_{x}^{*})^{-1}\chi}^{r}(\mathfrak{g}_{x})
.

(2)

For each simple subquotient
L^{\prime}
of
DS_{x}(M)
there exists

\chi^{\prime}\in(\eta_{x}^{*})^{-1}(\chi)
such that
L^{\prime}\in\operatorname{mod}_{\chi^{\prime}}^{1}(\mathfrak{g}_{x})
.
In particular,
DS_{x}(M)=0
if
\chi\not\in\,Im(\eta_{x}^{*})
.

Proof.

View
M
as a
\mathcal{Z}(\mathfrak{g})
-module. Note that
DS_{x}(M)

can be viewed as a subquotient
of this module.
Take
z\in\operatorname{Ker}\chi
and set
z_{x}:=\eta_{x}(z)
.
Since
M\in\operatorname{mod}_{\chi}^{r}(\mathfrak{g})
one has

z^{r}M=0
, so

z_{x}^{r}DS_{x}(M)=0
. This gives (i). For (ii)
take
\chi^{\prime}:\mathcal{Z}(\mathfrak{g}_{x})\to\mathbb{C}
such that

\operatorname{Ker}\chi^{\prime}L^{\prime}=0
. Then
(z_{x}-\chi^{\prime}(z_{x}))L^{\prime}=0
, so

\chi^{\prime}(z_{x})=0
. Hence

\eta_{x}(\operatorname{Ker}\chi)\subset\operatorname{Ker}\chi^{\prime}
which implies

\eta_{x}(\chi^{\prime})=\chi
as required.
∎

Corollary 3.2
.

If
M
admits a central character
\chi
and
M_{x}
has a subquotient admitting a central character
\zeta
,
then
\chi=\eta^{*}(\zeta)
.

Using Proposition
2.16
, we obtain the following interesting corollary.

Corollary 3.3
.

Assume that
\mathfrak{g}_{x}
can be embedded into
\mathfrak{g}
(i.e.,
\mathfrak{g}^{x}=\mathfrak{g}_{x}\ltimes[x,\mathfrak{g}]
).
If
M\in\operatorname{mod}_{\chi}(\mathfrak{g})
and
L^{\prime}\in\operatorname{mod}_{\chi^{\prime}}(\mathfrak{g}_{x})
is a simple
\mathfrak{g}_{x}
-module such that
[\operatorname{Res}^{\mathfrak{g}}_{\mathfrak{g}_{x}}M:L^{\prime}]<\infty
and
[\operatorname{Res}^{\mathfrak{g}}_{\mathfrak{g}_{x}}M:\Pi(L^{\prime})]<\infty
, then

[\operatorname{Res}^{\mathfrak{g}}_{\mathfrak{g}_{x}}M:L^{\prime}]=[\operatorname{Res}^{\mathfrak{g}}_{\mathfrak{g}_{x}}M:\Pi(L^{\prime})]\ \text{ if }\ \chi^{\prime}\not\in(\eta_{x}^{*})^{-1}(\chi).

4.
Description of
\mathfrak{g}_{x}
for classical Lie superalgebras
\mathfrak{g}

In this section, we describe
\mathfrak{g}_{x}
and realize
\mathfrak{g}_{x}
as a subalgebra of
\mathfrak{g}
for classical Lie superalgebras.

4.1.
Iso-sets and defect

Now we assume that
\mathfrak{g}_{0}
is a reductive Lie algebra and
\mathfrak{g}_{1}
is
a semisimple
\mathfrak{g}_{0}
-module. Such Lie superalgebras are called
quasireductive.

For a quasireductive Lie superalgebra
\mathfrak{g}
, we may choose a Cartan subalgebra
\mathfrak{t}\subseteq\mathfrak{g}_{0}
and obtain roots
\Delta\subseteq\mathfrak{t}^{*}\setminus\{0\}
by considering its adjoint action on
\mathfrak{g}
. We have subsets
\Delta_{i}\subseteq\Delta
for
i=0,1
consisting of roots which are either even or odd. In particular we have a root decomposition

\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{h}\oplus\bigoplus_{\alpha\in\Delta}\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}.

where
\mathfrak{h}
denotes the centralizer of
\mathfrak{t}
in
\mathfrak{g}
.
We write each
a\in\mathfrak{g}_{i}
(for
i=0,1
) in the form

a=\sum_{\alpha\in\operatorname{supp}(a)}a_{\alpha},\ \ \text{ where }\ a_{\alpha}\in\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}\setminus\{0\},\ \ \operatorname{supp}(a)\subset\Delta_{i}\cup\{0\}.

We say that
A\subset\Delta_{1}
is an
iso-set
if the elements of
A
are linearly independent and if for each
\alpha,\beta\in\Delta_{1}\cap(A\cup(-A))
one has
\alpha+\beta\not\in\Delta_{0}
.
We call the maximal cardinality of an iso-set the
defect
of
\mathfrak{g}
.
We let
\mathcal{S}
denote the set of iso-sets in
\Delta_{1}
. The Weyl group
W
of
\mathfrak{g}_{0}
acts on
\mathcal{S}
in the obvious
way. Put
\mathcal{S}_{k}=\left\{{A}\in\mathcal{S}\mid|{A}|=k\right\}
, with
\mathcal{S}_{0}=\left\{\varnothing\right\}
.

4.2.
Basic classical Lie superalgebras

Suppose
\mathfrak{g}
is a basic classical Lie superalgebra (see
1.2
). If
\mathfrak{g}\neq\mathfrak{gl}(m|n)
, then
\mathfrak{g}
is a simple Kac–Moody superalgebra (see
[
K1
,
H
]
). The Lie superalgebra
\mathfrak{gl}(m|n)
has as an ideal
\mathfrak{sl}(m|n)
, and when
m\neq n
,
\mathfrak{sl}(m|n)
is simple and
\mathfrak{gl}\left(m|n\right)\cong\mathfrak{sl}\left(m|n\right)\oplus{\mathbb{C}}
. The Lie superalgebra
\mathfrak{gl}(n|n)
has a unique simple subquotient
\mathfrak{psl}(n|n):=\mathfrak{sl}(n|n)/\operatorname{span}\{\operatorname{Id}\}
.

We fix a Cartan subalgebra
\mathfrak{h}\subset\mathfrak{g}
. Then
\mathfrak{h}
coincides with a Cartan subalgebra
\mathfrak{t}
of
\mathfrak{g}_{0}
, and each root space
\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}
is one dimensional. In this case, the parity of
\alpha\in\Delta
is by definition the parity of the root space
\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}
.

A finite-dimensional Kac–Moody superalgebra has a nondegenerate symmetric invariant bilinear form
\left(\cdot,\cdot\right)
. This form is not necessarily positive definite, and some roots can be isotropic. For a non-isotropic root
\beta
, we denote by
\beta^{\vee}
the element of
\mathfrak{t}
such that

\alpha\left(\beta^{\vee}\right)=\frac{2\left(\alpha,\beta\right)}{\left(\beta,\beta\right)}
. For an isotropic root
\beta
, set
\beta^{\vee}\in\mathfrak{t}
to be the element of
\mathfrak{t}
corresponding to
\beta
under the isomorphism
\mathfrak{t}\to\mathfrak{t}^{*}
induced by the form.

Remark 4.1
.

The notion of defect was originally introduced in
[
KW
]
for Kac–Moody superalgebras. Finite-dimensional Kac–Moody superalgebras are quasireductive, and in this case, the notion of iso-set corresponds to the well-known notion of isotropic set: a set of mutually orthogonal
linearly independent isotropic roots in
\Delta_{1}
. One can see that the defect of
\mathfrak{g}
in these cases is equal to the dimension of maximal isotropic subspace in
\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{R}}\Delta
.

For finite-dimensional Kac-Moody Lie superalgebras the defect has the following geometric interpretation: it is given by the dimension of the geometric quotient of
\mathfrak{g}_{1}
by
G_{0}
. In fact in these cases,
S(\mathfrak{g}_{1})^{G_{0}}
is a polynomial algebra, and the number of generators is given by the defect. For
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{q}(n),\mathfrak{p}(n)
,
S(\mathfrak{g}_{1})^{G_{0}}
is again a polynomial algebra, except the number of generators for
\mathfrak{q}(n)
is
n
while the number of generators of
\mathfrak{p}(n)
is
\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor
.

In
[
BKN1
]
, a cohomological definition of support varieties was given using the relative
\operatorname{Ext}
functor. There, they define the defect of a
\mathfrak{g}
to be the dimension of
\operatorname{Ext}_{\mathcal{F}(\mathfrak{g})}^{\bullet}(\mathbb{C},\mathbb{C})\cong S(\mathfrak{g}_{1})^{G_{0}}
.

4.3.
The Lie superalgebras
\mathfrak{p}(n)
and
\mathfrak{q}(n)

4.3.1.
\mathfrak{p}(n)

The periplectic Lie superalgebra
\mathfrak{p}(n)
and the queer Lie superalgebra
\mathfrak{q}(n)
are quasireductive Lie superalgebras and can be realized as subalgebras of
\mathfrak{gl}(n|n)
.

With respect to a suitable basis, the periplectic Lie superalgebra
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{p}(n)
consists of block matrices of the form

\left(\begin{array}[]{c|r}A&B\\
\hline\cr C&-A^{t}\end{array}\right),

where
B
is symmetric,
C
is skew-symmetric, and
\mathfrak{t}:=\mathfrak{h}_{0}
is the diagonal Cartan subalgebra of
\mathfrak{g}_{0}\cong\mathfrak{gl}(n)
.
Then
\mathfrak{g}
has a
\mathbb{Z}
-grading

\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{g}^{1}\oplus\mathfrak{g}^{0}\oplus\mathfrak{g}^{-1}
such that
\mathfrak{g}^{0}=\mathfrak{g}_{0}
,

\mathfrak{g}_{1}=\mathfrak{g}^{1}\oplus\mathfrak{g}^{-1}
, and corresponding sets of roots

\Delta_{0}=\Delta(\mathfrak{g}^{0})=\{\varepsilon_{i}-\varepsilon_{j}\mid 1\leq i\neq j\leq n\}
,

\Delta(\mathfrak{g}^{-1})=\{-(\varepsilon_{i}+\varepsilon_{j})\mid 1\leq i<j\leq n\}
, and

\Delta(\mathfrak{g}^{1})=\{\varepsilon_{i}+\varepsilon_{j}\mid 1\leq i\leq j\leq n\}
.
Imposing the additional condition that
\operatorname{tr}A=0
defines the Lie superalgebra
\mathfrak{\mathbf{s}p}(n)
, which is simple when
n\geq 3
; however,

\mathfrak{\mathbf{s}p}(n)
does not admit a nondegenerate (even or odd) invariant bilinear form.

4.3.2.
\mathfrak{q}(n)

With respect to an appropriate basis of
\mathbb{C}^{n|n}
, the queer Lie superalgebra
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{q}(n)
consists of block matrices of the form

(4.1)

T_{A,B}:=\left(\begin{array}[]{c|r}A&B\\
\hline\cr B&A\end{array}\right),

such that
\mathfrak{t}:=\mathfrak{h}_{0}
is the diagonal Cartan subalgebra of
\mathfrak{g}_{0}\cong\mathfrak{gl}(n)
.
The set of roots for
\mathfrak{q}(n)
is
\Delta=\{\pm(\varepsilon_{i}-\varepsilon_{j})\mid 1\leq i<j\leq n\}
, and each root
\alpha\in\Delta
is both even and odd since
\operatorname{dim}(\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha})_{0}=\operatorname{dim}(\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha})_{1}=1
.
Imposing the additional condition
\operatorname{tr}B=0
defines the Lie superalgebra
\mathfrak{sq}(n)
, and since
\operatorname{Id}\in\mathfrak{sq}(n)
we can also define the Lie superalgebra
\mathfrak{psq}(n):=\mathfrak{sq}(n)/\langle\operatorname{Id}\rangle
, which is simple for
n\geq 3
.

Suppose

The following table describes

Note that in the last three rows the defect of

The functor

Let

be one of the Lie superalgebras listed in the above table, and let

is as follows:

•

•

•

Remark 4.3
.

Note that if

then

.

Remark 4.4
.

For
\mathfrak{g}\mathfrak{l}(m|n)
,
\mathfrak{sl}(m|n)
and

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a classical Lie superalgebra with

, and let
x\in X
. We now explain how to realize

as a subalgebra of

, in such a way that

. For
\mathfrak{p}(n)
we will also have such an embedding, but the construction is more ad-hoc, so we state it separately.

Thus we assume
\mathfrak{g}\neq\mathfrak{p}(n)
, and as above, we let
{A}=\left\{\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{k}\right\}\in\mathcal{S}
and
x=x_{\beta_{1}}+\dots+x_{\beta_{k}}
for some nonzero
x_{\beta_{i}}\in(\mathfrak{g}_{\beta_{i}})_{1}

where

In particular,

For each
\beta_{i}\in A

\mathfrak{h}_{\beta_{i}}=\left[(\mathfrak{g}_{\beta_{i}})_{1},(\mathfrak{g}_{-\beta_{i}})_{1}\right]

(4.2)

\mathfrak{h}_{{A}}:=\mathfrak{h}_{\beta_{1}}\oplus\dots\oplus\mathfrak{h}_{\beta_{k}},\quad\mathfrak{h}_{A}^{\perp}:=\operatorname{Ker}\beta_{1}\cap\dots\cap\operatorname{Ker}\beta_{k},\quad\Delta_{x}:={A}^{\perp}\cap(\Delta\backslash\left({A}\cup-{A}\right)).

We have the following.

Proposition 4.5
.

Suppose
\mathfrak{g}\neq\mathfrak{p}(n)
is a classical Lie superalgebra, and let
A\in\mathcal{S}
with corresponding

. Then
\mathfrak{g}_{x}
is isomorphic to the root subalgebra generated by

and a splitting of
\mathfrak{h}_{x}\cong\mathfrak{h}_{A}^{\perp}/\mathfrak{h}_{A}
of
\mathfrak{h}_{A}
, and this splitting

will define a Cartan subalgebra of

. If we identify
\mathfrak{g}_{x}
with its image in

\mathfrak{g}^{x}=\mathfrak{g}_{x}\ltimes[x,\mathfrak{g}]

Proof.

First, note that there is an isomorphism

\mathfrak{g}_{x}\cong(\mathfrak{g}^{0}\cap\mathfrak{g}^{x})/(\mathfrak{g}^{0}\cap\left[x,\mathfrak{g}\right]).

We observe that

\mathfrak{g}^{0}\cap\mathfrak{g}^{x}=\mathfrak{h}_{{A}}^{\perp}\oplus\bigoplus\limits_{\alpha\in{A}^{\perp}\cap(\Delta\backslash-{A})}\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}\text{, }\hskip 28.45274pt\mathfrak{g}^{0}\cap\left[x,\mathfrak{g}\right]=\mathfrak{h}_{{A}}\oplus\mathfrak{g}_{\beta_{1}}\oplus\dots\oplus\mathfrak{g}_{\beta_{k}}.

The above equalities follow from the representation theory of

. Now it is clear that one can choose

in such a way that

\mathfrak{g}_{x}=\left(\mathfrak{h}_{x}\oplus\oplus_{\alpha\in\Delta_{x}}\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}\right)

\mathfrak{g}
.
∎

5.1.
G_{0}
-orbits on

Theorem 5.1
.

Suppose
\mathfrak{g}
is a basic classical Lie superalgebra or
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{q}(n)
. Then
there are finitely many
G_{0}
-orbits on
X

are in one-to-one correspondence with

-orbits in

via the map

(5.1)

\Phi:\mathcal{S}\to X/G_{0}

defined by

{A}=\left\{\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{k}\right\}\mapsto G_{0}x,

where
x=x_{\beta_{1}}+\dots+x_{\beta_{k}}
for some nonzero
x_{\beta_{i}}\in\mathfrak{g}_{\beta_{i}}

Proof.

To see that
\Phi\left({A}\right)
does not depend on a choice of

note that since
\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{k}
are linearly independent, for any other choice

x^{\prime}=\Sigma x_{\beta_{i}}^{\prime}=\Sigma c_{i}x_{\beta_{i}}

there is

such that

and therefore

x^{\prime}=\exp\left(\operatorname{ad}_{h}\right)\left(x\right).

If
{\text{B}}=w\left({A}\right)
for some

, then clearly
\Phi\left({\text{B}}\right)
and
\Phi\left({A}\right)

\Phi
induces the map
\overline{\Phi}:\mathcal{S}/W\to X/G_{0}
. We check
case by case that

is injective and surjective.

If
\mathfrak{g}
is
{\mathfrak{s}}{\mathfrak{l}}\left(m|n\right)

\mathfrak{g}{\mathfrak{l}}\left(n|n\right)

\mathfrak{g}
has a natural
{\mathbb{Z}}

\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{g}^{1}\oplus\mathfrak{g}^{0}\oplus\mathfrak{g}^{-1}

,

\mathfrak{g}_{1}=\mathfrak{g}^{1}\oplus\mathfrak{g}^{-1}

are

5.2.
The stabilizer and normalizer of

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a basic classical Lie superalgebra.
Let

of

is connected.
Furthermore,
G_{0}^{x}
is a semidirect product of a reductive group

and the normal unipotents

(\mathfrak{g}_{x})_{0}
and

Proof.

The second assertion follows from Proposition
4.5
.
To check the connectedness we use the explicit construction of orbits given in the proof of Theorem

.

Let
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{gl}(m|n)
. Then
G_{0}=GL(m)\times GL(n)
, consider the parabolic subgroups

which stabilizes the flag

\operatorname{Im}x^{+}\subset\operatorname{Ker}x^{-}
and

which stabilizes the flag

\operatorname{Im}x^{-}\subset\operatorname{Ker}x^{+}

K_{1}
of

is isomorphic to

and the Levi subgroup of
K_{2}
of
P_{2}
is

GL(p)\times GL(q)\times GL(n-k)
. Let
K\simeq K_{1}\times K_{2}
is the subgroup isomorphic to
GL(p)\times GL(q)\times GL(m-k)\times GL(n-k)

where

and

are embedded diagonally. Then

, where
U
is the unipotent normal subgroup of

.

Let
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{osp}(m|2n)
. Then
G_{0}=SO(m)\times SP(2n)
, consider the parabolic subgroups
P_{1}\subset SO(m)
which stabilizes
\operatorname{Im}x^{*}

and
P_{2}\subset SP(n)
which stabilizes

\operatorname{Im}x
. The Levi subgroup of
K_{1}
of
P_{1}
is isomorphic to
GL(k)\times SO(m-2k)
and the Levi subgroup of
K_{2}
of
P_{2}
is
isomorphic to
GL(k)\times SP(2n-2k)
. Let
K\simeq K_{1}\times K_{2}
is the subgroup isomorphic to
GL(k)\times SO(m-2k)\times SP(2n-2k)

where
GL(k)
is embedded diagonally. Then
G_{0}^{x}=K\rtimes U
, where
U
is the unipotent normal subgroup of
P_{1}\times P_{2}
.

In all exceptional cases
x
is a highest weight vector in
\mathfrak{g}_{1}
and
G_{0}^{x}
is a subgroup of codimension
1
in the parabolic subgroup
P\subset G_{0}
,
the latter is the stabilizer of
\mathbb{C}x
in the projectivization of
\mathfrak{g}_{1}
.
∎

Remark 5.5
.

It follows from above proof that there exists a parabolic subgroup
P\subset G_{0}
such that
G_{0}^{x}
is a subgroup of
P
and the maximal normal unipotent
subgroup of
G_{0}^{x}
is equal to that of
P
.

Remark 5.6
.

For the
\mathfrak{q}(n)
it remains true that
G_{0}^{x}
is connected; this follows from a result of Springer and Steinberg, (see Chpt. IV, Sec. 1.7 of
[
SpSt
]
).

We write
N_{0}^{x}
for the normalizer of
x
in
G_{0}
.

Corollary 5.7
.

For
\mathfrak{g}
basic classical and for
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{q}(n)
,

N_{0}^{x}
is connected.

Proof.

We have an exact sequence

1\to G_{0}^{x}\to N_{0}^{x}\xrightarrow{\alpha}\mathbb{G}_{m}\to 1,

where
\mathbb{G}_{m}
is the one-dimensional torus and

\alpha(g)=(g\cdot x)/x
, where
g\cdot x
is the action of
g
on
x
. By a case-by-case check, the map
\alpha
is always surjective and split. We may now use Lemma
5.4
.
∎

Remark 5.8
.

For
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}
, we have an equality of functors
DS_{x}=DS_{\lambda x}
. It follows that
N_{0}^{x}
acts naturally on the functor
DS_{x}
. We have shown that

N_{0}^{x}=G_{0}^{x}\rtimes\mathbb{G}_{m}.

For
\mathfrak{g}
basic classical or
\mathfrak{q}(n)
, we have shown
G_{0}^{x}
is connected, and thus its symmetries are encompassed in
(\mathfrak{g}_{x})_{0}
. It follows that the only additional symmetries we obtain in this fashion are from the extra action of
\mathbb{G}_{m}
.

Remark 5.9
.

We note that for the
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{p}(n)
, Lemma
5.4
and Corollary
5.7
are not true for all choices of
x
. Issues arise due to the orthogonal group being disconnected and the lack of a splitting for
\alpha
in general.

Throughout the rest of Section

Using the explicit description of

Lemma 5.10
.

Let

(1)

If

is a linear combination of roots from

\alpha\in{A}\cup-{A}

(2)

If

(3)

\Phi\left({A}\right)

\Phi\left({\text{B}}\right)
iff
w\left({A}\right)\subset{\text{B}}

Recall the definition of

Theorem 5.11
.

Let
{A}\in\mathcal{S}
. Then
\operatorname{dim}\Phi\left({A}\right)=\frac{|\Delta_{1}\backslash{A}^{\perp}|}{2}+|{A}|
.

Proof.

Let
{A}=\left\{\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{k}\right\}
,
x=x_{\beta_{1}}+\dots+x_{\beta_{k}}
for some choice of
x_{\beta_{i}}\in\mathfrak{g}_{\beta_{i}}
,

y=y_{\beta_{1}}+\dots+y_{\beta_{k}}
for some
y_{\beta_{i}}\in\mathfrak{g}_{-\beta_{i}}
. Let
h=\left[x,y\right]
,
h_{\beta_{i}}=\left[x_{\beta_{i}},y_{\beta_{i}}\right]
. Clearly,

h=h_{\beta_{1}}+\dots+h_{\beta_{k}}
and
h,x,y
generate an
\mathfrak{sl}\left(1|1\right)
-subalgebra in
\mathfrak{g}
. As a module over this subalgebra

\mathfrak{g}
has a decomposition

\mathfrak{g}=\oplus_{\mu}\mathfrak{g}^{\mu},

where

\mathfrak{g}^{\mu}=\left\{g\in\mathfrak{g}\mid\left[h,g\right]=\mu(h)g\right\}.

Note that

\operatorname{dim}\left[\mathfrak{g},x\right]=\sum_{\mu}\operatorname{dim}\left[\mathfrak{g}^{\mu},x\right],

and from the description of irreducible
\mathfrak{sl}\left(1|1\right)
-modules for
\mu\not=0

\operatorname{dim}\left[\mathfrak{g}^{\mu},x\right]=\frac{\operatorname{dim}\mathfrak{g}^{\mu}}{2}.

On the other hand, for
\mu\not=0
,
\operatorname{sdim}\mathfrak{g}^{\mu}=0
and therefore

\operatorname{dim}\mathfrak{g}^{\mu}=2\operatorname{dim}\mathfrak{g}_{1}^{\mu}.

Observe that for a generic choice of
x_{\beta_{i}}\in\mathfrak{g}_{\beta_{i}}
,
\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}\subset\mathfrak{g}^{0}
iff
\left(\alpha,\beta_{i}\right)=0
for
all
i\leq k
. Indeed, for generic
choice of
x_{\beta_{i}}
the condition
\alpha\left(h\right)=0
implies
\alpha\left(h_{\beta_{i}}\right)=0
for all
i
, and
therefore
\left(\alpha,\beta_{i}\right)=0
for all
i
. Hence

\oplus_{\mu\not=0}\mathfrak{g}_{1}^{\mu}=\oplus_{\alpha\in\Delta_{1}\backslash{A}^{\perp}}\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}

and

\sum_{\mu\not=0}\operatorname{dim}\left[\mathfrak{g}^{\mu},x\right]=\sum_{\mu\not=0}\operatorname{dim}\mathfrak{g}_{1}^{\mu}=|\Delta_{1}\backslash{A}^{\perp}|.

To calculate
\operatorname{dim}\left[\mathfrak{g}^{0},x\right]
note that

\mathfrak{g}^{0}=\mathfrak{h}\oplus\oplus_{\alpha\in\Delta\cap{A}^{\perp}}\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}.

We claim that

\left[\mathfrak{g}^{0},x\right]=\oplus_{i=1}^{k}{\mathbb{C}}h_{\beta_{i}}\oplus\oplus_{i=1}^{k}\mathfrak{g}_{\beta_{i}},

hence
\operatorname{dim}\left[\mathfrak{g}^{0},x\right]=2k
. Indeed, if
\left(\alpha,\beta_{i}\right)=0,\alpha\not=\pm\beta_{i}
then
\alpha\pm\beta_{i}\notin\Delta
. Therefore

\left[x,\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}\right]=0
for any
\alpha\in\Delta\cap{A}^{\perp},\alpha\not=-\beta_{i}
. Furthermore,
\left[x,\mathfrak{g}_{-\beta_{i}}\right]={\mathbb{C}}h_{\beta_{i}}
and
\left[x,\mathfrak{h}\right]=\oplus_{i=1}^{k}\mathfrak{g}_{\beta_{i}}
.
Thus, we obtain

(5.2)

\operatorname{dim}\left[\mathfrak{g},x\right]=|\Delta_{1}\backslash{A}^{\perp}|+2k.

Now the statement will follow from the lemma.

Lemma 5.12
.

\operatorname{sdim}\left[\mathfrak{g},x\right]=0
.

Proof.

Define an odd skew-symmetric form
\omega
on
\mathfrak{g}
by

\omega\left(y,z\right):=\left(x,\left[y,z\right]\right).

Obviously the kernel of
\omega
coincides with the centralizer
\mathfrak{g}^{x}
. Thus,
\omega
induces a
non-degenerate odd skew-symmetric form on the quotient

\operatorname{sdim}\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{g}^{x}=0
.
But

\left[\mathfrak{g},x\right]\cong\Pi\left(\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{g}^{x}\right)
, which implies the lemma.∎

Now Lemma
5.12
implies
\operatorname{dim}\left[\mathfrak{g}_{0},x\right]=\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{dim}\left[\mathfrak{g},x\right]
. Since
\operatorname{dim}G_{0}x=\operatorname{dim}\left[\mathfrak{g}_{0},x\right]
,
Theorem
5.11
follows from (
5.2
).
∎

6.
Central characters and atypicality for classical
\mathfrak{g}\neq\mathfrak{p}(n)

Throughout this section,
\mathfrak{g}
denotes a basic classical Lie superalgebra or
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{q}(n)
. We define the notion of atypicality for a central character, and see how it is affected by the
DS
functor. Furthermore, we describe the associated variety of an irreducible module in terms of its degree of atypicality.

6.1.
The Weyl group and Weyl vector

Let us fix a Borel subalgebra
{\mathfrak{b}}\subset\mathfrak{g}
by choosing a decomposition

\Delta=\Delta^{+}\cup\Delta^{-}
. Note that in general this choice is not unique but our consideration will
not depend on it. Later we will use different Borel subalgebras in some
proofs. Set

\rho=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\alpha\in\Delta_{0}^{+}}\alpha-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\alpha\in\Delta_{1}^{+}}\alpha.

Define the shifted action of
W
on
\mathfrak{h}^{*}
by

w\cdot\lambda:=w\left(\lambda+\rho\right)-\rho.

Note that for
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{q}(n)
,
\rho=0
, so there is no shift in the
W
-action.

For
\mathfrak{g}
basic classical, recall that in Section
4.2
we defined for each
\alpha\in\Delta_{1}
a coroot
\alpha^{\vee}\in\mathfrak{t}=\mathfrak{h}_{0}
. In this section, for
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{q}(n)
we will denote by
\alpha^{\vee}
a non-zero element of
[(\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha})_{1},(\mathfrak{g}_{-\alpha})_{1}]
. Notice that in the basic classical cases we have
w{\alpha}^{\vee}=({w\alpha})^{\vee}
for every
w\in W
(under the non-shifted action); for
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{q}(n)
we impose this condition on the set of
\alpha^{\vee}
. We say that an iso-set
A\subset\Delta_{1}
is orthogonal to

\mu
if
\mu(\alpha^{\vee})=0
for each
\alpha\in A
, and we write
A\subset\mu^{\perp}
and
\mu\in A^{\perp}
. Note that this is compatible with our definition of
A^{\perp}
in (
4.2
).

6.2.
Central characters

Recall that
Z(\mathfrak{g})
denotes the center of the universal enveloping algebra
\mathcal{U}\left(\mathfrak{g}\right)
. For
\lambda\in\mathfrak{t}^{*}
and chosen, fixed Borel subalgebra
\mathfrak{b}
, we denote by
M(\lambda)
the Verma module of highest
\lambda
and by
L(\lambda)
the
unique irreducible quotient of
M(\lambda)
.
We say that
\lambda\in\mathfrak{t}^{*}
is
dominant

if
L(\lambda)
is finite-dimensional.
One can see that any
z\in Z(\mathfrak{g})
acts as a scalar
\chi_{\lambda}\left(z\right)
on
M(\lambda)
and
L(\lambda)
. Therefore
\lambda\in\mathfrak{t}^{*}

defines a central character
\chi_{\lambda}:Z(\mathfrak{g})\to{\mathbb{C}}
. We emphasize that
\chi_{\lambda}
depends also on the choice of Borel subalgebra.
For a central character
\chi
, let

\mathfrak{t}^{*}_{\chi}=\left\{\mu\in\mathfrak{t}^{*}\mid\chi_{\mu}=\chi\right\}.

For every
\lambda\in\mathfrak{t}^{*}
set

\mathcal{S}_{\lambda}:=\left\{{A}\in\mathcal{S}\mid{A}\subset\left(\lambda+\rho\right)^{\perp}\right\}.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
\chi=\chi_{\lambda}
,
{A}\in\mathcal{S}_{\lambda}
be maximal. Then

\mathfrak{t}^{*}_{\chi}=\bigcup_{w\in W}w\cdot(\lambda+\operatorname{span}A).

Proof.

This easily follows from the description of
Z(\mathfrak{g})
formulated in

[
K2
]
and proven in
[
G1
,
Ser1
]
.∎

We call
\lambda

regular
if there is a unique maximal
A\in\mathcal{S}_{\lambda}
. For every
\chi
there exists a regular
\lambda\in\mathfrak{t}_{\chi}^{*}
.

6.3.
Degree of atypicality

We define the degree of atypicality following
[
KW
]
. For a central character
\chi
set

\mathcal{S}_{\chi}=\bigcup_{\lambda\in\mathfrak{t}_{\chi}^{*}}\mathcal{S}_{\lambda}.

Lemma 6.2
.

There exists a number
k
such that
\mathcal{S}_{\chi}=\bigcup_{i\leq k}\mathcal{S}_{i}
.

Proof.

It follows easily from Lemma
6.1
that
\mathcal{S}_{\chi}
is
W
-invariant.
Furthermore, if
{A}\in\mathcal{S}_{\chi}
and
{A}^{\prime}
is obtained from
{A}
by multiplication of some
roots in
{A}
by
-1
, then
{A}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}_{\chi}
. Hence the statement follows from Lemma
5.10

(1) and (2), which also holds for
\mathfrak{q}(n)
.∎

The number
k
is called the
degree of atypicality
of

\chi
. It is clear
that the degree of atypicality of
\chi
is not bigger than the defect of
\mathfrak{g}
. The degree of atypicality of a weight
\lambda
is by definition the degree of atypicality
of
\chi_{\lambda}
. If
k=0
, then
\chi
is called
typical
. We say a module is typical if it lies in
\bigoplus\limits_{\chi\text{ typical}}\operatorname{mod}_{\chi}(\mathfrak{g})
.

Let
X_{k}=\Phi\left(\mathcal{S}_{k}\right)
,
\overline{X}_{k}
denote the closure of
X_{k}
. Lemma
5.10

\left(3\right)
implies
that

\overline{X}_{k}=\bigcup_{i=0}^{k}X_{i}.

Theorem 6.3
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a basic classical Lie superalgebra or
\mathfrak{q}(n)
, and let
M
be a

\mathfrak{g}
-module which admits central character
\chi
, with degree of atypicality of

equal to

. Then we have
X_{M}\subset\overline{X}_{k}
.

The proof of Theorem
6.3
will be given in Section

.

Theorem 6.4
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a basic classical Lie superalgebra.
For any integral dominant
\lambda\in\mathfrak{t}^{*}
with degree of atypicality
k
,
X_{L(\lambda)}=\overline{X}_{k}
.

This theorem is proven in
[
S2
]
for the Lie superalgebras
\mathfrak{osp}(m|2n)
and
\mathfrak{gl}(m|n)
.
For exceptional Lie superalgebras it is a consequence of results in
[

]
.

Remark 6.5
.

Theorem

is easy for typical

since in this case

is projective.

Remark 6.6
.

Theorem

fails for

-module

. However

has atypicality 1.

6.4.
Proof of Theorem
6.3

We assume that
\mathfrak{g}
is as in Theorem
6.3
. Recall that up to conjugacy, we may present
x\in X_{k}
as
x=x_{1}+\dots+x_{k}
where
x_{i}
is a non-zero element of
(\mathfrak{g}_{\beta_{i}})_{1}
for an odd root
\beta_{i}
. Here

will be an iso-set. We begin with a lemma.

Lemma 6.7
.

For suitable choices of Borel subalgebras
\mathfrak{b}\subseteq\mathfrak{g}
and
\mathfrak{b}_{x}\subseteq\mathfrak{g}_{x}
, for each
\lambda^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{t}_{x}^{*}
there exists
\lambda\in\mathfrak{t}^{*}
such that

•

\lambda|_{\mathfrak{t}_{x}}=\lambda^{\prime}
;

•

\operatorname{atyp}\lambda=\operatorname{atyp}\lambda^{\prime}+k
;

•

[DS_{x}(L_{\mathfrak{g}}(\lambda)):L_{\mathfrak{g}_{x}}(\lambda^{\prime})]=1
.

In particular,
\eta^{*}(\chi_{\lambda^{\prime}})=\chi_{\lambda}
and thus
\operatorname{atyp}\chi_{\lambda^{\prime}}=\operatorname{atyp}\eta^{*}(\chi_{\lambda^{\prime}})-k
.

Proof.

We can always choose a Borel subalgebra
\mathfrak{b}\subset\mathfrak{g}

such that
\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{k}
are simple roots. Note that in this case
(\rho,\beta_{i})=0
for all
i=1,\dots,k
. Further recall from Proposition
4.5
that we may realize
\mathfrak{g}_{x}
in
\mathfrak{g}
such that
\mathfrak{h}_{x}
will be a subalgebra of
\mathfrak{h}
, and
\mathfrak{t}_{x}:=(\mathfrak{h}_{x})_{0}
will lie in
\ker\beta_{1}\cap\cdots\cap\ker\beta_{k}
. Moreover,
\mathfrak{g}_{x}
will admit a natural Borel subalgebra
\mathfrak{b}_{x}\subseteq\mathfrak{b}
containing
\mathfrak{h}_{x}
.

Let
\lambda\in\mathfrak{t}^{*}
be a weight such that
\lambda|_{\mathfrak{t}_{x}}=\lambda^{\prime}
and
\lambda(\beta_{i}^{\vee})=0
for all
i
. For
\mathfrak{q}(n)
we strengthen our assumption on
\lambda

(\lambda,\epsilon_{i_{1}})=(\lambda,\epsilon_{i_{2}})=0
, where
\beta_{i}=\epsilon_{i_{1}}-\epsilon_{i_{2}}
, for all

.

Now to prove our statement with this choice of

, we observe that a nonzero highest weight vector
v_{\lambda}\in L_{\mathfrak{g}}(\lambda)_{\lambda}
satisfies
xv_{\lambda}=0
and
v_{\lambda}\notin xL_{\mathfrak{g}}(\lambda)
. The former statement is obvious because it is a highest weight vector. For the latter statement, we show that

for all

, which clearly is sufficient. In the basic classical case this follows from the fact that

and
\beta_{i}
is simple. In the
\mathfrak{q}(n)
case, the statement follows from the representation theory of the
\mathfrak{q}(2)
-subalgebra associated to each simple root
\beta_{i}
, again using that the
\beta_{i}
are simple.

Now to finish the proof of Lemma

\eta^{*}(\chi_{\lambda^{\prime}})=\chi_{\lambda}
by Proposition

, and that
\operatorname{atyp}\lambda^{\prime}=\operatorname{atyp}\lambda-k
.
∎

Lemma

implies Theorem

and the following.

Theorem 6.8

Let

be a
\mathfrak{g}
-module that admits a central character with degree of

, and

Recall, from the notation of Section

6.5.
The preimage of
\eta^{*}

Now we will compute the preimage

Define the following subgroup of the Weyl group

It is clear that

.
Obviously,

.

Lemma 6.10
.

Fix

. Let

be a central character of atypicality degree

where

is some

|(\eta^{*})^{-1}(\chi)|=\frac{|W_{A}\cdot p_{A}(\lambda)|}{|W_{\mathfrak{g}_{x}}\cdot p_{A}(\lambda)|}.

Proof.

Lemma
6.1
implies the following equality

(\mathfrak{h}^{*}_{\chi})_{reg}\cap A^{\perp}=W_{A}\cdot\lambda+\operatorname{span}A.

The condition that
\eta^{*}(\chi_{p_{A}(\lambda)})=\eta^{*}(\chi_{p_{A}(\nu)})
for
\lambda,\nu\in(\mathfrak{h}^{*}_{\chi})_{reg}
is equivalent to

p_{A}(\nu)\in W_{\mathfrak{g}_{x}}\cdot p_{A}(\lambda)
. Hence the statement.
∎

Lemma 6.11
.

Consider the action homomorphism
a:W_{A}\to\operatorname{Aut}(\mathfrak{t}_{x})
. Let
k=\operatorname{rk}x
. If
\sigma_{x}=\text{id}
(i.e.,

, or

. If

(i.e.,

or

where by abuse of notation we also write
\sigma_{x}
for the involution induced on
\mathfrak{t}
by
\sigma_{x}
.

Proof.

If
\mathfrak{osp}(2m+1|2n)
or

\mathfrak{g}_{x}=\mathfrak{osp}(2m+1-2k|2n-2k)
or

For

and

For

If

∎

Theorem 6.12
.

If
\mathfrak{g}\not=\mathfrak{osp}\left(2l|2n\right)
,

or

, then

is injective.
If
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{osp}\left(2l|2n\right),

, or

, then a preimage of
\eta^{*}

has at most two elements.

Proof.

In the case when
\operatorname{rk}(x)=|A|=k
equals the atypicality degree of
\chi
, we have
(\eta^{*})^{-1}(\chi)

has at most two elements by Lemma

and Lemma

.
If

is less than the atypicality degree of

, then consider the embedding

with

equal to the degree of atypicality. Let

with

7.
Superdimensions and supercharacters for basic classical Lie superalgebras

In this section,
\mathfrak{g}
denotes a basic classical Lie superalgebra. We explore connections between the superdimension and supercharacter of a
\mathfrak{g}
-module
M
and of the corresponding associated variety
X_{M}
.

7.1.
Superdimensions

Recall that
\operatorname{sdim}M:=\operatorname{dim}M_{0}-\operatorname{dim}M_{1}
, and that by Lemma
2.5
,
\operatorname{sdim}M=

\operatorname{sdim}M_{x}
. So we have the following.

Lemma 7.1
.

If
X_{M}\not=X
, then
\operatorname{sdim}M=0
. In particular, if a
finite-dimensional module
M
admits a central character whose degree of
atypicality is less than the defect of
\mathfrak{g}
, then
\operatorname{sdim}M=0
.

Remark 7.2
.

In fact, Serganova proved a stronger statement, namely the Kac–Wakimoto conjecture: a simple finite-dimensional module over a basic classical Lie superalgebra has nonzero superdimension if and only if it has maximal degree of atypicality (i.e., equal to the defect of
\mathfrak{g}
). For a proof, see
[
S2
]
. A version of the Kac–Wakimoto conjecture for periplectic Lie superalgebras
\mathfrak{p}(n)
was proven in
[
ES3
]
. Meanwhile, for
\mathfrak{q}(n)
it is known that
\operatorname{sdim}L=0
for all nontrivial finite-dimensional simple modules
L
, see
[
Che
]
.

7.2.
Supercharacters

For a finite-dimensional
\mathfrak{g}
-module
M
with weight decomposition
M=\oplus_{\mu\in\mathfrak{h}^{*}}M^{\mu}
, the supercharacter of
M
is defined to be

(7.1)

\operatorname{sch}M=\sum_{\mu\in\mathfrak{h}^{*}}\left(\operatorname{sdim}\ M^{\mu}\right)e^{\mu}.

The supercharacter
\operatorname{sch}M
is a
W
-invariant analytic function on
\mathfrak{h}
, so we will also write it as

\operatorname{sch}_{M}\left(h\right)
,
for
h\in\mathfrak{h}
. Then
\operatorname{sch}_{M}\left(h\right)=\operatorname{str}(e^{h})
, and the Taylor
series for
\operatorname{sch}_{M}
at
h=0
is

\operatorname{sch}_{M}\left(h\right)=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}p_{i}\left(h\right),

where
p_{i}\left(h\right)
is a homogeneous polynomial of degree
i
on
\mathfrak{h}
. The
order
of
\operatorname{sch}_{M}

at zero is by definition the minimal
i
such that
p_{i}\not\equiv 0
.

Theorem 7.3
.

Assume that all odd roots of
\mathfrak{g}
are isotropic. Let
M
be a
finite-dimensional
\mathfrak{g}
-module,
s
be the
codimension of
X_{M}
in
X
. The order of
\operatorname{sch}_{M}
at zero is not less than
s
.
Moreover, the polynomial
p_{s}\left(h\right)
in Taylor series for
\operatorname{sch}_{M}
is determined
uniquely up to proportionality.

Proof.

The proof is based on the following lemma, the proof of this
lemma is similar to the proof of Lemma
2.5
(6). We leave it to the reader.

Lemma 7.4
.

Let
x\in X
,
h\in\mathfrak{h}_{0}
and
\left[h,x\right]=0
. Then
\operatorname{Ker}x
and
xM
are

h
-invariant and str
{}_{M}e^{h}=
str
{}_{M_{x}}e^{h}
.

Now we proceed to the proof of the Theorem
7.3
. If
X_{M}
contains an irreducible component of
X
, the statement is trivial since
s=0
. Otherwise
there exists
k
smaller than the defect of
\mathfrak{g}
such that

X_{M}\subset\cup_{{A}\in\mathcal{S}\text{, }|{A}|\leq k}\Phi\left({A}\right).

Let
{A}=\left\{\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{k+1}\right\}\in\mathcal{S}
,
x=x_{\beta_{1}}+\dots+x_{\beta_{k+1}}
for some nonzero
x_{\beta_{i}}\in\mathfrak{g}_{\beta_{i}}
. Then
M_{x}=\left\{0\right\}
.
If
h\in\mathfrak{h}
satisfies
\beta_{1}\left(h\right)=\dots=\beta_{k+1}\left(h\right)=0
, then
\left[h,x\right]=0
. Hence by Lemma
7.4

str
{}_{M}e^{h}=
str
{}_{M_{x}}e^{h}=0
. This we have proved the following property

(7.2)

\operatorname{sch}_{M}\left(\mathfrak{h}_{{A}}^{\perp}\right)=0\text{ for all }{A}\in\mathcal{S},\ |{A}|=k+1.

Let
p_{i}
be the
first nonzero polynomial in the Taylor series for
\operatorname{sch}_{M}
at zero. Then
p_{i}
also
satisfies (
7.2
). Let
{\text{B}}=\left\{\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{k}\right\}\in\mathcal{S}
and
\bar{p}_{i}
be
the restriction of
p_{i}
to
\mathfrak{h}_{{\text{B}}}^{\perp}
. If
\bar{p}_{i}\not=0
, then degree of
\bar{p}_{i}
is
i
.
Since
p_{i}\left(\mathfrak{h}_{{\text{B}}\cup\alpha}^{\perp}\right)=0
for any
\alpha\not=\pm\alpha_{i}
,
\alpha\in{\text{B}}^{\perp}
, then
\alpha
divides
\bar{p}_{i}
.
That gives the estimate on
i
. Indeed,
i
is not less than the number of all
possible
\alpha
, i.e.,
\frac{|\Delta_{1}\cap{\text{B}}^{\perp}|}{2}-|{\text{B}}|
. By Corollary
5.15

the latter number is the codimension
s
of
X_{M}
in
X
. Hence
i\geq s
.

To prove the second assertion we need to show
that if two homogeneous
W
-invariant polynomials
p
and
q
of degree
s
satisfy
(
7.2
), then
p=cq
for some
c\in{\mathbb{C}}
. After restriction to
\mathfrak{h}_{{\text{B}}}^{\perp}

\bar{p}=a\Pi_{\alpha\in\left(\Delta_{1}^{+}\cap{\text{B}}^{\perp}\right)\backslash\pm{\text{B}}}\alpha\text{, }\bar{q}=b\Pi_{\alpha\in\left(\Delta_{1}^{+}\cap{\text{B}}^{\perp}\right)\backslash\pm{\text{B}}}\alpha

for some constants
a
and
b
. Therefore there exists
f=p-cq
such that

f\left(\mathfrak{h}_{{\text{B}}}^{\perp}\right)=0
. Thus,
f
satisfies (
7.2
) for
k
instead of
k+1
. Then
\operatorname{deg}f>s
, which
implies
f=0
.∎

8.
Reduced Grothendieck rings and
ds_{x}

We retain the notation of Section
2
.
In this section we discuss the homomorphism
ds_{x}:{K}_{-}(\mathfrak{g})\rightarrow{K}_{-}(\mathfrak{g}_{x})
, for classical Lie superalgebras
\mathfrak{g}
,
where
{K}_{-}(\mathfrak{g})
and
{K}_{-}(\mathfrak{g}_{x})
stand for the reduced Grothendieck rings of
\mathcal{F}(\mathfrak{g})
and
\mathcal{F}(\mathfrak{g}_{x})
, respectively. The study of
ds_{x}
was initiated by Hoyt and Reif in
[
HR
]
for the basic classical Lie superalgebras. We consider

\mathfrak{g}_{x}
as a subalgebra of
\mathfrak{g}
using the
splitting
\mathfrak{g}^{x}=\mathfrak{g}_{x}\ltimes[x,\mathfrak{g}]
as in Propositions
4.5
and
4.6
.

8.1.
Subcategories of
\mathcal{F}(\mathfrak{g})
and the
DS
functor

Before discussing
ds_{x}
, we describe certain subcategories of
\mathcal{F}(\mathfrak{g})
and their relation to the
DS
functor. Let
\mathfrak{g}
be one of basic classical superalgebras and
\Lambda_{\mathcal{F}(\mathfrak{g})}
denote the abelian subgroup of
\mathfrak{t}^{*}
consisting of weights of
M\in{\mathcal{F}(\mathfrak{g})}
. For any subset
\Lambda\subset\Lambda_{\mathcal{F}(\mathfrak{g})}
we
denote by
\mathcal{F}^{\Lambda}(\mathfrak{g})
the full subcategory of
\mathcal{F}(\mathfrak{g})
consisting of modules with weights in
\Lambda
.

Let
G
be an algebraic supergroup with Lie superalgebra
\mathfrak{g}
. Then
G
is determined by the lattice
\Lambda_{G}\subset\Lambda_{\mathcal{F}(\mathfrak{g})}
and
the category
\mathcal{F}(G)
of finite-dimensional representations of
G
is equivalent to
\mathcal{F}^{\Lambda_{G}}(\mathfrak{g})
.

If
x\in\mathfrak{g}_{1}
is a self-commuting element and
G^{x}
is the centralizer of
x
in
G
then the Lie algebra of
G^{x}
is the kernel of
ad_{x}
.
We denote by
G_{x}
the quotient of
G^{x}
such that
\operatorname{Lie}G_{x}=\mathfrak{g}_{x}
.
It is clear that
DS_{x}
induces the functor
\mathcal{F}(G)\to\mathcal{F}(G_{x})
.

We denote by
G
the particular supergroup for every basic classical superalgebra:

(1)

If
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{gl}(m|n)
we set
G:=GL(m|n)
,
\Lambda_{G}:=\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbb{Z}\varepsilon_{i}+\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbb{Z}\delta_{j}
.

(2)

If
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{sl}(m|n)
we set
G:=SL(m|n)
,
\Lambda_{G}:=\Lambda_{GL(m|n)}\cap\mathfrak{h}^{*}
.

(3)

If
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{osp}(2m|2n)
or
\mathfrak{osp}(2m+1|2n)
we set
G:=SOSP(2m|2n)
(respectively,
SOSP(2m+1|2n)
),

\Lambda_{G}:=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbb{Z}\varepsilon_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{j}\mathbb{Z}\delta_{j}
.

(4)

If
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{p}(n)
we set
G:=P(n)
,
\Lambda_{G}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{Z}\varepsilon_{i}
.

(5)

If
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{q}(n)
we set
G:=Q(n)
,
\Lambda_{G}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{Z}\varepsilon_{i}
.

(6)

If
\mathfrak{g}
is an exceptional Lie algebra of type
G(3)
and
F(4)
then
G
is the adjoint group with
\Lambda_{G}
being the root lattice. One has

\Lambda_{\mathcal{F}(\mathfrak{g})}=\Lambda_{G}
for
G(3)
and
\Lambda_{\mathcal{F}(\mathfrak{g})}/\Lambda_{G}=\mathbb{Z}_{2}
for
F(4)
, see
[
M
]
.

(7)

For
\mathfrak{g}=D(2,1,a)
we consider the algebraic group
G
with
\Lambda_{G}:=\Lambda_{\mathcal{F}(\mathfrak{g})}
.

Next we set
\Lambda_{\mathfrak{g}}:=\Lambda_{G}
in all cases when
\mathfrak{g}=[\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}]
. In the remaining cases we set

\begin{array}[]{l}\Lambda_{\mathfrak{gl}(m|n)}:=\Lambda_{GL(m|n)}+\mathbb{C}\text{str}\ \ \text{ where }\text{str}:=\varepsilon_{1}+\dots+\varepsilon_{m}+\delta_{1}+\dots+\delta_{n},\\
\Lambda_{\mathfrak{p}(m)}:=\Lambda_{P(m)}+\mathbb{C}\text{str},\ \ \text{ where }\text{str}:=\varepsilon_{1}+\dots+\varepsilon_{m},\\
\Lambda_{\mathfrak{q}(m)}:=\Lambda_{Q(m)}+\mathbb{Z}\frac{\text{str}}{2},\ \ \text{ where }\text{str}:=\varepsilon_{1}+\dots+\varepsilon_{m}.\end{array}

8.1.1.

Consider the case when
\mathfrak{g}
is non-exceptional. Take
x\not=0
as in Prop. 4.5.
Let
\Lambda^{\prime}_{\mathfrak{g}}=\Lambda_{\mathcal{F}(\mathfrak{g})}\setminus\Lambda_{\mathfrak{g}}
. Then we have a decomposition

\mathcal{F}(\mathfrak{g})=\mathcal{F}^{\Lambda_{g}}(\mathfrak{g})\oplus\mathcal{F}^{\Lambda^{\prime}_{\mathfrak{g}}}(\mathfrak{g}).

Every module
M\in\mathcal{F}^{\Lambda^{\prime}_{\mathfrak{g}}}(\mathfrak{g})
is projective and hence
DS_{x}(M)=0
. Furthermore,
DS_{x}
induces the functor

\mathcal{F}^{\Lambda_{G}}(\mathfrak{g})\to\mathcal{F}^{\Lambda_{G_{x}}}(\mathfrak{g}_{x})
.

In the cases when
[\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}]\neq\mathfrak{g}
we can be more precise. Namely, when
\mathfrak{g}_{x}\neq 0
,
DS_{x}
restricts to the functor:

\mathcal{F}^{\Lambda_{G}+c\text{str}}(\mathfrak{g})\to\mathcal{F}^{\Lambda_{G_{x}}+c\text{str}}(\mathfrak{g}_{x}),

where
c\in\mathbb{C}
for
\mathfrak{gl}(m|n)
and
\mathfrak{p}(n)
,
c=0,\frac{1}{2}
for
\mathfrak{q}(m)
.
To see this for
\mathfrak{gl}(m|n)
and
\mathfrak{p}(n)
we just note that every
M\in\mathcal{F}^{\Lambda_{G}+c\text{str}}
can be obtained from
M_{0}\in\mathcal{F}(G)
by tensoring with
one dimensional character in
\chi_{c}\in(\mathfrak{g}/[\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}])^{*}
and therefore
it is suffices to compute
DS_{x}
in the case
c=0
and then use

DS_{x}(M\otimes\chi_{c})=DS_{x}(M)\otimes\chi_{c}
.
The case of
\mathfrak{q}(n)
is straightforward.

8.1.2.
Exceptional algebras

Take
x\not=0
. All such
x
are conjugate by the adjoint action of
G_{0}
.

For
D(2,1,a)
,
G_{x}=\mathbb{C}^{*}
. Therefore
DS_{x}:\mathcal{F}(D(2,1,a)\to\mathcal{F}(\mathbb{C}^{*})
.
By [Germoni] for

\mathfrak{g}=D(2,1,a)
with

a\not\in\mathbb{Q}
all atypical modules
in
\mathcal{F}(\mathfrak{g})
have zero central character. Using the filtration of projective modules obtained by Germoni, one can show (see
[
G4
]
) that
DS_{x}(L)

is a trivial
\mathbb{C}^{*}
-module
for any simple atypical
\mathfrak{g}
-module
L
and therefore for any
\mathfrak{g}
-module. In other words the image of
DS_{x}
lies in the category of
vector superspaces equipped with the trivial action of
\mathbb{C}^{*}
.

Combining the description of dominant weights (see
[
M
]
)
and (
4.3
) we obtain the following results for
G(3)
(with
\mathfrak{g}_{x}=\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
) and
F(4)
(with
\mathfrak{g}_{x}=\mathfrak{sl}_{3}
) :

DS_{x}(\mathcal{F}(G(3)))\subset\mathcal{F}(PSL(2))\ \ \ \ DS_{x}(\mathcal{F}(F(4)))\subset\mathcal{F}(PSL(3))

where
\mathcal{F}(G)
denotes the category of finite-dimensional representations of the algebraic group
G
. In fact, from
[
G3
]
and
[
M
]
we obtain that

DS_{x}(\mathcal{F}(F(4)))\subset\mathcal{F}(G^{\prime\prime}_{x})

where
G^{\prime\prime}_{x}
is a non-connected algebraic group fitting into a non-split exact sequence

(8.1)

1\to PSL(3)\to G^{\prime\prime}_{x}\to\mathbb{Z}_{2}\to 1,

compare to Remark
3.4
.

8.2.
Properties of
ds_{x}

We now begin the discussion of
ds_{x}
with general remarks which are valid for classical Lie superalgebras
\mathfrak{g}
satisfying
\mathfrak{h}=\mathfrak{t}
, i.e., classical
\mathfrak{g}\neq\mathfrak{q}(n)
. (The case of general
\mathfrak{g}
is considered in
[
GSS
]
). As already noted, by Section
4.5
, we have splittings

\mathfrak{g}^{x}=\mathfrak{g}_{x}\ltimes[x,\mathfrak{g}]
. Further we have
\mathfrak{t}=\mathfrak{t}_{x}\oplus\mathfrak{t}^{\prime}
, where
\mathfrak{t}_{x}\subseteq\mathfrak{g}_{x}
is a Cartan subalgebra.

In these cases the ring
{K}_{-}(\mathfrak{g})
is spanned by the images of the simple finite-dimensional modules. Since these modules are highest weight modules, the map

[N]\mapsto[\operatorname{Res}^{\mathfrak{g}}_{\mathfrak{h}}N]
gives an embedding of

{K}_{-}(\mathfrak{g})\to{K}_{-}(\mathfrak{h})
and we identify
{K}_{-}(\mathfrak{g})
with this ring.
The image is called the

ring of supercharacters
, since
[\operatorname{Res}^{\mathfrak{g}}_{\mathfrak{h}}N]=\operatorname{sch}N
.
By Lemma
2.12

\operatorname{sdim}DS_{x}(N)_{\mu^{\prime}}=\sum_{\mu\in\mathfrak{t}^{*}:\mu|_{\mathfrak{t}_{x}}=\mu^{\prime}}\operatorname{sdim}N_{\mu}.

for each
N\in\mathcal{F}(\mathfrak{g})
and
\mu^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{t}_{x}^{*}
.
Thus
ds_{x}
written for the supercharacter rings takes the form

(8.2)

ds_{x}\bigl(\sum_{\nu\in\mathfrak{t}^{*}}m_{\nu}e^{\nu}\bigr)=\sum_{\nu\in\mathfrak{t}^{*}}m_{\nu}e^{\nu|_{\mathfrak{t}_{x}}}.

coincides with the restriction of the map
f\mapsto f|_{\mathfrak{t}_{x}}
.
Using the representation theory of
\mathfrak{sl}(1|1)
it is easy to see that
if
x
can be embedded into an
\mathfrak{sl}(1|1)
-triple
x,y,\alpha^{\vee}

with
\alpha^{\vee}\in\mathfrak{t}
, then for

(8.3)

ds_{x}\bigl(\sum_{\nu\in\mathfrak{t}^{*}}m_{\nu}e^{\nu}\bigr)=\sum_{\nu\in\mathfrak{t}^{*}:\nu(\alpha^{\vee})=0}m_{\nu}e^{\nu|_{\mathfrak{t}_{x}}}.

These formulas appeared in
[
HR
]
;
for
\mathfrak{q}(n)
a similar formula is given in
[
GSS
]
.

8.2.1.

Now consider the case with
\mathfrak{g}
from the list (
1.2
).
By Lemma
2.12
one has
ds_{x}=ds_{y}
if
\mathfrak{g}_{x}=\mathfrak{g}_{y}
as embedded subalgebras.
For
x
of rank
r

we denote the map
ds_{x}:{K}_{-}(\mathfrak{g})\to{K}_{-}(\mathfrak{g}_{x})
by
ds^{r}
.
By Lemma
2.13
one has

ds^{i}=ds^{1}\circ ds^{1}\circ\ldots\circ ds^{1}

if
DS^{1}(DS^{1}\ldots(\mathfrak{g}))=DS^{i}(\mathfrak{g})
.
By Lemma
2.14
we have

\ker ds_{x}=\ker ds_{y}
if
x,y
are conjugated by an inner automorphism.
Note that
\operatorname{rk}x=\operatorname{rk}y
implies the existence of
x^{\prime}\in X

such that
\mathfrak{g}_{x^{\prime}}=\mathfrak{g}_{y}
and
x,x^{\prime}
are conjugated by an inner automorphism.
Hence the ideal
\ker ds^{r}\subset{K}_{-}(\mathfrak{g})
does not depend on the choice of
x
of rank
r
.

8.3.
The ring
{K}_{-}(\mathfrak{g})

For
\mathfrak{g}\not=\mathfrak{p}(n),\mathfrak{q}(n)
,
Sergeev and Veselov
interpreted the supercharacter ring as a ring of functions admitting certain supersymmetry conditions, see
[
SerV
]
.
For example, the supercharacter ring for the category
\mathcal{F}^{\mathbb{Z}}(\mathfrak{gl}(m|n))
can be realized as

\mathcal{J}\cong\left\{f\in\mathbb{Z}\left[x_{1}^{\pm 1},\ldots,x_{m}^{\pm 1},y_{1}^{\pm 1},\ldots,y_{n}^{\pm 1}\right]^{S_{m}\times S_{n}}\,\middle|\,f|_{x_{1}=y_{1}=t}\text{ is independent of }t\right\},

and in this case, if
\operatorname{rk}x=1
, then
ds_{x}\left(f\right)=f|_{x_{1}=y_{1}}
(see
[
HR
]
).
The supercharacter ring for
\mathfrak{p}(n)
was described in
[
IRS
]
using an inductive argument with the help of
ds^{2}
. Using a similar method Reif described the
ring
{K}_{+}(\mathfrak{q}_{n})
in
[
R
]

9.
The
DS
functor and
\mathfrak{sl}(\infty)
-modules

In this section, we discuss a connection between the
DS
functor and
\mathfrak{sl}(\infty)
-modules arising from
\mathfrak{gl}(m|n)
-representation theory, which was discovered and studied by Hoyt, Penkov and Serganova in
[
HPS
]
. We will recall some basic facts for
\mathfrak{sl}(\infty)
, and refer the reader to the book “Classical Lie algebras at infinity” by Penkov and Hoyt for an in-depth treatment of the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{sl}(\infty)
and other locally finite Lie algebras
[
PH
]
.

In the pioneering paper
[
B
]
, Brundan showed that the complexification of the Grothendieck group for the categories
\mathcal{F}(GL(m|n))
and the integral BGG category
\mathcal{O}^{\mathbb{Z}}_{m|n}
inherit a natural
\mathfrak{sl}(\infty)
-module structure from the action of translation functors
E_{i},F_{i}
. This action and general categorification methods were used by Brundan, Losev and Webster in
[
BLW
]
to develop Kazhdan-Lusztig theory for
\mathfrak{gl}(m|n)
.

Now since the
DS
functor commutes with translation functors, the induced homomorphism
ds
of reduced Grothendieck groups is, in fact, a homomorphism of
\mathfrak{sl}(\infty)
-modules
[
HPS
]
. This homomorphism
ds
was used in
[
HPS
]
to help obtain a description of the
\mathfrak{sl}(\infty)
-module structure of the reduced Grothendieck groups for both of the categories
\mathcal{F}^{\mathbb{Z}}_{m|n}
and
\mathcal{O}^{\mathbb{Z}}_{m|n}
of integral
\mathfrak{gl}(m|n)
-modules.

9.1.
The Lie algebra
\mathfrak{sl}(\infty)

The Lie algebra
\mathfrak{gl}\left(\infty\right)
can be defined by taking countable-dimensional vector spaces
\mathbf{V},\mathbf{V}_{*}
with bases
\{v_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{Z}},\{v_{j}^{*}\}_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}
, and letting
\mathfrak{gl}\left(\infty\right)=\mathbf{V}\otimes\mathbf{V}_{*}
with bracket (extended linearly) given by

[v_{i}\otimes v_{j}^{*},v_{k}\otimes v_{l}^{*}]=\langle v_{k},v_{j}^{*}\rangle v_{i}\otimes v_{l}^{*}-\langle v_{i},v_{l}^{*}\rangle v_{k}\otimes v_{j}^{*},

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle:\mathbf{V}\otimes\mathbf{V}_{*}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
is the nondegenerate pairing defined by

\langle v_{i},v_{j}^{*}\rangle=\delta_{ij}
.

We can identify
\mathfrak{gl}(\infty)
with the
space of infinite matrices
\left(a_{ij}\right)_{i,j\in\mathbb{Z}}

which have only finitely many nonzero entries, using the correspondence
v_{i}\otimes v_{j}^{*}\mapsto E_{ij}
, where
E_{ij}
is the matrix with
1
in the
i,j
-position and zeros elsewhere.
Under this identification,
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
is the trace map on
\mathfrak{gl}(\infty)
, and the kernel of
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
is the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{sl}\left(\infty\right)
.
The center of
\mathfrak{gl}(\infty)
is trivial, and the following exact sequence does not split:

0\to\mathfrak{sl}(\infty)\to\mathfrak{gl}(\infty)\to\mathbb{C}\to 0.

The Lie algebra

\mathfrak{sl}\left(\infty\right)
is generated by the elements
e_{i}:=E_{i,i+1}
,
f_{i}:=E_{i+1,i}
for
i\in\mathbb{Z}
.

We can realize
\mathfrak{sl}(\infty)
as a direct limit of finite-dimensional Lie algebras
\underrightarrow{\lim}\ \mathfrak{sl}\left(n\right)
, that is,

\mathfrak{sl}(\infty)
is isomorphic to a union
\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 2}}\mathfrak{sl}(n)
, of nested Lie algebras

\mathfrak{sl}(2)\subset\mathfrak{sl}(3)\subset\cdots\subset\mathfrak{sl}(n)\subset\mathfrak{sl}(n+1)\subset\cdots.

The Lie algebra obtained from this union is independent, up to isomorphism, of the choice of the inclusions
\mathfrak{sl}(n)\hookrightarrow\mathfrak{sl}(n+1)
.

9.2.
Modules over
\mathfrak{sl}(\infty)

The modules
\mathbf{V},\mathbf{V}_{*}
are the defining representations of
\mathfrak{sl}(\infty)
, and for
p,q>0
, the tensor modules
\mathbf{V}^{\otimes p}\otimes\mathbf{V}_{*}^{\otimes q}
,
p,q\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
are not semisimple.
Schur-Weyl duality for
\mathfrak{sl}(\infty)
implies that the module
\mathbf{V}^{\otimes p}\otimes\mathbf{V}_{*}^{\otimes q}
decomposes as

\mathbf{V}^{\otimes p}\otimes\mathbf{V}_{*}^{\otimes q}=\bigoplus_{|\boldsymbol{\lambda}|=p,|{\boldsymbol{\mu}}|=q}(\mathbb{S}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(\mathbf{V})\otimes\mathbb{S}_{\boldsymbol{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}}(\mathbf{V}_{*}))\otimes(Y_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\otimes Y_{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}),

where
Y_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
and
Y_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}
are irreducible
S_{p}
- and
S_{q}
-modules,
\mathbb{S}_{\boldsymbol{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}}
denotes the Schur functor corresponding to the Young diagram
\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
, and
|\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}|
is the size of
\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
.

The
\mathfrak{sl}(\infty)
-modules
\mathbb{S}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(\mathbf{V})\otimes\mathbb{S}_{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}(\mathbf{V}_{*})
are indecomposable, and their socle filtration was described by Penkov and Styrkas in
[
PStyr
]
. We recall that the
socle
of a module
\mathbf{M}
, denoted
\operatorname{soc}\mathbf{M}
,
is the largest semisimple submodule of
\mathbf{M}
, and that the
socle filtration
of
\mathbf{M}
is defined inductively by
\operatorname{soc}^{0}\mathbf{M}:=\operatorname{soc}\mathbf{M}
and
\operatorname{soc}^{i}\mathbf{M}:=p_{i}^{-1}(\operatorname{soc}(\mathbf{M}/(\operatorname{soc}^{i-1}\mathbf{M})))
, where
p_{i}:\mathbf{M}\to\mathbf{M}/(\operatorname{soc}^{i-1}\mathbf{M})
is the natural projection. We denote the layers of the socle filtration by
\overline{\operatorname{soc}}^{i}\mathbf{M}:=\operatorname{soc}^{i}\mathbf{M}/\operatorname{soc}^{i-1}\mathbf{M}
.
From Thm. 2.3 of
[
PStyr
]
, we have that the layers of
\mathbb{S}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(\mathbf{V})\otimes\mathbb{S}_{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}(\mathbf{V}_{*})
are

\overline{\operatorname{soc}}^{k}(\mathbb{S}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(\mathbf{V})\otimes\mathbb{S}_{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}(\mathbf{V}_{*}))\cong\bigoplus_{\boldsymbol{\lambda^{\prime}},\boldsymbol{\mu^{\prime}},|{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}|=k}N^{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\prime},{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}N^{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{\prime},{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}\mathbf{V}^{\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{\prime},{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{\prime}}

where

N^{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\prime},{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}
are the standard Littlewood-Richardson coefficients. In particular, the indecomposable module
\mathbb{S}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(\mathbf{V})\otimes\mathbb{S}_{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}(\mathbf{V}_{*})
has a simple socle, denoted by
\mathbf{V}^{\boldsymbol{\lambda},\boldsymbol{\mu}}
.
For example, the layers of
\Lambda^{m}\mathbf{V}\otimes\Lambda^{n}\mathbf{V}_{*}
are given by
\overline{\operatorname{soc}}^{i}(\Lambda^{m}\mathbf{V}\otimes\Lambda^{n}\mathbf{V}_{*})\cong\mathbf{V}^{\left(m-i\right)^{\perp},\left(n-i\right)^{\perp}}
, where
\perp
indicates the conjugate Young diagram.

An
\mathfrak{sl}(\infty)
-module is called a
tensor module
if it is isomorphic to a submodule of a finite direct sum of modules of the form
\mathbf{V}^{\otimes p_{i}}\otimes\mathbf{V}_{*}^{\otimes q_{i}}
for
p_{i},q_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
.
The category of tensor modules
\mathbb{T}_{\mathfrak{sl}(\infty)}
is by definition the full subcategory of
\mathfrak{sl}(\infty)\textrm{-mod}
consisting of tensor modules
[
DPS
]
.
The modules
\mathbf{V}^{\otimes p}\otimes\mathbf{V}_{*}^{\otimes q}
,
p,q\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
are injective in
\mathbb{T}_{\mathfrak{sl}(\infty)}
. Moreover, every indecomposable injective object of
\mathbb{T}_{\mathfrak{sl}(\infty)}
is isomorphic to an indecomposable direct summand of
\mathbf{V}^{\otimes p}\otimes\mathbf{V}_{*}^{\otimes q}
for some
p,q\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, which means, it is isomorphic to

\mathbb{S}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(\mathbf{V})\otimes\mathbb{S}_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(\mathbf{V}_{*})
for some
\boldsymbol{\lambda},\boldsymbol{\mu}

[
DPS
]
.

We define the translation functors

Let
X_{j},Y_{j}

\mathbb{Z}_{2}

\mathfrak{gl}(m|n)
with respect to the
\mathfrak{gl}(m|n)

\operatorname{str}(XY)

For a pair of

-modules

(respectively,

.
Then, as it follows from

(respectively,

(respectively,

\mathrm{E}_{i}
and
\mathrm{F}_{i}
are adjoint functors on
\mathcal{O}_{m|n}^{\mathbb{Z}}
.

9.4.
Grothendieck groups and the
\mathfrak{sl}(\infty)
-modules
\mathbf{K}_{m|n}
,
\mathbf{J}_{m|n}

We let
\mathbf{K}_{m|n}
(respectively,
\mathbf{J}_{m|n}

\mathcal{O}_{m|n}^{\mathbb{Z}}
(respectively, of
\mathcal{F}^{\mathbb{Z}}_{m|n}

\mathbf{K}_{m|n}:={K}_{-}(\mathcal{O}_{m|n}^{\mathbb{Z}})\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{C},\hskip 28.45274pt\mathbf{J}_{m|n}:={K}_{-}(\mathcal{F}^{\mathbb{Z}}_{m|n})\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{C}.

We will denote by

the linear operators that the translation functors
\mathrm{E}_{i},\mathrm{F}_{i}
induce on
\mathbf{K}_{m|n}
and
\mathbf{J}_{m|n}

Brundan showed in
[

that if we identify

with the Chevalley generators

of
\mathfrak{sl}(\infty)

\mathbf{J}_{m|n}
and
\mathbf{K}_{m|n}

Let
\mathbf{T}_{m|n}\subset\mathbf{K}_{m|n}

[M(\lambda)]

M(\lambda)
for
\lambda\in\Phi

\mathbf{W}_{m|n}\subset\mathbf{J}_{m|n}

10.
Projectivity criteria for quasireductive Lie superalgebras

In this section, we assume that
\mathfrak{g}
is a quasireductive Lie superalgebra, that is,
\mathfrak{g}_{0}
is reductive and acts semisimply on
\mathfrak{g}_{1}
.
We discuss to what extent the associated varieties for Lie superalgebras can be used to detect projectivity in the category of finite-dimensional
\mathfrak{g}
-modules.

10.1.
Projectivity and the associated variety

Let
\mathcal{F}(\mathfrak{g})
be the category of finite-dimensional
\mathfrak{g}
-modules which are semisimple over

\mathfrak{g}_{0}
. The latter condition is automatic if
\mathfrak{g}_{0}
is semisimple. The
category
\mathcal{F}(\mathfrak{g})
has enough projective modules and injective modules.
By duality, every injective object is projective and vice
versa. Moreover, every indecomposable projective module is a direct
summand of

\operatorname{Ind}^{\mathfrak{g}}_{\mathfrak{g}_{0}}L
for some simple
\mathfrak{g}_{0}
-module
L
.
We say that a subalgebra
\mathfrak{k}\subset\mathfrak{g}
is a quasireductive subalgebra if
\mathfrak{k}_{0}
is reductive
and
\mathfrak{g}
is a semisimple
\mathfrak{k}_{0}
-module.

The following fact is useful.

Proposition 10.1
.

Let
\mathfrak{k}
be a quasireductive subalgebra of
\mathfrak{g}
.
If
P
is projective in
\mathcal{F}(\mathfrak{g})
then

\operatorname{Res}_{\mathfrak{k}}P
is projective in
\mathcal{F}(\mathfrak{k})
.

Proof.

If
P
is projective then it is a direct summand of the
induced module
\operatorname{Ind}^{\mathfrak{g}}_{\mathfrak{g}_{0}}N

for some semisimple

\mathfrak{g}_{0}
-module
N
.
Furthermore, we have an isomorphism of
\mathfrak{g}_{0}
-modules:

\operatorname{Ind}^{\mathfrak{g}}_{\mathfrak{g}_{0}}N\simeq N\otimes S^{\bullet}(\mathfrak{g}_{1})

and an isomorphism of
\mathfrak{k}_{0}
-modules

\mathfrak{g}_{1}=\mathfrak{k}_{1}\oplus(\mathfrak{g}_{1}/\mathfrak{k}_{1}).

By Frobenius reciprocity the homomorphism of
\mathfrak{k}_{0}
-modules

N\otimes S^{\bullet}(\mathfrak{g}_{1}/\mathfrak{k}_{1})\to\operatorname{Ind}^{\mathfrak{g}}_{\mathfrak{g}_{0}}N

induces an isomorphism

\operatorname{Ind}^{\mathfrak{k}}_{\mathfrak{k}_{0}}(N\otimes S^{\bullet}(\mathfrak{g}_{1}/\mathfrak{k}_{1}))\simeq\operatorname{Res}_{\mathfrak{k}}\operatorname{Ind}^{\mathfrak{g}}_{\mathfrak{g}_{0}}N.

We obtain that
\operatorname{Res}_{\mathfrak{k}}P
is a direct summand of some module induced from a
semisimple
\mathfrak{k}_{0}
-module. Therefore
P
is projective
in
\mathcal{F}(\mathfrak{k})
.
∎

We can now give another proof of Lemma
2.21
.

Theorem 10.2
.

Suppose
\mathfrak{g}
is quasireductive.
If
M\in\mathcal{F}(\mathfrak{g})
is projective, then
X_{M}=\left\{0\right\}
.

Proof.

Let
x\in X
be nonzero, and consider the subalgebra
\mathfrak{k}=k\langle x\rangle
generated by
x
. Since
\mathfrak{k}
is quasireductive,
\operatorname{Res}_{\mathfrak{k}}M
is projective over
\mathfrak{k}
, which implies that
M_{x}=0
.
∎

10.2.
Criteria for type I Lie superalgebras

In this section we prove that for certain quasireductive Lie superalgebras the converse of Theorem
10.2
holds.
We start with the following.

Lemma 10.3
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be quasireductive and
[\mathfrak{g}_{1},\mathfrak{g}_{1}]=0
. If
X_{M}=\{0\}
then
M
is projective in
\mathcal{F}(\mathfrak{g})
.

Proof.

Since
\mathcal{U}(\mathfrak{g}_{1})
is isomorphic to the exterior algebra
\Lambda(\mathfrak{g}_{1})
we have that
X_{M}=\{0\}
implies that
M
is free over
\mathcal{U}(\mathfrak{g}_{1})
, see
[
AB
]
. Then an embedding of
\mathfrak{g}_{0}
-modules
M/\mathfrak{g}_{1}M\hookrightarrow M
induces an isomorphism
\operatorname{Ind}^{\mathfrak{g}}_{\mathfrak{g}_{0}}(M/{\mathfrak{g}_{1}}M)\simeq M
. Therefore
M
is projective.
∎

Theorem 10.4
.

Assume that
\mathfrak{g}_{0}
is reductive and there exists an element
h
in the center of
\mathfrak{g}_{0}
such that
\operatorname{ad}_{h}
acts diagonally on

\mathfrak{g}_{1}
with nonzero real eigenvalues. If
X_{M}=\{0\}
then
M
is projective in
\mathcal{F}(\mathfrak{g})
.

Proof.

Write down
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{g}_{+}\oplus\mathfrak{g}_{0}\oplus\mathfrak{g}_{-}
, where

\mathfrak{g}_{+}
(respectively,
\mathfrak{g}_{-}
)
denote the span of
\operatorname{ad}_{h}
-eigenvectors with positive (respectively, negative)
eigenvalues. Since
\mathfrak{g}_{\pm}
are purely odd subalgebras, they are commutative, hence
\mathfrak{p}_{+}:=\mathfrak{g}_{0}\oplus\mathfrak{g}_{+}
and
\mathfrak{p}_{-}:=\mathfrak{g}_{0}\oplus\mathfrak{g}_{-}
are
subalgebras satisfying the condition of Lemma
10.3
. In particular, if
X_{M}=\{0\}
, then
M
is projective in
\mathcal{F}(\mathfrak{p}_{\pm})
.
For a
\mathfrak{g}_{0}
-module
L
set

K^{\pm}(L):=\operatorname{Ind}^{\mathfrak{g}}_{\mathfrak{p}_{\pm}}L
. We claim that there exists a finite filtration

0=M_{0}\subset M_{1}\subset\dots\subset M_{k}

such that
M_{i}/M_{i-1}\simeq K^{-}(L_{i})
. Indeed, let
L_{1}
be
h
-eigenspace with maximal eigenvalue. Then
\mathfrak{g}_{+}L_{1}=0
and we have an embedding
K^{-}(L_{1})\subset M
.
The quotient
M/K^{-}(L_{1})
is again free over
\mathcal{U}(\mathfrak{g}_{+})
and projective in
\mathcal{F}(\mathfrak{p}_{+})
. Hence we can finish the proof by induction on dimension of
M
.
Similarly
M^{*}
has a finite filtration with quotients isomorphic to
K^{+}(N_{j})
. Therefore
M\otimes M^{*}
has a filtration with quotients isomorphic to

K^{+}(L_{i})\otimes K^{-}(N_{j})\simeq\operatorname{Ind}^{\mathfrak{g}}_{\mathfrak{g}_{0}}(L_{i}\otimes N_{j})
. In other words
M\otimes M^{*}
has a filtration by projective modules. Therefore
M\otimes M^{*}
is projective
in
\mathcal{F}(\mathfrak{g})
.
Then
M\otimes M^{*}\otimes M
is also projective. In any symmetric monoidal rigid category
M
is a direct summand of
M\otimes M^{*}\otimes M
.
Therefore
M
is projective.
∎

Corollary 10.5
.

Theorem
10.4
holds for
\mathfrak{gl}(m|n)
,
\mathfrak{sl}(m|n)
,
m\neq n
,
\mathfrak{osp}(2|2n)
and
\mathfrak{p}(n)
.

Remark 10.6
.

Let

\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{sl}(1|1)
. Then it is easy to construct a

\mathfrak{g}
-module
M
such that

X_{M}=0
and
M
is not projective. Recall that
\mathfrak{g}_{1}
has a basis
\{x,y\}
and
\mathfrak{g}_{0}=\mathbb{C}z

with
[x,y]=z
,
[z,x]=[z,y]=0
. Then
X=\mathbb{C}x\cup\mathbb{C}y
.
Let
M=\mathbb{C}^{1|1}
,
z
acts trivially on
M
, and both
x
and
y
act via the same matrix

\left(\begin{matrix}0&1\\
0&0\end{matrix}\right)
. Clearly
M
is
not projective. Note that the
\mathfrak{sl}(1|1)
-module
M
is not the restriction of a
\mathfrak{gl}(1|1)
-module.

10.3.
Rank varieties

From reductivity, we have a well-defined notion of semisimple elements in
\mathfrak{g}_{0}
, so the following definition makes sense.

Definition 10.7
.

We set

\mathfrak{g}_{1}^{hom}:=\{x\in\mathfrak{g}_{1}:[x,x]\text{ is semisimple}\}.

We refer to elements of
\mathfrak{g}_{1}^{hom}
as homological elements.

Remark 10.8
.

Clearly we have
X\subseteq\mathfrak{g}_{1}^{hom}
. Further
\mathfrak{g}_{1}^{hom}
is
G_{0}
-stable, just like
X
. However
\mathfrak{g}_{1}^{hom}
is no longer closed in
\mathfrak{g}_{1}
, and its geometric structure is much more complicated.

Let
x\in\mathfrak{g}_{1}^{hom}
and write
h=[x,x]
. Then for
M
in
\mathcal{F}(\mathfrak{g})
, if we consider
M^{h}
, the fixed points of
h
on
M
, it is
x
-stable and further
x
defines a square-zero endomorphism on it. Thus we may define

M_{x}:=(\ker x|_{M^{h}})/(\operatorname{Im}x|_{M^{h}}).

This defines a functor which we continue to call
DS_{x}
, the Duflo–Serganova functor for the element
x
. Note that the Duflo–Serganova functor as we defined it in Section
2

comes from the case when
h=0
.

Remark 10.9
.

It is easy to check that Lemma
2.2
and Lemma
2.4
hold for this generalization of the
DS
functor.

The following space was considered in
[
ES4
]
.

Definition 10.10
.

Let
M
be in
\mathcal{F}(\mathfrak{g})
and define the rank variety of
M
to be

X_{M}^{rk}:=\{x\in\mathfrak{g}_{1}^{hom}:M_{x}\neq 0\}

Again we have that
X_{M}^{rk}\subseteq\mathfrak{g}_{1}^{hom}
is
G_{0}
-stable; however as is hinted in Remark
10.8
, the geometric structure of
X_{M}^{rk}
can be quite complicated.

We note that rank varieties share many of the same properties as associated varieties; in particular, all properties from Lemma
2.20
continue to hold. In particular, we can use an analogous proof as in Theorem
10.2
to show that:

Proposition 10.11
.

Let
P
be projective in
\mathcal{F}(\mathfrak{g})
. Then
X_{P}^{rk}=\{0\}
.

We make the following conjecture:

Conjecture 10.12
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be quasireductive and suppose that
M
is in
\mathcal{F}(\mathfrak{g})
with
X_{M}^{rk}=\{0\}
. Then
M
is projective.

A proof of this conjecture is currently forthcoming, and is being worked on by the third and fourth authors with other collaborators.

Example 10.13
.

Consider the example given in Remark
10.6
. For
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{sl}(1|1)
we have
\mathfrak{g}_{1}^{hom}=\mathfrak{g}_{1}
. Clearly for the module
M
considered there,
X_{M}^{rk}=\{c(x-y):c\in\mathbb{C}\}
.

11.
Localization of the
DS
functor

In this section, we associate to every finite-dimensional
\mathfrak{g}
-module a vector bundle on
X
with a square-zero
\mathcal{O}_{X}
-module endomorphism, which interpolates the actions of the elements of
X
. We relate the cohomology of this operator to the associated variety of
M
, and apply it to a cohomology computation for
\mathfrak{gl}(m|n)
.

11.1.
Localization

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a finite-dimensional Lie superalgebra, and let
M
be a
\mathfrak{g}
-module. Let
{\mathcal{O}}_{X}
denote the structure sheaf of
X
. Then
{\mathcal{O}}_{X}\otimes M
is the sheaf of
sections of the trivial vector bundle with fiber isomorphic to
M
. Let
\partial:{\mathcal{O}}_{X}\otimes M\to{\mathcal{O}}_{X}\otimes M
be the map defined by

\partial\varphi\left(x\right)=x\varphi\left(x\right)

for any
x\in X
,
\varphi\in{\mathcal{O}}_{X}\otimes M
. Clearly

\partial^{2}=0
and the cohomology

{\mathcal{M}}
of
\partial
is a
quasi-coherent sheaf on
X
. If
M
is finite-dimensional, then
{\mathcal{M}}
is coherent.

For any
x\in X
denote by
{\mathcal{O}}_{x}
the local ring at
x
, by
{\mathcal{I}}_{x}
the maximal ideal.
Let
\tilde{\mathcal{M}}_{x}
be the cohomology of

\partial:{\mathcal{O}}_{x}\otimes M\to{\mathcal{O}}_{x}\otimes M
and
\mathcal{M}_{x}:=\tilde{\mathcal{M}}_{x}/{\mathcal{I}}_{x}\tilde{\mathcal{M}}_{x}
.
The evaluation
map
j_{x}:{\mathcal{O}}_{x}\otimes M\to M
satisfies
j_{x}\circ\partial=x\circ j_{x}
. Hence we have the maps

j_{x}:\operatorname{Ker}\partial\to\operatorname{Ker}x\text{, }j_{x}:\operatorname{Im}\partial\to xM.

The embedding
M\hookrightarrow{\mathcal{O}}_{x}\otimes M
ensures the surjectivity of the latter map. Thus,
j_{x}
induces
the map
\bar{j}_{x}\colon\tilde{\mathcal{M}}_{x}\to M_{x}
, and

\operatorname{Im}\bar{j}_{x}\cong{\mathcal{M}}_{x}
.

Remark 11.1
.

It is easy to see that
{\mathcal{M}}_{x}
is a
(\mathfrak{g}_{x})_{0}
-module and
\bar{j}_{x}
is a homomorphism of
(\mathfrak{g}_{x})_{0}
-modules.

Lemma 11.2
.

Let
M
be a finite-dimensional
\mathfrak{g}
-module.

(1)

The support of
{\mathcal{M}}
is
contained in

X_{M}
.

(2)

The map
\bar{j}_{x}
is surjective for a generic point
x\in X
. In particular,
if
X_{M}=X
, then
\operatorname{supp}{\mathcal{M}}=X
.

Proof.

First, we will show that for any
x\in X\backslash X_{M}
there exists a
neighborhood
U
of
x
such that
{\mathcal{M}}\left(U\right)=0
. Indeed, there exists

h_{x}\in\operatorname{End}_{\mathbb{C}}(M)
such that
x\circ h_{x}+h_{x}\circ x=id_{M}.
Therefore in some neighborhood
U
of
x
there
exists an
\mathcal{O}(U)
-morphism

h:{\mathcal{O}}\left(U\right)\otimes M\to{\mathcal{O}}\left(U\right)\otimes M
such that

\partial\circ h+h\circ\partial
is invertible and
h(x)=h_{x}
. Hence
the cohomology of
\partial:{\mathcal{O}}\left(U\right)\otimes M\to{\mathcal{O}}\left(U\right)\otimes M
are trivial.
In other words,
{\mathcal{M}}\left(U\right)=0
.
Thus,
x
does not belong to the support of
{\mathcal{M}}
and we have obtained that
\operatorname{supp}{\mathcal{M}}\subset X_{M}
.

To prove (2) let
x\in X
be a non-singular point such
that
\operatorname{dim}M_{x}
is minimal. Let
m\in\operatorname{Ker}x_{M}
. Then there exists some neighborhood
U
of
x
and

\varphi\in{\mathcal{O}}\left(U\right)\otimes M
such that

\partial\varphi=0
and
\varphi\left(x\right)=m
. By definition

\varphi\in{\mathcal{M}}_{x}
and
\bar{j}_{x}\left(\varphi\right)=m
.∎

Corollary 11.3
.

Let
x\in X
be a generic point, then in some neighborhood
U

of
x
, the sheaf
{\mathcal{M}}_{U}
coincides with the sheaf of sections of a vector bundle
with fiber
M_{x}
.

Let
X_{M}\not=X
. Then
{\mathcal{M}}
is the extension by zero of the sheaf
{\mathcal{M}}_{X_{M}}
and
{\mathcal{M}}_{X_{M}}
locally is the sheaf of
sections of a
vector bundle with fiber
\bar{j}_{x}({\mathcal{M}}_{x})
for a generic
x\in X_{M}
. Note that

\bar{j}_{x}({\mathcal{M}}_{x})\subset M_{x}
, but usually this is a
strict embedding, as one can see from the following example.

Example 11.4
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{gl}\left(1|1\right)
and
M
be the standard

\mathfrak{g}
-module. Then

X=\left\{\left(\begin{matrix}0&u\\
v&0\end{matrix}\right)\mid uv=0\right\}.

Therefore
X_{M}=\left\{0\right\}
, but a simple computation shows that
\mathcal{M}=0
, and in particular, the support of
\mathcal{M}
is empty.

For
x\in X
, let
G_{0}^{x}
denote the stabilizer of
x
in
G_{0}
.
The following statement illustrates a geometric meaning of
(\mathfrak{g}_{x})_{1}
.

Proposition 11.5
.

Let
x\in X
. Then the

12.2.
An overview of arc diagrams

We now begin the explanation of part (4) of Theorem
12.1
, which will consume the rest of this section. For the classical series
\mathfrak{g}\mathfrak{l}(m|n),\mathfrak{osp}(m|2n)
, and
\mathfrak{p}(n)
, there is a remarkable thread which links the computations of the composition factors of
DS_{x}L
for a simple module
L
, namely arc diagrams. (For
\mathfrak{gl}(m|n)
and
\mathfrak{p}(n)
these diagrams have also been called cap/cup diagrams, in
[
HsW
]
for
\mathfrak{gl}(m|n)
and in
[
ES2
]
for
\mathfrak{p}(n)
; we have changed the name for the sake of consistency.) These arc diagrams are defined individually for each superalgebra and provide a combinatorial tool to study this question.

We summarize the situation as follows. For each of the four Lie superalgebras listed above, we explain a procedure which associates to each simple module
L
an arc diagram, which consists of symbols lying on (half)-integer points on the real line, along with arcs which connect them. These arcs sometimes are nested within one another, giving rise to the notion of maximal arcs, those which do not lie beneath another arc. Then, as is stated in Theorem
12.1
, the composition factors of
DS_{x}L
are given by the simple modules whose associated arc diagram is obtained by removing one maximal arc from the arc diagram of
L
. As will be seen, the procedure for defining arc diagrams is different for each superalgebra.

The idea of using arc diagrams to study the representations of Lie superalgebras goes back to the work of Brundan and Stroppel, where they realized the category
\operatorname{Rep}GL(m|n)
as a certain diagram algebra of Khovanov type (see
[
BS
]
). Their arc diagrams are, notation aside, in essence the same as what will define for
\mathfrak{g}\mathfrak{l}(m|n)
below.

For the orthosymplectic supergroup, Gruson and Serganova used arc diagrams for ‘tailess’ dominant weights in
[
GrS2
]
. More recently, Ehrig and Stroppel have done similar work on realizing
\operatorname{Rep}OSp(m|2n)
as a certain diagram algebra, (see
[
EhSt
]
). Their diagram algebra is related to type D Khovanov algebras; however, their arc diagrams differ from those used in
[
GH1
]
to study the action of
DS_{x}
on simple modules. A dictionary to go between them is described in Appendix A of
[
GH1
]
.

Remark 12.6
.

There is an interesting link between arc diagrams and the computations of character formulas for
\mathfrak{g}\mathfrak{l}(m|n)
and
\mathfrak{osp}(m|2n)
(see
[
GH2
]
) as well as for
\mathfrak{q}(n)
(see
[
SuZh
]
). A similar connection is expected for
\mathfrak{p}(n)
as well.

-module with basis

\mathcal{B}
we have a short exact sequence

0\to M\to P(L^{i})\to L^{i}\to 0,

where
P(L^{i})
denotes the projective cover of
L^{i}
and
M
is its radical. By Lemma
2.7
and that
P(L^{i})_{x}=0
, we find that
M_{x}\cong\Pi L_{x}^{i}
. On the other hand by Lemma
12.28
, we have the short exact sequence

0\to L^{i}\to M\to\ \bigoplus\limits_{j\in Adj(i)}L^{j}\to 0

Suppose that
L^{i}_{x}
is pure, so that
\operatorname{Hom}(L^{i}_{x},M_{x})=0
. Then we obtain by Lemma
2.7
the short exact sequence (using that
M_{x}\cong\Pi L_{x}^{i}
):

0\to\Pi L_{x}^{i}\to\bigoplus\limits_{j\in Adj(i)}L^{j}_{x}\to\Pi L_{x}^{i}\to 0

Using connectedness of the extension graph, purity of
L_{x}^{j}
for any
j
easily follows, along with the formulas in the case of each type of block.
∎

In order to complete the description of the DS functor for exceptional Lie superalgebras, we need to compute the value of the
DS
functor on one module in every atypical block, and in particular check that it is pure so that Proposition
12.29
will apply.

Thus let
\mathfrak{g}
be one of the Lie superalgebras
D(2|1;a),G(3),F(4).

Let
\mathfrak{h}
be a Cartan subalgebra of
\mathfrak{g}_{0}
.
We denote by
W
the Weyl group of
\mathfrak{g}_{0}
and by
(-|-)
the symmetric non-degenerate form on
\mathfrak{h}^{*}
which is induced by a non-degenerate invariant
form on
\mathfrak{g}
.

Let
\Sigma
be a base of
\mathfrak{g}
which contains an isotropic root
\beta
.
Fix a non-zero
x\in\mathfrak{g}_{\beta}
. Set

{\Delta}_{x}:=(\beta^{\perp}\cap{\Delta})\setminus\{\beta,-\beta\}
.
By Proposition
4.5
,
{\mathfrak{g}}_{x}
can be identified
with a subalgebra of
{\mathfrak{g}}
generated by the root spaces

\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}
with
\alpha\in{\Delta}_{x}
and a Cartan subalgebra

{\mathfrak{h}}_{x}\subset\mathfrak{h}
. If
{\Delta}_{x}
is not empty, then

{\Delta}_{x}
is the root system of the Lie superalgebra
{\mathfrak{g}}_{x}
and one can choose

{\Sigma}_{x}
in
\Delta_{x}
such that

\Delta^{+}({\Sigma}_{x})=\Delta^{+}\cap{\Delta}_{x}
.
For

\mathfrak{g}=D(2|1;a),G(3),F(4)
one has
{\mathfrak{g}}_{x}=\mathbb{C},\mathfrak{sl}_{2},\mathfrak{sl}_{3}
respectively.

Lemma 12.30
.

Let
L:=L(\lambda)
be a finite-dimensional module and
(\lambda|\beta)=0
.
Set
L^{\prime}:=L_{\mathfrak{g}_{x}}(\lambda|_{\mathfrak{h}_{x}})
. One has

DS_{x}(L)\cong\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}L^{\prime}&\text{ for }&G(3)\\
L^{\prime}&\text{ for }&D(2|1;a),\ F(4)\text{ if }\ L^{\prime}\cong(L^{\prime})^{*}\\
L^{\prime}\oplus(L^{\prime})^{*}&\text{ for }&D(2|1;a),\ F(4)\text{ if }\ L^{\prime}\not\cong(L^{\prime})^{*}.\end{array}\right.

Proof.

It is easy to see that
[DS_{x}(L):L^{\prime}]=1
.
Set
\lambda^{\prime}:=\lambda|_{\mathfrak{h}_{x}}
.
By Section
6.5
,

DS_{x}(L)
is a typical module and each simple subquotient of
DS_{x}(L)

is of the form

L_{\mathfrak{g}_{x}}(\nu)
with
\nu\in\{\lambda^{\prime},\sigma(\lambda^{\prime})\}
, where
\sigma=\text{Id}

for
\mathfrak{g}=G(3)
,
\sigma=-\text{Id}
for
D(2|1;a)
, and

\sigma
is the Dynkin diagram automorphism of
\mathfrak{g}_{x}=\mathfrak{sl}_{3}
in
F(4)
-case.
This gives the first formula.
For
D(2|1;a)
,
F(4)
one has
L_{\mathfrak{g}_{x}}(\nu)^{*}\cong L_{\mathfrak{g}_{x}}(\sigma(\nu))
;
giving the second formula. Finally in
D(2|1;a),F(4)

the Weyl group contains
-\text{Id}
, so
L\cong L^{*}
and thus

DS_{x}(L)\cong DS_{x}(L^{*})
by Lemma
2.4
, implying the third formula.
∎

We fix a triangular decomposition of
\mathfrak{g}_{0}
and denote by
\Delta^{+}_{0}
the corresponding set of positive roots. We consider
all bases
\Sigma
for
\Delta
which satisfy

\Delta^{+}_{0}\subset\Delta^{+}(\Sigma)
.
We say that an isotropic root
\beta
is of the
first type

if
\beta
lies in a base
\Sigma
with
\Delta^{+}_{0}\subset\Delta^{+}(\Sigma)
.

Take any base
\Sigma
as above and denote by
\rho
the corresponding Weyl vector.
It is easy to see that a simple atypical module
L=L(\nu)
satisfies the assumptions of Lemma
12.30
for some
\Sigma^{\prime}
and
\beta\in\Sigma^{\prime}
if and only if
\nu+\rho
is orthogonal
to an isotropic root of the first type.

Let
\mathcal{B}
be an atypical block of
\mathfrak{g}
. We call the block containing the trivial module
L(0)

a
principal block
. Clearly,
DS_{x}(L(0))
is the trivial
\mathfrak{g}_{x}
-module,
so Proposition
12.29
gives
DS_{x}(L)
for each simple module
L
in
\mathcal{B}_{0})
.

Combining Proposition
12.29
and Lemma
12.30
, we see that in order to compute
DS_{x}(L)
for each simple
L
in
\mathcal{B}
, it is enough to find

L(\nu)\in\text{Irr}(\mathcal{B})
such that
\nu+\rho
is orthogonal to an isotropic root of the first type.
Below we will list such
\nu
for each non-principal atypical block for
D(2|1;a)
,
F(4)
and
G(3)
.

12.6.1.
Case
D(2|1;a)

For
\mathfrak{g}:=D(2|1;a)
one has
\mathfrak{g}_{x}=\mathbb{C}
.
The atypical blocks were described in
[
Ger
]
, Thm. 3.1.1.

The extension graph of the principal block
\mathcal{B}_{0}
is
D_{\infty}
, so for a simple
L^{i}
in
\mathcal{B}_{0}
we have

DS_{x}(L^{i})=\mathbb{C}

for
i=0,1
and
DS_{x}(L^{i})=\Pi^{i-1}(\mathbb{C})^{\oplus 2}

for
i>1
(where
\mathbb{C}
stands for the trivial even
\mathfrak{g}_{x}
-module).

If
a
is irrational, the principal block is
the only atypical block in
\mathcal{F}(\mathfrak{g})
. Consider the case when
a
is rational.
Recall that
\mathfrak{h}^{*}
has an orthogonal basis

\{\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2},\varepsilon_{3}\}
with

(\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{1})=-\frac{1+a}{2},\ \ \ (\varepsilon_{2},\varepsilon_{2})=\frac{1}{2},\ \ \ (\varepsilon_{3},\varepsilon_{3})=\frac{a}{2}.

One has

D(2|1;1)=\mathfrak{osp}(4|2),\ \ D(2|1;a)\cong D(2|1;-1-a)\cong D(2|1;a^{-1})

so we can assume that
0<a<1
and write
a=\frac{p}{q}
, where
p,q
are relatively prime positive integers.

The atypical blocks are
\mathcal{B}_{k}
for
k\in\mathbb{N}

(the principal block is
\mathcal{B}_{0}
). Consider the block
\mathcal{B}_{k}
with

k>0
. The extension graph of
\mathcal{B}_{k}
is

A^{\infty}_{\infty}
. By
[
Ger
]
, Thm. 3.1.1, the block

\mathcal{B}_{k}
contains a simple module
L
with the highest weight
\lambda_{k;0}

satisfying
(\lambda_{k;0}+\rho|\beta)=0
for

\beta:=\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}-\varepsilon_{3}.

Taking
x\in\mathfrak{g}_{\beta}
we can
identify
\mathfrak{g}_{x}
with
\mathbb{C}h
for

h:=\varepsilon_{1}^{*}-\varepsilon_{2}^{*}
(where
\varepsilon_{1}^{*},\varepsilon_{2}^{*},\varepsilon_{3}^{*}
is the dual basis in
\mathfrak{h}
).
By Lemma
12.30
we get

DS_{x}(L)=L_{\mathfrak{g}_{x}}(k)\oplus L_{\mathfrak{g}_{x}}(-k),

where
L_{\mathfrak{g}_{x}}(u)
stands for the even one-dimensional
\mathfrak{g}_{x}
-module
with
h
acting by
k(p+q)
.
By Proposition
12.29
,
DS_{x}(L^{i})\cong\Pi^{i}(DS_{x}(L))

for each
L^{i}\in\text{Irr}(\mathcal{B}_{k})
(for
k>0
).

12.6.2.
Case
G(3)

For
\mathfrak{g}:=G(3)
the atypical blocks were described in Thm. 4.1.1 of
[
Ger
]
.
The atypical blocks in
\mathcal{F}(\mathfrak{g})
are
\mathcal{B}_{k}
for
k\in\mathbb{N}
;
the extension graphs are
D_{\infty}
. The block

\mathcal{B}_{k}
contains a simple module with the highest weight
\lambda_{k;0}

satisfying
(\lambda_{k;0}+\rho|\beta)=0
for

\beta:=-\varepsilon_{1}+\delta.

Taking
\Sigma:=\{\delta-\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2}-\delta,\delta\}
and

x\in\mathfrak{g}_{\beta}
we can
identify
\mathfrak{g}_{x}
with

\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
-triple corresponding to the root
\alpha=\varepsilon_{1}+2\varepsilon_{2}
.
One has
\lambda_{k;0}=k\alpha
.
Combining Lemma
12.30
and Proposition
12.29
we get

DS_{x}(L^{0})\cong DS_{x}(L^{1})\cong L_{\mathfrak{sl}_{2}}(2k),\ \ \ DS_{x}(L^{i})=\Pi^{i-1}(L_{\mathfrak{sl}_{2}}(2k))^{\oplus 2}\ \text{ for }i>1.

12.6.3.
Case
F(4)

For
\mathfrak{g}:=F(4)
we have
\mathfrak{g}_{x}\cong\mathfrak{sl}_{3}
.
The integral weight lattice is spanned by
\varepsilon_{1}
,
\varepsilon_{2}
,

\frac{1}{2}(\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}+\varepsilon_{3})
and
\frac{1}{2}\delta
;
the parity is given by

p(\frac{\varepsilon_{i}}{2})=0
and
p(\frac{\delta}{2})=1
.

The atypical blocks are described
in Thm. 2.1 of
[
M
]
. These blocks are
parametrized by the pairs
(m_{1},m_{2})
, where

m_{1},m_{2}\in\mathbb{N}
,
m_{1}\geq m_{2}
, and
m_{1}-m_{2}
is divisible by 3.
We denote the corresponding block by
\mathcal{B}_{(m_{1};m_{2})}
.

The extension
graph of
\mathcal{B}_{(i;i)}
is
D_{\infty}
; the block

\mathcal{B}_{(0;0)}
is principal.
For
i>0
the block

\mathcal{B}_{(i;i)}
contains a simple module
L(\lambda)
with

\lambda+\rho=(i+1)(\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2})-\beta_{1},\ \text{ where }\beta_{1}:=\frac{1}{2}(-\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}-\varepsilon_{3}+\delta).

One has

(\lambda+\rho|\beta_{1})=0
. Take
x\in\mathfrak{g}_{\beta_{1}}
and consider the base

\Sigma_{1}:=\{\beta_{1};\frac{1}{2}(\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}-\varepsilon_{3}-\delta);\varepsilon_{3};\varepsilon_{1}-\varepsilon_{2}\}.

Then

\mathfrak{g}_{x}
can be identified with
\mathfrak{sl}_{3}
corresponding to the set of simple roots

\ \{\varepsilon_{2}+\varepsilon_{3};\varepsilon_{1}-\varepsilon_{3}\}
and Lemma
12.30
gives

DS_{x}(L(\lambda))=L_{\mathfrak{sl}_{3}}(i\omega_{1}+i\omega_{2}),

where
\omega_{1},\omega_{2}
are the fundamental weights of
\mathfrak{sl}_{3}
.
By Proposition
12.29
we get for the simple module
L^{j}
in
\mathcal{B}_{(i;i)}
:

DS_{x}(L^{0})\cong DS_{x}(L^{1})\cong L_{\mathfrak{sl}_{3}}(i\omega_{1}+i\omega_{2}),\ \ DS_{x}(L^{j})\cong\Pi^{j-1}(L_{\mathfrak{sl}_{3}}(i\omega_{1}+i\omega_{2}))^{\oplus 2}\ \text{ for }j>1.

Consider a block
\mathcal{B}_{(i_{1};i_{2})}
for
i_{1}\not=i_{2}
.
The extension
graph of this block is
A_{\infty}^{\infty}
and this
block contains a simple module
L:=L(\lambda^{\prime})
with

\lambda^{\prime}+\rho=i_{1}\varepsilon_{1}+i_{2}\varepsilon_{2}+(i_{1}-i_{2})\varepsilon_{3}.

In particular,
(\lambda^{\prime}+\rho|\beta_{2})=0
for

\beta_{2}:=\frac{1}{2}(-\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}+\varepsilon_{3}+\delta)
.
Taking
x\in\mathfrak{g}_{\beta_{1}}
and

\Sigma_{2}:=\{\beta_{2};\varepsilon_{2}-\varepsilon_{3};-\beta_{1};\frac{1}{2}(\varepsilon_{1}-\varepsilon_{2}-\varepsilon_{3}+\delta)\}

we identify

\mathfrak{g}_{x}
with
\mathfrak{sl}_{3}
corresponding to the set of simple roots

\{\varepsilon_{2}-\varepsilon_{3};\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{3}\}
. Combining Lemma
12.30

and Proposition
12.29
we get

DS_{x}(L)=L_{\mathfrak{sl}_{3}}(i_{1}\omega_{1}+i_{2}\omega_{2})\oplus L_{\mathfrak{sl}_{3}}(i_{2}\omega_{1}+i_{1}\omega_{2}),\ \ DS_{x}(L^{i})\cong\Pi^{i}(DS_{x}(L))

for each
L^{i}
in the block
\mathcal{B}_{(i_{1};i_{2})}
.
