Crossing antisymmetric Polyakov blocks + Dispersion relation


Abstract
Many CFT problems, e.g. ones with global symmetries, have correlation functions with a crossing antisymmetric sector. We show that such a crossing antisymmetric function can be expanded in terms of manifestly crossing antisymmetric objects, which we call the ‘ + + type Polyakov blocks’. These blocks are built from AdS d+1 Witten diagrams. In 1d they encode the ‘ + + type’ analytic functionals which act on crossing antisymmetric functions. In general d d we establish this Witten diagram basis from a crossing antisymmetric dispersion relation in Mellin space. Analogous to the crossing symmetric case, the dispersion relation imposes a set of independent ‘locality constraints’ in addition to the usual CFT sum rules given by the ‘Polyakov conditions’. We use the Polyakov blocks to simplify more general analytic functionals in d > 1 d>1 and global symmetry functionals.

Introduction

One of the most important tools available to theoretical physicists in CFT problems is crossing symmetry or the Conformal Bootstrap. For numerical analysis one typically approaches the 4-point crossing problem by introducing a set of functionals, which are simply a set of derivatives w.r.t. the cross ratios. From the recent works of
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
(see also
[
4
]
and futher generalizations
[
5
,
6
,
7
,
8
,
9
]
) we have learnt that there exists a more efficient set of functionals, the analytic functionals. These are built to optimize OPE coefficients, and are ‘dual’ to GFF or Generalized Free Fields (i.e. a basis expansion in terms of double trace conformal blocks).

The analytic functionals are perhaps most well understood in 1d, where they are related to another formulation of crossing symmetry: the Polyakov bootstrap
[
10
]
. The latter is an idea that a crossing symmetric correlator can be expanded in terms of a manifestly crossing symmetric sum of Witten diagrams (a Polyakov block) which must be equal to the usual conformal block expansion
[
11
,
12
,
13
]
. The validity of this has been rigorously proven by the 1d analytic functionals. In 1d, Polyakov blocks also give a simple way to determine action of the functionals, which are otherwise hard to compute. This connection has been recently used in
[
14
]
for general global symmetries to obtain a number of interesting numerical results.

Recently in
[
15
,
16
]
a crossing symmetric dispersion relation has been proposed which establishes the Polyakov Bootstrap in general
d
. The main idea here, which is based on
[
17
]
, is to work in Mellin space and map the Mellin variables to new variables that are manifestly crossing symmetric but with nonlocal singularities. A dispersion relation is then obtained in these variables, which reduces to a crossing symmetric sum of exchange and contact Witten diagrams when one sets the nonlocal singularities to zero (locality constraints). The dispersion relation is also used in the QFT context (replacing Mellin variables with Mandelstam variables) where these locality constraints have been used to derive interesting bounds on Effective Field Theories (EFTs).

Let us now address a situation where crossing symmetry of a 4-point function involves more than just crossing symmetric functions. E.g.
consider a CFT with global symmetry with a correlator
\mathcal{G}_{ijkl}(z,\bar{z})
of charged fields
\phi_{i}
transforming in some irrep
\mathfrak{r}
. If
\mathfrak{r}\otimes\mathfrak{r}
contains the irreps labelled
\mathfrak{a}
then the correlator decomposes as

\mathcal{G}_{ijkl}(z,\bar{z})=\sum_{\mathfrak{a}}T^{\mathfrak{a}}_{ij,kl}\mathcal{G}^{\mathfrak{a}}(z,\bar{z})

(1.1)

where
T^{\mathfrak{a}}
denotes the associated tensor structure of
\mathfrak{a}
. We use the notations of usual CFT kinematics, reviewed in section
2
.
The crossing equation for
\mathcal{G}^{\mathfrak{a}}
can be written as:

\mathcal{G}^{\mathfrak{a}}(z,\bar{z})=C^{\mathfrak{a}\mathfrak{b}}\mathcal{G}^{\mathfrak{b}}(1-z,1-\bar{z})\,.

(1.2)

Here
C^{\mathfrak{a}\mathfrak{b}}
is a crossing matrix (see e.g.
[
14
]
) that has eigenvalues
\pm 1
corresponding to eigenvectors
e^{\mathfrak{a}}_{\pm,s}
where
s
is a label. Now, while the functions
\mathcal{G}_{-}(z,\bar{z})=e^{\mathfrak{a}}_{+,s}\mathcal{G}^{\mathfrak{a}}(z,\bar{z})
, for any
s
, obey usual crossing symmetry
\mathcal{G}_{-}(z,\bar{z})=\mathcal{G}_{-}(1-z,1-\bar{z})
, the combinations
\mathcal{G}_{+}(z,\bar{z})=e^{\mathfrak{a}}_{-,s}\mathcal{G}^{\mathfrak{a}}(z,\bar{z})
are functions that have crossing antisymmetry:

\mathcal{G}_{+}(z,\bar{z})=-\mathcal{G}_{+}(1-z,1-\bar{z})\,.

(1.3)

An obvious question to ask is: does
\mathcal{G}_{+}
also allow a Witten diagram like expansion?
In 1d there exists a set of analytic functionals, called
+
type functionals, which can bootstrap such antisymmetric functions although it is not understood if they are related to a Polyakov block.
1
1

1

In
[
14
]
this was bypassed by considering analytic functionals dual to GFF for a certain global symmetry which are related to crossing symmetric Polyakov blocks of the same symmetry
[
18
,
19
]
.
It would be nice to understand this more generally in
d\geq 1
.

In this paper we show that, analogous to the crossing symmetric case, crossing antisymmetric functions can be expanded in terms of manifestly crossing antisymmetric objects as follows:

\mathcal{G}_{+}(z,\bar{z})=\sum_{\Delta,\ell}a_{\Delta,\ell}\mathcal{P}_{+,\Delta,\ell}(z,\bar{z})\,,

(1.4)

where
\mathcal{P}_{+,\Delta,\ell}
, the ‘
+
type Polyakov block’, is a linear combination of AdS
d+1
Witten diagrams. In 1d these blocks involve a finite number of diagrams similar to its crossing symmetric counterpart. They are related to the
+
type analytic functionals. We construct two sets of blocks corresponding to the bosonic and fermionic functional bases.

The idea of a
+
type Polyakov block is then extended to general dimensions, which requires an infinite number of exchange Witten diagrams and crossing antisymmetric contact diagrams. For this we show that a crossing antisymmetric function can be obtained from a dispersion relation in Mellin space. Analogous to the crossing symmetric case, it is characterized by an infinite number of nonlocal singularities that lead to a new set of locality constraints. The dispersion relation can be reformulated as the Witten diagram expansion once the locality conditions are satisfied. Finally we give a set of crossing antisymmetric sum rules.

We have numerically demonstrated how locality conditions work, and how a known crossing antisymmetric solution satisfies the new sum rules.
We show how the
+
type Polyakov blocks simplify computation of ‘product functionals’ which exist in even dimensions and are built by combining 1d functionals
[
20
]
.
We also show one can obtain a set of ‘simple functionals’ for global symmetry problems in general dimensions from the Polyakov blocks extending the arguments of
[
14
]
.

2
1d crossing antisymmetric functionals

2.1
Basic kinematics

Let us define some basic kinematics and notations. Consider a 4-point function
\mathcal{G}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4})
of scalars (say
\phi(x_{i})
) with identical dimension
\Delta_{\phi}
in a
d
-dimensional CFT which is given by

\mathcal{G}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4})=\frac{\mathcal{G}(z,\bar{z})}{x_{13}^{2\Delta_{\phi}}x_{24}^{2\Delta_{\phi}}}\,,\ \ z\bar{z}=\frac{x_{12}^{2}x_{34}^{2}}{x_{13}^{2}x_{24}^{2}}\,,\ (1-z)(1-\bar{z})=\frac{x_{14}^{2}x_{23}^{2}}{x_{13}^{2}x_{24}^{2}}\,.

(2.1)

We will sometimes use the notations
u=z\bar{z}
and
v=(1-z)(1-\bar{z})
. The quantity
\mathcal{G}(z,\bar{z})
can be decomposed into conformal blocks as follows:

\mathcal{G}(z,\bar{z})=\sum_{\Delta,\ell}a_{\Delta,\ell}G_{\Delta,\ell}^{(d)}(z,\bar{z}|\Delta_{\phi})\,.

(2.2)

Here
G_{\Delta,\ell}^{(d)}(z,\bar{z}|\Delta_{\phi})
is the
d
-dimensional conformal block (defined with a factor of
(z\bar{z})^{-\Delta_{\phi}}
) and
a_{\Delta,\ell}
denotes square of OPE coefficients. Typically the function
\mathcal{G}(z,\bar{z})=\mathcal{G}_{-}(z,\bar{z})
is crossing symmetric i.e.
\mathcal{G}_{-}(z,\bar{z})=\mathcal{G}_{-}(1-z,1-\bar{z})
(symmetry under
x_{1}\leftrightarrow x_{3}
exchange) and we may write

\sum_{\Delta,\ell}a_{\Delta,\ell}F_{-,\Delta,\ell}(z,\bar{z}|\Delta_{\phi})=0

(2.3)

where we defined the crossing symmetric bootstrap vector

F_{-,\Delta,\ell}(z,\bar{z}|\Delta_{\phi})=G_{\Delta,\ell}(z,\bar{z}|\Delta_{\phi})-G_{\Delta,\ell}(1-z,1-\bar{z}|\Delta_{\phi})\,.

(2.4)

As shown in (
1.3
) it is often necessary to consider
\mathcal{G}(z,\bar{z})=\mathcal{G}_{+}(z,\bar{z})
which is antisymmetric under
x_{1}\leftrightarrow x_{3}
exchange i.e.
\mathcal{G}_{+}(z,\bar{z})=-\mathcal{G}_{+}(1-z,1-\bar{z})
. If it decomposes into conformal blocks like (
2.2
) we have the crossing antisymmetry equation

\sum_{\Delta,\ell}a_{\Delta,\ell}F_{+,\Delta,\ell}(z,\bar{z}|\Delta_{\phi})=0

(2.5)

where we have the crossing antisymmetric bootstrap vector

F_{+,\Delta,\ell}(z,\bar{z}|\Delta_{\phi})=G_{\Delta,\ell}(z,\bar{z}|\Delta_{\phi})+G_{\Delta,\ell}(1-z,1-\bar{z}|\Delta_{\phi})\,.

(2.6)

We will sometimes loosely refer to the crossing antisymmetric equation as ‘anticrossing equation’. It becomes important in many interesting problems like bootstrapping CFTs with global symmetries or multiple correlators.

For the rest of this section we will focus on 1d CFTs. Here we have a single cross-ratio, so we set
z=\bar{z}
. The (anti)crossing equation is written as

\sum_{\Delta}a_{\Delta}F_{\pm,\Delta}(z|\Delta_{\phi})=0

(2.7)

where we have

F_{\pm}(z|\Delta_{\phi})=G_{\Delta}(z|\Delta_{\phi})\pm G_{\Delta}(1-z|\Delta_{\phi})

(2.8)

and
G_{\Delta}(z|\Delta_{\phi})=z^{\Delta-2\Delta_{\phi}}{}_{2}F_{1}(\Delta,\Delta,2\Delta,z)
is the
SL(2,\mathbb{R})
conformal block .

2.2
1d analytic functionals and Polyakov Bootstrap

The usual approach to constrain CFT data from the (anti)crossing equation is by introducing a set of linear functionals as follows (
\omega(\Delta|\Delta_{\phi})\equiv\omega[F_{\Delta}(z|\Delta_{\phi})]
)

\omega_{\pm}\Big[\sum_{\Delta}a_{\Delta}F_{\pm,\Delta}(z|\Delta_{\phi})\Big]=\sum_{\Delta}a_{\Delta}\omega_{\pm}(\Delta|\Delta_{\phi})=0

(2.9)

In the standard conformal bootstrap approach one chooses a basis of derivatives
\omega_{\pm}=\{\partial_{z},\partial_{z}^{2},\cdots\}
at
z=\frac{1}{2}
. In
[
2
,
3
]
a set of functionals that gives optimal bounds on OPE coefficients were proposed. These are the analytic functionals which correspond to a basis expansion of bootstrap vectors in terms of double trace operator blocks as follows:

F_{\pm,\Delta}(z|\Delta_{\phi})=\sum_{n}\Big[\alpha^{B,F}_{\pm,n}(\Delta)\,F_{\pm,\Delta_{n}^{B,F}}(z|\Delta_{\phi})+\beta^{B,F}_{\pm,n}\,\partial_{\Delta}F_{\pm,\Delta_{n}^{B,F}}(z|\Delta_{\phi})\Big]\,.

(2.10)

Here we have shown two set of bases: Bosonic (
B
) and Fermionic (
F
) which correspond to
\Delta_{n}^{B}=2\Delta_{\phi}+2n
and
\Delta_{n}^{F}=2\Delta_{\phi}+2n+1
. Each analytic functional
\omega_{\pm}=\alpha_{\pm},\beta_{\pm}
is dual to a basis element above. It is defined as follows:

\omega_{\pm}(\Delta|\Delta_{\phi})=\int_{\frac{1}{2}}^{\frac{1}{2}+i\infty}dz\,f_{\pm}(z)F_{\pm,\Delta}(z|\Delta_{\phi})+\int_{\frac{1}{2}}^{1}dz\,g_{\pm}(z)F_{\pm,\Delta}(z|\Delta_{\phi})\,.

(2.11)

The functions
f
and
g
satisfy the conditions

f_{\pm}(z)=\mp f_{\pm}(1-z)\,,\ \mathcal{R}_{z}f_{\pm}(z)=-g_{\pm}(z)\pm g_{\pm}(1-z)\,,\ g_{\pm}(z)=\epsilon(1-z)^{2\Delta_{\phi}-2}f_{\pm}(\frac{z}{z-1})\,.

(2.12)

Here
\mathcal{R}_{z}
denotes the real part, and
\epsilon=+1(-1)
for bosonic (fermionic) case. It is then possible to choose the kernel
f
so that the functionals satisfy the orthogonality properties:

\displaystyle\alpha_{\pm,n}^{B}(\Delta_{m}^{B})

\displaystyle=\delta_{mn}\,,\hskip 28.45274pt\partial_{\Delta}\alpha_{\pm,n}^{B}(\Delta_{m}^{B})=-c^{B}_{\pm,n}(\Delta_{\phi})\delta_{m0}\,,

\displaystyle\beta_{\pm,n}^{B}(\Delta_{m}^{B})

\displaystyle=0\,,\hskip 39.83368pt\partial_{\Delta}\beta_{\pm,n}^{B}(\Delta_{m}^{B})=\delta_{mn}-d_{\pm,n}^{B}(\Delta_{\phi})\delta_{m,0}\,.

(2.13)

Here we show the bosonic case with
\beta^{B}_{\pm,0}=0
and
c^{B}_{\pm,n},d^{B}_{\pm,n}
are known. The fermionic functionals satisfy similar conditions replacing
B\to F
and with
\beta^{F}_{\pm,0}\neq 0
,
c^{F}_{\pm,n}=d^{F}_{\pm,n}=0
. Explicit forms of all kernels can be found in Appendix A of
[
20
]
.

A feature of the kernels that will be important in our discussion is the Regge limit
z\to i\infty
. These are as follows:

\displaystyle\omega_{-}:

\displaystyle\ \ f(z)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle z\to i\infty}}{{\sim}}O(z^{-2})

\displaystyle\omega_{+}:

\displaystyle\ \ f(z)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle z\to i\infty}}{{\sim}}O(z^{-3})\,.

(2.14)

This feature implies that
\omega_{-}
(
\omega_{+}
) functionals can bootstrap (anti)crossing solutions with an
O(z^{0})
(
O(z)
) large
z
behavior.

It has been established that ‘
-
type’ analytic functionals are related to 1d Polyakov Bootstrap in an interesting way. The latter is the idea that a crossing symmetric correlator can be expanded in terms of manifestly crossing symmetric functions, called Polyakov blocks, in the following way

\mathcal{G}_{-}(z)=\sum_{\Delta}a_{\Delta}\mathcal{P}^{B,F}_{-,\Delta}(z)=\sum_{\Delta}a_{\Delta}G_{\Delta}(z)\,.

(2.15)

The equality on the right is the usual OPE. The Polyakov block
\mathcal{P}_{-,\Delta}(z)
is given by a crossing symmetric sum of 4-point Witten diagrams

\mathcal{P}^{B,F}_{-,\Delta}(z)=W_{\Delta,0}^{(s),B,F}(z)+W_{\Delta,0}^{(t),B,F}(z)+W_{\Delta,0}^{(u),B,F}(z)+\kappa_{-}^{B,F}\,\mathcal{C}_{-}(z)\,.

(2.16)

Here
W_{\Delta,0}^{(i),B,F}(z)
is a Witten exchange diagram (spin
0
and dimension
\Delta
exchange in
i=s,t,u
channels) with bosonic/fermionic legs and drawn with a suitable choice of vertex. The
\mathcal{C}_{-}(z)
is a crossing symmetric 4-point contact diagram. We require the Polyakov block to be Regge bounded i.e.
\mathcal{P}_{-,\Delta}(z)<\infty
as
z\to i\infty
. This allows a single contact diagram with bosonic external legs with
\Phi^{4}
vertex. We define the Witten diagrams in Appendix
A
.

The Witten diagrams above can be decomposed into conformal blocks of dimensions
\Delta_{n}^{B,F}
. We can choose Polyakov blocks such that their block decomposition computes functional actions (dropping
\Delta_{\phi}
dependence for convenience) as follows:

\mathcal{P}^{B,F}_{-,\Delta}(z)=G_{\Delta}(z)-\sum_{n}\Big[\alpha^{B,F}_{-,n}(\Delta)G_{\Delta^{B,F}_{n}}(z)+\beta^{B,F}_{-,n}(\Delta)\partial_{\Delta}G_{\Delta_{n}^{B,F}}(z)\Big]\,.

(2.17)

So the statement of Polyakov Bootstrap (
2.15
) is identical to the functional bootstrap equations. While for the fermionic case this is automatic (
\kappa_{-}^{F}=0
), for the bosonic case one can choose
\kappa_{-}^{B}
such that
\beta^{B}_{-,0}=0
and have the above correspondence. Notice that the basis (
2.10
) is nothing but the crossing symmetry equation for
\mathcal{P}_{-,\Delta}(z)
. For more details of Polyakov bootstrap in 1d see
[
3
,
14
]
.

2.3
The ‘+ type’ Polyakov blocks

An obvious question that one can now ask is if there exists an analogue of Polyakov blocks that computes ‘+ type’ functional actions. Indeed there is such a function which we call a ‘
+
type Polyakov block’. In this section we show how to build it from familiar Witten diagrams.

We introduce the following notation for convenience:

\displaystyle\Delta_{n,\ell}=2\Delta_{\phi}+2n+\ell\,.

(2.18)

This is for consistency with the following sections. Note that we have
\Delta_{n,0}=\Delta^{B}_{n}
and
\Delta_{n,1}=\Delta^{F}_{n}
.

2.3.1
Fermionic case

Let us first discuss the case of fermionic functionals. Since
+
type functionals act on crossing antisymmetric vectors, the
+
type Polyakov blocks must be crossing antisymmetric.
Consider spin 1 exchange Witten diagrams in AdS
2
with
bosonic
external legs. They have the conformal block decomposition (for this subsection we write
W_{\Delta,1}^{(i),B}\to W_{\Delta,1}^{(i)}
):

W_{\Delta,1}^{(s)}=G_{\Delta}(z)+\sum_{n}\big(a_{n,1}^{(s)}G_{\Delta_{n,1}}(z)+b_{n,1}^{(s)}\partial_{\Delta}G_{\Delta_{n,1}}(z)\big)\,.

(2.19)

In the crossed channels we have

\displaystyle W_{\Delta,1}^{(-)}(z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}\big(a_{n,1}^{(t)}G_{\Delta_{n,0}}(z)+b_{n,1}^{(t)}\partial_{\Delta}G_{\Delta_{n,0}}(z)\big)\,,

\displaystyle W_{\Delta,1}^{(+)}(z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}\big(\bar{a}_{n,1}^{(t)}G_{\Delta_{n,1}}(z)+\bar{b}_{n,1}^{(t)}\partial_{\Delta}G_{\Delta_{n,1}}(z)\big)\,.

(2.20)

Here we defined
W_{\Delta,1}^{(\pm)}=\frac{1}{2}\big(W_{\Delta,1}^{(t)}\pm W_{\Delta,1}^{(u)}\big)
. The expressions for
W^{(s)}_{\Delta,1}
and other useful details are given Appendix
A
(see
[
14
,
21
,
22
]
for more on the conformal block decomposition).
The crossed channel diagrams are related to the
s
-channel via:

W_{\Delta,1}^{(t)}(z)=W_{\Delta,1}^{(s)}(1-z)\,,\hskip 28.45274ptW_{\Delta,1}^{(u)}(z)=(1-z)^{-2\Delta_{\phi}}\text{Re}W_{\Delta,1}^{(s)}\big(\frac{1}{1-z}\big)\,.

(2.21)

We also have

W_{\Delta,1}^{(u)}(1-z)=-W_{\Delta,1}^{(u)}(z)\,.

(2.22)

A natural crossing antisymmetric Polyakov block is then

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{+,\Delta}^{F}(z)

\displaystyle=W_{\Delta,1}^{(s)}(z)-W_{\Delta,1}^{(t)}(z)-W_{\Delta,1}^{(u)}(z)\,.

\displaystyle=W_{\Delta,1}^{(s)}(z)-2W_{\Delta,1}^{(+)}(z)\,.

(2.23)

Notice that this object is totally crossing antisymmetric i.e.
\mathcal{P}_{+}(z)\to-\mathcal{P}_{+}(z)
under
x_{1}\leftrightarrow x_{3}
or
x_{1}\leftrightarrow x_{4}
. It is also antisymmetric in
x_{1}\leftrightarrow x_{2}
which is not obvious from (
2.3.1
) although it is clear from their Mellin amplitudes (see Appendix
A
and the next section).
2
2

2

All
x_{i}\leftrightarrow x_{j}
are equivalent to simple transformations of Mellin variables, e.g.:
x_{1}\leftrightarrow x_{3}
is equivalent to
s\leftrightarrow t+\Delta_{\phi}
.

The anticrossing property implies the following:

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{+\Delta}(z)=G_{\Delta}(z)-\sum_{n}\Big[\alpha^{F}_{+,n}(\Delta)G_{\Delta_{n,1}}(z)+\beta^{F}_{+,n}(\Delta)\partial_{\Delta}G_{\Delta_{n,1}}(z)\Big]

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle F_{+,\Delta}(z)=\sum_{n}\Big[\alpha^{F}_{+,n}(\Delta|\Delta_{\phi})F_{+,\Delta_{n,1}}(z)+\beta^{F}_{+,n}(\Delta|\Delta_{\phi})\partial_{\Delta}F_{+,\Delta_{n,1}}(z)\Big]\,,

(2.24)

where the coefficients are given by

\displaystyle\alpha^{F}_{+,n}(\Delta|\Delta_{\phi})

\displaystyle=-a_{n,1}^{(s)}+2\bar{a}_{n,1}^{(t)}\,,

\displaystyle\beta^{F}_{+,n}(\Delta|\Delta_{\phi})

\displaystyle=-b_{n,1}^{(s)}+2\bar{b}_{n,1}^{(t)}\,.

(2.25)

The coefficients
\alpha^{F}_{+,n}(\Delta|\Delta_{\phi})
and
\beta^{F}_{+,n}(\Delta|\Delta_{\phi})
are named as such since they are equivalent to the + type fermionic functional actions. It can be verified by explicitly evaluating the actions from (
2.11
) that they match the expressions (
2.3.1
).

We now point out an interesting feature. For the
-
type bosonic functionals the missing
\beta_{-,0}^{B}
functional is attributed to the ambiguity of addition by a
\Phi^{4}
contact Witten diagram to any crossing solution that preserves Regge boundedness
[
3
]
. Similarly for the fermionic case there is no such ambiguity, and hence all
\omega_{-,n}^{F}
are present.
Now recall from (
2.2
) that
\omega_{+}
functionals can bootstrap anti-crossing symmetric solutions with a fall-off
O(z)
. It turns out that there is no anticrossing ‘contact’ diagram i.e. a crossing antisymmetric solution which decomposes into blocks and their derivatives of dimenisions
\Delta_{n,1}
(only), and also has the Regge fall-off
O(z)
.
3
3

3

In section
3.1
we will see that the anticrossing contact diagram with the strictest Regge behavior is
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle s\to\infty}}{{\sim}}O(s^{3})
in Mellin representation. Typically for an
s^{2j}
behavior in Mellin space we have in position space

\mathcal{G}(z)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle z\to i\infty}}{{\sim}}z^{2j-1}\,.

(2.26)

This can be proved by assuming a vertex of the form
(\partial^{j}\Phi)^{4}
in AdS

and computing a 4-point contact diagram

,

This is consistent with the fact that fermionic

exist for all

2.3.2
Bosonic case

Now let us consider the other case, bosonic functionals. One can guess that they require Witten diagrams with fermionic external legs. In
[
25
]
the general
d
fermionic Witten diagrams with a scalar exchange was computed (see also
[
26
]
). The
s
-channel scalar exchange Witten diagram in the AdS
2
case is given in terms of the bosonic Witten diagram as follows

W^{(s),F}_{\Delta,0}(z|\Delta_{\phi})=zW^{(s),B}_{\Delta,0}\big(z|\Delta_{\phi}+\frac{1}{2}\big)\,.

(2.27)

The factor of
z
is to account for the
z^{-2\Delta_{\phi}}
included in our definitions. In a similar way let us define the following object:

W^{(s),F}_{\Delta,1}(z|\Delta_{\phi})=\frac{1}{z}W^{(s),B}_{\Delta,1}\big(z|\Delta_{\phi}-\frac{1}{2}\big)\,.

(2.28)

We will refer to this as the ‘fermionic Witten diagram’ with an
s
-channel spin 1 exchange. Note that computing the Witten diagram with four boundary-bulk fermionic propagators and bulk-bulk spin 1 propagator is a non-trivial task which we do not perform. However it should be related to the above object by addition of a suitable contact diagram. The diagram we defined above already has everything we need for our discussion.

If the fermionic Witten
s
-channel diagram is defined as above, the corresponding crossed channel diagrams would be given by

W_{\Delta,1}^{(t),F}(z|\Delta_{\phi})=W_{\Delta,1}^{(s),F}(1-z)\,,\hskip 28.45274ptW_{\Delta,1}^{(u),F}(z|\Delta_{\phi})=(1-z)^{-2\Delta_{\phi}}\text{Re}W_{\Delta,1}^{(s),F}\big(\frac{1}{1-z}\big)\,.

(2.29)

Once again we have

W_{\Delta,1}^{(u),F}(1-z|\Delta_{\phi})=-W_{\Delta,1}^{(u),F}(z|\Delta_{\phi})\,.

(2.30)

The block decomposition of the above objects are as follows:

\displaystyle W_{\Delta,1}^{(s),F}(z)

\displaystyle=G_{\Delta}(z)+\sum_{n}\big(a_{n,1}^{(s),F}G_{\Delta_{n,0}}(z)+b_{n,1}^{(s),F}\partial_{\Delta}G_{\Delta_{n,0}}(z)\big)\,.

\displaystyle W_{\Delta,1}^{(-),F}(z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}\big(a_{n,1}^{(t),F}G_{\Delta_{n,1}}(z)+b_{n,1}^{(t),F}\partial_{\Delta}G_{\Delta_{n,1}}(z)\big)\,,

\displaystyle W_{\Delta,1}^{(+),F}(z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}\big(\bar{a}_{n,1}^{(t),F}G_{\Delta_{n,0}}(z)+\bar{b}_{n,1}^{(t),F}\partial_{\Delta}G_{\Delta_{n,0}}(z)\big)\,,

(2.31)

where
W_{\Delta,1}^{(\pm),F}=\frac{1}{2}\big(W_{\Delta,1}^{(t),F}\pm W_{\Delta,1}^{(u),F}\big)
.

With this we define an anticrossing object as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{+}^{\text{exc}}(z)

\displaystyle=W_{\Delta,1}^{(s),F}(z)-W_{\Delta,1}^{(t),F}(z)-W_{\Delta,1}^{(u),F}(z)\,.

\displaystyle=W_{\Delta,1}^{(s),F}(z)-2W_{\Delta,1}^{(+),F}(z)\,.

(2.32)

The ‘exc’ stands for ‘exchange’. Under block decompositon
\mathcal{P}_{+}^{\text{exc}}
gives a
G_{\Delta}(z)
and
G_{\Delta_{n,0}}(z)

\forall n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+,0}
.

However
\mathcal{P}_{+}^{\text{exc}}

\mathcal{P}_{+}^{\text{Con}}(z)=\mathcal{C}_{+}^{(s),F}(z)-\mathcal{C}_{+}^{(t),F}(z)-\mathcal{C}_{+}^{(u),F}(z)\,.

(2.33)

Here the first term on r.h.s. can be written in terms of a general dimension Mellin representation as follows:

\mathcal{C}_{+}^{(s),F}(z)=z^{-2\Delta_{\phi}}\Bigg[\int_{-i\infty}^{i\infty}ds\,dt\,z^{2s}\,(1-z)^{2t}\Gamma^{2}(\Delta_{\phi}+\frac{1}{2}-s)\Gamma^{2}(-t)\Gamma^{2}(s+t)\big(s+2t\big)\Bigg]\,.

(2.34)

The other two terms (crossed channels) are given by

4

4

Here
\mathcal{C}_{+}^{(t),F}
and
\mathcal{C}_{+}^{(u),F}

\Delta_{\phi}+\frac{1}{2}-s\leftrightarrow s+t

(2.35)

\mathcal{C}_{+}^{(u),F}(1-z)=-\mathcal{C}_{+}^{(u),F}(z)\,.

(2.36)

Furthermore we have the block decomposition

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{+}^{(s),F}(z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}\big(a_{n,C}^{(s),F}G_{\Delta_{n,0}}(z)+b_{n,C}^{(s)}\partial_{\Delta}G_{\Delta_{n,0}}(z)\big)\,.

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{+}^{(-),F}(z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}\big(a_{n,C}^{(t),F}G_{\Delta_{n,1}}(z)+b_{n,C}^{(t)}\partial_{\Delta}G_{\Delta_{n,1}}(z)\big)\,,

where
\mathcal{C}_{+}^{(\pm),F}=\frac{1}{2}\big(\mathcal{C}_{+}^{(t),F}\pm\mathcal{C}_{+}^{(u),F}\big)

At large

\mathcal{P}_{+}^{\text{Con}}(z)\sim O(z)

Under block decomposition the Polyakov block must have one coefficient zero, so to reflect this ambiguity in the sum rules i.e. functional actions (see the last paragraph of previous subsection).

Hence we define the

anti-crossing symmetric Polyakov block as follows

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{+}(z)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{P}_{+}^{\text{exc}}(z)+k\,\mathcal{P}_{+}^{\text{Con}}(z)\,

\displaystyle=G_{\Delta}(z)-\sum_{n}\Big[\alpha^{B}_{+,n}(\Delta)G_{\Delta_{n,0}}(z)+\beta^{B}_{+,n}(\Delta)\partial_{\Delta}G_{\Delta_{n,0}}(z)\Big]\,,

(2.38)

which implies the following relation

F_{+,\Delta}(z)=\sum_{n}\Big[\alpha_{+,n}^{B}(\Delta|\Delta_{\phi})F_{+,\Delta_{n,0}}(z)+\beta_{+,n}^{B}(\Delta|\Delta_{\phi})\partial_{\Delta}F_{+,\Delta_{n,0}}(z)\Big]\,.

(2.39)

The constant

and

are actions of the bosonic + type functionals
\alpha_{+,n}^{B}
and
\beta_{+,n}^{B}
respectively. This can be easily verified.

To summarize this section we have shown the existence of two + type Polyakov blocks, ‘bosonic’ and ‘fermionic’. They are built from known Witten diagrams in AdS
2
. Through their conformal block decomposition shown in (

) and (

) they compute the actions of + type analytic functionals.

3
General
d
Polyakov blocks and dispersion relation

In this section we inititate a new formulation in general dimension to analyse crossing antisymmetric functions. This is analogous to the usual Polyakov Bootstrap for the crossing symmetric case. We propose a crossing antisymmetric basis built from Witten diagrams. This is then explicitly demonstrated by setting up a dispersion relation for Mellin amplitudes of the functions. Most of the discussions in this section is formulated in Mellin space.

Let us define a new Polyakov block
\mathcal{P}_{+,\Delta,\ell}(u,v)
in terms of which an anticrossing correlator can be expanded. We would like to have
\mathcal{P}_{+,\Delta,\ell}(u,v)
to be a completely crossing antisymmetric object. By this we mean the following:

\mathcal{P}_{+,\Delta,\ell}(u,v)=-\mathcal{P}_{+,\Delta,\ell}(v,u)=-u^{-\Delta_{\phi}}\mathcal{P}_{+,\Delta,\ell}(1/u,v/u)=-v^{-\Delta_{\phi}}\mathcal{P}_{+,\Delta,\ell}(u/v,1/v)\,.

It is possible to write such an object in terms of Witten exchange diagrams

\mathcal{P}_{+,\Delta,\ell}(u,v)=W_{\Delta,\ell}^{(s)}(u,v)-W_{\Delta,\ell}^{(t)}(u,v)-W_{\Delta,\ell}^{(u)}(u,v)+\text{contacts}\,.

It is convenient to write them in the Mellin representation as follows:

W_{\Delta,\ell}(u,v)=\int ds_{1}ds_{2}\,u^{s_{1}+\frac{2\Delta_{\phi}}{3}}v^{s_{2}-\frac{\Delta_{\phi}}{3}}\Bigg[\prod_{i=1}^{3}\Gamma^{2}\big(\frac{\Delta_{\phi}}{3}-s_{i}\big)\Bigg]M_{\Delta,\ell}(s_{1},s_{2})\,,

where

The totally crossing antisymmetric property (

The exchange Witten diagrams have the following conformal block decomposition:

\displaystyle W_{\Delta,\ell}^{(s)}(u,v)

\displaystyle=G_{\Delta,\ell}(z)+\sum_{n}\big[a_{n,\ell}^{(s)}(\Delta|\Delta_{\phi})G_{\Delta_{n,\ell},\ell}(u,v)+b_{n,\ell}^{(s)}(\Delta|\Delta_{\phi})\partial_{\Delta}G_{\Delta_{n,\ell},\ell}(u,v)\big]\,,

\displaystyle W_{\Delta,\ell}^{(-)}(u,v)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}\sum_{\ell^{\prime}\text{even}}\big[a_{n,\ell^{\prime}|\ell}^{(t)}(\Delta|\Delta_{\phi})G_{\Delta_{n,\ell^{\prime}},\ell}(u,v)+b_{n,\ell^{\prime}|\ell}^{(t)}(\Delta|\Delta_{\phi})\partial_{\Delta}G_{\Delta_{n,\ell^{\prime}},\ell^{\prime}}(u,v)\big]\,,

\displaystyle W_{\Delta,\ell}^{(+)}(u,v)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}\sum_{\ell^{\prime}\text{odd}}\big[\bar{a}_{n,\ell^{\prime}|\ell}^{(t)}(\Delta|\Delta_{\phi})G_{\Delta_{n,\ell^{\prime}},\ell^{\prime}}(u,v)+\bar{b}_{n,\ell^{\prime}|\ell}^{(t)}(\Delta|\Delta_{\phi})\partial_{\Delta}G_{\Delta_{n,\ell^{\prime}},\ell^{\prime}}(u,v)\big]\,.

(3.4)

Here
W_{\Delta,\ell}^{(\pm)}=\frac{1}{2}\big(W_{\Delta,\ell}^{(t)}\pm W_{\Delta,\ell}^{(u)}\big)
and
\Delta_{n,\ell}=2\Delta_{\phi}+2n+\ell

Then antisymmetry of (

F_{+,\Delta,\ell}(u,v)=\sum_{n}\sum_{\ell^{\prime}\text{odd}}\Big[\alpha_{n,\ell|\ell^{\prime}}F_{+,\Delta_{n,\ell^{\prime}},\ell}(u,v)+\beta_{n,\ell|\ell^{\prime}}\partial_{\Delta}F_{+,\Delta_{n,\ell^{\prime}},\ell}(u,v)\Big]\,.

(3.5)

The

\displaystyle\alpha_{n,\ell^{\prime}|\ell}(\Delta|\Delta_{\phi})

(3.6)

\displaystyle\beta_{n,\ell^{\prime}|\ell}(\Delta|\Delta_{\phi})

\displaystyle=-b_{n,\ell}^{(s)}(\Delta|\Delta_{\phi})\delta_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}+2\bar{b}_{n,\ell^{\prime}|\ell}^{(t)}(\Delta|\Delta_{\phi})+\cdots\,.

(3.7)

Here

Similar to the usual crossing symmetric case this implies a basis of expansion for

\sum_{\Delta,\ell}a_{\Delta,\ell}\alpha_{n,\ell|\ell^{\prime}}(\Delta|\Delta_{\phi})=\sum_{\Delta,\ell}a_{\Delta,\ell}\beta_{n,\ell|\ell^{\prime}}(\Delta|\Delta_{\phi})=0\,.

3.1
Contact diagrams

We now address the missing piece of this formulation: the contact diagrams.
For a contact diagram

M_{\mathcal{C},+}(s_{1},s_{2})=(s_{1}-s_{2})(s_{2}-s_{3})(s_{3}-s_{1})M_{\mathcal{C},-}(s_{1},s_{2})\,.

The superscripts ‘

Note that

We may now define the Polyakov block as follows:

\mathcal{P}_{+,\Delta,\ell}(u,v)=W_{\Delta,\ell}^{(s)}(u,v)-W_{\Delta,\ell}^{(t)}(u,v)-W_{\Delta,\ell}^{(u)}(u,v)+\mathcal{C}_{+}(u,v)(u,v)\,.

The contact diagram

\mathcal{C}_{+}(u,v)=\sum_{n}\sum_{\ell^{\prime}\text{odd}}\big[a_{n,\ell^{\prime}|\mathcal{C}}(\Delta_{\phi})G_{\Delta_{n,\ell^{\prime}}}(u,v)+b_{n,\ell^{\prime}|\mathcal{C}}(\Delta_{\phi})\partial_{\Delta}G_{\Delta_{n,\ell^{\prime}}}(u,v)\big]\,.

3.2
Dispersion relation

We would like to show that an expansion of an anticrossing correlator in
\mathcal{P}_{+,\Delta,\ell}
is indeed valid, thereby
fixing the coefficients

[

,
15

. The idea in those papers was to write down a dispersion relation for the correlator, which is manifestly crossing symmetric. This leads to introduction of new spurious poles in the Mellin amplitude, that are called ‘nonlocal’ terms. Subtracting out these terms one is left with crossing symmetric sums of exchange Witten diagrams and polynomial terms. The latter fixes the required contact term ambiguities. We review the details briefly in Appendix
B
.

Consider the Mellin amplitude
\mathcal{M}_{+}(s_{1},s_{2})
of a crossing antisymmetric function. We will drop the ‘
+

5

\mathcal{M}(s_{1},s_{2})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle s_{1}\to\infty}}{{\sim}}O(s_{1}^{2-\epsilon})\,\ \text{with}\ \epsilon>0\,,\text{ $s_{2}$ fixed}\,.

(3.13)

We will write down a twice subtracted dispersion relation for this in a manifestly crossing antisymmetric way, following a strategy similar to

16

s_{i}=a-\frac{a(z-z_{i})^{3}}{z^{3}-1}\,,

where

a=\frac{s_{1}s_{2}s_{3}}{s_{1}s_{2}+s_{2}s_{3}+s_{3}s_{1}}=\frac{y}{x}\,.

(3.15)

The variable

is crossing symmetric.
6

6

The reader should not confuse this

The ‘physical cuts’ (analogous to QFT scattering amplitudes) in each

\mathcal{M}(s_{1},s_{2})=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\tau^{(0)}}^{\infty}\frac{ds_{1}^{\prime}}{s_{1}^{\prime}}\mathcal{A}(s_{1}^{\prime};s_{2}^{+}(s_{1}^{\prime},a))H(s_{1}^{\prime};s_{1},s_{2},s_{3})\,.

(3.17)

Finally in the absorptive part we also have:

\displaystyle s_{2}^{+}(s,a)

\displaystyle=-\frac{s}{2}\Big[{1-\sqrt{\frac{s+3a}{s-a}}}\Big]\,.

(3.18)

\mathcal{M}(s_{1},s_{2})=w\sum_{p,q=0}^{\infty}\mathcal{M}_{p,q}x^{p+q}a^{q}\,,

The counterparts of these conditions in the crossing symmetric case were shown in

Now let us see how the dispersion relation reproduces the Polyakov block

Then we get from (

\mathcal{M}(s_{1},s_{2})=\sum_{\Delta,\ell,k}c_{\Delta,\ell}^{(k)}\mathcal{M}_{\Delta,\ell,k}(s_{1},s_{2})\,,

(3.23)

\mathcal{M}_{\Delta,\ell,k}^{(0)}(s_{1},s_{2})=\frac{P_{\Delta,\ell}(s_{1},s_{2})}{s_{1}-\tau_{k}}-\frac{P_{\Delta,\ell}(s_{2},s_{1})}{s_{2}-\tau_{k}}-\frac{P_{\Delta,\ell}(s_{3},s_{2})}{s_{3}-\tau_{k}}\,,

(i\neq j)

We now expand

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{\Delta,\ell,k}(s_{1},s_{2})=

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{\Delta,\ell,k}^{(0)}(s_{1},s_{2})+w\sum_{p,q=0}^{2p+3q\leq L}C_{p,q}^{(\ell)}x^{p+q}a^{q}

\displaystyle+w\sum_{p<0}\widetilde{C}^{(\ell)}_{p,q}x^{p+q}a^{q}\,.

For

3.3

Let us discuss how to bootstrap the Mellin amplitude to obtain the OPE data. For this let us reinstate the subscripts (

)

In terms of

\mathcal{G}_{\pm}(u,v)=\int ds_{1}ds_{2}\,u^{s_{1}+\frac{2\Delta_{\phi}}{3}}v^{s_{2}-\frac{\Delta_{\phi}}{3}}\Bigg[\prod_{i=1}^{3}\Gamma^{2}\big(\frac{\Delta_{\phi}}{3}-s_{i}\big)\Bigg]\mathcal{M}_{\pm}(s_{1},s_{2})\,,

Since the only poles in

\mathfrak{F}_{\pm,p}(s_{2})=0

) and summing over

\mathfrak{F}^{(r)}_{\pm,p}(s_{2})=\sum_{\Delta,\ell}a_{\Delta,\ell}\,\mathfrak{F}^{(r)}_{\pm,p,\Delta,\ell}(s_{2})

We may Taylor expand this around

to get a set of infinite sum rules as follows:
8
8

8

For the
-

\displaystyle\Omega_{\pm,p_{1},p_{2},p_{3}}(s_{2})=

\displaystyle-\Big[\frac{\mathfrak{F}_{\pm,p_{1}}}{(p_{1}-p_{2})(p_{1}+p_{3}+s_{2}+\frac{\Delta_{\phi}}{3})}+(p_{1}\leftrightarrow p_{2})\Big]-\frac{\mathfrak{F}_{\pm,p_{3}}}{(p_{1}+p_{3}+s_{2}+\frac{\Delta_{\phi}}{3})(p_{2}+p_{3}+s_{2}+\frac{\Delta_{\phi}}{3})}\,

We point out that the description of

Product Functionals in even

In this subsection we only focus on

(4.1)

The crossing vector is given by:

F^{d=2}_{-,\Delta,\ell}(z,\bar{z}|\Delta_{\phi})=\frac{1}{4}\big[F_{-,\frac{\tau}{2}}(z|\text{\tiny$\frac{\Delta_{\phi}}{2}$})F_{+,\frac{\rho}{2}}(\bar{z}|\text{\tiny$\frac{\Delta_{\phi}}{2}$})+F_{-,\frac{\rho}{2}}(z|\text{\tiny$\frac{\Delta_{\phi}}{2}$})F_{+,\frac{\tau}{2}}(\bar{z}|\text{\tiny$\frac{\Delta_{\phi}}{2}$})+(z\leftrightarrow\bar{z})\big]\,.

Notice that the
z
and
\bar{z}
dependencies have separated out. If we ensure that the

F_{-}
, and
\bar{z}
through
F_{+}
then we can write a functional for

\omega_{-}
and
\omega_{+}
. Therefore we choose the 2d functionals as follows:

\omega^{(1)}_{-}\otimes\omega^{(2)}_{+}(\Delta,\ell)=2\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{dzd{\bar{z}}}{\pi^{2}}h^{(1)}_{-}(z)h^{(2)}_{+}(\bar{z})\big[\mathcal{I}_{z}\mathcal{I}_{\bar{z}}F_{-,\Delta,\ell}(z,\bar{z})+\mathcal{I}_{z}\mathcal{I}_{\bar{z}}F_{-,\Delta,\ell}(z,1-\bar{z})\big]\,.

The structure is symmetrized in
\bar{z}\to 1-\bar{z}
to have the above mentioned feature. All kernels

are chosen from 1d functional kernels such that we have the following four types of functionals (suppressing

superscripts as any bosonic/fermionic functional should work):

\displaystyle\omega^{(1)}_{-}\omega^{(2)}_{+}\in\Big\{\alpha_{-,n}\alpha_{+,m}\,,\ \alpha_{-,n}\beta_{+,m}\,,\ \beta_{-,n}\alpha_{+,m}\,,\ \beta_{-,n}\beta_{+,m}\Big\}\,.

We have simplified the notation
\omega^{(1)}_{-}\omega^{(2)}_{+}\equiv\omega^{(1)}_{-}\otimes\omega^{(2)}_{+}
.
Each functional action is given by

\omega^{(1)}_{-}\omega^{(2)}_{+}(\Delta,\ell)=\frac{1}{2}\big[\omega^{(1)}_{-}(\tau|\Delta_{\phi}|2)\omega^{(2)}_{+}(\rho|\Delta_{\phi}|2)+\omega^{(1)}_{-}(\rho|\Delta_{\phi}|2)\omega^{(2)}_{+}(\tau|\Delta_{\phi}|2)\big]\,.

(4.5)

Here we defined for convenience

\omega_{\pm}(\Delta|\Delta_{\phi}|2)=\omega_{\pm}(\frac{\Delta}{2}|\frac{\Delta_{\phi}}{2})
. Using the results from section

these are entirely determined from conformal block decomposition of Witten diagrams.
The

) ensures the positive behavior of product functional actions. This was used in

]
with

Let us also explore the case of crossing antisymmetric vector
F^{d=2}_{+,\Delta,\ell}(z,\bar{z})
which can be written as

F^{d=2}_{+,\Delta,\ell}(z,\bar{z}|\Delta_{\phi})=\frac{1}{4}\big[F_{-,\frac{\tau}{2}}(z|\text{\tiny$\frac{\Delta_{\phi}}{2}$})F_{-,\frac{\rho}{2}}(\bar{z}|\text{\tiny$\frac{\Delta_{\phi}}{2}$})+F_{+,\frac{\rho}{2}}(z|\text{\tiny$\frac{\Delta_{\phi}}{2}$})F_{+,\frac{\tau}{2}}(\bar{z}|\text{\tiny$\frac{\Delta_{\phi}}{2}$})+(z\leftrightarrow\bar{z})\big]\,.\,.

(4.6)

As before we have to symmetrize/antisymmetrize in
z\to 1-z
to have the necessary variable separation. So we choose the functionals as follows:

\omega^{(1)}_{\pm}\omega^{(2)}_{\pm}(\Delta,\ell)=2\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{dzd{\bar{z}}}{\pi^{2}}h^{(1)}_{\pm}(z)h^{(2)}_{\pm}(\bar{z})\big[\mathcal{I}_{z}\mathcal{I}_{\bar{z}}F_{+,\Delta,\ell}(z,\bar{z})\pm\mathcal{I}_{z}\mathcal{I}_{\bar{z}}F_{+,\Delta,\ell}(z,1-\bar{z})\big]\,.

(4.7)

The crossing antisymmetric product functionals and their respective functional actions are as follows

\displaystyle\omega^{(1)}_{\pm}\omega^{(2)}_{\pm}\in\Big\{\alpha_{\pm,n}\alpha_{\pm,m}\,,\ \alpha_{\pm,n}\beta_{\pm,m}\,,\ \beta_{\pm,n}\beta_{\pm,m}\Big\}\,.

(4.8)

\displaystyle\omega^{(1)}_{\pm}\omega^{(2)}_{\pm}(\Delta,\ell)=\omega^{(1)}_{\pm}(\tau|\Delta_{\phi}|2)\omega^{(2)}_{\pm}(\rho|\Delta_{\phi}|2)+\omega^{(2)}_{\pm}(\tau|\Delta_{\phi}|2)\omega^{(1)}_{\pm}(\rho|\Delta_{\phi}|2)\,.

(4.9)

4.2
Global symmetry - Simple functionals

4.2.1
d=1

An important application for crossing antisymmetric functionals is in the problem of global symmetries.
Let us recall from section
1
that if we write the correlator of charged scalar fields
\phi_{i}
in terms of the components
\mathcal{G}^{\mathfrak{a}}
for each irrep
\mathfrak{a}
appearing in the OPE, then under crossing we get the relation:

\mathcal{G}^{\mathfrak{a}}(z)=C^{\mathfrak{a}\mathfrak{b}}\mathcal{G}^{\mathfrak{b}}(1-z)\,,

(4.10)

where
C^{\mathfrak{b}\mathfrak{a}}
is the crossing matrix. We can define the projector matrices
P^{\mathfrak{b}\mathfrak{a}}_{\pm}=\frac{1}{2}(\delta^{\mathfrak{b}\mathfrak{a}}+C^{\mathfrak{b}\mathfrak{a}})
in terms of which one may write the crossing vectors

\mathcal{F}^{\mathfrak{b}|\mathfrak{a}}(z)=P^{\mathfrak{b}\mathfrak{a}}_{+}F_{-,\Delta}(z)+P^{\mathfrak{b}\mathfrak{a}}_{-}F_{+,\Delta}(z)\,.

(4.11)

With the above crossing vectors one can write the crossing equation (
4.10
) as follows:

\sum_{\mathfrak{b}}\sum_{\Delta}a_{\Delta}^{\mathfrak{b}}\mathcal{F}^{\mathfrak{b}|\mathfrak{a}}(z)=0\,.

(4.12)

In
[
14
]
a set of global symmetry functionals, called ‘simple functionals’ were introduced for the above problem. They have the following structure:

\omega^{\mathfrak{b}|\mathfrak{a}}=P^{\mathfrak{b}\mathfrak{a}}_{+}\omega_{-}+P^{\mathfrak{b}\mathfrak{a}}_{-}\omega_{+}\,.

(4.13)

Here
\omega_{\pm}\in\{\alpha_{\pm,n}^{B,F},\beta_{\pm,n}^{B,F}\}
i.e. the 1d functionals of uncharged case. In the above notation for
\omega^{\mathfrak{b}|\mathfrak{a}}
the superscript
\mathfrak{b}
denotes a label and
|\mathfrak{a}
a component.
The action of these functionals on the crossing vectors
\mathcal{F}^{\mathfrak{c}|\mathfrak{a}}
(
4.11
) is given by

\omega^{\mathfrak{b}}(\mathfrak{c},\Delta)=P^{\mathfrak{b}\mathfrak{c}}_{+}\omega_{-}(\Delta)+P^{\mathfrak{b}\mathfrak{c}}_{-}\omega_{+}(\Delta)\,.

(4.14)

The action of these functionals on crossing symmetry (
4.12
) are entirely determined by our knowledge of the
\omega_{\pm}(\Delta)
, which are given by Witten diagram decomposition coefficients (section
2.3
). For numerical applications simple functionals are useful as they retain the positivity properties of the uncharged functionals. For instance any bound obtained using 1d functionals (say
\omega_{-}
) can be easily generalized to global symmetries if one can write a linear combination of
\omega^{\mathfrak{b}}
whose action is proportional to only
\omega_{-}
with positive coefficients.

4.2.2
d=2
with product functionals

The idea of simple functionals is not restricted to 1d. Indeed for any
d
one can consider (
4.13
) choosing
\omega_{\pm}
as appropriate in that dimension. In 2d we may choose them to be the product functionals

\omega_{-}\to\omega_{-}^{(1)}\omega_{+}^{(2)}\,,\ \ \omega_{+}\to\omega_{\pm}^{(3)}\omega_{\pm}^{(4)}\,.

(4.15)

The action of 2d simple product functionals is given by

\omega^{\mathfrak{b}}(\mathfrak{c},\Delta,\ell)=P_{+}^{\mathfrak{b}\mathfrak{c}}\big[\omega_{-}^{(1)}\omega_{+}^{(2)}(\Delta,\ell)\big]+P_{-}^{\mathfrak{b}\mathfrak{c}}\big[\omega_{\pm}^{(3)}\omega_{\pm}^{(4)}(\Delta,\ell)\big]\,.

(4.16)

This allows us to translate any bound found with product functionals in CFTs without global symmetry to bounds for global symmetry problems. A simple demonstration is done in Appendix
D.3
where we extend an analytic bound for OPE density for 2d uncharged CFTs to the charged case. As mentioned in section
4.1
the positivity properties of product functionals can be useful to obtain sharp numerical bounds for global symmetry problems.

Of course the
d=2
simple product functionals case can easily be generalized to
d=4
and
6
(see Appendix
D
).

4.2.3
General
d

To obtain simple functionals for general dimensions we may use the Polyakov condition sum rules (
3.3
). In this case we may use
\omega_{\pm}\to\mathfrak{F}_{\pm,p},\widetilde{\mathfrak{F}}_{\pm,p}
so that simple functional actions are given by

\omega^{\mathfrak{b}}({\mathfrak{c},\Delta,\ell})\in\big\{P_{+}^{\mathfrak{b}\mathfrak{c}}\mathfrak{F}^{(r)}_{-,p,\Delta,\ell}+P_{-}^{\mathfrak{b}\mathfrak{c}}\mathfrak{F}^{(r)}_{+,p,\Delta,\ell};r\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\big\}\,,

(4.17)

and similarly with
\widetilde{\mathfrak{F}}
.
The quantities
\mathfrak{F}^{(r)}_{-,p,\Delta,\ell}
were defined in (
3.29
). It should be noted that these simple functionals are a variation of those discussed in
[
14
]
. As we use only bosonic Witten diagrams, in the language of 1d functionals it is like using ‘bosonic functionals’ for the
-
case and ‘fermionic functionals’ for the
+
case. They are not dual to any interesting CFT e.g. GFF, although they are perfectly good functionals for global symmetry problems.

The idea of simple functionals can be extended to QFTs with global symmetries. Here the
\pm
functional actions should be replaced with the locality constraints of the
\mathcal{M}_{\pm}(s_{1},s_{2})
. We leave this for future work.

5
Conclusion

In this paper we show that a crossing antisymmetric correlator can be expanded in a manifestly crossing antisymmetric basis. Similar to Polyakov’s original proposal for crossing symmetric correlators, this basis is mapped to AdS Witten diagrams. We explore its relation to analytic functionals, and set up a dispersion relation that proves it.

We call the building blocks of the basis the
+
type Polyakov blocks. In 1d the simplest of these, the fermionic
+
type Polyakov blocks, are constructed from a manifestly crossing antisymmetric sum of AdS
2
spin 1 exchange bosonic Witten diagrams. Their conformal block decomposition coefficients compute 1d fermionic
+
type functional actions. The
+
type functionals allow a more relaxed Regge behavior compared to the
-
type. This is used to obtain the bosonic Polyakov block by suitably combining spin 1 fermionic Witten diagrams and a manifestly anticrossing function which we call a ‘contact diagram’. Once again under OPE decomposition this exactly reproduces the bosonic
+
functional action.

In general dimensions the
+
type Polyakov blocks are built from odd spin Witten exchange diagrams and crossing antisymmetric contact diagrams. We formulate this in Mellin space. To obtain the exact structure of the blocks we introduce a crossing antisymmetric dispersion relation for a general crossing antisymmetric Mellin amplitude. This utilises a change of variables that results in nonlocal singularities. Imposing the locality constraints i.e. setting these nonlocal terms to zero each odd spin sector of the dispersion relation reproduces a sum of odd spin exchange Witten diagrams and finite number of contact diagrams. Finally we give a set of sum rules corresponding to the ‘Polyakov conditions’ i.e. the residue at spurious double trace poles in Mellin variables should be zero. Using the dispersion relation the sum rules can be implemented for any spin. We have numerical checked with a simple example how locality conditions work, and how the Polyakov conditions are satisfied for the antisymmetric sector of a 2d WZW model, elaborately discussed in the Appendix.

Our results imply a number of simplifications for bootstrapping 4-point functions where crossing antisymmetry becomes important. The most significant ones being product functionals in even
d
and various simple functionals for global symmetry problems in general
d
. There are many other interesting directions that one can pursue:

1.

Constraints on QFTs
: A version of crossing symmetric dispersion relation has been used in QFT context
[
15
]
to derive constraints on effective field theories (equivalent to
[
27
,
28
]
) and scattering cross sections. They key element in that analysis was the locality constraints. These are similar to our conditions (
3.20
) which are independent and should lead to new constraints on scattering amplitudes when they have a crossing antisymmetric component e.g. when global symmetries are present.

2.

Geometric Function Theory
: An interesting new direction in constraining scattering amplitudes using ideas of Geometric Function theory (GFT) has been initiated in
[
30
,
31
]
. The crossing symmetric dispersion relation allows a formulation in terms of ‘typically real functions’ where GFT constraints (e.g. Bieberbach-Rogonski inequalities) can be applied. In
[
32
]
this approach was extended to
O(N)
theories building on the work of
[
33
]
. It will be interesting to see how the crossing antisymmetric dispersion relation can be tied into the GFT framework and if the associated constraints/sum rules are connected to
[
33
,
32
]
or independent ones.

3.

Correlator bounds/Master functionals
: An interesting problem of obtaining bounds on CFT correlators was addressed in
[
34
,
14
]
. To prove e.g. the minimization of 1d correlators one can construct a ‘master functional’ which packages analytic functionals in a certain way. Interestingly the master functional action is equivalent to a Polyakov block. It would be interesting to see how a crossing (anti)symmetric Polyakov block in higher dimensions is encoded in bounds (e.g. the numerical bounds obtained in
[
35
]
in 3d) on CFT correlators.

4.

Fermionic/Multiple correlator bootstrap
: Crossing antisymmetry also appears in interesting problems like bootstrapping correlators of fermions in higher
d
or a set of correlators with unequal scalars
[
36
,
37
,
38
]
. Our results are hence applicable to these cases (see e.g.
[
39
]
). However when fermions or unequal scalars are involved the conformal blocks are different, and also we may lose full crossing (anti)symmetry. So a modification of analytic functionals or Polyakov Bootstrap may be necessary.

5.

Nonperturbative Mellin amplitude/dispersive sum rules
: As shown in
[
16
]
the Polyakov conditions from crossing symmetric dispersion relation are equivalent to the analogous constraints proposed in
[
40

29

for nonperturbative Mellin amplitudes. These conditions can also be mapped to the “dispersive sum rules” from position space dispersion relation or general

analytic functionals
[
8

. It would be interesting to see if there is a similar picture for anticrossing correlators and if the associated sum rules are related to the crossing antisymmetric dispersion relation.

D.3
Bounds with product functionals

In this subsection we use the simple functionals built with the 2d product functionals (see section
4.2.2
) to extend the bounds on OPE density obtained in
d=2

[
20
]
to a CFT with general global symmetry.
For the uncharged case the bound was obtained by choosing the following combination of 1d functionals:

\tilde{\alpha}_{\pm,m}=\alpha_{\pm,m}+c_{\pm}\beta_{\pm,m},\ \ \text{$c_{\pm}$ chosen such that}\ \ f_{\alpha_{\pm,m}}\sim O(z^{\frac{1}{2}(9\pm 1)})\,.

(D.14)

These modified functionals have the following positivity and asymptotic properties respectively:

\displaystyle\tilde{\alpha}_{+,m}(\tau)\geq 0,\ \ \forall\ \tau\geq 0\,,\ \ \ \ \tilde{\alpha}_{-,m}(\tau)\geq 0,\ \ \forall\ \tau\geq\tau_{0}(\Delta_{\phi})\,,

(D.15)

\displaystyle\tilde{\alpha}_{\pm,m}(0)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle m\to\infty}}{{=}}\pm a_{h_{m}}^{\text{free}}\,,

(D.16)

where

a_{h}^{\text{free}}=\frac{2\Gamma(\frac{h}{2})^{2}}{\Gamma(h-1)\Gamma(\Delta_{\phi})^{2}}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{h+2\Delta_{\phi}-2}{2})}{\Gamma(\frac{h+2\Delta_{\phi}+2}{2})}\,,\ \ \ \ h_{m}=2+2\Delta_{\phi}+4m\,.

(D.17)

For
h,h_{m}\gg 1
with
h-h_{m}
fixed we have

\alpha_{\pm,m}(h)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle h,h_{m}\to\infty}}{{=}}\left(\frac{a_{h_{m}}^{\text{free}}}{a_{h}^{\text{free}}}\right)\left(\frac{4}{\pi}\frac{\sin(\frac{\pi}{4}(h-h_{m}))}{h-h_{m}}\right)^{2}\,.

(D.18)

Now the action of the product functional
\tilde{\alpha}_{-,m}\tilde{\alpha}_{+,n}
on the bootstrap equation implies the following bound:

\sum_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle|\Delta-\Delta^{\text{prod}}_{m,\ell}|\leq 2}}{{\ell=2(m-n)}}}(1+\delta_{\ell,0})a_{\Delta,\ell}\tilde{\alpha}_{-,m}(\tau)\tilde{\alpha}_{+,n}(\rho)\leq-\sum_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 0\leq\tau\leq\tau_{0}}}{{\ell=0,2,\cdots}}}a_{\Delta,\ell}\big[\tilde{\alpha}_{-,m}(\tau)\tilde{\alpha}_{+,n}(\rho)+\tilde{\alpha}_{+,n}(\tau)\tilde{\alpha}_{-,m}(\rho)\big]\,.

(D.19)

Here
\Delta_{m,\ell}^{\text{prod}}=2+2\Delta_{\phi}+4m+\ell
.
Using the above properties of
\tilde{\alpha}_{\pm,m}
we get the following upper bound on OPE density:

\lim\limits_{m,n\to\infty}\sum_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle|\Delta-\Delta^{\text{prod}}_{m,\ell}|\leq 2}}{{\ell=2(m-n)}}}\left(\frac{a_{\Delta,\ell}}{a_{\Delta,\ell}^{\text{prod}}}\right)\left(\frac{4}{\pi}\frac{\sin(\frac{\pi}{4}(\Delta-\Delta_{m,\ell}))}{\Delta-\Delta_{m,\ell}}\right)^{4}\leq 1\,,

(D.20)

where
a_{\Delta,\ell}^{\text{prod}}=\frac{2}{1+\delta_{\ell,0}}a_{\rho}^{\text{free}}a_{\tau}^{\text{free}}
.

Recall from section
4.2
that the global symmetry problem is formulated in terms of a crossing matrix
C^{\mathfrak{ab}}
. It has the eigenvalues
+1
with
r_{+}
degeneracy and
-1
with
r_{-}
degeneracy.
It was shown in
[
14
]
that there can be
r_{+}-r_{-}
eigenvectors with
+1

S

E_{+}^{\mathfrak{a}}=\begin{cases}\frac{2+d_{\mathfrak{r}}}{2},&\mathfrak{a}=\textbf{S}\\
\sqrt{d_{\mathfrak{r}}},&\eta^{\mathfrak{a}}=1\\
0,&\eta^{\mathfrak{a}}=-1\end{cases}\,,\ \ \tilde{E}_{+}^{\mathfrak{a}}=\begin{cases}\frac{d_{\mathfrak{r}}}{2},&\mathfrak{a}=\textbf{S}\\
\sqrt{d_{\mathfrak{r}}},&\eta^{\mathfrak{a}}=1\\
0,&\eta^{\mathfrak{a}}=-1\end{cases}\,,

(D.21)

which are also
+1
eigenvectors (and
\eta

, see (
1.1

In fact the other linearly independent

Now we note that for any eigenvector
e_{\pm}
we must have
\sum_{\mathfrak{b}}e_{\pm}^{\mathfrak{b}}P_{\mp}^{\mathfrak{a}\mathfrak{b}}=0
.
Choosing a non-negative eigenvectors as above (lets say

), we can consider the combination of simple functionals
E_{+,s}^{\mathfrak{b}}\omega^{\mathfrak{b}|\mathfrak{c}}
whose action would be proportional to

\omega_{-}^{(1)}\omega_{-}^{(2)}=\tilde{\alpha}_{-,m}\tilde{\alpha}_{+,n}
its action translates the OPE denisity upper bound (

\lim\limits_{m,n\to\infty}\sum_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle|\Delta-\Delta^{\text{prod}}_{m,\ell}|\leq 2}}{{|\ell-2(m-n)|\leq 1}}}\left(\frac{\sum_{\mathfrak{a}}E^{\mathfrak{a}}_{+,s}a^{\mathfrak{a}}_{\Delta,\ell}}{a_{\Delta,\ell}^{\text{prod}}}\right)\left(\frac{4}{\pi}\frac{\sin(\frac{\pi}{4}(\Delta-\Delta_{m,\ell}))}{\Delta-\Delta_{m,\ell}}\right)^{4}\leq\sum_{\mathfrak{b}}E^{\mathfrak{b}}_{+,s}a_{0}^{\mathfrak{b}}\,.

Here

\Delta=0,\ell=0

\mathfrak{a}
(with
a^{\textbf{S}}_{0}=1
). Since all components

are positive this implies the following individual bound:

\lim\limits_{m,n\to\infty}\sum_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle|\Delta-\Delta^{\text{prod}}_{m,\ell}|\leq 2}}{{|\ell-2(m-n)|\leq 1}}}\left(\frac{E^{\mathfrak{a}}_{+,s}a^{\mathfrak{a}}_{\Delta,\ell}}{a_{\Delta,\ell}^{\text{prod}}}\right)\left(\frac{4}{\pi}\frac{\sin(\frac{\pi}{4}(\Delta-\Delta_{m,\ell}))}{\Delta-\Delta_{m,\ell}}\right)^{4}\leq\frac{1}{E_{+,s}^{\mathfrak{a}}}\sum_{\mathfrak{b}}E^{\mathfrak{b}}_{+,s}a_{0}^{\mathfrak{b}}\,.

(D.23)
