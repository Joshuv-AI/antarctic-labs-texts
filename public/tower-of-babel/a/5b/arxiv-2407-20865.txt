Auxiliary-Free Replica Shadows: Efficient Estimation of Multiple Nonlinear Quantum Properties
Qing Liu, Zihao Li, Xiao Yuan, Huangjun Zhu, You Zhou

Abstract
Efficient estimation of nonlinear properties is a significant yet challenging task from quantum information processing to many-body physics. Current methodologies often suffer from an exponential sampling cost or require auxiliary qubits and deep quantum circuits. To address these limitations, we propose an efficient auxiliary-free replica shadow (AFRS) framework, which leverages the power of the joint entangling operation on a few input replicas while integrating the mindset of shadow estimation. We rigorously prove that AFRS can offer exponential improvements in estimation accuracy compared with the conventional shadow method, and facilitate the simultaneous estimation of various nonlinear properties, unlike the destructive swap test. Additionally, we introduce an advanced local-AFRS variant tailored to estimating local observables with constant-depth quantum circuits, significantly simplifying the experimental implementation. Our work paves the way for efficient and practical estimation of nonlinear properties on near-term quantum devices.

Original shadow estimation—
We begin by briefly reviewing the original shadow (OS) estimation protocol
[
22
]
.
For a quantum state
\rho
on a
d
-dimensional Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{d}
, OS proceeds by first applying a random unitary
V
on
\rho
, which is sampled from a suitable ensemble
\mathcal{E}
.
Typical choices for
\mathcal{E}
include the
n
-qubit Cliﬀord group
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{Cl}}
and the
n
-fold tensor product of the single-qubit Clifford group
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{LCl}}

[
22
,
38
,
39
,
40
]
.
After applying the unitary
V
, the evolved state
V(\rho)\equiv V\rho V^{\dagger}
is then measured in the computational basis
\left\{\ket{\mb{b}}\right\}_{\mathbf{b}\in[d]}
with
[d]:=\{1,2,\ldots,d\}
, generating outcome
\mathbf{b}
with probability
\Pr(\mb{b}|V)=\bra{\mb{b}}V(\rho)\ket{\mb{b}}
.
From each measurement, a classical snapshot of the state is constructed through the estimator
\hat{\rho}=\mathcal{M}^{-1}(V^{{\dagger}}\outerproduct{\mb{b}}{\mb{b}}V)
, where
\mathcal{M}^{-1}
is the inverse of the measurement channel, whose specific form depends on
\mathcal{E}
.
This estimator equals the original state
\rho
in expectation, i.e.,
\mathbb{E}[\hat{\rho}]=\rho
.
Repeating such “measurement + post-process” procedure
M
times generates a shadow set
\{\hat{\rho}_{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{M}
, which enables efficient estimation of observable expectation value through
\tr(O\hat{\rho})
, particularly for local observables and fidelities.
The estimation variance is upper bounded by the squared shadow norm, denoted by
\|O\|^{2}_{\mathrm{sh},\mathcal{E}}
.
Such norm is closely related to the Hilbert-Schmidt norm for ensemble
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{Cl}}
, while scales exponentially in the locality of target observables for ensemble
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{LCl}}

[
22
]
.

For a challenging nonlinear function like
\tr(O\rho^t)
, one can compute
\tr(O\hat{\rho}_1\hat{\rho}_2\cdots\hat{\rho}_t )
using
t
distinct snapshots from the shadow set.
However, the variance of such a nonlinear estimator grows exponentially with the system size
n

[
22
,
6
]
, making the sampling cost prohibitive even for medium-scale systems.

Framework of AFRS—
To address the above limitation of OS, we propose the framework of the AFRS protocol, which enables efficient estimation of nonlinear quantum properties through two coordinated stages. As illustrated in

1

The AFRS protocol centers on generating effective measurement statistics that behave as if measuring
\rho^{t}
directly, despite
\rho^{t}
not being a physical quantum state. This is achieved through careful design of the entangling operation
\mathcal{R}
, which correlates information across
t
replicas of
\rho
. The measurement statistics are generated according to the corresponding
pseudo
‘conditional probability’

\Pr(\mb{b}|V)=\bra{\mb{b}}V(\rho^{t})\ket{\mb{b}}=\tr\left[S_{t}Q_{\mathbf{b}}V(\rho)^{\otimes t}\right],

(1)

where
S_{t}
is the clockwise shift operation that cyclically permutes the
t
replicas, acting as
S_{t}\ket{\mb{x}_1 \mb{x}_2\cdots\mb{x}_t}=\ket{\mb{x}_2 \mb{x}_3\cdots\mb{x}_1}
; and
Q_{\mathbf{b}}=t^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{t}\outerproduct{\mb{b}}{\mb{b}}_{i}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{d}^{\otimes(t-1)}
is a symmetric observable that is diagonal in the computational basis.
It is not hard to check that
S_{t}
and
Q_{\mathbf{b}}
commute and thus share a common eigenbasis
\{\ket{\psi_\mb{x}}\}
, leading to a crucial decomposition:

Observation 1
.

The pseudo ‘conditional probability’ in
Eq.

1
can be written as

\Pr(\mb{b}|V)=\sum_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}\in[d]^{t}}f(\mathbf{x})\Pr(\mb{b}|\mb{x})\Pr(\mb{x}|V),

(2)

where
f(\mathbf{x}):=\bra{\psi_\mb{x}}S_{t}\ket{\psi_\mb{x}}
is a function depending on the measurement result
\mathbf{x}
, while
\Pr(\mb{b}|\mb{x}):=\bra{\psi_\mb{x}}Q_{\mathbf{b}}\ket{\psi_\mb{x}}
and
\Pr(\mb{x}|V):=\bra{\psi_\mb{x}}V(\rho)^{\otimes t}\ket{\psi_\mb{x}}
represent classical and quantum
legal
conditional probabilities, respectively.

It is straightforward to verify that
\Pr(\mb{b}|\mb{x})\geq 0
and
\sum_{\mathbf{b}}\Pr(\mb{b}|\mb{x})=1
. We refer to this conditional probability as the
mapping strategy
, denoted as
\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{x}\to\mathbf{b}}:=\Pr(\mb{b}|\mb{x})
, since it is generated by classical random assignments.
Meanwhile, the second probability
\Pr(\mb{x}|V)
can be generated in a quantum manner, i.e., by measuring
V(\rho)^{\otimes t}
in the basis
\{\ket{\psi_\mb{x}}\}
.
This measurement can thus be physically implemented by applying a suitable unitary transformation
\mathcal{R}
on
\mathcal{H}_{d}^{\otimes t}
, followed by the computational basis measurement, where
\mathcal{R}
satisfies
\mathcal{R}\ket{\psi_{\mb{x}}}=\ket{\mb{x}}
for all
\mathbf{x}
. Consequently, the overall quantum circuit shown in
Fig.

1
is obtained.
Further elaboration and explicit details are provided in END MATTER.

Theorem 1
.

For a single implementation of the circuit in
Fig.

1
(i.e., single-shot,
M=1
), the estimator

\widehat{\rho^{t}}:=\mathrm{Re}(f(\mathbf{x}))\cdot\mathcal{M}^{-1}\left(V^{{\dagger}}\outerproduct{\mb{b}}{\mb{b}}V\right)

(3)

reproduces
\rho^{t}
in expectation, i.e.,
\mathbb{E}_{\{V,\mathbf{x},\mathbf{b}\}}\widehat{\rho^{t}}=\rho^{t}
. Here,

\mathbf{x}\in[d]^{t}
is the actual measurement outcome of the quantum circuit; function
f(\mathbf{x})
and mapping result
\mathbf{b}
derive from
Observation

1
;
the inverse channel
\mathcal{M}^{-1}
depends only on the system dimension and the chosen unitary ensemble
\mathcal{E}
.

The nonlinear property
\tr(O\rho^t)
can be estimated by
\widehat{o_{t}}={\rm tr}(O\widehat{\rho^{t}})
,
whose variance satisfies

\mathrm{Var}(\widehat{o_{t}})\leq\left\|O_{0}\right\|_{\mathrm{sh},\mathcal{E}}^{2}+\|O\|_{\infty}^{2},

(4)

where
O_{0}=O-\operatorname{tr}(O)\mathbb{I}_{d}/d
is the traceless part of
O
, and
\|\cdot\|_{\mathrm{sh},\mathcal{E}}
is the shadow norm depending on
\mathcal{E}

[
22
]
.

Theorem

1
indicates that AFRS achieves exponential improvements in sampling efficiency for nonlinear estimation compared with OS.
In particular, the variance of estimating
\tr(O\rho^t)
with AFRS is almost the same as the variance of estimating linear property
\tr(O\rho)
with OS, both with the squared shadow norm
\|O_{0}\|^{2}_{\mathrm{sh},\mathcal{E}}
serving as the dominant term.
Here, we ignore the second term
\|O\|_{\infty}^{2}
in
Eq.

4
, which is usually a constant.
By comparison, when estimating
\tr(O\rho^t)
using OS, one has to compute
\text{tr}\big(O_{t}\bigotimes_{j=1}^{t}\hat{\rho}_{j}\big)

with
O_{t}:=(O\otimes{\mathbb{I}}_{d}^{t-1})S_{t}
being a global and high-rank operator; as a result, the estimation variance bounded by
\|O_{t}\|_{\mathrm{sh},\mathcal{E}}^{2}
scales exponentially with the qubit number
n
.

This advantage is demonstrated numerically for two key regimes
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{LCl}}
and
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{Cl}}
in
Fig.

2
, with further discussion and applications, including purity estimation, Fisher information bounds and Rényi entropies, presented in the SM
[

The practicality of AFRS protocol is enhanced by three key features: (i) Following the mindset of shadow estimation, measurement data can be reused to estimate a collection of observables
\{O_{i}\}
with the sampling time only scales logarithmically with the number of observables, such advantage is analyzed later; (ii) In the implementation of AFRS, no auxiliary qubits are needed; and (iii) The entangling operation
\mathcal{R}
requires at most
n+1
layers of elementary quantum (Clifford) gates for the important case of
t=2
. We systematically compile
\mathcal{R}
(with the subsequent projective measurement) in END MATTER using the techniques of quantum error correction code (QECC)
[
56

and mid-circuit measurement
[

58

Note that the global nature of the joint unitary may present implementation challenges for some architectures with limited connectivity. In the following, for the sake of practical realizations on near-term hardware, we develop an advanced shallow-circuit variant that further reduces these requirements while preserving the protocol’s advantages.

Local-AFRS—
Consider the observable
O
of interest being local and acting nontrivially on the subsystem
A
, say,
O=O_{A}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{\bar{A}}

We propose the local-AFRS protocol for such local observables, which significantly reduces the difficulty of implementing the entangling unitary

In particular, we show that one can estimate

on

\rho^{t}_{A}:=\tr_{\bar{A}}(\rho^{t})
with a greatly simplified quantum circuit, even to a constant depth when the qubit number in

is

is chosen from a unitary ensemble on

In addition, the entangling operation before the measurement can also take the product form

Finally, we perform the computational basis measurement on the

In the classical post-processing stage of local-AFRS, we can use the unitary

Estimating a collection of observables—
As an application of local-AFRS, we consider the important problem of estimating a collection of local observables
\mathcal{W}=\{O_{l}\}_{l=1}^{L}
, which may have different local supports.
Divide the collection as
\mathcal{W}=\cup_{k=1}^{K}\mathcal{W}_{k}
, where each subset
\mathcal{W}_{k}
satisfies

\text{$\supp(O_{l})\cap\supp(O_{l^{\prime}})=\emptyset$}\ \text{or\, $\supp(O_{l})\subseteq\supp(O_{l^{\prime}})$},

(6)

\forall O_{l},O_{l^{\prime}}\in\mathcal{W}_{k}
.
We can use local-AFRS to estimate the observables in each
subset
\mathcal{W}_{k}
in the following way:
first apply a random unitary
V\in\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{LCl}}
for each replica, and then perform the entangling unitary
U_{k}=\bigotimes_{j}\mathcal{R}_{A_{j}}
before the final projective measurement,
such that each
O_{l}\in\mathcal{W}_{k}
is supported in one of the subsystems
\{A_{j}\}_{j}
.
Note that one needs to apply
K
different
U_{k}
in the experimental stage one at a time. In the case of
t=2
, the depth of each circuit is at most
\max_{l}\supp(O_{l})+2
.
Consider the terms in the Hamiltonian of a transverse-field Ising model for example, one has

with
\mathcal{W}_{\text{odd/even}}=\{Z_{j}Z_{j+1},X_{j}\}_{\text{odd/even}\ j}

The performance of our estimation is shown as follows, which inherits the multiplex nature of shadow estimation and maintains the sampling advantage of AFRS.

Corollary 1
.

Consider a collection of local observables
\mathcal{W}=\{O_{l}\}_{l=1}^{L}=\cup_{k=1}^{K}\mathcal{W}_{k}
that satisfies
Eq.

6
.
In order to estimate
o_{t}^{(l)}=\tr(O_l\rho^t)
within error

with success probability at least

it suffices to run the local-AFRS circuit the following number of times,

\displaystyle M=\frac{68K}{\epsilon^{2}}\max_{O_{l}\in\mathcal{W}}\mathrm{Var}\Big(\widehat{o^{(l)}_{t}}\Big)\log\left(\frac{2L}{\delta}\right),

(7)

where
\widehat{o^{(l)}_{t}}
is the single-shot estimator for

.

In particular, when all

have constant supports, the variance
of each single-shot estimator can be bounded by some constant according to
Eq.

S97
.
In this case, the number of required runs is only

, which scales linearly in

due to the implementation of

, and scales logarithmically in

Overall, the sample complexity scales linearly with the number of replicas

, and the prefactor depends only on the structure of the observables, such as their locality or Frobenius norm.

First, when

Second, compared with Ref.

Third, compared with Ref.

Fourth, the auxiliary-free protocol based on Bell-like measurements proposed in Refs.

The matrix form of
\mathcal{R}
—
Here, we give the matrix form of the unitary
\mathcal{R}
expressed in the computational basis
\{|\mathbf{x}\rangle\}
.
We first introduce some notation.
Two strings
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}[d^{t}]}
are said to be in the same class if there is an integer
r
such that
\tau^{r}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{y}
. That is, they can be transformed to each other by the one-step cyclic permutation
\tau
, which corresponds to the unitary
S_{t}
on
t
copies, i.e.,
S_{t}\ket{\mathbf{x}}=\ket{\tau(\mathbf{x})}
.
For instance, the strings
01353
and
35301
are in the same class in
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}[6]^{5}}
.
Note that two distinct classes have no intersection,
and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}[d^{t}]}
is the union of all distinct classes.
For each class of
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}[d^{t}]}
, we choose its smallest element
\mathbf{z}
as the representative, and use
[\mathbf{z}]
to represent the class.
For example,
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}[2]^{4}}
can be divided into six distinct classes, and one of them is
[0011]=\{0011,0110,1100,1001\}
.
For any class
[\mathbf{z}]
of
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}[d]^{t}}
, we denote by
|[\mathbf{z}]|
its cardinality, and define its corresponding normalized
t
-qudit states by Fourier transformation as

\displaystyle\ket{\Psi_{[\mathbf{z}]}^{(k)}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{|[\mathbf{z}]|}}\sum_{r=0}^{|[\mathbf{z}]|-1}\exp\left(\frac{rk\cdot 2\pi\rm{i}}{|[\mathbf{z}]|}\right)\ket{\tau^r(\mb{z})}

(8)

for
k=0,\dots,|[\mathbf{z}]|-1
.
The following lemma shows that the set of such states forms the common orthonormal eigenbasis
\{\ket{\psi_\mb{x}}\}
of the operators
S_{t}
and
Q_{\mathbf{b}}
.

Lemma 1
.

\left\{\ket{\Psi_{[\mb{z}]}^{(k)}}\right\}_{[\mathbf{z}],k}

forms an orthonormal basis on
\mathcal{H}_{d}^{\otimes t}
.
In addition,
\ket{\Psi_{[\mb{z}]}^{(k)}}
is the common eigenstate of
S_{t}
and
Q_{\mathbf{b}}
, with corresponding eigenvalue

\exp\big(\!-\frac{k\cdot 2\pi\rm{i}}{|[\mb{z}]|}\big)

and
t^{-1}\times(
the number of
\mathbf{b}
in the string
\mathbf{z})
, respectively.

Based on the basis in
Lemma

1
, it is direct to write down the corresponding entangling unitary as

\mathcal{R}=\sum_{[\mathbf{z}]}\sum_{k=0,\dots,|[\mathbf{z}]|-1}\ket{\tau^k(\mb{z})}\bra{\Psi_{[\mathbf{z}]}^{(k)}}.

(9)

According to the eigenvalue of
S_{t}
shown in
Lemma

1
, the real part of the function
f(\mathbf{x})
in
Theorem

1
reads
\mathrm{Re}(f(\mathbf{x}))=\cos(\frac{k\cdot 2\pi}{|[\mb{z}]|})
,
where
[\mathbf{z}]
is the class to which
\mathbf{x}
belongs, and the integer
k
satisfies
\tau^{k}(\mathbf{z})=\mathbf{x}
.

It is also worth noting, from the eigenvalue of
Q_{\mathbf{b}}
in
Lemma

1
, that the mapping strategy
\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{x}\to\mathbf{b}}
is just to randomly choose
\mathbf{b}
from
\{\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{t}\}
of
\mathbf{x}
with uniform probability
1/t
.
In practice, instead of such a random assignment, one can alternatively choose
\mathbf{b}
as the average of all
\mathbf{x}_{i}
.
Such kind of treatment directly simulates the random assignment process and inherits the multi-shot nature
[
67
,
68
]
, and would further reduce the variance in
Eq.

4
by a factor of
t
in some cases. We give the form of the new estimator and the detailed variance analysis in
SM
[
55
]
.

\mathcal{R}

The circuit compilation of

with the subsequent projective measurement is significant for the realization in quantum computing platforms.
Here, we focus on the

scenario, which is the most promising to demonstrate on near-term devices. In this case,

acting on

is Hermitian and has a matrix form in

.
We start from the single-qubit case with

-qubit case with

\mathcal{R}

\mathcal{V}^{\perp}={\rm span}(\ket{00},\ket{11})

We can understand the structure of the circuit in

(b) as follows. Note that the subspaces
\mathcal{V}
and

depend on the parity information of the two input qubits, and

\mathcal{V}
or
\mathcal{V}^{\perp}

c
, one adds a
classical
controlled-
H

but not in
\mathcal{V}^{\perp}

x_{1}
, and

One can extend the circuit in

The circuit compilation in the many-qubit case is non-trivial and indeed not a direct extension from the single-qubit case, since
\mathcal{R}\neq\bigotimes_{i}\mathcal{R}_{(i)}
, i.e.,
\mathcal{R}
cannot be written in a tensor-product form. Here,
\mathcal{R}_{(i)}
denotes the local unitary on the
i
-th qubit pair.
Note that the standard Bell measurement nevertheless can be written into the qubit-wise tensor-product form
[
17
,
35
]
, which highlights the significant difference between these two bases.
The main strategy of the following compilation is to add more entangling operations inside the first and(or) second replicas, beyond the product structure where the initial
\mathcal{R}_{(i)}
only supplies entanglement across two replicas.

Use
\mathbf{a}
and
\mathbf{b}
to label the
n
-bit string for the first and second replicas, respectively.
Without loss of generality, assume that the first
n-l
bits of
\mathbf{a}
and
\mathbf{b}
share the same string, and thus the last
l
bits are different (opposite indeed). That is,
\mathbf{a}=\mathbf{a}^{[n-l]}\mathbf{a}^{\lfloor l\rfloor}
and
\mathbf{b}=\mathbf{a}^{[n-l]}\overline{\mathbf{a}^{\lfloor l\rfloor}}
. Here,
\mathbf{a}^{[n-l]}
with
[n-l]:=\{1,2,\ldots,n-l\}
denotes the restricted bit-string on the subsystem of the first
n-l
qubits;
\mathbf{a}^{\lfloor l\rfloor}
with
\lfloor l\rfloor:=\{n-l+1,\ldots,n-1,n\}
denotes the restricted bit-string of the last
l
qubits; and
\overline{\mathbf{a}^{\lfloor l\rfloor}}
denotes the bit-flip of
\mathbf{a}^{\lfloor l\rfloor}
.
By
Eq.

9
we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}\ket{\mb{a}^{[n-l]}\mb{a}^{\lfloor l \rfloor}}_{1}\ket{\mb{a}^{[n-l]}\overline{\mb{a}^{\lfloor l \rfloor}}}_{2}=\ket{\mb{a}^{[n-l]}}_{1}\ket{\mb{a}^{[n-l]}}_{2}\otimes\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\!\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\ket{\overline{\mb{a}^{\lfloor l \rfloor}}}_{1}\ket{\mb{a}^{\lfloor l \rfloor}}_{2}+\ket{\mb{a}^{\lfloor l \rfloor}}_{1}\ket{\overline{\mb{a}^{\lfloor l \rfloor}}}_{2}\right),\quad\mathbf{a}^{\lfloor l\rfloor}<\overline{\mathbf{a}^{\lfloor l\rfloor}},\\[8.61108pt]
\!\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\ket{\overline{\mb{a}^{\lfloor l \rfloor}}}_{1}\ket{\mb{a}^{\lfloor l \rfloor}}_{2}-\ket{\mb{a}^{\lfloor l \rfloor}}_{1}\ket{\overline{\mb{a}^{\lfloor l \rfloor}}}_{2}\right),\quad\mathbf{a}^{\lfloor l\rfloor}>\overline{\mathbf{a}^{\lfloor l\rfloor}},\end{array}\right.

where we just extract the common state on the first
n-l
qubits on both replicas; and the inequality
\mathbf{a}^{\lfloor l\rfloor}<\overline{\mathbf{a}^{\lfloor l\rfloor}}
(
\mathbf{a}^{\lfloor l\rfloor}>\overline{\mathbf{a}^{\lfloor l\rfloor}}
) means that the first bit of
\mathbf{a}^{\lfloor l\rfloor}
is 0 (1).
It is not hard to see that the state on the last
l
qubits is a GHZ-like state, which inspire the following compilation.

Let us summarize the action of
\mathcal{R}
in
Section

I

a_{i}=b_{i}
,
\mathcal{R}
does nothing; and for the rest of qubits in

with

\mathcal{V}={\rm span}\left(\ket{\mb{a}^{\lfloor l \rfloor}}\ket{\overline{\mb{a}^{\lfloor l \rfloor}}},\ket{\overline{\mb{a}^{\lfloor l \rfloor}}}\ket{\mb{a}^{\lfloor l \rfloor}}\right)
, which degenerates to
{\rm span}\left(\ket{01},\ket{10}\right)
in the single-qubit case. By applying the technique of QECC, we construct the quantum circuit as shown in
Fig.

c^{i}=1

[
56
]
and can be realized in a fault-tolerant way in the future
[
87
]
.

Some remarks on the structure of the many-qubit quantum circuit in

for local observables.

A.2.1
Unbiasedness of the estimator
\widehat{\rho^{t}}

Our estimator
\widehat{\rho^{t}}
for
\rho^{t}
is defined as

\widehat{\rho^{t}}:=\mathrm{Re}(f(\mathbf{x}))\cdot\mathcal{M}^{-1}\left(V^{{\dagger}}\outerproduct{\mb{b}}{\mb{b}}V\right),

(S16)

where
\mathcal{M}^{-1}
is the inverse of the following measurement channel introduced in Ref.
[

]

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(\cdot):=\mathbb{E}_{V\sim\mathcal{E}}\sum_{\mathbf{b}}\bra{\bf{b}}V(\cdot)\ket{\bf{b}}V^{{\dagger}}\outerproduct{\mb{b}}{\mb{b}}V.

(S17)

By construction,
\mathcal{M}
is linear, invertible, and depends only on the system dimension and ensemble
\mathcal{E}

\mathcal{M}(\cdot)=\frac{(\cdot)+\tr(\cdot)\mathbb{I}}{d+1}
and
\mathcal{M}^{-1}(\cdot)=(d+1)(\cdot)-\tr(\cdot)\mathbb{I}
, with
d=2^{n}
. For the local Clifford (or Pauli) ensemble, the channel factorizes qubit-wise.

The expectation of
\widehat{\rho^{t}}
is

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\{V,\mathbf{x},\mathbf{b}\}}\widehat{\rho^{t}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\{V,\mathbf{x},\mathbf{b}\}}\,{\mathrm{Re}}(f(\mathbf{x}))\cdot\mathcal{M}^{-1}\left(V^{{\dagger}}\outerproduct{\mb{b}}{\mb{b}}V\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{V\sim\mathcal{E}}\sum_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{b}}\Pr(\mb{x},\mb{b}|V)\,{\mathrm{Re}}(f(\mathbf{x}))\mathcal{M}^{-1}\left(V^{{\dagger}}\outerproduct{\mb{b}}{\mb{b}}V\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{V\sim\mathcal{E}}\sum_{\mathbf{b}}\mathcal{M}^{-1}\left(V^{{\dagger}}\outerproduct{\mb{b}}{\mb{b}}V\right)\left[\sum_{\mathbf{x}}\Pr(\mb{x}|V)\Pr(\mb{b}|\mb{x})\,{\mathrm{Re}}(f(\mathbf{x}))\right].

(S18)

Here, the third equality holds because
\Pr(\mb{x},\mb{b}|V)=\Pr(\mb{x}|V)\Pr(\mb{b}|\mb{x})
, since the mapping result
\mathbf{b}
only depends on the real measurement outcome
\mathbf{x}
according to the mapping strategy
\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{x}\to\mathbf{b}}
in the main text.

Since
\Pr(\mb{x}|V)
and
\Pr(\mb{b}|\mb{x})
are real numbers, the last term in the parentheses of

\displaystyle{\mathrm{Re}}\left[\sum_{\mathbf{x}}\Pr(\mb{x}|V)\Pr(\mb{b}|\mb{x})f(\mathbf{x})\right]={\mathrm{Re}}\left[\Pr(\mb{b}|V)\right]=\bra{\bf{b}}V(\rho^{t})\ket{\bf{b}},

(S19)

where the first equality follows from Observation 1 in the main text.
By plugging this relation into
S18
, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\{V,\mathbf{x},\mathbf{b}\}}\widehat{\rho^{t}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{V\sim\mathcal{E}}\sum_{\mathbf{b}}\bra{\bf{b}}V(\rho^{t})\ket{\bf{b}}\mathcal{M}^{-1}\left(V^{{\dagger}}\outerproduct{\mb{b}}{\mb{b}}V\right)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{M}^{-1}\left[\mathbb{E}_{V\sim\mathcal{E}}\sum_{\mathbf{b}}\bra{\bf{b}}V(\rho^{t})\ket{\bf{b}}V^{{\dagger}}\outerproduct{\mb{b}}{\mb{b}}V\right]=\mathcal{M}^{-1}\left[\mathcal{M}(\rho^{t})\right]=\rho^{t},

(S20)

where we have used the definition of
\mathcal{M}
in

S17

In conclusion,
\widehat{\rho^{t}}

A.2.2

Since
\widehat{\rho^{t}}
and

are Hermitian operators, the estimator

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(\widehat{o_{t}})=\mathbb{E}_{\{V,\mathbf{x},\mathbf{b}\}}\left[\widehat{o_{t}}^{2}\right]-\left(\mathbb{E}_{\{V,\mathbf{x},\mathbf{b}\}}\widehat{o_{t}}\right)^{2}=\mathbb{E}_{\{V,\mathbf{x},\mathbf{b}\}}\left[\widehat{o_{t}}^{2}\right]-\left[\tr(O\rho^t)\right]^{2}.

Note that the inverse of
\mathcal{M}

\tr\!\left[X\mathcal{M}^{-1}(Y)\right]=\tr\!\left[\mathcal{M}^{-1}(X)Y\right]

So we have

\displaystyle\widehat{o_{t}}=\tr(O\widehat{\rho^t})={\mathrm{Re}}(\bra{\psi_\mb{x}}S_{t}\ket{\psi_\mb{x}})\tr\left[O\mathcal{M}^{-1}\left(V^{{\dagger}}\outerproduct{\mb{b}}{\mb{b}}V\right)\right]={\mathrm{Re}}(\bra{\psi_\mb{x}}S_{t}\ket{\psi_\mb{x}})\bra{\mb{b}}V\mathcal{M}^{-1}(O)V^{{\dagger}}\ket{\mb{b}}.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\{V,\mathbf{x},\mathbf{b}\}}\left[\widehat{o_{t}}^{2}\right]

Proof of Lemma 1 in END MATTER

Suppose
[\mathbf{z}]
,
[\mathbf{y}]
are two classes of
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}[d]^{t}}
, integers
k_{1}\in\{0,1,\dots,|[\mathbf{z}]|-1\}
and
k_{2}\in\{0,1,\dots,|[\mathbf{y}]|-1\}
.
When
[\mathbf{z}]\neq[\mathbf{y}]
, the two states
\ket{\Psi_{[\mathbf{z}]}^{(k_1)}}
and
\ket{\Psi_{[\mathbf{y}]}^{(k_2)}}
are orthogonal since
\langle\tau^{r_{2}}(\mathbf{z})|\tau^{r_{1}}(\mathbf{y})\rangle=0
for any integers
r_{1}
and
r_{2}
.
When
[\mathbf{z}]=[\mathbf{y}]
but
k_{1}\neq k_{2}
, the two states
\ket{\Psi_{[\mathbf{z}]}^{(k_1)}}
and
\ket{\Psi_{[\mathbf{y}]}^{(k_2)}}
are still orthogonal since

\displaystyle\left\langle\Psi_{[\mathbf{z}]}^{(k_{1})}\Big|\Psi_{[\mathbf{y}]}^{(k_{2})}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|[\mathbf{z}]|}\sum_{r=0}^{|[\mathbf{z}]|-1}\exp\left(\frac{r(k_{2}-k_{1})\cdot 2\pi\rm{i}}{|[\mathbf{z}]|}\right)=0,

(S27)

where the last inequality holds because
k_{2}-k_{1}\neq 0\mod|[\mathbf{z}]|
.
In addition, notice that the size of the set
\left\{\ket{\Psi_{[\mathbf{z}]}^{(k)}}\right\}_{[\mathbf{z}],k}
equals
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}d^{t}}
, and thus equals the dimension of

\mathcal{H}_{d}^{\otimes t}
.
Therefore,
\left\{\ket{\Psi_{[\mathbf{z}]}^{(k)}}\right\}_{[\mathbf{z}],k}

forms an orthonormal basis on
\mathcal{H}_{d}^{\otimes t}
.

Recall that
S_{t}\ket{\mathbf{x}}=\ket{\tau(\mathbf{x})}
for
\mathbf{x}\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}[d]^{t}}
.
So we have

\displaystyle S_{t}\ket{\Psi_{[\mathbf{z}]}^{(k)}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{|[\mathbf{z}]|}}\sum_{r=0}^{|[\mathbf{z}]|-1}\exp\left(\frac{rk\cdot 2\pi\rm{i}}{|[\mathbf{z}]|}\right)S_{t}\ket{\tau^r(\mathbf{z})}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{|[\mathbf{z}]|}}\sum_{r=0}^{|[\mathbf{z}]|-1}\exp\left(\frac{rk\cdot 2\pi\rm{i}}{|[\mathbf{z}]|}\right)\ket{\tau^{r+1}(\mathbf{z})}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{|[\mathbf{z}]|}}\sum_{r^{\prime}=0}^{|[\mathbf{z}]|-1}\exp\left(\frac{(r^{\prime}-1)k\cdot 2\pi\rm{i}}{|[\mathbf{z}]|}\right)\ket{\tau^{r'}(\mathbf{z})}

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-\frac{k\cdot 2\pi\rm{i}}{|[\mathbf{z}]|}\right)\frac{1}{\sqrt{|[\mathbf{z}]|}}\sum_{r^{\prime}=0}^{|[\mathbf{z}]|-1}\exp\left(\frac{r^{\prime}k\cdot 2\pi i}{|[\mathbf{z}]|}\right)\ket{\tau^{r'}(\mathbf{z})}

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-\frac{k\cdot 2\pi\rm{i}}{|[\mathbf{z}]|}\right)\ket{\Psi_{[\mathbf{z}]}^{(k)}}.

(S28)

Hence,
\ket{\Psi_{[\mathbf{z}]}^{(k)}}
is an eigenstate of
S_{t}
with eigenvalue

\exp\left(-\frac{k\cdot 2\pi\rm{i}}{|[\mathbf{z}]|}\right)
.

For
\mathbf{x}\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}[d]^{t}}
, we have

\displaystyle\left(\outerproduct{\mb{b}}{\mb{b}}_{i}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{d}^{\otimes(t-1)}\right)\ket{\mathbf{x}}=\begin{cases}\ket{\mathbf{x}}&\text{the $i$-th number of $\mathbf{x}$ equals $\mathbf{b}$},\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(S29)

Consequently, we have

\displaystyle Q_{\mathbf{b}}\ket{\mathbf{x}}=\frac{1}{t}\sum_{i}\left(\outerproduct{\mb{b}}{\mb{b}}_{i}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{d}^{\otimes(t-1)}\right)\ket{\mathbf{x}}=\frac{\text{the number of $\mathbf{b}$ in the string $\mathbf{x}$}}{t}\ket{\mathbf{x}},

(S30)

and

\displaystyle Q_{\mathbf{b}}\ket{\Psi_{[\mathbf{z}]}^{(k)}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{|[\mathbf{z}]|}}\sum_{r=0}^{|[\mathbf{z}]|-1}\exp\left(\frac{rk\cdot 2\pi\rm{i}}{|[\mathbf{z}]|}\right)Q_{\mathbf{b}}\ket{\tau^r(\mathbf{z})}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{|[\mathbf{z}]|}}\sum_{r=0}^{|[\mathbf{z}]|-1}\exp\left(\frac{rk\cdot 2\pi\rm{i}}{|[\mathbf{z}]|}\right)\frac{\text{the number of $\mathbf{b}$ in the string $\tau^{r}(\mathbf{z})$}}{t}\ket{\tau^r(\mathbf{z})}

\displaystyle=\frac{\text{the number of $\mathbf{b}$ in the string $\mathbf{z}$}}{t}\times\frac{1}{\sqrt{|[\mathbf{z}]|}}\sum_{r=0}^{|[\mathbf{z}]|-1}\exp\left(\frac{rk\cdot 2\pi\rm{i}}{|[\mathbf{z}]|}\right)\ket{\tau^r(\mathbf{z})}

\displaystyle=\frac{\text{the number of $\mathbf{b}$ in the string $\mathbf{z}$}}{t}\ket{\Psi_{[\mathbf{z}]}^{(k)}},

(S31)

where the third equality holds because the strings
\mathbf{z}
and
\tau^{r}(\mathbf{z})
contains the same number of
\mathbf{b}
for any integer
r
.
Hence,
\ket{\Psi_{[\mathbf{z}]}^{(k)}}
is an eigenstate of
Q_{\mathbf{b}}
with eigenvalue
t^{-1}\times(
the number of
\mathbf{b}
in the string
\mathbf{z})
.
This completes the proof.

Appendix C
More details on the circuit compilation of
\mathcal{R}

C.1
Single-qudit case

C.1.1
Circuit compilation

One can follow the route of the single-qubit case in END MATTER to find the circuit compilation of
\mathcal{R}
in the general single-qudit cases.
For
t=2
, the permutation
\tau
of
S_{2}
is just the swap operation, and any string in
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}[d]^{2}}
can be written as
ab
for
a,b\in\{0,\dots,d-1\}
.
If
a=b
, the class
[ab]=\{ab\}
; if
a<b
, the class
[ab]=\{ab,ba\}
.
In this way, the unitary
\mathcal{R}
acting on
\mathcal{H}_{d}^{\otimes 2}
satisfies

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}|ab\rangle=\begin{cases}|ab\rangle&a=b,\\
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|ba\rangle+|ab\rangle)&a<b,\\
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|ba\rangle-|ab\rangle)&a>b,\\
\end{cases}

(S32)

where
\mathcal{R}=\mathcal{R}^{{\dagger}}
is Hermitian for
t=2
.

The compilation strategy is similar to the single-qubit case: first use a measurement on the second replica to project the input into a sum-subspace, then apply a controlled single-qudit unitary on the first replica that creates the required symmetric/antisymmetric superpositions on each two-dimensional block.
Concretely, write the integer sum
c=a+b
with
a,b\in\{0,\dots,d-1\}
, and consider the subspace
\mathcal{V}_{c}={\rm span}\left(\{\ket{ab}\}_{a+b=c}\right)
with
c=0,1,\dots,2d-2
.
Each
\mathcal{V}_{c}
can be written as a direct sum of several 1- or 2-dimensional subspace
\mathcal{V}_{a,b}={\rm span}\left(\ket{ab},\ket{ba}\right)
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{c}=\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}a,b\in\{0,1,\dots,d-1\}\\
a+b=c\end{subarray}}\mathcal{V}_{a,b},

(S33)

Hence, after the projection onto
\mathcal{V}_{c}
, one can create the desired superposition on each
\mathcal{V}_{a,b}
by performing an appropriate unitary
U_{c}
on the first replica.

To make the integer sum
c=a+b
an unambiguous label, we enlarge the local dimension of the second replica from
d
to
2d
.
Physically, this means the second replica has computational basis
\{\ket{0},\dots.\ket{2d-1}\}
, so that each integer
c\in\{0,1,\dots,2d-2\}
can be represented by a distinct basis state
\ket{c}
. Operationally, we perform a controlled
2d
-shift from the first to the second replica,
X_{2d}=\sum_{j=0}^{2d-1}\ket{j+1 \ (\text{mod}\ 2d)}\bra{j}
, so that a controlled-
X_{2d}
maps
\ket{a}\ket{b}\mapsto\ket{a}\ket{a+b\ (\text{mod}\ 2d)}
. Measuring the second replica in the
\{|i\rangle\}_{i=0}^{2d-1}
basis then gives the integer
c
; because the protocol populates only
c\in\{0,\dots,2d-2\}
, the modulo-
2d
wrap-around does not create ambiguity and the measured
c
directly identifies
\mathcal{V}_{c}
.

\mathcal{R}
in the single-qudit case.

After obtaining
c
, we set
q_{1}:=\max\{0,c-d+1\}
and
q_{2}:=\min\{c,d-1\}
, so that the allowed values of
a
with
a+b=c
are
a\in\{q_{1},\dots,q_{2}\}
.

The form of the unitary
U_{c}
controlled by the measurement result
c
depends on the parity of
c
.
For odd
c
, we have

\displaystyle U_{c}=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=q_{1}}^{\frac{c-1}{2}}\left(\frac{|j\rangle+|c-j\rangle}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\langle j|+\sum_{j=\frac{c+1}{2}}^{q_{2}}\left(\frac{|c-j\rangle-|j\rangle}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\langle j|+\sum_{j<q_{1},j>q_{2}}\outerproduct{j}{j}.

(S34)

For even
c
, we have

\displaystyle U_{c}=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=q_{1}}^{\frac{c}{2}-1}\left(\frac{|j\rangle+|c-j\rangle}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\langle j|+\outerproduct{\frac{c}{2}}{\frac{c}{2}}+\sum_{j=\frac{c}{2}+1}^{q_{2}}\left(\frac{|c-j\rangle-|j\rangle}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\langle j|+\sum_{j<q_{1},j>q_{2}}\outerproduct{j}{j}.

(S35)

The index ranges are algebraically consistent: with
q_{1}:=\max\{0,c-d+1\}
and
q_{2}:=\min\{c,d-1\}
, one always has
q_{2}\geq q_{1}
for every
c\in{0,\dots,2d-2}
. Hence,
U_{c}
is well-defined.
Besides,
U_{c}
only introduces some superposition on selected pairs of computational bases of a single-qudit, and we remark such operations could be realized, for example, in optical systems by path encoding.
Finally, we measure the first replica in the computational basis
\{|i\rangle\}_{i=0}^{d-1}
, and denote the measurement result as
x_{1}
. By classical post-processing, we let
x_{1}
and
x_{2}=c-x_{1}
be the final output of the first and second replica, respectively.

The procedure is illustrated in
Fig.

S2
: we expand the second replica to
2d
levels, perform controlled-
X_{2d}
, measure the second replica to obtain
c
, apply
U_{c}
on the first replica, and finally measure the first replica. The same idea can be generalized to higher
t
, where class decomposition and conditional block unitaries become more involved.

Note that the
2d
-expansion is an enlargement of the local levels of the existing second replica. In practice, many platforms allow access to higher local levels (or multiple paths/modes) and thus implementing a
2d
-level system can be experimentally more convenient than introducing and controlling an additional qudit. Examples include higher internal or motional levels in trapped ions, multi-path/multi-mode encodings in photonics, and higher excited levels in superconducting circuits.

C.3
Single-qubit case for
t=3

As discussed in the main text, the joint unitary
\mathcal{R}
of Eq. (9) is defined to simultaneously diagonalize the cyclic permutation
S_{t}
and the projectors

.
For the case

, this reduces to the symmetric/antisymmetric decomposition, which admits a Clifford-only circuit implementation as demonstrated in the previous subsection.
However, for

, and thus the exact compilation of

may involve non-Clifford rotations. Systematic synthesis of such unitaries is a highly valuable open problem, and we regard it as an important direction for future work.

Although this explicit construction is limited to the single-qubit,

Notably, such small-scale constructions can be directly applied with local-AFRS, making them practically useful even for multi-qubit states.

In addition to explicit construction, larger-

93

D.1

After each experiment of the AFRS protocol, one obtains a measurement outcome string
\mathbf{x}=\{\mathbf{x}_{1}\mathbf{x}_{2}\cdots\mathbf{x}_{t}\}\in[d]^{t}

Therefore, the overall time complexity of Algorithm
2
for task B is
T_{B}=\mathcal{O}(nt^{2})
.

Third, task C is trivial and can be solved in
\mathcal{O}(\text{poly log}\;t)
time using an ordinary calculator, as both
k
and
|[\mathbf{z}]|
are
\mathcal{O}(t)
and do not depend on
n
.

In summary, the computation of
f(\mathbf{x})
can be done efficiently in
\mathcal{O}(nt^{2})
time.

D.2
Sample complexity for estimating a collection of observables

Our AFRS protocol inherits the mindset of shadow estimation that
the experimental procedure is independent of the specific properties to be predicted.
This feature enables it to simultaneously predict multiple different nonlinear properties of the system without losing high efficiency.
In this subsection, we analyze the sample cost of AFRS for estimating
\tr(O_l\rho^t)
with a collection of
L
observables
\mathcal{W}=\{O_{l}\}_{l=1}^{L}
.

In each experimental round of our AFRS protocol, we employ
t
copies of the unknown state
\rho
to construct an unbiased estimator
\hat{o}_{t}
for
\tr(O\rho^t)
.
After
M
experiments, we obtain a collection of estimators
\{\hat{o}_{t,(i)}\}_{i=1}^{M}
. For estimating a single
\tr(O\rho^t)
with precision
\epsilon
, the empirical mean estimator
\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\hat{o}_{t,(i)}
can be employed, and Chebyshev’s inequality dictates that the required number of experimental repetitions scales as
M\sim\mathrm{Var}(\widehat{o_{t}})\epsilon^{-2}
.
When the goal is to simultaneously estimate multiple properties
\{\tr(O_l\rho^t)\}_{l=1}^{L}
, we adopt the median-of-means technique
[
22
,
94
]
. This approach requires
M=NR
repetitions, where
R\sim\log(L)
and
N\sim\max_{l}\mathrm{Var}\Big(\widehat{o_{t}^{(l)}}\Big)\epsilon^{-2}
.
More concretely, for each observable
O_{l}\in\mathcal{W}
, we construct
R
independent sample means of size
N
, and output their median as our estimator for
\tr(O_l\rho^t)
.
Thus, the sample cost of our AFRS protocol is

Mt=NR\,t\sim\frac{t\log(L)}{\epsilon^{2}}\cdot\max_{l}\mathrm{Var}\Big(\widehat{o_{t}^{(l)}}\Big).

(S78)

As shown in Theorem 1 of the main text, the variance admits the following upper bound:

\mathrm{Var}(\widehat{o_{t}})\leq\|O_{0}\|^{2}_{\mathrm{sh},\mathcal{E}}+\|O\|^{2}_{\infty},

(S79)

where
O_{0}
denotes the traceless part of observable
O
,
and
\|O_{0}\|_{\mathrm{sh},\mathcal{E}}
represents the shadow norm of
O_{0}
, which depends on the unitary ensemble
\mathcal{E}
.
We explicitly characterize the scaling for two practically important ensembles:

a)

For the local Clifford ensemble
\mathcal{E}_{\rm LCl}
,
Eq. (S17) in Ref.
[
22
]
establishes:

\|O_{0}\|^{2}_{\mathrm{sh,LCl}}\leq 4^{\text{locality}(O)}\|O\|_{\infty}^{2},

(S80)

where
\text{locality}(O)
counts the number of qubits on which
O
acts nontrivially.
Combining this with Eqs. (
S78
) and (
S79
) yields the sample complexity

Mt\sim\frac{t\log(L)}{\epsilon^{2}}\cdot\max_{l}\left[\left(4^{\text{locality}(O_{l})}+1\right)\|O_{l}\|_{\infty}^{2}\right].

(S81)

The scaling is linear in
t
and independent of the total qubit number
n
.

b)

For the Global Clifford ensemble
\mathcal{E}_{\rm Cl}
, Eq. (S16) in Ref.
[
22
]
gives:

\|O_{0}\|^{2}_{\mathrm{sh,Cl}}\leq 3\,\|O_{0}\|_{2}^{2}.

(S82)

Combining this with Eqs. (
S78
) and (
S79
) leads to the sample complexity of AFRS:

Mt\sim\frac{t\log(L)}{\epsilon^{2}}\cdot\max_{l}\Big(3\,\|(O_{l})_{0}\|_{2}^{2}+\|O_{l}\|_{\infty}^{2}\Big),

(S83)

which similarly exhibits linear
t
-dependence and no explicit scaling with
n
.

When the observables
\{O_{l}\}_{l=1}^{L}
of interest have small localities, one can further employ the local-AFRS protocol to significantly reduce the circuit depth, thereby improving practicality.
According to Corollary 1 in the main text, to estimate all
\tr(O_l\rho^t)
with error at most
\epsilon
, it suffices to run the local-AFRS circuit the following number of times:

M\sim\frac{K\log(L)}{\epsilon^{2}}\max_{l}\mathrm{Var}\Big(\widehat{o^{(l)}_{t}}\Big),

(S84)

where
K\leq L
is the number of subsets obtained by partitioning
\{O_{l}\}_{l=1}^{L}
[see Eq. (6) in the main text].
In local-AFRS, we adopt the local Clifford ensemble
\mathcal{E}_{\rm LCl}
, so the variance of the estimator satisfies

\mathrm{Var}(\widehat{o_{t}})\leq\left\|O_{0}\right\|_{\mathrm{sh},\mathcal{E}}^{2}+\|O\|_{\infty}^{2}\leq\left(4^{\text{locality}(O)}+1\right)\|O\|_{\infty}^{2}.

(S85)

Moreover, each round of experiments utilizes
t
copies of the unknown state
\rho
. Combining these observations, we obtain the following sample complexity for the local-AFRS protocol:

Mt\sim\frac{tK\log(L)}{\epsilon^{2}}\max_{l}\left[\left(4^{\text{locality}(O_{l})}+1\right)\|O_{l}\|_{\infty}^{2}\right].

(S86)

Again, the scaling is still linear in
t
and independent of
n
.
In particular, if
L=1
and the observable of interest is the identity operator
O=\mathbb{I}
(i.e., we aim to estimate the moment
P_{t}=\tr(\rho^t)
), then we have
K=1
,
\text{locality}(O)=0
, and the sample complexity reduces to
Mt\sim t\epsilon^{-2}
.

In summary, for estimating a collection of
L
nonlinear properties

\{\tr(O_l\rho^t)\}_{l=1}^{L}
, the sample complexities of our AFRS and local-AFRS protocols both scale linearly in
t
and logarithmically in
L
; they have no explicit dependence on the qubit number
n
, but depend on the observable structure, i.e., the locality when using the local Clifford ensemble
\mathcal{E}_{\rm LCl}
, and the Frobenius norm when using the global Clifford ensemble
\mathcal{E}_{\rm Cl}
.
All these scaling behaviors are the best that one can expect.
Compared to the original shadow protocol
[
22
]
for estimating linear properties
\{\tr(O_l\rho)\}_{l=1}^{L}
, our sample costs only increase by a factor of
\mathcal{O}(t)
. In fact, as shown by recent works Refs.
[
95
,
96
]
, this linear dependence in
t
is inevitable, even when estimating a single nonlinear property
\tr(O\rho^t)
. Therefore, our AFRS and local-AFRS protocol achieve the optimal scaling behavior in
t
.

E.1
Key observation behind local-AFRS

Consider the observable
O
of interest being local and acting nontrivially on the subsystem
A
, say,
O=O_{A}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{\bar{A}}
.
With respect to the locality property, the random unitary used in local-AFRS can be taken as the product form
V=V_{A}\otimes V_{\bar{A}}
.
Similar to the framework of AFRS introduced in the main text, here one needs to effectively generate the
pseudo
but now
marginal
conditional probability

\Pr(\mb{b}^{A}|V_{A})=\bra{\mb{b}^{A}}V_{A}(\tr_{\bar{A}}(\rho^{t}))\ket{\mb{b}^{A}},

(S87)

where
\mathbf{b}^{A}
labels the bit-string restricted on
A
. Actually, Observation 1 in the main text
can be applied to the local case here, but with the observable
Q_{\mathbf{b}}
updated to

Q^{\mathrm{local}}(\mathbf{b}^{A})=\left[t^{-1}\sum_{i}\ket{\mb{b}^{A}}\bra{\mb{b}^{A}}_{i}\otimes\mathbb{I}^{\otimes(t-1)}_{A}\right]\otimes\mathbb{I}^{\otimes t}_{\bar{A}}=Q_{\mathbf{b}^{A}}\otimes\mathbb{I}^{\otimes t}_{\bar{A}}.

(S88)

Here,
Q_{\mathbf{b}^{A}}
is symmetric on all
t
replicas, but restricted on
A
; the complement subsystem
\bar{A}
takes the identity
\mathbb{I}_{\bar{A}}
for all
t
replicas.

Recall that the key step to develop AFRS is to find the common eigenbasis of both
S_{t}
and
Q_{\mathbf{b}}
.
Now the updated
Q^{\mathrm{local}}(\mathbf{b}^{A})
is in the tensor-product form. Also,
S_{t}
can be factorized into
S_{t}=S_{t}^{A}\otimes S_{t}^{\bar{A}}
, and even further into the qubit level operators on
t
replicas.
In this way, for any
\mathbf{x}^{A}
and
\mathbf{x}^{\bar{A}}
, the state

\mathcal{R}^{{\dagger}}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{R}^{{\dagger}}_{\bar{A}}\ket{\mb{x}^{A},\mb{x}^{\bar{A}}}=\ket{\psi_{\mb{x}^{A}}}\ket{\psi_{\mb{x}^{\bar{A}}}}

(S89)

is a common eigenstate for
Q^{\mathrm{local}}(\mathbf{b}^{A})
and
S_{t}
, with
\mathbf{x}^{A}=\{\mathbf{x}_{1}^{A}\mathbf{x}_{2}^{A}\cdots\mathbf{x}_{t}^{A}\}

(
\mathbf{x}^{\bar{A}}=\{\mathbf{x}_{1}^{\bar{A}}\mathbf{x}_{2}^{\bar{A}}\cdots\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\bar{A}}\}
)
being the bit-strings restricted on subsystem
A
(
\bar{A}
) for all replicas.
Accordingly, for local-AFRS here, the function
f(\mathbf{x})
, mapping strategy
\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{x}\to\mathbf{b}}
, and the quantum probability
\Pr(\mb{x}|V)
in Observation 1 are updated to

\displaystyle f(\mathbf{x}^{A},\mathbf{x}^{\bar{A}})=\bra{\phi_{\mb{x}}}S_{t}^{A}\otimes S_{t}^{\bar{A}}\ket{\phi_{\mb{x}}},\quad\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{x}^{A}\to\mathbf{b}^{A}}:\Pr(\mb{b}^{A}|\mb{x}^{A})=\bra{\psi_{\mb{x}^{A}}}Q_{\mathbf{b}^{A}}\ket{\psi_{\mb{x}^{A}}},\quad\Pr(\mb{x}|V)=\bra{\phi_{\mb{x}}}V(\rho)^{\otimes t}\ket{\phi_{\mb{x}}},

(S90)

respectively, where we denote by
\ket{\phi_{\mb{x}}}:=\ket{\psi_{\mb{x}^{A}}}\otimes\ket{\psi_{\mb{x}^{\bar{A}}}}
for simplicity, with
\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{x}^{A}\mathbf{x}^{\bar{A}}
.

The following observation clarifies the relation between
\Pr(\mb{b}^A|V_A)
,
f(\mathbf{x}^{A},\mathbf{x}^{\bar{A}})
,
\Pr(\mb{b}^A|\mb{x}^A)
, and
\Pr(\mb{x}|V)
. It is the counterpart of Observation 1 in the main text.

Observation S2
.

The pseudo and marginal conditional probability
\Pr(\mb{b}^A|V_A)
can be written as

\displaystyle\Pr(\mb{b}^A|V_A)

\displaystyle=\tr\left[S_{t}\left(Q_{\mathbf{b}^{A}}\otimes{\mathbb{I}}_{\bar{A}}^{\otimes t}\right)V(\rho)^{\otimes t}\right]

(S91)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{x}}\bra{\phi_{\mb{x}}}S_{t}\ket{\phi_{\mb{x}}}\bra{\phi_{\mb{x}}}\left(Q_{\mathbf{b}^{A}}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{\bar{A}}^{\otimes t}\right)\ket{\phi_{\mb{x}}}\bra{\phi_{\mb{x}}}V(\rho)^{\otimes t}\ket{\phi_{\mb{x}}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{x}}f(\mathbf{x}^{A},\mathbf{x}^{\bar{A}})\Pr(\mb{b}^A|\mb{x}^A)\Pr(\mb{x}|V).

Proof of Observation
S2
.

Recall that
\Pr(\mb{b}^A|V_A)=\bra{\mb{b}^A}V_{A}(\tr_{\bar{A}}(\rho^{t}))\ket{\mb{b}^A}
and
Q_{\mathbf{b}^{A}}=t^{-1}\sum_{i}\outerproduct{\mb{b}^{A}}{\mb{b}^{A}}_{i}\otimes\mathbb{I}^{\otimes(t-1)}_{A}
.
So the first line of
Eq.

S91
can be proved by showing that

\bra{\mb{b}^A}V_{A}(\tr_{\bar{A}}(\rho^{t}))\ket{\mb{b}^A}=\tr\left(S_{t}\left[\left(\outerproduct{\mb{b}^A}{\mb{b}^A}_{i}\otimes{\mathbb{I}}_{A}^{\otimes(t-1)}\right)\otimes{\mathbb{I}}_{\bar{A}}^{\otimes t}\right]V(\rho)^{\otimes t}\right)\quad\forall i=1,2,\cdots,t.

(S92)

And we graphically show the derivation of this relation in
Fig.

S5
.

Supplementary Figure S5:
\justifying

S92
for local-AFRS protocol. Here, the yellow lines represent the subsystem
\bar{A}
of
\rho
, and the gray dashed lines denote the trace operation. It indicates in the right sketch that
V_{\bar{A}}
is not essential as

.
Thus, in practice, we can set

Recall that
S_{t}^{A}
and
Q_{\mathbf{b}^{A}}
can be diagonalized simultaneously under the basis
\left\{\ket{\psi_{\mb{x}^{A}}}\right\}
, and

can be diagonalized in the basis
\left\{\ket{\psi_{\mb{x}^{\bar{A}}}}\right\}
. Hence, the operators
\outerproduct{\phi_{\mb{x}}}{\phi_{\mb{x}}}=\outerproduct{\psi_{\mb{x}^{A}}}{\psi_{\mb{x}^{A}}}\otimes\outerproduct{\psi_{\mb{x}^{\bar{A}}}}{\psi_{\mb{x}^{\bar{A}}}}

, and

Using this fact, the second line of

\displaystyle\tr\left[S_{t}\left(Q_{\mathbf{b}^{A}}\otimes{\mathbb{I}}_{\bar{A}}^{\otimes t}\right)V(\rho)^{\otimes t}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{x}}\bra{\phi_{\mb{x}}}S_{t}\left(Q_{\mathbf{b}^{A}}\otimes{\mathbb{I}}_{\bar{A}}^{\otimes t}\right)V(\rho)^{\otimes t}\ket{\phi_{\mb{x}}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{x}}\bra{\phi_{\mb{x}}}S_{t}\ket{\phi_{\mb{x}}}\bra{\phi_{\mb{x}}}\left(Q_{\mathbf{b}^{A}}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{\bar{A}}^{\otimes t}\right)\ket{\phi_{\mb{x}}}\bra{\phi_{\mb{x}}}V(\rho)^{\otimes t}\ket{\phi_{\mb{x}}}.

(S93)

Finally, the third line of
Eq.

S91
holds because

\displaystyle\bra{\phi_{\mb{x}}}\left(Q_{\mathbf{b}^{A}}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{\bar{A}}^{\otimes t}\right)\ket{\phi_{\mb{x}}}=\bra{\psi_{\mb{x}^A}}Q_{\mathbf{b}^{A}}\ket{\psi_{\mb{x}^A}}\bra{\psi_{\mb{x}^{\bar{A}}}}\ket{\psi_{\mb{x}^{\bar{A}}}}=\Pr(\mb{b}^A|\mb{x}^A).

(S94)

This completes the proof.
∎

We remark that, as mentioned in the main text, one can set
V_{\bar{A}}=\mathbb{I}_{\bar{A}}
here, which does not change the relation in

S92
, and thus the final conclusion. This is also demonstrated by
Fig.

S5
.
Moreover, the used eigenbasis
\ket{\phi_{\mb{x}}}:=\ket{\psi_{\mb{x}^{A}}}\otimes\ket{\psi_{\mb{x}^{\bar{A}}}}
can also be simplified. To get the result in
S93
, one just need to find a basis on
\bar{A}
of
t

S_{t}^{\bar{A}}
. As mentioned in the main text, one thus can choose

, which can be realized by
\mathcal{R}_{A}\otimes\bigotimes_{i\in\bar{A}}\mathcal{R}_{(i)}

In summary,
Observation

, and
\ket{\phi_{\mb{x}}}=\ket{\psi_{\mb{x}^{A}}}\otimes\bigotimes_{i\in\bar{A}}\ket{\psi_{\mb{x}^{i}}}
, with the function

\displaystyle f(\mathbf{x}^{A},\mathbf{x}^{\bar{A}})

\displaystyle\rightarrow\bigg(\bra{\psi_{\mb{x}^{A}}}\otimes\bigotimes_{i\in\bar{A}}\bra{\psi_{\mb{x}^{i}}}\bigg)\bigg(S_{t}^{A}\otimes\bigotimes_{i\in\bar{A}}S_{t}^{i}\bigg)\bigg(\ket{\psi_{\mb{x}^{A}}}\otimes\bigotimes_{i\in\bar{A}}\ket{\psi_{\mb{x}^{i}}}\bigg)

\displaystyle\qquad=f(\mathbf{x}^{A})\prod_{i\in\bar{A}}f(\mathbf{x}^{i}).

(S95)

With such changes, the results of the estimator and the variance given in Supplementary Note

E.2
The estimator and performance of local-AFRS

The experimental procedure of local-AFRS is described in the main text.
Let
V_{A}
be the random unitary performed on subsystem

of each replica, and
\mathbf{x}=\{\mathbf{x}^{A},\mathbf{x}^{\bar{A}}\}
represent the actual measurement outcome [see Fig. 3 (a) of the main text].
In the postprocessing stage, we first map
\mathbf{x}^{A}
to a string
\mathbf{b}^{A}
with probability

\mathbf{b}^{A}
,
\mathbf{x}^{A}
,
\mathbf{x}^{\bar{A}}

The detailed form of

Theorem S3
.

For a single implementation of the circuit in Fig. 3 (a) (single-shot,

). The unbiased estimator of

\displaystyle\widehat{\rho^{t}_{A}}=\mathrm{Re}\left(f(\mathbf{x}^{A},\mathbf{x}^{\bar{A}})\right)\mathcal{M}_{A}^{-1}\left(V_{A}^{{\dagger}}\outerproduct{\mb{b}^A}{\mb{b}^A}V_{A}\right).

\mathcal{E}^{(A)}
, the function

E.2.1
Unbiasedness of the estimator
\widehat{\rho^{t}_{A}}

The expectation of the estimator
\widehat{\rho^{t}_{A}}
is given as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\{V,\mathbf{x},\mathbf{b}^{A}\}}\widehat{\rho^{t}_{A}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\{V,\mathbf{x},\mathbf{b}^{A}\}}{\mathrm{Re}}\left(f(\mathbf{x}^{A},\mathbf{x}^{\bar{A}})\right)\mathcal{M}_{A}^{-1}\left(V_{A}^{{\dagger}}\outerproduct{\mb{b}^A}{\mb{b}^A}V_{A}\right)

(S98)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{V\sim\mathcal{E}}\sum_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{b}^{A}}\Pr(\mb{x},\mb{b}^A|V){\mathrm{Re}}\left(f(\mathbf{x}^{A},\mathbf{x}^{\bar{A}})\right)\mathcal{M}_{A}^{-1}\left(V_{A}^{{\dagger}}\outerproduct{\mb{b}^A}{\mb{b}^A}V_{A}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{V\sim\mathcal{E}}\sum_{\mathbf{b}^{A}}\mathcal{M}_{A}^{-1}\left(V_{A}^{{\dagger}}\outerproduct{\mb{b}^A}{\mb{b}^A}V_{A}\right)\left[\sum_{\mathbf{x}}\Pr(\mb{x}|V)\Pr(\mb{b}^A|\mb{x}^A){\mathrm{Re}}\left(f(\mathbf{x}^{A},\mathbf{x}^{\bar{A}})\right)\right],

where we used that
\Pr(\mb{x},\mb{b}^A|V)=\Pr(\mb{x}|V)\Pr(\mb{b}^A|\mb{x}^A)
.
Since
\Pr(\mb{x}|V)
and
\Pr(\mb{b}^A|\mb{x}^A)
are real numbers, the last term in the parentheses can be
further written as

\displaystyle{\mathrm{Re}}\left[\sum_{\mathbf{x}}\Pr(\mb{x}|V)\Pr(\mb{b}^A|\mb{x}^A)f(\mathbf{x}^{A},\mathbf{x}^{\bar{A}})\right]={\mathrm{Re}}\left[\Pr(\mb{b}^A|V_A)\right]=\bra{\mb{b}^A}V_{A}\left(\tr_{\bar{A}}(\rho^{t})\right)\ket{\mb{b}^A},

(S99)

where the first equality follows from

Observation

S2
.
By plugging this relation into

S98
, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\{V,\mathbf{x},\mathbf{b}^{A}\}}\widehat{\rho^{t}_{A}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{V\sim\mathcal{E}}\sum_{\mathbf{b}^{A}}\mathcal{M}_{A}^{-1}\left(V_{A}^{{\dagger}}\outerproduct{\mb{b}^A}{\mb{b}^A}V_{A}\right)\bra{\mb{b}^A}V_{A}\left(\tr_{\bar{A}}(\rho^{t})\right)\ket{\mb{b}^A}

(S100)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{M}_{A}^{-1}\left[\mathbb{E}_{V_{A}\sim\mathcal{E}^{(A)}}\sum_{\mathbf{b}^{A}}\bra{\mb{b}^A}V_{A}\left(\tr_{\bar{A}}(\rho^{t})\right)\ket{\mb{b}^A}V_{A}^{{\dagger}}\outerproduct{\mb{b}^A}{\mb{b}^A}V_{A}\right]

E.2.2
Proof of the estimation variance in

S97

The variance of

reads

\mathrm{Var}(\widehat{o_{t}})=\mathbb{E}_{\{V,\mathbf{x},\mathbf{b}^{A}\}}[\widehat{o_{t}}^{2}]-\left[\mathbb{E}_{\{V,\mathbf{x},\mathbf{b}^{A}\}}\widehat{o_{t}}\right]^{2}=\mathbb{E}_{\{V,\mathbf{x},\mathbf{b}^{A}\}}[\widehat{o_{t}}^{2}]-\left[\tr(O_A \rho^t)\right]^{2}.

(S101)

\mathcal{M}_{A}^{-1}
,
the estimator
\widehat{o_{t}}

\displaystyle\widehat{o_{t}}=\tr\!\left[O_{A}\widehat{\rho^{t}_{A}}\right]

\displaystyle={\mathrm{Re}}\left(f(\mathbf{x}^{A},\mathbf{x}^{\bar{A}})\right)\tr\left[O_{A}\mathcal{M}_{A}^{-1}\!\left(V_{A}^{\dagger}\outerproduct{\mb{b}^A}{\mb{b}^A}V_{A}\right)\right]

\displaystyle={\mathrm{Re}}\left(\bra{\phi_\mb{x}}S_{t}\ket{\phi_\mb{x}}\right)\bra{\mb{b}^A}V_{A}\mathcal{M}_{A}^{-1}(O_{A})V_{A}^{\dagger}\ket{\mb{b}^A}.

(S102)

It follows that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\{V,\mathbf{x},\mathbf{b}^{A}\}}\left[\widehat{o_{t}}^{2}\right]

(S103)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{V\sim\mathcal{E}}\sum_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{b}^{A}}\Pr(\mb{x}|V)\Pr(\mb{b}^A|\mb{x}^A)\,\widehat{o_{t}}^{2}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{=}}\mathbb{E}_{V\sim\mathcal{E}}\sum_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{b}^{A}}\bra{\phi_{\mb{x}}}V(\rho)^{\otimes t}\ket{\phi_{\mb{x}}}\bra{\psi_{\mb{x}^{A}}}Q_{\mathbf{b}^{A}}\ket{\psi_{\mb{x}^{A}}}\left[{\mathrm{Re}}\left(\bra{\phi_\mb{x}}S_{t}\ket{\phi_\mb{x}}\right)\right]^{2}\bra{\mb{b}^A}V_{A}\mathcal{M}_{A}^{-1}(O_{A})V_{A}^{\dagger}\ket{\mb{b}^A}^{2}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{\leq}}\mathbb{E}_{V\sim\mathcal{E}}\sum_{\mathbf{b}^{A}}\bra{\mb{b}^A}V_{A}\mathcal{M}_{A}^{-1}(O_{A})V_{A}^{\dagger}\ket{\mb{b}^A}^{2}\underbrace{\left(\sum_{\mathbf{x}}\bra{\phi_{\mb{x}}}V(\rho)^{\otimes t}\ket{\phi_{\mb{x}}}\bra{\psi_{\mb{x}^{A}}}Q_{\mathbf{b}^{A}}\ket{\psi_{\mb{x}^{A}}}\right)}_{(*)},

where
(a)
holds because

\operatorname{Pr}[|\hat{o}_{l}(R_{k},N_{k})-\tr(O_l\rho^t)|\geq\epsilon]\leq\delta^{\prime}_{k}/|\mathcal{W}_{k}|
for all

within error

\displaystyle\Pr({\rm succ})\geq\prod_{k=1}^{K}\left(1-\frac{\delta^{\prime}_{k}}{|\mathcal{W}_{k}|}\right)^{|\mathcal{W}_{k}|}\geq\prod_{k=1}^{K}\left(1-\delta_{k}^{\prime}\right)\geq 1-\sum_{k=1}^{K}\delta_{k}^{\prime}=1-\frac{\delta}{L}\sum_{k=1}^{K}|\mathcal{W}_{k}|=1-\delta,

(S125)

where the last equality holds because
\sum_{k=1}^{K}|\mathcal{W}_{k}|=L
.

Note that the total repeating measurement time used by our protocol reads

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}M_{k}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{68}{\epsilon^{2}}\max_{O_{l}\in\mathcal{W}}\mathrm{Var}\left(\widehat{o^{(l)}_{t}}\right)\log\left(\frac{2\,|\mathcal{W}_{k}|}{\delta_{k}^{\prime}}\right)=\frac{68K}{\epsilon^{2}}\max_{O_{l}\in\mathcal{W}}\mathrm{Var}\left(\widehat{o^{(l)}_{t}}\right)\log\left(\frac{2L}{\delta}\right),

(S126)

which equals
M
given in Eq. (7) of the main text. This completes the proof of Corollary 1.

H.4
Estimation of the quantum Fisher information bound

The
quantum Fisher information
(QFI) is a fundamental quantity that plays a central role in quantum metrology and many other fields, as it provides the ultimate precision limit for estimating an unknown parameter and serves as a witness for multipartite entanglement. The QFI is defined with respect to a given Hermitian operator
O
and a quantum state
\rho
, and can be expressed as
[
51
]

\displaystyle F_{Q}=2\tr\!\left[\frac{(\rho\otimes\mathbb{I}-\mathbb{I}\otimes\rho)^{2}}{\rho\otimes\mathbb{I}+\mathbb{I}\otimes\rho}\mathcal{S}(O\otimes O)\right]

(S134)

where
\mathcal{S}
is the swap operator.
However, the direct evaluation of
F_{Q}
is experimentally challenging.
To overcome this, recent work
[
51
]
introduced a hierarchy of lower bounds to the QFI using polynomials of the density matrix:

\displaystyle F_{m}=2\tr\!\left[\,\sum_{\ell=0}^{m}(\rho\otimes\mathbb{I}-\mathbb{I}\otimes\rho)^{2}(\mathbb{I}\otimes\mathbb{I}-\rho\otimes\mathbb{I}-\mathbb{I}\otimes\rho)^{\ell}\mathcal{S}(O\otimes O)\right],\qquad m=0,1,2,\dots.

(S135)

It was proved that
F_{m}
converges exponentially with
m
to
F_{Q}
, and thus provides an accurate approximation of the QFI
[
51
]
.
Based on OS, Ref.
[
51
]
proposed a protocol for estimating
F_{m}
, but its sample complexity scales exponentially with the qubit number
n
, making the protocol resource-consuming.
Whether
F_{m}
can be estimated with polynomially many samples remains an open problem.

In this subsection, we show that
F_{m}
can be estimated efficiently via a variant of our local-AFRS protocol, provided that
m
is a constant and the observable
O
admits a decomposition into
\mathcal{O}(\text{poly}(n))
local observables, i.e.,
O=\sum_{i}O_{i}
, where each
O_{i}
has support on a constant number of qubits.
Actually, most thermodynamic quantities in quantum many-body physics can be written as observables of this form, such as geometrically-local Hamiltonians.
In this case,
F_{m}
can be expressed as a summation of
\mathcal{O}(\text{poly}(n))
terms of the form
\tr(O_i\rho^{t_1} O_j\rho^{t_2})
, with
t_{1},t_{2}\geq 0
being constant integers.
Therefore, the task of estimating
F_{m}
reduces to estimating each such term to sufficient accuracy.
Denote by

the smallest subsystem on which both
O_{i}
and
O_{j}
act nontrivially (i.e.,

),
and by

its complementary subsystem;

O_{j}=O_{j,A}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{\bar{A}}

H.5
Estimation of entropic quantities

In this subsection, we illustrate a concrete, practically relevant application of AFRS by estimating the Petz-Rényi relative entropy,
D_{\alpha}(\rho\|\sigma):=\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\log_{2}\tr(\rho^\alpha\sigma^{1-\alpha})
, at order
\alpha=2
we have

D_{2}(\rho\|\sigma)=\log_{2}\tr(\rho^2\sigma^{-1}).

(S147)

We treat the important special case
\sigma={\mathbb{I}}_{d}/d
(the maximally mixed state), for which the relative entropy reduces to a simple function of the purity estimation and is frequently used for quantum mixedness testing
[
97
]
and quantum error correction
[
98
]
. In this case,

D_{2}\left(\rho\left\|\frac{{\mathbb{I}}_{d}}{d}\right.\right)=\log_{2}\left(d\tr(\rho^2)\right)=n+\log_{2}P_{2},\ P_{2}:=\tr(\rho^2),

(S148)

with
d=2^{n}
. Hence estimating
D_{2}(\rho\|{\mathbb{I}}_{d}/d)
is equivalent to estimating the purity
P_{2}
, which is directly accessible with the
t=2
AFRS estimator introduced in the main text.
Below, we describe the estimator, error propagation, and the numerical simulation details.

Let
\widehat{P_{2}}
denote the unbiased AFRS estimator of
P_{2}
, which is constructed as in
Eq.

S133
.
We estimate the elative entropy by
\widehat{D_{2}}=n+\log_{2}\widehat{P_{2}}
. For small additive estimation error
\delta P_{2}=\widehat{P_{2}}-P_{2}
, a first-order expansion gives

\widehat{D_{2}}-D_{2}\approx\frac{1}{\ln 2}\frac{\delta P_{2}}{P_{2}}.

(S149)

Thus, to estimate
D_{2}
within error
\epsilon
, it suffices to estimate
P_{2}
within error
\epsilon^{\prime}\approx(\ln 2)\epsilon P_{2}
. According to our previous discussions, this accuracy can be efficiently achieved provided that
P_{2}
is not too small.

In
Fig.

S9
(b), we numerically simulate the error of estimating
D_{2}
using the OS and local-AFRS protocol respectively.
We instantiate the above discussion on the depolarized
n
-qubit GHZ states,
\rho=(1-p)\ket{\mathrm{GHZ}_n}\bra{\mathrm{GHZ}_n}+p{\mathbb{I}}_{d}/d
, with
p=0.3
for the numerical simulation demonstrated here.
The analytical purity of
\rho
together with the Rényi entropy
D_{2}(\rho\|{\mathbb{I}}_{d}/d)
can be therefore calculated and used as ground truth to compute the estimation error.
For a fair resource comparison, both methods are given the same sampling number of the quantum state
N=1000
. The numerical data confirm the theoretical expectations. The empirical mean-squared error of estimating
D_{2}
using OS grows rapidly with the qubit number
n
, which is consistent with
Eq.

S132
. In contrast, the estimation error of AFRS remains nearly flat (or grows only mildly) in the tested range; consequently, AFRS attains a given precision with far fewer measurements than OS, and the relative advantage widens rapidly with
n
. Note that increasing the shot budget reduces the error for both methods, with AFRS reaching target errors using substantially fewer shots.

Beyond these illustrative example, we note that efficient estimation of nonlinear quantities is also crucial in a wide range of tasks, including protocols for quantum virtual cooling and the detection of mixed-state quantum phases
[
99
]
. Taken together, the analyses and simulations presented here demonstrate that (local-)AFRS provides a powerful and versatile framework for estimation nonlinear properties for quantum states, with broad potential impact for both near-term experiments and future applications in quantum information science.
