Pointwise attractors which are not strict
Magdalena Nowak

Abstract
. We deal with the finite family ℱ \mathcal{F} of continuous maps on the Hausdorff space X X . A nonempty compact subset A A of such space is called a strict attractor if it has an open neighborhood U U such that A = lim n → ∞ ℱ n ​ ( S ) A=\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{F}^{n}(S) for every nonempty compact S ⊂ U S\subset U . Every strict attractor is a pointwise attractor, which means that the set { x ∈ X ; lim n → ∞ ℱ n ( x ) = A } \{x\in X;\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{F}^{n}(x)=A\} contains A A in its interior. We present a class of examples of pointwise attractors – from the finite set to the Sierpiński carpet – which are not strict when we add to the system one nonexpansive map.

1.
Introduction

Consider the following general version of an iterated function system.

Definition 1.1
.

A pair
(X,\mathcal{F})
is called an
iterated function system
(short IFS) if
X
is a Hausdorff space and
\mathcal{F}
is a finite family of continuous maps
X\to X
. By
\mathcal{K}(X)
we denote the hyperspace of every nonempty compact subsets of
X
with the Vietoris topology. Define also a
Barnsley-Hutchinson
operator
\mathcal{F}\colon\mathcal{K}(X)\to\mathcal{K}(X)
associated with the IFS
(X,\mathcal{F})
such that for every
S\in\mathcal{K}(X)

\mathcal{F}(S)=\bigcup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}f(S).

We also use this formula for arbitrary
S\subset X
and, for singletons we will write
\mathcal{F}^{n}(x)
instead of
\mathcal{F}^{n}(\{x\})
.

Following by the paper
[
2
]
we define several two version of attractors for a given IFS
(X,\mathcal{F})
.

Definition 1.2
.

A
strict attractor
of IFS
(X,\mathcal{F})
is a set
A\in\mathcal{K}(X)
which has an open neighborhood
U
, such that
A=\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{F}^{n}(S)
for every
S\in\mathcal{K}(U)
. The maximal open set
U
with the above property is called the
basin
of the attractor
A
.

In the classical theory of IFS, where
\mathcal{F}
is a family of contractions on the complete metric space
X
, due to the Hutchinson theorem, the whole space
X
is the basin of the unique attractor of the given IFS. For example the singleton
\{0\}
is a strict attractor of IFS
(\mathbb{R},\{\frac{x}{2}\})
, the unit interval
[0,1]
is a strict attractor of
(\mathbb{R},\{\frac{x}{2},\frac{x+1}{2}\})
and the unit square
[0,1]^{2}
is a strict attractor of
(\mathbb{C},\{\frac{x}{2},\frac{x+1}{2},\frac{x+i}{2},\frac{x+1+i}{2}\})
.

Definition 1.3
.

A
pointwise attractor
of IFS
(X,\mathcal{F})
is a set
A\in\mathcal{K}(X)
satisfying
A\subset\mathrm{int}~B_{p}(A,\mathcal{F})
, where

B_{p}(A,\mathcal{F})=\{x\in X;\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{F}^{n}(x)=A\}

is called a
pointwise basin
of the attractor
A
.

Both attractors defined above are fixed points of operator
\mathcal{F}
and their basins are open (see
[
1
]
). Moreover, the space homeomorphic to such set remains an attractor of the corresponding IFS. It is easy to note that every strict attractor is pointwise one. Barnsley, Leśniak and Rypka shown in
[
2
]
that the converse implication is true if
\mathcal{F}
contains only nonexpansive maps on the metric space, but it is not true in general. The first counterexample, given by D. Kwietniak, is the following.

Example 1.4
.

Let
S^{1}=\{z\in\mathbb{C};|z|=1\}
be the unit circle which may be projected to the set
\hat{\mathbb{R}}=\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
. Consider
\hat{\mathbb{R}}
with an Euclidean metric on the circle and define a continuous map
\varphi\colon\hat{\mathbb{R}}\to\hat{\mathbb{R}}
such that
\varphi(x)=x+1
for
x\neq\infty
and
\varphi(\infty)=\infty
. Note that the singleton
\{\infty\}

is a pointwise attractor of
(\hat{\mathbb{R}},\{\varphi\})

and
B_{p}(\{\infty\},\{\varphi\})=\hat{\mathbb{R}}
. However, it is not a strict attractor of such IFS, cause
\{\infty\}\neq\lim_{n\to\infty}\varphi^{n}(K)
for every compact set
K=\{\infty\}\cup(-\infty,x]
.

In this paper we describe a wider class of counterexamples like that. In most of them only one map in the IFS is not nonexpansive.

2.
Preliminaries and notation

Consider the space
\mathcal{K}(X)
for a given Hausdorff space
X
. We endow it with the Vietoris topology
\tau_{V}
generated by the basis of sets

\langle U_{1},\dots,U_{n}\rangle:=\{A\in\mathcal{K}(X);A\subset\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}U_{i}\text{ and }A\cap U_{i}\neq\emptyset\text{ for every }i=1,\dots,n\}

where
U_{1},\dots,U_{n}
are open subsets of
X
. In this topology
\mathcal{K}(X)
is a Hausdorff space (see
[
5
]
).

If
X
is compact, then the convergence in the Vietoris topology in
\mathcal{K}(X)
is equivalent with the Kuratowski convergence (so-called L-convergence), cf.
[
4
]
. We say that a set
A\subset X

is a Kuratowski limit (or topological limit)
of the sequence
(A_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of subsets of
X
if
A=\operatorname{Li}A_{n}=\operatorname{Ls}A_{n}
where:

•

\operatorname{Li}A_{n}=\{x\in X;
for every open neighborhood
U
of
x,A_{n}\cap U\neq\emptyset
for almost every
n\in\mathbb{N}\}

•

\operatorname{Ls}A_{n}=\{x\in X;
for every open neighborhood
U
of
x,A_{n}\cap U\neq\emptyset
for infinitely many
n\in\mathbb{N}\}.

We denote this by
A=\operatorname{Lt}A_{n}
.

Note that
x\in\operatorname{Ls}A_{n}
iff there exists
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
such that
a_{n}\in A_{n}
and its subsequence
(a_{n_{k}})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
such that
x=\lim_{k\to\infty}a_{n_{k}}
. The following properties can be easily proved or found in
[
4
,
5
]
.

Remark 2.1
.

For every sequences
(A_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
,
(B_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(C_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of subsets of topological space
X

(i)

if
X
is a regular Hausdorff space then

A=\lim_{n\to\infty}A_{n}\text{ in the Vietoris topology }\Rightarrow A=\operatorname{Lt}A_{n}

(ii)

if
X
is a compact Hausdorff space then

A=\lim_{n\to\infty}A_{n}\text{ in the Vietoris topology }\Leftrightarrow A=\operatorname{Lt}A_{n}

(iii)

if
D\in\mathcal{K}(X)
and
A_{n}\in\mathcal{K}(D)
for every
n
then

A=\lim_{n\to\infty}A_{n}\text{ in the Vietoris topology }\Leftrightarrow A=\operatorname{Lt}A_{n}

(iv)

if there exists
\lim_{n\to\infty}A_{n}
and
\lim_{n\to\infty}B_{n}
in the Vietoris topology then

\lim_{n\to\infty}(A_{n}\cup B_{n})=\lim_{n\to\infty}A_{n}\cup\lim_{n\to\infty}B_{n}

(v)

if
A_{n}\subset B_{n}
for every
n
then
\operatorname{Li}A_{n}\subset\operatorname{Li}B_{n}
and
\operatorname{Ls}A_{n}\subset\operatorname{Ls}B_{n}

(vi)

if
A_{n}\subset B_{n}\subset C_{n}
and
\lim_{n\to\infty}A_{n}=\lim_{n\to\infty}C_{n}
then
\lim_{n\to\infty}B_{n}=\lim_{n\to\infty}A_{n}
.

3.
Attracting maps with a local repellor

Let
\varphi:X\to X
be a continuous map on the Hausdorff space
X
.
By
\operatorname{Fix}(\varphi)
we denote the set of fixed points of the function
\varphi
. It is a closed set by continuity of
\varphi
.

Definition 3.1
.

We say that a point
a\in\operatorname{Fix}(\varphi)
is a
local repellor of

\varphi
if it has a sequence
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
X
converges to the point
a
, called a
witnessing sequence
, such that
x_{0}\notin\operatorname{Fix}(\varphi)
and
\varphi(x_{n+1})=x_{n}
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Note that, in fact, no point in the witnessing sequence is a fixed point of
\varphi
.

Theorem 3.2
.

If for a set
A\subset X
, the map
\varphi:X\to X
has a local repellor
a\in A
with the witnessing sequence
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
such that
x_{0}\notin A
, then
A
cannot be a strict attractor for the IFS containing
\varphi
.

Proof.

By the fact that
a=\lim_{n\to\infty}x_{n}
, for every open
U\supset A
there exists
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
x_{n}\in U
for
n\geq n_{0}
. Then the set
K:=\{a\}\cup\bigcup_{n\geq n_{0}}\{x_{n}\}
is a compact subset of
U
. If the map
\varphi
is contained in some finite family
\mathcal{F}
of continuous maps
X\to X
, then

x_{0}=\varphi^{n}(x_{n})\subset\varphi^{n}(K)\subset\mathcal{F}^{n}(K)

for every
n\geq n_{0}
.
Therefore
A\neq\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{F}^{n}(K)
, otherwise

x_{0}
must be an element of
A
.
∎

As a consequence of that theorem
we immediately obtain

Corollary 3.3
.

If the map
\varphi
has a local repellor, then the set
\operatorname{Fix}(\varphi)
is not a strict attractor of any IFS containing
\varphi
.

The map
\varphi
from Kwietniak’s Example
1.4
satisfies the above corollary cause the only fixed point
\infty
is also a local repellor of
\varphi
with a witnessing sequence
(-n)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
. This function is also obeys the following definition which guarantees, in that case, that
\{\infty\}
is a pointwise attractor.

Definition 3.4
.

A continuous map
\varphi\colon X\to X
on a Hausdorff space
X
is called an
attracting map
when for every
x\in X
there exists a limit
\lim_{n\to\infty}\varphi^{n}(x)\in\operatorname{Fix}(\varphi)
.

We will use attracting maps to preserve a pointwise attractivity in the following way.

Theorem 3.5
.

If
\varphi
is an attracting map on
X
and
\operatorname{Fix}(\varphi)
is a pointwise attractor of IFS
(X,W)
such that
W(X)\subset\operatorname{Fix}(\varphi)
, then the set
\operatorname{Fix}(\varphi)
is a pointwise attractor of IFS
(X,W\cup\{\varphi\})
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{F}:=W\cup\{\varphi\}
. We obtain the assertion by the fact that

W^{n}(x)\subset\mathcal{F}^{n}(x)\subset\operatorname{Fix}(\varphi)\cup\{\varphi^{n}(x)\}

for every
x\in B_{p}(\operatorname{Fix}(\varphi),W)
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and by the squeezing argument (cf. Remark
2.1
(
vi
)).
∎

These results reveal the importance of attracting maps with local repellor, so called
ALR-maps
.

Corollary 3.6
.

If
\varphi
is an attracting map with local repellor (ALR-map) such that
\operatorname{Fix}(\varphi)
is a pointwise attractor of IFS
(X,W)
and
W(X)\subset\operatorname{Fix}(\varphi)
, then the set
\operatorname{Fix}(\varphi)
is a pointwise but not strict attractor of IFS
(X,W\cup\{\varphi\})
.

3.1.
Examples of ALR-maps

Let us present several examples of attracting maps with local repellor on
\mathbb{R}
, the unit circle
S^{1}
and the unit ball in
\mathbb{C}
.

Maps
x^{2}
and
\sqrt{x}
are simple examples of ALR-maps on the unit interval. In the following lemma we characterize a wider class of attracting maps with local repellor on the closed intervals in
\mathbb{R}
.

Lemma 3.7
.

For a given interval
[a,b]\subset\mathbb{R}
, each continuous map
\varphi\colon[a,b]\to[a,b]
where
\operatorname{Fix}(\varphi)=\{a,b\}
, is an ALR-map on
[a,b]
.

Proof.

Note that if
a
and
b
are the only fixed points of
\varphi
, then
\varphi(x)<x
[or
\varphi(x)>x
] for every
x\in(a,b)
.

For proving attractivity of
\varphi
observe that the sequence
(\varphi^{n}(x))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
starting from arbitrary
x\in[a,b]
is
decreasing [respectively increasing]
or constant for
n
big enough. This means that there exist
g=\lim_{n\to\infty}\varphi^{n}(x)
which has to be a fixed point of
\varphi
, so
g\in\{a,b\}
.

Next, we show that
b
is a local repellor if
\varphi(x)<x
for every
x\in(a,b)
. Indeed, we can construct a witnessing sequence
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
starting from the arbitrary
x_{0}\in(a,b)
. If
x_{n}\in(a,b)
, then

x_{n}\in[x_{n},b]\subset[\varphi(x_{n}),\varphi(b)]\subset\varphi([x_{n},b]).

Therefore, for any natural
n
we can find
x_{n+1}\in[x_{n},b]
such that
\varphi(x_{n+1})=x_{n}
. Note that
x_{n}\neq b
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Otherwise for some
N\in\mathbb{N}
we would have
x_{0}=\varphi^{N}(b)=b
which contradicts
x_{0}\in(a,b)
.
Thus we construct increasing sequence
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
[x_{0},b)
which should have a limit in the set of fixed points of
\varphi
. Hence
b=\lim_{n\to\infty}x_{n}
.

Analogously we can proof that, if
\varphi(x)>x
for every
x\in(a,b)
, then
a
is a local repellor of
\varphi
.
∎

Therefore we have the ensuing

Example 3.8
.

On the arbitrary interval
[a,b]
in
\mathbb{R}
the following functions are attracting maps with a local repellor:

\frac{(x-a)^{2}}{b-a}+a\quad\text{ or }\quad\sqrt{(x-a)(b-a)}+a.

In the Figure
1
we present graphs of several ALR-maps on
[a,b]
and diagrams of their dynamics: black points
a
and
b
are fixed points and
other elements from the interval are attracted to the fixed points according to the arrows.

[a,b]
.

The following two lemmas will help us construct other ALR-maps.

Lemma 3.9
.

Assume that, for every index
i\in I
, the function
\varphi_{i}
is an attracting map on the Hausdorff space
X_{i}
and

(i)

\varphi_{i_{0}}
has a local repellor for some
i_{0}\in I

(ii)

X_{i}\cap X_{j}\subset\operatorname{Fix}(\varphi_{i})\cap\operatorname{Fix}(\varphi_{j})
for distinct
i,j\in I
.

Then the union of maps
\Phi=\bigcup_{i\in I}\varphi_{i}
(the function
\Phi\colon\bigcup_{i\in I}X_{i}\to\bigcup_{i\in I}X_{i}
such that

\Phi|_{X_{i}}=\varphi_{i}
for every
i\in I
) is an ALR-map and
\operatorname{Fix}(\Phi)=\bigcup_{i\in I}\operatorname{Fix}(\varphi_{i})
.

Proof.

The map
\Phi
is well defined, cause for
x\in X_{i}\cap X_{j}
we have
\Phi(x)=\varphi_{i}(x)=\varphi_{j}(x)=x
. It is easy to show that
\operatorname{Fix}(\Phi)=\bigcup_{i\in I}\operatorname{Fix}(\varphi_{i})
and
\Phi
is an attracting map.

Note also that the local repellor
a
of the map
\varphi_{i_{0}}
is also a local repellor of
\Phi
. Indeed, the point
a\in\operatorname{Fix}(\varphi_{i_{0}})\subset\operatorname{Fix}(\Phi)
is a limit of the witnessing sequence
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
such that
x_{n}\in X_{i_{0}}\setminus\operatorname{Fix}(\varphi_{i_{0}})
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and, by the condition (
ii
),
x_{n}\notin X_{j}
for any
j\neq i_{0}
. Thus
x_{0}\notin\operatorname{Fix}(\Phi)
and
\Phi(x_{n+1})=\varphi_{i_{0}}(x_{n+1})=x_{n}
.
∎

For an ALR-map

X
and for homeomorphism
h\colon X\to Y

h\circ\varphi\circ h^{-1}

Y
.

Example 3.11
.

Using the homeomorphism
h\colon[0,2\pi)\to S^{1}
of the formula
h(x)=e^{ix}
, we can construct an ALR-map on the arbitrary

a,b\in S^{1}

\alpha=arg(a),\beta=arg(b)

b

\varphi(x)=\exp\big(i\big(\frac{(\arg x-\alpha)^{2}}{\beta-\alpha}+\alpha\big)\big),

Example 3.12
.

We can also construct an ALR-map on the closed unit ball
B(0,1)\subset\mathbb{C}

a,b\colon B(0,1)\to S^{1}
such that for every
x+iy\in B(0,1)

Thus, for each
z\in B(0,1)
points
a(z),z,b(z)
are collinear. Then, the bijection

\varphi(z)=\Big|\frac{z-a(z)}{b(z)-a(z)}\Big|(z-a(z))+a(z)

is an ALR-map on
B(0,1)
, constant on the circle
S^{1}
(see the first diagram in the Figure

z\in B(0,1)\setminus S^{1}

•

the point
\varphi(z)

z

, so
|\varphi(z)-a(z)|<|z-a(z)|

a(\varphi(z))=a(z)
and

4.
Examples of pointwise and nonstrict attractors

Still let
\varphi
be a continuous function on the Hausdorff space
X
. Looking for maps satisfying the assumptions of Corollary
3.6
we noted the following

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
A=\bigcup_{k=1}^{m}A_{k}
for some natural
m\geq 1
and for every
k=1,\dots,m
, the set
A_{k}
is a retract of
X
(by the retraction
r_{k}\colon X\to A_{k}
) and a pointwise attractor of IFS
(X,W_{k})
.
Then for
\mathcal{F}=\bigcup_{k=1}^{m}\{w\circ r_{k};w\in W_{k}\}
the set
A
is a pointwise attractor for IFS
(X,\mathcal{F})
and
\mathcal{F}(X)=A
.

Proof.

Define
\tilde{W}_{k}:=\{w\circ r_{k};w\in W_{k}\}
for every
k=1,\dots,m
and
\mathcal{F}:=\bigcup_{k=1}^{m}\tilde{W}_{k}
. Note that

\tilde{W}_{k}(X)=W_{k}(r_{k}(X))=W_{k}(A_{k})=A_{k},

for each
k
, so also
\mathcal{F}(X)=A
. We show that
A
is a pointwise attractor of
(X,\mathcal{F})
and
B_{p}(A,\mathcal{F})=X
. Take
x\in X
and note that

\bigcup_{k=1}^{m}\tilde{W}^{n}_{k}(x)\subset\mathcal{F}^{n}(x)\subset A
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
Moreover,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\bigcup_{k=1}^{m}\tilde{W}^{n}_{k}(x)=\bigcup_{k=1}^{m}\lim_{n\to\infty}\tilde{W}^{n}_{k}(x)=\bigcup_{k=1}^{m}\lim_{n\to\infty}{W}^{n}_{k}(r_{k}(x))=\bigcup_{k=1}^{m}A_{k}=A.

This implies that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{F}^{n}(x)=A
, so
A
is a pointwise attractor of
(X,\mathcal{F})
.
∎

Consequently, by the Corollary
3.6
we have the following main theorem.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
\varphi\colon X\to X
be an attracting map with local repellor such that
\operatorname{Fix}(\varphi)
is a finite union of sets each of which is a retract of
X
and a pointwise attractor on
X
. Then there exists a family
\mathcal{F}
containing
\varphi
, such that
\operatorname{Fix}(\varphi)
is a pointwise attractor of IFS
(X,\mathcal{F})
with a pointwise basin
X
but it is not a strict attractor of
(X,\mathcal{F})
.

As a corollary of the above theorem, using ALR-maps on intervals and arcs we can construct several examples of pointwise attractors which are not strict.

Example 4.3
.

If
A
is a finite union of closed arcs or singletons in
S^{1}
, then there exists an IFS
(S^{1},\mathcal{F})
for which
A
is not a strict attractor but it is a pointwise attractor.

Let
A=\bigcup_{k=1}^{m}A_{k}
is a nonempty, finite union of singletons and closed arcs
A_{k}
. We can assume that the above union is disjoint. Each set
A_{k}
is a pointwise (and strict) attractor and a retract of
S^{1}
.
The set
S^{1}\setminus A
is a disjoint union of
m
open arcs
I_{k}=(a_{k},b_{k})
. For every
k=1,\dots,m
we can find an ALR-map
\varphi_{k}
on
\overline{I_{k}}
such that
\operatorname{Fix}(\varphi_{k})=\{a_{k},b_{k}\}
(see Example
3.11
). Then, by the Lemma
3.9
, the union
\varphi=\bigcup_{k=1}^{m}\varphi_{k}\cup id_{A}
is an ALR-map on
S^{1}
(see Figure
5
). Thus, by the Theorem
4.2
, the set
A=\operatorname{Fix}(\varphi)
is a pointwise but not strict attractor for some IFS.

.

A\subset S^{1}

\varphi

w\circ\varphi

w|_{I_{k}}\colon I_{k}\to I_{(k+1)mod{(m)}}

5.
Classical fractals as no strict attractors

The results obtained in the previous section cannot be applied to many classical IFS-attractors that are not a finite union of retracts. Therefore, we had to develop some new approach were the added function
\varphi
must cooperate with other maps from IFS (for example, compositions of that maps must be commutative on a certain set).

Recall that, for
W,V
two families of maps, we denote by
W\circ V=\{w\circ v;w\in W,v\in V\}
the set of all compositions of maps from both sets. Inductively define
W^{0}=\{id\}
,
W^{n+1}=W^{n}\circ W
and
W^{<\omega}=\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}W^{n}
.
In order to avoid notation with multiple indexes, we will use the following symbols for subsequences and their limits. For a given sequence
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and an infinite
N\subset\mathbb{N}
, by
(x_{n})_{n\in N}
we denote the subsequence of
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and its limit (if exists) by

\lim_{n\in N}x_{n}.

Theorem 5.1
.

If
A
is a pointwise attractor of
(X,W)
on the regular Hausdorff space
X
and there exist a compact set
D
such that

1.

A\subset W(D)\subset D\subset B_{p}(A,W)

2.

D\setminus A
is contained in the union of a family
\mathcal{I}
of disjoint open sets such that for every
I\in\mathcal{I}
the set
W_{I}:=\{w\in W^{<\omega};w(D)\cap I\neq\emptyset\}
is finite

3.

there exists an ALR-map
\varphi\colon X\to X
such that

a)

\operatorname{Fix}(\varphi)=A

b)

\varphi(X)\subset D
and
\varphi(I)=I
for every
I\in\mathcal{I}

c)

\varphi\circ w(x)=w\circ\varphi(x)
for every
x\in D
and
w\in W
(commutativity on
D
)

then
A
is a pointwise attractor but not a strict attractor for IFS
(X,W\cup\{\varphi\})
.

Proof.

Denote
\mathcal{F}=\tilde{W}\cup\{\varphi\}
and observe that
\mathcal{F}(D)\subset D
. By the Corollary
3.3
we immediately have that
A
is not a strict attractor of
(X,\mathcal{F})
. Now we will show that
A
is a pointwise attractor of
(X,\mathcal{F})
and
B_{p}(A,\mathcal{F})\supset B_{p}(A,W)
. We divide this proof into several steps.

STEP 1:

A\subset\operatorname{Li}\mathcal{F}^{n}(x)
for every
x\in B_{p}(A,W)
.

We obtain this fact by Remark
2.1
(
i
) and (
v
). Indeed

A=\lim_{n\to\infty}W^{n}(x)=\operatorname{Li}{W}^{n}(x)\subset\operatorname{Li}\mathcal{F}^{n}(x).

STEP 2:

\operatorname{Ls}\mathcal{F}^{n}(x)\subset A
for every
x\in B_{p}(A,W)
.

Take
x\in B_{p}(A,W)
and
y\in\operatorname{Ls}\mathcal{F}^{n}(x)
, which means that
y=\lim_{n\in N}f_{n}(x)
for some
(f_{n})_{n\in N}
such that
f_{n}\in\mathcal{F}^{n}
and for infinite set
N\subset\mathbb{N}
. Let us exclude two cases:

Case 1.
y\notin D
. Then, by regularity of
X
, we can find two disjoint open sets:
V\supset D
and
U\ni y
. The set
V
is also open neighborhood of the attractor
A
so for
n\in N
big enough
W^{n}(x)\subset V
and

f_{n}(x)\in\mathcal{F}^{n}(x)\subset W^{n}(x)\cup D\subset V,

thus such points cannot be contained in the set
U
. This leads to a contradiction to
y=\lim_{n\in N}f_{n}(x)
.

Case 2.
y\in D\setminus A
. Then, for all but finite
n\in N
, in the composition
f_{n}
the map
\varphi
appears at least once. Otherwise
f_{n}\in W^{n}
for
n
from some infinite set
M\subset N
and

y=\lim_{n\in M}f_{n}(x)\in\lim_{n\in M}W^{n}(x)=A.

This means that, by commutativity on
D\supset\varphi(X)
, for
n\in N
big enough we must have

f_{n}(x)=w_{n}\circ\varphi^{n-k_{n}-s_{n}}\circ v_{n}(x)\in w_{n}(D),

where
w_{n}\in W^{k_{n}},v_{n}\in W^{s_{n}}
and
0\leq k_{n}+s_{n}<n
for some sequences of natural numbers
(k_{n})_{n\in N},(s_{n})_{n\in N}
.
Since
y\in D\setminus A=\bigcup\mathcal{I}
, then
y\in I
for some
I\in\mathcal{I}
and also
f_{n}(x)\in I
for
n\in N
big enough. Hence, for every
n
from some infinite set
N^{\prime}\subset N
we have
f_{n}(x)\in I\cap w_{n}(D)
, so also
w_{n}
must be an element of the finite set
W_{I}
. Here we will use the so-called pigeonhole principle, which states that, if the expressions of a given sequence are in a finite union of sets, then one of these sets must contain its subsequence. Thus, there exists a continuous map
\textbf{w}\in W_{I}
which is a composition from
W^{K}
for some natural
K
, such that for infinitely many
n
(say for
n
from some infinite set
N^{\prime\prime}\subset N^{\prime}
)

f_{n}(x)=\textbf{w}\circ\varphi^{n-K-s_{n}}\circ v_{n}(x)=\varphi^{n-K-s_{n}-1}\circ\textbf{w}\circ\varphi\circ v_{n}(x),

thanks of the commutativity on
D
.

From the regularity of
X
, take an open disjoint sets
U\ni y
and
V\supset A\supset\textbf{w}(\varphi(A))
. Then by the continuity of
\textbf{w}\circ\varphi
there exists
V^{\prime}\supset A
such that
\textbf{w}(\varphi(V^{\prime}))\subset V
. According to the fact that
A
is a pointwise attractor of
(X,W)
, there exists
s_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all but finite
s\geq s_{0}
we have
W^{s}(x)\subset V^{\prime}
and also
\textbf{w}(\varphi(W^{s}(x)))\subset V
. Thus,
\textbf{w}\circ\varphi\circ v_{n}(x)\in U\cap I
only for finitely many maps
v_{n}\in\bigcup_{s<s_{0}}W^{s}
. For those functions and
n\in N^{\prime\prime}
points
f_{n}(x)=\varphi^{n-K-s_{n}-1}\circ\textbf{w}\circ\varphi\circ v_{n}(x)
have a chance to be an elements of the set
I
. Then again, by the pigeonhole principle, there exists a constant
S<s_{0}
and a map
\textbf{v}\in W^{S}
such that
f_{n}(x)=\varphi^{n-K-S-1}\circ\textbf{w}\circ\varphi\circ\textbf{v}(x)\in I
for
n
from infinite set
N^{\prime\prime\prime}\subset N^{\prime\prime}
. This means that, taking
z:=\textbf{w}\circ\varphi\circ\textbf{v}(x)
we obtain

y=\lim_{n\in N^{\prime\prime\prime}}f_{n}(x)=\lim_{n\in N^{\prime\prime\prime}}\varphi^{n-K-S-1}(z)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\varphi^{n}(z)\in\operatorname{Fix}(\varphi)=A

which is a contradiction.

This means that
y
have to be an element of the set
A
, so
\operatorname{Ls}\mathcal{F}^{n}(x)\subset A
. Steps 1, 2 and the fact that
\operatorname{Li}\mathcal{F}^{n}(x)\subset\operatorname{Ls}\mathcal{F}^{n}(x)
imply that
\operatorname{Lt}\mathcal{F}^{n}(x)=A
.

STEP 3:

A=\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{F}^{n}(x)
.

We obtain this fact by Remark
2.1
(
iii
). Indeed, for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
the finite set
\mathcal{F}^{n}(x)
is a subset of
D\cup\bigcup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}W^{k}(x)
, which is a compact set cause
\lim_{k\to\infty}W^{k}(x)=A\subset D
. This ends the proof.
∎

Despite the long list of assumptions in the above theorem, there are several examples that satisfy them. It is known that the classical fractal like ternary Cantor set, Sierpiński triangle and Sierpiński carpet are strict and pointwise attractors. Our result says that, adding only one nonexpansive map to the IFS, we can spoil their strict attractivity.

Example 5.2
.

The Cantor set in
\mathbb{R}
or
\hat{\mathbb{R}}
has an IFS for which it is a pointwise attractor but not a strict attractor.

Let
A
be the ternary Cantor set in the unit interval
D:=[0,1]
. Take the standard IFS for the Cantor set:
W=\{\frac{x}{3},\frac{x+2}{3}\}
on
\mathbb{R}
(or their continuous extension to
\hat{\mathbb{R}}
). The pointwise basin
B_{p}(A,W)=\mathbb{R}\supset D\supset W(D)\supset A
. The set
D\setminus A
is a union of countable family
\mathcal{I}
of disjoint open intervals.
Note that the set
W_{I}:=\{w\in W^{<\omega};w([0,1])\cap I\neq\emptyset\}
is finite for every
I\in\mathcal{I}
. Indeed for
I\in\mathcal{I}
there exists a number
N
such that
W^{N}(D)\cap I=\emptyset
for every
n\geq N
cause
W^{n}(D)
is the
n
-th step of the standard construction of the ternary Cantor set.

Denote
I_{0}=(\frac{1}{3},\frac{2}{3})
and observe that for each
I\in\mathcal{I}
there exists a unique affine bijection
w_{I}
in
W_{I}
such that
w_{I}(I_{0})=I
. Moreover for every
w\in W^{<\omega}
the set
w(I)
is always an element of
\mathcal{I}
, so

(1)

\displaystyle w_{w(I)}=w\circ w_{I},

cause
w\circ w_{I}(I_{0})=w(I)
.

Let us define a map
\varphi
satisfying the last assumption of the Theorem
5.1
. Take an arbitrary ALR-map
\varphi_{0}\colon\overline{I_{0}}\to\overline{I_{0}}
such that
\operatorname{Fix}(\varphi_{0})=\{\frac{1}{3},\frac{2}{3}\}
(like in the Example
3.8
). Then

\varphi(x)=\begin{cases}x&\text{for }x\in A,\\
w_{I}\circ\varphi_{0}\circ w_{I}^{-1}(x)&\text{for }x\in I\text{ where }I\in\mathcal{I},\\
r(x)&\text{for }x\in\mathbb{R}\setminus D~(\text{or }\hat{\mathbb{R}}\setminus D).\end{cases}

where
r
is an arbitrary retraction on
[0,1]
. It is easy to check that
\varphi
is an ARL-map and
A=\operatorname{Fix}(\varphi)
. Moreover the image
\mathrm{im}\varphi=D
and
\varphi(I)=I
for every interval
I\in\mathcal{I}
. The commutativity of maps on
D
goes form the equation (
1
).
Indeed, for an element
x\in D=[0,1]
and the arbitrary map
w\in W
if
x\in A
then
w(x)\in A
so
\varphi(w(x))=w(x)=w(\varphi(x))
. If
x\in D\setminus A
then
x\in I
for some
I\in\mathcal{I}
and

w\circ\varphi(x)=w\circ w_{I}\circ\varphi_{0}\circ w_{I}^{-1}(x)=w_{w(I)}\circ\varphi_{0}\circ w_{w(I)}^{-1}\circ w(x)=\varphi\circ w(x).

All assumptions of the Theorem
5.1
are satisfied which means that the ternary Cantor set
A
is a pointwise but not strict attractor for IFS
(\mathbb{R},W\cup\{\varphi\})
and
(\hat{\mathbb{R}},W\cup\{\varphi\})
.

The similar construction can be made also for the Sierpiński triangle and the Sierpiński carpet. Then the set
D
should be a convex hull of the fractal and
\varphi_{0}
is an arbitrary ALR-map on the closure of biggest ”hole”
I_{0}
(see Figure
8
).
