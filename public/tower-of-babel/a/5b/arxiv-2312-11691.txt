SUSY QED with Lorentz-asymmetric fermionic matter and a glance at the electron’s EDM


Abstract
This contribution sets out to pursue the investigation of a supersymmetric electrodynamics model with Lorentz-symmetry violation (LSV) manifested by a space-time unbalance in the propagation of the fermionic charged matter. Despite violation of Lorentz symmetry, the supersymmetry algebra is kept untouched. A superspace approach is then adopted to build up an 𝒩 = 1 \mathcal{N}=1 -supersymmetric Abelian gauge theory in presence of a Lorentz-violating background supermultiplet that accommodates the space-time asymmetry parameter of the charged matter. It is described, in this scenario, how the particular Lorentz-symmetry breaking, brought about by the fermionic matter, affects its (matter) scalar partners and the photon/photino that minimally couple to charged matter. From the (modified) Dirac, Klein-Gordon and Maxwell field equations, the corresponding dispersion relations are worked out to inspect and discuss the physical effects of the LSV Majorana fermion condensates that naturally emerge from the background supermultiplet. Finally, efforts are targeted to investigate the Gordon decomposition of the charged lepton electromagnetic current. This is carried out by iterating the (fermion and scalar) matter field equations, which points to an effective contribution to the electron’s electric dipole moment. This result allows us to attain an estimate of the pseudo-vector condensate of the (LSV) Majorana background fermion. Keywords: Lorentz-symmetry violation, supersymmetric QED, modified dispersion relations, electron’s EDM.

Introduction

Lorentz invariance stands as an essential cornerstone of Quantum Field Theory, manifesting as a fundamental symmetry within the framework of General Relativity and the Standard Model of Particle Physics.
However, in recent decades, the possibility that some spacetime symmetries could be violated at energies close to the Planck scale
(M_{\textrm{Planck}}\sim 10^{19}\,\textrm{GeV})
has arisen in the context of different approaches, such as, e.g., String Theory
[
1
,
2
]
, Loop Quantum Gravity
[
3
,
4
]

and Modified Electrodynamics
[
5
]
(see also ref.
[
6
]
for a general review on Lorentz violation). At the same time, in this high-energy regime, Supersymmetry (SUSY) is expected to keep unbroken
[
7
]
. In this way, it becomes pretty natural to wonder about the relationship between the Lorentz-symmetry violation (LSV) and SUSY breaking. Berger and Kostelecký were pioneers in proposing a method for incorporating Lorentz-breaking terms into a SUSY scenario; this can be accomplished by introducing the background anisotropies of LSV into the SUSY algebra
[
7
,
8
]
. Subsequently, a number of approaches to inspect supersymmetric extensions of Quantum Electrodynamics (QED) with LSV has emerged. These investigations have led to important phenomena such as vacuum birefringence
[
9
]
, as well as the manifestation of SUSY breaking that arises from the mass splitting of superpartners
[
10
]
. Alternatively to the original proposal presented by Berger and Kostelecký, the framework we adopt here to study LSV in a supersymmetric scenario consists in treating the Lorentz-breaking parameters as components of special (background) superfields, while SUSY algebra is kept unchanged. In this case, the central idea consists in seeking a supersymmetric formulation for the background tensor structures that constitute the spacetime anisotropies responsible for the LSV. This allows us to present a microscopic origin for the LSV parameters in terms of components of non-dynamical superfields, as it shall be subsequently described.

On the other hand, the possibility of LSV in other scenarios gives rise to interesting developments, e.g., in Condensed Matter Physics (CMP), where both SUSY and Lorentz invariance may appear as emergent symmetries.
The first indication of SUSY in CMP has come out from the study of a two-dimensional Ising model
[
11
]
, where an emergent SUSY becomes apparent near the critical point. In this context, the fundamental degrees of freedom may not exhibit SUSY, but an effective description via a supersymmetric action can be obtained from them. From this perspective, more recent works have contemplated scenarios of emergent SUSY in a
(2+1)
-dimensional system composed of a monolayer of carbon atoms arranged in a honeycomb lattice
[
12
,
13
,
14
]
. Moreover, such scenarios can also arise dynamically as pointed out, e.g., in refs.
[
15
,
16
,
17
,
18

. It should also be mentioned that there are systems in low-energy CMP where the emergent Lorentz-symmetry plays an important role; it is the case of the so-called Dirac and Weyl materials

and graphene
[

. At the same time, in these scenarios, the violation of this emergent symmetry has been extensively explored in refs.
[

37

39

.
In this regard, the interesting work
[
40

presents a comprehensive exploration of quasi-particle excitations in the band structures of Dirac and Weyl materials. According to the authors, the emergent Lorentz-symmetry features in these materials shed light on Lorentz violation within the Standard Model Extension (SME) framework
[

.

Considering this perspective, the efforts of the present contribution are motivated by the theoretical study published by Isobe and Nagaosa of quantum critical phenomena in the phase transition between trivial and topological insulators in
(3+1)
dimensions, which is described by a Dirac fermion coupled to the electromagnetic field
[
44
]
. In their model, LSV is realized by a constant parameter present only in the fermionic piece of the action. Our proposal consists in building up an N=1-supersymmetric
U(1)
gauge model as an extended version of the Isobe-Nagaosa’s paper with the LSV parameter no longer constant, but now taken to be space-time dependent. The main purpose is to understand how the LSV, initially manifested only in the fermionic sector, affects the sector of (charged) scalar partners in the gauge-invariant theory. For this, we shall adopt an alternative approach to implement LSV in connection with SUSY, as discussed in refs.
[
45
,
46
]
, in which LSV parameters are located in background superfields and they are therefore accompanied by background fermionic partners that condensate to give origin to bosonic LSV parameters.

In an interesting paper
[
47
]
, Katz and Shadmi contemplate the situation in which SUSY is broken together with Poincaré invariance, once a certain field acquires a Poincaré-violating (
x_{\mu}
-dependent) vacuum expectation value. This field yields a spontaneous (and, therefore, a soft) SUSY-breaking. A similar framework will be presented in details in the Subsection
3.3.1
of this work. And we shall see, in the scenario we study, that a fermion, partner of the background scalar that violates Poincaré symmetry, can form (bilinear) condensates that, in turn, break Lorentz symmetry.

The outline of this paper is as follows: In Sect.

These results shall be used, in what follows, to obtain the rest energy and group velocity for some particular cases. In Sect.

2
The extended Isobe-Nagaosa model

We start off from the following extended version of the model proposed by Isobe-Nagaosa (see ref.
[
48
]
for more details),

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}=i\bar{\Psi}\Big[\gamma^{0}\partial_{0}-\textrm{Re}(\xi)\gamma^{i}\partial_{i}\Big]\Psi-\dfrac{1}{2}\Big\{\partial_{i}\textrm{Im}\big[\xi(x)\big]\Big\}\bar{\Psi}\gamma^{i}\Psi-m\bar{\Psi}\Psi,

(1)

where
\Psi
is a Dirac fermion and
\xi
is a factor that breaks Lorentz-symmetry. In the extended model (
1
), we introduce a space-time dependence on the background parameter
\xi
, since, in a general situation, such a parameter may not be constant and/or homogeneous, and it would be pertinent to exploit consequences of the non-constancy and non-homogeneity. In order to guarantee the reality of the action associated with (
1
), it follows that the real part of
\xi
,
\textrm{Re}(\xi)
, must be a real constant parameter, while its imaginary part,
\textrm{Im}\big[\xi(x)\big]\equiv I(x)
, carries all the eventual space-time dependence of the
\xi
factor. The original Isobe-Nagaosa model
[
44
]
is rescued considering
I(x)
constant.

Before moving on, it is important to stress that, as it is explicit in the Isobe-Nagaosa’s topological insulator QED model, the extended version we investigate in what follows (
1
) also exhibits local U(1) invariance.

In what follows, it is convenient to introduce the covariant notation in the form

\displaystyle\tilde{\partial}_{\mu}

\displaystyle=(\partial_{0},\textrm{Re}(\xi)\partial_{i}),

(2)

\displaystyle v_{\mu}

\displaystyle=(0,-\partial_{i}I(x)).

(3)

Then, the Lagrangian (
1
) can be brought into the form below:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{i}{2}\bar{\Psi}\gamma^{\mu}(\tilde{\partial}_{\mu}\Psi)-\dfrac{i}{2}(\tilde{\partial}_{\mu}\bar{\Psi})\gamma^{\mu}\Psi+\dfrac{1}{2}v_{\mu}\bar{\Psi}\gamma^{\mu}\Psi-m\bar{\Psi}\Psi.

(4)

As a step further, considering a Dirac spinor in the Weyl representation, one can arrange the
\Psi
-components according to their chirality,

\displaystyle\Psi=\begin{pmatrix}\psi_{\alpha}\\
\bar{\chi}^{\dot{\alpha}}\end{pmatrix},

(5)

where the Van der Waerden notation has been adopted. Defining
\sigma^{\mu}=(I_{2},\sigma^{i})
and
\bar{\sigma}^{\mu}=(I_{2},-\sigma^{i})
, the
\gamma
-matrices in Weyl representation are written as

\displaystyle\gamma^{\mu}=\begin{pmatrix}0&\sigma^{\mu}\\
\bar{\sigma}^{\mu}&0\end{pmatrix}\;\;\;\;\;\textrm{and}\;\;\;\;\;\gamma_{5}=i\gamma^{0}\gamma^{1}\gamma^{2}\gamma^{3}=\begin{pmatrix}-I_{2}&0\\
0&I_{2}\end{pmatrix}.

(6)

Thus, the Lagrangian (
1
) acquires the form

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}=

\displaystyle\;\dfrac{i}{2}\left[\bar{\psi}\bar{\sigma}^{\mu}(\tilde{\partial}_{\mu}\psi)-(\tilde{\partial}_{\mu}\bar{\psi})\bar{\sigma}^{\mu}\psi+\bar{\chi}\bar{\sigma}^{\mu}(\tilde{\partial}_{\mu}\chi)-(\tilde{\partial}_{\mu}\bar{\chi})\bar{\sigma}^{\mu}\chi\right]

\displaystyle+\,\dfrac{1}{2}v_{\mu}(\bar{\psi}\bar{\sigma}^{\mu}\psi-\bar{\chi}\bar{\sigma}^{\mu}\chi)-m(\bar{\psi}\bar{\chi}+\chi\psi),

(7)

with two chiral sectors, left and right, described by the
\psi
- and
\chi
-spinors, respectively.

2.1
The SUSY-extended model in superspace

In order to incorporate SUSY in the framework of the extended fermionic Lagrangian (
2
), and to carry out its coupling with the photon and photino, we adopt an approach that keeps SUSY algebra intact. We call into question that, instead of modifying SUSY algebra to include the LSV parameters, the latter are rather accommodated as components of a background superfield. To implement this point of view, one should remember the definition of four-divergence operator,
\tilde{\partial}_{\mu}
, presented in (
2
) and define the position four-vector as follows:

\displaystyle\tilde{x}_{\mu}=\bigg(x_{0},-\dfrac{1}{\textrm{Re}(\xi)}x_{i}\bigg),

(8)

in such a way that
\tilde{\partial}_{\mu}\tilde{x}^{\nu}=\delta^{\nu}_{\mu}
. Then, we define the superspace coordinates, adopting the Salam-Strathdee formulation
[
49
]

(see, e.g., in ref.
[
50
]
for the textbook level introduction), as
\big\{\tilde{x}^{\mu};\,\theta_{\alpha},\,\bar{\theta}_{\dot{\alpha}}\big\}

with
\mu
being a space-time index and
\alpha=(1,2)
, as well as
\dot{\alpha}=(1,2)
, being indices of Grassmannian nature. That said, one can obtain the form of the SUSY generators,

\displaystyle Q_{\alpha}=-i\partial_{\alpha}-(\sigma^{\mu})_{\alpha\dot{\beta}}\bar{\theta}^{\dot{\beta}}\tilde{\partial}_{\mu},\;\;\;\;\;\bar{Q}_{\dot{\alpha}}=i\bar{\partial}_{\dot{\alpha}}+\theta^{\beta}(\sigma^{\mu})_{\beta\dot{\alpha}}\tilde{\partial}_{\mu},

(9)

and establish the anti-commutation relations

\displaystyle\left\{D_{\alpha},Q_{\beta}\right\}=\left\{\bar{D}_{\dot{\alpha}},\bar{Q}_{\dot{\beta}}\right\}=\left\{D_{\alpha},\bar{Q}_{\dot{\beta}}\right\}=\left\{\bar{D}_{\dot{\alpha}},Q_{\beta}\right\}=0,

(10)

with the SUSY covariant derivative operators given by

\displaystyle D_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\partial_{\alpha}+i(\sigma^{\mu})_{\alpha\dot{\beta}}\bar{\theta}^{\dot{\beta}}\tilde{\partial}_{\mu},

(11)

\displaystyle\bar{D}_{\dot{\alpha}}

\displaystyle=\bar{\partial}_{\dot{\alpha}}+i\theta^{\beta}(\sigma^{\mu})_{\beta\dot{\alpha}}\tilde{\partial}_{\mu}.

(12)

From (
9
), it is clear that the SUSY algebra is left untouched, as already mentioned.

3
\mathcal{N}=1
-supersymmetric
U(1)
gauge-field sector

In what follows, we shall apply the ideas presented in the previous Section to build up a supersymmetric version of QED. First, let us investigate the modifications the formulation of superspace, based on the fermionic matter sector, in the gauge field sector.

3.1
The Maxwell sector

Consider a real vector superfield,
A(\tilde{x},\theta,\bar{\theta})
, in the Wess-Zumino gauge
(see, e.g., the textbook in ref.
[
50
]
for more details),

\displaystyle A_{\textrm{WZ}}=i\theta^{2}\bar{\theta}\bar{\lambda}-i\bar{\theta}^{2}\theta\lambda+(\theta\sigma^{\mu}\bar{\theta})\tilde{a}_{\mu}+\dfrac{1}{2}\theta^{2}\bar{\theta}^{2}K,

(13)

where
\lambda
is the Weyl component of a Majorana spinor,
\tilde{a}_{\mu}
is the four-vector and
K
is an auxiliary field. To be consistent with the definitions of superspace and superfields we are considering,
\tilde{a}_{\mu}
must have the form

\displaystyle\tilde{a}_{\mu}=\left(A_{0},-\frac{1}{\textrm{Re}(\xi)}A_{i}\right).

(14)

Here, it is clear that the Lorentz violation in the Maxwell sector is signaled exclusively by the presence of
\textrm{Re}(\xi)
. Hence, we can expect that this sector is not affected by the space-time dependence of the background.

In this case, the generalization of the Abelian gauge condition for the modified electromagnetic four-potential is given by

\displaystyle\tilde{a}_{\mu}\rightarrow\tilde{a}^{\prime}_{\mu}=\tilde{a}_{\mu}-\tilde{\partial}_{\mu}\big[2\textrm{Im}(z)\big],

(15)

with
\textrm{Im}(z)
standing for the imaginary part of the complex scalar component of some chiral superfield.
The kinetic term for
\tilde{a}_{\mu}
can be generated by the contractions

\displaystyle W^{\alpha}W_{\alpha}\Big|_{\sim\theta^{2}}

\displaystyle=-\dfrac{1}{2}\mathcal{F}^{\mu\nu}\mathcal{F}_{\mu\nu}+\dfrac{i}{2}{{}^{*\!\!}\mathcal{F}^{\mu\nu}}\mathcal{F}_{\mu\nu}-2i\lambda\sigma^{\mu}\tilde{\partial}_{\mu}\bar{\lambda}+K^{2},

(16)

\displaystyle\bar{W}_{\dot{\alpha}}\bar{W}^{\dot{\alpha}}\Big|_{\sim\bar{\theta}^{2}}

\displaystyle=-\dfrac{1}{2}\mathcal{F}^{\mu\nu}\mathcal{F}_{\mu\nu}-\dfrac{i}{2}{{}^{*\!\!}\mathcal{F}^{\mu\nu}}\mathcal{F}_{\mu\nu}+2i(\tilde{\partial}_{\mu}\lambda)\sigma^{\mu}\bar{\lambda}+(K^{*})^{2},

(17)

where
\mathcal{F}_{\mu\nu}
is the electromagnetic field strength and
\lambda
is identified as the photino spinor field. In the expressions (
16
) and (
17
), we define the spinorial superfield,
W_{\alpha}=-\dfrac{1}{4}\bar{D}\bar{D}D_{\alpha}A_{\textrm{WS}}
, with
\bar{W}_{\dot{\alpha}}
representing its complex conjugate, and use the compact notations

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\mu\nu}=(\tilde{\partial}_{\mu}\tilde{a}_{\nu}-\tilde{\partial}_{\nu}\tilde{a}_{\mu}),\;\;\;\;\;{{}^{*\!\!}\mathcal{F}^{\mu\nu}}=\dfrac{1}{2}\varepsilon^{\mu\nu\kappa\rho}\mathcal{F}_{\kappa\rho}.

(18)

With all described above, the supersymmetric gauge field action reads as follows:

\displaystyle S_{\textrm{M}}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{4}\int d^{4}x\,\biggl\{\int d^{2}\theta\,W^{2}+\int d^{2}\bar{\theta}\,\bar{W}^{2}\biggr\}

(19)

or, in terms of the component fields,

\displaystyle S_{\textrm{M}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int d^{4}x\bigg\{-\dfrac{1}{2}\mathcal{F}^{2}_{\mu\nu}+i\Big[(\tilde{\partial}_{\mu}\bar{\lambda})\bar{\sigma}^{\mu}\lambda-\bar{\lambda}\bar{\sigma}^{\mu}\tilde{\partial}_{\mu}\lambda\Big]+|K|^{2}\bigg\}.

(20)

In Equation (
19
) we use the compact notations
W^{2}
and
\bar{W}^{2}
for the definitions (
16
) and (
17
), respectively.
It is important to notice that, in our case, the modified Maxwell sector is obtained from the matter sector as a consequence of SUSY. In Section
4
, we shall explicitly see the modifications in Maxwell’s equations due to the prescription (
14
).

3.2
The matter sector

Our next task is to supersymmetrize the matter sector based on the Lagrangian (
2
). This procedure will lead to the kinetic and mass terms for the scalar supersymmetric partners of
\psi
and
\chi
.

To have a supersymmetric version of the left sector in (
2
), we define a chiral superfield,

\displaystyle\Phi_{\textrm{L}}

\displaystyle=e^{i(\theta\sigma^{\mu}\bar{\theta})\tilde{\partial}_{\mu}}\left(\varphi+\sqrt{2}\theta\psi+\theta^{2}f\right)

\displaystyle=\varphi+\sqrt{2}\theta\psi+\theta^{2}f+i(\theta\sigma^{\mu}\bar{\theta})\tilde{\partial}_{\mu}\varphi-\frac{i}{\sqrt{2}}\theta^{2}(\tilde{\partial}_{\mu}\psi)\sigma^{\mu}\bar{\theta}-\dfrac{1}{4}\theta^{2}\bar{\theta}^{2}\widetilde{\square}\varphi,

(21)

and its anti-chiral associated superfield,

\displaystyle\bar{\Phi}_{\textrm{L}}

\displaystyle=\varphi^{*}+\sqrt{2}\bar{\theta}\bar{\psi}+\bar{\theta}^{2}f^{*}-i(\theta\sigma^{\mu}\bar{\theta})\tilde{\partial}_{\mu}\varphi^{*}+\frac{i}{\sqrt{2}}\bar{\theta}^{2}\theta\sigma^{\mu}\tilde{\partial}_{\mu}\bar{\psi}-\dfrac{1}{4}\theta^{2}\bar{\theta}^{2}\widetilde{\square}\varphi^{*},

(22)

where
\varphi
is a charged scalar field,
\psi
is the Weyl component of a Dirac spinor and
f
is an auxiliary field.
Similarly, for the right sector, we have

\displaystyle\bar{\Phi}_{\textrm{R}}

\displaystyle=s+\sqrt{2}\bar{\theta}\bar{\chi}+\bar{\theta}^{2}g-i(\theta\sigma^{\mu}\bar{\theta})\tilde{\partial}_{\mu}s+\frac{i}{\sqrt{2}}\bar{\theta}^{2}\theta\sigma^{\mu}\tilde{\partial}_{\mu}\bar{\chi}-\dfrac{1}{4}\theta^{2}\bar{\theta}^{2}\widetilde{\square}s,

(23)

\displaystyle\Phi_{\textrm{R}}

\displaystyle=s^{*}+\sqrt{2}\theta\chi+\theta^{2}g^{*}+i(\theta\sigma^{\mu}\bar{\theta})\tilde{\partial}_{\mu}s^{*}-\frac{i}{\sqrt{2}}\theta^{2}(\tilde{\partial}_{\mu}\chi)\sigma^{\mu}\bar{\theta}-\dfrac{1}{4}\theta^{2}\bar{\theta}^{2}\widetilde{\square}s^{*}.

(24)

Here,
s
,
\chi
and
g
are the right component fields analogous to
\varphi
,
\psi
and
f
in the left sector, respectively. It is important to notice that the introduction of two chiral superfields with independent fermionic components,
\psi
and
\chi
, is necessary in order to compose the (charged) Dirac fermion
\Psi
.
The mass terms arise from the combinations

\Phi_{\textrm{R}}\Phi_{\textrm{L}}\Big|_{\sim\theta^{2}}=s^{*}f+g^{*}\varphi-\chi\psi\;\;\;\textrm{and}\;\;\;\bar{\Phi}_{\textrm{L}}\bar{\Phi}_{\textrm{R}}\Big|_{\sim\bar{\theta}^{2}}=f^{*}s+\varphi^{*}g-\bar{\psi}\bar{\chi}.

(25)

To implement the Abelian gauge symmetry in the matter sector, we consider the transformation of chiral superfields under the global
U(1)
gauge transformation by the relations

\displaystyle\Phi_{\textrm{L}}^{\prime}=e^{ie\Lambda}\Phi_{\textrm{L}},\;\;\;\;\bar{\Phi}_{\textrm{L}}^{\prime}=e^{-ie\bar{\Lambda}}\bar{\Phi}_{\textrm{L}},

(26)

\displaystyle\bar{\Phi}_{\textrm{R}}^{\prime}=e^{ie\bar{\Lambda}}\bar{\Phi}_{\textrm{R}},\;\;\;\;\Phi_{\textrm{R}}^{\prime}=e^{-ie\Lambda}\Phi_{\textrm{R}},

(27)

where
e
is the
U(1)
charge of the superfield
\Phi
. Let us remark that the mass term in the supersymmetric action is automatically gauge invariant.

After some algebra, one can verify that the kinetics terms in (
2
) are generated by combinations like
\big(\Phi_{\textrm{L}}e^{eA_{\textrm{WZ}}}\bar{\Phi}_{\textrm{L}}+\Phi_{\textrm{R}}e^{-eA_{\textrm{WZ}}}\bar{\Phi}_{\textrm{R}}\big)\big|_{\sim\theta^{2}\bar{\theta}^{2}}
.

3.3
The
v_{\mu}\bar{\Psi}\gamma^{\mu}\Psi
-term

The less immediate structure to supersymmetrize is the term

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{2}v_{\mu}\left(\bar{\psi}\bar{\sigma}^{\mu}\psi-\bar{\chi}\bar{\sigma}^{\mu}\chi\right).

(28)

The central point of our approach is to consider LSV in a supersymmetric scenario. The background supermultiplet which accommodates the Lorentz-symmetry violating parameters realizes the (explicit) breaking of Lorentz symmetry and, contemporarily, induces spontaneous SUSY breaking
[
51
,
52
,
53
]
.

We have actually three alternatives to accomplish this program:
1)
to introduce an extra chiral-superfield and then identify
v_{\mu}
as four-gradient of a (background) scalar;
2)
to define a vector superfield and consider
v_{\mu}
as a genuine four-vector;
3)
to introduce the LSV four-vector
v_{\mu}
by means of an explicit breaking of SUSY, so that not only Lorentz symmetry but also SUSY be also explicitly broken. In this case, the background four-vector would not have background fermionic partners. The latter give rise to bilinears that may be identified with background vector or tensors. Since we are interested in considering this situation, we are going to discard path (3).

From a conceptual point of view, we can consider that the anisotropy arises from some kind of more fundamental field that may condensate to form the LSV background. As we argue, it is important to look for a microscopic origin for the anisotropy and, in this case, the first approach becomes more natural, since the background field does not appear by itself; contrary, it is derived from some more fundamental object; in our case, a scalar, as we shall soon see. Let us remember that, in general, it is the scalars that trigger transitions between different phases of the primordial Universe. Hence, they may have played an important role in the formation of anisotropic structures during the evolution of the Universe. These are motivations that support our choice of the background as a chiral superfield and not a four-vector supermultiplet.

In what follows, we shall adopt the first scenario. For completeness, though we are not going to adopt the alternative (2), we cast in Appendix
A
the results obtained from the alternative approach, i.e., the case of a vector superfield.

3.3.1
Background chiral superfield

Let us introduce an additional chiral superfield
\Omega
, which is gauge invariant from the perspective of the transformation in (
26
) and (
27
). The
\theta
-expansion of
\Omega
reads

\displaystyle\Omega

\displaystyle=\omega+\sqrt{2}\theta\zeta+\theta^{2}h+i(\theta\sigma^{\mu}\bar{\theta})\tilde{\partial}_{\mu}\omega-\frac{i}{\sqrt{2}}\theta^{2}(\tilde{\partial}_{\mu}\zeta)\sigma^{\mu}\bar{\theta}-\dfrac{1}{4}\theta^{2}\bar{\theta}^{2}\widetilde{\square}\omega,

(29)

where
\omega
is a scalar field,
\zeta
is the Weyl component of a Majorana spinor and

is an auxiliary field. One should also notice that, differently from the physical propagating superfields, the background superfield
\Omega

The products of superfields which can reproduce (

\displaystyle(\Omega+\bar{\Omega})\Phi_{\textrm{L}}e^{eA_{\textrm{WZ}}}\bar{\Phi}_{\textrm{L}}\Big|_{\sim\theta^{2}\bar{\theta}^{2}}=

\displaystyle-\frac{i}{2}\tilde{\partial}_{\mu}(\omega-\omega^{*})\bar{\psi}\bar{\sigma}^{\mu}\psi+(\omega+\omega^{*})\bigg\{\frac{i}{2}\Big[(\tilde{\partial}_{\mu}\bar{\psi})\bar{\sigma}^{\mu}\psi-\bar{\psi}\bar{\sigma}^{\mu}\tilde{\partial}_{\mu}\psi\Big]

\displaystyle-\dfrac{1}{2}\Big[(\widetilde{\square}\varphi^{*})\varphi+\varphi^{*}\widetilde{\square}\varphi\Big]+f^{*}f\bigg\}+f^{*}(h\varphi-\zeta\psi)+f(h^{*}\varphi^{*}-\bar{\zeta}\bar{\psi})

\displaystyle+\dfrac{1}{2}\tilde{\partial}_{\mu}(\omega-\omega^{*})\Big[(\tilde{\partial}^{\mu}\varphi^{*})\varphi-\varphi^{*}\tilde{\partial}^{\mu}\varphi\Big]+\frac{i}{2}\Big[\varphi(\tilde{\partial}_{\mu}\bar{\psi})\bar{\sigma}^{\mu}\zeta-\varphi\bar{\psi}\bar{\sigma}^{\mu}\tilde{\partial}_{\mu}\zeta

\displaystyle-\bar{\psi}\bar{\sigma}^{\mu}\zeta\tilde{\partial}_{\mu}\varphi\Big]-\frac{i}{2}\Big[\varphi^{*}\bar{\zeta}\bar{\sigma}^{\mu}\tilde{\partial}_{\mu}\psi-\varphi^{*}(\tilde{\partial}_{\mu}\bar{\zeta})\bar{\sigma}^{\mu}\psi-\bar{\zeta}\bar{\sigma}^{\mu}\psi\tilde{\partial}_{\mu}\varphi^{*}\Big]

\displaystyle-\frac{e(\omega+\omega^{*})}{2}\bigg\{i\sqrt{2}(\bar{\lambda}\bar{\psi}\varphi-\lambda\psi\varphi^{*})-i(\varphi^{*}\tilde{\partial}^{\mu}\varphi-\varphi\tilde{\partial}^{\mu}\varphi^{*})\tilde{a}_{\mu}

\displaystyle+(\bar{\psi}\bar{\sigma}^{\mu}\psi)\tilde{a}_{\mu}-\bigg(K+\frac{e}{2}\tilde{a}^{2}\bigg)\varphi^{*}\varphi\bigg\}+\frac{e}{2}\bigg\{i\tilde{\partial}^{\mu}(\omega-\omega^{*})\,\varphi^{*}\varphi\,\tilde{a}_{\mu}

\displaystyle-\big[(\bar{\psi}\bar{\sigma}^{\mu}\zeta)\varphi+(\bar{\zeta}\bar{\sigma}^{\mu}\psi)\varphi^{*}\big]\tilde{a}_{\mu}-i\sqrt{2}(\bar{\lambda}\bar{\zeta}-\lambda\zeta)\varphi^{*}\varphi\bigg\},

(30)

and an analog structure working to reproduce the right sector.

Choosing the scalar component
\omega
of the background supermultiplet
\Omega
as it is done in ref.
[
45
]
,

(\omega+\omega^{*})=0\;\;\;\textrm{and}\;\;\;(\omega-\omega^{*})=2i\varpi,

(31)

with
\varpi=v_{\nu}\tilde{x}^{\nu}
, we must impose that
\tilde{\partial}_{\mu}v_{\nu}=0
, i.e., that the imaginary part of parameter
\xi
in Equation (
3
) has an only linear dependence on the coordinates.

At this point, we again call into question the paper by Katz and Shadmi
[
47
]
, where an
x_{\mu}
-dependent scalar field violates Poincaré symmetry and, as a consequence, softly breaks SUSY. This is exactly what is happening above, where the imaginary part of the
\omega
-background field is taken linearly dependent on
\tilde{x}_{\mu}
. Actually, this field plays the role of the spurion considered in ref.
[
47
]
. In due time, we should mention the paper by Nibbelink and Pospelov
[
54
]
, where the authors, classify LSV operators compatible with exact SUSY, restricting their analysis to vector and tensor backgrounds, and show that LSV operators in the context of the MSSM must have at least dimension five; they are therefore Planck mass-suppressed. This is not what we are doing here, since we are not based on exact SUSY; contrary, we associate a soft (spontaneous) SUSY breaking with Poincaré symmetry violation. We then justify that we have LSV operators with dimensions less than five because we are not bound to considering exact SUSY. As already anticipated in the Introduction, the
\zeta
-fermion given above, partner of the spurion in the
\Omega
-superfield, will be responsible for condensates that realize LSV contributions to the matter action.

From these considerations, we can write our supersymmetric model as follows below:

\displaystyle S=

\displaystyle\,\int d^{4}x\,\bigg\{\int d^{2}\theta d^{2}\bar{\theta}\,\bigg[\bar{\Phi}_{\textrm{L}}e^{eA_{\textrm{WZ}}}\Phi_{\textrm{L}}+\bar{\Phi}_{\textrm{R}}e^{-eA_{\textrm{WZ}}}\Phi_{\textrm{R}}+\frac{1}{2}(\Omega+\bar{\Omega})\big(\bar{\Phi}_{\textrm{L}}e^{eA_{\textrm{WZ}}}\Phi_{\textrm{L}}

\displaystyle-\,\bar{\Phi}_{\textrm{R}}e^{-eA_{\textrm{WZ}}}\Phi_{\textrm{R}}\big)\bigg]+\frac{1}{4}\bigg(\int d^{2}\theta\,W^{2}+c.c.\bigg)+m\bigg(\int d^{2}\theta\,\Phi_{\textrm{R}}\Phi_{\textrm{L}}+c.c.\bigg)\bigg\}.

(32)

Using the field equations for the auxiliary fields, (
3.3.1
) can be formulated in terms of the physical fields:

\displaystyle S_{1}=

\displaystyle\,\int d^{4}x\,\bigg\{-\frac{1}{4}\mathcal{F}^{2}_{\mu\nu}+\frac{i}{2}\Big[(\tilde{\partial}_{\mu}\bar{\lambda})\bar{\sigma}^{\mu}\lambda-\bar{\lambda}\bar{\sigma}^{\mu}\tilde{\partial}_{\mu}\lambda\Big]-\dfrac{i}{2}\Big[\bar{\psi}\bar{\sigma}^{\mu}\tilde{\partial}_{\mu}\psi-(\tilde{\partial}_{\mu}\bar{\psi})\bar{\sigma}^{\mu}\psi+\bar{\chi}\bar{\sigma}^{\mu}\tilde{\partial}_{\mu}\chi

\displaystyle-\,(\tilde{\partial}_{\mu}\bar{\chi})\bar{\sigma}^{\mu}\chi\Big]+\dfrac{1}{2}v_{\mu}(\bar{\psi}\bar{\sigma}^{\mu}\psi-\bar{\chi}\bar{\sigma}^{\mu}\chi)-m(\bar{\psi}\bar{\chi}+\chi\psi)+\Big[(\tilde{\partial}^{\mu}\varphi^{*})\tilde{\partial}_{\mu}\varphi+(\tilde{\partial}^{\mu}s^{*})\tilde{\partial}_{\mu}s\Big]

\displaystyle+\,\dfrac{i}{2}v_{\mu}\Big[(\tilde{\partial}^{\mu}\varphi^{*})\varphi-\varphi^{*}\tilde{\partial}^{\mu}\varphi+(\tilde{\partial}^{\mu}s^{*})s-s^{*}\tilde{\partial}^{\mu}s\Big]-\bigg(m^{2}+\frac{1}{4}h^{*}h\bigg)(\varphi^{*}\varphi+s^{*}s)

\displaystyle+\,\frac{i}{2}\Big[\varphi(\tilde{\partial}_{\mu}\bar{\psi})\bar{\sigma}^{\mu}\zeta-\varphi^{*}\bar{\zeta}\bar{\sigma}^{\mu}\tilde{\partial}_{\mu}\psi+s\bar{\zeta}\bar{\sigma}^{\mu}\tilde{\partial}_{\mu}\chi-s^{*}(\tilde{\partial}_{\mu}\bar{\chi})\bar{\sigma}^{\mu}\zeta\Big]-\frac{1}{2}\bigg[\varphi\bigg(m\,\zeta\chi-\frac{1}{2}h\bar{\zeta}\bar{\psi}\bigg)

\displaystyle+\,\varphi^{*}\bigg(m\,\bar{\zeta}\bar{\chi}-\frac{1}{2}h^{*}\zeta\psi\bigg)-s\bigg(m\,\bar{\zeta}\bar{\psi}+\frac{1}{2}h^{*}\zeta\chi\bigg)-s^{*}\bigg(m\,\zeta\psi+\frac{1}{2}h\bar{\zeta}\bar{\chi}\bigg)\bigg]

\displaystyle-\,\frac{1}{4}\Big(\bar{\zeta}\bar{\psi}\zeta\psi+\bar{\zeta}\bar{\chi}\zeta\chi\Big)-\frac{e}{2}\Big[i\sqrt{2}(\varphi\bar{\lambda}\bar{\psi}-\varphi^{*}\lambda\psi+s\lambda\chi-s^{*}\bar{\lambda}\bar{\chi})+(\bar{\psi}\bar{\sigma}^{\mu}\psi-\bar{\chi}\bar{\sigma}^{\mu}\chi)\tilde{a}_{\mu}

\displaystyle+\,i(\varphi\,\tilde{\partial}^{\mu}\varphi^{*}-\varphi^{*}\tilde{\partial}^{\mu}\varphi+s\,\tilde{\partial}^{\mu}s^{*}-s^{*}\tilde{\partial}^{\mu}s)\tilde{a}_{\mu}\Big]-\frac{e}{4}\Big[(\bar{\psi}\bar{\sigma}^{\mu}\zeta)\varphi+(\bar{\zeta}\bar{\sigma}^{\mu}\psi)\varphi^{*}+(\bar{\chi}\bar{\sigma}^{\mu}\zeta)s^{*}

\displaystyle+\,(\bar{\zeta}\bar{\sigma}^{\mu}\chi)s\Big]\tilde{a}_{\mu}-\frac{e}{2}(\varphi^{*}\varphi+s^{*}s)\bigg[v^{\mu}\tilde{a}_{\mu}+\frac{i}{\sqrt{2}}(\bar{\lambda}\bar{\zeta}-\zeta\lambda)-\frac{e}{2}\tilde{a}^{2}\bigg]

\displaystyle-\,\frac{e^{2}}{8}(\varphi^{*}\varphi-s^{*}s)^{2}\bigg\}.

(33)

Despite the lengthy expression in
S_{1}
, it is immediate to identify the fermionic model (
1
) in the first two lines. In order to speed up the analysis of the contributions arising from the presence of the background, we group the terms according to sectors.
It proves much more useful to rewrite the result (
3.3.1
) in terms of 4-component spinors.

In the gauge sector, we have the following standard structure

\displaystyle S_{\textrm{gauge}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{4}\int d^{4}x\,\bigg\{\mathcal{F}^{2}_{\mu\nu}-i\Big[(\tilde{\partial}_{\mu}\bar{\Lambda})\gamma^{\mu}\Lambda-\bar{\Lambda}\gamma^{\mu}\tilde{\partial}_{\mu}\Lambda\Big]\bigg\},

(34)

where the gaugino (photino) is a Majorana fermion written as

\displaystyle\Lambda=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_{\alpha}\\
\bar{\lambda}^{\dot{\alpha}}\end{pmatrix}.

(35)

It should be recalled that that the background component-fields are not present in the photon/photino sector, in contrast to other scenarios with LSV, such as, e.g., the supersymmetric extensions of the Carroll-Field-Jackiw model of refs.
[
45
,
55
]
. The reason for this is that, in our case, the gauge sector is affected only by the real part of the background parameter
\xi
. In fact, the modifications in the gauge sector, induced by the background, only manifest as “dilations” in Maxwell’s equations, as we shall see in the next Section.

Using (
5
) and defining the covariant derivative
\tilde{D}_{\mu}\equiv\tilde{\partial}_{\mu}-\dfrac{ie}{2}\tilde{a}_{\mu}
, we can organize the fermionic sector, including the gauge coupling field, in the form

\displaystyle S_{\textrm{fermion}}=

\displaystyle\,\int d^{4}x\,\bigg\{\dfrac{i}{2}\Big[(\tilde{D}^{*}_{\mu}\bar{\Psi})\gamma^{\mu}\Psi-\bar{\Psi}\gamma^{\mu}\tilde{D}_{\mu}\Psi\Big]+\dfrac{1}{2}v_{\mu}(\bar{\Psi}\gamma^{\mu}\Psi)-m\bar{\Psi}\Psi

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{16}(\bar{Z}\gamma_{\mu}\gamma_{5}Z)(\bar{\Psi}\gamma^{\mu}\gamma_{5}\Psi)\bigg\},

(36)

with the Majorana spinor
Z
(the background fermion), given by

\displaystyle Z=\begin{pmatrix}\zeta_{\alpha}\\
\bar{\zeta}^{\dot{\alpha}}\end{pmatrix}.

(37)

The term with dependence on the background fermionic condensate
\bar{Z}\gamma_{\mu}\gamma_{5}Z
that appears in the fermionic sector is an additional contribution to the mass of the Dirac spinor
\Psi
.
In Equation (
29
), in defining the
\Omega
-superfield, the component fields

\omega,\,\zeta
and
h
are space-time dependent. However, since
\Omega
is non-dynamical (it is a background superfield), we are allowed to
fix its components. Indeed, in Equation (
31
), by taking
\textrm{Re}(\omega)=0
and

\textrm{Im}(\omega)=v^{\mu}\tilde{x}_{\mu}
,
\zeta
and
h
are taken to be constant component fields,
and this choice is compatible with the SUSY transformations
of the component fields. Therefore, the
Z
-bilinear is constant, so that
it is legitimate to state that it is a contribution to the mass-like

\bar{\Psi}\gamma^{\mu}\gamma_{5}\Psi
-term.
It is interesting to notice that this correction can be identified as the coefficient
b_{\mu}
in one of the CPT-odd terms introduced in the SME
[
41
]
in order to implement the violation of the Lorentz symmetry.

The (gauge-invariant) sector of matter scalar partners reads as given below:

\displaystyle S_{\textrm{scalar}}=

\displaystyle\,\int d^{4}x\,\bigg\{(\tilde{D}^{\mu}\varphi^{*})\tilde{D}_{\mu}\varphi+(\tilde{D}^{\mu}s^{*})\tilde{D}_{\mu}s-iv_{\mu}\big(\varphi^{*}\tilde{D}^{\mu}\varphi+s^{*}\tilde{D}^{\mu}s\big)

\displaystyle-\bigg(m^{2}+\frac{1}{4}h^{*}h\bigg)(\varphi^{*}\varphi+s^{*}s)\bigg\}.

(38)

As in the fermionic sector, there is a correction to the scalar masses,
\varphi
and
s

h^{*}h
. This non-degeneracy in the mass spectrum indicates a SUSY breaking due to the presence of the background. Furthermore, there is also a contribution of the background vector,
v_{\mu}
, to the dynamic part of the scalar sector. On the other hand, in the fermionic case,
v_{\mu}
only affects the mass term, as we can see in the first line of (
3.3.1
).

Finally, we have the gaugino and matter self-interaction contributions to the Lagrangian:

\displaystyle S_{\textrm{mixing$+$int}}=

\displaystyle-\int d^{4}x\,\bigg\{\frac{ie}{\sqrt{2}}\Big[\bar{\Psi}(\varphi\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{R}}+s\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{L}})\Lambda-\bar{\Lambda}(\varphi^{*}\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{L}}+s^{*}\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{R}})\Psi\Big]

\displaystyle+\,\frac{i}{2}\Big[\bar{Z}\gamma^{\mu}(\varphi^{*}\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{L}}-s^{*}\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{R}})\tilde{D}_{\mu}\Psi-(\tilde{D}^{*}_{\mu}\bar{\Psi})\gamma^{\mu}(\varphi\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{L}}-s\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{R}})Z\Big]

\displaystyle+\,\frac{1}{2}\bar{\Psi}\bigg[\bigg(m\varphi-\frac{1}{2}h^{*}s\bigg)\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{L}}-\bigg(ms+\frac{1}{2}h\varphi\bigg)\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{R}}\bigg]Z

\displaystyle+\,\frac{1}{2}\bar{Z}\bigg[\bigg(m\varphi^{*}-\frac{1}{2}hs^{*}\bigg)\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{R}}-\bigg(ms^{*}+\frac{1}{2}h^{*}\varphi^{*}\bigg)\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{L}}\bigg]\Psi

\displaystyle+\,\frac{e}{2}\bigg[\frac{i}{2\sqrt{2}}(\bar{\Lambda}\gamma_{5}Z+\bar{Z}\gamma_{5}\Lambda)\bigg](\varphi^{*}\varphi+s^{*}s)+\frac{e^{2}}{8}(\varphi^{*}\varphi-s^{*}s)^{2}\bigg\},

(39)

with the parity operators,

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{R}}=\frac{I_{4}+\gamma_{5}}{2}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&I_{2}\end{pmatrix}\;\;\;\;\;\textrm{and}\;\;\;\;\;\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{L}}=\frac{I_{4}-\gamma_{5}}{2}=\begin{pmatrix}I_{2}&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}.

(40)

It is worth mentioning that the new couplings induced by the background fermion,
Z
, except for those involving the degrees of freedom of the gauge sector (

and

4
Field equations and dispersion relations

With the supersymmetric action (
3.3.1
) in hand, we proceed, in this Section, to a detailed analysis of the field equations in both the matter and gauge sectors. Subsequently, in each case, we shall derive the modified dispersion relations within this framework, aiming to understand the implications of LSV in connection with SUSY.

4.1
The modified Maxwell’s equations

The modified Maxwell’s equations in the vacuum come from the
\mathcal{F}^{2}_{\mu\nu}
part of the action (
34
),

\displaystyle\tilde{\partial}_{\mu}\mathcal{F}^{\mu\nu}=0.

(41)

Let us recall that
\mathcal{F}_{\mu\nu}=(\tilde{\partial}_{\mu}\tilde{a}_{\nu}-\tilde{\partial}_{\nu}\tilde{a}_{\mu})
. From (
41
) we obtain the modified version of the Gauss’s law for the electric field and the Ampère-Maxwell equation, in the simplified form,

\displaystyle\textrm{Re}(\xi)\nabla\cdot\mathbfcal{E}=0,

(42)

\displaystyle\textrm{Re}(\xi)\nabla\times\mathbfcal{B}=\partial_{t}\mathbfcal{E},

(43)

where, in an analogy with the usual electrodynamics, we define an effective electric field given by
\mathbfcal{E}=-\textrm{Re}(\xi)\nabla\phi-\partial_{t}\textbf{A}/\textrm{Re}(\xi)
, while the magnetic field remains unchanged in this scenario, i.e.,
\mathbfcal{B}=\nabla\times\textbf{A}
.

On the other hand, from the Bianchi identity,

\displaystyle\tilde{\partial}_{\mu}{{}^{*\!\!}\mathcal{F}_{\mu\nu}}=0,\;\;\;\;\;{{}^{*\!\!}\mathcal{F}_{\mu\nu}}=\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon^{\mu\nu\alpha\beta}\mathcal{F}_{\alpha\beta}

(44)

there follow the modified Faraday-Lenz (for
\nu=i
) and the Gauss’s law for the magnetic field (for
\nu=0
):

\displaystyle\textrm{Re}(\xi)\nabla\times\mathbfcal{E}=-\partial_{t}\mathbfcal{B},

(45)

\displaystyle\textrm{Re}(\xi)\nabla\cdot\mathbfcal{B}=0.

(46)

Following the standard procedure for obtaining the wave equation, let us apply a time derivative on (
45
), and use the Equations (
42
) and (
43
). For non-trivial field configurations, the photonic scattering relation arises from the requirement that

\omega=\pm\textrm{Re}(\xi)|\textbf{k}|.

(47)

For the photino, the field equation is
\gamma^{\mu}\tilde{\partial}_{\mu}\Lambda=0
, the dispersion relation has the same form as (
47
).

One of the main consequences of SUSY is the mass degeneracy between a particle and its corresponding superpartner. Since the photon and photino share the same modified dispersion relation, it is immediate to verify that they are both massless. This is simply a confirmation that in the gauge sector there is no SUSY breaking.

4.2
The modified Dirac and Klein-Gordon equations

As we have seen in Section
3.3
, due to the fact that the SUSY background is not dynamic, the mixing terms involving the spinors,
\Psi
and
Z

\displaystyle S_{\textrm{Dirac}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int d^{4}x\,\bigg\{\dfrac{i}{2}\Big[(\tilde{\partial}_{\mu}\bar{\Psi})\gamma^{\mu}\Psi-\bar{\Psi}\gamma^{\mu}\tilde{\partial}_{\mu}\Psi\Big]+\dfrac{1}{2}v_{\mu}(\bar{\Psi}\gamma^{\mu}\Psi)-m\bar{\Psi}\Psi-\frac{1}{16}(\bar{\Psi}\gamma^{\mu}\gamma_{5}\Psi)(\bar{Z}\gamma_{\mu}\gamma_{5}Z)

(48)

\displaystyle-\frac{i}{2}\Big[\bar{Z}\gamma^{\mu}(\varphi^{*}\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{L}}-s^{*}\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{R}})\tilde{\partial}_{\mu}\Psi-(\tilde{\partial}_{\mu}\bar{\Psi})\gamma^{\mu}(\varphi\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{L}}-s\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{R}})Z\Big]-\frac{1}{2}\bar{\Psi}\bigg[\bigg(m\varphi-\frac{1}{2}h^{*}s\bigg)\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{L}}

\displaystyle-\bigg(ms+\frac{1}{2}h\varphi\bigg)\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{R}}\bigg]Z-\frac{1}{2}\bar{Z}\bigg[\bigg(m\varphi^{*}-\frac{1}{2}hs^{*}\bigg)\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{R}}-\bigg(ms^{*}+\frac{1}{2}h^{*}\varphi^{*}\bigg)\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{L}}\bigg]\Psi\bigg\}.

The modified Dirac equation in momentum space is given by

\displaystyle\left[(\tilde{p}-\zeta)_{\mu}\gamma^{\mu}+m+R_{\mu}\gamma^{\mu}\gamma_{5}\right]\Psi_{0}=

\displaystyle-\dfrac{1}{2}\bigg\{\left[(\tilde{p}_{\mu}\gamma^{\mu}+m)\varphi_{0}-\dfrac{1}{2}h^{*}s_{0}\right]\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{L}}

\displaystyle-\left[(\tilde{p}_{\mu}\gamma^{\mu}+m)s_{0}+\dfrac{1}{2}h\varphi_{0}\right]\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{R}}\bigg\}Z,

(49)

where
\zeta_{\mu}=\dfrac{1}{2}v_{\mu}
and

\xi

v_{\mu}

Similarly, the action for the matter scalars reads as below:

\displaystyle S_{\textrm{KG}}=

\displaystyle\,\int d^{4}x\,\bigg\{(\tilde{\partial}^{\mu}\varphi^{*})\tilde{\partial}_{\mu}\varphi+(\tilde{\partial}^{\mu}s^{*})\tilde{\partial}_{\mu}s+\dfrac{i}{2}v_{\mu}\Big[(\tilde{\partial}^{\mu}\varphi^{*})\varphi-\varphi^{*}\tilde{\partial}^{\mu}\varphi+(\tilde{\partial}^{\mu}s^{*})s-s^{*}\tilde{\partial}^{\mu}s\Big]

\displaystyle-\bigg(m^{2}+\frac{1}{4}|h|^{2}\bigg)(\varphi^{*}\varphi+s^{*}s)-\frac{i}{2}\Big[\bar{Z}\gamma^{\mu}(\varphi^{*}\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{L}}-s^{*}\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{R}})\tilde{\partial}_{\mu}\Psi-(\tilde{\partial}_{\mu}\bar{\Psi})\gamma^{\mu}(\varphi\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{L}}-s\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{R}})Z\Big]

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\bar{\Psi}\bigg[\bigg(m\varphi-\frac{1}{2}h^{*}s\bigg)\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{L}}-\bigg(ms+\frac{1}{2}h\varphi\bigg)\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{R}}\bigg]Z-\frac{1}{2}\bar{Z}\bigg[\bigg(m\varphi^{*}-\frac{1}{2}hs^{*}\bigg)\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{R}}

\displaystyle-\bigg(ms^{*}+\frac{1}{2}h^{*}\varphi^{*}\bigg)\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{L}}\bigg]\Psi\bigg\},

(50)

which gives the following modified Klein-Gordon equations:

\displaystyle\Theta\varphi_{0}=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\bar{Z}\bigg[\bigg(\tilde{p}_{\mu}\gamma^{\mu}-\frac{1}{2}h^{*}\bigg)\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{L}}+m\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{R}}\bigg]\Psi_{0},

(51)

\displaystyle\Theta s_{0}=

\displaystyle\,\frac{1}{2}\bar{Z}\bigg[\bigg(\tilde{p}_{\mu}\gamma^{\mu}+\frac{1}{2}h\bigg)\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{R}}+m\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{L}}\bigg]\Psi_{0},

As expected from our analysis of the mixing part (

4.3
The matter sector and the associated dispersion relations

To obtain the fermionic dispersion relation, let us first substitute the expressions for
\varphi_{0}
and
s_{0}
, i.e., (
51
) and (
52
), into the modified Dirac equation (
4.2
). The Fierz rearrangement,

\displaystyle\left(\bar{Z}M\Psi_{0}\right)NZ

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{4}\left[\left(\bar{Z}Z\right)NM+\left(\bar{Z}\gamma_{5}Z\right)N\gamma_{5}M+\left(\bar{Z}\gamma_{\alpha}\gamma_{5}Z\right)N\gamma^{\alpha}\gamma_{5}M\right]\Psi_{0},

(53)

where
M
and
N
are arbitrary 4×4 matrices, allows us to rewrite the pieces involving the mixing of
\Psi_{0}
and
Z
in terms of background fermionic condensates, which we will denote just as
\theta=\bar{Z}Z
,
\tau=\bar{Z}\gamma_{5}Z
and
C_{\alpha}=\bar{Z}\gamma_{\alpha}\gamma_{5}Z
. After some simplifications using the relations,
\gamma^{\mu}\gamma^{\alpha}\gamma^{\nu}=\eta^{\mu\alpha}\gamma^{\nu}+\eta^{\alpha\nu}\gamma^{\mu}-\eta^{\nu\mu}\gamma^{\alpha}-i\epsilon^{\mu\alpha\nu\beta}\gamma_{\beta}\gamma_{5}

and
\Sigma^{\mu\alpha}=\dfrac{i}{4}\big[\gamma^{\mu},\,\gamma^{\alpha}\big]
, the field equation for
\Psi_{0}
can be written in compact form as

\displaystyle\Big[(\tilde{p}-\zeta)_{\mu}\gamma^{\mu}+m-\mathcal{R}_{\mu}\gamma^{\mu}\gamma_{5}-W_{\mu\alpha}\Sigma^{\mu\alpha}\gamma_{5}\Big]\Psi_{0}

\displaystyle=0,

(54)

where we define the nonlocal structures

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\mu}=\frac{1}{16\Theta}\bigg[2\tilde{p}_{\mu}\tilde{p}_{\nu}C^{\nu}-2\bigg(\tilde{p}\cdot\zeta+m^{2}+\frac{1}{4}|h|^{2}\bigg)C_{\mu}+\tilde{p}_{\mu}(h^{*}-h)\tau-\frac{1}{2}\tilde{p}_{\mu}(h^{*}+h)\theta\bigg],

\displaystyle W_{\mu\alpha}=\frac{im}{4\Theta}\tilde{p}_{\mu}C_{\alpha}.

(55)

The derivation of dispersion relations for the matter sector is a more involved task. In the fermionic case, in particular, the presence of the last term,
\Sigma^{\mu\alpha}
, in Equation (
54
) makes the structure of the Dirac-equation operator more complicated to manipulate. Following the approach presented in ref.
[
41
]
, we adopt the standard squaring procedure and apply the Dirac operator with an opposite mass sign to the (
54

\mathcal{O}_{2\textrm{-der}}\Psi_{0}=0,

(56)

where the operator is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{2\textrm{-der}}=

\displaystyle\;(\tilde{p}-\zeta)^{2}-m^{2}-\mathcal{R}^{2}+\dfrac{1}{4}W_{\nu\beta}W_{\mu\alpha}\big[\left(\eta^{\nu\mu}\eta^{\beta\alpha}-\eta^{\nu\alpha}\eta^{\beta\mu}\right)+2i(\eta^{\nu\alpha}\Sigma^{\beta\mu}+\eta^{\beta\mu}\Sigma^{\nu\alpha})\big]

\displaystyle-i(\tilde{p}-\zeta-\mathcal{R})_{\nu}W_{\mu\alpha}(\eta^{\nu\mu}\gamma^{\alpha}-\eta^{\nu\alpha}\gamma^{\mu}).

(57)

To eliminate the off-diagonal terms in spinor space, we repeat the squaring procedure to Equation (
56
). Now, let us consider the operator
\mathcal{O}_{2\textrm{-der}}
with opposite sign for the off-diagonal piece, i.e.,
+i(\tilde{p}-\zeta-\mathcal{R})_{\kappa}W_{\lambda\rho}(\eta^{\kappa\lambda}\gamma^{\rho}-\eta^{\kappa\rho}\gamma^{\lambda})
. This procedure gives a fourth-order equation satisfied by each component of the Dirac spinor
\Psi_{0}
, such that (for non-trivial field configurations)

\displaystyle 0=

\displaystyle\;\tilde{p}^{4}-2\tilde{p}^{2}\bigg[4(\tilde{p}\cdot\zeta)+m^{2}-\zeta^{2}+\frac{1}{4}|h|^{2}\bigg]+8(\tilde{p}\cdot\zeta)\big[3(\tilde{p}\cdot\zeta)+m^{2}\big]

\displaystyle+m^{2}\bigg(m^{2}-2\zeta^{2}+\frac{1}{2}|h|^{2}\bigg),

(58)

where we neglect higher-order terms in the Lorentz-breaking parameters, since it is reasonable to consider them to be small (see, e.g., in refs.
[
56
,
41
]
for theoretical discussions). The result (
4.3
) is the most general expression up to second order in Lorentz breaking for the fermionic dispersion relation of model (
3.3.1
).
It is clear its non-dependence on the background fermionic condensates
\theta
,
\tau
and
C_{\alpha}
. Therefore, we can see that, up to second order in LSV, the explicit dependence on the background is manifested only through the auxiliary complex scalar
h
and vector
\zeta_{\mu}=\dfrac{1}{2}v_{\mu}
fields.
An interesting consequence of this is that the results obtained from (
4.3
) are automatically parity invariant.

In the case of scalar fields,
\varphi_{0}
and
s_{0}
, the right-hand side of their respective Equations, (
51

52

\theta
,
\tau
and
C_{\alpha}

\Psi_{0}
, which can then be substituted into the modified Klein-Gordon equations.
After some algebra, we get

\displaystyle\Theta\varphi_{0}=

\displaystyle\,\frac{1}{4\Xi}\big[X^{(1)}(\varphi_{0},s_{0})\theta+\mathcal{M}^{(1)}(\varphi_{0},s_{0})\tau+Y^{(1)}_{\alpha}(\varphi_{0},s_{0})C^{\alpha}\big],

(59)

\displaystyle\Theta s_{0}=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{4\Xi}\big[X^{(2)}(\varphi_{0},s_{0})\theta+\mathcal{M}^{(2)}(\varphi_{0},s_{0})\tau+Y^{(2)}_{\alpha}(\varphi_{0},s_{0})C^{\alpha}\big],

(60)

where
\Xi=\big[(\tilde{p}-\zeta)^{2}-m^{2}-R^{2}\big]^{2}+4(\tilde{p}-\zeta)^{2}R^{2}-4\big[(\tilde{p}-\zeta)\cdot R\big]^{2}
, and the explicit form of

\mathcal{M}
’s and

’s can be found in Appendix
B
.
Finally, from (
59
) and (
60

\displaystyle 0=

\displaystyle\;\tilde{p}^{4}-4\tilde{p}^{2}\bigg[3(\tilde{p}\cdot\zeta)+\frac{1}{2}m^{2}-\zeta^{2}+\frac{1}{8}|h|^{2}\bigg]+60(\tilde{p}\cdot\zeta)^{2}

\displaystyle+4m^{2}\bigg[3(\tilde{p}\cdot\zeta)+\dfrac{1}{32}(\tilde{p}\cdot C)+\frac{1}{4}m^{2}-\zeta^{2}+\frac{1}{8}|h|^{2}\bigg].

(61)

In contrast to the fermionic case, Equation (

), the modified dispersion relation for the scalar sector has a non-trivial dependence on the background fermionic condensate

, in addition to terms proportional to

and
v_{\mu}

Now, we are in a position to exploit the effects of this LSV background on the mass spectrum of the fermionic and scalar matter sectors. Let us take the rest frame, where the linear momentum is zero,
\bm{p}=0
, and the rest energy is nothing but the rest mass. From (

\displaystyle E_{0,\,\textrm{fermion}}=\pm\sqrt{m^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\big(\bm{v}^{2}+|h|^{2}\big)}.

(62)

In this scenario, the fermion in question does not allow the existence of tachyonic mass modes. Therefore, considering that LSV terms are very small compared to the mass scale of the particles, we can take the limit
m^{2}\rightarrow\infty
, which corresponds to the regime
m^{2}\gg\bm{v}^{2}
and
m^{2}\gg|h|^{2}
, in the positive solution of the spectra (
62

6
Conclusive Considerations and Perspectives

In this contribution, we have studied the implications of a Lorentz-violating background supermultiplet of an
\mathcal{N}=1
-supersymmetric
U(1)
gauge model that extends the topological insulator Isobe-Nagaosa’s QED model
[
44
]
. In our endeavor, we consider that the LSV parameters may have their microscopic origin traced back to a background SUSY multiplet, a chiral superfield. Instead of taking the LSV parameters for granted and extending the SUSY algebra, we keep the ordinary SUSY structure and assume the existence of the background mentioned above. Condensates of the fermionic component of the background superfield appear that are in the backstage of the scalar, pseudoscalar, and pseudovector objects that correspond to LSV parameters.

The modifications in the gauge sector that arise as a consequence of LSV in the fermionic matter consist of nothing but a dilation factor in Maxwell’s equations. Indeed, the photon-photino partners are influenced only by the real component of the Lorentz-violating parameter and, therefore, we do not observe any physically relevant modifications in this sector, at least initially. On the other hand, the scalar matter is modified in both its dynamic and mass terms. Furthermore, the fermionic sector also receives an additional contribution that is proportional to the background fermionic condensate,
\bar{Z}\gamma_{\mu}\gamma_{5}Z
. In this case, we believe that such a contribution arises due to the soft SUSY breaking induced by LSV. In this sense, it is interesting to recall that our model successfully reproduces the CPT-odd terms of the SME, with one of these terms appearing naturally as a direct consequence of SUSY breaking.

Considering the dispersion relations, we verify that, in the case of fermions, the terms proportional to the background fermionic condensates appear only as higher-order contributions in the Lorentz violation parameters. Therefore, by assuming that the latter are small compared to the mass
m
, such condensates do not provide relevant contributions to the rest energy nor to the fermionic group velocity. On the other hand, the scalar dispersion relation receives contribution from the background condensate
\bar{Z}\gamma_{\mu}\gamma_{5}Z
already in the second order.

Finally, our results from Section
5
indicate a possible connection between the EDM and the SUSY breaking induced by LSV. In this scenario, it is shown that SUSY matter scalars play a supportive role in the process of generating the EDM of the charged fermion.

The introduction of additional Lorentz-violating terms in the gauge sector, which induces significant modifications in the photon-photino sector, is a path to be followed as a step forward. The motivation to do that is to keep track of the interference between the effects of the different LSV terms present in the matter and the gauge sectors.

Furthermore, based on the theoretical framework developed here, we aim to explore potential applications to those condensed matter systems that exhibit (low-energy) SUSY and Lorentz symmetry as emergent phenomena in the dynamics of quasiparticle excitations. On the other hand, the modified photonic and electronic dispersion relations including both SUSY and LSV effects may be studied in connection with photon-photon, Breit-Wheeler and Compton scatterings which may be used to impose upper limits on the parameters with the help of astrophysical data, such as measurements of (non-)attenuation of gamma-rays from active galactic nuclei (AGNs), gamma-ray bursts (GRBs) and extra galactic background light (EBL). Also, we keep in mind that QED high-precision tests can also provide estimates and constraints on the parameters of the model.
