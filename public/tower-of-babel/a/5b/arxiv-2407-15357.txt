Lower bound for simulation cost of open quantum systems: Lipschitz continuity approach
Zhiyan Ding, Marius Junge, Philipp Schleich, Peixue Wu

Abstract
Simulating quantum dynamics is one of the most promising applications of quantum computers. While the upper bound of the simulation cost has been extensively studied through various quantum algorithms, much less work has focused on establishing the lower bound, particularly for the simulation of open quantum system dynamics. In this work, we present a general framework to calculate the lower bound for simulating a broad class of quantum Markov semigroups. Given a fixed accessible unitary set, we introduce the concept of convexified circuit depth to quantify the quantum simulation cost and analyze the necessary circuit depth to construct a quantum simulation scheme that achieves a specific order. Our framework can be applied to both unital and non-unital quantum dynamics, and the tightness of our lower bound technique is illustrated by showing that the upper and lower bounds coincide in several examples.

Introduction

Simulating physics was one of the primary motivations for the development of quantum computers, as initially suggested by
Manin (1980)
;
Benioff (1980)
;
Feynman (1982)
. The feasibility of such computations was first discussed in the context of the physical Church-Turing theorem
Deutsch (1985)
, which focuses on simulating closed quantum dynamics. In this direction, Hamiltonian simulation has long been considered the most promising application of quantum computers. Significant efforts have been dedicated to uncovering the potential of quantum computers for Hamiltonian evolution and developing efficient quantum algorithms. Several efficient quantum algorithms have been proposed for Hamiltonian evolution
Berry et al. (2007)
;
Berry et al. (2014)
;
Berry et al. (2015)
;
Low and Chuang (2019)
;
Gilyén et al. (2019)
;
Childs et al. (2021)
;
Childs and Wiebe (2012)
.

Recently, there has been increasing interest in using quantum computers to capture and simulate more general quantum dynamics, specifically open quantum system dynamics. Unlike closed quantum systems, open quantum systems involve interactions with an environment that affect the system during its evolution. Understanding and capturing the effect of the environment not only helps us track the evolution of physical systems but also serves as a new tool for state preparation, such as thermalizing quantum systems
Rall et al. (2023)
;
Chen et al. (2023)
;
Ding et al. (2024)
and preparing ground states
Ding et al. (2023)
. In addition, the concept of the “environment” can also represent the noise that occurs in current quantum devices. The ability to simulate noise may also serve as a verifier for existing experiments and as a testing platform for understanding various aspects of quantum devices. Along with increasing interest in open quantum systems, efficient simulation algorithms have been developed in this area. Similarly to the general argument for closed quantum systems,
Kliesch et al. (2011)
argue that quantum computers in a gate-based model representing pure states are sufficiently resourceful to efficiently simulate open-system dynamics as well. They refer to this as the dissipative quantum Church-Turing theorem.
Subsequently, several quantum algorithms have been developed for simulating the Lindblad quantum master equation
Kliesch et al. (2011)
;
Cleve and Wang (2017)
;
Li and Wang (2022)
;
Chen et al. (2023)
;
Ding et al. (2024)
;
Pocrnic et al. (2024)
.

Although the upper bound of quantum cost for simulating quantum dynamics has been well studied through various quantum algorithms under different oracle assumptions, much less work has been done on establishing the lower bound. Specifically, we ask:

Given a set of accessible unitary gates and a quantum dynamics
T_{t}
, what is the minimum number of gates (minimal circuit depth) needed to simulate
T_{t}
?

Studying the lower bound of circuit depth can not only help us understand the efficiency of our quantum simulation algorithms but also illustrate the inherent limitations of a predetermined accessible unitary gate set. The latter is crucial for understanding the capabilities of practical quantum computers, given the often limited availability of unitary gates.

In this work, our main contribution is that we present a general framework to provide a lower bound on the simulation cost of a broad class of quantum dynamics given by
T_{t}=\exp(tL)
. Here,

L(\rho)=-i[H,\rho]+\sum_{j=1}^{m}L_{V_{j}}(\rho),\quad L_{V}(\rho)=V\rho V^{*}-\frac{1}{2}(V^{*}V\rho+\rho V^{*}V)

In our analysis, In our analysis, we provide a general framework for calculating the necessary circuit depth of a quantum simulation scheme using a predetermined accessible unitary gate set that achieves a specific order. Detailed discussion of the main result can be found in the next section. Additionally, our results indicate a lower bound on the simulation cost with a fixed target time and precision. Our lower bound is established via a resource-dependent convexified gate length introduced in

After applying these gates, the ancilla qubits are traced out (decoding) to obtain the updated system qubits, concluding one iteration. This simulation framework closely mirrors the behavior of open physical quantum systems, where the use of ancilla qubits is analogous to the “environment”.

Related works and open questions

There is a great amount of different quantum algorithms have been proposed for Hamiltonian evolution
Berry et al. (2007)
;
Berry et al. (2014)
;
Berry et al. (2015)
;
Low and Chuang (2019)
;
Gilyén et al. (2019)
;
Childs et al. (2021)
;
Childs and Wiebe (2012)
, each assuming different oracle access to the Hamiltonian
H
. For example,
Berry et al. (2007)
assumes oracle access to the matrix entries of of the sparse Hamiltonian
H
and the quantum cost is defined by the number of queries to the matrix entries. Based on the higher order Trotter splitting and sparse matrix decomposition, they present an efficient algorithm to simulation
\exp(-itH)
with number of queries almost linearly in
t
and logarithmic in the number of qubit
n
. A notable result of nearly optimal algorithm is given in
Berry et al. (2015)
and the authors provide a linear in time lower bound for sparse Hamiltonian.

More recently, as the applications of open quantum systems continue to expand, more attention has been focused on their simulation, especially for the Lindblad quantum master equation
Kliesch et al. (2011)
;
Cleve and Wang (2017)
;
Li and Wang (2022)
;
Chen et al. (2023)
;
Ding et al. (2024)
;
Borras and Marvian (2024)
;
Chen et al. (2024)
;
Childs and Li (2016)
. Because open quantum dynamics are often non-unital and irreversible, their simulation becomes much more difficult than that of the Hamiltonian simulation. Although the quantum oracle assumptions and simulation methods vary across different works, the simulation of the Lindblad master equation often requires a similar encoding and decoding process by using additional ancilla qubits, such as in
Cleve and Wang (2017)
;
Chen et al. (2023)
;
Ding et al. (2024)
. The first-order encoding process was initially proposed by Cleve et al.
Cleve and Wang (2017)
, who also introduced a novel compression scheme and oblivious amplitude amplification to improve the scheme’s scaling to nearly optimal levels. Similar concepts are employed in
Chen et al. (2023)
, where the authors assume slightly different oracle access to manage a large number of jump operators. In
Ding et al. (2024)
, the authors present an alternative encoding process that achieves arbitrarily high-order accuracy but involves a more complex Hamiltonian simulation.

The lower bound analysis is only derived for some special cases. In
Berry et al. (2015)
;
Bu et al. (2024)
, the authors provide some lower bounds for cost of Hamiltonian simulation. In
(
Childs and Li, 2016
, Section 7)
, linear in time lower bound for certain single Lindblad generators are constructed – Theorem

in this paper captures that behavior as well. It is straightforward to extend our framework to more complex encoding and decoding processes, provided one has access to more sophisticated unitary gates and knows how to calculate the Lipschitz complexity of the corresponding quantum channels with the new resource set. We leave more concrete analysis when the coherent part exists for future research.

Preliminaries on simulation cost of quantum channels

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a Hilbert space and
\mathbb{B}(\mathcal{H})
be the space of linear bounded operators on
\mathcal{H}
. For any
X\in\mathbb{B}(\mathcal{H})
, we denote the Schatten
p
-norm
\|X\|_{p}:=[\Tr(|X|^p)]^{1/p}
for
p\in[1,\infty]
, where
\Tr
is the trace and
\|X\|_{\infty}
is the operator norm given by the largest singular value of
X
. In this paper,
\|X\|_{\infty}
is denoted as
\|X\|
for simplicity. For any linear map
\Phi:\mathbb{B}(\mathcal{H})\to\mathbb{B}(\mathcal{H})
, denote

\displaystyle\|\Phi\|_{p\to q}:=\sup_{\|X\|_{p}\leq 1}\|\Phi(X)\|_{q},\quad\|\Phi\|^{cb}_{p\to q}:=\sup_{X^{(n)}\in\mathbb{B}(\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n}):\|X^{(n)}\|_{p}\leq 1}\|(\Phi\otimes id_{n})(X^{(n)})\|_{q},

where
id_{n}:\mathbb{B}(\mathbb{C}^{n})\to\mathbb{B}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
is the identity map. In particular, the diamond norm of a linear map
\Phi:\mathbb{B}(\mathcal{H})\to\mathbb{B}(\mathcal{H})
is given by

\|\Phi\|_{\diamond}=\|\Phi\|^{cb}_{1\to 1}.

(2.1)

A quantum channel
\Phi:\mathbb{B}(\mathcal{H})\to\mathbb{B}(\mathcal{H})
is a linear map such that it is trace preserving and completely positive. For any unitary operator
u\in\mathbb{B}(\mathcal{H})
, we denote
{\mathrm{ad}}_{u}(\cdot)=u\cdot u^{*}
as the quantum channel induced by
u
. The notion of simulation cost of quantum channels with respect to a given unitary set
\mathcal{U}\subseteq\mathbb{B}(\mathcal{H})
is defined as

l^{\mathcal{U}}_{\delta}(\Phi):=\inf\left\{m\geq 1\hskip 2.84544pt|\hskip 2.84544pt\exists\mu^{m}\in\mathcal{P}(\uu^{m}):\left\|\Phi-\int_{u_{1},\cdots,u_{m}\in\uu}{\mathrm{ad}}_{u_{1}}\cdots{\mathrm{ad}}_{u_{m}}d\mu^{m}(u_{1},\cdots,u_{m})\right\|_{\diamond}<\delta\right\},

(2.2)

where
\mathcal{P}(\uu^{m})
is the set of all probability distributions on
\uu^{m}=\uu\times\cdots\times\uu
.

In this work, (
2.2
) is referred to as the convexified notion of circuit depth. In contrast, the traditional (approximate) circuit depth of a unitary
U
is defined as
Haferkamp et al. (2022)
;
Haferkamp (2023)

\text{depth}_{\delta}^{\uu}(U):=\inf\left\{m\geq 1\hskip 2.84544pt|\exists u_{1},\cdots,u_{m}\in\uu:\left\|U-u_{1}\cdots u_{m}\right\|<\delta\right\}.

(2.3)

Different from traditional circuit depth (
2.3
), the convexified notion (
2.2
) allows any kind of convex combination of
{\mathrm{ad}}_{u_{i}}
.

Although previous work focuses mainly on the depth of traditional circuits (
2.3
), similar convexified ideas have appeared in various quantum simulation algorithms before. For example, the qDrift protocol for Hamiltonian simulation,
U=e^{itH}
, where
H=\sum_{j=1}^{L}h_{j}H_{j}
, was introduced by the authors in
Campbell (2019)
. Unlike first-order trotter splitting
U\approx\left(\Pi^{L}_{j=1}\exp(itH_j/N)\right)^{N}+O(t^{2}/N)
, qDrift approximates
U
using random Hamiltonian evolution:

U\approx\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\left(\Pi^{N}_{n=1}\left(\exp(itH_{r_n}/N)\right)\right)}_{\text{convexified simulation}}=\Pi^{N}_{n=1}\mathbb{E}\left(\exp(itH_{r_n}/N)\right)\approx\Pi^{N}_{n=1}\exp(itH_{r_n}/N),\quad N\gg 1\,,

(2.4)

where
\{r_{n}\}^{N}_{n=1}
are independently sampled from the uniform distribution over the set
[1,\dots,L]
.
Given a fixed
N
, the circuit depth for the first-order Trotter is
NL
, while the circuit depth for qDrift is
N
. A detailed analysis of the complexity differences between qDrift and Trotter splitting must take into account the impact of randomness, and the reader is directed to
Campbell (2019)
for further details.
Beyond approximating unitary processes, a more significant application of convexified simulation involves simulating non-unitary CPTP maps, such as Lindblad dynamics
Cleve and Wang (2017)
;
Ding et al. (2024)
. Due to the non-unitary nature of Lindblad dynamics, simulations often require an encoding and decoding process. The decoding process is often implemented by measuring and resetting (tracing out) of ancilla qubits. This tracing out mechanism ultimately relates to a convexified simulation approach as described in (
2.2
). In this paper, we further explore the upper and lower bounds of the convexified circuit depths for this type of simulation algorithms, with more detailed explanation provided in Sections
3
and
4
. It is also worth noting that the convex combination of unitary gates is different from linear combinations of unitary gates (LCU)
Childs and Wiebe (2012)
. When implementing LCU on a quantum computer, we need to construct a preparation gate for the coefficients with some ancilla qubits. Additionally, in each step, we must measure the ancilla qubits and obtain the correct outcome to ensure the success of the algorithm. In contrast, the convex combination of unitary gates is often implemented in the expectation sense. Specifically, we implement random gates according to the distribution
\mu^{m}
. The tracing out also renders the outcome of the ancilla qubit measurement irrelevant.

In this paper, our main interest lies in analysing the simulation costs of quantum dynamics that can be described as a quantum Markov semigroup:

Definition 2.1
.

Suppose
\{T_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
is a family of
\sigma
-weakly continuous quantum channels acting on
\mathbb{B}(\mathcal{H})
. We say that it forms a quantum Markov semigroup if

•

T_{0}=id
.

•

T_{s}T_{t}=T_{t}T_{s}=T_{t+s}
,
\forall s,t\geq 0
.

•

T_{t}
converges to
T_{0}

\sigma
-weakly as
t
goes to 0.

It is well-known
Lindblad (1976)
that if
\lim_{t\to 0}\|T_{t}-T_{0}\|_{\infty\to\infty}=0
, then
T_{t}
can be written as
T_{t}=\exp(tL)
, where
L:\mathbb{B}(\mathcal{H})\to\mathbb{B}(\mathcal{H})
can be written as:

\displaystyle L(\rho)=-i[H,\rho]+\sum_{j=1}^{m}L_{V_{j}}(\rho),\quad L_{V}(\rho)=V\rho V^{*}-\frac{1}{2}(V^{*}V\rho+\rho V^{*}V).

To quantify the simulation cost of a quantum Markov semigroup, we establish the definition of uniform maximal simulation cost as follows.

Definition 2.2
.

Suppose
\{T_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
is a quantum Markov semigroup of quantum channels. Suppose
\alpha>0,\beta>1
and
I\subseteq[0,\infty)
. Denote the uniform maximal simulation cost of
\{T_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
within approximation error at most
\alpha t^{\beta}
by

\mathrm{M}_{\alpha,\beta}^{I}\left(\{T_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}\right)=\sup_{t\in I}l_{\alpha t^{\beta}}^{\mathcal{U}}\left(T_{t}\right).

(2.5)

Note that when
u,u^{-1}\in\mathcal{U}
, we at least have a linear growth simulation bound for
T_{t}
:

\|T_{t}-uu^{-1}\|_{\diamond}=\|T_{t}-id\|_{\diamond}\leq\min\left\{t\|L\|_{\diamond}\exp(t\|L\|_{\diamond}),2\right\}\leq\exp(2)\|L\|_{\diamond}t\,,

(2.6)

where we use
\|T_{t}-id\|_{\diamond}\leq\|T_{t}\|_{\diamond}+\|id\|_{\diamond}
in the first inequality. This implies that simulating
T_{t}
within an error scale linearly in
t
is always possible. Furthermore, linear behavior in
t
is not very useful in practical applications, as it ultimately results in a zeroth-order accuracy algorithm. Therefore, our work focuses mainly on scenarios in which the simulation error behaves as
t^{\beta}
with
\beta>1
.

In the following part of this section, we introduce several important properties that can be directly inferred from the definition and will be useful for our forthcoming analysis. We start with a simple one using the semigroup property:

Lemma 2.3
.

Suppose
\{T_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
is a quantum Markov semigroup of quantum channels. Then for any
\delta>0,t\geq 0
and integer
m\geq 1
, we have

l_{m\delta}^{\mathcal{U}}(T_{t})\leq ml_{\delta}^{\mathcal{U}}(T_{t/m}).

(2.7)

The proof of the above Lemma is standard in numerical analysis, relying on the contraction property of semigroups. For completeness, we still include a short proof here.

Proof.

We only need to consider the case where
l_{\delta}^{\mathcal{U}}(T_{t/m})<\infty
as the statement is trivial otherwise. Suppose that there exists a random unitary channel
\Psi_{t/m}
with length
l=l_{\delta}^{\mathcal{U}}(T_{t/m})<\infty
such that

\|T_{t/m}-\Psi_{t/m}\|_{\diamond}<\delta.

(2.8)

By the semigroup contraction property and the telescoping argument,

\displaystyle\|T_{t/m}^{m}-\Psi_{t/m}^{m}\|_{\diamond}

\displaystyle=\|\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}(T_{t/m})^{m-i}(\Psi_{t/m})^{i}-(T_{t/m})^{m-i-1}(\Psi_{t/m})^{i+1}\|_{\diamond}

(2.9)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}\|(T_{t/m})^{m-i-1}(T_{t/m}-\Psi_{t/m})(\Psi_{t/m})^{i}\|_{\diamond}

(2.10)

\displaystyle\leq m\|T_{t/m}-\Psi_{t/m}\|_{\diamond}<m\delta.

(2.11)

This implies that
\Psi_{t/m}^{m}
is a random unitary channel of length
ml_{\delta}^{\mathcal{U}}(T_{t/m})
such that the approximation error to
T_{t}
is within
m\delta
. Therefore, by definition we have

l_{m\delta}^{\mathcal{U}}(T_{t})\leq\text{depth}\left(\Psi_{t/m}^{m}\right)=ml_{\delta}^{\mathcal{U}}(T_{t/m}).

(2.12)

∎

By employing a similar semigroup contraction argument as used in the proof above, we can also demonstrate the following relation between the maximum uniform length
\mathrm{M}_{\alpha,\beta}
and the upper bound on the convexified length
l^{{\mathcal{U}}}_{\delta}
. In particular, if we know that the maximum length to simulate up to a certain time is finite, it follows that for any finite time at least as large as this reference time, the
upper
bound on the simulation length will also remain finite.

Lemma 2.4
.

Suppose there exists
t_{0}>0
such that

\mathrm{M}_{\alpha,\beta}^{[0,t_{0}]}<\infty\hskip 2.84544pt\,,

then for any
\delta>0
and
t>0

, we have

l_{\delta}^{\mathcal{U}}(T_{t})\hskip 2.84544pt\leq\hskip 2.84544pt\left(\max\left\{\left(\frac{\alpha}{\delta}\right)^{\frac{1}{\beta-1}}t^{\frac{\beta}{\beta-1}},\frac{t}{t_{0}}\right\}+1\right)\hskip 2.84544pt\mathrm{M}_{\alpha,\beta}^{[0,t_{0}]}.

(2.13)

Proof.

Let
t>0
. Choose
n
such that we have
t/n\leq t_{0}
and hence there exists a channel
\Psi_{t/n}
of length
l\leq\mathrm{M}_{\alpha,\beta}^{[0,t_{0}]}<\infty
such that

\|T_{t/n}-\Psi_{t/n}\|_{\diamond}<\alpha(t/n)^{\beta}

Using the same contraction and telescoping argument as in the previous lemma, we have

\|T_{t}-\Psi_{t/n}^{n}\|_{\diamond}<n^{1-\beta}\alpha t^{\beta}.

If
n\hskip 2.84544pt\geq\hskip 2.84544pt(\frac{\alpha}{\delta})^{\frac{1}{\beta-1}}t^{\frac{\beta}{\beta-1}}
, we have

\|T_{t}-\Psi_{t/n}^{n}\|_{\diamond}<n^{1-\beta}\alpha t^{\beta}\leq\delta.

(2.14)

Therefore, we find a random unitary channel given by
\Psi_{t/n}^{n}
of length
ln
, such that
\|T_{t}-\Psi_{t/n}^{n}\|_{\diamond}<\delta
. Choosing

n=\left\lfloor\max\left\{\left(\frac{\alpha}{\delta}\right)^{\frac{1}{\beta-1}}t^{\frac{\beta}{\beta-1}},\frac{t}{t_{0}}\right\}\right\rfloor+1,

(2.15)

we have

l_{\delta}^{\mathcal{U}}(T_{t})\leq\text{depth}\left(\Psi_{t/n}^{n}\right)\leq ln\leq\left(\max\left\{\left(\frac{\alpha}{\delta}\right)^{\frac{1}{\beta-1}}t^{\frac{\beta}{\beta-1}},\frac{t}{t_{0}}\right\}+1\right)\hskip 2.84544pt\mathrm{M}_{\alpha,\beta}^{[0,t_{0}]}.

(2.16)

∎

Although the definition of
\mathrm{M}^{[0,\infty)}_{\alpha,\beta}
considers the supremum over all
t\geq 0
, it suffices to focus on the interval where
t
is below a certain threshold. In particular, for
t>\left(\frac{2}{\alpha}\right)^{1/\beta}
and any
u\in\mathcal{U}
,

\|T_{t}-ad_{u}\|_{\diamond}\leq\|T_{t}\|_{\diamond}+\|ad_{u}\|_{\diamond}\leq 2<\alpha t^{\beta}\,.

(2.17)

By definition, this implies

\displaystyle l_{\alpha t^{\beta}}^{\mathcal{U}}(T_{t})\leq 1,\quad\forall t>\left(\frac{2}{\alpha}\right)^{1/\beta}.

Thus, to find an upper bound of
\mathrm{M}^{[0,\infty)}_{\alpha,\beta}(\{T_{t}\})
, we only focus on the a small region where
t\in\left[0,\left(\frac{2}{\alpha}\right)^{1/\beta}\right]
. Without loss of generality, when the underlying accessible unitary and quantum Markov semigroup are clear, we define

\mathrm{M}_{\alpha,\beta}:=\sup_{0\leq t\leq(\frac{2}{\alpha})^{1/\beta}}l_{\alpha t^{\beta}}^{\mathcal{U}}(T_{t}).

(2.18)

In our later analysis, we can often derive the upper and lower bounds of
\mathrm{M}_{\alpha,\beta}
for a large
\alpha
. Thefollwoing lemma can help rescale these bounds to arbitrary
\alpha
up to a constant factor.

Lemma 2.5
.

For fixed
\beta>1
, and any
0<\alpha^{\prime}\leq\alpha
, we have

\mathrm{M}_{\alpha,\beta}\leq\mathrm{M}_{\alpha^{\prime},\beta}\leq 2\left(\frac{\alpha}{\alpha^{\prime}}\right)^{\frac{1}{\beta-1}}\mathrm{M}_{\alpha,\beta}.

(2.19)

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we assume
\mathrm{M}_{\alpha,\beta}<+\infty
. Then the assumption in Lemma
2.4
holds true for
t_{0}=(\frac{2}{\alpha})^{1/\beta}
.

For any
t\in\left(0,(\frac{2}{\alpha^{\prime}})^{1/\beta}\right]
, we choose
\delta=\alpha^{\prime}t^{\beta}
. Then using Lemma
2.4
we have

\displaystyle l_{\alpha^{\prime}t^{\beta}}^{\mathcal{U}}(T_{t})

\displaystyle\leq\left(\max\left\{\left(\frac{\alpha}{\alpha^{\prime}t^{\beta}}\right)^{\frac{1}{\beta-1}}t^{\frac{\beta}{\beta-1}},\frac{t}{t_{0}}\right\}+1\right)\hskip 2.84544pt\mathrm{M}_{\alpha,\beta}

\displaystyle\leq 2\max\left\{\left(\frac{\alpha}{\alpha^{\prime}}\right)^{\frac{1}{\beta-1}},\left(\frac{\alpha}{\alpha^{\prime}}\right)^{\frac{1}{\beta}}\right\}\mathrm{M}_{\alpha,\beta}=2\left(\frac{\alpha}{\alpha^{\prime}}\right)^{\frac{1}{\beta-1}}\mathrm{M}_{\alpha,\beta}.

Taking the supremum over
t\in\left(0,(\frac{2}{\alpha^{\prime}})^{1/\beta}\right]
, we get the desired result.
∎

3
Upper estimates for uniform maximal simulation cost of unital quantum dynamics.

In this section, we demonstrate that for a specific category of unital quantum Markov semigroups and a suitably selected unitary set
\mathcal{U}
acting on the (sub)system
\mathcal{H}
only, efficient simulation of
T_{t}
is achievable. Specifically, we present two instances where an upper bound for the uniform maximal simulation cost can be established in Sections
3.1
and
3.2
.

3.1
Symmetric noise generators: Trotter approximation

The first specific instance we consider are quantum dynamics with symmetric jump operators. In this case, we can simplify the quantum dynamics simulation to a unitary simulation, using the Trotter formula to establish an upper bound for the depth of the circuit
Childs et al. (2021)
.

We look at the dynamical semigroup that is generated by the following symmetric generator:

L(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{m}a_{j}xa_{j}-\frac{1}{2}\left(xa_{j}^{2}+a_{j}^{2}x\right),\quad a_{j}=a_{j}^{*}\,,

(3.1)

where
a^{*}_{j}
is the adjoint of
a_{j}
. Note that
a_{j}
can be non-local when the underlying system is
n
-qubit system. We assume the accessible unitary set:

\mathcal{U}=\{\exp(ita_j):|t|\leq\tau,1\leq j\leq m\}.

(3.2)

The upper bound of
\mathrm{M}_{\alpha,\beta}
is summarized in the following theorem:

Theorem 3.1
.

Suppose
T_{t}=e^{tL}
with
L
given by (
3.1
) and
t>0
. Then, for any
\alpha>0
and
1<\beta\leq 3
, we have
\mathrm{M}_{\alpha,\beta}<+\infty
. Moreover, there exists a universal constant
C>0
depending only on
\max_{i}\|a_{i}\|<+\infty
such that

\mathrm{M}_{\alpha,\beta}\leq C\frac{\max\left\{m^{\frac{5}{2}},m^{2}/\tau\right\}}{\sqrt{\alpha}}.

(3.3)

In the proof of the above theorem, we construct a second-order simulation scheme for
\exp\left(t\sum_{j}L_{a_{j}}\right)
. The second-order approximation primarily arises from two sources: (1) the second-order simulation scheme for
\exp(tL_{a_j})
, and (2) the second-order Trotter splitting of
\exp\left(t\sum_{j}L_{a_{j}}\right)
. Although it is possible to improve the former to arbitrarily high order, the latter splitting error remains the bottleneck for improving the
5/2
power in
m
. Since we do not assume further locality assumptions on
a_{j}
, it is not a trivial task to split
\exp\left(t\sum_{j}L_{a_{j}}\right)
to achieve a higher-order scheme.

To prove the above theorem, we first establish the following Lemma for Lindbladian dynamics with a single jump operator.

Lemma 3.2
.

For a self-adjoint matrix
a=a^{*}
, denote

L_{a}(x):=2axa-(a^{2}x+xa^{2}).

(3.4)

Then there exists a universal constant
C>0
such that

\left\|\exp(tL_a)-\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathrm{ad}}_{\exp(i \sqrt{2t}Za)}\right]\right\|_{\diamond}\leq C\|a\|^{6}t^{3}\,.

(3.5)

where
Z
is a random variable such that

\mathbb{P}(Z=2)=\mathbb{P}(Z=-2)=1/12,\quad\mathbb{P}(Z=1)=\mathbb{P}(Z=-1)=1/6,\quad\mathbb{P}(Z=0)=1/2\,.

(3.6)

Proof.

To prove (
3.5
), we first let
Z
be an arbitrary random variable with real value. We diagonalize
a=\sum_{j}\lambda_{j}e_{j}
where
e_{j}
are orthogonal projections. Then,

e^{-i\sqrt{2t}Za}\rho e^{i\sqrt{2t}Za}\hskip 2.84544pt=\hskip 2.84544pt\sum_{jl}e^{i\sqrt{2t}Z(\lambda_{l}-\lambda_{j})}e_{j}\rho e_{l}\hskip 2.84544pt=\hskip 2.84544pt\sum_{jl}\sum_{k}\frac{i^{k}(2t)^{k/2}Z^{k}}{k!}(\lambda_{l}-\lambda_{j})^{k}e_{j}\rho e_{l}\hskip 2.84544pt\,.

(3.7)

Because
L^{k}_{a}(\rho)=\sum_{jl}(-1)^{k}(\lambda_{l}-\lambda_{j})^{2k}e_{j}\rho e_{l}
, we have

{\mathbb{E}}\vskip 2.84544pt\left(e^{-i\sqrt{2t}Za}\rho e^{i\sqrt{2t}Za}\right)\hskip 2.84544pt=\hskip 2.84544pt\sum_{k}\frac{(2t)^{k}}{(2k)!}{\mathbb{E}}\vskip 2.84544pt\left(Z^{2k}\right)L_{a}^{k}(\rho)=\exp(tL_a)(\rho)\,.

Note that if
Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
, standard Gaussian random variable, we have
\mathbb{E}(Z^{2k})=(2k)!/(k!2^{k})
thus

{\mathbb{E}}\vskip 2.84544pt\left(e^{-i\sqrt{2t}Za}\rho e^{i\sqrt{2t}Za}\right)=\exp(tL_a)(\rho)
.

Now, to find an approximation of order
m
, we construct a symmetric bounded random variable
Z
such that
|Z|\leq b
and
\mathbb{E}(Z^{2k})=(2k)!/(k!2^{k})
for
k=1,...,m-1
. Then

\displaystyle\left\|{\mathbb{E}}\vskip 2.84544pt\left({\mathrm{ad}}_{e^{i\sqrt{t}Za}}\right)-e^{-tL_{a}}\right\|_{\diamond}\leq\sum_{k\geq m}\frac{(2t)^{k}}{(2k)!}b^{2k}\|L_{a}\|_{\diamond}^{k}+\sum_{k\geq m}\frac{t^{k}}{k!}\|L_{a}\|_{\diamond}^{k}\hskip 2.84544pt\leq\hskip 2.84544pt\frac{t^{m}}{m!}\left(e^{\sqrt{2t}b\|L_{a}\|_{\diamond}}+e^{t\|L_{a}\|_{\diamond}}\right)\leq\mathcal{O}(\|a\|t^{m})

holds for
|t|\leq 1
. Noticing that (
3.6
) satisfies the above condition with
m=3
and
b=2
, we conclude the proof.
∎

This lemma demonstrates how to simulate
\exp(tL_a)
using few unitary gates and is the key tool that we will use to obtain the upper bound of
\mathrm{M}_{\alpha,\beta}
. As seen above, the proof of this lemma relies on Taylor expansion and moment matching for Gaussian random variables. For more than one term
L=\sum_{j}L_{a_{j}}
, we will apply the usual Suzuki-Trotter approximation. In particular, for
\exp\left(t\left(\sum^{m}_{i=1}L_{i}\right)\right)
, we consider second-order Trotter splitting:

\displaystyle S_{2}(t):=\exp(tL_1/2)\cdots\exp(tL_m/2)\exp(tL_m/2)\cdots\exp(tL_1/2)\,.

(3.8)

According to
Childs et al. (2021)
;
Suzuki (1976)
, we have

\left\|S_{2}(t)-\exp\left(t\sum^{m}_{j=1}L_{j}\right)\right\|_{\diamond}\leq O\left(\left(\sum^{m}_{j=1}\|L_{j}\|_{\diamond}\right)^{3}t^{3}\right)\,,\;\forall t>0\,.

(3.9)

Now we are ready to provide the proof of Theorem
3.1
:

Proof.

Because
l^{\mathcal{U}}_{\alpha t^{\beta}}
is an increasing function in
\beta
when
0<t<1
, we have

\mathrm{M}^{[0,1]}_{\alpha,\beta}\leq\mathrm{M}^{[0,1]}_{\alpha,3}

if
1<\beta\leq 3
. According to Lemma
2.4
, to prove
\mathrm{M}_{\alpha,\beta}<\infty
for
1<\beta\leq 3
, it suffices to show
\mathrm{M}_{\alpha,3}<\infty
.

First, we assume that
\left\{Z_{j}\right\}^{m}_{j=1}
is a sequence of independent probability distributions that is identically distributed as Lemma
3.2
(
3.6
). According to Lemma
3.2
,

\displaystyle\left\|\exp(tL_{a_j})-\mathbb{E}\left[ad_{\exp(i \sqrt{2t}Z_ja)}\right]\right\|_{\diamond}\leq C\|a_{j}\|^{6}t^{3}\,.

(3.10)

Using the second-order Trotter approximation, we have

\left\|\exp(tL)-S_{2}(tL_{a_{1}},\cdots,tL_{a_{m}})\right\|_{\diamond}\leq O\left(\left(\sum^{m}_{j=1}\|L_{a_{j}}\|_{\diamond}\right)^{3}t^{3}\right)\leq C_{2}\left(\sum^{m}_{j=1}\|a_{j}\|^{2}\right)^{3}t^{3},

(3.11)

where
S_{2}(tL_{a_{1}},\cdots,tL_{a_{m}})
is defined in (
3.8
).

Combining (
3.10
) and (
3.11
), we have

\displaystyle\left\|\exp(tL)-\prod_{j=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}\left[ad_{\exp(i \sqrt{t}Za)}\right]\prod_{j=m}^{1}\mathbb{E}\left[ad_{\exp(i \sqrt{t}Za)}\right]\right\|_{\diamond}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\exp(tL)-\prod_{j=1}^{m}\exp(tL_{a_j}/2)\prod_{j=m}^{1}\exp(tL_{a_j}/2)\right\|_{\diamond}

\displaystyle+\left\|\prod_{j=1}^{m}\exp(tL_{a_j}/2)\prod_{j=m}^{1}\exp(tL_{a_j}/2)-\prod_{j=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}\left[ad_{\exp(i \sqrt{t}Za)}\right]\prod_{j=m}^{1}\mathbb{E}\left[ad_{\exp(i \sqrt{t}Za)}\right]\right\|_{\diamond}

\displaystyle\leq C_{2}\left(\sum^{m}_{j=1}\|a_{j}\|^{2}\right)^{3}t^{3}+2C\sum_{j=1}^{m}\|a_{j}\|^{6}t^{3}\leq C_{1}m^{3}t^{3}.

For the second term in the second last inequality, we use a telescoping argument and the contraction property of the semigroup.
According to the above inequality, for
\alpha_{0}=C_{1}m^{3}
, we get an upper bound for
l^{\mathcal{U}}_{\alpha_{0}t^{3}}(T_{t})
. This is the length of

\displaystyle\prod_{j=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}\left[ad_{\exp(i \sqrt{t}Za)}\right]\prod_{j=m}^{1}\mathbb{E}\left[ad_{\exp(i \sqrt{t}Za)}\right]

Recall that our accessible unitary set is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}=\{\exp(it a_j):|t|\leq\tau,1\leq j\leq m\},

thus the length is given by

\begin{cases}2m|Z|,\quad\sqrt{t}\leq\tau,\\
2m|Z|\left\lceil\frac{\sqrt{t}}{\tau}\right\rceil,\quad\sqrt{t}\geq\tau\,,\end{cases}

(3.12)

where
\left\lceil\frac{\sqrt{t}}{\tau}\right\rceil
is the smallest integer that is greater than
\frac{\sqrt{t}}{\tau}
. Finally, because
|Z|\leq 2
,

l^{\mathcal{U}}_{\alpha_{0}t^{3}}(T_{t})\leq 4m\max\left\{1,\left\lceil\frac{\sqrt{t}}{\tau}\right\rceil\right\}.

(3.13)

Taking the supremum over
0\leq t\leq\left(\frac{2}{\alpha_{0}}\right)^{1/3}
where
\alpha_{0}=C_{1}m^{3}
, we get

\mathrm{M}_{\alpha_{0},3}\leq\max\left\{4m,\frac{2^{\frac{13}{6}}\sqrt{m}}{C_{1}^{\frac{1}{6}}\tau}\right\}\,.

Finally, applying Lemma
2.5
, the above inequality implies

\displaystyle\mathrm{M}_{\alpha,3}\leq 2\left(\frac{\alpha_{0}}{\alpha}\right)^{1/2}\mathrm{M}_{\alpha_{0},3}\leq 2\sqrt{\frac{C_{1}}{\alpha}}\max\left\{4m^{\frac{5}{2}},\frac{2^{\frac{13}{6}}m^{2}}{C_{1}^{\frac{1}{6}}\tau}\right\}\sim m^{\frac{5}{2}}/\sqrt{\alpha}\,,

which concludes the proof.
∎

3.2
Unitary noise generators: Poisson model

Suppose
T_{t}=\exp(tL)
with

L(x)=\sum_{j}\lambda_{j}L_{u_{j}}(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\lambda_{j}(u_{j}xu_{j}^{*}-x)\,,

(3.14)

where the jump operators
\{u_{j}\}^{m}_{j=1}
are unitary and
\lambda_{j}>0
. Without loss of generality, we assume normalization in the sense that

\displaystyle\sum_{j}\lambda_{j}=1,

otherwise we replace
\lambda_{j}
by
\frac{\lambda_{j}}{\sum_{j}\lambda_{j}}
.
We assume the accessible unitary set

\mathcal{U}=\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{m}\}.

The following theorem shows that the uniform maximal simulation cost for the above
T_{t}
is always finite for any approximation model with
\alpha>0,\beta>1
:

Theorem 3.3
.

Suppose
T_{t}=\exp(tL)
with
L
given by (
3.14
). For any
\alpha>0,\beta>1
, we have

\mathrm{M}_{\alpha,\beta}:=\sup_{0\leq t\leq\left(\frac{2}{a}\right)^{1/\beta}}l_{\alpha t^{\beta}}^{\uu}(T_{t})<N\,,

(3.15)

where
N
is a smallest positive integer that satisfies the following two inequalities:

\displaystyle N>\left(\frac{2}{\alpha}\right)^{1/\beta},\quad\frac{1}{\beta}\ln\left(\frac{2}{\alpha}\right)<\frac{(\frac{2}{\alpha})^{1/\beta}+\ln(\alpha/2)+(N+1)\ln(N+1)-N-1}{N+1-\beta}.

(3.16)

To prove Theorem
3.3
, we first observe that by Taylor expansion, and the commuting property of
\Phi=\sum_{j}\lambda_{j}\,ad_{u_{j}}
and the identity operator,
T_{t}
is given by

T_{t}=\exp(tL)=\exp(t(\Phi- I))=e^{-t}e^{t\Phi}=\sum_{k\geq 0}\frac{t^{k}e^{-t}}{k!}\Phi^{k},\quad\Phi=\sum_{j}\lambda_{j}\,{\mathrm{ad}}_{u_{j}}\,.

Then, given our resource set
{\mathcal{U}}=\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{m}\}
, we can implement a channel truncated in order
N
with

T_{t}^{[N]}=\frac{1}{\sum_{k=0}^{N}\frac{t^{k}e^{-t}}{k!}}\sum_{k=0}^{N}\frac{t^{k}e^{-t}}{k!}\Phi^{k}.

It is worth noting that
T^{[N]}_{t}
should not be implemented using LCU. In practice,
T^{[N]}_{t}
should be implemented in the expectation sense. Specifically, we implement
{\mathrm{ad}}_{u_{1}}{\mathrm{ad}}_{u_{2}}\cdots{\mathrm{ad}}_{u_{k}}
with
\{u_{i}\}
being i.i.d. samples of the distribution
\mathbb{P}(u=i)=\lambda_{i}
, and
k
randomly chosen according to the Poisson distribution
\mathbb{P}(k=l)=\frac{t^{l}e^{-t}}{l!}\left/\sum_{l=0}^{N}\frac{t^{l}e^{-t}}{l!}\right.
.

Lemma 3.4
.

For any
N\geq 1
, we have

\left\|T_{t}-T_{t}^{[N]}\right\|_{\diamond}\hskip 2.84544pt\leq\hskip 2.84544pt2e^{N+1-t-(N+1)\ln\frac{N+1}{t}},\quad\forall t\in(0,N+1).

(3.17)

Proof.

The key ingredient of the proof is the following estimate:
Define
A(n,t):=e^{-t}\sum_{k=n}^{\infty}\frac{t^{k}}{k!}
, then

A(n,t)\leq e^{n-t-n\ln\frac{n}{t}}

for any
n\geq 1,t>0
with
n>t
. The estimate is straightforward and we provide a simple proof here. Suppose
N_{t}
is a Poisson random variable with parameter
t>0
, then for any
\lambda>0

\displaystyle A(n,t)=\mathbb{P}(N_{t}\geq n)\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}\exp(e^{\lambda N_t})}{e^{\lambda n}}=\exp((e^{\lambda} - 1)t - \lambda n).

Now we choose
\lambda=\ln\frac{n}{t}
and we get the desired estimate. Using the above estimate, we have

\displaystyle\left\|T_{t}-T_{t}^{[N]}\right\|_{\diamond}=\left\|\sum_{k\geq 0}\frac{t^{k}e^{-t}}{k!}\Phi^{k}-\frac{1}{\sum_{m=0}^{N}\frac{t^{m}e^{-t}}{m!}}\sum_{k=0}^{N}\frac{t^{k}e^{-t}}{k!}\Phi^{k}\right\|_{\diamond}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\sum_{k=0}^{N}\left(e^{-t}-\frac{e^{-t}}{\sum_{m=0}^{N}\frac{e^{-t}t^{m}}{m!}}\right)\frac{t^{k}\Phi^{k}}{k!}\right\|_{\diamond}+\left\|\sum_{k=N+1}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-t}t^{k}}{k!}\Phi^{k}\right\|_{\diamond}

\displaystyle=\left\|\sum_{k=0}^{N}-\frac{e^{-t}\sum_{m=N+1}^{\infty}\frac{t^{m}}{m!}}{\sum_{m=0}^{N}\frac{t^{m}}{m!}}\frac{t^{k}\Phi^{k}}{k!}\right\|_{\diamond}+\left\|\sum_{k=N+1}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-t}t^{k}}{k!}\Phi^{k}\right\|_{\diamond}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{m=N+1}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-t}t^{m}}{m!}\left\|\sum_{k=0}^{N}\frac{1}{\sum_{m=0}^{N}\frac{t^{m}}{m!}}\frac{t^{k}\Phi^{k}}{k!}\right\|_{\diamond}+\left\|\sum_{k=N+1}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-t}t^{k}}{k!}\Phi^{k}\right\|_{\diamond}

\displaystyle\leq 2\sum_{k=N+1}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-t}t^{k}}{k!}\leq 2e^{N+1-t-(N+1)\ln\frac{N+1}{t}}.

∎

Now, we are ready to prove Theorem
3.3
:

Proof.

According to the argument in the beginning of this section, it suffices to consider
t\in[0,(\frac{2}{\alpha})^{1/\beta}]
. We notice that
N
satisfies

\displaystyle N+1-(N+1)\ln(N+1)+(N+1-\beta)\ln t<t+\ln\alpha/2\leq\left(\frac{2}{\alpha}\right)^{1/\beta}+\ln\alpha/2\,.

Using the above lemma, when
t<N+1
, we have

\displaystyle\left\|T_{t}-T_{t}^{[N]}\right\|_{\diamond}\leq 2e^{N+1-t-(N+1)\ln\frac{N+1}{t}}<\alpha t^{\beta}\,.

Because
N>(\frac{2}{\alpha})^{1/\beta}
, this implies that

\sup_{t\in[0,(\frac{2}{\alpha})^{1/\beta}]}l^{\mathcal{U}}_{\alpha t^{\beta}}\left(T_{t}\right)\leq N\,.

∎

4
Lower estimates for uniform maximal simulation cost of unital quantum dynamics

In this section, we provide a lower bound estimation for the uniform maximal simulation cost
\mathrm{M}_{\alpha,\beta}
defined in Definition
2.2
. Although the uniform maximal simulation cost considers the supremum of the circuit depth, according to the linear growth property of
l^{\mathcal{U}}
stated in Lemma
2.3
, the value of
\mathrm{M}_{\alpha,\beta}
is mainly determined by
l^{\mathcal{U}}_{\alpha t^{\beta}}
when
t
is sufficiently small. The lower bound of
\mathrm{M}_{\alpha,\beta}
essentially informs us about the necessary circuit depth of constructing a
\beta-1
-th order scheme for the simulation of
T_{t}
.

4.1
Generic lower bound estimates via Lipschitz complexity

To estimate the lower bound of
\mathrm{M}_{\alpha,\beta}
given an accessible unitary set
\mathcal{U}
, our main tool is a resource-dependent convex cost function obtained from Lipschitz norms induced by a proper resource set
S
, also known as Lipschitz complexity, introduced in
Araiza et al. (2023)
. By comparing the Lipschitz complexity of the quantum semigroup with that of the simulation channel, we derive a lower bound for
l^{\mathcal{U}}_{\delta}
.

4.1.1
Preliminary on Lipschitz complexity

For a given resource set
S\subseteq{\mathbb{B}}(\mathcal{H})
, we define the induced Lipschitz (semi-)norm
\||\cdot|\|_{S}
on
\mathbb{B}(\mathcal{H})
as

\||x|\|_{S}:=\sup_{s\in S}\|[s,x]\|_{\infty},\ x\in\mathcal{{\mathbb{B}}}(\mathcal{H}),

(4.1)

where
[s,x]:=sx-xs
is the commutator and
\|x\|_{\infty}
is the Schatten-
\infty
norm of
x
. For a quantum channel
\Phi
, the Lipschitz complexity is then defined as

C_{S}(\Phi):=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{B}(\mathcal{H}),\||x|\|_{S}\leq 1}\|\Phi^{*}(x)-x\|_{\infty}\,,

(4.2)

where
\Phi^{*}
is the dual map of
\Phi
defined by

\Tr(\Phi(x) y)=\Tr(x \Phi^*(y)),\forall x,y\in\mathbb{B}(\mathcal{H}).

(4.3)

Note that
C_{S}(\Phi)<+\infty
only if
\Phi^{*}
restricted on
S^{\prime}
is an identity map. Here,
S^{\prime}
is the centralizer of
S
, defined by

S^{\prime}=\{x\in\mathbb{B}(\mathcal{H}):xs=sx,\forall s\in S\}.

(4.4)

Remark 4.1
.

We illustrate how the commutator
[s,x]
helps us build the lower bound. Suppose
U=u_{m}\cdots u_{1}
. Denote
\Phi(\rho)={\mathrm{ad}}_{U}(\rho)=U\rho U^{*}
, then for any
x\in\mathbb{B}(\mathcal{H})

\displaystyle\|\Phi^{*}(x)-x\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\|u_{1}^{*}\cdots u_{m}^{*}xu_{m}\cdots u_{1}-x\|_{\infty}=\|xu_{m}\cdots u_{1}-u_{m}\cdots u_{1}x\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\|xu_{m}\cdots u_{1}-u_{m}xu_{m-1}\cdots u_{1}+u_{m}xu_{m-1}\cdots u_{1}-u_{m}\cdots u_{1}x\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\|[u_{m},x]u_{m-1}\cdots u_{1}\|_{\infty}+\|u_{m}[u_{m-1}\cdots u_{1},x]\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\|[u_{m},x]\|_{\infty}+\|[u_{m-1}\cdots u_{1},x]\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{m}\|[u_{j},x]\|_{\infty}\leq m\sup_{i}\|[u_{i},x]\|_{\infty}

where we used the property that
\|x\|_{\infty}
remains unchanged if we multiply
x
by a unitary. The second-last inequality follows from induction. Therefore, if we define
S=\{u_{1},\cdots,u_{m}\}
, the minimal length of a unitary using elementary gate set
S
is always lower bounded by
C_{S}({\mathrm{ad}}_{U})
.

The Lipschitz complexity can also be written compactly as

C_{S}(\Phi)=\|\Phi^{*}-id:({\mathbb{B}}(\mathcal{H}),\||\cdot|\|_{S})\to{\mathbb{B}}(\mathcal{H})\|_{\rm op}.

Recall that for a linear map
T_{1}:(\mathcal{X},\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{X}})\to(\mathcal{Y},\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{Y}})
where
(\mathcal{X},\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{X}}),(\mathcal{Y},\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{Y}})
are normed spaces, the induced operator norm of the linear map
T_{1}
is defined by

\|T_{1}:(\mathcal{X},\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{X}})\to(\mathcal{Y},\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{Y}})\|_{\rm op}=\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}:\|x\|_{\mathcal{X}}\leq 1}\|T_{1}(x)\|_{\mathcal{Y}}

(4.5)

A useful property of the induced operator norm of linear maps is submultiplicativity:
If
T_{1}:(\mathcal{X},\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{X}})\to(\mathcal{Y},\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{Y}}),T_{2}:(\mathcal{Y},\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{Y}})\to(\mathcal{Z},\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{Z}})
are two linear maps definded on the corresponding normed spaces, then

\displaystyle\|T_{1}\circ T_{2}:(\mathcal{X},\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{X}})\to(\mathcal{Z},\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{Z}})\|_{\rm op}

\displaystyle\leq\|T_{1}:(\mathcal{X},\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{X}})\to(\mathcal{Y},\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{Y}})\|_{\rm op}\cdot\|T_{2}:(\mathcal{Y},\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{Y}})\to(\mathcal{Z},\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{Z}})\|_{\rm op}.

To be consistent with the diamond norm of quantum channel, we extend the definition in (
4.2
) to the complete version of the Lipschitz complexity:

C_{S}^{cb}(\Phi):=\sup_{n\geq 1}\left\|id_{n}\otimes(\Phi^{*}-id):(\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathbb{B}(\mathcal{H})),\||\cdot|\|^{n}_{S})\to\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathbb{B}(\mathcal{H}))\right\|_{\rm op},

(4.6)

where
\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathbb{B}(\mathcal{H}))\cong\mathbb{M}_{n}\otimes\mathbb{B}(\mathcal{H})
and the Lipschitz norm of the
n
-th amplification is defined by

\||f|\|^{n}_{S}=\sup_{s\in S}\|[I_{n}\otimes s,f]\|_{\infty},\quad f\in\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathbb{B}(\mathcal{H})).

(4.7)

It is straightforward to see that
C_{S}(\Phi)\leq C^{cb}_{S}(\Phi)
. We also compactly rewrite
C_{S}^{cb}(\Phi)
as

\displaystyle C_{S}^{cb}(\Phi)=\|\Phi^{*}-id:({\mathbb{B}}(\mathcal{H}),\||\cdot|\|_{S})\to{\mathbb{B}}(\mathcal{H})\|_{cb}.

Before we present more properties of
C_{S}
, we calculate two simple examples to illustrate how to estimate
C_{S}
in practice.

Example 4.2

(Replacer channel, single qubit)
.

Suppose
\mathcal{H}=\mathbb{C}^{2}
and the resource set is given by

S=\left\{X=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix},\ Y=\begin{pmatrix}0&i\\
-i&0\end{pmatrix}\right\}

(4.8)

We consider the replacer channel
\Phi_{1}
defined by

\Phi_{1}(\rho)=\Tr(\rho)\outerproduct{0}{0}.

(4.9)

Then,

1\leq C_{S}(\Phi_{1})\leq C_{S}^{cb}(\Phi_{1})\leq 2.

(4.10)

Proof.

For the lower bound, we can choose
Z=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix}
as the test operator. Notice the Kraus representation

\Phi_{1}^{*}(\rho)=K_{0}^{*}\rho K_{0}+K_{1}^{*}\rho K_{1},\quad K_{0}=\outerproduct{0}{0},\ K_{1}=\outerproduct{0}{1}\,,

(4.11)

and

\|\Phi_{1}^{*}(Z)-Z\|_{\infty}=2,\quad\||Z|\|_{S}=\sup\{\|[X,Z]\|_{\infty},\|[Y,Z]\|_{\infty}\}=2.

(4.12)

Thus, the lower bound is given by

C_{S}(\Phi_{1})\geq\frac{\|\Phi_{1}^{*}(Z)-Z\|_{\infty}}{\||Z|\|_{S}}=1.

(4.13)

For the upper bound, we apply the Kraus representation of
\Phi_{1}^{*}
again and note that

K_{0}=\frac{iXY+I}{2},\quad K_{1}=\frac{X-iY}{2}\,.

(4.14)

For any
x\in\mathbb{B}(\mathcal{H})
,

\displaystyle\|\Phi_{1}^{*}(x)-x\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\|K_{0}^{*}xK_{0}+K_{1}^{*}xK_{1}-(K_{0}^{*}K_{0}+K_{1}^{*}K_{1})x\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\|K_{1}^{*}[K_{1},x]+K_{0}^{*}[K_{0},x]\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\left(\|[X,x]\|_{\infty}+\|[Y,x]\|_{\infty}+\|[XY,x]\|_{\infty}\right).

Finally, using the commutator identity

\displaystyle[XY,x]=X[Y,x]+[X,x]Y,

we have

\displaystyle\|\Phi_{1}^{*}(x)-x\|_{\infty}\leq 2\sup\{\|[X,x]\|_{\infty},\|[Y,x]\|_{\infty}\}\leq 2\||x|\|_{S}\,.

This implies that
C_{S}(\Phi_{1})\leq 2
. Note that the above argument remains the same if we replace
X,Y,K_{0},K_{1}
by
X\otimes I_{n},Y\otimes I_{n},K_{0}\otimes I_{n},K_{1}\otimes I_{n}
for any
n\geq 1
, thus
C^{cb}_{S}(\Phi_{1})\leq 2
.

∎

Although simple, the above example includes the necessary techniques to obtain the upper and lower bound of
C_{S}
. For the lower bound, we need to find a “certificate” operator so that
C_{S}
is large. For the upper bound, we need to reconstruct the quantum channel using linear combinations and products of the operators in the resource set.
In what follows, we provide another example exhibiting the above ideas. This example will be used later to provide a sharp lower bound for the simulation cost of the
n
-qubit Pauli noise model.

Example 4.3

(Replacer channel,
n
qubits)
.

Suppose
\mathcal{H}=(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes n}
, denote
X_{j}
as the Pauli-
X
operator
X=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}
acting on the
j
-th system, i.e.,

\displaystyle X_{j}=I_{2}\otimes\cdots I_{2}\otimes\underbrace{X}_{\text{j-th system}}\otimes I_{2}\cdots\otimes I_{2}.

We define
Y_{j}
as the Pauli-
Y
operator
Y=\begin{pmatrix}0&i\\
-i&0\end{pmatrix}
acting on the
j
-th system in a similar manner. The resource set is given by

S=\{X_{j},Y_{j}:1\leq j\leq n\}.

(4.15)

We consider the maximally mixed replacer channel defined by

\Phi_{2}(\rho_{n})=\Tr(\rho_n)\frac{I_{2^{n}}}{2^{n}},\quad\rho_{n}\in\mathbb{B}(\mathcal{H}).

(4.16)

Then we can show that

\frac{n}{2}\leq C_{S}(\Phi_{2})\leq C^{cb}_{S}(\Phi_{2})\leq n.

(4.17)

Proof.

Following a similar approach as in the proof of Example
4.2
, we first consider the “certificate” operator
\sum_{j=1}^{n}X_{j}
and note that

\displaystyle\left\|\left|\sum_{j=1}^{n}X_{j}\right|\right\|_{S}=\sup_{k}\left\|\left[Y_{k},\sum_{j=1}^{n}X_{j}\right]\right\|_{\infty}=\left\|[Y,X]\right\|_{\infty}=2,\quad\left\|\sum_{j=1}^{n}X_{j}\right\|_{\infty}=n,\quad\Phi_{2}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}X_{j}\right)=0\,.

This provides a lower bound of
C_{S}(\Phi_{2})
:

\displaystyle C_{S}(\Phi_{2})\geq\frac{\left\|\sum_{j=1}^{n}X_{j}\right\|_{\infty}}{\left\|\left|\sum_{j=1}^{n}X_{j}\right|\right\|_{S}}=\frac{n}{2}.

For the upper bound, we can use the fact that the
C_{S}
is comparable to the Wasserstein complexity introduced in
Li et al. (2022)
(see
(
Araiza et al., 2023
, Corollary 3.15)
for a proof). Here, we include a simple proof for completeness. For any operator
\rho
on
\mathbb{C}^{2}
,

\displaystyle E_{\tau}(\rho):=\Tr(\rho)\frac{I_{2}}{2}=\frac{1}{4}(\sigma_{0}\rho\sigma_{0}+\sigma_{1}\rho\sigma_{1}+\sigma_{2}\rho\sigma_{2}+\sigma_{3}\rho\sigma_{3}),

where we take
\sigma_{0}=I_{2},\sigma_{1}=X,\sigma_{2}=Y,\sigma_{3}=Z
. In fact, the above holds for any orthonormal basis, see
(
Junge and Wu, 2022
, Section 4)
for the general result.

Then, we have

\displaystyle\Phi_{2}=E_{\tau}^{\otimes n}.

Using sub-additivity under concatenation, we have
C_{S}(\Phi_{2})\leq nC_{S_{0}}(E_{\tau})\leq n
where
S_{0}=\{X,Y\}
. The calculation
C^{cb}_{S_{0}}(E_{\tau})\leq 1
follows from the same argument as in the above example.
∎

Remark
. Note that using submultiplicativity of the norm, one can show that for any
n
-qubit channel
\Phi
, we have
C^{cb}_{S}(\Phi)\leq C^{cb}_{S}(\Phi_{2})\|\Phi-\Phi_{2}\|_{\diamond}\leq 2n
, which is a simpler proof of an upper bound without using the property of Wasserstein distance of order 1, introduced in
De Palma et al. (2021)
.

Finally, we summarize several useful properties of (complete version) Lipschitz complexities that we will use later:

Lemma 4.4
.

Given any resource set
S\subset{\mathbb{B}}({\mathcal{H}})
,
C_{S}
and
C^{cb}_{S}
as defined in (
4.2
),(
4.6
) satisfy:

1.

C^{cb}_{S}(id)=C_{S}(id)=0
.

2.

Subadditivity under concatenation:

C^{cb}_{S}(\Phi\Psi)\leq C^{cb}_{S}(\Phi)+C^{cb}_{S}(\Psi),\quad C_{S}(\Phi\Psi)\leq C_{S}(\Phi)+C_{S}(\Psi)

for any quantum channels
\Phi,\Psi
.

3.

Convexity: For any probability distribution
\{p_{i}\}_{i\in I}
and any channels
\{\Phi_{i}\}_{i\in I}
, we have

C^{cb}_{S}\left(\sum_{i\in I}p_{i}\Phi_{i}\right)\leq\sum_{i\in I}p_{i}C^{cb}_{S}\left(\Phi_{i}\right),\quad C_{S}\left(\sum_{i\in I}p_{i}\Phi_{i}\right)\leq\sum_{i\in I}p_{i}C_{S}\left(\Phi_{i}\right)

(4.18)

These results can be found in
(
Araiza et al., 2023
, Lemma 2.2)
and the proof idea is to apply subadditivity and submultiplicativity properties of the induced norm of the quantum channels.

4.1.2
Generic lower bound approach: Lipschitz continuity.

The Lipschitz complexities defined in (
4.2
) and (
4.6
) are crucial quantities that we will use to derive the lower bound of
l_{\delta}(T_{t})
. We first present the sketch of the roadmap here. Let
\Phi=T_{t}
be the exact quantum Markov semigroup and

\Psi=\int_{\mathcal{U}^{m}}\operatorname{ad}_{u_{1}}\cdots\operatorname{ad}_{u_{m}}\,d\mu^{m}(u_{1},\ldots,u_{m})

be a simulation channel for
\Phi
with
\delta
-diamond error, meaning
\|\Phi-\Psi\|_{\diamond}\leq\delta
. If we choose a suitable resource set
S
and
C^{cb}_{S}
is a Lipschitz function in diamond norm with the Lipschitz constant
\kappa^{cb}(S)
, we obtain that

\displaystyle|

\displaystyle C^{cb}_{S}(\Phi)-C^{cb}_{S}(\Psi)|\leq\kappa^{cb}(S)\delta\quad

(4.19)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\quad

\displaystyle C^{cb}_{S}(\Phi)\leq C^{cb}_{S}(\Psi)+\kappa^{cb}(S)\delta\leq m\sup_{u\in\mathcal{U}}C^{cb}_{S}(\operatorname{ad}_{u})+\kappa^{cb}(S)\delta\,,

where we use the subadditivity and convexity of
C_{S}
in the last inequality. If we further assume a maximum length per unitary
\sup_{u\in\mathcal{U}}C^{cb}_{S}(\operatorname{ad}_{u})\leq D
, where
D>0
is a uniform constant, (
4.19
) implies

m\geq\frac{C^{cb}_{S}(\Phi)-\kappa^{cb}(S)\delta}{D}\,.

This provides a lower bound for the circuit depth if we can further provide an estimate of
C^{cb}_{S}(\Phi)=C^{cb}_{S}(T_{t})
. The above argument and lower bound rely on two key aspects:

1.

Demonstrating the “Lipschitz” property of
C^{cb}_{S}
and calculating the corresponding Lipschitz constant;

2.

Estimating
C^{cb}_{S}(\Phi)=C^{cb}_{S}(T_{t})
.

Both aspects require a proper choice of the resource set
S
and additional assumptions to ensure the compatibility of the resource set
S
with the admissible unitary set
\mathcal{U}
. Specifically, we will show that:

1.

The Lipschitz constant of
C^{cb}_{S}
is determined by the
conditional expectation
onto
S^{\prime}
.

2.

The estimation of
C^{cb}_{S}(\Phi)=C^{cb}_{S}(T_{t})
is determined by the
S
-induced mixing time, which is defined in Definition
4.12
, and the Lipschitz constant of
C^{cb}_{S}
.

Figure 3
:
Overview of determining lower bounds of noise models using Lipschitz complexity. We are given simulation parameters
\alpha,\beta>0
, a resource set of bounded operators and a set of admissible unitary gates
\mathcal{U}
so that every gate in the set has at most complexity
D
under the Lipschitz complexity induced by the resource set (
D
-compatible). Then, the lower bound can be determined as a function of the resource-set induced mixing time and the expected length
\kappa(S)
, which both rely on knowledge about the conditional expectation on fixed-point densities
E_{\infty}
of the open system channel.

Remark 4.5
.

In the roadmap above, as well as in the subsequent calculations, we mainly use
C^{cb}_{S}
to derive the lower bound. However, the same argument also applies to
C_{S}
, meaning that
C^{cb}_{S}
can always be replaced with
C_{S}
in these contexts. The only difference is that
C^{cb}_{S}
is always larger than
C_{S}
. This means that the lower bound derived using
C^{cb}_{S}
could potentially be sharper than the one derived using
C_{S}
.

We first briefly review the notion of (non-commutative) conditional expectation.
Recall that for
C^{*}
-algebra
\mathcal{N}\subset\mathbb{B}(\mathcal{H})
, the conditional expectation
E_{\mathcal{N}}
of
\mathbb{B}(\mathcal{H})
onto
\mathcal{N}
is a positive linear map from
\mathbb{B}(\mathcal{H})
to
\mathcal{N}
, such that
E_{\mathcal{N}}(I_{\mathcal{H}})=I_{\mathcal{H}}
where
I_{\mathcal{H}}
is the identity operator on
\mathcal{H}
, and for all
X\in\mathbb{B}(\mathcal{H}),
any two elements
S_{1},S_{2}\in\mathcal{N}
, we have

E_{\mathcal{N}}(S_{1}XS_{2})=S_{1}E_{\mathcal{N}}(X)S_{2}.

(4.20)

According to
(
Kadison, 2004
, Proposition 1)
, any conditional expectation is contractive and completely positive. Thus
E_{\mathcal{N}}
is a unital completely positive map and its dual
E_{\mathcal{N}}^{*}
is a quantum channel.

Note that a conditional expectation
E_{\mathcal{N}}
might not be unique. Recall that a finite-dimensional
C^{*}
-algebra
\mathcal{N}
can always be expressed (up to isomorphism) as a direct sum of matrix algebras with multiplicity, i.e.,

\mathcal{N}=\bigoplus_{i}\mathbb{B}(\mathcal{H}_{i})\otimes 1_{\mathcal{K}_{i}},\quad\mathcal{H}=\bigoplus_{i}\mathcal{H}_{i}\otimes\mathcal{K}_{i}

(4.21)

Denote
P_{i}
as the projection onto
\mathcal{H}_{i}\otimes\mathcal{K}_{i}
. The conditional expecation
E_{\mathcal{N}}
can be expressed as

E_{\mathcal{N}}(X)=\bigoplus_{i}\Tr_{\mathcal{K}_{i}}(P_{i}XP_{i}(1_{\mathcal{H}_{i}}\otimes\tau_{i}))\otimes 1_{\mathcal{K}_{i}}\,,

(4.22)

where the density operators
\{\tau_{i}\}
on the system
\mathcal{K}_{i}
can be chosen arbitrarily. The dual map of
E_{\mathcal{N}}
can be calculated as

E_{\mathcal{N}}^{*}(\rho)=\bigoplus_{i}\Tr_{\mathcal{K}_{i}}(P_{i}\rho P_{i})\otimes\tau_{i}.

(4.23)

Different choices of states
\{\tau_{i}\}
acting on
\mathcal{K}_{i}
could possibly give us different conditional expectation. However, if there exists a state
\rho
acting on
\mathcal{H}
such that

\Tr(\rho E_{\mc N}(s))=\Tr(\rho s),\forall s\in\mathcal{N}

(4.24)

then
E_{\mathcal{N}}
is uniquely determined by
\rho
. For instance, any state
\rho
with the form
\bigoplus_{i}p_{i}\sigma_{i}\otimes\tau_{i}
satisfies (
4.24
). From this perspective, if
E_{\mathcal{N}}
satisfies (
4.24
), then we denote it as
E_{\mathcal{N},\rho}
to emphasize the dependence on the state. Without the dependence on a certain state, we implicitly assume that it is trace-preserving, i.e., the state
\rho
is given by maximally mixed state and we denote

E_{\mathcal{N}}:=E_{\mathcal{N},\Tr}

(4.25)

In this case
E_{\mathcal{N}}
is self-adjoint, i.e.,
E_{\mathcal{N}}=E_{\mathcal{N}}^{*}
.

Remark 4.6
.

For readers familiar with classical probability theory, recall that on a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
, for a
\sigma
-subalgebra
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{F}
, to define the conditional expectation
\mathbb{E}[X\big|\mathcal{F}^{\prime}]
for
X
that is measurable w.r.t.
\mathcal{F}
, one has to specify the subalgebra (corresponding to the
C^{*}
-subalgebra) and the probability measure (corresponding to the invariant state).

The techniques we use in this paper also require some additional technical assumptions on the resource set
S
and the associated set of admissible unitaries
\mathcal{U}
. To this end, we introduce the notion of compatibility of the accessible unitaries
\mathcal{U}
and the resource set
S
.

Definition 4.7
.

We say that the accessible unitaries
\mathcal{U}
and the resource set
S
are
D
-compatible with
D>0
if for any
u\in\mathcal{U}
,

C_{S}^{cb}({\mathrm{ad}}_{u})\leq D.

(4.26)

Assumption (A)
:
S
is *-closed, i.e., if
s\in S
, then
s^{*}\in S
.

Assumption (B)
: There exists a conditional expectation
E_{\rm fix}
such that for any
u\in\mathcal{U}
, we have
E_{\rm fix}\circ{\mathrm{ad}}_{u}=E_{\rm fix}
.

Assumption (C)
: The accessible unitaries
\mathcal{U}
and the resource set
S
are
D
-compatible according to Definition
4.7
for some
D>0
.

Let us remark that
Assumption (A)
ensures that
S^{\prime}
is a *-algebra. In a finite-dimensional matrix algebra setting,
S^{\prime}
is automatically a
C^{*}
-algebra, since the matrices satisfy
C^{*}
-identity. In this case, if
E_{S^{\prime}}\circ{\mathrm{ad}}_{u}=E_{\rm fix}
, we often set
E_{\rm fix}=E_{S^{\prime}}
to satisfy
Assumption (B)
.
Assumption (C)
ensures that one can use the intuitive approach sketched in (
4.19
). An illustration of the above assumptions is given as follows:

Proposition 4.8
.

Suppose
S
is given by a set of Hamiltonians:
S=\{H_{1},\cdots,H_{m}\,:\,H_{j}=H_{j}^{*}\}
and we take the set of admissible unitaries to be

\mathcal{U}=\{\exp(itH_j):|t|\leq\tau,j=1,\cdots,m\}.

Then
S
and
\mathcal{U}
satisfy
Assumptions (A), (B),
and
(C)
.

Proof.

Assumption (A)
is obvious since
S
consists of self-adjoint operators. For
Assumption (B)
, we can choose
E_{\rm fix}=E_{S^{\prime}}
. Then for any
u=\exp(itH_j)\in\mathcal{U}
and
x\in\mathbb{B}(\mathcal{H})
, one has

\displaystyle E_{S^{\prime}}({\mathrm{ad}}_{u}(x))=E_{S^{\prime}}(\exp(itH_j)x\exp(-itH_j))=\exp(itH_j)E_{S^{\prime}}(x)\exp(-itH_j)=E_{S^{\prime}}(x).

The first equality follows from the definition. The second equality follows from the fact that
\exp(itH_j)
commutes with any element in
S^{\prime}
, and we apply (
4.20
). For the last equality, we use again the fact that
\exp(itH_j)
commutes with any element in
S^{\prime}
, thus we have

\exp(itH_j)E_{S^{\prime}}(x)\exp(-itH_j)=E_{S^{\prime}}(x).

For
Assumption (C)
, we use the fact that

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\big(\exp(-itH_j)x\exp(itH_j)\big)=-i\exp(-itH_j)[H_{j},x]\exp(itH_j),

so we have

\displaystyle\|{\mathrm{ad}}_{u}^{*}(x)-x\|=\|\exp(-itH_j)x\exp(itH_j))-x\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\left\|-i\int_{0}^{t}\exp(-isH_j)[H_{j},x]\exp(isH_j))ds\right\|_{\infty}\leq|t|\|[H_{j},x]\|_{\infty}\leq\tau\sup_{j}\|[H_{j},x]\|_{\infty}.

Now we can use the definition of the Lipschitz complexity
C_{S}(\cdot)
from (
4.2
), and obtain
C_{S}({\mathrm{ad}}_{\exp(itH_j)})\leq\tau=:D
. Using the same argument and replacing the infinity norm by the completely bounded norm, we get the same result
C_{S}^{cb}({\mathrm{ad}}_{\exp(itH_j)})\leq D
.
∎

We are now ready to establish the connection between
C^{cb}_{S}(\cdot)
and
l_{\delta}^{\mathcal{U}}
, and state the rigorous version of (
4.19
). This is summarized in the following lemma:

Lemma 4.9
.

Let
\Phi
be a channel such that
\Phi^{*}\circ E_{\rm fix}=E_{\rm fix}
for some conditional expectation
E_{\rm fix}
. Under
Assumptions (B),(C)
, we have that for any
\delta>0
,

C_{S}^{cb}(\Phi)\hskip 2.84544pt\leq\hskip 2.84544ptDl^{\mathcal{U}}_{\delta}(\Phi)+C_{S}^{cb}(E_{\rm fix}^{*})\delta\hskip 2.84544pt.

Remark 4.10
.

Although it is not explicitly stated in Lemma
4.9
above, the proof of it makes it straightforward to see the “Lipschitz property” of
C_{S}^{cb}
:

\left|C^{cb}_{S}(\Phi)-C^{cb}_{S}(\Psi)\right|\leq C_{S}^{cb}(E_{\rm fix}^{*})\left\|\Phi-\Psi\right\|_{\diamond}\,.

Proof of Lemma
4.9
.

We only need to prove the case when
l_{\delta}^{\mathcal{U}}(\Phi)=m<\infty
. Let

\Psi=\int_{\mathcal{U}^{m}}{\mathrm{ad}}_{u_{1}}\cdots{\mathrm{ad}}_{u_{m}}d\mu^{m}(u_{1}\cdots u_{m})

be an approximating random unitary channel such that
\|\Phi-\Psi\|_{\diamond}<\delta
. Then, we claim that

C_{S}^{cb}(\Phi)\hskip 2.84544pt\leq\hskip 2.84544ptC_{S}^{cb}(\Psi)+C_{S}^{cb}(E_{\rm fix}^{*})\delta\hskip 2.84544pt.

(4.27)

In fact, under the assumptions (
B
), we have
\Phi^{*}\circ E_{\rm fix}=\Psi^{*}\circ E_{\rm fix}=E_{\rm fix}
, and

\displaystyle\Phi^{*}-id=\Phi^{*}-\Psi^{*}+\Psi^{*}-id=(\Phi^{*}-\Psi^{*})(id-E_{\rm fix})+\Psi^{*}-id.

Then by subadditivity of
C_{S}^{cb}
and submultiplicativity of the norm, we have

\displaystyle C_{S}^{cb}(\Phi)

\displaystyle=\|\Phi^{*}-id:({\mathbb{B}}(\mathcal{H}),\||\cdot|\|_{S})\to{\mathbb{B}}(\mathcal{H})\|_{\rm cb}

\displaystyle=\|(\Phi^{*}-\Psi^{*})(id-E_{\rm fix})+\Psi^{*}-id:({\mathbb{B}}(\mathcal{H}),\||\cdot|\|_{S})\to{\mathbb{B}}(\mathcal{H})\|_{\rm cb}

\displaystyle\leq\|\Psi^{*}-id:({\mathbb{B}}(\mathcal{H}),\||\cdot|\|_{S})\to{\mathbb{B}}(\mathcal{H})\|_{\rm cb}+\|(\Phi^{*}-\Psi^{*})(id-E_{\rm fix}):({\mathbb{B}}(\mathcal{H}),\||\cdot|\|_{S})\to{\mathbb{B}}(\mathcal{H})\|_{\rm cb}

\displaystyle\leq C_{S}^{cb}(\Psi)+\|(id-E_{\rm fix}):({\mathbb{B}}(\mathcal{H}),\||\cdot|\|_{S})\to{\mathbb{B}}(\mathcal{H})\|_{\rm cb}\cdot\|(\Phi^{*}-\Psi^{*}):{\mathbb{B}}(\mathcal{H})\to{\mathbb{B}}(\mathcal{H})\|_{\rm cb}

\displaystyle=C_{S}^{cb}(\Psi)+C_{S}^{cb}(E_{\rm fix}^{*})\|\Phi^{*}-\Psi^{*}\|_{\rm cb}=C_{S}^{cb}(\Psi)+C_{S}^{cb}(E_{\rm fix}^{*})\|\Phi^{*}-\Psi^{*}\|_{\diamond}.

Finally, using the convexity and subadditivity under concatenation, it follows that

\displaystyle C_{S}^{cb}(\Psi)

\displaystyle=C_{S}^{cb}\left(\int_{\mathcal{U}^{m}}{\mathrm{ad}}_{u_{1}}\cdots{\mathrm{ad}}_{u_{m}}d\mu^{m}(u_{1}\cdots u_{m})\right)\leq\int_{\mathcal{U}^{m}}C_{S}^{cb}({\mathrm{ad}}_{u_{1}}\cdots{\mathrm{ad}}_{u_{m}})d\mu^{m}(u_{1}\cdots u_{m})

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathcal{U}^{m}}\sum_{j=1}^{m}C_{S}^{cb}({\mathrm{ad}}_{u_{j}})d\mu^{m}(u_{1}\cdots u_{m})\leq\int_{\mathcal{U}^{m}}Dmd\mu^{m}(u_{1}\cdots u_{m})=Dl_{\delta}^{\mathcal{U}}(\Phi).

Therefore, we get
C_{S}^{cb}(\Phi)\leq C_{S}^{cb}(\Psi)+C_{S}^{cb}(E_{\rm fix}^{*})\|\Phi-\Psi\|_{\diamond}\leq Dl_{\delta}^{\mathcal{U}}(\Phi)+C_{S}^{cb}(E_{\rm fix}^{*})\delta
.
∎

We demonstrate how to relate the Lipschitz complexity of
T_{t}
to the uniform maximum length, the cost-per-unitary
D
and the Lipschitz constant. This will help us later on to find more specific expressions for the Lipschitz constant.
To this end, recall that we use the following definition for the uniform maximum length

\mathrm{M}_{\alpha,\beta}:=\mathrm{M}_{\alpha,\beta}^{[0,(2/\alpha)^{1/\beta}]}.

(4.28)

Then, using Lemma
4.9
, we can show the following proposition:

Proposition 4.11
.

Let
\alpha>0
,
\beta>1
. Under
Assumptions (A),(C)
and assuming that
T_{t}
is a unital semigroup satisfying
T^{*}_{t}\circ E_{S^{\prime}}=E_{S^{\prime}}
for all
t\geq 0
, we have

C_{S}^{cb}(T_{t})\hskip 2.84544pt\leq\hskip 2.84544pt\begin{cases}3(\alpha\kappa^{cb}(S))^{1/\beta}(D\mathrm{M}_{\alpha,\beta})^{1-\frac{1}{\beta}}t,\ \text{if}\ t\geq(\frac{D\mathrm{M}_{\alpha,\beta}}{\alpha\kappa^{cb}(S)})^{1/\beta},\\
2D\mathrm{M}_{\alpha,\beta},\ \text{if}\ t\leq(\frac{D\mathrm{M}_{\alpha,\beta}}{\alpha\kappa^{cb}(S)})^{1/\beta},\end{cases}

(4.29)

where we define

\kappa^{cb}(S)=C_{S}^{cb}(E_{S^{\prime}}).

(4.30)

Proof.

First, using subadditivity
C^{cb}_{S}(\cdot)
under concatenation, the semigroup property and Lemma
4.9
with
\delta=\alpha(\frac{t}{n})^{\beta},n\geq 1
, we have

\displaystyle C_{S}^{cb}(T_{t})\hskip 2.84544pt=\hskip 2.84544ptC_{S}^{cb}(T_{t/n}^{n})\leq nC_{S}^{cb}(T_{t/n})

\displaystyle\leq nDl_{\alpha(t/n)^{\beta}}^{\mathcal{U}}(T_{t/n})+n^{1-\beta}\kappa^{cb}(S)\alpha t^{\beta}

(4.31)

\displaystyle\leq nD\mathrm{M}_{\alpha,\beta}+n^{1-\beta}\kappa^{cb}(S)\alpha t^{\beta}.

We try to minimize the right hand side by optimizing over
n
. Thus, we define

b=\left(\frac{\alpha\kappa^{cb}(S)}{D\mathrm{M}_{\alpha,\beta}}\right)^{1/\beta}t\hskip 2.84544pt.

If
b\hskip 2.84544pt\geq\hskip 2.84544pt1
, we choose
n=\lfloor b\rfloor
the largest integer number smaller than
b
. In this case, we have
n-1\leq b\leq n
and
b\leq n\leq 2b
. Therefore,

\displaystyle C_{S}^{cb}(T_{t})

\displaystyle\leq 2bD\mathrm{M}_{\alpha,\beta}+b^{1-\beta}\alpha\kappa^{cb}(S)t^{\beta}=2D\mathrm{M}_{\alpha,\beta}\left(\frac{\alpha\kappa^{cb}(S)}{D\mathrm{M}_{\alpha,\beta}}\right)^{1/\beta}t+\left(\frac{\alpha\kappa^{cb}(S)}{D\mathrm{M}_{\alpha,\beta}}\right)^{1/\beta-1}\alpha\kappa^{cb}(S)t

\displaystyle=3(\alpha\kappa^{cb}(S))^{1/\beta}D\mathrm{M}_{\alpha,\beta}^{1-1/\beta}t.

If
b<1
, we define
n=1
. Then,

\displaystyle C_{S}^{cb}(T_{t})

\displaystyle\leq D\mathrm{M}_{\alpha,\beta}+\alpha\kappa^{cb}(S)t^{\beta}.

Note that for
b<1
, which is equivalent to
\alpha\kappa^{cb}(S)t^{\beta}<D\mathrm{M}_{\alpha,\beta}
, it then holds that

\displaystyle C_{S}^{cb}(T_{t})

\displaystyle\leq 2D\mathrm{M}_{\alpha,\beta}.

∎

Based on Proposition
4.11
, we can go one step further by replacing
C^{cb}_{S}(T_{t})
on the left-hand side with
\kappa^{cb}(S)
and the mixing time. First, we note that under the assumptions in Proposition
4.11
, via Proposition
5.9
we have

\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}T^{*}_{s}ds=E_{S^{\prime}}.

(4.32)

To replace the Lipschitz complexity
C^{cb}_{S}(T_{t})
with the Lipschitz constant
\kappa^{cb}(S)
, we define the
complexity-induced mixing time
as follows:

Definition 4.12
.

For any
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, the complexity-induced mixing time of
T_{t}
is defined by

t_{\rm mix}^{S}(\varepsilon)=\inf\{t\geq 0:C^{cb}_{S}(T_{t})\geq(1-\varepsilon)\kappa^{cb}(S)\}.

(4.33)

Remark 4.13
.

The mixing time
t_{\rm mix}
defined via the diamond norm is

t_{\rm mix}(\varepsilon):=\inf\{t\geq 0:\|T_{t}-E_{S^{\prime}}\|_{\diamond}\leq\varepsilon\}.

(4.34)

Note that the complexity-induced mixing time
t^{S}_{\rm mix}
is usually strictly smaller than the diamond norm mixing time
t_{\rm mix}
. We prove a more general case in Lemma
5.12
.

If the complexity-induced mixing time is finite,
t_{\rm mix}^{S}(\varepsilon)<\infty
for some small
\varepsilon>0
, we can now relate the complexity
C^{cb}_{S}
to the Lipschitz constant
\kappa^{cb}(S)
and combine this with Proposition
4.11
,

(1-\varepsilon)\kappa^{cb}(S)\leq C_{S}^{cb}(T_{t_{\rm mix}^{S}(\varepsilon)})\leq\begin{cases}3(\alpha\kappa^{cb}(S))^{1/\beta}(D\mathrm{M}_{\alpha,\beta})^{1-\frac{1}{\beta}}t_{\rm mix}^{S}(\varepsilon),\ \text{if}\ t_{\rm mix}^{S}(\varepsilon)\geq(\frac{D\mathrm{M}_{\alpha,\beta}}{\alpha\kappa^{cb}(S)})^{1/\beta},\\
2D\mathrm{M}_{\alpha,\beta},\ \text{if}\ t_{\rm mix}^{S}(\varepsilon)\leq(\frac{D\mathrm{M}_{\alpha,\beta}}{\alpha\kappa^{cb}(S)})^{1/\beta}.\end{cases}

Simplifying the above formula, we get the following theorem:

Theorem 4.14
.

Under the assumptions of Proposition
4.11
and
t^{S}_{\rm mix}(\varepsilon)<\infty
for some
\varepsilon>0
, we have

\mathrm{M}_{\alpha,\beta}\geq C_{\alpha,\beta}\frac{\kappa^{cb}(S)}{D},\quad C_{\alpha,\beta}:=\min\left\{\alpha^{-\frac{1}{\beta-1}}\left(\frac{1-\varepsilon}{3t_{\rm mix}^{S}(\varepsilon)}\right)^{\frac{\beta}{\beta-1}},\frac{1-\varepsilon}{2}\right\}

(4.35)

4.1.3
Lower bound with fixed target time and precision

Now, we relate our framework to the classical lower bound problems introduced in
Kliesch et al. (2011)
. The problem can be stated as follows:

Given an accessible unitary gate set
\mathcal{U}
and a quantum channel
T_{t}
, what is the minimal number of gates we need to simulate
T_{t}
within error
\delta
in diamond norm for fixed
t>0
and
\delta>0
?

In general, the estimates of the number of gates defined in (
2.2
) should be expressed in terms of
t,\delta
, the underlying dimension of the system, and the number of local operators we need to express the jump operators and the Hamiltonian. Following the roadmap appearing in the previous section, we are able to prove the following theorem answering the above question:

Theorem 4.15
.

For a
D
-compatible resource set
S
given accessible unitary
\mathcal{U}
such that the assumption of Proposition
4.11
holds, we have

l^{\mathcal{U}}_{\alpha\tau^{\beta}}(T_{\tau})\geq\tau\frac{\kappa^{cb}(S)}{8Dt_{\rm{mix}}^{S}}

(4.36)

for any
\alpha>0,\beta>1,\tau>0
such that
t_{\rm mix}^{S}\alpha\tau^{\beta-1}<\frac{1}{8}
, where
t_{\rm mix}^{S}
is defined as
t_{\rm mix}^{S}(\frac{1}{2})
in Definition
4.12
.

Proof.

Recall (
4.31
) in the proof of Proposition
4.11
: if we choose a
D
-compatible resourse set
S
given accessible unitary
\mathcal{U}
, for any
t>0,m\geq 1
,

\displaystyle C_{S}^{cb}(T_{t})\leq mDl^{\mathcal{U}}_{\alpha\left(\frac{t}{m}\right)^{\beta}}\left(T_{\frac{t}{m}}\right)+m^{1-\beta}\kappa^{cb}(S)\alpha t^{\beta}.

If we take
\tau=\frac{t}{m}
, then

\displaystyle C_{S}^{cb}(T_{m\tau})\leq mDl^{\mathcal{U}}_{\alpha\tau^{\beta}}(T_{\tau})+m\kappa^{cb}(S)\alpha\tau^{\beta},

from which we get a lower bound by optimizing
m
:

\displaystyle l^{\mathcal{U}}_{\alpha\tau^{\beta}}(T_{\tau})\geq\sup_{m\geq 1}\frac{C_{S}^{cb}(T_{m\tau})-m\kappa^{cb}(S)\alpha\tau^{\beta}}{Dm}.

(4.37)

Recall that
t_{\rm mix}^{S}:=\inf\{t>0:C_{S}^{cb}(T_{t})\geq\frac{1}{2}\kappa^{cb}(S)\}
. Choosing
m
such that

t_{\rm mix}^{S}\leq m\tau\leq 2t_{\rm mix}^{S}\,,

(4.38)

we have

l^{\mathcal{U}}_{\alpha\tau^{\beta}}(T_{\tau})\geq\tau\left(\frac{1}{2}-2t_{\rm mix}^{S}\alpha\tau^{\beta-1}\right)\frac{\kappa^{cb}(S)}{2Dt_{\rm{mix}}^{S}}.

(4.39)

Finally, if
t_{\rm mix}^{S}\alpha\tau^{\beta-1}<\frac{1}{8}
, the desired lower bound is derived via (
4.39
).
∎

Note that the flexibility of the choice of
\alpha,\beta,\tau
can provide us lower bounds when the target time
\tau
is small or large. If we fix
\alpha,\beta
then the above estimate is a lower bound when
\tau
is small, i.e.,
\tau^{\beta-1}<\frac{1}{8t_{\rm mix}^{S}\alpha}
. If we fix the target time
\tau>0
, then we can choose
\alpha
small enough so that our lower bound is valid. As a corollary, we can provide a lower bound answering the general question proposed at the beginning of this subsection:

Corollary 4.16
.

A lower bound on the minimal number of gates we need to simulate
T_{t}
for any
t>8\delta t_{\rm mix}
within constant error
\delta>0
is given by

l^{\mathcal{U}}_{\delta}(T_{t})\geq t\frac{\kappa^{cb}(S)}{8Dt_{\rm{mix}}^{S}}

(4.40)

where the resource set
S
and the given accessible unitary
\mathcal{U}
are
D
-compatible such that the assumption of Proposition
4.11
holds.

4.2
An illustrating example:
n
-qubit Pauli noise

We provide a simple example illustrating Theorem
4.14
. The model is defined on
n
-qubit system
\mathcal{H}_{2}^{\otimes n}=(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes n}
. Denote
X_{j}
as the Pauli-
X
operator
X=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}
acting on the
j
-th system, i.e.,

\displaystyle X_{j}=I_{2}\otimes\cdots I_{2}\otimes\underbrace{X}_{\text{j-th system}}\otimes I_{2}\cdots\otimes I_{2}.

We define
Y_{j}
as the Pauli-
Y
operator
Y=\begin{pmatrix}0&i\\
-i&0\end{pmatrix}
acting on the
j
-th system in a similar manner.
We consider the following 1-local model given by the Lindbladian generator

L(x):=\sum_{j=1}^{n}(L_{X_{j}}+L_{Y_{j}})(x),\ x\in\mathbb{B}(\mathcal{H}_{2}^{\otimes n}).

(4.41)

Here we denote
L_{X_{j}}(x)=X_{j}xX_{j}-\frac{1}{2}(X_{j}^{2}x+xX_{j}^{2})=X_{j}xX_{j}-x
. Note that this model is a symmetric model and a Poisson model at the same time. We show later that in terms of the number of qubits, the approach for Poisson model provides a better simulation protocol, meaning less simulation cost.
By the commuting property of
L_{X_{j}}+L_{Y_{j}}
for different qubits
j
, the semigroup
T_{t}=\exp(tL)
is given by independent copies of a single-qubit semigroup. In fact, suppose
\mathcal{L}(x):=(L_{X}+L_{Y})(x),\ x\in\mathbb{B}(\mathcal{H}_{2})
and
\mathcal{T}_{t}=\exp(t\mc L)
, then

T_{t}=\mathcal{T}_{t}^{\otimes n}.

(4.42)

A natural choice of accessible unitary set
\mathcal{U}
is given by individual Hamiltonian simulation of the generators,

\mathcal{U}=\{\exp(itX_j),\,\exp(itY_j):|t|\leq\tau,1\leq j\leq n\}.

(4.43)

Applying Theorem
4.14
, we demonstrate the lower bound of
\mathrm{M}_{\alpha,\beta}
given accessible unitary
\mathcal{U}
in the following theorem:

Theorem 4.17
.

For any
\alpha>0,\beta>1
with
\mathrm{M}_{\alpha,\beta}<+\infty
. We have

\mathrm{M}_{\alpha,\beta}\geq C_{\alpha,\beta}\frac{n}{\tau},\quad C_{\alpha,\beta}:=\frac{1}{2}\min\left\{\alpha^{-\frac{1}{\beta-1}}\left(\frac{1}{6\ln 2}\right)^{\frac{\beta}{\beta-1}},\frac{1}{8}\right\}.

(4.44)

Proof.

First, we choose the resource set
S=\{X_{j},Y_{j}:1\leq j\leq n\}
. Then via Proposition
4.8
,
S
and
\mathcal{U}
satisfy
Assumptions (A), (B), (C)
with the conditional expectation given by

E_{S^{\prime}}(x):=\Tr(x)\frac{I_{2^{n}}}{2^{n}}.

(4.45)

Then via Example
4.3
, we have

\frac{n}{2}\leq C_{S}(E_{S^{\prime}})\leq C_{S}^{cb}(E_{S^{\prime}})=\kappa^{cb}(S)\leq n.

(4.46)

To show that
t_{\rm mix}^{S}(\varepsilon)
is upper bounded by a universal constant, we consider the certificate operator
\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}
. We have

\sum_{j}\left(L_{X_{j}}+L_{Y_{j}}\right)\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\right)=\sum_{i}L_{Y_{i}}(X_{i})=-2\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}
, which implies

\displaystyle T_{t}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\right)=e^{-2t}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\,.

Recalling the definition of Lipschitz complexity,

\displaystyle C_{S}^{cb}(T_{t})\geq C_{S}(T_{t})=\sup\left\{\frac{\|T_{t}(x)-x\|_{\infty}}{\left\|\left|x\right|\right\|_{S}}:x\in\mathbb{B}(\mathcal{H}_{2}^{\otimes n}),\left\|\left|x\right|\right\|_{S}\neq 0\right\}

(4.47)

\displaystyle\geq(1-e^{-2t})\frac{\|\sum_{j=1}^{n}X_{j}\|}{\left\|\left|\sum_{j=1}^{n}X_{j}\right|\right\|_{S}}=\left(1-e^{-2t}\right)\frac{n}{2}\geq\frac{1-e^{-2t}}{2}\kappa^{cb}(S).

Here
\left\|\left|\sum_{j=1}^{n}X_{j}\right|\right\|_{S}=2,\left\|\sum_{j=1}^{n}X_{j}\right\|=n
follows from direct calculation. Using the definition of
t_{\rm mix}^{S}
in Definition
4.12
, we have

\displaystyle C_{S}^{cb}(T_{t})\geq\frac{\kappa^{cb}(S)}{4},\quad t\geq\frac{\ln 2}{2}\implies t_{\rm mix}^{S}\left(\frac{3}{4}\right)\leq\frac{\ln 2}{2}.

Finally, we apply Theorem
4.14
with
\varepsilon=\frac{3}{4}
there, we have

\mathrm{M}_{\alpha,\beta}\geq C_{\alpha,\beta}\frac{n}{\tau},\quad C_{\alpha,\beta}:=\frac{1}{2}\min\left\{\alpha^{-\frac{1}{\beta-1}}\left(\frac{1}{6\ln 2}\right)^{\frac{\beta}{\beta-1}},\frac{1}{8}\right\}

(4.48)

∎

Similar to what we have done in Section
3
, we now discuss upper bounds of
\mathrm{M}_{\alpha,\beta}
via Trotter and Poisson approaches.

4.2.1
Trotter approach

Motivated by the Trotter estimate,
For any
t>0
,
\alpha>0
and
\beta=3
, we have shown before in Theorem
3.1
that there exists an approximate random unitary channel
\Phi_{t}
such that

\|\mathcal{T}_{t}-\Phi_{t}\|_{\diamond}<\alpha t^{3}.

(4.49)

The length of
\Phi_{t}
is bounded above by
\frac{C_{\alpha}}{\tau}
where
C_{\alpha}
is a constant depends on
\alpha.
Applying a telescoping argument,

\|\mathcal{T}_{t}^{\otimes n}-\Phi_{t}^{\otimes n}\|_{\diamond}<n\alpha t^{3}

(4.50)

The length of
\Phi_{t}^{\otimes n}
is bounded above by
\frac{nC_{\alpha}}{\tau}
.
This gives us an upper bound on
\mathrm{M}_{n\alpha,3}
, the uniform maximal simulation cost for
T^{\otimes n}_{t}
, namely

\mathrm{M}_{n\alpha,3}\leq\frac{nC_{\alpha}}{\tau}.

(4.51)

Applying Lemma
2.5
, for any
\alpha>0
and
\beta=3
, we have

\mathrm{M}_{\alpha,3}\leq\frac{n^{3/2}\widetilde{C}_{\alpha}}{\tau}.

(4.52)

The above upper bound improves upon the result in Theorem
3.1
, where
\mathrm{M}_{\alpha,3}\leq\frac{n^{5/2}C_{\alpha}}{\tau}
. This key improvement stems from the equality in (
4.42
) due to the local properties of
X_{j}
and
Y_{j}
. In contrast, the proof of Theorem
3.1
introduces additional error when decomposing
\exp(tL)
into products of
\exp\left(tL_{a_{j}}\right)
, which in general do not exhibit the same commuting property.

4.2.2
Poisson approach

Now we exhibit another upper bound of
\mathrm{M}_{\alpha,\beta}
using the Poisson approach. Note that
X_{j},Y_{j}
are also unitaries, our model
T_{t}=\mathcal{T}_{t}^{\otimes n}
fits the form of a Poisson model from Section
3.2
. Using the Poisson approach, we can provide a scheme with upper bound behaving like
\frac{n}{\tau}
. This means our lower bound captures the correct behavior of
n,\tau
.

In fact, we note that

\displaystyle\exp(itX)=\begin{pmatrix}\cos t&i\sin t\\
i\sin t&\cos t\end{pmatrix},\quad\exp(itY)=\begin{pmatrix}\cos t&-\sin t\\
\sin t&\cos t\end{pmatrix}.

We have
\exp(i\frac{\pi}{2}X)=iX,\exp(i\frac{\pi}{2}Y)=-iY
. Therefore, there exist
u_{0},u_{1}\leq\tau
and integers
k_{0},k_{1}\leq\frac{\pi}{\tau}
such that

\displaystyle{\mathrm{ad}}^{k_{0}}_{\exp(iu_0X_j)}={\mathrm{ad}}_{X_{j}},\quad{\mathrm{ad}}^{k_{1}}_{\exp(iu_1X_j)}={\mathrm{ad}}_{Y_{j}},

(4.53)

where
{\mathrm{ad}}^{k}_{U}
is
k
iterations of
{\mathrm{ad}}_{U}
. A candidate for approximation is given as

\displaystyle\Phi=\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}({\mathrm{ad}}_{X_{j}}+{\mathrm{ad}}_{Y_{j}}),

which has length at most
\frac{\pi}{\tau}
.
N
uses of
\Phi
at time
t
following a certain probability is defined as follow:

\displaystyle\Phi_{t}^{N}:=\frac{1}{\sum_{k=0}^{N}\frac{(2nt)^{k}}{k!}}\sum_{k=0}^{N}\frac{(2nt)^{k}}{k!}\Phi^{k}.

Using the Poisson estimate in Lemma
3.4
, we have

\displaystyle\|T_{t}-\Phi_{t}^{N}\|_{\diamond}\leq 2e^{-2nt}e^{(N+1)(1-\ln(N+1))}(2nt)^{N+1},\quad 2nt<N+1.

Note that
(1-\ln(N+1))
tend to
-\infty
as
N
tends to infinity. If we choose
N=nC_{\alpha,\beta}
with
C_{\alpha,\beta}
reasonably large(the explicit form is given in Proposition
3.3
), we have

2e^{-2nt}e^{(N+1)(1-\ln(N+1))}(2nt)^{N+1}\leq\alpha t^{\beta},\quad t\leq(\frac{2}{\alpha})^{1/\beta}.

(4.54)

The length of
\Phi_{t}^{N}
is given by
C_{\alpha,\beta}\pi\frac{n}{\tau}
thus
\mathrm{M}_{\alpha,\beta}\leq C_{\alpha,\beta}\pi\frac{n}{\tau}
which scales like
\frac{n}{\tau}
. Thus the Poisson approach provides a tight upper bound in terms of
n
.

Remark 4.18
.

Using the result in Corollary
4.16
, we have a lower bound

l^{\mathcal{U}}_{\delta}(T_{t})\geq\Omega(nt),

(4.55)

and the upper bound provided by the above Poisson approach is

l^{\mathcal{U}}_{\delta}(T_{t})\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\log(\frac{1}{\delta})nt\right).

(4.56)

In Appendix
8.2
, using one ancilla qubit, a factor of
\log(\frac{1}{\delta})
can be removed. Thus lower bound and upper bound are both given by
\Theta(nt)
using the result in Section
5
.

5
Estimates for the uniform maximal simulation cost of non-unital quantum dynamics.

In this section, we consider the simulation of non-unital quantum dynamics on the system
\mathcal{H}_{A}
. Unlike the unital case, achieving an approximation in this case with an error of
O\left(t^{\beta}\right)
with
\beta>1
is not possible if solely considering a set of accessible unitary gates without an ancillary quantum environment. We summarize the no-go result in the following proposition.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
T_{t}
be a completely positive and trace preserving semigroup but non-unital, meaning
T_{t}(I_{A})\neq I_{A}
, where
I_{A}
is the identity operator on
\mathcal{H}_{A}
. Then for any random unitary channel
\Phi:\mathbb{B}(\mathcal{H}_{A})\to\mathbb{B}(\mathcal{H}_{A})
, there exists
t_{0}>0
and
C>0
such that

\left\|T_{t}-\Phi\right\|_{\diamond}\geq Ct,\quad\forall t\in[0,t_{0}].

(5.1)

The above proposition shows that if we were to proceed with the same approach as in the unital case, the diamond error of the approximating channel is at least linear in
t
and thus
\beta\not>1
.

Proof.

Because
T_{t}=\exp(tL)
is non-unital,
L(I_{A})\neq 0
. Thus
\|L(I)\|_{1}\neq 0
. Note that for random unitary channel
\Phi
, we always have
\Phi(I_{A})=I_{A}
. This implies

\displaystyle\|T_{t}(I_{A})-\Phi(I_{A})\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\left\|\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{t^{k}L(I_{A})^{k}}{k!}\right\|_{1}\geq t\|L(I_{A})\|_{1}-\sum_{k\geq 2}\frac{t^{k}\|L(I_{A})\|_{1}^{k}}{k!}

\displaystyle\geq t\|L(I_{A})\|_{1}\big(1-t\|L(I_{A})\|_{1}\exp( t \|L(I_A)\|_1)\big)\geq\frac{t}{2}\|L(I_{A})\|_{1}.

for any
t\leq t_{0}:=\frac{1}{c(d)\|L(I)\|_{1}}
where
c(d)
is a constant depending on the dimension of
\mathcal{H}_{A}
. Since
\|T_{t}(I)-\Phi(I)\|_{\diamond}\geq\|T_{t}(I)-\Phi(I)\|_{1}
, this concludes the proof.

∎

5.1
Environment-assisted simulation cost

According to Proposition
5.1
, it is not possible to approximate non-unital quantum dynamics using only unitaries acting on the quantum system
\mathcal{H}_{A}
. To approximate non-unital dynamics, we need to include two additional steps beyond the unitary approximation: Encoding and decoding via an assisting environment. For simplicity, the assisting environment system
\mathcal{H}_{E}
is denoted as
E
and
\mathcal{H}_{A}
is denoted as
A
.

Our general simulation protocol for non-unital dynamics is as follows:

•

An encoding scheme from
A
to
A\otimes E
, where
E
is another quantum system which we call environment.

In this paper, we mainly use the encoding scheme given by the quantum channel
\mathcal{E}(\rho):=\rho\otimes\outerproduct{0^k}{0^k}
, where
k
is the size of
E
. This encoding scheme is commonly used in previous works on Lindblad simulation
Cleve and Wang (2017)
. The value of
k
should be chosen appropriately to achieve a high-order simulation scheme
Ding et al. (2024)
.

•

A set of accessible unitaries
\mathcal{U}
acting on
A\otimes E
. Analogous to the simulation of unitary dynamics, we assume that the accessible unitaries
\mathcal{U}
acting on
A\otimes E
are straightforward and easily implemented, such as those given by the time evolution of Hamiltonians.

•

A decoding scheme from
A\otimes E
to
A
, modelled as a quantum channel
\mathcal{D}:\mathbb{B}(A\otimes E)\to\mathbb{B}(A)
. In this paper, we mainly use the decoding scheme given by the partial trace
\Tr_{E}
, which can be implemented by measuring and resetting the ancilla qubits of
E
.

The quantum channel that implements the above three steps is summarized in Figure
4
. Although we perform a measurement in the decoding process, it is a trace-out operation and does not reduce the success probability.

\lvert 0\rangle_{E}
\lvert\psi\rangle_{A}
\mathrm{enc}
e.g.
U_{1}
U_{m}
\cdots
\cdots
\scriptstyle{\mathrm{dec}}

:
Quantum circuit for non-unital quantum dynamics simulation (a single step).

Based on the above simulation protocal, we modify Definition
2.2
and define the simulation cost of non-unital quantum dynamics as follows:

Definition 5.2
.

Suppose
\mathcal{E}:\mathbb{B}(A)\to\mathbb{B}(A\otimes E)
and

\mathcal{D}:\mathbb{B}(A\otimes E)\to\mathbb{B}(A)
are fixed. Then the simulation cost of non-unital
T_{t}
, with accessible unitary
\mathcal{U}_{A\otimes E}
acting on
A\otimes E
is defined as

\widehat{l}^{\mathcal{U}_{A\otimes E}}_{\delta}(T_{t})=\inf\left\{\sum_{j=1}^{M}m_{j}\middle|\exists\mu_{j}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{U}_{A\otimes E}^{m_{j}}),\left\|T_{t}-\prod_{j=1}^{M}\mathcal{D}\circ\Phi_{\mu_{j}}\circ\mathcal{E}\right\|_{\diamond}<\delta\right\}\,,

(5.2)

where
m_{j}
is the circuit depth of
\Phi_{\mu_{j}}
.

Note that the above definition provides an upper bound for the circuit depth defined in Equation
2.2
when there is no environment. Specifically, when there is no environment, encoding and decoding are trivial
\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{D}=\text{id}
. Then,
\prod_{j=1}^{M}\mathcal{D}\circ\Phi_{\mu_{j}}\circ\mathcal{E}
is equal to
\Phi_{\mu}
if one takes
\mu=\mu_{1}\times\cdots\times\mu_{M}
and each
\mu_{j}
with length
m_{j}
.

Similar to the uniform maximum simulation length in the unital case from Definition
2.2
, we aim to approximate
T_{t}
with an error of
O(t^{\beta})
for
\beta>1
. We define the modified maximal uniform length:

Definition 5.3
.

For
\alpha>0,\ \beta>1
, and non-unital quantum dynamics
T_{t}
and a set of accessible unitaries
\mathcal{U}_{A\otimes E}
, define the maximum uniform length with environment
\widehat{\mathrm{M}}_{\alpha,\beta}
by

\widehat{\mathrm{M}}_{\alpha,\beta}(\{T_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}):=\sup_{0\leq t\leq(\frac{2}{\alpha})^{1/\beta}}\widehat{l}^{\mathcal{U}_{A\otimes E}}_{\alpha t^{\beta}}(T_{t}).

(5.3)

When the context is unambiguous, we omit the dependence of
\{T_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
for simplicity. We refer the reader to
Ding et al. (2024)
for a general approach to construct the accessible unitary set given by a single Hamiltonian.

5.2
Lower bound via environment-assisted resource-dependent complexity

To establish the lower bound of
\widehat{\mathrm{M}}_{\alpha,\beta}
for non-unital quantum dynamics, we introduce a compatible Lipschitz complexity that incorporates the environment. One might initially think to define Lipschitz complexity directly on the extended Hilbert space
A\otimes E
by tensoring with identity on the environment. However, this direct extension is not suitable in our case, as it does not account for the encoding and decoding processes. Specifically, we select a resource set
S_{A\otimes E}
, a set of operators on the combined system
A\otimes E
, which is compatible with the accessible unitary set
\mathcal{U}_{AE}
.

Definition 5.4
.

Suppose
\mathcal{E}:\mathbb{B}(A)\to\mathbb{B}(A\otimes E)
and

\mathcal{D}:\mathbb{B}(A\otimes E)\to\mathbb{B}(A)
are fixed encoding and decoding schemes. For a resource set
S_{A\otimes E}\subseteq\mathbb{B}(A\otimes E)
and a quantum channel
\Phi:\mathbb{B}(A)\to\mathbb{B}(A)
, we define

\widehat{C}_{S_{A\otimes E}}(\Phi):=\|(\mathcal{E}(\Phi-id_{A})\mathcal{D})^{*}:(\mathbb{B}(A\otimes E),\||\cdot|\|_{S_{A\otimes E}})\to\mathbb{B}(A\otimes E)\|_{\rm op}.

(5.4)

Similarly, we can define the complete version
\widehat{C}^{cb}_{S_{A\otimes E}}(\Phi)
by defining the norm of arbitrary amplifications as in (
4.6
).

Our goal is to show a crucial lemma as in Lemma
4.9
that allows us to relate the Lipschitz complexity to the lower bound of the length and the expected length given the resource set expressed through the Lipschitz constant, under resonable assumptions on
\mathcal{E},\mathcal{D}
, the resource set
S_{A\otimes E}
, and accessible unitaries
\mathcal{U}_{A\otimes E}
. Then, one can establish a similar lower bound as in Section
4
.

First, we demonstrate that
\widehat{C}_{S_{A\otimes E}}
and
\widehat{C}^{cb}_{S_{A\otimes E}}
shares the same fundamental properties as
C_{S}
and
C^{cb}_{S}
. Since the proof relies solely on the fact that the definition can be rewritten in the form of norms, the argument proceeds similarly to the case without environment.

Lemma 5.5
.

Given any resource set
S_{A\otimes E}\subseteq\mathbb{B}(A\otimes E)
,
\widehat{C}_{S_{A\otimes E}}
and
\widehat{C}^{cb}_{S_{A\otimes E}}
satisfy:

1.

\widehat{C}^{cb}_{S_{A\otimes E}}(id_{A})=\widehat{C}_{S_{A\otimes E}}(id)=0
.

2.

Subadditivity under concatenation:

\widehat{C}_{S_{A\otimes E}}(\Phi\Psi)\leq\widehat{C}_{S_{A\otimes E}}(\Phi)+\widehat{C}_{S_{A\otimes E}}(\Psi),\quad\widehat{C}^{cb}_{S_{A\otimes E}}(\Phi\Psi)\leq\widehat{C}^{cb}_{S_{A\otimes E}}(\Phi)+\widehat{C}^{cb}_{S_{A\otimes E}}(\Psi)

for any quantum channels
\Phi,\Psi:\mathbb{B}(A)\to\mathbb{B}(A)
.

3.

Convexity: For any probability distribution
\{p_{i}\}_{i\in I}
and any quantum channels
\{\Phi_{i}\}_{i\in I}
acting on the operators on
A
, we have

\widehat{C}^{cb}_{S_{A\otimes E}}\left(\sum_{i\in I}p_{i}\Phi_{i}\right)\leq\sum_{i\in I}p_{i}\widehat{C}^{cb}_{S_{A\otimes E}}\left(\Phi_{i}\right),\quad\widehat{C}_{S_{A\otimes E}}\left(\sum_{i\in I}p_{i}\Phi_{i}\right)\leq\sum_{i\in I}p_{i}\widehat{C}_{S_{A\otimes E}}\left(\Phi_{i}\right).

(5.5)

Similar to Remark
4.10
, we show the following continuity argument for
\widehat{C}
.

Lemma 5.6
.

Suppose
E_{\rm fix}^{A}
is a conditional expectation on the system
A
and that
\Phi,\Psi:\mathbb{B}(A)\to\mathbb{B}(A)
are two quantum channels with

\Phi^{*}\circ E_{\rm fix}^{A}=\Psi^{*}\circ E_{\rm fix}^{A}=E_{\rm fix}^{A}.

(5.6)

Then, we have

\left|\widehat{C}^{cb}_{S_{A\otimes E}}(\Phi)-\widehat{C}^{cb}_{S_{A\otimes E}}(\Psi)\right|\leq\widehat{C}^{cb}_{S_{A\otimes E}}\left(\left(E_{\rm fix}^{A}\right)^{*}\right)\|\Phi-\Psi\|_{\diamond}.

(5.7)

Proof.

We notice that

\displaystyle\Phi-id_{A}=\Phi-\Psi+\Psi-id_{A}=(id_{A}-\left(E_{\rm fix}^{A}\right)^{*})(\Phi-\Psi)+\Psi-id_{A}\,.

By the definition, subadditivity, and submultiplicativity of semi-norms, we have

\displaystyle\widehat{C}^{cb}_{S_{A\otimes E}}(\Phi)

\displaystyle=\|(\mathcal{E}(\Phi-id_{A})\mathcal{D})^{*}:(\mathbb{B}(A\otimes E),\||\cdot|\|_{S_{A\otimes E}})\to\mathbb{B}(A\otimes E)\|_{cb}

\displaystyle=\|(\mathcal{E}\big((id_{A}-\left(E_{\rm fix}^{A}\right)^{*})(\Phi-\Psi)+\Psi-id_{A}\big)\mathcal{D})^{*}:(\mathbb{B}(A\otimes E),\||\cdot|\|_{S_{A\otimes E}})\to\mathbb{B}(A\otimes E)\|_{cb}

\displaystyle\leq\widehat{C}^{cb}_{S_{A\otimes E}}\left(\left(E_{\rm fix}^{A}\right)^{*}\right)\|\mathcal{E}(\Phi-\Psi)\mathcal{D}\|_{\diamond}+\|(\mathcal{E}(\Psi-id_{A})\mathcal{D})^{*}:(\mathbb{B}(A\otimes E),\||\cdot|\|_{S_{A\otimes E}})\to\mathbb{B}(A\otimes E)\|_{cb}

\displaystyle\leq\widehat{C}^{cb}_{S_{A\otimes E}}(\Psi)+\widehat{C}^{cb}_{S_{A\otimes E}}\left(\left(E_{\rm fix}^{A}\right)^{*}\right)\|\Phi-\Psi\|_{\diamond}.

Exchanging the order of
\Phi
and
\Psi
, we can also show

\widehat{C}^{cb}_{S_{A\otimes E}}(\Psi)\leq\widehat{C}^{cb}_{S_{A\otimes E}}(\Phi)+\widehat{C}^{cb}_{S_{A\otimes E}}\left(\left(E_{\rm fix}^{A}\right)^{*}\right)\|\Phi-\Psi\|_{\diamond}\,,

which concludes the proof.
∎

To establish the lower bound like in Section
4
, we need further assumptions on
S_{A\otimes E}
and
\mathcal{U}_{A\otimes E}
. Similar to Definition
4.7
, we need the following definition of compatible
\mathcal{U}_{A\otimes E}
and
S_{A\otimes E}
under the encoding and decoding schemes:

Definition 5.7

(
\widehat{D}
-compatibility with environment)
.

Suppose
\mathcal{E}:\mathbb{B}(A)\to\mathbb{B}(A\otimes E)
and

\mathcal{D}:\mathbb{B}(A\otimes E)\to\mathbb{B}(A)
are fixed encoding and decoding schemes. For an accessible unitary set
\mathcal{U}_{A\otimes E}
acting on
A\otimes E
and a resource set
S_{A\otimes E}\subseteq\mathbb{B}(A\otimes E)
, we say that
\mathcal{U}_{A\otimes E}
and
S_{A\otimes E}
are
\widehat{D}
-compatible with
\widehat{D}>0
if for any
u\in\mathcal{U}_{A\otimes E}
, we have

\widehat{C}^{cb}_{S_{A\otimes E}}(\mathcal{D}\circ{\mathrm{ad}}_{u}\circ\mathcal{E})\leq\widehat{D}.

(5.8)

Assumption (O)
:
\mathcal{D}\circ\mathcal{E}=id_{A}
.

Assumption (B’)
: There exists a conditional expectation
E_{\rm fix}^{A}
acting on system
A
such that for any
u\in\mathcal{U}_{A\otimes E}
, we have
(\mathcal{D}\circ{\mathrm{ad}}_{u}\circ\mathcal{E})^{*}\circ E_{\rm fix}^{A}=E_{\rm fix}^{A}
.

Assumption (C’)
:
\mathcal{U}_{A\otimes E}
and
S_{A\otimes E}
are
\widehat{D}
-compatible.

We note that Assumptions
(B’),(C’)
is similar to Assumptions
(B),(C)
in the unital case. The condition
(\mathcal{D}\circ{\mathrm{ad}}_{u}\circ\mathcal{E})^{*}\circ E_{\rm fix}^{A}=E_{\rm fix}^{A}
is used to ensure that the approximation channel also satisfies property (
5.6
). The
\widehat{D}
-compatibility condition is used to ensure the boundedness property in Lemma
5.6
.

Remark 5.8
.

We provide some sufficient conditions for the assumptions
(O)
,
(B’),(C’)
to hold. To argue about existence of the conditional expectation
E_{\rm fix}^{A}
, we can choose it as the conditional expectation onto the fixed point algebra of
T_{t}^{*}
when it is a true von Neumann algebra. A sufficient condition for the fixed point set to be a von Neumann algebra is given by the following.

Proposition 5.9
.

Suppose
T_{t}
has a unique fixed state, or there exists a faithful invariant state for
T_{t}
, then the fixed point set for
T_{t}^{*}
is a von Neumann algebra.

Proof.

If
T_{t}
has a unique fixed state, then by
(
Burgarth et al., 2013
, Theorem 2)
, the fixed point set of the adjoint map
T_{t}^{*}
is characterized as

\{cI_{A}:c\in\mathbb{C}\},

(5.9)

which is certainly a von Neumann algebra. If there exists a faithful invariant state for
T_{t}
, then by
(
Deschamps et al., 2016
, Proposition 8)
, the fixed point set of
T_{t}^{*}
is given by a von Neumann algebra, specifically

\{V_{j},V_{j}^{*},H\}^{\prime},

(5.10)

where
L(x)=i[H,x]+\sum_{j}\big(V_{j}^{*}xV_{j}-\frac{1}{2}(V_{j}^{*}V_{j}x+xV_{j}^{*}V_{j})\big)
.
∎

Based on the above assumption and the continuity statement in Lemma
5.6
, we demonstrate a result on the relationship of the environment-assisted Lipschitz complexity and the lower bound on the simulation length and the expected length, which is similar to Lemma
4.9
.

Lemma 5.10
.

Under
Assumptions (O), (B’),(C’)
, for any quantum channel
\Phi
with
\Phi^{*}\circ E_{\rm fix}^{A}=E_{\rm fix}^{A}
and any
\delta>0
, we have

\widehat{C}^{cb}_{S_{A\otimes E}}(\Phi)\leq\widehat{D}\widehat{l}^{\mathcal{U}_{A\otimes E}}_{\delta}(\Phi)+\widehat{C}^{cb}_{S_{A\otimes E}}\left(\left(E_{\rm fix}^{A}\right)^{*}\right)\delta.

(5.11)

Proof.

The proof is similar as the proof of Lemma
4.9
. Suppose there exist
\mu_{1}\cdots\mu_{j}\cdots\mu_{M}
with
\Phi_{\mu_{j}}=\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{j}}{\mathrm{ad}}_{u_{1}\cdots u_{m_{j}}}
such that

\displaystyle\left\|\Phi-\prod_{j=1}^{M}\mathcal{D}\circ\Phi_{\mu_{j}}\circ\mathcal{E}\right\|_{\diamond}<\delta.

Then, similar to the argument in Lemma
4.9
, we have

\displaystyle\widehat{C}^{cb}_{S_{A\otimes E}}(\Phi)

\displaystyle=\left\|(\mathcal{E}(\Phi-id_{A})\mathcal{D})^{*}:(\mathbb{B}(A\otimes E),\||\cdot|\|_{S_{A\otimes E}})\to\mathbb{B}(A\otimes E)\right\|_{cb}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|(\mathcal{E}\big(\Phi-\mathcal{D}\circ\prod_{j=1}^{M}\Phi_{\mu_{j}}\circ\mathcal{E}\big)\mathcal{D})^{*}:(\mathbb{B}(A\otimes E),\||\cdot|\|_{S_{A\otimes E}})\to\mathbb{B}(A\otimes E)\right\|_{cb}

\displaystyle+\left\|(\mathcal{E}\big(\mathcal{D}\circ\prod_{j=1}^{M}\Phi_{\mu_{j}}\circ\mathcal{E}-id_{A})\mathcal{D}\big)^{*}:(\mathbb{B}(A\otimes E),\||\cdot|\|_{S_{A\otimes E}})\to\mathbb{B}(A\otimes E)\right\|_{cb}

\displaystyle\leq\widehat{C}^{cb}_{S_{A\otimes E}}\left(\left(E_{\rm fix}^{A}\right)^{*}\right)\delta+\widehat{C}^{cb}_{S_{A\otimes E}}(\mathcal{D}\circ\prod_{j=1}^{M}\Phi_{\mu_{j}}\circ\mathcal{E})

\displaystyle\leq\widehat{C}^{cb}_{S_{A\otimes E}}\left(\left(E_{\rm fix}^{A}\right)^{*}\right)\delta+\widehat{D}\sum_{j=1}^{M}m_{j}=\widehat{C}^{cb}_{S_{A\otimes E}}\left(\left(E_{\rm fix}^{A}\right)^{*}\right)\delta+\widehat{D}\widehat{l}^{\mathcal{U}_{A\otimes E}}_{\delta}(\Phi).

∎

Now, we are ready to state the lower bound for
\widehat{\mathrm{M}}_{\alpha,\beta}
which can be derived similarly as Theorem
4.14
.

Theorem 5.11
.

Under
Assumptions (O), (B’),(C’)
, suppose
\{T_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
is a non-unital semigroup acting on operators on
A
and
E_{\rm fix}^{A}
is a conditional expectation on the system
A
such that

\displaystyle T_{t}^{*}\circ E_{\rm fix}^{A}=E_{\rm fix}^{A},\quad\forall t>0.

Define

t_{\rm mix}^{S_{A\otimes E}}(\varepsilon):=\inf\left\{t\geq 0:\widehat{C}^{cb}_{S_{A\otimes E}}(T_{t})\geq(1-\varepsilon)\widehat{C}^{cb}_{S_{A\otimes E}}\left(\left(E_{\rm fix}^{A}\right)^{*}\right)\right\}

(5.12)

If
t_{\rm mix}^{S_{A\otimes E}}(\varepsilon)<+\infty
is finite, then we have

\widehat{\mathrm{M}}_{\alpha,\beta}\geq C_{\alpha,\beta}\frac{\widehat{C}^{cb}_{S_{A\otimes E}}\left(\left(E_{\rm fix}^{A}\right)^{*}\right)}{\widehat{D}},\quad C_{\alpha,\beta}:=\min\left\{\alpha^{-\frac{1}{\beta-1}}\left(\frac{1-\varepsilon}{3t_{\rm mix}^{S_{A\otimes E}}(\varepsilon)}\right)^{\frac{\beta}{\beta-1}},\frac{1-\varepsilon}{2}\right\}.

(5.13)

With the above theorem, we have a general routine to compute a concrete lower bound of
\widehat{\mathrm{M}}_{\alpha,\beta}
for a non-unital semigroup
T_{t}
with accessible unitary
\mathcal{U}_{A\otimes E}
:

•

We first find the fixed point set of
T_{t}^{*}
. Then one can choose
E_{\rm fix}^{A}
as the conditional expectation onto the fixed point algebra of
T_{t}^{*}
. This ensures

T_{t}^{*}\circ E_{\rm fix}^{A}=E_{\rm fix}^{A}.

(5.14)

•

Based on the structure of
\mathcal{U}_{A\otimes E}
, we can choose a certain compatible resource set
S_{A\otimes E}
which satisfies Assumption (B’).

•

Finally, applying Theorem
5.11
, we can get a lower bound estimate of
\widehat{\mathrm{M}}_{\alpha,\beta}
if we have an upper bound of
t_{\rm mix}^{S_{A\otimes E}}
and lower estimate of
\widehat{C}^{cb}_{S_{A\otimes E}}\left(\left(E_{\rm fix}^{A}\right)^{*}\right)
.

One upper bound of
t_{\rm mix}^{S_{A\otimes E}}
is given by the usual mixing time, as stated in the following proposition.

Proposition 5.12
.

Under the assumption of Theorem
5.11
, define the usual mixing time of
T_{t}
by

t_{\rm mix}(\varepsilon):=\inf\{t\geq 0:\left\|T_{t}-\left(E_{\rm fix}^{A}\right)^{*}\right\|_{\diamond}\leq\varepsilon\}

(5.15)

Then
t_{\rm mix}
is an upper bound of
t_{\rm mix}^{S_{A\otimes E}}
, i.e.,

t_{\rm mix}^{S_{A\otimes E}}(\varepsilon)\leq t_{\rm mix}(\varepsilon),\quad\forall\varepsilon\in(0,1).

(5.16)

Proof.

Note that
T_{t}^{*}\circ E_{\rm fix}^{A}=E_{\rm fix}^{A},\quad\forall t>0
, which means we can say that

\displaystyle(T_{t}^{*}-E_{\rm fix}^{A})\circ(id_{A}-E_{\rm fix}^{A})=T_{t}^{*}-E_{\rm fix}^{A},\quad\forall t>0.

Then apply Lemma
5.6
, by choosing
\Phi=T_{t},\Psi=(E_{\rm fix}^{A})^{*}

\displaystyle\left|\widehat{C}^{cb}_{S_{A\otimes E}}(T_{t})-\widehat{C}^{cb}_{S_{A\otimes E}}((E_{\rm fix}^{A})^{*})\right|\leq\widehat{C}^{cb}_{S_{A\otimes E}}\left((E_{\rm fix}^{A})^{*}\right)\|T_{t}-(E_{\rm fix}^{A})^{*}\|_{\diamond}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\widehat{C}^{cb}_{S_{A\otimes E}}(T_{t})\geq(1-\varepsilon)\widehat{C}^{cb}_{S_{A\otimes E}}((E_{\rm fix}^{A})^{*})

if
\|T_{t}-\left(E_{\rm fix}^{A}\right)^{*}\|_{\diamond}\leq\varepsilon
, thus proving that
t_{\rm mix}^{S_{A\otimes E}}(\varepsilon)\leq t_{\rm mix}(\varepsilon)
.
∎

Before we discuss illustrating examples, we remark that for fixed time and error, we have a similar lower bound as in Section
4.1.3
:

Theorem 5.13
.

Under the assumption of Theorem
5.11
, we have

\widehat{l}^{\mathcal{U}_{A\otimes E}}_{\alpha\tau^{\beta}}(T_{\tau})\geq\tau\frac{\widehat{C}^{cb}_{S_{A\otimes E}}\left(\left(E_{\rm fix}^{A}\right)^{*}\right)}{8\widehat{D}t_{\rm mix}^{S_{A\otimes E}}}

(5.17)

for any
\alpha>0,\beta>1,\tau>0
such that
t_{\rm mix}^{S_{A\otimes E}}\alpha\tau^{\beta-1}<\frac{1}{8}
, where
t_{\rm mix}^{S_{A\otimes E}}
is defined as
t_{\rm mix}^{S_{A\otimes E}}(\frac{1}{2})
in (
5.12
).

5.3
Noise generated by non-symmetric and non-unitary jump operators

In Sections
3.1
and
3.2
, we studied the simulation of symmetric or unitary jump operators using unitary gates. Now, we consider a more general case, involving non-symmetric and non-unitary jump operators. Suppose the non-unital quantum dynamics
T_{t}=\exp(tL)
is purely dissipative, i.e.,

L(x):=\sum_{j=1}^{m}L_{V_{j}}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left[V_{j}xV_{j}^{*}-\frac{1}{2}(V_{j}^{*}V_{j}x+xV_{j}^{*}V_{j})\right],

(5.18)

where the jump operators
V_{j}
are non-selfadjoint and non-unitary. Furthermore, we assume that the fixed point set of
T_{t}^{*}
for any
t>0
is given by the von Neumann algebra:

\mathcal{N}_{\rm fix}=\{V_{j},V_{j}^{*}:1\leq j\leq m\}^{\prime}=\{x\in\mathbb{B}(\mathcal{H}):xV_{j}=V_{j}x,xV_{j}^{*}=V_{j}^{*}x\}.

(5.19)

Note that (
5.19
) can be ensured by the assumption of Proposition
5.9
.

5.3.1
A general construction of accessible unitaries.

Motivated by
Cleve and Wang (2017)
, a natural choice of accessible unitary with environment
E=\mathbb{C}^{2}=\text{span}\{\ket{0}_{E},\ket{1}_{E}\}
is given by

\mathcal{U}_{A\otimes E}=\left\{\exp(it H_j):|t|\leq\tau,\;H_{j}=V_{j}^{*}\otimes\outerproduct{0}{1}_{E}+V_{j}\otimes\outerproduct{1}{0}_{E}\right\}.

(5.20)

Note that
H_{j}
is Hermitian by construction. Similar to Theorems
3.1
and
3.3
, we have the following theorem for the upper bound on the maximum uniform length
\widehat{\mathrm{M}}_{\alpha,\beta}
:

Proposition 5.14
.

Suppose we have the encoding scheme
\mathcal{E}:\rho\mapsto\rho\otimes\outerproduct{0}{0}_{E}
, and decoding scheme via the partial trace
\mathcal{D}:\widetilde{\rho}\mapsto\Tr_{E}(\widetilde{\rho})
, the maximum uniform length of order 2 is finite:

\widehat{\mathrm{M}}_{\alpha,2}<\infty,\quad\forall\alpha>0.

(5.21)

Proof.

Note that
H_{j}
is hermitian and by Lemma
3.2
, for each
1\leq j\leq m
, there exists
\Phi_{t}^{j}=\int_{\mathcal{U}_{A\otimes E}^{m_{j}}}{\mathrm{ad}}_{u_{m_{j}}\cdots u_{1}}d\mu_{j}(u_{m_{j}}\cdots u_{1})
with length
m_{j}
such that

\left\|\exp(tL_{H_j})-\Phi_{t}^{j}\right\|_{\diamond}=O\left(\left\|V_{j}\right\|^{6}t^{3}\right).

(5.22)

Furthermore, by direct calculation, we get

\mathcal{D}\circ L_{H_{j}}\circ\mathcal{E}=L_{V_{j}}.

(5.23)

Thus via first order expansion
\|\exp(tL)-id_{\mathbb{B}(\mathcal{H})}-tL\|_{\diamond}=O(\|L\|^{2}_{\diamond}t^{2})
(see Appendix of
Cleve and Wang (2017)
), we have

\left\|\exp(tL_{V_j})-\mathcal{D}\circ\exp(tL_{H_j})\circ\mathcal{E}\right\|_{\diamond}=O(\|V_{j}\|^{4}\,t^{2}).

(5.24)

Combining the above two equalities, we have

\left\|\exp(tL_{V_j})-\mathcal{D}\circ\Phi_{t}^{j}\circ\mathcal{E}\right\|_{\diamond}=O(\|V_{j}\|^{4}\,t^{2}).

(5.25)

Applying first-order Suzuki-Trotter formula, we have

\left\|\exp\left(t\sum_{j}L_{V_{j}}\right)-\prod_{j}\mathcal{D}\circ\Phi_{t}^{j}\circ\mathcal{E}\right\|_{\diamond}=O\left(\left(\sum_{j}\|V_{j}\|^{4}\right)t^{2}\right)\,.

(5.26)

This implies that
\prod_{j}\mathcal{D}\circ\Phi_{t}^{j}\circ\mathcal{E}
is a second-order approximation scheme for
\exp\left(t\sum_{j}L_{V_{j}}\right)
with finite circuit depth. The remaining steps are same as the proof of Theorem
3.1
in the unital case and we omit it.
∎

We set
E_{\rm fix}^{A}
as the conditional expectation onto the fixed point algebra of
T_{t}^{*}
. To derive a lower bound on the simulation length, following the framework introduced in the previous section, we need to check the following three conditions:

1.

T_{t}^{*}\circ E_{\rm fix}^{A}=E_{\rm fix}^{A}
for some conditional expectation.

2.

(\mathcal{D}\circ{\mathrm{ad}}_{u}\circ\mathcal{E})^{*}\circ E_{\rm fix}^{A}=E_{\rm fix}^{A}
for any
u\in\mathcal{U}_{A\otimes E}
.

3.

\mathcal{U}_{A\otimes E}
and
S_{A\otimes E}
are compatible, i.e., there exists
\widehat{D}>0
such that for any
u\in\mathcal{U}_{A\otimes E}
,

\widehat{C}_{S_{A\otimes E}}^{cb}(\mathcal{D}\circ{\mathrm{ad}}_{u}\circ\mathcal{E})\leq\widehat{D}.

(5.27)

We check them one by one:

1.

This condition 1 is straightforward from the definition of
E^{A}_{\rm fix}
.

2.

For the second condition, for any
u\in\mathcal{U}_{A\otimes E}
, we denote it as

u=\exp(is (V_j^* \otimes\ketbra{0}{1}_E + V_j \otimes\ketbra{1}{0}_E)),\quad|s|\leq\tau.

Recall that the encoding and decoding schemes are defined by

\mathcal{E}:\rho\mapsto\rho\otimes\outerproduct{0}{0}_{E}
, and
\mathcal{D}:\widetilde{\rho}\mapsto\Tr_{E}(\widetilde{\rho})
. Hence by definition, the dual map is given explicitly as

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{*}:\mathbb{B}(AE)\to\mathbb{B}(A):X_{AE}\mapsto\bra{0}_{E}X_{AE}\ket{0}_{E},

(5.28)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{*}:\mathbb{B}(A)\to\mathbb{B}(AE):X_{A}\mapsto X_{A}\otimes I_{E}.

Then for any
X_{A}\in\mathbb{B}(A)
, we have

\displaystyle(\mathcal{D}\circ{\mathrm{ad}}_{u}\circ\mathcal{E})^{*}\circ E_{\rm fix}^{A}(X_{A})=\mathcal{E}^{*}\circ{\mathrm{ad}}_{u^{*}}\circ\mathcal{D}^{*}(E_{\rm fix}^{A}(X_{A}))

\displaystyle=\mathcal{E}^{*}\left(\exp(-is (V_j^* \otimes\ketbra{0}{1}_E + V_j \otimes\ketbra{1}{0}_E))(E_{\rm fix}^{A}(X_{A})\otimes I_{E})\exp(is (V_j^* \otimes\ketbra{0}{1}_E + V_j \otimes\ketbra{1}{0}_E))\right)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{E}^{*}(E_{\rm fix}^{A}(X_{A})\otimes I_{E})=E_{\rm fix}^{A}(X_{A}).

Note that apart from the definition, the main property we used is

\displaystyle\exp(is (V_j^* \otimes\ketbra{0}{1}_E + V_j \otimes\ketbra{1}{0}_E))(E_{\rm fix}^{A}(X_{A})\otimes I_{E})

\displaystyle=(E_{\rm fix}^{A}(X_{A})\otimes I_{E})\exp(is (V_j^* \otimes\ketbra{0}{1}_E + V_j \otimes\ketbra{1}{0}_E)).

To see why the above is true, note that
E_{\rm fix}^{A}(X_{A})
is in the fixed point algebra of
T_{t}^{*}
and thus commutes with
V_{j},V_{j}^{*}
. Then using Taylor expansion and the fact that

\displaystyle(V_{j}^{*}\otimes\outerproduct{0}{1}_{E}+V_{j}\otimes\outerproduct{1}{0}_{E})(E_{\rm fix}^{A}(X_{A})\otimes I_{E})=(E_{\rm fix}^{A}(X_{A})\otimes I_{E})(V_{j}^{*}\otimes\outerproduct{0}{1}_{E}+V_{j}\otimes\outerproduct{1}{0}_{E}).

we get the desired condition
(\mathcal{D}\circ{\mathrm{ad}}_{u}\circ\mathcal{E})^{*}\circ E_{\rm fix}^{A}=E_{\rm fix}^{A}
.

3.

Finally, to show that the last condition holds, we choose our resource set
S_{A\otimes E}
as

S_{A\otimes E}=\{X_{E}\otimes I_{A},Y_{E}\otimes I_{A},H_{j}=V_{j}^{*}\otimes\outerproduct{0}{1}_{E}+V_{j}\otimes\outerproduct{1}{0}_{E}:1\leq j\leq m\}.

(5.29)

Then our claim is as follows:

Proposition 5.15
.

Suppose
S_{A\otimes E}
is given as (
5.29
), then for any
u\in\mathcal{U}_{A\otimes E}
with
\mathcal{U}_{A\otimes E}
given in (
5.20
), we have

\widehat{C}_{S_{A\otimes E}}^{cb}(\mathcal{D}\circ{\mathrm{ad}}_{u}\circ\mathcal{E})\leq\tau+6=:\widehat{D}

(5.30)

Proof.

For any
u\in\mathcal{U}_{A\otimes E}
, suppose
u=\exp(is H_j)
, then by definition

\displaystyle\widehat{C}^{cb}_{S_{A\otimes E}}(\mathcal{D}\circ{\mathrm{ad}}_{u}\circ\mathcal{E})=\left\|(\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{D}\circ{\mathrm{ad}}_{u}\circ\mathcal{E}-id_{A})\mathcal{D})^{*}:(\mathbb{B}(A\otimes E),\||\cdot|\|_{S_{A\otimes E}})\to\mathbb{B}(A\otimes E)\right\|_{cb}

\displaystyle=\left\|(\mathcal{E}\circ\mathcal{D})^{*}\,{\mathrm{ad}}_{u}^{*}\,(\mathcal{E}\circ\mathcal{D})^{*}-(\mathcal{E}\circ\mathcal{D})^{*}:(\mathbb{B}(A\otimes E),\||\cdot|\|_{S_{A\otimes E}})\to\mathbb{B}(A\otimes E)\right\|_{cb}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|(\mathcal{E}\circ\mathcal{D})^{*}\,{\mathrm{ad}}_{u}^{*}\,(\mathcal{E}\circ\mathcal{D})^{*}-id:(\mathbb{B}(A\otimes E),\||\cdot|\|_{S_{A\otimes E}})\to\mathbb{B}(A\otimes E)\right\|_{cb}

\displaystyle+\left\|(\mathcal{E}\circ\mathcal{D})^{*}-id:(\mathbb{B}(A\otimes E),\||\cdot|\|_{S_{A\otimes E}})\to\mathbb{B}(A\otimes E)\right\|_{cb}

\displaystyle=C_{S_{A\otimes E}}^{cb}((\mathcal{E}\circ\mathcal{D})\circ{\mathrm{ad}}_{u}\circ(\mathcal{E}\circ\mathcal{D}))+C_{S_{A\otimes E}}^{cb}(\mathcal{E}\circ\mathcal{D})

\displaystyle\leq C_{S_{A\otimes E}}^{cb}({\mathrm{ad}}_{u})+3C_{S_{A\otimes E}}^{cb}(\mathcal{E}\circ\mathcal{D}),

where we used the standard definition of
C_{S_{A\otimes E}}(\Phi)
for a quantum channel
\Phi
acting on operators on
A\otimes E
. Note that

C_{S_{A\otimes E}}^{cb}(\mathcal{E}\circ\mathcal{D})=C_{S_{A\otimes E}}^{cb}(id_{A}\otimes E_{0}^{E*})\leq C_{S_{E}}^{cb}(E_{0}^{E*}),\quad S_{E}=\{X_{E},Y_{E}\}

(5.31)

where
E_{0}^{E}:\mathbb{B}(E)\to\mathbb{B}(E),X_{E}\mapsto\bra{0}_{E}X_{E}\ket{0}_{E}I_{E}
and
E_{0}^{E*}
is the replacer channel defined by
E_{0}^{E*}(\rho_{E})=\Tr(\rho_E)\outerproduct{0}{0}_{E}
. Then using Example
4.2
, we have
C_{S_{E}}^{cb}(E_{0}^{E*})\leq 2
and using a similar argument as in Proposition
4.8
, we have
C_{S_{A\otimes E}}^{cb}({\mathrm{ad}}_{u})\leq\tau
thus we have
\widehat{C}_{S_{A\otimes E}}^{cb}(\mathcal{D}\circ{\mathrm{ad}}_{u}\circ\mathcal{E})\leq\tau+6=:\widehat{D}
.
∎

Remark 5.16
.

Given a Lindbladian as in (
5.18
), the choice of the resource set is not unique. Another general way to construct
S_{A\otimes E}
is given as follows:

S_{A}\hskip 2.84544pt=\hskip 2.84544pt\{V_{j}|1\leq j\leq m\}\cup\{V_{j}^{*}|1\leq j\leq m\}\hskip 2.84544pt,

S_{E}=\{X_{E},Y_{E}\}
which leads to the combined resource set

S_{A\otimes E}\hskip 2.84544pt=\hskip 2.84544ptS_{A}\otimes I_{E}\cup I_{A}\otimes S_{E}\hskip 2.84544pt.

It is clear that the resource set
S_{A}
on the system
A
has the property that

S_{A}^{\prime}=\mathcal{N}_{\rm fix}\implies E_{S_{A}^{\prime}}\hskip 2.84544pt=\hskip 2.84544ptE_{\rm fix}^{A}

which is consistent with the assumption in Section
4
. Moreover, we notice that,

\displaystyle H_{j}=V_{j}^{*}\otimes\outerproduct{0}{1}_{E}+V_{j}\otimes\outerproduct{1}{0}_{E}=V_{j}\otimes\frac{X-iY}{2}+V_{j}\otimes\frac{X+iY}{2}

Similar to the proof of Example
4.2
, we can show that if one resource set can be represented as linear combinations of products of the other resource set, then they are comparable. This implies
\widehat{C}_{S_{A\otimes E}}^{cb}
are comparable for the above two choices of the resourse set.

The summary of the lower bound for the model with Lindbladian as in (
5.18
) is stated as follows:

Theorem 5.17
.

Suppose
T_{t}=\exp(tL)
with
L
given by (
5.18
). Additionally, we assume
E_{\rm fix}^{A}
is the conditional expectation onto the fixed point algebra of
T_{t}^{*}
. Then for
E=\mathbb{C}^{2}
, the set of accessible unitaries

\mathcal{U}_{A\otimes E}=\{\exp(it H_j):|t|\leq\tau,\;H_{j}=V_{j}^{*}\otimes\outerproduct{0}{1}_{E}+V_{j}\otimes\outerproduct{1}{0}_{E}\}.

(5.32)

and the encoding and decoding schemes given by
\mathcal{E}:\rho\mapsto\rho\otimes\outerproduct{0}{0}_{E}
,
\mathcal{D}:\widetilde{\rho}\mapsto\Tr_{E}(\widetilde{\rho})
, we have the following lower bound:

\widehat{\mathrm{M}}_{\alpha,\beta}\geq C_{\alpha,\beta}\frac{\widehat{C}^{cb}_{S_{A\otimes E}}\left(\left(E_{\rm fix}^{A}\right)^{*}\right)}{\widehat{D}},\quad C_{\alpha,\beta}:=\min\left\{\alpha^{-\frac{1}{\beta-1}}\left(\frac{1-\varepsilon}{3t_{\rm mix}^{S_{A\otimes E}}(\varepsilon)}\right)^{\frac{\beta}{\beta-1}},\frac{1-\varepsilon}{2}\right\}.

(5.33)

where
\widehat{D}
can be given by
\tau+6
in (
5.30
).

Remark 5.18
.

We have seen in (
1.6
) that if
\frac{C_{S}^{cb}(E_{\infty})}{t_{\rm mix}}
is large, our estimates are meaningful. For the tensor product model, we are able to show that this can occur indeed. This trick works for unital and non-unital quantum dynamics and we consider the non-unital case for generality. For example, consider the new generator is given by
L^{(n)}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\pi_{j}(L)
where
\pi_{j}(L)
is acting on the
j
-th register and
L
is a fixed Lindblad generator. Then we have (see the details in Appendix
8
)

\frac{\widehat{C}^{cb}_{S_{A^{\otimes n}\otimes E}}\left(\left(E_{\rm fix}^{A}\right)^{*\otimes n}\right)}{t_{\rm mix}(L^{(n)})}\hskip 2.84544pt\geq\hskip 2.84544pt\Omega\left(n\frac{\widehat{C}^{cb}_{S_{A\otimes E}}\left(\left(E_{\rm fix}^{A}\right)^{*}\right)}{t_{\rm mix}(L)}\right)\hskip 2.84544pt.

Thus, we obtain a lower bound of order
n
for the simulation cost of tensor product models.

5.3.2
A different construction:
n
-qubit amplitude damping noise

In the previous subsection, we present a general framework that works for a large class of non-unitary and non-Hermitian jump operators. In this subsection, we present a different construction of accessible unitaries which work for
n
-qubit amplitude damping noise.
The model is defined on
n
-qubit system
A^{\otimes n}=(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes n}
. Denote
a_{j}
as the operator
a=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}
acting on the
j
-th system, i.e.,

\displaystyle a_{j}=I_{2}\otimes\cdots I_{2}\otimes\underbrace{a}_{\text{j-th system}}\otimes I_{2}\cdots\otimes I_{2}.

Denote

\displaystyle L(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}L_{a_{j}}(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\big(a_{j}xa_{j}^{*}-\frac{1}{2}(a_{j}^{*}a_{j}x+xa_{j}^{*}a_{j})\big),\quad x\in\mathbb{B}(A),

we have by the commuting property,

T_{t}=\exp(tL)=\mathcal{N}_{t}^{\otimes n},

(5.34)

where
\mathcal{N}_{t}=\exp(t \mc L_a)
is acting on a single qubit system
A=\mathbb{C}^{2}
. The accessible unitary here is given by

\mathcal{U}_{A^{n}\otimes E}=\{\exp(it (Y_j\otimes X_E)),\exp(it ( X_j\otimes Y_E)):|t|\leq\tau,1\leq j\leq n\}.

(5.35)

and the standard encoding and decoding schemes given by
\mathcal{E}:\rho\mapsto\rho\otimes\outerproduct{0}{0}_{E}
,
\mathcal{D}:\widetilde{\rho}\mapsto\Tr_{E}(\widetilde{\rho})
. Then, we have

\mathcal{N}_{t}^{\otimes n}=\prod_{j=1}^{n}\mathcal{D}\circ{\mathrm{ad}}_{\exp(\frac{i \arccos(e^{-t})}{2}Y_A \otimes X_E )}{\mathrm{ad}}_{\exp(\frac{-i \arccos(e^{-t})}{2} X_A \otimes Y_{E} )}\circ\mathcal{E},\quad\forall t\geq 0.

(5.36)

Details on this elementary calculation can be found in appendix
8.1
.
Note that
0\leq\arccos(e^{-t})\leq\frac{\pi}{2}
, which allows us to obtain the upper bound

\widehat{\mathrm{M}}_{\alpha,\beta}\leq\frac{\pi n}{\tau},\quad\forall\alpha>0,\beta>1.

(5.37)

To show the lower bound, we choose the resource set

S_{A^{n}E}=\{I_{A^{\otimes n}}\otimes X_{E},I_{A^{\otimes n}}\otimes Y_{E},X_{j}\otimes Y_{E},Y_{j}\otimes X_{E}:1\leq j\leq n\}

(5.38)

and the conditional expectation

E_{\rm fix}^{A^{n}}:\mathbb{B}(A^{\otimes n})\to\mathbb{B}(A^{\otimes n}),\rho_{n}\mapsto\bra{0^n}\rho_{n}\ket{0^n}I_{A^{\otimes n}}.

(5.39)

Our goal is to show the following conditions.

1.

T_{t}^{*}\circ E_{\rm fix}^{A^{n}}=E_{\rm fix}^{A^{n}}
for some conditional expectation.

2.

(\mathcal{D}\circ{\mathrm{ad}}_{u}\circ\mathcal{E})^{*}\circ E_{\rm fix}^{A^{n}}=E_{\rm fix}^{A^{n}}
for any
u\in\mathcal{U}_{{A^{n}}\otimes E}
.

3.

\mathcal{U}_{{A^{n}}\otimes E}
and
S_{{A^{n}}\otimes E}
are compatible, i.e., there exists
\widehat{D}>0
such that for any
u\in\mathcal{U}_{{A^{n}}\otimes E}
,

\widehat{C}_{S_{{A^{n}}\otimes E}}^{cb}(\mathcal{D}\circ{\mathrm{ad}}_{u}\circ\mathcal{E})\leq\widehat{D}.

(5.40)

The first and second condition are obvious because
T_{t}^{*}
and
(\mathcal{D}\circ{\mathrm{ad}}_{u}\circ\mathcal{E})^{*}
are unital. For the last condition, using the same argument as Proposition
5.15
, we have

\widehat{C}_{S_{A\otimes E}}^{cb}(\mathcal{D}\circ{\mathrm{ad}}_{u}\circ\mathcal{E})\leq\tau+6=:\widehat{D},\quad\forall u\in\mathcal{U}_{A\otimes E}.

(5.41)

Applying Theorem
5.11
, we can get a lower bound for
\widehat{\mathrm{M}}_{\alpha,\beta}
:

\widehat{\mathrm{M}}_{\alpha,\beta}\geq C_{\alpha,\beta}\frac{\widehat{C}^{cb}_{S_{A\otimes E}}\left(\left(E_{\rm fix}^{A^{n}}\right)^{*}\right)}{\widehat{D}},\quad C_{\alpha,\beta}:=\min\left\{\alpha^{-\frac{1}{\beta-1}}\left(\frac{1-\varepsilon}{3t_{\rm mix}^{S_{A\otimes E}}(\varepsilon)}\right)^{\frac{\beta}{\beta-1}},\frac{1-\varepsilon}{2}\right\}.

(5.42)

Finally, we provide estimates of
\widehat{C}^{cb}_{S_{A\otimes E}}\left(\left(E_{\rm fix}^{A^{n}}\right)^{*}\right)
and
t_{\rm mix}^{S_{A\otimes E}}(\varepsilon)
. In fact, we choose the certificate operator
\sum_{j=1}^{n}X_{j}\otimes I_{E}
and recall the definition of
\widehat{C}_{S_{A\otimes E}}
:

\displaystyle\widehat{C}_{S_{A\otimes E}}\left(\left(E_{\rm fix}^{A^{n}}\right)^{*}\right)

\displaystyle=\sup_{X_{AE}\in\mathbb{B}(AE),\||X_{AE}|\|_{S_{A\otimes E}}\neq 0}\frac{\|(\mathcal{E}((E_{\rm fix}^{A^{n}})^{*}-id_{A^{n}})\mathcal{D})^{*}(X_{AE})\|_{\infty}}{\||X_{AE}|\|_{S_{A\otimes E}}}

\displaystyle=\sup_{X_{AE}\in\mathbb{B}(AE),\||X_{AE}|\|_{S_{A\otimes E}}\neq 0}\frac{\|\mathcal{D}^{*}(E_{\rm fix}^{A^{n}}-id_{A^{n}})\mathcal{E}^{*}(X_{AE})\|_{\infty}}{\||X_{AE}|\|_{S_{A\otimes E}}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\|\mathcal{D}^{*}(E_{\rm fix}^{A^{n}}-id_{A^{n}})(\sum_{j=1}^{n}X_{j})\|}{\||\sum_{j=1}^{n}X_{j}\otimes I_{E}|\|_{S_{A\otimes E}}}=\frac{\|\sum_{j=1}^{n}X_{j}\|_{\infty}}{\sup_{k}\|[Y_{k},\sum_{j=1}^{n}X_{j}]\otimes X_{E}\|_{\infty}}=\frac{n}{2}.

This directly implies
\widehat{C}^{cb}_{S_{A\otimes E}}\left(\left(E_{\rm fix}^{A^{n}}\right)^{*}\right)\geq\widehat{C}_{S_{A\otimes E}}\left(\left(E_{\rm fix}^{A^{n}}\right)^{*}\right)\geq\frac{n}{2}
. Similarly, we can check that

\displaystyle\widehat{C}^{cb}_{S_{A\otimes E}}(T_{t})

\displaystyle\geq\widehat{C}_{S_{A\otimes E}}(T_{t})=\sup_{X_{AE}\in\mathbb{B}(AE),\||X_{AE}|\|_{S_{A\otimes E}}\neq 0}\frac{\|(\mathcal{E}(T_{t}-id_{A^{n}})\mathcal{D})^{*}(X_{AE})\|_{\infty}}{\||X_{AE}|\|_{S_{A\otimes E}}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\|\mathcal{D}^{*}(T_{t}^{*}-id_{A^{n}})(\sum_{j=1}^{n}X_{j})\|}{\||\sum_{j=1}^{n}X_{j}\otimes I_{E}|\|_{S_{A\otimes E}}}=(1-e^{-t})\frac{\|\sum_{j=1}^{n}X_{j}\|}{\||\sum_{j=1}^{n}X_{j}\otimes I_{E}|\|_{S_{A\otimes E}}}=\frac{(1-e^{-t})n}{2},

thus we can show that
t_{\rm mix}^{S_{A\otimes E}}
is bounded by a universal constant. Using Theorem
5.11
, we find a tight lower bound for
\widehat{\mathrm{M}}_{\alpha,\beta}
in terms of the number of qubits
n
:

C_{\alpha,\beta}\frac{n}{\tau+6}\leq\widehat{\mathrm{M}}_{\alpha,\beta}\leq\frac{\pi n}{\tau}.

(5.43)

Remark 5.19
.

Using Theorem
5.13
, we can get the desirable behavior of linear dependency

\widehat{l}_{\delta}^{\mathcal{U}_{A\otimes E}}(T_{t})=\Theta(nt).

6
Conclusion

Our technique for the lower bound of simulation cost of open quantum systems is based on the convex cost function defined in Section
4
. The linear in time behavior of the lower bounds that already have been observed in
Berry et al. (2015)
;
Childs and Li (2016)
is captured in our Theorem
4.15
. Moreover, our dimension factor
\frac{\kappa^{cb}(S)}{t_{\rm{mix}}^{S}}
exhibits a new phenomenon, which works for a more general class of generators than presented in previous works. Explicit estimates of the dimension factor are given for
n
qubit Pauli-type and amplitude damping noise and the tightness of our lower bound is illustrated by showing that the upper and lower bounds coincide in those two examples. We leave the concrete analysis for more complicated generators for future research.
