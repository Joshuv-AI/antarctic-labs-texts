Introduction

Density of subsets.

Let
A
be a subset of a finite set
E
. Denote
|E|
,
|A|
their cardinalities. In
[
9
]
9.A, M. Gromov defined the
density
of
A
in
E
as

\dens_{E}(A):=\log_{|E|}(|A|).

Namely,
\dens_{E}(A)
is the number
d\in\{-\infty\}\cup[0,1]
such that
|A|=|E|^{d}
. Note that
d=-\infty
if and only if
A=\textup{\O}
. If the set
E
is fixed, we omit the subscript and simply denote the density by
\dens A
.

In
[
9
]
p.270, the
intersection formula
is stated as follows:
Random subsets
A
and
B
of a finite set
E
satisfy

\dens(A\cap B)=\dens A+\dens B-1

with the convention

\dens A<0\iff A=\textup{\O}.

If
E
is a finite-dimensional vector space over a finite field, every affine subspace
A
satisfies
\dens A=\dim A/\dim E
. The intersection formula is then a "random subset version" of the well-known result for affine subspaces:
Transversal affine subspaces
A
and
B
of a vector space
E
satisfy

\dim(A\cap B)=\dim A+\dim B-\dim E

with the convention

\dim A<0\iff A=\textup{\O}.

Purpose of the paper.

In
[
9
]
p.270 "explanation", Gromov did not give a precise definition of a random subset with density. In
[
9
]
p.272, he proposed that one can consider the class of random subsets defined by measures
invariant under the permutations of
E
.

In this article, we discuss two basic models of random subsets that are contained in the permutation invariant model: The
uniform density model
and the
Bernoulli density model
. The first one is defined by the uniform distribution on all subsets of
E
with cardinality
\lfloor|E|^{d}\rfloor
. This model is used by Y. Ollivier in
[
15
]
,
[
16
]
and
[
18
]
to study the density model of random groups, and by A. Żuk in
[
20
]
to construct random triangular groups. For the Bernoulli density model, every element in
E
is taken independently with the same probability
|E|^{d-1}
. This model is considered by Antoniuk-Łuczak-Świątkowski in
[
1
]
to study random triangular groups.

The aim of this article is to establish a general framework for the study of random subsets with densities, and to proof the intersection formula for the class of random subsets that are
densable
and
permutation invariant
.

Random subsets and the intersection formula.

In the first section, we introduce the notion of
densable sequences of random subsets
. Let
E
be a finite set. A
random subset
of
E
is a
\mathcal{P}(E)
-valued random variable, where
\mathcal{P}(E)
is the set of subsets of
E
. Note that
|A|
is a usual real-valued random variable. The
density
of
A
in
E
, defined by
\dens_{E}A:=\log_{|E|}(|A|)
, is hence a random variable with values in
\{-\infty\}\cup[0,1]
.

As our approach is asymptotic when
|E|\to\infty
, we consider a sequence of finite sets
\boldsymbol{E}=(E_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
where
|E_{n}|\to\infty
. A
sequence of random subsets
of
\boldsymbol{E}
is a sequence
\boldsymbol{A}=(A_{n})
where
A_{n}
is a random subset of
E_{n}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Such a sequence is
densable
with density
d\in\{-\infty\}\cup[0,1]
if the sequence of random variables
\dens_{E_{n}}(A_{n})
converges weakly (i.e. converges in distribution) to the constant
d
(c.f.
[
9
]
p.272). For a sequence of properties
\boldsymbol{Q}=(Q_{n})
, we say that
Q_{n}
is
asymptotically almost surely
(a.a.s.) satisfied if the probability that
Q_{n}
is satisfied goes to
1
when
n\to\infty
. For example, for a sequence of random subsets
\boldsymbol{A}=(A_{n})
,
\dens\boldsymbol{A}=-\infty
if and only if a.a.s.
A_{n}=\textup{\O}
.

In Section 2 we work on the permutation invariant model in
[
9
]
p.272. Let
E
be a finite set. A random subset
A
of
E
is
permutation invariant
if its law is invariant under the permutations of
E
. Namely, for any subset
a\in\mathcal{P}(E)
and any permutation
\sigma\in\mathcal{S}(E)
, we have
\mathbf{Pr}(A=a)=\mathbf{Pr}(A=\sigma(a))
.

Consider a sequence of finite sets
\boldsymbol{E}=(E_{n})
with
|E_{n}|\to\infty
. Denote by
\mathcal{D}(\boldsymbol{E})
the class of
densable sequences of permutation invariant random subsets
of
\boldsymbol{E}
. We prove the intersection formula stated as follows:

Theorem 1

(The intersection formula, Theorem
2.9
)
.

Let
\boldsymbol{A}=(A_{n}),\boldsymbol{B}=(B_{n})
be independent sequences of random subsets in
\mathcal{D}(\boldsymbol{E})
with densities
\alpha,\beta
. If
\alpha+\beta\neq 1
, then the sequence of random subsets
\boldsymbol{A}\cap\boldsymbol{B}
is also in
\mathcal{D}(\boldsymbol{E})
. In addition:

\dens(\boldsymbol{A}\cap\boldsymbol{B})=\begin{cases}\alpha+\beta-1&\textup{ if }\alpha+\beta>1\\
-\infty&\textup{ if }\alpha+\beta<1.\end{cases}

The density
-\infty
means that a.a.s. the random subset is empty.

In Section 3, we study the intersection between a random subset and a
fixed
subset. We develop a generalized form: the
multi-dimensional intersection formula
. Let
\boldsymbol{E}=(E_{n})
be a sequence of finite sets with
|E_{n}|\to\infty
. Denote
E_{n}^{(k)}
the set of pairwise distinct
k
-tuples of the set
E_{n}
. Let
\boldsymbol{A}
be a sequence of random subsets in
\mathcal{D}(\boldsymbol{E})
(densable and permutation invariant). We are interested in the intersection between
\boldsymbol{A}^{(k)}
and a densable sequence of subsets
\boldsymbol{X}
of
\boldsymbol{E}^{(k)}
.

For
k\geq 2
, the intersection formula is in general not correct (see example
3.3
). We show that by an additional
self-intersection condition
on
\boldsymbol{X}
, we can achieve an intersection formula.

Theorem 2

(The multi-dimensional intersection formula, Theorem
3.7
)
.

Let
\boldsymbol{A}=(A_{n})
be a sequence of random subsets in
\mathcal{D}(\boldsymbol{E})
with density
0<d<1
. Let
\boldsymbol{X}=(X_{n})
be a densable sequence of fixed subsets of
\boldsymbol{E}^{(k)}
with density
\alpha
.

(i)

If
d+\alpha<1
, then a.a.s.

A_{n}^{(k)}\cap X_{n}=\textup{\O}.

(ii)

If
d+\alpha>1
and
\boldsymbol{X}
satisfies the
d
-small self intersection condition (Definition
3.6
), then the sequence of random subsets
\boldsymbol{A}^{(k)}\cap\boldsymbol{X}
is densable and

\dens(\boldsymbol{A}^{(k)}\cap\boldsymbol{X})=\alpha+d-1.

The intersection formula in
\boldsymbol{E}
between a random subset and a fixed subset is a special case of this theorem by taking
k=1
.

Applications to group theory: random groups.

The last section is dedicated to applications to group theory, more precisely to small cancellation theory.

The first mention of generic property for finitely presented groups appears in the late 80’s in the works of V. S. Guba
[
11
]
and M. Gromov
[
8
]
. In
[
11
]
, the author showed that for "almost every" group presented by
m\geq 4
generators and one "long" relator, any
2
-generated subgroup is free. In
[
8
]
, Gromov defined two models of random group presentations with fixed number of generators and relators.

In 1993, Gromov introduced the
density model
of random groups in
[
9
]
9.B. The number of generators is still fixed, but the number of relators
grows exponentially
with the length of the relators, determined by a density parameter
d
. A
phase transition phenomenon
is then stated as follows: if
d<1/2
, then a.a.s. the random group is infinite hyperbolic; whereas if
d>1/2
, then a.a.s. the random group is trivial.

In a 1996 paper
[
2
]
, G. Arzhantseva and A. Ol’shanskii generalized Guba’s result. They proved that for "almost every" group presented by
m\geq 2
generators and
k\geq 1
long relators , any
(m-1)
-generated subgroup is free. In their model, the number of generators
k
is fixed, as in Gromov’s 1987 model
[
8
]
. This model is called the Arzhantseva-Ol’shanskii model, or the
few relator model
of random groups.

For more detailed surveys on random groups, see (in chronological order)
[
7
]
by E. Ghys,
[
17
]
by Y. Ollivier,
[
14
]
by I. Kapovich and P. Schupp and
[
6
]
by F. Bassino, C. Nicaud and P. Weil.

Fix a set of alphabets
X=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{m}\}
as generators of groups. Denote by
B_{\ell}
the set of cyclically reduced words of
X^{\pm}
of lengths at most
\ell
. If
S_{\ell}
is the set of cyclically reduced words of length exactly
\ell
, it is clear that
2m(2m-1)^{\ell-2}(2m-2)\leq|S_{\ell}|\leq 2m(2m-1)^{\ell-1}
. So

\frac{2m}{2m-1}\left[(2m-1)^{\ell}-1\right]\leq|B_{\ell}|\leq\frac{2m}{2m-2}\left[(2m-1)^{\ell}-1\right].

As we are interested in asymptotic behaviors when
\ell\to\infty
, we can write
|B_{\ell}|=(2m-1)^{\ell+O(1)}
. Consider
\boldsymbol{B}=(B_{\ell})_{\ell\geq 1}
as our ambient sequence of sets. Let
d\in\{-\infty\}\cup[0,1]
. A
sequence of random groups
with density
d
, denoted by
\boldsymbol{G}(m,d)=(G_{\ell}(m,d))
, is defined by random presentations
G_{\ell}(m,d)=\langle X|R_{\ell}\rangle
where
\boldsymbol{R}=(R_{\ell})
is a densable sequence of permutation invariant random subsets of
\boldsymbol{B}
with density
d
.

The first mention of the
\lambda/2
phase transition for the
C^{\prime}(\lambda)
-small cancellation condition is by Gromov in
[
9
]
p.273, showing that if
2d<\lambda
then a random group at density
d
satisfies
C^{\prime}(\lambda)
. He remarked also that, in particular, if
d<1/12
then the group is hyperbolic; and if
d>1/12
then the group is not
C^{\prime}(1/6)
. Ollivier-Wise gave a detailed proof of
d<\lambda/2
implying
C^{\prime}(\lambda)
in
[
19
]
Proposition 1.8. In
[
17
]
p.31 Ollivier stated the phase transition : if
d>\lambda/2
then
C^{\prime}(\lambda)
does not hold. However, his "dimension reasoning" is the
2
-dimensional intersection formula between a random subset (pairs of distinct relators in a random group) and a
fixed
subset (pairs of distinct relators denying
C^{\prime}(\lambda)
), which does not hold in general (as Example 3.3 shows).

Bassino-Nicaud-Weil gave a proof of
d>\lambda/2
implying non-
C^{\prime}(\lambda)
in
[
6
]
p.7 (Theorem 2.1). Their argument showed that the pairs of distinct relators
in a random group
denying
C^{\prime}(\lambda)
is not empty, but did not give its density.

The
d
-small self-intersection condition (Definition 3.6) for a fixed subset is introduced to rule out this difficulty. By the multi-dimensional intersection formula (
Theorem 2
), we show that if
d>\lambda/2
, then the pairs of distinct relators in a random group denying
C^{\prime}(\lambda)
is with density
d-\lambda/2
and hence not empty.

Theorem 3

(Phase transition at density
\lambda/2
, Theorem
4.3
)
.

Let
\boldsymbol{G}(m,d)=(G_{\ell}(m,d))
be a random group with
m
generators and with density
d
. Let
\lambda\in]0,1[
.

1.

If
d<\lambda/2
, then a.a.s.
G_{\ell}(m,d)
satisfies
C^{\prime}(\lambda)
.

2.

If
d>\lambda/2
, then a.a.s.
G_{\ell}(m,d)
does not satisfy
C^{\prime}(\lambda)
.

It was given as an "interesting problem" in
[
17
]
I.3.c that some algebraic properties of random groups at density
0
(
[
2
]
by Arzhantseva-Ol’shanskii,
[
3
]
,
[
4
]
,
[
5
]
by Arzhantseva, and
[
13
]
by Kapovich-Schupp) may extend to some positive density
d
. In
[
14
]
Theorem 7.5, Kapovich and Schupp extends Arzhantzeva’s "all
L
-generated subgroups of infinite index are free" result
[
3
]
(for a fixed
L>0
) to some density
d>0
independent of
m
. A property is called "low-density random" by Kapovich-Schupp in
[
14
]
p.3 if the corresponding density

is positive but converges to

when
m
goes to infinity. They claimed that Arzhantseva-Ol’shanskii’s "all
(m-1)
-generated subgroups are free" result
[

is a low-density random property (
[

Theorem 1.1 (2), Theorem 5.4 (2)), but the density

In our study, the number of generators

is fixed, and we look for a density

that extends Arzhantseva-Ol’shanskii’s result in

from density

to density

Theorem 4

(Every
(m-1)
-generated subgroup is free, Theorem
4.5
)
.

Let
(G_{\ell}(m,d))
be a sequence of random groups with

generators and with density

Then a.a.s. every

1
Definitions and basic models

1.1
Densable sequences of random subsets

Let
E
be a finite set, denote
|E|
its cardinality. The following definition is due to M. Gromov in
[
9
]
.

Definition 1.1
.

Let
E
be a finite non-empty set and
A\subset E
. The density of
A
in
E
is defined by

\dens_{E}A:=\log_{|E|}|A|=\frac{\log|A|}{\log|E|}.

So that
d\in[0,1]\cup\{-\infty\}
is a real number such that
|E|^{d}=|A|
.

We will omit the subscript
E
if the set is fixed and simply denote the density by
\dens A
. Note that
\dens A=-\infty
if and only if
A=\textup{\O}
.

Definition 1.2
.

Let
E
be a finite set. Denote
\mathcal{P}(E)
the set of subsets of
E
. A random subset
A
of
E
is a
\mathcal{P}(E)
-valued random variable.

In this article, we use upper-case letters
A,B,C,\dots
to denote random subsets and lower-case letters
a,b,c,\dots
to denote fixed subsets. The law of a random subset
A
is determined by instances
\mathbf{Pr}(A=a)
through all subsets
a\in\mathcal{P}(E)
(or
a\subset E
). Its cardinality
|A|
is a usual real-valued random variable.

Here we give some basic examples of random subsets.

Example 1.3
.

(Examples of random subsets)

(i)

(Dirac model) A fixed subset
c\subset E
can be regarded as a constant random subset. Its law is

\mathbf{Pr}(A=a)=\begin{cases}1&\textup{ if }a=c\\
0&\textup{ if }a\neq c.\end{cases}

(ii)

(Uniform random subset) Fix an integer
k\leq|E|
. Let
A
be the uniform distribution on all subsets of
E
of cardinality
k
. Its law is

\mathbf{Pr}(A=a)=\begin{cases}\binom{|E|}{k}^{-1}&\textup{ if }|a|=k\\
0&\textup{ if }|a|\neq k.\end{cases}

(iii)

(Bernoulli random subset) Let
A
be the Bernoulli sampling of parameter
p\in[0,1]
on the set
E
: The events
\{x\in A\}
through all
x\in E
are independent of the same probability
p
. The law of
A
is

\mathbf{Pr}(A=a)=p^{|a|}(1-p)^{|E|-|a|}.

In this case
|A|
follows the binomial law
\mathcal{B}(|E|,p)
.

As usual random variables, a random subset can be constructed by other random subsets.

Example 1.4
.

(Set theoretic operations)
The intersection of two random subsets
A,B
of a finite set
E
is another random subset. The law of
A\cap B
is

\mathbf{Pr}(A\cap B=c)=\sum_{a,b\in\mathcal{P}(E);a\cap b=c}\mathbf{Pr}(A=a,B=b).

In particular, if
A,B
are
independent
random subsets, then

\mathbf{Pr}(A\cap B=c)=\sum_{a,b\in\mathcal{P}(E);a\cap b=c}\mathbf{Pr}(A=a)\mathbf{Pr}(B=b).

The union of two subsets and the complement of a subset are similarly defined.

We are interested in the asymptotic behavior of random subsets when
|E|\to\infty
. Consider a sequence of finite sets
\boldsymbol{E}=(E_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
with
|E_{n}|\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}\infty
. Recall that the density of a subset
a\subset E
is defined by
\dens_{E}(a):=\log_{|E|}|a|
.

Definition 1.5

(Densable sequence of random subsets)
.

(i)

A sequence of (fixed) subsets of
\boldsymbol{E}=(E_{n})
is a sequence
\boldsymbol{a}=(a_{n})
such that
a_{n}\subset E_{n}
for all
n
.

A sequence of subsets
\boldsymbol{a}
is
densable
with density
d\in[0,1]\cup\{-\infty\}
if

\dens_{E_{n}}(a_{n})=\log_{|E_{n}|}|a_{n}|\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}d.

(ii)

Similarly, a sequence of random subsets of
\boldsymbol{E}
is a sequence
\boldsymbol{A}=(A_{n})
such that
A_{n}
is a random subset of
E_{n}
for all
n
.

A sequence of random subsets
\boldsymbol{A}
is
densable
with density
d\in[0,1]\cup\{-\infty\}
if the sequence of real-valued random valuables
\dens_{E_{n}}(A_{n})=\log_{|E_{n}|}|A_{n}|
converges in distribution to the constant
d
.

(iii)

Two sequences of random subsets
\boldsymbol{A}=(A_{n})
,
\boldsymbol{B}=(B_{n})
of
\boldsymbol{E}
are independent if
A_{n}
,
B_{n}
are independent random subsets of
E_{n}
for all
n
.

Here we give some examples of sequences of random subsets.

Example 1.6

(Examples of densable sequences of random subsets)
.

(i)

For a fixed sequence of subsets
\boldsymbol{a}=(a_{n})
,
\dens(\boldsymbol{a})=-\infty
if and only if
a_{n}=\textup{\O}
for large enough
n
.

(ii)

A densable sequence of subsets
\boldsymbol{a}=(a_{n})
can be regarded as a densable sequence of random subsets (Dirac model on each term). If we take
|a_{n}|=\lfloor|E_{n}|^{d}\rfloor
with some
0\leq d\leq 1
, then
\boldsymbol{a}
is densable with density
d
.

(iii)

(Uniform density model) Let
\boldsymbol{A}=(A_{n})
be a sequence of random subsets of
\boldsymbol{E}
.
\boldsymbol{A}
is a sequence of uniform random subsets with density
d
if
A_{n}
is the uniform distribution on all subsets of
E_{n}
of cardinality
\lfloor|E_{n}|^{d}\rfloor
. Its law is

\mathbf{Pr}(A_{n}=a)=\begin{cases}\binom{|E_{n}|}{\lfloor|E_{n}|^{d}\rfloor}^{-1}&\textup{ if }|a|=\lfloor|E_{n}|^{d}\rfloor\\
0&\textup{ if }|a|\neq\lfloor|E_{n}|^{d}\rfloor.\end{cases}

(iv)

(Bernoulli density model) Let
d>0
. If
A_{n}
is a Bernoulli sampling of
E_{n}
with parameter
|E_{n}|^{d-1}
, then
\boldsymbol{A}=(A_{n})
is a sequence of densable random subsets of
\boldsymbol{E}
. It is rather not obvious that such sequences are densable (see Proposition
1.12
).

Definition 1.7
.

Let
\boldsymbol{Q}=(Q_{n})
be a sequence of events. The event
Q_{n}
is
asymptotically almost surely
true if
\mathbf{Pr}(Q_{n})\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}1
.

Equivalently, for any
p<1
arbitrary close to
1
we have
\mathbf{Pr}(Q_{n})>p
for
n
large enough. We denote briefly a.a.s.
Q_{n}
.

For example, if
\boldsymbol{A}
is a sequence of random subsets with
\dens\boldsymbol{A}=-\infty
, then
\mathbf{Pr}(|A_{n}|=0)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}1
. Which is equivalent to a.a.s.
|A_{n}|=0
, or a.a.s.
A_{n}=\textup{\O}
.

Proposition 1.8

(Characterization of densability)
.

Let
\boldsymbol{A}
be a sequence of random subsets of
\boldsymbol{E}
. Let
d\geq 0
.
\boldsymbol{A}
is densable with density
d
if and only if

\forall\varepsilon>0\textup{ a.a.s. }|E_{n}|^{d-\varepsilon}\leq|A_{n}|\leq|E_{n}|^{d+\varepsilon}.

Proof.

The convergence in distribution to a constant is equivalent to the convergence in probability. So
\log_{|E_{n}|}|A_{n}|
converges in distribution to
d
if and only if

\forall\varepsilon>0\quad\mathbf{Pr}(|\log_{|E_{n}|}|A_{n}|-d|\leq\varepsilon)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}1,

which gives the estimation

\forall\varepsilon>0\textup{ a.a.s. }|E_{n}|^{d-\varepsilon}\leq|A_{n}|\leq|E_{n}|^{d+\varepsilon}.

∎

In general, the intersection of two densable sequences is not necessarily densable. The intersection formula is then
not
satisfied by the class of densable sequences of random subsets. Here is a simple example.

Example 1.9
.

Let
\boldsymbol{E}=(E_{n})
be a sequence of sets with
|E_{n}|=2n
. Let
\boldsymbol{a}=(a_{n})
,
\boldsymbol{b}=(b_{n})
be sequences of subsets of
\boldsymbol{E}
such that
b_{n}=E_{n}\backslash a_{n}
and
|a_{n}|=|b_{n}|=n
. They are both densable subsets with density
1
because
\log(n)/\log(2n)\to 1
. Whereas
\dens(\boldsymbol{a}\cap\boldsymbol{b})=-\infty
. They do not verify the intersection formula.

Define another sequence of subset
\boldsymbol{c}=(c_{n})
by
c_{n}:=a_{n}
if
n
is odd and
c_{n}:=b_{n}
if
n
is even. By its definition,
\boldsymbol{c}
is densable with density
1
. But the intersection
\boldsymbol{b}\cap\boldsymbol{c}
is empty when
n
is odd and non-empty when
n
is even, so
\boldsymbol{b}\cap\boldsymbol{c}
is not densable.

1.2
The Bernoulli density model

Let
\boldsymbol{E}=(E_{n})
with
|E_{n}|\to\infty
be the ambient sequence of sets.

Definition 1.10

(Bernoulli density model)
.

Let
d\leq 1
. Let
\boldsymbol{A}=(A_{n})
be a sequence of random subsets of
\boldsymbol{E}
. It is a sequence of Bernoulli random subsets with density
d
if
A_{n}
is a Bernoulli sampling of
E_{n}
with parameter
|E_{n}|^{d-1}
.

This model is particularly easy to manipulate. We will see that it is densable, closed under intersection and verifies the intersection formula.

Recall that the real-valued random variable
|A_{n}|
follows the binomial law
\mathcal{B}(|E_{n}|,|E_{n}|^{d-1})
. Thus
\mathbb{E}(|A_{n}|)=|E_{n}|^{d}
.

Lemma 1.11

(Concentration lemma)
.

Let
\boldsymbol{A}
be a sequence of Bernoulli random subsets with density
d>0
. Then a.a.s.

\left||A_{n}|-|E_{n}|^{d}\right|\leq\frac{1}{2}|E_{n}|^{d}.

Proof.

By Chebyshev’s inequality,

\mathbf{Pr}\left(\left||A_{n}|-|E_{n}|^{d}\right|>\frac{1}{2}|E_{n}|^{d}\right)\leq\frac{\Var(|A_{n}|)}{\frac{1}{4}|E_{n}|^{2d}}\leq\frac{4|E_{n}|^{d}(1-|E_{n}|^{d-1})}{|E_{n}|^{2d}}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0.

∎

Proposition 1.12
.

Let
\boldsymbol{A}
be a sequence of Bernoulli random subsets with density
d
. If
d\neq 0
, then
\boldsymbol{A}
is densable and:

\dens\boldsymbol{A}=\begin{cases}d&\textup{ if }\;0<d\leq 1\\
-\infty&\textup{ if }\;d<0.\end{cases}

Proof.

(i)

If
d<0
, by Markov’s inequality

\mathbf{Pr}(|A_{n}|\geq 1)\leq|E_{n}|^{d}\to 0,

so
\mathbf{Pr}(A_{n}=\textup{\O})\to 1
and
\mathbf{Pr}(\log_{|E_{n}|}|A_{n}|=-\infty)\to 1
.

(ii)

If
0<d\leq 1
, by Lemma
1.11
a.a.s.

\frac{1}{2}|E_{n}|^{d}\leq|A_{n}|\leq\frac{3}{2}|E_{n}|^{d}.

For every
\varepsilon>0
, the inequality
|E_{n}|^{d-\varepsilon}<\frac{1}{2}|E_{n}|^{d}<\frac{3}{2}|E_{n}|^{d}<|E_{n}|^{d+\varepsilon}
holds for
n
large enough. Thus a.a.s.

|E_{n}|^{d-\varepsilon}\leq|A_{n}|\leq|E_{n}|^{d+\varepsilon}.

Hence
\boldsymbol{A}=(A_{n})
is densable with density
d
by Proposition
1.8
.

∎

Theorem 1.13

(The intersection formula for Bernoulli density model)
.

Let
\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{B}
be independent sequences of Bernoulli random subsets of
\boldsymbol{E}=(E_{n})
with densities
\alpha,\beta
. Then
\boldsymbol{A}\cap\boldsymbol{B}
is a sequence of Bernoulli random subsets of
\boldsymbol{E}
with density
\alpha+\beta-1
, and

\dens(\boldsymbol{A}\cap\boldsymbol{B})=\begin{cases}\alpha+\beta-1&\textup{ if }\alpha+\beta>1\\
-\infty&\textup{ if }\alpha+\beta<1.\end{cases}

Proof.

For every elements
x\in E_{n}
,
\mathbf{Pr}(x\in A_{n}\cap B_{n})=\mathbf{Pr}(x\in A_{n})\mathbf{Pr}(x\in B_{n})=|E_{n}|^{(\alpha+\beta-1)-1}
. In addition, for every pair of distinct elements
x,y
in
E_{n}

\begin{split}\mathbf{Pr}(x,y\in A_{n}\cap B_{n})&=\mathbf{Pr}(x,y\in A_{n})\mathbf{Pr}(x,y\in B_{n})\\
&=\mathbf{Pr}(x\in A_{n})\mathbf{Pr}(y\in A_{n})\mathbf{Pr}(x\in B_{n})\mathbf{Pr}(y\in B_{n})\\
&=\mathbf{Pr}(x\in A_{n}\cap B_{n})\mathbf{Pr}(y\in A_{n}\cap B_{n}).\end{split}

So
\boldsymbol{A}\cap\boldsymbol{B}
is a sequence of Bernoulli random subsets with density
\alpha+\beta-1
. Proposition
1.12
gives its density.
∎

As the theorem shows, the class of Bernoulli random subsets is
closed under intersections
. Thereby the intersection formula works for multiple independent sequences of random subsets. The formula is more concise in terms of
codensities
.

Definition 1.14

(c.f.
[
9
]
p.269)
.

Let
\boldsymbol{A}
be a densable sequence of random subsets such that
\dens\boldsymbol{A}\in[0,1]
. Then the codensity of
\boldsymbol{A}
is defined by:

\codens{\boldsymbol{A}}=1-\dens{\boldsymbol{A}}.

Theorem
1.13
can be rephrase as (compare
[
9
]
p.270):

Theorem
1.13
’

(The intersection formula by codensities)
.

Let
\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{B}
be independent sequences of Bernoulli random subsets of
\boldsymbol{E}
with positive densities. If
\codens\boldsymbol{A}+\codens\boldsymbol{B}<1
, then

\codens(\boldsymbol{A}\cap\boldsymbol{B})=\codens\boldsymbol{A}+\codens\boldsymbol{B}.

If
\codens\boldsymbol{A}+\codens\boldsymbol{B}>1
, then
\dens(\boldsymbol{A}\cap\boldsymbol{B})=-\infty
.

Corollary 1.15

(Generalized intersection formula by codensities)
.

Let
\boldsymbol{A}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{A}_{k}
be independent sequences of Bernoulli random subsets with positive densities. If
\displaystyle{\sum_{i=1}^{k}\codens\boldsymbol{A}_{i}<1}
, then

\codens\left(\bigcap_{i=1}^{k}\boldsymbol{A}_{i}\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\codens\boldsymbol{A}_{i}.

If
\displaystyle{\sum_{i=1}^{k}\codens\boldsymbol{A}_{i}>1}
, then
\dens\left(\bigcap_{i=1}^{k}\boldsymbol{A}_{i}\right)=-\infty
.

As an exception, a Bernoulli sequence of random subsets with density
d=0
is
not densable
.

Proposition 1.16
.

Let
\boldsymbol{A}
be a Bernoulli sequence with density
d=0
. Then
\boldsymbol{A}
is
not
densable. In fact,

\mathbf{Pr}(\dens A_{n}=-\infty)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}1/e\;.

Proof.

\mathbf{Pr}(|A_{n}|=0)=(1-|E_{n}|^{-1})^{|E_{n}|}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}1/e
, which gives

\mathbf{Pr}(\dens A_{n}=-\infty)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}1/e.

This justifies that the sequence of random variables
(\dens_{E_{n}}A_{n})
does not converge to any constant distribution.
∎

1.3
The uniform density model

The uniform density model is the first example of densable sequences of random subsets. It is introduced by M.Gromov
[
9
]
to construct random groups with fixed generators, and later developed by Y. Ollivier
[
17
]
. It is also used by A. Żuk
[
20
]
to study random triangular groups.

Let
\boldsymbol{E}=(E_{n})
be a sequence of sets. To simplify, we assume that
|E_{n}|=n
in this subsection. For an arbitrary sequence
\boldsymbol{E}
with
|E_{n}|\to\infty
we can proceed similar proofs by replacing
n
by
|E_{n}|
. Note that
|E_{n}|^{d}=n^{d}\sim\lfloor n^{d}\rfloor
while
n\to\infty
for
d\in[0,1]
.

Recall that a sequence of uniform random subsets (example
1.6
(iv)) of
(E_{n})
with density
d
is a sequence of random subsets
(A_{n})
with the following laws:

\mathbf{Pr}(A_{n}=a)=\begin{cases}\binom{n}{\lfloor n^{d}\rfloor}^{-1}&\textup{ if }|a|=\lfloor n^{d}\rfloor\\
0&\textup{ if }|a|\neq\lfloor n^{d}\rfloor.\end{cases}

We give here a concentration lemma for uniform density model, similar to Lemma
1.11
. For the proof we need Lemma
2.2
and Lemma
2.3
in the next section.

Lemma 1.17

(Concentration lemma for uniform density model)
.

Let
\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{B}
be independent sequences of uniform random subsets of
\boldsymbol{E}
with densities
\alpha,\beta\in[0,1]
. Then:

(i)

n^{\alpha+\beta-1}-2\leq\mathbb{E}(|A_{n}\cap B_{n}|)\leq n^{\alpha+\beta-1}
.

(ii)

If
\alpha<1
and
\beta<1
, then
\Var(|A_{n}\cap B_{n}|)\sim n^{\alpha+\beta-1}
.

Moreover, if
n\geq 3
, then
\Var(|A_{n}\cap B_{n}|)\leq 3n^{\alpha+\beta-1}
.

(iii)

Let
0<c<1
. If
\alpha+\beta-1>0
and
n\geq\left(\frac{4}{c}\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha+\beta-1}}
, then

\mathbf{Pr}\left(\left||A_{n}\cap B_{n}|-n^{\alpha+\beta-1}\right|>cn^{\alpha+\beta-1}\right)\leq\frac{12}{c^{2}n^{\alpha+\beta-1}}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0.

In particular, a.a.s.

\left||A_{n}\cap B_{n}|-n^{\alpha+\beta-1}\right|\leq cn^{\alpha+\beta-1}.

Proof.

(i)

By Lemma
2.2
,
A_{n}\cap B_{n}
is a permutation invariant random set of
E_{n}
. Apply Lemma
2.3
:

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}(|A_{n}\cap B_{n}|)&=n\mathbf{Pr}(x\in A_{n}\cap B_{n})=n\mathbf{Pr}(x\in A_{n})\mathbf{Pr}(x\in B_{n})\\
&=n\frac{\mathbb{E}(|A_{n}|)}{n}\frac{\mathbb{E}(|B_{n}|)}{n}=\lfloor n^{\alpha}\rfloor\lfloor n^{\beta}\rfloor n^{-1}\sim n^{\alpha+\beta-1}.\end{split}

For the inequality, as
\alpha,\beta\leq 1
:

n^{\alpha+\beta-1}-2\leq n^{\alpha+\beta-1}-n^{\alpha-1}-n^{\beta-1}+n^{-1}\leq\lfloor n^{\alpha}\rfloor\lfloor n^{\beta}\rfloor n^{-1}\leq n^{\alpha+\beta-1}.

(ii)

Let
x,y
be distinct elemensts in
E
. The number of subsets of
E
containing
x,y
of cardinality
\lfloor n^{\alpha}\rfloor
is
\binom{n-2}{\lfloor n^{\alpha}\rfloor-2}
, so

\mathbf{Pr}(x,y\in A_{n})=\frac{\binom{n-2}{\lfloor n^{\alpha}\rfloor-2}}{\binom{n}{\lfloor n^{\alpha}\rfloor}}=\frac{\lfloor n^{\alpha}\rfloor(\lfloor n^{\alpha}\rfloor-1)}{n(n-1)}.

Similarly,

\mathbf{Pr}(x,y\in B_{n})=\frac{\lfloor n^{\beta}\rfloor(\lfloor n^{\beta}\rfloor-1)}{n(n-1)}.

Denote
k=\lfloor n^{\alpha}\rfloor
and
l=\lfloor n^{\beta}\rfloor
to simplify the notation. Note that
k=o(n)
and
l=o(n)
as
\alpha<1
and
\beta<1
. Recall that
\mathbb{E}(|A_{n}\cap B_{n}|)=kln^{-1}
. Apply Lemma
2.3
, the variance of
|A_{n}\cap B_{n}|
is

\begin{split}\Var(|A_{n}\cap B_{n}|)&=kln^{-1}+n(n-1)\mathbf{Pr}(x,y\in A_{n})\mathbf{Pr}(x,y\in B_{n})-(kln^{-1})^{2}\\
&=kln^{-1}+\frac{k(k-1)l(l-1)}{n(n-1)}-(kln^{-1})^{2}\\
&=\frac{kl}{n^{2}(n-1)}(n^{2}-n+nkl-nl-nk+n-nkl+kl)\\
&\sim\frac{kl}{n^{2}(n-1)}\cdot n^{2}\sim n^{\alpha+\beta-1}.\end{split}

Moreover, if
n\geq 3
then:

\Var(|A_{n}\cap B_{n}|)=\frac{kl}{n^{2}(n-1)}(n^{2}-nl-nk+kl)\\
\leq\frac{2kl}{n-1}\leq\frac{2n^{\alpha+\beta}}{n-1}\leq 3n^{\alpha+\beta-1}.

(iii)

By
(i)
if
n\geq\left(\frac{4}{c}\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha+\beta-1}}\geq 4
, then

\left|\mathbb{E}(|A_{n}\cap B_{n}|)-n^{\alpha+\beta-1}\right|\leq\frac{c}{2}n^{\alpha+\beta-1}.

If
\alpha=1
or
\beta=1
then the result is true as the
A_{n}=E_{n}
or
B_{n}=E_{n}
. Otherwise by
(ii)
and Chebyshev’s inequality, if
n\geq\left(\frac{4}{c}\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha+\beta-1}}
then

\begin{split}&\quad\mathbf{Pr}\left(\left||A_{n}\cap B_{n}|-n^{\alpha+\beta-1}\right|>cn^{\alpha+\beta-1}\right)\\
&\leq\mathbf{Pr}\left(\left||A_{n}\cap B_{n}|-\mathbb{E}(|A_{n}\cap B_{n}|)\right|>\frac{c}{2}n^{\alpha+\beta-1}\right)\\
&\leq\frac{4\Var(|A_{n}\cap B_{n}|)}{c^{2}n^{2\alpha+2\beta-2}}\leq\frac{12}{c^{2}n^{\alpha+\beta-1}}.\end{split}

∎

Proposition 1.18

(The intersection formula for uniform density model)
.

Let
\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{B}
be independent sequences of uniform random subsets of
\boldsymbol{E}
with densities
\alpha,\beta
. If
\alpha+\beta\neq 1
, then the sequence
\boldsymbol{A}\cap\boldsymbol{B}
is densable and

\dens(\boldsymbol{A}\cap\boldsymbol{B})=\begin{cases}\alpha+\beta-1&\textup{ if }\alpha+\beta>1\\
-\infty&\textup{ if }\alpha+\beta<1.\end{cases}

Proof.

(i)

If
\alpha+\beta<1
, then by Markov’s inequality and Lemma
1.17

(i)
:

\mathbf{Pr}(|A_{n}\cap B_{n}|\geq 1)\leq\mathbb{E}(|A_{n}\cap B_{n}|)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0,

which implies a.a.s.
A_{n}\cap B_{n}=\textup{\O}
and
\dens(A\cap B)=-\infty
.

(ii)

If
\alpha+\beta>1
, by Lemma
1.17

(iii)
(with
c=1/2
) a.a.s.

\left||A_{n}\cap B_{n}|-n^{\alpha+\beta-1}\right|\leq\frac{1}{2}n^{\alpha+\beta-1},

so for all
\varepsilon>0
a.a.s.

n^{\alpha+\beta-1-\varepsilon}\leq|A_{n}\cap B_{n}|\leq n^{\alpha+\beta-1+\varepsilon}.

Hence by Proposition
1.8

\boldsymbol{A}\cap\boldsymbol{B}
is densable with density
\alpha+\beta-1
.

∎

The cardinality of
A_{n}\cap B_{n}
is close to
n^{\alpha+\beta-1}
with high probability, but not always. If
\alpha\neq 1
and
\beta\neq 1
, then for
n
large enough
\lfloor n^{\alpha}\rfloor+\lfloor n^{\beta}\rfloor<n
, so
\mathbf{Pr}(A_{n}\cap B_{n}=\textup{\O})\neq 0
.

Which means that
\boldsymbol{A}\cap\boldsymbol{B}
is not a sequence of uniform random subsets, so the class of sequences of uniform random subsets is
not
closed under intersection.

2
The general model: densable and permutation invariant

2.1
Densable sequences of permutation invariant random subsets

Let
E
be a finite set with cardinality
|E|=n
. Denote
\mathcal{S}(E)
as the group of permutations of
E
. The action of
\mathcal{S}(E)
on
E
can be extended on
\mathcal{P}(E)
, defined by
\sigma(\{x_{1},\dots,x_{k}\}):=\{\sigma(x_{1}),\dots,\sigma(x_{k})\}
.

Note that this action has
(n+1)
orbits of the form
\{a\in\mathcal{S}(E)\mid|a|=k\}
for
k\in\{0,\dots,n\}
. Moreover, the action commutes with set theoretic operations:
\sigma(E\backslash a)=E\backslash\sigma(a)
,
\sigma(a\cap b)=\sigma(a)\cap\sigma(b)
and
\sigma(a\cup b)=\sigma(a)\cup\sigma(b)
.

Definition 2.1

(Permutation invariant random subsets)
.

Let
A
be a random subset of
E
. It is permutation invariant if its law is invariant by the permutations of
E
. i.e.

\forall a\in\mathcal{P}(E)\;\forall\sigma\in\mathcal{S}(E)\quad\mathbf{Pr}(A=a)=\mathbf{Pr}(A=\sigma(a)).

Equivalently, subsets of
E
of the same cardinality are equiprobable. There exists real numbers
p_{0},\dots,p_{n}\in[0,1]
satisfying

\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}p_{k}=1

such that

\forall a\in\mathcal{P}(E)\quad|a|=k\Rightarrow\mathbf{Pr}(A=a)=p_{k}.

By definition, uniform random subsets and Bernoulli random subsets are permutation invariant. The advantage of such class of random subsets is that it is closed under set theoretic operations, especially under intersections.

Lemma 2.2

(Closed under set operations)
.

Let
E
be a finite set. The class of permutation invariant random subsets of
E
is closed under set theoretic operations (union, complement and intersection).

Proof.

(i)

(Complement) Let
A
be a permutation invariant random subset. Let
a\in\mathcal{P}(E)
and
\sigma\in\mathcal{S}(E)
. Then

\begin{split}\mathbf{Pr}(E\backslash A=a)&=\mathbf{Pr}(A=E\backslash a)=\mathbf{Pr}(A=\sigma(E\backslash a))\\
&=\mathbf{Pr}(A=E\backslash\sigma(a))=\mathbf{Pr}(E\backslash A=\sigma(a)).\end{split}

(ii)

(Intersection) Let
A,B
be independent permutation invariant random subsets. Then for
\sigma\in\mathcal{S}(E)

\begin{split}\mathbf{Pr}(A\cap B=c)&=\sum_{a,b\in\mathcal{P}(E);a\cap b=c}\mathbf{Pr}(A=a)\mathbf{Pr}(B=b)\\
&=\sum_{a,b\in\mathcal{P}(E);\sigma(a)\cap\sigma(b)=\sigma(c)}\mathbf{Pr}(A=\sigma(a))\mathbf{Pr}(B=\sigma(b))\\
&=\sum_{a^{\prime},b^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}(E);a^{\prime}\cap b^{\prime}=\sigma(c)}\mathbf{Pr}(A=a^{\prime})\mathbf{Pr}(B=b^{\prime})\quad(\textup{by substitution})\\
&=\mathbf{Pr}(A\cap B=\sigma(c)).\end{split}

(iii)

(Union) Let
A,B
be independent permutation invariant random subsets. Then
A\cup B=E\backslash((E\backslash A)\cap(E\backslash B))
. So
A\cup B
is permutation invariant.

∎

We shall express the expectation and the variance of the random variable
|A|
by
\mathbf{Pr}(x\in A)
and
\mathbf{Pr}(x\in A,y\in A)
where
x,y
are distinct elements in
E
.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
A
be a permutation invariant random subset of
E
. Let
x,y
be distinct elements in
E
. Then

(i)

\mathbb{E}(|A|)=n\mathbf{Pr}(x\in A)
,

(ii)

\Var(|A|)=\mathbb{E}(|A|)+n(n-1)\mathbf{Pr}(x\in A,y\in A)-\mathbb{E}(|A|)^{2}
.

Proof.

(i)

By definition the probability
\mathbf{Pr}(z\in A)
does not depend on the choice of element
z\in E
. So

\mathbb{E}(|A|)=\mathbb{E}\left(\sum_{z\in E}\mathbbm{1}_{z\in A}\right)=\sum_{z\in E}\mathbf{Pr}(z\in A)=n\mathbf{Pr}(x\in A).

(ii)

By the same argument, the probability
\mathbf{Pr}(z\in A,w\in A)
does not depend on the choice of pair of distinct elements
(z,w)
in
E
. So

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}(|A|^{2})&=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{z\in E}\mathbbm{1}_{z\in A}\right)^{2}\right]\\
&=\sum_{z\in E}\mathbf{Pr}(z\in A)+\sum_{(z,w)\in E^{2};z\neq w}\mathbf{Pr}(z\in A,w\in A)\\
&=\mathbb{E}(|A|)+n(n-1)\mathbf{Pr}(x\in A,y\in A).\end{split}

∎

A permutation invariant random subset can be decomposed into uniform random subsets.

Proposition 2.4

(Decomposition into uniform random subsets)
.

Let
A
be a permutation invariant random subset of
E
.

(i)

If
\mathbf{Pr}(|A|=k)\neq 0
, then the random subset
A
under the condition
\{|A|=k\}
is a uniform random subset on all subsets of
E
of cardinality
k
.

(ii)

Let
Q
be an event described by
A
(for example,
Q=\{x\in A\}
). Denote
\mathbb{N}_{A}=\{k\in\mathbb{N}\,|\,\mathbf{Pr}(|A|=k)\neq 0\}
, then

\mathbf{Pr}(Q)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{A}}\mathbf{Pr}(Q\mid|A|=k)\mathbf{Pr}(|A|=k).

Proof.

Suppose that
\mathbf{Pr}(|A|=k)\neq 0
. Let
a\subset E
of cardinal
k
. As
A
is permutation invariant,

\mathbf{Pr}(|A|=k)=\binom{n}{k}\mathbf{Pr}(A=a).

Hence

\mathbf{Pr}\left(A=a\,\middle|\,|A|=k\right)=\frac{\mathbf{Pr}(A=a)}{\mathbf{Pr}(|A|=k)}=\binom{n}{k}^{-1}.

If
|a|\neq k
then
\mathbf{Pr}\left(A=a\,\middle|\,|A|=k\right)=0
.

The second assertion is the formula of total probability.
∎

Definition 2.5
.

Let
\boldsymbol{A}=(A_{n})
be a sequence of random subsets of
\boldsymbol{E}=(E_{n})
. It is a sequence of permutation invariant random subset if
A_{n}
is a permutation invariant random subset of
E_{n}
for all
n
.

Notation
.

Let
\boldsymbol{E}=(E_{n})
be a sequence of finite sets. Denote
\mathcal{D}(\boldsymbol{E})
the class of densable sequences of permutation invariant random subsets of
\boldsymbol{E}
.

Example 2.6
.

1.

Sequences of Bernoulli random subsets of
\boldsymbol{E}
with density
d\neq 0
are in the class
\mathcal{D}(\boldsymbol{E})
.

2.

Sequences of uniform random subsets of
\boldsymbol{E}
are in the class
\mathcal{D}(\boldsymbol{E})
.

3.

Let
\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{B}
be independent sequences of uniform random subsets. By Lemma
2.2
, the sequence
\boldsymbol{A}\cap\boldsymbol{B}
is permutation invariant. By Proposition
1.18
, if
\dens\boldsymbol{A}+\dens\boldsymbol{B}\neq 1
, then
\boldsymbol{A}\cap\boldsymbol{B}
is densable. In this case the sequence
\boldsymbol{A}\cap\boldsymbol{B}
is in the class
\mathcal{D}(\boldsymbol{E})
.

Except for some special cases, the class
\mathcal{D}(\boldsymbol{E})
is closed under set theoretic operations:

Proposition 2.7
.

Let
\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{B}\in\mathcal{D}(\boldsymbol{E})
with densities
\alpha,\beta
. Then the union
\boldsymbol{A}\cup\boldsymbol{B}
is in
\mathcal{D}(\boldsymbol{E})
and
\dens(\boldsymbol{A}\cup\boldsymbol{B})=\max(\alpha,\beta)
.

Proof.

By Lemma
2.2
the sequence of random subset
\boldsymbol{A}\cup\boldsymbol{B}
is permutation invariant. The cases
\alpha=0
or
\beta=0
can be easily shown. Without loss of generality, assume that
\alpha\geq\beta\geq 0
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
. By densabilities of
\boldsymbol{A}
and
\boldsymbol{B}
, a.a.s.

n^{\alpha-\varepsilon/2}\leq|A_{n}|\leq n^{\alpha+\varepsilon/2},

n^{\beta-\varepsilon/2}\leq|B_{n}|\leq n^{\beta+\varepsilon/2}.

Thus a.a.s.

n^{\alpha-\varepsilon}\leq|A_{n}|\leq|A_{n}\cup B_{n}|\leq n^{\alpha+\varepsilon/2}+n^{\beta+\varepsilon/2}\leq 2n^{\alpha+\varepsilon/2}\leq n^{\alpha+\varepsilon}.

∎

Proposition 2.8
.

Let
\boldsymbol{A}\in\mathcal{D}(\boldsymbol{E})
with density
\alpha<1
. Then the complement
\boldsymbol{E}\backslash\boldsymbol{A}
is in
\mathcal{D}(\boldsymbol{E})
and
\dens(\boldsymbol{E}\backslash\boldsymbol{A})=1
.

Proof.

Again by Lemma
2.2
the sequence of random subset
\boldsymbol{E}\backslash\boldsymbol{A}
is permutation invariant.

Let
0<\varepsilon<(1-\alpha)/2
. By densablility of
\boldsymbol{A}
, a.a.s.

|A_{n}|\leq n^{\alpha+\varepsilon}.

As
n^{\alpha+\varepsilon}+n^{1-\varepsilon}\leq n
for
n
large enough, a.a.s.

|E_{n}\backslash A_{n}|\geq n-n^{\alpha+\varepsilon}\geq n^{1-\varepsilon}.

∎

2.2
The intersection formula

In this subsection we shall prove the intersection formula for the class of densable sequences of permutation invariant random subsets.

Theorem 2.9

(The intersection formula)
.

Let
\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{B}
be independent sequences in
\mathcal{D}(\boldsymbol{E})
with densities
\alpha,\beta
. If
\alpha+\beta\neq 1
, then the sequence
\boldsymbol{A}\cap\boldsymbol{B}
is in
\mathcal{D}(\boldsymbol{E})
and

\dens(\boldsymbol{A}\cap\boldsymbol{B})=\begin{cases}\alpha+\beta-1&\textup{ if }\alpha+\beta>1\\
-\infty&\textup{ if }\alpha+\beta<1.\end{cases}

Lemma 2.10
.

Let
\alpha,\beta\in[0,1]
such that
\alpha+\beta>1
. Let
0<\varepsilon<\alpha+\beta-1
. Let
\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{B}
independent sequences of uniform random subsets of
\boldsymbol{E}
with densities
\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime}
with
\alpha^{\prime}\in[\alpha-\varepsilon/3,\alpha+\varepsilon/3]
and
\beta^{\prime}\in[\beta-\varepsilon/3,\beta+\varepsilon/3]
. If
n\geq\max\left\{2^{3/\varepsilon},8^{1/(\alpha+\beta-1-\varepsilon)}\right\}
, then:

\mathbf{Pr}\left(n^{\alpha+\beta-1-\varepsilon}\leq|A_{n}\cap B_{n}|\leq n^{\alpha+\beta-1+\varepsilon}\right)\geq 1-\frac{48}{n^{\alpha+\beta-1-\varepsilon}}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}1.

Proof.

By hypothesis
\alpha^{\prime}+\beta^{\prime}-1\geq\alpha+\beta-2\varepsilon/3-1>0
. Apply Lemma
1.17

(iii)
with
c=\frac{1}{2}
, for
n\geq 8^{1/(\alpha+\beta-1-\varepsilon)}\geq 8^{1/(\alpha^{\prime}+\beta^{\prime}-1)}
:

\mathbf{Pr}\left(\left||A_{n}\cap B_{n}|-n^{\alpha^{\prime}+\beta^{\prime}-1}\right|\geq\frac{1}{2}n^{\alpha^{\prime}+\beta^{\prime}-1}\right)\leq\frac{48}{n^{\alpha^{\prime}+\beta^{\prime}-1}}.

This can be rewrite as

\mathbf{Pr}\left(\frac{1}{2}n^{\alpha^{\prime}+\beta^{\prime}-1}<|A_{n}\cap B_{n}|<\frac{3}{2}n^{\alpha^{\prime}+\beta^{\prime}-1}\right)>1-\frac{48}{n^{\alpha^{\prime}+\beta^{\prime}-1}}.

Again by hypothesis
\alpha+\beta-1-2\varepsilon/3\leq\alpha^{\prime}+\beta^{\prime}-1\leq\alpha+\beta-1+2\varepsilon/3
. If
n\geq 2^{3/\varepsilon}
, then

n^{\alpha+\beta-1-\varepsilon}\leq\frac{1}{2}n^{\alpha+\beta-1-2\varepsilon/3}\leq\frac{3}{2}n^{\alpha+\beta-1+2\varepsilon/3}\leq n^{\alpha+\beta-1+\varepsilon},

so:

\begin{split}&\mathbf{Pr}\left(n^{\alpha+\beta-1-\varepsilon}\leq|A_{n}\cap B_{n}|\leq n^{\alpha+\beta-1+\varepsilon}\right)\\
\geq&\mathbf{Pr}\left(\frac{1}{2}n^{\alpha+\beta-1-2\varepsilon/3}\leq|A_{n}\cap B_{n}|\leq\frac{3}{2}n^{\alpha+\beta-1+2\varepsilon/3}\right)\\
\geq&\mathbf{Pr}\left(\frac{1}{2}n^{\alpha^{\prime}+\beta^{\prime}-1}<|A_{n}\cap B_{n}|<\frac{3}{2}n^{\alpha^{\prime}+\beta^{\prime}-1}\right).\end{split}

Combine two estimations on
n
. If
n\geq\max\left\{2^{3/\varepsilon},8^{1/(\alpha+\beta-1-\varepsilon)}\right\}
, then:

\mathbf{Pr}\left(n^{\alpha+\beta-1-\varepsilon}\leq|A_{n}\cap B_{n}|\leq n^{\alpha+\beta-1+\varepsilon}\right)\geq 1-\frac{48}{n^{\alpha^{\prime}+\beta^{\prime}-1}}\geq 1-\frac{48}{n^{\alpha+\beta-1-\varepsilon}}.

As
\alpha+\beta-1-\varepsilon>0
, when
n
goes to infinity

\frac{48}{n^{\alpha+\beta-1-\varepsilon}}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0.

∎

Proof of Theorem
2.9
.

By Lemma
2.2
the intersection
\boldsymbol{A}\cap\boldsymbol{B}
is a sequence of permutation invariant random subsets. In either cases, denote
(Q_{n})
the sequence of events defined by

Q_{n}=\{n^{\alpha-\varepsilon/3}\leq|A_{n}|\leq n^{\alpha+\varepsilon/3}\textup{ and }n^{\beta-\varepsilon/3}\leq|B_{n}|\leq n^{\beta+\varepsilon/3}\}

for some small
\varepsilon>0
. By the densabilities of
\boldsymbol{A}
and
\boldsymbol{B}
, a.a.s.
Q_{n}
is true. Note that
Q_{n}
is a union of events of type
\{|A_{n}|=k,|B_{n}|=l\}
. Denote

\begin{split}\mathbb{N}^{2}_{\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{B},n,\varepsilon}:=&\left\{(k,l)\in\mathbb{N}^{2}\,\middle|\,n^{\alpha-\varepsilon/3}\leq k\leq n^{\alpha+\varepsilon/3},n^{\beta-\varepsilon/3}\leq l\leq n^{\beta+\varepsilon/3}\right.\\
&\textup{ and }\mathbf{Pr}(|A_{n}|=k,|B_{n}|=l)\neq 0\Big\}.\end{split}

For
(k,l)\in\mathbb{N}^{2}_{\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{B},n,\varepsilon}
, we may do a change of variables
k=n^{\alpha^{\prime}}
,
l=n^{\beta^{\prime}}
so that

\alpha-\varepsilon/3\leq\alpha^{\prime}\leq\alpha+\varepsilon/3\textup{ and }\beta-\varepsilon/3\leq\beta^{\prime}\leq\beta+\varepsilon/3.

(i)

Suppose that
\alpha+\beta<1
. Let
0<\varepsilon<1-\alpha-\beta
. We shall prove that a.a.s.
A_{n}\cap B_{n}=\textup{\O}
.

By the formula of total probability and Markov’s inequality,

\begin{split}\mathbf{Pr}(A_{n}\cap B_{n}\neq\textup{\O})&\leq\mathbf{Pr}\left(|A_{n}\cap B_{n}|\geq 1\,\middle|\,Q_{n}\right)\mathbf{Pr}(Q_{n})+\mathbf{Pr}(\overline{Q_{n}})\\
&\leq\sum_{(k,l)\in\mathbb{N}^{2}_{\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{B},n,\varepsilon}}\Big[\mathbf{Pr}\left(|A_{n}\cap B_{n}|\geq 1\,\middle|\,|A_{n}|=k,|B_{n}|=l\right)\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad\mathbf{Pr}(|A_{n}|=k,|B_{n}|=l|)\Big]+\mathbf{Pr}(\overline{Q_{n}}).\\
&\leq\sum_{(k,l)\in\mathbb{N}^{2}_{\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{B},n,\varepsilon}}\Big[\mathbb{E}\left(|A_{n}\cap B_{n}|\,\middle|\,|A_{n}|=k,|B_{n}|=l\right)\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad\mathbf{Pr}(|A_{n}|=k,|B_{n}|=l|)\Big]+\mathbf{Pr}(\overline{Q_{n}}).\end{split}

For any
(k,l)\in\mathbb{N}^{2}_{\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{B},n,\varepsilon}
, by Lemma
1.17
(i)

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\left(|A_{n}\cap B_{n}|\,\middle|\,|A_{n}|=k,|B_{n}|=l\right)&=\mathbb{E}\left(|A_{n}\cap B_{n}|\,\middle|\,|A_{n}|=n^{\alpha^{\prime}},|B_{n}|=n^{\beta^{\prime}}\right)\\
&\leq n^{\alpha^{\prime}+\beta^{\prime}-1}\leq n^{\alpha+\beta+2/3\varepsilon-1}\leq n^{-1/3\varepsilon}.\end{split}

Hence

\mathbf{Pr}(A_{n}\cap B_{n}\neq\textup{\O})\leq n^{-1/3\varepsilon}\mathbf{Pr}(Q_{n})+\mathbf{Pr}(\overline{Q_{n}})\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0.

(ii)

Suppose that
\alpha+\beta>1
. Let
0<\varepsilon<\alpha+\beta-1
. We shall prove that a.a.s.

n^{\alpha+\beta-1-\varepsilon}\leq|A_{n}\cap B_{n}|\leq n^{\alpha+\beta-1+\varepsilon}.

By the formula of total probability,

\begin{split}&\quad\mathbf{Pr}(n^{\alpha+\beta-1-\varepsilon}\leq|A_{n}\cap B_{n}|\leq n^{\alpha+\beta-1+\varepsilon})\\
&\geq\mathbf{Pr}\left(n^{\alpha+\beta-1-\varepsilon}\leq|A_{n}\cap B_{n}|\leq n^{\alpha+\beta-1+\varepsilon}\,\middle|\,Q_{n}\right)\mathbf{Pr}(Q_{n})\\
&=\sum_{(k,l)\in\mathbb{N}^{2}_{\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{B},n,\varepsilon}}\Big[\mathbf{Pr}\left(n^{\alpha+\beta-1-\varepsilon}\leq|A_{n}\cap B_{n}|\leq n^{\alpha+\beta-1+\varepsilon}\,\middle|\,|A_{n}|=k,|B_{n}|=l\right)\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad\mathbf{Pr}(|A_{n}|=k,|B_{n}|=l|)\Big].\end{split}

By Lemma
2.10
and Proposition
2.4
. If
n\geq\max\left\{2^{3/\varepsilon},8^{1/(\alpha+\beta-1-\varepsilon)}\right\}
, then for any
(k,l)\in\mathbb{N}^{2}_{\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{B},n,\varepsilon}
:

\begin{split}&\quad\mathbf{Pr}\left(n^{\alpha+\beta-1-\varepsilon}\leq|A_{n}\cap B_{n}|\leq n^{\alpha+\beta-1+\varepsilon}\,\middle|\,|A_{n}|=k,|B_{n}|=l\right)\\
&=\mathbf{Pr}\left(n^{\alpha+\beta-1-\varepsilon}\leq|A_{n}\cap B_{n}|\leq n^{\alpha+\beta-1+\varepsilon}\,\middle|\,|A_{n}|=n^{\alpha^{\prime}},|B_{n}|=n^{\beta^{\prime}}\right)\\
&\geq 1-\frac{48}{n^{\alpha+\beta-1+\varepsilon}}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}1.\end{split}

Hence for
n\geq\max\left\{2^{3/\varepsilon},8^{1/(\alpha+\beta-1-\varepsilon)}\right\}
:

\begin{split}&\quad\mathbf{Pr}(n^{\alpha+\beta-1-\varepsilon}\leq|A_{n}\cap B_{n}|\leq n^{\alpha+\beta-1+\varepsilon})\\
&\geq\sum_{(k,l)\in\mathbb{N}^{2}_{\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{B},n,\varepsilon}}\left(1-\frac{48}{n^{\alpha+\beta-1+\varepsilon}}\right)\mathbf{Pr}(|A_{n}|=k,|B_{n}|=l)\\
&\geq\left(1-\frac{48}{n^{\alpha+\beta-1+\varepsilon}}\right)\mathbf{Pr}(Q_{n})\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}1.\end{split}

∎

Remark that when
\alpha+\beta=1
the density is not determined , as Proposition
1.16
showed for Bernoulli random subsets. As the class is closed under intersection, we can conclude on multiple intersections.

Corollary 2.11
.

Let
\boldsymbol{A}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{A}_{k}
be independent sequences in
\mathcal{D}(\boldsymbol{E})
of positive densities. If
\displaystyle{\sum_{i=1}^{k}\codens\boldsymbol{A}_{i}<1}
, then

2.3
Another model: random functions

We give here another natural model of random subsets : image of a random function, which can be found in
[
9
]
p.271 by Gromov. This is also a variance of random groups considered by Ollivier in
[
17
]
Lemma 59. In this subsection we prove that such a model is densable and permutation invariant.

Definition 2.12
.

Let
E,F
be finite subsets of cardinalities
n,m
. Denote
E^{F}
the set of functions from
F
to
E
. A random function
\Phi
from
F
to
E
is a
E^{F}
-valued random variable.

Let
\Phi
be a random function from
F
to
E
. Its law is determined by

\mathbf{Pr}(\Phi=\varphi)

through all
\varphi\in E^{F}
.

The random function
\Phi
can be regarded as a vector of
E
-valued random variables (or random elements of
E
)
(\Phi(y))_{y\in F}
indexed by
F
. Note that these random elements are not necessarily independent. The image
\im(\Phi)=\Phi(F):=\{\Phi(y)|y\in F\}
is then a random subset of
E
.

Example 2.13
.

(Uniform random function) Let
\Phi
be the uniform distribution on all functions from
F
to
E
. Its law is

\mathbf{Pr}(\Phi=\varphi)=\frac{1}{|E^{F}|}=\frac{1}{n^{m}}

through all
\varphi\in E^{F}.

Proposition 2.14
.

Let
\Phi
be a uniform random function from
F
to
E
. Then the random elements
(\Phi(y))_{y\in F}
are independent (identical) uniform distributions on
E
.

Proof.

Let
x\in E
,
y\in F
. The number of functions from
F
to
E
such that
\phi(y)=x
is
n^{m-1}
. So the law of
\Phi(y)
is

\mathbf{Pr}(\Phi(y)=x)=\frac{n^{m-1}}{n^{m}}=\frac{1}{n}.

Which is an uniform distribution on
E
.

Denote
F=\{y_{1},\dots,y_{m}\}
. Let
(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})
a vector of
m
elements in
E
. Let
\varphi\in E^{F}
such that
\varphi(y_{i})=x_{i}
for all
1\leq i\leq m
. Then

\mathbf{Pr}\left(\bigwedge\limits_{i=1}^{m}\Phi(y_{i})=x_{i}\right)=\mathbf{Pr}(\Phi=\varphi)=\frac{1}{n^{m}}=\prod_{i=1}^{m}\mathbf{Pr}(\Phi(y_{i})=x_{i}).

∎

Proposition 2.15
.

The image of an uniform random function is a permutation invariant random subset.

Proof.

Let
\Phi
be an uniform random function from
F
to
E
. Let
\sigma\in\mathcal{S}(E)
, then for all
\varphi\in E^{F}
:

\mathbf{Pr}(\Phi=\varphi)=\mathbf{Pr}(\Phi=\sigma\circ\varphi)=\mathbf{Pr}(\sigma^{-1}\circ\Phi=\varphi).

The random function
\sigma^{-1}\circ\Phi
has the same law of
\Phi
. Now let
a\subset E

\mathbf{Pr}(\im(\Phi)=a)=\mathbf{Pr}(\im(\sigma^{-1}\circ\Phi)=a)=\mathbf{Pr}(\im(\Phi)=\sigma(a)).

∎

3
The multi-dimensional intersection formula

Let
\boldsymbol{E}=(E_{n})
be a sequence of finite sets with
|E_{n}|=n
and
k\geq 2
be an integer. The set of pairwise different
k
-tuples of
E_{n}
is

E_{n}^{(k)}:=\{(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})\in E_{n}^{k}\;|\;x_{i}\neq x_{j}\;\forall i\neq j\}.

Denote
\boldsymbol{E}^{(k)}=(E_{n}^{(k)})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
.

Similarly, for a sequence of random subsets
\boldsymbol{A}=(A_{n})
of
\boldsymbol{E}
, we can define

A_{n}^{(k)}:=\{(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})\in A_{n}^{k}\;|\;x_{i}\neq x_{j}\;\forall i\neq j\},

which is a random subset of
E_{n}^{(k)}
. Denote also
\boldsymbol{A}^{(k)}=(A_{n}^{(k)})
. We will establish an intersection formula between a sequence of random subsets of type
\boldsymbol{A}^{(k)}
and a sequence of fixed subsets
\boldsymbol{X}=(X_{n})
of
\boldsymbol{E}^{(k)}
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
\boldsymbol{A}
be a densable sequence of random subsets of
\boldsymbol{E}
with density
d>0
. Then
\boldsymbol{A}^{(k)}
is a densable sequence of random subsets of
\boldsymbol{E}^{(k)}
with density
d
. Namely,

\dens_{\boldsymbol{E}^{(k)}}(\boldsymbol{A}^{(k)})=\dens_{\boldsymbol{E}}(\boldsymbol{A}).

Proof.

Note that
n^{k}-k^{2}(n-1)^{k}\leq|E_{n}^{(k)}|\leq n^{k}
, so
|E_{n}^{(k)}|=n^{k+o(1)}
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
. By densability a.a.s.
n^{d-\varepsilon/2}\leq|A_{n}|\leq n^{d+\varepsilon/2}
. By the same argument above a.a.s.
|A_{n}^{(k)}|=|A_{n}|^{k+o(1)}
as random variables. Hence a.a.s.

(n^{k})^{d-\varepsilon/2+o(1)}\leq|A_{n}^{(k)}|\leq(n^{k})^{d+\varepsilon/2+o(1)},

so a.a.s.

|E_{n}^{(k)}|^{d-\varepsilon}\leq|A_{n}^{(k)}|\leq|E_{n}^{(k)}|^{d+\varepsilon}.

∎

Although the densability is preserved, it is not the case for being permutation invariant. Given a permutation invariant random subset
A_{n}
of
E_{n}
, the random subset
A_{n}^{(k)}
is
not
permutation invariant in
E_{n}^{(k)}
for
k\geq 2
. See the following example.

Example 3.2
.

Let
(A_{n})
be a sequence of Bernoulli random subsets of
(E_{n})
with density
0<d<1
. Recall that subsets of the same cardinality have the same probability to be included in a permutation invariant random subset. Let
x_{1},\dots,x_{4}
be distinct elements in
E_{n}
.

\mathbf{Pr}\left(\{(x_{1},x_{2}),(x_{3},x_{4})\}\subset A_{n}^{(2)}\right)=\mathbf{Pr}\left(\{x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4}\}\subset A_{n}\right)=n^{4(d-1)},

while

\mathbf{Pr}\left(\{(x_{1},x_{2}),(x_{2},x_{3})\}\subset A_{n}^{(2)}\right)=\mathbf{Pr}\left(\{x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}\}\subset A_{n}\right)=n^{3(d-1)}.\qed

As a result the classical intersection formula (Theorem
2.9
) can not be applied in this context. Actually, for
k\geq 2
the intersection formula
does not
work for some choices of
\boldsymbol{X}
. We give here a counter example.

Example 3.3
.

Let
\boldsymbol{A}
be a sequence of random subsets in
\mathcal{D}(\boldsymbol{E})
with density
3/4
. Let
\boldsymbol{X}=(X_{n})
be a sequence of subsets defined by

X_{n}=\{x_{n}\}\times(E_{n}\backslash\{x_{n}\})\subset E_{n}^{(2)}

with some
x_{n}\in E_{n}
. By its construction
\dens_{\boldsymbol{E}^{(2)}}(\boldsymbol{X})=1/2
, so we expected that
\dens(\boldsymbol{A}^{(2)}\cap\boldsymbol{X})=3/4+1/2-1=1/4
. However, we have

\dens(\boldsymbol{A}^{(2)}\cap\boldsymbol{X})=0

because a.a.s.
A_{n}\cap\{x_{n}\}=\textup{\O}
.

For the intersection formula between
\boldsymbol{A}^{(k)}
and
\boldsymbol{X}
, we need an additional condition on
\boldsymbol{X}
. More precisely,
\boldsymbol{X}
can not have too much "self-intersection". We will discuss this condition in subsection 4.1.

Following the path for proving the intersection formula (Theorem
2.9
), we shall study the case that
\boldsymbol{A}
is a sequence of Bernoulli random subsets with density
d
(subsection 4.2). We then adapt the proof for the uniform density model by estimating the probabilities
\mathbf{Pr}\left(\{x_{1},\dots,x_{r}\}\subset A_{n}\right)
(subsection 4.3).

For the general case (subsection 4.4), according to Proposition
2.4
, we can decompose a permutation invariant random subset into uniform random subsets. We then need to bound
|A_{n}^{(k)}\cap X_{n}|
for sequences of uniform random subsets, uniformly in a small neighborhood of densities
d^{\prime}\in[d-\varepsilon,d+\varepsilon]
.

3.1
Statement of the theorem

Definition 3.4

(Self-intersection partition)
.

Let
\boldsymbol{X}=(X_{n})
be a sequence of fixed subsets of
\boldsymbol{E}^{(k)}
with density
\alpha
. For
0\leq i\leq k
, the
i
-th self-intersection of
X_{n}
is

Y_{i,n}:=\{(x,y)\in X_{n}^{2}\,|\,|x\cap y|=i\}

where
|x\cap y|
is the number of common elements of
x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})
and
y=(y_{1},\dots,y_{k})
. In particular
Y_{0,n}
is the set of pairs of
X_{n}
having no intersection.

Note that
(Y_{i,n})_{0\leq i\leq k}
is a partition of
X_{n}^{2}
, called the self-intersection partition of
X_{n}
. Namely,

X_{n}^{2}=\bigsqcup_{i=0}^{k}Y_{i,n}.

Denote
\boldsymbol{Y}_{i}=(Y_{i,n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
the
i
-th self intersection of
\boldsymbol{X}
, and
(\boldsymbol{Y}_{i})_{0\leq i\leq k}
is called the self-intersection partition of
\boldsymbol{X}
. Namely,

\boldsymbol{X}^{2}=\bigsqcup_{i=0}^{k}\boldsymbol{Y}_{i}.

Remark that the sequences
\boldsymbol{X}^{2}
and
\boldsymbol{Y}_{i}
are sequences of fixed subsets of
(\boldsymbol{E}^{(k)})^{2}=\left((E_{n}^{(k)})^{2}\right)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
. Note that
\dens_{(\boldsymbol{E}^{(k)})^{2}}(\boldsymbol{X}^{2})=\dens_{\boldsymbol{E}^{(k)}}(\boldsymbol{X})=\alpha
. To give a condition on
\boldsymbol{Y}_{i}
, we need the notion of
upper density
, defined by an upper limit:

Definition 3.5
.

Let
\boldsymbol{Y}=(Y_{n})
be a sequence of subsets of
\boldsymbol{E}=(E_{n})
. The upper density of
\boldsymbol{Y}
in
\boldsymbol{E}
is

\overline{\dens}_{\boldsymbol{E}}\boldsymbol{Y}:=\varlimsup_{n\to\infty}\log_{|E_{n}|}(|Y_{n}|).

We introduce here, for a sequence of densable fixed subsets
\boldsymbol{X}
of
\boldsymbol{E}^{(k)}
with density
\alpha
, the small self-intersection condition:

Definition 3.6
.

Let
\boldsymbol{X}
be a sequence of subsets of
\boldsymbol{E}^{(k)}
with density
\alpha
and let
(\boldsymbol{Y}_{i})_{0\leq i\leq k}
be its self-intersection partition. Let
d>1-\alpha
. We say that
\boldsymbol{X}
has
d
-small self-intersection if for every
1\leq i\leq k-1

\overline{\dens}_{(\boldsymbol{E}^{(k)})^{2}}\left(\boldsymbol{Y}_{i}\right)<\alpha-(1-d)\times\frac{i}{2k}.

(1)

Remark that the right-hand side of inequality (
3.6
) is between
0
and
\alpha
because
\alpha>1-d>0
. Note that
|Y_{k,n}|=|\{(x,y)\in X_{n}^{2}\,|\,x=y\}|=|X_{n}|
so

\dens_{(\boldsymbol{E}^{(k)})^{2}}\boldsymbol{Y}_{k}=\frac{\alpha}{2}<\alpha-(1-d)\frac{k}{2k},

which verifies (
3.6
) automatically. On the other hand, as densities of
\boldsymbol{Y}_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq k
are all smaller then
\alpha
and
|Y_{0,n}|=|X_{n}^{2}|-\sum_{i=1}^{k}|Y_{i,n}|
, by Proposition
2.8

\dens\boldsymbol{Y}_{0}=\dens\boldsymbol{X}^{2}=\alpha.

The purpose of this section is to demonstrate the following theorem.

Theorem 3.7

(Multi-dimensional intersection formula)
.

Let
\boldsymbol{A}
be a densable sequence of permutation invariant random subsets of
\boldsymbol{E}
with density
0<d<1
. Let
\boldsymbol{X}=(X_{n})
be a sequence of (fixed) subsets of
\boldsymbol{E}^{(k)}
with density
\alpha
.

(i)

If
d+\alpha<1
, then
\boldsymbol{A}^{(k)}\cap\boldsymbol{X}
is densable and

\dens(\boldsymbol{A}^{(k)}\cap\boldsymbol{X})=-\infty.

(ii)

If
d+\alpha>1
and
\boldsymbol{X}
has
d
-small self intersection (condition (
3.6
)), then
\boldsymbol{A}^{(k)}\cap\boldsymbol{X}
is densable and

\dens(\boldsymbol{A}^{(k)}\cap\boldsymbol{X})=\alpha+d-1.

Note that by taking
k=1
, we have the intersection formula between a random subset and a fixed subset. In this case we do not need to worry about the self-intersection.

Corollary 3.8

(Random-fixed intersection formula)
.

Let
\boldsymbol{A}
be a densable sequence of permutation invariant random subsets of
\boldsymbol{E}
with density
d
. Let
\boldsymbol{X}
be a sequence of (fixed) subsets of
\boldsymbol{E}
with density
\alpha
. If
d+\alpha\neq 1
, then the sequence of random subsets
\boldsymbol{A}\cap\boldsymbol{X}
is densable and

\dens(\boldsymbol{A}\cap\boldsymbol{X})=\begin{cases}d+\alpha-1&\textup{ if }d+\alpha>1\\
-\infty&\textup{ if }d+\alpha<1.\end{cases}

We shall first represent the expected value and the variance of the random variable
|A_{n}^{(k)}\cap X_{n}|
by probabilities of the type
\mathbf{Pr}\left(\{x_{1},\dots,x_{r}\}\subset A_{n}\right)
. The following result generalize Lemma
2.3
.

Lemma 3.9
.

Let
\boldsymbol{E}
,
\boldsymbol{A}
and
\boldsymbol{X}
given by Theorem
3.7
and let
(\boldsymbol{Y}_{i})_{0\leq i\leq k}
be the self-intersection partition of
\boldsymbol{X}
. Let
x_{1},\dots,x_{2k}
be distinct
2k
elements of
E_{n}
.

1.

\mathbb{E}\left(|A_{n}^{(k)}\cap X_{n}|\right)=|X_{n}|\mathbf{Pr}\left(\{x_{1},\dots,x_{k}\}\subset A_{n}\right)
.

2.

\Var\left(|A_{n}^{(k)}\cap X_{n}|\right)=

\displaystyle|X_{n}|^{2}\Big(\mathbf{Pr}(\{x_{1},\dots,x_{2k}\}\subset A_{n})-\mathbf{Pr}(\{x_{1},\dots,x_{k}\}\subset A_{n})^{2}\Big)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}|Y_{i,n}|\Big(\mathbf{Pr}(\{x_{1},\dots,x_{2k-i}\}\subset A_{n})-\mathbf{Pr}(\{x_{1},\dots,x_{2k}\}\subset A_{n})\Big).

Proof.

1.

As
A_{n}
is permutation invariant, the probability
\mathbf{Pr}(\{x_{1},\dots,x_{k}\}\subset A_{n})
does not depend on the choice of
\{x_{1},\dots,x_{k}\}
. So

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}(|A_{n}^{(k)}\cap X_{n}|)&=\mathbb{E}\left(\sum_{x\in X_{n}}\mathbbm{1}_{x\in A_{n}^{(k)}}\right)=\sum_{x\in X_{n}}\mathbf{Pr}\left(x\in A_{n}^{(k)}\right)\\
&=|X_{n}|\mathbf{Pr}\left(\{x_{1},\dots,x_{k}\}\subset A_{n}\right).\end{split}

2.

By the same reason
\mathbf{Pr}(\{x_{1},\dots,x_{r}\}\subset A_{n})
does not depend on the choice of
\{x_{1},\dots,x_{r}\}
for all
r\in\mathbb{N}
. Note that

\Var(|A_{n}^{(k)}\cap X_{n}|)=\mathbb{E}\left(|A_{n}^{(k)}\cap X_{n}|^{2}\right)-\mathbb{E}\left(|A_{n}^{(k)}\cap X_{n}|\right)^{2}.

If
(x,y)\in Y_{i,n}
, then there are
2k-i
different elements of
E_{n}
in
x
and
y
, so
\mathbf{Pr}\left(x,y\in A_{n}^{(k)}\right)=\mathbf{Pr}(\{x_{1},\dots,x_{2k-i}\}\subset A_{n})
. Hence

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(|A_{n}^{(k)}\cap X_{n}|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left(\left(\sum_{x\in X_{n}}\mathbbm{1}_{x\in A_{n}^{(k)}}\right)^{2}\right)=\sum_{x,y\in X_{n}}\mathbf{Pr}\left(x,y\in A_{n}^{(k)}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{k}\sum_{(x,y)\in Y_{i,n}}\mathbf{Pr}\left(x,y\in A_{n}^{(k)}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{k}|Y_{i,n}|\mathbf{Pr}(\{x_{1},\dots,x_{2k-i}\}\subset A_{n}).

Recall that
|Y_{0,n}|=|X_{n}^{2}|-\sum_{i=1}^{k}|Y_{i,n}|
. The above can be rewrite as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(|A_{n}^{(k)}\cap X_{n}|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(|X_{n}^{2}|-\sum_{i=1}^{k}|Y_{i,n}|\right)\mathbf{Pr}(\{x_{1},\dots,x_{2k}\}\subset A_{n})

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{i=1}^{k}|Y_{i,n}|\mathbf{Pr}(\{x_{1},\dots,x_{2k-i}\}\subset A_{n})

\displaystyle=|X_{n}^{2}|\mathbf{Pr}(\{x_{1},\dots,x_{2k}\}\subset A_{n})

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\Big(\mathbf{Pr}(\{x_{1},\dots,x_{2k-i}\}\subset A_{n})-\mathbf{Pr}(\{x_{1},\dots,x_{2k}\}\subset A_{n})\Big).

Combined with
\mathbb{E}\left(|A_{n}^{(k)}\cap X_{n}|\right)^{2}=|X_{n}|^{2}\mathbf{Pr}\left(\{x_{1},\dots,x_{k}\}\subset A_{n}\right)^{2}
, we have

\displaystyle\Var(|A_{n}^{(k)}\cap X_{n}|)

\displaystyle=|X_{n}|^{2}\Big(\mathbf{Pr}(\{x_{1},\dots,x_{2k}\}\subset A_{n})-\mathbf{Pr}(\{x_{1},\dots,x_{k}\}\subset A_{n})^{2}\Big)

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=1}^{k}|Y_{i,n}|\Big(\mathbf{Pr}(\{x_{1},\dots,x_{2k-i}\}\subset A_{n})-\mathbf{Pr}(\{x_{1},\dots,x_{2k}\}\subset A_{n}\Big).

∎

Remark that Lemma
2.3
is a special case Lemma
3.9
, by taking
k=1
and
X_{n}=E_{n}
. Note that if
k=1
, then
\boldsymbol{X}^{2}=\boldsymbol{Y}_{0}\sqcup\boldsymbol{Y}_{1}
and there is no need to introduce condition (
3.6
).

3.2
The Bernoulli density model

Let
\boldsymbol{X}
be a fixed sequence of subsets of
\boldsymbol{E}^{(k)}
with density
\alpha
. In this subsection, we study the intersection
\boldsymbol{A}^{(k)}\cap\boldsymbol{X}
in the case that
\boldsymbol{A}
is a sequence of Bernoulli random subsets of
\boldsymbol{E}
with density
0<d<1
. Note that for any integer
r\in\mathbb{N}
and any distinct elements
x_{1},\dots,x_{r}
in
E_{n}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left(\{x_{1},\dots,x_{r}\}\subset A_{n}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{Pr}\left(\{x_{1}\in A_{n}\},\dots,\{x_{r}\in A_{n}\}\right)

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{r}\mathbf{Pr}(x_{i}\in A_{n})=n^{r(d-1)}

by independence of the events
\mathbf{Pr}(x_{i}\in A_{n})
. Because of this equality, the proof of Theorem
3.7
for the Bernoulli density model is much simpler.

Proof of Theorem
3.7
for Bernoulli density model
.

(i)

Suppose that
\alpha+d<1
. To prove that
\dens(\boldsymbol{A}^{(k)}\cap\boldsymbol{X})=-\infty
, it is enough to prove that
\mathbf{Pr}\left(A_{n}^{(k)}\cap X_{n}\neq\textup{\O}\right)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0
.

By Markov’s inequality and Lemma
3.9

\begin{split}\mathbf{Pr}\left(A_{n}^{(k)}\cap X_{n}\neq\textup{\O}\right)&=\mathbf{Pr}\left(|A_{n}^{(k)}\cap X_{n}|\geq 1\right)\\
&\leq\mathbb{E}\left(|A_{n}^{(k)}\cap X_{n}|\right)=|X_{n}|\mathbf{Pr}\left(\{x_{1},\dots,x_{k}\}\subset A_{n}\right)\\
&\leq n^{k\alpha+o(1)}n^{k(d-1)}\\
&\leq n^{k(\alpha+d-1)+o(1)}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0\end{split}

as
\alpha+d-1<0
. ∎

(ii)

Suppose that
\alpha+d>1
. To simplify the notation, denote
B_{n}=A_{n}^{(k)}\cap X_{n}
and
\boldsymbol{B}=\boldsymbol{X}\cap\boldsymbol{A}^{(k)}
.

We shall prove that
\dens\boldsymbol{B}=\alpha+d-1
. Let
\varepsilon>0
be an arbitrary small real number. We need prove that a.a.s.

n^{k(\alpha+d-1-\varepsilon)}\leq|B_{n}|\leq n^{k(\alpha+d-1+\varepsilon)}.

By Lemma
3.9

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(|B_{n}|\right)

\displaystyle=|X_{n}|\mathbf{Pr}\left(\{x_{1},\dots,x_{k}\}\subset A_{n}\right)=|X_{n}|n^{k(d-1)}

\displaystyle=n^{k(\alpha+d-1)+o(1)}.

For
n
large enough

n^{k(\alpha+d-1-\varepsilon)}<\frac{1}{2}n^{k(\alpha+d-1)+o(1)}<\frac{3}{2}n^{k(\alpha+d-1)+o(1)}<n^{k(\alpha+d-1+\varepsilon)}.

So it is enough to prove that a.a.s.

\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\left(|B_{n}|\right)<|B_{n}|<\frac{3}{2}\mathbb{E}\left(|B_{n}|\right),

which means that a.a.s.

\left||B_{n}|-\mathbb{E}\left(|B_{n}|\right)\right|<\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\left(|B_{n}|\right).

By Chebyshev’s inequality

\mathbf{Pr}\left(\left||B_{n}|-\mathbb{E}\left(|B_{n}|\right)\right|\geq\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\left(|B_{n}|\right)\right)\leq\frac{4\Var\left(|B_{n}|\right)}{\mathbb{E}\left(|B_{n}|\right)^{2}}.

We shall prove that this quantity goes to zero when
n
goes to infinity. By Lemma
3.9

\displaystyle\Var(|B_{n}|)

\displaystyle=|X_{n}|^{2}\Big(\mathbf{Pr}(\{x_{1},\dots,x_{2k}\}\subset A_{n})-\mathbf{Pr}(\{x_{1},\dots,x_{k}\}\subset A_{n})^{2}\Big)

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{i=1}^{k}|Y_{i,n}|\Big(\mathbf{Pr}(\{x_{1},\dots,x_{2k-i}\}\subset A_{n})-\mathbf{Pr}(\{x_{1},\dots,x_{2k}\}\subset A_{n}\Big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{k}|Y_{i,n}|\left(n^{(2k-i)(d-1)}-n^{2k(d-1)}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}|Y_{i,n}|n^{(2k-i)(d-1)}

Note that
n^{(2k-i)(d-1)}>n^{2k(d-1)}
because
d<1
. By the
d
-small self-intersection condition (
3.6
), there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that for all
1\leq i\leq k

|Y_{i,n}|\leq n^{2k\left(\alpha+(d-1)\frac{i}{2k}\right)-\varepsilon}

for
n
large enough.

Hence for
n
large enough

\Var(|B_{n}|)\leq kn^{2k(\alpha+d-1)-\varepsilon}.

Recall that
\mathbb{E}\left(|B_{n}|\right)^{2}=n^{2k(\alpha+d-1)+o(1)}
, so

\frac{4\Var\left(|B_{n}|\right)}{\mathbb{E}\left(|B_{n}|\right)^{2}}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0.

∎

3.3
The uniform density model

Note that when
\boldsymbol{A}
is a sequence of Bernoulli random subsets with density
d
, we have

\mathbf{Pr}(\{x_{1},\dots,x_{r}\}\subset A_{n})=n^{r(d-1)},

and consequently

\mathbf{Pr}(\{x_{1},\dots,x_{k}\}\subset A_{n})^{2}-\mathbf{Pr}(\{x_{1},\dots,x_{2k}\}\subset A_{n})=0.

In order to proceed the same proof, we shall estimate these two quantities for the uniform density model.

Lemma 3.10
.

Let
\boldsymbol{A}
be a sequence of uniform random subsets of
\boldsymbol{E}
with density
d
. Let
0<\varepsilon<d
be a small real number and let
k\geq 1
be an integer. If
n\geq(1+2k)^{\frac{1}{\varepsilon}}
, then

(i)

For all integers
1\leq r\leq 2k

n^{r(d-1-\varepsilon)}\leq\mathbf{Pr}(\{x_{1},\dots,x_{r}\}\subset A_{n})\leq n^{r(d-1+\varepsilon)}.

(ii)

0\leq\mathbf{Pr}(\{x_{1},\dots,x_{k}\}\subset A_{n})^{2}-\mathbf{Pr}(\{x_{1},\dots,x_{2k}\}\subset A_{n})\leq n^{2k(d-1+\varepsilon)-d}

Proof.

Recall that
|E_{n}|=n
and that
A_{n}
is uniform on all subsets of
E_{n}
of cardinality
\lfloor n^{d}\rfloor
.

(i)

Note that
\lfloor n^{d}\rfloor\geq n^{\varepsilon}-1\geq 2k\geq r
. Among all subsets of
E_{n}
of cardinality
\lfloor n^{d}\rfloor
, there are
\binom{n-r}{\lfloor n^{d}\rfloor-r}
subsets that include
\{x_{1},\dots,x_{r}\}
. So

\mathbf{Pr}(\{x_{1},\dots,x_{r}\}\subset A_{n})=\frac{\binom{n-r}{\lfloor n^{d}\rfloor-r}}{\binom{n}{\lfloor n^{d}\rfloor}}=\frac{\lfloor n^{d}\rfloor\dots(\lfloor n^{d}\rfloor-r+1)}{n\dots(n-r-1)}.

We estimate that

\left(\frac{n^{d}-r}{n}\right)^{r}\leq\frac{\lfloor n^{d}\rfloor\dots(\lfloor n^{d}\rfloor-r+1)}{n\dots(n-r-1)}\leq\left(\frac{n^{d}}{n-r}\right)^{r}.

The condition
n\geq(1+2k)^{\frac{1}{\varepsilon}}\geq(1+r)^{\frac{1}{\varepsilon}}
implies

\begin{cases}n\geq n^{1-\varepsilon}(1+r)\\
n^{d}\geq n^{d-\varepsilon}(1+r),\end{cases}

so

\begin{cases}n^{1-\varepsilon}\leq n-r\\
n^{d-\varepsilon}\leq n^{d}-r.\end{cases}

Hence

\left(n^{d-1-\varepsilon}\right)^{r}\leq\frac{\lfloor n^{d}\rfloor\dots(\lfloor n^{d}\rfloor-r+1)}{n\dots(n-r-1)}\leq\left(n^{d-1+\varepsilon}\right)^{r}.

∎

(ii)

By the same argument

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}(\{x_{1},\dots,x_{k}\}\subset A_{n})^{2}-\mathbf{Pr}(\{x_{1},\dots,x_{2k}\}\subset A_{n})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\lfloor n^{d}\rfloor\dots(\lfloor n^{d}\rfloor-k+1)}{n\dots(n-k-1)}\right)^{2}-\frac{\lfloor n^{d}\rfloor\dots(\lfloor n^{d}\rfloor-2k+1)}{n\dots(n-2k-1)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\lfloor n^{d}\rfloor\dots(\lfloor n^{d}\rfloor-k+1)}{n\dots(n-k-1)}\right)\left(\frac{\lfloor n^{d}\rfloor\dots(\lfloor n^{d}\rfloor-k+1)}{n\dots(n-k-1)}-\frac{(\lfloor n^{d}\rfloor-k)\dots(\lfloor n^{d}\rfloor-2k+1)}{(n-k)\dots(n-2k-1)}\right).

This quantity is positive because

\frac{\lfloor n^{d}\rfloor-i}{n-i}\geq\frac{\lfloor n^{d}\rfloor-i-k}{n-i-k}

for every
0\leq i\leq k-1
.

Now we estimate that

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}(\{x_{1},\dots,x_{k}\}\subset A_{n})^{2}-\mathbf{Pr}(\{x_{1},\dots,x_{2k}\}\subset A_{n})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(\frac{n^{d}}{n-k}\right)^{k}\left(\frac{n^{dk}}{(n-k)^{k}}-\frac{(n^{d}-2k)^{k}}{(n-k)^{k}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{n^{dk}}{(n-k)^{2k}}\left(n^{dk}-\sum_{i=0}^{k}\binom{k}{i}n^{d(k-i)}(-2k)^{i}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{n^{dk}}{(n-k)^{2k}}(1+2k)^{k}n^{d(k-1)}=\left(\frac{n^{d}\sqrt{1+2k}}{n-k}\right)^{2k}n^{-d}.

As
n^{\varepsilon}\geq 1+2k
, we have

\displaystyle n-k

\displaystyle\geq n^{1-\varepsilon}(1+2k)-k

\displaystyle\geq n^{1-\varepsilon}(1+k)

\displaystyle\geq n^{1-\varepsilon}\sqrt{1+2k}.

Hence
\mathbf{Pr}(\{x_{1},\dots,x_{k}\}\subset A_{n})^{2}-\mathbf{Pr}(\{x_{1},\dots,x_{2k}\}\subset A_{n})\leq n^{2k(d-1+\varepsilon)-d}
.

∎

Notation
.

Let
\boldsymbol{X}
be a sequence of subsets of
\boldsymbol{E}^{(k)}
with density
\alpha
and let
(\boldsymbol{Y}_{i})_{0\leq i\leq k}
be its self-intersection partition. Denote the density difference

\varepsilon_{0}(d)=\min_{1\leq i\leq k}\left\{\alpha+(d-1)\frac{i}{2k}-\overline{\dens}\boldsymbol{Y}_{i}\right\}.

Remark that
\boldsymbol{X}
has
d
-small self-intersection if and only if
\varepsilon_{0}(d)>0
. In addition, for every small real number
0<\varepsilon<\frac{\varepsilon_{0}(d)}{10}
there exists
n_{\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
n\geq n_{\varepsilon}
we have, simultaneously for all
1\leq i\leq k
,

|Y_{n,i}|\leq n^{2k\left(\alpha+(d-1)\frac{i}{2k}-10\varepsilon\right)}=n^{2k\alpha+(d-1)i-2k\times 10\varepsilon}.

By densability of
\boldsymbol{X}
, we can choose
n_{\varepsilon}
such that at the same time

n^{k(\alpha-\varepsilon)}\leq|X_{n}|\leq n^{k(\alpha+\varepsilon)}.

Combined with Lemma
3.10
, we can now estimate the expected value and the variance of
|A_{n}^{(k)}\cap X_{n}|
for the uniform density model.

Lemma 3.11
.

Let
\boldsymbol{A}
be a sequence of uniform random subsets of
\boldsymbol{E}
with density
d
. Let
\boldsymbol{X}
be a sequence of subsets of
\boldsymbol{E}^{(k)}
with density
\alpha
. Let
0<\varepsilon<\min\{\frac{\varepsilon_{0}(d)}{10},d\}
be a small real number. If
n\geq\max\left\{n_{\varepsilon},(1+2k)^{\frac{1}{\varepsilon}}\right\}
, then

(i)

n^{k(\alpha+d-1-2\varepsilon)}\leq\mathbb{E}\left(|A_{n}^{(k)}\cap X_{n}|\right)\leq n^{k(\alpha+d-1+2\varepsilon)}
.

(ii)

If in addition
\alpha+d-1>2\varepsilon>0
and
\boldsymbol{X}
has
d
-small self-intersection, then

\Var\left(|A_{n}^{(k)}\cap X_{n}|\right)\leq kn^{2k(\alpha+d-1-9\varepsilon)}.

Proof.

(i)

By Lemma
3.9

\mathbb{E}\left(|A_{n}^{(k)}\cap X_{n}|\right)=|X_{n}|\mathbf{Pr}\left(\{x_{1},\dots,x_{k}\}\subset A_{n}\right).

So by Lemma
3.10
and
n^{k(\alpha-\varepsilon)}\leq|X_{n}|\leq n^{k(\alpha+\varepsilon)}
we have

n^{k(\alpha-\varepsilon)}n^{k(d-1-\varepsilon)}\leq\mathbb{E}\left(|A_{n}^{(k)}\cap X_{n}|\right)\leq n^{k(\alpha+\varepsilon)}n^{k(d-1+\varepsilon)}.

∎

(ii)

By Lemma
3.10
(ii)
\mathbf{Pr}(\{x_{1},\dots,x_{2k}\}\subset A_{n})-\mathbf{Pr}(\{x_{1},\dots,x_{k}\}\subset A_{n})^{2}\leq 0
. Apply Lemma
3.9
, eliminate negative parts.

\displaystyle\Var\left(|A_{n}^{(k)}\cap X_{n}|\right)

\displaystyle=|X_{n}|^{2}\Big(\mathbf{Pr}(\{x_{1},\dots,x_{2k}\}\subset A_{n})-\mathbf{Pr}(\{x_{1},\dots,x_{k}\}\subset A_{n})^{2}\Big)

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=1}^{k}|Y_{i,n}|\Big(\mathbf{Pr}(\{x_{1},\dots,x_{2k-i}\}\subset A_{n})-\mathbf{Pr}(\{x_{1},\dots,x_{2k}\}\subset A_{n})\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}|Y_{i,n}|\mathbf{Pr}(\{x_{1},\dots,x_{2k-i}\}\subset A_{n}).

By Lemma
3.10
(i) and
|Y_{i,n}|\leq n^{2k\alpha+i(d-1)+2k\times 10\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\Var\left(|A_{n}^{(k)}\cap X_{n}|\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}n^{2k\alpha+i(d-1)-2k\times 10\varepsilon}n^{(2k-i)(d-1+\varepsilon)}

\displaystyle\leq kn^{2k(\alpha+d-1-9\varepsilon)}.

∎

Proof of Theorem
3.7
for uniform density model
.

(i)

Suppose that
\alpha+d<1
. We shall prove that
\mathbf{Pr}\left(A_{n}^{(k)}\cap X_{n}\neq\textup{\O}\right)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
such that

\varepsilon<\min\left\{\frac{1-d-\alpha}{2},\frac{\varepsilon_{0}(d)}{10},d\right\}.

By Markov’s inequality and Lemma
3.11
. If
n\geq\max\{n_{\varepsilon},(1+2k)^{\frac{1}{\varepsilon}}\}
, then

\begin{split}\mathbf{Pr}\left(A_{n}^{(k)}\cap X_{n}\neq\textup{\O}\right)&=\mathbf{Pr}\left(|A_{n}^{(k)}\cap X_{n}|\geq 1\right)\\
&\leq\mathbb{E}\left(|A_{n}^{(k)}\cap X_{n}|\right)\\
&\leq n^{k(\alpha+d-1+2\varepsilon)}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0.\end{split}

∎

(ii)

Suppose that
\alpha+d>1
. Denote
B_{n}=A_{n}^{(k)}\cap X_{n}
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
be an arbitrary small number, with

\varepsilon<\min\left\{\frac{\alpha+d-1}{3},\frac{\varepsilon_{0}(d)}{10},d\right\}.

We shall prove that a.a.s.

n^{k(\alpha+d-1-3\varepsilon)}\leq|B_{n}|\leq n^{k(\alpha+d-1+3\varepsilon)}.

By Lemma
3.11
, if
n\geq\max\{n_{\varepsilon},(1+2k)^{\frac{1}{\varepsilon}}\}
, then

\displaystyle n^{k(\alpha+d-1-2\varepsilon)}\leq\mathbb{E}\left(|B_{n}|\right)\leq n^{k(\alpha+d-1+2\varepsilon)}.

In addition, if
n\geq 2^{\frac{1}{k\varepsilon}}
, we have

n^{k(\alpha+d-1-3\varepsilon)}\leq\frac{1}{2}n^{k(\alpha+d-1-2\varepsilon)}\leq\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\left(|B_{n}|\right)

and

\frac{3}{2}\mathbb{E}\left(|B_{n}|\right)\leq\frac{3}{2}n^{k(\alpha+d-1+2\varepsilon)}\leq n^{k(\alpha+d-1+3\varepsilon)}.

So it is enough to prove that a.a.s.

\left||B_{n}|-\mathbb{E}\left(|B_{n}|\right)\right|\leq\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\left(|B_{n}|\right).

By Chebyshev’s inequality

\mathbf{Pr}\left(\left||B_{n}|-\mathbb{E}\left(|B_{n}|\right)\right|>\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\left(|B_{n}|\right)\right)\leq\frac{4\Var\left(|B_{n}|\right)}{\mathbb{E}\left(|B_{n}|\right)^{2}}.

Combined with Lemma
3.11
, if
n\geq\max\left\{n_{\varepsilon},(1+2k)^{\frac{1}{\varepsilon}},2^{\frac{1}{k\varepsilon}}\right\}
, then

\frac{4\Var\left(|B_{n}|\right)}{\mathbb{E}\left(|B_{n}|\right)^{2}}\leq\frac{4kn^{2k(\alpha+d-1-9\varepsilon)}}{n^{2k(\alpha+d-1-2\varepsilon)}}\leq\frac{4k}{n^{14k\varepsilon}}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0.

∎

3.4
The general model (densable and permutation invariant)

Let
\boldsymbol{X}
be a sequence of subsets of
\boldsymbol{E}^{(k)}
having the
d
-small intersection condition. Recall that
\varepsilon_{0}(d)=\min_{1\leq i\leq k}\left\{\alpha+(d-1)\frac{i}{2k}-\overline{\dens}\boldsymbol{Y}_{i}\right\}>0
. Note that if
d^{\prime}<d
then
\varepsilon(d^{\prime})<\varepsilon(d)
.

In order to apply lemma
3.11
in a small interval
[d-\varepsilon,d+\varepsilon]
, we choose
0<\varepsilon<\min\left\{\frac{\varepsilon_{0}(d)}{20},\frac{d}{2}\right\}
so that
\varepsilon<\min\left\{\frac{\varepsilon_{0}(d-\varepsilon)}{10},d-\varepsilon\right\}\leq\min\left\{\frac{\varepsilon_{0}(d^{\prime})}{10},d^{\prime}\right\}
for every
d^{\prime}\in[d-\varepsilon,d+\varepsilon]
.

By the definition of
\varepsilon_{0}
and the densability of
\boldsymbol{X}
, we choose again
n_{\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
n\geq n_{\varepsilon}

|Y_{n,i}|\leq n^{2k\alpha+(d-1)i-2k\times 20\varepsilon}\leq n^{2k\alpha+(d^{\prime}-1)i-2k\times 10\varepsilon}\quad\forall 1\leq i\leq k

and

n^{k(\alpha-\varepsilon)}\leq|X_{n}|\leq n^{k(\alpha+\varepsilon)}.

Lemma 3.12
.

Let
0<\varepsilon<\min\left\{\frac{\varepsilon_{0}(d)}{20},\frac{d}{2}\right\}
be a small real number. Let
\boldsymbol{A}
be a sequence of uniform random subsets of
\boldsymbol{E}
with density
d^{\prime}\in[d-\varepsilon,d+\varepsilon]
. Let
\boldsymbol{X}
be a sequence of subsets of
\boldsymbol{E}^{(k)}
with density
\alpha
. If
n\geq\max\left\{n_{\varepsilon},(1+2k)^{\frac{1}{\varepsilon}}\right\}
, then

(i)

n^{k(\alpha+d-1-3\varepsilon)}\leq\mathbb{E}\left(|A_{n}^{(k)}\cap X_{n}|\right)\leq n^{k(\alpha+d-1+3\varepsilon)}
.

(ii)

If in addition
\alpha+d-1>3\varepsilon>0
and
\boldsymbol{X}
has
d
-small self-intersection, then

\Var(|A_{n}^{(k)}\cap X_{n}|)\leq kn^{2k(\alpha+d-1-8\varepsilon)}
.

Proof.

(i)

Recall from the above discussion that
\varepsilon<\min\left\{\frac{\varepsilon_{0}(d^{\prime})}{10},d^{\prime}\right\}
. By Lemma
3.11

n^{k(\alpha+d^{\prime}-1-2\varepsilon)}\leq\mathbb{E}\left(|A_{n}^{(k)}\cap X_{n}|\right)\leq n^{k(\alpha+d^{\prime}-1+2\varepsilon)}.

We then have the inequality by
d-\varepsilon\leq d^{\prime}\leq d+\varepsilon
.

(ii)

Because
\varepsilon_{0}(d^{\prime})>0
,
\boldsymbol{X}
has
d^{\prime}
-small self-intersection. By Lemma
3.11
and the fact that
d^{\prime}\leq d+\varepsilon
,

\displaystyle\Var(|A_{n}^{(k)}\cap X_{n}|)\leq kn^{2k(\alpha+d^{\prime}-1-9\varepsilon)}\leq kn^{2k(\alpha+d-1-8\varepsilon)}.

∎

Lemma 3.13

(Concentration lemma)
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
be an arbitrary small real number. Let
\boldsymbol{A}
and
\boldsymbol{X}
given as the previous lemma, with
\alpha+d-1>4\varepsilon>0
and
\boldsymbol{X}
having
d
-small self-intersection. If
\varepsilon<\min\{\frac{\varepsilon_{0}}{10},\frac{d}{2}\}
and
n\geq\max\left\{n_{\varepsilon},(1+2k)^{\frac{1}{\varepsilon}}\right\}
, then

\mathbf{Pr}\left(n^{k(\alpha+d-1-4\varepsilon)}\leq|A_{n}^{(k)}\cap X_{n}|\leq n^{k(\alpha+d-1+4\varepsilon)}\right)>1-kn^{-10k\varepsilon}.

Proof.

Denote
B_{n}=A_{n}^{(k)}\cap X_{n}
. By
3.12
(i)
and
n^{k\varepsilon}\geq 2
, we have

\displaystyle n^{k(\alpha+d-1-4\varepsilon)}\leq\frac{1}{2}n^{k(\alpha+d-1-3\varepsilon)}\leq\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\left(|B_{n}|\right)

and

\displaystyle\frac{3}{2}\mathbb{E}\left(|B_{n}|\right)\leq\frac{3}{2}n^{k(\alpha+d-1+3\varepsilon)}\leq n^{k(\alpha+d-1+4\varepsilon)}.

By Chebyshev’s inequality

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left(n^{k(\alpha+d-1-4\varepsilon)}\leq|B_{n}|\leq n^{k(\alpha+d-1+4\varepsilon)}\right)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left(\Big||B_{n}|-\mathbb{E}\left(|B_{n}|\right)\Big|\leq\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\left(|B_{n}|\right)\right)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle 1-\frac{4\Var(|B_{n}|)}{\mathbb{E}\left(|B_{n}|\right)^{2}}.

Again by Lemma
3.12

\displaystyle\frac{4\Var(|B_{n}|)}{\mathbb{E}\left(|B_{n}|\right)^{2}}\leq

\displaystyle\frac{kn^{2k(\alpha+d-1-8\varepsilon)}}{n^{2k(\alpha+d-1-3\varepsilon)}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle kn^{-10k\varepsilon}.

∎

Proof of the Theorem
3.7
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
be an arbitrary small number, with
\varepsilon<\min\left\{\frac{d}{2},\frac{\varepsilon_{0}(d)}{20}\right\}
as given in Lemma
3.12
. Denote
Q_{n}=\{n^{d-\varepsilon}\leq|A_{n}|\leq n^{d+\varepsilon}\}
and

\mathbb{N}_{\boldsymbol{A},\varepsilon,n}:=\left\{\ell\in\mathbb{N}\;|\;n^{d-\varepsilon}\leq\ell\leq n^{d+\varepsilon}\textup{ and }\mathbf{Pr}(|A_{n}|=\ell)>0\right\}.

By densability of
\boldsymbol{A}
we have
\mathbf{Pr}(Q_{n})\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}1
. Denote by
\mathbf{Pr}_{Q_{n}}:=\mathbf{Pr}\left(\cdot\;\middle|\;Q_{n}\right)
the probability measure under the condition
Q_{n}
. Define similarly
\mathbb{E}_{Q_{n}}
and
\Var_{Q_{n}}
.

In order to prove that some sequence of properties
(R_{n})
is a.a.s. true, by the inequality

\mathbf{Pr}(\overline{R_{n}})\leq\mathbf{Pr}(Q_{n})\mathbf{Pr}_{Q_{n}}({\overline{R_{n}}})+\mathbf{Pr}(\overline{Q_{n}}),

it is enough to prove that
\mathbf{Pr}_{Q_{n}}({\overline{R_{n}}})\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0
.

(i)

Suppose that
\alpha+d<1
. Assume in addition that
\varepsilon<\frac{1-d-\alpha}{3}
.

We shall prove that

\mathbf{Pr}_{Q_{n}}(A_{n}^{(k)}\cap X_{n}\neq\textup{\O})=\mathbf{Pr}_{Q_{n}}(|A_{n}^{(k)}\cap X_{n}|\geq 1)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0.

By the formula of total probability and Markov’s inequality:

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}_{Q_{n}}\left(|A_{n}^{(k)}\cap X_{n}|\geq 1\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{l\in\mathbb{N}_{A,\varepsilon,n}}\mathbf{Pr}_{Q_{n}}(A_{n}=l)\mathbf{Pr}\left(|A_{n}^{(k)}\cap X_{n}|\geq 1\;\middle|\;|A_{n}|=l\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{l\in\mathbb{N}_{A,\varepsilon,n}}\mathbf{Pr}_{Q_{n}}(A_{n}=l)\mathbb{E}\left(|A_{n}^{(k)}\cap X_{n}|\;\middle|\;|A_{n}|=l\right).

By a change of variable
l=n^{d^{\prime}}
with
d-\varepsilon\leq d^{\prime}\leq d+\varepsilon
, apply Lemma
3.12

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}_{Q_{n}}\left(|A_{n}^{(k)}\cap X_{n}|\geq 1\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{l\in\mathbb{N}_{A,\varepsilon,n}}\mathbf{Pr}_{Q_{n}}(A_{n}=l=n^{d^{\prime}})n^{\alpha+d-1+3\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\leq n^{\alpha+d-1+3\varepsilon}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0.

∎

(ii)

Suppose that
\alpha+d>1
. Assume in addition that
\varepsilon<\frac{\alpha+d-1}{4}
, so that we can apply Lemma
3.13
.

We shall prove that

\mathbf{Pr}_{Q_{n}}\left(n^{k(\alpha+d-1-4\varepsilon)}\leq|A_{n}^{(k)}\cap X_{n}|\leq n^{k(\alpha+d-1+4\varepsilon)}\right)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}1.

By the formula of total probability, Lemma
3.13
and a change of variables
l=n^{d^{\prime}}
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}_{Q_{n}}\left(n^{k(\alpha+d-1-4\varepsilon)}\leq|A_{n}^{(k)}\cap X_{n}|\leq n^{k(\alpha+d-1+4\varepsilon)}\right)

\displaystyle=\,

\displaystyle\sum_{l\in\mathbb{N}_{A,\varepsilon,n}}\mathbf{Pr}_{Q_{n}}(A_{n}=l)\mathbf{Pr}\left(n^{k(\alpha+d-1-4\varepsilon)}\leq|A_{n}^{(k)}\cap X_{n}|\leq n^{k(\alpha+d-1+4\varepsilon)}\;\middle|\;|A_{n}|=l\right)

\displaystyle\geq\,

\displaystyle\sum_{l\in\mathbb{N}_{A,\varepsilon,n}}\mathbf{Pr}_{Q_{n}}(A_{n}=l=n^{d^{\prime}})\left(1-kn^{-10k\varepsilon}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\,

\displaystyle 1-kn^{-10k\varepsilon}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}1.

∎

4
Applications to group theory

Fix an alphabet
X=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{m}\}
as generators of groups. Let
B_{\ell}
be the set of cyclically reduced words of length at most
\ell
on
X^{\pm}
. Recall that
|B_{\ell}|=(2m-1)^{\ell+O(1)}
.

We are interested in asymptotic behaviors, when
\ell
goes to infinity, of group presentations
\langle X|R_{\ell}\rangle
where
R_{\ell}
is a random subset of
B_{\ell}
.

Definition 4.1

(Random groups with density)
.

Let
d\in]0,1]
. Let
\boldsymbol{R}=(R_{\ell})
be a densable sequence of permutation invariant random subsets with density
d
of the sequence
\boldsymbol{B}=(B_{\ell})
.

Denote
G_{\ell}=G_{\ell}(m,d)
the random presentation defined by
\langle X|R_{\ell}\rangle
. The sequence
\boldsymbol{G}=\boldsymbol{G}(m,d)=(G_{\ell}(m,d))_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}}
is called a sequence of random groups with density
d
.

For example, if
d=1
, then
G_{\ell}(m,1)
is isomorphic to the trivial group.

A sequence of events
\boldsymbol{Q}=(Q_{\ell})
described by
\boldsymbol{G}(m,d)
is
asymptotically almost surely
satisfied if
\mathbf{Pr}(Q_{\ell})\xrightarrow[m\to\infty]{}1
. We denote briefly a.a.s.
Q_{\ell}
.

4.1
Phase transition at density
1/2

Theorem 4.2

(Gromov, phase transition at density 1/2)
.

Let
(G_{\ell}(m,d))
be a sequence of random groups with density
d
.

(i)

If
d>1/2
, then a.a.s
G_{\ell}(m,d)
is isomorphic to the trivial group.

(ii)

If
d<1/2
, then a.a.s
G_{\ell}(m,d)
is a hyperbolic group.

In
[
16
]
2.1 (or
[
17
]
I.2.b), Ollivier proved the first assertion by probabilistic pigeon-hole principle. We give a proof here by the intersection formulae (Theorem
2.9
and Corollary
3.8
).

Proof of Theorem
4.2

(i)
.

Let
x\in X
. Let
A_{\ell}
be the set of cyclically reduced words that does not start or end by
x
, of lengths at most
\ell-1
(so that
xA_{\ell}\subset B_{\ell}
). It is easy to check that the sequences
(A_{\ell})
and
(xA_{\ell})
are sequences of fixed subsets of
\boldsymbol{B}=(B_{\ell})
of density
1
. By the random-fixed intersection formula (Corollary
3.8
), the sequences
(x(R_{\ell}\cap A_{\ell}))
and
(R_{\ell}\cap xA_{\ell})
are sequences of permutation invariant random subsets of
(xA_{\ell})
of density
d
.

By the intersection formula (Theorem
2.9
), their intersection
(xR_{\ell}\cap R_{\ell}\cap xA_{\ell})
is a sequence of permutation invariant random subsets of
(xA_{\ell})
of density
(2d-1)>0
, which is a.a.s. not empty. Thus, a.a.s. there exists a word
w\in A_{\ell}
such that
w\in R_{\ell}
and
xw\in R_{\ell}
, so a.a.s.
x=1
in
G_{\ell}
by canceling
w
.

The argument above works for any generator
x\in X
. By intersecting a finite number of a.a.s. satisfied events, a.a.s. all generators
x\in X
are trivial in
G_{\ell}
. Hence a.a.s.
G_{\ell}
is isomorphic to the trivial group.
∎

The proof of Theorem
4.2
(ii) needs
van Kampen diagrams
and will not be treated here. See
[
9
]
9.B. for the original idea by Gromov, and
[
16
]
2.2 or
[
17
]
Section V for a detailed proof by Ollivier.

4.2
Phase transition at density
\lambda/2

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
\boldsymbol{G}(m,d)=(G_{\ell}(m,d))
be a sequence of random groups with density
d
. Let
\lambda\in]0,1[
.

1.

If
d<\lambda/2
, then a.a.s.
G_{\ell}(m,d)
satisfies
C^{\prime}(\lambda)
.

2.

If
d>\lambda/2
, then a.a.s.
G_{\ell}(m,d)

does not
satisfy
C^{\prime}(\lambda)
.

Proof.

1.

Recall that (
[
12
]
p.240) a
piece
with respect to a set of relators is a cyclic sub-word that appears at least twice. There are two cases to verify.

(a)

Let
A_{\ell}
be the set of cyclically reduced words of length at most
\ell
having a piece appearing twice on itself (figure 1) that is longer than
\lambda
times itself. We shall prove that a.a.s. the intersection
A_{\ell}\cap R_{\ell}
is empty.
r
figure 1

We estimate first the number of relators of length
t\leq\ell
with a piece of length
s\geq\lambda t
. There are
2t
ways (including orientations) to choose the first position of the piece, and
2t-s
ways the choose the second position (note that because
r
is reduced, it can not overlay the first one if they are with opposite orientations). For each way of positioning we can determine freely
t-s
letters, each with
(2m-1)
choices, except for the first letter and the last letter having respectively
2m
and
2m-2
or
2m-1
choices. So this number is
2t(2t-s)C(m)(2m-1)^{t-s}
where
C(m)
is a real number that depends only on

. Hence

|A_{\ell}|=\sum_{t=1}^{\ell}\sum_{s=\lfloor\lambda t\rfloor}^{t}2t(2t-s)C(m)(2m-1)^{t-s}=(2m-1)^{(1-\lambda)\ell+o(\ell)},

which means that
(A_{\ell})
is a sequence of fixed subsets of
(B_{\ell})
with density
1-\lambda
. By the intersection formula (Corollary
3.8
), because
1-\lambda+d<1
, we have a.a.s.

A_{\ell}\cap R_{\ell}=\textup{\O}.

(b)

Let
X_{\ell}
be the set of distinct pairs of relators
r_{1},r_{2}
in

having a piece (figure 2) longer than
\lambda\min\{|r_{1}|,|r_{2}|\}
. It is a fixed subset of
B_{\ell}^{(2)}
. We shall prove that a.a.s the intersection

is empty.
r_{1}
r_{2}
figure 2

There are
4\ell^{2}

choices for

which means that
(X_{\ell})
is a sequence of fixed subsets of

with density

. By the multi-dimension intersection formula (Theorem

(i)), because
1-\frac{\lambda}{2}+d<1
, we have a.a.s.

X_{\ell}\cap R_{\ell}^{(2)}=\textup{\O}.

2.

Take the sequence of sets
\boldsymbol{X}=(X_{\ell})
constructed in 1(b). We shall prove that a.a.s. the intersection

is
not
empty. We have already

\dens\boldsymbol{X}+\dens\boldsymbol{R}^{(2)}>1.

To apply Theorem

(ii), we need to calculate the size of the self-intersection

Take

4.3
Every
(m-1)
-generated subgroup is free

Fix the set of
m
generators
X=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{m}\}
. Recall that
B_{\ell}
is the set of
(2m-1)^{\ell+o(\ell)}
cyclically reduced words on
X^{\pm}=\{x_{1}^{\pm},\dots,x_{m}^{\pm}\}
of length at most
\ell
. The few relator model of random groups is constructed as follows: fix a number
k\in\mathbb{N}
and let

G_{\ell}=\langle x_{1},\dots,x_{m}|r_{1},\dots,r_{k}\rangle

where
R_{\ell}=\{r_{1},\dots,r_{k}\}

given by the uniform probability on all subsets of

with cardinality

.

The sequence
(G_{\ell})_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}}
is called a
sequence of random groups with
k
relators
. As
k
is independent of
\ell
, the sequence
(G_{\ell})
is a sequence of random groups with density
d=0
. By Proposition
4.3
, a.a.s.

satisfies

for arbitrary small

.

In
[

]
, Arzhantseva and Ol’shanskii proved the following result:

(Arzhantseva-Ol’shanskii,
[

]

Let

be a sequence of random groups with

relators. Then a.a.s. every

Combining the intersection formula and their arguments, we prove:
