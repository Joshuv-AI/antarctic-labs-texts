Explicit and exact solutions concerning the Antarctic Circumpolar Current with variable density in spherical coordinates Thanks: ∗ Corresponding author
Calin Iulian Martin ∗, Ronald Quirchmayr

Abstract
. We use spherical coordinates to devise a new exact solution to the governing equations of geophysical fluid dynamics for an inviscid and incompressible fluid with a general density distribution and subjected to forcing terms. The latter are of paramount importance for the modeling of realistic flows-that is, flows that are observed in some averaged sense in the ocean. Owing to the employment of spherical coordinates we do not need to resort to approximations (e.g. of f f - and β \beta -plane type) that simplify the geometry in the governing equations. Our explicit solution represents a steady purely-azimuthal stratified flow with a free surface, that—thanks to the inclusion of forcing terms and the consideration of the Earth’s geometry via spherical coordinates—makes it suitable for describing the Antarctic Circumpolar Current and enables an in-depth analysis of the structure of this flow. In line with the latter aspect, we employ functional analytical techniques to prove that the free surface distortion is defined in a unique and implicit way by means of the pressure applied at the free surface. We conclude our discussion by setting out relations between the monotonicity of the surface pressure and the monotonicity of the surface distortion that concur with the physical expectations. Keywords : Azimuthal flows, Antarctic Circumpolar Current, spherical coordinates, Coriolis force, exact explicit and implicit solutions Mathematics Subject Classification : Primary: 35Q31, 35Q35. Secondary: 35Q86.

2.
Physical problem and governing equations

In this section we provide the governing equations for geophysical flows written in spherical coordinates to accommodate the shape of the Earth, together with the boundary conditions for the free surface and a rigid bed.

We will work in a system of right handed coordinates
(r,\theta,\varphi)
where
r
denotes the distance to the centre of the sphere,
\theta\in[0,\pi]
is the polar angle
(the convention being that
\pi/2-\theta
is the angle of latitude)
\varphi\in[0,2\pi]
is the azimuthal angle (the angle of longitude). While in this coordinate system
the North and South poles are located at
\theta=0,\pi
, respectively, the Equator sits on
\theta=\pi/2
, the Antarctic Circumpolar Current is situated at
\theta=3\pi/4
.
The unit vectors in this system are

\displaystyle{\bf e}_{r}

\displaystyle=(\sin\theta\cos\varphi,\sin\theta\sin\varphi,\cos\theta),

\displaystyle{\bf e}_{\theta}

\displaystyle=(\cos\theta\cos\varphi,\cos\theta\sin\varphi,-\sin\theta),

\displaystyle{\bf e}_{\varphi}

\displaystyle=(-\sin\varphi,\cos\varphi,0),

with
\bf{e}_{\varphi}
pointing from West to East and
\bf{e}_{\theta}
from North to South, cf. Figure
2
.

Figure 2.
The spherical coordinate system:
\theta
is the polar angle,
\varphi
is the azimuthal angle (the angle of longitude) and
r
represents the distance to the origin.

Throughout this paper we make the following simplifying assumption on the location of the Antarctic Circumpolar Current. We assume that the angle of latitude
\theta
satisfies

\frac{3\pi}{4}-\frac{\pi}{18}\leq\theta\leq\frac{3\pi}{4}+\frac{\pi}{18}.

(2.1)

We are guided in our study by the observations made in Maslowe
[
44
]
asserting that the Reynolds number is, in general, extremly large for this oceanic flows.
Accordingly we will consider incompressible and inviscid flows. However, our analysis incorporates a general density distribution
\rho=\rho(r,\theta)
.

Denoting with

{\bf u}=u{\bf e}_{r}+v{\bf e}_{\theta}+w{\bf e}_{\varphi}

the velocity field we have that, cf.
[
13
]
, the governing equations in the
(r,\theta,\varphi)

coordinate system are the Euler’s equations,

\begin{array}[]{rcl}u_{t}+uu_{r}+\frac{v}{r}u_{\theta}+\frac{w}{r\sin\theta}u_{\varphi}-\frac{1}{r}(v^{2}+w^{2})&=&-\frac{1}{\rho}p_{r}+F_{r}\\
&&\\
v_{t}+uv_{r}+\frac{v}{r}v_{\theta}+\frac{w}{r\sin\theta}v_{\varphi}+\frac{1}{r}(uv-w^{2}\cos\theta)&=&-\frac{1}{\rho}\frac{1}{r}p_{\theta}+F_{\theta}\\
&&\\
w_{t}+uw_{r}+\frac{v}{r}w_{\theta}+\frac{w}{r\sin\theta}w_{\varphi}+\frac{1}{r}(uw+vw\cot\theta)&=&-\frac{1}{\rho}\frac{1}{r\sin\theta}p_{\varphi}+F_{\varphi},\end{array}

(2.2)

(where
p(r,\theta,\varphi)
is denotes the pressure in the fluid and
(F_{r},F_{\theta},F_{\varphi})
is the body-force vector) and the equation of mass conservation

\frac{1}{r^{2}}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}(\rho r^{2}u)+\frac{1}{r\sin\theta}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}(\rho v\sin\theta)+\frac{1}{r\sin\theta}\frac{\partial(\rho w)}{\partial\varphi}=0.

(2.3)

To capture effects of Earth’s rotation we associate
({\bf e}_{r},{\bf e}_{\theta},{\bf e}_{\varphi})
with a fixed point on the sphere which is rotating about its polar axis.
Consequently, we consider on the left of (
2.2
) the Coriolis term

2{\bf\Omega}\times{\bf u},

where

{\bf\Omega}=\varOmega({\bf e}_{r}\cos\theta-{\bf e}_{\theta}\sin\theta),

(with
\varOmega\approx 7.29\times 10^{-5}
rad
s^{-1}
being the constant rotation speed of Earth) and the centripetal acceleration

{\bf\Omega}\times({\bf\Omega}\times{\bf r}),

where
{\bf r}=r{\bf e}_{r}
. The latter two quantities add up to

\displaystyle 2

\displaystyle\varOmega\big[-w(\sin\theta){\bf e}_{r}-w(\cos\theta){\bf e}_{\theta}+(u\sin\theta+v\cos\theta){\bf e}_{\varphi}\big]

\displaystyle-r\varOmega^{2}\big[(\sin^{2}\theta){\bf e}_{r}+(\sin\theta\cos\theta){\bf e}_{\theta}\big]

with respect to the
({\bf e}_{r},{\bf e}_{\theta},{\bf e}_{\varphi})
basis.

Concerning the body-force vector it holds that (cf.
[
14
]
) the gravitational constant acts in
r
-direction, a general body-force
G(r,\theta)
acts in
\theta
-direction, no force is acting in
\varphi
-direction. That is, the body-force vector is

(-g,G(r,\theta),0).

The equations of motion are filled up by the boundary conditions as follows.
On the free surface
r=R+h(\theta,\varphi)
we have the dynamic boundary condition

p=P(\theta,\varphi),

(2.4)

and the kinematic boundary condition

u=\frac{v}{r}\frac{\partial h}{\partial\theta}+\frac{w}{r\sin\theta}\frac{\partial h}{\partial\varphi}.

(2.5)

We ask that the bottom of the ocean, described by
r=d(\theta,\varphi)
, is an impenetrable boundary by demanding

u=\frac{v}{r}\frac{\partial d}{\partial\theta}+\frac{w}{r\sin\theta}\frac{\partial d}{\partial\varphi}

(2.6)

to hold there.

The solutions that we are seeking for, represent a steady flow that propagates only in the azimuthal direction with no variations in this direction. Thus, the velocity field is characterized by

u=v=0
and
w=w(r,\theta)
. Moreover,
p=p(r,\theta)
,
h=h(\theta)
,
d=d(\theta)
.
Consequently, while the two kinematic boundary conditions (
2.5
) and (
2.6
) and the equation of mass conservation (
2.3
)
are trivially satisfied, the Euler equations governing such special
flows read

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl}-\frac{w^{2}}{r}-2\varOmega w\sin\theta-r\varOmega^{2}\sin^{2}\theta&=&-\frac{1}{\rho}p_{r}-g,\\
&&\\
-\frac{w^{2}}{r}\cot\theta-2\varOmega w\cos\theta-r\varOmega^{2}\sin\theta\cos\theta&=&-\frac{1}{\rho r}p_{\theta}+G(r,\theta),\\
&&\\
0&=&-\frac{1}{\rho}\frac{1}{r\sin\theta}p_{\varphi}.\end{array}\right.

(2.7)

3.
Exact explicit and implicit solutions

3.1.
Explicit solutions for azimuthal velocity and pressure

The first outcomes of this section concern the velocity
w
and the pressure
p
. These two quantities can be determined by means of explicit formulas. To derive the latter we
use the third equation in (
2.7
) to infer that
p
is independent of
\varphi
, i.e.
p=p(r,\theta)
.
System (
2.7
) can now be simplified as

\displaystyle\rho\frac{(w+\Omega r\sin\theta)^{2}}{r}

\displaystyle=p_{r}+g\rho

(3.1)

\displaystyle\rho r\cot\theta\frac{(w+\Omega r\sin\theta)^{2}}{r}

\displaystyle=p_{\theta}-\rho rG(r,\theta).

(3.2)

Let us denote

Z=Z(r,\theta)\coloneqq\frac{(w+\Omega r\sin\theta)^{2}}{r}

(3.3)

to rewrite the above system as

\displaystyle\rho Z

\displaystyle=p_{r}+g\rho

(3.4)

\displaystyle(\rho r\cot\theta)Z

\displaystyle=p_{\theta}-\rho rG(r,\theta).

(3.5)

The elimination of the pressure
p
from the system (
3.4
)–(
3.5

\left(\rho Z\right)_{\theta}-\left(\rho r\cot\theta Z\right)_{r}=g\rho_{\theta}+\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\big(\rho rG(r,\theta)\big)

-r\cos\theta\left(\rho rZ\right)_{r}+\sin\theta\left(\rho rZ\right)_{\theta}=(r\sin\theta)\left[g\rho_{\theta}(r,\theta)+\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\big(\rho rG(r,\theta)\big)\right].

(3.6)

We further set

U\coloneqq\rho rZ

(3.7)

and appeal to the method of characteristics to solve (
3.6
); i.e., we set out to solve

-(r\cos\theta)U_{r}+(\sin\theta)U_{\theta}=(r\sin\theta)\left[g\rho_{\theta}(r,\theta)+\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\big(\rho rG(r,\theta)\big)\right].

(3.8)

Along these lines we seek curves

s\rightarrow(r(s),\theta(s))
satisfying

\displaystyle\dot{r}(s)=-r(s)\cos\theta(s),\qquad\dot{\theta}(s)=\sin\theta(s).

(3.9)

Note that any

that satisfies the previous system also obeys the equation

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}\Big(r(s)\sin\theta(s)\Big)=0\quad{\rm for}\,\,{\rm all}\quad s\in\mathbb{R}

(3.10)

The choice (
3.9

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}\Big(&U\big(r(s),\theta(s)\big)\Big)=\\

The integration of (

\displaystyle\tilde{r}(s)=c_{1}\mathrm{e}^{s}+c_{2}\mathrm{e}^{-s},\quad c_{1},c_{2}\in\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle\tilde{\theta}(s)=\arccos\left(\frac{1-c\mathrm{e}^{2s}}{1+c\mathrm{e}^{2s}}\right),\quad c\geq 0,

(3.13)

where
c,c_{1},c_{2}
are constants of integration. To find
U
as a function of

and
\theta

for given
(r,\theta)
, we search for an

such that

\tilde{\theta}(s_{0})=\theta,\quad\tilde{r}(s_{0})=r.

(3.14)

We choose now the constant

\tilde{\theta}(0)=\frac{\pi}{2}
. This gives

, that is

\tilde{\theta}(s)=\arccos\left(\frac{1-\mathrm{e}^{2s}}{1+\mathrm{e}^{2s}}\right),

(3.15)

From the property (
3.10
) we see that

\tilde{r}(0)\sin\big(\tilde{\theta}(0)\big)=\tilde{r}(s_{0})\sin\big(\tilde{\theta}(s_{0})\big)=r\sin\theta,

\tilde{r}(0)=r\sin\theta

It is easy to see that

s_{0}=\frac{1}{2}\ln\frac{1-\cos\theta}{1+\cos\theta}\eqqcolon f(\theta)

is the unique
element satisfying the first equation in (
3.14
). To determine
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
we solve

\displaystyle\tilde{r}(f(\theta))=r

(3.16)

\displaystyle\tilde{r}(0)=r\sin\theta

(3.17)

that is equivalent to

\displaystyle c_{1}+c_{2}+(c_{2}-c_{1})\cos\theta=r\sin\theta

\displaystyle c_{1}+c_{2}=r\sin\theta,

whose unique solution (for

Let us denote with

(\overline{r}(s),\overline{\theta}(s))
the special characteristic solution to (

That is,

\overline{r}(s)=\frac{r\sin\theta}{2}(\mathrm{e}^{s}+\mathrm{e}^{-s}),\quad\overline{\theta}(s)=\arccos\left(\frac{1-\mathrm{e}^{2s}}{1+\mathrm{e}^{2s}}\right).

Inserting

s

we obtain

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} w(r,\theta)=&-\Omega r\sin\theta\\

\displaystyle=-g\rho(r,\theta)+\frac{1}{r}\bigg(F(r\sin\theta)+r\sin\theta\int^{f(\theta)}_{0}[g\rho_{\theta}(\overline{r},\overline{\theta})+H_{r}(\overline{r},\overline{\theta})]\,\mathrm{d}s\bigg),

3.2.
Implicit exact solution for the free surface

By an application of the implicit function theorem we will show the existence of a unique function
h=h(\theta)
representing disturbances of the flat water surface
caused by deviations of the pressure distribution from the pressure required to maintain a free surface following the curvature of the Earth.

According to the dynamic boundary condition at the surface (
2.4
)
and our assumption of a purely azimuthal flow that may only vary in
\theta
, the pressure
p
given by (
3.27
) and evaluated at the free surface
r=R+h(\theta)
,
denoted by
P(\theta)
, satisfies

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} P(\theta)=b&-g\int^{R+h(\theta)}_{a}\rho(r,\theta)\,\mathrm{d}r+\int^{[R+h(\theta)]\sin\theta}_{a\sin\theta}\bigg[\frac{F(y)}{y}+\mathcal{F}(y,\theta)\bigg]\,\mathrm{d}y\\
&+\int^{\theta}_{3\pi/4}\big[F(a\sin\tilde{\theta})\cot\tilde{\theta}+\mathcal{F}(a\sin\tilde{\theta},\tilde{\theta})\,a\cos\tilde{\theta}+H(a,\tilde{\theta})\big]\,\mathrm{d}\tilde{\theta}.\end{aligned}

(3.28)

Formula (
3.28
) relates the pressure
P(\theta)
acting onto the ocean surface to deformations of the free surface described by
h(\theta)
. To ensure comparability of the involved variables, we bring equation (
3.28
) in dimensionless form. For this purpose we set
h\equiv 0
in (
3.28
). This corresponds to the undisturbed ocean surface following the Earth’s curvature. The pressure
P_{0}
that corresponds to the flat surface shape is given by

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} P_{0}(\theta)=b&-g\int^{R}_{a}\rho(r,\theta)\,\mathrm{d}r+\int^{R\sin\theta}_{a\sin\theta}\bigg[\frac{F(y)}{y}+\mathcal{F}(y,\theta)\bigg]\,\mathrm{d}y\\
&+\int^{\theta}_{3\pi/4}\big[F(a\sin\tilde{\theta})\cot\tilde{\theta}+\mathcal{F}(a\sin\tilde{\theta},\tilde{\theta})\,a\cos\tilde{\theta}+H(a,\tilde{\theta})\big]\,\mathrm{d}\tilde{\theta}.\end{aligned}

(3.29)

Let us denote by
P_{a}
the atmospheric pressure at the ocean surface, which we assume to be constant along the entire range of the ACC. This is a reasonable simplification which, in combination with (
3.29
), implies that the constant
P_{a}
can be written as

P_{a}\equiv b-g\int^{R}_{a}\rho(r,3\pi/4)\,\mathrm{d}r+\int^{\frac{R}{\sqrt{2}}}_{\frac{a}{\sqrt{2}}}\bigg[\frac{F(y)}{y}+\mathcal{F}(y,3\pi/4)\bigg]\,\mathrm{d}y.

(3.30)

Dividing (
3.28
) by
P_{a}
yields that the dimensionless pressure
\mathcal{P}\coloneqq P/P_{a}
satisfies

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} 0=&-\mathcal{P}(\theta)+\frac{b}{P_{a}}-\frac{g}{P_{a}}\int_{a}^{[1+\mathcal{h}(\theta)]R}\rho(r,\theta)\,\mathrm{d}r+\frac{1}{P_{a}}\int_{a\sin\theta}^{[1+\mathcal{h}(\theta)]R\sin\theta}\left[\frac{F(y)}{y}+\mathcal{F}(y,\theta)\right]\,\mathrm{d}y\\
&+\frac{1}{P_{a}}\int_{3\pi/4}^{\theta}[F(a\sin\tilde{\theta})\cot\tilde{\theta}+\mathcal{F}(a\sin\tilde{\theta},\tilde{\theta})a\cos\tilde{\theta}+H(a,\tilde{\theta})]\,\mathrm{d}\tilde{\theta}\eqqcolon\mathfrak{F}(\mathcal{h},\mathcal{P}),\end{aligned}

(3.31)

where the dimensionless
\mathcal{h}(\theta)\coloneqq\frac{h(\theta)}{R}
measures the deviation from the flat ocean surface at
r=R
.

According to our assumption (
2.1
) we restrict the range of
\theta
to the compact interval

I\coloneqq\bigg[\frac{3\pi}{4}-\frac{\pi}{18},\frac{3\pi}{4}+\frac{\pi}{18}\bigg]

and denote by
\mathcal{C}(I)
the space of continuous functions
f\colon I\to{\mathbb{R}}
, equipped with the supremum norm

\|f\|=\sup_{\theta\in I}|f(\theta)|.

(3.32)

Furthermore we denote by
B
the open ball of radius
10^{-5}
centered at the origin in
\mathcal{C}(I)
; we note that any realistic disturbance
\mathcal{h}
is contained in
B
.
We write now equation (
3.31
) in the operatorial form

\mathfrak{F}\colon B\times\mathcal{C}(I)\to\mathcal{C}(I),

(3.33)

where
\mathfrak{F}(\mathcal{h},\mathcal{P})
is the right-hand side of (
3.31
).
We aim to implicitly resolve the dimensionless surface disturbance
\mathcal{h}
as a function of the dimensionless surface pressure
\mathcal{P}
by means of the operator equation

\mathfrak{F}(\mathcal{h},\mathcal{P})=0.

(3.34)

We note first that (
3.34
) is clearly satisfied for
\mathcal{h}\equiv 0
and the corresponding dimensionless pressure
\mathcal{P}_{0}
, which is obtained by dividing both sides in (
3.29
) by
P_{a}
:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathcal{P}_{0}(\theta)=\frac{b}{P_{a}}&-\frac{g}{P_{a}}\int^{R}_{a}\rho(\tilde{r},\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\tilde{r}+\frac{1}{P_{a}}\int^{R\sin\theta}_{a\sin\theta}\bigg[\frac{F(y)}{y}+\mathcal{F}(y,\theta)\bigg]\,\mathrm{d}y\\
&+\frac{1}{P_{a}}\int^{\theta}_{3\pi/4}\big[F(a\sin\tilde{\theta})\cot\tilde{\theta}+\mathcal{F}(a\sin\tilde{\theta},\tilde{\theta})\,a\cos\tilde{\theta}+H(a,\tilde{\theta})\big]\,\mathrm{d}\tilde{\theta}.\end{aligned}

(3.35)

That is, we have that

\mathfrak{F}(0,\mathcal{P}_{0})=0.

(3.36)

Having obtained a trivial solution of (
3.34
) we aim now to apply the implicit function theorem in order to find solution flows with non-flat free surface. To this
end we need to compute the
derivative of
\mathfrak{F}
with respect to the first variable
\mathcal{h}
at the point
(0,\mathcal{P}_{0})\in B\times\mathcal{C}(I)
,

D_{\mathcal{h}}\mathfrak{F}(0,\mathcal{P}_{0})\mathcal{h}=\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{\mathfrak{F}(t\mathcal{h},\mathcal{P}_{0})-\mathfrak{F}(0,\mathcal{P}_{0})}{t},

(3.37)

A plain calculation yields that

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} D_{\mathcal{h}}\mathfrak{F}(0,\mathcal{P}_{0})\,\mathcal{h}(\theta)&=-\frac{gR}{P_{a}}\rho(R,\theta)\,\mathcal{h}(\theta)+\frac{1}{P_{a}}\Big[F(R\sin\theta)+R\sin\theta\,\mathcal{F}(R\sin\theta,\theta)\Big]\,\mathcal{h}(\theta)\\
&=\frac{\rho(R,\theta)}{P_{a}}\Big[-gR+\big(w(R,\theta)+\Omega R\sin\theta\big)^{2}\Big]\,\mathcal{h}(\theta),\end{aligned}

where we employed (
3.20
) to obtain the last equality. Realistic assumptions on the maximal size of
w
yield that

gR\gg(w+\Omega R\sin\theta)^{2}
for all
\theta\in I
, cf. Fig.
1
. This allows us to infer that

D_{\mathcal{h}}\mathfrak{F}(0,\mathcal{P}_{0})\colon\mathcal{C}(I)\to\mathcal{C}(I)

(3.38)

defines a linear topological homeomorphism. By the implicit function theorem in Banach spaces (cf.
[
2
]
) there exists for every sufficiently
small perturbation
\mathcal{P}
of
\mathcal{P}_{0}
a unique function
\mathcal{h}\in\mathcal{C}(I)
such that (
3.31
)
holds true; this
\mathcal{h}
is nontrivial.

4.
Monotonicity relations

While in the previous section we have exploited the non-dimensional relation (
3.31
) to infer that the free surface in the region of the ACC
can locally—around the undisturbed state
(\mathcal{h}\equiv 0,\mathcal{P}_{0})
—be described as a function of the imposed surface pressure, we establish in
this section relations between the monotonicity of the dimensionless surface pressure
\mathcal{P}
and surface elevation
\mathcal{h}
,
see Proposition
4.1
below, which are in concordance with physical expectations.
To this end we remark that a bootstrapping procedure
[
2
]
enables us to transfer smoothness properties of
\mathcal{P}
to
\mathcal{h}
.
Hence assuming a continuously differentiable pressure
\mathcal{P}
, which we differentiate now with respect to
\theta
in (
3.31
) yields

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}^{\prime}(\theta)=

\displaystyle-\frac{g}{P_{a}}\int_{a}^{[1+\mathcal{h}]R}\rho_{\theta}(r,\theta)\,\mathrm{d}r-\frac{g}{P_{a}}\rho\big((1+\mathcal{h})R,\theta\big)\,\mathcal{h}^{\prime}(\theta)R

\displaystyle+\frac{\cot\theta}{P_{a}}F\big((1+\mathcal{h})R\sin\theta\big)+\frac{\mathcal{h}^{\prime}(\theta)}{P_{a}}\,\frac{F\big((1+\mathcal{h})R\sin\theta\big)}{1+\mathcal{h}}-\frac{\cot\theta}{P_{a}}F(a\sin\theta)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{P_{a}}\,\mathcal{F}\big((1+\mathcal{h})R\sin\theta,\theta\big)\Big((1+\mathcal{h})R\cos\theta+\mathcal{h}^{\prime}(\theta)\,R\sin\theta\Big)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{P_{a}}\,\mathcal{F}(a\sin\theta,\theta)\,a\cos\theta+\frac{1}{P_{a}}\int_{a}^{[1+\mathcal{h}]R}[g\rho_{\theta}(r,\theta)+H_{r}(r,\theta)]\,\mathrm{d}r

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{P_{a}}\,\Big(F(a\sin\theta)\cot\theta+\mathcal{F}(a\sin\theta,\theta)\,a\cos\theta+H(a,\theta)\Big),

thus

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}^{\prime}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathcal{h}^{\prime}(\theta)}{P_{a}}\Bigg[-gR\rho\big((1+\mathcal{h})R,\theta\big)+R\,\sin\theta\,\mathcal{F}\big((1+\mathcal{h})R\sin\theta,\theta\big)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\;\,+\frac{F\big((1+\mathcal{h})R\sin\theta\big)}{1+\mathcal{h}}\Bigg]+\frac{1}{P_{a}}\,H\big((1+\mathcal{h})R,\theta\big)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\cot\theta}{P_{a}}\left[F\big((1+\mathcal{h})R\sin\theta\big)+(1+\mathcal{h})R\,\sin\theta\,\mathcal{F}\big((1+\mathcal{h})R\sin\theta,\theta\big)\right];

for convenience we wrote simply
\mathcal{h}
to mean the evaluation
\mathcal{h}(\theta)
in the above calculation.
Making use of the formula for the azimuthal velocity (
3.20
) we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{P_{a}}{\rho\big((1+\mathcal{h})R,\theta\big)}\,\mathcal{P}^{\prime}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\left[-gR+\frac{\Big(w\big((1+\mathcal{h})R,\theta\big)+\Omega(1+\mathcal{h})R\sin\theta\Big)^{2}}{1+\mathcal{h}}\right]\mathcal{h}^{\prime}(\theta)

(4.1)

\displaystyle+\Big(w\big((1+\mathcal{h})R,\theta\big)+\Omega(1+\mathcal{h})R\sin\theta\Big)^{2}\cot\theta

\displaystyle+(1+\mathcal{h})R\,G\big((1+\mathcal{h})R,\theta\big).

It is apparent from the above formula that, in order
to discuss monotonicity properties of
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{h}
, we need additional information on the size of the forcing term
G
. Along these lines, we note that,
according to the discussion in Constantin and Johnson
[
14
]
, the nonlinear advection terms in (
2.7
) are small perturbations of the linear flow
w_{0}
which is governed by
the system

\begin{array}[]{rcl}-2\Omega w_{0}\sin\theta&=&-\frac{1}{\rho}\tilde{p}_{r}\\
&&\\
-2\Omega w_{0}\cos\theta&=&-\frac{1}{\rho}\cdot\frac{1}{r}\tilde{p}_{\theta}+G(r,\theta),\end{array}

(4.2)

with
\tilde{p}=p+\rho gr-\frac{1}{2}\rho r^{2}\Omega^{2}\sin^{2}\theta
being the modified pressure absorbing the centripetal and gravitational acceleration.
Using that
\sin\theta\approx\cos\theta
in the region of the ACC (recall that we restrict
\theta
to the interval
I=\big[\frac{3\pi}{4}-\frac{\pi}{18},\frac{3\pi}{4}+\frac{\pi}{18}\big]
, cf. (
2.1
)), and that the meridional pressure gradient is relatively small compared to the radial gradient, we have from
(
4.2
) that, at the leading order balance,

G(r,\theta)\approx-2\Omega w_{0}\cos\theta.

(4.3)

We are now able to formulate the following monotonicity result.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
\theta\in I
and assume that the forcing term
G
satisfies (
4.3
).
Then the following assertions hold true.

(i)

If
\mathcal{h}^{\prime}(\theta)\geq 0
then
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}(\theta)<0
.

(ii)

If
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}(\theta)\geq 0
then
\mathcal{h}^{\prime}(\theta)<0
.

Proof.

Let us first consider the sum

\Big(w((1+\mathcal{h})R,\theta)+\Omega(1+\mathcal{h})R\sin\theta\Big)^{2}\cot\theta+(1+\mathcal{h})R\,G\big((1+\mathcal{h})R,\theta\big)

and expand it as

\displaystyle\Big(w((1+\mathcal{h})R,\theta)^{2}+2\Omega R(1+\mathcal{h})w((1+\mathcal{h})R,\theta)\sin\theta\Big)\cot\theta

(4.4)

\displaystyle+\Omega R(1+\mathcal{h})\left[\Omega R(1+\mathcal{h})\sin\theta-2w_{0}((1+\mathcal{h})R,\theta)\right]\cos\theta.

Given that the maximal speed of the ACC does not exceed
3
m s
-1
(cf.
[
14
]
), clearly

\Omega R(1+\mathcal{h})\sin\theta
is by far greater than
2w_{0}
. Therefore, the expression in (
4.4
) is clearly negative for all

\theta\in[\frac{3\pi}{4}-\frac{\pi}{18},\frac{3\pi}{4}+\frac{\pi}{18}]
.
Also, appealing to the same arguments pertaining to the sizes of
w_{0},\Omega
and
R
we can conclude that

-gR+\frac{\Big(w((1+\mathcal{h})R,\theta)+\Omega(1+\mathcal{h})R\sin\theta\Big)^{2}}{1+\mathcal{h}}<0

within
I
. Thus, assuming

\mathcal{h}^{\prime}(\theta)\geq 0
, we see from (
4.1
) that
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}(\theta)<0
, which shows (
i
).
The proof of (
ii
) follows by the same arguments.
∎
