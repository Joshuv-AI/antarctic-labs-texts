Balanced Binary Tree Schemes for Computing Zernike Radial Polynomials
Hong-Yan Zhang, Yu Zhou, Zhi-Qiang Feng

Abstract
Zernike radial polynomials (ZRP) play a significant role in application areas such as optics design, imaging systems, and image processing systems. Currently, there are two kinds of numerical schemes for computing the ZRP automatically with computer programs: one is based on the definition in which the factorial operations may lead to the overflow problem and the high order derivatives are troublesome, and the other is based on recursion which is either unstable or with high computational complexity. In this paper, our emphasis is focused on exploring the balanced binary tree (BBT) schemes for computing the ZRP: firstly an elegant formulae for computation is established; secondly the recursive and iterative algorithms based-on BBT are proposed; thirdly the computational complexity of the algorithms are analyzed rigorously; finally the performance of BBT schemes by testing the running time is verified and validated. Theoretical analysis shows that the computational complexity of balanced binary tree recursive algorithm (BBRTA) and iterative algorithm are exponential and quadratic respectively, which coincides with the running time test very well. Experiments show that the time consumption is about 1 ∼ 10 1\sim 10 microseconds with different computation platforms for the balanced binary tree iterative algorithm (BBTIA), which is stable and efficient for real-time applications. In the sense of STEM education, the connection of the BBT and ZRP exhibits the beauty and applications of discrete mathematical structure behind the engineering problem, which is worthy of introducing to the college students, computer programmers and optics engineers. Keywords: Optics design, Zernike radial polynomials (ZRP), High-precision computation, Balanced binary tree (BBT), Recursion and Iteration, Computational complexity, Real-time application

\operatorname{R}_{n}^{m}(\rho)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\rho,&n=1;\\

There are two significant points for the stopping condition:

•

it has significant impacts on the time complexity and space complexity of the computation;

•

the choice of stopping condition is not unique, which leads to different performance for the numeric schemes adopted for the objective of computation.

For computing the
\operatorname{R}_{n}^{m}(\rho)
, Chong et. al. proposed a simple stopping condition for recursive process in 2003

\operatorname{R}_{n}^{m}(\rho)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\rho^{m},&n=m;\\

\operatorname{R}_{n}^{m}(\rho)=\rho\left[\operatorname{R}_{n-1}^{\left|m-1\right|}(\rho)+\operatorname{R}_{n-1}^{m+1}(\rho)\right]-\operatorname{R}_{n-2}^{m}(\rho),\quad n\geq 2

\displaystyle=\left(h_{2}+\frac{h_{3}}{\rho^{2}}\right)\operatorname{R}_{n}^{m-2}(\rho)+h_{1}\operatorname{R}_{n}^{m}(\rho),\quad m\geq 4

In computer science, we know that the essence of recursion lies in two facts: there must be a stopping condition for the recursive procedure/function which calls itself; the recursion depth should not be large otherwise the computational complexity will be too large due to the massive memory consumption and long time consumption caused by the push-pop stacking processes.
For the available recursive schemes of computing

•

Both the recursive and iterative algorithms for computing the ZRP are designed, which refreshes the state-of-the-art of the computational complexity.

3.3.1
Space Complexity of BBTRA

The space complexity of computing

is determined by the memories needed. For

, we just need one variable to record the value of
\operatorname{R}_{n}^{m}(\rho)
once the radius

\spacomp_{\textsc{CalcRadiPolyBBTRA}}{\left(n,m\right)}=2^{k}-1=\mathscr{O}\left({2^{\frac{n-m}{2}}}\right).

In consequence, the space complexity
\mathscr{O}\left({2^{n-m}}\right)
is exponential, which increases very fast with the difference

. In the sense of space complexity or memory consumption, the recursive scheme is not recommended for large value of
n-m
. Particularly, for
n=m
and
n=m+2
, the space complexity is
\mathscr{O}\left({1}\right)
.

3.3.2
Time Complexity of BBTRA

The crucial result in our development of time complexity will be the following fundamental equation

\timcomp_{\texttt{op}}{\left(\operatorname{R}_{n-1}^{m+1}\right)}=\timcomp_{\texttt{op}}{\left(\operatorname{R}_{n-2}^{m}\right)},\quad\texttt{op}\in\left\{*,+\right\}

(27)

for the non-leaf nodes of the BBT, which simplifies the analysis of computational complexity in a surprising way. Let

f_{\texttt{op}}(n,m)=\timcomp_{\texttt{op}}{\left(\operatorname{R}_{n}^{m}\right)},

(28)

then we have

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}f_{+}(n-1,m+1)=f_{+}(n-2,m),&n-m\geq 4;\\
f_{*}(n-1,m+1)=f_{*}(n-2,m),&n-m\geq 4.\end{array}\right.

(29)

According to (
20
) and (
21
), for
m\geq 1
we can obtain

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}f_{+}(n,m)=3,&m\neq 0,n-m=2;\\
f_{+}(n,m)=0,&m\neq 0,n-m=0.\end{array}\right.

(30)

and

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}f_{*}(n,m)=3+\left\lceil{\log_{2}m}\right\rceil,&m\neq 0,n-m=2;\\
f_{*}(n,m)=\left\lceil{\log_{2}m}\right\rceil,&m\neq 0,n-m=0.\end{array}\right.

(31)

For
m=0
, simple algebraic calculations show that

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}f_{+}(n,m)=3,&m=0,n-m=2;\\
f_{+}(n,m)=0,&m=0,n-m=0.\end{array}\right.

(32)

and

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}f_{*}(n,m)=3,&m=0,n-m=2;\\
f_{*}(n,m)=0,&m=0,n-m=0.\end{array}\right.

(33)

For the addition operations, equation (
25
) implies that

\begin{split}\timcomp_{+}{\left(\operatorname{R}_{n}^{m}\right)}&=\timcomp_{+}{\left(\rho F_{1}\operatorname{R}_{n-1}^{m+1}\right)}+\timcomp_{+}{\left(F_{2}\operatorname{R}_{n-2}^{m}\right)}+1\\
&=\timcomp_{+}{\left(\operatorname{R}_{n-1}^{m+1}\right)}+\timcomp_{+}{\left(\rho\frac{2n}{n-m}\right)}+\timcomp_{+}{\left(1-F_{1}\right)}+\timcomp_{+}{\left(\operatorname{R}_{n-2}^{m}\right)}+1\\
&=2\timcomp_{+}{\left(\operatorname{R}_{n-1}^{m+1}\right)}+3\end{split}

(34)

Thus

f_{+}(n,m)=2f_{+}(n-1,m+1)+3,\quad n-m\geq 4

(35)

Let

G^{+}_{p}=f_{+}(n,m)=f_{+}(n-m),\quad p=n-m\in\left\{0,2,4,\cdots\right\}

(36)

then

G^{+}_{p-2}=f_{+}(n-1,m+1)=f_{+}(n-2,m),

(37)

hence we have the discrete difference equation

G^{+}_{p}=2G^{+}_{p-2}+3,\quad G^{+}_{2}=3,G^{+}_{0}=0.

(38)

According to (
16
) and (
17
), the solution to (
38
) must be

G^{+}_{p}=2^{\frac{p}{2}-1}G^{+}_{2}+3(2^{\frac{p}{2}-1}-1)=3(2^{\frac{p}{2}}-1),\quad p=0,2,4,\cdots

(39)

Hence we have time flops for the addition operation

f_{+}(n,m)=\timcomp_{+}{\left(\operatorname{R}_{n}^{m}\right)}=3(2^{\frac{n-m}{2}}-1),\quad n-m=0,2,4,\cdots

(40)

Similarly, for the multiplication operations, we have

\begin{split}\timcomp_{*}{\left(\operatorname{R}_{n}^{m}\right)}=\timcomp_{*}{\left(\operatorname{R}_{n-1}^{m+1}\right)}+\timcomp_{*}{\left(\operatorname{R}_{n-2}^{m}\right)}+\timcomp_{*}{\left(\rho F_{1}\right)}+\timcomp_{*}{\left(F_{2}\right)}+2=2\timcomp_{*}{\left(\operatorname{R}_{n-1}^{m+1}\right)}+5.\end{split}

(41)

In this way, we immediately obtain the following discrete difference equation

G^{*}_{p}=2G^{*}_{p-2}+5,\quad p=2,4,6,\cdots

(42)

and the initial value

G^{*}_{2}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\left\lceil{\log_{2}m}\right\rceil,&m\geq 1;\\
3,&m=0.\end{array}\right.

(43)

With the help of (
16
), (
17
), (
42
) and (
43
), we can deduce that

\begin{split}G^{*}_{p}=2^{\frac{p}{2}-1}G^{*}_{2}+5\left(2^{\frac{p}{2}-1}-1\right)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}8\cdot 2^{\frac{n-m}{2}-1}-5,&m=0;\\
\left(\left\lceil{\log_{2}m}\right\rceil+8\right)2^{\frac{n-m}{2}-1}-5,&m\geq 1.\end{array}\right.\end{split}

(44)

Therefore,

\begin{split}f_{*}(n,m)=\timcomp_{*}{\left(\operatorname{R}_{n}^{m}\right)}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\left(\left\lceil{\log_{2}m}\right\rceil+8\right)2^{\frac{n-m}{2}-1}-5,&m\neq 0,n-m\geq 2;\\
\left\lceil{\log_{2}m}\right\rceil,&m\neq 0,n-m=0;\\
1,&m=0,n-m=0\\
8\cdot 2^{\frac{n-m}{2}-1}-5,&m=0,n-m\geq 2\\
\end{array}\right.\end{split}

(45)

The total time flops for computing
\operatorname{R}_{n}^{m}(\rho)
is

\begin{split}\Tf(\operatorname{R}_{n}^{m})=\timcomp_{*}{\left(\operatorname{R}_{n}^{m}\right)}+\timcomp_{+}{\left(\operatorname{R}_{n}^{m}\right)}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\left[7+\frac{\left\lceil{\log_{2}m}\right\rceil}{2}\right]2^{\frac{n-m}{2}}-5,&m\neq 0,n-m\geq 2\\
\left\lceil{\log_{2}m}\right\rceil,&m\neq 0,n-m=0\\
7\cdot 2^{\frac{n-m}{2}}-5,&m=0,n-m\geq 2\\
1,&m=0,n-m=0\end{array}\right.\end{split}

(46)

which can be expressed with the big-O notation as follows

\displaystyle\Tf(\operatorname{R}_{n}^{m})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathscr{O}\left({\log_{2}\left|m\right|2^{\frac{n-\left|m\right|}{2}}}\right),&m\neq 0,n-\left|m\right|\geq 4;\\
\mathscr{O}\left({\log_{2}\left|m\right|}\right),&m\neq 0,n-\left|m\right|=0,2;\\

\end{array}\right.

(47)

Clearly, in the sense of time complexity or time consumption, the recursive scheme is not recommended for the large value of
n-\left|m\right|
.

4
Iterative Scheme for Computing the Zernike Radial Polynomials

For the recursive
Algorithm

3.2
, its space complexity is exponential and the time complexity varies with the configuration of double indices
n
and
m
according to (
47
). For large value of
n-\left|m\right|
, it is necessary for us to reduce the computational complexity with iterative scheme instead of recursive scheme for computing
\operatorname{R}_{n}^{m}(\rho)
.

4.1
Computing the Radial Polynomials Iteratively

The key issue of iterative computation lies in two points: firstly, we set the initial condition in an iterative scheme with the stopping condition in the corresponding recursive scheme; then we iterate by updating the state of nodes of interest via the primitive recursive formulae in a reverse direction. For the recursive problem with a single integer parameter, say
n
, this can be done easily since there are just two directions for the variation of
n
for the 1-dim problems. However, for the recursive problem with double integer parameters, say
n
and
m
, the iteration may be trouble because there is no simple direction for the updating process. Fortunately, for computing the ZRP, it is possible to iterate simply with the structure of BBT. Actually, what we should do is just accessing the nodes in BBT from the leaf nodes to the root node.

FIGURE
2
shows the iterative process intuitively. For
m\geq 0
and
n-m\geq 2
, we start with the initial condition specified by
(
11
) in the leaves in the
k
-th level, then compute the value of the upper nodes in the
(k-1)
-th level according to (
23
), finally update the state of the nodes. The formula in (
25
) can be used for both recursion and iteration, which depends on the concrete
usages. Moreover, it is obvious that there are some repetitive nodes in FIGURE
2
, which implies that we can save their calculation to accelerate the computation and reduce memories needed.

FIGURE
3
demonstrates the simplification of the BBT with a 1-dim array to store the nodes of interest. By removing the redundant nodes, there are just
k
independent leaves in the
k
-th level of the BBT. We can use a sequence
\texttt{v}=\langle v_{0},v_{1},\cdots,v_{k-1}\rangle
to store the initial values with the expression

\begin{split}v_{i}=\operatorname{R}_{m+k+1-i}^{m+k-1-i}(\rho)=\rho^{m+k-i-1}[(m+k-i+1)\rho^{2}-(m+k-i)]\end{split}

(48)

for

With the help of (

), we can deduce that the iterative formulae for updating must be

v_{i}^{(t+1)}=\rho F_{1}v_{i}^{(t)}+F_{2}v_{i+1}^{(t)},\quad i,t\in\left\{0,1,\cdots,k-1\right\}

(49)

where
t
denotes the
t

t=k-\ell.

(50)

In other words, the variable
t
for iteration can be replaced by the index
\ell
for the levels of the BBT. When
t=k-1
, the iterative process stops and we have

\operatorname{R}_{n}^{m}(\rho)=v_{0}^{(k-1)}.

(51)

When the iteration ends, we obtain the byproduct stored in the sequence
v

\begin{split}\langle v_{0}^{(k-1)},v_{1}^{(k-2)},\cdots,v_{k-1}^{(0)}\rangle=\langle\operatorname{R}_{n}^{m}(\rho),\operatorname{R}_{n-2}^{m}(\rho),\cdots,\operatorname{R}_{m+2}^{m}(\rho)\rangle,\end{split}

(52)

in which only
v_{0}^{(k-1)}
is the computation result of
\operatorname{R}_{n}^{m}(\rho)
and the others are discarded.

The numerical computation of ZRP is a challenging problem due to the stability and the computational complexity. Our formulae
(

) for computing

•

Appendix A
Notations for Integers and Computational Complexity

A.1
Integers and Lower Integer for Real Number

For an integer
n\in\mathbb{Z}=\left\{0,\pm 1,\pm 2,\cdots\right\}
, it is even if and only if
2\mid n
, i.e.,
2
divides
n
or equivalently
n\equiv 0\penalty\ (\MOD 2)
; otherwise, it is odd if and only if
2\nmid n
or equivalently
n\equiv 1\penalty\ (\MOD 2)
.
The set of non-negative integers are denoted by

\mathbb{Z}^{+}=\left\{0,1,2,\cdots\right\}
.
For any real number
x\in\mathbb{R}
, the maximal lower bound
n\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
n\leq x
is called the floor of
x
and it is denoted by

\left\lfloor{x}\right\rfloor=n=\arg\max_{m}\left\{m\in\mathbb{Z}:x\geq m\right\}.

(59)

Similarly, the minimal upper bound
n\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
x\leq n
is called the ceiling of
x
and it is denoted by

\left\lceil{x}\right\rceil=n=\arg\min_{m}\left\{m\in\mathbb{Z}:x\leq m\right\}.

(60)

A.2
Time and Space Complexities

Let

\timcomp_{*}{\left(\texttt{expr}\right)}
and

\timcomp_{+}{\left(\texttt{expr}\right)}

be the counting or times of multiplication and addition in some operation expression
expr
. The
time complexity vector of computation
(TCVC) for
expr
is defined by

\timcomp(\texttt{expr})=[\timcomp_{*}{\left(\texttt{expr}\right)},\timcomp_{+}{\left(\texttt{expr}\right)}].

(61)

Note that we just list two components of
\timcomp(\texttt{expr})
here since the subtraction and division can be treated as addition and multiplication respectively for real numbers.
The
time flops
(TF)
flops

[
16
]
, for computing
expr
is

\Tf(\texttt{expr})=\timcomp_{*}{\left(\texttt{expr}\right)}+\timcomp_{+}{\left(\texttt{expr}\right)}

(62)

in which
\timcomp_{*}{\left(\texttt{expr}\right)}
and
\timcomp_{+}{\left(\texttt{expr}\right)}
are the time flops of multiplication and addition respectively.

Similarly, we use
\Tc(\texttt{expr})
to denote the computation time for
expr
. We also use
\Tc_{*}(\texttt{expr})
and
\Tc_{+}(\texttt{expr})
to represent the computation time for the multiplications and additions involved in
expr
.
Given the time units
\tau_{*}
and
\tau_{+}
for multiplication and addition respectively, then

\bm{\tau}=[\tau_{*},\tau_{+}]

(63)

will be the vector of time units. The time for computing
expr
can be represented by

\begin{split}\Tc(\texttt{expr})=&\langle\timcomp(\texttt{expr})|\bm{\tau}\rangle=\timcomp_{*}{\left(\texttt{expr}\right)}\tau_{*}+\timcomp_{+}{\left(\texttt{expr}\right)}\tau_{+}=\Tc_{*}(\texttt{expr})+\Tc_{+}(\texttt{expr})\end{split}

(64)

if only multiplication and addition are essential for the total time consumed.
For the purpose of analyzing time complexity theoretically instead of estimating practical running time of programs, we can regard the units
\tau_{*}
and
\tau_{+}
as
1
, thus
the time consumption is equal to the flops involved.

Let
\spacomp{\left(\texttt{expr}\right)}
denote the space complexity of computing
expr
,
which means the memories required. For allocating memories for a sequence
\texttt{v}=\langle v_{0},v_{1},\cdots,v_{\ell-1}\rangle
with positive length
\ell\geq 1
, the space complexity will be
\spacomp{\left(\texttt{v}\right)}=\ell
. If the memory consumption for the single element of
\texttt{v}_{i}
is
\delta=\texttt{sizeof}(v_{i})
, then the total memories for the sequence of
v
will be
\ell\delta
. Just like the analysis of time complexity, the memory unit
\delta
can be regarded as
1
. Consequently, the key problem of estimating space complexity is to estimate the
counting of memories instead of concrete memory units.

For the algorithm named with
Alg
, its TCVC is

\begin{split}\timcomp{\left(\textsc{Alg}\right)}=\sum_{\texttt{expr}\in\textsc{Alg}}\timcomp{\left(\texttt{expr}\right)}=[\timcomp_{*}{\left(\textsc{Alg}\right)},\timcomp_{+}{\left(\textsc{Alg}\right)}]\end{split}

(65)

and the time for computation is

\begin{split}\Tc(\textsc{Alg})=\langle\timcomp{\left(\textsc{Alg}\right)}|\bm{\tau}\rangle=\timcomp_{*}{\left(\textsc{Alg}\right)}\tau_{*}+\timcomp_{+}{\left(\textsc{Alg}\right)}\tau_{+}\end{split}

(66)

Similarly, the space complexity of the algorithm
Alg
is

\spacomp{\left(\textsc{Alg}\right)}=\sum_{\texttt{expr}\in\textsc{Alg}}\spacomp{\left(\texttt{expr}\right)}

(67)

If there are some parameters
n,m,\cdots
for
Alg
, then we will take one of the following notations

\timcomp_{\textsc{Alg}}{\left(n,m,\cdots\right)},\Tc_{\textsc{Alg}}(n,m,\cdots),\Tf_{\textsc{Alg}}(n,m,\cdots),\spacomp_{\textsc{Alg}}{\left(n,m,\cdots\right)}

to represent the computational complexity according to practical requirements and interests.

In algorithm analysis, we take the big-O notation
[
17
,
18
]

\mathscr{O}\left({g(n)}\right)=\left\{h(n):\exists c>0,\exists n_{0}>0,\forall n\geq n_{0},\quad 0\leq h(n)\leq c\cdot g(n)\right\}

(68)

to represent time or space complexity of interest with an upper bound
g(n)
where both
c\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
and
n_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
are constant. In this paper we will use the following fact

\mathscr{O}\left({g(n)\log_{2}n}\right)=\mathscr{O}\left({g(n)\left\lceil{\log_{2}n}\right\rceil}\right),\quad\forall g(n)

in the analysis of time complexity.
