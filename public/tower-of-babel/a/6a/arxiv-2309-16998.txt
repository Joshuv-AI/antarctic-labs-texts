Natural dualities for varieties generated by finite positive MV-chains
Wolfgang Poiger

Abstract
. We provide a simple natural duality for the varieties generated by the negation- and implication- free reduct of a finite MV-chain. We study these varieties through the dual equivalence thus obtained. For example, we fully characterize their algebraically closed, existentially closed and injective members. We also explore the relationship between this natural duality and Priestley duality in terms of distributive skeletons and Priestley powers.



1991 Mathematics Subject Classification
08C20, 06D35, 06D50

1.
Introduction

Just like distributive lattices are the negation-free subreducts of Boolean algebras,
positive
\mathsf{MV}
-algebras
(recently introduced in
[
1
]
), are the negation-free subreducts of
\mathsf{MV}
-algebras. While the variety
\mathsf{MV}
of
\mathsf{MV}
-algebras provides algebraic semantics for Łukasiewicz infinite-valued logic (see,
e.g.
,
[
7
, Chapter 4]
), its subvarieties
\mathsf{MV}_{n}=\mathbb{H}\mathbb{S}\mathbb{P}(\text{\bf\L}_{n})
, generated by finite
\mathsf{MV}
-chains
\text{\bf\L}_{n}
, provide algebraic semantics for Łukasiewicz finitely-valued logics (the subvarieties
\mathsf{MV}_{n}
were first studied in
[
12
]
). In this paper, we study the varieties
\mathsf{PMV}_{n}=\mathbb{H}\mathbb{S}\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n})
generated by finite
positive

\mathsf{MV}
-chains
\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n}
(that is, negation-free reducts of
\text{\bf\L}_{n}
). Our main tool for this study is the theory of
natural dualities
.

In its simplest form, natural duality theory
[
8
]
provides a general framework to obtain a dual equivalence between a quasi-variety
\mathcal{A}=\mathbb{I}\mathbb{S}\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{M})
generated by a finite algebra
\mathbf{M}
and a category of structured Stone spaces
\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{I}\mathbb{S}_{c}\mathbb{P}^{+}(\utilde{\mathbf{M}})
generated by a discrete structure
\utilde{\mathbf{M}}
based on the same set as
\mathbf{M}
(and therefore called an
alter ego
of
\mathbf{M}
). Examples of natural dualities are
Stone duality
, which arises if
\mathbf{M}
is the two-element Boolean algebra and its alter ego
\utilde{\mathbf{M}}
is the two-element discrete space, and
Priestley duality
, which arises if
\mathbf{M}
is the two-element distributive lattice and its alter-ego
\utilde{\mathbf{M}}
is the two-element discrete space with order
0\leq 1
. The great utility of these dualities may be seen as a consequence of the fact that the alter-egos described above are very simple structures. A general theme of natural duality theory may be phrased as
simple structures yield useful dualities
.

For the varieties
\mathsf{MV}_{n}
, natural dualities were developed and studied in
[
20
]
. Since finite
\mathsf{MV}
-chains are
semi-primal
, it is easy to come up with simple alter-egos
\utilde{\text{\bf\L}}_{n}
. Indeed, by
[
8
, Theorem 3.3.14]
the only structure relevant for this duality is given by the collection of subalgebras
\mathbb{S}(\text{\bf\L}_{n})
. One reason why this is sufficient is that every subalgebra of
\text{\bf\L}_{n}\times\text{\bf\L}_{n}
is a direct product of subalgebras. This is
not
true anymore in the case of
\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n}
(for example, the order relation
\leq
itself is a subalgebra of
\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n}\times\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n}
). We show that, instead, a simple alter-ego of
\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n}
can be obtained from a certain collection of subalgebras of the order
\leq
which can be easily computed algorithmically.

To make the case for the utility of these alter-egos, we investigate the dualities they yield to derive various results about the varieties
\mathsf{PMV}_{n}
. For example, we completely characterize the injective, algebraically closed and existentially closed members of
\mathsf{PMV}_{n}
. We also explore the relationship to Priestley duality, which can be expressed in terms of
distributive skeletons
and
Priestley powers
. We show that these constructions give rise to an adjunction between
\mathsf{DL}
(the variety of distributive lattices) and
\mathsf{PMV}_{n}
, similar to the adjunction
[
17
, Section 4]
between
\mathsf{BA}
(the variety of Boolean algebras) and
\mathsf{MV}_{n}
obtained from functors taking the Boolean skeleton and the Boolean power, respectively. We expect this to prove useful in future applications, exploring modal logic over
\mathsf{PMV}_{n}
as an analogue of Dunn’s
positive modal logic

[
10
]
in the setting of modal finitely-valued Łukasiewicz logic
[
13
]
.

The paper is structured as follows. In Section
2
, we recall the most important background information on
\mathsf{MV}
- and
\mathsf{MV}_{n}
-algebras (Subsection
2.1
) and on natural dualities (Subsection
2.2
). In Section
3
, we begin our study of finite positive
\mathsf{MV}
-chains
\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n}
and the varieties
\mathsf{PMV}_{n}
they generate (Subsection
3.1
). We proceed to develop the natural dualities for these varieties (Subsection
3.2
). In Section
4
, we explore some ramifications of this duality. More specifically, we give an explicit axiomatization of the category dual to
\mathsf{PMV}_{2}
generated by the three-element chain (Subsection
4.1
), we explore the relationship between
\mathsf{PMV}_{n}
and
\mathsf{DL}
as described above (Subsection
4.2
) and we characterize the algebraically and existentially closed algebras in
\mathsf{PMV}_{n}
via this relationship (Subsection
4.3
). In the concluding Section
5
, we collect some open questions and ideas for further research.

Preliminaries

In this section, we give short overviews of the two most important topics related to this paper. In Subsection
2.1
, we recall the basics of
\mathsf{MV}
-algebras, with a focus on finite
\mathsf{MV}
-chains and the varieties
\mathsf{MV}_{n}
they generate. In Subsection
2.2
, we recall important prerequisites from the theory of natural dualities. For further information on these topics, the reader may consult the textbooks
[

]
about
\mathsf{MV}
-algebras and
[

about natural dualities (in particular, we will often refer to the latter throughout this entire paper).

2.1.
MV-algebras

It is well-known that Boolean algebras provide an appropriate algebraic counterpart to classical propositional logic. Similarly, to Łukasiewicz infinitely-valued logic, an appropriate algebraic counterpart is provided by
\mathsf{MV}
-algebras
, introduced by Chang
[
6
]
in 1958. The variety
\mathsf{MV}
of
\mathsf{MV}
-algebras is generated by the
standard
\mathsf{MV}
-algebra

\langle[0,1],\odot,\oplus,\wedge,\vee,\neg,0,1\rangle

based on the real unit interval with its usual bounded lattice structure and additional operations

x\odot y=\mathsf{max}\{0,x+y-1\},\text{ }x\oplus y=\mathsf{min}\{1,x+y\},\text{ }\neg x=1-x.

For a detailed overview of
\mathsf{MV}
-algebras and their relationship to many-valued logic, we refer the reader to
[
7
]
(and
[
19
]
for more advanced topics). In this paper, we focus on the finite subalgebras of the standard
\mathsf{MV}
-algebra, which are all of the following form.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
n\geq 1
be a natural number. The
(n+1)
-element
\mathsf{MV}
-chain
is given by

\text{\bf\L}_{n}=\langle\{0,\tfrac{1}{n},\dots\tfrac{n-1}{n},1\},\wedge,\vee,\odot,\oplus,\neg,0,1\rangle,

considered as a subalgebra of the standard
\mathsf{MV}
-algebra. We use
\mathsf{MV}_{n}
to denote the variety
\mathbb{H}\mathbb{S}\mathbb{P}(\text{\bf\L}_{n})
generated by
\text{\bf\L}_{n}
(these varieties were first axiomatized by Grigolia in
[
12
]
).

Note that
\text{\bf\L}_{1}
is simply the two-element Boolean algebra and, therefore,
\mathsf{MV}_{1}
is the variety of Boolean algebras, the algebraic counterpart to classical propositional logic. The varieties
\mathsf{MV}_{n}
with
n\geq 2
provide appropriate algebraic counterparts to Łukasiewicz
finitely-valued logics
. In particular, Łukasiewicz three-valued logic with
\text{\bf\L}_{2}
as algebra of truth-degrees is a popular research topic in non-classical logic.

It was shown in
[
20
, Proposition 2.1]
that every finite
\mathsf{MV}
-chain
\text{\bf\L}_{n}
is
semi-primal

[
11
]
, meaning that every operation
f\colon\{0,\frac{1}{n},\dots,\frac{n-1}{n},1\}^{k}\to\{0,\frac{1}{n},\dots,\frac{n-1}{n},1\}
(
k\geq 1
) which preserves subalgebras of
\text{\bf\L}_{n}
(that is,
f(\mathbf{S}^{k})\subseteq\mathbf{S}
for all subalgebras
\mathbf{S}\subseteq\text{\bf\L}_{n}
) is term-definable in
\text{\bf\L}_{n}
(note that this is a straightforward generalization of the two-element Boolean algebra
\text{\bf\L}_{1}
being
primal
, meaning that
every
operation
f\colon\{0,1\}^{k}\to\{0,1\}
can be expressed by a Boolean term). Some important consequences of this are that the variety
\mathsf{MV}_{n}
coincides with the quasi-variety
\mathbb{I}\mathbb{S}\mathbb{P}(\text{\bf\L}_{n})
generated by
\text{\bf\L}_{n}
, and that every
\mathsf{MV}_{n}
-algebra is a
Boolean product
(see,
e.g.
,
[
5
, Chapter IV]
) of subalgebras of
\text{\bf\L}_{n}
.

It is well-known that the subalgebras of
\text{\bf\L}_{n}
are exactly given by

\text{\bf\L}_{k}\cong\langle\{0,\tfrac{\ell}{n},\dots,\tfrac{(k-1)\ell}{n},1\},\wedge,\vee,\odot,\oplus,\neg,0,1\rangle,

where
n=k\cdot\ell
. Therefore, the lattice
\mathbb{S}(\text{\bf\L}_{n})
of subalgebras of
\text{\bf\L}_{n}
is isomorphic to the bounded lattice of divisors of
n
.

It is also well-known (and another immediate consequence of
\text{\bf\L}_{n}
being semi-primal) that, for every
d\in\text{\bf\L}_{n}
, the unary operation

\tau_{d}(x)=\begin{cases}1&\text{ if }d\leq x,\\
0&\text{ otherwise }\end{cases}

is term-definable in
\text{\bf\L}_{n}
. This means that, as shown in
[
14
]
,
\mathsf{MV}_{n}
can be identified with a (proper, for
n\geq 5
) subvariety of the variety
\mathsf{LM}_{n}
of
n
-valued
Łukasiewicz-Moisil algebras
, generated by the
n
-element chain with
\neg
defined as for
\text{\bf\L}_{n}
and all
\tau_{d}
as fundamental operations (see
[
2
]
for an overview of Łukasiewicz-Moisil algebras).

The unary terms
\tau_{d}
can be very useful, for example, they are important in the algebraic study of modal extensions of Łukasiewicz finitely-valued logic
[
3
,
13
]
. Notably, as shown in
[
21
, pp. 344–345]
, only the operations
\odot
and
\oplus
are required to define these unary terms in
\text{\bf\L}_{n}
. Thus, they will still be available in our study of finite positive
\mathsf{MV}
-chains (also see Lemma
3.3
).

2.2.
Natural dualities

The theory of natural dualities provides a common framework
to develop dual equivalences between quasi-varieties of algebras and structured Stone spaces. In particular, the theory encompasses and generalizes Stone duality for Boolean algebras and Priestley duality for distributive lattices. In this subsection, we give a selective overview of this theory. For more information, we refer the reader to the book
[
8
]
, which we often cite throughout this paper.

Let
\mathbf{M}
be a finite algebra (with underlying set
M
) and let
\mathcal{A}=\mathbb{I}\mathbb{S}\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{M})
be the quasi-variety it generates. An
alter ego
of
\mathbf{M}
is a discrete topological structure (also with underlying set
M
) of the form

\utilde{\mathbf{M}}=\langle M,\mathcal{G},\mathcal{H},\mathcal{R},\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{dis}}\rangle,

where
\mathcal{G}
is a collection of (total) homomorphisms
\mathbf{M}^{n}\to\mathbf{M}
(possibly nullary, which corresponds to
constants
),
\mathcal{H}
is a collection of
partial homomorphisms
, that is, homomorphisms from a subalgebra of
\mathbf{M}^{n}
to
\mathbf{M}
and
\mathcal{R}
is a collection of
algebraic relations
, that is, subalgebras
\mathbf{R}\subseteq\mathbf{M}^{n}
. Lastly,
\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{dis}}
is the discrete topology on
M
.

The
topological quasi-variety

\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{I}\mathbb{S}_{c}\mathbb{P}^{+}(\utilde{\mathbf{M}})
generated by
\utilde{\mathbf{M}}
consists of structured Stone spaces (recall that a
Stone space
is a topological space
(X,\mathcal{T})
which is compact, Hausdorff and totally disconnected)

\mathbf{X}=\langle X,\mathcal{G}^{\mathbf{X}},\mathcal{H}^{\mathbf{X}},\mathcal{R}^{\mathbf{X}},\mathcal{T}\rangle,

of the same type as
\utilde{\mathbf{M}}
which are isomorphic to a closed substructure of a non-empty product of
\utilde{\mathbf{M}}
. The category
\mathcal{X}
with structure-preserving continuous maps as morphisms is often described using the Preservation Theorem
[
8
, Theorem 1.4.3]
and the Separation Theorem
[
8
, Theorem 1.4.3]
.

By the Preduality Theorem
[
8
, Theorem 1.5.2]
, there exists a dual adjunction between
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{X}
given by the contravariant hom-functors
\mathsf{D}\colon\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{X}
and
\mathsf{E}\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{A}
defined by

\mathsf{D}(\mathbf{A})=\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{M})\text{ and }\mathsf{E}(\mathbf{X})=\mathcal{X}(\mathbf{X},\utilde{\mathbf{M}})

for all
\mathbf{A}\in\mathcal{A}
and
\mathbf{X}\in\mathcal{X}
. The natural transformations
e\colon 1_{\mathcal{A}}\to\mathsf{ED}
and
\varepsilon\colon 1_{\mathcal{X}}\to\mathsf{DE}
corresponding to this adjunction are given by evaluations

\displaystyle e_{\mathbf{A}}(a)(u)=u(a)\qquad

\displaystyle\text{for all }\mathbf{A}\in\mathcal{A},u\in\mathsf{D}(\mathbf{A})\text{ and }a\in A,

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{\mathbf{X}}(x)(\alpha)=\alpha(x)\qquad

\displaystyle\text{for all }\mathbf{X}\in\mathcal{X},\alpha\in\mathsf{E}(\mathbf{X})\text{ and }x\in X.

If
e
is a natural isomorphism, we say that
\utilde{\mathbf{M}}

yields a duality for
\mathcal{A}
(this is also known as
dual representation
). If
\varepsilon
is a natural isomorphism as well, we say that
\utilde{\mathbf{M}}
yields a
full
duality for
\mathcal{A}
(meaning that
\mathsf{D}
and
\mathsf{E}
establish a
dual equivalence
). In fact, in this paper we exclusively deal with
strong dualities

[
8
, Chapter 3]
, which are full dualities with the additional property that
\utilde{\mathbf{M}}
is injective in
\mathcal{X}
.

In particular, for lattice-based algebras, strong dualities can often be obtained via the NU Strong Duality Corollary
[
8
, Corollary 3.3.9]
.

Corollary 2.2
.

[
8
]

Let
\mathbf{M}
have a majority term, and let all subalgebras of
\mathbf{M}
be subdirectly irreducible. Then

\utilde{\mathbf{M}}=\langle M,K,P_{1},\mathbb{S}(\mathbf{M}\times\mathbf{M}),\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{dis}}\rangle,

yields a strong duality on
\mathcal{A}
, where

K
is the union of trivial (
i.e.
, one-element) subalgebras of
\mathbf{M}
, the set
P_{1}
consists of all unary partial homomorphisms
\mathbf{M}\to\mathbf{M}
and
\mathbb{S}(\mathbf{M}\times\mathbf{M})
consists of all binary algebraic operations.

While this corollary narrows down the structure needed to obtain a strong duality, this
\utilde{\mathbf{M}}
is usually still more complicated than it necessarily has to be. This is where (strong)
entailment
comes into play. We say that another alter ego
\utilde{\mathbf{M}}^{\prime}=\langle M,\mathcal{G}^{\prime},\mathcal{H}^{\prime},\mathcal{R}^{\prime},\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{dis}}\rangle

strongly entails

\utilde{\mathbf{M}}
if whenever
\utilde{\mathbf{M}}
yields a strong duality on
\mathcal{A}
, the same is true for
\utilde{\mathbf{M}^{\prime}}
. Similarly, we say that members of
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}\cup\mathcal{H}^{\prime}\cup\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
strongly entail members of
\mathcal{G}\cup\mathcal{H}\cup\mathcal{R}
. In the following, we give a list of admissible constructs for strong entailment relevant for this paper (see
[
8
, Chapter 9]
for a complete list of admissible constructs for entailment).

(1)

Any set of relations strongly entails the full product
\mathbf{M}^{2}
, the diagonal
\Delta_{\mathbf{M}}=\{(m,m)\mid m\in\mathbf{M}\}
of
\mathbf{M}
and the identity
\mathsf{id}_{\mathbf{M}}
on
\mathbf{M}
.

(2)

Any binary relation
\mathbf{R}
strongly entails its converse
\mathbf{R}^{-1}=\{(b,a)\mid(a,b\in\mathbf{R})\}
and
\pi_{1}(\mathbf{R}\cap\Delta_{\mathbf{M}})
.

(3)

Relations
\mathbf{S},\mathbf{R}\subseteq\mathbf{M}^{n}
strongly entail their intersection
\mathbf{S}\cap\mathbf{R}
.

(4)

Arbitrary relations
\mathbf{S}
and
\mathbf{R}
entail their product
\mathbf{S}\times\mathbf{R}
.

(5)

\utilde{\mathbf{M}}^{\prime}
strongly entails
\utilde{\mathbf{M}}
if it is obtained from
\utilde{\mathbf{M}}
by deleting a partial operation
h\in\mathcal{H}
which has an extension in
\mathcal{G}
and adding its domain to
\mathcal{R}
.

We say that
\utilde{\mathbf{M}}
yields an
optimal
strong duality if
\mathcal{G}\cup\mathcal{H}\cup\mathcal{R}
is not strongly entailed by any of its proper subsets.

We illustrate the concepts introduced in this subsection by explaining how to obtain natural dualities for
\mathsf{MV}_{n}
(these dualities have been explored in
[
20
]
). This example is a specific instance of the proof of the Semi-primal Strong Duality Theorem
[
8
, Theorem 3.3.14]
.

Example 2.3
.

Let
n\geq 1
. The discrete structure

\utilde{\text{\bf\L}}_{n}=\langle\{0,\tfrac{1}{n},\dots\tfrac{n-1}{n},1\},\mathbb{S}(\text{\bf\L}_{n}),\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{dis}}\rangle,

where members of
\mathbb{S}(\text{\bf\L}_{n})
are understood as unary relations, yields a strong duality on
\mathsf{MV}_{n}
.

Proof.

By Corollary
2.2
, the structure

\langle\{0,\tfrac{1}{n},\dots\tfrac{n-1}{n},1\},K,P_{1},\mathbb{S}(\text{\bf\L}_{n}\times\text{\bf\L}_{n}),\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{dis}}\rangle

yields a strong duality on
\mathsf{MV}_{n}
(where
K
is the union of one-element subalgebras and
P_{1}
is the collection of all unary partial homomorphisms). Since
\text{\bf\L}_{n}
is based on a bounded lattice, it has no one-element subalgebras, therefore
K=\varnothing
. Furthermore, the only homomorphism
\text{\bf\L}_{k}\to\text{\bf\L}_{n}
defined on a subalgebra
\text{\bf\L}_{k}\subseteq\text{\bf\L}_{n}
is the natural embedding of
\text{\bf\L}_{k}
. Using the strong entailment constructs (1) and (5) above, it can be replaced by its domain
\text{\bf\L}_{k}\in\mathbb{S}(\text{\bf\L}_{n})
. Every subalgebra
\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{S}(\text{\bf\L}_{n}\times\text{\bf\L}_{n})
is simply a product of subalgebras of
\text{\bf\L}_{n}
. Therefore, by (4) above, they are strongly entailed by
\mathbb{S}(\text{\bf\L}_{n})
as well.
∎

It follows from
[
8
, Theorem 9.2.6]
that modifying the structure from Example
2.3
to only include the meet-irreducible members of the lattice
\mathbb{S}(\text{\bf\L}_{n})
yields an
optimal
strong duality (also see
[
8
, Theorem 8.3.2]
).

In the next section, we aim to come up with a similarly simple natural duality for varieties generated by
positive

\mathsf{MV}
-chains.

3.
Natural dualities for varieties generated by positive MV-chains

In Subsection
3.1
, we introduce the varieties
\mathsf{PMV}_{n}
of positive
\mathsf{MV}_{n}
-algebras, generated by the positive
\mathsf{MV}_{n}
-chains
\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n}
. We prove some basic facts about congruences and subalgebras of
\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n}
and show that the variety generated by
\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n}
coincides with the quasi-variety generated by
\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n}
. In Subsection
3.2
, we develop our natural dualities for the varieties
\mathsf{PMV}_{n}
. In particular, to this end the systematic study of subalgebras of the order relation
\leq
(which is itself a subalgebra of
\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n}\times\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n}
) plays an important role.

3.1.
Positive MV-chains

Following the recent paper
[
1
]
, we use the term positive
\mathsf{MV}
-algebra to refer to a negation-free (and implication-free) subreduct of an
\mathsf{MV}
-algebra. In particular, we focus on finite positive
\mathsf{MV}
-chains defined as follows.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
n\geq 1
be a natural number. The
(n+1)
-element positive
\mathsf{MV}
-chain
is given by

\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n}=\langle\{0,\tfrac{1}{n},\dots\tfrac{n-1}{n},1\},\wedge,\vee,\odot,\oplus,0,1\rangle,

understood as a reduct of
\text{\bf\L}_{n}
. We write
\mathsf{PMV}_{n}
for the variety
\mathbb{H}\mathbb{S}\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n})
generated by
\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n}
, and we refer to members of
\mathsf{PMV}_{n}
as
positive
\mathsf{MV}_{n}
-algebras
or
\mathsf{PMV}_{n}
-algebras.

Our first result about
\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n}
is that its subalgebras are the same as the subalgebras of
\text{\bf\L}_{n}
and, therefore (recall Subsection
2.1
), the subalgebra-lattice
\mathbb{S}(\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n})
is isomorphic to the bounded lattice of diviors of
n
.

Proposition 3.2
.

The subalgebras of
\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n}
are exactly given by the subuniverses

\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{k}\cong\{0,\tfrac{\ell}{n},\dots,\tfrac{(k-1)\ell}{n},1\},

where
n=k\cdot\ell
.

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{L}\subseteq\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n}
be an arbitrary subalgebra and let
\frac{\ell}{n}
be the unique minimal element of
\mathbf{L}
which is not zero. If
\ell=n
, then
\mathbf{L}=\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{1}
holds, so assume
\ell<n
. Note that this implies
\frac{\ell}{n}\leq\frac{1}{2}
, since otherwise
\frac{\ell}{n}\odot\frac{\ell}{n}
would be an element of
\mathbf{L}
greater than zero but strictly smaller than
\frac{\ell}{n}
, contradicting our choice of
\ell
. Furthermore,
\ell
needs to be a divisor of
n
, since otherwise we can find natural numbers
x\geq 1
and
0<r<\ell
with
n=x\ell+r
. But then
\frac{x\ell}{n}\odot\frac{\ell}{n}=\frac{r}{n}
is a member of
\mathbf{L}
above zero but strictly below
\frac{\ell}{n}
, again contradicting our choice of
\ell
. Thus we showed that
\ell
divides
n
and therefore, by closure of
\mathbf{L}
under
\oplus
, we showed that
\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{k}
as in the proposition is contained in
\mathbf{L}
.

Suppose towards contradiction that there is some
\frac{s}{n}\in\mathbf{L}{\setminus}\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{k}
. Then
\ell<s
holds by the above assumption and we can find natural numbers
k>x>1
and
0<r<\ell
such that
s=x\ell+r
. This is equivalent to

r+n=s-x\ell+n=s+(k-x)\ell.

Therefore, we conclude that
\frac{r}{n}=\frac{s}{n}\odot\frac{(k-x)\ell}{n}
is in
\mathbf{L}
, once more contradicting minimality in our choice of
\ell
.
∎

As noted at the end of Subsection
2.1
, the unary operations
\tau_{d}
can be defined from
\odot
and
\oplus
alone. This fact will be of high importance in many proofs later on.

Lemma 3.3
.

[
21
]

For every
d\in\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n}
, the unary operation
\tau_{d}\colon\text{\L}_{n}\to\text{\L}_{n}
given by

\tau_{d}(x)=\begin{cases}1&\text{ if }d\leq x,\\
0&\text{ otherwise }\end{cases}

is term-definable in
\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n}
.

Remark 3.4
.

While we chose to exclusively focus on
\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n}
in this paper, all results up until Lemma
3.9
actually hold for
every
finite algebra
\mathbf{D}
which has a bounded-lattice reduct and in which
\tau_{d}
defined as above is term-definable in
\mathbf{D}
for every
d\in\mathbf{D}
. In particular, this encompasses the negation-free reducts of the finite
Łukasiewicz-Moisil chains
(see, e.g.,
[
2
]
).

Our first goal is to show that the variety
\mathsf{PMV}_{n}
coincides with the quasi-variety
\mathbb{I}\mathbb{S}\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n})
generated by
\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n}
. For this, we essentially only have to show the following.

Lemma 3.5
.

Every subalgebra
\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{k}\subseteq\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n}
(including
\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n}
itself) is simple.

Proof.

Let
\theta
be a congruence relation on
\mathbf{S}
and let
c,d\in\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{k}
be distinct elements with
(c,d)\in\theta
. We show that
\theta
is the trivial congruence identifying all members of
\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{k}
. Without loss of generality, we assume
c<d
.
Since
\tau_{d}
from Lemma
3.3
is term-definable in
\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n}
, we have
(0,1)=(\tau_{d}(c),\tau_{d}(d))\in\theta
and
(1,0)\in\theta
by symmetry. Now, for arbitrary
x,y\in\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{k}
, we have

(x,y)=\big((1,0)\wedge(x,x)\big)\vee\big((0,1)\wedge(y,y)\big)\in\theta,

which implies
\theta=\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{k}^{2}
.
∎

Since
\mathsf{PMV}_{n}
is congruence distributive (because
\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n}
is lattice-based and thus has a majority term), a standard application of Jónsson’s Lemma
[
16
]
yields the following (see,
e.g.
,
[
8
, Theorem 1.3.6]
).

Corollary 3.6
.

\mathsf{PMV}_{n}=\mathbb{I}\mathbb{S}\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n})
.

This allows us to study the variety
\mathsf{PMV}_{n}
via the theory of natural dualities in what follows.

3.2.
The natural dualities

This subsection is dedicated to finding a simple alter-ego
\utilde{\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}}_{n}
of
\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n}
which yields a ‘useful’
[
8
, Chapter 6]
strong duality on
\mathsf{PMV}_{n}
. Since
\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n}
has a bounded lattice reduct, it has a majority term and no trivial subalgebras. Furthermore, by Lemma
3.5
we know that every subalgebra of
\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n}
is subdirectly irreducible. Therefore, we may use Corollary
2.2
(
i.e.
, the NU Strong Duality Corollary
[
8
, Corollary 3.3.9]
) as our starting point. This states that

(1)

\langle\{0,\tfrac{1}{n},\dots,\tfrac{n-1}{n},1\},P_{1},\mathbb{S}(\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n}\times\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n}),\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{dis}}\rangle,

yields a strong duality for
\mathsf{PMV}_{n}
, where
P_{1}
is the set of all unary partial homomorphisms
\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n}\to\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n}
. In the following we show that, as for
\text{\bf\L}_{n}
, the only partial homomorphisms of this kind are the identities of subalgebras of
\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n}
.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{k}\subseteq\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n}
be a subalgebra. Then the only homomorphism
\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{k}\to\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n}
is the identity on
\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{k}
followed by inclusion.

Proof.

Let
h\colon\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{k}\to\mathbf{D}
be a homomorphism. Suppose the are some
s\in\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{k}
and
d\in\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n}
such that
h(s)=d
and
s\neq d
. Recall that
\tau_{d}
and
\tau_{s}
from Lemma
3.3
are term-definable and thus preserved by
h
.
If
s<d
then
1=\tau_{d}(h(s))=h(\tau_{d}(s))=h(0)=0
yields a contradiction.
If
r<s
then
1=h(\tau_{s}(s))=\tau_{s}(h(s))=\tau_{s}(d)=0
also yields a contradiction. Thus no such elements
s
and
d
can exist and we showed that
h(s)=s
holds for all
s\in\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{k}
.
∎

Therefore, as in Example
2.3
, the collection
P_{1}
of unary partial homomorphisms is strongly entailed by the collection of unary algebraic relations
\mathbb{S}(\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n})
. Now we take a closer look at the binary algebraic relations in
\mathbb{S}(\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n}\times\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n})
. Contrary to
\text{\bf\L}_{n}
, the algebra
\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n}\times\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n}
has subalgebras which are not direct products of subalgebras of
\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n}
. For example, since all operations of
\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n}
are order-preserving, the relation
\leq
and its converse
\geq
are clearly subalgebras of
\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n}\times\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n}
. In the following, we show that every other subalgebra of
\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n}
which is not a direct product of subalgebras is contained in one of those.

Lemma 3.8
.

Every subalgebra
\mathbf{R}\subseteq\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n}\times\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n}
which is not a direct product of subalgebras of
\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n}
is a subalgebra of
\leq
or of
\geq
.

Proof.

Suppose that
\mathbf{R}
is neither a subset of
\leq
nor of
\geq
. We show that this implies that
\mathbf{R}
is a direct product of subalgebras of
\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n}
. Since
\mathbf{R}
is not a subset of
\leq
, there is
(d_{1},c_{1})\in\mathbf{R}
with
d_{1}>c_{1}
. Similarly, there is
(c_{2},d_{2})\in\mathbf{R}
with
c_{2}<d_{2}
. This implies that
(1,0)=\tau_{d_{1}}(d_{1},c_{1})
and
(0,1)=\tau_{d_{2}}(c_{2},d_{2})
are both in
\mathbf{R}
. As in the proof of Lemma
3.5
, with this we can show that
\mathbf{R}
is the full direct product of its two projections
\mathsf{pr}_{1}(\mathbf{R})
and
\mathsf{pr}_{2}(\mathbf{R})
.
∎

Since every binary relation strongly entails its converse and all products of subalgebras of
\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n}
are strongly entailed by
\mathbb{S}(\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n})
, it follows that the structure

(2)

\langle\{0,\tfrac{1}{n},\dots,\tfrac{n-1}{n},1\},\mathbb{S}(\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n})\cup\mathbb{S}(\leq),\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{dis}}\rangle

yields a strong duality for
\mathsf{PMV}_{n}
, since it strongly entails the structure from Equation (
1
).

While the structure given in Equation (
2
) is already much simpler than that in Equation (
1
), it is still far from optimal. Therefore, we keep on studying
\mathbb{S}(\leq)
in order to further simplify this alter ego.

A somewhat special role is played by the subalgebra of the order
\lhd\in\mathbb{S}(\leq)
given by

\lhd=\{(x,y)\mid x=0\text{ or }y=1\}.

It is easy to see that this is a subalgebra since
0\wedge x=0\odot x=0
and
1\vee x=1\oplus x=1
for all
x\in\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n}
. Unfortunately, except for the case
n=2
, this is not the only non-diagonal proper subalgebra of the order relation. However, it is minimal among those subalgebras in the following sense.

Lemma 3.9
.

Let
\mathbf{R}\subseteq\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n}\times\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n}
be a subalgebra of the order
\leq
, which is not the diagonal of a subalgebra of
\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n}
, and
\mathbf{S}=\mathsf{pr}_{1}(\mathbf{R})\times\mathsf{pr}_{2}(\mathbf{R})
. Then
\lhd{\mid}_{\mathbf{S}}\subseteq\mathbf{R}\subseteq{\leq}{\mid}_{\mathbf{S}}
.

Proof.

Since
\mathbf{R}
is not a diagonal, there is a pair
(x,y)\in\mathbf{R}
with
x\neq y
, implying
x<y
. Therefore,
\tau_{y}(x,y)=(0,1)\in\mathbf{R}
as well. Now, for any
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in\mathbf{R}
we find that

(0,y^{\prime})=(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\wedge(0,1)\text{ and }(x^{\prime},1)=(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\vee(0,1)

are also members of
\mathbf{R}
, finishing the proof.
∎

Since diagonals of subalgebras are strongly entailed by
\mathbb{S}(\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n})
already, we only need to consider subalgebras in-between (restrictions of)
\lhd
and
\leq
. In order to describe these subalgebras, the following ‘closure’ downwards in the first and upwards in the second component will be crucial.

Definition 3.10
.

Let
\mathbf{S}=\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{k}\times\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{k^{\prime}}

3.2
). Let
(x,y)\in\mathbf{S}
with
x\leq y
. We denote by
C_{(x,y),\mathbf{S}}
the following subset of
\mathbf{S}
and
\leq
.

C_{(x,y),\mathbf{S}}=\{(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in\mathbf{S}\mid x^{\prime}\leq x\text{ and }y\leq y^{\prime}\}.

If
\mathbf{S}=\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n}\times\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n}

C_{(x,y)}
instead of
C_{(x,y),\mathbf{S}}
.

In the next lemma, we show that non-diagonal subalgebras of the order are closed under these subsets in the following sense.

Lemma 3.11
.

Let
\mathbf{R}\subseteq\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n}\times\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n}
be a subalgebra of the order
\leq
which is not the diagonal of a subalgebra of
\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n}
, and
\mathbf{S}=\mathsf{pr}_{1}(\mathbf{R})\times\mathsf{pr}_{2}(\mathbf{R})
. If
(x,y)\in\mathbf{R}
, then
C_{(x,y),\mathbf{S}}\subseteq\mathbf{R}
as well.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.9
we know that
\lhd{\mid}_{\mathbf{S}}\subseteq\mathbf{R}
. Now let
(x,y)\in\mathbf{R}
, and say
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in\mathbf{S}
satisfies
x^{\prime}\leq x
and
y\leq y^{\prime}
. Then
(x^{\prime},y)=(x,y)\wedge(x^{\prime},1)
is in
\mathbf{R}
and, thus,
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})=(x^{\prime},y)\vee(0,y^{\prime})
is also in
\mathbf{R}
.
∎

Therefore, clearly every
\mathbf{R}
as in the above lemma is a union of sets of the form
C_{(x,y),\mathbf{S}}
. However, not all unions of sets of this form necessarily yield subalgebras. In the following, we identify exactly those unions which
do
give rise to subalgebras of
\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n}\times\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n}
.

Proposition 3.12
.

Let
\mathbf{S}=\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{k}\times\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{k^{\prime}}
be a product of subalgebras of
\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n}
.

(1)

Let
\mathbf{R}\subseteq\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n}\times\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n}
be a subalgebra of
\leq
, which is not the diagonal of a subalgebra of
\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n}
, with
\mathsf{pr}_{1}(\mathbf{R})\times\mathsf{pr}_{2}(\mathbf{R})=\mathbf{S}
. Then
\mathbf{R}
can be expressed as

\mathbf{R}=\bigcup_{i=0}^{k}C_{(\frac{i}{k},y_{i}),\mathbf{S}}

where
y_{i}
is the minimal element of
\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{k^{\prime}}
with
(\frac{i}{k},y_{i})\in\mathbf{R}
(in particular,
y_{0}=0
and
y_{k}=1
).

(2)

Let
y_{0},\dots,y_{k}
be an increasing sequence of elements of
\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{k^{\prime}}
with
y_{0}=0,y_{k}=1
and
\frac{i}{k}\leq y_{i}
for all
i=1,\dots,k-1
. Then

\mathbf{R}=\bigcup_{i=0}^{k}C_{(\frac{i}{k},y_{i}),\mathbf{S}}

is a subalgebra of
\mathbf{S}
if and only if the conditions

(\tfrac{i}{k},y_{i})\odot(\tfrac{j}{k},y_{j})\in\mathbf{R}\text{ and }(\tfrac{i}{k},y_{i})\oplus(\tfrac{j}{k},y_{j})\in\mathbf{R}

hold for all
i,j\in\{1,\dots,k-1\}
.

Proof.

(1): By Lemma
3.11
we have
\bigcup_{i=0}^{k}C_{(\frac{i}{k},y_{i}),\mathbf{S}}\subseteq\mathbf{R}
. Conversely, if
(\frac{i}{k},y)\in\mathbf{R}
then
y_{i}\leq y
by minimality of
y_{i}
and therefore
(\frac{i}{k},y)\in C_{(\frac{i}{k},y_{i}),\mathbf{S}}
.

(2): Clearly these conditions are necessary for
\mathbf{R}
to be a subalgebra. We show that they are also sufficient. So, supposing these condition hold, we want to show that
\mathbf{R}
is indeed a subalgebra. First note that
\lhd{\mid}_{\mathbf{S}}=C_{(0,0),\mathbf{S}}\cup C_{(1,1),\mathbf{S}}\subseteq\mathbf{R}
, in particular this implies that both constants
(0,0)
and
(1,1)
are contained in
\mathbf{R}
. Now let
(x,y)
and
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
be two elements of
\mathbf{R}
, say
(x,y)\in C_{(\frac{i}{k},y_{i}),\mathbf{S}}
and
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in C_{(\frac{j}{k},y_{j}),\mathbf{S}}
. Furthermore, without loss of generality we assume
i\leq j
.

We first establish the closure under the lattice operations. To show closure under meets, we note that

(x,y)\wedge(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})=\begin{cases}(x,y)&\text{if }x\leq x^{\prime},y\leq y^{\prime},\\
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})&\text{if }x^{\prime}\leq x,y^{\prime}\leq y,\\
(x^{\prime},y)&\text{if }x^{\prime}\leq x,y\leq y^{\prime},\\
(x,y^{\prime})&\text{if }x\leq x^{\prime},y^{\prime}\leq y.\end{cases}

In the first two cases the meet is obviously still in
\mathbf{R}
. In the third case the two inequalities
x^{\prime}\leq x\leq\frac{i}{k}
and
y_{i}\leq y
imply
(x^{\prime},y)\in C_{(\frac{i}{k},y_{i}),\mathbf{S}}
. In the fourth and final case the two inequalities
x\leq x^{\prime}\leq\frac{j}{k}
and
y_{j}\leq y^{\prime}
imply
(x,y^{\prime})\in C_{(\frac{j}{k},y_{j}),\mathbf{S}}
. Closure under joins is established analogously since

(x,y)\vee(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})=\begin{cases}(x,y)&\text{if }x^{\prime}\leq x,y^{\prime}\leq y,\\
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})&\text{if }x\leq x^{\prime},y\leq y^{\prime},\\
(x^{\prime},y)&\text{if }x\leq x^{\prime},y^{\prime}\leq y,\\
(x,y^{\prime})&\text{if }x^{\prime}\leq x,y\leq y^{\prime}.\end{cases}

Note that in the third case we get
(x^{\prime},y)\in C_{(\frac{j}{k},y_{j}),\mathbf{S}}
and in the fourth case we get
(x,y^{\prime})\in C_{(\frac{i}{k},y_{i}),\mathbf{S}}
.

Now let
\ast\in\{\odot,\oplus\}
, and note that
(x,y)\in C_{(\frac{i}{k},y_{i}),\mathbf{S}}
and
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in C_{(\frac{j}{k},y_{j}),\mathbf{S}}
together with monotonicity of
\ast
imply

x\ast x^{\prime}\leq\tfrac{i}{k}\ast\tfrac{j}{k}\text{ and }y_{i}\ast y_{j}\leq y\ast y^{\prime}.

However, by assumption we have
(\frac{i}{k}\ast\frac{j}{k},y_{i}\ast y_{j})\in\mathbf{R}
say it is contained in
C_{(\frac{h}{k},y_{h}),\mathbf{S}}
. Thus

x\ast x^{\prime}\leq\tfrac{i}{k}\ast\tfrac{j}{k}\leq\tfrac{h}{k}\text{ and }y_{h}\leq y_{i}\ast y_{j}\leq y\ast y^{\prime}

immediately implies that
(x,y)\ast(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
is also contained in
C_{(\frac{h}{k},y_{h}),\mathbf{S}}
, finishing the proof.
∎

Definition 3.13
.

Let
\mathcal{S}_{n}\subseteq\mathbb{S}(\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n}\times\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n})
be the collection of all subalgebras
\mathbf{R}\subseteq\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n}\times\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n}
which satisfy
\lhd\subseteq\mathbf{R}\subseteq{\leq}
.

It is clear by definition that
\mathcal{S}_{n}
is a bounded sublattice of
\mathbb{S}(\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n}\times\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n})
with lower bound
\lhd
and upper bound
\leq
.

In the next two (technical) lemmas, we show that the set of relations
\mathcal{S}_{n}
strongly entails
\mathbb{S}(\leq)
and
\mathbb{S}(\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n})
. The first lemma shows that relations
\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{S}(\leq)
with
\mathsf{pr}_{1}(\mathbf{R})\times\mathsf{pr}_{2}(\mathbf{R})\neq\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n}\times\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n}
are strongly entailed by
\mathbb{S}(\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n})
and
\mathcal{S}_{n}
.

Lemma 3.14
.

Let
\mathbf{R}\subseteq\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n}\times\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n}
be a subalgebra of the order
\leq
, which is not the diagonal of a subalgebra of
\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n}
, and let
\mathbf{S}=\mathsf{pr}_{1}(\mathbf{R})\times\mathsf{pr}_{2}(\mathbf{R})=\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{k}\times\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{k^{\prime}}
for some divisors
k,k^{\prime}
of
n
. Then there exists a subalgebra
\overline{\mathbf{R}}\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
with
\mathbf{R}=\overline{\mathbf{R}}\cap\mathbf{S}
.

Proof.

By Proposition
3.12
(1), we know that
\mathbf{R}
can be expressed as union

\mathbf{R}=\bigcup_{i=0}^{k}C_{(\frac{i}{k},y_{i}),\mathbf{S}}

where
y_{i}
is the minimal element of
\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{k^{\prime}}
with
(\frac{i}{k},y_{i})\in\mathbf{R}
. Let
n=k\cdot\ell
. We define
\overline{\mathbf{R}}
by

\overline{\mathbf{R}}=\bigcup_{j=0}^{n}C_{(\frac{j}{n},\hat{y}_{j})}

where we stipulate
\hat{y}_{0}=0
and

\hat{y}_{j}=\begin{cases}y_{1}&\text{if }1\leq j\leq\ell,\\
y_{2}&\text{if }\ell+1\leq j\leq 2\ell,\\
\vdots&\vdots\\
y_{k-1}&\text{if }(k-2)\ell+1\leq j\leq(k-1)\ell,\\
y_{k}=1&\text{if }(k-1)\ell+1\leq j\leq n.\end{cases}

We show that
\overline{\mathbf{R}}
is a subalgebra of
\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n}\times\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n}
using Proposition
3.12
(2). That is, for any
j_{1},j_{2}\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}
, we want to show that
(\frac{j_{1}}{n},\hat{y}_{j_{1}})\ast(\frac{j_{2}}{n},\hat{y}_{j_{2}})\in\overline{\mathbf{R}}
holds for the
\mathsf{MV}
-operations
\ast\in\{\odot,\oplus\}
.

Let
i_{1},i_{2}\in\{1,\dots,k\}
be the unique elements satisfying

(i_{1}-1)\ell<j_{1}\leq i_{1}\ell\text{ and }(i_{2}-1)\ell<j_{2}\leq i_{2}\ell,

which by definition means
\hat{y}_{j_{1}}=y_{i_{1}}
and
\hat{y}_{j_{2}}=y_{i_{2}}
. Since
\mathbf{R}
is a subalgebra, we know that
(\frac{i_{1}}{k},y_{i_{1}})\ast(\frac{i_{2}}{k},y_{i_{2}})\in\mathbf{R}
, say it is in
C_{(\frac{h}{k},y_{h}),\mathbf{S}}
. Now because
\frac{j_{1}}{n}\leq\frac{i_{1}\ell}{n}=\frac{i_{1}}{k}
and similarly for
j_{2},i_{2}
, we have

\frac{j_{1}}{n}\ast\frac{j_{2}}{n}\leq\frac{i_{1}}{k}\ast\frac{i_{2}}{k}\leq\frac{h}{k}=\frac{h\ell}{n}

and furthermore

y_{h}\leq y_{i_{1}}\ast y_{i_{2}}=\hat{y}_{j_{1}}\ast\hat{y}_{j_{2}}.

Because
\hat{y}_{h\ell}=y_{h}
, this shows that
(\frac{j_{1}}{n},\hat{y}_{j_{1}})\ast(\frac{j_{2}}{n},\hat{y}_{j_{2}})\in C_{(\frac{h\ell}{n},\hat{y}_{h\ell})}\subseteq\overline{\mathbf{R}}
, finishing the proof.
∎

Our second lemma shows that the collection
\mathbb{S}(\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n})
is strongly entailed by
\mathcal{S}_{n}
.

Lemma 3.15
.

For every
\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{k}\in\mathbb{S}(\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n})
, there exists a
\mathbf{R}\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
such that
\mathbf{R}\cap\Delta_{\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n}}=\Delta_{\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{k}}
(where
\Delta_{\mathbf{A}}
denotes the diagonal of the corresponding algebra
\mathbf{A}
).

Proof.

Let
n=k\cdot\ell
and
\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{k}
be given as in Proposition
3.2
. We define
\mathbf{R}
by

\mathbf{R}=\bigcup_{i=0}^{n}C_{(\frac{i}{n},y_{i})}

where we stipulate
y_{0}=0
and

y_{i}=\begin{cases}\frac{\ell}{n}&\text{if }1\leq j\leq\ell,\\
\frac{2\ell}{n}&\text{if }\ell+1\leq j\leq 2\ell,\\
\vdots&\vdots\\
\frac{(k-1)\ell}{n}&\text{if }(k-2)\ell+1\leq j\leq(k-1)\ell,\\
1&\text{if }(k-1)\ell+1\leq j\leq n.\end{cases}

By definition it is clear that
\mathbf{R}\cap\Delta_{\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n}}=\Delta_{\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{k}}
, so we only have to show that
\mathbf{R}
is a subalgebra. For this, we again use Proposition
3.12
(2). Let
i_{1},i_{2}\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}
and let
j_{1},j_{2}
be the unique elements of
\{1,\dots,k\}
with

(j_{1}-1)\ell<i_{1}\leq j_{1}\ell\text{ and }(j_{2}-1)\ell<i_{2}\leq j_{2}\ell,

which means that
y_{i_{1}}=\frac{j_{1}\ell}{n}
and
y_{i_{2}}=\frac{j_{2}\ell}{n}
. Furthermore, let
\frac{j_{1}\ell}{n}\ast\frac{j_{2}\ell}{n}=\frac{h\ell}{n}
(note that such an
h
exists because
\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{k}
is a subalgebra). Then

\frac{i_{1}}{n}\ast\frac{i_{2}}{n}\leq\frac{j_{1}\ell}{n}\ast\frac{j_{2}\ell}{n}=\frac{h\ell}{n}

implies

(\tfrac{i_{1}}{n},y_{i_{2}})\ast(\tfrac{i_{2}}{n},y_{i_{2}})\in C_{(\frac{h\ell}{n},\frac{h\ell}{n})}\subseteq\mathbf{R},

which finishes the proof.
∎

With these two lemmas at hand, we are ready to state and easily prove the main theorem of this section.

Theorem 3.16
.

Let
n\geq 1
. The discrete relational structure

\utilde{\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}}_{n}=\langle\{0,\tfrac{1}{n},\dots,\tfrac{n-1}{n},1\},\mathcal{S}_{n},\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{dis}}\rangle

yields a strong duality for
\mathsf{PMV}_{n}
.

Proof.

By the discussion after Lemma
3.8
, we know that the structure given in Equation (
2
), that is
\langle\{0,\tfrac{1}{n},\dots,\tfrac{n-1}{n},1\},\mathbb{S}(\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n})\cup\mathbb{S}(\leq),\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{dis}}\rangle
, yields a strong duality for
\mathsf{PMV}_{n}
. By Lemma
3.14
, we know that every
\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{S}(\leq)
is an intersection of (and thus strongly entailed by) a product of subalgebras of
\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n}
and a relation from
\mathcal{S}_{n}
. By Lemma
3.15
subalgebras of
\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n}
are strongly entailed by
\mathcal{S}_{n}
as well.
∎

In light of Proposition
3.12
, it is fairly straightforward to find the lattice
\mathcal{S}_{n}
in a systematic way. Indeed, in Appendix
A
we provide an easy algorithm to compute this lattice. Also note that, to obtain an
optimal duality
(see
[
8
, Chapters 8 and 9]
), we could simplify the above structure further by only including meet-irreducible elements of
\mathcal{S}_{n}
(this follows from
[
8
, Theorem 9.2.6]
). However, since it won’t make a significant difference in this paper, we keep working with the alter ego from Theorem
3.16
.

Definition 3.17
.

For all
n\geq 1
, let
\mathcal{X}_{n}
be the topological quasi-variety
\mathbb{I}\mathbb{S}_{c}\mathbb{P}^{+}(\utilde{\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}}_{n})
generated by the structure from Theorem
3.16
. Furthermore, let
\mathsf{D}_{n}\colon\mathsf{PMV}_{n}\to\mathcal{X}_{n}
and
\mathsf{E}_{n}\colon\mathcal{X}_{n}\to\mathsf{PMV}_{n}
be the (hom-)functors establishing the corresponding dual equivalence.

Note that these dualities can be seen as many-valued generalizations of Priestley duality, which is recovered in the case where
n=1
.

In the following, we collect some consequences of Theorem
3.16
which can be immediately derived from the general theory of natural dualities.

Corollary 3.18
.

The categories
\mathsf{PMV}_{n}
and
\mathcal{X}_{n}
have the following properties.

(1)

\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n}
is injective in
\mathsf{PMV}_{n}
and
\utilde{\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}}_{n}
is injective in
\mathcal{X}_{n}
.

(2)

The injectives in
\mathsf{PMV}_{n}
are exactly the Boolean powers
\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n}[\mathbf{B}]
, where
\mathbf{B}
is a non-trivial complete Boolean algebra.

(3)

\mathsf{PMV}_{n}
has the amalgamation property.

(4)

A morphism
\varphi\colon\mathbf{X}_{1}\to\mathbf{X}_{2}
in
\mathcal{X}_{n}
is an embedding (a surjection) if and only if
\mathsf{E}_{n}(\varphi)
is a surjection (an embedding). A homomorphism
h\colon\mathbf{A}_{1}\to\mathbf{A}_{2}
in
\mathsf{PMV}_{n}
is an embedding (a surjection) if and only if
\mathsf{D}_{n}(h)
is a surjection (an embedding).

(5)

The congruence lattice of
\mathbf{A}\in\mathsf{PMV}_{n}
is dually isomorphic to the lattice of closed substructures of
\mathsf{D}_{n}(\mathbf{A})
.

(6)

Coproducts in
\mathcal{X}_{n}
are given by direct union (
i.e.
, the duality is
logarithmic
).

Proof.

The second part of statement (1) follows from the definition of strong duality, the first part follows from
[
8
, Lemma 3.2.10]
and the fact that
\utilde{\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}}_{n}
is a total structure. Statement (2) follows from
[
8
, Theorem 5.5.15]
because all relations
\mathbf{R}\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
avoid binary products. Statement (3) follows from
[
8
, Lemma 5.3.4]
. Statement (4) follows from (1) and
[
8
, Lemmas 3.2.6 and 3.2.8]
. Statement (5) follows from
[
8
, Theorem 3.2.1]
. Lastly, statement (6) follows from
[
8
, Theorem 6.3.3]
.
∎

This corollary already demonstrates how useful these dualities are. In the next section, we investigate it further to derive more results about the varieties
\mathsf{PMV}_{n}
.

4.
Further explorations of the dualities

In this section, we delve deeper into various aspects of the natural dualities established in the previous section. In Subsection
4.1
, we give a concrete axiomatization of the category
\mathcal{X}_{2}
dual to the variety
\mathsf{PMV}_{2}
generated by the three-element positive
\mathsf{MV}

4.1.
The dual category for the three-element positive MV-chain.

Among the finitely-valued Łukasiewicz logics, arguably the most popular is the three-valued logic corresponding to the variety
\mathsf{MV}_{2}
generated by the three-element
\mathsf{MV}
-chain
\text{\bf\L}_{2}
. In this section, we focus on the variety
\mathsf{PMV}_{2}
generated by the positive three-element
\mathsf{MV}
-chain
\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{2}
. More specifically, we provide an explicit description of the category
\mathcal{X}_{2}
dual to
\mathsf{PMV}_{2}
.

Theorem 4.1
.

A structured Stone space
\mathbf{X}=\langle X,\lhd^{\mathbf{X}},\leq^{\mathbf{X}},\mathcal{T}\rangle
with binary relations
\lhd^{\mathbf{X}}
and
\leq^{\mathbf{X}}
closed in
X^{2}
is a member of
\mathcal{X}_{2}
if and only if it satisfies the following axioms.

(a)

x\lhd^{\mathbf{X}}y\Rightarrow x\leq^{\mathbf{X}}y
.

(b)

(X,\leq^{\mathbf{X}},\mathcal{T})
is a Priestley space, that is,
\leq^{\mathbf{X}}
is a partial order and
if
x\not\leq^{\mathbf{X}}y
, then there exists a clopen upset
U
containing
x
but not
y
.

(c)

If
x\ntriangleleft^{\mathbf{X}}y
but
x\leq^{\mathbf{X}}y
, then there exist a clopen upset
U
and a clopen downset
D
with the following properties

•

x\notin D
and
y\notin U
,

•

For all
z,z^{\prime}\in X
, if
z\lhd^{\mathbf{X}}z^{\prime}
then
z\in D
or
z^{\prime}\in U
.

Proof.

First we show that every member
\mathbf{X}=\langle X,\lhd^{\mathbf{X}},\leq^{\mathbf{X}},\mathcal{T}\rangle
of
\mathcal{X}_{2}
satisfies (a)-(c). The formula (a) is quasi-atomic and holds in
\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n}
, therefore, by the Preservation Theorem
[
8
, Theorem 1.4.3]
, it also holds for all members of
\mathcal{X}_{2}
.

To see condition (b) that
(X,\leq^{\mathbf{X}},\mathcal{T})
is a Priestley space, assume that
x\not\leq\mathbf{X}y
. By the Separation Theorem
[
8
, Theorem 1.4.4]
, there exists a
\mathcal{X}_{2}
-morphism
\varphi\colon\mathbf{X}\to\utilde{\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}}_{2}
with
\varphi(x)>\varphi(y)
. If
\varphi(x)=1
, choose
U=\varphi^{-1}(\{1\})
and if
\varphi(x)=\frac{1}{2}
, choose
U=\varphi^{-1}{\{\frac{1}{2}\}}\cup\varphi^{-1}(\{1\})
. In both cases,
U
is a clopen (because
\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{2}
carries the discrete topology and
\varphi
is continuous) upset (because
\varphi
is order-preserving) which contains
x
but not
y
.

To see (c), assume
x\ntriangleleft^{\mathbf{X}}y
but
x\leq^{\mathbf{X}}y
. Then, again by the Separation Theorem, there exists a morphism
\varphi\colon\mathbf{X}\to\utilde{\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}}_{2}
with
\varphi(x)\ntriangleleft\varphi(y)
but
\varphi(x)\leq\varphi(y)
. Since
\lhd={\leq}{\setminus}\{(\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2})\}
, this implies
\varphi(x)=\varphi(y)=\frac{1}{2}
. The clopen upset
U=\varphi^{-1}(\{1\})
and the clopen downset
D=\varphi^{-1}(\{0\})
satisfy the two subconditions of (c), the first one since
\varphi(x)=\varphi(y)=\frac{1}{2}
and the second one since
z\lhd\mathbf{X}z^{\prime}
and
\varphi(z)=\varphi(z^{\prime})=\frac{1}{2}
would yield a contradiction
\varphi(z)\ntriangleleft\varphi(z^{\prime})
to
\varphi
being a morphism.

For the converse, assuming that
\mathbf{X}=(X,\lhd^{\mathbf{X}},\leq^{\mathbf{X}},\mathcal{T})
satisfies (a)-(c), we want to show that it is a member of
\mathcal{X}_{2}
. We apply the Separation Theorem again.

Suppose
x\not\leq^{\mathbf{X}}y
. Using that
(X,\leq^{\mathbf{X}},\mathcal{T})
is a Priestley space, we can find a clopen upset
U
which contains
x
but not
y
. We define a continuous map
\varphi\colon X\to\{0,\frac{1}{2},1\}
by
\varphi(z)=1
if
z\in U
and
f(z)=0
otherwise. This clearly is order-preserving, and it also preserves
\lhd
, because
\lhd
is a subset of
\leq
by (a) and, in
\utilde{\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}}_{2}
the relations
\lhd
and
\leq
coincide on the subset
\{0,1\}
. Clearly this morphism satisfies
\varphi(x)\not\leq\varphi(y)
.

In particular, the above covers the case where
x\neq y
and the case where
x\ntriangleleft^{\mathbf{X}}y
and
x\not\leq^{\mathbf{X}}y
hold. Now assume
x\ntriangleleft^{\mathbf{X}}{y}
but
x\leq^{\mathbf{X}}y
. Take a clopen upset
U
and a clopen downset
D
as given in (c). Replacing
U
by the clopen upset
U^{\prime}:=U{\setminus}D
, the properties of (c) are still satisfied, since
z\lhd^{\mathbf{X}}z^{\prime}
and
z\notin D
imply
z^{\prime}\in U
, and
z^{\prime}\in D
would yield the contradiction
z\in D
, so
z^{\prime}\in U^{\prime}
. Let the continuous map
\varphi\colon X\to\{0,\frac{1}{2},1\}
be defined via

\varphi(z)=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }z\in D,\\
1&\text{if }z\in U^{\prime},\\
\frac{1}{2}&\text{if }z\in X{\setminus}(D\cup U^{\prime}).\end{cases}

This is a well-defined continuous map since
D
,
U^{\prime}
and
X{\setminus}(D\cup C)
forms a clopen partition of
X
. Furthermore, since
x\notin D
(which implies
y\notin D
) and
y\notin U
(which implies
x\notin U
) implies
\varphi(x)=\varphi(y)=\frac{1}{2}
(
i.e.
,
\varphi(x)\ntriangleleft\varphi(y)
), it remains to be shown that
\varphi
preserves
\leq
and
\lhd
. Order-preservation follows immediately from the fact that
U
is an upset and
D
is a downset. Now suppose
z\lhd^{\mathbf{X}}z^{\prime}
. Then
z\in D
, which implies
\varphi(z)=0
holds, or
z^{\prime}\in U^{\prime}
, which implies
\varphi(z^{\prime})=1
holds. In both cases,
\varphi(z)\lhd\varphi(z^{\prime})
is assured.
∎

In the next subsection, we give a similar but more ‘implicit’ axiomatization of the categories
\mathcal{X}_{n}
for
n>2
as well. Since (as we’ve already seen in the case
n=2
) all structures
\mathbf{X}\in\mathcal{X}_{n}
have underlying Priestley spaces, we then proceed to explore various functors relating our natural dualities to Priestley duality.

4.2.
The relationship to Priestley duality

We continue to denote the functors establishing the duality from Theorem
3.16
by
\mathsf{D}_{n}\colon\mathsf{PMV}_{n}\to\mathcal{X}_{n}
and
\mathsf{E}_{n}\colon\mathcal{X}_{n}\to\mathsf{PMV}_{n}
. In particular, for
n=1
this coincides with Priestley duality between the variety of distributive lattices
\mathsf{DL}=\mathsf{PMV}_{1}
and the category of Priestley spaces
\mathsf{Priest}=\mathcal{X}_{1}
. In this case, we simply use
\mathsf{D}\colon\mathsf{DL}\to\mathsf{Priest}
and
\mathsf{E}\colon\mathsf{Priest}\to\mathsf{DL}
instead of
\mathsf{D}_{1}
and
\mathsf{E}_{1}
.

In this subsection, we show that there are functors
\mathfrak{S}\colon\mathsf{PMV}_{n}\to\mathsf{DL}
taking the
distributive skeleton
and
\mathfrak{P}\colon\mathsf{DL}\to\mathsf{PMV}_{n}
taking a
Priestley power
with
\mathfrak{S}
being left-adjoint to
\mathfrak{P}
. This is similar to the adjunction between the Boolean skeleton functor
\mathsf{MV}_{n}\to\mathsf{BA}
and the Boolean power functor
\mathsf{BA}\to\mathsf{MV}_{n}
(which exists for any variety generated by a semi-primal lattice extension) from
[
17
, Section 4]
.

While, in theory, the Separation Theorem
[
8
, Theorem 1.4.3]
always gives an ‘implicit’ description of the dual categories, the reader can imagine that for
n>2
, it gets increasingly complicated to come up with more ‘explicit’ descriptions of the categories
\mathcal{X}_{n}
similar to Theorem
4.1
. Therefore, in these cases we content ourselves with the following.

Proposition 4.2
.

A structured Stone space
\mathbf{X}=\langle X,(\mathbf{R}^{\mathbf{X}}\mid\mathbf{R}\in\mathcal{S}_{n}),\mathcal{T}\rangle
with closed binary relations
\mathbf{R}^{\mathbf{X}}
is a member of
\mathcal{X}_{n}
if and only if it satisfies the following:

(a)

x\mathbf{R}_{1}^{\mathbf{X}}y\Rightarrow x\mathbf{R}_{2}^{\mathbf{X}}y
for all
\mathbf{R}_{1}\subseteq\mathbf{R}_{2}
in
\mathcal{S}_{n}
.

(b)

\langle X,\leq^{\mathbf{X}},\mathcal{T}\rangle
is a Priestley space.

(c)

For all
\mathbf{R}\in\mathcal{S}_{n}{\setminus}\{\leq\}
, if
(x,y)\notin\mathbf{R}^{\mathbf{X}}
, then there is a structure-preserving continuous map
\varphi\colon\mathbf{X}\to\utilde{\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}}_{n}
with
(\varphi(x),\varphi(y))\notin\mathbf{R}
.

Proof.

Every member
\mathbf{X}
of
\mathcal{X}_{n}
satisfies the quasi-atomic formulas from (a). Furthermore, both (b) and (c) are immediate consequences of the Separation Theorem. To see that
\langle X,\leq^{\mathbf{X}},\mathcal{T}\rangle
is a Priestley space), assume
x\not\leq^{\mathbf{X}}y
. By the Separation Theorem there is a morphism
\varphi\colon\mathbf{X}\to\utilde{\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}}_{n}
with
\varphi(x)\not\leq\varphi(y)
. Let
\varphi(x)=\frac{i}{n}
. Then
U=\varphi^{-1}(\{\frac{1}{n}\})\cup\varphi^{-1}(\{\frac{i+1}{n}\})\cup\dots\cup\varphi^{-1}(\{\frac{n-1}{n}\})\cup\varphi^{-1}(\{1\})
is a clopen upset which contains
x
but not
y
. The converse is also a straightforward application of the Separation Theorem.
∎

Therefore, there always is a forgetful functor
\mathsf{U}\colon\mathcal{X}_{n}\to\mathsf{Priest}
sending an object of
\mathcal{X}_{n}
to its underlying Priestley space and a
\mathcal{X}_{n}
-morphism to itself. In the following, we show that the dual of
\mathsf{U}
is given by the
distributive skeleton functor

\mathfrak{S}\colon\mathsf{PMV}_{n}\to\mathsf{DL}
. This is similar to the
Boolean skeleton functor

\mathsf{MV}_{n}\to\mathsf{BA}
, which is dual to the corresponding forgetful functor from the category dual to
\mathsf{MV}_{n}
to
\mathsf{Stone}

[
17
, Subsection 4.2]
.
The distributive skeleton of a
\mathsf{PMV}_{n}
algebra is defined completely analogous to the Boolean skeleton of an
\mathsf{MV}_{n}
algebra (see,
e.g.
,
[
7
, Section 1.5]
.

Definition 4.3
.

Let
\mathbf{A}\in\mathsf{PMV}_{n}
. The
distributive skeleton
of
\mathbf{A}
is the bounded distributive lattice

\mathfrak{S}({\mathbf{A}})=\langle\mathfrak{S}(A),\wedge,\vee,0,1\rangle

defined on the carrier set
\mathfrak{S}(A)=\{a\in A\mid a\oplus a=a\}
, with the operations
\wedge,\vee
and constants
0,1
inherited from
\mathbf{A}
.

To turn this into a functor
\mathfrak{S}\colon\mathsf{PMV}_{n}\to\mathsf{DL}
, for a homomorphism
h\colon\mathbf{A}\to\mathbf{A}^{\prime}
between
\mathsf{PMV}_{n}
-algebras, simply define the homomorphism
\mathfrak{S}h\colon\mathfrak{S}(\mathbf{A})\to\mathfrak{S}(\mathbf{A}^{\prime})
by restriction
\mathfrak{S}h=h{\mid}_{\mathfrak{S}(\mathbf{A})}
.

Theorem 4.4
.

The functor
\mathfrak{S}\colon\mathsf{PMV}_{n}\to\mathsf{DL}
is dual to the functor
\mathsf{U}\colon\mathcal{X}_{n}\to\mathsf{Priest}
, that is,
\mathsf{D}\mathfrak{S}
is naturally isomorphic to
\mathsf{U}\mathsf{D}_{n}

Proof.

By definition, natural in the choice of
\mathbf{A}\in\mathsf{PMV}_{n}
, we want to find an order-preserving homeomorphism

\Phi_{\mathbf{A}}\colon(\mathsf{PMV}_{n}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n}),\leq)\to(\mathsf{DL}(\mathfrak{S}(\mathbf{A}),\mathbf{2}),\leq),

where
\mathbf{2}
denotes the two-element distributive lattice. We claim that

\Phi_{\mathbf{A}}(u)=u{\mid}_{\mathfrak{S}(\mathbf{A})}

has these desired properties.

To see that
\Phi_{\mathbf{A}}
is injective, suppose that
u\neq u^{\prime}
are two distinct homomorphisms
\mathbf{A}\to\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n}
. Let
a\in\mathbf{A}
be such that
u(a)\neq u^{\prime}(a)
, without loss of generality say
u(a)<u^{\prime}(a)
. Then, for
d=u^{\prime}(a)

u(\tau_{d}(a))=\tau_{d}(u(a))=0
and
u^{\prime}(\tau_{d}(a))=\tau_{d}(u^{\prime}(a))=1
. Since
\tau_{d}(a)\in\mathfrak{S}(\mathbf{A})
holds, this shows that
\Phi(u)\neq\Phi(u^{\prime})
.

\Phi
is surjective. Let

be a homomorphism. We construct a homomorphism

with

. Given

preserves

Now let

which is therefore also satisfied in
\mathbf{A}
. But now, if we assume that

implies

, which is a contradiction to

. Therefore,

is a homomorphism. The restriction of
u_{p}
to

is equal to

Definition 4.5

Let

Let

and let

defined by

\alpha(x)=f(\alpha_{1}(x),\dots,\alpha_{n}(x))
is continuous and order-preserving. Order-preservation is easy, since if

we know that
\alpha_{i}(x)\leq\alpha_{i}(y)
for all

and since

is order-preserving we have

\alpha(x)=f(\alpha_{1}(x),\dots,\alpha_{n}(x))\leq f(\alpha_{1}(y),\dots,\alpha_{n}(y))\leq\alpha(y).

To see that
\alpha
is continuous, we show that
\alpha^{-1}(\{m\})
is clopen for every

be the finite set of tuples

with

. Then we have

\alpha^{-1}(\{m\})=\bigcup_{(m_{1},\dots,m_{n})\in N}\alpha_{1}^{-1}(\{m_{1}\})\cap\dots\cap\alpha_{n}^{-1}(\{m_{n}\}),

which is clopen because

is finite and all
\alpha_{i}
are continuous.

In the last subsection, we describe the algebraically and existentially closed members of
\mathsf{PMV}_{n}
via their duals. For this, Boolean powers (rather than Priestley powers) play an essential role. However, since Boolean powers arise as special cases of Priestley powers, the results of this subsection will prove useful towards this end.

4.3.
Algebraically and existentially closed algebras

A standard application of natural dualities is the classification of algebraically closed and existentially closed algebras via their duals (see,
e.g.
,
[
8
, Sections 5.3 and 5.4]
). In this subsection, we give full classifications of the algebraically closed and existentially closed members of
\mathsf{PMV}_{n}
via Boolean powers. Note that, for a complemented bounded distributive lattice
\mathbf{B}
, the Priestley power
\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n}[\mathbf{B}]
from Definition
4.5
coincides with the usual Boolean power
\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n}[\mathbf{B}]
. Since the structure
\utilde{\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}}_{n}
is total, we can use the AC-EC Theorem
[
8
, Theorem 5.3.5.]
to characterize algebraically and existentially closed members of
\mathsf{PMV}_{n}
.

Before we state this theorem, we recall that
\mathbf{X}\in\mathcal{X}_{n}
has the
dual finite homomorphism property

(\text{FHP})^{\ast}
if, for all finite
\mathbf{Y},\mathbf{Z}\in\mathcal{X}_{n}
and surjective morphisms
\varphi\colon\mathbf{X}\to\mathbf{Z}
,
\psi\colon\mathbf{Y}\to\mathbf{Z}
, there exists a morphism
\lambda\colon\mathbf{X}\to\mathbf{Y}
such that
\varphi=\psi\circ\lambda
.

\text{(FHP})^{\ast}

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbf{X}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbf{Z}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbf{Y}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\varphi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\exists\lambda}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\psi}

The
dual finite embedding property

(\text{FEP})^{\ast}
is the similar same, except that
\lambda
is also required to be surjective.

Theorem 4.9
.

[
8
]

Let
\mathbf{A}\in\mathsf{PMV}_{n}
.

(1)

\mathbf{A}
is algebraically closed if and only if
\mathsf{D}_{n}(\mathbf{A})
has the dual finite homomorphism property
(\text{FHP})^{\ast}
.

(2)

\mathbf{A}
is existentially closed if and only if
\mathsf{D}_{n}(\mathbf{A})
has the finite embedding property
(\text{FEP})^{\ast}
.

We now show that algebraically and existentially closed members of
\mathsf{PMV}_{n}
stem from Boolean algebras in the following sense.

Theorem 4.10
.

Let
\mathbf{A}\in\mathsf{PMV}_{n}
.

(1)

\mathbf{A}
is algebraically closed if and only if
\mathbf{A}
is isomorphic to a Boolean power
\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n}[\mathbf{B}]
, where
\mathbf{B}\in\mathsf{BA}
is an arbitrary Boolean algebra.

(2)

\mathbf{A}
is existentially closed if and only if
\mathbf{A}
is isomorphic to a Boolean power
\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n}[\mathbf{B}]
, where
\mathbf{B}\in\mathsf{BA}
is an atomless Boolean algebra.

Proof.

By (the proof of) Theorem
4.7
, we know that the duals of Boolean powers
\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n}[\mathbf{B}]
in
\mathcal{X}_{n}
are exactly the structures isomorphic to some

where

the discrete order and
\mathbf{R}^{\mathbf{X}}
is empty for all other
\mathbf{R}\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
. We first show by contrapositive that if
\mathbf{X}
has the finite homomorphism property, then it needs to be of this form.

Let
\mathbf{X}\in\mathcal{X}_{n}
not be of the form described above. If
\leq
is not discrete, there are distinct

with
x<y
. Let
U
be an upset of
X
containing
y
but not
x
. Define
\mathbf{Z}\in\mathcal{X}_{n}
to consist of two points

(and all other relations empty), and let
\mathbf{Y}

with the discrete order (and all other relations empty). Let

. However, this is impossible since this would mean

and

5.
Conclusion

We developed a logarithmic optimal natural duality for the variety
\mathsf{PMV}_{n}
of positive
\mathsf{MV}_{n}
-algebras, generated by
\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n}
, the negation-free reduct of the finite
\mathsf{MV}
-chain
\text{\bf\L}_{n}
. We explored the relationship between this duality and Priestley duality, showing that there is an adjunction between
\mathsf{DL}
and
\mathsf{PMV}_{n}
given by the distributive skeleton functor
\mathfrak{S}\colon\mathsf{PMV}_{n}\to\mathsf{DL}
and the Priestley power functor
\mathfrak{P}\colon\mathsf{DL}\to\mathsf{PMV}_{n}
. Specializing this relationship to Boolean powers, we gave a full characterization of algebraically and existentially closed members of
\mathsf{PMV}_{n}
. In the following, a few open questions, remarks and ideas for further research are collected.

(1)

As noted in Remark
3.4
, the results of Subsection
3.2
up until Lemma
3.9
, as well as Lemma
3.5
and Corollary
3.6
from Subsection
3.1
hold not only for
\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n}
, but for every lattice-based algebra
\mathbf{D}
in which
\tau_{d}
is term-definable for every
d\in\mathbf{D}
. Furthermore, the existence of these
\tau_{d}
together with a ‘weak form of negation’ is equivalent to semi-primality of a lattice-based algebra
[
17
, Proposition 2.8]
. Is there a sensible definition of ‘lattice-semi-primal’ algebras (similar to lattice-primal algebras), such that the results of this paper be seen as a specific instance of a more general result about natural dualities for such lattice-semi-primal algebras?

(2)

In Corollary
4.8
, we showed that every
\mathsf{PMV}_{n}
-algebra can be embedded into the Priestley power of its distributive skeleton, similarly to how every
\mathsf{MV}_{n}
-algebra can be embedded into the Boolean power of its Boolean skeleton. Based on this, a category equivalent to
\mathsf{MV}_{n}
was described in
[
9
]
. This equivalence was explained from the point of view of natural dualities in
[
20
]
. Is there a similar categorical equivalence for
\mathsf{PMV}_{n}
? To deal with this question, the more constructive description of Priestley powers from
[
18
]
could prove useful.

(3)

In this paper, the logical aspects of
\mathsf{PMV}_{n}
-algebras were only hinted at. In future work, we plan to explore these aspects further. In particular, we aim to investigate modal extensions of
\mathsf{PMV}_{n}
-algebras to deal with an analogue of Dunn’s positive modal logic
[
10
]
in the setting of modal finitely-valued Łukasiewicz logic
[
13
]
.

and the sequence
y_{i}=\frac{i}{n}
corresponds to
\leq
. However, not every such sequence corresponds to a subalgebra. By Proposition
3.12
, the sequences which correspond to subalgebras, which we call
good
sequences, are the ones which satisfy

(\tfrac{j}{n},y_{j})\odot(\tfrac{j^{\prime}}{n},y_{j^{\prime}})\in\bigcup_{i=0}^{n}C_{(\frac{i}{n},y_{i})}\text{ and }(\tfrac{j}{n},y_{j})\oplus(\tfrac{j^{\prime}}{n},y_{j^{\prime}})\in\bigcup_{i=0}^{n}C_{(\frac{i}{n},y_{i})}

for all
j,j^{\prime}\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}
. In fact, similar to the proof of Lemma
3.14
, it can be shown that it is sufficient to check this condition only for the ‘corner elements’, that is, at indices
i
which satisfy
y_{i}<y_{i+1}
(with
y_{n}=1
).

Thus, the set
\mathcal{S}_{n}
is in bijective correspondence with the set of good sequences. Furthermore, the union associated to
y_{1},\dots,y_{n-1}
is contained in the union associated to
y_{1}^{\prime},\dots,y_{n-1}^{\prime}
if and only if
y_{i}^{\prime}\leq y_{i}
holds for
i=1,\dots,n-1
. Thus we can also retrieve the lattice structure of
\mathcal{S}_{n}
. Altogether, we proved that the following algorithm yields the lattice
\mathcal{S}_{n}
.

Step 1.
Generate the set
Y
of all sequences
[y_{1},\dots,y_{n}]
of elements of
\mathbf{P\text{\bf\L}}_{n}
with
y_{1}\leq,\dots,\leq y_{n-1}
and
\frac{i}{n}\leq y_{i}
for all
i=1,\dots,n-1
.

Step 2.
Start with
G=\emptyset
and do the following for every sequence
[y_{1},\dots,y_{n-1}]\in Y
. Let
J\subseteq\{1,\dots,n-1\}
be the collection of all indices
i
with
y_{i}<y_{i+1}
(where
y_{n}:=1
). Check whether

(\tfrac{j}{n},y_{j})\odot(\tfrac{j^{\prime}}{n},y_{j^{\prime}})\in\bigcup_{i=0}^{n}C_{(\frac{i}{n},y_{i})}\text{ and }(\tfrac{j}{n},y_{j})\oplus(\tfrac{j^{\prime}}{n},y_{j^{\prime}})\in\bigcup_{i=0}^{n}C_{(\frac{i}{n},y_{i})}

holds for all
j,j^{\prime}\in J
. Add
[y_{1},\dots,y_{n-1}]
to
G
if and only if this holds.

Step 3.
Order the set
G
obtained after completing Step 2 by

[y_{1},\dots,y_{n-1}]\leq[y_{1}^{\prime},\dots,y^{\prime}_{n-1}]\Leftrightarrow y^{\prime}_{i}\leq y_{i}\text{ for }i=1,\dots,n-1.

This results in
G\cong\mathcal{S}_{n}
(note that the order on
G
is simply the component-wise converse order
\geq
).

As an example, we compute the set
\mathcal{S}_{4}
using the above algorithm.

Step 1.
The set
Y
consists of the following
14
sequences:

\displaystyle[1,1,1],

\displaystyle[\tfrac{3}{4},1,1],

\displaystyle[\tfrac{3}{4},\tfrac{3}{4},1],

\displaystyle[\tfrac{3}{4},\tfrac{3}{4},\tfrac{3}{4}],

\displaystyle[\tfrac{2}{4},1,1],

\displaystyle[\tfrac{2}{4},\tfrac{3}{4},1],

\displaystyle[\tfrac{2}{4},\tfrac{3}{4},\tfrac{3}{4}],

\displaystyle[\tfrac{2}{4},\tfrac{2}{4},1],

\displaystyle[\tfrac{2}{4},\tfrac{2}{4},\tfrac{3}{4}],

\displaystyle[\tfrac{1}{4},1,1],

\displaystyle[\tfrac{1}{4},\tfrac{3}{4},1],

\displaystyle[\tfrac{1}{4},\tfrac{3}{4},\tfrac{3}{4}],

\displaystyle[\tfrac{1}{4},\tfrac{2}{4},1],

\displaystyle[\tfrac{1}{4},\tfrac{2}{4},\tfrac{3}{4}].

Step 2.

The set
G
of good sequences consists of the following
7
sequences:

\displaystyle[1,1,1],

\displaystyle[\tfrac{3}{4},1,1],

\displaystyle[\tfrac{3}{4},\tfrac{3}{4},1],

\displaystyle[\tfrac{2}{4},1,1],

\displaystyle[\tfrac{2}{4},\tfrac{3}{4},1],

\displaystyle[\tfrac{2}{4},\tfrac{2}{4},1],

\displaystyle[\tfrac{1}{4},\tfrac{2}{4},\tfrac{3}{4}].

The other sequences are not good for the following reasons.

•

The sequences
[\tfrac{3}{4},\tfrac{3}{4},\tfrac{3}{4}]
and
[\tfrac{2}{4},\tfrac{3}{4},\tfrac{3}{4}]
are not good because
(\frac{3}{4},\frac{3}{4})\odot(\frac{3}{4},\frac{3}{4})=(\frac{2}{4},\frac{2}{4})\notin C_{(\frac{2}{4},\frac{2}{4})}
.

•

The sequence
[\frac{2}{4},\frac{2}{4},\frac{3}{4}]
is not good because
(\frac{2}{4},\frac{2}{4})\odot(\frac{3}{4},\frac{3}{4})=(\frac{1}{4},\frac{1}{4})\notin C_{(\frac{1}{4},\frac{2}{4})}
.

•

The sequences
[\tfrac{1}{4},1,1]
,
[\tfrac{1}{4},\tfrac{3}{4},1]
and
[\tfrac{1}{4},\tfrac{3}{4},\tfrac{3}{4}]
are not good because
(\frac{1}{4},\frac{1}{4})\oplus(\frac{1}{4},\frac{1}{4})=(\frac{2}{4},\frac{2}{4})\notin C_{(\frac{2}{4},\frac{3}{4})}
.

•

The sequence
[\tfrac{2}{4},\tfrac{2}{4},1]
is not good because
(\frac{1}{4},\frac{1}{4})\oplus(\frac{2}{4},\frac{2}{4})=(\frac{3}{4},\frac{3}{4})\notin C_{(\frac{3}{4},1}
.

Step 3.
The lattice
\mathcal{S}_{4}
looks as follows:

[1,1,1]
[\tfrac{3}{4},1,1]
[\tfrac{2}{4},1,1]
[\tfrac{3}{4},\tfrac{3}{4},1]
[\tfrac{2}{4},\tfrac{3}{4},1]
[\tfrac{2}{4},\tfrac{2}{4},1]
[\tfrac{1}{4},\tfrac{2}{4},\tfrac{3}{4}]

To obtain an optimal strong duality for
\mathsf{PMV}_{4}
, we only need to consider the meet-irreductible elements of this lattice,
i.e.
, we remove the relation corresponding to the sequence
[\tfrac{3}{4},1,1]
.
