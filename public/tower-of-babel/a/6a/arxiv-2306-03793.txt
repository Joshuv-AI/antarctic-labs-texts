U-Statistic Reduction: Higher-Order Accurate Risk Control and Statistical-Computational Trade-Off, with Application to Network Method-of-Moments
Meijia Shao

Abstract
U-statistics play central roles in many statistical learning tools but face the haunting issue of scalability. Significant efforts have been devoted into accelerating computation by U-statistic reduction. However, existing results almost exclusively focus on power analysis, while little work addresses risk control accuracy – comparatively, the latter requires distinct and much more challenging techniques. In this paper, we establish the first statistical inference procedure with provably higher-order accurate risk control for incomplete U-statistics. The sharpness of our new result enables us to reveal how risk control accuracy also trades off with speed for the first time in literature, which complements the well-known variance-speed trade-off. Our proposed general framework converts the long-standing challenge of formulating accurate statistical inference procedures for many different designs into a surprisingly routine task. This paper covers non-degenerate and degenerate U-statistics, and network moments. We conducted comprehensive numerical studies and observed results that validate our theory’s sharpness. Our method also demonstrates effectiveness on real-world data applications.



Keywords:

Nonparametrics,
statistical learning,
Edgeworth expansion,
fast computation.

1.1
Background: U-statistics in statistical learning

A
U-statistic
,
associated with
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
randomly sampled from a general probability space and a degree-
r
permutation-invariant kernel function
h(x_{1},\ldots,x_{r})
such that
h(x_{1},\ldots,x_{r})=h(x_{\pi(1)},\ldots,x_{\pi(r)})
for any bijection
\pi:[1:r]\leftrightarrow[1:r]

U_{n}:=\binom{n}{r}^{-1}\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<\ldots<i_{r}\leq n}h(X_{i_{1}},\ldots,X_{i_{r}})=:\binom{n}{r}^{-1}\sum_{I_{r}\in{\cal C}_{n}^{r}}h(X_{I_{r}}),

(1)

where
{\cal C}_{n}^{k}:=\{(i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}):1\leq i_{1}<\ldots<i_{k}\leq n\}
and
X_{I_{k}}:=(X_{i_{1}},\ldots,X_{i_{k}})
for
k\in[1:r]
.
U-statistics play central roles in many contemporary statistical learning methods.

(Example 1 of

)

Test the symmetry of the distribution of

Computational challenge: why we need U-statistic reduction

One major obstacle in the practical use of U-statistics lies in its heavy computational cost. It takes

1.3
Two aspects of computational-statistical trade-off

There are two kinds of prices we must pay for computation reduction.
First, this reduction inflates
\mathrm{Var}(U_{J})
, which further determines: (i) the confidence interval radius; and (ii) the minimum separation condition
|\mu_{H_{a}}-\mu_{H_{0}}|
for consistently testing
H_{0}:\mu=\mu_{H_{0}}
versus
H_{a}:\mu=\mu_{H_{a}}
. Here
\mu:=\mathbb{E}U_{n}
.
This aspect of computational-statistical trade-off is easy to quantify thus well-understood.
The overwhelming majority of existing literature on U-statistic reduction regards this aspect.
The pioneering work
Blom 1976
provides the earliest systematic study,
followed up by many works aiming at designing
{\cal J}_{n,\alpha}
smartly to minimize
\mathrm{Var}(U_{J})
under a given computational budget
O(n^{\alpha})

(
Lee 1979
;
Lee 1982
;
Lee 2019
;
Rempala and
Wesolowski 2003
;
Clémençon et al. 2016
;
Kong and
Zheng 2021
;
Dürre and
Paindaveine 2021
)
.

The second kind of price for speeding-up is the depreciation of
risk control accuracy
in statistical inference. The risk control accuracy refers to: (i)
|\mathbb{P}(\textrm{true }\mu\in\textrm{CI})-(1-\beta)|
(confidence interval); and (ii)
|\mathbb{P}(\textrm{actual type I error rate})-\beta|
(hypothesis testing), where the target confidence and significance levels are
1-\beta
and
\beta
, respectively.
Technically speaking, it is governed by the accuracy of approximating the distribution of a properly-designed studentization of
U_{J}
.
Most existing works only prove asymptotic normality of
U_{J}

(
Brown and
Kildea 1978
;
Janson 1984
;
Chen and
Peng 2021
)
; while to our best knowledge,
Chen and
Kato 2019a
is the only existing work that provides finite-sample error bound result in this regard.
However, revealing the computational-statistical trade-off in risk control accuracy requires more accurate characterization of the sampling distribution of studentized incomplete U-statistic that is not yet available in existing literature
2
2

2

For example, the “computational-statistical trade-off” in Remark 3.1 of
Chen and
Kato 2019a
refers to the
\mathrm{Var}(U_{J})
aspect; while their Berry-Esseen bound on distribution approximation (Theorem 3.3) does not characterize how risk control accuracy depreciates as computation reduces in the most interesting regime
N\gg n
.
. Our paper aims to fill this blank.

1.4
Our contributions

In this article, we present the first comprehensive study on
risk control accuracy
in statistical inference for incomplete U-statistics.
Our results cover non-degenerate, degenerate and noisy U-statistics.
For non-degenerate U-statistics, we established the first higher-order accurate distribution approximation to incomplete U-statistics with general designs.
This leads to Cornish-Fisher CI’s and tests with higher-order accurate risk controls.
Our approach only requires the design to satisfy two natural, weak and easy-to-verify assumptions.
This converts the challenging task of analyzing many different designs into routine calculations.
Our method strictly complies with the
O(n^{\alpha})
computational budget and permits convenient parallelization.
For degenerate U-statistics,
Weber 1981
and
Chen and
Kato 2019a
observed a very interesting phenomenon that the incompleteness of reduced U-statistics, apart from speeding up, also reinstates normality. Pushing their results forward, we established the first finite-sample higher-order accurate distribution approximation for the studentization of an incomplete degenerate U-statistic.
Thirdly, we applied our theory and methods to incomplete network U-statistics, commonly known as
network moments

(
Bickel
et al. 2011
;
Zhang and
Xia 2022
)
.

Across all three scenarios, our method’s accuracy significantly improves over the best existing results.
The sharpness of our error bounds allows us to reveal the trade-off between speed and the risk control accuracy of incomplete U-statistics, for the first time in literature.
For practitioners,
our method provides fast and easy-to-implement solutions with guidance on how much computation would be required if the user aims at a certain risk control accuracy goal under
finite sample size
.
Our analysis discovers interesting differences between the three scenarios.
They are reflected in the variation of practical guidance.
For non-degenerate U-statistics, achieving higher-order risk control accuracy only requires
\alpha>1
; and we can reduce the computation cost from
O(n^{r})
to
O(n^{2})
almost “for free”: it does not deteriorate risk control accuracy at all and only inflates
\mathrm{Var}(U_{J})
imperceptibly.
In sharp contrast, for most network moments (except the
edge
motif), higher-order accurate inference requires
\alpha>2
even for densest networks;
in presence of network sparsity, the guidance will further vary accordingly.

The theory part of this paper is majorly different from complete U-statistic literature
Helmers 1991
;
Maesono 1997
;
Putter and van
Zwet 1998
and features several innovations.
The incompleteness nature of our studied U-statistic not only introduces new and complicated leading terms, but moreover breaches the symmetry of remainder terms, making existing bounds inapplicable.
We addressed this using a different technique.
A second example is that the formulation of a succinct and weak Assumption
2
, a highlighted contribution of this paper, was also distilled from our theoretical exploration.
In the proof of Lemma
2.3
, a key supporting result accompanying Assumption
2
, we handled the challenging dependency in some random sampling schemes.
On nonparametric network analysis, our Theorem
4.2
significantly expands the classical analysis of triangles and V-shapes under sparse Erdös-Renyi model in
Gao and
Lafferty 2017
to all motifs under general sparse graph models.
The proof features our novel martingale analysis.

In summary, what our paper caters to is not just one specific application or data structure, but the infrastructural question of
risk control accuracy
in U-statistic reduction.
The comprehensive theory & methods toolbox we developed here provides an urgently needed (but currently missing) service to many U-statistic-based learning methods who want to properly control their inference risks while scaling up.

Reduction of non-degenerate noiseless U-statistics

2.1
Method and theory for general designs

Recall that
{\cal J}_{n,\alpha}\subseteq{\cal C}_{n}^{r}
denotes the index set for the reduced U-statistic
U_{J}
defined in (
3
).
Following the convention
(
Lee 2019
;
Chen and
Kato 2019a
)
, we call
{\cal J}_{n,\alpha}
the
design
.
Throughout this subsection,
{\cal J}_{n,\alpha}
is given and fixed.
For any
I_{k}\in{\cal C}_{n}^{k}
,
define
a_{n,\alpha;k}(I_{k}):=\big|\{\widetilde{I}_{r}\in{\cal J}_{n,\alpha}:I_{k}\subseteq\widetilde{I}_{r}\}\big|
to be the number of elements in
{\cal J}_{n,\alpha}
that contain
I_{k}
as a subset. An immediate fact is
\sum_{I_{k}\in{\cal C}_{n}^{k}}a_{n,\alpha;k}(I_{k})={r\choose k}|{\cal J}_{n,\alpha}|
for any
k\in[1:r]
.
By
Han and
Qian 2018
, we have

\displaystyle U_{J}=

\displaystyle~\mu+|{\cal J}_{n,\alpha}|^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{n,\alpha;1}(i)g_{1}(X_{i})+|{\cal J}_{n,\alpha}|^{-1}\sum_{k=2}^{r}\sum_{I_{k}\in{\cal C}_{n}^{k}}a_{n,\alpha;k}(I_{k})g_{k}(X_{I_{k}}),

(4)

where
\mu:=\mathbb{E}[h(X_{[1:r]})]
, and define projection terms:

g_{1}(X_{1}):=\mathbb{E}[h(X_{[1:r]})|X_{1}]-\mu
, for all
k\in[2:r]
,

g_{k}(X_{[1:k]}):=\mathbb{E}[h(X_{[1:r]})|X_{[1:k]}]-\mu-\sum_{k^{\prime}=1}^{k-1}\sum_{I_{k^{\prime}}\in{\cal C}_{[1:k]}^{k^{\prime}}}g_{k^{\prime}}(X_{I_{k^{\prime}}})
.
All
g_{k}
terms are mutually uncorrelated.
We call the U-statistic

if
\xi_{1}^{2}:=\mathrm{Var}(g_{1}(X_{1}))\geq{\rm constant}>0
and call it

if
\xi_{1}=0
.
Here, we focus on the non-degenerate case and relegate the degenerate case to Section
3
.
In both Sections
2
and
3
, we observe
h(X_{I_{r}})
without observational error – we call such U-statistics
noiseless
. In Section
4
, we study network moments as
noisy
U-statistics, where we only observe noise-contaminated
h(X_{I_{r}})
’s.

Next, we estimate
\mathrm{Var}(U_{J})
for studentizing
U_{J}
.
This seemingly straightforward task
(
Han and
Qian 2018
)
now requires careful thoughts due to incompleteness.
Specifically, we need to choose between estimating
\sigma_{J}^{2}:=\mathrm{Var}(U_{J})
or

\sigma_{J;1}^{2}:=\mathrm{Var}(|{\cal J}_{n,\alpha}|^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{n,\alpha;1}(i)g_{1}(X_{i}))
.
This question does not exist in the complete U-statistic setting where the two quantities differ by
O(n^{-2})

(
Maesono 1997
;
Zhang and
Xia 2022
)

, but now, we have

|\sigma_{J}^{2}-\sigma_{J;1}^{2}|\asymp n^{-\alpha}
,
which cannot be directly ignored when
\alpha\in(1,2]
.
In this paper, we choose to estimate

\sigma_{J;1}

for two reasons: formulation cleanness, and computation convenience.
Fortunately, the bias caused by using
\sigma_{J;1}
instead of
\sigma_{J}
to studentize
U_{J}
will be properly accounted for by the Edgeworth correction terms, see Remark
2.3
.
Now since
\sigma_{J;1}^{2}=|{\cal J}_{n,\alpha}|^{-2}\big\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{n,\alpha;1}^{2}(i)\big\}\xi_{1}^{2}
, it suffices to estimate
\xi_{1}^{2}:=\mathrm{Var}(g_{1}(X_{1}))
by

\displaystyle\widetilde{\xi}_{1}^{2}:=

\displaystyle~n^{-\alpha}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{d=1}^{n^{\alpha-1}}h(X_{[i:d:(i+(r-1)d)]})h(X_{[i:(-d):(i-(r-1)d)]})-\widetilde{\mu}^{2},

(5)

where

\widetilde{\mu}^{2}:=n^{-\alpha}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{d=1}^{n^{\alpha-1}}h(X_{[i:d:(i+(r-1)d)]})h(X_{[(i+rd):d:(i+(2r-1)d)]}).

Computing
\widetilde{\xi}_{1}^{2}
costs
O(n^{\alpha})
. Denote the shorthand
M_{\alpha}:=|{\cal J}_{n,\alpha}|^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{n,\alpha;1}^{2}(i)\asymp n^{\alpha-1}
. Let
\{B_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
and
\{b_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
be sequences of random variables and deterministic values, respectively. We write
B_{n}={\widetilde{O}_{p}}(b_{n})
if
\mathbb{P}(B_{n}\geq C\cdot b_{n})=O(n^{-1})
for some absolute constant
C>0
and when
n
is large enough. The plug-in bias of
\widetilde{\xi}_{1}^{2}
is characterized by the following lemma.

Lemma 2.1
.

Set
\alpha\in(1,2)
, we have

\displaystyle\widetilde{\xi}_{1}^{2}-\xi_{1}^{2}=

\displaystyle~\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big\{g_{1}^{2}(X_{i})-\xi_{1}^{2}\big\}+\frac{1}{n{M_{\alpha}}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{d=1}^{{M_{\alpha}}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{r-1}g_{1}(X_{i})\Big\{g_{2}(X_{i},X_{i+\ell d})+g_{2}(X_{i},X_{i-\ell d})\Big\}

\displaystyle+{\widetilde{O}_{p}}(n^{-\alpha/2}\log^{1/2}n).

(6)

With Lemma
2.1
, we are ready to formulate and decompose the studentization of
U_{J}
.

\displaystyle T_{J}:=

\displaystyle~\frac{U_{J}-\mu}{|{\cal J}_{n,\alpha}|^{-1}\big\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{n,\alpha;1}^{2}(i)\big\}^{1/2}\cdot\widetilde{\xi}_{1}}=({\cal T}_{1}+{\cal T}_{2})(1+{\cal T}_{3})^{-1/2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~{\cal T}_{1}+{\cal T}_{2}-\frac{1}{2}{\cal T}_{1}{\cal T}_{3}+{\widetilde{O}_{p}}\big(n^{-\alpha/2}\log n\big),

where we define the following shorthand

\displaystyle{\cal T}_{1}:=

\displaystyle~\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{n,\alpha;1}(i)g_{1}(X_{i})}{\big\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{n,\alpha;1}^{2}(i)\big\}^{1/2}\xi_{1}},\quad{\cal T}_{2}:=\frac{\sum_{k=2}^{r}\sum_{I_{k}\in{\cal C}_{n}^{k}}a_{n,\alpha;k}(I_{k})g_{k}(X_{I_{k}})}{\big\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{n,\alpha;1}^{2}(i)\big\}^{1/2}\xi_{1}},

\displaystyle{\cal T}_{3}:=

\displaystyle~\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{g_{1}^{2}(X_{i})-\xi_{1}^{2}}{n\xi_{1}^{2}}+\frac{1}{n{M_{\alpha}}\xi_{1}^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{d=1}^{{M_{\alpha}}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{r-1}g_{1}(X_{i})\Big\{g_{2}(X_{i},X_{i+\ell d})+g_{2}(X_{i},X_{i-\ell d})\Big\}.

(8)

Next, we formulate the sampling distribution of
T_{J}
with a fixed design
{\cal J}_{n,\alpha}
. We aim at addressing general
{\cal J}_{n,\alpha}
’s, but of course it cannot be completely arbitrary.
Fortunately, it turns out that we only need two weak and natural regularity conditions on
{\cal J}_{n,\alpha}
.

Assumption 1
.

The design of
{\cal J}_{n,\alpha}
is data-oblivious, namely,

\displaystyle{\cal J}_{n,\alpha}\perp(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}).

(9)

For a deterministic
{\cal J}_{n,\alpha}
, (
9
) means
{\cal J}_{n,\alpha}
is designed without consulting the data
X_{[1:n]}
.

Assumption
1
is a very common assumption in U-statistic literature.
We will briefly discuss a recent but seminal paper
Kong and
Zheng 2021
on data-aware designs in Section
7
, but the quick gist is that being data-aware usually requires (possibly expensive) additional computation.
A second motivation for data-oblivious designs comes from the analysis of network U-statistics, where
X_{[1:n]}
are latent
(
Zhang and
Xia 2022
)
.

Our second assumption is motivated by the natural idea of avoiding bad designs such as
{\cal J}_{n,\alpha}:=\big([1:r],\ldots,[1:r]\big)
.
Existing literature suggests that a good design should be balanced, i.e.,
g_{k}(X_{I_{k}})
’s with different
I_{k}
’s should have similar frequencies in (
4
). Our next assumption formalizes this intuition.

Assumption 2
.

Set
\alpha\in(1,r)
.
It holds simultaneously for all
k\in[1:r]
and
I_{k}\in{\cal C}_{n}^{k}
that

\displaystyle a_{n,\alpha;k}(I_{k})\in\begin{cases}[C_{2},C_{3}]n^{\alpha-k},&\textrm{ if }k<\alpha,\\
[0,C_{3}]\log n,&\textrm{ if }k=\alpha,\\
[0,C_{3}],&\textrm{ if }k>\alpha,\end{cases}

(10)

where
C_{2},C_{3}:0<C_{2}<C_{3}
are universal constants.
Moreover, for all
k\in[1:r]

\displaystyle\sum_{I_{k}\in{\cal C}_{n}^{k}}a_{n,\alpha;k}^{2}(I_{k})\asymp

\displaystyle~n^{\alpha}\cdot n^{(\alpha-k)\vee 0}.

(11)

One might expect to see more assumptions, but surprisingly, Assumptions
1
and
2

This is one of the highlighted contributions of this paper.
These two assumptions are satisfied by our proposed deterministic “no-waste” design in Section

Kato 2019a

that four typical random designs satisfy Assumption

Aside from Assumptions
1
and

, another common assumption in U-statistic literature is Cramér’s condition

;

)

\limsup_{t\to\infty}\big|\mathbb{E}[e^{{\mathbbm{i}}t\xi_{1}^{-1}\cdot g_{1}(X_{1})}]\big|<1.

We will show that

3

Let

Reduction of network moments as noisy U-statistics

Sections

Now, we turn our attention to an important class of noisy U-statistics, called

Consider an undirected network with binary edges, where each node is associated with a latent position

Uniform

\displaystyle~\binom{n}{r}^{-1}\sum_{I_{r}\in{\cal C}_{n}^{r}}h(A_{I_{r}}).

Notice that in (

Therefore, data-aware reduction techniques such as

\displaystyle\widehat{U}_{J}:=\big|{\cal J}_{n,\alpha}\big|^{-1}\sum_{I_{r}\in{\cal J}_{n,\alpha}}h(A_{I_{r}})=\big|{\cal J}_{n,\alpha}\big|^{-1}\sum_{I_{r}\in{\cal C}_{n}^{r}}a_{n,\alpha;r}(I_{r})h(A_{I_{r}}).

\displaystyle\widehat{T}_{J}^{\prime}:=

\displaystyle~\frac{\widehat{U}_{J}-\mu}{\widehat{\sigma}_{J}^{\prime}}.

(58)

The next theorem establishes the asymptotic normality of
\widehat{T}_{J}^{\prime}
.
Define
\widecheck{M}_{\alpha}(\rho_{n},n,\alpha;R)
to almost equal
\widetilde{\cal M}_{\alpha}(\rho_{n},n,\alpha;R)
, except for replacing
\rho_{n}^{-s/2}n^{-(\alpha-1)/2}
by
\rho_{n}^{-s/2}n^{-\alpha/2}
.

Theorem 4.2
.

Assume the conditions of Theorem
4.1
hold, except replacing the condition
\widetilde{\cal M}_{\alpha}(\rho_{n},n,\alpha;R)\to 0

by
\widecheck{M}_{\alpha}(\rho_{n},n,\alpha;R)\to 0
.
Then we have

\displaystyle\widehat{T}_{J}^{\prime}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}N(0,1).

(59)

In Theorem
4.2
, we still need the assumption
\rho_{n}^{s}n^{\alpha}\to\infty
, which is not much weaker than
\rho_{n}^{s}n^{\alpha-1}\to\infty
, but based on the empirical evidence from our simulation result (Section
5.3
, Figure
4
), we conjecture that the assumption
\rho_{n}^{s}n^{\alpha}\to\infty
should be minimal
13
13

13

As a theoretical evidence: consider the simplest motif:
R=
Edge. When
\rho_{n}\cdot n^{\alpha}\to\lambda
(constant), the limiting distribution of
\widehat{T}_{J}^{\prime}

(

Discussion

Our work assumes that the degree of degeneracy
k_{0}
is known.
This is a common practice in the analysis of degenerate U-statistics and seemingly cannot be circumvented by bootstraps: as Remark 3.2 in
Chen and
Kato 2019a
pointed out, the knowledge of
k_{0}
is critical for correctly setting how many
r
-tuples to be randomly sampled.
Fortunately, our proofs imply that each
\xi_{k}
’s can be estimated by
\widehat{\xi}_{k}
defined in Section
2
with concentration bound
|\widehat{\xi}_{k}-\xi_{k}|={\widetilde{O}_{p}}(n^{-1/2}\log^{k/2}n)
.
Therefore, one can simply test
H_{0}(k):\xi_{k}=0
versus
H_{a}(k):\xi_{k}={\rm constant}>0
by comparing
\widehat{\xi}_{k}
to
n^{-c_{0}}
,
c_{0}\in(0,1/2)
to find
k_{0}
.
Another approach, called the “blocking trick”, by
Ho and Shieh 2006
, breaks the data
X_{[1:n]}
into
K_{0}
blocks
X_{[\{(k-1)(n/K_{0})+1\}:(kn/K_{0})]},k\in[1:K_{0}]
, computes a complete U-statistic on each block, and makes inference using a CLT for the sample mean of
K_{0}
i.i.d. terms.
Its unification of degenerate and non-degenerate cases and automatic adaptation to
k_{0}
, however, comes at the price of heavily wasting data.

Our study throughout this paper exclusively focuses on
data-oblivious
reduction schemes. Recently,
Kong and
Zheng 2021
proposed a
data-aware
reduction scheme, based on their key observation that
X_{[1:r]}\approx Y_{[1:r]}
implies
h(X_{[1:r]})\approx h(Y_{[1:r]})
, thus by clustering
X_{i}
’s, one can effectively reduce the U-statistic’s computation.
While their method shows very attractive performance, finite-sample higher-order analysis for their method poses an interesting open challenge.
There is also a computational price for
being data-aware
.
For example, in the setting considered by
Moon and
Chen 2022
, the clustering of all
X_{i}
’s in some Banach space requires computing at least
O(n^{2})
many potentially expensive (like in our second data example) pairwise distances.
