Bayesian learning with Gaussian processes for low-dimensional representations of time-dependent nonlinear systems
Shane A. McQuarrie, Anirban Chaudhuri, Karen E. Willcox, Mengwu Guo

Abstract
This work presents a data-driven method for learning low-dimensional time-dependent physics-based surrogate models whose predictions are endowed with uncertainty estimates. We use the operator inference approach to model reduction that poses the problem of learning low-dimensional model terms as a regression of state space data and corresponding time derivatives by minimizing the residual of reduced system equations. Standard operator inference models perform well with accurate training data that are dense in time, but producing stable and accurate models when the state data are noisy and/or sparse in time remains a challenge. Another challenge is the lack of uncertainty estimation for the predictions from the operator inference models. Our approach addresses these challenges by incorporating Gaussian process surrogates into the operator inference framework to (1) probabilistically describe uncertainties in the state predictions and (2) procure analytical time derivative estimates with quantified uncertainties. The formulation leads to a generalized least-squares regression and, ultimately, reduced-order models that are described probabilistically with a closed-form expression for the posterior distribution of the operators. The resulting probabilistic surrogate model propagates uncertainties from the observed state data to reduced-order predictions. We demonstrate the method is effective for constructing low-dimensional models of two nonlinear partial differential equations representing a compressible flow and a nonlinear diffusion-reaction process, as well as for estimating the parameters of a low-dimensional system of nonlinear ordinary differential equations representing compartmental models in epidemiology.



Keywords:
data-driven model reduction; uncertainty quantification; operator inference; Gaussian process; prediction uncertainty; parameter estimation; scientific machine learning

1
Introduction

Engineering design and analysis often involves many-query tasks, such as sensitivity analysis, uncertainty quantification, parameter estimation, and optimization, that require simulating a complex, expensive-to-simulate nonlinear system. To enable computationally efficient many-query simulations, we need data-driven surrogate models that can embed known physics to enable predictions outside of the training regime with respect to time and/or model inputs and build trust in such approximations by understanding the uncertainty in the predictions. This paper focuses on physics-based model reduction, the process of constructing low-dimensional systems as approximation models for high-dimensional processes, in the context of complex time-dependent dynamical systems. However, data-driven model reduction does not typically provide an assessment of uncertainty in the predictions. We develop an interpretable, physics-aware probabilistic surrogate that addresses the challenge of characterizing prediction uncertainty in data-driven reduced-order modeling for nonlinear dynamical systems with sparse and/or noisy data.

Model reduction strategies confine system dynamics to a low-dimensional linear subspace or nonlinear manifold and define evolution equations for the coordinates of the original state in this reduced space
[
6
,
54
]
. Classical reduced-order models (ROMs) are
intrusive
in the sense that a numerical solver for the system of interest, called the full-order model (FOM), is used explicitly in the construction of the ROM dynamics. In terms of implementation, intrusive ROMs are constructed by acting directly on code objects from the FOM. By contrast,
non-intrusive
or
data-driven
ROMs are constructed from system data alone, without needing access to any codes for solving the system of interest. This is a major advantage in scenarios without a FOM (e.g., experimental data), or in which the FOM can only be queried as a black box (e.g., proprietary, classified, or legacy codes). Furthermore, non-intrusive ROMs can learn from data that has been preprocessed with variable transformations or other advantageous alterations, whereas such preprocessing may severely complicate or even preclude intrusive ROM construction
[
52
,
66
]
. Although non-intrusive ROMs can be constructed in many scenarios where intrusive ROMs are unavailable, they usually lack the accuracy guarantees and/or uncertainty estimates that many intrusive ROMs enjoy, such as in
[
32
]
. Data-driven ROMs, especially those of low intrinsic dimensions, do not usually come with rigorous accuracy guarantees and must therefore be equipped with probabilistic estimates to characterize uncertainty in the predictions, especially in the presence of sparse and/or noisy data. We focus on operator inference (OpInf) as the underlying ROM methodology in our work and build a probabilistic ROM.

Operator inference is a regression-based method for non-intrusively constructing interpretable ROMs from state data
[
25
,
38
,
48
]
. A polynomial model form is chosen based on the governing dynamics (or a variable transformation of the dynamics
[
52
]
), thereby embedding the physics structure of the problem into the ROM. The regression requires state data and their time derivatives. These time derivatives may be provided by a FOM, but more often they must be estimated from the state data, typically via finite differences. In the presence of sparse and/or noisy state data, the time derivative estimation and the OpInf regression problem in general suffer from accuracy issues. Uncertainty due to noise in the states, inaccuracies in the time derivative estimates, or model form error can be partially accounted for by introducing a regularization to the regression
[
42
,
43
,
51
,
57
]
or by enforcing physics-motivated constraints
[
24
,
27
,
36
,
61
,
62
]
. Dealing with data noise remains a challenge, but some progress has been made in recent years. In
[
70
]
, the re-projection scheme of
[
47
]
is coupled with an active-learning paradigm for strategically querying a FOM with noisy states. The procedure partially filters the training data, produces a data-driven ROM that can be related to an intrusive ROM, and decreases the estimated bias in the predicted states by at least one order of magnitude.
In
[
69
]
, a nonconvex output-based optimization is used instead of a residual-based regression to learn a ROM with polynomial structure. The method is computationally expensive compared with traditional OpInf but was shown to perform well in several scenarios with sparse data and significant noise pollution. However, OpInf ROMs do not come with rigorous characterization of uncertainty in the predictions. Our previous work
[
29
]
interprets OpInf as a Bayesian linear regression and develops a framework for producing probabilistic OpInf ROMs. The method poses a Gaussian noise model for the derivative of the state but still relies on high-quality time derivative data. In this paper, we utilize Gaussian processes to pose a noise model for the state, not its time derivative, and simultaneously obtain time derivative data in an analytical fashion along with a Bayesian version of OpInf.

Gaussian process (GP) regression
[
56
]
is a powerful tool for function approximation from data. The nonparametric Bayesian nature of GPs facilitates uncertainty quantification in the predictions, while its kernel-method essence ensures robustness in dealing with noisy and/or scarce data. Notwithstanding, GPs suffer from poor performance in temporal predictions beyond training coverage due to a general lack of physical consistency, as GP surrogate modeling is conducted in a black-box manner and no information about the governing equations is incorporated. Several recent strategies aim to overcome this challenge by synthesizing GP regression and physics-based models
[
14
,
31
,
35
,
55
,
74
]
. In data-driven model reduction specifically, GP regression has been used to approximate the reduced trajectory over the time-parameter domain of interest
[
4
,
15
,
28
,
30
]
. In this work, we combine GPs with OpInf to develop physics-aware probabilistic surrogates.

We develop a GP-based Bayesian OpInf method (GP-BayesOpInf) for predictive modeling of nonlinear dynamical systems that produces probabilistic predictions. Specifically, GPs are used for the likelihood definition in a Bayesian scheme of OpInf, combining the advantage of GP emulation in smoothing and interpolating imperfect data, and the predictive power of OpInf guaranteed by the preservation of formulation structure of the governing equations in projection-based model reduction. Based on the fact that Gaussianity is preserved through linear operations, differential-equation constraints of reduced states are embedded in the likelihood function. This does not require direct time-derivative approximations as in a Bayesian linear regression for equation fitting
[
29
]
and thus avoids generating low-quality derivative data when training trajectories are noisy and/or scarce. Thereafter, the resulting posterior estimate of the reduced operators can be formulated as a generalized least squares, in which an improved time derivative approximation is enabled by kernel regression and endowed with quantifiable uncertainty. We derive a closed form for the posterior distribution of the operators in OpInf, thus providing an interpretable, low-dimensional probabilistic surrogate model. The resulting physics-aware probabilistic surrogate can then sample realizations of operators from this posterior and integrate the low-dimensional dynamical system for each realization to provide predictions of the states of the dynamical system along with uncertainty in the predictions stemming from training data and modeling process.

2
Reduced-order models via operator inference

Consider a high-dimensional system of ODEs,

\displaystyle\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}t}{\mathbf{q}}(t)={\mathbf{f}}({\mathbf{q}}(t),{\mathbf{u}}(t))\,,\quad\mathbf{q}(t_{0})=\mathbf{q}_{0}\,,\quad t\in[t_{0},t_{f}]\,,

(2.1)

where
\mathbf{q}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
is the state of the system at time
t
,

\mathbf{u}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
represents inputs at time
t
(e.g., time-dependent source terms and/or boundary conditions),

\mathbf{f}:\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}\to\mathbb{R}^{N}
specifies the evolutionary system dynamics,

t_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
is the initial time,

\mathbf{q}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
is the initial condition,
and
t_{f}>t_{0}
is the final time for which we want to know the state
\mathbf{q}(t)
.
High-dimensional ODE systems with large
N
arise often from the spatial discretization of PDEs.
A ROM of
eq.

2.1
is a system of ODEs with substantially fewer degrees of freedom whose solution—computed much more efficiently than directly solving
eq.

2.1
—can be used to approximate the solution to
eq.

2.1
.
The cost reduction resulting from using a ROM is crucial for applications requiring multiple solves of
eq.

2.1
, e.g., with different input functions or initial conditions.

Projection-based ROMs introduce an affine approximation for the high-dimensional state,

\displaystyle\mathbf{q}(t)\approx\mathbf{V}\hat{\mathbf{q}}(t)+\bar{\mathbf{q}},

(2.2)

where
\mathbf{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times r}
is the basis matrix,
\hat{\mathbf{q}}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
is the reduced state at time
t
,
\bar{\mathbf{q}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
is
a fixed reference vector, and
r\ll N
is the dimension of the reduced state.
Both
\mathbf{V}
and
\bar{\mathbf{q}}
, and hence the reduced state dimension
r
, are either inferred from training observations of the state
\mathbf{q}(t)
or chosen based on problem-specific knowledge (e.g.,
\bar{\mathbf{q}}
may encode constant boundary conditions or represent a time-averaged solution).
We typically require
\mathbf{V}
to have orthonormal columns, in which case dynamics for
\hat{\mathbf{q}}(t)
are derived by substituting
eq.

2.2
into
eq.

2.1
and multiplying both sides by
\mathbf{V}^{\mathsf{T}}
, resulting in the system

\displaystyle\frac{\textrm{d}}{\textrm{d}t}\hat{\mathbf{q}}(t)=\hat{\mathbf{f}}(\hat{\mathbf{q}}(t),\mathbf{u}(t)):=\mathbf{V}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{V}\hat{\mathbf{q}}(t)+\bar{\mathbf{q}},\mathbf{u}(t))\,,\quad\hat{\mathbf{q}}(t_{0})=\mathbf{V}^{\mathsf{T}}(\mathbf{q}_{0}-\bar{\mathbf{q}})\,,\quad t\in[t_{0},t_{f}]\,,

(2.3)

where
\hat{\mathbf{f}}:\mathbb{R}^{r}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}\to\mathbb{R}^{r}
.
For a wide class of problems, this approach preserves the dynamical structure of
eq.

2.1
in the ROM
eq.

2.3

[
6
]
.
In particular, if
\mathbf{f}
has a polynomial form, then so does
\hat{\mathbf{f}}
:
for example, defining
\mathbf{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
,

\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
,

\mathbf{H}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N^{2}}
, and

\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times p}
,
writing

\displaystyle\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}t}\mathbf{q}(t)=\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{q}(t),\mathbf{u}(t))=\mathbf{c}+\mathbf{A}\mathbf{q}(t)+\mathbf{H}[\mathbf{q}(t)\otimes\mathbf{q}(t)]+\mathbf{B}\mathbf{u}(t),

(2.4a)

there exist corresponding
\hat{\mathbf{c}}\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
,

\hat{\mathbf{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r}
,

\hat{\mathbf{H}}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r^{2}}
, and

\hat{\mathbf{B}}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times p}

such that

\displaystyle\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}t}\hat{\mathbf{q}}(t)=\hat{\mathbf{f}}(\hat{\mathbf{q}}(t),\mathbf{u}(t))=\hat{\mathbf{c}}+\hat{\mathbf{A}}\hat{\mathbf{q}}(t)+\hat{\mathbf{H}}[\hat{\mathbf{q}}(t)\otimes\hat{\mathbf{q}}(t)]+\hat{\mathbf{B}}\mathbf{u}(t).

(2.4b)

In this work, we consider the scenario in which reduced-order operators such as
\hat{\mathbf{c}}
,
\hat{\mathbf{A}}
,
\hat{\mathbf{H}}
, and
\hat{\mathbf{B}}
cannot be computed explicitly, as occurs when working with large, proprietary, or classified code bases, or in systems that must be transformed through a change of variables to first expose a polynomial structure similar to
eq.

2.4a

[
52
]
.
We will see examples of the latter in
Section

5
.
In these cases, the polynomial structure of
\mathbf{f}
is known, but
\mathbf{f}
itself is computationally inaccessible and cannot be used directly to form
\hat{\mathbf{f}}
.

Operator inference (OpInf) is a non-intrusive projection-based method for learning polynomial ROMs by minimizing the model residual with respect to a collection of training data
[
38
,
48
]
.
In general, OpInf constructs ROMs that can be written as

\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}t}\hat{\mathbf{q}}(t)=\hat{\mathbf{O}}\mathbf{d}(\hat{\mathbf{q}}(t),\mathbf{u}(t))\,,\quad\hat{\mathbf{q}}(t_{0})=\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{0}\,,\quad t\in[t_{0},t_{f}]\,,

(2.5)

where
\hat{\mathbf{O}}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times d(r,p)}
is the operator matrix,

\mathbf{d}:\mathbb{R}^{r}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}\to\mathbb{R}^{d(r,p)}
is a known (typically nonlinear) function of the reduced state and input,
and
d(r,p)\in\mathbb{N}
is the operator dimension.
The system
eq.

2.5
is linear with respect to the operator matrix
\hat{\mathbf{O}}
but is usually nonlinear in the reduced state
\hat{\mathbf{q}}(t)
and/or the input
\mathbf{u}(t)
due to
\mathbf{d}(\hat{\mathbf{q}}(t),\mathbf{u}(t))
.
For example, the quadratic system
eq.

2.4b
can be written as
eq.

2.5
with

d(r,p)=1+r+r^{2}+p
and

\hat{\mathbf{O}}=[~\hat{\mathbf{c}}~~\hat{\mathbf{A}}~~\hat{\mathbf{H}}~~\hat{\mathbf{B}}~]\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times d(r,p)},\qquad\mathbf{d}(\hat{\mathbf{q}}(t),\mathbf{u}(t))=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}1\\
\hat{\mathbf{q}}(t)\\
\hat{\mathbf{q}}(t)\otimes\hat{\mathbf{q}}(t)\\
\mathbf{u}(t)\end{array}\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{d(r,p)}.

(2.6)

There are several ways to approach problems whose nonpolynomial nonlinear structure does not directly motivate the reduced structure
eq.

2.5
. Many problems can be posed in polynomial form after a change of variables
[
52
]
, a strategy that has found application in several model reduction contexts (see, e.g.,
[
39
]
for an example of variable transformation enabling balanced truncation ROMs). OpInf models have also been demonstrated to be effective in some scenarios where the true dynamics can only be partially represented through a polynomial form
[
21
,
42
,
51
,
66
]
. Additionally, a large class of nonpolynomial nonlinear terms may be incorporated via discrete empirical interpolation
[
5
]
.

Given state-input pairs
\{(\mathbf{q}_{j},\mathbf{u}_{j})\}_{j=1}^{m-1}
describing the solution of (2.1) at
m
discrete time instances
t_{0},\ldots,t_{m-1}
, a reference vector
\bar{\mathbf{q}}
, and a basis matrix
\mathbf{V}
, OpInf learns an operator matrix
\hat{\mathbf{O}}
by compressing the observed states as
\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{j}=\mathbf{V}^{\mathsf{T}}(\mathbf{q}_{j}-\bar{\mathbf{q}})\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
, then solving the regression

\displaystyle\min_{\hat{\mathbf{O}}}\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\left\|\hat{\mathbf{O}}\mathbf{d}(\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{j},\mathbf{u}_{j})-\dot{\hat{\mathbf{q}}}_{j}\right\|_{2}^{2}=\min_{\hat{\mathbf{O}}}\left\|\hat{\mathbf{O}}\mathbf{D}^{\mathsf{T}}-\dot{\hat{\mathbf{Q}}}\right\|_{F}^{2}=\min_{\hat{\mathbf{O}}}\left\|\mathbf{D}\hat{\mathbf{O}}^{\mathsf{T}}-\dot{\hat{\mathbf{Q}}}^{\mathsf{T}}\right\|_{F}^{2}\,,

(2.7a)

\displaystyle\dot{\hat{\mathbf{Q}}}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cccc}&&&\\
\dot{\hat{\mathbf{q}}}_{0}&\dot{\hat{\mathbf{q}}}_{1}&\cdots&\dot{\hat{\mathbf{q}}}_{m-1}\\
&&&\end{array}\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times m}\,,\qquad\mathbf{D}=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{d}(\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{0},\mathbf{u}_{0})^{\mathsf{T}}\\
\mathbf{d}(\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{1},\mathbf{u}_{1})^{\mathsf{T}}\\
\vdots\\
\mathbf{d}(\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{m-1},\mathbf{u}_{m-1})^{\mathsf{T}}\\
\end{array}\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d(r,p)}\,.

Here,
\dot{\hat{\mathbf{q}}}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
is an estimate of the time derivative
\frac{\textrm{d}}{\textrm{d}t}\hat{\mathbf{q}}(t)=\mathbf{V}^{\mathsf{T}}\frac{\textrm{d}}{\textrm{d}t}{\mathbf{q}}(t)
at time
t_{j}
, usually computed from finite differences of the reduced snapshots
\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{0},\ldots,\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{m-1}
, and
the matrix
\mathbf{D}
is called the data matrix.
Because
\mathbf{V}
,
\bar{\mathbf{q}}
, and the observations
\{(\mathbf{q}_{j},\mathbf{u}_{j})\}
are known, the only unknowns in
eq.

2.7
are the entries of the operator matrix
\hat{\mathbf{O}}
.
Unless additional constraints on the unknown
\hat{\mathbf{O}}
are imposed,
eq.

2.7
is a linear least-squares problem since
eq.

2.5
is linear with respect to
\hat{\mathbf{O}}
.

As a regression for
\hat{\mathbf{O}}
,
eq.

2.7a
decouples into
r
independent regressions,

\displaystyle\min_{\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i}}\left\|\mathbf{D}\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i}-\mathbf{z}_{i}\right\|_{2}^{2}\,,

(2.8)

where
\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d(r,p)}
is the
i
-th row of
\hat{\mathbf{O}}
and
\mathbf{z}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
is the
i
-th row of
\dot{\hat{\mathbf{Q}}}
.
It is often important to regularize the regression
eq.

2.8
in order for the resulting
\hat{\mathbf{O}}
to produce a stable ROM
[
42
,
57
]
.
The most common way to do this is with a Tikhonov regularizer, i.e.,

\displaystyle\min_{\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i}}\left\{\left\|\mathbf{D}\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i}-\mathbf{z}_{i}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\left\|{\bf\it\Gamma}_{i}\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right\}\,,

(2.9)

for some
{\bf\it\Gamma}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d(r,p)\times d(r,p)}
.
Alternatively, some scenarios call for constraints to be added to the regression, for example when preserving Hamiltonian or Lagrangian dynamics
[
27
,
61
,
62
]
.

3
Bayesian operator inference with Gaussian processes

In deterministic OpInf
eq.

2.8
, the training data are constructed from whatever (reduced) snapshot data are available, be they uniform or sparse in time, smooth or corrupted by observational noise.
Here, we perform GP regression using the snapshot data and use the updated process to produce time derivative estimates and, ultimately, a probabilistic description for the reduced-order operator matrix
\hat{\mathbf{O}}
.
The overall strategy is abbreviated as GP-BayesOpInf.
See
Appendix
A
for a brief overview of GPs.

3.1
Bayesian inference

In this section, we formulate a linear Bayesian inference for the operator matrix
\hat{\mathbf{O}}
by separately considering each of its rows
\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{1},\ldots,\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{r}
, following the OpInf paradigm of learning each
\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i}
individually.

3.1.1
Likelihood and prior

We begin by modeling the reduced states
\hat{q}_{1}(t),\ldots,\hat{q}_{r}(t)
with independent GPs:

\hat{q}_{i}\sim\mathcal{GP}(0,\kappa_{i}(\cdot,\cdot))\,,\qquad i=1,\ldots,r,

(3.1)

in which the mean function is assumed to be zero and
\kappa_{i}:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}

is a kernel function specifying the covariance over the GP.
If the kernel
\kappa_{i}
is at least once differentiable in each argument, then the time-derivative of each
\hat{q}_{i}
also follows a GP, and we have the joint processes
[
55
,
56
]

\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\hat{q}_{i}\\
\dot{\hat{q}}_{i}\end{array}\right]\sim\text{vec-}\mathcal{GP}\left(\left[\begin{array}[]{c}0\\
0\end{array}\right],\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\kappa_{i}(\cdot,\cdot)&\partial_{2}\kappa_{i}(\cdot,\cdot)\\
\partial_{1}\kappa_{i}(\cdot,\cdot)&\partial_{1}\partial_{2}\kappa_{i}(\cdot,\cdot)\end{array}\right]\right)\,,\quad

(3.2)

in which
\partial_{1}
and
\partial_{2}
respectively denote the partial derivatives with respect to the first and the second arguments.

Let
{\bf\it t}=(t_{0},\ldots,t_{m-1})^{\mathsf{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
denote the
m\in\mathbb{N}
time instances for which we have (reduced) snapshot data
\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{0},\ldots,\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{m-1}\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
.
Define
{\bf\it Y}_{i}=\hat{q}_{i}({\bf\it t})
to be the random
m
-vector whose entries are the Gaussian random variables produced by evaluating the GP
\hat{q}_{i}
at each time instance in
{\bf\it t}
, and let
\mathbf{y}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
denote the snapshot data for the
i
-th reduced state
\hat{q}_{i}
at times
{\bf\it t}
, that is,

\displaystyle\left[\begin{array}[]{cccc}&&&\\
\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{0}&\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{1}&\cdots&\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{m-1}\\
&&&\end{array}\right]

\displaystyle=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{y}_{1}^{\mathsf{T}}\\
\vdots\\
\mathbf{y}_{r}^{\mathsf{T}}\end{array}\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times m}.

Note that
\mathbf{y}_{i}
is an observation of
{\bf\it Y}_{i}
.
Likewise, let
{\bf\it t}^{\prime}=(t_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,t_{m^{\prime}-1}^{\prime})^{\mathsf{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m^{\prime}}
be the
m^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}
time instances (typically with
m^{\prime}\geq m
and
[t_{0}^{\prime},t_{m^{\prime}-1}^{\prime}]\subset[t_{0},t_{m-1}]
) where the GP is to produce estimates, and define
{\bf\it Z}_{i}=\dot{\hat{q}}_{i}({\bf\it t}^{\prime})
to be the random
m^{\prime}
-vector with entries given by the time derivative GP
\dot{\hat{q}}_{i}
at times
{\bf\it t}^{\prime}
.

The entries of the observational data vector
\mathbf{y}_{i}
are noisy, which we model by an additive white noise term with Gaussian distribution
\mathcal{N}(0,\chi_{i})
.
With this setting, the joint distribution of
{\bf\it Y}_{i}
and
{\bf\it Z}_{i}
is deduced from
eq.

3.2
to be

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}{\bf\it Y}_{i}\\
{\bf\it Z}_{i}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{K}^{yy}_{i}&\mathbf{K}^{yz}_{i}\\
\mathbf{K}^{zy}_{i}&\mathbf{K}^{zz}_{i}\end{bmatrix}\right)\,,

with

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathbf{K}_{i}^{yy}&=\kappa_{i}({\bf\it t},{\bf\it t})+\chi_{i}\mathbf{I}_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}\,,\\
\mathbf{K}_{i}^{zy}&=\partial_{1}\kappa_{i}({\bf\it t}^{\prime},{\bf\it t})=(\mathbf{K}_{i}^{yz})^{\mathsf{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m^{\prime}\times m}\,,\\
\mathbf{K}_{i}^{zz}&=\partial_{1}\partial_{2}\kappa_{i}({\bf\it t}^{\prime},{\bf\it t}^{\prime})\in\mathbb{R}^{m^{\prime}\times m^{\prime}}\,,\end{aligned}

(3.9)

where
\mathbf{I}_{m}
is the
m\times m
identity matrix
and, for two vectors
{\bf\it a}=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n_{a}})^{\mathsf{T}}
and
{\bf\it b}=(b_{1},\ldots,b_{n_{b}})^{\mathsf{T}}
, the shorthand
\kappa({\bf\it a},{\bf\it b})
denotes the
n_{a}\times n_{b}
matrix whose
(i,j)
-th entry is
\kappa(a_{i},b_{j})
.

Remark 3.1

(Choosing
m^{\prime}
)
.

The integer
m^{\prime}
must be selected
a priori
by the practitioner. If
m^{\prime}
is too large, fitting and using each GP becomes unnecessarily expensive; if
m^{\prime}
is too small, the subsequent Bayesian inference may have insufficient data and produce unsatisfactory results.
For the numerical examples in
Section

5
and
Section

6
, we base
m^{\prime}
on the interval length
t_{m-1}-t_{0}
and uniformly space the entries of
{\bf\it t}^{\prime}
in the interval
[t_{0},t_{m-1}]
, i.e.,
t_{0}=t^{\prime}_{0}<t^{\prime}_{1}<\ldots<t^{\prime}_{m^{\prime}-1}=t_{m-1}
with
t^{\prime}_{j+1}=t^{\prime}_{j}+(t_{m-1}-t_{0})/(m^{\prime}-1)
.

For a given reduced-order operator vector
\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d(r,p)}
and a data matrix
\tilde{\mathbf{D}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m^{\prime}\times d(r,p)}
corresponding to the estimation times
{\bf\it t}^{\prime}
, let
{\bf\it R}_{i}:={\bf\it Z}_{i}-\tilde{\mathbf{D}}\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i}
be the random
m^{\prime}
-vector describing the residual for the
i
-th reduced-order system equation
eq.

2.5
at times
{\bf\it t}^{\prime}
.
We may now define a likelihood function, not only on the direct state observations
{\bf\it Y}_{i}=\mathbf{y}_{i}
, but also on the alignment of the time derivatives
{\bf\it Z}_{i}
with
\tilde{\mathbf{D}}\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i}
, i.e., the satisfaction of residuals
{\bf\it R}_{i}=\mathbf{0}
:

\pi_{\texttt{like}}(\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i}):=p({\bf\it Y}_{i}=\mathbf{y}_{i},{\bf\it R}_{i}=\mathbf{0}\mid\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i},\tilde{\mathbf{D}},{\bf\it\theta}_{i})\propto\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{y}_{i}\\
\tilde{\mathbf{D}}\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i}\end{bmatrix}^{\mathsf{T}}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{K}^{yy}_{i}&\mathbf{K}^{yz}_{i}\\
\mathbf{K}^{zy}_{i}&\mathbf{K}^{zz}_{i}\end{bmatrix}^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{y}_{i}\\
\tilde{\mathbf{D}}\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i}\end{bmatrix}\right)\,,

(3.10)

which is derived by substituting
{\bf\it Z}_{i}=\tilde{\mathbf{D}}\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i}
(i.e.,
{\bf\it R}_{i}=\mathbf{0}
) into
eq.

3.9
.
Here,
{\bf\it\theta}_{i}
consists of the noise variance
\chi_{i}
and the hyperparameters of the kernel
\kappa_{i}
.
The likelihood depends on
{\bf\it\theta}_{i}
through the definition of the matrices
\mathbf{K}_{i}^{yy}
,
\mathbf{K}_{i}^{yz}
,
\mathbf{K}_{i}^{zy}
, and
\mathbf{K}_{i}^{zz}

in
eq.

3.9
.

Remark 3.2

(Interpretation of the likelihood)
.

In the likelihood definition (
3.10
), the direct observations
{\bf\it Y}_{i}=\mathbf{y}_{i}
introduce the factor of GP regression where noisy and scarce data can be smoothed and interpolated, while
{\bf\it R}_{i}=\mathbf{0}
incorporates the constraints of differential equations into the inference process, explicitly ensuring physical consistency in the resulting ROMs.

Remark 3.3

(Gaussianity preserved by affine reconstruction)
.

The independent reduced state GP representations
\hat{q}_{1},\ldots,\hat{q}_{r}
of
eq.

3.1
can be expressed together as a single vector-valued GP,

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{q}}(t)\sim\text{vec-}\mathcal{GP}(\mathbf{0},\text{diag}(\kappa_{1}(t,t^{\prime}),\ldots,\kappa_{r}(t,t^{\prime})))\,.

(3.11a)

If these reduced states are used in the approximation
eq.

2.2
, the reconstruction of full-order states is given by

\displaystyle\mathbf{q}(t)\approx\mathbf{V}\hat{\mathbf{q}}(t)+\bar{\mathbf{q}}\sim\text{vec-}\mathcal{GP}(\bar{\mathbf{q}},\mathbf{V}\text{diag}(\kappa_{1}(t,t^{\prime}),\ldots,\kappa_{r}(t,t^{\prime}))\mathbf{V}^{\mathsf{T}})\,,

(3.11b)

in which the covariance
\mathbf{V}\text{diag}(\kappa_{1}(t,t^{\prime}),\ldots,\kappa_{r}(t,t^{\prime}))\mathbf{V}^{\mathsf{T}}
is a low-rank approximation of
\operatorname{Cov}[\mathbf{q}(t),\mathbf{q}(t^{\prime})]
.
Hence, the GP-based modeling for the reduced states
provides a quantification of uncertainties in the states on the full-order level as well.

We also define the prior on the reduced-order operators
\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i}
to be independent normal distributions, i.e.,

\pi_{\texttt{prior}}(\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i}):=p(\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i}\mid{\bf\it\gamma}_{i})\propto\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\|{\bf\it\Gamma}_{i}\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}\right)\,,\quad\text{with}\quad{\bf\it\Gamma}_{i}:=\text{diag}({\bf\it\gamma}_{i})\,,

(3.12)

where the entries of
{\bf\it\gamma}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d(r,p)}
are nonnegative and are considered hyperparameters.

3.1.2
Posterior

Combining
eq.

3.10
–
eq.

3.12
, Bayes’ rule gives the posterior distribution

\pi_{\texttt{post}}(\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i}):=p(\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i}\mid{\bf\it Y}_{i}=\mathbf{y}_{i},{\bf\it R}_{i}=\mathbf{0},\tilde{\mathbf{D}},{\bf\it\theta}_{i},{\bf\it\gamma}_{i})\propto\pi_{\texttt{like}}(\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i})\,\pi_{\texttt{prior}}(\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i})\,.

(3.13)

From Schur’s formula for block matrix inversion
[
1
]
, one has

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{K}^{yy}_{i}&\mathbf{K}^{yz}_{i}\\
\mathbf{K}^{zy}_{i}&\mathbf{K}^{zz}_{i}\end{bmatrix}^{-1}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{W}^{yy}_{i}&\mathbf{W}^{yz}_{i}\\
\mathbf{W}^{zy}_{i}&\mathbf{W}^{zz}_{i}\end{bmatrix}\,,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathbf{W}^{yy}_{i}&=\left(\mathbf{K}^{yy}_{i}-\mathbf{K}^{yz}_{i}(\mathbf{K}^{zz}_{i})^{-1}\mathbf{K}^{zy}_{i}\right)^{-1}\,,\\
\mathbf{W}^{zy}_{i}&=-\mathbf{K}^{zz}_{i}\mathbf{K}^{zy}_{i}(\mathbf{K}^{yy}_{i})^{-1}=(\mathbf{W}^{yz}_{i})^{\mathsf{T}}\,,\\
\mathbf{W}^{zz}_{i}&=\left(\mathbf{K}^{zz}_{i}-\mathbf{K}^{zy}_{i}(\mathbf{K}^{yy}_{i})^{-1}\mathbf{K}^{yz}_{i}\right)^{-1}\,.\end{aligned}

(3.14)

Therefore,

\begin{split}\pi_{\texttt{post}}(\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i})&\propto\pi_{\texttt{like}}(\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i})\,\pi_{\texttt{prior}}(\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i})\\
&\propto\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathbf{y}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{W}^{yy}_{i}\mathbf{y}_{i}+2\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}\tilde{\mathbf{D}}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{W}_{i}^{zy}\mathbf{y}_{i}+\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}\tilde{\mathbf{D}}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{W}_{i}^{zz}\tilde{\mathbf{D}}\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i}+\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}{\bf\it\Gamma}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}{\bf\it\Gamma}_{i}\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i}\right)\right)\\
&\propto\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\left(\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i}-{\bf\it\mu}_{i}\right)^{\mathsf{T}}{\bf\it\Sigma}_{i}^{-1}\left(\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i}-{\bf\it\mu}_{i}\right)\right)\,,\\
\text{i.e.,}\quad\pi_{\texttt{post}}(\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i})&=\mathcal{N}\left(\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i}\mid{\bf\it\mu}_{i},{\bf\it\Sigma}_{i}\right)\,.\end{split}

(3.15)

Here, the posterior mean of
\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i}
coincides with a
maximum a posteriori
(MAP) estimator, written as

\begin{split}{\bf\it\mu}_{i}=\left(\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i}\right)_{\text{MAP}}&=-(\tilde{\mathbf{D}}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{W}_{i}^{zz}\tilde{\mathbf{D}}+{\bf\it\Gamma}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}{\bf\it\Gamma}_{i})^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{D}}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{W}_{i}^{zy}\mathbf{y}_{i}\\
&=(\tilde{\mathbf{D}}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{W}_{i}^{zz}\tilde{\mathbf{D}}+{\bf\it\Gamma}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}{\bf\it\Gamma}_{i})^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{D}}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{W}^{zz}_{i}\mathbf{K}^{zy}_{i}(\mathbf{K}^{yy}_{i})^{-1}\mathbf{y}_{i}\,,\end{split}

(3.16)

and the posterior covariance matrix of
\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i}
is

{\bf\it\Sigma}_{i}=\left(\tilde{\mathbf{D}}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{W}_{i}^{zz}\tilde{\mathbf{D}}+{\bf\it\Gamma}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}{\bf\it\Gamma}_{i}\right)^{-1}\,.

(3.17)

Note that

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{i}:=\mathbf{K}^{zy}_{i}(\mathbf{K}^{yy}_{i})^{-1}\mathbf{y}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{m^{\prime}}

(3.18)

describes the GP estimate of the time derivative
\frac{\textrm{d}}{\textrm{d}t}\hat{q}_{i}
evaluated over
{\bf\it t}^{\prime}
(see
eq.

4.13
).
Thus,
eq.

3.16
can be abbreviated as

{\bf\it\mu}_{i}={\bf\it\Sigma}_{i}\tilde{\mathbf{D}}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{W}^{zz}_{i}\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{i}\,.

3.1.3
Least squares formulation

The posterior mean
{\bf\it\mu}_{i}
can be interpreted as a Tikhonov-regularized, generalized least-squares estimator of
\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i}
with the weight matrix

\mathbf{W}_{i}^{zz},

whose inverse is the posterior covariance matrix of

\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{i}

through the GP regression of
({\bf\it t},\mathbf{y}_{i})
.
Specifically,
{\bf\it\mu}_{i}
solves the problem

\displaystyle\min_{{\bf\it\eta}}\left\{\left\|\tilde{\mathbf{D}}{\bf\it\eta}-\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{i}\right\|_{\mathbf{W}_{i}^{zz}}^{2}+\left\|{\bf\it\Gamma}_{i}{\bf\it\eta}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right\},

(3.19)

where
\left\|\bullet\right\|_{\mathbf{W}_{i}^{zz}}=(\bullet^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{W}_{i}^{zz}\bullet)^{1/2}
.
Furthermore, an equivalent
2
-norm least-squares problem is given by

\displaystyle\min_{{\bf\it\eta}}

\displaystyle\left\|\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\left(\mathbf{W}_{i}^{zz}\right)^{1/2}\tilde{\mathbf{D}}\\
{\bf\it\Gamma}_{i}\end{array}\right]{\bf\it\eta}-\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\left(\mathbf{W}_{i}^{zz}\right)^{1/2}\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{i}\\
\mathbf{0}\end{array}\right]\right\|_{2}^{2}\,.

We use this least-squares formulation in the computation of
{\bf\it\mu}_{i}
as it is more stable than solving the generalized normal equations in
eq.

3.16
. This requires computing
(\mathbf{W}_{i}^{zz})^{1/2}
, which can be obtained by taking an eigendecomposition

\displaystyle(\mathbf{W}_{i}^{zz})^{-1}=\mathbf{K}^{zz}_{i}-\mathbf{K}^{zy}_{i}(\mathbf{K}^{yy}_{i})^{-1}\mathbf{K}^{yz}_{i}={\bf\it\Psi}_{i}{\bf\it\Xi}_{i}{\bf\it\Psi}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}\,,

(3.24)

then setting
(\mathbf{W}_{i}^{zz})^{1/2}={\bf\it\Psi}_{i}{\bf\it\Xi}_{i}^{-1/2}{\bf\it\Psi}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}
.

Remark 3.4

(Regularization of the covariance computation)
.

In practice, computing
eq.

3.24
directly may be unstable due to the round-off errors introduced from solving for
\mathbf{C}_{i}:=\mathbf{K}^{zy}_{i}(\mathbf{K}^{yy}_{i})^{-1}\mathbf{K}^{yz}_{i}
. As a remedy, we symmetrize
\mathbf{C}_{i}
and add a small amount of numerical regularization, replacing
eq.

3.24
with

\displaystyle(\mathbf{W}_{i}^{zz})^{-1}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{K}^{zz}_{i}-\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathbf{C}_{i}+\mathbf{C}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}\right)+\tau\mathbf{I}_{m^{\prime}}={\bf\it\Psi}_{i}{\bf\it\Xi}_{i}{\bf\it\Psi}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}},

(3.25)

where
0<\tau\ll 1
.
The constant
\tau
should be just large enough that the eigendecomposition of the above matrix yields all positive eigenvalues.
The numerical examples in
Section

5
and
Section

6
use
\tau=10^{-8}
.

3.2
Probabilistic reduced-order model predictions

Now that the reduced operator vectors
\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i}
are estimated with probability distributions
\pi_{\texttt{post}}(\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i})
,
1\leq i\leq r
, they may be used to produce predictions over time through the ROM
eq.

2.5
.
In fact, the posterior distribution for the entire reduced operator matrix
\hat{\mathbf{O}}
can be written as

\begin{split}\pi_{\texttt{post}}(\hat{\mathbf{O}})&=p\left(\hat{\mathbf{O}}~\big|~\left\{{\bf\it Y}_{i}=\mathbf{y}_{i},{\bf\it R}_{i}=\mathbf{0}\right\}_{i=1}^{r},\tilde{\mathbf{D}},\left\{{\bf\it\theta}_{i},{\bf\it\gamma}_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{r}\right)\\
&\propto p\left(\left\{{\bf\it Y}_{i}=\mathbf{y}_{i},{\bf\it R}_{i}=\mathbf{0}\right\}_{i=1}^{r}~\big|~\hat{\mathbf{O}},\tilde{\mathbf{D}},\left\{{\bf\it\theta}_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{r}\right)p\left(\hat{\mathbf{O}}~\big|~\left\{{\bf\it\gamma}_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{r}\right)\\
&=\prod_{i=1}^{r}p\left({\bf\it Y}_{i}=\mathbf{y}_{i},{\bf\it R}_{i}=\mathbf{0}~\big|~\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i},\tilde{\mathbf{D}},{\bf\it\theta}_{i}\right)\prod_{i=1}^{r}p\left(\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i}\mid{\bf\it\gamma}_{i}\right)=\prod_{i=1}^{r}\pi_{\texttt{like}}(\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i})\pi_{\texttt{prior}}(\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i})\,.\end{split}

(3.26a)

The likelihood separates into a product because (1) the OpInf regression
eq.

2.7a
–
eq.

2.7
decouples the inference of the operator matrix
\hat{\mathbf{O}}
into its rows
\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{1},\ldots,\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{r}
,
and (2) we model the reduced modes independently with individual one-dimensional GPs
eq.

3.1
.
The separation of the prior is a result of the independence in the prior definition (
3.12
). Hence the posterior of
\hat{\mathbf{O}}
can be treated row-by-row independently, i.e.,

\pi_{\texttt{post}}(\hat{\mathbf{O}})=\prod_{i=1}^{r}\pi_{\texttt{post}}(\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i})\,,

(3.26b)

and
\hat{\mathbf{O}}
is sampled from the product of the posterior distributions
\pi_{\texttt{post}}(\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{1}),\ldots,\pi_{\texttt{post}}(\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{r})
.

For a given sample of
\hat{\mathbf{O}}
, we evaluate the reduced state
\hat{\mathbf{q}}(t)
by solving the corresponding ROM
eq.

2.5
. The reduced state
\hat{\mathbf{q}}(t)
therefore has the following distribution:

\begin{split}p\left(\hat{\mathbf{q}}(t)~\big|~\left\{{\bf\it Y}_{i}=\mathbf{y}_{i},{\bf\it R}_{i}=\mathbf{0}\right\}_{i=1}^{r},\tilde{\mathbf{D}},\left\{{\bf\it\theta}_{i},{\bf\it\gamma}_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{r}\right)=~&\int p(\hat{\mathbf{q}}(t)\mid\hat{\mathbf{O}})\,\pi_{\texttt{post}}(\hat{\mathbf{O}})~\differential\hat{\mathbf{O}}\\
=~&\int p(\hat{\mathbf{q}}(t)\mid\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{1},\ldots,\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{r})\prod_{i=1}^{r}\pi_{\texttt{post}}(\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i})~\differential\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i}\,,\end{split}

(3.27)

in which all the
\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i}
’s are marginalized.
Here, the notation
p(\hat{\mathbf{q}}(t)|\,\hat{\mathbf{O}})
indicates a deterministic model evaluation for a given instance of
\hat{\mathbf{O}}
; indeed, one can write the reduced state
\hat{\mathbf{q}}(t)
as a function of
\hat{\mathbf{O}}
, i.e.,
\hat{\mathbf{q}}(t;\hat{\mathbf{O}})
.
Note, however, that the distribution of
\hat{\mathbf{q}}
is not necessarily Gaussian, even though the posterior of
\hat{\mathbf{O}}
is, primarily because
\hat{\mathbf{q}}(t;\hat{\mathbf{O}})
is nonlinear with respect to
\hat{\mathbf{O}}
.
Having constructed a posterior distribution for
\hat{\mathbf{O}}
, we use Monte Carlo sampling over the operator posterior to estimate the moments of
\hat{\mathbf{q}}(t)
.
Specifically, an individual sample
\hat{\mathbf{O}}_{k}
defines an instance of the (deterministic) ROM
eq.

2.5
,

\displaystyle\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}t}\hat{\mathbf{q}}(t)=\hat{\mathbf{O}}_{k}\mathbf{d}(\hat{\mathbf{q}}(t),\mathbf{u}(t))\,,\quad\hat{\mathbf{q}}(t_{0})=\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{0}\,,\quad t\in[t_{0},t_{f}]\,,

which is solved numerically for
\hat{\mathbf{q}}(t)
over
[t_{0},t_{f}]
using standard time-stepping integration methods.
Each numerical solve is computationally inexpensive due to the low dimensionality of the system.
After repeating this procedure for
k=1,\ldots,K
, the collection of
K
trajectories
is used to estimate the mean and higher-order moments of the reduced state at time
t
, thereby establishing an uncertainty indicator for the model predictions.

3.3
Multiple trajectories

In many situations we have data for
\ell>1
trajectories of a single system due to variable initial conditions or input functions (but with the same set of system parameters, such as material properties).
In this scenario, observed states from each of the
\ell
trajectories are used together to construct a global basis of rank
r
, then
r
GPs—one for each reduced mode, as before—are trained for each trajectory.
The observation times
{\bf\it t}
may vary by trajectory, but we use the same estimation times
{\bf\it t}^{\prime}
for each trajectory and mode.

The GP estimates for the
i
-th mode from each trajectory are used jointly to learn a posterior for the operator matrix row
\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i}
.
Using a superscript
(l)
to represent the
l
-th trajectory over
1\leq l\leq\ell
,
for each
i=1,\ldots,r
we are concerned with the posterior

\begin{split}\pi_{\texttt{post}}(\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i})=p\left(\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i}~\Big|~\left\{{\bf\it Y}_{i}^{(l)}=\mathbf{y}_{i}^{(l)},{\bf\it R}_{i}^{(l)}=\mathbf{0},~\tilde{\mathbf{D}}^{(l)},{\bf\it\theta}_{i}^{(l)}\right\}_{l=1}^{\ell},{\bf\it\gamma}_{i}\right)\propto\pi_{\texttt{prior}}(\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i})\prod_{l=1}^{\ell}\pi_{\texttt{like}}^{(l)}(\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i})\,,&\\
\text{with}\quad\pi_{\texttt{like}}^{(l)}(\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i})=p\left({\bf\it Y}_{i}^{(l)}=\mathbf{y}_{i}^{(l)},{\bf\it R}_{i}^{(l)}=\mathbf{0}~\big|~\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{i}\,,\tilde{\mathbf{D}}_{i}^{(l)},{\bf\it\theta}_{i}^{(l)}\right)\,,&\end{split}

(3.28)

where
\tilde{\mathbf{D}}^{(l)}
is formed from the GP state estimates for the
l
-th trajectory, i.e.,
\tilde{\mathbf{y}}_{1}^{(l)},\ldots,\tilde{\mathbf{y}}_{r}^{(l)}
.
The posterior mean and covariance respectively become

\displaystyle{\bf\it\mu}_{i}

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{l=1}^{\ell}(\tilde{\mathbf{D}}^{(l)})^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{W}_{i}^{zz\,(l)}\tilde{\mathbf{D}}^{(l)}+{\bf\it\Gamma}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}{\bf\it\Gamma}_{i}\right)^{-1}\sum_{l=1}^{\ell}(\tilde{\mathbf{D}}^{(l)})^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{W}_{i}^{zz\,(l)}\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{i}^{(l)}\,,

(3.29a)

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Sigma}_{i}

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{l=1}^{\ell}(\tilde{\mathbf{D}}^{(l)})^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{W}_{i}^{zz\,(l)}\tilde{\mathbf{D}}^{(l)}+{\bf\it\Gamma}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}{\bf\it\Gamma}_{i}\right)^{-1},

(3.29b)

in which
\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{i}^{(l)}:=\mathbf{K}^{zy\,(l)}_{i}(\mathbf{K}^{yy\,(l)}_{i})^{-1}\mathbf{y}_{i}^{(l)}

and

\mathbf{W}_{i}^{zz\,(l)}:=(\mathbf{K}^{zz\,(l)}_{i}-\mathbf{K}^{zy\,(l)}_{i}(\mathbf{K}^{yy\,(l)}_{i})^{-1}\mathbf{K}^{yz\,(l)}_{i})^{-1}

is the corresponding weight matrix.
In this case, the generalized least-squares problem
eq.

3.19
for
{\bf\it\mu}_{i}
becomes

\displaystyle\min_{{\bf\it\eta}}\left\{\sum_{l=1}^{\ell}\left\|\tilde{\mathbf{D}}^{(l)}{\bf\it\eta}-\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{i}^{(l)}\right\|_{\mathbf{W}_{i}^{zz\,(l)}}^{2}+\left\|\mathbf{\Gamma}_{i}{\bf\it\eta}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right\}.

(3.30)

We can write this in a form that is similar to
eq.

3.19
by noting that

\sum_{l=1}^{\ell}\left\|\tilde{\mathbf{D}}^{(l)}{\bf\it\eta}-\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{i}^{(l)}\right\|_{\mathbf{W}_{i}^{zz\,(l)}}^{2}=\left\|\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\tilde{\mathbf{D}}^{(1)}\\
\vdots\\
\tilde{\mathbf{D}}^{(\ell)}\end{array}\right]{\bf\it\eta}-\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{i}^{(1)}\\
\vdots\\
\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{i}^{(\ell)}\end{array}\right]\right\|_{\mathbf{W}_{i}}^{2}\,,

(3.31)

where
\mathbf{W}_{i}
is the block-diagonal matrix
\mathbf{W}_{i}=\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{W}_{i}^{zz\,(1)},\cdots,\mathbf{W}_{i}^{zz\,(\ell)})
.
Hence, the multiple-trajectory case can be written as the original case with a concatenated data matrix, block diagonal weights, and concatenated time derivative estimates.

Remark 3.5

(Parametric problems)
.

The strategy presented here assumes that the underlying reduced operators comprising
\hat{\mathbf{O}}
are constant across all trajectories, which is the case when only initial conditions or input terms are altered.
Parametric problems in which the reduced operators vary by trajectory require additional specialized treatment (see, e.g.,
[
43
]
) and remain a subject for future work.

4
Algorithmic procedure

The inference to define the posterior parameters
eq.

3.16
–
eq.

3.17
depends on the choice of kernel hyperparameters
{\bf\it\theta}_{1},\ldots,{\bf\it\theta}_{r}
, the data matrix
\tilde{\mathbf{D}}
, and the prior variance parameters
{\bf\it\gamma}_{1},\ldots,{\bf\it\gamma}_{r}
.
This section provides strategies for selecting each of these quantities and details the associated numerical algorithms.
For simplicity we return to the single-trajectory PDE setting, but the approach readily generalizes to cases with data from multiple trajectories
(see
Appendix
B
).

4.1
Kernel hyperparameter selection

For each
i=1,\ldots,r
, we select
{\bf\it\theta}_{i}
through the one-dimensional GP regression of the (noisy, reduced) snapshot data
({\bf\it t},\mathbf{y}_{i})
.
Specifically, we approximately solve the simplified maximum marginal log-likelihood problem

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} {\bf\it\theta}_{i}^{*}&=\operatorname{argmax}_{{\bf\it\theta}_{i}}\mathcal{L}({\bf\it\theta}_{i};{\bf\it t},\mathbf{y}_{i})\\
&=\operatorname{argmax}_{{\bf\it\theta}_{i}}\left\{-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{y}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}\left(\kappa({\bf\it t},{\bf\it t};{\bf\it\theta}_{i})+\chi_{i}\mathbf{I}_{m}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{y}_{i}-\frac{1}{2}\log|\kappa({\bf\it t},{\bf\it t};{\bf\it\theta}_{i})+\chi_{i}\mathbf{I}_{m}\big|-\frac{m}{2}\log(2\pi)\right\}\,,\end{aligned}

(4.1)

where
\chi_{i}
is included in
{\bf\it\theta}_{i}
.
Exact solutions of this nonconvex optimization are difficult to obtain, but there are several open-source resources for obtaining satisfactory approximate solutions.
Our implementation utilizes the Python library
scikit-learn

[
46
]
, which applies the limited-memory bound-constrained BFGS algorithm
[
9
]
available from
scipy.optimize

[
72
]
.

5.2
Nonlinear diffusion-reaction equation

In this section, we use GP-BayesOpInf to construct probabilistic surrogates for a nonlinear diffusion-reaction equation using data from multiple trajectories.
Each trajectory uses the same initial conditions but a different instance of a parameterized nonlinear source term. The efficacy of the probabilistic surrogate is shown for prediction in both time and in parameters defining the source term with noisy, sparse training data.

5.2.1
Governing equations

Consider the following forced heat diffusion model with a cubic reaction term,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial q}{\partial t}&=\mu\frac{\partial^{2}q}{\partial x^{2}}-q^{3}+u,&&x\in(0,1),t\in(0,T],\\
q(0,t)&=0,\quad q(1,t)=1,&&t\in(0,T],\\
q(x,0)&=q_{0}(x),&&x\in[0,1],\end{aligned}

(5.14a)

where
\mu=0.005
is the diffusion constant and where the source term
u
, parameterized by constants
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
, is given by

\displaystyle u(x,t;a,b)=\frac{a\sin(2\pi t)}{1+100\left(x-\frac{1}{4}\right)^{2}}+\frac{b\sin(4\pi t)}{1+100\left(x-\frac{3}{4}\right)^{2}}\,.

(5.14b)

The initial conditions are defined to be

\displaystyle q_{0}(x)

\displaystyle=x(1-x)\left(6(1-x)^{2}e^{-x}-10e^{x}\sin(x/6)\right)+x,

(5.14c)

a nonpolynomial function satisfying the boundary conditions.
In the following experiments, we use training data from
\ell=5
trajectories obtained by solving
eq.

5.14a
–
eq.

5.14c
for the
(a,b)
pairs
(-2,0)
,
(-1,-2)
,
(0,1)

(1,-1)

5.2.2
ROM structure

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial q}{\partial t}&=\mu\frac{\partial^{2}q}{\partial x^{2}}-qw+u,&\qquad\frac{\partial w}{\partial t}&=2\mu q\frac{\partial^{2}q}{\partial x^{2}}-w^{2}+qu.\end{aligned}

(5.15)

Let

Using a centered-POD approximation

(5.17)

where

6
GP-based parameter estimation for ODEs

The GP-BayesOpInf framework can be applied, with a minor adjustment, to a wide class of systems of ODEs that are not the result of spatially discretizing a PDE. Our approach allows for parameters to appear in multiple equations, treats all equations jointly, and leverages the prior variance selection framework developed in
Section

4
.

6.1
Derivation

We consider dynamical systems modeled by ODEs that may be nonlinear in state but which are linear in a set of scalar parameters, i.e.,

\displaystyle\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\hat{\mathbf{q}}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{S}(\hat{\mathbf{q}}(t),\mathbf{u}(t))\hat{\mathbf{o}},

(6.1)

where
\hat{\mathbf{q}}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
and
\mathbf{u}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
are the state and the input (as in the PDE case),

\mathbf{S}:\mathbb{R}^{r}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}\to\mathbb{R}^{r\times d(r,p)}
describes the (potentially nonlinear) system dynamics,
and
\hat{\mathbf{o}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d(r,p)}
are the system parameters.
We use
\hat{\mathbf{q}}(t)
to be consistent with previous notation, but in this setting the state
\hat{\mathbf{q}}(t)
is
not
the result of compressing a high-dimensional state variable.
Our objective is to construct a probability distribution for the parameter vector
\hat{\mathbf{o}}
given (noisy) observations of the state and the corresponding inputs.

If data are available for
\hat{\mathbf{q}}(t)
, a Bayesian inference for
\hat{\mathbf{o}}
can be formulated similar to
eq.

3.13
.
Let
{\bf\it t}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{i}}
denote the
m_{i}\in\mathbb{N}
times at which the
i
-th component of
\hat{\mathbf{q}}(t)
is observed as the (possibly noisy) vector
\mathbf{y}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{i}}
,
i=1,\ldots,r
, and define the concatenated state data vector

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{y}_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\mathbf{y}_{r}\end{array}\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{m},\qquad m=\sum_{i=1}^{r}m_{i}.

As in
eq.

3.1
, let
\hat{q}_{i}(t)
denote a GP for the
i
-th component of
\hat{\mathbf{q}}(t)
.
Define
{\bf\it Y}
to be the random
m
-vector whose entries are Gaussian random variables produced by evaluating
\hat{q}_{i}
over
{\bf\it t}_{i}
for each
i=1,\ldots,r
and concatenating the results.
Hence,
\mathbf{y}
is an observation of
{\bf\it Y}
.
Similarly, for estimation times
{\bf\it t}_{i}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{m^{\prime}}
, let
{\bf\it Z}
be the random vector with
rm^{\prime}
entries given by concatenating the time derivative GPs
\dot{\hat{q}}_{i}
evaluated at
{\bf\it t}^{\prime}
,
i=1,\ldots,r
.
Finally, let
\tilde{\mathbf{D}}\in\mathbb{R}^{rm^{\prime}\times d(r,p)}
contain state and input data at the estimation times
{\bf\it t}^{\prime}

following the structure of
\mathbf{S}

and define
{\bf\it R}={\bf\it Z}-\tilde{\mathbf{D}}\hat{\mathbf{o}}
.
We then have the parameter vector inference

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \pi_{\texttt{post}}(\hat{\mathbf{o}})=p(\hat{\mathbf{o}}\mid{\bf\it Y}=\mathbf{y},{\bf\it R}=\mathbf{0},\tilde{\mathbf{D}},{\bf\it\theta}_{1},\ldots,{\bf\it\theta}_{r},{\bf\it\gamma})\propto\pi_{\texttt{prior}}(\hat{\mathbf{o}})\pi_{\texttt{like}}(\hat{\mathbf{o}}),\quad\text{with}\\
\pi_{\texttt{prior}}(\hat{\mathbf{o}})=p(\hat{\mathbf{o}}\mid{\bf\it\gamma})\propto\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\|\operatorname{diag}({\bf\it\gamma})\hat{\mathbf{o}}\|_{2}^{2}\right)\,,\\
\pi_{\texttt{like}}(\hat{\mathbf{o}})=p({\bf\it Y}=\mathbf{y},{\bf\it R}=\mathbf{0}\mid\hat{\mathbf{o}},\tilde{\mathbf{D}},{\bf\it\theta}_{1},\ldots,{\bf\it\theta}_{r})\,,\end{aligned}

(6.5)

where, as before,
{\bf\it\theta}_{i}
denotes the hyperparameters of the GP
\hat{q}_{i}
.
This likelihood aligns with the gradient matching likelihood used in computational statistics
[
11
,
68
,
73
]
.

The mean and covariance of the posterior
\hat{\mathbf{o}}\sim\mathcal{N}({\bf\it\mu},{\bf\it\Sigma})
are given by

\displaystyle{\bf\it\mu}

\displaystyle={\bf\it\Sigma}\tilde{\mathbf{D}}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{W}\tilde{\mathbf{z}}\,,

\displaystyle{\bf\it\Sigma}

\displaystyle=\left(\tilde{\mathbf{D}}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{W}\tilde{\mathbf{D}}+{\bf\it\Gamma}^{\mathsf{T}}{\bf\it\Gamma}\right)^{-1}\,,

(6.6a)

in which

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{z}}=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{r}\end{array}\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{rm^{\prime}},\qquad\mathbf{W}=\operatorname{diag}\left(\mathbf{W}_{1}^{zz},\ldots,\mathbf{W}_{r}^{zz}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{rm^{\prime}\times rm^{\prime}},\qquad{\bf\it\Gamma}=\operatorname{diag}({\bf\it\gamma})\in\mathbb{R}^{d(r,p)\times d(r,p)},

where
\mathbf{W}_{i}^{zz}\in\mathbb{R}^{m^{\prime}\times m^{\prime}}
and
\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{m^{\prime}}
are defined by the GP
\hat{q}_{i}
as in
eq.

3.14
and
eq.

3.18
, respectively.
The generalized least-squares problem
eq.

3.19
for the posterior mean
{\bf\it\mu}
is

\displaystyle\min_{{\bf\it\eta}}\left\|\tilde{\mathbf{D}}{\bf\it\eta}-\tilde{\mathbf{z}}\right\|_{\mathbf{W}}^{2}+\|{\bf\it\Gamma\xi}\|_{2}^{2}.

(6.7)

6.2
SEIRD epidemiological model

In this section, we consider a system of five ODEs with five scalar parameters that defines the SEIRD compartmental model for infectious disease spreading through a population
[
37
]
:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}t}\hat{q}_{S}(t)&=-\beta\hat{q}_{S}(t)\hat{q}_{I}(t),&\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}t}\hat{q}_{R}(t)&=(1-\alpha)\gamma\hat{q}_{I}(t),\\

\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}t}\hat{q}_{I}(t)&=\delta\hat{q}_{E}(t)-(1-\alpha)\gamma\hat{q}_{I}(t)-\alpha\rho\hat{q}_{I}(t).\end{aligned}

(6.8)

The state variables represent the proportions of the population that are susceptible to the disease (
\hat{q}_{S}(t)
), exposed but not yet infected (
\hat{q}_{E}(t)
), currently infected (
\hat{q}_{I}(t)
), recovered (and immune) post-infection (
\hat{q}_{R}(t)
), and deceased post-infection (
\hat{q}_{D}(t)
).
The system parameters describe the rates at which individuals transition from one state to another:

\beta>0
is the expected number of people an infected person exposes to the disease per day,

\delta>0
is the incubation period for transition from exposed to infected,

\gamma>0
is the recovery rate of an infected individual,

\alpha>0
is the infection fatality rate, and

\rho>0
is the inverse of the average number of days for an infected person to die if they do not recover.
Our goal is to give a probabilistic estimate of the system parameters given noisy and/or sparse state observations.

To use the methodology of
Section

6.1
, we recognize that

6.8
can be written as

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}t}\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\hat{q}_{S}(t)\\
\hat{q}_{E}(t)\\
\hat{q}_{I}(t)\\
\hat{q}_{R}(t)\\
\hat{q}_{D}(t)\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}[]{cccc}-\hat{q}_{S}(t)\hat{q}_{I}(t)&0&0&0\\
\hat{q}_{S}(t)\hat{q}_{I}(t)&-\hat{q}_{E}(t)&0&0\\
0&\hat{q}_{E}(t)&-\hat{q}_{I}(t)&-\hat{q}_{I}(t)\\
0&0&\hat{q}_{I}(t)&0\\
0&0&0&\hat{q}_{I}(t)\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\beta\\
\delta\\

\alpha\rho\end{array}\right],\end{aligned}

Remark 6.1

(Equation fitting vs. trajectory fitting)
.

For Bayesian parameter estimation in ODEs, the likelihood setting in this work essentially fits given equations to data. Time derivatives on the left-hand side of the ODEs are estimated using GPs, and the parameters on the right-hand side are estimated through a linear regression problem. This is fundamentally different from a likelihood that compares the evolution of the dynamical model—time integration of the ODEs—with the observed data, i.e., fitting the trajectories. The latter may also improve the robustness to data noise and sparsity, but the training process often demands more computational effort. A typical example is the (Bayesian) neural ODE approach
[
13
,
16
]
, which requires solving adjoint state equations to evaluate the gradients of loss functions.

7
Concluding remarks

This work presents a new Bayesian method for OpInf that uses a probabilistic formulation for the reduced-order operators, thereby combining physics-based and data-driven modeling. In particular, GP emulation incorporates both reduced state data and their differential equation structure into the definition of the likelihood function for the reduced operators. The power of GPs in smoothing and interpolating noisy and/or sparse data enables probabilistic ROMs in scenarios where standard OpInf is not feasible.
The closed-form posterior estimates of the reduced operators provide an interpretable low-dimensional representation of complex physics-based models.

The numerical results demonstrate that GP-BayesOpInf is effective at constructing probabilistic surrogates for high-dimensional dynamical systems with polynomial structure given only data and knowledge of the model structure. The method was shown to perform well with abundant but noisy data, as well as in low-noise scenarios with sparse data. GP-BayesOpInf also applies to problems with multiple trajectories stemming from different initial conditions or input functions, as well as to a large class of differential systems with parameters shared across the equations.

There are several avenues for future work related to GP-BayesOpInf.
Our experiments used squared-exponential GP kernels and linear dimensionality reduction by POD, but the methodology is flexible and can be applied with any sufficiently differentiable GP kernel and with any type of compression (including nonlinear manifold approximators such as in
[
3
,
22
,
23
,
34
,
58
]
). It would be interesting to explore if embedding desirable properties in the kernel can improve model performance for problems with specific characteristics (for example, periodicity).
In the presence of large amounts of noise, the number of reliable reduced modes that can be obtained through POD is limited. To reduce the projection error by including more reduced modes in GP-BayesOpInf, it may be advantageous to leverage filtering strategies as shown in
[
20
]
prior to applying dimensionality reduction. We also plan to explore more advanced GP techniques, such as sparse GPs
[
64
,
65
]
to reduce the trajectory data needed for regression (i.e., selecting
m^{\prime}
automatically), multifidelity GPs
[
2
,
12
,
40
,
45
,
49
,
50
]
to fuse data from different sources, as well as a single vector-valued GP for all reduced modes to incorporate physically meaningful spatial correlations. Another promising direction is to leverage the quantified uncertainties to guide the active selection of initial conditions and/or input functions for parsimoniously generating informative training trajectories.

Appendix A
Gaussian process regression

A Gaussian process is a collection of random variables, any finite number of which obeys a joint normal distribution. In Gaussian process regression, the prior on the regression function is assumed to follow a Gaussian process, i.e., for
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\in\Omega\times\Omega
,

f(\mathbf{x})\sim\mathcal{GP}(b(\mathbf{x}),\kappa(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}))\,,

(A.1)

where
\Omega
is the input domain of interest,
b:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
is a given mean function, and
\kappa:\Omega\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
is a covariance function which often depends on kernel hyperparameters
{\bf\it\theta}
. We also assume that the observation of
f(\mathbf{x})
, denoted by
y(\mathbf{x})
, may be corrupted by an independent white noise term, i.e.,
y(\mathbf{x})=f(\mathbf{x})+\epsilon
with
\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\chi)
.

Given a finite number
m
of training input locations
\mathbf{X}=[~\mathbf{x}_{1}~|~\mathbf{x}_{2}~|~\cdots~|~\mathbf{x}_{m}~]^{\mathsf{T}}
in the domain
\Omega
, a joint Gaussian is thus defined for the corresponding observed outputs
\mathbf{y}=\{y(\mathbf{x}_{1}),y(\mathbf{x}_{2}),\cdots,y(\mathbf{x}_{m})\}^{\mathsf{T}}
:

\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{X}~\sim~\mathcal{N}(b(\mathbf{X}),\mathbf{K})\,,\quad\mathbf{K}=\kappa(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{X})+\chi\mathbf{I}_{m}\,.

(A.2)

Using the standard rule for conditioning Gaussians, the (predictive) posterior distribution for any new test input
\mathbf{s}\in\Omega
conditioned on the training data can be formulated as a new Gaussian process
f^{*}(\mathbf{s})
:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &f^{*}(\mathbf{s})|\mathbf{X},\mathbf{y}~\sim~\mathcal{GP}(b^{*}(\mathbf{s}),\kappa^{*}(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{s}^{\prime}))\,,\quad&&\text{with}\\
&b^{*}(\mathbf{s})=b(\mathbf{s})+\kappa(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{X})\mathbf{K}^{-1}(\mathbf{y}-b(\mathbf{X}))\,,\quad&&\text{and}\qquad\kappa^{*}(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{s}^{\prime})=\kappa(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{s}^{\prime})-\kappa(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{X})\mathbf{K}^{-1}\kappa(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{s}^{\prime})\,.\end{aligned}

(A.3)

The values of hyperparameters can be tuned to optimize the predictive performance. A widely used technique is the empirical Bayes approach of maximizing marginal likelihood. With a standard gradient-based optimizer, the optimal hyperparameters
{\bf\it\theta}^{*}
can be estimated through the following maximization problem:

\begin{split}{\bf\it\theta}^{*}&=\arg\max_{{\bf\it\theta}}~\log p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{X},{\bf\it\theta})\\
&=\arg\max_{{\bf\it\theta}}\left\{-\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{y}-b(\mathbf{X}))^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{K}^{-1}({\bf\it\theta})(\mathbf{y}-b(\mathbf{X}))-\frac{1}{2}\log\absolutevalue{\vb{K}(\vb*{\theta})}-\frac{m}{2}\log(2\pi)\right\}\,.\end{split}

(A.4)

Here,
p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{X},{\bf\it\theta})
is the marginal likelihood function of
{\bf\it\theta}
, which is given by the density function value of the observations
\mathbf{y}
over the input locations
\mathbf{X}
given the hyperparameters
{\bf\it\theta}
.
