Can one hear supercontinents in the tides of ocean planets?
Pierre Auclair-Desrotour, Mohammad Farhat, Gwenaël Boué, Mickaël Gastineau, Jacques Laskar

Abstract
Context. Recent observations and theoretical progress made about the history of the Earth-Moon system suggest that tidal dissipation in oceans primarily drives the long term evolution of orbital systems hosting ocean planets. Particularly, they emphasise the key role played by the geometry of land-ocean distributions in this mechanism. However, the complex way continents affect oceanic tides still remains to be elucidated. Aims. In the present study, we investigate the impact of a single supercontinent on the tidal response of an ocean planet and the induced tidally dissipated energy. Methods. The adopted approach is based on the linear tidal theory. By simplifying the continent to a spherical cap of given angular radius and position on the globe, we proceed to a harmonic analysis of the whole planet’s tidal response including the coupling with the solid part due to ocean loading and self-attraction variations. In this framework, tidal flows are formulated analytically in terms of explicitly defined oceanic eigenmodes, as well as the resulting tidal Love numbers, dissipated power, and torque. Results. The analysis highlights the symmetry breaking effect of the continent, which makes the dependence of tidal quantities on the tidal frequency become highly irregular. The metric introduced to quantify this continentality effect reveals abrupt transitions between polar and non-polar configurations, and between small-sized and medium-sized continents. Additionally, it predicts that a continent similar to South America or smaller ( ∼ 30 ° {\sim}30^{\degree} -angular radius) does not alter qualitatively the tidal response of a global ocean whatever its position on the planet.

Introduction

‘Can one hear the shape of a drum?’ This question, raised by
Kac 1966
, emphasises the link connecting the geometry of a vibrating system to the frequencies at which it can vibrate. Mathematically, it defines a broad class of problems referred to as spectral geometry, which aims to establish relationships between the eigenmodes of Riemannian manifolds and their geometric features. In the aforementioned study, Kac investigates whether it is possible or not to infer some information about the shape of a vibrating drumhead from the sound it makes, which amounts to recovering the drumhead’s geometry from its acoustic signature in an inverse problem approach. Unfortunately, the answer to this question was shown to be negative in the general case, as two different shapes are able to produce the very same sound
(
Gordon et al. 1992
, e.g.)
. Nevertheless, it remains possible to infer some specific geometric features of the system to a certain extent.

Interestingly, this statement is also applicable to tidally forced ocean planets, which may be regarded as giant celestial vibrating drumheads. In the general case, one calls ‘ocean planets’ rocky bodies that are partly or totally covered by a surface liquid layer

Tides are the main mechanism that controls the evolution of planetary systems over timescales ranging from millions to billions of years
(
Hut 1981
;
Correia & Laskar 2001
;
Levrard et al. 2009
, e.g.)
. Apart from the atmospheric thermal tides that are induced by insolation variations
(

;
Leconte et al. 2015
;
Auclair-Desrotour et al. 2019b
, e.g.)
, they result from the mutual gravitational interactions between celestial bodies, which undergo the differential attraction of their neighbours. Combined with dissipative processes, this tidal forcing generates a delayed mass redistribution, thereby leading to exchanges of angular momentum between the orbit of the tidal perturber and the spin of the tidally forced body
(
Correia et al. 2014
, e.g.)
. Additionally, tides are accompanied with energy dissipation where the mechanical energy lost by the orbital system is converted into heat. While it is negligible on Earth, this tidal heating is sometimes able to partly melt the body and to generate surface volcanism, as observed on the Jovian moon Io
(
Peale et al. 1979
, e.g.)
.

Oceanic tides take the form of frequency-resonant surface gravity modes distorted by the planet’s rotation
(
Auclair-Desrotour et al. 2018
, e.g.)
, which strongly differs from the smooth viscoelastic elongation of solid bodies
(
Correia et al. 2014
, e.g.)
. As a consequence, tidal dissipation in oceans may be increased by several orders of magnitude while crossing a resonance associated with a predominant mode
(
Arbic & Garrett 2010
, e.g.)
. This resonant behaviour notably explains why, for present day Earth, the oceanic contribution (
{\sim}2.5\penalty\
TW) to the semidiurnal component (
M_{2}
) of dissipated tidal energy is an order of magnitude greater than the solid counterpart
(
Lambeck 1977
;
Provost & Lyard 1997
;
Egbert & Ray 2001
;
Tyler 2021
)
. Analogously, oceanic tidal flows presumably warm up the icy moons of the Solar system outer planets that are suspected to harbour subsurface oceans, such as Europa, Callisto, or Titan
(
Tyler 2008
;
Tyler 2014
;
Kamata et al. 2015
;
Beuthe 2016
;
Matsuyama et al. 2018
, e.g.)
.

Starting with
MacDonald 1964
, several authors investigated the role played by oceanic tides in the evolution of the Earth-Moon system, using either semi-analytical approaches with simplified geometries
(
Webb 1980
;
Webb 1982
;
Bills & Ray 1999
;

, e.g.)
– including global ocean models
(
Auclair-Desrotour et al. 2018
;
Motoyama et al. 2020
;

, e.g.)
–, or numerical methods based upon realistic land-ocean distributions
(

;
Le Provost et al. 1998
;
Arbic et al. 2010
;
Kodaira et al. 2016
;
Green et al. 2017
;

;

, e.g.)
. For a didactical review of the pioneering studies of the field, the reader is referred to
Lambeck 1977
and
Bills & Ray 1999
. This long series of works particularly emphasises the strong interplay between the resonant oceanic tidal flows and continents, which shapes the four billion year-history of the Earth’s length of the day (LOD) and Earth-Moon distance
(
Daher et al. 2021
;
Farhat et al. 2022a
, see e.g.)
. Moreover,
Green et al. 2017
show that the topography significantly increases tidal dissipation, and
Blackledge et al. 2020
underline the sensitivity of the latter to coastlines’ fractality. However, the way the large-scale geometry of the ocean basin alters the planet’s tidal response cannot be easily disentangled from other effects in realistic models due to its complexity.

The present work is an attempt to address this issue by generalising the semi-analytical tidal theory of hemispherical oceans
(
Longuet-Higgins & Pond 1970
;
Webb 1980
;
Webb 1982
;
Farhat et al. 2022a
)
to ocean basins of arbitrary sizes. In this approach, the oceanic depth is assumed to be uniform and thin compared to the planet radius. Adopting the so-called ’shallow water’ approximation
(
Vallis 2006
, e.g.)
, we ignore both the ocean stratification and the associated baroclinic component of tidal flows – that is the contribution of internal gravity waves
(
Gerkema & Zimmerman 2008
, e.g.)
– so that the described tide is purely barotropic. Also, the geometry of the continent is simplified to a spherical cap of specified angular radius in order to avoid mathematical complications. Finally, bottom friction is modelled by a standard Rayleigh drag, and the coupling effect between the ocean and the deformable solid surface is taken into account. Analogously with the examples of the global and hemispherical oceans, this formalism allows the planet’s tidal response and the resulting dissipated energy to be formulated in terms of explicit eigenmodes, each of these eigenmodes being resonant for a specific tidal frequency. Additionally, the ocean dynamics is controlled by a small number of dimensionless parameters, which provides an appropriate framework for probing the parameter space and characterising the continentality effect.

In Sect.
2
, we detail the theory by successively introducing the Laplace’s Tidal Equations (LTEs) that govern the oceanic tidal response, the eigenmodes of the ocean basin, and the expressions of the tidal Love numbers, torque, and power. In Sect.
3
, we examine the frequency-behaviour of an Earth-sized planet with an equatorial hemispherical ocean. This reference case is used in Sect.
4
to characterise the sensitivity of the planet’s tidal response to the position of the continent on the globe, its size, the oceanic depth, the dissipation timescale, the elasticity of the solid part, and its anelasticity timescale. We elaborate on the continentality effect in Sect.
5
by introducing a metric that quantifies the sensitivity of the tidal torque to the position and size of the supercontinent. Finally, in Sect.
6
, the conclusions of the study are summarised. We stress here that Sect.
2
details technical aspects of the developed model. Thus we invite the reader that might be only interested in the application of the theory to skip this section and to jump directly to Sect.
3
. It is also noteworthy that all the notations introduced in the main text can be retrieved in the nomenclature made in Appendix
A

Oceanic tidal response

We establish here the equations governing the tidal dynamics of the ocean basin harboured by a rocky planet of radius
R_{\rm p}
. These equations are based on the commonly used shallow water approach, where the ocean is considered as a thin liquid layer of uniform density and depth
H\ll R_{\rm p}

(
Vallis 2006
, e.g.)
. In this approach, the fluid is supposed to be incompressible. The tidal response thus described is said to be barotropic because it does not depend on the vertical structure of the ocean
(
Vallis 2006
)
. The continental geometry is simplified to a spherical cap of angular radius
\theta_{{\rm c}}
, as shown by Fig.
1
. Accordingly, the angular radius of the ocean basin is defined as
\theta_{0}\equiv\pi-\theta_{{\rm c}}
.

2.1
Geometry of the ocean basin

To write down the equations that describe the oceanic tidal waves, we shall preliminarily introduce two frames of references and their associated systems of coordinates. First, we denote by
\mathcal{R}{:}\left(O,\boldsymbol{{\rm e}}_{X},\boldsymbol{{\rm e}}_{Y},\boldsymbol{{\rm e}}_{Z}\right)
the frame of reference rotating with the planet and having its centre of gravity, O, as origin. The Cartesian basis of unit vectors
\left(\boldsymbol{{\rm e}}_{X},\boldsymbol{{\rm e}}_{Y},\boldsymbol{{\rm e}}_{Z}\right)
is such that
\boldsymbol{{\rm e}}_{X}
and
\boldsymbol{{\rm e}}_{Y}
correspond to two orthogonal directions in the planet’s equatorial plane, and
\boldsymbol{{\rm e}}_{Z}
to the direction of the spin vector. The unit vector
\boldsymbol{{\rm e}}_{Z}
thus designates the position of the North pole on the unit sphere. The geocentric frame of reference
\mathcal{R}
is associated with the usual spherical coordinates
\left(\hat{r},\hat{\theta},\hat{\varphi}\right)
, where
\hat{r}
,
\hat{\theta}
, and
\hat{\varphi}
designate the radial, colatitudinal and longitudinal coordinates, respectively. The position of the circular supercontinent on the globe is defined by the coordinates of its centre,
\left(\hat{\theta}_{{\rm S}},\hat{\varphi}_{{\rm S}}\right)
. As the centre of the oceanic basin corresponds to the antipodal point, its coordinates on the globe are given by
\left(\hat{\theta}_{{\rm oc}},\hat{\varphi}_{{\rm oc}}\right)=\left(\pi-\hat{\theta}_{{\rm S}},\pi+\hat{\varphi}_{{\rm S}}\right)
.

The oceanic frame of reference,
\mathcal{R}_{{\rm oc}}{:}\left(O,\boldsymbol{{\rm e}}_{x},\boldsymbol{{\rm e}}_{y},\boldsymbol{{\rm e}}_{z}\right)

\boldsymbol{{\rm e}}_{z}
points towards the centre of the ocean basin, while
\boldsymbol{{\rm e}}_{x}
and
\boldsymbol{{\rm e}}_{y}
define two orthogonal axes in the plane normal to
\boldsymbol{{\rm e}}_{z}
. This frame of reference is associated with the spherical coordinates
\left(r,\theta,\varphi\right)

is the radial coordinate,
\theta
the colatitude (
\theta=0
at the centre of the ocean basin), and
\varphi
the longitude. The change of basis vectors

2.2
Laplace’s tidal equations

The planet is subject to the effects of the tidal gravitational potential generated by the perturber (the Moon or the Sun in the Earth case), which is expressed, in the frame of reference
\mathcal{R}
, as

U_{\rm T}\equiv\frac{GM_{{\rm s}}}{\left|R_{\rm p}\boldsymbol{{\rm e}}_{r}-\boldsymbol{r}_{{\rm s}}\right|}-\frac{GM_{{\rm s}}}{r_{{\rm s}}^{2}}R_{\rm p}\cos\hat{\theta}-\frac{GM_{{\rm s}}}{r_{{\rm s}}},

(4)

where the constant component and the component responsible for the Keplerian dynamics of the two-body system are removed (second and third terms in the right-hand member of Eq. (
4
)). In this equation,
G
is the universal gravitational constant,
M_{{\rm s}}
the mass of the perturber,
\boldsymbol{r}_{{\rm s}}
its position vector, and
r_{{\rm s}}\equiv\left|\boldsymbol{r}_{{\rm s}}\right|
the planet-perturber distance. We note that the symbol
\equiv
is used throughout the text to distinguish between definitions and equalities. The tidal force per unit mass exerted by the perturber on the planet is given by
\boldsymbol{F}\equiv\nabla U_{\rm T}
. Following the formalism introduced in earlier works
(
Tyler 2011
;
Matsuyama 2014
;
Auclair-Desrotour et al. 2018
;
Auclair-Desrotour et al. 2019a
;
Motoyama et al. 2020
;
Farhat et al. 2022a
, e.g.)
, we write down the momentum and mass conservation equations, respectively, as

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{V}+\sigma_{\rm R}\boldsymbol{V}+\boldsymbol{f}\times\boldsymbol{V}+g\nabla\left(\Gamma_{{\rm D}}\zeta-\Gamma_{{\rm T}}\zeta_{{\rm eq}}\right)

\displaystyle=0,

(5)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\zeta+\nabla\cdot\left(H\boldsymbol{V}\right)

\displaystyle=0,

(6)

with
t
designating the time,
g
the surface gravity at rest,
\sigma_{\rm R}
the Rayleigh drag frequency used to describe the action of dissipative mechanisms,
\boldsymbol{V}
the horizontal velocity vector – defined from the horizontal displacement vector
\boldsymbol{\xi}
as
\boldsymbol{V}\equiv\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\xi}
–,
\boldsymbol{f}
the Coriolis parameter,
\zeta
the vertical displacement of the ocean’s surface with respect to the oceanic floor, and
\zeta_{{\rm eq}}\equiv U_{\rm T}/g
the equilibrium displacement corresponding to the equipotential surface induced by the tidal gravitational potential.

In the momentum equation, the notations
\Gamma_{{\rm D}}
and
\Gamma_{{\rm T}}
refer to non-trivial linear operators accounting for the effects of ocean loading, self-attraction, and deformation of the solid regions of the planet
(
Hendershott 1972
, e.g.)
. These operators are called the ‘solid deformation operators’ in the following. They encompass the complex coupling between the oceanic shell and the solid interior described by Poisson’s equation, and the momentum and rheological equations governing the tidal dynamics of the solid part. The two operators simplify to
\Gamma_{{\rm D}}=\Gamma_{{\rm T}}=1
if one neglects both the tidal deformation of the solid part (infinite-rigidity approximation) and the variation of self-attraction induced by the oceanic tidal response
(
Cowling 1941
;
Unno et al. 1989
, Cowling approximation; see)
. In the general case, they are determined by the rheological behaviour of the solid part in its response to gravitational and surface forcings.

5

6

(

. We note that
\zeta_{{\rm eq}}
results from the coupled oceanic tidal response and tidal deformation of the solid part, which includes both gravitational and loading interactions, as discussed further. The Coriolis parameter is expressed as a function of the colatitude of the current point in
\mathcal{R}
,

\boldsymbol{f}\equiv 2\Omega\cos\hat{\theta}\,\boldsymbol{{\rm e}}_{r},

(7)

where
\Omega>0
is the planet’s spin angular velocity. The horizontal gradient operator and divergence of the horizontal velocity are expressed in
\mathcal{R}_{{\rm oc}}

\left(\boldsymbol{{\rm e}}_{\theta},\boldsymbol{{\rm e}}_{\varphi}\right)

\displaystyle\nabla\equiv

\displaystyle\ R_{\rm p}^{-1}\left[\boldsymbol{{\rm e}}_{\theta}\partial_{\theta}+\boldsymbol{{\rm e}}_{\varphi}\left(\sin\theta\right)^{-1}\partial_{\varphi}\right],

(8)

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{V}=

\displaystyle\left(R_{\rm p}\sin\theta\right)^{-1}\left[\partial_{\theta}\left(\sin\theta V_{\theta}\right)+\partial_{\varphi}V_{\varphi}\right].

(9)

\sigma_{\rm R}\boldsymbol{V}

\sigma_{\rm R}

2.3
Nondimensional tidal equations

The nondimensional momentum and continuity equations are obtained by choosing as reference time and velocity scales the inertial time,
t_{0}
, and the typical velocity of long-wavelength surface gravity waves,
V_{0}
, which are defined, respectively, as

\displaystyle t_{0}\equiv\left(2\Omega\right)^{-1},

\displaystyle V_{0}\equiv\sqrt{gH}.

(10)

Introducing the normalised time
\tilde{t}
, Coriolis parameter
\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}}
, horizontal gradient
\tilde{\nabla}
, the complex horizontal displacement vector
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
, and vertical displacements
\tilde{\zeta}
and
\tilde{\zeta}_{{\rm eq}}
, such that

\begin{array}[]{lll}t=t_{0}\tilde{t},&\boldsymbol{f}=t_{0}^{-1}\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}}&\nabla=R_{\rm p}^{-1}\tilde{\nabla},\\
\boldsymbol{\xi}=\Re\left(R_{\rm p}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\right),&\zeta=\Re\left(H\tilde{\zeta}\right),&\zeta_{{\rm eq}}=\Re\left(H\tilde{\zeta}_{{\rm eq}}\right),\end{array}

(11)

with
\Re
referring to the real part of a complex number, we end up with the nondimensional complex LTEs given by

\displaystyle\left[\partial_{\tilde{t}\tilde{t}}+\left(\tilde{\sigma}_{{\rm R}}+\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}}\times\right)\partial_{\tilde{t}}\right]\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}}+\tilde{\sigma}_{\rm G}^{2}\tilde{\nabla}\tilde{\Xi}

\displaystyle=0,

(12)

\displaystyle\tilde{\zeta}+\tilde{\nabla}\cdot\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}}

\displaystyle=0,

(13)

with
\tilde{\Xi}\equiv\Gamma_{{\rm D}}\tilde{\zeta}-\Gamma_{{\rm T}}\tilde{\zeta}_{{\rm eq}}
. In the above equations, tidal dynamics are controlled by two dimensionless parameters,

\displaystyle\tilde{\sigma}_{\rm G}\equiv\frac{V_{0}}{2\Omega R_{\rm p}}=\frac{\sqrt{gH}}{2\Omega R_{\rm p}},

\displaystyle\tilde{\sigma}_{{\rm R}}\equiv\frac{\sigma_{\rm R}}{2\Omega}.

(14)

The first parameter,
\tilde{\sigma}_{\rm G}
, can be considered as a normalised Rossby deformation length since it compares the typical propagation velocity of surface gravity waves (
V_{0}
) with the Earth’s rotation velocity. If
\tilde{\sigma}_{\rm G}\ll 1
(fast rotator regime), the inertial forces resulting from Coriolis acceleration predominate with respect to the restoring forces of surface gravity waves (pressure forces and gravity). Conversely, if
\tilde{\sigma}_{\rm G}\gg 1
(slow rotator regime), Coriolis terms are not strong enough to significantly deviate free surface gravity waves. As it describes the ratio of drag forces to Coriolis forces, the second dimensionless parameter,
\tilde{\sigma}_{{\rm R}}
, may be regarded as an Ekman number. If
\tilde{\sigma}_{{\rm R}}\ll 1
, the drag does not alter the tidal response much. Conversely,
\tilde{\sigma}_{{\rm R}}\gg 1
characterises a frictional (or viscous) regime where inertial effects are annihilated by the strong damping associated with the drag.

In addition to
\tilde{\sigma}_{\rm G}
and
\tilde{\sigma}_{{\rm R}}
, the time-derivative operators in Eq. (
12
) introduce a third dimensionless parameter describing the ratio of tidal forces to Coriolis forces,

\tilde{\sigma}\equiv\frac{\sigma}{2\Omega}.

(15)

The notation
\sigma
in the above equation designates the typical frequency of tidal flows, which will serve as the tidal frequency of the considered tidal force in the Fourier expansion of tidal quantities detailed further. If
\tilde{\sigma}\ll 1
(sub-inertial regime), the acceleration term of the momentum equation can be neglected with respect to Coriolis terms. Conversely, if
\tilde{\sigma}\gg 1
(super-inertial regime), the flow is strongly driven by the tidal forcing and it is thus hardly distorted by the planet’s rotation. We note that
\tilde{\sigma}
is the inverse of the so-called spin parameter that is commonly used to characterise the oscillations of rotating fluids in planetary and stellar hydrodynamics
(
Lee & Saio 1997
, e.g.)
. The dimensionless parameters that control the planet’s tidal response are summarised in Table

.

Proudman 1920

\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}}=\tilde{\nabla}\Phi+\tilde{\nabla}\Psi\times\boldsymbol{{\rm e}}_{r}.

(16)

The curl-free (
\tilde{\nabla}\times\left(\tilde{\nabla}\Phi\right)=0
) and divergence-free (
\tilde{\nabla}\cdot\left(\tilde{\nabla}\Psi\times\boldsymbol{{\rm e}}_{r}\right)=0
) components of
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
are defined from the divergent displacement potential
\Phi
and the rotational displacement streamfunction
\Psi
, respectively
(
Webb 1980
;
Tyler 2011
)
. We emphasise that the Helmholtz decomposition is not unique for bounded domains such as the considered ocean basin. This results from the fact that additional physical constraints on the boundary condition are necessary to define
\Phi
and
\Psi

(
Fox-Kemper et al. 2003
, e.g.)
. However choosing boundary conditions for the two functions is not straightforward given that the two components of Eq. (
16
) cannot be disentangled in the total flux. For instance, the impermeability condition at the coastline is formulated as
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}=0
, where
\boldsymbol{n}
designates the outward pointing unit vector defining the normal to the coast. This theoretically requires to find another boundary condition and to solve it for
\tilde{\nabla}\Phi
and
\tilde{\nabla}\Psi
together with the first condition, which may lead to significant mathematical complications.

To circumvent this difficulty, it is convenient to adopt a commonly used trick
(
Webb 1980
;
Webb 1982
;
Gent & McWilliams 1983
;
Watterson 2001
;
Han & Huang 2020
;
Farhat et al. 2022a
, e.g.)
, which consists in applying the impermeability condition to both components of Eq. (
16
),

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{n}\cdot\tilde{\nabla}\Phi=0,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{n}\cdot\left(\tilde{\nabla}\Psi\times\boldsymbol{{\rm e}}_{r}\right)=0.

(17)

The first condition of Eq. (
17
) means that the gradient of
\Phi
is zero in the direction normal to the coastline, which is equivalent to Neumann condition
(specified value of the derivative of the solution applied at the boundary of the domain; e.g.
Morse & Feshbach 1953
, Section 6.1)
. The second condition may be reformulated as
\left(\boldsymbol{{\rm e}}_{r}\times\boldsymbol{n}\right)\cdot\tilde{\nabla}\Psi=0
, implying that the streamfunction is a constant along the coastline. This corresponds to a Dirichlet condition
(specified value of the solution itself;
Morse & Feshbach 1953
, Section 6.1)
. Since both
\Phi
and
\Psi
are defined to a constant, the value of
\Psi
at the coastline is set to zero, which simplifies the second condition of Eq. (
17
) to
\Psi=0
. Interestingly, this condition induces the orthogonality of the curl-free and divergence-free components of the horizontal displacement
(e.g.
Farhat et al. 2022a
, Appendix E)
, formulated as

\int_{\mathcal{O}}\overline{\tilde{\nabla}\Phi}\cdot\left(\tilde{\nabla}\Psi\times\boldsymbol{{\rm e}}_{r}\right){\rm d}S=0,

(18)

with
{\rm d}S=\sin\theta{\rm d}\theta{\rm d}\varphi
being an infinitesimal surface element of the unit sphere,
\mathcal{O}
the domain occupied by the ocean basin, and
\overline{\tilde{\nabla}\Phi}
the complex conjugate of
\tilde{\nabla}\Phi
. This property leads to appreciable simplifications in the LTEs, as discussed further.

The divergent potential function and the streamfunction are defined on a compact connected domain, the ocean basin (
\mathcal{O}
), and they satisfy either Neumann or Dirichlet conditions at its boundary,
\partial\mathcal{O}
. As a consequence, the two functions can be expanded in terms of the complete sets of orthogonal eigenfunctions
\left\{\Phi_{k}\right\}_{1\leq k\leq\infty}
or
\left\{\Psi_{k}\right\}_{1\leq k\leq\infty}
that are the solutions of the wave equations given by

\displaystyle\left(\tilde{\nabla}^{2}+\lambda\right)\Phi=0\ \mbox{on}\ \mathcal{O},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{n}\cdot\tilde{\nabla}\Phi=0\ \mbox{at}\ \partial\mathcal{O},

(19)

\displaystyle\left(\tilde{\nabla}^{2}+\nu\right)\Psi=0\ \mbox{on}\ \mathcal{O},

\displaystyle\Psi=0\ \mbox{at}\ \partial\mathcal{O},

(20)

where
\tilde{\nabla}^{2}
designates the normalised Laplacian operator defined, for any function
f
, as

\tilde{\nabla}^{2}f\equiv\left(\sin\theta\right)^{-2}\left[\sin\theta\partial_{\theta}\left(\sin\theta\partial_{\theta}f\right)+\partial_{\varphi\varphi}f\right].

(21)

The solutions of the colatitudinal component of Eqs. (
19
) and (
20
) are the associated Legendre functions of the first kind (ALFs) of real degrees, which are detailed in Appendix
B
. The corresponding eigenfunctions
\Phi_{k}
are associated with the eigenvalues
\lambda_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
, and the eigenfunctions
\Psi_{k}
with the eigenvalues
\nu_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
, the relationship between the real degrees of the ALFs and the eigenvalues being detailed in Appendix
C
(see Table
5
). These eigenfunctions are obtained by multiplying the ALFs to the solutions of the longitudinal component of Eqs. (
19
) and (
20
), namely
\exp\left(\pm im\varphi\right)
, with
i
being the imaginary number. The outcome is a set of functions known as spherical cap harmonics
(
Haines 1985
;
Hwang & Chen 1997
;
Thébault et al. 2004
;
Thébault et al. 2006
)
, and denoted by ‘SCHs’ in the following (see Eq. (
142
), in Appendix
C
). Thus, the set
\left\{\Phi_{k}\right\}_{1\leq k\leq\infty}
is refered to as the ‘SCHNs’ (Neumann condition), and the set
\left\{\Psi_{k}\right\}_{1\leq k\leq\infty}
as the ‘SCHDs’ (Dirichlet condition). For example, Figure
2
shows the first basis functions of the two sets for a supercontinent of angular radius
\theta_{{\rm c}}=10^{\degree}
(i.e.
\theta_{0}=170^{\degree}
).

One shall notice that the SCHs and their associated eigenvalues both depend on the boundary condition applied at the coastline, meaning that the sets
\left\{\Phi_{k}\right\}_{1\leq k\leq\infty}
and
\left\{\Psi_{k}\right\}_{1\leq k\leq\infty}
are necessarily different by construction. In each set, the basis functions are orthogonal to each other and normalised so that

\int_{\mathcal{O}}\overline{\Phi_{k}}\Phi_{j}{\rm d}S=\int_{\mathcal{O}}\overline{\Psi_{k}}\Psi_{j}{\rm d}S=\delta_{k,j},

(22)

where
\delta_{k,j}
designates the Kronecker delta function, such that
\delta_{k,j}=1
for
k=j
and
\delta_{k,j}=0
otherwise. However, two functions belonging to different sets are not orthogonal in the general case,

\int_{\mathcal{O}}\overline{\Phi_{k}}\Psi_{j}{\rm d}S\neq 0,

(23)

since the sets of basis functions
\left\{\Phi_{k}\right\}_{1\leq k\leq\infty}
and
\left\{\Psi_{k}\right\}_{1\leq k\leq\infty}
are not the same.

Introducing the time-dependent coefficients
p_{k}
and
p_{-k}
, we write down the divergent potential function and the streamfunction as

\displaystyle\Phi\left(\theta,\varphi,\tilde{t}\right)=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}p_{k}\left(\tilde{t}\right)\Phi_{k}\left(\theta,\varphi\right),

(24)

\displaystyle\Psi\left(\theta,\varphi,\tilde{t}\right)=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}p_{-k}\left(\tilde{t}\right)\Psi_{k}\left(\theta,\varphi\right).

(25)

By substituting Eq. (
24
) into Eq. (
13
), the continuity equation becomes

\tilde{\zeta}+\tilde{\nabla}^{2}\Phi=0,

(26)

which implies that

\tilde{\zeta}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{k}p_{k}\Phi_{k}.

(27)

However, such a simple expression cannot be obtained for the equilibrium displacement (
\tilde{\zeta}_{{\rm eq}}
) in the general case since it is naturally expanded in series of spherical harmonics (hereafter, SPHs) in the coordinate system associated with the planet’s spin, which do not satisfy Dirichlet or Neumann conditions at coastlines. The form of the expression given by Eq. (
27
) can be obtained for
\tilde{\zeta}_{{\rm eq}}
only in the particular case of the hemispheric ocean (
\theta_{0}=90^{\degree}
), where the set of eigenfunctions
\left\{\Phi_{k}\right\}_{1\leq k\leq\infty}
actually corresponds to a subset of the SPHs for the hemispherical domain, as shown by
Farhat et al. 2022a
.

2.5
Temporal equations

The method used to establish the temporal differential equations for the coefficients
p_{k}
and
p_{-k}
introduced in Eqs. (
24
) and (
25
) closely follows that used for the hemispheric ocean configuration
(
Webb 1980
;
Webb 1982
;
Farhat et al. 2022a
, e.g.)
. As a first step, we substitute
\Phi
and
\Psi
by their series expansions in Eq. (
16
) and in the momentum equation given by Eq. (
12
). As a second step, we do the dot product of the equation thus obtained by
\overline{\tilde{\nabla}\Phi_{k}}
, and we integrate it over the domain of the ocean basin. It follows

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\left\{\left[\partial_{\tilde{t}\tilde{t}}+\tilde{\sigma}_{{\rm R}}\partial_{\tilde{t}}\right]p_{j}\int_{\mathcal{O}}\overline{\tilde{\nabla}\Phi_{k}}\cdot\tilde{\nabla}\Phi_{j}{\rm d}S\right.

(28)

\displaystyle+\left(\partial_{\tilde{t}\tilde{t}}+\tilde{\sigma}_{{\rm R}}\partial_{\tilde{t}}\right)p_{-j}\int_{\mathcal{O}}\overline{\tilde{\nabla}\Phi_{k}}\cdot\left(\tilde{\nabla}\Psi_{j}\times\boldsymbol{{\rm e}}_{r}\right){\rm d}S

\displaystyle+\partial_{\tilde{t}}p_{-j}\int_{\mathcal{O}}\overline{\tilde{\nabla}\Phi_{k}}\cdot\left[\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}}\times\left(\tilde{\nabla}\Psi_{j}\times\boldsymbol{{\rm e}}_{r}\right)\right]{\rm d}S

\displaystyle\left.+\partial_{\tilde{t}}p_{j}\int_{\mathcal{O}}\!\!\!\overline{\tilde{\nabla}\Phi_{k}}\cdot\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}}\times\tilde{\nabla}\Phi_{j}\right){\rm d}S\!\right\}+\tilde{\sigma}_{\rm G}^{2}\int_{\mathcal{O}}\!\overline{\tilde{\nabla}\Phi_{k}}\cdot\tilde{\nabla}\tilde{\Xi}{\rm d}S=0.

The dot product of two eigenfunctions that appears in the first term of Eq. (
28
) is computed by invoking, successively, Green’s first identity
(e.g.
Strauss 2007
, Chapter 7)
,

\int_{\mathcal{O}}\overline{\tilde{\nabla}\Phi_{k}}\cdot\tilde{\nabla}\Phi_{j}{\rm d}S=\int_{\partial\mathcal{O}}\Phi_{j}\left(\overline{\tilde{\nabla}\Phi_{k}}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}\right){\rm d}\ell-\int_{\mathcal{O}}\Phi_{j}\overline{\tilde{\nabla}^{2}\Phi_{k}}{\rm d}S,

(29)

the impermeability boundary condition on
\Phi_{k}
given by Eq. (
17
), the fact that the
\Phi_{k}
are eigenfunctions of the Laplacian operator as described by Eq. (
19
), and the orthogonality property given by Eq. (
22
). This yields

\int_{\mathcal{O}}\overline{\tilde{\nabla}\Phi_{k}}\cdot\tilde{\nabla}\Phi_{j}{\rm d}S=\lambda_{k}\delta_{k,j},

(30)

and for the forcing term,

\int_{\mathcal{O}}\overline{\tilde{\nabla}\Phi_{k}}\cdot\tilde{\nabla}\tilde{\Xi}{\rm d}S=\lambda_{k}\int_{\mathcal{O}}\overline{\Phi_{k}}\tilde{\Xi}{\rm d}S.

(31)

Since the tidal gravitational potential is expressed in terms of the SPHs associated with the coordinates
\left(\hat{\theta},\hat{\varphi}\right)
, the calculation of the above integral requires computing the transition matrix between these SPHs and the oceanic eigenfunctions. This calculation is achieved in two steps. First, one computes the rotated SPHs associated with the change of coordinates
\left(\hat{\theta},\hat{\varphi}\right)\rightarrow\left(\theta,\varphi\right)
, as detailed in Appendices
D
and
E
. Second, the transition matrices between the non-rotated SPHs and the SCHs are evaluated following the method described in Appendix
F
.

The integral of the second term in the left-hand member of Eq. (
28
) actually vanishes owing to the orthogonality property of the curl-free and divergence-free components of the horizontal displacement (Eq. (
18
)),

\int_{\mathcal{O}}\overline{\tilde{\nabla}\Phi_{k}}\cdot\left(\tilde{\nabla}\Psi_{j}\times\boldsymbol{{\rm e}}_{r}\right){\rm d}S=0.

(32)

Finally, the integrals of the third and fourth terms of Eq. (
28
) are simplified with the help of vectorial identities, yielding

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{O}}\overline{\tilde{\nabla}\Phi_{k}}\cdot\left[\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}}\times\left(\tilde{\nabla}\Psi_{j}\times\boldsymbol{{\rm e}}_{r}\right)\right]{\rm d}S=\int_{\mathcal{O}}\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}}\cdot\boldsymbol{{\rm e}}_{r}\right)\overline{\tilde{\nabla}\Phi_{k}}\cdot\tilde{\nabla}\Psi_{j}{\rm d}S,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{O}}\!\!\overline{\tilde{\nabla}\Phi_{k}}\cdot\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}}\times\tilde{\nabla}\Phi_{j}\right){\rm d}S=-\int_{\mathcal{O}}\!\!\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}}\cdot\left(\overline{\tilde{\nabla}\Phi_{k}}\times\tilde{\nabla}\Phi_{j}\right){\rm d}S.

(33)

The above simplifications lead to the first set of temporal equations for the coefficients
p_{j}
and
p_{-j}
,

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\left\{\left[\partial_{\tilde{t}\tilde{t}}+\tilde{\sigma}_{{\rm R}}\partial_{\tilde{t}}\right]p_{j}\lambda_{k}\delta_{k,j}+\partial_{\tilde{t}}p_{-j}\int_{\mathcal{O}}\!\!\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}}\cdot\boldsymbol{{\rm e}}_{r}\right)\overline{\tilde{\nabla}\Phi_{k}}\cdot\tilde{\nabla}\Psi_{j}{\rm d}S\right.

\displaystyle\left.-\partial_{\tilde{t}}p_{j}\int_{\mathcal{O}}\!\!\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}}\cdot\left(\overline{\tilde{\nabla}\Phi_{k}}\times\tilde{\nabla}\Phi_{j}\right){\rm d}S\right\}+\tilde{\sigma}_{\rm G}^{2}\lambda_{k}\int_{\mathcal{O}}\overline{\Phi_{k}}\tilde{\Xi}{\rm d}S=0.

(34)

The second set of equations is obtained by repeating the same steps with
\overline{\tilde{\nabla}\Psi_{k}}\times\boldsymbol{{\rm e}}_{r}
instead of
\overline{\tilde{\nabla}\Phi_{k}}
in the dot product operation,

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\left\{\left(\partial_{\tilde{t}\tilde{t}}+\tilde{\sigma}_{{\rm R}}\partial_{\tilde{t}}\right)p_{j}\int_{\mathcal{O}}\left(\overline{\tilde{\nabla}\Psi_{k}}\times\boldsymbol{{\rm e}}_{r}\right)\cdot\tilde{\nabla}\Phi_{j}{\rm d}S\right.

(35)

\displaystyle+\partial_{\tilde{t}}p_{j}\int_{\mathcal{O}}\left(\overline{\tilde{\nabla}\Psi_{k}}\times\boldsymbol{{\rm e}}_{r}\right)\cdot\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}}\times\tilde{\nabla}\Phi_{j}\right){\rm d}S

\displaystyle+\partial_{\tilde{t}}p_{-j}\int_{\mathcal{O}}\left(\overline{\tilde{\nabla}\Psi_{k}}\times\boldsymbol{{\rm e}}_{r}\right)\cdot\left[\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}}\times\left(\tilde{\nabla}\Psi_{j}\times\boldsymbol{{\rm e}}_{r}\right)\right]{\rm d}S

\displaystyle\left.+\left(\partial_{\tilde{t}\tilde{t}}+\tilde{\sigma}_{{\rm R}}\right)p_{-j}\int_{\mathcal{O}}\left(\overline{\tilde{\nabla}\Psi_{k}}\times\boldsymbol{{\rm e}}_{r}\right)\cdot\left(\tilde{\nabla}\Psi_{j}\times\boldsymbol{{\rm e}}_{r}\right){\rm d}S\right\}

\displaystyle-\tilde{\sigma}_{\rm G}^{2}\int_{\mathcal{O}}\boldsymbol{{\rm e}}_{r}\cdot\left(\overline{\tilde{\nabla}\Psi_{k}}\times\tilde{\nabla}\tilde{\Xi}\right){\rm d}S=0,

which, using the orthogonality properties of the eigenfunctions and the relations

\int_{\mathcal{O}}\overline{\tilde{\nabla}\Psi_{k}}\cdot\tilde{\nabla}\Psi_{j}{\rm d}S=\nu_{k}\delta_{k,j},

(36)

simplifies to

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\left\{\left(\partial_{\tilde{t}\tilde{t}}+\tilde{\sigma}_{{\rm R}}\right)p_{-j}\nu_{k}\delta_{k,j}-\partial_{\tilde{t}}p_{j}\int_{\mathcal{O}}\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}}\cdot\boldsymbol{{\rm e}}_{r}\right)\left(\overline{\tilde{\nabla}\Psi_{k}}\cdot\tilde{\nabla}\Phi_{j}\right){\rm d}S\right.

\displaystyle\left.-\partial_{\tilde{t}}p_{-j}\!\!\int_{\mathcal{O}}\!\!\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}}\!\cdot\!\left(\overline{\tilde{\nabla}\Psi_{k}}\times\tilde{\nabla}\Psi_{j}\right){\rm d}S\!\right\}\!-\tilde{\sigma}_{\rm G}^{2}\!\int_{\mathcal{O}}\!\!\boldsymbol{{\rm e}}_{r}\!\cdot\!\left(\overline{\tilde{\nabla}\Psi_{k}}\times\tilde{\nabla}\tilde{\Xi}\right)\!{\rm d}S=0.

(37)

Thus, following the formalism used in earlier studies
(
Webb 1980
;
Webb 1982
;
Farhat et al. 2022a
)
, Eqs. (
34
) and (
37
) form an infinite linear system in the coefficients
p_{k}
and
p_{-k}
, written as

\left(\partial_{\tilde{t}\tilde{t}}+\tilde{\sigma}_{{\rm R}}\partial_{\tilde{t}}\right)p_{k}+\lambda_{k}^{-1}\!\!\!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=-\infty\\
j\neq 0\end{subarray}}^{+\infty}\!\!\beta_{k,j}\partial_{\tilde{t}}p_{j}+\tilde{\sigma}_{\rm G}^{2}\int_{\mathcal{O}}\!\!\overline{\Phi_{k}}\tilde{\Xi}{\rm d}S=0,

(38)

\displaystyle\left(\partial_{\tilde{t}\tilde{t}}+\tilde{\sigma}_{{\rm R}}\partial_{\tilde{t}}\right)p_{-k}+\nu_{k}^{-1}\!\!\!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=-\infty\\
j\neq 0\end{subarray}}^{+\infty}\!\!\beta_{-k,j}\partial_{\tilde{t}}p_{j}

(39)

\displaystyle-\frac{\tilde{\sigma}_{\rm G}^{2}}{\nu_{k}}\int_{\mathcal{O}}\boldsymbol{{\rm e}}_{r}\cdot\left(\overline{\tilde{\nabla}{\Psi_{k}}}\times\tilde{\nabla}\tilde{\Xi}\right){\rm d}S

\displaystyle=0.

In the above equations, the symbols
\beta_{k,j}
designate the so-called ‘gyroscopic coefficients’
(
Proudman 1920
;
Longuet-Higgins & Pond 1970
;
Webb 1980
, e.g.)
,

\displaystyle\beta_{k,j}\equiv

\displaystyle-\int_{\mathcal{O}}\cos\hat{\theta}\,\boldsymbol{{\rm e}}_{r}\cdot\left(\overline{\nabla\Phi_{k}}\times\nabla\Phi_{j}\right){\rm d}S,

(40)

\displaystyle\beta_{k,-j}\equiv

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \int_{\mathcal{O}}\cos\hat{\theta}\left(\overline{\nabla\Phi_{k}}\cdot\nabla\Psi_{j}\right){\rm d}S,

(41)

\displaystyle\beta_{-k,j}\equiv

\displaystyle-\int_{\mathcal{O}}\cos\hat{\theta}\left(\overline{\nabla\Psi_{k}}\cdot\nabla\Phi_{j}\right){\rm d}S,

(42)

\displaystyle\beta_{-k,-j}\equiv

\displaystyle-\int_{\mathcal{O}}\cos\hat{\theta}\,\boldsymbol{{\rm e}}_{r}\cdot\left(\overline{\nabla\Psi_{k}}\times\nabla\Psi_{j}\right){\rm d}S.

(43)

These coefficients account for the coupling effect of the Coriolis terms and the geometry of the ocean basin, which affects how the gradients of the eigenfunctions overlap. Their behaviour and properties are examined in Appendix
G
. The expression of the forcing term in Eq. (
39
) is given here for generality. This term is actually zero, as discussed in the next section, meaning that Eq. (
39
) does not depend on
\tilde{\Xi}
.

2.6
Ocean loading and self-attraction variation

At this stage, we still have to express
\tilde{\Xi}
as a function of
p_{j}
and
p_{-j}
. To do so, we consider the fact that the gravitational tidal force acts on the planet as a periodic perturbation oscillating in time and longitude. Therefore, the tidal gravitational potential given by Eq. (
4
) can be expanded in Fourier series of time and series of SPHs. In the general case,
U_{\rm T}
is expressed as

U_{\rm T}=V_{0}^{2}\Re\left\{\sum_{\sigma}\sum_{l=2}^{+\infty}\sum_{m=-l}^{l}\tilde{U}_{{\rm T};l}^{m,\sigma}\hat{Y}_{l}^{m}\left(\hat{\theta},\hat{\varphi}\right){\rm e}^{i\sigma t}\right\},

(44)

where
\sigma
is the tidal frequency,
V_{0}
the reference velocity introduced in Eq. (
10
),
\hat{Y}_{l}^{m}
the complex SPH of degree
l
and order
m
associated with the coordinate system
\left(\hat{\theta},\hat{\varphi}\right)
, defined in Eq. (
132
), and
\tilde{U}_{{\rm T};l}^{m,\sigma}\in\mathbb{C}
the associated frequency-dependent normalised component of the tidal gravitational potential. In the following, these notations are shortened for convenience. Similarly as the eigenfunctions of the ocean basin,
\Phi_{k}
and
\Psi_{k}
, the SPHs are simply denoted by
\hat{Y}_{k}=\hat{Y}_{l_{k}}^{m_{k}}
, and the corresponding coefficients by
\tilde{U}^{\sigma}_{{{\rm T}};k}=U_{{\rm T};l_{k}}^{m_{k},\sigma}
, the index
k
referring to an element of the set of SPHs,
\left\{\hat{Y}_{k}\right\}_{1\leq k\leq\infty}
. Moreover, all the indices used from now on (
k
,
j
,
n
,
q
) are supposed to run from one to infinity when bounds are not specified. It is noteworthy that the Fourier series expansion in Eq. (
44
) can always be written in term of positive tidal frequencies as long as
m
runs from
-l
to
l
. Therefore, we assume that
\sigma\geq 0
, and that the frequencies are all different from each other (no resonance).

Since the components associated with two different tidal frequencies are not correlated in the linear tidal theory, the resulting tidal responses can be treated separately. We thus consider the contribution of the component associated with a given tidal frequency,
\tilde{U}_{{\rm T}}^{\sigma}
, and the associated forcing term,
\tilde{\Xi}^{\sigma}
, expressed as

\displaystyle\tilde{U}_{{\rm T}}^{\sigma}=\sum_{j}\tilde{U}^{\sigma}_{{{\rm T}};j}\hat{Y}_{j}\left(\hat{\theta},\hat{\varphi}\right),

\displaystyle\tilde{\Xi}^{\sigma}=\sum_{j}\tilde{\Xi}^{\sigma}_{j}\hat{Y}_{j}\left(\hat{\theta},\hat{\varphi}\right).

(45)

As highlighted in earlier studies
(
Matsuyama 2014
;
Matsuyama et al. 2018
;
Auclair-Desrotour et al. 2019a
, e.g.)
the combined contribution of ocean loading, self-attraction variation, and deformation of solid regions may be formulated as

\tilde{\Xi}^{\sigma}_{j}=\gamma_{{\rm D};l_{j}}^{\sigma}\left(\sum_{n}\langle\hat{Y}_{j},\Phi_{n}\rangle\lambda_{n}p_{n}\right)-\gamma_{{\rm T};l_{j}}^{\sigma}\tilde{U}^{\sigma}_{{{\rm T}};j},

(46)

where we have made use of the scalar product defined by Eq. (
135
) and introduced the solid deformation factors,
\gamma_{{\rm D};l}^{\sigma}
and
\gamma_{{\rm T};l}^{\sigma}

(
Hendershott 1972
, see e.g.)
,

\displaystyle\gamma_{{\rm T};l}^{\sigma}

\displaystyle\equiv 1+k_{l}^{\sigma}-h_{l}^{\sigma},

(47)

\displaystyle\gamma_{{\rm D};l}^{\sigma}

\displaystyle\equiv 1-\left(1+k_{{\rm L};l}^{\sigma}-h_{{\rm L};l}^{\sigma}\right)\frac{3}{\left(2l+1\right)}\frac{\rho_{{\rm w}}}{\overline{\rho}}.

(48)

The factors
\gamma_{{\rm D};l}^{\sigma}
and
\gamma_{{\rm T};l}^{\sigma}
are independent of the order
m
since they are expressed as functions of the Love numbers of the solid part, which is commonly assumed to be spherically symmetric in the tidal theory. The parameters
k_{l}^{\sigma}
and
h_{l}^{\sigma}
in the first expression are known as the tidal gravitational and displacement Love numbers, respectively. They describe the response of the solid body to a gravitational tidal forcing, which takes the form of a self-attraction variation and a surface displacement. By analogy, the loading Love numbers
k_{{\rm L};l}^{\sigma}
and
h_{{\rm L};l}^{\sigma}
account for the gravitational and mechanical responses of solid regions to the cumulated gravitational and pressure forces generated by the oceanic mass redistribution, respectively. The second solid deformation factor,
\gamma_{{\rm T};l}^{\sigma}
, also depends on the ratio of seawater density to the mean density of the solid regions,
\overline{\rho}
. We remark that
\gamma_{{\rm D};l}^{\sigma}
and
\gamma_{{\rm T};l}^{\sigma}
are sometimes considered as real factors
(
Matsuyama 2014
;
Motoyama et al. 2020
, e.g.)
, which corresponds to an adiabatic elastic response of the solid part, where dissipative mechanisms are ignored. However, the correspondence principle established by
Biot 1954
makes it possible to treat dissipative anelastic cases similarly as long as the anelasticity is linear. As a consequence, the expressions given by Eqs. (
47
) and (
48
) can be extended to any rheological model including dissipative processes. In that case, the solid Love numbers are complex transfer functions describing the visco-elastic response of solid regions when subjected to a harmonic tidal force
(
Remus et al. 2012
;
Auclair-Desrotour et al. 2019a
;
Farhat et al. 2022b
)
.

Using the expression given by Eq. (
46
) and proceeding to a change of basis functions between the oceanic eigenfunctions and the SPHs associated with the coordinate system
\left(\hat{\theta},\hat{\varphi}\right)
, we expand the integrals depending on the forcing in Eqs. (
38
) and (
39
) in series of
p_{j}
and components of the tidal gravitational potential,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{O}}\overline{\Phi_{k}}\tilde{\Xi}{\rm d}S=

\displaystyle\sum_{n}\left[\sum_{j}\langle\Phi_{k},\hat{Y}_{j}\rangle\gamma_{{\rm D};l_{j}}^{\sigma}\langle\hat{Y}_{j},\Phi_{n}\rangle\right]\lambda_{n}p_{n}

(49)

\displaystyle-\sum_{j}\langle\Phi_{k},\hat{Y}_{j}\rangle\gamma_{{\rm T};l_{j}}^{\sigma}\tilde{U}^{\sigma}_{{{\rm T}};j},

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{O}}\!\!\boldsymbol{{\rm e}}_{r}\!\cdot\!\left(\overline{\tilde{\nabla}\Psi_{k}}\times\tilde{\nabla}\tilde{\Xi}\right)\!{\rm d}S=

\displaystyle\sum_{n}\left[\sum_{j,q}\upsilon_{k,q}\langle Y_{q},\hat{Y}_{j}\rangle\gamma_{{\rm D};l_{j}}^{\sigma}\langle\hat{Y}_{j},\Phi_{n}\rangle\right]\lambda_{n}p_{n}

\displaystyle-\sum_{j,q}\upsilon_{k,q}\langle Y_{q},\hat{Y}_{j}\rangle\gamma_{{\rm T};l_{j}}^{\sigma}\tilde{U}^{\sigma}_{{{\rm T}};j},

(50)

where
\upsilon_{k,q}
designates the coefficients defined as

\upsilon_{k,q}\equiv\int_{\mathcal{O}}\!\!\boldsymbol{{\rm e}}_{r}\!\cdot\!\left(\overline{\tilde{\nabla}\Psi_{k}}\times\tilde{\nabla}Y_{q}\right)\!{\rm d}S.

(51)

These coefficients are all zero as shown in Appendix
G
, which implies that

\int_{\mathcal{O}}\!\!\boldsymbol{{\rm e}}_{r}\!\cdot\!\left(\overline{\tilde{\nabla}\Psi_{k}}\times\tilde{\nabla}\tilde{\Xi}\right)\!{\rm d}S=0.

(52)

We remark that the quadrupolar component of the tidal gravitational potential (the component associated with the
\hat{Y}_{2}^{2}
SPH) is far greater than the components of higher degrees if the size of the planet is small compared with the planet-perturber distance. These components can thus be ignored in standard two-body systems. The series in the formulation of the tidal gravitational given by Eq. (
45
) then reduces to one term only. This removes the summation over
j
in the second term of the right-hand member of Eq. (
49
).

2.7
The tidal solution

As a last step, the tidal equations given by Eqs. (
38
) and (
39
) are written down in the Fourier domain. To compute the solution numerically, all the sets of basis functions are truncated, meaning that the infinite spaces of functions they describe are approximated by finite spaces of functions. As the tidal gravitational tidal potential varies over planetary length scales, the tidal response is essentially described by the eigenmodes of largest wavelengths. As a consequence, the truncation does not alter much the solution as long as the number of eigenmodes of the set is sufficiently large. The numbers of basis functions for the sets
\left\{\Phi_{j}\right\}
,
\left\{\Psi_{j}\right\}
, and
\left\{\hat{Y}_{j}\right\}
are denoted by
M
,
N
, and
K
, respectively, while the vectors describing the tidal response and the tidal gravitational potential in these sets are expressed as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Phi}

\displaystyle=\left[p_{1},\ldots,p_{j},\ldots,p_{M}\right]^{\rm T},

(53)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Psi}

\displaystyle=\left[p_{-1},\ldots,p_{-j},\ldots,p_{-N}\right]^{\rm T},

(54)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\tilde{U}}_{{\rm T}}

\displaystyle=\left[\tilde{U}^{\sigma}_{{{\rm T}};1},\ldots,\tilde{U}^{\sigma}_{{{\rm T}};j},\ldots,\tilde{U}^{\sigma}_{{{\rm T}};K}\right]^{\rm T},

(55)

with
T
designating the transpose of a matrix. Converted into an algebraic form, the forcing term given by Eq. (
49
) is written as

\int_{\mathcal{O}}\overline{\Phi_{k}}\tilde{\Xi}{\rm d}S=\boldsymbol{E}_{M,k}^{\rm T}\left(\boldsymbol{A}_{\Phi}\boldsymbol{\Phi}+\boldsymbol{R}_{\Phi}\boldsymbol{\tilde{U}}_{{\rm T}}\right),

(56)

where we have introduced the unit vectors
\boldsymbol{E}_{M,k}
defined as

\boldsymbol{E}_{M,k}\equiv\left[\delta_{1,k},\ldots,\delta_{j,k},\ldots,\delta_{M,k}\right]^{\rm T},

(57)

and the matrices
\boldsymbol{A}_{\Phi}
, and
\boldsymbol{R}_{\Phi}
,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{A}_{\Phi}=\overline{\boldsymbol{P}_{\hat{Y},\Phi}}^{\rm T}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{{\rm D}}\boldsymbol{P}_{\hat{Y},\Phi}\boldsymbol{\Lambda},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{R}_{\Phi}=-\overline{\boldsymbol{P}_{\hat{Y},\Phi}}^{\rm T}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{{\rm T}}.

(58)

In the above expressions,
\boldsymbol{P}_{\hat{Y},\Phi}
designates the transition matrix from the SPHs
\hat{Y}_{k}
to the eigenfunctions
\Phi_{k}
. The other matrices are defined as

\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{{\rm D}}\equiv\begin{bmatrix}\gamma_{{\rm D};l_{1}}^{\sigma}&0&\ldots&\ldots&0\\
0&\ddots&\ddots&&\vdots\\
\vdots&\ddots&\gamma_{{\rm D};l_{j}}^{\sigma}&\ddots&\vdots\\
\vdots&&\ddots&\ddots&0\\
0&\ldots&\ldots&0&\gamma_{{\rm D};l_{K}}^{\sigma}\end{bmatrix},

(59)

\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{{\rm T}}\equiv\begin{bmatrix}\gamma_{{\rm T};l_{1}}^{\sigma}&0&\ldots&\ldots&0\\
0&\ddots&\ddots&&\vdots\\
\vdots&\ddots&\gamma_{{\rm T};l_{j}}^{\sigma}&\ddots&\vdots\\
\vdots&&\ddots&\ddots&0\\
0&\ldots&\ldots&0&\gamma_{{\rm T};l_{K}}^{\sigma}\end{bmatrix},

(60)

\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\equiv\begin{bmatrix}\lambda_{1}&0&\ldots&\ldots&0\\
0&\ddots&\ddots&&\vdots\\
\vdots&\ddots&\lambda_{j}&\ddots&\vdots\\
\vdots&&\ddots&\ddots&0\\
0&\ldots&\ldots&0&\lambda_{M}\end{bmatrix},

(61)

\boldsymbol{N}\equiv\begin{bmatrix}\nu_{1}&0&\ldots&\ldots&0\\
0&\ddots&\ddots&&\vdots\\
\vdots&\ddots&\nu_{j}&\ddots&\vdots\\
\vdots&&\ddots&\ddots&0\\
0&\ldots&\ldots&0&\nu_{N}\end{bmatrix},

(62)

Also, we introduce the matrix of gyroscopic coefficients

\boldsymbol{B}\equiv\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{-1}\boldsymbol{B}_{\Phi,\Phi}&\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{-1}\boldsymbol{B}_{\Phi,\Psi}\\
\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}\boldsymbol{B}_{\Psi,\Phi}&\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}\boldsymbol{B}_{\Psi,\Psi}\end{bmatrix},

(63)

where the block matrices
\boldsymbol{B}_{\Phi,\Phi}
,
\boldsymbol{B}_{\Phi,\Psi}
,
\boldsymbol{B}_{\Psi,\Phi}
and
\boldsymbol{B}_{\Psi,\Psi}
are defined in terms of the
\beta_{k,j}
defined in Eqs. (
40
-
43
) as

\boldsymbol{B}_{\Phi,\Phi}\equiv\begin{bmatrix}\beta_{1,1}&\ldots&\beta_{1,j}&\ldots&\beta_{1,M}\\
\vdots&&\vdots&&\vdots\\
\beta_{k,1}&\ldots&\beta_{k,j}&\ldots&\beta_{k,M}\\
\vdots&&\vdots&&\vdots\\
\beta_{M,1}&\ldots&\beta_{M,j}&\ldots&\beta_{M,M}\end{bmatrix},

(64)

\boldsymbol{B}_{\Phi,\Psi}\equiv\begin{bmatrix}\beta_{1,-1}&\ldots&\beta_{1,-j}&\ldots&\beta_{1,-N}\\
\vdots&&\vdots&&\vdots\\
\beta_{k,-1}&\ldots&\beta_{k,-j}&\ldots&\beta_{k,-N}\\
\vdots&&\vdots&&\vdots\\
\beta_{M,-1}&\ldots&\beta_{M,-j}&\ldots&\beta_{M,-N}\end{bmatrix},

(65)

\boldsymbol{B}_{\Psi,\Phi}\equiv\begin{bmatrix}\beta_{-1,1}&\ldots&\beta_{-1,j}&\ldots&\beta_{-1,M}\\
\vdots&&\vdots&&\vdots\\
\beta_{-k,1}&\ldots&\beta_{-k,j}&\ldots&\beta_{-k,M}\\
\vdots&&\vdots&&\vdots\\
\beta_{-N,1}&\ldots&\beta_{-N,j}&\ldots&\beta_{-N,M}\end{bmatrix}=-\overline{\boldsymbol{B}_{\Phi,\Psi}}^{\rm T},

(66)

\boldsymbol{B}_{\Psi,\Psi}\equiv\begin{bmatrix}\beta_{-1,-1}&\ldots&\beta_{-1,-j}&\ldots&\beta_{-1,-N}\\
\vdots&&\vdots&&\vdots\\
\beta_{-k,-1}&\ldots&\beta_{-k,-j}&\ldots&\beta_{-k,-N}\\
\vdots&&\vdots&&\vdots\\
\beta_{-N,-1}&\ldots&\beta_{-N,-j}&\ldots&\beta_{-N,-N}\end{bmatrix}.

(67)

Finally, the matrix accounting for the gravitational and pressure effects of the oceanic surface displacement,
\boldsymbol{A}
, the matrix describing the coupling between the forcing tidal gravitational potential and the oceanic eigenmodes,
\boldsymbol{R}
, and the solution vector,
\boldsymbol{X}
, are respectively expressed as

\begin{array}[]{lll}\boldsymbol{A}\equiv\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{A}_{\Phi}&0\\
0&0\end{bmatrix},&\boldsymbol{R}\equiv\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{R}_{\Phi}\\
0\end{bmatrix},&\boldsymbol{X}\equiv\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\Phi}\\
\boldsymbol{\Psi}\end{bmatrix}.\end{array}

(68)

The temporal tidal equations given by Eqs. (
38
) and (
39
) thus lead to the solution

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{X}=\boldsymbol{H}\left(\tilde{\sigma},\tilde{\sigma}_{{\rm R}},\tilde{\sigma}_{\rm G},\theta_{{\rm c}},\hat{\theta}_{{\rm S}};\gamma_{{\rm D};l}^{\sigma},\gamma_{{\rm T};l}^{\sigma}\right)\boldsymbol{\tilde{U}}_{{\rm T}},

(69)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{H}\equiv\tilde{\sigma}_{\rm G}^{2}\left[\tilde{\sigma}\left(\tilde{\sigma}-i\tilde{\sigma}_{{\rm R}}\right)\boldsymbol{I}-i\tilde{\sigma}\boldsymbol{B}-\tilde{\sigma}_{\rm G}^{2}\boldsymbol{A}\right]^{-1}\boldsymbol{R},

(70)

with
\boldsymbol{I}
being the identity matrix. In the above expression, the oceanic tidal response to the tidal perturbation in the Fourier domain is expressed as a complex frequency-dependent matrix (
\boldsymbol{H}
), which links the tidal gravitational potential (
\boldsymbol{\tilde{U}}_{{\rm T}}
) to the potential and stream functions that describe the horizontal tidal displacement (
\boldsymbol{X}
).

2.8
Tidal Love numbers, torque, and power

To close this theoretical section, it remains to introduce the parameters that quantify the tidally dissipated energy and its effect on the evolution of planetary systems. The tidal mass redistribution itself is quantified by self-attraction variations, which is derived from the gravitational potential of the tidally distorted body,
U_{{\rm D}}
, defined at its surface as

U_{{\rm D}}=V_{0}^{2}\Re\left\{\sum_{\sigma}\sum_{l=0}^{+\infty}\sum_{m=-l}^{l}\tilde{U}_{{\rm D};l}^{m,\sigma}\hat{Y}_{l}^{m}\left(\hat{\theta},\hat{\varphi}\right){\rm e}^{i\sigma t}\right\}.

(71)

The components
\tilde{U}_{{\rm D};l}^{m,\sigma}
of this gravitational potential are expressed as

\tilde{U}_{{\rm D};l}^{m,\sigma}=k_{l}^{\sigma}\tilde{U}_{{\rm T};l}^{m,\sigma}+\left(1+k_{{\rm L};l}^{\sigma}\right)\frac{3}{2l+1}\frac{\rho_{{\rm w}}}{\overline{\rho}}\tilde{\zeta}_{l}^{m,\sigma},

(72)

where
\tilde{U}_{{\rm T};l}^{m,\sigma}
is the corresponding component of the forcing tidal potential introduced in Eq. (
44
), and
\tilde{\zeta}_{l}^{m,\sigma}
the corresponding component of the normalised surface elevation,

\tilde{\zeta}_{l}^{m,\sigma}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\langle\hat{Y}_{l}^{m},\Phi_{k}\rangle\lambda_{k}p_{k}.

(73)

The first term of Eq. (
72
) is the self-attraction variation due to the distortion of the solid part generated by the gravitational tidal force, while the second term is the contribution of the ocean loading, which includes both the potential generated by the oceanic mass redistribution and the potential resulting from the distortion of the solid part induced by ocean loading.

The complex Love numbers are the transfer functions accounting for the intrinsic response of an extended body undergoing a tidal gravitational forcing
(
Ogilvie 2014
, e.g.)
. They are defined, for each SPH, as the ratio of the gravitational potential of the tidal response to the forcing tidal potential evaluated at the body’s surface,

k_{{\rm D};l}^{m,\sigma}\left(\tilde{\sigma},\tilde{\sigma}_{{\rm R}},\tilde{\sigma}_{\rm G},\theta_{{\rm c}},\hat{\theta}_{{\rm S}};\gamma_{{\rm D};l}^{\sigma},\gamma_{{\rm T};l}^{\sigma}\right)\equiv\frac{\tilde{U}_{{\rm D};l}^{m,\sigma}}{\tilde{U}_{{\rm T};l}^{m,\sigma}},

(74)

which, using the expression of the tidal potential given by Eq. (
72
), yields

k_{{\rm D};l}^{m,\sigma}=k_{l}^{\sigma}+\left(1+k_{{\rm L};l}^{\sigma}\right)\frac{3}{2l+1}\frac{\rho_{{\rm w}}}{\overline{\rho}}\frac{\tilde{\zeta}_{l}^{m,\sigma}}{\tilde{U}_{{\rm T};l}^{m,\sigma}}.

(75)

Considering the above equation, we note that the Love number depends both on the order
m
and the degree
l
of the associated SPH in the general case contrary to the solid Love numbers, which only depend on the degrees (see Eqs. (
47
) and (
48
)). This is due to the fact that the oceanic tidal response is not spherically symmetric owing to the geometry of coastlines and Coriolis effects.

The long-term effect of tides on the evolution of the planet-perturber system is quantified by the generated tidal torques. The main contributor to this evolution is the time-averaged tidal torque exerted about the spin axis of the planet
(
Zahn 1966
)
,

\mathcal{T}_{z}\equiv\left\langle\int_{\mathcal{V}}\partial_{\hat{\varphi}}U_{\rm T}\delta\rho{\rm d}V\right\rangle,

(76)

which affects the long-term variation rate of the planet-perturber distance and the planet’s spin angular velocity
(
Ogilvie 2014
, e.g.)
. In the above equation,
\mathcal{V}
designates the volume filled by the planet,
{\rm d}V
an infinitesimal volume parcel, and
\delta\rho
the local density variations associated with the tidal response. These density variations are related through Poisson’s equation to the tidal gravitational potential of the distorted planet,
U_{{\rm D}}
, namely

\nabla^{2}U_{{\rm D}}=-4\pi G\delta\rho.

(77)

This allows the tidal torque to be expressed in terms of the forcing and deformation tidal gravitational potentials,

\mathcal{T}_{z}=-\frac{1}{4\pi G}\left\langle\int_{\mathcal{V}}\partial_{\hat{\varphi}}U_{\rm T}\nabla^{2}U_{{\rm D}}{\rm d}V\right\rangle.

(78)

The latter integral is defined over the volume filled by the tidally deformed planet (
\mathcal{V}
), but it can actually be carried out over any region that includes this volume. By making use of this property,
Ogilvie 2013
demonstrates that both the tidal torque and the tidally dissipated power are straightforwardly related to the Fourier components of the two gravitational potentials. As a first step, one shall notice that
\nabla^{2}U_{\rm T}=0
outside of the perturber, by virtue of Poisson’s equation, which implies that

\nabla^{2}\left(\partial_{\hat{\varphi}}U_{\rm T}\right)=\partial_{\hat{\varphi}}\left(\nabla^{2}U_{\rm T}\right)=0,

(79)

since the Laplacian and
\partial_{\hat{\varphi}}
operators can be permuted. Let
\mathcal{V}_{*}
be any region that is simply connected and does not include the perturber. Then, the fact that
\nabla^{2}\left(\partial_{\hat{\varphi}}U_{\rm T}\right)=0
in
\mathcal{V}_{*}
allows the integral of Eq. (
78
) to be rewritten as

\int_{\mathcal{V}_{*}}\!\!\!\partial_{\varphi}U_{\rm T}\nabla^{2}U_{{\rm D}}{\rm d}V=\int_{\mathcal{V}_{*}}\!\!\!\left[\partial_{\hat{\varphi}}U_{\rm T}\nabla^{2}U_{{\rm D}}-U_{{\rm D}}\nabla^{2}\left(\partial_{\hat{\varphi}}U_{\rm T}\right)\right]{\rm d}V.

(80)

By virtue of Green’s second identity
(e.g.
Arfken & Weber 2005
, Sect. 1.11)
, Eq. (
80
) yields

\int_{\mathcal{V}_{*}}\!\!\!\partial_{\varphi}U_{\rm T}\nabla^{2}U_{{\rm D}}{\rm d}V=\oint_{\partial\mathcal{V}_{*}}\!\!\!\left[\partial_{\hat{\varphi}}U_{\rm T}\nabla U_{{\rm D}}-U_{{\rm D}}\nabla\left(\partial_{\hat{\varphi}}U_{\rm T}\right)\right]\cdot{\rm d}\boldsymbol{S},

(81)

the notation
\partial\mathcal{V}_{*}
referring to the boundary of the domain
\mathcal{V}_{*}
, and
{\rm d}\boldsymbol{S}
to and outward pointing infinitesimal surface element vector.

By applying the time-averaging operator to the integral we obtain

\mathcal{T}_{z}=\sum_{\sigma}\frac{V_{0}^{4}}{8\pi G}\Re\left\{\oint_{\partial\mathcal{V}_{*}}\!\!\!\left[\overline{\tilde{U}_{{\rm D}}^{\sigma}}\nabla\left(\partial_{\hat{\varphi}}\tilde{U}_{{\rm T}}^{\sigma}\right)-\overline{\partial_{\hat{\varphi}}\tilde{U}_{{\rm T}}^{\sigma}}\nabla\tilde{U}_{{\rm D}}^{\sigma}\right]\cdot{\rm d}\boldsymbol{S}\right\},

(82)

with
\tilde{U}_{{\rm T}}^{\sigma}
and
\tilde{U}_{{\rm D}}^{\sigma}
defined as

\displaystyle U_{\rm T}\left(r,\hat{\theta},\hat{\varphi},t\right)=V_{0}^{2}\Re\left\{\sum_{\sigma}\tilde{U}_{{\rm T}}^{\sigma}\left(r,\hat{\theta},\hat{\varphi}\right){\rm e}^{i\sigma t}\right\},

(83)

\displaystyle U_{{\rm D}}\left(r,\hat{\theta},\hat{\varphi},t\right)=V_{0}^{2}\Re\left\{\sum_{\sigma}\tilde{U}_{{\rm D}}^{\sigma}\left(r,\hat{\theta},\hat{\varphi}\right){\rm e}^{i\sigma t}\right\}.

(84)

In practice,
\mathcal{V}_{*}
can be chosen such that its boundary is a sphere of some radius
r_{*}
centred on the planet.

In the vicinity of
\partial\mathcal{V}_{*}
, the forcing tidal potential and the deformation tidal potential can be represented using interior and exterior multipole expansions in SPHs, respectively,

\displaystyle\tilde{U}_{{\rm T}}^{\sigma}\left(r,\hat{\theta},\hat{\varphi}\right)=

\displaystyle\sum_{l=2}^{+\infty}\sum_{m=-l}^{l}\tilde{U}_{{\rm T};l}^{m,\sigma}\left(\frac{r}{R_{\rm p}}\right)^{l}\hat{Y}_{l}^{m}\left(\hat{\theta},\hat{\varphi}\right),

(85)

\displaystyle\tilde{U}_{{\rm D}}^{\sigma}\left(r,\hat{\theta},\hat{\varphi}\right)=

\displaystyle\sum_{l=0}^{+\infty}\sum_{m=-l}^{l}\tilde{U}_{{\rm D};l}^{m,\sigma}\left(\frac{r}{R_{\rm p}}\right)^{-\left(l+1\right)}\hat{Y}_{l}^{m}\left(\hat{\theta},\hat{\varphi}\right).

(86)

By substituting Eqs. (
85
) and (
86
) into Eq. (
82
), and for arbitrary radius
r_{*}
, we end up with the formulation of the torque as a function of the imaginary part of Love numbers,

\mathcal{T}_{z}=\sum_{\sigma}\sum_{l=2}^{+\infty}\sum_{m=-l}^{l}\frac{m\left(2l+1\right)R_{\rm p}V_{0}^{4}}{8\pi G}\left|\tilde{U}_{{\rm T};l}^{m,\sigma}\right|^{2}\Im\left\{k_{{\rm D};l}^{m,\sigma}\right\}.

(87)

For the quadrupolar tidal gravitational potential
(
Ogilvie 2014
, e.g.)
,

\tilde{U}_{{\rm T};2}^{2,\sigma}=\sqrt{\frac{6\pi}{5}}\left(\frac{GM_{{\rm s}}}{aV_{0}^{2}}\right)\left(\frac{R_{\rm p}}{a}\right)^{2},

(88)

with
a
being the semi-major axis of the perturber, we recover the well-known relationship between the tidal torque and the quadrupolar Love number
(
Makarov & Efroimsky 2013
;
Auclair-Desrotour et al. 2019a
, e.g.)
,

\mathcal{T}_{z;2}=\frac{3}{2}GM_{{\rm s}}^{2}\frac{R_{\rm p}^{5}}{a^{6}}\Im\left\{k_{{\rm D};2}^{2,\sigma}\right\}.

(89)

The time-averaged power input by the tidal force can be determined in a similar way. This power is defined as the work per unit time done by the tidal force on the tidal motions
(
Ogilvie 2013
, e.g.)
,

\mathcal{P}_{{\rm T}}\equiv\left\langle\int_{\mathcal{V}_{*}}\rho\boldsymbol{V}\cdot\nabla U_{\rm T}{\rm d}V\right\rangle,

(90)

where
\rho
is the local density of the planet. We note that
\boldsymbol{V}
designates here the velocity field of tidal motions in the whole planet and not only in the ocean basin. By proceeding to an integration by parts, we rewrite the above expression as

\mathcal{P}_{{\rm T}}=\oint_{\partial\mathcal{V}_{*}}U_{\rm T}\rho\boldsymbol{V}\cdot{\rm d}\boldsymbol{S}-\int_{\mathcal{V}_{*}}U_{\rm T}\nabla\cdot\left(\rho\boldsymbol{V}\right){\rm d}V.

(91)

The first term of the right-hand member in Eq. (
91
) is zero since the boundary
\partial\mathcal{V}_{*}
is outside of the planet, where
\rho=0
. By combining together the continuity equation,

\partial_{t}\delta\rho+\nabla\cdot\left(\rho\boldsymbol{V}\right)=0,

(92)

and Poisson’s equation, given by Eq. (
77
), we express
\nabla\cdot\left(\rho\boldsymbol{V}\right)
as a function of the deformation tidal gravitational potential,

\nabla\cdot\left(\rho\boldsymbol{V}\right)=\frac{1}{4\pi G}\nabla^{2}\left(\partial_{t}U_{{\rm D}}\right),

(93)

which yields

\mathcal{P}_{{\rm T}}=-\frac{1}{4\pi G}\left\langle\int_{\mathcal{V}_{*}}U_{\rm T}\nabla^{2}\left(\partial_{t}U_{{\rm D}}\right){\rm d}V\right\rangle.

(94)

Using Green’s second identity, the above expression becomes

\mathcal{P}_{{\rm T}}=\sum_{\sigma}\frac{V_{0}^{4}\sigma}{8\pi G}\Im\left\{\oint_{\partial\mathcal{V}_{*}}\left[\overline{\tilde{U}_{{\rm T}}^{\sigma}}\nabla\tilde{U}_{{\rm D}}^{\sigma}-\tilde{U}_{{\rm D}}^{\sigma}\overline{\nabla\tilde{U}_{{\rm T}}^{\sigma}}\right]\cdot{\rm d}\boldsymbol{S}\right\}.

(95)

Finally we use the expressions of
\tilde{U}_{{\rm T}}^{\sigma}
and
\tilde{U}_{{\rm D}}^{\sigma}
given by Eqs. (
85
) and (
86
), and the definition of the Love number given by Eq. (
74
). It follows

\mathcal{P}_{{\rm T}}=-\sum_{\sigma}\sum_{l=2}^{+\infty}\sum_{m=-l}^{l}\frac{\left(2l+1\right)R_{\rm p}V_{0}^{4}\sigma}{8\pi G}\left|\tilde{U}_{{\rm T};l}^{m,\sigma}\right|^{2}\Im\left\{k_{{\rm D};l}^{m,\sigma}\right\}.

(96)

The tidal input power in the ocean is expressed as a function of the harmonics of the surface tidal elevation only,

\mathcal{P}_{{\rm T};{\rm oc}}=-\frac{3R_{\rm p}V_{0}^{4}}{8\pi G}\left(\frac{\rho_{{\rm w}}}{\overline{\rho}}\right)\sum_{\sigma}\sum_{l=2}^{+\infty}\sum_{m=-l}^{l}\sigma\Im\left\{\overline{\tilde{U}_{{\rm T};l}^{m,\sigma}}\tilde{\zeta}_{l}^{m,\sigma}\right\}.

(97)

By noting that
\tilde{U}_{{\rm T}}=\tilde{\zeta}_{{\rm eq}}
and expanding the component of the forcing tidal potential in series of oceanic eigenmodes,

\tilde{U}_{{\rm T};l}^{m,\sigma}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\langle\hat{Y}_{l}^{m},\Phi_{k}\rangle\tilde{\zeta}_{{\rm eq};k},

(98)

we can rewrite the sum on degrees and orders in Eq. (
97
) as

\sum_{l=0}^{+\infty}\sum_{m=-l}^{l}\overline{\tilde{U}_{{\rm T};l}^{m,\sigma}}\tilde{\zeta}_{l}^{m,\sigma}=\sum_{k,j}\left[\sum_{l=0}^{+\infty}\sum_{m=-l}^{l}\langle\Phi_{k},\hat{Y}_{l}^{m}\rangle\langle\hat{Y}_{l}^{m},\Phi_{j}\rangle\right]\overline{\tilde{\zeta}_{{\rm eq};k}}\lambda_{j}p_{j}.

(99)

Therefore, owing to the orthogonality of the
\Phi_{k}
eigenfunctions, Eq. (
97
) simplifies to

\mathcal{P}_{{\rm T};{\rm oc}}=\frac{1}{2}R_{\rm p}^{2}H^{2}g\rho_{{\rm w}}\sum_{\sigma}\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}\sigma\Im\left\{\lambda_{k}p_{k}\overline{\tilde{\zeta}_{{\rm eq};k}}\right\},

(100)

which corresponds to the usual expression of the work per unit time done by the tidal force on the oceanic tidal motions
(
Webb 1980
;
Farhat et al. 2022a
, e.g.)
.

Similarly, the tidally dissipated power within the oceanic layer is defined as

\mathcal{P}_{{\rm diss};{\rm oc}}\equiv\left\langle\int_{\mathcal{O}}\rho_{{\rm w}}H\sigma_{\rm R}\boldsymbol{V}\cdot\boldsymbol{V}{\rm d}S\right\rangle,

(101)

By making use of the orthogonality properties of the oceanic eigenmodes described by Eqs. (
30
) and (
36
), this power is expressed as
(
Webb 1980
, e.g.)

\mathcal{P}_{{\rm diss};{\rm oc}}=\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{\rm R}\rho_{{\rm w}}R_{\rm p}^{4}H\sum_{\sigma}\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}\sigma^{2}\left(\lambda_{k}\left|p_{k}\right|^{2}+\nu_{k}\left|p_{-k}\right|^{2}\right).

(102)

The dissipated power
\mathcal{P}_{{\rm diss};{\rm oc}}
is equal to the input tidal power given by Eq. (
97
) if the solid regions are rigid, as assumed in earlier studies
(
Webb 1980
;
Auclair-Desrotour et al. 2018
, e.g.)
.

When the solid part is deformable, the total input tidal power given by Eq. (
96
),
\mathcal{P}_{{\rm T}}
, and the total tidally dissipated power,
\mathcal{P}_{{\rm diss}}
, are the sum of the solid and oceanic contributions,

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{{\rm T}}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{P}_{{\rm T};{\rm sol}}+\mathcal{P}_{{\rm T};{\rm oc}},

(103)

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{{\rm diss}}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{P}_{{\rm diss};{\rm sol}}+\mathcal{P}_{{\rm diss};{\rm oc}},

(104)

where
\mathcal{P}_{{\rm T};{\rm sol}}\neq\mathcal{P}_{{\rm diss};{\rm sol}}
and
\mathcal{P}_{{\rm T};{\rm oc}}\neq\mathcal{P}_{{\rm diss};{\rm oc}}
in the general case. Particularly, we note that the two components of the input tidal power can take negative values due to the energy exchanges between the ocean and solid regions resulting from the loading and gravitational coupling, while
\mathcal{P}_{{\rm diss};{\rm sol}}\geq 0
and
\mathcal{P}_{{\rm diss};{\rm oc}}\geq 0
. In the following, the input and tidally dissipated powers of the solid regions,
\mathcal{P}_{{\rm T};{\rm sol}}
and
\mathcal{P}_{{\rm diss};{\rm sol}}
, are evaluated from the oceanic contributions given by Eqs. (
97
) and (
102
), and the total input tidal power, expressed in Eq. (
96
), by using the fact that
\mathcal{P}_{{\rm diss}}=\mathcal{P}_{{\rm T}}
.

At this stage, we have established the expressions of all the tidal quantities that characterise the dissipative tidal response of the planet in the general case. Study of a reference case

Before making any attempt to explore the parameter space, it seems appropriate to elucidate the tidal response of the planet for a well-chosen reference configuration. In this section, we detail the physical setup of this configuration and we compute the frequency spectra of the quantities that characterise the semidiurnal tidal response of the planet. Considering this point, we shall emphasise that we do not expect to recover exactly the estimates obtained from more sophisticated numerical models owing to the numerous simplifications made in the present approach. According to the frequency spectra of Fig.

, the present parameters of the Earth-Moon system (dashed grey line at
\chi\approx 0.963
) places the oceanic tidal response close to a resonance, which corresponds to a maximum of the tidal dissipation rate. This feature of the spectra is in agreement with the increasing dissipation rate inferred from geological data in the past tens of millions of years
(see e.g.
Farhat et al. 2022a
, and references therein)
and the predictions of more sophisticated models including realistic bathymetries and land-ocean distributions
(
Green et al. 2017
;
Daher et al. 2021

. In the low-frequency range, the tidal response of the planet is dominated by the oceanic dynamical tide, which is characterised by resonant peaks. Conversely, in the high-frequency range, the resonances associated with oceanic modes are attenuated by the visco-elastic adjustment of the solid part, and the latter becomes the predominant contributor to the tidally dissipated energy. This change of regime can be simply understood by considering the fact that the solid tidal torque scales as
\,\propto\,\sigma^{-\alpha}
with
\alpha=0.25
(see Table
2
), while the non-resonant background of the oceanic tidal torque scales as
\,\propto\,\sigma^{-3}

(e.g.
Auclair-Desrotour et al. 2019a
, Eqs. (47) and (55), respectively)
.

We note that the tidal input power and tidally dissipated power are not equivalent for a given layer – solid part or oceanic basin – whereas they are strictly equal for the whole planet. This discrepancy is due to the solid-ocean energy transfer allowed by the elasticity of the solid part through ocean loading and self-attraction variations. The energy transfer strongly affects the tidal elongation of the solid part when the oceanic resonances predominate because this elongation is essentially controlled by the ocean loading in this regime. As a consequence, the tidal torque exerted on the solid part can be accelerating instead of being decelerating as observed in the signs of the tidal input power, imaginary part of the Love number, and tidal torque.

Another change of sign can be noticed near the zero-frequency limit (
\sigma\rightarrow 0
). This behaviour actually results from the maximum reached by the solid tidal torque for tidal periods close to the Maxwell and Andrade times, which are both much larger than the typical timescales associated with the ocean dynamics
(
Bolmont et al. 2020
, e.g.)
. At these timescales, the ocean basin is no longer resonant and the dissipative component of its tidal response thus tends to vanish, which allows the solid tide to be predominant in the very low-frequency range. However, the solid tidal torque also tends to zero for
\sigma\ll\min\left(\tau_{\rm M}^{-1},\tau_{\rm A}^{-1}\right)
, and
\mathcal{T}_{z}=0
at
\sigma=0
.

Finally, Fig.
3
highlights the symmetry breaking effect induced by the continent. Whereas the tidal response of a global ocean takes the form of a regular series of harmonics
(see e.g.
Auclair-Desrotour et al. 2018
, Fig. 5)
, the continent interferes with the forced wave by deviating tidal flows. As a consequence, additional oceanic eigenmodes are excited by the tidal forcing and the energy concentrated into one mode of the global ocean is spread over several modes. As they are each associated with one resonant peak, these new modes alter the general aspect of the spectra by breaking their regularity and by flattening the resonances of the dominating tidal modes. In the next sections, we show quantitatively that the former effect can actually be identified as the typical signature of a supercontinent in the tidal response of an ocean planet.

4
Parametric study

As the reference case is now well understood, we can apply the harmonic analysis performed in Sect.
3
to various configurations. The fact that the tidal response is controlled by a relatively small number of physical parameters in our approach (see Table
2
) makes it possible to explore the parameter space in an exhaustive way. This is the purpose of the present section.

4.1
Preliminary physical analysis

Before all, we shall briefly proceed to a selection of key parameters representing well the parameter space. We recall that the oceanic tidal response is fully determined by the dimensionless control parameters summarised in Table
1
. Notwithstanding the deformation of the solid part, which intervenes as a corrective effect through the deformation factors (
\gamma_{{\rm D};l}^{\sigma},\gamma_{{\rm T};l}^{\sigma}
), this set of parameters can essentially be reduced to five numbers: three numbers governing the dynamics of oceanic tidal flows (
\tilde{\sigma},\tilde{\sigma}_{\rm G},\tilde{\sigma}_{{\rm R}}
) and two numbers defining the geometry of the ocean basin (
\theta_{{\rm c}},\hat{\theta}_{{\rm S}}
). The parameter space of the oceanic tidal response thus simplifies to five dimensions.

Moreover,
\tilde{\sigma}\approx 1
for the semidiurnal tide as long as
n_{{\rm s}}\ll\Omega
, indicating that this number can be roughly considered as invariant over time for planet-satellite systems similar to the Earth-Moon system. Therefore, the parameter space can be limited to the set
\left(\tilde{\sigma}_{\rm G},\tilde{\sigma}_{{\rm R}},\theta_{{\rm c}},\hat{\theta}_{{\rm S}}\right)
for the semidiurnal oceanic tide of a rapidly rotating Earth-like planet. For convenience, instead of varying the dimensionless parameters governing the ocean dynamics, we choose to vary the physical parameters they depend on, namely the oceanic depth
H
for
\tilde{\sigma}_{\rm G}
, and the Rayleigh drag frequency
\sigma_{\rm R}
for
\tilde{\sigma}_{{\rm R}}
. The first parameter defines the water volume of the ocean, while the second one characterises the efficiency of the dissipative mechanisms.

Concerning the geometry of the ocean basin, we note that the size of the continent is linked to the oceanic depth through the volume of sea water. Since one expects the water volume to be conserved as the size of the continent evolves, it seems more appropriate to vary
\theta_{{\rm c}}
at constant volume rather than at constant depth. Consequently, when we vary the continental radius, we shall vary the oceanic depth by using the expression of the seawater volume in the thin layer approximation,

Effect of the continental position.

First, we consider the role played by the geometry of the ocean basin. As the colatitude of the continental centre evolves from
0^{\degree}
(polar continent) to
90^{\degree}
(equatorial continent), the frequency spectrum of the Love number rapidly becomes irregular (see Fig.
4
, top left panel). For
\hat{\theta}_{{\rm S}}=0^{\degree}
, the coastline of the hemispherical continent exactly corresponds to the planet’s equator. As a consequence, it does not interfere with the latitudinal component of tidal flows, which is already zero at the equator in the absence of a continent. This explains why we recover approximately the spectrum obtained for the global ocean
(
Auclair-Desrotour et al. 2018
)
up to a factor of 2, the noticed slight discrepancy being due to the contribution of the solid part. This spectrum is composed of a regular series of harmonics that results from the coupling – caused by Coriolis forces – between the oceanic normal modes and the quadrupolar tidal potential. For a non-rotating planet, only one resonance would be observed in that configuration. A slight inclination of the continental centre with respect to the pole (Fig.
4
, top left panel,
\hat{\theta}_{{\rm S}}=10^{\degree}
) appears to be sufficient to break the axis symmetry and to alter significantly the regularity of the spectrum. The harmonics of the polar case are split into more modes, which induces intermediate relative maxima and decreases the dissipation rate reached while crossing the main resonances.

Effect of the continental size.

Such an effect can also be observed as the angular radius of the continent varies from
0^{\degree}
(global ocean) to
90^{\degree}
(hemispherical ocean) at constant water volume (Fig.
4
, top right panel). As the size of the continent increases, the peaks of the global ocean get progressively altered by the interferences of coastlines with tidal flows. However, we remark that this effect really becomes significant only for
\theta_{{\rm c}}\geq 30^{\degree}
, which suggests that continents smaller than South America hardly alter the tidal response of the global ocean even if they are located at the equator. Moreover, the spectra highlight the fact that oceanic resonances are shifted to the right as the size of the continent increases, which also means resonances become of smaller amplitudes, so less dissipative. This effect is directly related to the ocean’s geometry. Acoustically, the ocean basin is analogous to a vibrating drumhead, with its size and depth standing for the drumhead’s diameter and tension, respectively. On one hand, the eigenfrequencies of the oceanic normal modes increase as the size of the basin decreases since the latter corresponds to the wavelength of the lowest tidal modes. On the other hand, the speed of gravity waves scales as
\,\propto\,\sqrt{H}
, meaning that it increases as the size of the ocean basin decreases at constant volume.

Effect of the oceanic depth.

Consistently with the above drumhead analogy, increasing the oceanic depth essentially induces a dilation of the frequency spectrum without changing its global aspect (Fig.
4
, middle left panel). This is related to the way the resonances are controlled by the oceanic depth. Both the heights and frequencies of resonant peaks scale as
\propto\sqrt{H}

(e.g.
Auclair-Desrotour et al. 2019a
, Eq (55))
, which explains why their relative positions and amplitudes are conserved as
H
varies.

Effect of dissipative mechanisms.

Similarly, we analyse the evolution of the frequency spectrum with the Rayleigh drag frequency (Fig.
4
, middle right panel) using the closed-form solutions provided by the analytical theory
(
Auclair-Desrotour et al. 2018
, e.g.)
. The plot highlights the transition from the quasi-adiabatic regime (
\sigma_{\rm R}=10^{-6}\ {\rm s^{-1}}
) to the frictional regime (
\sigma_{\rm R}=10^{-3}\ {\rm s^{-1}}
). In the former, the resonances of oceanic modes are weakly damped, while they are completely annihilated in the latter.

The two asymptotic regimes can be considered separately. In the quasi-adiabatic regime, the frequencies of resonances are almost independent of the Rayleigh drag frequency, whereas their heights scale as
\,\propto\,\sigma_{\rm R}^{-1}
, their widths as
\,\propto\,\sigma_{\rm R}
, and the non-resonant background of the oceanic dissipation rate as
\,\propto\,\sigma_{\rm R}

(
Auclair Desrotour et al. 2015
;
Auclair-Desrotour et al. 2018
;
Auclair-Desrotour et al. 2019a
)
. As a consequence, the number of visible peaks increases as
\sigma_{\rm R}
decreases. The new peaks appear in the high-frequency range since they are associated with oceanic harmonics of high degrees. In the zero-drag limit (
\sigma_{\rm R}\rightarrow 0
), the number of resonant peaks exactly corresponds to the number of harmonics with eigenfrequencies less than the upper bound of the considered frequency interval. In the frictional regime, the spectrum is regular and only one maximum is visible, for a frequency scaling as
\,\propto\,\sigma_{\rm R}^{-1}
. This behaviour is described by a closed-form solution that will be detailed in a forthcoming article. Considering the estimate of
{\sim}10^{-5}\ {\rm s^{-1}}
found for the Earth’s Rayleigh drag frequency from model adjustments to the current dissipation rate
(
Webb 1980
;
Farhat et al. 2022a
)
, we note that this value is just slightly less than the critical value marking the transition between the quasi-adiabatic and frictional regimes in Fig.
4
(middle right panel), namely
\sigma_{\rm R}\approx 3.2\times 10^{-5}\ {\rm s^{-1}}
(see also Sect.
5
).

Effect of the solid elasticity and anelasticity.

We finally examine the role played by the parameters of the solid part (Fig.
4
, bottom panels). As discussed above, the elasticity of the solid part acts to attenuate the resonances of oceanic tidal modes by elastic adjustment. The load caused by a local water mass surplus lowers the oceanic floor, which decreases the gravitational potential of the planet’s tidal response. If the solid part is rigid, this tidal response corresponds to the oceanic tide, as observed for
\mu=300
GPa. The Andrade time only affects the non-resonant background of the spectrum through the scaling factor
\tau_{\rm A}^{-\alpha}
of the solid dissipation rate, as shown by Eq. (
106
). As
\tau_{\rm A}
increases, the non-resonant background decreases. This effect is visible in the high-frequency range, in particular. Table
3
summarises the effects of all the studied parameters on specific features of the frequency spectra.

Ignored effects and model limitations.

As the spectral features detailed above for tidal dissipation have been obtained from the simplified theoretical framework of Sect.
2
, we shall end this section by discussing the main ignored effects that may alter them. First, although the single continent configuration appears as a convenient setup to introduce the anisotropic effect induced by continentality, realistic land-ocean distributions are more complex. Consequently, the oceanic tidal response cannot be simply related to the geometry of the ocean basin in the general case, as highlighted by numerical studies performed for Earth and exoplanetary land-ocean distributions
(
Egbert & Ray 2001
;
Egbert & Ray 2003
;
Green et al. 2017
;
Blackledge et al. 2020
)
. However, since the oceanic tidal waves are planetary scale, one may reasonably expect that they shall mainly interfere with large scale continental features. The predominant modes that shape the tidal dissipation spectrum of the planet are thus related to the largest length scales of the continental distribution.

That said, as shown by
Green et al. 2017
, the smoothness of the continental geometry tends to yield overestimates of the tidally dissipated energy. The coastline fractality thus both acts to attenuate and broaden the resonances. Similarly, we remark that the eigenfrequencies of the oceanic tidal modes vary with the oceanic depth, given that the phase speed of gravity waves scale as
\,\propto\,H^{1/2}
. As a consequence, planetary scale variations of the oceanic depth presumably act to attenuate and broaden the resonant peaks. Complex bathymetries are associated with spatially dependent dissipation rates, the dissipation coefficients being strongly determined by small-scale topographic features
(
Egbert & Ray 2001
;
Carter et al. 2008
;
Arbic et al. 2010
;
Green & Huber 2013
;
Merdith et al. 2021
, e.g.)
. It is also noteworthy that predominant dissipative mechanisms such as turbulent bottom drag scale non-linearly with the speed of tidal flows, which precludes resonances to reach very high amplitudes. 5
A metric for the signature of supercontinents

Until now, we have been investigating the direct problem that consists in generating frequency spectra of the tidal quantities from sets of values assigned to the model parameters. Particularly, in Sect.
4
, we made an attempt to infer the tidal response of planets hosting ocean basins from knowledge of their geometry. The relationships between spectral and geometric features actually correspond to a very broad field of mathematical problems known as spectral geometry. Through popular case studies of this field, such as Kac’s drum
(
Kac 1966
)
, it was shown that the geometry of a vibrating system can be predicted to a certain extent if its spectral features are known. This is the so-called inverse problem that we aim to address in the present section by investigating the extent to which one can infer the geometry of the ocean basin from the spectral features of the planet’s tidal response. Particularly, we define further a metric that quantifies the specific signature of a supercontinent in the evolution of the tidal torque with the tidal frequency. This frequency dependence of the tidal torque is straightforwardly connected to the orbital evolution of the satellite or the rotational evolution of the planet, as any crossed resonance induces a rapid variation of the orbital parameters over a short time period
(
Auclair-Desrotour et al. 2014
;
Farhat et al. 2022a
, see e.g.)
.

The parametric study of Sect.
4
shows evidence of the numerous degeneracies characterising the dependences of the frequency spectra on the system’s parameters (see Table
3
). Several features – such as the height of resonant peaks or the non-resonant background – appear to be sensitive to three key parameters at the same time. Particularly, these results highlight the predominant role played by the Rayleigh drag frequency, which affects almost all of the spectral features listed in Table
3
. Nevertheless, we also remark that the only parameters having an impact on the regularity of the spectra – notwithstanding the effect of the Rayleigh drag frequency – are those defining the geometry of the ocean basin, namely the colatitude of the continental centre and the angular radius of the continent. This argues for the existence of spectral quantities sensitive to these parameters only. Such quantities are metrics of the geometry. Ideally, they shall vary with
\hat{\theta}_{{\rm S}}
,
\theta_{{\rm c}}
, and
\sigma_{\rm R}
, while being insensitive to the other parameters.

In Fig.
4
, the irregularity of spectra seems to be mainly characterised by a significant dispersion of the frequency intervals separating consecutive relative maxima of the tidal torque, denoted by
\Delta\sigma_{k}\equiv\sigma_{k+1}-\sigma_{k}
in the following, with
\sigma_{k}
being the frequency of the
k
-th maximum. In order to make these frequency intervals insensitive to the dilation-contraction effect induced by oceanic depth variations (see Fig.
4
, middle left panel), they are normalised by their average, given by

\overline{\Delta\sigma}\equiv\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\Delta\sigma_{k},

(107)

where
N
designates the total number of intervals. We thus denote by
\hat{\Delta\sigma}_{k}\equiv\Delta\sigma_{k}/\overline{\Delta\sigma}
the normalised intervals. Finally, sorting the intervals in ascending order and using the subscript
j
such that
\Delta\sigma_{j}<\Delta\sigma_{j+1}
for
j=1,\ldots,N
, we introduce the cumulative distribution function (CDF) of the normalised frequency intervals,

{\rm CDF}\left(\hat{\Delta\sigma}\right)\equiv\frac{1}{N}\underset{\hat{\Delta\sigma}_{j}\leq\hat{\Delta\sigma}}{{\rm argmax}}\left\{\hat{\Delta\sigma}_{j}\right\}.

(108)

Figure
5
shows the CDFs of the frequency intervals separating two consecutive maxima for the spectra plotted in Fig.
4
. In these plots, the quasi-uniformly spaced peaks of the global and polar hemispherical oceans are characterised by a sharp step in the CDF, which abruptly switches from 0 to 1. Conversely, the symmetry breaking effect of continental shelves induces a smooth transition. As noted qualitatively with the spectra of Fig.
4
, the slope of the CDF essentially varies with the position of the continent on the globe, its size, and the dissipative time scale. This leads us to consider, as an appropriate metric for the continentality effect, the normalised standard deviation of the frequency intervals separating consecutive maxima of the tidal torque,

\hat{\sigma}\equiv\sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left(\frac{\Delta\sigma_{k}}{\overline{\Delta\sigma}}-1\right)^{2}}.

(109)

The thus defined normalised standard deviation is such that
0\leq\hat{\sigma}\leq 1
, where
\hat{\sigma}=0
corresponds to uniformly spaced relative maxima or to
N=1
(two maxima), and
\hat{\sigma}=1
to an extreme dispersion of the resonant peaks. By convention, we set
\hat{\sigma}
to zero when one unique maximum is observed.

By reproducing the methodology detailed in Sect.
4
, we computed the tidal response of the reference planet defined in Table
2
for various values of the colatitude of the continental centre, angular radius of the continent at constant seawater volume, oceanic depth, Rayleigh drag frequency, effective shear modulus of the solid part, and Andrade time, each parameter being modified independently of the others. In all cases, we calculated the values of
\hat{\sigma}
from the frequency spectra obtained for the imaginary part of the quadrupolar Love number. These values are plotted in Fig.
6
.

The quantity
\hat{\sigma}
appears to be almost insensitive to variations of
H
,
\mu
, and
\tau_{\rm A}
, whereas it varies over several orders of magnitude when
\hat{\theta}_{{\rm S}}
,
\theta_{{\rm c}}
, or
\sigma_{\rm R}
are modified. Moreover, we recover quantitatively the tendencies identified in the parametric study (Table
3
), namely
\hat{\sigma}
really accounts for the role played by the geometry of the ocean basin and the dissipative timescale in the planet’s tidal response. Thus, the normalised standard deviation defined by Eq. (
109
) can be considered as a spectral metric of the planet’s geometric features, both on the global scale pertaining to the location or spread of land, if any, along with the local topographical scale dictating the dissipative timescale in the ocean.

Interestingly, the dependence of
\hat{\sigma}
on
\hat{\theta}_{{\rm S}}
,
\theta_{{\rm c}}
, or
\sigma_{\rm R}
, is not even. Instead, threshold effects may be observed, similar to those of Fig.
5
. First, the evolution of the metric with
\hat{\theta}_{{\rm S}}
(Fig.
6
, top left panel) illustrates the fact that a small inclination of the continent on the globe with respect to the pole is sufficient to break the axi-symmetry. By switching from
\hat{\theta}_{{\rm S}}=0^{\degree}
(polar continent) to
\hat{\theta}_{{\rm S}}=10^{\degree}
, the metric skyrockets to reach a plateau around
\hat{\sigma}\sim 0.4
. This plateau suggests that changing the position of the continental centre from
10^{\degree}
to
90^{\degree}
does not affect the regularity of the frequency spectrum. Basically, one observes here a saturation regime where the signature of the supercontinent through the chosen metric is the same regardless of its position on the globe.

A similar behaviour is observed as the angular radius of the continent grows from
0^{\degree}
(global ocean) to
90^{\degree}
(hemispherical ocean) at constant water volume (Fig.
6
, top right panel). While the metric does not evolve much with
\theta_{{\rm c}}
in general, it increases drastically around
\theta_{{\rm c}}\approx 35^{\degree}
, which is the critical size identified from the plotted spectra in the parametric study. This value is consistent with the highest value of
\theta_{{\rm c}}
for which the frequency spectrum still exhibits a regular series of resonant peaks that resembles the tidal response of the global ocean (Fig.
4
, top right panel). Beyond
35^{\degree}
, the spectrum becomes complex and irregular due to the strong interference of the continent with tidal flows. We note that the metric remains stable for
\theta_{{\rm c}}\gtrsim 40^{\degree}
, which suggests that the regularity of the frequency spectrum is insensitive to the size of the continent beyond this value. This highlights another saturation regime, similar to that observed for the position of the continent, in which the signature of the supercontinent is the same regardless of its size. It is noteworthy that the way the continent interferes with the oceanic tidal flows is related to its size with respect to the typical horizontal wavelengths of the predominating tidal modes, which are planetary scale. As a consequence, the critical angular radius marking the regime transition for the studied Earth-sized planet would be similar for a super-Earth or a smaller planet.

Finally, the metric decays monotonically as the Rayleigh drag frequency increases, with a sharp variation occurring around
\sigma_{\rm R}\sim 3.2\times 10^{-5}\ {\rm s^{-1}}
(Fig.
6
, middle right panel). This critical value marks the transition between the quasi-adiabatic and frictional regimes (see Table
1
). In the latter, the resonances associated with oceanic modes are damped by the strong friction of tidal flows with the oceanic floor, which makes the tidal torque evolve smoothly with the tidal frequency, as shown by Fig.
4
(middle right panel). Therefore, the regularity of the spectrum conveys no information about the geometry of the ocean basin in this regime. Conversely, in the quasi-adiabatic regime,
\hat{\sigma}
increases as
\sigma_{\rm R}
decays due to the growing number of visible resonant peaks.

6
Conclusions

In this paper we have examined the linear response of an ocean planet hosting a supercontinent to gravitational tidal forcing. This problem may be regarded as a simplified model of the tides raised on Earth by the Moon and their interactions with the land-ocean distribution, which predominantly drives the evolution of the Earth-Moon system over long time scales
(
Bills & Ray 1999
;
Daher et al. 2021
;
Tyler 2021
;
Farhat et al. 2022a
, e.g.)
. The theory developed in the present study expands on earlier works that investigated the tidal response of hemispherical or global oceans
(
Longuet-Higgins 1968
;
Longuet-Higgins & Pond 1970
;
Webb 1980
;
Webb 1982
;
Auclair-Desrotour et al. 2018
;
Farhat et al. 2022a
)
. In this approach, the continent is represented by a spherical cap of arbitrary size and position, and the LTEs are written in the shallow water approximation for an oceanic layer of uniform depth. These simplifications allow the problem to be formulated analytically in terms of explicitly defined oceanic eigenmodes, which provides a deep insight into the physics governing the planet’s tidal response. Additionally, such a formalism appears to be well suited to the harmonic analysis of the tidal dynamics since it relies on a small set of key parameters.

As a first step, we established the equations describing the dynamics of the forced tidal flows. These equations include the gravitational and surface couplings that result from the deformation of the solid part generated by the ocean loading and the tidal forces exerted by the perturber. The visco-elastic response of the solid part to tidal forcings is described in a generic way by gravitational and load Love numbers. We computed it in practice by making use of the closed-form solutions provided by the Andrade model for a homogeneous body, following the prescriptions obtained from 1D models taking the planet’s internal structure into account. The LTEs were expressed as an algebraic system describing the evolution of coupled eigenmodes in the frequency domain. This system is controlled by a small set of clearly identified dimensionless numbers that characterise the regime of oceanic tidal waves and the geometry of the ocean basin. We finally used the harmonic expansion of tidal flows to express the corresponding input tidal power, tidally dissipated power, tidal Love numbers, and tidal torque exerted about the spin axis.

As a second step, we applied the theory to a reference case, which is essentially defined as a simplified Earth-Moon system. In this configuration, the continent is hemispherical and located at the equator. By computing the evolution of the aforementioned tidal quantities with the forcing tidal frequency, we recovered the solutions obtained in
Farhat et al. 2022a
. We showed that the planet’s tidal dissipation rate is dominated by the contribution of the ocean, while the solid part determines it mainly in the high-frequency range, that is for a fast rotating planet. Moreover, the obtained results highlight the symmetry breaking effect of the supercontinent, which splits the modes of the global ocean into more modes by interfering with tidal flows. As a result, the spectra of tidal quantities are flattened and they exhibit additional relative maxima corresponding to the resonances of the basin modes.

As a third step, we carried out a parametric study for a set of six key parameters, by using the previously defined reference case as a template configuration. The obtained results reveal the degeneracies affecting the evolution of the main spectral features with the selected parameters. However, they also indicate that the specific signature of the geometry can be unravelled to a certain extent by characterising the erraticity of the spectra. As the position or angular radius of the continent increase, the frequency spectrum of the tidal torque becomes irregular, while the other parameters only alter the frequencies of the resonant peaks, their widths, their heights, and the non-resonant background. The only exception to this statement is the Rayleigh drag frequency, which affects the regularity of frequency spectra as well.

The above qualitative results were refined in a quantitative way as a last step. By following an inverse problem approach inspired from spectral geometry, we showed that some geometric features of the ocean basin may be inferred from the spectral irregularity, which is quantified by the normalised standard deviation of the frequency intervals separating consecutive relative maxima in the frequency spectrum of the tidal Love number. The sensitivity of this quantity to the geometrical parameters is characterised by threshold effects. As regards the position of the continent on the globe, the metric only allows the poles to be distinguished from other locations. Similarly, it seems to indicate that the signatures of continents with angular radii larger than
40^{\degree}
are indistinguishable from each other.

Nevertheless, the harmonic analysis also suggests that a continent similar to South America (
{\sim}30^{\degree}
-angular radius) or smaller would not alter, qualitatively, the oceanic tidal response even if it is located at the equator, where the interference of the coastlines with tidal flows tends to be maximal. In such configurations, the tidally dissipated energy predicted by the harmonic analysis is approximately the same as in the absence of continent. Finally the metric appears to be sensitive to the transition between the quasi-adiabatic and frictional regimes, which occurs for
\sigma_{\rm R}\approx 3.2\times 10^{-5}\ {\rm s^{-1}}
. This critical value is slightly greater than that estimated for Earth
(
Garrett & Munk 1971
;
Webb 1973
;
Tyler 2021
;
Farhat et al. 2022a
,
{\sim}10^{-6}-10^{-5}\ {\rm s^{-1}}
, e.g.)
, which is consistent with the frequency-resonant behaviour of the Earth’s tidal dissipation rate inferred from Lunar laser ranging and geological data
(
Bills & Ray 1999
;
Tyler 2021
, e.g.)
.

Both the spectral method and the metric for continentality effects elaborated in the present work are highly relevant to studies dealing with the long-term tidal evolution of bodies with surface oceans, such as extrasolar rocky planets orbiting in the habitable zone of their host star. Particularly, the studied spectral irregularity of the tidal torque has direct implications on the history of planet-satellite systems. Tidal resonances cause irregular evolutions of the satellite’s orbit and planet’s rotation, thus leading to abrupt LOD or obliquity variations that might result in significant climatic effects. We will examine specifically the consequences of continentality on the long-term evolution of the Earth-Moon system in a separate dedicated study by using the theory established in the present work.

As this theory forms a basis for more sophisticated descriptions of continentality effects, we will consider refining the geometry in the future in order to account for the role played by the fractality of non-smooth coastlines, the complex bathymetry of the ocean basin, or the spatial dependence of energy dissipation. Also, while we have characterised the signature of one unique supercontinent in the planet’s tidal response, the combined effects of several continents still needs to be elucidated. All these questions require further work improving meanwhile the theory and the quality of observational constraints on the past history of the Earth-Moon system. We will address them through several forthcoming studies.

Appendix B
Associated Legendre Functions (ALFs)

The ALFs, usually denoted by
P_{l}^{m}
, are the solutions of the equation

\frac{1}{\sin\theta}\dfrac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}\theta}\left(\sin\theta\dfrac{{\rm d}h}{{\rm d}\theta}\right)+\left[l\left(l+1\right)-\frac{m^{2}}{\sin^{2}\theta}\right]h=0,

(110)

which is found by separating the longitude (
\varphi
) and the colatitude (
\theta
) in the eigenvalues problems associated with the Laplace equation in spherical coordinates (see Eq. (
124
)). The function
h\left(\theta\right)
in Eq. (
110
) is assumed to be a regular function of the colatitude. The parameters
l
and
m
are commonly referred to as the degree and the order of the Legendre function, respectively. They may be integral, non-integral, or complex depending of the eigenvalues of the problem,
m^{2}
and
l\left(l+1\right)
, which are determined both by the boundary conditions and the geometry of the domain of definition of the solutions on the unit sphere.

In the general case, the ALFs are defined, for
z\in\mathbb{C}
such that
\left|z\right|<1
and arbitrary complex constants,
l
and
m
, as
(
Abramowitz & Stegun 1972
, Eq. (8.1.2))

P_{l}^{m}\left(z\right)\equiv\frac{1}{\Gamma\left(1-m\right)}\left(\frac{1+z}{1-z}\right)^{\frac{m}{2}}{}_{2}F_{1}\left(-l,l+1;1-m;\frac{1-z}{2}\right).

(111)

In the above equation,
\Gamma
is the Gamma function introduced in Eq. (
106
), which is defined for
\Re\left(z\right)>0
as
(
Abramowitz & Stegun 1972
, Eq. (6.1.1))

\Gamma\left(z\right)\equiv\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{z-1}{\rm e}^{-t}{\rm d}t.

(112)

The hypergeometric function
{}_{2}F_{1}
is defined as
(
Abramowitz & Stegun 1972
, Eq. (15.1.1))

{}_{2}F_{1}\left(a,b;c;d\right)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{\left(a\right)_{j}\left(b\right)_{j}}{\left(c\right)_{j}}\frac{x^{j}}{j!},

(113)

where
\left(a\right)_{j}
designates the Pochhammer symbol
(
Abramowitz & Stegun 1972
, Eq. (6.1.22))
,

\displaystyle\left(a\right)_{0}\equiv 1,

(114)

\displaystyle\left(a\right)_{j}\equiv a\left(a+1\right)\ldots\left(a+j-1\right)=\frac{\Gamma\left(a+j\right)}{\Gamma\left(a\right)}.

(115)

The derivatives of the ALFs with respect to
z
are given by

\partial_{z}P_{l}^{m}=\frac{1}{z^{2}-1}\left[zlP_{l}^{m}\left(z\right)-\left(m+l\right)P_{l-1}^{m}\left(z\right)\right].

(116)

We note that the functions
\Gamma
and
{}_{2}F_{1}
in Eq. (
111
) are defined only if their arguments satisfy certain conditions. In practice, the ALFs are computed for any values of these arguments by using the symmetry properties of the regularised hypergeometric function
{}_{2}\tilde{F}_{1}
, which is defined as
(
Johansson 2016
, e.g.)

{}_{2}\tilde{F}_{1}\left(a,b;c;z\right)\equiv\frac{{}_{2}F_{1}\left(a,b;c;z\right)}{\Gamma\left(c\right)}.

(117)

For
m\in\mathbb{Z}
,
l\in\mathbb{R}
and
z=x\in\mathbb{R}
such that
-1\leq x\leq 1
, which corresponds to the case of the SCHs, the general definition given by Eq. (
111
) simplifies to
(
Thébault et al. 2006
, e.g.)

\displaystyle P_{l}^{m}\left(x\right)=

\displaystyle\frac{\left(-1\right)^{\frac{m+\left|m\right|}{2}}}{2^{\left|m\right|}\left|m\right|!}\frac{\Gamma\left(l+\left|m\right|+1\right)}{\Gamma\left(l-m+1\right)}\left(1-x^{2}\right)^{\left|m\right|/2}

(118)

\displaystyle\times{}_{2}\tilde{F}_{1}\left(\left|m\right|-l,l+\left|m\right|+1;\left|m\right|+1;\frac{1-x}{2}\right).

Here, the hypergeometric function is expressed as

{}_{2}\tilde{F}_{1}\left(\left|m\right|-l,l+\left|m\right|+1;\left|m\right|+1;\frac{1-x}{2}\right)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}a_{j}\left(\frac{1-x}{2}\right)^{j},

(119)

with the coefficients
a_{j}
recursively defined as

\displaystyle a_{0}=1,

(120)

\displaystyle a_{j+1}=\frac{\left(j+\left|m\right|-l\right)\left(j+\left|m\right|+l+1\right)}{\left(j+1\right)\left(j+1+\left|m\right|\right)}a_{j}.

(121)

In the general case, the sum of Eq. (
118
) converges only for
-1<x\leq 1
, which corresponds to the inequality
0\leq\theta<\pi
in the change of coordinates
x=\cos\theta
. However the sum of Eq. (
118
) is not infinite any more when
l
is an integer since
a_{j+1}=0
as long as
j>l-\left|m\right|
. As a consequence it converges also for
\theta=\pi
in this case.

Since the degrees
l
tend to integral values (
l=0,1,2,\ldots
) as
\theta\rightarrow\pi
, the generalised ALFs given by Eqs. (
111
) and (
118
) continuously converge towards the well-known ALFs of the SPHs, which are defined for
-1\leq x\leq 1
, and
l
and
m
integers such that
l\geq\left|m\right|
, as
(
Abramowitz & Stegun 1972
;
Arfken & Weber 2005
)

P_{l}^{m}\left(x\right)\equiv\left(-1\right)^{m}\left(1-x^{2}\right)^{m/2}\dfrac{{\rm d}^{m}}{{\rm d}x^{m}}P_{l}\left(x\right),

(122)

the
P_{l}
designating the Legendre polynomials, given by

P_{l}\left(x\right)\equiv\frac{1}{2^{l}l!}\dfrac{{\rm d}^{l}}{{\rm d}x^{l}}\left[\left(x^{2}-1\right)^{l}\right].

(123)

Owing to the large number of terms that is sometimes required to compute the sum of Eq. (
119
) at machine precision, the numerical evaluation of the generalised ALFs given by Eq. (
118
) may be tricky in some cases. Particularly, the series converges very slowly when
x\approx-1
, which occurs if the ocean is almost global. In practice, the evaluation is achieved by making use of the build-in hypergeometric function
{}_{2}F_{1}
of the
TRIP
algebraic manipulator
(
Gastineau & Laskar 2011
)
.

The SPHs and the SCHs are found by solving – for specific boundary conditions discussed further – the eigenvalue-eigenfunction problem described by the Helmholtz equation
(
Haines 1985
, e.g.)
,

\nabla^{2}f=-\lambda f,

(124)

where
\nabla^{2}
designates the Laplace operator, defined for any function
f\left(\theta,\varphi\right)
as

\nabla^{2}f\equiv\frac{1}{\sin\theta}\partial_{\theta}\left(\sin\theta\partial_{\theta}f\right)+\frac{1}{\sin^{2}\theta}\partial_{\varphi\varphi}f,

(125)

the notation
\partial_{x}
or
\partial_{xx}
referring to the first or second partial derivatives with respect to the
x
coordinate. In both case, the variables
\theta
and
\varphi
can be separated, which allows the problem to be solved as two decoupled eigenvalue problems.

Since the functions
f\left(\theta,\varphi\right)
in Eq. (
124
) are defined for all
\varphi
, the boundary conditions on
\varphi
are those of continuity,

\displaystyle f\left(\theta,\varphi\right)

\displaystyle=f\left(\theta,\varphi+2\pi\right),

(126)

\displaystyle\partial_{\varphi}f\left(\theta,\varphi\right)

\displaystyle=\partial_{\varphi}f\left(\theta,\varphi+2\pi\right),

(127)

which restricts the values of

m\in\mathbb{Z}
). These conditions hold both for the ordinary SPHs and for the SCHs. Analogously, the two sets of basis functions share the same regularity condition on
\theta
at the pole (
\theta=0

\displaystyle\partial_{\theta}f\left(0,\varphi\right)=0

\displaystyle m=0,

(128)

\displaystyle f\left(0,\varphi\right)=0

\displaystyle m\neq 0.

(129)

However they differ as regards the second boundary condition. The SPHs are obtained by solving the Helmholtz equation given by Eq. (
124
) on the entire sphere,
\mathcal{S}{:}\left(\theta,\varphi\right)\in\left[0,\pi\right]\times\left[0,2\pi\right[
, with regularity boundary conditions at
\theta=\pi

\theta=0

\displaystyle\partial_{\theta}f\left(\pi,\varphi\right)=0

\displaystyle m=0,

(130)

\displaystyle f\left(\pi,\varphi\right)=0

\displaystyle m\neq 0.

(131)

They are associated with integral degrees
l\in\mathbb{N}
such that

Following
Varshalovich et al. 1988
, we use, for the complex SPHs, the convention given by
(
Varshalovich et al. 1988
, Sect. 5.2, Eq. (1))

(132)

or, equivalently,

(133)

122

\lambda_{l}=l\left(l+1\right).

(134)

132

\displaystyle\langle f,g\rangle

\displaystyle\equiv\int_{\mathcal{S}}\overline{f}g{\rm d}S,

\displaystyle=\int_{\varphi=0}^{2\pi}\int_{\theta=0}^{\pi}\overline{f\left(\theta,\varphi\right)}g\left(\theta,\varphi\right)\sin\theta\,{\rm d}\theta{\rm d}\varphi.

the notation

\mathcal{S}

(136)

Hence, for any pair of SPHs

(137)

\displaystyle\partial_{\theta}f\left(\theta_{0},\varphi\right)=0

\displaystyle f\left(\theta_{0},\varphi\right)=0

As

). Similarly, the SCHs evolve from the cylindrical harmonics, where there is no curvature effect (small-sea configuration), to the SPHs given by Eq. (

In other words, the presence of a small island still alters the eigenmodes of the oceanic tide locally although this effect is negligible at a global scale. The impact of the island on the horizontal structure of tidal flows is quantified by the differences of degrees,
\left|l_{n}-n\right|
, and overlap coefficients,
\left|1-\langle\Theta_{l_{n}}^{m},Y_{n}^{m}\rangle\right|
, both of them tending to zero as the size of the island decreases.

Appendix D
Euler rotation matrix

We consider the frame of reference
\mathcal{R}{:}\left(O,\boldsymbol{{\rm e}}_{X},\boldsymbol{{\rm e}}_{Y},\boldsymbol{{\rm e}}_{Z}\right)
having the planet’s centre of gravity as origin and associated with the Cartesian coordinates
\left(X,Y,Z\right)
. As shown by Fig.
9
, any rotation
\mathcal{R}{:}\left(O,\boldsymbol{{\rm e}}_{X},\boldsymbol{{\rm e}}_{Y},\boldsymbol{{\rm e}}_{Z}\right)\rightarrow\mathcal{R}_{{\rm rot}}{:}\left(O,\boldsymbol{{\rm e}}_{x},\boldsymbol{{\rm e}}_{y},\boldsymbol{{\rm e}}_{z}\right)
of this frame of reference may be performed by three rotations about the coordinate axes
(e.g.
Varshalovich et al. 1988
, Sect. 1.4)
: (i) a rotation about the
Z
-axis through an angle
\alpha
such that
0\leq\alpha<2\pi
, (ii) a rotation about the new
Y^{\prime}
-axis through an angle
\beta
such that
0\leq\beta\leq\pi
, and (iii) a rotation about the new axis
z
through an angle
\gamma
such that
0\leq\gamma\leq 2\pi
. The frame of reference
\mathcal{R}_{{\rm rot}}{:}\left(O,\boldsymbol{{\rm e}}_{x},\boldsymbol{{\rm e}}_{y},\boldsymbol{{\rm e}}_{z}\right)
is associated with the Cartesian coordinates
\left(x,y,y\right)
. The three rotation angles,
\alpha
,
\beta
, and
\gamma
, are the so-called Euler angles.

Figure 9:
Euler angles corresponding to the standard Euler rotation matrix defined by Eq. (
150
).

Introducing the notations
C_{\phi}=\cos\phi
and
S_{\phi}=\sin\phi
for
\phi=\alpha,\beta,
or
\gamma
, the standard Euler rotation matrix is defined in terms of the Euler angles as
(e.g.
Varshalovich et al. 1988
, Eq. (54) p. 30)

\boldsymbol{R}_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}\equiv\begin{bmatrix}C_{\alpha}C_{\beta}C_{\gamma}-S_{\alpha}S_{\gamma}&-C_{\alpha}C_{\beta}S_{\gamma}-S_{\alpha}C_{\gamma}&C_{\alpha}S_{\beta}\\
S_{\alpha}C_{\beta}C_{\gamma}+C_{\alpha}S_{\gamma}&-S_{\alpha}C_{\beta}S_{\gamma}+C_{\alpha}C_{\gamma}&S_{\alpha}S_{\beta}\\
-S_{\beta}C_{\gamma}&S_{\beta}S_{\gamma}&C_{\beta}\end{bmatrix},

(150)

which is formed by the product of three rotation matrices,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{R}_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}=

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{R}_{3,\alpha}\boldsymbol{R}_{2,\beta}\boldsymbol{R}_{3,\gamma}

(151)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}C_{\alpha}&-S_{\alpha}&0\\
S_{\alpha}&C_{\alpha}&0\\
0&0&1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}C_{\beta}&0&S_{\beta}\\
0&1&0\\
-S_{\beta}&0&C_{\beta}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}C_{\gamma}&-S_{\gamma}&0\\
S_{\gamma}&C_{\gamma}&0\\
0&0&1\end{bmatrix}.

The inverse rotation matrix
\boldsymbol{R}^{-1}
is given by

\boldsymbol{R}^{-1}_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}=\boldsymbol{R}^{\rm T}_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}=\boldsymbol{R}_{-\gamma,-\beta,-\alpha}.

(152)

The Euler rotation matrix and its inverse correspond to the change of basis matrices between the initial and rotated vectorial bases,
\left(\boldsymbol{{\rm e}}_{X},\boldsymbol{{\rm e}}_{Y},\boldsymbol{{\rm e}}_{Z}\right)\rightarrow\left(\boldsymbol{{\rm e}}_{x},\boldsymbol{{\rm e}}_{y},\boldsymbol{{\rm e}}_{z}\right)
, which is expressed as

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{{\rm e}}_{x}\\
\boldsymbol{{\rm e}}_{y}\\
\boldsymbol{{\rm e}}_{z}\end{bmatrix}=\boldsymbol{R}_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{{\rm e}}_{X}\\
\boldsymbol{{\rm e}}_{Y}\\
\boldsymbol{{\rm e}}_{Z}\end{bmatrix},

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{{\rm e}}_{X}\\
\boldsymbol{{\rm e}}_{Y}\\
\boldsymbol{{\rm e}}_{Z}\end{bmatrix}=\boldsymbol{R}_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{{\rm e}}_{x}\\
\boldsymbol{{\rm e}}_{y}\\
\boldsymbol{{\rm e}}_{z}\end{bmatrix}.

(153)

Besides the rotation
\boldsymbol{U}\rightarrow\boldsymbol{U}_{{\rm rot}}
of any vector
\boldsymbol{U}
through the Euler angles
\alpha,\beta,
and
\gamma
in the Cartesian coordinate system
S\left\{X,Y,Z\right\}
is expressed as
\boldsymbol{U}_{{\rm rot}}=\boldsymbol{R}_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}\boldsymbol{U}
.

In spherical coordinates, the transformation relations are deduced from Eq. (
153
) by making use of trigonometric identities. We denote by
\left(\hat{r},\hat{\theta},\hat{\varphi}\right)
the spherical coordinates associated with the initial frame of reference,
\mathcal{R}
, where
\hat{r}
designates the radial coordinate,
\hat{\theta}
the colatitude, and
\hat{\varphi}
the longitude. The analogous coordinates in the rotated frame of reference,
\mathcal{R}_{{\rm rot}}
, are denoted by
\left(r,\theta,\varphi\right)
, with
r=\hat{r}
. In the forward transformation,
\left(\hat{r},\hat{\theta},\hat{\varphi}\right)\rightarrow\left(r,\theta,\varphi\right)

\left(\hat{\theta},\hat{\varphi}\right)
and
\left(\theta,\varphi\right)
are given by
(
Varshalovich et al. 1988
, Sect. 1.4.1, Eqs (2-3))

\displaystyle\cos\theta

\displaystyle=\cos\hat{\theta}\cos\beta+\sin\hat{\theta}\sin\beta\cos\left(\hat{\varphi}-\alpha\right),

(154)

\displaystyle{\rm cot}\left(\varphi+\gamma\right)

\displaystyle={\rm cot}\left(\hat{\varphi}-\alpha\right)\cos\beta-\frac{{\rm cot}\hat{\theta}\sin\beta}{\sin\left(\hat{\varphi}-\alpha\right)},

(155)

while the inverse relations are

\displaystyle\cos\hat{\theta}

\displaystyle=\cos\theta\cos\beta-\sin\theta\sin\beta\cos\left(\varphi+\gamma\right),

(156)

\displaystyle{\rm cot}\left(\hat{\varphi}-\alpha\right)

\displaystyle={\rm cot}\left(\varphi+\gamma\right)\cos\beta+\frac{{\rm cot}\theta\sin\beta}{\sin\left(\varphi+\gamma\right)}.

(157)

As an example, we consider the rotation generating the coordinate system of the ocean basin from the geocentric coordinate system rotating with the planet. From the definition of the ocean centre in the usual geocentric frame of reference, we obtain

\boldsymbol{{\rm e}}_{z}=\cos\hat{\varphi}_{{\rm oc}}\sin\hat{\theta}_{{\rm oc}}\boldsymbol{{\rm e}}_{X}+\sin\hat{\varphi}_{{\rm oc}}\sin\hat{\theta}_{{\rm oc}}\boldsymbol{{\rm e}}_{Y}+\cos\hat{\theta}_{{\rm oc}}\boldsymbol{{\rm e}}_{Z}.

(158)

\boldsymbol{{\rm e}}_{Z}
through a rotation of Euler angles
\alpha
,
\beta
, and
\gamma
, we can write
\boldsymbol{{\rm e}}_{z}=\boldsymbol{R}_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}\left(0,0,1\right)^{\rm T}
, where we made use of the standard Euler rotation matrix defined in Eq. (
150
). It follows

\boldsymbol{{\rm e}}_{z}=\cos\alpha\sin\beta\boldsymbol{{\rm e}}_{X}+\sin\alpha\sin\beta\boldsymbol{{\rm e}}_{Y}+\cos\beta\boldsymbol{{\rm e}}_{Z}.

(159)

By comparing Eqs. (
158
) and (
159
), we identify
\beta=\hat{\theta}_{{\rm oc}}
and
\alpha=\hat{\varphi}_{{\rm oc}}
. Considering the inverse transformation,
\boldsymbol{{\rm e}}_{Z}=\boldsymbol{R}_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}^{\rm T}\left(0,0,1\right)^{\rm T}
, we get

\boldsymbol{{\rm e}}_{Z}=-\sin\beta\cos\gamma\boldsymbol{{\rm e}}_{x}+\sin\beta\sin\gamma\boldsymbol{{\rm e}}_{y}+\cos\beta\boldsymbol{{\rm e}}_{z},

(160)

which shows that
\boldsymbol{{\rm e}}_{Z}
does not depend on the rotation through the angle
\alpha
in the oceanic frame of reference.

We note that the angle
\gamma
describes a rotation of coordinates around the axis defined by
\boldsymbol{{\rm e}}_{z}
. Since the ocean is circular, the geometry remains unchanged through this rotation. The angle
\gamma
can therefore be set to
\gamma=0
, which allows the position of the North pole in the ocean’s coordinate system to be simplified to
\boldsymbol{{\rm e}}_{Z}=-\sin\beta\boldsymbol{{\rm e}}_{x}+\cos\beta\boldsymbol{{\rm e}}_{z}

\left(\theta_{{\rm NP}},\varphi_{{\rm NP}}\right)=\left(\beta,\pi\right)
. At the end, the ocean’s coordinate system is simply obtained from the usual geocentric coordinate system through a rotation of Euler angles
\left(\alpha,\beta,\gamma\right)=\left(\hat{\varphi}_{{\rm oc}},\hat{\theta}_{{\rm oc}},0\right)
. We shall emphasise that the gyroscopic coefficients of the tidal theory only depend on the colatitude of the supercontinent’s centre since
\beta
is the only angle necessary to define the position of the spin axis in the coordinate system of the ocean basin. Moreover, the rotation of angle
\alpha
can be ignored owing to the periodicity of the tidal response across the longitudinal coordinate,
\hat{\varphi}
, which implies that the longitude of the supercontinent’s centre can be set to
\hat{\varphi}_{{\rm S}}=0
without any loss of generality.

D_{l,-l}^{l}&\ldots&D_{l,m}^{l}&\ldots&D_{l,l}^{l}\end{bmatrix}.

Thus, the set of SPHs associated with the rotated coordinate system, denoted by

(167)

which is analogous to the change of basis equation given by Eq. (

(168)

We remark that

, the symbol
\boldsymbol{I}
referring to the identity matrix. As a consequence, the rotation transformation is conservative: going back and forth through the transformation does not induce any loss of information. Figure

\beta_{\pm k,\pm j}\left(\hat{\theta}_{{\rm oc}}=\pi\right)=-\beta_{\pm k,\pm j}\left(\hat{\theta}_{{\rm oc}}=0\right)
. If the ocean is equatorial (
\hat{\theta}_{{\rm oc}}=\pi/2
), the gyroscopic coefficients only mix up eigenmodes of neighbouring orders (
\left|m_{j}-m_{k}\right|=1
). Between these two extremal configurations, the gyroscopic coefficients are the sum of one component in
\cos\hat{\theta}_{{\rm oc}}
associated with eigenmodes having the same order, and one component in
\sin\hat{\theta}_{{\rm oc}}
associated with eigenmodes having neighbouring orders. The gyroscopic coefficients that do not mix up potential and stream functions (
\beta_{k,-j}
and
\beta_{-k,-j}
) are pure imaginary numbers. Conversely, the coefficients that mix up the functions (
\beta_{k,-j}
and
\beta_{-k,j}
) are pure real numbers.

By comparing the expressions given by Eqs. (
193
) and (
196
), we remark that the
\beta_{k,j}
and
\beta_{-k,-j}
converge towards the same asymptotic values as
\theta_{0}\rightarrow 180^{\degree}
since both the SCHNs and SCHDs converge towards the standard SPHs for the
\ell^{2}
-norm introduced in Eq. (
136
), as illustrated by Fig.
8
. To our knowledge, no recurrence formula exist between the ALFs of real degrees of the SCHNs and SCHDs except in the specific case of the hemispherical ocean, where the degrees are integers
(see
Webb 1980
;
Webb 1982
;
Farhat et al. 2022a
, and Table
4
)
. As a consequence, the integrals in Eqs. (
193
-
196
) are evaluated numerically in practice.

Figure

shows the gyroscopic coefficients characterising an ocean basin of angular radius
\theta_{0}=130^{\degree}
for three typical configuration:
\hat{\theta}_{{\rm oc}}=0^{\degree}
(polar ocean),
\hat{\theta}_{{\rm oc}}=45^{\degree}
(mid-latitude ocean), and
\hat{\theta}_{{\rm oc}}=90^{\degree}

k
and

are sorted in ascending order of degrees and orders in the displayed plots, which emphasises the fact that gyroscopic coefficients only couple eigenmodes with the same or neighbouring orders.

The coefficients
\upsilon_{k,q}
introduced in Eq. (
51
) are evaluated similarly as the gyroscopic coefficients. These coefficients are defined as

\upsilon_{k,q}\equiv\int_{\mathcal{O}}\!\!\boldsymbol{{\rm e}}_{r}\!\cdot\!\left(\overline{\tilde{\nabla}\Psi_{k}}\times\tilde{\nabla}Y_{q}\right)\!{\rm d}S,

(197)

where the SPHs
Y_{q}
are written as products of functions of separated variables,

Y_{q}\left(\theta,\varphi\right)=\eta_{q}H_{q}\left(\theta\right){\rm e}^{im_{q}\varphi}.

(198)

After an integration by part, the coefficients are simply expressed as

\upsilon_{k,q}=i2\pi\gamma_{k}\eta_{q}m_{k}\delta_{m_{k},m_{q}}\left[G_{k}\left(\theta_{0}\right)H_{q}\left(\theta_{0}\right)-G_{k}\left(0\right)H_{q}\left(0\right)\right].

(199)

We note that the
\upsilon_{k,q}
are zero if
m_{k}=0
. Besides, if
\left|m_{k}\right|>0
, the Dirichlet condition satisfied by the
\Psi_{k}
at
\theta_{0}
implies that
G_{k}\left(\theta_{0}\right)=0
, and the polar condition of
Y_{q}
implies that
H_{q}\left(0\right)=0
. As a consequence,
\upsilon_{k,q}=0
for all
k
and
q
.

Appendix H
Solid tidal Love numbers

The gravitational and load Love numbers introduced in Eqs. (
47
) and (
48
) describe the tidal response of the planet’s solid part in the general case as far as the latter is spherically symmetric. In the present study, these parameters are formulated analytically from the prescriptions given by
Bolmont et al. 2020
for solid Earth (see Table 2 of the article). This formulation is based on the tidal model described in
Tobie et al. 2005
, which integrates the equations of the elasto-gravitational theory
(
Takeuchi & Saito 1972
)
for realistic radial profiles of background quantities. As a first step,
Bolmont et al. 2020
use this tidal model with the background profiles computed from the internal structure model of
Sotin et al. 2007
. As a second step, they fit the parameters of simplified models to the obtained solutions. These simplified models describe the rheology of equivalent bodies with uniform interiors, for which the Love numbers can be explicitly formulated in terms of the degrees of the associated SPHs
(
Munk & MacDonald 1960
)
.

The degree-
l
Love numbers of a homogeneous solid body of mass
M_{\rm core}
, radius
R_{\rm core}
, and uniform shear modulus
\mu
, are expressed as
(
Munk & MacDonald 1960
, e.g.)

\left\{k_{l}^{\sigma},h_{l}^{\sigma},k_{{\rm L};l}^{\sigma},h_{{\rm L};l}^{\sigma}\right\}\!=\!\frac{1}{1+\tilde{\mu}_{l}^{\sigma}}\!\left\{\!\frac{3}{2\left(l-1\right)},\frac{2l+1}{2\left(l-1\right)},-1,-\frac{2l+1}{3}\!\right\}\!.

(200)

In the above equation, the degree-
l
dimensionless shear modulus
\tilde{\mu}_{l}^{\sigma}
is defined as

\tilde{\mu}_{l}^{\sigma}\equiv A_{l}\tilde{\mu},

(201)

where
\tilde{\mu}
designates the dimensionless visco-elastic rigidity accounting for the rheology of the material, and
A_{l}
the dimensionless parameter given by
(
Castillo-Rogez et al. 2011
;
Efroimsky 2012
)

A_{l}\equiv\frac{4\left(2l^{2}+4l+3\right)\pi R_{\rm core}^{4}\mu}{3lGM_{\rm core}^{2}}.

(202)

Since the oceanic layer is thin in the present study, the mass and radius of the solid part can be approximated by the planet mass and radius, respectively, which yields
M_{\rm core}\approx M_{\rm p}
and
R_{\rm core}\approx R_{\rm p}
.

The normalised rigidity
\tilde{\mu}
is a complex parameter that describes the rheological behaviour of the solid part in the frequency domain. This parameter is determined by the adopted rheological model. For standard models such as the Maxwell visco-elastic rheology
(
Correia et al. 2014
, e.g.)
, it is expressed as a simple function of the tidal frequency,
\sigma
. Following
Bolmont et al. 2020
, we opt for an Andrade rheology. Similarly as the Maxwell rheology, the Andrade model describes the visco-elastic deformation of the material. However, it additionally accounts for the unrecoverable creep forming the anelastic component of the response, which affects the frequency-dependence of tidal dissipation in the high-frequency range
(
Efroimsky 2012
, e.g.)
. In the Andrade rheology,
\tilde{\mu}
is expressed as
(
Castillo-Rogez et al. 2011
, e.g.)

\tilde{\mu}=\frac{1}{1+\left(i\sigma\tau_{\rm A}\right)^{-\alpha}\Gamma\left(1+\alpha\right)+\left(i\sigma\tau_{\rm M}\right)^{-1}},

(203)

where
\tau_{\rm M}
designate the Maxwell time,
\tau_{\rm A}
the Andrade time,
\Gamma
the Gamma function introduced in Eq. (
112
), and
\alpha
the parameter determining the duration of the transient response in the primary creep
(
Castelnau et al. 2008
;
Castillo-Rogez et al. 2011
)
.
