Carl M. Bender, Daniel W. Hook

Abstract
It is generally assumed that a Hamiltonian for a physically acceptable quantum system (one that has a positive-definite spectrum and obeys the requirement of unitarity) must be Hermitian. However, a 𝒫 ​ 𝒯 \mathcal{PT} -symmetric Hamiltonian can also define a physically acceptable quantum-mechanical system even if the Hamiltonian is not Hermitian. The study of 𝒫 ​ 𝒯 \mathcal{PT} -symmetric quantum systems is a young and extremely active research area in both theoretical and experimental physics. The purpose of this Review is to provide established scientists as well as graduate students with a compact, easy-to-read introduction to this field that will enable them to understand more advanced publications and to begin their own theoretical or experimental research activity. The ideas and techniques of 𝒫 ​ 𝒯 \mathcal{PT} symmetry have been applied in the context of many different branches of physics. This Review introduces the concepts of 𝒫 ​ 𝒯 \mathcal{PT} symmetry by focusing on elementary one-dimensional 𝒫 ​ 𝒯 \mathcal{PT} -symmetric quantum and classical mechanics and relies in particular on oscillator models to illustrate and explain the basic properties of 𝒫 ​ 𝒯 \mathcal{PT} -symmetric quantum theory.

I
Introduction to
\mathcal{PT}
symmetry

The study of
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric quantum mechanics began in 1998 with the observation that a quantum-mechanical Hamiltonian that is invariant under combined space reflection (parity)
\mathcal{P}
and time reversal
\mathcal{T}
can have a spectrum that is entirely real and bounded below even if the Hamiltonian is not Hermitian
Bender and Boettcher 1998b
. This letter was followed shortly thereafter by a second which demonstrated that when the spectrum of a
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric Hamiltonian is real and bounded below, the Hamiltonian defines a
unitary
(probability-conserving) quantum-mechanical theory
Bender et al. 2002
. Thus, the mathematical constraint that a Hamiltonian operator be Hermitian in the Dirac sense
H^{\dagger}=H
, where
{\dagger}
represents combined complex conjugation and matrix transposition, can be weakened to the more physically intuitive constraint of spacetime reflection invariance (
\mathcal{PT}
symmetry):

[H,\mathcal{PT}]=0.

(1.1)

Because the condition of
\mathcal{PT}
invariance is a
weaker
constraint than Hermiticity, it allows one to study vastly many new non-Hermitian quantum theories, some of which exhibit surprising physical properties. Thus,
\mathcal{PT}
symmetry offers a rich area for theoretical and experimental investigation. There are already over 10,000 papers published and numerous reviews, books, and theses written on the subject of
\mathcal{PT}
symmetry. Many research grants have been awarded and numerous conferences and symposia have been held. Research activity on
\mathcal{PT}
symmetry is accelerating (see Fig.
1
).

.

I.1
Mathematical background

The study of
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric quantum mechanics draws heavily on complex analysis because the condition of
\mathcal{PT}
symmetry arises when we deform a Hermitian theory, such as the quantum harmonic oscillator, into the non-Hermitian
complex
domain. We will see that
\mathcal{PT}
symmetry marries the fundamental concepts of quantum physics with the mathematical theory of complex variables.

While complex numbers have been studied by mathematicians for many centuries, complex numbers did not play an essential role in the formulation of physical theories until the twentieth century when the study of quantum mechanics began. The complex number
i
does not even appear in early studies of special and general relativity. It was not until many years later, when the representations of the homogeneous Lorentz group were analyzed in depth, did it become clear that fermionic spinor representations, which require complex numbers, were physically relevant. In particular, the
fundamental
spin-
\tfrac{1}{2}
representation of the Lorentz group is complex
Gelfand et al. 1963
;
Gelfand and Naimark 1962
.

The homogeneous Lorentz group is defined as the set of all
real

4\times 4
matrices (matrices whose 16 elements are all real numbers) that map the spacetime vector
x^{\mu}
into another spacetime vector
x^{\prime\mu}
in such a way that
x^{\mu}x_{\mu}
remains invariant:

x^{2}+y^{2}+z^{2}-t^{2}=x^{\prime 2}+y^{\prime 2}+z^{\prime 2}-t^{\prime 2}.

(1.2)

This condition guarantees that the speed of light is the same in all inertial frames of reference.

In the context of the Lorentz group,
\mathcal{PT}
symmetry appears naturally and at a fundamental level. The parity matrix
\mathcal{P}
is an element of the Lorentz group because it flips the sign of the spatial coordinates,

\mathcal{P}:\,(x,y,z,t)\to(-x,-y,-z,t),

and thus leaves
x^{2}+y^{2}+z^{2}-t^{2}
invariant. The time-reversal matrix
\mathcal{T}
is also an element of the Lorentz group because it flips the sign of the time coordinate,

\mathcal{T}:\,(x,y,z,t)\to(x,y,z,-t),

and again leaves
x^{2}+y^{2}+z^{2}-t^{2}
invariant. The
\mathcal{PT}
matrix changes the sign of all four spacetime coordinates,

\mathcal{PT}:\,(x,y,z,t)\to(-x,-y,-z,-t),

and thus it too is an element of the Lorentz group.

Mathematically, the real Lorentz group is a six-parameter continuous group consisting of four
disconnected
components, as indicated schematically in Fig.
2
. The first part, as shown at the top of Fig.
2
, is called the
proper orthochronous Lorentz group
(POLG). The POLG contains all the elements of the homogeneous Lorentz group that are continuously connected in group space to the
identity
element, which is the transformation that leaves the vector
(x,y,z,t)
unchanged. The POLG is a subgroup of the homogeneous Lorentz group. The second component of the Lorentz group (the left part of Fig.
2
) consists of all of the Lorentz transformations in the POLG multiplied by
\mathcal{P}
. This part of the Lorentz group is not a subgroup because it does not contain an identity element. The third component of the Lorentz group (right part of Fig.
2
) consists of all of the Lorentz transformations in the POLG multiplied by
\mathcal{T}
. Again, this part of the Lorentz group is not a subgroup. The fourth part of the Lorentz group consists of all of the elements of the POLG multiplied by the spacetime reflection operator
\mathcal{PT}
and it too is not a subgroup.

Figure 2:
Schematic picture of the group space of the homogeneous real Lorentz group. This continuous group consists of four disconnected parts: At the top of the diagram is the proper orthochronous Lorentz group (POLG); the POLG contains the identity element and is a subgroup of the Lorentz group. The other three components of the homogeneous real Lorentz group are the POLG multiplied by the parity (space-reflection) operator
\mathcal{P}
, the time-reversal operator
\mathcal{T}
, and the spacetime reflection operator
\mathcal{PT}
. If we extend the real Lorentz group to the
complex
Lorentz group
Gelfand et al. 1963
, there are now only
two
disconnected parts. Continuous paths through complex group space (indicated as vertical and horizontal lines) connect the top and bottom components and the left and right components of the real Lorentz group.

For many years after the discovery of special relativity, the homogeneous Lorentz group pictured in Fig.
2
was believed to be the fundamental symmetry group of the natural world. However, Lee and Yang theorized that space reflection (parity)
\mathcal{P}
is not a fundamental symmetry of the universe
Lee and Yang 1956
; the 1957 Nobel Prize was awarded for this work. The work of Lee and Yang implies that an experiment can be designed that achieves different experimental results in left-handed and right-handed laboratories; that is, two laboratories that are spatial reflections (mirror images) of one another. Such an experiment was performed by Wu
Wu et al. 1957
, for which she was awarded the Wolf Prize in 1978.

Subsequently, Cronin and Fitch were awarded the 1980 Nobel Prize for demonstrating that time reversal
\mathcal{T}
is also not a symmetry of nature
Christenson et al. 1964
. Their experiment demonstrates, in principle, that a laboratory traveling forward in time is experimentally distinguishable from a physically identical laboratory that is traveling backward in time. The implication of all this work is that the Lorentz transformations in the left and the right portions of the Lorentz group illustrated in Fig.
2
are not fundamental symmetries of the universe.

These discoveries regarding parity and time reversal raise an obvious question
regarding the Lorentz transformations in the lower portion of Fig.
2
. If we have two laboratories, one traveling forward in time and the other space-reflected and traveling backward in time, is there an experiment that will yield different results in these two laboratories? Are these two laboratories experimentally distinguishable or is combined
\mathcal{PT}
reflection (spacetime reflection) a fundamental discrete symmetry of nature?

To examine this question, it helps to extend the real homogeneous Lorentz group to the
complex
homogeneous Lorentz group, which is the set of all
complex

4\times 4
matrices that leave
x^{2}+y^{2}+z^{2}-t^{2}
invariant. The complex homogeneous Lorentz group consists of two, and not four, disconnected parts. Figure
2
shows that there is a continuous path (horizontal line) through complex-Lorentz-group space connecting the left and right portions of the real Lorentz group. There is also a continuous path (vertical line) connecting the upper and lower parts of the real Lorentz group
Streater and Wightman 2000
. This complex group structure suggests that since it is natural to extend the real number system to the complex number system and since complex numbers appear commonly in modern physics, one should consider
\mathcal{PT}
reflection as a possible symmetry of some physical theories.

I.2
\mathcal{CPT}
theorem

Extending the real homogeneous Lorentz group to the complex Lorentz group is the key ingredient in the proof of a profound theorem in quantum field theory and particle physics known as the
\mathcal{CPT}
theorem
Streater and Wightman 2000
. This theorem states that two identical physical measurements, where the first measurement is done in a laboratory traveling forward in time and consisting of ordinary matter and the second is done in a space-reflected laboratory traveling backward in time and consisting of antimatter, will return identical results.

The proof of the
\mathcal{CPT}
theorem requires several crucial assumptions made in axiomatic quantum field theory about the nature of particle interactions, such as unitarity, locality, causality, positivity of the particle spectrum, and Hermiticity of the Hamiltonian. These assumptions allow one to extend the real Lorentz group to the complex Lorentz group and to perform an analytic continuation in group space from the upper to the lower portion of Fig.
2
. Along this path the state of an elementary particle undergoes a transition from the original particle to its corresponding antiparticle. [The proof of the
\mathcal{CPT}
theorem fills an entire book
Streater and Wightman 2000
.]

We do not discuss quantum field theory, antiparticles, and the charge-conjugation operator in particle physics here; this Review is limited to a discussion of quantum mechanics. However, we will see that there is a fascinating connection between the
\mathcal{CPT}
theorem and the results on
\mathcal{PT}
symmetry presented in this Review. The development in this Review can be viewed as establishing the quantum-mechanical
converse
of the
\mathcal{CPT}
theorem.

We begin by explaining how to construct various non-Hermitian quantum-mechanical theories (not field theories) that are
\mathcal{PT}
invariant and we examine their properties. The spectra of such theories are often entirely real and bounded below. We then show that when the spectrum of a
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric Hamiltonian is real, the Hamiltonian has a
hidden invariance
; there exists a new quantum-mechanical operator
\mathcal{C}
that commutes with the
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric Hamiltonian. (We call this operator
\mathcal{C}
because its properties closely resemble those of the charge-conjugation operator
\mathcal{C}
used in the
\mathcal{CPT}
theorem of particle physics.) We use this
\mathcal{C}
operator to construct an inner product endowed with a positive-definite norm and establish that the
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric Hamiltonian defines a
unitary
quantum-mechanical theory.

The organization of this Review is straightforward: Section
II
discusses the construction and properties of
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric Hamiltonians with special emphasis on the calculation of eigenvalues. It is shown how to obtain
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric quantum-mechanical theories as complex deformations of Hermitian theories. These examples emphasize the intimate connection between the physical constraint of
\mathcal{PT}
symmetry and the mathematical theory of complex variables. Section
III
examines the process of complex analytical deformation in detail and Sec.
IV
gives a quantum and classical analysis of
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric upside-down potentials. Section
V
describes the remarkable properties of the
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric quartic anharmonic oscillator at both the quantum-mechanical and the classical-mechanical levels.

Having discussed the properties of
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric eigenvalue problems for a variety of quantum-mechanical models, we then address some crucial theoretical issues regarding
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric quantum mechanics. In Sec.
VI
we construct the
\mathcal{C}
operator, which in turn is used to define the
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric inner product and the Hilbert space. (In conventional Hermitian quantum mechanics we already know what the inner product is even before we know the Hamiltonian, but in
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric quantum mechanics the inner product is determined by the Hamiltonian itself.) Section
VII
briefly summarizes some of the current research on
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric quantum-mechanical systems and suggests possibilities for future research.

Because the study of
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric quantum theory involves heavy use of complex-variable theory, we include an Appendix that briefly summarizes the historical interest in extending real mathematics to complex mathematics, the study of which began in earnest in the sixteenth century. Complex calculus is powerful and can often solve problems that the calculus of real variables is incapable of solving. Physicists have long understood the power of complex analysis as a calculational tool, but complex numbers played no role in formulating physical theories until the 20th century. Since its appearance in quantum theory, the number
i
has played an increasingly important role in physics.

II
Examples of
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric Hamiltonians

How does one construct a
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric quantum-mechanical theory? An elementary recipe for creating a
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric Hamiltonian is to begin with a quantum-mechanical Hamiltonian that is both Hermitian and
\mathcal{PT}
symmetric, and then to perform a
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric
deformation
of this Hamiltonian
Bender and Boettcher 1998b
;
Bender 2005
;
Bender et al. 2019
. The resulting Hamiltonian, which is a complex analytic continuation of the original Hermitian Hamiltonian, is still
\mathcal{PT}
symmetric but it is no longer Hermitian. Nevertheless, the deformed Hamiltonian operator may continue to have an entirely real spectrum whose energy levels remain well separated and do not cross. [For further examples see
Fring and Znojil 2008
;
Znojil and Tater 2001
;
Lévai and Znojil 2000
;
Lévai and Znojil 2001
].

II.1
An elementary
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric Hamiltonian

To illustrate the construction of a
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric Hamiltonian we perform a simple
\mathcal{PT}
deformation of the quantum harmonic-oscillator Hamiltonian

H=\mbox{$\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}$}p^{2}+\mbox{$\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}$}x^{2}.

(2.1)

This Hamiltonian is Hermitian
H^{\dagger}=H
and the eigenvalues
E_{n}
of this Hamiltonian are all real and positive:

E_{n}=n+\mbox{$\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}$}\quad(n=0,\,1,\,2,\,...).

(2.2)

The
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{T}
operators are conventionally defined as follows: The parity operator
\mathcal{P}
is a linear Hermitian operator that performs space reflection, so
\mathcal{P}^{2}=I
(
I
is the unity operator) and
\mathcal{P}^{-1}=\mathcal{P}
. Under parity reflection both the position operator
x
and the momentum operator
p
change sign:

\mathcal{P}x\mathcal{P}^{-1}=-x,\qquad\mathcal{P}p\mathcal{P}^{-1}=-p.

(2.3)

The time-reversal operator
\mathcal{T}
is also a reflection operator and for bosonic systems
\mathcal{T}^{2}=I
, so
\mathcal{T}^{-1}=\mathcal{T}
. Time reversal leaves
x
invariant but changes the sign of
p
:

\mathcal{T}x\mathcal{T}^{-1}=x,\qquad\mathcal{T}p\mathcal{T}^{-1}=-p.

(2.4)

However, unlike the
\mathcal{P}
operator, the
\mathcal{T}
operator is not linear;
\mathcal{T}
is said to be
antilinear
because it changes the sign of the complex number
i
:

\mathcal{T}i\mathcal{T}^{-1}=-i.

(2.5)

The operations of reversing space and reversing time are independent and thus
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{T}
commute
[\mathcal{P},\mathcal{T}]=0
.

According to the definitions above we see immediately that the Hamiltonian (
2.1
) satisfies (
1.1
) and thus this Hamiltonian is
\mathcal{PT}
symmetric as well as Hermitian. (In this case,
H
is
independently
invariant under
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{T}
reflections.) The combination
ix
is not Hermitian, but it is
\mathcal{PT}
symmetric. Therefore, we can use the
ix
operator to perform a
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric deformation of the Hamiltonian
H
: Let
\varepsilon
be a
real
parameter called a
deformation parameter
. The simplest kind of deformation is an
additive
deformation, so we use an additive deformation to construct the deformed Hamiltonian

H=\mbox{$\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}$}p^{2}+\mbox{$\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}$}x^{2}+\varepsilon ix.

(2.6)

The Hamiltonian (
2.6
) is not Hermitian when
\varepsilon\neq 0
because
ix
is not Hermitian, but
H

is

\mathcal{PT}
symmetric because
H
commutes with the
\mathcal{PT}
operator.

A brief calculation in which we replace
x
by
x-i\varepsilon
in the time-independent Schrödinger equation associated with this Hamiltonian verifies the surprising result that the eigenvalues of
H
in (
2.6
) are all
real
:

E_{n}=n+\mbox{$\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}$}\big(1+\varepsilon^{2})\quad(n=0,\,1,\,2,\,...).

(2.7)

Although the eigenvalues of
H
in (
2.6
) are real, the corresponding eigenfunctions
\psi_{n}(x)
are complex. However, they still satisfy the original undeformed boundary condition on
\psi_{n}(x)
; namely, that
\psi_{n}(x)
vanishes exponentially as
x\to\pm\infty
. An important property of these eigenfunctions is that they are all
\mathcal{PT}
symmetric; that is,
\psi_{n}(x)
remains invariant (apart from an overall multiplicative phase) if we replace
i
by
-i
(that is, take the complex conjugate) and replace
x
by
-x
. Furthermore, the deformed eigenfunctions continue to be orthogonal,

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx\,\psi_{m}(x)\psi_{n}(x)=0\quad(m\neq n),

for all real
\varepsilon
.

This example suggests that there are infinitely many more
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric Hamiltonians than Hermitian Hamiltonians. In place of the single Hermitian harmonic-oscillator Hamiltonian in (
2.1
), we now have a continuously infinite class of non-Hermitian Hamiltonians in (
2.6
) parametrized by
\varepsilon
. All of these Hamiltonians have entirely real positive spectra and they define perfectly acceptable quantum-mechanical theories. The Hermitian Hamiltonian corresponding to
\varepsilon=0
is just one special case that is not fundamentally different from the other Hamiltonians. This shows that while Hermiticity is a sufficient condition for having a real spectrum, it is not a necessary condition. This also suggests that we might adopt the view that
\mathcal{PT}
symmetry is a fundamental property of a quantum Hamiltonian with Hermiticity being a mere special case.

II.2
Some
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric Hamiltonians have complex spectra

The example above might lead one to conjecture that the property of
\mathcal{PT}
symmetry in (
1.1
) is strong enough to establish that the eigenvalues of
any

\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric Hamiltonian are all real. This conjecture is false, but it is nevertheless instructive to examine the following incorrect attempt to prove this conjecture
Bender 2005
;
Bender et al. 2019
.

Let
\psi
be a (time-independent) eigenstate of the Hamiltonian operator
H
with eigenvalue
E
:

H\psi=E\psi.

(2.8)

Assume that
H
is
\mathcal{PT}
symmetric; that is,
H
commutes with
\mathcal{PT}
:
[H,\mathcal{PT}]=0
. Next, recall a theorem from linear algebra that is often quoted in elementary quantum-mechanics courses:
If two operators
A
and
B
commute, then they are simultaneously diagonalizable
; that is, an eigenstate of
A
is also an eigenstate of
B
. For example, if
H
commutes with
\mathcal{P}
, then the energy eigenstates of
H
have definite parity. On the basis of this theorem, one might think that if
H
commutes with
\mathcal{PT}
, then
\psi
must also be an eigenstate of
\mathcal{PT}
:

\mathcal{PT}\psi=\lambda\psi,

(2.9)

where
\lambda
is an eigenvalue of the
\mathcal{PT}
operator. (Warning: This assumption is wrong!)

Let us examine the eigenvalue equation (
2.9
). Because
\mathcal{P}^{2}=\mathcal{T}^{2}=I
and
[\mathcal{P},\mathcal{T}]=0
it follows that
(\mathcal{PT})^{2}=I
. Also,
\mathcal{T}
performs complex conjugation.
Thus, if we multiply (
2.9
) on the left by
\mathcal{PT}
, we obtain

\psi=\lambda^{*}\lambda\psi.

(2.10)

This proves that
|\lambda|=1
. Thus,
the eigenvalues of the
\mathcal{PT}
operator lie on the unit circle in the complex plane.
This is an interesting complex generalization of the result that the eigenvalues of the
\mathcal{P}
operator are
\pm 1
; that is, that the eigenvalues of
\mathcal{P}
lie on a
one-dimensional
unit circle on the real axis (instead of on a two-dimensional circle in the complex plane).

Finally, we multiply the eigenvalue equation (
2.8
) on the left by the
\mathcal{PT}
operator and divide the resulting equation by
\lambda
, which is allowed because we have shown that
\lambda
is nonzero. We are therefore tempted to conclude that

E=E^{*}.

(2.11)

While it is correct that
|\lambda|=1
, this conclusion is false!

The mistake in this argument is that we misquoted the elementary theorem above; we neglected to say that
A
and
B
must be
linear
operators. It is not necessarily true that an eigenstate of
H
is also an eigenstate of
\mathcal{PT}
because while
\mathcal{PT}
commutes with
H
,
\mathcal{PT}
is not a
linear
operator;
\mathcal{PT}
is
antilinear
because
\mathcal{T}
performs complex conjugation. However, we have established an important result:
If an eigenstate of a
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric Hamiltonian is also an eigenstate of
\mathcal{PT}
, then the corresponding energy eigenvalue is real.

Unfortunately, proving that an eigenstate of a
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric Hamiltonian is simultaneously an eigenstate of the
\mathcal{PT}
operator can be nontrivial; this must be done on a case-by-case basis for each
\mathcal{PT}
symmetric Hamiltonian. In some cases the proof may be difficult and one might have to resort to a convincing numerical analysis instead of a mathematical proof. Proving that the eigenvalues of
H
in (
2.6
) are real is easy, but a general technique for proving spectral reality for a given
\mathcal{PT}
symmetric Hamiltonian is unlikely to be found.

Nevertheless, there are infinitely many more non-Hermitian
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric Hamiltonians that have entirely real spectra than Hermitian Hamiltonians. These
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric Hamiltonians often describe physical systems having novel and experimentally verifiable properties. This is why the theoretical and experimental study of
\mathcal{PT}
symmetry has become so active.

II.3
Reality of eigenvalues

An energy measurement performed on an isolated quantum system will always return a real number. This number is the expectation value of the Hamiltonian
H
in the quantum state of the system and it is equal to one of the eigenvalues of
H
. Thus, the eigenvalues of a Hamiltonian that describes an isolated quantum system are always real.

Let us recall the standard proof that the energy eigenvalues of a Hermitian Hamiltonian are real. For simplicity, we assume that the quantum system is one-dimensional and defined on the whole real line and that the time-independent Schrödinger eigenvalue equation (
2.8
) has the simple form

-\psi^{\prime\prime}(x)+V(x)\psi(x)=E\psi(x)\quad(-\infty<x<\infty).

(2.12)

We then multiply (
2.12
) on the left by
\psi^{*}(x)
, integrate from
-\infty
to
\infty
, and integrate the first term on the left side by parts. The resulting equation reads

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx\,|\psi^{\prime}(x)|^{2}+\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx\,V(x)|\psi(x)|^{2}

\displaystyle\quad=E\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx\,|\psi(x)|^{2}.

(2.13)

Finally, we note that the potential
V(x)
is real if
H
is Hermitian, so all three integrals in (
2.13
) are real. This implies that
E
is real.

The key step in this argument is the integration by parts. Integration by parts is possible only if the eigenfunction
\psi(x)
vanishes sufficiently fast as
|x|\to\infty
. Thus, the proof that the eigenspectrum is real requires
more
than just the Hermiticity of
H
. Hermiticity, like the condition of
\mathcal{PT}
symmetry, is a
local
condition that holds (or does not hold) at each value of
x
; thus, one can immediately see if a Hamiltonian is Hermitian or
\mathcal{PT}
symmetric by mere inspection. However, the condition that the eigenfunctions vanish fast enough as
|x|\to\infty
to allow integration by parts is a nontrivial
global
(nonlocal) condition. These local and global conditions taken together are called
self-adjointness
. (Note that there is no difference between the condition of Hermiticity and self-adjointness for a
matrix
Hamiltonian because in that case the proof that the eigenvalues are real does not require an integration by parts.)

This condition of self-adjointness is crucial in quantum mechanics because it implies that the quantum theory is unitary (probability-conserving). In Sec.
VI
we show that if the eigenspectrum of a
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric Hamiltonian
H
is entirely real, then
H
defines a unitary theory of quantum mechanics. For many nontrivial
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric Hamiltonians, the spectrum has been proved to be entirely real, as we see in the next example.

II.4
Multiplicative deformation

Let us perform a
multiplicative
deformation of the harmonic-oscillator Hamiltonian (
2.1
) to obtain the non-Hermitian
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric class of Hamiltonians

H=p^{2}+x^{2}(ix)^{\varepsilon}.

(2.14)

This nontrivial Hamiltonian is not Hermitian, but the spectrum of
H
is entirely real and positive if
\varepsilon\geq 0

Bender and Boettcher 1998b
. In addition, the eigenfunctions are all
\mathcal{PT}
symmetric if
\varepsilon\geq 0
. However, if
\varepsilon<0
, only a finite number of the lowest-lying eigenvalues are real and the corresponding eigenfunctions are
\mathcal{PT}
symmetric. The rest of the eigenvalues are complex and the corresponding eigenfunctions are not
\mathcal{PT}
symmetric.

A plot of the lowest seven eigenvalues for
-1<\varepsilon<0.5
is shown in Fig.
3
. The range
\varepsilon>0
, in which the eigenvalues are all real, is called the
region of unbroken
\mathcal{PT}
symmetry
. In this region the eigenvalues are positive, discrete, and increase monotonically with increasing
\varepsilon
. At
\varepsilon=0
, the low edge of the region of unbroken
\mathcal{PT}
symmetry, the eigenvalues are just the harmonic-oscillator eigenvalues given in (
2.2
). The range
\varepsilon<0
is called the
region of broken
\mathcal{PT}
symmetry
. The boundary between these two regions, which we call the
\mathcal{PT}
transition

In general, whenever a
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric deformation of a
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric Hermitian Hamiltonian

{\rm det}(H-IE)=0,

(2.15)

which is a polynomial in
E

I=\mathcal{PT}\mathcal{PT}

\mathcal{PT}
through

\mathcal{PT}
cyclically around so that the operator
\mathcal{PT}\mathcal{PT}

\mathcal{PT}

\mathcal{PT}

\mathcal{PT}
commutes past

{\rm det}(H-IE^{*})=0.

(2.16)

Thus, if

are either real or come in complex-conjugate pairs, which is consistent with the plot in Fig.

\mathcal{PT}

The original optical-fiber experiments make use of two identical and parallel fibers

Furthermore, the energy flow into the fiber with gain is adjusted so that it balances the energy flowing out of the fiber with loss (see Fig.

Figure 5:
Schematic picture of the light fiber experiment.

There are two possibilities: (i) If the fiber system is strongly coupled, the light energy that flows into the fiber with gain readily flows across the boundary between the two fibers, where it then flows out of the fiber with loss. In this case the optical-fiber system is in
dynamic equilibrium
; that is, the total energy at each point along the fiber remains constant in time. (ii) If the fiber system is weakly coupled, the energy that is flowing into the fiber with gain is unable to flow easily across the boundary between the fibers and into the fiber with loss. In this case the optical-fiber system is not in dynamic equilibrium; the energy in the fiber with gain grows in time and the energy in the fiber with loss decays in time.

This optical-fiber system can be interpreted as being
\mathcal{PT}
symmetric if we define the
\mathcal{P}
operation as an exchange of the two fibers. The system is not
\mathcal{P}
symmetric because the fiber with loss becomes the fiber with gain, and the fiber with gain becomes the fiber with loss. However, if we also reverse time, the inflow of energy becomes outflow, outflow becomes inflow, and the original configuration is recovered.

The behavior of this classical optical-fiber system can be described mathematically by a
2\times 2
matrix that is
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric. This matrix contains a parameter
g
that represents the strength of the optical coupling between the two fibers. We will
reinterpret
this matrix as a
Hamiltonian
that describes a two-state quantum-mechanical system in which the coupling parameter
g
plays the role of a deformation parameter. An elementary study of this matrix predicts that the physical system will exhibit a
\mathcal{PT}
transition.

Let us consider first a simple quantum-mechanical system described by the
1\times 1
matrix Hamiltonian

H_{1}=[a+ib],

(2.17)

where
a
and
b
are real numbers. If the parameter
b
is nonzero, this Hamiltonian is non-Hermitian. The system described by
H_{1}
has no spatial extent; we can think of it as existing at just one point in space. The time-dependent Schrödinger equation associated with
H_{1}
is the first-order differential equation

i\psi^{\prime}(t)=(a+ib)\psi,

(2.18)

whose solution is

\psi(t)=Ce^{(-ai+b)t}\quad(C\penalty\ {\rm constant}).

Note that the wave function
\psi(t)
oscillates with frequency
a
, and if
b\neq 0
,
\psi(t)
grows or decays in time so the system described by
H_{1}
in (
2.17
) is not in equilibrium;
\psi(t)
grows or decays exponentially when
b
is positive or negative. (If
b
is negative, we have an elementary model of radioactive decay, where
b
is a measure of the half-life of the state.) The system described by
H_{1}
is a simple quantum-mechanical analog of a single classical optical fiber with loss or gain.

Next, we construct a
2\times 2
quantum-mechanical matrix Hamiltonian that describes
two
noninteracting systems like that in (
2.17
), one whose wave function grows in time and the other whose wave function decays in time:

H_{2}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}a+ib&0\\
0&a-ib\end{array}\right].

(2.19)

This matrix Hamiltonian describes two subsystems, one with loss and the other with gain. Thus, it serves as a quantum-mechanical analog of two independent optical fibers, one with gain and the other with loss, which are not in contact and thus cannot exchange energy.

While the physical system described by the Hamiltonian
H_{2}
is not in equilibrium if
b\neq 0
, we can achieve dynamic equilibrium by
coupling
the two subsystems. We model this coupling mathematically by replacing the zeros in the off-diagonal elements of
H_{2}
with the real coupling parameter
g
:

H_{\rm coupled}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}a+ib&g\\
g&a-ib\end{array}\right].

(2.20)

Now the subsystems with loss and with gain are symmetrically coupled and so this Hamiltonian models the situation in which the two optical fibers are in contact and light energy can leak from one fiber into the other.

The Hamiltonian matrix
H_{\rm coupled}
is not Hermitian, but it is
\mathcal{PT}
symmetric. To see why this is so, we define the parity operator
\mathcal{P}
, which interchanges the two subsystems, as the linear matrix operator

\mathcal{P}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}0&1\\
1&0\end{array}\right].

(2.21)

Observe that
\mathcal{P}
behaves as a
reflection operator
because
\mathcal{P}^{2}=I
(
I
is the identity matrix). Also,
\mathcal{P}
is conventionally Hermitian
\mathcal{P}^{\dagger}=\mathcal{P}
. Next, we define the time-reversal operation as complex conjugation because this changes the sign of
t
in the coupled Schrödinger equations for these matrix Hamiltonians. (As mentioned earlier in this section,
\mathcal{T}
is an antilinear operator.) With these definitions, both
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{T}
are reflection operators:

\mathcal{P}^{2}=I\penalty\ {\rm and}\penalty\ \mathcal{T}^{2}=I.

Also, as discussed earlier, parity reflection and time reversal are independent operations, so
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{T}
commute:

[\mathcal{P},\mathcal{T}]=0.

With these definitions of
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{T}
it is clear that while the Hamiltonian
H_{\rm coupled}
is not Hermitian,

H_{\rm coupled}^{\dagger}\neq H_{\rm coupled},

it is
\mathcal{PT}
symmetric:

\big[H_{\rm coupled},\mathcal{PT}\big]=0.

(2.22)

Even though
H_{\rm coupled}
is not Hermitian, if
g
is sufficiently large, the eigenvalues of
H_{\rm coupled}
become real. The eigenvalues
E
are the roots of the secular polynomial

\det\left(H_{\rm coupled}-IE\right)=E^{2}-2aE+a^{2}+b^{2}-g^{2},

(2.23)

where
I
is the identity matrix. As we expect from the discussion in
Subsec.
II.4
, this secular polynomial is
real
.

The roots of the polynomial (
2.23
) are

E_{\pm}=a\pm\sqrt{g^{2}-b^{2}}.

(2.24)

Hence, if the two subsystems are weakly coupled (
g^{2}<b^{2}
), then the eigenvalues are complex. In this case the total system is not in equilibrium because one eigenstate grows in time and the other decays in time. This is the region of
broken
\mathcal{PT}
symmetry
. However, if the subsystems are strongly coupled (
g>b
or
g<-b
), then the eigenvalues in (
2.24
) become real and the entire composite system is in dynamic equilibrium; the eigenstates oscillate in time and do not grow or decay. These are the regions of
unbroken
\mathcal{PT}
symmetry
. So, as a function of coupling parameter
g
, there are
two
transition points and there are
two
regions of unbroken
\mathcal{PT}
symmetry with a region of broken
\mathcal{PT}
symmetry sandwiched between them.

Interestingly, the situation here is unlike that for the Hamiltonian (
2.14
) discussed in Subsec.
II.4
. For that Hamiltonian there is just one transition point and at that transition point the Hamiltonian is Hermitian. In the current case, at each of the two transition points there is an abrupt transition between nonequilibrium and equilibrium states, but at these transition points the Hamiltonian does not become Hermitian.

This matrix model illustrates an important distinction between Hermitian and
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric systems. An unbroken
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric system resembles a closed and isolated Hermitian system because its energy levels are real and it is in dynamic equilibrium. However, it is
not
closed because it is still in contact with the external environment. In this optical model light energy is flowing (in a balanced way) into and out of the system. In contrast, a broken
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric system resembles an open system because it is not in dynamic equilibrium; however, unlike a typical open system the net probability flux vanishes and the system has
\mathcal{PT}
symmetry. Thus,
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric systems are a new class of systems whose physical properties are intermediate between open and closed systems.

The quantum harmonic-oscillator Hamiltonian

III.1
Complex asymptotic approximations

To explain complex deformation and to understand the differences between
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric quantum mechanics and Hermitian quantum mechanics it is essential to understand the nature of asymptotic approximations in the complex plane. An
asymptotic approximation
is a relation between two functions that is expressed as a limit
Bender and Orszag 1999
. This relation approximates the behavior of a function
f(x)
by a simpler function
g(x)
as
x
approaches a point
a
(which may be
\infty
). The symbol
\sim
is used to express an asymptotic approximation:

f(x)\sim g(x)\quad(x\to a).

This statement means that
\lim_{x\to a}[f(x)/g(x)]=1
.

In this Review we are specifically interested in the behavior of eigenfunctions as they approach the boundary of a region where the eigenfunctions are required to obey boundary conditions. The boundary is often the point at
|x|=\infty
. Usually, these eigenfunctions are so complicated that only very rarely are we able to solve for them in closed form and thus we need to use asymptotic methods to approximate the eigenfunctions.

Complex asymptotic relations are generally not valid as
x
approaches
a
in all complex directions; asymptotic approximations are typically valid in wedge-shaped regions of the complex plane called
Stokes sectors
. To describe a Stokes sector we specify two pieces of information: First, we specify the complex limit; for example,
|x|\to\infty
or
|x|\to a
. Second, we give the
angular range
of
x
in which this limit is valid:
\theta_{1}<{\rm arg}\,x<\theta_{2}
.

As an elementary example of a complex asymptotic approximation, let us approximate the function
\sinh(x)=\mbox{$\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}$}\big(e^{x}-e^{-x}\big)
for large
|x|
. For large positive
x
,
\sinh(x)
is well approximated by the simpler function
\mbox{$\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}$}e^{x}
because
e^{-x}
is negligible compared with
e^{x}
. This approximation continues to be valid for complex
x
if
{\rm Re}\,x
is large and positive; that is, if
|x|\to\infty
in a complex Stokes sector that is centered about the positive-real-
x
axis and that has an opening angle of
\pi
:

\sinh(x)\sim\mbox{$\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}$}e^{x}\quad\big(|x|\to\infty,\penalty\ -\tfrac{\pi}{2}<{\rm arg}\,x<\tfrac{\pi}{2}\big).

Outside this Stokes wedge
\sinh(x)
has a
different
asymptotic behavior because for large
|x|
with
{\rm Re}\,x<0
,
e^{x}
is negligible compared with
e^{-x}
:

\sinh(x)\sim-\mbox{$\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}$}e^{-x}\quad\big(|x|\to\infty,\penalty\ \tfrac{\pi}{2}<{\rm arg}\,x<\tfrac{3\pi}{2}\big).

This abrupt change in the complex asymptotic behavior of
\sinh(x)
for large
|x|
, which occurs at the edges
{\rm arg}\,x=\pm\tfrac{\pi}{2}
of the Stokes sectors, is a general phenomenon that is typical of asymptotic approximations. The edges of Stokes sectors are called
Stokes lines
.

As we see in the next subsection, because the boundary conditions on solutions to the time-independent Schrödinger equation are valid inside Stokes sectors, these
boundary conditions distinguish
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric quantum theories from conventional Hermitian quantum theories
. In conventional quantum mechanics we are ordinarily interested in the behavior of solutions to the time-independent Schrödinger equation on the real axis subject to boundary conditions that are also imposed on the real axis, and we are usually not concerned with the behavior of solutions for complex
x
. However, for the case of
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric quantum mechanics the boundary conditions are often specified in the complex domain.

III.2
Boundary conditions for the Sextic Hamiltonian

Let us consider the Hamiltonian
H
in (
2.14
) for the sextic case

\varepsilon=4
:

H=p^{2}+x^{6}.

(3.1)

The time-independent Schrödinger equation associated with
H
in (
3.1
)
is

-\psi^{\prime\prime}(x)+x^{6}\psi(x)=E\psi(x).

(3.2)

The exact solutions to this deceptively simple-looking differential equation are much too complicated to be expressed in closed form. However, we will show that elementary asymptotic analysis using WKB techniques easily gives the possible asymptotic behaviors of the solutions
\psi(x)
to this Schrödinger equation for large
|x|

Bender and Orszag 1999
.

For the Schrödinger differential equation
y^{\prime\prime}(x)=Q(x)y(x)
where
\lim_{|x|\to\infty}|Q(x)|=\infty
, WKB theory provides a simple formula for the two possible exponential components of the asymptotic behavior: The solutions can either
grow
or
decay
exponentially like

\exp\left[\pm\int^{x}dt\,\sqrt{Q(t)}\right].

(3.3)

The expression in (
3.3
) is called a
geometrical-optics
approximation
Bender and Orszag 1999
. We emphasize that (
3.3
) is
not
a leading-order WKB approximation (called a
physical-optics
approximation); it is just the
exponential component
of the WKB approximation. From (
3.3
) we see that the possible exponential behaviors of solutions to (
3.2
) for large
|x|
are
\exp\big(\pm\tfrac{1}{4}x^{4}\big)
.

If we were to treat
H
in (
3.1
) as a conventional Hermitian Hamiltonian, we would require that the eigenfunctions vanish as
|x|\to\infty
on the real-
x
axis. A numerical calculation would then show that the eigenvalues are all discrete, real, and positive. The approximate values of the lowest five of these eigenvalues are

\displaystyle E_{0}=1.145...,\penalty\ \penalty\ E_{1}=4.339...,\penalty\ E_{2}=9.073...,

\displaystyle\quad E_{3}=14.935...,\penalty\ \penalty\ E_{4}=21.714...\,.

(3.4)

However, if we treat
H
as a
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric Hamiltonian, we find that while the eigenvalues are again all discrete, real, and positive, they are different from and larger than those in (

). The approximate values of the lowest five eigenvalues are

\displaystyle E_{0}=2.439...,\penalty\ E_{1}=11.882...,\penalty\ E_{2}=25.412...,

\displaystyle\quad E_{3}=42.024...,\penalty\ \penalty\ E_{4}=61.222...\,.

(3.5)

These are the
\mathcal{PT}
eigenvalues that are shown in Fig.
4
.

It is remarkable that one Hamiltonian can have two completely different spectra. The reason for this is that a Hamiltonian specifies only the
local
behavior but not the
global
behavior of the eigenfunctions. That is, the Hamiltonian
H
specifies the Schrödinger differential equation, which in turn determines the relationships between the eigenfunctions and their derivatives
at the point

x
. However,
H
does not specify the global
boundary conditions
on the solutions to the differential equation.

Treating (
3.1
) as a conventional Hermitian Hamiltonian, we require that the solutions to (
3.2
) be normalizable (square-integrable) on the real-
x
axis. This means that the solutions must decay like
\exp\big(-\mbox{$\textstyle{\frac{1}{4}}$}x^{4}\big)
as
x\to\infty
and as
x\to-\infty
; we exclude the solutions to the Schrödinger equation that blow up like
\exp\big(\mbox{$\textstyle{\frac{1}{4}}$}x^{4}\big)
as
x\to\infty
or as
x\to-\infty
. Like the function
{\rm sinh}\,x
discussed in Subsec.
III.1
, the validity of these exponentially decaying behaviors is not limited to the real-
x
axis; these behaviors continue to be valid in the complex-
x
plane in two Stokes sectors, each having an angular opening of
\tfrac{\pi}{4}
. One sector is centered about the positive-real axis and the other is centered about the negative-real axis.

To summarize, the conventional quantum-mechanical eigenfunctions
\psi(x)
, which satisfy the differential equation (
3.2
) and vanish asymptotically as
x
approaches
\pm\infty
,
continue
to vanish exponentially as

III.3
Complex deformation of the harmonic oscillator

As explained earlier in this section, when we perform a deformation of an eigenvalue problem associated with a Hamiltonian, we must be careful to deform the boundary conditions on the eigenfunctions in addition to deforming the Schrödinger equation. If we neglect to do so, we may encounter contradictions and paradoxes.

For example, let us consider the quantum harmonic-oscillator Hamiltonian

H=p^{2}+\omega^{2}x^{2},

(3.9)

where
\omega
is a positive parameter associated with the classical spring constant. The Schrödinger eigenvalue problem for this elementary Hamiltonian reads

-\psi^{\prime\prime}(x)+\omega^{2}x^{2}\psi(x)=E\psi(x)\,.

(3.10)

The conventional Hermitian boundary conditions on the eigenfunctions are

\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}\psi(x)=0\,.

(3.11)

This is an exactly solvable eigenvalue problem. As we saw in Subsec.
II.1
, the eigenfunctions are Hermite functions (Hermite polynomials multiplied by Gaussians) and the eigenvalues are all positive:

E_{n}=(2n+1)\omega\quad(n=0,\,1,\,2,\,\dots)\,.

(3.12)

Let us now perform a smooth complex deformation of the Hamiltonian (
3.9
) by rotating the parameter
\omega
, which is initially real and positive, into the complex plane:

\omega\to\omega e^{i\theta}.

(3.13)

If we rotate
\omega
by an angle
\pi
, that is,
\theta:\,0\to\pi
, then
\omega
changes sign. Under this rotation the Hamiltonian (
3.9
) remains
unchanged
. However, its eigenvalues all change sign! Thus, this Hamiltonian possesses
two
different spectra; it has an entirely negative spectrum in addition to the usual positive spectrum (
3.12
). Given our previous discussion of the
x^{6}
potential (Subsec.
III.2
), this conclusion is not surprising.

The advantage of this harmonic-oscillator example is that the simplicity of the Hamiltonian allows us to understand clearly what happens as we continuously deform the boundary conditions (
3.11
) together with the differential equation (
3.10
). Initially, when
\theta=0
, the eigenfunctions
\psi_{n}(x)
all vanish exponentially on the positive-
x
and the negative-
x
axes like

\exp\big(-\mbox{$\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}$}\omega x^{2}\big)\quad(x\to\pm\infty).

(3.14)

This exponentially-decaying Gaussian behavior persists into a pair of Stokes sectors of angular opening
\tfrac{\pi}{2}
that are centered about the positive-
x
and negative-
x
axes. However, when we rotate
\omega
into the complex plane according to (
3.13
), we must also replace the formula for the exponential Gaussian decay in (
3.14
) by

\exp\big(-\mbox{$\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}$}e^{i\theta}\omega x^{2}\big).

(3.15)

Thus, as
\theta
increases from 0 to
\pi
and
\omega
rotates anticlockwise, the pair of Stokes sectors (in which the eigenfunctions decay exponentially) correspondingly rotates
clockwise
like a propeller by an angle of
\mbox{$\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}$}\pi
. The opening angles of the two sectors remain constant during this rotation, but the orientation of the Stokes sectors changes and the wedges end up centered about the positive-imaginary-
x
and negative-imaginary-
x
axes when
\theta=\pi
.

This argument makes it clear that the Hamiltonian (
3.9
) has two independent spectra, one entirely positive and the other entirely negative, just like the energy levels associated with the red pair of Stokes sectors in Fig.
8
. The positive-spectrum case is physically relevant. However, while a negative spectrum may be interesting from a mathematical point of view, a negative spectrum is physically unacceptable because the energy levels are not bounded below. This violates the basic physical requirement of stability, namely, that a physical system must have a lowest-energy (ground) state.

III.4
Boundary conditions for
H=p^{2}+x^{2}(ix)^{\varepsilon}

Let us now return to the sextic Hamiltonian discussed in Subsec.
III.2
. The question that we must answer is, Why do we require that the eigenfunctions of the
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric Hamiltonian
H=p^{2}+x^{2}(ix)^{\varepsilon}
in (
2.14
) with
\varepsilon=4
satisfy the vanishing boundary conditions shown in Fig.
8
? (And more generally for any Hamiltonian, What determines the appropriate pair of Stokes sectors in which to impose the boundary conditions on the eigenfunctions?) To address this question, we go back to the WKB formula in (
3.3
). This formula implies that the eigenfunctions of
H
either grow or decay exponentially for large
|x|
like

\exp\left(\pm\tfrac{2}{4+\varepsilon}i^{\varepsilon/2}x^{2+\varepsilon/2}\right).

(3.16)

To determine the appropriate choice of signs for particular values of
\varepsilon
, we begin by setting
\varepsilon=0
, which reduces
H
to the harmonic-oscillator Hamiltonian. As explained in Subsec.
III.2
, we get a real positive spectrum if we require that the eigenfunctions of the harmonic-oscillator Hamiltonian vanish in Stokes sectors centered about the positive-real-
x
and negative-real-
x
axes. The angular opening of these sectors is
\tfrac{\pi}{2}
. From (
3.16
) we see that if we
continuously
deform

by smoothly increasing the real parameter

, both Stokes sectors rotate

towards the negative-imaginary-
x

Specifically, for
\varepsilon\geq 0

and is centered about the angle

\theta_{\rm center,\,right}=-\tfrac{\pi\varepsilon}{8+2\varepsilon}

(3.17)

and the left Stokes sector rotates
anticlockwise
and is centered about the angle

\theta_{\rm center,\,left}=-\pi+\tfrac{\pi\varepsilon}{8+2\varepsilon}.

(3.18)

In addition, the angular opening
\theta_{\rm opening\,angle}
of each Stokes

\theta_{\rm opening\,angle}=\tfrac{2\pi}{4+\varepsilon}.

(3.19)

Thus, the sectors become thinner (
\theta_{\rm opening\,angle}
decreases) as
\varepsilon
increases.

This pair of Stokes sectors is shown in Fig.

for four values of

\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric

-symmetric Hamiltonian in Subsec.

. As

, the sectors become infinitely thin and lie along the negative-imaginary axis. In this limit we obtain the

-symmetric square well

III.5
Sextic potential revisited

Let us return to the Hamiltonian (
3.1
) discussed in Subsec.
III.1
. We have stated that this sextic Hamiltonian has two positive real spectra depending on the choice of boundary conditions; that is, depending on the pair of Stokes sectors inside of which the eigenfunctions are required to vanish as
|x|\to\infty
. The conventional Hermitian eigenvalues are associated with the left-right-symmetric pair of Stokes sectors that contain the real axis and the first five of these eigenvalues are listed in (
3.4
). The
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric eigenvalues are associated with the left-right-symmetric pair of Stokes sectors below and adjacent to the Hermitian pair and the first five eigenvalues associated with this pair of Stokes sectors are listed in (
3.5
). These two pairs of sectors are shown in Fig.
8
and the second pair of Stokes sectors is also shown in the fourth diagram of Fig.
9
.

There is a third pair of left-right-symmetric Stokes sectors not shown in Fig.
9
. This pair (shown as gray in Fig.
8
) lies above and adjacent to the Hermitian pair of Stokes sectors. The eigenvalues associated with this third pair of sectors are identical to those associated with the second pair of Stokes sectors. This is because the sextic Hamiltonian is
real
, and therefore in addition to
\mathcal{PT}
symmetry (left-right symmetry), it also has complex-conjugate (up-down) symmetry in the complex plane.

An easy way to verify that the sextic Hamiltonian has two different spectra is simply to calculate the eigenvalues. Of course, the Schrödinger equation (
3.2
) cannot be solved analytically. However, WKB theory gives a good semiclassical approximation to both spectra. The WKB procedure is straightforward
Bender and Orszag 1999
: First, we find the turning points
x_{\pm}
that separate the classically allowed and classically forbidden regions; these turning points satisfy the equation
V(x)=E
.
Then, we evaluate the integral in the WKB quantization condition

\int_{x_{-}}^{x_{+}}dx\,\sqrt{V(x)-E}\sim\big(n+\tfrac{1}{2}\big)\pi\quad(n\to\infty).

(3.20)

Finally, we solve the resulting equation algebraically for
E_{n}
. The accuracy of this WKB calculation increases rapidly with increasing
n
.

For the conventional Hermitian theory, the turning points are the real roots of the equation
x^{6}=E
:

x_{\pm}=\pm E^{1/6}.

Thus, the quantization condition (
3.20
) for the Hermitian theory reads

\int_{-E^{1/6}}^{E^{1/6}}dx\,\sqrt{E-x^{6}}=\big(n+\tfrac{1}{2}\big)\pi.

We evaluate this integral in terms of Gamma functions and solve for
E_{n}
:

E_{n}\sim\left[\frac{(4n+2)\sqrt{\pi}\,\Gamma(2/3)}{\Gamma(1/6)}\right]^{3/2}\quad(n\to\infty).

(3.21)

From this formula we numerically read off the first five WKB approximations to the Hermitian eigenvalues:

\displaystyle E_{0}=0.801...,\penalty\ \penalty\ E_{1}=4.161...,\penalty\ \penalty\ E_{2}=8.954...,

\displaystyle\quad E_{3}=14.832...,\penalty\ \penalty\ E_{4}=21.622...\,.

(3.22)

These approximations are in good agreement with the numerical results in (
3.4
) and the accuracy of the WKB calculation improves rapidly with increasing
n
.

For the
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric sextic theory in Fig.
9
the turning points lie inside the lower pair of Stokes sectors at
x_{-}=E^{1/6}e^{-2i\pi/3}
and
x_{+}=E^{1/6}e^{-i\pi/3}
. To evaluate the integral in the WKB quantization formula (
3.20
), it is simplest to integrate along a radial line from
x_{-}
to the origin and then along a second radial line from the origin out to
x_{+}
. Evaluating these two integrals in terms of Gamma functions and then combining the results gives a formula that differs from that in (
3.21
) for the Hermitian case by a multiple of
2^{3/2}
:

E_{n}\sim\left[\frac{(8n+4)\sqrt{\pi}\,\Gamma(2/3)}{\Gamma(1/6)}\right]^{3/2}\quad(n\to\infty).

(3.23)

From this formula we read off the first five WKB approximations to the
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric eigenvalues:

\displaystyle E_{0}=2.265...,\penalty\ \penalty\ E_{1}=11.770...,\penalty\ \penalty\ E_{2}=25.954...,

\displaystyle\quad E_{3}=41.950...,\penalty\ \penalty\ E_{4}=61.157...\,.

(3.24)

Once again, these approximations are in good agreement with the numerical calculations reported in (
3.5
). As noted in Subsec.
III.2
, the
\mathcal{PT}
eigenvalues are larger than the corresponding Hermitian eigenvalues.

As explained in Subsec.
III.2
,
any
pair of Stokes sectors (regardless of whether they have a
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric orientation) provides a setting for a specific eigenvalue problem with its own tower of eigenvalues. However, we cannot impose exponentially vanishing boundary conditions on solutions in
adjacent
pairs of Stokes sectors. This is because an exponentially decaying solution becomes an exponentially growing solution as we cross the Stokes line separating the two sectors. Thus, we must cross at least two Stokes lines to formulate an eigenvalue problem in which the solutions decay exponentially in two directions.

There are
twenty
nonadjacent pairs of Stokes sectors in Fig.
8
inside of which we can formulate eigenvalue problems. Three left-right pairs give rise to positive spectra (blue-blue, yellow-yellow, gray-gray), three up-down pairs give rise to negative spectra (gray-blue, red-red, gray-blue), and the fourteen other nonadjacent pairs give rise to complex spectra.

Having examined the multiple-spectrum phenomenon at the quantum and at the semiclassical (WKB) level, it intriguing to study it at the
classical
level. For the sextic Hamiltonian
H=p^{2}+x^{6}

\displaystyle x^{\prime}(t)=\frac{\partial H}{\partial p}=2p,

\displaystyle p^{\prime}(t)=-\frac{\partial H}{\partial x}=-6x^{5}\,.

(3.25)

These equations imply that the classical energy
E=H

3.25

E=\sqrt{\tfrac{1}{4}[x^{\prime}(t)]^{2}+[x(t)]^{6}}\,.

(3.26)

Without loss of generality we can fix the value for
E
, say
E=1
, and choose an initial position
x(0)
for the particle. Then, (
3.26

x(t)
of the particle for all times

III.6
Measuring the position of a particle

The central region in Fig.
10
contains a continuously infinite number of classical paths that are all up-down and left-right symmetric. At the quantum level, the expectation value of the
x
operator is the functional average over all classical paths, so by symmetry we know immediately that this expectation value is 0. This result is correct: The particle spends as much time to the right of the origin as to the left of the origin and as much time above the real axis as below the real axis, so if we could perform many experiments in which we measure the instantaneous complex position of the particle and then average these measurements, we would find that
\langle x\rangle=0
. Indeed, for the Hermitian
x^{6}
theory the expectation value of the
x
operator is 0.

However, if we were to average the
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric paths in the upper-half or lower-half plane in Fig.
10
, we would find that the expectation value of the
x
operator for these
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric theories is a positive-imaginary or a negative-imaginary number. How can the expectation value of an operator be imaginary? The short answer to this question is simply that in
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric quantum theory the
position operator is not an observable
. Indeed, when we extend a Hermitian quantum theory to a non-Hermitian
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric quantum theory, particle trajectories are no longer confined to the real-
x
axis and particles travel through the complex-
x
plane. Since the outcome of a position measurement in nonrelativistic quantum mechanics should be a real number, we can no longer say that the
x
operator is an observable.

This statement should not be surprising because in general the position of a quantum particle is not an observable. In quantum electrodynamics, for example, the expectation value of the electron field is not measurable because the result would be complex. We can, in principle, measure the instantaneous position of the
energy density
or the
charge density
of the electron even though we cannot measure the actual position of the electron.

In general, in relativistic particle physics the location of a particle cannot be an observable because if we attempt to trap the particle in an arbitrarily small box, the uncertainty principle implies that the momentum of the particle, and thus the energy of the particle, would be arbitrarily large. The particle would therefore pair-produce and not be in the box at all! The center of energy of a system, such as an atom, is measurable, but not the positions of the components of the system.

Even in elementary nonrelativistic Hermitian quantum mechanics, when a particle tunnels it is a
virtual particle
and its instantaneous position or its tunneling trajectory cannot be observed or measured. One can view the process of tunneling as one in which the particle is following a complex trajectory
Bender et al. 2008
;
Bender and Hook 2011
;
Turok 2014
. Only in elementary quantum mechanics, which is a nonrelativistic approximation to relativistic quantum mechanics, can the
x
operator be treated as an observable. (We examine these issues further in Sec.
VI
when we discuss the
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric inner product and expectation values.)

Nevertheless, in
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric quantum mechanics there are other measurable observables, the most important being the Hamiltonian operator. In any physical theory the ability to make an energy measurement is a fundamental requirement. In Hermitian quantum mechanics when we try to observe the position of a particle, we are actually performing an energy measurement. Energy absorbed and emitted from the particle
if the particle is on the real axis
enters a detector and is transferred to a film (or a nerve cell in a retina). We then infer from this energy transfer where the particle is located. In any viable physical theory, whether or not it is possible to measure the position of a particle, it is crucial that we can still perform an energy measurement, and the outcome of such a measurement must be a real number. (Observables are discussed further in Subsec.
VI.8
.)

Because energy is a measurable observable, one may wonder how an
upside-down
potential such as
-x^{4}
can have a positive-energy spectrum (see Fig.
4
for
\varepsilon=2
, for example). One might think that an upside-down potential would be unstable and could not bind positive-energy bound states. We will see in Sec.
IV
that an upside-down potential can indeed have stable positive-energy states. This is because extending the real numbers to the complex numbers gives up an important property of the real numbers, namely, the
ordering principle
. That is, if
a
and
b
are complex numbers, inequalities such as
a>b
or
a<b
no longer make sense. When we say that a potential is unstable because it is unbounded below, we are implicitly using a
real
inequality that, as we emphasize in Sec.
IV
, no longer applies in the context of
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric quantum theory!

IV
Upside-down potentials

This section examines Hamiltonians whose potentials have the general form

V(x)=x^{4n}\quad(n=1,\,2,\,3,\,...).

Like the potentials discussed in Sec.
III
, these potentials are real if
x
is real and they have an infinite number of positive-energy
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric bound states (see Figs.
3
and
4
). We obtain these upside-down potentials by setting
\varepsilon=2,\,6,\,10,\,...
in (
2.14
). These potentials are profoundly different from the right-side-up
x^{2}
,
x^{6}
, and
x^{10}
potentials discussed in Sec.
III
because if the theories associated with these upside-down potentials are treated as Hermitian quantum theories, they do not have real energy levels. However, as
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric theories, their energy levels are all real, positive, and discrete.

The conventional (real-axis-only) treatment of upside-down potentials precludes the possibility that such potentials could have bound states. Rather, upside-down potentials are normally used as one-dimensional models of tunneling. One might think that an upside-down potential cannot confine a bound state simply because at the classical level the potential exerts a real force on a classical particle that tends to push the particle
away
from the peak of the potential. However, this argument fails in the context of
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric quantum mechanics because in the complex plane the intuitive concept of a potential being unbounded below does not apply. As explained in
Subsec.
III.6
, the complex number system is not
ordered
; one cannot say that a complex number is greater than or less than another complex number. The notion of “unbounded below” relies on the ordering principle, which only holds in the real number system.

This section examines the subtle question of how an upside-down potential can actually confine particles and possess bound states and we explain this both physically and mathematically. We begin with a detailed examination of the
-x^{4}
potential.

IV.1
Classical particle in a quartic upside-down potential

Setting
\varepsilon=2
in (
2.14
) leads to the quartic Hamiltonian

H=p^{2}-x^{4}.

(4.1)

This real
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric Hamiltonian is special because it is
independently
symmetric under space-reflection
\mathcal{P}
and time reversal
\mathcal{T}
. As discussed above, treating
H
conventionally, we would say that a particle with positive energy and subject to forces provided by the upside-down
-x^{4}
potential cannot be in a stable bound state. If a classical particle with positive energy and subject to this potential is initially located at a point

and a negative force if
x<0

Let us use complex analysis to examine this deceptively simple problem. At the classical level it is easy to solve the equations of motion and to calculate the time for a classical particle under the influence of an upside-down quartic potential to escape to infinity.

4.1

-\psi^{\prime\prime}(x)-x^{4}\psi(x)=E\psi(x).

(4.7)

Following the classical analysis in Subsec.
IV.1
, we require that the solution
\psi(x)
to this equation satisfy
complex boundary conditions
; namely, that
\psi(x)
vanishes in the Stokes sectors shown in Fig.
9
for
\varepsilon=2
as
|x|\to\infty
. A numerical calculation shows that the spectrum is real, positive, and discrete (see Fig.
4
). The first five eigenvalues are

\displaystyle E_{0}=1.477...,\penalty\ E_{1}=6.003...,\penalty\ E_{2}=11.804...,

\displaystyle\quad E_{3}=18.459...,\penalty\ \penalty\ E_{4}=25.792...\,.

(4.8)

This eigenvalue problem cannot be solved in closed form. However, we can perform a leading-order semiclassical WKB calculation using the turning points in the lower-half
x
-plane that are shown in Fig.
18
. The standard WKB quantization condition is

\int_{x_{-}}^{x_{+}}dx\,\sqrt{E+x^{4}}\sim\big(n+\mbox{$\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}$}\big)\quad(n\to\infty),

where in this case the turning points are given by

x_{-}=E^{1/4}e^{-3i\pi/4}\penalty\ \penalty\ {\rm and}\penalty\ \penalty\ x_{+}=E^{1/4}e^{-i\pi/4}.

To evaluate the WKB integral we integrate from
x_{-}
to the origin and then from the origin to
x_{+}
along radial lines in the complex-
x
plane. The resulting integrals are easy to evaluate in terms of Gamma functions:

E_{n}\sim\left[\frac{(6n+3)\sqrt{2\pi}\,\Gamma(3/4)}{2\Gamma(1/4)}\right]^{4/3}\quad(n\to\infty).

(4.9)

This WKB formula (
4.9
) gives the following approximations to the first five eigenvalues:

\displaystyle E_{0}=1.377...,\penalty\ E_{1}=5.956...,\penalty\ E_{2}=11.769...,

\displaystyle\quad E_{3}=18.432...,\penalty\ \penalty\ E_{4}=25.769...\,.

(4.10)

As we can see, the WKB approximation in (
4.9
) provides increasingly accurate approximations to the numerical values of the eigenvalues in (
4.8
) as
n
increases.

The remarkable feature of the eigenvalues in (
4.8
), of course, is that these quantum-mechanical energy levels are all
real
. Thus, as in the classical case, a quantum particle in this upside-down potential is in a
stable bound state
. Even though the potential is upside down, the particle wave function remains localized at the origin; the quantum particle is permanently confined and does not escape to infinity.

IV.3
Elementary proof of spectral reality

We cannot calculate the eigenvalues and eigenfunctions of the Hamiltonians
H=p^{2}+x^{2}(ix)^{\varepsilon}

(\varepsilon>0)
analytically but, as mentioned in Sec.
II
, it was proved using sophisticated mathematical techniques that the spectra of these Hamiltonians are all real, positive, and discrete
Dorey et al. 2001
. However, the upside-down quartic Hamiltonian (
4.1
) is special; the unique value
\varepsilon=2
is the only known case for which one can prove reality, positivity, and discreteness by using nothing more advanced than elementary calculus. An early study of the
\varepsilon=2
case in
Buslaev and Grecchi 1993
was followed by two more papers
Jones and Mateo 2006
;
Bender et al. 2006a
. We present a short proof of reality below.

We begin with the time-independent Schödinger eigenvalue differential equation associated with
H
in (
4.1
),

\displaystyle-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\psi^{\prime\prime}(x)-gx^{4}\psi(x)=E\psi(x),

(4.11)

where we have inserted the dimensional parameters
\hbar
,
g
, and
m
for clarity in the subsequent discussion. The boundary conditions on
\psi(x)
, as shown in Fig.
9
for the case
\varepsilon=2
, are that
\lim_{|x|\to\infty}\psi(x)=0
in two Stokes sectors of angular opening
\tfrac{\pi}{3}
adjacent to and below the positive and negative real-
x
axis.

For the differential equation (
4.11
) we impose vanishing boundary conditions on a specially chosen curve in the complex plane that we express in parametric form as

x=-2iL\sqrt{1+iy/L}\quad(-\infty<y<+\infty),

where
y
is a real variable. Note that this curve terminates
inside the Stokes sectors
at the angles
-\tfrac{\pi}{4}
and
-\tfrac{3\pi}{4}
.

We use the parametric representation above to replace the independent variable
x
in (
4.11
) by
y
and get the differential equation

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\!-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\left(1+\frac{iy}{L}\right)\phi^{\prime\prime}(y)-\frac{i\hbar^{2}}{4Lm}\phi^{\prime}(y)

\displaystyle\qquad-16gL^{4}\left(1+\frac{iy}{L}\right)^{2}\phi(y)=E\phi(y),

(4.12)

where
\psi(x)=\phi(y)
. The parameter
L
has units of length,

L=\lambda\left(\frac{\hbar^{2}}{mg}\right)^{1/6},

where
\lambda
is an arbitrary dimensionless number. The constant
L
serves to make the subsequent equations dimensionally consistent. (This parameter will drop out at the end of the calculation.)

Next, we take a Fourier transform of (
4.12
). This transform has the general form

\tilde{f}(p)\equiv\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dy\,e^{-iyp/\hbar}f(y).

Since the Fourier transforms of
f^{\prime}(y)
and
yf(y)
are
ip\tilde{f}(p)/\hbar
and
i\hbar\tilde{f}^{\prime}(p)
, the Fourier transform of (
4.12
) is

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\!\frac{1}{2m}\left(1-\frac{\hbar}{L}\frac{d}{dp}\right)p^{2}\tilde{\phi}(p)+\frac{\hbar}{4Lm}p\tilde{\phi}(p)

\displaystyle\qquad-16gL^{4}\left(1-\frac{\hbar}{L}\frac{d}{dp}\right)^{2}\tilde{\phi}(p)=E\tilde{\phi}(p).

(4.13)

We simplify (
4.13
) and get

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!-16gL^{2}\hbar^{2}\tilde{\phi}^{\prime\prime}(p)+\left(-\frac{\hbar p^{2}}{2mL}+32gL^{3}\hbar\right)\tilde{\phi}^{\prime}(p)

\displaystyle+\left(\frac{p^{2}}{2m}-\frac{3p\hbar}{4mL}-16gL^{4}\right)\tilde{\phi}(p)=E\tilde{\phi}(p).

(4.14)

Note that the variable
p
in this equation is not a conventional momentum variable. Here, considered as an operator,
p
represents
-i\hbar\tfrac{d}{dy}
and not
-i\hbar\frac{d}{dx}
.

The next step is to convert (
4.14
) to a conventional Schrödinger equation by eliminating the one-derivative term. This is done by making the elementary change of dependent variable

\tilde{\phi}(p)=e^{Q(p)/2}\Phi(p).

Then, to eliminate
\Phi^{\prime}(p)
from the resulting equation we require that
Q(p)
satisfy a first-order differential equation whose solution is

Q(p)=\frac{2L}{\hbar}p-\frac{1}{96gmL^{3}\hbar}p^{3}.

The Schrödinger equation satisfied by
\Phi(p)
is then

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\!\!-16gL^{2}\hbar^{2}\Phi^{\prime\prime}(p)+\left(-\frac{\hbar p}{4mL}+\frac{p^{4}}{256gm^{2}L^{4}}\right)\Phi(p)

\displaystyle\qquad=E\Phi(p).

Finally, we perform a scaling transformation of the independent variable,

p=zL\sqrt{32mg}.

This replaces the
p
variable, which has units of momentum, by
z
, which is a coordinate variable having units of length. The resulting eigenvalue equation, posed on the
real
-
z
axis, is

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\!\!\!-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\Phi^{\prime\prime}(z)+\left(-\hbar\sqrt{\frac{2g}{m}}\,z+4gz^{4}\right)\Phi(z)=E\Phi(z).

(4.15)

This new eigenvalue equation, which no longer contains the length parameter
L
, is the final result of the calculation. The new independent variable
z
has dimensions of length although it is not a conventional coordinate variable because it is
odd under time reversal
.

The eigenvalue equation (
4.15
) is similar in structure to the original eigenvalue equation (
4.11
), but there are two significant differences: (i) The quartic term in the potential has
changed sign
, so
the upside-down quartic potential has become a conventional right-side-up quartic potential
. (ii) There is a new linear potential term proportional to Planck’s constant
\hbar
. This linear term is called a
quantum anomaly
, which means that it has no classical analog. (The effect of this parity-anomaly term is discussed in Subsec.
IV.4
.)

The Hamiltonian
\tilde{H}
for which (
4.15
) is the eigenvalue equation is

\tilde{H}=\frac{\tilde{p}^{2}}{2m}-\hbar\sqrt{\frac{2g}{m}}\,z+4gz^{4}.

(4.16)

This Hamiltonian is quite different from

H=\frac{p^{2}}{2m}-gx^{4},

(4.17)

which is the Hamiltonian
H
associated with (
4.11
). At the quantum level the Hamiltonian
\tilde{H}
in (
4.16
) is conventionally Hermitian and the potential is bounded below on the real-
z
axis;
\tilde{H}
is also
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric because the sequence of transformations used to obtain
\tilde{H}
all respect
\mathcal{PT}
symmetry. Note that the potential in (
4.16
) is
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric because while
z
and
\tilde{p}
are canonically conjugate operators satisfying the usual commutation relation
[z,\tilde{p}]=i
, the new variable
z
behaves like a
momentum
rather than a coordinate variable.

The point of this calculation is simply that while we cannot calculate exactly the eigenvalues of
H
(with
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric boundary conditions imposed in the Stokes sectors shown in Fig.
9
for
\varepsilon=2
) or the eigenvalues of
\tilde{H}
(with conventional boundary conditions imposed on the real axis), we have shown that these two Hamiltonians are
isospectral
; that is, they have exactly the same eigenvalues. Because the potential of
\tilde{H}
rises to
+\infty
as
x\to\pm\infty
, we know that the entire spectrum of
\tilde{H}
is real and discrete. This proves that the eigenvalues of the
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric Hamiltonian
H
are also real and discrete.

This elementary calculation easily generalizes if we include a
quadratic
term in the
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric quartic Hamiltonian:

H=p^{2}+\mu^{2}x^{2}-gx^{4}.

(4.18)

The corresponding isospectral Hermitian Hamiltonian is just slightly more complicated than that in (
4.16
):

{\tilde{H}}=p^{2}-2\hbar\sqrt{g}z+4g\left(z^{2}-\frac{\mu^{2}}{4g}\right)^{2}.

(4.19)

[This isospectral Hamiltonian is also derived in
Buslaev and Grecchi 1993
;
Jones and Mateo 2006
;
Bender et al. 2006a
.] However, the calculation in this subsection does not generalize to any other Hamiltonians of the form (
2.14
); it only works for
\varepsilon=2
. The techniques used in this calculation
do
extend to the higher-derivative
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric class of Hamiltonians of the form

H_{n}=p^{n}-g(ix)^{n^{2}}\quad(n=2,\,3,\,4,\,...)

but the resulting isospectral Hamiltonians are quite complicated
Bender and Hook 2008
.

IV.4
Discussion of the parity anomaly

The equations of motion for the quartic Hamiltonian (

4.16

\mathcal{PT}

IV.5
Comparison of
x^{4}
and
-x^{4}
Potentials

As explained in Sec.
II
, to obtain the quartic
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric Hamiltonian
H=p^{2}-x^{4}
, we smoothly deform in
\varepsilon
the Hamiltonian
H=p^{2}+x^{2}(ix)^{\varepsilon}
from the harmonic-oscillator Hamiltonian
H=p^{2}+x^{2}
at
\varepsilon=0
to the quartic
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric Hamiltonian
H=p^{2}-x^{4}
at
\varepsilon=2
. This exponential analytic continuation in the variable
\varepsilon
is quite elaborate, and one might wonder if one can obtain this
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric Hamiltonian by a simpler procedure such as a complex rotation of a parameter. That is, one might begin with the Hermitian Hamiltonian
H=p^{2}+x^{4}
and insert the
multiplicative
parameter
e^{i\theta}
into the potential term:

H_{\theta}=p^{2}+e^{i\theta}x^{4}.

(4.20)

Now, if
\theta=0
,
H_{\theta}
has the right-side-up quartic potential of the Hermitian Hamiltonian, and if we rotate smoothly to
\theta=\pi
,
H_{\theta}
has the upside-down potential of the
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric Hamiltonian.

If we perform such a rotation, we
do
indeed obtain the Hamiltonian operator
H=p^{2}-x^{4}
, but this rotation procedure fails to recover the
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric properties of the
H=p^{2}-x^{4}
Hamiltonian because
the eigenvalue problems associated with the Hermitian Hamiltonian and the
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric Hamiltonian are not analytic continuations of one another.

To understand why we cannot rotate smoothly from a conventional Hermitian quartic Hamiltonian to a
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric quartic Hamiltonian, let us examine the eigenvalue problem associated with (
4.20
). Following the procedure explained in Subsec.
III.3
for performing a rotation of the harmonic-oscillator eigenvalue problem, we note that when
\theta=0
, the possible large-
|x|
exponential behaviors of solutions to the eigenvalue differential equation associated with
H_{0}
are
\exp\big(\pm\tfrac{1}{3}x^{3}\big)
. For large-positive
x
the eigenfunctions decay like
\exp\big(-\tfrac{1}{3}x^{3}\big)
and for large-negative
x
the solutions decay like
\exp\big(\tfrac{1}{3}x^{3}\big)
. These asymptotic behaviors continue to be valid in Stokes sectors of angular opening
\tfrac{\pi}{3}
centered about the positive-real-
x
and negative-real-
x
axes.

There are two ways to perform the
\theta
rotation of
H_{\theta}
; we can rotate clockwise from
\theta=0
to
-\pi
or anticlockwise from
\theta=0
to
\pi
. If we rotate clockwise, the two sectors in which the eigenfunctions vanish exponentially rotate anticlockwise together (like a propeller) by an angle of
\tfrac{\pi}{6}
. If we rotate anticlockwise, these two sectors rotate together (again like a propeller) by an angle of
\tfrac{\pi}{6}
but in the clockwise direction. In both cases one sector rotates downward and the other rotates upward; one sector ends up
above
the real axis with its lower edge on the real axis and the other ends up
below
the real axis with its upper edge on the real axis. These two orientations of the sectors are shown in Fig.
22
a and Fig.
22
b.

Figure 22:
Stokes sectors in the complex-
x
plane in which the eigenfunctions for the upside-down quartic Hamiltonian
H=p^{2}-x^{4}
are required to vanish exponentially. In cases a and b the pair of Stokes sectors is symmetric under parity reflection
\mathcal{P}
and the eigenvalues are all complex. However, in cases c and d the pairs of Stokes sectors are
\mathcal{PT}
symmetric and the eigenvalues are all real and positive. Thus, there are
two different phases
of the Hamiltonian
H
, and these are distinguished by having different global symmetries,
\mathcal{P}
in the case of a and b, and
\mathcal{PT}
in the case of c and d.

In both orientations (a and b) the original parity symmetry of the Hamiltonian
H_{\theta}
at
\theta=0
is preserved. Under a reflection through the origin the two sectors are interchanged and a point
x
in one sector goes into the corresponding reflected point
-x
in the other sector. In both cases the configurations of these sectors are
\mathcal{P}
symmetric but
not

\mathcal{PT}
symmetric (left-right symmetric).

The energy eigenvalues in the parity-symmetric case are all complex. The
\theta
-rotated Hamiltonian has no bound states; depending on the sign of the imaginary part of the energy eigenvalue, the states either grow or decay exponentially in time. In contrast, the diagrams in Fig.
22
c and Fig.
22
d
are

\mathcal{PT}
symmetric (just like the Stokes sectors in the
\varepsilon=2
diagram in Fig.
9
). In this case the energy eigenvalues are all real and positive.

The Hamiltonians in both the
\mathcal{P}
-symmetric and the
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric cases
are the same, and thus the
time-independent Schrödinger equation in both cases is the same. However, the boundary conditions at
|x|=\infty
are different. These two different models
may be regarded as distinct
phases
of the Hamiltonian
H=p^{2}-x^{4}
because they exhibit different global symmetries; namely,
\mathcal{P}-
reflection and
\mathcal{PT}
-reflection symmetries. (This distinction resembles the difference in the long-range global symmetries between an ice crystal and liquid water.)

To better understand what is happening in the
\mathcal{P}
-symmetric case, let us examine the large-
|x|
exponential behaviors of the eigenfunctions of the rotated Hamiltonian
H=p^{2}-x^{4}
. An exponential WKB approximation to the eigenfunctions shows that on the real-
x
axis the eigenfunctions are wavelike; they behave for large
|x|
like
\exp\big(\pm\tfrac{1}{3}ix^{3}\big)
. Depending on the orientation of the Stokes sectors, waves either
approach
the origin symmetrically from the right and left or waves propagate away from the origin symmetrically to the right and left:

{\bf\longrightarrow}\penalty\ \penalty\ (x=0)\penalty\ \penalty\ {\bf\longleftarrow}\qquad{\rm or}\qquad{\bf\longleftarrow}\penalty\ \penalty\ (x=0)\penalty\ {\bf\longrightarrow}.

(4.21)

If the waves in (
4.21
) propagate inward, the state is populating in time; if the waves recede from the origin, the state is depopulating in time. In either case, these systems are not in equilibrium. Because the rotated Hamiltonian is real, these systems must be invariant under complex conjugation (up-down reflection), and as we can see, the outgoing and incoming configurations of Stokes sectors become interchanged under this symmetry operation. Correspondingly, the imaginary parts of the eigenvalues change sign under complex conjugation. We conclude that the rotation procedure fails to recover
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric boundary conditions.

In contrast, for
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric boundary conditions the waves propagate inward on one side of the origin and outward on the other side:

{\bf\longrightarrow}\penalty\ \penalty\ (x=0)\penalty\ \penalty\ {\bf\longrightarrow}\qquad{\rm or}\qquad{\bf\longleftarrow}\penalty\ \penalty\ (x=0)\penalty\ \penalty\ {\bf\longleftarrow}.

(4.22)

This means that the
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric eigenstates are in dynamic equilibrium; they neither populate nor decay with time. Thus, the energy eigenvalues are real.

Just as there are two
\mathcal{P}
-symmetric configurations in (
4.21
) of the Stokes sectors of the rotated Hamiltonian that are interchanged under complex conjugation (up-down reflection), there are also two
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric (left-right-symmetric) configurations of the Stokes sectors. In addition to the configuration of Stokes sectors adjacent to and below the real axis (
\varepsilon=2
diagram in Fig.
9
), there is a another possible configuration of the
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric (left-right-symmetric) Stokes sectors adjacent to and
above
the real axis, as shown in Fig.
22
d. We obtain these Stokes sectors by smoothly deforming (analytically continuing) the Hamiltonian
H=p^{2}+x^{2}(-ix)^{\varepsilon}
from
\varepsilon=0
to
\varepsilon=2
. This deformation again breaks parity symmetry but preserves
\mathcal{PT}
symmetry. The resulting
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric Hamiltonian has the same form as (
4.1
), but now the eigenfunctions vanish exponentially in a
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric pair of Stokes sectors lying
above
the real axis. The corresponding eigenvalues are identical to the eigenvalues for the configuration in Fig.
9
, third diagram. There is no physical difference between these up-up and down-down configurations because one is obtained from the other by complex conjugation; that is, changing the sign of
i
. (The sign of the complex number
i
is arbitrary and cannot have any physical consequences.)

The state of dynamic equilibrium exhibited in (
4.22
) is in perfect analogy with the energy flow in the optical system pictured in Fig.
5
; the incoming and outgoing waves to the left and right of the origin in (
4.22
) show that there is an exact balance of loss and gain. Thus, the system is in dynamic equilibrium. This balance was emphasized and investigated at the quantum level in
Ahmed et al. 2005
. We can also see this dynamic balance at the classical level in Fig.
18
, which shows that classical particles near the real axis are always traveling in the same direction; these particles approach the origin on one side and recede from the origin on the other.

This balanced loss-and-gain behavior has been seen in many optical laser experiments performed on
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric systems and is referred to as
unidirectional propagation

Ahmed et al. 2005
;
Mostafazadeh 2014
and
reflectionless scattering

Chong et al. 2010
. The generic phenomenon of reflectionless scattering suggests the possibility of performing new tabletop macroscopic quantum-mechanical experiments in which a one-dimensional particle beam is incident on an upside-down quartic potential (or other upside-down potentials such as
-x^{6}
or
-x^{8}
). In such experiments one measures the amplitude of the reflected wave. If the energy of the incident wave is adjusted so that there is no reflected wave, one has a
direct measurement of a
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric energy level
. This kind of experiment was originally proposed in
Bender and Gianfreda 2018
; in a recent paper such an experiment was proposed for a cold-atom beam incident on the much wider class of real Hamiltonians of the form
H=p^{2}-|x|^{\varepsilon}

Soley et al. 2023
. Such Hamiltonians are independently
\mathcal{P}
-symmetric and
\mathcal{T}
-symmetric.

V
Anharmonic oscillators

Until now we have focused on
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric Hamiltonians whose potentials are monomials in
x
and noninteger powers of
x
. In this section we discuss potentials of quartic anharmonic form
ax^{2}+bx^{4}
. The Hermitian anharmonic oscillator, whose Hamiltonian is

H=\mbox{$\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}$}p^{2}+\mbox{$\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}$}m^{2}x^{2}+gx^{4}\quad(g>0),

(5.1)

has been studied intensively for many decades by mathematical physicists. The time-independent Schrödinger differential equation associated with this Hamiltonian is

-\mbox{$\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}$}\psi^{\prime\prime}(x)+\mbox{$\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}$}m^{2}x^{2}\psi(x)+gx^{4}\psi(x)=E\psi(x).

(5.2)

This differential equation cannot be solved in closed form, but the anharmonic-oscillator model that it represents has been examined in depth because it is an excellent intellectual laboratory in which to analyze perturbation theory, summation theory, Padé theory, dispersion relations, diagrammatic expansions, and variational and semiclassical techniques. This model is especially interesting because it is a quartic quantum field theory in one-dimensional spacetime and does not have the problem of infinities requiring renormalization. Thousands of papers have been written on the properties of the quantum anharmonic oscillator following the early studies
Bender and Wu 1973
;
Bender and Wu 1969
;
Bender and Wu 1968
;
Simon and Dicke 1970
.

The
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric Hamiltonian

\displaystyle H=\frac{1}{2m}p^{2}+\frac{\mu^{2}}{2}x^{2}-gx^{4}\quad(g>0).

(5.3)

is obtained formally by changing the sign of
g
in (
5.1
). This sign change leads to a significant change in the nature
of the physics depending on whether the unstable boundary conditions in (

V.1
Particle-physics analysis of
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric bound states

In four-dimensional spacetime a
-\phi^{4}
quantum field theory is of particular interest because it might be a good model for describing the dynamics of the Higgs sector of the standard model. This is because the
-\phi^{4}
theory is asymptotically free and thus is nontrivial
Symanzik 1975
;
Bender et al. 2000b
;
Bender and Jones 2011
. Furthermore, the one-point Green’s function
\langle 0|\phi|0\rangle
is nonvanishing
Bender et al. 2001b
.

A four-dimensional quantum field theory is an extremely complicated mathematical construct. However, as stated earlier in this section, the quantum anharmonic oscillator (
5.3
) is a model quantum field theory for the simple case of one-dimensional spacetime. Such a model describes a universe that has no spatial extent, so nothing moves; all that happens is that this elementary system evolves in time. Nevertheless, this model is instructive because its nontrivial energy spectrum
E_{0}
,
E_{1}
,
E_{2}
,
\dots
can be interpreted in particle-theory language as the energies of multiparticle states.

We begin by interpreting the quantum-mechanical ground state of (
5.3
) as the particle-physics
vacuum state
. In particle physics the
vacuum state
is the state of no particle excitations, and because there is no absolute energy scale, the vacuum state is
defined
to have zero energy.

The one-particle state is the lowest-energy excitation above the vacuum state. In particle-physics language this energy is called the
mass gap
and it represents the renormalized mass
M
of a free particle:

M\equiv E_{1}-E_{0}.

The state associated with the second quantum-mechanical energy level
E_{2}
is the two-particle state. In particle physics the energy of this state is again measured relative to the vacuum state:
E_{2}-E_{0}
. The two-particle state is interpreted as a
bound state
if it has negative binding energy
B_{2}
:

B_{2}=(E_{2}-E_{0})-2M<0.

This inequality means that the energy of the two-particle state is
less
than the mass
2M
of two free particles. If
B_{2}
is negative, one can think of the two particles as having an
attractive force
; energy would be required to decompose the state into two free particles. Since this quantum system has no spatial extent, this force has no spatial dependence. Conversely, if
E_{2}-E_{0}
is greater than
2M
, this force is interpreted to be
repulsive
.

The energies of the higher excitations are also measured relative to the vacuum energy,
E_{n}-E_{0}
(
n=2,\,3,\,4,\,...
), and if the energy
E_{n}-E_{0}
of an
n
-particle state is less than the mass
nM
of
n
free particles,

B_{n}\equiv(E_{n}-E_{0})-nM<0,

(5.5)

we interpret this state as a bound state. Positive energy would be required to decompose this bound state into its
n
particle constituents. Conversely, if
B_{n}
is positive, the state is unbound.

Note that the Hamiltonians whose spectra are shown in Figs.
6
and
7
have no quadratic term, and therefore in particle-theory language these are massless field theories. These spectra become more widely separated with increasing energy and thus the binding energies
B_{n}
in (
5.5
) are all positive. This indicates that these theories have no bound states.

In contrast, the Hamiltonian (
5.3
) describes a theory having a more elaborate physical structure. The numerical calculations in
Bender et al. 2001a
show that for small positive
g
the first few states of
H
in (
5.3
) are bound states. That paper found that as
g
increases, the number of bound states decreases until when
g/\mu^{3}
exceeds a critical value of about
0.0465
, there are no bound states at all. Thus, (
5.3
) describes a system in which there are a finite number of stable particles if
g
is small.

We have shown that the
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric Hamiltonian
H
in (
5.3
) and the Hermitian Hamiltonian
\tilde{H}
in (
5.4
) are isospectral.
It is easier to calculate the eigenvalues of a Hermitian than a non-Hermitian Hamiltonian and a straightforward study of
\tilde{H}
using the Arnoldi algorithm
Arnoldi 1951
shows that
the bound states are a direct consequence of the anomaly term in this Hamiltonian.
To demonstrate this, we insert a multiplicative parameter
\alpha
that quantifies the strength of the anomaly term in
\tilde{H}
and for simplicity we set
m=\mu=\hbar=1
:

\displaystyle\tilde{H}_{\alpha}=\frac{\tilde{p}^{2}}{2}-\alpha\hbar\sqrt{2g}\,z+4g\left(z^{2}-\frac{1}{8g}\right)^{2}.

(5.6)

Setting
\alpha=0
eliminates the parity-anomaly term in
\tilde{H}_{\alpha}

symmetric
double well, as shown in Fig.

M
associated with

is small. An elementary WKB calculation shows that for large

V.2
Classical symmetric double-well potentials

We have seen in Subsec.
V.1
that if we eliminate the parity-breaking anomaly term, which is proportional to
\hbar
, from the quartic Hamiltonian
\tilde{H}
in (

anharmonic potentials that no longer exhibit the particle-physics feature of quantum bound states. However, at the

The complex classical tunneling behaviors described in this section appear to be generic, so we restrict our discussion here to just one special case of the Hamiltonian in (

:

(5.7)

For each initial condition

that a typical complex trajectory is infinitely long, nonintersecting, and space filling. The set of these complex trajectories possesses some unusual topological features. For example, we can plot a classical trajectory for long times and record the points on the imaginary axis where the trajectory crosses this axis. We find that there are intervals containing these points inside of which all trajectories crossing this axis represent particles that are moving in the same direction. In one such case the imaginary axis contains just two such intervals; all trajectories crossing above a point on the imaginary axis represent particles traveling to the right and all trajectories below this point represent particles traveling to the left.

In other cases we find adjacent (arbitrarily close) trajectories crossing the imaginary axis on which particles are moving in
opposite directions
. This can happen because the classical equation of motion (Newton’s law) is
second order
in time, so two trajectories can be arbitrarily close to one another and yet represent particles moving in opposite directions without violating continuity. (This cannot happen in first-order linear dynamical systems.) To understand this nonviolation of continuity, note that the Hamiltonian equation of motion has the form

of the particle at time

square
of its velocity
p^{2}
at time

locally
by the equation of motion; rather, it is determined
globally
by the initial condition
x(0)
.

V.3
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric quasi-exactly solvable Hamiltonians

In studying
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric Hamiltonians one may find new non-Hermitian quantum-mechanical models that exhibit unusual behaviors that were previously discovered for Hermitian Hamiltonians. For example, Hermitian Hamiltonians have been found for which it is possible to calculate exactly some (but not all) of the eigenfunctions and corresponding eigenvalues. Such Hamiltonians are said to be
quasi-exactly solvable
(QES).

Before the introduction of
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric Hamiltonians in 1998, it was believed that the lowest-degree one-dimensional QES polynomial potential is sextic
Ushveridze 1994
. This belief was based on the assumption that a Hamiltonian must be Hermitian. However, the quartic
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric anharmonic potential

V(x)=-x^{4}+2iax^{3}+(a^{2}-2b)x^{2}+2i(ab-J)x,

(5.9)

where
a
and
b
are continuous real parameters and
J
is a positive integer, provides a simpler and even more general two-parameter family of QES quartic potentials
Bender and Boettcher 1998a
. This potential is an additive generalization of the

V.4
Hermitian and
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric anharmonic oscillators

As mentioned previously, for decades the quartic anharmonic oscillator has served as a intellectual laboratory for understanding physical mechanisms and developing calculational tools. This subsection summarizes what has been learned from the study of the Hermitian and the
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric anharmonic oscillators.

V.4.1
Hermitian anharmonic oscillator

The motivation to study the Hermitian anharmonic oscillator can be traced back to the classic
Dyson 1952
paper on the divergence of perturbation theory in quantum electrodynamics (QED). This paper argued that the weak-coupling perturbation expansion in QED, which is a formal series in powers of the fine-structure constant
\alpha\approx\tfrac{1}{137}
, is divergent.

The reasoning in Dyson’s paper goes as follows. The classical Coulomb force between a positive and a negative charge is proportional to
\alpha
and is attractive. But, if the sign of
\alpha
were changed, this force would become repulsive. A convergent perturbation series in powers of
\alpha
would define an
analytic
(smooth) function of
\alpha
in the neighborhood of
\alpha=0
. This neighborhood would contain both positive-real and negative-real values of
\alpha
.

The vacuum state (ground state) in QED is stable for positive
\alpha
and consists of a cloud of virtual electron-positron pairs. However, for negative
\alpha
, like charges would attract and unlike charges would repel, so virtual pairs of electrons and positrons in the vacuum would repel. This suggests that the
vacuum state for
\alpha<0
would be unstable
; it would tunnel to a lower-energy state containing growing heaps of electrons and positrons at opposite ends of the universe. Hence, one would expect there to be an abrupt discontinuity in the physics at
\alpha=0
. This implies that a perturbation series expansion in powers of
\alpha
would have a zero radius of convergence.

The conclusion of Dyson’s novel argument is correct: In general, perturbation series in powers of a coupling constant diverge in quantum mechanics and in quantum field theory. However, the subtle problem with this argument, as the study of the
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric anharmonic oscillator shows, is that a repulsive potential can still confine an infinite spectrum of real-energy bound states. In particular, the lowest-energy state (the vacuum state) of a repulsive potential can be stable.

The two anharmonic-oscillator Hamiltonians discussed at the beginning of this section reveal a possible flaw in Dyson’s argument. The Hermitian Hamiltonian (
5.1
) has a positive coupling constant and an attractive potential while the
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric Hamiltonian (
5.3
) has a negative coupling constant and a repulsive potential (on the real axis). Yet the eigenvalues of
both
anharmonic-oscillator Hamiltonians are entirely real and positive and the states are stable.

Nevertheless, as Dyson argued, the point
\alpha=0
in the complex-
\alpha
plane is indeed a singularity. This singular point is the boundary between two
different theories
, one with
\alpha>0
for which there there is an attractive force between an electron and a positron and the other with
\alpha<0
for which there is a repulsive force between an electron and a positron. However, the vacuum state in both theories could well be stable.

There is a simple reason why quantum-mechanical and field-theoretic perturbation expansions diverge: The
n
th term in the perturbation expansion is a sum of Feynman diagrams having
n
vertices. The average size of an
n
-vertex Feynman diagram is typically of order
c^{n}
, where
c
is a constant, but the
number
of Feynman diagrams grows
factorially
for large
n
. This is why perturbation series diverge.

This factorial growth is a direct consequence of graph counting and combinatorics. Following the publication of Dyson’s seminal paper, many papers were published in which the perturbation expansions for various field-theoretic models were shown to be divergent
Hurst 1952
;
Thirring 1953
;
Petermann 1953
;
Glimm and Jaffe 1968
. These papers established bounds on the size of Feynman diagrams and estimated the growth in the number of such diagrams. Because of this broad interest in the nature of perturbation theory, the Hermitian anharmonic oscillator was identified as an ideal model in which to study perturbation theory in depth.

An analysis of the Hermitian anharmonic oscillator in 1968 showed that the underlying reason for the divergence of perturbation series is the presence of square-root branch-point singularities in the complex coupling-constant plane (the complex-
g
plane)
Bender and Wu 1968
;
Bender and Wu 1969
. Originally, these singularities were discovered in the context of the Hermitian anharmonic oscillator model (
5.1
), but the appearance of square-root singularities in the complex-coupling constant plane is a generic phenomenon. These singularities, originally called
Bender-Wu singularities
, are now usually referred to as
exceptional points
. Bender and Wu showed that there are infinite sequences of square-root singularities in the complex-
g
plane and that these sequences approach the origin
g=0
. Thus, the origin in the coupling-constant plane is a
nonisolated
singularity. This explains at a deep level why perturbation series in powers of a coupling constant have a vanishing radius of convergence.

The Bender-Wu singularities provide a clear
physical
explanation of the divergence of perturbation series. At each square-root branch-point singularity two different energy levels
cross
; that is, two energy levels analytically continue into one another. Thus, in perturbative eigenvalue problems an eigenvalue spectrum is not just a set of unrelated energies
\{E_{n}(g)\}
. Rather, there is an analytic
eigenvalue function

E(g)
that is defined on a multisheeted Riemann surface. The
n
th eigenvalue
E_{n}(g)
is the value of the complex function
E(g)
on the
n
th sheet of the Riemann surface. We arrive at an elegant picture of quantization: The quantization of eigenvalues is topological; the
n
th eigenvalue corresponds to the
n
th sheet of a complex-
g
Riemann surface.

V.4.2
Elementary model of topological quantization

The
2\times 2
Hermitian matrix Hamiltonian

H=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}a&0\\
0&b\end{array}\right)+g\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&1\\
1&0\end{array}\right)

(5.14)

illustrates this topological picture of quantization very clearly. This Hamiltonian describes two states of energy
a
and
b
that interact with coupling strength
g
. To calculate the eigenvalues of
H
, we construct the secular equation, which is the quadratic polynomial

{\rm det}(H-IE)=E^{2}-E(a+b)+ab-g^{2}.

(5.15)

The roots of this polynomial are the two eigenvalues
E_{\pm}(g)
of
H
:

E_{\pm}(g)=\mbox{$\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}$}\left(a+b\pm\sqrt{(a-b)^{2}+4g^{2}}\right).

(5.16)

The eigenvalues
E(g)
are real and distinct when
g
is real because
H
in (
5.14
) is Hermitian. However, as functions of complex
g
, these eigenvalues become analytic continuations of one another. The function
E(g)
has two square-root branch-point singularities in the complex-
g
plane; these branch points lie on the imaginary-
g
axis at

g=\pm\tfrac{1}{2}|a-b|i.

(5.17)

Thus, the function
E(g)
is defined on a two-sheeted Riemann surface. These two sheets are joined at the branch cut connecting the two branch points.

This complex picture of quantization is completely general and extends from matrix Hamiltonians to continuum Hamiltonians. For the general perturbed Hamiltonian

H=p^{2}+V(x)+gW(x),

(5.18)

if we construct the perturbation series

E_{N}(g)\sim\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}g^{n}

(5.19)

for the
N
th eigenvalue of
H
, the regions of convergence of these series are determined by square-root singularities in the complex-
g
plane where the
N
th eigenvalue crosses with other eigenvalues
Bender et al. 1974

There are rare examples of Hamiltonians for which the singularities are not square-root singularities; there may be cube-root or other higher-order algebraic singularities. [See, for example, the study of the
D
-dimensional square-well in
Bender et al. 1992
.] However, the generic picture is universal: At branch-point singularities the eigenvalues cross; each eigenvalue is associated with a sheet in a complex-
g
Riemann surface.

This discussion of perturbation theory suggests a comprehensive research project that has not yet been published. It would be interesting to locate and classify the branch-point singularities in the complex-
g
plane associated with the quartic
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric Hamiltonian (
5.3
) (as well as for other
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric Hamiltonians).

V.4.3
Large-order behavior of perturbation theory

The Hermitian anharmonic oscillator is an ideal model to study the

of the coefficients in a perturbation series; that is, the asymptotic growth of the coefficients

in (

The eigenvalues of the Hermitian oscillator (

a_{n}\sim\frac{m}{2}\frac{\sqrt{6}}{\pi^{3/2}}(-1)^{n+1}\Gamma\big(n+\tfrac{1}{2}\big)\left(\frac{24g}{m^{3}}\right)^{n}\quad(n\to\infty).

The dispersion-relation technique for finding the large-order behavior of perturbation coefficients became known as

\mathcal{PT}

Brézin et al. 1977

It is necessary to know the large-order behavior of the perturbation coefficients in order to justify using

V.4.4
Summation of divergent perturbation series

An important property of real summable divergent perturbation series is that the coefficients in the series alternate in sign. If this condition holds, Borel and Padé summation typically converge to a real number. If the coefficients in the perturbation series are real but do not alternate in sign, the summation may not converge or may converge to a complex number even if the perturbation series represents a real number.

The
cubic
anharmonic-oscillator Hamiltonian

H=p^{2}+x^{2}+gx^{3}\quad(g\penalty\ {\rm real})

(5.20)

has perturbation expansions that do
not
oscillate in sign. For example, the perturbation series about the harmonic-oscillator ground-state energy
E_{0}(0)=1
has the form

E_{0}(g)\sim\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}g^{2n}\quad(g\to 0).

(5.21)

This series in powers of
g^{2}
diverges; the perturbation coefficients
a_{n}
are real and grow factorially with
n
. However, the
a_{n}
do not alternate in sign with increasing
n
, so this series is not uniquely Borel or Padé summable. The sign of the imaginary part of the Borel sum may be either positive or negative. The series coefficients are real but the series represents a
complex
number. This is because the eigenstate of this cubic Hamiltonian either grows or decays in time depending on the boundary conditions on the eigenfunctions.

V.4.5
Perturbation series for

-symmetric oscillators

If we replace

As a second example, let us consider the Hermitian quartic anharmonic oscillator (

The question is, What happens if we replace

\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric quartic Hamiltonian (

As explained in Subsec.

-symmetric theory whose boundary conditions are pictured in (

In the second case, the energy is real because we now have a dynamically stable configuration with balanced outgoing and incoming waves (balanced loss and gain).

The perturbation series that correctly represents the energy levels of the
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric quartic Hamiltonian (
5.3

Perhaps the most beautiful feature of the
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric quantum anharmonic oscillator is this cancellation between perturbative and nonperturbative contributions. This cancellation allows the
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric anharmonic oscillator to be a viable quantum-mechanical theory with a

VI
This is quantum mechanics (not just complex analysis)

In the first five sections of this Review we have used complex-variable techniques to study the spectral properties of
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric Hamiltonians. We have shown that a
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric Hamiltonian can possess an eigenvalue spectrum that is real and bounded below even if the Hamiltonian is non-Hermitian. By itself, this is a remarkable result because the conventional view is that spectral reality and positivity are properties that are reserved for Hermitian Hamiltonians. However, quantum mechanics is more than just the spectral properties of a Hamiltonian. The deeper question is whether a non-Hermitian Hamiltonian can actually define a physical theory of quantum mechanics.

The Hamiltonian for an isolated quantum system must have a real energy spectrum because an energy measurement must give a real number. Furthermore, the energy spectrum must be bounded below to satisfy the physical requirement that an isolated system have a stable ground state. However, the systems discussed in this Review are more general than conventional Hermitian quantum systems because they are coupled to the environment.

Hermiticity is a powerful condition because if
H
is Hermitian, every eigenvalue of
H
is real. We proved this in Sec.
II
but a quick formal proof goes as follows: Take the Hermitian conjugate of the eigenvalue equation
H|E\rangle=E|E\rangle
to get
\langle E|H^{\dagger}=\langle E|E^{*}
and then multiply this equation on the right by
|E\rangle
. This gives
E\langle E|E\rangle=E^{*}\langle E|E\rangle
. Therefore,
E=E^{*}
.

The condition of
\mathcal{PT}
symmetry is weaker than the condition of Hermiticity. As shown in Subsec.
II.4
, if the Hamiltonian is
\mathcal{PT}
symmetric, all we know is that the
secular equation
is real.
Ordinarily, systems that are coupled to the environment are not Hermitian because they have loss to the environment (e.g., radioactive decay) or gain from the environment (e.g., scattering). The
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric systems discussed here are novel because they are coupled to the environment in such a way that loss to the environment is exactly balanced by gain from the environment. This is illustrated in Figs.
5
and
24
(right diagram). Although one would expect that the Hamiltonian for a system coupled to the environment would have a complex spectrum, in the unbroken phase a
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric system has an entirely real spectrum.

Moreover, a
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric system in the unbroken phase satisfies the additional requisites to be a quantum-mechanical system.
A quantum-mechanical system must have a Hilbert space of state vectors that is endowed with an inner product having a
positive norm
. (The norm of a state represents a probability, which must be positive.) Also, the Hamiltonian of the theory must generate
unitary time evolution
because probability must be preserved in time. These requirements are met if the Hamiltonian is Hermitian
H^{\dagger}=H
.

These requirements are
also met if the Hamiltonian satisfies the less restrictive condition of unbroken
\mathcal{PT}
symmetry. In this section we demonstrate that these requisites are met if the
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric Hamiltonian has an unbroken
\mathcal{PT}
symmetry and is
also
symmetric. (All Hamiltonians discussed in this Review are symmetric.) However, this technical assumption of symmetry is in fact not necessary; these requisites are also met if the
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric Hamiltonian is not symmetric, but the argument is more complicated because it requires the use of a biorthogonal basis
Weigert 2003
;
Curtright and Mezincescu 2007
;
Brody 2014
;
Krejčiřík et al. 2006
.

VI.1
Finding the inner product

The principal difficulty with showing that a non-Hermitian
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric Hamiltonian in a region of unbroken
\mathcal{PT}
symmetry defines a theory of quantum mechanics is finding a
positive-definite
inner product with respect to which the eigenstates of the Hamiltonian are orthogonal. For the case of a Hermitian Hamiltonian this is not a problem because the inner product is the same for all such Hamiltonians. In coordinate space, the Hermitian inner product is

\langle\phi|\psi\rangle=\int dx\,\phi^{\dagger}(x)\psi(x),

(6.1)

where the adjoint symbol
{\dagger}
indicates combined matrix transpose and complex conjugation.

However, for a
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric Hamiltonian we do not know in advance the definition of the inner product. Rather, in
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric quantum mechanics the Hamiltonian itself determines its own Hilbert space and associated inner product.

Let us assume that we have found the eigenvalues
E_{n}
and eigenfunctions
\psi_{n}(x)

\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric Hamiltonian

unbroken
\mathcal{PT}
symmetry

of

of
\mathcal{PT}
.
4
4

4

Unlike the case of Hermitian Hamiltonians, the zeros of subsequent eigenfunctions of non-Hermitian

-symmetric Hamiltonians do not interlace. These eigenfunctions exhibit the phenomenon of

;
Eremenko et al. 2008
;
Hezaro 2008
;
Schindler and Bender 2018

Having found the eigenfunctions of

\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric Hamiltonian we begin by making a plausible guess for what the inner product might be. We argue by analogy: For a Hermitian Hamiltonian, where

\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric Hamiltonian, where

\displaystyle(\psi,\phi)^{\mathcal{PT}}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\int_{C}dx\,[\psi(x)]^{\mathcal{PT}}\phi(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{C}dx\,[\psi(-x)]^{*}\phi(x),

Although the

VI.2
The
\mathcal{C}

\mathcal{CPT}

If the parameters of a
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric Hamiltonian

\mathcal{PT}

\mathcal{C}
that commutes with both

\mathcal{PT}

\displaystyle\psi_{0}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp\left(-\mbox{$\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}$}x^{2}\right),

\displaystyle\psi_{1}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle x\exp\left(-\mbox{$\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}$}x^{2}\right),

\displaystyle\psi_{2}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(2x^{2}-1)\exp\left(-\mbox{$\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}$}x^{2}\right),

\displaystyle\psi_{3}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(2x^{3}-3x)\exp\left(-\mbox{$\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}$}x^{2}\right).

We normalize these eigenfunctions so that they are also eigenfunctions of the

\mathcal{PT}
operator with eigenvalue
1
:

\displaystyle\phi_{0}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a_{0}\exp\left(-\mbox{$\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}$}x^{2}\right),

\displaystyle\phi_{1}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a_{1}ix\exp\left(-\mbox{$\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}$}x^{2}\right),

\displaystyle\phi_{2}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a_{2}(2x^{2}-1)\exp\left(-\mbox{$\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}$}x^{2}\right),

\displaystyle\phi_{3}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a_{3}i(2x^{3}-3x)\exp\left(-\mbox{$\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}$}x^{2}\right).

The real numbers
a_{n}
are chosen so that the integral in (
6.6
) evaluates to
(-1)^{n}
for all
n
. If we then substitute the eigenfunctions
\phi_{n}(x)
into (
6.7
) and perform the summation, we obtain the delta function in (
6.7
).

Finally, we construct the
\mathcal{C}
operator. Recall from (
6.6
) that half of the energy eigenstates of
H
have positive
\mathcal{PT}
norm and half have negative
\mathcal{PT}
norm. This condition is similar to what Dirac encountered in formulating the spinor wave equation for fermionic quantum theory
Dirac 1942
. To solve this problem Dirac interpreted the negative-norm states as antiparticles and constructed a new inner product that changed the negative signs to positive signs. We follow Dirac’s construction: Note that if the Hamiltonian
H
has an unbroken
\mathcal{PT}
symmetry, there exists an additional symmetry of
H
represented by a linear operator that we call
\mathcal{C}
. This operator arises because there are equal numbers of positive-
\mathcal{PT}
-norm and negative-
\mathcal{PT}
-norm states all with real energy eigenvalues. In coordinate space the operator
\mathcal{C}
that embodies this symmetry is a sum over the
\mathcal{PT}
-normalized eigenfunctions of the
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric Hamiltonian:

\mathcal{C}(x,y)\equiv\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\phi_{n}(x)\phi_{n}(y).

(6.8)

This equation looks like the unusual completeness condition in (
6.7
) except that
the factor of
(-1)^{n}
is absent
. We can use (
6.6
) and (
6.7
) to verify that the square of
\mathcal{C}
is unity:
\mathcal{C}^{2}={\bf 1}
. In coordinate space this equation reads

\int_{C}dy\,\mathcal{C}(x,y)\mathcal{C}(y,z)=\delta(x-z).

(6.9)

The eigenvalues of
\mathcal{C}
are
\pm 1
, so
\mathcal{C}
is a reflection operator. Also, it is easy to verify that
[\mathcal{C},H]=0
, and because
\mathcal{C}
is linear it follows that the eigenstates of
H
have definite
\mathcal{C}
values:

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}\phi_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{C}dy\,\mathcal{C}(x,y)\phi_{n}(y)

(6.10)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\phi_{m}(x)\int_{C}dy\,\phi_{m}(y)\phi_{n}(y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(-1)^{n}\phi_{n}(x).

As a reflection operator, the
\mathcal{C}
operator resembles the parity operator in quantum mechanics or the charge-conjugation operator in quantum field theory.

The precise meaning of
\mathcal{C}
is that it represents the measurement of the sign of the
\mathcal{PT}
norm in (
6.6
). The operators
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{C}
are both reflection operators and they are distinct square roots of the unity operator
\delta(x-y)
. That is,
\mathcal{P}^{2}=\mathcal{C}^{2}=1
. However,
\mathcal{P}\neq\mathcal{C}
. The parity operator in coordinate space is real because
\mathcal{P}(x,y)=\delta(x+y)
, but the
\mathcal{C}(x,y)
operator is complex because it is a sum of products of complex functions. The operators
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{C}
do not commute, but
\mathcal{C}

does
commute with
\mathcal{PT}
.

Let us now examine the properties of the
\mathcal{CPT}
inner product defined in (
6.3
). Like the
\mathcal{PT}
inner product in (
6.2
), this new inner product is phase independent. Also, because the time-evolution operator is
e^{-iHt}
(as in ordinary quantum mechanics) and because
H
commutes with
\mathcal{PT}
and with
\mathcal{CPT}
, both the
\mathcal{PT}
inner product and the
\mathcal{CPT}
inner product remain time independent as the states evolve. However, the advantage of the
\mathcal{CPT}
inner product is that, unlike the
\mathcal{PT}
inner product, the
\mathcal{CPT}
inner product is
positive definite
. This is because
\mathcal{C}
contributes a factor of
-1
when it acts on states with negative
\mathcal{PT}
norm. In terms of the
\mathcal{CPT}
conjugate, the completeness condition in coordinate space reads

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\phi_{n}(x)[\mathcal{CPT}\phi_{n}(y)]=\delta(x-y).

(6.11)

We can also construct representations of other linear operators in terms of the eigenstates
\phi_{n}(x)
: The coordinate-space representation of the parity operator is

\mathcal{P}(x,y)=\delta(x+y)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}\phi_{n}(x)\phi_{n}(-y),

and the coordinate-space representations of the Hamiltonian and of the Green’s function (the inverse of the Hamiltonian operator) are

\displaystyle H(x,y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}E_{n}\phi_{n}(x)\phi_{n}(y),

\displaystyle G(x,y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}\frac{1}{E_{n}}\phi_{n}(x)\phi_{n}(y).

From the equation for
G(x,y)
above, we can verify that this Green’s function is the
functional inverse
of
H
:
\int dy\,H(x,y)G(y,z)=\delta(x-z)
. For example, for the
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric Hamiltonian (
2.14
) the Green’s function
G(x,y)
satisfies the differential equation

\left[-\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}+x^{2}(ix)^{\varepsilon}\right]G(x,y)=\delta(x-y).

We solve this differential equation in terms of associated Bessel functions in the two regions
x>y
and
x<y
and then patch the solutions together at
x=y
to obtain a closed-form coordinate-space expression for
G(x,y)

Mezincescu 2000
;
Bender and Wang 2001
. We then integrate
G(x,x)
with respect to
x
to obtain an exact formula for the spectral
\zeta
function for all values of
\varepsilon>0
:

\displaystyle\zeta(\varepsilon)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{E_{n}(\varepsilon)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[1+\frac{\cos\left(\frac{3\varepsilon\pi}{2\varepsilon+8}\right)\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{4+\varepsilon}\right)}{\cos\left(\frac{\varepsilon\pi}{4+2\varepsilon}\right)\sin\left(\frac{3\pi}{4+\varepsilon}\right)}\right]

\displaystyle\quad\times\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{4+\varepsilon}\right)\Gamma\left(\frac{2}{4+\varepsilon}\right)\Gamma\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{4+\varepsilon}\right)}{(4+\varepsilon)^{\frac{4+2\varepsilon}{4+\varepsilon}}\Gamma\left(\frac{1+\varepsilon}{4+\varepsilon}\right)\Gamma\left(\frac{2+\varepsilon}{4+\varepsilon}\right)}.

This formula has been verified numerically to over 20 decimal places
Bender and Wang 2001
.

VI.3
Discussion of the
\mathcal{CPT}
-inner product

For a quantum theory defined by a Hermitian Hamiltonian, the Hilbert space of physical states is specified even before the Hamiltonian
H
is known. The inner product for this Hilbert space is defined with respect to Dirac Hermitian conjugation (combined complex conjugation and matrix transposition). Once a Hermitian Hamiltonian has been selected, the standard procedure is to find the eigenvectors and eigenvalues of this Hamiltonian.

In contrast, for a
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric quantum theory, the inner product is determined by the Hamiltonian itself, and thus is determined
dynamically
. One must first solve for the eigenvalues and eigenstates of
H
and then show that we are in an unbroken
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric region before we can know what the Hilbert space and the associated inner product of the theory are. This is because the
\mathcal{C}
operator is constructed from the eigenstates of the Hamiltonian, and the
\mathcal{CPT}
inner product is determined by these eigenstates. The Hilbert space consists of all complex linear combinations of the eigenstates of
H
.

The
\mathcal{C}
operator does not exist as a distinct entity in conventional Hermitian quantum mechanics. For example, if we let the parameter
\varepsilon
in (
2.14
) tend to 0, the
\mathcal{C}
operator in this limit becomes identical to
\mathcal{P}
and the
\mathcal{CPT}
operator becomes
\mathcal{T}
, which performs complex conjugation. Hence, as
\varepsilon
tends to 0, the inner product defined with respect to
\mathcal{CPT}
conjugation reduces to the inner product of conventional quantum mechanics and (
6.7
) reduces to the usual Hermitian statement of completeness for a symmetric Hamiltonian:

\sum_{n}\phi_{n}(x)\phi_{n}^{*}(y)=\delta(x-y).

The
\mathcal{CPT}
inner product in (
6.3
) is independent of the choice of integration contour
C
as long as
C
terminates inside the Stokes sectors associated with the boundary conditions for the eigenvalue problem. [In ordinary quantum mechanics the positive-definite inner product has the form
\int dx\,f^{*}(x)g(x)
, where the integral is taken along the real axis. The path of integration cannot be deformed into the complex plane because the integrand is not analytic.] The path integrals representing both the
\mathcal{PT}
inner product and the
\mathcal{CPT}
inner product have the advantage of analyticity and path independence. The
\mathcal{PT}
inner product is not positive-definite, but it is satisfying that one can still construct a positive-definite metric by using
\mathcal{CPT}
conjugation without disturbing the path independence of the inner-product integral.

The operator
e^{-iHt}
performs time evolution regardless of whether the theory is determined by a
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric Hamiltonian or a conventional Hermitian Hamiltonian. To show that time evolution in a
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric quantum system is unitary, we must demonstrate that the norm of the state does not change as the state vector evolves in time: Let
\psi_{0}(x)
be a vector at
t=0
in the Hilbert space spanned by the energy eigenstates. It evolves into the state
\psi_{t}(x)
at time
t
according to

\psi_{t}(x)=e^{-iHt}\psi_{0}(x).

With respect to the
\mathcal{CPT}
inner product the norm of
\psi_{t}(x)
does not change in time because
H
commutes with
\mathcal{CPT}
.

VI.4
Example:
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric
2\times 2
matrix
Hamiltonian

We illustrate the formal procedures for calculating the
\mathcal{C}
operator and the inner product by using the
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric matrix Hamiltonian in (
2.20
), which we rewrite in polar form as

H=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}re^{i\theta}&g\cr g&re^{-i\theta}\end{array}\right).

(6.12)

The three parameters
r
,
s
, and
\theta
are real. This symmetric Hamiltonian is not Hermitian. However, as shown in Subsec.
II.5
, it is
\mathcal{PT}
symmetric, where the parity operator is given in (
2.21
) and the
\mathcal{T}
operator performs complex conjugation.

Following the procedures outlined above, we first calculate the eigenvalues and eigenfunctions of the Hamiltonian (
6.12
). From (
2.23
) we obtain

E_{\pm}=r\cos\theta\pm\sqrt{g^{2}-r^{2}\sin^{2}\theta}.

(6.13)

As we saw for
H
in (
2.20
), there are two parametric regions for
H
in (
6.12
), one for which the square root is real and the other for which it is imaginary. In the region of broken
\mathcal{PT}
symmetry
g^{2}<r^{2}\sin^{2}\theta
, the eigenvalues form a complex-conjugate pair. In the region of unbroken
\mathcal{PT}
symmetry
g^{2}\geq r^{2}\sin^{2}\theta
, the eigenvalues are real and the simultaneous eigenstates of the operators
H
and
\mathcal{PT}
are

\displaystyle|E_{+}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\cos\alpha}}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}e^{i\alpha/2}\cr e^{-i\alpha/2}\end{array}\right),

\displaystyle|E_{-}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{i}{\sqrt{2\cos\alpha}}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}e^{-i\alpha/2}\cr-e^{i\alpha/2}\end{array}\right),

where
\sin\alpha=\tfrac{r}{g}\,\sin\theta
.

Next we calculate the
\mathcal{PT}
inner product:

(E_{\pm},E_{\pm})^{\mathcal{PT}}=\pm 1\quad{\rm and}\quad(E_{\pm},E_{\mp})=0,

(6.20)

where
(u,v)^{\mathcal{PT}}=(\mathcal{PT}u)\cdot v
. As expected from (
6.6
), the
\mathcal{PT}
inner product has a metric of signature
(+,-)
. Also, note that if the condition
g^{2}>r^{2}\sin^{2}\theta
for unbroken
\mathcal{PT}
symmetry is violated, the states (
VI.4
) are
not
eigenstates of
\mathcal{PT}
because
\alpha
is imaginary. Also, in the region of broken
\mathcal{PT}
symmetry, the
\mathcal{PT}
norms of the energy eigenstates vanish.

We then use (
6.8
) to construct the
\mathcal{C}
operator:

\mathcal{C}=\frac{1}{\cos\alpha}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}i\sin\alpha&1\cr 1&-i\sin\alpha\end{array}\right).

(6.21)

A brief calculation verifies that, as predicted, the
\mathcal{C}
operator commutes with
H
and satisfies
\mathcal{C}^{2}=1
. Also, as expected, the eigenvectors of
H
are simultaneously eigenvectors of
\mathcal{C}
with eigenvalues
+1
and
-1
:

\mathcal{C}|E_{\pm}\rangle=\pm|E_{\pm}\rangle.

(6.22)

Again, as expected, the eigenvalues of
\mathcal{C}
are the signs of the
\mathcal{PT}
norms of the corresponding eigenstates.

Finally, we construct the
\mathcal{CPT}
inner product

\langle u|v\rangle^{\mathcal{CPT}}=(\mathcal{CPT}u)\cdot v

by using the operator
\mathcal{C}
. This inner product is positive definite because
\langle E_{\pm}|E_{\pm}\rangle=1
. The two-dimensional Hilbert space spanned by
|E_{\pm}\rangle
and with inner product
\langle\cdot|\cdot\rangle
has the signature
(+,+)
. This completes the construction of the Hilbert space.

It is instructive to demonstrate that the
\mathcal{CPT}
norm of
any
vector in the Hilbert space (that is, any linear combination of the eigenvectors) is positive. For the vector
\psi=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}a\\
b\end{array}\right)
, where
a
and
b
are arbitrary complex numbers, we calculate that

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}\psi=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}a^{*}\cr b^{*}\end{array}\right),\qquad\mathcal{PT}\psi=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}b^{*}\cr a^{*}\end{array}\right),

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}\mathcal{PT}\psi=\frac{1}{\cos\alpha}\,\left(\begin{array}[]{c}a^{*}+ib^{*}\sin\alpha\cr b^{*}-ia^{*}\sin\alpha\end{array}\right).

Hence,

\displaystyle\langle\psi|\psi\rangle^{\mathcal{CPT}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\mathcal{C}\mathcal{PT}\psi)\cdot\psi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\cos\alpha}\big[a^{*}a+b^{*}b+i\big(b^{*}b-a^{*}a\big)\sin\alpha\big].

Thus, if we take
a=x+iy
and
b=u+iv
with
x
,
y
,
u
, and
v
real, then

\displaystyle\langle\psi|\psi\rangle^{\mathcal{CPT}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\cos\alpha}\big[x^{2}+v^{2}+y^{2}+u^{2}

(6.31)

\displaystyle\quad+2(xv-yu)\sin\alpha\big].

It is easy to prove that this
\mathcal{CPT}
inner product is positive and that it vanishes only if
x=y=u=v=0
.

We can now formulate the statement of completeness for the
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric Hamiltonian (
6.12
). Following the notation used in conventional quantum mechanics, we denote the
\mathcal{CPT}
-conjugate of
|u\rangle
as
\langle u|
; that is, the
\mathcal{CPT}
adjoint operator converts the
ket
vector into the
bra
vector. The completeness condition then has the form

|E_{+}\rangle\langle E_{+}|+|E_{-}\rangle\langle E_{-}|=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&0\cr 0&1\end{array}\right).

(6.32)

Furthermore, using the
\mathcal{CPT}
conjugate
\langle E_{\pm}|
, we can represent the
\mathcal{C}
operator for
H
in (
6.12
) as

\mathcal{C}=|E_{+}\rangle\langle E_{+}|-|E_{-}\rangle\langle E_{-}|.

(6.33)

The change in sign between (
6.32
) and (
6.33
) is the two-dimensional analog of the sign change in the formulas for completeness in (
6.7
) and for the
\mathcal{C}
operator in (
6.8
).

Note that as
\theta
approaches
0
, the Hamiltonian (
6.12
) becomes Hermitian and the
\mathcal{C}
operator reduces to the parity operator
\mathcal{P}
. This illustrates that for a symmetric Hermitian matrix,
\mathcal{CPT}
invariance reduces to the conventional condition of Hermiticity:
H=H^{*}
.

VI.5
Calculating the
\mathcal{C}
Operator

The difference between
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric quantum mechanics and Hermitian quantum mechanics is the existence of a
\mathcal{C}
operator. Only a non-Hermitian
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric Hamiltonian possesses a
\mathcal{C}
operator that is distinct from the parity operator
\mathcal{P}
. However, although the
\mathcal{C}
operator is represented as the formal infinite series in (
6.8
), it is not easy to calculate
\mathcal{C}
by summing this series directly. In Subsec.
II.4
we evaluated the sum for the case of a
2\times 2
matrix, but evaluating the sum for a continuum theory would require that we find analytic expressions for all of the eigenfunctions
\phi_{n}(x)
of
H
. Moreover, such a procedure would be hopeless in quantum field theory because in field theory there is no simple analog of the Schrödinger eigenvalue equation.

The first calculation of the
\mathcal{C}
operator was perturbative
Bender et al. 2003
. This paper considers the cubic
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric Hamiltonian

H=\tfrac{1}{2}p^{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}x^{2}+i\varepsilon x^{3}

(6.34)

and treats
\varepsilon
as a small real parameter. When
\varepsilon=0
, the Hamiltonian
H
reduces to the harmonic oscillator Hamiltonian whose eigenfunctions are Hermite functions. Each eigenfunction of
H
is then expressed as a perturbation series in powers of
\varepsilon
and the first few terms of these perturbation series are calculated. The perturbation series are substituted into (
6.8
), and the summation over the eigenfunctions is done for the first few powers of
\varepsilon
. The result is a formal perturbation expansion of the
\mathcal{C}
operator in powers of
\varepsilon
.

This calculation is complicated but instructive because it shows that the result for
\mathcal{C}
simplifies dramatically if the
\mathcal{C}
operator is written as the exponential of an operator
Q
(which in quantum mechanics is a real function of the operators
x
and
p
) multiplying the parity operator
\mathcal{P}
:

\mathcal{C}=e^{Q(x,p)}\mathcal{P}.

(6.35)

Furthermore, it shows that
\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}Q=0
, which verifies that
\mathcal{C}
approaches
\mathcal{P}
in the limit as the Hamiltonian becomes Hermitian.

We now exploit the representation (
6.35
) for the
\mathcal{C}
operator for two elementary cases. First, we consider the complex shifted harmonic oscillator
H=\mbox{$\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}$}p^{2}+\mbox{$\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}$}x^{2}+i\varepsilon x
in (
2.6
). As shown in Subsec.
II.1
, this Hamiltonian has an unbroken
\mathcal{PT}
symmetry for all real
\varepsilon
and its eigenvalues
E_{n}=n+\mbox{$\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}$}+\mbox{$\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}$}\varepsilon^{2}
are all real. The
exact
formula for
\mathcal{C}
for this Hamiltonian is
\mathcal{C}=e^{Q}\mathcal{P}
, where

Q=-\varepsilon p.

(6.36)

In the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
the shifted Hamiltonian (
2.6
) becomes Hermitian and
\mathcal{C}
reduces to the parity operator
\mathcal{P}
.

Next, we reconsider the non-Hermitian
2\times 2
matrix Hamiltonian (
6.12
). We rewrite the
\mathcal{C}
operator in (
6.21
) for this Hamiltonian as
\mathcal{C}=e^{Q}\mathcal{P}
, where

Q=\mbox{$\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}$}\sigma_{2}\log\left(\frac{1-\sin\alpha}{1+\sin\alpha}\right)

(6.37)

and
\sigma_{2}
is the Pauli matrix

\sigma_{2}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&-i\cr i&0\end{array}\right).

(6.38)

Once again, in the limit
\theta\to 0
, the Hamiltonian (
6.12
) becomes Hermitian and the
\mathcal{C}
operator reduces to the parity operator
\mathcal{P}
.

VI.6
Algebraic calculation of the
\mathcal{C}
operator

Calculating the
\mathcal{C}
operator is difficult and only rarely has it been done exactly. However, in this subsection we show that there is a simple-looking set of three
algebraic
equations that serve as a general starting point for the calculation. These algebraic equations avoid the formidable task of evaluating the sum over eigenstates in (
6.8
). Furthermore, the algebraic technique generalizes from quantum mechanics, where the eigenstates are known at least in principle, to quantum field theory.

For any
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric Hamiltonian
H
the
\mathcal{C}
operator has three fundamental algebraic properties. First,
\mathcal{C}
commutes with the spacetime reflection operator
\mathcal{PT}
:

\displaystyle[\mathcal{C},\mathcal{PT}]=0,

(6.39)

although
\mathcal{C}
does not commute with
\mathcal{P}
or
\mathcal{T}
separately. Second,
\mathcal{C}
is a reflection operator, so

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{2}=1.

(6.40)

These first two conditions are
kinematic
in character and are valid for any
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric Hamiltonian in the unbroken
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric region. The third condition is that
\mathcal{C}
commutes with the Hamiltonian

\displaystyle[\mathcal{C},H]=0,

(6.41)

so
\mathcal{C}
is time independent. This third condition is
dynamical
in character because it depends on the Hamiltonian. To summarize,
\mathcal{C}
is a time-independent
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric reflection operator.

To solve (
6.39
)-(
6.41
) for a quantum-mechanical system defined by a Hamiltonian of the form
H=H(x,p)
, we substitute the operator representation for
\mathcal{C}
in (
6.35
) into (
6.39
)–(
6.41
) and try to solve the resulting equations for
Q(x,p)
. Substituting (
6.35
) into (
6.39
) gives

e^{Q(x,p)}=\mathcal{PT}e^{Q(x,p)}\mathcal{PT}=e^{Q(-x,p)}.

This implies that
Q(x,p)
is an
even
function of
x
. Next, we substitute (
6.35
) into (
6.40
):

e^{Q(x,p)}\mathcal{P}e^{Q(x,p)}\mathcal{P}=e^{Q(x,p)}e^{Q(-x,-p)}=1.

This equation implies that
Q(x,p)=-Q(-x,-p)
, and because
Q(x,p)
is an even function of
x
, it must be an
odd
function of
p
. These two properties of
Q
are general and hold for any
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric Hamiltonian..

The third condition (
6.41
) imposes the dynamics specified by the Hamiltonian that defines the quantum-mechanical theory. Substituting
\mathcal{C}=e^{Q(x,p)}\mathcal{P}
into (
6.41
), we obtain an exact equation satisfied by
Q
:

e^{Q(x,p)}[\mathcal{P},H]+[e^{Q(x,p)},H]\mathcal{P}=0.

(6.42)

Finding an exact solution to (
6.42
) is difficult, but perturbative methods may be used to solve for
Q
.
[Nonperturbative techniques, such as semiclassical approximations, may also be helpful
Bender and Jones 2004
.] To illustrate the perturbative approach, we examine (
6.42
) for the case of the cubic Hamiltonian
H
in (
6.34
). We write
H=H_{0}+\varepsilon H_{1}
, where
H_{0}=\mbox{$\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}$}p^{2}+\mbox{$\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}$}x^{2}
is the harmonic-oscillator Hamiltonian, which commutes with
\mathcal{P}
, and
H_{1}=ix^{3}
, which anticommutes with
\mathcal{P}
. Then, (
6.42
) simplifies to

2\varepsilon e^{Q(x,p)}H_{1}=[e^{Q(x,p)},H].

(6.43)

Next, we expand
Q(x,p)
as a perturbation series in
odd
powers of
\varepsilon
,

\displaystyle Q(x,p)=\varepsilon Q_{1}(x,p)+\varepsilon^{3}Q_{3}(x,p)+\cdots,

(6.44)

and substitute this expansion into the exponential
e^{Q(x,p)}
. This leads to a sequence of equations that can be solved systematically for the operator functions
Q_{n}(x,p)

(n=1,3,5,...)
subject to the symmetry constraints that ensure that the conditions (
6.39
) and (
6.40
) are met. The first two of these equations are

\displaystyle\big[H_{0},Q_{1}\big]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2H_{1},

\displaystyle\big[H_{0},Q_{3}\big]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\tfrac{1}{6}\big[Q_{1},\big[Q_{1},H_{1}\big]\big].

(6.45)

To solve these equations we substitute a polynomial with arbitrary coefficients for

and then calculate the coefficients. For example, to solve the first equation in (

), we substitute for
Q_{1}
the most general Hermitian

polynomial that is

in

Q_{1}(x,p)=Ap^{3}+Bxpx,

where

and

VI.7
Isospectral Hermitian and
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric Hamiltonians

In Sec.
V
we constructed a quartic Hermitian Hamiltonian whose eigenvalues are the same as the eigenvalues of a quartic
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric Hamiltonian. The transformation from the
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric to the Hermitian Hamiltonian in that case was simple and explicit, but to date no other nontrivial examples of exact isometric pairs of
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric and Hermitian Hamiltonians of the form
H=p^{2}+V(x)
have been found. However, it is possible to construct a
formal
(nonexplicit) similarity mapping from a
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric Hamiltonian
H
to an equivalent Hermitian Hamiltonian
h
:

h=e^{-Q/2}He^{Q/2}.

(6.46)

This mapping makes use of the
square root
of the positive operator
e^{Q}

Mostafazadeh 2002a
;
Mostafazadeh 2003a
;
Mostafazadeh 2003b
;
Mostafazadeh 2002b
;
Mostafazadeh 2002c
.

The new Hamiltonian
h
is equivalent to
H
in the sense that it is isospectral to (has the same eigenvalues as)
H
. To explain the similarity transformation (
6.46
) we begin by recalling from (
6.35
) that the
\mathcal{C}
operator has the general form
\mathcal{C}=e^{Q}\mathcal{P}
, where
Q=Q(x,p)
is a real function of the dynamical operator variables
x
and
p
of the quantum theory. We then multiply
\mathcal{C}
on the right by the parity operator
\mathcal{P}
to obtain
e^{Q}
:

e^{Q}=\mathcal{C}\mathcal{P}.

(6.47)

This shows that
\mathcal{C}\mathcal{P}
is a positive and invertible operator.

To show that
h
in (
6.46
) is Hermitian, we take the Hermitian conjugate of (
6.46
),

h^{\dagger}=e^{Q/2}H^{\dagger}e^{-Q/2},

(6.48)

and rewrite this formula as
h^{\dagger}=e^{-Q/2}e^{Q}H^{\dagger}e^{-Q}e^{Q/2}
. We then
use (
6.47
) to replace
e^{Q}
by
\mathcal{C}\mathcal{P}
and
e^{-Q}
by
\mathcal{P}\mathcal{C}
:

h^{\dagger}=e^{-Q/2}\mathcal{C}\mathcal{P}H^{\dagger}\mathcal{P}\mathcal{C}e^{Q/2}.

(6.49)

Next, we use (
6.12
) to replace
H^{\dagger}
by
\mathcal{P}H\mathcal{P}
. This gives

h^{\dagger}=e^{-Q/2}\mathcal{C}\mathcal{P}\mathcal{P}H\mathcal{P}\mathcal{P}\mathcal{C}e^{Q/2}=e^{-Q/2}\mathcal{C}H\mathcal{C}e^{Q/2}.

Finally, recalling that
\mathcal{C}
commutes with
H
[see (
6.41
)] and that the square of
\mathcal{C}
is unity [see (
6.40
)], we reduce the right side of (
6.49
) to the right side of (
6.46
). This verifies that
h
is Dirac Hermitian.

We have shown that for a non-Hermitian
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric Hamiltonian
H
whose
\mathcal{PT}
symmetry is unbroken, it is possible, at least in principle, to use (
6.46
) to construct a Hermitian Hamiltonian
h
that is isospectral to
H
. Assuming that
H
has an
unbroken

\mathcal{PT}
symmetry is crucial because this allows us to construct
\mathcal{C}
, which in turn allows us to construct the similarity operator
e^{Q/2}
.

This similarity transformation raises a crucial question that has not yet been answered: Are
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric Hamiltonians physically new and distinct from ordinary Hermitian Hamiltonians or do they describe exactly the same physical processes that ordinary Hermitian Hamiltonians describe? That is, is
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric quantum mechanics physically new and different from conventional Hermitian quantum mechanics or are
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric Hamiltonians just complicated similarity transformations of conventional Hermitian Hamiltonians?

One possible response to this question is calculational in character. Equation (
6.46
) is a
formal
similarity transformation that maps a
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric Hamiltonian
H
having unbroken
\mathcal{PT}
symmetry to a Hermitian Hamiltonian
h
that is isospectral with
H
. However, except in rare cases, actually constructing this transformation is hopelessly difficult. [See, for example, the discussion of the
\mathcal{C}
operator for a
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric square well
Bender et al. 2019
;
Znojil 2006
;
Bender et al. 1999a
;
Bagchi et al. 2002
;
Znojil 2001
.] Of course, perturbative calculations can be performed, but these are also complicated and not likely to give transparent analytical insight. A
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric Hamiltonian
H
may have a simple local structure that makes it easy to use in performing calculations, but the isospectral Hermitian
h
is typically
nonlocal
because its interaction term has arbitrarily high powers of the variables
x
and
p

Bender et al. 2006c
. Thus, it is not just difficult to calculate using the Hermitian Hamiltonian
h
; it is virtually impossible because the usual regulation schemes, especially in quantum field theory, are inadequate.

Even for the one nontrivial example in Sec.
V
for which there is actually a closed-form quartic potential for both
H
and
h
, it is not even possible to construct the
\mathcal{C}
operator in simple closed form. This is because the mapping from
H
to
h
involves a Fourier transform and thus the mapping is nonlocal (see Subsec.
IV.3
).

Moreover, while the
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric
H
and the Hermitian
h
are isospectral, we saw in Subsecs.
IV.3
and
IV.4
that there are profound differences between these Hamiltonians at the
classical
level;
h
has a quantum anomaly, that is,
h
depends explicitly on Planck’s constant
\hbar
. We conclude that although the formal mapping from
H
to
h
is theoretically interesting, it does not have any practical calculational value.

A second response to the question of whether
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric Hamiltonians are physically new is of greater significance because it has far-reaching theoretical implications. The mapping from
H
to
h
in (
6.46
) is a
similarity
transformation and not a
unitary
transformation. A unitary transformation is
bounded
, but the similarity transformation operator
e^{Q}
is unbounded. Thus, the Hamiltonians
H
and
h
are
formally
isospectral, but the mapping between the Hamiltonians is unlikely to be one-to-one because it may not map all the vectors in the Hilbert space associated with
h
onto all the vectors in the Hilbert space associated with
H
.

Of course, for a finite-dimensional matrix Hamiltonian like that in (
6.12
), the operator
e^{Q}
is bounded [see (
6.21
) and (
6.37
)]. However, for continuum Hamiltonians such as (
2.14
) the mapping
e^{Q}
is unbounded. Thus, there may be experimentally measurable physical differences between
H
and
h
, perhaps some sort of Bohm-Aharanov-like effect. This issue has theoretical importance because it relates to whether
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric quantum mechanics is physically different from ordinary quantum mechanics. An entire chapter of
Bender et al. 2019
,
Chap. 8, is devoted to a discussion of this question.

Finally, we emphasize that the formal mapping from a
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric Hamiltonian
H
to a Hermitian Hamiltonian
h
is not possible unless
H
has an unbroken
\mathcal{PT}
symmetry. The mapping from
H
to
h
cannot be analytically continued from the unbroken to the broken region. For example, the entire analysis in Subsec.
VI.4
fails if
g^{2}<r^{2}\sin^{2}(\theta)
. If
H
has a broken
\mathcal{PT}
symmetry, there are usually a finite number of real eigenvalues and an infinite number of complex eigenvalues, as in Fig.
3
. In fact, this is what we see in the real world; there are a small number of stable fundamental particles and the rest are unstable
Bender et al. 2016
.

VI.8
Observables

In Hermitian quantum mechanics a linear operator
A
must be Hermitian to be an observable:
A=A^{\dagger}
. This condition ensures that the expectation value of
A
in a state is real. Furthermore, operators in the Heisenberg picture evolve in time according to
A(t)=e^{iHt}A(0)e^{-iHt}
, so the Hermiticity condition is maintained in time.

In
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric quantum mechanics the condition for an operator
A
to be an observable is

A^{\rm T}=\mathcal{CPT}A\,\mathcal{CPT},

(6.50)

where
A^{\rm T}
is the
matrix transpose
of
A

Bender et al. 2002
;
Bender et al. 2004
. If (
6.50
) holds at
t=0
, then it continues to hold for
t>0
because we have assumed that the Hamiltonian
H
is symmetric (
H=H^{\rm T}
). This condition also guarantees that the expectation value of
A
in any state is real. As stated above, the symmetry requirement (
6.50
) for
A
to be an observable involves matrix transposition. Thus, (
6.50
) is more restrictive than necessary and it has been generalized in
Mostafazadeh and Batal 2004
;
Mostafazadeh 2005
;
Jones 2005
.
Again, if the matrix-transpose-symmetry condition on the Hamiltonian is removed, we must introduce a biorthogonal basis
Weigert 2003
;
Curtright and Mezincescu 2007
;
Brody 2014
.

The operator
\mathcal{C}
itself satisfies (
6.50
), so it is an observable. The Hamiltonian is also an observable. However, as explained in Sec.
III.6
, the
x
operator in
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric quantum theory is not an observable and the
p
operator is also not an observable. Indeed, as noted earlier, the expectation value of
x
in the ground state is a negative imaginary number. Thus,
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric quantum mechanics is somewhat similar to fermionic quantum field theories, in which the fermion field is complex and does not have a classical limit.

To see why the expectation value of the
x
operator in
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric quantum mechanics is a negative imaginary number, we examine the classical trajectories shown in Fig.
9
. All these classical trajectories have left-right (
\mathcal{PT}
) symmetry, but not up-down symmetry. Furthermore, the classical paths spend more time in the lower-half complex-
x
plane. Thus, the average classical position must be a negative imaginary number. Just as the classical particle moves about in the complex plane, the quantum probability current flows about in the complex plane
Bender et al. 2010c
;
Bender et al. 2010b
. One plausible interpretation of
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric quantum mechanics is that it describes extended rather than pointlike objects, where these objects extend in the imaginary as well as in the real direction.

Appendix A
Increasing role of complex variables in physics

The theme of this Review is that although we can only observe and measure real quantities, the dynamical behavior of a quantum-mechanical system is determined in part by what happens in the complex plane. For example, in particle physics a resonance in a scattering amplitude is associated with a Breit-Wigner pole in the complex-energy plane. In quantum mechanics when a particle tunnels, it vanishes on one side of a potential barrier and reappears on the other side; the motion of the particle as it passes through the barrier can be expressed as a sum over
complex
semiclassical trajectories.

As mentioned in Sec.
I
, complex numbers did not play a role in physical theories until the advent of quantum mechanics. This Appendix emphasizes the importance of complex numbers in the formulation of modern physical theories as well as in mathematics, and in particular in
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric quantum mechanics.

The complex number system has been studied for centuries. In the sixteenth century G. Cardano found that complex numbers were required to represent the roots of some polynomial equations and that the real number system alone was not sufficient. (Cardano was particularly interested in cubic equations.) In the ensuing years it became increasingly clear to mathematicians that the real number system was incomplete and that the real number line naturally extends to the complex plane.

There is a subtle difference between extending the real line to the real plane and extending the real line to the
complex
plane. A point in the real plane represents a pair of unrelated real numbers
x
and
y
, whereas a point in the complex plane is a
single
complex number
z=x+iy
. In complex analysis one studies functions of
one
argument, namely, the complex variable
z
, and not functions of two independent real arguments
x
and
y
.

Real analysis is empowered by embedding the real line in the complex plane; new techniques become available that can be used to solve difficult problems, and some features of real functions can be better understood. For example, complex analysis easily explains why the Taylor series

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-x^{2})^{n}
,
which represents the real rational function
f(x)=\frac{1}{1+x^{2}}
, ceases to converge when
|x|\geq 1
even though
f(x)
is smooth and infinitely differentiable for all real
x
. (This is due to singularities in the complex-
x
plane at
\pm i
.) This example shows that what happens in the complex plane strongly affects what happens on the real axis. In modern physics we find that while the complex world is invisible to inhabitants of the real world, what happens in the complex plane has a powerful influence on what we observe in the real world.

There are countless examples of the power of complex-variable theory in explaining the behavior of real mathematics. For example, just a few lines of complex analysis establishes the fundamental theorem of algebra. Moreover, complex analysis explains the nature of real multivalued functions such as
\sqrt{x}
and
\log(x)
by defining them on Riemann surfaces. (Without resorting to a Riemann surface one cannot explain how the square-root function can be double-valued and still be a function.)

Complex-variable techniques are often needed to explain the behavior of
real
integrals. For example, Laplace’s method (a standard tool of real analysis) fails to determine the asymptotic behavior of the real integral

I(x)=\int_{0}^{1}dt\,e^{-4xt^{2}}\cos\big(5xt-xt^{3}\big)

for large positive
x
. However, in just a few lines the complex method of steepest descents readily establishes that the asymptotic behavior of
I(x)
is given by

I(x)\sim\sqrt{\pi/x}\,e^{-2x}\quad(x\to+\infty)

Bender and Orszag 1999
.
Even though the integral
I(x)
is a
real
function of
x
, real analysis alone is
insufficient to derive this asymptotic approximation.

Like the case of the Taylor series for the function
1/(x^{2}+1)
, the asymptotic behavior of
I(x)
is determined by a
saddle point
that lies off the real axis and in the complex plane at
t=i
. If we insist on using Laplace’s method to obtain the asymptotic behavior, we run into trouble: Using real analysis, we would argue that for large positive
x
the contribution to the integral comes from a localized range of
t
around
t=0
because the integrand contains a Gaussian. This statement is correct, but it suggests (wrongly) that for large
x
we can replace the cosine term in the integrand by 1 (its value at
t=0
) and evaluate the resulting Gaussian integral. If we do so, we obtain the
incorrect
result

I(x)\sim\mbox{$\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}$}\sqrt{\pi/x}\quad({\rm wrong!}).

If we try to improve this argument by approximating the cosine term by
\cos(5xt)
instead of 1 (neglecting
t^{3}
compared with
t
) and then evaluate the resulting Gaussian integral, we obtain a different but still incorrect result:

I(x)\sim\mbox{$\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}$}\sqrt{\pi/x}\exp(-25x/16)\quad({\rm wrong!}).

Why is complex analysis so powerful? One can only approach a point on the real line from the left and from the right. In contrast, one can approach a point in the complex domain from infinitely many directions. Because limits are required to construct a quantity such as the derivative of a function, the properties of the limiting quantity are more constrained in complex-variable theory than they are in real-variable theory.

For the case of a derivative, this constraint takes the form of the Cauchy-Riemann equations and leads to the property of
analyticity
. In real-variable theory if the derivative
f^{\prime}(x)
exists in an open region of the real-
x
axis, we cannot know whether higher derivatives of
f(x)
exist in that region. However, in complex-variable theory if
f^{\prime}(z)
exists in an open region
R
of the complex-
z
plane, then
f(z)
is analytic in
R
and all higher derivatives of
f(z)
exist in
R
as well. Furthermore, the Taylor series for
f(z)
about
z_{0}\in R
converges inside the largest circle about
z_{0}
that fits inside
R
. Analyticity enables us to use path independence and residue theory (for which there is no real-variable analog) to evaluate difficult real integrals and to perform complex transforms. Analyticity enables us to derive dispersion relations, which are immensely powerful tools in optics and scattering theory.

Another consequence of the complex limiting process is that, unlike the case of real-variable theory where there are two different infinite numbers,
+\infty
and
-\infty
, in complex-variable theory there is a
unique
point at infinity
Copson 1960
. The point
\infty
in the complex plane is defined as
\lim_{z\to 0}\,1/z
, where
z
approaches 0
along any path
in the complex-
z
plane. This is crucial in understanding how a classical particle at
x=0
in a
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric
-x^{4}
potential can drift off to
x=+\infty
and then come back from
x=-\infty
(see Fig.
18
) or how a particle in a
-5x^{2}+x^{4}
potential can drift off to
x=i\infty
and come back from
x=-i\infty
(see Figs.
34
and
35
). Of course, a particle that begins at
x=0
in a
-x^{4}
potential may slide down to
x=+\infty
in finite time and simply
stay there
and never come back. Such a trajectory is not forbidden, but complex-variable theory
allows
such a particle to exhibit an alternative behavior by identifying the points
+\infty
and
-\infty
at opposite ends of the real axis.

Despite the analytical power of complex-variable theory, which was understood and used by theoretical physicists for centuries, complex variables were not incorporated into the many physical theories that were developed in the nineteenth century. Complex analysis provided powerful tools for solving differential equations, evaluating integrals, or deriving asymptotic approximations, but the number
i
did not appear in the physical theories of classical mechanics, fluid dynamics, classical gravity, or electrodynamics. The number
i
did not even appear in the formulation of special and general relativity in the early twentieth century.

Virtually all of the fundamental physical phenomena that were studied before the discovery of quantum mechanics exhibit three kinds of characteristic behaviors, propagation, equilibrium, and diffusion. These three behaviors can be modeled by three classical linear partial differential equations. Propagation is modeled by the hyperbolic wave equation
u_{tt}=u_{xx}
, equilibrium is modeled by the elliptic equation
u_{xx}+u_{yy}=0
(Laplace’s equation), and diffusion is modeled by the parabolic equation
u_{t}=u_{xx}
. In none of these equations does the number
i
appear explicitly and the only solutions that are of physical interest are real. (Interestingly, the real solutions to the two-dimensional Laplace’s equation are the real or the imaginary parts of analytic functions.)

It was not until the theory of nonrelativistic quantum mechanics was formulated in the 1920s that the complex number
i
first appeared in a fundamental way in the Schrödinger equation
i\psi_{t}=-\psi_{xx}+V(x)\psi
. Among the early successes of nonrelativistic quantum mechanics was the development of BCS theory in 1958; the complex number
i
is essential in formulating this theory. Analyticity of scattering amplitudes is the key assumption in S-matrix theory
Chew et al. 1962
, which had its heyday in the 1950s, and this led to studies of complex angular momentum
Regge 1960
;
Veneziano 1968
. The Dirac equation and Fermi statistics rely explicitly on the complex number
i
.

The central mathematical content of quantum theory is that while a physical probabilistic prediction is a real number, one obtains this real number from a complex amplitude, which is determined by solving the complex Schrödinger equation or by evaluating a complex path integral. The principal point of this Review is that if we extend and deform real potentials into the complex domain and interpret these new potentials using complex-variable techniques, we obtain new, viable, and physically-allowed
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric quantum theories that can be studied both theoretically and experimentally.
