Positive magnetoresistance induced by hydrodynamic fluctuations in chiral media
Noriyuki Sogabe, Naoki Yamamoto, and Yi Yin

Abstract
We analyze the combined effects of hydrodynamic fluctuations and chiral magnetic effect (CME) for a chiral medium in the presence of a background magnetic field. Based on the recently developed non-equilibrium effective field theory, we show fluctuations give rise to a CME-related positive contribution to magnetoresistance, while the early studies without accounting for the fluctuations find a CME-related negative magnetoresistance. At zero axial relaxation rate, the fluctuations contribute to the transverse conductivity in addition to the longitudinal one.

Introduction

The transport properties of a chiral medium (many-body system involving chiral fermions) and their deep connection to quantum anomalies have attracted significant interests recently.
Of particular importance is the behavior of electric conductivity (or its inverse, electric resistance) under the external magnetic field
{\bm{B}}
.
In table-top experiments, the negative magnetoresistance is proposed as a signature of the chiral magnetic effect (CME), the anomaly-induced vector current in the presence of magnetic field and chiral charge imbalance
Fukushima:2008xe
;
Kharzeev:2007jp
;
Nielsen:1983rb
;
Vilenkin:1980fu
.
Indeed, as shown in refs.
Son:2012bg
;
Stephanov:2014dma
;
Fukushima:2019ugr
, the balance between the axial charge density
n_{\rm A}
production due to the chiral anomaly and axial charge relaxation requires that in a steady state in the presence of electric field
{\bm{E}}
, the axial charge density
n_{\rm A}\propto C{{\bm{E}}}\cdot{{\bm{B}}}/r
, where
r
denotes the axial charge relaxation rate and
C
is the anomaly coefficient. Then, with the CME, one finds an additional contribution to the longitudinal conductivity

\displaystyle\Delta\sigma_{\rm L}\propto\frac{C^{2}{\bm{B}}^{2}}{r}\,.

(1)

The measurements of magnetoresistance in Weyl and Dirac semimetals have been reported in refs.
Li:2014bha
;
Xiong413
;
Huang:2015eia
;
Arnold_2016
.

Nevertheless, the fluctuation effects have not yet been taken into account in eq. (
1
).
It is well-known that in an ordinary fluid,
the fluctuations and interactions among sound and diffusive modes lead to significant effects on the behavior of transport coefficients
POMEAU197563
;
Kovtun:2003vj
;
Kovtun:2012rj
;
PhysRevA.16.732
;
Kovtun:2014nsa
;
Grossi:2020ezz
;
Grossi:2021gqi
.
Therefore, one may naturally ask how fluctuations would modify the magnetoresistance in a chiral medium.
Addressing this question is the primary goal of the present work.
For definiteness, we shall consider the fluctuations of both vector and axial charge densities.
As in previous studies
Son:2012bg
;
Hattori:2017usa
, we assume that
r
is parametrically small compared with the microscopic relaxation rate, and hence we include the axial charge density as a slow mode.

We here use the recently developed non-equilibrium effective field theory (EFT) for hydrodynamics fluctuations
Crossley:2015
;
Crossley:2017
(see ref.
Glorioso:2018wxw
for a review and refs.
Haehl:2015foa
;
Jensen:2017kzi
for related developments), including the effects of quantum anomaly
Glorioso:2017lcn
, to perform our analysis.
Compared with the traditional methods, the EFTs are derived based on the symmetries and action principle and provide a basis for the systematic analysis.
In some situations, such as the one considered in ref.
Chen-Lin:2018kfl
, EFT calculations lead to different results as compared with traditional analysis.
Previous work including the fluctuations of a single chiral charge and CME can be found in ref.
Delacretaz:2020jis
.
See refs.
Fukushima:2017lvb
;
Fukushima:2019ugr
for the diagrammatic calculation of magnetoresistance for quark-gluon plasma (QGP) based on perturbative QCD.

In two situations,
r\neq 0
and
r=0
, we determine specific corrections to the conductivity due to the combined effects of the CME and fluctuations in the small
{\bm{B}}
regime (see eq. (
96
) and eq. (
121
), respectively).
Physically, those CME-related contributions have two origins.
First, the CME is proportional to the axial chemical potential which generically depends on charge density non-linearly and gives rise to non-linear coupling among density fluctuations.
Second, the CME modifies the dispersion relation of fluctuations modes
Kharzeev:2010gd
;
Stephanov:2014dma
.
Given the difference in physical origin,
we should not be surprised to see that
the fluctuation corrections are in marked difference from eq. (
1
).
One important qualitative feature we observe is that the sign of fluctuation contributions is opposite to that of eq. (
1
), meaning they give rise to positive magnetoresistance.
Moreover, we find a non-zero contribution to transverse conductivity when
r=0
.
As already noticed in some references
Baumgartner:2017kme
;
Fukushima:2017lvb
;
Fukushima:2019ugr
,
other mechanisms unrelated to the anomaly could cause magnetoresistance.
The present work aims to demonstrate that even if one only focuses on the effects of the chiral anomaly, the contribution from fluctuations to magnetoresistance can be qualitatively different from that at “tree-level.”
Our results might apply to physical systems, such as the QGP created by heavy-ion collisions, Weyl semimetals, and the electroweak plasma in the primordial Universe.

2
Non-equilibrium effective field theory

2.1
The action

We are interested in the fluctuation dynamics of vector charge density
n_{\rm V}
and axial charge density
n_{\rm A}
in a chiral medium. As already mentioned in the Introduction,
we shall assume the relaxation rate of
n_{\rm A}
is small compared with the microscopic relaxation rate. Furthermore, we shall limit ourselves to situations that temperature is much smaller than vector chemical potential
\mu_{\rm V}
and/or axial chemical potential
\mu_{\rm A}
.
In this regime, we could ignore the mixing of
n_{\rm V}
and
n_{\rm A}
with the energy density. We also note in electron systems including Weyl semimetals, the mean free path of momentum-relaxing scattering (e.g., impurity scattering) can typically be shorter than the mean free path of momentum-conserving scattering (electron-electron scattering). In such a situation, the momentum is not a hydrodynamic variable, and ignoring the coupling of (charge) density modes to sound/shear modes can be well justified. Therefore, in long-time and large-distance limits, we can integrate out other modes and obtain the effective action
I_{{\rm eff}}
describing the remaining slow modes
n_{\rm V}
and
n_{\rm A}
.
In general, it is difficult to obtain
I_{\rm eff}
directly from microscopic theories.
Instead, one should construct
I_{\rm eff}
based on the symmetries together with other physical requirements, as we shall do below following the formalism developed by refs.
Crossley:2015
;
Crossley:2017
;
Glorioso:2017lcn
(see ref.
Glorioso:2018wxw
for a pedagogical introduction).

We begin with the path integral representation of the generating functional on the Schwinger-Keldysh contour,

\displaystyle{\rm e}^{{\rm i}W[A^{\rm r},A^{\rm a}]}=\int\prod_{\alpha={\rm V,A}}\prod_{s={\rm a},{\rm r}}\,{\cal D}\psi_{\alpha}^{s}\,{\rm e}^{{\rm i}I_{{\rm eff}}[\psi^{\rm a},\psi^{\rm r};A^{\rm a},A^{\rm r}]}\,.

(2)

Here, we have introduced the external gauge fields
A^{\rm r}_{\alpha}
and
A^{\rm a}_{\alpha}
and the dynamical fields
\psi^{\rm r}_{\alpha}
and
\psi^{\rm a}_{\alpha}
associated with charge density
n_{\alpha}
, in the “r-” and “a-” basis.
One can interpret
\psi^{{\rm r}}_{\alpha}
and
\psi^{{\rm a}}_{\alpha}
as the
\rm{U}(1)
phase rotations of each fluid element (see refs.
Crossley:2015
;
Glorioso:2018wxw
for more details).
The
\rm r
-variables are related to the physical observables, while the
\rm a
-variables are the associated noise variables.

Next, we list various symmetries and consistency requirements which
I_{{\rm eff}}
should satisfy:

1.

Gauge symmetries:
I_{\rm eff}
has to be invariant under
{\rm U}(1)_{\rm V}
gauge symmetry. Furthermore, we require
{\rm U}(1)_{\rm A}
gauge symmetry for
I_{\rm eff}
in the limit that the axial relaxation and the chiral anomaly are absent.
Here,

{\rm U}(1)_{\alpha}
gauge transformation can be written explicitly as

\displaystyle A^{s}_{\mu,\alpha}\to A^{s}_{\mu,\alpha}-\partial_{\mu}\phi_{\alpha}^{s}\,,\quad\psi_{\alpha}^{s}\to\psi_{\alpha}^{s}+\phi_{\alpha}^{s}\,,

(3)

where
\phi_{\alpha}^{s}
is an arbitrary
{\rm U}(1)_{\alpha}
phase. For a term invariant under the
{\rm U}(1)_{\alpha}
symmetry, its dependence on
A^{\mu,s}_{\alpha}
and
\psi_{\alpha}^{s}
should come through the gauge-invariant combination:

\displaystyle{\cal A}^{s}_{\mu,\alpha}=A^{s}_{\mu,\alpha}+\partial_{\mu}\psi_{\alpha}^{s}\,.

(4)

In particular, the vector and axial chemical potentials are expressed as

\displaystyle\mu_{\alpha}={\cal A}_{0,\alpha}^{\rm r}.

(5)

2.

Shift symmetries:
For each fluid element, it should have the freedom of making independent
{\rm U}(1)_{\rm\alpha}
phase rotations as far as those phases
\zeta_{\alpha}(\bm{x})
are time-independent:

\displaystyle\psi_{\alpha}^{\rm r}\rightarrow\psi_{\alpha}^{\rm r}+\zeta_{\alpha}({\bm{x}})\,.

(6)

Note that shift symmetries will be absent when the global
{\rm U}(1)_{\rm\alpha}
symmetry is spontaneously broken (see refs.
Dubovsky:2011sj
;
Crossley:2015
for further details).

3.

Dynamical Kubo-Martin-Schwinger (KMS) symmetry:
Suppose the microscopic theory is invariant under a
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
anti-unitary transformation
\Theta
,
then
I_{{\rm eff}}
is invariant under the KMS transformation
Crossley:2017
, which, in the “classical” limit that quantum fluctuations are small compared with the thermodynamic fluctuations, is defined as

\displaystyle{\cal A}^{{\rm r}}_{\mu}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\Theta{\cal A}^{{\rm r}}_{\mu}\,,\quad{\cal A}^{{\rm a}}_{\mu}\rightarrow\Theta{\cal A}^{{\rm a}}_{\mu}+\frac{\rm i}{T}\Theta\partial_{t}{\cal A}^{{\rm r}}_{\mu}\,,

(7a)

\displaystyle\mu_{\alpha}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\Theta\mu_{\alpha}\,,\quad\psi_{\alpha}^{\rm a}\rightarrow\Theta\psi_{\alpha}^{\rm a}+\frac{\rm i}{T}\Theta\partial_{t}\psi_{\alpha}^{\rm r}\,,

(7b)

where
T
is the background temperature.
The dynamical KMS symmetry is motivated by the KMS condition satisfied by a thermal system and can be viewed as a definition of local thermal equilibrium. Generically, one can take
\Theta
that includes
\cal T
, i.e.,
\Theta
can be
\cal T
itself, or any combination of
{\cal C},{\cal P}
with
{\cal T}

Crossley:2017
, depending on the systems of interest.
The presence of background magnetic field and vector charge will break the symmetries under
{\mathcal{T}}
and
{\mathcal{C}}
, respectively, so we shall take
\Theta=\mathcal{C}\mathcal{P}\mathcal{T}
in this work.

4.

Unitarity:
The unitarity of the underlying system requires that (suppressing
\alpha
and
\mu
indices)

\displaystyle\,I_{\rm eff}[\psi^{\rm r},A^{\rm r};\psi^{\rm a}=0,A^{\rm a}=0]=0\,,

(8)

\displaystyle\,I^{*}_{{\rm eff}}[\psi^{\rm r},A^{\rm r};\psi^{\rm a},A^{\rm a}]=-I_{\rm eff}[\psi^{\rm r},A^{\rm r};-\psi^{\rm a},-A^{\rm a}]\,.

(9)

To consider the low-energy regime of the system, we also perform a derivative expansion based on the basic philosophy of EFT. For definiteness, we take the following counting scheme in this paper:
{\cal A}_{0,\alpha}^{\rm a}\sim\partial_{t}{\cal A}_{0,\alpha}^{\rm r}\sim\epsilon^{2}
,
{\cal A}_{i,\alpha}^{\rm a}\sim\partial_{t}{\cal A}_{i,\alpha}^{\rm r}\sim\epsilon
(such that
\partial_{t}\psi_{\alpha}^{\rm a}\sim\epsilon^{2}
,
{\bm{\nabla}}\psi_{\alpha}^{\rm a}\sim\epsilon
), and
\psi_{\alpha}^{\rm a}\sim\partial_{t}\psi_{\alpha}^{\rm r}\sim\epsilon^{0}
, where
\epsilon
is a small expansion parameter.

Now, we are ready to write down the non-equilibrium action
I_{{\rm eff}}
explicitly.
Because of eq. (
8
),
{\cal L}
has to contain at least one power of
\rm a
-field.
We shall study
{\cal L}
up to quadratic order in
\rm a
-field.
More explicitly, we consider the Lagrangian density
{\cal L}
, which is related to
I_{\rm eff}
through the standard relation

\displaystyle I_{\rm eff}[\psi^{\rm r},A^{\rm r};\psi^{\rm a},A^{\rm a}]=\int_{x}{{\cal L}}[\psi^{\rm r},A^{\rm r};\psi^{\rm a},A^{\rm a}]\,,

(10)

and divide
{\cal L}
into three parts:

\displaystyle{\cal L}={\cal L}_{\rm inv}+{\cal L}_{\rm damp}+{\cal L}_{\rm anom}\,.

(11)

Here,
{\cal L}_{\rm inv}
corresponds to
{\cal L}
in the limit that both the effects of the axial charge damping and chiral anomaly are absent.
In this case,
n_{\rm A}
is also conserved. Hence,
\mathcal{L}_{\rm inv}
up to
{\cal O}(\epsilon^{2})
should be of the same form as the hydrodynamic effective action with two conserved charges as derived in ref.
Crossley:2015
(see also ref.
Chen-Lin:2018kfl
):

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}_{\rm inv}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha}n_{\alpha}{\cal A}_{0,\alpha}^{{\rm a}}-\sum_{i,j,\alpha,\beta}\sigma^{ij}_{\alpha\beta}\left({\cal A}^{\rm a}_{i,\alpha}\partial_{t}{\cal A}^{\rm r}_{j,\beta}-{\rm i}T{\cal A}^{\rm a}_{i,\alpha}{\cal A}^{\rm a}_{j,\beta}\right)\,,

(12)

where
\sigma^{ij}_{\alpha\beta}
is the conductivity matrix which is symmetric with respect to
(i,j)
and
(\alpha,\beta)
.
Because of the shift symmetries,

{\cal L}_{\rm inv}
is independent of
\mathcal{A}^{{\rm r}}_{i,\alpha}
.

Turning to
{\cal L}_{\rm anom}
, which describes the effects of the chiral anomaly, we explicitly have

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}_{\rm anom}

\displaystyle=C\psi_{\rm A}^{\rm a}{\bm{E}}\cdot{\bm{B}}+C\mu_{{\rm V}}{\bm{B}}\cdot{\bm{\nabla}}\psi_{\rm A}^{\rm a}+C\mu_{{\rm A}}{\bm{B}}\cdot\bm{{\cal A}}_{{\rm V}}^{\rm a}\,,

(13)

where
C=1/(2\pi^{2})
denotes the anomaly coefficient and the electric and magnetic fields are defined by
{\bm{E}}={\bm{\nabla}}A^{\rm r}_{0,{\rm V}}-\partial_{t}\bm{A}^{\rm r}_{\rm V}
and
{\bm{B}}={\bm{\nabla}}\times\bm{A}^{\rm r}_{\rm V}
. To simplify the expression, we shall consider the cases in the absence of the axial gauge fields here and from now on. The first term in eq. (
13
) leads to the anomaly contribution to the non-conservation of the axial current (see eq. (
18
) below).
The second and the third terms correspond to the chiral separation effect (CSE)
Son:2004tq
;
Metlitski:2005pr
and CME, respectively.
In appendix
A
, we present the derivation of eq. (
13
) by generalizing the formulation for a single chiral charge in ref.
Glorioso:2017lcn
.

Finally, we
postulate
to use

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}_{\rm damp}

\displaystyle=-\gamma\left[\mu_{{\rm A}}\psi_{\rm A}^{\rm a}-{\rm i}T(\psi_{\rm A}^{\rm a})^{2}\right]\,,

(14)

to describe the axial charge relaxation. Here,
\gamma
denotes the axial damping coefficient, which is assumed to be
{\cal O}(\epsilon^{2})
so that
\mathcal{L}_{\rm damp}
contributes to the same order as the other terms in eq. (
11
).
Equation (
14
) satisfies all requirements as listed above.

We here point out that the equations of motion from
I_{\rm eff}
is equivalent to the (non-)conservation equations for the currents.
For the vector charge density, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\delta I_{\rm eff}}{\delta\psi^{\rm a}_{\rm V}}=0\leftrightarrow\partial_{\mu}J^{\mu,{\rm r}}=0\,,\quad J^{\mu,{\rm r}}=\frac{\delta I_{\rm eff}}{\delta A^{{\rm a}}_{\mu,{\rm V}}}\,,

(15)

since
I_{\rm eff}
only depends on the combination
A^{\rm a}_{\mu,{\rm V}}+\partial_{\mu}\psi^{\rm a}_{\rm V}
but not on
A^{\rm a}_{\mu,{\rm V}}
and
\psi^{\rm a}_{\rm V}
individually.
For later purpose, we obtain the expressions for
\bm{J}^{\rm r}
and
\bm{J}^{\rm a}
by differentiating (
11
) with respect to
\bm{A}^{\rm a}
and
\bm{A}^{\rm r}
, respectively:

\displaystyle{\bm{J}}^{\rm r}

\displaystyle=-\sigma_{\rm V}{\bm{\nabla}}\mu_{\rm V}+2{\rm i}T\sigma_{\rm V}{\bm{\nabla}}\psi_{\rm V}+C\mu_{\rm A}\bm{B}\,,

(16)

\displaystyle{\bm{J}}^{\rm a}

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}\left(\sigma_{\rm V}{\bm{\nabla}}\psi_{\rm V}+C\psi_{\rm A}\bm{B}\right)+C{\bm{\nabla}}\times\left(\mu_{\rm A}{\bm{\nabla}}\psi_{{\rm V}}+\mu_{{\rm V}}{\bm{\nabla}}\psi_{\rm A}+{\bm{E}}\psi_{A}\right)\,.

(17)

We can also define the axial current
J^{\mu}_{\rm A}
through the variation of
I_{\rm eff}
. In that case, we find that the equation of motion for
\psi^{\rm a}_{\rm A}
is nothing but the non-conservation equation for the axial current due to the chiral anomaly and axial charge damping,

\displaystyle\partial_{\mu}J^{\mu,{\rm r}}_{\rm A}=C{{\bm{E}}}\cdot{{\bm{B}}}-\gamma\mu_{\rm A}+2{\rm i}\gamma T\psi^{{\rm a}}_{\rm A}\,.

(18)

In summary, we shall use the following effective action up to
{\cal O}(\epsilon^{2})
for the subsequent analysis for systems with a background magnetic field
{\bm{B}}
based on eqs. (
12
), (
13
), and (
14
):

\displaystyle{\cal L}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha}(\partial_{t}\psi_{\alpha})n_{\alpha}-\sum_{\alpha}\sigma_{\alpha}\left[({\bm{\nabla}}\psi_{\alpha})\cdot{\bm{\nabla}}\mu_{\alpha}-{\rm i}T({\bm{\nabla}}\psi_{\alpha})^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\quad+C\mu_{\rm A}\bm{B}\cdot{\bm{\nabla}}\psi_{\rm V}+C\mu_{\rm V}\bm{B}\cdot{\bm{\nabla}}\psi_{\rm A}-\gamma\left[\psi_{\rm A}\mu_{\rm A}-{\rm i}T(\psi_{\rm A})^{2}\right]\,.

(19)

For notational brevity, here and hereafter, we suppress the
{\rm a}
-index for
\psi^{\rm a}_{\alpha}
. Note that
{\bm{B}}={\cal O}(\epsilon)
in our counting scheme above, and we may ignore the
{\bm{B}}
-dependence of
\sigma_{\alpha}
and
\gamma
at the level of this effective Lagrangian.

In this work, our goal is to showcase the non-trivial interplay among the axial charge density relaxation, CME, and fluctuations in the simplest possible settings.
In eq. (
2.1
), we have assumed that at the tree level,
\sigma^{ij}_{\alpha\beta}=\sigma_{\alpha}\,\delta^{ij}\delta_{\alpha\beta}
, which is sufficient for the present illustrative purpose.
In the same spirit, we shall use the susceptibility matrix
\chi_{\alpha\beta}\equiv\partial n_{\alpha}/\partial\mu_{\beta}
which is also diagonalized,
\chi_{\alpha\beta}=\chi_{\alpha}\delta_{\alpha\beta}
.

Conversely, one might construct an action of a similar form to eq. (

;

Expansion around thermal equilibrium

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{T}{2}\sum_{\alpha,\beta}\left(g_{\rm V}n_{{\rm A};\alpha\beta}{\bm{v}}_{\rm A}\cdot{\bm{\nabla}}\psi_{\rm V}+g_{\rm A}n_{{\rm V};\alpha\beta}{\bm{v}}_{\rm V}\cdot{\bm{\nabla}}\psi_{\rm A}\right)\lambda_{\alpha}\lambda_{\beta}

\displaystyle\quad-Tg_{\rm A}r\sum_{\alpha}\gamma_{;\alpha}\lambda_{\alpha}\psi_{\rm A}\left(\lambda_{\rm A}-{\rm i}\psi_{\rm A}\right)+\frac{Tg_{\rm A}r}{2}\sum_{\alpha,\beta}n_{{\rm A};\alpha\beta}\psi_{\rm A}\lambda_{\alpha}\lambda_{\beta}\,,

with

\displaystyle D_{\alpha}\equiv\frac{\sigma_{\alpha}}{\chi_{\alpha}}\,,\quad{\bm{v}}\equiv\frac{C{\bm{B}}}{\sqrt{\chi_{\rm V}\chi_{\rm A}}}\,,\quad{\bm{v}}_{\alpha}\equiv\frac{C{\bm{B}}}{\chi_{\alpha}}\,,\quad r\equiv\frac{\gamma}{\chi_{\rm A}}\,.

Here,

and

correspond to the bare axial relation rate and the velocity of the chiral magnetic wave (CMW)

;
Newman:2005hd

The first and the third lines in eq. (

) arise from the non-linearity due to the charge diffusion and axial charge relaxation, respectively.
Since

is generically a non-linear function of
n_{{\rm V}}
and

, the CME/CSE induce non-linear couplings among fluctuating fields, as is shown in the second line of eq. (
2.2

In the cubic action (

),
the terms involving two a-fields correspond to the multiplicative noise contribution, which is necessary to ensure the KMS invariance.

3
Conductivity at finite axial relaxation rate

3.1
Conductivity

From the symmetrized correlator of the vector current
J^{\mu}
,
{\cal C}^{\mu\nu}\sim\langle J^{\mu}J^{\nu}\rangle
, we can determine the (AC) conductivity tensor through the standard Kubo formula

\displaystyle\sigma^{ij}(\omega)=\lim_{{\bm{k}}\rightarrow{\bm{0}}}\frac{1}{2T}\mathcal{C}^{ij}(K)\,.

(48)

Alternatively, we can extract
\sigma^{ij}
from the retarded correlator (see eq. (
99
) in section
4
).
The conductivity tensor in the presence of the external magnetic field can be decomposed as

\displaystyle\sigma^{ij}=\sigma_{\parallel}\hat{B}^{i}\hat{B}^{j}+\sigma_{\perp}(\delta^{ij}-\hat{B}^{i}\hat{B}^{j})\,,

(49)

where
\sigma_{\parallel}
and
\sigma_{\perp}
are the longitudinal and transverse conductivity, respectively. In this work, we will not consider the Hall conductivity.
From the Ward-Takahashi identity,
we also have

\displaystyle\lim_{{\bm{k}}\rightarrow{\bm{0}}}\frac{1}{2T}\frac{\omega^{2}}{{\bm{k}}^{2}}\mathcal{{\cal C}}^{00}(K)=\lim_{{\bm{k}}\rightarrow{\bm{0}}}\frac{1}{2T}\hat{k}^{i}\hat{k}^{j}\mathcal{C}^{ij}(K)=\sigma_{\perp}(\omega)+[\sigma_{\parallel}(\omega)-\sigma_{\perp}(\omega)](\hat{\bm{k}}\cdot\hat{\bm{B}})^{2}\,,

(50)

which allows us to extract
\sigma_{\parallel}
and
\sigma_{\perp}
from the small
k
behavior of
{\cal C}^{00}
.

In what follows, we determine
{\cal C}^{00}
and hence
\sigma^{ij}(\omega)
from the relation,

\displaystyle{\cal C}^{00}=T\chi_{\rm V}{\cal G}_{\rm VV}^{\rm rr}\,,

(51)

which follows from the definitions of the rescaled fields, eq. (
20
). At tree level,
an explicit calculation using eq. (
39
) yields

\displaystyle\lim_{\bm{k}\to{\bm{0}}}\frac{\omega^{2}}{2{\bm{k}}^{2}}G^{\rm rr}_{\rm VV}(K)=D_{\rm V}+\frac{{\bm{v}}^{2}r}{\omega^{2}+r^{2}}\,.

(52)

It then follows from eqs. (
50
) and (
49
) that

\displaystyle(\sigma_{\parallel})_{{\rm tree}}(\omega)=(\sigma_{\rm V})_{0}+\frac{C^{2}{\bm{B}}^{2}r}{\chi_{\rm A}(r^{2}+\omega^{2})}\,,\quad(\sigma_{\perp})_{{\rm tree}}(\omega)=(\sigma_{\rm V})_{0}\,,

(53)

which reproduces the well-known CME-induced negative magnetoresistance
Son:2012bg
. However, the tree level result (
53
) does not take into account the fluctuation effects. We shall study the one-loop corrections to
\sigma^{ij}
by computing
{\cal G}^{\rm rr}_{\rm VV}
dressed by the self-energies.

4
Conductivity at zero relaxation rate

To complement the analysis in the previous section, we shall work on the limit that the axial relaxation rate
\gamma
vanishes in this section. By sending

in the tree-level result (
53

Instead of extracting conductivity tensor
\sigma^{ij}
from relevant self-energies as was done in the previous section and in appendix

, in this section, we will employ a complementary approach which determines
\sigma^{ij}
from the retarded correlator of the vector current:

\displaystyle\sigma^{ij}(\omega)=\lim_{\bm{k}\to{\bm{0}}}\frac{1}{\omega}{\rm Im}\,{{\cal C}}^{ij}_{\rm R}(K)\,.

(99)

Here, we have used the fluctuation-dissipation relation

\displaystyle{\cal C}^{ij}(K)=\frac{2T}{\omega}{\rm Im}\,{\cal C}^{ij}_{\rm R}(K)\,.

(100)

Let us begin with the retarded correlator expressed in terms of the vector currents
{\bm{J}}^{{\rm r}}
and
{\bm{J}}^{{\rm a}}
:

\displaystyle{\cal C}^{ij}_{\rm R}(K)={\rm i}\langle J^{i,{\rm r}}(K)J^{j,{\rm a}}(-K)\rangle\,.

(101)

Here and hereafter,
\langle\ldots\rangle
denotes the average weighted by the path-integral (
2
) with
\gamma=0
. At one-loop order,
{\cal C}^{ij}_{\rm R}
is given by the correlation of
{\bm{J}}^{\rm r}
and
{\bm{J}}^{\rm a}
expanded to the quadratic order in the fluctuating fields. In details,
we use the expressions given by (
16
) and (
17
) with
{\bm{E}}={\bm{0}}
and have

\displaystyle\left[C^{ij}_{\rm R}(K)\right]_{{\rm loop}}

\displaystyle={\rm i}\langle(J^{i,{\rm r}})_{1}(K)\,(J^{j,{\rm a}})_{2}(-K)\rangle+{\rm i}\langle(J^{i,{\rm r}})_{2}(K)\,(J^{j,{\rm a}})_{1}(-K)\rangle+{\rm i}\langle(J^{i,{\rm r}})_{2}(K)\,(J^{j,{\rm a}})_{2}(-K)\rangle\,,

(102)

where

\displaystyle({\bm{J}}^{{\rm r}})_{1}

\displaystyle=C\bm{B}(\mu_{\rm A})_{1}+{\cal O}(\bm{\nabla})\,,

(103a)

\displaystyle({\bm{J}}^{{\rm r}})_{2}

\displaystyle=C(\mu_{\rm A})_{2}\bm{B}-(\sigma_{\rm V})_{1}{\bm{\nabla}}(\mu_{\rm V})_{1}+2{\rm i}T(\sigma_{\rm A})_{1}{\bm{\nabla}}\psi_{\rm V}\,,

(103b)

\displaystyle({\bm{J}}^{{\rm a}})_{1}

\displaystyle=C\partial_{t}\psi_{\rm A}\bm{B}+{\cal O}(\bm{\nabla})\,,

(103c)

\displaystyle({\bm{J}}^{{\rm a}})_{2}

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}\left[(\sigma_{\rm V})_{1}{\bm{\nabla}}\psi_{\rm V}\right]+{\cal O}(\bm{\nabla})\,.

(103d)

\displaystyle\lim_{\bm{k}\to{\bm{0}}}\langle(J^{i,{\rm r}})_{1}(K)(J^{j,{\rm a}})_{2}(-K)\rangle=0\,,\quad\lim_{\bm{k}\to{\bm{0}}}\langle(J^{i,{\rm r}})_{2}(K)(J^{j,{\rm a}})_{1}(-K)\rangle=0\,.

\displaystyle\langle\lambda_{\alpha}(x)({\bm{J}}^{{\rm a}})_{2}(x^{\prime})\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{\beta}\left(\int_{y}G^{\rm rr}_{\alpha\beta}(x,y)\left<\frac{\delta I_{{\rm int}}}{\delta\lambda_{\beta}(y)}({\bm{J}}^{{\rm a}})_{2}(x^{\prime})\right>_{0}+G^{\rm ra}_{\alpha\beta}(x,y)\left<\frac{\delta I_{{\rm int}}}{\delta\psi_{\beta}(y)}({\bm{J}}^{{\rm a}})_{2}(x^{\prime})\right>_{0}\right)

(105)

Here,

and
\left<...\right>_{0}
denotes the average weighted by the Gaussian part of the effective action, as this suffices to the present one-loop calculations.
In eq. (

\displaystyle\left<\frac{\delta I_{{\rm int}}}{\delta\lambda_{\beta}(y)}({\bm{J}}^{{\rm a}})_{2}(x^{\prime})\right>_{0}=0\,,

which can be shown by causality (see ref.

for a general discussion) and the fact that both

and

On the other hand, in the absence of

5
Discussion

5.1
Positive magnetoresistance

We presented in this paper the diagrammatic calculation of the modifications of conductivity (the inverse of resistance) for a chiral medium with the chiral magnetic effect (CME) based on the non-equilibrium effective field theory (EFT) approach.
We consider a generic vector charge density and axial charge density as slow variables and study the intertwined effects from their fluctuations and CME.
For the first time, we obtain the CME-related modifications to the conductivity tensors due to fluctuations for systems with finite and vanishing axial relaxation rate
r
, as summarized in eq. (
96
) and eq. (
121
), respectively.

Contrary to the common statement that the CME leads to a negative magnetoresistance, we find that CME-related effects due to fluctuations give rise to a
positive
magnetoresistance.
Whether the
net
magnetoresistance is positive or negative is determined by the competition between the two terms inside
[...]
in eq. (
96
).
Note, to apply our one-loop results, we should require the fluctuation contribution to be much smaller than the tree-level expression, which includes both the first term and second term on the right-hand side of eq. (
96
) (c.f. eq. (
53
)).
Since we are working in the weak
{\bm{B}}
limit, the tree-level contribution is dominated by
(\sigma)_{0}
, which is indeed much larger than the one-loop correction, i.e., the third term on the right-hand side of eq. (
96
).
However, this does not necessarily mean that the third term has to be smaller than the second term. Therefore, the net CME-related contribution can in principle be dominated by the fluctuations effects. We have made a number of simplifications in our analysis, but we hope this qualitative feature might have some implication to real physical systems.
Interestingly, a positive magnetoresistance might have already been seen in Weyl semimetals when the magnetic field is small (e.g., ref.
Li:2014bha
).

It is truly striking that the CME contributes to the conductivity even in the limit
r=0
(see eq. (
121
)).
This is in a marked difference from the result, which does not account for fluctuations that the CME contribution vanishes in this limit.
Moreover, the fluctuation modifies both longitudinal and transverse conductivities, while at finite
r
, only longitudinal conductivity receives the correction from the CME.

The parametric behavior of the ratio of fluctuation effects to the tree-level contribution is very instructive.
From eqs. (
96
) and (
121
), we schematically have

\displaystyle\frac{\rm(Hydrodynamic\ fluctuation)}{\rm(Bare\ contribution)}\sim(Tu)^{2}g^{2}q_{*}^{3}\,,

(122)

where
q_{*}
is characteristic momentum of fluctuating modes. For the case with
r\neq 0
,
q_{*}=\sqrt{r/D}
, resulting from the competition between the diffusion of vector charge and the damping of the axial charge, whereas for
r=0
,
q_{*}=|{\bm{v}}|/D
originating from the competition between diffusion and propagation of the chiral magnetic wave (CMW).
Equation (
122
) indicates that the relative importance of fluctuation corrections is determined by two factors, a) the strength of non-linearity
(Tu)^{2}
and b) the ratio between the phase space volume of the long-wavelength fluctuating modes,
q^{3}_{*}
, and that of the whole system,
g^{-2}=\chi T
.

Let us end this section by comparing the role the CME played in two cases under study.
For
r\neq 0
, the CME gives rise to non-linear couplings among charge fluctuations but plays no role in determining the IR scale.
In contrast, for
r=0
and at vanishing background vector charge, the non-linearity relevant to the finite corrections to the conductivity solely comes from the density-dependence of diffusive constant and conductivity but does not rely on the CME.
However, the competition between CMW propagation and charge diffusion leads to the emergent IR scale.
Due to the differences explained above,
C|{\bm{B}}|
dependence of
\Delta\sigma
is different. The correction to the conductivity scales as
(C{\bm{B}})^{2}
in the former case and scales as
(C|{\bm{B}}|)^{3}
in the latter case.

5.2
Remarks on hydrodynamic fluctuations

Another motivation of the present study is to advance our understanding of general aspects of hydrodynamic fluctuations.
Here, we employ the recently developed non-equilibrium EFT for the present studies.
Our exercise here demonstrates this EFT approach allows us to use powerful (and familiar) field theory techniques to analyze hydrodynamic fluctuations. By construction, the EFT automatically takes into account the constraints from symmetries.
For example, in appendix
D
, we show explicitly how fluctuations-dissipation theorem and the Ward-Takahashi identity are satisfied at one-loop order.
In the traditional method, special care is needed to ensure those relations (see the recent work
Chao:2020kcf
for the former).

Remarkably, the fluctuation contribution to the conductivity is finite due to the CME. This should be contrasted with the case of an ordinary fluid that fluctuation corrections to transport coefficients (at zero frequency limit) are typically zero.
Such difference is related to the emergent IR momentum scale behavior in the loop integration,
q_{*}
.
Generically, the corrections to transport coefficients should scale with
q_{*}
to an appropriate (and positive) power for
d=3
.
For a normal fluid,
q_{*}\sim\sqrt{\omega}
, giving rise to the renowned long-time tail phenomena
POMEAU197563
;
Kovtun:2003vj
;
Kovtun:2012rj
, and consequently vanishes in the limit
\omega\rightarrow 0
(see also refs
Akamatsu:2016llw
;
Stephanov:2017ghc
;
Jain:2020hcu
for related discussion).
Therefore, to obtain finite corrections to transport coefficients in this limit, there must be additional soft scales.
Such scales are generated by the magnetic field and/or axial relaxation rate in the present study.
Given the generality of the discussion above, we anticipate that the CME and hydrodynamic fluctuations together might contribute to other transports coefficients.
A natural follow-up would be to include fluctuations from energy and momentum densities and study the effects on shear and bulk viscosities.
We leave these and other extensions of this work to future studies.

Appendix A
Derivation of

In this section, we shall present the derivation of
I_{{\rm anom}}
in eq. (
13
) used in the main text following the method of ref.
Glorioso:2017lcn
.
We shall see the anomaly relation combined with KMS invariance uniquely fix the form of CME.
This should be contrasted with the derivation of CME in hydrodynamics from the second law of thermodynamics
Son:2009tf
;
Hattori:2017usa
.

Let us first consider the low-energy effective action describing the slow mode associated with a single chiral charge.
We consider the action divided into two parts,

\displaystyle I=I_{{\rm inv}}+I_{{\rm anom}}\,,

(123)

where
I_{{\rm inv}}
is identical to the hydrodynamic action of a conserved charge (see eq. (
12
)). We shall focus on the anomaly-related action
I_{{\rm anom}}
from now on.

Because of the anomaly,
the consistent
{\rm r}
-current,

\displaystyle J^{\mu}=\frac{\delta I}{\delta A^{\rm a}_{\mu}}\,

(124)

obeys the anomaly equation

\displaystyle\partial_{\mu}J^{\mu}=-\frac{\kappa C}{24}F_{\mu\nu}\tilde{F}^{{\mu\nu}}\,,

(125)

where
\kappa=\pm 1
correspond to the right-handed and left-handed charge, respectively.
In this appendix, we shall keep the index “
{\rm a}
” but suppress the index “
{\rm r}
.”
To obtain eq. (
125
),
we use the consistent anomaly equation on the two segments of Schwinger-Keldysh contour labeled by “1,2”:

\displaystyle\partial_{\mu}J^{\mu}_{1}=-\frac{\kappa C}{24}F_{\mu\nu,1}\tilde{F}_{1}^{\mu\nu}\,,\quad\partial_{\mu}J^{\mu}_{2}=-\frac{\kappa C}{24}F_{\mu\nu,2}\tilde{F}_{2}^{\mu\nu}\,,

(126)

to compute
\partial_{\mu}J^{\mu}=\partial_{\mu}\left(J^{\mu}_{1}+J^{\mu}_{2}\right)
.
We have not included a contribution quadratic in
\rm a
-field on the right-hand side of eq. (
125
) since such contribution should arise from the action involving three powers of
\rm a
-field.
Similar to the discussion presented in the main text, the equation of motion for
\psi^{\rm a}
,

\displaystyle\frac{\delta I}{\delta\psi^{\rm a}}=0\,,

(127)

should be equivalent to the consistent anomaly equation (
125
).
Therefore, the anomaly part of the action takes the form

\displaystyle I_{{\rm anom}}

\displaystyle=\int_{x}\left(-\frac{\kappa C}{24}\psi^{\rm a}F_{\mu\nu}\tilde{F}^{\mu\nu}+J_{\rm cons}^{\mu}\mathcal{A}^{\rm a}_{\mu}\right)\,.

(128)

Here,
{\cal A}^{\rm a}_{\mu}=A^{\rm a}_{\mu}+\partial_{\mu}\psi^{\rm a}
as given in eq. (
4
) and
J_{\rm cons}
(
J_{{\rm cov}}
) denotes the contribution to the consistent (covariant) current only from
I_{{\rm anom}}
, distinguished with the current
J
from the total action
I
. The difference between
J_{\rm cons}
and
J_{{\rm cov}}
defines the Chern-Simons (CS) current as

\displaystyle J_{{\rm cov}}=J_{{\rm cons}}+J_{{\rm CS}}\,,

(129)

where

\displaystyle J^{\mu}_{{\rm CS}}=-\frac{\kappa C}{6}\tilde{F}^{\mu\nu}\,A_{\nu}\,.

(130)

Similar to our previous treatment of the consistent anomaly equation (
125
),
we have dropped terms quadratic in a-fields in eq. (
130
).
Substituting eqs. (
130
) and (
129
) into eq. (
128
),
we now have

\displaystyle I_{{\rm anom}}=\int_{x}\,\left(-\frac{C\kappa}{24}\psi^{\rm a}F_{\mu\nu}\tilde{F}^{\mu\nu}+\frac{\kappa C}{6}\tilde{F}^{\mu\nu}A_{\nu}{\cal A}^{\rm a}_{\mu}+J_{{\rm cov}}^{\mu}{\cal A}^{\rm a}_{\mu}\right)\,.

(131)

We shall consider the following form for
J^{\mu}_{\rm cov}
:

\displaystyle J^{\mu}_{\rm cov}=\xi(\mu)B^{\mu}\,,

(132)

where
\xi(\mu)
is a function of
\mu
and we have defined
E^{\mu}=F^{\mu\nu}\ell_{\nu}
and
B^{\mu}=\tilde{F}^{\mu\nu}\ell_{\nu}
with
\ell^{\mu}=(1,{\bm{0}})
denoting the frame of the medium.
We shall also use below that
\mu=\ell\cdot{\cal A}
(see eq. (
5
)).
Since
I_{{\rm anom}}
is invariant under the KMS transformations (
7a
) and (
7b
),

\displaystyle{\cal A}^{\rm r}_{\mu}

\displaystyle\to\Theta{\cal A}^{\rm r}_{\mu}\,,\quad\psi^{\rm r}\to\Theta\psi^{\rm r}\,,

\displaystyle{\cal A}^{\rm a}_{\mu}

\displaystyle\to\Theta{\cal A}^{\rm a}_{\mu}+\frac{\rm i}{T}\Theta\left(\ell^{\alpha}\partial_{\alpha}{\cal A}_{\mu}\right)

\displaystyle=\Theta{\cal A}^{\rm a}_{\mu}+\frac{\rm i}{T}\Theta\ell^{\alpha}\left(F_{\alpha\mu}+\partial_{\mu}{\cal A}_{\alpha}\right)=\Theta{\cal A}^{\rm a}_{\mu}+\frac{\rm i}{T}\Theta\left(-E_{\mu}+\partial_{\mu}\mu\right)\,,

\displaystyle\psi^{\rm a}

\displaystyle\to\Theta\psi^{\rm a}+\frac{\rm i}{T}\Theta\left(\ell^{\alpha}\partial_{\alpha}\psi\right)\,.

(133)

The variance of the first two terms in eq. (
131
) under the KMS transformation should precisely cancel that from the last term.
This requirement uniquely fixes
\xi(\mu)
, as we shall explain below.

Indeed, for
\Theta={\cal CPT}
, we have

\displaystyle\delta_{{\rm KMS}}\,\int_{x}\,\psi^{\rm a}F_{\mu\nu}\tilde{F}^{\mu\nu}=-\frac{\rm i}{T}\,\int_{x}\,(\ell^{\alpha}\partial_{\alpha}\psi)F_{\mu\nu}\tilde{F}^{\mu\nu}\,,

(134)

where we have used
\Theta\mathcal{A}^{\mu}(x)=-\mathcal{A}^{\mu}(-x)
,
\Theta\tilde{F}^{\mu\nu}(x)=\tilde{F}^{\mu\nu}(-x)
, and
\Theta\ell^{\mu}(x)=\ell^{\mu}(-x)
.
Similarly,

\displaystyle\,\delta_{{\rm KMS}}\int_{x}\,\tilde{F}^{\mu\nu}A_{\nu}{\cal A}^{\rm a}_{\mu}=-\frac{\rm i}{T}\int_{x}\tilde{F}^{\mu\nu}A_{\nu}(-E_{\mu}+\partial_{\mu}\mu)=\frac{\rm i}{T}\int_{x}\left[\frac{1}{4}(\ell\cdot A)+\frac{\mu}{2}\right]F_{\mu\nu}\tilde{F}^{\mu\nu}\,,

(135)

where we have used the identity

\displaystyle\tilde{F}^{\mu\nu}A_{\nu}E_{\mu}=\left(\ell^{\mu}B^{\nu}-\ell^{\nu}B^{\mu}+\epsilon^{\mu\nu\alpha\beta}\ell_{\alpha}E_{\beta}\right)\,A_{\nu}E_{\mu}=-B\cdot E(\ell\cdot A)=\frac{1}{4}F_{\mu\nu}\tilde{F}^{\mu\nu}(\ell\cdot A)\,.

(136)

Putting all pieces together, we find

\displaystyle\,\delta_{{\rm KMS}}\int_{x}\,\left(-\frac{C\kappa}{24}\psi^{\rm a}F_{\mu\nu}\tilde{F}^{\mu\nu}+\frac{\kappa C}{6}\tilde{F}^{\mu\nu}A_{\nu}{\cal A}^{\rm a}_{\mu}\right)=\frac{{\rm i}\kappa C}{6T}\int_{x}\left[\frac{1}{4}(\ell\cdot\partial)\psi^{\rm a}+\frac{1}{4}(\ell\cdot A)+\frac{\mu}{2}\right]F_{\mu\nu}\tilde{F}^{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=\frac{{\rm i}\kappa C}{8T}\int_{x}\mu F_{\mu\nu}\tilde{F}^{\mu\nu}\,.

(137)

On the other hand,

\displaystyle\delta_{{\rm KMS}}\,\int_{x}J^{\mu}_{\rm cov}{\cal A}^{\rm a}_{\mu}=\frac{\rm i}{T}\int_{x}\xi(-\mu)B^{\mu}\left(-E_{\mu}+\partial_{\mu}\mu\right)=\frac{\rm i}{4T}\int_{x}\xi(-\mu)F_{\mu\nu}\tilde{F}^{\mu\nu}\,,

(138)

where we used the fact that
\xi(-\mu)B^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}\mu
is a total derivative.
For eq. (
A
) to cancel eq. (
138
), we have the appropriate form for the CME:

\displaystyle\xi(\mu)=\frac{\kappa C}{2}\mu\,,\quad J^{\mu}_{\rm cov}=\frac{\kappa C}{2}\mu B^{\mu}\,.

(139)

Now, we generalize the discussion above to the system with both axial and vector charges.
From the consistent anomaly relation,

\displaystyle\partial_{\mu}J^{\mu}_{{\rm V}}

\displaystyle=0\,,

(140)

\displaystyle\partial_{\mu}J^{\mu}_{{\rm A}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{C}{12}\left(3F_{\mu\nu,{\rm V}}\tilde{F}^{\mu\nu}_{\rm V}+F_{\mu\nu,{\rm A}}\tilde{F}^{\mu\nu}_{\rm A}\right)\,.

(141)

Thus, we have, in analogous to eq. (
128
), the expression for
I_{{\rm anom}}
:

\displaystyle I_{\rm anom}

\displaystyle=\int_{x}\left[-\frac{C}{12}\psi_{\rm A}^{\rm a}\left(3F_{\mu\nu,{\rm V}}\tilde{F}^{\mu\nu}_{\rm V}+F_{\mu\nu,{\rm A}}\tilde{F}^{\mu\nu}_{\rm A}\right)\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\quad\left.+\left(J^{\mu}_{\rm cov,{\rm V}}+C\tilde{F}^{\mu\nu}_{\rm V}A_{\nu,{\rm A}}\right)\mathcal{A}^{\rm a}_{\mu,{\rm V}}+\left(J^{\mu}_{\rm cov,{\rm A}}+\frac{C}{3}\tilde{F}^{\mu\nu}_{\rm A}A_{\nu,{\rm A}}\right)\mathcal{A}^{\rm a}_{\mu,{\rm A}}\right]\,.

(142)

By imposing the KMS symmetry to eq. (
A
), we find

\displaystyle J^{\mu}_{\rm cov,V}=C\left(\mu_{{\rm A}}B^{\mu}_{\rm V}+\mu_{{\rm V}}B^{\mu}_{\rm A}\right)\,,\quad J^{\mu}_{\rm cov,A}=C\left(\mu_{{\rm V}}B^{\mu}_{\rm V}+\mu_{{\rm A}}B^{\mu}_{\rm A}\right)\,.

(143)

Substituting eq. (
143
) into eq. (
A
) leads to the desired expression for
I_{{\rm anom}}
, which reduces to eq. (
13
) when the external axial gauge field is absent.

(177)

From eqs. (
41
) and (
40
), it is straightforward to show that

\displaystyle{\cal F}^{ij,\rm rr}(K)={\rm i}{\cal F}^{ij,{\rm ra}}(K)+{\rm i}{\cal F}^{ij,{\rm ar}}(K)\,,\quad{\cal F}^{ij,{\rm ra}}(K)=-({\cal F}^{ij,{\rm ar}})^{*}(K)\,.

(178)

We therefore have

\displaystyle\left[{{\cal C}}^{ij,{\rm dif}}(K)\right]_{\rm loop}=(TD{\tilde{u}}_{\sigma})^{2}\left[-{\rm i}{\cal F}^{ij,{\rm ra}}(K)-{\rm i}{\cal F}^{ij,{\rm ar}}(K)\right]=2(TD{\tilde{u}}_{\sigma})^{2}\,{\rm Im}\,{\cal F}^{ij,{\rm ra}}(K)\,.

(179)

On the other hand, the one-loop corrections to the retarded correlator is given by

\displaystyle\left[{\cal C}^{ij,{\rm dif}}_{\rm R}(K)\right]_{\rm loop}

\displaystyle={\rm i}\langle(J^{i,{\rm r}})^{\rm dif}_{2}(K)\,(J^{j,{\rm a}})^{\rm dif}_{2}(-K)\rangle

\displaystyle=\omega T(D_{\rm V}\tilde{u}_{\sigma})^{2}\langle\left(\lambda_{\rm A}\partial^{i}(\lambda_{\rm V}-2{\rm i}\psi_{\rm A})\right)(K)\,(\lambda_{\rm A}\partial^{j}\psi_{\rm V})(-K)\rangle

\displaystyle=\omega T(D_{\rm V}\tilde{u}_{\sigma})^{2}\mathcal{F}^{ij,{\rm ra}}(K)\,.

(180)

Comparing eq. (
179
) with eq. (
D
), we immediately verify

\displaystyle\left({{\cal C}}^{ij,{\rm dif}}\right)_{\rm loop}=\frac{2T}{\omega}{\rm Im}\left({\cal C}^{ij,{\rm dif}}_{\rm R}\right)_{\rm loop}\,.

(181)

Note that if we had ignored the multiplicative noise contributions, i.e., the last two terms in eq. (
D
),
we would obtain the wrong relation

\displaystyle\left[{\cal C}^{ij,{\rm dif}}(K)\right]_{{\rm loop}}=(TD{\tilde{u}}_{\sigma})^{2}{\cal F}^{\rm rr}(K)=-\frac{2T}{\omega}{\rm Im}\left[C^{ij,{\rm dif}}_{\rm R}(K)\right]_{{\rm loop}}\,.

(182)

Furthermore, one would also get a wrong relation between
\mathcal{C}^{00}

C
and

:

\displaystyle\lim_{{\bm{k}}\rightarrow{\bm{0}}}\frac{1}{2T}\hat{k}^{i}\hat{k}^{j}\left[{\cal C}^{ij,{\rm dif}}(K)\right]_{\rm loop}=-\lim_{{\bm{k}}\rightarrow{\bm{0}}}\frac{1}{2T}\frac{\omega^{2}}{{\bm{k}}^{2}}\mathcal{C}^{00}(K)\,,

(183)
