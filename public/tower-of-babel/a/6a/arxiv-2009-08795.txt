Point Forces in Elasticity Equation and Their Alternatives in Multi Dimensions
Q. Peng, F.J. Vermolen

Abstract
We consider several mathematical issues regarding models that simulate traction forces exerted by cells. Since the size of cells is much smaller than the size of the domain of computation, one often considers point forces, modelled by Dirac Delta distributions on boundary segments of cells. In the current paper, we treat the forces that are directed normal to the cell boundary and that are directed toward the cell centre. Since it can be shown that there exists no smooth solution, at least not in 𝑯 𝟏 \bm{H^{1}} for solutions to the governing momentum balance equation, we analyse the convergence and quality of approximation. Furthermore, the expected finite element problems that we get necessitate to scrutinize alternative model formulations, such as the use of smoothed Dirac distributions, or the so-called smoothed particle approach as well as the so-called ’hole’ approach where cellular forces are modelled through the use of (natural) boundary conditions. In this paper, we investigate and attempt to quantify the conditions for consistency between the various approaches. This has resulted into error analyses in the L 2 L^{2} -norm of the numerical solution based on Galerkin principles that entail Lagrangian basis functions. The paper also addresses well-posedness in terms of existence and uniqueness. The current analysis has been performed for the linear steady-state (hence neglecting inertia and damping) momentum equations under the assumption of Hooke’s law. Key words: Point forces, Singular solution, Immersed boundary approach, ’Hole’ approach, Smoothed Particle Approach

Introduction

Wound healing is a complicated process of a sequence of cellular events contributing to resurfacing, reconstitution and restoration of the tensile strength of injured skin. Significant damage of dermal tissue often leads to skin contraction. If the contraction of the skin near a joint is large then it may result into a decrease of functionality. If the patient’s daily life is impacted as result of the contraction, then one speaks of a contracture.

In order to improve the patient’s quality of life, one aims at reducing the contractile behavior of the skin. To reduce the severity of the contraction, one needs to know the physiological dynamics and time evolution of the underlying biological mechanisms. According to
[
5
,
7
,
10
]
, the contraction starts developing during the proliferative phase of wound healing. This proliferative phase sets in after the inflammatory phase, in which the immune system is clearing up the debris that resulted from the damage. The proliferative phase usually starts from the second day post-wounding, and commonly lasts two to four weeks. Besides the closure of the epidermis (that is the top layer of skin), the proliferative phase is characterized by ingress of fibroblasts from the surrounding undamaged tissue and differentiation to myofibroblasts, and by the regeneration of collagen by the (myo)fibroblasts. Despite the relatively quick closure of the epidermis, often the restoration of the underlying dermis is still in progress. After closure of the epidermis, the damaged region in the dermis is referred to as a scar instead of a wound. Next to the regeneration of collagen, the (myo)fibroblasts exert contractile forces on their direct surroundings, which will result into contraction of the scar tissue. In human skin, typically volume reductions of 5 - 10% are commonly observed
[
6
]
.

The current manuscript contains an extension of the work in
[
8
]
, which treats a model for the contractile forces exerted by the (myo)fibroblasts. The forces are distinguished into two categories: (1) temporary forces that are exerted as long as the (myo)fibroblasts are actively pulling; and (2) permanent or plastic forces, which are imaginary forces that are introduced to describe the localized plastic deformations of the tissue. This formalism was firstly developed by
Vermolen and Gefen 2015
, and later extended by
Boon et al. 2016
. The formalism is based on the point forces, which are mathematically incorporated by means of linear combinations of Dirac Delta distributions. The irregular nature of Dirac Delta distributions make the solution to the elliptic boundary value problem from the balance of momentum have a singular solution in the sense that for dimensionality higher than one, no formal solutions in the finite-element space
\bm{H^{1}}
exist. Although in classical finite-element strategies, one uses for instance piecewise linear Lagrangian elements, of which the basis functions are in
\bm{H^{1}}
, and therewith one attempts to approximate the solution (which is not in
\bm{H^{1}}
) as well as possibly by a function in
\bm{H^{1}}
.
Bertoluzza et al. 2018
demonstrated the convergence of finite-element solutions by means of piecewise linear Lagrangian elements in multiple dimensions. In our earlier studies
[
11
,
12
]
, we proved the convergence of solutions obtained by regularization of Dirac Delta distributions, the so-called smoothed particle approach and the so-called ’hole’ approach to the solution obtained by Dirac Delta distributions in the one- and two-dimensional cases. 2
Elasticity Equation with Point Sources in Multi Dimensions

Let
\Omega
be a bounded domain in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, then we consider the following balance of momentum where inertial effects have been neglected:

-\nabla\cdot\bm{\sigma}=\bm{f}.

(2.1)

Here
\bm{\sigma}
denotes the stress tensor and
\bm{f}
represents a body force that is exerted within
\Omega
. We consider a linear, homogeneous, isotropic and continuous material; hence, Hooke’s Law is used here for the relation between the stress and strain tensors:

\bm{\sigma}=\frac{E}{1+\nu}\left\{\bm{\epsilon}+\tr(\bm{\epsilon})\left[\frac{\nu}{1-2\nu}\right]\bm{I}\right\},

(2.2)

where
E
is the stiffness of the computational domain,
\nu
is Poisson’s ratio and
\bm{\epsilon}
is the infinitesimal Eulerian strain tensor:

\bm{\epsilon}=\frac{1}{2}\left[\bm{\nabla u}+(\bm{\nabla u})^{T}\right].

(2.3)

Within the domain of computation,
\Omega
, we consider the presence of a biological cell, which occupies the portion
\Omega_{C}
that is completely embedded within
\Omega
(hence
\Omega_{C}
is a strict subset of
\Omega
). The boundary of the cell
\Gamma_{C}
is divided into surface elements. On the centre of each surface element, a point force by means of Dirac Delta distributions, is exerted in the direction of the normal vector that is directed inward into the cell. This results into (see
[
14
]
):

\bm{f}_{t}=\sum_{j=1}^{N_{S}}P(\bm{x}_{j},t)\bm{n}(\bm{x}_{j})\delta(\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{j}(t))\Delta S(\bm{x}_{j}(t)),

(2.4)

where
N_{S}
is the number of surface elements of the cell,
P(\bm{x},t)
is the magnitude of the pulling force exerted at point
\bm{x}
and time
t
per unit of measure (being area in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
or length in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
),
\bm{n}(\bm{x})
is the unit inward pointing normal vector (towards the cell centre) at position
\bm{x}
,
\bm{x}_{j}(t)
is the midpoint on surface element
j
of the cell at time
t
and
\Delta S(\bm{x}_{j})
is the measure of the surface element
j
. In the general model where we use this principle, we consider transient effects due to migration and possible deformation of the cells. However, since we predominantly focus on the mathematical issues in the current manuscript, we will not consider any time-dependencies and hence
t
will be removed from the expressions in the remainder of the paper.

In the n-dimensional case, we are solving the boundary value problems described in Eq (
2.1
), (
2.2
) and (
2.3
). The body force is given in Eq (
2.4
). Therefore, the immersed boundary value problem that we are going to consider is given by

(BVP)\left\{\begin{aligned} -\nabla\cdot\bm{\sigma}(\bm{x})&=\sum_{j=1}^{N_{S}}P(\bm{x}_{j})\bm{n}(\bm{x}_{j})\delta(\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{j})\Delta S(\bm{x}_{j}),&\mbox{in $\Omega$,}\\
\bm{u}&=\bm{0},&\mbox{on $\partial\Omega$.}\end{aligned}\right.

Next to this boundary value problem, we consider the continuous immersed boundary
counterpart, given by

(BVP_{\infty})\left\{\begin{aligned} -\nabla\cdot\bm{\sigma}(\bm{x})&=\int_{\Gamma_{C}}P(\bm{x}^{\prime})\bm{n}(\bm{x}^{\prime})\delta(\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{\prime})dS(\bm{x}^{\prime}),&\mbox{in $\Omega$,}\\
\bm{u}&=\bm{0},&\mbox{on $\partial\Omega$,}\end{aligned}\right.

where we take
N_{s}\rightarrow\infty
. Thus, the body force reads as

\bm{f}_{t}^{\infty}=\int_{\Gamma_{C}}P(\bm{x}^{\prime})\bm{n}(\bm{x}^{\prime})\delta(\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{\prime})dS(\bm{x}^{\prime}).

(2.5)

Due to the irregular nature of the Dirac Delta distributions, the solutions do not exist in
\bm{H^{1}}
. We attempt to approximate the solution by the functions in
\bm{H^{1}}
via the Galerkin form of
(BVP)
and
(BVP_{\infty})
. In this manuscript, piecewise linear Lagrangian basis functions are selected. Further, the convergence of the finite-element solutions using linear Lagrangian elements in general dimensionality has been proved in
[
2
]
.

To construct the Galerkin form, we introduce the bilinear form

a(.,.)

a(\bm{u}_{h},\bm{\phi}_{h})=\int_{\Omega}\bm{\sigma}(\bm{u}_{h}):\nabla\bm{\phi}_{h}d\Omega=\int_{\Omega}\bm{\sigma}(\bm{u}_{h}):\bm{\epsilon}(\bm{\phi}_{h})d\Omega,

(2.6)

where the last step is motivated by symmetry of the stress tensor

\bm{\sigma}
.
Since the solution
\bm{u}
is not in
\bm{H^{1}}(\Omega)
, we consider a subspace of
\bm{H^{1}}(\Omega)
, which is defined as
\bm{V_{h}}(\Omega)=\Span\{\bm{\phi^{1}},\bm{\phi^{2}},\dots,\bm{\phi^{N}}\}

[
13
]
. Here,
\bm{\phi^{i}}
for
i=\{1,2,\dots,N\}
is the linear Lagrangian basis function, which is piecewise smooth and continuous over
\Omega
. Hence, these basis functions are in
\bm{H^{1}}
. Subsequently, the Galerkin form is

(GF)\left\{\begin{aligned} &\text{Find $\bm{u}_{h}\in\bm{V}_{h}(\Omega)$, such that }\\
&a(\bm{u}_{h},\bm{\phi}_{h})=(\bm{\phi}_{h},\bm{f}_{t})=\sum_{j=1}^{N_{S}}P(\bm{x}_{j})\bm{n}(\bm{x}_{j})\bm{\phi}_{h}(\bm{x}_{j})\Delta S(\bm{x}_{j}),\\
&\text{for all $\bm{\phi}_{h}\in\{\bm{\phi^{1}},\bm{\phi^{2}},\dots,\bm{\phi^{N}}\}\subset\bm{V}_{h}(\Omega)$.}\end{aligned}\right.

We further consider the solution to the continuous immerse boundary problem, with the
following Galerkin form:

(GF_{\infty})\left\{\begin{aligned} &\text{Find $\bm{u}_{h}\in\bm{V}_{h}(\Omega)$, such that }\\
&a(\bm{u}_{h},\bm{\phi}_{h})=(\bm{\phi}_{h},\bm{f}^{\infty}_{t})=\int_{\Gamma_{C}}P(\bm{x}^{\prime})\bm{n}(\bm{x}^{\prime})\bm{\phi}_{h}(\bm{x}^{\prime})dS(\bm{x}^{\prime}),\\
&\text{for all $\bm{\phi}_{h}\in\{\bm{\phi^{1}},\bm{\phi^{2}},\dots,\bm{\phi^{N}}\}\subset\bm{V}_{h}(\Omega)$.}\end{aligned}\right.

Before we proceed to claim the existence and the uniqueness of the Galerkin solution in
(GF)
, we state Korn’s Inequality in multiple dimensions:

Lemma 2.1
.

(Korn’s Second Inequality
[
4
]
)

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be an open, bounded and connected domain.
Then there exists a positive constant
K
, such that for any vector-valued function
\bm{u}\in\bm{H^{1}_{0}}(\Omega)
,

\int_{\Omega}||\bm{\epsilon}(\bm{u})||^{2}d\Omega\geqslant K\|\bm{u}\|^{2}_{\bm{H^{1}}(\Omega)}.

We note that Korn’s Second Inequality can be generalised to cases in
which the boundary condition
\bm{u}=\bm{0}
is
imposed only on a non-zero measure part of the boundary. Using Korn’s Second
inequality gives the following lemma:

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be an open, bounded and connected domain.
Then there exists a positive constant
K
, such that for any vector-valued function

a(\bm{u},\bm{u})=\int_{\Omega}\bm{\sigma}(\bm{u}):\bm{\epsilon}(\bm{u})d\Omega\geqslant K||\bm{u}||^{2}_{\bm{H^{1}}(\Omega)}.

Proof.

The lemma directly follows from the definition of the stress tensor,
let
\bm{u}\in\bm{H^{1}_{0}}(\Omega)
:

\displaystyle a(\bm{u},\bm{u})

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\bm{\sigma}(\bm{u}):\bm{\epsilon}(\bm{u})d\Omega=\int_{\Omega}\frac{E}{1+\nu}\left\{\bm{\epsilon}(\bm{u})+\text{tr}(\bm{\epsilon}(\bm{u}))\frac{\nu}{1-2\nu}\bm{I}\right\}:\bm{\epsilon}(\bm{u})d\Omega

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\frac{E}{1+\nu}||\bm{\epsilon}(\bm{u})||^{2}+\frac{E\nu}{(1+\nu)(1-2\nu)}(\tr(\bm{\epsilon}(\bm{u})))^{2}d\Omega

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{E}{1+\nu}K||\bm{u}||^{2}_{\bm{H^{1}}(\Omega)}.

The last step follows from Lemma

f(\bm{x}(s))
. Therefore, we obtain that

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\Delta S_{i}}\bm{f}(\bm{x})dS-\|\bm{x}_{i+1}-\bm{x}_{1}\|\bm{f}(\bm{x}_{i+1/2})\right|

\displaystyle=\displaystyle\frac{1}{24}\|\bm{x}_{i+1}-\bm{x}_{i}\|\cdot|(\bm{x}_{i+1}-\bm{x}_{i})^{T}\bm{H}(\bm{x}(\hat{s}))(\bm{x}_{i+1}-\bm{x}_{i})|

\displaystyle\leqslant\displaystyle\frac{1}{24}\|\bm{x}_{i+1}-\bm{x}_{i}\|\tilde{K}\|\bm{x}_{i+1}-\bm{x}_{i}\|^{2}.

Since
\bm{f}(\bm{x})\in\bm{C^{2}}(\Omega)
, it follows that there exists a
\tilde{K}>0
, such that

|(\bm{x},\bm{H}(\bm{x}))|\leqslant\tilde{K}\|\bm{x}\|^{2}.

Therefore, considering the summation of the boundary elements over
\partial\Omega_{C}
,

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\Delta S_{i}}\bm{f}(\bm{x})dS-\sum_{i=1}^{N_{S}}\|\bm{x}_{i+1}-\bm{x}_{1}\|\bm{f}(\bm{x}_{i+1/2})\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{i=1}^{N_{S}}\displaystyle\frac{1}{24}\|\bm{x}_{i+1}-\bm{x}_{i}\|\tilde{K}\|\bm{x}_{i+1}-\bm{x}_{i}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\displaystyle\frac{1}{24}\tilde{K}\Delta S_{max}^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{S}}\|\bm{x}_{i+1}-\bm{x}_{i}\|

\displaystyle\leqslant\displaystyle\frac{1}{24}\tilde{K}\Delta S_{max}^{2}|\Gamma_{C}|,

where
\Delta S_{max}=\max_{i\in\{1,\ldots,N_{S}\}}||\bm{x}_{i+1}-\bm{x}_{i}||
is the maximal length of the line segment over
\Gamma_{C}
, and
|\Gamma_{C}|
is the perimeter of the polygon
\Gamma_{C}
. It can be concluded that there exists a positive constant
K
, such that

|\bm{u}_{h}^{\infty}-\bm{u}_{h}^{\Delta S}|\leqslant K\Delta S_{max}^{2}.

In three dimensions, the surface element is a triangle. We map the triangle in
(x,y,z)
-space to the reference triangle in
(s,t)
-space with points
(0,0),(0,1)
and
(1,0)
. Suppose there is a surface element
e_{j}
with nodal points
\bm{x}_{1},\bm{x_{2}}
and
\bm{x_{3}}
, then the centre point of
e_{j}
is
\bm{x}_{c}=(\bm{x}_{1}+\bm{x}_{2}+\bm{x}_{3})/3
. The map from the
reference triangle
e_{0}
to the physical triangle
e_{j}
is given by

\bm{x}(s,t)=\bm{x}_{1}(1-s-t)+s\bm{x}_{2}+t\bm{x}_{3},\quad 0\leqslant s,t\leqslant 1.

For any function
\bm{f}(\bm{x})\in C^{2}(\Omega)
, the integral over the original triangle is given by

\int_{e_{j}}\bm{f}(\bm{x})d\bm{x}=\int_{e_{0}}\bm{f}(\bm{x}(s,t))|\sqrt{\det(\bm{J}^{T}\bm{J})|}d(s,t),

where
\bm{J}
is the Jacobian matrix, given by

\bm{J}=\frac{\partial(x,y,z)}{\partial(s,t)}=\begin{pmatrix}x_{2}-x_{1}&x_{3}-x_{1}\\
y_{2}-y_{1}&y_{3}-y_{1}\\
z_{2}-z_{1}&z_{3}-z_{1}\end{pmatrix},

and
\sqrt{|\det(\bm{J}^{T}\bm{J})|}
is twice the area of the original triangle
e_{j}
, i.e.

|\Delta_{j}|:=\sqrt{|\det(\bm{J}^{T}\bm{J})|}=||(\bm{x}_{2}-\bm{x}_{1})\times(\bm{x}_{3}-\bm{x}_{1})||.

We conduct the same process as for the two dimensional case, we obtain, where

\bm{x}(\frac{1}{3},\frac{1}{3})=\bm{x}_{c}
coincides with the midpoint
of element
e_{j}
, and where Taylor’s Theorem for
multi-variate functions is used:

\displaystyle\int_{e_{j}}\bm{f}(\bm{x})d\bm{x}=\int_{e_{0}}\bm{f}(\bm{x}(s,t))|\Delta_{j}|d(s,t)

\displaystyle=|\Delta_{j}|\int_{e_{0}}\bm{f}(\bm{x}(s,t))d(s,t)

\displaystyle=|\Delta_{j}|\int_{e_{0}}\bm{f}(\bm{x}_{c})+(\bm{x}(s,t)-\bm{x}_{c})\cdot\nabla\bm{f}(\bm{x}_{c})|

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}(\bm{x}(s,t)-\bm{x}_{c})^{T}\bm{H}(\bm{x}(\hat{s},\hat{t}))(\bm{x}(s,t)-\bm{x}_{c})d(s,t)

\displaystyle=|\Delta_{j}|[\frac{1}{2}\bm{f}(\bm{x}_{c})+0+\frac{1}{2}\int_{e_{0}}(\bm{x}(s,t)-\bm{x}_{c})^{T}\bm{H}(\bm{x}(\hat{s},\hat{t}))(\bm{x}(s,t)-\bm{x}_{c})d(s,t)].

Due to
\bm{f}(\bm{x})\in\bm{C^{2}}(\Omega)
, then for the Hessian matrix of
\bm{f}(\bm{x})
, there exists
\tilde{K}>0
, such that

|(\bm{x},\bm{H}(\bm{x}))|\leqslant\tilde{K}\|\bm{x}\|^{2}.

It yields

\displaystyle\left|\int_{e_{j}}\bm{f}(\bm{x})d\bm{x}-\frac{|\Delta_{j}|}{2}\bm{f}(\bm{x}_{c})\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\left|\frac{|\Delta_{j}|}{2}\int_{e_{0}}(\bm{x}(s,t)-\bm{x}_{c})^{T}\bm{H}(\bm{x}(\hat{s},\hat{t}))(\bm{x}(s,t)-\bm{x}_{c})d(s,t)\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{|\Delta_{j}|}{4}\tilde{K}h_{max}^{2},

where
h_{max}^{2}
is the largest diameter in the original triangle
e_{j}
. Considering all the surface elements over
\Gamma_{C}
, we compute

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\Gamma_{C}}\bm{f}(\bm{x})d\bm{x}-\sum_{j=1}^{N_{S}}\frac{|\Delta_{j}|}{2}\bm{f}(\bm{x}_{j})\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{\tilde{K}}{4}h_{max}^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{S}}\frac{|\Delta_{j}|}{2}\leqslant\frac{\tilde{K}}{4}h_{max}^{2}|\Gamma_{C}|,

where
h_{max}^{2}
is the maximal diameter among all the surface element (i.e. triangle) and
|\Gamma_{C}|
is the sum of the measure (area in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
) of all the surface elements over
\Gamma_{C}
. Therefore, in three dimensions, we can conclude that there exists a positive constant
K
, such that for the unique Galerkin solution to both
(GF)
and
(GF_{\infty})
,

|\bm{u}_{h}^{\infty}-\bm{u}_{h}^{\Delta S}|\leqslant Kh_{max}^{2}.

∎

The above proof and theorem can easily be extended to
higher dimensionalities.

3
Alternative Approaches for Elasticity Equation with Point Sources in Multi Dimensions

3.1
The ’Hole’ Approach

A different approach is based on considering cellular forces
on the cell boundary by means of a boundary condition. In
this alternative approach, one ’removes’ the cell region
from the domain of computation. Herewith, one creates a ’hole’
in the domain. We consider the balance of momentum
over
\Omega\setminus\overline{\Omega}_{C}
. This gives
the following boundary value problem:

(BVP_{H})\left\{\begin{aligned} -\nabla\cdot\bm{\sigma}&=0,\qquad&\mbox{in $\Omega\backslash\overline{\Omega}_{C}$,}\\
\bm{\sigma}\cdot\bm{n}&=P(\bm{x})\bm{n}(\bm{x}),\qquad&\mbox{on $\partial\Omega_{C}$,}\\
\bm{u}&=\bm{0},\qquad&\mbox{on $\partial\Omega$,}\end{aligned}\right.

where
\bm{\sigma}
is defined in Eq (
2.2
) with stiffness
E
. Let
D\subset\Omega
, then we introduce the following
notation:

a_{D,E}(\bm{u},\bm{v}):=\int_{D}\bm{\sigma}(\bm{u}):\bm{\epsilon}(\bm{v})d\Omega.

Note that the stiffness can be a constant or a function of space over the domain
D
.

The corresponding weak form is stated below:

(WF_{H})\left\{\begin{aligned} &\text{Find $\bm{u}^{H}\in\bm{H^{1}}(\Omega\setminus\Omega_{C})$
such that}\\
&a_{\Omega\setminus\Omega_{C},E}(\bm{u}^{H},\bm{\phi})=\int_{\Gamma_{C}}P(\bm{x})\bm{n}(\bm{x})\cdot\bm{\phi}dS(\bm{x}),\text{for all $\bm{\phi}\in\bm{H^{1}}(\Omega\setminus\Omega_{C})$.}\end{aligned}\right.

Since
\bm{\phi}\in\bm{H^{1}}(\Omega\setminus\Omega_{C})
, it follows
from the Trace Theorem
[
4
]
, and by noting that
\bm{\phi}|_{\partial\Omega}=0
, that there is a

C_{1}>0
such that
||\bm{\phi}||_{\bm{L^{2}}(\Gamma_{C})}\leq C_{1}||\bm{\phi}||_{\bm{H^{1}}(\Omega)}
, which implies that the right-hand
side in the weak form is bounded. Subsequently one
combines Korn’s Inequality with Lax-Milgram’s Lemma
to conclude that a unique solution in
\bm{H^{1}}
exists.

We compare the immersed boundary method with the ’hole’ approach by taking
\beta\geqslant 0
, then we adjust the immersed boundary method such that

E(\bm{x})=\begin{cases}\beta E,&\text{in }\Omega_{C},\\
E,&\text{\color[rgb]{1,0,0} in $\Omega\setminus\overline{\Omega}_{C}$.}\end{cases}

(3.1)

Regarding the adjusted immersed boundary approach where the stiffness is given by Eq (
3.1
), we
have the following Galerkin form

(GF_{\beta})\left\{\begin{aligned} &\text{Find $\bm{u}_{h}^{\beta}\in\bm{V}_{h}(\Omega)$
such that for all $\bm{\phi}_{h}\in\bm{V}_{h}(\Omega)$, we have}\\
&\beta a_{\Omega_{C},E}(\bm{u}_{h}^{\beta},\bm{\phi}_{h})+a_{\Omega\setminus\Omega_{C},E}(\bm{u}_{h}^{\beta},\bm{\phi}_{h})=\int_{\Gamma_{C}}P(\bm{x})\bm{n}(\bm{x})\cdot\bm{\phi}_{h}(\bm{x})dS(\bm{x}),\end{aligned}\right.

where
\bm{V}_{h}(\Omega)
is defined in Theorem
2.1
in Section
2
.

For the ’hole’ approach, we have the following Galerkin
form

(GF_{H})\left\{\begin{aligned} &\text{Find $\bm{u}_{h}^{H}\in\bm{V}_{h}(\Omega\setminus\Omega_{C})$
such that for all $\bm{\phi}_{h}\in\bm{V}_{h}(\Omega\setminus\Omega_{C})$
we have}\\
&a_{\Omega\setminus\Omega_{C},E}(\bm{u}_{h}^{H},\bm{\phi}_{h})=\int_{\Gamma_{C}}P(\bm{x})\bm{n}(\bm{x})\cdot\bm{\phi}_{h}dS(\bm{x}).\end{aligned}\right.

We will prove that the adjusted immersed boundary
method is a perturbation of the ’hole’ approach:

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
\bm{u}_{h}^{H}
and
\bm{u}_{h}^{\beta}
,
respectively, satisfy
Galerkin forms
(GF_{H})
and
(GF_{\beta})
, then there
is a
C>0
such that

||\bm{u}_{h}^{H}-\bm{u}_{h}^{\beta}||_{\bm{H^{1}}(\Omega\setminus\Omega_{C})}\leqslant C\sqrt{\beta}\|\bm{u}_{h}^{\beta}\|^{1/2}_{\bm{H^{1}}(\Omega_{C})}
.

Proof.

First we note that, as in the spirit of Theorem
2.1
, we consider Galerkin solutions in a subset of
\bm{H^{1}}
whereas the solution to the ’continuous’ weak formulation is not in
\bm{H^{1}}
. Formally
(GF_{H})
and
(GF_{\beta})
hold for test functions
\bm{\phi}_{h}
from different sets, namely
\bm{V}_{h}(\Omega)
and
\bm{V}_{h}(\Omega\setminus\Omega_{C})
. If we choose
\bm{V}_{h}(\Omega_{C})
to correspond to Lagrangian basis functions associated to internal nodes in
\Omega_{C}
, then these basis functions vanish at
\Gamma_{C}
. Furthermore, within the set of Lagrangian basis functions that are associated with
\Omega\setminus\Omega_{C}
, there are Lagrangian basis functions associated with
\Gamma_{C}
, which have a compact, hence limited, support over
\Omega_{C}
and in
\Omega\setminus\Omega_{C}
, then let
\bm{v}=\bm{u}_{h}^{\beta}-\bm{u}_{h}^{H}
, then subtraction of problems

(GF_{H})
and
(GF_{\beta})
gives

a_{\Omega\setminus\Omega_{C},E}(\bm{v},\bm{\phi}_{h})=-\beta a_{\Omega_{C},E}(\bm{u}_{h}^{\beta},\bm{\phi}_{h}).

The left-hand side is a bounded and coercive form on
which we can apply Korn’s Inequality. Furthermore,
boundedness of the right-hand side in

\bm{V}_{h}(\Omega\setminus\Omega_{C})
follows by
application of the Cauchy-Schwartz Inequality, hence
there is an
L>0
such that

|a_{\Omega_{C},E}(\bm{u}_{h}^{\beta},\bm{\phi}_{h})|\leqslant L\|\bm{u}_{h}^{\beta}\|_{\bm{H^{1}}(\Omega_{C})}\|\bm{\phi}_{h}\|_{\bm{H^{1}}(\Omega_{C})}
. Herewith, we arrive at

-\beta L\|\bm{u}_{h}^{\beta}\|_{\bm{H^{1}}(\Omega_{C})}\|\bm{\phi}_{h}\|_{\bm{H^{1}}(\Omega_{C})}\leqslant a_{\Omega\setminus\Omega_{C},E}(\bm{v},\bm{\phi}_{h})\leqslant\beta L\|\bm{u}_{h}^{\beta}\|_{\bm{H^{1}}(\Omega_{C})}\|\bm{\phi}_{h}\|_{\bm{H^{1}}(\Omega_{C})},\text{ for all }\bm{\phi}_{h}\in\bm{V}_{h}(\Omega\setminus\Omega_{C}).

Note that the
a_{\Omega\setminus\Omega_{C}}(\bm{v},\bm{\phi}_{h})
contains
\bm{v}
and
\bm{\phi}_{h}
in
\Omega\setminus\Omega_{C}
, whereas the right-hand side of the inequality contains norms over
\Omega_{C}
. Using Korn’s Inequality, and upon setting
\bm{\phi}_{h}=\bm{v}
in
\Omega\setminus\Omega_{C}
, we arrive at

\displaystyle K||\bm{v}||^{2}_{\bm{H^{1}}({\Omega\setminus\Omega_{C}})}\leqslant a_{\Omega\setminus\Omega_{C},E}(\bm{v},\bm{v})\leqslant\beta L\|\bm{u}_{h}^{\beta}\|_{\bm{H^{1}}(\Omega_{C})}\|\bm{\phi}_{h}\|_{\bm{H^{1}}(\Omega_{C})}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow||\bm{v}||_{\bm{H^{1}}({\Omega\setminus\Omega_{C}})}\leqslant C\sqrt{\beta}\|\bm{u}_{h}^{\beta}\|^{1/2}_{\bm{H^{1}}(\Omega_{C})},\text{ where }C=\sqrt{\frac{L}{K}}\|\bm{\phi}_{h}\|^{1/2}_{\bm{H^{1}}(\Omega_{C})}.

∎

For the case of a spring-force boundary condition on
\partial\Omega
one can derive
a compatibility condition. To this extent, we consider the following boundary value
problems, for the ’hole’ problem:

(BVP_{H}^{\prime})\left\{\begin{aligned} -\nabla\cdot\bm{\sigma}&=0,\qquad&\mbox{in $\Omega\backslash\overline{\Omega}_{C}$,}\\
\bm{\sigma}\cdot\bm{n}&=P(\bm{x})\bm{n}(\bm{x}),\qquad&\mbox{on $\partial\Omega_{C}$,}\\
\bm{\sigma}\cdot\bm{n}+\kappa\bm{u}&=\bm{0},\qquad&\mbox{on $\partial\Omega$,}\end{aligned}\right.

and for the immersed boundary problem:

(BVP_{I}^{\prime})\left\{\begin{aligned} -\nabla\cdot\bm{\sigma}&=\int_{\Gamma_{C}}P(\bm{x}^{\prime})\bm{n}(\bm{x}^{\prime})\delta(\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{\prime})dS(\bm{x}),\qquad&\mbox{in $\Omega$,}\\
\bm{\sigma}\cdot\bm{n}+\kappa\bm{u}&=\bm{0},\qquad&\mbox{on $\partial\Omega$,}\end{aligned}\right.

Next we give a proposition regarding compatibility for the ’hole’ approach and the
immersed boundary method for the case of a spring boundary condition:

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
\bm{u}_{H}
and
\bm{u}_{I}
, respectively, be solutions to the ’hole’ approach, see
(BVP_{H}^{\prime})
and to the immersed boundary approach, see
(BVP_{I}^{\prime})
. Let
\Gamma_{C}
denote the boundary of the cell, over which internal forces are exerted, and let
\partial\Omega
be the outer boundary of
\Omega
. Then

\int_{\partial\Omega}\kappa\bm{u}_{H}dS=\int_{\partial\Omega}\kappa\bm{u}_{I}dS=\int_{\Gamma_{C}}P(\bm{x})\bm{n}(\bm{x})dS.

Proof.

To prove that the above equation holds true, we integrate the PDE of both approaches over the computational domain
\Omega
.

For the immersed boundary approach, we get

-\int_{\Omega}\nabla\cdot\bm{\sigma}d\Omega=\int_{\Omega}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{S}}P(\bm{x}_{j})\bm{n}(\bm{x}_{j})\delta(\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{j})\Delta S(\bm{x}_{j})d\Omega,

then after applying Gauss Theorem in the LHS and simplifying the RHS, we obtain

-\int_{\partial\Omega}\bm{\sigma}\cdot\bm{n}(\bm{x})dS=\sum_{j=1}^{N_{S}}P(\bm{x}_{j})\bm{n}(\bm{x}_{j})\Delta S(\bm{x}_{j}).

By substituting the Robin’s boundary condition and letting
N_{S}\rightarrow\infty
, i.e.
\Delta S(\bm{x}_{j})\rightarrow 0
, the equation becomes

\int_{\partial\Omega}\kappa\bm{u_{I}}dS=\int_{\Gamma_{C}}P(\bm{x})\bm{n}(\bm{x})dS.

(3.2)

Subsequently, we do the same thing for the ’hole’ approach. Then, we get

-\int_{\Omega}\nabla\cdot\bm{\sigma}d\Omega=0,

and we apply Gauss Theorem:

-\int_{\partial\Omega\cup\Gamma_{C}}\bm{\sigma}\cdot\bm{n}(\bm{x})dS=0,

which implies

-\int_{\partial\Omega}\bm{\sigma}\cdot\bm{n}(\bm{x})dS-\int_{\Gamma_{C}}\bm{\sigma}\cdot\bm{n}(\bm{x})dS=0.

Using the boundary conditions, we get

\int_{\partial\Omega}\kappa\bm{u}_{H}dS=\int_{\Gamma_{C}}P(\bm{x})\bm{n}(\bm{x})dS,

which is exactly the same as Eq (
3.2
). Hence we proved that

\int_{\partial\Omega}\kappa\bm{u}_{H}dS=\int_{\partial\Omega}\kappa\bm{u}_{I}dS=\int_{\Gamma_{C}}P(\bm{x})\bm{n}(\bm{x})dS.

∎

Hence, the two different approaches are consistent in the sense of global conservation of momentum and therefore the results from both approaches should be comparable.

3.2
The Smoothed Particle Approach

The Gaussian distribution is used here as an approximation for the Dirac Delta distribution. Hereby, we show that in the
n
-dimensional case, the Gaussian distribution is a proper approximation for the Dirac Delta distribution.

Lemma 3.1
.

For an open domain
\Omega=(x_{1,1},x_{1,2})\times(x_{2,1},x_{2,2})\times\cdots\times(x_{n,1},x_{n,2})\subset\mathbb{R}^{n},n\geqslant 2
, let

\delta_{\varepsilon}(\bm{x}-\bm{x^{\prime}})=\displaystyle\frac{1}{(2\pi\varepsilon^{2})^{n/2}}\exp\{-\displaystyle\frac{\|\bm{x}-\bm{x^{\prime}}\|^{2}}{2\varepsilon^{2}}\},

where
\bm{x^{\prime}}=(x^{\prime}_{1},\dots,x^{\prime}_{n})\in\Omega
, then

(i)
\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}\delta_{\varepsilon}(\bm{x}-\bm{x^{\prime}})\rightarrow 0,
for all
\bm{x}\neq\bm{x^{\prime}}
;

(ii) Let
f(\bm{x})\in\mathbb{C}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
\|f(\bm{x})\|\leqslant M<+\infty
, then
there is a
C>0
such that

|\int_{\Omega}\delta_{\varepsilon}(\bm{x}-\bm{x^{\prime}})f(\bm{x})d\Omega-f(\bm{x^{\prime}})|\leqslant C\varepsilon^{2}\text{ as }\varepsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}.

Proof.

(i) Since
\bm{x}\neq\bm{x^{\prime}}
,
\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}\exp\{-\displaystyle\frac{\|\bm{x}-\bm{x^{\prime}}\|^{2}}{2\varepsilon^{2}}\}\rightarrow 0
. Thus,

\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}\delta_{\varepsilon}(\bm{x}-\bm{x^{\prime}})\rightarrow 0,\mbox{ for all $\bm{x}\neq\bm{x^{\prime}}$.}

(ii) Now we consider

\int_{\Omega}\delta_{\varepsilon}(\bm{x}-\bm{x^{\prime}})f(\bm{x})d\Omega=\int_{\Omega}\displaystyle\frac{1}{(2\pi\varepsilon^{2})^{n/2}}\exp\{-\displaystyle\frac{\|\bm{x}-\bm{x^{\prime}}\|^{2}}{2\varepsilon^{2}}\}f(\bm{x})d\Omega.

Firstly, we integrate over the infinite domain:

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\delta_{\varepsilon}(\bm{x}-\bm{x^{\prime}})f(\bm{x})d\Omega

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi\varepsilon^{2})^{n/2}}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\cdots\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\exp\{-\displaystyle\frac{\|\bm{x}-\bm{x^{\prime}}\|^{2}}{2\varepsilon^{2}}\}f(\bm{x})dx_{n}\cdots dx_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi\varepsilon^{2})^{n/2}}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\exp\{-\displaystyle\frac{(x_{1}-x^{\prime}_{1})^{2}}{2\varepsilon^{2}}\}\cdots\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\exp\{-\displaystyle\frac{(x_{n}-x^{\prime}_{n})^{2}}{2\varepsilon^{2}}\}

\displaystyle f(\bm{x})dx_{n}\cdots dx_{1}.

Again let
s_{i}=\frac{(x_{i}-x_{i}^{\prime})-\frac{x_{i,1}+x_{i,2}}{2}}{\sqrt{2}\varepsilon},
and furthermore
\xi_{i}=s_{i}+\frac{x_{i,1}+x_{i,2}}{2},i=\{1,2,\dots,n\}
. We denote
\bm{x_{1}}=(x_{1,1},x_{2,1},\dots,x_{n,1})
,
\bm{x_{2}}=(x_{1,2},x_{2,2},\dots,x_{n,2})
and
\bm{x^{\prime}}=(x^{\prime}_{1},x^{\prime}_{2}\dots,x^{\prime}_{n})
.
By Taylor Expansion,
f(\bm{x})
can be rewritten as

\displaystyle f(\bm{x})=f(\sqrt{2}\varepsilon\bm{s}+\frac{\bm{x_{1}}+\bm{x_{2}}}{2}+\bm{x^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=f(\bm{x^{\prime}})+\nabla f(\bm{x^{\prime}})(\sqrt{2}\varepsilon\bm{s}+\frac{\bm{x_{1}}+\bm{x_{2}}}{2})

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2!}(\sqrt{2}\varepsilon\bm{s}+\frac{\bm{x_{1}}+\bm{x_{2}}}{2})^{T}\bm{H}(\bm{x^{\prime}})(\sqrt{2}\varepsilon\bm{s}+\frac{\bm{x_{1}}+\bm{x_{2}}}{2})+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{3})

\displaystyle=f(\bm{x^{\prime}})+\nabla f(\bm{x^{\prime}})\sqrt{2}\varepsilon(\bm{s}+\frac{\bm{x_{1}}+\bm{x_{2}}}{2\sqrt{2}\varepsilon})

\displaystyle+\varepsilon^{2}(\bm{s}+\frac{\bm{x_{1}}+\bm{x_{2}}}{2\sqrt{2}\varepsilon})^{T}\bm{H}(\bm{x^{\prime}})(\sqrt{2}\varepsilon\bm{s}+\frac{\bm{x_{1}}+\bm{x_{2}}}{2\sqrt{2}\varepsilon})+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{3})

\displaystyle=f(\bm{x^{\prime}})+\nabla f(\bm{x^{\prime}})\sqrt{2}\varepsilon\bm{\xi}+\varepsilon^{2}\bm{\xi}^{T}\bm{H}(\bm{x^{\prime}})\bm{\xi}+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{3})

where
\bm{H}(\bm{x^{\prime}})
is Hessian matrix of
f(\bm{x})
. For any non-negative integer
d
,

\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}z^{d}e^{-z^{2}}dz=\left\{\begin{aligned} &0,&\mbox{if $d$ is odd,}\\
&\Gamma(\displaystyle\frac{d+1}{2}),&\mbox{if $d$ is even.}\end{aligned}\right.

First we calculate

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\delta_{\varepsilon}(\bm{x}-\bm{x^{\prime}})f(\bm{x})d\Omega

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi\varepsilon^{2})^{n/2}}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\exp\{-\displaystyle\frac{(x_{1}-x^{\prime}_{1})^{2}}{2\varepsilon^{2}}\}\cdots\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\exp\{-\displaystyle\frac{(x_{n}-x^{\prime}_{n})^{2}}{2\varepsilon^{2}}\}

\displaystyle f(\bm{x})dx_{n}\cdots dx_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi^{n/2}}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\exp\{(-s_{1}+\frac{x_{1,1}+x_{1,2}}{2})^{2}\}\cdots\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\exp\{(-s_{n}+\frac{x_{n,1}+x_{n,2}}{2})^{2}\}

\displaystyle f(\sqrt{2}\varepsilon\bm{s}+\frac{\bm{x_{1}}+\bm{x_{2}}}{2}+\bm{x^{\prime}})ds_{n}\cdots ds_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi^{n/2}}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}e^{-\xi_{1}^{2}}\cdots\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}e^{-\xi_{n}^{2}}f(\sqrt{2}\varepsilon\bm{\xi}+\bm{x^{\prime}})d\xi_{n}\cdots d\xi_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi^{n/2}}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}e^{-\xi_{1}^{2}}\cdots\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}e^{-\xi_{n}^{2}}[f(\bm{x^{\prime}})+\nabla f(\bm{x^{\prime}})\sqrt{2}\varepsilon\bm{\xi}+\varepsilon^{2}\bm{\xi}^{T}\bm{H}(\bm{x^{\prime}})\bm{\xi}

\displaystyle+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{3})]d\xi_{n}\cdots d\xi_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{f(\bm{x^{\prime}})}{\pi^{n/2}}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}e^{-\xi_{1}^{2}}\cdots\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}e^{-\xi_{n}^{2}}d\xi_{n}\cdots d\xi_{1}

\displaystyle+\frac{\sqrt{2}\varepsilon}{\pi^{n/2}}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}e^{-\xi_{1}^{2}}\xi_{1}f^{\prime}_{x_{1}}(\bm{x^{\prime}})\cdots\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}e^{-\xi_{n}^{2}}\xi_{n}f^{\prime}_{x_{n}}(\bm{x^{\prime}})d\xi_{n}\cdots d\xi_{1}

\displaystyle+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{\pi^{n/2}}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}e^{-\xi_{1}^{2}}(\sqrt{2}\xi_{1}+f^{\prime\prime}_{x_{1},x_{1}}(\bm{x^{\prime}})\xi_{1}^{2}+\sum_{i=1,i\neq 1}^{n}f^{\prime\prime}_{x_{1},x_{i}}(\bm{x^{\prime}})\xi_{1}\xi_{i}\cdots

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}e^{-\xi_{n}^{2}}(\sqrt{2}\xi_{1}+(f^{\prime\prime}_{x_{n},x_{n}})(\bm{x^{\prime}})\xi_{n}^{2}+\sum_{i=1,i\neq n}^{n}f^{\prime\prime}_{x_{n},x_{i}}(\bm{x^{\prime}})\xi_{n}\xi_{i}d\xi_{n}\cdots d\xi_{1}+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{3})

\displaystyle=f(\bm{x^{\prime}})+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{\sqrt{\pi}}\Gamma(\frac{3}{2})\sum_{i=1}^{d}f^{\prime\prime}_{x_{i},x_{i}}(\bm{x^{\prime}})+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{3})\rightarrow f(\bm{x^{\prime}}),\mbox{as $\varepsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}$}.

For the integral over the given domain
\Omega=(x_{1,1},x_{1,2})\times\cdots\times(x_{n,1},x_{n,2})
, it can be written as

\displaystyle\int_{x_{1,1}}^{x_{1,2}}\cdots\int_{x_{n,1}}^{x_{n,2}}dx_{n}\cdots dx_{1}

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\cdots\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dx_{n}\cdots dx_{1}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{x_{1,1}}^{x_{1,2}}\cdots\int_{-\infty}^{x_{i,1}}\cdots\int_{x_{n,1}}^{x_{n,2}}dx_{n}\cdots dx_{1}

\displaystyle-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{x_{1,1}}^{x_{1,2}}\cdots\int_{x_{i,2}}^{+\infty}\cdots\int_{x_{n,1}}^{x_{n,2}}dx_{n}\cdots dx_{1}

\displaystyle=(\sqrt{2}\varepsilon)^{n}\left[\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\cdots\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}ds_{n}\cdots ds_{1}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{s_{1,1}}^{s_{1,2}}\cdots\int_{-\infty}^{s_{i,1}}\cdots\int_{s_{n,1}}^{s_{n,2}}ds_{n}\cdots ds_{1}\right.

\displaystyle\left.-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{\xi_{1,1}}^{\xi_{1,2}}\cdots\int_{\xi_{i,2}}^{+\infty}\cdots\int_{\xi_{n,1}}^{\xi_{n,2}}d\xi_{n}\cdots d\xi_{1}\right]

\displaystyle=(\sqrt{2}\varepsilon)^{n}\left[\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\cdots\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}d\xi_{n}\cdots d\xi_{1}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{\xi_{1,1}}^{\xi_{1,2}}\cdots\int_{-\infty}^{\xi_{i,1}}\cdots\int_{\xi_{n,1}}^{\xi_{n,2}}d\xi_{n}\cdots d\xi_{1}\right.

\displaystyle\left.-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{\xi_{1,1}}^{\xi_{1,2}}\cdots\int_{\xi_{i,2}}^{+\infty}\cdots\int_{\xi_{n,1}}^{\xi_{n,2}}d\xi_{n}\cdots d\xi_{1}\right],

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\Omega}\delta_{\varepsilon}(\bm{x}-\bm{x^{\prime}})f(\bm{x})d\Omega-f(\bm{x^{\prime}})\right|

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\pi^{n/2}}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{\xi_{1,1}}^{\xi_{1,2}}e^{-\xi_{1}}\cdots\int_{-\infty}^{\xi_{i,1}}e^{-\xi_{i}}\cdots\int_{\xi_{n,1}}^{\xi_{n,2}}e^{-\xi_{n}}f(\sqrt{2}\varepsilon\bm{\xi}+\bm{x^{\prime}})d\xi_{n}\cdots d\xi_{1}\right.

\displaystyle\left.\left.+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{\xi_{1,1}}^{\xi_{1,2}}e^{-\xi_{1}}\cdots\int_{\xi_{i,2}}^{+\infty}e^{-\xi_{i}}\cdots\int_{\xi_{n,1}}^{\xi_{n,2}}e^{-\xi_{n}}f(\sqrt{2}\varepsilon\bm{\xi}+\bm{x^{\prime}})d\xi_{n}\cdots d\xi_{1}\right]-f(\bm{x^{\prime}})\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\left|\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{\sqrt{\pi}}\Gamma(\frac{3}{2})\sum_{i=1}^{d}f^{\prime\prime}_{x_{i},x_{i}}(\bm{x^{\prime}})+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{3})\right|

\displaystyle+\frac{M}{2^{n-1}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\prod_{i=1,i\neq j}^{n}[\erf(\xi_{j,2})-\erf(\xi_{j,1})+2][\erf(\xi_{i,2})-\erf(\xi_{i,1})]

\displaystyle\leqslant\left|\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{\sqrt{\pi}}\Gamma(\frac{3}{2})\sum_{i=1}^{d}f^{\prime\prime}_{x_{i},x_{i}}(\bm{x^{\prime}})+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{3})\right|

\displaystyle+\frac{M}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{n}[\erf(\xi_{j,1})-\erf(\xi_{j,2})+2]\rightarrow 0,\mbox{as $\varepsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}$,}

since

|\int_{\Omega}\delta_{\varepsilon}(\bm{x}-\bm{x^{\prime}})f(\bm{x})d\Omega-f(\bm{x^{\prime}})|\leqslant C\varepsilon^{2}\text{ as }\varepsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}.

∎

5
Conclusion

For the dimensionality exceeding one, the existence of Dirac Delta
distributions in the elasticity equation results into a singular
solution. We analyse the solutions based on Galerkin approximations
with Lagrangian basis functions for different approaches that are
consistent if cell sizes and smoothness parameters tend to zero.
We have shown that all the alternative approaches are numerically consistent
with the immersed boundary approach. The current paper has
investigated and extended earlier findings to multi-dimensionality. The current analysis has been
carried out for simple, linear elasticity. In the future,
we plan to extend our findings to the visco-elasticity
equations. This visco-elastic model contains a damping
term, and still retains a linear nature.
Furthermore, we are also interested in analyzing the
above considered principles for a morphoelastic model.
A morpho-elastic model has the major advantage of incorporating
permanent deformations. A major complication is its
nonlinear nature.
