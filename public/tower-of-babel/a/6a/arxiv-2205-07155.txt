Characterization of blowups via time change in a mean-field neural network
Thibaud Taillefumier, Phillip Whitman

Abstract
Idealized networks of integrate-and-fire neurons with impulse-like interactions obey McKean-Vlasov diffusion equations in the mean-field limit. These equations are prone to blowups: for a strong enough interaction coupling, the mean-field rate of interaction diverges in finite time with a finite fraction of neurons spiking simultaneously, thereby marking a macroscopic synchronous event. Characterizing these blowup singularities analytically is the key to understanding the emergence and persistence of spiking synchrony in mean-field neural models. However, such a resolution is hindered by the first-passage nature of the mean-field interaction in classically considered dynamics. Here, we introduce a delayed Poissonian variation of the classical integrate-and-fire dynamics for which blowups are analytically well defined in the mean-field limit. Albeit fundamentally nonlinear, we show that this delayed Poissonian dynamics can be transformed into a noninteracting linear dynamics via a deterministic time change. We specify this time change as the solution of a nonlinear, delayed integral equation via renewal analysis of first-passage problems. This formulation also reveals that the fraction of simultaneously spiking neurons can be determined via a self-consistent, probability-conservation principle about the time-changed linear dynamics. We utilize the proposed framework in a companion paper to show analytically the existence of singular mean-field dynamics with sustained synchrony for large enough interaction coupling.

Introduction

1.1
Background

This work introduces neural network models for which the emergence of synchrony can be studied analytically in the idealized, mean-field limit of infinite-size networks.
By synchrony, we refer to the possibility that a finite fraction of the network’s neurons simultaneously spikes.
Dynamics exhibiting such synchrony can serve as models to study the maintenance of precise temporal information in neural networks
[
23
,
17
,
3
]
.
The maintenance of precise temporal information in the face of neural noise remains a debated issue from an experimental and computational perspective.
Mathematical approaches to understand synchrony involve making simplifying assumptions about the individual neuronal processing as well as about the network supporting their interactions.

Integrate-and-fire neurons
[
19
,
18
]
constitute perhaps the simplest class of models susceptible to displaying synchrony
[
27
]
.
In integrate-and-fire models, the internal state of a neuron
i
is modeled as a continuous-time diffusive process
X_{i,t}
, whose dynamics stochastically integrates past neural interactions.
Spiking times are then defined as first-passage times of this diffusive process to a spiking boundary
L
.
Upon spiking, the process
X_{i,t}
resets to a base real value
\Lambda
.
In other words,
X_{i,t^{+}}=\Lambda
whenever neuron
i
spikes at time
t
.
There is no loss of generality in assuming that
L=0
and
\Lambda>0
, so that
X_{i,t}
has nonnegative state space.
Moreover, classical integrate-and-fire models assume that
X_{i,t}
follows a Wiener diffusive dynamics with negative drift
-\nu<0

[
9
]
.
Drifted Wiener processes are the simplest diffusive dynamics for which spikes occur in finite time with probability one, even in the absence of interactions.

A key feature of integrate-and-fire models is that they allow for the occurrence of synchronous spiking events.
This is most conveniently seen by considering a finite neural network with instantaneous, homogeneous, impulse-like excitatory interactions.
For such interactions, if neuron
i
spikes at time
t
, downstream neurons
j\neq i
instantaneously update their internal states according to
X_{j,t^{+}}=X_{j,t^{+}}-w
, where
w>0
is the size of the impulse-like interaction.
Thus, the spiking of neuron
i
causes the states of all other neurons to move toward the zero spiking boundary, leading to two possible outcomes for downstream neuron
j
:
either
X_{j,t^{+}}>w
and the update merely hastens the next spiking time of neuron
j
, or
X_{j,t^{+}}\leq w
and the interaction causes neuron
j
to spike in synchrony with
i

[
30
,
29
]
.
The latter synchronous spiking events occurs with finite probability, as we generically have
\mathbb{P}\left[X_{j,t}\in(0,w]\right]>0
for regular diffusion processes.
In turn, the synchronous spiking of downstream neuron
j
can trigger additional synchronous spiking events in the network, via branching processes referred to as spiking avalanches.
Spiking avalanches are well-defined under the modeling assumptions that neurons transiently enter a post-spiking refractory state and always exit this refractory state by reseting to
\Lambda

[
26
]
.
Under such assumptions, neurons can spike at most once within an avalanche and synchronously spiking neurons can be distinguished according to their generation number
[
12
]
.

Tellingly, the finite probability to observe a spiking avalanche is maintained in certain simplifying limit, such as the thermodynamic mean-field limit
[
1
,
14
,
31
,
24
]
.
For a homogeneous, excitatory, integrate-and-fire network, the thermodynamic mean-field limit considers a network of
N
exchangeable neurons in the infinite-size limit,
N\to\infty
, with vanishingly small impulse size
w_{N}=\lambda/N\to 0
, where
\lambda
is a parameter quantifying the interaction coupling.
In this mean-field limit, individual neurons only interact with one another via a deterministic population-averaged firing rate
f(t)

[
6
,
8
,
9
]
.
Specifically, the dynamics of a representative process
X_{t}
obeys a nonlinear partial differential equation (PDE) of the McKean-Vlasov type

\displaystyle\partial_{t}p=\big(\nu+\lambda f(t)\big)\partial_{x}p+\partial_{x}^{2}p/2+f(t^{-})\delta_{\Lambda}\,,\quad\mathrm{with}\quad p(t,x)\,\mathrm{d}x=\mathbb{P}\left[X_{t}\in\mathrm{d}x\right]\,,

(1)

and where the effective drift features the firing rate
f(t)
.
The nonlinearity of the above equation stems from the conservation of probability, which equates
f(t)
with a boundary flux of probability:

\displaystyle f(t)=\partial_{x}p(t,0)/2\,.

(2)

In the following, we refer to equation (
1
) and (
2
) as the classical McKean-Vlasov (cMV) equations and to the underlying dynamics supporting these equations as the classical mean-field (cMF) dynamics.
Within the setting of cMF dynamics, a blowup occurs at time
T_{0}
if the spiking rate diverges when
t\to T_{0}^{-}
and a synchronous event happens if a fraction of the neurons
\pi_{0}>0
synchronously spikes in
T_{0}
.

1.2
Motivation

Following on seminal computational work in
[
5
,
4
]
, the cMF dynamics was first investigated in a PDE setting by Cáceres
et al.

[
6
]
, who established the occurrence of blowups.
The existence and regularity of solutions to (
1
) and (
2
) have been considered from the standpoint of stochastic analysis by several authors
[
12
,
11
,
15
,
22
,
21
]
.
These authors combined results from the theory of interacting-particle systems
[
20
,
25
]
and of the convergence of probability measures
[
2
]
to establish criteria for the existence of global solutions
[
12
,
11
]
and to classify the type of singularities displayed by these solutions
[
15
,
22
,
21
]
.
However, the analytical characterization of blowup singularities have proven rather challenging.
Here, we propose a modified interacting-particle system with Poisson-like attributes that is also prone to blowup, the so-called delayed Poissonian mean-field (dPMF) model.
By contrast with
[
11
]
and in line with
[
6
,
8
]
, we only conjecture that the propagation of chaos holds to
motivate the form of the corresponding mean-field PDE problem.
This conjecture is numerically supported in the weak interaction regime
\lambda<\Lambda
and for the strong interaction regime
\lambda>\Lambda
.
The interest of the proposed framework lies in introducing a mean-field model where blowups, including full blowup whereby a finite fraction of neurons fires synchronously, can be studied analytically.
In particular, we utilize this framework in
[
28
]
to show the existence of global solutions defined on the whole real lines with an infinite but countable number of blowups for large interaction parameters
\lambda\gg\Lambda
.

1.3
Approach

The crux of our approach is the introduction of an analytically tractable neural-network model that is closely related to the cMF model, the so-called delayed Poissonian mean-field (dPMF) dynamics.
dPMF dynamics are derived from the classical ones by considering that
(a)
neurons are driven by noisy inputs with Poisson-like attributes and that
(b)
neurons exhibit a post-spiking refractory period.
Concretely, assumption
(a)
corresponds to approximating the counting process registering neuronal inputs in the thermodynamic limit by a Gaussian Markov process with time-dependent drift
-\big(\nu+\lambda f(t)\big)
and unit Fano factor, i.e., with variance and drift of identical magnitude.
At the same time, assumption
(b)
corresponds to enforcing that neurons remain in a noninteracting, inactive state for a duration
\epsilon
after reaching the zero spiking threshold and before reseting in
\Lambda
.
In addition of being relevant from a modeling standpoint, the inclusion of a finite refractory period
\epsilon
allows for the unambiguous definition of dPMF dynamics during synchrony.
Specifically, refractory period enforces that every neuron engaging in an instantaneous spiking avalanche at time
t
spikes only once and resets in
\Lambda
at time
t+\epsilon
.
Overall, the delayed Poissonian version of the nonlinear McKean-Vlasov dynamics (
1
) reads

\displaystyle\partial_{t}p=\big(\nu+\lambda f(t)\big)\left(\partial_{x}p+\partial_{x}^{2}p/2\right)+f(t-\epsilon)\delta_{\Lambda}\,,

where we will see that the conservation of probability imposes that

\displaystyle f(t)=\frac{\nu\partial_{x}p(t-\epsilon,0)}{2-\lambda\partial_{x}p(t,0)}\,.

(3)

The above relation directly indicates the criterion for blowups in dPMF dynamics:
blowups occur whenever
\partial_{x}p(t,0)/2
, the instantaneous flux through the absorbing boundary, reaches the value
1/\lambda
.
In other words, blowups emerge at finite boundary flux, which allows for the continuous maintenance of the absorbing boundary condition:
p(t,0)=0
.
This is by contrast with cMF dynamics for which blowups involve diverging fluxes at times
T_{0}
, for which the absorbing boundary condition must locally fail:
p(T_{0},0)\geq 1/\lambda

[
22
,
21
]
.
Such singular behavior is a major hurdle to elucidating blowup analytically in cMF dynamics.
The expected regularized behavior of dPMF dynamics during blowups is the primary motivation for their introduction.

Ideally, the PDE problem that defines dPMF dynamics shall be established as the mean-field limit of the corresponding finite-size interacting-particle system.
The present work only conjectures that such a mean-field limit holds, which is supported by numerical simulations (except possibly for interaction parameter
\lambda\simeq\Lambda
).
Then, the core idea of our approach is to solve the PDE problem defining dPMF dynamics by formally introducing the time change

\displaystyle\sigma=\Phi(t)=\nu t+\lambda F(t)\,,\quad\mathrm{with}\quad F(t)=\int_{0}^{t}f(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\,,

(4)

which is a smooth increasing function in the absence of blowups.
Due to the Poissonian attributes of the neuronal drives, the time change
\Phi
can serve to parametrize the dPMF dynamics of a representative process as
X_{t}=Y_{\Phi(t)}
, where
Y_{\sigma}
is a process obeying a linear, noninteracting dynamics.
The dynamics of
Y_{\sigma}
is that of a Wiener process absorbed in zero, with constant negative unit drift and with reset in
\Lambda
, but with time-inhomogeneous refractory period specified via a
\Phi
-dependent delay function
\sigma\mapsto\eta[\Phi](\sigma)
.
In the following, we will refer to
\eta
as the backward delay function associated to
\Phi
.
Concretely, this means that assuming the backward-delay function
\eta
known, the transition kernel of
Y_{\sigma}
denoted by
(\sigma,x)\mapsto q(\sigma,x)
satisfies the time-changed PDE problem

\displaystyle\partial_{\sigma}q

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\partial_{x}q+\frac{1}{2}\partial^{2}_{x}q+\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\sigma}[G(\sigma-\eta(\sigma))]\delta_{\Lambda}\,,

(5)

with absorbing and conservation conditions respectively given by

\displaystyle q(\sigma,0)=0\quad\mathrm{and}\quad\partial_{\sigma}G(\sigma)=\partial_{x}q(\sigma,0)/2\,.

(6)

In equations (
5
) and (
6
),
G
denotes the
\eta
-dependent cumulative flux of
Y_{\sigma}
through the zero threshold.
By definition of the time change
\Phi
,
G
is related to the cumulative flux
F
via
F=G\circ\Phi
.
Moreover, in the absence of blowups, the functional dependence of
\eta
on the time change
\Phi
is given by

\displaystyle\eta(\sigma)=\sigma-\Phi(\Psi(\sigma)-\epsilon)\,.

where
\Psi=\Phi^{-1}
refers to the inverse time change of
\Phi
.
Thus,
G=G[\Phi]
actually depends on
\Phi
via
\eta
, which motivates considering equation (
4
) as a self-consistent equation specifying admissible time changes:

\displaystyle\Phi(t)=\nu t+\lambda G[\Phi](\Phi(t))\,.

(7)

Our approach then elaborates on the fact that in the absence of blowups, dPMF dynamics are fully parametrized by the time-change function that uniquely solves equation (
7
) for some reasonable initial conditions.
Given such a solution
\Phi
, the transition kernel of a representative dPMF dynamics
X_{t}
is found as
(t,x)\mapsto p(t,x)=q(\Phi(t),x)
, where
q
uniquely solves the time-changed PDE for the corresponding backward-delay function
\eta[\Phi]
.
From there, our general aim is to show that this time-changed characterization is preserved in the presence of blowups, thereby justifying dPMF dynamics as a convenient modeling framework to analytically study mean-field dynamics with blowups.

1.4
Results

Our main result is to characterize explosive dPMF dynamics via a fixed-point problem bearing on a regularized time-changed dynamics.
To state this fixed-point problem, we first need to define a notion of initial conditions in the time-changed picture.
In principle, the most general initial conditions for the original dPMF dynamics are specified by two measures
(p_{0},f_{0})
in
\mathcal{M}(\mathbbm{R}^{+})\times\mathcal{M}([-\epsilon,0))
, where

\mathcal{M}(I)
denotes the space of nonnegative measures over the interval
I\subset\mathbbm{R}
.
Moreover, to represent a probability measure,
(p_{0},f_{0})
must also satisfy the normalization condition:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}p_{0}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{-\epsilon}^{0}f_{0}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t=1\,.

With this in mind, the time-changed version of the above initial conditions is specified as follows:

Definition 1.1
.

Given normalized initial conditions
(p_{0},f_{0})
in
\mathcal{M}(\mathbbm{R}^{+})\times\mathcal{M}([-\epsilon,0))
, the initial conditions for the time-changed problem are defined by
(q_{0},g_{0})
in
\mathcal{M}(\mathbbm{R}^{+})\times\mathcal{M}([\xi_{0},0))
such that

\displaystyle q_{0}=p_{0}\quad\mathrm{and}\quad g_{0}=\frac{\mathrm{d}G_{0}}{\mathrm{d}\sigma}\quad\mathrm{with}\quad G_{0}=(\mathrm{id}-\nu\Psi_{0})/\lambda\,,

where the function
\Psi_{0}
and the number
\xi_{0}
are given by:

\displaystyle\Psi_{0}(\sigma)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\inf\left\{t\geq 0\,\bigg|\,\nu t+\lambda\int_{0}^{t}f_{0}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s>\sigma\right\}\,,

\displaystyle\xi_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\nu\epsilon-\lambda\int_{-\epsilon}^{0}f_{0}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t<0\,.

Equipped with the above notion of initial conditions, we are in a position to state the fixed-point problem characterizing possibly explosive dPMF dynamics.
This fixed-point problem will be most conveniently formulated in term of the inverse time change
\Psi=\Phi^{-1}
, which can generically be assumed to be a continuous, nondecreasing function.
Given an inverse time change
\Psi
, the time change
\Phi
can be recovered as the right-continuous inverse of
\Psi
.
In the time-changed picture, blowups happen if the inverse time change
\Psi
becomes locally flat and a synchronous event happens if
\Psi
remains flat for a finite amount of time.
Informally, flat sections of
\Psi
unfold blowups by freezing time in the original coordinate
t
, while allowing time to pass in the time-changed coordinate
\sigma
.
Such unfolding of blowups in the time-changed picture will allow for the following characterization of inverse time change
\Psi
, which remains valid for explosive dPMF dynamics.

Theorem 1.2
.

Given time-changed initial conditions
(q_{0},g_{0})
in
\mathcal{M}(\mathbbm{R}^{+})\times\mathcal{M}([\xi_{0},0))
, the inverse time change
\Psi
satisfies the fixed-point problem

\displaystyle\forall\;\sigma\geq 0\,,\quad\Psi(\sigma)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}\left(\sigma-\lambda\int_{0}^{\sigma}g_{0}(\xi)\mathrm{d}\xi\right)/\nu&\quad\mathrm{if}&-\xi_{0}\leq\sigma<0\,,\\
\sup_{0\leq\xi\leq\sigma}\big(\xi-\lambda G[\eta](\xi)\big)/\nu&\quad\mathrm{if}&\sigma\geq 0\,.\end{array}\right.

where
G[\eta]
is the smooth cumulative flux associated to a linear diffusion dynamics with time-inhomogeneous backward-delay function
\eta:\mathbbm{R}^{+}\to\mathbbm{R}^{+}
.
Given a backward-delay function
\eta
, the cumulative flux
G
is given as the unique solution to the quasi-renewal equation

\displaystyle G(\sigma)=\int_{0}^{\infty}H(\sigma,x)q_{0}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{0}^{\sigma}H(\sigma-\tau,\Lambda)\,\mathrm{d}G(\tau-\eta(\tau))\,,

(10)

where by convention we set
G(\sigma)=G_{0}(\sigma)=\int_{0}^{\sigma}g_{0}(\xi)\mathrm{d}\xi
if
-\xi_{0}\leq\sigma<0
.
Moreover, the integration kernel featured in (
10
) is specified as
\sigma\mapsto H(\sigma,x)=\mathbb{P}\left[\tau_{x}\leq\sigma\right]
, where
\tau_{x}
is the first-passage time to zero of a Wiener process started in
x>0
and with negative unit drift.
Finally, the fixed-point nature of the problem follows from the definition of the backward-delay function
\eta
as the
\Psi
-dependent time-wrapped version of the constant delay
\epsilon
:

\displaystyle\eta(\sigma)=\sigma-\Phi(\Psi(\sigma)-\epsilon)\quad\mathrm{with}\quad\Phi(t)=\inf\left\{\sigma\geq\xi_{0}\,\big|\,\Psi(\sigma)>t\right\}\,,

for which we consistently have
\eta(0)=-\Phi(-\epsilon)=\xi_{0}
.

We will show that the time-changed formulation of dPMF dynamics yields the existence and uniqueness of dPMF dynamics under an additional assumption about the initial conditions.
That assumption bears on the distribution of active processes at starting time and states that
q_{0}
is a locally smooth near zero with
q_{0}(0)=0
and
\partial_{x}q_{0}(0)/2<1/\lambda
.
In view of (
3
), such an additional assumption precludes a blowup from happening instantaneously.
In turn, this will allow us to show the following existence and uniqueness result:

Theorem 1.3
.

Given time-changed initial conditions
(q_{0},g_{0})
in
\mathcal{M}(\mathbbm{R}^{+})\times\mathcal{M}([\xi_{0},0))
such that
q_{0}(0)=0
and
\partial_{x}q_{0}(0)/2<1/\lambda
, the fixed-point problem defined in Theorem
1.2
admits a unique smooth solution
\Psi
on
[0,S_{1}]
, where

\displaystyle S_{1}=\inf\{\sigma>0\,|\,\Psi^{\prime}(\sigma)\leq 0\}>0\,.

Moreover, if
S_{1}<\infty
and
\Psi^{\prime\prime}(S_{1}^{-})<0
, this solution can be uniquely continued on
[S_{1},S_{1}+\lambda\pi_{1})
as a constant, where
\pi_{1}
solves the self-consistent equation:

\displaystyle\pi_{1}=\inf\left\{p\geq 0\,\bigg|\,p>\int_{0}^{\infty}H(\lambda p,x)q(S_{1},x)\,\mathrm{d}x\right\}\,.

(11)

The above result indicates how the time-changed process
Y_{\sigma}
resolves a blowup episode by alternating two types of dynamics.
Before blowups, the dynamics
Y_{\sigma}
is that of an absorbed linear diffusion with resets.
These resets occur with time-inhomogeneous delays, which depends on the inverse time change
\Psi
.
At the blowup onset
S_{1}
,
\Psi
becomes locally flat, indicating that the original time
t=\Psi(\sigma)
freezes, thereby stalling resets.
As a result, after the blowup onset in
S_{1}
, the dynamics of
Y_{\sigma}
remains that of an absorbed linear diffusion but without resets.
Such a dynamics persists until a self-consistent blowup exit condition is met in
S_{1}+\lambda\pi_{1}
.
This condition follows from (
11
) and states that it must take
\lambda\pi_{1}
time-changed units for a fraction
\pi_{1}
of processes to inactivate during a blowup episode.
Finally, note that the generic condition that
\lim_{\sigma\to S_{1}}\partial^{2}_{\sigma}\Psi(\sigma)<0
, which we refer to the full-blowup condition, implies that the blowup is marked for the original dynamics
X_{t}=Y_{\Phi(t)}
at
T_{1}=\Psi(S_{1})
by a well-characterized rate divergence:
f(t)\sim_{t\to T_{1}^{-}}1/\sqrt{T_{1}-t}
.

In principle, dPMF dynamics could be continued past a blowup episode, and possibly even extended to the whole half-line
\mathbbm{R}^{+}
.
Showing this in our framework would require to check that
(1)
the so-called
(i)
nonexplosive exit conditions
\partial_{\sigma}q(S_{1}+\lambda\pi_{1},0)/2<1/\lambda
and
(ii)
full-blowup condition
\partial^{2}_{\sigma}\Psi(S_{1}^{-})<0
are constitutively satisfied and that
(2)
blowup times do not have an accumulation point.
This program is beyond the scope of this work and is the topic of another manuscript
[
28
]
, where we show that these conditions hold for large enough interaction parameter
\lambda
.
Here, we only state the main result of
[
28
]
about the existence of global explosive dPMF dynamics:

Theorem 1.4
.

For large enough
\lambda>\Lambda
, there exists explosive dPMF dynamics defined over the whole half-line
\mathbbm{R}^{+}
, with a countable infinity of blowups.
These blowups occurs at consecutive times
T_{k}
with size
\pi_{k}
,
k\in\mathbbm{N}
, and are such that
\pi_{k}
and
T_{k+1}-T_{k}
are both bounded away from zero.

1.5
Methodology

Overall, the main interest of our approach lies in our ability to resolve a singular dynamics by mapping it onto a regular dynamics, but via possibly discontinuous change of time.
Such an approach avoids resorting to convergence arguments in sample-path spaces equipped with the Skorokhod topology. Our approach proceeds in four steps:

First, we infer the interacting-particle systems approximating the dPMF dynamics by modifying the systems known to approximate cMF dynamics
[
12
,
11
]
.
This involves considering noisy synaptic interactions, whereby spiking updates in downstream neurons are i.i.d. following a normal law with mean and variance equal to
w_{N}=\lambda/N
, where
N
denotes the number of neurons.
Conjecturing propagation of chaos
[
25
]
in the infinite size limit
N\to\infty
allows us to justify the form of the PDE problem associated to dPMF dynamics, which is only well-posed for nonexplosive dynamics.
In order to extend this PDE characterization to explosive dPMF dynamics, we must give a weak formulation to the associated PDE problem.
Due to the mean-field nature of dPMF dynamics, this weak formulation involves considering the cumulative flux
F
as an auxiliary unknown function.

Second, we define the linear, time-inhomogeneous PDE problem associated to the process
Y_{\sigma}
obtained by time change of nonexplosive dPMF dynamics:
X_{t}=Y_{\Phi(t)}
.
The hypothesis of nonexplosive dynamics is necessary to ensure that the time change
\Phi
introduced in (
4
) is smooth.
However, considerations from renewal analysis show that the obtained time-changed PDE problem is actually unconditionally well-posed, independent on the assumption of smoothness of
\Phi
.
By this, we mean that for any choice of nondecreasing function
\Phi
, the latter PDE problem has well-behaved solutions
(\sigma,x)\mapsto q[\Phi](\sigma,x)
, in the sense that by contrast with
F=G\circ\Phi
, the associated cumulative flux
G
is always a smooth function of time.
Therefore, this time-changed picture provides us with a natural framework to define a notion of explosive dPMF dynamics, with possibly many blowups.

Third, we show that among solutions parametrized by increasing functions
\Phi
, candidate solutions of the form
(t,x)\mapsto p(t,x)=q[\Phi](\Phi(t),x)
are also weak solutions for dPMF dynamics if and only if the time change
\Phi
satisfies the self-consistent equation (
7
).
Technically, showing this point relies on the substitution formula for nonsmooth changes of variable
[
13
]
as well as on the Vol’pert superposition principle
[
32
,
10
]
.
This result justifies reducing the analysis of dPMF dynamics to the study of a delayed, nonlinear, integral equation defining the fixed-point problem of Theorem (
1.2
).
Crucially, for bearing on possibly discontinuous increasing time change
\Phi
, this approach fully captures explosive dPMF dynamics.

Fourth, we show that the fixed-point problem of Theorem (
1.2
) admits local solutions for a class of initial conditions that exclude instantaneous blowup.
Under such initial conditions, we establish the existence of initial smooth dPMF dynamics via a contraction argument.
This contraction argument relies on the fact that for nonzero refractory period
\epsilon>0
, the quasi-renewal equation (
10
) loses its renewal character at small enough timescale.
Then, repeated application of the Banach fixed-point theorem allows one to specify a smooth solution
\Phi
up to the first putative blowup time
T_{1}
.
In the time-changed coordinate
\sigma
, the onset of a blowup episode at
S_{1}=\Phi(T_{1})
corresponds to withholding resets, which leads to a natural self-consistent equation for blowup sizes
\pi_{1}
.
Such blowups are certain in the large interaction regime
\lambda\geq\Lambda
and can be shown to have physical size, in the sense that they must correspond to a finite fraction
0<\pi_{1}<1
.

1.6
Structure

In Section
2
, we introduce the delayed Poissonian (dPMF) dynamics as the conjectured mean-field limit of a certain interacting-particle systems and define its associated weak PDE formulation.
In Section
3
, we show that in the absence of blowups, dPMF dynamics are fully determined by a time change that maps the original time-homogeneous nonlinear dynamics on time-inhomogeneous linear dynamics.
In Section
4
, we exploit the weak formulation of dPMF dynamics to show that the proposed time-changed formulation remains valid in the presence of blowups, exhibiting the fixed-point problem that characterizes admissible time changes.
In Section
5
, we show that the fixed-point problem admits local solutions with blowups and resolve analytically these blowups.

2
The delayed Poisson-McKean-Vlasov dynamics

In this section, we justify the consideration of dPMF dynamics to study the emergence and persistence of blowups in mean-field neural models.
Conjecturing that propagation of chaos holds in the infinite-size limit, we justify the McKean-Vlasov equations defining dPMF dynamics in the absence of blowups.
We then leverage these equations to elaborate a weak formulation that allows for the consideration of explosive dPMF dynamics.

2.1
Finite-size stochastic model

We start by defining the finite-size version of the dPMF dynamics in terms of a particle system whose dynamics is unconditionally well-posed.
This particle system consists of a network of
N
interacting processes
X_{N,i,t}
,
1\leq i\leq N
, whose interaction dynamics is as follows:

(i)
Whenever a process
X_{N,i,t}
hits the spiking boundary at zero, it instantaneously enters an inactive refractory state.

(ii)
At the same time, all the other active processes
X_{N,j,t}
(which are not in the inactive refractory state) are respectively decreased by amounts
w_{N,j,t}
, which are independently drawn from a normal law with mean and variance equal to
\lambda/N
.

(iii)
After an inactive (refractory) period of duration
\epsilon>0
, the process
X_{N,i,t}
restarts its autonomous stochastic dynamics from the reset state
\Lambda>0
.

(iv)
In between spiking/interaction times, the autonomous dynamics of active processes follow independent drifted Wiener processes with negative drift
-\nu
.
Correspondingly, an initial condition for the network is given by specifying the starting values of the active processes, i.e.
X_{N,i,0}>0
if
i
is active, and the last hitting time of the inactive processes, i.e.,
-\epsilon\leq\rho_{N,i,0}\leq 0
, if
i
is inactive.

The above dynamics can be conveniently recapitulated in terms of the stochastic differential equations governing the interacting processes
X_{N,i,t}
,
1\leq i\leq N
.
For
1\leq i\leq N
, these equations takes the general form

\displaystyle X_{N,i,t}=X_{N,i,0}-\int_{0}^{t}\mathbbm{1}_{\{X_{N,i,s^{-}}>0\}}\mathrm{d}Z_{N,i,s}+\Lambda M_{N,i,t-\epsilon}\,,

(12)

where
Z_{N,i,t}
,
1\leq i\leq N
, denote continuous-time driving processes with Poisson-like attributes and
where
M_{N,i,t}
,
1\leq i\leq N
, are increasing processes counting the number of times that
X_{N,i,t}
hits the threshold
\Lambda
before
t
.
The driving processes
Z_{N,i,t}
are specified in term of cumulative drift functions
\Phi_{N,i}
according to

\displaystyle Z_{N,i,t}=\Phi_{N,i}(t)+W_{i,\Phi_{N,i}(t)}\,,

where
W_{i,t}
,
1\leq i\leq N
, are independent Wiener processes.
Thus-defined, the driving processes
Z_{N,i,t}
exhibit Poisson-like attributes in the sense that they have constitutive unit Fano factor.
By contrast, classical particle-system approaches consider driving processes with variable Fano Factor of the form
\Phi_{N,i}(t)+W_{i,t}
.
The assumption of a constant Fano factor is the key to making an analytical treatment of blowups possible in the mean-field limit.

As generic cumulative functions, the functions
\Phi_{N,i}
are only assumed to be right-continuous with left limits, which we refer to as being
càdlàg
following classical probabilistic conventions.
These
càdlàg
cumulative functions
\Phi_{N,i}
are naturally defined in terms of the counting processes
M_{N,j,t}
as

\displaystyle\Phi_{N,i}(t)=\nu t+\frac{\lambda}{N}\sum_{j\neq i}M_{N,j,t}\,,

showing that the jump discontinuities in
\Phi_{N,i}
model interneuronal interactions.
In turn, the counting processes
M_{N,j,t}
are defined as

\displaystyle M_{N,j,t}=\sum_{n>0}\mathbbm{1}_{[0,t]}(\rho_{N,j,n})\,,

where
\rho_{N,i,n}
,
n\geq 0
, denote the successive first-passage times of
X_{N,i,t}
to the zero spiking threshold.
These times are formally defined for all
n\geq 0
by

\displaystyle\rho_{N,i,n+1}=\inf\left\{t>r_{N,i,n}\,\big|\,X_{N,i,t}\leq 0\right\}\,.

where by convention, we set
r_{N,i,0}=0
for processes that are active in zero with
X_{N,i,0}>0
and where
r_{N,i,n}=\rho_{N,i,n}+\epsilon
,
n\geq 1
, denote the successive delayed reset times.

Thus defined, the particle-system dynamics is self exciting:
every spiking event of a neuron
i
hastens the spiking of other neurons
j\neq i
by bringing their states closer to the zero threshold boundary.
Moreover, the particle-system dynamics allows for synchronous spiking as whenever neuron
i
spikes due to its autonomous dynamics, we generically have that
\mathbb{P}\left[X_{N,j,t}\in(0,w_{N,j,t}]\right]>0
.
If the process
X_{N,i,t}
first hits zero at time
\rho
, (
12
) implies that
X_{N,i,t}
remains in zero for all
t
in
(\rho,\rho+\epsilon)
, until it receives an instantaneous kick that enforces a reset in
\Lambda
at time
r=\rho+\epsilon
.
Thus, (
12
) formally identifies the refractory state with zero.
However, it will prove more convenient to consider the inactive state as an isolated inactive state away from zero.
The reason for this is that such a consideration avoid modeling inactive processes via Dirac-delta mass in zero, so that regular absorbing boundary conditions in zero can be enforced.

Mathematically, the benefit of including an inactive period
\epsilon
is to ensure the uniqueness of the particle-system dynamics during spiking avalanches, thereby ensuring that the overall dynamics is well-posed.
Spiking avalanches occurs when the spiking of a neuron triggers the instantaneous spiking of other neurons.
Neurons that engage in a spiking avalanche can be sorted out according to a generation number.
Generation
0
contains the lone triggering neuron which is driven to the absorbing boundary by its autonomous dynamics.
Generation
1
comprises all those neurons that spike due to interactions with the triggering neuron alone.
In general, generation
k>1
, comprises all the neurons that spike from interacting with the neurons of the previous generations alone.
In the absence of a post-spiking inactive period (
\epsilon=0
), it is ambiguous whether the neurons from previous generations are impacted by the spiking of neurons from the following generations.
However, in the presence of an inactive period (
\epsilon>0
), neurons from previous generations are unresponsive to neurons from following generations due to post-spiking transient inactivation.
Accordingly, as a variation on
[
11
]
, we resolve the ambiguity of spiking avalanche in the absence of inactive period by only considering the so-called “physical dynamics”, obtained from delayed dynamics in the limit
\epsilon\to 0^{+}
.
These “physical dynamics” assume that independent of their generation number, every neuron engaging in a spiking avalanche at time
t
spikes only once and resets in
\Lambda
at
t^{+}
.
We conclude by noting that the delayed dynamics introduced here differ from those considered in
[
11
]
, where the delay bears on the interactions rather than the resets.
This distinction is important as by contrast with reset-delayed dynamics, interaction-delayed dynamics are not prone to explosions.

2.2
Mean-field dynamics under propagation of chaos

The particle-system dynamics introduced above primarily differs from the classically considered one by its Poisson-like attributes.
In classically defined particle systems, the jump discontinuities of the driving inputs have fixed size
\lambda/N
instead of being i.i.d according to a normal law with mean and variance equal to
\lambda/N
.
In
[
11
]
, Delarue
et al.
show that the property of propagation of chaos holds in the infinite-size limit of classically defined particle systems.
This property establishes that in the infinite-size limit, a representative process
X_{t}=\lim_{N\to\infty}X_{N,i,t}
follows a mean-field dynamics satisfying the PDE problem (
1
) and (
2
) originally introduced in
[
6
,
8
]
.
This particle-system-based approach automatically yields the existence of—possibly explosive—solutions to the PDE problem (
1
) and (
2
).
Here, by contrast with
[
11
]
and in line with
[
6
,
8
]
, we only conjecture propagation of chaos to motivate the form of the PDE problem defining a novel mean-field dynamics that is prone to blowup.
We then consider these dynamics on their own merit, independent of the conjecture of propagation of chaos.

The propagation of chaos states that for exchangeable initial conditions, the processes
X_{N,i,t}
,
1\leq i\leq N
, become i.i.d. in the limit of infinite-size networks
N\to\infty
, so that each individual process follows a mean-field dynamics.
We refer to such a mean-field dynamics as a cMF dynamics for the classical model and a dPMF dynamics for the Poisson-like model.
For both models, the mean-field interaction governing the dynamics of a representative process
X_{t}
is mediated by a deterministic cumulative drift
\Phi=\lim_{N\to\infty}\Phi_{N,i}
.
Formally, this deterministic drift is defined as
\Phi(t)=\nu t+\lambda\mathbb{E}\left[M_{t}\right]
, where the process
M_{t}
counts the successive first-passage times of the representative process
X_{t}
to the zero spiking threshold:

\displaystyle M_{t}=\sum_{n>0}\mathbbm{1}_{[0,t]}(\rho_{n})\,,\quad\mathrm{with}\quad\rho_{n+1}=\inf\left\{t>r_{n}=\rho_{n}+\epsilon\,\big|\,X_{t}\leq 0\right\}\,.

(13)

In the following, we will denote the increasing function
\mathbb{E}\left[M_{t}\right]
by
F(t)
.
In the context of the associated PDE problem, we will refer to
F
as the cumulative flux function through the zero absorbing boundary.
Observe that by definition, the function
F
is an increasing
càdlàg
function.
This allows one to define the instantaneous firing rate
f
in the distribution sense as the Radon-Nikodym derivative of
F
with respect to the Lebesgue measure

f=\mathrm{d}F/\mathrm{d}t
.
Correspondingly, synchronous events whereby a finite fraction of processes spike simultaneously are marked by Dirac-delta mass in
f
.

By contrast with cMF models, the deterministic cumulative drift
\Phi(t)=\nu t+\lambda F(t)
constitutively impacts neurons with Poissonian attributes in dPMF models, i.e., via a process
Z_{t}=\Phi(t)+W_{\Phi(t)}
, where
W
is a driving Wiener process.
Accordingly, the stochastic dPMF dynamics of a representative process is given by

\displaystyle X_{t}=X_{0}-\int_{0}^{t}\mathbbm{1}_{\{X_{s^{-}}>0\}}\mathrm{d}Z_{s}+\Lambda M_{t-\epsilon}\,.

(14)

The above equation fully defines dPMF dynamics.
Because of the self-interaction terms, dPMF dynamics are prone to blowups for large enough interaction coupling and/or for initial conditions that are concentrated near the boundary.
Actually, just as for cMF models, we will see that the cumulative drift
\Phi
can exhibit
(i)
singular blowups, corresponding to a divergence of the reset rate
f
in finite time and
(ii)
jump discontinuities whereby a finite fraction of the processes spike at the same time.
Our goal is to characterize analytically the emergence of these blowups.
This will require first defining the PDE problem associated to dPMF dynamics in the absence of blowups.

2.3
McKean-Vlasov equations under smoothness assumptions

For weak interaction, i.e.,
\lambda<\Lambda
, we expect dPMF dynamics to be nonexplosive for initial conditions far enough from the spiking threshold, e.g.,
p(0,x)=\delta_{x_{0}}(x)
with sufficiently large
x_{0}>0
.
This motivates defining the PDE problem associated to dPMF dynamics under strong regularity assumptions.
Specifically, let us assume that
t\mapsto F(t)=\mathbb{E}\left[M_{t}\right]
is smooth on
[0,T)
for some
T>0
.
Then,
f(t)=F^{\prime}(t)
represents the nonnegative, smooth, mean-field rate of inactivation.
Under such regularity assumptions, a representative process
X_{t}
satisfying (
14
) admits a probability density
(t,x)\mapsto p(t,x)
which solves the Fokker-Plank equation

\displaystyle\partial_{t}p=(\nu+\lambda f(t))\left(\partial_{x}p+\partial^{2}_{x}p/2\right)+\mathbbm{1}_{\{t>\epsilon\}}f(t-\epsilon)\delta_{\Lambda}\,,

(15)

with absorbing boundary condition in
p(t,0)=0
.
The latter absorbing condition ensures that the process becomes inactive upon reaching zero.
The Dirac-delta source term models the reset in
\Lambda
of newly activated processes, which happens in
t
with delayed rate
f(t-\epsilon)
.

To be consistent, the mean-field dynamics specified by (
15
) needs to conserve the total probability.
This conservation requirement implies that

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}\!p(t,x)\,\mathrm{d}x\right)=\mathbbm{1}_{\{t>\epsilon\}}f(t-\epsilon)-f(t)\,.

(16)

Using (
15
), we can evaluate the left term above as

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}\!p(t,x)\,\mathrm{d}x\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\big(\nu+\lambda f(t)\big)\left(\partial_{x}p(t,x)+\partial^{2}_{x}p(t,x)/2\right)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbbm{1}_{\{t>\epsilon\}}f(t-\epsilon)\delta_{\Lambda}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\,.

Performing integration by parts with absorbing boundary condition in zero then yields

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}\!p(t,x)\,\mathrm{d}x\right)=-\big(\nu\!+\!\lambda f(t)\big)\partial_{x}p(0,t)/2+f(t-\epsilon)\,,

which together with (
16
) imposes the self-consistent conservation condition
f(t)=(\nu\!+\!\lambda f(t))\partial p_{x}(t,0)/2
, ultimately yielding:

\displaystyle f(t)=\frac{\nu\partial_{x}p(0,t)}{2-\lambda\partial_{x}p(0,t)}\,.

The Fokker-Planck equation (
15
) and the above conservation condition fully specify the mean-field dynamics.
As the coefficients of the Fokker-Plank equation depends on its solution via a boundary flux term, the mean-field dynamics is actually a nonlinear Markov evolution of the McKean-Vlasov type.

Finally, observe that to avoid blowups, we have only considered initial conditions of the form
p_{0}(x)=\delta_{x_{0}}(x)
, with initially empty inactive state.
However, the PDE problem defined above can be considered for more generic initial conditions, at the possible cost of not having any regular solutions.
According to the delayed nature of the dynamics, these generic initial conditions are naturally specified by

\displaystyle p(0,x)=p_{0}(x)\quad\mathrm{and}\quad f(t)=f_{0}(t)\,,\quad\epsilon\leq t<0\,.

with
(p_{0},f_{0})
in
\mathcal{M}(\mathbbm{R}^{+})\times\mathcal{M}([-\epsilon,0))
,
where
(p_{0},f_{0})
satisfies the normalization condition

\displaystyle\int_{-\epsilon}^{0}f_{0}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s=1-\int_{0}^{\infty}p_{0}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\,.

(17)

Given this notion of initial conditions, we define the McKean-Vlasov PDE problem associated to smooth dPMF dynamics as:

Definition 2.1
.

Given normalized initial conditions
(p_{0},f_{0})
in
\mathcal{M}(\mathbbm{R}^{+})\times\mathcal{M}([-\epsilon,0))
, the PDE problem associated to a dPMF dynamics consists in finding the density function
(t,x)\mapsto p(t,x)=\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}\left[0<X_{t}<x\right]/\mathrm{d}x
solving

\displaystyle\partial_{t}p

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\big(\nu+\lambda f(t)\big)\left(\partial_{x}p+\partial^{2}_{x}p/2\right)+f(t-\epsilon)\delta_{\Lambda}\,,

(18)

on
[0,T)\times[0,\infty)
for some (possibly infinite)
T>0
and with absorbing and conservation conditions given by

\displaystyle p(t,0)=0\quad\mathrm{and}\quad f(t)=\frac{\nu\partial_{x}p(t,0)}{2-\lambda\partial_{x}p(t,0)}\,.

(19)

The challenge posed by the emergence of blowups is to make sense of (
18
) for instantaneous flux
f
exhibiting finite-time divergence, possibly followed by Dirac-delta mass, corresponding to a jump discontinuity in
F
.
Such singularities present themselves whenever an initially smooth dynamics is such that
t\mapsto\partial_{x}p(t,0)/2
reaches
1/\lambda
in finite time.
As intuition suggests, this blowup criterion will generally be met for sufficiently large interaction parameter.
In particular, we will see that given initial conditions of the form
p_{0}=\delta_{x_{0}}
with
x_{0}>0
, there is a constant
C_{x_{0}}
which only depends on
x_{0}
such that a blowup occurs in finite time for all
\lambda>C_{x_{0}}
.

2.4
Weak formulation for explosive dPMF dynamics

In this section, our goal is to propose a weak formulation of the PDE problem
2.1
that is amenable to capture explosive dPMF dynamics.
This formulation bears on candidate density functions
(t,x)\mapsto p(t,x)
in the space of distributions defined as the dual of
C_{0,\infty}([-\epsilon,T)\times\mathbbm{R})
, the set of compactly supported, smooth functions
u:[-\epsilon,T)\times\mathbbm{R}\rightarrow\mathbbm{R}
.
We derive the announced weak formulation by first considering nonexplosive solutions of the PDE problem
2.1
.
For all nonexplosive solutions
(t,x)\mapsto p(t,x)
and all test functions
u
in
C_{0,\infty}([-\epsilon,T)\times\mathbbm{R})
, we must have

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{\infty}\left[\big(\nu+\lambda f(t)\big)\mathcal{L}[p](t,x)+f(t-\epsilon)\delta_{\Lambda}-\partial_{t}p(t,x)\right]u(t,x)\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}x\,,

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{\infty}\big[\big(\nu+\lambda f(t)\big)\mathcal{L}[p](t,x)-\partial_{t}p(t,x)\big]u(t,x)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\hskip 200.0pt+\int_{0}^{T}f(t-\epsilon)u(t,\Lambda)\,\mathrm{d}t\,,

where
\mathcal{L}
denotes the operator
\mathcal{L}=\partial_{x}+1/2\partial^{2}_{x}
for brevity.
Taking into account the absorbing boundary condition, integration by parts with respect to space yields

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathcal{L}[p](t,x)u(t,x)\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{0}^{\infty}p(t,x)\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}[u](t,x)\,\mathrm{d}x+\frac{1}{2}\partial_{x}p(t,0)u(t,0)\,,

so that integration by part with respect to time produces

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\big[p(T,x)u(T,x)-p(0,x)u(0,x)\big]\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hskip-120.0pt\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{\infty}p(t,x)\big[\big(\nu+\lambda f(t)\big)\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}[u](t,x)+\partial_{t}u(t,x)\big]\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\hskip-120.0pt+\int_{0}^{T}\big[f(t-\epsilon)u(t,\Lambda)-\big(\nu+\lambda f(t)\big)\partial_{x}p(t,0)u(t,0)/2\big]\,\mathrm{d}t\,.

Remembering the flux conservation condition
\partial_{x}p(t,0)=f(t)/(\nu+\lambda f(t))
, we obtain the following weak characterization for nonexplosive solutions

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\big[p(T,x)u(T,x)-p(0,x)u(0,x)\big]\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hskip-120.0pt\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{\infty}p(t,x)\big[\big(\nu+\lambda f(t)\big)\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}[u](t,x)+\partial_{t}u(t,x)\big]\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\hskip-120.0pt+\int_{0}^{T}\big[f(t-\epsilon)u(t,\Lambda)-f(t)u(t,0)\big]\,\mathrm{d}t\,.

The above characterization involves the instantaneous flux
f
as an unknown, which can be safely assumed to be a nonnegative integrable function.
With that in mind, one can see that the proposed characterization derived for nonexplosive solutions is well-posed for any candidate density function in the space of integrable distributions.
This leads to defining the notion of weak solution for the dPMF dynamics in the presence of blowups as follows:

Definition 2.2
.

Given normalized initial conditions
(p_{0},f_{0})
in
\mathcal{M}(\mathbbm{R}^{+})\times\mathcal{M}([-\epsilon,0))
, the density function
(t,x)\mapsto p(t,x)
is a weak solution of the dPMF dynamics if and only if there is a bounded nondecreasing
càdlàg
function
F:[-\epsilon,T)\to\mathbbm{R}
with
\mathrm{d}F/dt=f_{0}
on
[-\epsilon,0)
such that for all
u
in
C_{0,\infty}([-\epsilon,T)\times\mathbbm{R})
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\big[p(T,x)u(T,x)-p(0,x)u(0,x)\big]\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=

(20)

\displaystyle\hskip-140.0pt\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{\infty}p(t,x)\big[\nu\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}[u](t,x)+\partial_{t}u(t,x)\big]\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\hskip-140.0pt+\>\lambda\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{\infty}p(t^{-},x)\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}[u](t,x)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}F(t)

\displaystyle\hskip-140.0pt+\int_{0}^{T}u(t,\Lambda)\,\mathrm{d}F(t-\epsilon)-\int_{0}^{T}u(t,0)\,\mathrm{d}F(t)\,.\

Clearly, all nonexplosive solutions
(t,x)\mapsto p(t,x)
of the PDE problem
2.1
are weak solutions of the PDE problem
2.2
for
F
equal to the cumulative flux integrating
f
as defined in (
19
).
It is also clear that by contrast with the PDE problem
2.1
, the definition of weak solutions allows for discontinuous function
F
.
In that respect, observe that we enforce that
F
is
càdlàg
to be consistent with the definition of the counting process
M_{t}
given in (
13
).
When choosing
F
to be
càdlàg
, it is then necessary to specify the type of continuity of the integrand for integrals with respect to
F
as the integrator to be well-defined.
In view of the predictable integrand in stochastic equation (
14
), we consistently impose that the integrand be left-continuous whenever
F
features as the integrator.
Intuitively, we expect the function
F
featuring in Definition
2.2
to be uniquely related to a weak solution
(t,x)\mapsto p(t,x)
, just as for nonexplosive solutions.
This fact is established by the following proposition:

Proposition 2.1
.

There is a unique nondecreasing
càdlàg
function
F
such that the density function
(t,x)\mapsto p(t,x)
is a weak solution of the dPMF dynamics.

Observe that for integrable density functions, the defining property of weak solutions also holds for smooth test functions with bounded derivatives of all orders.
Then, specifying (
20
) for
u=1
yields

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}p(T,x)\,\mathrm{d}x-\int_{0}^{\infty}p(0,x)\,\mathrm{d}x=\big(F(T-\epsilon)-F(-\epsilon)\big)-\big(F(T)-F(0)\big)\,.

(21)

Consider two functions
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
such that
p
is a weak solution of the dPMF dynamics. The initial conditions impose that we have

\displaystyle F_{1}(0)-F_{1}(-\epsilon)=F_{2}(0)-F_{2}(-\epsilon)=\int_{-\epsilon}^{0}f_{0}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\,.

Therefore, specifying (
21
) for
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
and forming the difference yields

\displaystyle F_{1}(t)-F_{1}(t-\epsilon)=F_{2}(t)-F_{2}(t-\epsilon)\,,

so that,
F_{1}-F_{2}
is an
\epsilon
-periodic function.
As
F_{1}=F_{2}
on the interval
[-\epsilon,0)
, we necessarily have
F_{1}=F_{2}
for all
t\leq 0
by
\epsilon
-periodicity.
∎

In the following our strategy will be to use the above notion of weak solutions to screen candidate explosive solutions defined via time change for
bona fide
dPMF dynamics.

3
Linearization via implicitly defined time change

In this section, we show that under certain regularity assumptions, dPMF dynamics can be turned into noninteracting linear dynamics via a time change.
We then interpret these dynamics probabilistically via renewal analysis to establish that they are constitutively well-posed, independent of the time-change function.
Such a realization provides the basis to define explosive dPMF dynamics in the time-changed picture.

3.1
Conditionally linear dynamics

Informally, dPMF dynamics admit blowups at those times
T
for which the instantaneous inactivation flux
f
diverges:
\lim_{t\to T^{-}}f(t)=\infty
.
Characterizing such blowup times analytically entails studying the PDE problem
2.1
with the drift, diffusion, and reset coefficients that are all allowed to locally diverge.
In general, this is a hard problem that cannot be tackled analytically.
However, for drift and diffusion coefficients with Poisson-like attributes, the problem is tractable thanks to the availability of a regularized time-changed formulation.
Not surprisingly, the function
\Phi
operating this time change can be guessed as the integral function of the drift:

\displaystyle t\mapsto\Phi(t)=\nu t+\lambda F(t)\,.

(22)

This approach suggests considering that solutions to the PDE problem
2.1
as parametrized by a time change
\Phi
, which shall be viewed as the fundamental unknown of the problem.
In light of (
22
), we shall look for solution time change
\Phi
in the following class of functions:

Definition 3.1
.

We define the class of valid time changes
\mathcal{T}
as the set of
càdlàg
functions
\Phi:[-\epsilon,\infty)\to[\xi_{0},\infty)
, such that their difference quotients are lower bounded by
\nu
: for all
y,x\leq-\epsilon
,
x\neq y
, we have

\displaystyle w_{\Phi}(y,x)=\frac{\Phi(y)-\Phi(x)}{y-x}\geq\nu\,.

In general, to be a valid time change, we only require a function
\Phi:\mathbbm{R}^{+}\to\mathbbm{R}^{+}
to be a nondecreasing
càdlàg
function.
This means that time changes
\Phi
must exclude time-reversal point at which the changed time
\sigma=\Phi(t)
would start flowing backward when the original time
t
keeps moving forward.
Here, the time change Definition
3.1
additionally imposes that
\Phi
has no flat region as we have
\nu>0
.
As a result, specifying the inverse time change
\Phi^{-1}:\mathbbm{R}^{+}\to\mathbbm{R}^{+}
as the right-continuous generalized inverse of
\Phi
, actually yields a continuous function
\Psi
.
It is then clear that
\Phi
is the right-continuous inverse of
\Psi
.

Definition 3.2
.

Given a time change
\Phi
in
\mathcal{T}
, the inverse time change
\Phi^{-1}:[\xi_{0},\infty)\to[-\epsilon,\infty)
is defined as the continuous function

\displaystyle\sigma\mapsto\Psi(\sigma)=\Phi^{-1}(\sigma)=\inf\left\{t\geq 0\,|\,\Phi(t)>\sigma\right\}\,.

Importantly, valid time changes include function
\Phi
with discontinuous jumps—or equivalently flat regions for
\Psi
.
Such discontinuities will correspond to the occurrence of synchronous events, at those times for which inactivation on the absorbing boundary has finite probability.
The key to unlocking these synchronous events is that the time change
\Phi
maps the dynamics of an eventually singular, interacting dynamics onto that of a constitutively regular, noninteracting one.
When unfolding along the new time coordinate
\sigma=\Phi(t)
, this regular dynamics will only depend on
\Phi
via the time wrapping of the refractory period
\epsilon
.
Such time wrapping is captured by the so-called backward-delay function, which is defined as follows:

Definition 3.3
.

Given the time change
\Phi
in
\mathcal{T}
, we define the corresponding backward-delay function
\eta:[0,\infty)\to\mathbbm{R}^{+}
by

\displaystyle\eta(\sigma)=\sigma-\Phi\left(\Psi(\sigma)-\epsilon\right)\,,\quad\sigma\geq 0\,.

We denote the set of backward functions
\{\eta[\Phi]\}_{\Phi\in\mathcal{T}}
by
\mathcal{W}
.

As
w_{\Phi}\geq\nu
for all
\Phi
in
\mathcal{T}
, it is clear that for all
\eta
in
\mathcal{W}
, we actually have
\eta\geq\nu\epsilon
, so that all delays are bounded away from zero.
Time-wrapped-delay function
\eta
in
\mathcal{W}
will serve to parametrize the time-changed dynamics obtained via
\Phi
in
\mathcal{T}
.
These time-changed dynamics will be that of a modified Wiener process
Y_{\sigma}
with negative unit drift, inactivation on the zero boundary, and reset in
\Lambda
after a refractory period specified by
\eta
.
Consequently, we define time-changed dynamics as the processes
Y_{\sigma}
solutions to the following stochastic evolution:

Definition 3.4
.

Denoting the canonical Wiener process by
W_{\sigma}
, we define the time-changed processes
Y_{\sigma}
as solutions to the stochastic evolution

\displaystyle Y_{\sigma}=-\sigma+\int_{0}^{\sigma}\mathbbm{1}_{\{Y_{\xi^{-}}>0\}}\,\mathrm{d}W_{\xi}+\Lambda N_{\sigma-\eta(\sigma)}\,,\quad\mathrm{with}\quad N_{\sigma}=\sum_{n>0}\mathbbm{1}_{[\xi_{0},t]}(\xi_{n})\,,

(23)

where the process
N_{\sigma}
counts the successive first-passage times
\xi_{n}
of the process
Y_{\sigma}
to the absorbing boundary:

\displaystyle\xi_{n+1}=\inf\left\{\sigma>0\,\big|\,\sigma-\eta(\sigma)>\xi_{n},Y_{\sigma}\leq 0\right\}\,.

A time-changed process
Y_{\sigma}
is uniquely specified by imposing elementary initial condition, which takes an alternative formulation: either the process is active
Y_{0}=x>0
and
N_{0}=0
, either the process has entered refractory period at some earlier time
\xi
so that
Y_{0}=0
and
N_{\sigma}=\mathbbm{1}_{\sigma\geq\xi}
for
\xi_{0}\leq\sigma<0
.
Generic initial conditions are given by considering that
(x,\xi)
is sampled from some probability distribution on
\{(0,\infty)\times\{0\}\}\cup\{\{0\}\times[\xi_{0},0)\}
.
This amounts to choosing a normalized pair of distributions
(q_{0},g_{0})
in
\mathcal{M}(\mathbbm{R}^{+})\times\mathcal{M}([\xi_{0},0))
.
The ensuing dynamics is well-defined as long as the backward-delay function
\eta\geq\nu\epsilon
is locally bounded, which is always the case for valid time change
\Phi
.
Of particular interest is the fact that such dynamics can accommodate jump discontinuities in
\eta
.
This is perhaps best seen by considering the so-called backward-time function
\mathbbm{R}^{+}\to[\xi_{0},\infty)
,
\sigma\mapsto\sigma-\eta(\sigma)
, featured in the time-delayed counting process of (
23
).
When unambiguous, we will denote this backward-time function by
\xi
and refer to it as the “function
\xi
” to differentiate from when
\xi
plays the role of a real variable.
By construction, the function
\xi
satisfies
\xi(\sigma)=\sigma-\eta(\sigma)=\Phi\left(\Psi(\sigma)-\epsilon\right)
, and is thus a nondecreasing
càdlàg
function, possibly admitting discontinuities and flat regions.
Specifically, denoting by
\mathcal{D}_{\Phi}
the countable set of discontinuous time of
\Phi
in
[-\epsilon,\infty)
, the function
\xi
has discontinuities on the countable set

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\xi}=\cup_{t\in\mathcal{D}_{\Phi}}\{\inf\Psi^{-1}(\{t+\epsilon\})\}=\cup_{t\in\mathcal{D}_{\Phi}}\{\inf\{\sigma\,|\,\Psi(\sigma)=t+\epsilon\}\}\,,

whereas it is flat on the countable disjoint union of discontinuity intervals of
\Phi
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{\xi}=\mathcal{J}_{\Psi}=\cup_{t\in\mathcal{D}_{\Phi}}\{\Psi^{-1}(\{t\})\}=\cup_{t\in\mathcal{D}_{\Phi}}[\Phi(t^{-}),\Phi(t)]\,.

The discontinuities and flat regions of the function
\xi
will play the central part in explaining the occurrence of synchronous events in the original dPMF dynamics from analyzing the dynamics of
Y_{\sigma}
.
In this perspective, it is worth completing the time-changed picture of the dPMF dynamics by stating the PDE problem attached to the dynamics of
Y_{\sigma}
:

Definition 3.5
.

Given a backward-delay function
\eta
in
\mathcal{W}
and some normalized initial conditions
(q_{0},g_{0})
in
\mathcal{M}(\mathbbm{R}^{+})\times\mathcal{M}([\xi_{0},0))
,
\xi_{0}=-\eta(0)
, the density function
(\sigma,x)\mapsto q(\sigma,x)=\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}\left[0<Y_{\sigma}\leq x\right]/\mathrm{d}x
solves the time-changed PDE problem

\displaystyle\partial_{\sigma}q

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\partial_{x}q+\frac{1}{2}\partial^{2}_{x}q+\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\sigma}[G(\sigma-\eta(\sigma))]\delta_{\Lambda}\,,

(24)

with absorbing and conservation conditions respectively given by

\displaystyle q(\sigma,0)=0\quad\mathrm{and}\quad g(\sigma)=\partial_{\sigma}G(\sigma)=\partial_{x}q(\sigma,0)/2\,.

(25)

We refer to the time-changed PDE problem
3.5
as a regularized one because the emergence of synchrony will only involves discontinuities in the backward-delay function rather than diverging drift and diffusion coefficients.
This is obvious from the fact that (
24
) features constant unit drift and diffusion coefficients, so that all the interactions present in the original problem will be mediated by
\eta
in the time-changed dynamics.
The boundary condition (
25
) identifies
g
as the absorbing boundary flux of
Y_{\sigma}
, i.e., as its instantaneous inactivation rate.
Because of the nonhomogeneity of
\eta
, the reset rate with which
Y_{\sigma}
activates is generally distinct from the inactivation rate, as shown by the prefactor of the Dirac-delta source term in (
24
).
Technically, this prefactor is defined as the Radon-Nikodym derivative of the measure specified by the cumulative function
\sigma\mapsto G(\sigma-\eta(\sigma))
with respect to the Lebesgue measure on
[0,\infty)
.
This definition is justified by the fact that for all
\Phi
in
\mathcal{T}
,
\sigma\mapsto\sigma-\eta(\sigma)=\Phi\left(\Psi(\sigma)-\epsilon\right)
is a nondecreasing function and that
G
is defined as a cumulative flux function.
Moreover, this definition allows for possibly discontinuous backward-delay functions
\eta
, as we will see that
G
is uniquely determined as smooth function in the next section.

3.2
Time-inhomogeneous renewal process

The cumulative flux
G
is instrumental in specifying the dynamics of the time-changed process
Y_{\sigma}
, whose density function solves the PDE problem
3.5
.
Given generic initial conditions, it is clear that
G
shall only depend on the backward-delay functions
\eta
.
In the next section, we will make this
\eta
-dependence explicit by adapting results from elementary renewal analysis.
As a preliminary to this objective, we devote this section to exhibiting the renewal character of the time-changed dynamics.
In this perspective, let us introduce
\{\tau_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
, the increasing sequence of reset times to be distinguished from the sequence of inactivation times
\{\xi_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
, with the convention that
\tau_{0}=0
.
These newly-introduced reset times can also be defined in terms of the backward-delay function
\eta
as:

Proposition 3.1
.

Given a backward-delay function
\eta
in
\mathcal{W}
, the reset times
\{\tau_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
of the time-changed dynamics
Y_{\sigma}
satisfy

\displaystyle\tau_{k}=\tau(\xi_{k})\,,\quad\mathrm{with}\quad\tau(\xi)=\inf\{\sigma>0\,|\,\xi(\sigma)=\sigma-\eta(\sigma)\geq\xi\}\,,

(26)

where the forward function
\tau
is the left-continuous generalized inverse of the nondecreasing function
\xi=\mathrm{id}-\eta
.

Again, just as for the function
\xi
, we will refer to the “function
\tau
” when
\tau
designates the forward function defined in (
26
) rather than a real variable.

This follows from the fact that the delayed process
N_{\sigma-\eta(\sigma)}
involved in (
23
) results from the composition of the
\emph{c\`{a}dl\`{a}g}
counting process
N_{t}
with the
\emph{c\`{a}dl\`{a}g}
nondecreasing function
\xi=\mathrm{id}-\eta
.
To see why, suppose that
Y_{\sigma}
inactivates in
\xi_{1}
, i.e., that
N_{\sigma}
has a jump discontinuity in
\xi_{1}
.
Then,
Y_{\sigma}
remains in zero for
\sigma>\xi_{1}
until the first discontinuity time of the reset counting process
N_{\xi(\sigma)}
.
If
\xi^{-1}(\{\xi_{1}\})=\{\tau_{1}\}
is a singleton, we set
\tau(\xi_{1})=\tau_{1}
.
Otherwise,
\xi^{-1}(\{\xi_{1}\})
is an interval including its left endpoint denoted by
\tau_{1}
.
By right-continuity of
N_{\sigma}
, the first discontinuity time of the composed process
N_{\xi(\sigma)}
must be
\tau_{1}
, which is defined as:

\displaystyle\tau_{1}=\inf\{\sigma>0\,|\,\xi(\sigma)=\xi_{1}\}=\inf\{\sigma>0\,|\,\xi(\sigma)\geq\xi_{1}\}\,.

This justifies defining the function
\tau
as the left-continuous generalized inverse of
\xi
.
∎

By definition (
26
), for all
k\geq 1
,
\tau_{k}
satisfies
\tau_{k}-\xi_{k}\geq\eta(\tau_{k})
with equality if
\xi_{k}
is a continuity point of
\tau
.
Otherwise, we can only say that
\tau_{k}-\xi_{k}\geq\eta(\tau_{k})
. Thus the refractory period of
Y_{\sigma}
does not necessarily coincide with the backward-delay function
\eta
at the reset time.
However, if
\eta
is uniformly bounded by
\|\eta\|_{0,\infty}
on
\mathbbm{R}^{+}
, we have

\displaystyle\tau(\xi)\leq\inf\Big\{\sigma>0\,\Big|\,\sigma-\sup_{s\geq 0}\eta(s)\geq\xi\Big\}=\xi+\|\eta\|_{0,\infty}\,,

(27)

Thus, in general, we have
\eta(\tau_{k})\leq\tau_{k}-\xi_{k}\leq\|\eta\|_{0,\infty}
.

Clarifying the possible continuity issues of the time-changed dynamics motivates introducing one more delay function, the so-called forward-delay function defined by
\gamma=\tau-\mathrm{id}
.
By contrast with the backward-delay function,
\gamma
allows us to consider the refractory period as a function of the inactivation time:
\tau_{k}-\xi_{k}=\tau(\xi_{k})-\xi_{k}=\gamma(\xi_{k})
.
Backward and forward delay functions are naturally related via the following properties:

Proposition 3.2
.

(i)
For all backward delay functions
\eta
in
\mathcal{W}
, the forward-delay function
\gamma
is specified by:

\displaystyle\forall\;\xi\geq\xi_{0}\,,\quad\gamma(\xi)=\inf\{\sigma>0\,|\,\sigma\geq\eta(\sigma+\xi)\}\geq\nu\epsilon\,.

(28)

(ii)
Given two backward-delay function
\eta_{a}
and
\eta_{b}
in
\mathcal{W}
with
\eta_{a}\geq\eta_{b}
, their corresponding forward-delay functions
\gamma_{a}
and
\gamma_{b}

satisfy
\gamma_{a}\geq\gamma_{b}
.

(i)
By definition of the forward function
\tau
in (
26
), for all
\xi\leq\xi_{0}
:

\displaystyle\gamma(\xi)=\tau(\xi)-\xi=\inf\{\sigma>\xi\,|\,\sigma-\eta(\sigma)\geq\xi\}-\xi=\inf\{\sigma>0\,|\,\sigma\geq\eta(\sigma+\xi)\},.

In particular, we have
\gamma(\xi)\geq\inf_{\sigma\geq 0}\eta(\sigma)\geq\nu\epsilon
.

(ii)
If
\eta_{a}\geq\eta_{b}
, we have
\{\sigma>\,|\,\sigma\geq\eta_{a}(\sigma+\xi)\}\subset\{\sigma>\,|\,\sigma\geq\eta_{b}(\sigma+\xi)\}
so that by the characterization given in
(i)
, we have
\gamma_{a}\geq\gamma_{b}
.
∎

Notice that characterization (
28
) together with (
27
) implies that
\|\eta\|_{0,\infty}=\|\gamma\|_{0,\infty}
.

It is now straightforward to exhibit the renewal character of the dynamics of
Y_{\sigma}
.
Unless stated otherwise, we assume the initial condition
p_{\sigma_{0}}=\delta_{x}
,
x>0
, for simplicity.
Given a backward-delay functions
\eta
in
\mathcal{W}
, the sequences of times
\{\xi_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
and
\{\tau_{k}\}_{k\geq}
are interwoven, i.e.,
\xi_{0}<\tau_{0}=0<\xi_{1}<\tau_{1}<\xi_{2}<\tau_{2}\ldots
.
The refractory periods
\{\tau_{k}-\xi_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
are determined by the forward-delay function
\tau_{k}-\xi_{k}=\gamma(\xi_{k})
, which was precisely introduced to that end.
In between consecutive reset and inactivation times, the dynamics of the time-changed process
Y_{\sigma}
is simply that of a Wiener process with unit negative drift.
Thus, the random variables
\{\xi_{k+1}-\tau_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
are i.i.d. according to
\mathbb{P}\left[\xi_{k+1}-\tau_{k}\leq\sigma\right]=H(\sigma,\Lambda)
for all
k\geq 1
and to
\mathbb{P}\left[\tau_{1}\leq\sigma\,|\,Y_{0}=x\right]=H(\sigma,x)
otherwise, where
H
denotes the first-passage cumulative distribution
[
16
]

\displaystyle H(\sigma,x)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathrm{Erfc}\left(\frac{x-\sigma}{\sqrt{2\sigma}}\right)+e^{2x}\mathrm{Erfc}\left(\frac{x+\sigma}{\sqrt{2\sigma}}\right)\right)\,.

By convention, we set
H(\sigma,x)=0
for
\sigma<0
, so that
H
admits the density function

\displaystyle h(\sigma,x)=\partial_{\sigma}H(\sigma,x)=\mathbbm{1}_{\{\sigma\geq 0\}}\frac{xe^{-(x-\sigma)^{2}/2\sigma}}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{3}}}\,.

In turn, the inter-inactivation epochs
\{\xi_{k+1}-\xi_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}

are independently distributed according to time-inhomogeneous distributions:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\xi_{k+1}\leq\sigma\,|\,\xi_{k}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bar{H}_{\Lambda}(\sigma,\xi_{k})=H\big(\sigma-\tau(\xi_{k}),\Lambda\big)=H\big(\sigma-\xi_{k}-\gamma(\xi_{k}),\Lambda\big)\,,

This shows that the sequence
\{\xi_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
constitutes a time-inhomogenous renewal process.

Recognizing the renewal character of the time-changed dynamics
Y_{\sigma}
suggests that its associated cumulative flux
G
satisfies a renewal-type integral equation.
In order to establish this equation in the next section, we will need the following result, which shows that forward and backward functions
\xi
and
\tau
are well-behaved inverse functions of one another.

Proposition 3.3
.

Given a backward-delay function
\eta
in
\mathcal{W}
, for all
\sigma>0
, we have

\displaystyle\{\xi>\xi_{0}\,|\,\tau(\xi)=\xi+\gamma(\xi)\leq\sigma\}=\{\xi>\xi_{0}\,|\,\xi\leq\xi(\sigma)=\sigma-\eta(\sigma)\}\,.

In order to prove the proposed set identity, we use the following characterization:

\displaystyle\{\xi^{\prime}>\xi_{0}\,|\,\tau(\xi^{\prime})\leq\sigma\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{\xi^{\prime}>\xi_{0}\,|\,\inf\{\tau^{\prime}>\xi^{\prime}\,|\,\xi(\tau^{\prime})\geq\xi^{\prime}\}\leq\sigma\}\,,

(29)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\cap}_{n\geq 1}\left\{\xi^{\prime}>\xi_{0}\,|\,\exists\,\tau^{\prime}>\xi^{\prime}\,,\;\xi(\tau^{\prime})\geq\xi^{\prime},\tau^{\prime}\leq\sigma+1/n\right\}\,,

(30)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\cap}_{n\geq 1}\left\{\xi^{\prime}>\xi_{0}\,|\,\exists\,\tau^{\prime}>\xi^{\prime}\,,\;\xi(\tau^{\prime})\geq\xi^{\prime},\sigma\leq\tau^{\prime}\leq\sigma+1/n\right\}\,,

(31)

where the last equality follows from the fact that
\xi=\mathrm{id}-\eta
is nondecreasing.

Consider
\xi^{\prime}>\xi_{0}
such that
\xi^{\prime}\leq\xi(\sigma)
, then for all
n\geq 1
, take any
\tau^{\prime}
such that
\sigma\leq\tau^{\prime}\leq\sigma+1/n
, we have
\xi^{\prime}\leq\xi(\sigma)=\sigma-\eta(\sigma)<\sigma\leq\tau^{\prime}
and
\xi(\tau^{\prime})\geq\xi(\sigma)\geq\xi^{\prime}
.
Thus
\{\xi^{\prime}>\xi_{0}\,|\,\xi^{\prime}\leq\xi(\sigma)\}\subset\{\xi^{\prime}>\xi_{0}\,|\,\tau(\xi^{\prime})\leq\sigma\}
.

Reciprocally, consider
\xi^{\prime}>\xi_{0}
such that
\tau(\xi^{\prime})\leq\sigma
. Then by characterization (
29
), there is a sequence
\{\tau_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
such that
\xi(\tau_{n})\geq\xi^{\prime}
and
\sigma\leq\tau_{n}\leq\sigma+1/n
.
Suppose that
\xi^{\prime}>\xi(\sigma)
, we then have

\displaystyle\xi(\sigma)<\xi^{\prime}\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\xi(\tau_{n})=\lim_{\tau\to\sigma^{+}}\xi(\tau)\,,

which contradicts the right continuity of
\xi
.
Thus we must have
\xi^{\prime}\leq\xi(\sigma)
, which means that
\{\xi^{\prime}>\xi_{0}\,|\,\tau(\xi^{\prime})\leq\sigma\}\subset\{\xi^{\prime}>\xi_{0}\,|\,\xi^{\prime}\leq\xi(\sigma)\}
.
∎

A direct consequence of the above proposition is that
\mathbb{P}\left[\tau(\xi)\leq\sigma\right]=\mathbb{P}\left[\xi\leq\sigma-\eta(\sigma)\right]
. This result will feature prominently in establishing a renewal-type equation for the cumulative flux function
G
.

3.3
Quasi-renewal equation

Here, our goal is to adapt elementary results from renewal analysis to characterize the cumulative flux
G
as the unique (smooth) solution of a renewal-type equation.
For the elementary initial condition
q_{0}=\delta_{x}
,
this renewal-type equation can be deduced from the representation of
G
as a probabilistic series

\displaystyle G(\sigma)=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{\xi_{k}<\sigma\right\}}\,\bigg|\,Y_{0}=x\right]=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left[\xi_{k}\leq\sigma\,|\,Y_{0}=x\right]\,.

(32)

Observe that by nonnegativity of forward-delay functions
\gamma
, each of the probabilities involved in the series is upper bounded by its counterpart in the compactly converging series representation without delay:
\mathbb{P}\left[\xi_{k}\leq\sigma\,|\,Y_{0}=x\right]\leq H(\sigma,x+(k-1)\Lambda)
.
This justifies the validity of the series representation (
32
).
Moreover, by divisibility of the first-passage distribution, one can check that

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}H(\sigma,x+(k-1)\Lambda)=H(\sigma,x)+\int H(\sigma-\tau,\Lambda)\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}H(\tau,x+(k-1)\Lambda)\,\mathrm{d}\tau\,,

yielding the classical renewal integral equation satisfied by
G
in the absence of delays. We extend this result for nonzero delays in the following proposition:

Proposition 3.4
.

Given a backward-delay function
\eta
in
\mathcal{W}
, the cumulative flux function
G
associated to the PDE problem
3.5
is the unique solution of the renewal-type equation:

\displaystyle G(\sigma)=\int_{0}^{\infty}H(\sigma,x)q_{0}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{0}^{\sigma}H(\sigma-\tau,\Lambda)\,\mathrm{d}G(\tau-\eta(\tau))\,.

(33)

It is enough to show the result for the elementary initial condition
q_{0}=\delta_{x}
.
Let us consider
\xi_{k+1}
, the
(k+1)
-th inactivation time of
Y_{\sigma}
, which is necessarily preceded by the
k
-th reset time:
\xi_{k+1}>\tau_{k}>0
.
Conditioning on
\tau_{k}
for
k\leq 1
yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\xi_{k+1}\leq\sigma\,|\,Y_{0}=x\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{P}\left[\xi_{k+1}\leq\sigma\,|\,\tau_{k}\right]\,|\,Y_{0}=x\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[H(\sigma-\tau_{k},\Lambda)\,|\,Y_{0}=x\right]\,,

where the last equality uses the fact that
\{\xi_{k+1}-\tau_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
are i.i.d. according to the distribution
H(\cdot,\Lambda)
.
Thus, singling out the first term, the series representation of
G
given in (
32
) reads

\displaystyle G(\sigma)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\xi_{1}\leq\sigma\,|\,Y_{0}=x\right]+\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[H(\sigma-\tau_{k-1},\Lambda)\,|\,Y_{0}=x\right]\,,

(34)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle H(\sigma,x)+\int_{0}^{\sigma}H(\sigma-\tau,\Lambda)\,\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}\left[\tau_{k}\leq\tau\,|\,Y_{0}=x\right]\,.

We conclude by expressing the series appearing as an integrator function above in terms of
G
.
Invoking Proposition
3.3
, we have the compact convergence

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left[\tau_{k}\leq\sigma\,|\,Y_{0}=x\right]=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left[\xi_{k}\leq\sigma-\eta(\sigma)\,|\,Y_{0}=x\right]=G(\sigma-\eta(\sigma))\,,

which leads to (
33
) upon substitution in (
34
).

To show uniqueness, suppose
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
both solves (
33
).
Then,
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
are necessarily smooth functions with derivatives
g_{1}
and
g_{2}
satisfying

\displaystyle g_{1}(\sigma)-g_{2}(\sigma)=\int_{0}^{\sigma}h(\sigma-\tau,\Lambda)\mathrm{d}\big[G_{1}(\xi(\tau))-G_{2}(\xi(\tau)))\,.

Introducing the function
\tau(\xi)
allows on to perform the generalized change of variable
\xi=\xi(\tau)
.
For small enough
\sigma>0
, such a change of variable yields:

\displaystyle|g_{1}(\sigma)-g_{2}(\sigma)|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bigg|\int_{0}^{\sigma}h(\sigma-\tau(\xi),\Lambda)\mathrm{d}\big[G_{1}(\xi)-G_{2}(\xi))\bigg|\,,

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\sigma}h(\sigma-\tau(\xi),\Lambda)\big|g_{1}(\xi)-g_{2}(\xi))\big|\,\mathrm{d}\xi\,,

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(\int_{0}^{\sigma}h(\sigma-\tau(\xi),\Lambda)\mathrm{d}\xi\right)\sup_{0\leq\xi\leq\sigma}|g_{1}(\xi)-g_{2}(\xi)|\,,

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sigma\|h(\cdot,\Lambda)\|_{\infty}|g_{1}(\xi)-g_{2}(\xi)|\,,

where
\|h(\cdot,\Lambda)\|_{\infty}
denotes the finite infinity norm of
\sigma\mapsto h(\sigma,\Lambda)
.
This inequality establishes that
g_{1}=g_{2}
on any interval
[0,\sigma)
with
\sigma<1/\|h(\cdot,\Lambda)\|_{\infty}
.
This local uniqueness result transfers to
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
by virtue of
G_{1}(0)=G_{2}(0)=0
.
Finally, global uniqueness can be recovered by standard methods of continuation.
∎

The above result makes it clear that as a solution to (
33
), the cumulative flux function
G
inherits all the regularity properties of
\sigma\mapsto H(\sigma,\Lambda)
, i.e.,
G
is a smooth function for
\sigma>0
.
Moreover, with
G
specified as a solution to (
33
), the full solution of the inhomogeneous PDE (
24
) can be expressed in terms of the corresponding homogeneous solutions by Duhamel’s principle.
These homogeneous solutions are known in closed form
[
16
]
:

\displaystyle\kappa(\sigma,y,x)=\frac{e^{-\frac{(y-x+\sigma)^{2}}{2\sigma}}}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma}}\left(1-e^{-\frac{2xy}{\sigma}}\right)\,.

Thus, Proposition
3.4
admits the following corollary:

Corollary 3.6
.

Given a backward-delay function
\eta
in
\mathcal{W}
and normalized initial conditions
(q_{0},g_{0})
in
\mathcal{M}(\mathbbm{R}^{+})\times\mathcal{M}([\xi_{0},0))
, there is a unique solution density function
(\sigma,x)\mapsto q(\sigma,x)=\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}\left[0<Y_{\sigma}\leq x\right]/\mathrm{d}x
to the PDE problem
3.5
which admits the integral representation

\displaystyle q(\sigma,y)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\kappa(\sigma,y,x)q_{0}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{0}^{\sigma}\kappa(\sigma-\tau,y,\Lambda)\,\mathrm{d}G(\tau-\eta(\tau))\,,

where
G
is the solution to the renewal-type equation (
33
).

The above renewal analysis has allowed us to justify the existence, uniqueness, and regularity of time-changed dynamics assuming the backward-delay function
\eta
known.
However,
\eta
is actually an unknown of the problem for being ultimately defined in term of the time change
\Phi
via Definition
3.3
.
We devote the next section to exhibiting under which conditions
\eta[\Phi]
parametrizes an admissible dPMF dynamics.

4
Fixed-point problem

In this section, we establish our main result, Theorem
1.2
, by establishing that dPMF dynamics are equivalent to certain constitutively well-posed time-changed dynamics.
To do so, for any candidate time change
\Phi
in
\mathcal{T}
, we consider the time-changed dynamics
Y_{\sigma}
that is uniquely defined by the backward delay function
\eta[\Phi]
in
\mathcal{W}
.
Then, we look for possibly explosive, weak solutions to our original PDE problem
2.1
under the form
(t,x)\mapsto q[\Phi](\Phi(t),\sigma)
, where
q[\Phi]
is the density function of the process
Y_{\sigma}
.
This leads to exhibiting a natural condition on
\Phi
to parametrize such a weak solution, as stated in the following propositon:

Proposition 4.1
.

Given a time change
\Phi
in
\mathcal{T}
, let

be the solution of the time-changed PDE problem

associated with
\eta[\Phi]
in
\mathcal{W}

, weakly solves the PDE problem

if and only if

\displaystyle\forall\;t\geq 0\,,\quad\Phi(t)=\nu t+\lambda G(\Phi(t))\,.

(35)

In particular, the cumulative flux function of the dPMF dynamics is given by

.

We proceed in two steps:
(i)
we characterize the time-changed dynamics associated with

as a solution to a weak PDE problem,
(ii)
we show that this weak formulation is equivalent to that of Definition
2.2
if and only if the proposed criterion holds.

(i)
As a solution to the PDE problem
3.5
,

is also a weak solution in the usual sense:
for all
S>0
and for all test functions

in
C_{0,\infty}([-\xi_{0},S)\times\mathbbm{R})
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{S}\int_{0}^{\infty}\left[\mathcal{L}[q](\sigma,x)+\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\sigma}[G(\sigma-\eta(\sigma))]\delta_{\Lambda}-\partial_{\sigma}q(\sigma,x)\right]v(\sigma,x)\,\mathrm{d}\sigma\mathrm{d}x\,.

In turn, performing integration by parts in the distributional sense yields

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\big[q(S,x)v(S,x)-q(0,x)v(0,x)\big]\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hskip-140.0pt\int_{0}^{S}\int_{0}^{\infty}q(\sigma,x)\big[\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}[v](\sigma,x)+\partial_{\sigma}v(\sigma,x)\big]\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}\sigma

\displaystyle\hskip-140.0pt+\int_{0}^{S}v(\sigma,\Lambda)\,\mathrm{d}G(\sigma-\eta(\sigma))-\int_{0}^{S}v(\sigma,0)\,\mathrm{d}G(\sigma)\,.

Our goal is to perform the change of variable
\sigma=\Phi(t)
to recover the weak form of solutions from Definition
2.2
.
Given any nondecreasing function

and

with

right-continuous, for any bounded Borel measurable function

[

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,\mathrm{d}(N\circ M)(x)=\int_{M(a)}^{M(b)}f(X(y))\,\mathrm{d}N(y)\,,

where

.
Moreover, if

is continuous,

can be any generalized inverse of

.
Thus, we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{S}v(\sigma,\Lambda)\,\mathrm{d}G(\sigma-\eta(\sigma))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{S}v(\sigma,\Lambda)\,\mathrm{d}(G\circ\Phi)(\Psi(\sigma)-\epsilon)\,,

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{S}v(\Phi(t^{-}),\Lambda)\,\mathrm{d}(G\circ\Phi)(t-\epsilon)\,.

Therefore, defining

and

, we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\big[q(\Phi(T),x)u(T,x)-q(\Phi(0),x)u(0,x)\big]\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hskip-240.0pt\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{\infty}q(\Phi(t^{-}),x)\big[\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}[u](t^{-},x)+\partial_{\sigma}v(\Phi(t^{-}),x)\big]\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}\Phi(t)

\displaystyle\hskip-240.0pt+\int_{0}^{T}u(t^{-},\Lambda)\,\mathrm{d}(G\circ\Phi)(t-\epsilon)-\int_{0}^{T}u(t^{-},0)\,\mathrm{d}(G\circ\Phi)(t)\,.

Given a smooth test function

, for all

, the function

is a function with bounded variation.
Accordingly, we can apply the generalized chain rule involving Vol’pert superposition principle
[

,

]
to obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}u}{\mathrm{d}t}(\cdot,x)=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}[v(\Phi(\cdot),x)]=\partial_{\sigma}\hat{v}(\Phi,x)(\cdot)\frac{\mathrm{d}\Phi}{\mathrm{d}t}\,,

where

denotes the average superposition of

:

\displaystyle\partial_{\sigma}\hat{v}(\Phi,x)(t)=\int_{0}^{1}\partial_{\sigma}v\big(\Phi(t^{-})+z(\Phi(t)-\Phi(t^{-}))\big)\,\mathrm{d}z\,.

Now, we can always restrict our choice of test functions

to these functions in
C_{0,\infty}([-\epsilon,\infty)\times\mathbbm{R})
such that for all

in the discontinuity set
\mathcal{D}_{\Phi}
, we have

\displaystyle v(\Phi(t^{-}))=v(\Phi(t))\quad\mathrm{and}\quad\partial_{\sigma}v(\Phi(t^{-}))=\partial_{\sigma}v(\Phi(t))=\partial_{\sigma}\hat{v}(\Phi,x)(t)\,,

so that
t\mapsto u(x,t)
is continuously differentiable. This means in particular that for all test functions
u
in
C_{0,\infty}([-\epsilon,S)\times\mathbbm{R})
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\big[q(\Phi(T),x)u(T,x)-q(\Phi(0),x)u(0,x)\big]\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=

(36)

\displaystyle\hskip-240.0pt\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{\infty}q(\Phi(t^{-}),x)\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}[u](t,x)\,\mathrm{d}\Phi(t)+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{\infty}q(\Phi(t^{-}),x)\partial_{t}u(t,x)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\hskip-240.0pt+\int_{0}^{T}u(t,\Lambda)\,\mathrm{d}(G\circ\Phi)(t-\epsilon)-\int_{0}^{T}u(t,0)\,\mathrm{d}(G\circ\Phi)(t)\,.

(ii)
Let us now prove the proposition.
If
\Phi(t)=\nu t+\lambda G(\Phi(t))
, we directly see that substituting
\mathrm{d}\Phi(t)=\nu\,\mathrm{d}t+\lambda\,\mathrm{d}(G\circ\Phi)(t)
in the above equation shows that
(t,x)\mapsto p(t,x)=q(\Phi(t),x)
is a weak solution of PDE problem
2.1
for

.

Reciprocally, let
(t,x)\mapsto p(t,x)=q(\Phi(t),x)
be a weak solution of the PDE problem
2.1
.
Specifying the defining property of

as a weak solution for

with

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{\infty}\partial_{t}p(t,x)u(t)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t=\int_{0}^{T}u(t)\,\mathrm{d}F(t-\epsilon)-\int_{0}^{T}u(t)dF(t)\,,

whereas specifying (

yields an alternative expression for the same quantity

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{\infty}\partial_{t}q(\Phi(t),x)u(t)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t=\int_{0}^{T}u(t)\,\mathrm{d}(G\circ\Phi)(t-\epsilon)-\int_{0}^{T}u(t)\,\mathrm{d}(G\circ\Phi)(t)\,.

Moreover, for
\Psi
being the right-continuous generalized inverse of the strictly increasing function
\Phi,
we have
\Psi\circ\Phi=\mathrm{id}
on
\mathbbm{R}^{+}
and the initial condition

implies that

on
[-\epsilon,0)
.
Then, by the same reasoning as in Proposition

,

for all

.
In turn, subtracting (

) with

identified to

from equation (

identified to

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{\infty}p(t^{-},x)\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}[u](t,x)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}\Phi(t)=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{\infty}p(t^{-},x)\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}[u](t,x)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}\big[\nu t+\lambda F(t)\big]\,.

It is clear that the above equality holds for all smooth functions such that
\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}[u]
remains bounded on
[0,T)\times(0,\infty)
.
This observation allows us to specify the above equation for test functions of the form

with

in

, so that we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}(1-r(t))w(t)\,\mathrm{d}\big[\nu t+\lambda F(t)-\Phi(t)\big]=0\,,

where

is the probability of being in the refractory state at

.
As for all locally bounded backward-delay functions

,

remains bounded away from one, and both

and

are

functions, this implies that

\displaystyle\forall t\geq 0\,,\quad\Phi(t)=\nu t+\lambda F(t)=\nu t+\lambda G(\Phi(t))\,,

which concludes the proof.
∎

Proposition
4.1
shows that the existence and uniqueness of dPMF dynamics amounts to the existence and uniqueness of a time change
\Phi
in
\mathcal{T}
solving (
35
).
In this light, (
35
) appears as a self-consistent relation rather than a mere definition as in (
22
).
In fact, (
35
) defines a fixed-point problem satisfied by these time changes
\Phi
that parametrized dPMF dynamics.
The fixed-point nature of (
35
) follows from the fact that the cumulative flux function
G
involved in (
35
) is functionally dependent on
\Phi
via
\eta
.

To account for blowups, solutions
\Phi
to (
35
) are allowed to be discontinuous, which leads to possible degeneracy issues.
Indeed, as the cumulative flux
G
must be smooth,
\Phi
can only become discontinuous if (
35
) is degenerate in the sense that
\sigma=\nu t+G(\sigma)
admits multiple solutions
\sigma
for some times
t
.
These times at which (
35
) becomes degenerate will actually mark the occurrence of blowups in the original dPMF dynamics.
To avoid dealing with such degeneracies, it is actually desirable to reformulate the fixed-point problem in terms of the inverse time change.
This is because by contrast with the possibly discontinuous increasing function
\Phi
, the inverse time change
\Psi=\Phi^{-1}
is defined as a continuous nondecreasing function.
Such a formulation yields our main result stated in Theorem
1.2
, which directly follows from the following proposition:

Proposition 4.2
.

The inverse time-change
\Psi
parametrizes a dPMF dynamics if and only if it solves the fixed-point problem

\displaystyle\forall\;\sigma\geq 0\,,\quad\Psi(\sigma)=\sup_{0\leq\xi\leq\sigma}\big(\xi-\lambda G[\Psi](\xi)\big)/\nu\,.

(37)

Consider
\Phi
in
\mathcal{T}
solving (
35
). Then, for all
\sigma\geq 0
, writing
\sigma=\Phi(t)
and
t=\Psi(\sigma)
in (
35
), we have

\displaystyle\Psi(\sigma)=\big(\sigma-\lambda G[\Psi](\sigma)\big)/\nu=\sup_{0\leq\xi\leq\sigma}\big(\xi-\lambda G[\Psi](\xi)\big)/\nu\,,

where the last equality follows from
\Psi=\Phi^{-1}
being
càdlàg
increasing by definition of
\mathcal{T}
.

Reciprocally, consider
\Phi
solving the fixed-point problem (
37
).
Then,
\Phi
is necessarily a nonnegative,
càdlàg
, nondecreasing function for being defined as a running maximum function with
\Psi(0)=0
.
Moreover, the function
\sigma\mapsto\eta(\sigma)=\sigma-\Psi^{-1}(\Psi(\sigma)-\epsilon)
is a nonnegative,
càdlàg
function so that
G
is a well-defined cumulative function satisfying the renewal-type equation (
33
).
In particular
G
is a nondecreasing smooth function so that
w_{\Psi}
satisfies
w_{\Psi}\leq 1/\nu
. This shows that
\Phi=\Psi^{-1}
belongs to
\mathcal{T}
.
Finally, we conclude by observing that

\displaystyle\Phi(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\inf\Big\{\sigma\geq 0\,\Big|\,\Psi(\sigma)>t\Big\}\,,

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\inf\Big\{\sigma\geq 0\,\Big|\,\sup_{0\leq\xi\leq\sigma}\big(\xi-\lambda G[\Psi](\xi)\big)/\nu>t\Big\}\,,

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\inf\Big\{\sigma\geq 0\,\Big|\,\big(\sigma-\lambda G[\Psi](\sigma)\big)/\nu>t\Big\}\,.

Thus, by continuity of
G
, we have

\displaystyle\Phi(t)=\inf\Big\{\sigma\geq 0\,\Big|\,\big(\sigma-\lambda G[\Psi](\sigma)\big)/\nu=t\Big\}\,,

so that when
\Phi(t)
exists, it necessarily satisfies (
35
).
∎

The above proposition proves our main result stated in the introduction as Theorem
1.2
.
The next section establishes its practical usefulness by showing that the corresponding fixed-point problem admits solutions parametrizing explosive dPMF dynamics.

5
Local blowup solutions

In this section, we establish that for large enough interaction parameters, the fixed-point problem
4.2
locally admits solutions with analytically well-defined blowups.
To show this, we first define a contracting, regularized fixed-point map
\mathcal{F}_{\delta}
over an appropriately chosen Banach space of candidate functions.
We then show that
\Psi=\lim_{\delta\to 0^{+}}\Psi_{\delta}
, where
\Psi_{\delta}
uniquely solves the fixed-point equation
\Psi=\mathcal{F}_{\delta}[\Psi]
, defines locally the unique maximum smooth inverse time change up to the first putative blowup.
Finally, we analytically resolve the ensuing blowup episode by interpreting blowup in the time-changed picture as linear dynamics with absorption but without reset.

5.1
Regularized fixed-point problem

The direct resolution of the dPMF fixed-point problem
4.2
is not possible by standard analysis when allowing for discontinuous time changes.
To remedy this point, we consider a set of regularized fixed-point problems which approximates the original one, but for which delay functions will be continuous.
These approximate problems are defined on the following restricted space of candidate solutions:

Definition 5.1
.

Given a real
\delta
such that
0<\delta<1/\nu
and for all
\xi>0
, we restrict the candidate space for inverse time changes to

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\delta}([\xi_{0},\xi]))=\left\{\Psi\in C_{0}([\xi_{0},\xi])\,\Bigg|\,\begin{array}[]{ccc}\Psi(\xi)=\Psi_{0}(\xi)&,&\xi_{0}\leq\xi\leq 0\\
\delta\leq w_{\Psi}(y,x)\leq 1/\nu&,&0\leq x,y\leq\xi\end{array}\right\}\,,

where
w_{\Psi}
is the difference quotient
w_{\Psi}(y,x)=(\Psi(y)-\Psi(x))/(y-x)
.

For all
\xi>0
, the candidate space
\mathcal{C}_{\delta}([\xi_{0},\xi]))
is a Banach space with respect to the uniform norm, denoted by
\|\cdot\|_{\xi_{0},\xi}
.
Every candidate functions
\Psi
in
\mathcal{C}_{\delta}([\xi_{0},\infty))
can naturally serve as an inverse time change, i.e.,
\Psi^{-1}
belongs to
\mathcal{T}
.
Moreover, choosing
\delta>0
enforces that every
\Psi
in
\mathcal{C}_{\delta}([\xi_{0},\infty))
is a strictly increasing, continuous function on
\mathbbm{R}^{+}
, so that
\Psi^{-1}
is also a strictly increasing, continuous function on
\mathbbm{R}^{+}
with difference quotient satisfying
\nu\leq w_{\Psi^{-1}}(y,x)\leq 1/\delta
.
As a result, the forward function
\tau[\Psi]
is continuous in
C([\xi_{0},\infty))
and so is the backward function
\xi[\Psi]
in
C([0,\infty))
, being defined as:

\displaystyle\tau[\Psi](\xi)=\Psi^{-1}\big(\Psi(\xi)+\epsilon\big)\quad\mathrm{and}\quad\xi[\Psi](\sigma)=\Psi^{-1}\big(\Psi(\sigma)-\epsilon\big)\,.

In the absence of discontinuities, we have the equivalence
\tau=\tau[\Psi](\xi)\Leftrightarrow\xi[\Psi](\tau)=\xi
, which implies that
\gamma[\Psi](\xi)=\eta[\Psi](\tau)
.
The key reason to introduce the space
\mathcal{C}_{\delta}([\xi_{0},\xi]))
is the following uniform Lipschitz property:

Proposition 5.1
.

For all
\Psi
in
\mathcal{C}_{\delta}([\xi_{0},\infty))
, the map
\Psi\mapsto\xi[\Psi]
are
1/\delta
-Lipschitz with respect to the uniform norm on
\mathcal{C}_{\delta}([\xi_{0},\infty))
.

Every function
\Psi
in
\mathcal{C}_{\delta}([\xi_{0},\xi]))
admits a continuous inverse function
\Psi^{-1}
on
\mathbbm{R}^{+}
with bounded difference quotient such that
\nu\leq w_{\Psi^{-1}}\leq 1/\delta
.
Therefore, for all
\sigma>0
:

\displaystyle|\xi[\Psi_{a}](\sigma)-\xi[\Psi_{b}](\sigma)|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|\Psi^{-1}_{a}(\Psi_{a}(\sigma)-\epsilon)-\Psi^{-1}_{b}(\Psi_{b}(\sigma)-\epsilon)|\,

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle|\Psi_{a}(\sigma)-\Psi_{b}(\sigma)|/\delta\,.

∎

Our goal is to formulate the dPMF fixed-point problem
4.2
on the candidate Banach space
\mathcal{C}_{\delta}([\xi_{0},\infty))
.
However, it turns out that the natural fixed-point map

\displaystyle\Psi\mapsto\Big\{\sigma\mapsto\sup_{\xi_{0}\leq\xi\leq\sigma}\big(\xi-\lambda G[\Psi](\xi)\big)/\nu\Big\}\,.

does not stabilize
\mathcal{C}_{\delta}([\xi_{0},\infty))
in the sense that it can produce functions with difference quotient below
\delta
.
To define a fixed-point map that stabilizes
\mathcal{C}_{\delta}([\xi_{0},\infty))
, we need to introduce the function
\mathcal{S}_{\delta}:\mathcal{C}_{-\infty}([\xi_{0},\infty))\rightarrow\mathcal{C}_{\delta}([\xi_{0},\infty))
defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\delta}[\psi](\sigma)=\sup_{0\leq s\leq\sigma}\left\{\psi(s)-\delta s\right\}+\delta\sigma\,.

The stabilizing role of
\mathcal{S}_{\delta}
follows from noticing that for all
0\leq x\leq y
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\delta}[\psi](y)-\mathcal{S}_{\delta}[\psi](x)=\!\sup_{0\leq s\leq y}\left\{\psi(s)-\delta s\right\}-\!\sup_{0\leq s\leq x}\left\{\psi(s)-\delta s\right\}+\delta(y-x)\geq\delta(y-x)\,.

This shows that
w_{\mathcal{S}_{\delta}[\psi]}\geq\delta
, so that
\mathcal{S}_{\delta}
stabilizes
\mathcal{C}_{\delta}([\xi_{0},\infty))
from below.
In turn, we can show that composing the natural fixed-point map of Definition
4.2
with
\mathcal{S}_{\delta}
defines a well-posed map on
\mathcal{C}_{\delta}([\xi_{0},\infty))
.

With these conventions, the regularized fixed-point map is specified as follows.

Proposition 5.2
.

Given
\Psi
in
\mathcal{C}_{\delta}([\xi_{0},\infty))
and initial conditions
(q_{0},g_{0})
, setting

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\delta}[\Psi]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\delta}[\psi]\quad\mathrm{with}\quad\psi(\sigma)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}\displaystyle\frac{1}{\nu}\big(\sigma-\lambda G[\Psi](\sigma)\big)&\>\mathrm{if}&\sigma\geq 0\,,\\
\Psi_{0}(\sigma)&\>\mathrm{if}&\xi_{0}\leq\sigma<0\,.\end{array}\right.

defines a map from
\mathcal{C}_{\delta}([\xi_{0},\infty))
to
\mathcal{C}_{\delta}([\xi_{0},\infty))
such that
\psi
is a smooth function on
(0,\infty)
.

Let us check that for all
\Psi
in
\mathcal{C}_{\delta}([\xi_{0},\infty))
,
\mathcal{F}_{\delta}[\Psi]
is also in
\mathcal{C}_{\delta}([\xi_{0},\infty))
.
Given
\Psi
in
\mathcal{C}_{\delta}([\xi_{0},\infty))
,
\gamma[\Psi]
is a positive, continuous, bounded function, which represents a forward-delay function compatible with the initial condition
g_{0}
on
[\xi_{0},0)
.
In turn, we can interpret
G[\Psi]
as the associated cumulative flux, which is a smooth function on
(0,\infty)
for satisfying the renewal-type equation (
33
).
In particular,
G[\Psi]
is continuously differentiable on
C((0,\infty))
, with positive derivative denoted
g[\Psi]
.
Then, the definition of
\psi
in terms of

implies that
\psi
belongs to
\mathcal{C}_{-\infty}([\xi_{0},\infty))
, so that
\Psi=\mathcal{S}_{\delta}[\Psi]
belongs to
\mathcal{C}_{\delta}([\xi_{0},\infty))
.
∎

5.2
Contraction argument

We establish in the following proposition that the mapping
\mathcal{F}_{\delta}
from Proposition
5.2
is a contraction on the Banach spaces
\mathcal{C}_{\delta}([\xi_{0},\sigma))
for small enough
\sigma>0
.
The proof will rely on the fact that for
\sigma>0
smaller than the time-wrapped refractory periods, the mapping
\mathcal{F}_{\delta}
loses its renewal character.
Then, the Lipschitz continuity of the mapping
\Psi\mapsto\xi[\Psi]
on
\mathcal{C}_{\delta}([\xi_{0},\sigma])
and the vanishing behavior of the first-passage kernel
H
for small time will directly yield the result.

Proposition 5.3
.

For small enough
\sigma>0
, the map
\mathcal{F}_{\delta}
is a contraction on
\mathcal{C}_{\delta}([\xi_{0},\sigma))
for the uniform norm denoted by
\|\cdot\|_{0,\sigma}
.

We proceed in two steps:
(i)
we justify that
\mathcal{G}_{\delta}
induces a mapping
\mathcal{C}_{\delta}([\xi_{0},\sigma))\rightarrow\mathcal{C}_{\delta}([\xi_{0},\sigma))
that loses its renewal character for small enough
\sigma>0
;
(ii)
we show that for small enough
\sigma>0
,
\mathcal{F}_{\delta}
is a contraction for the uniform norm
\|\cdot\|_{0,\sigma}
, i.e., for all
\Psi_{a},\Psi_{b}
in
\mathcal{C}_{\delta}([\xi_{0},\sigma))
,
\big\|\mathcal{F}_{\delta}[\Psi_{a}]-\mathcal{F}_{\delta}[\Psi_{b}]\big\|_{0,\sigma}\leq K\|\Psi_{a}-\Psi_{b}\|_{0,\sigma}

with
K<1
.

(i)

The fact that
\mathcal{F}_{\delta}[\psi]
belongs to
\mathcal{C}_{\delta}([\xi_{0},\infty))
follows from Proposition
5.2
.
Let us show that
\mathcal{F}_{\delta}[\psi]
loses its renewal character when considered on
[0,\Psi^{-1}(\epsilon))
.
As
\delta\leq w_{\Psi}\leq 1/\nu
and
\Psi(0)=0
, we must have
\nu\epsilon\leq\Psi^{-1}(\epsilon)\leq 1/\delta
.
Moreover,
\Psi^{-1}(\epsilon)
is also defined as the time-changed refractory period after zero:

\displaystyle\Psi^{-1}(\epsilon)=\Psi^{-1}\left(\Psi(0)+\epsilon\right)=\gamma[\Psi](0)\,.

This implies that on
[0,\Psi^{-1}(\epsilon))
, no more than one first-hitting time may occur in the inhomogeneous renewal processes determined by
\gamma[\Psi]
.
Correspondingly, over the time interval
[0,\Psi^{-1}(\epsilon)]\supset[0,\nu\epsilon]
, the inhomogeneous renewal-type equation (
33
) loses its renewal character to read

\displaystyle G[\Psi](\sigma)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}H(\sigma,x)q_{0}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{0}^{\sigma}H(\sigma-\tau,\Lambda)\,\mathrm{d}G_{0}(\xi[\Psi](\tau))\,.

(40)

As the initial conditions prescribe
\Psi
to coincide with
\Psi_{0}
on
[\xi_{0},0]
, this means that for all

, we have

\displaystyle\xi[\Psi](\tau)=\Phi_{0}\left(\Psi(\tau)-\epsilon\right)\,,\quad 0\leq\tau\leq\nu\epsilon\,,

so that remembering that

allows one to write (
40
) as

\displaystyle G[\Psi](\sigma)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}H(\sigma,x)q_{0}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{0}^{\sigma}H(\sigma-\tau,\Lambda)\,\mathrm{d}F_{0}(\Psi(\tau)-\epsilon)\,.

This shows that specifying
\mathcal{F}_{\delta}[\Psi]
, on
[0,\sigma)
only requires knowledge of
\Psi
on
[0,\sigma)
for
\sigma\leq\nu\epsilon
.
Therefore,
\mathcal{F}_{\delta}
is a mapping
\mathcal{C}_{\delta}([\xi_{0},\sigma))\rightarrow\mathcal{C}_{\delta}([\xi_{0},\sigma))
for all
0<\sigma\leq\nu\epsilon
.

(ii)

Let us now consider two functions
\Psi_{a}
and
\Psi_{b}
in
\mathcal{C}_{\delta}([\xi_{0},\infty))
.
For ease of notation, indexation by

and

will indicate throughout the proof relation to
\Psi_{a}
and to
\Psi_{b}
, respectively.
For instance, we write
G_{a}=G[\Psi_{a}]
and
G_{b}=G[\Psi_{b}]
.
By
(i)
, both cumulative functions
G_{a}
and
G_{b}
satisfy a nonrenewal equation (

) with identical regular initial conditions on the interval
[0,\nu\epsilon]
.
As a result, the integral terms arising from
q_{0}
in (

) are identical for both
G_{a}
and
G_{b}
,
which allows one to write
\Delta G(\sigma)=G_{a}(\sigma)-G_{b}(\sigma)
for all

as

\displaystyle\Delta G(\sigma)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\sigma}H(\sigma-\tau,\Lambda)\,\mathrm{d}\big[F_{0}(\Psi_{a}(\tau)-\epsilon)-F_{0}(\Psi_{b}(\tau)-\epsilon)\big]\,.

where
F_{0}=G_{0}\circ\Phi_{0}
denotes the initial conditions for the cumulative flux of the original process
X_{t}
in
\mathcal{M}([-\epsilon,0))
.
With no loss of generality, let us assume that
t_{a}=\Psi_{a}(\sigma)\leq\Psi_{b}(\sigma)=t_{b}
.
Then, performing the change of variables

and
t=\Psi_{b}(\tau)
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\sigma}H(\sigma-\tau,\Lambda)\!\!\!\!\!\!

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\!\left[F_{0}\big(\Psi_{a}(\tau)-\epsilon\big)-F_{0}\big(\Psi_{b}(\tau)-\epsilon\big)\right]

(41)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t_{a}}H(\sigma-\Phi_{a}(t),\Lambda)\,\mathrm{d}F_{0}\big(t-\epsilon\big)-\int_{0}^{t_{b}}H(\sigma-\Phi_{b}(t),\Lambda)\,\mathrm{d}F_{0}\big(t-\epsilon\big)\,,

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t_{a}}\big[H(\sigma-\Phi_{a}(t),\Lambda)-H(\sigma-\Phi_{b}(\tau)\big]\,\mathrm{d}F_{0}\big(t-\epsilon\big)\,

\displaystyle-\int_{t_{a}}^{t_{b}}H(\sigma-\Phi_{b}(t),\Lambda)\,\mathrm{d}F_{0}\big(t-\epsilon\big)

In turn, performing the change of variables
\tau=\Psi_{a}(t)
in the first integral term of the equation above, denoted by

\displaystyle I_{1}(\sigma)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t_{a}}\big|H(\sigma-\Phi_{a}(t),\Lambda)-\,H(\sigma-\Phi_{b}(t),\Lambda)\big|\,\mathrm{d}F_{0}\big(t-\epsilon\big)\,,

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\sigma}\big|H(\sigma-\tau,\Lambda)-H(\sigma-\Phi_{b}(\Psi_{a}(\tau)),\Lambda)\big|\,\mathrm{d}F_{0}\big(\Psi_{a}(\tau)-\epsilon\big)\,,

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|h(\cdot,\Lambda)\|_{0,\sigma}\int_{0}^{\sigma}|\tau-\Phi_{b}(\Psi_{a}(\tau))|\,\mathrm{d}F_{0}\big(\Psi_{a}(\tau)-\epsilon\big)\,,

where the last inequality follows from the fact that
H(\cdot,\Lambda)
is
\|h(\cdot,\Lambda)\|_{0,\sigma}
-Lipschitz on
[0,\sigma]
.
Then, utilizing that
\Phi_{b}
is necessarily
1/\delta
-Lipschitz for
\Psi_{b}
in
\mathcal{C}_{\delta}([\xi_{0},\infty))
, we have

\displaystyle I_{1}(\sigma)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|h(\cdot,\Lambda)\|_{0,\sigma}\int_{0}^{\sigma}|\Phi_{b}(\Psi_{b}(\tau))-\Phi_{b}(\Psi_{a}(\tau))|\,\mathrm{d}F_{0}\big(\Psi_{a}(\tau)-\epsilon\big)\,,

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\|h(\cdot,\Lambda)\|_{0,\sigma}}{\delta}\int_{0}^{\sigma}|\Psi_{b}(\tau)-\Psi_{a}(\tau)|\,\mathrm{d}F_{0}\big(\Psi_{a}(\tau)-\epsilon\big)\,,

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\|h(\cdot,\Lambda)\|_{0,\sigma}}{\delta}\left[F_{0}(t_{a}-\epsilon\big)-F_{0}\big(-\epsilon)\right]\|\Psi_{a}-\Psi_{b}\|_{0,\sigma}

where the last inequality follows from the fact
F_{0}
is an increasing function.
The last integral term in (

, can be bounded via similar argument as

\displaystyle I_{2}(\sigma)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{t_{a}}^{t_{b}}H(\sigma-\Phi_{b}(t),\Lambda)\,\mathrm{d}F\big(t-\epsilon\big)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle H(\sigma-\Phi_{b}(t_{a}),\Lambda)\,\left[F(t_{b}-\epsilon\big)-F\big(t_{a}-\epsilon),\Lambda)\right]\,,

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|h(\cdot,\Lambda)\|_{0,\sigma}|\Phi_{b}(\Psi_{b}(\sigma))-\Phi_{b}(\Psi_{a}(\sigma))|\,\left[F(t_{b}-\epsilon\big)-F\big(t_{a}-\epsilon)\right]\,,

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\|h(\cdot,\Lambda)\|_{0,\sigma}}{\delta}\left[F(t_{b}-\epsilon\big)-F\big(t_{a}-\epsilon)\right]\,\|\Psi_{a}-\Psi_{b}\|_{0,\sigma}\,,

Since by conservation of probability
F(t_{b}-\epsilon\big)-F\big(-\epsilon)\leq 1
, we have for all
\sigma\geq 0

\displaystyle|\Delta G(\sigma)|=\frac{\lambda}{\nu}\big|I_{1}(\sigma)+I_{2}(\sigma)\big|\leq\frac{\lambda\|h(\cdot,\Lambda)\|_{0,\sigma}}{\nu\delta}\|\Psi_{a}-\Psi_{b}\|_{0,\sigma}\,.

Moreover, for all real valued functions
\psi_{a}
and
\psi_{b}
over
\mathbbm{R}^{+}
, we have

\displaystyle\big|\mathcal{S}_{\delta}(\psi_{a})-\mathcal{S}_{\delta}(\psi_{b})\big|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Big|\sup_{0\leq s\leq\sigma}\{\psi_{a}(s)-\delta s\}-\sup_{0\leq s\leq\sigma}\{\psi_{b}(s)-\delta s\}\Big|\,,

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\Big|\sup_{0\leq s\leq\sigma}\{\psi_{a}(s)-\psi_{b}(s)\}\Big|\,,

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sup_{0\leq s\leq\sigma}\big|\psi_{a}(s)-\psi_{b}(s)\big|=\|\psi_{a}-\psi_{b}\|_{0,\sigma}\,,

so that we have the inequality

\displaystyle\big|\mathcal{F}_{\delta}[\Psi_{b}](\sigma)-\mathcal{F}_{\delta}[\Psi_{a}](\sigma)\big|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\big|\mathcal{S}_{\delta}[(\mathrm{id}-\lambda G_{b})/\nu](\sigma)-\mathcal{S}_{\delta}[(\mathrm{id}-\lambda G_{a})/\nu](\sigma)\big|\,,

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\big\|\lambda G_{b}/\nu-\lambda G_{a}/\nu]\big\|_{0,\sigma}\,,

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda\|h(\cdot,\Lambda)\|_{0,\sigma}}{\nu\delta}\|\psi_{a}-\psi_{b}\|_{0,\sigma}\,.

We conclude by noticing that
\lim_{\sigma\to 0}h(\sigma,\Lambda)=0
, so that for small enough

,
K=\lambda\,h(\sigma,\Lambda)/(\nu\delta)<1
, which shows that the map
\mathcal{F}_{\delta}
is a contraction on the space
\mathcal{C}_{\delta}(0,\sigma)
for the uniform norm
\|\cdot\|_{0,\sigma}
.
∎

By the Banach fixed-point theorem, Proposition

implies the existence of a unique local solution to the regularized fixed-point problem of Proposition

.
The following corollary shows that for all

.

Corollary 5.2
.

For all finite refractory periods
\epsilon>0

, there is a unique global solution
\psi_{\delta}
in

.

For fixed

is locally contracting irrespective of the initial conditions.
More specifically, assuming the solution defined up to

, the continuation process past

with

serving as initial flux conditions,

∎

We are now in a position to exhibit a local solution to the dPMF fixed-point problem

by considering the function
\Psi=\lim_{\delta\to 0^{+}}\Psi_{\delta}
.
For this solution to be uniquely defined on a nonzero interval

Specifically, we assume that the density
q_{0}

q_{0}(0)=0
and
\partial_{x}q_{0}(0)/2<1/\lambda
.
This amounts to imposing that all solutions
\Psi_{\delta}
are such that
{\Psi^{\prime}_{0}}(0^{+})=1-\lambda\partial_{x}q_{0}(0)/2
is bounded away from zero, so that for all

for small enough

.
In turn, such a local solution

can be uniquely continued up to the first time

becomes zero, therefore giving a criterion to maximally define

.
This leads to the following proposition:

Theorem 5.3
.

For all normalized initial conditions
(q_{0},g_{0})
in
\mathcal{M}(\mathbbm{R}^{+})\times\mathcal{M}([\xi_{0},\infty))
such that
q_{0}(0)=0
and
\partial_{x}q_{0}(0)/2<1/\lambda
, there is a unique smooth solution to the fixed-point problem
\Psi=\mathcal{F}_{0}[\Psi]
up to the possibly infinite time

\displaystyle S_{1}=\inf\left\{\sigma>0\,\big|\,\Psi^{\prime}(\sigma)\leq 0\right\}=\inf\left\{\sigma>0\,\big|\,g[\Psi](\sigma)\geq 1/\lambda\right\}>0\,.

(42)

Suppose there exists a solution
\Psi
to the fixed-point problem
\Psi=\mathcal{F}_{0}[\Psi]
.
Then, the smooth function
\psi=(\mathrm{id}-\lambda G[\Psi])/\nu
in
\mathcal{C}_{0}([\xi_{0},\infty))
is such that
\Psi=\mathcal{S}_{0}[\psi]
with
\psi^{\prime}_{0}(0^{+})=\Psi^{\prime}_{0}(0^{+})=(1-\lambda\partial_{x}q_{0}(0)/2)/\nu>0
.
Let us then introduce the positive time

\displaystyle S_{1,\delta}=\inf\left\{\sigma>0\,\big|\,\psi^{\prime}(\sigma)\leq\delta\right\}>0\,,

so that
\Psi=\psi

For all

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\delta}[\Psi](\sigma)=\mathcal{S}_{\delta}\left[\big(\sigma-\lambda G[\Psi](\sigma)\big)/\nu\right]=\mathcal{S}_{\delta}\left[\psi\right]=\psi=\Psi\,,\quad 0\leq\sigma\leq S_{1,\delta}\,.

in

.
Thus, the time

\displaystyle S_{1,\delta}=\inf\left\{\sigma>0\,\big|\,\psi^{\prime}_{\delta}(\sigma)\geq\delta\right\}>0\,,\quad\mathrm{with}\quad\psi_{\delta}=\big(\sigma-\lambda G[\Psi_{\delta}](\sigma)\big)/\nu\,,

and

.
Moreover, for all

.
Finally, it remains to check that

is also defined as (

For all

, there is
\delta>0

, so that we have

.
There is nothing more to show if

.

5.3
Analytical characterization of full blowups

Assuming the dynamics starting at time zero to be initially smooth with
q_{0}(0)=0
and
\partial_{x}q_{0}(0)/2<1/\lambda
, the first blowup time
T_{1}
occurs when the cumulative flux
f
, or equivalently
\Phi^{\prime}=1+\lambda f
, first diverges, i.e.,

\displaystyle T_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\inf\left\{T>0\,\Big|\,\lim_{t\to T^{-}}\Phi^{\prime}(t)=\infty\right\}\,,

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\inf\left\{\Psi(S)>0\,\Big|\,\lim_{\sigma\to S^{-}}\Psi^{\prime}(\sigma)=0\right\}=\Psi(S_{1})>0\,.

Thus, in the time-changed picture, the blowup condition corresponds precisely to the definition of
S_{1}
, the terminal point of the interval over which Theorem
5.3
guarantees the existence of smooth, increasing solutions.
This justifies defining the following blowup conditions with respect to the time-changed dynamics:

Definition 5.4
.

A blowup occurs if
T_{1}=\Psi(S_{1})<\infty
which is equivalent to

\displaystyle S_{1}=\inf\big\{\sigma>0\,\big|\,g(\sigma)\geq 1/\lambda\big\}<\infty\,,

where
g=\partial_{\sigma}G
is the instantaneous inactivation flux for the time-changed process
Y_{\sigma}
.

As stated at the end of Section
2.3
, the emergence of blowup clearly depends on the initial conditions.
However, for large enough interaction parameter, blowups will generically occur in finite time.
To see this, consider for instance initial conditions of the form
(q_{0},g_{0})=(\delta_{x_{0}},0)
.
For such initial conditions, the fixed-point problem
\Psi=\mathcal{F}_{\delta}[\Psi]
admits an initially smooth solution
\Psi
with instantaneous flux
g
satisfying

\displaystyle g(\sigma)=h(\sigma,x_{0})+\int_{0}^{\sigma}h(\sigma-\tau,\Lambda)\,\mathrm{d}G(\xi(\tau))\geq h(\sigma,x)\,,

where
h(\sigma,x)=\partial_{\sigma}H(\sigma,x)\geq 0
represents the first-passage density to zero of a Wiener process started in
x
with negative unit drift:

\displaystyle h(\sigma,x)=\frac{x}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{3}}}\exp\left(-\frac{(x-\sigma)^{2}}{2\sigma}\right)\,.

(43)

Moreover, we have
\sup_{\sigma\geq 0}h(\sigma,x_{0})\geq h(x_{0},x_{0})=1/\sqrt{2\pi x_{0}}
.
This implies that the blowup condition
g(\sigma)\leq 1/\lambda
is satisfied in finite time whenever
\lambda>C_{x_{0}}=\sqrt{2\pi x_{0}}
.

From now on, let us consider that the blowup condition is first met in
S_{1}<\infty
.
On
[0,S_{1}]
, the inverse time change
\Psi
is a smooth function with
\lim_{\sigma\to S_{1}^{-}}\Psi^{\prime}(\sigma)=0
.
Thus, the diverging behavior of
f=(\Phi^{\prime}-\nu)/\lambda
is determined by the order of the first nonzero left-derivative of
\Psi
in
S_{1}
, which is always larger or equal to two.
In all generality, this order depends on the initial conditions.
However, for generic initial conditions, we expect that

\displaystyle\inf\left\{n\geq 1\,\Big|\,\lim_{\sigma\to S_{1}^{-}}\Psi^{(n)}(\sigma)\neq 0\right\}=2\,.

Moreover, given that we necessarily have
\Psi^{\prime}>0
on
[0,S_{1})
,
S_{1}
must be a local maximum so that the criterion
\lim_{\sigma\to S_{1}^{-}}\Psi^{(n)}(\sigma)\neq 0
is actually equivalent to
\lim_{\sigma\to S_{1}^{-}}\Psi^{(n)}(\sigma)>0
.
The above observations lead us to introduce an additional condition for blowups, which we refer to as the full-blowup condition:

Definition 5.5
.

The blowup time
T_{1}=\Phi(S_{1})
satisfies the full-blowup condition if
\lim_{\sigma\to S_{1}^{-}}\partial_{\sigma}g(\sigma)>0
.

The definition of the full-bowup condition naturally follows from the fact that
\Psi^{\prime}=(1-\lambda g)/\nu
on
[0,S_{1}]
, so that
\lim_{\sigma\to S_{1}^{-}}\Psi^{\prime\prime}(\sigma)=\lim_{\sigma\to S_{1}^{-}}\partial_{\sigma}g(\sigma)
.
It is straightforward to check that full blowups are marked by Hölder singularity with exponent
1/2
for the time change
\Phi
:

Proposition 5.4
.

Under the full-blowup condition, the flux density
f
diverges in
T_{1}
as

\displaystyle f(t)\mathrel{{\mathop{\sim}\limits}}\frac{\lambda}{\sqrt{2a_{1}(T_{1}-t)}}\,,\quad\mathrm{with}\quad a_{1}=(\lambda/\nu)\partial_{\sigma}g(S_{1})\,.

The generic blowup condition implies that the inverse time change
\Psi
admits a zero left derivative when
t\to T_{1}^{-}
.
The full-blowup condition further ensures that
\Psi
behaves locally quadratically in the left vicinity of
S_{1}
.
Specifically, we have

\displaystyle\Psi(\sigma)=T_{1}-a_{1}(\sigma-S_{1})^{2}/2+o\big((\sigma-S_{1})^{2}\big)\,,\quad\sigma<S_{1}\,,

where the quadratic coefficient is given by

\displaystyle a_{1}=-\partial^{2}_{\sigma}\lim_{\sigma\to S_{1}}\Psi(\sigma)=(\lambda/\nu)\partial_{\sigma}g(S_{1})>0\,.

Thus, for
t<T_{1}
, just before blowup, the time change
\Phi
behaves as

\displaystyle\Phi(t)=S_{1}-\sqrt{2(T_{1}-t)/a_{1}}+o\big(\sqrt{T_{1}-t}\big)\,.

In turn, this implies a blowup divergence as the reciprocal of a square root:

\displaystyle f(t)=\frac{\nu g\left(\Phi(t)\right)}{1-\lambda g\left(\Phi(t)\right)}\mathrel{{\mathop{\sim}\limits}}\frac{\nu\lambda}{\lambda\partial_{\sigma}g(S_{1})\big(S_{1}-\Phi(t)\big)}=\frac{\lambda}{\sqrt{2a_{1}(T_{1}-t)}}\,.

∎

A synchronization event occurs in
T_{1}
if the time change
\Phi
exhibit a jump discontinuity in
T_{1}
after a blowup.
Such a discontinuity corresponds to the inverse time change
\Psi
being flat on a non-empty interval
[S_{1},U_{1})
, with
S_{1}=\Phi(T_{1}^{-})<U_{1}=\Phi(T_{1})
.
By smoothness of
\Psi
on
[0,S_{1}]
, every synchronization event is triggered by a blowup but in all generality, a blowup need not trigger a synchronization event, which corresponds to the marginal case
S_{1}=\Phi(T_{1}^{-})=U_{1}=\Phi(T_{1})
.
However, under the full-blowup condition, a blowup always trigger a synchronization event, i.e.,
S_{1}=\Phi(T_{1}^{-})<U_{1}=\Phi(T_{1})
.

Theorem 5.6
.

Suppose
S_{1}
is a full blowup time for the time-changed dynamics with normalized initial conditions
(q_{0},g_{0})
such that
q_{0}(0)=0
and
\partial_{x}q_{0}(0)/2<1/\lambda
.
Then, the solution to the fixed-point problem
\Psi=\mathcal{F}_{0}[\Psi]
can be uniquely extended as a constant function on
[S_{1},U_{1}]
with
U_{1}=S_{1}+\lambda\pi_{1}
where
\pi_{1}
satisfies
0<\pi_{1}<\|q(S_{1},\cdot)\|_{1}\leq 1
and is defined as

\displaystyle\pi_{1}=\inf\left\{p\geq 0\,\bigg|\,p>\int_{0}^{\infty}H(\lambda p,x)q(S_{1},x)\,\mathrm{d}x\right\}\,.

(44)

The proof proceeds in two steps:

(i)
We show that if it is possible to locally extend a solution
\Psi
past a full blowup in
S_{1}
, such an extension is uniquely determined on the maximum interval
[S_{1},U_{1}]
, where
\Psi
is constant.

(ii)
We show that under full-blowup conditions, it is always possible to extend a solution past
S_{1}
, i.e.,
S_{1}<U_{1}
.

(i)
Consider the smooth solution
\Psi
of the fixed-point problem
\Psi=\mathcal{F}_{\delta}[\Psi]
on
[0,S_{1}]
.
Suppose there exists
U>S_{1}
such that the solution
\Psi
can be extended on
[0,U]
.
Then on
[0,U]
, there is a smooth function
\psi
such that
\Psi=\mathcal{S}_{0}[\psi]
with
\psi=(\mathrm{id}-\lambda G[\Psi])/\nu
.
Moreover, the full-blowup condition entails that
\psi^{\prime}(S_{1})=\lim_{\sigma\to S_{1}^{-}}\Psi(\sigma)=0
and
\psi^{\prime\prime}(S_{1})=\lim_{\sigma\to S_{1}^{-}}\Psi^{\prime\prime}(\sigma)<0
.
Thus,
\psi
must be locally decreasing for
\sigma>S_{1}
, so that

\displaystyle U^{\dagger}=\sup\left\{\sigma\in[0,U]\,\big|\,\psi(\sigma)\leq\psi(S_{1})=\Psi(S_{1})\right\}>0\,.

Then, for all
0\leq\sigma\leq U^{\dagger}
, we have
\psi(\sigma)\leq\Psi(S_{1})
so that
\Psi=\mathcal{S}_{0}[\psi]
implies that
\Psi(\sigma)=\Psi(S_{1})
on
[S_{1},U^{\dagger}]
.
This shows that under the full blowup condition, smooth solution
\Psi
can only be continued locally as a constant function, if it is possible at all.
Suppose that
\Psi
is constant for all
0\leq\sigma\leq U_{1}
, for some real value
U_{1}>S_{1}
.
Then the backward function
\xi(\sigma)=\sigma-\eta(\sigma)=\Phi(\Psi(\sigma)-\epsilon)=\Phi(-\epsilon)
is also constant.
Consequently, the integral term in the renewal-type equation (
33
) attached to the fixed-point problem
4.2
vanishes on
[S_{1},U_{1}]
and for all
S_{1}\leq\sigma\leq U_{1}
, we must have
\Psi=\mathcal{S}_{0}[\psi]
where:

\displaystyle\psi(\sigma)=\big(\sigma-\lambda G(\sigma)\big)/\nu\quad\mathrm{with}\quad G(\sigma)=G(S_{1})+\int_{0}^{\infty}H(\sigma,x)q(S_{1},x)\,dx\,.

Thus the auxiliary function
\psi
is uniquely determined on
[0,U_{1}]
.
For this determination to be consistent, we must have that for all
0\leq\sigma\leq U_{1}
,
\psi(\sigma)\leq\Psi(S_{1})
, which is equivalent to

\displaystyle\nu\big(\psi(\sigma)-\Psi(S_{1})\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sigma-S_{1}-\lambda\big(G(\sigma)-G(S_{1})\big)\,,

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sigma-S_{1}+\lambda\int_{0}^{\infty}H(\sigma,x)q(S_{1},x)\,dx\leq 0\,.

This shows that the maximum possible value
U_{1}
ensuring that
\Psi
is constant on
[0,U_{1}]
is:

\displaystyle\quad\quad U_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\inf\left\{\sigma>0\,\big|\,\psi(\sigma)>0\right\}=\lambda\inf\left\{p>0\,\bigg|\,p-\int_{0}^{\infty}H(\lambda p,x)q_{0}(x)\,dx>0\right\}=\lambda\pi_{1}\,.

(ii)
To conclude, it remains to show that the interval
S_{1}<U_{1}
is nonempty under full-blowup condition, so that
\Psi
can be maximally continued as a constant function on a nonempty interval
[S_{1},U_{1})
.
To that end, we will actually show that the number
\pi_{1}
, as defined in (
44
), is such that
0<\pi_{1}<\|q_{0}\|_{1}
under full-blowup condition..
Let us consider the smooth function
\zeta
defined on
[0,\infty)
by

\displaystyle\zeta(p)=p-\int_{0}^{\infty}H(\lambda p,x)q(S_{1},x)\,\mathrm{d}x\,.

Our first goal is to prove that
\pi_{1}
exists as a root of the function
\zeta
and is such that
0<\pi_{1}\leq\|q_{0}\|_{1}\leq 1
.
To show this, observe that
\zeta
is a smooth function on
[0,\infty)
such that for all
p>\|q(S_{1},\cdot)\|_{1}
, we have

\displaystyle\zeta(p)\geq p-\|H(\lambda p,\cdot)\|_{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}q(S_{1},x)\,\mathrm{d}x=p-\|q(S_{1},\cdot)\|_{1}>0\,,

Thus to establish the existence of a root in
(0,\|q(S_{1},\cdot)\|_{1})
, it is enough to show that
\zeta
takes negative values in
(0,\|q(S_{1},\cdot)\|_{1})
.
In fact, we will show that
\zeta
is negative in the vicinity of zero under full blowup condition.
As
\zeta
satisfies
\lim_{p\to 0^{+}}\zeta(p)=0
, we first consider the asymptotic behavior of its derivative function given by

\displaystyle\zeta^{\prime}(p)=1-\lambda\int_{0}^{\infty}h(\lambda p,x)q(S_{1},x)\,\mathrm{d}x\,,

where
h
is defined as the first-passage density in (
43
).
The limit behavior of
\zeta^{\prime}
is

\displaystyle\lim_{p\to 0^{+}}\zeta^{\prime}(p)=1-\lambda\lim_{p\to 0^{+}}\int_{0}^{\infty}h(\lambda p,x)q(S_{1},x)\,\mathrm{d}x=1-\lambda\partial_{x}q(S_{1},0)/2\,,

where the last equality follows from the absorbing boundary condition in zero and the asymptotic property of first-passage density
h
:
\lim_{t\to 0}h(t,x)=\delta(x)-\delta^{\prime}(x)/2
in the sense of generalized distributions given by
A.1
.
From there, under blowup condition, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{p\to 0}\zeta^{\prime}(p)=1-\lambda\partial_{x}q(S_{1},0)/2=1-\lambda g(S_{1},0)=0\,.

Next, we evaluate the limit of the second derivative
\zeta^{\prime\prime}
.
To do so, we utilize the asymptotic result obtained in
A.2
.
To apply this result, we utilize the facts that
(1)

x\mapsto q(S_{1},x)
admits a locally bounded fourth derivative in zero with
q(0,x)=0

and that
(2)
injecting
q(\sigma,0)=0
for all
\sigma>0
in (
24
) yields
\partial_{x}q(S_{1},0)+\partial^{2}_{x}q(S_{1},0)/2=0
.
The asymptotic result from
A.2
implies that

\displaystyle\lim_{p\to 0^{+}}\zeta^{\prime\prime}(p)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\lambda^{2}\lim_{p\to 0^{+}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\partial_{\sigma}h(\lambda p,x)q(S_{1},x)\,\mathrm{d}x\,,

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}\left(\partial^{2}_{x}q(S_{1},0)+\partial^{3}_{x}q(S_{1},0)/2\right)\,.

Differentiating (
24
) with respect to
x
below the reset site, i.e., for
x<\Lambda
, we get

\displaystyle\partial_{x}\partial_{\sigma}q=\partial^{2}_{x}q+\partial^{3}_{x}q/2\,,

so that specifying the above relation in
(S_{1},0)
yields:

\displaystyle\lim_{p\to 0^{+}}\zeta^{\prime\prime}(p)=-\lambda^{2}\partial_{x}\partial_{\sigma}q(S_{1},0)/2\,.

Finally, permuting the order of the partial derivatives in the cross-derivative term yields
\partial_{\sigma}\partial_{x}q(S_{1},0)/2=\partial_{\sigma}g(S_{1})
so that, under the full blowup condition, we get

\displaystyle\lim_{p\to 0}\zeta^{\prime\prime}(p)=-\lambda^{2}\partial_{\sigma}g(S_{1})<0\,.

This implies that as a root of
\zeta
,
\pi_{1}
exists and is such that
0<\pi_{1}\leq\|q_{0}\|_{1}\leq 1
.
∎

The above proposition has a direct interpretation in terms of the original dPMF dynamics.
In the event of a full blowup, the dynamics of the time-changed process
Y
cannot unfold smoothly after the blowup time
S_{1}
as it would imply that
\Psi^{\prime}=(1-\lambda g)/\nu<0
on some nonempty interval to the left of
S_{1}
.
In other word, as a decreasing function, the function
\Psi
would implement a time-reversal in
S_{0}
, which is not physically admissible.
Physical solutions resolve this conundrum by freezing the clock for the original time at
T_{0}=\Psi(S_{0})
, while letting the clock for the changed time run past
S_{0}
.
In the time-changed picture, this corresponds to stalling the reset of inactive processes, while letting active processes inactivate according to their linear, noninteracting dynamics.
Such a non-reset dynamics continues in the time-changed picture until the original clock can start running again, which happens at time
U_{1}=S_{1}+\lambda\pi_{1}
.
Incidentally, the number
0<\pi_{1}<1
is the fraction of processes that synchronously inactivates at time
T_{0}
, which is marked by a discontinuity of size
\lambda\pi_{1}
in the time change
\Phi=\Psi^{-1}
.
We summarize the above discussion by stating the following corollary.

Corollary 5.7
.

Under the full-blowup condition at time
T_{1}=\Psi(S_{1})
, a synchronization event occurs with size
0<\big(\Phi(T_{1})-\Phi(T_{1}^{-})\big)/\lambda=\pi_{1}<1
.

Observe that the definitions of the generic blowup trigger time
S_{1}
and of the blowup exit time
U_{1}=S_{1}+\lambda\pi_{1}
is rather imprecise with respect to the behavior of
\Psi
in the immediate vicinity of
S_{1}
and
U_{1}
.
These imprecisions are the sources of difficulties in extending the existence of a solution over the whole real half-line
\mathbbm{R}^{+}
.
To exhibit such a solution using our prior results, we need to check that the auxiliary function
\psi=(\mathrm{id}-\lambda G)/\nu
is such that
\psi^{\prime}(U_{1})>0
at blowup exit time
U_{1}
, so that we can invoke Theorem
5.3
to continue the solution over some nonempty interval
[U_{1},S_{2})
, where
S_{2}
is the next putative blowup time where
\lim_{\sigma\to S_{2}-}\Psi^{\prime}(\sigma)=0
.
Then, if
S_{2}<\infty
, invoking Theorem
5.6
to further continue the solution via blowup resolution necessitates checking the full-blowup condition:
\lim_{\sigma\to S_{2}-}\Psi^{\prime\prime}(\sigma)<0
.
Assuming that all these conditions check ad infinitum, exhibiting a solution over the whole real half-line
\mathbbm{R}^{+}
will finally require to exclude the occurrence of accumulation points, whereby an infinite number of vanishingly small blowups happens in finite time.
The main result of
[
28
]
is to show that all these checks and requirements are met for sufficiently large interaction parameter
\lambda
and sufficiently small refractory period
\epsilon>0
.
Establishing this result relies on a detailed analysis of the time-changed dynamics and is beyond the scope of this work, which is mainly concerned with introducing the time-changed picture to characterize mean-field dynamics with blowups.

A
Asymptotic behavior of
h(\sigma,\cdot)
and
\partial_{\sigma}h(\sigma,\cdot)
when
\sigma\to 0^{+}

This appendix comprises two useful results about the short-time asymptotics of the first-passage kernel
(\sigma,x)\mapsto h(\sigma,x)
and its time-derivative
(\sigma,x)\mapsto\partial_{\sigma}h(\sigma,x)
.
The first result follows from classical work in
[
7
]
, whereas the second result requires original analysis.

Proposition A.1
.

Consider a continuous function
q:\mathbbm{R}\to\mathbbm{R}
.
Suppose moreover that
q
is continuously differentiable on
[0,\delta]
for some
\delta>0
, then

\displaystyle\lim_{\sigma\to 0^{+}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\partial_{\sigma}h(\sigma,x)q(x)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle q(0)+\partial_{x}q(0)/2\,.

(45)

After a simple change of variable
y=x-t
, one can check that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}h(\sigma,x)q(x)\,dx=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{ye^{-\frac{y^{2}}{2\sigma}}}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{3}}}f(y+\sigma)\,dy+\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-\frac{(x-\sigma)^{2}}{2\sigma}}}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma}}f(x)\,dx

(46)

where we recognize the drifted heat kernel in the last integral term.
The asymptotic behavior of the first integral term follows the analysis in
[
7
]
, which shows that

\displaystyle\lim_{\sigma\to 0+}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{xe^{-\frac{x^{2}}{2\sigma}}}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{3}}}q(x)\,dx=\partial_{x}q(0)/2\,,

(47)

for all functions
q
with locally bounded derivative in zero.
Thus, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{\sigma\to 0+}h(\sigma,x)=\delta_{0}(x)-\delta^{\prime}_{0}(x)/2\,.

(48)

in the distribution sense.
∎

Proposition A.2
.

Consider a continuous function
q:\mathbbm{R}\to\mathbbm{R}
with nonnegative value and polynomial growth on
\mathbbm{R}^{+}
.
Suppose moreover that
q
is four times continuously differentiable on
[0,\delta]
for some
\delta>0
and
q(0)=0
and
\partial_{x}q(0)+\partial^{2}_{x}q(0)/2=0
, then

\displaystyle\lim_{\sigma\to 0^{+}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\partial_{\sigma}h(\sigma,x)q(x)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(\partial^{2}_{x}q(0)+\partial^{3}_{x}q(0)/2\right)\,.

(49)

As
q:\mathbbm{R}\to\mathbbm{R}
has polynomial growth, i.e., there is an integer
d>0
such that
q(x)\leq K(1+x^{d})
on
\mathbbm{R}^{+}
for some real
K>0
.
For all real
\delta>0
and all integers
m,n\geq 0
, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{\sigma\to 0^{+}}\frac{1}{\sigma^{m}}\int_{\delta}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{(x-\sigma)^{2}}{2\sigma}}x^{n}q(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=0\,.

(50)

This follows from the fact that
x\mapsto e^{-\frac{(x-\sigma)^{2}}{2\sigma}}x^{n}
is decreasing for
x>\left(\sigma+\sqrt{\sigma(4n+\sigma)}\right)
. Then for
0\leq\sigma<\delta^{2}/(n+\delta)
, we have:

\displaystyle\sup_{x\geq\delta}e^{-\frac{(x-\sigma)^{2}}{2\sigma}}x^{n}=e^{-\frac{(\delta-\sigma)^{2}}{2\sigma}}\delta^{n}\,.

(51)

This allows one to write for
0\leq\sigma<\delta^{2}/(n+d+2+\delta)

\displaystyle\int_{\delta}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{(x-\sigma)^{2}}{2\sigma}}x^{n}q(x)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{\delta}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{(x-\sigma)^{2}}{2\sigma}}x^{n}\big(1+x^{d+2}\big)\frac{K(1+x^{d})}{1+x^{d+2}}\,\mathrm{d}x\,,

(52)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\,e^{-\frac{(\delta-\sigma)^{2}}{2\sigma}}\delta^{n}K\big(1+\delta^{d+2}\big)\int_{\delta}^{\infty}\frac{1+x^{d}}{1+x^{d+2}}\,\mathrm{d}x\,,

(53)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle K_{d,\delta}\,\delta^{n}e^{-\frac{(\delta-\sigma)^{2}}{2\sigma}}\,,

(54)

where the constant
K_{d,\delta}
only depends on
d
via the Gamma function:

\displaystyle K_{d,\delta}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle K\big(1+\delta^{d+2}\big)\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1+x^{d}}{1+x^{d+2}}\,\mathrm{d}x\,,

(55)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle K\big(1+\delta^{d+2}\big)\left(2\Gamma\left[\frac{1+d}{2+d}\right]\Gamma\left[\frac{3+d}{2+d}\right]\right)<\infty\,.

(56)

We conclude by observing that

\displaystyle 0\leq\frac{1}{\sigma^{m}}\int_{\epsilon}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{(x-\sigma)^{2}}{2\sigma}}x^{n}q(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\leq K_{d,\delta}\,\delta^{n}e^{-\frac{(\delta-\sigma)^{2}}{2\sigma}}/\sigma^{m}\xrightarrow{\sigma\to 0^{+}}0

(57)

The above observation implies that if
q
is a function with polynomial growth, then for all
\delta>0
we have

\displaystyle\lim_{\sigma\to 0^{+}}\int_{\delta}^{\infty}|\partial_{\sigma}h(\sigma,x)|q(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=0\,,

(58)

so that if the limits at stake exist, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{\sigma\to 0^{+}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\partial_{\sigma}h(\sigma,x)q(x)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{\sigma\to 0^{+}}\int_{0}^{\delta}\partial_{\sigma}h(\sigma,x)q(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\,.

(59)

Moreover, if there exists
\delta>0
such that
|\partial^{4}_{x}q|\leq B_{\delta}<\infty
on
[0,\delta]
with
q(0)=0
, we have

\displaystyle\Bigg|\int_{0}^{\delta}\partial_{\sigma}h(\sigma,x)\left(q(x)-\sum_{n=1}^{3}\frac{\partial^{n}_{x}q(0)}{n!}x^{n}\right)\,\mathrm{d}x\Bigg|\leq B_{\delta}\int_{0}^{\delta}|\partial_{\sigma}h(\sigma,x)|x^{4}\,\mathrm{d}x\,.

(60)

Let us then introduce the integrals

\displaystyle I_{n}(\sigma)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\partial_{\sigma}h(\sigma,x)x^{n}\,\mathrm{d}x,\quad 1\leq n\leq 3\,,

(61)

\displaystyle J_{4}(\sigma)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}|\partial_{\sigma}h(\sigma,x)|x^{4}\,\mathrm{d}x,

(62)

The latter integral can be evaluated in closed form as

\displaystyle J_{4}(\sigma)=e^{-\sigma/2}(36+\sigma(61+\sigma(16+\sigma)))\sqrt{\frac{\sigma}{2\pi}}+

(64)

\displaystyle\hskip 40.0pt\sigma(5+\sigma)(15+\sigma(12+\sigma))\left(1+\mathrm{Erf}\left(\sqrt{\frac{\sigma}{2}}\right)\right)\,.

The above expression shows that
\lim_{\sigma\to 0^{+}}J_{4}(\sigma)=0
so that

\displaystyle\lim_{\sigma\to 0^{+}}\int_{0}^{\delta}|\partial_{\sigma}h(\sigma,x)|x^{4}\,\mathrm{d}x=\lim_{\sigma\to 0^{+}}J_{4}(\sigma)-\lim_{\sigma\to 0^{+}}\int_{\delta}^{\infty}|\partial_{\sigma}h(\sigma,x)|x^{4}\,\mathrm{d}x=0\,.

(65)

This shows that if the limits at stake exist, we must have

\displaystyle\lim_{\sigma\to 0^{+}}\int_{0}^{\delta}\partial_{\sigma}h(\sigma,x)q(x)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{\sigma\to 0^{+}}\sum_{n=1}^{3}\frac{\partial^{n}_{x}q(0)}{n!}\int_{0}^{\delta}\partial_{\sigma}h(\sigma,x)x^{n}\,\mathrm{d}x\,,

(66)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{\sigma\to 0^{+}}\sum_{n=1}^{3}\frac{\partial^{n}_{x}q(0)}{n!}I_{n}(\sigma)\,,

(67)

where the last equality follows from (
58
).
For
n=1,2,3
, we find that

\displaystyle I_{1}(\sigma)=\frac{e^{-\sigma/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma}}+\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\mathrm{Erf}\left(\sqrt{\frac{\sigma}{2}}\right)\right)\,,

(68)

\displaystyle I_{2}(\sigma)=\frac{e^{-\sigma/2}(1+2\sigma)}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma}}+\left(\frac{3}{2}+\sigma\right)\left(1+\mathrm{Erf}\left(\sqrt{\frac{\sigma}{2}}\right)\right)\,,

(69)

\displaystyle I_{3}(\sigma)=3\left(e^{-\sigma/2}(3+\sigma)\sqrt{\frac{\sigma}{2\pi}}+\left(\frac{1}{2}+\sigma\left(2+\frac{\sigma}{2}\right)\right)\left(1+\mathrm{Erf}\left(\sqrt{\frac{\sigma}{2}}\right)\right)\right)\,,

(70)

where one can observe that
I_{1}(\sigma)
and
I_{2}(\sigma)
diverge when
\sigma\to 0^{+}
.
Such diverging behaviors cancel out under the assumption that
\partial_{x}q(0)+\partial^{2}_{x}q(0)/2=0
, as we then have

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{3}\frac{\partial^{n}_{x}q(0)}{n!}I_{n}(\sigma)=\frac{\partial^{2}_{x}q(0)}{2}(I_{2}(\sigma)-I_{1}(\sigma))+\frac{\partial^{2}_{x}q(0)}{6}I_{3}(\sigma)\,,

(71)

with
\lim_{\sigma\to 0^{+}}I_{2}(\sigma)-I_{1}(\sigma)=1
and
\lim_{\sigma\to 0^{+}}I_{3}(\sigma)=3/2
.
∎
