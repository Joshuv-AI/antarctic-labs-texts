On the Elementary Proof of the Inverse ErdŐs-Heilbronn Problem
Shengning Zhang

Abstract
. In this article, we studied the inverse Erdős-Heilbronn problem with the restricted sumset from two components A A and B B that are not necessarily the same. We give a completely elementary proof for the problem in ℤ \mathbb{Z} and some partial results that contributes to the elementary proof of the problem in ℤ / p ​ ℤ \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z} , avoiding the usage of the powerful polynomial method and the Combinatorial Nullstellensatz.



1.
Introduction

A basic object in Additive Number Theory is the sumset of sets
A
and
B
:

A+B=\{a+b:a\in A,b\in B\}.

A simple problem in Additive Number Theory is: Given two subsets
A
and
B
of sets of integers, what properties can we determined about
A+B
? A classic result is the Cauchy-Davenport Theorem proved by Cauchy
[
1
]
in 1813 and independently by Davenport
[
2
]
in 1935. Let
p
be a prime, if
A
and
B
are nonempty subsets of
\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
, then the theorem asserts that

\left|A+B\right|\geq\min\{p,|A|+|B|-1\}.

Similarly, we can define the restricted sumsets of sets
A
and
B
:

A\widehat{+}B=\{a+b:a\in A,b\in B,a\neq b\}.

In 1964, P. Erdős and H. Heilbronn
[
3
]
conjectured that

\left|A\widehat{+}A\right|\geq\min\{p,2|A|-3\},

where
p
is a prime and
A
is a nonempty subset of
\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
. The conjecture was then proved by J. A. Dias da Silva and Y. O. Hamidoune
[
4
]
in 1994 through the usage of methods from linear algebra. The more general case

\left|A\widehat{+}B\right|\geq\min\{p,|A|+|B|-3\}

was established and proved by N. Alon, Melvin B. Nathanson, and Imre Z. Ruzsa
[
5
]
in 1995 using the polynomial method.

These theorems above depict the properties of sumsets and restricted sumsets given the knowledge of the individual sets that make up the sumsets or restricted sumsets. Thus, one may consider the inverse directions of these problems and there indeed exists several beautiful results. In particular, Gy. Károlyi
[
6
]
points out the following inverse problem of the Erdős-Heilbronn conjecture:

Let sets
A,B\subseteq\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
be nonempty sets such that
p\geq|A|+|B|-2
, where
p
is a prime. Then
|A\widehat{+}B|=|A|+|B|-3
if and only if
A=B
and one of the following holds:

(1)

|A|=2
or
|A|=3
;

(2)

|A|=4
and
A=\{a,a+d,c,c+d\}
;

(3)

|A|\geq 5
and
A
is an arithmetic progression.

This paper is motivated to give an elementary proof of the results. Firstly, the original proof of this statement
[
6
]
relies on the combinatorial Nullstellensatz and the polynomial method through the entire proof of the theorem. Inspired by
[
7
]
, we believe that this should be unnecessary for such an elementary statement. Secondly, we hope to obtain a more explicit view to the structures of the sumsets in
\mathbb{Z}
and
\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
by an elementary proof.

Our strategy is to first prove the inverse problem of the Erdős-Heilbronn conjecture for
\mathbb{Z}
, which is of independent interests. Our result generalizes Nathanson’s proof
[
8
]
, where the case
A=B
is studied. In our work
A
and
B
are not necessarily the same. We then give some partial results for the elementary proof of the case in
\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
through Theorem 5, Theorem 6 and Theorem 7.

2.
Notations

To simplify our proofs, we will use the following notations throughout the rest of the paper:

(1)

Let
G
be the group
\mathbb{Z}
or
\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
. For nonempty finite sets
A
,
B\subseteq G
with
|A|
,
|B|\geq 2
, note

A=\{a_{1},a_{2},\cdots,a_{m}\}\ (m=|A|)

and

B=\{b_{1},b_{2},\cdots,b_{n}\}\ (n=|B|).

If
G=\mathbb{Z}
, we always assume that
a_{1}<a_{2}<\cdots<a_{m}
and
b_{1}<b_{2}<\cdots<b_{n}
; If
G=\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
, we identity it as set with
\{0,1,2,\cdots,p-1\}
, and without the abuse of notation, we also assume
a_{1}<a_{2}<\cdots<a_{m}
and
b_{1}<b_{2}<\cdots<b_{n}
;

(2)

For nonempty finite sets
A
,
B\subseteq G
with
|A|,|B|\geq 2
, define the restricted sumset of
A
and
B
as
A\widehat{+}B
.
A\widehat{+}B=\{a+b:a\in A,b\in B,a\neq b\}
if
G=\mathbb{Z}
and
A\widehat{+}B=\{a+b\mod{p}:a\in A,b\in B,a\neq b\}
if
G=\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
;

(3)

For nonempty finite sets
A
,
B\subseteq G
with
|A|,|B|\geq 2
, if

|A\widehat{+}B|=|A|+|B|-3,

then we call the unordered pair
(A,B)
a critical pair;

(4)

For nonempty finite set
A\subseteq G
with
|A|\geq 2
, if
A
can be written into the form
A=\{\tau+id:i=0,1,\cdots,|A|-1\}
for some
\tau,d\in G
with
d\neq 0
, then we call set
A
a standard set;

(5)

If sets
A
and
B
are both standard sets and
A=B
, then we call the unordered pair
(A,B)
a standard pair.

(6)

Suppose
m,n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}\cup\{0\}
and
p
is a prime. Let
X\in\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
be a nonempty set with
|X|\geq 2
. Suppose
d
is a given generator of
\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
. Then
[m,n]_{d}
is called a gap with respect to
d
in
X
if
rd\notin X
for
\forall r\in\{m,m+1,\cdots,n\}
. The length of the gap
[m,n]_{d}
is
(n-m+1)
.

3.
The Cases of
\mathbb{Z}
for
2\leq|A|\leq 4

Theorem 1
.

Let finite sets
A,B\subseteq\mathbb{Z}
be nonempty sets. If
|A|=2
, then
(A,B)
is a critical pair if and only if
A=B
.

Proof.

We first consider the sufficiency of the conditions. If
A=B
, then assume that
A=B=\{a_{1},a_{2}\}
. Thus,

|A\widehat{+}B|=|\{a_{1}+a_{2}\}|=1=|A|+|B|-3.

For the necessity of the conditions, suppose
A=\{a_{1},a_{2}\}
. If
a_{2}\neq b_{n}
, due to
a_{2}>a_{1}
and
b_{n}=\max_{x\in B}\{x\}
, we have

a_{2}+b_{n}\in(A\widehat{+}B)\setminus(\{a_{1}\}\widehat{+}B).

Together with

(\{a_{1}\}\widehat{+}B)\cup\{a_{2}+b_{n}\}\subseteq A\widehat{+}B,

there is

|A\widehat{+}B|\geq|(\{a_{1}\}\widehat{+}B)\cup\{a_{2}+b_{n}\}|=|B|-1+1>|A|+|B|-3.

A contradiction! Therefore,
a_{2}=b_{n}
.

Similarly, if
a_{1}\neq b_{1}
, we have

a_{1}+b_{1}\in(A\widehat{+}B)\setminus(\{a_{2}\}\widehat{+}B),

because
a_{1}<a_{2}
and
b_{1}=\min_{x\in B}\{x\}
. Thus, combining with

(\{a_{2}\}\widehat{+}B)\cup\{a_{1}+b_{1}\}\subseteq A\widehat{+}B,

there is

|A\widehat{+}B|\geq|(\{a_{2}\}\widehat{+}B)\cup\{a_{1}+b_{1}\}|=|B|-1+1>|A|+|B|-3.

Consequently,
a_{1}=b_{1}
.

Since
a_{1}
,
a_{2}\in B
,
|\{a_{1}\}\widehat{+}B|=|\{a_{2}\}\widehat{+}B|=|B|-1=|A|+|B|-3=|A\widehat{+}B|.
Thus, due to
\{a_{1}\}\widehat{+}B
,
\{a_{2}\}\widehat{+}B\subseteq A\widehat{+}B,
there is
\{a_{1}\}\widehat{+}B=\{a_{2}\}\widehat{+}B=A\widehat{+}B.
Now, because
a_{1}\neq b_{2}
, we have
a_{1}+b_{2}\in\{a_{1}\}\widehat{+}B=\{a_{2}\}\widehat{+}B.
Therefore,
\exists b_{i}\in B\ (i\in\mathbb{Z}_{+})
, such that
a_{1}+b_{2}=a_{2}+b_{i}
. Notice that
a_{2}>a_{1}
, so we must have
b_{i}<b_{2}
. According to the definition of
B
,
b_{i}=b_{1}
. Together with
a_{1}=b_{1}
, we have
b_{n}=a_{2}=b_{2}.
So, we have proven that
A=B
.

∎

Theorem 2
.

Let finite sets
A,B\subseteq\mathbb{Z}
be nonempty sets. If
|A|=3
, then
(A,B)
is a critical pair if and only if
A=B
.

Proof.

Considering the sufficiency of the conditions, if
A=B
, we may assume that
A=B=\{a_{1},a_{2},a_{3}\}
, then

|A\widehat{+}B|=|\{a_{1}+a_{2},a_{1}+a_{3},a_{2}+a_{3}\}|=3=|A|+|B|-3.

In order to prove the necessity of the conditions, we will first prove that
A\subseteq B
. If
a_{1}\notin B
, then

|\{a_{1}\}\widehat{+}B|=|B|=|A|+|B|-3=|A\widehat{+}B|.

Since
\{a_{1}\}\widehat{+}B\subseteq A\widehat{+}B
and
\{a_{2},a_{3}\}\widehat{+}B\subseteq A\widehat{+}B
, we have

\{a_{2},a_{3}\}\widehat{+}B\subseteq\{a_{1}\}\widehat{+}B=A\widehat{+}B.

This indicates that for
\forall b_{i}\in B\ (i\in\mathbb{Z}_{+})
with
b_{i}\neq a_{2}
,
\exists b_{j}\in B\ (j\in\mathbb{Z}_{+})
such that
a_{2}+b_{i}=a_{1}+b_{j}.
Suppose
a_{2}\neq b_{n}
, then
a_{2}+b_{n}=a_{1}+b_{j}
. However, for
\forall b_{k}\in B\ (k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+})
, there is
a_{2}+b_{n}>a_{1}+b_{k}
. A contradiction! Thus, we must have
a_{2}=b_{n}
. Notice that for
\forall b_{k}\in B\ (k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
), there is
a_{3}+b_{n}>a_{1}+b_{k}
. Therefore, by applying the same argument, there is
a_{3}=b_{n}
, which contradicts to
a_{3}>a_{2}
. Consequently, we must have
a_{1}\in B
.

If
a_{3}\notin B
, then

|\{a_{3}\}\widehat{+}B|=|B|=|A|+|B|-3=|A\widehat{+}B|.

Since
\{a_{3}\}\widehat{+}B\subseteq A\widehat{+}B
and
\{a_{1},a_{2}\}\widehat{+}B\subseteq A\widehat{+}B
, we have

\{a_{1},a_{2}\}\widehat{+}B\subseteq\{a_{3}\}\widehat{+}B=A\widehat{+}B.

This indicates that for
\forall b_{i}\in B\ (i\in\mathbb{Z}_{+})
with
b_{i}\neq a_{2}
,
\exists b_{j}\in B\ (j\in\mathbb{Z}_{+})
such that
a_{2}+b_{i}=a_{3}+b_{j}.
Suppose
a_{2}\neq b_{1}
, then
a_{2}+b_{1}=a_{3}+b_{j}
. However, we know that for
\forall b_{k}\in B\ (k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+})
, there is
a_{2}+b_{1}<a_{3}+b_{k}
. A contradiction! Thus, we must have
a_{2}=b_{1}
. Notice that for
\forall b_{k}\in B\ (k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
), there is
a_{1}+b_{1}<a_{2}+b_{k}
. Therefore, by applying the same argument, there is
a_{1}=b_{1}
, which contradicts to
a_{2}>a_{1}
. Consequently, we must have
a_{3}\in B
.

Suppose
a_{1}\neq b_{1}
, then
a_{1}+b_{1}\in(A\widehat{+}B)\setminus(\{a_{3}\}\widehat{+}B)
, for
a_{1}<a_{3}
and
b_{1}=\min_{x\in B}\{x\}
. Thus,
a_{2}=b_{1}
, or else we can get
a_{2}+b_{1}\in(A\widehat{+}B)\setminus(\{a_{3}\}\widehat{+}B)
with the same argument above. Apparently,
(\{a_{3}\}\widehat{+}B)\cup\{a_{1}+b_{1},a_{2}+b_{1}\}\subseteq A\widehat{+}B.
However, because
(A,B)
is a critical pair,

\displaystyle|(\{a_{3}\}\widehat{+}B)\cup\{a_{1}+b_{1},a_{2}+b_{1}\}|

\displaystyle=|\{a_{1}\}\widehat{+}B|+|\{a_{1}+b_{1},a_{2}+b_{1}\}|

\displaystyle=(|B|-1)+2

\displaystyle=|A|+|B|-2

\displaystyle=|A\widehat{+}B|+1

leads to a contradiction! Therefore,
a_{1}=b_{1}
.

Suppose
a_{3}\neq b_{n}
, then
a_{3}+b_{n}\in A\widehat{+}B\setminus\{a_{1}\}\widehat{+}B
, for
a_{1}<a_{3}
and
b_{n}=\max_{x\in B}\{x\}
. So,
a_{2}=b_{n}
, or else we can get
a_{2}+b_{n}\in(A\widehat{+}B)\setminus(\{a_{1}\}\widehat{+}B)
with the same argument above. Apparently,
(\{a_{1}\}\widehat{+}B)\cup\{a_{2}+b_{n},a_{3}+b_{n}\}\subseteq A\widehat{+}B.
However, because
(A,B)
is a critical pair,

\displaystyle|(\{a_{1}\}\widehat{+}B)\cup\{a_{2}+b_{n},a_{3}+b_{n}\}|

\displaystyle=|\{a_{1}\}\widehat{+}B|+|\{a_{2}+b_{n},a_{3}+b_{n}\}|

\displaystyle=(|B|-1)+2

\displaystyle=|A|+|B|-2

\displaystyle=|A\widehat{+}B|+1

leads to a contradiction! Therefore,
a_{3}=b_{n}
.

If
a_{2}\notin B
, then

|\{a_{2}\}\widehat{+}B|=|B|=|A|+|B|-3=|A\widehat{+}B|.

Since
\{a_{2}\}\widehat{+}B\subseteq A\widehat{+}B
and
\{a_{1},a_{3}\}\widehat{+}B\subseteq A\widehat{+}B
, we have

\{a_{1},a_{3}\}\widehat{+}B\subseteq\{a_{2}\}\widehat{+}B=A\widehat{+}B.

Through Theorem 2 we know that
|B|\geq 3
, for
|B|=2
indicates that
|A|=2
. Thus,
b_{n-1}\notin\{b_{1},b_{n}\}
. Using this condition, we have
a_{3}+b_{n-1}\in A\widehat{+}B=\{a_{2}\}\widehat{+}B.
This implies that
\exists b_{i}\in B\ (i\in\mathbb{Z}_{+})
such that
a_{3}+b_{n-1}=a_{2}+b_{j}.
Since
a_{3}>a_{2}
, there must be
b_{j}>b_{n-1}.
Thus,
b_{j}=b_{n}
. Together with
a_{3}=b_{n}
, we have
a_{2}=b_{n-1}\in B.
A contradiction! Therefore,
a_{2}\in B
. Combining with the arguments above, we have
A\subseteq B
.

Since
a_{1}\in B
, we have
|\{a_{1}\}\widehat{+}B|=|B|-1
. Due to
b_{n-1}\notin\{b_{1},b_{n}\}=\{a_{1},a_{3}\}
,
a_{1}<b_{n-1}
. So,
a_{1}+b_{n}<a_{3}+b_{n-1}
. Therefore,
a_{3}+b_{n-1}\in(A\widehat{+}B)\setminus(\{a_{1}\}\widehat{+}B)
. Consequently,

|\{a_{3}+b_{n-1}\}\cup(\{a_{1}\}\widehat{+}B)|=|B|=|A|+|B|-3=|A\widehat{+}B|.

Since
(\{a_{3}+b_{n-1}\})\cup(\{a_{1}\}\widehat{+}B)\subseteq A\widehat{+}B
, we have
\{a_{3}+b_{n-1}\}\cup(\{a_{1}\}\widehat{+}B)=A\widehat{+}B.
Apparently,
a_{2}+b_{n}\in(A\widehat{+}B)\setminus(\{a_{1}\}\widehat{+}B)
. With the same argument, there is
\{a_{2}+b_{n}\}\cup(\{a_{1}\}\widehat{+}B)=A\widehat{+}B.
This together with the previous argument above leads to

\{a_{3}+b_{n-1}\}\cup(\{a_{1}\}\widehat{+}B)=\{a_{2}+b_{n}\}\cup(\{a_{1}\}\widehat{+}B).

Thus,
a_{3}+b_{n-1}=a_{2}+b_{n},
indicating that
a_{2}=b_{n-1}
.

Suppose
|B|\geq 4
, then
\exists b_{n-2}\in B\setminus\{b_{1},b_{n-1},b_{n}\}
. If
a_{3}+b_{n-2}\in\{a_{1}\}\widehat{+}B
, then
\exists b_{i}\in B\setminus\{a_{1}\}\ (i\in\mathbb{Z}_{+})
such that
a_{1}+b_{i}=a_{3}+b_{n-2}.
Since
a_{1}<a_{3}
, we must have
b_{i}>b_{n-2}.
Thus,
b_{i}=b_{n}
. This leads to
a_{2}=b_{n-2}
. A contradiction! Therefore,
a_{3}+b_{n-2}\notin\{a_{1}\}\widehat{+}B.
Combining

\{a_{3}+b_{n-2}\}\cup(\{a_{1}\}\widehat{+}B)\subseteq A\widehat{+}B

with

|\{a_{3}+b_{n-2}\}\cup(\{a_{1}\}\widehat{+}B)|=|B|=|A\widehat{+}B|,

we have
\{a_{3}+b_{n-2}\}\cup(\{a_{1}\}\widehat{+}B)=A\widehat{+}B.
Recalling that

\{a_{2}+b_{n}\}\cup(\{a_{1}\}\widehat{+}B)=A\widehat{+}B,

we can get
a_{2}+b_{n}=a_{3}+b_{n-2}
. Thus,
a_{2}=b_{n-2}
, which is not possible! Therefore,
|B|=3
. This implies that
A=B
.

∎

Theorem 3
.

Let finite sets
A,B\subseteq\mathbb{Z}
be nonempty sets. If
|A|=4
, then
(A,B)
is a critical pair if and only if
A=B
and

A=\{a,c,a+d,c+d\}.

Proof.

We first consider the sufficiency of the conditions. If
A=B
and

A=\{a,a+d,c,c+d\}
, then we have

|A\widehat{+}B|=|\{a+c,2a+c,2c+d,a+c+d,a+c+2d\}|=5=|A|+|B|-3.

Now, consider the necessity of conditions. Apparently,

|(\{a_{4}\}\widehat{+}B)\cup(\{b_{1}\}\widehat{+}A)|=|\{a_{4}\}\widehat{+}B|+|\{b_{1}\}\widehat{+}A|-|(\{a_{4}\}\widehat{+}B)\cap(\{b_{1}\}\widehat{+}A)|.

Noticing that
(\{a_{4}\}\widehat{+}B)\cap(\{b_{1}\}\widehat{+}A)=\{a_{4}+b_{1}\},
we have

|(\{a_{4}\}\widehat{+}B)\cup(\{b_{1}\}\widehat{+}A)|\geq(|A|-1)+(|B|-1)-1=|A\widehat{+}B|.

Since
(\{a_{4}\}\widehat{+}B)\cup(\{b_{1}\}\widehat{+}A)\subseteq A\widehat{+}B
, there is
(\{a_{4}\}\widehat{+}B)\cup(\{b_{1}\}\widehat{+}A)=A\widehat{+}B
. Thus,
|\{a_{4}\}\widehat{+}B|=|B|-1
and
|\{b_{1}\}\widehat{+}A|=|A|-1
. This gives us
a_{4}\in B
and
b_{1}\in A
. Using the same argument, we have
(\{a_{1}\}\widehat{+}B)\cup(\{b_{n}\}\widehat{+}A)=A\widehat{+}B
,
a_{1}\in B
and
b_{n}\in A.
Through the definition of
a_{1},a_{4},b_{1}
and
b_{n}
, there must be
a_{1}=b_{1}
and
a_{4}=b_{n}
. Then, there are

\displaystyle A\widehat{+}B

\displaystyle=(\{a_{4}\}\widehat{+}B)\cup(\{b_{1}\}\widehat{+}A)

\displaystyle=\{a_{4}+b_{1},a_{4}+b_{2},\cdots,a_{4}+b_{n-1}\}\cup\{b_{1}+a_{2},b_{1}+a_{3}\}

\displaystyle=\{a_{4}+b_{1},a_{4}+b_{2},\cdots,a_{4}+b_{n-1}\}\cup\{a_{1}+a_{2},a_{1}+a_{3}\}

and

\displaystyle A\widehat{+}B

\displaystyle=(\{a_{1}\}\widehat{+}B)\cup(\{b_{n}\}\widehat{+}A)

\displaystyle=\{a_{1}+b_{2},a_{1}+b_{3},\cdots,a_{1}+b_{n}\}\cup\{b_{n}+a_{2},b_{n}+a_{3}\}.

Rearrange the elements in
A\widehat{+}B
, and then we have

\displaystyle A\widehat{+}B

\displaystyle=\{a_{1}+a_{2},a_{1}+a_{3},a_{4}+b_{1},a_{4}+b_{2},\cdots,a_{4}+b_{n-1}\}

\displaystyle=\{a_{1}+b_{2},a_{1}+b_{3},\cdots,a_{1}+b_{n},a_{2}+b_{n},a_{3}+b_{n}\}.

Notice that
a_{1}+a_{2}<a_{1}+a_{3}<\cdots<a_{4}+b_{n-1}
and
a_{1}+b_{2}<a_{1}+b_{3}<\cdots<a_{3}+b_{n}
, then there must be
a_{4}+b_{n-1}=a_{3}+b_{n}
and
a_{1}+a_{2}=a_{1}+b_{2}
. So, we can get
a_{2}=b_{2}
, which further indicates that
a_{3}=b_{3}
. What’s more,
a_{4}=b_{n}
shows that
a_{3}=b_{n-1}
. As a result,
b_{3}=b_{n-1}
. Therefore,

A=\{a_{1},a_{2},a_{3},a_{4}\}=\{b_{1},b_{2},b_{3},b_{4}\}=B.

It’s now easy for us to find that
A\widehat{+}B=\{a_{1}+a_{4},a_{2}+a_{4},a_{3}+a_{4},a_{1}+a_{2},a_{1}+a_{3}\}.
Since we also have
a_{2}+a_{3}\in A\widehat{+}B
, it’s apparent that
a_{2}+a_{3}=a_{1}+a_{4}
. This means
a_{3}-a_{1}=a_{4}-a_{2}
. By letting
a_{3}-a_{1}=d
,
a_{1}=a
and
a_{2}=c
, we have
A=\{a,c,a+d,c+d\}
, which completes the proof.

∎

4.
The Cases of
\mathbb{Z}
for
|A|\geq 5

Theorem 4
.

Let sets
A,B\subseteq\mathbb{Z}
be nonempty sets. If
|A|\geq 5
, then
(A,B)
is a critical pair if and only if
(A,B)
is a standard pair.

Proof.

We still first consider the sufficiency of the conditions. Since
(A,B)
is a standard pair, we may assume that

A=B=\{a_{1},a_{2},\cdots,a_{m}\}=\{a+id:i=0,1,\cdots,m-1\}

for some
a,d\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
. Define
A^{\prime}=B^{\prime}=\{id:i=0,1,\cdots,m-1\}
. For
\forall x+y\in A\widehat{+}B
, there is
(x-a)+(y-a)\in A^{\prime}\widehat{+}B^{\prime}
. Similarly, for
\forall u+w\in A^{\prime}\widehat{+}B^{\prime}
, there is
(u+a)+(w+a)\in A\widehat{+}B
. So,
|A\widehat{+}B|=|A^{\prime}\widehat{+}B^{\prime}|
. Now, according to the definition of sumset and restricted sumset, we have

A^{\prime}\widehat{+}B^{\prime}\subseteq A^{\prime}+B^{\prime}

and

|A^{\prime}+B^{\prime}|=|\{id:i=0,1,\cdots,2m-2\}|=|A^{\prime}|+|B^{\prime}|-1\geq|A^{\prime}\widehat{+}B^{\prime}|.

Apparently, there are
0\notin A^{\prime}\widehat{+}B^{\prime}
and
(2m-2)d\notin A^{\prime}\widehat{+}B^{\prime}
. Thus,
A^{\prime}\widehat{+}B^{\prime}\subseteq\{id:i=1,\cdots,2m-3\}
. For
\forall x\in\left[1,m-1\right]\cap\mathbb{Z}
, we can choose
0\in A^{\prime}
and
xd\in B^{\prime}
. Since
0\neq xd
, we have
xd=0+xd\in A^{\prime}\widehat{+}B^{\prime}
. For
\forall x\in\left[m,2m-3\right]\cap\mathbb{Z}
, we can choose
(m-1)d\in A^{\prime}
and
(x-m+1)d\in B^{\prime}
. Since
x\in\left[m,2m-3\right]\cap\mathbb{Z}
,

(x-m+1)d<(m-1)d.

Therefore, we have
xd=(m-1)d+(x-m+1)d\in A^{\prime}\widehat{+}B^{\prime}
. So, for
\forall x\in\left[1,2m-3\right]
,
xd\in A^{\prime}\widehat{+}B^{\prime}.
Combining with
A^{\prime}\widehat{+}B^{\prime}\subseteq\{id:i=1,\cdots,2m-3\}
, we have
A^{\prime}\widehat{+}B^{\prime}=\{id:i=1,\cdots,2m-3\}
. As a result,

|A\widehat{+}B|=|A^{\prime}\widehat{+}B^{\prime}|=|\{id:i=1,\cdots,2m-3\}|=2m-3=|A|+|B|-3.

To prove the necessity of the conditions, let’s consider the following lemmas.

Lemma 1
.

If finite sets
A,B\subseteq\mathbb{Z}
are nonempty sets, then
|A+B|\geq|A|+|B|-1.

Proof.

Since
A
and
B
are finite sets, define the element with the largest absolute value in
A
as
a_{x}
and the element with the largest absolute value in
B
as
b_{y}
. Apparently,
\exists p
such that
p
is a prime number and
p>\max\{a_{m},b_{n},|a_{x}|+|b_{y}|,|A|+|B|-1\}
. Thus, applying the Cauchy-Davenport theorem in
\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}

[
1
]
, we have
|A+B|\geq\min\{p,|A|+|B|-1\}=|A|+|B|-1.

∎

Lemma 2
.

If finite sets
A,B\subseteq\mathbb{Z}
are nonempty sets, then
|A\widehat{+}B|\geq|A|+|B|-3.

Proof.

Similar with Lemma 1, since
A
and
B
are finite sets, define the element with the largest absolute value in
A
as
a_{x}
and the element with the largest absolute value in
B
as
b_{y}
. Apparently,
\exists p
such that
p
is a prime number and
p>\max\{a_{m},b_{n},|a_{x}|+|b_{y}|,|A|+|B|-3\}
. Thus, applying the general case of the Erdős-Heilbronn conjecture in
\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}

[
5
]
, we have
|A\widehat{+}B|\geq\min\{p,|A|+|B|-3\}=|A|+|B|-3.

∎

Lemma 3
.

(A,B)
is a critical pair and
|A|\geq 5
. If
B\subseteq A
, then
a_{m}=b_{n}
and
(X,Y)
is also a critical pair, where
X=A\setminus\{a_{m}\}
and
Y=B\setminus\{a_{n}\}
.

Proof.

Since
B\subseteq A
, we have
a_{m}\geq b_{n}
. Notice that
(A\widehat{+}\{b_{1}\})\cap(\{a_{m}\}\widehat{+}B)=\{a_{m}+b_{1}\}
. Since

(A\widehat{+}\{b_{1}\})\cup(\{a_{m}\}\widehat{+}B)\subseteq A\widehat{+}B,

we have

|A\widehat{+}B|\geq|(A\widehat{+}\{b_{1}\})\cup(\{a_{m}\}\widehat{+}B)|=|A\widehat{+}\{b_{1}\}|+|\{a_{m}\}\widehat{+}B|-1.

If
a_{m}>b_{n}
, then
a_{m}\notin B
. Thus,

|A\widehat{+}\{b_{1}\}|+|\{a_{m}\}\widehat{+}B|-1=(|A|-1)+|B|-1=|A|+|B|-2.

A contradiction! Therefore,
a_{m}=b_{n}
.

Apparently,

\displaystyle|(X\widehat{+}Y)\cap(\{a_{m}\}\widehat{+}A)|

\displaystyle=|(X\widehat{+}Y)\cap((\{a_{m}\}\widehat{+}A)\setminus\{a_{m-1}+a_{m}\})|

\displaystyle\leq|(\{a_{m}\}\widehat{+}A)\setminus\{a_{m-1}+a_{m}\}|

\displaystyle=|A|-2.

Now, because
B\subseteq A
, we have
A\widehat{+}B=(X\widehat{+}Y)\cup(\{a_{m}\}\widehat{+}A).
Thus, according to Lemma 2,

\displaystyle|(X\widehat{+}Y)\cap(\{a_{m}\}\widehat{+}A)|

\displaystyle=|X\widehat{+}Y|+|\{a_{m}\}\widehat{+}A|-|(X\widehat{+}Y)\cup(\{a_{m}\}\widehat{+}A)|

\displaystyle=|X\widehat{+}Y|+|\{a_{m}\}\widehat{+}A|-|A\widehat{+}B|

\displaystyle\geq(|X|+|Y|-3)+(|A|-1)-(A|+|B|-3)

\displaystyle=|A|-3

Thus,
|(X\widehat{+}Y)\cap(\{a_{m}\}\widehat{+}A)|\in\{|A|-2,|A|-3\}.

If
|(X\widehat{+}Y)\cap(\{a_{m}\}\widehat{+}A)|=|A|-2
, then

(X\widehat{+}Y)\cap(\{a_{m}\}\widehat{+}A)=(\{a_{m}\}\widehat{+}A)\setminus\{a_{m-1}+a_{m}\}.

This implies that for
\forall a_{i}\in X\setminus\{a_{m-1}\}\ (i\in\mathbb{Z}_{+})
,
\exists x\in X
and
\exists y\in Y
with
x\neq y
such that

a_{m}+a_{i}=x+y
. By taking
a_{i}=a_{m-2}
, we have

a_{m}+a_{m-2}=x+y.

Since
a_{m}>\max\{x,y\}
, then we must have
x>a_{m-2}
and
y>a_{m-2}
. Thus,
x=y=a_{m-1}
. A contradiction! Consequently,
|(X\widehat{+}Y)\cap(\{a_{m}\}\widehat{+}A)|=|A|-3.

Through the following computation,

\displaystyle|X\widehat{+}Y|

\displaystyle=|(X\widehat{+}Y)\cup(\{a_{m}\}\widehat{+}A)|-|\{a_{m}\}\widehat{+}A)|+|(X\widehat{+}Y)\cap(\{a_{m}\}\widehat{+}A)|

\displaystyle=|A\widehat{+}B|-(|A|-1)+(|A|-3)

\displaystyle=(|A|+|B|-3)-(|A|-1)+(|A|-3)

\displaystyle=(|A|-1)+(|B|-1)-3

\displaystyle=|X|+|Y|-3,

we complete the proof that
(X,Y)
is a critical pair.

∎

Lemma 4
.

Let finite sets
A,B\subseteq\mathbb{Z}
be nonempty sets.
(A,B)
is a critical pair and
|A|\geq 5
. If
B\subseteq A
, then
(A,B)
is a standard pair.

Proof.

Let’s use induction on
|A|
to prove Lemma 4.

When
|A|=5
, define
X=A\setminus\{a_{5}\}
and
Y=B\setminus\{b_{n}\}.
According to Lemma 3,
a_{5}=b_{n}
and
(X,Y)
is a critical pair. Since
|X|=4
, according to Theorem 3, we have
X=Y=\{a_{1},a_{2},a_{3},a_{4}\}
and
X\widehat{+}Y=\{a_{1}+a_{4},a_{2}+a_{4},a_{3}+a_{4},a_{1}+a_{2},a_{1}+a_{3}\}.
Apparently,
A=B=\{a_{1},a_{2},a_{3},a_{4},a_{5}\}
. From the proof of Lemma 3, we know that

|(X\widehat{+}Y)\cap({a_{5}}\widehat{+}A)|=|A|-3=2.

It’s easy for us to observe that
a_{2}+a_{4}=a_{5}+a_{1}
and
a_{3}+a_{4}=a_{5}+a_{2}
. By the proof of Theorem 3, we also have
a_{2}+a_{3}=a_{1}+a_{4}
. Consequently,

a_{5}-a_{4}=a_{4}-a_{3}=a_{3}-a_{2}=a_{2}-a_{1}.

Thus,
(A,B)

is a standard pair.

Now, suppose Lemma 4 is true for
|A|\leq m-1\ (m\in\mathbb{Z},m>5)
. Let’s consider the case when
|A|=m
. Note
A^{\prime}=A\setminus\{a_{m}\}
and
B^{\prime}=B\setminus\{b_{n}\}
, then
(A^{\prime},B^{\prime})

is a critical pair and
a_{m}=b_{n}
according to Lemma 3. Thus, by the induction hypothesis,
(A^{\prime},B^{\prime})

is a standard pair. Without loss of generality, for some
\tau
,
d\in\mathbb{Z}
, we may assume that

A^{\prime}=B^{\prime}=\{\tau,\tau+d,\tau+2d,\cdots,\tau+(m-2)d\}

with
\tau<\tau+d<\tau+2d<\cdots<\tau+(m-2)d
.

From the proof of Lemma 3, we know that

|(A^{\prime}\widehat{+}B^{\prime})\cap({a_{m}}\widehat{+}A)|=|A|-3=|{a_{m}}\widehat{+}A|-2.

Thus, for
\forall u\in A^{\prime}\setminus\{\tau+(m-3)d,\tau+(m-2)d\}
,
\exists v\in A^{\prime},w\in B^{\prime}
such that

a_{m}+u=v+w.

When
u=\tau+(m-4)d
,
\max\{v,w\}\geq\tau+(m-3)d
. Without loss of generality, assume that
v\geq w
. Thus,
v\in\{\tau+(m-3)d,\tau+(m-2)d\}.

If
v=\tau+(m-3)d
, then
a_{m}=w+d
. From the definition of
a_{m}
, we know that
a_{m}>\tau+(m-2)d
. Because
w\leq\tau+(m-2)d
, we must have
w=\tau+(m-2)d
. Consequently,
a_{m}=\tau+(m-1)d=b_{n}.

If
v=\tau+(m-2)d
, then
a_{m}=w+2d
. According to the definition of
a_{m}
, we know that
a_{m}>\tau+(m-2)d
. Thus, since
w\leq\tau+(m-2)d
,
w\in\{\tau+(m-3)d,\tau+(m-2)d\}
. Note that
w\neq v
, then there must be
w=\tau+(m-3)d.
Therefore,
a_{m}=\tau+(m-1)d=b_{n}.

As a result, we always have

A=B=\{\tau,\tau+d,\tau+2d,\cdots,\tau+(m-2)d,\tau+(m-1)d\}

with
\tau<\tau+d<\tau+2d<\cdots<\tau+(m-2)d<\tau(m-1)d
. This completes our induction.
∎

Lemma 5
.

Let finite sets
A,B\subseteq\mathbb{Z}
be nonempty sets. If
|A|,|B|\geq 2
and
B\subsetneqq A
, then
|A\widehat{+}B|\geq|A|+|B|-2.

Proof.

Since
B\subsetneqq A
, according to Lemma 2, we have

|A\widehat{+}B|\geq|A|+|B|-3.

According to Lemma 4, if
|A\widehat{+}B|=|A|+|B|-3
, then
A=B
. However, this contradicts with
B\subsetneqq A
. Thus,

|A\widehat{+}B|>|A|+|B|-3.

Therefore, we have
|A\widehat{+}B|\geq|A|+|B|-2
.
∎

Lemma 6
.

Let finite sets
A,B\subseteq\mathbb{Z}
be nonempty sets. If
|A|,|B|\geq 2
and
A\neq B
, then
|A\widehat{+}B|\geq|A|+|B|-2.

Proof.

Using Lemma 5, we only need to consider the case when
A\neq B
and
B\not\subset A
. Without loss of generality, assume
|A|\geq|B|
. If
|A\cap B|=0
, then according to Lemma 1, we have

|A\widehat{+}B|=|A+B|\geq|A|+|B|-1>|A|+|B|-2.

If
|A\cap B|=1
, then note
X=A\cup B
. According to Lemma 1, we have

|A\widehat{+}B|\geq|A\widehat{+}(B\setminus X)|=|A+(B\setminus X)|\geq|A|+|B\setminus X|-1\geq|A|+|B|-2.

Thus, we only need to consider the case when
|A\cup B|\geq 2
. Since
B\not\subset A
, we have
A\cap B\subsetneqq A\cup B
. Moreover,
A\cap B
,
A\cup B\in\mathbb{Z}
and
|A\cup B|\geq 2
. Thus, according to Lemma 5, we have

|(A\cup B)\widehat{+}(A\cap B)|\geq|A\cup B|+|A\cap B|-2=|A|+|B|-2.

Notice that for any
x\neq y
with
x\in A\cup B

and
y\in A\cap B
, if
x\in A
, then
y\in B
. Thus,
x+y\in A\widehat{+}B
. Similarly, if
x\in B
, then
y\in A
and thus
x+y\in A\widehat{+}B
. As a result,

(A\cup B)\widehat{+}(A\cap B)\subseteq A\widehat{+}B.

Therefore,
|A\widehat{+}B|\geq|(A\cup B)\widehat{+}(A\cap B)|\geq|A|+|B|-2
.

∎

Back to the main problem, if
(A,B)
is a critical pair, Lemma 6 indicates that
A=B
. Thus, we have
B\subseteq A
. According to Lemma 4,
(A,B)
is a standard pair when
|A|\geq 5
. This finishes our proof for the sufficiency of the conditions.
∎

Partial Results in
\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}

Theorem 5
.

Let sets
A,B\subseteq\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
be nonempty sets such that
p\geq|A|+|B|-2
and
a_{m}+b_{n}<p
. Then
|A\widehat{+}B|=|A|+|B|-3
if and only if
A=B
and one of the following holds:

(1)

|A|=2
or
|A|=3
;

(2)

|A|=4
and
A=\{a,a+d,c,c+d\}
;

(3)

|A|\geq 5
and
A
is an arithmetic progression.

Proof.

Since
a_{m}+b_{n}<p
, we have
a+b<p
for
\forall a\in A,b\in B
. This leads to

\{a+b:a\in A,b\in B,a\neq b\}=\{a+b\mod{p}:a\in A,b\in B,a\neq b\}.

This equation here indicates that the proof is equivalent to the case in
\mathbb{Z}
, which we have completed.

∎

Theorem 6
.

Let sets
A,B\subseteq\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
be nonempty sets such that
p\geq|A|+|B|
, where
p
is a prime. Suppose
|A|\geq 5
and
(A,B)
is a critical pair. Given
d
as an arbitrary generator of
\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
, then length of the longest gap in
B

with respect to
d
is not less than
|A|
.

Proof.

Let
A=\{\tau+id:i=0,1,\cdots,|A|-1\}
and
B=\{x_{i}d:i=0,1,\cdots,|B|-1\}
, where
\tau\in\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
and
x_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}\cup\{0\}
. Define the length of the longest gap in
B
with respect to
d
as
t
and suppose
t\leq|A|-1
.

Without loss of generality, assume
\tau=0
, or else use

A^{\prime}=\{id:i=0,1,\cdots,|A|-1\}

and

B^{\prime}=\{x_{i}d-\tau:i=0,1,\cdots,|B|-1\}

to represent
A
and
B
. Since
0\in A
, apparently
B\setminus\{0\}\subseteq A\widehat{+}B
.

Define sets
B_{i}\subseteq B
as the following:

B_{i}=\{m_{i}d,(m_{i}+1)d,\cdots,\widehat{m_{i}}d\}\ (m_{i}\leq\widehat{m_{i}}),

where
m_{i+1}-\widehat{m_{i}}\geq 1
. By definition, we can get a partition of
B
, where
I
a finite set of positive integers and
B=\sqcup_{i\in I}B_{i}
. Note
\mathfrak{B}_{1}=\{B_{i}:\widehat{m_{i}}>t\}
and
\mathfrak{B}_{2}=\{B_{i}:\widehat{m_{i}}\leq t\}=\{B_{1},B_{2},\cdots,B_{k}\}\ (1\leq k\leq|B|)
.

We first claim that for
\forall g\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}\cup\{0\}
,
gd\in A\widehat{+}B
if there
\exists j\in I
such that
\widehat{m_{j}}<g<m_{j+1}
and
\widehat{m_{j}}>\widehat{m_{k}}
.

In particular, if
0\leq g<m_{1}
then
gd\in A\widehat{+}B
. Observing that
g-\widehat{m_{j}}<m_{j+1}-\widehat{m_{j}}\leq t+1\leq|A|,
we have
g-\widehat{m_{j}}\leq|A|-1
and thus
(g-\widehat{m_{j}})d\in A
. Since
\widehat{m_{j}}>\widehat{m_{k}}
,
\widehat{m_{j}}>t\geq g-\widehat{m_{j}}.
Then
gd=(g-\widehat{m_{j}})d+\widehat{m_{j}}d\in A\widehat{+}B.

We now proof that for
\forall xd\in A
, if
x\neq 2\widehat{m_{i}}\ (i=1,2,\cdots,k)
, then
xd\in A\widehat{+}B.

To prove this claim, assume that
\widehat{m_{i}}<x<m_{i+1}
for some
i\in I
. Again

xd=(x-\widehat{m_{i}})d+\widehat{m_{i}}d\in A\widehat{+}B,

unless
(x-\widehat{m_{i}})d=\widehat{m_{i}}d
, which indicates
x=2\widehat{m_{i}}
.

For
i=1,2,\cdots,k-1
, if
|B_{i}|\geq 2
, then
2\widehat{m_{i}}d\in A\widehat{+}B
, because
(\widehat{m_{i}}-1)d\in B
and
2\widehat{m_{i}}d=(\widehat{m_{i}}+1)d+(\widehat{m_{i}}-1)d
.

For
i=2,3\cdots,k-1
, if there exists
\widehat{m_{i-1}}
and
m_{i+1}
such that
\widehat{m_{j-1}}<\widehat{m_{i}}<m_{i+1}
, then we claim that
2\widehat{m_{i}}d\in A\widehat{+}B.

Note that
0\leq\widehat{m_{i-1}}<\widehat{m_{i}}<m_{i+1}
. If
2\widehat{m_{i}}<m_{i+1}
, then

m_{i+1}-\widehat{m_{i}}>\widehat{m_{i}}\geq\widehat{m_{i}}-\widehat{m_{i-1}}.

So,
2\widehat{m_{i}}-\widehat{m_{i-1}}<m_{i+1}\leq\widehat{m_{i+1}}\leq t\leq|A|-1
. Thus,
(2\widehat{m_{i}}-\widehat{m_{i-1}})d\in A.
This further leads to
2\widehat{m_{i}}d=(2\widehat{m_{i}}-\widehat{m_{i-1}})d+\widehat{m_{i-1}}d\in A\widehat{+}B.
Similarly, if
2\widehat{m_{i}}\geq m_{i+1}
, then we have
2\widehat{m_{i}}-m_{i+1}\in A
. Thus,

2\widehat{m_{i}}d=(2\widehat{m_{i}}-\widehat{m_{i+1}})d+\widehat{m_{i+1}}d\in A\widehat{+}B.

Combining all these claims, we find the only possible elements in
\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}

that are not in
A\widehat{+}B
:

(1)

\widehat{m_{1}}
, if
\widehat{m_{1}}=0
;

(2)

2\widehat{m_{1}}
;

(3)

2\widehat{m_{k}}.

Apparently, the first two cases can not happen together at the same time unless
\widehat{m_{1}}=2\widehat{m_{1}}=0.
So, at most two elements in
\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
are not in
A\widehat{+}B.
This gives
|A\widehat{+}B|\geq p-2\geq|A|+|B|-2.
A contradiction! Thus, we must have
t\geq|A|.

∎

Theorem 7
.

Let sets
A,B\subseteq\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
be nonempty sets such that
p\geq|A|+|B|
, where
p
is a prime. Suppose
|A|\geq 5
and
(A,B)
is a critical pair. If
A

is a standard set, then
(A,B)
is a standard pair.

Proof.

Define
A
and
B
in the same way as in Theorem 6. According to Theorem 6, the length of the largest gap in
B
with respect to
d
is not less than
|A|
. Denote the largest gap as
[u,v]_{d}
, then
(u-1)d\in B
.
A=\{id:i=0,1,\cdots,|A|-1\}
gives

\{(u-1)d\}\widehat{+}A=\{(u-1+i)d:i=0,1,\cdots,|A|-1\}\subseteq A\widehat{+}B.

Since
0\in A
,
\{0\}\widehat{+}B\subseteq A\widehat{+}B
. According to the definition of
u
, we know that

(\{(u-1)d\}\widehat{+}A)\cap(\{0\}\widehat{+}B)=\{(u-1)d\}.

Observing that

\displaystyle|A\widehat{+}B|

\displaystyle=|A|+|B|-3

\displaystyle\geq|(\{(u-1)d\}\widehat{+}A)\cup(\{0\}\widehat{+}B)|

\displaystyle=|(\{(u-1)d\}\widehat{+}A)|+|(\{0\}\widehat{+}B)|-1

\displaystyle\geq(|A|-1)+(|B|-1)-1

\displaystyle=|A|+|B|-3,

all equalities must hold. Therefore,
\{(u-1)d\}\in A
,
0\in B
and

(5.1)

A\hat{+}B=(\{(u-1)d\}\widehat{+}A)\cup(\{0\}\widehat{+}B).

Define
X=(\{(u-1)d\}\widehat{+}A)\cup(\{0\}\widehat{+}B)
. We can prove that
(w+1)d\in B
for
\forall wd\in B
with
w\neq 1
and
w\neq(u-1).
If not,
(w+1)d\in(A\widehat{+}B)\setminus X,
which is not possible. Applying this argument, we get
d\in B
, for
0\in B
. Similarly, consider
0+2d
, then
0+2d=2d\in B
. So we have
xd\in B
for
\forall x\notin[u,v]_{d}
by repeating the previous argument. In particular,
B
is a standard set and
B=\{b+td:t=0,1,\cdots,|B|-1\}
.

Now, we can apply a similar argument as
[
7
]
to prove that
(A,B)
is a standard pair. We have
A\widehat{+}B=\{sd+(b+td):0\leq s\leq|A|-1,0\leq t\leq|B|-1,sd\neq b+td\}
. Since
sd+(b+td)=(s\pm 1)d+b+(t\mp 1)d
, then even if
sd=b+td
, we have the sum be written into the sum of two distinct elements from
A
and
B
unless
s=t=0
or
s=|A|-1
and
t=|B|-1
. Therefore,
A\widehat{+}B=A+B
unless
b=0
or
(|A|-1)d=b+(|B|-1)d
. By putting
b^{\prime}=b+(|B|-1)d
and forming the arithmetic progression with the common difference
d^{\prime}=-d
, we can reduce the second case to the first one. So,
A\widehat{+}B=A+B
unless
b=0
. Since
A+B\geq\min\{p,|A|+|B|-1\}>|A|+|B|-3
, we must have
b=0
.

Thus, we can write

A\widehat{+}B=\{sd+td\mod{p}:sd\in A,td\in B,sd\neq td\}.

If
sd=td
for
s\neq t
(say without the loss of generality that
s>t
), then

(s-t)d\equiv 0\mod{p}.

Because
d\not\equiv 0\mod{p}
,
p\mid(s-t)
. By our definition for
p
,

|A|\geq(s-t)\geq p>|A|+|B|>|A|,

which is a contradiction. We now must conclude that
sd=td
implies
s=t
.

Again, for the case where
sd=td
, we can write

sd+td=(s\pm 1)d+(t\mp 1)d,

unless
s,t=0
or
s=t=|A|-1=|B|-1
. However, if
|A|\neq|B|
, then we only get the case
s=t=0
, which means that

|A\widehat{+}B|\geq|A+(B\setminus\{0\})|\geq|A|+(|B|-1)-1=|A|+|B|-2,

which is also a contradiction. Therefore, we must have
|A|=|B|
and this completes the proof.

∎

6.
Concluding Remarks & Further Thoughts

We have given an elegant and completely elementary proof for the inverse Erdős-Heilbronn problem in
\mathbb{Z}
and give some partial results on the elementary proof for the inverse Erdős-Heilbronn problem in
\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
. Our strategies in
\mathbb{Z}
need to be further improved to apply to
\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
, for we are considering the equations under the meaning of module
p
in
\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
.

For Theorem 5, we add the technical condition of
