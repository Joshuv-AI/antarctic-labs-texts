AcademiaOS: Automating Grounded Theory Development in Qualitative Research with Large Language Models
Thomas Übellacker

Abstract
AcademiaOS is a first attempt to automate grounded theory development in qualitative research with large language models. Using recent large language models’ language understanding, generation, and reasoning capabilities, AcademiaOS codes curated qualitative raw data such as interview transcripts and develops themes and dimensions to further develop a grounded theoretical model, affording novel insights. A user study (n=19) suggests that the system finds acceptance in the academic community and exhibits the potential to augment humans in qualitative research. AcademiaOS has been made open-source for others to build upon and adapt to their use cases.

1
Introduction

Qualitative researchers are confronted with tedious and costly language tasks when making sense of interview or focus group transcriptions, reports, policies, field notes from observational research, diaries, case studies, and other sources. Transcribing and coding a typical interview takes several hours (
Pope et al., 2000
;
Barrett and Twycross, 2018
p. 63). A transcript or set of codes is often just the beginning, as the researcher tries to make sense of the raw data and transform it into something useful to gain insight and knowledge and develop theories that may describe patterns and phenomena.

Qualitative data may be collected through various tools, while purposive sampling directs the research (
Chun Tie et al., 2019
, p. 3). Interviews are a direct and easy way of exploring phenomena, from open, unstructured formats that allow researchers to dive deeper into studied participants’ narratives and experiences to more rigidly structured formats, essentially questionnaires, that provide more control to the researcher. Interviews are usually recorded and transcribed for further processing (
Barrett and Twycross, 2018
p. 63). A more relaxed way of studying subjects’ views is focus groups, where the researcher speaks to a small group of study participants to guide the conversation and debate about a given topic and to create rich data in the form of notes or transcription (
Kitzinger, 1995
p. 301). Another qualitative data collection methodology, prevalent primarily in ethnography, is field observation, where the researcher observes study subjects in a natural setting (
Emerson et al., 2001
, p. 352). (
Emerson et al., 2001
, pp. 356 - 358) describe generating data from ethnographic qualitative research as a process, starting with a researcher’s mental notes from immersing in the situation, followed by writing down jotted notes, to use as support for later generated, more extensive field notes. Ethnographic research also often builds on diaries and written notes, while organizational research might build on case studies, reports, policies, and other secondary sources. The bandwidth of qualitative data sources is broad, but most are textual or have established ways to transform them into textual data (i.e., transcribing).

Researchers often follow established coding practices to manage large volumes of unstructured textual sources. Coding often involves systematically generating a codebook (
Weston et al., 2001
) to code transcriptions. Another popular approach is the "Gioia method" (
Gioia et al., 2013
), where the researcher extracts emerging patterns and concepts from the source documents directly to aggregate and interpret them only in the following steps. These developed codes can then be further used for qualitative data analysis and theory development. The notion of developing theoretical models from data is called grounded theory development (
Chun Tie et al., 2019
).

Gioia et al., 2013
define a transparent process for analyzing qualitative data to develop theoretical models from interviews. Their process is meant to move a researcher from raw qualitative data to increasingly abstract conceptual categories, starting with initial coding, where a researcher codes and underlines relevant concepts in the data, resulting in an extensive list of first-order concepts still stated in the language of the source document. They then use these first-order concepts to produce a list of more abstract second-order themes that try to encapsulate the idea of the first-order codes in more academic language. Lastly, they aggregate the second-order themes into even more abstract "aggregate dimensions." These are then used as the basis for developing theories.
Gioia et al., 2013
mention the importance of understanding the dynamic relationships between these concepts but have yet to offer a concrete methodology to get to these. They assume that by following this "Gioia method," a researcher has familiarized themselves well enough with the underlying literature to understand these relationships.

Another approach to grounded theory development is the
Eisenhardt, 1989
method, which focuses on constructing models from case studies. This approach starts with a within-case analysis to gain familiarity with the data and generate a preliminary theory. From there starts a highly iterative process, which
Eisenhardt, 1989
calls "Shaping Hypotheses," iteratively comparing data and developed constructs and verifying that the emergent relationships between the developed constructs fit with the evidence in the data. They treat case studies as replications of experiments, either strengthening or weakening a hypothesis.

Structured approaches to qualitative analysis and theory development, such as those introduced by
Gioia et al., 2013
and
Eisenhardt, 1989
, were criticized for being used as mere "templates," often oversimplifying or ignoring the underlying methodological assumptions to fit in cases where those assumptions do not hold (
Gioia et al., 2022
, p. 241) or that using these templates limit methodological innovation in qualitative research (
Gioia et al., 2022
, p. 242). Even Gioia emphasizes that the methodology named after him should not be understood as a "cookbook" but as a tool adapted to each research context. He highlights the importance of researchers accounting for informants’ interpretations and constructions of reality in their reporting and not merely applying the methodology as a rigid framework (
Gioia et al., 2022
, p. 235). Building on the existing literature, it is clear that structured approaches like those of
Gioia et al., 2013
and
Eisenhardt, 1989
provide a framework for grounded theory development. However, they also highlight the necessity for flexibility and adaptation in qualitative research. Qualitative research tasks, both data collection and analysis, are time-consuming and expensive and limit the empirical data that a single research team can study.
Kindsiko and Poltimäe, 2019
support this notion by outlining that sample size in empirical research depends on funding and the size of the research team. However,
Bowen, 2008
outlines how the sample size influences research validity and suggests limiting sample size by saturation, i.e., stopping the addition of more interviews, case studies, or other samples when those do not add significant new information. Now, how do we add research rigor by increasing sample size while keeping manual effort low? The answer might be found in computational automation.

In quantitative research, automating data preparation and theory development is an actively researched topic under names such as "data mining" or "machine learning," where computer programs learn from observations to develop mathematical models, allowing them to estimate future instances in a positivist paradigm. Qualitative research problems, however, come with less structured or codifiable information and rely on the knowledge and interpretation of the researcher. At the same time, with the rise of large language models (LLMs), we have access to a technology platform, shifting the paradigm of computational understanding and reasoning on textual data to a level close to humans, paired with extensive general knowledge. This new technology platform presents an opportunity to simulate well-defined research processes at scale. Tasks such as coding 100 interview transcripts are time-consuming for a single researcher. The hypothesis is that with the proper setup, LLMs can process all transcripts in parallel within a few minutes. A researcher in the field of organization theory might consider using two or three case studies to develop a theoretical model. When finding empirical evidence in case studies is an automated, parallelizable process, the barrier to using 20 - 30 different case studies is drastically minimized, offering opportunities for more statistically relevant qualitative studies.

Thus, the potential for automating parts of the qualitative research process with LLMs is ripe for exploration. This paper explores the following research question: "How can a foundational open-source platform be effectively designed and implemented to leverage large language models to automate grounded theory development?". To that end, AcademiaOS is proposed and implemented as an open-source platform for automating or augmenting grounded theory development tasks such as coding, dimension aggregation, and theory development. AcademiaOS offers the scientific community a new methodology for conducting qualitative research that is transparent, accessible, and extensible (through its open-source character) and allows for broader evidence through the cost-effectiveness of parallelizing analysis of multiple qualitative sources simultaneously. This system could have a far-reaching impact in the social sciences, particularly in organization theory, but also in other disciplines where qualitative data is relevant.

The rest of this paper describes AcademiaOS and the technology behind it in more detail. In addition, an exploratory survey has been conducted to learn how students, professionals, and researchers use AcademiaOS and to guide future development. The platform is made open-source on GitHub under the MIT license, and a running instance is hosted on academia-os.org.

2
Earlier Work

There have been attempts to automate qualitative analysis.
Berente et al., 2019
make an effort to develop a theoretical process for computationally intensive grounded theory development, proposing a theoretical, computational methodology to automate the following four steps for grounded theory development: (1) Sampling and data collection, (2) synchronic analysis, (3) lexical framing and (4) diachronic analysis using artificial-intelligence-based tools. They describe the computational process as evolving around a predefined but dynamic lexicon rather than synchronously "coding" emerging concepts. They propose to mine concepts using taxonomies.

Marathe and Toyama, 2018
discuss the possibility of automating the coding of interviews based on predefined human-annotated codebooks. Similar approaches have been implemented by
Lennon et al., 2021
, according to their own analyses resulting in human-level accuracy.
Rietz and Maedche, 2021
propose a semi-automated supervised machine-learning solution that learns coding rules from human annotators and applies them to a broader dataset. Still, defining rules or annotating a codebook beforehand assumes the researcher already has assumptions about the patterns in the analyzed qualitative literature. Furthermore, the academic papers mentioned above used old machine-learning techniques and ignored LLMs, while some commercial platforms are slowly adopting them. The commercial platform
ATLAS.ti, 2023
announced test versions of their automated coding features in early 2023, splitting qualitative literature into paragraphs and coding them one by one using OpenAI’s LLMs. Other commercial platforms like elicit.org emerged, primarily using LLMs to automate literature review processes. Still, more is needed to know about how researchers use these new AI features on those platforms. Also, these applications automate only small portions of qualitative research processes and have yet to dive into the field of automating grounded theory development. This leads to the question of whether grounded theory development can be automated with LLMs.

3
Large Language Models

Large language models (LLMs) are a new technology platform based on transformer models, pre-trained on large datasets using self-supervision, encoding general and transferable knowledge in billions of parameters
Roberts et al., 2020
. Unexpected capabilities such as reasoning and interaction emerged from learning "simple" next-token prediction at scale. These pre-trained foundation models are often fine-tuned to follow instructions (
Ouyang et al., 2022
), to return structured output, or to be conversational (as seen with ChatGPT). While older models such as BERT would generally be regarded as LLM, in this paper, the term is exclusively used for models with a performance similar to or better than the GPT-3 base models. LLMs have been popularized and mass-adopted with the recent release of ChatGPT at the end of 2022. They have been applied to automate processes throughout industries (
Wulf and Meierhofer, 2023
, p. 4).

An inference interaction with an LLM typically consists of a natural language prompt (input) and completion (response). In OpenAI’s conversational models used throughout this paper (GPT-3.5 and newer), the inference prompt may consist of multiple "messages": generic system messages that set the frame and a history of user and assistant messages (see Appendices 1-11 for examples).

Without the need for fine-tuning, LLMs exhibit the ability to learn and generalize from information in the inference prompt (
Brown et al., 2020
). One-shot or few-shot learning refers to passing samples in the prompt, whereas zero-shot learning refers to providing no samples but having the model complete an explicit instruction. This type of inference contrasts with common task-specific fine-tuning and is commonly called "in-context learning" (
Dong et al., 2022
).

Although being pre-trained, LLMs store tremendous amounts of implicit knowledge in their parameters, that knowledge remains limited in its depth and currency and requires costly training to update. Therefore, augmenting the LLM inference with an information retrieval system, an architecture commonly called "Retrieval-Augmented Generation" (RAG), has been shown to reduce hallucination and improve factualness and interpretability (
Lewis et al., 2020
). As general models, LLMs can measure semantic similarity between two text strings. Their similarity can be calculated computationally efficiently using cosine similarity on their LLM-internal vector representation. RAG uses this straightforward way of information retrieval to concatenate retrieved, relevant text for in-context learning (
Lewis et al., 2020
). Augmenting LLM inference with information retrieval from raw input documents may help automate theory development.

4
Approach

To explore the current possibilities of automating grounded theory development with LLMs, I propose, develop, and test a piece of software that orchestrates LLM inferences with human supervision. AcademiaOS is a platform for qualitative researchers to automate their grounded theory development. The platform guides the user through a predefined process, and while most of the data analysis and theory development parts are automated, the user owns the supervision and control. To ensure user privacy and high maintainability, for potential future developers and open-source contributors to not worry about frontend-backend interaction, most of the computation is performed locally in the browser, consuming external APIs such as the OpenAI developer platform for LLM inference directly.

4.1
Data Curation

Qualitative research is a broad field working with various qualitative information sources, from interviews over policies to organizational case studies. To ensure versatility, the platform affords users to curate their source documents in multiple ways. If the user already has a corpus of curated sources, they can directly upload one or many source documents. The system extracts textual information for further use directly from documents in PDF, JSON, or TXT format. To support scanned PDF documents, the system pre-processes problematic PDF files by applying optical character recognition via Adobe PDF Services. Every file is transformed into a text string and handled as an individual source document.

A second option helps the user curate relevant academic literature from free-text search. The search term is used to retrieve academic literature from the SemanticScholar search engine that indexes more than 200 million academic papers. To interface with the SemanticScholar REST API, SemanticScholarJS was introduced as an open-source TypeScript library and published under the MIT license. SemanticScholar returns at most 100 papers, which are then vector-embedded using OpenAI’s text embeddings and re-ranked according to their abstracts’ semantic similarity to the initial search query and correspondingly re-ranked. Any papers without an abstract are filtered out, resulting in a few documents relevant to the user’s search query.

4.2
Data Coding

Assuming that the user might have an idea about their research’s direction, the platform affords the user to add a free-text remark or choose from an LLM-generated research question, which is used for in-context learning in the following inference prompts. This interactivity respects the expertise of the researcher while providing AI-assisted efficiency.

Following the Gioia method (
Gioia et al., 2013
), the system initializes a three-step data analysis process. Implementing the Gioia method aligns the platform with established qualitative research practices, lending academic rigor to the process. This structured approach facilitates the systematic transformation of raw data into initial codes, second-order themes, and aggregate dimensions, used later for grounded theory development.

First, the system creates initial codes from the raw documents. Initial codes are short text strings describing emergent themes, concepts, and patterns in the language of the raw document. To that end, every input document is split into chunks of max. 10,000 characters to overcome context-size limitations and process arbitrarily large input. A chunk overlap of 50 characters was introduced to mitigate losing information in the intersection of two chunks. Every chunk is then inserted as context in the LLM inference prompt with a natural language instruction to return a JSON array. The output is then parsed and stored in a global array of initial codes.

Next, the initial codes are used to develop second-order themes. Second-order themes aggregate and interpret semantically similar initial codes and are expressed in a more abstract, academically correct language. To that end, the initial codes are split into reasonably-sized chunks and used in-context with a natural language instruction to return a JSON object of second-order themes as keys and arrays of related initial codes as values. This JSON object is again parsed and stored as a global object.

Finally, the second-order themes are used to develop aggregate dimensions. These aggregate dimensions are even more abstract, quantifiable concepts. Since second-order themes already limit the number of codes to a manageable count, further splitting into chunks is not necessary and the second-order themes are directly used in-context together with a natural language instruction to return a JSON object of aggregate dimensions as keys and arrays of related second-order themes as values. This JSON object is again parsed and stored as a global object for further use.

In the next step, the developed initial codes, second-order themes, and aggregate dimensions are used to develop grounded theories. (
Wacker, 1998
, pp. 364-367) found that to be considered ’good,’ a theory must exhibit the following virtues: uniqueness, conservatism, generalizability, fecundity, simplicity, internal consistency, empirical riskiness, and abstraction. To ensure some of those virtues, such as uniqueness, conservatism, and fecundity, the aggregate dimensions are used for the machine to "brainstorm" applicable theories that exist in the literature. As this brainstormed output is not intended to return accurate information but merely to scrutinize the obvious and increase serendipity, this step happens in a single inference prompt without information retrieval.

Next, to ground any interrelationships of concepts used in the final model in the data, the LLM first creates concept tuples, then uses these concept tuples for retrieval-augmented generation, searching for relevant paragraphs (chunks of max. 1000 characters, 50-character chunk-overlap) in the raw documents using cosine similarity search on their OpenAI vector embeddings and the concept tuples. Next, given the four most similar paragraphs, the LLM is prompted to generate a one-sentence summary about the relationship of a given concept tuple. This summary might include information about any evidence for correlation or causation, directedness, strength, mediatedness, conditional interaction, dynamics, or feedback loops.

Previously developed applicable theories, interrelationships, aggregate dimensions, second-order codes, and the user’s free-hand remarks are then fed into an LLM prompt, instructed to describe a comprehensive, novel theoretical model.

Next, another one-shot LLM inference transforms a given model description into a MermaidJS visualization. MermaidJS is a scripting language for creating flowcharts and similar graphics. Converting model descriptions into visualizations using MermaidJS not only aids in understanding complex theories but also provides a straightforward, communicative tool for sharing findings. First-order and second-order codes provided in the prompt are used to visualize evidence and support the notion of groundedness.

Finally, a last zero-shot LLM inference criticizes the developed model for novelty, conciseness, clarity, and theoretical insight. The user can then decide whether to start another modeling iteration with the critique fed into the modeling prompt, resulting in a feedback loop with human oversight.

4.4
Notes on the Technical Implementation

The system was initially considered a conglomerate of separate frontend and backend services. While this design decision would have come with the advantage of controlling any requests that go to the OpenAI developer platform, it would have slowed down development, maintenance, and hosting for the course of the project and beyond for future developers and open-source contributors. Thus, the decision was to run the application entirely in the browser. TypeScript as a primary programming language was chosen for its strong typing compared to vanilla JavaScript and, thus, higher maintainability and more robust code quality. Additionally, TypeScript’s extensive web development community offers abundant resources, libraries, and support, which is crucial for both the initial development and future iterations of the platform. Moreover, the decision to run the entire application in the browser, using ReactJS as a frontend framework and AntDesign as a component library, was driven by the need for an accessible, responsive, user-friendly interface. The ReactJS framework, through its component-based architecture, allows for efficient rendering and state management, which is essential in a dynamic environment where the state can frequently change through user interactions. The browser-based approach also aligns with the privacy concerns inherent in qualitative research. AcademiaOS minimizes the risks associated with data transfer and storage on remote servers by processing data locally on the user’s machine.

All results reported in this paper are based on OpenAI’s gpt-4-1106-preview model. To increase traceability and transparency, AcademiaOS affords users to easily set up a proxy to Helicone, an open-source monitoring platform specifically for LLM applications. Through Helicone, users maintain complete control and insight over their OpenAI API usage and the corresponding costs. In addition, Helicone logs all LLM inference requests. Monitoring is convenient during development to see where the LLM goes wrong and tune prompts. In addition, the platform affords saving and restoring developed codes and models for further development using JSON serialization and deserialization. This comes in especially handy during the development and testing phases to mitigate performing redundant steps and to facilitate a smooth development and testing workflow. The serialized data can also easily be shared between multiple users.

The field of large language models is actively researched and worked on. During this project, OpenAI and other vendors released new models with new capabilities. OpenAI’s most recent model, GPT-4-turbo, allows for larger context windows in a single inference prompt but also comes with an optional "JSON-mode" parameter, which ensures that the inference response is in a valid, parsable JSON format. Since many of AcademiaOS’ inference prompts expect data in a structured format (e.g., arrays of values), enforcing the JSON mode significantly increased the application’s robustness. Still, as LLM inference can occasionally yield invalid or unexpected results, rerunning the system on the same input often results in varied outputs, thereby increasing the probability of obtaining a correct response with subsequent attempts.

4.5
Notes on Epistemology and Ontology

Grounded theory development, as practiced by
Gioia et al., 2013
or
Eisenhardt, 1989
, generally follows a constructivist epistemology (
Charmaz and Thornberg, 2021
). Constructivism in grounded theory proposes that knowledge is actively constructed through the interaction between the researcher and the researched subject. It argues for a subjective nature of reality and knowledge generation where the researcher’s perceptions shape the interpretative process (
Levers, 2013
, pp. 3-4). Further, grounded theory assumes that multiple realities of subjective constructions of individuals exist in a fluid nature of the reality of social interactions. As grounded theory development generates theories from observations of empirical data without previously established theories, grounded theory development is an inherently inductive approach.

Automating grounded theory development with large language models challenges epistemological and ontological assumptions. Epistemologically, Introducing LLMs adds an element of objectivity to the traditionally subjective approach. They process qualitative data in a way independent of a researcher’s biases. However, the researcher keeps the responsibility of interpreting and contextualizing the LLM’s output, preserving the constructivist nature of the process. At the same time, LLMs ontologically add a digital reality to the existing subjective realities. This digital reality offers an algorithmically created perspective on the processed qualitative data that can support or challenge the researcher’s interpretation.

4.6
Notes on Temperature, Robustness and Serendipity

To get an intuition on how model temperature changes the system’s output, outputs of gradual uniform temperature increase are recorded and qualitatively analyzed. To accommodate deterministic reproducibility, a seed parameter (324234) has been utilized with the employed gpt-4-1106-preview model for grounded theory development from a single podcast transcript from the How I Built This podcast.

Appendices 61 - 65 show that the three attempts with temperatures of 0, 0.5, and 1 resulted in consistent and robust outputs. In contrast, the attempt with a temperature of 1.5 became unstable, resulting in non-sensical text in the latter part of the model description and an invalid MermaidJS visualization. The attempts with temperatures of 0 and 0.5 resulted in internally consistent and integrated models. The attempt with a temperature of 1 produced a theoretical model with less integrity, meaning not or only loosely connected aspects and thus missing a consistent narrative. An increased temperature could, in this case, not be observed to lead to a higher degree of serendipity, that is, the quality of pleasant yet unexpected results. This might highlight the still objective, reproducible, and deterministic nature of grounded theory development and that randomness in the data analysis does not necessarily lead to better results. However, while providing a sense of intuition, the sample size of this experiment (n=1) might need to be larger to generalize.

5
Survey

An exploratory, qualitative survey has been conducted to assess user interaction with AcademiaOS and to guide future development. Researchers, professionals, and students with a background in qualitative research were chosen via convenience sampling for this evaluation. The participants (n=19), who were deliberately selected for their relevance to the platform’s intended user base and thus thought to yield the most informative and relevant data for assessing AcademiaOS, were provided with a link to a survey hosted on the Qualtrics platform.

The study’s methodology involved guiding participants through the AcademiaOS platform. This was achieved by direct interaction with the platform, through a video demonstration, or a combination, depending on the participant’s preference. Eight respondents reported testing the platform with a corpus of university policies on ChatGPT use that was provided alongside the survey. Other participants reported using datasets such as interview transcripts or academic papers from a basic literature review. Only 4 participants based their answers solely on the video demonstration (see Appendix 17). Following their experience with the platform, participants were asked to share their insights and opinions. The survey primarily consisted of open-ended questions, allowing for detailed and nuanced feedback.

The feedback encompasses various aspects of the platform, including user interface design, assessing the user’s initial interaction, feature exploration, and usability, but also the effectiveness of the system’s output and perceived impact on the user’s immediate research environment. Additional exploratory questions were asked about their current qualitative research processes and context.

Partial survey responses have been recorded. However, the 28 responses have been filtered and cleaned only to include 19 finished responses for further analysis. The other responses were very sparse, and it could not be trusted that they spent sufficient time familiarizing themselves with the platform. Another participant stated, "I don’t generally work with qualitative data," whose response was also filtered due to not belonging to the target population. Of the 19 residual responses, 14 were students, and the remaining participants were professionals and academic researchers (see Appendix 12). This might skew the responses to students’ perspectives.
Peterson and Merunka, 2014
argue that convenience samples of students are problematic as they do not generalize to the general consumer population. However, they also explain that students might be an appropriate sample if they represent a population of interest. As the sampled students have at least some levels of experience in qualitative research, they sufficiently represent the studied group.

6.1
Status Quo

Participants were asked to reflect on their current qualitative research methodology. They reported using primary sources such as interviews, observations, surveys, and panels and secondary data sources such as case studies, reports, meta-analyses, historical data, reports, and expert opinions (Appendix 13).

Asked about their research methods, they reported data collection tools such as voice recordings, Microsoft Forms, or Google Forms for online surveys, data analysis methods such as thematic analysis, content analysis, literature review and case law analysis, empirical research, and knowledge discovery from existing datasets. One respondent mentioned testing qualitative data with quantitative research tools, while other respondents mentioned statistics and natural language processing techniques (such as named-entity recognition and topic modeling), hinting that there are tools to "quantify" qualitative data and that in research settings, the line between qualitative and quantitative research is often blurred. Interestingly, one respondent even mentioned using language models and the previously mentioned RAG architecture for their qualitative research (see Appendix 14). For systematic theory development, respondents mentioned grounded theory methodology, conceptual framework development, mind maps, and flowcharts, while some respondents see themselves merely as model users and less as creators or are unsure about their methodologies (see Appendix 15).

Inquiring about the AI tools they utilize in their research (see Appendix 16), some participants reported limited to no use of AI tools, not having used them or only using them rarely. Many respondents mentioned using ChatGPT with varying degrees of reliance and purposes such as brainstorming, idea generation, speeding up coding processes, or narrowing down searches. One respondent even noted the need for extensive fact-checking due to high error rates with ChatGPT. Other participants mentioned more specific AI and machine learning platforms and libraries: Fairseq, HuggingFace, Pytorch, OpenAI, AzureML, TensorFlow, scikit-learn, RapidMiner, KNIME, NLTK, SpaCy, Gensim and BERT. These reported AI tools underline the perceived importance of quantifying qualitative data through natural language processing.

6.2
Initial Interaction and Feature Exploration

The second part of the survey aimed to learn how participants perceived the platform’s initial interaction and feature exploration. The participants generally rated the platform as "somewhat easy" to learn, with some variance (see Appendix 18). Participants mentioned that the video helped them sufficiently understand how the system works. One participant suggested adding popups to guide the user, and another suggested that the affordances were not entirely clear: "[…] you should mention that the user can also upload non-pdf files […]" (appendix 19). Overall, participants reported finding it easy to find and use the option to search for academic papers (see Appendix 20) and to upload their own sources (see Appendix 21). However, they had more issues finding and using the options to "code" their sources (see Appendix 22) and to develop the theory (see Appendix 23). In general, participants found the user interface to be "somewhat" to "strongly" intuitive and user-friendly, with only one outlier who "strongly disagrees" (see Appendix 24).

As for difficulties navigating the platform (see Appendix 25), participants reported they expected more descriptive prompts and guidance: "I couldn’t find the coding help function," "the insert key thing was rather hidden," and "about the code part, didn’t really understand how to use it." One user noted that some buttons needed to be visible on mobile and experienced long loading times. Some improvement suggestions (see appendix 26) included "make it […] less technical", to add "filters for finding papers", to "add examples" and an "instructions page" to better guide users. Furthermore, better error handling with retry policies was suggested, together with the idea to "combine different searches or datasets in one coding tab." These feedback points are actionable and specific enough to guide the platform’s development.

6.3
Usability, Effectiveness, and User Satisfaction

Study participants seem to be "somewhat" to "extremely" satisfied with the coding process, with only one "extremely dissatisfied" outlier (see Appendix 27). Even more promisingly, participants think the system would create better codes than when they code manually (see Appendix 28). Only one participant thinks the system-generated output codes would be "somewhat worse" than their own. This is to be taken with a grain of salt as most respondents will have answered that only intuitively, without any actual comparison. Challenges in coding lie in multilingual documents (see Appendix 29). One respondent noted that when the source documents were not purposefully selected and had too much variation in their content, the machine could not generate meaningful codes: "[…] it was not able to catch that and make codes relevant to the research question". This seems to be an issue related to the paper retrieval and less so with the coding process. The average participant generally rated AcademiaOS as "very effective" in augmenting the coding process on qualitative data (see Appendix 30).

Regarding the theory development process, participants reported being somewhat to extremely satisfied (see Appendix 31). The respondents also reported that the system-produced output was perceived as better than potential manual development, with only three respondents each reporting that the system would do "somewhat worse" or "about the same" than when done manually (see Appendix 32). When asked about instances where the produced theory did not meet their expectations (see Appendix 33), one participant mentioned, "It was kind of complex, and I often didn’t really get where it was going with the theory.", indicating that they would have preferred a more straightforward model, while another one wrote "[…] theory seems to cover the content of the sources pretty generally but is not really about the research question I posed." indicating that there was a mismatch between the free-hand remarks and the model output. Despite these instances, respondents perceived the platform to be moderately effective to extremely effective, with a skew to the latter (see Appendix 34).

6.4
Participant-Reported Impact

When asked about whether AcademiaOS would impact their qualitative research process, most participants answered with "probably yes" to "definitely yes" (Appendix 35), mentioning reasons such as speeding up their research processes ("Much faster," "It will speed up the research," "It would make the coding and theory production much faster"), to act as a tool for inspiration ("Multiple draft iterations to inspire/shortcut the manual process," "I would use it to rapidly prototype theories […]", "[…] compare and maybe find things I missed before."), and as general research support ("It will urge many scientists to increase their throughput […] potential to mitigate the information retrieval and retention of humans", "[…] it would help me to link concepts easier", "Making it easier for me to do my research, especially in hours where I cannot concentrate that much") (see Appendix 36). When asked about how AcademiaOS would change the output of their academic research (see Appendix 37), participants noted "more varied and tested theories, better theories through more input ideas, more feedback," "use the models created as part of a clear visualization" and "[…] to be more reproducible". All respondents agree that AcademiaOS helps them ground their research on more data (see Appendix 41) and that it helps them conduct research faster (see Appendix 42). At the same time, most participants also agree that it helps them find more impactful and unexpected results (see Appendix 43).

However, participants also noted certain limitations such as ethical considerations ("It feels like cheating to me… I would not feel comfortable using it.", "It would make me feel too replaceable"), doubts about quality ("I also don’t quite trust the quality (mostly from experience with ChatGPT), I would always want to check it for what it misses.", "[…] it might fail to generate very latent codes") and doubts of potential (reinforced) biases ("[…] it may cause people to not properly look at the nuances of their interviews or papers", "Currently it is using a single AI model, which makes it difficult to rule out its biases.") (see Appendix 38). In addition, some participants fear being replaced by AI (see Appendix 40).

Respondents generally feel excited about the new possibilities that AcademiaOS provides (see Appendix 39). Most participants even stated they would continue using the system for their research (see Appendix 45). To an even greater extent, they would recommend AcademiaOS to their friends (see Appendix 46), indicating that they might know potential users who fit the intended target group better. Participants generally also feel excited about future versions of AcademiaOS (see Appendix 47), pointing out that despite the discussed doubts and limitations, the technology is promising for their research field.

7
Example Applications

This section presents a series of illustrative use-cases for AcademiaOS.

7.1
Modeling University Policies on ChatGPT Use

A corpus of 46 scraped public university policies on ChatGPT and LLMs were uploaded to AcademiaOS to build a theoretical model to answer the research question: "How can educational institutions adapt their academic integrity policies to effectively address the challenges posed by AI writing tools like ChatGPT?". From the 46 papers, AcademiaOS produced 1019 first-order codes, which in the next step were reduced and interpreted to 67 second-order themes and, in turn, reduced to 7 aggregate dimensions (see Appendix 49). After brainstorming applicable existing theories (see Appendix 50), the system then investigated the interrelationships between concept tuples (see Appendix 51) to generate the first iteration of a theoretical model (see Appendix 52). The system then critiqued the model (see Appendix 53) to produce the second iteration of the theoretical model (see Appendix 54). Note that the user is in control of the number of iterations.

The result was the Integrated Education Adaptation Framework for AI Readiness (IEAFAR) as a comprehensive model for educational adaptation to artificial intelligence. Its six dimensions, AI Integration and Educational Dynamics (AI-ED), Adaptive Compliance and Policy Evolution (ACPE), Academic and Instructional Resilience (AIR), AI-Driven Educational Innovation (ADEI), Ethical, Legal, and Societal Harmony (ELSH), and Collaborative Stakeholder Engagement (CSE) emphasize the importance of holistic, iterative processes in adapting to AI, with interconnected relationships among these dimensions. IEAFAR promotes stakeholder collaboration, focusing on continuous learning, flexible adaptation, and balancing innovation and ethical, legal, and societal considerations.

7.2
Exploring the “How I Built This” Transcript Dataset

The "How I Built This" podcast is a format interviewing innovators about the things (such as products or companies) they have created. The hypothesis is that AcademiaOS can explore the rich and extensive interview dataset to detect interesting patterns in how entrepreneurs start projects. The system coded the transcripts of 4 podcast episodes to 499 first-order codes, 35 second-order themes, and 7 aggregate dimensions (see Appendix 55). It then generated applicable theories and explored the interrelationships between concept tuples before generating the first iteration of the theoretical model. This time, the AI-generated the "Dynamic Entrepreneurial Synthesis Model (DESM)" (see appendix 56) grounded in the podcast transcripts.

The Dynamic Entrepreneurial Synthesis Model describes the multifaceted journey of business development, integrating personal growth with the development of innovative products. Community, market understanding, social responsibility, and strategic financial planning are central. Further, the model suggests that strong relationships, market traction, and effective leadership are core to successful entrepreneurship.

7.3
Modeling the Cardiovascular Health Effects of Alcohol

The following example is slightly different, as policies or interview transcripts are not uploaded. Instead, it starts with a free-text search for academic papers to further explore, code, and model the topic. AcademiaOS selected 26 academic publications from SemanticScholar to use for further processing. It then codes the abstracts of these publications to 395 first-order codes (see appendix 57), 35 second-order themes, and 7 aggregate dimensions. These codes are then used to create the first-iteration model (see Appendix 58), which gets critiqued (see Appendix 59) to create the second-iteration model (see Appendix 60). In this case, the system was able to create an easy-to-grasp model called "The Dynamic Socio-bio-psychological Model of Alcohol Impact (DSBPAI)," connecting the "environmental context grid" with the "individual health trajectory," facilitating new research angles. The model shows reciprocal feedback mechanisms and causal correlations, highlighting the multidimensionality of influences from molecular to societal scales.

8
Discussion

The following section critically examines the findings from the implementation and the user study of AcademiaOS. This discussion aims to contextualize these findings within the broader scope of existing research, address implications, acknowledge limitations, and suggest directions for future research. The primary objective of this study was to design and implement an open-source platform to automate or augment grounded theory development. An accompanying user survey was designed to assess the platform’s effectiveness. The findings suggest that the platform is well-received within the academic community. AcademiaOS’ capabilities in typically labor-intensive tasks such as coding qualitative data and developing theoretical models constitute a significant step in augmenting human researchers in qualitative research.

The user survey provides relevant insight into the target population’s opinion and experience with AcademiaOS. Respondent’s diverse use of primary and secondary qualitative sources and various data collection and analysis methods emphasize the multifaceted nature of qualitative research. The inclusion of AI tools such as ChatGPT and other natural language processing technologies highlights the trend toward integrating quantitative methods into qualitative research. This blurs the boundaries between traditional qualitative research and quantitative research. By leveraging technologies like AcademiaOS, researchers can uncover patterns and insights that might otherwise remain obscured due to the voluminous nature of qualitative data. This new technology could shift how qualitative research is conducted, making it more efficient and comprehensive while maintaining and following the established framework and corresponding research paradigms of grounded theory development (
Gioia et al., 2013
;
Eisenhardt, 1989
). AcademiaOS embodies a pragmatic approach, joining the subjectivity of human interpretation with the efficiency and scale of LLMs.

The general satisfaction with the coding process and the belief that the system could generate better codes than manual coding is promising. However, participants also noted challenges in coding multilingual documents and dealing with varied content sources, suggesting that there is room for improvement in the system’s sensitivity to other data types. Also, the participant’s satisfaction with the theory development process and illustrated example applications such as policy or interview analysis appear promising. However, user feedback suggests that the system might still have issues aligning closely with a user’s research goals.

The reported potential impact of AcademiaOS speeding up research processes and providing inspiration is a significant positive outcome. However, ethical concerns and doubts about quality and bias indicate the need for a robust framework ensuring ethical use and quality control. The participant’s excitement about the technology’s potential and willingness to recommend and continue using the platform signals a positive outlook for AcademiaOS in the academic community.

9
Limitations

While AcademiaOS introduces a novel approach to automating grounded theory development, this work has several limitations. First, through its reliance on LLMs, the system inherits some of the common limitations those models exhibit.
Chen et al., 2023
found that LLMs perform worse than common information retrieval systems in answering questions factually correctly, especially in a few-shot in-context learning context. The theory development use-case, however, is not per se a knowledge generation task that requires high levels of accuracy or informativeness, as long as those metrics are present enough together with coherency, relevancy, helpfulness, and validity, all measures where
Chen et al., 2023
found good performance. As LLMs’ output is open, sometimes unexpected out-of-range completions, such as incorrect MermaidJS visualization script syntax or wrong JSON fields, can occur. This can only partly be mitigated by writing stricter prompts (e.g., specifying the output schema or providing a specific example).
Kocoń et al., 2023
found that state-of-the-art AI solutions generally outperform current LLMs on common NLP tasks, suggesting that specialized models could also perform better on some functions where AcademiaOS employed LLMs, such as the coding process. A benchmarking of different technologies was not in the scope of this work. The inherent bias of models like GPT-4 (
Bubeck et al., 2023
, pp. 86 - 89) might pose challenges to sensitive topics, such as when working with protected attributes. However, since the idea of grounded theory development is to base any hypotheses on the curated source data, there is little room for biased interpretation, neither for human nor machine inference.

While the designed LLM-supported process allows for coding qualitative documents for grounded theory development at scale, documents such as interview transcripts and their antecedent codes might miss latent information and nuances that a researcher who conducted the interviews themselves might possess, immersing themselves more profoundly in the studied environment. An LLM can only partially compensate for that with the extensive general knowledge it draws on, potentially leading to misinterpretations or overgeneralizations in the developed theories. Therefore, qualitative research might develop to be a joint effort between human and machine inference.
Jiang et al., 2021
studied human-AI interaction in qualitative research. They pointed out another possible limitation: researchers might be reluctant to have AI to eliminate the "uncertainty" from their research efforts. They argue that researchers value the inefficiencies in working with qualitative data and that mistakes in interview coding, for instance, lead to higher serendipity and new perspectives. Automation might be a hindrance in this process. However, survey participants reported expecting AcademiaOS to help them gain more unexpected results (see Appendix 43).
Bouschery et al., 2023
explore the same aspect in product innovation teams whose methodologies are similar to those of academic researchers and found that those teams profit from larger problem spaces and solution spaces when working jointly with AI.

There are also data privacy concerns. AcademiaOS, in its current form, draws on the OpenAI developer platform for LLM inferences. Therefore, sensitive data not meant to be shared with external entities can not be processed with the proposed system for ethical and legal reasons. However, to ensure data privacy, the system can be modified to run on a self-hosted LLM (such as a Llama2 or a Mistral 7B instance), ensuring complete data control.

Another limitation is scope. AcademiaOS is primarily designed for qualitative research in fields such as organization theory and may not be as effective for other research areas where qualitative data plays a secondary role or where grounded theory development is not an appropriate methodology. Also, AcademiaOS is far from automating the entire qualitative research process that researchers should overly rely on. Instead, AcademiaOS is a tool supporting the researcher in understanding the data and modeling interesting patterns that might guide or inspire their qualitative research.

As for methodological limitations, the user survey was designed to be exploratory and might not give a representative image of AcademiaOS’ performance in the field. While the study gave informative and extensive subjective insight, the participant sample was too small to reach data saturation, and insights were given on initial impressions rather than on actual work using this new tool. Further research might be necessary to understand the technology’s impact more accurately.

10
Ethics, Risks, and Trustworthy AI

What follows is a critical reflection on the potential impact and systemic risks associated with using the tool in qualitative research. Autonomous systems are often criticized or feared for not aligning with human values. This is a complex problem, as it is hard to identify a correct set of values the AI should align with, living in a world of different moral stances, leaving the option to identify a common ground of values that would hold in a pluralist reality (
Gabriel, 2020
, pp. 14-16). This might also draw on political discourse on how to aggregate different stances (see, e.g.,
Rawls, 2017
) or which universal values to agree on (e.g., the Universal Declaration of Human Rights). The
European Commission et al., 2019
made an attempt to translate liberal European values into concrete guidelines for AI systems.

O’neil, 2017
introduces the concept of "Weapons of Math Destruction," arguing that algorithms, automation, and big data potentially lead to undesirable outcomes. She explores multiple effects that might make autonomous systems dangerous. Firstly, the author argues that while seemingly objective, these systems can amplify or perpetuate bias in the data they are fed. This might be problematic in fields where a biased system can lead to unfair outcomes. Secondly, O’Neil criticizes opaque algorithms and lack of transparency, which can lead to accountability issues. Thirdly, she emphasizes that algorithms, unlike human agents, can work at scale, affecting thousands of people at once. Lastly, she criticizes potentially destructive feedback loops.

I will argue that AcademiaOS is designed around trustworthy AI standards that aim to mitigate these kinds of destructive effects.
Thiebes et al., 2021
identify five foundational principles of trustworthy AI that are largely compatible with established guidelines from public institutions, such as the Ethics Guidelines by the
European Commission et al., 2019
: Beneficence, non-maleficence, autonomy, justice, and explicability. In the following, I will analyze AcademiaOS using these five principles.

Firstly, beneficence is defined as the quality of promoting the well-being of humans and respecting basic human rights (
Thiebes et al., 2021
).
European Commission et al., 2019
further specifies these as "fundamental rights," that is, respect for human dignity, freedom of the individual, respect for democracy, justice and the rule of law, equality, non-discrimination and solidarity, and citizens’ rights. AcademiaOS implicitly promotes the well-being of humans by promoting scientific discovery and, therefore, the advancement of knowledge and education. This helps researchers conduct rigorous research more efficiently and promotes broader access to qualitative research methods with minimal violation of individuals’ fundamental rights. According to
Thiebes et al., 2021
, these qualities promote trusting beliefs such as benevolence, helpfulness, and purpose, adding to the notion of trustworthy AI.

Secondly, non-maleficence is the quality of preventing harm to people (
Thiebes et al., 2021
. That entails the protection of human dignity and mental and physical integrity. Galactica from Meta is a recent case study for malicious science communication with LLMs. Galactica is an LLM trained on scientific literature to produce corresponding content. It was shut down only three days after being opened to the public for being criticized for hallucinating false information while articulating it in a scientifically convincing way (
Heaven, 2022
). This might have led to severe consequences, such as polluted journals with made-up "knowledge."
Boiko et al., 2023
published another example of LLM-powered scientific discovery, specifically in chemical research. They outlined the risk of dual-use application, allowing non-scientists to synthesize dangerous and illegal substances by a simple prompt to the autonomous research agent. One could argue that AcademiaOS is prone to be misused in a scenario similar to the Galactica case, where an AI system autonomously publishes papers at scale and pollutes academic databases with false information. However, AcademiaOS is not an agent autonomously interacting with the world and creating harm on its own. In its current form, it is a mere tool controlled by a human researcher at every step. While AcademiaOS might lower the entry barrier to grounded theory development and thus also for potential misuse, through the still high computational cost, the necessary human component, and therefore time spent generating theoretical models with AcademiaOS, this is not an issue of scalable impact as
O’neil, 2017
would have warned about. Furthermore, even with malevolent misuse, e.g., through the spreading of wrong information, the accountability question does not change to when done manually.

Other risks for harm, as pointed out by
O’neil, 2017
, are reflected or amplified bias and potential perpetuation or destructive feedback loops. To illustrate this, consider a hypothetical scenario set in the realm of social sciences research in the 1950s. An AI system is employed to analyze social data. The AI, constrained by the prevailing societal norms and limited data of its time, could inadvertently reinforce existing biases. It might generate theories that perpetuate gender or racial stereotypes because the data of that era is heavily skewed. This could have significant ethical implications and potentially end in a destructive feedback loop. I would argue that this naïve empiricism is inherent in grounded theory development – whether automated or not. The notion of groundedness assumes that the curated input data offers an accurate account of the studied subject.
Gioia et al., 2013
are very clear in their assumption that the informants (for example, the interviewees) are knowledgeable agents whom the researcher should try to capture an adequate account from. This naïve empiricism has been criticized before (e.g.,
Thornberg, 2012
;
Gioia et al., 2013
), and there might be disciplines where other research methodologies with varying epistemological assumptions might be better applicable. Nonetheless, grounded theory development is an established and accepted methodology and in some disciplines through its transparency and replicability, even the dominant way of capturing informants’ accounts and representing data analysis. AcademiaOS is designed to follow the established grounded theory development process as closely as possible. Therefore, it is unlikely that AcademiaOS increases the risk inherent with naïve empiricism compared to the non-automated way. On the contrary, through the potentially decreasing cost of grounded theory development through automation, more data can be analyzed in smaller intervals, potentially balancing local biases and accounting for a more temporarily accurate picture of lived experiences.
Thiebes et al., 2021
attribute these non-maleficence qualities to the trust beliefs of integrity, reliability, and process, supporting the notion of trustworthiness.

Thirdly, in the realm of trustworthy AI, autonomy is the quality of promoting human agency and control – and, therefore, restricting, where necessary, the autonomy of the AI system. Human agency revolves around the ability of users to make informed and autonomous decisions. AcademiaOS is designed to assist the researcher in their creative work but, through its design, ensures that the final decision-making power and interpretative control rests with the user. While AcademiaOS automates data analysis and theory development, it requires the user to curate the data, supervise and control the processes, and put its output into use. This human-in-command approach ensures the researcher decides how to use the system and its produced output and integrate them into their broader research agenda. At every step in the automated process, the researcher has the power to control and stop further processing, essentially putting a human in the loop, promoting human autonomy and, in turn, the notion of trustworthy AI.

Fourthly,
Thiebes et al., 2021
define justice as the quality of amending past inequities, sharing the benefits of AI use, and mitigating new harms and inequities. LLMs are known to exhibit bias emerging from training data, underlying algorithms, labeling and annotation, product design decisions, and policy decisions (
Ferrara, 2023
). It is, therefore, essential to use them responsibly. The approach of grounded theory development inherently constrains the interpretative freedom of the executing agent – whether human or machine – and thus leaves less room for bias than in an unconstrained environment. The human-in-the-loop approach affords real-time moderation. Researchers can detect and correct biased or incorrect responses (
Ferrara, 2023
). Popular LLMs, such as those used by AcademiaOS, are constantly subject to thorough study of their biases (e.g.,
Zack et al., 2024
). With its open-source character, AcademiaOS is transparent in its use of LLMs. It allows the researcher to be educated and to assess the appropriateness of using a potentially biased language model for their work. Furthermore, emphasizing user experience in the system’s development increases accessibility and allows less tech-savvy demographics to use AcademiaOS. Survey participants majorly confirm that AcademiaOS is easy to learn (see Appendix 18). Addressing these justice issues promotes the trust beliefs of integrity, reliability, and process and, therefore, again, the system’s trustworthiness (
Thiebes et al., 2021
).

Fifthly, explicability is the quality of upholding interpretability (
Thiebes et al., 2021
). The
European Commission et al., 2019
further specifies that for an AI system to be considered transparent, it must be explainable, traceable, and clearly communicated. AcademiaOS’ open-source character promotes explainability by being transparent with its exact implementation. A transparent, stepwise presentation of the sub-results aims to explain and communicate how the machine comes up with the end result. The Helicone integration promotes traceability. Furthermore, AcademiaOS does not present itself as human in its communication, and users are not misled by the fact that they communicate with an AI system. These design choices on explicability contribute to the trust beliefs of competence, functionality, and performance (
Thiebes et al., 2021
) and, therefore, further to the notion of trustworthy AI.

While AI systems pose new threats to human values, AcademiaOS addresses critical issues around beneficence, non-maleficence, autonomy, justice, and explicability to ensure its trustworthiness. This reflection may guide users and open-source contributors in developing and using the system appropriately and responsibly.

11
Conclusion

AcademiaOS marks a first tangible step in automating grounded theory development with large language models. The system develops theoretical models grounded in the curated qualitative raw data using a coding process borrowed from
Gioia et al., 2013
and an iterative multi-prompt modeling process. This system demonstrates the feasibility and efficiency of employing LLMs to analyze and gain insight from qualitative data, which traditionally requires extensive human effort and time. A first-user survey (n=19) suggests great acceptance and potential within the academic community. Users appreciated the system’s ability to handle large volumes of data, providing insights that might be overlooked in manual analysis. However, the study also highlighted inherent limitations and risks that future iterations of the platform might be able to mitigate. The platform’s open-source nature and user-friendly design make it accessible and adaptable for various research contexts. While not replacing human researchers anytime soon, AcademiaOS serves as a trustworthy AI tool to augment and enhance qualitative research and a basis for future research.

Appendix 53: Critique for First Iteration Model for University Policies

As there is no specific theoretical model provided for critique, I will offer general guidance on how to assess a theoretical model in qualitative research: 1. **Novelty**: A novel theoretical model should contribute new perspectives or ways of understanding phenomena. In assessing novelty, consider whether the model offers a unique integration of concepts or identifies previously unexplored relationships between variables. Also, consider if it fills a gap in the literature, challenges existing paradigms, or proposes new hypotheses for investigation. 2. **Conciseness**: Theoretical models should be as simple as necessary but as complex as needed to explain the phenomena in question. Check for superfluous elements or overly convoluted relationships that can be streamlined. Conciseness also involves ensuring that each aspect of the model is essential and that the model is not redundant with existing theories unless it significantly improves on or clarifies them. 3. **Clarity**: A clear theoretical model is one that can be easily understood and applied by other researchers. Clarity involves precise definitions of the model’s components, understandable logic connecting the components, and clear visualization if a graphical representation is used. The model should be free of jargon, or if specialized terms are necessary, they should be well-defined. 4. **Theoretical Insight**: Evaluate the model for its depth of insight into the research problem. A model with strong theoretical insight will not only describe or classify elements but will explain processes, predict outcomes, and provide a framework for understanding complex relationships. It should have the potential to guide future research, suggesting new questions or methods of investigation. When brainstorming potential new patterns to discover in the data, the following steps might be useful: - Look for inconsistencies or contradictions in the data that do not fit the existing model. These might indicate areas where the model needs to be expanded or revised. - Analyze outliers or exceptional cases to understand why they do not conform to the expected patterns. These cases can sometimes lead to the identification of new variables or relationships. - Use alternative analytical lenses or perspectives to re-interpret the data. This might involve interdisciplinary approaches or the application of theories from different fields. - Conduct comparative analysis across different groups, settings, or time periods to identify if and how patterns may vary, which can lead to new dimensions being incorporated into the model. - Employ a grounded theory approach, generating theoretical insights from the data through iterative coding and analysis, allowing new patterns and relationships to emerge organically. Remember that the ultimate goal of a theoretical model is to advance understanding, guide research, and offer meaningful explanations that can be empirically tested and validated through qualitative or quantitative methods.
