Abstract
Many parameters of interest in economics and other social sciences depend on generated regressors. Examples in economics include structural parameters in models with endogenous variables estimated by control functions and in models with sample selection, treatment effect estimation with propensity score matching, and marginal treatment effects. More recently, Machine Learning (ML) generated regressors are becoming ubiquitous for these and other applications such as imputation with missing regressors, dimension reduction, including autoencoders, learned proxies, confounders and treatments, and for feature engineering with unstructured data, among others. We provide the first general method for valid inference with regressors generated from ML. Inference with generated regressors is complicated by the very complex expression for influence functions and asymptotic variances. Additionally, ML-generated regressors may lead to large biases in downstream inferences. To address these problems, we propose Automatic Locally Robust/debiased GMM estimators in a general three-step setting with ML-generated regressors. We illustrate our results with treatment effects and counterfactual parameters in the partially linear and nonparametric models with ML-generated regressors. We provide sufficient conditions for the asymptotic normality of our debiased GMM estimators and investigate their finite-sample performance through Monte Carlo simulations. Keywords: Local robustness; Machine learning; Bias; Generated regressors. JEL Classification: C13; C14; C21; D24

To the best of our knowledge, there are currently no generally available and valid inference methods for these parameters that allow for regressors generated by ML.

The goal of this paper is to
propose Automatic Locally Robust/Debiased estimators of and inference on
structural parameters in such models. Automatic debiased estimators with
generated regressors are useful for two main reasons. First, debiased estimators correct for the
large regularization and model selection biases from using ML-generated
regressors. Second, it is hard to
derive the analytic form of the parameter of interest’s influence function and the asymptotic variance of three-step estimators in the presence of generated regressors. The estimators and inferences that we propose are automatic in
the sense that influence functions and asymptotic variances are estimated
automatically from data and identifying moments. There is no need to find
their analytic shape or to bootstrap their asymptotic distributions, which
may be computationally costly and theoretically hard to justify with ML-generated regressors.

Employing ML-generated regressors is useful to avoid misspecification biases and to handle high-dimensional covariates, but it introduces large regularization and model selection biases in downstream inferences.

To reduce these biases, we consider inferences based on so-called Debiased/Orthogonal/Locally Robust moments. These moments are less sensitive to the generated regressors and significantly reduce regularization and model selection biases from ML-generated regressors. To construct automatic debiased/orthogonal moment functions in the presence of ML-generated regressors, we generalize some results in the debiasing
literature from a two-step setting to our three-step setting. Our three-step
setting is described as follows: In a first step, a function identified by generalized
orthogonality conditions generates some regressors. This setting
is general enough to cover a wide variety of important applications,
including imputation, ML-estimated propensity scores, and control functions with
high-dimensional covariates. A second step nuisance function is then
constructed as a (possibly high-dimensional) least squares projection using the generated regressors and
possibly other observed regressors. Finally, the parameter of interest is
identified in a third step by a Generalized Method of Moments (GMM) criteria
involving the first two steps and data observations. Existing debiasing
methods could be used in the model that treats the generated regressors as
known or the second step as known, hence reducing the three steps to two
steps. However, treating the generated regressors as known, though commonly
done in the literature, generally leads to invalid inferences.

Our generalization of the existing debiasing ML literature proceeds by

between the first and second steps is crucial here and substantially complicates our problem. In particular, we characterize the implicit indirect effect
that the generated regressors have on the second step (as conditioning

the second step influence function. Specifically, we show that if the second step effect is zero, the indirect effect from the generated regressors as conditioning variables is also zero, generalizing a remark in
Hahn and Ridder, 2013
to ML-generated regressors in a broader class of second and third steps.

A useful and practical implication of this result is the recommendation to consider identifying moments for the parameter of interest that are orthogonal to the second step when they are analytically available and easy to construct, such as, for example, for the partial linear model with ML-generated controls. If analytical formulas for such orthogonal moments are not available, we provide a general algorithm that automatically estimates them.

The generated regressors could be generated, for example, from a control function approach with a Lasso, Random Forest, Deep Learning, or a Boosting first stage; from an ML estimator of the propensity score, such as a high-dimensional Logit-Lasso; or from learned confounders via autoencoders. The second application on learned confounders for ATE estimation in a partial linear model accounts for biases from the generated regressors in a recent but growing empirical literature that deals with unstructured data (images, text, video, etc) in causal studies, see
Klaassen et al., 2024

This application relates to an extensive literature that goes under different names in the machine learning literature (e.g., Domain Adaptation, Distribution or Covariate Shift, Transfer Learning, Sample Selection Bias, Off-Policy Evaluation, and Weighted Empirical Risk Minimization). We allow for endogeneity and a flexible non-separable structural model, as is important in many applications where the counterfactuals of interest are on endogenous variables such as prices. To deal with the endogeneity, we use a control function approach that introduces a generated regressor. ML estimation of the generated regressor is well motivated in many empirical settings where high-dimensional exogenous covariates are available or the researcher wants to model the first stage flexibly. All these different applications are representative of the modern uses of ML-generated regressors in causal estimation.

We provide mild sufficient conditions for the asymptotic normality of the
debiased GMM estimator in general, and we illustrate the results with our examples. The finite sample performance of the proposed debiased estimator for the ATE parameter is evaluated through Monte Carlo simulations (see the previous version of this paper for Monte Carlo simulations for the CASF). Our Monte Carlo results confirm that plug-in estimators are substantially biased. Correcting the moment condition for the second step estimation, as in DML, marginally reduces the bias, but a first step correction must be
added to remove the bias of the ML-generated regressors and to obtain valid inferences.

Setting and Examples

2.1
Three-step Setting

We observe data
W=(Y,D,Z)
from a cumulative distribution function (cdf)
F_{0}
. We describe our three-step setting as follows:

First step
. There is a first step
g_{0}(Z)
solving the moment restrictions

\mathbb{E}[\delta_{1}(Z)\epsilon(W,g_{0})]=0\text{ for all }\delta_{1}\in\Delta_{1},

(2.1)

where
\epsilon(W,g_{0})
is a generalized error depending on the data
observations
W
and a function
g_{0}\in\Delta_{1}
, for
\Delta_{1}
a
linear and closed subspace of
L_{2}(Z)
. Henceforth, for a generic random
variable
U
, we denote by
L_{2}(U)
the Hilbert space of square-integrable
functions of
U
, i.e.
g\in L_{2}(U)
iff
\mathbb{E}[g^{2}(U)]<\infty
. This setting covers a wide variety of semiparametric and
non-parametric first steps
g_{0}
. For example, when
\epsilon(W,g_{0})=D-g_{0}(Z)
and
\Delta_{1}=L_{2}(Z)
, we have
g_{0}(Z)=\mathbb{E}[D|Z]
. If
\operatorname{dim}(Z)
is high, this nonparametric first step may not be feasible to implement. We can then consider a high-dimensional additive regression model with the same error but with
\Delta_{1}=\sum_{j=1}^{\operatorname{dim}(Z)}\Delta_{1,j}
, where
\Delta_{1,j}
is a subset of
L_{2}(Z_{j})
for the
j
-th component of
Z
, see Chapter 7 in
Wainwright, 2019
. When
\Delta_{1}
is the mean square limit of linear combinations
\sum_{k=1}^{K}\beta_{0k}c_{k}(Z)
for
K\in\mathbb{N}
, a sequence of real numbers
(\beta_{0k})_{k=1}^{\infty}
, a dictionary
(c_{k})_{k=1}^{\infty}
of
functions in
L_{2}(Z),
and
\epsilon(W,g_{0})=D-\Lambda(g_{0}(Z))
for
the logistic cdf
\Lambda,
this setting covers the high-dimensional
Logistic regression, which is commonly used for propensity score and classification
modeling. For numerous other examples of
\epsilon(W,g_{0}),
including

Ichimura and Newey, 2022
. For
general parametric first steps see Remark
4.1

The first step
g_{0}
in (
2.1

regressors

V\equiv\varphi(D,Z,g_{0}),

where
\varphi
is a known function of observed variables

Note the simplified notation

A high-dimensional extension of propensity score matching in

is a “not missing”

g_{0}(Z_{2})=\mathbb{E}[D|Z_{1}=1,Z_{2}]
for observed covariates

and control function

for an endogenous variable

and exogenous variables

Our setting covers these and other genenerated
regressors.

Second step
. Let
S
and
X
denote some components (or all of)
(Y,D)

and
(D,Z)
, respectively. The second step links
S
with
X
, and the
generated regressor
V
, through the moment restrictions

\mathbb{E}[\delta_{2}(X,V)(S-h_{0}(X,V))]=0\text{ for all }\delta_{2}\in\Delta_{2}(g_{0}),

(2.2)

where
\Delta_{2}(g_{0})
is a linear and closed subspace of
L_{2}(X,V)

(note
\Delta_{2}
depends on

because

depends on
g_{0}
). This dependence of the parameter space
\Delta_{2}(g_{0})
on the first step
g_{0}
is our point of departure from the existing debiasing literature in, e.g.,
Chernozhukov et al., 2022a
.
Hahn and Ridder, 2013
and
Mammen et al., 2016

consider cases where the second step

is a non-parametric regression
of

(X,V)
, corresponding to
\Delta_{2}(g_{0})=L_{2}(X,V)
. A useful semiparametric specification that we consider is the partially linear model, where
\Delta_{2}(g)=\{\beta^{\prime}x+\kappa(v)\colon\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{p},\kappa\in\Delta_{\kappa}(V(g))\}
, where
\Delta_{\kappa}(V(g))
is a linear and closed subspace (or all) of

. This setting includes high-dimensional regression for

. When

, and hence

has dimension

we understand (

) as being
applied to each component of

Third step
. Let
\Theta\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{p}
denote the
parameter space where the parameter of interest lies. Consider
the moment function
m\colon\mathbb{R}^{\operatorname{dim}(W)}\times L_{2}(Z)\times L_{2}(X,V)^{\operatorname{dim}(S)}\times\Theta\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{q}
,
q\geq p.
The
parameter of interest

is identified in a third step by a GMM

\mathbb{E}[m(W,g_{0},h_{0},\theta_{0})]=0.

Here we assume that

is identified by these moments, i.e. that

is the unique solution to
\mathbb{E}[m(W,g_{0},h_{0},\theta)]=0

2.2
Examples

The following examples are used to illustrate the main results of this paper.

Example 1

(ATE with a high-dimensional Lasso-Logit propensity score)

We observe
W\equiv(Y,D,Z)
, where
D
is a treatment indicator,
Z
are pre-treatment covariates or features and
Y=Y_{0}+D(Y_{1}-Y_{0})
is the observed outcome —
Y_{1}
and
Y_{0}
are the potential outcomes for treated and non-treated, respectively. As in
Rosenbaum and Rubin, 1983
, the Selection on Observables assumption, namely
(Y_{0},Y_{1})\perp D|Z
, implies that the propensity score is a balancing score, i.e., the conditional independence assumption holds when conditioning on the propensity score.
Heckman et al., 1998
proposes to estimate the Average Treatment Effect
\theta_{0}\equiv\mathbb{E}[Y_{1}-Y_{0}]
by matching in the propensity score in a low-dimensional setting.

In the case of high-dimensional regressors
Z
, or a high-dimensional set of technical regressors, the propensity score cannot be estimated by a usual low-dimensional logit or probit regression, nor by kernel-type regressions. We propose to estimate the propensity score by a high-dimensional Lasso-Logit. This setting corresponds to our first step with

\mathbb{E}[\delta_{1}(Z)\underbrace{(D-\Lambda(g_{0}(Z)))}_{=\epsilon(W,g_{0})}]=0\text{ for all }\delta_{1}\in\Delta_{1},

where, again,
\Lambda
is the Logistic cdf and
\Delta_{1}
is the mean square limit of linear and sparse
combinations
\sum_{k=1}^{K}\gamma_{0k}c_{k}(Z)
for a sequence of real
numbers
(\gamma_{0k})_{k=1}^{\infty}
and a dictionary
(c_{k})_{k=1}^{\infty}
of functions in
L_{2}(Z)
. Such moment restrictions on
g_{0}(Z)
are the first-order conditions of the conditional maximum likelihood estimator under the Logit specification. The generated regressor is the propensity score:

V\equiv\varphi(D,Z,g_{0})=\Lambda(g_{0}(Z)).

This setup generalizes related estimators in
Heckman et al., 1998
;
Hahn and Ridder, 2013
;
Mammen et al., 2016
to allow for ML and debiasing for valid inference.

Once the propensity score
V
is learned, there are several approaches to estimating the ATE. In this example, we propose to estimate it in a partially linear model (see Example
3
for a nonparametric framework). The partially linear model simplifies computations and deserves attention on its own. We consider that

Y=\theta_{0}D+\kappa_{0}(V)+\varepsilon\text{ with }\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon|D,V]=0.

The above partially linear model may be derived from the following model for potential outcomes:
Y_{0}=\eta_{0}(Z)+U
,
Y_{1}=\theta_{0}+Y_{0}
, and
U\perp D|V
. In that case,
\kappa_{0}(V)=\mathbb{E}[\eta_{0}(Z)+U|V]
.
5
5

5

More generally,
\theta_{0}
has a causal interpretation in a heterogenous treatment effect model under correct specification of the high-dimensional propensity score.

Following
Robinson, 1988
, the ATE
\theta_{0}
is identified in the partially linear model by the moment condition:

\mathbb{E}\left[(Y-\mathbb{E}[Y|V]-\theta_{0}(D-\mathbb{E}[D|V]))\cdot(D-\mathbb{E}[D|V])\right]=0.

Under correct specification of the high-dimensional Logit-Lasso,
\mathbb{E}[D|V]=V
. See the next example for a relaxation of this assumption. The second step is a regression of the outcome on the propensity score
h_{0}(V)\equiv\mathbb{E}[Y|V]
. That is, we set
S=Y
,
X=\emptyset
, and
\Delta_{2}(g_{0})=L_{2}(V)
. We allow for ML estimation of the second step
h_{0}
. The moment condition in the third step is then

m(w,g_{0},h_{0},\theta)=(y-h_{0}(v)-\theta(d-v))\cdot(d-v),

(2.3)

where
v=\varphi(d,z,g_{0})=\Lambda(g_{0}(z))
, corresponding to OLS of
Y-h_{0}(V)
on

. This OLS estimator will be bias by the ML-generated regressors. We propose a debiased ATE estimator, which is robust to the ML-estimated propensity score, in equation (
5.1
).

Example 2

(Partially linear model with generated regressors: Autoencoders)

The discussion about estimation of the ATE in a partially linear model from Example
1
readily generalizes to any partially linear model with generated regressors. We observe
W\equiv(Y,D,Z)
, where
Z
is a (potentially) high-dimensional regressor. For example, unstructured data such as images (pixel data), text (token sequences from a high-dimensional vocabulary), audio (spectrograms), and videos (image sequences) are often very high-dimensional and are rarely used directly in empirical studies. Instead, low-dimensional embeddings (e.g., autoencoders) are used as covariates, controls or confounders. In this paper, we build on a well-grounded theoretical and empirical literature (the semiparametric and DML literatures) to construct locally robust estimators that account for the estimation of the low-dimensional embeddings, illustrating with autoencoders.
6
6

6

We focus on Feedforward neural networks for simplicity of exposition, but one can easily imagine our methods being applied to many different embeddings and the existing variants of autoencoders.

The problem is estimating the effect of
D
on

, where
D
now could be a vector of discrete, continuous or mixed variables, in the presence of a high-dimensional confounder
Z
that enters the model through the generated regressor
V\equiv\varphi(D,Z,g_{0})
, so that

Y=\theta_{0}^{\prime}D+\kappa_{0}(V)+\varepsilon\text{ with }\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon|D,V]=0,

(2.4)

where
\theta_{0}^{\prime}
denotes the transpose of
\theta_{0}
.

This framework allows for several first steps that identify
g_{0}
, i.e., that allow to learn the generated regressor
V
. In Example
1
,

came from a Logit-Lasso regression of

. In a Control Function setup
(
Blundell and Powell, 2003
, see)
, if

may be learned as a first step residual:
V=D-g_{0}(Z)
. In learned confounders via autoencoders,
V
is the latent feature representation obtained from a Feed-Forwards Neural Network fit. In all these cases, controlling for the learned confounders allows to consistently estimate
\theta_{0}
.

It is worth noting that, if one is willing to impose parametric assumptions on
\kappa_{0}
, the results in
Chernozhukov et al., 2022a

\kappa_{0}(v)=\kappa(v;\beta_{0})
for a known transformation
\kappa

and an unknown finite-dimensional parameter

Chernozhukov et al., 2022a

We innovate by allowing the generated confounders to enter in a flexible way, leaving
\kappa_{0}
as an unknown function, and by accounting for the generated regressor in inference. We propose a general
three-step
procedure to build a debiased estimator of

Step 1:

Generate the regressor
V=\varphi(D,Z,g_{0})
depending on the first step

Step 2:

Estimate the second-step expectations:

h_{0Y}(v)\equiv\mathbb{E}[Y|V=v]\text{ and }h_{0D}(v)\equiv\mathbb{E}[D|V=v].

Here
S=(Y,D)
,
h_{0}\equiv(h_{0Y},h_{0D})
, and
\Delta_{2}(g_{0})=L_{2}(V)^{1+\operatorname{dim}(D)}
.

Step 3:

Following
Robinson, 1988
, the moment condition

m(W,g_{0},h_{0},\theta_{0})=(Y-h_{0Y}(V)-\theta_{0}^{\prime}(D-h_{0D}(V)))\cdot(D-h_{0D}(V))

identifies
\theta_{0}
under suitable conditions. Note that

enters the moment condition through

.

In Section

Example 3

(Nonparametric ATE estimation with Boosting: An alternative to TWANG)

Among existing causal methods that use machine learning for estimating the propensity score, twang (gradient-boosting) is the most popular one in empirical work, particularly in combination with doubly robust augmented inverse probability weighted (AIPW) estimation, see
Leite et al., 2024
. However, the high sensitivity of AIPW to the estimation of the propensity score has been well-documented in the literature, see, in particular, the recent insightful analysis by
Yadlowsky, 2022
based on high-dimensional asymptotics. Here, we propose an alternative nonparametric estimator of the ATE based on nonparametric regression on a gradient-boosting propensity score estimator. That is, the first step learns
V=g_{0}(Z)
from a gradient-boosting nonparametric estimation of
D
onto
Z
, corresponding to
\varepsilon(W,g)=D-g(Z)
. The second step fits
h_{0}(d,v)=\mathbb{E}[Y|D=d,V=v]
. That is,
\Delta_{2}(g_{0})=L_{2}(D,V)
. Thus, in this example we relax the partially linear specification. Under the Selection on Observables assumption, the ATE is identified by

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}[Y|D=1,V]-\mathbb{E}[Y|D=0,V]\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}[Y_{1}|V]-\mathbb{E}[Y_{0}|V]\right]=\mathbb{E}[Y_{1}-Y_{0}]\equiv\theta_{0}.

Therefore, the moment condition in the third step is

m(w,g_{0},h_{0},\theta_{0})=h(1,v)-h(0,v)-\theta_{0},

where
v=\varphi(d,z,g_{0})=g_{0}(z)
. In equation (
5.5
) we give a debiased estimator of the ATE in the nonparametric setting. This estimator is locally robust to the twang estimator in the first step, and generalizes estimators discussed in
Hahn and Ridder, 2013
and
Mammen et al., 2016
to a ML first and second steps and to debiased inferences. Our proposal here relates to the
L_{2}
-boosting treatment effect estimation results of
Kueck et al., 2023
, and the literature on balancing weights, see, e.g.,
Athey et al., 2018
, with the key difference that, in our setting, the estimated propensity score is a ML-generated regressor.

Example 4

(CASF with a Control Function Approach in a non-separable model)

We observe
W=(Y,D,Z)
satisfying the model
Y=H(X,U)
, for an
unknown function
H
. The main feature of this model is that
D
, a
component of
X
, may be an endogenous regressor. We assume that the
endogenous regressor satisfies
D=g_{0}(Z)+V
, with
U
and
V
being
unobserved correlated error terms. The function
g_{0}
could be identified
by a conditional mean restriction, as in equation (
2.1
) with
\epsilon(W,g_{0})=D-g_{0}(Z)
. We assume a
Control Function approach:
U|D,Z\sim U|X,V\sim U|V
, where
\sim
denotes
equally distributed. Thus, the generated regressor is a first step residual:

V\equiv\varphi(X,Z,g_{0})=D-g_{0}(Z).

As in
Blundell and Powell, 2003
, the Control Function
assumption implies

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Y|X=x,V=v]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[H(X,U)|X=x,V=v]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[H(x,U)|V=v]\equiv h_{0}(x,v).

This defines the second step, which satisfies (
2.2
) with
\Delta_{2}(g_{0})=L_{2}(X,V)
.

The Control Function assumption allows us to identify the Average Structural
Function (ASF) at a point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{\operatorname{dim}(X)}
:

\operatorname{ASF}_{0}(x)\equiv\mathbb{E}[H(x,U)]=\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[H(x,U)|V]]=\mathbb{E}[h_{0}(x,V)].

Some well-known conditions on the support of the random vectors are needed
for the above equation to hold
(
Blundell and Powell, 2004
;
Imbens and Newey, 2009
, see)
.

In this setup, a parameter of interest is the CASF, given by

\theta_{0}=\int\operatorname{ASF}_{0}(x^{\ast})dF^{\ast}(x^{\ast}),

for a counterfactual distribution
F^{\ast}
. When
F^{\ast}
is implied
by a certain policy, the CASF may be used to measure the effect of the
policy
(
Stock, 1989
;
Stock, 1991
;
Blundell and Powell, 2004
, see)
. By Fubini’s Theorem, the CASF can be written as a function of
(g_{0},h_{0})
:

\theta_{0}=\int\mathbb{E}[h_{0}(x^{\ast},D-g_{0}(Z))]dF^{\ast}(x^{\ast})=\mathbb{E}\left[\int h_{0}(x^{\ast},D-g_{0}(Z))dF^{\ast}(x^{\ast})\right].

Hence, the moment function that identifies the CASF is:

m(w,g_{0},h_{0},\theta_{0})=\int h_{0}(x^{\ast},d-g_{0}(z))dF^{\ast}(x^{\ast})-\theta_{0}.

We propose in (
5.7
) a novel debiased estimator for the CASF allowing and accounting for ML first and second steps. The CASF
parameter here generalizes the automatic debiased inferences of trained
regression averages under covariate shits, as in
Chernozhukov et al., 2023
and references therein, but with generated
regressors
V
to deal with the endogeneity. A remarkable feature of the CASF example is that to find the value of the
moment condition for a certain point
w=(y,d,z)
, one needs to know the
whole shape of the second-step regression
h_{0}
. Thus, even in the low-dimensional case, it does not fall in
Hahn and Ridder, 2013
;
Hahn and Ridder, 2019
’s setup.

3
Debiased Estimation with ML Generated Regressors

The fundamental property that allows us to develop debiased estimators is Neyman orthogonality, also referred to as Local Robustness, see
Neyman, 1959
,
Chernozhukov et al., 2018
,
Chernozhukov et al., 2022a
. We show that Neyman orthogonal moments are constructed by adding to the original identifying moments the first and second step Influence Functions (IF). The second step IF accounts for the effect that estimating the second step has on the moment condition. This second step effect is standard and has been studied by
Newey, 1994
.

This paper focuses on debiasing in a three-step setup, where a regressor
V
is generated in the first step. The first step IF accounts for two effects (see Figure
1
and the discussion in Section
3.2
). On the one hand, it accounts for the effect that estimating the first step
g_{0}
has on the moment condition (the
direct
or
evaluation effect
). This effect can be dealt with by considering multiple nuisance parameters in the debiasing ML literature. On the other hand, the first step IF also accounts for the effect that estimating the first step has on estimating the second step (the
indirect, conditioning
or
estimation effect
).
7
7

7

A simple example may help to fix ideas. Suppose a linear regression model
\mathbb{E}[Y|V(g)]=\beta_{g}V(g)
. The indirect and direct effects are quantified, respectively, by the mappings
g\rightarrow\beta_{g}
and
g\rightarrow V(g)
. The indirect effect is more complex, as it can be seen from
\beta_{g}=\mathbb{E}[YV(g)]/\mathbb{E}[V^{2}(g)]
. We generalize this simple example to ML.
Studying the indirect effect goes beyond the existing debiasing ML literature, and it is one of the main contributions of this paper.

We show that the debiased moment function takes the form:

\displaystyle\psi(w,g_{0},h_{0},\alpha_{0},\theta)

\displaystyle=m(w,g_{0},h_{0},\theta)

(3.1)

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\alpha_{01}(z)\cdot\epsilon(W,g_{0})}_{=\phi_{1}(w,g_{0},\alpha_{01},\theta)}

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\alpha_{02}(x,\varphi(d,z,g_{0}))^{\prime}[s-h_{0}(x,\varphi(d,z,g_{0}))]}_{=\phi_{2}(w,h_{0},\alpha_{02},\theta)},

where
\alpha_{0}\equiv(\alpha_{01},\alpha_{02})
are the so-called Riesz representers. In the case of multiple moment conditions (
p>1
), one separately debiases each of them following the above expression.

The function
\phi_{1}
in equation (
3.1
) is the first step IF, which accounts for the effect of the generated regressors on identifying moments. It is generally different from zero, invalidating inference that does not account for generated regressors.
8
8

8

One instance where
\phi_{1}=0
and inference that does not account for generated regressors is valid is when the sample size used to construct the generated regressors is larger asymptotically than the sample size used to estimate the main parameter, see Remark
4.1
for a formal statement.
The first step IF depends on the first step Riesz representer
\alpha_{01}
and the identifying errors from the first step
\epsilon(w,g_{0})
. The explicit expression for
\alpha_{01}
is rather complex, but we provide automatic estimators for
\alpha_{01}
that do no require this complex analytic form. The second step IF
\phi_{2}
is more standard
(
Newey, 1994
, see)
. Nevertheless, automatic estimation of the second step Riesz representer
\alpha_{02}
and the corresponding asymptotic theory must generalize existing debiasing results to account for the presence of the generated regressors as inputs to
\alpha_{02}
.

3.1
The debiased estimator

Automatic debiased estimation with generated regressors is based on the moment condition in equation (
3.1
), where the Riesz representers
\alpha_{01}
and
\alpha_{02}
are automatically estimated (see Section
4
). We propose to construct the sample moment conditions using cross-fitting, as
in
Chernozhukov et al., 2018
. That is, we split the sample so that
\psi(W_{i},g,h,\alpha,\theta)
is averaged over observations
i
that are
not used to estimate
(g,h,\alpha,\theta)
. Cross-fitting (i) eliminates the
“own observation bias”, helping remainders to converge faster to zero, and
(ii) eliminates the need for Donsker conditions for the estimators of
(g,h,\alpha)
, which is important for first and second step ML estimators
(
Chernozhukov et al., 2018
, see the discussion in)
.

We partition the sample
(W_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
into
L
groups
I_{\ell}
, for
\ell=1,...,L
. For each group, we have estimators
\hat{g}_{\ell}
,
\hat{h}_{\ell}
and
\hat{\alpha}_{\ell}=(\hat{\alpha}_{1\ell},\hat{\alpha}_{2\ell})

that use observations that are not in
I_{\ell}
. We construct automatic
estimators of
\alpha_{0}
satisfying this property in Section
4.3
. Moreover, for each group, we consider an
initial estimator of
\theta_{0}
, namely
\tilde{\theta}_{\ell}
, which does
not use the observations in
I_{\ell}
. Commonly used choices are
L=5
for medium-size datasets and
L=10
for small datasets.

The debiased sample moment functions are

\hat{\psi}(\theta)\equiv\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\sum_{i\in I_{\ell}}\hat{\psi}_{i\ell}(\theta),

with,

\hat{\psi}_{i\ell}(\theta)\equiv m(W_{i},\hat{g}_{\ell},\hat{h}_{\ell},\theta)+\hat{\alpha}_{1\ell}(Z_{i})\cdot\epsilon(W_{i},\hat{g}_{\ell})+\hat{\alpha}_{2\ell}(X_{i},\hat{V}_{i\ell})^{\prime}(S_{i}-\hat{h}_{\ell}(X_{i},\hat{V}_{i\ell})),

(3.2)

for
\hat{V}_{i\ell}\equiv\varphi(D_{i},Z_{i},\hat{g}_{\ell})
. Note
that the original moment condition is evaluated at
\theta
. The initial estimators
\tilde{\theta}_{\ell}
are used to estimate
\alpha_{01}
and
\alpha_{02}
. When there is more than one moment condition, each of them must be debiased. This means that a different correction term must be
estimated for each component of
m
—we will need to estimate as many Riesz representers
\hat{\alpha}_{\ell}
as moment conditions.

We base the debiased GMM estimator on the debiased sample moment functions:

\hat{\theta}=\operatornamewithlimits{argmin}_{\theta\in\Theta}\hat{\psi}(\theta)^{\prime}\hat{\Upsilon}\hat{\psi}(\theta),

(3.3)

where
\hat{\Upsilon}
is a positive semi-definite weighting matrix of
dimension
\operatorname{dim}(m)\times\operatorname{dim}(m)
. Under some conditions (see
Section
6
), the above estimator will be asymptotically
normal with the usual GMM asymptotic variance.

Example 5

(continues=ex:ATE_PLM)

We sketch how to build a three-step debiased and cross-fitted ML estimator of the ATE in the partially linear model. Let
\mathbf{c}_{K}(z)\equiv(c_{1}(z),\dots,c_{K}(z))^{\prime}
be a dictionary with
K
atoms. In case of a high-dimensional
Z_{i}
, this dictionary corresponds to including every regressor:
\mathbf{c}_{K}(z)=(z_{1},\dots z_{K})^{\prime}
. We propose to estimate
g_{0}
, without using observations in
I_{\ell}
, by
\hat{g}_{\ell}(z)=\mathbf{c}_{K}(z)^{\prime}\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{K\ell}
, where
\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{K\ell}
solves

\operatornamewithlimits{min}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}}\left\{-\sum_{i\notin I_{\ell}}\left[D_{i}\log\Lambda(\mathbf{c}_{K}(Z_{i})^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{K})+(1-D_{i})\log(1-\Lambda(\mathbf{c}_{K}(Z_{i})^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{K}))\right]+\lambda\lVert\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{K}\rVert_{1}\right\},

where
\lambda
is a penalization parameter, and
\lVert\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{K}\rVert_{1}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}|\gamma_{k}|
. The propensity score (generated regressor) is estimated by
\hat{V}_{i\ell}=\Lambda(\hat{g}_{\ell}(Z_{i}))
.

To estimate the second step
h_{0}(v)=\mathbb{E}[Y|V=v]
, we consider a dictionary
\mathbf{b}_{J}(v)=(b_{1}(v),...,b_{J}(v))^{\prime}
. For instance, one may consider the power-series expansion of degree
J-1
:
b_{j}(v)=v^{j-1}
for
j=1,\dots,J
. Then, the regression of the outcome on the propensity score is estimated by
\hat{h}_{\ell}(v)=\mathbf{b}_{J}(v)^{\prime}\hat{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{J\ell}
, where
\hat{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{J\ell}
solves

\operatornamewithlimits{min}_{\boldsymbol{\eta}_{J}\in\mathbb{R}^{J}}\left\{\sum_{i\notin I_{\ell}}\left[Y_{i}-\mathbf{b}_{J}(\hat{V}_{i\ell})^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{J\ell}\right]^{2}+\lambda\lVert\boldsymbol{\eta}_{J}\rVert_{1}\right\},

where the penalty parameter
\lambda
may be different from the one in the first-step.

Regarding the debiased moment condition in equation (
2.3
), a standard pathwise derivative calculation shows that it is locally robust to the second step. That is,
\alpha_{02}=0
(see Section
5.2
). Therefore, a natural extension of the DML estimator of
Chernozhukov et al., 2018
satisfies

\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\sum_{i\in I_{\ell}}m(W_{i},\hat{g}_{\ell},\hat{h}_{\ell},\hat{\theta}_{DML})=0\Leftrightarrow\hat{\theta}_{DML}=\frac{\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\sum_{i\in I_{\ell}}\left(Y_{i}-\hat{h}_{\ell}(\hat{V}_{i\ell})\right)\left(D_{i}-\hat{V}_{i\ell}\right)}{\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\sum_{i\in I_{\ell}}\left(D_{i}-\hat{V}_{i\ell}\right)^{2}}.

(3.4)

This estimator does not account for the effect of learning the propensity score
\hat{V}_{i\ell}=\Lambda(\hat{g}_{\ell}(Z_{i}))
.

We propose a three-step debiased and cross-fitted estimator for the ATE which is locally robust to the regressor generation step. Our estimator is

\hat{\theta}=\hat{\theta}_{DML}+\frac{\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\sum_{i\in I_{\ell}}\hat{\alpha}_{1\ell}(Z_{i})\cdot(D_{i}-\hat{V}_{i\ell})}{\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\sum_{i\in I_{\ell}}\left(D_{i}-\hat{V}_{i\ell}\right)^{2}}.

The specific details of how
\hat{\alpha}_{1\ell}
is constructed are given in Section
5.1
(see Section
4.3
for a general procedure). Regularity conditions and
asymptotic theory are investigated in Section
6
. We note here that, in general,
\alpha_{01}\neq 0
so the DML estimator must be debiased to obtain an estimator that is locally robust to the generated regressors. Our estimator is an LR, high-dimensional, generalization of the series estimators in
Newey, 2009
and
Hsu et al., 2022
.

The following sections are more technical than the previous sections, and thus the applied reader could jump directly to Section
4.3
, where we introduce the automatic estimators for the Riesz representers. In particular, Section
4.3.3
provides a schematic view of the algorithm to estimate the debiased moment function in equation (

Remark 3.1

(Comparison with two-step approaches)

The dependence of the second step on the first step makes the results in

not applicable to our current setting. That is, we require that for each

breaks this linearity. Our results rely on alternative assumptions (see Assumption
4.3
) to deal with this parameter space.

4
Automatic Estimation of the Riesz Representers

The orthogonal moments require a consistent estimator
\hat{\alpha}_{\ell}
of the Riesz representers
\alpha_{0}\equiv(\alpha_{01},\alpha_{02})
. When the shape of
\alpha_{0}
is known, one can plug-in nonparametric
estimators of the unknown components of
\alpha_{0}
to form
\hat{\alpha}_{\ell}
. This is the case, for example, of
\alpha_{02}
in the partially linear model
.
In the
generated regressors setup, however, the nuisance parameters (especially
\alpha_{01}
) have a complex analytical shape (see the result in equation (
B.6
) in the Appendix, the examples in Section
5
, and
Hahn and Ridder, 2013
,
Hahn and Ridder, 2013
). Therefore, the plug-in estimators may be cumbersome to compute in practice.

To ease exposition and without loss of generality, in this section, we consider that there is a single moment condition (
p=q=1
). Recall that in the multi-dimensional case one must estimate Riesz representers
\alpha_{0}
for each moment condition.

4.1
Separate Identification of Riesz Representers

We provide separate orthogonality conditions that will serve as a basis
for the identification and automatic estimation of the Riesz representers
\alpha_{01}
and
\alpha_{02}
. Define the following moment functions:
\psi_{1}(w,g,\alpha_{1},\theta)\equiv m(w,g,h(F_{0},g),\theta)+\phi_{1}(w,g,\alpha_{1},\theta)
for the first step, and
\psi_{2}(w,h,\alpha_{2},\theta)\equiv m(w,g_{0},h,\theta)+\phi_{2}(w,h,\alpha_{2},\theta)
for the
second step.
Since, individually, the spaces
\Delta_{1}
and
\Delta_{2}(g_{0})
are linear, an application of
Theorem 3 in
Chernozhukov et al., 2022a
to each step leads to

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\tau}\mathbb{E}[\psi_{1}(W,g_{0}+\tau\delta_{1},\alpha_{01},\theta)]

\displaystyle=0\text{ for all }\delta_{1}\in\Delta_{1}\text{ and}

(4.1)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\tau}\mathbb{E}[\psi_{2}(W,h_{0}+\tau\delta_{2},\alpha_{02},\theta)]

\displaystyle=0\text{ for all }\delta_{2}\in\Delta_{2}(g_{0}),

(4.2)

for all
\theta\in\Theta
, where
\delta_{1}
represents a possible
direction of deviation of
g(F)
from
g_{0}
and
\delta_{2}
represents a
possible deviation of
h(F,g_{0})
from
h_{0}
. The innovation relative to
Chernozhukov et al., 2022a
is that we can
compute the IFs
\phi_{1}
and
\phi_{2}
by separately studying
\psi_{1}
and
\psi_{2}
, respectively. This means we can
separately identify
\alpha_{01}
and
\alpha_{02}
from (
4.1
) and (
4.2
), even though
\psi_{1}
and
\psi_{2}
are not LR moment functions. We stress that Theorem 3 in
Chernozhukov et al., 2022a
is not applicable
to our three-step setting where the second step depends on the first step.

Likewise, rather than joint
identification from the analytical derivatives of the original identifying
moments as in
Chernozhukov et al., 2022a
, which are not available with generated regressors, we propose an approach that uses the linearization and orthogonality of
\psi_{1}
and
\psi_{2}
with respect to
g
and
h
, respectively, to construct
separate estimators of
\alpha_{01}
and
\alpha_{02}
. This approach does not
require knowing the shape of
\alpha_{0}
. It is “automatic” in only requiring the orthogonal moment functions and data for
the construction of
\hat{\alpha}_{\ell}
. Moreover, an automatic estimator can be
constructed separately for each step.

The key ingredients for our approach are (i) the shape of the IFs and (ii) a consistent estimator of the linearization of the moment condition with respect to each
parameter —
g
for the first step and
h
for the second. Section
4.2
provides the formal development. For a detailed construction of the automatic estimators, we refer to Section
4.3
.

4.2
First and Second step linearization

We start with the linearization of the second step effect because this will
show up in the first step linearization effect. This link between first and
second steps is a useful contribution of this paper. The linearization of
the second step with a known first step is a well established result in the
literature
(see,
e.g.,
Newey, 1994
, Equation 4.1)
, and it will follow immediately if

\bar{m}(g_{0},h,\theta)
is linear in
h
.

Before introducing the result, we note that throughout this section (i)
\tau\mapsto h_{\tau}\equiv h(F_{\tau},g_{0})
denotes a differentiable path in
L_{2}(X,V)
, i.e.,
0\mapsto h_{0}
and
dh_{\tau}/d\tau
exists (equivalently for
g_{\tau}
) and (ii)
H
is regular in
the sense that, for
F_{\tau}\equiv(1-\tau)F_{0}+\tau H
,
g(F_{\tau})
is a
differentiable path in
L_{2}(Z)
. When an assumption is stated for
h_{0}
or
h_{\tau}
, it is understood that it applies to each of its components.

We assume that
\bar{m}
can be linearized with respect to the second step
parameter:

Assumption 4.1

There exists a function
D_{2}(w,h,\theta)
such that

\frac{d\bar{m}(g_{0},h_{\tau},\theta)}{d\tau}=\frac{d\mathbb{E}[D_{2}(W,h_{\tau},\theta)]}{d\tau}

for every
\theta\in\Theta
. Moreover,
h\mapsto\mathbb{E}[D_{2}(W,h,\theta)]

is linear and continuous in
L_{2}(X,V)
.

The same assumption has been considered in
Newey, 1994
. A necessary and sufficient condition for the linearity and continuity part is the existence of
r_{2}\in L_{2}(X,V)
such that
\mathbb{E}[D_{2}(W,h,\theta)]=\mathbb{E}[r_{2}(X,V)h(X,V)]
for all
h\in L_{2}(X,V)
. We
can then get the shape of the second step IF:

Proposition 4.1

Under Assumption
4.1
:

(Lin)

We can linearize the effect of the second
step estimation:

\frac{d}{d\tau}\bar{m}(g_{0},h(F_{\tau},g_{0}),\theta)=\frac{d}{d\tau}\mathbb{E}[D_{2}(W,h(F_{\tau},g_{0}),\theta)].

(IF)

There exists an
\alpha_{02}\in\Delta_{2}(g_{0})
such that the function

\phi_{2}(w,h_{0},\alpha_{02},\theta)=\alpha_{02}(x,\varphi(d,z,g_{0}))^{\prime}\{s-h_{0}(x,\varphi(d,z,g_{0}))\},

satisfies equation (
3.8
) and is thus the Second Step IF.

The Riesz Representer
\alpha_{02}
is the orthogonal projection of
r_{2}(X,V)
onto
\Delta_{2}(g_{0})
. Therefore, an important observation is that if
r_{2}
is zero, then
\alpha_{02}
is also zero. We also note that, since
\bar{m}
is linearized at
(g_{0},h_{0},\theta)
,
\alpha_{02}
may also depend
on
(g_{0},h_{0},\theta)
. This is omitted for notational simplicity, but it is of course accounted for in the theory of this paper, and it
will become relevant to construct feasible automatic estimators (see Section
4.3
).

We now move to the more complicated and novel linearization of the first
step effect. Note that if the chain rule can be applied:

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\tau}\bar{m}(g(F_{\tau}),h(F_{0},g(F_{\tau})),\theta)

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{d\tau}\bar{m}(g(F_{\tau}),h_{0},\theta)

(4.3)

\displaystyle+\frac{d}{d\tau}\bar{m}(g_{0},h(F_{0},g(F_{\tau})),\theta).

(4.4)

The first derivative in the RHS can be easily analyzed if we linearize
\bar{m}(g,h_{0},\theta)
in
g
:

Assumption 4.2

There exists a function
D_{11}(w,g)
such that

\frac{d\bar{m}(g_{\tau},h_{0},\theta)}{d\tau}=\frac{d\mathbb{E}[D_{11}(W,g_{\tau},\theta)]}{d\tau}

for every
\theta\in\Theta
. Moreover,
g\mapsto\mathbb{E}[D_{11}(W,g,\theta)]
is linear and continuous in
L_{2}(Z)
.

The term
D_{11}
is responsible for the direct effect of the first step (the evaluation effect). To study the indirect effect,
d\bar{m}(g_{0},h(F_{0},g(F_{\tau})),\theta)/d\tau
, we generalize the
key Lemma 1 in
Hahn and Ridder, 2013
to allow for ML second
steps as in equation (
2.2
):

Lemma 4.1

Assume that the chain rule can be applied along
the path
\tau\mapsto g_{\tau}
. Then, for every
\delta_{2}\in L_{2}(X,V)
satisfying that there exists an
\varepsilon>0
such that
\delta_{2}\in\cap_{\tau<\varepsilon}\Delta_{2}(g_{\tau})
:

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\tau}\mathbb{E}[\delta_{2}(X,V)\cdot h(F_{0},g_{\tau})(X,V)]

\displaystyle=-\frac{d}{d\tau}\mathbb{E}[\delta_{2}(X,V)\cdot h_{0}(X,V(g_{\tau}))]

(4.5)

\displaystyle+\frac{d}{d\tau}\mathbb{E}\left[\delta_{2}(X,V(g_{\tau}))\cdot(S-h_{0}(X,V))\right].

(4.6)

The condition that the function
\delta_{2}
belongs to every set
\Delta(g)

for
g
close to
g_{0}
is new and it is related to “regularity” of
\Delta_{2}(g)
. If the
functions in the set
\{\Delta_{2}(g):g\in\Delta_{1}\}

have the same shape, one would expect
that many
\delta_{2}
’s satisfy the condition in the above lemma. The
condition allows to take derivatives in equation (
3.5
)
along the path
(F,g)=(F_{0},g_{\tau})
.

To linearize the first step, we ask the second-step nuisance parameter
\alpha_{02}
to satisfy the condition for
\delta_{2}
in Lemma
4.1
. We
should also impose some additional assumptions on the paths
\tau\mapsto h(F_{0},g_{\tau})
. This allows us to express
d\bar{m}(g_{0},h(F_{0},g(F_{\tau})),\theta)/d\tau
as an inner product.

Assumption 4.3

For every path
\tau\mapsto g_{\tau}
there exits an
\varepsilon>0
such that

a.

\alpha_{02}\in\cap_{\tau<\varepsilon}\Delta_{2}(g_{\tau})
, and

b.

h(F_{0},g_{\tau})\in\Delta_{2}(g_{0})
for all
0\leq\tau<\varepsilon
.

Assumptions like
4.3
and the related assumption in Lemma
4.1
appear to be missing in the literature of generated regressors. As we have emphasized, this assumption is related to “regularity” in the shape of the functions in
\Delta_{2}(g)
. It is needed to deal with a non-linear parameter space for
(g,h)
. In both the
non-parametric case
\Delta_{2}(g)=L_{2}(X,V(g))
and the partially linear
case
\Delta_{2}(g)=\{\beta^{\prime}x+\kappa(v)\colon\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{p},\kappa\in L_{2}(V(g))\}
the assumption translates into
square-integrability conditions (see Appendix
A
). What
Assumption
4.3
rules out is to specify a partially linear model
for some
g
and a non-parametric regression for others.

Once we can apply Lemma
4.1
, the remaining step is to
linearize the terms
h_{0}(X,\varphi(D,Z,g(F_{\tau})))
and
\alpha_{02}(X,\varphi(D,Z,g(F_{\tau})))
. To achieve this, we require
h_{0}
,
\alpha_{0}
, and

\varphi
to be differentiable in an appropriate sense:

Assumption 4.4

h_{0}(x,v)
and
\alpha_{02}(x,v)
are a.s.
differentiable w.r.t.
v
. Moreover, the function
\varphi(d,z,g)
,
understood as a mapping
g\mapsto\varphi(d,z,g)
from
L_{2}(Z)
to
L_{2}(D,Z)
, is Hadamard differentiable at
g_{0}
, with derivative
D_{\varphi}
.

The Hadamard derivative of
\varphi
is a linear and continuous map
D_{\varphi}\colon L_{2}(Z)\rightarrow L_{2}(D,Z)
such that

\frac{d}{d\tau}\varphi(d,z,g_{\tau})=\frac{d}{d\tau}D_{\varphi}g_{\tau}.

To illustrate, if
\varphi(d,z,g)=g(z)
(first step prediction)
or
\varphi(d,z,g)=d-g(z)
(first step residual), then

D_{\varphi}g=g
or
D_{\varphi}g=-g
, respectively.

To identify the first step Riesz representer
\alpha_{01}
while allowing for general residuals
\epsilon(W,g)
, we need the following assumption
(cf.
Ichimura and Newey, 2022
, Ass. 2 and the discussion below)
.

Assumption 4.5

The moment
\mathbb{E}[\epsilon(W,g+a)|Z=z]
is differentiable
at
a=0
with derivative
r_{e}(z)
such that
r_{e}(z)<0
is bounded and
bounded away from zero.

The next theorem gives the shape of the first step IF:

Theorem 4.1

Under Assumptions
4.1
-
4.5
:

(Lin)

The function

{}D_{1}(w,g,\theta)\equiv D_{11}(w,g,\theta)+\frac{\partial}{\partial v}\left[\alpha_{02}(x,v)^{\prime}(s-h_{0}(x,v))\right]\cdot D_{\varphi}g,

(4.7)

where the derivative is evaluated at
v=\varphi(d,z,g_{0})
, satisfies

\frac{d}{d\tau}\bar{m}(g(F_{\tau}),h(F_{0},g(F_{\tau})),\theta)=\frac{d}{d\tau}\mathbb{E}[D_{1}(W,g(F_{\tau}),\theta)].

(IF)

There exists an
\alpha_{01}\in\Delta_{1}

such that the function

\phi_{1}(w,g_{0},\alpha_{01},\theta)=\alpha_{01}(z)\cdot\epsilon(w,g_{0}),

satisfies equation (
3.9
) and is thus the First Step IF.

The shape of the first step Riesz representer
\alpha_{01}
is given in
equation (
B.6
) in the Appendix. It generally is a rather
complex function. Indeed, the linearization with respect to the first step
effect is also complex (see its definition in equation (
4.7
)).
The first term corresponds to the linearization of the
direct

effect of
g
. It is given by
D_{11}
, the linearization of
d\bar{m}(g_{\tau},h_{0},\theta)/\tau
. The second term corresponds to the
indirect
effect. Consistent estimation of the second term generally
requires estimators for (i)
g_{0}
, (ii)
h_{0}
, (iii)
\partial h_{0}/\partial v
, (iv)
\alpha_{02}
, and (v)
\partial\alpha_{02}/\partial v
. Section
4.3.2
provides the details on how
to estimate
\mathbb{E}[D_{1}(W,g,\theta)]
. We also note that some simplifications
and variations on the expression for
\mathbb{E}[D_{1}(W,g,\theta)]
and for
\phi_{1}
occur under different scenarios.

Remark 4.1

(General Parametric First Steps)

Suppose we replace our definition of
g_{0}
and
\Delta_{1}
in (
2.1
) by a generic parametric fit
g_{0}(z)=G(z,\zeta_{0})
, where
G
has a known functional form and
\zeta_{0}
is an unknown finite-dimensional parameter in a parameter space
B\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{\operatorname{dim}(\zeta)}
. We allow for
\zeta_{0}
to be identified by parametric or semiparametric restrictions. For example, this setting includes semiparametric estimators (e.g., single-index models with
g_{0}(z)=z^{\prime}\zeta_{0}
). Let
\hat{\zeta}
be an estimator for
\zeta_{0}
satisfying

\sqrt{n}(\hat{\zeta}-\zeta_{0})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\psi(D_{i},Z_{i},\xi_{0})+o_{P}(1),

where the IF
\psi
has zero mean and finite variance, and
\xi_{0}
contains
\zeta_{0}
and may contain additional nuisance parameters. We assume that the pathwise derivative of
\mathbb{E}[\psi(D_{i},Z_{i},\zeta_{0},\xi)]
with respect to these additional nuisance parameters at
\zeta_{0}
is zero. Then, all our previous results apply with the previous adjustment term
\phi_{1}
replaced by
\phi_{1}=-\alpha_{01}^{\prime}\psi
, where
\alpha_{01}
is a solution to the linear system

H\alpha_{01}=\mathbb{E}[D_{1}(W,\dot{G},\theta_{0})],

where
H=\partial\mathbb{E}[\psi(D_{i},Z_{i},\xi_{0})]/\partial\zeta
and
\dot{G}(Z,\zeta_{0})=\partial G(Z,\zeta_{0})/\partial\zeta
. In particular, if
\psi=0
, i.e., if
\sqrt{n}(\hat{\zeta}-\zeta_{0})=o_{P}(1),
and other mild conditions are satisfied (to apply a Delta method argument), then there is no estimation effect from the generated regressors. This is typically the case when
\hat{\zeta}
is constructed from a large sample (with a large sample size relative to
n
). The result of no impact of the generated regressors given here extends to a more general
g_{0}
by applying the same argument to the plug-in (uncorrected) GMM estimator that results from fixing the first step to
g
, denoting the estimator by
\hat{\theta}(g)
. Then, if
\sqrt{n}(\hat{\theta}(\hat{g})-\hat{\theta}(g_{0}))=o_{P}(1),
and other mild conditions are satisfied, then there is no estimation effect from the generated regressors. A simplified version of our asymptotic results can be used to check this condition.

Remark 4.2

(Simplifications for moments locally robust to second steps)

If the original identifying moments are such that
\alpha_{02}\equiv 0,
then the first step effect simplifies to:

D_{1}(w,g,\theta)\equiv D_{11}(w,g,\theta).

This link between the second and first steps effects is new and useful in our setting. A
special case of this implication was considered in Remark 3 of
Hahn and Ridder, 2013
, where the authors consider fully nonparametric first and second steps, and
m(w,h,\theta)=\eta(w,h(x,v))-\theta
for a known function
\eta
. In this special case,
\alpha_{02}(x,v)=\mathbb{E}\left[\left.\partial\eta(W,h(x,v)/\partial h\right|X=x,V=v\right]
. In the general case, we recommend using as identifying moment
\psi_{2}=m+\phi_{2}
whenever this is analytically easy to compute (as in, e.g., the partially linear model). For this identifying moment, the corresponding Riesz representer for the second step is zero by construction (the new
\alpha_{02}\equiv 0
). If an analytical expression for
\psi_{2}
is not available, our method gives estimators for it.

Remark 4.3

(Influence function under an index restriction)

Under the index restriction
\mathbb{E}[S|D,Z]=\mathbb{E}[S|X,V]
, the first step effect simplifies to:

D_{1}(w,g,\theta)\equiv D_{11}(w,g,\theta)-\frac{\partial h_{0}}{\partial v}(x,v)\alpha_{02}(x,v)D_{\varphi}g.

(4.8)

That is, the term depending on the derivative of
\alpha_{02}
is no longer
present. Simplifications like these are well known in the literature, see, e.g.,
Hahn and Ridder, 2013
,
Escanciano et al., 2014
,
Mammen et al., 2016
and references therein.

Remark 4.4

(Other First Steps)

There are examples of first steps that are not included in (
2.1
), such as functions
identified by orthogonality conditions with instruments, where
g
depends on
other variables different from
Z
, or the control function approach of
Imbens and Newey, 2009
. Nevertheless, much of our analysis is still useful for these other cases. In particular, the expression for
D_{1}(w,g,\theta)
remains the same, and our results can be readily extended to other first steps by characterizing the corresponding first step IF
\phi_{1}
such that

\frac{d}{d\tau}\mathbb{E}[D_{1}(W,g_{\tau},\theta)]=\int\phi_{1}(w,g_{0},\alpha_{01},\theta)dH(w).

(4.9)

We illustrate with the triangular model of
Imbens and Newey, 2009
, where the control function is
V=g_{0}(D,Z)=F_{D|Z}(D)
for the conditional distribution function
F_{D|Z}
of an endogeneous continuous variable
D
given the continuous instrument
Z
. Generalizing Proposition 2 in
Hahn and Ridder, 2019
, one shows that (
4.9
) holds with

\phi_{1}(w,g_{0},\alpha_{01},\theta)=\mathbb{E}\left[\left.\alpha_{01}(D,Z)1(d\leq D)\right|Z=z\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[\left.\alpha_{01}(D,Z)V\right|Z=z\right],

where
\alpha_{01}(X,Z)
is the Riesz representer satisfying the identity
\mathbb{E}[D_{1}(W,g_{\tau},\theta)]=\mathbb{E}[\alpha_{01}(D,Z)g_{\tau}(D,Z)]
for all parametric submodels
g_{\tau}(D,Z)=F_{D|Z,\tau}
corresponding to the the deviations
F_{\tau}=(1-\tau)F_{0}+\tau H
.

4.3
Cross-fitted automatic estimators

The debiased sample moment functions are estimated using cross-fitting, where we partition the sample
(W_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
into
L
groups
I_{\ell}
, for
\ell=1,\dots,L
. Estimation of the debiased moment function
\psi
for an observation
i\in I_{\ell}
requires estimators of the Riesz representers
(\hat{\alpha}_{1\ell},\hat{\alpha}_{2\ell})
based only on observations not in
I_{\ell}
. This
section is devoted to the construction of automatic estimators satisfying
this property. Through the section, we consider that the researcher has
at her disposal first and second step estimators,
\hat{g}_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}
and
\hat{h}_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}
, and an initial estimator,
\tilde{\theta}_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}
, that use only observations not in
I_{\ell}\cup I_{\ell^{\prime}}
; and estimators
(\hat{g}_{\ell\ell^{\prime}\ell^{\prime\prime}}
,
\hat{h}_{\ell\ell^{\prime}\ell^{\prime\prime}},\tilde{\theta}_{\ell\ell^{\prime}\ell^{\prime\prime}})
that use only
observations not in
I_{\ell}\cup I_{\ell^{\prime}}\cup I_{\ell^{\prime\prime}}
. Depending on the application, the latter estimators may not be needed (see, e.g., the ATE estimator in the partially linear model).

Our approach to automatically estimate the Riesz representers relies on the orthogonality conditions discussed in Section
4.1
. We can combine the orthogonality conditions with the linearization results in Section
4.2
to obtain sets of moment conditions for the estimation of the Riesz representers. In particular, a combination of equation (
4.1
) and Theorem
4.1
gives

\mathbb{E}[D_{1}(W,\delta_{1},\theta)]=\mathbb{E}[-\alpha_{01}(Z)r_{e}(Z)\delta_{1}(Z)],\text{ for each }\delta_{1}\in\Delta_{1},

(4.10)

where
D_{1}
is the linearization of the identifying moment function
m
with respect to the first step and
r_{e}
is the linearization of the generalized error function
\epsilon(w,g)
(cf. Assumption
4.5
). Varying
\delta_{1}
, the above equation provides a set of moment conditions that identify
\alpha_{01}
. Likewise, identification of the second step Riesz representer
\alpha_{02}
follows from equation (
4.2
) and Proposition
4.1
:

\mathbb{E}[D_{2}(W,\delta_{2},\theta)]=\mathbb{E}[\alpha_{02}(X,V)\delta_{2}(D,Z)]=0,\text{ for each }\delta_{2}\in\Delta_{2}(g_{0}),

(4.11)

where
D_{2}
is the linearization of the identifying moment function
m
with respect to the second step. The shape of the linearizations
D_{1}
,
D_{2}
, and
r_{e}
may vary with the problem (see Section
5
for some examples).

Equations (
4.10
) and (
4.11
) form the basis for automatic estimation of the Riesz representers. These require finding consistent estimators of the linearizations of the identifying moment functions. In
this section, we will write
D_{2}(w,h|g_{0},h_{0},\theta)
to make
explicit that the linearization with respect to
h
may depend on
(h_{0},g_{0},\theta)
(see
our examples). For the linearization of the effect of first step estimation,
we will write
D_{1}(w,g|g_{0},h_{0},\alpha_{02},\theta)
, to emphasize
that it may also depend on the second step Riesz Representer.
D_{1}

generally also depends on the derivatives
\partial h_{0}/\partial v
and
\partial\alpha_{02}/\partial v
. We do not make this explicit, but we will
address the issue in this section.

4.3.1
Automatic estimation of the second step Riesz Representer

We start with the automatic estimator for the Second Step Riesz Representer
\alpha_{02}
. We now assume that there is a dictionary
(b_{j})_{j=1}^{\infty}
whose closed linear span is
\Delta_{2}(g_{0})
. That is, any function
in
\Delta_{2}(g_{0})
can be approximated, in the
L_{2}
sense, by a
linear combination of
b_{j}
’s. Thus,
\alpha_{02}
can be approximated by

\mathbf{b}_{J}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0J}
, where
\mathbf{b}_{J}=(b_{1},...,b_{J})^{\prime}
and
\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0J}=(\rho_{01},...,\rho_{0J})^{\prime}
. We can now plug in
\mathbf{b}_{J}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0J}
into equation (
4.11
) for
\delta_{2}=b_{j}
,
j=1,...,J
. This gives the following
J
moment
conditions:

\mathbb{E}[D_{2}(W,\mathbf{b}_{J},\theta)]=\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{b}_{J}(X,V)\mathbf{b}_{J}(X,V)^{\prime}]\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0J},

where
D_{2}(w,\mathbf{b}_{J},\theta)\equiv(D_{2}(w,b_{1},\theta),...,D_{2}(w,b_{J},\theta))^{\prime}
. Note that
\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0J}
implicitly depends on
\theta
.

The above moment conditions can be used to construct an OLS-like estimator
of
\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0J}
. Note, however, that in high-dimensional
settings
\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{b}_{J}(X,V)\mathbf{b}_{J}(X,V)^{\prime}]
may
be near singular. Therefore, we use a regularized estimator. The above
J

moment conditions are the first-order conditions of the minimization
problem:

\min_{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{J}\in\mathbb{R}^{J}}\left\{-2\mathbb{E}[D_{2}(W,\mathbf{b}_{J},\theta)^{\prime}]\boldsymbol{\rho}_{J}+\boldsymbol{\rho}_{J}^{\prime}\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{b}_{J}(X,V)\mathbf{b}_{J}(X,V)^{\prime}]\boldsymbol{\rho}_{J}\right\}.

We can regularize the problem by adding a penalty to the above objective
function. Let
\lVert\boldsymbol{\rho}_{J}\rVert_{q}\equiv(\sum_{j=1}^{J}|\rho_{j}|^{q})^{1/q}
for
q\geq 1
. For a tuning
parameter
\lambda\geq 0
, we can estimate
\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0J}
by
minimizing:

\min_{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{J}\in\mathbb{R}^{J}}\left\{-2\mathbb{E}[D_{2}(W,\mathbf{b}_{J},\theta)^{\prime}]\boldsymbol{\rho}_{J}+\boldsymbol{\rho}_{J}^{\prime}\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{b}_{J}(X,V)\mathbf{b}_{J}(X,V)^{\prime}]\boldsymbol{\rho}_{J}+\lambda\lVert\boldsymbol{\rho}_{J}\rVert_{q}^{q}\right\}.

(4.12)

For
q=1
, the above is the Lasso objective function, while
q=2

corresponds to Ridge Regression. Additionally, we could consider elastic net
type penalties, where
\lambda(\xi\lVert\boldsymbol{\rho}_{J}\rVert_{2}^{2}+(1-\xi)\lVert\boldsymbol{\rho}_{J}\rVert_{1})
, for
\xi\in[0,1]
, replaces the
\ell_{q}
penalization.

For a given
\ell\in{1,...,L}
, the automatic estimator
\hat{\alpha}_{2\ell}
is based on the sample version of the objective function in equation (
4.12
). We estimate
\mathbb{E}[D_{2}(W,\mathbf{b}_{J},\theta_{0})]
by

\hat{D}_{2\ell}\equiv\frac{1}{n-n_{\ell}}\sum_{\ell^{\prime}\neq\ell}\sum_{i\in I_{\ell^{\prime}}}D_{2}(W_{i},\mathbf{b}_{J}|\hat{g}_{\ell\ell^{\prime}},\hat{h}_{\ell\ell^{\prime}},\tilde{\theta}_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}),

where
n_{\ell}
is the number of observations in
I_{\ell}.
In turn,
\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{b}_{J}(X,V)\mathbf{b}_{J}(X,V)^{\prime}]
is estimated
by

\hat{B}_{\ell}\equiv\frac{1}{n-n_{\ell}}\sum_{\ell^{\prime}\neq\ell}\sum_{i\in I_{\ell^{\prime}}}\mathbf{b}_{J}(X_{i},\varphi(D_{i},Z_{i},\hat{g}_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}))\mathbf{b}_{J}(X_{i},\varphi(D_{i},Z_{i},\hat{g}_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}))^{\prime}.

With this, we can build an automatic estimator of the second step Riesz Representer that only uses observations not in
I_{\ell}
. It is given by
\hat{\alpha}_{2\ell}=\mathbf{b}_{J}^{\prime}\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{J\ell}
, where

\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{J\ell}=\operatornamewithlimits{argmin}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{J}\in\mathbb{R}^{J}}\left\{-2\hat{D}_{2\ell}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\rho}_{J}+\boldsymbol{\rho}_{J}^{\prime}\hat{B}_{\ell}\boldsymbol{\rho}_{J}+\lambda\lVert\boldsymbol{\rho}_{J}\rVert_{q}^{q}\right\}.

(4.13)

The tuning parameter
\lambda
can be chosen by cross-validation.

4.3.2
Automatic estimation of the first step Riesz Representer

We also assume that there is a dictionary
(c_{k})_{k=1}^{\infty}
that spans
\Delta_{1}
. Thus,
\alpha_{01}(z)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\beta_{0k}c_{k}(z)

for a sequence of real numbers
(\beta_{0k})_{k=1}^{\infty}
. This means that
\alpha_{01}
can be approximated by
\mathbf{c}_{K}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0K}
, where
\mathbf{c}_{K}=(c_{1},...,c_{K})^{\prime}
and
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0K}=(\beta_{01},...,\beta_{0K})^{\prime}
. We can
now plug in
\mathbf{c}_{K}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0K}
into equation (
4.10
) for
\delta_{1}=c_{k}
,
k=1,...,K
. This gives
the following
K
moment conditions:

\mathbb{E}[D_{1}(W,\mathbf{c}_{K},\theta)]=-\mathbb{E}[r_{e}(Z,g_{0})\mathbf{c}_{K}(Z)\mathbf{c}_{K}(Z)^{\prime}]\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0K},

where
D_{1}(w,\mathbf{c}_{K},\theta)\equiv(D_{1}(w,c_{1},\theta),...,D_{1}(w,c_{K},\theta))^{\prime}
. Recall that
r_{e}
is the derivative of the generalized error
\epsilon
(see Assumption
4.5
). In this section we make explicit its dependence on
g_{0}
.

We use these conditions as a basis to construct
the objective function to estimate
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0K}
:

\min_{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}}\left\{-2\mathbb{E}[D_{1}(W,\mathbf{c}_{K},\theta)^{\prime}]\boldsymbol{\beta}_{K}+\boldsymbol{\beta}_{K}^{\prime}\mathbb{E}[-r_{e}(Z,g_{0})\mathbf{c}_{K}(Z)\mathbf{c}_{K}(Z)^{\prime}]\boldsymbol{\beta}_{K}+\lambda\lVert\boldsymbol{\beta}_{K}\rVert_{q}^{q}\right\},

(4.14)

where the tuning parameter
\lambda
may be different from that of the
second step. The automatic estimator for the first step Riesz Representer is build with the sample version of the above equation. The estimator is
given by
\hat{\alpha}_{1\ell}=\mathbf{c}_{K}^{\prime}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{K\ell}
, where

\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{K\ell}=\operatornamewithlimits{argmin}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}}\left\{-2\hat{D}_{1\ell}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{K}+\boldsymbol{\beta}_{K}^{\prime}\hat{C}_{\ell}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{K}+\lambda\lVert\boldsymbol{\beta}_{K}\rVert_{q}^{q}\right\},

(4.15)

with

\hat{C}_{\ell}\equiv\frac{-1}{n-n_{\ell}}\sum_{\ell^{\prime}\neq\ell}\sum_{i\in I_{\ell^{\prime}}}r_{e}(Z_{i},\hat{g}_{\ell\ell^{\prime}})\mathbf{c}_{K}(Z_{i})\mathbf{c}_{K}(Z_{i})^{\prime},

(4.16)

and

\hat{D}_{1\ell}\equiv\frac{1}{n-n_{\ell}}\sum_{\ell^{\prime}\neq\ell}\sum_{i\in I_{\ell^{\prime}}}D_{1}(W_{i},\mathbf{c}_{K}|\hat{g}_{\ell\ell^{\prime}},\hat{h}_{\ell\ell^{\prime}},\hat{\alpha}_{2\ell\ell^{\prime}},\tilde{\theta}_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}).

(4.17)

Note that the estimator for the first step linearization
\hat{D}_{1\ell}
requires estimators of the second step Riesz Representer
\hat{\alpha}_{2\ell\ell^{\prime}}
that do not include observations in
I_{\ell}\cup I_{\ell^{\prime}}
. These estimators can be obtained using the
methodology of the previous section. To construct
\hat{\alpha}_{2\ell\ell^{\prime}}=\mathbf{b}_{J}^{\prime}\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{J\ell\ell^{\prime}}
, we let
\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{J\ell\ell^{\prime}}
solve the optimization problem in equation (
4.13
), with
\hat{D}_{2\ell}
and
\hat{B}_{\ell}
replaced by

\displaystyle\hat{D}_{2\ell\ell^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{1}{n-n_{\ell}-n_{\ell^{\prime}}}\sum_{\ell^{\prime\prime}\notin\{\ell,\ell^{\prime}\}}\sum_{i\in I_{\ell^{\prime\prime}}}D_{2}(W_{i},\mathbf{b}_{J}|\hat{g}_{\ell\ell^{\prime}\ell^{\prime\prime}},\hat{h}_{\ell\ell^{\prime}\ell^{\prime\prime}},\tilde{\theta}_{\ell\ell^{\prime}\ell^{\prime\prime}})\text{ and }

\displaystyle\hat{B}_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{1}{n-n_{\ell}-n_{\ell^{\prime}}}\sum_{\ell^{\prime\prime}\notin\{\ell,\ell^{\prime}\}}\sum_{i\in I_{\ell^{\prime\prime}}}\mathbf{b}_{J}(X_{i},\varphi(D_{i},Z_{i},\hat{g}_{\ell\ell^{\prime}\ell^{\prime\prime}}))\mathbf{b}_{J}(X_{i},\varphi(D_{i},Z_{i},\hat{g}_{\ell\ell^{\prime}\ell^{\prime\prime}}))^{\prime},

respectively.

Furthermore,
D_{1}
may depend on the derivatives
\partial h_{0}/\partial v
and
\partial\alpha_{02}/\partial v
(see equation (
4.7
)). We thus need to provide consistent estimators of these derivatives to build
\hat{D}_{1\ell}
. It is straightforward to construct
an estimator
\partial\hat{\alpha}_{2\ell\ell^{\prime}}/\partial v
of the
derivative of
\alpha_{02}
based on the cross-fitted Lasso estimator. Since we have already estimated
\hat{\alpha}_{2\ell\ell}=\mathbf{b}_{J}^{\prime}\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{J\ell\ell^{\prime}}
, if each
b_{j}
is differentiable w.r.t.
v
, we have
that
\partial\hat{\alpha}_{2\ell\ell^{\prime}}/\partial v\equiv(\partial\mathbf{b}_{J}/\partial v)^{\prime}\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{J\ell\ell^{\prime}}
.

Estimation of
\partial h_{0}/\partial v
may be more tricky. It will depend
on the shape of the estimator
\hat{h}_{\ell\ell}
. Note that, since
h_{0}\in\Delta_{2}(g_{0})
, we may use the dictionary
(b_{j})_{j=1}^{\infty}
to approximate the parameter. In this case,
\hat{h}_{\ell\ell}

will be a Lasso or Ridge Regression estimator and we can estimate the
derivative of
h_{0}
as we have estimated the derivative of
\alpha_{02}
.
Moreover, if estimating
h_{0}
is a low dimensional problem, we can often take
\hat{h}_{\ell\ell}
as a Kernel or a Local
Linear Regression estimator, as in
Heckman et al., 1998
. Then, the derivatives of
h_{0}
can be
estimated by finding the analytical expression of the derivatives of the
kernel function.

For a general ML estimator
\hat{h}_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}
(e.g., Random
Forest), we propose a numerical derivative approach to estimate
\partial h_{0}/\partial v
. Let
t_{n}
be a tuning parameter depending on the sample
size with
t_{n}\downarrow 0
. We propose to estimate
\partial h_{0}(x,v)/\partial v
by

\frac{\partial\hat{h}_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}}{\partial v}(x,v)\equiv\frac{\hat{h}_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}(x,v+t_{n})-\hat{h}_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}(x,v)}{t_{n}}.

(4.18)

This approach has been used and justified theoretically in
Bravo et al., 2020
in a two-step setting. Note that, usually, we need to compute the derivative evaluated at
(X_{i},\varphi(D_{i},Z_{i},\hat{g}_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}))
. Alternative estimators that achieve optimal rates for partial derivatives are discussed in
Dai et al., 2016
.

5.1
ATE with high-dimensional propensity score

Robinson, 1988

\hat{\theta}=\frac{\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\sum_{i\in I_{\ell}}\left[\left(Y_{i}-\hat{h}_{\ell}(\hat{V}_{i\ell})\right)\left(D_{i}-\hat{V}_{i\ell}\right)+\hat{\alpha}_{1\ell}(Z_{i})\cdot\left(D_{i}-\hat{V}_{i\ell}\right)\right]}{\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\sum_{i\in I_{\ell}}\left(D_{i}-\hat{V}_{i\ell}\right)^{2}},

(5.1)

where
\hat{V}_{i\ell}=\Lambda(\hat{g}_{\ell}(Z_{i}))
and
\Lambda
is the Logistic function. Cross-fitted estimators
(\hat{g}_{\ell},\hat{h}_{\ell})
are discussed in Example
5
(p.

). In this section, we detail the estimation of the Riesz representer
\alpha_{01}
. As discussed in Section
4.3
, we propose to estimate the Riesz representer by
\hat{\alpha}_{1\ell}(z)=\mathbf{c}_{K}^{\prime}(z)\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{K\ell}
with
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{K\ell}
solving (
4.15
). We show how to construct
\hat{C}_{\ell}
and
\hat{D}_{1\ell}
in equation (
4.15
).

The term
\hat{C}_{\ell}
depends on the linearization of the first step generalized error. In this case, the first step is a Logit Lasso, based on the orthogonality conditions
\mathbb{E}[\delta_{1}(Z)\epsilon(W,g_{0})]=0
for
\delta_{1}\in\Delta_{1}
and
\epsilon(w,g)=d-\Lambda(g(z))
. Therefore, the linearization of the generalized error is

r_{e}(z,g_{0})=\frac{\partial}{\partial a}\mathbb{E}[D-\Lambda(g_{0}(Z)+a)|Z]=-\frac{\partial}{\partial a}\Lambda(g_{0}(Z)+a)=-\Lambda(g_{0}(Z))[1-\Lambda(g_{0}(Z))],

where the derivative is evaluated at

. Therefore,

\hat{C}_{\ell}=\frac{1}{n-n_{\ell}}\sum_{\ell^{\prime}\neq\ell}\sum_{i\in I_{\ell^{\prime}}}\hat{V}_{i\ell\ell^{\prime}}(1-\hat{V}_{i\ell\ell^{\prime}})\mathbf{c}_{K}(Z_{i})\mathbf{c}_{K}(Z_{i})^{\prime},\text{ where }\hat{V}_{i\ell\ell^{\prime}}\equiv\Lambda(\hat{g}_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}(Z_{i})).

(5.2)

\hat{D}_{1\ell}
, note that, since
\alpha_{02}=0
, we have that
D_{1}=D_{11}
and

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\tau}\mathbb{E}[m(W,g_{\tau},h_{0},\theta)]=\frac{\partial}{\partial\tau}\mathbb{E}\left[\{Y-h_{0}(\Lambda(g_{\tau}(Z)))-\theta(D-\Lambda(g_{\tau}(Z)))\}\cdot(D-\Lambda(g_{\tau}(Z)))\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\left\{-\left(Y-h_{0}(V)-\theta(D-V)\right)+\left(-\dot{h}_{0}(V)+\theta\right)\cdot(D-V)\right\}\dot{\Lambda}(g_{0}(Z))\frac{\partial g_{\tau}}{\partial\tau}\right],

D_{1}(W_{i},\mathbf{c}_{K}|\hat{g}_{\ell\ell^{\prime}},\hat{h}_{\ell\ell^{\prime}})=-\left[Y_{i}-\hat{h}_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}(\hat{V}_{i\ell\ell^{\prime}})\right]\hat{V}_{i\ell\ell^{\prime}}\left(1-\hat{V}_{i\ell\ell^{\prime}}\right)\mathbf{c}_{K}(Z_{i}).

5.2
Partially Linear Model with Generated Regressors: Autoencoders

The partial linear model has become a workhorse model for the application of debiased machine learning methods, see
Ahrens et al., 2025
and references therein. In this section, we propose a debiased estimator for the partially linear model in Example
2
, which is robust to ML-generated regressors. We study a general
V=\varphi(D,Z,g_{0})
, where
g_{0}
is identified by the orthogonality conditions in (
2.1
), and we illustrate the general theory with an application to learned confounders via autoencoders.

In the partially linear model with generated regressors, it is convenient to introduce the second steps
h_{0S}(v)=\mathbb{E}[S|V=v]
for
S=Y
or
S=D
, with an identifying moment function for the parameter of interest as in DML,

m(W,g_{0},h_{0},\theta_{0})=(Y-h_{0Y}(V)-\theta_{0}^{\prime}(D-h_{0D}(V)))\cdot(D-h_{0D}(V)),

where
h_{0}\equiv(h_{0Y},h_{0D})
.

As we have considered throughout the paper, the above moment function is locally robust to the second step. Indeed,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\tau}\mathbb{E}[m(W,h_{\tau Y},h_{0D},g_{0},\theta)]=-\frac{\partial}{\partial\tau}\mathbb{E}[(D-h_{0D}(V))h_{\tau Y}(V)]=0,

and

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\tau}\mathbb{E}[m(W,h_{0Y},h_{\tau D},g_{0},\theta)]

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial\tau}\mathbb{E}[\theta^{\prime}h_{\tau D}(V)(D-h_{0D}(V))

\displaystyle-(Y-h_{0Y}(V)-\theta^{\prime}(D-h_{0D}(V))h_{\tau D}(V)]=0.

Both results are derived from iterated expetations, conditioning on
V
. This means that
\alpha_{02}=0
. Therefore, if
(h_{0Y},h_{0D})
is estimated by some ML method, such as, e.g., Lasso, Random Forest, or Neural Nets, the moments in
m
are the DML moments of
Chernozhukov et al., 2018
. These moments have been shown useful in practice. However, are the DML inferences valid in our setting with generated regressors? As we have seen in Section
5.1
,
\alpha_{01}
could be different from zero, so the answer is generally no.

We propose a debiased three step estimator of the parameter on interest which is robust to the first step. Suppose that
D
has dimension
p
. The estimator solves

\hat{\theta}=\operatornamewithlimits{argmin}_{\theta\in\Theta}\hat{\psi}(\theta)^{\prime}\hat{\Upsilon}\hat{\psi}(\theta),

where
\hat{\Upsilon}
is a weighting matrix and the debiased moments are

\hat{\psi}(\theta)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\sum_{i\in I_{\ell}}\left[\left(Y_{i}-\hat{h}_{\ell Y}(\hat{V}_{i\ell})-\theta^{\prime}(D_{i}-\hat{h}_{\ell D}(\hat{V}_{i\ell}))\right)(D_{i}-\hat{h}_{\ell D}(\hat{V}_{i\ell}))+\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{1\ell}(Z_{i})\epsilon(W_{i},\hat{g}_{\ell})\right],

(5.3)

where
\hat{V}_{i\ell}=\varphi(D_{i},Z_{i},\hat{g}_{\ell})
,
\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{1\ell}(z)
is a
p
-dimensional vector with entries
\hat{\alpha}_{1j\ell}(z)
,
j=1,\dots,p
, corresponding to the automatic estimator of the first step Riesz Representer for each of the moment condition, and
\epsilon(w,g)
is the one-dimensional generalized error that identifies
g_{0}
. The extension to multiple generated regressors is straightforward:
\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{1\ell}(z)
would be a
p\times dim(V)
matrix and
\epsilon(w,g)
a
dim(V)\times 1
vector.

Each element of
\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{1\ell}
may be constructed as in Section
5.2
:
\hat{\alpha}_{1j\ell}(z)=\mathbf{c}_{K}(z)^{\prime}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{Kj\ell}
, with
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{Kj\ell}
solving equation (
4.15
). The construction of
\hat{C}_{\ell}
will depend on the shape of the generalized error
\epsilon(w,g)
. For instance, in the Control Function setup with one dimensional
D
and generated regressor
V=D-g_{0}(Z)
, one has that
\epsilon(w,g)=d-g(z)
and
r_{e}(z,g)=-1
.

Construction of
\hat{D}_{1j\ell}
for each moment condition parallels the one in Section
5.1
. For each moment condition (and each element of
h_{0D}
, denoted
h_{0D_{j}}
), we have that the indirect
effect of the generated regressor is zero because

with

\displaystyle\hat{\varepsilon}_{i\ell\ell^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=Y_{i}-\hat{h}_{\ell\ell^{\prime}Y}(\hat{V}_{i\ell\ell^{\prime}})-\tilde{\theta}_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}^{\prime}\left(D_{i}-\hat{h}_{\ell\ell^{\prime}D}(\hat{V}_{i\ell\ell^{\prime}})\right)\text{ and }

\displaystyle\dot{\varepsilon}_{i\ell\ell^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=-\dot{h}_{\ell\ell^{\prime}Y}(\hat{V}_{i\ell\ell^{\prime}})+\tilde{\theta}_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}^{\prime}\dot{h}_{\ell\ell^{\prime}D}(\hat{V}_{i\ell\ell^{\prime}}).

In the above equations,

are cross-fitted estimators of
\partial h_{0S}/\partial v
, for
S=Y,D_{1},\dots,D_{p}
. The cross-fitted generated regressor satisfies
\hat{V}_{i\ell\ell^{\prime}}=\varphi(D_{i},Z_{i},\hat{g}_{\ell\ell^{\prime}})
. Also,
\hat{D}_{\varphi i\ell\ell^{\prime}}
is a cross-fitted estimator of the linearization of the generated regressors with respecto to

(D_{i},Z_{i})
and
\hat{g}_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}

\hat{D}_{\varphi i\ell\ell^{\prime}}=-1

In general, none of the first step Riesz representers
\alpha_{01}(z)
will be zero. As we have discussed, the Riesz representer is identified as the orthogonal
projection of
-\dot{h}_{0D}(V)\varepsilon+\dot{\varepsilon}[D-h_{0D}(V)]
onto
\Delta_{1}
, being
\varepsilon\equiv Y-h_{0Y}(V)-\theta_{0}^{\prime}(D-h_{0D}(V))
and
\dot{\varepsilon}\equiv\partial\varepsilon/\partial v
. This can be obtained as a weighted Lasso fit with generated “dependent” variable. This dependent variable can be further simplified to
\dot{\varepsilon}\cdot[D-h_{0D}(V)]
if
\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon|D,Z]=0.
Even in this case,
\alpha_{01}(z)
is generally non-zero, and inference not accounting for generated regressors will be invalid. To further illustrate this point, assume also that
V=D-g_{0}(Z)
is independent of
Z
and that
\Delta_{1}=L_{2}(Z)

\alpha_{01}(z)=\mathbb{E}[\dot{\varepsilon}]\cdot(g_{0}(z)-\mathbb{E}[g_{0}(Z)])
, which is non-zero when
\mathbb{E}[\dot{\varepsilon}]\neq 0
and
g_{0}(Z)
is not a constant. Under standard regularity conditions, we further have that
\mathbb{E}[\dot{\varepsilon}]=\mathbb{E}[\frac{\partial\kappa_{0}(V)}{\partial v}]
. This average derivative is expected to be non-zero in most applications.

We illustrate the previous results with an application to learned confounders via autoencoders. Autoencoders were introduced in
Rumelhart et al., 1986b
with the goal of learning latent representations efficiently. Here we describe the general
structure of an autoencoder, which has three main components: (i) the
encoder function

which is a function of the input controls
Z

representation,

which is our generated regressor; and (iii)
the decoder function
v\mapsto d_{0}(v).
The encoder and decoder
functions are identified from the objective function

(e_{0},d_{0})=\arg\min_{e\in\mathcal{E},d\in\mathcal{D}}\mathbb{E}[\ell(Z,d(e(Z)))],

where
\ell

\mathcal{E}\
and
\mathcal{D}
are
classes of functions for the encoder and decoder, respectively. For concreteness, we consider the Euclidean loss
\ell(Z,f)=\left|Z-f\right|^{2}
and Feed-Forward Neural
Networks (FNN) for
\mathcal{E}\
and
\mathcal{D}
indexed by a high-dimensional parameter
\zeta\in\mathbb{R}^{\dim(\zeta)}

e_{0}(Z)\equiv e_{\zeta_{0}}(Z)
and

where
\zeta_{0}
is identified by

\zeta_{0}=\arg\min_{\zeta\in B}\mathbb{E}[\ell(Z,f_{\zeta}(Z))],

f_{\zeta}(Z)=d_{\zeta}(e_{\zeta}(Z))
,
\zeta
are the parameters of the FNN (weights and biases), and
B
is the parameter space where the parameter
\zeta
lies. Another ingredient of a FNN is the activation function
\sigma(\cdot)
. The two most popular choices are the ReLU and sigmoid functions.

10

When the activation function is linear, we recover Principal Components Analysis (PCA) as a special case. Hence, autoencoders can be viewed as nonlinear generalizations of PCA. A key feature of autoencoders is to have common parameters in the encoder and
decoder functions, a type of sparsity, and to create a “bottleneck”,

5.3
Nonparametric ATE with Boosted propensity score

From our results, the debiased moment has the expression

5.4
CASF with a Control Function Approach

6.1
General results

This section gives general conditions for asymptotic normality of the

estimate the linear approximations of

and

at a certain rate

Monte Carlo simulations: ATE in the partially linear model with generated regressors

We study the finite sample performance of the ATE estimator in the partially linear model via a Monte Carlo simulation. See the first version of this paper for simulations for the CASF. The available data is
(Y,D,Z)
, with
Z\equiv(Z_{j})_{j=1}^{10}
. Outcome and treatment equations are the following:

\displaystyle Y

\displaystyle=D+Z_{1}+Z_{2}+\varepsilon\text{ and }

\displaystyle D

\displaystyle=\mathbf{1}\left(g_{0}(Z)-\nu\geq 0\right).

The error terms
\varepsilon
and
\nu
are independent, with
\varepsilon\sim N(0,1)
. The distribution of
\nu
varies with the specification: it can be logistic or standard normal. Regressors
Z
are independent from each other and are uniformly distributed in
[-1,1]
. Regressors are also independent of
\nu
. On the other hand, the first two regressors
(Z_{1},Z_{2})
and
\varepsilon
are linked by a Gaussian copula. That is, the distribution of
(\varepsilon,Z_{1},Z_{2})
is

\displaystyle F(\varepsilon,z_{1},z_{2})

\displaystyle=C(\Phi(\varepsilon),U(z_{1}),U(z_{2})),

\displaystyle C(u_{1},u_{2},u_{3})

\displaystyle\equiv\Phi_{\Sigma}(\Phi^{-1}(u_{1}),\Phi^{-1}(u_{3}),\Phi^{-1}(u_{3})),

\displaystyle\Sigma

\displaystyle\equiv\begin{pmatrix}1&1/2&1/2\\
1/2&1&0\\
1/2&0&1\end{pmatrix},

where
U
is the cdf of a uniform in
[-1,1]
,
\Phi
is the standard normal cdf, and
\Phi_{\Sigma}
is the cdf of a multivariate normal centered at zero and with covariance matrix
\Sigma
. Since all specifications include
(Z_{1},Z_{2})
in the treatment equation, this makes treatment
D
endogenous.

We consider two specifications for the treatment selection index: a linear model
g_{0}(z)=(z_{1}+z_{2}+z_{3}+z_{4}+z_{5}+z_{6})/C_{g}
and a non-linear model
g_{0}(z)=(z_{1}+z_{1}^{2}+z_{2}-z_{2}^{2}+z_{3}z_{4}+z_{5}z_{6})/C_{g}
. The constant
C_{g}
is chosen so that the propensity score is supported on
[0.01,0.99]
. For instance, in the non-linear specification with logistic error,
C_{g}=\Lambda^{-1}(0.99)/5
, with
\Lambda
the logistic cdf. In every specification the ATE is 1 and is identified by the moment condition in equation (
2.3
).

We evaluate the performance of two estimators:

•

The DML estimator
\hat{\theta}_{DML}
, which is robust to the second step but not to the first. The estimator is given in equation (
3.4
).

•

The 3-Step Debiased (3SD) estimator
\hat{\theta}
, which is robust to the generated propensity score, given in equation (
5.1
).
13
13

13

Due to computational speed, we use
(\hat{g}_{\ell},\hat{h}_{\ell})
to build the automatic estimator
\hat{\alpha}_{1\ell}
, instead of the doubly cross-fitted
(\hat{g}_{\ell\ell^{\prime}},\hat{h}_{\ell\ell^{\prime}})
. Simulations with a smaller number of replications show that results were similar.

For both estimators, we set the number of cross-fitting partitions to
L=10
. The propensity score is estimated by a Logit-Lasso of
D
onto the regressors
Z
, with tuning parameter chosen by cross-validation with 3 folds. That is, we choose the dictionary

The asymptotic variance of each estimator is the usual GMM asymptotic variance. To be precise, let

\displaystyle\hat{M}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\sum_{i\in I_{\ell}}\left(D_{i}-\hat{V}_{i\ell}\right)^{2},

8
Conclusion

We propose Automatic Locally Robust estimators for structural parameters in
the presence of ML-generated regressors. We show that the debiasing correction
term can be decomposed into terms accounting for the first-step and the second-step
estimation. Each of the first and second steps IF depends on an additional
Riesz representers, which can be automatically estimated (i.e., estimated
without finding their analytic shape).

We apply our results to construct Automatic Locally Robust estimators for
the ATE and CASF under different modelling assumptions (partially linear model and nonparametric model) and different generated regressors (high-dimensional propensity score, autoencoders, control function, etc). The analytic shape of the Riesz representers in these two cases is
particularly complex. For the ATE, our automatic debiased estimator overcomes the large biases of the state-of-the-art method, the DML, which does not account for the generated regressors. For the CASF parameter, the moment condition depends on the whole shape of the second step parameter (not only its pointwise value), making existing results on generated regressors not applicable even in low-dimensional scenarios. Therefore,
automatic estimation is particularly well suited for these problems. We have
shown that commonly used plug-in or DML methods lead to highly biased inferences
for the ATE (also for the CASF). Locally Robust estimators correct the bias and deliver much more accurate inference in a complex setting with
ML-generated regressors.
