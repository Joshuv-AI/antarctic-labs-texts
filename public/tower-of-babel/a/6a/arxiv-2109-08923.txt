Test Martingales for bounded random variables
Harrie Hendriks

Abstract
Given a positive random variable X X , X ≥ 0 X\geq 0 a.s., a null hypothesis H 0 : 𝐄 ⁡ ( X ) ≤ μ H_{0}:{\bf E}(X)\leq\mu and a random sample of infinite size of X X , we construct test supermartingales for H 0 H_{0} , i.e. positive processes that are supermartingale if the null hypothesis is satisfied. We test hypothesis H 0 H_{0} by testing the supermartingale hypothesis on a test supermartingale. We construct test supermartingales that lead to tests with power 1. We derive confidence lower bounds. For bounded random variables we extend the techniques to two-sided tests of H 0 : 𝐄 ⁡ ( X ) = μ H_{0}:{\bf E}(X)=\mu and to the construction of confidence intervals. In financial auditing random sampling is proposed as one of the possible techniques to gather enough evidence to justify rejection of the null hypothesis that there is a ’material’ misstatement in a financial report. The goal of our work is to provide a mathematical context that could represent such process of gathering evidence by means of repeated random sampling, while ensuring an intended significance level.

Primary 62L12; Secondary 60G42, 62G10, 62G15

Example:
In the context of financial auditing,
we have in mind that

The auditor has to assure himself that

The randomness of

is introduced by the auditor
who has a procedure to select randomly an item

\Omega

such that any

to be selected,
where

One is interested in a characteristic of

with respect to

.

For example
\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{L}
are identifiers of items underlying a financial report.
For
\omega\in\Omega
one has its book value

and the so-called taint or tainting

In this context we will assume that

,

The total book value is

The auditor is interested in the total misstatement

\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}B(\omega)-A(\omega)=B_{\hbox{\scriptsize tot}}\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}T(\omega)B(\omega)/B_{\hbox{\scriptsize tot}}=B_{\hbox{\scriptsize tot}}\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}T(\omega)p(\omega)=B_{\hbox{\scriptsize tot}}{\bf E}(T),

Clearly we do not offer a way out of handling missing items in the report (or items with book value 0).

The problem is that
\Omega
is a large set,
so that it is not practical to determine all

-values.
Often it will be sufficient to determine an upper bound for

(or, equivalently, a lower bound for

),

.
Sampling may be carried out as follows.
Given a number

\sum_{i=1}^{\ell-1}p(\omega_{i})<z\leq\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}p(\omega_{i})
.
A random sample

Notice that all

.

Given

2
Supermartingales

Suppose given an integrable process
\{Y_{k}\}_{k=0}^{\infty}
,
adapted to the filtration
\{\mathcal{F}_{k}\}_{k}
.
Recall the
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{F}_{k}
represents the information available at time
k
, including
the values of
Y_{i}
for
i\leq k
.
The process
\{Y_{k}\}_{k}
is a
supermartingale

if
Y_{k}\geq{\bf E}[Y_{\ell}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]
for
0\leq k<\ell<\infty
.
It is called a
martingale
if
Y_{k}={\bf E}[Y_{\ell}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]
for
0\leq k<\ell<\infty
.
Suppose
\{Y_{k}\}_{k}
is a positive supermartingale.
The ’maximal lemma’ (
[
4
, Ch. V, Thm. 20]
, cf. Ville’s gambler’s ruin theorem
[
14
, Thm. 1 p.84]
)
implies that

\forall\lambda\geq 0:\lambda\,{\mathbb{P}}\{\sup_{k}Y_{k}\geq\lambda\}\leq{\bf E}[Y_{0}].

We apply this result in the following practical, but actually equivalent, form:

Lemma 1

If
\{Y_{k}\}_{k}
is a positive supermartingale,
then

\forall\lambda\geq 0:\lambda\,{\mathbb{P}}\{\exists\ell:Y_{\ell}\geq\lambda\}\leq{\bf E}[Y_{0}].

Proof
Consider the random variable
N
which is
the first time
k
that the process
\{Y_{k}\}_{k}
reaches or exceeds level
\lambda
,
or
\infty
if the process does not exceed level
\lambda
.

N
is a stopping time.
If one stops the supermartingale
\{Y_{k}\}_{k}
at that time,
the stopped process
\{Y_{k\wedge N}\}_{k}
is still a supermartingale (
[
5
, Thm. 5.2.6]
).
Thus for any
\ell
we have
{\bf E}[Y_{0}]={\bf E}[Y_{0\wedge N}]\geq{\bf E}[Y_{\ell\wedge N}]
while
for the positive random variable
Y_{\ell\wedge N}
we have

{\bf E}[Y_{\ell\wedge N}]\geq\lambda{\mathbb{P}}\{Y_{\ell\wedge N}\geq\lambda\}
. The lemma then follows since the events

\{Y_{\ell\wedge N}\geq\lambda\}=\{N\leq\ell\}
form an increasing sequence for increasing
\ell

whose union is
\{N<\infty\}=\{\exists\ell:Y_{\ell}\geq\lambda\}
.

We follow
[
6
]
and
[
13
]
where
the significance of the above ideas
for
statistical hypothesis testing is worked out.
Be given a statistical hypothesis
H_{0}
.
A
test (super)martingale
(for
H_{0}
) is a process
\{Y_{k}\}_{k=0}^{\infty}

such that, if
H_{0}
is satisfied,

\{Y_{k}\}_{k}
is a positive (super)martingale and
{\bf E}[Y_{0}]>0
.
Be given a significance level
\alpha
,
0<\alpha\leq 1
.
A practical test consists of observing sequentially
Y_{k}
,
k=1,2,\ldots

and stop at time
n
if
Y_{n}\geq{\bf E}(Y_{0})/\alpha
or stop at some other time
n
.
In the first case
Y_{n}\geq{\bf E}(Y_{0})/\alpha
and one may reject
H_{0}
, otherwise one cannot reject
H_{0}
.
The size of such test is the probability to reject
H_{0}
under the assumption that
H_{0}
is satisfied.
By Lemma
1
it is at most
\alpha
:

{\mathbb{P}}\{\hbox{Reject }H_{0}\}\leq{\mathbb{P}}\{\exists n:Y_{n}\geq{\bf E}(Y_{0})/\alpha\}\leq({\bf E}(Y_{0})/\alpha)^{-1}{\bf E}(Y_{0})=\alpha.

To show the sharpness of Lemma
1

we give the example of a classical test about the probability of success of a Bernoulli variable.
The basic idea behind the example is the following.
Let
H_{0}
be a simple
null hypothesis about a rv
Z

and let
R
be the critical region of
a test of
H_{0}
based on a random sample
Z_{1},Z_{2},\ldots
of
Z
.
Then
the conditional probabilities

Y_{i}={\mathbb{P}}(R\mid Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{i})
of
R
under hypothesis
H_{0}
, constitute a test martingale.

Remark 1

Let
Z
be a Bernoulli variable
Z\sim{\mathcal{B}}(\nu)
,
\nu
unknown,
and consider null hypothesis
H_{0}:{\bf E}(Z)\geq\mu
,
that we want to test against
{\bf E}(Z)<\mu
.
Take the test to reject
H_{0}
if in a sample of fixed size
n
at most
k
successes are found.
It will have significance level
\alpha=F(k;n,\mu)
,
where
F(k;n,\mu)
denotes the cumulative distribution function
of the binomial distribution with parameters
(n,\mu)
evaluated at
k
.

We construct a test supermartingale
\{Y_{i}\}_{i}
as follows.
Given a random sample
Z_{1},Z_{2},\ldots
of
Z
, let
S_{i}=Z_{1}+\cdots+Z_{i}

and let

Y_{0}=F(k;n,\mu);\quad Y_{i}=F(k-S_{i};n-i,\mu)\quad(1\leq i\leq n);\quad Y_{i}=Y_{n}\quad(i>n).

If
\nu={\bf E}(Z)\geq\mu
,
this process is a supermartingale because of a well-known recursion formula for
binomial distribution functions:

\displaystyle{\bf E}(Y_{i}\mid S_{i-1})

\displaystyle={\bf E}(F(k-S_{i};n-i,\mu)\mid S_{i-1})

\displaystyle=\nu F(k-(S_{i-1}+1);n-i,\mu)+(1-\nu)F(k-S_{i-1};n-i,\mu)

\displaystyle\leq\mu F(k-(S_{i-1}+1);n-i,\mu)+(1-\mu)F(k-S_{i-1};n-i,\mu)

\displaystyle=F(k-S_{i-i};n-(i-1),\mu)=Y_{i-1}.

Notice that
[S_{n}\leq k]\Leftrightarrow[Y_{n}=1]
and
[S_{n}>k]\Leftrightarrow[Y_{n}=0]
.
If
\nu=\mu
then
H_{0}
holds and

\alpha=F(k;n,\mu)={\mathbb{P}}\{Y_{n}=1\}\leq{\mathbb{P}}\{\exists\ell:Y_{\ell}\geq 1\}\leq{\bf E}(Y_{0})=\alpha
.

3
Test supermartingales for positive random variables

Given
\mu>0
, we will construct test supermartingales to test null hypothesis
{\bf E}(X)\leq\mu

for a positive integrable rv
X
.
First we find functions
f:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
satisfying

[X\geq 0\hbox{ a.s. \& }{\bf E}(X)\leq\mu]\Rightarrow[f(X)\geq 0\hbox{ a.s. \& }{\bf E}(f(X))\leq 1].

(1)

Applied to the case of two-point distributions let
0\leq x<\mu<y
and consider
X
such that

{\mathbb{P}}\{X=x\}=(y-\mu)/(y-x)
,
{\mathbb{P}}\{X=y\}=(\mu-x)/(y-x)
, so that

{\bf E}(X)=\mu
.
We need
f(x)(y-\mu)/(y-x)+f(y)(\mu-x)/(y-x)\leq 1
, or equivalently

(1-f(x))/(\mu-x)\geq(f(y)-1)/(y-\mu)
.
This must hold for all
x,y
with
0\leq x<\mu<y
.
Thus there is
b
such that

(1-f(x))/(\mu-x)\geq b\geq(f(y)-1)/(y-\mu)
for all
x,y
with
0\leq x<\mu<y
.
It follows that
f(t)\leq 1+b(t-\mu)=(1-b\mu)+bt
for all
t\geq 0
.
In order that
f(t)\geq 0
for all
t\geq 0
we need

1-b\mu\geq 0
and
b\geq 0
, that is
0\leq b\mu\leq 1
.
Thus we propose the following functions: for
0\leq c\leq 1

f(t)=(1-c)+c\cdot\frac{t}{\mu}.

(2)

We would like to draw attention to its relation to likelihood ratios.
Suppose
0<c<1
, take
y>\mu
and choose
\theta
such that
c=(\theta-\mu)/(y-\mu)
.
Consider the two-point distributions
\ell_{\mu}
and
\ell_{\theta}
with support
\{0,y\}

defined as

\ell_{\nu}(y)=\nu/y
,
\ell_{\nu}(0)=1-\nu/y
for
\nu=\mu
resp.
\nu=\theta
.
Then
f(y)=(1-c)+c\,y/\mu=\theta/\mu=\ell_{\theta}(y)/\ell_{\mu}(y)

and

f(0)=1-c=(y-\theta)/(y-\mu)=\ell_{\theta}(0)/\ell_{\mu}(0)
.
Thus
f
is the affine extrapolation of the likelihood ratio

\ell_{\theta}/\ell_{\mu}
at the points 0 and
y
.

See Section
4
for some alternatives to the condition
X\geq 0
a.s. in (
1
).

Suppose
X
is an integrable random variable
such that
X\geq 0
a.s..
Consider the null hypothesis

H_{0}:{\bf E}(X)\leq\mu.

We will construct
test supermartingales for
H_{0}
using factors modelled after (
2
).
Consider a random sample
X_{1},X_{2},\ldots
of the random variable
X
.
It defines a filtration
\{\mathcal{F}_{k}\}_{k}
by
\sigma
-algebras

\mathcal{F}_{k}=\sigma(X_{1},\ldots,X_{k})
for
k\geq 1
and
the trivial
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{F}_{0}
.
In particular
{\bf E}(X_{k}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k-1})={\bf E}(X_{k})={\bf E}(X)
.
We let
M_{0}=1
.
At time
(k-1)

the variables
X_{1},\ldots,X_{k-1}
and
M_{k-1}
are observed, having values
x_{1},\ldots,x_{k-1}
and
m_{k-1}
.
One chooses
test parameter

c_{k}\in[0,1]
which should not depend on
X_{k},X_{k+1},\ldots
in any conceivable way
(this makes the process of test parameters
\{c_{k}\}_{k}
‘predictable’, i.e. for all
k
,
c_{k}
is
\mathcal{F}_{k-1}
-measurable).
and one defines

M_{k}=m_{k-1}\cdot\left((1-c_{k})+c_{k}\frac{X_{k}}{\mu}\right).

(3)

For our choice of filtration the functional dependence of
c_{k}
on the observations of
X_{1},\ldots,X_{k-1}

should have been
fixed before any observation was available.
But see Remark
2
for a broader, more practical class of filtrations.
The process
\{M_{k}\}_{k}
is a test supermartingale for
H_{0}:{\bf E}(X)\leq\mu
.
We obtain a test with significance level
\alpha
if we reject
H_{0}
at a time
n
with

\max\{M_{k}\mid k\leq n\}\geq 1/\alpha
.

As in
[
6
]
(and
[
16
]
) we will express the above construction in a
gambling metaphor
,
that we present as a
’martingale transform’ of a supermartingale by a positive predictable process (see e.g.
[
5
, Thm. 5.2.5]
,
[
17
, Section 10.6]
).
We consider the hypothesis
H_{0}:{\bf E}(X)\leq\mu
that we would like to reject.
Based on the random sample
X_{1},X_{2},\ldots
of
X
,
consider the process
Y=\{Y_{k}\}_{k}
with
Y_{0}=0
and

Y_{k}=Y_{k-1}-1+X_{k}/\mu
.
Under
H_{0}
the process
Y
is a supermartingale
with respect to the filtration
\{\mathcal{F}_{k}=\sigma(X_{1},\ldots,X_{k})\}_{k}
.
Consider a lottery that takes place at time
k
and pays out
X_{k}/\mu

per unit stake, so that the net gain per unit stake is

-1+X_{k}/\mu=Y_{k}-Y_{k-1}
.
We start with an initial unit amount of capital
m_{0}=1
.
At time
k-1
we have accumulated a capital of
m_{k-1}
and
we decide to stake an amount of
H_{k}=c_{k}m_{k-1}
in this lottery,
0\leq H_{k}\leq m_{k-1}
.
Then at time
k
our capital will become

m_{k-1}+H_{k}(Y_{k}-Y_{k-1})=m_{k-1}+c_{k}m_{k-1}(-1+X_{k}/\mu)=M_{k}
.
If
X
satisfies hypothesis
H_{0}
, we have a fair or loss-making game.
In particular, if we succeed in ending up with a large gain, we have reason to assert that
{\bf E}(X)>\mu
.
With this metaphor it should be intuitively clear, that
it is wrong to change the stake amount
c_{k}m_{k-1}
, after having observed
X_{k}
.
Moreover, if one wants to switch over to a new gambling game to reject
H_{0}
,
one has to continue with the capital left after the preceding game.

Choosing the test parameters, integrated test supermartingales

A plausible choice for
c_{k}
in the construction
(
3
) of a test supermartingale
is that value of
c
that maximizes

m_{k-1}(c)=\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}(1-c+c\,x_{i}/\mu)
, where some prudence is necessary to avoid
c_{k}=1
.
We will consider a different proposal.
Start with some probability density
\pi
on
[0,1]
,
typically the uniform probability density on the interval
[c_{0},1]
for some
c_{0}\geq 0
.
Define
\{M_{k}(c)\}_{k}
to be the test supermartingale based on the choice
c_{k}=c
, all
k
,
and consider the
integrated test supermartingale
with respect to
test measure

\pi
:

\displaystyle M_{k}(\pi)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}M_{k}(c)\pi(c)dc=\int_{0}^{1}M_{k-1}(c)\cdot(1-c+c\,X_{k}/\mu)\pi(c)dc

(4)

\displaystyle=M_{k-1}(\pi)\cdot(1-c_{k}+c_{k}X_{k}/\mu),\hbox{ where }

\displaystyle c_{k}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}c\cdot M_{k-1}(c)\pi(c)dc/M_{k-1}(\pi).

Notice that
c_{k}
is the expectation
of the probability density
p_{k-1}
defined by
p_{k-1}(c)=M_{k-1}(c)\pi(c)/M_{k-1}(\pi)
,
and that
M_{k-1}(c)
is a log-concave function in
c
(see Remark
4
).
In case
\pi
is the uniform probability distribution on [0,1],
for large
k
, density
p_{k-1}
will be concentrated around the value of
c
for which

M_{k-1}(c)
is largest.

In a numerical implementation one may choose a finite number of points

b_{1},\ldots,b_{r}\in[0,1]
and assign to them probabilities
\pi(b_{i})=\pi_{i}
where

\pi_{i}\geq 0
and
\pi_{1}+\cdots+\pi_{r}=1
and consider

M_{k}(\pi)=\sum_{i=1}^{r}M_{k}(b_{i})\pi(b_{i})
.

See
[
16
]
where the above ideas are worked out in much detail in the context of
constructing confidence bounds.

Test supermartingales for the null hypothesis
H_{0}:{\bf E}(X)\geq\mu
or
H_{0}:{\bf E}(X)=\mu

Given
X\leq\tau
a.s. and
\mu<\tau
, we obtain test supermartingales for the
null hypothesis
H_{0}:{\bf E}(X)\geq\mu
by transforming it into
H_{0}:{\bf E}(\tau-X)\leq\tau-\mu

leading to multiplication factors

1-c_{k}+c_{k}\,\frac{\tau-X_{k}}{\tau-\mu},\penalty\ k=1,2,\ldots,

(5)

where
c_{k}
is
\mathcal{F}_{k-1}
measurable and
0\leq c_{k}\leq 1
.

If
0\leq X\leq\tau
a.s., one can combine test supermartingales
\{M_{n}^{+}\}_{n}
for
H_{0}:{\bf E}(X)\geq\mu
and

\{M_{n}^{-}\}_{n}
for
H_{0}:{\bf E}(X)\leq\mu
, based on the same data,
by taking
\{\rho^{+}M_{n}^{+}+\rho^{-}M_{n}^{-}\}_{n}
for any
\rho^{+},\rho^{-}\geq 0
with
\rho^{+}+\rho^{-}=1
.
This will be a test supermartingale for
H_{0}:{\bf E}(X)=\mu
.

Filtrations

Remark 2

In practice the observation of a rv
X_{k}
is accompanied by some, possibly random, attributes
like the time and the monetary cost needed to determine the value of
X_{k}
.
In the financial auditing example as described in the Introduction one could also think of the
book item
and the book and audit value associated with the observation of the tainting.
In particular the actual filtration that one would like to adopt is much richer than

\{\mathcal{F}_{k}^{o}=\sigma(X_{1},\ldots,X_{k})\}_{k}
,
and may include these attributes,
as well as for example the mental condition of the investigator.

In order to stay close to the intuition for a random, iid, sample
X_{1},X_{2},\ldots
,
a suitable extra condition
on the sample is that
X_{k},X_{k+1},\ldots
and their attributes are independent
of all information contained in
\mathcal{F}_{k-1}
.
One can reach this by actually
hiding
previous to time
k
all information about the rv’s
X_{\ell}
and their attributes for
\ell\geq k

until it is decided to determine and process the value of
X_{k}
. On the other hand, a richer filtration might typically allow
test parameters
c_{k}
, depending not only on the observed values of
X_{1},\ldots,X_{k-1}
,
but for example also on the built-up insights of the investigator up to time
k-1
.

Inspired by
[
16
]
, as an illustration we present the example of

sampling without replacement
.
We consider the situation of a filtration

\{\mathcal{F}_{k}\}_{k=0}^{\infty}

and a sequence of random variables
\{X_{k}\}_{k=0}^{\infty}
adapted to this filtration, such that the null hypothesis
{\bf E}(X)\leq\mu
is equivalent to the sequence of hypotheses

{\bf E}(X_{k}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k-1})\leq m_{k}
where
m_{k}
can be determined at time
k-1
,
based on the information available in
\mathcal{F}_{k-1}
.
In the financial auditing example described in the Introduction one could consider sampling without replacement, items
\omega
with probability proportional to the book value
B(\omega)
, yielding a random ordering
(\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{L})
of
\Omega
.
Null hypothesis
{\bf E}(T)\geq\mu
is then equivalent to

{\bf E}(T(\omega_{k})\mid\mathcal{F}_{k-1})\geq m_{k}
for
k=1,\ldots,L
with

m_{k}=\frac{\mu B_{\hbox{tot}}-\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}T(\omega_{i})B(\omega_{i})}{B_{\hbox{tot}}-\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}B(\omega_{i})}.

If
m_{k}<0
at some time
k
, one knows that
H_{0}
is satisfied and sampling can be stopped.
One inductively defines a test supermartingale
\{M_{k}\}_{k}
by
M_{0}=1
and

M_{k}=M_{k-1}f_{k}(T_{k})\hbox{ with }f_{k}(t)=(1-c_{k})+c_{k}\cdot\frac{1-t}{1-m_{k}}

where
m_{k}
is determined as before and
c_{k}
is chosen at time
k-1
.

To conclude we sketch a way to handle
stratified sampling
.
Suppose
\Omega_{1},\ldots,\Omega_{r}
is a partition of sample space
\Omega
.
Let
p_{i}={\mathbb{P}}(\Omega_{i})>0
be known. Let
X:\Omega\to{\mathbb{R}}
be a random variable such that
X\geq 0
a.s. and
denote by
\nu_{i}
the conditional expectation
\nu_{i}={\bf E}(X\mid\Omega_{i})

so that
\nu={\bf E}(X)=\sum_{i}\nu_{i}p_{i}
.
We wish to test
H_{0}:{\bf E}(X)\leq\mu
.

Proposal:
For
m:\{1,\ldots,r\}\to(0,\infty)
, let
M_{0}^{m}=1
.
At time
k-1
, when
\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{k-1}
have been sampled and
M_{k-1}^{m}
is determined,
one decides from which stratum
\Omega_{j_{k}}
to
randomly sample
\omega_{k}
.
Also at time
k-1
one chooses
c_{k}
, such that
0\leq c_{k}<1
.
One then defines

M_{k}^{m}=M_{k-1}^{m}(1-c_{k}+c_{k}X(\omega_{k})/m(j_{k})).

Reject
H_{0}
if at some time
k

it holds that

M_{k}^{m}\geq 1/\alpha

for all
m
such that
\sum_{i}m(i)p_{i}\leq\mu
.
For
m
defined by
m(i)=\nu_{i}
,

\{M_{k}^{m}\}_{k}
is a martingale so that it exceeds
1/\alpha

with probability at most
\alpha
.
In particular, if
{\bf E}(X)=\nu\leq\mu
the probability to reject
H_{0}
is at most
\alpha
,
so that the significance level of the test is at most
\alpha
.

Suppose in each stratum
\Omega_{j}

at some time
i\leq k
,
\omega_{i}\in\Omega_{j}
has been sampled such that
c_{i}X(\omega_{i})>0
.
Then
\ell(m)=\log(M_{k}^{m})
has a minimum in the region
\sum_{i}m(i)p_{i}\leq\mu
.
Since
\ell(m)
is decreasing in each
m(i)
, the minimum lies in the region
\sum_{i}m(i)p_{i}=\mu
, which is convex.
Since
\ell(m)
is strictly convex in
m
this minimum is unique.

It remains an open question how to choose at time
k-1
stratum
j_{k}
and test parameters
c_{k}
depending on the ideas
the investigator has, for example on the conditional expectations
\nu_{i}

and the conditional variances, or how to optimize these choices based on the sample

\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{k-1}
up to time
k-1
.

4
Alternative test supermartingales

In this section we present some variants of test supermartingales based on bounds
on the moment generation function of the random variable,
rather than its positiveness.
Further elaboration of the proposals in this section can be found in
[
16
]
.

Remark 3

Consider the random variable
X
and let
Z=X-{\bf E}(X)
.
Suppose random variable
Z
is sub-Gaussian
(see
[
9
]
),
meaning that there exists
\tau>0
such that

{\bf E}(\exp(hZ))\leq\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}h^{2}\tau^{2}\right)\hbox{ for all }h\in{\mathbb{R}}.

Suppose
Z
is sub-Gaussian.
The Gauss deviation
\tau(Z)
of
Z
is the minimum
\tau
for which this inequality holds.
It follows that all moments of
Z
and
X
exist and are finite, and that
{\rm Var}(X)={\rm Var}(Z)\leq\tau(X)^{2}
.

A normal variable
X
with standard deviation
\sigma

has the property that
Z=X-{\bf E}(X)

is sub-Gaussian with Gauss deviation
\sigma
.

Hoeffding’s Lemma
[
8
, Inequality (4.16)]
implies that a random variable
X
with values in
an interval
[a,b]
has the property that
Z=X-{\bf E}(X)

is sub-Gaussian with Gauss deviation at most
\frac{1}{2}(b-a)
.

For each
h\geq 0
and
\mu
, function

f(t)=\frac{\exp(ht)}{\exp(h\mu+\frac{1}{2}h^{2}\tau^{2})}

satisfies the condition:
If
X-{\bf E}(X)
is sub-Gaussian with Gauss deviation at most
\tau
and

{\bf E}(X)\leq\mu
then

f(X)\geq 0
and
{\bf E}(f(X))\leq 1
.

Test supermartingale factor
f(t)
as defined in Remark
3
corresponds to a likelihood ratio,
namely of two normal distributions with standard deviation
\tau
:
with
\varphi(t)=(2\pi)^{-1/2}\exp(-t^{2}/2)
and
h=(\nu-\mu)/\tau^{2}
we have

f(t)=\frac{\exp(ht)}{\exp(h\mu+\frac{1}{2}h^{2}\tau^{2})}=\frac{\varphi((t-\nu)/\tau)}{\varphi((t-\mu)/\tau)}.

Its inverse is the point of departure for a likelihood ratio ratio test of the null hypothesis
that
X\sim N(\mu,\tau^{2})
versus alternative
X\sim N(\nu,\tau^{2})
.

For rv’s with values in
[0,1]
,
inspired by likelihood ratio tests for Bernoulli distributions,
one may consider for
0<\mu<1
,
0<\nu<1

g(t,\nu,\mu)=\frac{\nu^{t}(1-\nu)^{1-t}}{\mu^{t}(1-\mu)^{1-t}}=\frac{\exp(ht)}{(1-\mu)+\exp(h)\mu}\hbox{ with }h=\log\left(\frac{\nu(1-\mu)}{\mu(1-\nu)}\right).

One can show that
{\bf E}(g(X,\nu,\mu))\leq 1
if
0\leq X\leq 1
a.s. such that
({\bf E}(X)-\mu)(\nu-\mu)\leq 0
.
Here the corresponding test supermartingale can be expressed in terms of
densities of Beta distributions.
Taking
H_{0}:{\bf E}(X)\leq\mu
and
\nu>\mu
,
notice that approach (
2
) with
c=(\nu-\mu)/(1-\mu)
corresponds to affine interpolation and
improves on
g(t,\nu,\mu)
which is convex in
t
so that

\frac{\nu^{t}(1-\nu)^{1-t}}{\mu^{t}(1-\mu)^{1-t}}\leq\frac{1-\nu}{1-\mu}(1-t)+\frac{\nu}{\mu}t=(1-c)+c\frac{t}{\mu},\quad\hbox{ for }0\leq t\leq 1.

We make a side-step to a probability upper bound of Hoeffding (
[
8
]
).
We reformulate his Theorem 1, Ineq. (2.1), as follows and recast his proof
in terms of a supermartingale.

Theorem 2

Suppose
X_{1},X_{2},\ldots,X_{n}
iid sample of
X
with values in
[0,1]
and
{\bf E}(X)=\mu
,
0<\mu<1
.
Let
\overline{X}=(X_{1}+\cdots+X_{n})
and
0<\mu<\theta<1
.
Then

{\mathbb{P}}\{\overline{X}\geq\theta\}\leq\left\{\left(\frac{\theta}{\mu}\right)^{\theta}\left(\frac{1-\theta}{1-\mu}\right)^{1-\theta}\right\}^{-n}.

Proof
For
h\geq 0
, define the supermartingale
\{M_{k}(h)\}_{k}

under
H_{0}:{\bf E}(X)\leq\mu
:

M_{k}(h)=\prod_{i=1}^{k}\frac{\exp(hX_{i})}{(1-\mu)+\mu\exp(h)}=\frac{\exp(h(X_{1}+\cdots+X_{k}))}{((1-\mu)+\mu\exp(h))^{k}}.

Let
h=\log(\theta(1-\mu)/(\mu(1-\theta)))
. Then
h>0
.
The event that at time
n
we have
\overline{X}\geq\theta
equals the event that

M_{n}(h)\geq\frac{\exp(nh\theta)}{((1-\mu)+\mu\exp(h))^{n}}=\left\{\left(\frac{\theta}{\mu}\right)^{\theta}\left(\frac{1-\theta}{1-\mu}\right)^{1-\theta}\right\}^{n}.

A warning is in place, here.
A supermartingale was found to prove the validity of an upperbound of the probability of the event
\{\overline{X}\geq\theta\}
.
It is definitely not the case that
\{\sup_{h\geq 0}M_{k}(h)\}_{k}
is a test supermartingale.

Inspired by Poisson distributions we get

g(t,\nu,\mu)=\frac{\nu^{t}\exp(-\nu)}{\mu^{t}\exp(-\mu)}=\frac{\exp(ht)}{\exp(\mu(\exp(h)-1))}\hbox{ with }h=\log\left(\frac{\nu}{\mu}\right).

One can show that
{\bf E}(g(X,\nu,\mu))\leq 1
if
0\leq X\leq 1
a.s.
such that
({\bf E}(X)-\mu)(\nu-\mu)\leq 0
.
Here the corresponding test supermartingale can be expressed in terms of
densities of Gamma distributions.
See Section
6
for a testing method used in practice in statistical auditing which is said to be based on the Poisson distribution and which appears in the Audit Guide Audit Sampling
[
1
]
.

5
Behavior of the test supermartingales
\{M_{k}(c)\}_{k}

Given
\mu>0
, we discuss the behavior of the test supermartingales
\{M_{k}(c)\}_{k}

for null hypothesis
H_{0}:{\bf E}(X)\leq\mu
constructed according to
(
3
) with
c_{k}=c
,
for all
k
,
0\leq c<1
.
Let
X
be a positive integrable random variable and

an iid sample of
X
.
We have

\frac{1}{k}\log(M_{k}(c))=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\log((1-c)+c\,X_{i}/\mu).

Let
Z=X/\mu
, then
{\bf E}(Z)
exists and

Z\geq 0
a.s..
Consider the function

\lambda(c)={\bf E}(\log((1-c)+c\,Z)),\quad 0\leq c<1.

It is well defined since

\log(1-c)\leq\log((1-c)+c\,Z)\leq-c+c\,Z\leq Z.

(6)

It also follows by dominated convergence that
\lambda(c)
is continuous in
c
for
0\leq c<1
.

Theorem 3

\lambda(c)
is twice continuously differentiable and concave in
c
for
0<c<1
.

\lambda(0)=0
,
\lim_{c\downarrow 0}\lambda^{\prime}(c)=({\bf E}(X)-\mu)/\mu
.
If
{\mathbb{P}}\{X=\mu\}<1
, it is strictly concave.

Proof
Notice that

\frac{d}{dc}\log((1-c)+cz)=\frac{z-1}{(1-c)+cz};\quad\frac{d^{2}}{dc^{2}}\log((1-c)+cz)=-\frac{(z-1)^{2}}{((1-c)+cz)^{2}}.

(7)

Moreover, for
0<c_{0}<c<c_{1}<1

-\frac{1}{1-c_{1}}\leq\frac{z-1}{(1-c)+cz}\leq\frac{1}{c_{0}}.

(8)

Thus differentiation of
\lambda(c)
behaves decently
with respect to expected value in the range
c_{0}<c<c_{1}
(see
[
5
, Thm. A.5.2]
)
for all
0<c_{0}<c_{1}<1
, and we have with
Z=X/\mu
,

\displaystyle\lambda^{\prime}(c)

\displaystyle={\bf E}((Z-1)/(1-c+cZ))

\displaystyle\lambda^{\prime\prime}(c)

\displaystyle={\bf E}(-[(Z-1)/(1-c+c\,Z)]^{2})\leq 0.

By inequality (
8
) and dominated convergence
it holds that
\lambda^{\prime\prime}(c)
is continuous in
c
for
0<c<1
.
Since
-1/(1-c_{1})\leq(z-1)/((1-c)+cz)\leq z
for
z\geq 0
and
0\leq c\leq c_{1}

we obtain by dominated convergence

\displaystyle\lim_{c\downarrow 0}\lambda^{\prime}(c)

\displaystyle=\lim_{c\downarrow 0}{\bf E}\left(\frac{Z-1}{1-c+c\,Z}\right)={\bf E}\left(\lim_{c\downarrow 0}\frac{Z-1}{1-c+c\,Z}\right)={\bf E}(Z-1)=\frac{1}{\mu}({\bf E}(X)-\mu).

If
{\mathbb{P}}\{X=\mu\}={\mathbb{P}}\{Z=1\}<1
, then
\lambda^{\prime\prime}(c)<0
for
0<c<1

so that
\lambda(c)
is strictly concave in
c
.

Remark 4

From Equation (
7
) it follows that any realization
of

(based on observations of

)
is concave in

.
Thus

is log-concave in

.

Corollary 4

Suppose
{\mathbb{P}}\{X=\mu\}<1
.
If
{\bf E}(X)\leq\mu
and
0<c<1
, then
\lambda(c)<0
, so that
\lim_{n\to\infty}M_{n}(c)=0
.
If
{\bf E}(X)>\mu
, then there is
c_{\max}>0
such that for

0<c<c_{\max}
we have
\lambda(c)>0
.
If
\lambda(c)>0

then
\lim_{n\to\infty}M_{n}(c)=\infty
a.s..
If moreover
{\mathbb{P}}\{X=0\}>0
, then
c_{\rm max}<1
.

Proof
Suppose
{\bf E}(X)\leq\mu
and
{\mathbb{P}}\{X=\mu\}<1
, then
\lambda^{\prime}(0)\leq 0
and
from the strict concavity of
\lambda(c)
in
c
, it follows that
\lambda(c)<0
for all
0<c<1
.
According to the strong law of large numbers, it follows that

\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{1}{k}\log(M_{k}(c))=\lambda(c)<0

and therefore that
\lim_{k\to\infty}M_{k}(c)=0
a.s.
(despite the fact that in case

{\bf E}(M_{k})=1
for all

, cf.
[

If
{\bf E}(X)>\mu
, then
\lambda^{\prime}(0)>0
and there is
c_{\max}>0
such that
\lambda(c)>0

for
0<c<c_{\max}
.
If

we have
\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{1}{k}\log(M_{k}(c))=\lambda(c)>0

If

then

\lim_{c\uparrow 1}\lambda(c)=-\infty
.

(Consistency)

Suppose

Then there is

Performance depending on
X

In the remainder of the section we will give some tools to evaluate the performance of the test
using test supermartingales as above using an iid sample of
X
,
in case the rv
X
is known.
We are interested in the mean value of the sample size
N
needed to reject the null hypothesis.

Lemma 6

Let
Y
be an integrable random variable such that
Y\geq c
a.s. for some
c>0
.
Then the moment generating function

\phi(h)={\bf E}(\exp(h\,\log(Y)))={\bf E}(Y^{h})
is finite for all
h\leq 1
.
In particular all moments of
\log(Y)
are finite.

Proof
For
h=1
we have
\exp(h\log(Y))=Y
which is integrable, so that
\phi(1)<\infty
.
For
h\leq 0
we have
0\leq\exp(h\log(Y))\leq\exp(h\log(c))=c^{h}
so that
\exp(h\log(Y))
is integrable,
and
\phi(h)<\infty
for
h\leq 0
.
Together this implies that
\phi(h)<\infty
for all
h\leq 1
,
that
\phi(h)
is infinitely differentiable in the region
h<1
, and that the
k
-th order moment of
\log(Y)
equals
the
k
-th derivative of
\phi(h)
at
h=0
(see e.g.
[
2
, Sec. 21]
).

Let
X
be a rv with
X\geq 0
a.s. such that
{\bf E}(X)=\nu>\mu
.
Let

0<c<1
such that with

Y=(1-c)+c\,X/\mu

we have
\omega={\bf E}(\log(Y))>0
.
Let
\rho^{2}={\rm Var}(\log(Y))
which is finite because of Lemma
6
.
Given an iid sample
X_{1},X_{2},\ldots
of
X
, let
M_{n}(c)
be the test supermartingale
with
i
-th factor
Y_{i}=(1-c)+c\,X_{i}/\mu
.
For
0<\alpha<1

let

N=N_{\alpha}=\inf\{n\mid M_{n}(c)\geq 1/\alpha\}=\inf\{n\mid\log(M_{n}(c))=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log(Y_{i})\geq\log(1/\alpha)\}.

By Corollary
4
we have
N_{\alpha}<\infty
a.s..
From Theorem 2.5 in
[
3
, Ch. 2.5]
on first passage times,
it follows that for all
0<\alpha<1
we have
{\bf E}(N_{\alpha})<\infty

and
{\bf E}(N_{\alpha}^{2})<\infty
and

\lim_{\alpha\downarrow 0}\frac{{\bf E}(N_{\alpha})}{\log(1/\alpha)}=\frac{1}{\omega}\hbox{ and }\lim_{\alpha\downarrow 0}\frac{{\rm Var}(N_{\alpha})}{\log(1/\alpha)}=\frac{\rho^{2}}{\omega^{3}}.

(9)

Lindeberg’s condition (2.29)
l.c.
holds as
\{\log(Y_{i})\}_{i}
is an iid sample of square integrable rv
\{\log(Y)\}_{i}
.
Since
{\bf E}(N_{\alpha})<\infty
Wald’s equation (
[
5
, Thm. 4.1.5]
) gives the more precise expression

{\bf E}(\log(M_{N_{\alpha}}(c)))={\bf E}(\log(Y)){\bf E}(N_{\alpha})=\omega\,{\bf E}(N_{\alpha}).

(10)

Recall Lorden’s inequality (
[
11
]
) for the expected excess,
which is independent of
\alpha

{\bf E}(\log(M_{N_{\alpha}}(c)))-\log(1/\alpha)\leq\frac{{\bf E}((Z^{+})^{2})}{{\bf E}(Z)}\leq\omega+\frac{\rho^{2}}{\omega}.

With Wald’s equation (
10
) this gives

\log(1/\alpha)/\omega\leq{\bf E}(N_{\alpha})\leq\log(1/\alpha)/\omega+1+\rho^{2}/\omega^{2}.

Dependence on the mean and variance of
X

In this subsection we will consider in more detail the performance
of the test supermartingales
\{M_{n}(c)\}
for
H_{0}:{\bf E}(X)\leq\mu
, based on a sample
of
X
with
{\bf E}(X)=\theta>\mu
and finite variance
{\rm Var}(X)\leq\sigma^{2}
.
Let
X_{0}
be a 2-point distribution with values 0 and
\tau=(\theta^{2}+\sigma^{2})/\theta

and
{\mathbb{P}}\{X=\tau\}=\theta/\tau
so that
{\bf E}(X)=\theta
,
{\bf E}(X^{2})=\tau^{2}\theta/\tau=\theta^{2}+\sigma^{2}

and
{\rm Var}(X_{0})=\sigma^{2}
.
We will show that among rv’s
X
as above the test supermartingales perform worst for
X_{0}
.

Lemma 7

Let
\ell:[0,\infty)\to{\mathbb{R}}
be a three times continuous differentiable function
such that
\ell^{\prime\prime}(x)\leq 0
and

\ell^{\prime\prime\prime}(x)\geq 0
for all
x\geq 0
.
Then
{\bf E}(\ell(X))\geq{\bf E}(\ell(X_{0}))
.

Proof
Let
h(x)=u+vx+wx^{2}
be such that

h(0)=\ell(0)
,
h(\tau)=\ell(\tau)
and
h^{\prime}(\tau)=\ell^{\prime}(\tau)
then
\ell(X_{0})=h(X_{0})
and
we will show
\ell(x)\geq h(x)
for all
x\geq 0
.
Function
L(x)=\ell(x)-h(x)
satisfies the ordinary differential equation

L^{\prime\prime\prime}(x)=\ell^{\prime\prime\prime}(x)
for
x\geq 0

\frac{1}{2}\frac{u^{2}}{\tau^{2}}(x-\tau)^{2},\hbox{ if }0<u<\tau\hbox{ and }u<x\\
(\tau-u)\frac{x}{\tau}((u-x)+\frac{1}{2}\frac{x}{\tau}(\tau-u)),\hbox{ if }0<x<u<\tau\\
0,\hbox{ if }\tau<u\hbox{ and }x<u\end{cases}

, and

In particular

.

Application in audit sampling

In this section we consider the test supermartingales
(
3
) and (
4
) developed in Section

,
under the assumption that the random variable

is bounded, that is, there are
\tau_{0}<\tau_{1}
such that
\tau_{0}\leq X\leq\tau_{1}
a.s..
By transforming

to

we will restrict our attention to
null hypotheses of the form

H_{0}:{\bf E}(T)\geq\mu
under the condition that

a.s..
This is the context closest to the intended application in audit sampling.

We summarize some relevant facts of the test procedure.
Suppose that
0\leq T\leq 1
a.s. and
0\leq{\bf E}(T)=\nu<\mu<1
.
We consider test supermartingales
\{M_{n}(c)\}_{n}
for
H_{0}:{\bf E}(1-T)\leq 1-\mu
,
or equivalently for
H_{0}:{\bf E}(T)\geq\mu

Given a random sample
T_{1},T_{2},\ldots
of

, test parameter
c
such that
0\leq c<1

and time
n

M_{n}(c)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}(1-c+c(1-T_{i})/(1-\mu)).

The test starts with the choice of
c
at time 0 and and the sequential calculation of
M_{n}(c)
and leads to rejection of
H_{0}

if there is
n=N_{\alpha}
for which
M_{n}(c)\geq 1/\alpha
at which time one may stop sampling.

It follows from Theorem
8
that worst case in terms of the expectation of
N_{\alpha}
, is achieved for Bernoulli variable

T^{\nu}
with success probability
\nu
,
T^{\nu}\sim{\mathcal{B}}(\nu)
.
For this variable the optimal choice of
c
is
c_{0}=(\nu-\mu)/\mu
.
Moreover, for the original variable
T
the optimal value of

satisfies
c\geq c_{0}
.
If

satisfies
{\bf E}(\log(1-c+c(1-T)/(1-\mu)))>0

This is the case at least for

If

at which

can be rejected when using

This holds for
c=c_{0}
with
\omega={\bf E}(\log(1-c_{0}+c_{0}(1-T^{\nu})/(1-\mu)))=D_{\rm KL}({\mathcal{B}}(\nu)\|{\mathcal{B}}(\mu))>0
.

The condition at rejection time

that
M_{n}(c)\geq 1/\alpha
ensures that the
significance level of the testing procedure is

.
In the next theorem we put some bounds on the actual probability of Type I error.

Theorem 9

Let
T
be a random variable such that
0\leq T\leq 1
a.s.,
{\bf E}(T)=\mu

and

Let

In particular, if

and
\mu

Proof
When applied to a random sample of
T
, the process

is a martingale.
Let

, so that

if
M_{n}<1/\alpha
for all

.
Then

is a stopped martingale, and therefore a martingale.
Let

then (see Cor.
4

Because of dominated convergence we have

1={\bf E}(M_{0})=\lim_{k\to\infty}{\bf E}(M^{\alpha}_{k})={\bf E}(M^{\alpha}_{\infty})

and

\alpha^{-1}{\mathbb{P}}\{M^{\alpha}_{\infty}\geq\alpha^{-1}\}\leq{\bf E}(M^{\alpha}_{\infty})<\alpha^{-1}(1-c+c/(1-\mu)){\mathbb{P}}\{M^{\alpha}_{\infty}\geq\alpha^{-1}\}

7
Confidence regions

As one will have noticed we did not include a provision in our tests to avoid infinite sample size,
as could easily happen e.g. in case
H_{0}
is satisfied.
In practice it may be a more important issue to find a suitable confidence lower bound or
a confidence interval.
We will investigate ways to reuse the sample without loss of confidence if at any time one decides to switch from testing

H_{0}:{\bf E}(T)\leq\mu_{0}
to determining a confidence lower bound.
One may consult Waudby-Smith and Ramdas,
[
16
]
, for a thorough treatment of the construction of confidence
intervals using test supermartingales, especially from the perspective of large sample sizes.

Confidence lower bounds

Choose a confidence level
(1-\alpha)
with
0<\alpha<1
, for example
\alpha=0.05

We will construct an adapted process

\mu>0
we have maintained a process
\{M^{\mu}_{k}\}_{k}
, such that

is test supermartingale for the hypothesis
H_{0}:{\bf E}(T)\leq\mu
for a given integrable rv

such that
T\geq 0
a.s..

is continuous and decreasing in
\mu

for all

the family of null hypotheses
\{H_{0}:{\bf E}(T)\leq\mu\}_{\mu}

is convex in

We give some examples:

\pi
is a probability density on
[0,1]

(determined independently of the sample)
the family of integrated test supermartingales

\{M_{k}^{\mu}(\pi)\}_{k}
is a convex decreasing family, where

M_{k}^{\mu}(\pi)=\int_{0}^{1}M_{k}^{\mu}(c)\pi(c)dc\hbox{ with }M_{k}^{\mu}(c)=\prod_{i=1}^{k}((1-c)+c\,T_{i}/\mu)

(13)

Families of this form will be called
integrated decreasing family.

If
0\leq T\leq 1
a.s., the following (non convex) decreasing families of test supermartingales
for
0<\Delta<1
may also be useful in finding a confidence lower bound
greater than
{\bf E}(T)-\Delta
, cf. Theorem
8
.

M_{k}^{\mu}(\Delta)=\prod_{i=1}^{k}\left((1-\frac{\Delta}{1-\mu})+\frac{\Delta}{1-\mu}\,\frac{T_{i}}{\mu}\right),\hbox{ for }0\leq\mu\leq 1-\Delta.

(14)

Suppose
\{M^{\mu}_{k}\}_{k,\mu}
is a decreasing family.
Let
B^{l}_{k}
be the statistic

B^{l}_{k}=-\infty\hbox{ if }M^{\mu}_{k}<1/\alpha\hbox{ for all }\mu>0,\hbox{ and }B^{l}_{k}=\sup\{\mu\mid M^{\mu}_{k}\geq 1/\alpha\}\hbox{ otherwise. }

If
B^{l}_{k}=\mu^{-}\geq 0
, then it follows from continuity that
M_{k}^{\mu^{-}}\geq 1/\alpha
.
Notice that for
\mu\geq 0
we have the equivalence
\mu\leq B_{k}^{l}\Leftrightarrow M^{\mu}_{k}\geq 1/\alpha
.
In particular, if
{\bf E}(T)=\mu
,
then

{\mathbb{P}}\{\exists k:\mu\leq B^{l}_{k}\}={\mathbb{P}}\{\exists{k}:M^{\mu}_{k}\geq 1/\alpha\}\leq\alpha
,
so that
{\mathbb{P}}\{\forall k:\mu>B^{l}_{k}\}\geq 1-\alpha
.

Theorem 10

Suppose
\{M^{\mu}_{k}\}_{k,\mu}
is a decreasing family of test supermartingales for the null hypotheses

H_{0}:{\bf E}(T)\leq\mu
, where
T\geq 0
a.s..

Then the statistics
B^{l}_{k}
satisfy

{\mathbb{P}}\{\forall k:{\bf E}(T)>B^{l}_{k}\}\geq 1-\alpha
.

Thus, at time
k
,
\max\{B^{l}_{j}\mid j\leq k\}
is a
(1-\alpha)
-confidence lower bound.
Moreover, if its value at time
k
is not convenient, one may continue sampling
in the hope to find
a better lower bound without losing confidence.

Also, if initially the investigation of
T
started off by testing
H_{0}:{\bf E}(T)\leq\mu_{0}
for
a fixed
\mu_{0}
at significance level
\alpha
using some test supermartingale
\{M_{k}\}_{k}

(e.g. as in (
3
) or (
4
))
then at any time
k
one may change one’s mind
and
reuse the sample
to construct a
(1-\alpha)
-confidence lower bound by means of a decreasing family
\{M_{k}^{\mu}\}_{k,\mu}
provided
M_{i}^{\mu_{0}}=M_{i}
for
i\leq k

(equality as random variables).
The reason is that under these conditions rejection of
H_{0}
at significance level
\alpha

is equivalent to finding a
(1-\alpha)
-confidence lower bound that is at most

\mu_{0}
.

We present the following example using
\{M_{k}^{\mu}(1)\}_{k,\mu}

as defined in (
13
):

Remark 8

Suppose
T\sim{\mathcal{B}}(\nu)
, let
T_{1},T_{2},\ldots
be an iid sample of
T

and define
S_{k}=T_{1}+\cdots+T_{k}
.

Consider the test supermartingales
\{M_{k}^{\mu}(1)\}_{k,\mu}

constructed with the factors
f(T_{i})=T_{i}/\mu
.
Then
M_{k}^{\mu}=(1/\mu)^{k}
if
S_{k}=k
,
M_{k}^{\mu}=0
if
S_{k}<k
.
The corresponding
(1-\alpha)
-confidence lower bound is

B^{l}_{k}=\alpha^{1/k}
if
S_{k}=k
and otherwise
M_{k}^{\mu}=0
for all
\mu

so that

B^{l}_{k}=-\infty
.
Let
K
be the stopping time defined by
K=k
if
T_{i}=1
for
i<k
and
T_{k}=0
.
Then
\mu^{l}=\max_{k}B^{l}_{k}=B^{l}_{K-1}=\alpha^{1/(K-1)}

(or
-\infty
if
K=1
) is a
(1-\alpha)
-confidence lower bound.

Consider a convex decreasing family
\{M^{\mu}_{k}\}_{k,\mu}
as defined by equation
(
12
) of Remark
7
.
Suppose for given
k
one has observed
T_{1}=t_{1},\ldots,T_{k}=t_{k}
.
Since
\log
is a concave function, Jensen’s inequality implies that for

m=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}((1-c_{i})+c_{i}\,t_{i}/\mu)
we have
\frac{1}{k}\log(M^{\mu}_{k})\leq\log(m)
.
If there is
i\leq k
such that
c_{i}t_{i}>0
we may solve equality
m=1
for
\mu
and get

M^{\mu}_{k}\leq 1\hbox{ if }\mu=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{k}c_{i}t_{i}}{\sum_{i=1}^{k}c_{i}}.

It follows that for integrated decreasing families

\{M_{k}^{\mu}\}_{k,\mu}
as defined by equation (
13
) in Remark
7

we have
M^{\mu}_{k}(c)\leq 1
and
M^{\mu}_{k}(\pi)\leq 1
for
\mu=\overline{t}_{k}=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}t_{i}
.

Remark 9

Let
\{M^{\mu}_{k}(c)\}_{k,\mu}
be decreasing family of the form (
12
) with test parameters
c_{k-1}=c
for all
k
.
If for some
c
we have
M^{\mu}_{k}(c)>1
, then

\mu<\overline{t}_{k}
.

It follows that for an integrated decreasing family
\{M_{k}^{\mu}(\pi)\}_{k,\mu}

of the form (
13
) also
M_{k}^{\mu}(\pi)>1

implies that
\mu<\overline{t}_{k}
.

In particular for decreasing families
\{M_{k}^{\mu}(c)\}_{k,\mu}
and
\{M_{k}^{\mu}(\pi)\}_{k,\mu}

the confidence lower bounds satisfy
B^{l}_{k}<\overline{t}_{k}
for all
k
.

If one decides to stop sampling at time
k
depending on the combination of

\overline{t}_{k}
and
B_{k}^{l}
, one should realize that
it is quite possible that the sample mean
\overline{t}_{k}
as an estimator of
{\bf E}(T)

is
biased
.
Of course, if the stopping time
k
does not depend on the sampling history,
the sample mean is unbiased estimator of
{\bf E}(T)
.
Anyway, the behavior of
(\overline{T}_{k}-{\bf E}(T))
for large sample sizes is described by the Law of the Iterated Logarithm.

For rv’s
T
which are bounded from above, say
T\leq 1
a.s.

(1-\alpha)
-
confidence upper bounds

can be constructed as follows:
Given a decreasing family
\{M_{k}^{\mu}\}_{k,\mu}
,
for example modelled after (
12
) or (
13
),
one may consider
\hat{M}_{k}^{\mu}=M_{k}^{1-\mu}
applied to a sample
1-T_{1},1-T_{2},\ldots

of
1-T
.
Then
\{\hat{M}_{k}^{\mu}\}_{k}
is a test supermartingale for
H_{0}:{\bf E}(1-T)\leq 1-\mu
,
equivalent to
H_{0}:{\bf E}(T)\geq\mu
.
Furthermore
\hat{M}_{k}^{\mu}
is increasing in
\mu
for all
k
.
We may refer to the family
\{\hat{M}_{k}^{\mu}\}_{k,\mu}
as a
increasing
family.
It is convex if
\mu\mapsto\hat{M}_{k}^{\mu}
is a convex function for all
k
and all
realizations of the sample of
T
.
Let
B_{k}^{l}
be an
(1-\alpha)
-confidence lower bound for
{\bf E}(1-T)
,
then
B_{k}^{u}=1-B_{k}^{l}
is a
(1-\alpha)
-confidence upper bound for
{\bf E}(T)
.
It satisfies

B_{k}^{u}=\infty\hbox{ if }\hat{M}^{\mu}_{k}<1/\alpha\hbox{ for all }\mu>0,\hbox{ and }B_{k}^{u}=\inf\{\mu\mid\hat{M}^{\mu}_{k}\geq 1/\alpha\}\hbox{ otherwise. }

Confidence intervals

It is natural to associate confidence
intervals
(rather than regions) for

{\bf E}(T)
with a family of
tests of the hypotheses
H_{0}:{\bf E}(T)=\mu
, where
\mu\in{\mathbb{R}}
.
A desirable property of the family of tests then is that
if
H_{0}:{\bf E}(T)=\mu
cannot be rejected for
\mu=\mu_{1}
and
\mu=\mu_{2}
,
it cannot be rejected for all
\mu
between
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
.
We will produce a family of test supermartingales
designed to produce confidence intervals for
{\bf E}(T)
.
As we will restrict to bounded rv’s
T
, we will assume that
0\leq T\leq 1
a.s..

Theorem 11

Suppose
T_{1},T_{2},...
is an iid sample of
T

and let
\{M_{k}^{\mu,-}\}_{k,\mu}
and
\{M_{k}^{\mu,+}\}_{k,\mu}

be a convex decreasing resp. convex increasing family of test supermartingales
for the null hypotheses
\{H_{0}:{\bf E}(T)\leq\mu\}_{\mu}
, resp.
\{H_{0}:{\bf E}(T)\geq\mu\}_{\mu}
.
Let
p^{-},p^{+}\geq 0
,
p^{-}+p^{+}=1
, and
M_{k}^{\mu}=p^{-}M_{k}^{\mu,-}+p^{+}M_{k}^{\mu,+}
.

For all
0<\mu<1
the process
\{M_{k}^{\mu}\}_{k}
is a test supermartigale for
H_{0}:{\bf E}(T)=\mu
.
Any realization of

\mu\mapsto M_{k}^{\mu}
is a convex function.
Let
0<\alpha<1
.
The region

R=\{\mu\mid\forall k:M_{k}^{\mu}<1/\alpha\}
is a
(1-\alpha)
-confidence interval.
Thus, for any
k
,
R_{k}=\{\mu\mid\forall n\leq k:M_{n}^{\mu}<1/\alpha\}

as well as
R^{\prime}_{k}=\{\mu\mid M_{k}^{\mu}<1/\alpha\}

are
(1-\alpha)
-confidence intervals.
When for the decreasing and increasing family, families of the form
(
13
)
with test measures
\pi^{-}
, resp.
\pi^{+}
are used,
confidence interval
R^{\prime}_{k}
contains the
k
-th sample average
\overline{t}_{k}
.

Proof
First of all, suppose
{\bf E}(T)=\nu
.
Then
\{M^{\nu}_{n}(\pi)\}_{n}
is a supermartingale, so

{\mathbb{P}}\{\nu\in R\}={\mathbb{P}}\{\forall n:M^{\nu}_{n}(\pi)<1/\alpha\}\geq 1-\alpha
.
This means that
R
is a
(1-\alpha)
-confidence region.
Since a convex combination of convex functions is convex,
\mu\mapsto M_{n}^{\mu}
is a convex function.
Thus for each
k
the set

\{\mu\mid M_{k}^{\mu}<1/\alpha\}
is an interval, as well as their intersection

\{\mu\mid\forall k:M_{k}^{\mu}<1/\alpha\}
.
According to Remark
9
, for
\mu=\overline{t}_{k}
we have
M_{n}^{\mu,+}\leq 1

and
M_{n}^{\mu,-}\leq 1
if
\{M_{k}^{\mu,-}\}_{k,\mu}
and
\{M_{k}^{\mu,+}\}_{k,\mu}

are of the form (
13
) with test measures
\pi^{-}
resp
\pi^{+}

so that also
M^{\mu}_{n}\leq 1
.

Notice that if one set off the investigation of
T
by looking for a suitable lower bound
but at some time discovered that also a suitable upper bound is necessary,
it is not safe to switch to the above method and reuse the data!
On the other hand, if one is interested in a
(1-\alpha^{-})
-confidence lower bound
using some decreasing family of test supermartingales for the family of null hypotheses

H_{0}:{\bf E}(T)\leq\mu
,
one could as well have maintained at the same time an increasing family of test supermartingales for the null hypotheses

H_{0}:{\bf E}(T)\geq\mu
, for constructing

(1-\alpha^{+})
-confidence upper bounds.
We may construct
(1-\alpha^{-}-\alpha^{+})
-confidence intervals as follows:

Theorem 12

Suppose
T_{1},T_{2},...
is an iid sample of
T

and let
\{M_{k}^{\mu,-}\}_{k,\mu}
and
\{M_{k}^{\mu,+}\}_{k,\mu}

be a decreasing resp. increasing family of test supermartingales
for the family of null hypotheses
\{H_{0}:{\bf E}(T)\leq\mu\}_{\mu}
, resp.
\{H_{0}:{\bf E}(T)\geq\mu\}_{\mu}
.
Let
\alpha^{+}>0
and
\alpha^{-}>0
.
Let
B_{k}^{l}
be the
(1-\alpha^{-})
-confidence lower bounds based on

\{M_{k}^{\mu,-}\}_{k,\mu}
, and
B_{k}^{u}
be the
(1-\alpha^{+})
-confidence upper bounds based on

\{M_{k}^{\mu,+}\}_{k,\mu}
.
Then the intervals
(\max_{i\leq k}B_{i}^{l},\min_{i\leq k}B_{i}^{u})
are
(1-\alpha^{+}-\alpha^{-})
-confidence intervals.
If one used the decreasing and increasing familiy of the form (
13
) it holds that

B_{k}^{l}<\overline{t}_{k}<B_{k}^{u}
for all
k
.

It is an unpleasant feature of the above procedures
that it may happen (of course with probability at most
\alpha
, resp.
\alpha^{-}+\alpha^{+}
)
that the constructed confidence interval ends up empty.
If one would like to avoid weird conclusions, one could stick to one of the
confidence intervals
R_{k}
that is not empty or to
R^{\prime}_{k}
for some
k
.
