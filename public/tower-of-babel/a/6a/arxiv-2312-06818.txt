Abstract
. We show that orientations and Floer gradings for elliptic differential operators can be propagated through bordisms. This is based on a new perspective on APS indices for elliptic boundary value problems over the real numbers. Several applications to moduli spaces of this new bordism-theoretic point of view will be given in the sequel.

1.
Introduction

In the study of moduli spaces of solutions to elliptic differential equations, orientations and Floer gradings are an important ingredient for the construction of enumerative invariants
[
9
,
10
,
8
,
11
,
22
]
. Examples include moduli spaces of instantons or moduli spaces of calibrated submanifolds. Constructing orientations is often a challenging problem due to the lack of a theoretical framework. This paper and the sequels
[
14
,
15
]
develop a new such framework based on bordism theory.

The present paper develops the analytic foundation of this new bordism-theoretic perspective and exhibits a new relationship between ‘categorical indices’ and certain elliptic boundary conditions. Recall that the bordism-invariance of the index in
KO
-theory for real Dirac operators is a consequence of the Atiyah–Singer Index Theorem
[
4
,
5
]
. The main result of this paper, Theorem
3.1
, can be viewed as a higher-categorical rendition of this classical result.

Motivated by the properties of the real Dirac operator in dimension
\ell
we introduce the notion of an ‘
\ell
-adapted’ operator; in general,
\ell
may be different from the dimension. Problems involving twisted Dirac operators often give
\ell
-adapted operators. We then construct the
orientation torsor

\mathrm{O}_{\ell}(D_{X})
of an ‘
\ell
-adapted’ elliptic differential operator
D_{X}
on a compact manifold without boundary, which encodes orientations of
\Ker(D_{X}),

\Coker(D_{X}),
and Floer gradings of the spectrum of
D_{X}.
The orientation torsor is a kind of categorification of the classical index. Our main result shows that every

(\ell+1)
-adapted elliptic differential operator
D_{W}
on a bordism
W
from
(X_{0},D_{X_{0}})
to
(X_{1},D_{X_{1}})
induces a canonical isomorphism
\mathrm{O}_{\ell}[D_{W}]\colon\mathrm{O}_{\ell}(D_{X_{0}})\to\mathrm{O}_{\ell}(D_{X_{1}})
of orientation torsors. In this sense a
categorical symmetry
for orientation torsors is established, where the symmetry is described not by a group, but by a category (the elliptic bordism category
{\mathfrak{Bord}}{}_{n}^{\operatorname{Ell}_{\ell}}
defined below). For example, from our point of view the action of the gauge group on the orientations on moduli spaces of connections as in Donaldson–Kronheimer
[
8
]
becomes the isomorphism induced by a cylinder bordism, where we use the gauge transformation to define the boundary identifications. As will be shown in Joyce–Upmeier
[
14
,
15
]
our method is very powerful for constructing orientations because it allows the passage from a manifold to a simpler, bordant manifold. We will construct coherent orientations for DT4-invariants of Calabi–Yau 4-folds, thereby settling (affirmatively) an important open question for Donaldson–Thomas type invariants
[
10
]
. In dimensions 7 and 8, we will also give new applications to orientations on the moduli space of Cayley submanifolds and to Floer gradings on the moduli space of
G_{2}
-instantons.

The main idea behind the categorical symmetry is a new approach concerning index theory of real elliptic boundary value problems. The classical papers (Atiyah–Patodi–Singer
[
3
]
and Lockhart–McOwen
[
17
]
) construct indices of elliptic boundary value problems with prescribed decay rates near the boundary, leading to an index that depends on the choice of decay rate. From our perspective on APS indices for elliptic boundary value problems, the orientation torsor allows us to construct an invariantly defined APS index that is independent of choices. We introduce the concept of a ‘nearly APS boundary condition’ and show that an orientation on the boundary fixes an elliptic boundary condition up to deformation.

Thus, the index of an elliptic differential operator on a manifold
with boundary
should be regarded not as an element of an abelian group (a ‘number’), but rather as an element of a
torsor
constructed from the induced differential operator
on
the boundary. From this perspective a choice of boundary condition determines a trivialization of the torsor and changes of trivialization agree with the change in index when passing to a different boundary condition.

2.
The elliptic bordism category

This section introduces a variant of the idea of a bordism category, where tangential structures are replaced by ‘adapted’ elliptic differential operators, defined below. The key point is to specify a standard ‘cylindrical’ form of the adapted differential operator over a collar neighborhood of the boundary of the bordism.

We also define here the orientation bundle
\mathrm{O}_{\ell}(\mathcal{D}_{X})
of a family of
\ell
-adapted elliptic differential operators on a compact manifold without boundary.

These definitions are used in the statements of our main results in §

Definition 2.3
.

Let

4.
Categorical index theory and boundary conditions

Differential operators on manifolds with boundary are not Fredholm operators unless suitable boundary conditions are imposed. Usually there is no canonical choice of boundary condition, even up to deformation. In this section we will show that the orientation torsor
\mathrm{O}_{\ell}(\mathcal{D}_{X}),
a kind of categorification of the usual index, parameterizes deformation classes of ‘nearly APS’ boundary conditions. This will then be used to prove Theorem
3.2
.

4.1.
Background on boundary conditions

The framework of elliptic boundary conditions from Ballmann–Bär
[
6
]
proves Fredholm results for a large class of boundary conditions. After reviewing these briefly, we prove some additional elementary results, Propositions
4.2
and
4.3
.

Let
Y
be a Riemannian manifold with boundary
\partial Y\cong X
. Let
F_{0},F_{1}\to Y
be vector bundles over
\mathbb{K}
with metrics on the fibers, and let
D_{Y}\colon\Gamma^{\infty}(F_{0})\to\Gamma^{\infty}(F_{1})
be a
\mathbb{K}
-linear elliptic differential operator on
Y.
Suppose that over a collar neighborhood
\jmath_{X}\colon X\times[0,T)\to Y
of the boundary the operator
D_{Y}
has the form

(4.1)

\jmath_{X}^{*}(D_{Y})=\sigma\bigl(\partial/\partial t+A_{X}\bigr),

where
\sigma\colon F_{0}|_{X}\overset{\smash{\cong}}{\longrightarrow}F_{1}|_{X}
is orthogonal and
A_{X}\colon\Gamma^{\infty}(F_{0}|_{X})\to\Gamma^{\infty}(F_{0}|_{X})
is a self-adjoint elliptic differential operator on
X.
Note that
\partial/\partial t
points
inward
and
\sigma_{{\rm d}t}(D_{Y})=\sigma
is the principal symbol. Since
X
is compact without boundary, standard elliptic theory implies that
A_{X}
has a discrete spectrum consisting entirely of eigenvalues Lawson–Michelsohn
[
16
]
*Ch. III, §5.

An
elliptic boundary condition
for
D_{Y}
is a subspace
B\subset\Gamma_{L^{2}}(F_{0}|_{X})
of a certain special form as defined in
[
6
]
*Def. 7.5.

Let
\mathcal{R}\colon\Gamma^{\infty}(F_{0})\to\Gamma^{\infty}(F_{0}|_{X})
be the restriction to the boundary.

Theorem 4.1

(see
[
6
]
*Cor. 8.6)
.

If
D_{Y}
is an elliptic differential operator on a compact Riemannian manifold with boundary, of the form (
4.1
) over a collar, and if
B
is an elliptic boundary condition for
D_{Y},
then

(4.2)

\operatorname{dom}(D_{Y,B})=\left\{\,\psi\in\Gamma^{\infty}(F_{0})\;\middle|\;\mathcal{R}(\psi)\in B\,\right\}\xrightarrow{D_{Y,B}}\Gamma^{\infty}(F_{1})

is an unbounded Fredholm operator. The Fredholm index of (
4.2
) can be expressed in terms of the adjoint boundary condition
B^{\mathrm{adj}}=\sigma(B)^{\perp}
as

(4.3)

\mathop{\rm ind}\nolimits D_{Y,B}=\dim_{\mathbb{K}}\bigl(\Ker D_{Y,B}\bigr)-\dim_{\mathbb{K}}\bigl(\Ker D_{Y,B^{\mathrm{adj}}}^{*}\bigr).

For example, for every
\delta\in\mathbb{R}
we have the
APS boundary condition

B_{\mathrm{APS}}(\delta)=\operatorname{Eig}_{(-\infty,\delta)}(A_{X}).

APS boundary conditions are equivalent to exponential decay conditions on the extended manifold
\widehat{Y}=Y\amalg_{X}[0,\infty)
with non-critical decay rate as in Lockhart–McOwen
[
17
]
.

To turn a formally self-adjoint or skew-adjoint elliptic differential operator on a manifold with boundary into a self-adjoint or skew-adjoint Fredholm operator (
4.2
), it is important to work with self-adjoint boundary conditions
B^{\mathrm{adj}}=B.

Decompose
Y
as
Y^{b}\amalg Y^{c}
where every connected component of
Y^{b}
has a nonempty boundary and
Y^{c}
is a closed manifold with
\partial Y^{c}=\emptyset.
Hence
X=\partial Y=\partial Y^{b}.
Decompose also
D_{Y}=D_{Y^{b}}\oplus D_{Y^{c}},
where
D_{Y^{b}}\colon\Gamma^{\infty}(F_{0}|_{Y^{b}})\to\Gamma^{\infty}(F_{1}|_{Y^{b}})
and
D_{Y^{c}}\colon\Gamma^{\infty}(F_{0}|_{Y^{c}})\to\Gamma^{\infty}(F_{1}|_{Y^{c}}),
where
\Gamma^{\infty}(F_{j})
for
j=0,1
is a direct sum of
\Gamma^{\infty}(F_{j}|_{Y^{c}})
and
\Gamma^{\infty}(F_{j}|_{Y^{b}}),
viewed as subsets of
\Gamma^{\infty}(F_{j})
by the extension of sections by zero. By the Unique Continuation Theorem of Aronszajn
[
1
]
, the restriction of
\mathcal{R}
to
\Ker D_{Y^{b}}\subset\Gamma^{\infty}(F_{0}|_{Y^{b}})
is injective.

Proposition 4.2
.

\Ker D_{Y,B_{\mathrm{APS}}(-\delta)}=\Ker D_{Y^{c}}
for all sufficiently large
\delta.

Proof.

Firstly, if
\varepsilon>\delta,
then
\Ker D_{Y,B_{\mathrm{APS}}(-\varepsilon)}\subset\Ker D_{Y,B_{\mathrm{APS}}(-\delta)}.
Secondly, we have
\bigcap_{\delta\in\mathbb{R}}\Ker D_{Y,B_{\mathrm{APS}}(-\delta)}=\Ker D_{Y^{c}}
because if
\psi\in\Ker D_{Y}
satisfies
\mathcal{R}(\psi)\in B_{\mathrm{APS}}(-\delta)\allowbreak=\operatorname{Eig}_{(-\infty,\delta)}(A_{X})
for all
\delta\in\mathbb{R},
then
\mathcal{R}(\psi)=0
and thus
\psi|_{Y^{b}}=0
by the Unique Continuation Theorem. Of course,
\Ker D_{Y^{c}}\subset\Ker D_{Y,B_{\mathrm{APS}}(-\delta)}
for all
\delta
and the previous two facts imply that the dimensions of these subspaces are the same for
\delta
sufficiently large, so these subspaces are in fact equal.
∎

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
B_{1},

B_{2}
be elliptic boundary conditions for
D_{Y}
with
B_{1}\subset B_{2}
and
\dim_{\mathbb{K}}B_{1}^{\perp}\cap B_{2}<\infty.
Let
\pi\colon\Gamma_{L^{2}}(F_{0}|_{X})\to B_{1}^{\perp}\cap B_{2}
be the orthogonal projection. The maps
\pi\mathcal{R}|_{\Ker D_{Y,B_{2}}},

\pi\sigma^{*}\mathcal{R}|_{\Ker D_{Y,B_{1}^{\mathrm{adj}}}^{*}}
factor through embeddings

(4.4)

\displaystyle\Ker D_{Y,B_{2}}/\Ker D_{Y,B_{1}}

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{\pi\mathcal{R}}B_{1}^{\perp}\cap B_{2},

(4.5)

\displaystyle\Ker D_{Y,B_{1}^{\mathrm{adj}}}^{*}/\Ker D_{Y,B_{2}^{\mathrm{adj}}}^{*}

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{\pi\sigma^{*}\mathcal{R}}B_{1}^{\perp}\cap B_{2},

whose images are orthogonal complements of each other. In particular,

(4.6)

\displaystyle B_{1}^{\perp}\cap B_{2}={}

\displaystyle\pi\mathcal{R}\Bigl(\Ker D_{Y,B_{2}}/\Ker D_{Y,B_{1}}\Bigr)

\displaystyle\oplus\pi\sigma^{*}\mathcal{R}\Bigl(\Ker D_{Y,B_{1}^{\mathrm{adj}}}^{*}/\Ker D_{Y,B_{2}^{\mathrm{adj}}}^{*}\Bigr).

Proof.

From
B_{1}\subset B_{2}
we have
B_{2}=B_{1}\oplus(B_{1}^{\perp}\cap B_{2}).
Suppose
\pi\mathcal{R}(\psi)=0
with
\psi\in\Ker D_{Y,B_{2}}.
Then
D_{Y}(\psi)=0,

\mathcal{R}(\psi)\in B_{2},
and
\mathcal{R}(\psi)\perp(B_{1}^{\perp}\cap B_{2}).
Hence
\mathcal{R}(\psi)\in B_{1}
and so
\psi\in\Ker D_{Y,B_{1}}.
This shows that
\pi\mathcal{R}|_{\Ker D_{Y,B_{2}}}
factors through an embedding (
4.4
); the proof for (
4.5
) is similar.

Let
\psi_{1}\in\Ker D_{Y,B_{1}^{\mathrm{adj}}}^{*},

\psi_{2}\in\Ker D_{Y,B_{2}}.
Then
D_{Y}^{*}(\psi_{1})=0,

\sigma^{*}\mathcal{R}(\psi_{1})\in B_{1}^{\perp},

D_{Y}(\psi_{2})=0,
and
\mathcal{R}(\psi_{2})\in B_{2}.
By Green’s formula
[
6
]
*Lem. 2.6 we have

0=\int_{Y}\bigl\langle D_{Y}^{*}(\psi_{1}),\psi_{2}\bigr\rangle+\int_{Y}\bigl\langle\psi_{1},D_{Y}(\psi_{2})\bigr\rangle=\int_{X}\bigl\langle\mathcal{R}(\psi_{1}),\sigma(\mathcal{R}(\psi_{2}))\bigr\rangle.

Thus
\langle\sigma^{*}\mathcal{R}(\psi_{1}),\mathcal{R}(\psi_{2})\rangle_{L^{2}}=0
and since
\sigma^{*}\mathcal{R}(\psi_{1})\in B_{1},

\mathcal{R}(\psi_{2})\in B_{2}^{\perp}
the inner product can also be evaluated after taking the orthogonal projection,

\bigl\langle\pi\sigma^{*}\mathcal{R}(\psi_{1}),\pi\mathcal{R}(\psi_{2})\bigr\rangle_{B_{1}^{\perp}\cap B_{2}}=\bigl\langle\sigma^{*}\mathcal{R}(\psi_{1}),\mathcal{R}(\psi_{2})\bigr\rangle_{L^{2}}=0.

It follows that the images of the embeddings (
4.4
), (
4.5
) are perpendicular.

For elliptic boundary conditions
B_{1}\subset B_{2},

[
6
]
*Cor. 8.8 shows that
B_{2}/B_{1}
is finite-dimensional and

(4.7)

\dim_{\mathbb{K}}B_{2}/B_{1}=\mathop{\rm ind}\nolimits D_{Y,B_{2}}-\mathop{\rm ind}\nolimits D_{Y,B_{1}}.

Now (
4.3
) and (
4.7
) show that the dimension of the sum of the images of the embeddings (
4.4
), (
4.5
) agrees with the dimension of the ambient space
B_{1}^{\perp}\cap B_{2},
so the images are indeed complementary subspaces.
∎

4.2.
Cauchy data and nearly APS boundary conditions

This section introduces the concept of a nearly APS boundary condition.

Definition 4.4
.

The subspace of
Cauchy data
for the operator
D_{Y}
is

C(D_{Y})=\mathcal{R}(\Ker D_{Y})=\left\{\phi\in\Gamma^{\infty}(F_{0}|_{X})\;\middle|\;\exists\psi\in\Ker D_{Y}:\mathcal{R}(\psi)=\phi\right\}.

If
B
is an elliptic boundary condition, then
\mathcal{R}
restricts to an isomorphism

(4.10)

\mathcal{R}|_{\Ker D_{Y^{b},B}}\colon\Ker D_{Y^{b},B}\overset{\cong}{\longrightarrow}C(D_{Y})\cap B.

In particular, Theorem
4.1
implies that
C(D_{Y})\cap B
is finite-dimensional. It is sometimes possible to replace this intersection of infinite-dimensional subspaces by an intersection inside a finite-dimensional space.

Definition 4.5
.

A boundary condition is
nearly APS
if it can be written as
B=B_{\mathrm{APS}}(-\delta)\oplus L_{\delta}
for some
\delta\geqslant 0
and a finite-dimensional subspace
L_{\delta}\subset\operatorname{Eig}_{[-\delta,\delta]}(A_{X}).
For all
0\leqslant\delta<\varepsilon
it is then again possible to write
B=B_{\mathrm{APS}}(-\varepsilon)\oplus L_{\varepsilon}
with
L_{\varepsilon}=\operatorname{Eig}_{[-\varepsilon,-\delta)}(A_{X})\oplus L_{\delta}.

Definition 4.6
.

Let
\pi_{\delta}
be the orthogonal projection of
\Gamma_{L^{2}}(F_{0}|_{X})
onto the closed subspace
\operatorname{Eig}_{[-\delta,\delta]}(A_{X}).
The space of
\delta
-projected Cauchy data
for
D_{Y}
is

C_{\delta}(D_{Y})=\left\{\varphi\in\operatorname{Eig}_{[-\delta,\delta]}(A_{X})\;\middle|\;\exists\psi\in\Ker D_{Y}:\mathcal{R}(\psi)-\varphi\in B_{\mathrm{APS}}(-\delta)\right\}.

Proposition 4.7
.

Let
B
be a nearly APS boundary condition, written as
B=B_{\mathrm{APS}}(-\delta)\oplus L_{\delta}
for large
\delta\geqslant 0.
Then for all sufficiently large
\delta\geqslant 0
the map
\pi_{\delta}\circ\mathcal{R}\colon\Gamma^{\infty}(F_{0})\to\operatorname{Eig}_{[-\delta,\delta]}(A_{X})
restricts to an embedding of
\Ker D_{Y^{b},B}
into
\operatorname{Eig}_{[-\delta,\delta]}(A_{X}).
For all
\delta\geqslant 0
the image is
\pi_{\delta}(C(D_{Y})\cap B)=C_{\delta}(D_{Y})\cap L_{\delta}.

Proof.

The kernel of
\pi_{\delta}\mathcal{R}|_{\Ker D_{Y^{b},B}}
is the set of all
\psi\in\Gamma^{\infty}(F_{0}|_{Y^{b}})
satisfying
D_{Y}(\psi)=0,

\mathcal{R}(\psi)\in B,
and
\mathcal{R}(\psi)\in\operatorname{Eig}_{(-\infty,-\delta)\cup(\delta,+\infty)}(A_{X}).
For large
\delta
we have
B=B_{\mathrm{APS}}(-\delta)\oplus L_{\delta}
and thus
B\cap\operatorname{Eig}_{(-\infty,-\delta)\cup(\delta,+\infty)}(A_{X})=B_{\mathrm{APS}}(-\delta).
By Proposition
4.2
,
B_{\mathrm{APS}}(-\delta)\cap\Ker D_{Y^{b},B}=\{0\}
for all sufficiently large
\delta,
hence
\pi_{\delta}\mathcal{R}|_{\Ker D_{Y^{b},B}}
is injective for large
\delta,
with image
\pi_{\delta}(C(D_{Y})\cap B),
see (
4.10
).

Next, we identify the image. Clearly
\pi_{\delta}(C(D_{Y})\cap B)\subset\pi_{\delta}(C(D_{Y}))\cap\pi_{\delta}(B)\subset C_{\delta}(D_{Y})\cap L_{\delta}.
Conversely, if
\varphi\in C_{\delta}(D_{Y})\cap L_{\delta}
then there is
\psi\in\Ker D_{Y}
such that
\mathcal{R}(\psi)-\varphi\in B_{\mathrm{APS}}(-\delta).
Therefore
\mathcal{R}(\psi)\in\varphi+B_{\mathrm{APS}}(-\delta)\subset L_{\delta}\oplus B_{\mathrm{APS}}(-\delta)=B
and hence
\mathcal{R}(\psi)\in C(D_{Y})\cap B.
Moreover,
\pi_{\delta}\mathcal{R}(\psi)=\pi_{\delta}(\varphi+B_{\mathrm{APS}}(-\delta))=\varphi,
thus showing
\varphi\in\pi_{\delta}(C(D_{Y})\cap B).

∎

If follows that for a nearly APS boundary condition and sufficiently large
\delta
we may equivalently take the intersection
C(D_{Y})\cap B
in the
finite-dimensional
space
\operatorname{Eig}_{[-\delta,\delta]}(A_{X})
in the sense that of an isomorphism
\pi_{\delta}\colon C(D_{Y})\cap B\to C_{\delta}(D_{Y})\cap L_{\delta}.

Proposition 4.8
.

If
D_{Y}
is formally self-adjoint or formally skew-adjoint, then there is an orthogonal decomposition
\operatorname{Eig}_{[-\delta,\delta]}(A_{X})=C_{\delta}(D_{Y})\oplus\sigma(C_{\delta}(D_{Y})).

Proof.

Putting the condition
D_{Y}^{*}=\pm D_{Y}
into (
4.1
) shows
\sigma^{*}=\sigma^{-1}=\pm\sigma
and
A_{X}\sigma^{*}=\pm\sigma A_{X},
hence
A_{X}
and
\sigma
anti-commute in both cases. In particular,
\sigma
preserves
\operatorname{Eig}_{[-\delta,\delta]}(A_{X})
and commutes with the projection
\pi_{\delta}
onto this subspace. Moreover, for
B_{1}=\operatorname{Eig}_{(-\infty,-\delta)}(A_{X}),

B_{2}=\operatorname{Eig}_{(-\infty,\delta]}(A_{X})
we have
B_{1}^{\mathrm{adj}}=B_{2},

B_{2}^{\mathrm{adj}}=B_{1}.
Now put this into Proposition
4.3
, observing that
B_{1}^{\perp}\cap B_{2}=\operatorname{Eig}_{[-\delta,\delta]}(A_{X}),

\pi=\pi_{\delta},
and that by the description of the image in Proposition

applied to

and

the subspace that appears in (

) is
\pi\mathcal{R}\bigl(\Ker D_{Y^{b},B_{2}}/\Ker D_{Y^{b},B_{1}}\bigr)=C_{\delta}(D_{Y}).

∎

Appendix A

This appendix recalls the construction of the determinant line bundle, Pfaffian line bundle, and spectral covering, and fixes several sign conventions for these.

A.1.
Sign convention

Already the construction of the determinant and Pfaffian line bundles requires a sign convention. We use the supersymmetric sign convention as in Upmeier
[
21
]
*§3, which determines the signs in various isomorphisms according to principle that passing two vectors past each other causes a sign.

Let
\mathbb{K}
be a field. A

is a pair
((-1)^{n},L)
where
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}
and

is a
1
-dimensional vector space over
\mathbb{K}.
The

of an

over
\mathbb{K}

The non-zero elements of

and are represented by

\omega=v_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge v_{n}
where
v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}
is an
ordered basis
of
V
over
\mathbb{K}.
In this section, all tensor products, dimensions, bases, and exterior powers will be taken over the (skew) field
\mathbb{K},
which we sometimes drop from the notation.

Let

be graded lines in degrees
