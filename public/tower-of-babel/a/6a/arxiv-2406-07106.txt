Displacement versus velocity memory effects from a gravitational plane wave
Jibril Ben Achour, Jean-Philippe Uzan

Abstract
This article demonstrates that additionally to the well-known velocity memory effect, a vacuum gravitational plane wave can also induce a displacement memory on a couple of test particles. A complete classification of the conditions under which a velocity or a displacement memory effect occur is established. These conditions depend both the initial conditions of the relative motion and on the wave profile. The two cases where the wave admits a pulse or a step profile are treated. Our analytical expressions are then compared to numerical integrations to exhibit either a velocity or a displacement memory, in the case of these two families of profiles. Additionally to this classification, the existence of a new symmetry of polarized vacuum gravitational plane wave under Möbius reparametrization of the null time is demonstrated. Finally, we discuss the resolution of the geodesic deviation equation by means of the underlying symmetries of vacuum gravitational plane wave.

Generic properties of a gravitational plane wave

In this section, we review the main properties of a gravitational plane wave and fix our notations. While most of the results presented in this first section are well-known, we clarify some of them and present also new formulations, exhibiting in particular a new symmetry of vacuum gravitational plane waves which does not seem to have been noticed so far.

Consider the metric of a gravitational plane wave in the so-called BJR coordinates,

\displaystyle\textrm{d}s^{2}=2\textrm{d}u\textrm{d}v+A_{ij}(u)\textrm{d}x^{i}\textrm{d}x^{j}

(1.1)

with
A_{ij}
a symmetric
2\times 2
matrix describing the wave profile such that

A_{ij}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}A_{11}&A_{12}\\
A_{12}&A_{22}\end{array}\right)\,.

(1.2)

The Minkowski metric is trivially recovered by
A_{ij}=\delta_{ij}
. This radiative geometry is defined by exhibiting a covariant constant null vector
\xi^{\alpha}\partial_{\alpha}=\partial_{v}
, i.e. which satisfies
\nabla_{\alpha}\xi_{\beta}=0
. The BJR coordinates are usually not those used to investigate the properties of the wave as they are not globally defined and thus fail to cover the whole spacetime, as discussed in Ref.
[
83
]
. Within this coordinate system, the Einstein field equations take the form

\displaystyle\partial_{u}\left(A^{i\ell}\partial_{u}A_{i\ell}\right)+\frac{1}{2}A^{i\ell}A^{jk}\partial_{u}A_{j\ell}\partial_{u}A_{ik}=8\pi T_{uu}

(1.3)

where
T_{uu}
is stress-energy tensor of some source. As it is well-known, this field equation takes a much simpler form in the Brinkmann coordinates system which we shall discuss later. However, in the vacuum and in the case of polarized wave for which
A_{12}=0
, this non-linear equation can be dramatically simplified; see Appendix
B
for a discussion on the polarisations. Indeed, in such a case,
A^{ii}=1/A_{ii}
and field equation reduces to

\displaystyle\left[\frac{\ddot{A}_{11}}{A_{11}}-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\dot{A}_{11}}{A_{11}}\right)^{2}\right]+\left[\frac{\ddot{A}_{22}}{A_{22}}-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\dot{A}_{22}}{A_{22}}\right)^{2}\right]=0\,.

(1.4)

In general, solving this non-linear equation is challenging so that most of the analysis rely on numerical investigations. However, it can be dramatically simplified by setting

A_{ii}(u)\equiv{\cal A}^{2}_{i}(u)\,,

(1.5)

which allows one to write

\frac{\ddot{A}_{ii}}{A_{ii}}-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\dot{A}_{ii}}{A_{ii}}\right)^{2}=\frac{2\ddot{\cal A}_{i}}{{\cal A}_{i}}

while the dynamical equation (
1.4
) simplifies to

\frac{\ddot{\cal A}_{1}}{{\cal A}_{1}}+\frac{\ddot{\cal A}_{2}}{{\cal A}_{2}}=0\,.

With this notation, the polarized wave-profile takes the form

A_{ij}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}{\cal A}_{1}^{2}&0\\
0&{\cal A}^{2}_{2}\end{array}\right)\,.

As we shall see, working with
{\mathcal{A}}_{i}
instead of
A_{ii}
allows us to write the conditions for the different memory effects in a very compact form. Before presenting these results, let us show that the non-linear wave equation (
1.4
) can be reformulated so as to reveal a hidden symmetry.

Conformal invariance of the wave profile

In order to make this hidden symmetry explicit, it is useful to introduce the auxiliary fields

A_{11}=\frac{1}{\dot{F}}\qquad A_{22}=\frac{1}{\dot{G}}\,.

(1.6)

Then, one can recast the field equations as

\displaystyle\text{Sch}[F]+\text{Sch}[G]=0

(1.7)

where
\text{Sch}[f]
denotes the Schwarzian derivative of the function
f
defined by

\text{Sch}[f]=\frac{\dddot{f}}{\dot{f}}-\frac{3}{2}\frac{\ddot{f}^{2}}{\dot{f}^{2}}\,.

(1.8)

The Schwarzian derivative being Möbius invariant, i.e. for any given function
f
,

\text{Sch}[M\circ f]=\text{Sch}[f]\qquad\text{where}\qquad M(u)=\frac{au+b}{cu+d}\qquad ad-bc\neq 0\,,

(1.9)

it follows that knowing one couple of solution
(F,G)
, one can generate another inequivalent solution to the wave equation by a Möbius transformation of the seed profile
5
5

5

It is also useful to rewrite the Schwarzian derivative as follows

\text{Sch}[f]=\frac{\textrm{d}^{2}}{\textrm{d}u^{2}}\log{f^{\prime}}-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\textrm{d}}{\textrm{d}u}\log{f^{\prime}}\right)^{2}\,.

(1.10)

where
f^{\prime}:=\frac{\textrm{d}f}{\textrm{d}u}
. In general, the Einstein equations can be understood as a balance equation between the Schwarzian derivative of each component of the polarized wave-matrix
A_{ij}
.

The simplest wave profile solving the field equation (
1.7
) corresponds to the case where each Schwarzian derivative vanishes independantly, which is solved for

F(u)=\frac{au+b}{cu+d}\,,\qquad G(u)=\frac{\tilde{a}u+\tilde{b}}{\tilde{c}u+\tilde{d}}

(1.11)

where the eight constants satisfy
ad-bc\neq 0
and
\tilde{a}\tilde{d}-\tilde{b}\tilde{c}\neq 0

such that

A_{11}(u)=(cu+d)^{2}\,,\qquad A_{22}(u)=(\tilde{c}u+\tilde{d})^{2}\,.

(1.12)

where the constants in Eq (
1.12
) are rescaled versions of the constants entering in Eq (
1.11
).
This specific solution corresponds to one of the simplest example of a vacuum gravitational plane wave. Notice that while the profile is non-trivial, such a plane wave geometry can always be brought back to the Minkowski metric (in light-cone coordinate) thanks to the following diffeomorphism

\displaystyle u\rightarrow u\,,

(1.13)

\displaystyle v\rightarrow v-\frac{1}{2}c(cu+d)x^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\tilde{c}(\tilde{c}u+\tilde{d})y^{2}\,,

(1.14)

\displaystyle x\rightarrow(cu+d)x\,,

(1.15)

\displaystyle y\rightarrow(\tilde{c}u+\tilde{d})y\,.

(1.16)

This diffeomorphism can be considered as a large gauge transformation.
From the point of view of the solution generating map, the simple solution (
1.12
) stands as a fixed point of the solution space, as it remains invariant under a Möbius transformation.

Beyond this simple example, an infinite set of solutions for the wave profile exists, which are simply constrained by the balance equation (
1.7
) between the two Schwarzian derivatives. In the following, we shall be interested in distinguishing between the wave profile that induces a constant displacement memory and those that instead induce a constant velocity memory. Having presented the structure of the vacuum field equations, we now turn to the symmetries of the vacuum plane wave metric.

Symmetries

The symmetries of pp-wave have already been investigated and classified long time ago
[
58
,
59
,
60
,
61
]
. It was shown by Souriau
[
57
]
that one can express the conformal Killing vector (KV) fields in a close form provided one uses the BJR coordinates; see Ref.
[
62
]
for a recent review of this result. Consider the metric (
1.1
)
without
the restriction
A_{12}=0
. Consider then the conformal Killing equation

{\mathcal{L}}_{\xi}g_{\mu\nu}=\Omega g_{\mu\nu}\,,

(1.17)

where
\Omega(u,v,x^{i})
is the conformal factor. In BJR coordinates, it decomposes as

\displaystyle\partial_{v}\xi^{u}

\displaystyle=0\,,

(1.18)

\displaystyle\partial_{u}\xi^{v}

\displaystyle=0\,,

(1.19)

\displaystyle\partial_{i}\xi^{u}+A_{ij}\partial_{v}\xi^{j}

\displaystyle=0\,,

(1.20)

\displaystyle\partial_{i}\xi^{v}+A_{ij}\partial_{u}\xi^{j}

\displaystyle=0\,,

(1.21)

\displaystyle\partial_{v}\xi^{v}+\partial_{u}\xi^{u}

\displaystyle=\Omega\,,

(1.22)

\displaystyle\xi^{u}\partial_{u}A_{ij}+A_{ik}\partial_{j}\xi^{k}+A_{jk}\partial_{i}\xi^{k}

\displaystyle=\Omega A_{ij}\,.

(1.23)

It is straightforward to check that the vector fields

{\mathcal{N}}^{\alpha}\partial_{\alpha}=\partial_{v}\;,\qquad P_{+}^{\alpha}\partial_{\alpha}=\partial_{x}\qquad P^{\alpha}_{-}\partial_{\alpha}=\partial_{y}

(1.24)

solve these Killing equations (
1.18
-
1.23
). Moreover, using the Souriau matrix defined by

H^{ij}(u_{0},u)\equiv\int^{u}_{u_{0}}A^{ij}(w)\textrm{d}w

(1.25)

one can show that the two additional vector fields given by

\displaystyle{\mathcal{B}}^{\alpha}_{+}\partial_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=H^{xx}(u_{0},u)\partial_{x}+H^{xy}(u_{0},u)\partial_{y}-x\partial_{v}

(1.26)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{B}}^{\alpha}_{-}\partial_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=H^{yy}(u_{0},u)\partial_{y}+H^{yx}(u_{0},u)\partial_{x}-y\partial_{v}

(1.27)

also solve the Killing equations (
1.18
-
1.23
). This completes the number of Killing vector fields for this spacetime geometry. They can be written in a compact form as

\displaystyle\xi^{\alpha}\partial_{\alpha}=\left\{h+b_{i}x^{i}\right\}\partial_{v}+\left[\chi^{i}+b_{j}H^{ij}(u_{0},u)\right]\partial_{i}\,.

(1.28)

The isometry group is thus parametrized by the five constants
(h,\chi^{i},b_{i})
. As we shall see in the last section, these gauge parameters can be directly linked to the initial conditions of the geodesic deviation and shown to label the different contributions to the memories.

Let us emphasize that we never had to explicitly fix the wave profile
A_{ij}
to obtain the Killing vectors in a closed form. This implies that these Killing vector fields are a common properties of
any gravitational plane wave
described by the metric (
1.1
). We stress that identifying the full set of symmetries of the plane gravitational wave is greatly simplified by working in BJR coordinates (
1.1
), where the metric appears to be homogeneous in the
u
-coordinate. Computing the Lie algebra of these vector fields show that the only non-vanishing bracket is

[B_{\pm},P_{\pm}]={\mathcal{N}}\,,

(1.29)

which can be identified with the Carrolian Lie algebra in
2+1
dimensions without rotation.

Let us now consider the case
\Omega\neq 0
. Indeed, it is well-known that any pp-wave possesses a homothetic Killing vector (HKV) rescaling the wavefront. One can show that for general wave profile
A_{ij}
, the only solution to the conformal Killing equation is
\Omega=2
while the conformal vector field takes the form

\displaystyle Z^{\alpha}\partial_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=2v\partial_{v}+x^{i}\partial_{i}\,.

(1.30)

Computing its brackets with the KVs, one finds that it acts as a dilatation through

\displaystyle[P_{\pm},Z]=P_{\pm}\,,\qquad[B_{\pm},Z]=B_{\pm}\,,\qquad[{\mathcal{N}},Z]

\displaystyle=2{\mathcal{N}}\,.

(1.31)

Quite remarkably, the existence of this HKV allows us to further identify an additional hidden symmetry.

Indeed, as initially shown by Koutras
[
64
]
, if a spacetime admits both a gradient Killing vector and a HKV then it also admits a non-trivial rank-2 Killing tensor (KT) which generates an additional symmetry
[
65
]
. With
\xi_{\alpha}\textrm{d}x^{\alpha}=(\partial_{\alpha}\Phi)\mathrm{d}x^{\alpha}
the gradient KV and
Z_{\alpha}\textrm{d}x^{\alpha}
the HKV, the KT is explicitely given by

K_{\mu\nu}\textrm{d}x^{\mu}\textrm{d}x^{\nu}=\left[Z_{(\mu}\xi_{\nu)}-\Phi g_{\mu\nu}\right]\textrm{d}x^{\mu}\textrm{d}x^{\nu}.

(1.32)

In the case we consider, in which the gradient KV is the covarianty constant null vector, ii.e.
{\mathcal{N}}^{\alpha}\partial_{\alpha}=\partial_{v}
, the KT is given by

\displaystyle K_{\mu\nu}\textrm{d}x^{\mu}\textrm{d}x^{\nu}

\displaystyle=2v\textrm{d}u^{2}-u(2\textrm{d}u\textrm{d}v+A_{ij}\textrm{d}x^{i}\textrm{d}x^{j})+A_{ij}x^{j}\textrm{d}u\textrm{d}x^{i}\,.

(1.33)

Note that its expression holds, again, for any wave profile. It corresponds to the Killing tensor described by Keane and Tupper in Ref.
[
63
]
. As we shall see, the fact that the Killing tensor is not a symmetrized product of Killing vectors implies that it provides a new charge for the geodesic motion. We will now use these symmetries to analytically solve both the geodesic motion and the geodesic deviation. This will reveal useful when investigating the memory effects.

Geodesic motion

Let us now analyze the geodesic motion using BJR coordinates. Consider a geodesic
x^{\mu}(\tau)
with affine parameter
\tau
and tangent vector
u^{\mu}=\textrm{d}x^{\mu}/\textrm{d}\tau
normalized to

u^{\mu}u_{\mu}=\epsilon\quad\hbox{with}\quad\epsilon=\{0,-1\}

(1.34)

respectively for null and timelike geodesics. For the metric (
1.1
), the geodesic Lagrangian,
L=\frac{1}{2}g_{\mu\nu}\dot{x}^{\mu}\dot{x}^{\nu}
, reduces to

\displaystyle L=\dot{u}\dot{v}+\frac{1}{2}A_{ij}\dot{x}^{i}\dot{x}^{j}

(1.35)

with a dot referring to a derivative w.r.t. to the proper time
\tau
. The geodesic equation
u^{\mu}\nabla_{\mu}u^{\nu}=0
decomposes into

\displaystyle\ddot{v}-\frac{1}{2}A^{\prime}_{ij}\dot{x}^{i}\dot{x}^{j}

\displaystyle=0\,,

(1.36)

\displaystyle A_{ij}\ddot{x}^{j}+A^{\prime}_{ij}\dot{x}^{j}\dot{u}

\displaystyle=0\,,

(1.37)

\displaystyle\ddot{u}

\displaystyle=0\,,

(1.38)

with a prime refering to a derivative w.r.t.
u
. In the following, we review how to algebraically integrate these equations using the conserved charges associated to the underlying symmetries, including the KT charge.

Conserved charges

Let us first write the Hamiltonian formulation of this system in order to discuss its symmetries. The momenta are given by

\displaystyle p_{u}

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta{\mathcal{L}}}{\delta\dot{u}}=\dot{v}\,,

(1.39)

\displaystyle p_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta{\mathcal{L}}}{\delta\dot{x}^{i}}=A_{ij}\dot{x}^{j}\,,

(1.40)

\displaystyle p_{v}

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta{\mathcal{L}}}{\delta\dot{v}}=\dot{u}\,,

(1.41)

such that the symplectic structure reads

\{v,p_{v}\}=\{u,p_{u}\}=1\,,\qquad\{x^{i},p_{j}\}=\delta^{i}{}_{j}\,.

(1.42)

Then, the Hamiltonian of the geodesic motion takes the form

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=p_{u}p_{v}+\frac{1}{2}A^{ij}p_{i}p_{j}=\frac{\epsilon}{2}\,.

(1.43)

This system admits six conserved charges associated to the symmetries identified in Section
1.2
. Let us recall that for each isometry generated by
\xi^{\alpha}\partial_{\alpha}
, the scalar
{\mathcal{O}}=\xi^{\alpha}p_{\alpha}
provides the associated conserved charge. Moreover, for the Killing tensor
K_{\mu\nu}
the charge is quadratic in the momenta and reads
K=K^{\mu\nu}p_{\mu}p_{\nu}
. In our case, one finds the following conserved quantities:

•

Translations
. Since the Hamiltonian does not depend on neither
v
nor
x^{i}
,
p_{v}
and
p_{i}
are automatically conserved. We denote them as

{\mathcal{N}}=p_{v}\,,\qquad P_{+}=p_{x}\,,\qquad P_{-}=p_{y}\,,

(1.44)

which provide 3 constants of motion associated to the KV (
1.24
).

•

Carrolian boost
. The conserved charges generating the boosts (
1.26
) are given by

\displaystyle{\mathcal{B}}_{+}

\displaystyle=H^{xx}p_{x}+H^{xy}p_{y}-p_{v}x\,,

(1.45)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{B}}_{-}

\displaystyle=H^{yy}p_{y}+H^{yx}p_{x}-p_{v}y\,.

(1.46)

A simple computation shows that

\displaystyle\{{\mathcal{B}}_{\pm},H\}

\displaystyle=0\,,

(1.47)

so that these new charges are indeed conserved. These two charges allow one to integrate the geodesic motion.

•

Conformal charge
. The HKV (
1.30
) is associated to the charge

\displaystyle{\mathcal{Z}}

\displaystyle=p_{v}2v+p_{i}x^{i}\,,

(1.48)

the bracket of which with the Hamiltonian reduces to

\displaystyle\{{\mathcal{Z}},H\}

\displaystyle=2H\,.

(1.49)

This charge is conserved only when
H=0
, i.e. for null geodesics, as expected for a conformal Killing vector.

•

Hidden Killing tensor charge
. The charge coming from the Killing tensor (
1.33
) can be expressed in a simple form as

\displaystyle{\mathcal{K}}=K^{\mu\nu}p_{\mu}p_{\nu}

\displaystyle=p_{v}{\mathcal{Z}}-2uH\,.

(1.50)

As expected, it is independent of the charges inherited by the conformal isometries.

From the explicit expressions of the six charges
({\mathcal{N}},P_{\pm},{\mathcal{B}}_{\pm},{\mathcal{K}})
, we can now compute their algebra. First, one finds that the five charges
({\mathcal{N}},P_{\pm},{\mathcal{B}}_{\pm})
form a
2+1
Carrolian algebra without rotation
[
62
]
, i.e the only non-vanishing bracket reads

\displaystyle\{P_{\pm},{\mathcal{B}}_{\pm}\}={\mathcal{N}}\,.

(1.51)

This is the well-known 5-parameters isometry group of the gravitational plane wave. Then, the Killing tensor charge
{\mathcal{K}}
builds up the conformal extension of the Carrolian algebra given by

\displaystyle\{P_{\pm},{\mathcal{K}}\}={\mathcal{N}}P_{\pm}\,,\qquad\{B_{\pm},{\mathcal{K}}\}={\mathcal{N}}B_{\pm}\,,\qquad\{{\mathcal{N}},{\mathcal{K}}\}

\displaystyle=2{\mathcal{N}}^{2}\,,

(1.52)

which closes the algebra of conserved charges. The system is integrable in the sense that the four charges
({\mathcal{N}},{\mathcal{B}}_{\pm},H)
are in involution.

Algebraic integration of geodesic flow

Now, let us review how these conserved charges allow for an algebraic integration of the geodesic equation (
1.37
-
1.38
). First, we use the conservation of
{\mathcal{N}}
, i.e. of
p_{v}
, to integrate the
u
-trajectory equation (
1.38
) or equivalently (
1.41
) and write

u=p_{v}\tau={\mathcal{N}}\tau.

(1.53)

Then, using the two charges

To finish, this expression allows us to derive the optical scalars associated to the congruence of time-like geodesics. Using that the velocity vector

\displaystyle\sigma

Solving the transport parallel condition

Consider the transport parallel (PT) equation

In order to provide an analytic expression of the tetrad, we focus our analysis on polarized plane wave for which

Using the redefinition

\displaystyle E^{1}{}_{A}(u)=\frac{{\mathcal{A}}_{1}(u_{0})}{{\mathcal{A}}_{1}(u)}E^{1}{}_{A}(u_{0})\,,\qquad E^{2}{}_{A}(u)=\frac{{\mathcal{A}}_{2}(u_{0})}{{\mathcal{A}}_{2}(u)}E^{2}{}_{A}(u_{0})\,.

\displaystyle\ddot{E}^{1}{}_{A}=-\left(\ddot{{\mathcal{A}}}_{1}-\frac{2\dot{{\mathcal{A}}^{2}_{1}}}{{\mathcal{A}}_{1}}\right)\frac{{\mathcal{A}}_{1}(u_{0})}{{\mathcal{A}}^{2}_{1}(u)}E^{1}{}_{A}(u_{0})\,,\qquad\ddot{E}^{2}{}_{A}=-\left(\ddot{{\mathcal{A}}}_{2}-\frac{2\dot{{\mathcal{A}}^{2}_{2}}}{{\mathcal{A}}_{2}}\right)\frac{{\mathcal{A}}_{2}(u_{0})}{{\mathcal{A}}^{2}_{2}(u)}E^{2}{}_{A}(u_{0})\,.

\displaystyle H_{+}=\frac{1}{2}\left[\frac{\ddot{A}_{11}}{A_{11}}-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\dot{A}_{11}}{A_{11}}\right)^{2}\right]=-\frac{1}{2}\left[\frac{\ddot{A}_{22}}{A_{22}}-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\dot{A}_{22}}{A_{22}}\right)^{2}\right]\,.

Transverse memories: displacement versus velocity memory

Generalities

We now have all the elements to discuss the memory effects depending on the form of the wave-profile. To simplify the discussion, we focus on wave-profiles which satisfy at early time
u_{0}

A_{ij}(u_{0})=\delta_{ij}\,.

(2.1)

Notice that this
does not
that imply
\dot{A}_{ij}(u_{0})=0
at
u_{0}
. Consider a test particle which is either massive, i.e.
\epsilon=-1
, or massless, i.e.
\epsilon=0
, and let us analyze its motion in the 2d plane orthogonal to the direction of the propagation of the wave for a general
\epsilon
. The motion in this 2d space does not depend on the value of
\epsilon
.
Plugging the solution of the geodesic motion (
1.56
) into Eq. (
1.82

\displaystyle X^{A}

\displaystyle=E^{A}{}_{i}\;\left(H^{ij}p_{j}-{\mathcal{B}}^{i}\right)\,,

(2.2)

\displaystyle\dot{X}^{A}

\displaystyle=\dot{E}^{A}{}_{i}\left(H^{ij}p_{j}-{\mathcal{B}}^{i}\right)+E^{A}{}_{i}A^{ij}p_{j}\,.

(2.3)

There are parametrized by the two 2d vectors
(p_{i},{\mathcal{B}}^{i})
, which are constants of motion related to the initial conditions we have introduced in the previous section. From Eqs. (
2.2
) and (
2.3
), the initial position and velocity of the test particle in the 2d transverse plane are related to the constants

X^{A}(u_{0})=-\delta^{A}{}_{i}{\mathcal{B}}^{i}\,,\qquad\dot{X}^{A}(u_{0})=\delta^{A}{}_{i}\left(\delta^{ij}p_{j}-\dot{{\mathcal{A}}}_{i}(u_{0}){\mathcal{B}}^{i}\right)\,.

Note that

represents the 2d projection of the relative tangent vector, which is different from the 2d-projection of the 3-velocity,

Now, consider a couple of test particles and choose one of them as the reference geodesic, i.e. set

and let us denote the geodesic of the other test particle by

. Both satisfy Eqs. (

(2.5)

so that

\displaystyle E^{A}{}_{i}={\mathcal{A}}_{i}(u)E^{A}{}_{i}(u_{0})\,,\qquad\dot{E}^{A}{}_{i}=\dot{{\mathcal{A}}}_{i}(u)E^{A}{}_{i}(u_{0})\,.

(2.6)

Using these expressions, the physical distance in the Fermi coordinates between the test particle following the trajectory

(2.8)

Notice that because the matrix are diagonal, there are no summation on the indice

H^{ij}\equiv H^{ii}\delta^{ij}\quad\hbox{with}\quad H^{ii}(u_{0},u)=\int_{u_{0}}^{u}\frac{\mathrm{d}v}{{\cal A}^{2}_{i}(v)}.

(2.9)

Using

and the expression for

\displaystyle\dot{\zeta}^{i}(u)

(2.11)

Note that Eq. (

(2.12)

without summation on

Classification of the memory effects

We can now use these expressions to classify the different conditions for having a displacement or a velocity memory effect. We are interested in the situations for which asymptotycally, i.e. for
u<u_{0}
and
u>u_{f}
, the relative distance is non-zero and the relative acceleration vanishes, which read

\zeta^{i}\neq 0\qquad\text{and}\qquad\ddot{\zeta}^{i}=0\,.

(2.13)

We shall distinguish three different cases of memory effects that satisfy the above condition.

•

Velocity Memory (VM)
: When the relative velocity in the two asymptotic regions
u<u_{0}
and
u>u_{f}
satisfies

\Delta\dot{\zeta}=\dot{\zeta}_{f}-\dot{\zeta}_{0}\neq 0

(2.14)

the dynamics exhibits a
constant velocity memory effect
, i.e. the passage of the gravitational wave induces a constant shift on the asymptotic value of the relative velocity.

•

Vanishing Velocity Memory (VM0)
: When the shift between the asymptotic relative velocities satisfies

\Delta\dot{\zeta}=\dot{\zeta}_{f}-\dot{\zeta}_{0}=0

(2.15)

there is clearly no velocity memory effect since the relative velocity is the same in the two asymptotic regions. Yet, as we shall see, the wave can still have some interesting effects on the couple of test particles.

•

Displacement Memory (DM)
: this situation is characterized by the fact that in the asymptotic future, the relative velocity vanishes, i.e. the solution is such that

\dot{\zeta}_{f}=0\,.

(2.16)

This means that the two test particles settle at a constant distance for
u>u_{f}
independently of any condition on
\dot{\zeta}_{0}
. In particular, the case where
\dot{\zeta}_{0}\neq 0
corresponds to a configuration where the two particles have a relative velocity for
u<u_{0}
and in which the gravitational wave cancels this initial relative motion so that the particles settle at a constant relative distance.

Thanks to results of the Section
2.1
we have all the elements to analyze the conditions leading to three cases: VM, VM0 or DM. To that goals, one needs to distinguish two kind of profiles: the pulse and step wave-profiles, for which the asymptotic behaviors at early and late times differ.

Pulse profiles

A pulse profile is characterized by the property that the Brinkmann profile
H_{+}(u)
vanishes in both asymptotic regimes, i.e. for
u<u_{0}
and
u>u_{f}
. It follows that the asymptotic behaviors of
\{{\mathcal{A}}_{1}(u),{\mathcal{A}}_{2}(u)\}
, obtained by solving Eq. (
1.93
), automatically satisfy

\ddot{{\mathcal{A}}}_{i}=0\,.

(2.17)

Both modes behave identically and independently so that we temporarily drop the indices
i
in
{\mathcal{A}}_{i}
and
\zeta^{i}
. We now analyze their asymptotic behavior.

First, in the remote past, where
u<u_{0}
, it is obvious from Eqs. (
2.13
) and (
2.17

{\mathcal{A}}(u)
and
\zeta(u)
behave as

\displaystyle{\mathcal{A}}(u)

\displaystyle=\dot{{\mathcal{A}}}_{0}(u-u_{0})+{\mathcal{A}}_{0}\,,

(2.18)

\displaystyle\zeta(u)

\displaystyle=\dot{\zeta}_{0}(u-u_{0})+\zeta_{0}\,

(2.19)

with
({\mathcal{A}}_{0},\dot{{\mathcal{A}}}_{0},\zeta_{0},\dot{\zeta}_{0})
constant. This solution is indeed consistent with Eq. (
1.93

(2.20)

which remains well-defined even if
{\cal A}
crosses zero (see Appendix
C
for a general discussion on this issue). From Eq. (
2.12

\dot{\zeta}_{0}{{\mathcal{A}}_{0}}-\zeta_{0}\dot{{\mathcal{A}}}_{0}=p\,.

(2.21)

To be consistent, the relation (
2.8
) shall hold for all
u<u_{0}
, which implies that the constants of motion
(p,{\mathcal{B}})
are related to the initial conditions in
u_{0}
by

one can always find a unique solution

so that given a pulse profile, there exists a solution that interpolate between

and

\dot{{\mathcal{A}}}_{0}/{\mathcal{A}}_{f}-\dot{\mathcal{A}}_{f}/{\mathcal{A}}_{0}+\dot{{\mathcal{A}}}_{0}\dot{{\mathcal{A}}}_{f}H_{0f}\not=0
. It follows that these solutions in the two asymptotic regimes allow one to easily analyze the conditions for the different memory effects to occur. We restrict to the 3 cases defined in §
2.2
.

A
VM
occurs under the condition
\dot{\zeta}_{f}\neq\dot{\zeta}_{0}
. Since pulses enjoy a constant asymptotic velocities both for
u<u_{0}
and
u>u_{f}
, they generically lead to a constant VM whatever the profile
H_{+}(u)
and the constants of motions
({\mathcal{B}},p)
or, similarly, whatever the initial conditions
(\zeta_{0},\dot{\zeta}_{0})
. This is a generic properties of pulse illustrated on Fig.
2
.

A
VM0
occurs in the special cases in which the change of the relative velocity vanish, i.e. when the condition (
2.15
) holds. This condition, i.e.
\dot{\zeta}_{f}=\dot{\zeta}_{0}
, translates into

\displaystyle(\dot{{\mathcal{A}}}_{f}-\dot{{\mathcal{A}}}_{0}){\mathcal{B}}=-p\left[\frac{{\mathcal{A}}_{f}-{\mathcal{A}}_{0}}{{\mathcal{A}}_{f}{\mathcal{A}}_{0}}-\dot{{\mathcal{A}}}_{f}H_{0f}\right]\,.

(2.31)

This equality can be achieved in different ways.

1.

If
\dot{\mathcal{A}}_{f}-\dot{\mathcal{A}}_{0}\not=0
, then a VM0 occurs if only if the momenta
p
and the initial position
{\mathcal{B}}
are related by

\displaystyle{\mathcal{B}}=-p\,\frac{\frac{{\mathcal{A}}_{f}-{\mathcal{A}}_{0}}{{\mathcal{A}}_{f}{\mathcal{A}}_{0}}-\dot{{\mathcal{A}}}_{f}H_{0f}}{\dot{{\mathcal{A}}}_{f}-\dot{{\mathcal{A}}}_{0}}\,.

(2.32)

Since
{\mathcal{B}}\not=0
, the VM0 is possible if and only if
p\not=0
and only for some specific geodesics satisfying (
2.32
). This case is illustrated in Fig
3
. It shows that even if there is no change in the asymptotic relative velocity, the wave induces a switch of the positions of the two particles.

2.

If
\dot{\mathcal{A}}_{f}-\dot{\mathcal{A}}_{0}=0
then,

•

whatever the wave profile and whatever
{\mathcal{B}}
, one has a VM0 if

p=0\,.

(2.33)

•

if
p\not=0
, then the wave profile shall satisfy

H_{0f}=\frac{{\mathcal{A}}_{f}-{\mathcal{A}}_{0}}{{\mathcal{A}}_{0}{\mathcal{A}}_{f}\dot{{\mathcal{A}}}_{f}}\,,

(2.34)

which is a non-trivial condition between
{\cal A}
in the two asymptotic regions and the shape
H_{+}(u)
of the pulse through
H_{0f}
.

Finally, a
DM
occurs when the condition (
2.16
) holds, i.e. when
\dot{\zeta}_{f}=0
. Using Eq. (
2.28
), this translates into a specific tuning between the two constants of motion,

\dot{{\mathcal{A}}}_{f}{\mathcal{B}}=p\left(\frac{1}{{\mathcal{A}}_{f}}+\dot{{\mathcal{A}}}_{f}H_{0f}\right)\,.

(2.35)

This condition can be satisfied in several ways.

1.

If
\dot{\mathcal{A}}_{f}\not=0
, then a pure DM requires
p
and
{\mathcal{B}}
to be related by

\displaystyle{\mathcal{B}}=p\,\frac{1+{\mathcal{A}}_{f}\dot{{\mathcal{A}}}_{f}H_{0f}}{{\mathcal{A}}_{f}\dot{{\mathcal{A}}}_{f}}\,.

(2.36)

Since
{\mathcal{B}}\not=0
, the DM is possible if and only if
p\not=0
.

2.

If
\dot{\mathcal{A}}_{f}=0
then,

•

whatever the wave profile and whatever
{\mathcal{B}}
, one has a DM if

p=0\,.

(2.37)

•

if
p\not=0
, then the wave profile shall satisfy

H_{0f}=-\frac{1}{{\mathcal{A}}_{f}\dot{{\mathcal{A}}}_{f}}\,,

(2.38)

which is a non-trivial condition between
{\cal A}
in the asymptotic after zone and the shape
H_{+}(u)
of the pulse through
H_{0f}
.

This is illustrated on the bottom line of Fig.

2.32
) holds (middle column) and a pure DM if Eq. (
2.35
) is satisfied (left column).

This completes the classification of the conditions for the realization of the different memories in the case of a pulse profile. We now turn to the step profiles.

Step profiles

Now, we consider the second family of wave profiles. A
step
profile is characterized by the property that the Brinkmann profile
H_{+}(u)
is constant and non-vanishing in both asymptotic regimes, i.e.
H_{+}\rightarrow\lambda_{0,f}\not=0
respectively for
u<u_{0}
and
u>u_{f}
. Let us stress that while pulse profiles have been studied at length in the literature, step profiles have been largely ignored in the context of vacuum gravitational plane wave. They are nevertheless relevant examples as they appear to be interesting models of the non-oscillating contribution of the strain in the perturbative approach. See for example the discussion in Ref.
[
80
]
. In the two asymptotic regions,
\{{\mathcal{A}}_{1}(u),{\mathcal{A}}_{2}(u)\}
are obtained by solving (
1.93
) and automatically satisfy

\ddot{{\mathcal{A}}}_{1}=\lambda_{0,f}^{2}{\mathcal{A}}_{1},\qquad\ddot{{\mathcal{A}}}_{2}=-\lambda_{0,f}^{2}{\mathcal{A}}_{2}

(2.39)

We assume
\lambda_{0,f}>0
without loss of generality. Examples of step profiles and of geometries
({\mathcal{A}}_{1},{\mathcal{A}}_{2})
are depicted on Fig.
4
.

First, in the past region
u<u_{0}
in which that
H_{+}=\lambda^{2}_{0}>0
, the solutions of the equations of evolution (
1.93
) are easily obtained as

\displaystyle{\mathcal{A}}_{1}(u)

\displaystyle={\mathcal{A}}_{0}\cosh\left[\lambda_{0}(u-u_{0})\right]+\frac{\dot{{\mathcal{A}}}_{0}}{\lambda_{0}}\sinh\left[\lambda_{0}(u-u_{0})\right]

(2.40)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{A}}_{2}(u)

\displaystyle={\mathcal{A}}_{0}\cos\left[\lambda_{0}(u-u_{0})\right]+\frac{\dot{{\mathcal{A}}}_{0}}{\lambda_{0}}\sin\left[\lambda_{0}(u-u_{0})\right]\,.

(2.41)

Contrary to the pulse profile, we shall discuss the asymptotic memory effects, i.e. between the regions
(-)
and
(+)
for
\rightarrow\pm\infty
respectively. It is clear that

{\mathcal{A}}^{-}_{1}\sim\frac{1}{2}\left({\mathcal{A}}_{0}-\frac{\dot{\mathcal{A}}_{0}}{\lambda_{0}}\right)\hbox{e}^{-\lambda_{0}(u-u_{0})}\,\qquad\dot{{\mathcal{A}}}^{-}_{1}=-\lambda_{0}A^{-}_{1}\,\qquad\ddot{{\mathcal{A}}}^{-}_{1}=\lambda_{0}^{2}A^{-}_{1}\,.

(2.42)

Hence, obviously, unless
{\mathcal{A}}_{0}-\frac{\dot{{\mathcal{A}}}_{0}}{\lambda_{0}}=0
,
{\mathcal{A}}_{1}(u)
is exponentially diverging when
u\ll u_{0}
and
{\mathcal{A}}_{2}(u)
is either oscillating or strictly vanishing. In this regime, the Souriau matrix (
2.9
) becomes

\displaystyle H^{ii}(u_{0},u)=\int^{u}_{u_{0}}\frac{\textrm{d}v}{{\mathcal{A}}_{i}^{2}(v)}=\frac{1}{\lambda_{0}{\mathcal{A}}_{i}(u){\mathcal{A}}_{i}(u_{0})}\times\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\sinh[\lambda_{0}(u-u_{0})]&i=1\\
\;\;\sin[\lambda_{0}(u-u_{0})]&i=2\end{array}\right.\,.

The solutions for the mode
{\mathcal{A}}_{2}
(and hence
\zeta_{2}
) are obtained from the expression of the mode
{\mathcal{A}}_{1}
(and
\zeta_{1}
) by the transformation
\lambda_{0}\rightarrow i\lambda_{0}
. Again, since both modes behave identically and independently, this allows us to drop the indices
i
and work with
{\mathcal{A}}={\mathcal{A}}_{1}
. The expressions for the mode 2 can then be easily obtained but one has to keep in mind the difference of behavior since
{\mathcal{A}}_{1}
behaves exponentially while
{\mathcal{A}}_{2}
remains bounded as it oscillates and crosses 0 periodically. Again we stress that
H(u_{0},u)
remains well-defined even if
{\cal A}
crosses 0 (see Appendix
C
for a general discussion on this issue).

Since Eq. (
2.10
) rewrites
\dot{\zeta}{\cal A}-\dot{\cal A}\zeta=p
for all
u
, it again implies that

\dot{\zeta}_{0}{{\mathcal{A}}_{0}}-\zeta_{0}\dot{{\mathcal{A}}}_{0}=p\,.

(2.45)

Then, the relation (
2.8
) gives

\displaystyle\zeta(u)

\displaystyle=p\frac{\sinh{[\lambda_{0}(u-u_{0})]}}{\lambda_{0}{\mathcal{A}}_{0}}-{\mathcal{B}}{\mathcal{A}}(u)\,.

(2.46)

Since it shall hold for all
u<u_{0}
, it implies that the constants of motion
(p,{\mathcal{B}})
are related to the initial conditions in
u_{0}
by

\displaystyle\zeta_{0}=-{\mathcal{B}}{\mathcal{A}}_{0}\,,\qquad\dot{\zeta}_{0}=\frac{p}{{\mathcal{A}}_{0}}-{\mathcal{B}}\dot{{\mathcal{A}}}_{0}\,,\qquad\ddot{\zeta}_{0}=-{\mathcal{B}}\ddot{{\mathcal{A}}}_{0}\,,

(2.47)

which are indeed compatible with Eq. (
2.45
). Interestingly they take the same form as for a pulse.

Now, we perform the same analysis in the future asymptotic regime for
u>u_{f}
, in which
H_{+}:=\lambda^{2}_{f}>0
. It follows that
({\mathcal{A}}_{1}(u),{\mathcal{A}}_{2}(u))
are given by

\displaystyle{\mathcal{A}}_{1}(u)

\displaystyle={\mathcal{A}}_{f}\cosh\left[\lambda_{f}(u-u_{f})\right]+\frac{\dot{{\mathcal{A}}}_{f}}{\lambda_{f}}\sinh\left[\lambda_{f}(u-u_{f})\right]

(2.48)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{A}}_{2}(u)

\displaystyle={\mathcal{A}}_{f}\cos\left[\lambda_{f}(u-u_{f})\right]+\frac{\dot{{\mathcal{A}}}_{f}}{\lambda_{f}}\sin\left[\lambda_{f}(u-u_{f})\right]\,.

(2.49)

. This illustrates the existence of a VM that is in general not bounded. Note that even if
H_{+}
is discontinuous,
\zeta

\dot{\zeta}
remain continuous.

As previously, it is clear that

{\mathcal{A}}^{+}_{1}\sim\frac{1}{2}\left({\mathcal{A}}_{f}+\frac{\dot{\mathcal{A}}_{f}}{\lambda_{f}}\right)\hbox{e}^{+\lambda_{f}(u-u_{f})}\,\qquad\dot{{\mathcal{A}}}^{+}_{1}=\lambda_{f}A^{+}_{1}\,\qquad\ddot{{\mathcal{A}}}^{+}_{1}=\lambda_{f}^{2}A^{+}_{1}\,,

(2.50)

so that, obviously, unless
{\mathcal{A}}_{f}+\frac{\dot{{\mathcal{A}}}_{f}}{\lambda_{f}}=0
,
{\mathcal{A}}_{1}(u)
is exponentially diverging when
u\gg u_{f}
and
{\mathcal{A}}_{2}(u)
is either oscillating or strictly vanishing. In this regime, the Souriau matrix (
2.9
) becomes

\displaystyle H^{ii}(u_{0},u)

\displaystyle=\int^{u_{f}}_{u_{0}}\frac{\textrm{d}v}{{\mathcal{A}}_{i}^{2}}+\int^{u}_{u_{0}}\frac{\textrm{d}v}{{\mathcal{A}}_{i}^{2}}=H_{0f}+\frac{1}{\lambda_{f}{\mathcal{A}}_{i}(u){\mathcal{A}}_{i}(u_{f})}\times\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\sinh{[\lambda_{f}(u-u_{f})]}&i=1\\
\;\;\sin{[\lambda_{f}(u-u_{f})]}&i=2\end{array}\right.\,.

We conclude that the evolution of
\zeta(u)
in this regime is given by

\displaystyle\zeta(u)

\displaystyle=p\left\{{\mathcal{A}}(u)H_{0f}+\frac{\sinh{[\lambda_{f}(u-u_{f})]}}{\lambda_{f}{\mathcal{A}}_{f}}\right\}-{\mathcal{B}}{\mathcal{A}}(u)

(2.53)

These solutions, depicted on Fig.
5
allow us to discuss the conditions for the different memories. The first condition to impose is that the relative acceleration vanishes, i.e. that
\ddot{\zeta}=0
in the asymptotic regimes. Contrary to a pulse profile, this condition is in general not met as
\ddot{\zeta}
diverges exponentially unless it is tuned. Therefore, the first task is to compute the leading contribution to the relative acceleration and find the conditions such that it vanishes.

In the asymptotic past, i.e. when
u<u_{0}
, the solution (
2.46
) implies, using Eq. (
2.47
), that

\ddot{\zeta}(u<u_{0})=\frac{p\lambda_{0}}{{\mathcal{A}}_{0}}\sinh{[\lambda_{0}(u-u_{0})]}-{\mathcal{B}}\lambda_{0}^{2}{\mathcal{A}}(u<u_{0})

(2.54)

so that

\displaystyle\ddot{\zeta}_{-}=\lim_{u\to-\infty}\ddot{\zeta}(u)

\displaystyle\simeq-\frac{\lambda_{0}}{2}\left[\frac{p}{{\mathcal{A}}_{0}}+\lambda_{0}{\mathcal{B}}\left({\mathcal{A}}_{0}-\frac{\dot{{\mathcal{A}}}_{0}}{\lambda_{0}}\right)\right]\hbox{e}^{-\lambda_{0}(u-u_{0})}\quad\text{when}\quad u\rightarrow-\infty\,.

(2.55)

Therefore, the only possibility to kill this divergence in relative acceleration is either to set
\lambda_{0}=0
(to be back to the pulse situation), or to enforce the condition

(\dot{{\mathcal{A}}}_{0}-\lambda_{0}{\mathcal{A}}_{0}){\mathcal{B}}=\frac{p}{{\mathcal{A}}_{0}}\,.

(2.56)

Similarly, at late time when
u>u_{f}
, the leading contribution to the relative acceleration reads

\displaystyle\ddot{\zeta}_{+}=\lim_{u\to+\infty}\ddot{\zeta}\simeq\frac{\lambda_{f}}{2}\left[\frac{p}{{\mathcal{A}}_{f}}+\lambda_{f}(H_{0f}p-{\mathcal{B}})\left({\mathcal{A}}_{f}+\frac{\dot{{\mathcal{A}}}_{f}}{\lambda_{f}}\right)\right]\hbox{e}^{\lambda_{f}(u-u_{f})}\quad\text{when}\quad u\rightarrow+\infty\,.

(2.57)

Demanding that
\ddot{\zeta}=0
when
u\gg u_{f}
requires that either
\lambda_{f}=0
or that the condition

(\dot{{\mathcal{A}}}_{f}+\lambda_{f}{\mathcal{A}}_{f}){\mathcal{B}}=p\left[H_{0}f\left(\dot{{\mathcal{A}}}_{f}+\lambda_{f}{\mathcal{A}}_{f}\right)+\frac{1}{{\mathcal{A}}_{f}}\right]

(2.58)

holds. Indeed, the previous case of pulse profiles is recovered when
\lambda_{0}=\lambda_{f}=0
which explains that no further conditions had to be imposed. For step profiles at least one of the two
\lambda
shall be non-vanishing. Note also that this discussion assumes that the branching between the asymptotic regions is
\lambda_{0}>0\rightarrow\lambda_{f}>0
in which case
\Delta\ddot{\zeta}_{1}
diverges unless Eqs. (
2.56
) and (
2.58
) hold while
\Delta\ddot{\zeta}_{2}
is bounded and oscillating. Indeed one can also consider step profiles such that
\lambda_{0}<0\rightarrow\lambda_{f}>0
or
\lambda_{0}>0\rightarrow\lambda_{f}<0
in which case
\Delta\ddot{\zeta}_{1}
oscillates in the past, resp. in the future, and for which one needs to impose (
2.58
) in the future, resp. (
2.56
) in the past.

Our goal is not to discuss all these situations and we restrict ourselves to the more natural choice, the one of a step profile that satisfies
\lambda_{0}=0
and
\lambda_{f}>0
free, so that we only need to impose the condition (
2.58
) in the future asymptotic region. Examples of such step profiles and the associated
({\mathcal{A}}_{1},{\mathcal{A}}_{2})
are depicted on Fig.
4
. The associated profiles for the displacement and velocity for generic initial conditions are shown on Fig.
5
, which illustrates the generic exponential divergence at late time for
{\mathcal{A}}_{1}
and the oscillations of
{\mathcal{A}}_{2}
.

Once the condition (
2.58
) is imposed ensuring we have selected a configuration in which the asymptotic relative acceleration vanishes, one has to compute the asymptotic behavior of the relative velocities. Thanks to Eqs. (
2.42
) and (
2.50
) together with Eqs. (
2.46
) and (
2.53
), one obtains

\displaystyle\dot{\zeta}_{+}=\lim_{u\to+\infty}\dot{\zeta}(u)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left[\frac{p}{{\mathcal{A}}_{f}}+\lambda_{f}(pH_{0f}-{\mathcal{B}})\left({\mathcal{A}}_{f}+\frac{\dot{{\mathcal{A}}}_{f}}{\lambda_{f}}\right)\right]\hbox{e}^{\lambda_{f}(u-u_{f})}=0

(2.59)

\displaystyle\dot{\zeta}_{-}=\lim_{u\to-\infty}\dot{\zeta}(u)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left[\frac{p}{{\mathcal{A}}_{0}}+{\mathcal{B}}\left(\lambda_{0}{\mathcal{A}}_{0}-\dot{{\mathcal{A}}}_{0}\right)\right]\hbox{e}^{\lambda_{0}(u-u_{0})}=\frac{1}{2}\left[\frac{p}{{\mathcal{A}}_{0}}-{\mathcal{B}}\dot{{\mathcal{A}}}_{0}\right]

(2.60)

where we have used the condition (
2.58
) for the first limit and
\lambda_{0}=0
for the second. We conclude that in general the asymptotic velocity does vanish in the after zone provided Eq. (
2.58
) holds even if the asymptotic velocity is non-vanishing in the past asymptotic region.

From the classification of §
2.2
, we thus have the following three possibilities.

VM
: Without any further restrictions than
\lambda_{0}=0
and the condition (
2.58
), a wave with a step profile generically leads to a rather surprising velocity memory effect where
\dot{\zeta}_{-}\neq 0
while
\dot{\zeta}_{+}=0
. Thus the effect of the wave is to stop the relative motion by cancelling the relative velocity between the two particles.

VM0
: It occurs when one further has
\dot{\zeta}_{-}=0
, which requires

\frac{p}{{\mathcal{A}}_{0}}={\mathcal{B}}\dot{{\mathcal{A}}}_{0}\,.

(2.61)

This means that a VM0 requires that
(p,{\mathcal{B}})
satisfy both Eq. (
2.58
) and (
2.61
). This requires that the geometry be such that

\frac{1}{{\mathcal{A}}_{0}\dot{\mathcal{A}}_{0}}=H_{0f}+\frac{1}{{\mathcal{A}}_{f}(\dot{\mathcal{A}}_{f}+\lambda_{f}{\mathcal{A}}_{f})},

(2.62)

which is a non-trivial property of the spacetime geometry. Then, note that one can further impose
\zeta_{+}=\zeta_{-}
which, thanks to the condition (
2.12
), requires that

\dot{{\mathcal{A}}}_{+}=\dot{{\mathcal{A}}}_{-}

(2.63)

at all time.

DM
: A displacement memory effect requires the relative velocity to vanish in both asymptotic regimes, i.e.
\dot{\zeta}_{+}=\dot{\zeta}_{-}=0
while the relative distance is shifted, i.e.
\zeta_{+}\neq\zeta_{-}
. Thanks to the condition (
2.12
), this implies that one needs

\dot{{\mathcal{A}}}_{+}\neq\dot{{\mathcal{A}}}_{-}\,

(2.64)

together with the constraint (
2.62
).

This concludes the classification of the different memories. Figure
6
compares a generic solution to the case in which the constraints (
2.58
) is imposed, hence leading to a VM. Note that this discussions holds for the class of step profiles that interpolates from
\lambda_{0}=0
to
\lambda_{f}>0
. To finish let us mention that if the step interpolates between
\lambda_{0}>0
and
\lambda_{f}>0
then the condition for a finite
\Delta\dot{\zeta}
implies that
\Delta\dot{\zeta}=0
but also that
\Delta\zeta=0
asymptotically.

Geodesic deviation, symmetries and memories

Now, we consider the geodesic deviation equation (GDE) and show how the symmetries and their generators are related to its solutions.

To recall the construction of the GDE, consider a congruence of geodesics
X^{\mu}(\tau,\sigma)
parametrized by
(\tau,\sigma)
. The first parameter
\tau
stands for the proper time or affine parameter along each curve while
\sigma
parametrizes the different curves. For curves which are very close to each other, we fix a central geodesic denoted
X^{\alpha}(\tau):=x^{\alpha}(\tau,0)
and follow the small deviations w.r.t. to that reference geodesic. This is done by expanding the position of the curve at fixed
\tau
around
\sigma=0
. The position of the nearby curves is given by

\displaystyle\Delta X^{\mu}(\tau,\sigma)

\displaystyle=X^{\mu}(\tau,\sigma)-X^{\mu}(\tau,0)

\displaystyle=\zeta^{\mu}(\tau,\sigma)-\bar{\Gamma}^{\mu}{}_{\alpha\beta}\zeta^{\alpha}(\tau,\sigma)\zeta^{\beta}(\tau,\sigma)+\mathrm{O}(\sigma^{3})

(3.1)

where a tilde means that the quantity is evaluated at
\sigma=0
, i.e. on the central geodesic. Here,
\zeta^{\mu}
is the deviation vector which takes the form

\zeta^{\mu}=\sigma N^{\mu}+{\mathcal{O}}(\sigma^{2})\,.

(3.2)

Since by construction,
\bar{\Gamma}^{\mu}{}_{\alpha\beta}=0
in the adapted Fermi coordinates, the vector
\zeta^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}
encodes the relative distance between the two nearby geodesics of the congruences.
Neglecting second order corrections, one recovers the standard geodesic deviation equation

\displaystyle\frac{D^{2}N^{\mu}}{\textrm{d}\tau^{2}}

\displaystyle=\bar{R}^{\mu}{}_{\alpha\beta\gamma}\bar{u}^{\alpha}\bar{u}^{\beta}N^{\gamma}

(3.3)

where
D/\textrm{d}\tau=u^{\alpha}\nabla_{\alpha}
. This equation admits eight possible solutions. Let us briefly review how to decompose and to integrate this equation when symmetries are present.

Integrability of the geodesic deviation equation

Decomposing the deviation in a longitudinal contribution
N_{\|}
, i.e. along
\bar{u}^{\alpha}\partial_{\alpha}
, and a contribution
N_{\perp}
orthogonal to the
4
-velocity, such that

N=N_{\|}+N_{\perp}\qquad N_{\|}=f(\tau)\bar{u}

(3.4)

and inserting this decomposition in the GDE, one finds that
f(\tau)
is an affine function, i.e.

f(\tau)=C_{1}\tau+C_{2}\,.

(3.5)

These two parameters are related to the freedom to rescale and translate the affine parameter (or proper time) of the reference geodesic, i.e. to the symmetry
\tau\rightarrow\tau+\epsilon
and
\tau\rightarrow\epsilon\tau
. With these solutions, one recovers the two trivial solutions to the GDE given by
N^{\mu}=\{\bar{u}^{\mu},\tau\bar{u}^{\mu}\}
. We thus focus on this six remaining solutions. The transverse contribution
N_{\perp}
satisfies

\displaystyle\frac{D^{2}N^{\mu}_{\perp}}{\textrm{d}\tau^{2}}

\displaystyle=\bar{R}^{\mu}{}_{\alpha\beta\gamma}\bar{u}^{\alpha}\bar{u}^{\beta}N^{\gamma}_{\perp}\,.

(3.6)

Solving this equation in a general spacetime turns out to be a difficult task. Nevertheless, exact analytical solutions can be constructed when i) the geodesic equation can be solved analytically too and ii) when the spacetime geometry admits non-trivial Killing tensors.

Indeed, as first shown in Refs.
[
52
,
53
,
55
]
and emphasized in Ref.
[
56
]
, the existence of a rank-
p
affine tensor defined as

\nabla_{(\mu}K_{\alpha_{1}...\alpha_{p})}=h_{(\alpha_{1}...\alpha_{p})}\qquad\nabla_{\rho}h_{(\alpha_{1}...\alpha_{p})}=0

(3.7)

implies that

N^{\mu}=\bar{K}^{\mu}{}_{\alpha_{1}...\alpha_{p}}\bar{u}^{\alpha_{1}}....\bar{u}^{\alpha_{p}}

(3.8)

is a solution of Eq. (
3.3
). In general, the space of rank-
p
affine tensors, i.e. satisfying
\nabla_{\rho}\nabla_{(\mu}J_{\alpha_{1}...\alpha_{p})}=0
, is larger than the space of rank-
p
Killing tensors but a full characterization does not exist. Yet, when focusing on rank-
2
tensor, such a classification can be done. It follows that except for Petrov type N geometry, affine and Killing rank-
2
tensors are the very same object
[
54
]
. For Petrov type N which possesses a covariantly constant null vector
\ell_{\mu}\textrm{d}x^{\mu}=\textrm{d}\Phi
, i.e.
\nabla_{\mu}\ell_{\nu}=0
, any affine tensor can be decomposed into a Killing tensor
K_{\mu\nu}
, satisfying
\nabla_{(\alpha}K_{\mu\nu)}=0
, and a contribution coming from the covariantly constant null vector such that any rank-2 affine tensor
M_{\mu\nu}
can be decomposed as

M_{\mu\nu}=K_{\mu\nu}+\beta\Phi\ell_{(\mu}\ell_{\nu)}

(3.9)

thus providing a deformation of the solution based on the Killing tensor alone.

When such a geometrical object is present, it is straightforward to see that the associated conserved charge for the geodesic motion corresponds to the projection of the deviation vector at first order, i.e.
N^{\mu}
, along the tangent vector of the geodesic congruence

M=\bar{M}^{\mu}{}_{\alpha_{1}...\alpha_{p}}\bar{u}^{\alpha_{1}}....\bar{u}^{\alpha_{p}}\bar{u}_{\mu}=N^{\mu}\bar{u}_{\mu}=f(\tau)

(3.10)

When
\beta=0
, the affine tensor
M_{\mu\nu}
reduces to the Killing tensor
K_{\mu\nu}
. From the expression (
3.10
), one could misleadingly conclude that
f(\tau)
coincides with the Killing tensor conserved charge, such that it reduces to a constant, but it is not the case. Indeed, the evaluation of the above quantities on the reference geodesic truncates the Killing tensor and as we shall see, this scalar product is in general linear in the proper time, as expected from Eq. (
3.5
).

To summarize, if
\xi^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}
is a Killing vector and
K_{\mu\nu}\textrm{d}x^{\mu}\textrm{d}x^{\nu}
is a Killing tensor for the geometry, the GDE admits the following solutions

N^{\mu}=\{\bar{\xi}^{\mu},\bar{K}^{\mu}{}_{\nu}\bar{u}^{\nu}\}

(3.11)

where a bar corresponds to evaluate the object on the reference geodesic, i.e. imposing (
1.68
). Using this result, we now show how the different symmetries uncovered in the first section are related to the different memory effects.

Killing vectors and transverse memory

Consider first one of the non-trivial Killing vector (
1.26
) generating the Carrolian boost, say
B^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}
. In the Fermi coordinates, it is given by

\displaystyle B^{I}\partial_{I}

\displaystyle=b_{k}E^{A}{}_{i}H^{ki}\partial_{A}-b_{k}\left[E^{k}{}_{A}-\frac{1}{2}\dot{A}_{ij}H^{ki}E^{j}{}_{A}\right]X^{A}\partial_{v}

(3.12)

where
b_{k}
is the symmetry parameter.
Evaluating this vector on the reference geodesic, i.e imposing (
1.68
), one finds

\displaystyle\bar{B}^{I}\partial_{I}

\displaystyle=b_{k}E^{A}{}_{i}H^{ki}\partial_{A}\,.

(3.13)

Let us now check that it provides indeed a solution to the GDE. In the Fermi coordinates, the GDE reads

\displaystyle\ddot{\zeta}_{A}

\displaystyle=R_{AUUB}\zeta^{B}

(3.14)

\displaystyle=R_{iuuj}E^{i}{}_{A}E^{j}{}_{B}\zeta^{B}

(3.15)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\ddot{A}_{ij}-\frac{1}{2}A^{km}\dot{A}_{ki}\dot{A}_{mj}\right)E^{i}{}_{A}E^{j}{}_{B}\zeta^{B}\,.

(3.16)

Using the solution
\zeta^{A}=\bar{B}^{A}
and the parallel transport condition (
1.86
), one easily shows that

\ddot{\bar{B}}^{A}=b_{k}\ddot{E}^{A}{}_{i}H^{ki}\,.

(3.17)

Plugging this in the previous equation, it reduces to

\delta_{AB}\ddot{E}^{B}{}_{m}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\ddot{A}_{im}-\frac{1}{2}A^{kn}\dot{A}_{ki}\dot{A}_{nm}\right)E^{i}{}_{A}

(3.18)

which is nothing else than the time derivative of the parallel transport condition (
1.86
). Therefore, the Carrolian boosts
(\bar{B}_{+},\bar{B}_{-})
encode the non-trivial solutions to the GDE corresponding to the transverse memory effects in the presence of a non-vanishing initial velocity between the two test particles. Indeed, the solution (
3.13
) reproduces the first contribution in Eq. (
2.2
). Thus, the choice of symmetry parameter
b_{k}
for the boosts corresponds to the choice of momenta
p_{i}
in (
2.2
).

The very same exercise can be reproduced using instead the translational Killing vectors given by
P^{\alpha}\partial_{\alpha}=\chi^{i}\partial_{i}
where
\chi^{i}=(\chi^{x},\chi^{y})
is a constant vector. Evaluated on the reference geodesic, it reads

\bar{P}^{I}\partial_{I}=E^{A}{}_{i}(\tau)\chi^{i}\partial_{A}

(3.19)

such that
\ddot{\bar{P}}^{A}=\ddot{E}^{A}{}_{i}\chi^{i}
. Plugging this into the GDE, one finds again that the equation reduces to Eq. (
3.18
). One recognizes in this solution the second term in (
2.2
). The choice of parameter
\chi^{i}
thus corresponds to a choice of charge
{\mathcal{B}}^{i}
in (
2.2
).

Therefore, the Killing symmetries of the geometry naturally carry the relevant information on the different contributions to the memory effects in the transverse direction. In particular, the gauge parameters
(b_{i},\chi^{i})
can be related to the different parameters
p^{Y}_{i},{\mathcal{B}}^{i}_{Y}
(i.e. the initial conditions) which set up the initial configuration of the relative motion between the two test particles.

Killing tensors and the longitudinal rescaling

Let us now discuss how to the trivial solutions
\{\bar{u},\tau\bar{u}\}
to the GDE can be related to the hidden symmetries of the wave. The first trivial solution can be constructed using the CZS theorem stated above by considering the metric as a trivial Killing tensor. Then, the GDE admits the trivial solution given by
N_{\alpha}=\bar{g}_{\alpha\beta}\bar{u}^{\beta}=\bar{u}_{\alpha}
.

The second trivial solution can be related to the non-trivial Killing tensor (
1.33
). At the level of the geodesic motion, this KT encodes the conformal extension of the symmetry group, and it is thus natural that it provides the object allowing to construct the trivial solution to the GDE associated to the freedom to rescale the affine parameter of the reference geodesic. To see this, we first evaluate the KT on the reference geodesic, which reduces in the Fermi coordinates to

\displaystyle\bar{K}_{IJ}\textrm{d}X^{I}\textrm{d}X^{J}=-U\left[2\textrm{d}U\textrm{d}V+\delta_{AB}\textrm{d}X^{A}\textrm{d}X^{B}\right]\,.

(3.20)

The solution to the GDE is given by

N_{I}\textrm{d}X^{I}=\bar{K}_{IJ}\bar{u}^{J}\textrm{d}X^{I}=-U\textrm{d}V\,.

(3.21)

This is nothing else than the
4
-velocity of the reference geodesic written in Fermi coordinates rescaled by a factor
-U
. Indeed, transforming this co-vector in the BJR coordinates, one finds

\displaystyle N_{\alpha}\textrm{d}x^{\alpha}=\frac{\partial X^{I}}{\partial x^{\beta}}N_{I}\textrm{d}x^{\beta}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial V}{\partial v}N_{V}\textrm{d}v+\frac{\partial V}{\partial u}N_{V}\textrm{d}u=-u\textrm{d}v

(3.22)

where we recognize in the parenthesis the expression (
1.65
). Therefore, we have

N_{\alpha}\textrm{d}x^{\alpha}=-\tau\bar{u}_{\alpha}\textrm{d}x^{\alpha}

(3.23)

where we have used that
U=\tau
. As expected, it corresponds to one of the trivial solution to the geodesic deviation equation labelled in (
3.5
) by the parameter
C_{1}
. This trivial solution corresponds to the freedom to rescale the affine parameter or proper time of the reference geodesic.

Discussion

In this work, have shown that, additionally to the well-known velocity memory, a vacuum gravitational plane wave can also induce a displacement memory on a couple of test particles. The main result of this work is the detailed classification of the conditions under which velocity or displacement memory effects are realized. These conditions have been identified by first solving analytically the parallel transport equation (
1.86
) for the polarized wave, providing a starting point for the analysis of the geodesic deviation and its general solution given by Eq. (
2.8
).

We have shown that one has to distinguish between pulse and step profiles in order to properly analyze the conditions for the realization of the different memories. We have focused from the start on the configuration where the relative acceleration
\ddot{\zeta}
asymptotically vanishes. Then, we have introduced a distinction between i) a velocity memory (VM) effect corresponding to a constant shift in the relative velocity, ii) the case where one has no change in the asymptotic relative velocity (VM0) and iii) a constant displacement memory (DM) effect which corresponds to a constant shift in the asymptotic relative distance between the two particles. Notice that in the case of a VM0, one can find configurations where there is no DM but the wave still induce a change in the particles positions, for example by switching their position. This is why one has to distinguish this case from the others.

We have found that pulse profiles generically enjoy a constant VM. The VM0 is realized if the condition (
2.31
) is satisfied which corresponds to the following 3 situations:

1.

whatever the waves profile if
\dot{\mathcal{A}}_{f}-\dot{\mathcal{A}}_{0}\not=0
and if
({\mathcal{B}},p)
are related by Eq. (
2.32
). This case is illustrated in Fig

;

2.

for

and all

if the waves profile is such that

;

3.

for all

when the wave profile satisfies the condition (
2.38
).

The DM is realized if condition (
2.35
) is satisfied which corresponds in turn to the following three cases:

1.

If
\dot{\mathcal{A}}_{f}\not=0
, then a pure DM requires
p
and
{\mathcal{B}}
to be related by Eq. (
2.36
).
Since
{\mathcal{B}}\not=0
, the DM is possible if and only if

2.

If
\dot{\mathcal{A}}_{f}=0
then, whatever the wave profile and whatever
{\mathcal{B}}
, one has a DM if
p=0
. If
p\neq 0
, then a DM occurs if and only if Eq. (

This set of conditions provides the main result of our work and a first concrete classification for the memories to occur.

For step profiles characterized by the two constants

Additionally to the derivation of the analytic conditions for the realization of the different memories, we have provided explicit examples of wave profiles satisfying the different conditions, illustrating the consistency of our classification. In the end, our results provide a clean understanding, both analytical and numerical, of the displacement and velocity memory effects in a vacuum gravitational plane wave. In particular, it allows one to understand the recent finding presented in Ref.

To finish, we have presented two additional results on the physics of vacuum gravitational plane waves. First, we have shown that the vacuum Einstein equations (

This provides a clean picture of the equivalence between the geodesic deviation equation and the geodesic equation in a vacuum gravitational plane wave from the point of view of its underlying symmetries. This approach can be extended to more complicated radiative spacetimes to analyze the memories, a task which we plan to work out in the near future. Appendix A
Christoffels and curvature

The matricial form of the metric is given by

\displaystyle g_{\mu\nu}=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}0&1&0\\
1&0&0\\
0&0&A_{ij}\end{array}\right)\qquad g^{\mu\nu}=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}0&1&0\\
1&0&0\\
0&0&A^{ij}\end{array}\right)

such that the inverse metric reads

\textrm{d}s^{2}=2\partial_{u}\partial_{v}+A^{ij}\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\,.

(A.7)

The non vanishing Christoffel symbols are not modified and are given by

\displaystyle\Gamma^{v}{}_{ij}=-\frac{1}{2}\dot{A}_{ij}\;\qquad\Gamma^{i}{}_{uj}=\frac{1}{2}A^{i\ell}\dot{A}_{j\ell}\,.

(A.8)

These formulas are coherent with mathematica.
Now, let us recall that

\dot{A}^{ik}A_{kj}=-A^{ik}\dot{A}_{kj}\qquad\dot{A}_{\ell j}=-A_{i\ell}A_{kj}\dot{A}^{ik}\,.

(A.9)

We can compute the Riemann and Ricci tensors. They are given by

\displaystyle R^{\alpha}{}_{\beta\gamma\delta}=\partial_{\gamma}\Gamma^{\alpha}{}_{\beta\delta}-\partial_{\delta}\Gamma^{\alpha}{}_{\beta\gamma}+\Gamma^{\alpha}{}_{\mu\gamma}\Gamma^{\mu}{}_{\beta\delta}-\Gamma^{\alpha}{}_{\mu\delta}\Gamma^{\mu}{}_{\beta\gamma}\,.

(A.10)

Since one has
\Gamma^{u}{}_{\beta\gamma}=0
and
\Gamma^{\alpha}{}_{v\gamma}=0
, we have that
R^{u}{}_{\beta\gamma\delta}=0
and
R^{\alpha}{}_{\beta\gamma v}=0
. Now, we list the non-vanishing components which will be useful for the geodesic deviation equation.

First, we find that the only non-vanishing component of
R^{i}{}_{\beta\gamma j}
and
R^{i}{}_{\beta\gamma u}
are

\displaystyle R^{i}{}_{uuj}=-R^{i}{}_{uju}=\dot{\Gamma}^{i}{}_{uj}+\Gamma^{i}{}_{ku}\Gamma^{k}{}_{uj}-\Gamma^{i}{}_{\mu j}\Gamma^{\mu}{}_{uu}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\partial_{u}(A^{i\ell}\dot{A}_{\ell j})+\frac{1}{4}A^{i\ell}A^{km}\dot{A}_{k\ell}\dot{A}_{mj}

(A.11)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}A^{i\ell}\ddot{A}_{\ell j}+\frac{1}{2}\dot{A}^{i\ell}\dot{A}_{\ell j}+\frac{1}{4}A^{i\ell}A^{km}\dot{A}_{k\ell}\dot{A}_{mj}

(A.12)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}A^{i\ell}\ddot{A}_{j\ell}-\frac{1}{4}A^{i\ell}A^{km}\dot{A}_{k\ell}\dot{A}_{mj}

(A.13)

\displaystyle=-R^{i}{}_{uju}

(A.14)

while one has

\displaystyle R^{v}{}_{jui}

\displaystyle=\partial_{u}\Gamma^{v}{}_{ij}-\Gamma^{v}{}_{ki}\Gamma^{k}{}_{jk}=-\frac{1}{2}\left[\ddot{A}_{ij}-\frac{1}{2}A^{k\ell}\dot{A}_{ki}\dot{A}_{j\ell}\right]\,.

(A.15)

This gives us

\displaystyle R_{ujui}=g_{uv}R^{v}{}_{jui}=-\frac{1}{2}\left[\ddot{A}_{ij}-\frac{1}{2}A^{k\ell}\dot{A}_{ki}\dot{A}_{j\ell}\right],,\qquad R_{iuuj}=g_{im}R^{m}{}_{uuj}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\ddot{A}_{ij}-\frac{1}{2}A^{k\ell}\dot{A}_{ki}\dot{A}_{nj}\right)\,.

(A.16)

It follows that the only non-vanishing component of the Ricci tensor reads

\displaystyle R_{\beta\delta}=g^{\mu\gamma}R_{\mu\beta\gamma\delta}\,,\qquad R_{uu}=g^{\mu\gamma}R_{\mu uu\gamma}=A^{ij}R_{iuuj}=\frac{1}{2}\left(A^{ij}\ddot{A}_{ij}-\frac{1}{2}A^{ij}A^{k\ell}\dot{A}_{ki}\dot{A}_{nj}\right)\,.

(A.17)

and one has obviously that
R=0
.

so that the vacuum Einstein equations reduce to

\frac{\ddot{\cal A}_{1}}{{\cal A}_{1}}+\frac{\ddot{\cal A}_{2}}{{\cal A}_{2}}=0\,.

Then, the evolution of the dyad,
\dot{E}^{i}_{A}=-\frac{1}{2}A^{ik}\dot{A}_{kj}E^{j}_{A}=-\frac{1}{2}A^{ij}\dot{A}_{ij}E^{j}_{A}
,simplifies to

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}u}({R^{\ell}}_{i}E^{i}_{A})=-\frac{1}{2}\tilde{A}^{\ell k}\dot{\tilde{A}}_{kp}({R^{p}}_{j}E^{j}_{A})\,.

Hence the vector base after rotation,
\tilde{E}^{i}_{A}\equiv{R^{\ell}}_{i}E^{i}_{A}
, it becomes

\dot{\tilde{E}}^{i}_{A}=-\frac{\dot{\cal A}_{i}}{{\cal A}_{i}}\tilde{E}^{i}_{A}

(B.14)

without summation on
i
,
since

-\frac{1}{2}\tilde{A}^{ij}\dot{\tilde{A}}_{ij}=-\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\frac{\dot{\cal A}_{1}}{{\cal A}_{1}}&0\\
0&\frac{\dot{\cal A}_{2}}{{\cal A}_{2}}\end{array}\right)

which integrates easily as

\tilde{E}^{i}_{A}(u)=\frac{{\cal A}_{i}(u_{0})}{{\cal A}_{i}(u)}\tilde{E}^{i}_{A}(u_{0}).

(B.15)

To finish

H_{AB}=h_{ij}E^{i}_{A}E^{j}_{B}={R_{k}}^{i}\tilde{h}_{k\ell}{R^{\ell}}_{j}E^{i}_{A}E^{j}_{B}\quad\hbox{with}\quad\tilde{h}_{k\ell}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}{\cal A}_{1}\ddot{\cal A}_{1}&0\\
0&{\cal A}_{2}\ddot{\cal A}_{2}\end{array}\right)\,.

(B.16)

Appendix C
Apparent divergences in the algebraic solution (
2.8
) of the equation of motion

In this appendix, we discuss why the divergence in Eq. (
2.8
) is only an apparent divergence without physical meaning. As can be seen from Eq. (
2.8
) the displacement,

\zeta^{i}(u)={\cal A}_{i}(u)\left[H^{ii}(u_{0},u)p_{i}-{\mathcal{B}}^{i}\right]

may seem singular when
{\cal A}_{1}
or
{\cal A}_{2}
changes sign. In particular because of Eq. (
1.93
) one concludes that it shall happen at least for one of the two modes. The term
H^{ii}(u_{0},u)
may diverge when
{\cal A}_{i}
vanishes. But this divergence shall be compensated in a way that
{\cal A}_{i}H^{ii}(u_{0},u)
remains regular since indeed the differential equation
(\ddot{\zeta}_{1},\ddot{\zeta}_{2})=(H_{+}\zeta_{1},-H_{+}\zeta_{2})
is well-behaved as long as
H_{+}
is non-singular.

To show the singularity of the analytic solution is only apparent, let us consider the function

{\cal A}(u)\int_{u_{0}}^{u}\frac{\mathrm{d}v}{{\cal A}^{2}(v)}

in a neighborhood of
u_{*}>u_{0}
where
{\cal A}(u_{*})=0
. Expanding locally
{\cal A}
around
u_{*}
as

{\cal A}(u)=\dot{\cal A}_{*}(u-u_{*})

with
\dot{\cal A}_{*}\not=0
, which shall be the case since otherwise the differential equation (
1.93
) for
{\cal A}
would imply that
{\cal A}_{*},\dot{\cal A}_{*}
and
\ddot{\cal A}_{*}
vanish so that
{\cal A}=0
for all
u
, which we exclude. It follows that

\displaystyle{\cal A}(u)\int_{u_{0}}^{u}\frac{\mathrm{d}v}{{\cal A}^{2}(v)}

\displaystyle={\cal A}(u)\left[\int_{u_{0}}^{u_{*}-\epsilon}\frac{\mathrm{d}v}{{\cal A}^{2}(v)}+\int_{u_{*}-\epsilon}^{u}\frac{\mathrm{d}v}{{\cal A}^{2}(v)}\right]

\displaystyle=\dot{\cal A}_{*}(u-u_{*})\int_{u_{0}}^{u_{*}-\epsilon}\frac{\mathrm{d}v}{{\cal A}^{2}}+\dot{\cal A}_{*}(u-u_{*})\int_{u_{*}-\epsilon}^{u}\frac{\mathrm{d}v}{\dot{\cal A}_{*}^{2}(v-u_{*})^{2}}

\displaystyle=\dot{\cal A}_{*}(u-u_{*})\int_{u_{0}}^{u_{*}-\epsilon}\frac{\mathrm{d}v}{{\cal A}^{2}}-\frac{1}{\dot{\cal A}_{*}}(u-u_{*})\left[\frac{1}{\epsilon}+\frac{1}{u-u_{*}}\right]

\displaystyle=\dot{\cal A}_{*}(u-u_{*})\int_{u_{0}}^{u_{*}-\epsilon}\frac{\mathrm{d}v}{{\cal A}^{2}}-\frac{1}{\dot{\cal A}_{*}}\left[\frac{(u-u_{*})}{\epsilon}+1\right]\,.

(C.1)

Hence

{\cal A}(u_{*})\int_{u_{0}}^{u_{*}}\frac{\mathrm{d}v}{{\cal A}^{2}(v)}=-\frac{1}{\dot{\cal A}_{*}}\,,

(C.2)

which is finite even though
\int_{u_{0}}^{u_{*}}\frac{\mathrm{d}v}{{\cal A}^{2}(v)}
is infinite and
{\cal A}(u_{*})=0
.

Appendix D
Integrability of the geodesic deviation equation

In this appendix, we review the proof of the CZS theorem, showing that for a rank-2 Killing tensor, the vector

N_{\mu}=\bar{K}_{\mu\nu}\bar{u}^{\nu}

(D.1)

is an exact solution to the first order geodesic deviation equation (
3.3
). Here, a bar means that the quantity is evaluated on the reference geodesic.

To prove this result, we will use the definition of the Killing tensor and the commutation rules of the covariant derivative, i.e.

\displaystyle\nabla_{\mu}K_{\nu\alpha}+\nabla_{\nu}K_{\alpha\mu}

\displaystyle=-\nabla_{\alpha}K_{\mu\nu}

(D.2)

\displaystyle[\nabla_{\rho},\nabla_{\alpha}]K_{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=+R_{\mu\lambda\alpha\rho}K^{\lambda}{}_{\nu}+R^{\lambda\nu\alpha\rho}K_{\mu}{}^{\lambda}\,.

(D.3)

Notice here that there is a choice in the definition of the Riemann tensor.
Now, taking the covariant derivative along a vector
u^{\rho}\partial_{\rho}
and projecting along the Killing tensor directions, we have the following equalities

\displaystyle u^{\mu}u^{\nu}u^{\rho}\nabla_{\rho}(\nabla_{\mu}K_{\nu\alpha}+\nabla_{\nu}K_{\alpha\mu})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}2u^{\mu}u^{\nu}u^{\rho}\nabla_{\rho}(\nabla_{\mu}K_{\nu\alpha})&\\
-u^{\mu}u^{\nu}u^{\rho}(\nabla_{\rho}\nabla_{\alpha}K_{\mu\nu})&\end{array}\right.

where the first one is simply using the symmetry in
(\mu\nu)
and the second is the Killing tensor equation. Starting from this, we write

\displaystyle 2u^{\mu}u^{\nu}u^{\rho}\nabla_{\rho}(\nabla_{\mu}K_{\nu\alpha})

\displaystyle=-u^{\mu}u^{\nu}u^{\rho}(\nabla_{\rho}\nabla_{\alpha}K_{\mu\nu})

\displaystyle=-u^{\mu}u^{\nu}u^{\rho}(\nabla_{\alpha}\nabla_{\rho}K_{\mu\nu}+R^{\lambda}{}_{\mu\alpha\rho}K_{\lambda\nu}+R^{\lambda}{}_{\nu\alpha\rho}K_{\mu\lambda})

\displaystyle=-2R^{\lambda}{}_{\nu\alpha\rho}K_{\lambda\mu}u^{\mu}u^{\nu}u^{\rho}-u^{\mu}u^{\nu}u^{\rho}\nabla_{\alpha}\nabla_{\rho}K_{\mu\nu}\,.

(D.6)

In the second line, we have used the commutation relation of the covariant derivative (
D.3
). The last line is obtained using again the appropriate symmetry in the indices. The resulting equality is given by

\displaystyle 2u^{\mu}u^{\nu}u^{\rho}\nabla_{\rho}(\nabla_{\mu}K_{\nu\alpha})+2R^{\lambda}{}_{\nu\alpha\rho}K_{\lambda\mu}u^{\mu}u^{\nu}u^{\rho}=-u^{\mu}u^{\nu}u^{\rho}\nabla_{\alpha}\nabla_{\rho}K_{\mu\nu}\,.

(D.7)

Now, using that
u^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}
is geodesic, i.e.
u^{\mu}\nabla_{\mu}u^{\rho}=0
, we can write

\displaystyle u^{\mu}u^{\rho}\nabla_{\rho}\nabla_{\mu}(K_{\alpha\nu}u^{\nu})+R^{\lambda}{}_{\nu\alpha\rho}(K_{\lambda\mu}u^{\mu})u^{\nu}u^{\rho}=-\frac{1}{2}u^{\mu}u^{\nu}u^{\rho}\nabla_{\alpha}\nabla_{\rho}K_{\mu\nu}\,.

(D.8)

Finally, introducing the vector
N_{\mu}=K_{\mu\nu}u^{\nu}
and symmetrizing the last term, we find

\displaystyle u^{\mu}u^{\rho}\nabla_{\rho}\nabla_{\mu}n_{\alpha}+R^{\lambda}{}_{\nu\alpha\rho}n_{\lambda}u^{\nu}u^{\rho}=-\frac{1}{6}u^{\mu}u^{\nu}u^{\rho}\nabla_{\alpha}\nabla_{(\rho}K_{\mu\nu)}=0

(D.9)

where we have used in the final step the definition of the Killing tensor, i.e.
\nabla_{(\rho}K_{\mu\nu)}=0
. It follows that

\bar{u}^{\mu}\bar{u}^{\rho}\nabla_{\rho}\nabla_{\mu}N_{\alpha}=\bar{R}_{\alpha\rho\nu\lambda}\bar{u}^{\nu}\bar{u}^{\rho}N^{\lambda}

(D.10)

which is the first order geodesic deviation equation.

Appendix E
Longitudinal memory

In this appendix, we complete our classification on the conditions for having a displacement or velocity memory effect by analyzing the longitudinal motion.
Let us consider the motion of the test particles in the direction of the propagation of the wave. Using the coordinates

\displaystyle Z(u)=\frac{1}{2}U(u)+V(u)\,,\qquad T(u)=V(u)-\frac{1}{2}U(u)\,,

(E.1)

it is clear that the longitudinal motion is determined only by the function
V(u)
given by Eq. (
1.81
). Thanks to the expressions (
1.59
) and (
1.56
) for
x^{i}(u)
and
v(u)
, one gets

\displaystyle V(u)

\displaystyle=v_{0}+\frac{\epsilon}{2}(u-u_{0})+\frac{1}{4}\dot{A}_{ij}{\mathcal{B}}^{i}{\mathcal{B}}^{j}+\frac{1}{4}\left[\left(\dot{A}_{ij}H^{ik}H^{j\ell}-2H^{k\ell}\right)p_{\ell}+\frac{1}{2}\dot{A}_{ij}{\mathcal{B}}^{i}H^{jk}\right]p_{k}\,.

(E.2)

Proceeding as in the previous section, we consider two geodesics. The reference one defined by the constants of motion
\bar{p}_{i}=0
and
\bar{{\mathcal{B}}}^{i}=0
admits the following V-trajectory

\bar{V}(u)=\frac{\epsilon}{2}(u-u_{0})

(E.3)

which consists in the standard inertial drift in Minkowski. The initial position and momentum of the second test particle is still labelled by
(p_{i},{\mathcal{B}}^{i})
. The relative longitudinal distance between the two geodesics is given by

\displaystyle\zeta^{Z}

\displaystyle=Z(u)-\bar{Z}(u)=V(u)-\bar{V}(u)

(E.4)

which allows one to compute

\displaystyle\zeta^{Z}

\displaystyle=v_{0}+\frac{1}{4}\dot{A}_{ij}{\mathcal{B}}^{i}{\mathcal{B}}^{j}+\frac{1}{4}\left[\left(\dot{A}_{ij}H^{ik}H^{j\ell}-2H^{k\ell}\right)p_{\ell}+\frac{1}{2}\dot{A}_{ij}{\mathcal{B}}^{i}H^{jk}\right]p_{k}

(E.5)

\displaystyle\dot{\zeta^{Z}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\ddot{A}_{ij}{\mathcal{B}}^{i}{\mathcal{B}}^{j}+\frac{1}{4}\left[\ddot{A}_{ij}\left(H^{ik}H^{j\ell}p_{\ell}+\frac{1}{2}{\mathcal{B}}^{i}H^{jk}\right)+\dot{A}_{ij}(A^{ik}H^{j\ell}+A^{j\ell}H^{ik}+\frac{1}{2}A^{jk}{\mathcal{B}}^{i})\right]p_{k}\,.

(E.6)

It provides the general expressions to analyze the relative longitudinal motion w.r.t. the initial conditions
(p_{i},{\mathcal{B}}^{i})
. In the following, we shall be interested in the case where
p_{i}=0
, which corresponds to the second particle being at rest initially in the 2D transverse plane. Then, the conditions simply reduce to

\displaystyle\zeta^{Z}=v_{0}+\frac{1}{4}\dot{A}_{ij}{\mathcal{B}}^{i}{\mathcal{B}}^{j}\qquad\dot{\zeta}^{Z}=\frac{1}{4}\ddot{A}_{ij}{\mathcal{B}}^{i}{\mathcal{B}}^{j}

(E.7)

From these simple expressions, we obtain

•

a constant longitudinal velocity memory effect provided

\dddot{A}_{ij}(u_{f})=0\qquad\ddot{A}_{ij}(u_{f})\neq 0

(E.8)

•

a constant longitudinal displacement memory effect if

\ddot{A}_{ii}(u_{f})=0\qquad\dot{A}_{ii}(u_{f})\neq 0

(E.9)

•

no longitudinal memory effects if

\ddot{A}_{ii}(u_{f})=0\qquad\dot{A}_{ii}(u_{f})=0

(E.10)

This concludes the discussion on memory effects and the associated conditions on the asymptotic form of the wave profile
A_{ii}
.
