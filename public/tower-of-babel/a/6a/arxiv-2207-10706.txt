From Schwartz space to Mellin transform
Mateusz Krukowski

Abstract
The primary motivation behind this paper is an attempt to provide a thorough explanation of how the Mellin transform arises naturally in a process akin to the construction of the celebrated Gelfand transform. We commence with a study of a class of Schwartz functions 𝒮 ⁡ ( ℝ + ) , \mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+}), where ℝ + \mathbb{R}_{+} is the set of all positive real numbers. Various properties of this Fréchet space are established and what follows is an introduction of the Mellin convolution operator, which turns 𝒮 ⁡ ( ℝ + ) \mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+}) into a commutative Fréchet algebra. We provide a simple proof of Mellin-Young convolution inequality and go on to prove that the structure space Δ ⁡ ( 𝒮 ⁡ ( ℝ + ) , ⋆ ) \Delta(\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+}),\star) (the space of nonzero, linear, continuous and multiplicative functionals m : 𝒮 ⁡ ( ℝ + ) ⟶ ℝ m:\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+})\longrightarrow\mathbb{R} ) is homeomorphic to ℝ . \mathbb{R}. Finally, we show that the Mellin transform arises in a process which bears a striking resemblance to the construction of the Gelfand transform.



Keywords :
Schwartz space, structure space, Mellin transform

Introduction

Mellin transform is a less popular, yet still very well-known twin brother of the celebrated Fourier transform. It is named after the Finnish mathematician Hjalmar Mellin, who introduced it in a paper published in 1897. Although well over a century has passed, the Mellin transform still enjoys an undiminishing attention from the mathematical community. In order to support that claim, let us mention just a couple of examples from the past two decades, which by no means exhaust the vast literature on the subject:

Seeing that the topic of Mellin transform is still an active field of mathematical research, we have decided to contribute by investigating the parallels between the constructions of Gelfand and Mellin transform. Let us summarize the structure of the paper in order to facilitate the comprehension of the “big picture” before delving into technical subtleties.

In Section
2
we define the class of Schwartz functions on positive real numbers. This is the base setting in which the Mellin transform will “be born”. We explain why the Schwartz class is a Fréchet space but not a Banach space (since it is not normable). The section concludes with the introduction of a dense subset of the Schwartz class, which is easier to work with than the whole class itself.

Section
3
focuses on the study of the Mellin transform. The climactic point of this section is a proof that the Schwartz class together with the Mellin transform as multiplication constitutes a Fréchet algebra. What follows is a brief digression on Mellin-Young convolution inequality.

Section
4
is devoted to the structure space of the Schwartz class. This is a space of all nonzero, linear and multiplicative functionals on the Schwartz class, which we equip with the weak* topology. We prove that the structure space is bijective, and later on in fact homeomorphic to
\mathbb{R}
with the standard topology – this is reminiscent of the classical results in standard Gelfand theory.

The final Section
5
serves as an epilogue and capitalizes on previously proven results. We explain how the Mellin transform arises in a similar vein to the construction of the Gelfand transform. At the same time, we stay aware and keep track of the differences between the two transforms. Lastly, the paper concludes with a bibliography.

2
Schwartz functions on positive reals

We commence our study with a definition of a vector space of Schwartz functions on positive real numbers
\mathbb{R}_{+}
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+}):=\bigg\{f\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+})\ :\ \forall_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}\\
\beta\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\end{subarray}}\ \exists_{M_{\alpha,\beta}>0}\ \sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}}\ |x^{\alpha}f^{(\beta)}(x)|<M_{\alpha,\beta}\bigg\},

(1)

where
\mathbb{Z}
and
\mathbb{N}_{0}
stand for integer and natural numbers with
0
, respectively. Our definition is reminiscent of the classical Schwartz class (see Chapter 8.1 in
[
17
]
):

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}):=\bigg\{f\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})\ :\ \forall_{\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\ \exists_{M_{\alpha,\beta}>0}\ \sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\ (1+|x|)^{\alpha}|f^{(\beta)}(x)|<M_{\alpha,\beta}\bigg\}.

(2)

We usually say that the classical Schwartz functions decrease rapidly in
\pm
infinity. Since the functions in
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
are defined on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
, which is “less symmetric” than
\mathbb{R}
, we may say that they are rapidly decreasing both at
0
and
+
infinity. Hence, we use
x^{\alpha},\ \alpha\in\mathbb{Z}
in definition (
1
) rather than
(1+|x|)^{\alpha},\ \alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
as in definition (
2
).

\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
can be turned into a locally convex vector space (see Theorem 1.37 in
[
25
]
, p. 27) by a family of separating seminorms
p_{\alpha,\beta}:\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+})\longrightarrow[0,\infty)
given by

p_{\alpha,\beta}(f):=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}}\ \left|x^{\alpha}f^{(\beta)}(x)\right|.

A family of sets of the form

V_{p_{\alpha_{1},\beta_{1}}}(\varepsilon)\cap\ldots\cap V_{p_{\alpha_{n},\beta_{n}}}(\varepsilon)

for some
(\alpha_{k})_{k=1}^{n}\subset\mathbb{Z},\ (\beta_{k})_{k=1}^{n}\subset\mathbb{N}_{0},\ \varepsilon>0,
where

V_{p_{\alpha_{k},\beta_{k}}}(\varepsilon):=\bigg\{f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+})\ :\ p_{\alpha_{k},\beta_{k}}(f)<\varepsilon\bigg\}

forms a neighbourhood base at zero for the locally convex topology of
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+}).
Furthermore, since
(p_{\alpha,\beta})_{\alpha\in\mathbb{Z},\beta\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
is a countable family, then the topology of
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
is metrizable (see Lemma 5.75 in
[
3
]
, p. 206) by a translation-invariant metric
d_{\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+})}:\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+})\times\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+})\longrightarrow[0,\infty)
given by the formula

d_{\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+})}(f,g):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha\in\mathbb{Z},\\
\beta\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\end{subarray}}\ \frac{2^{-(|\alpha|+|\beta|)}p_{\alpha,\beta}(f-g)}{1+p_{\alpha,\beta}(f-g)}.

In particular this means that a sequence
(f_{m})
converges to
f
in
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
if and only if

\forall_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha\in\mathbb{Z},\\
\beta\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\end{subarray}}\ \lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}\ p_{\alpha,\beta}(f_{m}-f)=0.

This also means that a convergent sequence
(f_{m})
is topologically bounded (see Lemma 5.76 in
[
3
]
, p. 206), because
d_{\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+})}-
boundedness is obvious (recall that in general topological boundedness and
d_{\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+})}-
boundedness need not coincide – see Chapter 1.29 in
[
25
]
, p. 23).

The following result will be very useful in the sequel:

Lemma 1
.

Let
B\subset\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
be a topologically bounded set. For every
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z},\ \beta\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
p\geqslant 1
there exists a constant
M>0
such that

\forall_{f\in B}\ \int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\ \left|x^{\alpha}f^{(\beta)}(x)\right|^{p}\ dx<2M^{p}.

Proof.

Since
B
is topologically bounded set then (see Lemma 5.76 in
[
3
]
, p. 206 or Theorem 1.37(b) in
[
25
]
, p. 28) there exists a constant
M>0
such that

\forall_{f\in B}\ p_{\alpha,\beta}(f)+p_{\alpha+2,\beta}(f)<M.

In particular, for every
f\in B
we have

\displaystyle\forall_{x\in(0,1]}\ x^{\alpha}\left|f^{(\beta)}(x)\right|<M\hskip 11.38092pt\text{and}\hskip 11.38092pt\forall_{x\in[1,\infty)}\ x^{\alpha+2}\left|f^{(\beta)}(x)\right|<M.

In order to see that
M
is the desired constant, we compute

\forall_{f\in B}\ \int_{0}^{\infty}\ \left|x^{\alpha}f^{(\beta)}(x)\right|^{p}\ dx<M^{p}+\int_{1}^{\infty}\ \frac{M^{p}}{x^{2p}}\ dx\leqslant 2M^{p},

which concludes the proof.
∎

Our next goal is to prove that
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
is a Fréchet space. Given what we said above, the only piece of information we are missing is whether
d_{\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+})}
is
complete
or not. In order to solve this puzzle we will need the following lemma:

Lemma 2
.

If the sequence
(f_{n})\subset C^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
converges uniformly to
f
and
(f_{n}^{\prime})
converges uniformly to
g
then
f\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
and
f^{\prime}=g.

Proof.

Since
(f_{n}^{\prime})
is uniformly convergent to
g,
then
g\in C(\mathbb{R}_{+}).
Furthermore, for fixed numbers
x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
h\in(-x,\infty)
we have

\displaystyle\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\ \frac{f_{n}(x+h)-f_{n}(x)}{h}=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\ \frac{\int_{x}^{x+h}\ f_{n}^{\prime}(t)\ dt}{h}=\frac{\int_{x}^{x+h}\ g(t)\ dt}{h}.

Consequently, we get

\displaystyle\lim_{h\rightarrow 0}\ \frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}=\lim_{h\rightarrow 0}\ \frac{\int_{x}^{x+h}\ g(t)\ dt}{h}=\lim_{h\rightarrow 0}\ g(x+h)=g(x),

where we have applied d’Hospital’s rule. This concludes the proof.
∎

Theorem 3
.

(\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+}),d_{\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+})})
is a complete metric space.

Proof.

Suppose that
(f_{n})
is a
d_{\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+})}-
Cauchy sequence. This implies that for every
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}
and
\beta\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
the sequence
(x\mapsto x^{\alpha}f_{n}^{(\beta)}(x))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a Cauchy sequence in the supremum norm (on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
). Consequently, every such sequence is uniformly convergent to some
f_{\alpha,\beta}\in C^{b}(\mathbb{R}_{+}).

We put
f:=f_{0,0},
i.e.,
f
is a uniform limit of the sequence
(f_{n}).
By Lemma
2
(and an elementary induction) we have
f\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
and
f^{(\beta)}=f_{0,\beta}.
Finally, the sequence
(x\mapsto x^{\alpha}f_{n}^{(\beta)}(x))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is (by definition) uniformly convergent to
f_{\alpha,\beta},
so

\forall_{x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}}\ f_{\alpha,\beta}(x)=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\ x^{\alpha}f_{n}^{(\beta)}(x)=x^{\alpha}f^{(\beta)}(x).

This proves that
f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
and concludes the proof.
∎

At this point we have shown that
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
is a Fréchet space. Encouraged by this fact we could hope that
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
is even a normed space but, unfortunately, this is not true and we will use the following result to prove it:

Lemma 4
.

There exists a function
\eta\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
such that

1.

\text{supp}(\eta)=[-1,1],

2.

\eta(t)>0
for every
t\in(-1,1),

3.

\eta(0)=1,

4.

\eta^{\prime}(t)=2(\eta(2t+1)-\eta(2t-1))
for every
t\in\mathbb{R},

5.

for
\beta\in\mathbb{N}
and
t\in[-1,1]
we have

\eta^{(\beta)}(t)=2^{\binom{\beta+1}{2}}\sum_{l=0}^{2^{\beta}}\ (-1)^{s(l)}\eta\left(2^{\beta}t+2^{\beta}-2l-1\right),

where
s(l)
is the sum of the digits of
l
when written in base
2
,

6.

for
\beta\in\mathbb{N}
we have

\displaystyle\eta^{(\beta)}(t_{\beta})=2^{\binom{\beta+1}{2}},

(3)

where
t_{\beta+1}:=2t_{\beta}+1
and
t_{1}:=-\frac{1}{2}.

Proof.

The existence of function
\eta
satisfying points
1. – 4.
stems from Theorem 1 whereas point
5.
stems from Theorem 4 in
[
2
]
.

As far as point
6.
is concerned, we prove it by induction – for
\beta=1
we have

\eta^{\prime}(t_{1})=2(\eta(2t_{1}+1)-\eta(2t_{1}-1))=2\eta(0)=2.

Next, assuming (
3
) holds true for
\beta\in\mathbb{N}
we observe that

\eta^{(\beta+1)}(t_{\beta+1})=2^{\beta+1}(\eta^{(\beta)}(2t_{\beta+1}+1)-\eta^{(\beta)}(2t_{\beta+1}-1))=2^{\beta+1}\eta^{(\beta)}(t_{\beta})=2^{\beta+1}2^{\binom{\beta+1}{2}}=2^{\binom{\beta+2}{2}},

which concludes the proof.
∎

Theorem 5
.

\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
is not normable.

Proof.

By Theorem 1.39 in
[
25
]
, p. 30 (or Theorem 5.77 in
[
3
]
, p. 206) the normability of a topological vector space like
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
is equivalent to the existence of a convex, bounded, open (and nonempty) neighbourhood of the origin. For the sake of contradiction, we suppose that such a neighbourhood exists in
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
. Then it must contain a base set of the form

V:=V_{p_{\alpha_{1},\beta_{1}}}(\varepsilon)\cap\ldots\cap V_{p_{\alpha_{n},\beta_{n}}}(\varepsilon)

for some
(\alpha_{k})_{k=1}^{n}\subset\mathbb{Z},\ (\beta_{k})_{k=1}^{n}\subset\mathbb{N}_{0},\ \varepsilon>0,
where

V_{p_{\alpha_{k},\beta_{k}}}(\varepsilon):=\bigg\{f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+})\ :\ p_{\alpha_{k},\beta_{k}}(f)<\varepsilon\bigg\}.

Without loss of generality, we assume that
\beta_{1}\leqslant\beta_{2}\leqslant\ldots\leqslant\beta_{n}.

Our approach boils down to the construction of a sequence
(f_{m})\subset\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
such that
(f_{m})\subset V
but

\displaystyle\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}\ p_{0,\beta_{n}+2}(f_{m})=\infty.

(4)

First, we define a function
f:\mathbb{R}_{+}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
by the formula
f(x):=\eta(x-2),
where
\eta
is the function from Lemma
4
. In particular, by point
5.
in Lemma
4
we have

\forall_{x\in[1,3]}\ f^{(\beta)}(x)=2^{\binom{\beta+1}{2}}\sum_{l=0}^{2^{\beta}}\ (-1)^{s(l)}f\left(2^{\beta}x+2^{\beta}-2l+1\right).

This leads to the estimate

\displaystyle\forall_{x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}}\ |f^{(\beta)}(x)|\leqslant 2^{\binom{\beta+1}{2}}\left(2^{\beta}+1\right)\|f\|_{\infty}<2^{\binom{\beta+1}{2}+\beta+1}\|f\|_{\infty}.

(5)

Next, we define a sequence of functions
f_{m}:\mathbb{R}_{+}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
by the formula

f_{m}(x):=\frac{\varepsilon}{3^{\max\{|\alpha_{1}|,\ldots,|\alpha_{n}|\}}\cdot 2^{\binom{\beta_{n}+1}{2}+\beta_{n}+1}\cdot\|f\|_{\infty}}\cdot m^{-1}\cdot f\left(m^{\frac{1}{b_{n}+1}}x\right).

Applying the estimate (
5
) we have

\forall_{m\in\mathbb{N}}\ \sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}}\ |f_{m}^{(\beta)}(x)|<\frac{\varepsilon}{3^{\max\{|\alpha_{1}|,\ldots,|\alpha_{n}|\}}\cdot 2^{\binom{\beta_{n}+1}{2}+\beta_{n}+1}}\cdot m^{\frac{\beta}{\beta_{n}+1}-1}\cdot 2^{\binom{\beta+1}{2}+\beta+1}.

Since
\text{supp}(f)=[1,3]
and
x^{\alpha}\leqslant 3^{|\alpha|}
for
x\in[1,3]
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}
we have

\forall_{m\in\mathbb{N}}\ \sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}}\ |x^{\alpha}f_{m}^{(\beta)}(x)|<\varepsilon\cdot\frac{3^{|\alpha|}}{3^{\max\{|\alpha_{1}|,\ldots,|\alpha_{n}|\}}}\cdot\frac{2^{\binom{\beta+1}{2}+\beta+1}}{2^{\binom{\beta_{n}+1}{2}+\beta_{n}+1}}\cdot m^{\frac{\beta}{\beta_{n}+1}-1}.

Consequently, if
\alpha\in\{\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}\}
and
\beta\in\{\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n}\}
then

\forall_{m\in\mathbb{N}}\ \sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}}\ |x^{\alpha}f_{m}^{(\beta)}(x)|<\varepsilon

which means that
(f_{m})\subset V.

We will now prove (
4
) and then explain how it results in nonormability of
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
. By Lemma
4
we have

\forall_{\beta\in\mathbb{N}}\ f^{(\beta)}(t_{\beta}+2)=\eta^{(\beta)}(t_{\beta})\geqslant 2^{\binom{\beta+1}{2}}.

This implies that

\begin{split}\forall_{\beta,m\in\mathbb{N}}\ \sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}}\ |f_{m}^{(\beta)}(x)|&\geqslant f_{m}^{(\beta)}\left(\frac{t_{\beta}+2}{m^{\frac{1}{b_{n}+1}}}\right)\geqslant\frac{\varepsilon}{3^{\max\{|\alpha_{1}|,\ldots,|\alpha_{n}|\}}\cdot 2^{\binom{\beta_{n}+1}{2}+\beta_{n}+1}\cdot\|f\|_{\infty}}\cdot m^{\frac{\beta}{\beta_{n}+1}-1}f^{(\beta)}(t_{\beta}+2)\\
&\geqslant\frac{\varepsilon}{3^{\max\{|\alpha_{1}|,\ldots,|\alpha_{n}|\}}}\cdot\frac{2^{\binom{\beta+1}{2}}}{2^{\binom{\beta_{n}+1}{2}+\beta_{n}+1}}\cdot\frac{m^{\frac{\beta}{\beta_{n}+1}-1}}{\|f\|_{\infty}},\end{split}

which in particular means that

\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}\ \sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}}\ |f_{m}^{\beta_{n}+2}(x)|=\infty.

We are finally ready to finish off the proof. We have just established the existence of a sequence of functions
(f_{m})\subset\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
such that
(f_{m})\subset V
and limit (
4
) holds. This means that
V
is not bounded, since otherwise (by Theorem 5.76 in
[
3
]
, p. 206 or Theorem 1.37(b) in
[
25
]
, p. 28) the seminorm
p_{0,\beta_{n}+2}
would be bounded on the sequence
(f_{m})
. Consequently, we arrive at a contradiction since
V
was supposed to be a subset of a bounded set. This concludes the proof.
∎

Our final objective in this section is to find a dense subset of
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
, which is relatively easier to work with than
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
as a whole. To this end, we will employ the following lemma:

Lemma 6
.

There exists a sequence of functions
(\theta_{n})\subset\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
such that for every
n\in\mathbb{N},\ n\geqslant 2
we have

1.

\text{supp}(\theta_{n})=\left[\frac{1}{n},n+1\right],

2.

\theta_{n}(x)=1
for
\left[\frac{2}{n},n\right],

3.

|\theta_{n}^{(\beta)}(x)|\leqslant n^{\beta}2^{\frac{(\beta+1)\beta}{2}}
for every
x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
\beta\in\mathbb{N}.

Proof.

Let
\theta:[0,1]\longrightarrow[0,1]
be the Fabius function (see
[
15
]
) and define the sequence of functions
(\theta_{n})\subset\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
by

\displaystyle\theta_{n}(x):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}\theta(nx-1)&\text{for }\ x\in\left[\frac{1}{n},\frac{2}{n}\right],\\
1&\text{for }\ x\in\left[\frac{2}{n},n\right],\\
\theta(n+1-x)&\text{for }\ x\in[n,n+1],\\
0&\text{for }\ x\not\in\left[\frac{1}{n},n+1\right].\end{array}\right.

Since
\theta^{\prime}(x)=2\theta(2x)
for
x\in\left[0,\frac{1}{2}\right],
an easy induction shows that for every
\beta\in\mathbb{N}
we have

\forall_{x\in\left[0,\frac{1}{2}\right]}\ |\theta^{(\beta)}(x)|\leqslant 2^{\frac{(\beta+1)\beta}{2}}.

Consequently, we have

\forall_{x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}}\ |\theta_{n}^{(\beta)}(x)|\leqslant n^{\beta}2^{\frac{(\beta+1)\beta}{2}}

which concludes the construction.
∎

Theorem 7
.

The set

\mathcal{S}_{c}(\mathbb{R}_{+}):=\bigg\{f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+})\ :\ \text{supp}(f)\text{ is compact}\bigg\}

is dense in
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+}).

Proof.

Fix
f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+}).
We prove that the sequence of functions
(\theta_{n}\cdot f)
converges to
f
in
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+}),
i.e.,

\forall_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha\in\mathbb{Z},\\
\beta\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\end{subarray}}\ \lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\ p_{\alpha,\beta}(\theta_{n}\cdot f-f)=0.

Firstly, we fix
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}
and
\beta\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Since
f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
then there exists a constant
M_{1}>0
such that

\displaystyle\forall_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\ \forall_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=0,\ldots,\beta\\
x\in\left(0,\frac{2}{n}\right]\end{subarray}}\ |x^{\alpha}f^{(k)}(x)|\leqslant M_{1}x^{\beta+1}\leqslant M_{1}\left(\frac{2}{n}\right)^{\beta+1}.

(6)

Similarly, we argue the existence of a constant
M_{2}>0
such that

\displaystyle\forall_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\ \forall_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=0,\ldots,\beta\\
x\in[n,\infty)\end{subarray}}\ |x^{\alpha}f^{(k)}(x)|\leqslant\frac{M_{2}}{x^{\beta+1}}\leqslant\frac{M_{2}}{n^{\beta+1}}.

(7)

Next, let us note that for every
\beta\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and every
x\in\left[\frac{1}{n},\frac{2}{n}\right]\cup[n,n+1]
we have

\displaystyle(\theta_{n}\cdot f)^{(\beta)}(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{\beta-1}\ \binom{\beta}{k}\theta_{n}^{(\beta-k)}(x)f^{(k)}(x)+f^{(\beta)}(x)

so

\begin{split}|x^{\alpha}(\theta_{n}\cdot f-f)^{(\beta)}(x)|&\leqslant\sum_{k=0}^{\beta-1}\ \binom{\beta}{k}|\theta_{n}^{(\beta-k)}(x)||x^{\alpha}f^{(k)}(x)|\\
&\mathrel{{\mathop{\leqslant}\limits}}\sum_{k=0}^{\beta-1}\ \binom{\beta}{k}n^{\beta-k}2^{\frac{(\beta-k+1)(\beta-k)}{2}}|x^{\alpha}f^{(k)}(x)|\\
&\leqslant n^{\beta}2^{\frac{(\beta+1)\beta}{2}}\sum_{k=0}^{\beta-1}\ \binom{\beta}{k}|x^{\alpha}f^{(k)}(x)|\\
&\mathrel{{\mathop{\leqslant}\limits}}n^{\beta}2^{\frac{(\beta+1)\beta}{2}}\left(M_{1}\left(\frac{2}{n}\right)^{\beta+1}+\frac{M_{2}}{n^{\beta+1}}\right)\sum_{k=0}^{\beta-1}\ \binom{\beta}{k}\\
&=\frac{2^{\frac{(\beta+1)\beta}{2}}(2^{\beta+1}M_{1}+M_{2})(2^{\beta}-1)}{n}\end{split}

(8)

Finally, we have

\begin{split}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}}\ |x^{\alpha}(\theta_{n}\cdot f-f)^{(\beta)}(x)|&\leqslant\sup_{x\in\left(0,\frac{1}{n}\right]}\ |x^{\alpha}f^{(\beta)}(x)|+\sup_{x\in\left[\frac{1}{n},\frac{2}{n}\right]\cup\left[n,n+1\right]}\ |x^{\alpha}(\theta_{n}\cdot f-f)^{(\beta)}(x)|+\sup_{x\in[n+1,\infty)}\ |x^{\alpha}f^{(\beta)}(x)|\\
&\mathrel{{\mathop{\leqslant}\limits}}M_{1}\left(\frac{2}{n}\right)^{\beta+1}+\frac{2^{\frac{(\beta+1)\beta}{2}}(2^{\beta+1}M_{1}+M_{2})(2^{\beta}-1)}{n}+\frac{M_{2}}{n^{\beta+1}},\end{split}

which converges to zero as
n\rightarrow\infty.
This concludes the proof.
∎

3
Mellin convolution

The previous section focused on proving topological properties of
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
such as the fact that it is a Fréchet space. In the current section we want to enhance our understanding of
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
by studying the algebraic aspect of this space. Thus, we begin by introducing the
Mellin convolution
operator
\star:\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+})\times\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+})\longrightarrow\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
with the formula:

\displaystyle\forall_{x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}}\ f\star g(x):=\int_{0}^{\infty}\ y^{-1}f\left(xy^{-1}\right)g(y)\ dy.

(9)

This formula is well-known in the literature concerning Mellin transform – see Definition 4 in
[
9
]
or Chapter 8.3 in
[
6
]
. The novelty introduce in this paper is the domain and codomain of the Mellin convolution as well as the study of the algebraic properties of this operator.

Looking at the formula (
9
), the question that springs to mind is whether Mellin convolution is well-defined? In other words, does
f\star g
define a Schwartz function? Prior to giving a positive answer to this question we show that given two functions
f,g\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+}),
the value
f\star g(x)
as defined by (
9
) is finite for every value
x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
and that
f\star g\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+}).

Theorem 8
.

If
f,g\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+}),\ \alpha\in\mathbb{Z}
and
\beta\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
then

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\ y^{-(\alpha+1)}f^{(\beta)}\left(xy^{-1}\right)g(y)\ dy

(10)

is well-defined, i.e., finite for every
x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}.
Furthermore, the equality

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dx}\int_{0}^{\infty}\ y^{-(\alpha+1)}f^{(\beta)}\left(xy^{-1}\right)g(y)\ dy=\int_{0}^{\infty}\ y^{-(\alpha+2)}f^{(\beta+1)}\left(xy^{-1}\right)g(y)\ dy

(11)

holds, which implies that
f\star g\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+}).

Proof.

To begin with, since
f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
then there exists a constant
M_{1}>0
such that

\forall_{z\in\mathbb{R}_{+}}\ |f^{(\beta)}(z)|<M_{1}\cdot z^{-(\alpha+1)}.

Consequently, for every
x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
we have

\displaystyle\left|\int_{0}^{\infty}\ y^{-(\alpha+1)}f^{(\beta)}\left(xy^{-1}\right)g(y)\ dy\right|<M_{1}x^{-(\alpha+1)}\int_{0}^{\infty}\ |g(y)|\ dy<\infty,

since
g\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
by Lemma
1
.

We proceed with the second part of the theorem and prove that formula (
11
) holds at an arbitrary point
x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}.
We begin by choosing
\varepsilon>0
such that
[x-\varepsilon,x+\varepsilon]\subset\mathbb{R}_{+}
and investigating the limit

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\ \int_{0}^{\infty}\ y^{-(\alpha+1)}\cdot\frac{f^{(\beta)}\left(\frac{x+h_{n}}{y}\right)-f^{(\beta)}\left(\frac{x}{y}\right)}{h_{n}}\cdot g(y)\ dy,

where
(h_{n})\subset(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)
is an arbitrary sequence convergent to zero. By the mean value theorem we have

\forall_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\in\mathbb{N}\\
y\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\end{subarray}}\ \left|\frac{f^{(\beta)}\left(\frac{x+h_{n}}{y}\right)-f^{(\beta)}\left(\frac{x}{y}\right)}{\frac{h_{n}}{y}}\right|\leqslant\sup_{z\in(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)}\ \left|f^{(\beta+1)}\left(\frac{x+z}{y}\right)\right|<M_{2}

for some constant
M_{2}>0.
Consequently, we obtain

\forall_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\in\mathbb{N}\\
y\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\end{subarray}}\ \bigg|\frac{f^{(\beta)}\left(\frac{x+h_{n}}{y}\right)-f^{(\beta)}\left(\frac{x}{y}\right)}{h_{n}}\bigg|\leqslant M_{2}y^{-1}.

and since
y\mapsto y^{-(\alpha+2)}g(y)
is integrable over
\mathbb{R}_{+}
(by Lemma
1
) then by the dominated convergence theorem we have

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\ \int_{0}^{\infty}\ y^{-(\alpha+1)}\cdot\frac{f^{(\beta)}\left(\frac{x+h_{n}}{y}\right)-f^{(\beta)}\left(\frac{x}{y}\right)}{h_{n}}\cdot g(y)\ dy=\int_{0}^{\infty}\ y^{-(\alpha+2)}f^{(\beta+1)}\left(xy^{-1}\right)g(y)\ dy.

Since the sequence
(h_{n})
was chosen arbitrarily, we conclude that (
11
) holds true.
∎

Corollary 9
.

If
f,g\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
then
f\star g\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+}).

Proof.

By Theorem
8
we already know that
f\star g\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
and

\forall_{\beta\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\ \frac{d^{\beta}}{dx^{\beta}}f\star g(x)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\ y^{-(\beta+1)}f^{(\beta)}\left(xy^{-1}\right)g(y)\ dy.

Furthermore, since
f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
then for every
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z},\beta\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
there exists a constant
M>0
such that

\forall_{z\in\mathbb{R}_{+}}\ |f^{(\beta)}(z)|<Mz^{-\alpha},

Hence we have

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}}\ |x^{\alpha}(f\star g)^{(\beta)}(x)|=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}}\ \bigg|x^{\alpha}\int_{0}^{\infty}\ y^{-(\beta+1)}f^{(\beta)}\left(xy^{-1}\right)g(y)\ dy\bigg|\leqslant M\int_{0}^{\infty}\ y^{\alpha-(\beta+1)}|g(y)|\ dy<\infty,

where the last inequality follows from the fact that
y\mapsto y^{\alpha-(\beta+1)}g(y)
is integrable over
\mathbb{R}_{+}
(by Lemma
1
). This concludes the proof.
∎

At this point we might get a feeling that the space
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
is a “natural habitat” for the Mellin convolution to live in. However, this is by far not the end of the story. Recall that a Fréchet space
\mathcal{F},
together with multiplication
\odot:\mathcal{F}\times\mathcal{F}\longrightarrow\mathcal{F}
is called a Fréchet algebra if

•

for every
c\in\mathbb{R}
and
f,g\in\mathcal{F}:

c\cdot(f\odot g)=(c\cdot f)\odot g=f\odot(c\cdot g),

•

multiplication is associative, i.e. for every
f,g,h\in\mathcal{F}:

(f\odot g)\odot h=f\odot(g\odot h),

•

multiplication is distributive, i.e. for every
f,g,h\in\mathcal{F}:

\begin{split}f\odot(g+h)=f\odot g+f\odot h,\\
(f+g)\odot h=f\odot h+g\odot h,\end{split}

•

multiplication is continuous.

In addition, if
f\odot g=g\odot f
for every
f,g\in\mathcal{F}
then
(\mathcal{F},\odot)
is called a commutative Fréchet algebra.

Theorem 10
.

Fréchet space
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
, together with Mellin convolution, is a commutative Fréchet algebra.

Proof.

It is trivial to see that for every
c\in\mathbb{R}
and
f,g\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
we have

c\cdot(f\star g)=(c\cdot f)\star g=f\star(c\cdot g).

Furthermore, distributivity of
\star
is also obvious, so it remains to prove that it is associative, continuous and commutative. We accomplish this task in three steps.

Step 1.
Let
f,g,h\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+}).
In order to see that
\star
is associative, it suffices to use Theorem B.3.1 in
[
13
]
, p. 289 (or Theorem 8.8 in
[
24
]
, p. 164) and calculate that

\begin{split}\forall_{x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}}\ (f\star g)\star h(x)&=\int_{0}^{\infty}\ y^{-1}f\star g\left(xy^{-1}\right)h(y)\ dy\\
&=\int_{0}^{\infty}\ y^{-1}\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}\ z^{-1}f\left(xy^{-1}z^{-1}\right)g(z)\ dz\right)h(y)\ dy\\
&=\int_{0}^{\infty}\ \int_{0}^{\infty}\ y^{-1}z^{-1}f\left(xy^{-1}z^{-1}\right)g(z)h(y)\ dzdy\\
&\mathrel{{\mathop{=}\limits}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\ \int_{0}^{\infty}\ y^{-1}u^{-1}f\left(xu^{-1}\right)g\left(uy^{-1}\right)h(y)\ dudy\\
&=\int_{0}^{\infty}\ u^{-1}f\left(xu^{-1}\right)\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}\ y^{-1}g\left(uy^{-1}\right)h(y)\ dy\right)du\\
&=\int_{0}^{\infty}\ u^{-1}f\left(xu^{-1}\right)g\star h(u)\ du=f\star(g\star h)(x).\end{split}

Step 2.
Suppose that
d_{\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+})}(f_{n},f)\longrightarrow 0
and
d_{\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+})}(g_{n},g)\longrightarrow 0.
We prove that

\displaystyle d_{\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+})}(f_{n}\star g_{n},f\star g)\longrightarrow 0,

(12)

which establishes continuity of Mellin convolution.

Fix
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z},\beta\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. By Lemma
1
there exists a constant
M>0
such that

\displaystyle\forall_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\ \int_{0}^{\infty}\ y^{\alpha-(\beta+1)}|g_{n}(y)|\ dy<M\hskip 11.38092pt\text{and}\hskip 11.38092pt\int_{0}^{\infty}\ z^{\alpha-1}|f^{(\beta)}(z)|\ dz<M.

(13)

Consequently, we have

\begin{split}p_{\alpha,\beta}&(f_{n}\star g_{n}-f\star g)=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}}\left|x^{\alpha}\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}\ y^{-1}f_{n}(xy^{-1})g_{n}(y)\ dy-\int_{0}^{\infty}\ y^{-1}f(xy^{-1})g(y)\ dy\right)^{(\beta)}\right|\\
&\mathrel{{\mathop{=}\limits}}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}}x^{\alpha}\left|\int_{0}^{\infty}\ y^{-(\beta+1)}f_{n}^{(\beta)}(xy^{-1})g_{n}(y)\ dy-\int_{0}^{\infty}\ y^{-(\beta+1)}f^{\beta}(xy^{-1})g(y)\ dy\right|\\
&=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}}x^{\alpha}\left|\int_{0}^{\infty}\ y^{-(\beta+1)}(f_{n}^{(\beta)}(xy^{-1})-f^{(\beta)}(xy^{-1}))g_{n}(y)\ dy-\int_{0}^{\infty}\ y^{-(\beta+1)}f^{(\beta)}(xy^{-1})(g(y)-g_{n}(y))\ dy\right|\\
&\leqslant\int_{0}^{\infty}\ y^{-(\beta+1)}|g_{n}(y)|\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}}x^{\alpha}|f_{n}^{(\beta)}(xy^{-1})-f^{(\beta)}(xy^{-1})|\ dy+\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}}\ x^{\alpha}\int_{0}^{\infty}\ y^{-(\beta+1)}|f^{(\beta)}(xy^{-1})||g(y)-g_{n}(y)|\ dy\\
&\leqslant p_{\alpha,\beta}(f_{n}-f)\int_{0}^{\infty}\ y^{\alpha-(\beta+1)}|g_{n}(y)|\ dy+\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}}\ x^{\alpha-\beta}\int_{0}^{\infty}\ z^{-(\beta+1)}|f^{(\beta)}(z^{-1})||g(xz)-g_{n}(xz)|\ dz\\
&\leqslant p_{\alpha,\beta}(f_{n}-f)\int_{0}^{\infty}\ y^{\alpha-(\beta+1)}|g_{n}(y)|\ dy+\int_{0}^{\infty}\ z^{-(\beta+1)}|f^{(\beta)}(z^{-1})|\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}}\ x^{\alpha-\beta}|g(xz)-g_{n}(xz)|\ dz\\
&\leqslant p_{\alpha,\beta}(f_{n}-f)\int_{0}^{\infty}\ y^{\alpha-(\beta+1)}|g_{n}(y)|\ dy+p_{\alpha-\beta,0}(g-g_{n})\int_{0}^{\infty}\ z^{-(\alpha+1)}|f^{(\beta)}(z^{-1})|\ dz\\
&=p_{\alpha,\beta}(f_{n}-f)\int_{0}^{\infty}\ y^{\alpha-(\beta+1)}|g_{n}(y)|\ dy+p_{\alpha-\beta,0}(g-g_{n})\int_{0}^{\infty}\ z^{\alpha-1}|f^{(\beta)}(z)|\ dz\\
&\mathrel{{\mathop{\leqslant}\limits}}M(p_{\alpha,\beta}(f_{n}-f)+p_{\alpha-\beta,0}(g-g_{n}))\end{split}

Since
d_{\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+})}(f_{n},f)\longrightarrow 0
implies
p_{\alpha,\beta}(f_{n}-f)\longrightarrow 0
and
d_{\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+})}(g_{n},g)\longrightarrow 0
implies
p_{\alpha-\beta,0}(g-g_{n})\longrightarrow 0
we conclude that
p_{\alpha,\beta}(f_{n}\star g_{n}-f\star g)\longrightarrow 0.
Values
\alpha
and
\beta
were chosen arbitrarily, which finally implies (
12
).

Step 3.
To see that the Mellin convolution is commutative, we compute

\begin{split}\forall_{x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}}\ f\star g(x)&=\int_{0}^{\infty}\ y^{-1}f\left(xy^{-1}\right)g(y)\ dy\mathrel{{\mathop{=}\limits}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\ z^{-1}f\left(z^{-1}\right)g(zx)\ dz\\
&\mathrel{{\mathop{=}\limits}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\ u^{-1}f(u)g(xu^{-1})\ du=g\star f(x).\end{split}

∎

Before we proceed with the study of the structure space of the Schwartz class, let us pause for a moment and deduce a counterpart for Young’s convolution inequality.

Theorem 11
.

Let
p,q,r\in[1,\infty)
be such that

\frac{1}{r}=\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}-1.

For every
f,g\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
we have

\displaystyle\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}\ y^{-1}|f\star g(y)|^{r}\ dy\right)^{\frac{1}{r}}\leqslant\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}\ y^{-1}|f(y)|^{p}\ dy\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}\ y^{-1}|g(y)|^{q}\ dy\right)^{\frac{1}{q}}.

(14)

Proof.

Let
F:=f\circ\exp
and observe that

\|F\|_{p}^{p}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\ \left|f\left(e^{x}\right)\right|^{p}\ dx\mathrel{{\mathop{=}\limits}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\ y^{-1}|f(y)|^{p}\ dy<\infty

by Lemma
1
. An analogous estimate holds for
G:=g\circ\exp
. Next, we observe that

\begin{split}\forall_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\ f\star g\left(e^{x}\right)&=\int_{0}^{\infty}\ y^{-1}f\left(e^{x}y^{-1}\right)g(y)\ dy=\int_{0}^{\infty}\ y^{-1}F(x-\ln(y))g(y)\ dy\\
&\mathrel{{\mathop{=}\limits}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\ F(x-z)G(z)\ dz=F\star_{c}G(x),\end{split}

where
\star_{c}
is the classical convolution. By classical Young’s convolution inequality (Theorem 3.9.4 in
[
7
]
, p. 205-206) we have

\|(f\star g)\circ\exp\|_{r}=\|F\star_{c}G\|_{r}\leqslant\|F\|_{p}\|G\|_{q},

which translates to

\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}\ \left|f\star g\left(e^{x}\right)\right|^{r}\ dx\right)^{\frac{1}{r}}\leqslant\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}\ \left|f\left(e^{x}\right)\right|^{p}\ dx\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}\ \left|g\left(e^{x}\right)\right|^{q}\ dx\right)^{\frac{1}{q}}.

The substitution
y=e^{x}
finally proves (
14
).
∎

4
Structure space of the Schwartz class

Our goal in this section is to study the structure space
\Delta(\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+}),\star)
of the Schwartz class
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+}).
This is a space of all nonzero, linear and continuous functionals
m:\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+})\longrightarrow\mathbb{R},
which are multiplicative:

\forall_{f,g\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+})}\ m(f\star g)=m(f)m(g).

We endow the structure space
\Delta(\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+}),\star)
with weak* topology and aim to prove that it is homeomorphic to
\mathbb{R}
(with the standard topology). This is highly reminiscent of the classical result that
\Delta(L^{1}(G),\star_{c})
, where
G
is a locally compact, abelian group and
\star_{c}
is the classical convolution, is homeomorphic to the dual group
\widehat{G}
(see Theorems 2.7.2 and 2.7.5 in
[
20
]
, p. 89-92 or Theorem 4.2 in
[
16
]
, p. 88).

The strategy we adopt is to first prove that
\Delta(\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+}),\star)
is in bijective correspondence with
\mathbb{R}
(Theorem
14
) and only then establish that these spaces are in fact homeomorphic (Theorems
16
and
18
). Without further ado, we set out on our journey by showing the following technical results:

Lemma 12
.

Let
x,y\in\mathbb{R}_{+}.
If
g\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
, then

\displaystyle D_{y}g\star D_{x}g=g\star D_{xy}g,

(15)

where for every
v\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
the operator
D_{v}:\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+})\longrightarrow\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
is given by

\displaystyle\forall_{u\in\mathbb{R}_{+}}\ D_{v}f(u):=f\left(uv^{-1}\right).

Proof.

For every
u\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
we have

\begin{split}D_{y}g\star D_{x}g(u)&=\int_{0}^{\infty}\ v^{-1}D_{y}g\left(uv^{-1}\right)D_{x}g(v)\ dv\\
&=\int_{0}^{\infty}\ v^{-1}g\left(u(vy)^{-1}\right)g\left(vx^{-1}\right)\ dv\\
&\mathrel{{\mathop{=}\limits}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\ w^{-1}g\left(uw^{-1}\right)g\left(w(xy)^{-1}\right)\ dw=g\star D_{xy}g(u),\end{split}

which concludes the proof.
∎

Lemma 13
.

For every
g\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
the function
x\mapsto D_{x}g
is continuous.

Proof.

To prove that the function in question is continuous it suffices to prove that for every sequence
(x_{n})\subset\mathbb{R}_{+}
convergent to
x_{*}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
and fixed
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z},\ \beta\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
we have

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\ p_{\alpha,\beta}(D_{x_{n}}g-D_{x_{*}}g)=0.

(16)

Before we prove this equality in full generality, let us focus on a simpler case, when
g\in\mathcal{S}_{c}(\mathbb{R}_{+}).

We have

\begin{split}p_{\alpha,\beta}(D_{x_{n}}g-D_{x_{*}}g)&=\sup_{u\in\mathbb{R}_{+}}\ \left|u^{\alpha}\left(g\left(ux_{n}^{-1}\right)-g\left(ux_{*}^{-1}\right)\right)^{(\beta)}\right|\\
&=\sup_{u\in\mathbb{R}_{+}}\ \left|u^{\alpha}\left(x_{n}^{-\beta}g^{(\beta)}\left(ux_{n}^{-1}\right)-x_{*}^{-\beta}g^{(\beta)}\left(ux_{*}^{-1}\right)\right)\right|\\
&\mathrel{{\mathop{=}\limits}}x_{*}^{\alpha-\beta}\sup_{v\in\mathbb{R}_{+}}\ \left|v^{\alpha}\left(\left(x_{*}x_{n}^{-1}\right)^{\beta}g^{(\beta)}\left(vx_{*}x_{n}^{-1}\right)-g^{(\beta)}(v)\right)\right|\end{split}

(17)

Fix
\varepsilon>0
and observe that by uniform continuity (Lemma 1.3.6 in
[
13
]
, p. 11) of the function
v\mapsto v^{\alpha}g^{(\beta)}(v)
there exists
\delta>0
such that

\displaystyle\forall_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\\
y\in[1-\delta,1+\delta]\end{subarray}}\ \left|v^{\alpha}\left(y^{\alpha}g^{(\beta)}(vy)-g^{(\beta)}(v)\right)\right|\leqslant\frac{\varepsilon}{2x_{*}^{\alpha-\beta}}.

(18)

We may decrease
\delta
(if necessary) so that we have

\displaystyle\forall_{y\in[1-\delta,1+\delta]}\ |y^{\beta-\alpha}-1|\leqslant\frac{\varepsilon}{2p_{\alpha,\beta}(g)x_{*}^{\alpha-\beta}}.

(19)

Next, we observe the implication

v\not\in\text{supp}\left(g^{(\beta)}\right)y^{-1}\ \Longrightarrow\ g^{(\beta)}(vy)=0,

which implies that for
v\not\in K_{\delta}:=\text{supp}\left(g^{(\beta)}\right)\cdot\left[\frac{1}{1+\delta},\frac{1}{1-\delta}\right]
we have

\forall_{y\in[1-\delta,1+\delta]}\ g^{(\beta)}(vy)=0.

This means that for
y\in[1-\delta,1+\delta]
we have

\begin{split}\sup_{v\in\mathbb{R}_{+}}\ \left|v^{\alpha}\left(y^{\beta}g^{(\beta)}(vy)-g^{(\beta)}(v)\right)\right|&=\sup_{v\in K_{\rho}}\ \left|v^{\alpha}\left(y^{\beta}g^{(\beta)}(vy)-g^{(\beta)}(v)\right)\right|\\
&\leqslant\sup_{v\in K_{\rho}}\ \left|v^{\alpha}g^{(\beta)}(vy)\left(y^{\beta}-y^{\alpha}\right)\right|+\sup_{v\in K_{\rho}}\ \left|v^{\alpha}\left(y^{\alpha}g^{(\beta)}(vy)-g^{(\beta)}(v)\right)\right|\\
&\mathrel{{\mathop{\leqslant}\limits}}p_{\alpha,\beta}(g)|y^{\beta-\alpha}-1|+\frac{\varepsilon}{2x_{*}^{\alpha-\beta}}\mathrel{{\mathop{\leqslant}\limits}}\frac{\varepsilon}{x_{*}^{\alpha-\beta}}.\end{split}

Comparison between the above estimate and (
17
) reveals that (
16
) is indeed true for
g\in\mathcal{S}_{c}(\mathbb{R}_{+}).

It remains to prove (
16
) for an arbitrary function in
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+}).
Let
g\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
and
\varepsilon>0.
By Theorem
7
there exists
h\in\mathcal{S}_{c}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
such that
d_{\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+})}(g,h)<\varepsilon.
Observe that

\begin{split}\forall_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\ p_{\alpha,\beta}(D_{x_{n}}g-D_{x_{n}}h)&=\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}_{+}}\ \left|y^{\alpha}\left(g(yx_{n}^{-1})-h(yx_{n}^{-1})\right)^{(\beta)}\right|\\
&=x_{n}^{-\beta}\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}_{+}}\ \left|y^{\alpha}\left(g^{(\beta)}(yx_{n}^{-1})-h^{(\beta)}(yx_{n}^{-1})\right)\right|\\
&\mathrel{{\mathop{=}\limits}}x_{n}^{\alpha-\beta}\sup_{z\in\mathbb{R}_{+}}\ \left|z^{\alpha}\left(g^{(\beta)}(z)-h^{(\beta)}(z)\right)\right|=x_{n}^{\alpha-\beta}p_{\alpha,\beta}(g-h)\end{split}

and, analogously,
p_{\alpha,\beta}(D_{x_{*}}g-D_{x_{*}}h)=x_{*}^{\alpha-\beta}p_{\alpha,\beta}(g-h).
Finally, we have

\begin{split}\forall_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\ p_{\alpha,\beta}(D_{x_{n}}g-D_{x_{*}}g)&\leqslant p_{\alpha,\beta}(D_{x_{n}}g-D_{x_{n}}h)+p_{\alpha,\beta}(D_{x_{n}}h-D_{x_{*}}h)+p_{\alpha,\beta}(D_{x_{*}}h-D_{x_{*}}g)\\
&\leqslant x_{n}^{\alpha-\beta}p_{\alpha,\beta}(g-h)+p_{\alpha,\beta}(D_{x_{n}}h-D_{x_{*}}h)+x_{*}^{\alpha-\beta}p_{\alpha,\beta}(g-h),\end{split}

so passing to the limit as
n\longrightarrow\infty,
we obtain

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\ p_{\alpha,\beta}(D_{x_{n}}g-D_{x_{*}}g)\leqslant 2x_{*}^{\alpha-\beta}p_{\alpha,\beta}(g-h)+\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\ p_{\alpha,\beta}(D_{x_{n}}h-D_{x_{*}}h)=2x_{*}^{\alpha-\beta}p_{\alpha,\beta}(g-h)<2x_{*}^{\alpha-\beta}\varepsilon.

Since
\varepsilon
was chosen arbitrarily, we conclude the proof.
∎

We are now ready to prove that there exists a bijective correspondence between
\Delta(\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+}),\star)
and
\mathbb{R}
.

Theorem 14
.

If
m_{s}:\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+})\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
is given by

\displaystyle m_{s}(f):=\int_{0}^{\infty}\ x^{s-1}f(x)\ dx

(20)

for some
s\in\mathbb{R},
then
m_{s}\in\Delta(\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+}),\star).
Furthermore, for every
m\in\Delta(\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+}),\star)
there exists a unique
s\in\mathbb{R}
such that
m=m_{s}.

Proof.

Obviously, if
m_{s}
is given by formula (
20
) then it is a linear functional. To see that it is continuous we choose a sequence
(f_{n})\subset\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+}),
which is convergent to
f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
and prove that

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\ m_{s}(f_{n})=m_{s}(f)

The convergence of the sequence
(f_{n})
means in particular that for every
\varepsilon>0
there exists
N_{\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for every
n\geqslant N_{\varepsilon}
we have

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in(0,1)}\ x^{\lfloor s-1\rfloor}|f_{n}(x)-f(x)|\leqslant\varepsilon

(21)

and

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in[1,\infty)}\ x^{\lceil s-1\rceil+2}|f_{n}(x)-f(x)|\leqslant\varepsilon.

(22)

Consequently, for every
n\geqslant N_{\varepsilon}
we have

\int_{0}^{1}\ x^{s-1}|f_{n}(x)-f(x)|\ dx\leqslant\varepsilon\cdot\int_{0}^{1}\ \frac{x^{s-1}}{x^{\lfloor s-1\rfloor}}\ dx\leqslant\varepsilon

and

\int_{1}^{\infty}\ x^{s-1}|f_{n}(x)-f(x)|\ dx\leqslant\varepsilon\cdot\int_{1}^{\infty}\ \frac{x^{s-1}}{x^{\lceil s-1\rceil}}\cdot x^{-2}dx\leqslant\varepsilon.

We conclude that

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\ \int_{0}^{\infty}\ x^{s-1}|f_{n}(x)-f(x)|\ dx=0

and thus every functional
m_{s}
is continuous.

Next, in order to show that
m_{s}\in\Delta(\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+}),\star)
we need to demonstrate that
m_{s}
is muliplicative. For every
f,g\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
we have

\begin{split}m_{s}(f\star g)&=\int_{0}^{\infty}\ x^{s-1}f\star g(x)\ dx\\
&=\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{s-1}\bigg(\int_{0}^{\infty}\ y^{-1}f\left(xy^{-1}\right)g(y)\ dy\bigg)\ dx\\
&=\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}\ x^{s-1}f\left(xy^{-1}\right)\ dx\right)y^{-1}g(y)\ dy\\
&\mathrel{{\mathop{=}\limits}}\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}\ z^{s-1}f(z)\ dz\right)\cdot\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}\ y^{s-1}g(y)\ dy\right)=m_{s}(f)m_{s}(g),\end{split}

where we have used Fubini’s theorem (see Theorem B.3.1(b) in
[
13
]
, p. 287 or Theorem 8.8 in
[
24
]
, p. 164). This concludes the first part of the proof.

For the second part of the proof, we fix an element
m\in\Delta(\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+}),\star)
and try to show that there exists a unique
s\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
such that
m=m_{s}.
We fix a nonzero function
g_{*}\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
and note that by Lemma
12
we have

\forall_{x,y\in\mathbb{R}_{+}}\ D_{y}g_{*}\star D_{x}g_{*}=g_{*}\star D_{xy}g_{*}.

Applying the functional
m
to this equation and using its multiplicativity we obtain

\displaystyle\forall_{x,y\in\mathbb{R}_{+}}\ m(D_{y}g_{*})m(D_{x}g_{*})=m(g_{*})m(D_{xy}g_{*}).

(23)

Next, we define the function
\phi:\mathbb{R}_{+}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
by the formula

\displaystyle\phi(x):=\frac{m(D_{x}g_{*})}{m(g_{*})},

(24)

which is nonzero (because
\phi(1)=1
) and continuous (by Lemma
13
). Dividing equation (
23
) by
m(g_{*})^{2}
we may rewrite it in the form

\forall_{x,y\in\mathbb{R}_{+}}\ \phi(y)\phi(x)=\phi(xy),

which is known as Cauchy’s multiplicative functional equation. It follows (see Theorem 3 in
[
1
]
, p. 41) that
\phi(y)=y^{s}
for some
s\in\mathbb{R}.
We have

\begin{split}\forall_{f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+})}\ \int_{0}^{\infty}\ y^{s-1}f(y)\ dy&=\int_{0}^{\infty}\ y^{-1}f(y)\phi(y)\ dy\\
&\mathrel{{\mathop{=}\limits}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\ y^{-1}f(y)\cdot\frac{m(D_{y}g_{*})}{m(g_{*})}\ dy\\
&=\frac{1}{m(g_{*})}\cdot m\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}\ y^{-1}f(y)D_{y}g_{*}\ dy\right)\\
&=\frac{1}{m(g_{*})}\cdot m(g_{*}\star f)=\frac{1}{m(g_{*})}\cdot m(g_{*})m(f)=m(f),\end{split}

where the third equality holds true due to Lemma 11.45 in
[
3
]
, p. 427 (or Proposition 7 in
[
14
]
, p. 123). We have thus proved that
m=m_{s}.

Finally, the fact that (
24
) is a unique
\phi
such that
m=m_{\phi}
follows from a technique, which is well-known in the field of variational calculus: suppose that there exist two nonzero, continuous functions
\phi_{1},\phi_{2}
such that
m=m_{\phi_{1}}=m_{\phi_{2}}.
Consequently, we have

\displaystyle\forall_{f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+})}\ \int_{0}^{\infty}\ f(x)\bigg(\phi_{1}(x)-\phi_{2}(x)\bigg)\ dx=0.

(25)

Assuming the existence of an element
x_{*}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
such that
\phi_{1}(x_{*})\neq\phi_{2}(x_{*})
there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that
\phi_{1}-\phi_{2}
is of (without loss of generality) positive sign on
[x_{*}-\varepsilon,x_{*}+\varepsilon].
Let
f_{*}\in\mathcal{S}_{c}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
be a function defined by the formula

\displaystyle f_{*}(x):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}(\phi_{1}(x)-\phi_{2}(x))\cdot\theta\left(\frac{2}{\varepsilon}(x-x_{*}+\varepsilon)\right)&x\in\left[x_{*}-\varepsilon,x_{*}-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\right]\\
\phi_{1}(x)-\phi_{2}(x)&x\in\left[x_{*}-\frac{\varepsilon}{2},x_{*}+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\right]\\
(\phi_{1}(x)-\phi_{2}(x))\cdot\theta\left(2-\frac{2}{\varepsilon}(x-x_{*})\right)&x\in\left[x_{*}+\frac{\varepsilon}{2},x_{*}+\varepsilon\right]\\
0&x\not\in[x_{*}-\varepsilon,x_{*}+\varepsilon]\end{array}\right.

where
\theta
is the Fabius function. It remains to observe that

0\mathrel{{\mathop{=}\limits}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\ f_{*}(x)\bigg(\phi_{1}(x)-\phi_{2}(x)\bigg)\ dx\geqslant\int_{x_{*}-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}}^{x_{*}+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}}\ \bigg(\phi_{1}(x)-\phi_{2}(x)\bigg)^{2}\ dx>0,

which is a contradiction. This means that
\phi_{1}=\phi_{2}
and concludes the proof.
∎

In summary, Theorem
14
establishes that the function
\omega:\Delta(\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+}),\star)\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
given by
\omega(m):=s
(where
s\in\mathbb{R}
is a unique element such that
m=m_{s}
) is a bijection. Our next task is to prove that
\omega
is in fact more than just a bijection of two sets. We intend to show that
\omega
is a continuous map and ultimately a homeomorphism. We commence with the following result:

Lemma 15
.

For every constant
c\in\mathbb{R}
the function
E_{c}:\mathbb{R}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R},
defined by

E_{c}(s):=\int_{1}^{3}\ (x^{s}-x^{c})\exp\left(-\frac{1}{(3-x)(x-1)}\right)\ dx,

is increasing.

Proof.

By Theorem 3.2 in
[
21
]
, p. 337-339 we have

\begin{split}\frac{\partial}{\partial s}E_{c}(s)&=\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\int_{1}^{3}\ (x^{s}-x^{c})\exp\left(-\frac{1}{(3-x)(x-1)}\right)\ dx\\
&=\int_{1}^{3}\ x^{s}\ln(x)\exp\left(-\frac{1}{(3-x)(x-1)}\right)\ dx>0,\end{split}

which implies that
E_{c}
is increasing and concludes the proof.
∎

We are ready to prove continuity of
\omega:

Theorem 16
.

Function
\omega:(\Delta(\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+}),\star),\tau^{*})\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
is continuous.

Proof.

We fix
s_{*}\in\mathbb{R}
as well as its arbitrary open neighbourhood
U_{\varepsilon}:=(s_{*}-\varepsilon,s_{*}+\varepsilon)
where
\varepsilon>0.
Our task is to prove that

\omega^{-1}(U_{\varepsilon})=\bigg\{m\in\Delta(\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+}),\star)\ :\ |\omega(m)-s_{*}|<\varepsilon\bigg\}

is weak* open and we do it by fixing an arbitrary element
m_{**}\in\omega^{-1}(U_{\varepsilon})
and constructing its weak* open neighbourhood
W_{**}
contained in
\omega^{-1}(U_{\varepsilon}),
i.e.,

m_{**}\in W_{**}\subset\omega^{-1}(U_{\varepsilon}).

To begin with, since
m_{**}\in\omega^{-1}(U_{\varepsilon})
then
s_{**}:=\omega(m_{**})
satisfies
|s_{**}-s_{*}|<\delta\varepsilon
for some
\delta\in(0,1).
Next, by Lemma
15
we know that the function
E_{s_{**}}
is increasing and since
E_{s_{**}}(s_{**})=0
there exists
\rho>0
such that

\displaystyle|E_{s_{**}}(s)|<\rho\ \Longrightarrow\ |s-s_{**}|<(1-\delta)\varepsilon.

(26)

We pick
f\in\mathcal{S}_{c}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
defined by

\displaystyle f(x):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{(3-x)(x-1)}\right)&\text{if }\ x\in[1,3],\\
0&\text{otherwise,}\end{array}\right.

and claim that

W_{**}:=\bigg\{m\in\Delta(\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+}),\star)\ :\ |m(f)-m_{**}(f)|<\rho\bigg\}

is the desired weak* open neighbourhood of
m_{**}.
Indeed, for every
m\in\Delta(\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+}),\star)
we have

m(f)-m_{**}(f)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\ (x^{s}-x^{s_{**}})f(x)\ dx\mathrel{{\mathop{=}\limits}}\int_{1}^{3}\ (x^{s}-x^{s_{**}})\exp\left(-\frac{1}{(3-x)(x-1)}\right)\ dx=E_{s_{**}}(s)

where
s=\omega(m).
If
m\in W_{**}
then
|E_{s_{**}}(s)|<\rho,
so by (
26
) we have
|s-s_{**}|<(1-\delta)\varepsilon.
Finally, we have

\forall_{m\in W_{**}}\ |s-s_{*}|\leqslant|s-s_{**}|+|s_{**}-s_{*}|<(1-\delta)\varepsilon+\delta\varepsilon=\varepsilon,

which proves that
m_{**}\in W_{**}\subset\omega^{-1}(U_{\varepsilon}).

∎

We are on the right track to prove that
\omega
is a homeomorphism between
\Delta(\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+}))
and
\mathbb{R}.
We already know that it is a conitnuous map so it suffices to prove that it is also open. To this end we will apply the following result:

Lemma 17
.

Let
(f_{k})_{k=1}^{n}\subset\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
be a finite sequence of functions and let
s_{*}\in\mathbb{R}.
For every
\varepsilon>0
there exist an open neighbourhood
W_{*}
of
s_{*}
and constants
a,b\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
such that
a<1<b
and

\forall_{\begin{subarray}{c}s\in W_{*}\\
k=1,\ldots,n\end{subarray}}\ \int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}\backslash[a,b]}\ |f_{n}(x)||x^{s}-x^{s_{*}}|\ dx<\varepsilon.

Proof.

We divide the proof with respect to the value
s_{*}:

•

Suppose that
s_{*}>0
and set
W_{s_{*}}:=\left(\frac{1}{2}s_{*},\frac{3}{2}s_{*}\right),
which implies

\displaystyle\forall_{\begin{subarray}{c}s\in W_{s_{*}}\\
x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\end{subarray}}\ x^{s}\leqslant x^{\frac{1}{2}s_{*}}+x^{\frac{3}{2}s_{*}}.

(27)

Furthermore, since
(f_{k})_{k=1}^{n}\subset\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
then there exist constants
M_{1},M_{2}>0
such that

\displaystyle\forall_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in(0,1)\\
k=1,\ldots,n\end{subarray}}\ |f_{k}(x)|\cdot\left(x^{\frac{1}{2}s_{*}-1}+x^{s_{*}-1}+x^{\frac{3}{2}s_{*}-1}\right)<M_{1}

(28)

and

\displaystyle\forall_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in(1,\infty)\\
k=1,\ldots,n\end{subarray}}\ |f_{k}(x)|\cdot\left(x^{\frac{1}{2}s_{*}+2}++x^{s_{*}+2}+x^{\frac{3}{2}s_{*}+2}\right)<M_{2}.

(29)

Finally, if
a,b
are chosen such that
a<1<b
and

\frac{M_{1}a^{2}}{2}+\frac{M_{2}}{b}<\varepsilon

then we have

\displaystyle\forall_{\begin{subarray}{c}s\in W_{s_{*}}\\
k=1,\ldots,n\end{subarray}}\ \int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}\backslash[a,b]}\ |f_{k}(x)||x^{s}-x^{s_{*}}|\ dx\mathrel{{\mathop{\leqslant}\limits}}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}\backslash[a,b]}\ |f_{k}(x)|\cdot\left(x^{\frac{1}{2}s_{*}}+x^{s_{*}}+x^{\frac{3}{2}s_{*}}\right)\ dx

\displaystyle\leqslant\int_{0}^{a}\ |f_{k}(x)|\cdot\left(x^{\frac{1}{2}s_{*}}+x^{s_{*}}+x^{\frac{3}{2}s_{*}}\right)\ dx+\int_{b}^{\infty}\ |f_{k}(x)|\cdot\left(x^{\frac{1}{2}s_{*}}+x^{s_{*}}+x^{\frac{3}{2}s_{*}}\right)\ dx

\displaystyle\mathrel{{\mathop{\leqslant}\limits}}\int_{0}^{a}\ M_{1}x\ dx+\int_{b}^{\infty}\ \frac{M_{2}}{x^{2}}\ dx=\frac{M_{1}a^{2}}{2}+\frac{M_{2}}{b}<\varepsilon.

•

If
s_{*}=0
set
W_{s_{*}}:=(-1,1),
which implies

\displaystyle\forall_{\begin{subarray}{c}s\in W_{s_{*}}\\
x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\end{subarray}}\ x^{s}\leqslant x^{-1}+x.

(30)

Furthermore, since
(f_{k})_{k=1}^{n}\subset\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
then there exist constants
M_{1},M_{2}>0
such that

\displaystyle\forall_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in(0,1)\\
k=1,\ldots,n\end{subarray}}\ |f_{k}(x)|\cdot\left(x^{-2}+x^{-1}+1\right)<M_{1}

(31)

and

\displaystyle\forall_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in(1,\infty)\\
k=1,\ldots,n\end{subarray}}\ |f_{k}(x)|\cdot\left(x+x^{2}+x^{3}\right)<M_{2}.

(32)

Finally, if
a,b
are chosen such that
a<1<b
and

\frac{M_{1}a^{2}}{2}+\frac{M_{2}}{b}<\varepsilon

then we have

\displaystyle\forall_{\begin{subarray}{c}s\in W_{s_{*}}\\
k=1,\ldots,n\end{subarray}}\ \int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}\backslash[a,b]}\ |f_{k}(x)||x^{s}-1|\ dx\mathrel{{\mathop{\leqslant}\limits}}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}\backslash[a,b]}\ |f_{k}(x)|\cdot\left(x^{-1}+1+x\right)\ dx

\displaystyle\leqslant\int_{0}^{a}\ |f_{k}(x)|\cdot\left(x^{-1}+1+x\right)\ dx+\int_{b}^{\infty}\ |f_{k}(x)|\cdot\left(x^{-1}+1+x\right)\ dx

\displaystyle\mathrel{{\mathop{\leqslant}\limits}}\int_{0}^{a}\ M_{1}x\ dx+\int_{b}^{\infty}\ \frac{M_{2}}{x^{2}}\ dx=\frac{M_{1}a^{2}}{2}+\frac{M_{2}}{b}<\varepsilon.

•

The case when
s_{*}<0
is completely analogous to the one where
s_{*}>0.

∎

Finally, we are ready to prove openness of
\omega
:

Theorem 18
.

\omega:(\Delta(\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+}),\star),\tau^{*})\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
is an open map.

Proof.

Our task is to prove that the image (under
\omega
) of an arbitrary weak* open set

\displaystyle U:=\bigg\{m\in\Delta(\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+}),\star)\ :\ \forall_{k=1,\ldots,n}\ |m(f_{k})-m_{s_{*}}(f_{k})|<\varepsilon\bigg\},

where
\varepsilon>0,\ s_{*}\in\mathbb{R}
and
(f_{k})_{k=1}^{n}\subset\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
are fixed, is open in
\mathbb{R}
. We fix
s_{**}\in\omega(U),
which means that there exists
\delta\in(0,1)
such that

\displaystyle\forall_{k=1,\ldots,n}\ |m_{s_{**}}(f_{k})-m_{s_{*}}(f_{k})|<\delta\varepsilon.

(33)

Furthermore, by Lemma
17
we pick
W_{s_{**}}
and
a,b\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
such that
a<1<b
and

\displaystyle\forall_{\begin{subarray}{c}s\in W_{s_{**}}\\
k=1,\ldots,n\end{subarray}}\ \int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}\backslash[a,b]}\ |f_{k}(x)||x^{s}-x^{s_{**}}|\ dx\leqslant\frac{(1-\delta)\varepsilon}{4}.

(34)

Last but not least, we put

\displaystyle V:=\bigg\{s\in\mathbb{R}\ :\ \sup_{x\in[a,b]}\ \left|x^{s}-x^{s_{**}}\right|<\frac{(1-\delta)\varepsilon}{2\max_{k=1,\ldots,n}\ \|f_{k}\|_{1}}\bigg\}\cap W_{s_{**}},

(35)

which is an open neighbourhood of
s_{**}.
Finally, we calculate that for every
s\in V
and
k=1,\ldots,n
we have

\begin{split}|m_{s}(f_{k})-m_{s_{*}}(f_{k})|&\leqslant|m_{s}(f_{k})-m_{s_{**}}(f_{k})|+|m_{s_{**}}(f_{k})-m_{s_{*}}(f_{k})|\\
&\mathrel{{\mathop{\leqslant}\limits}}|m_{s}(f_{k})-m_{s_{**}}(f_{k})|+\delta\varepsilon\\
&\leqslant\int_{a}^{b}\ |f_{k}(x)|\left|x^{s}-x^{s_{**}}\right|\ dx+\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}\backslash[a,b]}\ |f_{k}(x)|\left|x^{s}-x^{s_{**}}\right|\ dx+\delta\varepsilon\\
&\mathrel{{\mathop{\leqslant}\limits}}\int_{a}^{b}\ |f_{k}(x)||x^{s}-x^{s_{**}}|\ dx+\frac{(1-\delta)\varepsilon}{2}+\delta\varepsilon\\
&\mathrel{{\mathop{<}\limits}}\frac{(1-\delta)\varepsilon}{2\max_{k=1,\ldots,n}\ \|f_{k}\|_{1}}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\ |f_{k}(x)|\ dx+\frac{(1+\delta)\varepsilon}{2}\leqslant\varepsilon.\end{split}

We conclude that
V
is a open neighbourhood of (an arbitrarily chosen)
s_{**}
and
V\subset\omega(U).
Thus
\omega
is an open map.
∎

5
Final remarks on Mellin transform

Entering the final section of the paper, we aim to prove that the Mellin transform is constructed in a similar fashion to the classical Gelfand transform (see Chapter 2.4 in
[
13
]
or Chapter 2 in
[
20
]
). The classical Gelfand theory states that if
\mathcal{A}
is a commutative Banach algebra then there exists a (norm-decreasing) algebra homomorphism

\gamma:\mathcal{A}\longrightarrow C_{0}(\Delta(\mathcal{A}))
given by the formula

\forall_{m\in\Delta(\mathcal{A})}\ \gamma(f)(m):=m(f).

The map
\gamma
is referred to as the Gelfand transform. Although (as we showed earlier in Theorem
5
)
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
is not a commutative Banach algebra, we can still consider a map
\mathfrak{M}
which to every
f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
assigns a function
\mathfrak{M}_{f}
defined on the structure space
\Delta(\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+}))\cong\mathbb{R}
by the formula

\forall_{s\in\mathbb{R}}\ \mathfrak{M}_{f}(s):=m_{s}(f)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\ x^{s-1}f(x)\ dx.

The map
\mathfrak{M}:f\mapsto\mathfrak{M}_{f}
is obviously the Mellin transform, whose codomain is
C(\mathbb{R})
due to the following lemma:

Lemma 19
.

If
f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
then
\mathfrak{M}_{f}\in C(\mathbb{R}).

Proof.

In order to see that
\mathfrak{M}_{f}
is continuous, let
s_{*}\in\mathbb{R}
and let
(s_{n})\subset\mathbb{R}
be a sequence convergent to
s_{*}.
Consider the function

\displaystyle g(x):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cr}x^{\lfloor s_{*}-1\rfloor-1}|f(x)|&\text{if }\ x\in(0,1],\\
x^{\lceil s_{*}-1\rceil+1}|f(x)|&\text{if }\ x\in[1,\infty),\end{array}\right.

which is integrable over
\mathbb{R}_{+}
(since
f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
) and satisfies

|x^{s_{n}-1}f(x)|\leqslant g(x)

for every
x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
and almost every
n\in\mathbb{N}.
By dominated convergence theorem it follows that

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\ \mathfrak{M}_{f}(s_{n})=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\ \int_{0}^{\infty}\ x^{s_{n}-1}f(x)\ dx=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\ \int_{0}^{\infty}\ x^{s_{*}-1}f(x)\ dx=\mathfrak{M}_{f}(s_{*}),

which establishes continuity of
\mathfrak{M}_{f}.

∎

In the classical Gelfand theory, Riemann-Lebesgue lemma (see Lemma 3.3.2 in
[
12
]
, p. 47 or Theorem 1.30 in
[
16
]
, p. 15) states that the Gelfand transform of an element
f
(in a commutative Banach algebra) is not only continuous, but also vanishes at infinity, i.e.,
\gamma(f)\in C_{0}
. Unfortunately, the case of the Mellin transform is different. To see this, it suffices to take
\theta_{2}
from Lemma
6
and observe that

\lim_{s\rightarrow\infty}\ \mathfrak{M}_{\theta_{2}}(s)=\lim_{s\rightarrow\infty}\ \int_{0}^{\infty}\ x^{s-1}\theta_{2}(x)\ dx\geqslant\lim_{s\rightarrow\infty}\ \int_{1}^{2}\ x^{s-1}\ dx=\lim_{s\rightarrow\infty}\ \frac{2^{s}-1}{s}=\infty.

Luckily, the Mellin transform is still an algebra homomorphism (just as Gelfand transform) since for every
s\in\mathbb{R}
we have

\displaystyle\mathfrak{M}_{f\star g}(s)=m_{s}(f\star g)=m_{s}(f)m_{s}(g)=\mathfrak{M}_{f}(s)\mathfrak{M}_{g}(s).

(36)

In other words, we have

\forall_{s\in\mathbb{R}}\ \int_{0}^{\infty}\ x^{s-1}f\star g(x)\ dx=\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}\ x^{s-1}f(x)\ dx\right)\cdot\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}\ x^{s-1}g(x)\ dx\right),

which is the Mellin counterpart of the classical convolution theorem.
