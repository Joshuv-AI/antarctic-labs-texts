Unitarization of the one-loop graviton-graviton scattering amplitudes and study of the graviball
J. A. Oller, Marcela Peláez

Abstract
From the graviton-graviton scattering amplitudes calculated perturbatively in quantum gravity to the one-loop order, we develop further a formalism that allows one to calculate infrared-finite partial-wave amplitudes fulfilling perturbative unitarity. As a result of this process a parameter dubbed ln ⁡ a \ln a emerges that separate between infrared and typical external momenta. The resulting partial-wave amplitudes are next unitarized by employing the Inverse Amplitude Method and the algebraic- N / D N/D method. Then, the graviball resonance, with a similar pole position, is confirmed in the S S -wave partial-wave amplitude for all unitarization methods, also with respect to the unitarization of only the leading-order amplitude. Although the spectrum of the theory is independent of the specific value of ln ⁡ a \ln a , the requirement for a well-behaved unitarized effective field theory of gravity identifies the optimal range of ln ⁡ a \ln a for our next-to-leading-order calculations as 0.5 ≲ ln ⁡ a ≲ 1.7 0.5\lesssim\ln a\lesssim 1.7 . Briefly, we discuss the D D -wave scattering that is weaker than the S − S- wave scattering, repulsive and non-resonant for ln ⁡ a ≈ 1 \ln a\approx 1 .

2
Formalism: Infrared-finite scattering amplitudes and partial-wave amplitudes

For the generic scattering process
\alpha\to\beta
, the relation between the full
S
-matrix
S_{\beta\alpha}
, and the one without infrared divergences
S_{\beta\alpha}^{0}
, was established by Weinberg in
[
27
]
for gravity and QED. By employing dimensional regularization (DR) we would write

\displaystyle S_{\beta\alpha}

\displaystyle=S_{\beta\alpha}^{0}\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}\int^{\Lambda}d^{D}qB(q)\right)~,

(2.1)

where
D=4-2\epsilon
,
and the integration variables correspond to the components of
q
, which is the momentum of and exchanged soft graviton. Here,
\Lambda
is a cutoff that separates between soft,
|{\boldsymbol{q}}|<\Lambda
, and hard,
|{\boldsymbol{q}}|>\Lambda
, gravitons
[
27
]
.
The exponent in Eq. (
2.1
) is denoted by
\Phi_{\beta\alpha}
and it is given by the expression
[
27
]
(in our convention the Minkowski metric is
\text{diag}(-1,+,+,+)
),

\displaystyle\Phi_{\beta\alpha}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{1}{2}\int d^{D}qB(q)=-i4\pi G\sum_{n,m}\eta_{n}\eta_{m}\int^{\Lambda}\frac{d^{D}q}{(2\pi)^{D}}\frac{(p_{n}\cdot p_{m})^{2}-\frac{1}{2}m_{n}^{2}m_{m}^{2}}{(q^{2}+i\varepsilon)(p_{n}\cdot q+i\eta_{n}\varepsilon)(p_{m}\cdot q-i\eta_{m}\varepsilon)}~,

(2.2)

with
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
, and the indices
m
and
n
run both over all the particles involved in the initial and final states. In this equation the mass and momentum of the
n_{\text{th}}
particle are designed by
m_{n}
and
p_{n}
(
p_{n}^{2}=m_{n}^{2}
), respectively. The sign function

\eta_{n}=+1(-1)
for out(in)going particles.

We have performed
the
calculation
of
\Phi_{\beta\alpha}
within DR, since graviton-graviton scattering amplitudes from Ref.
[
1
]
are calculated with this regularization scheme.
Dimensional regularization regularizes the result for the
q\to 0
part of the integral in Eq. (
2.2
).
More details of its calculation will be given elsewhere
[
49
]
. The result
in the massless limit (
m_{n}\to 0
)
is

\displaystyle\Phi_{\beta\alpha}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\left(\frac{1}{\hat{\epsilon}}-\ln\Lambda^{2}\right)\sum_{n,m}\eta_{n}\eta_{m}p_{n}\cdot p_{m}\ln(-\eta_{n}\eta_{m}2p_{n}\cdot p_{m})~,

(2.3)

with
\frac{1}{\hat{\epsilon}}=\frac{1}{\epsilon}+\ln 4\pi-\gamma_{E}
. It also follows from Eq. (
2.1
) that the
S
matrix free of infrared divergences
S_{\beta\alpha}^{0}
corresponds to
S^{0}_{\beta\alpha}=S_{\beta\alpha}\exp\left(-\Phi_{\beta\alpha}\right)
.
Since
S_{\beta\alpha}
does not depend on
\Lambda
, Eqs. (
2.1
) and (
2.3
) imply that the two infrared finite
S
matrices calculated with the cutoffs
\Lambda
and
\Lambda^{\prime}
are related by the factor

\displaystyle\frac{S^{0}_{\beta\alpha}(\Lambda)}{S^{0}_{\beta\alpha}(\Lambda^{\prime})}=\exp\left(\frac{1}{\pi}\ln\frac{\Lambda}{\Lambda^{\prime}}\sum_{n,m}\eta_{n}\eta_{m}p_{n}\cdot p_{m}\ln(-\eta_{n}\eta_{m}2p_{n}\cdot p_{m})\right)\,.

(2.4)

The relation between the
S
and
T
matrices is
S^{0}_{\beta\alpha}=\delta(\beta-\alpha)+iT_{\beta\alpha}
. The graviton-graviton scattering amplitude
T_{\beta\alpha}
in the loop expansion of gravity as a low-energy EFT is written as

\displaystyle T_{\beta\alpha}

\displaystyle=T_{\beta\alpha}^{(0)}+T_{\beta\alpha}^{(1)}+\ldots

(2.5)

where the
superscript between brackets indicates the number of loops involved in the corresponding scattering amplitude, and the ellipsis represents higher orders. We consider here contributions up to the one-loop order or
{\cal O}(G^{2})
. Then, the
T
matrix up to
{\cal O}(G^{2})
gets dressed as

\displaystyle T^{(0)}_{\beta\alpha}+T^{(1)}_{\beta\alpha}-T^{(0)}_{\beta\alpha}\,\Phi_{\beta\alpha}~.

(2.6)

As indicated in the introduction, when applied to QED this procedure is equivalent to the FK method
[
25
,
26
]
, and a proof to all orders in perturbative gravity is given in Ref.
[
13
]
.
Moreover, there is another contribution that stems from the identity operator in the
S
matrix times the expansion of the phase factor,

\displaystyle\mathbb{I}\,\exp\left(-i\,{\rm Im}\Phi_{\beta\alpha}\right).

(2.7)

The real part of
\phi_{\beta\alpha}
does not enter in the dressing the identity operator because the normalization of the states is fixed.
This is explicitly demonstrated for two-body scattering below Eq. (
2.17
).

Equation (
2.7
) is essential for the calculation of infrared finite PWAs
[
29
,
28
]
, because the angular integration over the whole scattering solid angle picks up the identity operator contribution.

2.1
Explicit formulas for the infrared-finite scattering amplitudes

We specifically consider the graviton-graviton scattering process

|{\boldsymbol{p}}_{1}\lambda_{1}\rangle|{\boldsymbol{p}}_{2}\lambda_{2}\rangle\to|{\boldsymbol{p}}_{3}\lambda^{\prime}_{1}\rangle|{\boldsymbol{p}}_{4}\lambda^{\prime}_{2}\rangle
,
where
|{\boldsymbol{p}}_{i}\lambda_{i}\rangle
denotes a graviton state with linear momentum
{\boldsymbol{p}}_{i}
and helicity
\lambda_{i}
. We denote the scattering amplitudes directly calculated from perturbative gravity by
T_{\lambda^{\prime}_{1}\lambda_{2}^{\prime};\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}}(s,t,u)
, where
s
,
t
and
u
are the usual Mandelstam variables:
s=(p_{1}+p_{2})^{2}
,
t=(p_{1}-p_{3})^{2}
and
u=(p_{1}-p_{4})^{2}
. In turn, the infrared-finite graviton-graviton scattering amplitudes are denoted as
A_{\lambda_{1}^{\prime}\lambda_{2}^{\prime};\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}}(s,t,u)
. Since the helicity of a graviton is
\pm 2
, we will only indicate its sign for simplicity.
We can express the different graviton-graviton scattering amplitudes just in terms of three of them, being the others related by crossing and
P
and
T
invariance. At the tree level we have
[
1
]
,

\displaystyle T^{(0)}_{++;++}(s,t,u)

\displaystyle=\frac{8\pi Gs^{4}}{stu}~,

(2.8)

\displaystyle T^{(0)}_{+-;++}(s,t,u)

\displaystyle=0~,

(2.9)

\displaystyle T^{(0)}_{--;++}(s,t,u)

\displaystyle=0~.

At the one-loop level the expressions are
[
1
]
,

\displaystyle T^{(1)}_{++;++}(s,t,u)

\displaystyle=\frac{8G^{2}s^{4}}{stu}\left[\frac{1}{\hat{\epsilon}}f_{1}(s,t,u)+f_{2}(s,t,u)\right]+4(Gs)^{2}h(s,t,u)~,

(2.10)

where

\displaystyle f_{1}(s,t,u)

\displaystyle=s\ln(-s)+t\ln(-t)+u\ln(-u)~,

(2.11)

\displaystyle f_{2}(s,t,u)

\displaystyle=s\ln(-t)\ln(-u)+t\ln(-u)\ln(-s)+u\ln(-s)\ln(-t)~,

\displaystyle h(s,t,u)

\displaystyle=\frac{1922t^{4}+9143t^{3}u+14622t^{2}u^{2}+9143tu^{3}+1922u^{4}}{180s^{4}}

\displaystyle+\frac{(t+2u)(2t+u)(2t^{4}+2t^{3}u-t^{2}u^{2}+2tu^{3}+2u^{4})}{s^{6}}\left(\ln^{2}\frac{t}{u}+\pi^{2}\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{(t-u)(341t^{4}+1609t^{3}u+2566t^{2}u^{2}+1609tu^{3}+341u^{4})}{30s^{5}}\ln\frac{t}{u}~.

For the other two helicity configurations the expressions for the one-loop scattering amplitudes are simpler,

\displaystyle T^{(1)}_{+-;++}(s,t,u)

\displaystyle=\frac{G^{2}(s^{2}+t^{2}+u^{2})}{90}~,

(2.12)

\displaystyle T^{(1)}_{--;++}(s,t,u)

\displaystyle=-\frac{G^{2}(s^{2}+t^{2}+u^{2})}{30}~.

All the scattering amplitudes explicitly displayed are
t\leftrightarrow u
symmetric because gravitons are Bosons and they appear with the same helicity in the final state.
The general expression for
\Phi_{\beta\alpha}
in Eq. (
2.3
), that is independent of helicity, particularizes for graviton-graviton scattering as

\displaystyle\Phi(s,t,u)

\displaystyle=\frac{G}{\pi}\left(\frac{1}{\hat{\epsilon}}-\ln\Lambda^{2}\right)\left[s\ln(-s)+t\ln(-t)+u\ln(-u)\right]~.

(2.13)

Then, the infrared-finite one-loop scattering amplitude
A^{(1)}_{++;++}(s,t,u)
, according to Eq. (
2.6
), is given by

\displaystyle A_{++;++}^{(1)}(s,t,u)=T^{(1)}_{++;++}-T^{(0)}_{++;++}\Phi=\hat{f}_{2}(s,t,u)+4(Gs)^{2}h(s,t,u)\,,

(2.14)

where, for on-shell scattering
s+t+u=0
, it is straightforward to prove that
[
50
]

\displaystyle\hat{f}_{2}(s,t,u)\equiv\ln\Lambda^{2}f_{1}(s,t,u)+f_{2}(s,t,u)

\displaystyle=s\ln\frac{-t}{\Lambda^{2}}\ln\frac{-u}{\Lambda^{2}}+t\ln\frac{-u}{\Lambda^{2}}\ln\frac{-s}{\Lambda^{2}}+u\ln\frac{-t}{\Lambda^{2}}\ln\frac{-s}{\Lambda^{2}}.

(2.15)

Importantly, now all the
\log
functions appear with dimensionless arguments and the scattering amplitude can be directly used for meaningful computations, contrarily to
T^{(1)}_{++;++}(s,t,u)
in Eq. (
2.10
). The absence of infrared-divergences in Eq. (
2.12
) reflects the infrared-divergent structure given in Eq. (
2.6
)
[
27
]
, which is zero at the one-loop level for a vanishing tree-level amplitude, cf. Eq. (
2.9
).

2.2
Explicit formulas for the infrared-finite partial-wave amplitudes

Let us discuss first the angular integration over the scattering angle
\int_{-1}^{+1}d\cos\theta\big(\cdots\big)
of the real part of
A^{(1)}_{++;++}(s,t,u)
, Eq. (
2.14
). Along the physical region,
s>0
, its imaginary part stems from
\ln(-s)=\ln(s)-i\pi
, with
s+i\varepsilon
and
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
. In terms of the scattering angle
\theta
and the variable
s
, one has the well-known formulas
t=-s\sin^{2}\frac{\theta}{2}
, and
u=-s\cos^{2}\frac{\theta}{2}
.
The infrared-finite angular projection of
A_{++;++}^{(0)}
was discussed in Ref.
[
29
]
, and we briefly review it below. It is clear that the angular integration for
A^{(1)}_{+-;++}(s,t,u)
and
A^{(1)}_{--;++}(s,t,u)
does not pose any problem. For the real part of
A_{++;++}^{(1)}(s,t,u)
the only term that needs special discussion is the one divided by
stu
, since the rest of terms are polynomials in
t
and
u
, sometimes multiplying
\ln t/u
or
\ln^{2}t/u
, which are integrable.
Thus, the limits in the integral that require further analysis are
t\to 0
(or
\theta\to 0
) and
u\to 0
(or
\theta\to\pi
). Let us take
\theta\to 0
, for
\theta\to\pi
one can proceed analogously with
t\leftrightarrow u
. Then,
u\to-s
and

\displaystyle s\ln\frac{-t}{\Lambda^{2}}\ln\frac{-u}{\Lambda^{2}}+t\ln\frac{-u}{\Lambda^{2}}\ln\frac{s}{\Lambda^{2}}+u\ln\frac{-t}{\Lambda^{2}}\ln\frac{s}{\Lambda^{2}}={\cal O}(t\ln\frac{-t}{\Lambda^{2}})~,

(2.16)

so that when divided by
stu
the angular integration is finite. Now, let us discuss the partial-wave projection for the imaginary part of
A^{(1)}_{++;++}(s,t,u)
. According to Eq. (
2.14
) we have that

\displaystyle{\rm Im}A^{(1)}_{++;++}(s,t,u)=-8\pi G^{2}s^{3}\left(\frac{1}{u}\ln\frac{-u}{\Lambda^{2}}+\frac{1}{t}\ln\frac{-t}{\Lambda^{2}}\right)~.

(2.17)

In order to end with a meaningful infrared-finite partial-wave projection for
{\rm Im}A_{++;++}^{(1)}(s,t,u)
, we have to consider Eq. (
2.7
) from the redefinition of the
S
matrix.
In this equation only the imaginary part of
\Phi
enters in the exponent with its sign reversed. One can explicitly check from the expression of
\Phi(s,t,u)
given in Eq. (
2.13
) that the real part of
\Phi
vanishes for
\theta=0
and
\pi
, which are the angles that enter in the normalization condition of two gravitons accompanying the identity operator. This explicitly proves that Eq. (
2.7
) is a consequence of the general formula Eq. (
2.1
) that relates the infrared finite and diverging
S
matrices.

For two-graviton scattering

{\rm Im}\Phi=-2Gs\ln\mu/\Lambda
in the physical region, where
\mu\to 0^{+}
is a graviton mass that acts as an infrared regulator, used in Sec. V of Ref.
[
27
]
too.
1
1

1

At this point, this regularization is included to avoid having to treat with a partial-wave expansion in any dimension as it is the case with DR.
This generates an
{\cal O}(G^{2})
contribution to a PWA given by

\displaystyle i\frac{4G^{2}s^{2}}{\pi}\ln^{2}\left(\frac{\mu}{\Lambda}\right)\left(\delta_{\lambda^{\prime}_{1}\lambda_{1}}\delta_{\lambda^{\prime}_{2}\lambda_{2}}+(-1)^{J}\delta_{\lambda^{\prime}_{1}\lambda_{2}}\delta_{\lambda^{\prime}_{2}\lambda_{1}}\right)~,

(2.18)

It follows then that the complete formula for calculating a PWA for the one-loop scattering amplitude is

\displaystyle A^{J(1)}_{\lambda^{\prime}_{1}\lambda^{\prime}_{2};\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}}(s)

\displaystyle\!=\!\frac{1}{8\pi^{2}}\!\!\int_{-1}^{+1}\!\!d\cos\theta\,d_{\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}^{J}(\theta)\,A^{(1)}_{\lambda^{\prime}_{1}\lambda^{\prime}_{2};\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}}(s,t,u)+i\frac{4G^{2}s^{2}}{\pi}\ln^{2}\left(\frac{\mu}{\Lambda}\right)\left(\delta_{\lambda^{\prime}_{1}\lambda_{1}}\delta_{\lambda^{\prime}_{2}\lambda_{2}}+(-1)^{J}\delta_{\lambda^{\prime}_{1}\lambda_{2}}\delta_{\lambda^{\prime}_{2}\lambda_{1}}\right)\,.

(2.19)

Here,
d^{J}_{\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}(\theta)
is the Wigner

-matrix, and
\lambda=\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1}
, and
\lambda^{\prime}=\lambda^{\prime}_{2}-\lambda^{\prime}_{1}
.
Let us exemplify Eq. (
2.19
) for

with
\lambda_{i}=\lambda^{\prime}_{i}=+2

2.17
) that

\displaystyle{\rm Im}A^{0(1)}_{++;++}(s)

\displaystyle\!=\!\frac{8G^{2}s^{2}}{\pi}\ln^{2}\frac{\mu}{\Lambda}-\frac{2G^{2}s^{3}}{\pi}\int_{-1}^{+1}\!\!\!d\cos\theta\frac{\ln(\frac{-t+\mu^{2}}{\Lambda^{2}})}{t-\mu^{2}}\!=\!\frac{8G^{2}s^{2}}{\pi}\ln^{2}\frac{\mu}{\Lambda}+\frac{8G^{2}s^{2}}{\pi}\ln\frac{2p}{\mu}\ln\frac{2p\mu}{\Lambda^{2}}\!=\!\frac{8G^{2}s^{2}}{\pi}\ln^{2}\frac{2p}{\Lambda}~,

.
Then, the partial-wave projection to

\displaystyle A^{J(0)}_{\lambda^{\prime}_{1}\lambda^{\prime}_{2};\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}}(s)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{8\pi^{2}}\int_{-1}^{+1}d\cos\theta\,d_{\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}^{J}(\theta)\,A^{(0)}_{\lambda^{\prime}_{1}\lambda^{\prime}_{2};\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}}(s,t,u)+\frac{4Gs}{\pi}\ln\!\left(\frac{\mu}{\Lambda}\right)\left(\delta_{\lambda^{\prime}_{1}\lambda_{1}}\delta_{\lambda^{\prime}_{2}\lambda_{2}}+(-1)^{J}\delta_{\lambda^{\prime}_{1}\lambda_{2}}\delta_{\lambda^{\prime}_{2}\lambda_{1}}\right).

\displaystyle{\rm Im}A^{J(1)}_{\lambda^{\prime}_{1}\lambda^{\prime}_{2};\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}}(s)=\frac{\pi}{8}\left(A^{J(0)}_{\lambda^{\prime}_{1}\lambda^{\prime}_{2};\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}}\right)^{2}\,,\,s\geq 0\,,

The analytical extrapolation in the complex

3
Unitarized graviton-graviton scattering and the graviball

Given the PWA
A^{J}_{\lambda^{\prime}_{1}\lambda^{\prime}_{2};\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}}(s)
, its associated
S
matrix is

\displaystyle S^{J}_{\lambda^{\prime}_{1}\lambda^{\prime}_{2};\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}}(s)

\displaystyle=1+i\frac{\pi}{4}2^{|\lambda|/4}A^{J}_{\lambda^{\prime}_{1}\lambda^{\prime}_{2};\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}}(s)~.

(3.1)

For single-channel scattering, unitarity in PWAs implies that
|S^{J}_{\lambda^{\prime}_{1}\lambda^{\prime}_{2};\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}}(s)|^{2}=1
which, at the level of the inverse of the PWA, reads
[
54
]

\displaystyle{\rm Im}\frac{1}{A^{J}_{\lambda^{\prime}_{1}\lambda^{\prime}_{2};\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}}(s)}=-\frac{\pi 2^{|\lambda|/4}}{8}~,

(3.2)

valid for
s+i\varepsilon
, with
s>0
. In this work we concentrate on studying the PWA
A^{0}_{++;++}(s)
, and more briefly
A^{2}_{++;++}(s)
in the single-channel approximation. We do not study here the impact of the PWAs
A^{J}_{--;++}(s)
, cf. Eq. (
2.12
), which in principle couple to
A^{J}_{++;++}(s)
, because the former is very much suppressed (
A^{(1)}_{--;++}
is reduced by a factor around 1500 compared to
A^{(1)}_{++;++}
).
Results considering also PWAs built out of
A^{(1)}_{--;++}(s,t,u)
will be shown in
[
49
]
. Since we restrict in the following to helicities equal to
+2
we drop them, and keep in mind that
\lambda=\lambda^{\prime}=0
.

Taking advantage of Eq. (
3.2
), one can fulfill unitarity by implementing a unitarization method, which should also respect proper analytical properties, like the existence of the unitarity or right-hand cut and of the left-hand cut. Two popular algebraic unitarization methods fulfilling these requirements are the IAM and the algebraic-
N/D
methods.
The IAM
[
36
,
37
,
38
,
39
]
can be straightforwardly obtained by performing the expansion of
1/A^{J}
in powers of
G
up to NLO, and then directly inverting the outcome. The PWA obtained is denoted by
A^{J}_{{\text{IAM}}}(s)
and is given by

\displaystyle A^{J}_{{\text{IAM}}}(s)

\displaystyle=\frac{A^{J(0)}(s)^{2}}{A^{J(0)}(s)-A^{J(1)}(s)}~.

(3.3)

A^{J}_{{\text{IAM}}}(s)
fulfills unitarity, Eq. (
3.2
), or equivalently that
{\rm Im}A^{J}_{{\text{IAM}}}(s)=|A^{J}_{{\text{IAM}}}(s)|^{2}\pi/8
, as a consequence of
A^{J(1)}(s)
satisfying perturbative unitarity, Eq. (
2.22
).
The PWA resulting by the algebraic-
N/D
method
[
33
,
34
]
,
A^{J}_{{\text{ND}}}(s)
, satisfies unitarity by implementing the parameterization

\displaystyle A^{J}_{{\text{ND}}}(s)=\frac{R^{J}(s)}{1+R^{J}(s)g(s)}~,

(3.4)

where
\displaystyle{g(s)=\frac{1}{8}\ln\frac{-s}{Q^{2}}}
.
We notice that
g(s)
is an analytical function
with only a right-hand cut,
and its imaginary part along the cut is
-\pi/8
, matching
{\rm Im}A^{J}(s)^{-1}
in Eq. (
3.2
). It follows then that

{\rm Im}R^{J}(s)=0
for
s>0
, so that it has only left-hand cut. This function is calculated perturbatively in powers of
G
by reproducing the perturbative expansion of
A^{J}_{{\text{ND}}}(s)=A^{J(0)}+A^{J(1)}+{\cal O}(G^{2})
order by order. Thus,
R^{J}=R^{J(0)}+R^{J(1)}+{\cal O}(G^{2})
with

\displaystyle R^{J(0)}(s)

\displaystyle=A^{J(0)}(s)~,

(3.5)

\displaystyle R^{J(1)}(s)

\displaystyle=A^{J(1)}(s)+A^{J(0)}(s)^{2}g(s)~.

(3.6)

At every order, the resulting function
R^{J}(s)
is then substituted in Eq. (
3.4
). Notice that
1/A^{J}(s)
has the nice feature that it vanishes whenever
A^{J}(s)
(in the second Riemann sheet) has as resonance pole.

Then, we distinguish between the application of the algebraic-
N/D
method at LO, with
R^{J}(s)=R^{J(0)}(s)
, and at NLO, with
R^{J}=R^{J(0)}+R^{J(1)}
. The former is denoted in short as ND0 and the latter as ND1, with the corresponding PWAs called
A^{J}_{\text{ND0}}
and
A^{J}_{\text{ND1}}
, respectively.
The unitarization scale

in

{\cal O}(G^{3})
since, by construction, the algebraic-
N/D

{\cal O}(G^{2})
. Following the discussion in the introduction we take

Let us elaborate on the meaning of the optimized interval of values for
\ln a
around 1 within our method.
From the point of view of EFT, we already remarked above that the parameter
\ln a
controls the size of the ultraviolet scale
Q^{2}\simeq\pi G/\ln a
, that we determine by studying the unitarity corrections
[
28
,
29
]
from the combination
A^{J(0)}_{++;++}g(s)\to s\frac{G\ln a}{\pi}\equiv s/Q^{2}
. The corrections from the imaginary part of
g(s)
are larger by a factor
\pi
at NLO. Within the unitarized theory the larger corrections from
\text{Im}g(s)
are immediately accounted for, as well as a calculation of those unitarity corrections from the real part of the unitarity loops by invoking analyticity along the right-hand cut. From the EFT perspective, it does not make sense for
\ln a
to be too large, as this would make
Q^{2}
too small, compromising the convergence properties of the perturbative expansion needed for the calculation of
R^{J}
, cf. Eqs. (
3.5
) and (
3.6
). On the other hand, if
\ln a
were too small,
Q^{2}
would be an overly optimistic (too large) estimate for the actual ultraviolet scale. Thus, from the EFT viewpoint,
\ln a
should be
{\cal O}(1)
; otherwise, the situation could be considered fine tuned. Our analysis, which considers corrections from LO to NLO and compares different unitarization methods, provides a more accurate determination of the optimal interval for
\ln a
, which is
0.5\lesssim\ln a\lesssim 1.7
. In general, as the order of calculation increases, the dependence of the poles on
\ln a
for
\ln a={\cal O}(1)
is expected to decrease, while the range of values for which this holds should expand and become broader. This trend is clearly illustrated in Fig.
2
: the pole positions for ND1 (orange points) are clustered within a remarkably narrow region, whereas the pole positions for ND0 at LO remain unbounded as
\ln a\to 0^{+}
.

, which indicates the repulsive character of the

, and for

changes sign, so that the

the attractive interaction has enough strength to give rise to resonance poles with

, that become lighter as

4
Conclusions and outlook

Summarizing, we have discussed how to calculate infrared-finite partial-wave amplitudes (PWAs) for graviton-graviton scattering, and apply it to one loop in perturbative gravity
[
1
]
. For removing the infrared divergences we have first followed the results of Ref.
[
27
]
. However, when performing the angular integrals needed to calculate a PWA new divergences arise. To remove them, one has to account for a redefinition by a phase factor
[
29
]
of the identity operator, which is part of the relation between the
S

T
