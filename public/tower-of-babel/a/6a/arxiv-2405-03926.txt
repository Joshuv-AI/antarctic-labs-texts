Generalized Nash equilibrium problems with Quasi-linear constraints
Jiyoung Choi, Jiawang Nie, Xindong Tang, Suhan Zhong

Abstract
. We study generalized Nash equilibrium problems (GNEPs) such that objectives are polynomial functions, and each player’s constraints are linear in their own strategy. For such GNEPs, the KKT sets can be represented as unions of simpler sets by Carathéodory’s theorem. We give a convenient representation for KKT sets using partial Lagrange multiplier expressions. This produces a set of branch polynomial optimization problems, which can be efficiently solved by Moment-SOS relaxations. By doing this, we can compute all generalized Nash equilibria or detect their nonexistence. This method may not be very scalable to large scale GNEPs. Numerical experiments are provided to demonstrate the computational efficiency.

1.
Introduction

The generalized Nash equilibrium problem (GNEP) is a class of games that determines strategies for a group of players so that each player’s benefit cannot be improved for the given strategy of other players.
Suppose there are
N
players, and the
i
th player’s strategy is represented by
the
n_{i}
-dimensional real vector

x_{i}\coloneqq(x_{i,1},\ldots,x_{i,n_{i}})\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{i}}
.
The tuple
x\coloneqq(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})

denotes the set of all players’ strategies, with the total dimension

n\coloneqq n_{1}+\cdots+n_{N}.

When the
i
th player’s strategy
x_{i}
is focused, for convenience, we also write

x=(x_{i},x_{-i}),\quad\mbox{with}\quad x_{-i}\,\coloneqq\,(x_{1},\ldots,x_{i-1},x_{i+1},\ldots,x_{N}).

Assume the
i
th player’s decision optimization problem is

(1.1)

\mbox{F}_{i}(x_{-i}):\,\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\min\limits_{x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{i}}}&f_{i}(x_{i},x_{-i})\\
\mathit{s.t.}&g_{i}(x_{i},x_{-i})\geq 0,\end{array}\right.

where
g_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m_{i}}
is an
m_{i}
-dimensional vector-valued function.
For convenience, we only consider inequality constrained GNEPs.
The discussion for GNEPs with equality constraints is quite similar.
A tuple of strategies
u=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{N})
is said to be a
generalized Nash
equilibrium
(GNE) if each
u_{i}
is a minimizer of
\mbox{F}_{i}(u_{-i})
.
Throughout the paper, an optimizer means a global optimizer,
unless its meaning is specified.
For each
i=1,\ldots,N
and for a given
x_{-i}
, we let

\mathcal{S}_{i}(x_{-i})
denote the set of minimizers for
\mbox{F}_{i}(x_{-i})
.
Therefore, the set
\mathcal{S}
of all GNEs can be written as

(1.2)

\mathcal{S}\,=\,\Big\{(u_{1},\ldots,u_{N}):\ u_{i}\in\mathcal{S}_{i}(u_{-i})\,\,\mbox{for}\,\,i=1,\ldots,N\Big\}.

In this paper, we consider a broad class of GNEPs such that
all objectives
f_{i}
are polynomials in
x
,
while the optimization problem
\mbox{F}_{i}(x_{-i})
has

constraints that are linear in
x_{i}

(they may be polynomial in
x_{-i}
, hence we call them quasi-linear constraints).
We assume that
g_{i}=(g_{i,1},\ldots,g_{i,m_{i}})

is such that for each
j\in[m_{i}]\coloneqq\{1,\ldots,m_{i}\},

it holds

(1.3)

g_{i,j}(x)\,=\,a_{i,j}^{T}x_{i}-b_{i,j}(x_{-i}),

where each
a_{i,j}
is a constant
n_{i}
-dimensional real vector
and
b_{i,j}(x_{-i})
is a scalar polynomial in
x_{-i}
.
For convenience, denote

A_{i}\,\coloneqq\,\begin{bmatrix}a_{i,1}\ \ldots\ a_{i,m_{i}}\end{bmatrix}^{T},\quad b_{i}(x_{-i})\,\coloneqq\,\begin{bmatrix}b_{i,1}(x_{-i})\ \ldots\ b_{i,m_{i}}(x_{-i})\end{bmatrix}^{T},

where the superscript
T
means the transpose of a matrix or vector.
Then
g_{i}(x)=A_{i}x_{i}-b_{i}(x_{-i})
and the
i
th player’s feasible set can be written as

(1.4)

X_{i}(x_{-i})\,\coloneqq\,\left\{x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{i}}:\,A_{i}x_{i}-b_{i}(x_{-i})\,\geq\,0\right\}.

The entire feasible set of the GNEP is

(1.5)

X\,\coloneqq\,\Big\{(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}):\,A_{i}x_{i}-b_{i}(x_{-i})\,\geq\,0\,\,\text{for all}\,\,i=1,\ldots,N\Big\}.

The GNEP (
1.1
) is called a
Nash equilibrium problem
(NEP) if
each feasible set
X_{i}(x_{-i})
is independent of
x_{-i}
.
A solution to the NEP is then called a
Nash equilibrium
(NE).
The GNEP is said to be
convex
if each

\mbox{F}_{i}(x_{-i})
is a convex optimization problem in
x_{i}

for every given
x_{-i}
such that
X_{i}(x_{-i})\neq\emptyset
.
GNEPs were originally introduced to model economic problems.
They are now widely used in various applications, such as transportation, telecommunications, and machine learning.
We refer to
[
6
,
13
,
14
,
15
,
38
,
39
]

for applications and surveys of GNEPs.

Solving GNEPs is typically a challenging task, primarily due to the interactions among different players’ strategies concerning the objectives and feasible sets.
There may be none or a finite number of distinct GNEs,
even for strictly convex NEPs
[
34
]
.
Much earlier work exists to solve GNEPs.
Some of them apply classical nonlinear optimization methods,
such as the penalty method
[
2
,
14
]

and Augmented-Lagrangian method
[
22
]
.
Variational inequality and quasi-variational inequality reformulations
are also frequently used to solve GNEPs; see the work

[
11
,
38
,
42
]
.
The Nikaido-Isoda function type methods are proposed in

[
9
,
44
]
.
The ADMM-type methods for GNEPs in Hilbert spaces are introduced in
[
3
]
.
The Gauss-Seidel type methods are proposed in
[
16
]
.
Methods based on
Karush-Kuhn-Tucker
(KKT) conditions
are given in
[
8
,
12
]
.
Certain convexity assumptions are often needed for these methods to be guaranteed to compute a GNE.
Numerical algorithms for solving linear GNEPs are studied in
[
10
]
.
It is generally quite challenging to solve nonconvex GNEPs.
As an alternative, for nonconvex GNEPs, some work aims to find quasi-NEs introduced in
[
6
,
39
]
.
For more detailed introductions to GNEPs, we refer to

[
13
,
15
]
.

Contributions

GNEPs given by polynomial or rational functions are studied in

[
33
,
35
,
36
]
.
Particularly, in
[
33
,
36
]
, Moment-SOS
relaxation methods are proposed to find GNEs or to detect their nonexistence.
These methods require
Lagrange multiplier expressions
(LMEs),
which are known and convenient for some common constraints like simplices, balls, or cubes.
However, for more general constraints, LMEs are quite expensive to obtain.
In particular, for GNEPs with many linear constraints, the usage of LMEs is quite inconvenient.
In this paper, we study this kind of GNEP, which has many quasi-linear constraints.
The linear property of constraints can be used to get computationally convenient expressions for Lagrange multipliers.
This novel approach greatly improves the efficiency of solving GNEPs.

Note that
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})
is a GNE if and only if
every
x_{i}\in\mathcal{S}_{i}(x_{-i})
. In computation,
one can relax
x_{i}\in\mathcal{S}_{i}(x_{-i})
by KKT conditions.
For the
i
th player’s decision problem
\mbox{F}_{i}(x_{-i})
,
these conditions are

(1.6)

\boxed{\begin{array}[]{c}\nabla_{x_{i}}f_{i}(x)-A_{i}^{T}\lambda_{i}=0,\\
0\leq\,\lambda_{i}\,\perp\,\big(A_{i}x_{i}-b_{i}(x_{-i})\big)\,\geq 0.\end{array}}

In the above,
\nabla_{x_{i}}
denotes the gradient in the subvector
x_{i}
and

\lambda_{i}=\begin{bmatrix}\lambda_{i,1}&\cdots&\lambda_{i,m_{i}}\end{bmatrix}^{T}

is the vector of Lagrange multipliers.
The notation
\lambda_{i}\perp g_{i}
means that
\lambda_{i}
and
g_{i}(x)
are perpendicular to each other.
The strategy vector
x
is called a KKT point
if for each
i\in[N]
, there exists
\lambda_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{i}}

such that
(x,\lambda_{i})
satisfies (
1.6
).
We remark that when
all
f_{i}
and
b_{i}
are generic polynomials
and each
A_{i}
is a generic matrix,
the GNEP with quasi-linear constraints has finitely many KKT points.
This is shown in
[
32
]
.

It is usually not easy to solve (
1.6
) directly to get a KKT point,
since there are Lagrange multiplier variables like
\lambda_{i}
.
To solve (
1.6
) more efficiently,
LMEs are introduced in
[
33
,
36
]
.
Generally, there exists a vector function

\tau_{i}(x)
such that

(1.7)

\lambda_{i}=\tau_{i}(x)\,\mbox{ satisfies (\ref{eq:KKT1}) for every KKT point $x$. }

Such
\tau_{i}(x)
is called a Lagrange multiplier expression.
For
A_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{i}\times n_{i}}
, the transpose
A_{i}^{T}
is
n_{i}
-by-
m_{i}
.
For the special case that
\mbox{rank}\,A_{i}=m_{i}
,

\lambda_{i}\,=\,(A_{i}A_{i}^{T})^{-1}A_{i}\nabla_{x_{i}}f_{i}(x).

However, for general cases where
\mbox{rank}\,A_{i}<m_{i}
,
the above LMEs are not applicable.

When GNEPs have quasi-linear constraints,
we propose a computationally efficient way to get LMEs.
For each fixed GNE
x
, the KKT system (
1.6
)
has a Lagrange multiplier vector
\lambda_{i}

with at most
r_{i}\coloneqq\mbox{rank}\,A_{i}
nonzero entries.
This is implied by Carathéodory’s theorem.
Suppose
J_{i}\subseteq[m_{i}]
is the label set of
nonzero entries of
\lambda_{i}
, with the cardinality
|J_{i}|=r_{i}
.
Let
A_{i,J_{i}}\in\mathbb{R}^{r_{i}\times n_{i}}
be the submatrix of
A_{i}
whose rows are labelled by
J_{i}
,
and we define
\lambda_{i,J_{i}},b_{i,J_{i}}
respectively in a similar way.
Then (
1.6
) implies the equation

\nabla_{x_{i}}f_{i}(x)-A_{i,J_{i}}^{T}\lambda_{i,J_{i}}=0.

Assume
A_{i,J_{i}}
is invertible (i.e.,
r_{i}=n_{i}
). Then,

\lambda_{i,J_{i}}=(A_{i,J_{i}}^{T})^{-1}\nabla_{x_{i}}f_{i}(x).

When
r_{i}<n_{i}
, expressions of
\lambda_{i,J_{i}}
are given in Subsection
3.1
.
The above right-hand side is a polynomial function in
x
.
We call it a
partial Lagrange multiplier expression
(pLME)
[
37
]
.
Then, (
1.6
) simplifies to

(1.8)

0\leq\,(A_{i,J_{i}}^{T})^{-1}\nabla_{x_{i}}f_{i}(x)\,\perp\,\big(A_{i,J_{i}}x_{i}-b_{i,J_{i}}(x_{-i})\big)\,\geq 0,\quad i=1,\ldots,N.

In computational practice, the label set
J_{i}
is usually unknown.
However, we can enumerate all such

J_{i}\subseteq[m_{i}]
with
|J_{i}|=r_{i}
.
Therefore, the KKT set
\mathcal{K}
can be represented as

(1.9)

\mathcal{K}\,=\,\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}J_{i}\subseteq[m_{i}],|J_{i}|=r_{i}\end{subarray}}\Big\{x\in X:x\,\,\mbox{satisfies}\,\,(\ref{LME:Ji:perp})\Big\}.

Using pLMEs for the KKT set, we propose a method for finding all GNEs,
for both convex and nonconvex GNEPs.
Our major contributions are:

•

We give a pLME representation for the KKT set as in (
1.9
).
The pLMEs are explicitly given in closed formulae.
They are computationally convenient and efficient.

•

Based on partial Lagrange multiplier expressions,
we give a method for computing GNEs for both convex and nonconvex GNEPs.
When the KKT set
\mathcal{K}
is finite (this is the generic case),
we can find
all
GNEs or detect their nonexistence.
For non-generic GNEPs, our method is still applicable, but it may or may not get all GNEs.

•

We remark that our method is
not
enumerating active constraining sets
since we only consider label sets
J_{i}\subseteq[m_{i}]
with
|J_{i}|=r_{i}
.
The number of our enumerations depends on the gap
m_{i}-r_{i}
.
To the best of our knowledge, this is the first work to apply this approach to solve GNEPs.

•

The proposed method is effective for solving GNEPs
with quasi-linear constraints for problems of moderate size.
However, we acknowledge that it may not be scalable to large-scale GNEPs.
It is still a computational challenge to solve large-scale GNEPs.
This is important future work.

reviews notation and some basics for polynomial optimization.
Section

In Section
4
, we give algorithms for solving GNEPs.
Section
5
introduces the Moment-SOS relaxations for solving polynomial optimization.
Numerical experiments are presented in Section
6
.
Conclusions and some discussions are given in Section
7
.

,

.

,

n>0
,

, while
\mathbf{1}

\mathbf{0}_{n_{1}\times n_{2}}

,
|T|
denotes its cardinality.

.

(2.1)

[x]_{d}\coloneqq[1\quad x_{1}\quad\cdots\quad x_{n}\quad x_{1}^{2}\quad x_{1}x_{2}\quad\cdots\quad x_{n-1}x_{n}^{d-1}\quad x_{n}^{d}].

,

)

2.1.
Ideals and quadratic modules

For a polynomial
p\in\mathbb{R}[x]
and subsets
I,J\subseteq\mathbb{R}[x]
, define the product and the Minkowski sum

p\cdot I\coloneqq\{pq:q\in I\},\,\,\,I+J\coloneqq\{a+b:a\in I,b\in J\}.

A subset
I\subseteq\mathbb{R}[x]
is an ideal of
\mathbb{R}[x]

if
I+I\subseteq I
and
p\cdot I\subseteq I
for all
p\in\mathbb{R}[x]
.
For a tuple
h=(h_{1},\ldots,h_{m})
of polynomials in
\mathbb{R}[x]
,
the ideal generated by
h
is

\mbox{Ideal}[h]\,\coloneqq\,h_{1}\cdot\mathbb{R}[x]+\cdots+h_{m}\cdot\mathbb{R}[x].

For a degree
d\geq\deg(h)
, we denote the degree-
d
truncation of
\mbox{Ideal}[h]

as

\mbox{Ideal}[h]_{d}\coloneqq h_{1}\cdot\mathbb{R}[x]_{d-\deg(h_{1})}+\cdots+h_{m}\cdot\mathbb{R}[x]_{d-\deg(h_{m})}.

The real zero set of
h
is

Z(h)\coloneqq\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:h_{1}(x)=\cdots=h_{m}(x)=0\}.

A polynomial
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}[x]
is said to be a sum of squares (SOS)
if there are polynomials
p_{1},\ldots,p_{k}\in\mathbb{R}[x]
such that
\sigma=p_{1}^{2}+\cdots+p_{k}^{2}
.
The set of all SOS polynomials in
x
is denoted as
\Sigma[x]
.
In computation, we often work with the degree-
d
truncation

\Sigma[x]_{d}\coloneqq\Sigma[x]\cap\mathbb{R}[x]_{d}.

For a polynomial tuple
g\coloneqq(g_{1},\ldots,g_{t})
, denote

S(g)\coloneqq\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:g_{1}(x)\geq 0,\ldots,g_{t}(x)\geq 0\}.

The quadratic module generated by
g
is:

\mbox{QM}[g]\coloneqq\Sigma[x]+g_{1}\cdot\Sigma[x]+\cdots+g_{t}\cdot\Sigma[x].

The degree-
d
truncation of
\mbox{QM}[g]
is similarly defined as

\mbox{QM}[g]_{d}\coloneqq\Sigma[x]_{d}+g_{1}\cdot\Sigma[x]_{d-\deg(g_{1})}+\cdots+g_{t}\cdot\Sigma[x]_{d-\deg(g_{t})}.

If
f\in\mbox{Ideal}[h]+\mbox{QM}[g]
, then it is clear that
f\geq 0
on
Z(h)\cap S(g)
.
The reverse is not necessarily true.
The set
\mbox{Ideal}[h]+\mbox{QM}[g]
is said to be
archimedean

if there exists
q\in\mbox{Ideal}[h]+\mbox{QM}[g]
such that

S(q)
is a compact set. When

\mbox{Ideal}[h]+\mbox{QM}[g]
is archimedean, if
f>0
on
Z(h)\cap S(g)
,
then
f\in\mbox{Ideal}[h]+\mbox{QM}[g]
.
This conclusion is referenced as
Putinar’s Positivstellensatz

[
40
]
.
Interestingly, if
f\geq 0
on
Z(h)\cap S(g)
, we also have
f\in\mbox{Ideal}[h]+\mbox{QM}[g]
, under some standard optimality conditions
[
30
]
.

2.2.
Localizing and moment matrices

A real vector
y
is called a
truncated multi-sequence
(tms) of degree
2k
if it is labeled as

y\,=\,(y_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n}_{2k}}.

For a tms
y\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}^{n}_{2k}}
and a polynomial

f=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n}_{2k}}f_{\alpha}x^{\alpha}
,
define the operation

(2.2)

\langle f,y\rangle\,\coloneqq\,\sum\limits_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n}_{2k}}f_{\alpha}y_{\alpha}.

In particular, if
y=[\hat{x}]_{2k}
for some
\hat{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
then
\langle f,y\rangle=\langle f,[\hat{x}]_{2k}\rangle=f(\hat{x})
,
where
[\hat{x}]_{2k}
is the monomial vector as in (
2.1
).
For
q\in\mathbb{R}[x]_{2k}
and
t=k-\lceil\mbox{deg}(q)/2\rceil
,
the product
q\cdot[x]_{t}[x]_{t}^{T}
is a symmetric matrix polynomial of length
\binom{n+t}{t}
,
which can be expressed as

q\cdot[x]_{t}[x]_{t}^{T}=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n}_{2k}}x^{\alpha}Q_{\alpha}

for some symmetric matrices
Q_{\alpha}
.
For
y\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}^{n}_{2k}}
, denote the matrix

L_{q}^{(k)}[y]\coloneqq\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n}_{2k}}y_{\alpha}Q_{\alpha}.

It is called the
k
th order
localizing matrix
of
q
generated by
y
.
In particular, if
q=1
(the constant
1
polynomial), then

L_{q}^{(k)}[y]
is reduced to the moment matrix

M_{k}[y]\,\coloneqq\,L_{1}^{(k)}[y].

2.3.
Moment-SOS relaxations

Quadratic modules, moment, and localizing matrices are useful
for solving polynomial optimization.
Consider the polynomial optimization problem:

(2.3)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\min\limits_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}&f(x)\\
\mathit{s.t.}&h(x)=0,\quad g(x)\geq 0,\end{array}\right.

For each positive integer
k
such that
2k\geq\max\{\deg(f),\deg(h),\deg(g)\}
, the
k
th order SOS relaxation of the above problem is:

(2.4)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\max\limits_{\gamma\in\mathbb{R}}&\gamma\\
\mathit{s.t.}&f-\gamma\in\mbox{Ideal}[h]_{2k}+\mbox{QM}[g]_{2k}.\end{array}\right.

When
\mbox{Ideal}[h]+\mbox{QM}[g]
is archimedean, the optimal value of (
2.4
) converges to that of (
2.3
)
as
k
goes to infinity.
Problem (
2.4
) is a semidefinite program,
whose dual problem is the
k
th order moment relaxation of (
2.3
):

(2.5)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\min&\langle f,y\rangle\\
\mathit{s.t.}&y\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}_{2k}^{n}},\,y_{0}=1,\,M_{k}[y]\succeq 0,\\
&L_{h_{i}}^{(k)}[y]=0\,(i\in[m]),\\
&L_{g_{j}}^{(k)}[y]\succeq 0\,(j\in[t]).\end{array}\right.

The primal-dual pair (
2.4
)–(
2.5
) forms
the
k
th order level of the Moment-SOS hierarchy associated with (
2.3
).
Polynomial optimization and the Moment-SOS hierarchy are also discussed in Section
5

with more details.
We refer to
[
19
,
23
,
24
,
28
]

for a thorough introduction to this field.

2.4.
Carathéodory’s theorem and Lagrange multipliers

Carathéodory’s theorem is useful for deriving pLMEs for GNEPs.

Theorem 2.1
.

[
28
, Theorem 1.2.4]

Let
S
be a nonempty set of a vector space
V
of dimension
m
.
Then, every point in the conic hull of
S
can be expressed as a conic combination
of at most
m
points.

The following is an example of applying
Carathéodory’s theorem to get pLMEs.

Example 2.2
.

Consider the linear programming:

(2.6)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\min\limits_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}}&x_{1}+x_{2}+x_{3}\\
\mathit{s.t.}&Ax\geq b,\end{array}\right.

where
b=\begin{bmatrix}0&0&0&1&1&1&2&3&3&2\end{bmatrix}^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{10}
and

A=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0&1&1&0&1&2&1&2\\
0&1&0&1&0&1&1&1&2&1\\
0&0&1&0&1&1&2&0&0&1\end{bmatrix}^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{10\times 3}.

The set of minimizers of (
2.6
) is identical to the set of KKT points.
Let
u=(1,1,0)^{T}.

We verify that
u
is the minimizer of (
2.6
) by checking the KKT conditions.
Note that

Au-b=\begin{bmatrix}1\ \ 1\ \ 0\ \ 1\ \ 0\ \ 0\ \ 0\ \ 0\ \ 0\ \ 1\end{bmatrix}^{T}.

Since
u
is active at six constraints labeled with

J_{1}=\{3,5,6,7,8,9\}
, if it is a KKT point, then the corresponding Lagrange multiplier vector

\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{10})
must satisfy
\lambda_{j}=0
for all
j\notin J_{1}
, and

A^{T}\lambda=\mathbf{1},\quad\lambda_{j}\geq 0,\quad\forall j\in J_{1}.

The above is an underdetermined system over the nonnegative orthant,
which has multiple solutions.
By Carathéodory’s theorem, it has a solution with at most three nonzero entries.
Let
J_{2}=\{3,8,9\}\subseteq J_{1}
and consider the following induced system

\begin{bmatrix}0&2&1\\
0&1&2\\
1&0&0\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\lambda_{3}\\
\lambda_{8}\\
\lambda_{9}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}1\\
1\\
1\end{bmatrix},\quad\lambda_{j}\geq 0\,(\forall j\in J_{2}),\quad\lambda_{j}=0\,(\forall j\notin J_{2}).

It admits a unique solution that gives the vector of Lagrange multipliers such that

\lambda_{3}=1,\quad\lambda_{8}=\lambda_{9}=\frac{1}{3},\quad\lambda_{j}=0\,(\forall j\notin J_{2}).

This implies that
u
is an optimizer of (
2.6
).

3.
Partial Lagrange Multiplier Expressions

This section discusses how to find a convenient representation for the KKT set with
partial Lagrange multiplier expressions
(pLMEs).
We consider GNEPs with quasi-linear constraints.
The
i
th player’s decision optimization problem
\mbox{F}_{i}(x_{-i})
reads

(3.1)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\min\limits_{x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{i}}}&f_{i}(x_{i},x_{-i})\\
\mathit{s.t.}&A_{i}x_{i}-b_{i}(x_{-i})\geq 0,\end{array}\right.

where
A_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{i}\times n_{i}}
and
b_{i}(x_{-i})
is a polynomial vector in
x_{-i}
.
Recall the notation

A_{i}\,=\,\begin{bmatrix}a_{i,1}&\cdots&a_{i,m_{i}}\end{bmatrix}^{T}.

Since the constraints of (
3.1
) are linear,
for every optimizer
x_{i}\in\mathcal{S}_{i}(x_{-i})
, there exists a Lagrange multiplier vector

\lambda_{i}=(\lambda_{i,1},\ldots,\lambda_{i,m_{i}})
such that

(3.2)

\boxed{\begin{array}[]{c}\nabla_{x_{i}}f_{i}(x)-A_{i}^{T}\lambda_{i}=0,\\
0\leq\lambda_{i}\perp(A_{i}x_{i}-b_{i}(x_{-i}))\geq 0.\end{array}}

The set of all KKT points is

(3.3)

\mathcal{K}\,=\,\left\{x\in X\left|\begin{array}[]{c}\mbox{$\exists\,(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{N})$ such that for each $i\in[N]$,}\\
\nabla_{x_{i}}f_{i}(x)-A_{i}^{T}\lambda_{i}=0\,\,,\\
0\leq\lambda_{i}\perp(A_{i}x_{i}-b_{i}(x_{-i}))\geq 0\end{array}\right.\right\}.

When
\mbox{rank}\,A_{i}=m_{i}\leq n_{i}
, we can get the following expression for
\lambda_{i}
:

\lambda_{i}\,=\,(A_{i}A_{i}^{T})^{-1}A_{i}\nabla_{x_{i}}f_{i}(x).

When
m_{i}>n_{i}
, the above expression is not available
since
A_{i}A_{i}^{T}
is singular.
Indeed, for the case
m_{i}>n_{i}
, a polynomial expression for
\lambda_{i}

typically does not exist, since
b_{i}(x_{-i})
depends on
x_{-i}
,
but a rational expression for
\lambda_{i}
always exists.
This is shown in
[
33
,
36
]
.
However, such an expression for
\lambda_{i}
may be too complicated to be practical.
The expression becomes more complicated if the gap
m_{i}-n_{i}
is large
(see
[
31
, Proposition 4.1]
).

We look for more convenient expressions for
\lambda_{i}
. Let

(3.4)

r_{i}\,\coloneqq\,\mbox{rank}\,A_{i}.

For each
x\in\mathcal{K}
, the KKT system (
3.2
)
has a solution
\lambda_{i}
that has at most
r_{i}
nonzero entries by Carathéodory’s Theorem.
This means that
m_{i}-r_{i}
entries of such a
\lambda_{i}
must be zeros.
If the label set
J_{i}
of nonzero entries of
\lambda_{i}
is known, then the expression for
\lambda_{i}
can be simplified.
This gives a pLME for
\lambda_{i}
.

3.1.
The pLMEs

To find pLMEs, consider the linear system

(3.5)

\nabla_{x_{i}}f_{i}(x)\,=\,A_{i}^{T}\lambda_{i},\quad\lambda_{i}\geq 0.

For a subset
J_{i}\subseteq[m_{i}]
,
let
A_{i,J_{i}}
denote the submatrix of
A_{i}

whose row labels are in
J_{i}
, and so is
\lambda_{i,J_{i}}
.
That is,

A_{i,J_{i}}\coloneqq\begin{bmatrix}a_{i,j}^{T}\end{bmatrix}_{j\in J_{i}},\quad\lambda_{i,J_{i}}\coloneqq\begin{bmatrix}\lambda_{i,j}\end{bmatrix}_{j\in J_{i}}.

Let
r_{i}
be the rank as in (
3.4
).
Denote the set of label sets

(3.6)

\mathcal{P}_{i}\,\coloneqq\,\Big\{J_{i}\subseteq[m_{i}]:|J_{i}|=r_{i},\,\,\mbox{rank}\,A_{i,J_{i}}=r_{i}\Big\}.

Since
A_{i}
is
m_{i}
-by-
n_{i}
, we know
r_{i}\leq\min\{m_{i},n_{i}\}

and each
\mathcal{P}_{i}
is nonempty.
For each
J_{i}\in\mathcal{P}_{i}
, the vector

\lambda_{i}=(\lambda_{i,1},\ldots,\lambda_{i,m_{i}})\geq 0

is said to be a
basic feasible solution
of (
3.5
)
with respect to
J_{i}
if
\lambda_{i,j}=0
for all
j\not\in J_{i}
and

(3.7)

\nabla_{x_{i}}f_{i}(x)\,=\,\sum\limits_{j\in J_{i}}\lambda_{i,j}a_{i,j}\,=\,A_{i,J_{i}}^{T}\lambda_{i,J_{i}}.

Basic feasible solutions can be conveniently expressed by pLMEs.
Multiplying
A_{i,J_{i}}
on both sides of (
3.7
), we get

(A_{i,J_{i}}A_{i,J_{i}}^{T})\lambda_{i,J_{i}}\,=\,A_{i,J_{i}}\nabla_{x_{i}}f_{i}(x).

Since
\mbox{rank}\,A_{i,J_{i}}=r_{i}
, this gives the pLME:

(3.8)

\lambda_{i,J_{i}}\,=\,\lambda_{i,J_{i}}(x)\,\coloneqq\,(A_{i,J_{i}}A_{i,J_{i}}^{T})^{-1}A_{i,J_{i}}\nabla_{x_{i}}f_{i}(x).

In particular, for the case
r_{i}=n_{i}
, the above simplifies to

(3.9)

\lambda_{i,J_{i}}(x)\,=\,(A_{i,J_{i}})^{-T}\nabla_{x_{i}}f_{i}(x).

Here, the superscript denotes the transpose of the inverse.

Example 3.1
.

Consider the 2-player GNEP with

\displaystyle\text{F}_{1}(x_{-1}):\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\min\limits_{x_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}}&\|x_{1}\|^{2}\\
\mathit{s.t.}&\left[\begin{array}[]{rr}-1&-1\\
1&0\\
0&1\\
6&1\end{array}\right]x_{1}\geq\left[\begin{array}[]{c}-2\\
0\\
0\\
1+\mathbf{1}^{T}x_{2}\end{array}\right],\end{array}\right.

\displaystyle\text{F}_{2}(x_{-2}):\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\min\limits_{x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}}&\|x_{2}\|^{2}\\
\mathit{s.t.}&\left[\begin{array}[]{rr}-1&1\\
1&0\\
0&1\\
-1&0\end{array}\right]x_{2}\geq\left[\begin{array}[]{c}-2x_{1,1}+2\\
0\\
0\\
x_{1,1}-x_{1,2}-2\end{array}\right].\end{array}\right.

One can see that
m_{1}=m_{2}=4
,
r_{1}=r_{2}=2
and

\begin{array}[]{lll}\mathcal{P}_{1}=\big\{\,\{1,2\},\,\{1,3\},\,\{1,4\},\,\{2,3\},&\vline&\mathcal{P}_{2}=\big\{\,\{1,2\},\,\{1,3\},\,\{1,4\},\,\{2,3\},\\
\qquad\quad\{2,4\},\,\{3,4\}\,\big\},&\vline&\qquad\quad\{3,4\}\,\big\}.\end{array}

The pLMEs are given as in (
3.9
). For instance,

\lambda_{1,\{1,4\}}=\left[\begin{array}[]{rr}-1&-1\\
6&1\end{array}\right]^{-T}\left[\begin{array}[]{c}2x_{1,1}\\
2x_{1,2}\end{array}\right]=\frac{2}{5}\left[\begin{array}[]{c}x_{1,1}-6x_{1,2}\\
x_{1,1}-x_{1,2}\end{array}\right],

\lambda_{2,\{1,2\}}=\left[\begin{array}[]{rr}-1&1\\
1&0\end{array}\right]^{-T}\left[\begin{array}[]{c}2x_{2,1}\\
2x_{2,2}\end{array}\right]=2\left[\begin{array}[]{c}x_{2,2}\\
x_{2,1}+x_{2,2}\end{array}\right].

In contrast, if we do not use pLMEs,
the full Lagrange multiplier expressions as in

[
33
,
36
]

are much more complicated for this GNEP.

Remark 3.2
.

For the GNEP (
1.1
) with quasi-linear constraints, the pLMEs always exist for each
i\in[N]
.
Furthermore, at every KKT point
x
, there always exist an associated Lagrange multiplier vector
\lambda_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{i}}
and a label set
J_{i}\in\mathcal{P}_{i}
such that
\lambda_{i,J_{i}}(x)=\lambda_{i,J_{i}}
and
\lambda_{i,j}=0\ (j\notin J_{i})
for all
i\in[N]
.
In other words, the pLMEs give exact values for Lagrange multipliers at every KKT point of the GNEP.

For
x=(x_{i},x_{-i})\in X
and
J_{i}\in\mathcal{P}_{i}
,
if
\lambda_{i,J_{i}}(x)\geq 0
, then
x
is a KKT point of
\mbox{F}_{i}(x_{-i})
,
since (
3.7
) is satisfied for
\lambda_{i,J_{i}}(x)
.
Thus,
x_{i}
is a KKT point of
\mbox{F}_{i}(x_{-i})
if

(3.28)

\boxed{\begin{array}[]{c}\exists J_{i}\in\mathcal{P}_{i},\,\nabla_{x_{i}}f_{i}(x)=A_{i,J_{i}}^{T}\lambda_{i,J_{i}}(x),\\
0\leq\lambda_{i,J_{i}}(x)\perp\big(A_{i,J_{i}}x_{i}-b_{i,J_{i}}(x_{-i})\big)\geq 0.\end{array}}

Interestingly, the above is also necessary for

x_{i}
to be a KKT point of
\mbox{F}_{i}(x_{-i})
,
as shown in the following theorem.

Theorem 3.3
.

For
x=(x_{i},x_{-i})\in X
, the
x_{i}
is a KKT point of
\mbox{F}_{i}(x_{-i})

if and only if it satisfies (
3.28
).

Proof.

Suppose (
3.28
) is satisfied.
Let
\lambda_{i}
be the extension of
\lambda_{i,J_{i}}(x)

by adding zero entries. Then
(x,\lambda_{i})
satisfies the KKT system (
3.2
),
so
x_{i}
is a KKT point of
\mbox{F}_{i}(x_{-i})
.
Conversely, suppose
x_{i}
is a KKT point of
\mbox{F}_{i}(x_{-i})
.
Then there exists
\lambda_{i}
satisfying (
3.2
).
So, the solution set for the linear system (
3.5
) is nonempty.
By Carathéodory’s Theorem,
\nabla_{x_{i}}f_{i}(x)
can be represented as a conic combination of linearly independent vectors from
A_{i}^{T}
. Thus, a
basic feasible solution must exist for (
3.5
).
This means that (
3.28
) holds.
∎

4.
Solving GNEPs with quasi-linear constraints

We discuss how to solve the GNEP with quasi-linear constraints as in (
3.1
).
Since every GNE
x
is a KKT point, there exists

J\in\mathcal{P}
such that
x\in\mathcal{K}_{J}
.
Since
\mathcal{P}
is a finite set, there are only finitely many choices of
J
.
Moreover, under some genericity assumptions, the KKT set
\mathcal{K}
is finite.
For these cases, the subset
\mathcal{K}_{J}
is also finite for every
J\in\mathcal{P}
.
This inspires how to find all GNEs.

4.1.
Finding all GNEs in
\mathcal{K}_{J}
KJ

We introduce how to find GNEs in
\mathcal{K}_{J}
for a fixed
J\in\mathcal{P}
.
For the given
J
, pLMEs are given by (
3.8
), so the set
\mathcal{K}_{J}
can be represented by equalities and inequalities of polynomials in the variable
x
, as shown in (
3.30
).
Let
\Theta\in\mathbb{R}^{(n+1)\times(n+1)}
be a symmetric positive definite matrix.
Consider the following polynomial optimization problem

(4.1)

\,\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\min&\theta(x)\coloneqq[x]_{1}^{T}\Theta[x]_{1}\\
\mathit{s.t.}&x\in\mathcal{K}_{J}.\end{array}\right.

If
\mathcal{K}_{J}\neq\emptyset
, then (
4.1
)
has a unique minimizer
u
when
\Theta
is generic
(see
[
33
, Theorem 5.4]
), and
u
is a KKT point.
Otherwise, (
4.1
) is infeasible, and there is no GNE in
\mathcal{K}_{J}
.
We will show how to solve (
4.1
) in Section
5
.
The following result is obvious.

Theorem 4.1
.

For the GNEP as in (
3.1
),
if the optimization problem (
4.1
) is infeasible, then there is no KKT point in
\mathcal{K}_{J}
.
Otherwise, each minimizer
u
of (
4.1
) is a KKT point.
Moreover, if the GNEP is convex,
u
is a GNE.

For convex GNEPs, once we find a minimizer
u
for (
4.1
),
then
u
must be a GNE.
However, when the GNEP is nonconvex,
u
may or may not be a GNE.
For nonconvex GNEPs, we can check if
u
is a GNE or not by solving polynomial optimization problems.
By definition,
u
is a GNE if and only if
\epsilon_{i}\geq 0
for every
i\in[N]
,
where
\epsilon_{i}
is the optimal value

(4.2)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccl}\epsilon_{i}\coloneqq&\min&f_{i}(x_{i},u_{-i})-f_{i}(u_{i},u_{-i})\\
&\mathit{s.t.}&x_{i}\in X_{i}(u_{-i}).\end{array}\right.

Therefore, once we get a KKT point
u
, we solve (
4.2
)
for every
i\in[N]
.
If
\epsilon_{i}\geq 0
for all
i\in[N]
, then we certify that
u
is a GNE;
otherwise, it is not.

If
u
is not a GNE, one needs to find other KKT points to solve this GNEP.
Also, when
\mathcal{K}_{J}\neq\emptyset
but
\mathcal{K}_{J}\cap\mathcal{S}=\emptyset
,
we may need to find all points in
\mathcal{K}_{J}
to certify nonexistence of GNEs in
\mathcal{K}_{J}
.
Moreover, people are usually interested in finding all GNEs.
In the following, we discuss how to find all GNEs or detect their nonexistence
in
\mathcal{K}_{J}
.

Suppose
u=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{N})\in\mathcal{K}_{J}

is the minimizer of (
4.1
).
Then we have

\theta(u)\,\leq\,\theta(x)\quad\text{for all}\,\,x\in\mathcal{K}_{J}.

When the matrix
\Theta
is generic, the above inequality holds strictly for all
x\in\mathcal{K}_{J}\setminus\{u\}
.
Suppose that
u
is an isolated point of
\mathcal{K}_{J}
.
This is the case when the GNEP is generic, as shown in
[
32
, Theorem 3.1]
.
Then, there exists
\delta>0
such that

(4.3)

\theta(u)+\delta\leq\theta(x),\quad\text{for all}\,\,u\neq x\in\mathcal{K}_{J}.

For such a
\delta>0
, consider the optimization problem

(4.4)

\,\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\min&\theta(x)\\
\mathit{s.t.}&x\in\mathcal{K}_{J},\quad\theta(x)\geq\theta(u)+\delta.\end{array}\right.

If (
4.4
) is infeasible,
then the set
\mathcal{K}_{J}
does not have any KKT points other than
u
.
Otherwise, it must have a minimizer
\hat{u}
(since
\Theta
is positive definite),
which is a KKT point different from
u
.
For the new KKT point
\hat{u}
, we may solve polynomial optimization problems like (
4.2
) to check if it is a GNE or not.

Indeed, more GNEs can be computed by repeating these steps.
Suppose we have obtained the KKT points

u^{(1)},u^{(2)},\ldots,u^{(j)}\in\mathcal{K}_{J}
for some
j\geq 1
, in the order that

(4.5)

\theta(u^{(1)})<\theta(u^{(2)})<\cdots<\theta(u^{(j)}).

Suppose
u^{(j+1)}
is a new KKT point such that

\theta(u^{(j+1)})\,=\,\min_{x\in\mathcal{C}_{j}}\theta(x),\quad\text{where}\quad\mathcal{C}_{j}\coloneq\mathcal{K}_{J}\setminus\{u^{(1)},u^{(2)},\ldots,u^{(j)}\}.

If there exists a scalar
\delta
satisfying

(4.6)

0<\delta<\theta(u^{(j+1)})-\theta(u^{(j)}),

then
u^{(j+1)}
can be obtained by computing the minimizer of

(4.7)

\,\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\min&\theta(x)\\
\mathit{s.t.}&\theta(x)\geq\theta(u^{(j)})+\delta,\\
&x\in\mathcal{K}_{J}.\end{array}\right.

The inequality (
4.6
) can be checked as follows.
We can first assign a priori value for
\delta
(say,
0.5
),
then solve the maximization problem

(4.8)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl}\theta_{\max}\coloneqq&\max&\theta(x)\\
&\mathit{s.t.}&x\in\mathcal{K}_{J},\quad\theta(x)\leq\theta(u^{(j)})+\delta.\end{array}\right.

Since
u^{(j)}
is a feasible point,
it always holds
\theta_{\max}\geq\theta(u^{(j)})
.
There are two possibilities:

•

If
\theta_{\max}=\theta(u^{(j)})
, then
u^{(j)}

is a maximizer of (
4.8
).
This implies that
u^{(j+1)}
is infeasible for (
4.8
),
so (
4.6
) is satisfied.

•

If
\theta_{\max}>\theta(u^{(j)})
, then there exists
v\in\mathcal{K}_{J}
such that

\theta(u^{(j)})<\theta(v)\leq\theta(u^{(j)})+\delta.

This means that
\delta
is too large and violates (
4.6
).
We need to decrease the value of
\delta
(e.g., by replacing
\delta
with
\delta/2
)
and solve (
4.8
) again.

In light of the above, we get the following algorithm for finding all GNEs in
\mathcal{K}_{J}
.

Algorithm 4.2
.

For the GNEP as in (
3.1
) and for a given
J\in\mathcal{P}
,
select a generic symmetric positive definite matrix
\Theta

and a small positive value (say,
0.5
) for
\delta
.
Let
\mathcal{S}_{J}\coloneqq\emptyset
and
j\coloneqq 1
.
Then, do the following:

Step 1

Solve the optimization problem (
4.1
).
If it is infeasible, output the nonexistence of GNEs in
\mathcal{K}_{J}
and stop.
Otherwise, solve (
4.1
)
for a minimizer
u^{(1)}
and go to Step 2.

Step 2

For each

\epsilon_{i}
of

) for

If

for every

Step 3

Compute the maximum value

of (
4.8
).

Step 4

If

, then go to Step 5;

Solve the optimization problem (

).
If it is infeasible, output that

Otherwise, update

If the GNEP is convex, every KKT point is a GNE,

The properties of Algorithm

are summarized as follows.

Proposition 4.3
.

For the GNEP as in (
3.1
),
the following properties hold for Algorithm
4.2
:

(i)

If
\theta_{\max}=\theta(u^{(j)})
and (

) is infeasible, then

S_{J}
is the set of all GNEs in
\mathcal{K}_{J}
.

(ii)

If
\theta_{\max}=\theta(u^{(j)})
and

is the minimizer of (

),
then
\delta
satisfies (
4.6
).

(iii)

Assume

are isolated points of
\mathcal{K}_{J}
.
Suppose
\Theta
is a generic symmetric positive definite matrix,
then there exists
\delta>0
such that
\theta_{\max}=\theta(u^{(j)})
,
i.e.,
u^{(j)}
is the maximizer of (
4.8
).

Proof.

(i) When
\theta_{\max}=\theta(u^{(j)})
, the KKT point
u^{(j)}
is the maximizer of (
4.8
).
If there is
v\in\mathcal{K}_{J}

other than

, then

\theta(v)>\theta(u^{(j)})+\delta.

On the other hand, when (
4.7
) is infeasible,
every

must satisfy

\theta(x)<\theta(u^{(j)})+\delta.

Therefore, if
\theta_{\max}=\theta(u^{(j)})
and
(
4.7
) is infeasible, then there are no KKT points in
\mathcal{K}_{J}
except

u^{(1)},\ldots,u^{(j)}
.
This implies that all GNEs in
\mathcal{K}_{J}
are contained in
\mathcal{S}_{J}
.

(ii) If
\theta_{\max}=\theta(u^{(j)})
and

u^{(j+1)}
exists, then as in (i), we can get

\theta(u^{(j+1)})>\theta(u^{(j)})+\delta,

which means that (
4.6
) holds.

(iii) For
\epsilon>0
, let
\mathbb{S}_{\epsilon}
denote the set of
all
(n+1)
-by-
(n+1)
symmetric positive definite matrices
whose largest eigenvalue equals one and

.
The set of all
(n+1)
-by-
(n+1)
symmetric positive definite matrices
of unit

is the union
\bigcup_{l=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{S}_{1/l}.

For each

, we show that the conclusion holds for all

\Theta\in\mathbb{S}_{1/l}

except a set of Lebesgue measure zero.

Let
\Theta\in\mathbb{S}_{1/l}
be an arbitrary matrix.
By the selection of
u^{(1)},\ldots,u^{(j)}
, it holds

\nu_{1}\coloneqq\theta(u^{(1)})<\nu_{2}\coloneqq\theta(u^{(2)})<\cdots<\nu_{j}\coloneqq\theta(u^{(j)}).

We consider the case

for convenience
because the proof is almost the same for
j=1
.
When
\mathcal{K}_{J}
has no other points except
u^{(1)},\ldots,u^{(j)}
,
we have
\theta_{\max}=\theta(u^{(j)})
for all
\delta>0
.
So, we consider the opposite case and
suppose that
\bar{u}
is a point in
\mathcal{K}_{J}

that is different from
u^{(1)},\ldots,u^{(j)}
,
and that
\nu_{j}\leq\theta(\bar{u})
.
If
x
is a minimizer of (
4.7
) in previous loops, then

e_{1}^{T}\Theta e_{1}+\|\bar{u}\|^{2}\geq[\bar{u}]_{1}^{T}\Theta[\bar{u}]_{1}\geq[x]_{1}^{T}\Theta[x]_{1}\geq e_{1}^{T}\Theta e_{1}+\|x\|^{2}/l,

with

.
Thus, all minimizers of (
4.7
) in previous loops are contained in the ball

B\coloneqq\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\|x\|^{2}\leq l\cdot\|\bar{u}\|^{2}\right\},

which implies

for each

.
Recall the notation
[x]_{d}
as in (

).
Since each

is an isolated point of
\mathcal{K}_{J}
, the set

T_{1}\coloneqq\big\{[x]_{2}:\,x\in\mathcal{K}_{J}\cap B,x\neq u^{(k)},1\leq k\leq j-1\big\}

is compact.
Since

,
we have

\langle\theta,[u^{(1)}]_{2}\rangle<\cdots<\langle\theta,[u^{(j-1)}]_{2}\rangle<\min_{y\in T_{1}}\langle\theta,y\rangle.

The right most minimization in the above is equivalent to

(4.9)

\,\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\min&\langle\theta,y\rangle\\
\mathit{s.t.}&y\in\mbox{conv}(T_{1}).\end{array}\right.

The convex hull
\mbox{conv}(T_{1})
is a compact convex set.
Observe that if (

) has more than one minimizer,
then
\theta
is a singular normal vector of the convex body
\mbox{conv}(T_{1})
.
The set of singular normal vectors of a convex body has Lebesgue measure zero.
This is shown in
[

, Theorem 2.2.11]
.
So, when
\Theta
is generic in

,
the linear optimization (

) has the unique minimizer
[u^{(j)}]_{2}
.
Let

T_{2}\coloneqq T_{1}\setminus\{[u^{(j)}]_{2}\}.

Since
u^{(j)}
is an isolated point of
\mathcal{K}_{J}
,
the set

is also compact, so

\langle\theta,[u^{(j)}]_{2}\rangle<\min_{y\in T_{2}}\langle\theta,y\rangle.

Then there must exist
\delta>0
such that

\langle\theta,[u^{(j)}]_{2}\rangle+\delta<\min_{y\in T_{2}}\langle\theta,y\rangle.

For the above

, we must have
\theta_{\max}=\theta(u^{(j)})
.
This means that the conclusion holds for all

\Theta\in\mathbb{S}_{1/l}

except for a set of Lebesgue measure zero,
for each

.
This completes the proof.
∎

.

.

),

Remark 4.7
.

(i) Theorem
4.6
implies the finite convergence of Algorithm
4.5
.
That is, when the set
\mathcal{K}
is finite and
\Theta
is generically positive definite,
our method can get all GNEs by solving finitely many polynomial optimization subproblems.
Furthermore, as shown in Section
5
, the Moment-SOS hierarchy is guaranteed to find global minimizers for these subproblems, under some genericity assumptions.
Therefore, for generic GNEPs, our method can find all GNEs if there exist finitely many ones,
or detect their nonexistence, by solving finitely many semidefinite programs.

(ii) Computing all GNEs is a challenging task.
To the best of the authors’ knowledge, there are few
efficient methods for doing this.
For GNEPs given by polynomial or rational functions, the semidefinite relaxation method in
[
34
,
36
]
guarantees to find all GNEs, under the assumption that
|\mathcal{K}|<\infty
and
feasible extensions
exist;
the polyhedral homotopy method in
[
25
]
finds all GNEs if the number of roots for the complex KKT system equals the mixed volume (with the origin included in the support).
In contrast, Algorithm
4.5
does not require such conditions to find all GNEs.

(iii) The assumption that
\mathcal{K}
is a finite set holds when every
f_{i}
and
b_{i}
are given by generic polynomials and each
A_{i}
is a generic matrix.
This is shown in
[
32
]
.
However, it is possible that
|\mathcal{K}|
is infinite when the GNEP is not generic; see Example
6.9
.
For these cases, if the GNEP is convex, then our method still guarantees finding at least one GNE, but it may fail to find all of them;
otherwise, our method may or may not find GNEs and check the completeness of the solution set.
Besides that, the assumption that
\Theta
is a generic symmetric positive definite matrix is independent of the problem.
Indeed, Theorem
4.6
implies that if we set
\Theta\coloneqq RR^{T}

with a randomly generated
R\in\mathbb{R}^{(n+1)\times(n+1)}
whose entries obey the normal distribution, then Algorithm
4.5
can almost always find all GNEs as long as
\mathcal{K}
is finite.

(iv) To find all GNEs or detect their nonexistence,
Algorithm
4.5
requires enumerating all

J\in\mathcal{P}
, where
|\mathcal{P}|=|\mathcal{P}_{1}|\times\cdots\times|\mathcal{P}_{N}|
.
For each
i
, the cardinality of
|\mathcal{P}_{i}|
is at most
\binom{m_{i}}{r_{i}}
, which
may increase at a polynomial (or even linear) rate for many interesting cases.
For instance, if
m_{i}=r_{i}+1
, then
\binom{m_{i}}{r_{i}}=m_{i}
and the growth rate is linear.
Moreover, even if
|\mathcal{P}_{i}|
increases exponentially as the problem size increases,
the cardinality of
\mathcal{P}_{i}
is usually less than
\binom{m_{i}}{r_{i}}
(e.g., consider the boxed constraint
L_{i,j}\leq x_{i,j}\leq U_{i,j}
with scalars
L_{i,j}<U_{i,j}
for each
i,j
).
Last, if we aim to find a single GNE, then we typically do not need to enumerate all
J\in\mathcal{P}
, as shown in Section
6
.

To end this section, we discuss a practical strategy based on constraint screening to reduce the number of sets
J
for enumeration in computational practice.
If one has a priori information that some constraints cannot be active simultaneously at GNEs,
then all
J\in\mathcal{P}
such that the labels of these constraints are contained in
J
can be precluded from the enumeration.
For instance, suppose that
n_{1}=n_{2}
and consider the two player GNEP with joint box constraints:

(4.10)

X_{i}(x_{-i})=\left\{x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{i}}\left|\begin{array}[]{cl}0\leq x_{1,j}+x_{2,j}\leq 1,&\forall j\in[n_{i}],\\
\hat{A}_{i}x_{i}\geq\hat{b}_{i}(x_{-i})&\end{array}\right.\right\}.

For every
j=1,\ldots,n_{1}
,
let
\lambda_{i,2j-1}
and
\lambda_{i,2j}
be the Lagrange multipliers associated to
x_{1,j}+x_{2,j}\geq 0
and
1-x_{1,j}-x_{2,j}\geq 0
, respectively.
Since these two constraints cannot be simultaneously active, at least one of

\lambda_{1,2j-1}
and
\lambda_{2,2j}
must be zero.
Similarly, at most one of
\lambda_{2,2j-1}
and
\lambda_{1,2j}
can be nonzero.
Thus for this GNEP, if we let

\mathcal{Q}\coloneq\left\{J\in\mathcal{P}\left|\begin{array}[]{c}\mbox{$\exists$ $i\in[2]$ and $j\in[n_{1}]$ such that}\\
2j-1\in J_{i},\ 2j\in J_{3-i}\end{array}\right.\right\},

then one may skip all
J\in\mathcal{Q}
for finding GNEs.
To illustrate the impact, if we momentarily disregard the constraints
\hat{A}_{i}x_{i}\geq\hat{b}_{i}(x_{-i})
, then the cardinality
|\mathcal{P}|=4^{n_{1}}
, whereas the number of valid sets
J
to enumerate becomes
|\mathcal{P}\setminus\mathcal{Q}|=2^{n_{1}}
.

5.
Solving Polynomial Optimization

We now show how to solve polynomial optimization problems
that appear in Algorithms
4.2
and
4.5
.
They can be generally expressed in the form:

(5.1)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl}f_{min}\coloneqq&\min\limits_{z}&f(z)\\
&\mathit{s.t.}&p(z)=0\,\,(\forall p\in\Phi),\\
&&q(z)\geq 0\,\,(\forall q\in\Psi),\end{array}\right.

where the variable
z
represents either
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
or
x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{i}}
for the
i
th player, and
\Phi
,
\Psi
are finite sets of equality and inequality constraining polynomials, respectively.
The Moment-SOS relaxations are efficient
for solving (
5.1
) globally.
We refer to the books
[
19
,
23
,
24
,
28
]
for a more detailed introduction.

Denote the degrees

\begin{array}[]{rcl}d_{0}&\coloneqq&\max\big\{\left\lceil\deg(p)/2\right\rceil:\,p\in\Phi\cup\Psi\big\},\\
d_{1}&\coloneqq&\max\big\{\left\lceil\deg(f)/2\right\rceil,d_{0}\big\}.\end{array}

Let
\ell
be the dimension of
z
. For a degree
k\geq d_{1}
,
the
k
th order moment relaxation for solving (
5.1
) is

(5.2)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl}f_{mom,k}\coloneqq&\min&\langle f,y\rangle\\
&\mathit{s.t.}&y\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}_{2k}^{\ell}},\,y_{0}=1,L_{p}^{(k)}[y]=0\,(p\in\Phi),\\
&&M_{k}[y]\succeq 0,\,L_{q}^{(k)}[y]\succeq 0\,(q\in\Psi).\end{array}\right.

The dual optimization of (
5.2
) is the
k
th order SOS relaxation

(5.3)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl}f_{sos,k}\coloneqq&\max&\gamma\\
&\mathit{s.t.}&f-\gamma\in\textrm{Ideal}[\Phi]_{2k}+\textrm{QM}[\Psi]_{2k}.\end{array}\right.

We refer to Section
2
for the notation
\langle f,y\rangle
,
L_{p}^{(k)}[y]
,
M_{k}[y]
,
\textrm{Ideal}[\Phi]_{2k}
,
\textrm{QM}[\Psi]_{2k}
in the above.
It is worth remarking that (
5.2
)-(
5.3
) is a primal-dual pair of semidefinite programs.
For
k=d_{1},d_{1}+1,\ldots
, the primal-dual pair (
5.2
)-(
5.3
) is called the Moment-SOS hierarchy.
Its convergence property can be summarized as follows.
When
\textrm{Ideal}[\Phi]+\textrm{QM}[\Psi]
is archimedean, we have
f_{mom,k}\to f_{min}
as
k\to\infty
.
Moreover, if the linear independence constraint qualification, strict complementarity condition, and second order sufficient optimality conditions hold at every minimizer, then
f_{sos,k}=f_{min}
for all
k
that is big enough (see
[
28
,
30
]
).

In the following, we show how to extract minimizers for
(
5.1
) from the moment relaxation.
Suppose
y^{(k)}
is a minimizer of (
5.2
).
If
y^{(k)}
satisfies the flat truncation:
there exists a degree
t\in[d_{1},k]
such that

(5.4)

\mbox{rank}\,M_{t}[y^{(k)}]\,=\,\mbox{rank}\,M_{t-d_{0}}[y^{(k)}],

then
f_{min}=f_{mom,k}

and we can extract
r\coloneqq\mbox{rank}\,M_{t}[y^{(k)}]
minimizers
for (
5.1
) (see
[
20
,
27
,
28
]
).
Indeed, flat truncation is a sufficient and almost necessary condition
for extracting minimizers. This is shown in
[
27
]
.

The Moment-SOS algorithm for solving (
5.1
) is as follows.

Algorithm 5.1
.

For the polynomial optimization (
5.1
), initialize

.

Step 1

).
If it is infeasible, then (

) is infeasible and stop.
Otherwise, solve it for a minimizer

.

Step 2

satisfies the rank condition (

).
If (

) holds,
then extract

minimizers of (
5.1
) and stop.
Otherwise, let
k\coloneqq k+1
and go to Step 1.

in the software

[

]

[

For Algorithms

Recall that

The following is the convergence property of Algorithm

Theorem 5.2

Suppose the optimization problem (

6.1.
GNEPs from real-world applications

GNEPs arise in a variety of real-world scenarios.
In this subsection, we present an example that illustrates how GNEPs can be used to model such practical applications.

Example 6.8
.

Consider the environmental pollution control problem for two countries under the mechanism of
joint implementation

[
5
]

For each
i,j\in[2]

x_{i,0}
is the (gross) emissions for the industrial outputs of country

For the country

and the environmental cost incurred by pollution is quadratic in

&x_{1,0}-\gamma_{i}(x_{1,1}+x_{2,1})\geq 0,\\
&x_{2,0}-\gamma_{i}(x_{1,2}+x_{2,2})\geq 0,\end{array}\right.

where parameters are set as

\begin{array}[]{llllllll}b_{1}=2,&b_{2}=3,&c_{1,1}=0.2,&c_{1,2}=0.3,&c_{2,1}=0.4,&c_{2,2}=0.2,\\

6.2.
GNEPs with shared constraints

We consider GNEPs with shared constraints,
i.e., all players have the same constraints.
Solving this class of GNEPs is particularly hard.
This is because when there are shared constraints, the complex variety given by the KKT equations has a positive dimension (see
[
32
]
).
Consequently, the critical set
\mathcal{K}
is often an infinite set.
For this class of GNEPs, our method may not be able to find all GNEs.
However, it is still applicable for such GNEPs that have quasi-linear constraints,
and it may still get some GNEs.
For instance, when the GNEP is convex, our method guarantees to find at least one GNE,
if there exists one; see Theorem
4.1
.
The following is an example that has shared constraints.

Example 6.9
.

Consider the 2-player GNEP with shared constraints:

\begin{array}[]{clccl}\underset{x_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{4}}{\text{min}}&f_{1}(x_{1},x_{2})&\vline&\underset{x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{4}}{\text{min}}&f_{2}(x_{1},x_{2})\\
\mathit{s.t.}&0\leq x_{1,j}\leq 1,\ (j\in[4]),&\vline&\mathit{s.t.}&-1\leq x_{2,j}\leq 1,\ (j\in[4]),\\

7.
Conclusion and Discussions

This paper studies GNEPs with quasi-linear constraints that are defined by polynomials.
We propose a new partial Lagrange multiplier expression approach with KKT conditions.
By using partial Lagrange multiplier expressions,
we represent KKT sets of such GNEPs by a union of simpler sets with convenient expressions.
This helps to relax GNEPs into finite groups of branch polynomial optimization problems.
The latter can be solved efficiently by Moment-SOS relaxations.
Under some genericity assumptions, we develop algorithms that either find all GNEs
or detect their nonexistence.
Numerical experiments are given to show the efficiency of our method.
There is great potential for our method.
It can be interesting future work to apply our method for solving GNEPs arising from machine learning and data science applications.

We remark that GNEPs with quasi-linear constraints are typically more difficult than
GNEPs with linear constraints.
To see this, we compare their algebraic degrees,
which count numbers of complex solutions to KKT systems
[
32
]
.
For the convenience of our discussion, we suppose that for each
i\in[N]
,

f_{i}(x)
is a quadratic polynomial in
x
,
A_{i}
is a
m_{i}
-by-
n_{i}
matrix,
and every
b_{i,j}\,(j\in[m_{i}])
is a polynomial in
x_{-i}
whose degree equals
d_{i,j}
.
Without loss of generality, we also assume that
m_{i}\leq n_{i}
for each
i\in[N]
,
and all constraints are active at every KKT point (otherwise,
one may compute the algebraic degree by enumerating all active sets,
see
[
32
, Theorem 5.2]
).
When all
f_{i}(x)
,
A_{i}
and
b_{i}(x_{-i})
are generic, the algebraic degree is

\prod_{i=1}^{N}\prod_{j=1}^{m_{i}}\max(1,d_{i,j}).

In particular, when the GNEP has only linear constraints
(i.e.,
d_{i,j}\leq 1
for all
i,j
),
the algebraic degree is equal to
1
, which is much less than that
for general cases of quasi-linear constraints
(i.e.,
d_{i,j}
are greater than
1
).
For instance, when
N=2
,
m_{1}=m_{2}=4
, and
d_{i,j}=2
for all
i,j
,
the algebraic degree for the GNEP with quasi-linear constraints is
2^{8}=256
,
under some genericity assumptions.
We refer to
[
32
]
for more details about algebraic degrees.

Moreover, we note that our method aims at finding GNEs and uses the KKT conditions to compute candidate solutions.
When the GNEP is convex, GNEs are equivalent to the KKT points; thus, our method finds GNEs directly.
However, for nonconvex GNEPs that are of the primary interest of this paper, we need to select GNEs from the KKT points by solving polynomial optimization problems.
Note that for nonconvex GNEPs, all players’ nonconvex optimization subproblems are globally minimized at a GNE.
There exist approaches characterizing GNEs directly by some equivalent reformulations for GNEPs, such as the Nikaido-Isoda function reformulation (see
[
13
]
), and the reformulation that uses Putinar’s Positivstellensatz in
[
7
]
.
However, the reformulations are usually extremely difficult to solve in computational practice when the GNEP is nonconvex.
Also, the Gauss-Seidel method can be viewed as an approach that computes GNEs directly.
Nonetheless, its convergence is not guaranteed in general.
It is interesting future work to find computationally efficient approximations for the set of GNEs that are better than the set of KKT points.

Last, we acknowledge that our proposed method may not be efficient for solving large scale GNEPs. This is because it requires solving semidefinite programming relaxations,
whose sizes grow fast when the number of variables increases.
Moreover, though the instances of
|\mathcal{P}|
are much smaller than the number of all possible active sets (as shown in Table
1
), typically one still needs to enumerate a large number of sets
J
when the number of constraints
m_{i}
is relatively large (say, more than
20
).
We refer to Example
6.6
for the numerical performance of our method with increasing values of
n_{i}
and
m_{i}
. Generally, solving large scale GNEPs is computationally challenging.
This is important future work.
