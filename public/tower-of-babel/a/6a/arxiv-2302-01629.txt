Beyond the Universal Law of Robustness: Sharper Laws for Random Features and Neural Tangent Kernels
Simone Bombari, Shayan Kiyani, Marco Mondelli

Abstract
Machine learning models are vulnerable to adversarial perturbations, and a thought-provoking paper by Bubeck and Sellke has analyzed this phenomenon through the lens of over-parameterization: interpolating smoothly the data requires significantly more parameters than simply memorizing it. However, this “universal” law provides only a necessary condition for robustness, and it is unable to discriminate between models. In this paper, we address these gaps by focusing on empirical risk minimization in two prototypical settings, namely, random features and the neural tangent kernel (NTK). We prove that, for random features, the model is not robust for any degree of over-parameterization, even when the necessary condition coming from the universal law of robustness is satisfied. In contrast, for even activations, the NTK model meets the universal lower bound, and it is robust as soon as the necessary condition on over-parameterization is fulfilled. This also addresses a conjecture in prior work by Bubeck, Li and Nagaraj. Our analysis decouples the effect of the kernel of the model from an “interaction matrix”, which describes the interaction with the test data and captures the effect of the activation. Our theoretical results are corroborated by numerical evidence on both synthetic and standard datasets (MNIST, CIFAR-10).

Introduction

Despite the deployment of deep neural networks in a wide set of applications, these models are known to be vulnerable to adversarial perturbations

Specifically, the recent paper by

When does over-parameterization become a sufficient condition to achieve adversarial robustness?

In this paper, we consider a

Main contribution.

Our key results are summarized below.

•

For random features, we show that ERM leads to a model which is
not robust

any
degree of over-parameterization. Specifically, we tackle a regime in which the universal law of robustness by
[
10

trivializes, and we provide a more refined bound.

•

For NTK with an even activation function, we give an upper bound on the ERM solution that

[

At the technical level, our analysis involves the study of the spectra of RF and NTK random matrices, and it provides the following insights.

•

We introduce in (
4.11

•

Our analytical characterization captures the role of the activation function: it turns out that a specific symmetry (e.g., being even) boosts the adversarial robustness of the models.

Related work

From the vast literature studying adversarial examples, we succintly review related works focusing on linear models, random features and NTK models.

Linear regression.

In light of the universal law of robustness, in the interpolation regime, linear regression cannot achieve adversarial robustness (as
p=d
). For linear models,
[
16
,
26
]
have focused on adversarial training (as opposed to ERM, which is considered in this work), showing that over-parameterization hurts the robust generalization error. We also point out that, in some settings, even the standard generalization error is maximized when the model is under-parametrized
[
22
]
. Precise asymptotics on the robust error in the classification setting are provided in
[
25
,
43
]
. A different approach is pursued by
[
46
]
, who study a linear model that exhibits a trade-off between generalization and robustness.

Random features (RF).

The RF model introduced by
[
38
]
can be regarded as a two-layer neural network with random first layer weights, and it solves the lack of freedom in the number of trainable parameters, which is constrained to be equal to the input dimension for linear regression. The popularity of random features derives from their analytical tractability, combined with the fact that the model still reproduces behaviors typical of deep learning, such as the double-descent curve
[
31
]
. Our analysis crucially relies on the spectral properties of the kernel induced by this model. Such properties have been studied by
[
28
]
, and tight bounds on the smallest eigenvalue of the feature kernel have been provided by
[
35
]
for ReLU activations. Theoretical results on the adversarial robustness of random features have been shown for both adversarial training
[
21
]
and the ERM solution
[
14
,
15
]
. We will discuss the comparison with
[
14
,
15
]
in the next paragraph.

Neural tangent kernel (NTK).

The NTK can be regarded as the kernel obtained by linearizing the neural network around the initialization
[
24
]
. A popular line of work has analyzed its spectrum
[
18
,
3
,
49
]
and bounded its smallest eigenvalue
[
41
,
35
,
33
,
8
]
. The behavior of the NTK is closely related to memorization
[
33
]
, optimization
[
4
,
17
]
and generalization
[
5
]
properties of deep neural networks.
The NTK has also been recently exploited to identify non-robust features learned with standard training
[
45
]
and gain insight on adversarial training
[
29
]
.
More closely related to our work, the robustness of the NTK model is studied in
[
53
,
15
,
14
]
. Specifically,
[
53
]
analyze the interplay between width, depth and initialization of the network, thus improving upon results by
[
23
,
51
]
. However, the perturbation stability considered by
[
53
]
captures an average robustness, rather than an adversarial one (see the detailed comparison in Section
3
), which makes these results not immediately comparable to ours. In contrast, our notion of sensitivity is close to that analyzed in
[
15
,
14
]
, which establish trade-offs between robustness and memorization for both the RF and the NTK model. However,
[
15
]
study a teacher-student model with quadratic teacher and infinite data, which also does not allow for a direct comparison. Finally,
[
14
]
studies the ERM solution and focuses on
(i)
infinite-width networks, and
(ii)
finite-width networks where the number of neurons scales linearly with the input dimension. We remark that this last setup does not lead to lower bounds on the sensitivity tighter than those by
[
10
]
, and our results on the NTK model provide the first upper bounds on the sensitivity (which also match the lower bounds by
[
10
]
).

Preliminaries

Notation.

Given a vector
v
, we indicate with
\left\|v\right\|_{2}
its Euclidean norm. Given
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{v}}
and
u\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}}
, we denote by
v\otimes u\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{v}d_{u}}
their Kronecker product. Given a matrix
A
, let
\left\|A\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}
be its operator norm,
\left\|A\right\|_{F}
its Frobenius norm and
\lambda_{\rm min}\left(A\right)
and
\lambda_{\rm max}\left(A\right)
its smallest and largest eigenvalues respectively. We denote by
\left\|\varphi\right\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}
the Lipschitz constant of the function
\varphi
. All the complexity notations
\Omega(\cdot)
,
\mathcal{O}(\cdot)
,
o(\cdot)
and
\Theta(\cdot)
are understood for sufficiently large data size
N
, input dimension
d
, number of neurons
k
, and number of parameters
p
. We indicate with
C,c>0
numerical constants, independent of
N,d,k,p
.

Generalized linear regression.

Let
(X,Y)
be a labelled training dataset, where
X=[x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times d}
contains the training samples on its rows and
Y=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{N})\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
contains the corresponding labels. Let
\Phi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{p}
be a generic
feature map
, from the input space to a feature space of dimension
p
. We consider the following
generalized linear model

f(x,\theta)=\Phi(x)^{\top}\theta,

(3.1)

where
\Phi(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
is the feature vector associated with the input sample
x
, and
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
is the set of the trainable parameters of the model. Our supervised learning setting involves solving the optimization problem

\mathop{\rm min}\nolimits_{\theta}\left\|\Phi(X)^{\top}\theta-Y\right\|_{2}^{2},

(3.2)

where
\Phi(X)\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times p}
is the feature matrix, containing
\Phi(x_{i})
in its
i
-th row.
From now on, we use the shorthands
\Phi:=\Phi(X)
and
K:=\Phi\Phi^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
, where
K
denotes the kernel associated with the feature map. If we assume
K
to be invertible (i.e., the model is able to fit any set of labels
Y
), it is well known that gradient descent converges to the interpolator which is the closest in
\ell_{2}
norm to the initialization, see e.g.
[
20
]
. In formulas,

\theta^{*}=\theta_{0}+\Phi^{\top}K^{-1}(Y-f(X,\theta_{0})),

(3.3)

where
\theta^{*}
is the gradient descent solution,
\theta_{0}
is the initialization and
f(X,\theta_{0})=\Phi(X)^{\top}\theta_{0}
is the output of the model (
3.1
) at initialization.

Sensitivity.

We measure the robustness of a model
f(z,\theta)
as a function of the test sample
z
. In particular, we are interested in a quantity that expresses how
sensitive
is the output when small perturbations are applied to the input
z
. More specifically, we could imagine an adversarial example
“built around”

z
as

z_{\textup{adv}}=z+\Delta_{\textup{adv}},\quad\mbox{with }\left\|\Delta_{\textup{adv}}\right\|_{2}\leq\delta\left\|z\right\|_{2}.

(3.4)

Here,
\Delta_{\textup{adv}}
is a
small
adversarial perturbation, in the sense that its
\ell_{2}
norm is only a fraction
\delta
of the
\ell_{2}
norm of
z
. Crucially, we expect
\delta
not to depend on the scalings of the problem. For example, if
z
represents an image with
d
pixels, this can correspond to perturbing every pixel by a given amount. In this case, the robustness depends on the amount of perturbation per pixel, and not on the number of pixels
d
. This in turn implies that
\delta
is a numerical constant independent of
d
.

We are interested in the response of the model to
z_{\textup{adv}}
, and how this relates to the natural scaling of the output, which we assume to be
\Theta(1)
.
2
2

2

This is a natural choice, as e.g. the output label is not expected to grow with the input dimension. However, any scaling is in principle allowed and would not change our final results.
Up to first order, we can write

\displaystyle\left|f(z_{\textup{adv}},\theta)-f(z,\theta)\right|

\displaystyle\simeq\left|\nabla_{z}f(z,\theta)^{\top}\Delta_{\textup{adv}}\right|

(3.5)

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\Delta_{\textup{adv}}\right\|\left\|\nabla_{z}f(z,\theta)\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\delta\left\|z\right\|_{2}\left\|\nabla_{z}f(z,\theta)\right\|_{2}.

The first inequality is saturated if the attacker builds an adversarial perturbation that perfectly aligns with
\nabla_{z}f(z,\theta)
, and the second inequality follows from (
3.4
). Hence, we define the
sensitivity
of the model evaluated in
z
as

\mathcal{S}_{f_{\theta}}(z):=\left\|z\right\|_{2}\left\|\nabla_{z}f(z,\theta)\right\|_{2}.

(3.6)

Recall that both
\delta
and the output of the model are constants, independent of the scaling of the problem. Hence, a robust model needs to respect
\mathcal{S}_{f_{\theta}}(z)=\mathcal{O}\left(1\right)
for its test samples
z
. In contrast, if
\mathcal{S}_{f_{\theta}}(z)\gg 1
(e.g., the sensitivity grows with the number of pixels of the image), we expect the model to be adversarially vulnerable when evaluated in
z
. In a nutshell, the goal of this paper is to provide bounds on
\mathcal{S}_{f_{\theta}}(z)
for random features and NTK models, thus establishing their robustness.

Related notions of sensitivity.

Measures of robustness similar to (
3.6
) have been used in the related literature. In particular,
[
15
]
study
\mathbb{E}_{z\sim P_{X}}[\mathcal{S}^{2}_{f_{\theta}}(z)]
in the context of a teacher-student setting with a quadratic target.
[
14
]
considers the model obtained from solving the generalized regression problem (
3.2
) and characterizes a sensitivity measure given by the Sobolev semi-norm. This quantity is again similar to
\mathbb{E}_{z\sim P_{X}}[\mathcal{S}^{2}_{f_{\theta}}(z)]
, the only difference being that the gradient is projected onto the sphere (namely, onto the data manifold) before taking the norm.
[
53
]
study a notion of perturbation stability given by
\mathbb{E}_{z,\hat{z},\theta}\left|\nabla_{z}f(z,\theta)^{\top}(z-\hat{z})\right|
, where
z
is sampled from the data distribution and
\hat{z}
is uniform in a ball centered at
z
with radius
\delta
. This would be similar to averaging over
\Delta_{\textup{adv}}
in (
3.5
), instead of choosing it adversarially. Hence,
[
53
]
capture a weaker notion of
average robustness
, as opposed to this work which studies the
adversarial robustness
.
Finally,
[
9
,
10
]
consider
\left\|f(z,\theta)\right\|_{\text{Lip}}
, where the Lipschitz constant is with respect to
z
, which is equivalent to
\mathop{\rm max}\nolimits_{z}\mathcal{S}_{f_{\theta}}(z)/\left\|z\right\|_{2}
. While this is a more stringent condition than (
3.6
), we remark that the adversarial robustness corresponds to choosing adversarially
\Delta_{\textup{adv}}
(and not
z
), as done in (
3.5
). We also note that our main results will hold with high probability over the distribution of
z
.

To conclude, we remark that, differently from previous work
[
10
,
14
]
, we opt for a
scale invariant
definition of sensitivity. This derives from the term
\left\|z\right\|_{2}
appearing in the RHS of (
3.6
), and it allows us to apply the definition to data with arbitrary scaling. In particular, if we set
\left\|z\right\|_{2}=1
, then (
3.4
) would yield
\left\|\Delta_{\rm adv}\right\|_{2}\leq\delta
. Both
[
10
]
and
[
14
]
consider this scaling of the data: the former paper uses
\mathcal{O}\left(1\right)
Lipschitz constant as an indication of a robust model, and the latter considers a term similar to
\left\|\nabla_{z}f(z,\theta)\right\|_{2}
.

Robustness of generalized linear regression.

For generalized linear models with feature map
\Phi
, plugging (
3.1
) in (

) gives

\mathcal{S}_{f_{\theta}}(z)=\left\|z\right\|_{2}\left\|\nabla_{z}\Phi(z)^{\top}\theta\right\|_{2}.

(3.7)

This expression can also provide the sensitivity of the model trained with gradient descent. Let us assume that
f(x,\theta_{0})=0
for all

and, as before, that the kernel of the feature map is invertible. Then, by plugging the value of

from (

) in the previous equation, we get

\mathcal{S}_{f_{\theta^{*}}}(z)=\left\|z\right\|_{2}\left\|\nabla_{z}\Phi(z)^{\top}\Phi^{\top}K^{-1}Y\right\|_{2}.

(3.8)

Main Result for Random Features

In this section, we provide our law of robustness for the
random features
model. In particular, we will show that, for a wide class of activations, the sensitivity of random features is
\gg 1
and, therefore, the
model is not robust
.

We assume the data labels
Y\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
to be given by
Y=g(X)+\epsilon
. Here,
g:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is the ground-truth function applied row-wise to
X\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times d}
and
\epsilon\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
is a noise vector independent of
X
, having independent sub-Gaussian entries with zero mean and variance
\mathbb{E}[\epsilon_{i}^{2}]=\varepsilon^{2}
for all
i
. The
random features (RF) model
takes the form

f_{\textup{RF}}(x,\theta)=\Phi_{\textup{RF}}(x)^{\top}\theta,\qquad\Phi_{\textup{RF}}(x)=\phi(Vx),

(4.1)

where
V
is a
k\times d
matrix, such that
V_{i,j}\mathbin{\overset{}{\sim}}_{\rm i.i.d.}\mathcal{N}(0,1/d)
, and
\phi
is an activation function applied component-wise. The number of parameters of this model is
k
, as
V
is fixed and
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
contains the trainable parameters. We initialize the model at
\theta_{0}=0
so that
f_{\textup{RF}}(x,\theta_{0})=0
for all
x
. After training, we can write the sensitivity using (
3.8
), which gives

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\textup{RF}}(z)

\displaystyle=\left\|z\right\|_{2}\left\|\nabla_{z}\Phi_{\textup{RF}}(z)^{\top}\Phi_{\textup{RF}}^{\top}K_{\textup{RF}}^{-1}Y\right\|_{2},

(4.2)

where we use the shorthands
\Phi_{\textup{RF}}:=\Phi_{\textup{RF}}(X)=\phi(XV^{\top})\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times k}
for the RF map and
K_{\textup{RF}}:=\Phi_{\textup{RF}}\Phi_{\textup{RF}}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
for the RF kernel. We will show that the kernel is in fact invertible in the argument of our main result, Theorem
1
. Throughout this section, we make the following assumptions.

Assumption 1

(Data distribution)
.

The input data
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})
are
N
i.i.d. samples from the distribution
P_{X}
, which satisfies the following properties:

1.

\left\|x\right\|_{2}=\sqrt{d}
, i.e., the data are distributed on the sphere of radius
\sqrt{d}
.

2.

\mathbb{E}[x]=0
, i.e., the data are centered.

3.

P_{X}
satisfies the
Lipschitz concentration property
. Namely, there exists an absolute constant
c>0
such that, for every Lipschitz continuous function
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
, we havefor all
t>0
,

\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\varphi(x)-\mathbb{E}_{X}[\varphi(x)]\right|>t\right)\leq 2e^{-ct^{2}/\left\|\varphi\right\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}^{2}}.

(4.3)

The first two assumptions can be achieved by simply pre-processing the raw data. For the third assumption, we remark that the family of Lipschitz concentrated distributions covers a number of important cases, e.g., standard Gaussian
[
47
]
, uniform on the sphere and on the unit (binary or continuous) hypercube
[
47
]
, or data obtained via a Generative Adversarial Network (GAN)
[
40
]
. The third requirement is common in the related literature
[
35
,
8
]
and it is equivalent to the isoperimetry condition required by
[
10
]
. We remark that the three conditions of Assumption
1
are often replaced by a stronger requirement (e.g., data uniform on the sphere), see
[
33
,
14
]
.

Finally, we note that our choice of data scaling is different from the related work
[
10
,
14
]
, as they both consider
\left\|z\right\|_{2}=1
. We choose
\left\|z\right\|_{2}=\sqrt{d}
, as we believe it to be more natural in practice. Consider, for example, the case in which
z
is an image and
d
is the number of pixels. If the pixel values are renormalized in a fixed range, then
\left\|z\right\|_{2}
scales as
\sqrt{d}
. This choice shouldn’t worry the reader, as our definition of sensitivity (
3.6
) is scale invariant, facilitating the comparison between previous results and ours.

Assumption 2

(Activation function)
.

The activation function
\phi
satisfies the following properties:

1.

\phi
is a non-linear
L
-Lipschitz function.

2.

Its first and second order derivatives
\phi^{\prime}
and
\phi^{\prime\prime}
are respectively
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
-Lipschitz functions.

These requirements are satisfied by common activations, e.g. smoothed ReLU, sigmoid, or
\tanh
.

Assumption 3

(Over-parameterization)
.

\displaystyle N\log^{3}N=o(k),

(4.4)

\displaystyle d\log^{4}d=o(k).

(4.5)

In words, the number of neurons
k
scales faster than the input dimension
d
and the number of data points
N
. Such an over-parameterized setting allows to
(i)
perfectly interpolate the data (this follows directly from the invertibility of the kernel, which can be readily deduced from the proof of Theorem
1
), and
(ii)
achieve minimum test error, see
[
31
]
. This is also in line with the current trend of heavily over-parametrized deep-learning models
[
34
]
.

Assumption 4

(High-dimensional data)
.

\displaystyle N\log^{3}N=o\left(d^{3/2}\right),

(4.6)

\displaystyle\log^{8}k=o(d).

(4.7)

While the second condition is rather mild, the first one lower bounds the input dimension

in a way that appears to be crucial to prove our main Theorem

. Understanding whether (

) can be relaxed is left as an open question.

At this point, we are ready to state our main result for random features.

Theorem 1
.

Let Assumptions
1
,
2
,
3
and
4
hold, and let
Y=g(X)+\epsilon
, such that the entries of
\epsilon
are sub-Gaussian with zero mean and variance
\mathbb{E}[\epsilon_{i}^{2}]=\varepsilon^{2}
for all
i
, independent between each other and from everything else. Let
z\sim P_{X}
be a test sample independent from the training set
(X,Y)
, and let the derivative of the activation function satisfy
\mathbb{E}_{\rho\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)}[\phi^{\prime}(\rho)]\neq 0
. Define
\mathcal{S}_{\textup{RF}}(z)
as in (
4.2
). Then,

\mathcal{S}_{\textup{RF}}(z)=\Omega\left(\sqrt[6]{N}\right)\gg 1,

(4.8)

with probability at least
1-\exp(-c\log^{2}N)
over
X
,
z
,
V
and
\epsilon
.

In words, Theorem
1
shows that a vast class of over-parameterized RF models is not robust against adversarial perturbations. A few remarks are now in order.

Beyond the universal law of robustness.

We recall that the results by
[
10
]
give a lower bound on the sensitivity of order
\sqrt{Nd/k}
. A lower bound of the same order is obtained by
[
14
]
, whose results in the finite-width setting require
N,d,k
to follow a proportional scaling (i.e.,
N=\Theta(d)=\Theta(k)
). This leaves as an open question the characterization of the robustness for sufficiently over-parameterized random features (i.e., when
k\gg Nd
).
Our Theorem
1
resolves this question by showing that the RF model is in fact not robust for
any
degree of over-parameterization (as long as
k\gg N,d
and Assumption
4
is satisfied).

Impact of the activation function and numerical results.

For the result of Theorem
1
to hold, we require the additional assumption
\mathbb{E}_{\rho\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)}[\phi^{\prime}(\rho)]\neq 0
. This may not be just a technical requirement. In fact, if
\mathbb{E}_{\rho\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)}[\phi^{\prime}(\rho)]=0
, then a key term appearing in the lower bound for the sensitivity (i.e., the Frobenius norm of the
interaction matrix
defined in (
4.11
)) has a drastically different scaling, see Theorem
4
in the proof outline below. The importance of the condition
\mathbb{E}_{\rho\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)}[\phi^{\prime}(\rho)]\neq 0
is also confirmed by our numerical simulations in the synthetic setting considered in Figure

4.1
Outline of the Argument of Theorem
1

The proof of Theorem
1
can be divided into three steps. It will be convenient to define the shorthand

A(z):=\nabla_{z}\Phi_{\textup{RF}}(z)^{\top}\Phi_{\textup{RF}}^{\top}K_{\textup{RF}}^{-1}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times N}.

(4.9)

Step 1. Fitting the noise lower bounds the sensitivity.

First, we lower bound the sensitivity of our trained model with a quantity which does not depend on the labels and grows proportionally with the noise.

Theorem 2
.

Let Assumptions
1
,
2
,
3
and
4
hold. Define
A(z)
as in (
4.9
).
Then, we have

\mathcal{S}_{\textup{RF}}(z)\geq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\|z\|_{2}\left\|A(z)\right\|_{F},

(4.10)

with probability at least
1-\exp\left(-c\left\|A(z)\right\|_{F}^{2}/\left\lVert A(z)\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}^{2}\right)
over
\epsilon
, where
c
is a numerical constant.

The proof exploits the independence between
g(X)
and
\epsilon
and the Hanson-Wright inequality. The details are contained in Appendix
C.1
.

Theorem
2
removes the labels from the expression, and reduces the problem of estimating the robustness to characterizing the Frobenius norm of
A(z)
(as long as
\left\|A(z)\right\|_{F}\gg\left\lVert A(z)\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}
, so that (
4.10
) holds with high probability). This is in line with
[
10
,
14
]
, which provide upper bounds on the robustness of models fitting the data below noise level.

Step 2. Splitting between interaction and kernel components.

Next, we split the matrix
A(z)
into two separate objects. The first is the
interaction matrix
given by

\mathcal{I}_{\textup{RF}}(z):=\nabla_{z}\Phi_{\textup{RF}}(z)^{\top}\tilde{\Phi}_{\textup{RF}}^{\top},

(4.11)

where we use the shorthand
\tilde{\Phi}_{\textup{RF}}:=\Phi_{\textup{RF}}-\mathbb{E}_{X}[\Phi_{\textup{RF}}]\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times k}
. The second is the
centered kernel

\tilde{K}_{\textup{RF}}:=\tilde{\Phi}_{\textup{RF}}\tilde{\Phi}_{\textup{RF}}^{\top}
, whose spectrum can be studied separately.

Theorem 3
.

Let Assumptions
1
,
2
,
3
and
4
hold. Define
A(z)
as in (
4.9
). Then, we have

\displaystyle\left\|A(z)\right\|_{F}\geq\lambda_{\mathop{\rm max}\nolimits}^{-1}\left(\tilde{K}_{\textup{RF}}\right)\left\|\mathcal{I}_{\textup{RF}}(z)\right\|_{F}-C\sqrt{N+d}/d,

(4.12)

\displaystyle\left\|A(z)\right\|_{F}\leq\lambda_{\mathop{\rm min}\nolimits}^{-1}(\tilde{K}_{\textup{RF}})\left\|\mathcal{I}_{\textup{RF}}(z)\right\|_{F}+C\sqrt{N+d}/d,

which implies

\displaystyle\left\|A(z)\right\|_{F}\geq C_{1}\frac{d}{kN}\left\|\mathcal{I}_{\textup{RF}}(z)\right\|_{F}-C\sqrt{N+d}/d,

(4.13)

\displaystyle\left\|A(z)\right\|_{F}\leq C_{2}k^{-1}\left\|\mathcal{I}_{\textup{RF}}(z)\right\|_{F}+C\sqrt{N+d}/d,

with probability at least
1-\exp(-c\log^{2}N)
over
X
and
V
, where
c
,
C
,
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
are absolute constants.

Proof sketch.
To prove the claim, first we characterize the extremal eigenvalues of the kernel
K_{\textup{RF}}:=\Phi_{\textup{RF}}\Phi_{\textup{RF}}^{\top}
and of its centered counterpart
\tilde{K}_{\textup{RF}}:=\tilde{\Phi}_{\textup{RF}}\tilde{\Phi}_{\textup{RF}}^{\top}
, with
\tilde{\Phi}_{\textup{RF}}=\Phi_{\textup{RF}}-\mathbb{E}_{X}\Phi_{\textup{RF}}
, see Appendix
C.2
. Then, we perform a delicate centering step on the matrix
A(z)
defined in (

). Informally, we show that

\|\nabla_{z}\Phi_{\textup{RF}}(z)^{\top}\Phi_{\textup{RF}}^{\top}K_{\textup{RF}}^{-1}\|_{F}\approx\|\nabla_{z}\Phi_{\textup{RF}}(z)^{\top}\tilde{\Phi}_{\textup{RF}}^{\top}\tilde{K}_{\textup{RF}}^{-1}\|_{F},

(4.14)

see Lemma
C.13
for a precise statement.
This is the key technical ingredient, since the rank-one components coming from the average feature matrix have a large operator norm, which would trivialize the lower bound on the sensitivity. More specifically, the centering leads to two rank-one terms which are successively removed via the Sherman-Morrison formula and a number of ad-hoc estimates, see Appendix
C.3
. Finally, the proof of (
4.12
)-(
4.13
) follows by combining (

) with the bounds on the smallest/largest eigenvalues of
\tilde{K}_{\textup{RF}}
, and it appears at the end of Appendix
C.3
. ∎

Theorem
3
unveils the crucial role played by the interaction matrix
\mathcal{I}_{\textup{RF}}(z)
on the robustness of the model. This term is the only one containing the test sample
z
, and it depends on how the term
\nabla_{z}\Phi_{\textup{RF}}(z)

aligns
(or
“interacts”
) with the centered feature map of the training data
\tilde{\Phi}_{\textup{RF}}
.

Step 3. Estimating
\left\|\mathcal{I}_{\textup{RF}}(z)\right\|_{F}
.

Finally, we provide a precise estimate on the norm of the interaction matrix
\left\|\mathcal{I}_{\textup{RF}}(z)\right\|_{F}
. To do so, we assume that the test point
z
is independently sampled from the data distribution
P_{X}
.

Theorem 4
.

Let Assumptions
1
,
2
,
3
and
4
hold. Let
z\sim P_{X}
be sampled independently from the training set
(X,Y)
, and
\mathcal{I}_{\textup{RF}}(z)
be defined as in (
4.11
). Then,

\left|\left\|\mathcal{I}_{\textup{RF}}(z)\right\|_{F}-\mathbb{E}_{\rho}^{2}[\phi^{\prime}(\rho)]\frac{k\sqrt{N}}{\sqrt{d}}\right|=o\left(\frac{k\sqrt{N}}{\sqrt{d}}\right),

(4.15)

with probability at least
1-\exp(-c\log^{2}k)
over
X
,
z
and
V
, where
c
is an absolute constant.

Proof sketch.
A direct calculation gives that

\left\|\mathcal{I}_{\textup{RF}}(z)\right\|_{F}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\|V^{\top}\text{diag}\left(\phi^{\prime}(Vz)\right)\tilde{\phi}(Vx_{i})\right\|_{2}^{2},

and we bound separately each term of the sum. Note that the three terms
V^{\top}
,
\text{diag}\left(\phi^{\prime}(Vz)\right)
and
\tilde{\phi}(Vx_{i})
are correlated by the presence of
V
. However,
V
contributes to the last two terms only via a single projection (along the direction of
z
and

, respectively). Hence, the key idea is to use a Taylor expansion to split
\text{diag}\left(\phi^{\prime}(Vz)\right)
and
\tilde{\phi}(Vx_{i})
into a component correlated with

and an independent one. The correlated components are computed exactly and the remaining independent term is shown to have a negligible effect. The details are in Appendix
C.4
. ∎

At this point, the proof of Theorem

follows by combining the results of Theorems
2
-
4
. The details are in Appendix
C.5
.

We note that the results of Theorems
2
-
4
do not require the assumption
\mathbb{E}_{\rho\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)}[\phi^{\prime}(\rho)]\neq 0
. In fact, the role of this additional condition is clarified by the statement of Theorem

\mathbb{E}_{\rho\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)}[\phi^{\prime}(\rho)]\neq 0
, then
\left\|\mathcal{I}_{\textup{RF}}(z)\right\|_{F}
is of order

; otherwise,
\left\|\mathcal{I}_{\textup{RF}}(z)\right\|_{F}
is drastically smaller. This provides an explanation to the qualitatively different behavior of the sensitivity for
\phi(x)=\tanh(x)
and
\phi(x)=x^{2}
displayed in Figure

. To conclude, the interaction matrix appears to be the key quantity capturing the impact of the activation function on the robustness of the ERM solution.

5
Main Result for NTK Regression

In this section, we provide our law of robustness for the
NTK
model. In particular, we will show that, for a class of
even
activations, the sensitivity of NTK can be
\mathcal{O}\left(1\right)
and, therefore, the
model is robust
for some scaling of the parameters.

We consider the following two-layer neural network

f_{\textup{NN}}(x,w)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\phi\left(W^{1}_{i:}x\right)-\sum_{i=1}^{k}\phi\left(W^{2}_{i:}x\right).

(5.1)

Here, the hidden layer contains
2k
neurons;
\phi
is an activation function applied component-wise;
W^{1},W^{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times d}
denote the first and second half of the weights of the hidden layer, respectively; for
j\in\{1,2\}
,
W^{j}_{i:}
denotes the
i
-th row of
W^{j}
; the first
k
weights of the second layer are set to
1
, and the last
k
weights to
-1
. We indicate with
w
the vector containing the parameters of this model, i.e.,
w=[\text{vec}(W^{1}),\text{vec}(W^{2})]\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, with
p=2kd
. For convenience, we initialize the network so that its output is
0
. This has been shown to be necessary to have a robust model after lazy training
[
15
,
48
]
. Specifically, we let
w_{0}=[\text{vec}(W_{0}^{1}),\text{vec}(W_{0}^{2})]
be the vector of the parameters at initialization, where we take
[W^{1}_{0}]_{i,j}\mathbin{\overset{}{\sim}}_{\rm i.i.d.}\mathcal{N}(0,1/d)
and
W^{2}_{0}=W^{1}_{0}
. Here, with a slight abuse of notation, we use the subscript 0 to refer to the initialization, and not to indicate matrix rows. This readily implies that
f_{\textup{NN}}(x,w_{0})=0
, for all
x
.

Now, the
NTK regression model
takes the form

f_{\textup{NTK}}(x,\theta)\hskip-1.49994pt=\hskip-1.49994pt\Phi_{\textup{NTK}}(x)^{\top}\hskip-1.49994pt\theta,\,\,\Phi_{\textup{NTK}}(x)\hskip-1.49994pt=\hskip-1.49994pt\nabla_{w}f_{\textup{NN}}(x,w)|_{w=w_{0}}.

(5.2)

Here, the vector trainable parameters is
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, again with
p=2kd
, which is initialized with
\theta_{0}=w_{0}
. This is the same model considered by
[
15
,
33
]
. We remark that
f_{\textup{NTK}}(x,\theta)
is equivalent to the linearization of
f_{\textup{NN}}(x,w)
around the initial point
w_{0}
, see e.g.
[
24
,
6
]
.

An application of the chain rule gives

\Phi_{\textup{NTK}}(x)=\left[x\otimes\phi^{\prime}(W^{1}_{0}x),-x\otimes\phi^{\prime}(W^{2}_{0}x)\right]=:\left[\Phi_{\textup{NTK}}^{\prime}(x),-\Phi_{\textup{NTK}}^{\prime}(x)\right],

(5.3)

where the last equality follows from the fact that
W^{1}_{0}=W^{2}_{0}
, and the definition
\Phi_{\textup{NTK}}^{\prime}(x):=x\otimes\phi^{\prime}(W^{1}_{0}x)
. Thus, since
\theta_{0}=w_{0}=[\text{vec}(W^{1}_{0}),\text{vec}(W^{2}_{0})]=[\text{vec}(W^{1}_{0}),\text{vec}(W^{1}_{0})]
, we have

f_{\textup{NTK}}(x,\theta_{0})=\Phi_{\textup{NTK}}(x)^{\top}\theta_{0}=\left[\Phi_{\textup{NTK}}^{\prime}(x),-\Phi_{\textup{NTK}}^{\prime}(x)\right]^{\top}[\text{vec}(W^{1}_{0}),\text{vec}(W^{1}_{0})]=0.

(5.4)

This means that our model’s output is 0 at initialization. Then, we can use (
3.8
) to express the sensitivity of the trained NTK regression model as

\mathcal{S}_{\textup{NTK}}(z)=\left\|z\right\|_{2}\left\|\nabla_{z}\Phi_{\textup{NTK}}(z)^{\top}\Phi_{\textup{NTK}}^{\top}K_{\textup{NTK}}^{-1}Y\right\|_{2},

(5.5)

where
\Phi_{\textup{NTK}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times p}
contains in its
i
-th row the feature map of the
i
-th training sample
\Phi_{\textup{NTK}}(x_{i})
and
K_{\textup{NTK}}=\Phi_{\textup{NTK}}\Phi_{\textup{NTK}}^{\top}
. The invertibility of the kernel will again follow from the proof of our main result, Theorem
5
. Throughout this section, we make the following assumptions.

Assumption 5

(Activation function)
.

The activation function
\phi
satisfies the following properties:

1.

\phi
is a non-linear, even function.

2.

Its first order derivative
\phi^{\prime}
is an
L
-Lipschitz function.

We restrict our analysis to an even activation function, as this requirement significantly simplifies the derivations. The impact of the activation on the robustness will also be discussed at the end of this section.

Assumption 6

(Minimum over-parameterization)
.

N\log^{8}N=o(kd).

(5.6)

Eq. (
5.6
) provides the weakest possible requirement on the number of parameters of the model capable to guarantee interpolation for generic data points, as it leads to a lower bound on the smallest eigenvalue of
K_{\textup{NTK}}

[
8
]
.

Assumption 7

(High-dimensional data)
.

k=\mathcal{O}\left(d\right).

(5.7)

This requirement is purely technical, and we leave as a future work the interesting problem of characterizing the sensitivity of the NTK regression model when
d=o(k)
.

We will still use Assumption
1
on the data distribution
P_{X}
, and the only assumption on the labels is that
\left\|Y\right\|_{2}=\Theta(\sqrt{N})
, which corresponds to the natural requirement that the output is
\Theta(1)
. At this point, we are ready to
state our main result for NTK regression.

Figure 2:
Sensitivity as a function of the number of parameters
p
, for an NTK model with synthetic data (first and second plot), and with inputs taken from two classes of the MNIST and CIFAR-10 datasets (third and fourth plot respectively). The rest of the setup is similar to that of Figure
1
.

Theorem 5
.

Let Assumptions
1
,
5
,
6
and
7
hold. Then, we have

\mathcal{S}_{\textup{NTK}}(z)=\mathcal{O}\left(\log k\left(1+\sqrt{\frac{N}{k}}\right)\sqrt{\frac{N}{k}}\right),

(5.8)

with probability at least
1-Ne^{-c\log^{2}k}-e^{-c\log^{2}N}
over
X
and
w_{0}
.

Furthermore, when
N=\mathcal{O}\left(k\right)
, (
5.8
) simplifies to

\mathcal{S}_{\textup{NTK}}(z)=\mathcal{O}\left(\log k\sqrt{\frac{Nd}{p}}\right),

(5.9)

where
p=2dk
denotes the number of parameters of the model.

Proof sketch.
As for the RF model, we crucially separate the contribution of the interaction matrix and of the kernel of the model. Specifically, we upper bound the RHS of (
5.5
) with

\left\|z\right\|_{2}\left\lVert\nabla_{z}\Phi_{\textup{NTK}}(z)^{\top}\Phi_{\textup{NTK}}^{\top}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\left\lVert K_{\textup{NTK}}^{-1}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\left\|Y\right\|_{2}.

(5.10)

Now, each term in (
5.10
) is treated separately. First, by assumption,
\left\|Y\right\|_{2}=\Theta(\sqrt{N})
. Next, we have that
\left\lVert K_{\textup{NTK}}^{-1}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}=\lambda_{\mathop{\rm min}\nolimits}^{-1}(K_{\textup{NTK}})=\mathcal{O}\left((dk)^{-1}\right)
, which can be deduced from
[
8
]
, see Lemma
D.1
. Finally, the bound on
\left\lVert\nabla_{z}\Phi_{\textup{NTK}}(z)^{\top}\Phi_{\textup{NTK}}^{\top}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}
is computed explicitly through an application of the chain-rule (see Lemma
D.3
), and it critically depends on the data being 0-mean and on the activation being even.
∎

In words, Theorem
5
shows that, for even activations and
k
scaling super-linearly in
N
,
\mathcal{S}_{\textup{NTK}}(z)=\mathcal{O}\left(1\right)
, namely, the NTK model is robust against adversarial perturbations. A few remarks are now in order.

Saturating the lower bound from the universal law of robustness.

We highlight that our upper bound on the sensitivity in (
5.9
) exhibits the same scaling as the lower bound by
[
10
]
, thus saturating the universal law of robustness. A lower bound on the sensitivity of the same order is also provided by
[
14
]
, albeit restricted to the setting in which
k=\Theta(d)
and
k=\mathcal{O}\left(N\right)
. Note that Theorem
5
upper bounds the sensitivity of the model obtained by running gradient descent (over
\theta
) on
f_{\textup{NTK}}(x,\theta)
. It is well known, see e.g.
[
12
]
, that a similar solution is obtained by running gradient descent (over
w
) on
f_{\textup{NN}}(x,w)
. This result holds after suitably rescaling the initialization of
f_{\textup{NN}}(x,w)
, and the rescaling does not affect our Theorem
5
. As a result, Theorem
5
proves that a class of two-layer neural networks has sensitivity of order
\sqrt{Nd/p}
(up to logarithmic factors), thus resolving in the affirmative Conjecture 2 of
[
9
]
.

Impact of the activation function and numerical results.

Theorem
5
applies to even activation functions. At the technical level, the symmetry in
\phi
directly “centers” the kernel in the expression (
5.5
) of the sensitivity, which largely simplifies our argument. We conjecture that this symmetry may in fact be fundamental to guarantee the robustness of the model. In fact, the first plot of Figure
2
shows that, for
\phi(x)=x^{2}
, the sensitivity keeps decreasing, as the number of neurons
k
(and, therefore, the number of parameters
p
) grows. In contrast, for
\phi(x)=\tanh(x)
, the sensitivity plateaus at a value which is an order of magnitude larger than for
\phi(x)=x^{2}
(see the second plot of Figure
2
).
This difference is still visible even for real-world datasets (MNIST, CIFAR-10), as displayed in the third and fourth plot of Figure
2
.

6
Conclusions

Our paper provides a precise and quantitative characterization of how the robustness of the solution obtained via empirical risk minimization depends on the model at hand: for random features and non-even activations, over-parameterization does not help, and we provide bounds tighter than the universal law of robustness proposed by
[
10
]
; for NTK regression and even activations, the model is robust as soon as
p>dN
, i.e., the universal law of robustness is saturated. Numerical results on synthetic and standard datasets confirm the impact of the model on the robustness of the solution. The present contribution focuses on empirical risk minimization, which represents the cornerstone of deep learning training algorithms. In contrast, a number of recent papers has focused on adversarial training, considering the trade-off between generalization and robust error, see e.g.
[
37
,
52
,
13
,
11
,
32
,
21
]
and references therein. We conclude by mentioning that the technical tools developed in this work could be applied also to the models deriving from adversarial training, and we leave such an analysis as future work.

Appendix A
Additional Notations and Remarks

Given a sub-exponential random variable
X
, let
\|X\|_{\psi_{1}}=\inf\{t>0\,\,:\,\,\mathbb{E}[\exp(|X|/t)]\leq 2\}
.
Similarly, for a sub-Gaussian random variable, let
\|X\|_{\psi_{2}}=\inf\{t>0\,\,:\,\,\mathbb{E}[\exp(X^{2}/t^{2})]\leq 2\}
.
We use the analogous definitions for vectors. In particular, let
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a random vector, then
\left\|X\right\|_{\psi_{2}}:=\sup_{\left\|u\right\|_{2}=1}\left\|u^{\top}X\right\|_{\psi_{2}}
and
\left\|X\right\|_{\psi_{1}}:=\sup_{\left\|u\right\|_{2}=1}\left\|u^{\top}X\right\|_{\psi_{1}}
. Notice that if a vector has independent, mean 0, sub-Gaussian (sub-exponential) entries, then its sub-Gaussian (sub-exponential). This is a direct consequence of Hoeffding’s inequality and Bernstein’s inequality (see Theorems 2.6.3 and 2.8.2 in
[
47
]
).
It is useful to also define the generic Orlicz norm of order
\alpha
of a real random variable
X
as

\left\|X\right\|_{\psi_{\alpha}}:=\inf\{t>0\,\,:\,\,\mathbb{E}[\exp(|X|^{\alpha}/t^{\alpha})]\leq 2\}.

(A.1)

From this definition, it follows that
\left\||X|^{\gamma}\right\|_{\psi_{\alpha/\gamma}}=\left\|X\right\|_{\psi_{\alpha}}^{\gamma}
. Finally, we recall the property that if
X
and
Y
are scalar random variables, we have
\left\|XY\right\|_{\psi_{1}}\leq\left\|X\right\|_{\psi_{2}}\left\|Y\right\|_{\psi_{2}}
.

We say that a random variable respects the Lipschitz concentration property if there exists an absolute constant
c>0
such that, for every Lipschitz continuous function
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
, we have
\mathbb{E}|\varphi(X)|<+\infty
, and for all
t>0
,

\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\varphi(x)-\mathbb{E}_{X}[\varphi(x)]\right|>t\right)\leq 2e^{-ct^{2}/\left\|\varphi\right\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}^{2}}.

(A.2)

When we state that a random variable or vector
X
is sub-Gaussian (or sub-exponential), we implicitly mean
\left\|X\right\|_{\psi_{2}}=\mathcal{O}\left(1\right)
, i.e. it doesn’t increase with the scalings of the problem. Notice that, if
X
is Lipschitz concentrated, then
X-\mathbb{E}[X]
is sub-Gaussian.
If
X\in\mathbb{R}
is sub-Gaussian and
\varphi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is Lipschitz, we have that
\varphi(X)
is sub-Gaussian as well. Also, if a random variable is sub-Gaussian or sub-exponential, its
p
-th momentum is upper bounded by a constant (that might depend on
p
).

In general, we indicate with
C
and
c
absolute, strictly positive, numerical constants, that do not depend on the scalings of the problem, i.e. input dimension, number of neurons, or number of training samples. Their value may change from line to line.

Given a matrix
A
, we indicate with
A_{i:}
its
i
-th row, and with
A_{:j}
its
j
-th column. Given two matrices
A,B\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
, we denote by
A\circ B
their Hadamard product, and by
A\ast B=[(A_{1:}\otimes B_{1:}),\ldots,(A_{m:}\otimes B_{m:})]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n^{2}}
their row-wise Kronecker product (also known as Khatri-Rao product). We denote
A^{*2}=A\ast A
. We say that a matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is positive semi definite (p.s.d.) if it’s symmetric and for every vector
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
we have
v^{\top}Av\geq 0
.

Appendix B
Useful Lemmas

Lemma B.1
.

Let
Z
be a matrix with rows
z_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, for
i\in[N]
, such that
N=o(n/\log^{4}n)
. Assume that
\{z_{i}\}_{i\in[N]}
are independent sub-exponential random vectors with
\psi=\mathop{\rm max}\nolimits_{i}\left\|z_{i}\right\|_{\psi_{1}}
. Let
\eta_{\textup{min}}=\mathop{\rm min}\nolimits_{i}\left\|z_{i}\right\|_{2}
and
\eta_{\textup{max}}=\mathop{\rm max}\nolimits_{i}\left\|z_{i}\right\|_{2}
. Set
\xi=\psi K+K^{\prime}
, and

\Delta:=C\xi^{2}N^{1/4}n^{3/4}
. Then, we have that

\lambda_{\rm min}\left(ZZ^{\top}\right)\geq\eta_{\textup{min}}^{2}-\Delta,\qquad\lambda_{\rm max}\left(ZZ^{\top}\right)\leq\eta_{\textup{max}}^{2}+\Delta

(B.1)

holds with probability at least

1-\exp\left(-cK\sqrt{N}\log\left(\frac{2n}{N}\right)\right)-\mathbb{P}\left(\eta_{\text{max}}\geq K^{\prime}\sqrt{n}\right),

(B.2)

where
c
and
C
are numerical constants.

Proof.

Following the notation in
[
2
]
, we define

B:=\sup_{u\in\mathbb{R}^{N}:\left\|u\right\|_{2}=1}\left|\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{N}u_{i}z_{i}\right\|_{2}^{2}-\sum_{i=1}^{N}u_{i}^{2}\left\|z_{i}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right|^{\frac{1}{2}}.

(B.3)

Then, for any
u\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
with unit norm, we have that

\begin{split}\left\|Zu\right\|_{2}^{2}&=\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{N}u_{i}z_{i}\right\|_{2}^{2}-\sum_{i=1}^{N}u_{i}^{2}\left\|z_{i}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{N}u_{i}^{2}\left\|z_{i}\right\|_{2}^{2}\geq\mathop{\rm min}\nolimits_{i}\left\|z_{i}\right\|_{2}^{2}-B^{2},\end{split}

(B.4)

which implies that

\lambda_{\rm min}\left(ZZ^{\top}\right)=\inf_{u\in\mathbb{R}^{N}:\left\|u\right\|_{2}=1}\left\|Zu\right\|_{2}^{2}\geq\mathop{\rm min}\nolimits_{i}\left\|z_{i}\right\|_{2}^{2}-B^{2}.

(B.5)

In the same way, it can also be proven that

\lambda_{\rm max}\left(ZZ^{\top}\right)\leq\mathop{\rm max}\nolimits_{i}\left\|z_{i}\right\|_{2}^{2}+B^{2}.

(B.6)

In our case,
z_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. In the statement of Theorem 3.2 of
[
2
]

, and

By combining (

Lemma B.2
.

Let
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}

Lemma B.3
.

Let
z\sim P_{Z}
be a mean-0 and Lipschitz concentrated random vector (see (
A.2

\Gamma(z)=z\otimes z-\mathbb{E}_{z}\left[z\otimes z\right].

(B.12)

(B.13)

where

is a numerical constant.

We have that

\left\|\Gamma(z)\right\|_{\psi_{1}}=\sup_{\left\|u\right\|_{2}=1}\left\|u^{\top}\Gamma(z)\right\|_{\psi_{1}}=\sup_{\left\|U\right\|_{F}=1}\left\|z^{\top}Uz-\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[z^{\top}Uz\right]\right\|_{\psi_{1}}.

[

\mathbb{P}\left(|z^{\top}Uz-\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[z^{\top}Uz\right]|>t\right)<2\exp\left(-\frac{1}{C_{1}}\mathop{\rm min}\nolimits\left(\frac{t^{2}}{\left\|U\right\|^{2}_{F}},\frac{t}{\left\lVert U\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}}\right)\right),

(B.15)

where
C_{1}
is a numerical constant. Thus, by Lemma 5.5 of
[
41

(B.16)

for some numerical constant
C
, which gives the desired result.
∎

Lemma B.4
.

Let

be a sub-Gaussian vector such that

Furthermore, we have that

\left\|z\right\|_{\psi_{2}}:=\sup_{w^{\prime}\text{s.t.}\left\|w^{\prime}\right\|_{2}=1}\left\|(w^{\prime})^{\top}z\right\|_{\psi_{2}}\geq\left\|w^{\top}z\right\|_{\psi_{2}}\geq\frac{1}{C}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left[(w^{\top}z)^{2}\right]},

(B.19)

where

[
47
]
. By combining (
B.18
) and (
B.19
) we get our desired result.

Proof of Theorem
2
.

We have

\left\|AY\right\|_{2}^{2}=\left\|A\left(g(X)+\epsilon\right)\right\|_{2}^{2}=\epsilon^{\top}A^{\top}A\epsilon+g(X)^{\top}A^{\top}Ag(X)+2g(X)^{\top}A^{\top}A\epsilon.

(C.2)

Setting

in the previous equation, we readily get

\mathbb{P}(|g(X)^{\top}A^{\top}A\epsilon|<y\left\lVert A\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}M)>1-2\exp\left(-cy^{2}\right).

We will condition on this event for the rest of the proof.

On the other hand, a direct application of Theorem 6.3.2 in

(C.4)

\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\left\|A\epsilon\right\|_{2}-\varepsilon\left\|A\right\|_{F}\right|>z\left\lVert A\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\right)<2\exp\left(-c_{1}z^{2}\right).

C.2

Lemma C.1
.

Let
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be distributed as
V_{j:}
, for some
j\in[k]
, independent from
X
. Then, we have

\left\|\phi(Xv)\right\|_{\psi_{2}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{N}\right),

(C.9)

where the sub-Gaussian norm is intended over the probability space of
v
. This result holds with probability
1
over
X
.

Proof.

By triangular inequality, we have

\left\|\phi(Xv)\right\|_{\psi_{2}}\leq\left\|\phi(Xv)-\mathbb{E}_{v}\left[\phi(Xv)\right]\right\|_{\psi_{2}}+\left\|\mathbb{E}_{v}\left[\phi(Xv)\right]\right\|_{\psi_{2}}.

(C.10)

Let’s bound the two terms separately. As
\phi
is an
L
-Lipschitz function, and
\sqrt{d}v
is a Lipschitz concentrated random vector, we have

\left\|\phi(Xv)-\mathbb{E}_{v}\left[\phi(Xv)\right]\right\|_{\psi_{2}}\leq CL\frac{\left\lVert X\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}}{\sqrt{d}}\leq CL\frac{\left\|X\right\|_{F}}{\sqrt{d}}=CL\sqrt{N},

(C.11)

where in the last equality we used the fact that
\left\|x_{i}\right\|_{2}=\sqrt{d}
.

For the second term of (
C.10
), we have

\left\|\mathbb{E}_{v}\left[\phi(Xv)\right]\right\|_{\psi_{2}}\leq C\left\|\mathbb{E}_{v}\left[\phi(Xv)\right]\right\|_{2}.

(C.12)

Let
(\phi(Xv))_{i}
denote the
i
-th component of the vector
\phi(Xv)
. As
v
is a Gaussian vector with covariance
I/d
and since
\left\|x_{i}\right\|=\sqrt{d}
for all
i\in[N]
, we have
\mathbb{E}_{v}\left[(\phi(Xv))_{i}\right]=\mathbb{E}_{\rho}[\phi(\rho)]=\mathcal{O}\left(1\right)
, where
\rho
is a standard Gaussian random variable, and the last equality is true since
\phi
is Lipschitz. Thus, we have

\left\|\mathbb{E}_{v}\left[\phi(Xv)\right]\right\|_{\psi_{2}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{N}\right).

(C.13)

Putting together (
C.10
), (
C.11
), and (
C.13
), we get the thesis.
∎

Lemma C.2
.

Let
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be distributed as
V_{j:}
, for some
j\in[k]

Lemma C.3

Let

Proof.

First, we want to compare

\tilde{Y}\tilde{Y}^{\top}

\tilde{Y}:=X^{*2}-\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[X^{*2}\right].

(C.18)

Let’s also define

\mathbf{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}

i

Lemma C.4
.

.
To be precise, we define

\bar{\Phi}_{:j}=\phi(Xv_{j})\chi\left(\left\|\phi(Xv_{j})\right\|_{2}^{2}\leq R\right),

(C.37)

where
\chi
is the indicator function. In this case,
\chi=1
if
\left\|\phi(Xv_{j})\right\|^{2}_{2}\leq R
, and
\chi=0
otherwise. As this is a column-wise truncation, it’s easy to verify that

\Phi\Phi^{\top}\succeq\bar{\Phi}\bar{\Phi}^{\top}.

(C.38)

Denoting with
v
a generic weight
v_{i}
, we can also write

G=k\mathbb{E}_{v}\left[\phi\left(Xv\right)\phi\left(Xv\right)^{\top}\right],

(C.39)

\bar{G}=k\mathbb{E}_{v}\left[\phi\left(Xv\right)\phi\left(Xv\right)^{\top}\chi\left(\left\|\phi\left(Xv\right)\right\|^{2}_{2}\leq R\right)\right].

(C.40)

Since all the columns of
\bar{\Phi}
have limited norm with probability one, we can use Matrix Chernoff Inequality (see Theorem 1.1 of
[
44
]
). In particular, for
\delta\in[0,1]
, we have,

\mathbb{P}\left(\lambda_{\rm min}\left(\bar{\Phi}\bar{\Phi}^{\top}\right)\leq(1-\delta)\lambda_{\rm min}\left(\bar{G}\right)\right)\leq N\left(\frac{e^{-\delta}}{(1-\delta)^{1-\delta}}\right)^{\lambda_{\rm min}\left(\bar{G}\right)/R},

(C.41)

where we used the fact that
\lambda_{\rm max}\left(\bar{\Phi}_{:j}\bar{\Phi}_{:j}^{\top}\right)\leq R
and
\bar{\Phi}\bar{\Phi}^{\top}=\sum_{j}\bar{\Phi}_{:j}\bar{\Phi}_{:j}^{\top}
.
Setting
\delta=1/2
in the previous equation gives

\mathbb{P}\left(\lambda_{\rm min}\left(\bar{\Phi}\bar{\Phi}^{\top}\right)\leq\lambda_{\rm min}\left(\bar{G}\right)/2\right)\leq\exp\left(-c\lambda_{\rm min}\left(\bar{G}\right)/R+c_{1}\log N\right),

(C.42)

where this probability is over
V
.

Finally, we prove that the matrices
G
and
\bar{G}
are
“close”
. In particular, we have

\displaystyle\left\lVert G-\bar{G}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}

\displaystyle=k\left\lVert\mathbb{E}_{v}\left[\phi\left(Xv\right)\phi\left(Xv\right)^{\top}\chi\left(\left\|\phi\left(Xv\right)\right\|^{2}_{2}>R\right)\right]\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}

(C.43)

\displaystyle\leq k\mathbb{E}_{v}\left[\left\lVert\phi\left(Xv\right)\phi\left(Xv\right)^{\top}\chi\left(\left\|\phi\left(Xv\right)\right\|^{2}_{2}>R\right)\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\right]

\displaystyle=k\mathbb{E}_{v}\left[\left\|\phi\left(Xv\right)\right\|_{2}^{2}\chi\left(\left\|\phi\left(Xv\right)\right\|^{2}_{2}>R\right)\right]

\displaystyle=k\int_{s=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}_{v}\left(\left\|\phi\left(Xv\right)\right\|_{2}\chi\left(\left\|\phi\left(Xv\right)\right\|^{2}_{2}>R\right)>\sqrt{s}\right)\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=k\int_{s=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}_{v}\left(\left\|\phi\left(Xv\right)\right\|_{2}>\mathop{\rm max}\nolimits(\sqrt{R},\sqrt{s})\right)\mathrm{d}s,

where we applied Jensen inequality in the second line.
Let’s define
\psi:=\left\|\phi\left(Xv\right)\right\|_{\psi_{2}}
, where the sub-Gaussian norm is intended over the probability space of
v
. We have

\displaystyle\left\lVert G-\bar{G}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}

\displaystyle\leq k\int_{s=0}^{\infty}\exp\left(-c_{2}\frac{\mathop{\rm max}\nolimits(R,s)}{\psi^{2}}\right)\mathrm{d}s

(C.44)

\displaystyle\leq k\int_{s=0}^{\infty}\exp\left(-c_{2}\frac{R+s}{2\psi^{2}}\right)\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=Ck\psi^{2}\exp\left(-c_{2}\frac{R}{2\psi^{2}}\right),

where the first inequality follows from the definition of sub-Gaussian random variable, and the second one from
\mathop{\rm max}\nolimits(R,s)\geq(R+s)/2
.

By Lemma
C.1
, we have
\psi=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{N}\right)
. Then, setting
R=k/\log^{2}N
, we get

\left\lVert G-\bar{G}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\leq Ck\psi^{2}\exp\left(-c\frac{R}{2\psi^{2}}\right)\leq C_{1}k\exp\left(-c_{2}\frac{k}{N\log^{2}N}+\log N\right)=o(k),

(C.45)

where the last equality is a consequence of Assumption
3
. Notice that this happens with probability 1 over
v
.

Due to Lemma
C.4
, we have that
\lambda_{\rm min}\left(G\right)=\Omega(k)
, with probability at least
1-\exp\left(-c_{3}\log^{2}N\right)
over
X
. Conditioning on such event, we have

\lambda_{\rm min}\left(\bar{G}\right)\geq\lambda_{\rm min}\left(G\right)-\left\lVert G-\bar{G}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}=\Omega(k)-o(k)=\Omega(k).

(C.46)

To conclude, looking back at (
C.42
), we also get
\lambda_{\rm min}\left(\bar{\Phi}\bar{\Phi}^{\top}\right)=\Omega(k)
with probability (over
V
) at least

1-\exp\left(-c\lambda_{\rm min}\left(\bar{G}\right)/R+c_{1}\log N\right)\geq 1-\exp\left(-c_{4}\frac{k\log^{2}N}{k}+c_{1}\log N\right)\geq 1-\exp\left(-c_{5}\log^{2}N\right).

(C.47)

This concludes the proof.
∎

Lemma C.6
.

We have that

\lambda_{\rm min}\left(\mathbb{E}_{V}\left[\tilde{K}\right]\right)=\Omega(k),

(C.48)

with probability at least
1-\exp(-c\log^{2}N)
over
X
, where
c
is an absolute constant.

Proof.

Indicating with
x_{i}
the
i
-th row of
X
, and with
\tilde{\phi}(x_{i}^{\top}v):=\phi(x_{i}^{\top}v)-\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[\phi(x_{1}^{\top}v)\right]
, we can write

\mathbb{E}_{V}\left[\tilde{\Phi}\tilde{\Phi}^{\top}\right]=\mathbb{E}_{V}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{k}\tilde{\phi}(XV_{i:}^{\top})\tilde{\phi}(XV_{i:}^{\top})^{\top}\right]=k\mathbb{E}_{v}\left[\tilde{\phi}(Xv)\tilde{\phi}(Xv)^{\top}\right]:=kM,

(C.49)

where we use the shorthand
v
to indicate a random variable distributed as
V_{1:}
.

Exploiting the Hermite expansion of
\phi
, we can write

\displaystyle M_{ij}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{v}\left[\tilde{\phi}(x_{i}^{\top}v)\tilde{\phi}(x_{j}^{\top}v)\right]

(C.50)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{v}\left[\left(\phi(x_{i}^{\top}v)-\mathbb{E}_{z}\left[\phi(z^{\top}v)\right]\right)\left(\phi(x_{j}^{\top}v)-\mathbb{E}_{y}\left[\phi(y^{\top}v)\right]\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{vzy}\left[\phi(x_{i}^{\top}v)\phi(x_{j}^{\top}v)+\phi(z^{\top}v)\phi(y^{\top}v)-\phi(x_{i}^{\top}v)\phi(y^{\top}v)-\phi(x_{j}^{\top}v)\phi(z^{\top}v)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{zy}\left[\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}\mu_{n}^{2}\frac{\left(x_{i}^{\top}x_{j}\right)^{n}+\left(z^{\top}y\right)^{n}-\left(x_{i}^{\top}y\right)^{n}-\left(x_{j}^{\top}z\right)^{n}}{d^{n}}\right],

where
\mu_{n}
is the
n
-th Hermite coefficient of
\phi
, and
z\sim P_{X}
and
y\sim P_{X}
are two independent random variables, distributed as the data and independent from them. Notice that the previous expansion was possible since
\left\|x_{i}\right\|=\sqrt{d}
for all
i\in[N]
.
As
\phi
is non-linear, there exists
n\geq 2
such that
\mu_{n}^{2}>0
. Let
m
denote one of such
n
’s (i.e,
m
is such that
\mu_{m}^{2}>0
), and define
\phi^{*}
as the function that shares the same Hermite expansion as
\phi
, but with its
m
-th coefficient being
0
. We have

\displaystyle M_{ij}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{zy}\left[\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}\mu_{n}^{2}\frac{\left(x_{i}^{\top}x_{j}\right)^{n}+\left(z^{\top}y\right)^{n}-\left(x_{i}^{\top}y\right)^{n}-\left(x_{j}^{\top}z\right)^{n}}{d^{n}}\right]

(C.51)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{zy}\left[\sum_{n\neq m}\mu_{n}^{2}\frac{\left(x_{i}^{\top}x_{j}\right)^{n}+\left(z^{\top}y\right)^{n}-\left(x_{i}^{\top}y\right)^{n}-\left(x_{j}^{\top}z\right)^{n}}{d^{n}}\right]

\displaystyle+\frac{\mu_{m}^{2}}{d^{m}}\left(\left(x_{i}^{\top}x_{j}\right)^{m}+\mathbb{E}_{zy}\left[\left(z^{\top}y\right)^{m}-\left(x_{i}^{\top}y\right)^{m}-\left(x_{j}^{\top}z\right)^{m}\right]\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{v}\left[\tilde{\phi}^{*}(x_{i}^{\top}v)\tilde{\phi}^{*}(x_{j}^{\top}v)\right]+\frac{\mu_{m}^{2}}{d^{m}}\left(\left(x_{i}^{\top}x_{j}\right)^{m}+\mathbb{E}_{zy}\left[\left(z^{\top}y\right)^{m}-\left(x_{i}^{\top}y\right)^{m}-\left(x_{j}^{\top}z\right)^{m}\right]\right)

\displaystyle=:\mathbb{E}_{v}\left[\tilde{\phi}^{*}(Xv)\tilde{\phi}^{*}(Xv)^{\top}\right]_{ij}+\mu_{m}^{2}\left[M_{m}\right]_{ij}.

From the last equation we deduce that
M\succeq\mu_{m}^{2}M_{m}
, since
\mathbb{E}_{v}\left[\tilde{\phi}^{*}(Xv)\tilde{\phi}^{*}(X^{\top}v)\right]
is PSD. Proving the thesis becomes therefore equivalent to prove that
\lambda_{\rm min}\left(M_{m}\right)=\Omega(1)
.

Let’s consider the case
m=2
. We can write

\displaystyle\left[M_{m}\right]_{ij}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d^{2}}\mathbb{E}_{zy}\left[\left(x_{i}^{\top}x_{j}-z^{\top}x_{j}+x_{i}^{\top}y-z^{\top}y\right)\left(x_{i}^{\top}x_{j}+z^{\top}x_{j}-x_{i}^{\top}y-z^{\top}y\right)\right]

(C.52)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d^{2}}\mathbb{E}_{zy}\left[\left((x_{i}-z)^{\top}(x_{j}+y)\right)\left((x_{i}+z)^{\top}(x_{j}-y)^{\top}\right)\right],

as the cross-terms will be linear in at least one between
y
and
z
, and
\mathbb{E}[x]=\mathbb{E}[z]=0
.

Going back in matrix notation, we can write

M_{m}=\frac{1}{d^{2}}\mathbb{E}_{ZY}\left[\left((X+Z)(X-Y)^{\top}\circ(X-Z)(X+Y)^{\top}\right)\right],

(C.53)

where
Y
and
Z
are
N\times d
matrices respectively containing
N
copies of
y
and
z
in their rows.

We can now expand this term as

\displaystyle M_{m}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d^{2}}\mathbb{E}_{ZY}\left[\left((X-Z)*(X+Z)\right)\left((X+Y)*(X-Y)\right)^{\top}\right]

(C.54)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d^{2}}\mathbb{E}_{Z}\left[(X-Z)*(X+Z)\right]\mathbb{E}_{Y}\left[(X+Y)*(X-Y)\right]^{\top}.

Since
\mathbb{E}[Y]=\mathbb{E}[Z]=0
, the cross-terms cancel out again, giving

\mathbb{E}_{Z}\left[(X-Z)*(X+Z)\right]=X*X-\mathbb{E}_{Z}\left[Z*Z\right]=X*X-\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[X*X\right],

(C.55)

where the last equality is because all the rows of
Z
are distributed accordingly to
P_{X}
. The same equation holds for the term with
Y
in (
C.54
). Thus, we can conclude that

\lambda_{\rm min}\left(M_{m}\right)=\frac{1}{d^{2}}\lambda_{\rm min}\left(TT^{\top}\right),

(C.56)

where we defined
T=X*X-\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[X*X\right]
. As all the rows of
X
are mean-0, independent and Lipschitz concentrated, such that
\left\|x_{i}\right\|=\sqrt{d}
, we can apply Lemma
C.3
with
\alpha=0
to readily get

\lambda_{\rm min}\left(M_{m}\right)=\Omega(1),

(C.57)

with probability at least
1-\exp(-c\log^{2}N)
over
X
.

Let’s now consider the case
m\geq 3
. Since
x
is a sub-Gaussian random variable, we have that
\left\|\frac{x^{\top}y}{\sqrt{d}}\right\|_{\psi_{2}}=C
in the probability space of
x
. Notice that this holds for all
y
, as
\left\|y\right\|_{2}=\sqrt{d}
. Then, its
m
-th momentum its bounded by
m^{m/2}
, up to a multiplicative constant. As
m
doesn’t depend by the scalings of the problem, we can simply write

\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\left(\frac{x^{\top}y}{\sqrt{d}}\right)^{m}\right]=\mathcal{O}\left(1\right).

(C.58)

In the same way, we can prove that

\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\left(\frac{x^{\top}x_{i}}{\sqrt{d}}\right)^{m}\right]=\mathcal{O}\left(1\right),\qquad\mathbb{E}_{y}\left[\left(\frac{y^{\top}x_{j}}{\sqrt{d}}\right)^{m}\right]=\mathcal{O}\left(1\right).

(C.59)

Also, for
i\neq j
we can exploit again the fact that
x_{i}
is sub-Gaussian and independent from
x_{j}
, obtaining

\mathbb{P}\left(\left|x_{i}^{\top}x_{j}\right|>t\sqrt{d}\right)<\exp\left(-ct^{2}\right).

(C.60)

Performing a union bound we also get

\mathbb{P}\left(\mathop{\rm max}\nolimits_{i\neq j}\left|x_{i}^{\top}x_{j}\right|>t\sqrt{d}\right)<N^{2}\exp\left(-ct^{2}\right).

(C.61)

By Gershgorin circle theorem, and setting
t=\log N
, we have that

\displaystyle\lambda_{\rm min}\left(M_{m}\right)

\displaystyle\geq 1-\mathcal{O}\left(d^{-m/2}\right)-\mathop{\rm max}\nolimits_{i}\sum_{j\neq i}\frac{\left|\left(x_{i}^{\top}x_{j}\right)^{m}+\mathbb{E}_{xy}\left[\left(x^{\top}y\right)^{m}-\left(x_{i}^{\top}y\right)^{m}-\left(x_{j}^{\top}x\right)^{m}\right]\right|}{d^{m}}

(C.62)

\displaystyle\geq 1-\mathcal{O}\left(d^{-m/2}\right)-N\mathop{\rm max}\nolimits_{i\neq j}\frac{\left|\left(x_{i}^{\top}x_{j}\right)^{m}+\mathbb{E}_{xy}\left[\left(x^{\top}y\right)^{m}-\left(x_{i}^{\top}y\right)^{m}-\left(x_{j}^{\top}x\right)^{m}\right]\right|}{d^{m}}

\displaystyle\geq 1-\mathcal{O}\left(Nd^{-m/2}\right)-\frac{N\log^{m}N}{d^{m/2}}=1-o(1),

where the third inequality holds with probability at least
1-N^{2}\exp\left(-c\log^{2}N\right)=1-\exp\left(-c_{1}\log^{2}N\right)
, and the last equality is a consequence of Assumption
4
. This concludes the proof.
∎

Lemma C.7
.

We have that

\lambda_{\rm min}\left(\tilde{K}\right)=\Omega(k),

(C.63)

with probability at least
1-\exp(-c\log^{2}N)
over
V
and
X
, where
c
is an absolute constant.

Proof.

The proof follows the same exact path as Lemma
C.5
. The only difference is that now
\psi
(defined right before (

)) will be

Lemma C.8
.

We have that

\left\lVert\tilde{K}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{k}{d}(N+d)\right),

(C.64)

with probability at least
1-k\exp(-cd)
over
V
and
X
.

Proof.

We have that

\tilde{K}=\tilde{\Phi}\tilde{\Phi}^{\top}=\sum_{i=1}^{\lceil k/d\rceil}\tilde{\Phi}^{i}(\tilde{\Phi}^{i})^{\top},

(C.65)

where we define
\tilde{\Phi}^{i}
as the
N\times d
matrix containing the columns of
\tilde{\Phi}
between
(i-1)d+1
and
id
. If
i=\lceil k/d\rceil
, we have that the number of columns is
k-d(\lceil k/d\rceil-1)\leq d
.

Every
\tilde{\Phi}^{i}
has mean-0, i.i.d. rows
\tilde{\phi}(V^{i}x_{j})
(in the probability space of
X
), where
V^{i}
contains the rows of
V
between
(i-1)d+1
and
id
. Notice that, besides corner cases,
V^{i}
is a
d\times d
matrix.Since the
x_{j}
are Lipschitz concentrated, we also have

\left\|\tilde{\phi}(V^{i}x_{j})\right\|_{\psi_{2}}\leq C\left\lVert V^{i}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}=\mathcal{O}\left(1\right),

(C.66)

where the last equality follows from Theorem 4.4.5 of
[
47
]
and from the fact that both dimensions of
V^{i}
are less or equal than
d
, and holds with probability
1-\exp(-cd)
over
V^{i}
.
Conditioning on such event, a straightforward application of Lemma
B.5
gives

\left\lVert\tilde{\Phi}^{i}(\tilde{\Phi}^{i})^{\top}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}=\mathcal{O}\left(N+d\right),

(C.67)

with probability at least
1-\exp(-c_{1}d)
over
X
.

Thus, performing a union bound over the
i
-s, we get

\mathop{\rm max}\nolimits_{i}\left\lVert\Phi^{i}(\tilde{\Phi}^{i})^{\top}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}=\mathcal{O}\left(N+d\right),

(C.68)

with probability at least
1-k\exp(-c_{2}d)
.
Conditioning over such event, we have

\left\lVert\tilde{K}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{\lceil k/d\rceil}\left\lVert\tilde{\Phi}^{i}(\tilde{\Phi}^{i})^{\top}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{k}{d}(N+d)\right),

(C.69)

which gives the thesis.
∎

C.3
Centering and Estimate of
\left\|A(z)\right\|_{F}

In the following section it will be convenient to define
E(z):=\nabla_{z}\phi(Vz)^{\top}\phi(XV^{\top})^{\top}
. Notice that this gives
A(z)=E(z)K^{-1}
. Furthermore, we will use the notation
\tilde{\cdot}
to indicate quantities involving a centering with respect to the random variable
X
. In particular, we indicate

\tilde{\Phi}=\Phi-\mathbb{E}_{X}[\Phi],\qquad\tilde{K}=\tilde{\Phi}\tilde{\Phi}^{\top},\qquad\tilde{E}:=\nabla_{z}\phi(Vz)^{\top}\tilde{\Phi}^{\top},\qquad\tilde{A}=\tilde{E}\tilde{K}^{-1}.

(C.70)

For the sake of compactness, we will not re-introduce any of these quantities in the statements or the proofs of the following lemmas, and we will drop the dependence on
z
of
A
,
\tilde{A}
,
E
and
\tilde{E}
.

Note the results of this section will be applied only when
\nu:=\mathbb{E}_{x_{i}}\left[\phi(Vx_{i})\right]
is not the all-0 vector. Therefore, we will assume
\left\|\nu\right\|_{2}>0
through all the proofs here. Along the section it will be convenient to recall that
\nabla_{z}\phi(Vz)^{\top}=V^{\top}\text{diag}\left(\phi^{\prime}(Vz)\right)
.

Lemma C.9
.

Let
\mathbf{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
denote a vector full of ones. Then,
the kernel matrix
K
can be written as

K=\tilde{K}+\eta\eta^{\top}-\lambda\lambda^{\top},

(C.71)

where

\eta:=\left\|\nu\right\|_{2}\mathbf{1}+\tilde{\Phi}\frac{\nu}{\left\|\nu\right\|_{2}}=\Phi\frac{\nu}{\left\|\nu\right\|_{2}},

(C.72)

\lambda:=\tilde{\Phi}\frac{\nu}{\left\|\nu\right\|_{2}}.

(C.73)

Proof.

Note that
\Phi=\tilde{\Phi}+\mathbf{1}\nu^{\top}
. Then,

\displaystyle K

\displaystyle=\left(\tilde{\Phi}+\mathbf{1}\nu^{\top}\right)\left(\tilde{\Phi}+\mathbf{1}\nu^{\top}\right)^{\top}

(C.74)

\displaystyle=\tilde{\Phi}\tilde{\Phi}^{\top}+\tilde{\Phi}\nu\mathbf{1}^{\top}+\mathbf{1}\nu^{\top}\tilde{\Phi}^{\top}+\left\|\nu\right\|_{2}^{2}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top}

\displaystyle=\tilde{\Phi}\tilde{\Phi}^{\top}+\left(\left\|\nu\right\|_{2}\mathbf{1}+\frac{\tilde{\Phi}\nu}{\left\|\nu\right\|_{2}}\right)\left(\left\|\nu\right\|_{2}\mathbf{1}+\frac{\tilde{\Phi}\nu}{\left\|\nu\right\|_{2}}\right)^{\top}-\frac{\tilde{\Phi}\nu\nu^{\top}\tilde{\Phi}^{\top}}{\left\|\nu\right\|_{2}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\tilde{\Phi}\tilde{\Phi}^{\top}+\eta\eta^{\top}-\lambda\lambda^{\top}.

∎

Lemma C.10
.

We have that

\left|\left\|EK^{-1}\right\|_{F}-\left\|\tilde{E}K^{-1}\right\|_{F}\right|=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\sqrt{N+d}}{d}\right),

(C.75)

with probability at least
1-\exp(-c\log^{2}N)-k\exp(-cd)
over
X
and
V
, where
c
is a numerical constant.

Proof.

We have

E=\tilde{E}+\nabla_{z}\phi(Vz)^{\top}\nu\mathbf{1}^{\top}.

(C.76)

By triangle inequality, we can write

\left|\left\|EK^{-1}\right\|_{F}-\left\|\tilde{E}K^{-1}\right\|_{F}\right|\leq\left\|\nabla_{z}\phi(Vz)^{\top}\nu\mathbf{1}^{\top}K^{-1}\right\|_{F}=\left\|\nabla_{z}\phi(Vz)^{\top}\nu\right\|_{2}\left\|K^{-1}\mathbf{1}\right\|_{2}.

(C.77)

Let’s look at the two factors separately. The first one can be upper bounded as follows

\left\|\nabla_{z}\phi(Vz)^{\top}\nu\right\|_{2}=\left\|V^{\top}\text{diag}\left(\phi^{\prime}(Vz)\right)\nu\right\|_{2}\leq\left\lVert V\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}L\left\|\nu\right\|_{2}=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{\frac{k}{d}}\left\|\nu\right\|_{2}\right),

(C.78)

where the first inequality holds as
\phi
is
L
-Lipschitz, and the second equality holds with probability at least
1-e^{-cd}
due to (
C.183
).
Notice that from (
C.72
) and (
C.73
), we can write

\mathbf{1}=\frac{\eta}{\left\|\nu\right\|_{2}}-\frac{\lambda}{\left\|\nu\right\|_{2}}.

(C.79)

By plugging this in the second factor of (
C.77
) and applying the triangle inequality, we have

\left\|K^{-1}\mathbf{1}\right\|_{2}=\frac{1}{\left\|\nu\right\|_{2}}\left\|K^{-1}\left(\eta-\lambda\right)\right\|_{2}\leq\frac{1}{\left\|\nu\right\|_{2}}\left(\left\|K^{-1}\eta\right\|_{2}+\left\|K^{-1}\lambda\right\|_{2}\right).

(C.80)

Again, let’s control these last two terms separately. Since we can write
\eta=\Phi\nu/\left\|\nu\right\|_{2}
, we get

\left\|K^{-1}\eta\right\|_{2}=\left\|K^{-1}\Phi\nu/\left\|\nu\right\|_{2}\right\|_{2}\leq\left\lVert K^{-1}\Phi\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}=\left\lVert\Phi^{+}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}=\lambda_{\rm min}\left(K\right)^{-1/2}=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{\frac{1}{k}}\right),

(C.81)

where the last equality holds with probability at
1-\exp(-c\log^{2}N)
, due to Lemma
C.5
. We will condition on such event for the rest of the proof.
For the second term, we have

\left\|K^{-1}\lambda\right\|_{2}=\left\|K^{-1}\tilde{\Phi}\nu/\left\|\nu\right\|_{2}\right\|_{2}\leq\left\lVert K^{-1}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\left\lVert\tilde{\Phi}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}=\lambda_{\rm min}\left(K\right)^{-1}\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{\frac{k(N+d)}{d}}\right)=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{\frac{N+d}{kd}}\right),

(C.82)

where the first equality holds due to Lemma
C.8
with probability at least
1-k\exp(-c_{1}d)
. Let’s condition on this event too.
Therefore, putting together (
C.80
), (
C.81
) and (
C.82
), we have

\left\|K^{-1}\mathbf{1}\right\|_{2}=\frac{1}{\left\|\nu\right\|_{2}}\left(\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{\frac{1}{k}}\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{\frac{N+d}{kd}}\right)\right)=\frac{1}{\left\|\nu\right\|_{2}}\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{\frac{N+d}{kd}}\right).

(C.83)

By combining this last expression with (
C.77
)-(
C.78
), we conclude

\left|\left\|EK^{-1}\right\|_{F}-\left\|\tilde{E}K^{-1}\right\|_{F}\right|=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{\frac{k}{d}}\left\|\nu\right\|_{2}\right)\frac{1}{\left\|\nu\right\|_{2}}\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{\frac{N+d}{kd}}\right)=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\sqrt{N+d}}{d}\right),

(C.84)

which gives the thesis.
∎

Lemma C.11
.

We have that

\left|\left\|\tilde{E}K^{-1}\right\|_{F}-\left\|\tilde{E}\left(K+\lambda\lambda^{\top}\right)^{-1}\right\|_{F}\right|=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\sqrt{N+d}}{d}\right),

(C.85)

with probability at least
1-\exp(-c\log^{2}N)-k\exp(-cd)
over
X
and
V
, where
c
is a numerical constant.

Proof.

A direct application of the Sherman-Morrison formula gives

\left(K+\lambda\lambda^{\top}\right)^{-1}=K^{-1}-\frac{K^{-1}\lambda\lambda^{\top}K^{-1}}{1+\lambda^{\top}K^{-1}\lambda}.

(C.86)

By triangle inequality we know that
\left|\left\|a\right\|_{2}-\left\|b\right\|_{2}\right|\leq\left\|a-b\right\|_{2}
. This implies

\left|\left\|\tilde{E}K^{-1}\right\|_{F}-\left\|\tilde{E}\left(K+\lambda\lambda^{\top}\right)^{-1}\right\|_{F}\right|\leq\left\|\tilde{E}K^{-1}-\tilde{E}\left(K+\lambda\lambda^{\top}\right)^{-1}\right\|_{F}=\left\|\tilde{E}\frac{K^{-1}\lambda\lambda^{\top}K^{-1}}{1+\lambda^{\top}K^{-1}\lambda}\right\|_{F},

(C.87)

where the last equality is a consequence of (
C.86
).
This term can be bounded as

\left\|\tilde{E}\frac{K^{-1}\lambda\lambda^{\top}K^{-1}}{1+\lambda^{\top}K^{-1}\lambda}\right\|_{F}\leq\left\lVert\tilde{E}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\left\|\frac{K^{-1}\lambda\lambda^{\top}K^{-1}}{1+\lambda^{\top}K^{-1}\lambda}\right\|_{F}=\left\lVert\tilde{E}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\frac{\lambda^{\top}K^{-2}\lambda}{1+\lambda^{\top}K^{-1}\lambda}.

(C.88)

The first factor can be bounded as follows

\left\lVert\tilde{E}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}=\left\lVert\nabla_{z}\phi(Vz)^{\top}\tilde{\Phi}^{\top}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\leq\left\lVert V^{\top}\text{diag}\left(\phi^{\prime}(Vz)\right)\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\left\lVert\tilde{\Phi}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{\frac{k}{d}}\right)\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{\frac{k(N+d)}{d}}\right)=\mathcal{O}\left(k\frac{\sqrt{N+d}}{d}\right),

(C.89)

with probability at least
1-k\exp(-cd)
. Here, we used the fact that
\phi
is Lipschitz, and we applied Lemma
C.8
and Equation (
C.183
). Furthermore, since
\left\lVert M\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}M-M^{2}
is PSD for every symmetric PSD matrix
M
, the following relation holds

\lambda^{\top}K^{-2}\lambda\leq\left\lVert K^{-1}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\lambda^{\top}K^{-1}\lambda.

(C.90)

Thus,

\frac{\lambda^{\top}K^{-2}\lambda}{1+\lambda^{\top}K^{-1}\lambda}\leq\left\lVert K^{-1}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\frac{\lambda^{\top}K^{-1}\lambda}{1+\lambda^{\top}K^{-1}\lambda}\leq\left\lVert K^{-1}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}=\frac{1}{\lambda_{\rm min}\left(K\right)}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{k}\right),

(C.91)

where the last equality holds with probability at least
1-\exp(-c\log^{2}N)
, because of Lemma
C.5
.
Plugging the last equation in (
C.88
) together with (
C.89
), and comparing with (
C.87
), gives the desired result.
∎

Lemma C.12
.

We have that

\left|\left\|\tilde{E}\left(\tilde{K}+\eta\eta^{\top}\right)^{-1}\right\|_{F}-\left\|\tilde{E}\tilde{K}^{-1}\right\|_{F}\right|=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\sqrt{N+d}}{d}\right),

(C.92)

with probability at least
1-\exp(-c\log^{2}N)-k\exp(-cd)
over
X
and
V
, where
c
is a numerical constant.

Proof.

An application of the Sherman-Morrison formula gives

\left(\tilde{K}+\eta\eta^{\top}\right)^{-1}=\tilde{K}^{-1}-\frac{\tilde{K}^{-1}\eta\eta^{\top}\tilde{K}^{-1}}{1+\eta^{\top}\tilde{K}^{-1}\eta}.

(C.93)

This implies

\left|\left\|\tilde{E}\left(\tilde{K}+\eta\eta^{\top}\right)^{-1}\right\|_{F}-\left\|\tilde{E}\tilde{K}^{-1}\right\|_{F}\right|\leq\left\|\tilde{E}\left(\tilde{K}+\eta\eta^{\top}\right)^{-1}-\tilde{E}\tilde{K}^{-1}\right\|_{F}=\left\|\tilde{E}\frac{\tilde{K}^{-1}\eta\eta^{\top}\tilde{K}^{-1}}{1+\eta^{\top}\tilde{K}^{-1}\eta}\right\|_{F},

(C.94)

where the last equality is a consequence of (
C.93
).
This term can be bounded as

\left\|\tilde{E}\frac{\tilde{K}^{-1}\eta\eta^{\top}\tilde{K}^{-1}}{1+\eta^{\top}\tilde{K}^{-1}\eta}\right\|_{F}\leq\left\lVert\tilde{E}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\left\|\frac{\tilde{K}^{-1}\eta\eta^{\top}\tilde{K}^{-1}}{1+\eta^{\top}\tilde{K}^{-1}\eta}\right\|_{F}=\left\lVert\tilde{E}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\frac{\eta^{\top}\tilde{K}^{-2}\eta}{1+\eta^{\top}\tilde{K}^{-1}\eta}.

(C.95)

The first factor can be bounded as in (
C.89
), in Lemma
C.11
, giving
\left\lVert\tilde{E}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}=\mathcal{O}\left(k\frac{\sqrt{N+d}}{d}\right)
with probability at least
1-k\exp(-cd)
.
Similarly to (
C.90
), we can write

\eta^{\top}\tilde{K}^{-2}\eta\leq\left\lVert\tilde{K}^{-1}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\eta^{\top}\tilde{K}^{-1}\eta.

(C.96)

Thus,

\frac{\eta^{\top}\tilde{K}^{-2}\eta}{1+\eta^{\top}\tilde{K}^{-1}\eta}\leq\left\lVert\tilde{K}^{-1}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\frac{\eta^{\top}\tilde{K}^{-1}\eta}{1+\eta^{\top}\tilde{K}^{-1}\eta}\leq\left\lVert\tilde{K}^{-1}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}=\frac{1}{\lambda_{\rm min}\left(\tilde{K}\right)}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{k}\right),

(C.97)

where the last equality holds with probability at least
1-\exp(-c\log^{2}N)
, because of Lemma
C.7
.
Plugging the last equation in (
C.95
) together with the bound on
\left\lVert\tilde{E}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}
, and comparing with (
C.94
), gives the desired result.
∎

Lemma C.13
.

We have that

\left|\left\|A\right\|_{F}-\left\|\tilde{A}\right\|_{F}\right|=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\sqrt{N+d}}{d}\right),

(C.99)

\displaystyle+\left|\left\|\tilde{E}K^{-1}\right\|_{F}-\left\|\tilde{E}\left(K+\lambda\lambda^{\top}\right)^{-1}\right\|_{F}\right|

∎

.

.

For the other bound of the first equation, we have

and

.

The second equation directly follows from Lemma

.

C.4
Estimate of
\left\|\mathcal{I}_{\textup{RF}}(z)\right\|_{F}

The goal of this sub-Section is to estimate the value of the Frobenius norm of the Interaction Matrix
\left\|\mathcal{I}_{\textup{RF}}\right\|_{F}
, defined in (
4.11
). To do so, we will estimate the squared
\ell_{2}
norms of its columns. In particular, we define

\mathcal{I}_{x}:=\left\|\nabla_{z}\phi(Vz)^{\top}\tilde{\phi}(Vx)\right\|_{2}.

(C.102)

Thus, we have
\left\|\mathcal{I}_{\textup{RF}}\right\|_{F}^{2}=\sum_{i}\mathcal{I}^{2}_{x_{i}}
. In this section, with
x\sim P_{X}
we refer to a generic input data, where we drop the index for compactness. Also, we will assume
z\sim P_{X}
, independent from the training set.

Recalling that

\nabla_{z}\phi(Vz)^{\top}=V^{\top}\text{diag}\left(\phi^{\prime}(Vz)\right),

(C.103)

we have

\mathcal{I}_{x}=\left\|V^{\top}\text{diag}\left(\phi^{\prime}(Vz)\right)\tilde{\phi}(Vx)\right\|_{2}.

(C.104)

Along the proof, the following shorthands and notations will be useful

\mathcal{X}_{-jl}=\sum_{m\neq j}V_{lm}x_{m},\qquad\mathcal{X}_{l}=\sum_{m=1}^{d}V_{lm}x_{m},

(C.105)

\mathcal{Z}_{-jl}=\sum_{m\neq j}V_{lm}z_{m},\qquad\mathcal{Z}_{l}=\sum_{m=1}^{d}V_{lm}z_{m},

(C.106)

I_{j}=\sum_{l=1}^{k}V_{lj}\phi^{\prime}\left(\mathcal{Z}_{l}\right)\tilde{\phi}\left(\mathcal{X}_{l}\right),

(C.107)

which imply

\mathcal{I}^{2}_{x}=\sum_{j=1}^{d}I_{j}^{2}.

(C.108)

It will also be convenient to use the following notation

B_{-jl}:=\left(\phi^{\prime\prime}\left(\mathcal{Z}_{-jl}\right)\tilde{\phi}\left(\mathcal{X}_{-jl}\right)z_{j}+\phi^{\prime}\left(\mathcal{Z}_{-jl}\right)\phi^{\prime}\left(\mathcal{X}_{-jl}\right)x_{j}\right),

(C.109)

B_{jl}:=\left(\phi^{\prime\prime}\left(\mathcal{Z}_{l}\right)\tilde{\phi}\left(\mathcal{X}_{l}\right)z_{j}+\phi^{\prime}\left(\mathcal{Z}_{l}\right)\phi^{\prime}\left(\mathcal{X}_{l}\right)x_{j}\right).

(C.110)

For compactness, we will not necessarily re-introduce such quantities in the statements or the proofs of the lemmas in this section.

Lemma C.14
.

Indicating with
c
a numerical constant, the following statements hold

\mathop{\rm max}\nolimits_{l}\left|\tilde{\phi}(\mathcal{X}_{l})\right|=\mathcal{O}\left(\log k\right),

(C.111)

with probability at least
1-\exp(-c\log^{2}k)
, over
x
and
V
;

\mathop{\rm max}\nolimits_{l}\left|\tilde{\phi}(\mathcal{X}_{-jl})\right|=\mathcal{O}\left(\log k\right),

(C.112)

with probability at least
1-\exp(-c\log^{2}k)
, over
x
and
\{V_{li}\}_{i\neq j,1\leq l\leq k}
;

\left|\sum_{l=1}^{k}V_{lj}\phi^{\prime}\left(\mathcal{Z}_{-jl}\right)\tilde{\phi}\left(\mathcal{X}_{-jl}\right)\right|=o\left(\frac{k}{d}\right),

(C.113)

with probability at least
1-\exp(-c\log^{2}k)
, over
x
and
V
.

Proof.

For the first result, as
\phi
is Lipschitz and centered with respect to
x
, its sub-Gaussian norm (in the probability space of
x
) is
\mathcal{O}\left(\left\|V_{l:}\right\|_{2}\right)=\mathcal{O}\left(1\right)
, where the last equality holds with probability at least
1-e^{-c_{0}d}
over
V_{l:}
, because of Theorem 3.1.1 in
[
47
]
. In particular, with a union bound over
V_{l:}
, we have
\mathcal{O}\left(\mathop{\rm max}\nolimits_{l}\left\|V_{l:}\right\|_{2}\right)=\mathcal{O}\left(1\right)
with probability at least
1-ke^{-c_{0}d}\geq 1-e^{-c_{1}d}
, where the last inequality is justified by Assumption
4
. Thus, by Lemma
B.2
,
\mathop{\rm max}\nolimits_{l}\left|\tilde{\phi}(\mathcal{X}_{l})\right|=\mathcal{O}\left(\log k\right)
, with probability at least
1-\exp(-c_{1}d)-\exp(-c_{2}\log^{2}k)\geq 1-\exp(-c_{3}\log^{2}k)
, over
x
and
V
.

Notice that, with the same argument, we can prove that
\mathop{\rm max}\nolimits_{l}\left|\tilde{\phi}(\mathcal{X}_{-jl})\right|=\mathcal{O}\left(\log k\right)
, with probability at least
1-\exp(-c_{4}\log^{2}k)
, over
x
and
\{V_{li}\}_{i\neq j,1\leq l\leq k}
. We will condition on such event for the rest of the proof.

For the last statement, we have

\sum_{l=1}^{k}V_{lj}\phi^{\prime}\left(\mathcal{Z}_{-jl}\right)\tilde{\phi}\left(\mathcal{X}_{-jl}\right)=:V_{:j}^{\top}u,

(C.114)

where
u
is a vector independent from
V_{:j}
such that

\left\|u\right\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{k}L\mathop{\rm max}\nolimits_{l}\left|\tilde{\phi}\left(\mathcal{X}_{-jl}\right)\right|=\mathcal{O}\left(\log(k)\sqrt{k}\right).

(C.115)

Since
\left\|V_{:j}\right\|_{\psi_{2}}\leq C/\sqrt{d}
, we have

\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\sum_{l=1}^{k}V_{lj}\phi^{\prime}\left(\mathcal{Z}_{-jl}\right)\tilde{\phi}\left(\mathcal{X}_{-jl}\right)\right|>\frac{\sqrt{d}}{\sqrt{k}/\log^{2}k}\frac{k}{d}\right)<\exp(-c_{5}\log^{2}k),

(C.116)

where the probability is intended over
V_{:j}
. Thus, using Assumption
3
, we readily get the desired result.
∎

Lemma C.15
.

We have that

\left\|\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\phi^{\prime}\left(\mathcal{X}_{-jl}\right)x_{j}\right]\right\|_{\psi_{2}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\right),

(C.117)

where the norm is intended over the probability space of
\{V_{li}\}_{i\neq j}
.

Proof.

Let’s define an auxiliary random variable
\rho\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1/d)
, independent from any other random variable in the problem. Let’s also define
V^{\prime}_{l:}
as the copy of
V_{l:}
where we replaced the
j
-th component with
\rho
. Notice that
V^{\prime}_{l:}
is a Gaussian vector with covariance
I/d
. Let’s also define
\mathcal{X}^{\prime}_{l}
in the same way, i.e., replacing
V_{lj}
with
\rho
in its expression. All the tail norms
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\psi_{r}}
in the proof will be referred to the probability space of
V^{\prime}_{l:}
.

Since
\phi^{\prime}
is a Lipschitz function, we can write

\left|\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\phi^{\prime}\left(\mathcal{X}_{-jl}\right)x_{j}\right]-\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\phi^{\prime}\left(\mathcal{X}^{\prime}_{l}\right)x_{j}\right]\right|\leq L_{1}\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[|\rho|x_{j}^{2}\right]\leq C|\rho|,

(C.118)

where we used the fact that
x_{j}
is sub-Gaussian in the last inequality, and where
C
is a numerical constant.
Let’s now look at the function

\varphi(v):=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\phi^{\prime}\left(v^{\top}x\right)x_{j}\right].

(C.119)

We have

\displaystyle\left|\varphi(v+v^{\prime})-\varphi(v)\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\left(\phi^{\prime}\left((v+v^{\prime})^{\top}x\right)-\phi^{\prime}\left(v^{\top}x\right)\right)x_{j}\right]\right|

(C.120)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\left|\left(\phi^{\prime}\left((v+v^{\prime})^{\top}x\right)-\phi^{\prime}\left(v^{\top}x\right)\right)\right||x_{j}|\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[L_{1}|v^{\prime\top}x||x_{j}|\right].

Since
x
is sub-Gaussian (and therefore also
x_{j}
is) we have that
|v^{\prime\top}x||x_{j}|
is sub-exponential with norm (with respect to the probability space of
x
) upper bounded by
C_{1}\left\|v^{\prime}\right\|_{2}
. Then, we have

\left|\varphi(v+v^{\prime})-\varphi(v)\right|\leq\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[L_{1}|v^{\prime\top}x||x_{j}|\right]\leq C_{2}\left\|v^{\prime}\right\|_{2}.

(C.121)

This implies that
\left\|\varphi\right\|_{\text{Lip}}\leq C_{2}
.

As
V^{\prime}_{l:}
is Gaussian (and hence Lipschitz concentrated) with covariance
I/d
, we can write

\left\|\varphi(V^{\prime}_{l:})-\mathbb{E}_{V^{\prime}_{l:}}\left[\varphi(V^{\prime}_{l:})\right]\right\|_{\psi_{2}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\right).

(C.122)

Furthermore,

\mathbb{E}_{V^{\prime}_{l:}}\left[\varphi(V^{\prime}_{l:})\right]=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[x_{j}\mathbb{E}_{V^{\prime}_{l:}}\left[\phi^{\prime}\left({V^{\prime}_{l:}}^{\top}x\right)\right]\right]=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[x_{j}\right]\mathbb{E}_{\rho^{\prime}}\left[\phi^{\prime}\left(\rho^{\prime}\right)\right]=0,

(C.123)

where
\rho^{\prime}
is a standard Gaussian distribution independent of
x
. Note that the second step holds since
\left\|x\right\|_{2}=\sqrt{d}
, and the last step is justified by
\mathbb{E}[x]=0
.

This last equation implies

\displaystyle\left\|\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\phi^{\prime}\left(\mathcal{X}_{-jl}\right)x_{j}\right]\right\|_{\psi_{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\phi^{\prime}\left(\mathcal{X}_{-jl}\right)x_{j}\right]-\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\phi^{\prime}\left(\mathcal{X}^{\prime}_{l}\right)x_{j}\right]\right\|_{\psi_{2}}+\left\|\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\phi^{\prime}\left(\mathcal{X}^{\prime}_{l}\right)x_{j}\right]\right\|_{\psi_{2}}

(C.124)

\displaystyle\leq C\left\|\rho\right\|_{\psi_{2}}+\left\|\varphi(V^{\prime}_{l:})\right\|_{\psi_{2}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\right),

where the first term in the last line is justified by (
C.118
). In fact, the random variable on the LHS is (with probability one) upper bounded by
C|\rho|
. Then, the bound subsists also between their tail norms.

Thus,

\mathbb{P}_{\{V_{li}\}_{i\neq j}}\left(\left|\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\phi^{\prime}\left(\mathcal{X}_{-jl}\right)x_{j}\right]\right|>t\right)=\mathbb{P}_{V^{\prime}_{l:}}\left(\left|\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\phi^{\prime}\left(\mathcal{X}_{-jl}\right)x_{j}\right]\right|>t\right)<2\exp\left(-cd\,t^{2}\right),

(C.125)

where with
\mathbb{P}_{\{V_{li}\}_{i\neq j}}
we refer to the probability space of
\{V_{li}\}_{i\neq j}
. Note that the first equality is true as the statement in the probability does not depend on the value of
\rho
. From this, we readily get the thesis.
∎

Lemma C.16
.

We have that

\left|I_{j}-\sum_{l}V_{lj}^{2}\left(\phi^{\prime\prime}\left(\mathcal{Z}_{-jl}\right)\tilde{\phi}\left(\mathcal{X}_{-jl}\right)z_{j}+\phi^{\prime}\left(\mathcal{Z}_{-jl}\right)\phi^{\prime}\left(\mathcal{X}_{-jl}\right)x_{j}\right)\right|=o\left(\frac{k}{d}\right).

(C.126)

with probability at least
1-\exp(-c\log^{2}k)
over
x
,
z
and
V
, where
c
is an absolute constant.

Proof.

Exploiting Taylor’s Theorem, and the fact that both
\phi^{\prime}
and
\phi^{\prime\prime}
are Lipschitz, we can write

\phi^{\prime}\left(\mathcal{Z}_{l}\right)=\phi^{\prime}\left(\mathcal{Z}_{-jl}\right)+\phi^{\prime\prime}\left(\mathcal{Z}_{-jl}\right)V_{lj}z_{j}+a_{1lj},

(C.127)

\tilde{\phi}\left(\mathcal{X}_{l}\right)=\phi\left(\mathcal{X}_{-jl}\right)+\phi^{\prime}\left(\mathcal{X}_{-jl}\right)V_{lj}x_{j}+a_{2lj}-\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\phi\left(\mathcal{X}_{-jl}\right)+\phi^{\prime}\left(\mathcal{X}_{-jl}\right)V_{lj}x_{j}+a_{2lj}\right],

(C.128)

where
|a_{1lj}|=\mathcal{O}\left(V_{lj}^{2}z_{j}^{2}\right)
and
|a_{2lj}|=\mathcal{O}\left(V_{lj}^{2}x_{j}^{2}\right)
. In the previous two equations, we expanded
\phi^{\prime}
around
\mathcal{Z}_{-jl}
and
\phi
around
\mathcal{X}_{-jl}
. For the rest of the proof (except from when expectations with respect to
x
are taken) we condition on the events that both
|x_{j}|
and
|z_{j}|
are
\mathcal{O}\left(\log k\right)
. As they are sub-Gaussian, this happens with probability at least
1-e^{-c_{1}\log^{2}k}
over
x
and
z
.

Invoking Lemma
B.6
, and setting
t=k/d^{1/\alpha}
, we can write (for
\alpha\in(0,2]
)

\sum_{l=1}^{k}|V_{lj}|^{2/\alpha}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{k}{d^{1/\alpha}}\right),

(C.129)

with probability at least
1-\exp(-c_{2}k^{\alpha/2}/\log k)
. This is true since
\mathbb{E}_{\rho}\left[\rho^{2/\alpha}\right]=C_{\alpha}
, where
\rho
is a standard Gaussian random variable. Thus, with probability at least
1-\exp(-c_{3}k^{1/5}/\log k)
over
V
, we jointly have

\sum_{l=1}^{k}|V_{lj}|^{2}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{k}{d}\right),\qquad\sum_{l=1}^{k}|V_{lj}|^{3}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{k}{d^{3/2}}\right),\qquad\sum_{l=1}^{k}|V_{lj}|^{5}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{k}{d^{5/2}}\right).

(C.130)

We will condition on these events too. It will also be convenient to condition on the high probability events described by Lemma
C.14
. Thus, our discussion is now restricted to a probability space over
x
,
z
and
V
of measure at least
1-e^{-c_{4}\log^{2}k}-e^{-c_{5}d}
.

We are now ready to estimate the cross terms of the product
V_{lj}\phi^{\prime}\left(\mathcal{Z}_{l}\right)\tilde{\phi}\left(\mathcal{X}_{l}\right)
, exploiting the expansions in (
C.127
) and (
C.128
). Let’s control the sums over
l
of all of them separately.

(a)

\phi^{\prime}\left(\mathcal{Z}_{l}\right)\left(a_{2lj}-\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[a_{2lj}\right]\right)

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{l=1}^{k}V_{lj}\phi^{\prime}\left(\mathcal{Z}_{l}\right)\left(a_{2lj}-\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[a_{2lj}\right]\right)\right|\leq C_{1}\sum_{l=1}^{k}|V_{lj}|L\left(|a_{2lj}|+\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[|a_{2lj}|\right]\right)

(C.131)

\displaystyle\leq C_{2}\left(x_{j}^{2}+1\right)\sum_{l=1}^{k}|V_{lj}|^{3}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{k\log^{2}k}{d^{3/2}}\right)=o\left(\frac{k}{d}\right),

where the second inequality is justified by the fact that
x_{j}
is sub-Gaussian, and the last equality by Assumption
4
.

(b)

\tilde{\phi}\left(\mathcal{X}_{l}\right)a_{1jl}

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{l=1}^{k}V_{lj}\tilde{\phi}\left(\mathcal{X}_{l}\right)a_{1jl}\right|\leq C_{3}z_{j}^{2}\sum_{l=1}^{k}|V_{lj}|^{3}\left|\tilde{\phi}\left(\mathcal{X}_{l}\right)\right|\leq C_{3}z_{j}^{2}\mathop{\rm max}\nolimits_{l}\left|\tilde{\phi}\left(\mathcal{X}_{l}\right)\right|\sum_{l=1}^{k}|V_{lj}|^{3}

(d)

\phi^{\prime}\left(\mathcal{Z}_{-jl}\right)\tilde{\phi}\left(\mathcal{X}_{-jl}\right)

\phi^{\prime\prime}\left(\mathcal{Z}_{-jl}\right)V_{lj}z_{j}\phi^{\prime}\left(\mathcal{X}_{-jl}\right)V_{lj}x_{j}

(C.136)

Then, we are left only with two cross terms, and we can write

I_{j}=\sum_{l}V_{lj}^{2}\left(\phi^{\prime\prime}\left(\mathcal{Z}_{-jl}\right)\tilde{\phi}\left(\mathcal{X}_{-jl}\right)z_{j}+\phi^{\prime}\left(\mathcal{Z}_{-jl}\right)\phi^{\prime}\left(\mathcal{X}_{-jl}\right)x_{j}\right)+\textup{rest},

(C.137)

where

\left|I_{j}-\sum_{l}V_{lj}^{2}\left(\phi^{\prime\prime}\left(\mathcal{Z}_{-jl}\right)\tilde{\phi}\left(\mathcal{X}_{-jl}\right)z_{j}+\phi^{\prime}\left(\mathcal{Z}_{-jl}\right)\phi^{\prime}\left(\mathcal{X}_{-jl}\right)x_{j}\right)\right|=o\left(\frac{k}{d}\right)

x

Lemma C.17
.

We have that

\left|\sum_{l=1}^{k}V_{lj}^{2}B_{-jl}-\frac{1}{d}\sum_{l=1}^{k}B_{-jl}\right|=o\left(\frac{k}{d}\right),

(C.139)

with probability at least
1-\exp(-c\log^{2}k)
over
x
,
z
and
V
, where
c
is an absolute constant.

Proof.

We have

\sum_{l=1}^{k}V_{lj}^{2}B_{-jl}=\sum_{l=1}^{k}\left(V_{lj}^{2}-\mathbb{E}_{V}\left[V_{lj}^{2}\right]\right)B_{-jl}+\frac{1}{d}\sum_{l=1}^{k}B_{-jl},

(C.140)

since
\mathbb{E}_{V}\left[V_{lj}^{2}\right]=1/d
for all

.

We call
v_{l}:=V_{lj}^{2}-\mathbb{E}_{V}\left[V_{lj}^{2}\right]
. Then,
v=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{k})
is a mean-0 vector with independent and sub-exponential entries with norm
\mathcal{O}\left(1/d\right)
. This implies
\left\|v\right\|_{\psi_{1}}=\mathcal{O}\left(1/d\right)
. Thus,

\mathbb{P}_{v}\left(|v^{\top}u|>t\right)<2\exp\left(-c\frac{dt}{\left\|u\right\|_{2}}\right),

(C.141)

for every vector
u
independent from
v
. If we define

, we have

\left\|u\right\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{k}\left(\mathop{\rm max}\nolimits_{l}|B_{-jl}|\right)\leq\sqrt{k}\left(L^{2}|x_{j}|+L_{1}|z_{j}|\mathop{\rm max}\nolimits_{l}\left|\tilde{\phi}(\mathcal{X}_{-jl})\right|\right)=\mathcal{O}\left(\log^{2}(k)\sqrt{k}\right),

(C.142)

where the last equality comes from the fact that
x_{j}
and
z_{j}
are sub-Gaussian, and from the second statement in Lemma
C.14
. This holds with probability at least
1-e^{-c_{1}d}-e^{-c_{2}\log^{2}k}
over
x
,
z
and
\{V_{li}\}_{i\neq j,1\leq l\leq k}
, and we will condition on this event.

Then, merging this last result with (
C.141

\mathbb{P}_{v}\left(|v^{\top}u|>\frac{\log^{4}k\sqrt{k}}{d}\right)<2\exp\left(-c_{3}\log^{2}k\right).

(C.143)

Performing a union bound gives the thesis.

Lemma C.18

Lemma C.20
.

Indicating with
\rho
a standard Gaussian random variable, we have that

\left|\mathbb{E}_{V}[B_{j1}]-x_{j}\mathbb{E}_{\rho}^{2}[\phi^{\prime}(\rho)]\right|=o(1),

(C.158)

with probability at least
1-\exp(-c\log^{2}k)
over
x
and
z
.

Proof.

Let’s define the vector
z^{\prime}
as follows

z^{\prime}=\frac{\sqrt{d}\left(I-\frac{xx^{\top}}{d}\right)z}{\left\|\left(I-\frac{xx^{\top}}{d}\right)z\right\|_{2}}.

(C.159)

Notice that, by construction,
x^{\top}z^{\prime}=0
, and
\left\|z^{\prime}\right\|_{2}=\sqrt{d}
. Also, consider a vector
y
orthogonal to both
z
and
x
. Then, a fast computation returns
y^{\top}z^{\prime}=0
. This means that
z^{\prime}
is the vector on the
\sqrt{d}
-sphere, lying on the same plane of
z
and
x
, orthogonal to
x
. Thus, we can easily compute

\frac{\left|z^{\top}z^{\prime}\right|}{d}=\sqrt{1-\left(\frac{x^{\top}z}{d}\right)^{2}}\geq 1-\left(\frac{x^{\top}z}{d}\right)^{2},

(C.160)

where the last inequality derives from
\sqrt{1-a}\geq 1-a
for

. Then,

\left\|z-z^{\prime}\right\|_{2}^{2}=\left\|z\right\|_{2}^{2}+\left\|z\right\|_{2}^{2}-2z^{\top}z^{\prime}\leq 2d\left(1-\left(1-\left(\frac{x^{\top}z}{d}\right)^{2}\right)\right)=2\frac{\left(x^{\top}z\right)^{2}}{d}.

(C.161)

As
x
and
z
are both sub-Gaussian, mean-0 vectors, with
\ell_{2}
norm equal to
\sqrt{d}
, we have that

\mathbb{P}\left(\left\|z-z^{\prime}\right\|_{2}>t\right)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(|x^{\top}z|>\sqrt{d}t/\sqrt{2}\right)<\exp(-ct^{2}),

(C.162)

where
c
is an absolute constant. Here the probability is referred to the space of
x
, for a fixed
z
. Thus,
\left\|z-z^{\prime}\right\|_{2}

Let’s now introduce the shorthands
v:=V_{1j}
and
\mathcal{Z}_{1}^{\prime}=v^{\top}z^{\prime}
. We have

\left|\mathbb{E}_{v}\left[\phi^{\prime\prime}(\mathcal{Z}_{1})\phi(\mathcal{X}_{1})\right]-\mathbb{E}_{v}\left[\phi^{\prime\prime}(\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}_{1})\phi(\mathcal{X}_{1})\right]\right|\leq L_{2}\mathbb{E}_{v}\left[|v^{\top}(z-z^{\prime})||\phi(\mathcal{X}_{1})|\right].

(C.163)

Since
\phi
is Lipschitz,
\phi(\mathcal{X}_{1})
is sub-Gaussian with respect to the probability space of
v
. Since
z
and
z^{\prime}
are independent from
v
, we deduce that

\left\|v^{\top}(z-z^{\prime})\right\|_{\psi_{2}}\leq\left\|z-z^{\prime}\right\|_{2}\frac{C}{\sqrt{d}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\log k}{\sqrt{d}}\right),

(C.164)

with probability at least
1-\exp(-c\log^{2}k)
over
x
. If we condition on such event, this implies that the expectation in (
C.163
) can be bounded as

\mathbb{E}_{v}\left[|v^{\top}(z-z^{\prime})||\phi(\mathcal{X}_{1})|\right]=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\log k}{\sqrt{d}}\right).

(C.165)

This is true since we are computing the first moment of a sub-exponential random variable.

A similar computation gives

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}_{v}\left[\phi^{\prime\prime}(\mathcal{Z}_{1})\mathbb{E}_{x}[\phi(\mathcal{X}_{1})]\right]-\mathbb{E}_{vx}\left[\phi^{\prime\prime}(\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}_{1})\phi(\mathcal{X}_{1})\right]\right|

\displaystyle\leq L_{2}\mathbb{E}_{x}\mathbb{E}_{v}\left[|v^{\top}(z-z^{\prime})||\phi(\mathcal{X}_{1})|\right]\leq C\frac{\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\left\|z-z^{\prime}\right\|_{2}\right]}{\sqrt{d}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\right),

C.162

Notice that this, together with the previous argument between (
C.163
) and (
C.165

\left|\mathbb{E}_{v}\left[\phi^{\prime\prime}(\mathcal{Z}_{1})\tilde{\phi}(\mathcal{X}_{1})\right]-\left(\mathbb{E}_{v}\left[\phi^{\prime\prime}(\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}_{1})\phi(\mathcal{X}_{1})\right]-\mathbb{E}_{vx}\left[\phi^{\prime\prime}(\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}_{1})\phi(\mathcal{X}_{1})\right]\right)\right|=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\log k}{d}\right).

(C.167)

With an equivalent argument of the one between (
C.163
) and (
C.165

\left|\mathbb{E}_{v}\left[\phi^{\prime}(\mathcal{Z}_{1})\phi^{\prime}(\mathcal{X}_{1})\right]-\mathbb{E}_{v}\left[\phi^{\prime}(\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}_{1})\phi^{\prime}(\mathcal{X}_{1})\right]\right|=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\log k}{\sqrt{d}}\right),

(C.168)

with probability at least
1-\exp(-c\log^{2}k)
over
x

C.169

Proof of Theorem
4
.

Let’s focus on
\mathcal{I}^{2}_{x_{1}}=\sum_{j=1}^{d}I_{j}^{2}
, where with a slight abuse of notation we now refer to
I_{j}
as the quantity defined in (
C.107
), with the variable
x_{1}
instead of
x

x_{1}\sim P_{X}

We have, by triangle inequality,

\displaystyle\left|I_{j}-x_{j}\mathbb{E}_{\rho}^{2}[\phi^{\prime}(\rho)]\frac{k}{d}\right|\leq\left|I_{j}-\sum_{l=1}^{k}V_{lj}^{2}B_{-jl}\right|+\left|\sum_{l=1}^{k}V_{lj}^{2}B_{-jl}-\frac{1}{d}\sum_{l=1}^{k}B_{-jl}\right|

(C.175)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{d}\left|\sum_{l=1}^{k}B_{-jl}-\sum_{l=1}^{k}B_{jl}\right|+\frac{1}{d}\left|\sum_{l=1}^{k}B_{jl}-k\,\mathbb{E}_{V}[B_{j1}]\right|+\frac{k}{d}\left|\mathbb{E}_{V}[B_{j1}]-x_{j}\mathbb{E}_{\rho}^{2}[\phi^{\prime}(\rho)]\right|

\displaystyle=o\left(\frac{k}{d}\right)+o\left(\frac{k}{d}\right)+\frac{1}{d}o\left(k\right)+\frac{1}{d}o\left(k\right)+\frac{k}{d}o\left(1\right)=o\left(\frac{k}{d}\right),

where the five terms in the third line are justified respectively by Lemmas
C.16
,
C.17
,
C.18
,
C.19
and
C.20
, and jointly hold with probability at least
1-\exp(-c_{2}\log^{2}k)
over
x_{1}
and
z
and
V
.

This holds for every
1\leq j\leq d
with probability at least
1-d\exp(-c_{2}\log^{2}k)\geq 1-\exp(-c_{3}\log^{2}k)
. Thus, we can write

I_{x_{1}}\geq\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{d}\left(|x_{j}|\mathbb{E}_{\rho}^{2}[\phi^{\prime}(\rho)]\frac{k}{d}-o\left(\frac{k}{d}\right)\right)^{2}}\geq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{\rho}^{4}[\phi^{\prime}(\rho)]\frac{k^{2}}{d^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{d}x_{j}^{2}-o\left(\frac{k^{2}}{d}\right)-o\left(\frac{k^{2}\sum_{j}|x_{j}|}{d^{2}}\right)}.

(C.176)

By Cauchy-Schwartz, we have that
\sum_{j}|x_{j}|\leq\sqrt{d\sum_{j}x_{j}^{2}}=d
. If
\mathbb{E}_{\rho}[\phi^{\prime}(\rho)]\neq 0
, we have

I_{x_{1}}\geq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{\rho}^{4}[\phi^{\prime}(\rho)]\frac{k^{2}}{d}-o\left(\frac{k^{2}}{d}\right)}=\mathbb{E}_{\rho}^{2}[\phi^{\prime}(\rho)]\frac{k}{\sqrt{d}}\sqrt{1-o(1)}=\mathbb{E}_{\rho}^{2}[\phi^{\prime}(\rho)]\frac{k}{\sqrt{d}}-o\left(\frac{k}{\sqrt{d}}\right).

(C.177)

Following the same strategy, we can also prove that

I_{x_{1}}\leq\mathbb{E}_{\rho}^{2}[\phi^{\prime}(\rho)]\frac{k}{\sqrt{d}}+o\left(\frac{k}{\sqrt{d}}\right),

(C.178)

which finally gives

\left|I_{x_{1}}-\mathbb{E}_{\rho}^{2}[\phi^{\prime}(\rho)]\frac{k}{\sqrt{d}}\right|=o\left(\frac{k}{\sqrt{d}}\right).

(C.179)

Notice that we easily retrieve the same result also in the case
\mathbb{E}_{\rho}[\phi^{\prime}(\rho)]=0
.

Performing now a union bound over all the data, and recalling that
\left\|\mathcal{I}_{\textup{RF}}\right\|_{F}=\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{N}I^{2}_{x_{i}}}\geq\sqrt{N}\mathop{\rm min}\nolimits_{i}I_{x_{i}}
we have

\left\|\mathcal{I}_{\textup{RF}}\right\|_{F}\geq\mathbb{E}_{\rho}^{2}[\phi^{\prime}(\rho)]\frac{k\sqrt{N}}{\sqrt{d}}-o\left(\frac{k\sqrt{N}}{\sqrt{d}}\right),

(C.180)

with probability at least
1-N\exp(-c_{3}\log^{2}k)\geq 1-\exp(-c_{4}\log^{2}k)
over
X
and
z
and
V
. The same argument proves also the upper bound, and the thesis readily follows.
∎

C.5
Proof of Theorem
1

First, we state and prove an auxiliary result upper bounding
\left\lVert A\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}
.

Lemma C.21
.

Let
A=\nabla_{z}\phi(Vz)^{\top}\phi(XV^{\top})^{\top}K^{-1}
. Then, we have

\left\lVert A\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\right),

(C.181)

with probability at least
1-\exp(-c\log^{2}N)-\exp(-cd)
over
V
and
X
, where
c
is an absolute constant.

Proof.

Recalling that
\nabla_{z}\phi(Vz)^{\top}=V^{\top}\text{diag}\left(\phi^{\prime}(Vz)\right)
, we have

\displaystyle\left\lVert A\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}

\displaystyle=\left\lVert\nabla_{z}\phi(Vz)^{\top}\phi(XV^{\top})^{\top}K^{-1}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}

(C.182)

\displaystyle=\left\lVert V^{\top}\text{diag}\left(\phi^{\prime}(Vz)\right)\phi(XV^{\top})^{\top}K^{-1}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert V\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\left\lVert\text{diag}\left(\phi^{\prime}(Vz)\right)\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\left\lVert\Phi^{+}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert V\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}L\,\lambda_{\rm min}\left(K\right)^{-1/2}.

where in the last line we use that
\phi
is an
L
-Lipschitz function and that
K^{-1}=(\Phi^{+})^{\top}(\Phi^{+})
.
By Theorem 4.4.5 of
[
47
]
, we have (as
d=o(k)
by Assumption
3
) that

\left\lVert V\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}=\mathcal{O}\left(1+\sqrt{\frac{k}{d}}\right)=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{\frac{k}{d}}\right),

(C.183)

with probability at least
1-\exp(-cd)
over
V
. Lemma
C.5
readily gives
\lambda_{\rm min}\left(K\right)=\Omega(k)
, with probability at least
1-\exp(-c\log^{2}N)
over
V
and
X
. Taking a union bound on these two events gives the thesis.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1
.

By Theorem
4
, we have
\left\|\mathcal{I}_{\textup{RF}}\right\|_{F}\geq C_{1}\frac{k\sqrt{N}}{\sqrt{d}}
, with probability at least
1-\exp(-c\log^{2}k)
, which implies, by Theorem

,

\left\|A(z)\right\|_{F}\geq C_{2}\frac{d}{kN}\frac{k\sqrt{N}}{\sqrt{d}}-C\sqrt{N+d}/d=C_{2}\frac{\sqrt{d}}{\sqrt{N}}-C\sqrt{N+d}/d\geq C_{3}\frac{\sqrt{d}}{\sqrt{N}},

(C.184)

where the last inequality follows from Assumption
4
, and the first holds with probability at least
1-\exp(-c\log^{2}N)-k\exp(-cd)
.
This also means that

\mathcal{S}(z)\geq C_{4}\sqrt{d}\left\|A(z)\right\|_{F}

(C.185)

holds with probability at least
1-\exp\left(-cd^{2}/N\right)
, because of Theorem
2
and Lemma
C.21
.
Thus, we can conclude that

\mathcal{S}(z)\geq C_{4}\left\|z\right\|\left\|A(z)\right\|_{F}\geq C_{5}\frac{d}{\sqrt{N}}\geq C_{6}\sqrt[6]{N},

where the last inequality is a consequence of Assumption

.

Appendix D
Proofs for NTK Regression

In this section, we will indicate with
X\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times d}
the data matrix, such that its rows are sampled independently from
P_{X}
(see Assumption
1
). We will indicate with
W\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times d}
the first half of the weight matrix at initialization, such that
W_{ij}\mathbin{\overset{}{\sim}}_{\rm i.i.d.}\mathcal{N}(0,1/d)
. The feature vector will be therefore be (see (
5.3
))

\Phi_{\textup{NTK}}(x)=\left[x\otimes\phi^{\prime}(Wx),-x\otimes\phi^{\prime}(Wx)\right]

(D.1)

where
\phi
is the activation function, applied component-wise to the pre-activations
Wx
.

Notice that, for compactness, we dropped the subscripts
“NTK”
from these quantities, as this section will only treat the proofs related to Section
5
. Always for the sake of compactness, we will not re-introduce such quantities in the statements or the proofs of the following Lemmas.

Lemma D.1
.

We have that

\lambda_{\rm min}\left(K\right)=\Omega(kd),

(D.2)

with probability at least
1-Ne^{-c\log^{2}k}-e^{-c\log^{2}N}
over
X
and
W
.

Proof.

The thesis follows from Theorem 3.1 of
[
8
]
. Notice that our assumptions on the data distribution
P_{X}
are stronger, and that our initialization of the very last layer (which differs from their Gaussian initialization) does not change the result. Our assumption
k=\mathcal{O}\left(d\right)
respects the
loose pyramidal topology
condition, and we left the overparameterization assumption unchanged. A main difference is that we do not assume the activation function
\phi
to be Lipschitz anymore. This, however, stop being a necessary assumption since we are working with a 2-layer neural network, and
\phi
doesn’t appear in the expression of Neural Tangent Kernel.
∎

Lemma D.2
.

We have that

\mathop{\rm max}\nolimits_{i}\left|\phi^{\prime}(Wx_{i})^{\top}\phi^{\prime}(Wz)\right|=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{k}\log k\right),

(D.3)

with probability at least
1-\exp(-c\log^{2}k)
over
X
and
W
.

Proof.

Let’s look at the term
i=1
, and let’s call
x:=x_{1}
for compactness.
As in Lemma
C.20
, we define
z^{\prime}
as follows

z^{\prime}=\frac{\sqrt{d}\left(I-\frac{xx^{\top}}{d}\right)z}{\left\|\left(I-\frac{xx^{\top}}{d}\right)z\right\|_{2}}.

(D.4)

We have
x^{\top}z^{\prime}=0
, and
\left\|z^{\prime}\right\|_{2}=\sqrt{d}
. Furthermore, by (
C.162
), we also have that
\left\|z-z^{\prime}\right\|_{2}
is a sub-Gaussian random variable, in the probability space of
x
. It will also be convenient to characterize
\left\|\phi^{\prime}(Wz^{\prime})\right\|_{2}
. We have, indicating with
w_{j}
the
j
-th row of
W
,

\displaystyle\left\|\phi^{\prime}(Wz^{\prime})\right\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\phi^{\prime 2}(w_{j}^{\top}z^{\prime})=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\mathbb{E}_{W}\left[\phi^{\prime 2}(w_{j}^{\top}z^{\prime})\right]+\sum_{j=1}^{k}\left(\phi^{\prime 2}(w_{j}^{\top}z^{\prime})-\mathbb{E}_{W}\left[\phi^{\prime 2}(w_{j}^{\top}z^{\prime})\right]\right)

(D.5)

\displaystyle=k\mathbb{E}_{\rho}\left[\phi^{\prime 2}(\rho)\right]+\sum_{j=1}^{k}\left(\phi^{\prime 2}(\rho_{j})-\mathbb{E}_{\rho}\left[\phi^{\prime 2}(\rho)\right]\right),

where the
\rho
and
\rho_{j}
’s indicate independent standard Gaussian random variables. Since
\phi^{\prime}
is Lipschitz and non-zero, we have that
\mathbb{E}_{\rho}\left[\phi^{\prime 2}(\rho)\right]=\Theta(1)
. Furthermore, by Bernstein inequality (cf. Theorem 2.8.1 in
[
47
]
), we have
\sum_{j=1}^{k}\left(\phi^{\prime 2}(\rho_{j})-\mathbb{E}_{\rho}\left[\phi^{\prime 2}(\rho)\right]\right)=\mathcal{O}\left(k\right)
with probability at least
1-2\exp(-ck)
. This means that, with this probability (which is over
W
), we have

\left\|\phi^{\prime}(Wz^{\prime})\right\|_{2}\leq C\sqrt{k},

(D.6)

where
C
is a numerical constant. Notice that the same result holds for
\left\|\phi^{\prime}(Wx)\right\|_{2}
. We also have
\left\lVert W\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}=\mathcal{O}\left(1\right)
, which happens with probability at least
1-\exp(-ck)
over
W
, because of Theorem 4.4.5 of
[
47
]
and Assumption
7
.

Since
\phi^{\prime}
is Lipschitz, we can write

\left|\phi^{\prime}(Wx)^{\top}\phi^{\prime}(Wz)\right|\leq\left|\phi^{\prime}(Wx)^{\top}\phi^{\prime}(Wz^{\prime})\right|+L\left\|\phi^{\prime}(Wx)\right\|_{2}\left\lVert W\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\left\|z-z^{\prime}\right\|_{2}.

(D.7)

Let’s look at the first term of the LHS. Since
x^{\top}z^{\prime}=0
, and
\left\|x\right\|_{2}=\left\|z^{\prime}\right\|_{2}=\sqrt{d}
, we have

\phi^{\prime}(Wx)^{\top}\phi^{\prime}(Wz^{\prime})=\phi^{\prime}(\hat{\rho}_{a})^{\top}\phi^{\prime}(\hat{\rho}_{b}),

(D.8)

where
\hat{\rho}_{a}
and
\hat{\rho}_{b}
are two independent Gaussian vectors, each of them with independent entries. As
\phi
is an even function (see Assumption
5
), we have that
\phi^{\prime}(\hat{\rho}_{a})
and
\phi^{\prime}(\hat{\rho}_{b})
are independent sub-Gaussian vectors, with norm upper bounded by a numerical constant. Thus,

\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\phi^{\prime}(Wx)^{\top}\phi^{\prime}(Wz^{\prime})\right|>C\sqrt{k}\log k\right)<2\exp(-c_{1}\log^{2}k),

(D.9)

where this holds due to (
D.6
).
Let’s now look at the second term of the LHS of (
D.7
). We have

\left\|\phi^{\prime}(Wx)\right\|_{2}\left\lVert W\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\left\|z-z^{\prime}\right\|_{2}\leq C\sqrt{k}C_{1}\left\|z-z^{\prime}\right\|_{2}=\mathcal{O}\left(\log k\sqrt{k}\right),

(D.10)

where the inequality holds with probability at least
1-\exp(-ck)
over
W
, and the equality holds with probability at least
1-2\exp(-c_{2}\log^{2}k)
over
x
.
Taking the intersection between the previously described high probability events, from (
D.7
) we get

\left|\phi^{\prime}(Wx)^{\top}\phi^{\prime}(Wz)\right|=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{k}\log k\right),

(D.11)

with probability at least
1-2\exp(-c\log^{2}k)
over
x
and
W
.
Thus, performing a union bound over the different
x_{i}
’s, we get the thesis.
∎

Lemma D.3
.

\left\lVert\nabla_{z}\Phi(z)^{\top}\Phi(X)^{\top}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\log k\left(\sqrt{k}+\sqrt{N}\right)\sqrt{d}\right),

(D.12)

with probability at least
1-\exp(-c\log^{2}k)
over
X
,
W
and
z
.

Proof.

We have, by application of the chain rule,

\displaystyle\nabla_{z}\Phi(z)^{\top}\Phi(X)^{\top}

\displaystyle=2\,\nabla_{z}\Phi^{\prime}(z)^{\top}\Phi^{\prime}(X)^{\top}

(D.13)

\displaystyle=2\,\nabla_{z}\left(z\otimes\phi^{\prime}(Wz)\right)^{\top}\left(X*\phi^{\prime}(XW^{\top})\right)^{\top}

\displaystyle=2\left(I*\mathbf{1}\phi^{\prime\top}(Wz)+\mathbf{1}z^{\top}*W^{\top}\textup{diag}\left(\phi^{\prime\prime}(Wz)\right)\right)\left(X*\phi^{\prime}(XW^{\top})\right)^{\top}

\displaystyle=2\,X^{\top}\circ\mathbf{1}\left(\phi^{\prime}(XW^{\top})\phi^{\prime}(Wz)\right)^{\top}+2\,\mathbf{1}\left(Xz\right)^{\top}\circ W^{\top}\textup{diag}\left(\phi^{\prime\prime}(Wz)\right)\phi^{\prime}(XW^{\top})^{\top}

\displaystyle=2\,X^{\top}\textup{diag}\left(\phi^{\prime}(XW^{\top})\phi^{\prime}(Wz)\right)+2\,W^{\top}\textup{diag}\left(\phi^{\prime\prime}(Wz)\right)\phi^{\prime}(XW^{\top})^{\top}\textup{diag}\left(Xz\right).

Let’s now condition on the event
\left\lVert W\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}=\mathcal{O}\left(1\right)
, which happens with probability at least
1-\exp(-cd)
over
W
, because of Theorem 4.4.5 of
[
47
]
and Assumption
7
.

Let’s now look at the two terms separately. For the first, we have

\displaystyle\left\lVert X^{\top}\textup{diag}\left(\phi^{\prime}(XW^{\top})\phi^{\prime}(Wz)\right)\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert X\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\mathop{\rm max}\nolimits_{i}\left|\phi^{\prime}(Wx_{i})^{\top}\phi^{\prime}(Wz)\right|

(D.14)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\left(\log k\sqrt{k}\left(\sqrt{N}+\sqrt{d}\right)\right),

where the last equality is true because of Lemmas
B.5
and
D.2
and holds with probability at least
1-\exp(-cd)-\exp(-c\log^{2}k)
over
X
and
W
.
For the other term, we have

\displaystyle\left\lVert W^{\top}\textup{diag}\left(\phi^{\prime\prime}(Wz)\right)\phi^{\prime}(XW^{\top})\textup{diag}\left(Xz\right)\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert W\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}L\left\lVert\phi^{\prime}(XW^{\top})\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\mathop{\rm max}\nolimits_{i}\left|x_{i}^{\top}z\right|

(D.15)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\left(\log k\sqrt{d}\left(\sqrt{N}+\sqrt{k}\right)\right).

The last step uses Lemma
B.5
and the fact that we are taking the maximum over
k
sub-Gaussian random variables such that
\left\|x_{i}^{\top}z\right\|_{\psi_{2}}\leq C\left\|z\right\|_{2}=C\sqrt{d}
, and it holds with probability at least
1-2\exp(-ck)-\exp(-c\log^{2}k)
over
X
.

Putting everything together, and using Assumption
7
, we get

\displaystyle\left\lVert\nabla_{z}\Phi(z)^{\top}\Phi(X)^{\top}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\left(\log k\sqrt{k}\left(\sqrt{N}+\sqrt{d}\right)\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(\log k\sqrt{d}\left(\sqrt{N}+\sqrt{k}\right)\right)

(D.16)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\left(\log k\left(\sqrt{k}+\sqrt{N}\right)\sqrt{d}\right),

which gives the thesis.
∎

D.1
Proof of Theorem
5

Proof of Theorem
5
.

We have

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}(z)

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{d}\left\lVert\nabla_{z}\Phi(z)^{\top}\Phi(X)^{\top}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\left\lVert K^{-1}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\left\|Y\right\|_{2}

(D.17)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{d}\mathcal{O}\left(\log k\left(\sqrt{k}+\sqrt{N}\right)\sqrt{d}\right)\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{dk}\right)\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{N}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\left(\log k\left(1+\sqrt{N/k}\right)\frac{\sqrt{N}}{\sqrt{k}}\right),

where the second line is justified by Lemmas
D.1
and
D.3
, and holds with probability at least
1-Ne^{-c\log^{2}k}-e^{-c\log^{2}N}
over
X
and
W
. The second statement is immediate.
∎
