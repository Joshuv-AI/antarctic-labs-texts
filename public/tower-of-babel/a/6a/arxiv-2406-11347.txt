Ergodic Estimation and Model Assessment for Dynamic Exceedance Times
Åsmund Hausken Sande

Abstract
. This article concerns the estimation of hitting time statistics for potentially non-stationary processes. The main focus is exceedance times of environmental processes. To this end we consider an empirical estimator based on ergodic theory under the assumption that the considered process is a deterministic transformation of some ergodic process. This estimator is empirically analysed and rigorous convergence results, including a central limit theorem, are covered. Using our estimator, we compute confidence intervals for mean exceedance times of empirical wind data. This serves as a baseline for assessing the performance of several models in terms of predicted mean exceedance time. Special attention is given to the model class known as Gaussian copula processes, which models the environmental process as a deterministic, possibly time-dependent, transformation of a stationary parent Gaussian process.

A flexible and convenient class of models are Gaussian copula processes. This class was recently suggested as a universal approach to modelling environmental processes in

]

[

]
, and have been used to estimate return periods of extreme environmental conditions
[

, including applications to seasonally non-stationary processes
[

. As such, one of the main goals of this article will be to assess and analyse the performance of this type of model.

To this end we consider an empirical estimator for hitting time statistics for possibly non-stationary processes. The estimator is based on the assumption that our process
V
is a deterministic, possibly time dependent, transformation of an ergodic process
\widetilde{V}
. The main idea of the estimator is the following. Consider the first time
V
enters some set
\beta
. We can the equivalently consider the first time the ergodic
\widetilde{V}
enters a time dependent set
\alpha
. This allows us to extend results from ergodic theory to our original process which provides us with convergence results, including a central limit theorem.

This estimator can serve as a way to estimate statistical properties of hitting times with a minimal amount of model assumptions. We can also use the empirically estimated return periods as a benchmark to assess the performance of several models in terms of their predicted return periods. Special attention is here given to the aforementioned class of Gaussian copula processes.

We also analyse various properties of our estimator in order to ensure the validity of our empirical results. This includes the aforementioned convergence results, but also a short study based on synthetic data. The main purpose of this study is to examine our method’s inherent bias in the estimation of average exceedance times.

2.
Standard Ergodic Estimators

In this article we will consider a complete probability space
\left(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P}\right)
. We will denote the set of all non-negative integers, including
0
, by
\mathbb{N}
, while
\overline{\mathbb{N}}
will denote
\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
.

The presented estimators will be based on ergodic theory and central limit theory for strong-mixing processes. For an overview we refer to e.g.
[
8
,
3
]
. We will here mainly consider processes in discrete time.

Definition 2.1
.

By a discrete process, we refer to some sequence of random variables
X=\{X_{t}\}_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
, where, for some
N\in\mathbb{N},\,N\geq 1
, we have
X_{t}(\omega)\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
for any
\omega\in\Omega,\,t\in\mathbb{Z}
.
We will in this article omit the notation of
\omega
.

The main result we will use is the mean ergodic theorem. This result holds for any discrete processes that are sufficiently invariant under time shifts.

Definition 2.2
.

Define the shift operator
S
as the mapping
\{X_{t}\}_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}\mapsto\{X_{t+1}\}_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
, i.e.
(SX)_{t}=X_{t+1},

for any discrete process
X
. We also denote
(S^{s}X)_{t}=X_{t+s},
for any
s\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Definition 2.3
.

We will refer to a discrete process
X
as ergodic if the following conditions hold for any
E\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{N})^{\otimes\mathbb{Z}}
, the product
\sigma
-algebra of
(\mathbb{R}^{N})^{\mathbb{Z}}
.

\mathbb{P}(X\in E)=\mathbb{P}(SX\in E),

S^{s}X\in E,\text{ for all $s\in\mathbb{Z}$ }\Rightarrow\mathbb{P}(X\in E)\in\{0,1\}.

Remark 2.4
.

The first condition in
Definition

2.3
, is usually referred to as strict stationarity, and can equivalently be stated as

\mathbb{P}(X_{t_{1}}\in E_{1},\cdots,X_{t_{n}}\in E_{n})=\mathbb{P}(X_{t_{1}+1}\in E_{1},\cdots,X_{t_{n}+1}\in E_{n}),

for all finite sequences,
\{E_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
, of Borel sets in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
and sequences
\{t_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
,
t_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Theorem 2.5

(The Mean Ergodic Theorem)
.

Let
X
be an ergodic process and
f
be any Borel measurable functional
f:(\mathbb{R}^{N})^{\mathbb{Z}}\to\mathbb{R}
such that
\mathbb{E}\left[|f(X)|\right]<\infty
. We then have almost surely that

\mathbb{E}\left[f(X)\right]=\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}f(S^{t}X).

For a proof, see e.g.
[
8
]
.

This allows us to compute moments of various functionals. In particular, we will use the fact that hitting times can be considered functionals of the future path of the underlying process.

Additionally, under some additional technical assumptions we can estimate the error of estimates from
Theorem

2.5
by the following central limit theorem.

Definition 2.6
.

For any ergodic process,
X
, we define the strong mixing coefficient

\alpha(X,s)=\sup_{A\in\mathcal{F}_{-\infty}^{0},\,B\in\mathcal{F}_{s}^{\infty}}\left|\mathbb{P}(A\cap B)-\mathbb{P}(A)\mathbb{P}(B)\right|,

where
\mathcal{F}_{a}^{b}
is the
\sigma
-algebra generated by
\{X_{t}\}_{t=a}^{b}
. The process,
X
is said to be strongly mixing if
\lim_{s\to\infty}\alpha(X,s)=0
.

Theorem 2.7
.

Assume
X
is a strongly mixing process satisfying
\mathbb{E}[X_{0}^{2}]<\infty
. We may then define
\mathcal{M}_{T}=\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}X_{t}
and
\sigma_{T}^{2}=\mathbb{V}ar[\mathcal{M}_{T}]
. If
\lim_{T\to\infty}\sigma_{T}=\infty
the following are equivalent.

•

The family
\{\mathcal{M}_{T}^{2}/\sigma_{T}^{2}\}_{T\in\mathbb{N}}
is uniformly integrable.

•

(\mathcal{M}_{T}-\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{M}_{T}])/\sigma_{T}
converges in distribution to a standard normal variable.

For a proof, see e.g.
[
3
]
.

Remark 2.8
.

There are many alternative conditions to the uniform integrability of
\{S_{T}^{2}/\sigma_{T}^{2}\}_{T\in\mathbb{N}}
. A common sufficient criterion (the Lyapunov condition) is given in e.g.
[
7
]
. Here it is assumed that
\mathbb{E}[|X_{0}|^{2+\delta}]<\infty
and
\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\alpha(X,t)^{\delta/(2+\delta)}<\infty
for some
\delta>0
. Under this assumption, or similar ones, the central limit theorem will still hold.

3.
Empirical Estimation of Exceedance Times

In order to apply ergodic theory to hitting times we will need to slightly modify the estimators to account for censoring. The technical details for the fully general case is given in
Appendix

A
, and a simplified version will be given in the remainder of this section.

We now consider a discrete process
V:\Omega\times\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{R}^{N}
. In this article
V
will model some environmental process measured at all points
t\in\mathbb{Z},

0\leq t<T
for some final time-point
T
.

The main quantity of interest is the following.

Definition 3.1
.

Assume we have a discrete process
V
, a time
t\in\mathbb{Z}
, and a set-valued threshold function
\beta:\,\mathbb{N}\to\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
, where
\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
denotes the collection of Borel sets of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
. We define the hitting time of
V
, with respect to
\beta
, as the random time,
\tau:

(\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{N}))^{\mathbb{N}}\times(\mathbb{R}^{N})^{\mathbb{Z}}\times\mathbb{Z}\to\overline{\mathbb{N}}
given by

\tau_{\beta}(V,t)=\min\{s\geq 0:V_{s+t}\in\beta_{s}\},

unless
V_{s+t}\notin\beta_{s}
for all
s\geq 0
, in which case we set
\tau_{\beta}(V,t)=\infty
. Whenever
\beta
is constant, i.e.
\beta_{s}=\widetilde{\beta}
for all
s\in\mathbb{N}
, we will denote
\tau_{\beta}(V,t)
by
\tau_{\widetilde{\beta}}(V,t)
.

Remark 3.2
.

In this article we mainly consider
N=1
with a constant
\beta_{t}=(b,\infty)
.
We will, in this setting, refer to
\tau_{\beta}(V,t)
as an exceedance time.

While we also include arbitrary
N
for the sake of generality, we will later need a time-varying
\beta
in order to account for non-stationarity in
V
.

3.1.
Stationary Case

In order to apply our ergodic theory we will in this section assume that the discrete process
\{\tau_{\beta}(V,t)\}_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
is ergodic. Note that ergodicity of
V
would imply the ergodicity of
\tau_{\beta}(V,\cdot)
. This will, in particular, imply that
\tau_{\beta}(V,t)=\tau_{\beta}(V,s)
in law for any
t,s\in\mathbb{Z}
. Because of this we may ignore the dependence of
\tau
on
t
whenever we are taking expectations. Most importantly, we can apply
Theorem

2.5
to evaluate
\mathbb{E}\left[f(\tau_{\beta}(V,0))\right]
for any function
f:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{R}
. Mainly, we will be interested in the
return period

\mathbb{E}\left[\tau_{\beta}(V,0)\right]
, which can be computed by

(3.1)

\mathbb{E}\left[\tau_{\beta}(V,0)\right]=\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\tau_{\beta}(V,t).

For practical implementations of estimators such as (
3.1
), we would want to omit the limit
T\to\infty
and use
T
equal to the size of our dataset. However, if
\tau_{\beta}(V,t)\geq T-t
for any
0\leq t<T
, these estimators would still potentially rely on
V_{t}
for
t\geq T
. This constitutes a right-censoring of the data. The easiest way to deal with this is to discard all censored data. For example, we could consider a
\widetilde{T}\leq T
such that
\tau_{\beta}(V,t)<T-t
for all
t<\widetilde{T}
and then use the estimator

(3.2)

\mathbb{E}\left[\tau_{\beta}(V,0)\right]\approx\frac{1}{\widetilde{T}}\sum_{t=0}^{\widetilde{T}-1}\tau_{\beta}(V,t).

This could unfortunately ignore a lot of data, and may consequently yield a higher estimation uncertainty. To remedy this we may replace
\tau_{\beta}(t)
with some other quantity which makes use of all the data. Specifically, we will consider

(3.3)

\mathbb{E}\left[\tau_{\beta}(V,0)\right]\approx\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\widehat{\tau}_{\beta}(T;V,t),

where

\widehat{\tau}_{\beta}(T;V,t)\triangleq\begin{cases}\tau_{\beta}(V,t)&\tau_{\beta}(V,t)<T-t\\
T-t+\Lambda(T;V,t)&\tau_{\beta}(V,t)\geq T-t.\end{cases}

Here
\Lambda:\mathbb{N}\times(\mathbb{R}^{N})^{\mathbb{Z}}\times\mathbb{N}\mapsto\overline{\mathbb{N}}
is defined such that
\Lambda(T;V,t)
depends on
V_{t}
only for
0\leq t<T
. Specifically, we will for the rest of the article, except for
Appendix

A
, consider the case where

(3.4)

\Lambda(T;V,t)=\begin{cases}\tau_{S^{T-t}\beta}(V,0)&\tau_{S^{T-t}\beta}(V,0)<T\\
\infty&\tau_{S^{T-t}\beta}(V,0)\geq T.\end{cases}

Note that we here define
S^{s}\beta,
for some
s\in\mathbb{N}
, by
S^{s}\beta(t)=\beta(t+s)
for all
t\in\mathbb{N}
.

Remark 3.3
.

The estimator (
3.2
) behaves very similar to (
3.3
). Consequently, the applications of
Section

5
could potentially be carried out with either estimator. Nevertheless, (
3.2
) appears to perform worse in certain cases. Especially in the case where
\beta_{s}
is a decreasing function, i.e.
\beta_{s}\supset\beta_{t}
for
s\leq t
. It can be observed from
Proposition

3.4
that this corresponds to the case where
V
has an increasing trend (see
Section

3.2
for how (
3.3
) is applied to handle a non-stationary
V
). Furthermore, (
3.3
) is monotone in
\beta
for
\Lambda
given by (
3.4
). This monotonicity is taken in the sense that
\widehat{\tau}_{\beta}(T;V,t)\leq\widehat{\tau}_{\alpha}(T;V,t)
for all
t\leq T
whenever
\alpha_{s}\subseteq\beta_{s}
for all
s\in\mathbb{N}
.

In order to justify our specific choice of
\Lambda
, we consider the following.
Assume that
\tau_{\beta}(V,t)\geq T-t
for some
t<T
, this implies

\tau_{\beta}(V,t)=T-t+\tau_{S^{T-t}\beta}(V,T).

Note that if
t\mapsto\tau_{\beta}(V,t)
is ergodic then
\tau_{S^{T-t}\beta}(V,T)=\tau_{S^{T-t}\beta}(V,0)
in law. Our choice of
\Lambda
then yields that

(3.5)

\tau_{\beta}(V,t)=\widehat{\tau}_{\beta}(T;V,t),

in law for any
0\leq t<T
conditional on
\tau_{S^{T-t}\beta}(V,0)<T
. Equivalently, this equality in law holds conditional on
\widehat{\tau}_{\beta}(T;V,t)<\infty
.

For the sake of implementation, our choice of
\Lambda
is equivalent to looping the dataset. We essentially replace
V_{T+s}
with
V_{s}
in (
3.1
). Specifically, if
\widehat{\tau}_{\beta}(V,t)<\infty
we have

\widehat{\tau}_{\beta}(T;V,t)=\min\{s\geq 0:V_{t+s\text{ mod }T}\in\beta_{s}\},\quad a\text{ mod }b=a-b\lfloor a/b\rfloor.

As a consequence of
Lemma

A.4
, in
Appendix

A
, we may apply (
3.3
) to achieve a feasible estimator of
\mathbb{E}\left[\tau_{\beta}(V,T)\right]
. However, we need a technical requirement on
\Lambda
to guarantee convergence. Specifically, we require
\limsup_{T\to\infty}\sup_{0\leq t<T}\Lambda(T;V,t)<\infty
. To resolve this we consider the case where
\beta_{s}\supseteq\widetilde{\beta}
for some measurable
\widetilde{\beta}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N}
and for all
s\in\mathbb{N}
, with
\mathbb{E}[\tau_{\widetilde{\beta}}(V,0)]<\infty
. We then have

\lim_{T\to\infty}\sup_{0\leq t<T}\Lambda(T;V,t)=\sup_{t>0}\tau_{S^{t}\beta}(V,0)\leq\tau_{\widetilde{\beta}}(V,0)<\infty.

almost surely. This additionally guarantees that
\widehat{\tau}_{\beta}(T;V,t)
is almost always eventually finite for all
t\leq T
, given a sufficiently high
T
.

This yields the following results by
Theorem

A.6
(and
Remark

A.7
). Note that these equalities all hold almost surely.

(3.6)

\mathbb{P}\left(\tau_{\beta}(V,T)>s\right)=\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathds{1}(\widehat{\tau}_{\beta}(T;v,t)>s).

If
\beta_{s}\supseteq\widetilde{\beta}
for some measurable
\widetilde{\beta}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N}
and all
s\geq 0
, with
\mathbb{E}[\tau_{\widetilde{\beta}}(V,T)^{2}]<\infty
then

(3.7)

\mathbb{E}\left[\tau_{\beta}(V,T)\right]=\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\widehat{\tau}_{\beta}(T;V,t),

If we further assume that
\mathbb{E}[\tau_{\widetilde{\beta}}(V,T)^{3}]<\infty
then

(3.8)

\mathbb{E}\left[\tau_{\beta}(V,0)\tau_{\beta}(V,i)\right]=\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=i}^{T-1}\widehat{\tau}_{\beta}(T;V,t-i)\widehat{\tau}_{\beta}(T;V,t),

In the case where
\beta
is constant, i.e.
\beta_{s}=\widetilde{\beta}
for all
s
, we can derive a computationally cheap explicit formula for (
3.7
). First we define
\Theta
, the set of all observed points where the process is in
\widetilde{\beta}
, i.e.
\Theta\triangleq\{\theta\in\mathbb{N}:\theta<T,\,V_{\theta}\in\widetilde{\beta}\}
. We assume that the size of this set,
|\Theta|
, is at least
1
. We denote by
\theta_{i}
,
i\leq|\Theta|
the
i
’th element of
\Theta
, and define
\theta_{|\Theta|+1}=T+\theta_{1}
. We lastly introduce the shorthand
\Delta\theta_{i}=\theta_{i+1}-\theta_{i}
. This yields

(3.9)

\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\widehat{\tau}_{\beta}(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{|\Theta|}\frac{\Delta\theta_{i}(\Delta\theta_{i}-1)}{2T}.

Finally, in
Theorem

A.8
, we also prove a central limit theorem for our estimator. Specifically,

(3.10)

\frac{\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\widehat{\tau}_{\beta}(V,t)-\mathbb{E}[\tau_{\beta}(V,0)]}{{\text{sd}\left(\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}{\tau_{\beta}}(V,t)\right)}},

converges in distribution to a standard normal random variable when
T\to\infty
. In order to practically compute this we note that

\text{sd}\left(\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}{\tau_{\beta}}(V,t)\right)^{2}=T\,\mathbb{E}[\tau_{\beta}(V,0)^{2}]+2\sum_{i=1}^{T-1}\mathbb{E}\big[\tau_{\beta}(V,0)\tau_{\beta}(V,i)\big](T-i),

which is estimable from (
3.8
).

3.2.
Non-Stationary Case

The methods presented so far require a stationary
V
. This usually ignores several important factors, mainly seasonal behaviour and long-scale trends. In order to include and examine such effects we will need to slightly adjust our empirical estimator.

Proposition 3.4
.

Consider a discrete, possibly non-stationary, process
V
and a threshold function
\beta:\,\mathbb{N}\to\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
. Let
G:\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}^{M}
, for some
M\in\mathbb{N}
, be any measurable function such that
v\mapsto G_{t}(v)
is injective for any
t\in\mathbb{Z}
.

We define
\widetilde{V}
by
\widetilde{V}_{t}=G_{t}(V_{t})
for all
t\in\mathbb{Z}
. For any
u\in\mathbb{Z}
we define
\alpha_{s}=G_{u+s}(\beta_{s})
. We then have

\tau_{{\beta}}(V,u)=\tau_{\alpha}(\widetilde{V},u).

Proof.

Follows from the observation that

V_{u+s}\in{\beta_{s}}\Leftrightarrow G_{u+s}(V_{u+s})\in G_{u+s}({\beta})\Leftrightarrow\widetilde{V}_{u+s}\in\alpha_{s}.

∎

If
\widetilde{V}
in
Proposition

3.4
is ergodic we can apply the theory from
Section

3.1
.
This means we can estimate the average hitting time of
V
with respect to
\beta
by applying our estimator to
\widetilde{V}
and
\alpha
. Specifically, we have almost surely that

\mathbb{E}\left[\tau_{\beta}(V,u)\right]=\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\widehat{\tau}_{\alpha}(T;\widetilde{V},t),

if the technical conditions of (
3.7
) hold. In this context we would require
\alpha_{s}\supseteq\widetilde{\alpha}
for some measurable
\widetilde{\alpha}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{M}
and all
s\geq 0
, with
\mathbb{E}[\tau_{\widetilde{\alpha}}(\widetilde{V},T)^{2}]<\infty
.

4.
Analysis of Bias for Synthetic Data

In this section we aim to examine the bias and limitations of the estimator by means of synthetic data. This will also allow us to comment on the benefits of including non-stationarity in the modelling of
V
.

For the sake of clarity and simplicity, we will consider the average first exceedance time of a one-dimensional and ergodic
V
above a threshold
b
. Specifically, we will consider the functions

\mathcal{E}_{b}(V)\triangleq\mathbb{E}[\tau_{(b,\infty)}(V,0)],\quad\widehat{\mathcal{E}}_{b}(V,T)\triangleq\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\widehat{\tau}_{(b,\infty)}(V,t).

Note that the random variable
\widehat{\mathcal{E}}_{b}(V,T)
is taken to be infinite if
\widehat{\tau}_{(b,\infty)}(V,t)=\infty
for any
t
.

We may note from (
3.5
) that the estimator is unbiased conditional on the event event that it is finite, i.e.

\mathbb{E}\left[\tau_{(b,\infty)}(V,0)-\widehat{\mathcal{E}}_{b}(V,T)\Big|\widehat{\mathcal{E}}_{b}(V,T)<\infty\right]=0.

Additionally, we know that for a fixed
b
we have
\lim_{T\to\infty}\widehat{\mathcal{E}}_{b}(V,T)=\mathcal{E}_{b}(V)
.
However, for a dataset of fixed size
T
, if we compute the curve
b\mapsto\widehat{\mathcal{E}}_{b}(V,T)
for all
b
such that
\widehat{\mathcal{E}}_{b}(V,T)<\infty
, we introduce a bias. This bias comes from the fact that

\mathcal{E}_{b}(V)-\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{\mathcal{E}}_{b}(V,T)\Big|\widehat{\mathcal{E}}_{b}(V,T)<\infty\right]\geq 0,

where the inequality is usually strict. Fortunately, as we will see, this bias vanishes for smaller values of
b
.

4.1.
Stationary Case

To examine this effect we consider
V
as a standard normal AR(1) process. We can therefore define
V
by

(4.1)

V_{t}=\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}\sum_{s=-\infty}^{t}\rho^{t-s}X_{s}=\rho V_{t-1}+\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}X_{t},

for all
t\in\mathbb{Z}
. Here
\rho=97\%
and
\{X_{t}\}_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
is an independent sequence of standard normal random variables. Note that we could here equivalently define
V
as the unique standardised (mean 0 and variance 1) Gaussian process with autocorrelation
\rho(V,t)=\text{cor}(V_{0},V_{t})=\rho^{t}
.

Remark 4.1
.

Note that since
\mathcal{E}_{b}(sV+m)=\mathcal{E}_{(b-m)/s}(V)
, there is no loss of generality from considering a standardised process.

.

80\%
prediction interval (grey shaded area) for
b\mapsto\widehat{\mathcal{E}}_{b}(V,T)
with
T=20000
under the condition
\widehat{\mathcal{E}}_{b}(V,T)<\infty
. Also included are
b\mapsto\mathcal{E}_{b}(V)
(black dots), and the theoretical finite maximum
(T-1)/2
(horizontal red dashed line).

We then consider
\widehat{\mathcal{E}}_{b}(V,T)
for
T=20000
,
b\in[2.5,4.5]
, the resulting mean and upper/lower
10\%
quantiles are plotted in

1
. Note that by (

) we have that
\widehat{\mathcal{E}}_{b}(V,T)<\infty
implies
\widehat{\mathcal{E}}_{b}(V,T)\leq(T-1)/2
, which provides an upper bound on the finite values the estimator can take. As a specific consequence, if
\mathcal{E}_{b}(V)>(T-1)/2
then
\widehat{\mathcal{E}}_{b}(V,T)<\mathcal{E}_{b}(V,T)
and there must necessarily exist a negative bias. Similarly, in
Figure

, we see that the conditional mean of
\widehat{\mathcal{E}}_{b}(V,T)
deviates from
\mathcal{E}_{b}(V)
only when
\mathcal{E}_{b}(V)
approaches
(T-1)/2
.

4.2.
Non-Stationary Case

In the case where
V
is not stationary we again consider the AR1 process defined in (
4.1
), but scale it by a periodic function. Specifically

\displaystyle V_{t}

\displaystyle=Z_{t}S_{t},

\displaystyle Z_{t}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}\sum_{s=-\infty}^{t}\rho^{t-s}X_{s},

\displaystyle S_{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}{\left(1+\sin\left(\frac{t-\lfloor t/1000\rfloor}{1000}\,\pi\right)^{2}\right)}

for all

. Here
\rho=70\%
and
\{X_{t}\}_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
is an independent sequence of standard normal random variables. The function

starts at
1
for

, and then drops down to
0.5
for
t=500
, finally returning to
1
for
t=1000
. We also have

periodic with period

, thereby imitating a seasonal scale for
V
.

We may then apply employ
Proposition

3.4
in order to estimate
\mathcal{E}_{b}
by

\mathcal{E}_{b}(V)=\mathbb{E}\left[\tau_{(b,\infty)}(V,0)\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\tau_{\alpha}(Z,0)\right],

where
\alpha_{t}=[b/S_{t},\infty]
.

It is common to ignore the effects of seasonality, assuming that the seasonal effects will in some sense average out over time. To examine the effect of the inclusion of seasonality in the estimation of
\mathcal{E}_{b}(V)
we additionally compute our estimator under the assumption that
V
is stationary by applying (

For any
b\in\mathbb{R}
, we recall the definition
\Theta\triangleq\{\theta\in\mathbb{N}:\theta<T,\,V_{\theta}>b\}
. We denote by
\theta_{i}

the

\Theta

\theta_{|\Theta|+1}=T+\theta_{1}

\Delta\theta_{i}=\theta_{i+1}-\theta_{i}
. With this we denote

(4.2)

\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}_{b}(V,T)=\sum_{i=1}^{|\Theta|}\frac{\Delta\theta_{i}(\Delta\theta_{i}-1)}{2T},

unless
\Theta=\emptyset
in which case we set
\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}_{b}(V,T)=\infty

As a final note, it is worth mentioning the case where the non-stationarity of

. In cases with long-term trends, the non-stationarity of

might have a big impact on the tail behaviour of exceedance times. As such we would have no guarantee that a stationary estimate approaches the true return period for high thresholds as we see in the purely seasonal case. The inclusion of non-stationarity in the modelling of
V
is therefore particularly important in such cases.

5.
Comparison of Methods and Models for Empirical Data

After seeing how the estimator performs on synthetic data we can move on to empirical analyses. The goal will be to compare the average exceedance times of four different models with empirical estimates. The data considered for this example will be ERA5 reanalysis data
[
15
]
. Specifically, we will use hourly wind speeds in meters per second (m/s) at 100 meters elevation in an area located at 49°N, -8°W. The data is for every hour over the 41-year period
1980
-
2020
, yielding a total of 359 424 individual data points.

We again consider the quantities

\mathcal{E}_{b}(V)\triangleq\mathbb{E}[\tau_{(b,\infty)}(V,0)],\quad\widehat{\mathcal{E}}_{b}(V,T)\triangleq\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\widehat{\tau}_{(b,\infty)}(V,t).

The idea is to use
\widehat{\mathcal{E}}_{b}(V,T)
and its corresponding confidence interval as an estimate for
\mathcal{E}_{b}(V)
. Using this estimate as an approximate ground truth, we can evaluate and compare the performance of different models of
V
. This is done by computing
\mathcal{E}_{b}(V^{*})
, for some
V^{*}
taken from a selection of models for
V
.

5.1.
Stationary Models

For our initial analysis we will ignore the seasonal trends present in the data and treat
V
as an ergodic process satisfying the conditions of our central limit theorem (
Theorem

A.8
). This allows us to compute
b\mapsto\widehat{\mathcal{E}}_{b}(V,T)
,
T=359424
, along with an approximate
95\%
confidence interval for
\mathcal{E}_{b}(V)
.

In providing models for
V
we will here focus on two aspects. Firstly, we require the marginal distributions of
V
in the form of the cumulative distribution function
F_{V}(v)=\mathbb{P}(V_{t}\leq v)
. Note that as a consequence of ergodicity, this function is independent of
t
. Secondly, we will consider the autocorrelation structure
\rho(V,t)=\text{cor}(V_{0},V_{t})
. To compute
F_{V}
we assume that

F_{V}(v)=1-\text{exp}\left(-\left(\frac{v}{\lambda}\right)^{\alpha}\right),

i.e. that the marginals of
V
follow a Weibull distribution with scale
\lambda=11.05
and shape
\alpha=2.19
. Similarly, we estimate the autocorrelation structure of
V
as

(5.1)

\rho(V,t)=\left(1+\kappa\left(\frac{t}{\zeta}\right)^{\eta}\right)^{-\frac{1}{\eta\kappa}},

with
\zeta=10.23,\,\eta=1.63,\,\kappa=1.38
. Note that several parametric models for
\rho(V,\cdot)
were considered, see e.g.
[
26
]
for a list of suggested models. However, (
5.1
) was found to provide the best fit for our dataset. We also remark that the same parametric model was used for the autocorrelation structure of significant wave heights in
[
33
]
.

We will in particular consider a class of models known as Gaussian copula processes. Let
\Phi
be the cumulative distribution function of a standard normal Gaussian. We then have that
Z_{t}=\Phi^{-1}F_{V}(V_{t})
has a standard normal distribution for any
t
. It is important to note that this does not in general make
Z
a Gaussian process. However, modelling it as such allows for simple models that still capture the correct marginal distributions and autocorrelation structure of
V
. This approach was suggested for the modelling of environmental factors in
[
26
]
, and has been applied to compute return periods in the context of environmental contours
[
33
,
34
]
.

Following
[
26
,
33
]
we define
\mathcal{T}
as the transformation
\mathcal{T}(\rho(Z,t))=\rho(V,t)
, which can be computed as

\mathcal{T}(\rho)=\frac{\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}F_{V}^{-1}\Phi(x)F_{V}^{-1}\Phi(y)\phi(\rho;x,y)dxdy-\mu_{V}^{2}}{\sigma_{V}^{2}},

where
\phi(\rho;\cdot,\cdot)
is the density function of a bivariate normal distribution with correlation
\rho
. Additionally,
\mu_{V}=9.80
and
\sigma_{V}=4.71
are the mean and standard deviation, respectively, of
V
. We then aim to use the relation
\mathcal{T}(\rho(Z,t))=\rho(V,t)
, which is done in e.g.
[
33
,
26
]
, by using an accurate parametric approximation of
\mathcal{T}
. In our case it was found that

(5.2)

\mathcal{T}(\rho)\approx\frac{(1+\xi\rho)^{\upsilon}-1}{(1+\xi)^{\upsilon}-1},\quad\mathcal{T}^{-1}(\rho)\approx\frac{(1+\rho(1+\xi)^{\upsilon}-\rho)^{1/\upsilon}-1}{\xi},

for
\xi=0.065,\,\upsilon=0.373
. Note that under the assumption that
Z
is a strictly stationary standardised Gaussian process, it will be uniquely determined by
\rho(Z,\cdot)
.

The first model we consider, labeled
V^{(\text{IID})}
, will be the simplest one. We define
\{V^{(\text{IID})}_{t}\}_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
to be an i.i.d. sequence of random variables such that
V^{(\text{IID})}_{t}=V_{t}
in law for all
t\in\mathbb{Z}
. Consequently we can compute the average exceedance times of
V^{(\text{IID})}
by

\mathcal{E}_{b}\left(V^{(\text{IID})}\right)=\frac{F_{V}(b)}{1-F_{V}(b)}=\text{exp}\left(\left(\frac{b}{\lambda}\right)^{\alpha}\right)-1.

Note that we could equivalently consider
Z^{(\text{IID})}=\Phi^{-1}F_{V}\left(V^{(\text{IID})}\right)
, where
\{Z^{(\text{IID})}_{t}\}_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
is a sequence of i.i.d. standard normal variables.

For
V^{(\mathcal{G})}
we consider a general standard Gaussian process
Z^{(\mathcal{G})}
such that
V^{(\mathcal{G})}=F_{V}^{-1}\Phi(Z^{(\mathcal{G})})
has the same autocorrelation structure as
V
, i.e.
\rho(V^{(\mathcal{G})},t)=\rho(V,t)
. This allows us to compute
\rho\left(Z^{(\mathcal{G})},t\right)=\mathcal{T}^{-1}\rho(V,t)
by combining (
5.1
) and (
5.2
). Once
\rho\left(Z^{(\mathcal{G})},t\right)
is computed we can compute
\mathcal{E}_{b}\left(V^{(\mathcal{G})}\right)
by simulation of
Z^{(\mathcal{G})}
.

We further define
V^{(\text{AR1})}=F_{V}^{-1}\Phi Z^{(\text{AR1})}
. Here,
Z^{(\text{AR1})}
is the unique standardised Gaussian process with
\rho\left(Z^{(\text{AR1})},t\right)=\exp(-\theta t)
,
\theta=2.7\%
for any
t\in\mathbb{N}
. This makes
Z^{(\text{AR1})}
a Gaussian AR1 process. Note that
\theta=2.7\%
was chosen to minimise
\sum_{n=1}^{100}(\rho(V,n)-\rho(V^{(\text{AR1})},n))^{2}
. This simpler process can have several advantages over
Z^{(\mathcal{G})}
, such as being Markovian, which makes for more efficient simulation. Additionally, in
[
16
]
, this model was leveraged to enable an importance sampling technique for the construction of environmental contours.

Remark 5.1
.

It is common to calibrate AR1 models by considering only
\exp(-\theta)=\rho\left(Z^{(\text{AR1})},1\right)=\mathcal{T}^{-1}\rho\left(V,1\right)
which only requires
\rho\left(V,1\right)
. However, we can see from (
5.1
) and (
5.2
) that
\mathcal{T}^{-1}\rho\left(V,t\right)<(\mathcal{T}^{-1}\rho\left(V,1\right))^{t}
for all
1\leq t\leq 100
, which means that calibration based only on
\rho\left(V,1\right)
could significantly overestimate the autocorrelation. For reference,
\mathcal{T}^{-1}\rho\left(V,1\right)=\exp(-1.4\%)
which would nearly halve the value of
\theta
compared to the chosen method.

We can also consider a model in continuous time. Specifically, we define
V^{(\text{OU})}(t)=F_{V}^{-1}\Phi(Z^{(\text{OU})}_{t})
with

Z^{(\text{OU})}(t)=\sqrt{2\theta}\int_{-\infty}^{t}\exp\left(\theta(t-s)\right)dW_{s},

for any
t\in\mathbb{R}
where
W
is a standard Wiener process. This process is known as a standardised Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process and serves as a continuous interpolation of an AR1 process. In particular, the discrete process
t\mapsto Z^{(\text{OU})}(t),t\in\mathbb{Z}
equals
Z^{(\text{AR1})}
in distribution. We may also extend
\mathcal{E}_{b}(\cdot)
to our continuous-time process by
\mathcal{E}_{b}(V^{(\text{OU})})=\mathbb{E}\left[\inf\{t\in\mathbb{R},\,t\geq 0:V^{(\text{OU})}(t)>b\}\right]
. An explicit expression for this value (see e.g.
[
27
]
) is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{b}\left(V^{(\text{OU})}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\phi\left(\Phi^{-1}F_{V}(b)\right)-\phi\left(Z^{(\text{OU})}_{0}\right)\right)\mathds{1}(V^{(\text{OU})}_{0}<b)\right],

\displaystyle\phi(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\theta}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{(\sqrt{2}x)^{i}}{i!}\Gamma\left(\frac{i}{2}\right).

We also have a more convenient formulation in

\mathcal{E}_{b}\left(V^{(\text{OU})}\right)=\mathbb{E}\left[{\frac{\sqrt{2\pi}}{\theta}}\int_{Z^{(\text{OU})}_{0}\land\Phi^{-1}F_{V}(b)}^{\Phi^{-1}F_{V}(b)}\Phi(t)e^{t^{2}/2}dt\right],

which can be readily computed by Monte-Carlo simulation of
Z^{(\text{OU})}_{0}
, which has a standard normal distribution. Alternatively, we have the deterministic asymptotic approximation
\mathcal{E}_{b}\left(V^{(\text{OU})}\right)\approx\sqrt{2\pi}\exp((\Phi^{-1}F_{V}(b))^{2}/2)/(\theta\Phi^{-1}F_{V}(b))
, for high values of
b
.

It appears that the best fit is given by

Our other Markovian process,
V^{(\text{OU})}
, performs slightly worse. Since
V^{(\text{OU})}
is a continuous-time interpolation of
V^{(\text{AR1})}
we must have
\mathcal{E}_{b}(V^{(\text{OU})})<\mathcal{E}_{b}(V^{(\text{AR1})})
, which inherently makes
V^{(\text{OU})}
a more conservative model. It is also possible to consider
\mathcal{E}_{b}(V^{(\text{OU})})
as an approximation of
\mathcal{E}_{b}(V^{(\text{AR1})})
in the sense that their ratio approaches 1 for
\theta\to 0
. Consequently, we can use the explicit formula for
\mathcal{E}_{b}(V^{(\text{OU})})
to efficiently compute a conservative estimate of
\mathcal{E}_{b}(V^{(\text{AR1})})
, and hence
\mathcal{E}_{b}(V)
.

We have also included
V^{(\text{IID})}
as it is a commonly used simple and conservative model for the purposes of computing return periods. We here see that this model significantly underestimates the return period. In fact, one could consider this phenomenon theoretically by applying Slepian’s lemma. This result states that if
\rho(X,\cdot)\leq\rho(Y,\cdot)
for any two stationary standard normal Gaussian processes
X,Y
then

\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{0\leq t\leq t^{*}}X_{t}\leq x\right)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{0\leq t\leq t^{*}}Y_{t}\leq x\right),

for all
t^{*}\in\mathbb{N}
. Since
\rho(Z^{(\text{IID})},t)=0
for any
t>0
, and
0<\rho(Z^{(\mathcal{G})},\cdot),\rho(Z^{(\text{AR1})},\cdot)
, we get
\mathcal{E}_{b}(V^{(\text{IID})})\leq\mathcal{E}_{b}(V^{(\mathcal{G})}),\mathcal{E}_{b}(Z^{(\text{AR1})})
for all
b
. Comparison is more difficult for
Z^{(\text{IID})}
and
Z^{(\text{OU})}
, as
Z^{(\text{OU})}
is defined in continuous time. However, in
[
29
]
, it was shown that there exists some threshold
b^{*}
such that
\mathcal{E}_{b}(V^{(\text{IID})})>\mathcal{E}_{b}(V^{(\text{OU})})
for all
b>b^{*}
, while we generally see
\mathcal{E}_{b}(V^{(\text{IID})})<\mathcal{E}_{b}(V^{(\text{OU})})
for all
b<b^{*}
. For the calibrated value of
\theta
we have
b^{*}\approx
44.6 m/s, where we have
\mathcal{E}_{b^{*}}(V^{(\text{IID})})\approx\mathcal{E}_{b^{*}}(V^{(\text{OU})})\approx
1.7e7 years.

5.2.
Non-Stationary Models

As mentioned, there is a significant seasonal effect present in our dataset. In order to properly include this seasonality in our models we consider the following shape of the marginal distribution of
V
.

V_{t}\sim W(\lambda_{t},k_{t}),

i.e.
V_{t}
is Weibull distributed with scale
\lambda_{t}
and shape
k_{t}
. We further assume that
k
and
\lambda
are periodic functions with a period of one year. We may then model the cumulative distribution function of
V_{t}
by

F_{t}(x)=1-\exp\left(-\left(\frac{x}{\lambda_{t}}\right)^{k_{t}}\right).

This implies that
U_{t}\triangleq F_{t}(V_{t})
is constant in distribution. If we further assume that
U
is ergodic, we may apply
Proposition

3.4
to compute
\mathbb{E}[\tau_{\beta}(V,0)]
by
\mathbb{E}[\tau_{\alpha}(U,0)]
, with
\alpha_{s}=F_{s}(\beta_{s})
.

In calibrating
\lambda
and
k
we employ a similar method to what was used in
[
29
]
. To calibrate
k
we invert the equality

\frac{\Gamma(1+1/k(t))^{2}}{\Gamma(1+2/k(t))}=\frac{\mathbb{E}[V_{t}]^{2}}{\mathbb{E}[V_{t}^{2}]},

where
\Gamma
is the gamma function, and the expectations are computed using spline regression. We can then estimate
\lambda
by

\lambda_{t}=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{V_{t}}{\Gamma(1/k(t))}\right]

For the second non-stationary model we consider
t\mapsto F_{t}^{-1}\Phi(Z^{(\mathcal{G}2)}_{t})
where
Z^{(\mathcal{G}2)}
is a stationary standardised Gaussian process. We parametrise the autocorrelation of
Z^{(\mathcal{G}2)}

\rho(Z^{(\mathcal{G}2)},t)=\left(1+\kappa_{2}\left(\frac{t}{\zeta_{2}}\right)^{\eta_{2}}\right)^{-\frac{1}{\eta_{2}\kappa_{2}}},

where
\zeta_{2}=10.65,\,\eta_{2}=1.56,\,\kappa_{2}=0.83

\sum_{n=1}^{100}\left(\rho(Z,n)-\rho\left(Z^{(\mathcal{G}2)},n\right)\right)^{2},

where
Z_{t}=\Phi^{-1}F_{t}(V_{t})
.

Lastly, we consider a non-Gaussian copula process
V^{(\mathbf{t})}_{t}=F_{t}^{-1}\mathbf{t}_{\nu}(Y^{(\mathbf{t})}_{t})
, where
\mathbf{t}_{\nu}
is the student’s t cumulative distribution function with degrees of freedom
\nu
. Here,
Y^{(\mathbf{t})}
is the unique stationary Markovian process such that the joint distribution of
(Y^{(\mathbf{t})}_{0},Y^{(\mathbf{t})}_{1})

\nu=13.4
and correlation coefficient
\rho_{\mathbf{t}}=96.4\%
.

In order to compute the specified values for
\nu
and
\rho_{\mathbf{t}}
we consider the following.
The aim is to make
U^{(\mathbf{t})}=\mathbf{t}_{\nu}(Y^{(\mathbf{t})})

U

X

(5.3)

\lambda(X,b)\triangleq\mathbb{P}\left(X_{0}>b\big|X_{-1}>b\right).

\lim_{b\to 1}\lambda(U^{(\mathbf{t})},b)=2\mathbf{t}_{\nu+1}\left(-\sqrt{\frac{(\nu+1)(1-\rho_{\mathbf{t}})}{1+\rho_{\mathbf{t}}}}\right)

The parameters

were then chosen to minimise

\sum_{n=1}^{100}(\rho(U,n)-\rho(U^{(\mathbf{t})},n))^{2}

under the constraint of

\lim_{b\to 1}\lambda(U^{(\mathbf{t})},b)=\lambda(U,\text{1-1e-4})=61.5\%
.

It is worth mentioning that while
V^{({\mathcal{G}2})}
and
V^{(\mathbf{t})}
provide nearly identical return periods, they have quite different asymptotic properties. To examine these asymptotics we first denote
U^{(\mathbf{t})}=\mathbf{t}_{\nu}(Y^{(\mathbf{t})})
and
U^{(\mathcal{G}2)}=\Phi(Z^{(\mathcal{G}2)})
in order to reconcile their marginal distributions. We previously mentioned that
\lim_{b\to 1}\lambda(U^{(\mathbf{t})},b)=61.5\%
, and since
Z^{(\mathcal{G}2)}
is a Gaussian process we further have that
\lim_{b\to 1}\lambda(U^{(\mathcal{G}2)},b)=0
. This asymptotic tail dependence is directly related to another important concept, namely the
up-crossing rate
, defined for any stationary process,
X
, as

(5.5)

\mu(X,b)\triangleq\mathbb{P}\left(X_{0}>b,\,X_{-1}\leq b\right).

To see how (
5.3
) and (
5.5
) are related we note the following. For any stationary process
X
we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(X_{0}>b))=

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(X_{0}>b,\,X_{-1}\leq b\right)

\displaystyle+\mathbb{P}\left(X_{0}>b\big|X_{-1}\leq b\right)\mathbb{P}\left(X_{-1}>b\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mu(X,b)+\lambda(X,b)\mathbb{P}(X_{0}>b)),

which implies

(5.6)

1-\lambda(X,b)=\frac{\mu(X,b)}{\mathbb{P}(X_{0}>b))}.

A particular consequence of this is

(5.7)

\lim_{b\to 1}\frac{\mu(U^{(\mathbf{t})},b)}{\mu(U^{(\mathcal{G}2)},b)}=\lim_{b\to 1}\frac{\mu(U^{(\mathbf{t})},b)}{\mu(U^{(\text{IS})},b)}=1-\lim_{b\to 1}\lambda(U^{(\mathbf{t})},b)=38.5\%

A higher upcrossing rate usually implies a lower average time until next exceedance. As such we can expect that
\mathcal{E}_{b}(V^{({\mathcal{G}2})})/\mathcal{E}_{b}(V^{({\mathbf{t}})})
will behave similar to (
5.7
) for sufficiently high values of
b
.

As such, we have two models with significantly different asymptotic properties, that mostly agree in our chosen regime of moderate values of
b
. This serves as a reminder that model performance in our chosen regime is insufficient to guarantee that asymptotic properties of the true model are accurately captured.

We may finally note that since
\lambda(X,b)\to 0
for any ergodic Gaussian process
X
, we have
\lambda(X,b)\leq\lambda(Z,b)
for sufficiently large
b
, implying
\mu(X,b)\geq\mu(Z,b)
. Since
X
will exhibit more frequent upcrossings than
Z
we can usually expect that
\mathcal{E}_{b}(X)<\mathcal{E}_{b}(Z)
. Consequently, we can consider the use of Gaussian copula models as an asymptotically conservative estimate of
\mathcal{E}_{b}(V)
.

In summary, Gaussian copula models can accurately capture
\mathcal{E}_{b}(V)
in our chosen regime, while providing a conservative estimate in the asymptotic regime, where data is sparse to non-existent.

Remark 5.2
.

In order to expand on the implications of the difference in asymptotic properties for
U^{(\mathbf{t})}
and
U^{(\mathcal{G}2)}
we consider two concepts closely related to tail dependence, the extremal index and cluster sizes.

To define the extremal index of a stationary process,
\theta(X)
, we assume the following. If, for any
v\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
and sequence
\{u_{n}^{(v)}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathbb{R}
such that

\lim_{n\to\infty}n\mathbb{P}(X_{0}>u_{n}^{(v)})=v

then

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(\max_{t=1,\cdots,n}X_{t}\leq u_{n}^{(v)})=e^{-\theta(X)v},

if the extremal index exists. For a more comprehensive definition and discussion of the extremal index, including conditions for existence, we refer to
[
2
]
.

By cluster size, we refer to the average time spent above a threshold
b
after an upcrossing. Specifically, for any stationary process
X
, we define

C(X,b)=\mathbb{E}[\min\{i:X_{i}\leq b\}|X_{0}>b,X_{-1}\leq b].

In
[
9
]
they discuss estimating the extremal index by means of the tail dependence. For example, if
V
satisfies a specific technical condition (
D^{2}(u_{n}^{(v)})
, as introduced in
[
4
]
), it was proved in
[
10
]
that
\theta(V)=1-\lim_{b\to\infty}\lambda(V,b)
.

Similarly, in
[
25
]
, the relation
C(X,b)^{-1}=\mu(X,b)/\mathbb{P}(X_{0}>b)
was shown for a general ergodic process
X
. By (
5.6
) we therefore have
C(X,b)^{-1}=1-\lambda(X,b)
. Under
D^{2}(u_{n}^{(v)})
we would lastly get the relation
\theta(X)^{-1}=\lim_{b\to\infty}C(X,b)
.

Appendix A
Technical Results and Proofs

In this section we aim to provide a rigorous proof of convergence for our estimator

\mathbb{E}[f(\tau_{\beta}(V,0))]\approx\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}f(\widehat{\tau}_{\beta}(V,t))

where
f
is any function such that
\mathbb{E}[|f(\tau_{\beta}(V,0))|]<\infty
and

\widehat{\tau}_{\beta}(T;V,t)=\begin{cases}\tau_{\beta}(V,t)&\tau_{\beta}(V,t)<T-t\\
T-t+\Lambda(T;V,t)&\tau_{\beta}(V,t)\geq T-t.\end{cases}

Note that we, for the sake of generality, no longer assume the specific shape of
\Lambda
given in (
3.4
).

To guarantee convergence of our estimator we first introduce the concept of
transit times
.

Definition A.1
.

Assume
\widetilde{\beta}\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
. For any process
V
and time
T
we may define

\theta_{\widetilde{\beta}}^{-}(V,T)\triangleq\max(t\leq T:V_{t}\in\widetilde{\beta}),\quad\theta_{\widetilde{\beta}}^{+}(V,T)\triangleq\min(t\geq T:V_{t}\in\widetilde{\beta}).

Note the ordering
\theta_{\widetilde{\beta}}^{-}(V,T)\leq T\leq\theta_{\widetilde{\beta}}^{+}(V,T)
.
We can then further define the transit time at time
T\in\mathbb{Z}
by,

\tau^{\pm}_{\widetilde{\beta}}(V,T)\triangleq\max(\theta_{\widetilde{\beta}}^{+}(V,T)-\theta_{\widetilde{\beta}}^{-}(V,T)-1,0).

Using this concept we can bound the difference between sums of
f(\tau_{\beta}(V,t))
and sums of
f(\widehat{\tau}_{\beta}(V,t))

Lemma A.2
.

Assume the threshold function,
\beta
, satisfies
\beta_{s}\supseteq\widetilde{\beta}
for some measurable
\widetilde{\beta}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N}
and all
s\geq 0
. Further fix a function
f:\mathbb{N}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
and assume that

|f(t)|\leq C_{1}+C_{2}t^{p},

for some
p,\,C_{1},\,C_{2}\in[0,\infty)
.

Under these assumptions we have, for any
q\in[0,\infty)
that

\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\big|f(\tau_{\beta}(V,t))-f(\widehat{\tau}_{\beta}(T;V,t))\big|^{q}\leq\mathcal{P}_{p,q}(T,\overline{\Lambda}_{T}).

Here

\mathcal{P}_{p,q}(T,C)={\tau^{\pm}_{\widetilde{\beta}}(V,T)}\Big((C_{1}+C_{2}\tau^{\pm}_{\widetilde{\beta}}(V,T)^{p})^{q}+(C_{1}+C_{2}(\tau^{\pm}_{\widetilde{\beta}}(V,T)+C)^{p})^{q}\Big),

and

\overline{\Lambda}_{T}=\sup_{T^{\prime}\geq T}\sup_{0\leq t<T^{\prime}}\Lambda(T^{\prime};V,t).

Proof.

Note that if
t\leq\theta_{\widetilde{\beta}}^{-}(V,T)
then
\tau_{\beta}(V,t)\leq\theta_{\widetilde{\beta}}^{-}(V,T)-t\leq T-t
, which implies
\tau_{\beta}(V,t)=\widehat{\tau}_{\beta}(T;V,t)
. We also get, for any
t\in\mathbb{N}
with
\theta_{\widetilde{\beta}}^{-}(V,T)<t\leq\theta_{\widetilde{\beta}}^{+}(V,T)
, that
\tau_{\beta}(V,t)\leq\theta_{\widetilde{\beta}}^{+}(V,T)-t\leq\tau^{\pm}_{\widetilde{\beta}}(V,T)
.

This yields

\displaystyle\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\big|f(\tau_{\beta}(V,t))-f(\widehat{\tau}_{\beta}(T;V,t))\big|^{q}

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathds{1}_{\tau_{\beta}(t)\geq T-t}\big|f(\tau_{\beta}(V,t))-f(T-t+\Lambda(T;V,t))\big|^{q}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t=(\theta_{\widetilde{\beta}}^{-}(V,T)+1)\lor 0}^{\theta_{\widetilde{\beta}}^{+}(V,T)\land(T-1)}\big|f(\tau_{\beta}(V,t))-f(T-t+\Lambda(T;V,t))\big|^{q}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t=(\theta_{\widetilde{\beta}}^{-}(V,T)+1)\lor 0}^{\theta_{\widetilde{\beta}}^{+}(V,T)\land(T-1)}\left(\big|C_{1}+C_{2}\tau^{\pm}_{\widetilde{\beta}}(V,T)^{p}\big|^{q}+\big|C_{1}+C_{2}(\tau^{\pm}_{\widetilde{\beta}}(V,T)+\Lambda(T;V,t))^{p}\big|^{q}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\tau^{\pm}_{\widetilde{\beta}}(V,T)\Big((C_{1}+C_{2}\tau^{\pm}_{\widetilde{\beta}}(V,T)^{p})^{q}+(C_{1}+C_{2}(\tau^{\pm}_{\widetilde{\beta}}(V,T)+\overline{\Lambda}_{T})^{p})^{q}\Big).

∎

Since
\mathcal{P}_{p,q}(T,C)
is asymptotically a power function of
\tau^{\pm}_{\widetilde{\beta}}(V,T)
we can guarantee different forms of convergence under moment conditions on
\tau^{\pm}_{\widetilde{\beta}}(V,0)
. To simplify our assumptions we first prove that finite moments
\tau^{\pm}_{\widetilde{\beta}}(V,0)
is equivalent to moments of
\tau_{\widetilde{\beta}}(V,0)
.

Lemma A.3
.

Assume
\widetilde{\beta}\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
.

If, for any
\alpha\in[0,\infty)
, we have
\mathbb{E}[\tau_{\widetilde{\beta}}(V,t)^{\alpha}]<\infty
, then
\mathbb{E}[\tau^{\pm}_{\widetilde{\beta}}(V,t)^{\alpha}]<\infty
.

Proof.

First denote the backwards hitting time
\tau_{\widetilde{\beta}}^{-}(V,t)\triangleq\min\{s\geq 0:V_{t-s}\in\beta_{s}\}
.

Further define
H^{i}_{j}=\{\omega:\tau_{\widetilde{\beta}}^{-}(V,t)=i,\,\tau_{\widetilde{\beta}}(V,t)=j\}
, and note that by ergodicity we have
\mathbb{P}(H^{i}_{j})=\mathbb{P}(S^{i-1}(H^{i}_{j}))=\mathbb{P}(H^{1}_{j+i-1})
for
i,j\geq 1
. The disjoint sets
\{H^{i}_{j}\}_{i,j\in\mathbb{N}}
forms the basis of the
Kakutani skyscraper
, and are commonly applied in relation to transit times of ergodic processes (see e.g.
[
8
]
).

To simplify notation, we will introduce
k=i+j
as an index variable. For any
\alpha\in[0,\infty)
, this yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\tau_{\widetilde{\beta}}(V,t)^{\alpha}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(H^{i}_{j})j^{\alpha}=\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\mathbb{P}(H^{i}_{j})j^{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\mathbb{P}(H^{1}_{k-1})j^{\alpha}=\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(H^{1}_{k-1})\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}j^{\alpha}

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(H^{1}_{k-1})\int_{0}^{k-1}j^{\alpha}dj=\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(H^{1}_{k-1})\frac{(k-1)^{\alpha+1}}{\alpha+1}.

Similarly, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\tau^{\pm}_{\widetilde{\beta}}(V,t)^{\alpha}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(H^{i}_{j}){(i+j-1)}^{\alpha}=\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\mathbb{P}(H^{i}_{j})(i+j-1)^{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\mathbb{P}(H^{1}_{k-1})(k-1)^{\alpha}=\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(H^{1}_{k-1})\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}(k-1)^{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(H^{1}_{k-1})(k-1)^{\alpha+1}\leq(\alpha+1)\mathbb{E}[\tau_{\widetilde{\beta}}(V,t)^{\alpha}]<\infty.

∎

With this we can control the error arising from replacing
\tau_{\widetilde{\beta}}(V,t)
by
\widehat{\tau}_{\widetilde{\beta}}(V,t)
.

Lemma A.4
.

Assume the threshold function,
\beta
, satisfies
\beta_{s}\supseteq\widetilde{\beta}
for some measurable
\widetilde{\beta}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N}
and all
s\geq 0
with
\mathbb{E}\left[\tau_{\widetilde{\beta}}^{pq+1}\right]<\infty
for some
p,q\in[0,\infty)
.

Fix the functions
f:\mathbb{N}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
and
\Lambda:\mathbb{N}\times(\mathbb{R}^{N})^{\mathbb{Z}}\times\mathbb{N}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
and assume that

\limsup_{T}\sup_{0\leq t<T}|\Lambda(T;V,t)|<\infty,\quad|f(t)|\leq C_{1}+C_{2}t^{p},

almost surely for some
C_{1},\,C_{2}\in[0,\infty)
.

Under these assumptions we have that

\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\big|f(\tau_{\beta}(V,t))-f(\widehat{\tau}_{\beta}(T;V,t))\big|^{q}=0,

almost surely.

Proof.

We start by setting
C_{3}
to be any number such that
C_{3}>\limsup_{T}|\Lambda(T;V,t)|
and
C_{3}\in\mathbb{Q}
. We may then assume without loss of generality that
T
is sufficiently large to ensure
C_{3}\geq\sup_{T^{\prime}\geq T}\sup_{0\leq t<T^{\prime}}|\Lambda(T^{\prime};V,t)|
. By
Lemma

A.2
, we have

\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\big|f(\tau_{\beta}(V,t))-f(\widehat{\tau}_{\beta}(T;V,t))\big|^{q}\leq\frac{1}{T}\mathcal{P}_{p,q}(T,C_{3}).

We note, for any deterministic
C
, that
\mathcal{P}_{p,q}(T,C)
has a finite expectation due to our assuption of
\mathbb{E}\left[\tau_{\widetilde{\beta}}^{pq+1}\right]<\infty
and
Lemma

A.3
. This implies, by
Theorem

2.5
, that

\displaystyle\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\mathcal{P}_{p,q}(T,C)

\displaystyle=\lim_{T\to\infty}\left(\frac{T+1}{T}\right)\frac{1}{T+1}\sum_{t=0}^{T}\mathcal{P}_{p,q}(t,C)-\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathcal{P}_{p,q}(t,C)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{P}_{p,q}(0,C)\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{P}_{p,q}(0,C)\right]

\displaystyle=0,

almost surely. This further implies that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\mathcal{P}_{p,q}(T,C)=0\text{ for all }C\in\mathbb{Q}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcap_{C\in\mathbb{Q}}\left\{\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\mathcal{P}_{p,q}(T,C)=0\right\}\right)

\displaystyle=1-\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcup_{C\in\mathbb{Q}}\left\{\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\mathcal{P}_{p,q}(T,C)\neq 0\right\}\right)

\displaystyle\geq 1-\sum_{C\in\mathbb{Q}}\mathbb{P}\left(\left\{\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\mathcal{P}_{p,q}(T,C)\neq 0\right\}\right)

\displaystyle=1.

This ensures that
\mathcal{P}_{p,q}(T,C_{3})/T
converges almost surely to
0
, and therefore that
\lim_{T\to\infty}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\big|f(\tau_{\beta}(V,t))-f(\widehat{\tau}_{\beta}(T;V,t))\big|^{q}/T=0
almost surely.

∎

Remark A.5

In the case where

in the proof of

.

With

A.4
we can guarantee that our estimator converges almost surely to the desired moment. This result also allows for the computation of the distribution of

Theorem A.6
.

Assume that the threshold function
\beta
satisfies
\beta_{s}\supseteq\widetilde{\beta}
for some measurable
\widetilde{\beta}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N}
and all
s\geq 0
.

Fix the functions
f:\mathbb{N}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
and
\Lambda:\mathbb{N}\times(\mathbb{R}^{N})^{\mathbb{Z}}\times\mathbb{N}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
and assume that

\limsup_{T}\sup_{0\leq t<T}|\Lambda(T;V,t)|<\infty,\quad|f(\tau)|\leq C_{1}+C_{2}\tau^{p},

almost surely for some
C_{1},\,C_{2}\in[0,\infty)
. Under these conditions we have the following.

If
\mathbb{E}\left[\tau_{\widetilde{\beta}}\right]<\infty
, then

\mathbb{P}\left(f(\tau_{\beta}(V,0))>s\right)=\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathds{1}(f(\widehat{\tau}_{\beta}(T;v,t)>s)).

If
\mathbb{E}\left[\tau_{\widetilde{\beta}}^{p+1}\right]<\infty
, then

\mathbb{E}\left[f(\tau_{\beta}(V,0))\right]=\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}f(\widehat{\tau}_{\beta}(T;V,t)).

If
\mathbb{E}\left[\tau_{\widetilde{\beta}}^{2p+1}\right]<\infty
, then

\mathbb{E}\left[f(\tau_{\beta}(V,0))f(\tau_{\beta}(V,i))\right]=\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=i}^{T-1}f(\widehat{\tau}_{\beta}(T;V,t-i))f(\widehat{\tau}_{\beta}(T;V,t)).

Proof.

The first two equations are immediate consequences of
Lemma

A.4
with
q=1
. For the third equation, we define
a_{t,T}=f(\widehat{\tau}_{\beta}(T;V,t))
and
\alpha_{t}=f(\tau_{\beta}(V,t))
as shorthand notations. We then get by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality that

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}\left[f(\tau_{\beta}(V,0))f(\tau_{\beta}(V,i))\right]-\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=i}^{T-1}a_{t,T}a_{t-i,T}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=i}^{T-1}\left|a_{t,T}a_{t-i,T}-\alpha_{t}\alpha_{t-i}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=i}^{T-1}\Big(\left|a_{t,T}(a_{t-i,T}-\alpha_{t-i})\right|+\left|(a_{t,T}-\alpha_{t})\alpha_{t-1}\right|\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{T\to\infty}\sqrt{\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=i}^{T-1}a_{t,T}^{2}}\sqrt{\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=i}^{T-1}(a_{t-i,T}-\alpha_{t-i})^{2}}

\displaystyle\quad+\lim_{T\to\infty}\sqrt{\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=i}^{T-1}\alpha_{t-i}^{2}}\sqrt{\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=i}^{T-1}(a_{t,T}-\alpha_{t})^{2}}.

By
Theorem

2.5
and
Lemma

A.4
(for
q=2
), this limit is 0.

∎

Remark A.7
.

As per

. We may omit the conditions

Now that we have proven our law of large numbers, we can move on to our last major result, the central limit theorem for our adjusted estimator.

Theorem A.8
.

Assume the threshold function,
\beta
, satisfies
\beta_{s}\supseteq\widetilde{\beta}
for some measurable
\widetilde{\beta}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N}
and all
s\geq 0
with
\mathbb{E}\left[\tau_{\widetilde{\beta}}^{\max(2p,1+p)}\right]<\infty
for some
p\in[0,\infty)
.

Fix the functions
f:\mathbb{N}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
and
\Lambda:\mathbb{N}\times(\mathbb{R}^{N})^{\mathbb{Z}}\times\mathbb{N}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
and assume that

\limsup_{T}\sup_{0\leq t<T}|\Lambda(T;V,t)|<\infty,\quad|f(t)|\leq C_{1}+C_{2}t^{p},

almost surely for some
C_{1},\,C_{2}\in[0,\infty)
.

Define
\mathcal{M}_{T}=\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}f(\tau_{\beta}(V,t))
,
\widehat{\mathcal{M}}_{T}=\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}f(\widehat{\tau}_{\beta}(T;V,t))
, and
\sigma_{T}^{2}=\mathbb{V}ar[\mathcal{M}_{T}^{2}]
. Note that
\mathbb{E}\left[\tau_{\widetilde{\beta}}^{2p}\right]<\infty
implies that
\sigma_{T}<\infty
.

Further assume that
t\mapsto\tau_{\beta}(V,t)
satisfies the remaining conditions of
Theorem

2.7
. This entails that
t\mapsto\tau_{\beta}(V,t)
is a strongly mixing process with
\lim_{T\to\infty}\sigma_{T}=\infty
, and that the family
\{\mathcal{M}_{T}^{2}/\sigma_{T}^{2}\}_{T\in\mathbb{N}}
is uniformly integrable.

We then have that
(\widehat{\mathcal{M}}_{T}-\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{M}_{T}])/\sigma_{T}
converges in distribution to a standard normal random variable.

Proof.

From
Theorem

2.7
we know that
(\mathcal{M}_{T}-\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{M}_{T}])/\sigma_{T}
converges in distribution to a standard normal random variable.

We also have the following standard convergence result for sequences of random variables (see e.g.
[
31
]
). If two sequences,
\{A_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}},\,\{B_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies
A_{n}\to A
in distribution and
|A_{n}-B_{n}|\to 0
in probability, then
B_{n}\to A
in distribution.

Therefore it suffices to show that
|\mathcal{M}_{T}-\widehat{\mathcal{M}}_{T}|/\sigma_{T}\to 0
in probability. By
Lemma

A.2
we have

\frac{|\mathcal{M}_{T}-\widehat{\mathcal{M}}_{T}|}{\sigma_{T}}\leq\frac{\mathcal{P}_{p,1}(T,\overline{\Lambda}_{T})}{\sigma_{T}},

where
\overline{\Lambda}_{T}=\sup_{T^{\prime}\geq T}\sup_{0\leq t<T^{\prime}}\Lambda(T^{\prime};V,t)
.

Since
\limsup_{T\to\infty}\overline{\Lambda}_{T}<\infty
almost surely, we have
\lim_{T\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(\overline{\Lambda}_{T}=\infty)=0
. Consequently, for any
\delta>0
, there must be some
T_{1}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\mathbb{P}(\overline{\Lambda}_{T_{1}}=\infty)<\delta/3
. We can then further pick a
C_{\text{min}}\in[0,\infty)
such that
\mathbb{P}(\overline{\Lambda}_{T_{1}}>C_{\text{min}})<\delta/2
. Next, by the fact that
T\mapsto\overline{\Lambda}_{T}
is monotone non-increasing, we have

\mathbb{P}(\overline{\Lambda}_{T}>C_{\text{min}})<\delta/2,

for all
T\geq T_{1}
.

We further note, for any deterministic
C
, that
T\mapsto\mathcal{P}_{p,1}(T,C)
is a finite and stationary sequence. Consequently, since
\sigma_{T}\to\infty
, we get
{\mathcal{P}_{p,1}(T,C)}/{\sigma_{T}}\to 0
in probability. As such, there must be some
T_{2}\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{\mathcal{P}_{p,1}(T,C_{\text{min}})}{\sigma_{T}}>\epsilon\right)<\delta/2,

for all
T\geq T_{2}
.

Finally, we note that
C\mapsto\mathcal{P}_{p,1}(T,C)
is monotone non-decreasing, which implies

\mathcal{P}_{p,1}(T,C_{\text{min}})\geq\mathcal{P}_{p,1}(T,\overline{\Lambda}_{T}),

conditional on
\overline{\Lambda}_{T}\leq C_{\text{min}}
.

Combining these equations we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left({\mathcal{P}_{p,1}(T,\overline{\Lambda}_{T})}/{\sigma_{T}}>\epsilon\right)=\,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{P}_{p,1}(T,\overline{\Lambda}_{T})/\sigma_{T}>\epsilon\big|\overline{\Lambda}_{T}>C_{\text{min}}\right)\mathbb{P}\left(\overline{\Lambda}_{T}>C_{\text{min}}\right)

\displaystyle+\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{P}_{p,1}(T,\overline{\Lambda}_{T})/\sigma_{T}>\epsilon\big|\overline{\Lambda}_{T}\leq C_{\text{min}}\right)\mathbb{P}\left(\overline{\Lambda}_{T}\leq C_{\text{min}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\overline{\Lambda}_{T}>C_{\text{min}}\right)

\displaystyle+\mathbb{P}\left(\{\mathcal{P}_{p,1}(T,\overline{\Lambda}_{T})/\sigma_{T}>\epsilon\}\bigcap\{\overline{\Lambda}_{T}\leq C_{\text{min}}\}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\,

\displaystyle\delta/2+\mathbb{P}\left(\{\mathcal{P}_{p,1}(T,C_{\text{min}})/\sigma_{T}>\epsilon\}\bigcap\{\overline{\Lambda}_{T}\leq C_{\text{min}}\}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\,

\displaystyle\delta/2+\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{P}_{p,1}(T,C_{\text{min}})/\sigma_{T}>\epsilon\right)

\displaystyle<\,

\displaystyle\delta,

for all
T\geq\max(T_{1},T_{2})
. As such we have
{\mathcal{P}_{p,1}(T,\overline{\Lambda}_{T})}/{\sigma_{T}}\to 0
, in probability, which implies
(\widehat{\mathcal{M}}_{T}-T\mathbb{E}[f(\tau(V,0))])/\sigma_{T}
converges, in distribution, to a standard normal random variable.

∎

Lastly we prove the general form of (
3.9
), i.e. our simplified formula for our estimator in the case where
\beta
is constant.

Proposition A.9
.

Let
\beta_{s}=\widetilde{\beta}
for all
s
and define,
\Theta
, the set of all observed points where the process is outside
\widetilde{\beta}
, i.e.
\Theta\triangleq\{\theta\in\mathbb{N}:\theta<T,\,V_{\theta}\in\widetilde{\beta}\}
. We assume that the size of this set,
|\Theta|
, is at least
1
. We denote by
\theta_{i}
,
i\leq|\Theta|
the
i
’th element of
\Theta
, and define
\theta_{|\Theta|+1}=T+\theta_{1}
. We lastly introduce the shorthand
\Delta\theta_{i}=\theta_{i+1}-\theta_{i}
. This yields, for any
f:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{R}
, that

\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\widehat{\tau}_{\beta}(t)^{k}=\sum_{i=1}^{|\Theta|}\sum_{j=0}^{\Delta\theta_{i}-1}\frac{f(j)}{T},

where we again use

\widehat{\tau}_{\beta}(t)=\min\{s\geq 0:V_{t+s\text{ mod }T}\in\beta_{s}\},\quad a\text{ mod }b=a-b\lfloor a/b\rfloor.

Proof.

We note that for all
t
we have
t\leq\theta_{i(t)}
for some minimal
i(t)
, which implies
\widehat{\tau}_{\beta}(t)=\theta_{i(t)}-t
. This means that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}f(\widehat{\tau}_{\beta}(t))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\left(\sum_{t=0}^{\theta_{1}}f(\widehat{\tau}_{\beta}(t))+\sum_{i=1}^{|\Theta|-1}\sum_{t=\theta_{i}+1}^{\theta_{i+1}}f(\widehat{\tau}_{\beta}(t))+\sum_{t=\theta_{|\Theta|}}^{T-1}f(\widehat{\tau}_{\beta}(t))\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\left(\sum_{t=0}^{\theta_{1}}f\left(\theta_{1}-t\right)+\sum_{i=1}^{|\Theta|-1}\sum_{t=\theta_{i}+1}^{\theta_{i+1}}f\left(\theta_{i+1}-t\right)+\sum_{t=\theta_{|\Theta|}}^{T-1}f\left(\theta_{1}+T-t\right)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\left(\sum_{t=T}^{\theta_{1}+T}f\left(\theta_{1}+T-t\right)+\sum_{i=1}^{|\Theta|-1}\sum_{t=\theta_{i}+1}^{\theta_{i+1}}f\left(\theta_{i+1}-t\right)+\sum_{t=\theta_{|\Theta|}}^{T-1}f\left(\theta_{1}+T-t\right)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{|\Theta|}\sum_{t=\theta_{i}+1}^{\theta_{i+1}}f\left(\theta_{i+1}-t\right)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{|\Theta|}\sum_{j=0}^{\Delta\theta_{i}-1}\frac{f(j)}{T}.

∎
