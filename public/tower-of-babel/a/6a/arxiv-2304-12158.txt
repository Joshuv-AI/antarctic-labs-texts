On the Computability of Measures of Regular Sets of Infinite Trees Thanks: Work supported by the National Science Centre, Poland (grant no. 2021/41/B/ST6/03914).
Damian Niwiński, Paweł Parys, Michał Skrzypczak

Abstract
The Rabin tree theorem yields an algorithm to solve the satisfiability problem for monadic second - order logic over infinite trees. Here we solve the probabilistic variant of this problem. Namely, we show how to compute the probability that a randomly chosen tree satisfies a given formula. We additionally show that this probability is an algebraic number. This closes a line of research where similar results were shown for formalisms weaker than the full monadic second - order logic.



1
Introduction

The Rabin tree theorem had an essential impact on formal methods in computer science, thanks to both the large applicability of the result and the strength of techniques introduced in the proof
[
Rab69
]
.
Basically, it reduces the satisfiability problem for monadic second
-
order logic (MSO) over the infinite binary tree
to the non
-
emptiness problem of finite automata running over labelled infinite trees, which yields decidability.
The acceptance criterion used in the automata generalises previous ideas of Büchi
[
Büc62
]
.
The result gave rise to the whole area of automata-based methods in verification, whose realm of applications goes far beyond the original theory of the tree,
see e.g.,
[
Tho96
,
FGW08
,
DGL16
,
Pin21
]
for surveys.

The potential of automata on infinite trees for probabilistic questions in verification has been noted by several authors
[
CDK12
,
Mio12
,
CHS14
]
.
The space of trees can be naturally equipped with a Cantor
-
like topology as well as the probability measure,
where we assume that each letter is chosen independently at random (called
coin
-
flipping measure
).
The following question can be viewed as a probabilistic counterpart of the problem solved by Rabin.

Problem 1.1
.

Given a representation of a regular tree language
L
, find the probability that a randomly generated tree belongs to
L
.

An analogous question for regular languages of
\omega
-
words can be traced back to the early work on verification of finite-state probabilistic systems initiated, among others, by Vardi
[
Var85
]
.
An efficient solution follows from the work by Courcoubetis and Yannakakis
[
CY95
]
(see also
[
Var99
]
).
It has been noted by several authors that, for the coin
-
flipping measure, the probability in question is rational (see, e.g.,
[
CJH04
,
Izu01
]
).

In the case of infinite trees, it is not even evident that the probability in question always exists, as a regular tree language need not in general be Borel
[
NW03
]
.
However, it was established by Gogacz et al.
[
GMMS17
]
that all regular languages of trees are measurable.

Chen et al.
[
CDK12
]
addressed the question for trees in the case where the tree language
L
is recognised by a deterministic top
-
down parity automaton
and the measure is induced by a stochastic branching process, which then makes also a part of the input data.
Their algorithm compares the probability with any given rational number in polynomial space and with
0
or
1
in polynomial time.

Michalewski and Mio
[
MM15
]
gave an algorithm for a larger class of languages
L
given by so
-
called
game automata
.
Their algorithm (given for the coin
-
flipping measure) reduces the problem to computing the value of a Markov branching play, and uses Tarski’s decision procedure for the theory of reals.
These authors also discovered that the measure of a regular tree language can be irrational.
Later on, Niwiński et al.
[
NPS20
]
solved the problem for weak alternating automata, which capture precisely weak MSO logic (with quantifiers restricted to finite sets).

In this paper we ultimately solve
1.1
for nondeterministic parity tree automata, which capture the whole MSO.

Theorem 1.2
.

There is an algorithm that inputs a nondeterministic parity tree automaton
\mathcal{A}
and computes the coin
-
flipping measure of the language of trees recognised by
\mathcal{A}
.
Moreover, this measure is an algebraic number.

As a consequence, given additionally a rational (or just algebraic) number
q
, we can decide if the resulting measure is equal, smaller, or greater than
q
.

Our algorithm is able to produce a representation of the above measure in three
-
fold exponential time.
The decision problems with a fixed rational number
q
can be solved in two
-
fold exponential space.
These complexity upper bounds coincide with the ones provided by Przybyłko and Skrzypczak
[
PS20
]
in the case of safety automata.

Instead of considering the coin
-
flipping measure over a full binary tree,
one may also be interested in trees generated by a given stochastic branching process
[
CDK12
,
NPS20
]
.
It was already observed by Niwiński et al.
[
NPS20
, Section 7.1]
that such a situation reduces to the basic case of the coin
-
flipping measure:
essentially, instead of an automaton
\mathcal{A}
reading a tree generated by a branching process
\mathcal{P}
,
we may consider an automaton being a product of
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{P}
, reading a binary tree chosen randomly according to the coin-flipping measure.
We thus have the following corollary, with the same complexity bounds as for
Theorem

1.2
.

Corollary 1.3
.

There is an algorithm that inputs a stochastic branching process
\mathcal{P}
and a nondeterministic parity tree automaton
\mathcal{A}

and computes the measure induced by
\mathcal{P}
of the language of trees recognised by
\mathcal{A}
.
Again, this measure is an algebraic number.

It is worth to note that natural stronger probabilistic variants of the satisfiability problem are known to be undecidable.
For example, both the non
-
emptiness problem
[
Paz71
]
and the value
-
1
problem
[
GO10
]
for probabilistic finite automata, even for finite words, are undecidable.
Moreover, Baier et al.
[
BBG08
]
showed that it is undecidable whether there exists an
\omega
-
word that is accepted by a given probabilistic Büchi automaton with a positive probability.
So presumably we are close to the frontiers of decidability here.

One should note that our result does not entail the Rabin tree theorem—the satisfiability problem does not directly reduce to its probabilistic variant.
Also, the core of the construction of Rabin was a translation between an MSO formula and an automaton, whereas we begin our construction already with a given automaton.

The main difficulty while considering probabilistic questions about automata lies in nondeterminism, which breaks a correspondence between input objects and runs of the automaton.
It is known that in the case of infinite trees nondeterminism is inherent.
As a remedy, we take the
\mu
-
calculus perspective on tree automata.
Indeed, it is well-known that
\mu
-
calculus (even in a restricted form) interpreted over trees captures the full power of tree automata
[
Niw88
]

(the opposite direction follows easily from the Rabin tree theorem).

This helps to a large degree, but not fully.
Having two random events
A
,
B
we can speak about their union
A\cup B
and intersection
A\cap B
,
but knowing only the probabilities
\prob(A)
,
\prob(B)
we cannot say what is the probability of
\prob(A{\cup}B)
or
\prob(A{\cap}B)
.
A similar problem arises with other functions of arity greater than
1
.
In order to overcome this difficulty, we introduce a new formalism:
unary
\mu
-
calculus
.
We believe that this formalism is of independent interest, and can potentially find other applications.

This paper extends a conference version published at LICS 2023
[
NPS23
]
.

Basic concepts

Trees and automata

A
tree
over an
alphabet

\Sigma
is a function
t\colon\{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle L}},\mathtt{{\scriptstyle R}}\}^{\ast}\to\Sigma
.
Here
\mathtt{{\scriptstyle L}},\mathtt{{\scriptstyle R}}
represent the directions leading from a
node

v\in\{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle L}},\mathtt{{\scriptstyle R}}\}^{\ast}
to its children
v\mathtt{{\scriptstyle L}}
and
v\mathtt{{\scriptstyle R}}
.
The empty word
\varepsilon
is the
root
of the tree.
A
subtree
of a tree
t
starting in a node
v
is a tree denoted
t.v
defined by
t.v(w)=t(vw)
, for all
w\in\{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle L}},\mathtt{{\scriptstyle R}}\}^{\ast}
.
The set of all trees over
\Sigma
is denoted
\mathrm{Tr}_{\Sigma}
.

A
nondeterministic
\max
-
parity tree automaton
is defined to be
\mathcal{A}=\langle\Sigma,\allowbreak Q,\allowbreak q_{I},\allowbreak\gamma,\allowbreak\Omega\rangle
, where

\Sigma
is a finite input alphabet,

Q
a finite set of
states
,

q_{I}\in Q
an
initial state
,

\gamma\subseteq Q\times\Sigma\times Q\times Q
a
transition
relation,
and
\Omega\colon Q\to\{0,\ldots,d{-}1\}
assigns a
priority
to each state.
1
1

1

For the sake of readability, we decided to change the acceptance condition from
\min
-
parity to
\max
-
parity,
when comparing to the conference version of this paper
[
NPS23
]
.

We assume without loss of generality that
d>0
is an even number.
A
run
of such an automaton
\mathcal{A}
over a tree
t\in\mathrm{Tr}_{\Sigma}
is a function
\rho\colon\{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle L}},\mathtt{{\scriptstyle R}}\}^{\ast}\to Q
that assigns a state to every node of
t
,
such that
(\rho(v),t(v),\rho(v\mathtt{{\scriptstyle L}}),\rho(v\mathtt{{\scriptstyle R}}))\in\gamma
for all
v\in\{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle L}},\mathtt{{\scriptstyle R}}\}^{\ast}
.
A run
\rho
is
accepting
if, for any branch
w\in\{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle L}},\mathtt{{\scriptstyle R}}\}^{\omega}
, the maximal priority occurring infinitely often is even, that is,

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\Omega\big(\rho(w_{n})\big)
is even, where
w_{n}
is the prefix of
w
of length
n
.
A tree is
accepted
by the automaton
\mathcal{A}
if it admits an accepting run
\rho
starting from the initial state, that is,
\rho(\varepsilon)=q_{I}
.
The set of trees accepted by
\mathcal{A}
is denoted
\mathrm{L}(\mathcal{A})
.

Orders and fixed points

Let
(X,{\leq})
be a partial order.
The least upper bound of a subset
A\subseteq X
(if exists) is also called the
supremum
of
A
and denoted by
\sup A
,
and the greatest lower bound is the
infimum
denoted by
\inf A
.
A
complete lattice
is a partial order such that suprema and infima exist for all subsets.
In particular
\sup X=\top

and
\inf X=\bot
are the greatest and least elements of
X
.
The supremum and infimum of a two-element set
\{a,b\}
are denoted
a{\lor}b
and
a{\land}b
, respectively.

The celebrated Knaster
-
Tarski theorem states that if
(X,{\leq})
is a complete lattice
then any monotone (i.e., order
-
preserving) function
f\colon X\to X
has the least (
\mu
) and the greatest (
\nu
) fixed points satisfying

\displaystyle\mu x.\,f(x)

\displaystyle=\inf\,

\displaystyle\big\{a

\displaystyle\mid f(a)\leq a

\displaystyle\big\},

\displaystyle\nu x.\,f(x)

\displaystyle=\sup\,

\displaystyle\big\{a

\displaystyle\mid a\leq f(a)

\displaystyle\big\}.

For a monotone function of several arguments, we can apply fixed-point operators successively.
For example,
\nu y.\,\mu x.\,g(x,y)
is the greatest fixed point of the mapping
y\mapsto\mu x.\,g(x,y)
(it is easy to observe that this mapping is also monotone).

A
chain
in a partial order
(X,{\leq})
is any non
-
empty subset
C\subseteq X
linearly ordered by
{\leq}
.
A subset
A\subseteq X
is called a
chain
-
complete subset of
X
if the supremum and the infimum of every chain
C\subseteq A
exists and remains in
A
.
We write
\mathsf{P}_{\mathsf{cc}}(X)
for the set of all chain
-
complete subsets of
X
.

We say that a monotone function
f\colon X\to Y
between two complete lattices,
(X,{\leq})
and
(Y,{\leq})
, is
chain
-
continuous

if for every chain
C\subseteq X
it holds
\sup(f(C))=f(\sup C)
and
\inf(f(C))=f(\inf C)
.

Whenever we consider a space of the form
X^{n}
for a partial order

(X,{\leq})
and a natural number
n\geq 0
, we assume that
X^{n}
is ordered coordinate
-
wise,
that is,
(x_{0},\ldots,x_{n-1})\leq(y_{0},\ldots,y_{n-1})
if
x_{i}\leq y_{i}
for all
i\in\{0,\ldots,n{-}1\}
.

Measures

The set
\mathrm{Tr}_{\Sigma}
of trees over a finite alphabet
\Sigma
can be equipped with a topology generated by a basis consisting of all the sets
U_{f}
,
where
{f}\colon{\mathrm{dom}(f)}\to{\Sigma}
is a function with a finite domain
\mathrm{dom}(f)\subset\{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle L}},\mathtt{{\scriptstyle R}}\}^{*}
, and
U_{f}
consists of all trees
t
that coincide with
f
on
\mathrm{dom}(f)
.
If
\Sigma
has at least
2
elements then this topology is homeomorphic to the Cantor discontinuum
\{0,1\}^{\omega}
(see, e.g.,
[
PP04
]
).

The set of trees can be further equipped with the coin
-
flipping measure, where a letter in
\Sigma
is chosen uniformly at random independently at each node.
This is the standard Lebesgue measure on the product space defined on the basis by
\prob\left(U_{f}\right)=\big|\Sigma\big|^{-|\mathrm{dom}(f)|}
.

We use the well
-
known facts that the measure
\prob
is
Borel
(i.e., every Borel set is
\prob
-
measurable)
and that it is
complete
(i.e., every subset of a set of
\prob
-
measure
0
is measurable and of
\prob
-
measure
0
).

3
Fixed-point approach

Our proof relies on fixed
-
point definitions of regular tree languages in the powerset lattice of
\mathrm{Tr}_{\Sigma}
.
Such a definition can be derived from a parity automaton (either nondeterministic or alternating) recognising the given language
[
Niw88
,
Niw97
]
.
The operations involved are
a(\cdot,\cdot)
, for each
a\in\Sigma
, the binary set union
\cup
and intersection
\cap
, and the least (
\mu
) and greatest (
\nu
) fixed
-
point operators.
The aforementioned operation
a(L_{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle L}}},L_{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle R}}})
sends the languages
L_{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle L}}}
and
L_{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle R}}}
to the set of trees
t
, such that
t.\mathtt{{\scriptstyle L}}\in L_{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle L}}}
,
t.\mathtt{{\scriptstyle R}}\in L_{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle R}}}
, and
t(\varepsilon)=a
.
For example, an expression
\mu x.\,\nu y.\,a(x,x)\cup b(y,y)
defines the set of trees over the alphabet
\{a,b\}
where, on each branch,
a
occurs only finitely often.
In general, it is convenient to use
vectorial
fixed
-
point expressions of the form

\displaystyle\mu x_{d-1}.\,\nu x_{d-2}\ldots\nu x_{2}.\,\mu x_{1}.\,\nu x_{0}.\,F(x_{0},x_{1},\ldots,x_{d-1}),

(3.1)

where the vectorial variables
x_{i}=(x_{i,1},\ldots,x_{i,k})
, for
i\in\{0,1,\ldots,d{-}1\}
,
range over
k
-
tuples
of subsets of
\mathrm{Tr}_{\Sigma}
, and
F
is a
k
-
tuple of terms constructed from the basic operations mentioned above.
If we start with a parity tree automaton, the parameter
k
corresponds to the number of states, and the parameter
d
to the number of priorities (see, e.g.,
[
AN01
]
).
More specifically, if the
j
-
th state has priority
i
, it becomes a variable
x_{i,j}
.
Moreover, if the automaton is nondeterministic then no intersection
\cap
is used in the expression.

3.1
Non-emptiness for nondeterministic automata

In the case of nondeterministic automata, this fixed
-
point characterisation leads to a simple, albeit not very efficient,
algorithm to decide if a regular tree language is non
-
empty
[
Niw97
, Section 4.2]
.

The idea is to take the
k
-
th power of the Boolean lattice
\{0,1\}
, and evaluate
Eq.

3.1
there,
with the operation
a(x,y)
interpreted as
x\land y
(for any symbol
a
) and
\cup
as
\lor
.
Now the set
L_{q}
of trees accepted from the state
q
of the automaton is non
-
empty if and only if the respective component equals 1
(i.e., the component corresponding to the state
q
in
Eq.

3.1
evaluated in
\{0,1\}^{k}
).
The argument relies on good properties of a mapping
h\colon\mathsf{P}(\mathrm{Tr}_{\Sigma})\to\{0,1\}
that sends all non
-
empty sets to
1
and the empty set to
0
.
We have
a\big(h(L_{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle L}}}),h(L_{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle R}}})\big)=h\big(a(L_{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle L}}},L_{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle R}}})\big)
and
h(L_{1})\lor h(L_{2})=h\big(L_{1}\cup L_{2}\big)
.
It can be further shown that
h
preserves the fixed points in consideration as well;
here the argument relies on unravelling the involved expression
F
into a tree labelled by an accepting run of the automaton.

However, an analogous construction fails when an automaton is alternating.
Indeed, the aforementioned mapping
h
does not preserve
intersection
\cap
if we interpret it as
\wedge
.
Moreover, the mapping
h
is not
chain
-
continuous
:
there can be a sequence of non
-
empty sets
L_{0}\supseteq L_{1}\supseteq L_{2}\supseteq\ldots
with
\bigcap_{n}L_{n}=\emptyset
, which means that
\forall n.\ h(L_{n})=1
and
h\big(\bigcap_{n}L_{n}\big)=0
.

3.2
Unary
\mu
-
calculus

Is it possible to apply a fixed-point approach when, instead of non
-
emptiness, we are interested in the measure of a set?

On a high level, we would like to find some measure
-
theoretic analogue of the Boolean lattice
\{0,1\}
, so that an appropriate interpretation of
Eq.

3.1
would yield the researched measure.
A natural candidate is the space
[0,1]
along with the monotone mapping
\prob:L\mapsto\prob(L)
, which assigns to each (measurable) set
L
the probability of
L
.
In analogy to the mapping
h
from the previous section, the operation
a(\cdot,\cdot)
can be interpreted over measures of languages.
Indeed,
\prob\big(a(L_{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle L}}},L_{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle R}}})\big)=|\Sigma|^{-1}\cdot\prob(L_{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle L}}})\cdot\prob(L_{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle R}}})
.
Moreover, continuity of measure implies that the mapping
\prob
is chain
-
continuous:
\prob\big(\bigcap_{n}L_{n}\big)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\prob(L_{n})
whenever the languages
L_{n}
form a chain.

However, the measures of neither the union
\cup
nor the intersection
\cap
of two languages are determined by the measures of the languages themselves,
so it is not possible to interpret
\cup
nor
\cap
here.
This reflects a more general phenomenon: the knowledge of distributions of two random variables does not determine their joint distribution.
Thus, when working with measures, we should avoid using functions with more than one argument.

To overcome this obstacle, we introduce the main technical invention of our paper: unary
\mu
-
calculus.

In the classical
\mu
-
calculus, an application of a fixed
-
point operator, say
\mu
, to a mapping
F\colon X\to X
returns an element
\mu x.\,F(x)
of
X
, and if applied to a distinguished argument of a
(k{+}1)
-
ary function, it returns a
k
-
ary function, for example
\mu x_{0}.\,G(x_{0},x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})
, and so on.
In unary
\mu
-
calculus, an application of a fixed
-
point operator
to a unary monotone function returns another unary monotone function.
More precisely, when applied to a function
F\colon X\to X
, it returns a (partial) function
{F}{\uparrow}\colon X\rightharpoonup X
,
that sends an element
x\in X
to the least fixed point of
F

above

x
(if exists).
Similarly,
{F}{\downarrow}\colon X\rightharpoonup X
sends an element
x\in X
to the greatest fixed point of
F

below

x
(if exists).
Such fixed points need not always exist even if
X
is a complete lattice: although the respective subsets are complete sublattices, the function
F
need not preserve them.
However, when
F(x)\geq x
then
{F}{\uparrow}(x)
exists and can be obtained by taking a limit of an ascending chain of approximations (as in Knaster
-
Tarski theorem);
similarly for
{F}{\downarrow}(x)
, whenever
F(x)\leq x
.

An essential step of our construction is to define a formula of unary
\mu
-
calculus which, in the domain of tree languages, is equivalent to
Eq.

3.1
.
Since transitions of the considered automaton are already black
-
boxed into a single function
F
, the exact type of the automaton does not matter:
the construction works in the same manner for both nondeterministic and alternating automata.
We further show that the derived formula interpreted in an appropriate probabilistic domain yields the researched measure.

3.3
Computing measure

We now provide more details on our formula and its interpretation.

Viewing
\mathrm{Tr}_{\Sigma}
as a sample space, a measurable tree language
L\subseteq\mathrm{Tr}_{\Sigma}
can be identified with a random variable, say
\widetilde{L}\colon\mathrm{Tr}_{\Sigma}\to\{0,1\}
,
which is the characteristic function of
L
; we search for
\prob(\{t\mid\widetilde{L}(t)=1\})
.
Given an automaton, it is apparent that we need to consider the product variable

\displaystyle\big(\widetilde{L_{q}}\big){}_{q\in Q},

(3.2)

where
L_{q}
is the set of trees accepted from the state
q
.
Note that the variables
\widetilde{L_{q}}
are, in general, not independent (even pairwise).
In particular, the probability that a random tree
t
is accepted both from a state
p
and from a state
q
is not determined by the probabilities of these two events taken separately.
Therefore, if we would like to define the probability distribution of the variables
\widetilde{L_{q}}
as a nested fixed point,
we cannot rely on a direct product construction as in the non
-
emptiness problem (where we would just store a tuple of probabilities, one for each variable
\widetilde{L_{q}}
).

To proceed further, we find it convenient to present the product variable from
Eq.

3.2

as a single random variable
\tau_{\mathcal{A}}\colon\mathrm{Tr}_{\Sigma}\to\mathsf{P}(Q)
that sends a tree
t
to the set
\{q\mid t\in L_{q}\}
, that is,

\displaystyle\tau_{\mathcal{A}}(t)=\{q\mid\widetilde{L_{q}}(t)=1\}.

(3.3)

Clearly, the probability distribution of
\tau_{\mathcal{A}}
yields the measure of the language recognised by the automaton,
which amounts to the probability of the event
q_{I}\in\tau_{\mathcal{A}}(t)
(where
q_{I}
is the initial state).
We aim for a formula of unary
\mu
-
calculus that defines the probability distribution of the variable
\tau_{\mathcal{A}}
.

To this end, we consider the space of all functions
\tau\colon\mathrm{Tr}_{\Sigma}\to\mathsf{P}(Q)
, which we call
profiles
.
Clearly this space is a complete lattice with the component
-
wise ordering induced by the inclusion ordering on
\mathsf{P}(Q)
.
If we evoke the identification from
Eq.

3.3
, this ordering coincides with the component
-
wise inclusion on
(\mathsf{P}(\mathrm{Tr}_{\Sigma}))^{|Q|}
.
The fixed-point characterisation of regular tree languages
[
AN01
]
gives us a formula as in
Eq.

3.1
that defines the profile
\tau_{\mathcal{A}}
.
By a subtle analysis of the structure of nested fixed points, we manage to transform it into a formula of unary
\mu
-
calculus.

The next step is to define an appropriate structure on the space of probability distributions of measurable profiles.
As they take values in
\mathsf{P}(Q)
, we use here a general construction of
probabilistic powerdomain

which settles an appropriate ordering on the set of probability distributions on a complete lattice
\mathcal{R}
.
This structure is in general not a lattice, but it is chain
-
continuous, which is sufficient for unary
\mu
-
calculus.

Now, our formula of unary
\mu
-
calculus can be reinterpreted in the probabilistic powerdomain over a single lattice
\mathcal{R}
,
which in our case is
\mathsf{P}(Q\times\{0,1,\ldots,d{-}1\})
(i.e., each state is multiplied by all possible priorities).
The new interpretation yields the desired probability distribution of
\tau_{\mathcal{A}}
and the actual values can be expressed in the Tarski’s theory of reals.

Overview of the proof

Section

4
introduces the space of profiles and recalls the fixed-point characterisation of regular tree languages in terms of profiles
[
AN01
]
.

Section

5
introduces unary
\mu
-
calculus along with a typing system that allows us to verify if an expression of this calculus is well-defined.

Sections

6
–
9
are devoted to showing that the generic formula from
Eq.

3.1
can be translated into unary
\mu
-
calculus.
This is a general result on fixed-point definitions and the proof is carried out in an abstract setting, not restricted to the interpretation in the domain of trees.
We come back to trees in
Section

10
and verify that all the sets of interest for us are measurable.
This concerns not only the regular tree languages whose measurability is known
[
GMMS17
]
, but also the sets obtained in the iterative approximation of fixed points.
Knowing this, we can switch our perspective from profiles to probability distributions.
In
Section

11
we accomplish the main technical result of our paper
showing that the purchased distribution of the random variable
\tau_{\mathcal{A}}
associated with the automaton
can be obtained by reinterpreting our formula of unary
\mu
-
calculus in the probabilistic powerdomain in consideration.
The proof of
Theorem

1.2
is finished in
Section

12
,
where we show that formulae of unary
\mu
-
calculus interpreted in the probabilistic powerdomain can be expressed using Tarski’s first-order theory over reals.
Therefore, decidability of this theory implies decidability of our question expressed by a unary
\mu
-
calculus formula.
Finally, in
Section

13
we treat the case of trees generated by a stochastic branching process, proving

.

4
Profiles

As already mentioned, we work with two spaces: the space of profiles, and the space of probability distributions over profiles and vectors thereof.
For the sake of simplicity, we work with nondeterministic automata, see
Remark

4.4
for the case of alternating automata.
Thus, fix a nondeterministic parity automaton
\mathcal{A}=\langle\Sigma,Q,q_{I},\gamma,\Omega\rangle
.
Recall that we assume that the maximal priority
d
is even.

The space of
profiles
is
\mathcal{V}=\big(\mathrm{Tr}_{\Sigma}\to\mathsf{P}(Q)\big)
.
A single profile
\tau\in\mathcal{V}
is thus a function that assigns a set of states to each tree
t
in
\mathrm{Tr}_{\Sigma}
.
Ultimately, we are interested in the unique profile
\tau_{\mathcal{A}}\in\mathcal{V}
that to each tree
t

assigns the set of those states from which there exists an accepting run of
\mathcal{A}
on
t
.
In the meantime, we also consider other profiles.

We fix the order on
\mathcal{V}
defined for
\tau,\tau^{\prime}\in\mathcal{V}
by
\tau\leq\tau^{\prime}
if
\tau(t)\subseteq\tau^{\prime}(t)
for all trees
t\in\mathrm{Tr}_{\Sigma}
.
It is easy to see that
(\mathcal{V},{\leq})
is a complete lattice: a supremum (infimum) can be obtained by taking the union (intersection) for every tree
t\in\mathrm{Tr}_{\Sigma}
.

Let us now define a function
\delta\colon\mathcal{V}^{d}\to\mathcal{V}
corresponding to the transition function of the automaton.
For each
a\in\Sigma
we define
\delta_{a}\colon\mathsf{P}(Q)\times\mathsf{P}(Q)\to\mathsf{P}(Q)
: for
K^{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle L}}},K^{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle R}}}\in\mathsf{P}(Q)
we take

\displaystyle\delta_{a}(K^{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle L}}},K^{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle R}}})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\big\{q\in Q\mid\exists(q,a,q^{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle L}}},q^{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle R}}})\in\gamma.\,q^{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle L}}}\in K^{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle L}}}\land q^{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle R}}}\in K^{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle R}}}\big\}.

Then, for
\bar{\tau}=(\tau_{0},\ldots,\tau_{d-1})\in\mathcal{V}^{d}
and
t\in\mathrm{Tr}_{\Sigma}
we define

\displaystyle K^{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle L}}}_{t}(\bar{\tau})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\big\{q\in Q\mid q\in\tau_{\Omega(q)}(t.\mathtt{{\scriptstyle L}})\big\},

\displaystyle K^{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle R}}}_{t}(\bar{\tau})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\big\{q\in Q\mid q\in\tau_{\Omega(q)}(t.\mathtt{{\scriptstyle R}})\big\},

\displaystyle\delta(\bar{\tau})(t)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\delta_{t(\varepsilon)}\Big(K^{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle L}}}_{t}(\bar{\tau}),K^{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle R}}}_{t}(\bar{\tau})\Big).

The intuition is: a state
q
belongs to the profile
\delta(\bar{\tau})
in the root of a hypothetical tree
t

if after a single transition from a state
q

the two successor states
q^{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle L}}}
and
q^{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle R}}}
belong respectively to the profiles
\tau_{\Omega(q^{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle L}}})}
and
\tau_{\Omega(q^{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle R}}})}
applied to the two subtrees of
t
starting in the children of the root.
Note that
\delta
ignores most of the input profiles.
Namely, for every state
q
we check whether
q
belongs to
\tau_{i}(t)
only for
i=\Omega(q)
, while this information is meaningless for
i\neq\Omega(q)
.
In such a situation one may wonder if we can “compress” all the input profiles
\tau_{0},\dots,\tau_{d-1}
into a single one.
This would be problematic, however, because different components of the input
\tau_{0},\dots,\tau_{d-1}
should be treated differently with respect to fixed-point operators
(cf. the formula from
Proposition

4.2
below).

Lemma 4.1
.

The function
\delta\colon\mathcal{V}^{d}\to\mathcal{V}
is monotone and chain
-
continuous.

Proof.

Monotonicity of
\delta
follows from monotonicity of
\delta_{a}
.
To see that
\delta
is chain
-
continuous, consider a chain
C\subseteq\mathcal{V}^{d}
,
its supremum
\bar{\sigma}\in\mathcal{V}^{d}
, and the supremum
\sigma^{\prime}\in\mathcal{V}
of the chain
\{\delta(\bar{\tau})\mid\bar{\tau}\in C\}\subseteq\mathcal{V}
.
We have to prove that
\delta(\bar{\sigma})=\sigma^{\prime}
.
To this end, fix a tree
t\in\mathrm{Tr}_{\Sigma}
.
Notice that the pair
\big(K^{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle L}}}_{t}(\bar{\tau}),K^{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle R}}}_{t}(\bar{\tau})\big)
belongs to the finite set
\mathsf{P}(Q)^{2}
, and that the functions
K^{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle L}}}_{t}
,
K^{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle R}}}_{t}
are monotone.
Because the order in
\mathcal{V}
is defined for each tree separately, we have that
\big(K^{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle L}}}_{t}(\bar{\sigma}),K^{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle R}}}_{t}(\bar{\sigma})\big)
is the supremum of the finite chain

\big\{(K^{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle L}}}_{t}(\bar{\tau}),K^{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle R}}}_{t}(\bar{\tau}))\mid\bar{\tau}\in C\big\}
;
likewise
\sigma^{\prime}(t)
is the supremum of the finite chain
\big\{\delta(\bar{\tau})(t)\mid\bar{\tau}\in C\big\}
.
The supremum of a finite chain is just its greatest element, so there exists some
\bar{\tau}\in C
such that
K^{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle L}}}_{t}(\bar{\sigma})=K^{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle L}}}_{t}(\bar{\tau})
,
K^{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle R}}}_{t}(\bar{\sigma})=K^{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle R}}}_{t}(\bar{\tau})
, and
\sigma^{\prime}(t)=\delta(\bar{\tau})(t)
.
Next, notice that
\delta(\bar{\tau})(t)
depends only on
K^{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle L}}}_{t}(\bar{\tau})
and
K^{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle R}}}_{t}(\bar{\tau})
;
in other words,
\big(K^{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle L}}}_{t}(\bar{\sigma}),K^{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle R}}}_{t}(\bar{\sigma})\big)=\big(K^{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle L}}}_{t}(\bar{\tau}),K^{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle R}}}_{t}(\bar{\tau})\big)
implies
\delta(\bar{\sigma})(t)=\delta(\bar{\tau})(t)=\sigma^{\prime}(t)
.
We thus have the equality
\delta(\bar{\sigma})(t)=\sigma^{\prime}(t)
for all

t\in\mathrm{Tr}_{\Sigma}
, which simply means that
\delta(\bar{\sigma})=\sigma^{\prime}
,
as required.

The proof for the infimum of
C
is analogous.
∎

Recall the special profile
\tau_{\mathcal{A}}\in\mathcal{V}
corresponding to the automaton
\mathcal{A}
(note that
d
is assumed to be even).

Proposition 4.2
.

We have

\displaystyle\tau_{\mathcal{A}}=\mu x_{d-1}.\,\nu x_{d-2}.\,\mu x_{d-3}.\,\nu x_{d-4}\ldots\mu x_{1}.\,\nu x_{0}.\,\delta(x_{0},\ldots,x_{d-1}).

∎

This proposition expresses a well
-
known equivalence between
\mu
-
calculus and tree automata first observed by Niwiński
[
Niw88
]
.
A proof based on parity games can be found for instance in a book by Arnold and Niwiński
[
AN01
, Proposition 7.3.5]
,
showing that the two parity games corresponding to the automaton and to the formula, respectively, are equivalent.
The cited argument is done in the complete lattice
\mathsf{P}(\mathrm{Tr}_{\Sigma})
, but this domain corresponds to the space
\mathcal{V}

since a profile
\tau\colon\mathrm{Tr}_{\Sigma}\to\mathsf{P}(Q)
(with
Q=\{q_{0},\ldots,q_{m-1}\}
, say)
can be identified with an
m
-
vector of tree languages
(L_{0},\ldots,L_{m-1})
, where
L_{i}=\{t\in\mathrm{Tr}_{\Sigma}\mid q_{i}\in\tau(t)\}
.

Note that in this work we use functional
\mu
-
calculus rather than the modal one.
An analogous formula for the modal
\mu
-
calculus has been given by Janin and Walukiewicz
[
JW95
]
.

4.1
Alternating automata

An alternating tree automaton is defined analogously to a nondeterministic one, except that the transition relation
\gamma
assigns to each state
q\in Q
a formula
\gamma(q)
built using the syntax

\theta::=a(q_{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle L}}},q_{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle R}}})\mid\theta_{1}\land\theta_{2}\mid\theta_{1}\lor\theta_{2},

where
a\in\Sigma
and
q_{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle L}}},q_{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle R}}}\in Q
.
In other words, a transition is a positive Boolean combination of
deterministic
transitions of the form
a(q_{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle L}}},q_{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle R}}})
.
A nondeterministic automaton can be seen as a special case of an alternating one, where the formulae
\gamma(q)
are disjunctions of deterministic transitions.

The semantics of alternating automata is given in terms of acceptance game, see, for instance,
[
Kir01
]
,
[
BW18
]
, or
[
AN01
]
.
In particular,
\tau_{\mathcal{A}}(t)
can again be defined as the set of states from which
\mathcal{A}
accepts
t
.
Our goal is to provide an alternative definition of the function
\delta_{a}\colon\mathsf{P}(Q)\times\mathsf{P}(Q)\to\mathsf{P}(Q)
, which is now defined for an alternating automaton.
The remaining definitions of
K^{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle L}}}_{t}(\bar{\tau})
,
K^{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle R}}}_{t}(\bar{\tau})
, and
\delta(\bar{\tau})(t)
remain the same.

To define
\delta_{a}(K^{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle L}}},K^{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle R}}})
we proceed inductively over the structure of the formula
\gamma(q)
, defining auxiliary sets
K_{\theta}
for all subformulae of
\gamma(q)
as follows:

\displaystyle K_{a(q^{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle L}}},q^{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle R}}})}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\big\{q\in Q\mid q^{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle L}}}\in K^{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle L}}}\land q^{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle R}}}\in K^{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle R}}}\big\},

\displaystyle K_{b(q^{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle L}}},q^{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle R}}})}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\emptyset

\displaystyle\text{for $b\neq a$},

\displaystyle K_{\theta_{1}\lor\theta_{2}}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}K_{\theta_{1}}\cup K_{\theta_{2}},

\displaystyle K_{\theta_{1}\land\theta_{2}}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}K_{\theta_{1}}\cap K_{\theta_{2}}.

Finally, we put
\delta_{a}(K^{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle L}}},K^{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle R}}})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}K_{\gamma(q)}
.

The correctness of this construction relies on the following fact.

Fact 4.3
.

Assume that
\tau_{\mathcal{A}}(t.\mathtt{{\scriptstyle L}})=K^{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle L}}}
,
\tau_{\mathcal{A}}(t.\mathtt{{\scriptstyle R}})=K^{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle R}}}
, and
a=t(\varepsilon)
.
Then
\tau_{\mathcal{A}}(t)=\delta_{a}(K^{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle L}}},K^{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle R}}})
.
∎

Remark 4.4
.

Assume that the considered automaton is alternating and
\delta
is defined based on
\delta_{a}
as above.
Then,
Lemma

4.1
and the equivalence from
Proposition

4.2
hold.
Moreover, the rest of our proof, relating
\tau_{\mathcal{A}}
to the constructed formula of unary
\mu
-
calculus and then showing how to compute measures, remains also valid;
it works for any monotone function
\delta_{a}
.

5
Unary
\mu
-
calculus

We now proceed to define the notion of unary
\mu
-
calculus.
This is done in generality, for any partial order
(X,{\leq})
.

5.1
Intensional definition

Consider a partial order
(X,{\leq})
.
A
partial function

F\colon X\rightharpoonup X
is a function
F\colon\mathrm{dom}(F)\to X
for some
\mathrm{dom}(F)\subseteq X
.
Let
F,G\colon X\rightharpoonup X
be monotone partial functions.
We write
F;G
for their composition, defined by

\displaystyle(F;G)(x)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}G(F(x))\hskip 90.00014pt\mbox{with}

\displaystyle\mathrm{dom}(F;G)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\big\{x\in\mathrm{dom}(F)\mid F(x)\in\mathrm{dom}(G)\big\}.

We also define
\mathrm{Fix}(F)
as the set of fixed points of
F
, that is,
\mathrm{Fix}(F)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\{x\in\mathrm{dom}(F)\mid F(x)=x\}
.
Then, we define a partial function
{F}{\uparrow}\colon X\rightharpoonup X
taking

\displaystyle{F}{\uparrow}(x)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\min\big\{y\in\mathrm{Fix}(F)\mid y\geq x\big\}\hskip 90.00014pt\text{with}

\displaystyle\mathrm{dom}({F}{\uparrow})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\big\{x\in X\mid\exists y\in\mathrm{Fix}(F).\,\big(y\geq x\land\forall y^{\prime}\in\mathrm{Fix}(F).\,(y^{\prime}\geq x\Rightarrow y^{\prime}\geq y)\big)\big\},

That is,
x\in\mathrm{dom}({F}{\uparrow})
if there exists the least fixed point of
F
that is greater or equal
x
, and then
{F}{\uparrow}(x)
is this fixed point.
Note that the domain of
{F}{\uparrow}
need not be contained in the domain of
F
.
Dually, we define

\displaystyle{F}{\downarrow}(x)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\max\big\{y\in\mathrm{Fix}(F)\mid y\leq x\big\},

and
\mathrm{dom}({F}{\downarrow})
is defined accordingly.

We fix a finite set of symbols
\mathfrak{F}
that we call a
unary functional signature
(
signature
for short).
A partial order
(X,{\leq})
is expanded to a
structure over
\mathfrak{F}
if each symbol
H\in\mathfrak{F}
is interpreted by a monotone function
H_{X}\colon X\to X
.
We call the functions
H_{X}
the
basic functions
.

A formula of
unary
\mu
-
calculus
over a signature
\mathfrak{F}
is any term
F
obtained using the grammar

F::=H\mid F_{1};F_{2}\mid{F}{\uparrow}\mid{F}{\downarrow},

where
H\in\mathfrak{F}
.

Assume that
(X,{\leq})
is a structure over
\mathfrak{F}
.
We now define, for each formula
F
of unary
\mu
-
calculus, a partial function
F_{X}\colon X\rightharpoonup X
:

\displaystyle(H)_{X}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}H_{X},\qquad(F;G)_{X}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}F_{X};G_{X},\qquad({F}{\uparrow})_{X}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}{(F_{X})}{\uparrow},\qquad({F}{\downarrow})_{X}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}{(F_{X})}{\downarrow}.

The following fact follows easily from the definition.

Lemma 5.1
.

If
F
is a formula of unary
\mu
-
calculus then
F_{X}\colon X\rightharpoonup X
is a monotone partial function.
∎

5.2
Computing limits

For an ordinal
\eta>0
consider a sequence
(x_{\zeta})_{\zeta<\eta}
of elements of
X
.
We say that such a sequence is
increasing
if
x_{\zeta}\leq x_{\zeta^{\prime}}
whenever
\zeta<\zeta^{\prime}<\eta
.
We define the
limit
of such a sequence, denoted
\lim_{\zeta<\eta}x_{\zeta}
, as the supremum of the chain
\{x_{\zeta}\mid\zeta<\eta\}
.
Analogously we define a
decreasing
sequence, whose limit is
\inf\{x_{\zeta}\mid\zeta<\eta\}
.
A sequence is
monotonic
if it is either increasing or decreasing.

Definition 5.2
.

We say that a partial function
F\colon X\rightharpoonup X
is
ascending on
A\subseteq X
if:

F
is monotone,
A\subseteq\mathrm{dom}(F)
,
A
is a chain
-
complete subset of
X
, and for each
x\in A
we have
F(x)\in A
and
F(x)\geq x
.

F
is
descending on
A
if the analogue conditions are met with
F(x)\leq x
.

Assume that
F\colon X\rightharpoonup X
is either ascending or descending on
A\subseteq X
and
x\in A
.
For an ordinal
\eta
define inductively
F^{\eta}(x)
to be

•

x
if
\eta=0
,

•

F(F^{\eta-1}(x))
if
\eta
is a successor ordinal, and

•

\lim_{\zeta<\eta}F^{\zeta}(x)
if
\eta
is a limit ordinal.

This sequence is well-defined thanks to the assumption that
F
is monotone and ascending (descending) on
A
and
A
is chain complete;
in particular it is increasing (decreasing, respectively) and all its elements remain in
A
.
Then, for some ordinal
\eta_{\infty}
(at latest for the first ordinal of cardinality greater than
|X|
) the values must stabilise:
F^{\eta_{\infty}+1}(x)=F^{\eta_{\infty}}(x)
.
Define
F^{\infty}(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}F^{\eta_{\infty}}(x)
.

Lemma 5.3
.

Assume that
F\colon X\rightharpoonup X
is ascending on
A\subseteq X
.
Then
A\subseteq\mathrm{dom}({F}{\uparrow})
and for every
x\in A
we have
({F}{\uparrow})(x)=F^{\infty}(x)\in\mathrm{Fix}(F)\cap A
.

Analogously, if
F
is descending on
A\subseteq X
then
A\subseteq\mathrm{dom}({F}{\downarrow})
and for every
x\in A
we have
({F}{\downarrow})(x)=F^{\infty}(x)\in\mathrm{Fix}(F)\cap A
.

Proof.

We conduct the proof for
{F}{\uparrow}
; the case of
{F}{\downarrow}
is symmetric.
Fix some
x\in A
and recall the sequence
F^{\eta}(x)
used to define
F^{\infty}(x)
.
We have
F\big(F^{\eta_{\infty}}(x)\big)=F^{\eta_{\infty}+1}(x)=F^{\eta_{\infty}}(x)
, so
F^{\eta_{\infty}}(x)\in\mathrm{Fix}(F)
.
Moreover
F^{\eta_{\infty}}(x)\geq x
because
F^{\eta_{\infty}}(x)
belongs to an increasing sequence starting at
x
.

Take now any other fixed point
y^{\prime}\in\mathrm{Fix}(F)
such that
y^{\prime}\geq x
.
We can see by induction that
y^{\prime}\geq F^{\eta}(x)
for all ordinals
\eta
.
Indeed, for
\eta=0
this simply says that
y^{\prime}\geq x
.
If
\eta
is a successor ordinal, from the induction hypothesis
y^{\prime}\geq F^{\eta-1}(x)
we obtain

y^{\prime}=F(y^{\prime})\geq F\big(F^{\eta-1}(x)\big)=F^{\eta}(x)

by monotonicity of
F
.
Finally, if
\eta
is a limit ordinal and
y^{\prime}\geq F^{\zeta}(x)
for all
\zeta<\eta
, then
y^{\prime}
is an upper bound of the chain
\{F^{\zeta}(x)\mid\zeta<\penalty\eta\}
,
so the supremum
F^{\eta}(x)
of this chain satisfies
y^{\prime}\geq F^{\eta}(x)
.
It follows that
y^{\prime}\geq F^{\eta_{\infty}}(x)
.
Thus,
x\in\mathrm{dom}({F}{\uparrow})
and
({F}{\uparrow})(x)=F^{\eta_{\infty}}(x)
, as required.
∎

5.3
A type system

The definition of the partial function
F_{X}\colon X\rightharpoonup X
corresponding to a formula
F
of unary
\mu
-
calculus does not provide any natural way of proving that
x\in\mathrm{dom}(F_{X})
for a given
x\in X
.
Therefore, to prove statements of this form, we introduce a type system.
To this end, let
A,B\subseteq X
, and let
F
be a formula of unary
\mu
-
calculus.
We show how to derive a clause
F\!::A\to B
.
The type system is developed in a way that ensures the following
Section
.

Claim 5.4
.

If we can derive
F\!::A\to B
then
A\subseteq\mathrm{dom}(F_{X})
and moreover for each
x\in A
we have
F_{X}(x)\in B
.

The rules of the typing system follow.
A general form is

\frac{\mathit{clause}_{1},\ldots,\mathit{clause}_{k}}{\mathit{clause}}\hskip 7.0pt\mathit{condition},

where
condition
(possibly empty) can be any set
-
theoretic condition involving the formulae and sets appearing in the clauses.
The reading of a rule is: if
\mathit{clause}_{1}
, …,
\mathit{clause}_{k}
are already derived and the
condition
is satisfied, then
clause
is derived as well.

H\!::A\to B\ \qquad\qquad(F_{1};F_{2})\!::A\to B\lx@proof@logical@and F_{1}\!::A\to CF_{2}\!::C\to B

({F}{\uparrow})\!::A\to BF\!::A\to A

({F}{\downarrow})\!::A\to BF\!::A\to A

Note that the above conditions in the case of
({F}{\uparrow})
and
({F}{\downarrow})
boil down to
F_{X}
being ascending (respectively descending) on
A
.

5.4
above then follows by an easy induction on the structure of
F
, where in the last two cases we use
Lemma

5.3
.

6
Basic functions

In this section we define a structure in which we want to evaluate our formulae of unary
\mu
-
calculus.

Let
(\mathcal{V},{\leq})
be some complete lattice, with the least and the greatest elements denoted
\bot
and
\top
, respectively.
Let
d>0
be an even natural number.
Finally, we fix a monotone chain
-
continuous function
\delta\colon\mathcal{V}^{d}\to\mathcal{V}
.

While the results of this section hold in general, we actually need them for the particular
\mathcal{V}
and
\delta
defined in
Section

4
.
We work with the domain
\mathcal{V}^{d}
ordered component
-
wise, which, as already noted, is a complete lattice.
When working in
\mathcal{V}^{d}
we write
\bar{\bot}
for the vector
(\bot,\ldots,\bot)\in\mathcal{V}^{d}
.
For
\bar{\tau}\in\mathcal{V}^{d}
and
i\in\{0,\ldots,d{-}1\}
we write
\tau_{i}\in\mathcal{V}
for the respective coordinate of
\bar{\tau}
(i.e.,
\bar{\tau}=(\tau_{0},\ldots,\tau_{d-1})
).
For convenience, we additionally assume that
\tau_{d}=\top
and
\tau_{d+1}=\bot
.
Moreover, for
i\in\{0,\ldots,d\}
we define
\bar{\tau}{\downharpoonleft}_{i}=(\tau_{i},\tau_{i+1},\ldots,\tau_{d-1})\in\mathcal{V}^{d-i}
as the restriction of
\bar{\tau}
in which we cut off its first

coordinates.
In particular,
\bar{\tau}{\downharpoonleft}_{d}
is
()
, the empty tuple, while
\bar{\tau}{\downharpoonleft}_{0}=\bar{\tau}
.

6.1
Functions in
\mathcal{V}^{d}

Let
\mathfrak{F}=\{\Delta\}\cup\{\mathrm{Bid}_{0},\ldots,\mathrm{Bid}_{d-1}\}\cup\{\mathrm{Cut}_{1},\ldots,\mathrm{Cut}_{d-1}\}
.
Our goal is to define actual monotone functions with these names, which will turn
\mathcal{V}^{d}
into a structure over
\mathfrak{F}
.
All our work here is done in
\mathcal{V}^{d}
, and therefore we skip the subscript;
that is, the function
H_{\mathcal{V}^{d}}\colon\mathcal{V}^{d}\to\mathcal{V}^{d}
, for
H\in\mathfrak{F}
, is denoted just
H
, similarly for more complex formulae
F
.

Let
\Delta\colon\mathcal{V}^{d}\to\mathcal{V}^{d}
be defined for
\bar{\tau}=\big(\tau_{0},\ldots,\tau_{d-1}\big)\in\mathcal{V}^{d}
and
i\in\{0,\ldots,d{-}1\}
as

\Delta(\tau_{0},\ldots,\tau_{d-1})_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\delta\big(\tau_{i},\tau_{i},\ldots,\tau_{i},\tau_{i+1},\ldots,\tau_{d-1}\big).

Fact 6.1
.

For
0\leq i\leq d
and
\bar{\tau},\bar{\tau}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{V}^{d}
if
\bar{\tau}{\downharpoonleft}_{i}=\bar{\tau}^{\prime}{\downharpoonleft}_{i}
then

\Delta(\bar{\tau}){\downharpoonleft}_{i}=\Delta(\bar{\tau}^{\prime}){\downharpoonleft}_{i}.

Define, for
n\in\{0,\ldots,d{-}1\}
, a function
\mathrm{Bid}_{n}\colon\mathcal{V}^{d}\to\mathcal{V}^{d}
, which replaces the coordinates
0,1,\ldots,n
by the coordinate
n{+}2
:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Bid}_{n}(\bar{\tau})_{i}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\tau_{i}&\text{if $i>n$;}\\
\tau_{n+2}&\text{if $i\leq n$.}\end{array}\right.

For
n\geq d{-}2
we use the convention that
\tau_{d}=\top
and
\tau_{d+1}=\bot
.

Similarly, define for
n\in\{1,\ldots,d{-}1\}
a function
\mathrm{Cut}_{n}\colon\mathcal{V}^{d}\to\mathcal{V}^{d}
, which replaces the coordinates
0,1,\ldots,n
by the coordinate
n{-}1
:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cut}_{n}(\bar{\tau})_{i}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\tau_{i}&\text{if $i>n$;}\\
\tau_{n-1}&\text{if $i\leq n$.}\end{array}\right.

Fact 6.2
.

The functions
\Delta
,
\mathrm{Bid}_{n}
, and
\mathrm{Cut}_{n}
are all monotone.
∎

6.2
Invariants

The goal of this subsection is to define sets
\mathcal{S}_{n}\subseteq\mathcal{V}^{d}
for
n=0,\ldots,d
.
These sets are used later in the paper to derive types of certain functions on
\mathcal{V}^{d}
.

Define the
parity order
on
\{0,1,\ldots\}
as the order
\dots\prec 5\prec 3\prec 1\prec 0\prec 2\prec 4\prec\dots
,
that is, the unique linear order
\preceq
satisfying
2n+3\prec 2n+1\prec 2n\prec 2n+2
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
We often use the basic fact that if
n
is odd and
n^{\prime}
is even then
n\prec n^{\prime}
.

Consider
n\in\{-1,0,\ldots,d{-}1\}
and
\bar{\tau}=(\tau_{0},\ldots,\tau_{d-1})\in\mathcal{V}^{d}
.
We say that
\bar{\tau}
is:

•

ordered
if for
0\leq i,j\leq d{-}1
with
i\preceq j
we have
\tau_{i}\leq\tau_{j}
;

•

n
-
fixed
if for
i\in\{0,\ldots,n\}
we have
\tau_{i}=\tau_{n}
;

•

n
-
saturated
if
\Delta(\bar{\tau}){\downharpoonleft}_{n+1}=\bar{\tau}{\downharpoonleft}_{n+1}
.

In other words,
\bar{\tau}
is
n
-
fixed
if the first coordinates of
\bar{\tau}
, up to the
n
-
th, are all equal to each other;
while
\bar{\tau}
is
n
-
saturated
if the application of
\Delta
does not change the coordinates of
\bar{\tau}
above
n
.

For
n\in\{-1,0,\ldots,d{-}1\}
let
\mathcal{S}_{n}
be the subset of
\mathcal{V}^{d}
containing those
\bar{\tau}\in\mathcal{V}^{d}
that are ordered,
n
-
fixed,
n
-
saturated, and satisfy

•

\Delta(\bar{\tau})\geq\bar{\tau}
for
n
odd,

•

\Delta(\bar{\tau})\leq\bar{\tau}
for
n
even.

Finally, we say that a partial function
F\colon\mathcal{V}^{d}\rightharpoonup\mathcal{V}^{d}
is
n
-
stable on
A\subseteq\mathcal{V}^{d}

for
n\in\{-1,0,\ldots,d{-}1\}
if
A\subseteq\mathrm{dom}(F)
and for all
\bar{\tau}\in A
we have
F(\bar{\tau}){\downharpoonleft}_{n+1}=\bar{\tau}{\downharpoonleft}_{n+1}
.
In particular, the condition of being
n
-
saturated implies that
\Delta
is
n
-
stable on
\mathcal{S}_{n}
.

We now prove a number of properties of the sets
\mathcal{S}_{n}
and of
n
-
stability.

Fact 6.3
.

The least element
\bar{\bot}
of
\mathcal{V}^{d}
belongs to
\mathcal{S}_{d-1}
(recall that
d
is even).
∎

Fact 6.4
.

If
\bar{\tau}
is
n
-
saturated then it is
i
-
saturated for all
i\in\{n,\ldots,d{-}1\}
.
∎

Lemma 6.5

Take

\displaystyle\Delta(\bar{\tau})_{i}

\displaystyle=\delta\big(\tau_{i}

\displaystyle,\ldots

\displaystyle,\tau_{i}

\displaystyle,\tau_{i+1}

\displaystyle,\ldots,\tau_{n-1}

\displaystyle,\tau_{n},\tau_{n+1},\ldots,\tau_{d-1}

\displaystyle\big)

\displaystyle=\delta\big(\tau_{n}

\displaystyle,\ldots

\displaystyle,\tau_{n}

\displaystyle,\tau_{n}

\displaystyle,\ldots,\tau_{n}

\displaystyle,\tau_{n},\tau_{n+1},\ldots,\tau_{d-1}

\displaystyle\big)=\Delta(\bar{\tau})_{n};

where the middle equation follows from the fact that

is

-

∎

Lemma 6.6
.

Assume that
F\colon\mathcal{V}^{d}\rightharpoonup\mathcal{V}^{d}
is a partial function that is
n
-
stable on

.
If

is ascending on

, then
{F}{\uparrow}
is
n
-
stable on

Dually, if

is descending on

, then
{F}{\downarrow}
is
n
-

Proof.

Let
\bar{\tau}\in A
.
We use

to see that
({F}{\uparrow})(\bar{\tau})=F^{\infty}(\bar{\tau})
or
({F}{\downarrow})(\bar{\tau})=F^{\infty}(\bar{\tau})
, respectively.
The element

is defined by applying

and by taking limits of sequences.
Applying

preserves the coordinates above
n
by
n
-

on

.
On the other hand, if we have a sequence of elements of

∎

Lemma 6.7
.

For
n\in\{-1,0,\ldots,d{-}1\}
we have
\mathcal{S}_{n}\in\mathsf{P}_{\mathsf{cc}}(\mathcal{V}^{d})
, that is,
\mathcal{S}_{n}
is a chain
-
complete subset of
\mathcal{V}^{d}
.

Proof.

Consider a chain
C\subseteq\mathcal{S}_{n}
, and its supremum
\bar{\sigma}=(\sigma_{0},\ldots,\sigma_{d-1})\in\mathcal{V}^{d}
.
We have to prove that
\bar{\sigma}\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
; we also have to prove this for the infimum, but the proof is symmetric, so we continue only with the supremum.

To see that
\bar{\sigma}
is ordered, consider two coordinates
0\leq i,j\leq d{-}1
such that
i\preceq j
.
We have
\tau_{i}\leq\tau_{j}\leq\sigma_{j}
for all
\bar{\tau}\in C
, because
\bar{\tau}
is ordered and because
\sigma_{j}
is the supremum of
\{\tau_{j}\mid\bar{\tau}\in C\}
.
This means that
\sigma_{j}
is an upper bound of the chain
\{\tau_{i}\mid\bar{\tau}\in C\}
, hence the supremum
\sigma_{i}
of this chain satisfies
\sigma_{i}\leq\sigma_{j}
, as required.

The order in
\mathcal{V}^{d}
works on every coordinate
i\in\{0,\ldots,d{-}1\}
separately, that is,
\sigma_{i}
is the supremum of the chain
\{\tau_{i}\mid\bar{\tau}\in C\}
.
These chains for
i\in\{0,\ldots,n\}
are all the same, because all
\bar{\tau}\in C
are
n
-
fixed, which implies that the suprema of these chains are equal.
This means that
\bar{\sigma}
is
n
-
fixed.

While proving
n
-
saturation we use the assumption that
\delta
is chain
-
continuous.
It implies that the element
\Delta(\bar{\sigma})_{i}
, that is,
\delta(\sigma_{i},\sigma_{i},\ldots,\sigma_{i},\sigma_{i+1},\ldots,\sigma_{d-1})
is the supremum of the chain

\{\delta(\tau_{i},\tau_{i},\ldots,\tau_{i},\allowbreak\tau_{i+1},\ldots,\tau_{d-1})\mid\bar{\tau}\in C\}
, that is,
\{\Delta(\bar{\tau})_{i}\mid\bar{\tau}\in C\}
.
But all
\bar{\tau}\in C
are
n
-
saturated, so for
i\in\{n{+}1,\ldots,d{-}1\}
the set
\{\Delta(\bar{\tau})_{i}\mid\bar{\tau}\in C\}
equals
\{\tau_{i}\mid\bar{\tau}\in C\}
,
whose supremum is
\sigma_{i}
;
we obtain
\Delta(\bar{\sigma})_{i}=\sigma_{i}
, as required.

To see that
\Delta(\bar{\sigma})\geq\bar{\sigma}
for odd
n
we once again notice that
\Delta(\bar{\sigma})_{i}
is the supremum of
\{\Delta(\bar{\tau})_{i}\mid\bar{\tau}\in C\}
for all
i\in\{0,\ldots,d{-}1\}
;
in other words,
\Delta(\bar{\sigma})
is the supremum of
\{\Delta(\bar{\tau})\mid\bar{\tau}\in C\}
.
Then
\Delta(\bar{\sigma})\geq\Delta(\bar{\tau})\geq\bar{\tau}
for all
\bar{\tau}\in C
, that is,
\Delta(\bar{\sigma})
is an upper bound of
C
.
This implies that
\Delta(\bar{\sigma})\geq\bar{\sigma}
, because
\bar{\sigma}
is the supremum of
C
.
The inequality
\Delta(\bar{\sigma})\leq\bar{\sigma}
for even
n
is similar: we have
\Delta(\bar{\tau})\leq\bar{\tau}\leq\bar{\sigma}
for all
\bar{\tau}\in C
,
that is,
\bar{\sigma}
is an upper bound of the chain
\{\Delta(\bar{\tau})\mid\bar{\tau}\in C\}
whose supremum is
\Delta(\bar{\sigma})
.
∎

Lemma 6.8
.

For
n\in\{0,\ldots,d{-}1\}
we have
\mathrm{Fix}(\Delta)\cap\mathcal{S}_{n}\subseteq\mathcal{S}_{n-1}
.

Proof.

Consider
\bar{\tau}\in\mathrm{Fix}(\Delta)\cap\mathcal{S}_{n}
.
Because
\bar{\tau}\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
, we know that
\bar{\tau}
is ordered and
n
-
fixed, hence also
(n{-}1)
-
fixed.
On the other hand
\Delta(\bar{\tau})=\bar{\tau}
, which implies that
\bar{\tau}
is
n
-
saturated and satisfies both
\Delta(\bar{\tau})\geq\bar{\tau}
and
\Delta(\bar{\tau})\leq\bar{\tau}
.
∎

6.3
Typing the basic functions

We now prove a number of typing properties of the functions
\Delta
,
\mathrm{Bid}_{n}
, and
\mathrm{Cut}_{n}

Lemma 6.9
.

For
n\in\{0,\ldots,d{-}1\}
we can derive
\Delta\!::\mathcal{S}_{n}\to\mathcal{S}_{n}
.
Moreover,
\Delta
is
n
-
stable on
\mathcal{S}_{n}
.

Proof.

We have to prove that if
\bar{\tau}=(\tau_{0},\ldots,\tau_{d-1})\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
then
\Delta(\bar{\tau})\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
(then we can simply apply the first typing rule).
Consider such
\bar{\tau}
.

Lemma

6.5
implies that
\Delta(\bar{\tau})
is
n
-
fixed.

We now observe that
\Delta(\bar{\tau})
is
n
-
saturated, using
n
-
saturation of
\bar{\tau}
and
6.1

\displaystyle\Delta(\bar{\tau}){\downharpoonleft}_{n+1}=\bar{\tau}{\downharpoonleft}_{n+1}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\Delta(\Delta(\bar{\tau})){\downharpoonleft}_{n+1}=\Delta(\bar{\tau}){\downharpoonleft}_{n+1}.

Next, we prove that
\Delta(\bar{\tau})
is ordered.
By
n
-
saturation of
\bar{\tau}
, for
i\in\{n{+}1,\ldots,d{-}1\}
we know that
\Delta(\bar{\tau})_{i}=\tau_{i}
, and
\bar{\tau}
is ordered.
By the fact that
\Delta(\bar{\tau})
is
n
-
fixed, the first coordinates of
\Delta(\bar{\tau})
, up to the
n
-
th, are all equal.
Therefore, it remains to check the order of
\Delta(\bar{\tau})_{n}
with respect to the next two coordinates,
that is,
\Delta(\bar{\tau})_{n+1}
and
\Delta(\bar{\tau})_{n+2}
, if they exists.
Consider the case of odd
n
; the other case is symmetric.
Then one needs to check that
\Delta(\bar{\tau})_{n}\leq\Delta(\bar{\tau})_{n+1}
if
n<d{-}1
and
\Delta(\bar{\tau})_{n}\geq\Delta(\bar{\tau})_{n-2}
if
n<d{-}2
.
We have

\displaystyle\Delta(\bar{\tau})_{n}

\displaystyle=\delta\big(\tau_{n}

\displaystyle,\tau_{n}

\displaystyle,\ldots

\displaystyle,\tau_{n}

\displaystyle,\tau_{n+1}

\displaystyle,\tau_{n+2},\tau_{n+3},\ldots,\tau_{d-1}

\displaystyle\big)

\displaystyle\leq\delta\big(\tau_{n+1}

\displaystyle,\tau_{n+1}

\displaystyle,\ldots

\displaystyle,\tau_{n+1}

\displaystyle,\tau_{n+1}

\displaystyle,\tau_{n+2},\tau_{n+3},\ldots,\tau_{d-1}

\displaystyle\big)=\Delta(\bar{\tau})_{n+1}

because
\bar{\tau}
is ordered (i.e.,
\tau_{n}\leq\tau_{n+1}
) and
\delta
is monotone.
Likewise
\tau_{n+1}\geq\tau_{n}\geq\tau_{n+2}
, which implies

\displaystyle\Delta(\bar{\tau})_{n}

\displaystyle=\delta\big(\tau_{n}

\displaystyle,\tau_{n}

\displaystyle,\ldots

\displaystyle,\tau_{n}

\displaystyle,\tau_{n+1}

\displaystyle,\tau_{n+2},\tau_{n+3},\ldots,\tau_{d-1}

\displaystyle\big)

\displaystyle\geq\delta\big(\tau_{n+2}

\displaystyle,\tau_{n+2}

\displaystyle,\ldots

\displaystyle,\tau_{n+2}

\displaystyle,\tau_{n+2}

\displaystyle,\tau_{n+2},\tau_{n+3},\ldots,\tau_{d-1}

\displaystyle\big)=\Delta(\bar{\tau})_{n+2}.

Finally, monotonicity of
\Delta
allows us to deduce
\Delta(\Delta(\bar{\tau}))\geq\Delta(\bar{\tau})
from
\Delta(\bar{\tau})\geq\bar{\tau}
(for odd
n
)
and
\Delta(\Delta(\bar{\tau}))\leq\Delta(\bar{\tau})
from
\Delta(\bar{\tau})\leq\bar{\tau}
(for even

).

This finishes the proof of
\Delta(\bar{\tau})\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
, which allows us to derive
\Delta\!::\mathcal{S}_{n}\to\mathcal{S}_{n}
.
The fact that
\Delta
is
n
-
stable on
\mathcal{S}_{n}
is immediate: it is just another way of saying that elements of
\mathcal{S}_{n}
are
n
-
saturated.
∎

Lemma 6.10
.

For
n\in\{0,\ldots,d{-}1\}
we can derive

\displaystyle{\Delta}{\uparrow}

\displaystyle\!::\mathcal{S}_{n}\to\mathcal{S}_{n-1}

\displaystyle{\Delta}{\downarrow}

\displaystyle\!::\mathcal{S}_{n}\to\mathcal{S}_{n-1}

the assumption that

Lemma 6.11
.

\displaystyle,\tau^{\prime}_{n}

\displaystyle,\ldots

\displaystyle,\tau^{\prime}_{n}

\displaystyle,\tau^{\prime}_{n+1}

\displaystyle,\tau^{\prime}_{n+2}

\displaystyle,\tau^{\prime}_{n+3}

\displaystyle,\tau_{n+2}

\displaystyle,\ldots

\displaystyle,\tau_{n+2}

\displaystyle,\tau_{n+2}

In this section we recall some facts concerning the standard

Let

First, by the Knaster

), for an element

\tau\leq\nu x.\,f(x)

Second, we have the following fact.

Fact 8.1

(
[

, Corollary 1.3.8]
)
.

Let
f\colon\mathcal{V}^{n}\to\mathcal{V}
be monotone, let
\theta_{0},\ldots,\theta_{n-1}\in\{\mu,\nu\}
, and let
\sigma_{\bot},\sigma_{\top}\in\mathcal{V}
.
If
\sigma_{\bot}\leq\theta_{n-1}x_{n-1}\ldots\theta_{0}x_{0}.\,f(x_{0},\ldots,x_{n-1})\leq\sigma_{\top}
then

\displaystyle\theta_{n-1}x_{n-1}\ldots\theta_{0}x_{0}.\,f(x_{0}

\displaystyle,x_{1},\ldots,x_{n-1})

\displaystyle=\,

\displaystyle\theta_{n-1}x_{n-1}\ldots\theta_{0}x_{0}.\,f((x_{0}{\wedge}\sigma_{\top}){\vee}\sigma_{\bot}

\displaystyle,x_{1},\ldots,x_{n-1}).

∎

We now have a series of lemmata, which allow us to simplify some expressions of
\mu
-
calculus.
The first lemma can be shown using directly the Knaster
-
Tarski theorem.

Lemma 8.2
.

For every monotone function
f\colon\mathcal{V}^{2}\to\mathcal{V}
, we have

\displaystyle\nu y.\,\mu x.\,f(x{\land}y,y)=\nu y.\,\mu x.\,f(x,y),

\displaystyle\mu y.\,\nu x.\,f(x{\lor}y,y)=\mu y.\,\nu x.\,f(x,y).

Proof.

We prove only the first equality; the second one is symmetric.
Let
L=\nu y.\,\mu x.\,f(x{\wedge}y,y)
and
R=\nu y.\,\mu x.\,f(x,y)
.
The inequality
L\leq R
follows from monotonicity.
To show
R\leq L
, by the Knaster
-
Tarski theorem it is enough to show

\displaystyle R\leq\mu x.\,f(x{\wedge}R,R).

Let
R^{\prime}=\mu x.\,f(x{\wedge}R,R)
.
Since
R
itself is a fixed point,
R=\mu x.\,f(x,R)
, therefore, again by the Knaster
-
Tarski theorem, to show
R\leq R^{\prime}
,
it is enough to show
f(R^{\prime},R)\leq R^{\prime}
.
But we know that, by monotonicity,
R^{\prime}\leq R
, hence
R^{\prime}{\wedge}R=R^{\prime}
and then
R^{\prime}=f(R^{\prime}{\wedge}R,R)=f(R^{\prime},R)
.
Thus the claim is proved.
∎

In a proof of the next lemma we use the Knaster
-
Tarski theorem and

.

Lemma 8.3
.

For every monotone function
f\colon\mathcal{V}^{3}\to\mathcal{V}
, taking

\displaystyle L

\displaystyle=\mu z.\,\nu y.\,\mu x.\,f(x{\lor}z

\displaystyle,y,z),

\displaystyle R

\displaystyle=\mu z.\,\nu y.\,\mu x.\,f(x

\displaystyle,y,z),

we have
L=R
.

Proof.

Since
f(x{\lor}z,y,z)\geq f(x,y,z)
, by monotonicity of fixed-point operations we have
L=\mu z.\,\nu y.\,\mu x.\,f(x{\lor}z,y,z)\geq R
.
To show the other inequality, by the Knaster
-
Tarski theorem, it is enough to show

\displaystyle\nu y.\,\mu x.\,f(x{\lor}R,y,R)\leq R.

(8.1)

By
8.1
(used with
\sigma_{\bot}=R
and
\sigma_{\top}=\top
), we have

\displaystyle R=\mu z.\,\nu y.\,\mu x.\,f(x{\lor}R,y,z)=\nu y.\,\mu x.\,f(x{\lor}R,y,R)

(the last equality by definition of a fixed point).
This implies
Eq.

8.1
and completes the proof.
∎

The next lemma is again just a (somewhat tricky) consequence of the Knaster
-
Tarski theorem.

Lemma 8.4
.

For every monotone function
g\colon\mathcal{V}^{2}\to\mathcal{V}
, taking

\displaystyle L

\displaystyle=\mu y.\,\nu x.\,g\big(x,\mu z.\,g(z{\lor}y,z{\lor}y)\big),

\displaystyle R

\displaystyle=\mu y.\,\nu x.\,g(x,y),

we have
L=R
.

Proof.

We first show
L\leq R
.
By the Knaster
-
Tarski theorem, it is enough to show

\displaystyle\nu x.\,g\big(x,\mu z.\,g(z{\lor}R,z{\lor}R)\big)\leq R.

We have, by definition,
R=\nu x.\,g(x,R)
, so, by monotonicity, it is enough to show
\mu z.\,g(z{\lor}R,z{\lor}R)\leq R
.
But this follows immediately (again by the Knaster
-
Tarski theorem) from the fact that
R=g(R,R)=g(R{\lor}R,R{\lor}R)
.

To show
R\leq L
, we shall in turn show

\displaystyle\nu x.\,g(x,L)\leq L.

We have, by definition,
L=\nu x.\,g(x,M)
, where
M=\mu z.\,g(z{\lor}L,z{\lor}L)
.
It is then enough to show
L\leq M
.
But we have
L=g(L,M)
and
M=g(M{\lor}L,M{\lor}L)
, thus the claim follows by monotonicity.
∎

Our next lemma is a variant of
Lemma

8.4
, where we limit ourselves to the sublattice between
\sigma_{\bot}
and
\sigma_{\top}
.
The statement may look somewhat complicated, but this is exactly what we need in the next section.

Lemma 8.5
.

Let
f\colon\mathcal{V}^{2}\to\mathcal{V}
be monotone, let
\sigma_{\bot},\sigma_{\top}\in\mathcal{V}
, and let

\displaystyle L

\displaystyle=\mu y.\,\nu x.\,f\big((x{\lor}h(y)){\land}\sigma_{\top},h(y)\big)

where

\displaystyle h(y)

\displaystyle=\mu z.\,f(z{\lor}y{\lor}\sigma_{\bot},z{\lor}y{\lor}\sigma_{\bot})

and

\displaystyle R

\displaystyle=\mu y.\,\nu x.\,f\big((x{\lor}((y{\land}\sigma_{\top}){\lor}\sigma_{\bot})){\land}\sigma_{\top},(y{\land}\sigma_{\top}){\lor}\sigma_{\bot}\big).

If
\sigma_{\bot}\leq L\leq\sigma_{\top}
,
f(\sigma_{\top},\sigma_{\top})=\sigma_{\top}
, and
f(\sigma_{\bot},\sigma_{\bot})\geq\sigma_{\bot}
,
then
L=R
.

Proof.

First, because of
\sigma_{\bot}\leq L\leq\sigma_{\top}
, by

with
(x{\land}\sigma_{\top}){\lor}\sigma_{\bot}
and

(y{\land}\sigma_{\top}){\lor}\sigma_{\bot}

\displaystyle L=\mu y.\,\nu x.\,f\big(((x{\land}\sigma_{\top}){\lor}\sigma_{\bot}{\lor}h^{\prime}(y)){\land}\sigma_{\top},h^{\prime}(y))\big),

where

\displaystyle h^{\prime}(y)=\mu z.\,f\big(z{\lor}(y{\land}\sigma_{\top}){\lor}\sigma_{\bot},z{\lor}(y{\land}\sigma_{\top}){\lor}\sigma_{\bot}\big).

Next, for every value of

\sigma_{\bot}\leq(y{\land}\sigma_{\top}){\lor}\sigma_{\bot}\leq\sigma_{\top}
, so by monotonicity

\displaystyle\mu z.\,f(z{\lor}\sigma_{\bot},z{\lor}\sigma_{\bot})\leq h^{\prime}(y)\leq\mu z.\,f(z{\lor}\sigma_{\top},z{\lor}\sigma_{\top}).

On the one hand, we have

\displaystyle\sigma_{\bot}\leq f(\sigma_{\bot},\sigma_{\bot})=\mu z.\,f(\sigma_{\bot},\sigma_{\bot})\leq\mu z.\,f(z{\lor}\sigma_{\bot},z{\lor}\sigma_{\bot}).

On the other hand, the assumption
f(\sigma_{\top},\sigma_{\top})=\sigma_{\top}
says that
\sigma_{\top}
is a fixed point of the function

,
necessarily greater or equal than the least fixed point
\mu z.\,f(z{\lor}\sigma_{\top},z{\lor}\sigma_{\top})
.
We thus have
\sigma_{\bot}\leq h^{\prime}(y)\leq\sigma_{\top}

\displaystyle h^{\prime}(x)=\mu z.\,f\big(((z{\lor}y){\land}\sigma_{\top}){\lor}\sigma_{\bot},((z{\lor}y){\land}\sigma_{\top}){\lor}\sigma_{\bot}\big).

Due to
\sigma_{\bot}\leq h^{\prime}(y)\leq\sigma_{\top}
we can also change

into
(h^{\prime}(y){\land}\sigma_{\top}){\lor}\sigma_{\bot}
in the previous formula for

, which gives us

\displaystyle L=\mu y.\,\nu x.\,f\big(((x{\lor}h^{\prime}(y)){\land}\sigma_{\top}){\lor}\sigma_{\bot},(h^{\prime}(y){\land}\sigma_{\top}){\lor}\sigma_{\bot}\big).

Denote now

\displaystyle g(x,y)=f\big(((x{\lor}y){\land}\sigma_{\top}){\lor}\sigma_{\bot},(y{\land}\sigma_{\top}){\lor}\sigma_{\bot}\big);

then

\displaystyle L=\mu y.\,\nu x.\,g\big(x,\mu z.\,g(z{\lor}y,z{\lor}y)\big).

Using
Lemma

8.4
and expanding the definition of

\displaystyle L

\displaystyle=\mu y.\,\nu x.\,g(x,y)

\displaystyle=\mu y.\,\nu x.\,f\big(((x{\lor}y){\land}\sigma_{\top}){\lor}\sigma_{\bot}

\displaystyle,(y{\land}\sigma_{\top}){\lor}\sigma_{\bot}\big)

\displaystyle=\mu y.\,\nu x.\,f\big((x{\lor}((y{\land}\sigma_{\top}){\lor}\sigma_{\bot})){\land}\sigma_{\top}

\displaystyle,(y{\land}\sigma_{\top}){\lor}\sigma_{\bot}\big)=R.

∎

Proposition 9.1

\displaystyle\Phi_{d-1}(\bot)_{d-1}=\mu x_{d-1}.\,\nu x_{d-2}.\,\mu x_{d-3}.\,\nu x_{d-4}\ldots\mu x_{1}.\,\nu x_{0}.\,\delta(x_{0},\ldots,x_{d-1}).

At first, from the formulae
\Phi_{n}
of unary
\mu

We first give a general lemma about
{F}{\uparrow}
and
{F}{\downarrow}
for any partial function
F
.

Lemma 9.2
.

Let
F\colon\mathcal{V}^{d}\rightharpoonup\mathcal{V}^{d}
be a partial function that is
n
-
stable on
A\subseteq\mathcal{V}^{d}
, for some
n\in\{0,\ldots,d{-}1\}
.
Let also
\varphi\colon\mathcal{V}^{d-n}\to\mathcal{V}
be such that

\displaystyle F(\bar{\tau})_{n}=\varphi(\tau_{n},\dots,\tau_{d-1})

(9.1)

for all
\bar{\tau}=(\tau_{0},\ldots,\tau_{d-1})\in A
.
If
F
is ascending on
A
then, for all
\bar{\tau}\in A

({F}{\uparrow})(\bar{\tau})_{n}=\mu y_{n}.\,\varphi(y_{n}{\lor}\tau_{n},\tau_{n+1},\ldots,\tau_{d-1}).

Dually, if
F
is descending on
A
then, for all
\bar{\tau}\in A

({F}{\downarrow})(\bar{\tau})_{n}=\nu y_{n}.\,\varphi(y_{n}{\land}\tau_{n},\tau_{n+1},\ldots,\tau_{d-1}).

A proof of this lemma follows from the fact that
F
is ascending (resp. descending) on
A
,
and thus the operation
\lor
(resp.
\land
) on the first coordinate of the argument of
\varphi
boils down to taking the least (resp. greatest) fixed point which is
{\geq}
(resp.
{\leq}
)
\tau_{n}
.

Proof.

Let us concentrate on the case when
F
is ascending on
A
; the case of descending
F
is symmetric.
Fix some
\bar{\tau}\in A
and denote
\sigma=\mu y_{n}.\,\varphi(y_{n}{\lor}\tau_{n},\tau_{n+1},\ldots,\tau_{d-1})
.
First, by
Lemma

5.3
we have
({F}{\uparrow})(\bar{\tau})=F^{\infty}(\bar{\tau})
.
Recall the sequence
F^{\eta}(\bar{\tau})
used to define
F^{\infty}(\bar{\tau})
as
F^{\eta_{\infty}}(\bar{\tau})
.
By construction
F^{\eta}(\bar{\tau})\in A
and
F^{\eta}(\bar{\tau})\geq\bar{\tau}
for all ordinals
\eta
.

First, as in the proof of
Lemma

6.6
, we can see that
F^{\eta}(\bar{\tau})_{i}=\tau_{i}
for
i\in\{n{+}1,\dots,d{-}1\}
, for all ordinals
\eta
.

We now prove by induction on
\eta
that
F^{\eta}(\bar{\tau})_{n}\leq\sigma
.
For
\eta=0
we have

\displaystyle F^{0}(\bar{\tau})_{n}=\tau_{n}\leq F(\bar{\tau})_{n}

\displaystyle=\varphi(

\displaystyle\tau_{n}

\displaystyle,\tau_{n+1},\ldots,\tau_{d-1})

\displaystyle\leq\varphi(

\displaystyle\sigma{\lor}\tau_{n}

\displaystyle,\tau_{n+1},\ldots,\tau_{d-1})=\sigma.

For a successor ordinal
\eta
suppose that
F^{\eta-1}(\bar{\tau})_{n}\leq\sigma
.
Recalling that
F^{\eta-1}(\bar{\tau})_{n}\geq\tau_{n}
, we have

\displaystyle F^{\eta}(\bar{\tau})_{n}=F\big(F^{\eta-1}(\bar{\tau})\big)_{n}

\displaystyle=\varphi\big(

\displaystyle F^{\eta-1}(\bar{\tau})_{n}

\displaystyle,F^{\eta-1}(\bar{\tau})_{n+1}

\displaystyle,\ldots

\displaystyle,F^{\eta-1}(\bar{\tau})_{d-1}

\displaystyle\big)

\displaystyle=\varphi\big(

\displaystyle F^{\eta-1}(\bar{\tau})_{n}{\lor}\tau_{n}

\displaystyle,\tau_{n+1}

\displaystyle,\ldots

\displaystyle,\tau_{d-1}

\displaystyle\big)

\displaystyle\leq\varphi\big(

\displaystyle\sigma{\lor}\tau_{n}

\displaystyle,\tau_{n+1}

\displaystyle,\ldots

\displaystyle,\tau_{d-1}

\displaystyle\big)=\sigma,

as required.

Next, consider a limit ordinal
\eta
, and suppose that for all
\zeta<\eta
we have
F^{\zeta}(\bar{\tau})_{n}\leq\sigma
.
Recall that
F^{\eta}(\bar{\tau})
is the supremum of
\{F^{\zeta}(\bar{\tau})\mid\zeta<\eta\}
.
This supremum is taken coordinate
-

F^{\eta}(\bar{\tau})_{n}
is the supremum of
\{F^{\zeta}(\bar{\tau})_{n}\mid\zeta<\eta\}
.
Because
\sigma
is an upper bound of this chain, we have
F^{\eta}(\bar{\tau})_{n}\leq\sigma
.

The above in particular shows that
F^{\eta_{\infty}}(\bar{\tau})_{n}\leq\sigma
.
Simultaneously

\displaystyle F^{\eta_{\infty}}(\bar{\tau})_{n}=F^{\eta_{\infty}+1}(\bar{\tau})_{n}=F\big(F^{\eta_{\infty}}(\bar{\tau})\big)_{n}

\displaystyle=\varphi\big(F^{\eta_{\infty}}(\bar{\tau})_{n}

\displaystyle,F^{\eta_{\infty}}(\bar{\tau})_{n+1}

\displaystyle,\ldots

\displaystyle,F^{\eta_{\infty}}(\bar{\tau})_{d-1}

\displaystyle\big)

\displaystyle=\varphi\big(F^{\eta_{\infty}}(\bar{\tau})_{n}{\lor}\tau_{n}

\displaystyle,\tau_{n+1}

\displaystyle,\ldots

\displaystyle,\tau_{d-1}

\displaystyle\big).

This means that
F^{\eta_{\infty}}(\bar{\tau})_{n}
is a fixed point of the function

y_{n}\mapsto\varphi(y_{n}{\lor}\tau_{n},\tau_{n+1},\ldots,\allowbreak\tau_{d-1}),

whose least fixed point is
\sigma
.
We thus have
F^{\eta_{\infty}}(\bar{\tau})_{n}=\sigma
, which finishes the proof.
∎

∎

and

\Phi_{d-1}(\bot)_{d-1}=\psi_{d-1}()

(recall that

);

.

10
Measurability

In our final argument (
Section

12
) we want to describe profiles
\tau\in\mathcal{V}
by measures of sets like
\{t\in\mathrm{Tr}_{\Sigma}\mid\tau(t)=R\}
for some fixed
R
.
For this to make sense, we need to prove that such sets are measurable for all profiles
\tau
in consideration;
this is the goal of this section.

Recall that the set of profiles is
\mathcal{V}=\big(\mathrm{Tr}_{\Sigma}\to\mathsf{P}(Q)\big)
.
Define
\mathcal{R}
as the set
\mathsf{P}\big(Q\times\{0,\ldots,\allowbreak d{-}1\}\big)
.
Instead of
\mathcal{V}^{d}
we prefer to consider
\mathcal{W}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}(\mathrm{Tr}_{\Sigma}\to\mathcal{R})
.

There is a canonical bijection between
\mathcal{V}^{d}
and
\mathcal{W}
:
a tuple
\bar{\tau}=(\tau_{0},\ldots,\tau_{d-1})\in\mathcal{V}^{d}
is in bijection with a function in
\mathcal{W}
that maps a tree
t
to
\big\{(q,i)\mid q\in\tau_{i}(t)\big\}
.
The coordinate
-
wise order
\leq
on
\mathcal{V}^{d}
is then mapped to the order
\leq
on
\mathcal{W}
given for
\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}\in\mathcal{W}

by
\sigma\leq\sigma^{\prime}
if
\sigma(t)\subseteq\sigma^{\prime}(t)
for all trees
t\in\mathrm{Tr}_{\Sigma}
.
We can thus identify these two spaces,
\mathcal{V}^{d}
and
\mathcal{W}
.
We denote the elements of
\mathcal{W}
by normal letters
\tau
,
\sigma
, as they are no longer treated as vectors.

The measurability argument given in this section follows that from Gogacz et al.
[
GMMS17
, page 117]
—the proof that
E^{\alpha}_{v}
are all measurable and their measure converges to the measure of their union.
This is a variant of the technique used by Lusin and Sierpiński
[
LS18
]
to prove that the set
-
theoretic
Souslin operation

\mathcal{A}
preserves measurability.

Our proof (except of
Lemma

10.3
, talking about particular basic functions) works for a general space of the form
\mathcal{W}=(\mathrm{Tr}_{\Sigma}\to\mathcal{R})
,
with
(\mathcal{R},\subseteq)
being a finite complete lattice; in our case
\mathcal{R}=\mathsf{P}\big(Q\times\{0,\ldots,d{-}1\}\big)
, and
\subseteq
is inclusion of sets.

Definition 10.1
.

We say that
\tau\in\mathcal{W}
is a
random variable
if for every
R\in\mathcal{R}
the set of trees
\tau^{-1}(\{R\})
is measurable.

We say that a partial function
F\colon\mathcal{W}\rightharpoonup\mathcal{W}
is
local
on a set
A\subseteq\mathcal{W}
if
A\subseteq\mathrm{dom}(F)
and for all
\tau,\tau^{\prime}\in A
and all trees
t\in\mathrm{Tr}_{\Sigma}
,
if
\tau
and
\tau^{\prime}
agree on all the subtrees of
t
then also
F(\tau)
and
F(\tau^{\prime})
agree on all the subtrees of
t
.

We say that
F\colon\mathcal{W}\rightharpoonup\mathcal{W}

preserves measurability
on
A\subseteq\mathcal{W}
if
A\subseteq\mathrm{dom}(F)
and whenever
\tau\in A
is a random variable then so is
F(\tau)
.

Because the measure on
\mathrm{Tr}_{\Sigma}
is the product measure, where the probability in every node is defined in the same way, we have the following
Section
.

Fact 10.2
.

Let
u\in\{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle L}},\mathtt{{\scriptstyle R}}\}^{\ast}
.
If a set
S\subseteq\mathrm{Tr}_{\Sigma}
is measurable, then
\{t\in\mathrm{Tr}_{\Sigma}\mid t.u\in S\}
is measurable as well.
Moreover, the measure of these two sets is the same.
∎

Lemma 10.3
.

All the basic functions
\Delta
,
\mathrm{Bid}_{n}
,
\mathrm{Cut}_{n}
(when treated as functions on
\mathcal{W}
) are local and preserve measurability on the whole
\mathcal{W}
.

Proof.

We say that a function
F\colon\mathcal{W}\to\mathcal{W}
is
superficial

if there exists a function
F_{\mathcal{R}}\colon\mathcal{R}\to\mathcal{R}
such that
F(\tau)(t)=F_{\mathcal{R}}(\tau(t))
for all
\tau\in\mathcal{W}
and
t\in\mathrm{Tr}_{\Sigma}
.
Note that the functions
\mathrm{Bid}_{n}
and
\mathrm{Cut}_{n}
are superficial.
Clearly every superficial function
F\colon\mathcal{W}\to\mathcal{W}
is local on
\mathcal{W}
;
it also preserves measurability on
\mathcal{W}
because for any
\tau\in\mathcal{W}
and
R\in\mathcal{R}
the set

\big(F(\tau)\big)^{-1}\big(\{R\}\big)=\big\{t\in\mathrm{Tr}_{\Sigma}\mid F(\tau)(t)=R\big\}=\big\{t\in\mathrm{Tr}_{\Sigma}\mid F_{\mathcal{R}}(\tau(t))=R\big\}

is a finite union of sets of the form
\tau^{-1}\big(\{R^{\prime}\}\big)
ranging over certain
R^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}
.

Now consider
\Delta
.
Clearly, the value of
\Delta(\tau)(t)
depends only on
t(\varepsilon)\in\Sigma
and the values of
\tau(t.\mathtt{{\scriptstyle L}})
and
\tau(t.\mathtt{{\scriptstyle R}})
, so
\Delta
is also local on
\mathcal{W}
.
Next, observe that for any
\tau\in\mathcal{W}
and
R\in\mathcal{R}
the set

\big(\Delta(\tau)\big)^{-1}\big(\{R\}\big)=\big\{t\in\mathrm{Tr}_{\Sigma}\mid\Delta(\tau)(t)=R\big\},

is a finite union of the sets of the form

\big\{t\in\mathrm{Tr}_{\Sigma}\mid t(\varepsilon)=a\land\tau(t.\mathtt{{\scriptstyle L}})=R^{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle L}}}\land\tau(t.\mathtt{{\scriptstyle R}})=R^{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle R}}}\big\},

ranging over certain
a
,
R^{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle L}}}
,
R^{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle R}}}
.
These sets are measurable by
10.2
.
∎

Let
\zeta
be an ordinal and let
(x_{\zeta})_{\zeta<\eta}
be a sequence of real numbers.
We say that this sequence
converges
to
y\in\mathbb{R}
(or that
y
is the
limit
of
(x_{\zeta})_{\zeta<\eta}
)
if for every
\varepsilon>0
there exists
\zeta_{0}<\eta
such that for every
\zeta
satisfying
\zeta_{0}\leq\zeta<\eta
we have
|x_{\zeta}-y|<\varepsilon
.

Consider an ordinal
\eta
and take a sequence of random variables
(\tau_{\zeta})_{\zeta<\eta}
from
\mathcal{W}
.
We say that this sequence
converges in measure
to a random variable
\sigma\in\mathcal{W}

if for every set
R\in\mathcal{R}
the sequence of numbers
(x_{\zeta})_{\zeta<\eta}
defined as
x_{\zeta}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\prob\big(\tau_{\zeta}^{-1}(\{R\})\big)

converges to the number
\prob\big(\sigma^{-1}(\{R\})\big)
.

10.1
Taking limits

The crucial difficulty and potential source of non
-
measurability may come from the operations
{F}{\uparrow}
and
{F}{\downarrow}
.
This is taken care of by the following proposition.

Proposition 10.4
.

Let
F\colon\mathcal{W}\rightharpoonup\mathcal{W}
be a partial function that is ether ascending or descending on
A\subseteq\mathcal{W}
,
local on
A
, and preserves measurability on
A
.
Assume that
\tau\in A
is a random variable.
Then all
F^{\eta}(\tau)
from the definition of
F^{\infty}(\tau)
are random variables.
Moreover, for every limit ordinal
\eta
the sequence
(F^{\zeta}(\tau))_{\zeta<\eta}
converges in measure to
F^{\eta}(\tau)
.

The rest of the subsection is devoted to a proof of this
Proposition
.

Before starting, let us recall two known facts.
The first of them holds because the supremum for ordinals is defined as the union.

Fact 10.5
.

The supremum of a countable set of countable ordinals is countable.
∎

The next lemma is a classical result in measure theory.
It is usually stated for sequences indexed by naturals numbers (see, e.g., Oxtoby
[
Oxt80
, Theorem 3.17]
), but we need it in a slightly more general form.

Lemma 10.6
.

Let
\eta>0
be a countable ordinal and let
(V_{\zeta})_{\zeta<\eta}
be an increasing sequence of measurable sets.
Then the (countable) union
V\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\bigcup_{\zeta<\eta}V_{\zeta}
is measurable and the measures of
V_{\zeta}
converge to the measure of
V
.

Similarly for a decreasing sequence and its intersection.

Proof.

Consider the case of an increasing sequence
(V_{\zeta})_{\zeta<\eta}
; the part about a descending sequence can be deduced by switching to complements.
If
\eta
is a successor ordinal then
V=V_{\eta-1}
and the
Lemma
follows.

Assume that
\eta
is a limit ordinal.
Because
\eta
is countable, there are only countably many ordinals
\zeta<\eta
, which means that we can organise them into a sequence indexed by natural numbers.
Out of this sequence let us select only those elements that are greater than all elements before them.
This way we obtain an increasing sequence of ordinals
(\zeta_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
such that
\eta=\sup_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\zeta_{i}
.

We then have
V=\bigcup_{i\in\mathbb{N}}V_{\zeta_{i}}
with
(V_{\zeta_{i}})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
being an increasing sequence of sets,
indexed by natural numbers.
Thus, continuity of measure in its usual formulation
[
Oxt80
, Theorem 3.17]
implies that the measure of
V
is the limit of the measures of
V_{\zeta_{i}}
.
But this implies that the measures of
(V_{\zeta})_{\zeta<\eta}
also converge to the measure of
V
.
∎

Lemma

10.6
implies the following standard extension to random variables.

Lemma 10.7
.

Let
\eta>0
be a countable ordinal and let
(\tau_{\zeta})_{\zeta<\eta}
be a sequence of random variables from
\mathcal{W}
.
If the sequence is monotonic then its limit
\sigma
is a random variable and the sequence
(\tau_{\zeta})_{\zeta<\eta}
converges in measure to
\sigma
.

Proof.

Consider the case of an increasing sequence.
Let
\sigma
be the limit from the statement and take
R\in\mathcal{R}
.
We need to prove that
\sigma^{-1}(\{R\})
is a measurable subset of
\mathrm{Tr}_{\Sigma}
and that it is the limit of
\tau_{\zeta}^{-1}(\{R\})
.

For
\zeta<\eta
let
U_{\zeta}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\{t\in\mathrm{Tr}_{\Sigma}\mid\tau_{\zeta}(t)\supseteq R\}
and
V_{\zeta}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\{t\in\mathrm{Tr}_{\Sigma}\mid\tau_{\zeta}(t)\supsetneq R\}
.
Clearly all the sets
U_{\zeta}
,
V_{\zeta}
are measurable, as Boolean combinations of sets of the form
\tau_{\zeta}^{-1}(\{R^{\prime}\})
.
Moreover,
\tau_{\zeta}^{-1}(\{R\})=U_{\zeta}\setminus V_{\zeta}
.

Because the sequence
(\tau_{\zeta})_{\zeta<\eta}
is increasing, we know that also the two sequences of sets,
(U_{\zeta})_{\zeta<\eta}
and
(V_{\zeta})_{\zeta<\eta}
, are increasing.
Take
U\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\bigcup_{\zeta<\eta}U_{\zeta}
and
V\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\bigcup_{\zeta<\eta}V_{\zeta}
.
Apply
Lemma

10.6
to both these sets to notice that
U
and
V
are both measurable and
their measures are limits of the measures of
(U_{\zeta})_{\zeta<\eta}
and
(V_{\zeta})_{\zeta<\eta}
.
It follows that the measures of
\big(\tau_{\zeta}^{-1}(\{R\})\big)_{\zeta<\eta}
converge to the measure of
U\setminus V
.

It remains to prove that

\sigma^{-1}(\{R\})=U\setminus V.

To this end, consider a tree
t\in\mathrm{Tr}_{\Sigma}
.
Since the order in
\mathcal{W}
works separately on every tree, the sequence
\big(\tau_{\zeta}(t)\big)_{\zeta<\eta}
is increasing, and its limit equals
\sigma(t)
.
But this is an increasing sequence in a finite lattice
(\mathcal{R},\subseteq)
, so it must stabilise from some moment on:
for some
\zeta_{0}<\eta
we have
\sigma(t)=\tau_{\zeta}(t)
for all
\zeta
such that
\zeta_{0}\leq\zeta<\eta
.
It follows that
\sigma(t)\supseteq R
if and only if
t\in U
.
Similarly,
\sigma(t)\supsetneq R
if and only if
t\in V
.
Therefore,
t\in U\setminus V
if and only if
\sigma(t)=R
.
∎

We are now ready to start a proof of
Proposition

10.4
.
We consider the case when
F
is ascending on
A
; the dual case when
F
is descending on
A
is entirely symmetric.
We fix
\tau\in A
.

Claim 10.8
.

Let
t_{0}\in\mathrm{Tr}_{\Sigma}
, and let
\zeta
be an ordinal.
If
F^{\zeta+1}(\tau)
and
F^{\zeta}(\tau)
agree on all the subtrees of
t_{0}
,
then
F^{\eta}(\tau)
and
F^{\zeta}(\tau)
agree on all the subtrees of
t_{0}
(in particular
F^{\eta}(\tau)(t_{0})=F^{\zeta}(\tau)(t_{0})
), for all
\eta\geq\zeta
.

Proof.

Induction on
\eta\geq\zeta
.
For
\eta=\zeta
the statement is trivial.
For successor ordinals
\eta\geq\zeta+1
we have

\displaystyle F^{\eta}(\tau)(t)

\displaystyle=F(F^{\eta-1}(\tau))(t)=F(F^{\zeta}(\tau))(t)=F^{\zeta+1}(\tau)(t)=F^{\zeta}(\tau)(t)

for all the subtrees
t
of
t_{0}
, where the second equality is by the induction hypothesis and by locality of
F
on
A
.
If
\eta>\zeta
is a limit ordinal, then for every subtree
t
of
t_{0}
we have that
F^{\eta}(\tau)(t)

is the limit of the increasing sequence
\big(F^{\eta^{\prime}}(\tau)(t)\big)_{\eta^{\prime}<\eta}
that stabilises on
F^{\zeta}(\tau)(t)
by the induction hypothesis;
thus this limit equals
F^{\zeta}(\tau)(t)
.
∎

Below, we write
\omega_{1}
for the least uncountable ordinal.

Claim 10.9
.

We have
F^{\eta}(\tau)=F^{\omega_{1}}(\tau)
for all
\eta\geq\omega_{1}
.

Proof.

Consider a single tree
t\in\mathrm{Tr}_{\Sigma}
.
We prove that the value
F^{\eta}(\tau)(t)
is constant for all
\eta\geq\zeta_{t}
for some countable ordinal
\zeta_{t}
,
hence even more for all
\eta\geq\omega_{1}
.

Consider

Z_{t}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\big\{\eta<\omega_{1}\mid\exists v\in\{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle L}},\mathtt{{\scriptstyle R}}\}^{\ast}.\ F^{\eta+1}(\tau)(t.v)\neq F^{\eta}(\tau)(t.v)\big\}

and take
\zeta_{t}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\sup Z_{t}+1
.
Recall that
\big(F^{\eta}(\tau)\big)_{\eta<\omega_{1}}
is an increasing sequence.
Since
\mathcal{R}
is a finite lattice, the value of
F^{\eta}(\tau)(t.v)\in\mathcal{R}
on a single tree
t.v\in\mathrm{Tr}_{\Sigma}
can grow only finitely many times.
Because moreover there are only countably many nodes
v\in\{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle L}},\mathtt{{\scriptstyle R}}\}^{\ast}
, the set
Z_{t}
is countable;
in consequence,
\zeta_{t}<\omega_{1}
by
10.5
.
Simultaneously
\eta<\zeta_{t}
for all
\eta\in Z_{t}
, which means that
F^{\zeta_{t}+1}(\tau)
and
F^{\zeta_{t}}(\tau)
agree on all the subtrees of
t
.
By
10.8
we then have
F^{\eta}(\tau)(t)=F^{\zeta_{t}}(\tau)(t)
for all
\eta\geq\zeta_{t}
,
which finishes the proof.
∎

Because
F
preserves measurability on
A
and so does taking countable limits (see
Lemma

10.7
), we have the following
Section
.

Fact 10.10
.

All the elements
F^{\eta}(\tau)
for
\eta<\omega_{1}
are random variables.
Moreover, for every limit ordinal
\eta<\omega_{1}
the sequence
(F^{\zeta}(\tau))_{\zeta<\eta}
converges in measure to
F^{\eta}(\tau)
.
∎

Note that
10.10
gives us the thesis of
Proposition

10.4
for
\eta<\omega_{1}
.
Because
F^{\eta}(\tau)=F^{\omega_{1}}(\tau)
for all
\eta\geq\omega_{1}
(cf.
10.9
),
to finish the proof of
Proposition

10.4
it remains to prove that that
F^{\omega_{1}}(\tau)
is a random variable
and that the sequence
(F^{\zeta}(\tau))_{\zeta<\omega_{1}}
converges in measure to
F^{\omega_{1}}(\tau)
.

For all
\eta<\omega_{1}
,
R\in\mathcal{R}
, and
u\in\{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle L}},\mathtt{{\scriptstyle R}}\}^{\ast}
we define

E^{\eta}_{R,u}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\big\{t\in\mathrm{Tr}_{\Sigma}\mid R\subseteq F^{\eta}(\tau)(t.u)\big\}.

Claim 10.11
.

For each
R\in\mathcal{R}
and
u\in\{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle L}},\mathtt{{\scriptstyle R}}\}^{\ast}
the sequence of sets
\big(E^{\eta}_{R,u}\big)_{\eta<\omega_{1}}
is increasing in the subset order.
Moreover, all these sets are measurable.

Proof.

The fact that the sequence is increasing is obvious.
To see that each of the sets
E^{\eta}_{R,u}
is measurable it is enough to recall that
F^{\eta}(\tau)
is a random variable,
to use
10.2
for shifting from
t
to
t.u
,
and to observe that
R\subseteq\tau(t)
if and only if
t
belongs to the (finite) union of the sets
\tau^{-1}(\{R^{\prime}\})
for
R^{\prime}\supseteq R
.
∎

Fix
R\in\mathcal{R}
and
u\in\{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle L}},\mathtt{{\scriptstyle R}}\}^{\ast}
and consider the measures
m_{\eta}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\prob\big(E^{\eta}_{R,u}\big)
for
\eta<\omega_{1}
.
It is a nondecreasing sequence of real numbers in
[0,1]
indexed by ordinals.
Whenever
m_{\eta+1}>m_{\eta}
then there exists a rational number in the interval
(m_{\eta},m_{\eta+1})
.
Thus, the set

\displaystyle Z_{R,u}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\{\eta<\omega_{1}\mid m_{\eta+1}\neq m_{\eta}\}

is countable.
Let
\zeta_{R,u}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\sup Z_{R,u}+1
.
We have
\zeta_{R,u}<\omega_{1}
by
10.5
and

\prob\big(E^{\eta}_{R,u}\big)=\prob\big(E^{\zeta_{R,u}}_{R,u}\big)
for all
\eta\geq\zeta_{R,u}
by definition.
Let
\zeta
be the supremum of all the (countably many) numbers
\zeta_{R,u}
for
R\in\mathcal{R}
and
u\in\{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle L}},\mathtt{{\scriptstyle R}}\}^{\ast}
.
Using again
10.5
we see that
\zeta<\omega_{1}
.
Let

U\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\bigcup_{R\in\mathcal{R},u\in\{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle L}},\mathtt{{\scriptstyle R}}\}^{\ast}}\left(E^{\zeta+1}_{R,u}\setminus E^{\zeta}_{R,u}\right).

Notice that for every
R\in\mathcal{R}
and
u\in\{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle L}},\mathtt{{\scriptstyle R}}\}^{\ast}
we have
\prob\big(E^{\zeta+1}_{R,u}\big)=\prob\big(E^{\zeta}_{R,u}\big)
and, therefore, the set
E^{\zeta+1}_{R,u}\setminus E^{\zeta}_{R,u}
has measure
0
.
This means that also
U
is of measure
0
, as a countable union of such sets.

Claim 10.12
.

If
t_{0}\in\mathrm{Tr}_{\Sigma}
is such that
F^{\omega_{1}}(\tau)(t_{0})\neq F^{\zeta}(\tau)(t_{0})
then
t_{0}\in U
.

Proof.

Consider such a tree and assume for the sake of contradiction that
t_{0}\notin U
.
By the definition of
U
it means that for every
R\in\mathcal{R}
and
u\in\{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle L}},\mathtt{{\scriptstyle R}}\}^{\ast}
we have
t_{0}\in E^{\zeta}_{R,u}\Leftrightarrow t_{0}\in E^{\zeta+1}_{R,u}
.
This implies that whenever
t
is a subtree of
t_{0}
(i.e., whenever
t=t_{0}.u
for some
u\in\{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle L}},\mathtt{{\scriptstyle R}}\}^{\ast}
) then
F^{\zeta}(\tau)(t)=F^{\zeta+1}(\tau)(t)
.
Thus, by
10.8
we know that for every
\eta\geq\zeta
we have
F^{\eta}(\tau)(t_{0})=F^{\zeta}(\tau)(t_{0})
.
Due to
\zeta<\omega_{1}
this implies that
F^{\zeta}(\tau)(t_{0})=F^{\omega_{1}}(\tau)(t_{0})
, a contradiction.
∎

For every set
R\in\mathcal{R}
, denoting
V_{R}=\big(F^{\zeta}(\tau)\big)^{-1}\big(\{R\}\big)
, we thus have

\displaystyle V_{R}\subseteq\big(F^{\omega_{1}}(\tau)\big)^{-1}\big(\{R\}\big)\subseteq V_{R}\cup U.

Because
V_{R}
is measurable (by
10.10
) and
U
has measure
0
,
the set
\big(F^{\eta_{\infty}}(\tau)\big)^{-1}\big(\{R\}\big)
lies between two sets of the same measure; it is thus measurable.

Actually, the above argument gives us more: the measure of the sets
\big(F^{\eta}(\tau)\big)^{-1}\big(\{R\}\big)
for all
\eta\geq\zeta
is the same.
Due to
\zeta<\omega_{1}
this means that sequence of measures of the sets
\big(F^{\eta}(\tau)\big)^{-1}\big(\{R\}\big)
stabilises at (hence its limit is equal to)
the measure of
\big(F^{\zeta}(\tau)\big)^{-1}\big(\{R\}\big)
, which equals the measure of
\big(F^{\omega_{1}}(\tau)\big)^{-1}\big(\{R\}\big)
.
The above holds for every set
R\in\mathcal{R}
which by definition means that
\big(F^{\eta}(\tau)\big)_{\eta<\omega_{1}}
converges in measure to
F^{\omega_{1}}(\tau)
.
This finishes the proof of
Proposition

10.4
.

10.2
Unary
\mu
-calculus preserves measurability

Let
\mathfrak{F}
be a signature and assume that
(\mathcal{W},{\leq})
is a structure over
\mathfrak{F}
in such a way
that for every function name
H\in\mathfrak{F}
the function
H_{\mathcal{W}}\colon\mathcal{W}\to\mathcal{W}
is local and preserves measurability on the whole
\mathcal{W}
.
Our goal is to prove that each formula of unary
\mu
-
calculus is also local and preserves measurability on a certain domain,
as stated in
Lemmas

10.13
and
10.14
.

As shown in
Lemma

10.3
, the above assumptions are met by our structure with functions
\Delta
,
\mathrm{Bid}_{n}
, and
\mathrm{Cut}_{n}
,
but results of this subsection are not specific to this structure.

Lemma 10.13
.

If we can derive
F\!::A\to B
for a formula
F
of unary
\mu
-
calculus then the partial function
F\colon\mathcal{W}\rightharpoonup\mathcal{W}
is local on
A
.

Proof.

Induction on the structure of the formula.
Base functions
H\in\mathfrak{F}
are local on the whole
\mathcal{W}
by the assumption.
For
F;G
we directly use the induction hypothesis.
Consider a formula of the form
{F}{\uparrow}
.
We use
Lemma

5.3
to see that
{F}{\uparrow}(\tau)=F^{\infty}(\tau)
for
\tau\in A
.
We then recall that
F^{\infty}(\tau)
is defined by applying
F
and by taking limits of monotonic sequences.
Every application of
F
is local: if
F^{\eta-1}(\tau)
and
F^{\eta-1}(\tau^{\prime})
agree on all the subtrees of some
t_{0}
,
then also
F^{\eta}(\tau)=F(F^{\eta-1}(\tau))
and
F^{\eta}(\tau^{\prime})=F(F^{\eta-1}(\tau^{\prime}))
agree on all the subtrees of
t_{0}
.
For limits we have an even stronger property:
if
F^{\zeta}(\tau)
and
F^{\zeta}(\tau^{\prime})
agree on a tree
t
(a subtree of
t_{0}
) for every
\zeta<\eta
, where
\eta
is a limit ordinal,
then also the limits
F^{\eta}(\tau)
and
F^{\eta}(\tau^{\prime})
agree on
t
,
because the order considers every tree
t
separately.
It follows that if
\tau,\tau^{\prime}\in A
agree on all the subtrees of
t_{0}
, then
{F}{\uparrow}(\tau)
and
{F}{\uparrow}(\tau^{\prime})
agree on all the subtrees of
t_{0}
, that is,
{F}{\uparrow}
is local on
A
.
Finally, the case of
{F}{\downarrow}
is symmetric.
∎

Lemma 10.14
.

If we can derive
F\!::A\to B
for a formula
F
of unary
\mu
-
calculus then the partial function
F\colon\mathcal{W}\rightharpoonup\mathcal{W}
preserves measurability on
A
.

Proof.

We proceed inductively over the shape of
F
.
For
H\in\mathfrak{F}
the thesis follows from the assumption on
H
, and for
F;G
it is a direct consequence of the induction hypothesis.
For a formula of the form
{F}{\uparrow}
we know that
F
is ascending on
A
because we can derive
{F}{\uparrow}\!::A\to B
;
that
F
is local on

by

10.13
; and that
F
preserves measurability on

by the induction hypothesis.
Then
Proposition

10.4
says in particular that
if
\tau
is a random variable then

(which equals
({F}{\uparrow})(\tau)
by

The case of

is analogous.
∎

11
Distributions

Each random variable
\tau\in\mathcal{W}
has its
distribution
—a function assigning measures to possible values of
\tau
.
Recall that
\mathcal{W}=(\mathrm{Tr}_{\Sigma}\to\mathcal{R})
, with
\mathcal{R}
being a finite complete lattice (in our case
\mathcal{R}=\mathsf{P}(Q\times\{0,\ldots,d{-}1\})
).
Let
\mathcal{D}=\mathbb{D}(\mathcal{R})
be the set of probability distributions over
\mathcal{R}
, that is,
\mathcal{D}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\big\{\alpha\in[0,1]^{\mathcal{R}}\mid\sum_{R\in\mathcal{R}}\alpha(R)=1\big\}
.
The
distribution
of a random variable
\tau\in\mathcal{W}
, denoted
\yhwidehat{\tau}\in\mathcal{D}
, is defined for
R\in\mathcal{R}
as

\yhwidehat{\tau}(R)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\prob\big(\{t\in\mathrm{Tr}_{\Sigma}\mid\tau(t)=R\}\big).

Definition 11.1
.

Consider two distributions
\alpha,\beta\in\mathcal{D}
.
We say that
\alpha\preceq\beta
if for each upward
-
closed subset
\mathcal{U}\subseteq\mathcal{R}
we have

\sum_{R\in\mathcal{U}}\alpha(R)\leq\sum_{R\in\mathcal{U}}\beta(R).

The above order was first introduced by Saheb-Djahromi
[
Sah80
]
and is often called a
probabilistic powerdomain

[
JP89
]
.
It has been used in the preceding papers where measures of
the weakly MSO
-
definable sets of infinite trees were computed
[
PS20
,
NPS20
]
.

We first remark that
(\mathcal{D},{\preceq})
may not be a lattice, as expressed by the following fact.

Lemma 11.2
.

There exists a finite complete lattice
\mathcal{R}
such that
\mathcal{D}=\mathbb{D}(\mathcal{R})
is not a lattice.

Proof.

Take as
\mathcal{R}
the powerset
\mathsf{P}(\{p,q\})
for some two
-
element set
Q=\{p,q\}
.
Consider two distributions, written as formal convex combinations of the elements of
\mathcal{R}
:

\displaystyle\alpha

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\cdot\{p\}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\cdot\{q\},

\displaystyle\beta

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\cdot\emptyset

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\cdot Q.

Clearly
\alpha\npreceq\beta
nor
\beta\npreceq\alpha
.
To simplify notation, we say that
\gamma
is an
upper bound
if both
\gamma\succeq\alpha
and
\gamma\succeq\beta
.
For
\mathcal{D}
to be a lattice, there should be a unique smallest upper bound
\gamma
.
Consider

\displaystyle\gamma_{p}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\cdot\{p\}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\cdot Q,

\displaystyle\gamma_{q}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\cdot\{q\}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\cdot Q.

It is easy to check that both
\gamma_{p}
and
\gamma_{q}
are upper bounds.
Moreover,
\gamma_{p}\npreceq\gamma_{q}
nor
\gamma_{q}\npreceq\gamma_{p}
.
Consider any upper bound
\gamma
and assume that
\gamma_{p}\succeq\gamma
.
We will prove that
\gamma=\gamma_{p}
.
Since
\gamma_{p}\succeq\gamma\succeq\beta
, by taking
\mathcal{U}=\{Q\}
we know that
\gamma(Q)=\frac{1}{2}
.
Since
\gamma\succeq\alpha
, by taking
\mathcal{U}=\{Q,\{p\}\}
we know that
\gamma(Q)+\gamma(\{p\})=1
and therefore
\gamma(\{p\})=\frac{1}{2}
.
Thus,
\gamma=\gamma_{p}
.
Similarly,
\gamma=\gamma_{q}
for any upper bound
\gamma
such that
\gamma_{q}\succeq\gamma
.

This means that
\gamma_{p}
and
\gamma_{q}
are two distinct minimal upper bounds.
Thus, there is no unique smallest upper bound.
∎

On the other hand, every chain (but not every set) in
(\mathcal{D},{\preceq})
has a supremum and an infimum, as stated below
(this observation, however, is not used in our arguments).

Proposition 11.3
.

Every chain
C
in the structure
(\mathcal{D},{\preceq})
has a supremum and an infimum.

Proof.

Consider a chain
C\subseteq\mathcal{D}
.
By symmetry we assume that we need to find its supremum.
Let
\emptyset=\mathcal{U}_{0},\ldots,\mathcal{U}_{n-1}=\mathcal{R}
be the list of all upward
-
closed subsets
\mathcal{U}_{i}\subseteq\mathcal{R}
.
We can represent each distribution
\alpha\in\mathcal{D}
as
\vec{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, where
\vec{\alpha}_{i}=\sum_{R\in\mathcal{U}_{i}}\alpha(R)

(the notation
\vec{\alpha}
is used only for the sake of this proof).
Clearly the value
\vec{\alpha}_{i}
belongs to the interval
[0,1]
, as the probability of the event
R\in\mathcal{U}_{i}
.

By definition,

\alpha\preceq\beta\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\vec{\alpha}\leq\vec{\beta},

(11.1)

where we order the vectors
\vec{\alpha}
and
\vec{\beta}
by the coordinate
-
wise order in
\mathbb{R}
.
Let
(a_{0},\ldots,a_{n-1})
be the coordinate
-
wise supremum of
\{\vec{\alpha}\mid\alpha\in C\}
.
Due to this definition, the vector
(a_{0},\ldots,a_{n-1})
satisfies
a_{0}=0
,
a_{n-1}=1
, and if
\mathcal{U}_{i}\subseteq\mathcal{U}_{j}
then
a_{i}\leq a_{j}
.

We claim that
(a_{0},\ldots,a_{n-1})=\vec{\beta}
for some distribution
\beta\in\mathcal{D}
.
Note that in this case
\beta
must be the supremum of
C
, because of
Eq.

11.1
.

Take
R\in\mathcal{R}
and let
U_{R}=\{R^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}\mid R^{\prime}\geq R\}
,
V_{R}=\{R^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}\mid R^{\prime}>R\}
.
Both these sets are upward closed.
Assume that
U_{R}=\mathcal{U}_{i}
and
V_{R}=\mathcal{U}_{j}
and put
\beta(R)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}a_{i}-a_{j}
.
This value clearly belongs to
[0,1]
(we know that
\mathcal{U}_{j}\subseteq\mathcal{U}_{i}
).
Moreover, by induction over the size of
\mathcal{U}_{i}
we can show that
\sum_{R\in\mathcal{U}_{i}}\beta(R)=a_{i}
.
Hence,
\sum_{R\in\mathcal{R}}\beta(R)=a_{n-1}=1
, which means that
\beta\in\mathcal{D}
.
This implies that
\vec{\beta}
is well defined and equal to
(a_{0},\ldots,a_{n-1})
, which finishes the proof.
∎

The following two lemmata are rather standard.

Lemma 11.4
.

The mapping
\tau\mapsto\yhwidehat{\tau}
defined on the random variables
\tau\in\mathcal{W}
is monotone.

Proof.

Take
\tau,\tau^{\prime}\in\mathcal{W}
such that
\tau\leq\tau^{\prime}
, and take any upward
-
closed subset
\mathcal{U}\subseteq\mathcal{R}
.
Then

\displaystyle\sum_{R\in\mathcal{U}}\yhwidehat{\tau}(R)

\displaystyle=\sum_{R\in\mathcal{U}}\prob\big(\{t\in\mathrm{Tr}_{\Sigma}\mid\tau(t)=R\}\big)

\displaystyle=\prob\big(\{t\in\mathrm{Tr}_{\Sigma}\mid\tau(t)\in\mathcal{U}\}\big)

\displaystyle\leq\prob\big(\{t\in\mathrm{Tr}_{\Sigma}\mid\tau^{\prime}(t)\in\mathcal{U}\}\big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{R\in\mathcal{U}}\prob\big(\{t\in\mathrm{Tr}_{\Sigma}\mid\tau^{\prime}(t)=R\}\big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{R\in\mathcal{U}}\yhwidehat{\tau^{\prime}}(R),

where the middle inequality follows from the assumption that
\tau(t)\subseteq\tau^{\prime}(t)
for all
t\in\mathrm{Tr}_{\Sigma}
and the fact that
\mathcal{U}
is upward closed.
Having this for all
\mathcal{U}
implies that
\yhwidehat{\tau}\preceq\yhwidehat{\tau}^{\prime}

∎

Lemma 11.5
.

If
(\tau_{\zeta})_{\zeta<\eta}
is a monotonic sequence of random variables that converges in measure to a random variable
\tau
then
the sequence of distributions
\yhwidehat{\tau_{\zeta}}
is also monotonic and its limit is
\yhwidehat{\tau}
.

Proof.

To fix attention, suppose that the sequence
(\tau_{\zeta})_{\zeta<\eta}
is increasing; argumentation for a decreasing sequence is symmetric.

Lemma

11.4
immediately implies that the sequence of distributions
(\yhwidehat{\tau}_{\zeta})_{\zeta<\eta}
is increasing as well.

Convergence in measure by definition implies that, for every
R\in\mathcal{R}
, the sequence of numbers
\big(\yhwidehat{\tau}_{\zeta}(R)\big)_{\zeta<\eta}
converges to
\yhwidehat{\tau}(R)
.
Take any upward-closed subset
\mathcal{U}\subseteq\mathcal{R}
.
Because
\mathcal{U}
is finite, the sequence of sums
(s_{\zeta,\mathcal{U}})_{\zeta<\eta}
defined as
s_{\zeta,\mathcal{U}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\sum_{R\in\mathcal{U}}\yhwidehat{\tau}_{\zeta}(R)

converges to
s_{\mathcal{U}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\sum_{R\in\mathcal{U}}\yhwidehat{\tau}(R)
.
The sequence
(\yhwidehat{\tau}_{\zeta})_{\zeta<\eta}
is increasing, which by definition means that
(s_{\zeta,\mathcal{U}})_{\zeta<\eta}
is increasing as well.
In consequence
s_{\zeta,\mathcal{U}}\leq s_{\mathcal{U}}
for all
\zeta<\eta
(the limit of an increasing sequence of numbers is above its elements).
Having this for all
\mathcal{U}
implies
\yhwidehat{\tau}_{\zeta}\preceq\yhwidehat{\tau}
for all
\zeta<\eta
.

Consider now any other distribution
\beta
such that
\yhwidehat{\tau}_{\zeta}\preceq\beta
for all
\zeta<\eta
,
and take again any upward-closed subset
\mathcal{U}\subseteq\mathcal{R}
.
The inequality
\yhwidehat{\tau}_{\zeta}\preceq\beta
implies
s_{\zeta,\mathcal{U}}\leq s^{\prime}_{\mathcal{U}}
for all
\zeta<\eta
, where
s^{\prime}_{\mathcal{U}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\sum_{R\in\mathcal{U}}\beta(R)
.
But then also the limit of
(s_{\zeta,\mathcal{U}})_{\zeta<\eta}
, namely
s_{\mathcal{U}}
, satisfies
s_{\mathcal{U}}\leq s^{\prime}_{\mathcal{U}}
.
Having this for all
\mathcal{U}
implies
\yhwidehat{\tau}\preceq\beta
.

Thus
\yhwidehat{\tau}
is the least upper bound of the set
\{\yhwidehat{\tau}_{\zeta}\mid\zeta<\eta\}
, that is, the limit of
(\yhwidehat{\tau}_{\zeta})_{\zeta<\eta}
.
∎

We remark that the mapping
\tau\mapsto\yhwidehat{\tau}
does not
preserve limits of all chains, or at least this would contradict the continuum hypothesis.
This is the reason why we need results of the previous section, in particular
Proposition

10.4
, saying that the limits we take are convergent in measure.
Indeed, assuming the continuum hypothesis, we can organize all trees in a sequence of type
\omega_{1}
, that is, denote them
t_{\eta}
for
\eta<\omega_{1}
.
Then all the sets
U_{\eta}=\{t_{\zeta}\mid\zeta\leq\eta\}
for
\eta<\omega_{1}
have measure
0
(these are countable sets).
But the limit of the increasing sequence
(U_{\eta})_{\eta<\omega_{1}}
(i.e., union of these sets) is
\mathrm{Tr}_{\Sigma}
, a set of measure
1
.

11.1
Computing in
\mathcal{D}
—basic functions

Because now we consider both functions on
\mathcal{W}
and on
\mathcal{D}
, in order to distinguish them we use names with a subscript
\mathcal{W}
or
\mathcal{D}
.

Recall the definition given in the proof of
Lemma

10.3
:
a function
F_{\mathcal{W}}\colon\mathcal{W}\to\mathcal{W}
is
superficial
if there exists a function
F_{\mathcal{R}}\colon\mathcal{R}\to\mathcal{R}
such that
F_{\mathcal{W}}(\tau)(t)=F_{\mathcal{R}}(\tau(t))
for all
\tau\in\mathcal{W}
and
t\in\mathrm{Tr}_{\Sigma}
.
Having such a function
F_{\mathcal{W}}
we define its
lift to
\mathcal{D}
as the function
F_{\mathcal{D}}\colon\mathcal{D}\to\mathcal{D}

such that
F_{\mathcal{D}}(\alpha)(R)=\sum_{R^{\prime}\in F_{\mathcal{R}}^{-1}(\{R\})}\alpha(R^{\prime})
for all
\alpha\in\mathcal{D}
and
R\in\mathcal{R}
.

Lemma 11.6
.

If
F_{\mathcal{W}}\colon\mathcal{W}\to\mathcal{W}
is superficial then for each random variable
\tau\in\mathcal{V}
we have

\yhwidehat{F_{\mathcal{W}}(\tau)}=F_{\mathcal{D}}(\yhwidehat{\tau}).

Proof.

Fix a set
R\in\mathcal{R}
.
For every tree
t\in\mathrm{Tr}_{\Sigma}
we have

\displaystyle F_{\mathcal{W}}(\tau)(t)=R\

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\ F_{\mathcal{R}}(\tau(t))=R\ \Leftrightarrow\ \tau(t)\in F_{\mathcal{R}}^{-1}(\{R\})\Leftrightarrow\ \bigvee\nolimits_{R^{\prime}\in F_{\mathcal{R}}^{-1}(\{R\})}\tau(t)=R^{\prime}.

Moreover, at most one component of the above alternative is true (there is a unique set
R^{\prime}
such that
\tau(t)=R^{\prime}
).
It follows that

\displaystyle\yhwidehat{F_{\mathcal{W}}(\tau)}(R)

\displaystyle=\prob\big(\{t\in\mathrm{Tr}_{\Sigma}\mid F_{\mathcal{W}}(\tau)(t)=R\}\big)

\displaystyle=\sum\nolimits_{R^{\prime}\in F_{\mathcal{R}}^{-1}(\{R\})}\prob\big(\{t\in\mathrm{Tr}_{\Sigma}\mid\tau(t)=R^{\prime}\}\big)

\displaystyle=\sum\nolimits_{R^{\prime}\in F_{\mathcal{R}}^{-1}(\{R\})}\yhwidehat{\tau}(R^{\prime})=F_{\mathcal{D}}(\yhwidehat{\tau})(R).

∎

We now define probabilistic variants of functions
\Delta_{\mathcal{W}}
,
(\mathrm{Bid}_{n})_{\mathcal{W}}
and
(\mathrm{Cut}_{n})_{\mathcal{W}}
.
First,
(\mathrm{Bid}_{n})_{\mathcal{D}}
and
(\mathrm{Cut}_{n})_{\mathcal{D}}
are just lifts of
(\mathrm{Bid}_{n})_{\mathcal{W}}\colon\mathcal{W}\to\mathcal{W}
and
(\mathrm{Cut}_{n})_{\mathcal{W}}\colon\mathcal{W}\to\mathcal{W}
, which are superficial.

Now we need to define a variant of
\Delta_{\mathcal{W}}
working on distributions, which we denote
\Delta_{\mathcal{D}}\colon\mathcal{D}\to\mathcal{D}
.
We use the function
\delta_{a}\colon\mathsf{P}(Q)\times\mathsf{P}(Q)\to\mathsf{P}(Q)
defined in
Section

4
.
First, for
a\in\Sigma
and
R^{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle L}}},R^{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle R}}}\in\mathcal{R}
let us define

\displaystyle\Delta_{a}(R^{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle L}}},R^{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle R}}})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\bigcup\nolimits_{i=0}^{d-1}\delta_{a}\big(K^{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle L}}}_{i},K^{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle R}}}_{i}\big){\times}\{i\},

where

\displaystyle\hskip-40.00006ptK^{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle L}}}_{i}=\big\{q\in Q\mid(q,\max(i,\Omega(q)))\in R^{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle L}}}\big\}

and

\displaystyle\hskip-40.00006ptK^{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle R}}}_{i}=\big\{q\in Q\mid(q,\max(i,\Omega(q)))\in R^{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle R}}}\big\}.

Now take
\alpha\in\mathcal{D}
and
R\in\mathcal{R}
and let

\Delta_{\mathcal{D}}(\alpha)(R)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\sum_{a\in\Sigma}\frac{1}{|\Sigma|}\cdot\sum_{(R^{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle L}}},R^{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle R}}})\in\Delta_{a}^{-1}(\{R\})}\alpha(R^{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle L}}})\cdot\alpha(R^{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle R}}}).

(11.2)

The following lemma can be proved by a simple computation.

Lemma 11.7
.

For every random variable
\tau\in\mathcal{W}
we have

\yhwidehat{\Delta_{\mathcal{W}}(\tau)}=\Delta_{\mathcal{D}}(\yhwidehat{\tau}).

Proof.

Fix a random variable
\tau\in\mathcal{W}
and a set
R\in\mathcal{R}
.
First, recalling the definition of
\Delta_{\mathcal{V}^{d}}\colon\mathcal{V}^{d}\to\mathcal{V}^{d}
, of
\delta\colon\mathcal{V}^{d}\to\mathcal{V}
, and of the bijection between
\mathcal{V}^{d}
and
\mathcal{W}
,
we notice that
\Delta_{\mathcal{W}}\colon\mathcal{W}\to\mathcal{W}
can be defined by

\displaystyle\Delta_{\mathcal{W}}(\tau)(t)=\Delta_{t(\varepsilon)}\big(\tau(t.\mathtt{{\scriptstyle L}}),\tau(t.\mathtt{{\scriptstyle R}})\big).

From the above it follows that

\displaystyle\big\{t\in\mathrm{Tr}_{\Sigma}\mid\Delta_{\mathcal{W}}(\tau)(t)=R\big\}=\bigcup\nolimits_{a\in\Sigma}\bigcup\nolimits_{(R^{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle L}}},R^{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle R}}})\in\Delta_{a}^{-1}(\{R\})}(A_{a}\cap B_{R^{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle L}}}}\cap C_{R^{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle R}}}}),

where

\displaystyle A_{a}

\displaystyle=\big\{t\in\mathrm{Tr}_{\Sigma}\mid t(\varepsilon)=a\big\},

\displaystyle B_{R^{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle L}}}}

\displaystyle=\big\{t\in\mathrm{Tr}_{\Sigma}\mid\tau(t.\mathtt{{\scriptstyle L}})=R^{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle L}}}\big\},

and

\displaystyle C_{R^{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle R}}}}

\displaystyle=\big\{t\in\mathrm{Tr}_{\Sigma}\mid\tau(t.\mathtt{{\scriptstyle R}})=R^{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle R}}}\big\}.

Both unions in the above formula are disjoint unions: for every tree we have a unique letter
t(\varepsilon)
and unique sets
\tau(t.\mathtt{{\scriptstyle L}})
and
\tau(t.\mathtt{{\scriptstyle R}})
.
Moreover, the three random events,
A_{a}
,
B_{R^{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle L}}}}
, and

are independent:
a label in the root, labels in the left subtree, and labels in the right subtree are chosen independently.
We have
\prob(A_{a})=\frac{1}{|\Sigma|}
by definition, and
\prob(B_{R^{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle L}}}})=\yhwidehat{\tau}(R^{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle L}}})
and
\prob(C_{R^{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle R}}}})=\yhwidehat{\tau}(R^{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle R}}})
by
10.2
.
Thus

\displaystyle\yhwidehat{\Delta_{\mathcal{W}}(\tau)}(R)

\displaystyle=\prob\big(\{t\in\mathrm{Tr}_{\Sigma}\mid\Delta_{\mathcal{W}}(\tau)(t)=R\}\big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{a\in\Sigma}\sum_{(R^{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle L}}},R^{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle R}}})\in\Delta_{a}^{-1}(\{R\})}\!\!\!\prob(A_{a})\cdot\prob(B_{R^{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle L}}}})\cdot\prob(C_{R^{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle R}}}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{a\in\Sigma}\frac{1}{|\Sigma|}\cdot\sum_{(R^{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle L}}},R^{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle R}}})\in\Delta_{a}^{-1}(\{R\})}\!\!\!\yhwidehat{\tau}(R^{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle L}}})\cdot\yhwidehat{\tau}(R^{\mathtt{{\scriptstyle R}}})

\displaystyle=\Delta_{\mathcal{D}}(\yhwidehat{\tau})(R).

∎

11.2
Moving from
\mathcal{W}
to
\mathcal{D}

Assume that
\mathcal{W}
and
\mathcal{D}
are structures over
\mathfrak{F}
such that for every basic function
H\in\mathfrak{F}
,

1.

the function
H_{\mathcal{W}}
is local and preserves measurability on the whole
\mathcal{W}
, and

2.

H

commutes with computing distributions
, that is, if
\tau\in\mathcal{W}
is a random variable then
\yhwidehat{H_{\mathcal{W}}(\tau)}=H_{\mathcal{D}}(\yhwidehat{\tau})
.

Proposition 11.8
.

Assume the above conditions on the structures
\mathcal{W}
and
\mathcal{D}
.
Let
F
be a formula of unary
\mu
-
calculus such that we can derive
F\!::A\to B
for some
A,B\subseteq\mathcal{W}
.
Let also
\tau\in A
be a random variable.
Then
\yhwidehat{\tau}\in\mathrm{dom}(F_{\mathcal{D}})
and

\yhwidehat{F_{\mathcal{W}}(\tau)}=F_{\mathcal{D}}(\yhwidehat{\tau}).

Recall
Lemma

10.14
, which, in our situation, ensures that
F_{\mathcal{W}}
preserves measurability on
A
;
in particular
F_{\mathcal{W}}(\tau)
is a random variable and it makes sense to consider
\yhwidehat{F_{\mathcal{W}}(\tau)}
.

The rest of this subsection is devoted to a proof of this proposition.
We proceed by induction over the structure of
F
.

For the basic functions
H\in\mathfrak{F}
the statement follows from the assumptions that they commute with computing distributions.
For
F;G
the fact follows directly from the induction hypothesis.
Consider a formula of the form
{F}{\uparrow}
(the case of
{F}{\downarrow}
is symmetric) and take a random variable
\tau\in A
.
By the assumption that we can derive
{F}{\uparrow}\!::A\to B
, we know that
F
is ascending on
A
.
Therefore,
Lemma

5.3
implies that
({F}{\uparrow})_{\mathcal{W}}(\tau)=(F_{\mathcal{W}})^{\infty}(\tau)
.
Denote this random variable by
\sigma
.

The rest of the proof is realised by the following claim.

Claim 11.9
.

We have
\yhwidehat{\tau}\in\mathrm{dom}\big(({F}{\uparrow})_{\mathcal{D}}\big)
and
\yhwidehat{\sigma}=({F}{\uparrow})_{\mathcal{D}}(\yhwidehat{\tau})
.

Proof.

Recall the definition of
(F_{\mathcal{W}})^{\eta}(\tau)
used to define
(F_{\mathcal{W}})^{\infty}(\tau)
.
By
Proposition

10.4
all the elements
(F_{\mathcal{W}})^{\eta}(\tau)
are random variables.
Note that
\sigma
is a fixed point of
F_{\mathcal{W}}
, so the induction hypothesis implies that

F_{\mathcal{D}}(\yhwidehat{\sigma})=\yhwidehat{F_{\mathcal{W}}(\sigma)}=\yhwidehat{\sigma}.

Thus,
\yhwidehat{\sigma}\in\mathrm{Fix}(F_{\mathcal{D}})
.
By definition
\tau\leq\sigma
, so also
\yhwidehat{\tau}\preceq\yhwidehat{\sigma}
by
Lemma

11.4
.

Consider now another distribution
\beta\in\mathcal{D}
with
\yhwidehat{\tau}\preceq\beta
such that
F_{\mathcal{D}}(\beta)=\beta
.
We need to prove that
\yhwidehat{\sigma}\preceq\beta
.
To this end, we prove by induction on
\eta
that
\yhwidehat{(F_{\mathcal{W}})^{\eta}(\tau)}\preceq\beta
.
For
\eta=0
this follows from the assumption.
For a successor ordinal
\eta
we have

\displaystyle\yhwidehat{F_{\mathcal{W}}^{\eta}(\tau)}=\yhwidehat{F_{\mathcal{W}}\big(F_{\mathcal{W}}^{\eta-1}(\tau)\big)}=F_{\mathcal{D}}\big(\yhwidehat{F_{\mathcal{W}}^{\eta-1}(\tau)}\big)\preceq F_{\mathcal{D}}(\beta)=\beta,

where the second equality is the hypothesis of the external induction (about
F
)
and the next inequality follows from monotonicity of
F_{\mathcal{D}}
and from the hypothesis of the internal induction (about
\beta
).

Consider now a limit ordinal
\eta
.

Proposition

10.4
implies that the monotonic sequence
\big(F_{\mathcal{W}}^{\zeta}(\tau)\big)_{\zeta<\eta}
converges in measure to
F_{\mathcal{W}}^{\eta}(\tau)
.

Lemma

11.5
thus implies that
\big(\yhwidehat{F_{\mathcal{W}}^{\zeta}(\tau)}\big)_{\zeta<\eta}
has limit
\yhwidehat{F_{\mathcal{W}}^{\eta}(\tau)}
in
\mathcal{D}
.
Therefore, using the induction hypothesis that
\yhwidehat{F_{\mathcal{W}}^{\zeta}(\tau)}\preceq\beta
for all
\zeta<\eta
we also have
\yhwidehat{F_{\mathcal{W}}^{\eta}(\tau)}\preceq\beta
.

Thus, for the particular case of
\eta=\eta_{\infty}
we have
\yhwidehat{\sigma}=\yhwidehat{(F_{\mathcal{W}})^{\eta_{\infty}}(\tau)}\preceq\beta
.

We have just shown that
\yhwidehat{\sigma}
is the least fixed
-
point of
F_{\mathcal{D}}
above
\yhwidehat{\tau}
in
\mathcal{D}
and therefore
\yhwidehat{\tau}\in\mathrm{dom}\big(({F}{\uparrow})_{\mathcal{D}}\big)

and
({F}{\uparrow})_{\mathcal{D}}(\yhwidehat{\tau})=\yhwidehat{\sigma}
.
∎

Section

11.1
defines basic functions
\Delta_{\mathcal{D}}
,
(\mathrm{Bid}_{n})_{\mathcal{D}}
and
(\mathrm{Cut}_{n})_{\mathcal{D}}

in such a way that the assumptions of
Proposition

11.8
are met by our structures
\mathcal{W}
and
\mathcal{D}
.
This gives us the following corollary for the specific case of
F=\Phi_{d-1}
.

Corollary 11.10
.

For every random variable
\tau\in\mathcal{S}_{d-1}
we have
\yhwidehat{\tau}\in\mathrm{dom}\big((\Phi_{d-1})_{\mathcal{D}}\big)
and

\yhwidehat{(\Phi_{d-1})_{\mathcal{W}}(\tau)}=(\Phi_{d-1})_{\mathcal{D}}(\yhwidehat{\tau}).

11.3
An a posteriori explanation of the formula
\Phi_{d-1}

In this section we explain the reasons for the shape of the formula
\Phi_{d-1}
.
The source of difficulty of our construction can be explained already for
d=2
when
\Omega\colon Q\to\{0,1\}
(i.e., the
co
-
Büchi
case).
In this case the formula defining
\tau_{\mathcal{A}}
from
Proposition

4.2
has the form

\mu x_{1}.\,\nu x_{0}.\,\delta(x_{0},x_{1}).

(11.3)

When we move to distributions of random variables, it is clearly not enough to consider separately a distribution of
x_{0}
and a distribution of
x_{1}
,
because the distribution of
\delta(x_{0},x_{1})
depends on the joint distribution of its arguments.

Thus, one could try to work with pairs of profiles and consider functions
\mathcal{V}^{2}\to\mathcal{V}^{2}
.
This leads to a function

U_{2}\colon(x_{0},x_{1})\mapsto\big(\delta(x_{0},x_{1}),x_{1}\big),

which is a monotone and chain
-
continuous function on
\mathcal{V}^{2}
.
Moreover, the fixed point
\nu x_{0}.\,\delta(x_{0},x_{1})
can be computed in a descending way, that is, as the first coordinate of
{U_{2}}{\downarrow}(\top,x_{1})
.

The problem here is that again the operation
U_{2}
cannot be simulated on distributions of random variables:
the events used to compute the value of
\delta(\yhwidehat{\tau}_{0},\yhwidehat{\tau}_{1})
(i.e., the root, the left subtree, and the right subtree of a random tree)
and the event used to compute the value of
\yhwidehat{\tau}_{1}
(i.e., the whole tree) are not independent, unlike in the proof of
Lemma

11.7
.
Roughly speaking, to compute
U_{2}
one has to look simultaneously at the whole randomly generated tree
t
as well as its root label
t(\varepsilon)
and its two subtrees
t.\mathtt{{\scriptstyle L}}
and
t.\mathtt{{\scriptstyle R}}
.
The variables
\big(t(\varepsilon),t.\mathtt{{\scriptstyle L}},t.\mathtt{{\scriptstyle R}}\big)
are independent as a vector, but stop being independent when we also consider the whole tree
t
.

The above problem would not arise, if the involved operator had the form

U^{\prime}_{2}\colon(x_{0},x_{1})\mapsto\big(\delta(x_{0},x_{1}),\delta(x_{1},x_{1})\big).

This operation indeed can be translated to distributions in such a way that it commutes with computing distributions.
Moreover, if
\tau_{1}
is any fixed point of
x_{1}\mapsto\delta(x_{1},x_{1})
then obviously
U^{\prime}_{2}(\tau_{0},\tau_{1})=U_{2}(\tau_{0},\tau_{1})
.

This explains the motivation standing behind our formula
\Phi_{1}
: on some coordinates, instead of applying the identity
\tau_{1}\mapsto\tau_{1}
,
we can apply a proper variant of
\delta
if we make sure in advance that the current value of the given coordinate is already a fixed point of it
(see the definition of a
2
-
saturated tuple
(\tau_{0},\tau_{1})
from
Section

6.2
).
To achieve this, we need to add additional nested fixed
-
point operations on variants of
\delta
, as in the inductive definition of
\Phi_{n}
from
\Phi_{n-1}
.
The operations
\mathrm{Bid}_{n}
and
\mathrm{Cut}_{n}
, moving values between coordinates, give us a technical tool for switching between computations of particular fixed points from the original formula.

12
Tarski’s first order theory of reals

Recall that
\mathcal{D}
contains probability distributions over a finite lattice
\mathcal{R}
.
Let
n
be the cardinality of
\mathcal{R}
.
We now treat
\mathcal{D}
as a set of vectors
\vec{a}=(a_{0},\ldots,a_{n-1})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

In this section we think of
\mathbb{R}
as a logical structure with
0
,
1
, the operations of addition and multiplication, and the usual linear order, that is,
\langle\mathbb{R},0,1,{+},{*},{\leq}\rangle
.
Take the standard definition of first
-
order logic.
Our goal is to show that unary
\mu
-
calculus over distributions
\mathcal{D}
can be expressed in first
-
order logic over
\mathbb{R}
.
The technique closely follows an analogous argument given by Przybyłko and Skrzypczak
[
PS20
]
for the case of safety automata.

Tarski’s quantifier elimination
[
Tar51
]
implies that if
\varphi(x_{0},\allowbreak\ldots,\allowbreak x_{n-1})
is a formula of first
-
order logic over the reals
\mathbb{R}

then the set of vectors
\vec{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
satisfying it is a semialgebraic set
[
BCR98
, Chapter 2]
,
that is, a set represented by a Boolean combination of equations and inequalities involving polynomials in the variables
x_{0},\ldots,x_{n-1}
.
If this semialgebraic set is a singleton
\{\vec{x}\}
, then we can treat its representation as a representation of the vector
\vec{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Theorem 12.1

(
[
Col75
]
)
.

A semialgebraic representation of the set of vectors satisfying a given formula
\varphi(x_{0},\ldots,x_{n-1})
can be computed in time double
-
exponential in the size of
\varphi(x_{0},\ldots,x_{n-1})
.
∎

Our goal is to use this method to represent the distribution
\yhwidehat{\tau_{\mathcal{A}}}
.
To improve the complexity of the decision problems (e.g., whether
\prob\big(\mathrm{L}(\mathcal{A})\big)>0
) we rely on the following result.

Theorem 12.2

(
[
BOKR86
]
)
.

The theory of real
-
closed fields can be decided in deterministic exponential space.
In particular, given a formula
\varphi
with no free variables, it is decidable in deterministic exponential space whether
\varphi
holds.
∎

A set
A\subseteq\mathcal{D}
is
definable
if there exists a formula
\psi_{A}(x_{0},\ldots,x_{n-1})
such that
\vec{x}\in A
if and only if
\psi_{A}(\vec{x})
holds.
A distribution
\alpha\in\mathcal{D}
is
definable
if the singleton set
\{\alpha\}
is definable.
A partial function
F\colon\mathcal{D}\rightharpoonup\mathcal{D}
is
definable

if there exists a formula
\psi_{F}(x_{0},\ldots,x_{n-1},y_{0},\ldots,y_{n-1})
such that
\psi_{F}(\vec{x},\vec{y})
holds if and only if
\vec{x}\in\mathrm{dom}(F)
and
\vec{y}=F(\vec{x})
.
Similarly, we introduce a notion of a relation on
\mathcal{D}
being definable.

Lemma 12.3
.

The basic functions
(\mathrm{Bid}_{n})_{\mathcal{D}}
,
(\mathrm{Cut}_{n})_{\mathcal{D}}
, and
\Delta_{\mathcal{D}}
from
\mathcal{D}
to
\mathcal{D}
are definable.

Proof.

The case of
(\mathrm{Bid}_{n})_{\mathcal{D}}
and
(\mathrm{Cut}_{n})_{\mathcal{D}}
follows directly from the definition.
For
\Delta_{\mathcal{D}}
, it is enough to write the arithmetic expression from
Eq.

11.2
.
∎

Lemma 12.4
.

The set of distributions
\alpha
is definable.
Moreover, the order
\preceq
on distributions is also definable.

Proof.

A vector encodes a distribution if its values are between
0
and
1
and they sum up to
1
.

It remains to write a formula
\varphi_{\preceq}(\alpha,\beta)
which holds if and only if
\alpha\preceq\beta
.
One solution is just to write a large conjunction over all upward
-
closed subsets of
\mathcal{R}
, as in
Definition

11.1
.
However, one can simulate the quantification over upward
-
closed sets within first
-
order logic,
see the proof of Theorem 6.1 in Przybyłko and Skrzypczak
[
PS20
]
.
The formula given there is only polynomial in the cardinality of
\mathcal{R}
.
∎

Proposition 12.5
.

If all the basic functions
H\in\mathfrak{F}
are definable then for every formula
F
of unary
\mu
-
calculus we know that the partial function
F_{\mathcal{D}}\colon\mathcal{D}\rightharpoonup\mathcal{D}
is definable.

Proof.

The equations used to define
\mathrm{dom}(F_{\mathcal{D}})
and
F_{\mathcal{D}}(x)
in
Section

5.1
are clearly expressible in first
-
order logic over
\mathbb{R}
,
using the formulae for the basic functions and for the order
{\preceq}
on
\mathcal{D}
.
∎

The following fact follows directly from definitions.

Fact 12.6
.

If a partial function
F\colon\mathcal{D}\rightharpoonup\mathcal{D}
is definable and its argument
\vec{x}\in\mathrm{dom}(F)
is also definable then the value
F(\vec{x})
is also definable.
∎

We are now in place to prove our main theorem.

Proof of
Theorem

1.2
.

Input a nondeterministic (or alternating, see
Remark

4.4
) parity tree automaton
\mathcal{A}
.
Define
\mathcal{I}\subseteq\mathcal{R}
as the set
\{R\in\mathcal{R}\mid(q_{I},d{-}1)\in R\}
.
Observe that

\displaystyle\prob\big(\mathrm{L}(\mathcal{A})\big)

\displaystyle=\prob\Big(\big\{t\in\mathrm{Tr}_{\Sigma}\mid\tau_{\mathcal{A}}(t)\ni q_{I}\big\}\Big)

\displaystyle=\prob\Big(\big\{t\in\mathrm{Tr}_{\Sigma}\mid(\Phi_{d-1})_{\mathcal{V}^{d}}(\bot)_{d-1}(t)\ni q_{I}\big\}\Big)

\displaystyle=\prob\Big(\big\{t\in\mathrm{Tr}_{\Sigma}\mid(\Phi_{d-1})_{\mathcal{W}}(\bot)(t)\ni(q_{I},d{-}1)\big\}\Big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{R\in\mathcal{I}}\prob\Big(\big\{t\in\mathrm{Tr}_{\Sigma}\mid(\Phi_{d-1})_{\mathcal{W}}(\bot)(t)=R\big\}\Big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{R\in\mathcal{I}}\yhwidehat{(\Phi_{d-1})_{\mathcal{W}}(\bot)}(R)

\displaystyle=\sum_{R\in\mathcal{I}}(\Phi_{d-1})_{\mathcal{D}}(\yhwidehat{\bot})(R),

(12.1)

where:

•

the first equality is just the definition of
\mathrm{L}(\mathcal{A})
;

•

the second is the composition of equations from
Propositions

4.2
and
9.1
;

•

the third relies on the identification between
\mathcal{V}^{d}=\big(\mathrm{Tr}_{\Sigma}\to\mathsf{P}(Q)\big)^{d}
and
\mathcal{W}=(\mathrm{Tr}_{\Sigma}\to\mathcal{R})
where
\mathcal{R}=\mathsf{P}\big(Q\times\{0,\ldots,d{-}1\}\big)
;

•

the fourth is just additivity of measure (for distinct sets

the considered sets of trees are disjoint);

•

the fifth is just the definition of
\yhwidehat{\tau}
;

•

the sixth equality follows from
Corollary

11.10
.

Now, recall that
\bot\in\mathcal{W}
is the function constantly equal

.
Therefore, the distribution
\yhwidehat{\bot}
assigns probability

to
\emptyset
and

to all other

.
Thus, this distribution is definable and therefore

implies that the distribution

(treated as a vector) is definable.
In consequence, the sum of some of its coordinates as in

must also be definable.

Therefore, first of all, the number

Moreover, Tarski’s quantifier elimination allows us to effectively compute a representation of this number.
Finally,

says that the decision problems, like whether

for a given rational number

∎

12.1
Complexity

We provide here upper bounds on the complexity of the decision method provided in the proof of
Theorem

1.2
.
The situation is analogous to that from the cited proof of Theorem 6.1 in Przybyłko and Skrzypczak
[
PS20
]
.
Our parameter is
N=|Q|
, that is, the number of states of the given automaton
\mathcal{A}
.
The representation of the whole automaton
\mathcal{A}
is polynomial in
N
, and so is the value of
d\leq\max_{q\in Q}\Omega(q)+1

(we can assume that the values of
\Omega
form an interval and the least value equals either
0
or
1
).
Thus, the syntactic size of the formula
\Phi_{d-1}
defined at the beginning of
Section

7
is also polynomial in
N
(in fact linear).

Now, the cardinality of the set
\mathcal{R}=\mathsf{P}\big(Q\times\{0,\ldots,d{-}1\}\big)
is exponential in
N
.
Thus, so is
n
—the length of the vectors
\vec{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
which encode distributions
\alpha\in\mathcal{D}
.
Similarly, exponential in
N
are the formulae of first
-
order logic that define the function
\Delta_{\mathcal{D}}
and the order
{\preceq}

(see the comment in the proof of
Lemma

12.4
).

Lemma 12.7
.

The translation of
\Phi_{d-1}
into first
-
order logic over reals given by
Proposition

12.5
is exponential in the size of
\mathcal{A}
.

Sketch of the proof.

The proof is inductive on the sub
-
formula
\Phi_{i}
for
i\in\{0,\ldots,d{-}1\}
.
The translation of the formula
\Phi_{0}
is exponential in
N=|Q|
, because so are the vectors of quantified variables and the basic predicates for expressing
\Delta_{\mathcal{D}}
and
{\preceq}
.
Now, the translation of the formula
\Phi_{i+1}
is obtained from the translation of
\Phi_{i}
by copying it a constant number of times
(because of the definition of
{F}{\uparrow}(x)
and
{F}{\downarrow}(x)
) and combining with translations of
\Delta_{\mathcal{D}}
.
Thus, the translation of the final formula
\Phi_{d-1}
has size of the form
C^{N}\cdot E(N)
,
where
C
is a constant and
E
is an exponential function bounding the size of the basic predicates.
∎

Corollary 12.8
.

Given a (nondeterministic or alternating) automaton
\mathcal{A}
, a representation of the algebraic number
\prob\!\big(\mathrm{L}(\mathcal{A})\big)
can be computed in three
-
fold exponential time in the size of
\mathcal{A}
.
For every fixed rational number
q
, the decision problem whether
\prob\!\big(\mathrm{L}(\mathcal{A})\big)
is equal, smaller, or greater than
q

can be solved in two
-
fold exponential space in the size of
\mathcal{A}
.
∎

Note that the latter decision problem is known to be EXPTIME
-
hard
[
PS20
, Proposition 7.6]
.

13
Stochastic branching processes

In this section we consider measures on the set of trees induced by stochastic branching processes, showing how
Corollary

1.3
follows from
Theorem

1.2
.
Essentially, such a reduction (in a slightly different setting) was already given by Niwiński, Przybyłko, and Skrzypczak
[
NPS20
]
(with unpublished proofs in
[
NPS24
]
).
To make this paper self
-
contained, we provide here a similar reduction, with a minor improvement with respect to complexity:
we construct a product automaton whose size is polynomial in the size of the branching process (including the binary representations of the rational probabilities involved) and of the original automaton.

In this section, we slightly generalize the notion of a tree, as follows.
A
ranked alphabet

(\Sigma,\mathrm{ar})
consists of a finite set
\Sigma
and of an arity function
\mathrm{ar}\colon\Sigma\to\mathbb{N}
.
Given a number
s\in\mathbb{N}
we write
\Sigma_{s}
for
\{a\in\Sigma\mid\mathrm{ar}(a)=s\}
.
Then, a
tree
over
(\Sigma,\mathrm{ar})
is a partial function
t\colon\mathbb{N}^{*}\rightharpoonup\Sigma
whose domain
\mathrm{dom}(t)
is non
-
empty and closed under taking prefixes,
and such that for each
node

v\in\mathrm{dom}(t)
and
direction

d\in\mathbb{N}
we have
vd\in\mathrm{dom}(t)
if and only if
1\leq d\leq\mathrm{ar}(t(v))

(i.e.,
\mathrm{ar}(a)
specifies the number of children of every node labelled by
a
).
A binary tree as defined in
Section

2
can be then seen as a special case where the arity of every letter is
2
, if we identify the directions
\mathtt{{\scriptstyle L}}
,
\mathtt{{\scriptstyle R}}
with
1
,
2
.
The set of all trees over
(\Sigma,\mathrm{ar})
is denoted
\mathrm{Tr}_{\Sigma,\mathrm{ar}}
.

An automaton over such a tree is defined as previously, except that a transition relation is of the form
\gamma\subseteq Q\times\bigcup_{s=0}^{\infty}\Sigma_{s}\times Q^{s}
;
then, defining a run
\rho
we require that
\big(\rho(v),t(v),\rho(v1),\dots,\allowbreak\rho(vs)\big)\in\gamma
, where
s=\mathrm{ar}(t(v))
.

A
branching process
is a tuple
\mathcal{P}=\langle\Sigma,\mathrm{ar},R,r_{I},\Theta,\theta\rangle
, where
(\Sigma,\mathrm{ar})
is a finite ranked alphabet,
R
is a finite set of states,

r_{I}\in R
is an initial state,
\Theta\subseteq\bigcup_{s=0}^{\infty}\Sigma_{s}\times R^{s}
is a set of transitions, and
\theta\colon R\to\mathbb{D}(\Theta)
is a function assigning distributions of transitions to states.
We assume that all probabilities occurring in
\theta
are rational, with numerator and denominator encoded in binary.

Intuitively, in order to generate a tree, we start with the state
r_{I}
in the root;
then, while having a state
r
in some node
v
, the function
\theta(r)
specifies the probability of each tuple
(a,r_{1},\dots,r_{\mathrm{ar}(a)})\in\Theta
;
we choose at random one of such tuples, we put the label
a
in
v
, and we send the states
r_{1},\dots,r_{\mathrm{ar}(a)}
to the children of
v
.
Formally, a
tree prefix
is a partial function
f\colon\mathbb{N}^{*}\to\Sigma
such that for each node in
\mathrm{dom}(f)
of the form
vd
, where
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
and
d\in\mathbb{N}
, we have
v\in\mathrm{dom}(f)
and
1\leq d\leq\mathrm{ar}(f(v))
;
it
agrees
with a tree
t
if
t{\upharpoonright}_{\mathrm{dom}(f)}=f
.
A
run
of
\mathcal{P}
over a tree prefix
f
is a function
\rho\colon\mathrm{dom}(f)\to\Theta
such that for each node
v\in\mathrm{dom}(\rho)
, the first coordinate of
\rho(v)
equals
f(v)
.
Suppose that
\mathrm{dom}(\rho)
is finite.
For every state
r\in R
we define by induction on
|\mathrm{dom}(\rho)|
probabilities

p_{\rho}^{r}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1&\mbox{if }\mathrm{dom}(\rho)=\emptyset,\\
\theta(r)(\rho(\varepsilon))\cdot p_{\rho{\upharpoonright}_{1}}^{r_{1}}\cdots p_{\rho{\upharpoonright}_{s}}^{r_{s}}&\mbox{if }\rho(\varepsilon)=(a,r_{1},\dots,r_{s}).\end{array}\right.

Then, we define
U_{f}
as the set of trees
t
that agree with a tree prefix
f
,
and we equip
\mathrm{Tr}_{\Sigma,\mathrm{ar}}
with a topology generated by a basis consisting of sets
U_{f}
for all tree prefixes
f
having finite domain.
Finally, we consider the Lebesgue measure
\prob_{\mathcal{P}}
defined for the basis by
\prob_{\mathcal{P}}(U_{f})=\sum_{\rho}p_{\rho}^{r_{I}}
, where the sum is over all runs
\rho
of
\mathcal{P}
over
f
.

Our goal is to prove the following theorem, which allows us to deduce
Corollary

1.3
from
Theorem

1.2
.

Theorem 13.1
.

Given a branching process
\mathcal{P}
and a nondeterministic parity tree automaton
\mathcal{A}
over the same ranked alphabet
(\Sigma,\mathrm{ar})
,
one can construct a nondeterministic parity tree automaton
\mathcal{B}
over an unranked (i.e., with all letters of arity
2
) alphabet
\{0,1\}
such that
\prob(\mathrm{L}(\mathcal{B}))=\prob_{\mathcal{P}}(\mathrm{L}(\mathcal{A}))
.
Moreover, the size of
\mathcal{B}
can be polynomial in the size of
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{A}
.

As a first step, we show how to realize a random choice from a finite set according to an arbitrary distribution as a choice of a random tree.

Lemma 13.2
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{D}(\Theta)
be a probability distribution over a finite set
\Theta
, with all probabilities rational.
Then, there exists a function
e_{\alpha}\colon\mathrm{Tr}_{\{0,1\}}\to\Theta
such that for each
x\in\Theta

•

we can construct an automaton recognizing the set
e^{-1}_{\alpha}(x)
, of size polynomial in the size of the representation of
\alpha
, and

•

the measure of
e^{-1}_{\alpha}(x)
equals
\alpha(x)
.

Proof.

Let
x_{1},\dots,x_{k}
be all the elements of
\Theta
,
let
N
be the product of denominators of all probabilities in
\alpha
, and let
n
be the length of the binary representation of
N
.
Consider also the numbers
p_{i}=N\cdot\sum_{j\leq i}\alpha(x_{i})
for
i\in\{0,\dots,k\}
; these are integers, where
p_{0}=0
and
p_{k}=N
.

Given a tree
t\in\mathrm{Tr}_{\{0,1\}}
, we split its rightmost branch into (infinitely many) fragments of length
n
, and we read labels on these fragments as binary numbers between
0
and
2^{n}-1
.
If none of these numbers is smaller than
N
, we put
e_{\alpha}(t)=x_{1}
.
Otherwise, we choose the first number that is smaller that
N
, denote it
\ell_{t}
, and we define
e_{\alpha}(t)
to be the unique element
x_{i}
for which
p_{i-1}\leq\ell_{t}<p_{i}
.

It is easy to construct automata recognizing the sets
e^{-1}_{\alpha}(x_{i})
.
Moreover, note that
n
is of polynomial size (the length of a product is bounded by the sum of lengths of factors),
and that the automata do not need to remember the whole number written in a fragment of the rightmost branch, but rather they can compare it with
p_{i-1}
and
p_{i}
bit-by-bit.
This means that they can be of size polynomial in the size of the representation of
\alpha
.

For the second item of the thesis, note that the probability of obtaining a particular number in a given fragment of the rightmost branch is
2^{-n}
, and that results in different fragments are independent.
Thus, given
i\in\{1,\dots,k\}
and
j\geq 0
,
the probability that the first
j
fragments contain numbers above
N-1
equals
(1-N\cdot 2^{-n})^{j}
,
and the probability that the
j
-th fragment contains a number
\ell
satisfying
p_{n-1}\leq\ell<p_{n}
equals
N\cdot\alpha(x_{i})\cdot 2^{-n}
,
hence the probability that both these events hold equals
(1-N\cdot 2^{-n})^{j}\cdot N\cdot\alpha(x_{i})\cdot 2^{-n}
.
Moreover, all the fragments contain numbers above
N{-}1
with probability
\lim_{j\to\infty}(1-N\cdot 2^{-n})^{j}=0
; this way of obtaining
e_{\alpha}(t)=x_{1}
is negligible.

14
Conclusions

We have shown that the following question can be effectively solved.

What is the probability that a randomly chosen tree satisfies a given formula of MSO?

This can be viewed as a completion of the famous Rabin tree theorem by its probabilistic aspect.
If a formula is replaced by an equivalent nondeterministic or alternating parity automaton
\mathcal{A}
, our algorithm works in elementary time.

We believe that our results or techniques may find some further applications although—as mentioned in Introduction—a number of related problems in probabilistic verification of finite-state system is known to be undecidable.
One of the research directions points to logics whose original version reduces to MSO logic over trees, but the decidability status of the probabilistic version is unknown as, for instance PCTL*.

On the technical level, our work confirms the usefulness of the construction of the probabilistic powerdomain
\mathcal{D}
that comprises all the necessary information and is still manageable.
We have also discovered that the framework of the
\mu
-
calculus—originally based on complete lattices—can be transposed to more general partial orders.
The unary
\mu
-
calculus that we have introduced for this purpose has a potential of incorporating other formalisms, going beyond the MSO theory of the tree.
As a matter of fact, our construction provides a way to translate a generic formula of unary
\mu
-
calculus into a formula of Tarski’s first
-
order theory of reals,
which yields decidability of the formalism in question.
