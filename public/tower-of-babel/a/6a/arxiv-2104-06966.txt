Sums of Four Squareful numbers


Abstract
. We find an asymptotic formula for the number of primitive vectors ( z 1 , … , z 4 ) ∈ ( ℤ ≠ 0 ) 4 (z_{1},\ldots,z_{4})\in(\mathbb{Z}_{\neq 0})^{4} such that z 1 , … , z 4 z_{1},\ldots,z_{4} are all squareful and bounded by B B , and z 1 + ⋯ + z 4 = 0 z_{1}+\cdots+z_{4}=0 . Our result agrees in the power of B B and log ⁡ B \log B with the Campana–Manin conjecture of Pieropan, Smeets, Tanimoto and Várilly-Alvarado.



Alec Shute

1.
Introduction

The notion of
Campana points
, first discussed by Campana in
[
7
]
and
[
8
]
, and Abramovich in
[
1
]
, is receiving increasing attention in the field of arithmetic geometry. Campana points associated to an orbifold
(X,D)
can be viewed as rational points on
X
that are integral with respect to a weighted boundary divisor
D
, and thus provide a way to interpolate between integral and rational points.

The quantitative study of the arithmetic of Campana orbifolds was kick-started by the discussions in
[
1
]
and
[
22
]
. A motivating example for the development of the theory was given in
[
22
]
, where Poonen poses the problem of finding the number of coprime integers
z_{0},z_{1}
such that
z_{0},z_{1}
and
z_{0}+z_{1}
are all squareful and bounded by
B
(We recall that a nonzero integer
z
is
m
-
full
if for any prime
p|z
, we have
p^{m}|z
, and
squareful
if it is
2
-full). This corresponds to counting Campana points on the orbifold
(\mathbb{P}^{1},D)
, where
D
is the divisor
\frac{1}{2}[0]+\frac{1}{2}[1]+\frac{1}{2}[\infty]
. Since this problem seems too difficult at the moment, in
[
28
]
, Van Valckenborgh considers the higher-dimensional analogue
(\mathbb{P}^{k-2},D)
, where
D=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{1}{2}D_{i}
for hyperplanes
D_{1},\ldots,D_{k}
. This leads to the study of the asymptotic size
N_{k}(B)
of the set

(1.1)

\mathscr{N}_{k}(B)=\left\{\mathbf{z}\in(\mathbb{Z}_{\neq 0})^{k}_{\textrm{prim}}:|z_{i}|\leqslant B,z_{i}\textrm{ squareful for all }i,\sum_{i=1}^{k}z_{i}=0\right\},

as
B\rightarrow\infty
. The main result in
[
28
, Theorem 1.1]
is that for any
k\geqslant 5
, we have
N_{k}(B)=cB^{k/2-1}+O(B^{k/2-1-\delta})
for some constants
c,\delta>0
. The remaining cases of interest are
k=3
, corresponding to Poonen’s original question, and
k=4
. In this paper, we treat the case
k=4
. We define

(1.2)

\mathscr{T}=\{(z_{1},\ldots,z_{4})\in\mathbb{Z}^{4}_{\textrm{prim}}:z_{1}\cdots z_{4}=\square\},

and consider the counting problem

(1.3)

N(B)=\#(\mathscr{N}_{4}(B)\backslash\mathscr{T}).

Our main result is the following theorem.

Theorem 1.1
.

For any
\epsilon>0
we have

(1.4)

N(B)=cB+O(B^{734/735+\epsilon}),

for an absolute constant
c>0
given explicitly in (
5.10
) and Lemma
5.5
.

Interest in the quantitative arithmetic of Campana orbifolds has been further sparked by the recent work
[
21
]
, in which Pieropan, Smeets, Tanimoto and Várilly-Alvarado formulate a Manin-type conjecture for Fano Campana orbifolds. The authors establish their conjecture in the special case of vector group compactifications, using the height zeta function method developed by Chambert-Loir and Tschinkel in
[
9
]
and
[
10
]
.

In Section 2, we discuss the compatibility of Theorem
1.1
with the prediction from
[
21
, Conjecture 1.1]
, which is henceforth referred to as the
Campana–Manin conjecture
. We find that our result agrees with the Campana–Manin conjecture in the power of
B
and
\log B
, and the set
\mathscr{T}
that we remove corresponds to a thin set of Campana points. The leading constant
c
from Theorem
1.1
is discussed in Section
5
. However, in the light of our recent work
[
25
]
, we do not expect the leading constant to agree with the prediction from the Campana–Manin conjecture without the removal of further thin sets.

A number of other instances of the Campana–Manin conjecture have been studied in addition to the results mentioned above. In
[
6
]
, Browning and Yamagishi consider the orbifold
(\mathbb{P}^{n},D)
for
D=\sum_{i=0}^{n+1}(1-\frac{1}{m_{i}})D_{i}
, where
D_{0},\ldots,D_{n}
are coordinate hyperplanes, and
D_{n+1}
is a general hyperplane. Their main result is an asymptotic formula for the number of points on this orbifold under the assumptions that
m_{i}\geqslant 2
for all
i
, and that there exists some
j\in\{0,\ldots,n+1\}
such that

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leqslant i\leqslant n+1\\
i\neq j\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{m_{i}(m_{i}+1)}\geqslant 1.

In
[
20
]
, Pieropan and Schindler establish the Campana–Manin conjecture for complete smooth split toric varieties satisfying an additional technical assumption, by developing a very general version of the hyperbola method. In
[
29
]
, Xiao treats the case of biequivariant compactifications of the Heisenberg group over
\mathbb{Q}
, using the height zeta function method. Finally, in
[
27
]
, Streeter studies
m
-full values of norm forms by considering the orbifold
(\mathbb{P}_{K}^{d-1},(1-\frac{1}{m})\mathbb{V}(N_{E/K}))
, where
K
is a number field,
\mathbb{V}(N_{E/K})
the divisor cut out by a norm form associated to a degree-
d
Galois extension
E/K
, and
m\geqslant 2
is an integer which is coprime to
d
if
d
is not prime.

We now summarise the proof of Theorem
1.1
. The approach is broadly similar to previous results by Browning and Yamagishi in
[
6
]
and Van Valckenborgh in
[
28
]
. Every nonzero squareful integer
z
can be written uniquely in the form
z=y^{3}x^{2}
, where
y
is a nonzero square-free integer and
x
is a positive integer. For a fixed choice of
\mathbf{y}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{4})
, the equation
z_{1}+\cdots+z_{4}=0
defines a quadric
Q_{\mathbf{y}}
cut out by the polynomial
F_{\mathbf{y}^{3}}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{4}y_{i}^{3}x_{i}^{2}
. The condition
z_{1}\cdots z_{4}=\square
from (
1.2
) is equivalent to the condition
y_{1}\cdots y_{4}=\square
. Therefore

(1.5)

N(B)=\frac{1}{16}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{y}\in(\mathbb{Z}_{\neq 0})^{4}\\
y_{1},\ldots,y_{4}\textrm{ square-free}\\
y_{1}\cdots y_{4}\neq\square\end{subarray}}N_{\mathbf{y}}(B),

where

N_{\mathbf{y}}(B)=\#\left\{\mathbf{x}\in(\mathbb{Z}_{\neq 0})^{4}:F_{\mathbf{y}^{3}}(\mathbf{x})=0,\begin{tabular}[]{l}$\gcd(x_{1}y_{1},\ldots,x_{4}y_{4})=1$\\
$\max_{1\leqslant i\leqslant 4}|y_{i}^{3}x_{i}^{2}|\leqslant B$\end{tabular}\right\}.

The factor
1/16
comes from the fact that for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,4\}
, there are two choices for the sign of
x_{i}
corresponding to the same choice of
z_{i}
.

In Section
4
, we study the closely related counting problem

(1.6)

N_{\mathbf{a}}(B)=\#\left\{\mathbf{x}\in(\mathbb{Z}_{\neq 0})^{4}:F_{\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{x})=0,\max_{1\leqslant i\leqslant 4}|a_{i}x_{i}^{2}|\leqslant B\right\},

where we have removed the coprimality condition and replaced
\mathbf{y}
with an arbitrary vector
\mathbf{a}=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{4})\in(\mathbb{Z}_{\neq 0})^{4}
.

Remark 1.2
.

Rational points on non-singular quadric surfaces are known to conform to the classical Manin’s conjecture
[
13
]
. Let
A=a_{1}\cdots a_{4}
. It can be shown that the Picard group of the quadric
F_{\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{x})=0
has rank 1 when
A\neq\square
and rank 2 when
A=\square
. Thus Manin’s conjecture predicts that

(1.7)

N_{\mathbf{a}}(B)\sim\begin{cases}c_{\mathbf{a}}B,&\textrm{ if }A\neq\square\\
c^{\prime}_{\mathbf{a}}B\log B,&\textrm{ if }A=\square,\end{cases}

for constants
c_{\mathbf{a}},c^{\prime}_{\mathbf{a}}
(depending on
\mathbf{a}
) which can be expressed as a product of local densities. The extra factor of
\log B
in the case
A=\square
explains why it is necessary to remove the set
\mathscr{T}
in the formulation of Theorem
1.1
.

In order to estimate
N_{\mathbf{a}}(B)
, we apply a version of the circle method introduced by Duke, Friedlander and Iwaniec in
[
12
]
, and refined by Heath-Brown in
[
15
]
. The more classical forms of the circle method used in
[
6
]
and
[
28
]
are not sufficient for our purposes, but the version in
[
15
]
is particularly well-suited to dealing with quadratic forms. In fact, in
[
15
]
, Heath-Brown proves a vast generalisation of (
1.7
) by finding an asymptotic formula for the quantity

N_{w}(Q,B)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\\
Q(\mathbf{x})=0\end{subarray}}w(B^{-1}\mathbf{x}),

for any
n\geqslant 3
, any non-singular indefinite quadratic form
Q\in\mathbb{Z}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
, and any
w:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0}
belonging to a suitably defined class of smooth weight functions. The following theorem is the main result from Section
4
. It features the quantities

\Delta=\prod_{i=1}^{4}\gcd\left(a_{i},\prod_{j\neq i}a_{j}\right),\quad A=a_{1}\cdots a_{4},

as well as the
singular series

\mathfrak{G}_{\mathbf{a}}
and the
singular integral

\sigma_{\infty}(\boldsymbol{\epsilon})
, defined respectively in (
4.1
) and (
4.2
). Note that
\sigma_{\infty}(\boldsymbol{\epsilon})
only depends on
\epsilon_{i}=\operatorname{sgn}(a_{i})
.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
\mathbf{a}\in(\mathbb{Z}_{\neq 0})^{4}
be such that
A\neq\square
and
|A|\leqslant B^{4/7}
. Then

(1.8)

N_{\mathbf{a}}(B)=\frac{\mathfrak{G}_{\mathbf{a}}\sigma_{\infty}(\boldsymbol{\epsilon})B}{|A|^{1/2}}+O\left(\frac{B^{41/42+\epsilon}\Delta^{1/3}}{|A|^{11/24}}\right),

where the implied constant depends only on
\epsilon
.

To complete the proof of Theorem
1.1
, in Section
5
, we apply Theorem
1.3
together with an inclusion-exclusion argument in order to reinsert the coprimality condition and obtain an estimate for
N_{\mathbf{y}}(B)
. Returning to (
1.5
), we can take a sum over these estimates for
N_{\mathbf{y}}(B)
in the range
\max_{i}|y_{i}|\leqslant D
, where
D
is a small power of
B
. The contribution from the remaining range
\max_{i}|y_{i}|>D
is studied in Section
3
using an elementary argument, and forms part of the error term in Theorem
1.1
.

It is important in the proof of Theorem
1.1
that the dependence of the estimate in Theorem
1.3
on the coefficients
a_{1},\ldots,a_{4}
is made completely explicit.
Several authors have obtained estimates of this type for quadratic forms. In
[
2
, Theorem 2]
, Browning applies the machinery from
[
15
]
to find such an estimate for the counting problem corresponding to
N_{\mathbf{a}}(B)
, but in
n\geqslant 5
variables. Subsequently in
[
4
, Theorem 4.1]
, Browning and Heath-Brown carried this out in four variables. This latter result is nearly sufficient for our purposes, but Theorem
1.3
represents a refinement in which we drop the assumptions made in
[
4
, Theorem 4.1]
that
A
is nearly square-free and all the coefficients
a_{1},\ldots,a_{4}
are roughly the same size. Finally, we mention that using other techniques from the geometry of numbers, Comtat provides in
[
11
, Theorem 1.2]
a completely uniform estimate for the number of zeros
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}_{\textrm{prim}}
of a non-singular quadratic form
Q
in
n\geqslant 3
variables which lie in an arbitrary box
|x_{i}|\leqslant B_{i}
for
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
. However, the resulting bound
N_{\mathbf{a}}(B)\leqslant B/|A|^{1/4}
does not have a good enough dependence on
A
to be useful for our purposes.

Notation

We take
\mathbb{N}=\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 1}
. We denote by
(\mathbb{Z}_{\neq 0})^{n}_{\textrm{prim}}
the set of vectors
(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})
such that
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}
are nonzero integers and
\gcd(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})=1
. For a prime
p
, we let
\nu_{p}
denote the
p
-adic valuation. We write
e(\cdot)
for the function
e^{2\pi i(\cdot)}
and
e_{q}(\cdot)
for the function
e^{2\pi i(\cdot)/q}
. We use boldface letters to denote vectors with four components, for example
\mathbf{z}=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{4})
. For a vector
\mathbf{v}
, we define
|\mathbf{v}|=\max_{1\leqslant i\leqslant 4}|v_{i}|
. All implied constants will be allowed to depend on
\epsilon
, but nothing else unless otherwise stated. Moreover,
\epsilon
will denote a small positive number which for convenience we allow to take different values at different points in the argument.

2.
The Campana–Manin conjecture

In this section we recall from
[
21
, Section 3]
the definition of Campana points and the statement of the Campana–Manin conjecture. We demonstrate that the set
\mathscr{T}
removed in the definition of
N(B)
is a thin set of Campana points, and that the power of
B
and
\log B
in Theorem
1.1
is consistent with the prediction from the Campana–Manin conjecture.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
F
be a field. A
Campana orbifold
is a pair
(X,D)
, where
X
is a smooth variety over
F
and

D=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathscr{A}}\epsilon_{\alpha}D_{\alpha}

is an effective Weil
\mathbb{Q}
-divisor of
X
over
F
(where the
D_{\alpha}
are prime divisors) such that

(1)

For all
\alpha\in\mathscr{A}
, either
\epsilon_{\alpha}=1
or
\epsilon_{\alpha}
takes the form
1-1/m_{\alpha}
for some
m_{\alpha}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 2}
.

(2)

The support
D_{\textrm{red}}=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathscr{A}}D_{\alpha}
of
D
has strict normal crossings on
X
.

We say that a Campana orbifold is
klt
if
\epsilon_{\alpha}\neq 1
for all
\alpha\in\mathscr{A}
.

Let
(X,D)
be a Campana orbifold. Campana points will be defined as points
P\in X(F)
satisfying certain conditions. These conditions are dependent on a finite set
S
of places of
F
containing all archimedean places, and a choice of
good integral model
of
(X,D)
over
\mathscr{O}_{F,S}
. This model is defined to be a pair
(\mathscr{X},\mathscr{D})
, where
\mathscr{X}
is a flat, proper model of
X
over
\mathscr{O}_{F,S}
, with
\mathscr{X}
regular, and

\mathscr{D}=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathscr{A}}\epsilon_{\alpha}\mathscr{D}_{\alpha},

where
\mathscr{D}_{\alpha}
denotes the Zariski closure of
D_{\alpha}
in
\mathscr{X}
.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
P\in(X\backslash D_{\textrm{red}})(F)
. For a place
v\notin S
, let
\mathscr{P}_{v}
denote the induced point in
\mathscr{X}(\mathscr{O}_{v})
obtained via the valuative criterion for properness as stated in
[
14
, Theorem II.4.7]
. For
\alpha\in\mathscr{A}
, we define the
intersection multiplicity

n_{v}(\mathscr{D}_{\alpha},P)
of
\mathscr{D}_{\alpha}
and
P
at
v
to be the colength of the ideal
\mathscr{P}_{v}^{*}\mathscr{D}_{\alpha}
in
\mathscr{O}_{v}
. Then the
intersection number
of
P
and
\mathscr{D}
at
v
is defined to be

n_{v}(\mathscr{D},P)=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathscr{A}}\epsilon_{\alpha}n_{v}(\mathscr{D}_{\alpha},P).

Definition 2.3
.

Let
(X,D)
be a Campana orbifold with a good integral model
(\mathscr{X},\mathscr{D})
over
\mathscr{O}_{F,S}
. A point
P\in(X\backslash D_{\textrm{red}})(F)
is a
Campana

\mathscr{O}_{F,S}
-point
of
(\mathscr{X},\mathscr{D})
if for all
v\notin S
and all
\alpha\in\mathscr{A}
, we have

(1)

If
\epsilon_{\alpha}=1
, then
n_{v}(\mathscr{D}_{\alpha},P)=0
.

(2)

If
\epsilon_{\alpha}\neq 1
, so that
\epsilon_{\alpha}=1-1/m_{\alpha}
for some
m_{\alpha}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 2}
, then either
n_{v}(\mathscr{D}_{\alpha},P)=0
or
n_{v}(\mathscr{D}_{\alpha},P)\geqslant m_{\alpha}
.

We denote the set of Campana
\mathscr{O}_{F,S}
-points of
(\mathscr{X},\mathscr{D})
by
(\mathscr{X},\mathscr{D})(\mathscr{O}_{F,S})
.

Example 2.4
.

Campana points are related to
m
-full values of polynomials. We consider a smooth projective variety
X\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{n}
over
\mathbb{Q}
, and a divisor

(2.1)

D=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\left(1-\frac{1}{m_{i}}\right)D_{i},

where
m_{i}\geqslant 2
are integers, and
D_{i}
are prime divisors on
X
cut out by polynomial equations
f_{i}=0
. Choosing the obvious good integral model
(\mathscr{X},\mathscr{D})
, a rational point
z\in(X\backslash\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}D_{i})(\mathbb{Q})
, represented by
(z_{0},\ldots,z_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}^{n+1}_{\textrm{prim}}
is a Campana
\mathbb{Z}
-point of
(\mathscr{X},\mathscr{D})
if and only if
f_{i}(z_{0},\ldots,z_{n})
is
m_{i}
-full for all
i\in\{0,\ldots,k\}
.

Definition 2.5
.

For an irreducible variety
X
over
F
, a subset
A\subseteq X(F)
is
type I
if
A
is a proper closed subvariety of
X
, and
type II
if it can be written in the form
A=\varphi(V(F))
, where
V
is an integral projective variety with
\dim(V)=\dim(X)
and
\varphi\colon V\rightarrow X
is a generically surjective morphism of degree at least 2. A
thin
set of
X(F)
is a subset of a finite union of type I and type II sets. In
[
21
, Definition 3.7]
, a thin set of Campana
\mathscr{O}_{F,S}
-points is defined to be the intersection of a thin set of
X(F)
with the set of Campana points
(\mathscr{X},\mathscr{D})(\mathscr{O}_{F,S})
.

We now come to the statement of the Campana–Manin conjecture given in
[
21
, Conjecture 1.1]
. Let
K
be a number field, and let
(X,D)
be a Campana orbifold over
K
with a good integral model
(\mathscr{X},\mathscr{D})
over
\mathscr{O}_{K,S}
. Let
(\mathscr{L},\|\cdot\|)
be an adelically metrized big and nef line bundle on
X
and
[L]
the associated divisor class. We let
H_{\mathscr{L}}\colon X(K)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0}
denote the corresponding height function, as defined in
[
19
, Section 1]
.
We recall that the
effective cone

\Lambda_{\operatorname{eff}}
of a variety
X
is defined as

\Lambda_{\operatorname{eff}}=\{[D]\in\operatorname{Pic}(X):[D]\geqslant 0\}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{R}.

Definition 2.6
.

Let
[K_{X}]
denote the canonical divisor class. Given the above data, we define

a=\inf\{t\in\mathbb{R}:t[L]+[K_{X}]+[D]\in\Lambda_{\operatorname{eff}}\},

and we define
b
to be the codimension of the minimal supported face of
\Lambda_{\operatorname{eff}}
which contains
a[L]+[K_{X}]+[D]
.

Conjecture 2.7

(Pieropan, Smeets, Tanimoto, Várilly-Alvarado)
.

Suppose that
(X,D)
is a klt Campana orbifold, such that
-(K_{X}+D)
is ample (in this case we say that the orbifold is Fano). Assume that the set of Campana points
(\mathscr{X},\mathscr{D})(\mathscr{O}_{K,S})
is not itself thin. Then there is a thin set
\mathscr{T}
of Campana
\mathscr{O}_{K,S}
-points such that

\#\{P\in(\mathscr{X},\mathscr{D})(\mathscr{O}_{K,S})\backslash\mathscr{T}:H_{\mathscr{L}}(P)\leqslant B\}\sim cB^{a}(\log B)^{b-1},

as
B\rightarrow\infty
, where
a,b
are as in Definition
2.6
, and
c>0
is an explicit constant described in
[
21
, Section 3.3]
.

Remark 2.8
.

The hypothesis that the Campana points themselves are not thin is discussed by Nakahara and Streeter in
[
18
]
. The authors establish in
[
18
, Theorem 1.1]
a connection between thin sets of Campana points and weak approximation, in the spirit of Serre’s arguments in
[
24
, Theorem 3.5.7]
. Combining this with
[
18
, Corollary 1.4]
, it can be shown that this hypothesis holds for the orbifold we consider below.

We now put the counting problem
N(B)
from (
1.3
) into the context of Conjecture
2.7
. The Campana orbifold under consideration is a special case of Example (
2.4
). We consider the variety
X=\mathbb{P}^{2}
over
\mathbb{Q}
. Below we will use
z_{1},z_{2},z_{3}
to denote a representative of the point
[z_{1}:z_{2}:z_{3}]\in\mathbb{P}^{2}(\mathbb{Q})
such that
(z_{1},z_{2},z_{3})\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}_{\textrm{prim}}
. Let
D
be the divisor on
X
given by

D=\sum_{i=1}^{4}\frac{1}{2}D_{i},

where

D_{i}=\begin{cases}\{z_{i}=0\},&\textrm{if }1\leqslant i\leqslant 3,\\
\{z_{1}+z_{2}+z_{3}=0\},&\textrm{if }i=4.\end{cases}

The support of
D
has strict normal crossings on
\mathbb{P}^{2}
, and so
(\mathbb{P}^{2},D)
is a Campana orbifold. Let
(\mathscr{X},\mathscr{D})
denote the obvious smooth proper model of
(\mathbb{P}^{2},D)
over
\mathbb{Z}
. Here we have chosen the set of bad places
S
to consist only of the archimedean place
|\cdot|_{\infty}
.

Let
z\in(\mathbb{P}^{2}\backslash D_{\textrm{red}})(\mathbb{Q})
. The intersection multiplicity of
z
and
D_{i}
at a prime
p
is then the
p
-adic valuation
\nu_{p}(z_{i})
, when
i\in\{1,2,3\}
, and
\nu_{p}(z_{1}+z_{2}+z_{3})
when
i=4
. The condition for a point
z\in(X\backslash D_{\textrm{red}})(\mathbb{Q})
to be Campana
\mathbb{Z}
-point of
(\mathscr{X},\mathscr{D})
is therefore that
z_{1},z_{2},z_{3}
and
z_{1}+z_{2}+z_{3}
are all squareful.

For convenience we introduce a new variable
z_{4}
which is defined by the equation
z_{4}=z_{1}+z_{2}+z_{3}
. We choose the ample line bundle
\mathscr{L}=\mathscr{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{2}}(1)
, and the generating set
\{z_{1},z_{2},z_{3},z_{4}\}
for the global sections of
\mathscr{L}
. This choice of generating set gives rise to the height function

\displaystyle H:\mathbb{P}^{2}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0}

\displaystyle z=[z_{1}:z_{2}:z_{3}]

\displaystyle\mapsto\max(|z_{1}|,|z_{2}|,|z_{3}|,|z_{4}|).

We note that
(z_{1},z_{2},z_{3})\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}_{\textrm{prim}}
if and only if
(z_{1},\ldots,z_{4})\in\mathbb{Z}^{4}_{\textrm{prim}}
. Therefore, recalling the definition of
\mathscr{N}_{4}(B)
from (
1.1
), there is a 2-1 map

(2.2)

\begin{split}\varphi:\mathscr{N}_{4}(B)&\rightarrow\{z\in(\mathbb{P}^{2},\mathscr{D})(\mathbb{Z}):H(z)\leqslant B\}\\
(z_{1},\ldots,z_{4})&\mapsto[z_{1}:z_{2}:z_{3}].\end{split}

We now compute the constants
a
and
b
from Conjecture
2.7
in this example. We let
[L]
denote the hyperplane class corresponding to the line bundle
\mathscr{L}
, and
[K_{\mathbb{P}^{2}}]
the canonical divisor class. We recall that
\Pic\mathbb{P}^{2}\cong\mathbb{Z}
, with the isomorphism given by the degree. We have
\deg([L])=1
,
\deg([K_{\mathbb{P}^{2}}])=-3
and
\deg([D])=4\cdot 1/2=2
. Therefore

\displaystyle a

\displaystyle=\inf\{t\in\mathbb{R}:t[L]+[K_{\mathbb{P}^{2}}]+[D]\in\Lambda_{\operatorname{eff}}\}

\displaystyle=\inf\{t\in\mathbb{R}:t-3+2\geqslant 0\}

\displaystyle=1.

The minimal supported face of
\Lambda_{\operatorname{eff}}
which contains
a[L]+[K_{\mathbb{P}^{2}}]+[D]=[0]
is
\{0\}
, which has codimension one in
\Lambda_{\operatorname{eff}}
, so
b=1
. Conjecture
2.7
therefore states that there is a thin set of Campana points
\mathscr{Z}\subseteq(\mathbb{P}^{2},\mathscr{D})(\mathbb{Z})
, such that

\#\{P\in(\mathbb{P}^{2},\mathscr{D})(\mathbb{Z})\backslash\mathscr{Z}:H(P)\leqslant B\}=cB(1+o(1))

for some constant
c>0
. Theorem
1.1
states that this result holds true when
\mathscr{Z}=\varphi(\mathscr{T})
, where
\mathscr{T}
is defined in (
1.2
), except possibly for a different value for the leading constant. To conclude this section, we show that
\varphi(\mathscr{T})
is indeed thin.

Lemma 2.9
.

Let
\mathscr{T}
and
\varphi
be defined as in (
1.2
) and (
2.2
) respectively. Then
\varphi(\mathscr{T})\cap(\mathbb{P}^{2},\mathscr{D})(\mathbb{Z})
is a thin set of Campana points in
(\mathbb{P}^{2},\mathscr{D})(\mathbb{Z})
.

Proof.

It suffices to show that
\varphi(\mathscr{T})
is a thin set in
\mathbb{P}^{2}(\mathbb{Q})
. By abuse of notation we view
\mathscr{T}
as a subset of
\mathbb{P}^{3}(\mathbb{Q})
via the map
(z_{1},\ldots,z_{4})\mapsto[z_{1}:\cdots:z_{4}]
. We begin by showing that
\mathscr{T}
is a thin subset
\mathbb{P}^{3}(\mathbb{Q})
. Consider the weighted projective space
\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{Q}}(2,1,1,1,1)
with variables
t,z_{1},\ldots,z_{4}
(we refer the reader to
[
17
]
for the basic definitions pertaining to weighted projective spaces). We have an embedding

\displaystyle\nu:\mathbb{P}(2,1,1,1,1)

\displaystyle\hookrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{10}

\displaystyle[t:z_{1}:\cdots:z_{4}]

\displaystyle\mapsto[t:z_{1}^{2}:z_{1}z_{2}:\cdots:z_{4}^{2}],

which on the
z_{i}
-variables is the Veronese embedding of degree 2. The polynomial
f(t,z_{1},\ldots,z_{4})=t^{2}-z_{1}z_{2}z_{3}z_{4}
is weighted homogeneous of degree
4
, and so defines a subvariety
V
of
\mathbb{P}(2,1,1,1,1)
. Let
Y
denote the image of
\nu
and write
t,y_{11},\ldots,y_{44}
for variables on
\mathbb{P}^{10}
. Then
\nu(V)
is a hypersurface of
Y
defined by the equation
t^{2}=y_{12}y_{34}
. From this we see that
V
is integral, projective and of dimension 3.

Consider the morphism
\pi:V\rightarrow\mathbb{P}^{3}
defined by
[t:z_{1}:\cdots z_{4}]\rightarrow[z_{1}:\cdots:z_{4}]
. This is étale of degree 2 on the open subset
V^{\prime}
of
V
defined by
z_{1}\cdots z_{4}\neq 0
. The set
W\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{3}({\mathbb{Q}})
defined by the equation
z_{1}\cdots z_{4}=0
is a type I thin set, and
\mathscr{T}=\pi(V^{\prime}(\mathbb{Q}))\cup W
, so we deduce that
\mathscr{T}
is thin in
\mathbb{P}^{3}_{\mathbb{Q}}
.

We can now show that
\varphi(\mathscr{T})
is thin in
\mathbb{P}^{2}_{\mathbb{Q}}
. To do this, we intersect
\mathbb{P}^{3},V^{\prime}
and

with the hyperplane

defined by the equation

. The map

is ramified along the set

, which is a union of hyperplanes

given by
\{z_{i}=0\}
. Since the intersection of

with the

is smooth and transversal, it follows from
[
23
, Section 9.4]
that
\pi((V^{\prime}(\mathbb{Q})\cap H(\mathbb{Q}))\cup(W\cap H(\mathbb{Q}))
is thin in

. The image of this set under the obvious isomorphism

sending

to

is precisely

, so we conclude that

is a thin set in

.
∎

3.
Dealing with the large coefficients

Given a nonzero squareful number
z_{i}
, we let
x_{i},y_{i}
denote the unique integers such that
x_{i}\in\mathbb{N}
,
y_{i}
is square-free, and
z_{i}=y_{i}^{3}x_{i}^{2}
. We will also use the notation
Y=y_{1}\cdots y_{4}
. For
B,D\geqslant 1
, we define

\displaystyle M(B,D)

\displaystyle=\#\left\{\mathbf{z}\in(\mathbb{Z}_{\neq 0})^{4}_{\textrm{prim}}:\begin{tabular}[]{l}$\sum_{i=1}^{4}z_{i}=0,z_{i}\textrm{ squareful for all }i,$\\
$|\mathbf{z}|\leqslant B,|Y|\geqslant D$\\
\end{tabular}\right\}.

The aim of this section is to prove the following upper bound.

Proposition 3.1
.

We have
M(B,D)=O(B^{1+\epsilon}D^{-1/12})
.

The key result in the proof of Proposition
3.1
is the following upper bound for the quantity

\displaystyle N(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})

\displaystyle=\#\left\{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in(\mathbb{Z}_{\neq 0})^{4}:\begin{tabular}[]{l}$y_{1},\ldots,y_{4}\textrm{ square-free,}\sum_{i=1}^{4}x_{i}^{2}y_{i}^{3}=0,$\\
$|x_{i}|\leqslant X_{i},|y_{i}|\leqslant Y_{i}\textrm{ for all }i$\\
\end{tabular}\right\}.

Proposition 3.2
.

We have

N(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})=O((X_{1}\cdots X_{4})^{1/2+\epsilon}(Y_{1}\cdots Y_{4})^{2/3+\epsilon}).

We explain how to deduce Proposition
3.1
from Proposition
3.2
. We define

\displaystyle M_{1}(B;\mathbf{R})

\displaystyle=\#\left\{\mathbf{z}\in(\mathbb{Z}_{\neq 0})^{4}:\begin{tabular}[]{l}$\sum_{i=1}^{4}z_{i}=0,z_{i}\textrm{ squareful for all }i,$\\
$|z_{i}|\leqslant B,R_{i}\leqslant|y_{i}|<2R_{i}\textrm{ for all }i$\\
\end{tabular}\right\}.

Then

(3.1)

M(B,D)\ll\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{R}\textrm{ dyadic }\\
R_{1}\cdots R_{4}\geqslant D\end{subarray}}M_{1}(B;\mathbf{R}),

We observe that the conditions
|\mathbf{z}|\leqslant B
and
R_{i}\leqslant|y_{i}|
imply that
|x_{i}|^{2}\leqslant B/R_{i}^{3}
. Consequently,

M_{1}(B;\mathbf{R})\leqslant N\left(\left(\sqrt{\frac{B}{R_{1}^{3}}},\ldots,\sqrt{\frac{B}{R_{4}^{3}}}\right),\left(2R_{1},\ldots,2R_{4}\right)\right).

Applying Proposition
3.2
, we obtain

M_{1}(B;\mathbf{R})\ll B^{1+\epsilon}(R_{1}\cdots R_{4})^{-1/12}.

We conclude from (
3.1
) that

\displaystyle M(B,D)

\displaystyle\ll B^{1+\epsilon}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{R}\textrm{ dyadic }\\
R_{1}\cdots R_{4}\geqslant D\end{subarray}}(R_{1}\cdots R_{4})^{-1/12}\ll B^{1+\epsilon}D^{-1/12},

as claimed in Proposition
3.1
.

Proof of Proposition
3.2
.

For
k\ \in\{1,\ldots,4\}
, we define

S_{k}(\alpha)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x_{k}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\neq 0}\\
|x_{k}|\leqslant X_{k}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y_{k}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\neq 0}\\
|y_{k}|\leqslant Y_{k}\\
y_{k}\textrm{ square-free}\end{subarray}}e(x_{k}^{2}y_{k}^{3}).

Then

N(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})=\int_{0}^{1}\prod_{k=1}^{4}S_{k}(\alpha)\mathrm{d}\alpha.

By Hölder’s inequality, we have

(3.2)

N(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})\leqslant\left(\prod_{k=1}^{4}\int_{0}^{1}|S_{k}(\alpha)|^{4}\mathrm{d}\alpha\right)^{1/4}.

We fix
k\in\{1,\ldots,4\}
, and to ease notation we write
X_{k}=X,Y_{k}=Y
. Then

(3.3)

\int_{0}^{1}|S_{k}(\alpha)|^{4}\mathrm{d}\alpha=N(X,Y),

where

N(X,Y)=\#\left\{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in(\mathbb{Z}_{\neq 0})^{4}:\begin{tabular}[]{l}$y_{1},\ldots,y_{4}\textrm{ square-free, }$\\
$x_{1}^{2}y_{1}^{3}+x_{2}^{2}y_{2}^{3}=x_{3}^{2}y_{3}^{3}+x_{4}^{2}y_{4}^{3}$\\
$|\mathbf{x}|\leqslant X,|\mathbf{y}|\leqslant Y$\end{tabular}\right\}.\\

In view of (
3.2
), the task now is to show that

(3.4)

N(X,Y)=O(X^{2+\epsilon}Y^{8/3+\epsilon}).

Throughout the remainder of the argument, we make repeated use of the trivial estimate for the divisor function, namely that the number of divisors of a positive integer
d
is
O(d^{\epsilon})
.

We begin by considering the trivial cases
x_{i}=\pm x_{j}
for some
i\neq j
. Without loss of generality, suppose
i=1,j=2
. We obtain

(3.5)

x_{1}^{2}(y_{1}^{3}+y_{2}^{3})=-(x_{3}^{2}y_{3}^{3}+x_{4}^{2}y_{4}^{3}).

If both sides of (
3.5
) are zero, then
y_{1}=-y_{2}
, since we are assuming
x_{1}\neq 0
. Hence there are
O(XY)
choices for
x_{1},y_{1},y_{2}
. On the right hand side, since
y_{3},y_{4}
are square-free, it follows that
x_{3}=x_{4}
and
y_{3}=-y_{4}
, hence there are
O(XY)
choices for
x_{3},x_{4},y_{3},y_{4}
. This gives a total of
O(X^{2}Y^{2})
solutions. If both sides of the equation are nonzero, then there are
O(X^{2}Y^{2})
choices for
x_{3},x_{4},y_{3},y_{4}
, and for any such choice, there are
O(X^{\epsilon}Y^{\epsilon})
choices for
x_{1},y_{1},y_{2}
by the trivial estimate for the divisor function. Overall, in the case
x_{i}=\pm x_{j}
, we conclude that there are
O(X^{2+\epsilon}Y^{2+\epsilon})
solutions, which is satisfactory for establishing (
3.4
). From now on we assume
x_{i}\neq\pm x_{j}
for all
i\neq j
.

Returning to the integral representation of
N(X,Y)
from (
3.3
), we can apply the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality in two different ways to
|S_{k}(\alpha)|^{2}
. This gives the inequalities

\displaystyle|S_{k}(\alpha)|^{2}\leqslant X\sum_{|x|\leqslant X}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|y_{1}|,|y_{2}|\leqslant Y\\
y_{1},y_{2}\textrm{ square-free }\end{subarray}}e(\alpha x^{2}(y_{1}^{3}-y_{2}^{3})),

\displaystyle|S_{k}(\alpha)|^{2}\leqslant Y\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|y|\leqslant Y\\
y\textrm{ square-free }\end{subarray}}\sum_{|x_{1}|,|x_{2}|\leqslant X}e(\alpha y^{3}(x_{1}^{2}-x_{2}^{2})).

Applying these inequalities once each to
|S_{k}(\alpha)|^{4}
, we obtain

(3.6)

N(X,Y)\leqslant XYL^{\prime}(X,Y),

where

\displaystyle L^{\prime}(X,Y)

\displaystyle=\#\left\{\begin{tabular}[]{l}$(x,x_{1},x_{2},y,y_{1},y_{2})\in(\mathbb{Z}_{\neq 0})^{6}:$\\
$|x|,|x_{1}|,|x_{2}|\leqslant X,$\\
$|y|,|y_{1}|,|y_{2}|\leqslant Y\textrm{ and }y,y_{1},y_{2}\textrm{ square-free, }$\\
$x^{2}(y_{1}^{3}-y_{2}^{3})=y^{3}(x_{1}^{2}-x_{2}^{2}).$\end{tabular}\right\}.

It will be convenient to work with the quantity
L(X,Y)
defined to be
L^{\prime}(X,Y)
but with the additional assumption
U/2<|x|\leqslant U
, which we denote by
|x|\sim U
. We will then perform a sum over dyadic intervals for
U\leqslant 2X
at the end of the argument. We also need to extract from
L(X,Y)
the greatest common divisor
k=\gcd(x,y)
. Note that since
y
is square-free, so is
k
. Hence

L(X,Y)=\sum_{k\leqslant\min(U,Y)}\mu^{2}(k)L_{k}(X,Y),

where

\displaystyle L_{k}(X,Y)

\displaystyle=\#\left\{\begin{tabular}[]{l}$(x,x_{1},x_{2},y,y_{1},y_{2})\in(\mathbb{Z}_{\neq 0})^{6}:$\\
$|x|\sim U/k,|x_{1}|,|x_{2}|\leqslant X,$\\
$|y|\leqslant Y/k,|y_{1}|,|y_{2}|\leqslant Y,\textrm{ and }y,y_{1},y_{2}\textrm{ square-free, }$\\
$(x,y)=1,$\\
$x^{2}(y_{1}^{3}-y_{2}^{3})=ky^{3}(x_{1}^{2}-x_{2}^{2}).$\end{tabular}\right\}.

We now obtain two different estimates for
L_{k}(X,Y)
, depending on the size of
k
. Our first estimate is suitable for large values of
k
.

We have
ky^{3}(x_{1}^{2}-x_{2}^{2})\neq 0
, since
y\neq 0
, and the cases
x_{1}=\pm x_{2}
have already been dealt with above. Consequently, for any fixed
x,y_{1},y_{2},k
, there are
O((XY)^{\epsilon})
choices for
y,x_{1},x_{2}
such that
x^{2}(y_{1}^{3}-y_{2}^{3})=ky^{3}(x_{1}^{2}-x_{2}^{2})
, by the trivial estimate for the divisor function. Therefore

(3.7)

L_{k}(X,Y)\ll(XY)^{\epsilon}A(k),

where

A(k)=\#\{|x|\sim U/k,|y_{1}|,|y_{2}|\leqslant Y:k|x^{2}(y_{1}^{3}-y_{2}^{3})\}.

Now
k|x^{2}(y_{1}^{3}-y_{2}^{3})
implies that there exists integers
d,r
with
dr=k
, such that
d|x^{2}
and
r|(y_{1}^{3}-y_{2}^{3})
. Observe that since
k
is square-free,
d|x^{2}
if and only if
d|x
. Defining

\displaystyle N(r)

\displaystyle=\#\{y_{1},y_{2}\leqslant Y:r|(y_{1}^{3}-y_{2}^{3})\},

we therefore have

(3.8)

\displaystyle A(k)

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d,r\\
dr=k\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|x|\sim U/k\\
d|x\end{subarray}}N(r)\ll U\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d,r\\
dr=k\end{subarray}}\frac{N(r)}{dk}.

Note that
r\leqslant k\leqslant Y
, so
N(r)\ll Y^{2}\varrho(r)/r^{2}
, where we have defined

\varrho(r)=\#\{\eta_{1},\eta_{2}\ (\mathrm{mod}\ r):\eta_{1}^{3}\equiv\eta_{2}^{3}\}.

It is easy to see that
\varrho(r)\ll r^{1+\epsilon}
. Indeed, for a prime
p
and a fixed
\eta_{1}\ (\mathrm{mod}\ p)
, there are at most
3
choices for
\eta_{2}\ (\mathrm{mod}\ p)
such that
\eta_{1}^{3}\equiv\eta_{2}^{3}\ (\mathrm{mod}\ p)
, and so
\varrho(p)\leqslant 3p
. Since
r
is square-free and
\varrho
is multiplicative, it follows from the Chinese Remainder theorem that

\varrho(r)=\prod_{p|r}\varrho(p)\leqslant\prod_{p|r}3p\ll r^{1+\epsilon}.

Applying this bound on
\varrho(r)
, we conclude that
N(r)\ll Y^{2}/r^{1-\epsilon}\ll Y^{2+\epsilon}/r
, and hence from (
3.7
) and (
3.8
) we obtain

(3.9)

\displaystyle L_{k}(X,Y)

\displaystyle\ll\frac{(XY)^{\epsilon}U}{k}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d,r\\
dr=k\end{subarray}}\frac{Y^{2+\epsilon}}{dr}\ll\frac{X^{\epsilon}Y^{2+\epsilon}U}{k^{2}}.

We now find a different estimate for when
k
is small. If
(x,y)=1
and
x^{2}(y_{1}^{3}-y_{2}^{3})=ky^{3}(x_{1}^{2}-x_{2}^{2})
, then
x^{2}|k(x_{1}^{2}-x_{2}^{2})
, so we may define the integer
u=k(x_{1}^{2}-x_{2}^{2})/x^{2}
. Note
u\neq 0
by the assumption
x_{1}\neq\pm x_{2}
. We have
u\ll kX^{2}/(U/k)^{2}=k^{3}X^{2}/U^{2}
. Therefore

(3.10)

L_{k}(X,Y)\ll\sum_{u\ll\frac{k^{3}X^{2}}{U^{2}}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|x|\sim U/k\\
|x_{1}|,|x_{2}|\leqslant X\\
k(x_{1}^{2}-x_{2}^{2})=ux^{2}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|y|\leqslant Y/k\\
|y_{1}|,|y_{2}|\leqslant Y\\
uy^{3}=y_{1}^{3}-y_{2}^{3}\end{subarray}}1.

For a fixed
n=uy^{3}
, there are
O(Y^{\epsilon})
solutions
y_{1},y_{2}
to
y_{1}^{3}-y_{2}^{3}=n
, using the trivial estimate for the divisor function, and so the inner sum of (
3.10
) is
O(Y^{1+\epsilon}/k)
. Similarly, for a given
|x|\sim U/k
, there are
O(X^{\epsilon})
choices for
x_{1},x_{2}
in the middle sum. Hence

L_{k}(X,Y)\ll(XY)^{\epsilon}\sum_{u\ll\frac{k^{3}X^{2}}{U^{2}}}\frac{UY}{k^{2}}\ll\frac{kX^{2+\epsilon}Y^{1+\epsilon}}{U}.

Combining this with the estimate in (
3.9
), we obtain

\displaystyle L_{k}(X,Y)

\displaystyle\ll X^{\epsilon}Y^{1+\epsilon}\min\left(\frac{UY}{k^{2}},\frac{kX^{2}}{U}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant X^{\epsilon}Y^{1+\epsilon}\left(\frac{UY}{k^{2}}\right)^{2/3}\left(\frac{kX^{2}}{U}\right)^{1/3}

\displaystyle=\frac{X^{2/3+\epsilon}Y^{5/3+\epsilon}U^{1/3}}{k}.

Finally, we take a sum over
k\leqslant Y
and dyadic intervals
U\leqslant 2X
to obtain

\displaystyle L(X,Y)

\displaystyle\ll X^{2/3+\epsilon}Y^{5/3+\epsilon}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}U\,\rm{dyadic}\\
U\leqslant 2X\end{subarray}}\sum_{k\leqslant Y}\frac{U^{1/3}}{k}

\displaystyle\ll X^{1+\epsilon}Y^{5/3+\epsilon}.

Recalling (
3.6
), we have established (
3.4
), which from (
3.2
) gives the required bound for
N(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})
.
∎

4.
The circle method

In this section, we will use the circle method from
[
15
]
to count zeros of diagonal quadratic forms in four variables, the main goal being the proof of Theorem
1.3
. Before proceeding with the proof, we collect together some of the notation which we will use throughout this section. Much of the notation depends on a vector
\mathbf{a}=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{4})\in(\mathbb{Z}_{\neq 0})^{4}
, which remains fixed throughout this section.

•

F_{\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{x})
denotes the quadratic form
\sum_{i=1}^{4}a_{i}x_{i}^{2}.

•

\mathbf{P}=(P_{1},\ldots,P_{4})
, where
P_{i}=\sqrt{\frac{B}{|a_{i}|}}
for
i\in\{1,\ldots,4\}
.

•

N_{\mathbf{a}}(B)=\#\left\{\mathbf{x}\in(\mathbb{Z}_{\neq 0})^{4}:F_{\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{x})=0,|x_{i}|\leqslant P_{i}\right\}
, as defined in (
1.6
).

•

A=a_{1}\cdots a_{4}
.

•

\Delta=\Delta(\mathbf{a})=\prod_{i=1}^{4}\gcd\left(a_{i},\prod_{j\neq i}a_{j}\right)
.

•

\boldsymbol{\epsilon}=(\epsilon_{1},\ldots,\epsilon_{4})\in\{\pm 1\}^{4}
, where
\epsilon_{i}=\operatorname{sgn}(a_{i})=a_{i}/|a_{i}|
is the sign of
a_{i}
.

•

G(\mathbf{x})
is the quadratic form
\sum_{i=1}^{4}\epsilon_{i}x_{i}^{2}=F_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}(\mathbf{x})
.

•

\eta\in(0,1/4)
is a small real parameter depending only on
\mathbf{a}
and
B
.

•

w:\mathbb{R}^{4}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0}
is an infinitely differentiable smooth weight function, which has compact support and satisfies
w(\mathbf{x})=0
for all
|\mathbf{x}|\leqslant\eta
.

•

w_{1},w_{2}
are particular choices of such a smooth weight function, defined explicitly in (
4.7
).

•

Q=B^{1/2}
, and for a positive integer
q
, we write
r=q/Q
.

•

\mathbf{c}
denotes a vector in
\mathbb{Z}^{4}
, and we define
\mathbf{v}=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{4})
by
v_{i}=q^{-1}P_{i}c_{i}
.

•

C_{i}=\eta^{-1}B^{\epsilon}|a_{i}|^{1/2}
for
i\in\{1,\ldots,4\}.

Definition 4.1
.

The
singular integral

\sigma_{\infty}(\boldsymbol{\epsilon})
and the
singular series

\mathfrak{G}_{\mathbf{a}}
associated to
F_{\mathbf{a}}
are given respectively by the equations

(4.1)

\displaystyle\sigma_{\infty}(\boldsymbol{\epsilon})

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{[-1,1]^{4}}e(-\theta G(\mathbf{x}))\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\,\mathrm{d}\theta,

(4.2)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{G}_{\mathbf{a}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{q=1}^{\infty}q^{-4}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\bmod{q}\\
\gcd(k,q)=1\end{subarray}}\sum_{\mathbf{b}\bmod{q}}e_{q}(kF_{\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{b})).

For convenience we record again here the statement of Theorem
1.3
.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
\mathbf{a}\in(\mathbb{Z}_{\neq 0})^{4}
be such that
A\neq\square
and
|A|\leqslant B^{4/7}
. Then

(4.3)

N_{\mathbf{a}}(B)=\frac{\mathfrak{G}_{\mathbf{a}}\sigma_{\infty}(\boldsymbol{\epsilon})B}{|A|^{1/2}}+O\left(\frac{B^{41/42+\epsilon}\Delta^{1/3}}{|A|^{11/24}}\right).

The circle method from
[
15
]
uses of smooth weight functions
w:\mathbb{R}^{4}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0}
, which we will take to approximate the characteristic function on
[-1,1]^{4}
. We introduce a smoothly weighted version of
N_{\mathbf{a}}(B)
given by

(4.4)

N_{w,\mathbf{a}}(B)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{x}\in(\mathbb{Z}_{\neq 0})^{4}\\
F_{\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{x})=0\end{subarray}}w(P_{1}^{-1}x_{1},\ldots,P_{4}^{-1}x_{4}).

We also introduce a
weighted singular integral

(4.5)

\sigma_{\infty}(w)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}}w(\mathbf{x})e(-\theta G(\mathbf{x}))\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\,\mathrm{d}\theta.\\

We will need to assume that
w
is infinitely differentiable, has compact support, and vanishes in a neighborhood of the origin (
w(\mathbf{x})=0\textrm{ for }|\mathbf{x}|\leqslant\eta
). Whilst the arguments in this section can be applied for any such choice of
w
, with implied constants depending on
w
, in order to get the power saving stated in Theorem
1.1
, we will need to keep track of the dependence on
w
in our estimates. Therefore, for a given
\eta>0
, we fix a particular choice of smooth weight functions
w_{1},w_{2}:\mathbb{R}^{4}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0}
. We recall the
standard bump function

\psi:\mathbb{R}^{4}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0}
is defined by

(4.6)

\psi(\mathbf{x})=\begin{cases}\exp(-(1-|\mathbf{x}|^{2})^{-1}),&\textrm{ if }|\mathbf{x}|<1,\\
0,&\textrm{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

We then define
w_{1},w_{2}
to be

(4.7)

\begin{split}w_{1}(\mathbf{x})&=\begin{cases}0,&\mbox{if }|\mathbf{x}|\leqslant\eta,\\
e\psi\left(\frac{|\mathbf{x}|}{\eta}-2\right),&\mbox{if }\eta<|\mathbf{x}|\leqslant 2\eta,\\
1,&\mbox{if }2\eta<|\mathbf{x}|\leqslant 1-\eta,\\
e\psi\left(\frac{|\mathbf{x}|}{\eta}-\frac{1-\eta}{\eta}\right),&\mbox{if }1-\eta<|\mathbf{x}|\leqslant 1,\\
0,&\mbox{if }|\mathbf{x}|>1,\end{cases}\\
\\
w_{2}(\mathbf{x})&=\begin{cases}0,&\mbox{if }|\mathbf{x}|\leqslant\eta,\\
e\psi\left(\frac{|\mathbf{x}|}{\eta}-2\right),&\mbox{if }\eta<|\mathbf{x}|\leqslant 2\eta,\\
1,&\mbox{if }2\eta<|\mathbf{x}|\leqslant 1,\\
e\psi\left(\frac{|\mathbf{x}|}{\eta}-\frac{1}{\eta}\right),&\mbox{if }1<|\mathbf{x}|\leqslant 1+\eta,\\
0,&\mbox{if }|\mathbf{x}|>1+\eta.\end{cases}\end{split}

For any integers
j_{1},\ldots,j_{4}\geqslant 0
with
j_{1}+\cdots+j_{4}\leqslant N
, and for any
i\in\{1,2\}
, we have

(4.8)

\frac{\partial^{j_{1}+\cdots+j_{4}}}{\partial x_{1}^{j_{1}}\cdots\partial x_{4}^{j_{4}}}w_{i}(\mathbf{x})\ll_{N}\eta^{-N}.

We now state a smoothly weighted version of Theorem
4.2
.

Theorem 4.3
.

Suppose
\eta\in(0,1/4)
and
\mathbf{a}\in(\mathbb{Z}_{\neq 0})^{4}
. Let
w
be one of the weights
w_{1}
or
w_{2}
from (
4.7
). Suppose that
A\neq\square
. Then

(4.9)

\begin{split}N_{w,\mathbf{a}}(B)&=\frac{\mathfrak{G}_{\mathbf{a}}\sigma_{\infty}(w)B}{|A|^{1/2}}+O\left(\frac{\eta^{-6}B^{5/6+\epsilon}\Delta^{1/3}}{|A|^{5/24}}\right)+O(\eta^{-7}B^{1/2+\epsilon}).\end{split}

We explain how Theorem
4.2
can be deduced from Theorem
4.3
by applying the methods from
[
3
, Section 5.3]
and
[
5
, Section 2.4]
, together with the upper bound
\mathfrak{G}_{\mathbf{a}}\ll|A|^{\epsilon}\Delta^{1/4}
for the singular series, which we prove in Lemma
4.15
at the end of this section.

Let
1_{[-1,1]^{4}}
denote the characteristic function on
[-1,1]^{4}
, and for an integer
j\geqslant 0
, define
w_{2}^{(j)}(\mathbf{x})=w_{2}((2\eta)^{-j}\mathbf{x})
. Then for any
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{4}
, we have

w_{1}(\mathbf{x})\leqslant 1_{[-1,1]^{4}}(\mathbf{x})\leqslant\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}w_{2}^{(j)}(\mathbf{x}),

and consequently,

N_{w_{1},\mathbf{a}}(B)\leqslant N_{\mathbf{a}}(B)\leqslant\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}N_{w_{2}^{(j)},\mathbf{a}}(B)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}N_{w_{2},\mathbf{a}}((2\eta)^{j}B).

The assumption
|A|\leqslant B^{4/7}
in the statement of Theorem
4.2
implies that the error term
\eta^{-7}B^{1/2+\epsilon}
is dominated by the other error term in (
4.9
), provided that
\eta\gg B^{-5/14+\epsilon}
. For any
\eta\in(0,1/4)
satisfying this condition, it follows from Theorem
4.3
that

\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}N_{w_{2},\mathbf{a}}((2\eta)^{j}B)=\frac{(1+O(\eta))\mathfrak{G}_{\mathbf{a}}\sigma_{\infty}(w_{2})B}{|A|^{1/2}}+O\left(\frac{\eta^{-6}B^{5/6+\epsilon}\Delta^{1/3}}{|A|^{5/24}}\right).

As explained in
[
5
, Lemma 2.9]
, for
i\in\{1,2\}
, we have

|\sigma_{\infty}(w_{i})-\sigma_{\infty}(\boldsymbol{\epsilon})|\ll\eta\sigma_{\infty}(\boldsymbol{\epsilon})\ll\eta,

from which we deduce that

N_{\mathbf{a}}(B)=\frac{(1+O(\eta))\mathfrak{G}_{\mathbf{a}}\sigma_{\infty}(\boldsymbol{\epsilon})B}{|A|^{1/2}}+O\left(\frac{\eta^{-6}B^{5/6+\epsilon}\Delta^{1/3}}{|A|^{5/24}}\right).

We choose

\eta=\frac{1}{5}B^{-1/42}|A|^{1/24}.

Clearly
\eta\gg B^{-5/14+\epsilon}
. Moreover,
using the assumption
|A|\leqslant B^{4/7}
, we have that
\eta\in(0,1/4)
, as was required in order to apply Theorem
4.3
. Theorem
4.2
now follows from the estimate
\mathfrak{G}_{\mathbf{a}}\ll|A|^{\epsilon}\Delta^{1/4}
found in Lemma
4.15
.

We commence with the proof of Theorem
4.3
. It will be convenient to make the assumptions

(4.10)

\eta^{-1},|\mathbf{a}|\ll B^{R}

for some fixed
R\geqslant 0
, so that quantities bounded by an arbitrarily small power of
\eta^{-1}
or
|A|
are also
\ll B^{\epsilon}
for any
\epsilon>0
. If these assumptions do not hold, then the statement of Theorem
4.3
is trivial. Employing the machinery from
[
15
, Theorem 2]
, we have

(4.11)

N_{w,\mathbf{a}}(B)=\frac{C_{Q}}{Q^{2}}\sum_{\mathbf{c}\in\mathbb{Z}^{4}}\sum_{q=1}^{\infty}q^{-4}S_{q,\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{c})I_{q,\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{c}),

where
C_{Q}=1+O_{N}(Q^{-N})
, and
Q=B^{1/2}
. Here
I_{q,\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{c})
and
S_{q,\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{c})
are exponential sums and integrals defined analogously to
[
15
]
, via

(4.12)

\begin{split}S_{q,\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{c})&=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\bmod q\\
\gcd(k,q)=1\end{subarray}}\sum_{\mathbf{b}\bmod q}e_{q}(kF_{\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{b})+\mathbf{b}\cdot\mathbf{c}),\\
I_{q,\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{c})&=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}}w(P_{1}^{-1}x_{1},\ldots,P_{4}^{-1}x_{4})h\left(\frac{q}{Q},\frac{F_{\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{x})}{Q^{2}}\right)e_{q}(-\mathbf{c}\cdot\mathbf{x})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}.\end{split}

The smooth function
h:(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is as defined in
[
15
, Section 3]
and is given by

(4.13)

h(x,y)=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}(xj)^{-1}\left(u(xj)-u(|y|/xj)\right),

where
u=c\psi(4x-3)
is a linear transformation of the standard bump function (the one-dimensional analogue of (
4.6
)), scaled by a constant
c
so that its integral over
\mathbb{R}
is equal to 1. As observed in
[
15
, Section 3]
, it is straightforward to check from (
4.13
) that
h(x,y)\ll x^{-1}
and
h(x,y)=0
whenever
x>\max(1,2|y|)
.

We note that by changing variables
P_{i}^{-1}x_{i}
into
x_{i}
, we can rewrite
I_{q,\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{c})
as

\displaystyle I_{q,\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{c})

\displaystyle=P_{1}\cdots P_{4}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}}w(\mathbf{x})h\left(\frac{q}{Q},\frac{F_{\mathbf{a}}(P_{1}x_{1},\ldots,P_{4}x_{4})}{Q^{2}}\right)e(-\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}

(4.14)

\displaystyle=P_{1}\cdots P_{4}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}}w(\mathbf{x})h(r,G(\mathbf{x}))e(-\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}.

4.1.
The main term

The main term for
N_{w,\mathbf{a}}(B)
comes from the case
\mathbf{c}=\mathbf{0}
in (
4.11
). We define

(4.15)

M(B)=\frac{1}{Q^{2}}\sum_{q=1}^{\infty}q^{-4}S_{q,\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{0})I_{q,\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{0}).

We have

(4.16)

\mathfrak{G}_{\mathbf{a}}=\sum_{q=1}^{\infty}q^{-4}S_{q,\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{0}),

and from (
4.14
),

I_{q,\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{0})=P_{1}\cdots P_{4}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}}w(\mathbf{x})h\left(r,G(\mathbf{x})\right)\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}.

We let
w_{0}
denote a smooth weight function supported on
[-17,17]
and taking the value 1 in
[-16,16]
. Since
\operatorname{supp}(w)\subseteq[-1-\eta,1+\eta]\subset[-2,2]
, we have
w_{0}(G(\mathbf{x}))=1
whenever
\mathbf{x}\in\operatorname{supp}(w)
. We obtain

I_{q,\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{0})=P_{1}\cdots P_{4}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}}w(\mathbf{x})w_{0}(G(\mathbf{x}))h(r,G(\mathbf{x}))\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}.

The arguments at the beginning of
[
4
, Section 4.3]
can be applied to obtain

(4.17)

I_{q,\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{0})=P_{1}\cdots P_{4}\left(\lim_{\delta\rightarrow 0}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left(\frac{\sin(\pi\delta\theta)}{\pi\delta\theta}\right)^{2}J(w;\theta)L(\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\theta\right),

where

(4.18)

\displaystyle J(w;\theta)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}}w(\mathbf{x})e(-\theta G(\mathbf{x}))\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x},

(4.19)

\displaystyle L(\theta)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}w_{0}(t)h(r,t)e(\theta t)\,\mathrm{d}t.

We would like to compare the bracketed expression in (
4.17
) with the weighted singular integral
\sigma_{\infty}(w)
from (
4.5
), which by definition equals
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}J(w;\theta)\mathrm{d}\theta
. We apply
[
4
, Lemma 4.11]
to estimate
L(\theta)
. In addition to
N
, the implied constants in
[
4
, Lemma 4.11]
depend only on
\mathrm{supp}(w_{0})
. However, we note that we have chosen
w_{0}
independently of
w
, and hence independently of
\eta
. Therefore for any
q\leqslant Q
and
N\geqslant 1
, we have

(4.20)

L(\theta)=1+O_{N}(\{1+|\theta|^{N}\}r^{N}).

Our next task is to bound
J(w;\theta)
. In order to achieve this, we need to work with more general smooth weight functions
\widetilde{w}:\mathbb{R}^{4}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0}
which belong to a class
S
defined by the following properties.

Definition 4.4
.

We say
\widetilde{w}\in S
if

(1)

\widetilde{w}
is smooth,

(2)

\widetilde{w}
is supported on
[-2,2]^{4}
,

(3)

\widetilde{w}(\mathbf{x})=0
for
|\mathbf{x}|\leqslant\eta,

(4)

|\widetilde{w}(\mathbf{x})|\ll 1
for all
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{4}
,

(5)

The derivatives of
\widetilde{w}(\mathbf{x})
are bounded as in (
4.8
).

Since
w
is equal to
w_{1}
or
w_{2}
, we note in particular that
w\in S
.

Lemma 4.5
.

For any
\widetilde{w}\in S
and any
M\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}
, we have
J(\widetilde{w};\theta)\ll_{M}|\eta^{2}\theta|^{-M}.

Proof.

The case
M=0
is trivial since the integrand in the definition of
J(\widetilde{w};\theta)
is bounded and has compact support. For
M\geqslant 1
, we follow a similar argument to
[
15
, Lemma 10]
, by applying integration by parts and induction on
M
. We have

J(\widetilde{w};\theta)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}}\widetilde{w}(\mathbf{x})e(-\theta G(\mathbf{x}))\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\ll\max_{1\leqslant j\leqslant 4}\left|\int_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{4},\\
|x_{j}|=\max_{i}|x_{i}|\end{subarray}}\widetilde{w}(\mathbf{x})e(-\theta G(\mathbf{x}))\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\right|.

Without loss of generality, we may assume that
j=1
. We note that

e(-\theta G(\mathbf{x}))=\frac{1}{-4\pi i\epsilon_{1}\theta x_{1}}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{1}}e(-\theta G(\mathbf{x})).

We perform integration by parts with respect to
x_{1}
to obtain

\displaystyle J(\widetilde{w};\theta)\ll\left|\int_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{x}\in\operatorname{supp}{w}\\
|x_{1}|=\max_{i}|x_{i}|\end{subarray}}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{1}}\left(\frac{\widetilde{w}(\mathbf{x})}{4\pi i\theta x_{1}}\right)e(-\theta G(\mathbf{x}))\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\right|.

Since
|x_{1}|=\max_{i}|x_{i}|
, we have
|x_{1}|^{-1}=|\mathbf{x}|^{-1}\leqslant\eta^{-1}
for any
\mathbf{x}\in\operatorname{supp}{w}
. Therefore by the assumptions on the size of
\widetilde{w}
and its derivatives, we see that

\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{1}}\left(\frac{\widetilde{w}(\mathbf{x})}{4\pi i\theta x_{1}}\right)=\widetilde{w}^{(1)}(4\pi i\theta\eta^{2})^{-1}

for some
\widetilde{w}^{(1)}\in S
. Hence

J(\widetilde{w};\theta)\ll|\eta^{2}\theta|^{-1}|J(\widetilde{w}^{(1)};\theta)|.

Proceeding by induction, we obtain
J(\widetilde{w}(\mathbf{x});\theta)\ll_{M}|\eta^{2}\theta|^{-M}|J(\widetilde{w}^{(M)};\theta)|
for some
\widetilde{w}^{(M)}\in S
, from which we deduce the required result by applying the trivial bound
J(\widetilde{w}^{(M)};\theta)\ll 1
.
∎

From Lemma
4.5
, for any integer
M\geqslant 1
, we have

J(w;\theta)\ll_{M}(1+|\eta^{2}\theta|)^{-M}\leqslant\eta^{-2M}(1+|\theta|)^{-M}.

Combining this with (
4.17
), (
4.20
) and the fact that

\lim_{\delta\rightarrow 0}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left(\frac{\sin(\pi\delta\theta)}{\pi\delta\theta}\right)^{2}J(\theta;w)\operatorname{d}\theta=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}J(\theta;w)\operatorname{d}\theta=\sigma_{\infty}(w),

we obtain

|(P_{1}\cdots P_{4})^{-1}I_{q,\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{0})-\sigma_{\infty}(w)|\ll_{M,N}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{r^{N}\{1+|\theta|\}^{N}}{\eta^{2M}\{1+|\theta|\}^{M}}\,\mathrm{d}\theta.

In order to ensure that the integral converges, we make the choice
M=N+2
, and we conclude that

(4.21)

I_{q,\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{0})=P_{1}\cdots P_{4}\{\sigma_{\infty}(w)+O_{N}(\eta^{-2N-4}r^{N})\},

for any integer
N\geqslant 1
.

Let
R=B^{1/2-\epsilon}\eta^{2}
. We note that if
q\leqslant R
, then the error term in (
4.21
) can be made smaller than any negative power of
B
by appropriate choice of
N
(depending on
\epsilon
), due to the assumption in (
4.10
).

We now split the main term up. We have

(4.22)

\begin{split}M(B)=\frac{P_{1}\cdots P_{4}\sigma_{\infty}(w)}{B}\sum_{q\leqslant R}q^{-4}S_{q,\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{0})+O(T(R)+1),\end{split}

where

(4.23)

T(R)=\frac{1}{Q^{2}}\sum_{q>R}q^{-4}S_{q,\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{0})I_{q,\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{0}).

We will estimate
T(R)
using partial summation. The following lemma is similar to
[
4
, Lemma 4.3]
, and gives bounds for
I_{q,\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{c})
and its derivative. Only the case
\mathbf{c}=\mathbf{0}
is needed in the study of the main term, but the case
\mathbf{c}\neq\mathbf{0}
will be useful later.

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
\mathbf{c}\in\mathbb{Z}^{4}
and
k\in\{0,1\}
. Then

(1)

If
k=0
or
\mathbf{c}=\mathbf{0}
then
q^{k}\frac{\partial^{k}I_{q,\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{c})}{\partial q^{k}}\ll P_{1}\cdots P_{4}
,

(2)

If
k=1
and
\mathbf{c}\neq\mathbf{0}
then
q^{k}\frac{\partial^{k}I_{q,\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{c})}{\partial q^{k}}\ll\eta^{-1}P_{1}\cdots P_{4}
.

Proof.

Suppose that
\mathbf{c}=\mathbf{0}
. Recalling (
4.14
) and the notation
r=q/Q
, it is clear that

(4.24)

q^{k}\frac{\partial^{k}I_{q,\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{0})}{\partial q^{k}}=r^{k}P_{1}\cdots P_{4}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}}w(\mathbf{x})\frac{\partial^{k}h(r,G(\mathbf{x}))}{\partial r^{k}}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}.

From
[
15
, Lemma 5]
with
m=k,n=0
and
N=2
, we have

\frac{\partial^{k}h(r,G(\mathbf{x}))}{\partial r^{k}}\ll r^{-1-k}\left(r^{2}+\min\left\{1,\frac{r^{2}}{|G(\mathbf{x})|^{2}}\right\}\right),

and so

(4.25)

(P_{1}\cdots P_{4})^{-1}\frac{\partial^{k}I_{q,\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{c})}{\partial q^{k}}\ll r+r^{-1}\int_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{x}\in\operatorname{supp}w\\
|G(\mathbf{x})|\leqslant r\end{subarray}}1\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}+r\int_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{x}\in\operatorname{supp}w\\
|G(\mathbf{x})|>r\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{|G(\mathbf{x})|^{2}}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}.

We need to show that the right hand side of (
4.25
) is
O(1)
. We can assume that the first term
r
is
O(1)
, since
h(r,G(\mathbf{x}))=0
when
r>\max(1,|G(\mathbf{x})|)
, and
G(\mathbf{x})\ll 1
for
x\in\operatorname{supp}w
, and so
I_{q,\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{0})=0
unless
r\ll 1
. In order to estimate the integrals in (
4.25
), we consider for
z>0
the Lebesgue measure
m(z)
of the set

S(z)=\{\mathbf{x}\in\operatorname{supp}w:|G(\mathbf{x})|\leqslant z\}.

We can find distinct indices
i,j
such that
\epsilon_{i}=\epsilon_{j}
. Without loss of generality we may assume
i=1,j=2
, and in addition that
\epsilon_{1}=\epsilon_{2}=1
. For a fixed choice of
x_{3},x_{4}
, we define
c=\epsilon_{3}x_{3}^{2}+\epsilon_{4}x_{4}^{2}
, so that if
\mathbf{x}\in S(z)
then
x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}\in[-c-z,-c+z]
. The measure of the set of pairs
x_{1},x_{2}
satisfying this condition is
O(z)
. For
\mathbf{x}\in\operatorname{supp}w
, we require
|x_{3}|,|x_{4}|\ll 1
, and hence
m(z)=O(z)
. From this we see that the first integral in (
4.25
) is
O(r)
, and by breaking into dyadic intervals, the second integral is
O(r^{-1})
. Therefore the right hand side of (
4.25
) is
O(1)
, as required.

When
\mathbf{c}\neq\mathbf{0}
, we first note that for
k=0
, we can reduce to the case above by applying the trivial estimate to the extra exponential factor
e(-\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{x})
appearing in the integral defining
I_{q,\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{c})
. It remains only to deal with the case
\mathbf{c}\neq\mathbf{0},k=1
. Define the vector
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{4}
by
v_{i}=q^{-1}P_{i}c_{i}
. Similarly to (
4.24
), we obtain

\displaystyle(P_{1}\cdots P_{4})^{-1}q\frac{\partial I_{q,\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{c})}{\partial q}\ll r\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}}w(\mathbf{x})\frac{\partial\{h(r,G(\mathbf{x}))e(-\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{x})\}}{\partial r}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle=r\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}}w(\mathbf{x})\left(\frac{\partial h(r,G(\mathbf{x}))}{\partial r}e(-\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{x})+h(r,G(\mathbf{x}))\frac{-2\pi i\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{x}}{r}e(-\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{x})\right)\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}.

The first term can again be dealt with using the trivial estimate for
e(-\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{x})
and the argument for the case
\mathbf{c}=\mathbf{0}
given above. In order to estimate the second term, as in
[
15
, Lemma 14]
, we apply the divergence theorem

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}}\nabla\cdot(w(\mathbf{x})h(r,G(\mathbf{x})e(-\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{x})\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}=0

in order to remove the additional factor of
2\pi i\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{x}
. This yields

(4.26)

\begin{split}&\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}}w(\mathbf{x})h(r,G(\mathbf{x}))e(-\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{x})(2\pi i\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}}\widetilde{w}(\mathbf{x})h(r,G(\mathbf{x}))e(-\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\\
+&\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}}4w(\mathbf{x})h(r,G(\mathbf{x}))e(-\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}}2w(\mathbf{x})G(\mathbf{x})e(-\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{x})\frac{\partial h(r,G(\mathbf{x}))}{\partial G(\mathbf{x})}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x},\end{split}

where
\widetilde{w}(\mathbf{x})=(\mathbf{x}\cdot\nabla)w(\mathbf{x})
. The second integral on the right hand side of (
4.26
) can now be treated in the same way as the easier cases
k=0
or
\mathbf{c}=\mathbf{0}
using the trivial estimate for
e(-\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{x})
. The third integral can similarly be estimated by noting that
G(\mathbf{x})\ll 1
for
\mathbf{x}\in\operatorname{supp}(w)
, and applying
[
15
, Lemma 5]
with
n=1,m=0,N=0
. For the first integral, we note that
\widetilde{w}(\mathbf{x})
is uniformly bounded by
\eta^{-1}
. Hence the above arguments can be applied to the first integral as well, but with an extra factor of
\eta^{-1}
.
∎

We define

(4.27)

\Sigma(x;\mathbf{c})=\sum_{q\leqslant x}q^{-3}S_{q,\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{c}).

Moreover, we let
F^{*}_{\mathbf{a}}
denote the dual form of
F_{\mathbf{a}}
, i.e., the quadratic form given by the equation

F^{*}_{\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{c})=a_{2}a_{3}a_{4}c_{1}^{2}+a_{1}a_{3}a_{4}c_{2}^{2}+a_{1}a_{2}a_{4}c_{3}^{2}+a_{1}a_{2}a_{3}c_{4}^{2}.

We also define

(4.28)

\Delta_{\mathbf{c}}(\mathbf{a})=\prod_{i=1}^{4}\gcd\left(\gcd(a_{i},c_{i}),\prod_{j\neq i}\gcd(a_{j},c_{j})\right).

In particular we have
\Delta_{\mathbf{c}}(\mathbf{a})=\Delta
when
\mathbf{c}=\mathbf{0}
.

The following lemma is a slight modification of
[
4
, Lemma 4.9]
.

Lemma 4.7
.

Suppose
A\neq\square
. Let
\mathbf{c}\in\mathbb{Z}^{4}
be such that
F^{*}_{\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{c})=0
. Then

\Sigma(x;\mathbf{c})\ll A^{3/16+\epsilon}\Delta_{\mathbf{c}}(\mathbf{a})^{3/8}x^{1/2+\epsilon}.

Proof.

We use the fact that
S_{q,\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{c})
is multiplicative in
q
, as proved by Heath-Brown in the discussion following
[
15
, Lemma 28]
. This allows us to write

(4.29)

\Sigma(x;\mathbf{c})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}q_{2}\leqslant x\\
q_{2}|(2A)^{\infty}\end{subarray}}q_{2}^{-3}S_{q_{2},\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{c})\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}q_{1}\leqslant x/q_{2}\\
\gcd(q_{1},2A)=1\end{subarray}}q_{1}^{-3}S_{q_{1},\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{c}),

where the notation
q_{2}|(2A)^{\infty}
means that every prime dividing
q_{2}
also divides
2A
. By
[
4
, Lemma 4.6]
, we have

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}q_{1}\leqslant x/q_{2}\\
\gcd(q_{1},2A)=1\end{subarray}}q_{1}^{-3}S_{q_{1}}(\mathbf{c})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}q_{1}\leqslant x/q_{2}\\
\gcd(q_{1},2A)=1\end{subarray}}\left(\frac{A}{q_{1}}\right)\frac{\varphi(q_{1})}{q_{1}},

where
\varphi
denotes the Euler totient function. Applying the Burgess bound as in the proof of
[
4
, Lemma 4.9]
, we obtain

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}q_{1}\leqslant x/q_{2}\\
\gcd(q_{1},2A)=1\end{subarray}}q_{1}^{-3}S_{q_{1},\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{c})\ll\frac{|A|^{3/16+\epsilon}x^{1/2}}{q_{2}^{1/2}}.

Returning to (
4.29
), we have

(4.30)

\Sigma(x;\mathbf{c})\ll|A|^{3/16+\epsilon}x^{1/2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}q_{2}\leqslant x\\
q_{2}|(2A)^{\infty}\end{subarray}}\frac{|S_{q_{2},\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{c})|}{q_{2}^{7/2}}.

Now
[
4
, Lemma 4.5]
states that
S_{q,\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{c})\ll q^{3}\prod_{i=1}^{4}\gcd(q,a_{i},c_{i})
. Moreover, there are
O(x^{\epsilon}A^{\epsilon})
choices for
q_{2}\leqslant x
with
q_{2}|(2A)^{\infty}
. Therefore

(4.31)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}q_{2}\leqslant x\\
q_{2}|(2A)^{\infty}\end{subarray}}\frac{|S_{q_{2},\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{c})|}{q_{2}^{7/2}}\ll x^{\epsilon}A^{\epsilon}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}q|(2A)^{\infty}\end{subarray}}\left(\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{4}\gcd(q,a_{i},c_{i})^{1/2}}{q^{1/2}}\right).

Let
p
be a prime. We define

L_{p}=\nu_{p}\left(\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{4}\gcd(q,a_{i},c_{i})}{q}\right),\quad k_{p}=\nu_{p}(q).

For an index
i=1,\ldots,4
, we let
m_{i,p}
denote the
i
th smallest element of
\nu_{p}(\gcd(a_{1},c_{1})),\ldots,\nu_{p}(\gcd(a_{4},c_{4}))
.
Then

L_{p}=\sum_{i=1}^{4}\left(\min(k_{p},m_{i,p})-k_{p}\right).

It can be checked that the maximum possible value of
L_{p}
is attained by choosing
k_{p}=m_{3,p}
, and so
L_{p}\leqslant m_{1,p}+m_{2,p}+m_{3,p}
. We have
L_{p}\leqslant 0
unless
p|\Delta_{\mathbf{c}}(\mathbf{a})
. Hence the supremum in (
4.31
) is bounded by

\prod_{p|\Delta_{\mathbf{c}}(\mathbf{a})}p^{(m_{1,p}+m_{2,p}+m_{3,p})/2}.

For any prime
p
, we have

\nu_{p}(\Delta_{\mathbf{c}}(\mathbf{a}))=m_{1,p}+m_{2,p}+m_{3,p}+\min(m_{1,p}+m_{2,p}+m_{3,p},m_{4,p})\geqslant\frac{4}{3}(m_{2,p}+m_{3,p}),

and hence the supremum in (
4.31
) is bounded by
\Delta_{\mathbf{c}}(\mathbf{a})^{1/3}
. Recalling (
4.30
) we obtain the desired estimate for
\Sigma(x;\mathbf{c})
.
∎

We recall the estimate for

4.2.
The error term

We now study the error term coming from the case
\mathbf{c}\neq\mathbf{0}
. We begin with a lemma which is similar to
[
4
, Lemma 4.2 (i)]
.

Lemma 4.9
.

For any nonzero
\mathbf{c}\in\mathbb{Z}^{4}
and any integer
N\geqslant 0
, we have

I_{q,\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{c})\ll_{N}\frac{P_{1}\cdots P_{4}}{r}\left(1+\frac{r}{\eta}\right)^{N}\min_{1\leqslant i\leqslant 4}\left(\frac{|a_{i}|^{1/2}}{|c_{i}|}\right)^{N}.

Proof.

We apply integration by parts
N
times to the integral in (
4.14
), where we differentiate
f(\mathbf{x}):=w(\mathbf{x})h(r,G(\mathbf{x}))
and integrate
g(\mathbf{x}):=e(\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{x})
. Each integral of
g(\mathbf{x})
with respect to
x_{i}
introduces a factor of
(2\pi iq^{-1}P_{i}c_{i})^{-1}
.

We recall from (
4.8
) that for any
i\in\{1,\ldots,4\}
and any
N\geqslant 1
,

\left|\frac{\partial^{N}}{\partial x_{i}^{N}}w(\mathbf{x})\right|\ll_{N}\eta^{-N}.

Using the product rule, we have

\left|\frac{\partial^{N}}{\partial x_{i}^{N}}f(\mathbf{x})\right|\ll_{N}\max_{0\leqslant j\leqslant N}\left|\eta^{j-N}\frac{\partial^{j}}{\partial x_{i}^{j}}h(r,G(\mathbf{x}))\right|.

Clearly

\left|\frac{\partial^{j}}{\partial x_{i}^{j}}G(\mathbf{x})\right|\ll 1

for all
\mathbf{x}\in\operatorname{supp}w
. By
[
4
, Equation (4.10)]
, we have

(4.32)

\frac{\partial^{j}h(r,y)}{\partial y^{j}}\ll_{j}r^{-1-j}.

Hence by the chain rule, we have

(4.33)

\left|\frac{\partial^{N}}{\partial x_{i}^{N}}f(\mathbf{x})\right|\ll_{N}\max_{0\leqslant j\leqslant N}r^{-1-j}\eta^{j-N}=r^{-1}\min(r,\eta)^{-N}\leqslant r^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{r}+\frac{1}{\eta}\right)^{N}.

After performing integration by parts
N
times, we apply (
4.33
) to all derivatives of
f(\mathbf{x})
that appear, and the trivial estimate
|e(-\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{x})|\leqslant 1
, to obtain for any
i\in\{1,\ldots,4\}
,

\displaystyle I_{q,\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{c})

\displaystyle\ll_{N}\frac{P_{1}\cdots P_{4}}{r(q^{-1}|P_{i}c_{i}|)^{N}}\left(\frac{1}{r}+\frac{1}{\eta}\right)^{N}.

Recalling that
P_{i}=(B/|a_{i}|)^{1/2},Q=B^{1/2},r=q/Q
, and choosing the index
i
such that
|P_{i}c_{i}|
, is maximised, the last expression rearranges to give the desired estimate.
∎

Remark 4.10
.

Lemma
4.9
allows us to assume
|c_{i}|\ll\eta^{-1}|a_{i}|^{1/2}B^{\epsilon}=C_{i}
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,4\}
, since outside this range the estimate in Lemma
4.9
can be made smaller than any power of
B
by an appropriate choice of
N
.

The following lemma is a variant of the first derivative test and is based on
[
15
, Lemma 10]
.

Lemma 4.11
.

Let
f(\mathbf{x})=\theta G(\mathbf{x})-\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{x}
, where
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{4}
and
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
are such that
|\mathbf{v}|\geqslant 5|\theta|
. Then for any integer
N\geqslant 0
and any
w
in the class of smooth weight functions
S
from Definition
4.4
, we have

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}}w(\mathbf{x})e(f(\mathbf{x}))\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\ll_{N}(\eta|\mathbf{v}|)^{-N}.

Proof.

The case
N=0
is trivial. We define
f_{j}(\mathbf{x})=\partial f(\mathbf{x})/\partial x_{j}=\pm 2\theta x_{j}-v_{i}
. By the assumption
|\mathbf{v}|\geqslant 5|\theta|
, there is some index
j\in\{1,\ldots,4\}
such that
|f_{j}(\mathbf{x})|\gg|\mathbf{v}|
for any
\mathbf{x}\in[-2,2]^{4}
. Without loss of generality, we may assume
j=1
.

We write

w(\mathbf{x})e(f(\mathbf{x}))=\frac{w(\mathbf{x})}{2\pi if_{1}(\mathbf{x})}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{1}}e(f(\mathbf{x}))

and integrate by parts with respect to
x_{1}
to obtain

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}}w(\mathbf{x})e(f(\mathbf{x}))\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}=-(2\pi i\eta|\mathbf{v}|)^{-1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}}e(f(\mathbf{x}))\widetilde{w}(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x},

where

\widetilde{w}(\mathbf{x})=\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{1}}\left(\frac{\eta|\mathbf{v}|w(\mathbf{x})}{f_{1}(\mathbf{x})}\right)=\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{1}}(\eta w(\mathbf{x}))\frac{|\mathbf{v}|}{f_{1}(\mathbf{x})}+\eta w(\mathbf{x})\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{1}}\left(\frac{|\mathbf{v}|}{f_{1}(\mathbf{x})}\right).

It remains to show that
\widetilde{w}
belongs to the class of smooth weight functions
S
from Definition
4.4
, since the result then follows by induction on
N
. But this is immediate from the observations that for any
\mathbf{x}\in[-2,2]^{4},

\frac{|\mathbf{v}|}{f_{1}(\mathbf{x})}\ll 1,\quad\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{1}}\left(\frac{|\mathbf{v}|}{f_{1}(\mathbf{x})}\right)\ll 1,\quad\textrm{ and }\quad\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{1}}(\eta w)\in S.\qed

We require one further estimate for
I_{q,\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{c})
, which involves the second derivative test.

Lemma 4.12
.

Let
\mathbf{c}\neq\mathbf{0}
. Then

I_{q,\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{c})\ll\frac{\eta^{-4}B^{3/2+\epsilon}q}{|A|^{1/2}}\min_{1\leqslant i\leqslant 4}\left(\frac{|a_{i}|^{1/2}}{|c_{i}|}\right).

Proof.

We would like to apply Fourier inversion to
w(\mathbf{x})h(r,G(\mathbf{x}))e(-\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{x})
, the integrand appearing in the definition of
I_{q,\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{c})
. The function
\mathbf{x}\mapsto h(r,G(\mathbf{x}))
does not have compact support. We define the smooth weight
w_{0}:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0}
by
w_{0}(x)=\psi(x/17)
, where
\psi(x)
is the standard bump function in one variable. Then we define the smooth weight
w_{3}:R^{4}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0}
by

w_{3}(\mathbf{x})=\begin{cases}\frac{w(\mathbf{x})}{w_{0}(G(\mathbf{x}))},&\mbox{if }w_{0}(G(\mathbf{x}))\neq 0,\\
0,&\mbox{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Note that
w
is supported on
[-2,2]^{4}
, and in this region
G(\mathbf{x})
takes values in
[-16,16]
. Therefore
w_{0}(G(\mathbf{x}))\gg 1
for all
\mathbf{x}\in\mathrm{supp}(w)
. We deduce that
\mathrm{supp}(w_{3})=\mathrm{supp}(w)
and
w(\mathbf{x})=w_{3}(\mathbf{x})w_{0}(G(\mathbf{x}))
for all
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{4}
. Moreover, since all the derivatives of
w_{0}(G(\mathbf{x}))
are
O(1)
for any
\mathbf{x}\in\operatorname{supp}(w)
, we have
w_{3}\in S
. Applying Fourier inversion, we obtain

(4.34)

I_{q,\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{c})=P_{1}\cdots P_{4}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}p(\theta)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}}w_{3}(\mathbf{x})e(\theta G(\mathbf{x})-\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{x})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\,\mathrm{d}\theta,

where

p(\theta)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}w_{0}(k)h(r,k)e(-\theta k)\,\mathrm{d}k.

We have the estimate
p(\theta)\ll 1
. Indeed, taking
m=n=0
and
N=2
in
[
15
, lemma 5]
we see that

h(r,k)\ll r^{-1}(r^{2}+\min(1,(r/|k|)^{2}),

and so

\displaystyle p(\theta)\ll r^{-1}\left(\int_{|k|<r}1\mathrm{d}k+\int_{r\leqslant|k|\leqslant 17}\left(\frac{r}{|k|}\right)^{2}\mathrm{d}k\right)\ll r^{-1}(r+r^{2}\cdot r^{-1})\ll 1.

To deal with the inner integral in (
4.34
), which we denote by
I(\theta;\mathbf{v})
, we divide into the cases
5|\theta|\leqslant|\mathbf{v}|
and
5|\theta|\geqslant|\mathbf{v}|
. In the former case, we apply Lemma
4.11
with
N=2
to obtain
I(\theta;\mathbf{v})\ll(\eta|\mathbf{v}|)^{-2}
. The contribution to
I_{q,\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{c})
is

(\eta|\mathbf{v}|)^{-2}P_{1}\cdots P_{4}\int_{5|\theta|\leqslant|\mathbf{v}|}1\mathrm{d}\theta\ll\eta^{-2}|\mathbf{v}|^{-1}P_{1}\cdots P_{4}.

In the case
5|\theta|\geqslant|\mathbf{v}|
, we use the arguments from
[
16
, Lemma 3.2]
to obtain

(4.35)

I(\theta;\mathbf{v})\ll\left\{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}}|\widehat{w_{3}}(\mathbf{y})|\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}\right\}\sup_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{4}}\left|\prod_{i=1}^{4}\int_{[-2,2]}e(\theta\epsilon_{i}x_{i}^{2}+x_{i}(y_{i}\pm v_{i}))\mathrm{d}x_{i}\right|,

where
\widehat{w_{3}}
denotes the Fourier transform of
w_{3}
. The first factor on the right hand side of (
4.35
) is the
L^{1}
-norm of
\widehat{w_{3}}
, which we denote by
\|\widehat{w_{3}}\|_{L^{1}}
. The
v_{i}
’s can be absorbed into the supremum over
\mathbf{y}
, and so it remains to study

\int_{[-2,2]}e(\pm\theta x_{i}^{2}-y_{i}x_{i})\mathrm{d}x_{i}.

We can write the integrand as
e(\pm\theta\Phi(x_{i}))
, where
\Phi(x_{i})=x_{i}^{2}+x_{i}y_{i}\theta^{-1}
. Then
|\Phi^{\prime\prime}(x_{i})|\geqslant 1
throughout the interval
[-2,2]
, and so we can apply the second derivative test as found in
[
26
, Ch. 8, Proposition 2.3]
to bound this integral by
|\theta|^{-1/2}
. Therefore

I(\mathbf{v};\theta)\ll\|\widehat{w_{3}}\|_{L_{1}}|\theta|^{-2},

Returning to (
4.34
), the contribution to
I_{q,\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{c})
from the range
5|\theta|\geqslant|\mathbf{v}|
is bounded by

\|\widehat{w_{3}}\|_{L_{1}}P_{1}\cdots P_{4}\int_{|\theta|\gg|\mathbf{v}|}|\theta|^{-2}\mathrm{d}\theta\ll\|\widehat{w_{3}}\|_{L_{1}}P_{1}\cdots P_{4}|\mathbf{v}|^{-1}.

Since

\frac{P_{1}\cdots P_{4}}{|\mathbf{v}|}=\frac{B^{3/2}q}{|A|^{1/2}}\min_{1\leqslant i\leqslant 4}\left(\frac{|a_{i}|^{1/2}}{|c_{i}|}\right),

it remains only to show that
\|\widehat{w}_{3}\|_{L^{1}}\ll\eta^{-4}
. We have

\|\widehat{w}_{3}\|_{L^{1}}=\int_{|\mathbf{x}|\leqslant\eta^{-1}}|\widehat{w_{3}}(\mathbf{y})|\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}+\int_{|\mathbf{x}|\geqslant\eta^{-1}}|\widehat{w_{3}}(\mathbf{y})|\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}.

The first integral is trivially
O(\eta^{-4})
. For the second integral, we have

\int_{|\mathbf{x}|\geqslant\eta^{-1}}|\widehat{w_{3}}(\mathbf{y})|\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}\ll\int_{\begin{subarray}{c}|\mathbf{x}|\geqslant\eta^{-1}\\
|y_{1}|=\max_{i}|y_{i}|\end{subarray}}|\widehat{w_{3}}(\mathbf{y})|\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}.

We can now apply integration by parts five times with respect to
x_{1}
to obtain

\displaystyle\int_{|\mathbf{y}|\geqslant\eta^{-1}}|\widehat{w_{3}}(\mathbf{y})|\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}

\displaystyle\ll\int_{\begin{subarray}{c}|\mathbf{y}|\geqslant\eta^{-1}\\
|y_{1}|=\max_{i}|y_{i}|\end{subarray}}\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}}\frac{\partial^{5}w_{3}(\mathbf{x})}{\partial x_{1}^{5}}\frac{e(-\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{y})}{(-2\pi iy_{1})^{5}}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\right|\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}

\displaystyle\ll\eta^{-5}\int_{\begin{subarray}{c}|y_{1}|\geqslant\eta^{-1}\\
|y_{2}|,|y_{3}|,|y_{4}|\leqslant|y_{1}|\end{subarray}}y_{1}^{-5}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}

\displaystyle\ll\eta^{-5}\int_{y_{1}\geqslant\eta^{-1}}y_{1}^{-2}\,\mathrm{d}y_{1}

\displaystyle\ll\eta^{-4}.\qed

We recall the notation
C_{i}=\eta^{-1}B^{\epsilon}|a_{i}|^{1/2}
from Remark
4.10
. We are now ready to estimate the quantity

(4.36)

E_{\mathbf{a}}(B)=\frac{1}{Q^{2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{c}\in\mathbb{Z}^{4}\backslash\{\mathbf{0}\}\\
|c_{i}|\ll C_{i}\end{subarray}}\sum_{q=1}^{\infty}q^{-4}S_{q,\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{c})I_{q,\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{c}).

Note that
I_{q,\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{c})
vanishes for

by the properties of the function
h
, and so we may restrict the
q
-sum to a sum over
q\ll Q
. We recall the notation
\Sigma(x;\mathbf{c})=\sum_{q\leqslant x}S_{q,\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{c})
. We would like to use
Lemma

4.12
together with the bound for
\Sigma(x;\mathbf{c})
from
[
4
, Lemma 4.7]
to estimate the inner sum of (
4.36
). Unfortunately,
[
4

requires the dual form

not to vanish. This was always true in
[

due to the assumptions made in the setup, but we must treat this case separately. To this end, we let
E_{\mathbf{a}}(B)=E_{\mathbf{a},1}(B)+E_{\mathbf{a},2}(B)
, where in

F_{\mathbf{a}}^{*}(\mathbf{c})\neq 0
to the
\mathbf{c}
-sum and in

where

.

4.2.1.
Analysis of
E_{\mathbf{a},1}(B)

Using Lemma
4.12
, we have

E_{\mathbf{a},1}(B)\ll\frac{\eta^{-4}B^{1/2+\epsilon}}{|A|^{1/2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{c}\in\mathbb{Z}^{4}\backslash\{\mathbf{0}\}\\
|c_{i}|\ll C_{i}\end{subarray}}\min_{1\leqslant i\leqslant 4}\left(\frac{|a_{i}|^{1/2}}{|c_{i}|}\right)\sum_{q\ll Q}q^{-3}S_{q,\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{c}).

For a fixed choice of
\mathbf{c}
, let
j(\mathbf{c})\in\{1,\ldots,4\}
denote the index where
|a_{i}|^{1/2}|c_{i}|^{-1}
is minimized. Note in particular that
c_{j(\mathbf{c})}\neq 0
. We have

E_{\mathbf{a},1}(B)\ll\frac{\eta^{-4}B^{1/2+\epsilon}}{|A|^{1/2}}\sum_{j=1}^{4}|a_{j}|^{1/2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{c}\in\mathbb{Z}^{4}\backslash\{\mathbf{0}\}\\
|c_{i}|\ll C_{i}\\
j(\mathbf{c})=j\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{|c_{j}|}\sum_{q\ll Q}q^{-3}S_{q,\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{c}).

An application of
[
4
, Lemma 4.7]
with
x=Q
yields

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{c}\in\mathbb{Z}^{4}\backslash\{\mathbf{0}\}\\
|c_{i}|\ll C_{i}\\
j(\mathbf{c})=j\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{|c_{j}|}\sum_{q\ll Q}q^{-3}S_{q,\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{c})\ll B^{\epsilon}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{c}\in\mathbb{Z}^{4}\backslash\{\mathbf{0}\}\\
|c_{i}|\ll C_{i}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{|c_{j}|}\prod_{i=1}^{4}\gcd(a_{i},c_{i})^{1/2}.

For
i\neq j
, we have

\sum_{0\leqslant|c_{i}|\ll C_{i}}\gcd(a_{i},c_{i})^{1/2}\ll C_{i}B^{\epsilon},

and for
i=j
, we have

\sum_{0<|c_{j}|\ll C_{j}}\frac{\gcd(a_{j},c_{j})^{1/2}}{|c_{j}|}\ll B^{\epsilon}.

Hence

E_{\mathbf{a},1}(B)\ll\frac{\eta^{-4}B^{1/2+\epsilon}}{|A|^{1/2}}\sum_{j=1}^{4}|a_{j}|^{1/2}\prod_{i\neq j}C_{i}\ll\eta^{-7}B^{1/2+\epsilon}.

4.2.2.
Analysis of
E_{\mathbf{a},2}(B)

We write
E_{\mathbf{a},2}(B)=E_{\mathbf{a},2}^{(1)}(B)+E_{\mathbf{a},2}^{(2)}(B)
, where

\displaystyle E_{\mathbf{a},2}^{(1)}(B)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{c}\in\mathbb{Z}^{4}\backslash\{\mathbf{0}\}\\
|c_{i}|\ll C_{i}\\
F_{\mathbf{a}}^{*}(\mathbf{c})=0\end{subarray}}\sum_{q\leqslant M}q^{-4}S_{q,\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{c})I_{q,\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{c}),

\displaystyle E_{\mathbf{a},2}^{(2)}(B)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{c}\in\mathbb{Z}^{4}\backslash\{\mathbf{0}\}\\
|c_{i}|\ll C_{i}\\
F_{\mathbf{a}}^{*}(\mathbf{c})=0\end{subarray}}\sum_{M\leqslant q\ll Q}q^{-4}S_{q,\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{c})I_{q,\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{c}),

For a real parameter
M
to be determined later. To bound
E_{\mathbf{a},2}^{(1)}(B)
, we apply
Lemma

4.12
to obtain

E_{2,a}(P)\ll\frac{\eta^{-4}B^{1/2+\epsilon}}{|A|^{1/2}}\sum_{j=1}^{4}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{c}\in\mathbb{Z}^{4}\backslash\{\mathbf{0}\}\\
|c_{i}|\ll C_{i}\\
j(\mathbf{c})=j\end{subarray}}\frac{|a_{j}|^{1/2}}{|c_{j}|}\sum_{q\leqslant M}q^{-3}|S_{q,\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{c})|.

By
[
4
, Lemma 4.5]
, we have
|S_{q,\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{c})|\ll q^{3}\prod_{i=1}^{4}\gcd(a_{i},c_{i})^{1/2}
, and hence summing trivially over

and proceeding as before, we obtain

E_{\mathbf{a},2}^{(1)}(B)\ll\eta^{-7}B^{1/2+\epsilon}M.

To bound
E_{\mathbf{a},2}^{(2)}(B)
we require some cancellation from the sum over
q
. To achieve this, we use summation by parts, as in the treatment of the main term in Proposition
4.8
, and then apply Lemma
4.7
to obtain a better estimate for the exponential sums. We also perform the

-sum over

and later take a dyadic sum over

. Summation by parts yields

(4.37)

\sum_{R\leqslant q\leqslant 2R}q^{-4}S_{q,\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{c})I_{q,\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{c})\ll-\Sigma(R;\mathbf{c})\frac{I_{R,\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{c})}{R}-\int_{R}^{2R}\Sigma(x;\mathbf{c})\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(\frac{I_{x,\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{c})}{x}\right)\,\mathrm{d}x.

Therefore, from Lemma
4.6

(4.38)

\frac{1}{B}\sum_{R\leqslant q\leqslant 2R}q^{-4}S_{q,\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{c})I_{q,\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{c})\ll\frac{\eta^{-1}P_{1}\cdots P_{4}}{BR}\sup_{R\leqslant x\leqslant 2R}|\Sigma(x;\mathbf{c})|.

We recall the definition of
\Delta_{\mathbf{c}}(\mathbf{a})
from (

from Lemma

\displaystyle E_{\mathbf{a},2}^{(2)}(B)

\displaystyle\ll\frac{\eta^{-1}P_{1}\cdots P_{4}|A|^{3/16}}{B}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}R\textrm{ dyadic}\\
M\leqslant R\ll Q\end{subarray}}R^{-1/2+\epsilon}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{c}\in\mathbb{Z}^{4}\backslash\{\mathbf{0}\}\\
|c_{i}|\ll C_{i}\\
F_{\mathbf{a}}^{*}(\mathbf{c})=0\end{subarray}}\Delta_{\mathbf{c}}(\mathbf{a})^{3/8}

(4.39)

\displaystyle\ll\frac{\eta^{-1}B^{1+\epsilon}}{M^{1/2}|A|^{5/16}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{c}\in\mathbb{Z}^{4}\backslash\{\mathbf{0}\}\\
|c_{i}|\ll C_{i}\\
F_{\mathbf{a}}^{*}(\mathbf{c})=0\end{subarray}}\Delta_{\mathbf{c}}(\mathbf{a})^{3/8}.

It is possible to obtain the asymptotic formula from Theorem

(albeit with a smaller power saving) by bounding the sum in (

C_{1}\cdots C_{4}\Delta^{3/8}
. However, in order to obtain the error terms claimed in Theorem

, we now make use of the constraint

in the
\mathbf{c}

\mathbf{v}\mathbf{w}=(v_{1}w_{1},\ldots,v_{4}w_{4})
for vectors
\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{Z}^{4}

Lemma 4.13
.

For any
\mathbf{d},\mathbf{e}\in(\mathbb{Z}_{\neq 0})^{4}
, we have

\Delta(\mathbf{d}\mathbf{e})\leqslant(d_{1}\cdots d_{4})^{2}\Delta(\mathbf{e}).

Proof.

Fix a prime
p
, and define
\delta_{i}=\nu_{p}(d_{i}),\varepsilon_{i}=\nu_{p}(e_{i})
for
i\in\{1,\ldots,4\}
. Without loss of generality, we may assume that
\varepsilon_{1}\leqslant\cdots\leqslant\varepsilon_{4}
. Then

\displaystyle\nu_{p}(\Delta(\mathbf{d}\mathbf{e}))

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{4}\min\left(\delta_{i}+\varepsilon_{i},\sum_{j\neq i}(\delta_{j}+\varepsilon_{j})\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{i=1}^{3}(\delta_{i}+\varepsilon_{i})+\min\left(\delta_{4}+\varepsilon_{4},\sum_{i=1}^{3}(\delta_{i}+\varepsilon_{i})\right),

and

\nu_{p}(\Delta(\mathbf{e}))=\sum_{i=1}^{3}\varepsilon_{i}+\min\left(\varepsilon_{4},\sum_{i=1}^{3}\varepsilon_{i}\right).

Therefore

\displaystyle\nu_{p}(\Delta(\mathbf{d}\mathbf{e}))-\nu_{p}(\Delta(\mathbf{e}))

\displaystyle\leqslant\begin{cases}\sum_{i=1}^{3}2\delta_{i},&\textrm{ if }\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}+\varepsilon_{3}\leqslant\varepsilon_{4},\\
\sum_{i=1}^{4}\delta_{i},&\textrm{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

\displaystyle\leqslant\nu_{p}((d_{1}\cdots d_{4})^{2}).

Taking a product over all primes
p
completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

Lemma 4.14
.

We have

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{c}\in\mathbb{Z}^{4}\backslash\{\mathbf{0}\}\\
|c_{i}|\ll C_{i}\\
F_{\mathbf{a}}^{*}(\mathbf{c})=0\end{subarray}}\Delta_{\mathbf{c}}(\mathbf{a})^{3/8}\ll\eta^{-3}B^{\epsilon}\Delta^{1/2}.

Proof.

For a nonzero integer
m
, we define
\operatorname{sqf}(m)
to be the smallest positive integer
r
such that
|m|/r
is a square. For
i\in\{1,\ldots,4\}
, we decompose
a_{i}
into a product
a_{i}=e_{i}n_{i}k_{i}^{2}
, where

e_{i}=\gcd\left(a_{i},\prod_{j\neq i}a_{j}\right),\quad n_{i}=\operatorname{sqf}(a_{i}/e_{i}),\quad k_{i}^{2}=a_{i}/(e_{i}n_{i}).

By definition, we have
\prod_{i=1}^{4}e_{i}=\Delta
. For any
i\in\{1,\ldots,4\}
, it is clear that

(4.40)

F^{*}_{\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{c})=0\implies a_{i}|c_{i}^{2}\prod_{j\neq i}a_{j}\implies n_{i}k_{i}^{2}|c_{i}^{2}\implies n_{i}k_{i}|c_{i}.

We divide up the sum over
\mathbf{c}
according to how many of the coordinates
c_{1},\ldots,c_{4}
are equal to zero. At most two of the coordinates can be zero, due to the assumptions
\mathbf{c}\neq 0
and
F^{*}_{\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{c})=0
. Up to reordering the indices, we obtain

(4.41)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{c}\in\mathbb{Z}^{4}\backslash\{\mathbf{0}\}\\
|c_{i}|\ll C_{i}\\
F_{\mathbf{a}}^{*}(\mathbf{c})=0\end{subarray}}\Delta_{\mathbf{c}}(\mathbf{a})^{3/8}\ll T_{0}+T_{1}+T_{2},

where

\displaystyle T_{0}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{c}\in(\mathbb{Z}_{\neq 0})^{4}\\
|c_{i}|\ll C_{i}\\
F_{\mathbf{a}}^{*}(\mathbf{c})=0\end{subarray}}\Delta_{\mathbf{c}}(\mathbf{a})^{3/8},T_{1}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{c}\in(\mathbb{Z}_{\neq 0})^{3}\\
|c_{i}|\ll C_{i}\\
F_{\mathbf{a}}^{*}((\mathbf{c},0))=0\end{subarray}}\Delta_{(\mathbf{c},0)}(\mathbf{a})^{3/8},T_{2}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{c}\in(\mathbb{Z}_{\neq 0})^{2}\\
|c_{i}|\ll C_{i}\\
F_{\mathbf{a}}^{*}((\mathbf{c},0,0))=0\end{subarray}}\Delta_{(\mathbf{c},0,0)}(\mathbf{a})^{3/8}

We begin by studying
T_{0}
. Given that
F^{*}_{\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{c})=0
, we only need to take the sum over
c_{1},c_{2}
and
c_{3}
, because this implicitly determines (up to sign) the value of
c_{4}
. Together with (
4.40
), this implies that

(4.42)

\displaystyle T_{0}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}c_{1},c_{2},c_{3}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\neq 0}\\
|c_{i}|\ll C_{i}\\
n_{i}k_{i}|c_{i}\end{subarray}}\Delta_{\mathbf{c}}(\mathbf{a})^{3/8}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}c_{1},c_{2},c_{3}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\neq 0}\\
|c_{i}|\ll C_{i}/(n_{i}k_{i})\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{4}\gcd\left(n_{i}k_{i}\gcd(a_{i},c_{i}),\prod_{j\neq i}n_{j}k_{j}\gcd(a_{j},c_{j})\right)^{3/8}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{d}\in(\mathbb{Z}_{\neq 0})^{4}\\
d_{i}\ll C_{i}/(n_{i}k_{i}),\\
d_{i}|a_{i}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}c_{1},c_{2},c_{3}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\neq 0}\\
|c_{i}|\ll C_{i}/(n_{i}k_{i})\\
d_{i}|c_{i}\end{subarray}}\Delta(\mathbf{d}\mathbf{n}\mathbf{k})^{3/8}

(4.43)

\displaystyle\ll\left(\prod_{i=1}^{3}\frac{C_{i}}{n_{i}k_{i}}\right)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{d}\in(\mathbb{Z}_{\neq 0})^{4}\\
d_{i}\ll C_{i}/(n_{i}k_{i})\\
d_{i}|a_{i}\end{subarray}}\Delta(\mathbf{n}\mathbf{k})^{3/8}\prod_{i=1}^{4}d_{i}^{-1/4},

where in the last line we have applied Lemma
4.13
. However,
\Delta(\mathbf{n}\mathbf{k})=1
by the definition of
e_{1},\ldots,e_{4}
. There are
O(A^{\epsilon})
choices for
\mathbf{d}
in the sum in (
4.43
), and each summand is bounded by

. Therefore

(4.44)

T_{0}\ll\left(\prod_{i=1}^{3}\frac{C_{i}}{n_{i}k_{i}}\right)A^{\epsilon}.

Returning to (
4.44
) and recalling that
C_{i}=\eta^{-1}B^{\epsilon}|a_{i}|^{1/2}=\eta^{-1}B^{\epsilon}(e_{i}n_{i})^{1/2}k_{i}
, we conclude that

\displaystyle T_{0}

\displaystyle\ll\eta^{-3}B^{\epsilon}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{3}\frac{|a_{i}|^{1/2}}{n_{i}k_{i}}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\eta^{-3}B^{\epsilon}\Delta^{1/2}.

The approach for estimating
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
is very similar, so we will focus on the main differences. It is sufficient to only use the divisibility conditions from (
4.40
) in these cases. For
T_{1}
, all the sums in the above argument will be over vectors indexed by
\{1,2,3\}
, because we have fixed

. We have

\displaystyle T_{1}

\displaystyle\ll\left(\prod_{i=1}^{3}\frac{C_{i}}{n_{i}k_{i}}\right)A^{\epsilon}\Delta(n_{1}k_{1},n_{2}k_{2},n_{3}k_{3},a_{4})^{3/8}

\displaystyle=\left(\prod_{i=1}^{3}\frac{C_{i}}{n_{i}k_{i}}\right)A^{\epsilon}

\displaystyle\ll\eta^{-3}B^{\epsilon}\Delta^{1/2}.

For
T_{2}
, we only take sums over vectors indexed by
\{1,2\}
, since we have fixed

. We have

\displaystyle T_{2}

\displaystyle\ll\left(\prod_{i=1}^{2}\frac{C_{i}}{n_{i}k_{i}}\right)A^{\epsilon}\Delta(n_{1}k_{1},n_{2}k_{2},a_{3},a_{4})^{3/8}

\displaystyle=\left(\prod_{i=1}^{2}\frac{C_{i}}{n_{i}k_{i}}\right)A^{\epsilon}(a_{3},a_{4})^{3/4}

\displaystyle\leqslant\eta^{-2}B^{\epsilon}(e_{1}e_{2})^{1/2}(e_{3}e_{4})^{3/8}

\displaystyle\ll\eta^{-2}B^{\epsilon}\Delta^{1/2}.

Thus each of

is bounded by

∎

Recalling (

E_{2,\mathbf{a}}^{(2)}(B)\ll\frac{\eta^{-4}B^{1+\epsilon}\Delta^{1/2}}{M^{1/2}|A|^{5/16}}.

To combine with the error term

, we make the choice

This yields the estimate

(4.45)

E_{\mathbf{a}}(B)\ll\frac{\eta^{-6}B^{5/6+\epsilon}\Delta^{1/3}}{|A|^{5/24}}+\eta^{-7}B^{1/2+\epsilon}.

This estimate is larger than the error term from Proposition

.

The final ingredient in the proof of Theorem

Lemma 4.15
.

Let
\mathbf{a}\in(\mathbb{Z}_{\neq 0})^{4}
and assume that
A\neq\square
. Then

|\mathfrak{G}_{\mathbf{a}}|\ll|A|^{\epsilon}\Delta^{1/4}.

Proof.

Beginning with the definition of the singular series, we have

\mathfrak{G}_{\mathbf{a}}=\sum_{q=1}^{\infty}q^{-4}S_{q,\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{0})=\sum_{q\leqslant T}q^{-4}S_{q,\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{0})+\sum_{q>T}q^{-4}S_{q,\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{0})

for any
T\geqslant 1
. For the sum over
q>T
, we apply partial summation and Lemma
4.7
. We have

\displaystyle\sum_{q>T}q^{-4}S_{q,\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{0})

\displaystyle=\frac{\Sigma(T;\mathbf{0})}{T}-\int_{T}^{\infty}\Sigma(x;\mathbf{0})\frac{\partial}{\partial x}(x^{-1})\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\ll T^{-1/2+\epsilon}|A|^{3/16+\epsilon}\Delta^{3/8}.

By choosing
T=|A|^{2}
, we can ensure that the contribution from this region is negligible.

For the remaining sum over
q\leqslant T
, we follow a similar argument to the proof of Lemma
4.7
. Using the multiplicativity of
S_{q,\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{0})
in
q
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{q\leqslant T}q^{-4}S_{q,\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{0})

\displaystyle\ll\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}q_{1}\leqslant T\\
\gcd(q_{1},2A)=1\end{subarray}}\left(\frac{A}{q_{1}}\right)\frac{\varphi(q_{1})}{q_{1}^{2}}\right)\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}q_{2}\leqslant T/q_{1}\\
q_{2}|(2A)^{\infty}\end{subarray}}q_{2}^{-4}S_{q_{2},\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{0})\right)

\displaystyle\ll T^{\epsilon}|A|^{\epsilon}\max_{q|(2A)^{\infty}}\left(\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{4}\gcd(q,a_{i})^{1/2}}{q}\right),

where in the second line we have estimated the sum over
q_{1}
trivially, and the sum over
q_{2}
by applying
[
4
, Lemma 4.5]
.
We let
m_{i,p}
denote the
i
th smallest element of
\nu_{p}(a_{1}),\ldots,\nu_{p}(a_{4})
. We define

L_{p}=\nu_{p}\left(\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{4}\gcd(q,a_{i})}{q^{2}}\right),\quad k_{p}=\nu_{p}(q).

Then

L_{p}=\sum_{j=1}^{4}\min(k_{p},m_{i,p})-2k_{p}.

The maximum possible value of
L_{p}
is attained by choosing
k_{p}=m_{2,p}
, and so
L_{p}\leqslant m_{1,p}+m_{2,p}
. We have

\nu_{p}(\Delta)=m_{1,p}+m_{2,p}+m_{3,p}+\min(m_{1,p}+m_{2,p}+m_{3,p},m_{4,p})\geqslant 2(m_{1,p}+m_{2,p}),

and so
L_{p}\leqslant\nu_{p}(\Delta)/2
. Taking a product over
p|2A
completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

\mathbf{r}|\mathbf{y}
, this implies that
S|Y^{3}
.) Therefore
|Y|\leqslant D
implies that
|S^{2}Y^{3}|\leqslant D^{9}
. In particular,
|S^{2}Y^{3}|\leqslant B^{4/7}
when
D\leqslant B^{1/16}
. Hence we may apply Theorem
4.2
with
\mathbf{a}=\mathbf{s}^{2}\mathbf{y}^{3}
to obtain for any

,

(5.7)

\#\mathscr{N}(B;\mathbf{r},\mathbf{s},s_{0})=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\in\{\pm 1\}^{4}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|Y|\leqslant D\\
\operatorname{sgn}y_{i}=\epsilon_{i}\\
\mathbf{r}|\mathbf{y},Y\neq\square\\
y_{i}\textrm{ square-free}\end{subarray}}\left(\frac{\mathfrak{G}_{\mathbf{s}^{2}\mathbf{y}^{3}}\sigma_{\infty}(\boldsymbol{\epsilon})B}{S|Y|^{3/2}s_{0}^{2}}+O\left(\frac{B^{41/42+\epsilon}\Delta^{1/3}}{|S^{2}Y^{3}|^{11/24}}\right)\right),

where as in Section
4
, we define

\Delta=\Delta(\mathbf{s}^{2}\mathbf{y}^{3})=\prod_{i=1}^{4}\gcd\left(s_{i}^{2}y_{i}^{3},\prod_{j\neq i}s_{j}^{2}y_{j}^{3}\right).

We begin by studying the main term from (
5.7
). We would like to replace the sum over
|Y|\leqslant D
with a sum over all
\mathbf{y}\in(\mathbb{Z}_{\neq 0})^{4}
satisfying
\operatorname{sgn}y_{i}=\epsilon_{i}
for all
i
. In order to estimate the error term in doing so, we appeal to Lemma
4.15
. We define

E_{1}(D)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{r},\mathbf{s}\in\mathbb{N}^{4}\\
s_{0}\in\mathbb{N}\end{subarray}}\omega(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{s},s_{0})\sum_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\in\{\pm 1\}^{4}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{y}\in(\mathbb{Z}_{\neq 0})^{4}\\
Y>D,Y\neq\square\\
\operatorname{sgn}y_{i}=\epsilon_{i}\\
\mathbf{r}|\mathbf{y}\end{subarray}}\frac{\mu^{2}(y_{1})\cdots\mu^{2}(y_{4})\mathfrak{G}_{\mathbf{s}^{2}\mathbf{y}^{3}}\sigma_{\infty}(\boldsymbol{\epsilon})}{S|Y|^{3/2}s_{0}^{2}}.

Lemma 5.3
.

For any
D\geqslant 1
, we have
E_{1}(D)=O(D^{-1/4+\epsilon})
.

Proof.

From the observation
S|Y^{3}
made above, and the fact that
\sigma_{\infty}(\boldsymbol{\epsilon})\ll 1
, we have

E_{1}(D)\ll\sum_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\in\{\pm 1\}^{4}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{N}^{4}\\
Y>D,Y\neq\square\end{subarray}}Y^{-3/2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{s}\in\mathbb{N}^{4}\\
S|Y^{3}\end{subarray}}\frac{|\mathfrak{G}_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\mathbf{s}^{2}\mathbf{y}^{3}}|}{S}\sum_{s_{0}\in\mathbb{N}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{r}\in\mathbb{N}^{4}\\
R|Y\end{subarray}}\frac{|\omega(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{s},s_{0})|}{s_{0}^{2}}.

Since
|\omega(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{s},s_{0})|\leqslant 1
, the sum over
s_{0}
is convergent. The sum over
\mathbf{r}
only contributes
O(Y^{\epsilon})
by the trivial bound for the divisor function. Applying the estimate from Lemma
4.15
, we have
|\mathfrak{G}_{\mathbf{s}^{2}\mathbf{y}^{3}}|\ll(SY)^{\epsilon}\Delta(\mathbf{s}^{2}\mathbf{y}^{3})^{1/4}
whenever
Y\neq\square
. Therefore

(5.8)

E_{1}(D)\ll\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{N}^{4}\\
Y>D\end{subarray}}Y^{-3/2+\epsilon}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{s}\in\mathbb{N}^{4}\\
S|Y^{3}\end{subarray}}\frac{\Delta(\mathbf{s}^{2}\mathbf{y}^{3})^{1/4}}{S^{1-\epsilon}}.

From Lemma
4.13
, we have
\Delta(\mathbf{s}^{2}\mathbf{y}^{3})\leqslant S^{4}\Delta(\mathbf{y}^{3})
. Continuing from (
5.8
), we obtain the estimate

E_{1}(D)\ll\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{N}^{4}\\
Y>D\end{subarray}}\frac{\Delta(\mathbf{y}^{3})^{1/4}}{Y^{3/2-\epsilon}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{s}\in\mathbb{N}^{4}\\
S|Y^{3}\end{subarray}}S^{\epsilon}\ll\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{N}^{4}\\
Y>D\end{subarray}}\frac{\Delta(\mathbf{y})^{3/4}}{Y^{3/2-\epsilon}}.

We note that
\Delta(\mathbf{y})
is always squareful. Therefore, for any
R\geqslant 1
, there are
O(R^{1/2})
possible values for
\Delta(\mathbf{y})
in the range
[R,2R]
. We define the quantity
M(\mathbf{y})=Y/\Delta(\mathbf{y})
. Given
M,\Delta\geqslant 1
, there are
O((M\Delta)^{\epsilon})
choices for
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{N}^{4}
satisfying
M=M(\mathbf{y}),\Delta=\Delta(\mathbf{y})
. Breaking into dyadic intervals, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{N}^{4}\\
Y>D\end{subarray}}\frac{\Delta(\mathbf{y})^{3/4}}{Y^{3/2-\epsilon}}

\displaystyle\ll\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}R_{1},R_{2}\textrm{ dyadic}\\
R_{1}R_{2}>D\end{subarray}}R_{1}R_{2}^{1/2}\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{N}^{4}\\
R_{1}\leqslant M(\mathbf{y})\leqslant 2R_{1}\\
R_{2}\leqslant\Delta(\mathbf{y})\leqslant 2R_{2}\end{subarray}}M(\mathbf{y})^{-3/2+\epsilon}\Delta(\mathbf{y})^{-3/4+\epsilon}

\displaystyle\ll\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}R_{1},R_{2}\textrm{ dyadic}\\
R_{1}R_{2}>D\end{subarray}}R_{1}^{-1/2+\epsilon}R_{2}^{-1/4+\epsilon}

\displaystyle\ll D^{-1/4+\epsilon},

as required.
∎

We now study the error term

(5.9)

E_{2}(D)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{r},\mathbf{s}\in\mathbb{N}^{4}\\
s_{0}\in\mathbb{N}\end{subarray}}\omega(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{s},s_{0})\sum_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\in\{\pm 1\}^{4}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|Y|\leqslant D,Y\neq\square\\
\operatorname{sgn}y_{i}=\epsilon_{i}\\
\mathbf{r}|\mathbf{y},\\
y_{i}\textrm{ square-free}\end{subarray}}\frac{\Delta(\mathbf{s}^{2}\mathbf{y}^{3})^{1/3}}{|S^{2}Y^{3}|^{11/24}}.

Lemma 5.4
.

We have
E_{2}(D)\ll D^{11/8+\epsilon}.

Proof.

We proceed in a similar fashion to the proof of Lemma
5.3
. Let
M(\mathbf{y})
be as defined in Lemma
5.3
. Then

\displaystyle E_{2}(D)

\displaystyle\ll\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{N}^{4}\\
Y\leqslant D\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{s}\in\mathbb{N}^{4}\\
S|Y^{3}\end{subarray}}\frac{\Delta(\mathbf{s}^{2}\mathbf{y}^{3})^{1/3}}{(S^{2}Y^{3})^{11/24-\epsilon}}

\displaystyle\ll\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{N}^{4}\\
Y\leqslant D\end{subarray}}\frac{\Delta(\mathbf{y})}{Y^{11/8-\epsilon}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{s}\in\mathbb{N}^{4}\\
S|Y^{3}\end{subarray}}S^{5/12+\epsilon}

\displaystyle\ll\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}R_{1},R_{2}\textrm{ dyadic}\\
R_{1}R_{2}\leqslant D\end{subarray}}R_{1}R_{2}^{1/2}\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{N}^{4}\\
R_{1}\leqslant M(\mathbf{y})\leqslant 2R_{1}\\
R_{2}\leqslant\Delta(\mathbf{y})\leqslant 2R_{2}\end{subarray}}M(\mathbf{y})^{-1/8+\epsilon}\Delta(\mathbf{y})^{7/8+\epsilon}

\displaystyle\ll\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}R_{1},R_{2}\textrm{ dyadic}\\
R_{1}R_{2}\leqslant D\end{subarray}}R_{1}^{7/8+\epsilon}R_{2}^{11/8+\epsilon}

\displaystyle\ll D^{11/8+\epsilon}.\qed

We now conclude the proof of Theorem
1.1
. Combining (
5.1
), Lemma

, (

), Lemma

and Lemma

, for any

, we have

N(B)=cB+O(B^{1+\epsilon}D^{-1/12})+O(B^{41/42+\epsilon}D^{11/8}),

where

(5.10)

c=\frac{1}{16}\sum_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\in\{\pm 1\}^{4}}\sigma_{\infty}(\boldsymbol{\epsilon})\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{y}\in(\mathbb{Z}_{\neq 0})^{4}\\
\operatorname{sgn}(y_{i})=\epsilon_{i}\\
Y\neq\square\end{subarray}}\frac{\mu^{2}(y_{1})\cdots\mu^{2}(y_{4})}{|Y|^{3/2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{r},\mathbf{s}\in\mathbb{N}^{4}\\
s_{0}\in\mathbb{N}\\
\mathbf{r}|\mathbf{y}\end{subarray}}\frac{\omega(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{s},s_{0})\mathfrak{G}_{\mathbf{s}^{2}\mathbf{y}^{3}}}{Ss_{0}^{2}}.

Making the choice

.

5.1.
The leading constant

The expression for the leading constant in (
5.10
) is analogous to
[
6
, Equation (3.14)]
. Similarly to
[
6
, Equation (3.15)]
, we now demonstrate that the inner sum of (
5.10
) can be expressed as a product of local densities. We define for any prime
p
and any
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

M_{n}(\mathbf{y},p)=\#\left\{\mathbf{m}\ (\mathrm{mod}\ p^{n}):\sum_{i=1}^{4}y_{i}^{3}m_{i}^{2}\equiv 0\ (\mathrm{mod}\ p^{n}),p\nmid m_{j}y_{j}\textrm{ for some }j\right\}.

Lemma 5.5
.

We have

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{r},\mathbf{s}\in\mathbb{N}^{4}\\
s_{0}\in\mathbb{N}\\
\mathbf{r}|\mathbf{y}\end{subarray}}\frac{\omega(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{s},s_{0})\mathfrak{G}_{\mathbf{s}^{2}\mathbf{y}^{3}}}{Ss_{0}^{2}}=\prod_{p}\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\left(\frac{M_{N}(\mathbf{y},p)}{p^{3N}}\right).

Proof.

We first express the singular series
\mathfrak{G}_{\mathbf{s}^{2}\mathbf{y}^{3}}
as a product of local densities. Since
\mathfrak{G}_{\mathbf{s}^{2}\mathbf{y}^{3}}=\sum_{q=1}^{\infty}S_{q,\mathbf{s}^{2}\mathbf{y}^{3}}(\mathbf{0})
, and
S_{q,\mathbf{s}^{2}\mathbf{y}^{3}}(\mathbf{0})
is multiplicative in
q
, we have

\displaystyle\mathfrak{G}_{\mathbf{s}^{2}\mathbf{y}^{3}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{q=1}^{\infty}q^{-4}S_{q,\mathbf{s}^{2}\mathbf{y}^{3}}(\mathbf{0})

\displaystyle=\prod_{p}\left(1+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}p^{-4n}S_{p^{n},\mathbf{s}^{2}\mathbf{y}^{3}}(\mathbf{0})\right).

Moreover,

\displaystyle S_{p^{n},\mathbf{s}^{2}\mathbf{y}^{3}}(\mathbf{0})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{b}\bmod{p^{n}}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leqslant k<p^{n}\\
\gcd(k,p)=1\end{subarray}}e_{p^{n}}\left(k\sum_{i=1}^{4}s_{i}^{2}y_{i}^{3}b_{i}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=p^{n}N_{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{y}}(p^{n})-p^{n+3}N_{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{y}}(p^{n-1}),

where

N_{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{y}}(p^{n})=\#\left\{\mathbf{m}\ (\mathrm{mod}\ p^{n}):\sum_{i=1}^{4}s_{i}^{2}y_{i}^{3}m_{i}^{2}\equiv 0\ (\mathrm{mod}\ p^{n})\right\}.

Therefore

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}p^{-4n}S_{p^{n},\mathbf{s}^{2}\mathbf{y}^{3}}(\mathbf{0})

\displaystyle=\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left(p^{-3n}N_{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{y}}(p^{n})-p^{-3(n-1)}N_{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{y}}(p^{n-1})\right)

\displaystyle=\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\left(p^{-3N}N_{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{y}}(p^{N})\right)-1,

and hence

(5.11)

\mathfrak{G}_{\mathbf{s}^{2}\mathbf{y}^{3}}=\prod_{p}\left(\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\frac{N_{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{y}}(p^{N})}{p^{3N}}\right).

For the remainder of the proof, we will use a sum over
\mathbf{r}^{[p]},\mathbf{s}^{[p]},s_{0}^{[p]}
to denote a sum over all
\mathbf{r},\mathbf{s}\in\mathbb{N}^{4},s_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
, with
(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{s},s_{0})=(\mathbf{r}^{[p]},\mathbf{s}^{[p]},s_{0}^{[p]})
and
\mathbf{r}|\mathbf{y}
. Recalling that
\omega(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{s},s_{0})
is also multiplicative, we obtain

(5.12)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{r},\mathbf{s}\in\mathbb{N}^{4}\\
s_{0}\in\mathbb{N}\\
\mathbf{r}|\mathbf{y}\end{subarray}}\frac{\omega(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{s},s_{0})\mathfrak{G}_{\mathbf{s}^{2}\mathbf{y}^{3}}}{Ss_{0}^{2}}=\prod_{p}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{r}^{[p]},\mathbf{s}^{[p]},s_{0}^{[p]}\end{subarray}}\frac{\omega(\mathbf{r}^{[p]},\mathbf{s}^{[p]},s_{0}^{[p]})}{S^{[p]}s_{0}^{2[p]}}\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\left(\frac{N_{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{y}}(p^{N})}{p^{3N}}\right)\right).

To complete the proof, it suffices to show that for a fixed prime
p
, the factor on the right hand side of (
5.12
) is equal to
\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}(p^{-3N}M_{N}(\mathbf{y},p))
. We define

N_{\mathbf{s},s_{0},\mathbf{y}}(n)=\#\left\{\mathbf{m}\ (\mathrm{mod}\ p^{n}):\sum_{i=1}^{4}y_{i}^{3}m_{i}^{2}\equiv 0,\mathbf{s}|\mathbf{m},s_{0}|\mathbf{m}\right\}.

We claim that

(5.13)

M_{n}(\mathbf{y},p)=\sum_{\mathbf{r}^{[p]},\mathbf{s}^{[p]},s_{0}^{[p]}}\omega(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{s},s_{0})N_{\mathbf{s},s_{0},\mathbf{y}}(n).

To see this, we fix
\mathbf{m}\ (\mathrm{mod}\ p^{n})
such that
\sum_{i=1}^{4}y_{i}^{3}m_{i}^{2}\equiv 0
, and consider the quantity

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{r}^{[p]},\mathbf{s}^{[p]},s_{0}^{[p]}\\
\mathbf{s}|\mathbf{m},s_{0}|\mathbf{m}\end{subarray}}\omega(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{s},s_{0}).

This expression has already been encountered in (
5.4
), and from the proof of Lemma
5.2
, we see that it is equal to
1
if
p|y_{i}m_{i}
for some
i
, and zero otherwise. This establishes (
5.13
).

Changing variables from
m_{i}
to
s_{0}s_{i}m_{i}
, we have

\displaystyle N_{\mathbf{s},s_{0},\mathbf{y}}(n)

\displaystyle=\frac{s_{0}^{4[p]}N_{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{y}}(p^{n-2\nu_{p}(s_{0})})}{S^{[p]}}.

Therefore

\displaystyle\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\left(\frac{N_{\mathbf{s},s_{0},\mathbf{y}}(N)}{p^{3N}}\right)

\displaystyle=\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\left(\frac{N_{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{y}}(p^{N})}{S^{[p]}s_{0}^{2[p]}}\right).

Combining this with (
5.13
), we deduce that
\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}(p^{-3N}M_{N}(\mathbf{y},p))
matches the Euler factor from the right hand side of (
5.12
).
∎

We do not expect the leading constant
c
from (
5.10
) to agree with the the prediction from
[
21
, Conjecture 1.1]
. In
[
25
]
, we study the counting problem
N_{k}(B)
from (

) in the case
k=3
. This corresponds to the orbifold
(\mathbb{P}^{1},D)
, where

. The more detailed discussion around the case
k=3
in
[
25
]
is readily adapted to deal with the case
k=4
considered in this paper. Therefore, we content ourselves with giving a brief summary here.

We recall that
N_{\mathbf{y}}(B)
denotes the contribution to
N(B)
from a fixed choice of
\mathbf{y}\in(\mathbb{Z}_{\neq 0})^{4}
satisfying
Y\neq\square
and
\mu^{2}(y_{1})=\cdots=\mu^{2}(y_{4})=1
. We have shown that each

contributes a positive proportion to

, namely
N_{\mathbf{y}}(B)\sim c_{\mathbf{y}}B

Moreover, Manin’s conjecture can be applied to the quadric

given by the equation

, and the constant thus predicted is in agreement with

(as was expected due to Remark

). Hence the expression in (
5.10

\mathbf{y}
of leading constants arising from Manin’s conjecture applied to the quadrics

\mathbf{y}
, and it does not appear to be possible to express (

) as an Euler product. In contrast, the leading constant predicted in

21
, Conjecture 1.1]

is by definition an Euler product. In
[
25
]
, we find that when

5.10

[

A natural question that arises is whether thin sets could explain a discrepancy between

[
21
, Conjecture 1.1]
