Abstract
. The aim of this work is to further develop the calculus of (internal) relations for a regular 𝖮𝗋𝖽 \mathsf{Ord} -category ℂ \mathbb{C} . To capture the enriched features of a regular 𝖮𝗋𝖽 \mathsf{Ord} -category and obtain a good calculus, the relations we work with are precisely the ideals in ℂ \mathbb{C} . We then focus on an enriched version of the 1-dimensional algebraic 2 2 -permutable (also called Mal’tsev) property and its well-known equivalent characterisations expressed through properties on ordinary relations. We introduce the notion of 𝖮𝗋𝖽 \mathsf{Ord} -Mal’tsev category and show that these may be characterised through enriched versions of the above mentioned properties adapted to ideals. Any 𝖮𝗋𝖽 \mathsf{Ord} -enrichment of a 1-dimensional Mal’tsev category is necessarily an 𝖮𝗋𝖽 \mathsf{Ord} -Mal’tsev category. We also give some examples of categories which are not Mal’tsev categories, but are 𝖮𝗋𝖽 \mathsf{Ord} -Mal’tsev categories.

Introduction

The notion of
regular category

[
1
]
has been widely studied and explored in Category Theory over the past 50 years. Regular categories capture several nice exactness properties of abelian categories
[
7
]
, one of the notions in the genesis of Category Theory, but without requiring them to be additive. A handy exactness property of regular categories is the existence of (regular) images. This makes regular categories a good context to work with ordinary relations, since it is possible to define their composition and this composition is associative. The calculus of ordinary relations provides a well established and powerful tool for obtaining proofs in regular categories. Another good reason for the successful development of regular categories is the large number of examples. The category of sets, any elementary topos, abelian categories or any variety of universal algebras are all examples of Barr-exact categories
[
1
]
, which are regular categories. The category of topological groups gives an example of a regular category which is not Barr-exact (see
[
12
]
).

A variety of universal algebras, of a certain type, is defined through its signature and axioms, i.e. its theory admits specific operations satisfying given identities. For example, a variety of universal algebras is called a
2
-permutable variety

[
30
]
(they are also called
congruence permutable varieties
or
Mal’tsev varieties
) when its theory admits a ternary Mal’tsev operation
p
satisfying the identities
p(x,y,y)=x
and
p(x,x,y)=y
. The variety
\mathsf{Grp}
of groups is
2
-permutable, where
p(x,y,z)=xy^{-1}z
. Sometimes it is possible to extract from the operations and identities equivalent properties involving homomorphic relations. The translation of these properties on homomorphic relations to an appropriate categorical setting could be used to define the categorical counterpart of such type of variety. For example, the existence of a Mal’tsev operation of a
2
-permutable variety
\mathscr{V}
is equivalent to the fact that the composition of any pair of congruences
R,S
on any algebra
X
of
\mathscr{V}
is
2
-permutable (=commutative):
RS\cong SR

[
23
]
. It was shown in
[
22
]
that
2
-permutable varieties can also be characterised by the fact that any homomorphic relation
D
from an algebra
X
to an algebra
Y
is
difunctional
:

\left[\,(x,y)\in D\wedge(u,y)\in D\wedge(u,v)\in D\,\right]\Rightarrow(x,v)\in D,

where
x,u\in X
and
y,v\in Y
. The notion of
2
-permutable variety was generalised to a categorical context in
[
9
]
(see also
[
8
,
10
,
3
]
). This was achieved by translating the characteristic properties on homomorphic relations of
2
-permutable varieties into similar properties on ordinary relations for categories. A regular category
\mathsf{C}
is called a
Mal’tsev category
when any pair of ordinary equivalence relations
R,S
on any object
X
in
\mathsf{C}
is such that
RS\cong SR
. Without requiring any kind of exactness properties, a category
\mathsf{C}
is a Mal’tsev category when any ordinary relation
D\colon X{\rightarrow\hskip-10.33327pt{\mapstochar}\hskip 9.47217pt}Y
in
\mathsf{C}
is difunctional, i.e. the relation
\mathsf{C}(W,D)\colon\mathsf{C}(W,X){\rightarrow\hskip-10.33327pt{\mapstochar}\hskip 9.47217pt}\mathsf{C}(W,Y)
in
\mathsf{Set}
is difunctional, for every object
W
of
\mathsf{C}
(see Definition
2.1
). There are several alternative well-known characterisations of regular Mal’tsev categories given through other properties on ordinary relations, such as: every ordinary reflexive relation is an ordinary equivalence relation. They are recalled in Theorem
5.2
.

The aim of this work is to explore
2
-permutability in an
\mathsf{Ord}
-enriched context and define, what we call,
\mathsf{Ord}
-Mal’tsev category – Section
5
. To do so we consider an enriched version of the property concerning the difunctionality of ordinary relations. The appropriate enriched version of an ordinary relation turns out to be that of
ideal
(see Definition
4.1
). An
\mathsf{Ord}
-category
\mathbb{C}
is called an
\mathsf{Ord}
-Mal’tsev category
when every ideal
D\colon X\looparrowright Y
in
\mathbb{C}
satisfies the property: given morphisms
x,u,u^{\prime}\colon A\to X
,
y,y^{\prime},v\colon A\to Y
, the following implication holds

\left[\,(x,y)\in_{A}D\wedge y\preccurlyeq y^{\prime}\wedge(u,y^{\prime})\in_{A}D\wedge u\preccurlyeq u^{\prime}\wedge(u^{\prime},v)\in_{A}D\,\right]\Rightarrow(x,v)\in_{A}D.

Any
\mathsf{Ord}
-enrichment of a Mal’tsev category is necessarily an
\mathsf{Ord}
-Mal’tsev category. If an
\mathsf{Ord}
-category
\mathbb{C}
is regular (see Definition
1.4
below) then we obtain equivalent characterisations of regular
\mathsf{Ord}
-Mal’tsev categories through properties on ideals (Theorem
5.9
, which is the enriched version of Theorem
5.2
).

A fundamental part of this work concerns the characterisations of regular
\mathsf{Ord}
-Mal’tsev categories obtained in Theorem
5.9
. This is achieved by developing an enriched calculus of relations for ideals in the context of regular
\mathsf{Ord}
-categories – Section
4
. We adapt the calculus of relations given in
[
31
]
, which was done for regular
\mathsf{Pos}
-categories, to regular
\mathsf{Ord}
-categories and further explore the possible extensions of the known calculus of ordinary relations in the regular context (see
[
8
]
).

We give examples of categories which are not Mal’tsev categories, and provide them with an
\mathsf{Ord}
-enrichment for which they are
\mathsf{Ord}
-Mal’tsev categories – Section
6
. The example concerning the category
(V\mbox{-}\mathsf{Cat})^{\rm op}
relies on an object-wise approach to
\mathsf{Ord}
-Mal’tsev categories, which is developed in the Appendix.

1.
\mathsf{Ord}
-enriched categories

Let
\mathbb{C}
be an
\mathsf{Ord}
-category, i.e. a category enriched in the category
\mathsf{Ord}
of preordered sets (i.e. sets equipped with a reflexive and transitive relation) and monotone maps. This means that, for any objects
X
and
Y
of
\mathbb{C}
,
\mathbb{C}(X,Y)
is equipped with a preorder such that (pre)composition preserves it. We will denote this preorder of morphisms by
\preccurlyeq
. If we consider in
\mathbb{C}
the reverse preorder we obtain again an
\mathsf{Ord}
-enriched category which we denote, as usual, by
\mathbb{C}^{\mathrm{co}}
. Any category
\mathsf{C}
with the identity order (=discrete order) on morphisms can be considered an
\mathsf{Ord}
-category.

A morphism
m\colon X\to Y
is said to be
full
when: given morphisms
a,a^{\prime}\colon A\to X
such that
ma\preccurlyeq ma^{\prime}
, then
a\preccurlyeq a^{\prime}
; equivalently,
ma\preccurlyeq ma^{\prime}
if and only if
a\preccurlyeq a^{\prime}
. (Note that, in the
\mathsf{Ord}
-enriched context, all morphisms are faithful.) Such (mono)morphisms are also called
ff-(mono)morphisms
, where the “ff” stands for “fully faithful”; see
[
21
,
31
]
. If the preorder
\preccurlyeq
is also antisymmetric, so that
\mathbb{C}
is a
\mathsf{Pos}
-category, then an ff-morphism is necessarily a monomorphism; this is not the case when
\mathbb{C}
is an
\mathsf{Ord}
-category. We denote ff-monomorphisms with arrows of the type
\rightarrowtail
. We have similar properties for ff-(mono)morphisms as those of monomorphisms in ordinary categories.

.

Let

(2)

if

is an ff-(mono)morphism, then

is an ff-(mono)morphism;

(3)

the 2-pullback of an ff-(mono)morphism is an ff-(mono)morphism.

Recall that a category
\mathsf{C}
is a 1-dimensional
regular category

[
1
]
when:

•

\mathsf{C}
has finite limits;

•

\mathsf{C}
has coequalisers of kernel pairs;

•

\mathsf{C}
.

Regular categories may also be characterised as categories
\mathsf{C}
such that (see
[
4
]
, for example):

(r1)

\mathsf{C}
has finite limits;

(r2)

\mathsf{C}
admits a (strong epimorphism, monomorphism) factorisation system;

(r3)

strong epimorphisms are stable under pullbacks in
\mathsf{C}
;

(r4)

every strong epimorphism is a coequaliser (of its kernel pair).

When
\mathsf{C}
is a regular category the notions of regular epimorphism and strong epimorphism coincide, so that condition (r4) follows from (r1)-(r3).

This latter formulation led to the following definition of regular
\mathsf{Pos}

[
21
]
. A
\mathsf{Pos}
-category

is called

when:

(R1)

\mathbb{C}
has finite (weighted) limits;

(R2)

\mathbb{C}
admits an (so-morphism, ff-monomorphism) factorisation system;

(R3)

so-morphisms are stable under 2-pullbacks in
\mathbb{C}
;

(R4)

every so-morphism is a coinserter (of its comma object).

As in the 1-dimensional case, condition (R4) follows from (R1)-(R3), as shown in
[
31
]
.

[

An

(R3)

\mathbb{C}

(R4)

every so-morphism is a bicoinserter (of its comma object).

Remark 1.6
.

•

It is easy to prove that, in a regular
\mathsf{Ord}
-category, every bicoinserter
e\colon A\to E
will be the bicoinserter of its comma object projections
(\pi_{1}\colon e/e\to A,\pi_{2}\colon e/e\to A)
.

•

In a
\mathsf{Pos}
-enriched context, the notions of coinserter and bicoinserter coincide. It was shown in Proposition 2.16 of
[
31
]
that condition (R4) follows from (R1), (R2) and (R3). The proof is based on the fact that a certain morphism is an so-morphism and an ff-morphism, thus an isomorphism in that context. This argument cannot be used in the
\mathsf{Ord}
-enriched case, since an ff-morphism may not be a monomorphism. We do not know whether (R4) follows from (R1), (R2) and (R3) in the
\mathsf{Ord}
-enriched context.

. Since

. Given any morphism

and

are examples of difunctional ordinary relations.

Remark 2.5
.

An ordinary relation

in a category

is:

•

reflexive when

, meaning that there exists a morphism

such that

; equivalently

;

•

symmetric when
(r_{2},r_{1})\in_{R}R
, meaning that there exists a morphism

such that
r_{1}s=r_{2}
and
r_{2}s=r_{1}
; also

or, equivalently,
R^{\circ}\cong R
;

If

is a regular category, so that composition of ordinary relations exists,

is:

•

transitive when

•

an ordinary equivalence relation when it is reflexive, symmetric, and transitive, so that

,

and

(

4.
Order ideals and their calculus of relations

The bicategory
\mathsf{Rel_{ff}}(\mathbb{C})
does not capture entirely the enriched features of a regular
\mathsf{Ord}
-category. To do so, we shall consider relations with a kind of “compatibility” condition. Such relations were called weakening-closed in
[
20
,
31
]
. We prefer to follow
[
11
]
and call them (order) ideals.

Definition 4.1
.

A relation
X\stackrel{{\scriptstyle r_{1}}}{{\longleftarrow}}R\stackrel{{\scriptstyle r_{2}}}{{\longrightarrow}}Y
in an
\mathsf{Ord}
-category
\mathbb{C}
is called an
ideal
when, given generalised elements
x,x^{\prime}\colon A\to X
,
y,y^{\prime}\colon A\to Y
, we have

(4.i)

\left(x^{\prime}\preccurlyeq x\;\wedge\;(x,y)\in_{A}R\;\wedge\;y\preccurlyeq y^{\prime}\right)\Rightarrow(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in_{A}R.

Note that an ideal
X\stackrel{{\scriptstyle r_{1}}}{{\longleftarrow}}R\stackrel{{\scriptstyle r_{2}}}{{\longrightarrow}}Y
is a relation, by definition. So,
(r_{1},r_{2})
is jointly ff-monomorphic. We use the notation
R\colon X\looparrowright Y
for ideals.

Example 4.2
.

\mathsf{Ord}
-category
\mathbb{C}

If
\mathbb{C}

\mathsf{Ord}
-category, the composition of ideals is still an ideal. We denote by

Given an
\mathsf{Ord}
-category
\mathbb{C}
, any morphism

(see

-enriched case).

The following results are easy to prove and most can be found in
