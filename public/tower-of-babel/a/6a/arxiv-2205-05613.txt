Cross-Frame Potential
Roza Aceska, McKenna Kaczanowski

Abstract
We study a cross-frame potential function, which is closely tied to the cross correlation between a frame and its dual. We analyze the behavior of this new function to determine what information the cross-frame potential value can reveal about the relationship between the two frames. We also define a cross frame potential function for fusion frames, and study its properties for special types of fusion frames.



Keywords:
Finite frame, Canonical dual frame, Cross-frame potential

AMS subject classification:
42C15, 42C99

Introduction

Frames
[
7
]

have properties similar to bases, but in general they offer more flexi-bility to accommodate specific
design requirements,
and their redundancy allows for protection against information loss in data transmission.
In the past few decades frame representations of vectors have been studied by an army of mathematicians and applied scientists, interested in their mathematical properties and in the advantages they offer in numerous applications
[
17
,
15
]
.

Fusion frames
as generalizations of frames were first introduced in
[
5
]
as weighted sequences of
subspaces with controlled overlaps. These redundant subspaces ease the construction of frames by building them locally and then piecing the local frames together by employing a special structure of the set of subspaces.
The properties and applications of fusion frames (useful in distributed processing) were further explored in
[
6
,
4
,
1
]
and references within, while dual fusion frames were studied in detail in
[
12
]
.

The
frame potential
function
[
6
]
takes a frame as input and returns its “frame potential value”, which is simply the
\ell^{2}
sum of the inner products between the frame vectors. This value relates the geometric structure of the input frame to optimal properties with respect to frame potential
[
3
]
.
The concept was first studied in
[
2
]
where the question of minimizing the frame potential was initially answered - the potential is minimized when the input frame has specific geometry. Further results in this direction can be found in
[
3
]
, where alternative definitions and generalizations of the notion are also pursued. For a study of the frame potential function in finite-dimensional Banach spaces see
[
9
]
.

The notion of
fusion frame potential
was introduced in
[
11
]
, where the authors show that if the fusion frame’s subspaces are large in number but small in dimension compared to the dimension of the underlying
space, then fusion frames will always exist, with each being a minimizer of the fusion frame potential. This topic is further explored in
[
16
]
; the authors
related this problem to the index of the Hadamard product by positive matrices and use it to give different characterizations of the minimization of the fusion frame potential. In
[
14
]
, the authors provide a description of the local minimizers which projections are eigenoperators of the fusion frame operator.

In this paper we study several ways of evaluating the cross correlation between two frames in the finite-dimensional case. The
cross frame potential
is defined as

P_{F}(G)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}|\langle f_{i},g_{j}\rangle|^{2}.

(1)

Observe that (
1
) is an adaptation of the classical frame potential function (De- finition
6
) and it takes the potential of one frame
F
with respect to another frame
G
(one of its duals; Example
20
points out that the cross frame potential of a frame with nonduals will sometimes attain unusual values). This function is minimized for the canonical dual (Section
3
).

Formula (
1
) is naturally related to the
cross-Gramian
of a frame
F
with its dual frame
G
,
Gr(F,G)=\left(\langle f_{i},g_{j}\rangle\right)_{i,j=1}^{k}
, which in general is not a symmetric matrix (Example
31
).
Note that the mixed-frame potential function
\tilde{FP}(F,G)
as studied in
[
13
]
measures how close a frame pair is to a biorthogonal system, and it coincides in value with (
1
) in a special case: In the real domain for frame pairs with symmetric cross Grammians (such as the frame pairs in Example
32
and Example
31
),

P_{F}(G)=\tilde{FP}(F,G)
when
Gr(F,G)^{T}=Gr(F,G)
,

which holds for the canonical dual (as in Proposition
17
). Thus, the smaller the value of (
1
) is, the closer the frame pair
(F,G)
is to a biorthogonal system.

Motivated by the various ways of assessing frame potential in
[
3
]
and what its value says about the quality of the frame at hand, we extend the ideas of
p-
th frame potential
and
exponential potential
to frame pairs in Subsection
3.1
and Section
4
, where we highlight the special values for the cross frame potential with the canonical dual. In Section
4
we also point to some unanswered questions regarding the off-diagonal maximal magnitude and state a couple of conjectures, which can be seen as generalizations of results on the Gramian
[
18
,
10
]
.

We introduce the notion of cross potential for fusion frames in Section
5
, where we generalize the idea of fusion frame potential as discussed in

[
11
]
, and we obtain exact computations of the cross potential for special types of fusion frame pairs.

Background

By
\mathbb{H}=\mathbb{F}^{n}
we denote a finite-dimensional Hilbert space (
\mathbb{R}^{n}
or
\mathbb{C}^{n}
).

Definition 1
.

A sequence of vectors
F=\{f_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
, with
k\geq n
, in an
n
-dimensional Hilbert space
\mathbb{H}
is a
frame for
\mathbb{H}
if there exist real constants
0<A\leq B<+\infty
such that

A\lVert f\rVert^{2}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}|\langle f,f_{i}\rangle|^{2}\leq B\lVert f\rVert^{2}

(2)

for every
f\in\mathbb{H}
.

A
is called the lower frame bound for
\{f_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
, and
B
is called the upper frame bound.

In a finite-dimensional space
\mathbb{H}
, frames are simply spanning sets of
\mathbb{H}
.
All bases are frames by definition, but frames in general are not bases.

Example 2
.

F=\Bigg\{\begin{bmatrix}0\\
1\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}1\\
1\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}-1\\
1\end{bmatrix}\Bigg\}
is a frame for
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, but not a basis.

Each frame has an associated analysis, synthesis, and frame operator.
Given a frame
F=\{f_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
for
\mathbb{F}^{n}
, the
analysis operator

\theta_{F}
is defined for every
f\in\mathbb{H}
by

(\theta_{F}f)_{i}=\langle f,f_{i}\rangle
; the matrix of
\theta_{F}
has the frame elements of
F
as its rows. The
synthesis operator
is the adjoint operator of
\theta_{F}
, indicated by
\theta^{*}_{F}
; its matrix has the elements of frame
F
as columns, and we will label this matrix by
F
for simplicity. The
frame operator
of
F=\{f_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
is defined as
S=\theta^{*}_{F}\theta_{F}
. It shows that the frame operator is a positive, self-adjoint, invertible operator.

In signal processing, a frame is used to compute the analysis coefficients, while a
dual frame
is used for synthesis, to reconstruct the signal (or the other way around).

Definition 3
.

Let
\{f_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
be a frame for
\mathbb{H}
. A
dual frame
for
\{f_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
is a frame
\{g_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
such that for every
f\in\mathbb{H}
,

f=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\langle f,g_{i}\rangle f_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\langle f,f_{i}\rangle g_{i}.

Thus, a frame
G=\{g_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
is a dual frame for
F
if
\theta_{F}^{*}\theta_{G}=\theta_{G}^{*}\theta_{F}
is the identity map on
\mathbb{H}
; that is,
FG^{T}=GF^{T}=I_{n\times n}
.

A frame can have many dual frames.
The
canonical dual frame
for a frame
F=\{f_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
for
\mathbb{H}
with frame operator
S
is the frame
\tilde{F}=\{S^{-1}f_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
. The frame operator for
\tilde{F}
is
S^{-1}
.
The canonical dual frame has a minimization property:

Lemma 4
.

Let
F=\{f_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
be a frame for
\mathbb{H}
, let
\tilde{F}=\{\tilde{f}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
be the canonical dual frame for
F
, and let
H=\{h_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
be a dual frame (not necessarily the canonical dual) for
F
. Then for every
f\in\mathbb{H}
,

\sum_{i=1}^{k}|\langle f,\tilde{f}_{i}\rangle|^{2}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}|\langle f,h_{i}\rangle|^{2},

with equality if and only if
H=\tilde{F}
.

The
cross-Gramian

Gr(F,G)=\theta_{F}\theta_{G}^{*}
is a bounded, idempotent operator on
\mathbb{F}^{n}
whenever
G
is a dual frame of
F
; it is self-adjoint if
G
is the canonical dual frame of
F
. The matrix of
Gr(F,G)
is
F^{T}G
; thus
Gr(F,G)^{*}=Gr(G,F)
.

A frame is called
tight
if
A=B
in Definition
1
. One useful feature of tight frames is that its frame operator
S=\theta^{*}\theta
is a scalar multiple of the identity operator, where that scalar is the frame bound
A=B
. Thus, the canonical dual frame of a tight frame
F=\{f_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
, is simply
\{\frac{1}{A}f_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
. When a frame
F
is tight, the representation of a vector
f
in
\mathbb{H}
depends only on
F
, that is, we only need
F
for the analysis and synthesis.

Example 5
.

F=\Bigg\{\begin{bmatrix}0\\
1\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}-\sqrt{3}/2\\
-1/2\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}\sqrt{3}/2\\
-1/2\end{bmatrix}\Bigg\}

is a tight frame for
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, with frame operator
S=\frac{3}{2}I_{2}
; its canonical dual is
\frac{2}{3}F
.

2.1
Frame potential

The frame potential of a finite sequence of vectors is a theoretical version of potential energy in physics developed in
[
2
]
and later studied in
[
3
,
9
,
16
]
. In physics, a system of objects acting under a force will move to minimize its potential energy.

Definition 6
.

The
frame potential
of a frame
F=\{f_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
for
\mathbb{H}
,
P_{F}
, is

P_{F}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}|\langle f_{i},f_{j}\rangle|^{2}.

The frame potential function can also be written as the trace of the square of the frame operator:
P_{F}=tr(S^{2}).

Proposition 7
.

Let
F=\{f_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
be a sequence of vectors in an n-dimensional Hilbert space
\mathbb{H}
with
\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lVert f_{i}\rVert^{2}=L
. Then we have

P_{F}\geq\frac{L^{2}}{n},

with equality if and only if
F
is a tight frame.

Note that the tight frame
F
from Example
5

has frame potential
P_{F}=9/2
which is the expected minimum value (as
L=1+1+1=3
). The frame
F
from Example
2
has frame potential
P_{F}=13
, which is greater than the minimum value
25/2
(as expected for non-tight frames).

Proposition 8
.

[
3
]

Let
G=Gr(F,F)
be the Grammian for a Parseval frame
F
for
\mathbb{F}^{n}
, consisting of
k
vectors. Then

\sum_{i=1}^{k}|G_{i,i}|^{2}\geq\frac{n^{2}}{k},

with equality if and only if
G_{i,i}=\frac{n}{k}
for each
1\leq i\leq k
.

Other ways to compute frame potential were explored in
[
3
]
:

Definition 9
.

The
p
-th frame potential of a frame
F=\{f_{j}\}_{j=1}^{k}
for an
n
-dimensional Hilbert space
\mathbb{H}
is given by

\phi_{p}(F)=\sum_{i,j=1}^{k}|\langle f_{i},f_{j}\rangle|^{2p}\;\text{ for some }\;p>0.

Proposition 10
.

[
3
]

If two frames
F
and
F^{\prime}
are unitarily equivalent, then the
p
-th frame potential of
F
is equal to the
p
-th frame potential of
F^{\prime}
.

Theorem 11
.

[
3
]
Let
F=\{f_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
be a frame for
\mathbb{F}^{n}
,
and
\lVert f_{i}\rVert^{2}=n/k
for all
i\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}
, and let
p>1
. Then

\phi_{p}(F)=\sum_{i,j=1}^{k}|\langle f_{i},f_{j}\rangle|^{2p}\geq\frac{n^{2p}(k-1)^{p-1}+n^{p}(k-n)^{p}}{(k-1)^{p-1}k^{2p-1}}

and equality holds if and only if
F
is an equiangular Parseval frame.

2.2
Fusion frames and
fusion frame potential

Fusion frames are a generalization of traditional frames that are well-suited for distributed data processing: breaking up the processing job into chunks that different computers execute at the same time and then combining the results; the reason for this is that fusion frames are sequences of subspaces instead of vectors. Because of this, we can think of fusion frames as frames made up of frames for the subspaces.

Definition 12
.

A sequence of closed subspaces
\{W_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
of an
n
-dimensional Hilbert space
\mathbb{H}
is a
fusion frame for
\mathbb{H}
if there exist constants
0<A\leq B<\infty
such that for every
f\in\mathbb{H}
it holds

A\lVert f\rVert^{2}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}{\lVert P_{i}f\rVert^{2}}\leq B\lVert f\rVert^{2},

where
P_{i}
is the orthogonal projection onto subspace
W_{i}
for every
i\in I=\{1,2,\ldots,k\}
. A fusion frame is called
tight
if
A=B
.

We can view a fusion frame
\{W_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
as a collection
\{P_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
of orthogonal projections onto the respective subspaces.
The fusion frame operator
S
of a frame
\{P_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
is the sum of the projections:

S=\sum_{i=1}^{k}{P_{i}}.

Given a finite sequence of subspaces
\{W_{i}\}_{i\in I}
of
\mathbb{H}
, if
\{f_{i}^{j}\}_{j\in J_{i}}
is a frame for subspace
W_{i}
for an index set
J_{i}
,
i\in I
, then the union of the vectors in every
\{f_{i}^{j}\}_{j\in J_{i}}
is a frame for
\mathbb{H}
if and only if
\{W_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
is a fusion frame for
\mathbb{H}

[
6
]
.

Example 13
.

Let
W_{1}=\{(x,y,0)^{T}|x,y\in\mathbb{R}\}
,
W_{2}=\{(x,y,y)^{T}|x,y\in\mathbb{R}\}
. Then
P=\{W_{1},W_{2}\}
is a fusion frame for
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. We can also view
P
as the sequence
\{P_{1},P_{2}\}
of orthogonal projections onto
W_{1}
and
W_{2}
, where

P_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&1&0\\
0&0&0\end{bmatrix},P_{2}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&\frac{1}{2}&\frac{1}{2}\\
0&\frac{1}{2}&\frac{1}{2}\end{bmatrix},\;\text{ while}\;S=P_{1}+P_{2}=\begin{bmatrix}2&0&0\\
0&\frac{3}{2}&\frac{1}{2}\\
0&\frac{1}{2}&\frac{1}{2}\end{bmatrix}

is the fusion frame operator for
P
. We can take any frame
F_{1}
for
W_{1}
and any frame
F_{2}
for
W_{2}
, and the union
F=F_{1}\cup F_{2}
will be a traditional frame for
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. For example, let

F_{1}=\Bigg\{\begin{bmatrix}1\\
0\\
0\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}0\\
1\\
0\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}2\\
-1\\
0\end{bmatrix}\Bigg\},F_{2}=\Bigg\{\begin{bmatrix}0\\
1\\
1\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}1\\
0\\
0\end{bmatrix}\Bigg\}.

Then
F_{1}\cup F_{2}
is a traditional frame for
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

Definition 14
.

[
11
]

The fusion frame potential of a fusion frame
P=\{P_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
, where
P_{i}
are orthogonal projections, is

FFP(P)=Tr(\sum_{i=1}^{k}{P_{i}})^{2}.

Equivalently,

FFP(P)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}Tr(P_{i}P_{j}).

Similarly to traditional frame potential, fusion frame potential is minimized exactly when the fusion frame is tight.

Proposition 15
.

[
11
]

Let
P=\{P_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
be a sequence of orthogonal projections
P_{i}:\mathbb{F}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{F}^{n}
with
Tr(P_{i})=L_{i}
, where
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{C}
. Then

FFP(P)\geq\frac{1}{n}(\sum_{i=1}^{k}L_{i})^{2},

with equality if and only if
\{P_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
is a tight fusion frame.

In Proposition
15
, each
L_{i}
is also the rank of
P_{i}
since the trace of an orthogonal projection is equal to its rank - which means:
L_{i}=\dim W_{i}
,
1\leq i\leq k
.

2.2.1
On computing the canonical dual of a fusion frame

Viewing a fusion frame
P
as a sequence of subspaces
\{W_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
, the canonical dual frame
Q
is
\{S^{-1}W_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
, where
S
is the fusion frame operator for
P
. This makes sense, since it is the same process we followed to compute the canonical dual of a traditional frame. However, if the sequence of orthogonal projections onto each subspace is
\{P_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
, then
\{S^{-1}P_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
will not necessarily give us a fusion frame, since the products
S^{-1}P_{i}
are not necessary orthogonal projections.

In practice, when we want to state the canonical dual fusion frame as a sequence of orthogonal projections
\{Q_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
, we first compute the subspaces
S^{-1}W_{i}
of
Q
and then compute the orthogonal projection operators
Q_{i}
for subspaces
S^{-1}W_{i}
.

3
Cross frame potential

We build on the notion of frame potential function (Definition
6
) to study the potential of one frame
with respect to
another frame. We call this new function the
cross frame potential
function.

Definition 16
.

Let
F=\{f_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
and
G=\{g_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
be frames for
\mathbb{F}^{n}
. The
cross frame potential

P_{F}(G)
of
G
with respect to
F
is

P_{F}(G)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}|\langle f_{i},g_{j}\rangle|^{2}.

(3)

By definition,
P_{F}(G)=P_{G}(F)
. Clearly, the cross-frame potential of a frame
F
with itself is just the frame potential of
F
as per Definition
6
.
For a Parseval frame
F
, Proposition
7
implies that
P_{F}(F)=L^{2}/n
, which is the minimal value of the frame potential function. As the canonical dual frame of a Parseval frame
F
is
F
, and its frame potential is the minimal value, then it makes sense to expect that the cross frame potential function
P_{F}(G)
for any frame
F
is minimized when the input frame
G
is its canonical dual:

Proposition 17
.

Let
F
be a frame for
\mathbb{F}^{n}
and let
\tilde{F}
be the canonical dual frame for
F
. Then the cross- frame potential of
\tilde{F}
with respect to
F
is equal to
n
. That is,

P_{F}(\tilde{F})=n.

Proof.

Let
G=Gr(F,\tilde{F})=\theta_{F}\theta_{\tilde{F}}^{*}
denote the cross-Grammian of
F
with
G
.
Observe that
P_{F}(\tilde{F})
is equal to the sum of the squared entries of
G
, and so

P_{F}(\tilde{F})=Tr(G^{*}G).

Since
\tilde{F}
is the canonical dual for
F
,
G
is self-adjoint, since
\theta_{\tilde{F}}^{*}=S^{-1}\theta_{F}^{*}
, where
S
is the frame operator for
F
, and so

G^{*}=(\theta_{F}\theta_{\tilde{F}}^{*})^{*}=\theta_{\tilde{F}}\theta_{F}^{*}=(S^{-1}\theta_{F}^{*})^{*}\theta_{F}^{*}=\theta_{F}(S^{-1})^{*}\theta_{F}^{*}=\theta_{F}S^{-1}\theta_{F}^{*}=\theta_{F}\theta_{\tilde{F}}^{*}=G,

as
S
is an invertible, self-adjoint operator. In addition, because
F
and
\tilde{F}
are dual to one another,
\theta_{\tilde{F}}^{*}\theta_{F}=I_{n}
, and so

G^{2}=(\theta_{F}\theta_{\tilde{F}}^{*})(\theta_{F}\theta_{\tilde{F}}^{*})=\theta_{F}(\theta_{\tilde{F}}^{*}\theta_{F})\theta_{\tilde{F}}^{*}=\theta_{F}I_{n}\theta_{\tilde{F}}^{*}=G.

(4)

Thus
G
is an idempotent matrix, and so its trace is equal to its rank. Therefore,

P_{F}(\tilde{F})=Tr(G^{*}G)=Tr(G)=rank(G).

(5)

Since
\theta_{\tilde{F}}^{*}
is an
n\times k
matrix of rank
n
(the columns of
\theta_{\tilde{F}}^{*}
form a frame for
\mathbb{F}^{n}
),
rank(\theta_{F}\theta_{\tilde{F}}^{*})=rank(\theta_{F})=n
, as the rows of
\theta_{F}
form a frame for
\mathbb{F}^{n}
. Thus
P_{F}(\tilde{F})=n
when
\tilde{F}
is the canonical dual frame for
F
.
∎

The cross-frame potential
P_{F}(H)
is minimized exactly when
H
is the canonical dual frame of
F
; an equivalent result can be found in
[
8
]
:

Theorem 18
.

Let
F
be a frame for
\mathbb{F}^{n}
, and let
H
be a dual frame for
F
. Then

P_{F}(H)\geq n,

(6)

with
P_{F}(H)=n
if and only if
H
is the canonical dual frame for
F
.

The proof of Thoerem
18
follows from Lemma
4
and Proposition
17
.

Example 19
.

The canonical dual frame
G
of the frame
F
in Example
2

is

G=\Bigg\{\begin{bmatrix}0\\
\frac{1}{3}\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}\frac{1}{2}\\
\frac{1}{3}\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}-\frac{1}{2}\\
\frac{1}{3}\end{bmatrix}\Bigg\},\;\text{while }\;H=\Bigg\{\begin{bmatrix}0\\
1\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}\frac{1}{2}\\
0\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}-\frac{1}{2}\\
0\end{bmatrix}\Bigg\}

is another dual frame of
F
.
Then
P_{F}(G)=2
, as expected, and
P_{F}(H)=4>2
.

The minimization result of Theorem
18
only holds when the domain of the cross potential function
P_{F}
is limited to dual frames for
F
. We give two examples of cross frame potential computations with non-dual frames that match or fall below the minimum value
n
:

Example 20
.

Let
F=\{f_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
be a frame for
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and let
G=\{g_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
be its canonical dual. Suppose
\langle f_{i},g_{1}\rangle\neq 0
for some
f_{i}
in
F
. Then the frame
H=\{\frac{g_{1}}{2},g_{2},...,g_{k}\}
is not a dual frame for
F
. In particular, we have

\displaystyle P_{F}(H)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\left(|\langle f_{i},\frac{1}{2}g_{1}\rangle|^{2}+\sum_{j=2}^{k}|\langle f_{i},g_{j}\rangle|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{i=1}^{k}|\langle f_{i},g_{1}\rangle|^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{j=2}^{k}|\langle f_{i},g_{j}\rangle|^{2}

\displaystyle<\sum_{i=1}^{k}|\langle f_{i},g_{1}\rangle|^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{j=2}^{k}|\langle f_{i},g_{j}\rangle|^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}|\langle f_{i},g_{j}\rangle|^{2}=P_{F}(G)=n.

So
P_{F}(H)<n
;
H
is not a dual frame for
F
by Theorem
18
.

Example 21
.

Let
F=\{f_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
be a frame for
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and let
G=\{g_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
be its canonical dual, with
g_{1}\neq 0
. Then
H=\{-g_{1},g_{2},...,g_{k}\}
is not a dual frame of
F
. Yet, we have

\displaystyle P_{F}(H)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\left(|\langle f_{i},-g_{1}\rangle|^{2}+\sum_{j=2}^{k}|\langle f_{i},g_{j}\rangle|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\left(|\langle f_{i},g_{1}\rangle|^{2}+\sum_{j=2}^{k}|\langle f_{i},g_{j}\rangle|^{2}\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}|\langle f_{i},g_{j}\rangle|^{2}=P_{F}(G)=n.

Since
P_{F}(H)=n
, we conclude that
H

Proposition 23

Let

Proof.

Let

and

be frames for
\mathbb{F}^{n}

\mathbb{F}^{n}

Proposition 24
.

Let
F=\{f_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
be a frame for
\mathbb{F}^{n}
, and let

be a dual frame for

Proof.

Applying Jensen’s inequality, we have

\sum_{i=1}^{k}|\langle f_{i},g_{i}\rangle|^{2}\geq\frac{(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\langle f_{i},g_{i}\rangle)^{2}}{k},

with equality if and only if there is some constant

As stated in the proof of Proposition

\sum_{i=1}^{k}|\langle f_{i},g_{i}\rangle|^{2}\geq\frac{n^{2}}{k},

4
Off-Diagonal Magnitude

Let
F=\{f_{1},\ldots,f_{k}\}
be a frame for
\mathbb{F}^{n}
, and let
H=\{h_{1},\ldots,h_{k}\}
be a dual frame for
F
. We denote the cross-Gramian of

and

by

and we denote the

of

as

\mu(Gr(F,H)):=\max_{i\neq j}|\langle f_{i},h_{j}\rangle|.

Example 31
.

The cross Gramians of the frame
F
from Example
2
with its canonical dual

and the other dual frame

as listed in Example

are

2&5&-1\\
2&-1&5\end{bmatrix},\;\;\;Gr(F,H)=\frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}2&0&0\\
2&1&-1\\
2&-1&1\end{bmatrix},

with

\mu(Gr(F,G))=\frac{1}{3}
, while
\mu(Gr(F,H))=1
. In fact, any dual frame
D
of
F
has frame elements:

\begin{bmatrix}x\\
y\end{bmatrix},\;\frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}{1-x}\\
{1-y}\end{bmatrix},\frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}{-1-x}\\
{1-y}\end{bmatrix},\;\text{for some real $x$, $y$};

the cross Gramian of
F
and any dual frame
D
is

Gr(F,D)=\frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}2y&1-y&1-y\\
2(x+y)&2-(x+y)&\ -x-y\\
2(y-x)&x-y&x-y+2\end{bmatrix}.

If for some
x
,
y
we have

, we’d require

\max\{|x+y|,|y-x|,\frac{|1-y|}{2}\}<\frac{1}{3}.

(9)

If both

and

then
-\frac{2}{3}<2y<\frac{2}{3}

. But also,

\frac{5}{3}>y>\frac{1}{3}
, which is impossible. Thus

\min\{\mu(Gr(F,D))\,|\,\text{ $D$ is a dual frame for $F$}\}=\frac{1}{3},

which we see happens with the canonical dual

\theta_{d}^{*}=\begin{bmatrix}0&\frac{1}{2}&-\frac{1}{2}\\
\frac{1}{3}&\frac{1}{3}&\frac{1}{3}\end{bmatrix}

form a dual frame

for

\mu(Gr(F,F_{d}))=\frac{1}{3}
.

of a given frame

]

4.1
Exponential Potential

The
exponential potential
of
G=Gr(F,H)
is mapping each pairing
(f_{i},h_{j})
into

E^{\eta}_{i,j}(G):=e^{\eta|\langle f_{i},h_{j}\rangle|^{2}}\;\text{for any }\;\eta>0.\color[rgb]{0,0,0}

In particular, the
off-diagonal sum potential
of
G
is

\Phi^{\eta}_{od}(G):=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}(1-\delta_{i,j})E^{\eta}_{i,j}(G),

which will identify a Grassmanian pair as its minimizer (Corollary
36
).

Proposition 35
.

Given a frame
F
for
\mathbb{F}^{n}
and a dual frame
H
of
F
, it holds

\mu(G)^{2}=\lim_{\eta\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{\eta}\ln\Phi^{\eta}_{od}(G).

(16)

Proof.

Let
F
be a frame for
\mathbb{F}^{n}
and let
H
be a dual frame of
F
. Observe that for the cross Gramian
G=Gr(F,H)
we have

\displaystyle e^{\eta\mu(G)^{2}}

\displaystyle=e^{\eta\max_{i\neq j}|\langle f_{i},h_{j}\rangle|^{2}}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}(1-\delta_{i,j})e^{\eta|\langle f_{i},h_{j}\rangle|^{2}}

\displaystyle=\Phi^{\eta}_{od}(G)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}(1-\delta_{i,j})e^{\eta\mu(G)^{2}}=k(k-1)e^{\eta\mu(G)^{2}}.

(17)

By (
4.1
) we have

\ln\left(e^{\eta\mu(G)^{2}}\right)\leq\ln\left(\Phi^{\eta}_{od}(G)\right)\leq\ln\left(k(k-1)e^{\eta\mu(G)^{2}}\right),

that is

\eta\mu(G)^{2}\leq\ln\left(\Phi^{\eta}_{od}(G)\right)\leq\ln\left(k(k-1)\right)+\ln\left(e^{\eta\mu(G)^{2}}\right),

so

\mu(G)^{2}\leq\frac{1}{\eta}\ln\left(\Phi^{\eta}_{od}(G)\right)\leq\frac{\ln\left(k(k-1)\right)}{\eta}+\mu(G)^{2}.

(18)

If we allow
\eta\rightarrow\infty
in (
18
), we get (
16
).
∎

Now, let
F
form a Grassmannian pair with a dual frame
\tilde{F}
, and let
H
be any other dual frame of
F
such that
(F,H)
is not a Grassmannian pair. We use the notations
G=G(F,\tilde{F})
and
G^{\prime}=G(F,H)
. By combining (
15
) and (
18
) with the assumption that
\eta>\ln(k(k-1))/\epsilon
,
we obtain

\mu(G)^{2}\leq\frac{1}{\eta}\ln\left(\Phi^{\eta}_{od}(G)\right)<\mu(G)^{2}+\epsilon=\mu(G^{\prime})^{2}.

(19)

By (
19
) and (
4.1
) we see

e^{\eta\mu(G)^{2}}\leq\Phi^{\eta}_{od}(G)<e^{\eta\mu(G^{\prime})^{2}}\leq\Phi^{\eta}_{od}(G^{\prime})
:

Corollary 36
.

If
F
is a frame for
\mathbb{F}^{n}
which forms a Grassmannian pair with a dual frame
\tilde{F}
, then for
\eta>0
large enough,

\Phi^{\eta}_{od}(G)<\Phi^{\eta}_{od}(G^{\prime}),

where
G=Gr(F,\tilde{F})
is the cross Gramian with the Grassmannian dual
\tilde{F}
of
F
, and
G^{\prime}
is a cross Gramian with any dual frame
H
of
F
such that
(F,H)
is not a Grassmannian pair.

Let

If a frame pair

If a frame

is covered by Lemma
33
. Now, suppose
\sum_{i=1}^{k}|\langle f_{i},g_{i}\rangle|^{2}>n^{2}/k
. Given that

n\leq P_{F}(G)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}|\langle f_{i},g_{i}\rangle|^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{j\neq i}|\langle f_{i},g_{j}\rangle|^{2},

we have two lower bounds for
P_{F}(G)
:

P_{F}(G)>\frac{n^{2}}{k}+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{j\neq i}|\langle f_{i},g_{j}\rangle|^{2}\;\text{ and }\;P_{F}(G)\geq n.

It is either that

\text{(a)}\;\;n>\frac{n^{2}}{k}+\sum_{j\neq i}|\langle f_{i},g_{j}\rangle|^{2},\;\text{or}\;\;\;\;\text{(b) }\;\;\frac{n^{2}}{k}+\sum_{j\neq i}|\langle f_{i},g_{j}\rangle|^{2}\geq n.\;\;\;

Note that in the examples we have added to this paper, it never happens that (a) is true.

Now, if (b) is true then we have
\displaystyle\sum_{j\neq i}|\langle f_{i},g_{j}\rangle|^{2}\geq n-\frac{n^{2}}{k}.

We take the maximum in each term in the last inequality and get

k(k-1)\mu^{2}(Gr(F,G))=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{j\neq i}\mu^{2}(Gr(F,G))=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{j\neq i}\max_{i\neq j}|\langle f_{i},g_{j}\rangle|^{2}\geq n-\frac{n^{2}}{k},

thus

\mu^{2}(Gr(F,G))\geq\frac{1}{k(k-1)}\left(n-\frac{n^{2}}{k}\right),

that is,
\mu^{2}(Gr(F,G))\geq\frac{n-n^{2}/k}{k(k-1)}
and inequality (
21
) follows.

Note that the frame in Example
32
forms an
exclusive
Grassmannian pair with its canonical dual, that is, its canonical dual is the only dual frame that satisfies (
14
), while the frame in Example
31
has at least two duals which satisfy (
14
). We conclude that only frames with special structure form exclusive Grassmannian pairs and we offer a conjecture:

Conjecture 42
.

If a frame
F
for
\mathbb{F}^{n}
forms an exclusive Grassmannian pair with one of its duals, then that dual must be the canonical dual frame of
F
.

Example
28
and Example
32
indicate the following to be true:

Conjecture 43
.

A frame
F
forms an exclusive Grassmannian pair with a dual frame
\tilde{F}
if and only if the frame pair
(F,\tilde{F})
is co-equidistributed. When this is true, the dual frame
\tilde{F}
is the canonical dual of
F
.

5
Cross fusion frame potential

We propose a definition for the cross fusion frame potential and compute its value for special classes of fusion frames.

Definition 44
.

Let
P=\{P_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
and
Q=\{Q_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
be fusion frames for
\mathbb{F}^{n}
, where
P_{i}
and
Q_{i}
are orthogonal projections. Then the cross fusion frame potential of
Q
with respect to
P
is

\phi(P,Q):=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}Tr(P_{i}Q_{j}).

The trace of a matrix product is independent of the order of the matrix product, thus
\phi(P,Q)=\phi(Q,P)
. Just as the fusion frame potential equals the trace of the square of the frame operator, here we have

\phi(P,Q)=Tr(S_{P}S_{Q}),

where
S_{P}
is the fusion frame operator for
P
and
S_{Q}
is the fusion frame operator for
Q
:

\displaystyle\phi(P,Q)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}Tr(P_{i}Q_{j})=Tr\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}P_{i}Q_{j}\right)=Tr\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}P_{i}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{k}Q_{j}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=Tr\left(\sum_{i=1}P_{i}S_{Q}\right)=Tr\left(\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}P_{i}\right)S_{Q}\right)=Tr(S_{P}S_{Q}).

Definition 45
.

Let
W_{1},W_{2}
be subspaces such that

i.

W_{1}\cap W_{2}\neq\emptyset

ii.

there exists a non-trivial subspace
V_{1}\subset W_{1}
such that
V_{1}
is orthogonal to
W_{2}
and

dim(W_{1}\cap W_{2})+dim(V_{1})=dim(W_{1})

iii.

there exists a non-trivial subspace
V_{2}\subset W_{2}
such that
V_{2}
is orthogonal to
W_{1}
and

dim(W_{1}\cap W_{2})+dim(V_{2})=dim(W_{2}).

Then we say that
W_{1}
and
W_{2}
are
semi-orthogonal subspaces
.

For instance, the coordinate planes
xy
and
yz
are semi-orthogonal in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
Observe that for two semi-orthogonal subspaces
W_{1}
and
W_{2}
we have:

P_{W_{1}}P_{W_{2}}W_{1}=P_{W_{2}}P_{W_{1}}W_{2}=W_{1}\cap W_{2}.

When a fusion frame
P
has special geometry, then
P
is its canonical dual, and we can specify more closely the value of the cross potential:

Theorem 46
.

Let
P=\{W_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
be a fusion frame for an
n
-dimensional Hilbert space
\mathbb{F}^{n}
such that for every
1\leq i,j\leq k
with
i\neq j
, one of the following conditions holds:

i.

W_{i}=W_{j}

ii.

W_{i}
and
W_{j}
are orthogonal to one another, or

iii.

W_{i}
and
W_{j}
are semi-orthogonal to one another.

Let
Q
be the canonical dual of
P
. Then
Q=P
and

\phi(P,Q)=FFP_{P}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}dim(W_{i}\cap W_{j}).

Proof.

Let
P
be as stated and let
Q
be its canonical dual. Consider some
W_{i}
in
P
. Let
F_{i}
be a frame for
W_{i}
, and let
f\in F_{i}
. We consider what happens when we apply each
P_{j}
to
f
(
P_{j}
is the orthogonal projection onto
W_{j}
in
P
).

If
W_{i}=W_{j}
, then
P_{j}f=f
. If
W_{i}
and
W_{j}
are orthogonal to one another, then
P_{j}f
is the zero vector. Finally, if
W_{i}
and
W_{j}
are semi-orthogonal to one another, then
P_{j}f
is equal to
f
if
f\in W_{j}
or the zero vector if
f\not\in W_{j}
. Thus, if
S=\{P_{j}\}_{j=1}^{k}
is the frame operator for
P
, then
Sf=mf
, where
m>0
is an integer. Then
S^{-1}f=\frac{1}{m}f
. Then
S^{-1}W_{i}=W_{i}
, and so
Q=P
.

Now let
P_{i}
be the orthogonal projection onto a subspace
W_{i}
in
P
, and consider the trace of each product
P_{i}P_{j}
, when
1\leq j\leq k
. If
W_{i}=W_{j}
, then
P_{i}P_{j}=P_{i}
, and
Tr(P_{i})=dim(W_{i})
. If
W_{i}
and
W_{j}
are orthogonal to one another, then
P_{i}P_{j}
contains only zeros, and so
Tr(P_{i}P_{j})=0
. Finally, if
W_{i}
and
W_{j}
are semi-orthogonal to one another, then
P_{i}P_{j}
is the orthogonal projection onto
W_{i}\cap W_{j}
, and so
Tr(P_{i}P_{j})=dim(W_{i}\cap W_{j})
. The result follows.
∎

A special class of the type of fusion frame described above are what are called
orthonormal fusion bases
for
\mathbb{F}^{n}
, where
\mathbb{F}^{n}
is an orthogonal sum of the subspaces
W_{i}
in the orthonormal fusion basis.

Corollary 47
.

Let
P=\{W_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
be an orthonormal fusion basis for
\mathbb{F}^{n}
, and let
Q
be the canonical dual for
P
. Then

\phi(P,Q)=n.

Proof.

By Theorem
46
, we know that the canonical dual
Q
of
P
is itself. Since
P
is an orthogonal fusion basis, whenever
i\neq j
, we have
dim(W_{i}\cap W_{j})=0
. Thus,
\phi(P,Q)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}dim(W_{i})
. Since
\mathbb{F}^{n}
is an orthogonal sum of the subspaces in
P
, the sum of the dimensions of the subspaces is equal to the dimension of the space,
n
.
∎

We give three examples of computing the cross potential of the canonical dual fusion frame. In the first example, the frame is an orthonormal fusion basis with itself as its canonical dual. In the second, the frame is not an orthonormal fusion basis, but it is a frame of the type described in Theorem
46
, and so its canonical dual is also itself. Finally, the frame in the third example is not of the type described in Theorem
46
, and so its canonical dual is not itself, and the cross potential value does not match the value in Theorem
46
.

Example 48
.

In
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, let
W_{1}
be the
xy
-plane, and let
W_{2}
be the
z
-axis. Then
P=\{W_{1},W_{2}\}
is an orthonormal fusion basis.

The corresponding orthogonal projections are

P_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&1&0\\
0&0&0\end{bmatrix},P_{2}=\begin{bmatrix}0&0&0\\
0&0&0\\
0&0&1\end{bmatrix}.

Then the fusion frame operator
S=P_{1}+P_{2}
is the identity operator
I
, and so
S^{-1}=I
. Thus
S^{-1}f_{i,j}=f_{i,j}
, where
f_{1,i}
is a frame element of any frame
F_{1}
for
W_{1}
, and
f_{2,i}
is a frame element of any frame
F_{2}
for
W_{2}
. Therefore, our canonical dual fusion frame
Q
for
P
is the same as
P
, and so

\phi(P,Q)=FFP(P)=3.

In the following example,
P
is not an orthonormal fusion basis, but it is of the structure stated in Theorem
46
, so
P
is still its own canonical dual, and the cross potential value will be as stated in Theorem
46
.

Example 49
.

In
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, let
W_{1}
be the
xy
-plane, and let
W_{2}
be the plane given by the equation
x+y=0
. Then
P=\{W_{1},W_{2}\}
is a fusion frame for
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. The corresponding orthogonal projections are

P_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&1&0\\
0&0&0\end{bmatrix},P_{2}=\begin{bmatrix}1/2&-1/2&0\\
-1/2&1/2&0\\
0&0&1\end{bmatrix}.

The fusion frame operator for
P
is

S=P_{1}+P_{2}=\begin{bmatrix}3/2&-1/2&0\\
-1/2&3/2&0\\
0&0&1\end{bmatrix},\;\text{and so}\;S^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}3/4&1/4&0\\
1/4&3/4&0\\
0&0&1\end{bmatrix}.

We now create bases for each
W_{i}
:

F_{1}=\Bigg\{\begin{bmatrix}1\\
1\\
0\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}1\\
-1\\
0\end{bmatrix}\Bigg\},F_{2}=\Bigg\{\begin{bmatrix}1\\
-1\\
0\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}0\\
0\\
1\end{bmatrix}\Bigg\}.

We apply
S^{-1}
to each vector in
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
to get frames
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
for our canonical dual subspaces:

G_{1}=\Bigg\{\begin{bmatrix}1\\
1\\
0\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}1/2\\
-1/2\\
0\end{bmatrix}\Bigg\},G_{2}=\Bigg\{\begin{bmatrix}1/2\\
-1/2\\
0\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}0\\
0\\
1\end{bmatrix}\Bigg\}.

G_{1}
spans the
xy
-plane, and
G_{2}
spans the plane
x+y=0
, so our canonical dual fusion frame
Q
for
P
is the same as
P
. Thus
\phi(P,Q)=FFP_{P}(P)=6
.

Note that while the cross potential and regular potential share the same value in this example, that value is not the minimal value for
FFP(P)
, which is
\frac{1}{n}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}L_{i}\right)^{2}=\frac{1}{3}(2+2)^{2}=\frac{16}{3}
. This tells us that
P
is non-tight.

Finally, we give an example of a fusion frame where Theorem
46
does not apply. Here the canonical dual of our fusion frame
P
is
not
equal to
P
.

Example 50
.

In
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, let
W_{1}
be the
xy
-plane, and let
W_{2}
be the plane
y=z
. Then
P=\{W_{1},W_{2}\}
is a fusion frame, and the corresponding orthogonal projections are:

P_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&1&0\\
0&0&0\end{bmatrix},P_{2}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&1/2&1/2\\
0&1/2&1/2\end{bmatrix}.

Then our fusion frame operator and its inverse are:

S=P_{1}+P_{2}=\begin{bmatrix}2&0&0\\
0&3/2&1/2\\
0&1/2&1/2\end{bmatrix},\;\;S^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}1/2&0&0\\
0&1&-1\\
0&-1&3\end{bmatrix}.

We now choose bases for our subspaces:

F_{1}=\Bigg\{\begin{bmatrix}1\\
0\\
0\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}0\\
1\\
0\end{bmatrix}\Bigg\},F_{2}=\Bigg\{\begin{bmatrix}0\\
1\\
1\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}1\\
0\\
0\end{bmatrix}\Bigg\}.

Then we apply
S^{-1}
to each of the vectors in our frames to obtain frames
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
for the subspaces of our canonical dual:

G_{1}=\Bigg\{\begin{bmatrix}1/2\\
0\\
0\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}0\\
1\\
-1\end{bmatrix}\Bigg\},G_{2}=\Bigg\{\begin{bmatrix}0\\
0\\
2\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}1/2\\
0\\
0\end{bmatrix}\Bigg\}.

G_{1}
spans the plane
y+z=0
, and
G_{2}
spans the plane
y=0
, so the orthogonal projections of our canonical dual frame
Q
are:

Q_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&1/2&-1/2\\
0&-1/2&1/2\end{bmatrix},Q_{2}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&0&0\\

Then
\phi(P,Q)=5
. Note that
FFP(P)=7
, and the minimum value of
FFP(P)
for a fusion frame for
\mathbb{R}^{3}
with 2 subspaces of dimension
2
is
16/3
. Thus the cross potential of
Q
with respect to
P
is not equal to the frame potential of

by itself, and the cross potential is less than the minimum frame potential value.

Proposition 51
.

Let
P=\{W_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
and
Q=\{V_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
be fusion frames for
\mathbb{F}^{n}
, and let
U:\mathbb{F}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{F}^{n}
be a unitary or orthogonal operator. Then

UP=\{UW_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
and
UQ=\{UV_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}

are also fusion frames for
\mathbb{F}^{n}
, and
\phi(P,Q)=\phi(UP,UQ)
.

Proof.

Let
\{P_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
be the associated orthogonal projections for
P
, and let
\{Q_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
be the associated orthogonal projections for
Q
. Fix
1\leq i,j\leq k
. Then
P_{i}
can be written as
AA^{*}
, where
A
is a matrix whose columns form an orthonormal basis for
W_{i}
. Then the columns of
UA
will form an orthonormal basis for
UW_{i}
, since
U
preserves inner products. Thus the orthogonal projection in
\mathbb{F}^{n}
onto
UW_{i}
will be
(UA)(UA)^{*}=UP_{i}U^{*}
. Similarly, the orthogonal projection onto
V_{j}
will be
(UB)(UB)^{*}=UQ_{i}U^{*}
, where
B
is a matrix whose columns form an orthonormal basis for
V_{j}
.

First we show that
UP
is a fusion frame. Let
0<A,B<\infty
be constants that satisfy the inequality in Definition
12
for
P
, and let
f\in\mathbb{F}^{n}
. Then since
U^{*}f\in\mathbb{F}^{n}
,

A\lVert U^{*}f\rVert^{2}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lVert P_{i}U^{*}f\rVert^{2}\leq B\lVert U^{*}f\rVert^{2}.

As
U
and
U^{*}
preserve inner products:

A\lVert f\rVert^{2}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lVert UP_{i}U^{*}f\rVert^{2}\leq B\lVert f\rVert^{2}.

Since
UP_{i}U^{*}
is the orthogonal projection onto
UW_{i}
, we know that
UP
is a fusion frame. Similarly, we can show that
UQ
is a fusion frame.

Now consider the product between the orthogonal projections onto
UW_{i}
and
UV_{j}
:

(UP_{i}U^{*})(UQ_{j}U^{*})=UP_{i}(U^{*}U)Q_{j}U^{*}=UP_{i}Q_{j}U^{*}.

\displaystyle\text{Then}\;\;Tr((UP_{i}U^{*})(UQ_{j}U^{*}))

\displaystyle=Tr((UP_{i})(Q_{j}U^{*}))

\displaystyle=Tr((Q_{j}U^{*})(UP_{i}))=Tr(Q_{j}P_{i})=Tr(P_{i}Q_{j}),

and so the cross-frame potential values will be equivalent.
∎
