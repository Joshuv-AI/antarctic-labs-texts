Local Borsuk-Ulam, Stability, and Replicability
Zachary Chase, Bogdan Chornomaz, Shay Moran, Amir Yehudayoff

Abstract
We use and adapt the Borsuk-Ulam Theorem from topology to derive limitations on list-replicable and globally stable learning algorithms. We further demonstrate the applicability of our methods in combinatorics and topology. We show that, besides trivial cases, both list-replicable and globally stable learning are impossible in the agnostic PAC setting. This is in contrast with the realizable case where it is known that any class with a finite Littlestone dimension can be learned by such algorithms. In the realizable PAC setting, we sharpen previous impossibility results and broaden their scope. Specifically, we establish optimal bounds for list replicability and global stability numbers in finite classes. This provides an exponential improvement over previous works and implies an exponential separation from the Littlestone dimension. We further introduce lower bounds for weak learners, i.e., learners that are only marginally better than random guessing. Lower bounds from previous works apply only to stronger learners. To offer a broader and more comprehensive view of our topological approach, we prove a local variant of the Borsuk-Ulam theorem in topology and a result in combinatorics concerning Kneser colorings. In combinatorics, we prove that if c c is a coloring of all non-empty subsets of [ n ] [n] such that disjoint sets have different colors, then there is a chain of subsets that receives at least 1 + ⌊ n / 2 ⌋ 1+\lfloor n/2\rfloor colors (this bound is sharp). In topology, we prove e.g. that for any open antipodal-free cover of the d d -dimensional sphere, there is a point x x that belongs to at least t = ⌈ d + 3 2 ⌉ t=\lceil\frac{d+3}{2}\rceil sets.

Introduction

Topology is a field in mathematics that studies the properties of spaces that remain unchanged under continuous transformations, such as convergence, compactness, and connectedness. By examining the invariants of a space under continuous transformations, topology offers a lens through which the intrinsic structure of the space can be measured and understood. Topology brings important ideas to many areas of science, and
even though it focuses on the continuous, topology has furnished critical tools
even in discrete fields, like computer science and combinatorics.
In some sub-areas—like computational geometry—the contribution of topology is quite natural.
In some other sub-areas—like distributed computing, decision tree complexity and communication complexity— the contribution is more surprising.
A partial list of related papers includes
(
Monsky 1970
;
Lovász 1978
;
Alon 1987
;
Kahn et al. 1984
;
Smale 1987
;
Chaudhuri 1993
;
Borowsky and Gafni 1993
;
Herlihy and Shavit 1999
;
Saks and Zaharoglou 1993
;
Scheidweiler and Triesch 2013
;
Hatami et al. 2023
)
. For more details, we refer readers to the survey by
Björner 1996
and the book by
Matoušek 2003
.

One of the most applicable results in topology is
the Borsuk-Ulam (BU) theorem
(
Borsuk 1933
)
.
It is an extension of the intermediate point theorem to high dimensional space.
It deals with continuous ways to map
the
n
-dimensional sphere
\mathbb{S}^{n}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n+1}

into Euclidean space.
It says that mapping a sphere into a low
dimensional space leads to a collision
between antipodal points. Namely,
for every continuous function
f:\mathbb{S}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
, there is a point
x
in
\mathbb{S}^{n}
such that
f(x)=f(-x)
.

In this work, we adapt the Borsuk-Ulam theorem to establish limitations of replicable and globally stable learning algorithms. To give a broader perspective of our topological approach, and to demonstrate its applicability, we apply it to derive a local variant of Borsuk-Ulam and a variant of the Lovás-Kneser theorem in combinatorics. For convenience, our main results are denoted with letters:
A
to
E
. All other theorems and claims are numbered.

Replicable Learning.

Replicability is a foundational principle of the scientific method. A scientific study is deemed replicable if it consistently produces similar results when repeated under comparable conditions, even with new datasets. The exploration of replicable learning was initiated by
Impagliazzo et al. 2022
and further developed by
Bun et al. 2023
;
Kalavasis et al. 2023
;
Chase et al. 2023
;
Dixon et al. 2023

Chase et al. 2023

•

We first investigate the feasibility of globally stable and list-replicable algorithms in the agnostic PAC learning setting.
Our findings indicate that, besides trivial cases, both list-replicable and globally stable learning are unattainable (
C
). This sharply contrasts with the realizable PAC setting, where every class with a finite Littlestone dimension can be learned by such algorithms. Further amplifying this contrast is the fact that for other notions of algorithmic stability, such as differential privacy,
there is an equivalence between realizable and agnostic PAC learning.

To circumvent the above impossibility result, we propose relaxed variants of agnostic list-replicability in which the size of the list can depend on the desired accuracy (
Definition

7
and
Definition

8
).

•

In the realizable PAC setting we derive impossibility results, offering significant quantitative improvements over prior work in terms of accuracy and list-replicability numbers.

We show that the both the VC and dual VC dimensions lower bound the optimal list size achievable by weak learners, i.e. whose error is
<1/2
, only strictly better than a random guess (
D
). This improves upon previous bounds by
Chase et al. 2023
;
Dixon et al. 2023
that apply to stronger learners whose error is
O(1/d)
, where
d
is the VC dimension.

We also derive an optimal bound for the list-replicability and global stability numbers of finite classes (
E
).
This yields an exponential improvement over
Chase et al. 2023
.
We further use this bound to deduce that for every
d
there is a class with Littlestone dimension
d
for which the list-replicability number is at least exponential in
d
(
9
); this nearly matches an upper bound by
Ghazi et al. 2021
.

Combinatorics and Topology.

We believe that our adaptation of BU might find additional applications in theoretical computer science. Thus,
to provide a more comprehensive understanding of our technique and to illustrate its potential,
we apply it to derive two additional results:

•

A local variant of the classical Borsuk-Ulam theorem in topology.
We prove that for any open (or finite closed) cover of the
d
-dimensional sphere that is antipodal-free, there exists a point
x
included in at least
t=\lceil\frac{d+3}{2}\rceil
sets. Moreover, if such a cover consists of both open and closed sets (but only of such sets) then there is a point
x
included in a minimum of
\lceil t/2\rceil
sets. Somewhat surprisingly, both of these bounds are shown to be sharp (see
A
).

•

A combinatorial result related to Kneser colorings, which is inspired by the first demonstration of the topological method in combinatorics by
Lovász 1978
.
We prove that if
c
is a coloring of non-empty subsets of
[n]
such that disjoint sets receive different colors, then there exists a chain of subsets that is assigned at least
1+\lfloor n/2\rfloor
colors. This bound is sharp as witnessed by assigning some color
i\in A
to every nonempty subset
A\subseteq[n]
of size at most
n/2
, and a separate color
c
to all subsets of
[n]
of size greater than
n/2
(see
B
).

2
Main Results

To provide a streamlined progression of our topological approach, we begin with our results in topology and combinatorics. Thereafter, we discuss our results in learning theory. Readers particularly interested in the learning-theoretic results may choose to skip directly to that section.

2.1
Topology

The first result we present is a variant of Borsuk-Ulam theorem that demonstrates the local nature of our approach.
This variant is most similar to the Lusternik-Shcnirelmann (LS) theorem, which is one of the equivalent formulations of Borsuk-Ulam
(
Lyusternik and Shnirel’man 1930
)
.
1
1

1

In fact, Lusternik-Shcnirelmann proved this variant in 1930, three years before Borsuk’s publication.

Let
\mathbb{S}^{d}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}:\|x\|_{2}=1\}
denote the
d
-dimensional sphere. We say that
A\subseteq\mathbb{S}^{d}
is antipodal-free if
A\cap(-A)=\emptyset
, where
-A=\{-x:x\in A\}
.
We say that a family
\mathcal{F}
of (distinct) sets is antipodal-free if each
A\in\mathcal{F}
is antipodal-free.
We say that
\mathcal{F}
covers
\mathbb{S}^{d}
if every
x\in\mathbb{S}^{d}
belongs to some
F\in\mathcal{F}
.

Theorem 1

(
Lyusternik and Shnirel’man 1930
)
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a antipodal-free cover of the
d
-dimensional sphere
\mathbb{S}^{d}
such that
every set
A\in\mathcal{F}
is either open or closed. Then,
\lvert\mathcal{F}\rvert\geq d+2
.
This is sharp—there is such a cover of size
d+2
.

Theorem
1
says that to cover the sphere by open antipodal-free sets, we must use many sets. In the variant we develop, we imagine a large family of open antipodal-free sets
\mathcal{F}
that cover the sphere. So, the bound
|\mathcal{F}|\geq d+2
trivially holds.

Is there some local behavior that must hold? Can it be that every point in the sphere is covered only once?
Thinking of the circle
\mathbb{S}^{1}
, it seems that some points must be covered at least twice.
What happens in the
2
-dimensional sphere? Can every point be covered twice?

Definition 1

(Overlap-degree)
.

Define the
overlap-degree
of a family
\mathcal{F}
of sets as the maximal integer
k

such that there exist
k
sets
A_{1},\ldots,A_{k}\in\mathcal{F}
that overlap, i.e.
\cap_{i=1}^{k}A_{i}\neq\emptyset
.

Theorem A

(local LS)
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a finite antipodal-free cover of the
d
-dimensional sphere
\mathbb{S}^{d}
.

1.

If all sets in
\mathcal{F}
are open then
the overlap-degree of
\mathcal{F}
is at least
t:=\lceil(d+3)/2\rceil
.

2.

If all sets in
\mathcal{F}
are closed then
the overlap-degree of
\mathcal{F}
is at least
t
.

3.

If all sets in
\mathcal{F}
are either open or closed then the overlap-degree of
\mathcal{F}
is at least
\lceil t/2\rceil
.

All three bounds above are sharp

(
for the third item, there is an antipodal-free
cover with some sets open and some sets closed
with overlap-degree
\lceil t/2\rceil)
.

In other words, if we try to cover the sphere with a finite number of open antipodal-free sets, then somewhere in the sphere
there must be a large overlap between the sets.
As a curious observation, let us note that
the bound provided by the global LS theorem does not change if the sets in the cover are allowed to be either open or closed. The bound in the local version, however, drops by a factor of
\approx 2
.
As a simple example, the 1-dimensional circle can be partitioned into four sets, each of which is open or closed
by taking two disjoint open half-circles and two (closed) singletons.

Our proof of Theorem
A
relies on the BU theorem and a theorem by
Jaworowski 2000
.
The sharpness is proved by two constructions
that are defined using barycentric sub-divisions of the simplex (the construction for the closed case immediately implies a construction for the open case). The second construction demonstrating the tightness of
\lceil t/2\rceil
is more intricate. It involves applying two consecutive barycentric sub-divisions.

A slightly weaker bound in the special case when all the sets in the cover are open (or all the sets in the cover are closed) follows from a generalization of the BU that was proven by
Fan 1952
. Fan’s theorem is a generalization of Theorem
1
which asserts the following. Let
A_{1},\ldots,A_{m}
be an antipodal-free open (or closed) cover of the sphere
\mathbb{S}^{d}
. Then, there are indices
i_{1}<i_{2}<\ldots<i_{d+2}
and a point
x\in\mathbb{S}^{d}
such that
(-1)^{j}x\in A_{i_{j}}
for all
j=1,2,\ldots d+2
.
It follows that either
x
or
-x
belongs to at least
\lceil(d+2)/2\rceil
sets.
For some values of
d
, the bound
from Fan’s theorem is off by a
-1
from the true bound (on the overlap-degree of the cover).

Remark 2
.

Theorem
1
and Theorem
A
are valid and typically used in a more general setup.
The sphere
\mathbb{S}^{d}
can be replaced by any topological space
that is homeomorphic to it, and the negation
x\mapsto-x
can be replaced by a general continuous
involution
\nu
(that is,
\nu^{2}
is the identity map).
In this generality,
a set
A
is considered antipodal-free if
A\cap\nu(A)=\emptyset,
where
\nu(A)=\{\nu(x):x\in A\}
.
We assume this general setup throughout the paper when we use these theorems.

We finish this section by proposing a possible direction for future research.
It might be interesting to seek a local counterpart of the BU theorem (and not the LS theorem).
For example, consider a continuous
f:\mathbb{S}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
f(x)\neq f(-x)
for all
x\in\mathbb{S}^{d}
.
Is there always a point
f(x)
in the image of
f
that has a large local dimension?
By large local dimension we mean that every open neighborhood
U\subseteq\mathtt{Image}(f)
of
f(x)
has large dimension.
There are, of course, several reasonable options for a definition for “dimension of
U
”.

2.2
Combinatorics

We now present an application in combinatorics inspired by the celebrated Lovász-Kneser theorem
(
Lovász 1978
)
.
A Kneser coloring
c
assigns to every non-empty subset
A\subseteq[n]
a color
c(A)\in\mathbb{N}
such that if
A\cap B=\emptyset

then

c(A)\neq c(B)
.
Denote by
{[n]\choose k}
the set of all
k
-element subsets of
[n]
.

Theorem 2

(Lovász-Kneser)
.

Let
n
and
k
be positive integers with
n\geq 2k
. Any Kneser coloring of the sets in
{[n]\choose k}
requires at least
n-2k+2
distinct colors. This bound is optimal—there exists a Kneser coloring using precisely
n-2k+2
colors on the sets in
{[n]\choose k}
.

Lovász’s proof for the lower bound in Theorem
2
utilizies the BU theorem, and is often regarded as pioneering the topological methods in combinatorics. We refer the reader to Chapter 3.3 of
Matoušek 2003
for a short proof of Theorem
2
using Theorem
1
.

We prove a related theorem using the local BU approach.
The Lovász-Kneser theorem says that for every Kneser coloring, every antichain has many colors.
We prove that for every Kneser coloring, there is a chain with many colors.

Theorem B

(Colorful chains)
.

For any positive integer
n
and a Kneser coloring
c
of non-empty subsets of
[n]
, there exists a chain of subsets that receives at least
\lfloor n/2\rfloor+1
distinct colors. That is,
there are
n
distinct sets
A_{1}\subset A_{2}\subset\ldots\subset A_{n}\subseteq[n]

so that
c
has
\lfloor n/2\rfloor+1
many colors
on them.
This bound is sharp—there are Kneser colorings that assign at most
\lfloor n/2\rfloor+1
colors to all chains.

The upper bound in Theorem
B
is simple. Color every nonempty subset
A\subseteq[n]
of size at most
n/2
by some color
i\in A
, and assign a separate color to all subsets of
[n]
of size greater than
n/2
.
This coloring assigns no more than
\lfloor n/2\rfloor+1
colors to any chain. Moreover, the total number of colors in this construction is
n+1
, which almost matches the trivial lower bound
n
on the number of colors of any Kneser coloring.
The proof of the lower bound is more demanding,
and follows similar lines to the proof of
Theorem
A
.

We conclude this section by posing a question for future research.

Question 3
.

For
p\leq n
, a
p
-Kneser coloring
c
assigns a color
c(A)
to every non-empty subset
A\subseteq[n]
such that whenever
p
distinct sets
A_{1},\ldots,A_{p}
have the same color, their intersection
\cap_{i=1}^{p}A_{i}
is non-empty. A
2
-Kneser coloring is simply a Kneser coloring. Consider a
p
-Kneser coloring which assigns a color
i\in A
to each subset
A\subseteq[n]
of size at most
n(1-1/p)
and a distinct color to subsets of
[n]
larger than this size. This way each chain receives at most
\lfloor n\cdot(1-1/p)\rfloor+1
colors. Theorem
B
establishes this is optimal for
p=2
. Is this the case for all
p\leq n
?

2.3
Learning Theory

In this section, we outline our findings in learning theory.
We use standard notation from learning theory; we refer the reader to the book by
Shalev-Shwartz and Ben-David 2014
for a detailed introduction.
Let
X
be a set called the domain and let
Y=\{\pm 1\}
denote the label set. A hypothesis/concept/classifier is a mapping
h:X\to Y
.
A concept class is a set of hypotheses
\mathcal{H}\subseteq Y^{X}
. An example is an ordered pair
z=(x,y)\in X\times Y
. A sample
S
is a finite sequence
S=(z_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
of examples.

A classification problem is defined by a distribution
\mathcal{D}
over examples. The learner does not know
\mathcal{D}
, but is able to collect a sample
S
of i.i.d. examples from
\mathcal{D}

which she uses to build a classifier
h\colon X\to Y
.
Formally, a learning rule is a (possibly randomized) mapping
\mathcal{A}:(X\times Y)^{\star}\to Y^{X}
, where
(X\times Y)^{*}
is the set of all samples (i.e. finite sequences of examples).
The population loss of an hypothesis
h
with respect to a distribution
\mathcal{D}
, denoted
L_{\mathcal{D}}(h)
, is the probability that
h
misclassifies a random example from
\mathcal{D}
, namely

\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}(h)=\Pr_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}}[h(x)\neq y]
. The empirical loss of an hypothesis
h
with respect to a sample
S
, denoted
L_{S}(h)
, is the fraction of examples in
S
that are misclassified by
h
, namely
\mathcal{L}_{S}(h)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}1[h(x_{i})\neq y_{i}]
, where
S=(x_{i},y_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
.

Any effective theory of learning should somehow
restrict the generality we are operating in.
A natural option is to set some limitations on the data distribution
\mathcal{D}
. The PAC model proposes
to use an underlying concept class
\mathcal{H}\subseteq Y^{X}
,
which helps the analyst to reason about the problem.
The simplest assumption is that
\mathcal{D}
is
realizable
,
that
\mathcal{H}
contains hypotheses with arbitrarily small error (i.e.
\inf_{h\in\mathcal{H}}L_{\mathcal{D}}(h)=0
).
Another well-studied setting is the
agnostic
one in which we no longer assume that
\mathcal{D}
is realizable,
and accordingly we only require the learning rule to output hypotheses whose loss is competitive with
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathcal{H}):=\inf_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}(h)
.

In both settings, the class
\mathcal{H}
is used by the analyst to make algorithmic choices.
In the realizable setting, the analyst
assumes that the data comes from the class
\mathcal{H}
.
In the agnostic setting, the analyst does not assume anything on the input data,
and the class
\mathcal{H}
is thought of as the criterion of success; the output should be as good as any function in
\mathcal{H}
.

Replicability and Stability.

Replicability is a foundational principle of the scientific method. A scientific study is deemed replicable if it consistently produces similar results when repeated under comparable conditions, even with new datasets.

Replicability and the related concept of global stability (formally defined below) have been recently introduced and studied within the context of machine learning theory. Global stability was first introduced by
Bun et al. 2020
and was employed as an algorithmic tool for designing privacy-preserving learning rules by both
Bun et al. 2020
and
Ghazi et al. 2021
. The exploration of replicability in PAC learning began with the work of
Impagliazzo et al. 2022
.
Chase et al. 2023
studied global stability as a form of replicability and proved its equivalence with list-replicability, a concept introduced concurrently by
Dixon et al. 2023
.
Dixon et al. 2023
also defined the notion of
strong replicability
which is equivalent to global stability.

A learning rule
\mathcal{A}
is called a
(\rho,\varepsilon)
-globally stable learner
for
\mathcal{H}
if there exists
n
so that for every distribution
\mathcal{D}
that is realizable by
\mathcal{H}
, there exists a predictor
h=h_{\mathcal{D}}
such that
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}(h)<\varepsilon
and

\Pr_{S\sim\mathcal{D}^{n}}[\mathcal{A}(S)=h]\geq\rho.

The rule
\mathcal{A}
is called an agnostic
(\rho,\varepsilon)
-globally stable learner
for
\mathcal{H}
if there exists
n=n(\rho,\varepsilon)
so that for every distribution
\mathcal{D}
there exists a predictor
h=h_{\mathcal{D}}
such that
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}(h)<\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathcal{H})+\varepsilon
and

\Pr_{S\sim\mathcal{D}^{n}}[\mathcal{A}(S)=h]\geq\rho.

The class
\mathcal{H}
is
learnable
with global stability number
\rho

if for every
\varepsilon>0
there exists an
(\varepsilon,\rho)
-globally stable learner for
\mathcal{H}
.
Similarly,
\mathcal{H}
is
agnostic
learnable with global stability number
\rho
if
for every
\varepsilon>0
there exists an agnostic
(\varepsilon,\rho)
-globally stable learner for
\mathcal{H}
.

Definition 4

(Global stability number)
.

The global stability number
\rho(\mathcal{H})
of the class
\mathcal{H}
is the supremum over all
\rho\in[0,1]
for which
\mathcal{H}
is learnable with global stability number
\rho
.
We say that
\mathcal{H}
is globally stable learnable if
\rho(\mathcal{H})>0
.
The agnostic global stability parameter
\rho_{\mathtt{agn}}(\mathcal{H})
is defined analogously.
We say that
\mathcal{H}
is agnostically globally stable learnable if
\rho_{\mathtt{agn}}(\mathcal{H})>0
.

Notice that if
\mathcal{H}
is agnostically
globally stable learnable then it is also globally stable learnable and that
0\leq\rho_{\mathtt{agn}}(\mathcal{H})\leq\rho(\mathcal{H})
.

A learning rule
\mathcal{A}
is called
(\varepsilon,L)
-list replicable learner for
\mathcal{H}

if for every
\delta>0
, there exists
n=n(\varepsilon,L,\delta)
such that for every distribution
\mathcal{D}
that is realizable by
\mathcal{H}
,
there exist hypotheses
h_{1},h_{2},\ldots,h_{L}
such that

\Pr_{S\sim\mathcal{D}^{n}}[\mathcal{A}(S)\in\{h_{1},\ldots,h_{L}\}]\geq 1-\delta

and
for all
\ell\in[L]
,

\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}(h_{\ell})<\varepsilon.

The rule
\mathcal{A}
is called
(\varepsilon,L)
-list replicable
agnostic
learner for the class
\mathcal{H}

if for every
\delta>0
, there exists
n=n(\varepsilon,L,\delta)
such that for every distribution
\mathcal{D}
there exist hypotheses
h_{1},h_{2},\ldots,h_{L}
such that

\Pr_{S\sim\mathcal{D}^{n}}[\mathcal{A}(S)\in\{h_{1},\ldots,h_{L}\}]\geq 1-\delta
and
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}(h_{\ell})\leq\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathcal{H})+\varepsilon

for all
\ell\in[L]
.
We say that
\mathcal{H}
is learnable with replicability list size
L

if for every
\varepsilon>0
, there exists an
(\varepsilon,L)
-list replicable learner for
\mathcal{H}
.
The definition of
agnostically
learnable with replicability list size
L
is analogous.

Definition 5

(List replicability number)
.

The list replicability number of
\mathcal{H}
is defined as

\mathtt{LR}(\mathcal{H}):=\min\bigl\{L\in\mathbb{N}:\text{$\mathcal{H}$ is learnable with replicability list size $L$}\bigr\}.

We say that
\mathcal{H}
is replicable list learnable if
\mathtt{LR}(\mathcal{H})<\infty
.
The agnostic list replicability number
\mathtt{LR}_{\mathtt{agn}}(\mathcal{H})
is defined analogously.
We say that
\mathcal{H}
is agnostically replicable list learnable if
\mathtt{LR}_{\mathtt{agn}}(\mathcal{H})<\infty
.

Observe that
\mathtt{LR}(\mathcal{H})\leq\mathtt{LR}_{\mathtt{agn}}(\mathcal{H})\leq\infty
and thus if
\mathcal{H}
is agnostically replicable list learnable then it is also replicable list learnable.

Theorem 3

(
Chase et al. 2023
)
.

For every class
\mathcal{H}
,

\mathtt{LR}(\mathcal{H})\cdot\rho(H)=1\quad\text{and}\quad\mathtt{LR}_{\mathtt{agn}}(\mathcal{H})\cdot\rho_{\mathtt{agn}}(H)=1.

In particular,
\mathcal{H}
is (agnostically) replicable list learnable if and only if it is (agnostically) globally stable learnable.

Remark 6
.

In the above theorem we use the convention that
\infty\cdot 0=1
, thus
\mathtt{LR}(\mathcal{H})=\infty
if and only if
\rho(H)=0
.

Theorem
3
provides a quantitative equivalence between the list replicability and global stability numbers. We remark that the equivalence is algorithmic in the sense that globally stable algorithms can be efficiently converted to list replicable algorithms and vice versa.
Chase et al. 2023
prove Theorem
3
only in the realizable setting, however, the same proof applies in the agnostic setting.

2.3.1
Agnostic PAC Learning

The following theorem proved by
Bun et al. 2020
characterizes the classes that can be learned by a globally stable/list-replicable learning rule in the realizable setting.
The theorem relates
\rho(\mathcal{H})
to the Littlestone dimension

\mathtt{Ldim}(\mathcal{H})
of
\mathcal{H}
.

Theorem 4

(
Bun et al. 2020
)
.

The following statements are equivalent for a concept class
\mathcal{H}
:

•

\mathtt{Ldim}(\mathcal{H})<\infty
.

•

\rho(\mathcal{H})>0
(equivalently,
\mathtt{LR}(\mathcal{H})<\infty
).

Somewhat surprisingly, in the following result we show that Theorem
4
does not extend to the agnostic case. It says that if
\mathcal{H}
is agnostically learnable by a globally stable/list-replicable algorithm then
\mathcal{H}
must be finite.
In particular, list-replicable learnability in the realizable case does not imply list-replicable learnability in the agnostic case. This answers negatively a question posed by
Chase et al. 2023
.

Theorem C

(Agnostic replicability and global stability)
.

The following statements are equivalent for a concept class
\mathcal{H}
:

•

\mathcal{H}
is finite.

•

\rho_{\mathtt{agn}}(\mathcal{H})>0
(equivalently,
\mathtt{LR}_{\mathtt{agn}}(\mathcal{H})<\infty
).

Every finite class
\mathcal{H}
is trivially agnostically list replicable learnable by any agnostic proper learner (with the list being
|\mathcal{H}|
). The converse direction is proved using Theorem
A
and the notion of
\gamma
-interpolation from the work of
Alon et al. 2023
.

In other words,
C
asserts that agnostic list-replicable learning is possible only when it is trivially possible. Nevertheless, it might still be interesting to explore how the agnostic list-replicability number depends on the size of the class and on
n
in the best and worst cases.

Theorem
C
suggests that it could, perhaps, be beneficial to relax the definition of list replicable learnability in the agnostic setting.
We propose two possible relaxations:

Definition 7

(Excess-error dependent replicability)
.

We say that
\mathcal{H}
is agnostically learnable with excess-error dependent replicability
if for every
\varepsilon>0
there exists
L=L(\varepsilon)
and a learning rule
\mathcal{A}
with the following guarantees. For every
\delta>0
there exists
n=n(\delta)
such that for every distribution
\mathcal{D}
there is a list of
L(\varepsilon)
hypotheses
h_{1},\ldots h_{L}
such that
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}(h_{\ell})\leq\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathcal{H})+\varepsilon
for all
\ell
and

\Pr_{S\sim\mathcal{D}^{n}}[A(S)\in\{h_{1},\ldots,h_{L}\}]\geq 1-\delta.

Definition 8

(Class-error dependent replicability)
.

We say that
\mathcal{H}
is agnostically learnable with class-error dependent replicability
if for every
\gamma>0
there exists
L=L(\gamma)
and a learning rule
\mathcal{A}
with the following guarantees. For every
\varepsilon,\delta>0
there exists
n=n(\varepsilon,\delta)
such that for every distribution
\mathcal{D}
for which
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathcal{H})\leq\gamma
there is a list of
L(\gamma)
hypotheses
h_{1},\ldots h_{L}
such that
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}(h_{\ell})\leq\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathcal{H})+\varepsilon
for all
\ell
and

\Pr_{S\sim\mathcal{D}^{n}}[A(S)\in\{h_{1},\ldots,h_{L}\}]\geq 1-\delta.

What types of classes satisfy these definitions? Understanding the scope and nuances of these definitions could potentially shed light on the broader landscape of replicable learnability. We leave these questions for future work.

2.3.2
Realizable PAC Learning

We now turn to study quantitative bounds on the list-replicability and global stability numbers in the realizable case. For a class
\mathcal{H}
, the dual VC dimension of
\mathcal{H}
, denoted
\mathrm{VC}^{*}(\mathcal{H})
, is the VC dimension of the dual class
\mathcal{H}^{\star}\subseteq\{\pm 1\}^{\mathcal{H}}
of
\mathcal{H}
defined by
x(h)=h(x)
.

Chase et al. 2023
proved that
\mathtt{LR}(\mathcal{H})\geq\mathrm{VC}(\mathcal{H})
for every class
\mathcal{H}
. In the proof, they showed that every list replicable algorithm that learns
\mathcal{H}
with error
\varepsilon\leq O(1/d)
must use list size of at least
d
. Their proof, however, does not provide any information in the case when the error of the algorithm is larger (e.g. a small constant).
Likewise, the lower bound presented by
Dixon et al. 2023
also applies to learners whose error is
O(1/d)
.
This raises the question of whether it is possible to list replicably learn with error, say
\varepsilon=0.1
or even
\varepsilon=0.49
, and list size
\ll d
? The following result provides a negative answer, both in terms of
\mathrm{VC}(\mathcal{H})
and
\mathrm{VC}^{\star}(\mathcal{H})
. We define
\mathtt{LR}(\mathcal{H},\varepsilon)
to be the minimal
L
for which there is an
(\varepsilon,L)
-list replicable learner of
\mathcal{H}
. In particular,
\mathtt{LR}(\mathcal{H},\varepsilon)
is increasing in
\varepsilon
and
\mathtt{LR}(\mathcal{H})=\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\mathtt{LR}(\mathcal{H},\varepsilon)
.

Theorem D

(Lower bound for list replicable weak learners)
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a concept class. Then, for any
0<\varepsilon<1/2
, it holds

\mathtt{LR}(\mathcal{H},\varepsilon)\geq\max\{1+\lceil\mathrm{VC}(\mathcal{H})/2\rceil,1+\lfloor\mathrm{VC}^{\star}(\mathcal{H})/2\rfloor\}
.

As the
\mathrm{VC}^{*}(\mathcal{H})
bound on
\mathtt{LR}(\mathcal{H},\varepsilon)
is also a bound on
\mathtt{LR}(\mathcal{H})
, we have an easy corollary that applies to every class.

Corollary 9
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a concept class, then
\mathtt{LR}(\mathcal{H})\geq\max\{\mathrm{VC}(\mathcal{H}),1+\lfloor\mathrm{VC}^{\star}(\mathcal{H})/2\rfloor\}
. This bound is sharp in both parameters, that is, for all nontrivial values of
\mathrm{VC}(\mathcal{H})
and
\mathrm{VC}^{\star}(\mathcal{H})
, there are classes whose list-replicability number is
\mathrm{VC}(\mathcal{H})
, and those whose is
1+\lfloor\mathrm{VC}^{\star}(\mathcal{H})/2\rfloor
.

The sharpness of the
\mathrm{VC}
dimension bound can be found in
Chase et al. 2023
, and of the
\mathrm{VC}^{*}
bound is from
E
below.

Note that by going from
D
to
9
, that is, from learners that learn at least something to those that learn arbitrarily well, the
\mathrm{VC}^{*}
bound remains the same (and sharp), but the
\mathrm{VC}
bound increases by a factor of
2
.
It would thus be interesting to determine whether the
\mathrm{VC}
bound for weak learners can be improved and whether there are bounds for intermediate values of
\varepsilon
.
However, it is worth noting that the sharpness of
\mathrm{VC}(\mathcal{H})
bound on
\mathtt{LR}(\mathcal{H})
means that for the weak learners it also cannot exceed
\mathrm{VC}(\mathcal{H})
.

Another interesting question is whether a bound on
\mathtt{LR}(\mathcal{H})
also holds in the converse direction for finite classes; that is, whether
\mathtt{LR}(\mathcal{H})
can be upper bounded by a function of
\mathrm{VC}(\mathcal{H}),\mathrm{VC}^{\star}(\mathcal{H})
, provided that
\mathcal{H}
is finite.
2
2

2

The assumption that
\mathcal{H}
is finite is necessary as witnessed by the class of one-dimensional thresholds

For every class

Proof.

For an integer

, a topological space is

Lemma 14
.

Every normal space is
\infty

Proof.

Let
C_{1},\ldots,C_{m}

It will be convenient to set

Applying this claim for

The base case

F_{0}=C_{0}

For the step, let

\displaystyle H_{S}\cap\bigcap_{i\in S}A_{i}\neq\emptyset\ \ \Rightarrow\ \ C_{j+1}\cap\bigcap_{i\in S}A_{i}\neq\emptyset.

The proof relies on the following theorem by

Theorem 5
.

[
Jaworowski 2000
]
Let
P
be a finite
simplicial complex of dimension
d

If

is continuous and
2d\leq n

then there is
x\in S^{n}
so that

f(x)=f(-x)
.

The proof of Theorem
5
under the weaker assumption that
2d+1\leq n

follows from the following two known results.
The first is the realization theorem, first proved by Menger, stating that
P
can be realized in
\mathbb{R}^{2d+1}
.
The second is the Borsuk-Ulam theorem stating that
every continuous map from
S^{n}
to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
must collapse antipodal points.
We partition the proof into two cases.

Lower bound in open case.

Assume that
A_{1},\ldots,A_{k}
are open subsets of
S^{n}

that are antipodal-free and cover the sphere.
Define a continuous map
\tilde{g}:S^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{k}
as follows: for each
x\in S^{n}

and
i\in[k]

\tilde{g}_{i}(x)=\mathrm{dist}(x,S^{n}\setminus A_{i}),

where
\mathrm{dist}(x,A)=\inf\{\|x-y\|_{2}:y\in A\}
for
A\subset S^{n}
.
Define

g(x)=\frac{\tilde{g}(x)}{\|\tilde{g}(x)\|_{1}}.

Let
\ell=\max\{L(x,\{A_{1},\ldots,A_{k}\}):x\in S^{n}\}

Each

In addition, we claim that for all
x\in S^{n}
,

Indeed, for each

Because

Lower bound in open/closed case.

Thus, in particular, there exists

We will utilize some machinery from Section 3.3 in

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a finite class. Denote by
\Delta=\Delta_{\mathcal{H}}
the collection of
\mathcal{H}
-realizable distributions endowed with the
total-variation distance
(
\mathtt{TV}
). Recall that, by the definition, for distributions
\mathcal{D}_{1}
and

\displaystyle\mathtt{TV}(\mathcal{D}_{1},\mathcal{D}_{2})

\displaystyle=\sum_{e\colon\mathcal{D}_{1}(e)\geq\mathcal{D}_{2}(e)}(\mathcal{D}_{1}(e)-\mathcal{D}_{2}(e))=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{e}\left|\mathcal{D}_{1}(e)-\mathcal{D}_{2}(e)\right|.

We find it helpful to think of

as being isometrically embedded into

with half of the

norm of a difference as a metric. In particular,

is a closed subspace of a compact metric space, and hence is compact.

Theorem 6

(Theorem 7 in
Chase et al. 2023
)
.

The following statements are equivalent for a finite class
\mathcal{H}
and
\varepsilon>0

1.

\mathtt{LR}(\mathcal{H},\varepsilon)\leq L
;

2.

There exists
\delta>0
and a coloring
\mathcal{D}\mapsto h_{\mathcal{D}}
of
\Delta(\mathcal{H})
, where
h_{\mathcal{D}}\in\{\pm 1\}^{X}
is an hypothesis, such that
L_{\mathcal{D}}(h_{\mathcal{D}})\leq\varepsilon
and
\left|\left\{h_{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}}~:~\mathtt{TV}(\mathcal{D},\mathcal{D}^{\prime})\leq\delta\right\}\right|\leq L

\mathcal{D}\in\Delta
.

In the original statement of Theorem 7 in
Chase et al. 2023
, condition (1) was stated as “
\mathtt{LR}(\mathcal{H})\leq L
”, and (2) as “For every
\varepsilon>0

\dots
”. Our

Corollary 23
.

For a finite class
\mathcal{H}
,
\mathtt{LR}(\mathcal{H},\varepsilon)
is the minimal integer

\mathcal{F}=\left\{A_{h}~|~h\in\{\pm 1\}^{X}\right\}
of
\Delta
of overlap-degree at most
L
, such that
L_{\mathcal{D}}(h)\leq\varepsilon

h\in\{\pm 1\}^{X}

\mathcal{D}\in A_{h}

Proof.

The proof is by establishing the equivalence of the statement with
Theorem

6
(2).

In one direction, suppose that, for a given
\varepsilon>0
, we have
\delta
and a coloring
\zeta\colon\Delta\rightarrow\{\pm 1\}^{X}
that satisfy the conditions of
Theorem

6
(2). For an hypothesis
h
, let us define
A_{h}
as the closure of
\zeta^{-1}(h)
. By construction, it is a closed cover of
\Delta
. Let us take arbitrary
\mathcal{D}\in A_{h}

\mathcal{D}
can be approximated by the sequence
\mathcal{D}_{i}
of realizable distributions such that

\mathcal{D}
is covered by

, then there is
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
in the
\delta
-ball around
\mathcal{D}
for which

, and so there are at most

such sets.

In the other direction, again, for a given
\varepsilon>0
, suppose there is a closed cover
\mathcal{F}
from the statement of the corollary. Let us take
\zeta
to be an arbitrary coloring consistent with
\mathcal{F}

\mathcal{D}\in\Delta
,
\mathcal{D}\in A_{\zeta(\mathcal{D})}
. Trivially,

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a closed cover obtained from

by
\infty
-normality property, see

\mathcal{G}=\left\{B_{h}~|~h\in\{\pm 1\}^{X}\right\}

is. In particular,
\mathcal{G}
and

have the same overlap-degree, which is at most

\mathtt{TV}(A_{h},\Delta-B^{\circ}_{h})>0

. Let
\delta=\frac{1}{2}\min_{h}\mathtt{TV}(A_{h},\Delta-B^{\circ}_{h})
, and let us take an arbitrary

intersects the

around

, then
\mathcal{D}\in B_{h}
. So

\displaystyle\left|\left\{h_{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}}~:~\mathtt{TV}(\mathcal{D},\mathcal{D}^{\prime})\leq\delta\right\}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\left\{h~:~A_{h}\cap U_{\delta}(\mathcal{D})\neq\emptyset\right\}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\left\{h~:~\mathcal{D}\in B_{h}\right\}\right|\leq L,

where the last inequality is by the bound on the overlap-degree of

.
∎

It will be convenient to have an alternative to
\Delta

\mathcal{H}
. For
\mathcal{D}\in\Delta
, let

be defined as
\mathcal{D}_{\theta}(x)=\mathcal{D}(x,1)

and
\mathcal{D}_{\theta}(x)=-\mathcal{D}(x,-1)
otherwise. Let

. Note that, as
\mathcal{D}
is realizable, at most one of
\mathcal{D}(x,1)
and
\mathcal{D}(x,-1)
is nonzero, for every

is a bijection, with the inverse of

given by
\mathcal{D}(x,1)=\max(0,\mathcal{D}_{\theta}(x))
and

. Moreover, it is easy to notice that if we endow

with half of the
l_{1}
norm of a difference as a metric, just as we did for

becomes an isometry between

. Because of that, both

.

Theorem D

(Lower bound for list replicable weak learners)
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a concept class. Then, for any
0<\varepsilon<1/2
, it holds

\mathtt{LR}(\mathcal{H},\varepsilon)\geq\max\{1+\lceil\mathrm{VC}(\mathcal{H})/2\rceil,1+\lfloor\mathrm{VC}^{\star}(\mathcal{H})/2\rfloor\}
.

Proof.

The statement effectively consists of the two bounds:
L\geq 1+\lfloor\mathrm{VC}(\mathcal{H})/2\rfloor
, and
L\geq 1+\lfloor\mathrm{VC}^{*}(\mathcal{H})/2\rfloor
. We prove them separately, however, the proof strategy is similar: We identify the part of
\mathcal{H}
that witnesses the corresponding dimension, find a subcomplex isomorphic to a sphere in
\Delta
, argue that the coloring induced by the weak learner via
23
is antipodal-free, and apply the local LS bound (
A
) to it. Thus, we give a detailed proof of the
\mathrm{VC}^{\star}
lower bound, and then, for the proof of the
\mathrm{VC}
lower bound we gloss over the common part, and concentrate on the differences between the cases. Throughout the proof, we assume that
Y=\{0,1\}
, instead of
Y=\{\pm 1\}
used elsewhere.

(
L\geq 1+\lfloor\mathrm{VC}^{*}(\mathcal{H})/2\rfloor
).
As
\mathtt{LR}(\mathcal{H},\varepsilon)
is increasing in
\mathcal{H}
, without losing generality we can assume that
\mathcal{H}
is the minimal class witnessing
\mathrm{VC}^{\star}(\mathcal{H})=m
. That is,
\mathcal{H}
is the class
\mathcal{H}_{m}
from
E
.

We will identify the domain
X
with
\mathcal{P}([m])
. Again, let
\Delta=\Delta(\mathcal{H})
. For
A\subseteq[m]
such that
A\neq\emptyset,[m]
, let us define a distribution
\mathcal{D}_{A}
on
X\times{\{0,1\}}
that assigns probabilities
1/2
to examples
(A,1)
and
([m]-A,0)
. Note that, for any
i\in[m]
,
L(\mathcal{D}_{A},h_{i})=0
if
i\in A
and
L(\mathcal{D}_{A},h_{i})=1
if
i\in[m]-A
; in particular,
\mathcal{D}_{A}
is realizable by
\mathcal{H}_{m}
. Let us take a chain of sets
\emptyset\subsetneq A_{1}\subsetneq\dots\subsetneq A_{k}\subsetneq[m]
. For any
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{k}
such that
0<\lambda_{i}
and
\sum_{i=1,\dots,k}\lambda_{i}=1
, let

\mathcal{D}=\sum_{i=1,\dots,k}\lambda_{i}\mathcal{D}_{A_{i}}
. It is easy to see that thus constructed
\mathcal{D}
is also a distribution realizable by
\mathcal{H}_{m}
, with
L(\mathcal{D},h_{i})=0
for any
i\in A_{1}
and
L(\mathcal{D},h_{i})=1
for any
i\in[m]-A_{k}
; in particular,
\mathcal{D}
is realizable.

Now, let
\Delta_{R}\subseteq\Delta
be a simlicial complex with vertices
\mathcal{D}_{A}
, for all
A\subseteq[m]
such that
A\neq\emptyset,[m]
, and with simplices spanned by
\mathcal{D}_{A_{1}},\dots,\mathcal{D}_{A_{k}}
, for all chains
\emptyset\subsetneq A_{1}\subsetneq\dots\subsetneq A_{k}\subsetneq[m]
. As argued above, all distributions in
\Delta_{R}
are realizable, that is,
\Delta_{R}
is indeed a subspace of
\Delta
. Moreover, any two simplices of
\Delta_{R}
can intersect only on a subsimplex, and so
\Delta_{R}
is a valid geometric realization of the similarly constructed abstract simplicial complex. Note that as an abstract simplicial complex,
\Delta_{R}
is the barycentric subdivision of the boundary of the
(m-1)
-dimensional simplex. Thus,
\Delta_{R}
is a topological sphere
\mathbb{S}^{m-2}
.

The continuous fixed-point free involution
\nu\colon\Delta_{R}\rightarrow\Delta_{R}
is defined as in
Definition

15
. Namely, for
\mathcal{D}\in\Delta_{R}
with the barycentric representation
\mathcal{D}=\sum_{i=1,\dots,k}\lambda_{i}\mathcal{D}_{A_{i}}
,

\nu(\mathcal{D})=\sum_{i=1,\dots,k}\lambda_{i}\mathcal{D}_{[m]-A_{i}}.

We are now going to show that for
\varepsilon<1/2
, any closed cover
\mathcal{F}=\left\{C_{h}~|~h\in\{0,1\}^{X}\right\}
of
\Delta_{R}
that satisfies the population loss condition from
23
, that is, such that
L(\mathcal{D},h)\leq\varepsilon
for all
h\in\{0,1\}^{X}
and
\mathcal{D}\in C_{h}
, should have overlap-degree at least
1+\lfloor m/2\rfloor
. This will be done through an application of
A
. By
23
, this would clearly yield the desired lower bound on
\mathtt{LR}(\mathcal{H}_{m},\varepsilon)
.

So let
\mathcal{F}
be such closed cover. We argue that every
C_{h}
is antipodal-free. Towards a contradiction, suppose that
C_{h}
contains
\mathcal{D}=\sum_{i=1,\dots,k}\lambda_{i}\mathcal{D}_{A_{i}}
and
\nu(\mathcal{D})=\sum_{i=1,\dots,k}\lambda_{i}\mathcal{D}_{[m]-A_{i}}
. Then

\displaystyle L(\mathcal{D},h)

\displaystyle=L\left(\sum_{i=1,\dots,k}\lambda_{i}\mathcal{D}_{A_{i}},h\right)=\sum_{i=1,\dots,k}\lambda_{i}L(\mathcal{D}_{A_{i}},h)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1,\dots,k}\lambda_{i}\left(\frac{1}{2}(1-h(A_{i}))+\frac{1}{2}h\left([m]-A_{i}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1,\dots,k}h\left([m]-A_{i}\right)-h(A_{i}).

Similarly,

L(\nu(\mathcal{D}),h)=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1,\dots,k}h\left([m]-A_{i}\right)-h(A_{i}).

Thus,
L(\mathcal{D},h)+L(\nu(\mathcal{D}),h)=1

\mathcal{D}
and
\nu(\mathcal{D})
has loss less than
1/2

C_{h}
, for
\varepsilon<1/2
. After that, by
A
, the overlap-degree of
\mathcal{F}
is at least
\lceil(m-2+3)/2\rceil=1+\lfloor m/2\rfloor
, as needed.

(
L\geq 1+\lfloor\mathrm{VC}(\mathcal{H})/2\rfloor
).
As before, without losing generality, we assume that
\mathcal{H}
is the minimal class witnessing
\mathrm{VC}(\mathcal{H})=m
. That is,

and
\mathcal{H}
is the class of the characteristic functions of all subsets of

.

Let
\Theta=\Theta(\mathcal{H})
. Then

\Theta=\left\{\mathcal{D}_{\theta}\in[-1,1]^{X}~:~\|\mathcal{D}_{\theta}\|_{1}=1\right\},

where
\|\cdot\|_{1}
is an
l_{1}
norm, that is,
\|\mathcal{D}_{\theta}\|_{1}=\sum_{x\in X}|\mathcal{D}_{\theta}(x)|=\sum_{x,i\in X\times\{0,1\}}\mathcal{D}(x,i)
. In particular,
\Theta
is an
(m-1)
-dimensional

-ball, and thus is a topological sphere

. Let us define the continuous fixed-point free involution

, for every

.

Then, for
\varepsilon<1/2
, any closed cover
\mathcal{F}=\left\{C_{h}~|~h\in\{0,1\}^{X}\right\}

. By

, this implies the same lower bound on

\mathtt{LR}(\mathcal{H},\varepsilon)\geq 1+\lceil m/2\rceil

Theorem E

(Finite classes)
.

For every class
\mathcal{H}
of size
m
we have

\mathtt{LR}(\mathcal{H})\leq 1+\lfloor m/2\rfloor
.
This is sharp, as witnessed by the class
\mathcal{H}_{m}=\{h_{i}:i\leq m\}

of
m
projection functions on
X=\{0,1\}^{m}
:
h_{i}(x)=x_{i}
for every
x\in X
and
i
. For
\mathcal{H}_{m}
,
\mathrm{VC}^{*}(\mathcal{H}_{m})=m
, and so
\mathtt{LR}(\mathcal{H}_{m})=\mathtt{LR}(\mathcal{H}_{m},\varepsilon)=1+\lfloor m/2\rfloor
, for any
0<\varepsilon<1/2
.

It is possible, and would be easier, to prove the upper bound on the list replicability number of finite classes via
23
, that is, by presenting a suitable coloring of
\Delta(\mathcal{H})
. However, to make it more palpable, we do it directly, by presenting the required algorithm.

Proof.

We only need to prove the
\mathtt{LR}(\mathcal{H})\leq 1+\lfloor m/2\rfloor
bound for a class
\mathcal{H}
of size
m
.
We assume that the domain
\mathcal{X}

is finite: indeed, by identifying domain points that are same-valued by all hypotheses in
\mathcal{H}
we may assume without loss of generality that
\lvert\mathcal{X}\rvert=M\leq 2^{m}
. Let
h_{\mathrm{maj}}\colon X\rightarrow\{0,1\}
be the
majority vote
of
\mathcal{H}
, that is, an hypothesis such that for any
x\in X
,
\left|\left\{h\in\mathcal{H}~:~h(x)=h_{\mathrm{maj}}(x)\right\}\right|\geq|\mathcal{H}|/2
.

Let us pick arbitrary
\varepsilon>0
and
\delta>0
from the definition of a list-replicable learner, and let
n=n(\varepsilon,\delta)
, to be specified later, be big enough. For a realizable by
\mathcal{H}
distribution
\mathcal{D}
, let
\hat{\mathcal{D}}=\hat{\mathcal{D}}(S)
be the empirical distribution induced by a sample
S\sim\mathcal{D}^{n}
. Note that, by construction,
L_{S}(h)=L_{\hat{\mathcal{D}}}(h)
, for any
h\in\{0,1\}^{X}
. Also note that, for any distributions
\mathcal{D}_{1}
and
\mathcal{D}_{2}
and any
h\in\{0,1\}^{X}
,
|L_{\mathcal{D}_{1}}(h)-L_{\mathcal{D}_{2}}(h)|\leq\|\mathcal{D}_{1}-\mathcal{D}_{2}\|_{1}=2\mathtt{TV}(\mathcal{D}_{1},\mathcal{D}_{2})
. By taking
n
sufficiently big, we can ensure that for any
e>0
,
\hat{\mathcal{D}}
is
e
-close to
\mathcal{D}
in total variation with probability at least
1-\delta
. Note that this estimate uses the finiteness of
\mathcal{H}
and is independent of
\mathcal{D}
.

Let us now define a learning rule
\mathcal{A}
as follows:

An
(\varepsilon,L)
-list replicable learner
\mathcal{A}
of
\mathcal{H}
, for
|\mathcal{H}|=m
and
L\leq 1+\lfloor m/2\rfloor
.

Input:
A sample
S\sim\mathcal{D}^{n}
, where
\mathcal{D}
is some distribution, realizable by
\mathcal{H}
.

1.

If
L_{S}(h_{\mathrm{maj}})\leq(2M+2)e
, output
h_{\mathrm{maj}}
;

2.

Otherwise, output any
h\in\mathcal{H}
, consistent with
S
, with arbitrary probability.

If
\mathcal{D}
is such that the event

, is a subset of the event
[L_{S}(h_{\mathrm{maj}})\leq(2M+2)e]
, then, by construction,
\mathcal{A}(S)=h_{\mathrm{maj}}
with probability at least
1-\delta
. Also, for arbitrary
\hat{\mathcal{D}}=\hat{\mathcal{D}}(S)
, witnessing both events,
|L_{\mathcal{D}}(h_{\mathrm{maj}})-L_{S}(h_{\mathrm{maj}})|\leq 2\mathtt{TV}(\mathcal{D},\hat{\mathcal{D}})\leq 2e
, and so
L_{\mathcal{D}}(h_{\mathrm{maj}})\leq L_{S}(h_{\mathrm{maj}})+2e\leq(2M+4)e
. So, for such
\mathcal{D}
, the replicable list
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{D})
can be defined as just
\{h_{\mathrm{maj}}\}
.

Alternatively, suppose that
[\mathtt{TV}(\mathcal{D},\hat{\mathcal{D}})\leq e]\subsetneq[L_{S}(h_{\mathrm{maj}})\leq(2M+2)e]
. That is, there is
\hat{\mathcal{D}}=\hat{\mathcal{D}}(S)

L_{S}(h_{\mathrm{maj}})>(2M+2)e

L_{\mathcal{D}}(h_{\mathrm{maj}})>2Me

\mathcal{D}(x_{0},-h_{\mathrm{maj}}(x_{0}))>2e

L_{\mathcal{D}}(h_{\mathrm{maj}})\leq(2M+4)e

•

those
h\in\mathcal{H}-\{h_{\mathrm{maj}}\}
for which
L_{\mathcal{D}}(h)\leq 2e
.

Then for any
h\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{D})-\{h_{\mathrm{maj}}\}
, this implies

, as otherwise
L_{\mathcal{D}}(h)\geq\mathcal{D}(x_{0},-h_{\mathrm{maj}}(x_{0}))>2e
.
So, by the properties of

. Also,

is linearly bounded with

, for any
h\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{D})
. Finally, for any
S\sim\mathcal{D}^{n}

is either
h_{\mathrm{maj}}

In the first case,

, and so
L_{D}(h_{\mathrm{maj}})\leq L_{S}(h_{\mathrm{maj}})+2\mathtt{TV}(\mathcal{D},\hat{\mathcal{D}})\leq(2M+4)e
, and so
h_{\mathrm{maj}}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{D})
. In the second, for

, by construction,
L_{S}(h)=0
. Thus,
L_{D}(h)\leq L_{S}(h)+2\mathtt{TV}(\mathcal{D},\hat{\mathcal{D}})\leq 2e
, and again,

. So,
[\mathtt{TV}(\mathcal{D},\hat{\mathcal{D}})\leq e]\subseteq[\mathcal{A}(S)\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{D})]
, and so the probability that
\mathcal{A}(S)
is in
\mathcal{L}
is at least
1-\delta
. The proof is finished by taking

sufficiently big so that

.
∎
