On the first eigenvalue of the generalized laplacian
Julián Fernández Bonder and Ariel M. Salort

Abstract
. In this work we investigate the energy of minimizers of Rayleigh-type quotients of the form ∫ Ω A ⁡ ( | ∇ u | ) ​ 𝑑 x ∫ Ω A ⁡ ( | u | ) ​ 𝑑 x . \frac{\int_{\Omega}A(|\nabla u|)\,dx}{\int_{\Omega}A(|u|)\,dx}. These minimizers are eigenfunctions of the generalized laplacian defined as Δ a ​ u = div ⁡ ( a ⁡ ( | ∇ u | ) ​ ∇ u | ∇ u | ) \Delta_{a}u=\mathop{\text{div}}\left(a(|\nabla u|)\frac{\nabla u}{|\nabla u|}\right) where a ​ ( t ) = A ′ ​ ( t ) a(t)=A^{\prime}(t) and the Rayleigh quotient is comparable to the associated eigenvalue. On the function A A we only assume that it is a Young function but no Δ 2 \Delta_{2} condition is imposed. Since the problem is not homogeneous, the energy of minimizers is known to strongly depend on the normalization parameter α = ∫ Ω A ⁡ ( | u | ) ​ 𝑑 x \alpha=\int_{\Omega}A(|u|)\,dx . In this work we precisely analyze this dependence and show differentiability of the energy with respect to α \alpha and, moreover, the limits as α → 0 \alpha\to 0 and α → ∞ \alpha\to\infty of the Rayleigh quotient. The nonlocal version of this problem is also analyzed.



1.
Introduction

Let
A\colon\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
be convex function superlinear at 0 and at
\infty
. Given an open and bounded set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
and a fixed normalization parameter
\alpha>0
, we consider the
energy function

E(\alpha)
defined as

E(\alpha)=\inf\left\{\int_{\Omega}A(|\nabla u|)\,dx\colon u\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega),\int_{\Omega}A(|u|)\,dx=\alpha\right\}.

(1.1)

Without any further growth assumption on
A
, in
[
8
,
9
]
it is proved that the minimization problem
E(\alpha)
is solvable, i.e., there exists a function
u_{\alpha}
for which the infimum in
E(\alpha)
is achieved. Furthermore, through a generalized version of the Lagrange’s Multiplier Theorem, in
[
9
]
(see also
[
22
]
) it is proved that
u_{\alpha}
satisfies in the weak sense the equation

\displaystyle\begin{cases}-\Delta_{a}u=\lambda(\alpha)\frac{a(|u|)}{|u|}u&\quad\text{ in }\Omega\\
u=0&\quad\text{ in }\partial\Omega,\end{cases}

(1.2)

where the eigenvalue
\lambda(\alpha)
is the Lagrange multiplier,
a(t)=A^{\prime}(t)
and
\Delta_{a}u:=\mathop{\text{div}}\left(\frac{a(|\nabla u|)}{|\nabla u|}\nabla u\right)
is the so-called
generalized Laplacian
or the
a-
Laplacian operator
.

Existence of solutions to (
1.1
) and eigenpairs to (
1.2
) assuming diverse growth behaviors on
A
were widely studied, see for instance
[
8
,
9
,
17
,
18
,
20
,
22
]
. These problems, typically are non homogeneous and in general, the eigenvalue
\lambda(\alpha)
does not coincide with the ratio
E(\alpha)/\alpha
. One known exception arises when the problem is homogeneous, that is, when
A(t)=t^{p}
for some
p>1
and hence (
1.2
) turns out to be the well-known eigenvalue problem for the Dirichlet
p-
Laplace operator. In this case the first eigenvalue of such a problem admits the variational characterization

\lambda(1)=\lambda(\alpha)=\frac{E(\alpha)}{\alpha}=\inf_{0\neq u\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)}\frac{\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{p}dx}{\int_{\Omega}|u|^{p}\,dx}

and it is independent of the normalization parameter
\alpha>0
. For further information, see for instance
[
12
,
13
,
14
]
.

Although, in general, eigenvalues and energy may not coincide, when the Young function
A
satisfies the
\Delta_{2}-
condition (see Section
2
for the definition), it can be readily proved that the following comparison estimate

c\lambda(\alpha)\leq\frac{E(\alpha)}{\alpha}\leq C\lambda(\alpha),

(1.3)

holds for some constants
c,C>0
depending only on
A
. Moreover, in Section
3
the following elementary inequality is proved to hold:

\min\left\{\alpha^{p-1},\alpha^{\frac{1}{p}-1}\right\}E(1)\leq\frac{E(\alpha)}{\alpha}\leq\max\left\{\alpha^{p-1},\alpha^{\frac{1}{p}-1}\right\}E(1),

(1.4)

where
p>1
is a suitable constant depending only on
A
. However, while this estimate is straightforward, it lacks the depth necessary to provide meaningful insights into the behavior of
E(\alpha)
as
\alpha\downarrow 0
and
\alpha\uparrow\infty
. This lack of information serves as the primary motivation for this paper.

Our first results concern the regularity of the curve
E(\alpha)
. In Theorem
4.5
, without any additional assumptions regarding the behavior of
A
, we prove that the energy
E(\alpha)
is a Lipschitz continuous function of the parameter
\alpha>0
. Moreover, assuming a technical but fairly natural condition on
A
we are able to obtain its differentiability. More precisely, assuming that the map
t\mapsto ta(t)
is convex, in Theorem
4.6
we prove that
E(\alpha)
is differentiable for any
\alpha>0
and the following formulas hold:

\displaystyle\frac{dE}{d\alpha}(\alpha)=\lambda(\alpha),\qquad\frac{d}{d\alpha}\left(\frac{E(\alpha)}{\alpha}\right)=\frac{1}{\alpha}\left(\lambda(\alpha)-\frac{E(\alpha)}{\alpha}\right),

In these expressions, the eigenvalue
\lambda(\alpha)
and the energy quotient
E(\alpha)/\alpha
coincide if and only if both quantities are constants independent of
\alpha
. This is precisely the scenario where
A
is homogeneous.

In Section
5
we analyze the behavior of the ratio
E(\alpha)/\alpha
as
\alpha\downarrow 0
and
\alpha\uparrow\infty
. Our main result establishes that the behavior of that quotient can be described in terms of the the Matuszewska-Orlicz functions associated to the Young function
A
, which are defined as follows

M_{0}(t)=\limsup_{\tau\to 0}\frac{A(\tau t)}{A(\tau)}\qquad\text{and}\qquad M_{\infty}(t)=\limsup_{\tau\to\infty}\frac{A(\tau t)}{A(\tau)},

(see Section
2
for further information and properties of these function). We highlight that the behavior of the energy quotient only takes into account the local behavior of the Young function
A
near
0
and near
\infty
. More precisely, in Theorem
5.1
we prove that if
A
satisfies the
\Delta_{2}
condition near
i=0,\infty
, then

\limsup_{\alpha\to i}\frac{E(\alpha)}{\alpha}=\inf_{\tau>0}\frac{E_{i}(\tau)}{\tau}

where

E_{i}(\tau)=\inf\left\{\int_{\Omega}M_{i}(|\nabla u|)\,dx\colon u\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega),\,\int_{\Omega}M_{i}(|u|)\,dx=\tau\right\}.

This result can be refined. We prove that under a fairly general additional assumption on
A
, there exist numbers
p_{i}\in[1,\infty)
for which
M_{i}(t)=t^{p_{i}}
, and these result in

\lim_{\alpha\to i}\frac{E(\alpha)}{\alpha}=\lambda_{p_{i}},

where
\lambda_{p}
denotes the first eigenvalue of the
p-
Laplacian with Dirichlet boundary conditions in
\Omega
.

Although in the previous result the assumption of the
\Delta_{2}
condition on
A
seems restrictive, in fact this is somehow the interesting case of study since when this condition is removed, in broad terms, the Matuszewska-Orlicz functions associated to the Young function
A
becomes trivial:

M_{i}(t)=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }t<1\\
1&\text{if }t=1\\
\infty&\text{if }t>1\\
\end{cases}

for
i=0,\infty
. In this case, under suitable assumptions on the domain, the limit of
E(\alpha)/\alpha
can be characterized. More precisely, if
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is such that the inner radius is larger than 1, in Theorem
6.1
we obtain that

\lim_{\alpha\to i}\frac{E(\alpha)}{\alpha}=0.

Applying the same technique, we can extend our approach to handle the nonlocal version of (
1.1
) within the framework of fractional Orlicz-Sobolev spaces of order
s\in(0,1)
introduced in
[
6
]
. Specifically, for a fixed
\alpha>0
we analyze the minimization problem defined by

E^{s}(\alpha)=\inf\left\{\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}A\left(\frac{|u(x)-u(y)|}{|x-y|^{s}}\right)\,d\nu_{n}\colon u\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega),\int_{\Omega}A(|u|)\,dx=\alpha\right\}

(1.5)

where we denote
d\nu_{n}:=|x-y|^{-n}dxdy
. As in the local case, without any growth assumption on the Young function
A
, in
[
21
]
it is proved the existence of a function
u^{s}_{\alpha}
for which the infimum in (
1.5
) is attained. Moreover, this function satisfies in the weak sense the equation

\displaystyle\begin{cases}(-\Delta_{a})^{s}u=\lambda^{s}(\alpha)\frac{a(|u|)}{|u|}u&\quad\text{ in }\Omega\\
u=0&\quad\text{ in }\partial\Omega,\end{cases}

(1.6)

where the fractional
a-
Laplacian of order
s\in(0,1)
is defined as

(-\Delta_{a})^{s}u:=\text{p.v.}\,\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}a\left(\frac{|u(x)-u(y)|}{|x-y|^{s}}\right)\frac{u(x)-u(y)}{|u(x)-u(y)|}\frac{dy}{|x-y|^{n+s}}

and
\lambda^{s}(\alpha)
is the Lagrange’s multiplier. Problems (
1.5
) and (
1.6
) (and further variants) were widely studied, see for instance
[
3
,
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
,
19
,
21
]
.

2.1.
Young functions

A function
A\colon\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
is called a
Young function
if it can be written as

A(t)=\int_{0}^{t}a(\tau)\,d\tau,

where
a\colon\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
is nondecreasing, right continuous,
a(t)>0
if
t>0
,
a(0)=0
and
a(t)\to\infty
as
t\to\infty
.

From this definition, it can readily be checked that
A
is convex,
A(0)=0
and it is superlinear at
0
and at
\infty
, i.e.

\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{A(t)}{t}=0,\quad\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{A(t)}{t}=\infty.

Observe that the convexity of the Young function immediately gives that

A(\tau t)\leq\tau A(t)\quad\text{ for }0<\tau<1,\qquad A(\tau t)\geq\tau A(t)\quad\text{ for }\tau>1.

(2.1)

Given a Young function
A
, an important associated function is the so-called
complementary function
, denoted by
\bar{A}
, that is defined as

\bar{A}(t):=\sup\{\tau t-A(\tau)\colon\tau\geq 0\}.

The complementary function
\bar{A}
is also a Young function.

Observe that
\bar{A}
is the optimal Young function in the
Young-type inequality

\tau t\leq\bar{A}(\tau)+A(t)

for all
\tau,t\geq 0
. It is also easy to see that
\bar{\bar{A}}=A
.

We now define the class

\Delta_{2}^{\infty}:=\left\{A\colon\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}\colon\text{Young function such that }\limsup_{t\to\infty}\frac{A(2t)}{A(t)}<\infty\right\}

and

\Delta_{2}^{0}:=\left\{A\colon\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}\colon\text{Young function such that }\limsup_{t\to 0}\frac{A(2t)}{A(t)}<\infty\right\}.

It is easy to see that
A\in\Delta_{2}^{\infty}
if and only if there exists constants
C_{\infty}\geq 2
and
T_{\infty}>0
such that

A(2t)\leq C_{\infty}A(t)\quad\text{ for }t\geq T_{\infty},

and
A\in\Delta_{2}^{0}
if and only if there exists
C_{0}\geq 2
and
T_{0}>0
such that

A(2t)\leq C_{0}A(t)\quad\text{ for }t\leq T_{0}.

Finally, we define the class

\Delta_{2}=\Delta_{2}^{0}\cap\Delta_{2}^{\infty}

and
A\in\Delta_{2}
if and only if

\sup_{t>0}\frac{A(2t)}{A(t)}<\infty.

In
[
11
, Theorem 4.1]
it is shown that
A\in\Delta_{2}^{\infty}
(or
A\in\Delta_{2}^{0}
, or
A\in\Delta_{2}
) if and only if there exists
p>1
such that

\frac{ta(t)}{A(t)}\leq p\quad\text{for all }t\geq T_{\infty}\text{ (or $t\leq T_{0}$, or $t>0$)}.

(2.2)

In order to analyze the behavior of a Young function
A
at 0 and at
\infty
, we recall the Matuszewska-Orlicz functions that are defined as

M_{0}(t)=\limsup_{\tau\to 0}\frac{A(\tau t)}{A(\tau)}\qquad\text{and}\qquad M_{\infty}(t)=\limsup_{\tau\to\infty}\frac{A(\tau t)}{A(\tau)},

(2.3)

These functions were introduced by Matuszewska and Orlicz in
[
16
]
and the reader can consult with the book
[
15
]
for the results presented here. For
i=0,\infty
, we have that
M_{i}
is convex and sub-multiplicative, that is
M_{i}\colon\mathbb{R}_{+}\to[0,\infty]
verify

M_{i}(0)=0,\quad M_{i}(1)=1,\quad M_{i}(\tau t)\leq M_{i}(\tau)M_{i}(t).

When
A\in\Delta_{2}^{i}
, it follows that
M_{i}(t)<\infty
for every
t>0
.

In the case where
A\not\in\Delta_{2}^{i}
for some
i=0,\infty
, the Matuszewska function
M_{i}
turns out to be trivial. We were not able to find this result in the literature, so we include a proof of this fact.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
A
be a Young function. Then, if
A\not\in\Delta_{2}^{i}
,
i=0,\infty
, we have that

M_{i}(t)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }t=1\\
\infty&\text{if }t>1.\end{cases}

Moreover, if
A
satisfies the slightly stronger assumption

\liminf_{t\to i}\frac{A(2t)}{A(t)}=\infty,

(2.4)

then
M_{i}(t)=0
if
0<t<1
.

Proof.

We prove the result for
i=\infty
, the other case being analogous.

For
t>1
we argue as in
[
11
, Theorem 4.1]
, to obtain

A(st)\geq\int_{s}^{st}a(\tau)\,d\tau\geq(t-1)sa(s).

Then,

\limsup_{s\to\infty}\frac{sa(s)}{A(s)}\leq\frac{1}{t-1}\limsup_{s\to\infty}\frac{A(st)}{A(s)}=\frac{1}{t-1}M_{\infty}(t).

(2.5)

Therefore
A\not\in\Delta_{2}^{\infty}
implies that
M_{\infty}(t)=\infty
for
t>1
.

Observe that
M_{\infty}(1)=1
by definition.

Now, if (
2.4
) holds, then the limit in (
2.5
) exists. And so, for
0<t<1
, we have

\frac{A(st)}{A(s)}=\frac{1}{\frac{A(st\frac{1}{t})}{A(st)}}.

Therefore the result follows by taking the limit in the former expression.
∎

As we mentioned, if
A\in\Delta_{2}^{i}
, then
M_{i}(t)<\infty
for every
t>0
. In the case where the limit in the definition of
M_{i}
(
2.3
) exists, these Matuszewska function are power functions. This is the content of the next result.

Proposition 2.2
.

Assume
A\in\Delta_{2}^{i}
for some
i=0,\infty
. Assume moreover that the limit in (
2.3
) exists. Then, there exists
p_{i}\in[1,\infty)
such that

M_{i}(t)=t^{p_{i}}.

Proof.

First observe that, by (
2.1
), it follows that

M_{i}(t)\leq t\text{ for }0<t<1\quad\text{and}\quad M_{i}(t)\geq t\text{ for }t>1.

(2.6)

Now, the proof follows just observing that if the limit in (
2.3
) exists, then
M_{i}(t)
is multiplicative, i.e.
M_{i}(st)=M_{i}(s)M_{i}(t)
and
M_{i}(1)=1
.

Then, if we define
v_{i}\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
by
v_{i}(t)=\ln(M_{i}(e^{t}))
, the function
v_{i}
is additive, i.e.
v_{i}(s+t)=v_{i}(s)+v_{i}(t)
for any
s,t\in\mathbb{R}
. It is a well known fact that measurable additive functions are linear, therefore there exists
p_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
such that
v_{i}(t)=p_{i}t
from where we can deduce that
M_{i}(t)=t^{p_{i}}
. This fact, together with (
2.6
), implies that
p_{i}\geq 1
.
∎

2.1.1.
Examples

Example 2.3
.

Let
p>1
, and assume that

A(t)\sim t^{p}\quad\text{when }t\ll 1.

Then
A\in\Delta_{2}^{0}
and
M_{0}(t)=t^{p}
.

If we assume that

A(t)\sim t^{p}\quad\text{when }t\gg 1,

then
A\in\Delta_{2}^{\infty}
and
M_{\infty}(t)=t^{p}
.

As a special case we have the
(p,q)-
Laplacian: if
1<p<q<\infty

A(t)=\frac{t^{p}}{p}+\frac{t^{q}}{q},\quad\text{then }A\in\Delta_{2}\quad\text{and }M_{0}(t)=t^{p},\quad M_{\infty}(t)=t^{q}.

Example 2.4
.

Let
\alpha\geq 0
and
p\geq 1
. Then if

A(t)\sim t^{p}\ln^{\alpha}t\quad\text{when }t\ll 1,

then
A\in\Delta_{2}^{0}
and
M_{0}(t)=t^{p}
. If

A(t)\sim t^{p}\ln^{\alpha}t\quad\text{when }t\gg 1,

then
A\in\Delta_{2}^{\infty}
and
M_{\infty}(t)=t^{p}
.

As a special case, if

A(t)=\frac{t^{p}}{p}\ln^{\alpha}(1+t^{r}),

for some
r>0
, then
A\in\Delta_{2}
and
M_{0}(t)=t^{p+r\alpha}
,
M_{\infty}(t)=t^{p}
.

Example 2.5
.

For
\alpha,\beta\geq 0
and
p\geq 1
, assume that

A(t)\sim t^{p}\ln^{\alpha}(t)\ln^{\beta}(\ln t)\quad\text{when }t\gg 1.

Then
A\in\Delta_{2}^{\infty}
and
M_{\infty}(t)=t^{p}
.

Example 2.6
.

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, define
A(t)=e^{t}-\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{t^{k}}{k!}
. Then
A\in\Delta_{2}^{0}
, but
A\not\in\Delta_{2}^{\infty}
. Moreover,

M_{0}(t)=t^{n}\quad\text{and}\quad M_{\infty}(t)=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }0<t<1\\
1&\text{if }t=1\\
\infty&\text{if }t>1.\end{cases}

Example 2.7
.

For
\alpha>0
, assume that

A(t)\sim e^{-t^{-\alpha}}\quad\text{when }t\ll 1.

Then
A\not\in\Delta_{2}^{0}
and
M_{0}(t)=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }0<t<1\\
1&\text{if }t=1\\
\infty&\text{if }t>1.\end{cases}

Example 2.8
.

Assume that

A(t)\sim e^{e^{t}}\quad\text{when }t\gg 1.

Then,
A\not\in\Delta_{2}^{\infty}
and
M_{\infty}(t)=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }0<t<1\\
1&\text{if }t=1\\
\infty&\text{if }t>1.\end{cases}

Observe that all of the examples in this subsection satisfy the hypotheses of Proposition
2.1
or Proposition
2.2
.

2.2.
Orlicz and Orlicz-Sobolev spaces

As we mentioned in the introduction on this section, the main reference for Orlicz spaces is the book
[
11
]
. As for Orlicz-Sobolev spaces, the reader can consult, for instance, with
[
10
]
.

Fractional order Orlicz-Sobolev spaces, as we will use them here, were introduced in
[
6
]
and then further analyze by several authors. The results used in this paper are found in
[
1
,
2
]
and in
[
5
]
.

2.2.1.
Orlicz spaces

Given
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
a bounded domain and a Young function
A
, the Orlicz class is defined as

\mathcal{L}^{A}(\Omega):=\left\{u\in L^{1}_{\text{loc}}(\Omega)\colon\int_{\Omega}A(|u|)\,dx<\infty\right\}.

Then the Orlicz space
L^{A}(\Omega)
is defined as the linear hull of
\mathcal{L}^{A}(\Omega)
and is characterized as

L^{A}(\Omega)=\left\{u\in L^{1}_{\text{loc}}(\Omega)\colon\text{ there exists }k>0\text{ such that }\int_{\Omega}A\left(\frac{|u|}{k}\right)\,dx<\infty\right\}.

In general the Orlicz class is strictly smaller than the Orlicz space, and
\mathcal{L}^{A}(\Omega)=L^{A}(\Omega)
if and only if
A\in\Delta_{2}^{\infty}
.

The space
L^{A}(\Omega)
is a Banach space when it is endowed, for instance, with the
Luxemburg norm
, i.e.

\|u\|_{L^{A}(\Omega)}=\|u\|_{A}:=\inf\left\{k>0\colon\int_{\Omega}A\left(\frac{|u|}{k}\right)\,dx\leq 1\right\}.

This space
L^{A}(\Omega)
turns out to be separable if and only if
A\in\Delta_{2}^{\infty}
.

An important subspace of
L^{A}(\Omega)
is
E^{A}(\Omega)
that it is defined as the closure of the functions in
L^{A}(\Omega)
that are bounded. This space is characterized as

E^{A}(\Omega)=\left\{u\in L^{1}_{\text{loc}}(\Omega)\colon\int_{\Omega}A\left(\frac{|u|}{k}\right)\,dx<\infty\text{ for all }k>0\right\}.

This subspace
E^{A}(\Omega)
is separable, and we have the inclusions

E^{A}(\Omega)\subset\mathcal{L}^{A}(\Omega)\subset L^{A}(\Omega)

with equalities if and only if
A\in\Delta_{2}^{\infty}
.

The following duality relation holds

(E^{A}(\Omega))^{*}=L^{\bar{A}}(\Omega),

where the equality is understood via the standard duality pairing. Observe that this automatically implies that
L^{A}(\Omega)
is reflexive if and only if
A,\bar{A}\in\Delta_{2}^{\infty}
.

In the sequel, we will need a well known lemma (see for instance
[
11
]
), but we include the details for completeness.

Lemma 2.9
.

Let
A
be a Young function and
u\in L^{A}(\Omega)
. Then

\|u\|_{A}\leq\max\left\{1;\int_{\Omega}A(|u|)\,dx\right\}.

Proof.

We can assume that
u\in\mathcal{L}^{A}(\Omega)
, otherwise there is nothing to prove. Also, we can assume that
\|u\|_{A}>1
.
Then,

\int_{\Omega}A(|u|)\,dx=\int_{\Omega}A\left(\frac{|u|}{\|u\|_{A}}\|u\|_{A}\right)\,dx\geq\|u\|_{A},

where we have used (
2.1
) and the definition of the Luxemburg norm.
∎

2.2.2.
Orlicz-Sobolev spaces

Once the Orlicz spaces are defined, one can easily define the Orlicz-Sobolev spaces in the usual way, namely

W^{1}L^{A}(\Omega)=\{u\in L^{A}(\Omega)\colon\partial_{x_{i}}u\in L^{A}(\Omega),i=1,\dots,n\}

and

W^{1}E^{A}(\Omega)=\{u\in E^{A}(\Omega)\colon\partial_{x_{i}}u\in E^{A}(\Omega),i=1,\dots,n\}.

These two spaces coincide only if
A\in\Delta_{2}^{\infty}
and in this case we will denote the Orlicz-Sobolev space as

W^{1,A}(\Omega)=W^{1}L^{A}(\Omega)=W^{1}E^{A}(\Omega).

These spaces are endowed with the natural norm

\|u\|_{W^{1}L^{A}(\Omega)}=\|u\|_{1,A}=\|u\|_{A}+\|\nabla u\|_{A}.

With this norm,
W^{1}L^{A}(\Omega)
is a Banach space and
W^{1}E^{A}(\Omega)
is a closed subspace.

In order to work with Dirichlet boundary conditions it is necessary to define the Orlicz-Sobolev functions that vanish at the boundary. So, we define
W^{1}_{0}L^{A}(\Omega)
as the closure of
C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)
with respect to the topology
\sigma(W^{1}L^{A}(\Omega),W^{1}E^{\bar{A}}(\Omega))
and
W^{1}_{0}E^{A}(\Omega)
as the closure of
C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)
in norm topology.

Again, when
A\in\Delta_{2}^{\infty}
these spaces coincide and will be denoted as

W^{1,A}_{0}(\Omega)=W^{1}_{0}L^{A}(\Omega)=W^{1}_{0}E^{A}(\Omega).

These spaces are reflexive if and only if both
A,\bar{A}\in\Delta_{2}^{\infty}
. In any other case, what we have is that there exists a Banach space
X
such that
X^{*}=W^{1}_{0}L^{A}(\Omega)
. This Banach space
X
can be easily characterized in terms of distributions, but it will not be used in this article.

In
[
8
]
the following is proved.

Proposition 2.10
.

Let
A
be a Young function. Then the embedding
W^{1}_{0}L^{A}(\Omega)\subset L^{A}(\Omega)
is compact.

2.2.3.
Fractional Orlicz-Sobolev spaces

The construction of the fractional order Orlicz-Sobolev spaces is almost the same as the Orliz-Sobolev spaces. We will make only a sketch and the reader can consult with
[
5
]
.

Given a fractional parameter
s\in(0,1)
, we define the Hölder quotient of a function
u\in L^{A}(\Omega)
as

D^{s}u(x,y)=\frac{u(x)-u(y)}{|x-y|^{s}}.

Then, the fractional Orlicz-Sobolev space of order
s
is defined as

W^{s}L^{A}(\mathbb{R}^{n}):=\{u\in L^{A}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\colon D^{s}u\in L^{A}(\mathbb{R}^{2n},d\nu_{n})\},

where
d\nu_{n}=|x-y|^{-n}dxdy
and

W^{s}E^{A}(\mathbb{R}^{n}):=\{u\in E^{A}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\colon D^{s}u\in E^{A}(\mathbb{R}^{2n},d\nu_{n})\}.

Since we are now working in the whole space, in order for these spaces to coincide we require that
A\in\Delta_{2}
. In this case, we denote

W^{s,A}(\mathbb{R}^{n})=W^{s}L^{A}(\mathbb{R}^{n})=W^{s}E^{A}(\mathbb{R}^{n}).

As in the previous case, we have that
W^{s}L^{A}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is reflexive if and only if
A,\bar{A}\in\Delta_{2}
.

In these spaces the norm considered is

\|u\|_{W^{s}L^{A}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}=\|u\|_{s,A}=\|u\|_{A}+\|D^{s}u\|_{A,d\nu_{n}}.

Again, with this norm,
W^{s}L^{A}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is a Banach space and
W^{s}E^{A}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is a closed subspace.

The space
W^{s}_{0}L^{A}(\Omega)
is then defined as the closure of
C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)
with respect to the topology
\sigma(W^{s}L^{A}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),W^{s}E^{\bar{A}}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
and
W^{s}_{0}E^{A}(\Omega)
as the closure of
C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)
in norm topology.

Also, in
[
5
]
it is shown that for any
A
, there exists a Banach space
X
such that
X^{*}=W^{s}_{0}L^{A}(\Omega)
and that the extension of Proposition
2.10
to the fractional case holds:

Proposition 2.11
.

Let
A
be a Young function. Then the embedding
W^{s}_{0}L^{A}(\Omega)\subset L^{A}(\Omega)
is compact.

2.3.
A review on the existence of minimizers for the energy function

Given a Young function
A
without any further assumption on its behavior, in
[
8
, Theorem 3.1]
(see also
[
9
, Theorem 4.1]
) it is proved that for any election of the normalization
\alpha>0
, the minimization problem
E(\alpha)
is solvable:

Proposition 2.12
.

Let
E(\alpha)
be the energy function defined in (
1.1
). Then for every
\alpha>0
, there exists a function
u_{\alpha}\in W^{1}_{0}L^{A}(\Omega)
such that
E(\alpha)=\int_{\Omega}A(|\nabla u_{\alpha}|)\,dx
and
\int_{\Omega}A(|u_{\alpha}|)\,dx=\alpha

As mentioned in the introduction, if
A,\bar{A}\in\Delta_{2}
it is straightforward to see that by means of the Lagrange’s multiplier rule there exists
\lambda(\alpha)
such that

\int_{\Omega}a(|\nabla u_{\alpha}|)\frac{\nabla u_{\alpha}\cdot\nabla v}{|\nabla u_{\alpha}|}\,dx=\lambda(\alpha)\int_{\Omega}a(|u_{\alpha}|)\frac{u_{\alpha}v}{|u_{\alpha}|}\,dx\qquad\text{ for all }v\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega).

(2.7)

That is,
u_{\alpha}
solves the eigenvalue problem (
1.2
) in the weak sense.

By using a generalized version of the Lagrange’s multiplier rule, in
[
9
, Theorem 4.2]
the previous claim is proved to hold without any growth assumption on
A
:

Proposition 2.13
.

Let
\alpha>0
and let
u_{\alpha}
be a minimizer of
E(\alpha)
. Then there exists
\lambda(\alpha)>0
for which
u_{\alpha}
solves (
2.7
).

As for the fractional problem, this was tackled in
[
21
]
(see also
[
5
]
where higher order eigenvalues were also studied) and the following result was otained.

Proposition 2.14
.

For any
\alpha>0
, there exists a minimizer
u_{\alpha}^{s}\in W^{s}_{0}L^{A}(\Omega)
of the energy function
E^{s}(\alpha)
defined in (
1.5
). Moreover, there exists
\lambda^{s}(\alpha)
such that
u_{\alpha}^{s}
is a weak solution of (
1.6
), that is

\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}a(|D^{s}u_{\alpha}^{s}|)\frac{D^{s}u_{\alpha}^{s}D^{s}v}{|D^{s}u_{\alpha}^{s}|}\,d\nu_{n}=\lambda^{s}(\alpha)\int_{\Omega}a(|u_{\alpha}^{s}|)\frac{u_{\alpha}^{s}v}{|u_{\alpha}^{s}|}\,dx,

for all

.

3.
Elementary estimates on the energy function

In this section we will assume that the Young function
A
verifies the
\Delta_{2}-
condition either at 0 or at
\infty
and deduce some immediate bounds on the energy
E(\alpha)
and on the eigenvalue
\lambda(\alpha)
.

To this end, let us consider
u\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)
to be fixed such that

\int_{\Omega}A(|u|)\,dx=1.

Then, since
A
is continuous and nondecreasing, if we define the function

\phi(r)=\phi_{A}^{u}(r)=\int_{\Omega}A(r|u|)\,dx,

(3.1)

it follows that
\phi\colon\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
is continuous, nondecreasing,
\phi(0)=0
,
\phi(1)=1
and
\phi(\infty)=\infty
. Hence, for any
\alpha>0
, there exists
r=r_{\alpha}>0
such that
\phi(r_{\alpha})=\alpha
.

Recall that if
\alpha\leq 1
, then
r_{\alpha}\leq 1
and that if
\alpha\geq 1
then
r_{\alpha}\geq 1
.

We want to estimate
r_{\alpha}
in terms of
\alpha
. To this end, we recall an easy lemma regarding Young functions

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
A
be a Young function such that
A\in\Delta_{2}^{0}
(or
A\in\Delta_{2}^{\infty}
) and let
p>1
be defined in (
2.2
).

Then it holds that
\tau^{p}A(t)\leq A(\tau t)\leq\tau A(t)
for
0<\tau<1
and
t<T_{0}
(or
\tau A(t)\leq A(\tau t)\leq\tau^{p}A(t)
for
\tau>1
and
t>T_{\infty}
).

Hence, if
A\in\Delta_{2}
, then

\min\{\tau,\tau^{p}\}A(t)\leq A(\tau t)\leq\max\{\tau,\tau^{p}\}A(t),\quad\text{for $\tau>0$}.

The proof of this lemma is elementary and is omitted.

With the help of Lemma
3.1
we can obtain a simple estimate on
r_{\alpha}
, namely

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
A\in\Delta_{2}
and
u\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)
. Let
\phi=\phi_{A}^{u}
be defined in (
3.1
),
\alpha>0
and let
r_{\alpha}>0
be such
\phi(r_{\alpha})=\alpha
. Then

\min\{\alpha,\alpha^{\frac{1}{p}}\}\leq r_{\alpha}\leq\max\{\alpha,\alpha^{\frac{1}{p}}\},

where
p
is the constant defined in (
2.2
).

Proof.

It is immediate from the definition of
r_{\alpha}
and Lemma
3.1
.
∎

Next, we combine Lemmas
3.1
and
3.2
to obtain a bound for the energy
E(\alpha)
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Given
\alpha>0
, the energy of level
\alpha
is defined by (
1.1
). Then, if
A\in\Delta_{2}
, we have that

\min\{\alpha^{p},\alpha^{\frac{1}{p}}\}E(1)\leq E(\alpha)\leq\max\{\alpha^{p},\alpha^{\frac{1}{p}}\}E(1)

Proof.

Immediate from the previous lemmas.
∎

Next, we want to compare the energy
E(\alpha)
to the eigenvalue
\lambda(\alpha)
of problem (
1.2
).

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
A
be a Young function such that
A\in\Delta_{2}
and let
p
be defined in (
2.2
).

Let
\alpha>0
be a normalization parameter and let
u\in W^{1}_{0}L^{A}(\Omega)
be the minimizer associated to the energy
E(\alpha)
. Let
\lambda(\alpha)
be the eigenvalue of (
1.2
) associated to
u
. Then, we have

\frac{1}{p}\frac{E(\alpha)}{\alpha}\leq\lambda(\alpha)\leq p\frac{E(\alpha)}{\alpha}.

Proof.

First, observe that from
[
9
, Theorem 4.23]
, there exists a minimizer
u\in W^{1}_{0}L^{A}(\Omega)
for
E(\alpha)
and
u
is a weak solution to (
1.2
).

Now, if we use
u
as a test function in the weak formulation of (
1.2
), we get that

\lambda(\alpha)=\frac{\int_{\Omega}a(|\nabla u|)|\nabla u|\,dx}{\int_{\Omega}a(|u|)|u|\,dx}.

(3.2)

Next, just observe that we have the inequality

A(t)\leq a(t)t\leq pA(t),

(3.3)

where the first inequality follows from the monotonicity of
a(t)
and the second one is the
\Delta_{2}-
condition.

The result now follows just combining (
3.2
) and (
3.3
).
∎

Remark 3.5
.

Observe that combining Lemmas
3.3
and
3.4
we immediately get that there exist
c,C>0
such that

c\min\{\alpha^{p-1},\alpha^{\frac{1}{p}-1}\}\leq\frac{E(\alpha)}{\alpha},\lambda(\alpha)\leq C\max\{\alpha^{p-1},\alpha^{\frac{1}{p}-1}\}.

Also observe that these bounds trivialize when
\alpha\to 0
and when
\alpha\to\infty
.

4.
Continuity and differentiability of the energy function

In this section we study the regularity properties of the energy function
E(\alpha)
defined in (
1.1
). First, without assuming the
\Delta_{2}
condition on
A
nor
\bar{A}
, we prove the continuity of
E(\alpha)

Theorem 4.1
.

The energy function
E(\alpha)
is continuous for every
\alpha>0
.

We split the proof into two lemmas.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
A
be a Young function and let
E(\alpha)
be the energy function defined in (
1.1
). Then,
E
is upper semicontinuous.

Proof.

Let
\alpha_{0}>0
be fixed. Given
\varepsilon>0
, take
u_{0}=u_{\varepsilon,\alpha_{0}}\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)
be such that

\int_{\Omega}A(|\nabla u_{0}|)\,dx\leq E(\alpha_{0})+\varepsilon\quad\text{and}\quad\int_{\Omega}A(|u_{0}|)\,dx=\alpha_{0}.

Let
\phi(r)=\phi_{A}^{u_{0}}(r)
be the function defined in (
3.1
) and given
\alpha>0
, let
r_{\alpha}>0
be such that
\phi(r_{\alpha})=\alpha
.

Observe that
\lim_{\alpha\to\alpha_{0}}\phi(r_{\alpha})=\lim_{\alpha\to\alpha_{0}}\alpha=\alpha_{0}=\phi(1)
. But, since
\phi
is strictly increasing and continuous, it follows that
\phi^{-1}
is also continuous and strictly increasing, from where it follows that
r_{\alpha}\to 1
when
\alpha\to\alpha_{0}
.

Hence
r_{\alpha}u_{0}
is an admissible test for
E(\alpha)
and we get that

\limsup_{\alpha\to\alpha_{0}}E(\alpha)\leq\limsup_{\alpha\to\alpha_{0}}\int_{\Omega}A(r_{\alpha}|\nabla u_{0}|)\,dx=\int_{\Omega}A(|\nabla u_{0}|)\,dx\leq E(\alpha_{0})+\varepsilon.

Since
\varepsilon>0
is arbitrary, the result follows.
∎

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
A
be a Young function and let
E(\alpha)
be the energy function defined in (
1.1
). Then,
E
is lower semicontinuous.

Proof.

Let
\alpha_{0}>0
be fixed and consider
\alpha_{k}\to\alpha_{0}
as
k\to\infty
. Let
\varepsilon>0
and for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
let
u_{k}\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)
be such that

\int_{\Omega}A(|\nabla u_{k}|)\,dx\leq E(\alpha_{k})+\varepsilon\quad\text{and}\quad\int_{\Omega}A(|u_{k}|)\,dx=\alpha_{k}.

Observe that by Lemma
4.2
,
E(\alpha_{k})
is bounded, and hence
\int_{\Omega}A(|\nabla u_{k}|)\,dx
is bounded. Therefore, by Lemma
2.9
,
\{u_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is bounded in
W^{1}_{0}L^{A}(\Omega)
.

This allows to apply Proposition
2.10
to derive the existence of a function
u\in W^{1}_{0}L^{A}(\Omega)
such that

u_{k}\rightharpoonup u\text{ weakly* in }W^{1}_{0}L^{A}(\Omega)\quad\text{and}\quad u_{k}\to u\text{ strongly in }L^{A}(\Omega).

From this we conclude that
\int_{\Omega}A(|u|)\,dx=\alpha_{0}
and hence, it is an admissible competitor for
E(\alpha_{0})
, from where

E(\alpha_{0})\leq\int_{\Omega}A(|\nabla u|)\,dx\leq\liminf_{k\to\infty}\int_{\Omega}A(|\nabla u_{k}|)\,dx\leq\liminf_{k\to\infty}E(\alpha_{k})+\varepsilon,

where for the second inequality we have used
[
8
, Lemma 2.2]
. This estimate concludes the proof.
∎

From the proof of Theorem
4.1
one immediately obtains the following information:

Proposition 4.4
.

Let
\alpha_{0}>0
and let
\alpha_{k}\to\alpha_{0}
as
k\to\infty
. Let
\{u_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of minimizers of
E(\alpha_{k})
. Then,
\{u_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is weak* precompact in
W^{1}_{0}L^{A}(\Omega)
and each accumulation point
u\in W^{1}_{0}L^{A}(\Omega)
of the sequence in the weak*-topology is a minimizer for
E(\alpha_{0})
.

Moreover, if
u_{k_{j}}\rightharpoonup u
weakly* in
W^{1}_{0}L^{A}(\Omega)
, then

\int_{\Omega}A(|\nabla u|)\,dx=\lim_{j\to\infty}\int_{\Omega}A(|\nabla u_{k_{j}}|)\,dx.

Now, we analyze the differentiability of
E(\alpha)
. We stress that no
\Delta_{2}-
condition is imposed on
A
nor
\bar{A}
. In fact, we prove the following:

Theorem 4.5
.

Let
A
be a Young function and let
E(\alpha)
be the energy function defined in (
1.1
). Then
E(\alpha)
is Lipschitz continuous. Moreover,

0\leq\liminf_{\alpha\to\alpha_{0}}\frac{E(\alpha)-E(\alpha_{0})}{\alpha-\alpha_{0}}\leq\limsup_{\alpha\to\alpha_{0}}\frac{E(\alpha)-E(\alpha_{0})}{\alpha-\alpha_{0}}\leq\lambda(\alpha).

Proof.

Observe that the lower bound is immediate, since
E(\alpha)
is increasing.

Now, Let
\alpha_{0}>0
and let
u_{0}\in W^{1}_{0}L^{A}(\Omega)
be a minimizer of
E(\alpha_{0})
, that is,

E(\alpha_{0})=\int_{\Omega}A(|\nabla u_{0}|)\,dx\quad\text{and}\quad\int_{\Omega}A(|u_{0}|)\,dx=\alpha_{0}.

Define
F(r,\alpha)=\int_{\Omega}A(r|u_{0}|)\,dx-\alpha
. Observe that

\frac{\partial F}{\partial r}(r,\alpha)=\int_{\Omega}a(r|u_{0}|)|u_{0}|\,dx,\qquad\frac{\partial F}{\partial\alpha}(r,\alpha)=-1.

Moreover, since
u_{0}\neq 0
,
\frac{\partial F}{\partial r}(1,\alpha_{0})=\int_{\Omega}a(|u_{0}|)|u_{0}|\,dx>0
. Then we can apply the Implicit Function Theorem to obtain the existence of a function
r(\alpha)
defined for
\alpha\in I:=(\alpha_{0}-\delta,\alpha_{0}+\delta)
for some
\delta>0
, for which
r(\alpha_{0})=1
,
F(r(\alpha),\alpha)=0
for any
\alpha\in I
. Moreover,

r^{\prime}(\alpha_{0})=-\frac{\frac{\partial F}{\partial\alpha}(1,\alpha_{0})}{\frac{\partial F}{\partial r}(1,\alpha_{0})}=\frac{1}{\int_{\Omega}a(|u_{0}|)||u_{0}|\,dx}>0.

Observe that the function
G(\alpha):=\int_{\Omega}A(r(\alpha)|\nabla u_{0}|)\,dx
,
\alpha\in I
, near
\alpha_{0}
behaves as

\displaystyle G(\alpha)

\displaystyle=G(\alpha_{0})+G^{\prime}(\alpha_{0})(\alpha-\alpha_{0})+o(\alpha-\alpha_{0})

\displaystyle=E(\alpha_{0})+(\alpha-\alpha_{0})r^{\prime}(\alpha_{0})\int_{\Omega}a(|\nabla u_{0}|)|\nabla u_{0}|\,dx+o(\alpha-\alpha_{0}).

Since
F(r(\alpha),\alpha)=0
for any
\alpha\in I
, the function
r(\alpha)u_{0}
is admissible in the minimization problem
E(\alpha)
for any
\alpha\in I
, giving that

\frac{E(\alpha)-E(\alpha_{0})}{\alpha-\alpha_{0}}\leq\frac{G(\alpha)-E(\alpha_{0})}{\alpha-\alpha_{0}}=\frac{\int_{\Omega}a(|\nabla u_{0}|)|\nabla u_{0}|\,dx}{\int_{\Omega}a(|u_{0}|)|u_{0}|\,dx}+o(1)=\lambda(\alpha_{0})+o(1),

as we wanted to prove.
∎

In order to obtain the differentiability of the energy function, we need to impose some technical, but somewhat natural additional conditions on
A
.

Theorem 4.6
.

Let
A
be a Young function such that
t\mapsto a(t)t
is convex.

Let
E(\alpha)
be the energy function defined in (
1.1
). Then
E(\alpha)
is differentiable for any
\alpha>0
. Moreover,

\displaystyle E^{\prime}(\alpha)=\lambda(\alpha),\qquad\left(\frac{E(\alpha)}{\alpha}\right)^{\prime}=\frac{1}{\alpha}\left(\lambda(\alpha)-\frac{E(\alpha)}{\alpha}\right),

where
\lambda(\alpha)
is the eigenvalue defined in (
1.2
).

Proof.

In view of Theorem
4.5
, it remains to see that

\liminf_{\alpha\to\alpha_{0}}\frac{E(\alpha)-E(\alpha_{0})}{\alpha-\alpha_{0}}\geq\lambda(\alpha_{0}).

Let
\{\alpha_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
be such that
\alpha_{k}\to\alpha_{0}
when
k\to\infty
, and

\liminf_{\alpha\to\alpha_{0}}\frac{E(\alpha)-E(\alpha_{0})}{\alpha-\alpha_{0}}=\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{E(\alpha_{k})-E(\alpha_{0})}{\alpha_{k}-\alpha_{0}}.

Arguiying analogously as in the proof of Theorem
4.1
, we can assume that there exists
\{u_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subset W^{1}_{0}L^{A}(\Omega)
such that

E(\alpha_{k})=\int_{\Omega}A(|\nabla u_{k}|)\,dx,\qquad\int_{\Omega}A(|u_{k}|)\,dx=\alpha_{k}

and that

\begin{split}&u_{k}\rightharpoonup u\text{ weakly* in }W^{1}_{0}L^{A}(\Omega)\quad\text{ and }\\
&u_{k}\to u\text{ strongly in }L^{A}(\Omega)\text{ and pointwisely}.\end{split}

(4.1)

Moreover, by Proposition
4.4
,
u\in W^{1}_{0}L^{A}(\Omega)
is a minimizer for
E(\alpha_{0})
.

Now, proceeding as Theorem
4.5
for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
there exists a function
r_{k}(\alpha)
defined in
t\in I_{k}:=(\alpha_{k}-\delta,\alpha_{k}+\delta)
for some
\delta>0
for which

r_{k}(\alpha_{k})=1,\quad\int_{\Omega}A(r_{k}(\alpha)|u_{k}|)\,dx=\alpha,\quad r_{k}^{\prime}(\alpha_{k})=\frac{1}{\int_{\Omega}a(|u_{k}|)|u_{k}|\,dx}>0.

Hence, testing in
E(\alpha_{0})
with
r_{k}(\alpha_{0})u_{k}
and performing a Taylor expansion around
\alpha=\alpha_{k}
we get that

\displaystyle E(\alpha_{0})

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Omega}A(r_{k}(\alpha_{0})|\nabla u_{k}|)\,dx

\displaystyle=E(\alpha_{k})+(\alpha_{0}-\alpha_{k})r_{k}^{\prime}(\alpha_{k})\int_{\Omega}a(|\nabla u_{k}|)|\nabla u_{k}|\,dx+o(\alpha_{0}-\alpha_{k}).

Observe that, since
a(t)
is continuous and
u_{k}
is converging pointwisely, it follows that
o(\alpha_{0}-\alpha_{k})
is independent of
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{E(\alpha_{k})-E(\alpha_{0})}{\alpha_{k}-\alpha_{0}}

\displaystyle\geq\liminf_{k\to\infty}\frac{\int_{\Omega}a(|\nabla u_{k}|)|\nabla u_{k}|\,dx}{\int_{\Omega}a(|u_{k}|)|u_{k}|\,dx}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\int_{\Omega}a(|\nabla u|)|\nabla u|\,dx}{\int_{\Omega}a(|u|)|u|\,dx}=\lambda(\alpha_{0}),

where we have used
[
8
, Lemma 2.2]
and our convexity assumption on the function
t\mapsto a(t)t
.

Finally, using the expression for
E^{\prime}(\alpha)
we get that

\displaystyle\left(\frac{E(\alpha)}{\alpha}\right)^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\alpha}\left(E^{\prime}(\alpha)-\frac{E(\alpha)}{\alpha}\right)=\frac{1}{\alpha}\left(\lambda(\alpha)-\frac{E(\alpha)}{\alpha}\right).

The proof is complete.
∎

Remark 4.7
.

Observe that the eigenvalue
\lambda(\alpha)
and the energy quotient
E(\alpha)/\alpha
coincide if and only if they are constants. This is exactly the case where
A
is homogeneous, that is
A(t)=t^{p}
for some
p>1
.

5.
Sharp asymptotic behavior when
\alpha\to 0
and
\alpha\to\infty

In order to analyze the precise behavior at
0
and at
\infty
, we need to assume that
A\in\Delta_{2}^{i}
at
i=0
and
i=\infty
respectively. Moreover, we need to impose the slightly stronger assumption that the limit defining
M_{i}(t)
in (
2.3
) exists.

So, fixed a normalization parameter
\tau>0
we define the minimization problem for the associated Matuszewska functions,

\displaystyle E_{i}(\tau)

\displaystyle=\inf\left\{\int_{\Omega}M_{i}(|\nabla u|)\,dx\colon u\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega),\,\int_{\Omega}M_{i}(|u|)\,dx=\tau\right\}.

(5.1)

We then obtain the following result.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
A
be a Young function satisfying the
\Delta_{2}^{i}
condition for
i=0
or
i=\infty
and assume that the corresponding limit defining
M_{i}(t)
in (
2.3
) exists. Then, it holds that:

\limsup_{\alpha\to i}\frac{E(\alpha)}{\alpha}=\inf_{\tau>0}\frac{E_{i}(\tau)}{\tau}.

Proof.

We work in the case where
i=\infty
, the other case being identical. We will split the proof into two steps.

Step 1: Upper bound
.

Let
u\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)
be a fixed function such that
u\neq 0
and consider the function
\phi=\phi_{A}^{u}
defined in (
3.1
). Hence, given
\alpha>0
there exists a unique
r_{\alpha}>0
such that
\phi(r_{\alpha})=\alpha
. Since
\phi
is continuous and
u
is fixed we have that
r_{\alpha}\to\infty
if
\alpha\to\infty
.

Since we can write

\frac{\alpha}{A(r_{\alpha})}=\int_{\Omega}\frac{A(r_{\alpha}|u|)}{A(r_{\alpha})}\,dx,

according to the definition (
2.3
) and the Dominated Convergence Theorem, we get that

\lim_{\alpha\to\infty}\frac{\alpha}{A(r_{\alpha})}=\int_{\Omega}M_{\infty}(|u|)\,dx.

(5.2)

The equality
\phi(r_{\alpha})=\alpha
implies that the function
r_{\alpha}u
is admissible in the minimization problem for
E(\alpha)
, therefore

\begin{split}\limsup_{\alpha\to\infty}\frac{E(\alpha)}{\alpha}&\leq\lim_{\alpha\to\infty}\frac{1}{\alpha}\int_{\Omega}A(r_{\alpha}|\nabla u|)\,dx\\
&=\lim_{\alpha\to\infty}\frac{A(r_{\alpha})}{\alpha}\int_{\Omega}\frac{A(r_{\alpha}|\nabla u|)}{A(r_{\alpha})}\,dx\\
&=\frac{\int_{\Omega}M_{\infty}(|\nabla u|)\,dx}{\int_{\Omega}M_{\infty}(|u|)\,dx},\end{split}

(5.3)

where we have used (
5.2
) and (
2.3
)

Since
u\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)
is arbitrary, from (
5.3
) it is deduced that

\limsup_{\alpha\to\infty}\frac{E(\alpha)}{\alpha}\leq\inf_{\tau>0}\frac{E_{\infty}(\tau)}{\tau}

as needed.

Step 2: Lower bound
.
Let us derive the lower bound for the ratio
E(\alpha)/\alpha
. For this, let
u\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)
be fixed, and as before, let
r_{\alpha}>0
be such that
\phi(r_{\alpha})=\alpha
.

For
\varepsilon>0
we have that

\inf_{\tau>0}\frac{E_{\infty}(\tau)}{\tau}-\varepsilon\leq\frac{\int_{\Omega}M_{\infty}(|\nabla u|)\,dx}{\int_{\Omega}M_{\infty}(|u|)\,dx}-\varepsilon.

Then, according to (
2.3
), there exists
\alpha_{\infty}=\alpha_{\infty}(\varepsilon,u)
such that for
\alpha>\alpha_{\infty}
,

\frac{\int_{\Omega}M_{\infty}(|\nabla u|)\,dx}{\int_{\Omega}M_{\infty}(|u|)\,dx}-\varepsilon\leq\frac{\int_{\Omega}\frac{A(r_{\alpha}|\nabla u|)}{A(r_{\alpha})}\,dx}{\int_{\Omega}\frac{A(r_{\alpha}|u|)}{A(r_{\alpha})}\,dx}=\frac{1}{\alpha}\int_{\Omega}A(r_{\alpha}|\nabla u|)\,dx.

Taking infimum over
u\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)
, we conclude that

\inf_{\tau>0}\frac{E_{\infty}(\tau)}{\tau}-\varepsilon\leq\frac{E(\alpha)}{\alpha}\quad\text{for }\alpha>\alpha_{\infty},

from where we deduce that

\inf_{\tau>0}\frac{E_{\infty}(\tau)}{\tau}\leq\liminf_{\alpha\to\infty}\frac{E(\alpha)}{\alpha}

and this concludes the proof.
∎

Lemma
4.2
, and Theorem
5.1
lead to the uniform boundedness of the ratio
E(\alpha)/\alpha
:

Corollary 5.2
.

It holds that

As a direct consequence, we have the following.

With the same assumptions of Theorem

p_{i}
be the exponent given by Proposition

6.
The behavior of
E(\alpha)/\alpha
without the
\Delta_{2}-
condition

In this section we explore the limit behavior of the energy quotient
E(\alpha)/\alpha
in the case where the Young function
A
does not satisfy the
\Delta_{2}^{i}
condition for
i=0
or
i=\infty
.

We will assume the stronger condition (
2.4
), and hence, by Proposition
2.1
, the Matuszewska function
M_{i}(t)
is trivial. That is

\lim_{s\to i}\frac{A(ts)}{A(s)}=M_{i}(t)=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }0\leq t<1\\
1&\text{if }t=1\\
\infty&\text{if }t>1.\end{cases}

In this case, we have the following result.

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
A
be a Young function satisfying (
2.4
) for
i=0
or
i=\infty
. Then, if
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
has inner radius larger that 1,

\lim_{\alpha\to i}\frac{E(\alpha)}{\alpha}=0.

Proof.

Recall that the inner radius of
\Omega
is defined as

R_{\Omega}=\sup\{r>0\colon B_{r}(x)\subset\Omega\text{ for some }x\in\Omega\}.

If
R_{\Omega}>1
, then there exists
r>0
,
x_{0}\in\Omega
with
B_{r}(x_{0})=B_{r}\subset\Omega
and
u\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)
such that

u(x)=1\text{ for }x\in B_{r},\quad 0\leq u(x)\leq 1\quad\text{and}\quad|\nabla u(x)|<1\text{ for }x\in\Omega.

Then, as usual, for
\alpha>0
we define
r_{\alpha}
such that
\phi(r_{\alpha})=\phi_{A}^{u}(r_{\alpha})=\alpha
and therefore

\frac{E(\alpha)}{\alpha}\leq\frac{A(r_{\alpha})}{\alpha}\int_{\Omega}\frac{A(r_{\alpha}|\nabla u|)}{A(r_{\alpha})}\,dx=\frac{\int_{\Omega}\frac{A(r_{\alpha}|\nabla u|)}{A(r_{\alpha})}\,dx}{\int_{\Omega}\frac{A(r_{\alpha}|u|)}{A(r_{\alpha})}\,dx}.

But, since
r_{\alpha}\to i
as
\alpha\to i
, it follows from Proposition
2.1
that

\int_{\Omega}\frac{A(r_{\alpha}|u|)}{A(r_{\alpha})}\,dx\to|\{u=1\}|=|B_{r}|>0\quad\text{and}\quad\int_{\Omega}\frac{A(r_{\alpha}|\nabla u|)}{A(r_{\alpha})}\,dx\to 0,

and the proof is complete.

∎
