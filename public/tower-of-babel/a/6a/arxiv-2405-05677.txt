Stable quadrangulations and stable spheres
Eleanor Archer, Ariane Carrance, Laurent Ménard

Abstract
We consider scaling limits of random quadrangulations obtained by applying the Cori–Vauquelin–Schaeffer bijection to Bienaymé–Galton–Watson trees with stably-decaying offspring tails with an exponent α ∈ ( 1 , 2 ) \alpha\in(1,2) . We show that these quadrangulations admit subsequential scaling limits which all have Hausdorff dimension 2 ​ α α − 1 \frac{2\alpha}{\alpha-1} almost surely. We conjecture that the limits are unique and spherical, and we introduce a candidate for the limit that we call the α \alpha - stable sphere . In addition, we conduct a detailed study of volume fluctuations around typical points in the limiting maps, and show that the fluctuations share similar characteristics with those of stable trees.

Introduction

The purpose of this paper is to introduce a stable model of random planar quadrangulations and to start to investigate their scaling limits. The starting point is the celebrated convergence of uniform planar quadrangulations to the Brownian sphere
[
LG13
,
Mie13
]
, which utilises the well-known Cori–Vauquelin–Schaeffer (CVS) bijection between labelled trees and planar quadrangulations. In particular, a quadrangulation can be represented as the image of a labelled tree under the CVS bijection. Each edge of the tree becomes a face of the quadrangulation and the labels determine distances in the latter object. In this paper, we consider sequences of random quadrangulations obtained by applying the CVS bijection to labelled Bienaymé–Galton–Watson trees for which the offspring distribution has stably-decaying tails.

The Brownian sphere was first constructed in
[
MM06
]
as a candidate for the scaling limit of uniform quadrangulations with
n
faces, along with various associated convergence results. This followed on from the work of Chassaing and Schaeffer who already showed that the rescaled radius of a uniform quadrangulation converges to the width of the support of the Brownian snake
[
CS04
]
. The breakthrough in terms of its metric structure came in the landmark works of Le Gall
[
LG07
,
LG10
,
LG13
]
and Miermont
[
Mie13
]
who showed that the Brownian sphere is the unique scaling limit, in the Gromov-Hausdorff sense, of uniform
2p
-angulations, and triangulations, with
n
faces. Moreover, it is almost surely homeomorphic to the sphere
[
LG07
,
LGP08
,
Mie08
]
, and its Hausdorff dimension is equal to 4 almost surely
[
LG07
]
. Since these pioneering results, the universality class of random models of planar maps whose scaling limit is the Brownian sphere has been extended to general maps
[
BJM14
]
, bipartite maps
[
Abr16
]
, simple triangulations and quadrangulations
[
ABA17
]
, Boltzmann maps and maps with prescribed degree sequences in the Brownian regime
[
Mar22
]
, all odd
p
-angulations
[
ABA21
]
, bicolored triangulations
[
Car21
]
, cubic planar graphs
[
Stu22
,
AFL23
]
and block-weighted quadrangulations
[
FS23
]
.

The crucial ingredient in the proofs of both Le Gall and Miermont were bijections between labelled trees and random planar maps obtained earlier by Cori-Vauqielin
[
CV81
]
, Schaeffer
[
Sch98
]
and generalised by Bouttier, Di Francesco and Guitter
[
BDFG04
]
. In particular, a uniform quadrangulation with
n
faces can be sampled by taking a uniform rooted random plane tree with
n
edges, sampling a so-called label function that evolves as a simple random walk along each branch of the tree, and applying the CVS bijection to this rooted labelled tree. It should therefore be of no surprise that the Brownian sphere can be constructed from scaling limits of such labelled trees. The scaling limit of such a label process is known as the Brownian snake, which is informally Brownian motion indexed by the Brownian continuum random tree (CRT)
[
LG99
]
. The Brownian sphere can be constructed as a quotient space of the CRT, and the precise form of the quotient is determined by the associated Brownian snake. Moreover, the CRT plays the role of the cut-locus for the Brownian sphere.

The fact that the label process converges to the Brownian snake is therefore completely key to the scaling limit results of Le Gall and Miermont. Thanks to recent results of Marzouk
[
Mar20
]
, we also now understand how to take scaling limits of labelled trees in a much more general setting. In particular, he considers labelled critical Bienaymé–Galton–Watson trees with offspring distribution in the domain of attraction of an
\alpha
-stable law for some
\alpha\in(1,2)
. The metric space scaling limits of such trees (without labels) are well-known to be the
stable trees
introduced by Le Gall and Le Jan
[
LGLJ98
]
and developed by Duquesne and Le Gall
[
DLG02
]
– see Figure
2
for simulations. Like the CRT, these are continuous random trees but unlike the CRT they have the property that they contain branch points of infinite degree, known as hubs. In
[
Mar20
]
Marzouk shows that these discrete trees can be additionally endowed with a label process that converges to “Brownian motion indexed by the stable tree”. This will be referred to as the
stable snake
throughout this article.

This therefore begs the question of whether it is possible to emulate the construction of the Brownian sphere using a stable snake and to obtain convergence of the associated discrete model of random quadrangulations to this limiting object. The present paper is a natural step towards that aim. We consider convergence in the pointed Gromov-Hausdorff-Prokhorov topology (this is a straightforward extension of the Gromov-Hausdorff topology in which we also consider convergence of measures and roots). Just as in the Brownian case, the convergence of snakes of Marzouk ensures that we have tightness in this topology. The space of rooted compact finite metric measure spaces is Polish
[
ALW16
]
, hence this implies that these maps have subsequential limits, but we do not a priori know that limits are unique. However we conjecture that, similarly to the Brownian case, the limits are indeed unique and we moreover have a candidate for the limit, which is constructed from the stable snake analogously to the construction of the Brownian sphere from the Brownian snake (we plan to address this in future papers). We call this limit the
stable sphere
. In the present paper, we show that any subsequential limit of the discrete quadrangulations has Hausdorff dimension equal to
\frac{2\alpha}{\alpha-1}
almost surely, and obtain precise results on the corresponding volume fluctuations.

Let us now briefly describe our model. For
\alpha\in(1,2)
, we write
\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}
for the stable tree with index
\alpha
as introduced by Le Gall and Le Jan
[
LGLJ98
]
and developed by Duquesne and Le Gall
[
DLG02
]
; see Section
2
for details. The case
\alpha=2
corresponds to the CRT, introduced by Aldous
[
Ald91
,
Ald91a
,
Ald93
]
. We will denote by
\rho
the root of
\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}
and by
d_{\alpha}
the distance on the tree
\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}
. It will be useful to have in mind that
\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}
has both a genealogical and a lexicographical order, which is inherited from the left to right ordering of its branches in the plane.

The stable snake of Duquesne and Le Gall
[
DLG02
]
allows us to assign Brownian labels to the vertices of
\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}
as follows : given
\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}
, we consider a centred Gaussian process
(Z_{v})_{v\in\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}}
such that
Z_{\rho}=0
and such that
\mathrm{Var}(Z_{v}-Z_{v^{\prime}})=d_{\alpha}(v,v^{\prime})
for every
v,v^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}
. We will review this process in detail in Section
2.2.1
. Similarly to the Brownian sphere, we can define a pseudo-distance
D^{*}_{\alpha}
on
\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}
from these labels. First, we define the following mapping

\displaystyle D_{\alpha}^{\circ}\,:\,\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}\times\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}

\displaystyle\to\mathbb{R}_{+}

\displaystyle(v,v^{\prime})

\displaystyle\mapsto D_{\alpha}^{\circ}(v,v^{\prime})=Z_{v}+Z_{v^{\prime}}-2\inf_{u\in I_{v,v^{\prime}}}Z_{u},

(1)

where
I_{v,v^{\prime}}
denotes the lexicographical interval between
v
and
v^{\prime}
that minimises
D_{\alpha}^{\circ}(v,v^{\prime})
. Indeed, for any pair of vertices
v,v^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}
, there are always two lexicographical intervals between
v
and
v^{\prime}
corresponding to the two possible ways to go from
v
to
v^{\prime}
around the tree.

The function
D_{\alpha}^{\circ}
does not satisfy the triangle inequality, which leads us to introduce

D_{\alpha}^{*}(v,v^{\prime}):=\inf\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{k}D_{\alpha}^{\circ}(v_{i-1},v_{i})\,\Big\lvert\,k\geq 1,\,v_{i}\in\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}\,\forall i\in\{0,\dots,k\},v_{0}=v,v_{k}=v^{\prime}\right\},

(2)

which is by construction the largest pseudo-distance that satisfies
D_{\alpha}^{*}\leq D_{\alpha}^{\circ}
.
We also let
\approx
denote the equivalence relation on
\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}
defined by
v\approx v^{\prime}
if and only if
D_{\alpha}^{*}(v,v^{\prime})=0
and denote by
\mathbf{m}_{\alpha}
the quotient space
\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}/\approx
. We can also endow it with a measure
\nu_{\alpha}
and a root
\rho_{\alpha}
, defined as the pushforwards of the canonical measure and root of
\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}
under the quotient operation. The
stable sphere
is the random compact rooted metric measure space
\left(\mathbf{m}_{\alpha},D_{\alpha}^{*},\nu_{\alpha},\rho_{\alpha}\right)
. Note that the special case
\alpha=2
is the Brownian sphere, and that we will focus on
\alpha<2
. Like in the Brownian setting, we expect the stable tree
\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}
to play the role of the cut-locus of the stable sphere.

Our discrete model of random quadrangulations can be defined as follows. Fix
\alpha\in(1,2)
and let
T_{n}^{(\alpha)}
be a critical Bienaymé-Galton-Watson tree with offspring distribution
\mu_{\alpha}
in the domain of attraction of an
\alpha
-stable law conditioned to have
n
edges. For ease of notation we will in fact assume that there exists a constant
c\in(0,\infty)
such that
\mu_{\alpha}([x,\infty))\sim cx^{-\alpha}
as
x\to\infty
(we anticipate however that all of our results can be adapted to incorporate slowly-varying corrections). We add labels to the vertices of
T_{n}^{(\alpha)}
by labelling the root
0
and choosing i.i.d. centred increments in
\{-1,0,+1\}
along edges. Denote by
M_{n}^{(\alpha)}
the random rooted quadrangulation with
n
faces associated to
T_{n}^{(\alpha)}
by the Cori–Vauquelin–Schaeffer correspondence,
d_{n}
the graph distance on
M_{n}^{(\alpha)}
,
\nu_{n}
the uniform probability measure on its vertices, and
\rho_{n}
its root vertex. We conjecture the following convergence:

Conjecture 1.1
.

For
\alpha\in(1,2)
, there exists a constant
\kappa>0
that depends on
\mu_{\alpha}
, such that the following convergence in law holds in the pointed Gromov-Hausdorff-Prokhorov topology:

\left(M_{n}^{(\alpha)},n^{-\frac{\alpha-1}{2\alpha}}\,d_{n},\nu_{n},\rho_{n}\right)\underset{n\to\infty}{\rightarrow}\,\left(\mathbf{m}_{\alpha},\kappa D_{\alpha}^{*},\nu_{\alpha},\rho_{\alpha}\right).

The starting point of this paper is the following result.

Theorem 1.2
.

The sequence
\left(M_{n}^{(\alpha)},n^{-\frac{\alpha-1}{2\alpha}}\,d_{n},\nu_{n},\rho_{n}\right)_{n\geq 0}
is tight in the pointed Gromov-Hausdorff-Prokohorov topology. Moreover, any subsequential limit
(M,D,\nu,\rho)
can be defined as a quotient space of
\left(\mathbf{m}_{\alpha},D^{*}_{\alpha},\nu_{\alpha},\rho_{\alpha}\right)
.

The main contribution of the paper is in fact a study of volumes of balls at typical points in a limit
(M,D,\nu,\rho)
. We start with the Hausdorff dimension.

Theorem 1.3
.

Any subsequential limit
(M,D,\nu,\rho)
has Hausdorff dimension
\frac{2\alpha}{\alpha-1}
almost surely, as does
\left(\mathbf{m}_{\alpha},D^{*}_{\alpha},\nu_{\alpha},\rho_{\alpha}\right)
.

Our volume results are actually a lot more precise than this. As is the case for stable trees (and for similar reasons), the volumes of balls at typical points experience logarithmic fluctuations above their typical value.

Theorem 1.4
.

Take any
\varepsilon>0
. Let
(M,D,\nu,\rho)
be a subsequential limit appearing in Theorem
1.2
. Then, almost surely, it holds for
\nu
-almost every
x
in
M
that

\limsup_{r\downarrow 0}\frac{\nu(B(x,r))}{r^{\frac{2\alpha}{\alpha-1}}(\log r^{-1})^{\frac{2\alpha+\varepsilon}{\alpha-1}}}=0,\qquad\limsup_{r\downarrow 0}\frac{\nu(B(x,r))}{r^{\frac{2\alpha}{\alpha-1}}(\log r^{-1})^{\frac{1-\varepsilon}{\alpha-1}}}=\infty.

One can ask whether there is a precise regularly varying gauge function
g(r)
such that the limit supremum appearing in Theorem
1.4
is equal to a finite positive constant almost surely. In the case of stable trees, this question has been answered negatively by Duquesne
[
Duq10
, Proposition 1.9]
(see also
[
Duq10
, Theorem 1.10]
for a similar statement for the exact Hausdorff measure). We have no reason to expect otherwise for
(M,D,\nu,\rho)
.

Since
D\leq D_{\alpha}^{*}
, the first result of Theorem
1.4
clearly also holds for
\left(\mathbf{m}_{\alpha},D^{*}_{\alpha},\nu_{\alpha},\rho_{\alpha}\right)
, and in fact an inspection of the proof shows that the same is true for the second result.

We now turn to a discussion of fluctuations below typical values. The fluctuations are log-logarithmic, as is again the case for stable trees.

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
(M,D,\nu,\rho)
be a subsequential limit appearing in Theorem
1.2
. Then, almost surely, for
\nu
-almost every
x
in
M

\liminf_{r\downarrow 0}\frac{\nu(B(x,r))}{r^{\frac{2\alpha}{\alpha-1}}(\log\log r^{-1})^{-\frac{1}{\alpha-1}}}<\infty,\qquad\liminf_{r\downarrow 0}\frac{\nu(B(x,r))}{r^{\frac{2\alpha}{\alpha-1}}(\log\log r^{-1})^{-\frac{32\alpha}{\alpha-1}}}=\infty.

Moreover, almost surely,

\liminf_{r\downarrow 0}\frac{\inf_{x\in M}\nu(B(x,r))}{r^{\frac{2\alpha}{\alpha-1}}(\log r^{-1})^{-\frac{32\alpha}{\alpha-1}}}>0.

It was similarly shown in
[
Duq12
]
that stable trees admit
r^{\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}}(\log\log r)^{-\frac{1}{\alpha-1}}
as a regular packing gauge function, meaning that the limit infimum when renormalising volumes by this function is almost surely a finite, non-zero constant. In addition, Duquesne
[
Duq12
, Theorem 1.1]
showed that the packing measure associated with the gauge function
r^{\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}}(\log\log r^{-1})^{-\frac{1}{\alpha-1}}
coincides with the natural volume measure on stable trees; we leave it open whether a similar result is true for
(M,D,\nu,\rho)
. The exponent of
\frac{32\alpha}{\alpha-1}
appearing in the second and third results is certainly not optimal.

We remark that do not expect global supremal volumes to be as tightly concentrated as global infima; in fact it is fairly easy to show that there will exist balls
B(x,r)
in
M
of volume at least
r^{2}
. This is due to the fact that there are also polynomial volume fluctuations at hubs in the underlying stable tree (see Remark
4.14
).

The main tool to obtain lower bounds on volumes is the fact that
D\leq D_{\alpha}^{*}
and hence it is sufficient to lower bound the volumes of balls measured with respect to the metric
D_{\alpha}^{*}
. This can be achieved by controlling the fluctuations of the snake in various intervals. In order to obtain upper bounds on volumes of balls, we will instead need a lower bound on
D
. This is done by relating
D
to the metric on the associated cactus; see Lemma
3.14
for the precise result.

The heavier upper tails on the volumes allows us to make an important distinction between
(M,D)
and the Brownian sphere. Let
v^{*}
denote the point in
\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}
where the minimum of
Z
is attained (like in the Brownian case, it is unique almost surely, see Proposition
2.2
). By comparing the bounds in Theorem
1.4
with bounds on
\nu(B(v^{*},r))
obtained in
[
ACM24
]
, we deduce that
v^{*}
is not a typical point of
(M,D,\nu,\rho)
.

Corollary 1.6
.

The vertex
v^{*}
is not a typical point in
(M,D)
, meaning that
v^{*}
cannot be coupled with an independent point chosen according to
\nu
.

We end the introduction with some comments on related models. At first sight one might guess that the subsequential scaling limits of
\left(M_{n}^{(\alpha)},n^{-\frac{\alpha-1}{2\alpha}}\,d_{n},\nu_{n},\rho_{n}\right)_{n\geq 0}
may be related to the dual maps, or the images by Tutte’s bijection, of the stable maps of
[
LGM10
]
. Indeed, the latter is a random map model in which the size of typical
faces
have stably-decaying tails, while the typical
vertex
degrees are heavy-tailed in our model (see
Remark

3.8
for a more detailed discussion). However, our result on the Hausdorff dimension clearly rules this out; while our limits have Hausdorff dimension
\frac{2\alpha}{\alpha-1}
, the stable duals have dimension
\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-3/2}
when
\frac{3}{2}<\alpha<2
, and in fact have exponential volume growth when
\alpha<\frac{3}{2}

[
BC17
]
(in the critical case of
\alpha=\frac{3}{2}
the volume growth is stretched exponential
[
Kam23
]
). Nonetheless, like the limits of Theorem
1.2
, scaling limits of these dual graphs are believed to be spherical when
\frac{3}{2}<\alpha<2
. The stable maps themselves, on the other hand, are not believed to be spherical, and instead have more complicated fractal-like topology.

The tightness of Theorem
1.2
is very straightforward to establish; the main contributions of the paper are the volume results. Throughout most of the paper we will assume implicitly that we have already restricted to an appropriate subsequence where the maps converge, without explicitly stating it every time.

Organisation.
The paper is organised as follows. In Section
2
we give some background on stable trees and stable snakes, and in Section
3
we give the definitions of all of the map models. In Section
4
and
5
we respectively consider the upper and lower fluctuations of volumes around uniform points. Finally we conclude in Section
6
with a quick deduction of the Hausdorff dimension.

2.1.1
Stable Lévy processes and the Itô excursion measure

We consider a spectrally positive stable process Lévy

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\exp\{-\lambda X_{t}\}\right]=\exp\{-t\lambda^{\alpha}\}.

(3)

2.1.2
Stable height processes and exploration processes

We will see later in Section
3
that discrete plane trees can be coded by different functions. The same is true in the continuum; in the case of
\alpha
-stable trees, we first code using a spectrally positive
\alpha
-stable Lévy process or excursion
X
(this plays the same role as the Łukasiewicz path in the discrete setting - we will define this in Section
3.2
). The
height function

H
is then defined by setting, for
t\geq 0
,

H_{t}=\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{0}^{t}\mathbbm{1}\{X_{s}<I_{s}^{t}+\varepsilon\}ds,

(8)

where
I_{s,t}=\inf_{s\leq r\leq t}X_{r}
. For each
t\geq 0
, this limit exists in probability by
[
DLG02
, Lemma 1.2.1]
. Moreover, it follows from
[
DLG02
, Theorem 1.4.3]
that
H
almost surely has a continuous modification, and we will assume henceforth that
H
is indeed continuous.

The height function is not in general Markovian; to overcome this difficulty we introduce the exploration process, a Markovian random measure that encodes the height process. Let
M_{f}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
denote the set of finite measures on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
. The

(\rho_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is a process such that for each
t\geq 0
,
\rho_{t}
is the measure in
M_{f}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
satisfying

\displaystyle\langle\rho_{t},f\rangle=\int_{0}^{t}f(H_{s})d_{s}(I_{s,t})

(9)

for all bounded measurable functions
f:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}
. This implies that

\displaystyle\langle\rho_{t},1\rangle=I_{t,t}-I_{0,t}=X_{t}-I_{t}.

(10)

It follows from the definition that for each
t\geq 0
,
\text{supp }\rho_{t}=[0,H_{t}]
. In particular this means that
H
can be defined as a functional of
\rho
; for a given realisation
\rho^{\prime}
we denote the associated height process by
H(\rho^{\prime})
.

We also introduce the dual exploration process. Note that it follows from (

) that
\rho_{t}
can also be defined by

\displaystyle\rho_{t}(dr)=\sum_{0<s\leq t:X_{s-}\leq I_{s,t}}(I_{s,t}-X_{s-})\delta_{H_{s}}(dr)

for all
t>0,r\in[0,H_{t}]
. The
dual exploration process
is similarly defined by

\displaystyle{\hat{\rho}}_{t}(dr)=\sum_{0<s\leq t:X_{s-}\leq I_{s,t}}(X_{s}-I_{s,t})\delta_{H_{s}}(dr).

(11)

The height function appearing in (
8
) in fact codes a whole forest of trees. We will sometimes instead code a single tree by replacing
X
with
X^{\text{exc}}
in (
8
). This corresponds to the law of
H
under the measure
N^{(1)}
. By scaling, this means that we can also make sense of it under
N^{(s)}
for any

, and therefore under
N
using (

). The pair
(\rho_{t},{\hat{\rho}}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
can be similarly defined under
N^{(1)}
or
N
. Under
N
, it holds that (recall that
\sigma
denotes the lifetime of the excursion)

(\rho_{t},{\hat{\rho}}_{t})_{t\geq 0}\overset{(d)}{=}({\hat{\rho}}_{(\sigma-t)-},\rho_{(\sigma-t)-})_{t\geq 0}.

(12)

2.1.3
Stable trees

To define the stable tree under
N^{(1)}
, we first define
H
using (
8
) but with
X
replaced by
X^{\text{exc}}
, then for
s<t
define

m_{s,t}=\inf_{s\wedge t\leq r\leq s\vee t}H_{r}.

(13)

We then define a pseudodistance on
[0,1]
by

d_{\alpha}(s,t)=H_{s}+H_{t}-2m_{s,t}

(14)

whenever
s\leq t
. We then define an equivalence relation on
[0,1]
by saying
s\sim t
if and only if
d_{\alpha}(s,t)=0
, and set
\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}
to be the quotient space
([0,1]/\sim,d_{\alpha})
. We also define a measure
\lambda
on
\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}
as the image of Lebesgue measure on
[0,1]
under the quotient operation.

It follows from the construction that
\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}
is an
\mathbb{R}
-tree in the sense of
[
DMT96
]
, essentially meaning that it is a continuum object sharing standard properties of discrete trees (see
[
DLG05
, Definition 2.1]
for the precise definition).

Properties of
\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}
are determined by the process
X^{\text{exc}}
. Letting
p:[0,1]\rightarrow\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}
be the canonical projection, there is a distinguished vertex
\rho=p(0)
which we call the root of
\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}
. Also, for
u,v\in\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}
, we denote by
[[u,v]]
the unique geodesic between
u
and
v
, and we say
u\preceq v
if and only if
u\in[[\rho,v]]
. Given
u,v\in\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}
, we say
z
is the
most recent common ancestor
of
u
and
v
, written
z=u\wedge v
, if
z
is the unique element in
\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}
with
[[\rho,u]]\cap[[\rho,v]]=[[\rho,z]]
. We say that a subtree is
grafted to the path

[[u,v]]
if it is coded by an excursion interval of
X^{\text{exc}}
away from its infimum, and its root (i.e. the projection of the start point of this excursion) is in
[[u,v]]\setminus\{u,v\}
.

The relation
\preceq
on
\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}
can be recovered from
X^{\text{exc}}
by defining

s\preceq t\text{ if and only if }s\leq t\text{ and }X^{\text{exc}}_{s^{-}}\leq I_{s}^{t}.

It is possible to show that the two definitions of
\preceq
given above define partial orders on
\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}
and
[0,1]
respectively, and are compatible with
p
in the sense that, if
u,v\in\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}
, then
u\preceq v
if and only if there exist
s,t\in[0,1]
with
p(s)=u
,
p(t)=v
and such that
s\preceq t
. Additionally, if
s\wedge t
is the most recent common ancestor of
s
and
t
(with respect to
\preceq
), then it can be verified that
p(s\wedge t)=p(s)\wedge p(t)
.

The
multiplicity
of a vertex
u\in\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}
is defined as the number of connected components of
\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}\setminus\{u\}
. A vertex
u
is called a
leaf
if it has multiplicity one, and a
branch point
if it has multiplicity at least three. It can be shown that
\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}
only has branch points with infinite multiplicity, called
hubs
, and that
u\in\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}
is a hub if and only if there exists
s\in[0,1]
such that
p(s)=u
and
\Delta_{s}:=X^{\text{exc}}_{s}-X^{\text{exc}}_{s^{-}}>0
. See for instance Theorem 4.6 and Theorem 4.7 of
[
DLG05
]
. The quantity
\Delta_{s}
gives a measure of the number of children of
u
.

For any fixed
t\geq 0
, the quantity appearing in (
10
) can be interpreted as the sum of the sizes of the hubs appearing on the right hand side of the branch from the root to
p(t)
. Similarly
\langle{\hat{\rho}}_{t},1\rangle
can be interpreted as the sum of the sizes of the hubs appearing on the left hand side of the branch from the root to
p(t)
.

We can similarly define
\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}
under
N
, rather than
N^{(1)}
, by replacing
X^{\text{exc}}
with an excursion defined under
N
in the definition of
H
, or by using (
6
). We can similarly define a forest of trees by retaining
X
in (
8
) (each excursion of
X-I
above its infimum then codes a single tree). It is also a well-known fact that the genealogy of
\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}
encodes a continuous-state branching process with branching mechanism
\psi
, but we will not use this connection and refer to
[
DLG02
]
for further background.

2.2
Stable snake with Brownian spatial displacements

2.2.1
Definition

We now give a brief introduction to Lévy snakes in the particular case of snakes on stable trees with Brownian spatial displacements. We will often simply refer to this as a “stable snake” in what follows. A full introduction in the more general setting of Lévy trees is given in
[
DLG02
, Section 4]
; we refer there for further background.

Informally, a stable snake is simply a real-valued stochastic process indexed by a stable tree. Just as we had to introduce the exploration process as a Markovian version of the height process, it will be more convenient to keep track of the history of this stochastic process and we therefore instead consider a path-valued process. In particular, we let
\mathcal{W}
denote the set of continuous real-valued functions defined on a compact interval of the form
[0,\zeta]
for some
\zeta>0
. Given
w\in\mathcal{W}
, we denote its domain by
[0,\zeta_{w}]
and set
\widehat{w}=w(\zeta_{w})
. We call
\zeta_{w}
the
lifetime
of
w
and call
\widehat{w}
the
tip
of
w
.

We then define a topology on
\mathcal{W}
using the distance function

d_{\mathcal{W}}(w,w^{\prime})=|\zeta_{w}-\zeta_{w^{\prime}}|+\sup_{r\geq 0}|w(r\wedge\zeta_{w})-w^{\prime}(r\wedge\zeta_{w^{\prime}})|.

Before defining the stable snake, we first define a snake with Brownian spatial displacements driven by a deterministic continuous function
h:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
with initial condition
w_{0}:[0,h(0)]\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
. This is a random variable taking values in
\mathcal{W}
, or in other words in the space of path-valued processes (one should have in mind that
h
plays the role of a height function of a deterministic forest, and the snake is obtained by adding random spatial displacements).

For every
x\in\mathbb{R}
, we denote by
\Pi_{x}
the distribution of a standard linear Brownian motion
\xi=(\xi_{t})_{t\geq 0}
started at
x
. It is shown in
[
DLG02
, Section 4.1.1]
that for any such choice of
(h,w_{0})
there exists a unique probability measure on
\mathcal{W}
such that the following three properties hold.

1.

\zeta_{W_{s}}=h(s)
for all
s\in[0,\sigma]
.

2.

For all
s,s^{\prime}\geq 0
, we have that
W_{s}(t)=W_{s^{\prime}}(t)
for all
t\leq m_{s^{\prime},s}
.

3.

Conditionally on
W_{s^{\prime}}
, the path
W_{s}:[0,\zeta_{s}]\to\mathbb{R}
is such that
(W_{s}(m_{s^{\prime},s}+t)-W_{s}(m_{s^{\prime},s}))_{t\in[\zeta_{s}-m_{s^{\prime},s}]}
has the same law as the Brownian motion
(\xi_{t})_{t\in[0,\zeta_{s}-m_{s^{\prime},s}]}
under
\Pi_{W_{s^{\prime}}(m_{s^{\prime},s})}
.

In other words, conditionally on
W_{s^{\prime}}
, the path
W_{s}
satisfies the second point for
t\leq m_{s,s^{\prime}}
(which informally corresponds to the snake along the path from the root to the most recent common ancestor of
p(s)
and
p(s^{\prime})
), and for
t>m_{s,s^{\prime}}
evolves as an independent linear Brownian motion (which corresponds to the rest of the branch to
p(s)
). Given
h
and
w_{0}
as above we let
Q_{w_{0}}^{h}
denote the law of this process.

The
stable snake with Brownian spatial displacements
is the pair
(\rho,W)
obtained by replacing
h
with the random height function
H
defined from a Lévy process
X
as in (
8
).

We will also use the notation

\widehat{W}_{s}=W_{s}(\zeta_{s})

(15)

to denote the tip of the snake at time
s
.

We will sometimes refer to this construction as simply the “the snake” or “the stable snake” in this article.

2.2.2
Excursion measures for snakes

We will need to consider the law of a snake where the exploration process
\rho
is first sampled under
N
or
N^{(1)}
, and with initial condition
W_{0}(0)=x\in\mathbb{R}
. Informally, under
N^{(1)}
this is the law of a stable snake on a single stable tree of total mass
1
, started from the point

this is the law of a stable snake on a stable tree sampled according to the Itô measure, started from the point

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{N}_{x}(d\rho,dW)=N_{0}(d\rho)Q_{x}^{H(\rho)}(dW).

N
with

above. It is explained in

that the point

is regular and recurrent for the process

(which is actually Markov), and moreover that the process

is a local time at

for this process, and hence this definition makes sense as the excursion measure of
(\rho,W)
away from
(0,x)
associated with the local time
-I

By excursion theory, it moreover follows that, if
(\alpha_{i},\beta_{i})_{i\in\mathcal{I}}

\mathbb{P}_{x}

\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\delta(-I_{\alpha_{i}},\rho^{(i)},W^{(i)})

is a Poisson point measure with intensity
\mathbbm{1}_{[0,\infty)}(\ell)d\ell\mathbb{N}_{x}(d\rho,dW)
(where

denotes Lebesgue measure).

Note that, under
\mathbb{N}_{x}
, the function
(\widehat{W}_{s})_{s\in[0,\sigma]}
can also be viewed as a function of the tree

coded by the height function

driving the snake that appears in (

[

for any

such that

. We also will sometimes abuse notation and interchangeably use
Z_{a}

and

.

We denote by
\mathcal{R}
the

of the stable snake:

\mathcal{R}:=\left[\inf_{a\in\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}}Z_{a},\sup_{a\in\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}}Z_{a}\right].

(17)

The measure
\mathbb{N}_{x}
also inherits a scaling property from that of

and the Brownian scaling property. For any
\lambda>0
we define a mapping
\Phi_{\lambda}:(\rho,W)\to(\rho^{\lambda},W^{\lambda})

\displaystyle\rho^{\lambda}_{t}(dr)=\lambda^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}\rho_{t/\lambda}(d(r/\lambda^{1-\frac{1}{\alpha}})),\qquad W_{t}^{\lambda}(s)=\lambda^{\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)}W_{t/\lambda}(s/\lambda^{1-\frac{1}{\alpha}}),

(18)

so that the law of
(\rho^{\lambda},W^{\lambda})
under
\mathbb{N}_{x}
is equal to
\lambda^{1/\alpha}\mathbb{N}_{\lambda^{\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)}x}
.

Note that we can use (
6
) and (
18
) to calculate that

\displaystyle N\left(1-e^{-\lambda\sigma}\right)=\lambda^{1/\alpha}.

(19)

We also briefly recall a few useful results. The first is the following from
[
ACM24
, Equation (38)]
(recall the definition of the range from (
17
)):

\mathbb{N}_{x}\left[\mathbbm{1}\{0\in\mathcal{R}\}\right]=\left(\frac{\alpha+1}{(\alpha-1)^{2}}\,\frac{1}{x^{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha-1}},

(20)

The second concerns the Hölder continuity of the stable snake:

Lemma 2.1
.

(Lemma 6.1 in
[
ACM24
]
).

(a)

N^{(1)}
almost surely, for any
\gamma<1-\frac{1}{\alpha}
, the height function is Hölder continuous with parameter
\gamma
.

(b)

Take any
b\in(0,1/2)
. Then
\mathbb{N}^{(1)}
a.e. there exists
\varepsilon_{0}>0
such that for every
s,t\geq 0
with
d(s,t)\leq\varepsilon_{0}
,

|\widehat{W}_{t}-\widehat{W}_{s}|\leq d(s,t)^{b}.

The third concerns the location of the minimum of the snake over the interval
[0,\sigma]
, which is denoted by
s^{*}
.

Proposition 2.2

(Proposition 2.8 in
[
ACM24
]
)
.

\mathbb{N}
-almost everywhere, the time
s^{*}\in[0,\sigma]
at which
\widehat{W}

We also take this opportunity to define the
occupation measure of the snake re-rooted at its minimum
as the random measure
\overline{\mathcal{I}}
satisfying

\langle\overline{\mathcal{I}},f\rangle=\int_{0}^{\sigma}f(\widehat{W}_{s}-\widehat{W}_{s^{*}})ds.

(21)

2.2.3
Uniform re-rooting invariance

We will use a property of stable snakes know as
uniform re-rooting invariance
. To define this under
\mathbb{N}_{0}
for an excursion with lifetime
\sigma>0
, we follow the presentation of
[
LGW06
, Section 2.3]
for the Brownian case. We first need to define the notion of a stable tree and of a snake rooted at a time point
s\in[0,\sigma]
. To this end, given
\sigma>0
we first set

s\oplus r=\begin{cases}s+r&\text{ if }s+r\leq\sigma,\\
s+r-\sigma&\text{ if }s+r>\sigma.\end{cases}

We then define the re-rooted lifetime and snake-tip processes by

\displaystyle\zeta^{[s]}_{r}

\displaystyle=\zeta_{s}+\zeta_{s\oplus r}-2\inf_{u\in[s\wedge(s\oplus r),s\vee(s\oplus r)]}\zeta_{u}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\widehat{W}^{[s]}_{r}=\widehat{W}_{s\oplus r}-\widehat{W}_{s}

(22)

for all
r\in[0,\sigma]
. Note that the entire sequence of snake trajectories can be recovered directly from (
22
) via

\displaystyle{W}^{[s]}_{r}(t)=\widehat{W}^{[s]}_{\sup\{u\leq r:\zeta^{[s]}_{u}=t\}},

or equivalently (
22
) does indeed suffice to define the entire re-rooted process.

Informally,
(\zeta^{[s]}_{r})_{r\geq 0}
codes a stable tree rooted at the vertex previously labelled
s
, and
\widehat{W}^{[s]}_{r}
codes the spatial displacements along the branches of the new tree from the new root to the vertex which now has label
r
.

The re-rooting invariance statement is as follows.

Proposition 2.3

(Proposition 2.1 in
[
ACM24
]
)
.

For every non-negative function
F
on
\mathbb{R}_{+}\times C(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathcal{W})
it holds that

\mathbb{N}_{0}\left(\int_{0}^{\sigma}F(s,{W}^{[s]})ds\right)=\mathbb{N}_{0}\left(\int_{0}^{\sigma}F(s,W)ds\right).

2.2.4
Spinal decompositions

We now present an important spinal decomposition formula, which will allow us to express certain integral functionals of the stable snake under
\mathbb{N}_{0}
using a decomposition over subtrees and subtrajectories along a branch to a uniform point in the underlying tree.

To this end, we let
(U^{(1)},U^{(2)})
be a two dimensional subordinator defined on some probability space
(\Omega_{0},\mathcal{F}_{0},P^{0})
, started at
(0,0)
and with Laplace transform (recall that
\psi(\lambda)=\lambda^{\alpha}
)

\displaystyle E^{0}[\exp\{-\lambda_{1}U^{(1)}_{t}-\lambda_{2}U^{(2)}_{t}\}]=\begin{cases}\exp\left\{-\frac{t(\psi(\lambda_{1})-\psi(\lambda_{2}))}{\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}}\right\}&\text{ if }\lambda_{1}\neq\lambda_{2},\\
\exp\{-t\psi^{\prime}(\lambda_{1})\}&\text{ if }\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{2}.\end{cases}

(23)

The marginal of each of
U^{(1)}
and
U^{(2)}
is that of a subordinator with Laplace exponent
\tilde{\psi}(\lambda):=\frac{\psi(\lambda)}{\lambda}=\lambda^{\alpha-1}
, and the sum
U^{(1)}+U^{(2)}
is a subordinator with Laplace exponent
\psi^{\prime}(\lambda)=\alpha\,\lambda^{\alpha-1}
. Similarly to (
4
), this entails that

\displaystyle\begin{split}\tilde{\psi}(\lambda)&=\int_{0}^{\infty}(1-e^{-\lambda r})\tilde{\pi}(dr)=\frac{C_{\alpha}\lambda}{2-\alpha}+\int_{0}^{\infty}(1-e^{-\lambda r}-\lambda r\mathbbm{1}\{r\leq 1\})\tilde{\pi}(dr),\\
\psi^{\prime}(\lambda)&=\int_{0}^{\infty}(1-e^{-\lambda r})\pi^{\prime}(dr)=\frac{\alpha C_{\alpha}\lambda}{2-\alpha}+\int_{0}^{\infty}(1-e^{-\lambda r}-\lambda r\mathbbm{1}\{r\leq 1\})\pi^{\prime}(dr).\end{split}

(24)

where
\tilde{\pi}(dr)=C_{\alpha}r^{-\alpha}dr
and
\pi^{\prime}(dr)=\alpha C_{\alpha}r^{-\alpha}dr
(these can be calculated directly from (
4
) and (
5
) but see
[
Ber96
, Section III.1]
for further background on stable subordinators). We note only that
\tilde{\psi}
can be easily recovered from
\psi^{\prime}
by using Fubini’s theorem and noting that
U^{(1)}
is obtained from
U^{(1)}+U^{(2)}
through the fact that a jump at time
t
of
U^{(1)}
corresponds to a uniform portion of the corresponding jump at time
t
of
U^{(1)}+U^{(2)}

We will continue to use the notation
\psi,\psi^{\prime}
and
\tilde{\psi}
throughout the paper to refer to these three functions above.

For each
a>0
, let
(J_{a},\hat{J}_{a})
denote a pair of random measures given by

\displaystyle(J_{a},\hat{J}_{a})(dt)=(\mathbbm{1}_{[0,a]}(t)dU_{t}^{(1)},\mathbbm{1}_{[0,a]}(t)dU_{t}^{(2)})

(25)

For
h>0
, under
E^{0}\otimes\Pi_{x}
, conditionally on the measure
(J_{h}+\hat{J}_{h})
and the Brownian trajectory
(\xi_{s})_{0\leq s\leq h}
, we consider an independent Poisson point process on
[0,h]\times\mathcal{W}
, denoted by

\mathcal{P}:=\sum_{i\in I}\delta_{a_{i},w_{i}},

and with intensity

(J_{h}+\hat{J}_{h})(da)\otimes\mathbb{N}_{\xi_{a}}(dw).

(26)

The key result is as follows. In the proposition we let
\mathcal{R}
denote the
range
of the snake
W
, and we let
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{P}}
denote expectation with respect to the point process
\mathcal{P}
defined above. Also recall that
\Pi_{x}
denotes the law of a standard linear Brownian motion started at
x
.

Proposition 2.4

(Proposition 2.4 in
[
ACM24
]
)
.

For every
x\in\mathbb{R}
and every non-negative measurable functional
\phi
taking values in
M_{f}(\mathbb{R}_{+})^{2}\times\mathcal{W}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{N}_{x}\left[\int_{0}^{\sigma}\phi(\rho_{s},{\hat{\rho}}_{s},W_{s},\mathcal{R},\sigma)ds\right]

\displaystyle\qquad=\int_{0}^{\infty}dh\,E^{0}\otimes\Pi_{x}\left[\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{P}}\left[\phi\left(J_{h},\hat{J}_{h},(\xi_{s})_{0\leq s\leq h},\overline{\bigcup_{i\in I}\left(\zeta_{a_{i}}+\mathcal{R}(w_{i})\right)},\sum_{i\in I}\sigma(w_{i})\right)\middle|(J,\hat{J},\xi)\right]\right].

Informally, the proposition says that the sizes hubs along the branch from
\rho
to a uniform point are given by the jumps of the subordinator
(J,\hat{J})
, and that the subtrees grafted to the hubs form a Poisson process coded by the Itô excursion measure.

In order to carry out calculations for Poisson processes such as those appearing in
Proposition

2.4
, and with respect to the measures
J
and
\hat{J}
, we remind the reader of two standard formulas.

Firstly note that, using the Markov property and stationarity of increments, the formula (
23
) easily generalises to the following for continuous nonnegative functions
f
.

\displaystyle\begin{split}E^{0}\left[\exp\left\{-\int_{0}^{t}f(s)d(U^{(1)}+U^{(2)})_{s}\right\}\right]&=\exp\left\{-\int_{0}^{t}\psi^{\prime}(f(s))ds\right\}\\
E^{0}\left[\exp\left\{-\int_{0}^{t}f(s)dU^{(1)}_{s}\right\}\right]&=\exp\left\{-\int_{0}^{t}\tilde{\psi}(f(s))ds\right\}.\end{split}

(27)

Secondly, we will use the following formula for Poisson point processes several times.

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
P=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\delta(\omega_{i})
denote a Poisson point process on a space
\Omega
with intensity measure
\hat{\mu}
and let
f
be a function
\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
. Then

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\prod_{i\in\mathcal{I}}f(\omega_{i})\right]=\exp\left\{\int_{\Omega}(f(\omega)-1)\hat{\mu}(d\omega)\right\}.

2.2.5
Local time at level
a

It will also be useful to introduce the notion of the local time at a certain level of
\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}
. For

, Duquesne and Le Gall
[

showed that one can construct a local time measure, supported on vertices at height

in

, and moreover such that the canonical measure

satisfies

\upsilon=\int_{0}^{\infty}\ell^{a}da,

(28)

N

can therefore be interpreted as a vertex chosen “uniformly at level

” in
\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}
.

We will use the following first moment formula, which is a special case of
[
DLG02
, Proposition 4.3.2]
or
[
RRO22
, Equation (3.4)]
.

Proposition 2.6
.

For every domain
D
, every

, and every non-negative measurable functional

\displaystyle\mathbb{N}_{x}\left[\int_{0}^{\sigma}\ell^{a}(ds)\,\phi(\rho_{s},{\hat{\rho}}_{s},W_{s})\right]=E^{0}\otimes\Pi_{x}\left(\phi(J_{a},\hat{J}_{a},(\xi_{s})_{0\leq s\leq a})\right).

Note that this also implies that

3
Trees, maps and scaling limits

3.1
The pointed Gromov-Hausdorff-Prokhorov topology

To study the asymptotic metric-measure structure of a large random map, we will make use of the
pointed Gromov-Hausdorff-Prokhorov
distance, defined between (isometry classes of) compact rooted metric-measure spaces. In particular, we let
\mathbb{F}^{c}
denote the set of quadruples
(F,R,\mu,\rho)
such that
(F,R)
is a compact metric space,
\mu
is a locally finite Borel measure of full support on
F
, and
\rho
is a distinguished point of
F
, which we call the root, considered up to root-preserving, measure-preserving isometries.

Let
(E_{1},d_{1},\nu_{1},\rho_{1})
and
(E_{2},d_{2},\nu_{2},\rho_{2})
be two compact rooted metric-measure spaces in
\mathbb{F}^{c}
. The pointed Gromov-Hausdorff-Prokhorov distance between them is defined as

d_{GHP}(E_{1},E_{2})=\inf\left\{d_{H}(\varphi_{1}(E_{1}),\varphi_{2}(E_{2}))+d(\varphi_{1}(\rho_{1}),\varphi_{2}(\rho_{2}))+d_{P}(\varphi_{1}^{*}\nu_{1},\varphi_{2}^{*}\nu_{2})\right\},

(29)

where the infimum is over all isometric embeddings
\varphi_{i}:E_{i}\to E
of
E_{1}
and
E_{2}
into the same metric space
(E,d)
,
d_{H}
is the Hausdorff distance between compact subsets of
E
, and
d_{P}
denotes the Prokhorov distance between measures on
E
, as defined in
[
Bil68
, Chapter 1]
. It is well-known (for example, see
[
ADH13
, Theorem 2.3]
) that this defines a metric on the space of equivalence classes of
\mathbb{F}^{c}
, where we say that two spaces
(F,R,\mu,\rho)
and
(F^{\prime},R^{\prime},\mu^{\prime},\rho^{\prime})
are equivalent if there is a measure and root-preserving isometry between them.

We can also consider the
pointed Gromov-Hausdorff
distance
d_{GH}(\cdot,\cdot)
between two spaces in
\mathbb{F}^{c}
; this is defined by removing the term involving the Prokhorov distance from (
29
) above. A useful characterisation of the pointed Gromov-Hausdorff distance can be made via
correspondences
.

Definition 3.1
.

If
(E_{1},d_{1})
and
(E_{2},d_{2})
are two compact metric spaces, a
correspondence
between
E_{1}
and
E_{2}
is a subset
R\subseteq E_{1}\times E_{2}
such that, for every
x_{1}\in E_{1}
, there exists
x_{2}\in E_{2}
such that
(x_{1},x_{2})\in R
, and for every
x_{2}\in E_{2}
, there exists
x_{1}\in E_{1}
such that
(x_{1},x_{2})\in R
.

If
R
is a correspondence between
(E_{1},d_{1})
and
(E_{2},d_{2})
, the
distortion
of
R
is given by

\textnormal{dis}_{R}:=\sup\{\lvert d_{1}(x_{1},y_{1})-d_{2}(x_{2},y_{2})\rvert;\,(x_{1},x_{2}),(y_{1},y_{2})\in R\}.

As was done in the Brownian case, we will use the following theorem to establish tightness in the Gromov-Hausdorff topology.

Theorem 3.2

(Theorem 7.3.25 in
[
BBI01
]
)
.

Let
(E_{1},d_{1},\nu_{1},\rho_{1})
and
(E_{2},d_{2},\nu_{2},\rho_{2})
be in
\mathbb{F}^{c}
. Then

d_{GH}((E_{1},d_{1},\nu_{1},\rho_{1}),(E_{2},d_{2},\nu_{2},\rho_{2}))=\frac{1}{2}\inf\textnormal{dis}_{R},

where the infimum is taken over the set of correspondences between
E_{1}
and
E_{2}
containing the pair
(\rho_{1},\rho_{2})
.

In this article, we will prove (subsequential) pointed Gromov-Hausdorff-Prokhorov convergence by first proving pointed Gromov-Hausdorff convergence using correspondences, and then show Prokhorov convergence of the measures on the appropriate metric space.

3.2
Discrete trees

A
tree
is a connected graph (i.e. with vertices and edges) with no cycles. A
plane tree
is a tree that is embedded in the sphere (it can be equivalently embedded in the plane). In this paper we will deal mainly with rooted plane trees, meaning that there is a distinguished oriented edge known as the
root edge
. The initial vertex of the root edge is called the
root vertex
. This induces the notion of genealogy: we say that
u
is the
parent
of
v
, or
v
is a
child
of
u
if
u
and
v
are neighbours and
u
is strictly closer to the root vertex.

In what follows, we denote by
\mathbf{T}
the set of rooted plane trees, and by
\mathbf{T}_{n}
the set of rooted plane trees with
n
edges.

To describe rooted plane trees, we will make use of the
Ulam-Harris encoding
(see
[
Nev86
]
for further background), in which vertices of a tree
\mathfrak{t}\in\mathbf{T}
are labelled by elements of
\bigcup_{n\geq 0}\mathbb{N}^{n}
, where
\mathbb{N}=\{1,2,\ldots\}
and where we set
\mathbb{N}^{0}=\varnothing
. The root of
\mathfrak{t}
receives Ulam-Harris label
\varnothing
, and the other labels are determined recursively, with the children of vertex
v=v_{1}v_{2}\dotsm v_{h}\in\mathbb{N}^{h}
receiving Ulam-Harris labels
(vi,1\leq i\leq c_{\mathfrak{t}}(v))
(where
c_{\mathfrak{t}}(v)
is the number of children of
v
in
\mathfrak{t}
), in the left-to-right ordering given by the embedding of
\mathfrak{t}
in the plane, in which the outer endpoint of the root edge is designated to be the leftmost child of the root. For
v=v_{1}v_{2}\dotsm v_{h}
, we denote by
\lvert v\rvert=h
the generation of
v
.

Let
\mathfrak{t}\in\mathbf{T}
, and
u,v\in V(\mathfrak{t})
. We denote by
[[u,v]]
the set of vertices of
\mathfrak{t}
that lie on the unique shortest path from
u
to
v
in
\mathfrak{t}
, and
]]u,v[[=[[u,v]]\setminus\{u,v\}
. We also denote by
u\wedge v
the most recent common ancestor of
u
and
v
in
\mathfrak{t}
, which may be defined by the equality
[[\varnothing,u\wedge v]]=[[\varnothing,u]]\cap[[\varnothing,v]]
. This also leads to the notion of
lexicographical order
:
u
comes before
v
in this ordering, denoted
u\leq v
, if and only if
u_{u\wedge v+1}\leq v_{u\wedge v+1}
in the Ulam-Harris encoding.

Remark 3.3
.

To avoid confusion, we point out that elsewhere in the literature that we cite, rooted plane trees are often referred to as “rooted ordered plane trees” where the root is a vertex and the ordering is equivalent to the choice of the leftmost child of the root which, together with the planar embedding, fixes the Ulam-Harris/lexicographical ordering of the vertices. Since we started with a root edge, rather than a root vertex, we have already fixed this ordering. Sometimes they are also just referred to as “plane trees” and the root vertex and ordering is automatically included in this definition.

We denote by
\preceq
the genealogical relation on
\mathfrak{t}
: we write
u\preceq v
and say
u
is an
ancestor
of
v
, or
v
is a
descendant
of
u
, if
u\in[[\varnothing,v]]
. We use
\leq
for the lexicographical order on
\mathfrak{t}
, and
\ll
for the
reverse
lexicographical order:
u\ll v
means that the image of
v
comes before the image of
u
in the lexicographical order of the mirror tree. More precisely, if neither
u\prec v
nor
v\prec u
holds, then
u\ll v
iff
u\leq v
; if
u\prec v
, then
v\ll u
(and
u\leq v
). We also use the lexicographical order to define a projection
p_{\mathfrak{t}}
from
\{0,\ldots,|V(\mathfrak{t})|-1\}
to
\mathfrak{t}
:
p_{\mathfrak{t}}(i)
denotes the
(i+1)^{th}
vertex in the lexicographical ordering of
\mathfrak{t}
.

A tree
\mathfrak{t}\in\mathbf{T}_{n}
can be coded by three different functions as illustrated in Figure
3
. We define the
contour exploration
of
\mathfrak{t}
as the sequence
e_{0},e_{1},\dots,e_{2n-1}
of oriented edges bounding the unique face of
\mathfrak{t}
, starting with the root edge, and ordered counterclockwise around this face (it therefore traces the “outer boundary” of the tree in “left-to-right” fashion). For
0\leq i\leq 2n-1
, the
i^{th}
visited vertex in this contour exploration is the initial vertex
e^{-}_{i}
of
e_{i}
, and we set
C_{\mathfrak{t}}(i)=|e^{-}_{i}|
. Note that the contour ordering is such that the ordering of the vertices determined by the times of their first visit by the contour function is the same as their lexicographical ordering.

To define the
height function
, we now list the
n+1
vertices of
\mathfrak{t}
in lexicographical order as
u_{0},u_{1},\ldots,u_{n}
. We define the height process
H_{\mathfrak{t}}=(H_{\mathfrak{t}}(j))_{0\leq j\leq n}
by

H_{\mathfrak{t}}(j)=\lvert u_{j}\rvert,\,0\leq j\leq n.

(30)

We also define the
Łukasiewicz path

(X_{\mathfrak{t}}(m))_{0\leq m\leq n}
by setting
X_{\mathfrak{t}}(0)=0
, then by considering the vertices
u_{0},u_{1},\ldots,u_{n}
in lexicographical order and setting
X_{\mathfrak{t}}(m+1)=X_{\mathfrak{t}}(m)+c_{\mathfrak{t}}(u_{m})-1
(recall that
c_{\mathfrak{t}}(u)
denotes the number of children of
u
in
\mathfrak{t}
). The height function and Łukasiewicz path are connected by the following relation.

H_{\mathfrak{t}}(m)=|\{j\in\{0,1,\ldots,m-1\}:X_{\mathfrak{t}}(j)=\inf_{j\leq k\leq m}X_{\mathfrak{t}}(k)\}|.

(31)

The intuition behind this is that the times
j\in\{0,1,\ldots,m-1\}
satisfying
X_{\mathfrak{t}}(j)=\inf_{j\leq k\leq m}X_{\mathfrak{t}}(k)
correspond exactly to the ancestors of the vertex
u_{m}
.

Although the height function and the contour function directly encode the geometry of the tree, we will see below that in the setting of random trees, the Łukasiewicz path is often more convenient to work with.

Figure 3
:
Example of contour function, height function and Łukasiewicz path for the given tree.

Let
\mathfrak{t}
be a random element of
\mathbf{T}
. We say it has the
branching property
if, for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, conditionally on
\{c_{\mathfrak{t}}(\varnothing)=n\}
, the subtrees
(\mathfrak{t}_{1},\dotsm,\mathfrak{t}_{n})
of descendants of the
n
children of the root are independent and each distributed as the original tree
\mathfrak{t}
. The law of such a tree is characterised by its
offspring distribution
: for
\mu_{\alpha}
a probability measure on
\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, a random rooted plane tree
\mathfrak{t}
is a
Bienaymé–Galton–Watson tree with offspring distribution
\mu_{\alpha}
if it has the branching property and the distribution
c_{\mathfrak{t}}(\varnothing)
of the number of children of the root is
\mu_{\alpha}
. We say an offspring distribution is
critical
if it has mean
1
. Note that Bienaymé–Galton–Watson trees thus come equipped with a root edge and a lexicographical order.

Duquesne
[
Duq03
, Theorem 3.1]
showed that when
T_{n}^{(\alpha)}
is a Bienaymé–Galton–Watson tree with critical offspring distribution satisfying
\mu_{\alpha}[x,\infty)\sim cx^{-\alpha}
for some
\alpha\in(1,2)
and
c\in(0,\infty)
, conditioned to have
n
edges, and
X_{n},H_{n}
and
C_{n}
respectively denote its Łukasiewicz path, height function and contour function, then there exists
c^{\prime}\in(0,\infty)
(explicitly depending on
c
) such that the following joint convergence holds with respect to the Skorokhod-
J_{1}
product topology:

\Bigg(\frac{1}{c^{\prime}}n^{-\frac{1}{\alpha}}X_{n}(nt),c^{\prime}n^{-(1-\frac{1}{\alpha})}H_{n}(nt),c^{\prime}n^{-(1-\frac{1}{\alpha})}C_{n}(2nt)\Bigg)_{t\in[0,1]}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{(d)}\Big(X^{\text{exc}}_{t},H_{t},H_{t}\Big)_{t\in[0,1]},

(32)

where
(H_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
denotes the height function defined from
X^{\text{exc}}
as in (
8
). (The joint convergence including the Łukasiewicz path is not specifically stated in
[
Duq03
, Theorem 3.1]
, but is implicit in the proof.) A straightforward consequence is that

(T_{n}^{(\alpha)},c^{\prime}n^{-(1-\frac{1}{\alpha})}d_{n},\rho_{n})\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{(d)}(\mathcal{T}_{\alpha},d_{\alpha},\rho)

with respect to the pointed Gromov-Hausdorff topology, where
d_{n}
denotes the graph metric on
T_{n}^{(\alpha)}
,
\rho_{n}
its root vertex, and
(\mathcal{T}_{\alpha},d_{\alpha},\rho)
is the stable tree defined in
Section

2.1.3
. This can also be straightforwardly extended to include convergence of the uniform measure on
T_{n}^{(\alpha)}
to the measure
\upsilon
introduced in
Section

2.1.3
and therefore give convergence in the pointed Gromov-Hausdorff-Prokhorov topology.

3.3
Random planar maps

We now give some basic definitions related to maps.

A
planar map
is an embedding of a graph
G=(V,E)
into the sphere in such a way no two edges cross, considered up to orientation-preserving homeomorphism. In what follows we will sometimes drop the adjective “planar”, but all maps should be assumed to planar maps in this paper. A
rooted
planar map is a planar map equipped with a distinguished oriented edge, called its
root edge
. The starting vertex of the root edge is called the
root vertex
.

A
pointed
rooted planar map is a rooted planar map that is equipped with an additional distinguished vertex (this may or may not coincide with the root vertex).

We will also need the notion of corners: if we imagine all edges to be replaced by two (oppositely) directed edges, a
corner
in a map is an angular sector
c
between an incoming edge and an outgoing edge that follow each other in the clockwise cyclic order around a vertex
v
. The corner is canonically associated with this latter outgoing edge. If
v
is has degree 1, it is associated with one corner.

Definition 3.4
.

A
quadrangulation
is a planar map whose faces all have degree four. We denote by
\mathcal{Q}_{n}
the set of rooted planar quadrangulations with
n
faces, and by
\mathcal{Q}_{n}^{\bullet}
the set of pointed, rooted planar quadrangulations with
n
faces.

A map
\mathfrak{m}
induces a metric space by considering the set of its vertices
V(\mathfrak{m})
equipped with the graph distance; the latter is denoted by
d_{\mathfrak{m}}
. For
u\in V(\mathfrak{m})
and
r\geq 0
, we denote by

B_{\mathfrak{m}}(u,r)=\{v\in V(\mathfrak{m})\,\lvert\,d_{\mathfrak{m}}(u,v)\leq r\}

the
ball of radius
r
around
u
in
\mathfrak{m}
. A
geodesic
in
\mathfrak{m}
is a discrete path
\gamma=(\gamma(i),0\leq i\leq k)
taking values in
V(\mathfrak{m})
, such that
d_{\mathfrak{m}}(\gamma(i),\gamma(j))=\lvert i-j\rvert
for every
i,j\in\{0,\dotsm,k\}
.

3.4
The Cori–Vauquelin–Schaeffer bijection

Figure 4
:
The CVS bijection: an admissible labelled tree with 6 edges (in black), and the associated planar quadrangulation with 6 faces (in red), with its pointed vertex
v_{*}
.

Let us now get to the bijection between rooted planar quadrangulations with
n
faces, and a family of decorated trees with
n
edges, following very closely the presentation of
[
Mie14
, Section 2.3]
. This bijection was first introduced in the work of Cori and Vauquelin
[
CV81
]
and further developed in the thesis of Schaeffer
[
Sch98
]
. We will actually only use the inverse of the pointed version of this bijection, from trees to quadrangulations, which was constructed explicitly by Chassaing and Schaeffer
[
CS04
]
and refer to it as the
CVS bijection
.

Let
\mathfrak{t}
be a rooted plane tree with
n
edges, with root edge
e_{0}
and root vertex
u_{0}
.
An
admissible label function
on
\mathfrak{t}
is a function
\ell:V(\mathfrak{t})\to\mathbb{Z}
such that
\ell(u_{0})=0
, and, for every adjacent
u,v\in V(\mathfrak{t})
,
|\ell(u)-\ell(v)|\leq 1
. If
\mathfrak{t}
is a rooted plane tree, and
\ell
is an admissible label function on
\mathfrak{t}
, we say that the pair
(\mathfrak{t},\ell)
is a
admissible labelled tree
. We denote by
\mathbb{T}_{n}
the set of admissible labelled trees with
n
edges.

Consider a labelled tree
(\mathfrak{t},\ell)\in\mathbb{T}_{n}
. Let
e_{0},e_{1},\dots,e_{2n-1}
be the contour exploration of the oriented edges of
\mathfrak{t}
, as defined in
Section

3.2
. Recall that
e^{-}_{i}
is the initial vertex of
e_{i}
. It will be useful extend the sequences
(e_{i})_{i}
and
(e^{-}_{i})_{i}
to
\mathbb{N}
by periodicity. In what follows we will identify the oriented edge
e_{i}
with the associated corner, and thus use the notation
\ell(e_{i})
for
\ell(e^{-}_{i})
.

For every
i\geq 0
, we define the
successor
of
i
by

s(i):=\inf\{j>i\,|\,\ell(e_{j})=\ell(e_{i})-1\},

(33)

with the convention that
\inf\varnothing=\infty
. Note that
s(i)=\infty
if and only if
\ell(e_{i})=\min_{j}\ell(e_{j})
, as the values of
\ell
decrease only by unit steps. We introduce a new vertex, corresponding to a corner labelled
e_{\infty}
, that will play the role of successor for all such
i
. To do so, recall that
\mathfrak{t}
is embedded in the sphere, and consider a point
v_{*}
in the sphere that does not belong to the support of
\mathfrak{t}
. Denote by
e_{\infty}
a small neighbourhood of
v_{*}
not intersecting
\mathfrak{t}
or any of its corners. We will also refer to
e_{\infty}
as a corner in what follows. We set

\ell(v_{*})=\ell(e_{\infty})=\min_{j}\ell(e_{j})-1.

We can then define, for every
0\leq i<\infty
, the successor of the corner
e_{i}
as
s(e_{i})=e_{s(i)}
, where we consider
e_{s(i)}
to be its associated corner.

The reverse CVS construction involves drawing, for every
i\in\{0,1,...,2n-1\}
, an edge, that we will call an
arc
, from the corner
e_{i}
to the corner
s(e_{i})
, without crossing
\mathfrak{t}
,
v_{*}
, or another arc (see Figure
4
). This is always possible; see
[
Mie14
, Lemma 2.3.2]
. We denote by
\mathfrak{q}
the embedded graph with vertex set
V(\mathfrak{t})\cup{v_{*}}
, and edge set formed by the arcs.

A first property of
\mathfrak{q}
, which is contained in the stronger statement
Theorem

3.5
, is that it is a quadrangulation with
n
faces. Note that it comes equipped with a distinguished vertex
v_{*}
, but for now it is not a rooted quadrangulation. To fix this root, we use an extra parameter
\varepsilon\in\{-1,1\}
. If
\varepsilon=1
we let the root edge of
\mathfrak{q}
be the arc linking
e_{0}
with
s(e_{0})
, and oriented from
s(e_{0})
to
e_{0}
. If
\varepsilon=-1
, the root edge is this same arc, but oriented from
e_{0}
to
s(e_{0})
. We have thus defined a mapping
\Phi
from
\mathbb{T}_{n}\times\{-1,1\}
to the set
\mathcal{Q}^{\bullet}_{n}
of pointed rooted planar quadrangulations
(\mathfrak{q},v_{*})
.

Theorem 3.5

(Proposition 2 and Theorem 4 in
[
CS04
]
)
.

For every
n\geq 1
,
\Phi
is a bijection from
\mathbb{T}_{n}\times\{-1,1\}
onto
\mathcal{Q}^{\bullet}_{n}
.

A very important aspect of the CVS bijection is that the labels of the tree correspond to some of the distances in the quadrangulation. Indeed, if
(\mathfrak{q},v_{*})=\Phi((\mathfrak{t},\ell),\varepsilon)
, then, for every
v\in V(\mathfrak{q})
,

d_{\mathfrak{q}}(v,v_{*})=\ell(v)-\ell(v_{*}).

(34)

Remark 3.6
.

Another property of the CVS bijection is that every edge in
\mathfrak{t}
is inscribed inside a face of
\mathfrak{q}
. Hence, any path of adjacent vertices in
\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{m}
in
\mathfrak{t}
can be extended (not necessarily uniquely) to a path in
\mathfrak{q}
by adding at most one extra vertex between each pair of the form
\gamma_{i}
and
\gamma_{i+1}
. Hence, if the extended path intersects some set
A\subset V(\mathfrak{q})
, then (the image of) the original path is at distance at most
1
from
A
.

For
(\mathfrak{t},\ell)\in\mathbb{T}_{n}
, recall that
u_{0},u_{1},\ldots,u_{n}
denotes the lexicographical ordering of the tree vertices. Let us define for
0\leq i,j\leq n

d^{\circ}_{\mathfrak{t},\ell}(i,j):=\ell(u_{i})+\ell(u_{j})-2\inf_{i\wedge j\leq k\leq i\vee j}\ell(u_{k})+2.

(35)

We also write
d_{\mathfrak{q}}(i,j)
for
d_{\mathfrak{q}}(u_{i},u_{j})
. We then have the following bound.

Lemma 3.7

(Lemma 3.1 in
[
LG07
]
)
.

For every
i,j\in\{0,1,\dotsm,n\}
,

d_{\mathfrak{q}}(i,j)\leq d^{\circ}_{\mathfrak{t},\ell}(i,j).

In the rest of this paper, we will consider random admissible labelled trees
(T_{n}^{(\alpha)},\ell_{n})
, constructed as follows. Let
\mu_{\alpha}
be a probability measure on
\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
such that
\mu_{\alpha}(0)>0
and with expectation
1
and let
Y
be a real random variable. We assume that

\exists\,c\in(0,\infty)\,:\,\mu_{\alpha}[x,\infty)\sim cx^{-\alpha},\,p_{Y}(0)<1\textnormal{ and }p_{Y}(-1)=p_{Y}(1)=\frac{1-p_{Y}(0)}{2}.

(36)

We anticipate that all of our results are adaptable to any law
\mu_{\alpha}
in the domain of attraction of an
\alpha
-stable law, but we focus on the aforementioned case for ease of notation.

For each
n\geq 2
, let
T_{n}^{(\alpha)}
be a Bienaymé–Galton–Watson tree with offspring distribution
\mu_{\alpha}
, conditioned to have
n
edges (this will be well-defined for all sufficiently large
n
under our assumptions). Recall that with our conventions,
T_{n}^{(\alpha)}
comes pre-equipped with a root edge, going from the root vertex to its first child in the lexicographical ordering.

Conditionally on
T_{n}^{(\alpha)}
, we endow each vertex
u
of
T_{n}^{(\alpha)}
with a random label
\ell_{n}(u)\in\mathbb{R}
, setting
\ell_{n}(\varnothing)=0
for the root
\varnothing
of the tree, and, for
v\neq\varnothing
,

\ell_{n}(v)=\sum_{u\preceq v}Y(u),

(37)

where
(Y(u))_{u\in T_{n}^{(\alpha)}\setminus\{\varnothing\}}
are i.i.d. copies of
Y
. Note that
(T_{n}^{(\alpha)},\ell_{n})\in\mathbb{T}_{n}
.

We moreover choose
\varepsilon_{n}
uniformly in
\{-1,1\}
, independently for each
n
. The quadrangulation
M_{n}^{(\alpha)}
is defined as the image of the triple
(T_{n}^{(\alpha)},\ell_{n},\varepsilon_{n})
under the reverse CVS bijection, i.e.
\Phi(T_{n}^{(\alpha)},\ell_{n},\varepsilon_{n})
. We moreover let
d_{n}
denote the graph metric on
M_{n}^{(\alpha)}
, let
\partial_{n}
denote the extra vertex added when constructing
M_{n}^{(\alpha)}
, let
\rho_{n}
denote its root vertex, and let
\pi_{n}
denote the canonical projection from
\{0,1,\ldots,n\}
to
V(M_{n}^{(\alpha)})\setminus\{\partial_{n}\}
(via the vertex with that lexicographical index in
T_{n}^{(\alpha)}
: it is essentially the identity map). We also let
\nu_{n}
denote the uniform probability measure on the vertices of
M_{n}^{(\alpha)}
, and let
d^{\circ}_{n}
denote
d^{\circ}_{T_{n},\ell_{n}}
as defined in (
35
).

Remark 3.8
.

By construction, each vertex in
V(M_{n}^{(\alpha)})\setminus\{\partial_{n}\}
is incident to at least one edge per corner
c
that it had in
T_{n}^{(\alpha)}
(this edge is the arc that connects
c
to its successor), so that the vertex degrees in
M_{n}^{(\alpha)}
are at least as heavy-tailed as in
T_{n}^{(\alpha)}
. In that regard, our stable quadrangulations are similar to the images by Tutte’s bijection of Boltzmann stable maps, that are planar quadrangulations with stably-decaying vertex degrees, and that should have the same scaling limit as the duals of these Boltzmann maps. In our model however, it is not clear yet if typical vertex degrees are indeed stably-decaying, since each corner of
T_{n}^{(\alpha)}
is not only the origin of a successor arc, but potentially the recipient of many. We plan to address this question in future works.

3.5
Subsequential scaling limits of random quadrangulations

The aim of this subsection is to show that the sequence
(M_{n}^{(\alpha)},n^{-\frac{\alpha-1}{2\alpha}}d_{n},\nu_{n})

Let

T_{n}^{(\alpha)}
, let

be the height process of
T_{n}^{(\alpha)}
, both as defined in and below (

), and extend
H_{n}
,
X_{n}
,
\ell_{n}
,

and

to the entire time interval

and

by linear interpolation. Also let

be as in

. We denote by

the height function defined from

as in (

), and by

the Brownian snake driven by

as defined in

, with tip

. The key result is the following.

Theorem 3.9

(Special case of Theorem 1.1 in
[

]
)
.

Assume that (

to be the variance of

Then the following convergence holds jointly in distribution with respect to the Skorokhod-

\left(\frac{1}{c^{\prime}}n^{-\frac{1}{\alpha}}X_{n}(nt),c^{\prime}n^{-(1-\frac{1}{\alpha})}H_{n}(nt),\left(\frac{c^{\prime}n^{-(1-\frac{1}{\alpha})}}{\sigma_{Y}^{2}}\right)^{1/2}\ell_{n}(nt)\right)_{t\in[0,1]}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{(d)}\Big(X^{\text{exc}}_{t},H_{t},\widehat{W}_{t}\Big)_{t\in[0,1]}.

Although not stated in

From the convergence result of

]
, some preliminary results on subsequential limits for the distances in

. Like in the Brownian case, a first straightforward consequence of

is that

\left(\left(\frac{c^{\prime}n^{-(1-\frac{1}{\alpha})}}{\sigma_{Y}^{2}}\right)^{1/2}d^{\circ}_{n}(ns,nt)\right)_{0\leq s,t\leq 1}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{(d)}\left(D^{\circ}(s,t)\right)_{0\leq s,t\leq 1},

(38)

where

is defined as in (
1
). Together with
Lemma

, this immediately implies the following result, similarly to Proposition 3.2 and its corollary (11) in

]

Proposition 3.10
.

The sequence of the laws of the processes
\left(n^{-\frac{\alpha-1}{2\alpha}}d_{n}(ns,nt)\right)_{0\leq s,t\leq 1}
is tight in the space of probability measures on the continuous functions from
[0,1]^{2}
to
\mathbb{R}
.

In particular, subsequential limits exist: for any sequence
(n_{k})_{k\geq 1}
there exists a subsequence
(k_{m})_{m\geq 1}
for which there is a limiting function
D
from
[0,1]^{2}
to
\mathbb{R}_{+}
along which we have the following joint convergence in distribution:

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\left(\frac{1}{c^{\prime}}n^{-\frac{1}{\alpha}}X_{n}(nt),c^{\prime}n^{-\frac{\alpha-1}{\alpha}}H_{n}(nt),\left(\frac{c^{\prime}n^{-(1-\frac{1}{\alpha})}}{\sigma_{Y}^{2}}\right)^{1/2}\ell_{n}(nt),\left(\frac{c^{\prime}n^{-(1-\frac{1}{\alpha})}}{\sigma_{Y}^{2}}\right)^{1/2}d_{n}(ns,nt)\right)_{s,t\in[0,1]}\\
&\qquad\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{(d)}\left(X^{\text{exc}}_{t},H_{t},\widehat{W}_{t},D(s,t)\right)_{s,t\in[0,1]}.\end{split}

(39)

Proof.

The first statement has the same proof as
[
LG07
, Proposition 3.2]
. The second statement is the analogue of
[
LG07
, Equation (11)]
and comes from combining the first statement with
Theorem

3.9
.
∎

It is important to note that a priori, the limiting function
D
may depend on the subsequence. In what follows, we only consider limits along such a subsequence
(n_{k})_{k\geq 1}
. By the Skorokhod representation theorem, we can further assume that the convergence (
39
) holds almost surely. This has the following consequence, that is similar the first assertion of
[
LG07
, Theorem 3.4]
in the Brownian case.

Corollary 3.11
.

The sequence of the laws of the metric spaces
(V(M_{n}^{(\alpha)}),n^{-\frac{\alpha-1}{2\alpha}}d_{n},\nu_{n},\rho_{n})
is tight in the space of probability measures on the space
\mathbb{F}^{c}
of isometry classes of compact rooted metric spaces equipped with a finite Borel measure, endowed with the pointed Gromov-Hausdorff-Prokhorov topology. In particular, for any sequence
(n_{k})_{k\geq 1}
there exists a subsequence
(k_{m})_{m\geq 1}
for which there exists a limiting compact rooted metric-measure space
(M,D,\nu,\rho)
such that

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left(V(M^{(\alpha)}_{n_{k_{m}}}),\frac{\sqrt{c^{\prime}}}{\sigma_{Y}}n_{k_{m}}^{-\frac{\alpha-1}{2\alpha}}d_{n_{k_{m}}},\nu_{n_{k_{m}}},\rho_{n_{k_{m}}}\right)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{(d)}(M,D,\nu,\rho).\end{split}

(40)

Moreover,
M
is a quotient space of
\mathbf{m}_{\alpha}=\mathcal{T}/\approx
, as defined after (
2
), and in particular there is a canonical projection
\pi:[0,1]\to M
. The limiting metric is the pushforward of the function
D
appearing in (
39
) under
\pi
, the limiting measure
\nu
is the pushforward of Lebesgue measure under
\pi
, and the root
\rho
is the pushforward of
0
under
\pi
.

Proof.

Let us define
C^{\prime}=\sqrt{c^{\prime}}/\sigma_{Y}
for ease of notation. Throughout the proof we will consider the space
(V(M_{n}^{(\alpha)})\setminus\{\partial_{n}\},C^{\prime}n^{-\frac{\alpha-1}{2\alpha}}d_{n},\nu_{n},\rho_{n})
in place of
(V(M_{n}^{(\alpha)}),C^{\prime}n^{-\frac{\alpha-1}{2\alpha}}d_{n},\nu_{n},\rho_{n})
and for the rest of the proof let
\nu_{n}
denote uniform measure on
V(M_{n}^{(\alpha)})\setminus\{\partial_{n}\}
; by the triangle inequality, the two spaces necessarily have the same scaling limit.

Firstly, note that (
39
) implies that the sequence
(V(M_{n}^{(\alpha)})\setminus\{\partial_{n}\},C^{\prime}n^{-\frac{\alpha-1}{2\alpha}}d_{n})
is tight in the Gromov-Hausdorff topology by
[
BBI01
, Theorem 7.4.15]
. By the definitions of the various Gromov-Hausdorff and Gromov-Hausdorff-Prokhorov distances, it immediately follows that the spaces are also compact with respect to the pointed Gromov-Hausdorff-Prokhorov topology on adding the roots and measures. Since the space
\mathbb{F}^{c}
is Polish (this follows from a very minor extension of
[
Mie09
, Proposition 8]
), this implies that there are subsequential limits taking values in
\mathbb{F}^{c}
.

We now work on a probability space and along a subsequence where (
39
) holds almost surely. Our candidate for the limiting metric space is
([0,1]/\sim_{D},D)
, where
s\sim_{D}t\iff D(s,t)=0
. Note that
([0,1]/\sim_{D},D)
is indeed a compact metric space since all of the relevant properties are inherited directly from (
39
). We write
M=[0,1]/\sim_{D}
, with root
\{0\}
, and let
\pi
denote the canonical projection from
[0,1]
to
M
.

We first show that the pointed Gromov-Hausdorff distance between
(V(M_{n}^{(\alpha)})\setminus\{\partial_{n}\},C^{\prime}n^{-\frac{\alpha-1}{2\alpha}}d_{n},\rho_{n})
and
(M,D,\{0\})
vanishes almost surely using
Theorem

3.2
. In particular, since
M_{n}^{(\alpha)}
is defined as the image of
(T_{n}^{(\alpha)},\ell_{n},\varepsilon_{n})
under
\Phi
, it follows that
(V(M_{n}^{(\alpha)})\setminus\{\partial_{n}\},C^{\prime}n^{-\frac{\alpha-1}{2\alpha}}d_{n},\rho_{n})
is isometric to

\left([0,1]/\sim_{n},C^{\prime}n^{-\frac{\alpha-1}{2\alpha}}d_{n}(\lfloor sn\rfloor,\lfloor tn\rfloor),0\right),

where
\sim_{n}
denotes the equivalence relation
s\sim_{n}t\iff d_{n}\left(\lfloor sn\rfloor,\lfloor tn\rfloor\right)=0
. Recall from Section
3.4
that the two spaces are actually related via the projection
s\mapsto\pi_{n}(\lfloor sn\rfloor)\in V(M_{n}^{(\alpha)})\setminus\{\partial_{n}\}
.

We construct a correspondence
\mathcal{R}_{n}
between
(V(M_{n}^{(\alpha)})\setminus\{\partial_{n}\},C^{\prime}n^{-\frac{\alpha-1}{2\alpha}}d_{n},\rho_{n})
and
(M,D,\{0\})
by including all points of the form
(\pi_{n}(\lfloor ns\rfloor),\pi(s))
where
s\in[0,1]
. We denote its distortion by
r_{n}
; it then follows directly from (
39
) that
r_{n}\to 0
along the same subsequence.

To prove that the measures also converge on this space, we take the Gromov-Hausdorff embedding
F_{n}=(V(M_{n}^{(\alpha)})\setminus\{\partial_{n}\})\sqcup M
endowed with the metric

D_{F_{n}}(x,y)=\begin{cases}C^{\prime}n^{-\frac{\alpha-1}{2\alpha}}d_{n}(x,y)&\text{ if }x,y\in V(M_{n}^{(\alpha)})\setminus\{\partial_{n}\}\\
D(x,y)&\text{ if }x,y\in M\\
\inf_{u,v\in\mathcal{R}_{n}}(C^{\prime}n^{-\frac{\alpha-1}{2\alpha}}d_{n}(x,u)+D(y,v)+\frac{1}{2}r_{n})&\text{ if }x\in V(M_{n}^{(\alpha)})\setminus\{\partial_{n}\},y\in M.\end{cases}

We now claim that
d^{F_{n}}_{P}(\nu_{n},\nu)\rightarrow 0
as
n\rightarrow\infty
. To prove this, for each
0\leq i\leq n
we define the interval
I_{n,i}=[\frac{i}{n},\frac{i}{n}+\frac{1}{n})
. Now take a set
A_{n}
of vertices in
M_{n}^{(\alpha)}\setminus\{\partial_{n}\}
, and set

A^{\prime}_{n}=\bigcup_{i:\pi_{n}(i)\in A_{n}}I_{n,i}.

Let
A_{n}^{\prime\prime}=\pi(A^{\prime}_{n})
. We will show that
A_{n}^{\prime\prime}\subset A_{n}^{r_{n}}
, where
E^{\varepsilon}
denotes the
\varepsilon
-neighborhood of a set
E
. To this end, take any
v\in A_{n}^{\prime\prime}
. By our definitions, there exists
s\in A^{\prime}_{n}
with
v=\pi(s)
and
s\in I_{n,i}
for some
i
with
\pi_{n}(i)\in A_{n}
. Consequently, we must have that
i=\lfloor ns\rfloor
, and hence
(u_{i},v)\in\mathcal{R}_{n}
and
D_{F}(u_{i},v)=\frac{1}{2}r_{n}
. It follows that
v\in A_{n}^{r_{n}}
and hence that
A_{n}^{\prime\prime}\subset A_{n}^{r_{n}}
.

Also note that
\nu_{n}(A_{n})=\nu(A_{n}^{\prime\prime})
by construction, and so
\nu_{n}(A_{n})\leq\nu(A_{n}^{r_{n}})
.

Similarly, take any set
B\subset M
. We use the same argument to show that
\nu(B)\leq\nu_{n}(B^{r_{n}})
. Let
B^{\prime}=\pi^{-1}(B)
, and

B_{n}=\{\pi_{n}(i)\colon\exists\ s\in B^{\prime}\text{ with }s\in I_{n,i}\}.

Clearly
B^{\prime}\subset\bigcup_{i:\pi_{n}(i)\in B_{n}}I_{n,i}
and so

\nu(B)=Leb(B^{\prime})\leq Leb\left(\bigcup_{i:\pi_{n}(i)\in B_{n}}I_{n,i}\right)=\frac{|B_{n}|}{n}=\nu_{n}(B_{n}).

(41)

To conclude, note that if
v\in B_{n}
and
v=\pi_{n}(i)
, then (by our definitions) there necessarily exists
s\in B^{\prime}
with
s\in I_{n,i}
, hence
i=\lfloor sn\rfloor
and therefore
(v,\pi(s))\in\mathcal{R}_{n}
. Moreover
\pi(s)\in B
. Hence
B_{n}\subset B^{r_{n}}
, so
\nu_{n}(B_{n})\leq\nu_{n}(B^{r_{n}})
, so combining with (
41
) gives
\nu(B)\leq\nu_{n}(B^{r_{n}})
.

It follows that
d^{F_{n}}_{P}(\nu_{n},\nu)\leq r_{n}
, and hence converges to zero as
n\rightarrow\infty
.

Moreover, it follows from
Theorem

3.9
and from the construction of
D_{\alpha}^{*}
that
D\leq D_{\alpha}^{*}
, and therefore that
(M,D,\{0\})
is a quotient space of
(\mathcal{T}/\approx,D_{\alpha}^{*},\rho)
. Since the roots are the same we will henceforth identify them.
∎

Remark 3.12

Note that each limiting space
(M,D,\nu,\rho)

a priori depends on the subsequence. Similarly to the Brownian case, the construction allows us to immediately deduce some properties for

.

.

The following properties hold almost surely.

(1)

The function

•

•

(2)

For every

(3)

We end with a useful fact. Recall that

Lemma 3.14
.

For any

,

\displaystyle D(u,v)\geq Z_{u}+Z_{v}-2\min_{x\in[[u,v]]}Z_{x}.

This can be proved formally using
[

, Proposition 2.3.8(ii)]
, which gives the discrete analogue of this statement (and is deterministic), and taking appropriate limits.
Alternatively, one could also repeat the proof leading up to

4
Large volumes

The purpose of this section is to obtain some estimates on the probability of having unusually large volumes at typical points in
(M,D,\nu,\rho)
. Throughout this section,
U
will denote a uniform random variable on either
[0,\sigma]
or
[0,1]
(under
\mathbb{N}_{0}
or
\mathbb{N}^{(1)}_{0}
as appropriate), chosen independently of everything else,
p(U)
will denote its image in
\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}
, and
\pi(U)
will denote its image in the limiting space
M
.

The upper bounds on the corresponding tail probabilities are very straightforward; the lower bounds are more involved.

4.1
Upper tail bounds for large volumes

In this subsection we fix an
a>0
and consider the law of
M_{\varepsilon}(U_{a})
, where
M_{\varepsilon}(s):=\varepsilon^{-\frac{2\alpha}{\alpha-1}}\nu(B(s,\varepsilon))
and where
U_{a}
is chosen “uniformly at level
a
in the tree”. Formally, we sample a pointed rooted tree and snake
(\mathcal{T}_{\alpha},U_{a},Z)
according to
\mathbb{N}_{0}\times\ell^{a}
, where the local time measure
\ell^{a}
is as in (
28
). Note that this defines a probability measure on pointed rooted trees since
\langle\ell^{a},1\rangle=1
by
Proposition

2.6
.

Proposition 4.1
.

There exists
c<\infty
such that for all
a\in(0,\infty)
,
r>0
and
\lambda>1
,

\mathbb{N}_{0}\left(\nu(B(U_{a},r))\geq\lambda r^{\frac{2\alpha}{\alpha-1}}\right)\leq c\lambda^{-\frac{\alpha-1}{2\alpha}}.

Remark 4.2
.

We do not believe that the exponent for the tail decay is optimal.

Proof.

The proof is similar to that of
[
DLG06
, Proposition 5.1]
. We apply Proposition
2.6
and use the same notation, except that it will be more convenient to consider the linear Brownian motion
(\xi_{t})_{t\geq 0}
as starting at
U_{a}
rather than at the root. Let
\tau_{x}
denote the hitting time of
x
by
(\xi_{t})_{t\geq 0}
. It follows from Proposition
2.5
, Proposition
2.6
and Lemma
3.14
that, for any
\delta>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{N}_{0}\left(\int\ell^{a}(ds)e^{-\delta M_{\varepsilon}(s)}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\Pi_{0}\left(\exp\left\{-\alpha\int_{0}^{\tau_{-\varepsilon}\wedge a}\mathbb{N}_{0}(1-e^{-\delta\varepsilon^{-\frac{2\alpha}{\alpha-1}}\sigma})^{\alpha-1}dt\right\}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\Pi_{0}\left(\exp\left\{-\alpha\int_{0}^{\varepsilon^{-2}\tau_{-\varepsilon}}\varepsilon^{2}\mathbb{N}_{0}(1-e^{-\delta\varepsilon^{-\frac{2\alpha}{\alpha-1}}\sigma})^{\alpha-1}dt\right\}\right)

\displaystyle=\Pi_{0}\left(\exp\left\{-\alpha\int_{0}^{\tau_{-1}}\mathbb{N}_{0}(1-e^{-\delta\sigma})^{\alpha-1}dt\right\}\right).

Here in the second and third line we used Brownian scaling and the Itô scaling property (
18
).

Note that
\tau_{-1}
has density function

\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi x^{3}}}e^{-1/4x}dx,

and so a Tauberian theorem implies that there exists
c>0
such that
1-\Pi_{0}\left(\exp\left\{-\theta\tau_{-1}\right\}\right)\sim c\theta^{1/2}
as
\theta\downarrow 0
. Hence by (
19
) we can write for all sufficiently small
\delta>0
:

\displaystyle\Pi_{0}\left(\exp\left\{-\alpha\int_{0}^{\tau_{-1}}\mathbb{N}_{0}(1-e^{-\delta\sigma})^{\alpha-1}dt\right\}\right)

\displaystyle=\Pi_{0}\left(\exp\left\{-\alpha\delta^{\frac{\alpha-1}{\alpha}}\cdot{\tau_{-1}}\right\}\right)\geq 1-c\delta^{\frac{\alpha-1}{2\alpha}}.

Recall from below
Proposition

2.6
that
\mathbb{N}_{0}\left(\int\ell^{a}(ds)\right)=1
. Hence applying Markov’s inequality we deduce that

\displaystyle\mathbb{N}_{0}\left(\int\ell^{a}(ds)\mathbbm{1}\{M_{\varepsilon}(s)\geq\lambda\}\right)

\displaystyle\leq(1-e^{-1})^{-1}\mathbb{N}_{0}\left(\int\ell^{a}(ds)(1-e^{-\lambda^{-1}M_{\varepsilon}(s)})\right)

\displaystyle\leq c(1-e^{-1})^{-1}\lambda^{-\frac{\alpha-1}{2\alpha}}.\qed

This allows us to deduce the following.

Corollary 4.3
.

Take any
\varepsilon>0
. Then
\mathbb{N}_{0}
-almost surely, it holds for
\nu
-almost every
x
in
M
that

\limsup_{r\downarrow 0}\frac{\nu(B(x,r))}{r^{\frac{2\alpha}{\alpha-1}}(\log r^{-1})^{\frac{2\alpha+\varepsilon}{\alpha-1}}}=0.

Proof.

We start by fixing an arbitrary
a>0
and proving the result for
\ell^{a}
-almost every
x
; this then extends straightforwardly to
\nu
-almost every
x
using (
28
). By Fubini’s theorem, it is sufficient to prove the claim for
B(U_{a},r)
, where
U_{a}
is chosen according to
\ell^{a}
. This latter statement follows quite straightforwardly from
Proposition

4.1
. In particular, combining the tail estimate of
Proposition

4.1
with Borel-Cantelli along the subsequence
r_{k}=2^{-k}
and using monotonicity of volumes, we deduce that,
\mathbb{N}_{0}
-almost everywhere

\limsup_{r\downarrow 0}\frac{\nu(B(x,r))}{r^{\frac{2\alpha}{\alpha-1}}(\log r^{-1})^{\frac{2\alpha+\varepsilon}{\alpha-1}}}\leq 2^{\frac{2\alpha}{\alpha-1}}\limsup_{k\to\infty}\frac{\nu(B(x,r_{k}))}{r_{k}^{\frac{2\alpha}{\alpha-1}}(\log r_{k}^{-1})^{\frac{2\alpha+\varepsilon}{\alpha-1}}}=0,\text{ for }\ell^{a}\text{-almost every }x.

Moreover by considering the restriction to
\{1<\sigma<2\}
and rescaling, we deduce the same result under
\mathbb{N}^{(1)}_{0}
.
∎

4.2
Lower tail bounds for large volumes

We now turn to establishing a similar lower bound. We anticipate that the exponent
\alpha-1
appearing here is sharp.

Proposition 4.4
.

(a)

There exists
c>0
such that for all
r>0,1<\lambda<r^{-\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{N}^{(1)}_{0}\left(\nu(B(\pi(U),r))\geq\lambda r^{\frac{2\alpha}{\alpha-1}}\right)

\displaystyle\geq c\lambda^{-(\alpha-1)}.

(b)

There exists
c>0
such that for all
r>0,1<\lambda<\infty
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{N}_{0}\left(\nu(B(\pi(U),r))\in[\lambda r^{\frac{2\alpha}{\alpha-1}},2\lambda r^{\frac{2\alpha}{\alpha-1}}]\right)

\displaystyle\geq c\lambda^{-(\alpha-1)}r^{-\frac{2}{\alpha-1}}.

Remark 4.5
.

The bound in part (a) should really hold for all
1<\lambda\ll r^{-\frac{2\alpha}{\alpha-1}}
. The reason for the considering the interval
[\lambda r^{\frac{2\alpha}{\alpha-1}},2\lambda r^{\frac{2\alpha}{\alpha-1}}]
in part (b) will become apparent in the proof of Corollary
4.7
, where it will enable us to consider disjoint events for a decreasing sequence of radii.

Comparing with the following proposition for the volume of the ball around the minimum of the snake (recall this is denoted
\pi(s^{*})
) leads to a proof of Corollary
1.6
. This contrasts with the Brownian case, where
\pi(s^{*})
has the same law as a vertex chosen according to
\nu
.

Proposition 4.6
.

It holds that

\limsup_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\varepsilon^{-\frac{2\alpha}{\alpha-1}}\mathbb{N}^{(1)}_{0}\left(\nu(B(\pi(s^{*}),\varepsilon))\right)<\infty.

(42)

Proof.

This is a straightforward consequence of
[
ACM24
, Theorem 1.3]
and
Proposition

3.13
(3) (to obtain the result under
\mathbb{N}^{(1)}_{0}
we can restrict to the event
\{1<\sigma<2\}
and apply rescaling).
∎

Proof of Corollary
1.6
.

If
\pi(s^{*})
did have the same law as
\pi(U)
for an independently chosen
U
, then Markov’s inequality combined with Proposition
4.6
would imply that there exists a constant
C<\infty
such that
for all
r>0,1<\lambda<r^{-\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}}
,

\mathbb{N}^{(1)}_{0}\left(\nu(B(\pi(U),r))\geq\lambda r^{\frac{2\alpha}{\alpha-1}}\right)\leq C\lambda^{-1}.

Taking for example
\lambda=\log r^{-1}
we find that this result is incompatible with
Proposition

4.4
(a) for all sufficiently small
r
.
∎

Similarly to the previous subsection, this has consequences for volume fluctuations around typical points in
M
.

Corollary 4.7
.

Take any
\varepsilon>0
. Then
\mathbb{N}_{0}
-almost everywhere, it holds for
\nu
-almost every
x
in
M

is stochastically dominated by a Poisson random variable with parameter
c\varepsilon^{\frac{2}{\alpha-1}}
, for some
c<\infty
, and hence is non-zero with probability less than
c\varepsilon^{\frac{2}{\alpha-1}}
. In particular, it therefore follows from Fatou’s lemma that, conditionally on the subsequence
(n_{k})_{k}
,

\mathbb{N}_{0}((A_{n_{k}}^{l})^{c}\text{ for all }k\text{ large enough}|B_{n_{k}}\cap A_{n_{k}}^{r}\text{ i.o.})\leq c\delta^{\frac{2}{\alpha-1}}.

Moreover, as discussed above, on the event
B_{n}\cap A_{n}^{l}\cap A_{n}^{r}
it holds that
\nu(B(\pi(U),4r_{n})\geq\lambda_{n}r_{n}^{\frac{2\alpha}{\alpha-1}}
. Since
B_{n_{k}}\cap A_{n_{k}}^{r}\text{ i.o.}

\mathbb{N}_{0}
-almost everywhere, we deduce that

\mathbb{N}_{0}(\nu(B(\pi(U),4r_{n_{k}})\geq\lambda_{n_{k}}r_{n_{k}}^{\frac{2\alpha}{\alpha-1}}\text{ for all }k\text{ large enough})\geq 1-c\delta^{\frac{2}{\alpha-1}}.

Since
\delta>0
was arbitrary, this readily implies the statement of the corollary.

∎

Small volumes

We now turn to looking at the occurrence of unusually small volumes at typical points. As in Section
4.1
, we fix an
a>0
and consider the law of
M_{\varepsilon}(U_{a})
, where
M_{\varepsilon}(s):=\varepsilon^{-\frac{2\alpha}{\alpha-1}}\nu(B(s,\varepsilon))
and where

is as in the statement of Proposition

.

5.1
Upper tail bounds for small volumes

Proposition 5.1
.

There exist
\kappa>0,C<\infty,c>0
such that for all
r>0
,
\lambda>1
and
a\in(r^{2},\infty)
,

\mathbb{N}_{0}\left(\nu(B(U_{a},r))\leq\lambda^{-1}r^{\frac{2\alpha}{\alpha-1}}\right)\leq Ce^{-c\lambda^{\kappa}}.

In particular this holds for
\kappa=\frac{\alpha-1}{32\alpha}
.

Proof.

Outline.
We will construct an event
E_{r,\lambda}
on which
\nu(B(U_{a},r))\geq\lambda^{-1}r^{\frac{2\alpha}{\alpha-1}}
and show that the required tail bound holds for
E_{r,\lambda}
. To this end, we take
\beta,\beta^{\prime},\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}
to be positive constants that will be fixed later.

The setup and the argument is similar to that of the proof of Proposition
4.4
. We consider the spine from
U_{a}
to
\rho
, and the associated decomposition as in Proposition
2.6
. For concreteness we say that the half of the spine coming
after

U_{a}
in the contour order is the right hand side of the spine. We also let
I_{r}
denote the interval of the spine falling within distance
r^{2}\lambda^{-2\gamma^{\prime}}
of
U_{a}
. We also assume that the Brownian motion appearing in Proposition
2.6
is indexed by its distance from
U_{a}
rather than from
\rho
. In addition we assume that the values of
Z
are all shifted so that
Z_{U_{a}}=0
.

Figure 7
:
The setup between
U_{a}
and
h
on the event
E_{r,\lambda}
. Between
U_{a}
and
h
the hubs are small and the snake has only small fluctuations. The roots of larger subtrees where the snake exits the interval
[-r\lambda^{-\gamma},r\lambda^{-\gamma}]
are labelled in orange.

We then let
E_{r,\lambda}
be the intersection of the following events (see Figure
7
):

(1)

The Brownian motion
\xi
does not exit the interval
[-r\lambda^{-\gamma},r\lambda^{-\gamma}]
on the interval
I_{r}
.

(2)

There exists a hub of size at least
r^{\frac{2}{\alpha-1}}\lambda^{-\beta}
on
I_{r}
. On this event we let
t_{\text{hub}}
be the time index of the first of these (i.e. closest to
U_{a}
).

(3)

The sum of the sizes of the hubs appearing on the interval
[0,t_{\text{hub}})
is at most
r^{\frac{2}{\alpha-1}}\lambda^{\beta^{\prime}-\beta}
.

(4)

The number of subtrees grafted to the spine in the interval
I_{r}
in which the snake exits the interval
[-r\lambda^{-\gamma},r\lambda^{-\gamma}]
is at most
\frac{\lambda^{\gamma}}{8}
.

(5)

The volume of the ball of radius
\frac{r}{2}
centred at
h:=\pi(t_{\text{hub}})
is at least
\lambda^{-1}r^{\frac{2\alpha}{\alpha-1}}
.

Construction.
We first explain why
\nu(B(U_{a},r))\geq\lambda^{-1}r^{\frac{2\alpha}{\alpha-1}}
on the event
E_{r,\lambda}
. The logic is very similar to that used to prove Proposition
4.4
, and we will therefore omit some details. In particular, we let
N
be the number of subtrees appearing in point (4) above, we order them by their (increasing) distance from the root, and for
i=1,\ldots,N
we let
x_{i}
be the root of the
i^{th}
subtree. In addition we let
x_{0}=U_{a}
and
x_{N+1}=h
. Hence each
x_{i}
is on the spine from
U_{a}
to
\rho
, and by construction, it holds for each
i=0,\ldots,N
that there exist
t_{i},t_{i+1}\in[0,\sigma]
such that
p(t_{i})=x_{i},p(t_{i+1})=x_{i+1}
, and
\sup_{r,s\in[t_{i},t_{i+1}]}|Z_{r}-Z_{s}|\leq 4r\lambda^{-\gamma}
. Using that
D\leq D_{\alpha}^{*}
and combining with the triangle inequality therefore gives that

D(U_{a},h)\leq\frac{\lambda^{\gamma}}{8}\cdot 4r\lambda^{-\gamma}=\frac{r}{2}.

Tail estimates.
We now bound the probabilities of the complements of each of the above events in turn. We will then deduce the result using a union bound.

(1)

Using the Markov property of Brownian motion, we write

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\!\left((\xi_{t})_{0\leq t\leq r^{2}\lambda^{-2\gamma^{\prime}}}\not\subset[-r\lambda^{-\gamma},r\lambda^{-\gamma}]\right)=\mathbb{P}\!\left((\xi_{t})_{0\leq t\leq 1}\not\subset[-\lambda^{\gamma^{\prime}-\gamma},\lambda^{\gamma^{\prime}-\gamma}]\right)\leq Ce^{-c\lambda^{\gamma^{\prime}-\gamma}}.

(2)

By Proposition
2.6
, the number of such hubs is lower bounded by a Poisson random variable with parameter
c\lambda^{\beta(\alpha-1)-2\gamma^{\prime}}
, for an appropriate constant
c\in(0,\infty)
. Hence the probability that it is zero is at most
e^{-c\lambda^{\beta(\alpha-1)-2\gamma^{\prime}}}
.

(3)

Let
S_{r^{\frac{2}{\alpha-1}}\lambda^{-\beta}}
denote the sum in question. Then
S_{r^{\frac{2}{\alpha-1}}\lambda^{-\beta}}
concerns the hubs that all occur before
h
, and which are therefore all conditioned to have size at most
r^{\frac{2}{\alpha-1}}\lambda^{-\beta}
. The number of such hubs moreover has a geometric distribution with parameter
cr^{2}\lambda^{-\beta(\alpha-1)}
, and by standard thinning properties of Poisson processes this geometric time is independent of the sizes of the hubs. In addition applying the memoryless property of the geometric distribution along with scaling invariance, it therefore follows that for any
k>1
(with the obvious extension of notation)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\!\left(S_{r^{\frac{2}{\alpha-1}}\lambda^{-\beta}}>kr^{\frac{2}{\alpha-1}}\lambda^{-\beta}\right)\leq\mathbb{P}\!\left(S_{r^{\frac{2}{\alpha-1}}\lambda^{-\beta}}>r^{\frac{2}{\alpha-1}}\lambda^{-\beta}\right)^{k/2}=\mathbb{P}\!\left(S_{1}>1\right)^{k/2},

and so taking
k=\lambda^{\beta^{\prime}}
we deduce that the probability in question is bounded by
e^{-c\lambda^{\beta^{\prime}}}
.

(4)

It will suffice to bound the probability of this event conditionally on the high probability event in (3), since we can exclude the alternative using the previous estimate. In this case the number of such subtrees is upper bounded by a Poisson random variable with parameter
\lambda^{\frac{2\gamma}{\alpha-1}+\beta^{\prime}-\beta}
. Hence, by a Chernoff bound, the probability that it exceeds
\frac{\lambda^{\gamma}}{8}
is upper bounded by
C\exp\{-c\lambda^{\gamma-\frac{2\gamma}{\alpha-1}-\beta^{\prime}+\beta}\}
.

(5)

The final estimate is the most involved. We condition on the event in (2), and consider the collection of subtrees grafted to
h
in which the snake does not exit
[\xi_{h}-\frac{r}{8},\xi_{h}+\frac{r}{8}]
. Each of these subtrees are contained within
B(h,\frac{r}{2})
since
D\leq D_{\alpha}^{*}
and since we can bound fluctuations of the snake on the interval coding the subtree. It will in fact suffice to consider only the subtrees with total volume in
[r^{\frac{2\alpha}{\alpha-1}}\lambda^{-1},2r^{\frac{2}{\alpha-1}}\lambda^{-1}]
. For a tree with total volume in this interval, the probability that the snake does not exit the interval
[\xi_{h}-\frac{r}{8},\xi_{h}+\frac{r}{8}]
is uniformly bounded below by a positive constant by scaling invariance and since
\lambda>1
. Hence the total number of trees satisfying both of these properties is Poisson with parameter
c\lambda^{1/\alpha-\beta}
, for some
c\in(0,\infty)
. Hence the probability that this number is zero is
e^{-c\lambda^{1/\alpha-\beta}}
.

One can verify that it is possible to choose
\beta,\beta^{\prime},\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}>0
so that all of the probabilities above decay as stretched exponentials in
\lambda
. One viable option (which is not optimal) is to take

\beta=\frac{3}{4\alpha},\beta^{\prime}=\frac{1}{4\alpha},\gamma=\frac{\alpha-1}{32\alpha},\gamma^{\prime}=\frac{\alpha-1}{32\alpha},

which results in a final decay of
Ce^{-c\lambda^{\frac{\alpha-1}{32\alpha}}}
via a union bound on the above events.
∎

There exists

5.2
Lower tail bounds for small volumes

We now prove some bounds in the other direction.

Lemma 5.5
.

For
\nu
-almost every
x\in M
:

\liminf_{r\downarrow 0}\frac{\nu(B(x,r))}{r^{\frac{2\alpha}{\alpha-1}}(\log\log r^{-1})^{-\frac{1}{\alpha-1}}}<\infty.

Proof.

Again by Fubini’s theorem it is sufficient to prove the claim for
U
chosen according to
\nu
. It was proved in
[
Duq12
, Theorem 1.2 and Comment 1.1(c)]
that for
\mathbb{N}_{0}
-almost every realisation of the tree
\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}
and
\nu
-almost every
x\in\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}
:

\liminf_{r\downarrow 0}\frac{\upsilon(B_{\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}}(U,r))}{r^{\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}}(\log\log r^{-1})^{-\frac{1}{\alpha-1}}}=\alpha-1.

In particular, letting
A_{r}
denote the event that

\frac{\upsilon(B_{\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}}(U,r))}{r^{\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}}(\log\log r^{-1})^{-\frac{1}{\alpha-1}}}\leq 2C,

we know that
A_{r}
occurs infinitely often almost surely.

We use the same strategy for the volume upper bound as in the proof of Proposition
4.1
, and use the same notation. Take some
\delta>0
, and choose
K<\infty
such that
\mathbb{P}\!\left({\tau}_{r}<Kr^{2}\right)=\mathbb{P}\!\left({\tau}_{1}<K\right)>1-\delta
.

Now choose a subsequence
r_{n}\to 0
such that
A_{Kr_{n}^{2}}
occurs for all
n
. Since the Brownian motion on the spine is independent of the tree structure, we have that

\mathbb{P}\!\left({\tau}_{r_{n}}<Kr_{n}^{2}\right)\geq 1-\delta

for all
n
. Hence by Fatou’s lemma, it follows that, conditionally on the tree structure and our sequence
(r_{n})_{n\geq 1}
,

\mathbb{P}\!\left({\tau}_{r_{n}}<Kr_{n}^{2}\text{ i.o.}\right)\geq 1-\delta.

Now note that, by Lemma
3.14
, we have (for an appropriate choice of
C_{K}<\infty
)

\nu(B(U,r_{n}))\leq\upsilon(B_{\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}}(U,Kr_{n}^{2}))\leq C_{K}r_{n}^{\frac{2\alpha}{\alpha-1}}(\log\log r_{n}^{-1})^{-\frac{1}{\alpha-1}}.

on the event
\{{\tau}_{r_{n}}<Kr_{n}^{2}\}\cap A_{Kr_{n}^{2}}
. Hence we showed that the statement of the lemma holds with probability at least

, and hence with probability

since
\delta>0
was arbitrary.
∎

(the latter was in fact written for

Theorem 6.1
.

\mathbb{N}^{(1)}_{0}
-almost surely, the Hausdorff dimension of any subsequential limit of

6.2
Hausdorff dimension of

Recall the definition of the metric space
\left(\mathbf{m}_{\alpha},D_{\alpha}^{*}\right)
from (
2
) and the subsequent paragraph. Almost surely, it has the same Hausdorff dimension as any subsequential limit of
(M_{n}^{(\alpha)},n^{-\frac{2\alpha}{\alpha-1}}d_{n})
:

Theorem 6.2
.

Almost surely, the Hausdorff dimension of
\left(\mathbf{m}_{\alpha},D_{\alpha}^{*}\right)
is equal to
\frac{2\alpha}{\alpha-1}
.

Proof.

In the whole proof, we set

Lower bound.

It follows from
[

\limsup_{r\downarrow 0}\frac{\nu_{\alpha}(B(x,r))}{r^{d-\varepsilon}}=0.

Upper bound.

\frac{1}{d+\varepsilon}

\sup_{q,s\in[t,t+r^{d+2\varepsilon}]}|\widehat{W}_{s}-\widehat{W}_{q}|\leq r.

In particular, we deduce that the collection of balls around the points
