Nil-Hecke rings and the Schubert calculus
Edward Richmond, Kirill Zainoulline

Abstract
. The purpose of the present notes is to give a self-contained exposition on the use of the techniques of Nil-Hecke algebras in the localization approach to the equivariant Schubert calculus for cohomology of flag varieties. We also demonstrate how this techniques can be applied to non-crystallographic root systems as well as to study (connective) K K -theory of flag varieties.



1.
Introduction

Hecke-type algebras and the associated localization techniques play an essential role in the modern equivariant Schubert calculus. It was first observed by Demazure in
[
De73
]
and then by Konstant and Kumar in
[
KK86
,
KK90
]
that the algebras of divided-difference operators (nil-Hecke and
0
-Hecke algebras) can be efficiently used to describe and study (equivariant) cohomology and
K
-theory rings of flag varieties and Kac-Moody groups. Later this approach was applied to many other generalized cohomology theories (e.g., to algebraic and complex oriented cobordism in
[
BE87
,
BE90
]
and
[
HMSZ
,
CPZ
,
CZZ
,
CZZ1
,
CZZ2
]
). Indeed, almost all geometric structures which come with such generalized cohomology can be reconstructed in a purely algebraic way using the respective Hecke-type algebras. For example, the intersection product on cohomology can be defined as the dualized co-product of the respective nil-Hecke ring. Hence, all the properties of the intersection product can be deduced from those of the coproduct.

In these notes, we give a self-contained and detailed exposition of the interplay between nil-Hecke algebras, the coproduct structure and the cohomology of flag varieties. In particular, we showcase how nil-Hecke algebras can be used a practical tool to make calculations in cohomology. We also demonstrate how the same techniques can be applied to non-crystallographic root systems as well as to study (connective)
K
-theory of flag varieties. Many of the results stated in these notes readily extend to root systems and flag varieties of Kac-Moody type, however for the sake of exposition and simplicity, we restrict our focus to finite root systems (although not necessarily crystallographic) and finite dimensional flag varieties. For more on Kac-Moody nil-Hecke algebras and flag varieties, see
[
Ku02
]
.

Our paper is organized as follows. In section
2
we start by discussing root systems, root and weight lattices, Weyl and Coxeter groups.
These objects serve as the algebraic and combinatorial foundation for constructing and analyzing nil-Hecke algebras. In the next section
3
we define the nil-Hecke ring associated to a root datum
\Phi\hookrightarrow\Lambda^{\vee}
and give several examples. Observe that our definition applies to all root systems and lattices (not necessarily crystallographic).
We then realize the nil-Hecke ring as a sub-ring of a twisted group algebra and as a subring of certain operators on the polynomial ring of the root lattice. These two presentations provide important computational tools for dealing with nil-Hecke rings. Then in section
4
we equip the localized twisted group algebra
Q_{W}
with a co-product structure that plays an important role in the Schubert calculus of flag varieties. This coproduct naturally restricts to nil-Hecke subrings and also leads to the augmented coproduct (corresponding to the usual cohomology). We discuss several recursive formulas for its structure coefficients and provide various examples of computations. In section
5
we study duals of the twisted group algebra, nil-Hecke ring and its relations to Schubert calculus. In section
6
we outline the classical connections between torus-equivariant singular cohomology of flag varieties and nil-Hecke rings. In section
7
we demonstrate how the results of previous sections can be extended to the context of the so-called connective
K
-theory. Finally in the last section
8
we present some SageMath (Python) code for computing Schubert calculus structure coefficients for flag varieties of finite type. We remark that many of the results that appear in Section 4-6 on Nil-Hecke rings follow
[
Ku02
, Chapters 11-12]
.

2.
Root systems and Coxeter groups

In this section, we discuss root systems, root and weight lattices, Weyl and Coxeter groups.
These objects serve as the algebraic and combinatorial foundation for constructing and analyzing nil-Hecke algebras.
There are many references on these subjects. In our presentation we primarily follow
[
Hu90
]
and
[
Bou
]
.

We start from the following classical motivating example.

Let
n\geq 1
and consider the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
with standard basis
\{e_{0},\ldots,e_{n}\}
.
Let
\mathcal{H}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
be the hyperplane consisting of vectors whose sum of coordinates is zero

\mathcal{H}:=\Big\{\sum_{i=0}^{n}a_{i}e_{i}\colon\sum_{i=0}^{n}a_{i}=0\Big\}.

Let
\Phi
denote the subset of
\mathcal{H}
consisting of all differences of standard vectors

\Phi:=\{e_{i}-e_{j}\}_{i\neq j}.

Clearly
\Phi\cap m\Phi\neq\emptyset
if and only if
m=\pm 1
.
Observe that each vector from
\Phi
can be uniquely expressed as a
\mathbb{Z}
-linear combination of vectors from the subset

\Delta:=\{e_{i-1}-e_{i}\}_{i=1,\ldots,n}.

Moreover, if
\alpha\in\Phi
and

\alpha=\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}(e_{i-1}-e_{i}),

then either all coefficient
c_{i}\geq 0
, or all
c_{i}\leq 0
.
In other words,
\Phi
splits into two disjoint subsets
\Phi^{+}
and
\Phi^{-}
, where

\Phi^{+}:=\{e_{i}-e_{j}\}_{i<j}\quad\text{and}\quad\Phi^{-}:=\{e_{i}-e_{j}\}_{i>j}.

Let
\Lambda
denote the
\mathbb{Z}
-linear span of
\Delta
.
Observe that
\Lambda
is a free
\mathbb{Z}
-module of rank
n
and
\Lambda\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{R}\simeq\mathcal{H}
.

Given
\alpha\in\Phi
, consider the orthogonal reflection
s_{\alpha}
which fixes the hyperplane orthogonal to
\alpha=e_{i}-e_{j}
.
This reflection is given by the following formula

s_{\alpha}(\beta):=\beta-(\alpha,\beta)\,\alpha,

where
(\cdot,\cdot)
is the usual dot-product on
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
given by
(e_{i},e_{j}):=\delta_{i,j}
.
Observe that
s_{\alpha}
simply switches
e_{i}
and
e_{j}
(the
i
th and the
j
th coordinates)
and, hence, it leaves both
\Phi
and
\Lambda
invariant:

s_{\alpha}(\beta)\in\Phi\;(\text{resp.}\,\Lambda),\;\text{for all}\;\beta\in\Phi\;(\text{resp.}\,\Lambda).

Let
W
be the group generated by all reflections
s_{\alpha}
,
\alpha\in\Delta
,
where the multiplication is given by the composition.
Since it acts by permutations of the set of standard vectors
\{e_{0},\ldots,e_{n}\}
,
it can be identified with the symmetric group
S_{n+1}
on the set of indices
\{0,\ldots,n\}
.
By the very definition it leaves
\Phi
and
\Lambda
invariant.

The introduced subset of vectors
\Phi
provides an example of a
root system

of type
\mathrm{A}
and of rank
n
denoted
\mathrm{A}_{n}
:

•

elements of
\Phi
are called roots,

•

elements of
\Delta
are called simple roots,

•

elements of
\Phi^{+}
and
\Phi^{-}
are called positive and negative roots, respectively,

•

the free
\mathbb{Z}
-module
\Lambda
is called the root lattice,

•

the group
W
is called the Weyl group,

•

elements
s_{\alpha}\in W
,
\alpha\in\Delta
are called simple reflections.

2.1.
Root systems and lattices.

We now provide definitions of a general root system and of a root datum.
These generalize the previous example and serve as algebraic and combinatorial foundations for most of the objects discussed later.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
\Phi
be a finite (non-empty) subset of
\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\{0\}

(here we view
\mathbb{R}^{N}
as a Euclidean space as before).
For each vector
\alpha\in\Phi
we define a linear transformation
s_{\alpha}\colon\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}^{N}
by

s_{\alpha}(x):=x-\tfrac{2(\alpha,x)}{(\alpha,\alpha)}\,\alpha,\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}.

We say
\Phi
is a
root system
if the following conditions are satisfied:

(i)

If
m\in\mathbb{R}
and
\Phi\cap m\Phi\neq\emptyset
, then
m=\pm 1
, and

(ii)

s_{\alpha}(\Phi)\subseteq\Phi
for all
\alpha\in\Phi
.

Next, we define a root datum which pairs a root system with a lattice.
The definition follows the one given in
[
SGA3
, Exp. XXI, §1.1]
.

Definition 2.2
.

A
root datum
is a finitely generated free
\mathbb{Z}
-module
\Lambda
with a nonempty finite subset
\Phi
together with a set inclusion

\Phi\hookrightarrow\Lambda^{\vee},\quad\alpha\mapsto\alpha^{\vee}

into the dual
\Lambda^{\vee}:=\Hom_{\mathbb{Z}}(\Lambda,\mathbb{Z})
such that

(i)

If
m\in\mathbb{Z}
and
\Phi\cap m\Phi\neq\emptyset
, then
m=\pm 1
,

(ii)

\alpha^{\vee}(\alpha)=2
for all
\alpha\in\Phi
, and

(iii)

for all
\alpha,\beta\in\Phi
, we have

\beta-\alpha^{\vee}(\beta)\,\alpha\in\Phi\quad\text{and}\quad\beta^{\vee}-\beta^{\vee}(\alpha)\,\alpha^{\vee}\in\Phi^{\vee}.

Here
\Phi^{\vee}
denotes the image of
\Phi
in
\Lambda^{\vee}
,
and the elements of
\Phi
(resp.
\Phi^{\vee}
) are called roots (resp. coroots).

The
\mathbb{Z}
-submodule generated by
\Phi
in
\Lambda
is called the
root lattice
and is denoted by
\Lambda_{r}
.
A root datum is called
semisimple
if

\Lambda\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{Q}=\Lambda_{r}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{Q}.

From now on, by a root datum we will always mean a semisimple one.
The
\mathbb{Z}
-submodule of
\Lambda_{\mathbb{Q}}:=\Lambda\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{Q}
generated by all
x\in\Lambda_{\mathbb{Q}}

such that
\alpha^{\vee}(x)\in\mathbb{Z}
for all
\alpha\in\Phi
is called the
weight lattice
and is denoted by
\Lambda_{w}
.

By definition we have

\Lambda_{r}\subseteq\Lambda\subseteq\Lambda_{w}\quad\text{ and }\quad\Lambda_{r}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{Q}=\Lambda_{\mathbb{Q}}=\Lambda_{w}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{Q}.

The
\mathbb{Q}
-rank of
\Lambda_{\mathbb{Q}}
is called the
rank
of the root datum.
Each root datum
\Phi\hookrightarrow\Lambda^{\vee}
gives rise to a root system
by considering the real vector space
\Lambda_{\mathbb{R}}:=\Lambda\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{R}

equipped with a compatible Euclidean product
(\cdot,\cdot)
such that

\alpha^{\vee}(x)=\tfrac{2(\alpha,x)}{(\alpha,\alpha)}.

While a root datum determines a root system, the converse is not true.
For example, taking
\Lambda=\Lambda_{r}
compared to
\Lambda=\Lambda_{w}
can produce non-isomorphic root datum.

It can be shown that the root lattice
\Lambda_{r}
admits a
\mathbb{Z}
-basis
\Delta=\{\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}\}

such that each
\alpha\in\Phi
is a linear combination of
\alpha_{i}
’s with
either all positive or all negative coefficients and
n
is the rank of the root datum.
Hence the set
\Phi
splits into two disjoint subsets

\Phi=\Phi^{+}\sqcup\Phi^{-},

where
\Phi^{+}
(resp.
\Phi^{-}
) is called the set of positive (resp. negative) roots.
The
\mathbb{Z}
-basis
\Delta
is called the set of
simple roots
,
We define the set of
fundamental weights

\{\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{n}\}\subset\Lambda_{w}

to be Kronecker dual to the simple coroots
\{\alpha_{1}^{\vee},\ldots,\alpha_{n}^{\vee}\}
,
i.e.,

\alpha_{i}^{\vee}(\omega_{j}):=\delta_{i,j}.

The fundamental weights form a
\mathbb{Z}
-basis of the weight lattice
\Lambda_{w}
.
The
Cartan matrix

C:=[c_{ij}]_{1\leq i,j\leq n}
of the root datum is defined by the values

c_{ij}:=\alpha_{j}^{\vee}(\alpha_{i}).

By definition we have
\alpha_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}c_{ij}\,\omega_{j}
.
In other words, the Cartan matrix expresses simple roots in terms of fundamental weights.
Observe that the determinant of the Cartan matrix coincides
with the number of elements in the quotient group
\Lambda_{w}/\Lambda_{r}
.

Example 2.3
.

Consider the root system of type
\mathrm{A}_{2}
.
Here we consider the hyperplane in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
given by

\mathcal{H}=\{a_{1}e_{1}+a_{2}e_{2}+a_{3}e_{3}\ |\ a_{1}+a_{2}+a_{3}=0\}

and the subset of vectors

\Phi=\{\pm(e_{1}-e_{2}),\pm(e_{2}-e_{3}),\pm(e_{1}-e_{3})\}\subset\mathcal{H}.

The subset
\Phi
forms a root system of rank 2 where

\alpha_{1}:=e_{1}-e_{2}\quad\text{and}\quad\alpha_{2}:=e_{2}-e_{3}

is the standard choice of simple roots.
The Cartan matrix of type
\mathrm{A}_{2}
is

C=\left[\begin{matrix}2&-1\\
-1&2\end{matrix}\right].

We can diagrammatically represent this root system as follows:

\alpha_{1}
\alpha_{2}
\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}

Here the black vertices represent the root lattice
\Lambda_{r}
and the black and red dots together represent the weight lattice
\Lambda_{w}
.
In particular,
\Lambda_{r}
is the
\mathbb{Z}
-span of
\alpha_{1}
and
\alpha_{2}
and
\Lambda_{w}
is the
\mathbb{Z}
-span of fundamental weights

\omega_{1}:=\tfrac{1}{3}(2\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2})\quad\text{and}\quad\omega_{2}:=\tfrac{1}{3}(\alpha_{1}+2\alpha_{2}).

Note that calculating the fundamental weights in standard coordinates gives

\omega_{1}=e_{1}
and
\omega_{2}=e_{1}+e_{2}
.
The lattices
\Lambda_{r}
and
\Lambda_{w}
are two examples of a root datum of type
\mathrm{A}_{2}
.
The quotient group
\Lambda_{w}/\Lambda_{r}
is cyclic of order
3
.

2.2.
Dynkin diagrams and classification.

A root datum is called
irreducible
if it cannot be represented as a direct sum of root data,
i.e.,
\Lambda
cannot be written as
\Lambda=\Lambda_{1}\oplus\Lambda_{2}
,
where
\Phi_{1}\subset\Lambda_{1}
and
\Phi_{2}\subset\Lambda_{2}
are the root data.

To any irreducible root datum we associate a multi-graph
\mathcal{D}
called the
Dynkin diagram
.
Its vertices correspond to the set of simple roots
\Delta=\{\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}\}
, and
the number of edges connecting two different vertices
\alpha_{i}
and
\alpha_{j}
is determined
by the product
c_{ij}\cdot c_{ji}
of coefficients of the Cartan matrix.
If there is more than one edge connecting
\alpha_{i}
and
\alpha_{j}
,
we assign a direction to the edges between
\alpha_{i}
and
\alpha_{j}
.
Specifically if
c_{ij}<c_{ji}
, then
\alpha_{j}
points to
\alpha_{i}
.
In this case, we also have that
(\alpha_{i},\alpha_{i})<(\alpha_{j},\alpha_{j})
.
If there is a single edge, between
\alpha_{i}
and
\alpha_{j}
, then
(\alpha_{i},\alpha_{i})=(\alpha_{j},\alpha_{j})
.

It can be shown that an irreducible root datum is determined uniquely by

•

its Dynkin diagram
\mathcal{D}
and

•

the intermediate lattice
\Lambda_{r}\subseteq\Lambda\subseteq\Lambda_{w}
.

If
\Lambda=\Lambda_{w}
(resp.
\Lambda=\Lambda_{r}
), then the root datum is called simply connected (resp. adjoint),
and it will be denoted by
\mathcal{D}^{sc}_{n}
(resp.
\mathcal{D}_{n}^{ad}
),
where
\mathcal{D}={\rm A,B,C,D,E,F,G}

2.3.
Coxeter groups and Weyl groups

For each root
\alpha\in\Phi
, define a
\mathbb{Z}
-linear map
s_{\alpha}\colon\Lambda_{w}\to\Lambda_{w}
, by

s_{\alpha}(\lambda):=\lambda-\alpha^{\vee}(\lambda)\alpha,\quad\lambda\in\Lambda_{w}.

The linear map
s_{\alpha}
is called the
reflection
with respect to the root
\alpha
.
By definition we have
s_{\alpha}^{2}=\mathrm{id}
.
The group
W
generated by all reflections
s_{\alpha}
,
\alpha\in\Phi
is called the
Weyl group
of the root datum.
It can be shown that the Weyl group has a presentation

W=\langle s_{1},\ldots,s_{n}\colon(s_{i}s_{j})^{m_{ij}}=1\rangle

where
s_{i}=s_{\alpha_{i}}
denotes the generator corresponding to the simple root
\alpha_{i}\in\Delta
.
So
W
is the finite Coxeter group.
The
Coxeter exponents

m_{ij}
are determined by the coefficients of the Cartan matrix:

If
i=j
, then
m_{ii}:=1
. Otherwise, if
i\neq j
, then:

\displaystyle m_{ij}

\displaystyle:=2\text{ if }c_{ij}c_{ji}=0\text{ (no edge), }

\displaystyle m_{ij}

\displaystyle:=3\text{ if }c_{ij}c_{ji}=1\text{ (single edge), }

\displaystyle m_{ij}

\displaystyle:=4\text{ if }c_{ij}c_{ji}=2\text{ (double edge), }

\displaystyle m_{ij}

\displaystyle:=6\text{ if }c_{ij}c_{ji}=3\text{ (triple edge). }

(Since
\Phi
is finite, these are all possible values of Coxeter exponents.)

By definition each element of
W
can be written as a product of simple reflections

w=s_{i_{1}}s_{i_{2}}\cdots s_{i_{r}}.

The minimal number of generators required to express an element
w\in W
is unique
and is called the
length of
w
.
We denote the length by
\ell(w)
.
Any expression of
w
as a product of
\ell(w)
simple reflections is called
reduced
expression (or
reduced word
).

2.4.
Finite real root systems.

We now show how root datum can be generalized to finite real root systems.

Let
\Phi\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N}
be a root system and for each
\alpha\in\Phi
. We define
\alpha^{\vee}\in(\mathbb{R}^{N})^{*}
by

(*)

\alpha^{\vee}(x):=\tfrac{2(\alpha,x)}{(\alpha,\alpha)},\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}.

If
\alpha^{\vee}(\beta)\in\mathbb{Z}
for all
\alpha,\beta\in\Phi
, then
\alpha^{\vee}
defines an element in the dual
\mathbb{Z}
-module
\Lambda_{r}^{\vee}
,
and the map
\alpha\mapsto\alpha^{\vee}
gives rise to an inclusion
\Phi\hookrightarrow\Lambda_{r}^{\vee}
,
that satisfies the properties of a root datum.
This gives rise the following definition in
[
Hu90
, §2.9]

Definition 2.4
.

We say a finite root system
\Phi\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\{0\}
is
crystallographic

if the values
\tfrac{2(\alpha,\beta)}{(\alpha,\alpha)}\in\mathbb{Z}
for all
\alpha,\beta\in\Phi
.

If a root system is not crystallographic (i.e.
\alpha^{\vee}(\beta)\notin\mathbb{Z}
for some
\alpha,\beta\in\Phi
),
then assigning coroots using the formula (*) does not produce a well-defined root datum.
However, with an appropriate change in coefficient ring, a root datum can be formally defined for any root system (see below).

Let
\Phi\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\{0\}
be a root system and
define
\mathbb{Z}_{\Phi}
to be the subring of
\mathbb{R}
generated by the values
\alpha^{\vee}(\beta)
for all
\alpha,\beta\in\Phi
.
We call
\mathbb{Z}_{\Phi}
the
coefficient ring of the root system

\Phi
and
it can be viewed as the smallest subring of
\mathbb{R}
containing the values
\alpha^{\vee}(\beta)
.
If
\Phi
is crystallographic, then
\mathbb{Z}_{\Phi}=\mathbb{Z}
.
The following is a generalization of Definition
2.2
that allows for non-crystallographic root datum.

Definition 2.5
.

Let
\Phi\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\{0\}
be a root system and
for each
\alpha\in\Phi
, define the coroot
\alpha^{\vee}\in{(\mathbb{R}^{N})}^{*}
as in (*).
A
root datum with coefficients in
\mathbb{Z}_{\Phi}
is a free
\mathbb{Z}_{\Phi}
-module
\Lambda
containing
\Phi

together with a set inclusion

\Phi\hookrightarrow\Lambda^{\vee},\quad\alpha\mapsto\alpha^{\vee}

in the dual
\Lambda^{\vee}:=\Hom_{\mathbb{Z}_{\Phi}}(\Lambda,\mathbb{Z}_{\Phi})
such that

(i)

If
m\in\mathbb{Z}_{\Phi}
and
\Phi\cap m\Phi\neq\emptyset
, then
m=\pm 1
,

(ii)

\alpha^{\vee}(\alpha)=2
for all
\alpha\in\Phi
, and

(iii)

for any
\alpha,\beta\in\Phi
, we have

\beta-\alpha^{\vee}(\beta)\,\alpha\in\Phi\quad\text{and}\quad\beta^{\vee}-\beta^{\vee}(\alpha)\,\alpha^{\vee}\in\Phi^{\vee}.

Define the
root lattice

\Lambda_{r}
to be the
\mathbb{Z}_{\Phi}
-linear span of roots in
\Phi
.
Taking
\Lambda=\Lambda_{r}
provides an example of a root datum with coefficients in
\mathbb{Z}_{\Phi}
.
As with crystallographic root datum,
\Lambda_{r}
admits an
\mathbb{Z}_{\Phi}
-basis of simple roots

\Delta=\{\alpha_{1},\cdots,\alpha_{n}\}\subseteq\Phi
and
the Weyl group
W=\langle s_{1},\ldots,s_{n}\colon(s_{i}s_{j})^{m_{ij}}=1\rangle
.
Let
K
denote the field of fractions of
\mathbb{Z}_{\Phi}
.
If
\Phi
is crystallographic then
K=\mathbb{Q}
.
We say a root datum is
semisimple
if

\Lambda\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}_{\Phi}}K=\Lambda_{r}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}_{\Phi}}K.

Define the
weight lattice

\Lambda_{w}:=\{\lambda\in\Lambda_{r}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}_{\Phi}}K\colon\alpha^{\vee}(\lambda)\in{\mathbb{Z}_{\Phi}}\text{ for all }\alpha\in\Phi\}.

We prove the following lemma:

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
\Phi\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\{0\}
be a root system and
let
\Phi\hookrightarrow\Lambda^{\vee}
be a semisimple root datum with coefficients in
\mathbb{Z}_{\Phi}
.
Then the weight lattice
\Lambda_{w}
is a finitely generated free
\mathbb{Z}_{\Phi}
-module and
\Lambda_{r}\subseteq\Lambda\subseteq\Lambda_{w}
.

Proof.

Since it is semisimple, it immediately follows that
\Lambda_{r}\subseteq\Lambda\subseteq\Lambda_{w}
.
Let
\Delta:=\{\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}\}
denote the simple roots of
\Phi
.
If
\alpha\in\Phi
, then by
[
Hu90
, §1.5]
, there exists
w\in W
such that
\alpha=w(\alpha_{i})
for some
\alpha_{i}\in\Delta
.
By induction on the length of
w
, we have that
\alpha=\sum_{i=1}^{n}b_{i}\,\alpha_{i}
for some coefficients
b_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\Phi}
.
Hence, if
\alpha_{j}^{\vee}(\lambda)\in\mathbb{Z}_{\Phi}
for all
\alpha_{j}\in\Delta
,
then
\alpha^{\vee}(\lambda)\in\mathbb{Z}_{\Phi}
.
Let
\lambda\in\Lambda_{r}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}_{\Phi}}K
and write

\lambda=\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}\,\alpha_{i},\quad c_{i}\in K.

We have that
\lambda\in\Lambda_{w}
if and only if

\alpha_{j}^{\vee}(\lambda)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}\,\alpha_{j}^{\vee}(\alpha_{i})\in\mathbb{Z}_{\Phi}\;\text{ for all }\alpha_{j}\in\Delta.

These conditions are equivalent to
C\cdot(c_{1},\ldots,c_{n})^{\mathrm{t}}\in(\mathbb{Z}_{\Phi})^{n}
,
where
C
is the Cartan matrix of
\Phi
.
Hence
\Lambda_{w}=C^{-1}(\Lambda_{r})
is a finitely generated, free
\mathbb{Z}_{\Phi}
-module.
∎

As in the crystallographic case, we will only consider semisimple root datum.

Example 2.7
.

We contrast two examples of root system
\mathrm{B}_{2}
.
First, we have the crystallographic root system of type
\mathrm{B}_{2}
given by the Cartan matrix

C=\left[\begin{matrix}2&-2\\
-1&2\end{matrix}\right].

The root system and root lattice can be visualized by the following diagram:

\alpha_{1}
\alpha_{2}
\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}
\alpha_{1}+2\alpha_{2}

In this case, the coefficient ring is
\mathbb{Z}_{\Phi}=\mathbb{Z}
.

Second, we consider the “normalized” root system of type
\mathrm{B}_{2}
given by the Cartan matrix

C=\left[\begin{matrix}2&-\sqrt{2}\\
-\sqrt{2}&2\end{matrix}\right].

In this case, the root system is no longer crystallographic:

\alpha_{1}
\alpha_{2}
\sqrt{2}\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}
\alpha_{1}+\sqrt{2}\alpha_{2}

The coefficient ring is
\mathbb{Z}_{\Phi}=\mathbb{Z}(\sqrt{2})
and the root lattice
\Lambda_{r}
is the
\mathbb{Z}(\sqrt{2})
-span of
\alpha_{1}
and
\alpha_{2}
.
Note that, while
\Lambda_{r}
is a free
\mathbb{Z}
-module of rank 4, it is also dense (in the Euclidean sense) in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

2.5.
Coxeter diagrams and classification

Any Coxeter group

W=\langle s_{1},\ldots,s_{n}\colon(s_{i}s_{j})^{m_{ij}}=1\rangle

can be geometrically realized as a root system as follows.
Let
\{\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}\}
and
\{\alpha_{1}^{\vee},\ldots,\alpha_{n}^{\vee}\}
be bases of Euclidean spaces
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{*}
where

(\alpha_{i},\alpha_{i})=1\quad\text{and}\quad\alpha_{j}^{\vee}(\alpha_{i})=-2\cos(\pi/m_{ij}).

Then the orthogonal subgroup of
\GL(\mathbb{R}^{n})
generated by simple reflections

s_{i}(x)=x-\alpha_{i}^{\vee}(x)\,\alpha_{i},\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

gives rise to a root system whose Weyl group is isomorphic to
W
.
We call such realization the
finite (normalized) real root system corresponding to

W
.

Finite (normalized) real root systems are classified by
Coxeter diagrams

[
Hu90
, §2]
.
Similar to Dynkin diagrams, its vertices are in 1-1 correspondence with the set of simple roots
\{\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}\}
.
Different vertices
\alpha_{i}
and
\alpha_{j}
are connected by an edge only if
m_{ij}\geq 3
.
In addition, if
m_{ij}\geq 4
, then the respective edge is labelled by the Coxeter exponent
m_{ij}
.
The respective Cartan matrix
C=[\alpha_{j}^{\vee}(\alpha_{i})]_{ij}
is symmetric.
When
W
is not the Weyl group of a finite crystallographic root system,
the finite real root systems are classified by the Coxeter diagrams of the following types
(here
m=5
or
m\geq 7
):

\textstyle{\mathrm{I}_{2}(m):}
\textstyle{\bullet}
1
\scriptstyle{m}
\textstyle{\bullet}
2

\textstyle{\mathrm{H}_{3}:}
\textstyle{\bullet}
1
\scriptstyle{5}
\textstyle{\bullet}
2
\textstyle{\bullet}
3

\textstyle{\mathrm{H}_{4}:}
\textstyle{\bullet}
1
\scriptstyle{5}
\textstyle{\bullet}
2
\textstyle{\bullet}
3
\textstyle{\bullet}
4

Observe that
\mathrm{I}_{2}(2)
and
\mathrm{I}_{2}(3)
correspond to finite crystallographic root systems of type
\mathrm{A}_{1}\times\mathrm{A}_{1}
and
\mathrm{A}_{2}
, respectively.
Moreover,
\mathrm{I}_{2}(4)
and
\mathrm{I}_{2}(6)
can be viewed as the normalized crystallographic root systems of type
\mathrm{B}_{2}
(
=\mathrm{C}_{2}
) and
\mathrm{G}_{2}
, respectively. Indeed, these are obtained by scaling one of the simple roots by
2\cos(\pi/m)=\sqrt{2}
and
\sqrt{3}
, respectively (See Example
2.7
). In the following examples, we give an explicit construction of the normalized and polarized real root systems of the dihedral group
\mathrm{I}_{2}(m)
.

Example 2.8
.

We realize
\mathrm{I}_{2}(m)
as the Weyl group of a real root system as follows.
Under the standard identification of
\mathbb{C}\simeq\mathbb{R}^{2}
, let
\zeta
denote the
2m
-th primitive root of unity.
Consider the finite set of vectors
\Phi=\langle\zeta\rangle\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
For any
\alpha\in\Phi
, the orthogonal reflection (in complex coordinates) is given by

s_{\alpha}(z)=(-\alpha\bar{\alpha}^{-1})\,\bar{z}.

In particular, if
\alpha=\zeta^{j}
, then

s_{\zeta^{j}}(z)=-\zeta^{2j}\bar{z}=\zeta^{2j+m}\bar{z}.

Hence
s_{\zeta^{j}}(\zeta^{i})=\zeta^{2j+m-i}
and the set
\Phi
forms a root system with Weyl group

\mathrm{I}_{2}(m)=\langle s_{\alpha}\colon\alpha\in\Phi\rangle
.
Moreover, since

s_{\zeta^{j}}(\zeta^{i})=\zeta^{2j+m-i}=\zeta^{i}-(\zeta^{i-j}+\zeta^{j-i})\zeta^{j},

we conclude that the co-roots satisfy
(\zeta^{j})^{\vee}(\zeta^{i})=\zeta^{i-j}+\zeta^{j-i}
. If we set

\tau_{m}:=2\cos(\pi/m)=\zeta+\zeta^{-1}

and define simple roots

\Delta=\{\alpha_{1}=1,\alpha_{2}=\zeta^{m-1},\}

then
\alpha_{1}^{\vee}(\alpha_{2})=\alpha_{2}^{\vee}(\alpha_{1})=-\tau_{m}
and the simple reflections act by

s_{i}(\alpha_{j})=\alpha_{j}+\tau_{m}\,\alpha_{i}

for
i\neq j
. Thus,
\Phi
is a normalized root system. It is easy to see that
s_{1},s_{2}
are Coxeter generators of the Weyl group of
\mathrm{I}_{2}(m)
.
The coefficient ring

\mathbb{Z}_{\Phi}=\langle\zeta^{i-j}+\zeta^{j-i}\rangle_{i,j}=\mathbb{Z}(\tau_{m})

is the ring of integers in the real cyclotomic field
\mathbb{Q}(\tau_{m})
.

If
m=5
, then
\tau_{5}=\tfrac{1+\sqrt{5}}{2}
is a root of
x^{2}-x-1=0
. If
m=7
, then
\tau_{7}
is a root of
x^{3}-x^{2}-2x+1=0
.
The diagram below corresponds the normalized root system of type
\mathrm{I}_{2}(5)
.

\alpha_{1}
\alpha_{2}
\tau_{5}\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}
\alpha_{1}+\tau_{5}\alpha_{2}
\tau_{5}\alpha_{1}+\tau_{5}\alpha_{2}

In contrast to normalized root system, we next consider polarized root systems of type
\mathrm{I}_{2}(m)
in the case that
m
is even. When
m
is even, the action of
\mathrm{I}_{2}(m)
on the normalized root system
\Phi
has two orbits which are generated by the simple roots
1
and
\zeta^{m-1}
respectively. We define a new root system of type
\mathrm{I}_{2}(m)
by scaling the vectors in the orbit of
\zeta^{m-1}
by
\tau_{m}^{-1}
and leaving the orbit of 1 unchanged. Any root system isomorphic to this rank 2 root system is called the
polarized root system
of type
\mathrm{I}_{2}(m)
. In this case, we have simple roots

\Delta=\{\alpha_{1}=1,\alpha_{2}=\tau_{m}^{-1}\cdot\zeta^{m-1}\}.

with
\alpha_{1}^{\vee}(\alpha_{2})=-1
,
\alpha_{2}^{\vee}(\alpha_{1})=-(\tau_{m}^{2})
and the coefficient ring
\mathbb{Z}_{\Phi}=\mathbb{Z}(\tau_{m}^{2})
. The simple reflections act on the simple roots by

s_{1}(\alpha_{2})=\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}\quad\text{and}\quad s_{2}(\alpha_{1})=\alpha_{1}+\tau_{m}^{2}\alpha_{2}.

The
\mathrm{I}_{2}(m)
-orbits of
\alpha_{1}
and
\alpha_{2}
are called the long and short roots respectively. Note that the crystallographic root systems of type
\mathrm{B}_{2}
and
\mathrm{G}_{2}
are polarized root systems corresponding to
m=4
and
m=6
. Intuitively, polarized root systems can be thought of as root systems that are “as close as possible” to crystallographic.

If
m=8
, then
\tau_{8}=\sqrt{2+\sqrt{2}}
and
\mathbb{Z}_{\Phi}=\mathbb{Z}(\tau_{m}^{2})=\mathbb{Z}(\sqrt{2})
.
The diagram below corresponds the polarized root system of type
\mathrm{I}_{2}(8)
.

\alpha_{1}
\alpha_{2}
\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}
\alpha_{1}+(2+\sqrt{2})\alpha_{2}

We remark that when
m
is odd, then any non-normalized root system of type
\mathrm{I}_{2}(m)
must be of at least rank 3.

3.
Nil-Hecke rings and twisted group algebras

In this section we define the nil-Hecke ring associated to a root datum
\Phi\hookrightarrow\Lambda^{\vee}
and give several examples.
We then realize the nil-Hecke ring as a sub-ring of a twisted group algebra and as a subring of certain operators on the polynomial ring of the root lattice.
These two presentations provide important computational tools for dealing with nil-Hecke rings.
Our exposition follows the original construction of the nil-Hecke ring given by Kostant and Kumar in
[
KK86
]
and the generalized version of it in
[
CZZ
]
.

3.1.
The nil-Coxeter and the nil-Hecke ring

We start with the following definition

Definition 3.1
.

Let
W
be a Coxeter group with generating set
\{s_{1},\ldots,s_{n}\}

subject to the Coxeter relations
(s_{i}s_{j})^{m_{ij}}=1
for
1\leq i,j\leq n
.
The
nil-Coxeter ring

\mathcal{N}
associated to
W
is the unital ring with free generators
\{X_{i}\colon i=1,\ldots,n\}

modulo the
nilpotent relations

X_{i}^{2}=0\quad\text{for all $1\leq i\leq n$},

and
braid relations

\underbrace{X_{i}X_{j}X_{i}\ldots}_{m_{ij}}=\underbrace{X_{j}X_{i}X_{j}\ldots}_{m_{ij}}\quad\text{for all $i\neq j$}.

In other words,
\mathcal{N}
is obtained from the integral group ring of the Coxeter group
W
by substituting the relation
s_{i}^{2}=1
with
X_{i}^{2}=0
.
The following lemma follows immediately from the definition of nil-Coxeter ring.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\mathcal{N}
denote the nil-Coxeter ring associated to a Coxeter group
W
and let
w\in W
.
If
w=s_{i_{1}}s_{i_{2}}\ldots s_{i_{r}}=s_{j_{1}}s_{j_{2}}\ldots s_{j_{r}}
are two reduced expressions for
w
, then

X_{i_{1}}X_{i_{2}}\ldots X_{i_{r}}=X_{j_{1}}X_{j_{2}}\ldots X_{j_{r}}.

In particular, the product of generators
X_{w}:=X_{i_{1}}X_{i_{2}}\ldots X_{i_{r}}

does not depend on the reduced expression of
w
and is well-defined.

Furthermore, if
u,v\in W
, then

X_{u}\cdot X_{v}=\begin{cases}X_{uv}&if\quad\ell(u)+\ell(v)=\ell(uv),\\
0&if\quad\ell(u)+\ell(v)<\ell(uv).\end{cases}

Observe that Lemma
3.2
implies that the nil-Coxeter ring
\mathcal{N}
is a free
\mathbb{Z}
-module with basis
\{X_{w}\}_{w\in W}
.

Now suppose we are given a root datum
\Phi\hookrightarrow\Lambda^{\vee}

in the sense of either
2.2
or
2.5
.
By definition the lattice
\Lambda
is a free
\mathbb{Z}_{\Phi}
-module and, hence, we may consider the symmetric algebra

S(\Lambda):=\Sym_{\mathbb{Z}_{\Phi}}(\Lambda).

If we fix a
\mathbb{Z}_{\Phi}
-basis
\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}
of
\Lambda
, then
S(\Lambda)
is isomorphic to the polynomial ring
{\mathbb{Z}_{\Phi}}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
.
Note that the respective Coxeter group
W
acts on the lattice
\Lambda
and, therefore, it acts on the symmetric algebra
S(\Lambda)
which we denote simply by
S
.

Definition 3.3
.

Given a root datum
\Phi\hookrightarrow\Lambda^{\vee}
and a basis
\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}
of
\Lambda
,
the
nil-Hecke ring
associated to
\Phi\hookrightarrow\Lambda^{\vee}
is defined to be the left
S
-module

\mathfrak{N}:=S\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}_{\Phi}}\mathcal{N}

equipped with multiplication induced by the relations called the
affine relations
:

(Aff)

X_{i}\,x_{j}=s_{i}(x_{j})\,X_{i}+\alpha_{i}^{\vee}(x_{j})\quad\text{for all }1\leq i,j\leq n.

Note that the definition of the nil-Hecke ring does not depend on the choice of basis of
\Lambda
.
From the affine relations (Aff) it follows that
\mathfrak{N}
is a free left
S
-module with basis
\{X_{w}\}_{w\in W}
.
The relations (Aff) also induce the following relations in
\mathfrak{N}
:

Proposition 3.4
.

For any
w\in W
and
p\in\Lambda
we have the following relation in
\mathfrak{N}
:

X_{w}p=w(p)X_{w}+\sum_{w^{\prime}\xrightarrow{\beta}w}\beta^{\vee}(p)X_{w^{\prime}},

where
u\xrightarrow{\beta}w
means
w=us_{\beta}
for some root
\beta\in\Phi_{+}
with
\ell(w)=\ell(u)+1
.

Proof.

We proceed by induction on
\ell(w)
.
If
\ell(w)=1
, then the proposition follows directly from (Aff).
Now assume that the relation holds for all
u<w
and
write
w=s_{i}u
from some simple generator
s_{i}
where
\ell(w)=\ell(u)+1
.
By induction, we have

\displaystyle X_{w}p=X_{i}\cdot(X_{u}p)

\displaystyle=X_{i}\cdot\Big(u(p)X_{u}+\sum_{u^{\prime}\xrightarrow{\gamma}u}\gamma^{\vee}(p)X_{u^{\prime}}\Big)

\displaystyle=w(p)X_{w}+\alpha_{i}^{\vee}(u(p))X_{u}+\sum_{u^{\prime}\xrightarrow{\gamma}u}\gamma^{\vee}(p)\,X_{i}\cdot X_{u^{\prime}}.

First note that
u\xrightarrow{u^{-1}(\alpha_{i})}w
and, hence the second summand belongs to the desired sum in the relation.
Now suppose that
w^{\prime}\xrightarrow{\beta}w
and
u\neq w^{\prime}
and hence
w=w^{\prime}s_{\beta}=s_{i}u
.
This implies
u=(s_{i}w^{\prime})s_{\beta}
with
\ell(u)=\ell(s_{i}w^{\prime})+1
and thus
s_{i}w^{\prime}\xrightarrow{\beta}u
.
Conversely, if
u^{\prime}\xrightarrow{\gamma}u
, then
u^{\prime}=s_{i}w^{\prime}
from some
(w^{\prime})\xrightarrow{\gamma}w

if and only if
\ell(s_{i}u^{\prime})=\ell(u^{\prime})+1
.
Hence, the summand in the expression above yields the desired result.
∎

Example 3.5
.

Consider the Coxeter group of type
\mathrm{A}_{3}
and let
w=s_{1}s_{2}s_{3}
.
Then for any
p\in\Lambda
we have

X_{w}p=w(p)X_{w}+\alpha_{3}^{\vee}(p)X_{s_{1}s_{2}}+s_{3}(\alpha_{2})^{\vee}(p)X_{s_{1}s_{3}}+s_{3}s_{2}(\alpha_{1})^{\vee}(p)X_{s_{2}s_{3}}.

Remark 3.6
.

Observe that an embedding of intermediate lattices
\Lambda\hookrightarrow\Lambda^{\prime}
induces
the embedding of the respective polynomial rings
S(\Lambda)\to S(\Lambda^{\prime})
.
This further induces an embedding of the respective nil-Hecke rings
\mathfrak{N}\hookrightarrow\mathfrak{N}^{\prime}
.
This embedding becomes an isomorphism after inverting the order
|\Lambda^{\prime}/\Lambda|
.
In other words, we get an isomorphism between
\mathfrak{N}
and
\mathfrak{N}^{\prime}
if we extend the coefficient ring
\mathbb{Z}_{\Phi}
to
\mathbb{Z}_{\Phi}[\tfrac{1}{|\Lambda^{\prime}/\Lambda_{r}|}]
.
However, the rings
\mathfrak{N}
and
\mathfrak{N}^{\prime}
are not necessarily isomorphic over
\mathbb{Z}_{\Phi}
.

Example 3.7
.

Let
\Phi
be the root system of type
\mathrm{A}_{n}
.
The Weyl group
W
is the permutation group generated by
\{s_{1},\ldots,s_{n}\}
subject to the Coxeter relations:

s_{i}^{2}=1,\quad s_{i}s_{i+1}s_{i}=s_{i+1}s_{i}s_{i+1}\quad\text{and}\quad s_{i}s_{j}=s_{j}s_{i}\quad\text{if $|i-j|>1$.}

The nil-Coxeter group
\mathcal{N}
is generated by
\langle 1,X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\rangle
with the analogous relations:

(1)

X_{i}^{2}=0,\quad X_{i}X_{i+1}X_{i}=X_{i+1}X_{i}X_{i+1},\quad\text{and}\quad X_{i}X_{j}=X_{j}X_{i}\quad\text{if $|i-j|>1$.}

Consider the root datum
\Phi\hookrightarrow\Lambda_{w}^{\vee}
corresponding to the weight lattice (it corresponds to the simply connected simple group
\SL_{n+1}
).
The weight lattice has a basis of fundamental weights
\{\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{n}\}
.
Using the notation from Section
2
, we can write
\omega_{i}=e_{1}+e_{2}+\cdots+e_{i}
,
where
\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{n+1}\}
denotes the standard coordinate basis of
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
.
The nil-Hecke ring is then given by

\mathfrak{N}_{w}=\mathbb{Z}[\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{n}]\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathcal{N},

where
\mathcal{N}
is the respective nil-Coxeter ring, and
the affine relations (here we assume
\omega_{0}=\omega_{n+1}=0
) are

(2)

\begin{cases}X_{i}\omega_{j}=\omega_{j}X_{i}&\text{if }i\neq j,\\
X_{i}\omega_{i}=(\omega_{i-1}-\omega_{i}+\omega_{i+1})X_{i}+1&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Observe that
e_{1}=\omega_{1}
,
e_{i}=\omega_{i}-\omega_{i-1}
for
i=2,\ldots,n
, and
e_{n+1}=-\omega_{n}

(here we assume
e_{1}+\ldots+e_{n+1}=0
).
Substituting into (
2
) we obtain

X_{i}e_{j}=e_{j}X_{i},\text{ if }j\neq i,i+1,\;\text{ and }X_{i}e_{i}=e_{i+1}X_{i}+1,\;X_{i}e_{i+1}=e_{i}X_{i}-1

which can be rewritten as

(3)

X_{i}e_{j}-e_{s_{i}(j)}X_{i}=\delta_{i,j}-\delta_{i+1,j}.

If we substitute the polynomial ring
\mathbb{Z}[\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{n}]
by
\mathbb{Z}[e_{1},\ldots,e_{n+1}]

and forget the relation
e_{1}+\ldots+e_{n+1}=0
,
we then obtain the classical definition of the
nil-Hecke algebra
generated by
\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{n+1},X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\}

which satisfies the nil-Coxeter relations given in (
1
)
and the additional affine relations in (
3
).

Consider now the root datum
\Phi\hookrightarrow\Lambda_{r}^{\vee}
corresponding to the root lattice instead of the weight lattice
(this corresponds to the adjoint simple group
\PGL_{n+1}
).
The root lattice has a basis of simple roots
\Delta=\{\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}\}
, and in terms of the standard basis of
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}

we have
\alpha_{i}=e_{i}-e_{i+1}
.
The nil-Hecke ring is then

{\mathfrak{N}}_{r}=\mathbb{Z}[\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}]\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathcal{N}

with the additional affine relations:

\begin{cases}X_{i}\alpha_{j}=(\alpha_{i}+\alpha_{j})X_{i}-1&\text{if }|i-j|=1,\\
X_{i}\alpha_{j}=\alpha_{j}X_{i}&\text{if }|i-j|>1,\\
X_{i}\alpha_{i}=-\alpha_{i}X_{i}+2&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

By definition
{\mathfrak{N}}_{r}
is a proper subring of
{\mathfrak{N}}_{w}
.
For example, take
n=1
. Then

{\mathfrak{N}}_{r}=\mathbb{Z}[\alpha]\Big\langle X\colon{\scriptsize\begin{matrix}X^{2}=0,\\
X\alpha=-\alpha X+2\end{matrix}}\Big\rangle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\alpha\mapsto 2\omega}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathbb{Z}[\omega]\Big\langle X\colon{\scriptsize\begin{matrix}X^{2}=0,\\
X\omega=-\omega X+1\end{matrix}}\Big\rangle={\mathfrak{N}}_{w}.

Observe that the ring
{\mathfrak{N}}_{r}
is not isomorphic to
{\mathfrak{N}}_{w}
(over
\mathbb{Z}
),
as they are not even isomorphic modulo
2
(
{\mathfrak{N}}_{r}
is commutative, but
{\mathfrak{N}}_{w}
is not).
However, they become isomorphic after inverting
2
.

Next, we consider an example of non-crystallographic type.

Example 3.8
.

Consider the normalized real root system of type
\mathrm{I}_{2}(5)
(see Example
2.8
).
In this case
\mathbb{Z}_{\Phi}=\mathbb{Z}(\tau_{5})
where
\tau_{5}=2\cos(\tfrac{\pi}{5})=\tfrac{1+\sqrt{5}}{2}
.

The nil-Hecke ring
{\mathfrak{N}}_{r}
for
\Phi\hookrightarrow\Lambda_{r}^{\vee}
(the root lattice) is the quotient of

{\mathbb{Z}_{\Phi}}[\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}]\langle 1,X_{1},X_{2}\colon X_{1}^{2}=X_{2}^{2}=0,\;X_{1}X_{2}X_{1}X_{2}X_{1}=X_{2}X_{1}X_{2}X_{1}X_{2}\rangle

by (Aff) relations: (
i\neq j
,
i,j=1,2
)

\begin{cases}X_{i}\alpha_{i}=-\alpha_{i}X_{i}+2,\\
X_{i}\alpha_{j}=(\alpha_{j}+\tau_{5}\alpha_{i})X_{i}-\tau_{5}.\end{cases}

The nil-Hecke ring
{\mathfrak{N}}_{w}
for
\Phi\hookrightarrow\Lambda_{w}^{\vee}
(the weight lattice with
\alpha_{i}=2\omega_{i}-\tau_{5}\omega_{j}
) is the quotient of

{\mathbb{Z}_{\Phi}}[\omega_{1},\omega_{2}]\langle 1,X_{1},X_{2}\colon X_{1}^{2}=X_{2}^{2}=0,\;X_{1}X_{2}X_{1}X_{2}X_{1}=X_{2}X_{1}X_{2}X_{1}X_{2}\rangle

by (Aff) relations: (
i\neq j
,
i,j=1,2
)

\begin{cases}X_{i}\omega_{i}=(\tau_{5}\omega_{j}-\omega_{i})X_{i}+1,\\
X_{i}\omega_{j}=\omega_{j}X_{i}.\end{cases}

Direct computations show that
|\Lambda_{w}/\Lambda_{r}|=5
. So the inclusion
{\mathfrak{N}}_{r}\hookrightarrow{\mathfrak{N}}_{w}
becomes a ring isomorphism only after inverting
5
.

3.2.
The nil-Hecke subring of a localized twisted group algebra

Given a finite real root system, its Coxeter group and the associated root/weight lattice,
we introduce the localized twisted group algebra and show that the nil-Hecke ring is natural subring of this algebra.
The action of the twisted group algebra on the symmetric algebra over a root datum gives
an important representation of the nil-Hecke ring.
In this subsection we provide an overview of these constructions and explain its basic properties.

Consider a root datum
\Phi\hookrightarrow\Lambda^{\vee}
with coefficient ring
\mathbb{Z}_{\Phi}
.
Let
R
be a commutative
\mathbb{Z}_{\Phi}
-algebra. Let
S:=\Sym_{R}(\Lambda)
be the associated graded symmetric
R
-algebra.
The Coxeter group
W
acts on
\Lambda
by
\mathbb{Z}_{\Phi}
-linear automorphisms and, hence,
it acts on
S
by
R
-linear ring automorphisms.

Definition 3.9
.

We define the
twisted group algebra

S_{W}
to be the free left
S
-module

S_{W}:=S\otimes_{R}R[W].

Each element of
S_{W}
can be written as a
S
-linear combination

\sum_{w\in W}p_{w}\delta_{w},

where
\{\delta_{w}\}_{w\in W}
is the standard basis of the group ring
R[W]
and
p_{w}\in S
are coefficients.
For the identity element
e\in W
we set
\delta_{e}=1
.
We define the ring multiplication on
S_{W}
to be the one coming from
R[W]
, however, we require it to satisfy the twisted commuting relation with respect to coefficients:

w(p)\,\delta_{w}=\delta_{w}\,p,\quad p\in S,\;w\in W.

For example, if
p,q\in S
and
w,v\in W
, then

p\,\delta_{w}\cdot q\,\delta_{v}=p\,w(q)\,\delta_{wv}.

Let
\Delta=\{\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}\}\subset\Lambda
be the set of simple roots.
We further assume that each
\alpha_{i}\in\Delta
is regular in
S
(in other words,
\alpha_{i}
is a non-zero element which is not a zero-divisor).
Let
\alpha\in\Phi
. Since
\alpha=w(\alpha_{i})
for some
w\in W
and
\alpha_{i}\in\Delta
, we have
\alpha
is also regular in
S
.
A symmetric algebra
S
in which every root is regular is called
\Phi
-regular.

Remark 3.10
.

(cf.
[
CZZ
, Lemma 2.2]
)
The regularity of
\alpha_{i}
holds if either the root datum does not contain an irreducible component of simply-connected type
\mathrm{C}

or if
2
is regular in
R
.
Indeed, in the simply-connected type
\mathrm{C}_{n}
we have
\alpha_{n}=-2\omega_{n-1}+2\omega_{n}\equiv 0\mod 2
.

Definition 3.11
.

Let
Q:=S\left(\tfrac{1}{\alpha},\alpha\in\Phi\right)
denote the localization of the
\Phi
-regular algebra
S
at all
\alpha\in\Phi
. We define the
localized twisted group algebra
to be

Q_{W}:=Q\otimes_{R}R[W].

Observe that the canonical ring inclusion
S\hookrightarrow Q
induces the ring inclusion
S_{W}\hookrightarrow Q_{W}
.

We now realize the nil-Coxeter and nil-Hecke rings as sub-rings of
Q_{W}
.
For each simple root
\alpha_{i}
, define the element

X_{i}:=\tfrac{1}{\alpha_{i}}(\delta_{e}-\delta_{s_{i}})\in Q_{W}

and let
\mathcal{N}
denote the sub-ring of
Q_{W}
generated by
\{X_{i}\}_{i=1,\ldots,n}
.
It is proved in
[
KK86
]
that the generators

\{X_{i}\}_{i=1,\ldots,n}
satisfy the nilpotent and braid relations.
Hence, if
w=s_{i_{1}}\ldots s_{i_{r}}
is a reduced expression, we can define

X_{w}:=X_{i_{1}}\ldots X_{i_{r}}

with
X_{w}
being independent of choice of reduced expression of
w
.

Proposition 3.12
.

The sub-ring
\mathcal{N}\subseteq Q_{W}
is isomorphic to the nil-Coxeter ring of
W
as defined in Definition
3.1
.

Proof.

It suffices to show that the set
\{X_{w}\}_{w\in W}
is linearly independent over
S

and is therefore a
\mathbb{Z}
-basis of
\mathcal{N}
(following Lemma
3.2
).
Let
w\in W
and fix a reduced word
w=s_{i_{1}}\cdots s_{i_{r}}
.
Then expanding
X_{w}
in the
\{\delta_{u}\}_{u\in W}
basis of
Q_{W}
yields:

\displaystyle X_{w}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{\alpha_{i_{1}}}(\delta_{e}-\delta_{s_{i_{1}}})\cdots\tfrac{1}{\alpha_{i_{r}}}(\delta_{e}-\delta_{s_{i_{r}}})

\displaystyle=\tfrac{(-1)^{\ell(w)}}{\alpha_{i_{1}}s_{i_{1}}(\alpha_{i_{2}})\cdots(s_{i_{1}}s_{i_{2}}\cdots s_{i_{r-1}}(\alpha_{i_{r}}))}\,\delta_{w}+\sum_{u<w}c_{w,u}\,\delta_{u}.

In particular, the transition matrix from
\{X_{u}\}_{u\in W}
to
\{\delta_{u}\}_{u\in W}
is upper triangular with respect to Bruhat order
and is invertible over
Q
.
Hence
\{X_{u}\}_{u\in W}
is a
Q
-basis of
Q_{W}
which implies that
\{X_{u}\}_{u\in W}
is
S
-linearly independent.
∎

Note that the denominator of the coefficient
c_{w,w}
in the proof above is equal to the product of roots in
\Phi^{+}\cap w(\Phi^{-})
. This set of roots is called the inversion set of
w\in W
. We now define

\mathfrak{N}:=S\otimes_{R}\mathcal{N}\subset Q\otimes_{Q}Q_{W}=Q_{W}.

Proposition 3.13
.

The sub-ring
\mathfrak{N}\subseteq Q_{W}
is isomorphic to the nil-Hecke ring of
W
as defined in Definition
3.3
.

Proof.

To prove that
\mathfrak{N}
is isomorphic to the nil-Hecke ring,
it suffices to show that the relations (Aff) hold.
Indeed, let
p\in\Lambda.
Then for each
1\leq i\leq n
, we have

\displaystyle X_{i}p-s_{i}(p)X_{i}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{\alpha_{i}}(\delta_{e}-\delta_{s_{i}})p-\tfrac{s_{i}(p)}{\alpha_{i}}(\delta_{e}-\delta_{s_{i}})

\displaystyle=\tfrac{p}{\alpha_{i}}\delta_{e}-\tfrac{s_{i}(p)}{\alpha_{i}}\delta_{s_{i}}-\tfrac{s_{i}(p)}{\alpha_{i}}\delta_{e}+\tfrac{s_{i}(p)}{\alpha_{i}}\delta_{s_{i}}=\alpha_{i}^{\vee}(x)\delta_{e}.\qed

3.3.
The polynomial representation

From now on, we will view the nil-Hecke ring as an
S
-subalgebra of
Q_{W}
.
We define the
\circ
-action of the localized twisted group algebra
Q_{W}
on
Q
by
R
-linear endomorphisms via

(4)

p\delta_{w}\circ q:=pw(q).

Under this action the element
X_{i}
acts on
Q
as the divided-difference operator

X_{i}\circ q=\frac{q-s_{i}(q)}{\alpha_{i}},\quad q\in Q.

Divided-difference operators preserve the polynomial subalgebra
S
,
so the
\circ
-action gives a homomorphism of
R
-modules

Q_{W}\to\Hom_{R}(S,Q)

which restricts to a homomorphism of
R
-algebras which we denote by:

\rho\colon\mathfrak{N}\to\End_{R}(S).

Specifically, for any
y=\sum_{w\in W}a_{w}X_{w}\in\mathfrak{N}
and
p\in S
, we have

\rho(y)(p):=\sum_{w\in W}a_{w}(X_{w}\circ p)\in S.

Lemma 3.14
.

Let
p,q\in S
and
1\leq i\leq n
. Then the following are true:

(i)

If
p\in\Lambda
, then
X_{i}\circ p=\alpha_{i}^{\vee}(p)

(ii)

The operator
X_{i}
satisfies the twisted Leibniz rule:

X_{i}\circ(pq)=(X_{i}\circ p)\,q+s_{i}(p)\,X_{i}\circ q

Proof.

If
p\in\Lambda
, then

X_{i}\circ p=\tfrac{1}{\alpha_{i}}(p-(p-\alpha_{i}^{\vee}(p)\alpha_{i}))=\alpha_{i}^{\vee}(p).

This proves part (1). For part (2), we have

\displaystyle X_{i}\circ(pq)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{\alpha_{i}}(pq-s_{i}(pq))=\tfrac{1}{\alpha_{i}}(pq-s_{i}(p)q+s_{i}(p)q-s_{i}(pq))

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{\alpha_{i}}((p-s_{i}(p))q+s_{i}(p)(q-s_{i}(q)))=(X_{i}\circ p)\,q+s_{i}(p)\,X_{i}\circ q.\qed

Note that Lemma
3.14
.(i) implies that the affine relation (Aff) can be rewritten as

X_{i}\,p=s_{i}(p)X_{i}+X_{i}\circ p,\quad p\in\Lambda.

It is easy to see that
X_{i}\circ p=0
if and only if
s_{i}(p)=p
.
Otherwise, for any homogenous polynomial
p\in S
, we have

\deg(X_{i}\circ p)=\deg(p)-1

More generally, we have
\deg(X_{w}\circ p)=\deg(p)-\ell(w)
and
hence if
\deg(p)=\ell(w)
, then
X_{w}\circ p\in R
.
This leads to the following lemma:

Lemma 3.15
.

Let
w_{0}
be the element of maximal length in
W
.
Suppose there exists a homogeneous polynomial
u_{0}\in S
of degree
\ell(w_{0})
such that

X_{w_{0}}\circ u_{0}
is a regular element in
R
.
Then the representation
\rho
is faithful.

Proof.

Observe that
X_{w_{0}}\circ u_{0}=0
, if
u_{0}
belongs to the ideal of
W
-invariants of
S
.
Indeed, as in the proof of
[
CZZ1
, Thm. 7.10]
, we may assume that
X_{w_{0}}\circ u_{0}
is invertible in
R
.
Suppose
y=\sum_{w}a_{w}X_{w}
,
a_{w}\in S
acts by
0
(i.e,
\rho(y)=0
). Then for any
v\in W
,

(y\cdot X_{v})\circ u_{0}=\sum_{w}a_{w}(X_{wv}\circ u_{0})=0.

Taking
v=w^{-1}w_{0}
, we obtain that each
a_{w}=0
and hence
y=0
.
∎

Remark 3.16
.

Suppose that
R=\mathbb{Z}_{\Phi}
.
Consider the ideal
J
in
\mathbb{Z}_{\Phi}
generated by all elements
X_{w_{0}}\circ p
where
p\in S^{\ell(w_{0})}:=\Sym_{\mathbb{Z}_{\Phi}}^{\ell(w_{0})}(\Lambda)
.
Then the assumption of the lemma translates into assuming that
J
contains a regular element. The ideal
J
can be viewed as the generalization of the torsion index given in
[
CZZ1
]
. Indeed, in the crystallographic case
\mathbb{Z}_{\Phi}=\mathbb{Z}
, the only generator
g_{1}
of
J
gives precisely the torsion index of the root datum.
In general,
\mathbb{Z}_{\Phi}
is not necessarily a PID, but a Dedekind domain.
In the latter case the ideal
J
can be uniquely factorized into the product of prime ideals

J=\mathfrak{p}_{1}^{r_{1}}\ldots\mathfrak{p}_{s}^{r_{s}}.

Each of the prime ideals
\mathfrak{p}_{i}
in this factorization can be thought of as a torsion prime (prime ideal) of the root datum.

4.
The coproduct structure

In this section we equip the localized twisted group algebra
Q_{W}

with a co-product structure that plays an important role in the Schubert calculus of flag varieties.
This coproduct naturally restricts to nil-Hecke subrings as well as induces the respective augmented version. We discuss several recursive formulas for its structure coefficients and provide various examples of computations.

4.1.
The coproduct on twisted group algebras

We follow
[
CZZ
, §8]
and
[
KK86
]
.
Consider
Q_{W}\otimes_{Q}Q_{W}
over
Q
(as left
Q
-modules).
Specifically, we have

q\cdot(z\otimes z^{\prime})=qz\otimes z^{\prime}=z\otimes qz^{\prime}.

Define the coproduct

\mathit{\Delta}\colon Q_{W}\to Q_{W}\otimes_{Q}Q_{W}

to be the morphism of left
Q
-modules given by

\mathit{\Delta}(q\delta_{w})=q\delta_{w}\otimes\delta_{w}=\delta_{w}\otimes q\delta_{w}.

By definition the co-product
\mathit{\Delta}
is a morphism of
R
-algebras,
is
Q
-linear, coassociative, and cocommutative with counit
\epsilon\colon Q_{W}\to Q
given by
q\delta_{w}\mapsto q
.
Therefore,
Q_{W}
is a cocommutative coalgebra in the category of left
Q
-modules.

We equip the tensor algebra
Q_{W}\otimes_{Q}Q_{W}
with the following product.

(q\delta_{w}\otimes\delta_{v})\odot(q^{\prime}\delta_{w^{\prime}}\otimes\delta_{v^{\prime}}):=qw(q^{\prime})\,\delta_{ww^{\prime}}\otimes\delta_{wv^{\prime}w^{-1}v}.

It can be shown that this product gives rise to a ring structure on
Q_{W}\otimes_{Q}Q_{W}

and for any
q\delta_{w},z,z^{\prime}\in Q_{W}
, we have

(q\delta_{w}\otimes\delta_{w})\odot(z\otimes z^{\prime})=q\delta_{w}z\otimes\delta_{w}z^{\prime}.

In the next lemma we show that the ring structure is compatible with the twisted product on
Q_{W}
.

Lemma 4.1
.

The coproduct
\mathit{\Delta}\colon Q_{W}\to Q_{W}\otimes_{Q}Q_{W}
is a ring homomorphism.

Proof.

Clearly the coproduct is linear, so it suffices to check the compatibility of multiplication.
Let
q_{w}\delta_{w},q_{v}\delta_{v}\in Q_{W}
. Then

\mathit{\Delta}(q_{w}\delta_{w}\cdot q_{v}\delta_{v})=\mathit{\Delta}(q_{w}w(q_{v})\delta_{wv})=q_{w}w(q_{v})\delta_{wv}\otimes\delta_{wv}.

On the other hand,

\displaystyle\mathit{\Delta}(q_{w}\delta_{w})\odot\mathit{\Delta}(q_{v}\delta_{v})

\displaystyle=(q_{w}\delta_{w}\otimes\delta_{w})\odot(q_{v}\delta_{v}\otimes\delta_{v})

\displaystyle=q_{w}w(q_{v})\delta_{wv}\otimes\delta_{www^{-1}v}=q_{w}w(q_{v})\delta_{wv}\otimes\delta_{wv}.\qed

Next, we study how the co-product
\mathit{\Delta}
restricts to the nil-Hecke ring
\mathfrak{N}\subset Q_{W}
, but before we do this,
we consider a different
S
-basis of
\mathfrak{N}
.
For any
1\leq i\leq n
, define the
push-pull elements

Y_{i}:=\tfrac{1}{\alpha_{i}}(\delta_{s_{i}}-\delta_{e}).

For purposes of notation, we set
Y_{e}:=\delta_{e}=1
.
It is clear that
Y_{i}=-X_{i}
and hence the set
\{Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n}\}
satisfy the nilpotent and braid relations.
For any reduced expression
w=s_{i_{1}}\ldots s_{i_{r}}
, we define

Y_{w}:=Y_{i_{1}}\ldots Y_{i_{r}}.

The push-pull elements
Y_{w}
are independent of choice of reduced expression and
Y_{w}=(-1)^{\ell(w)}X_{w}
.
Propositions
3.12
and
3.13
imply that
the set
\{Y_{w}\}_{w\in W}
is an
S
-basis of the nil-Hecke ring
\mathfrak{N}
.
The analogous affine relations to those given in Proposition
3.4
are

(5)

Y_{w}\,p=w(p)\,Y_{w}-\sum_{w^{\prime}\xrightarrow{\beta}w}\beta^{\vee}(p)\,Y_{w^{\prime}},\quad p\in\Lambda.

4.2.
The coproduct on nil-Hecke rings

The theorem below describes the restriction of the co-product
\mathit{\Delta}
to
\mathfrak{N}

and the recursive structure of the co-product with respect to the push-pull basis. This formula first appears in
[
KK86
]
and has been subsequently been used in
[
BR15
]
and
[
GK21
]
.
For
w\in W
, we denote its left and right descent sets as:

\displaystyle D_{L}(w)

\displaystyle:=\{s_{i}\colon\ell(s_{i}w)=\ell(w)-1\},\text{ and}

\displaystyle D_{R}(w)

\displaystyle:=\{s_{i}\colon\ell(ws_{i})=\ell(w)-1\}.

Theorem 4.2
.

The co-product map
\mathit{\Delta}:Q_{W}\to Q_{W}\otimes_{Q}Q_{W}
restricts to a ring homomorphism of
S
-modules

\mathit{\Delta}\colon\mathfrak{N}\to\mathfrak{N}\otimes_{S}\mathfrak{N}.

Moreover, for any
i\leq n
, we have

\mathit{\Delta}(Y_{i})=Y_{i}\otimes 1+1\otimes Y_{i}+\alpha_{i}Y_{i}\otimes Y_{i},

and for any
w\in W
and
s_{i}\in D_{L}(w)
, if we write

\mathit{\Delta}(Y_{w})=\sum_{v,u\in W}p_{u,v}^{w}\ Y_{u}\otimes Y_{v},

then the coefficients satisfy the recursion

p_{u,v}^{w}=\begin{cases}Y_{i}\circ(p_{u,v}^{s_{i}w})&\text{if $s_{i}\notin D_{L}(u)\cup D_{L}(v)$}\\
Y_{i}\circ(p_{u,v}^{s_{i}w})+s_{i}(p_{s_{i}u,v}^{s_{i}w})&\text{if $s_{i}\in D_{L}(u)\setminus D_{L}(v)$}\\
Y_{i}\circ(p_{u,v}^{s_{i}w})+s_{i}(p_{u,s_{i}v}^{s_{i}w})&\text{if $s_{i}\in D_{L}(v)\setminus D_{L}(u)$}\\
Y_{i}\circ(p_{u,v}^{s_{i}w})+s_{i}(p_{s_{i}u,v}^{s_{i}w}+p_{u,s_{i}v}^{s_{i}w})+\alpha_{i}s_{i}(p_{s_{i}u,s_{i}v}^{s_{i}w})&\text{if $s_{i}\in D_{L}(u)\cap D_{L}(v)$}\end{cases}

with
p_{e,e}^{e}=1
.

Proof.

Since
\{Y_{w}\}_{w\in W}
forms a
Q
-basis of
Q_{W}
,
it suffices to show that the coefficients
p_{u,v}^{w}
are all elements of
S
and thus

\mathit{\Delta}(Y_{w})\in\mathfrak{N}\otimes_{S}\mathfrak{N}
.
We first consider the case that
w=s_{i}
. Indeed, we have

\displaystyle\mathit{\Delta}(Y_{i})

\displaystyle=\mathit{\Delta}\big(\tfrac{1}{\alpha_{i}}(\delta_{s_{i}}-\delta_{e})\big)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{\alpha_{i}}(\delta_{s_{i}}\otimes\delta_{s_{i}}-\delta_{e}\otimes\delta_{e})

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{\alpha_{i}}\big(\delta_{s_{i}}\otimes(\delta_{s_{i}}-\delta_{e})+(\delta_{s_{i}}-\delta_{e})\otimes\delta_{e}\big)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{\alpha_{i}}\big((\delta_{s_{i}}-\delta_{e})\otimes(\delta_{s_{i}}-\delta_{e})+(\delta_{s_{i}}-\delta_{e})\otimes\delta_{e}+\delta_{e}\otimes(\delta_{s_{i}}-\delta_{e})\big)

\displaystyle=\alpha_{i}(Y_{i}\otimes Y_{i})+Y_{i}\otimes 1+1\otimes Y_{i}.

Hence,
\mathit{\Delta}(Y_{i})\in\mathfrak{N}\otimes_{S}\mathfrak{N}
.

Now consider
w\in W
with
\ell(w)\geq 2
, and let
w=s_{i}w^{\prime}
from some
s_{i}\in D_{L}(w)
.
Write

\mathit{\Delta}(Y_{w^{\prime}})=\sum_{u,v}p_{u,v}^{w^{\prime}}\ Y_{u}\otimes Y_{v}

and assume for the sake of induction that
\mathit{\Delta}(Y_{w^{\prime}})\in\mathfrak{N}\otimes_{S}\mathfrak{N}
, or equivalently, each
p_{u,v}^{w^{\prime}}\in S
.
Then

\displaystyle\mathit{\Delta}(Y_{w})

\displaystyle=\mathit{\Delta}(Y_{i})\odot\mathit{\Delta}(Y_{w^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{\alpha_{i}}(\delta_{s_{i}}\otimes\delta_{s_{i}}-\delta_{e}\otimes\delta_{e})\odot\big(\sum_{u,v}p_{u,v}^{w^{\prime}}\ Y_{u}\otimes Y_{v}\big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{u,v}\ \tfrac{1}{\alpha_{i}}\big(s_{i}(p_{u,v}^{w^{\prime}})\ (\delta_{s_{i}}Y_{u}\otimes\delta_{s_{i}}Y_{v})-p_{u,v}^{w^{\prime}}\ (Y_{u}\otimes Y_{v})\big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{u,v}\ \tfrac{s_{i}(p_{u,v}^{w^{\prime}})}{\alpha_{i}}\Big((\delta_{s_{i}}-\delta_{e})Y_{u}\otimes(\delta_{s_{i}}-\delta_{e})Y_{v}+(\delta_{s_{i}}-\delta_{e})Y_{u}\otimes Y_{v}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+Y_{u}\otimes(\delta_{s_{i}}-\delta_{e})Y_{v}+Y_{u}\otimes Y_{u}\Big)-\tfrac{p_{u,v}^{w^{\prime}}}{\alpha_{i}}(Y_{u}\otimes Y_{u})

\displaystyle=\sum_{u,v}\ \alpha_{i}\cdot s_{i}(p_{u,v}^{w^{\prime}})\ (Y_{i}Y_{u}\otimes Y_{i}Y_{v})+s_{i}(p_{u,v}^{w^{\prime}})\ (Y_{i}Y_{u}\otimes Y_{v}+Y_{u}\otimes Y_{i}Y_{v})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+Y_{i}\circ p_{u,v}^{w^{\prime}}\ (Y_{u}\otimes Y_{v}).

Since the action of
\alpha_{i}s_{i}
,
s_{i}
and
Y_{i}
each preserve
S
,
we have
\mathit{\Delta}(Y_{w})\in\mathfrak{N}\otimes_{S}\mathfrak{N}
.
By induction,
\mathit{\Delta}(\mathfrak{N})\subseteq\mathfrak{N}\otimes_{S}\mathfrak{N}
.

Suppose that
Y_{u}=Y_{i}Y_{z}
in
\mathfrak{N}
. Since

4.4.
The expanded coproduct formula

In Example
4.5
, we see that the twisted Leibniz formula
is used in the application of Theorem
4.2
for computing the structure coefficients
p_{u,v}^{w}
.
In this section, we give a non-recursive formula for the coefficients
p_{u,v}^{w}
in terms of bounded bijections studied in
[
BR15
]
.
To begin, let
[n]=\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
and, following
[
CZZ
, §4]
,
we first give a generalized Leibniz formula a sequence
\mathbf{i}:=(i_{1},\ldots,i_{r})\in[n]^{r}
.
For any subset
E\subseteq[r]
, let
\mathbf{i}_{E}
denote the corresponding subsequence of
\mathbf{i}
.
In other words, if
E=\{j_{1}<\cdots<j_{k}\}
, then

\mathbf{i}_{E}:=(i_{j_{1}},\ldots,i_{j_{k}}).

Define the operator

Y_{\mathbf{i}}:=Y_{i_{1}}\circ\cdots\circ Y_{i_{r}}.

We also, for any pair of subsets
E_{1},E_{2}\subseteq[r]
, we define
p_{E_{1},E_{2}}^{\mathbf{i}}\in S
as

(6)

p_{E_{1},E_{2}}^{\mathbf{i}}:=B_{1}\cdots B_{r}(1)

with the operator
B_{j}\colon S\to S
defined by

B_{j}(p):=\begin{cases}\alpha_{i_{j}}s_{i_{j}}(p)&\text{if }j\in E_{1}\cap E_{2},\\
Y_{i_{j}}\circ p&\text{if }j\notin E_{1}\cup E_{2},\\
s_{i_{j}}(p)&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Observe that if
E_{1}\cap E_{2}=\emptyset
and
E_{1}\cup E_{2}\neq[m]
, then
p_{E_{1},E_{2}}^{\mathbf{i}}=0
since
Y_{i}\circ(1)=0
. The coefficients
p_{E_{1},E_{2}}^{\mathbf{i}}
are sometimes called Bott-Samelson coefficients since they can be identified with Schubert structure constants of a Bott-Samelson variety and have studied in this context in
[
Wi04
]
,
[
BR15
]
and
[
GK21
]
.

The next lemma follows from repeated applications of Lemma
3.14
.(ii) (see
[
CZZ
, Lemma 4.8]
):

Lemma 4.8
.

Let
\mathbf{i}:=(i_{1},\ldots,i_{r})\in[n]^{r}
.
For any
p,q\in S
we have

Y_{\mathbf{i}}\circ(pq)=\sum_{E_{1},E_{2}\subseteq[m]}p_{E_{1},E_{2}}^{\mathbf{i}}(Y_{\mathbf{i}_{E_{1}}}\circ p)\cdot(Y_{\mathbf{i}_{E_{2}}}\circ q).

Dualizing we obtain the corresponding formula for the coproduct on the nil-Hecke ring
\mathfrak{N}
(cf.
[
CZZ
, Prop. 9.5]
):

\mathit{\Delta}(Y_{\mathbf{i}})=\sum_{E_{1},E_{2}\subseteq[r]}p_{E_{1},E_{2}}^{\mathbf{i}}\,Y_{\mathbf{i}_{E_{1}}}\otimes Y_{\mathbf{i}_{E_{2}}}.

If
\mathbf{i}
corresponds to a reduced expression of
w\in W
where
r=\ell(w)
,
we obtain the following formula for the coproduct in the nil-Hecke ring
\mathfrak{N}
:

\mathit{\Delta}(Y_{w})=\sum_{u,v\in W}p_{u,v}^{w}\,Y_{u}\otimes Y_{v},

where

(7)

p_{u,v}^{w}=\sum\,p_{E_{1},E_{2}}^{\mathbf{i}},

where the sum is over all pairs
(E_{1},E_{2})\subseteq[r]^{2}
such that
\mathbf{i}_{E_{1}},\mathbf{i}_{E_{2}}
are reduced expressions for
u,v
respectively.
The expression (
7
) is closely related to the recursive formula for
p_{u,v}^{w}
given in Theorem
4.2
.
In particular, each summand
p_{E_{1},E_{2}}^{\mathbf{i}}
corresponds to a sequence of operators
B_{j}
that is consistent with a path in the recursion.

4.5.
The formula in terms of bounded bijections

Note that the coefficients
p_{E_{1},E_{2}}^{\mathbf{i}}
can be further expanded
by applying Lemma
3.14
to the operators
B_{j}
when
j\in E_{1}\cap E_{2}
.
We state this expansion in terms of bounded bijections which are defined as follows

Definition 4.9
.

Let
K,L
be subsets of
[r]
such that
|K|+|L|=r
.
We say a bijection

\phi\colon K\to[r]\setminus L

is
bounded
if
\phi(k)<k
for all
k\in K
.
If
K=\emptyset
and
L=[r]
, then we consider
\phi\colon K\to K
a
vacuous
bounded bijection.

For each bounded bijection
\phi\colon K\to[r]\setminus L
and
k\in[r]
, define the set

L(k):=L\cup\phi(K\cap[k]).

Now given a sequence
\mathbf{i}=(i_{1},\ldots,i_{r})\in[n]^{r}
and a bounded bijection
\phi\colon K\to[r]\setminus L
, we define a root
\alpha^{\mathbf{i}}_{k}
for each
k\in[r]
as follows. Let

\alpha^{\mathbf{i},\phi}_{k}:=w_{k}(\alpha_{i_{k}})\quad\text{where}\quad w_{k}:=\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\in L(k)\\
\phi(k)<j<k\end{subarray}}}\limits^{\longrightarrow}}s_{i_{j}}

where we set
\phi(k):=0
if
k\notin K
and the product
\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\prod}\limits^{\longrightarrow}}
is taken in the natural order induced by the sequence
[r]
.
If
K=\emptyset
, then we set
\alpha^{\mathbf{i},\phi}_{k}:=1
for all
k\in[r]
.

The following theorem is proved in
[
BR15
]
using a careful application of Lemma
3.14
on the definition of
p_{E_{1},E_{2}}^{\mathbf{i}}
given in (
6
).

Theorem 4.10
.

Given a sequence
\mathbf{i}=(i_{1},\ldots,i_{r})
and subsets
E_{1},E_{2}\subseteq[m]
, the coefficient

p_{E_{1},E_{2}}^{\mathbf{i}}=\sum_{(K,\phi)}\Big(\prod_{k\in K}\alpha^{\vee}_{i_{\phi(k)}}(\alpha_{\ell}^{\mathbf{i},\phi})\Big)\Big(\prod_{\ell\in(E_{1}\cap E_{2})\setminus K}\alpha_{\ell}^{\mathbf{i},\phi}\Big)

where the summation is over all pairs
(K,\phi)
such that

(1)

K
is a subset of
E_{1}\cap E_{2}
such that
|K|+|E_{1}\cup E_{2}|=r
; and

(2)

\phi\colon K\to[r]\setminus(E_{1}\cup E_{2})
is a bounded bijection.

Combining Theorem
4.10
with the formula (
7
) gives a non-recursive formula for the structure coefficients
p_{u,v}^{w}
. In the next theorem, we observe that several terms cancel in this combined expression. To do that we need the following

Definition 4.11
.

Given a sequence
\mathbf{i}=(i_{1},\ldots,i_{r})
and a subset
J\subseteq[r]
, we say that the subsequence
\mathbf{i}_{J}
is
admissible
if
i_{j}\neq i_{j+1}
for all
j\in J
.
We say a bounded bijection
\phi
is
\mathbf{i}
-admissible
if the subsequence
\mathbf{i}_{L(k)}
is admissible for all
k\in K
.

Note that if a sequence
\mathbf{i}
corresponds to a reduced word of some
w\in W
, then
\mathbf{i}
is admissible.

Theorem 4.12
.

Let
w,u,v\in W
and let
\mathbf{i}=(i_{1},\ldots,i_{r})
correspond to a reduced expression of
w
. Then the coefficient
p_{u,v}^{w}
can be computed as

p_{u,v}^{w}=\sum_{(E_{1},E_{2})}\sum_{(K,\phi)}\Big(\prod_{k\in K}\alpha^{\vee}_{i_{\phi(k)}}(\alpha_{k}^{\mathbf{i},\phi})\Big)\Big(\prod_{\ell\in(E_{1}\cap E_{2})\setminus K}\alpha_{\ell}^{\mathbf{i},\phi}\Big),

where the first sum is taken over all pairs
(E_{1},E_{2})\subseteq[r]^{2}
such that
\mathbf{i}_{E_{1}},\mathbf{i}_{E_{2}}

are reduced expressions for
u,v
respectively and the second sum is over all pairs
(K,\phi)
such that

(1)

K
is a subset of
E_{1}\cap E_{2}
such that
|K|+|E_{1}\cup E_{2}|=r
; and

(2)

\phi\colon K\to[m]\setminus(E_{1}\cup E_{2})
is an
\mathbf{i}
-admissible bounded bijection.

A proof of Theorem
4.12
can be found in
[

]
.
The general idea is that terms corresponding to bounded bijections that are not
\mathbf{i}
-admissible
can be “canceled” when grouping terms across various pairs

corresponding to reduced words of

and
v
.

-b&2\end{array}\right]

where
a,b
are any positive real numbers such that
\sqrt{ab}=2\cos(\pi/m)
.
If
m=2
, we set both
a=b=0
.
For any
r\leq m
, define

u_{r}:=\underbrace{\cdots s_{1}s_{2}s_{1}}_{r}\quad\text{and}\quad v_{r}:=\underbrace{\cdots s_{2}s_{1}s_{2}}_{r}.

If
1\leq r<m
, then
u_{r},v_{r}
are the two unique elements in
W
of length
r
.
Otherwise
u_{0}=v_{0}=1
and
u_{m}=v_{m}
is the longest element in
W
.
From Corollary
4.14
(also Theorem
4.2
),
it is not difficult to conclude that the augmented coefficients satisfy

c^{u_{r+t}}_{u_{r},u_{t}}=c^{v_{r+t+1}}_{v_{r+1},u_{t}}=c^{v_{r+t+1}}_{u_{r},v_{t+1}},\qquad c^{v_{r+t}}_{v_{r},v_{t}}=c^{u_{r+t+1}}_{u_{r+1},v_{t}}=c^{u_{r+t+1}}_{v_{r},u_{t+1}},

and

c^{v_{r+t}}_{u_{r},u_{t}}=c^{u_{r+t}}_{v_{r},v_{t}}=0.

Hence it suffices to analyze the coefficients
c^{u_{r+t}}_{u_{r},u_{t}}
and
c^{v_{r+t}}_{v_{r},v_{t}}
.
Define the sequences
A_{k}
and
B_{k}
by

A_{k}:=aB_{k-1}-A_{k-2}\quad\text{and}\quad B_{k}:=bA_{k-1}-B_{k-2}

where
A_{0}=B_{0}=0
and
A_{1}=B_{1}=1
.
For
k\leq m
, define the binomial coefficients

C(r,t):=\frac{A_{r+t}A_{r+t-1}\cdots A_{1}}{(A_{r}A_{r-1}\cdots A_{1})(A_{t}A_{t-1}\cdots A_{1})},

D(r,t):=\frac{B_{r+t}B_{r+t-1}\cdots B_{1}}{(B_{r}B_{r-1}\cdots B_{1})(B_{t}B_{t-1}\cdots B_{1})}.

We remark that the recursive structure of these sequences is related of Chebyshev polynomials of second kind.
The following theorem is proved by Kitchloo in
[
Ki14
, §10]
for crystallographic root systems,
by Berenstein and Kapovich in
[
BK11
]
for normalized root systems and
the first author and Berenstein for real root systems in
[
BR15
]
.

Theorem 4.16
.

Let
\Lambda
be a root system of rank 2 with the Weyl group
W
of type
\mathrm{I}_{2}(m)
and with the Cartan matrix (
8
).
For any
1\leq r,t\leq m
, we have

c^{u_{r+t}}_{u_{r},u_{t}}=C(r,t)\quad\text{and}\quad c^{v_{r+t}}_{v_{r},v_{t}}=D(r,t).

Proof.

We give an outline of the proof given in
[
BR15
]
for the coefficients
c^{u_{r+t}}_{u_{r},u_{t}}

(the proof for
c^{v_{r+t}}_{v_{r},v_{t}}
is similar).
Let
\mathbf{i}=(\ldots,2,1,2,1)
denote the repeating sequence of length
m^{\prime}:=r+t
.
Let
(E_{1},E_{1})\subseteq[m^{\prime}]^{2}
be a pair of subsets such that
\mathbf{i}_{E_{1}},\mathbf{i}_{E_{2}}
are reduced expressions of
u_{r},u_{t}
respectively.
It can be shown that each such pair such admits at most one
\mathbf{i}
-admissible bounded bijection

\phi\colon E_{1}\cap E_{2}\to[m^{\prime}]\setminus(E_{1}\cup E_{2})
.
Applying this fact to the formula in Corollary
4.14
, we get that

c^{u_{m^{\prime}}}_{u_{r},u_{t}}=\alpha_{i_{1}}^{\vee}(v_{m^{\prime}-2}(\alpha_{1}))\cdot c_{v_{r-1},v_{t-1}}^{v_{m^{\prime}-2}}+c_{u_{r-2},u_{t}}^{u_{m^{\prime}-2}}+c_{u_{r},u_{t-2}}^{u_{m^{\prime}-2}}.

Inducting on
m^{\prime}
gives the formula

c^{u_{m^{\prime}}}_{u_{r},u_{t}}=\alpha_{i_{1}}^{\vee}(v_{m^{\prime}-2}(\alpha_{1}))\cdot D(r-1,t-1)+C(r-2,t)+C(t,r-2).

It can also be shown that

\alpha_{i_{1}}^{\vee}(v_{m^{\prime}-2}(\alpha_{1}))=\begin{cases}A_{m^{\prime}}-A_{m^{\prime}-2}&\text{if $m^{\prime}$ is odd}\\
B_{m^{\prime}}-B_{m^{\prime}-2}&\text{if $m^{\prime}$ is even.}\end{cases}

A technical calculation (details can be found in
[
BR15
]
) shows that the binomial
C(r,t)
satisfies the same recursion and
hence
c^{u_{m^{\prime}}}_{u_{r},u_{t}}=C(r,t)
.
∎

Example 4.17
.

We consider two examples of root systems with with the Weyl group of type
\mathrm{I}_{2}(m)
.
The first is the normalized root system with

a=b=\tau_{m}=2\cos(\pi/m)=\zeta+\zeta^{-1},

where
\zeta
denotes a
m
-th primitive root of unity.
A simple calculation shows that

A_{k}=B_{k}=[k]_{\zeta}:=\zeta^{k-1}+\zeta^{k-3}+\cdots+\zeta^{3-k}+\zeta^{1-k}=\tfrac{\zeta^{k}-\zeta^{-k}}{\zeta-\zeta^{-1}},

and

c^{u_{r+t}}_{u_{r},u_{t}}=c^{v_{r+t}}_{v_{r},v_{t}}={\tbinom{r+t}{r}}_{\zeta}:=\frac{[r+t]_{\zeta}!}{[r]_{\zeta}!\cdot[t]_{\zeta}!},

where
[r]_{\zeta}!:=[r]_{\zeta}[r-1]_{\zeta}\cdots[1]_{\zeta}
.
Note that
[m]_{\zeta}=0
and hence
c^{u_{r+t}}_{u_{r},u_{t}}=0
if
r+t\geq m
.
The minimal polynomial of
\tau_{m}
can be used to given an alternate expression of the sequence
\{A_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
.
For example, if
m=7
, then
x^{3}-x^{2}-2x+1
is the minimal polynomial of
\tau_{7}
and the sequence
\{A_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
is

(0,1,\tau_{7},\tau_{7}^{2}-1,\tau_{7}^{2}-1,\tau_{7},1,0).

If we treat
\zeta
as an indeterminant, then
\displaystyle{\tbinom{r+t}{r}}_{\zeta}
is the Gaussian binomial
used in the study of quantum groups and
the Gaussian integers
[k]_{\zeta}
can be viewed as the characters of irreducible
\SL_{2}(\mathbb{C})
-representations.
Also note that if we set
\zeta=1
, then we recover classical binomial coefficients.
When
\zeta=1
, the corresponding Cartan matrix is for the infinite root system of the affine Lie group
\widehat{\SL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
.

Next, we consider the polarized root system with Weyl group
\mathrm{I}_{2}(m)
where
m
is even or
m=3
.
For the polarized root system, we set

a=\tau_{m}^{2}=(\zeta+\zeta^{-1})^{2}\quad\text{and}\quad b=1.

Here we have

A_{k}=\begin{cases}[k]_{\zeta}&\text{if $k$ is odd}\\
[2]_{\zeta}\cdot[k]_{\zeta}&\text{if $k$ is even}\end{cases}\qquad\text{and}\qquad B_{k}=\begin{cases}[k]_{\zeta}&\text{if $k$ is odd}\\
[2]_{\zeta}^{-1}\cdot[k]_{\zeta}&\text{if $k$ is even.}\end{cases}

Theorem
4.16
implies

\displaystyle c^{u_{r+t}}_{u_{r},u_{t}}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle[2]_{\zeta}\cdot{\tbinom{r+t}{r}}_{\zeta}&\text{if $r,t$ are both odd,}\\
\displaystyle{\tbinom{r+t}{r}}_{\zeta}&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

and

c^{v_{r+t}}_{v_{r},v_{t}}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle[2]_{\zeta}^{-1}\cdot{\tbinom{r+t}{r}}_{\zeta}&\text{if $r,t$ are both odd,}\\
\displaystyle{\tbinom{r+t}{r}}_{\zeta}&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Note that polarized root systems include crystallogrpahic root systems of types
\mathrm{A}_{2}
,
\mathrm{B}_{2}
and
\mathrm{G}_{2}
.
These cases correspond to taking
m=3,4,
and
6
respectively
(we can also recover
\mathrm{A}_{1}\times\mathrm{A}_{1}
if we allow
a=b=0
with
m=2
).
For example, the crystallographic root system of type
\mathrm{G}_{2}
(
m=6
) has
a=[2]_{\zeta}^{2}=3
and
b=1
.
In this case, the sequences
\{A_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
and
\{B_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
are respectively given by

(0,1,3,2,3,1,0)\quad\text{and}\quad(0,1,1,2,1,1,0).

Using these values, we compute augmented coefficients using Theorem
4.16
.
For example:

c^{u_{4}}_{u_{1},u_{3}}=\tfrac{3\cdot 2\cdot 3\cdot 1}{(1)(2\cdot 3\cdot 1)}=3\quad\text{and}\quad c^{v_{5}}_{v_{3},v_{2}}=\tfrac{1\cdot 1\cdot 2\cdot 1\cdot 1}{(2\cdot 1\cdot 1)(1\cdot 1)}=1.

If we consider the normalized root system of type
\mathrm{G}_{2}
, then
a=b=\sqrt{3}
and the sequence
A_{k}=B_{k}
is given by

(0,1,\sqrt{3},2,\sqrt{3},1,0)

and the coefficient

c^{u_{4}}_{u_{1},u_{3}}=\tfrac{\sqrt{3}\cdot 2\cdot\sqrt{3}\cdot 1}{(1)(2\cdot\sqrt{3}\cdot 1)}=\sqrt{3}.

Note that the polarized (crystallographic) and normalized coefficients of
c^{u_{4}}_{u_{1},u_{3}}
are off by a scalar of
[2]_{\zeta}=\sqrt{3}
.

5.
Dual twisted group algebras and nil-Hecke rings

In this section, we study duals of the twisted group algebra, nil-Hecke ring and its connections to Schubert calculus.

5.1.
The group basis and the Schubert basis

The localized ring
Q
is a commutative unital ring and
Q_{W}
is a free left
Q
-module of finite rank.
Furthermore,
Q_{W}
is a
Q
-co-algebra,
with linear co-product
{\mathit{\Delta}}\colon Q_{W}\to Q_{W}\otimes_{Q}Q_{W}
and counit
\epsilon\colon Q_{W}\to Q
.
Then the
Q
-linear dual

Q^{*}_{W}:=\Hom_{Q}(Q_{W},Q)

has a natural structure of
Q
-algebra with unit, given by dualizing the co-product and using the natural isomorphism

Q_{W}^{*}\otimes_{Q}Q_{W}^{*}\simeq(Q_{W}\otimes_{Q}Q_{W})^{*}
, and dualizing the co-unit,
(i.e,
\epsilon\colon Q_{W}\to Q
seen as an element of
Q_{W}^{*}
is the unit).
The algebra
Q_{W}^{*}
is commutative if and only if
Q_{W}
is co-commutative.
As a
Q
-vector space, addition and scaling on
Q_{W}^{*}
is the usual point-wise operations

(f+g)(\delta_{w})=f(\delta_{w})+g(\delta_{w}),\quad\text{and}\quad(qf)(\delta_{w})=q(f(\delta_{w}))

for
f,g\in Q_{W}^{*}
,
w\in W
and
q\in Q
. The multiplication induced by
\mathit{\Delta}
is given by

(f\cdot g)(\delta_{w})=f(\delta_{w})g(\delta_{w}).

Let
\{\psi_{w}\}_{w\in W}
and
\{\xi_{w}\}_{w\in W}
denote the Kronecker dual bases to

\{\delta_{w}\}_{w\in W}
and
\{Y_{w}\}_{w\in W}
respectively. In other words:

\psi_{w}(\delta_{v})=\xi_{w}(Y_{v}):=\delta_{w,v}.

We call
\{\psi_{w}\}_{w\in W}
the
group basis
and
\{\xi_{w}\}_{w\in W}
the
Schubert basis
of
Q_{W}^{*}
.
Using the group basis, the product structure can be interpreted as the component-wise multiplication

(9)

\Big(\sum_{w\in W}q_{w}\psi_{w}\Big)\cdot\Big(\sum_{w\in W}q_{w}^{\prime}\psi_{w}\Big)=\sum_{w\in W}q_{w}q_{w}^{\prime}\psi_{w}.

In terms of these bases, the unit in
Q_{W}^{*}
is given by

\xi_{e}=\sum_{w\in W}\psi_{w}.

5.2.
Transformation matrices and coefficients

We define the coefficients
d_{w,v}
by writing
\xi_{w}
in terms of the group basis

\xi_{w}=\sum_{v\in W}d_{w,v}\psi_{v}.

By definition we have

\xi_{w}(\delta_{v})=d_{w,v}.

We also define the analogous coefficients
c_{w,v}
by expanding

(10)

Y_{w}=\sum_{v\in W}c_{w,v}\delta_{v}

in the twisted group algebra
Q_{W}
.
By definition we have that

\delta_{w,v}=\xi_{w}(Y_{v})=\sum_{u\in W}c_{v,u}\xi_{w}(\delta_{u})=\sum_{u,u^{\prime}\in W}c_{v,u}d_{w,u^{\prime}}.

If we define matrices
C:=[c_{w,v}]_{w,v\in W}
and
D:=[d_{w,v}]_{w,v\in W}
, then the equation above translates to

D^{\mathrm{t}}=C^{-1}.

The following proposition appears in
[
KK86
]
and
[
Ku96
]
.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
w
,
v\in W
,
w=s_{i_{1}}\ldots s_{i_{r}}
be a reduced word, and let
\mathbf{i}:=(i_{1},\ldots,i_{r})
.
For any
k\leq r
and subsequence
\mathbf{j}=(j_{1},\ldots,j_{s})\subseteq\mathbf{i}
, let
(j_{1},\ldots,j_{t})
denote the prefix of
\mathbf{j}
that appears strictly before the entry
i_{k}
in
\mathbf{i}
and define

\beta_{k,\mathbf{j}}:=s_{j_{1}}\cdots s_{j_{t}}(\alpha_{i_{k}}).

Then

(11)

c_{w,v}=(-1)^{\ell(w)-\ell(v)}\sum_{\mathbf{j}\subseteq\mathbf{i}}\Big(\prod_{k\in\mathbf{i}}\beta_{k,\mathbf{j}}^{-1}\Big),

where the sum is taken over all subsequences
\mathbf{j}=(j_{1},\ldots,j_{s})\subseteq\mathbf{i}
for which

v=s_{j_{1}}\ldots s_{j_{s}}
(not necessarily reduced).

In particular, the following properties hold:

(1)

If
v\nleq w
, then
c_{w,v}=d_{v,w}=0
,

(2)

\displaystyle c_{w,w}^{-1}=d_{w,w}=\beta_{1,\mathbf{i}}\cdots\beta_{r,\mathbf{i}}=\prod_{\alpha\in\Phi^{+}\cap w\Phi^{-}}\alpha
, and

(3)

d_{e,v}=1
.

Proof.

Let
w=s_{i_{1}}\ldots s_{i_{r}}
be a reduced expression.
Then expanding
Y_{w}
gives

\displaystyle Y_{w}=Y_{i_{1}}\ldots Y_{i_{r}}=\tfrac{1}{\alpha_{i_{1}}}(\delta_{s_{i_{1}}}-\delta_{e})\ldots\tfrac{1}{\alpha_{i_{r}}}(\delta_{s_{i_{r}}}-\delta_{e}).

For any subset
\mathbf{j}=(j_{1},\ldots,j_{s})\subseteq\mathbf{i}
the coefficient of
\delta_{s_{j_{1}}}\ldots\delta_{s_{j_{s}}}
in the expansion of
Y_{w}
is

(-1)^{\ell(w)-s}\prod_{k\in\mathbf{i}}\beta_{k,\mathbf{j}}^{-1}.

If
v=s_{j_{1}}\ldots s_{j_{s}}
, then
\ell(v)=s\hskip-5.0pt\mod 2
which proves the first part of the proposition.
For the additional properties, note that the matrix
C
is lower triangular with respect to the Bruhat order.
Parts (1) and (2) now follow from the fact that
D^{\mathrm{t}}=C^{-1}
and
applying the formula given in Equation (
11
) to
c_{w,w}
.
To prove part (3), note that

\xi_{e}=\sum_{v\in W}\psi_{v}

is the identity element in
Q_{W}^{*}
and, therefore,
d_{e,v}=1
for all
v\in W
.
∎

For an example of Equation (
11
), see Example
6.9
. We will later prove an analogous formula for the coefficient
d_{w,v}
similar to Equation (
11
).
The coefficients above can be used to express the products of group basis elements with Schubert basis elements.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
u,v\in W
. Then

\psi_{u}\cdot\xi_{v}=\sum_{w\in W}(c_{w,u}\cdot d_{v,u})\,\xi_{w}.

Proof.

Let

\psi_{u}\cdot\xi_{v}=\sum_{w\in W}b_{u,v}^{w}\,\xi_{w}

for some coefficients
b_{u,v}^{w}\in Q
and note that
b_{u,v}^{w}=\psi_{u}\cdot\xi_{v}(Y_{w})
.
If we write
\displaystyle Y_{w}=\sum_{w^{\prime}\in W}c_{w,w^{\prime}}\delta_{w^{\prime}}
, then we get

b_{u,v}^{w}=\psi_{u}\cdot\xi_{v}\Big(\sum_{w^{\prime}\in W}c_{w,w^{\prime}}\delta_{w^{\prime}}\Big)=\sum_{w^{\prime}\in W}c_{w,w^{\prime}}\psi_{u}(\delta_{w^{\prime}})\cdot\xi_{v}(\delta_{w^{\prime}})=c_{w,u}\cdot d_{v,u}.\qed

5.3.
The Hecke action

Next, we define an action of
Q_{W}
on
Q_{W}^{*}
called the Hecke action.
This action plays an important role in understanding the structure of the dual
Q_{W}^{*}
. Many results from this subsection also appear in
[
Ku02
, Chapter 11]
.

Definition 5.3
.

We define the
Hecke action
of
Q_{W}
on
Q_{W}^{*}
by

q\delta_{v}\bullet f(\delta_{w}):=f(\delta_{w}(q\delta_{v}))=f(w(q)\delta_{wv})=w(q)f(\delta_{wv}).

For the group basis
\{\psi_{w}\}_{w\in W}
it can be shown that

(12)

q\delta_{v}\bullet\psi_{w}=wv^{-1}(q)\psi_{wv^{-1}}

and

(13)

Y_{i}\bullet\psi_{w}=\frac{1}{w(\alpha_{i})}(\psi_{ws_{i}}-\psi_{w}).

In the next two lemmas we show how
Q_{W}
acts on the Schubert basis of
Q_{W}^{*}
.

Lemma 5.4
.

For any
v,w\in W
, we have

Y_{v}\bullet\xi_{w}=\begin{cases}\xi_{wv^{-1}}&\text{if $\ell(w)=\ell(v)+\ell(wv^{-1})$}\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Proof.

Observe that the Hecke action of push-pull elements on the Schubert basis gives

(Y_{i}\bullet\xi_{w})(Y_{u})=\xi_{w}(Y_{u}Y_{i})=\begin{cases}1&\text{if $w=us_{i}$ with $\ell(w)=\ell(u)+1$}\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

and hence

(14)

Y_{i}\bullet\xi_{w}=\begin{cases}\xi_{ws_{i}}&\text{if $s_{i}\in D_{R}(w)$}\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

If
v=s_{i_{1}}\cdots s_{i_{r}}
is a reduced word,
then applying formula (
14
) to
Y_{v}:=Y_{i_{1}}\cdots Y_{i_{r}}
proves the lemma.
∎

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
p\in\Lambda
and
w\in W
. Then

(15)

(p\delta_{e})\bullet\xi_{w}=w(p)\xi_{w}-\sum_{w\xrightarrow{\beta}v}\beta^{\vee}(p)\,\xi_{v}.

Furthermore, for any
1\leq i\leq n
, we have

(\delta_{s_{i}})\bullet\xi_{w}=\begin{cases}\xi_{w}-w(\alpha_{i})\xi_{ws_{i}}-\sum_{ws_{i}\xrightarrow{\beta}v}\beta^{\vee}(\alpha_{i})\,\xi_{v}&\text{if $s_{i}\in D_{R}(w)$},\\
\xi_{w}&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Proof.

For the first part, write

(p\delta_{e})\bullet\xi_{w}=\sum_{v\in W}c_{v}\xi_{v}

for some coefficients
c_{v}\in Q
. By duality of bases and relation (
5
), we have

\displaystyle c_{v}=(p\delta_{e}\bullet\xi_{w})(Y_{v})

\displaystyle=\xi_{w}(Y_{v}p)

\displaystyle=\xi_{w}(v(p)\,Y_{v}-\sum_{v^{\prime}\xrightarrow{\beta}v}\beta^{\vee}(p)\,Y_{v^{\prime}}).

This implies

c_{v}=\begin{cases}w(p)&\text{if $v=w$},\\
\beta^{\vee}(p)&\text{if $w\xrightarrow{\beta}v$},\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

proving formula (
15
).
As for the second formula note that
\delta_{s_{i}}=\alpha_{i}Y_{i}+\delta_{e}
and, hence,

\delta_{s_{i}}\bullet\xi_{w}=\xi_{w}+(\alpha_{i}Y_{i})\bullet\xi_{w}=\xi_{w}+\alpha_{i}\delta_{e}\bullet(Y_{i}\bullet\xi_{w}).

The second part of the lemma follows
from formulas (
14
) and (
15
).
∎

Proposition 5.6
.

The local coefficients
d_{w,v}
satisfy the recursive formula

d_{w,vs_{i}}-d_{w,v}=\begin{cases}v(\alpha_{i})d_{ws_{i},v}&\text{if $s_{i}\in D_{R}(w)$},\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Furthermore, we have

d_{s_{i},v}=\omega_{i}-v(\omega_{i})

where
\omega_{i}
denotes the
i
-th fundamental weight.
In particular, if
w\leq v
, then the local coefficient
d_{w,v}
is a homogeneous polynomial in the simple roots

\{\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}\}
of degree
\ell(w)
.

Proof.

First note that
d_{e,v}=1
for all
v\in W
and thus the proposition is true for
w=e
.
Suppose that
\ell(w)\geq 1
.
Formula (
13
) implies that the Hecke action

Y_{i}\bullet\xi_{w}=\sum_{v\in W}d_{w,v}\,(Y_{i}\bullet\psi_{v})=\sum_{v\in W}\frac{d_{w,v}}{v(\alpha_{i})}\,(\psi_{vs_{i}}-\psi_{v}).

The recursive formula for
d_{w,v}
now follows from Lemma
5.4
.

We prove the second part of the proposition by induction.
First note that
d_{s_{i},e}=0
by Proposition
5.1
part (1).
Also note that simple reflections act on their corresponding fundamental weights by
s_{i}(\omega_{i})=\omega_{i}-\alpha_{i}
.
Now assume
\ell(v)\geq 1
and suppose that
s_{i}\in D_{R}(v)
.
Then the recursive formula gives

\displaystyle d_{s_{i},v}=d_{s_{i},vs_{i}}-v(\alpha_{i})d_{e,v}

\displaystyle=(\omega_{i}-vs_{i}(\omega_{i}))-v(\alpha_{i})

\displaystyle=\omega_{i}-v(s_{i}(\omega_{i})+\alpha_{i})=\omega_{i}-v(\omega_{i}).

Otherwise, if
s_{i}\notin D_{R}(v)
, then again by Proposition
5.1
part (4), we have

\displaystyle d_{s_{i},vs_{i}}=d_{s_{i},v}+v(\alpha_{i})d_{e,v}

\displaystyle=(\omega_{i}-v(\omega_{i}))+v(\alpha_{i})

\displaystyle=\omega_{i}-v(\omega_{i}-\alpha_{i})=\omega_{i}-vs_{i}(\omega_{i}).

Finally, the fact that
d_{w,v}
are homogeneous polynomials in the simple roots of degree
\ell(w)
follows inductively
from the recursive formula and that
d_{e,v}=1
for all
v\in W
.
∎

5.4.
Dual nil-Hecke ring

In this section we focus on the dual to the nil-Hecke ring and
give several formulas for the structure constants with respect to the Schubert basis.
Define
\mathfrak{N}^{*}
the
S
-submodule of
Q_{W}^{*}
generated by the Schubert basis

\{\xi_{w}\}_{w\in W}
.
It is easy to see that

\mathfrak{N}^{*}=\Hom_{S}(\mathfrak{N},S)

is the
S
-linear dual to the nil-Hecke ring
\mathfrak{N}
.
Theorem
4.2
implies that

(16)

\xi_{u}\cdot\xi_{v}=\sum_{w\in W}p_{u,v}^{w}\,\xi_{w}

with all coefficients
p_{u,v}^{w}\in S
.
Hence
\mathfrak{N}^{*}
is an
S
-subalgebra of the
Q
-algebra
Q_{W}^{*}
.
Lemma
5.4
implies that
\mathfrak{N}^{*}
is stable under the Hecke action restricted to the nil-Hecke ring
\mathfrak{N}\subset Q_{W}
.
It is easy to check that the twisted Leibniz rule from Lemma
3.14

translates to the following analogous property on Hecke action

(17)

Y_{i}\bullet(f\cdot g)=(Y_{i}\bullet f)\cdot g+(\delta_{s_{i}}\bullet f)\cdot(Y_{i}\bullet g)

Using the twisted Leibniz rule, we can prove the following formula for the structure coefficients
p_{u,v}^{w}
.

Theorem 5.7
.

Let
w\in W
and fix a reduced word
w=s_{i_{1}}\cdots s_{i_{r}}
, then for any
u,v\in W
we have

p_{u,v}^{w}=\sum_{(j_{1},\ldots,j_{s})}(Y_{i_{1}}\cdots\hat{Y}_{j_{1}}\cdots\hat{Y}_{j_{s}}\cdots Y_{i_{r}})\bullet\xi_{v}(\delta_{e})

where
\hat{Y}_{j}
means replace
Y_{j}
with
\delta_{s_{j}}
and
the sum is over all subsequences
(j_{1},\ldots,j_{s})
of
(i_{1},\ldots,i_{r})

for which
s_{j_{1}}\cdots s_{j_{r}}
is a reduced word of
u
.

Proof.

First note that

Next, we give a formula for

Theorem 5.10

Equivariant cohomology of the flag variety

In this section, we outline the classical connections between torus-equivariant singular cohomology of flag varieties and nil-Hecke rings following
[

.
For more details on the definition and properties of equivariant cohomology of flag varieties see
[

]
and

6.1.
The localization map

Let
G
be a split semi-simple linear algebraic group over a field
\mathds{k}
of characteristic
0
together with
a split maximal torus
T
and a Borel subgroup
B
containing
T
.
The (complete) flag variety of
G
is defined to be the quotient space
G/B
.
It is a smooth projective algebraic variety with a left
T
-action given by the usual group product.
The Weyl group
W:=N_{G}(T)/T
of
G
indexes the
B
-orbits of the flag variety in the Bruhat decomposition

G/B=\bigsqcup_{w\in W}BwB/B.

The
B
-orbits
BwB/B
are called the Schubert cells and their closures
\overline{BwB/B}
are called the Schubert varieties.

Consider the
T
-equivariant singular cohomology ring
\SC_{T}^{*}(G/B;\mathbb{Z})
of
G/B
with integer coefficients.
Its odd-degree part vanishes (since
G/B
is a cellular space),
and its even-degree part can be identified with the equivariant Chow ring
\CH_{T}^{*}(G/B)
(see
[
Br
]
).
Via this identification the coefficient ring gives the symmetric algebra:

\SC_{T}^{2*}(pt;\mathbb{Z})\simeq S=\Sym_{\mathbb{Z}}(\Lambda),

where
\Lambda
is the character group of
T
. For instance, if
G
is simply-connected,
then
\Lambda
is the weight lattice and
\SC_{T}^{2*}(pt;\mathbb{Z})\simeq S
(the isomorphism of graded rings)
is the polynomial ring in fundamental weights.
In what follows, we will always deal with the even-degree part
\SC^{2i}_{T}(G/B;\mathbb{Z})

which we denote by
\CH_{T}^{i}(G/B)
.

For each Schubert variety, we define the
T
-equivariant Schubert class

\sigma^{T}_{w}:=[\overline{BwB/B}]\in\CH_{T}^{*}(G/B).

It is well known that as
S
-modules:

\CH_{T}^{*}(G/B)\simeq S\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\left(\bigoplus_{w\in W}\mathbb{Z}\cdot\sigma^{T}_{w}\right).

Hence, the Schubert classes form a basis of
\CH_{T}^{*}(G/B)
as a
S
-module.
Each
B
-orbit contains a unique
T
-fixed point
wB/B
and, therefore, the Weyl group also indexes the fixed point set

(G/B)^{T}=\bigsqcup_{w\in W}\{wB/B\}.

The inclusion

\imath\colon(G/B)^{T}\hookrightarrow G/B

induces an injective ring homomorphism on
T
-equviarant cohomology

\imath^{*}\colon\CH_{T}^{*}(G/B)\to\CH_{T}^{*}((G/B)^{T})\simeq\bigoplus_{w\in W}S.

The induced map
i^{*}
is called the localization (moment) map on
T
-equivariant cohomology.
Recall that the dual to the nil-Hecke ring
\mathfrak{N}^{*}
is the
S
-submodule of

with Schubert basis
\{\xi_{w}\}_{w\in W}
.
We define a
S
-linear map

by mapping each Schubert basis element

Proposition
5.6
and Lemma

imply that

is well-defined.
In fact, it is not difficult to show that

is an injective
S
-algebra homomorphism.
Thus, the image of

identifies the dual of the nil-Hecke ring

as a

-subalgebra of

The image

, and thus there is a

-algebra isomorphism

In particular, the

Remark 6.5
.

It has been classically known for a long time that the augmented Schubert structure constants
c_{u,v}^{w}
are non-negative integers. The proof is geometric in nature and uses the intersection theory of flag varieties. In
[
Ga01
]
, Graham proved, using invariant cycles, that the
T
-equivariant Schubert structure constants
p_{u,v}^{w}
are non-negative in
S(\Lambda_{r})\subseteq S(\Lambda)
. In other words, he shows that
p_{u,v}^{w}
, as a polynomial in the simple roots, has non-negative integer coefficients. Later in
[
An07
]
, Anderson gave an alternate proof of this fact using intersection theory which is analogous to the classical geometric proof for augmented coefficients. It is an open question in algebraic combinatorics to find a combinatorial explanation for the positivity properties of the coefficients
c_{u,v}^{w}
and
p_{u,v}^{w}
. While the nil-Hecke ring provides many algebraic formulas for these coefficients, none have been shown to be combinatorially or algebraically positive outside a few exceptional cases.

6.4.
Smoothness criterion

We finish this section with a smoothness criteria for Schubert varieties derived from the nil-Hecke ring. This formula is proved by Kumar in
[
Ku96
]
. By definition the Schubert variety is the closure of a
B
-orbit in
G/B
and its boundary is given by the Bruhat partial order on
W
.

X(w):=\overline{BwB/B}=\bigsqcup_{v\leq w}BvB/B

In general, Schubert varieties are singular with its singular locus comprising of a union of
B
-orbits on the boundary of their open Schubert cell. To determine if a
B
-orbit is singular, it suffices to check if the corresponding
T
-fixed point within the orbit is singular. The singularity/smoothness of such points can be checked using the nil-Hecke ring. In fact, the nil-Hecke ring can also detect when a
T
-fixed point (and hence, a
B
-orbit) is rationally smooth. Recall the change of basis coefficients
c_{w,v}\in Q
defined in (
10
)
and for
v\leq w
, define the set

S(w,v):=\{\beta\in\Phi^{+}\ |\ s_{\beta}v\leq w\}.

The next proposition is proved in
[
Ku96
]
:

Proposition 6.6
.

For
v\leq w
, there exists a unique homogeneous polynomial
c^{\prime}_{w,v}\in S
of degree
|S(w,v)|-\ell(w)
such that

c_{w,v}=c^{\prime}_{w,v}\cdot(-1)^{\ell(w)-\ell(v)}\cdot\prod_{\beta\in S(w,v)}\beta^{-1}.

By Proposition
5.1
, if
v=w
, then
S(w,w)=\Phi^{+}\cap w\Phi^{-}
and
c^{\prime}_{w,w}=1
. The following theorem states Kumar’s criterion for smoothness of a
T
-fixed point of a Schubert variety.

Theorem 6.7
.

The following are true:

(i)

The point
vB
is a smooth in
X(w)
if and only if
c^{\prime}_{w,v}=1
.

(ii)

The point
vB
is a rationally smooth in
X(w)
if and only if
c^{\prime}_{w,y}
is a positive integer for all
v\leq y\leq w
.

We remark that if
c^{\prime}_{w,y}
is a constant, then it can be shown that it is in fact a positive integer. Since the singular locus of
X(w)
is closed, to check for global smoothness of
X(w)
, it suffices to check smoothness only at the point
eB
(note the same is not true for rational smoothness).

Kumar’s criterion readily extends to Schubert varieties of partial flags. Let
J\subseteq\mathcal{R}
denote a subset of simple generators and let
W_{J}\subseteq W
denote the Weyl subgroup generated by
J
. For any
v\in W^{J}
, let

X^{J}(v):=\overline{BvP/P}

denote the corresponding Schubert variety. The preimage of the map
\pi\colon G/B\to G/P_{J}
restricted to the
X^{J}(v)
is

\pi^{-1}(X^{J}(v))=X(vu_{0})

where
u_{0}
denotes the maximal element of
W_{J}
. Furthermore the projection
\pi
is a
P_{J}/B
-fiber bundle on
G/B
and hence, restricts to
P_{J}/B
-fiber bundle on
X(vu_{0})
with base
X^{J}(v)
. Since the fiber
P_{J}/B
is smooth, we get the following:

Corollary 6.8
.

Let
v\in W^{J}
. The following are equivalent:

(i)

The Schubert variety
X^{J}(v)
is smooth.

(ii)

The Schubert variety
X(vu_{0})
is smooth.

(iii)

The coefficient
c^{\prime}_{vu_{0},e}=1
.

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}+\alpha_{3}}{\alpha_{1}\,\alpha_{2}\,\alpha_{3}\,s_{2}(\alpha_{1})\,s_{2}(\alpha_{3})}

The set

Theorem

7.1.
Algebraic oriented cohomology and formal group laws

We first turn to generalized cohomology theories and formal group laws.

Let
\hh

oriented cohomology
theory (AOCT) introduced by Levine and Morel in
[
LM07

\hh
is a contravariant functor from the category of smooth quasi-projective varieties over a field
\mathds{k}
of characteristic

to the category of graded commutative rings which satisfies the axioms of
[

, Def.1.1.2]

The word ‘oriented’ here reflects the fact that there is a 1-1 correspondence between the Euler structures (characteristic classes) on
\hh

•

To each line bundle
\mathcal{L}
over a variety

one assigns the characteristic (Euler) class
c_{1}^{\hh}(\mathcal{L}):=s^{*}s_{*}(1_{X})\in\hh^{1}(X)
, where
s
is the zero-section of
\mathcal{L}
and
s^{*}
(resp.
s_{*}
) is the induced pull-back (resp. push-forward).

and the choices of a local parameter
x^{\hh}
(orientations):

•

Choose
x^{\hh}=c_{1}^{\hh}(\mathcal{O}(1))\in\hh^{1}(\mathbb{P}^{\infty})=R[[x]]^{(1)}
, where
R=\hh(pt)
is the graded coefficient ring. Observe that
x^{\hh}=a_{0}x+a_{1}x^{2}+\ldots
, where
a_{0}
is invertible in
R
and we assign
\deg a_{i}:=-i
.

Example 7.1
.

Basic examples of AOCTs are

(i)

The Chow theory (modulo rational equivalence)

\hh(X)=\CH(X)
over

[
LM07
, Ex.1.1.4]
.

(ii)

The graded
K
-theory
\hh(X)=\K(X)[t^{\pm 1}]
over the Lorentz polynomials

(here we assign
\deg t:=-1
and
\deg x:=0
for all

[
LM07
, Ex.1.1.5]
.

(iii)

The (graded) connective K-theory

of Cai
[

]
over the polynomial ring

.

A key property of any such theory is the Quillen formula
[

\mathcal{L}_{1}
,
\mathcal{L}_{2}
over

one has

c_{1}^{\hh}(\mathcal{L}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{L}_{2})=F(c_{1}^{\hh}(\mathcal{L}_{1}),c_{1}^{\hh}(\mathcal{L}_{2})),

where

is the associated
formal group law
over the coefficient ring
R
.

Recall that (e.g. see
[
La55

) a formal group law (FGL)

over a commutative unital ring

is a power series in two variables
F(x,y)\in R[[x,y]]
which is commutative
F(x,y)=F(y,x)

F(x,F(y,z))=F(F(x,y),z)
and has neutral element
F(x,0)=x
. By definition we have

F(x,y)=x+y+\sum_{i,j>0}a_{ij}x^{i}y^{j},\;\text{ for some coefficients }a_{ij}\in R.

To respect the grading on
\hh
we set
\deg a_{ij}=1-i-j
for the coefficients
a_{ij}
of

.

Example 7.2
.

For the Chow theory
F(x,y)=x+y
(
a_{ij}=0
for all
i,j
).

For both the graded
K
-theory and the connective
K
-theory it is

F(x,y)=x+y-txy

with
\deg t=-1
(
a_{11}=-t
and
a_{ij}=0
for all

).

The quotient of the polynomial ring in the variables
a_{ij}
s modulo relations imposed by the commutativity and associativity defines the Lazard ring
\mathbb{L}
.
The respective FGL over
\mathbb{L}
denoted
F_{U}
is the universal FGL in the following sense: for any FGL
F
over
R
there is a map
\mathbb{L}\to R
given by evaluating the coefficients

of

.

By the main result of
[

]
there is an algebraic oriented cohomology theory
\AC(-)

called algebraic cobordism which has the associated formal group law

over
\mathbb{L}=\AC(pt)
.
Given a FGL

over

\hh(-):=\AC(-)\otimes_{\mathbb{L}}R

(here

is the universal map introduced above).

Example 7.3
.

The Chow theory, the graded

-theory and the connective

[

]

Moreover, the connective

\scriptstyle{\mathbb{Z}[t]\hookrightarrow\mathbb{Z}[t^{\pm 1}]}

7.2.
Algebraic connective
K
-theory

We now recall the geometric definition and basic properties of the connective
K
-theory
\hh(-)=\CK(-)
following
[
HM21
]
.

Given a smooth algebraic variety
X
, for every integer
i
we denote by
\MM_{i}(X)

the abelian category of coherent
\mathcal{O}_{X}
-modules with dimension of support at most
i

\MM_{i}(X)=0
if
i<0

and
\MM_{i}(X)=\MM(X)
if
i\geq\dim X
. We then set the connective
K
-groups of

to be

\CK_{i}(X):=\image\big(\K(\MM_{i}(X))\to\K(\MM_{i+1}(X))\big),

where
\K(\MM_{i}(X))
denotes the Grothendieck group of the respective coherent
\mathcal{O}_{X}
-modules.

These groups are related to the Chow groups via the exact sequence

\CK_{i-1}(X)\xrightarrow{t}\CK_{i}(X)\to\CH_{i}(X)\to 0,

where
t
is the Bott homomorphism.
We can view the graded group
\CK(X)
as a module over the polynomial ring
\mathbb{Z}[t]
and there is an isomorphism of oriented theories

\CK(-)/t\CK(-)\simeq\CH(-).

As for the
K
-theory, there is an isomorphism of oriented theories

\CK(-)/(t-1)\CK(-)\simeq\K(-).

Recall that
\K(\MM(X))=\K(X)
, and there is a topological filtration on
\K(X)
defined by

\K(X)_{(i)}:=\image\big(\K(\MM_{i}(X))\to\K(X)\big).

The relation between the groups
\CK_{i}(X)
,
\CH_{i}(X)
and
\K(X)_{(i)}
is given by the following commutative diagram of surjective maps

\textstyle{\CK_{i}(X)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\K(X)_{(i)}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\CH_{i}(X)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\K(X)_{(i)}/\K(X)_{(i-1)}.}

7.3.
Connective group rings

Let
\Lambda
be a finitely generated free abelian group. Let
R=\mathbb{Z}[t]
be the graded polynomial ring in the variable
t
with
\deg t=-1
.
Consider the polynomial ring
R[x_{\lambda}\colon\lambda\in\Lambda]
in variables
x_{\lambda}

indexed by all non-zero elements of
\Lambda
(we assume
x_{0}=0
). It has the induced grading where
\deg x_{\lambda}=1
.

Let
I
be the ideal generated by all elements

x_{\alpha+\beta}-x_{\alpha}-x_{\beta}+tx_{\alpha}x_{\beta},\quad\alpha,\beta\in\Lambda.

The quotient

\mathcal{S}(\Lambda):=R[x_{\lambda}\colon\lambda\in\Lambda]/I

is the graded ring which we call a
connective group ring
. Indeed, this is a particular case of a more general construction of a formal group ring of
[
CPZ
, §2]
. Namely, the case corresponding to the formal group law
F
of the connective
K
-theory. For simplicity we write
\mathcal{S}
instead of
\mathcal{S}(\Lambda)
having in mind the corresponding lattice.

Remark 7.4
.

If we set
t:=0
, then
\mathcal{S}
turns into the symmetric algebra
S=\Sym_{R}(\Lambda)
of the previous sections (polynomial ring in a basis of
\Lambda
).
If we set
t=1
, then it turns into the usual group ring
R[\Lambda]
of
\Lambda
via
x_{\lambda}\mapsto 1-e^{-\lambda}
.
Hence, one may think of
\mathcal{S}
as a deformation between the polynomial ring
S
and the usual group ring
R[\Lambda]
of the lattice
\Lambda
.

Following
[
HM13
, §5]
given a free theory
\hh
with the associated FGL
F
over the coefficient ring
R=\hh(pt)
,
a split torus
T
and a smooth
T
-variety
X
over
\mathds{k}
, one may define the associated
T
-equivariant oriented cohomology
\hh_{T}(X)

as the limit of usual oriented cohomology taken over a certain system of
T
-representations (the so-called Borel construction).
We refer to
[
To99
,
EG98
,
Br97
]
for the definition and properties of the equivariant Chow theory and equivariant
K
-theory;
and to
[
KM22
]
for the equivariant connective
K
-theory.
All such
T
-equivariant theories satisfy the axioms of
[
CZZ2
, §2]
and, moreover,
the
T
-equivariant coefficient ring
\CK_{T}(pt)
of the connective
K
-theory is isomorphic to the connective group ring

\mathcal{S}(T^{*})
of the group of characters
T^{*}
of
T
:

\mathcal{S}(T^{*})\simeq\CK_{T}(pt).

Under this identification
x_{\lambda}
corresponds to the first characteristic class
c_{1}^{\CK}(\mathcal{L}_{\lambda})

of the associated
T
-equivariant line bundle
\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}
over
pt
.

We now extend most of the constructions and results of previous sections to the context of the connective
K
-theory and connective group rings following results of
[
CZZ
,
CZZ1
,
CZZ2
]
.

7.4.
The connective Hecke ring

As before let
G
be a split semisimple linear algebraic group
G
over a field
\mathds{k}
of characteristic
0
together with a a split maximal torus
T
and a Borel subgroup
B
containing
T
. Consider the associated finite crystallographic (semisimple) root datum
\Phi\hookrightarrow\Lambda^{\vee}
together with its subset of simple roots
\Delta
and the character lattice
\Lambda:=T^{*}
. The Weyl group
W
of
\Phi
acts on
T^{*}
and, hence, on the connective group ring
\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{S}(\Lambda)
. It is well-known that this action coincides with the natural action of
W
on the coefficient ring
\CK_{T}(pt)
.

Similarly to the definition of the twisted group algebra for
S

we define the twisted group algebra for
\mathcal{S}
to be the free left
\mathcal{S}
-module

\mathcal{S}_{W}:=\mathcal{S}\otimes_{R}R[W]

so that each element of
\mathcal{S}_{W}
can be written uniquely as a linear combination
\sum_{w\in W}p_{w}\delta_{w}
, where
\{\delta_{w}\}_{w\in W}
is the standard basis of the group ring
R[W]
and
p_{w}\in\mathcal{S}
.
It has the product induced by that of
R[W]
and the twisted commuting relation
w(p)\delta_{w}=\delta_{w}p
,
p\in\mathcal{S}
.

Definition 7.5
.

We consider the localization
\mathcal{Q}:=\mathcal{S}(\tfrac{1}{x_{\alpha}},\;\alpha\in\Phi)
and define the
R
-subalgebra
\mathfrak{D}
of

\mathcal{Q}_{W}
to be generated by the divided-difference elements corresponding to simple reflections

X_{i}:=\tfrac{1}{x_{\alpha_{i}}}(\delta_{e}-\delta_{s_{i}}),\quad i=1\ldots n,

and multiplications by elements from
\mathcal{S}
. We call it the
connective Hecke ring
.

The connective Hecke ring satisfies the same braid relations as the nil-Hecke ring (see e.g.
[
BE90
]
).
But instead of the nilpotent relation it satisfies the idempotent-type relation

\displaystyle X_{i}^{2}

\displaystyle=\big(\tfrac{1}{x_{\alpha_{i}}}-\tfrac{1}{x_{\alpha_{i}}}\delta_{s_{i}}\big)\big(\tfrac{1}{x_{\alpha_{i}}}-\tfrac{1}{x_{\alpha_{i}}}\delta_{s_{i}}\big)

\displaystyle=\big(\tfrac{1}{x_{-\alpha_{i}}^{2}}+\tfrac{1}{x_{\alpha_{i}}x_{-\alpha_{i}}}\big)-\big(\tfrac{1}{x_{\alpha_{i}}^{2}}+\tfrac{1}{x_{\alpha_{i}}x_{-\alpha_{i}}}\big)\delta_{i}

\displaystyle=\big(\tfrac{1}{x_{\alpha_{i}}}+\tfrac{1}{x_{-\alpha_{i}}})X_{i}.

Since in the connective group ring we have

0=x_{\alpha_{i}+(-\alpha_{i})}=x_{\alpha_{i}}+x_{-\alpha_{i}}-tx_{\alpha_{i}}x_{-\alpha_{i}},

we obtain

\tfrac{1}{x_{\alpha_{i}}}+\tfrac{1}{x_{-\alpha_{i}}}=t\in\mathcal{Q}.

So the idempotent relation then becomes

X_{i}^{2}=tX_{i}.

As for the affine relation (Aff), we get

\displaystyle X_{i}x_{\lambda}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{x_{\alpha_{i}}}(\delta_{e}-\delta_{s_{i}})x_{\lambda}=\tfrac{x_{\lambda}}{x_{\alpha_{i}}}\delta_{e}-\tfrac{s_{i}(x_{\lambda})}{x_{\alpha_{i}}}\delta_{\alpha_{i}}

\displaystyle=s_{i}(x_{\lambda})\tfrac{1}{x_{\alpha_{i}}}(\delta_{e}-\delta_{\alpha_{i}})+\tfrac{x_{\lambda}}{x_{\alpha_{i}}}-\tfrac{s_{i}(x_{\lambda})}{x_{\alpha_{i}}}

\displaystyle=s_{i}(x_{\lambda})X_{i}+X_{i}\circ x_{\lambda},

where
X_{i}\circ x_{\lambda}:=\tfrac{x_{\lambda}-s_{i}(x_{\lambda})}{x_{\alpha_{i}}}
.

Set
k:=\alpha_{i}^{\vee}(\lambda)
. We then obtain

\displaystyle s_{i}(x_{\lambda})

\displaystyle=x_{s_{i}(\lambda)}=x_{\lambda-\alpha_{i}^{\vee}(\lambda)\alpha_{i}}

\displaystyle=x_{\lambda}+x_{-k\alpha_{i}}-tx_{\lambda}x_{-k\alpha_{i}}

\displaystyle=x_{\lambda}-x_{-k\alpha_{i}}(tx_{\lambda}-1).

Therefore, in
\mathcal{S}
we get

X_{i}\circ x_{\lambda}=\tfrac{x_{-k\alpha_{i}}}{x_{\alpha_{i}}}(tx_{\lambda}-1).

Observe that in the connective group ring
\mathcal{S}
for any integer
k
and
\beta\in\Lambda
we have

1-tx_{k\beta}=(1-tx_{\beta})^{k}.

This allows to compute the quotient
\tfrac{x_{-k\alpha_{i}}}{x_{\alpha_{i}}}
which will be again in
\mathcal{S}
.

Example 7.6
.

If
t=1
(the usual
K
-theory case), then in the group ring
\mathbb{Z}[\Lambda]
using the exponential notation (
x_{\lambda}\mapsto 1-e^{-\lambda}
) we get

\tfrac{x_{-k\alpha_{i}}}{x_{\alpha_{i}}}=\tfrac{1-e^{k\alpha_{i}}}{1-e^{-\alpha_{i}}}=-e^{\alpha_{i}}\tfrac{1-e^{k\alpha_{i}}}{1-e^{\alpha_{i}}},

which gives

X_{i}\circ x_{\lambda}=\tfrac{1-e^{k\alpha_{i}}}{1-e^{\alpha_{i}}}e^{\alpha_{i}-\lambda}.

Remark 7.7
.

The algebra
\mathfrak{D}
can be viewed as a generalization of the nil-Hecke ring
\mathfrak{N}
(as the latter can be obtained by setting
t:=0
in
\mathcal{S}
).
Moreover, if
t=1
, then it gives the classical 0-affine Hecke algebra.

7.5.
The coproduct

Following arguments of section
4
(see
[
CZZ
]
) one constructs the cocommutative coproduct
\mathit{\Delta}
on
\mathfrak{D}
so that
\mathit{\Delta}
turns the
\mathcal{S}
-linear dual
\mathfrak{D}^{*}:=\Hom_{\mathcal{S}}(\mathfrak{D},\mathcal{S})
into a commutative
\mathcal{S}
-algebra.

Given
w\in W
and its reduced expression

w=s_{i_{1}}s_{i_{2}}\ldots s_{i_{r}}

we set

X_{w}:=X_{i_{1}}\ldots X_{i_{r}}\in\mathcal{Q}_{W}.

We then obtain the following formulas (cf Theorem
4.2
) for the coproduct
\mathit{\Delta}(X_{w})
in terms of
X_{i}
s:

Theorem 7.8
.

For any
i\leq n
, we have

\mathit{\Delta}(X_{i})=X_{i}\otimes 1+1\otimes X_{i}-x_{\alpha_{i}}X_{i}\otimes X_{i},

For any
w\in W
and
s_{i}\in D_{L}(w)
, if we write

\mathit{\Delta}(X_{w})=\sum_{v,u\in W}p_{u,v}^{w}\ X_{u}\otimes X_{v},

then the coefficients satisfy the recursion

p_{u,v}^{w}=\begin{cases}X_{i}\circ(p_{u,v}^{s_{i}w})&\text{if $s_{i}\notin D_{L}(u)\cup D_{L}(v)$}\\
X_{i}\circ(p_{u,v}^{s_{i}w})+s_{i}(p_{s_{i}u,v}^{s_{i}w}+tp_{u,v}^{s_{i}w})&\text{if $s_{i}\in D_{L}(u)\setminus D_{L}(v)$}\\
X_{i}\circ(p_{u,v}^{s_{i}w})+s_{i}(p_{u,s_{i}v}^{s_{i}w}+tp_{u,v}^{s_{i}w})&\text{if $s_{i}\in D_{L}(v)\setminus D_{L}(u)$}\\
X_{i}\circ(p_{u,v}^{s_{i}w})+s_{i}(p_{s_{i}u,v}^{s_{i}w}+p_{u,s_{i}v}^{s_{i}w}+2tp_{u,v}^{s_{i}w})&\\
\qquad\qquad-x_{\alpha_{i}}s_{i}(p_{s_{i}u,s_{i}v}^{s_{i}w}+t^{2}p_{u,v}^{s_{i}w})&\text{if $s_{i}\in D_{L}(u)\cap D_{L}(v)$}\end{cases}

with
p_{e,e}^{e}=1
.

Proof.

We only prove the formulas for the coefficients.
As in the cohomology case consider
w\in W
with
\ell(w)\geq 2
, and let
w=s_{i}w^{\prime}
from some
s_{i}\in D_{L}(w)
.
Write

\mathit{\Delta}(X_{w^{\prime}})=\sum_{u,v}p_{u,v}^{w^{\prime}}\ X_{u}\otimes X_{v}

and assume for the sake of induction that
\mathit{\Delta}(X_{w^{\prime}})\in\mathfrak{D}\otimes_{\mathcal{S}}\mathfrak{D}
, or equivalently, each
p_{u,v}^{w^{\prime}}\in\mathcal{S}
.
Then

\displaystyle\mathit{\Delta}(X_{w})

\displaystyle=\mathit{\Delta}(X_{i})\odot\mathit{\Delta}(X_{w^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{x_{\alpha_{i}}}(\delta_{e}\otimes\delta_{e}-\delta_{s_{i}}\otimes\delta_{s_{i}})\odot\big(\sum_{u,v}p_{u,v}^{w^{\prime}}\ X_{u}\otimes X_{v}\big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{u,v}\ \tfrac{1}{x_{\alpha_{i}}}\big(p_{u,v}^{w^{\prime}}(X_{u}\otimes X_{v})-s_{i}(p_{u,v}^{w^{\prime}})\ (\delta_{s_{i}}X_{u}\otimes\delta_{s_{i}}X_{v})\big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{u,v}\ \tfrac{s_{i}(p_{u,v}^{w^{\prime}})}{x_{\alpha_{i}}}\Big(-(\delta_{e}-\delta_{s_{i}})X_{u}\otimes(\delta_{e}-\delta_{s_{i}})X_{v}+(\delta_{e}-\delta_{s_{i}})X_{u}\otimes X_{v}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+X_{u}\otimes(\delta_{e}-\delta_{s_{i}})X_{v}-X_{u}\otimes X_{u}\Big)+\tfrac{p_{u,v}^{w^{\prime}}}{x_{\alpha_{i}}}(X_{u}\otimes X_{u})

\displaystyle=\sum_{u,v}\ -x_{\alpha_{i}}\cdot s_{i}(p_{u,v}^{w^{\prime}})\ (X_{i}X_{u}\otimes X_{i}X_{v})+s_{i}(p_{u,v}^{w^{\prime}})\ (X_{i}X_{u}\otimes X_{v}+X_{u}\otimes X_{i}X_{v})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+X_{i}\circ p_{u,v}^{w^{\prime}}\ (X_{u}\otimes X_{v}).

Consider the product
X_{i}X_{z}
in
\mathfrak{D}
.
Suppose that
s_{i}\notin D_{L}(z)
. Then
s_{i}z
is reduced, and
X_{i}X_{z}=X_{s_{i}z}
in
\mathfrak{D}
. As in the cohomology case, this corresponds to
s_{i}\in D_{L}(u)
, where
u=s_{i}z
.
Suppose that
s_{i}\in D_{L}(z)
. Then
z=s_{i}z^{\prime}
with
\ell(z^{\prime})=\ell(z)-1
. So
X_{i}X_{z}=X_{i}^{2}X_{z^{\prime}}=tX_{i}X_{z^{\prime}}=tX_{z}
which corresponds to the case
s_{i}\in D_{L}(u)
, where
u=z
. The recursive formulas then follow.
∎

Corollary 7.9
.

We have the following properties of the coefficients:

(1)

If

u,v\leq w
and

(2)

\displaystyle p_{w,w}^{w}=(-1)^{\ell(w)}\prod_{\alpha\in\Phi^{+}\cap w(\Phi^{-})}x_{\alpha}

(3)

p_{w,e}^{w}=p_{e,w}^{w}=1

Example 7.10
.

Similarly to Example
4.4
we then obtain for

\displaystyle\mathit{\Delta}(X_{w})=x_{\alpha_{2}}s_{1}(x_{\alpha_{1}})X(w)

\displaystyle-s_{2}(x_{\alpha_{1}})X(w,s_{1})-x_{\alpha_{2}}X(w,s_{2})
