On semidefinite programming characterizations of the numerical radius and its dual norm
Shmuel Friedland, Chi-Kwong Li

Abstract
. We state and give self contained proofs of semidefinite programming characterizations of the numerical radius and its dual norm for matrices. We show that the computation of the numerical radius and its dual norm within ε \varepsilon precision are polynomially time computable in the data and | log ⁡ ε | |\log\varepsilon| using either the ellipsoid method or the short step, primal interior point method. We apply our results to give a simple formula for the spectral and nuclear norm of 2 × n × m 2\times n\times m real tensor in terms of the numerical radius and its dual norm.

Introduction

Let
\mathbb{C}^{n}

A\succeq B\,(A\succ B)

The numerical methods discussed in this paper utilizes

The aim of this paper is to discuss SDP characterizations of the numerical radius of

\displaystyle r(C)

\displaystyle=\max_{r(F)\leq 1}\Re\tr F^{*}C,

\displaystyle=\max_{r^{\vee}(F)\leq 1}\Re\tr F^{*}C.

2.
Conic and semidefinite programming

2.1.
Slater condition for a conic programming problem

In this subsection we recall some known results on conic programming we use in this paper, that can be found in
[
9
, Chapter 4]
.
We call
\mathrm{K}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
a
proper cone
if the following conditions hold:

\displaystyle\textrm{Closure}(\mathrm{K})=\mathrm{K},

\displaystyle a\mathrm{K}\subseteq\mathrm{K}\textrm{ for }a\geq 0,

\displaystyle\mathrm{K}+\mathrm{K}=\mathrm{K},

\displaystyle\mathrm{K}\cap-\mathrm{K}=\{\mathbf{0}\},

\displaystyle\mathrm{K}-\mathrm{K}=\mathbb{R}^{n}.

In the literature, such a closed convex set is also called a pointed generating cone.
Note that
K
has interior.
Assume
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
is an inner product on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Then

\mathrm{K}^{\vee}=\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\,\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\rangle\geq 0,\textrm{ for }\mathbf{y}\in\mathrm{K}\}

is the dual cone of
\mathrm{K}
.

Let
L\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
, and view
L
as a linear transformation
L:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
.
In
\mathbb{R}^{m}
we assume that we have the standard inner product
\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\rangle=\mathbf{u}^{\top}\mathbf{v}
. Denote by
L^{\vee}:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
the linear transformation
satisfying:

(2.1)

\langle L^{\vee}\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x}\rangle=\mathbf{y}^{\top}L\mathbf{x}\textrm{ for all }\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}.

A conic programming problem in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is:

(2.2)

val=\inf\{\langle\mathbf{c},\mathbf{x}\rangle:\,L\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b},\mathbf{x}\in\mathrm{K}\},\quad\mathbf{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}

We say that (
2.2
) is feasible (infeasible).
if the set
\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathrm{K},L\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}\}
is nonempty (empty). If (
2.2
) is infeasible
then
val=\infty
.

The dual conic problem to (
2.2
) is:

(2.3)

val^{\vee}=\sup\{\mathbf{b}^{\top}\mathbf{y}:\,\mathbf{c}-L^{\vee}\mathbf{y}\in\mathrm{K}^{\vee}\}

If the dual conic problem is infeasible, we let
val^{\vee}=-\infty
.

It is straightforward to show the weak duality inequality
val\geq val^{\vee}
.
The Slater constraint condition is
[
9
, Theorem 4.7.1]
:

Theorem 2.1
.

Assume that the feasible set of (
2.2
) contains an interior point of the cone
\mathrm{K}
, and
val
is finite.
Then the dual problem (
2.3
) is feasible, and
val=val^{\vee}
.

We now make the following observations. Let us consider the linear system

(2.4)

L\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b},\quad\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}.

Use Gauss elimination to deduce that either the above system in not solvable, hence
(
2.2
) is unfeasible, or the affine set of solutions to the above system is

\{\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{x}_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{k}s_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}\},\,\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{k}\textrm{ linearly independent in }\mathbb{R}^{n},k\in[n]\cup\{0\}.

Here
k
is the dimension of the affine space given by (
2.4
).
Note we can assume that either
\mathbf{x}_{0}=\mathbf{0}
, or
\mathbf{x}_{0},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{k}
are linearly independent.
In particular, we can assume that

\langle\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{j}\rangle=\delta_{ij},i,j\in[k],\quad\langle\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{x}_{i}\rangle=0,i\in[k].

We restrict our discussion to the case that the system (
2.4
) is solvable, and corank
L=n-\rank L=k
.
In that case, the conic programming problem (
2.2
) is

(2.5)

val=\inf\left\{\langle\mathbf{c},\mathbf{x}\rangle:\,\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{x}_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{k}s_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}\in\mathrm{K},\mathbf{s}=(s_{1},\ldots,s_{k})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}\right\}.

To give a corresponding simple representation of the dual problem, complete the orthonormal set
\mathbf{x}_{,}\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{k}

to an orthonormal basis
\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{n}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Then it is straightforward to show that the dual problem to (
2.5
) is

(2.6)

\displaystyle val^{\vee}=\sup\left\{\langle\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{u}\rangle:\,\mathbf{u}=\sum_{j=1}^{n-k}t_{j}\mathbf{x}_{j+k},\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\left.\mathbf{c}-\mathbf{u}\in\mathrm{K}^{\vee},(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n-k})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n-k}\right\}.

2.2.
Semidefinite programming for real and complex matrices

Let
\mathbb{F}
be the field of real
\mathbb{R}
or complex numbers
\mathbb{C}
. Denote by
\mathrm{H}_{n}(\mathbb{F})
the real space of selfadjoint matrices
\{A\in\mathbb{F}^{n\times n},A^{*}=A\}
. Thus,
\mathrm{H}_{n}(\mathbb{C})=\mathrm{H}_{n}
, and
\mathrm{H}_{n}(\mathbb{R})=\mathrm{S}_{n}
-the space of real symmetric matrices of order
n
.
Clearly,
\mathrm{H}_{n}(\mathbb{R})\sim\mathbb{R}^{n(n+1)/2}
and
\mathrm{H}_{n}\sim\mathbb{R}^{n^{2}}
.
The inner product in
\mathrm{H}_{n}(\mathbb{F})
is
\langle A,B\rangle=\tr AB
. Thus
\|A\|_{F}=\sqrt{\langle A,A\rangle}
is the Frobenius norm of
A
. For
X_{0}\in\mathrm{H}_{n}(\mathbb{F})
and
r\geq 0
denote by
\mathrm{B}(X_{0},r)=\{X\in\mathrm{H}_{n}(\mathbb{F}),\|X-X_{0}\|_{F}\leq r\}
the closed ball in
\mathrm{H}_{n}(\mathbb{F})
centered at
X_{0}
with radius
r
.

Let
\mathrm{K}=\mathrm{H}_{n,+}(\mathbb{F})
-the cone of positive semidefinite matrices in
\mathrm{H}_{n}(\mathbb{F})
.
Recall that
\mathrm{K}^{\vee}=\mathrm{K}
. A conic programming problem discussed in subsection
2.1
is called a semidefinite programming problem.

A standard semidefinite program is:

(2.7)

val=\inf\left\{\langle A_{0},X\rangle:\,X\in\mathrm{H}_{n,+}(\mathbb{F}),\langle A_{j},X\rangle=b_{j},j\in[m]\right\},

where
A_{0},A_{j}\in\mathrm{H}_{n}(\mathbb{F}),b_{j}\in\mathbb{R}
for
j\in[m]
.
Denote by
\mathcal{F}
the feasible set

(2.8)

\mathcal{F}=\{X\in\mathrm{H}_{n,+}(\mathbb{F}):\,\langle A_{j},X\rangle=b_{j},j\in[m]\}.

Let

\mathrm{L}(A_{1},\ldots,A_{m},b_{1},\ldots,b_{m})=\{X\in\mathrm{H}_{n}(\mathbb{F}):\,\langle A_{j},X\rangle=b_{j},j\in[m]\}.

Then
\mathrm{L}(A_{1},\ldots,A_{m},b_{1},\ldots,b_{m})
is an affine subspace whose dimension is

k\in\{-1,0\}\cup[\dim\mathrm{H}_{n}(\mathbb{F})]
. So
k=-1
if and only if
\mathrm{L}(A_{1},\ldots,A_{m},b_{1},\ldots,b_{m})=\emptyset
, and
k\geq 0
if
\mathrm{L}(A_{1},\ldots,A_{m},b_{1},\ldots,b_{m})=X_{0}+\mathbf{U}
where

\mathbf{U}=\mathrm{L}(A_{1},\ldots,A_{m},0,\ldots,0)\subset\mathrm{H}_{n}(\mathbb{F})

is a subspace of dimension
k
.

By introducing a standard basis in
\mathrm{H}_{n}(\mathbb{F})
one can use Gauss elimination to determine
the dimension
d
of
\mathrm{L}(A_{1},\ldots,A_{m},b_{1},\ldots,b_{m})
. In particular, if
d=\dim\mathrm{H}_{n}(\mathbb{F})-\delta\geq 0
, then
m\geq\delta
, and there exists a subset
\{b_{i_{1}},\ldots,b_{i_{\delta}}\}
for some
\{1\leq i_{1}<\ldots<i_{\delta}\}\subset[m]
such that

(2.9)

\displaystyle\mathrm{L}(A_{1},\ldots,A_{m},b_{1},\ldots,b_{m})=\mathrm{L}(A_{i_{1}},\ldots,A_{i_{\delta}},b_{i_{1}},\ldots,b_{i_{\delta}}),

\displaystyle\dim\mathrm{L}(A_{1},\ldots,A_{m},b_{1},\ldots,b_{m})=d=\dim\mathrm{H}_{n}(\mathbb{F})-\delta\geq 0.

As explained in subsection
2.1
we can assume that a standard SDP problem is of the form
[
27
, Eq. (1)]
:

(2.10)

\displaystyle val=\inf\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{k}c_{i}s_{i}:\,X_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{k}s_{i}X_{i}\succeq 0,\hskip 57.81621pt\ \qquad\right.

\displaystyle\hskip 57.81621pt\ \left.X_{0},\ldots,X_{k}\in\mathrm{H}_{n}(\mathbb{F}),(s_{1},\ldots,s_{k})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}\right\},

(Here,
X_{0},\ldots,X_{k}
are fixed matrices.)
Then the dual problem is of the form
[
27
, Eq. (27)]
:

(2.11)

val^{\vee}=\sup\{-\tr X_{0}Z:\,\tr X_{i}Z=c_{i},i\in[k],Z\in\mathrm{H}_{n,+}(\mathbb{F})\}.

Theorem
2.1
yields

Corollary 2.2
.

Assume that the feasible set of (
2.10
) contains a positive definite matrix, and
val
is finite. Then the dual problem (
2.11
) is feasible, and
val=val^{\vee}
.

2.3.
Complexity results for semidefinite programming

We first recall the known complexity results for real SDP.
Assume that
A_{i},b_{i}
for
i\in[m]
have rational entries. Then the problem of

(1) finding if
\mathrm{L}(A_{1},\ldots,A_{m},b_{1},\ldots,b_{m})=\emptyset
, or otherwise

(2) finding the set
\{b_{i_{1}},\ldots,b_{i_{\delta}}\}
that satisfies (
2.9
)

has a simple polynomial complexity
[
1
, Sec. 4]
:
O(n^{2}m^{4}H)
. Here
H
is the maximal number of bits needed for each entry of
A_{j}
and
b_{j}
. (We ignored here the logarithmic factors.)

Without loss of generality we can assume that
0\leq m\leq n(n+1)/2-1
, and
\dim\mathrm{L}(A_{1},\ldots,A_{m},b_{1},\ldots,b_{m})=n(n+1)/2-m
.

A polynomial-time bit complexity (Turing complexity) of the SDP problem (
2.7
) for
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{R}

using the ellipsoid method was shown by Grötschel-Lovász-Schrijver, stated implicitly in
[
10
, Section 6]
. It used the ellipsoid method of Yudin-Nemirovski
[
29
]
, and was inspired by the earlier proof of Khachiyan
[
17
]
of the polynomial-time solvability of linear programming.

Let
\mathbb{Z}
,
\mathbb{Q}
,
\mathbb{Z}[\mathbf{i}]=\mathbb{Z}+\mathbf{i}\mathbb{Z}
, and
\mathbb{Q}[\mathbf{i}]
the the sets of integers, rationals, Gaussian integers and rationals respectively.
The explicit statement of Grötschel-Lovász-Schrijve result is given by
de Klerk-Vallentin
[
4
, Theorem 1.1]
:

Theorem 2.3
.

Consider the SDP problem (
2.7
) for
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{R}
.
Assume that
A_{j}\in\mathrm{H}_{n}(\mathbb{R})\cap\mathbb{Q}^{n\times n},b_{j}\in\mathbb{Q}
for
j\in[m]
, and

\dim\mathrm{L}(A_{1},\ldots,A_{m},b_{1},\ldots,b_{m})=n(n+1)/2-m\geq 1
.
Suppose that there exists
Y_{0}\in\mathrm{H}_{n,++}(\mathbb{R})\cap\mathbb{Q}^{n\times n}
in the feasible set
\mathcal{F}
given by (
2.8
) for
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{R}
and
0<r\leq R\in\mathbb{Q}
such that

(2.12)

\displaystyle\mathrm{L}(A_{1},\ldots,A_{m},b_{1},\ldots,b_{m})\cap\mathrm{B}(Y_{0},r)\subseteq\mathcal{F}

\displaystyle\subseteq\mathrm{L}(A_{1},\ldots,A_{m},b_{1},\ldots,b_{m})\cap\mathrm{B}(Y_{0},R).

Then for
A_{0}\in\mathrm{H}_{n}\cap\mathbb{Q}^{n\times n}
and a rational
\varepsilon>0
one can find
X^{\star}\in\mathcal{F}\cap\mathbb{Q}^{n\times n}
in poly-time using the ellipsoid method such that
\langle A_{0},X^{\star}\rangle-\varepsilon\leq val
, where the polynomial is in
n,\log R/r,|\log\varepsilon|
and the bit size of the data
Y_{0},A_{0},A_{1},\ldots,A_{m},b_{1},\ldots,b_{m}
.

The complexity of semidefinite programs using the ellipsoid method are discussed in
[
24
]
.
It was shown by Karmakar
[
16
]
that an IPM is superior to the ellipsoid method for linear programming problems.
The IPM are discussed in
[
23
]
and
[
26
]
. In particular, it is shown in
[

that the number of steps of the short step primal interior point method is

.
Here

is the complexity of the barrier describing
\mathcal{F}
.

To obtain the Turing complexity results for the above IPM one needs to estimate

,
and the bit complexity of each short step primal interior point method. To achieve that one needs to perform Diophantine
approximations. de Klerk-Vallentin
[

showed polynomial-time complexity for an SDP problem
using IPM method by applying the short step primal interior point method combined with Diophantine approximation under
the condition (

):

.

(de Klerk-Vallentin)
Let the assumptions of Theorem
2.3
hold.
Then for
A_{0}\in\mathrm{H}_{n}(\mathbb{R})\cap\mathbb{Q}^{n\times n}

one can find
X^{\star}\in\mathcal{F}\cap\mathbb{Q}^{n\times n}

, where the polynomial is in

We next observe that Theorem
2.4
extends to Hermitian matrices. Let
Z\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
and veiw

as a linear operator on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
:
\mathbf{z}\mapsto Z\mathbf{z}

Finally, for

3.
The dual norm of
r(\cdot)

The following lemma is well known to the experts, and we bring its proof for completeness.
We will let
\sigma_{1}(A)\geq\cdots\geq\sigma_{n}(A)
be the singular values of
A\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
,
and use the fact
that the dual norm
\|\cdot\|^{\vee}
of the spectral norm, known as the nuclear norm, is equal to

\|A\|^{\vee}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{i}(A)

[
5
, Corollary 7.5.13]
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
C\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
. Then

(a)

(3.1)

\displaystyle r(C)

\displaystyle=\max\{\Re\,e^{-\mathbf{i}\theta}\mathbf{x}^{*}C\mathbf{x}:\,\|\mathbf{x}\|=1,\theta\in[0,2\pi)\}

\displaystyle=\max\{\Re\tr C(e^{-\mathbf{i}\theta}\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{*}):\,\|\mathbf{x}\|=1,\theta\in[0,2\pi)\}.

(b)

The extreme points of the unit ball of the norm
r^{\vee}(\cdot)
is the set

(3.2)

\mathcal{E}=\{e^{-\mathbf{i}\theta}\mathbf{v}\mathbf{v}^{*}:\,\theta\in[0,2\pi),\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{C}^{n},\|\mathbf{v}\|=1\}.

(c)

(3.3)

r^{\vee}(C)=\min\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\|\mathbf{x}_{i}\|^{2}:\,C=\sum_{i=1}^{N}e^{-\mathbf{i}\theta_{i}}\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{*},\mathbf{x}_{i}\in\mathbb{C}^{n},\theta_{i}\in[0,2\pi)\right\},

where
N\leq 2n^{2}+1
.

(d)

(3.4)

\|C\|/2\leq r(C)\leq\|C\|,\quad\|C\|^{\vee}=\leq r^{\vee}(C)\leq 2\|C\|^{\vee}.

Proof.

(a) Assume that
z\in\mathbf{W}(C)
. Then
z=\mathbf{x}C\mathbf{x}^{*}
for some
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{C}^{n},\|\mathbf{x}\|=1
. Hence,

|z|=\max\{\Re\,e^{-\mathbf{i}\theta}\mathbf{x}^{*}C\mathbf{x}:\,\theta\in[0,2\pi)\}.

This proves (
3.1
).

(b)
Let
\mathcal{E}
be the set of the extreme points of the unit ball of
r^{\vee}(\cdot)

Clearly, the first characterization of
r(C)
in (
1.1
) can be replaced by
r(C)=\sup_{F\in\mathcal{E}}\Re\,F^{*}C
.
The second part of the characterization (
3.1
) yields that

\mathcal{E}\subseteq\{e^{-\mathbf{i}\theta}\mathbf{v}\mathbf{v}^{*}:\,\theta\in[0,2\pi),\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{C}^{n},\|\mathbf{v}\|=1\}.

Observe next that
