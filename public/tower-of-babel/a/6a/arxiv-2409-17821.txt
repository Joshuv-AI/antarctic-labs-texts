Intersecting families of polynomials over finite fields
Nika Salia, Dávid Tóth

Abstract
This paper establishes an analog of the Erdős–Ko–Rado theorem to polynomial rings over finite fields, affirmatively answering a conjecture of C. Tompkins. A k k -uniform family of subsets of a set of finite size n n is ℓ \ell -intersecting if any two subsets in the family intersect in at least ℓ \ell elements. The study of such intersecting families is a core subject of extremal set theory, tracing its roots to the seminal 1961 Erdős–Ko–Rado theorem, which establishes a sharp upper bound on the size of these families. Finite fields, or Galois fields, represent a well-studied object in discrete mathematics since the 19th century. This paper explores an extension of the Erdős–Ko–Rado theorem to polynomial rings over finite fields. Specifically, we determine the largest possible size of a family of monic polynomials, each of degree n n , over a finite field 𝔽 q \mathbb{F}_{q} , where every pair of polynomials in the family shares a common factor of degree at least ℓ \ell . We establish that the upper bound for this size is q n − ℓ q^{n-\ell} and characterize all extremal families that achieve this maximum size. Further extending our study to triple-intersecting families, where every triplet of polynomials shares a common factor of degree at least ℓ \ell , we prove that only trivial families achieve the corresponding upper bound. Moreover, by relaxing the conditions to include polynomials of degree at most n n , we affirm that only trivial families achieve the corresponding upper bound.



Keywords:
polynomial rings over finite fields; combinatorial number theory; Erdős-Ko-Rado theorem

1
Introduction

In 1961, Erdős, Ko and Rado
[
6
]

established a foundational result in extremal set theory by proving that for any finite family
\mathcal{F}
of
k
-subsets of a set with
n
elements,
[n]:=\{1,2,\dots,n\}
, where every pair of subsets shares at least
\ell\leq k
elements, the size of
\mathcal{F}
is bounded above by
\binom{n-\ell}{k-\ell}
, provided
n
is sufficiently large. This theorem has profoundly influenced the field due to its wide-ranging applications.
Note that this problem is an instance of Erdős matching
conjecture
[
7
]
.

The upper bound of the Erdős-Ko-Rado theorem is sharp and is achieved by a family
with a
unique structure.
For large enough
n
all extremal families are isomorphic to

\left\{\{n-\ell+1,\dots,n\}\cup A:A\in\binom{[n-\ell]}{k-\ell}\right\},

where
\binom{B}{k}
denotes all
k
-subsets of a set
B
.

Hilton and Milner
[
11
]
obtained stability of the Erdős-Ko-Rado theorem by establishing the maximal size of a
1
-intersecting family,
where no single element intersects all members of the family, and described
the structure of such extremal families.
Subsequently, using the Delta-system method, Frankl, Kostochka, and Mubayi
[
13
]
strengthened the Hilton-Milner theorem.

Intersecting families are
very well-studied.
Meagher and Purdy
[
17
]
extended the Erdős-Ko-Rado theorem for unbounded multisets, i.e., where each element has a uniform infinite bound, using the graph homomorphism method, see also
[
3
,
2
]
.
Furthermore, Füredi, Gerbner, and Vizer
[
8
]
expanded this result to an
\ell
-intersecting version for unbounded multisets, where the structure of the extremal set was determined by Meagher and Purdy
[
18
]
.
Very recently Liao, Lv, Cao, and Lu
[
16
]
extended the Erdős-Ko-Rado theorem for multisets with uniform multiplicity.
Thus this result may be considered as an extension of both the Erdős-Ko-Rado theorem and the result of Meagher and Purdy
[
17
]
.
These authors further refined their work by extending the Hilton-Milner theorem to uniformly bounded multisets
[
15
]
.

Inspired by the Erdős-Ko-Rado theorem, researchers have explored similar problems in different settings.
This includes studies on Erdős-Ko-Rado problems within permutation groups
[
5
,
14
,
12
]
.
Meagher, Spiga, and Tiep
[
19
]
further extended these ideas by adapting the theorem to intersecting sets of permutations in finite
2
-transitive groups. For a deeper understanding of versions of the Erdős-Ko-Rado theorem with algebraic nature see the book by Godsil and Meagher
[
10
]
.

Aguglia, Csajbók and Weiner
[
4
]
improved Adriaensen’s
[
1
]
result and obtained a version of the Erdős-Ko-Rado theorem for graphs of functions over a finite field.
Gadouleau, Mariot and
Mazzone
[
9
]
proved an analog of the
Erdős-Ko-Rado theorem for binary polynomials with pairwise linear common factors.

Building on some of the results discussed above, Tompkins
[
21
]
conjectured a version of the Erdős-Ko-Rado theorem for polynomials over finite fields.
While the challenges associated with polynomials over the complex field directly mirror the results of Meagher and Purdy for multisets, studying polynomials over finite fields introduces unique characteristics.
The varied structures of extremal families primarily drive this interest in these settings.
Specifically, we examine monic polynomials of fixed degrees over finite fields.

1.1
The extremal constructions

Let
\mathbb{F}_{q}[x]
be the polynomial ring over the finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
with
q
elements. A family
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}[x]
is termed
\ell
-intersecting
if for every pair of polynomials
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
in
\mathcal{F}
we have
\deg(\gcd(p_{1},p_{2}))\geq\ell
.
For a family
\mathcal{F}
of polynomials and a polynomial
p
, we denote by
\mathcal{F}\cdot p
the family generated from
\mathcal{F}
by multiplying each element by
p
. Similarly, we
write
\frac{\mathcal{F}}{p}:=\mathcal{F}\cdot\frac{1}{p}

for any non-zero
p
if all elements of

\mathcal{F}
are divisible by
p
.

A family that possesses a given property
\mathcal{P}
and has the largest possible size is called an
extremal family
for
\mathcal{P}
. If the property
\mathcal{P}
is understood from the context, we simply refer to the family as extremal without explicitly mentioning
\mathcal{P}
.
In the following, we restrict ourselves to

monic
polynomials
and examine extremal
\ell
-intersecting families of them.
The corresponding results for
general classes are easy consequences of the subsequent
theorems.

Motivated by the beauty of the topic,
Tompkins
[
21
]
conjectured that
a family consisting of all degree
n
multiples
of a fixed degree
\ell
polynomial
is an extremal
\ell
-intersecting
family of
degree
n
-polynomials,
and asked if these examples
are the only extremal
ones.
In the following, a family described above will be referred to as

trivial
.

Construction 1
.

Given any non-negative
integers
n
and
\ell
with
n\geq\ell
and a prime power
q
, consider a monic polynomial
g\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[x]
of degree
\ell
, and set

\mathcal{F}_{Triv}(g):=\left\{g\cdot p:p~\text{is a monic polynomial over }\mathbb{F}_{q},~\deg(p)=n-\ell\right\}.

Note that
\mathcal{F}_{Triv}(g)
is an
\ell
-intersecting
family of monic polynomials of degree
n
and
the size of it is
q^{n-\ell}
, and hence, by
Theorem
1
below,
it is of maximum size.
The extremal families with the same size and different structure will
be referred to as
non-trivial
.

As we will soon see, there are infinitely many non-trivial
extremal constructions for any
q
. To describe all of them,
we first note that once
\mathcal{F}
is
an extremal
\ell
-intersecting family of degree
n
polynomials and
g\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[x]

is any monic polynomial, then
\mathcal{F}\cdot g
is an
extremal

(\ell+\deg(g))
-intersecting family with elements of degree
n+\deg(g)

(this follows again from Theorem
1
).
Similarly, if a monic
polynomial
g
divides every element of the extremal
family
\mathcal{F}
,
then
\frac{\mathcal{F}}{g}
is still extremal. Loosely speaking,
it is enough to describe the extremal families up to polynomial
multiples.

Definition 1
.

A maximal size
\ell
-intersecting family
\mathcal{F}
of degree
n
polynomials over

\mathbb{F}_{q}
is
primary
if no non-unit polynomial divides every
element of
\mathcal{F}
.

To make our previous statement precise, it is clear that the
description of all extremal families is equivalent to the description
of all primary examples.
Note that a trivial extremal family is primary if and only if
it is
0
-intersecting and consists of all
monic polynomials of a fixed degree.

We give an example of primary extremal families.
Let
q>2
be a prime power,
\ell=q-2
,
n=q-1
,

H_{1}:=\prod_{a\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}(x-a)
and

\mathcal{F}_{1}:=\left\{\frac{H_{1}}{(x-a)}:a\in\mathbb{F}_{q}\right\}.

It is easy to see that

\mathcal{F}_{1}

is a non-trivial,
\ell
-intersecting set of degree
n
polynomials of size
q^{n-\ell}=q
.
Note that
H_{1}
is a monic polynomial that equals the least common multiple of all polynomials of degree one.
This example can easily be generalized to develop a broad class of constructions.
In the following construction, the constant
d
can be interpreted as the difference
n-\ell
.

Construction 2
.

Let
d
be a positive integer and
H_{d}
be the monic polynomial equal to the least common multiple of all
degree
d

polynomials
over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
.
We set

\mathcal{F}_{d}:=\left\{\frac{H_{d}}{p}:p~\text{is a monic polynomial over }\mathbb{F}_{q},~\deg(p)=d\right\}.

Note that
\mathcal{F}_{d}
is a
(\deg(H_{d})-2d)
-intersecting
family of degree
\deg(H_{d})-d
monic polynomials.
The size of
\mathcal{F}_{d}
is
q^{d}=q^{(\deg(H_{d})-d)-(\deg(H_{d})-2d)}
,
hence - as we
will soon prove - it is of maximum size.
Moreover, apart from the case
q=2
,

d=1
,
\mathcal{F}_{d}

is non-trivial, and since no non-unit polynomial divides
every element of the family,
it is also primary.
Remark that this construction can be viewed as similar to the one in the original Erdős–Ko–Rado theorem when the ground set is smaller relative to the sizes of the sets and their intersection size.

One of our main goals is to show that the aforementioned primary
constructions are the only possible ones with one single exception
that we give as the closure of this introductory section:

Construction 3
.

If
q=2
,
\ell=1
and
n=3
, then

\mathcal{F}=\{x^{2}(x+1),~x(x+1)^{2},~x(x^{2}+x+1),~(x+1)(x^{2}+x+1)\}

is a maximum size primary
1
-intersecting family
of degree
3
monic polynomials in
\mathbb{F}_{2}[x]

consisting of
q^{2}=q^{n-\ell}

elements. Observe that
\mathcal{F}
differs from the family
\mathcal{F}_{2}
defined
in Construction
2
.

2
Main Results

In this section, we give our main results about extremal

\ell
-intersecting polynomial families.
First, we determine the size of
such sets, then we continue by examining
the possible structures of them.

Theorem 1
.

Given any non-negative integers
n
and
\ell
with
n\geq\ell
and a prime power
q
, consider
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}[x]
, an
\ell
-intersecting family of monic polynomials of degree
n
.
Then we have

\left|\mathcal{F}\right|\leq q^{n-\ell}.

If
n>2\ell
, then any extremal
\mathcal{F}
is trivial with the sole exception
given in Construction
3
.

It follows now that the examples given in the
introductory section (including
Construction
1
,
2
,
3
)
are all extremal.
Also, by Theorem
1
, we have that all extremal families for which
n>2\ell+1
are trivial, while for
n=2\ell+1
there is a unique non-trivial extremal family.
A natural question is
if the constant
2
can be
lowered with an additional constraint.
In fact, by the following theorem, for every constant
c
greater than
1
and large enough
\ell
all extremal families are trivial.
Specifically, only a finite number of non-trivial extremal examples
exist for
n>c\ell
.
It is easy to see that the constant
c
must be greater than
1
by
Construction
2
.
Indeed, for every

d=n-\ell
and every
q
, we have a primary non-trivial extremal family (
2
) (except for the case
d=1
and
q=2
).

Theorem 2
.

For any constant

c>1

there is a threshold
\ell_{c}
such that any extremal
\ell
-intersecting family
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}[x]
of monic polynomials of degree
n
is trivial, provided
\ell>\ell_{c}
and
n>c\ell
.
The threshold
\ell_{c}
can be chosen independently of
q
.
Specifically, only a finite number of non-trivial extremal instances exist with
n>c\ell
, counting over all finite fields
\mathbb{F}_{q}
.

Next, we describe all the possible extremal
\ell
-intersecting
families. As it was detailed
in the introductory section,
this is equivalent to the description of
the primary extremal examples.
The trivial families were defined in Construction
1
, and all of them
belong to a
0
-intersecting primary family consisting
of all monic polynomials of a fixed (but otherwise arbitrary) degree.
In the forthcoming theorem, we provide a comprehensive characterization of all primary non-trivial extremal families.

Theorem 3
.

Every primary non-trivial extremal family different from Construction
3
is a family from Construction
2
.

Remark
.

As a corollary, we note that once we require any
k\geq 3
elements of a family
\mathcal{F}
of degree
n
monic polynomials to have a common divisor of degree at least
\ell
, then all extremal families are trivial.
Indeed, as such a family is
\ell
-intersecting, and the trivial construction has this property, the size of an extremal family is still
q^{n-\ell}
, and the non-trivial constructions in Theorem
3
do not have this stronger property.

Finally, we shortly address another version of the problem where one allows polynomials with
different
degrees in
\mathcal{F}
.

Theorem 4
.

For any non-negative integer
\ell
let
D_{\ell}
be a finite set of integers greater than

\ell
with
|D_{\ell}|\geq 2
, and let
q
be a prime power. Consider
a family
\mathcal{F}
of monic polynomials over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
whose degrees are in
D_{\ell}
such that any two
elements
of
\mathcal{F}
have a common divisor of degree at least
\ell
. Then

\left|\mathcal{F}\right|\leq\sum_{d\in D_{\ell}}q^{d-\ell},

where the upper bound is sharp
and every extremal
\mathcal{F}
is trivial.

Below we give two different proofs of this theorem. One of them
uses only Theorem
1
and
a lemma stated and proved in the course of its proof,
while the other one is an application of
Theorem
3
.

3
Proofs of Theorems

The subsequent proofs of the theorems stated above
have in big part a combinatorial flavor, but
we necessarily use some basic algebraic properties of
the ring
\mathbb{F}_{q}[x]
and
some simple number theoretic results.
Firstly, we will use the fact that
\mathbb{F}_{q}[x]
is Euclidean and
hence a unique factorization domain, in particular, every
irreducible element of it is prime.

Besides, we will need
some lower estimates on
the number
N_{q}(n)
of degree
n
monic irreducibles over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
.
This is given by the prime number theorem for polynomials over
finite fields, in fact, we use the explicit bound

N_{q}(n)\geq\frac{q^{n}}{n}-\frac{q^{\frac{n}{2}}}{n}-q^{\frac{n}{3}}

(1)

(see Theorem 2.2 in
[
20
]
and the estimate above it).
This result is an easy consequence of the well-known formula

N_{q}(n)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{d\mid n}\mu(n/d)q^{d}

(2)

(see the Corollary to Proposition 2.1 in
[
20
]
).
From these one deduces easily the following:

Claim 1
.

The number
N_{q}(n)
is at least

2
for every positive integer
n
and prime power
q
,
except for the case
q=n=2
.

This simple but in some situations useful little statement follows
directly from (
1
) in the cases
q\geq 4
;

q=3
,
n\geq 3
and
q=2
,
n\geq 5
, in all
other cases one may apply (
2
).
We are now ready to prove Theorem
1
.

Proof of Theorem
1
.

Assume for the sake of contradiction that
|\mathcal{F}|>q^{n-\ell}
. By the pigeonhole principle, there exist two distinct monic polynomials
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
in
\mathcal{F}
such that
the coefficients of
x^{n-1},\dots,x^{\ell}
are the same for both of the polynomials.
Hence we have

\deg(p_{1}-p_{2})\leq\ell-1.

Since
\mathcal{F}
is defined as
\ell
-intersecting, the degree of the greatest common divisor of polynomials
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
in
\mathcal{F}
is at least
\ell
. On the other hand
p_{1}-p_{2}
is a multiple of
\gcd(p_{1},p_{2})
, thus
\ell-1\geq\deg(p_{1}-p_{2})\geq\gcd(p_{1},p_{2})\geq\ell
, a contradiction.

The following statement has a key role in most arguments that follow,
hence we separate it as a lemma.

Lemma 1
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be an extremal family of
\ell
-intersecting monic polynomials of degree
n
over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
.
Then, for any irreducible monic polynomial
f
of degree
n-\ell
within
\mathbb{F}_{q}[x]
, there exists a polynomial
p
in
\mathcal{F}
divisible by
f
.

Proof.

As
\mathbb{F}_{q}[x]
is Euclidean, any polynomial
p
in
\mathcal{F}
can
be expressed as

p=f\cdot s+r,

where
s
is a monic polynomial of degree
\ell
and
r
is a polynomial with
degree at most
n-\ell-1
.
Suppose, for the sake of contradiction, that
r\neq 0
for all
p\in\mathcal{F}
.
Given that the cardinality of
\mathcal{F}
is
q^{n-\ell}
and the number of possible non-zero residues modulo
f
is one less, the pigeonhole principle implies that there must be two distinct polynomials,
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
, in
\mathcal{F}
with the same residue modulo
f
. Hence,
f
divides
p_{1}-p_{2}
.
As neither
p_{1}
nor
p_{2}
is divisible by
f
, it follows that
\gcd(p_{1},p_{2})
divides
\frac{p_{1}-p_{2}}{f}
, a non-zero polynomial of degree at most

n-1-(n-\ell)=\ell-1
,
contradicting the assertion that
\mathcal{F}
is
\ell
-intersecting.
∎

Suppose
n>2\ell
and let
\mathcal{F}
be an
\ell
-intersecting family of polynomials with the maximum size
q^{n-\ell}
. According to Claim
1
, there exist at least two distinct irreducible monic polynomials of degree
n-\ell

except for the case
q=2
,
\ell=1
, and
n=3
that will be handled at the end of the proof.

Consider
f_{1}
, an irreducible monic polynomial of degree
n-\ell
in
\mathbb{F}_{q}[x]
. As per Lemma
1
, there exists a polynomial
p_{1}
in
\mathcal{F}
that is divisible by
f_{1}
. Let
p_{1}=f_{1}\cdot g_{1}
, where
g_{1}
is a monic polynomial of degree
\ell
. Given that
\mathcal{F}
is
\ell
-intersecting, any polynomial in
\mathcal{F}
must be divisible by either
f_{1}
or
g_{1}
.
Therefore, either all polynomials in
\mathcal{F}
are divisible by
g_{1}
making
\mathcal{F}
trivial, or there exists another polynomial
p_{2}\in\mathcal{F}
such that
p_{2}=f_{1}\cdot g_{2}
with
g_{2}\neq g_{1}
.

By Claim
1
,
there is another irreducible monic polynomial

f_{2}
of degree
n-\ell
in
\mathbb{F}_{q}[x]
distinct from
f_{1}
.
According to Lemma
1
, there exists a polynomial
p
in
\mathcal{F}
that is divisible by
f_{2}
. Given that
p
is not divisible by
f_{1}
due to
n-\ell>\ell
, it follows that
p
must be divisible by
g_{1}
and
g_{2}
. However, since
g_{1}
and
g_{2}
are distinct monic polynomials each of degree
\ell
, their co-division of

is impossible, leading to a contradiction.

Assume now that
q=2
,
\ell=1
and
n=3
. The above argument still gives that in a non-trivial maximal size example there are two polynomials divisible by the unique irreducible degree
2

x^{2}+x+1
.
It is straightforward to conclude that
\mathcal{F}
must be
the family given in Construction

, i.e.

\mathcal{F}=\{x^{2}(x+1),~x(x+1)^{2},~x(x^{2}+x+1),~(x+1)~(x^{2}+x+1)\}.

∎

Proof of Theorem
2
.

We prove by induction, starting with the assertion that the statement holds when

n>c_{1}\ell
for

c_{1}=2
by Theorem
1
.
We then show that this implies that the statement holds for
c_{i+1}\ell<n\leq c_{i}\ell
and hence
for
c_{i+1}\ell<n

c_{i+1}=2-\frac{1}{c_{i}}
for any integer

.
Since
c_{i}
is strictly decreasing and converges to

,
this implies the statement of the theorem for every
c>1
.

The subsequent argument uses the existence of
k
distinct irreducible monic polynomials of degree

n-\ell
,
for some
k\in\mathbb{N^{+}}
.
We have that by
(
1
) for any fixed
k

except when

q
and
n-\ell
are small.
If
d:=n-\ell
is fixed, then

c_{i+1}\ell<n\leq c_{i}\ell\Longleftrightarrow\frac{d}{c_{i}-1}\leq\ell<\frac{d}{c_{i+1}-1},

i.e. for any
k\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
there are
k
distinct irreducibles except for finitely many
triples
(q,\ell,n)
.

Initially, we assume that for every pair of distinct

f,\tilde{f}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[x]

of degree

, there exists a polynomial
p\in\mathcal{F}
such that

and
\tilde{f}\nmid p
.
Let
f_{1},\dots,f_{k}
be distinct irreducible polynomials over
\mathbb{F}_{q}[x]
of degree
n-\ell
, and
let us apply the assumption with the choices
f=f_{1}
,
\tilde{f}=f_{2}
,
and
f=f_{2}
,
\tilde{f}=f_{1}
.
Then,
there exist
p_{1},p_{2}\in\mathcal{F}
such that
p_{1}=f_{1}\cdot g_{1}
,

p_{2}=f_{2}\cdot g_{2}
,
f_{2}\nmid p_{1}

and
f_{1}\nmid p_{2}
. Since the degree of
\gcd(p_{1},p_{2})
is at least
\ell
, we have
\gcd(p_{1},p_{2})=\gcd(g_{1},g_{2})=g_{1}=g_{2}
.
Now if
p_{3}=f_{1}\cdot g_{3}\in\mathcal{F}
and
f_{2}\nmid p_{3}
,
then
g_{3}=\gcd(p_{2},p_{3})=g_{2}=g_{1}
, i.e.

.
In other words,
p_{1}
is the only polynomial in
\mathcal{F}
that is a multiple of

but not of

.

If
p_{1}
is the only

f_{1}
, then every polynomial of
\mathcal{F}
is a multiple
g_{1}
, and
\mathcal{F}
is trivial.
Otherwise, we have a polynomial in
\mathcal{F}
that is a multiple of
f_{1}\cdot f_{2}
.
Let us fix such a
p_{0}=f_{1}\cdot f_{2}\cdot g_{0}\in\mathcal{F}
and consider an element

p\in\mathcal{F}
for which
f_{1}\nmid p
.
Then
\gcd(p,p_{1})=g_{1}=g_{2}
, i.e.
p=g_{2}\cdot h
.
Also,
\gcd(p,p_{0})=f_{2}\cdot g_{0}
and
\gcd(f_{2},g_{2})=1
, thus
h=f_{2}
, that is

.
Thus the only polynomial that is not divisible by
f_{1}
is
p_{2}
.
Repeating the argument for
f_{1}
and an irreducible polynomial from
\{f_{3},\dots,f_{k}\}

we conclude that
f_{2}\cdot f_{3}\cdots f_{k}
divides
p_{2}
, which is impossible if

k>\frac{c_{i}}{c_{i+1}-1}+1>\frac{n}{n-\ell}+1

since then
(k-1)(n-\ell)>n
holds.

Next assume that there are irreducibles
f_{1}
,
f_{2}
of degree
n-\ell
such that

f_{1}\mid p\Rightarrow f_{2}\mid p
holds
for every
p\in\mathcal{F}
.
That is, if
p\in\mathcal{F}
and
f_{1}\mid p
, then
p=f_{1}\cdot f_{2}\cdot g
for some
monic polynomial
g\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[x]
of degree
n-2(n-\ell)=2\ell-n
.
Let us fix such a
p\in\mathcal{F}
(that exists by Lemma
1
).
If there is a
\hat{p}\in\mathcal{F}
such that
f_{2}\nmid\hat{p}

and hence
f_{1}\nmid\hat{p}
, then the greatest common divisor of
p
and
\hat{p}
must
divide
g
and therefore its degree is at most
2\ell-n<\ell
that is impossible.
Consequently, any element of
\mathcal{F}
is divisible by
f_{2}
.
Note that if
\deg(f_{2})=n-\ell=\ell
(that is possible in the first step of the induction),
then
\mathcal{F}
is trivial, so in the following we may assume
n-\ell<\ell
.
Let us consider now the family

\mathcal{F}^{\prime}=\frac{\mathcal{F}}{f_{2}}.

Each polynomial of
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
has degree
\ell
and as
\mathcal{F}
is
\ell
-intersecting,

\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
is
(2\ell-n)
-intersecting.
Since
1-2c_{i}+c_{i}c_{i+1}=0
,
we have
\ell=c_{i}(2\ell-c_{i+1}\ell)>c_{i}(2\ell-n)
and if

i>1
, then
2\ell-n
is arbitrary large once
\ell
is large enough since

2\ell-n\geq(2-c_{i})\ell
holds.
Hence
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
and therefore
\mathcal{F}
must be trivial
for a large enough
\ell
by the inductive assumption.

In the situation of the previous paragraph, we must handle the case

i=1
separately, since then
c_{1}=2
and hence
2-c_{1}=0
hold.
But by Theorem
1
,
the inequality
\ell>2(2\ell-n)
implies that
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}

is trivial except for the case
q=2
,
2\ell-n=1
and
\ell=3
,
that implies
n=5
. Hence if
\ell
is bigger than
3
,
then
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
is trivial.

Finally, assume that
\mathcal{F}
is non-trivial. Since
c_{i+1}\ell<n\leq c_{i}\ell
, there are only finitely
many possibilities for
n
and
\ell
. Now for any fixed
n
and
\ell
,
if
q
and hence
k
is big enough, then the argument above yields a non-trivial

(2\ell-n)
-intersecting family
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
of degree
\ell
, and since

\ell>c_{i}(2\ell-n)
, we have finitely many possibilities for
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
, hence
we have finitely many choices for
q
as well. Lastly, for a fixed triple
(q,\ell,n)

there are finitely many maximal size sets of
\ell
-intersecting polynomials of degree
n
,
hence the induction step and the proof is complete.
∎

Proof of Theorem
3
.

Let us fix a prime power
q
and a difference
d>0
, and assume that
\mathcal{F}
is a maximal size primary
(non-trivial)
\ell
-intersecting family of degree
n
polynomials in
\mathbb{F}_{q}[x]
such that
n-\ell=d
.
First, we assume the existence of at least two distinct irreducible polynomials of degree
d
,
that is, we exclude the case
q=2=d
that will be handled later.

Notice that we already know a lot about the structure of
\mathcal{F}

based on the proof of Theorem
2
. Indeed, by
the argument of the fifth paragraph of that proof,
if
f_{1}
and

f_{2}
are distinct irreducibles of degree
n-\ell
and
f_{1}\mid p\Rightarrow f_{2}\mid p
for
any
p\in\mathcal{F}
, then any element of
\mathcal{F}
is divisible by
f_{2}
contradicting the assumption that

\mathcal{F}
is primary.

Hence for each pair of distinct irreducible monic polynomials
f_{i}
and
f_{j}
of degree
n-\ell
,
\mathcal{F}

must contain a polynomial which is divisible by
f_{i}
but not
by
f_{j}
.
In this case, as it was deduced in the fourth paragraph of the proof
of Theorem
2
, we get
that the structure of
\mathcal{F}
is the following.
Let
f_{1},f_{2},\dots,f_{k}
be all irreducible monic polynomials of degree
d=n-\ell
(
k\geq 2
) and let
F
be the product of them.
We must have

\mathcal{F}=\left\{\frac{F}{f_{i}}\cdot G:1\leq i\leq k\right\}\cup\left\{F\cdot\frac{G}{p}:p\mid G,~p~\text{monic},~\deg(p)=d=n-\ell\right\},

(3)

for some monic polynomial
G
coprime to
F
.

Note that in the second set above we need to take all the appropriate divisors of
G
because

\mathcal{F}
is of maximal size.
Since the first set on the right-hand side of (
3
)
has cardinality
N_{q}(d)
, the second one must contain
|\mathcal{F}|-N_{q}(d)=q^{d}-N_{q}(d)

polynomials, i.e.
G
must be divisible by
all
polynomials of degree
d
that are not irreducible.
Thus, we have

\mathcal{F}=\left\{\frac{F\cdot G}{p}:p\mid F\cdot G,~p~\text{monic},~\deg(p)=d=n-\ell\right\}.

Since
F\cdot G
is divisible by all degree
d
polynomials, it is a multiple of their least common multiple.
Moreover, since
\mathcal{F}
is a primary family,
F\cdot G
in fact equals the monic least common multiple of all degree
d
polynomials.
Thus
F\cdot G=H_{d}
and
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{F}_{d}
as in Construction
2
.

It remains to handle
the case
q=2=d
.
First note
that
|\mathcal{F}|=4
.
By
Lemma
1
, at least
one element of
\mathcal{F}
is divisible by
f(x)=x^{2}+x+1
, but
\mathcal{F}
is non-trivial, so

f
must divide in fact at least two elements. Also, since
\mathcal{F}
is primary,
f
cannot
divide all of the elements.

Assume that
f
divides exactly
2
elements of
\mathcal{F}
. Since
\mathcal{F}
is primary,
no irreducible of degree at least
3
divides any of its elements,
hence exactly two elements contain only linear factors.
It follows that
f^{2}
cannot divide any element as
d=2
. Therefore

\mathcal{F}=\{x^{\alpha_{1}}(x+1)^{\beta_{1}},\,x^{\alpha_{2}}(x+1)^{\beta_{2}},\,x^{\alpha_{3}}(x+1)^{\beta_{3}}f(x),\,x^{\alpha_{4}}(x+1)^{\beta_{4}}f(x)\}.

As
f\nmid x^{\alpha_{i}}(x+1)^{\beta_{i}}
for
i=1,2
and
d=2
, we have that

\alpha_{1}
and
\alpha_{2}
cannot be smaller than
\alpha_{3}
or
\alpha_{4}
.
Since
\mathcal{F}
is primary,
\alpha_{3}
or
\alpha_{4}
must be
0
. Without loss
of generality, we may assume
\alpha_{4}=0
. By a similar argument,
we get
\beta_{3}=0
.
Then the greatest common divisor of the last two polynomials
is
f
, and since
d=2
, we infer
0<\alpha_{3}=\beta_{4}\leq 2
.

If
\alpha_{3}=\beta_{4}=2
, then

\alpha_{1}+\beta_{1}=\alpha_{2}+\beta_{2}=4
.
By symmetry we may assume
\alpha_{1}>\alpha_{2}
that implies
\beta_{1}<\beta_{2}
.
Since
\ell=4-d=2
, we have
\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2}+\beta_{2}-\beta_{1}=2
, i.e.

\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2}=1=\beta_{2}-\beta_{1}
. Hence
\mathcal{F}
has the following form:

\mathcal{F}=\{x^{\alpha+1}(x+1)^{\beta},\,x^{\alpha}(x+1)^{\beta+1},\,x^{2}f(x),\,(x+1)^{2}f(x)\}.

But both
\alpha
and
\beta
must be at least
2
, that is impossible.

The case
\alpha_{3}=\beta_{4}=1
gives the family from Construction
3
in a similar way.

Finally, if exactly
3
elements of
\mathcal{F}
are
divisible by
f
,
then as above, we get

\mathcal{F}=\{x^{\alpha_{1}}(x+1)^{\beta_{1}},\,x^{\alpha_{2}}(x+1)^{\beta_{2}}f(x),\,x^{\alpha_{3}}(x+1)^{\beta_{3}}f(x),\,x^{\alpha_{4}}(x+1)^{\beta_{4}}f(x)\}.

Again, we can assume
\alpha_{4}=\beta_{3}=0
and
0<\alpha_{3}=\beta_{4}\leq 2
.
Since the degrees are the same and the last three polynomials are different,
the only possibility is
\alpha_{3}=\beta_{4}=2
, which forces the second polynomial
to be
x(x+1)f(x)
and the first one to be
x^{2}(x+1)^{2}
:

\mathcal{F}=\{x^{2}(x+1)^{2},\,x(x+1)f(x),\,x^{2}f(x),\,(x+1)^{2}f(x)\},

that is exactly Construction
2
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
4
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be an
\ell
-intersecting set of monic polynomials over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
with degrees in
D_{\ell}
, and
let
\mathcal{F}^{d}
denote the set of degree
d
elements of
\mathcal{F}
.
Then
\mathcal{F}^{d}
is still an
\ell
-intersecting set for any
d
, hence
|\mathcal{F}^{d}|\leq q^{d-\ell}

by Theorem
1
and
|\mathcal{F}|\leq\sum_{d\in D_{\ell}}q^{d-\ell}
follows.
If
|\mathcal{F}|
consists of all multiples of a fixed degree
\ell
polynomial with degrees in
D_{\ell}
,
then
|\mathcal{F}|=\sum_{d\in D_{\ell}}q^{d-\ell}
, hence the upper bound is sharp.

Assume that for some
\ell
and
D_{\ell}
there exists a non-trivial family
\mathcal{F}
with

|\mathcal{F}|=\sum_{d\in D_{\ell}}q^{d-\ell}
.
We can choose such
\mathcal{F}
so that
\ell
is minimal.
Note that
|\mathcal{F}^{d}|=q^{d-\ell}
must
hold for any
d\in D_{\ell}
, i.e.
\mathcal{F}^{d}
is an extremal
\ell
-intersecting family.

Let us set
n=\max(D_{\ell})
and let
f\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[x]
be an irreducible polynomial of degree
n-\ell
.
By Lemma
1
we have

f\cdot g\in\mathcal{F}^{n}
for some
g\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[x]
of degree
\ell
.
Assume that
p\in\mathcal{F}
,
\deg(p)<n
and
f\mid p
, i.e.
p=f\cdot h
where
\deg(h)<\ell
.
If
\hat{p}\in\mathcal{F}
, then since

\deg(\gcd(\hat{p},p))\geq\ell
must hold, we obtain
f\mid\hat{p}
.
That is, every element of
\mathcal{F}
is a multiple of
f
.
As
\mathcal{F}
is non-trivial, we must have
n-\ell<\ell
, contradicting the minimality of
\ell

since we could consider
\frac{\mathcal{F}}{f}
instead.
Therefore,
f\nmid p
must hold and hence
\gcd(f\cdot g,p)=g
giving
g\mid p
,
and this applies for any
p\in\mathcal{F}

of degree less than
n
.

Since
\mathcal{F}
is non-trivial, there must be an element in
\mathcal{F}
that is not divisible by
g
,
and by the previous paragraph, this element must be in
\mathcal{F}^{n}
. But
f\cdot g\in\mathcal{F}^{n}
,
hence such an element must be divisible by
f
, so there is an element
f\cdot\hat{g}\in\mathcal{F}^{n}

with
\deg(\hat{g})=\ell
,
\hat{g}\neq g
. Repeating the argument of the previous paragraph with
\hat{g}
instead
of
g
, we get that any
p\in\mathcal{F}

of degree less than
n
is divisible by
\hat{g}
. Since
\mathcal{F}^{d}
is
\ell
-intersecting
of maximal size for any
d\in D_{\ell}
and
\deg(g)=\deg(\hat{g})=\ell
,
we infer
g=\hat{g}
contradicting the choice of
\hat{g}
.
∎

Second proof of Theorem
4
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be an extremal
\ell
-intersecting set.
As in the previous proof we have
\left\lvert{\mathcal{F}}\right\rvert=\sum_{d\in D_{\ell}}q^{d-\ell}
, and
\left\lvert{\mathcal{F}^{d}}\right\rvert=q^{d-\ell}
where
\mathcal{F}^{d}
denotes the set of degree
d
polynomials of
\mathcal{F}
as above.

If all
\mathcal{F}^{d}
’s are trivial then let the monic polynomials
g_{1}
and
g_{2}
of degree
\ell
be the greatest common divisors of
\mathcal{F}^{d_{1}}
and
\mathcal{F}^{d_{2}}
respectively for some distinct

d_{1},d_{2}\in D_{\ell}
.
If there are two distinct elements
a_{1}
and
a_{2}
of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, such that the multiplicity of the
factor
x-a_{i}

is at least as large in
g_{i}

as it is
in
g_{3-i}
for
i\in\{1,2\}
then we have

\deg(\gcd(g_{1}(x-a_{1})^{d_{1}-\ell},g_{2}(x-a_{2})^{d_{2}-\ell}))=\deg(\gcd(g_{1},g_{2}))\leq\ell.

Since
g_{1}(x-a_{1})^{d_{1}-\ell},g_{2}(x-a_{2})^{d_{2}-\ell}

are polynomials of the
\ell
-intersecting family
\mathcal{F}
,
we have the inequality
\deg(\gcd(g_{1},g_{2}))\geq\ell
as well
and hence
g_{1}=g_{2}
.
Otherwise, without loss of generality, we may assume
that for every
a\in\mathbb{F}_{q}

the multiplicity of the
factor
x-a

in
g_{1}
is strictly greater than in
g_{2}
.
Therefore, the polynomial
g_{2}
is divisible by an irreducible polynomial
f
of degree at least two, where the multiplicity of
f
in
g_{2}
exceeds its multiplicity in
g_{1}
.
This simply implies

\deg(\gcd(g_{1},g_{2}))\leq\ell-\deg(f)
and

\deg(\gcd(g_{1}(x-a)^{d_{1}-\ell},g_{2}f^{\left\lfloor{\frac{d_{2}-\ell}{\deg(f)}}\right\rfloor}(x-a)^{d_{2}-\ell-\deg(f)\left\lfloor{\frac{d_{2}-\ell}{\deg(f)}}\right\rfloor}))\leq(\deg(f)-1)+\deg(\gcd(g_{1},g_{2}))<\ell

for any
a\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
,
that is impossible since
\mathcal{F}
is
\ell
-intersecting.

Thus
either we are done or, there is an
n\in D_{\ell}
such that
\mathcal{F}^{n}
is a non-trivial
\ell
-intersecting family of degree
n
monic polynomials.
Let
g
be the greatest common divisor of all polynomials in
\mathcal{F}^{n}

with
\deg(g)=\ell_{0}<\ell
.
Let us set
n_{1}:=n-\ell_{0}
,

\ell_{1}:=\ell-\ell_{0}
and
\mathcal{F}^{n_{1},\ell_{1}}:=\frac{\mathcal{F}^{n}}{g}
.
Then, by Theorem
3
, either

\mathcal{F}^{n_{1},\ell_{1}}=\mathcal{F}_{n_{1}-\ell_{1}}=\left\{\frac{H_{n_{1}-\ell_{1}}}{p}:p~\text{is a monic polynomial over }\mathbb{F}_{q},~\deg(p)=n_{1}-\ell_{1}\right\},

where
H_{n_{1}-\ell_{1}}
is the monic polynomial equal to the least common multiple of all degree
{n_{1}-\ell_{1}}
polynomials over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
,
or

\mathcal{F}^{n_{1},\ell_{1}}=\{x^{2}(x+1),~x(x+1)^{2},~x(x^{2}+x+1),~(x+1)(x^{2}+x+1)\}.

If there is an
f\in\mathcal{F}
such that
\deg(f)<n
then, by the maximality of
\mathcal{F}
, the polynomials
f\cdot x^{n-\deg(f)}
and
f\cdot(x-1)^{n-\deg(f)}
are in
\mathcal{F}^{n}
,
and hence they are multiples of
g
.
Therefore (as
x^{n-\deg(f)}
and
(x-1)^{n-\deg(f)}
are coprime)

f
is a multiple of
g
.

If
\mathcal{F}^{n_{1},\ell_{1}}
is the family from
Construction
3
,
and hence
q=2
,
n_{1}=3
,
\ell_{1}=1
, then

\deg(f)=n-1
since
n-2<\deg(f)<n
as
\mathcal{F}
is an
(n-2)
-intersecting family.
That is,
\mathcal{F}^{\deg(f)}
must contain two elements of
the form
g\cdot p
with
\deg(p)=2
.
As
p
has a common factor with every element of
\mathcal{F}^{n_{1},\ell_{1}}
,
p
must be
x(x+1)
, thus there is no choice for two such polynomials, a contradiction.

If

\mathcal{F}^{n_{1},\ell_{1}}=\mathcal{F}_{n_{1}-\ell_{1}}
, then
assume first that there is a monic irreducible polynomial
p
of degree
n_{1}-\ell_{1}
which is coprime to

\frac{f}{g}
.
Note that if
n_{1}-\ell_{1}=1
, then
q>2
, since otherwise there is no
\ell_{1}
-intersecting primary construction of degree
n_{1}
polynomials.
Then
without loss of generality, we may assume
p\neq x
and
p\neq x-1
. Thus
\frac{f}{g}\cdot x^{n-\deg(f)}
and
\frac{f}{g}\cdot(x-1)^{n-\deg(f)}
are coprime to
p
and by the structure of
\mathcal{F}^{n}

they are both equal to

\frac{H_{n_{1}-\ell_{1}}}{p}
,
a contradiction.

It follows that

\frac{f}{g}
is a multiple of all irreducible polynomials of degree
n_{1}-\ell_{1}
.
Let
f_{n_{1},\ell_{1}}
be an irreducible of degree

n_{1}-\ell_{1}=n-\ell
, note that it is coprime
to
\frac{H_{n_{1}-\ell_{1}}}{f_{n_{1},\ell_{1}}}
, and we have

\deg\left(\gcd\left(\frac{H_{n_{1}-\ell_{1}}}{f_{n_{1},\ell_{1}}}\cdot g,\frac{f}{g}\cdot g\right)\right)<\ell,

since
\frac{f}{g}
is a multiple of
f_{n_{1},\ell_{1}}
and
\deg(f)<n
.
A contradiction to
\mathcal{F}
being

\ell
-intersecting since
f
and
\frac{H_{n_{1}-\ell_{1}}}{f_{n_{1},\ell_{1}}}\cdot g\in\mathcal{F}
.

Finally, if
f\in\mathcal{F}
such that
\deg(f)>n
, then
\mathcal{F}^{\deg(f)}
is a trivial family by the previous argument, i.e. it consists
of all degree
\deg(f)
multiples of a degree
\ell
polynomial
h
.
Since
\mathcal{F}^{n}
is non-trivial, hence there
is a
p\in\mathcal{F}^{n}
such
that the degree of

\hat{h}=\gcd(h,p)
is
less than
\ell
.
Since
\gcd(p,hx^{\deg(f)-\deg(h)})
has degree at least
\ell
,
it follows that
p/\hat{h}
is a multiple of
x^{\ell-\deg(\hat{h})}
.
But this is impossible
since

\deg\left(\gcd\left(\left(\frac{px^{\deg(f)-n}}{\hat{h}x^{\ell-\deg(\hat{h})}}+1\right)h,p\right)\right)<\ell.

∎
