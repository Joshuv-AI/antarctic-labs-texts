On the Homology of Unions of Certain Non-Degenerate Quadrics in General Position
Maximilian Mühlbauer

Abstract
. Motivated by problems arising in the complex analysis of perturbative quantum field theory, we investigate the homology of finite unions of certain non-degenerate quadratic affine hypersurfaces of complex dimension n n in general position. The homology of such unions and the homology of ℂ n + 1 \mathbb{C}^{n+1} relative to such unions is decomposed into a direct sum of homology groups of the various possible intersections of these hypersurfaces. This allows us to compute the homology groups up to isomorphism. Furthermore, we compute an explicit set of generators for a specific arrangement of these hypersurfaces by constructing a sufficiently large CW-subcomplex of the union in question. The intersection indices of all generators with the Borel-Moore homology class of ( i ⋅ ℝ ) n + 1 (i\cdot\mathbb{R})^{n+1} in the complement of the union of surfaces are computed.

(a,r)=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{N},r_{1},\ldots,r_{N})\in(\mathbb{C}^{n+1})^{N}\times(\mathbb{C}^{\times})^{N}

S_{i}(a_{i},r_{i}):=\{z\in\mathbb{C}^{n+1}\;|\;(z-a_{i})^{2}=r_{i}^{2}\},\qquad\qquad i=1,\ldots,N

with radius

by

S_{\mathbb{C}}^{n}(a,r):=\{z\in\mathbb{C}^{n+1}\;|\;(z-a)^{2}=r^{2}\}.

Since

(3.4)

\partial_{z}((z-a)^{2}-r^{2})=2\cdot(z-a)\neq 0\qquad\forall z\in S_{\mathbb{C}}^{n}(a,r),

the complex sphere

also carries the structure of a complex analytic
n

[

]
). In particular,

). The

is path-connected (unless

consists of two points). Clearly, any two complex

-spheres

are homeomorphic via the homeomorphism

(3.5)

S_{\mathbb{C}}^{n}(a_{1},r_{1})\overset{\sim}{\to}S_{\mathbb{C}}^{n}(a_{2},r_{2}),\qquad z\mapsto\frac{r_{2}}{r_{1}}(z-a_{1})+a_{2}.

In particular, any complex
n
-sphere

is homeomorphic to the unit sphere
S_{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
. Furthermore, it is well-known that the restriction of
S_{\mathbb{C}}^{n}(a,r)
to the hyperplane

n

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
S_{\mathbb{C}}^{n}(a,r)
be a complex
n
-sphere and write
r=e^{i\phi}\cdot|r|
. Then
S_{\mathbb{C}}^{n}(a,r)
(strongly) deformation retracts to

(3.6)

\{z\in\mathbb{C}^{n+1}\;|\;\mathrm{Im}(e^{-i\phi}\cdot z)=\mathrm{Im}(e^{-i\phi}\cdot a),\;(\mathrm{Re}(e^{-i\phi}\cdot z)-\mathrm{Re}(e^{-i\phi}\cdot a))^{2}=|r|^{2}\}\simeq S^{n}.

Proof.

By applying the homeomorphism (
3.5
), we may assume
r=1
and
a=0
, i.e.
S_{\mathbb{C}}^{n}(a,r)=S_{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
, without loss of generality. This homeomorphism sends the set (
3.6
) to the real unit
n
-sphere
S^{n}
embedded in
\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
via the inclusion
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{C}^{n+1},\;x\mapsto x+i\cdot 0
. By a slight abuse of notation, we identify
i(S^{n})
with
S^{n}
.

We decompose the defining equation of
S_{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
into its real and imaginary part:

(3.7)

z^{2}=1\qquad\Leftrightarrow\qquad(\text{Re}\,z)^{2}-(\text{Im}\,z)^{2}=1\quad\land\quad\text{Re}\,z\cdot\text{Im}\,z=0.

Now let us define the continuous map

(3.8)

f:S_{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\times[0,1]\to\mathbb{R},\qquad(z,t)\mapsto\sqrt{\frac{1+(1-t)^{2}\cdot(\text{Im}\,z)^{2}}{(\text{Re}\,z)^{2}}}.

Note that for all
z\in S_{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
, we have
(\text{Re}\,z)^{2},1+(1-t)^{2}\cdot(\text{Im}\,z)^{2}\geq 1
, in particular
(\text{Re}\,z)^{2}>0
and
1+(1-t)^{2}\cdot(\text{Im}\,z)^{2}\geq 0
, so that

is well-defined. Also note that for all
z\in S_{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
, we have

(3.9)

f(z,0)=\sqrt{\frac{1+(\text{Im}\,z)^{2}}{(\text{Re}\,z)^{2}}}=\sqrt{\frac{(\text{Re}\,z)^{2}}{(\text{Re}\,z)^{2}}}=1.

This allows us to define the continuous map

(3.10)

g:S_{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\times[0,1]\to S_{\mathbb{C}}^{n},\qquad(z,t)\mapsto f(z,t)\cdot\text{Re}\,z+i\cdot(1-t)\cdot\text{Im}\,z.

First, we need to verify that the image of
g
is indeed contained in
S_{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
. For
(z,t)\in S_{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\times[0,1]
, we compute

(3.11)

\text{Re}\,g(z,t)\cdot\text{Im}\,g(z,t)=(1-t)\cdot f(z,t)\cdot\text{Re}\,z\cdot\text{Im}\,z\overset{\text{Re}\,z\cdot\text{Im}\,z=0}{=}0

and

(3.12)

\begin{split}(\text{Re}\,g(z,t))^{2}-(\text{Im}\,g(z,t))^{2}&=(f(z,t))^{2}\cdot(\text{Re}\,z)^{2}-(1-t)^{2}\cdot(\text{Im}\,z)^{2}\\
&=\frac{1+(1-t)^{2}\cdot(\text{Im}\,z)^{2}}{(\text{Re}\,z)^{2}}\cdot(\text{Re}\,z)^{2}-(1-t)^{2}\cdot(\text{Im}\,z)^{2}=1.\end{split}

Now we show that
g
is indeed the sought (strong) deformation retraction of
S_{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
to
S^{n}
. For any
z\in S_{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
, we have

(3.13)

g(z,0)=\underbrace{f(z,0)}_{=1}\cdot\,\text{Re}\,z+i\cdot(1-0)\cdot\text{Im}\,z=\text{Re}\,z+i\cdot\text{Im}\,z=z

and

(3.14)

g(z,1)=f(z,1)\cdot\text{Re}\,z+(1-1)\cdot\text{Im}\,z=f(z,1)\cdot\text{Re}\,z\quad\in\quad S^{n}.

Here, we used
(f(z,1))^{2}=\frac{1}{(\text{Re}\,z)^{2}}
in the last step. Furthermore, for any
(z,t)\in S^{n}\times[0,1]
, we have
f(z,t)=\frac{1}{|\text{Re}\,z|}
and thus

(3.15)

g(z,t)=f(z,t)\cdot\text{Re}\,z\quad\in\quad S^{n}.

This completes the proof.
∎

Using the previous Proposition

Corollary 3.2

For any

\widetilde{H}_{k}(S_{\mathbb{C}}^{n})\simeq\widetilde{H}_{k}(S^{n})\simeq\begin{cases}\mathbb{Z}&\mathrm{if}\text{ }k=n\\

n
-sphere contained within

.

Proof.

The existence of the first isomorphism follows immediately from Proposition

, the second is just the famous computation of the homology of the (real) sphere.
∎

Now, we focus on finite unions of complex spheres. The next proposition shows that, as long as the involved complex spheres are in general position, all such unions of a fixed number of complex spheres are topologically essentially the same (in a strong sense).

While from a homological point of view, a single complex sphere is no different than a real sphere, there clearly are some topological differences. For example, the complex sphere

Then the last equation reads

The fact that we do not leave the world of complex spheres by forming intersections turns out to be very useful for our computations. It allows us to apply useful induction arguments in our computation of the homology further below.

For our purposes, it is convenient to not only consider finite unions of complex spheres, but unions intersected with finitely many complex spheres. Due to Proposition

3.1.
Homology of Complex Sphere Arrangements

Now we turn to the task of understanding the homology of
\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
relative to an arrangement of complex spheres

to at least such a degree that we can name a set of generators and compute the intersection indices of them with

relative to any arrangement of complex spheres

by decomposing it into a direct sum which is trivial in all dimensions but

First we need the following simple lemma, which is well-known (see for example

Let

be a contractible topological space,

a non-empty subspace. Then the boundary homomorphism
\partial
induces an isomorphism

(3.29)

\partial^{\ast}:H_{k}(X,A)\overset{\sim}{\to}\widetilde{H}_{k-1}(A)

10

10

Proof.

We consider the long exact sequence

The above lemma is useful to translate the computation of the relative homology groups we are interested in to the computation of regular homology groups. . Now we show that
\widetilde{H}_{k}(S^{I,J})=0
for all
k\neq n-|I|
and that

(3.38)

\widetilde{H}_{n-|I|}(S^{I,J})\simeq\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}K\subset J\\
K\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}\widetilde{H}_{n-|I|-|K|+1}(\bigcap_{i\in I\cup K}S_{i}),

where the isomorphism is given by
g_{I,J}
from equation (
3.34
).

First, we equip
S^{I,J}
with the structure of a CW-complex such that each
S^{I^{\prime},J^{\prime}}
with
I\subset I^{\prime}
and
J^{\prime}\subset J
appears as a subcomplex. We conduct the proof by induction on
|J|
. If
|J|=0
, there is nothing to do: In this case, we have
J=\emptyset
and thus
S^{I,J}=\emptyset
. This means we trivially have

(3.39)

\widetilde{H}_{n-|I|}(S^{I,J})=0=\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}K\subset\emptyset\\
K\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}\widetilde{H}_{n-|I|-|K|+1}(\bigcap_{i\in I\cup K}S_{i}).

Now suppose we already know the theorem is true up to some some
|J|-1=m-1\geq 0
and want to show that it also holds for
|J|=m
. Let us write
J=\{j_{1},\ldots,j_{m}\}
. The reduced Mayer-Vietoris sequence (see Theorem
2.1
) associated to the decomposition
S=S^{I,\{j_{1}\}}\cup S^{I,J\backslash\{j_{1}\}}
reads

(3.40)

\cdots\to\widetilde{H}_{k}(S^{I\cup\{j_{1}\},J\backslash\{j_{1}\}})\to\widetilde{H}_{k}(S^{I,\{j_{1}\}})\oplus\widetilde{H}_{k}(S^{I,J\backslash\{j_{1}\}})\to\tilde{H}_{k}(S^{I,J})\to\widetilde{H}_{k-1}(S^{I\cup\{j_{1}\},J\backslash\{j_{1}\}})\to\cdots

We can apply the induction hypothesis to
S^{I\cup\{j_{1}\},J\backslash\{j_{1}\}}
,
S^{I,\{j_{1}\}}
and
S^{I,J\backslash\{j_{1}\}}
so that

(3.41)

\widetilde{H}_{k}(S^{I\cup\{j_{1}\},J\backslash\{j_{1}\}})=0

for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
k\neq n-|I\cup\{j_{1}\}|+1=n-|I|
and

(3.42)

\widetilde{H}_{k}(S^{I,\{j_{1}\}})=\widetilde{H}_{k}(S^{I,J\backslash\{j_{1}\}})=0

for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
k\neq n-|I|+1
. Thus, the sequence (
3.40
) contains an exact sequence

(3.43)

0\to\widetilde{H}_{k}(S^{I,J})\to 0

for all
k\neq n-|I|+1
, which means
\widetilde{H}_{k}(S^{I,J})=0
for all
k\neq n-|I|+1
. Additionally, (
3.40
) contains a short exact sequence

(3.44)

0\to\widetilde{H}_{n-|I|+1}(S^{I,\{j_{1}\}})\oplus\widetilde{H}_{n-|I|+1}(S^{I,J\backslash\{j_{1}\}})\overset{k_{\ast}-l_{\ast}}{\to}\widetilde{H}_{n-|I|+1}(S^{I,J})\overset{\delta_{j_{1}}}{\to}\widetilde{H}_{n-|I|}(S^{I\cup\{j_{1}\},J\backslash\{j_{1}\}})\to 0,

where
k:S^{I,\{j_{1}\}}\hookrightarrow S^{I,J}
and
l:S^{I,J\backslash\{j_{1}\}}\hookrightarrow S^{I,J}
are the natural inclusions, while
\delta_{j_{1}}
is the Mayer-Vietoris homomorphism with respect to the given decomposition. Now, again by the induction hypothesis, the group
\widetilde{H}_{n-|I|}(S^{I\cup\{j_{1}\},J\backslash\{j_{1}\}})
is free and hence this short exact sequence splits. Thus,

(3.45)

\widetilde{H}_{n-|I|+1}(S^{I,J})\simeq\widetilde{H}_{n-|I|+1}(S^{I,\{j_{1}\}})\oplus\widetilde{H}_{n-|I|+1}(S^{I,J\backslash\{j_{1}\}})\oplus\widetilde{H}_{n-|I|}(S^{I\cup\{j_{1}\},J\backslash\{j_{1}\}}),

where the isomorphism is given by a map
(f_{1},f_{2},\delta_{\ast})
such that

(3.46)

(f_{1},f_{2})\circ(k_{\ast}-l_{\ast})=\mathrm{id},

which is equivalent to

(3.47)

f_{1}\circ k_{\ast}=\mathrm{id},\quad f_{1}\circ l_{\ast}=0\qquad\text{and}\qquad f_{2}\circ k_{\ast}=0,\quad f_{2}\circ l_{\ast}=\mathrm{id}.

Using the induction hypothesis one more time, we have

(3.48)

\widetilde{H}_{n-|I|+1}(S^{I,J\backslash\{j_{1}\}})\overset{g_{I,J\backslash\{j_{1}\}}}{\simeq}\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}K\subset J\backslash\{j_{1}\}\\
K\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}\widetilde{H}_{n-|I|-|K|+1}(\bigcap_{i\in I\cup K}S_{i})

and

(3.49)

\begin{split}\widetilde{H}_{n-|I\cup\{j_{1}\}|+1}(S^{I\cup\{j_{1}\},J\backslash\{j_{1}\}})&\overset{g_{I\cup\{j_{1}\},J\backslash\{j_{1}\}}}{\simeq}\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}K\subset J\backslash\{j_{1}\}\\
K\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}\widetilde{H}_{n-|I\cup\{j_{1}\}|-|K|+1}(\bigcap_{i\in I\cup\{j_{1}\}\cup K}S_{i})\\
&\overset{\phantom{g_{I\cup\{j_{1}\},J\backslash\{j_{1}\}}}}{=}\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}K\subset J\\
j_{1}\in K,\;K\neq\emptyset,\{j_{1}\}\end{subarray}}\widetilde{H}_{n-|I|-|K|+1}(\bigcap_{i\in I\cup K}S_{i}).\end{split}

Plugging this into equation (
3.45
) and using
S^{I,\{j_{1}\}}=\bigcap_{i\in I\cup\{j_{1}\}}S_{i}
, we obtain

(3.50)

\widetilde{H}_{n-|I|+1}(S^{I,J})\overset{(f_{1},g_{I,J\backslash\{j_{1}\}}\circ f_{2},g_{I\cup\{j_{1}\},J\backslash\{j_{1}\}}\circ\delta_{j_{1}})}{\simeq}\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}K\subset J\\
K\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}\widetilde{H}_{n-|I|-|K|+1}(\bigcap_{i\in I\cup K}S_{i})

as claimed.

All that remains to do is prove the existence of the last isomorphism in (
3.32
), which is easy: For any non-empty
K\subset\{1,\ldots,N\}
, the space
\bigcap_{i\in K}S_{i}
is homeomorphic to a complex
(n-|K|+1)
-sphere according to Proposition
3.5
and thus we have

(3.51)

\widetilde{H}_{n-|I|+1}(\bigcap_{i\in K}S_{i})\simeq\begin{cases}\mathbb{Z}&\text{if }|K|\leq n+1\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}.

according to Corollary
3.2
. Thus,
\widetilde{H}_{n-|I|+1}(S^{I,J})
contains a summand
\mathbb{Z}
for every non-empty
K\subset J
with
|I\cup K|\leq n+1
. Since there are
2^{|J|}-1
such subsets of
J
if
N\leq n+1
and
2^{|J|}-2
if
N=n+2
, we are done.
∎

3.1.1.
Comparison with the Local Homology

The above Theorem
3.9
computes the homology of the pair
(\mathbb{C}^{n+1},S^{I,J})
up to isomorphism. But a priori, this does not tell us much about the discontinuity of singular integrals since the vanishing cycle, sphere and cell are homology classes in

(3.52)

H_{n+1-N}(U\cap\bigcap_{i\in I}S_{i}),\quad H_{n+1}(U-\bigcup_{i\in I}S_{i})\quad\text{and}\quad H_{n+1}(U,\,\bigcup_{i\in I}S_{i})

respectively, where
I\subset\{1,\ldots,N\}
is a non-empty subset and

is a small neighborhood around a pinch point (for details, the reader is referred to
[
Pha11
]
for the general theory or
[
Müh22
]
for this construction in the context of our setup of complex spheres). As is well-known, homology does not behave particularly nicely with respect to inclusions. In particular, if

is the natural inclusion, then the induced map
i_{\ast}
on the level of homology need neither be injective nor surjective. For arrangements of complex spheres however, there is a direct correspondence between the vanishing classes and the generators of the global homology:

Let

be an open neighborhood near pinch point where the

with

pinch such that the vanishing classes are defined. Then the vanishing cycle is given by the deformation retract of

to a real sphere contained in

is also generated by this real sphere and thus the inclusion

(3.53)

descends to an isomorphism on the level of homology. Furthermore, it is known that we have isomorphisms
[

]

(3.54)

\partial:H_{n+1}(U,\,\bigcup_{i\in I}S_{i})\overset{\sim}{\to}H_{n+1-N}(U\cap\bigcap_{i\in I}S_{i})

and

(3.55)

given by the iterated boundary
\partial:=\partial_{N}\circ\cdots\circ\partial_{1}
(see equation (
2.12
)) and the iterated Leray coboundary
\delta:=\delta_{1}\circ\cdots\circ\delta_{N}
(see equation (
2.13
)) respectively.

Theorem
3.9
tells us that the homology of

relative to an arrangement of complex spheres

(except in the case

for each

\phi_{i^{\prime}}^{\tau}:=\phi_{i}^{\tau}|_{e_{i^{\prime}}^{\tau}}:e_{i^{\prime}}^{\tau}\overset{\sim}{\to}\iota_{m+1}^{(1)}(\Delta^{m-1}\times\Delta^{k})\simeq\Delta^{m-1}\times\Delta^{k}.

Furthermore for
i^{\prime\prime}=(I_{1},\ldots,I_{k},J_{\leq},J_{\geq},\prec)\in\mathcal{I}
, we have the following identities:

(4.57)

\displaystyle\phi_{i}^{\tau}\circ(\phi_{i^{\prime}}^{\tau})^{-1}

\displaystyle=\iota_{m+1}^{(1)}

,

(4.58)

\displaystyle\phi_{i^{\prime\prime}}^{\tau}\circ(\phi_{(I_{1},\ldots,I_{k},J_{\leq}-\{j\},J_{\geq}-\{j\},\tau,\prec)}^{\tau})^{-1}

\displaystyle=\iota_{n_{i^{\prime\prime}}(j)}^{(1)}\qquad

\displaystyle\forall j\in J_{i},

(4.59)

\displaystyle\phi_{i^{\prime\prime}}^{\tau}\circ(\phi_{(I_{1},\ldots,I_{k},J_{\leq},J_{\geq},\prec)}^{0})^{-1}

\displaystyle=\iota_{m}^{(1)}\qquad

\displaystyle\text{if }\tau\neq 0,

(4.60)

\displaystyle\phi_{i^{\prime\prime}}^{\tau}\circ(\phi_{(I_{1},\ldots,\widehat{I}_{j},\ldots,I_{k},J_{\leq},J_{\geq},\prec)}^{\tau})^{-1}

\displaystyle=\iota_{j}^{(2)}\qquad

\displaystyle\forall j\in\{1,\ldots,k\},

Proof.

We start by construction the homeomorphism
\phi_{i}^{\tau}
. Let us denote
j_{0}:=\min I_{1}
and

(4.61)

c:\mathbb{C}^{n+1}\overset{\sim}{\to}(\mathbb{R}^{n+1})^{2},\qquad z\mapsto(\text{Re}\,z,\text{Im}\,z).

Then for every element
(x,y)\in c(e_{i}^{\tau})
, the first entry
x
can be expressed as

(4.62)

x=d_{j_{m}}\cdot v+\sum_{j\in J_{i}}d_{j}\cdot v_{j}

with
d_{j_{m}}\in\tau\cdot\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
as well as
d_{j}\in\mathbb{R}_{\leq 0}
if
j\in J_{\leq}
and
d_{j}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
if
j\in J_{\geq}
. Write
J_{i}:=\{j_{1},\ldots,j_{|J_{i}|}\}
with
j_{1}<\cdots<j_{|J_{i}|}
and denote

(4.63)

X:=(\tau_{1}\cdot\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0})\times\cdots\times(\tau_{|J_{i}|}\cdot\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0})\times(\tau\cdot\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}),

where
\tau_{k}=-1
if
j_{k}\in J_{\leq}
,
\tau_{k}=1
if
j_{k}\in J_{\geq}
and
\tau_{k}=0
otherwise.
Then there is a change of basis homeomorphism

(4.64)

g_{i}^{\tau}:V_{J_{\leq},J_{\geq}}^{\tau}\overset{\sim}{\to}X,\qquad x\mapsto(d_{j_{1}},\ldots,d_{j_{m}}).

The defining inequality for
(g_{i}^{\tau},\text{id}_{\mathbb{R}^{n+1}})(c(e_{i}^{\tau}))
reads

(4.65)

\sum_{k=1}^{n+1}(\sum_{k=1}^{m}d_{j_{k}})^{2}\leq 1+(y-e_{j_{0}})^{2}.

On the left hand side, we have a positive-definite quadratic form in the
d_{j_{1}},\ldots,d_{j_{m}}
and thus there exists a real invertible
m\times m
-matrix
M_{i}^{\tau}
, sending
(d_{j_{1}},\ldots,d_{j_{m}})
to
(d_{j_{1}}^{\prime},\ldots,d_{j_{m}}^{\prime})
such that equation (
4.65
) reads

(4.66)

\sum_{k=1}^{m}(d_{j_{k}}^{\prime})^{2}\leq 1+(y-e_{j_{0}})^{2}.

Now we divide by the modified radius

(4.67)

\tilde{r}_{i}(y):=\sqrt{1+(y-e_{j_{0}})^{2}}\quad\in\quad\mathbb{R}_{>0}

and apply the homeomorphism
h
from Lemma
4.4
. To simplify the notation, let us write

(4.68)

\begin{split}f_{i}^{\tau}:\{(d,y)\in X\times\Delta^{k}\;|\;d^{2}\leq\tilde{r}_{i}^{2}(y)\}&\to\Delta^{m}\times\Delta^{k},\\
(d,y)&\mapsto(h(\frac{M_{i}^{\tau}\cdot d}{\tilde{r}_{i}(y)}),y).\end{split}

The map
f_{i}
is a homeomorphism with continuous inverse

(4.69)

\begin{split}(f_{i}^{\tau})^{-1}:\Delta^{m}\times\Delta^{k}&\to\{(d,y)\in X\times\Delta^{k}\;|\;d^{2}\leq\tilde{r}_{i}^{2}(y)\},\\
(x,y)&\mapsto((M_{i}^{\tau})^{-1}\cdot(\tilde{r}_{i}(y)\cdot h^{-1}(x)),y).\end{split}

Indeed, for all
(d,y)\in\{(d,y)\in X\times\Delta^{k}\;|\;d^{2}\leq\tilde{r}_{i}^{2}(y)\}
we have

(4.70)

\begin{split}((f_{i}^{\tau})^{-1}\circ f_{i}^{\tau})(d,y)&=(f_{i}^{\tau})^{-1}(h(\frac{M_{i}^{\tau}\cdot d}{\tilde{r}_{i}(y)}),y)\\
&=((M_{i}^{\tau})^{-1}\cdot\tilde{r}_{i}(y)\cdot h^{-1}(h(\frac{M_{i}^{\tau}\cdot d}{\tilde{r}_{i}(y)})),y)\\
&=((M_{i}^{\tau})^{-1}\cdot M_{i}^{\tau}\cdot d,y)=(d,y)\end{split}

and for all
(x,y)\in\Delta^{m}\times\Delta^{k}
we have

(4.71)

\begin{split}(f_{i}^{\tau}\circ(f_{i}^{\tau})^{-1})(x,y)&=f_{i}^{\tau}((M_{i}^{\tau})^{-1}\cdot\tilde{r}_{i}(y)\cdot h^{-1}(x),y)\\
&=(h(\frac{M_{i}^{\tau}\cdot(M_{i}^{\tau})^{-1}\cdot\tilde{r}_{i}(y)\cdot h^{-1}(x)}{\tilde{r}_{i}(y)}),y)\\
&=(h(h^{-1}(x)),y)=(x,y).\end{split}

Then we can define the sought homeomorphism by

(4.72)

\phi_{i}^{\tau}:=f_{i}^{\tau}\circ(g_{i}^{\tau},\text{id}_{\mathbb{R}^{n+1}})\circ c\quad:\quad e_{i}^{\tau}\overset{\sim}{\to}\Delta^{m}\times\Delta^{k},

which has continuous inverse

(4.73)

(\phi_{i}^{\tau})^{-1}=c^{-1}\circ(g_{i}^{\tau},\text{id}_{\mathbb{R}^{n+1}})^{-1}\circ(f_{i}^{\tau})^{-1}\quad:\quad\Delta^{m}\times\Delta^{k}\overset{\sim}{\to}e_{i}^{\tau}.

Next, we consider the restriction
\phi_{i^{\prime}}^{\tau}
of
\phi_{i}^{\tau}
to
e_{i^{\prime}}^{\tau}
. It suffices to show that
\phi_{i}^{\tau}
sends elements in
e_{i^{\prime}}^{\tau}
to
\iota_{m+1}^{(1)}(\Delta^{m-1}\times\Delta^{k})
and the other way around for
(\phi_{i}^{\tau})^{-1}
. So let
z\in e_{i^{\prime}}^{\tau}
. Then applying
(g_{i}^{\tau},\text{id}_{\mathbb{R}^{n+1}})\circ c
sends
z
to some
(d,y)
. Applying the matrix
M_{i}^{\tau}
to
d
yields some
(d^{\prime},y)
such that
(d^{\prime})^{2}=(\tilde{r}_{i}(y))^{2}
. After diving
d^{\prime}
by
\tilde{r}_{i}(y)
, the result lies on the unit sphere. By Lemma
4.4
,
h
sends such an element to
\iota(\Delta^{m-1})
so that the claim follows after using the fact that
\iota_{m+1}^{(1)}=(\iota,\text{id})
. The other way around, let
(x,y)\in\iota_{m+1}^{(1)}(\Delta^{m-1}\times\Delta^{k})
. Then similarly to the first case, applying
h^{-1}
to
x
and multiplying by
\tilde{r}_{i}(y)
yields an element
(d,y)
with
d^{2}=(r_{i}(y))^{2}
. By the same logic as above, this gets send to some
z\in e_{i^{\prime}}^{\tau}
.

Now we prove the second part of the statement. Equation (
4.57
) follows immediately from the preceding discussion. For equation (
4.58
) to (
4.60
), we first compute

(4.74)

\phi_{i}^{\tau}\circ(\phi_{i^{\prime}}^{\tau})^{-1}=f_{i}^{\tau}\circ(g_{i}^{\tau},\text{id}_{\mathbb{R}^{n+1}})\circ(g_{i^{\prime}}^{\tau},\text{id}_{\mathbb{R}^{n+1}})^{-1}\circ(f_{i^{\prime}}^{\tau})^{-1}.

To show equation equation (
4.58
), denote
\tilde{i}=(I_{1},\ldots,I_{k},J_{\leq}-\{j\},J_{\geq}-\{j\},\prec)
. The homeomorphism
(f_{\tilde{i}}^{\tau})^{-1}
sends
(x,y)\in\Delta^{m-1}\times\Delta^{k}
to some
(d,y)
with
d^{2}\prec\tilde{r}_{\tilde{i}}^{2}(y)=\tilde{r}_{i}^{2}(y)
. Then
(g_{\tilde{i}}^{\tau})^{-1}
sends
d
to

(4.75)

d_{j_{m-1}}\cdot v+\sum_{j\in J_{i}-\{j\}}d_{j}\cdot v_{j},

which
g_{i^{\prime\prime}}^{\tau}
sends to
(d_{j_{1}},\ldots,d_{j_{n_{i}(j)}-1},0,d_{j_{n_{i}(j)}},\ldots,d_{j_{m-1}})
and the claim follows. Similarly, one shows equation (
4.59
). Equation (
4.60
) follows directly from the structure of the simplical complex with purely imaginary support.
∎

Using the established homeomorphisms, we can endow the set

(4.76)

Y:=\bigcup_{\tau\in\{-1,0,1\}}\bigcup_{i\in\mathcal{I}}e_{i}^{\tau}\quad\subset\quad\mathbb{C}^{n+1}

with the structure of a CW-complex of dimension
n+1
. To unify the notation, we employ the Kronecker-Delta symbol
\delta_{a,b}
which is 1 if
a=b
and 0 otherwise.

Corollary 4.6
.

The set
Y
has the structure of a regular CW-complex with cells
e_{i}^{\tau}
. Furthermore, the CW-structure is such that for all
i=(I_{1},\ldots,I_{k},J_{\leq},J_{\geq},\prec)\in\mathcal{I}
and all
\tau\in\{-1,0,1\}
, we have the following identity for the boundary
\partial e_{i}^{\tau}
of
e_{i}^{\tau}
:

(4.77)

\begin{split}\partial e_{i}^{\tau}&=\delta_{|\tau|,1}\cdot(-1)^{|J_{i}|}\cdot e_{(I_{1},\ldots,I_{k},J_{\leq},J_{\geq},\prec)}^{0}\\
&+\delta_{\prec,\leq}\cdot(-1)^{|J_{i}|+|\tau|}\cdot e_{(I_{1},\ldots,I_{k},J_{\leq},J_{\geq},=)}^{\tau}+\sum_{j\in J_{i}}(-1)^{n_{i}(j)-1}\cdot e_{(I_{1},\ldots,I_{k},J_{\leq}-\{j\},J_{\geq}-\{j\},\leq)}^{\tau}\\
&+(-1)^{|J_{i}|+|\tau|+\delta_{\prec,\leq}-1}\cdot\sum_{j=1}^{k}(-1)^{j-1}\cdot e_{(I_{1},\ldots,\widehat{I}_{j},\ldots,I_{k},J_{\leq},J_{\geq},\leq)}^{\tau}.\end{split}

Proof.

By the discussion above, in particular Proposition
4.5
, we can view each
e_{i}^{\tau}
as a cell and equations (
4.57
) to (
4.60
) show that the gluing maps are in fact homeomorphisms. By Lemma
4.3
, the
e_{i}^{\tau}
form a partition of
Y
into open cells. Thus,
Y
has the structure of a regular CW-complex with cells
e_{i}^{\tau}
and it remains to compute the boundary of each cell
e_{i}^{\tau}
.

Let
i=(I_{1},\ldots,I_{k},J_{\leq},J_{\geq},\prec)\in\mathcal{I}
,
\tau\in\{-1,0,1\}
and denote
m:=|J_{i}|+|\tau|
. We use the fact that the homomorphisms induced by continuous maps on the level of chains commute with the boundary:

(4.78)

\partial e_{i}^{\tau}=\partial((\phi_{i}^{\tau})^{-1}\circ\phi_{i}^{\tau})(e_{i}^{\tau})=(\phi_{i}^{\tau})^{-1}\partial(\phi_{i}^{\tau}(e_{i}^{\tau})).

Here, we denoted the homomorphisms induces by
\phi_{i}^{\tau}
and
(\phi_{i}^{\tau})^{-1}
on the level of chains by the same symbol to not clutter the notation further. If
\prec
is
\leq
, we calculate

(4.79)

\partial(\phi_{i}^{\tau}(e_{i}^{\tau}))=\partial(\Delta^{m}\times\Delta^{k})=\sum_{j=1}^{m+1}(-1)^{j-1}\iota_{j}^{(1)}(\Delta^{m-1}\times\Delta^{k})+(-1)^{m}\cdot\sum_{j=1}^{k+1}(-1)^{j-1}\iota_{j}^{(2)}(\Delta^{m}\times\Delta^{k-1}).

Similarly, if
\prec
is
=
, we calculate

(4.80)

\partial(\phi_{i}^{\tau}(e_{i}^{\tau}))=\partial(\Delta^{m-1}\times\Delta^{k})=\sum_{j=1}^{m}(-1)^{j-1}\iota_{j}^{(1)}(\Delta^{m-2}\times\Delta^{k})+(-1)^{m-1}\cdot\sum_{j=1}^{k+1}(-1)^{j-1}\iota_{j}^{(2)}(\Delta^{m-1}\times\Delta^{k-1}).

As for
\phi_{i}^{\tau}
and
(\phi_{i}^{\tau})^{-1}
, we denote the homomorphisms induced by
\iota_{k}^{(1)}
and
\iota_{k}^{(2)}
by the same symbol. Applying
(\phi_{i}^{\tau})^{-1}
to both sides yields

(4.81)

\begin{split}\partial e_{i}^{\tau}&=\delta_{|\tau|,1}\cdot(-1)^{m-1}\cdot e_{i}^{0}+(-1)^{m}\cdot e_{(I_{1},\ldots,I_{k},J_{\leq},J_{\geq},=)}^{\tau}+\sum_{j\in J_{i}}(-1)^{n_{i}(j)-1}\cdot e_{(I_{1},\ldots,I_{k},J_{\leq}-\{j\},J_{\geq}-\{j\},\leq)}\\
&+(-1)^{m}\cdot\sum_{j=1}^{k}(-1)^{j-1}\cdot e_{(I_{1},\ldots,\widehat{I}_{j},\ldots,I_{k},J_{\leq},J_{\geq},\leq)},\end{split}

resp.

(4.82)

\begin{split}\partial e_{i}^{\tau}&=\delta_{|\tau|,1}\cdot(-1)^{m-1}\cdot e_{i}^{0}+\sum_{j\in J_{i}}(-1)^{n_{i}(j)-1}\cdot e_{(I_{1},\ldots,I_{k},J_{\leq}-\{j\},J_{\geq}-\{j\},\leq)}\\
&+(-1)^{m-1}\cdot\sum_{j=1}^{k}(-1)^{j-1}\cdot e_{(I_{1},\ldots,\widehat{I}_{j},\ldots,I_{k},J_{\leq},J_{\geq},\leq)},\end{split}

according to Proposition
4.5
. Now combining these equations yields the desired equation (
4.77
).
∎

Now, the combinatorial part of this work begins. In the above corollary, the notation is somewhat unwieldy. To simplify the notation a bit, we make the following definitions, in particular to express the boundary of cells in
Y
in a more convenient manner. Let
i=(I_{1},\ldots,I_{k},J_{\leq},J_{\geq},\prec)\in\mathcal{I}
. If
\prec
is
\leq
, we denote

(4.83)

\mathcal{D}^{=}(i):=(I_{1},\ldots,I_{k},J_{\leq},J_{\geq},=).

Furthermore, for every
1\leq j\leq k
, we write

(4.84)

\mathcal{D}_{j}^{I}(i):=(I_{1},\ldots,\widehat{I}_{j},\ldots,I_{k},J_{\leq},J_{\geq},\prec)

and for every
j\in J_{i}
, we write

(4.85)

\mathcal{D}_{j}^{J}(i):=(I_{1},\ldots,I_{k},J_{\leq}-\{j\},J_{\geq}-\{j\},\prec).

Then result of Corollary
4.6
can be expressed as follows: For every
i=(I_{1},\ldots,I_{k},J_{\leq},J_{\geq},\prec)\in\mathcal{I}
and
\tau\in\{-1,0,1\}
, we have

(4.86)

\begin{split}\partial e_{i}^{\tau}&=\delta_{|\tau|,1}\cdot(-1)^{|J_{i}|}\cdot e_{i}^{0}+\delta_{\prec,\leq}\cdot(-1)^{|J_{i}|+|\tau|}\cdot e_{\mathcal{D}^{=}(i)}^{\tau}+\sum_{j\in J_{i}}(-1)^{n_{i}(j)-1}\cdot e_{\mathcal{D}_{j}^{J}(i)}^{\tau}\\
&+(-1)^{|J_{i}|+|\tau|+\delta_{\prec,\leq}-1}\cdot\sum_{j=1}^{k}(-1)^{j-1}\cdot e_{\mathcal{D}_{j}^{I}(i)}^{\tau}.\end{split}

To make our life a little easier, we group some of the cells together. It turns out that the cells
e_{i}^{0}
do not play a relevant role for our generators and that the cells
e_{i}^{1}
and
e_{i}^{-1}
always appear together. Therefore, we set

(4.87)

e_{i}:=e_{i}^{1}-e_{i}^{-1}

for every
i\in\mathcal{I}
. Then the
(\tau=0)
-faces cancel in the boundary of
e_{i}
:

Lemma 4.7
.

Let
i=(I_{1},\ldots,I_{k},J_{\leq},J_{\geq},\prec)\in\mathcal{I}
. Then

(4.88)

\partial e_{i}=\delta_{\prec,\leq}\cdot(-1)^{|J_{i}|+1}\cdot e_{\mathcal{D}^{=}(i)}+\sum_{j\in J_{i}}(-1)^{n_{i}(j)-1}\cdot e_{\mathcal{D}_{j}^{J}(i)}+(-1)^{|J_{i}|+\delta_{\prec,\leq}}\cdot\sum_{j=1}^{k}(-1)^{j-1}\cdot e_{\mathcal{D}_{j}^{I}(i)}.

Proof.

Using equation (
4.86
), this is obtained from a straightforward computation.
∎

In a next step, we want to group even more cells together. For this purpose, we consider the extended index set

(4.89)

\tilde{\mathcal{I}}:=\{(I_{1},\ldots,I_{k},J_{\leq},J_{\geq},\prec)\;|\;\begin{subarray}{c}\emptyset\neq I_{1}\subsetneq\cdots\subsetneq I_{k}\subset\{1,\ldots,n+1\},\;J_{\leq},J_{\geq}\subset\{1,\ldots,n+1\}\backslash I,\\
\prec\in\{\leq,=\}\end{subarray}\},

where
J_{\leq}
and
J_{\geq}
are allowed to overlap. Clearly,
\mathcal{I}\subset\tilde{\mathcal{I}}
. We can still use our notation
\mathcal{D}^{=}
,
\mathcal{D}_{j}^{J}
and
\mathcal{D}_{j}^{I}
for this extended set of indices in the obvious manner. For every
i=(I_{1},\ldots,I_{k},J_{\leq},J_{\geq},\prec)\in\tilde{\mathcal{I}}
, we now define

(4.90)

e_{i}:=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}A\cup B=J_{\leq}\cap J_{\geq}\\
A\cap B=\emptyset\end{subarray}}(-1)^{|A|-1}\cdot e_{(I_{1},\ldots,I_{k},(J_{\leq}\backslash(J_{\leq}\cap J_{\geq}))\cup A,(J_{\geq}\backslash(J_{\leq}\cap J_{\geq}))\cup B,\prec)}.

In other words, for overlapping
J_{\leq}
and
J_{\geq}
, we sum over all partitions, distributing the intersection
J_{\leq}\cap J_{\geq}
among
J_{\leq}
and
J_{\geq}
(with well-chosen signs). The content of the following lemma is that the boundary terms coming from
J_{\leq}\cap J_{\geq}
cancel.

Lemma 4.8
.

Let
i=(I_{1},\ldots,I_{k},J_{\leq},J_{\geq},\prec)\in\tilde{\mathcal{I}}
. Then we have

(4.91)

\partial e_{i}=\delta_{\prec,\leq}\cdot(-1)^{|J_{i}|+1}\cdot e_{\mathcal{D}^{=}(i)}+\sum_{j\in J_{i}\backslash(J_{\leq}\cap J_{\geq})}(-1)^{n_{i}(j)-1}\cdot e_{\mathcal{D}_{j}^{J}(i)}+(-1)^{|J_{i}|+\delta_{\prec,\leq}}\cdot\sum_{j=1}^{k}(-1)^{j-1}\cdot e_{\mathcal{D}_{j}^{I}(i)}.

Proof.

Let us denote
K:=J_{\leq}\cap J_{\geq}
as well as
\tilde{J}_{\leq}:=J_{\leq}\backslash K
,
\tilde{J}_{\geq}:=J_{\geq}\backslash K
and
\tilde{J}_{i}=J_{i}\backslash K
. Using equation (
4.86
), we compute

(4.92)

\begin{split}\partial e_{i}&=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}A\cup B=K\\
A\cap B=\emptyset\end{subarray}}(-1)^{|A|-1}\cdot\partial e_{(I_{1},\ldots,I_{k},\tilde{J}_{\geq}\cup A,\tilde{J}_{\geq}\cup B,\prec)}\\
&=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}A\cup B=K\\
A\cap B=\emptyset\end{subarray}}(-1)^{|A|-1}\cdot\Bigl(\delta_{\prec,\leq}\cdot(-1)^{|J_{i}|+1}\cdot e_{(I_{1},\ldots,I_{k},\tilde{J}_{\leq}\cup A,\tilde{J}_{\geq}\cup B,\tau,=)}\\
&+\sum_{j\in J_{i}}(-1)^{n_{i}(j)-1}\cdot e_{(I_{1},\ldots,I_{k},\tilde{J}_{\leq}\cup A-\{j\},\tilde{J}_{\geq}\cup B-\{j\},\prec)}+(-1)^{|J_{i}|+\delta_{\prec,\leq}}\cdot\sum_{j=1}^{k}(-1)^{j-1}\cdot e_{(I_{1},\ldots,\widehat{I}_{j},\ldots,I_{k},\tilde{J}_{\leq}\cup A,\tilde{J}_{\geq}\cup B,\prec)}\Bigr).\end{split}

For the first term, we immediately obtain

(4.93)

\begin{split}&\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}A\cup B=K\\
A\cap B=\emptyset\end{subarray}}(-1)^{|A|-1}\cdot\delta_{\prec,\leq}\cdot(-1)^{|J_{i}|+1}\cdot e_{(I_{1},\ldots,I_{k},\tilde{J}_{\leq}\cup A,\tilde{J}_{\geq}\cup B,\tau,=)}\\
=&\delta_{\prec,\leq}\cdot(-1)^{|J_{i}|+1}\cdot\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}A\cup B=K\\
A\cap B=\emptyset\end{subarray}}(-1)^{|A|-1}e_{(I_{1},\ldots,I_{k},\tilde{J}_{\leq}\cup A,\tilde{J}_{\geq}\cup B,\tau,=)}=\delta_{\prec,\leq}\cdot(-1)^{|J_{i}|+1}\cdot e_{\mathcal{D}^{=}(i)}.\end{split}

Similarly, for the last term, we obtain

(4.94)

\begin{split}&\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}A\cup B=K\\
A\cap B=\emptyset\end{subarray}}(-1)^{|A|-1}\cdot(-1)^{|J_{i}|+\delta_{\prec,\leq}}\cdot\sum_{j=1}^{k}(-1)^{j-1}\cdot e_{(I_{1},\ldots,\widehat{I}_{j},\ldots,I_{k},\tilde{J}_{\leq}\cup A,\tilde{J}_{\geq}\cup B,\prec)}\\
=&(-1)^{|J_{i}|+\delta_{\prec,\leq}}\cdot\sum_{j=1}^{k}(-1)^{j-1}\cdot\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}A\cup B=K\\
A\cap B=\emptyset\end{subarray}}(-1)^{|A|-1}\cdot e_{(I_{1},\ldots,\widehat{I}_{j},\ldots,I_{k},\tilde{J}_{\leq}\cup A,\tilde{J}_{\geq}\cup B,\prec)}\\
=&(-1)^{|J_{i}|+\delta_{\prec,\leq}}\cdot\sum_{j=1}^{k}(-1)^{j-1}\cdot e_{\mathcal{D}_{j}^{I}(i)}.\end{split}

To deal with the middle term, let
j\in K
. Then for every
A\cup B=K
with
A\cap B=\emptyset
and
j\in A
, there are
A^{\prime}:=A\backslash\{j\}
and
B^{\prime}:=B\cup\{j\}
which satisfy
A^{\prime}\cup B^{\prime}=K
and
A^{\prime}\cap B^{\prime}=\emptyset
. This means that we have
A-\{j\}=A^{\prime}=A^{\prime}-\{j\}
and
B^{\prime}-\{j\}=B=B-\{j\}
so that

(4.95)

e_{(I_{1},\ldots,I_{k},\tilde{J}_{\leq}\cup A-\{j\},\tilde{J}_{\geq}\cup B-\{j\},\prec)}=e_{(I_{1},\ldots,I_{k},\tilde{J}_{\leq}\cup A^{\prime}-\{j\},\tilde{J}_{\geq}\cup B^{\prime}-\{j\},\prec)}.

Furthermore,
|A|=|A^{\prime}|+1
which means
(-1)^{|A|-1}=-(-1)^{|A^{\prime}|-1}
. Hence, the terms with
j\in K
cancel pairwise and the middle term becomes

(4.96)

\begin{split}&\sum_{j\in\tilde{J}_{i}}(-1)^{n_{i}(j)-1}\cdot\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}A\cup B=K\\
A\cap B=\emptyset\end{subarray}}(-1)^{|A|-1}\cdot e_{(I_{1},\ldots,I_{k},\tilde{J}_{\leq}\cup A-\{j\},\tilde{J}_{\geq}\cup B-\{j\},\prec)}\\
=&\sum_{j\in\tilde{J}_{i}}(-1)^{n_{i}(j)-1}\cdot e_{\mathcal{D}_{j}^{J}(i)}.\end{split}

This completes the proof.
∎

In a final step, we form even larger groups of cells. For each pair of subsets
K_{1},K_{2}\subset\{1,\ldots,n+1\}
, we define the index set

(4.97)

\mathcal{I}_{K_{1},K_{2},\prec}:=\{i=(K_{1},I_{2},\ldots,I_{k},\{1,\ldots,n+1\}\backslash K_{2},\{1,\ldots,n+1\}\backslash I_{k},\prec)\in\tilde{\mathcal{I}}\;|\;\begin{subarray}{c}I_{k}\subset K_{2},\\
\forall 2\leq j\leq k:|I_{j-1}|=|I_{j}|-1\end{subarray}\}.

Note that
\mathcal{I}_{K_{1},K_{2},\prec}
can only be non-empty if
K_{1}\subset K_{2}
(for
\prec\in\{\leq,=\}
) and if
K_{1},K_{2}
are non-empty. Clearly, an element
i=(I_{1},\ldots,I_{k},J_{\leq},J_{\geq},\prec)
in
\mathcal{I}_{K_{1},K_{2},\prec}
only depends on
I_{1},\ldots,I_{k}
(actually only on
I_{2},\ldots,I_{k}
). Therefore, we simply write
i=(I_{1},\ldots,I_{k})
in this case in a slight abuse of notation. For every
i=(I_{1},\ldots,I_{k})\in\mathcal{I}_{K_{1},K_{2},\prec}
, we have

(4.98)

J_{i}=\{1,\ldots,n+1\}\backslash I_{k}\qquad\text{and}\qquad J_{\leq}\cap J_{\geq}=\{1,\ldots,n+1\}\backslash K_{2}.

The plan is to form linear combinations of all cells
e_{i}
with

once and now we want to trace another circle around
p_{\pm}
. Then
(i\cdot\mathbb{R})^{D}+(-1)^{\frac{(D+1)(D+2)}{2}}\cdot\tilde{e}_{+}
becomes

(4.190)

(i\cdot\mathbb{R})^{D}+(-1)^{\frac{(D+1)(D+2)}{2}}\cdot\tilde{e}_{+}+(-1)^{\frac{(D+1)(D+2)}{2}}\cdot\langle(i\cdot\mathbb{R})^{D}|\mathbf{e}_{\pm}\rangle\cdot\tilde{e}_{\pm}+\langle\tilde{e}_{+}|\mathbf{e}_{\pm}\rangle\cdot\tilde{e}_{\pm}

For
p_{+}
, we obtain

(4.191)

\begin{cases}(i\cdot\mathbb{R})^{D}&\text{if }D\text{ is even}\\
(i\cdot\mathbb{R})^{D}+4\cdot(-1)^{\frac{(D+1)(D+2)}{2}}\cdot\tilde{e}_{+}&\text{if }D\text{ is odd}.\end{cases}

So the function has a square-root behaviour if
D
is even and a logarithmic behaviour otherwise. For
p_{-}
, we obtain

(4.192)

\begin{cases}(i\cdot\mathbb{R})^{D}+(-1)^{\frac{(D+1)(D+2)}{2}}\cdot\tilde{e}_{+}+2\cdot(-1)^{\frac{D(D+1)}{2}}\cdot\tilde{e}_{-})&\text{if }D\text{ is even}\\
(i\cdot\mathbb{R})^{D}+(-1)^{\frac{(D+1)(D+2)}{2}}\cdot\tilde{e}_{+}&\text{if }D\text{ is odd}.\end{cases}

5.
Conclusion and Outlook

By constructing a decomposition of the relative homology groups
H_{k}(\mathbb{C}^{n+1},\bigcup_{i=1}^{n+1}S_{i})
into a direct sum, it is possible to find explicit generators for these groups in terms of a CW-complex. Having these generators at our disposal, it is possible to compute the relevant intersection indices. This is an important step on the way to understand Cutkosky’s Theorem and concludes a big chunk of the algebro-topological part of the problem. We have seen in the concluding example how the results of this article can be applied to understand the analytic structure of a Feynman integral. To solve the massive one-loop case completely, the two remaining steps are the following: First, the vanishing cell must be expressed as a linear combination of the found generators
\mathbf{e}_{I}
. Second, the setup from
[
Müh22
]
must be generalized so that it covers even dimensions. Both of these points will be addressed in the follow-up to the mentioned article.
