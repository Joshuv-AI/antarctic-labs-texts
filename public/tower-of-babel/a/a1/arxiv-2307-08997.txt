Deterministic Objective Bayesian Analysis for Spatial Models
Ryan Burn

Abstract
Berger et al. 2001 and Ren et al. 2012 derived noninformative priors for Gaussian process models of spatially correlated data using the reference prior approach ( Berger, Bernardo 1991 ) . The priors have good statistical properties and provide a basis for objective Bayesian analysis ( Berger 2006 ) . Using a trust-region algorithm for optimization with exact equations for posterior derivatives and an adaptive sparse grid at Chebyshev nodes, this paper develops deterministic algorithms for fully Bayesian prediction and inference with the priors. Implementations of the algorithms are available at https://github.com/rnburn/bbai .

Introduction

Suppose we observe a Gaussian process
Z(\cdot)
at sample points

{\boldsymbol{s}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{s}}_{n}
where

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left\{Z({\boldsymbol{s}})\right\}

\displaystyle=\beta_{1}x_{1}({\boldsymbol{s}})+\cdots+\beta_{p}x_{p}({\boldsymbol{s}})

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{x}({\boldsymbol{s}});

\displaystyle\operatorname{cov}\left\{Z({\boldsymbol{s}}),Z(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}})\right\}

\displaystyle=\sigma^{2}\left\{\operatorname{\psi}_{\ell}(\left\|{\boldsymbol{s}}-\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}}\right\|)+\eta\right\};

(1)

\boldsymbol{x}(\cdot)
and
\operatorname{\psi}_{\ell}(\cdot)
represent the known regressor function and correlation
function; and
\boldsymbol{\beta}
,
\sigma^{2}
,
\ell
, and
\eta
represent the unknown regression coefficients,
signal variance, length, and noise-to-signal ratio.

Let
\boldsymbol{\theta}
denote the unknown parameters
\left(\boldsymbol{\beta},\sigma^{2},\ell,\eta\right)^{\prime}
.
To reason about possible values at unobserved points, we’d like to know the distribution

\operatorname{P}\left(Z(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}})\mid\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{true}}\right)

where
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}}
is an unobserved point and
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}}
denotes the observations

\left(Z({\boldsymbol{s}}_{1}),\ldots,Z({\boldsymbol{s}}_{n})\right)^{\prime}
.
Of course, different values of
\boldsymbol{\theta}
could
reasonably produce
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}}
, so there’s no way we can identify
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{true}}
or construct prediction distributions
exactly. We need ways to approximate.

Approach 1: Maximize Likelihood

Suppose the likelihood function,
L(\boldsymbol{\theta};\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}})\propto\operatorname{P}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}}\mid\boldsymbol{\theta}\right)

is strongly peaked about an optimum,
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{ml}}

\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{true}}

\operatorname{P}\left(Z(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}})\mid\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{true}}\right)

But what happens if a broad range of parameters could reasonably produce
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}}

Approach 2: Integrate over Possible Parameters

We saw in Example
1.1
that using maximum likelihood parameters can lead
to poor results when the likelihood function isn’t strongly peaked
(
Berger et al. 1999
)
. Instead of approximating prediction distributions with only a single value of
\boldsymbol{\theta}
, let’s
consider every
\boldsymbol{\theta}
and weigh by a posterior distribution,
\operatorname{\pi}\left(\boldsymbol{\theta}\mid\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}}\right)
,

\displaystyle\operatorname{P^{\pi}}\left(Z(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}})\mid\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}}\right)=\int\operatorname{P}\left(Z(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}})\mid\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}},\boldsymbol{\theta}\right)\operatorname{\pi}\left(\boldsymbol{\theta}\mid\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}}\right)d\boldsymbol{\theta}.

\operatorname{\pi}\left(\boldsymbol{\theta}\mid\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}}\right)
measures our belief that parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}
generated the observations

\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}}
. To derive
\operatorname{\pi}\left(\boldsymbol{\theta}\mid\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}}\right)
, we apply Bayes’ theorem:

\operatorname{\pi}\left(\boldsymbol{\theta}\mid\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}}\right)\propto L(\boldsymbol{\theta};\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}})\times\operatorname{\pi}\left(\boldsymbol{\theta}\right)

where
\operatorname{\pi}\left(\boldsymbol{\theta}\right)
measures our prior belief that the model has parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}
.

Naturally, this leads to the question: How do we specify
\operatorname{\pi}\left(\boldsymbol{\theta}\right)
when
we know nothing particular about
\boldsymbol{\theta}
? Statisticians have grappled with the problem
of specifying so-called noninformative priors ever since Bayes and Laplace
first started applying the approach to the binomial model over 200 years ago.

While noninformative priors continue to be debated, fortunately, the
modern approach of reference priors gives a general path forward and,
particularly, for the case of Gaussian processes works quite well.

Before getting into the details (see §
3
and §
4
for descriptions of
the prior and prediction algorithm), let’s look at how the approach works on the Gaussian process
from Example
1.1
.

How to Specify Noninformative Priors

The goal of a noninformative prior is to represent “minimal information” so that
inference is driven by the data and the model rather than prior knowledge.

Making this goal exact is difficult; and it’s unlikely there will ever be a
universal approach to noninformative priors that’s optimal for all situations, as there
can be multiple reasonable definitions of “minimal information”. However, frequentist coverage
has emerged as one key metric to test whether a candidate noninformative prior is suitable for
objective Bayesian analysis. Here’s the basic idea:
Let
\Theta_{1}\times\cdots\times\Theta_{k}
denote the parameter space for the model,
pick
\alpha
to be something like
0.95
, and run Algorithm
1
for different
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}

varied across the model’s parameter space. If the prior is good, Algorithm
1
should
produce a result close to
\alpha
.

With Algorithm

noninformative priors.

Constant Prior

We begin with the simplest approach: Set
\operatorname{\pi}\left(\boldsymbol{\theta}\right)\propto 1
.
Immediately, we see one serious disadvantage of this approach: It’s not invariant under reparameterization.
If
\varphi(\cdot)
is some strictly increasing function onto
[a,b]
with continuous derivative, then

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}L(\theta;\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}})d\theta=\int_{\varphi^{-1}(a)}^{\varphi^{-1}(b)}L(\varphi(u);\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}})\dot{\varphi}(u)du.

Thus, different parameterizations with the constant prior lead to different posterior distributions.

Still, let’s try the approach out on some examples.

Jeffreys Prior

Dissatisfied with the inconsistency of the constant prior under reparameterization,
Harold Jeffreys searched for a better approach and proposed the prior

\operatorname{\pi}\left(\boldsymbol{\theta}\right)\propto\lvert\boldsymbol{\mathcal{I}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\rvert^{1/2}

where
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{I}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
is the Fisher information matrix,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{I}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})_{st}=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}}}\left\{\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{s}}\log\operatorname{P}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}}\mid\boldsymbol{\theta}\right)\right)\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{t}}\log\operatorname{P}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}}\mid\boldsymbol{\theta}\right)\right)\right\}.

We can check that unlike the constant prior, Jeffreys prior is invariant to reparameterization:
If
\boldsymbol{\varphi(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}})}
is an injective continuously differentiable function whose range includes
\Theta
and
whose Jacobian is never zero on
\boldsymbol{\varphi}^{-1}(\Theta)
, then the
change of variables formula gives us

\displaystyle\int_{\Theta}L(\boldsymbol{\theta};\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}})\lvert\boldsymbol{\mathcal{I}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\rvert^{1/2}d\boldsymbol{\theta}

\displaystyle=\int_{\boldsymbol{\varphi}^{-1}(\Theta)}L(\boldsymbol{\varphi}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}});\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}})\lvert\boldsymbol{\mathcal{I}}(\boldsymbol{\varphi}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}}))\rvert^{1/2}\mid\left(\lvert\boldsymbol{D\varphi}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}})\rvert\right)\mid d\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}},

where
\boldsymbol{D\varphi}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}})
denotes the Jacobian matrix

\boldsymbol{D\varphi}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}})_{st}=\frac{\partial\varphi_{s}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}})}{\partial u_{t}}
.
Let
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{I}}^{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}})
denote the Fisher information matrix with respect to the
reparameterization. Then

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{I}}^{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}})_{st}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}}}\left\{\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial u_{s}}\log\operatorname{P}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}}\mid\boldsymbol{\varphi}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}})\right)\right)\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial u_{t}}\log\operatorname{P}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}}\mid\boldsymbol{\varphi}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}})\right)\right)\right\}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}}}\left\{\left(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\log\operatorname{P}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}}\mid\boldsymbol{\theta}\right)^{\prime}\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\varphi}}{\partial u_{s}}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}})\right)\left(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\log\operatorname{P}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}}\mid\boldsymbol{\theta}\right)^{\prime}\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\varphi}}{\partial u_{t}}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}})\right)\right\}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\varphi}}{\partial u_{s}}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}})\right)^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{I}}(\varphi(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}}))\left(\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\varphi}}{\partial u_{t}}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}})\right).

Thus,

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{I}}^{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}})=\boldsymbol{D\varphi}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}})^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{I}}(\boldsymbol{\varphi}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}}))\boldsymbol{D\varphi}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}})

and

\displaystyle\int_{\Theta}L(\boldsymbol{\theta};\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}})\lvert\boldsymbol{\mathcal{I}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\rvert^{1/2}d\boldsymbol{\theta}

\displaystyle=\int_{\boldsymbol{\varphi}^{-1}(\Theta)}L(\boldsymbol{\varphi}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}});\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}})\lvert\boldsymbol{\mathcal{I}}^{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}}))\rvert^{1/2}d\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}}.

To compute the Fisher information matrix, we first differentiate
\log L(\mu;\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}})
,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\mu}\log L(\mu;\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}})

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial\mu}\left(-\frac{n}{2}\left(\mu-\bar{y}\right)^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=-n\left(\mu-\bar{y}\right).

Then we compute

\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}}}\left\{\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\mu}L(\mu;\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}})\right)^{2}\mid\mu\right\}=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}}}\left\{n^{2}\left(\mu-\bar{y}\right)^{2}\mid\mu\right\}
.

\bar{y}-\mu
is normally distributed with zero mean and variance
\frac{1}{n}
, so

\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}}}\left\{\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\mu}L(\mu;\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}})\right)^{2}\mid\mu\right\}=n
.

So far, Jeffreys prior performs excellently. In fact, for a single parameter,
Welch, Peers 1963

show that in the limiting case, coverage for
\left(1-\alpha\right)\%
credible sets using Jeffreys prior
approaches
\alpha
with an asymptotic error
o(n^{-1})
. Moreover, it’s the only prior with this
property, so starting with the goal of matching coverage naturally leads us to Jeffreys prior.

Let’s check how well Jeffeys prior performs in cases with more than a single variable.

.

Suppose we observe
n
normally distributed values,
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}}
, with unknown mean,
\mu
, and unknown variance,
\sigma^{2}
.
Then

\displaystyle L(\mu,\sigma^{2};\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}})\propto\left(\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\right)^{n/2}\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}}-\mu\boldsymbol{1}\right)^{\prime}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}}-\mu\boldsymbol{1}\right)\right\}.

We differentiate
\log L(\cdot;\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}})
to get

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\mu}\log L(\mu,\sigma^{2};\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}})

\displaystyle=\frac{n}{\sigma^{2}}\left(\bar{y}-\mu\right)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\sigma^{2}}\log L(\mu,\sigma^{2};\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}})

\displaystyle=-\frac{n}{2}\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}+\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\right)^{2}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}}-\mu\boldsymbol{1}\right)^{\prime}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}}-\mu\boldsymbol{1}\right).

We apply the derivations from Example
2.3
and Example
2.4
to
get the Fisher information matrix

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{I}}(\mu,\sigma^{2})=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{n}{\sigma^{2}}&0\\
0&\frac{n}{2\sigma^{4}}\\
\end{pmatrix}

and the Jeffreys prior

\operatorname{\pi}\left(\mu,\sigma^{2}\right)\propto\left(\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\right)^{3/2}
.
Let’s check coverage for
\sigma^{2}
. First, we integrate out
\mu
,

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}L(\mu,\sigma^{2};\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}})

\displaystyle\operatorname{\pi}\left(\mu,\sigma^{2}\right)d\mu

\displaystyle\propto\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\right)^{(n+3)/2}\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\left\|\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}}-\mu\boldsymbol{1}\right\|^{2}\right\}d\mu

\displaystyle\!\begin{multlined}=\left(\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\right)^{(n+3)/2}\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}}-n\bar{y}^{2}\right)\right\}\\

Unfortunately, the multiparameter case is not so easy; and as we see in Example
2.5
,
Jeffreys prior doesn’t perform nearly as well. Jeffreys considered modifications of his prior
to handle the multiparameter case better but never developed a rigorous approach. For that, we turn
to reference priors.

Reference Priors

If Jeffreys prior works well in the single parameter case, why not apply it to
parameters one at a time? In the reference prior approach
(
Berger, Bernardo 1991
)
, we build up a
multiparameter prior by marginalizing the likelihood with a conditional prior
of fewer parameters to form a new integrated likelihood function with only a single
parameter, to which we can apply Jeffreys prior.

Suppose
L(\theta_{1},\theta_{2};\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}})
is a likelihood function of two variables. We fix
\theta_{1}

and use Jeffreys’ approach to derive a conditional prior
\operatorname{\pi}\left(\theta_{2}\mid\theta_{1}\right)
. Then we
integrate out
\theta_{2}
,

\displaystyle L^{I}(\theta_{1};\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}})=\int_{\Theta_{2}}L(\theta_{1},\theta_{2};\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}})\operatorname{\pi}\left(\theta_{2}\mid\theta_{1}\right)d\theta_{2},

to get the integrated likelihood function
L^{I}(\cdot;\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}})
of only a single variable. We apply Jefferys’
approach again to the integrated likelihood function to get
\operatorname{\pi}\left(\theta_{1}\right)
and form the complete
prior

\operatorname{\pi}\left(\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\right)=\operatorname{\pi}\left(\theta_{1}\right)\times\operatorname{\pi}\left(\theta_{2}\mid\theta_{1}\right)
.
If the prior
\operatorname{\pi}\left(\cdot\mid\theta_{1}\right)
is improper, we can choose a sequence of compact subsets

A_{1}\subset A_{2}\subset\cdots\subset\Theta_{2}

such that

\lim_{t\to\infty}A_{t}=\Theta_{2}
,
apply the approach to
A_{t}
, and take the limit as
t\to\infty
.

Let’s try this out on Example
2.5
.

Now, we differentiate
L^{I}(\cdot;\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}})
to find the Fisher information matrix,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\sigma^{2}}\log L^{I}(\sigma^{2};\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}})

\displaystyle=-\frac{n-1}{2\sigma^{2}}+\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\right)^{2}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}}-n\bar{y}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\left\{\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}}-n\bar{y}^{2}\right)-(n-1)\right\}.

.

follows a chi-squared distribution with

\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}[Z]=n-1
,

\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}[Z^{2}]=2(n-1)+(n-1)^{2}

and

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{I}}(\sigma^{2})

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\right)^{2}\left\{\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}[Z^{2}]-2(n-1)\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}[Z]+(n-1)^{2}\right\}

\operatorname{\pi}\left(\mu,\sigma^{2}\right)=\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}

3
Noninformative Priors for Spatial Models

Let’s consider noninformative priors for the Gaussian process (
1
).

•

Using a constant prior isn’t a viable option. In addition to the problem of incoherence,
the resulting posterior would be improper
(
Berger 2006
)
. We might consider truncating the parameter space to
make the constant prior proper, but that doesn’t solve the problem as inference would be
highly dependent on the truncation bounds.

•

Certain modified forms of Jeffreys prior result in a proper posterior, but the credible
sets produced from the priors perform poorly
(
Ren et al. 2012
)
.

That brings us to the reference prior approach. Since the model has multiple parameters,
we’ll first integrate out
\boldsymbol{\beta}
and
\sigma^{2}
using the conditional prior

\operatorname{\pi}\left(\boldsymbol{\beta},\sigma^{2}\mid\ell,\eta\right)\propto\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}
.
Likelihood for Gaussian process (
1
) is given by

\displaystyle L(\boldsymbol{\beta},\sigma^{2},\ell,\eta;\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}})\propto\left(\sigma^{2}\right)^{-n/2}\lvert\boldsymbol{\mathrm{G}}\rvert^{-1/2}\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}}-\boldsymbol{\mathrm{X}}\boldsymbol{\beta}\right)^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{G}}^{-1}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}}-\boldsymbol{\mathrm{X}}\boldsymbol{\beta}\right)\right\}

where

\boldsymbol{\mathrm{X}}=\left(\boldsymbol{x}({\boldsymbol{s}}_{1}),\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}({\boldsymbol{s}}_{n})\right)^{\prime}
,

\boldsymbol{\mathrm{G}}=\eta\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{\mathrm{K}}(\ell)
, and

\boldsymbol{\mathrm{K}}(\ell)_{ij}=\operatorname{\psi}_{\ell}\left(\left\|{\boldsymbol{s}}_{i}-{\boldsymbol{s}}_{j}\right\|\right)
.
Integrating likelihood with the conditional prior gives us

\displaystyle L^{I}(\ell,\eta;\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}})

\displaystyle\propto\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{p}}L(\boldsymbol{\beta},\sigma^{2},\ell,\eta;\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}})\operatorname{\pi}\left(\boldsymbol{\beta},\sigma^{2}\mid\ell,\eta\right)d\boldsymbol{\beta}d\sigma^{2}

\displaystyle\propto\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(\sigma^{2}\right)^{-(n-p)/2}\lvert\boldsymbol{\mathrm{G}}\rvert^{-1/2}\lvert\boldsymbol{\mathrm{X}}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{G}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{X}}\rvert^{-1/2}\exp\left\{-\frac{S^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right\}\left(\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\right)d\sigma^{2}

\displaystyle\propto\lvert\boldsymbol{\mathrm{G}}\rvert^{-1/2}\lvert\boldsymbol{\mathrm{X}}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{G}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{X}}\rvert^{-1/2}\left(S^{2}\right)^{-(n-p)/2}

(3)

where

\displaystyle S^{2}=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{R}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}}\quad\boldsymbol{R}=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{G}}^{-1}-\boldsymbol{\mathrm{G}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{X}}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{X}}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{G}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{X}}\right)^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{X}}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{G}}^{-1}.

(4)

After computing the Fisher information matrix for
L^{I}(\cdot;\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}})
and forming its Jeffrey prior, we
derive the complete prior

\displaystyle\operatorname{\pi}\left(\boldsymbol{\beta},\sigma^{2},\ell,\eta\right)\propto\left(\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\right)\lvert\boldsymbol{\Sigma}(\ell,\eta)\rvert^{1/2}

(5)

where

\boldsymbol{\Sigma}(\ell,\eta)=\begin{pmatrix}\tr\left\{(\boldsymbol{R}\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\mathrm{K}}}{\partial\ell})^{2}\right\}&\tr(\boldsymbol{R}^{2}\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\mathrm{K}}}{\partial\ell})&\tr(\boldsymbol{R}\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\mathrm{K}}}{\partial\ell})\\[6.0pt]
*&\tr(\boldsymbol{R}^{2})&\tr(\boldsymbol{R})\\[6.0pt]
*&*&n-p\\[6.0pt]
\end{pmatrix}.

(6)

For a detailed derivation, see
Ren et al. 2012
.

To test the performance of the reference prior, we’ll run the same simulations used in

. Details of how to compute the integrals will be given in §

4
Deterministic Bayesian Inference

The key component for deterministic prediction and inference is an
accurate approximation to the posterior distribution for
\ell
and
\eta
that enables
efficient computation of integrals,

\operatorname{\tilde{\pi}}\left(\ell,\eta\mid\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}}\right)\approx L^{I}(\ell,\eta;\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}})\times\operatorname{\pi}\left(\ell,\eta\right)

where

\operatorname{\pi}\left(\ell,\eta\right)\propto\lvert\boldsymbol{\Sigma}(\ell,\eta)\rvert^{1/2}

and
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}(\cdot)
is defined in (
6
).

Given
\operatorname{\tilde{\pi}}\left(\cdot\mid\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}}\right)
, it’s relatively straightforward to derive
approximations for the marginal distributions

\displaystyle\operatorname{\pi}\left(\ell\mid\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}}\right)

\displaystyle\approx\int_{0}^{\infty}\operatorname{\tilde{\pi}}\left(\ell,\eta\mid\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}}\right)d\eta,

\displaystyle\operatorname{\pi}\left(\eta\mid\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}}\right)

\displaystyle\approx\int_{0}^{\infty}\operatorname{\tilde{\pi}}\left(\ell,\eta\mid\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}}\right)d\ell,

\displaystyle\operatorname{\pi}\left(\sigma^{2}\mid\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}}\right)

\displaystyle\approx\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\operatorname{P^{\pi}}\left(\sigma^{2}\mid\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}},\ell,\eta\right)\operatorname{\tilde{\pi}}\left(\ell,\eta\mid\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}}\right)d\ell d\eta

and approximations for prediction distributions,

\displaystyle\operatorname{P^{\pi}}\left(Z({\boldsymbol{s}})\mid\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}}\right)

\displaystyle\approx\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\operatorname{P^{\pi}}\left(Z({\boldsymbol{s}})\mid\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}},\ell,\eta\right)\operatorname{\tilde{\pi}}\left(\ell,\eta\mid\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}}\right)d\ell d\eta.

Outline of Algorithm

Assume
\varphi_{\ell}(\cdot)
and
\varphi_{\eta}(\cdot)
are strictly increasing
functions onto
(0,\infty)
with continuous derivatives. Put

\displaystyle f(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}})=\begin{multlined}-\log L^{I}(\varphi_{\ell}(u_{1}),\varphi_{\eta}(u_{2});\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}})\\
-\log\operatorname{\pi}\left(\varphi_{\ell}(u_{1}),\varphi_{\eta}(u_{2})\right)-\log\dot{\varphi}_{\ell}(u_{1})-\log\dot{\varphi_{\eta}}(u_{2}).\end{multlined}

f(\cdot)
is the negative log of the reparameterized posterior
\operatorname{\pi}\left(\ell,\eta\mid\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}}\right)
.
Approximation of
\exp(-f(\cdot))
naturally leads to approximation and integration of

\operatorname{\pi}\left(\ell,\eta\mid\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}}\right)
.

We’ll build an approximation in four steps.

Algorithm 3

Build a multivariate polynomial to approximate
\exp(-f(\cdot))
where
f(\cdot)
(
4
)
is the negative negative log of the reparameterized posterior
function
\operatorname{\pi}\left(\ell,\eta\mid\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}}\right)

1:

Using a trust-region optimizer and exact equations for
\nabla f
and
\nabla^{2}f
,
minimize
f
to find
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}}_{\textrm{map}}
.

2:

Let
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{v}}_{1}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{v}}_{2}
denote two orthonormal eigenvectors of the Hessian at
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}}_{\textrm{map}}
,

\nabla^{2}f(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}}_{\textrm{map}})
.
Find values
a_{1}<0<b_{1}
and
a_{2}<0<b_{2}
such that

\displaystyle-\left(f(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}}_{\textrm{map}}+a_{i}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{v}}_{i})-f(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}}_{\textrm{map}})\right)

\displaystyle=\log\varepsilon_{1}(a_{i})

\displaystyle-\left(f(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}}_{\textrm{map}}+b_{i}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{v}}_{i})-f(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}}_{\textrm{map}})\right)

\displaystyle=\log\varepsilon_{2}(b_{i})

for
i=1,\ 2
and
\varepsilon_{i}(\cdot)
small.
These values bracket
f(\cdot)
around a rectangular region oriented
along the eigenvectors
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{v}}_{1}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{v}}_{2}
that contains most of the probability mass.

3:

Find monotonic cubic splines
s_{1}(\cdot)
and
s_{2}(\cdot)
such that

s_{i}(0)=a_{i}
,
s_{i}(0.5)=0
, and
s_{i}(1)=b_{i}

for
i=1,2
.

4:

Put

\displaystyle g(\boldsymbol{x})=\exp\left\{-\left(f\left(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}}_{\textrm{map}}+s_{1}(x_{1})\boldsymbol{\mathrm{v}}_{1}+s_{2}(x_{2})\boldsymbol{\mathrm{v}}_{2}\right)-f(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}}_{\textrm{map}})\right)\right\}.

(9)

Using Chebyshev nodes and the eigenvectors
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{v}}_{1}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{v}}_{2}
for a basis, adaptively
build a sparse grid and interpolating polynomial to approximate
g(\cdot)
(and hence

\operatorname{\pi}\left(\ell,\eta\mid\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}}\right)
) over the region
[0,1]\times[0,1]
.

Proposition 7 and Proposition 9 from
Ren et al. 2012
show that
\operatorname{\pi}\left(\ell,\eta\mid\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}}\right)
is bounded as

\ell\to 0
or
\eta\to 0
and derive
\mathcal{O}\left(\cdot\right)
functions for when
\ell\to\infty
and
\eta\to\infty
.
Using suitable choices of
\varphi_{\ell}(\cdot)
,
\varphi_{\eta}(\cdot)
, and
\varepsilon_{i}(\cdot)
, we
can achieve bounds for the probability mass outside of the bracketing region in Step
2
.
Following
Gu et al. 2018
, we use the parameterization

\phi_{\ell}(t)=\phi_{\eta}(t)=\exp(t)
.
We’ll only consider the simple case of
\varepsilon_{i}
fixed to some small constant, but other choices could lead to tighter bounding.

We can use any decent root-finding algorithm (e.g., Newton’s method) for Step
2
;
we use the monotonic cubic algorithm from
Fritsch, Carlson 1980
for Step
3
.
Step
1
and Step

are more complicated, and I break them down in greater detail in the next sections.

Step
1
: Trust-region Optimization

Let
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{p}\to\mathbb{R}
denote a twice-differentiable objective function.
Trust-region methods are iterative, second-order optimization algorithms that produce a sequence

\left\{\boldsymbol{x}_{k}\right\}
where the
k
th
iteration is generated by updating the previous iteration
with a solution to the subproblem
(
Sorensen 1982
)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}_{k}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{x}_{k-1}+{\boldsymbol{s}}_{k}

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{s}}_{k}

\displaystyle=\argmin_{\boldsymbol{s}}\left\{\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x}_{k-1})^{\prime}{\boldsymbol{s}}+\frac{1}{2}{\boldsymbol{s}}^{\prime}\nabla^{2}f(\boldsymbol{x}_{k-1}){\boldsymbol{s}}\right\}

\displaystyle\phantom{=}\quad\quad\textrm{such that}\;\left\|{\boldsymbol{s}}\right\|\leq\delta_{k}.

The subproblem minimizes the second-order approximation of
f
at
\boldsymbol{x}_{k-1}
within the
neighborhood
\|\boldsymbol{s}\|\leq\delta_{k}
, called the trust region. Using the trust region,
we can restrict the second-order approximation to areas where it models
f
well. Efficient algorithms
exist to solve the subproblem regardless of whether
\nabla^{2}f(\boldsymbol{x}_{k-1})
is positive-definite,
making trust-region methods well-suited for non-convex optimization problems
(
Moré, Sorensen 1983
)
.
With proper rules for updating
\delta_{k}
and standard assumptions, such as Lipschitz continuity of

\nabla f
, trust-region methods are globally convergent. Moreover, if
\nabla^{2}f
is
Lipschitz continuous for all
\boldsymbol{x}
sufficiently close to a nondegenerate second-order stationary
point
\boldsymbol{x}_{*}
where
\nabla^{2}f(\boldsymbol{x}_{*})
is positive-definite, then trust-region methods
have quadratic local convergence
(
Nocedal, Wright 2006
)
.

Step

: Sparse Grid Approximation

We seek to approximate
g(\cdot)
(
9
) by a polynomial
\tilde{g}(\cdot)
that interpolates

g(\cdot)
at points in
[0,1]\times[0,1]
. If we choose the points well, we can achieve high accuracy
with a minimal number of points, making
\tilde{g}(\cdot)
cheaper to build and evaluate.

The simplest approach would be to interpolate at equispaced points, but polynomials at equispaced
points perform terribly (see Runge’s phenomenon). Much better is to interpolate at Chebyshev nodes.
Polynomials at Chebyshev nodes have excellent approximation performance
(
Trefethen 2019
)
, but interpolating
on a dense grid would still be expensive. We can achieve better efficiency if we interpolate on a sparse grid,
and we can achieve even better efficiency if we adaptively construct the sparse grid to avoid
unnecessary evaluations in areas that can be approximated well by lower-order polynomials.

Put

\displaystyle X^{i}

\displaystyle=\left\{x_{1}^{i},\ldots,x_{m_{i}}^{i}\right\},

\displaystyle m_{i}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}1&\text{if}\ i=0,\\
2^{i-1}+1&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

\displaystyle x_{j}^{i}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2}&\text{if}\ i=0,\\
\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\cos\frac{\pi(j-1)}{m_{i}-1}\right)&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

The Chebyshev-Gauss-Lobatto nodes,
\left\{X^{i}\right\}
, form a nested sequence
of points,
X^{i}\subset X^{i+1}
,
that serve as a building block for constructing interpolations and quadrature
rules for sparse grids
(
Barthelmann et al. 2000
;
Klimke 2006
)
.
Let
\psi_{j}^{i}(\cdot)
denote the unique
(m_{i}-1)
-degree polynomial where

\displaystyle\psi_{j}^{i}(x_{j^{\prime}}^{i})=\begin{cases}1&\text{if}\ j=j^{\prime},\\
0&\text{otherwise};\end{cases}

let
V^{i}
denote the vector space spanned by the basis functions

\left\{\psi_{j}^{i}\right\}
for
j=1,\ldots,m_{i}
;
and define

\Delta V^{0}=V^{0}
,

\Delta V^{i}=V^{i}-V^{i-1}
for
i>0
.
We will build an approximation using functions from vector spaces

\displaystyle W^{\mathcal{I}}=\bigoplus_{{\boldsymbol{i}}\in{\mathcal{I}}}\Delta V^{i_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\Delta V^{i_{d}}

where the index set

is required to be

:
if
{\boldsymbol{i}}\in{\mathcal{I}}
and

, then
{\boldsymbol{i}}-{\boldsymbol{e}}_{k}\in{\mathcal{I}}
. The vector spaces

are
a generalization of Smolyak sparse grids and allow for different dimensions to
have different levels of refinement
(
Gerstner, Griebel 2003
)
.

To build the sparse grid, we follow the algorithm from
Jakeman, Roberts 2011
and greedily
add indexes and nodes with the largest approximation errors until a target accuracy is
achieved. The algorithm adapts by
both dimension and locality.

Define

Marginals

Algorithm
5
gives us an interpolating function that is inexpensive to evaluate and accurately
approximates the
reparameterized posterior

\operatorname{\pi}\left(u_{1},u_{2}\mid\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}}\right)=\operatorname{\pi}\left(\varphi_{\ell}(u_{1}),\varphi_{\eta}(u_{2})\mid\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}}\right)\dot{\varphi}_{\ell}(u_{1})\dot{\varphi}_{\eta}(u_{2})
.
Now,

\displaystyle\operatorname{\pi}\left(u_{1}\mid\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}}\right)

\displaystyle=\int\operatorname{\pi}\left(u_{1},u_{2}\mid\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}}\right)du_{2}

\displaystyle\approx\sum_{k}w_{k}\operatorname{\tilde{\pi}}\left(u_{1},t_{k}\mid\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}}\right),

where
\left\{w_{k}\right\}
and
\left\{t_{k}\right\}
can be chosen by the Gauss-Legendre quadrature rule for the interval
determined by the bracketing region in Step
2
of Algorithm
3
. If we evaluate
\operatorname{\tilde{\pi}}\left(u_{1}\mid\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}}\right)
at Chebyshev nodes
across the range of
u_{1}
in the bracketing region, then we obtain a polynomial that approximates
\operatorname{\pi}\left(u_{1}\mid\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}}\right)
.

\operatorname{\pi}\left(u_{2}\mid\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}}\right)
can be similarly approximated using Chebyshev nodes.

If the error bounds from Step
2
are tight enough, the
polynomial approximations for
\operatorname{\pi}\left(u_{1}\mid\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}}\right)
and
\operatorname{\pi}\left(u_{2}\mid\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}}\right)
will be suitable
for estimating the CDFs. But since they cut off integration outside of the
bracketing region, they won’t accurately capture endpoint behavior and shouldn’t
be used for estimating moments. For example,
\operatorname{\pi}\left(\eta\mid\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}}\right)
has infinite mean,
which won’t be reflected by the polynomial approximation.

6
Discussion

I presented deterministic algorithms for fully Bayesian prediction and inference for spatial models. In
comparison to sampling methods such as MCMC, I would expect the algorithms to provide
more reproducible results and require less tuning, making a more turnkey approach to analysis
possible.

An area of future work could be to extend the algorithms to handle the Gaussian
process models used in model emulation and calibration. In contrast to spatial
Gaussian process models, models for emulation and calibration typically assume each
dimension of the input space has a different scale and use a product covariance function

(
Sacks et al. 1989
;
Gu 2019
)
,

\displaystyle\operatorname{cov}\left\{Z({\boldsymbol{s}}),Z(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}})\right\}

\displaystyle=\sigma^{2}\prod_{k=1}^{d}\psi_{\ell_{k}}(\mid s_{k}-u_{k}\mid).

Paulo 2005
and
Gu, Berger 2016
derived reference priors for
separable covariance functions with distinct length parameters. Provided
d
is not too large, it may be possible
to adopt the algorithms from §
4
to work for this case to achieve deterministic fully
Bayesian results. Additionally, certain modifications, such as using
second-order information at interpolation points or assuming that the posterior is separable along the eigenvectors of its optimum’s Hessian, could make the algorithms more efficient and larger values of
d

possible.

\displaystyle\operatorname{\pi}\left(\ell,\eta\mid\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}}\right)

,
