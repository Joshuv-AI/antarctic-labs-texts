Training Guarantees of Neural Network Classification Two-Sample Tests by Kernel Analysis
Varun Khurana, Xiuyuan Cheng, Alexander Cloninger

Abstract
We construct and analyze a neural network two-sample test to determine whether two datasets came from the same distribution (null hypothesis) or not (alternative hypothesis). We perform time-analysis on a neural tangent kernel (NTK) two-sample test. In particular, we derive the theoretical minimum training time needed to ensure the NTK two-sample test detects a deviation-level between the datasets. Similarly, we derive the theoretical maximum training time before the NTK two-sample test detects a deviation-level. By approximating the neural network dynamics with the NTK dynamics, we extend this time-analysis to the realistic neural network two-sample test generated from time-varying training dynamics and finite training samples. A similar extension is done for the neural network two-sample test generated from time-varying training dynamics but trained on the population. To give statistical guarantees, we show that the statistical power associated with the neural network two-sample test goes to 1 as the neural network training samples and test evaluation samples go to infinity. Additionally, we prove that the training times needed to detect the same deviation-level in the null and alternative hypothesis scenarios are well-separated. Finally, we run some experiments showcasing a two-layer neural network two-sample test on a hard two-sample test problem and plot a heatmap of the statistical power of the two-sample test in relation to training time and network complexity.



1
Introduction

The ability to compare whether two datasets
\widehat{P}\sim p
and
\widehat{Q}\sim q
came from the same data-generating process (i.e. checking if
p=q
or
p\neq q
) is a problem studied for many years. Traditionally, the methods to answer this question are called two-sample tests. As a non-exhaustive list of applications, two-sample testing is widely used in testing drug efficacy
[
12
]
, studying behavioral differences in psychology
[
4
]
, pollution impact studied in environmental science research
[
5
]
, and market research impact studies
[
6
]
. The most basic method to compare distributions is by comparing means with a
t
-test, proportions with a
z
-test, variances with Levene’s test, medians with a Mann-Whitney U test, or overall distributions with a Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. The advent of complex, high-dimensional data in fields like genomics, finance, and social media analytics has exposed limitations in these traditional methods, particularly in terms of handling non-linearity, complex interactions, and the curse of dimensionality. The flexibility and scalability of neural networks make them particularly suited to tackle the challenges posed by modern datasets, suggesting their potential to revolutionize two-sample testing.

This paper is not the first to explore the idea of using neural networks or classifiers for two-sample testing. In particular,
[
23
]
showed properties and analyzed the performance of the so-called Classifier Two-Sample Test (C2ST) and specifically showcasing theoretically what the statistical power of such two-sample tests.
To go further in the neural network direction,
[
9
]
expanded
[
15
]
’s work and used the neural tangent kernel (NTK) for the kernel involved in a maximum mean discrepancy (MMD) problem. Yet their analysis still did not relate the NTK MMD performance to the behavior of neural network classification two-sample tests. Moreover,
[
7
]
introduced a neural network-based two sample test statistic using the classification logit and showed theoretical guarantees for test power for sub-exponential density problems. One may be hesitant to use a neural network for two-sample tests since with a big enough neural network and long enough training time, a neural network could find a separation for data coming from the same distribution. Our approach alleviates this hesitation since we train the neural network on a small time-scale and ensure our network is initialized to output 0 for all values. We also conduct time analysis on two levels. First, we analyze the time needed for achieving a desired level of deviation or detection in the two-sample test. Second, we provide time approximations between different training regimes, which extends the analysis of the time needed for detection to different training regimes.

1.1
Main Contributions

Our main contributions to the field are the following:

1.

We perform some time analysis on the neural tangent kernel (NTK) derived from our neural network and show that the time it takes for the neural network two-sample test to learn does
not
depend on the entire spectrum of the NTK but rather only a subset of the spectrum on which the labels or witness function
f^{*}=\frac{p-q}{p+q}
non-trivially projects onto. This behavior is a result of averaging behavior of the neural network two-sample test.

2.

We approximate the population-level neural network dynamics and finite-sample neural network dynamics with the population-level NTK dynamics. This allows the time analysis performed on the NTK dynamics to transfer to the other two training regimes. Additionally, we notice here that there is a balancing act of not training the neural network too long so that the the approximations hold but long enough to detect differences in the datasets. This balancing act is further informed by the complexity of the neural network considered in relation to the difficulty of the two-sample test problem.

The efficacy of neural network-based two-sample tests hinges on the dynamics of training and network complexity. A key insight is that as soon as gradient descent begins, the two-sample test becomes significantly more effective. To illustrate this, we work out a toy example in

Previous Works and Our Work

2.1
Previous Works

Classification and two-sample testing.

As mentioned, classifier two-sample tests are not new
[
15
,
23
,
14
]
. These approaches demonstrated that a well-trained classifier could effectively identify distributional discrepancies. More traditional two-sample methods use methods such as kernel two-sample test and maximum mean discrepancy (MMD)
[
15
,
8
,
26
,
19
]
but adhere to the classification two-sample test spirit. More recently, there has been growing interest in developing two-sample tests based on neural networks, leveraging the power of deep learning to address some of the limitations of classical two-sample tests
[
22
,
7
,
20
,
11
]
.
[
3
]
applied MMD to generative adversarial netowrks (GANs) for imporoved distribution comparison. Kernel MMD was also extended to the neural tangent kernel (NTK) for two-sample testing by
[
9
]
. Variations of a neural network-based two-sample test are present in
[
18
]
and
[
7
]
. The analysis done in this paper goes further by using small time approximations between the NTK-based kernel machines and the actual neural network training dynamics. To accomplish this, we use very similar proof techniques to
[
25
]
, but rather than using a loss rescaling to get into the lazy training regime
[
10
]
, we are able to use small time approximations for the NTK.

Neural Tangent Kernels.

We heavily use the neural tangent kernel (NTK) in this paper for tractable analysis which is extended to realistic dynamics for the actual trained neural network. The concept of NTKs comes from the seminal work of
[
17
]
, who demonstrated that training dynamics of a neural network can be approximated by a kernel method, where the kernel is the NTK. Many studies have extended and refined NTK theory since the seminal work.
[
2
]
extended the NTK analysis to convolutional neural networks and showed how NTKs evolve during training. Other works
[
21
,
13
,
1
,
24
,
10
]
have studied the hierarchical structure of NTKs, convergence guarantees for training over-parameterized neural networks, and implicit regularization effects of NTKs. Many of these works simplify the NTK with either a large-width assumption or a small-time assumption where the NTK dynamics and neural network dynamics are similar. For this paper, our neural network must be large enough to capture the target function
f^{*}
but small enough to ensure small-time approximation holds between the NTK and the true neural network.

2.2
Difference of Our Work from Previous NTK Literature

Our NTK analysis introduces a novel perspective by balancing time scales and network complexity, distinguishing it from traditional NTK analysis. Specifically, we focus on the small-time regime to assess the two-sample test, which aims to identify when the alternative hypothesis is correct. Our approach relates the training time to the complexity of the zero-time NTK and the projection of the weighted difference of densities
\frac{p-q}{p+q}(x)
onto the zero-time NTK’s eigenfunctions. We analyze both population-level and finite-sample dynamics in relation to the zero-time NTK, ensuring our approximations hold within this short-time regime. This delicate balance depends on the problem’s complexity and the neural network’s complexity.

Consider the scenario where the densities
p
and
q
are significantly different. If the zero-time NTK’s larger eigenvalue functions correspond to low-frequency eigenfunctions, the two-sample test can produce positive results quickly since
\frac{p-q}{p+q}
will likely project onto low-frequency eigenfunctions. Conversely, when
p
and
q
are similar,
\frac{p-q}{p+q}
projects onto higher frequency eigenfunctions, requiring more time or a larger neural network for detection.

Moreover, balancing short-time and long-time scales necessitates managing neural network complexity. The complexity must be sufficient to capture
\frac{p-q}{p+q}
on the NTK’s eigenbasis, but the estimation accuracy of the zero-time NTK decreases with larger networks. Fortunately, the approximation depends on the product of neural network complexity and time, which allows for good detection even with small time scales, counteracting approximation losses.

Our empirical results span neural networks with various parameter-to-sample ratios, from severely under-parameterized to highly over-parameterized regimes, demonstrating the robustness of our NTK analysis in different settings.

and

3.1
Motivating Example

For our motivating example two-sample test scenario, we consider when our probability distributions of interest are two multivariate normals with the same covariance matrix but different means. In particular, with a fixed covariance matrix
\Sigma
, we let
p\sim N(\mu_{1},\Sigma)
and
q\sim N(\mu_{2},\Sigma)
with labels
1
and
-1
respectively. Assume that we work with a linear neural network given by

\displaystyle f(x;a,W,b)=\frac{1}{M_{\Theta}}a^{\top}\Big(Wx+b\Big)

where
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},a\in\mathbb{R}^{M_{\Theta}}
,
W\in\mathbb{R}^{M_{\Theta}\times d}
, and
b\in\mathbb{R}^{M_{\Theta}}
. For ease assume that
M_{\Theta}
is even, then for initialization, let
b=0
and opt to make
a_{i}=1
for
i\leq M_{\Theta}/2
and
a_{i}=-1
otherwise. For
W
, we will generate a random matrix
\widetilde{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{(M_{\Theta}/2)\times d}
and let
W=\begin{bmatrix}\widetilde{W}\\
-\widetilde{W}\end{bmatrix}
. These choices will ensure that
f(x)=0
for all
x
.

Recall the gradient of
f
with respect to its parameters is given by

\displaystyle\frac{\partial f}{\partial a}(x)=\frac{1}{M_{\Theta}}(Wx+b)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial f}{\partial W}(x)=\frac{1}{M_{\Theta}}ax^{\top}

\displaystyle\frac{\partial f}{\partial b}(x)=\frac{1}{M_{\Theta}}a.

We will show that just one population-level gradient descent step with this setup will allow the two-sample test to detect the difference in distributions with high probability. In particular, recall that with our initialization

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\widehat{L}(\theta_{0})}{\partial f}(x)=\begin{cases}-1&x\sim p\\
1&x\sim q\end{cases}.

Now with learning rate
\eta
, one gradient descent step gives us

\displaystyle a^{(1)}

\displaystyle=a-\eta\int\frac{\partial f}{\partial a}(x)\frac{\partial\widehat{L}}{\partial f}(x)d(p+q)(x)

\displaystyle=a-\eta\int\frac{1}{M_{\Theta}}Wxd(q-p)(x)=a-\frac{\eta}{M_{\Theta}}W(\mu_{2}-\mu_{1})

\displaystyle W^{(1)}

\displaystyle=W-\eta\int\frac{\partial f}{\partial W}(x)\frac{\partial\widehat{L}}{\partial f}(x)d(p+q)(x)

\displaystyle=W-\eta\int\frac{1}{M_{\Theta}}ax^{\top}d(q-p)(x)=W-\frac{\eta}{M_{\Theta}}a(\mu_{2}-\mu_{1})^{\top}

\displaystyle b^{(1)}

\displaystyle=b-\eta\int\frac{\partial f}{\partial b}(x)\frac{\partial\widehat{L}}{\partial f}(x)d(p+q)(x)

\displaystyle=b-\eta\int\frac{1}{M_{\Theta}}ad(q-p)(x)=b-0=0.

This means that after the first gradient descent step, we have

\displaystyle f(x;a^{(1)},W^{(1)},0)

\displaystyle=\bigg(a-\frac{\eta}{M_{\Theta}}W(\mu_{2}-\mu_{1})\bigg)^{\top}\Bigg(\bigg(W-\frac{\eta}{M_{\Theta}}a(\mu_{2}-\mu_{1})^{\top}\bigg)x\Bigg)

\displaystyle=\frac{\eta}{M_{\Theta}}\bigg(\|a\|^{2}\langle\mu_{1}-\mu_{2},x\rangle+\langle W(\mu_{1}-\mu_{2}),Wx\rangle

\displaystyle+\frac{\eta}{M_{\Theta}}\langle a,W(\mu_{2}-\mu_{1})\rangle\langle\mu_{2}-\mu_{1},x\rangle\bigg).

Now notice that if we consider the two-sample test

\displaystyle\int f(x;a^{(1)},W^{(1)},0)d(p-q)(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{\eta}{M_{\Theta}}\bigg(\|a\|^{2}\|\mu_{1}-\mu_{2}\|^{2}+\|W(\mu_{1}-\mu_{2})\|

\displaystyle+\frac{\eta}{M_{\Theta}}\langle a,W(\mu_{1}-\mu_{2})\rangle\|\mu_{1}-\mu_{2}\|\bigg).

In essence, if
\eta
is small enough the two-sample test will be positive and the farther
\mu_{1}
is away from
\mu_{2}
, the easier it becomes to detect.

In the case that we have
W
fixed and
M_{\Theta}
is large, we can see that
a
is trying to learn
W(\mu_{1}-\mu_{2})
. From a qualitative point of view, we only really need one row
w
of
W
to form a hyperplane that separates
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
, assuming that
0,\mu_{1},\mu_{2}
do not all fall on the same line (and
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
are not on opposite sides of 0). Moreover, the larger we pick
M_{\Theta}
, the random matrix
W
gets a greater probability of producing such a row
w
. Moreover, producing such a
w
becomes increasingly more likely when we center the data so that the origin is between the two means.

3.2
Relating Finite-sample and Population-level Loss

We now revert to the more general case of neural networks considered and recall the form of
\widehat{L}(\theta)
. Notice that when
n_{p},n_{q}\to\infty
, we get a population-level loss given by

\displaystyle L(\theta)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\big(f(x,\theta)-1\big)^{2}p(x)dx+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\big(f(x,\theta)+1\big)^{2}q(x)dx\bigg)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\big(f(x,\theta)^{2}p(x)-2f(x,\theta)p(x)+p(x)

\displaystyle+f(x,\theta)^{2}q(x)+2f(x,\theta)q(x)+q(x)\big)dx\bigg)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\big(f(x,\theta)^{2}-2f(x,\theta)\underbrace{\frac{p(x)-q(x)}{p(x)+q(x)}}_{f^{*}((x)}+1\big)(p(x)+q(x))dx\bigg).

Here we can notice that

\displaystyle\|f(\cdot,\theta)-f^{*}(\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}^{2}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\big(f(x,\theta)^{2}-2f(x,\theta)f^{*}(x)+(f^{*}(x))^{2}\big)(p(x)+q(x))dx.

If we add the constant

\displaystyle C=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}4\frac{p(x)q(x)}{p(x)+q(x)}dx,

we get that

\displaystyle L(\theta)=\frac{1}{2}\|f(\cdot,\theta)-f^{*}(\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}^{2}+C.

To see this, notice that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Big((f^{*}(x))^{2}(p(x)+q(x))+\frac{4p(x)q(x)}{p(x)+q(x)}\Big)dx

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Big(\frac{(p(x)-q(x))^{2}}{(p(x)+q(x))^{2}}(p(x)+q(x))+\frac{4p(x)q(x)}{p(x)+q(x)}\Big)dx

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Big(\frac{p(x)^{2}-2p(x)q(x)+q(x)^{2}+4p(x)q(x)}{p(x)+q(x)}\Big)dx

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Big(\frac{(p(x)+q(x))^{2}}{p(x)+q(x)}\Big)dx=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}p(x)+q(x)dx.

This means that minimizing
L(\theta)
is the same as minimizing
\|f-f^{*}\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}^{2}
as the constant doesn’t depend on
\theta
. Importantly, this means that our target function in the population-level training regimes will be

\displaystyle f^{*}(x):=\frac{p(x)-q(x)}{p(x)+q(x)}.

3.3
Two-Sample Test

Given probability densities
p
and
q
, the two-sample test assesses whether to accept the null hypothesis
H_{0}
or reject it for
H_{1}
, where

\displaystyle H_{0}:p=q,\hskip 85.35826ptH_{1}:p\neq q.

In words, our test is constructed using the average output of the neural network on measure
p
minus the average output of the neural network on measure
q
. This will give either population-level two-sample tests or finite-sample two-sample tests on the datasets
X_{test}
and
Z_{test}
. In particular, for the population-level statistic, we can define

\displaystyle\mu_{p}(\theta)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x,\theta)dp(x),\hskip 8.5359pt\mu_{q}(\theta)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x,\theta)dq(x)

\displaystyle T(\theta;p,q)=(\mu_{p}(\theta)-\mu_{q}(\theta))=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x,\theta)d(p-q)(x);

whereas, for the finite-sample statistic on
test
data, we can define

\displaystyle\mu_{\widehat{p}_{test}}(\theta)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x,\theta)d\widehat{p}_{test}(x),\hskip 8.5359pt\mu_{\widehat{q}_{test}}(\theta)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x,\theta)d\widehat{q}_{test}(x)

\displaystyle T(\theta;\widehat{p}_{test},\widehat{q}_{test})=(\mu_{\widehat{p}_{test}}(\theta)-\mu_{\widehat{q}_{test}}(\theta))=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x,\theta)d(\widehat{p}_{test}-\widehat{q}_{test})(x).

Here, we define the neural network two-sample test for a neural network
f(\cdot,\theta)
by
T(\theta;\widehat{p}_{test},\widehat{q}_{test})
. Given a test threshold
\tau>0
, we reject the null hypothesis if
|T(\theta;\widehat{p}_{test},\widehat{q}_{test})|>\tau
. Moreover, we control the false discovery of the null by finding the smallest
\tau
such that
\text{Pr}[|T(\theta;\widehat{p}_{test},\widehat{q}_{test})|>\tau|H_{0}]\leq\alpha
, where
0<\alpha<1
is the significance level. To find
\tau
, we use a permutation test.

In
Section

4
, we will consider different training regimes and each of these training regimes will have different notions of the two-sample test, which change by what the output of the neural network is and which probability measures the two-sample test statistic is computed on. Particularly, the training regime with the zero-time NTK will end up using not the neural network by the function that is trained under zero-time NTK dynamics. The specific notation regarding the two-sample test will be discussed there.

The Three Training Dynamics

We will consider the following three different training dynamics for our neural network. For all of scenarios, however, we assume the following.

Assumption 4.1
.

The neural network is initialized with parameters
\theta_{0}
such that
f(x,\theta_{0})=0
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

4.1
Realistic Dynamics

These finite-sample time-varying dynamics are the most realistic as these are the dynamics that will arise in practice. Here, we tend to denote all the associated quantities with a hat. Since we use gradient descent to optimize, we denote the parameters trained from finite-sample data as
\widehat{\theta}(t)
and will denote the associated neural network’s output as
\widehat{u}(x,t)=f(x,\widehat{\theta}(t))
. Now, let us inspect the equation used to optimize the parameters.

\displaystyle\widehat{L}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\big(f(x,\theta)-1\big)^{2}\widehat{p}(x)dx+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\big(f(x,\theta)+1\big)^{2}\widehat{q}(x)dx\bigg)

\displaystyle-\dot{\widehat{\theta}}(t)

\displaystyle=\partial_{\theta}\widehat{L}(\widehat{\theta}(t))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\nabla_{\theta}f(x,\widehat{\theta}(t))\Big(\big(f(x,\widehat{\theta}(t))-1\big)\widehat{p}(x)+\big(f(x,\widehat{\theta}(t))+1\big)\widehat{q}(x)\Big)dx\bigg)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\widehat{u}(x,t)

\displaystyle=\langle\nabla_{\theta}f(x,\widehat{\theta}(t)),\dot{\widehat{\theta}}\rangle_{\Theta}=-\langle\nabla_{\theta}f(x,\widehat{\theta}(t)),\partial_{\theta}\widehat{L}(\widehat{\theta}(t))\rangle_{\Theta}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\langle\nabla_{\theta}f(x,\widehat{\theta}(t)),\nabla_{\theta}f(x^{\prime},\widehat{\theta}(t))\rangle_{\Theta}\Big(\big(f(x^{\prime},\widehat{\theta}(t))-1\big)\widehat{p}(x^{\prime})

\displaystyle+\big(f(x^{\prime},\widehat{\theta}(t))+1\big)\widehat{q}(x^{\prime})\Big)dx^{\prime}\bigg).

We define the time-varying finite-sample neural tangent kernel by

\displaystyle\widehat{K}_{t}(x,x^{\prime})=\langle\nabla_{\theta}f(x,\widehat{\theta}(t)),\nabla_{\theta}f(x^{\prime},\widehat{\theta}(t))\rangle_{\Theta}.

We can further define density-specific residuals

\displaystyle\widehat{e}_{p}(x,t)=\big(f(x,\widehat{\theta}(t))-1\big)

\displaystyle\widehat{e}_{q}(x,t)=\big(f(x,\widehat{\theta}(t))+1\big).

This means that

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\widehat{u}(x,t)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\widehat{K}_{t}(x,x^{\prime})\Big(\big(f(x^{\prime},\widehat{\theta}(t))-1\big)\widehat{p}(x^{\prime})+\big(f(x^{\prime},\widehat{\theta}(t))+1\big)\widehat{q}(x^{\prime})\Big)dx^{\prime}\bigg)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\Big(\mathbb{E}_{x^{\prime}\sim\widehat{p}}\widehat{K}_{t}(x,x^{\prime})\widehat{e}_{p}(x^{\prime},t)+\mathbb{E}_{x^{\prime}\sim\widehat{q}}\widehat{K}_{t}(x,x^{\prime})\widehat{e}_{q}(x^{\prime},t)\Big).

In the context of the two sample test, since this training regime uses only finite training samples, we will study the two-sample test statistic’s behavior evaluated on the training samples, the test samples (
independent
from the training samples), and the population. We denote these different evaluated test statistics by

\displaystyle\widehat{T}_{train}(t):

\displaystyle=T(\widehat{\theta}(t);\widehat{p},\widehat{q})=\Big(\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\widehat{p}}-\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\widehat{q}}\Big)f(x,\widehat{\theta}(t))=\Big(\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\widehat{p}}-\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\widehat{q}}\Big)\widehat{u}(x,t),

\displaystyle\widehat{T}_{test}(t):

\displaystyle=T(\widehat{\theta}(t);\widehat{p}_{test},\widehat{q}_{test})=\Big(\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\widehat{p}_{test}}-\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\widehat{q}_{test}}\Big)f(x,\widehat{\theta}(t))

\displaystyle=\Big(\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\widehat{p}_{test}}-\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\widehat{q}_{test}}\Big)\widehat{u}(x,t),

\displaystyle\widehat{T}_{pop}(t):

\displaystyle=T(\widehat{\theta}(t);p,q)=\Big(\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p}-\mathbb{E}_{x\sim q}\Big)f(x,\widehat{\theta}(t))=\Big(\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p}-\mathbb{E}_{x\sim q}\Big)\widehat{u}(x,t),

where
\widehat{T},\widehat{T}_{test},\widehat{T}_{pop}
denote the evaluation on the training samples, test samples, and population, respectively.

4.2
Population Dynamics

These population dynamics are essentially what would happen as the number of samples in our datasets grows larger and larger, since here we train on the population yet still have time-varying dynamics. In this case, we denote the path of the parameters as simply
\theta(t)
and will denote the associated neural network’s output as
u(x,t)=f(x,\theta(t))
. We showed earlier that the population-level loss is equivalent to simply minimizing
\mathcal{L}(\theta)=\frac{1}{2}\|f(\cdot,\theta)-f^{*}(\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}
. This means that training the population-level neural network is equivalent to running gradient descent on
\mathcal{L}
. Moreover, we can define the population level error function as
e(x,t)=f(x,\theta(t))-f^{*}(x)
. Using these facts, we get the following

\displaystyle-\dot{\theta}(t)

\displaystyle=\partial_{\theta}\mathcal{L}(\theta(t))=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\nabla_{\theta}f(x,\theta(t))\big(f(x,\theta(t))-f^{*}(x)\big)(p+q)(x)dx

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u(x,t)

\displaystyle=\langle\nabla_{\theta}f(x,\theta(t)),\dot{\theta}(t)\rangle_{\Theta}=-\langle\nabla_{\theta}f(x,\theta(t)),\partial_{\theta}\mathcal{L}(\theta(t))\rangle_{\Theta}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\langle\nabla_{\theta}f(x,\theta(t)),\nabla_{\theta}f(x^{\prime},\theta(t))\rangle_{\Theta}\big(f(x^{\prime},\theta(t))-f^{*}(x^{\prime})\big)(p+q)(x^{\prime})dx^{\prime}.

Now we define the population level time-varying neural tangent kernel

\displaystyle K_{t}(x,x^{\prime})=\langle\nabla_{\theta}f(x,\theta(t)),\nabla_{\theta}f(x^{\prime},\theta(t))\rangle_{\Theta}.

This finally implies that

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u(x,t)=-\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{x^{\prime}\sim p+q}K_{t}(x,x^{\prime})e(x^{\prime},t)=\partial_{t}e(x,t).

Contrary to the realistic dynamics, we only care about the population-level two-sample test statistic with these population dynamics since these dynamics trains on the population itself. We denote the two-sample test statistic associated to these population-level time-varying dynamics by

\displaystyle T(t):=T(\theta(t);p,q)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u(x,t)d(p-q)(x),

where we are integrating the neural network output over the entire densities.

4.3
Zero-time Auxiliary Dynamics

The zero-time auxiliary dynamics are characterized by training on the population but having the dynamics driven by the zero-time NTK. With these zero-time auxiliary dynamics, we will denote related quantities with a bar so that the output of the trained function here becomes
\bar{u}(x,t)
with
\bar{u}(x,0)=f(x,\theta_{0})
. At this point, consider the zero-time NTK of the neural network
f(\cdot,\theta_{0})
by

\displaystyle K_{0}(x,x^{\prime})=\langle\nabla_{\theta}f(x,\theta_{0}),\nabla_{\theta}f(x^{\prime},\theta_{0})\rangle_{\Theta}.

According to
[
25
, Lemma 4.3]
, assuming that
\|\nabla_{\theta}f(x,\theta_{0})\|_{\Theta}
is squared integrable on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
against measure
(p(x)+q(x))dx
, the zero-time kernel can act as a kernel integral operator and admits a spectral decomposition, which we can write as

\displaystyle(L_{K_{0}}g)(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}K_{0}(x,x^{\prime})g(x^{\prime})(p+q)(x^{\prime})dx^{\prime}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{M}\lambda_{\ell}\langle g,u_{\ell}\rangle_{L^{2}(p+q)}u_{\ell}(x),

where
\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\dotsc\geq\lambda_{M}
. Although we can find an extended basis for
\ell>M
for
L^{2}(p+q)
, the associated eigenvalues of
K_{0}
are 0 on eigenfunctions
u_{\ell}
for
\ell>M
. Since
K_{0}
does not effectively have full basis for
L^{2}(p+q)
, the quantities that we work with will need to be projected onto the range of the operator
L_{K_{0}}
. This motivates the following definition.

Definition 4.2
.

Denote the projection operator onto the range of
L_{K_{0}}
by
\Pi_{K_{0}}
.

Now we can define the associated error function

\displaystyle\bar{e}(x,t)=\bar{u}(x,t)-\Pi_{K_{0}}(f^{*})(x).

Since
f^{*}
is fixed, the dynamics of our model that we care about will be given by

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\bar{u}(x,t)=-\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{x^{\prime}\sim p+q}K_{0}(x,x^{\prime})\bar{e}(x^{\prime},t)=\partial_{t}\bar{e}(x,t).

With the simplicity in the model dynamics, we can attain better analysis. Using this interpretation, we know that

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\bar{e}(\cdot,t)=-\frac{1}{2}(L_{K_{0}}\bar{e}(\cdot,t))=-\sum_{\ell=1}^{M}\lambda_{\ell}\langle\bar{e}(\cdot,t),u_{\ell}\rangle_{L^{2}(p+q)}u_{\ell}.

We formulate an ansatz of what
\bar{e}(\cdot,t)
would be so that it satisfies this differential equation. In particular, consider

\displaystyle\bar{e}(\cdot,t)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{M}e^{-t\lambda_{\ell}}\langle u_{\ell},\bar{e}(\cdot,0)\rangle_{L^{2}(p+q)}u_{\ell}.

The following proposition ensures that this indeed is a solution of the differential equation of interest.

Proposition 4.3

(Zero-Time Auxiliary Dynamics Solution)
.

The solution

\displaystyle\bar{e}(\cdot,t)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{M}e^{-t\lambda_{\ell}}\langle u_{\ell},\bar{e}(\cdot,0)\rangle u_{\ell}

solves the differential equation

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\bar{e}(\cdot,t)=-\frac{1}{2}(L_{K_{0}}\bar{e}(\cdot,t))=-\sum_{\ell=1}^{M}\lambda_{\ell}\langle\bar{e}(\cdot,t),u_{\ell}\rangle_{L^{2}(p+q)}u_{\ell}.

The proof of
Proposition

4.3
is given in
Appendix

A
.

For these zero-time auxiliary dynamics, we can now talk about the two-sample test statistic. In particular, recalling that
\bar{u}=\bar{e}+\Pi_{K_{0}}(f^{*})
, we will set

\displaystyle\overline{\mu_{p}}(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\bar{u}(x,t)dp(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(\bar{e}(x,t)+\Pi_{K_{0}}(f^{*})(x))p(x)dx,

\displaystyle\overline{\mu_{p}}(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{X}\bar{u}(x,t)dq(x)=\int_{X}(\bar{e}(x,t)+\Pi_{K_{0}}(f^{*})(x))q(x)dx.

Similar to the population (population-training data time-varying) dynamics, we only care about the population-level two-sample test statistic for this training regime since we use the entire population for training. For this training regime, we will denote the associated two-sample test by

\displaystyle\overline{T}(t):=\overline{\mu_{p}}(t)-\overline{\mu_{q}}(t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\bar{u}(x,t)d(p-q)(x).

Note that in this case,
\overline{T}(t)
is not determined by the parameters of the neural network changing but rather by the output of the NTK trained dynamics. With this in mind, we get the following lemma.

Lemma 4.4

(Zero-Time Auxiliary Statistic)
.

The population-level zero-time kernel dynamics two-sample test statistic is given by

\displaystyle\overline{T}(t)

\displaystyle=\|\Pi_{K_{0}}(f^{*})\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}^{2}-\sum_{\ell\geq 1}e^{-t\lambda_{\ell}}\langle u_{\ell},\Pi_{K_{0}}(f^{*})\rangle_{L^{2}(p+q)}^{2}.

The proof of
Lemma

4.4
is in
Appendix

A
.

Note that there is some time-analysis we can undertake at this point. First, you can notice that at
t=0
,

\displaystyle\overline{T}(0)=\|\Pi_{K_{0}}(f^{*})\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}^{2}-\underbrace{\sum_{\ell\geq 1}\langle u_{\ell},\Pi_{K_{0}}(f^{*})\rangle_{L^{2}(p+q)}^{2}}_{\|\Pi_{K_{0}}(f^{*})\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}^{2}}=0,

and for any time
t>0
, we have
\overline{T}(t)>0
. We will find a theoretical minimum time
t(\epsilon)
such that
\overline{T}(t(\epsilon))\geq\epsilon
. Let us define a few quantities before delving into the main result.

Definition 4.5
.

Let
S\subseteq\{1,\dotsc,M\}
, then we can consider how much of the norm
\|\Pi_{K_{0}}(f^{*})\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}^{2}
(and hence the norm of
f^{*}
) lies on the eigenbasis subset
V_{S}=\{u_{\ell}\}_{\ell\in S}
. In particular, we have

\displaystyle\|\Pi_{K_{0}}(f^{*})\|_{S}^{2}=\|f^{*}\|_{S}^{2}=\sum_{\ell\in S}\langle u_{\ell},f^{*}\rangle_{L^{2}(p+q)}^{2}=x_{S}\|f^{*}\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}^{2}.

Since
\langle u_{\ell},\Pi_{K_{0}}(f^{*})\rangle_{L^{2}(p+q)}=\langle u_{\ell},f^{*}\rangle_{L^{2}(p+q)}
for
\ell\leq M
, we can also define these quantities for
f^{*}
rather than just
\Pi_{K_{0}}(f^{*})
. Moreover, we can define the minimum and maximum eigenvalue that exist for the eigenvectors that lie in
V_{S}
by defining

\displaystyle\lambda_{\min}(S)=\min_{\ell\in S}\lambda_{\ell},\hskip 14.22636pt\lambda_{\max}(S)=\max_{\ell\in S}\lambda_{\ell}.

Then we have the following theorem.

Proposition 4.6

(Minimum Detection Time – Zero-Time Auxiliary)
.

Let
\epsilon>0
and assume that there exists a finite subset
S\subset\{1,\dotsc,M\}
such that

\displaystyle\|\Pi_{K_{0}}(f^{*})\|_{S}^{2}=\|f^{*}\|_{S}^{2}=\sum_{\ell\in S}\langle u_{\ell},f^{*}\rangle_{L^{2}(p+q)}^{2}>\epsilon

and
\lambda_{\min}(S)>0
. Then

\displaystyle t(\epsilon)\geq\lambda_{\min}(S)\log\bigg(\frac{\|\Pi_{K_{0}}(f^{*})\|_{S}^{2}}{\|\Pi_{K_{0}}(f^{*})\|_{S}^{2}-\epsilon}\bigg)=\lambda_{\min}(S)\log\bigg(\frac{\|f^{*}\|_{S}^{2}}{\|f^{*}\|_{S}^{2}-\epsilon}\bigg)

ensures that
\overline{T}(t(\epsilon))\geq\epsilon
.

The proof of
Proposition

4.6
is in
Appendix

A
.

Remark 4.7
.

Let us analyze the function

\displaystyle g(\epsilon,S)

\displaystyle=\min_{S\in\mathcal{S}_{1}(\epsilon)}\lambda_{\min}(S)\log\bigg(\frac{\|\Pi_{K_{0}}(f^{*})\|_{S}^{2}}{\|\Pi_{K_{0}}(f^{*})\|_{S}^{2}-\epsilon}\bigg).

Notice first that the largest that
\epsilon
can be is
\|\Pi_{K_{0}}(f^{*})\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}^{2}
because as
t\to\infty
, we get that
\overline{T}(t)\to\|\Pi_{K_{0}}(f^{*})\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}^{2}
and it is easy to see that
\overline{T}(t)
is monotonic in
t
. Now notice that as
\epsilon
gets larger, we need
S
to satisfy
\|f^{*}\|_{S}^{2}=\|\Pi_{K_{0}}(f^{*})\|_{S}^{2}>\epsilon
to make sure that
g(\epsilon,S)
is well-defined. Moreover, we need
\lambda_{\min}(S)>0
otherwise we find that
g(\epsilon,S)=0
which is the trivial bound. In particular, we will want the fraction
\epsilon/\|\Pi_{K_{0}}(f^{*})\|_{S}^{2}
to be as small as possible to give the smallest possible non-trivial time.

We have an analogous statement for when we want our test statistic to be less than
\epsilon
. In particular, we get that

Proposition 4.8

(Maximum Undetectable Time – Zero-Time Auxiliary)
.

Let
\epsilon>0
and assume that there exists a finite subset
S\subset\mathbb{N}
such that

\displaystyle\frac{\|\Pi_{K_{0}}(f^{*})\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}^{2}-\epsilon}{\|\Pi_{K_{0}}(f^{*})\|_{S}^{2}}>0

and
\lambda_{\max}(S)>0
. Then

\displaystyle t(\epsilon)\leq\lambda_{\max}(S)\log\Bigg\{\frac{\|\Pi_{K_{0}}(f^{*})\|_{S}^{2}}{\|\Pi_{K_{0}}(f^{*})\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}^{2}-\epsilon}\Bigg\}

ensures that
\overline{T}(t(\epsilon))\leq\epsilon
.

The proof of
Proposition

4.8
is in
Appendix

A
. Now if we optimize over all such subsets
S
, we get the following corollary.

Corollary 4.9

(Detection Times – Zero-Time Auxiliary)
.

Let
0<\epsilon<\|\Pi_{K_{0}}(f^{*})\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}^{2}
. Assume that the set

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{1}(\epsilon)

\displaystyle=\{S\subset\mathbb{N}:\|\Pi_{K_{0}}(f^{*})\|_{S}^{2}>\epsilon,\lambda_{\min}(S)>0\}\neq\emptyset

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{2}(\epsilon)

\displaystyle=\{S\subset\mathbb{N}:(\|\Pi_{K_{0}}(f^{*})\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}^{2}-\epsilon)/\|\Pi_{K_{0}}(f^{*})\|_{S}^{2}>0,\lambda_{\max}(S)>0\}\neq\emptyset.

Then

\displaystyle t\geq t_{1}^{*}(\epsilon):=\min_{S\in\mathcal{S}_{1}(\epsilon)}\lambda_{\min}(S)\log\Bigg(\frac{\|\Pi_{K_{0}}(f^{*})\|_{S}^{2}}{\|\Pi_{K_{0}}(f^{*})\|_{S}^{2}-\epsilon}\Bigg)

ensures that
\overline{T}(t)\geq\epsilon
whilst

\displaystyle t\leq t_{2}^{*}(\epsilon):=\max_{S\in\mathcal{S}_{2}(\epsilon)}\lambda_{\max}(S)\log\Bigg(\frac{\|\Pi_{K_{0}}(f^{*})\|_{S}^{2}}{\|\Pi_{K_{0}}(f^{*})\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}^{2}-\epsilon}\Bigg)

ensures that
\overline{T}(t)\leq\epsilon
.

Here let us remark what occurs in the case when our null hypothesis is correct versus when the alternative is correct.

Remark 4.10
.

If
H_{0}
is true (so that
p=q
), then
f^{*}=0
. We can’t apply the theorem above then since the assumption is not satisfied; however, we note by inspection that
\overline{T}(t)=0
for all
t
. If
\|\Pi_{K_{0}}(f^{*})\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}<\delta
for small
\delta>0
, then note that both
Proposition

4.6
and
Proposition

4.8
limit
\epsilon<\delta
. This means that
\overline{T}(t)
can only detect small changes. On the other hand, if we are under
H_{1}
(so that
p\neq q
) and we assume that
\|\Pi_{K_{0}}(f^{*})\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}>\delta
for some larger
\delta>0
, then
\epsilon
can be made much larger and should be more easy to detect.

5
Realistic and Population Dynamics Approximation

The results in this section are crucial and foundational for building the results in
Section

6
. In essence, we are trying to show that
\widehat{u}
and
u
are close to
\bar{u}
, which can easily imply that the associated two-sample statistics
\widehat{T}
and
T
are close to
\overline{T}
. After showing that the statistics are close the zero-time NTK statistic, we will show
\widehat{T}
and
T
are monotonically increasing in time in order to prove
Theorem

6.6
and
Theorem

6.2
.

The general process of showing
\widehat{u}
and
u
are close to
\bar{u}
requires

1.

Showing that
\widehat{\theta}(t)
and
\theta(t)
are close to
\theta(0)=\theta_{0}
in time. Along with
Assumption

5.2
, this is used to ensure that
K_{t}
is close to
K_{0}
. Note that this is all that is needed to show
u
is close to
\bar{u}
.

2.

Show that a neural network version of matrix Bernstein’s inequality holds for the sampled kernel
\widehat{K}_{t}
. Along with showing the parameters are close,
Assumption

5.2
and
Assumption

5.3
are used here to show that
\widehat{K}_{t}
is close to
K_{0}
.

Formally, there are a few assumptions that we will need to ensure the analysis works. To start, let
B_{R}
denote the open ball of radius
R
with center
\theta_{0}
and assume that
u(x,0)=\bar{u}(x,0)=f(x,\theta_{0})=0
for all
x
. For much of the analysis of realistic and population dynamics going forward, we will use the following lemma heavily.

Lemma 5.1
.

\mathcal{L}(\theta(0))=\|u(\cdot,0)-f^{*}\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}^{2}=\|\bar{u}(\cdot,0)-f^{*}\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}^{2}=\|f^{*}\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}^{2}
.

Proof.

Notice that since
u(\cdot,0)=\bar{u}(\cdot,0)=f(x,\theta_{0})=0
, we have the result.
∎

For both the realistic and population dynamics, we will need to assume bounded and Lipschitz gradient as shown in the following assumption.

Assumption 5.2

(Gradient Condition)
.

There exists positive constants
R,L_{1}
, and
L_{2}
such that

1.

(Boundedness) For any
\theta\in B_{R}
,
\sup_{x\in\text{supp}(p+q)}\|\nabla_{\theta}f(x,\theta)\|\leq L_{1}
.

2.

(Lipschitz) For any
\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\in B_{R}
,
\sup_{x\in\text{supp}(p+q)}\|\nabla_{\theta}f(x,\theta_{1})-\nabla_{\theta}f(x,\theta_{2})\|\leq L_{2}\|\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}\|
.

For only the realistic dynamics case, we will need to assume a large enough training sample size as is specified in the following assumption in order to get a matrix Bernstein type statement for our neural network.

Assumption 5.3

(Sample Size Condition)
.

For
A>0
(representing a desired probability level), consider the function

\displaystyle h(n)=\sqrt{2L_{1}^{2}(2L_{1}^{2}+3/2)\frac{A\log(n)+\log(2M_{\Theta})}{n}}.

Assume that the training sample sizes
n_{p}
and
n_{q}
are large enough that
h(n_{p})<\frac{3}{2}
and
h(n_{q})<\frac{3}{2}
.

5.1
Population Dynamics Approximation

As the population dynamics have no sample size associated to them, we do not need to worry about
n_{p}
or
n_{q}
. In this case, we use that the loss

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\theta)=\frac{1}{2}\|f(\cdot,\theta)-f^{*}(\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}

is decreasing to get the following lemma.

Lemma 5.4

(Population-Dynamics Parameter Bound)
.

Assume that
u(x,0)=\bar{u}(x,0)=0
, then

\displaystyle\|\theta(t)-\theta(0)\|

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{t}\|f^{*}\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}.

Moreover, if

\displaystyle t\leq\bigg(\frac{R}{\|f^{*}\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}}\bigg)^{2},

then
\theta(t)\in B_{R}
.

The proof of
Lemma

5.4
is contained in
Appendix

B
.
Now with
Assumption

5.2
, we can further bound the operator norm of the difference
K_{t}-K_{0}
with the following lemma.

Lemma 5.5

(Population-Dynamics NTK Bound)
.

Let
\theta(t)\in B_{R}
, then under
Assumption

5.2
, we have

\displaystyle\|K_{t}-K_{0}\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}\leq 2L_{1}L_{2}\sqrt{t}\|f^{*}\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}.

The proof of
Lemma

5.5
is contained in
Appendix

B

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\widehat{T}_{null}(t)>\tau|H_{0})=\alpha

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(\widehat{T}_{test}(t)>\tau\big|\Pi_{K_{0}}(f^{*})=f_{k}^{*}\Big)\xrightarrow{n_{p},n_{q},m_{p},m_{q}\to\infty}1.

This idea is formalized in the following corollary.

Corollary 6.10

(Realistic Test Power Analysis)
.

Let
\|\Pi_{K_{0}}(f^{*})\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}^{2}/2>\epsilon>0
be a detection level. Given training and test sample sizes
n_{p},n_{q},m_{p},m_{q}
, let

\displaystyle\widetilde{C}=\sqrt{2}L_{1}^{2}\Bigg(\sqrt{\frac{A\log(m_{p})}{m_{p}}}+\sqrt{\frac{A\log(m_{q})}{m_{q}}}+\sqrt{\frac{A\log(n_{p})}{n_{p}}}+\sqrt{\frac{A\log(n_{q})}{n_{q}}}\bigg)

and
C^{-}_{test}=C^{+}_{test}+C_{2}+C_{3}+C_{4}
, where constants
C^{+}_{test}
come from
Corollary

6.9
and
C_{2},C_{3},C_{4}
come from
Proposition

5.11
. If we assume

\displaystyle\|f_{k}^{*}\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}^{2}>\max_{a\in\{1,5/2\}}\frac{2\epsilon\exp\Big((\epsilon/(C^{-}_{test}+\widetilde{C}))^{1/a}/\lambda_{k}\Big)}{\exp\Big((\epsilon/(C^{-}_{test}+\widetilde{C}))^{1/a}/\lambda_{k}\Big)-1},

then for

\displaystyle t_{1}^{*}(2\epsilon)\leq t\leq\min_{a\in\{1,5/2\}}\bigg(\frac{\epsilon}{C_{test}^{-}+\widetilde{C}}\bigg)^{1/a},

we have
\widehat{T}_{test,k}(t)>\epsilon
and
\widehat{T}_{test,null}(t)<\epsilon
with probability
\geq 1-(n_{p}^{-A}+n_{q}^{-A}+m_{p}^{-A}+m_{q}^{-A})
. Moreover, for a two-sample level
0<\alpha<1
(typically
\alpha=0.05
) with
\alpha\geq n_{p}^{-A}+n_{q}^{-A}+m_{p}^{-A}+m_{q}^{-A}
and test threshold
\tau\leq\epsilon
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(\widehat{T}_{test}(t)>\tau\big|\Pi_{K_{0}}(f^{*})=f_{k}^{*}\Big)

\displaystyle\geq 1-n_{p}^{-A}+n_{q}^{-A}+m_{p}^{-A}+m_{q}^{-A}.

The proof is included in
Appendix

C
.

Remark 6.11
.

From the proof of
Corollary

6.9
, we can see that the maximum time separation level is governed by

\displaystyle\frac{\epsilon}{C^{+}}-\lambda_{k}\log\bigg(\frac{\|f^{*}_{k}\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}^{2}}{\|f^{*}_{k}\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}^{2}-2\epsilon}\bigg).

Notice that if
\epsilon=\|f_{k}^{*}\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}^{2}x
for some fraction
0<x<1
, then we can simplify this expression. In particular, we see that our expression changes to

\displaystyle\gamma(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{\|f_{k}^{*}\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}^{2}x}{C^{+}}-\lambda_{k}\log\bigg(\frac{1}{1-2x}\bigg)

\displaystyle=\frac{\|f_{k}^{*}\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}^{2}x}{C^{+}}-\lambda_{k}\log\bigg(\frac{1/2}{1/2-x}\bigg).

From this, we can see that it is necessary that
0<x<\frac{1}{2}
. Note that as
x\to 0
, we get
\gamma(x)\to 0
; but as
x\to\frac{1}{2}
, we get
\gamma(x)\to-\infty
. Since
\gamma(x)
is not decreasing, we can find a maximum for the time separation
\gamma(x)
. In particular, we see that

\displaystyle\gamma^{\prime}(x)=\frac{\|f_{k}^{*}\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}^{2}}{C^{+}}-\lambda_{k}\frac{1/2-x}{1/2}\frac{1/2}{(1/2-x)^{2}}=\frac{\|f_{k}^{*}\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}^{2}}{C^{+}}-\lambda_{k}\frac{1}{1/2-x}.

Notice that
\gamma^{\prime}(x)=0
implies that

\displaystyle x=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\lambda_{k}C^{+}}{\|f_{k}^{*}\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}^{2}}.

Moreover, we note that this is a maximum since

\displaystyle\gamma^{\prime\prime}(x)=-\lambda_{k}\frac{1}{(1/2-x)^{2}}<0.

Obviously, this only makes sense if

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}>\frac{\lambda_{k}C^{+}}{\|f_{k}^{*}\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}^{2}}\iff\|f_{k}^{*}\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}^{2}>2\lambda_{k}C^{+}.

This means that as long as
f_{k}^{*}
has large enough norm, our neural network two-sample test should be most trustworthy when we observe deviation
\epsilon=\frac{\|f_{k}^{*}\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}^{2}}{2}-\lambda_{k}C^{+}
since that is the deviation level with the maximum time separation between the assumption
\Pi_{K_{0}}(f^{*})=f_{k}^{*}
and the null hypothesis
p=q
.

Remark 6.12
.

It is instructive to note what are fixed parameters versus parameters to be chosen in
Corollary

6.9
. First, notice that the complexity of our neural network determines not only the constants
C^{+}
,
C^{-}
, and
\lambda_{k}
but also whether or not the assumption
\Pi_{K_{0}}(f^{*})=f^{*}_{k}

is. This means that the only choosable parameters are
\epsilon
and
\gamma
(which is upper bounded by
\epsilon

Proof of

We simply just need to take the derivative of the solution and show that it is exactly the differential equation, then the uniqueness of solutions of differential equations implies that our ansatz is indeed the solution. To see this, let us call the solution

\displaystyle e^{*}(\cdot,t)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{M}e^{-t\lambda_{\ell}}\langle u_{\ell},\bar{e}(\cdot,0)\rangle_{L^{2}(p+q)}u_{\ell}.

Now notice that

\displaystyle\partial_{t}e^{*}(\cdot,t)=-\sum_{\ell=1}^{M}\lambda_{\ell}e^{-t\lambda_{\ell}}\langle u_{\ell},\bar{e}(\cdot,0)\rangle_{L^{2}(p+q)}u_{\ell}.

On the other hand, let us plug in the ansatz
e^{*}
into the differential equation and see what we get. In particular,

\displaystyle-\sum_{j=1}^{M}\lambda_{j}\langle u_{j},e^{*}(\cdot,t)\rangle_{L^{2}(p+q)}u_{j}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{j=1}^{M}\lambda_{j}\langle u_{j},\sum_{\ell=1}^{M}e^{-t\lambda_{\ell}}\langle u_{\ell},\bar{e}(\cdot,0)\rangle_{L^{2}(p+q)}u_{\ell}\rangle_{L^{2}(p+q)}u_{j}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{j,\ell=1}^{M}\lambda_{j}e^{-t\lambda_{\ell}}\langle u_{\ell},\bar{e}(\cdot,0)\rangle_{L^{2}(p+q)}\underbrace{\langle u_{j},u_{\ell}\rangle_{L^{2}(p+q)}}_{\delta_{j\ell}}u_{j}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{\ell=1}^{M}\lambda_{\ell}e^{-t\lambda_{\ell}}\langle u_{\ell},\bar{e}(\cdot,0)\rangle_{L^{2}(p+q)}u_{\ell}

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}e^{*}(\cdot,t).

This shows the result.

Proof of
Lemma

.

Now our two-sample test statistic becomes

\displaystyle\overline{T}(t)

\displaystyle=\overline{\mu_{P}}(t)-\overline{\mu_{Q}}(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Pi_{K_{0}}(f^{*})(x)(p-q)(x)dx+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\bar{e}(x,t)d(p-q)(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Pi_{K_{0}}(f^{*})(x)\frac{p-q}{p+q}(x)d(p+q)(x)+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\bar{e}(x,t)d(p-q)(x)

\displaystyle=\langle\Pi_{K_{0}}(f^{*}),f^{*}\rangle_{L^{2}(p+q)}+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\bar{e}(x,t)d(p-q)(x).

Extending the eigenfunctions
\{u_{\ell}\}_{\ell=1}^{M}
to a full basis for
L^{2}(p+q)
given by
\{u_{\ell}\}_{\ell=1}^{\infty}
, we can see that the term with
f^{*}
reduces to

\displaystyle\langle\Pi_{K_{0}}(f^{*}),f^{*}\rangle_{L^{2}(p+q)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\underbrace{\langle\Pi_{K_{0}}(u_{\ell}),f^{*}\rangle_{L^{2}(p+q)}}_{\ell>M\implies 0}\langle u_{\ell},f^{*}\rangle_{L^{2}(p+q)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{M}\langle\Pi_{K_{0}}(u_{\ell}),f^{*}\rangle_{L^{2}(p+q)}\langle u_{\ell},f^{*}\rangle_{L^{2}(p+q)}

\displaystyle\overline{T}(t)

\displaystyle=\|\Pi_{K_{0}}(f^{*})\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}^{2}+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{M}e^{-t\lambda_{\ell}}\langle u_{\ell},\bar{e}(\cdot,0)\rangle_{L^{2}(p+q)}u_{\ell}(x)d(p-q)(x)

\theta_{0}

\displaystyle\langle u_{\ell},\bar{e}(\cdot,0)\rangle_{L^{2}(p+q)}

\displaystyle=\langle u_{\ell},-\Pi_{K_{0}}(f^{*})\rangle_{L^{2}(p+q)}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u_{\ell}(x)\Pi_{K_{0}}\Big(\frac{q-p}{p+q}\Big)(x)(p(x)+q(x))dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u_{\ell}(x)\frac{q-p}{p+q}(x)(p(x)+q(x))dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u_{\ell}(x)d(q-p)(x)

\displaystyle\implies\langle u_{\ell},\bar{e}(\cdot,0)\rangle_{L^{2}(p+q)}

\displaystyle=\|\Pi_{K_{0}}(f^{*})\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}^{2}-\sum_{\ell\geq 1}e^{-t\lambda_{\ell}}\langle u_{\ell},\bar{e}(\cdot,0)\rangle_{L^{2}(p+q)}^{2}.

We get the result by seeing that
\bar{e}(\cdot,0)=-\Pi_{K_{0}}(f^{*})
and that applying the square gets rid of the negative sign. So we’re done.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p+q}\mathbb{E}_{x^{\prime}\sim p+q}w(x)\langle\nabla_{\theta}f(x,\theta(t)),\nabla_{\theta}f(x^{\prime},\theta(t))\rangle_{\Theta}w(x^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\|\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p+q}\nabla_{\theta}f(x,\theta(t))w(x)\|_{\Theta}^{2}.

This means that
\langle w,\mathbb{E}_{x^{\prime}\sim p+q}(K_{t}-K_{0})(\cdot,x^{\prime})w(x^{\prime})\rangle_{L^{2}(p+q)}
equals

\displaystyle\|\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p+q}\nabla_{\theta}f(x,\theta(t))w(x)\|_{\Theta}^{2}-\|\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p+q}\nabla_{\theta}f(x,\theta(0))w(x)\|_{\Theta}^{2}

\displaystyle=(\|\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p+q}\nabla_{\theta}f(x,\theta(t))w(x)\|_{\Theta}+\|\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p+q}\nabla_{\theta}f(x,\theta(0))w(x)\|_{\Theta})

\displaystyle\cdot(\|\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p+q}\nabla_{\theta}f(x,\theta(t))w(x)\|_{\Theta}-\|\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p+q}\nabla_{\theta}f(x,\theta(0))w(x)\|_{\Theta}).

Now using Minkowski’s integral inequality and
Assumption

5.2
(1), we get

\displaystyle\|\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p+q}\nabla_{\theta}f(x,\theta(t))w(x)\|_{\Theta}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p+q}\|\nabla_{\theta}f(x,\theta(t))w(x)\|_{\Theta}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p+q}\|\nabla_{\theta}f(x,\theta(t))\|_{\Theta}\|w(x)\|

\displaystyle\leq L_{1}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p+q}\|w(x)\|

\displaystyle\leq L_{1}\|w\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}.

Notice that this implies

\displaystyle\|\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p+q}\nabla_{\theta}f(x,\theta(t))w(x)\|_{\Theta}+\|\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p+q}\nabla_{\theta}f(x,\theta(0))w(x)\|_{\Theta}\leq 2L_{1}\|w\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}.

Now using
Assumption

5.2
(2), we have

\displaystyle\|\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p+q}\nabla_{\theta}f(x,\theta(t))w(x)\|_{\Theta}-\|\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p+q}\nabla_{\theta}f(x,\theta(0))w(x)\|_{\Theta}

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p+q}(\nabla_{\theta}f(x,\theta(t))-\nabla_{\theta}f(x,\theta(0)))w(x)\|_{\Theta}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p+q}\|\nabla_{\theta}f(x,\theta(t))-\nabla_{\theta}f(x,\theta(0))\|_{\Theta}\|w(x)\|

\displaystyle\leq L_{2}\|\theta(t)-\theta(0)\|_{\Theta}\|w\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}

\displaystyle\leq L_{2}\sqrt{t}\|f^{*}\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}\|w\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}.

This means that

\displaystyle\langle w,\mathbb{E}_{x^{\prime}\sim p+q}K_{t}(\cdot,x^{\prime})w(x^{\prime})\rangle_{L^{2}(p+q)}\leq 2L_{1}L_{2}\sqrt{t}\|f^{*}\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}\|w\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}^{2}.

Finally this proves that
\|K_{t}-K_{0}\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}\leq 2L_{1}L_{2}\sqrt{t}\|f^{*}\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}
.
∎

Proof of
Proposition

5.6
.

Note that

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(u-\bar{u})(x,t)=\partial_{t}(e-\bar{e})(x,t)=\mathbb{E}_{x^{\prime}\sim p+q}\big[K_{0}(x,x^{\prime})\bar{e}(x^{\prime},t)-K_{t}(x,x^{\prime})e(x^{\prime},t)\big]

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}_{x^{\prime}\sim p+q}\big[(K_{t}(x,x^{\prime})-K_{0}(x,x^{\prime}))\bar{e}(x^{\prime},t)+K_{t}(x,x^{\prime})(e-\bar{e})(x^{\prime},t)\big].

Notice here that because
K_{t}(x,x^{\prime})=\langle\nabla_{\theta}f(x,\theta(t)),\nabla_{\theta}f(x^{\prime},\theta(t))\rangle
, we have that
K_{t}
is a positive semi-definite operator (we will use this later). Now if we take an inner product with
e-\bar{e}
on both sides of the equation, we get

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\frac{1}{2}\|(e-\bar{e})(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}^{2}

\displaystyle=\langle(e-\bar{e})(\cdot,t),\partial_{t}(e-\bar{e})(\cdot,t)\rangle_{L^{2}(p+q)}

\displaystyle=\langle(e-\bar{e})(\cdot,t),-\mathbb{E}_{x^{\prime}\sim p+q}\big[(K_{t}(x,x^{\prime})-K_{0}(x,x^{\prime}))\bar{e}(x^{\prime},t)

\displaystyle+K_{t}(x,x^{\prime})(e-\bar{e})(x^{\prime},t)\big]\rangle_{L^{2}(p+q)}

\displaystyle\leq|\langle(e-\bar{e})(\cdot,t),-\mathbb{E}_{x^{\prime}\sim p+q}\big[(K_{t}(x,x^{\prime})-K_{0}(x,x^{\prime}))\bar{e}(x^{\prime},t)\big]\rangle_{L^{2}(p+q)}|

\displaystyle\leq\|(e-\bar{e})(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}\|K_{t}-K_{0}\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}\|\bar{e}(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}

\displaystyle\leq\|(e-\bar{e})(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}\|K_{t}-K_{0}\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}\|\Pi_{K_{0}}(f^{*})\|_{L^{2}(p+q)},

where the first inequality comes from the fact that
K_{t}
is a positive semi-definite operator as well as using absolute values whilst the second inequality comes from using the Cauchy-Schwartz-Bunyakovsky inequality along with the kernel integral operator norm bound of
\|K_{t}-K_{0}\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}
. Now recalling that
\bar{e}(\cdot,0)=\Pi_{K_{0}}(f^{*})
and using Parseval’s identity, the last inequality comes from the fact that

\displaystyle\|\bar{e}(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{M}\underbrace{e^{-2t\lambda_{\ell}}}_{\leq 1}|\langle u_{\ell},\bar{e}(\cdot,0)\rangle|^{2}\leq\sum_{\ell=1}^{M}|\langle u_{\ell},\Pi_{K_{0}}(f^{*})\rangle|^{2}=\|\Pi_{K_{0}}(f^{*})\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}^{2}.

From
Lemma

5.5
, we get that

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\frac{1}{2}\|(e-\bar{e})(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2L_{1}L_{2}\sqrt{t}\|f^{*}\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}\cdot\|\Pi_{K_{0}}(f^{*})\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}\cdot\|(e-\bar{e})(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}.

Now, finally notice that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\|(e-\bar{e})(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\Big(\frac{d}{dt}\frac{1}{2}\|(e-\bar{e})(\cdot,s)\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}^{2}\Big)ds

\displaystyle\leq 2L_{1}L_{2}\|f^{*}\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}\|\Pi_{K_{0}}(f^{*})\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}\int_{0}^{t}\sqrt{s}\|(e-\bar{e})(\cdot,s)\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}ds.

Let
t^{*}\leq t
be the time such that

\displaystyle\sup_{s\in[0,t]}\|(e-\bar{e})(\cdot,s)\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}=\|(e-\bar{e})(\cdot,t^{*})\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}.

Then, we find that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\|(e-\bar{e})(\cdot,t^{*})\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2L_{1}L_{2}\|f^{*}\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}\|\Pi_{K_{0}}(f^{*})\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}\|(e-\bar{e})(\cdot,t^{*})\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}\int_{0}^{t^{*}}\sqrt{s}ds,

but this implies

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\|(e-\bar{e})(\cdot,t^{*})\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}

\displaystyle\leq 2L_{1}L_{2}\|f^{*}\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}\|\Pi_{K_{0}}(f^{*})\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}\frac{2}{3}(t^{*})^{3/2}

\displaystyle\implies\|(e-\bar{e})(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}

\displaystyle\leq 4L_{1}L_{2}\|f^{*}\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}\|\Pi_{K_{0}}(f^{*})\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}\frac{2}{3}(t^{*})^{3/2}

\displaystyle\leq(8/3)L_{1}L_{2}\|f^{*}\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}\|\Pi_{K_{0}}(f^{*})\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}(t)^{3/2},

where we use the fact that
\|(e-\bar{e})(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}\leq\|(e-\bar{e})(\cdot,t^{*})\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}
and
t^{*}\leq t
. Finally using that
\|\Pi_{K_{0}}(f^{*})\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}\leq\|f^{*}\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}
gives the result, so we’re done.
∎

\displaystyle\Big|T(t)-\overline{T}(t)\Big|

Proof of
Lemma

5.8
.

Note that the loss
\mathcal{L}(\theta(t))=\|f(\cdot,\theta(t))-f^{*}\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}^{2}
is monotonically decreasing since

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{L}(\theta(t))=\langle\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}(\theta(t)),\dot{\theta}(t)\rangle=-\|\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}(\theta(t))\|^{2}\leq 0.

So we have that
\mathcal{L}(\theta(s))\geq\mathcal{L}(\theta(t))
if
0\leq s\leq t\leq\tau
. Writing out the loss as
\mathcal{L}(\theta(s))=\|f(\cdot,\theta(s))\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}^{2}-2\langle f(\cdot,\theta(s)),f^{*}\rangle_{L^{2}(p+q)}+\|f^{*}\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}^{2}
, we can see

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\theta(s))

\displaystyle\geq\mathcal{L}(\theta(t))

\displaystyle\|f(\cdot,\theta(s))\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}^{2}-2\langle f(\cdot,\theta(s)),f^{*}\rangle_{L^{2}(p+q)}

\displaystyle\geq\|f(\cdot,\theta(t))\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}^{2}-2\langle f(\cdot,\theta(t)),f^{*}\rangle_{L^{2}(p+q)}

\displaystyle\langle f(\cdot,\theta(t))-f(\cdot,\theta(s)),f^{*}\rangle_{L^{2}(p+q)}

\displaystyle\geq|f(\cdot,\theta(t))\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}^{2}-\|f(\cdot,\theta(s))\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}.

Notice that because
u(x,t)=f(x,\theta(t))
and we assume that
\|u(x,t)\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}^{2}
is increasing in time, we know that

\displaystyle\langle f(\cdot,\theta(t))-f(\cdot,\theta(s)),f^{*}\rangle_{L^{2}(p+q)}

\displaystyle\geq\underbrace{|f(\cdot,\theta(t))\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}^{2}-\|f(\cdot,\theta(s))\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}}_{\geq 0}

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\big(u(x,t)-u(x,s)\big)\frac{p-q}{p+q}(x)d(p+q)(x)

\displaystyle\geq 0

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\big(u(x,t)-u(x,s)\big)d(p-q)(x)

\displaystyle\geq 0

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u(x,t)d(p-q)(x)

\displaystyle\geq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u(x,s)d(p-q)(x).

So we see that on the interval
[0,T]
, the two-sample test statistic
T(t)
is monotonically increasing.
∎

\displaystyle\underbrace{-\mathbb{E}_{x^{\prime}\sim\widehat{q}}\widehat{K}_{t}(\cdot,x^{\prime})(\widehat{e}_{q}(x^{\prime},t)-\bar{e}(x^{\prime},t))}_{I_{1,q}}+\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{x^{\prime}\sim\widehat{q}}(K_{0}-\widehat{K}_{t})(\cdot,x^{\prime})\bar{e}(x^{\prime},t)}_{I_{2,q}}

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\Big(\mathbb{E}_{x^{\prime}\sim q}-\mathbb{E}_{x^{\prime}\sim\widehat{q}}\Big)K_{0}(\cdot,x^{\prime})\bar{e}(x^{\prime},t)}_{I_{3,q}}.

Similar to the proof of
Proposition

5.6
, we will consider

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\|(\widehat{u}-\bar{u})(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}^{2}

\displaystyle=\langle(\widehat{u}-\bar{u})(\cdot,t),2\partial_{t}(\widehat{u}-\bar{u})(\cdot,t)\rangle_{L^{2}(p+q)}

\displaystyle=\langle(\widehat{u}-\bar{u})(\cdot,t),I_{1,p}+I_{1,q}\rangle_{L^{2}(p+q)}+\langle(\widehat{u}-\bar{u})(\cdot,t),I_{2,p}+I_{2,q}\rangle_{L^{2}(p+q)}

\displaystyle+\langle(\widehat{u}-\bar{u})(\cdot,t),I_{3,p}+I_{3,q}\rangle_{L^{2}(p+q)}

\displaystyle\leq\langle(\widehat{u}-\bar{u})(\cdot,t),I_{1,p}+I_{1,q}\rangle_{L^{2}(p+q)}

\displaystyle+\|(\widehat{u}-\bar{u})(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}\Big(\|I_{2,p}+I_{2,q}\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}+\|I_{3,p}+I_{3,q}\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}\Big).

So we’ll need to bound
I_{2,p},I_{2,q},I_{3,p}
, and
I_{3,q}
and will deal with the
I_{1,p}
and
I_{1,q}
terms at the end.

Before starting, let
A_{P}
be the event that

\displaystyle\|(\mathbb{E}_{x^{\prime}\sim p}-\mathbb{E}_{x^{\prime}\sim\widehat{p}})\nabla_{\theta}u(x^{\prime},\theta(0))\nabla_{\theta}u(x^{\prime},\theta(0))^{\top}\|\leq\sqrt{2L_{1}^{2}(2L_{1}^{2}+3/2)\frac{A\log(n_{p})+\log(2M_{\Theta})}{n_{p}}}

\displaystyle\|(\mathbb{E}_{x^{\prime}\sim q}-\mathbb{E}_{x^{\prime}\sim\widehat{q}})\nabla_{\theta}u(x^{\prime},\theta(0))\nabla_{\theta}u(x^{\prime},\theta(0))^{\top}\|\leq\sqrt{2L_{1}^{2}(2L_{1}^{2}+3/2)\frac{A\log(n_{q})+\log(2M_{\Theta})}{n_{q}}}.

Note that from
Lemma

5.10
, we know that
A_{P}
occurs with probability
\geq 1-n_{p}^{-A}
and
A_{Q}
occurs with probability
\geq 1-n_{q}^{-A}
. Since these events are disjoint, notice that

\displaystyle\text{Pr}\big(A_{P}\cap A_{Q}\big)=1-\text{Pr}\big(A_{P}^{c}\cup A_{Q}^{c}\big)=1-n_{p}^{-A}-n_{q}^{-A}

where

and
A_{Q}

A_{P}

A_{Q}

Bounding
I_{3,p}
and
I_{3,q}
:
We will first work with just
I_{3,p}
and will notice that the method of bounding
I_{3,q}
is the same. Then using the triangle inequality, we will get our bounds. Notice that

\displaystyle I_{3,p}

\displaystyle=\langle\nabla_{\theta}u(\cdot,\theta(0)),(\mathbb{E}_{x^{\prime}\sim p}-\mathbb{E}_{x^{\prime}\sim\widehat{p}})\nabla_{\theta}u(x^{\prime},\theta(0))\bar{e}(x^{\prime},t)\rangle_{\Theta}

\displaystyle\implies\|I_{3,p}\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}

\displaystyle\leq\Big\|\underbrace{\|\nabla_{\theta}u(\cdot,\theta(0))\|_{\Theta}}_{\leq L_{1}}\Big\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}\|(\mathbb{E}_{x^{\prime}\sim p}-\mathbb{E}_{x^{\prime}\sim\widehat{p}})\nabla_{\theta}u(x^{\prime},\theta(0))\bar{e}(x^{\prime},t)\|_{\Theta}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{2}L_{1}\|\underbrace{(\mathbb{E}_{x^{\prime}\sim p}-\mathbb{E}_{x^{\prime}\sim\widehat{p}})\nabla_{\theta}u(x^{\prime},\theta(0))\bar{e}(x^{\prime},t)}_{a_{3}}\|_{\Theta}.

Now we can use the fact that

\displaystyle\bar{e}(x,t)

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}_{y\sim p+q}K_{0}(x,y)\bar{e}(y,s)ds

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{t}\langle\nabla_{\theta}u(x,\theta(0)),\mathbb{E}_{y\sim p+q}\nabla_{\theta}u(y,\theta(0))\bar{e}(y,s)\rangle_{\Theta}ds.

This means that we can rewrite
a_{3}
as

\displaystyle a_{3}=-\int_{0}^{t}\big[(\mathbb{E}_{x^{\prime}\sim p}-\mathbb{E}_{x^{\prime}\sim\widehat{p}})\nabla_{\theta}u(x^{\prime},\theta(0))\nabla_{\theta}u(x^{\prime},\theta(0))^{\top}\big]\mathbb{E}_{y\sim p+q}\nabla_{\theta}u(y,\theta(0))\bar{e}(y,s)ds.

This would mean that
\|a_{3}\|_{\Theta}
is bounded by

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\|(\mathbb{E}_{x^{\prime}\sim p}-\mathbb{E}_{x^{\prime}\sim\widehat{p}})\nabla_{\theta}u(x^{\prime},\theta(0))\nabla_{\theta}u(x^{\prime},\theta(0))^{\top}\|\underbrace{\|\nabla_{\theta}u(y,\theta(0))\|_{\Theta}}_{\leq L_{1}}\mathbb{E}_{y\sim p+q}\|\bar{e}(y,s)\|ds

\displaystyle\leq tL_{1}\|\Pi_{K_{0}}(f^{*})\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}\|\|(\mathbb{E}_{x^{\prime}\sim p}-\mathbb{E}_{x^{\prime}\sim\widehat{p}})\nabla_{\theta}u(x^{\prime},\theta(0))\nabla_{\theta}u(x^{\prime},\theta(0))^{\top}\|.

Now recalling that
\bar{e}(\cdot,0)=\Pi_{K_{0}}(f^{*})
and using Parseval’s identity, the last inequality comes from the fact that

\displaystyle\|\bar{e}(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{M}\underbrace{e^{-2t\lambda_{\ell}}}_{\leq 1}|\langle u_{\ell},\bar{e}(\cdot,0)\rangle|^{2}\leq\sum_{\ell=1}^{M}|\langle u_{\ell},\Pi_{K_{0}}(f^{*})\rangle|^{2}=\|\Pi_{K_{0}}(f^{*})\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}^{2}.

So we only need to bound the operator norm of

\displaystyle\|(\mathbb{E}_{x^{\prime}\sim p}-\mathbb{E}_{x^{\prime}\sim\widehat{p}})\nabla_{\theta}u(x^{\prime},\theta(0))\nabla_{\theta}u(x^{\prime},\theta(0))^{\top}\|.

To this end, since we assume that we are working under event
A_{P}\cap A_{Q}
, we can again use
Lemma

5.10
and get that with probability greater than
1-{n_{p}}^{-A}-n_{q}^{-A}
,

\displaystyle\|(\mathbb{E}_{x^{\prime}\sim p}-\mathbb{E}_{x^{\prime}\sim\widehat{p}})\nabla_{\theta}u(x^{\prime},\theta(0))\nabla_{\theta}u(x^{\prime},\theta(0))^{\top}\|\leq\sqrt{2L_{1}^{2}(2L_{1}^{2}+3/2)\frac{A\log(n_{p})+\log(2M_{\Theta})}{n_{p}}}.

Now putting all these bounds together, we get that

\displaystyle\|I_{3,p}\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}\leq t\cdot\sqrt{2}L_{1}^{3}\|\Pi_{K_{0}}(f^{*})\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}\sqrt{2L_{1}^{2}(2L_{1}^{2}+3/2)\frac{A\log(n_{p})+\log(2M_{\Theta})}{n_{p}}}.

For ease later on, let us define

\displaystyle g_{3}(t,n)=t\cdot\sqrt{2}L_{1}^{3}\|\Pi_{K_{0}}(f^{*})\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}\sqrt{2L_{1}^{2}(2L_{1}^{2}+3/2)\frac{A\log(n)+\log(2M_{\Theta})}{n}}.

Note that because we are working under event
A_{P}\cap A_{Q}
, we know that with probability greater than
1-n_{p}^{-A}-{n_{q}}^{-A}

\displaystyle\|I_{3,q}\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}\leq t\cdot\sqrt{2}L_{1}^{3}\|\Pi_{K_{0}}(f^{*})\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}\sqrt{2L_{1}^{2}(2L_{1}^{2}+3/2)\frac{A\log(n_{q})+\log(2M_{\Theta})}{n_{q}}}=g_{3}(t,n_{q}).

Now let us bound
I_{2,p}
and
I_{2,q}
.

Bounding
I_{2,p}
and
I_{2,q}
:
We will again bound for
I_{2,p}
and essentially use the same logic for bounding
I_{2,q}
. Note that

\displaystyle\|I_{2,p}\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p+q}\Big|\mathbb{E}_{x^{\prime}\sim\widehat{p}}(\widehat{K}_{t}-K_{0})(x,x^{\prime})\bar{e}(x^{\prime},t)\Big|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p+q}\Big(\mathbb{E}_{x^{\prime}\sim\widehat{p}}|(\widehat{K}_{t}-K_{0})(x,x^{\prime})||\bar{e}(x^{\prime},t)|\Big)^{2}.

Using
Lemma

5.9
, note that

\displaystyle|\widehat{K}_{t}(x,x^{\prime})-K_{0}(x,x^{\prime})|

\displaystyle=|\langle\nabla_{\theta}u(x,\widehat{\theta}(t)),\nabla_{\theta}u(x^{\prime},\widehat{\theta}(t))\rangle-\langle\nabla_{\theta}u(x,\theta(0)),\nabla_{\theta}u(x^{\prime},\theta(0))\rangle|

\displaystyle\leq\|\nabla_{\theta}u(x,\widehat{\theta}(t))\|_{\Theta}\|\|\nabla_{\theta}u(x^{\prime},\widehat{\theta}(t))-\nabla_{\theta}u(x^{\prime},\theta(0))\|_{\Theta}

\displaystyle+\|\nabla_{\theta}u(x,\widehat{\theta}(t))-\nabla_{\theta}u(x,\theta(0))\|_{\Theta}\|\nabla_{\theta}u(x^{\prime},\theta(0))\|_{\Theta}

\displaystyle\leq 2L_{1}L_{2}\|\widehat{\theta}(t)-\theta(0)\|_{\Theta}\leq 2L_{1}L_{2}\sqrt{t}.

Now the only thing left to bound is
\mathbb{E}_{x^{\prime}\sim\widehat{p}}|\bar{e}(x^{\prime},t)|
. To do this, recalling the time-integrated form of
\bar{e}
, we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\widehat{p}}|\bar{e}(x,t)|=\|\bar{e}(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{1}(\widehat{p})}\leq\Big(\|\bar{e}(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{2}(\widehat{p})}^{2}\Big)^{1/2}.

Now notice that

\displaystyle\|\bar{e}(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{2}(\widehat{p})}^{2}=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\widehat{p}}|\bar{e}(x,t)|^{2}=(\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\widehat{p}}-\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p})|\bar{e}(x,t)|^{2}+\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p}|\bar{e}(x,t)|^{2}.

Because integrating a positive function over both
p
and
q
is an upper bound of just integrating over
p
, we know that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p}|\bar{e}(x,t)|^{2}=\|\bar{e}(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{2}(p)}^{2}\leq\|\bar{e}(\cdot,0)\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}^{2}=\|\Pi_{K_{0}}(f^{*})\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}^{2},

so we only need to deal with the first term. In particular, using the time-integrated form of
|\bar{e}(x,t)|^{2}
, we get that equals

\displaystyle\Big|(\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\widehat{p}}-\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p})|\bar{e}(x,t)|^{2}\Big|=\bigg|\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}_{y_{1},y_{2}\sim p+q}\bar{e}(y_{2},s_{2})\nabla_{\theta}u(y_{2},\theta(0))^{\top}

\displaystyle\cdot\Big[(\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\widehat{p}}-\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p})\nabla_{\theta}u(x,\theta(0)),\nabla_{\theta}u(x,\theta(0))^{\top}\Big]\nabla_{\theta}u(y_{1},\theta(0))\bar{e}(y_{1},s_{1})ds_{1}ds_{2}\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq L_{1}^{2}\|(\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\widehat{p}}-\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p})\nabla_{\theta}u(x,\theta(0)),\nabla_{\theta}u(x,\theta(0))^{\top}\|\underbrace{\bigg(\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p+q}|\bar{e}(x,s)|ds\bigg)^{2}}_{\leq(t\sqrt{2}\|\Pi_{K_{0}}(f^{*})\|_{L^{2}(p+q)})^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq 2L_{1}^{2}t^{2}\|\Pi_{K_{0}}(f^{*})\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}^{2}\Big\|(\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\widehat{p}}-\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p})\nabla_{\theta}u(x,\theta(0)),\nabla_{\theta}u(x,\theta(0))^{\top}\Big\|.

Again, since we are under event
A_{P}\cap A_{Q}
we can use
Lemma

5.10
and get that with probability greater than
1-n_{p}^{-A}-n_{q}^{-A}

\displaystyle\big|(\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\widehat{p}}-\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p})|\bar{e}(x,t)|^{2}\big|\leq 2L_{1}^{2}t^{2}\|\Pi_{K_{0}}(f^{*})\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}^{2}\sqrt{2L_{1}^{2}(2L_{1}^{2}+3/2)\frac{A\log(n_{p})+\log(2M_{\Theta})}{n_{p}}}.

Plugging this back, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\widehat{p}}|\bar{e}(x,t)|\leq\sqrt{2}\|\Pi_{K_{0}}(f^{*})\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}\Big(1+2L_{1}^{3}t^{2}\sqrt{2(2L_{1}^{2}+3/2)\frac{A\log(n_{p})+\log(2M_{\Theta})}{n_{p}}}\Big)^{1/2}.

Plugging back to our original expression for
I_{2,p}
and using the fact that
\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p+q}1=2
, we get that

\displaystyle\|I_{2,p}\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}\leq 4L_{1}L_{2}\sqrt{t}\|\Pi_{K_{0}}(f^{*})\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}\Big(1+2L_{1}^{3}t^{2}\sqrt{2(2L_{1}^{2}+3/2)\frac{A\log(n_{p})+\log(2M_{\Theta})}{n_{p}}}\Big)^{1/2}

with probability
\geq 1-n_{p}^{-A}-n_{q}^{-A}
. Similar to before, we define

\displaystyle g_{2}(t,n)=4L_{1}L_{2}\sqrt{t}\|\Pi_{K_{0}}(f^{*})\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}\Big(1+2L_{1}^{3}t^{2}\sqrt{2(2L_{1}^{2}+3/2)\frac{A\log(n)+\log(2M_{\Theta})}{n}}\Big)^{1/2}.

Using the same logical reasoning of being in the event
A_{P}\cap A_{Q}
, we get that with probability
\geq 1-n_{p}^{-A}-n_{q}^{-A}

\displaystyle\|I_{2,q}\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}

\displaystyle\leq 4L_{1}L_{2}\sqrt{t}\|f^{*}\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}\Big(1+2L_{1}^{3}t^{2}\sqrt{2(2L_{1}^{2}+3/2)\frac{A\log(n_{q})+\log(2M_{\Theta})}{n_{q}}}\Big)^{1/2}

\displaystyle=g_{2}(t,n_{Q}).

Working with the
I_{1}
terms:
Let us again work with
I_{1,p}
and use the same logic for
I_{1,q}
later. In particular, note that

\displaystyle\langle(\widehat{u}-\bar{u})(\cdot,t),I_{1,p}\rangle_{L^{2}(p+q)}

\displaystyle=\langle(\widehat{u}-\bar{u})(\cdot,t),-\mathbb{E}_{x^{\prime}\sim\widehat{p}}\widehat{K}_{t}(\cdot,x^{\prime})(\widehat{e}_{P}(x^{\prime},t)-\bar{e}(x^{\prime},t))\rangle_{L^{2}(p+q)}

\displaystyle=-\langle(\widehat{u}-\bar{u})(\cdot,t),\mathbb{E}_{x^{\prime}\sim\widehat{p}}\widehat{K}_{t}(\cdot,x^{\prime})(\widehat{u}(x^{\prime},t)-\bar{u}(x^{\prime},t))\rangle_{L^{2}(p+q)}

\displaystyle-\langle(\widehat{u}-\bar{u})(\cdot,t),\mathbb{E}_{x^{\prime}\sim\widehat{p}}\widehat{K}_{t}(\cdot,x^{\prime})(f^{*}(x^{\prime})-1)\rangle_{L^{2}(p+q)}

\displaystyle\leq-\langle(\widehat{u}-\bar{u})(\cdot,t),\mathbb{E}_{x^{\prime}\sim\widehat{p+q}}\widehat{K}_{t}(\cdot,x^{\prime})(f^{*}(x^{\prime})-1)\rangle_{L^{2}(p+q)},

where we get the inequality because
\widehat{K}_{t}
is a positive semi-definite operator so the first term is less than
0
. Now we can bound by the following

\displaystyle|\langle(\widehat{u}-\bar{u})(\cdot,t),I_{1,p}\rangle_{L^{2}(p+q)}|

\displaystyle\leq\|(\widehat{u}-\bar{u})(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}\|\mathbb{E}_{x^{\prime}\sim\widehat{p}}\widehat{K}_{t}(\cdot,x^{\prime})(f^{*}(x^{\prime})-1)\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}.

Here note that

\displaystyle\|\mathbb{E}_{x^{\prime}\sim\widehat{p}}\widehat{K}_{t}(\cdot,x^{\prime})(f^{*}(x^{\prime})-1)\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}

\displaystyle=\|\mathbb{E}_{x^{\prime}\sim\widehat{p}}\langle\nabla_{\theta}u(\cdot,\widehat{\theta}(t)),\nabla_{\theta}u(x^{\prime},\widehat{\theta}(t))\rangle_{\Theta}(f^{*}(x^{\prime})-1)\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}

\displaystyle\leq L_{1}^{2}\mathbb{E}_{x^{\prime}\sim\widehat{p}}|f^{*}(x^{\prime})-1|

\displaystyle\leq L_{1}^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\bigg|\frac{p-q}{p+q}(x)-1\bigg|d\widehat{p}(x)

\displaystyle=L_{1}^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\bigg|\frac{p(x)-q(x)-p(x)-q(x)}{p(x)+q(x)}\bigg|d\widehat{p}(x)

\displaystyle=L_{1}^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}2\bigg|\frac{q(x)}{p(x)+q(x)}\bigg|d\widehat{p}(x)

\displaystyle=2L_{1}^{2}\frac{1}{n_{p}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{p}}\underbrace{\bigg|\frac{q(x_{i})}{p(x_{i})+q(x_{i})}}_{\leq 1}\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq 2L_{1}^{2}\frac{1}{n_{p}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{p}}1=2L_{1}^{2}.

This means that

\displaystyle|\langle(\widehat{u}-\bar{u})(\cdot,t),I_{1,p}\rangle_{L^{2}(p+q)}|\leq 2L_{1}^{2}\|(\widehat{u}-\bar{u})(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}.

Using the same logic (but with the term
\frac{p(x)}{p(x)+q(x)}
), we can show that

\displaystyle|\langle(\widehat{u}-\bar{u})(\cdot,t),I_{1,q}\rangle_{L^{2}(p+q)}|\leq 2L_{1}^{2}\|(\widehat{u}-\bar{u})(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}.

Putting this altogether, we see that

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\|(\widehat{u}-\bar{u})(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq|\langle(\widehat{u}-\bar{u})(\cdot,t),I_{1,p}\rangle_{L^{2}(p+q)}|+|\langle(\widehat{u}-\bar{u})(\cdot,t),I_{1,q}\rangle_{L^{2}(p+q)}|

\displaystyle+\|(\widehat{u}-\bar{u})(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}\Big(\|I_{2,p}\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}+\|I_{2,q}\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}

\displaystyle+\|I_{3,p}\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}+\|I_{3,q}\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}\Big)

\displaystyle=\bigg(4L_{1}^{2}+g_{2}(t,n_{p})+g_{3}(t,n_{p})

\displaystyle+g_{2}(t,n_{q})+g_{3}(t,n_{q})\bigg)\|(\widehat{u}-\bar{u})(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}.

Using the same argument in
Proposition

5.6
, let
t^{*}\in[0,t]
be such that

\displaystyle\sup_{s\in[0,t]}\|(\widehat{u}-\bar{u})(\cdot,s)\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}=\|(\widehat{u}-\bar{u})(\cdot,t^{*})\|_{L^{2}(p+q)},

then we know that

\displaystyle\|(\widehat{u}-\bar{u})(\cdot,t^{*})\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{t^{*}}\bigg(4L_{1}^{2}+g_{2}(s,n_{p})+g_{3}(s,n_{p})

\displaystyle+g_{2}(s,n_{q})+g_{3}(s,n_{q})\bigg)\|(\widehat{u}-\bar{u})(\cdot,s)\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}ds

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{t^{*}}\bigg(4L_{1}^{2}+g_{2}(s,n_{p})+g_{3}(s,n_{p})

\displaystyle+g_{2}(s,n_{q})+g_{3}(s,n_{q})\bigg)\|(\widehat{u}-\bar{u})(\cdot,t^{*})\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}ds

\displaystyle\leq\|(\widehat{u}-\bar{u})(\cdot,t^{*})\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}\int_{0}^{t^{*}}\bigg(4L_{1}^{2}+g_{2}(s,n_{p})+g_{3}(s,n_{p})

\displaystyle+g_{2}(s,n_{q})+g_{3}(s,n_{q})\bigg)ds

\displaystyle\|(\widehat{u}-\bar{u})(\cdot,t^{*})\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{t^{*}}\bigg(4L_{1}^{2}+g_{2}(s,n_{p})+g_{3}(s,n_{p})+g_{2}(s,n_{q})+g_{3}(s,n_{q})\bigg)ds.

Now since
t^{*}\leq t
and

\displaystyle\|(\widehat{u}-\bar{u})(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}\leq\|(\widehat{u}-\bar{u})(\cdot,t^{*})\|_{L^{2}(p+q)},

we know that

\displaystyle\|(\widehat{u}-\bar{u})(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{t^{*}}\bigg(4L_{1}^{2}+g_{2}(s,n_{p})+g_{3}(s,n_{p})+g_{2}(s,n_{q})+g_{3}(s,n_{q})\bigg)ds.

Moreover, by inspection, we can see that

\displaystyle 4L_{1}^{2}+g_{2}(s,n_{p})+g_{3}(s,n_{p})+g_{2}(s,n_{q})+g_{3}(s,n_{q})

is monotone in
s
, which means that

\displaystyle\|(\widehat{u}-\bar{u})(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}

\displaystyle\leq\bigg(4L_{1}^{2}+g_{2}(s,n_{p})+g_{3}(s,n_{p})+g_{2}(s,n_{q})+g_{3}(s,n_{q})\bigg)t.

Putting this altogether and using the fact that we are working under the regime of event
A_{P}\cap A_{Q}
, we can use
Lemma

5.10
for the zero-time NTK for samples from
p
and
q
to get that with probability
\geq 1-n_{p}^{-A}-n_{q}^{-A}
, we have

\displaystyle\|(\widehat{u}-\bar{u})(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}

\displaystyle\leq 4L_{1}^{2}t+g_{2}(s,n_{p})t+g_{3}(s,n_{p})t+g_{2}(s,n_{q})t+g_{3}(s,n_{q})t,

but the right-hand side of the inequality is just

\displaystyle 4L_{1}^{2}t

\displaystyle+4L_{1}L_{2}t^{3/2}\|\Pi_{K_{0}}(f^{*})\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}\Big(1+2L_{1}^{3}t^{2}\sqrt{2(2L_{1}^{2}+3/2)\frac{A\log(n_{p})+\log(2M_{\Theta})}{n_{p}}}\Big)^{1/2}

\displaystyle+4L_{1}L_{2}t^{3/2}\|\Pi_{K_{0}}(f^{*})\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}\Big(1+2L_{1}^{3}t^{2}\sqrt{2(2L_{1}^{2}+3/2)\frac{A\log(n_{q})+\log(2M_{\Theta})}{n_{q}}}\Big)^{1/2}

\displaystyle+t^{2}\cdot\sqrt{2}L_{1}^{3}\|\Pi_{K_{0}}(f^{*})\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}\sqrt{2L_{1}^{2}(2L_{1}^{2}+3/2)\frac{A\log(n_{p})+\log(2M_{\Theta})}{n_{p}}}

\displaystyle+t^{2}\cdot\sqrt{2}L_{1}^{3}\|\Pi_{K_{0}}(f^{*})\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}\sqrt{2L_{1}^{2}(2L_{1}^{2}+3/2)\frac{A\log(n_{q})+\log(2M_{\Theta})}{n_{p}}}.

This means that

\|(\widehat{u}-\bar{u})(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}=O(t^{5/2}).

Moreover, we get the result using the fact that

\|\Pi_{K_{0}}(f^{*})\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}\leq\|f^{*}\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}.\qed

Proof of
Proposition

5.13
.

Consider following calculation

\displaystyle\big|\widehat{T}_{test}(t)-\overline{T}(t)\big|

\displaystyle=\bigg|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\widehat{u}(x,t)d(\widehat{p}_{test}-\widehat{q}_{test})(x)-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\bar{u}(x,t)d(p-q)(x)\bigg|

\displaystyle=\bigg|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\widehat{u}(x,t)d(\widehat{p}_{test}-\widehat{q}_{test})(x)-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\widehat{u}(x,t)d(p-q)(x)

\displaystyle+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\widehat{u}(x,t)d(p-q)(x)-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\bar{u}(x,t)d(p-q)(x)\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq\underbrace{\bigg|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\widehat{u}(x,t)d\big[(\widehat{p}_{test}-p)+(q-\widehat{q}_{test})\big](x)\bigg|}_{A_{1}}

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\bigg|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(\widehat{u}-\bar{u})(x,t)d(p-q)(x)\bigg|}_{A_{2}}.

Let us deal with
A_{2}
first and then with
A_{1}
. Note that

\displaystyle A_{2}

\displaystyle=\bigg|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(\widehat{u}-\bar{u})(x,t)dp(x)-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(\widehat{u}-\bar{u})(x,t)dq(x)\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq\bigg|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(\widehat{u}-\bar{u})(x,t)dp(x)\bigg|+\bigg|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(\widehat{u}-\bar{u})(x,t)dq(x)\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|(\widehat{u}-\bar{u})(x,t)|d(p+q)(x)

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{2}\|\widehat{u}-\bar{u}(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{2}(p+q)}.

So we can use
Proposition

C.1
for
A_{2}
and will use this as part of the final bound. Notice that
A_{1}
is actually bounds
|\widehat{T}_{test}(t)-\widehat{T}_{pop}(t)|
. Now let us bound
A_{1}
. First note that

\displaystyle A_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\underbrace{\bigg|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\widehat{u}(x,t)d(\widehat{p}_{test}-p)\bigg|}_{A_{1,p}}+\underbrace{\bigg|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\widehat{u}(x,t)d(q-\widehat{q}_{test})\bigg|}_{A_{1,q}}.

To bound
A_{1,p}
and
A_{1,q}
, we will aim to use Hoeffding’s inequality, but we must first show that
|\widehat{u}(x,t)|
is bounded. To this end, consider the time-integrated form of
\widehat{u}(x,t)=f(x,\widehat{\theta}(t))
. Recalling the density-specific residuals

\displaystyle\widehat{e}_{p}(x,t)=\big(f(x^{\prime},\widehat{\theta}(t))-1\big)

\displaystyle\widehat{e}_{q}(x,t)=\big(f(x^{\prime},\widehat{\theta}(t))+1\big),

and using
Assumption

5.2
, we have

\displaystyle|f(x,\widehat{\theta}(t))|

\displaystyle=\bigg|-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\bigg(\mathbb{E}_{x^{\prime}\sim\widehat{p}}\langle\nabla_{\theta}f(x,\widehat{\theta}(s)),\nabla_{\theta}f(x^{\prime},\widehat{\theta}(s))\rangle_{\Theta}\widehat{e}_{p}(x^{\prime},s)

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}_{x^{\prime}\sim\widehat{q}}\langle\nabla_{\theta}f(x,\widehat{\theta}(s)),\nabla_{\theta}f(x^{\prime},\widehat{\theta}(s))\rangle_{\Theta}\widehat{e}_{q}(x^{\prime},s)\bigg)ds\bigg|.

It is important to note that in the equation above,
\widehat{p}
and
\widehat{q}
are training datasets (
not

\widehat{p}_{test}
and
\widehat{q}_{test}
), and with this in mind, we continue as

\displaystyle|f(x,\widehat{\theta}(t))|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}_{x^{\prime}\sim\widehat{p}}\underbrace{|\langle\nabla_{\theta}f(x,\widehat{\theta}(s)),\nabla_{\theta}f(x^{\prime},\widehat{\theta}(s))\rangle_{\Theta}|}_{\leq L_{1}^{2}}|\widehat{e}_{p}(x^{\prime},s)|

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}_{x^{\prime}\sim\widehat{q}}\underbrace{|\langle\nabla_{\theta}f(x,\widehat{\theta}(s)),\nabla_{\theta}f(x^{\prime},\widehat{\theta}(s))\rangle_{\Theta}|}_{\leq L_{1}^{2}}|\widehat{e}_{q}(x^{\prime},s)|ds

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}L_{1}^{2}\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}_{x^{\prime}\sim\widehat{p}}|\widehat{e}_{p}(x^{\prime},s)|+\mathbb{E}_{x^{\prime}\sim\widehat{q}}|\widehat{e}_{q}(x^{\prime},s)|ds

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}L_{1}^{2}\int_{0}^{t}\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|f(x,\widehat{\theta}(s))-1|d\widehat{p}(x)+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|f(x,\widehat{\theta}(s))+1|d\widehat{q}(x)\bigg)ds.

Using
Lemma

D.1
with
a(x,t)=f(x,\widehat{\theta}(s))-1
and
b(x,t)=f(x,\widehat{\theta}(s))+1
, we know that the right hand side of the equation above is decreasing if
\widehat{L}(\widehat{\theta}(s))
is decreasing. Indeed, recall

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\widehat{L}(\widehat{\theta}(t))=\langle\nabla_{\theta}\widehat{L}(\widehat{\theta}(t)),\dot{\widehat{\theta}}(t)\rangle_{\Theta}=-\|\nabla_{\theta}\widehat{L}(\widehat{\theta}(t))\|_{\Theta}\leq 0.

This means that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|f(x,\widehat{\theta}(s))-1|d\widehat{p}(x)+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|f(x,\widehat{\theta}(s))+1|d\widehat{q}(x)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|f(x,\widehat{\theta}(0))-1|d\widehat{p}(x)+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|f(x,\widehat{\theta}(0))+1|d\widehat{q}(x)=2.

Plugging this back in, we get that

\displaystyle|f(x,\widehat{\theta}(t))|\leq L_{1}^{2}t.

Because we have boundedness, we can use
Lemma

D.2
. Reworking the probability and lower bound in Hoeffding’s inequality, we see that

\displaystyle A_{1,p}\leq L_{1}^{2}t\sqrt{2\frac{A\log(m_{p})}{m_{p}}}

with probability
\geq 1-m_{p}^{-A}

\displaystyle A_{1,q}\leq L_{1}^{2}t\sqrt{2\frac{A\log(m_{q})}{m_{q}}}

with probability
\geq 1-m_{q}^{-A}

\displaystyle A_{1}\leq L_{1}^{2}t\sqrt{2}\bigg(\sqrt{\frac{A\log(m_{p})}{m_{p}}}+\sqrt{\frac{A\log(m_{q})}{m_{q}}}\bigg)

\geq 1-m_{p}^{-A}-m_{q}^{-A}

A_{2}

C.1

and

\geq 1-(m_{p}^{-A}+m_{q}^{-A}+n_{p}^{-A}+n_{q}^{-A})

\geq 1-(m_{p}^{-A}+m_{q}^{-A}+n_{p}^{-A}+n_{q}^{-A})
we have

\displaystyle\big|\widehat{T}_{test}(t)-\overline{T}(t)\big|

\displaystyle\leq C_{1L_{1},A,m_{p},m_{q}}t+C_{L_{1},L_{2},f^{*}}t^{3/2}

\displaystyle+C_{L_{1},f^{*},n_{p},n_{q},M_{\Theta},A}t^{2}

\displaystyle+C_{L_{1},L_{2},f^{*},n_{p},n_{q},M_{\Theta},A}t^{5/2},

C.1

∎

\displaystyle\widehat{L}(\widehat{\theta}(s))=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\widehat{u}(x,s)-1|^{2}d\widehat{p}(x)+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\widehat{u}(x,s)+1|^{2}d\widehat{q}(x)

\widehat{T}_{train}(t)

Appendix D
Helper Lemmas

Lemma D.1
.

Let
a(x,t),b(x,t):\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
be differentiable functions in
t
and let
d\widehat{p}(x)
and
d\widehat{q}(x)
be discrete probability measures supported only on a finite number of Dirac masses. Then

\displaystyle g(t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|a(x,t)|^{2}d\widehat{p}(x)+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|b(x,t)|^{2}d\widehat{q}(x)

is decreasing if and only if

\displaystyle h(t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|a(x,t)|d\widehat{p}(x)+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|b(x,t)|d\widehat{q}(x)

is decreasing.

Proof.

We will take the derivatives of both
g(t)
and
h(t)
with respect to time and compare them, but we will restrict the integrals to
\text{supp}(\widehat{p})_{+}=\{x\in\text{supp}(\widehat{p}):|a(x,t)|>0\}
and
\text{supp}(\widehat{q})_{+}=\{x\in\text{supp}(\widehat{q}):|b(x,t)|>0\}
. In particular, consider

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}g(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{dt}\Bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|a(x,t)|^{2}d\widehat{p}(x)+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|b(x,t)|^{2}d\widehat{q}(x)\Bigg)

\displaystyle=\int_{\text{supp}(\widehat{p})_{+}}\partial_{t}|a(x,t)|^{2}d\widehat{p}(x)+\int_{\text{supp}(\widehat{q})_{+}}\partial_{t}|b(x,t)|^{2}d\widehat{q}(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{\text{supp}(\widehat{p})_{+}}2|a(x,t)|\text{sgn}(a(x,t))\partial_{t}a(x,t)d\widehat{p}(x)

\displaystyle+\int_{\text{supp}(\widehat{q})_{+}}2|b(x,t)|\text{sgn}(b(x,t))\partial_{t}b(x,t)d\widehat{q}(x).

For
h(t)
, we get

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}h(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{dt}\Bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|a(x,t)|d\widehat{p}(x)+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|b(x,t)|d\widehat{q}(x)\Bigg)

\displaystyle=\int_{\text{supp}(\widehat{p})_{+}}\partial_{t}|a(x,t)|d\widehat{p}(x)+\int_{\text{supp}(\widehat{q})_{+}}\partial_{t}|b(x,t)|d\widehat{q}(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{\text{supp}(\widehat{p})_{+}}\text{sgn}(a(x,t))\partial_{t}a(x,t)d\widehat{p}(x)+\int_{\text{supp}(\widehat{q})_{+}}\text{sgn}(b(x,t))\partial_{t}b(x,t)d\widehat{q}(x).

Because we are using points only in
\text{supp}(\widehat{p})_{+}
and
\text{supp}(\widehat{q})_{+}
and since the supports of
d\widehat{p}
and
d\widehat{q}
are discrete measures, we can define

\displaystyle C(t)

\displaystyle=2\max\bigg\{\max_{x\in\text{supp}(\widehat{p})_{+}}|a(x,t)|,\max_{x\in\text{supp}(\widehat{q})_{+}}|b(x,t)|\bigg\}>0

\displaystyle c(t)

\displaystyle=2\min\bigg\{\min_{x\in\text{supp}(\widehat{p})_{+}}|a(x,t)|,\min_{x\in\text{supp}(\widehat{q})_{+}}|b(x,t)|\bigg\}>0.

Notice that the assumption that
c(t)>0
heavily depends on that the measures
d\widehat{p}
and
d\widehat{q}
are composed of a finite number of Dirac measures. Now, notice that

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}g(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{\text{supp}(\widehat{p})_{+}}\underbrace{2|a(x,t)|}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\leq C(t)\\
\geq c(t)\end{subarray}}\text{sgn}(a(x,t))\partial_{t}a(x,t)d\widehat{p}(x)

\displaystyle+\int_{\text{supp}(\widehat{q})_{+}}\underbrace{2|b(x,t)|}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\leq C(t)\\
\geq c(t)\end{subarray}}\text{sgn}(b(x,t))\partial_{t}b(x,t)d\widehat{q}(x)

\displaystyle\implies c(t)\frac{d}{dt}h(t)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{d}{dt}g(t)\leq C(t)\frac{d}{dt}h(t).

Since
\frac{d}{dt}h(t)
and
\frac{d}{dt}g(t)
are off by positive factors, we see that if one is decreasing, the other must also be decreasing. This proves the lemma.
∎

D.1
Concentration Inequalities

Lemma D.2

(Hoeffding’s Inequality)
.

Suppose
\{X_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
are independent random variables with
|X_{i}|\leq L
, then for all
t\geq 0

\displaystyle\text{Pr}\bigg\{\Big|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(X_{i}-\mathbb{E}[X_{i}])\Big|\geq t\bigg\}\leq 2\exp\bigg(-\frac{nt^{2}}{2L^{2}}\bigg).

Lemma D.3

(Hoeffding’s Subgaussian Inequality)
.

Suppose
\{X_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
are independent
\sigma_{i}
-subgaussian random variables with
X_{i}
having mean
\mu
, then for all
t\geq 0

\displaystyle\text{Pr}\bigg\{\Big|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(X_{i}-\mu_{i})\Big|\geq t\bigg\}\leq 2\exp\bigg(-\frac{t}{2\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{i}^{2}}\bigg).

Lemma D.4

(Matrix Bernstein)
.

Let
X_{i}
be a sequence of
n
independent, random, real-valued matrices of size
d_{1}
-by-
d_{2}
. Assume that
\mathbb{E}X_{i}=0
and
\|X_{i}\|\leq L
for each
i
and
\nu>0
be such that

\displaystyle\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}X_{i}X_{i}^{\top}\|,\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}X_{i}^{\top}X_{i}\|\leq\nu.

Then for any
t\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\text{Pr}\Big[\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\|\geq t\Big]\leq(d_{1}+d_{2})\exp\Big\{-\frac{nt^{2}}{2(\nu+Lt/3)}\Big\}.
