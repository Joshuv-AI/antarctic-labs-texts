A categorical treatment of the Radon-Nikodym theorem and martingales
Ruben Van Belle

Abstract
In this paper we will give a categorical proof of the Radon-Nikodym theorem. We will do this by describing the trivial version of the result on finite probability spaces as a natural isomorphism. We then proceed to Kan extend this isomorphism to obtain the result for general probability spaces. Moreover, we observe that conditional expectation naturally appears in the construction of the right Kan extensions. Using this we can represent martingales, a special type of stochastic processes, categorically. We then repeat the same construction for the case where everything is enriched over 𝐂𝐌𝐞𝐭 \mathbf{CMet} , the category of complete metric spaces and 1 1 -Lipschitz maps. In the enriched context, we can give a categorical proof of a martingale convergence theorem, by showing that a certain functor preserves certain cofiltered limits.



Keywords
: Radon-Nikodym derivative, martingale, conditional expectation, Kan extension, enriched category.

Introduction

The Radon-Nikodym theorem gives a correspondence between random variables and measures, two important concepts in probability theory and measure theory. If we fix a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
, then we can look at the
random variables
on this probability space and the measures that are
absolutely continuous
with respect to
\mathbb{P}
. The Radon-Nikodym theorem tells us that there is a canonical correspondence between these two. The random variable associated to a measure
\mu
that is absolutely continuous with respect to
\mathbb{P}
is called the
Radon-Nikodym derivative with respect to
\mathbb{P}
. The classical proof gives a concrete construction of these Radon-Nikodym derivatives and relies on the Hahn decomposition theorem, e.g. Theorem 31.B in
[
5
]
, Theorem 4.2.2 in
[
2
]
and Theorem 3.2.2 in
[
1
]
. Important applications of this result are the existence of conditional expectation and the Girsanov theorem in stochastic calculus.

In section
2
of this paper, we will give a
categorical proof
for this result. Moreover, we will not only prove that there is a bijection between random variables and measures, but also that this bijection is an
isometry
. We will do this by starting with the trivial case, when
\Omega
is finite. We translate this categorically as a natural isomorphism between certain functors. We will then proceed by
Kan extending
the trivial, finite version of the result to the general result. This happens in two parts. The first part (section
2.2
) is straightforward and purely categorical, not relying on any results in measure theory. The second part of the proof (section
2.3
) does require some measure theory, in particular it relies on the Riesz-Fischer theorem (Theorem
2.1
). Furthermore, the concept of conditional expectation naturally arises from the Kan extension construction in section
2.3
.

In section
3
, we will focus on
martingales
, a special class of stochastic processes. Important examples of martingales are Brownian motion and unbiased random walks. Furthermore, martingales have nice convergence properties, which are described by
Doob’s martingale convergence theorem
. The proof of this result relies on stopping times, the optional stopping theorem and several lemmas about upcrossings by stochastic processes. The original proof can be found in section XI.14 in
[
3
]
. We will give a categorical proof of a weaker version of this result. We do this by showing that a certain class of functors preserve certain cofiltered limits (Theorem
3.16
). By applying this to the functors representing random variables from section
3.3
, we immediately obtain a proof for a weaker martingale convergence theorem. Moreover, if we apply the same result to the functor representing measures from section
3.3
, we find a Kolmogorov extension-type theorem. In this section we use the results from section
2
. However, we first lift everything to the
enriched
setting. We consider everything to be enriched over the category of
complete metric spaces
. This part is crucial to obtain the main result in this section (Theorem
3.16
).

In this paper we will consider every distance function to be an
extended pseudometric
, i.e. a function
d:X\times X\to[0,\infty]
on a set
X
such that for all
x,y\in X
,
d(x,x)=0
and
d(x,y)=d(y,x)
, satisfying the triangle-inequality. However,we will refer to an extended pseudometric
d
as just ‘
metric
’ and to the pair
(X,d)
as just ‘
metric space
’. The category of these metric spaces with the
1
-Lipschitz maps between them is denoted by
Met
and the full subcategory of
complete
metric spaces by
CMet
.

For a measurable map
f:X\to Y
and a measure
\mu
on
X
, we write
\mu\circ f^{-1}
to mean the
pushforward measure
of
\mu
along
f
, i.e. for every measurable subset
E
of
Y
,

\left(\mu\circ f^{-1}\right)(E):=\mu(f^{-1}(E)).

Furthermore, for two real number
a
and
b
, the minimum of the two is denoted by
a\wedge b
, while the maximum of the two is denoted by
a\vee b
.

2
The Radon-Nikodym theorem

The Radon-Nikodym theorem is an important result in measure theory and probability theory. The goal of this section is to give a
categorical
proof of this result. We will start this section by recalling the result and explaining its applications in probability theory. We will then discuss the trivial
finite
version of the Radon-Nikodym theorem and explain how this translate categorically. After that, we focus on ‘
Kan extending
’ the trivial finite result to the general result.

Probability theory studies
random variables
and
probability distributions
. The connection between these two is given by the Radon-Nikodym theorem.

Let
\mathbf{\Omega}:=(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
be a probability space. We say that two measurable functions
f,g:\Omega\to[0,\infty)
are
\mathbb{P}
-almost surely equal if
\mathbb{P}(f=g)=1
and we write
f=_{\mathbb{P}}g
. This defines an equivalence relation on the set
\textbf{Mble}(\Omega,[0,\infty))
of measurable functions
X\to[0,\infty)
.
The set

\textbf{Mble}(\Omega,[0,\infty))/=_{\mathbb{P}}

becomes a metric space, by endowing it with the
L^{1}
-metric
defined by

d_{L^{1}}(f,g):=\int\lvert f-g\rvert\text{d}\mathbb{P},

for all
f,g\in\textbf{Mble}(\Omega,[0,\infty))/=_{\mathbb{P}}
. We will denote this space of random variables by
\mathrm{RV}(\mathbf{\Omega})
. For a real number
r>0
, let
\mathrm{RV}_{r}(\mathbf{\Omega})
be the subspace of random variable
f
such that
\mathbb{P}(f\leq r)=1
.

An important result about the space of random variables that we will need later is the Riesz-Fischer theorem.

Theorem 2.1

(Riesz-Fischer)
.

Let
\mathbf{\Omega}:=(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
be a probability space and let
r>0
be a natural number. The metric spaces
\mathrm{RV}(\mathbf{\Omega})
and
\mathrm{RV}_{r}(\mathbf{\Omega})
are complete.

Proof.

This is Theorem 2.2 in
[
9
]
.
∎

For a measure on
\mu
on a measure space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F})
, we say that
\mu

is absolutely continuous with respect to
\mathbb{P}
if
\mu(A)=0
for all measurable subsets
A\subseteq\Omega
such that
\mathbb{P}(A)=0
; this is denoted as
\mu\ll\mathbb{P}
. The set of measures on
(\Omega,\mathcal{F})
that are absolutely continuous with respect to
\mathbb{P}
becomes a metric space, by endowing it with the
total variation metric
defined by

\displaystyle d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\mu,\nu)

\displaystyle:=\lvert\mu-\nu\rvert(\Omega)

\displaystyle=\sup\left\{\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\lvert\mu(A_{n})-\nu(A_{n})\rvert\mid(A_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}\text{ measurable partition of }\Omega\right\},

for all
\mu,\nu\ll\mathbb{P}
. We denote this space of measures by
\mathrm{M}(\mathbf{\Omega})
. For a real number
r>0
, we write
\mathrm{M}_{r}(\mathbf{\Omega})
for the subspace of measures
\mu
on
(\Omega,\mathcal{F})
such that
\mu\leq r\mathbb{P}
, i.e.
\mu(A)\leq r\mathbb{P}(A)
for all
A\in\mathcal{F}
.

We have the following well-known result, for which we will give a short proof. A proof can also be found in section III.7.4 in
[
4
]
.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
\mathbf{\Omega}:=(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
be a probability space and let
r>0
be a natural number. The metric spaces
\mathrm{M}(\mathbf{\Omega})
and
\mathrm{M}_{r}(\mathbf{\Omega})
are complete.

Proof.

Let
(\mu_{n})_{n}
be a Cauchy sequence in
\mathrm{M}(\mathbf{\Omega})
. Since
\lvert\mu_{p}(A)-\mu_{q}(A)\rvert\leq d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\mu_{p},\mu_{q})
for all
A\in\mathcal{F}
, it follows that
(\mu_{n}(A))_{n}
is a Cauchy sequence in
[0,\infty)
. Define a map
\mu:\mathcal{F}\to[0,\infty)
by

A\mapsto\lim_{n\to\infty}\mu_{n}(A).

It is clear that
\mu
is finitely additive.
Let
(A_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
be a decreasing sequence of measurable subsets such that
A_{n}\downarrow\emptyset
.

For
\epsilon>0
, there is an
N
such that for
d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\mu_{p},\mu_{q})<\frac{\epsilon}{2}
for all
p,q\geq N
. Since
\mu_{N}
is
\sigma
-additive, there is an
M
such that for
m\geq M
,
\mu_{N}(A_{m})<\frac{\epsilon}{2}
. Therefore, for
n\geq N
and
m\geq M
, we have

\mu_{n}(A_{m})\leq\mu_{N}(A_{m})+d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\mu_{n},\mu_{N})\leq\epsilon.

Taking
n\to\infty
, shows that
\mu(A_{m})<\epsilon
and therefore
\lim_{m\to\infty}\mu(A_{m})=0
. We conclude that
\mu
is
\sigma
-additive.

We finish the proof by showing that
(\mu_{n})_{n}
converges to
\mu
in
\mathrm{M}(\mathbf{\Omega})
. Let
K\geq 1
be a natural number.

Then for every countable partition
(A_{k})_{k=1}^{\infty}
and
p,q\geq N
,
\sum_{k=1}^{K}\lvert\mu_{p}(A_{k})-\mu_{q}(A_{k})\rvert\leq d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\mu_{p},\mu_{q})<\frac{\epsilon}{2}.

Taking first
p\to\infty
and then
K\to\infty
gives that

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\lvert\mu(A_{k})-\mu_{q}(A_{k})\rvert<\frac{\epsilon}{2},

for every countable partition
(A_{k})_{k=1}^{\infty}
and
q\geq N
.

By taking the supremum over all countable partitions, we find that

d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\mu,\mu_{q})<\frac{\epsilon}{2}

for all
q\geq N
. This shows that
\mu_{n}\to\mu
in
\mathrm{M}(\mathbf{\Omega})
.
∎

The space of random variables and the space of measures on
\mathbf{\Omega}
are connected in the following way. For a random variable
f\in\mathrm{RV}(\mathbf{\Omega})
, define a measure
\varphi(f)
on
(\Omega,\mathcal{F})
by the assignment

A\mapsto\int_{A}f\text{d}\mathbb{P}.

If
\mathbb{P}(A)=0
, then
\varphi(f)(A)=0
and therefore
\varphi(f)\in\mathrm{M}(\mathbf{\Omega})
. Moreover for
f,g\in\mathrm{RV}(\mathbf{\Omega})
and a measurable partition
(A_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
of
\Omega
,

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left\lvert\int_{A_{n}}f\text{d}\mathbb{P}-\int_{A_{n}}g\text{d}\mathbb{P}\right\rvert\leq\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\int_{A_{n}}\lvert f-g\rvert\text{d}\mathbb{P}=\int\lvert f-g\rvert\text{d}\mathbb{P}.

Taking the supremum over all such partitions gives that
d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\varphi(f),\varphi(g))\leq d_{L^{1}}(f,g)
. Therefore the map
\varphi:\mathrm{RV}(\mathbf{\Omega})\to\mathrm{M}(\mathbf{\Omega})
is
1
-Lipschitz.

Theorem 2.3

(Radon-Nikodym)
.

The map
\varphi
is an isomorphism. In particular, for every measure
\mu
such that
\mu\ll\mathbb{P}
there exists a
\mathbb{P}
-almost surely unique measurable map
f:\Omega\to[0,\infty)
such that for all
A\in\mathcal{F}
,

\mu(A)=\int_{A}f\text{d}\mathbb{P}.

The
f
in Theorem
2.3
is called the
Radon-Nikodym derivative of
\mu
with respect to
\mathbb{P}
and is denoted as
\frac{\text{d}\mu}{\text{d}\mathbb{P}}
.

The following example is an application of the Radon-Nikodym theorem in probability theory. The existence of
conditional expectation
can be proven using this result. The concept of conditional expectation is important in
martingale
theory, which we will discuss further in Section
3
.

Example 2.4

(Conditional expectation)
.

Consider two probability spaces
\mathbf{\Omega}_{1}:=(\Omega_{1},\mathcal{F}_{1},\mathbb{P}_{1})
and
\mathbf{\Omega}_{2}:=(\Omega_{2},\mathcal{F}_{2},\mathbb{P}_{2})
and let
g:\mathbf{\Omega}_{1}\to\mathbf{\Omega}_{2}
be a measure preserving map, i.e.
\mathbb{P}_{1}\circ g^{-1}=\mathbb{P}_{2}
.

For a random variable
f\in\mathrm{RV}(\mathbf{\Omega}_{1})
, we can define a measure
\mu
on
\mathbf{\Omega}_{2}
as

\mu(B)=\int_{g^{-1}(B)}f\text{d}\mathbb{P}_{1}

for all
B\in\mathcal{F}_{2}
. If
\mathbb{P}_{2}(B)=0
, then
\mathbb{P}_{1}(g^{-1}(B))=0
and therefore
\mu\in\mathrm{M}(\mathbf{\Omega}_{2})
.

By the Radon-Nikodym theorem (Theorem
2.3
), there exists a unique
\tilde{f}\in\mathrm{RV}(\mathbf{\Omega}_{2})
such that

\int_{g^{-1}(B)}f\text{d}\mathbb{P}_{1}=\mu(B)=\int_{B}\tilde{f}\text{d}\mathbb{P}_{2}.

(1)

The random variable
\tilde{f}
is called the
conditional expectation of
f
with respect to
g
and is denoted as
\mathbb{E}[f\mid g]
. Because of the uniqueness in the Radon-Nikodym theorem, equation (
1
) is the defining property for the random variable
\mathbb{E}[f\mid g]
.

2.1
The finite Radon-Nikodym theorem

In the case that we are working with a
finite
probability space, the Radon-Nikodym becomes trivial. We will discuss this trivial version in this section and explain how this can be expressed categorically. To do this we will define two functors, one expressing random variables and one expressing measures. The finite version of the Radon-Nikodym theorem then corresponds to saying that these functors are isomorphic.

A
finite probability space
is a probability space whose underlying set
A
is finite and whose
\sigma
-algebra is the whole powerset
\mathcal{P}(A)
. We will write
(A,p)
instead of
(A,\mathcal{P}(A),p)
. For an element
a\in A
, we write
p_{a}
or
p(a)
to mean
p(\{a\})
.

We denote the category of probability spaces and measure-preserving maps by
Prob
and the full subcategory of finite probability measure by
\textbf{Prob}_{f}
. The inclusion functor
\textbf{Prob}_{f}\to\textbf{Prob}
is denoted by
i
.

We start by defining a functor of measures
\mathrm{M}^{f}:\textbf{Prob}_{f}\to\textbf{Met}
. This functor sends a finite probability space
(A,p)
to
\mathrm{M}(A,p)
and a measure preserving map
s:(A,p)\to(B,q)
of finite probability spaces to the
1
-Lipschitz map
\mathrm{M}^{f}(s):\mathrm{M}(A,p)\to\mathrm{M}(B,q)
, which is defined by the assignment

m\mapsto m\circ s^{-1}.

Similarly we can define a functor
\mathrm{M}^{f}_{r}:\textbf{Prob}_{f}\to\textbf{Met}
for every positive real number
r
.

We can also define a functor of random variables
\mathrm{RV}^{f}:\textbf{Prob}_{f}\to\textbf{Met}
in the following way. On objects this functor is defined by sending a finite probability space
(A,p)
to the metric space
\mathrm{RV}(A,p)
. On morphisms this functor sends a measure preserving map
s:(A,p)\to(B,q)
of finite probability spaces to the
1
-Lipschitz map
\mathrm{RV}^{f}(s):\mathrm{RV}(A,p)\to\mathrm{RV}(B,q)
which is defined for
g\in\mathrm{RV}(A,p)
by

\mathrm{RV}^{f}(s)(g):(B,q)\to[0,\infty):b\mapsto\begin{cases}\frac{1}{q_{b}}\sum_{s(a)=b}p_{q}g(a)\text{ if }q_{b}\not=0\\
0\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

The map
\mathrm{RV}^{f}(s)(g)
does not depend on the representation of
g
and therefore it is well-defined. In a similar way we define functors
\mathrm{RV}^{f}_{r}:\textbf{Prob}_{f}\to\textbf{Met}
for real numbers
r>0
.

For a finite probability space
(A,p)
, we define the
1
-Lipschitz map
(\rho^{f}_{r})_{A}:\mathrm{RV}_{r}^{f}(A,p)\to\mathrm{M}^{f}_{r}(A,p)
by the assignment

g\mapsto(g(a)p_{a})_{a\in A}.

The finite version of the Radon-Nikodym theorem can now be expressed in the following way.

Proposition 2.5

(Finite bounded Radon-Nikodym)
.

The maps
((\rho^{f}_{r})_{A})_{(A,p)}
form a natural isomorphism
\rho^{f}_{r}:\mathrm{RV}_{r}^{f}\to\mathrm{M}_{r}^{f}
.

Proof.

It is easy to see that
(\rho^{f}_{r})_{A}
is well-defined and invertible for every finite probability space
(A,p)
. If follows now by Proposition
4.2
in
[
8
]
that this is an isomorphism of metric spaces. It is straightforward to check the naturality of
\rho^{f}_{r}
.
∎

Since
\mathrm{RV}_{r}^{f}
and
\mathrm{M}_{r}^{f}
are isomorphic by Proposition
2.5
, so are their right Kan extensions along the inclusion
i:\textbf{Prob}_{f}\to\textbf{Prob}
.

\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces q_{1_{E_{1},E_{2}}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces q_{1_{E_{1}}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathrm{M}_{r}^{f}(s_{1})}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces q_{1_{E_{2}}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathrm{M}_{r}^{f}(s_{2})}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces q_{1_{E_{1},E_{2}}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces q_{1_{E_{1}\cup E_{2}}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathrm{M}_{r}^{f}(s)}

Using the above diagrams we find

\mu_{y}(E_{1}\cup E_{2})=q_{1_{E_{1}\cup E_{2}}}(y)_{1}=q_{1_{E_{1},E_{2}}}(y)_{1}+q_{1_{E_{1},E_{2}}}(y)_{2}=q_{1_{E_{1}}}(y)_{1}+q_{1_{E_{2}}}(y)_{1}=\mu_{y}(E_{1})+\mu_{y}(E_{2}),

which shows that
\mu_{y}
is finitely additive.

Let
(E_{n})_{n}
be a sequence of measurable subsets of
\mathbf{\Omega}
that decreases to

. Because

, also

We can conclude that
\mu_{y}
is an element of
\mathrm{M}_{r}(\mathbf{\Omega})
.

The assignment

defines a map

. It can be checked that this map is
1
-Lipschitz and that it defines morphism of cones. Furthermore, this morphism of cones is unique. We can now conclude that
\mathrm{M}_{r}(\mathbf{\Omega})=\mathrm{Ran}_{i}\mathrm{M}_{r}(\mathbf{\Omega})
.
∎

We have just described how the functor
\mathrm{Ran}_{i}\mathrm{M}_{r}^{f}
behaves on objects. In the following proposition we will study how it acts on morphisms.

Proposition 2.7
.

For a measure preserving map of probability space

,

\mathrm{M}_{r}(f)(\mu)=\mu\circ f^{-1},

for all
\mu\in\mathrm{M}_{r}(\mathbf{\Omega}_{1}).

Proof.

By the universal property of right Kan extensions, we know that
M_{r}(f):M_{r}(\mathbf{\Omega}_{1})\to M_{r}(\mathbf{\Omega}_{2})
is the unique morphism such that

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathrm{M}_{r}(\mathbf{\Omega}_{1})}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathrm{M}_{r}(\mathbf{\Omega}_{2})}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathrm{M}_{r}^{f}(A,p)}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathrm{M}_{r}(f)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces p_{hf}^{1}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces p_{f}^{2}}

commutes for every measure-preserving map

, where
(A,p)
is some finite probability space. Here

and

are the projection maps defined in the proof of Theorem

. It is clear that the map
\mathrm{M}_{r}(\mathbf{\Omega}_{1})\to\mathrm{M}_{r}(\mathbf{\Omega}_{2})
defined by the assignment

satisfies this property and therefore the claim follows.
∎

Theorem
2.6
and Proposition
2.7
tell us that the functor
\mathrm{Ran}_{i}\mathrm{M}_{r}^{f}:\textbf{Prob}\to\mathbf{CMet}
expresses measures. We will therefore use the notation
\mathrm{M}_{r}:=\mathrm{Ran}_{i}\mathrm{M}_{r}^{f}
from now on.

Furthermore, note that in the proofs of Theorem

and Proposition

we have not used any non-trivial measure-theoretic results. The proofs in this section are straightforward categorical proofs.

For

, there is a natural transformation

and therefore a natural transformation
\mathrm{M}_{r}\to\mathrm{M}_{s}

for all probability spaces

. This gives us a diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\ldots.}

In the rest of this section, we will study what the colimit of this diagram looks like. The next proposition tells us how the colimiting functor acts on objects.

Proposition 2.8
.

Let
\mathbf{\Omega}
be a probability space, then
\mathrm{colim}D_{\mathrm{M}}(\mathbf{\Omega})=\mathrm{M}(\mathbf{\Omega}).

Proof.

Consider the subset

S:=\{\mu\in\mathrm{M}(\mathbf{\Omega})\mid\exists r>0:\mu\leq r\mathbb{P}\}.

We will show that
S
is dense in
\mathrm{M}(\mathbf{\Omega})
. For
\mu\in\mathrm{M}(\mathbf{\Omega})
, define

\mu_{n}:=\mu\wedge n\mathbb{P},

This means that

\mu_{n}(E)=\sup\left\{\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mu(E_{k})\wedge n\mathbb{P}(E_{k})\mid\bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty}E_{k}\subseteq E\right\},

and clearly
\mu_{n}\leq n\mathbb{P}
and
\mu_{n}\leq\mu
.

For a countable partition
(E_{k})_{k=1}^{\infty}
and a natural number
n\geq 1
, we find

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\lvert\mu(E_{k})-\mu_{n}(E_{k})\rvert=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mu(E_{k})-\mu_{n}(E_{k})=\mu(\Omega)-\mu(\Omega)\wedge n.

Taking the supremum over all countable partitions of
\Omega
gives

d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\mu,\mu_{n})\leq\mu(\Omega)-(\mu(\Omega)\wedge n)

and therefore
\mu_{n}\to\mu
in
\mathrm{M}(\mathbf{\Omega})
.

Thus, for any complete metric space
Y
and
1
-Lipschitz map
f:S\to Y
, there is a unique
1
-Lipschitz map
\tilde{f}:\mathrm{M}(\mathbf{\Omega})\to Y
. It follows that
\mathrm{colim}(D_{\mathrm{M}}(\mathbf{\Omega}))=\mathrm{M}(\mathbf{\Omega})
and therefore
(\mathrm{colim}D_{\mathrm{M}})(\mathbf{\Omega})=\mathrm{M}(\mathbf{\Omega})
.
∎

We will now discuss what the colimit of

does on morphisms.

Proposition 2.9
.

Let
\mathbf{\Omega}_{1}:=(\Omega_{1},\mathcal{F}_{1},\mathbb{P}_{1})
and
\mathbf{\Omega}_{2}:=(\Omega_{2},\mathcal{F}_{2},\mathbb{P}_{2})

-Lipschitz map
\mathrm{M}(\mathbf{\Omega}_{1})\to\mathrm{M}(\mathbf{\Omega}_{2})
defined by

\mu\mapsto\mu\circ f^{-1}.

Proof.

The map
(\mathrm{colim}D_{\mathrm{M}})(f)
is the unique map
\mathrm{M}(\mathbf{\Omega}_{1})\to\mathrm{M}(\mathbf{\Omega}_{2})
such that the following diagram commutes for every natural number
r>0

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathrm{M}(\mathbf{\Omega}_{1})}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathrm{M}(\mathbf{\Omega}_{2})}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathrm{M}_{r}(\mathbf{\Omega}_{1})}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathrm{M}_{r}(\mathbf{\Omega}_{2})}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathrm{M}_{r}(f)}

The

-Lipschitz maps

∎

and Proposition

tell us that the functor

2.3
The random variables functor
\mathrm{RV}

We will start this section by describing what the right Kan extension of the functor
\mathrm{RV}_{r}^{f}:\textbf{Prob}_{f}\to\mathbf{CMet}
along the functor

looks like.

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\textbf{Prob}_{f}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathbf{CMet}}}
Prob
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathrm{RV}_{r}^{f}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces i}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathrm{Ran}_{i}\mathrm{RV}_{r}^{f}}

We will do this by first showing how
\mathrm{Ran}_{i}\mathrm{RV}_{r}^{f}
acts on objects in Theorem
2.10
and then how it acts on morphisms in Proposition
2.11
. The conclusion will be that this right Kan extension describes bounded random variables on arbitrary probability spaces. We will therefore introduce the notation
\mathrm{RV}_{r}
to mean the functor
\mathrm{Ran}_{i}\mathrm{RV}_{r}^{f}:\textbf{Prob}\to\mathbf{CMet}
.

We will proceed to section by showing that these functors form a diagram

:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\ldots}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathrm{RV}_{1}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\ldots}

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\ldots}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathrm{RV}_{n}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\ldots}

In the remaining part of the section we will study the colimit of

. We will show that this colimiting functor describes random variables and we will therefore denote this functor as

Theorem 2.10
.

Let
\mathbf{\Omega}:=(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
be a probability space. Then

\textup{Ran}_{i}\mathrm{RV}_{r}^{f}(\mathbf{\Omega})=\mathrm{RV}_{r}(\mathbf{\Omega}).

Proof.

Let
U:\mathbf{\Omega}\downarrow i\to\textbf{Prob}_{f}
be the forgetful functor and let

denote the diagram

\mathbf{\Omega}\downarrow i\xrightarrow{U}\textbf{Prob}_{f}\xrightarrow{\mathrm{RV}_{r}^{f}}\mathbf{CMet}.

We will now show that
\mathrm{RV}_{r}(\mathbf{\Omega})=\lim D_{\mathbf{\Omega}}.

For a measure-preserving map
f:\mathbf{\Omega}\to(A,p)
where
(A,p)
is some finite probability space, define a map

by

p_{f}(g)(a):=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{p_{a}}\int_{f^{-1}(a)}g\text{d}\mathbb{P}\text{ if }p_{a}\not=0\\
0\text{ otherwise},\end{cases}

for every
g
in
\mathrm{RV}_{r}(\mathbf{\Omega})
and
a
in
A
. It is straightforward to check that the definition of
p_{f}
is independent of the choice of representative. It can also be checked that

is
1
-Lipschitz.
Consider a commutative diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbf{\Omega}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces(A,p)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces(B,q)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f_{1}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f_{2}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces s}

Let

in
\mathrm{RV}_{r}(\mathbf{\Omega})
and
b
in

, we have

\displaystyle\left[\left(\mathrm{RV}_{r}^{f}(s)\circ p_{f_{1}}(g)\right)(b)\right]q_{b}

\displaystyle=\sum_{a\in s^{-1}(b)}p_{a}p_{f_{1}}(g)(a)

\displaystyle=\sum_{a\in s^{-1}(b)}\int_{f^{-1}(a)}g\text{d}\mathbb{P}

\displaystyle=\int_{s^{-1}(b)}g\text{d}\mathbb{P}.

It now follows that
(\mathrm{RV}_{r}(\mathbf{\Omega}),(p_{f})_{f})
is a cone over the diagram
D_{\mathbf{\Omega}}
. We will now show that this cone is universal. To do that, we consider another cone
(Y,(q_{f})_{f})
over the diagram

.

For

and measure-preserving map
\mathbf{\Omega}\xrightarrow{f}(A,p)
, we define a simple function

as follows:

Consider a commutative diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbf{\Omega}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{(A,p)}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{(B,r)}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces g}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces s}

We find for every

It follows that

.
Note that
\mathbf{\Omega}\downarrow i
is cofiltered and therefore

forms a net in
\mathrm{RV}_{r}(\mathbf{\Omega})
. Suppose now that
(s_{f}^{y})_{f}
has a limit

in
\mathrm{RV}_{r}(\mathbf{\Omega})
. Then it is easy to see that

for all

. By the Riesz-Fischer theorem (Theorem

) we only need to show that

is a Cauchy net.

For this we will use the following two inequalities, which we will prove in the Appendix. For a commutative diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbf{\Omega}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{(A,p)}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{(B,r)}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces g}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces s}

we have

\mathbb{E}[\left(s_{g}^{y}\right)^{2}]\leq\mathbb{E}[(s_{f}^{y})^{2}].

(2)

and

0\leq d_{L^{1}}(s_{f}^{y},s_{g}^{y})^{2}\leq\mathbb{E}[(s_{f}^{y}-s_{g}^{y})^{2}]=\mathbb{E}[(s_{f}^{y})^{2}]-\mathbb{E}[(s_{g}^{y})^{2}]

(3)

Now by inequaltiy (

) we conclude that

is a bounded, monotone net and therefore it converges. Using (

) we can now conclude that

is a Cauchy net. The Riesz-Fischer theorem (Theorem

) tells us that the net
(s_{f}^{y})_{f}
converges to some

in
\mathrm{RV}_{r}(\mathbf{\Omega})
. This defines a map

. It can be checked that this is a

-Lipschitz map. Because

for every

in
\mathbf{\Omega}\downarrow i
it follows that this map is in fact a morphism of cones. Finally, it is straightforward to check that this is the unique morphism of cones. We can now conclude that
\mathrm{RV}_{r}(\mathbf{\Omega})=\mathrm{Ran}_{i}\mathrm{RV}_{r}^{f}(\mathbf{\Omega})
.
∎

We now know what the right Kan extension

does on objects, but not yet how it acts on morphisms. This is described in the following proposition.

3
The martingale convergence theorem

In this section we will focus on a special class of stochastic processes, namely
martingales
. These stochastic processes have nice convergence properties, of which we will prove one categorically later in this section. Important examples of martingales are Brownian motion and unbiased random walks.

Let
\mathbf{\Omega}:=(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
be a probability space and let
I
be a directed poset. A
filtration
is an indexed collection
(\mathcal{F}_{i})_{i\in I}
of
\sigma
-subalgebras of
\mathcal{F}
such that
\mathcal{F}_{i}\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{j}
for
i\leq j
and such that

\sigma\left(\bigcup_{i\in I}\mathcal{F}_{i}\right)=\mathcal{F}.

We say that
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\left(\mathcal{F}_{i}\right)_{i\in I},\mathbb{P})
is a
filtered probability space
. The probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F}_{i},\mathbb{P}\mid_{\mathcal{F}_{i}})
is denoted by
\mathbf{\Omega}_{i}
. For
i\leq j
in
I
, there is a measure preserving map
f_{ij}:\mathbf{\Omega}_{j}\to\mathbf{\Omega}_{j}
and for every
i\in I
there is a measure-preserving map
f_{i}:\mathbf{\Omega}\to\mathbf{\Omega}_{i}
.

An indexed collection
(X_{i})_{i\in I}
of random variables such that
X_{i}\in\mathrm{RV}(\mathbf{\Omega}_{i})
is called a
martingale
if

\mathbb{E}[X_{j}\mid f_{ij}]=X_{i}

for all
i\leq j
in
I
.

Martingales often have nice convergence properties. We will categorically prove a weaker version of the following
martingale convergence theorems
in section
3.4
.

Theorem 3.1

(Doob’s
L^{1}
martingale convergence theorem)
.

Let
(X_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
be a martingale such that

\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}\sup_{n}\mathbb{E}[X_{n}1_{\{X_{n}>\lambda\}}]=0,

then
(X_{n})_{n}
converges to a random variable
X
in
L^{1}
-norm and for all
n\geq 1
,

\mathbb{E}[X\mid f_{n}]=X_{n}.

Theorem 3.2

(Doob’s
L^{p}
martingale convergence theorem)
.

Let
p>1
and let
(X_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
be a martingale such that

\sup_{n}\mathbb{E}[X_{n}^{p}]<\infty,

then
(X_{n})_{n}
converges to a random variable
X
in
L^{p}
-norm and for all
n\geq 1
,

\mathbb{E}[X\mid f_{n}]=X_{n}.

To give a categorical proof, the setting from section
2
does not quite work. We need to change everything from section
2
to the
enriched
setting. We will enrich everything over the closed monoidal category
\mathbf{CMet}
, which we will discuss in section
3.1
. We then show in section
3.2
and section
3.3
that the results from section
2
still work when everything is enriched over
\mathbf{CMet}
. We then conclude section
3
by giving a categorical proof for a weaker version of the martingale convergence theorems in section
3.4
.

Remark 3.20
.

An alternative approach we could have taken is to define the category

Remark 3.21
