Hilton-Milnor’s theorem in ∞ \infty -topoi.
Samuel Lavenir

Abstract
. In this note we show that the classical theorem of Hilton-Milnor on finite wedges of suspension spaces remains valid in any ∞ \infty -topos. Our result relies on a version of James’ splitting proved in [ DH21 ] and uses only basic constructions native to any model of ∞ \infty -categories.



1.
Introduction

The aim of this article is to give an
\infty
-categorical treatment of the decomposition theorem for wedges of suspensions first proved by Hilton
[
Hil55
]
in the case of spheres, and generalized by Milnor
[
MAS72
]
to arbitrary suspension spaces. The classical form of this theorem states that given pointed connected CW-complexes
X_{1},\cdots,X_{n}
, there is a canonical homotopy equivalence

\prod_{w\in B(n)}\Omega\Sigma w(X_{1},\cdots,X_{n})\overset{\sim}{\mathrel{\mathchoice{{}\hbox{$\displaystyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\textstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle{\meno}$}}}\mathrel{\mkern-3.0mu}\rightarrow}\Omega\Sigma(X_{1}\vee\cdots\vee X_{n})

where
B(n)
denotes a basis for the free Lie algebra on
n
-generators. Several remarks are in order: first, given a word
w
in this free Lie algebra,
w(X_{1},\cdots,X_{n})
denotes the pointed CW-complex obtained by forming the smash product of the
X_{i}
in the order indicated by
w
. Secondly, the topology on the product space is
not
the product topology, but instead the
weak topology
with respect to the filtration by all finite products. Lastly, it should be noted that the assumption that the
X_{i}
are connected is essential and cannot be weakened.

Our main result is that the same statement holds true in any
\infty
-topos, in the sense of
[
Lur09
,
Rez10
,
TV05
]
. Aside from giving extended generality to the original statement, this new formulation makes the argument conceptually simpler; compare with traditional proofs
[
Hil55
, Theorem A]
,
[
Nei10
, Theorem 4.3.3]
,
[
Whi78
, Theorem 6.6]
. Our proof involves only basic categorical manipulations, as well as the combinatorics of Hall words which we review in
Section

4
and an
\infty
-categorical version of James’ splitting
[
DH21
]
. The homotopical input is entirely hidden in the higher coherences involved in the formation of limits and colimits in
\infty
-categories.

It should be noted that the contribution of this paper lies merely in the proof of Hilton-Milnor’s theorem using higher categorical techniques, rather than its statement. Indeed, the result could be simply derived from the usual theorem in the
\infty
-category of spaces by the fact that an
\infty
-topos
\mathcal{E}
admits a presentation

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces L:\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{C})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{E}:i}
\leftarrow
\rightarrow
\hookleftarrow
\rightarrow

as a left-exact localization of a presheaf
\infty
-category. Left-exactness of the left adjoint
L
implies that it commutes with all the operations
\Omega,\Sigma,\wedge
involved in the theorem, as well as with the weak product. It follows that the statement of the theorem in an arbitrary
\infty
-topos follows from the one in the
\infty
-category
\mathcal{S}
of spaces. In this work, we do not assume Hilton-Milnor’s theorem holds in spaces and prove it from first principles. The elementary form of our proof makes it a suitable reference for the reader familiar to
\infty
-categories and willing to take a modern approach on Hilton-Milnor’s theorem.

Categorical preliminaries

2.1.
Notational conventions

In this section we fix some conventions about our use of the language of
\infty
-categories. By an
\infty
-category we mean an
(\infty,1)
-category, ie. a weak
\omega
-category in which all
i
-morphisms are (weakly) invertible for
i>1
. Although we do not appeal to any such in the sequel, several equivalent models of
\infty
-categories are in use, amongst which the most common include quasicategories, simplicial categories, complete Segal spaces, Segal categories and relative categories. The sense in which all these models are equivalent is discussed extensively in the litterature, see for example
[
Ber07
]
and
[
JT07
]
. All of these models support well-defined notions of composition of maps, limits and colimits, functors, natural transformations, adjunctions, Kan extensions and so on.

In this paper we will be almost exclusively working inside a fixed
\infty
-category. Throughout we will be using the following abuse of terminology : when we say that a diagram
commutes
one should understand that there are homotopies filling all simplices of the diagram, and that these homotopies are
specified
as part of the data of the diagram. We never explicitly mention these homotopies since in all cases of interest to us they are either uniquely determined (as in the case of compositions), or provided by a universal property (as in the case of (co)limits). When we speak of limits and colimits, we really mean their homotopical analogues (any other notion would be meaningless in this context). Similarly, we say that an arrow is an
equivalence
(or that it is
invertible
) if it is weakly invertible, and the adjective
unique
means ‘up to a contractible space of choices’. For us a
space
is really an
\infty
-groupoid, and the
\infty
-category of these is denoted by
\mathcal{S}
. When necessary, we implicitly view 1-categories as
\infty
-categories through the nerve embedding.

If
\mathcal{C}
is an
\infty
-category, we write

for its

2.3.
Presentability and topoi

In this section we recast basic definitions and facts about presentable
\infty
-categories and
\infty
-toposes.

An
\infty
-category
\mathcal{E}
is

if it arises as an accessible localization of the
\infty
-category of presheaves on a small
\infty
-category. In other words, there exists a small
\infty
-category
\mathcal{C}
and an adjunction

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces L:\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{C})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{E}:i}

\rightarrow
\hookleftarrow
\rightarrow

where

is fully faithful and
L
is an accessible functor (ie. commutes with
\kappa

\kappa
). In this situation, we say that

is an accessible localization functor.

Definition 2.6
.

An
\infty
-category
\mathcal{E}
is an

\infty
-topos
if there exists a small
\infty
-category
\mathcal{C}
and an accessible left-exact localization functor
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{C})\mathrel{\mathchoice{{}\hbox{$\displaystyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\textstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle{\meno}$}}}\mathrel{\mkern-3.0mu}\rightarrow\mathcal{E}
.

An
\infty
-topos is in particular a presentable
\infty
-category and hence admits all small limits and colimits (
[
Lur09
, Proposition 5.5.2.4]
). Higher toposes are the
\infty
-categorical analogues of
Grothendieck toposes
. There is also a notion of

\infty
-topos
that is developped in
[
Ras18
]
. The essential difference is that, as in the 1-categorical case, an elementary
\infty
-topos need not be presentable. In general, an elementary
\infty
-topos only has finite limits and colimits. Since the presentability assumption will be crucial in this work, we will consider only
\infty
-toposes in the sense of Lurie.

.

Remark 2.8
.

Any
\infty

4.
Free Lie algebras and Hall words

In this section we introduce
Hall words
, and include a structure result which allows to compute them. These results are classical and we include them only for reference. What we call a set of Hall words on symbols
x_{1},\cdots,x_{n}
would traditionally be referred to as a
Hall basis
on these symbols. This is (one choice of) a basis for the
free Lie ring
(a Lie algebra over
\mathbb{Z}
) generated by the symbols
x_{i}
.

For the rest of this section, we fix a totally-ordered finite set of symbols
L=\{x_{1}<\cdots<x_{n}\}
and denote
\mathsf{Lie}(L)=\mathsf{Lie}(x_{1},\cdots,x_{n})
the free Lie ring generated by the
x_{i}
. As proved for example in
[
Nei10
, Proposition 8.5.1]
, the underlying abelian group of
\mathsf{Lie}(L)
is free. The purpose of a Hall basis is to provide an explicit basis for this free
\mathbb{Z}
-module.

The construction of a Hall basis is inductive. At each step
n
in the construction we build a countable list
L_{n}\subseteq\mathsf{Lie}(L)
with a total order on it. As usual we write
\mathsf{ad}(x)(y)=[x,y]
for the adjoint representation of a Lie algebra. Hence for
i\geq 0
,
\mathsf{ad}(x)^{i}(y)
is the iterated commutator
[x,[x,\cdots,[x,y]\cdots]]
where the symbol
x
appears
i
times. Given an iterated commutator
w
involving elements of
L
, its wordlength
\ell(w)
is the total number of elements of
L
appearing in the expression of
w
, counted with multiplicity. For instance,
\ell([x,[x,y]])=3
.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
L
be a finite totally-ordered set. Define inductively a sequence
L_{n}\subseteq\mathsf{Lie}(L)
of well-ordered sets as follows :

(1)

Set
L_{0}=L
with its given total order.

(2)

If
L_{r-1}
has been constructed and given a well order, define
x_{r}=\mathrm{min}\>L_{r-1}
and set

L_{r}=\{\mathsf{ad}(x_{r})^{i}(x)\>|\>i\geq 0,x\in L_{r-1},x\neq x_{r}\}.

Then choose
any
total order
<
on
L_{r}
that is compatible with word length. That is, we require that
x<y
whenever
\ell(x)<\ell(y)
for elements
x,y\in L_{r}
. This implies that
<
is a well order.

The resulting sequence
B=(x_{r})_{r\geq 1}
is called a
Hall basis
on the set
L
.

Remark 4.2
.

Note that at each step in the construction of a Hall basis on
L
, we are free to choose any total order compatible with word length. Different total orders will result in different Hall bases. Hence a Hall basis is not uniquely determined by
L
. Note that, taking
i=0
in the definition of
L_{r}
, we see that
L_{r-1}\setminus\{x_{r}\}\subseteq L_{r}
. A convenient convention is to take the order of
L_{r}
to extend the one already defined on
L_{r}
. With this convention, denoting
L=\{x_{1},\cdots,x_{n}\}
we see that the first
n
elements of a Hall basis are the generators
x_{1}<\cdots<x_{n}
.

We include the following two results for the interest of the reader. First, as advertised above a Hall basis on
L
is a
\mathbb{Z}
-basis for
\mathsf{Lie}(L)
. This is proved in
[
Hal50
]
, where elements of a Hall basis are called standard monomials.

Theorem 4.3
.

[
Hal50
, Theorem 3.1]

Any Hall basis on
L
provides a basis for the free abelian group underlying
\mathsf{Lie}(L)
. Denoting
B
such a Hall basis, there is an isomorphism of abelian groups

\mathsf{Lie}(L)\cong\mathbb{Z}[B].

The following gives an efficient algorithm for exhibiting words of length
\leq\ell
in a Hall basis.

Proposition 4.4
.

Define an increasing sequence of finite totally-ordered sets
(B_{\ell})_{\ell\geq 1}
of elements of
\mathsf{Lie}(L)=\mathsf{Lie}(x_{1},\cdots,x_{n})
, together with a rank function

(1)

Set

and

for

.

(2)

Suppose

has been constructed and given a total order together with a rank function

Define

B_{\ell}=B_{\ell-1}\cup\big\{\>[x_{i},x_{j}]\>|\>r(x_{j})\leq i<j\>,\>\ell(x_{i})+\ell(x_{j})=\ell\>\big\}

Then

The set

Since the smash product is commutative and associative (up to natural equivalence), the order in which we smash is of course irrelevant, but this notation helps keep track of the different factors that appear in Theorem
