Cooling and work extraction under memory-assisted Markovian thermal processes
Yuancheng Liu, Xueyuan Hu

Abstract
We investigate the limits on cooling and work extraction via Markovian thermal processes assisted by a finite-dimensional memory. Here the memory is a d d -dimensional quantum system with trivial Hamiltonian and initially in a maximally mixed state. For cooling a qubit system, we consider two paradigms, cooling under coherent control and cooling under incoherent control. For both paradigms, we derive the optimal ground-state populations under the set of general thermal processes (TP) and the set of Markovian thermal processes (MTP), and we further propose memory-assisted protocols, which bridge the gap between the performances of TP and MTP. For the task of work extraction, we prove that when the target system is a qubit in the excited state, the minimum extraction error achieved by TP can be approximated by Markovian thermal processes assisted by a large enough memory. Our results can bridge the performances of TP and MTP in thermodynamic tasks including cooling and work extraction.



I
Introduction

Laws of thermodynamics pose essential restrictions on processes such as cooling and work extraction.
Ever since Szilard’s discussion of Maxwell’s demon, where the amount of extracted work is related to the knowledge of the target system, the information-theoretic approach has been employed to investigate thermodynamic processes
Maruyama et al. 2009
. In particular, proposals for quantum engines have been proposed
Beyer et al. 2019
;
Park et al. 2013
;
Strasberg et al. 2017
;
Cavaliere et al. 2022
and experimentally demonstrated
Manzano et al. 2021
;
Naghiloo et al. 2018
;
Admon et al. 2018
;
Hu et al. 2020
. Further, quantum gates are employed to design schemes for cooling, where the entropy of the target system is transferred to an auxiliary system, which can then release the entropy to a heat bath. Such scheme of cooling is called heat bath algorithmic cooling
Boykin et al. 2002
;
Schulman et al. 2005
;
Raeisi and Mosca 2015
;
Clivaz et al. 2019
.

Recently, great progresses have been made in resource-theoretic approaches to quantum thermodynamics
Brandão et al. 2013
;
Lostaglio 2019
. In particular, conditions on state transformations under the set of thermal processes (TP) are established
Horodecki and Oppenheim 2013
. Based on these conditions, bounds on work extraction are derived
Horodecki and Oppenheim 2013
. Also, limitations on cooling in different regimes are derived
Francesco and Lorenza 2014
;
Allahverdyan et al. 2011
;
Wilming and Gallego 2017
;
Taranto et al. 2023
. Further, the heat-bath algorithmic cooling is extended to involve general thermal processes, such that the ground-state population of the target system goes to 1 exponentially fast in the number of rounds
Alhambra et al. 2019
.

When the set of thermal processes is restricted by the Markovian condition, which means that the evolution of states under such processes can be described by a master equation with the thermal state a fixed point, state transformations are restricted by stronger limitations. Such processes are defined as the set of Markovian thermal processes (MTP) in Ref.
Korzekwa and Lostaglio 2022
;
Lostaglio and Korzekwa 2022
, where it is proved that any operation in MTP can be realized by a sequence of elementary thermalizations. A direct consequence of this result is that under MTP and without any auxiliary system, a two-level system cannot be cooled to a temperature lower than that of the reservoir.

By noticing that memory effect of the environment sets the difference between Markovian and non-Markovian processes, we introduce a
d
-dimensional auxiliary system, with trivial Hamiltonian and initially in a maximally mixed state, to assist state transformations under MTP. Because this auxiliary system does not provide energy or non-equilibrium resources, it acts as a “memory” in state transformations.

In this paper, we study the limits on cooling and work extraction via memory-assisted Markovian thermal processes, and compare the performance with TP and MTP. For cooling a qubit system, we consider two paradigms, cooling under coherent control and cooling under incoherent control. For both paradigms, we derive the optimal ground-state populations under TP and MTP, and find that for the paradigm with coherence control, there is a gap between asymptotic ground-state populations (in the limit of infinite rounds) achieved by TP and MTP, while for the paradigm with incoherent control, both TP and MTP can reach the same asymptotic ground-state population but the convergence rate differs. We further propose memory-assisted protocols for cooling under coherent and incoherent control, which bridges the gap between the performances of TP and MTP. For the problem of extracting work from an out-of-equilibrium qubit state, we prove that if the qubit is initially in the excited state, the optimal strategy by TP can be approximated by MTP assisted by a large enough memory, indicating that memory-assisted MTP can outperform elementary thermal process when the dimension of the target is larger than 2. Our results may shed light on the study of memory effect of non-Markovian thermodynamic processes.

II
Prelimitaries

II.1
Markovian thermal processes and related concepts

Consider a quantum system
S
with Hamiltonian
H_{S}
and surrounded by a reservoir
R
at inverse temperature
\beta
. In equilibrium, the state of
S
reads
\tau\equiv e^{-\beta H_{S}}/\mathrm{Tr}(e^{-\beta H_{S}})
, which is also called a Gibbs state.

Here we briefly review three sets of completely positive and trace-preserving (CPTP) maps: the TP
Ćwikliński et al. 2015
;
Lostaglio et al. 2015
,the MTP
Korzekwa and Lostaglio 2022
;
Lostaglio and Korzekwa 2022
, and ETP
Lostaglio et al. 2018
.

A CPTP map
\Lambda
belongs to TP if and only if it satisfies the following two conditions
Ćwikliński et al. 2015
;
Lostaglio et al. 2015
.

(P1)
\Lambda
is time-translationally symmetric

\Lambda(e^{-iH_{S}t}\rho e^{iH_{S}t})=e^{-iH_{S}t}\Lambda(\rho)e^{iH_{S}t},\forall\rho,t.

(1)

(P2)
\Lambda
preserves the Gibbs state

\Lambda(\tau)=\tau.

(2)

Here and following, we focus on quantum states which are diagonal on the eigenbasis of Hamiltonian. Such states can be fully characterized as vectors
\bm{p}
of occupation probabilities
p_{k}=\langle k|\rho|k\rangle
, where
|k\rangle
are eigenstates of
H_{S}
corresponding to energy levels
E_{k}
. Then the action of a CPTP map on
\rho
can be described as a stochastic matrix acting on
\bm{p}

\rho^{\prime}=\Lambda(\rho)\Rightarrow\bm{p^{\prime}}=G\bm{p},

(3)

Here
G
is a matrix of transition probabilities
G_{k^{\prime}k}=p_{k^{\prime}|k}\equiv\langle k^{\prime}|\Lambda(|k\rangle\langle k|)|k^{\prime}\rangle
from state
|k\rangle
to state
|k^{\prime}\rangle
, and
\bm{p^{\prime}}
is a vector of occupation probabilities
p^{\prime}_{k}=\langle k|\rho^{\prime}|k\rangle
for the output state
\rho^{\prime}
.
From (P2), the population dynamics
G
induced by TP is a stochastic matrix which preserves the Gibbs distribution.
According to Ref.
Horodecki and Oppenheim 2013
;
Korzekwa 2016
,

\bm{p}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{TP}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\bm{p^{\prime}}\Leftrightarrow\bm{p}\succ_{{}_{T}}\bm{p^{\prime}},

(4)

where
\succ_{{}_{T}}
stands for thermo-majorization.

An elementary thermal process
Lostaglio et al. 2018
is realized as a sequence of thermal processes which involve only two energy levels at a time.
The set of ETP is equivalent to TP for the qubit case, while for high dimensional systems, ETP are strict subsets of TP
Lostaglio et al. 2018
.
When a thermal process involves only two energy levels
|i\rangle
and
|j\rangle
with energy gap
E_{ij}
, the reduced transition matrix in the subspace spanned by
\{|i\rangle,|j\rangle\}
can be written as

G^{ij}=(1-\lambda)\mathbb{1}^{ij}+\lambda\beta^{ij},

(5)

where
\lambda\in[0,1]
,
\mathbb{1}^{ij}
is the identity matrix, and
\beta^{ij}
is called
\beta
-swap between
i
and
j
, expressed as

\beta^{ij}=\begin{pmatrix}1-e^{-\beta E_{ij}}&1\\
e^{-\beta E_{ij}}&0\end{pmatrix}.

(6)

The following lemma follows directly from Eq. (
5
).

Lemma 1
.

Consider a qubit system with Hamiltonian
H_{S}=E|e\rangle\langle e|
, and two states which are incoherent on energy eigenbasis with occupation vectors
\bm{p}=[p,1-p]
and
\bm{p^{\prime}}=[p^{\prime},1-p^{\prime}]
. Then
\bm{p}
can be transformed to
\bm{p^{\prime}}
via TP, if and only if

\left\{\begin{matrix}p\leq p^{\prime}\leq p_{\beta},&p\leq\gamma,\\
p{{}_{\beta}}\leq p^{\prime}\leq p,&p\geq\gamma,\end{matrix}\right.

(7)

where
p_{\beta}=[\beta^{g,e}\bm{p}]_{g}=1-pe^{-\beta E}
is the ground-state population of the output state obtained from
\beta
-swap, and
\gamma\equiv 1/(1+e^{-\beta E})
is the ground-state population of the Gibbs state.

A Markovian thermal process is defined as a thermal process generated by a Markovian master equation. A Markovian process is memoryless, meaning that the evolution rate of a system at time
t
solely depends on its state at time
t
. As recently proved in Ref.
Korzekwa and Lostaglio 2022
, any state transformation induced by the set of MTP can be achieved by a sequence of elementary partial thermalizations, which involves two energy levels at a time. It follows that MTP are subsets of ETP. A partial thermalization between energy levels
E_{i}
and
E_{j}
(
E\equiv E_{j}-E_{i}>0
) is expressed as

T_{\lambda}^{ij}=\begin{pmatrix}1-\lambda(1-\gamma)&\lambda\gamma\\
\lambda(1-\gamma)&1-\lambda\gamma\end{pmatrix},

(8)

where
\lambda\in[0,1]
.
For
\lambda=1
,
T_{\lambda}^{ij}
transforms any state to a balanced distribution on
i
and
j
. It means that if
\bm{p^{\prime}}=T_{1}^{ij}\bm{p}
, then
p_{j}^{\prime}=p_{i}^{\prime}e^{-\beta E}
,
\forall\bm{p}
. This is called a full thermalization between
i
and
j
, which we will label
T^{ij}
in the following.

When a catalytic system is employed, the state transformations are enhanced
Brandão et al. 2015
;
Müller 2018
;
Lipka-Bartosik and Skrzypczyk 2021
;
Takagi and Shiraishi 2022
;
Henao and Uzdin 2021
;
Ding et al. 2021
;
Patryk et al. 2023
;
Son and Ng 2022
;
Son and Ng 2023
.
A catalyst is finite-dimensional ancilla, which interacts with the system via free operations and then returns to the exact original state.
When the set of free operations is TP, the initial state of a catalyst is non-equilibrium.
Interestingly, as recently discovered in Ref.
Son and Ng 2023
, with Gibbs state catalysts, elementary thermal processes can emulate any operation in TP.

II.2
Memory-assisted Markovian thermal process

Consider a qubit system with Hamiltonian
H_{S}=E|e\rangle\langle e|
and initially in state
\bm{p}=[p_{0},1-p_{0}]
.
According to the results in Refs.
Korzekwa and Lostaglio 2022
;
Lostaglio and Korzekwa 2022
,
\rho
can be transformed to state
\bm{p}^{\mathrm{MTP}}=[p^{\mathrm{MTP}},1-p^{\mathrm{MTP}}]
by MTP, if and only if,
\gamma\leq p^{\mathrm{MTP}}\leq p_{0}
for
p_{0}>\gamma
, and
p_{0}\leq p^{\mathrm{MTP}}\leq\gamma
for
p_{0}\leq\gamma
. In other words, any state which can be reached by MTP from a qubit state
\rho
, is a mixture of
\rho
and
\gamma
.

By employing a qubit ancillary with trivial Hamiltonian
H_{A}=0
and initially in a maximally mixed state
\tau^{(2)}_{A}=\frac{1}{2}(|1\rangle\langle 1|+|2\rangle\langle 2|)
, a global MTP can transform the state of
S
to
\rho^{(2)}=p^{(2)}|g\rangle\langle g|+(1-p^{(2)})|e\rangle\langle e|
with

p^{(2)}=\gamma+\gamma(1-\gamma)(\gamma-p_{0}).

(9)

See Appendix
B
for detailed calculations.
Because
p^{(2)}>\gamma
for
p_{0}<\gamma
and
p^{(2)}<\gamma
for
p_{0}>\gamma
,
\rho^{(2)}
cannot be reached from
\rho
via MTP.

It is worth mentioning that the auxiliary system considered here is similar to the catalyst system proposed in Ref.
Korzekwa and Lostaglio 2022
;
Lostaglio and Korzekwa 2022
, in the sense that the initial and final states of the auxiliary system are the same. Nevertheless, here we impose stronger requirements on the auxiliary system, i.e., trivial Hamiltonian and maximally mixed initial state. In other words, it does not provide energy or non-equilibrium resource for the state transformation of the target system. The reason why it can enable state transformations which cannot be realized by MTP is that, instead of fully thermalizing after each elementary thermalization, the assisted system memorizes its state as well as its correlation to the target system. Therefore, we will call state transformations which are assisted by such auxiliary systems the memory-assisted Markovian thermal processes.

Definition 1
.

(Memory-assisted
\mathrm{MTP}
,
\mathrm{MMTP}^{(d)}
) A quantum channel
\mathcal{E}
is a memory-assisted Markovian thermal process if there are an assisted system
A
with trivial Hamiltonian
H_{A}=0
and initially in a maximally mixed state
\tau_{A}=\mathbb{1}_{A}/d
and a joint Markovian thermal process
\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{MTP}}
such that

\mathcal{E}(\cdot)=\mathrm{Tr}_{A}[\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{MTP}}(\cdot\otimes\tau_{A})].

(10)

When the dimension of
A
is restricted to
d
, the set of memory-assisted Markovian thermal processes is labeled as
\mathrm{MMTP}^{(d)}
.

The above definition is similar to that proposed in the recent paper
Czartowski et al. 2023
, but we impose the trivial Hamiltonian condition to avoid energy exchange between the memory and the other systems. The following theorem is also proved in Ref.
Czartowski et al. 2023
. For consistency in this paper, we provide our proof in Appendix
B
.

Theorem 1
.

Let
\bm{p}
be an initial state of the target system, which undergoes
\Lambda^{ij}\in\mathrm{MMTP}^{(d)}
involving only two energy levels
E_{i}
and
E_{j}
(
E=E_{j}-E_{i}>0
). Then the state
\bm{p^{(d)}}
with
p_{i}^{(d)}
in the following form can be reached,

p_{i}^{(d)}=(1-e^{-\beta E})p_{i}+p_{j}+[(1-\gamma)p_{i}-\gamma p_{j}]\delta_{d}(\gamma),

(11)

where
\delta_{d}(\gamma)<o[(4\gamma(1-\gamma))^{d}d^{-3/2}]
.

Notice that the action of
\beta
-swap
\beta^{ij}
leads to
[\beta^{i,j}\bm{p}]_{i}=(1-e^{-\beta E})p_{i}+p_{j}
. Therefore,
\beta
-swap
can be simulated by
\mathrm{MMTP}^{(d)}
with error exponentially
decreasing in the dimension of the assisted memory.

Cooling

III.1
Settings and main results

Our target system is a qubit system with Hamiltonian
H_{S}=E|e\rangle\langle e|
and is surrounded by a reservoir
R
at inverse temperature
\beta
. Initially, the target system is thermalized to an equilibrium state
\tau=\gamma|g\rangle\langle g|+(1-\gamma)|e\rangle\langle e|

\gamma=1/(1+e^{-\beta E})
.

\Lambda^{\mathrm{F}}\in\mathrm{F}

Both paradigms consist of

\mathrm{MMTP}^{(d)}

In the paradigm of coherent control, the energy for cooling comes from a unitary operation, while the energy in incoherent control is provided by a hot bath

In the paradigm of coherent control, each round consists of two steps. In the first step, a unitary operation is applied to invert the populations of the target system, while the target system is decoupled from the heat bath. The second step is to maximize the ground-state population via TP, MTP or
\mathrm{MMTP}^{(d)}
. We denote the state of the target qubit after the
n

\rho_{n}^{\mathrm{F},coh}=p_{n}^{\mathrm{F},coh}|g\rangle\langle g|+(1-p_{n}^{\mathrm{F},coh})|e\rangle\langle e|
, where
\mathrm{F}=\mathrm{TP},\ \mathrm{MTP}
, or
\mathrm{MMTP}^{(d)}
denotes the allowed operations in the cooling process, and the superscript
coh
means that the energy comes from coherent control.
Our main result for coherent control is summarized in the following theorem.

Theorem 2
.

Under coherent control, the ground-state populations after the
n
th round, under cooling processes involving
\mathrm{TP}
and
\mathrm{MTP}
, are respectively upper bounded by

\displaystyle p_{n,*}^{\mathrm{TP},coh}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1-(1-\gamma)e^{-n\beta E},

(12)

\displaystyle p_{n,*}^{\mathrm{MTP},coh}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\gamma,

(13)

where the bounds are reachable.
Moreover, under
\mathrm{MMTP}^{(d)}
, the ground-state population can reach

p_{n,*}^{(d),coh}=p_{\max}^{(d),coh}-(e^{-\beta E}-\delta_{d}(\gamma))^{n}(p_{\max}^{(d),coh}-\gamma),

(14)

where

p_{\max}^{(d),coh}=1-\frac{\gamma}{1+(1-e^{-\beta E})/\delta_{d}(\gamma)}.

(15)

Detailed proof and discussion of the above theorem are in Sec.
III.2
.

In the paradigm of incoherent control, we employ an auxiliary qubit with Hamiltonian
H_{A}=(\mathcal{E}-E)|1_{A}\rangle\langle 1_{A}|
, where we set
\mathcal{E}>E
.
Initially, the target system
S
is thermalized in
R
while the auxiliary is thermalized in
H
. Each round of the protocol consists of two steps.
In the first step, the auxiliary is fully thermalized in
H
, absorbing the energy for cooling, while the state of target system is preserved.
In the second step, the target system and the auxiliary are brought in contact with
R
and undergo a thermal process.
After the
n
th round, the state of the target system becomes

\rho_{n}^{\mathrm{F},inc}=p_{n}^{\mathrm{F},inc}|g\rangle\langle g|+(1-p_{n}^{\mathrm{F},inc})|e\rangle\langle e|
, where the superscript

We prove the following.

Theorem 3
.

After the
n
th round, ground-state population of the target system under incoherent control can reach

p_{n}^{\mathrm{F},inc}=p_{*}^{inc}-v_{\mathrm{F}}^{n}(p_{*}^{inc}-\gamma),

(16)

\forall\mathrm{F}=\mathrm{TP},\mathrm{MTP},\mathrm{MMTP}^{(d)}
. the asymptotic ground-state population reads

p_{*}^{inc}=\frac{1}{1+e^{-\beta\mathcal{E}}e^{\beta_{H}(\mathcal{E}-E)}}.

(17)

The convergence rates for
\mathrm{F}=\mathrm{TP},\mathrm{MTP}
, and
\mathrm{MMTP}^{(d)}
are respectively

\displaystyle v_{\mathrm{TP}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\eta(1-e^{-\beta\mathcal{E}}),

(18)

\displaystyle v_{\mathrm{MTP}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle v_{\mathrm{TP}}+\frac{1-\eta+\eta e^{-\beta\mathcal{E}}}{1+e^{\beta\mathcal{E}}},

(19)

\displaystyle v_{\mathrm{MMTP}^{(d)}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle v_{\mathrm{TP}}+\frac{\eta-(2\eta-1)}{1+e^{-\beta\mathcal{E}}}\delta_{d}(\gamma_{\mathcal{E}}),

where

,

Further, for

It can be inferred from the theorem that, cooling processes under

H

reaches its maximum. Then we arrive at the following corollary.

Corollary 1
.

Under incoherent control, in the limit of infinity cycles, the ground-state population of a qubit target system is upper bounded by

(21)

where

stands for the largest energy gap of the composed system consisting of the target qubit

III.2
Cooling under coherence control

In the
n
th round, the state of the system first transforms under the unitary
\sigma_{x}:=|g\rangle\langle e|+|e\rangle\langle g|
, and then undergoes a process
\Lambda^{\mathrm{F}}\in\mathrm{F}

\displaystyle p_{n}^{\mathrm{F},coh}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle g|\Lambda^{\mathrm{F}}\left((1-p_{n-1}^{\mathrm{F},coh})|g\rangle\langle g|+p_{n-1}^{\mathrm{F},coh}|e\rangle\langle e|\right)|g\rangle,

(22)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle p^{\mathrm{F}}_{g|g}+(p^{\mathrm{F}}_{g|e}-p^{\mathrm{F}}_{g|g})p_{n-1}^{\mathrm{F},coh}.

Because
p_{0}^{\mathrm{F},coh}=\gamma
,
it follows that

p_{n}^{\mathrm{F},coh}=p_{*}^{\mathrm{F},coh}-v^{n}_{\mathrm{F},coh}(p_{*}^{\mathrm{F},coh}-\gamma),

where

Because

is not an identical operation, the ground-state population converges to

at a speed exponentially fast in the number of rounds.

For

.
Further, from Lemma

, as long as

In contrast, when

\displaystyle+1-e^{-\beta E}+(1-\gamma)\delta_{d}(\gamma)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle p_{\max}^{(d),coh}-[e^{-\beta E}-\delta_{d}(\gamma)](p_{\max}^{(d),coh}-p_{n-1}^{(d),coh})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle p_{\max}^{(d),coh}-[e^{-\beta E}-\delta_{d}(\gamma)]^{n}(p_{\max}^{(d),coh}-\gamma),

Cooling under incoherent control

R

\beta_{H}<\beta

\frac{p^{(0)}_{g1}}{\tau_{g1}}=\frac{p^{(0)}_{e1}}{\tau_{e1}}>\frac{p^{(0)}_{g0}}{\tau_{g0}}=\frac{p^{(0)}_{e0}}{\tau_{e0}}.

(27)

It means that one can neither increase the population on
|g1_{A}\rangle
, nor decrease the population on
|e0_{A}\rangle
via any operation in TP. The optimal strategy is then the
\beta
-swap between the
|g0_{A}\rangle
and
|e1_{A}\rangle
.
The ground-state population of
S
then becomes

p_{1}^{\mathrm{TP},inc}=1-\eta+\gamma\eta(1-e^{-\beta\mathcal{E}}).

(28)

Equivalently,

(p_{*}^{inc}-p_{1}^{\mathrm{TP},inc})=v_{\mathrm{TP}}(p_{*}^{inc}-\gamma),

(29)

where
p_{*}^{inc}
and

are defined in Eqs. (

) and (

p_{*}^{inc}>p_{1}^{\mathrm{TP},inc}
, because
p_{*}^{inc}>\gamma
and
v_{\mathrm{TP}}>0
.

In the second round, the auxiliary is first fully thermalized in
H
, resulting in state
[\eta,1-\eta]
. The joint state of
SA
becomes

\displaystyle\bm{p^{(1)}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle[p^{(1)}_{g0},p^{(1)}_{g1};p^{(1)}_{e0},p^{(1)}_{e1}]

(30)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle[p_{1}^{\mathrm{TP},inc}\eta,p_{1}^{\mathrm{TP},inc}(1-\eta);

\displaystyle(1-p_{1}^{\mathrm{TP},inc})\eta,(1-p_{1}^{\mathrm{TP},inc})(1-\eta)].

Then we check that

\frac{p^{(1)}_{g1}}{\tau_{g1}}>\frac{p^{(1)}_{e1}}{\tau_{e1}}>\frac{p^{(1)}_{g0}}{\tau_{g0}}>\frac{p^{(1)}_{e0}}{\tau_{e0}}.

(31)

Therefore, the best strategy is still the
\beta
-swap between the
|g0_{A}\rangle
and
|e1_{A}\rangle
. The resulted ground-state population
p_{2}^{\mathrm{TP},inc}
then satisfies

(p_{*}^{inc}-p_{2}^{\mathrm{TP},inc})=v_{\mathrm{TP}}(p_{*}^{inc}-p_{1}^{\mathrm{TP},inc}).

(32)

We apply the above discussion
n
times and obtain the recurrence relation

(p_{*}^{inc}-p_{n}^{\mathrm{TP},inc})=v_{\mathrm{TP}}(p_{*}^{inc}-p_{n-1}^{\mathrm{TP},inc}).

(33)

Importantly, this recurrence relation implies
p_{*}^{inc}>p_{n}^{\mathrm{TP},inc}
as long as
p_{*}^{inc}>p_{n-1}^{\mathrm{TP},inc}
, which in turn ensures that the ordering in Eq. (

) holds for
p^{(n)}
. This is the reason for employing the
\beta
-swap between
|g0_{A}\rangle
and
|e1_{A}\rangle
as the optimal cooling strategy in every round.
Combining Eqs. (

), we arrive at Eq. (
16
) in Theorem
3
for
\mathrm{F}=\mathrm{TP}
.

As for
\mathrm{F}=\mathrm{MTP}
, the discussions are similar. The only difference is that
\beta
-swap is not in
\mathrm{MTP}
. The optimal strategy in each cycle then becomes full thermalization between
|g0_{A}\rangle
and
|e1_{A}\rangle
. Therefore, we have the following recurrence relation

\displaystyle p_{n}^{\mathrm{MTP},inc}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\gamma[p_{n-1}^{\mathrm{MTP},inc}\eta+(1-p_{n-1}^{\mathrm{MTP},inc})(1-\eta)]

(34)

\displaystyle+(1-\eta)p_{n-1}^{\mathrm{MTP},inc}.

where
p_{0}^{\mathrm{MTP},inc}=\gamma
.
This is equivalent to

(p_{*}^{inc}-p_{n}^{\mathrm{MTP},inc})=v_{\mathrm{MTP}}(p_{*}^{inc}-p_{n-1}^{\mathrm{MTP},inc}).

(35)

Equation (
16
) then follows directly for
\mathrm{F}=\mathrm{MTP}
.
Interestingly, even though the allowed thermal process is restricted to be Markovian, one can still approach
p_{*}^{inc}
in the asymptotic limit. The difference between the performances of
\mathrm{TP}
and
\mathrm{MTP}
is the convergence rate. Because
v_{\mathrm{MTP}}>v_{\mathrm{TP}}
,
p_{n}^{\mathrm{TP},inc}
converges faster than
p_{n}^{\mathrm{MTP},inc}
.

For
\mathrm{F}=\mathrm{MMTP}^{(d)}

\beta
-swap between

in each cycle.
From Theorem

Still, the asymptotic limit of

IV
Work extraction

Consider a qubit
S
initially in a non-equilibrium state
[p_{0},1-p_{0}]
, and a work bit with Hamiltonian
H_{{}_{\tiny W}}={\tiny W}|1\rangle\langle 1|
initially in the ground state
[1,0]
.
The task of work extraction is to transform the state of the work bit to its exited state
|1\rangle
by joint thermal processes on
SW
.
Precisely, in a single-shot work extraction,

[p_{0},1-p_{0}]_{S}\otimes[1,0]_{{\tiny W}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{F}}}{{\longmapsto}}[\gamma,1-\gamma]_{S}\otimes[\epsilon,1-\epsilon]_{{\tiny W}},

(37)

where
\mathrm{F}=\mathrm{TP},\mathrm{MTP},\mathrm{ETP}
, or
\mathrm{MMTP}^{(d)}
, and
\epsilon
is called the error of work extraction. The task of work extraction is to minimize
\epsilon
over
\mathrm{F}
.

Here we analytically solve the above problem for the case where the target qubit is in the excited state, i.e.,
p_{0}=0
, and prove the following theorem.

Theorem 4
.

Consider a qubit
S
with Hamiltonian
H_{S}=E|e\rangle\langle e|
and initially in state
[0,1]_{S}
, and a work bit with Hamiltonian
H_{{}_{W}}=W|1\rangle\langle 1|
and initially in state
[1,0]_{W}
. By
\mathrm{MMTP}^{(d)}
, the error
\epsilon^{(d)}
of work extraction can reach

\epsilon^{(d)}(W)=I_{d}(\frac{1}{1+e^{-\beta(E-W)}},\frac{1}{1+e^{\beta W}}).

(38)

Moreover, for
d\rightarrow\infty
, the error can approach

\displaystyle\epsilon^{(\infty)}(W)=\epsilon^{\mathrm{TP}}(W)

(39)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{matrix}0,&{\tiny W}\leq W_{0},\\
1-e^{\beta(E-W)}-e^{-\beta{\tiny W}},&{\tiny W}>W_{0}.\end{matrix}\right.

where
Z\equiv 1+e^{-\beta E}
is the partition function of the target qubit,
W_{0}\equiv E+k_{B}T\ln Z
is the maximal work which can be extracted from
S
via
\mathrm{TP}
with vanishing error, and
\epsilon^{\mathrm{TP}}(W)
is the minimum error that can be reached via
\mathrm{TP}
for given
W
.

Here the function
I_{d}(x,y)
is defined in Eq. (
88
) in Appendix
A
.

This theorem provides an evidence in the regime of high-dimensional systems for the conjecture that
\mathrm{MMTP}^{(d)}
can approach TP for
d\rightarrow\infty
. This conjecture is not trivial, because if it is proved, then the thermal bath can be divided into two parts. The first one can exchange energy with the system but does not have memory effect, while the second one serves as a memory and does not exchange energy with the system.

IV.1
Error of work extraction via TP, MTP and ETP

In the following, we derive the minimum error of work extraction as a function of
W
, when the allowed operations are TP, MTP and ETP. In particular, for comparison with the performance of
\mathrm{MMTP}^{(d)}
, we focus on the case with
p_{0}=0
. On basis
\{|g0\rangle,|g1\rangle,|e0\rangle,|e1\rangle\}
, the initial state of
SW
then reads
\bm{p}_{0}=[0,0,1,0]
. The task of work extraction is then to minimize the sum of populations on
|g0\rangle
and
|e0\rangle
in the output by the allowed thermal processes.

First, we consider the case with
\mathrm{F}=\mathrm{TP}
. The optimal extraction error
\epsilon^{\mathrm{TP}}(W)
can be computed via the thermo-majorization condition

[0,1]_{S}\otimes[1,0]_{{\tiny W}}\succ_{{}_{T}}[\gamma,1-\gamma]_{S}\otimes[\epsilon,1-\epsilon]_{{\tiny W}}.

(40)

This condition leads to

\epsilon_{\mathrm{TP}}(W)=\left\{\begin{matrix}0,&{\tiny W}\leq W_{0},\\
1-e^{\beta(E-W)}-e^{-\beta{\tiny W}},&{\tiny W}>W_{0}.\end{matrix}\right.

(41)

where
W_{0}
is defined in theorem
4
. The optimal strategy is to implement a joint thermal operation described by the transition matrix
G^{\mathrm{TP}}
, followed by a local full thermalization on
S
. The form of
G^{\mathrm{TP}}
reads as follows.

For
W\leq E
,

G^{\mathrm{TP}}_{1}=\left(\begin{matrix}1&0&0&0\\
0&1-e^{-\beta(E-W)}&1&0\\
0&e^{-\beta(E-W)}&0&0\\
0&0&0&1\end{matrix}\right).

(42)

For
E<W\leq W_{0}
, which implies
e^{\beta E}<e^{\beta W}\leq 1+e^{\beta E}
,

G^{\mathrm{TP}}_{2}=\left(\begin{matrix}1&0&0&0\\
0&0&e^{-\beta(W-E)}&0\\
0&1&0&e^{\beta W}-e^{\beta E}\\
0&0&1-e^{-\beta(W-E)}&1-e^{\beta W}+e^{\beta E}\end{matrix}\right).

(43)

For
W>W_{0}
, which implies
e^{\beta W}>1+e^{\beta E}
,

G^{\mathrm{TP}}_{3}=\left(\begin{matrix}1&0&0&0\\
0&0&e^{-\beta(W-E)}&0\\
0&1&1-e^{-\beta W}(1+e^{\beta E})&1\\
0&0&e^{-\beta W}&0\end{matrix}\right).

(44)

The above form of
G^{\mathrm{TP}}
minimizes the sum of
G_{g0|e0}
and
G_{e0|e0}
, and thus minimizes
\epsilon
in the output.

Next we study the performance of MTP. The optimal strategy reads

G^{\mathrm{MTP}}=T_{S}T^{g1,e0}T^{g1,e1},

(45)

or

\tilde{G}^{\mathrm{MTP}}=T_{S}T^{g1,e1}T^{g1,e0},

(46)

where
T_{S}
denotes full thermalization of system
S
.
Both
G^{\mathrm{MTP}}
and
\tilde{G}^{\mathrm{MTP}}
give

\epsilon_{\mathrm{MTP}}(W)=\frac{1}{(1+e^{\beta(E-{\tiny W}})(1+e^{-\beta{\tiny W}})}.

(47)

Notice that, for
{\tiny W}\to 0
,
\epsilon_{\mathrm{MTP}}(W)\to\frac{1}{2}(1-\gamma)\neq 0
.

For
\mathrm{F}=\mathrm{ETP}
, we find that the optimal transition matrix is

G^{\mathrm{ETP}}=T_{S}\beta^{g1,e0}\beta^{g1,e1},

(48)

or

\tilde{G}^{\mathrm{ETP}}=T_{S}\beta^{g1,e1}\beta^{g1,e0}.

(49)

It then follows that

\epsilon_{\mathrm{ETP}}(W)=\left\{\begin{matrix}0,&{\tiny W}\leq E,\\
(1-e^{\beta\delta})(1-e^{-\beta{\tiny W}}),&{\tiny W}>E.\end{matrix}\right.

(50)

Figure 2:

The error
\bm{\epsilon}
of work extraction as a function of extracted work
\bm{W}
.
Work is extracted from a qubit
S
with energy gap
E
and initially in the excited state

.

Here We briefly mention that the Gibbs-preserving stochastic matrices
G_{2}^{\mathrm{TP}}
and
G_{3}^{\mathrm{TP}}
in Eqs. (
43
) and (
44
) satisfy the detailed balanced condition, i.e.,
G_{j|i}=e^{-\beta(E_{j}-E_{i})}G_{i|j}
. However, these transformations are impossible via ETP, because as shown in Fig.
2
, the error of work extraction achieved by these two stochastic matrices cannot be reached by ETP. This observation shows that detailed balance is a necessary but not sufficient condition for a thermal process to be realized as a sequence of thermal processes which involve only two energy levels at a time.

IV.2
Memory-assisted protocol for work extraction

Here we propose a protocol for work extraction from a qubit system in the excited state with the assistance of a
d
-dimensional memory.
The basis of the Hilbert space of the composed system
SWM
reads
\{|g\rangle,|e\rangle\}_{S}\otimes\{|0\rangle,|1\rangle\}_{W}\otimes\{|1\rangle,\dots,|d\rangle\}_{M}=\{|\xi\zeta k\rangle\}_{\xi=g,e;\zeta=0,1;k=1,\dots,d}
.
Initially, the system is in the excited state
|e\rangle
, the work bit is in the ground state
|0\rangle
, and the memory is in a maximally mixed state
\frac{\mathbb{1}}{d}
.
Our protocol is schematically depicted in Fig.
3
.

Scheme for work extraction under
\bm{\mathrm{MMTP}^{(d)}}
.

The color of each dot stands for the population distribution.
Initially, the qubit
S
is in the excited state and thus the composed state of
SW
reads
[0,0,1,0]_{SW}
, while the
d
-dimensional memory is in a maximally mixed state.
In step (I), MTP is applied to the subspace spanned by
\{|g11\rangle,\dots,|g1d\rangle;|e01\rangle,\dots,|e0d\rangle\}
.
In step (II), MTP is applied to the subspace spanned by
\{|e01\rangle,\dots,|e0d\rangle;|e11\rangle,\dots,|e1d\rangle\}
.
The error
\epsilon^{(d)}
of extraction equals to the sum of populations on
|e01\rangle,\dots,|e0d\rangle
.

Precisely, our protocol consist of two steps, which are summarized as

\displaystyle[0,0,1,0]\otimes[\overbrace{\frac{1}{d},...,\frac{1}{d}}^{d\ times}]

\displaystyle=[{0,...,0};0,...,0;\frac{1}{d},...,\frac{1}{d};0,...,0]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textbf{(I)}}}{{\longmapsto}}[0,...,0;a_{d}^{(1)},...,a_{d}^{(d)};b_{1}^{(d)},...,b_{d}^{(d)};0,...,0]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textbf{(II)}}}{{\longmapsto}}[0,...,0;a_{d}^{(1)},...,a_{d}^{(d)};\epsilon_{1},...,\epsilon_{d};c_{1}^{(d)},...,c_{d}^{(d)}].

In step (I), simulated
\beta
-swap is implemented between
|e\rangle_{S}\otimes|0\rangle_{W}
and
|g\rangle_{S}\otimes|1\rangle_{W}
. Notice that instead of fully thermalizing
M
after the above operation, we preserve the total state of
SWM
for the second step.
By combining Eqs. (
103
), (
105
) and (
107
) together in Appendix
B.2
, we obtain

\displaystyle b_{j}^{(d)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{(1-\gamma_{\delta})^{d}}{d}\sum^{j-1}_{j^{\prime}=0}f^{(j^{\prime})}_{d}\gamma_{\delta}^{j^{\prime}},

(51)

where
\delta=W-E
, and
\gamma_{\delta}=1/(1+e^{-\beta\delta})
. See Appendix
D
for detailed derivation. Notice that
b^{(d)}_{j}
is monotonically increasing with
j
.

Step (II) consists of
d
subroutines. In the
k
th subroutine, full elementary thermalizations are sequentially implemented between
|e0k\rangle
and
|e1j\rangle
with
j=1,\dots,d
. This results in

\epsilon_{k}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}b_{k+j-1}^{(d)}\gamma_{W}^{d}(1-\gamma_{W})^{j-1}f_{d}^{(j-1)}.

(52)

Consequently, we have

\displaystyle\epsilon^{(d)}=\sum^{d}_{k=1}\epsilon_{k}=I_{d}(\gamma_{\delta},1-\gamma_{W}).

(53)

The detailed derivation of the above formulas can be found in Appendix
D
. Further, in Appendix
A
, we prove that

\displaystyle\lim_{d\to\infty}I_{d}(x,y)

(54)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{matrix}1-\frac{x}{1-x}-\frac{y}{1-y},&\frac{x}{1-x}+\frac{y}{1-y}<1,\\
0,&\frac{x}{1-x}+\frac{y}{1-y}\geq 1.\end{matrix}\right.

This directly leads to Eq. (
39
) in Theorem
4
.
The comparison between the performances of TP, MTP, ETP and
\mathrm{MMTP}^{(d)}
with finite
d
is plotted in Fig.

. Interestingly, although
\epsilon^{(d)}(W)
with finite
d
is an analytic function, in the limit of
d\rightarrow\infty
, it approaches
\epsilon^{\mathrm{TP}}(W)
, whose first derivative on
W
is not continuous at
W=W_{0}
.

V
conclusion

We have introduced a finite-dimensional memory system with trivial Hamiltonian and initially in a maximally mixed state to bridge the gap between the performances of Markovian thermal processes and general thermal processes in thermodynamic tasks, including cooling and work extraction. We study two cooling paradigms, under coherent control and under incoherent control. In both paradigms, the ground-state population of the target qubit approaches a limit exponentially fast in the number of rounds, whether the set of allowed processes is TP, MTP or
\mathrm{MMTP}^{(d)}
. For the paradigm under coherence control, the limit of achievable ground-state population via
\mathrm{MMTP}^{(d)}
is monotonically increasing in
d
, which reduces to the limit for MTP when
d=1
, and approaches the limit for TP when
d\rightarrow\infty
. For the paradigm under incoherent control, we show that the limits of achievable ground-state population are the same for TP, MTP, and
\mathrm{MMTP}^{(d)}
, while the convergence rates are different. Still, the rate for
\mathrm{MMTP}^{(d)}
bridges the rates for TP and MTP.

In the problem of work extraction, the system which undergoes
\mathrm{MMTP}^{(d)}
consists of an out-of-equilibrium qubit
S
and a work qubit
W
. We derive the analytic expression for the error of work extraction achieved by
\mathrm{MMTP}^{(d)}
when
S
is initially in the excited state. Interestingly, the extraction error achieved by
\mathrm{MMTP}^{(d)}
, which is an analytic function of the energy gap
W
of the work bit, approaches in the limit
d\rightarrow\infty
to the extraction error achieved by TP, whose first-order derivation is not continuous.

Our results provide evidences for the conjecture that
\mathrm{MMTP}^{(d)}
can simulate transformations under TP with vanishing error, when the size
d
of the memory is large enough.

\displaystyle(1-x)^{n}[(1+x)^{n-1}+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=2}^{n-1}(n-j)C_{n+j-1}^{j}x^{j}-\sum_{j=2}^{n-1}C_{n-1}^{j}x^{j}],

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1-x)^{n}[(1+x)^{n-1}+\sum_{j=2}^{n-1}(\frac{n-j}{n}C_{n+j-1}^{j}-C_{n-1}^{j})x^{j}],

\displaystyle I^{(n)}_{n+1}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1-x)^{n}[(1-x)(1+x)(1+x)^{n-1}+(1-x)\sum_{j=2}^{n}(\frac{n+1-j}{n+1}C_{n+j}^{j}-C_{n}^{j})x^{j}].

(76)

It follows that

\displaystyle I^{(n)}_{n+1}(x)-I^{(n-1)}_{n}(x)

(77)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1-x)^{n}\{-x^{2}(1+x)^{n-1}+\sum_{j=2}^{n-1}[(1-x)(\frac{n+1-j}{n+1}C_{n+j}^{j}-C_{n}^{j})

\displaystyle-(\frac{n-j}{n}C_{n+j-1}^{j}-C_{n-1}^{j})]x^{j}+(1-x)x^{n}(\frac{C^{n}_{2n}}{n+1}-1)\},

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle x^{2}(1-x)^{n}\{-\sum^{n-1}_{j=0}C^{j}_{n-1}x^{j}+\sum_{j=0}^{n-3}[(1-x)(\frac{n-1-j}{n+1}C_{n+j+2}^{j+2}-C_{n}^{j+2})

\displaystyle-(\frac{n-2-j}{n}C_{n+j+1}^{j+2}-C_{n-1}^{j+2})]x^{j}+(1-x)x^{n-2}(\frac{C^{n}_{2n}}{n+1}-1)\},

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle x^{2}(1-x)^{n}\{\sum_{j=0}^{n-3}[-x(\frac{n-(j+1)}{n+1}C_{n+j+2}^{j+2}-C_{n}^{j+2})

\displaystyle-(-\frac{n-j}{n+1}C_{n+j+1}^{j+1}+C^{j+1}_{n})]x^{j}-(n-1)x^{n-2}-x^{n-1}+(1-x)x^{n-2}(\frac{C^{n}_{2n}}{n+1}-1)\},

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle x^{2}(1-x)^{n}\{\sum_{j=0}^{n-3}(C_{n}^{j+2}-\frac{n-(j+1)}{n+1}C_{n+j+2}^{j+2})x^{j+1}+\sum_{j=0}^{n-3}(\frac{n-j}{n+1}C_{n+j+1}^{j+1}-C^{j+1}_{n})x^{j}

\displaystyle-(n-1)x^{n-2}-x^{n-1}+(1-x)x^{n-2}(\frac{C^{n}_{2n}}{n+1}-1)\},

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle x^{2}(1-x)^{n}\{(n-\frac{2}{n+1}C^{n-1}_{2n-1})x^{n-2}-(n-1)x^{n-2}-x^{n-1}+(1-x)x^{n-2}(\frac{C^{n}_{2n}}{n+1}-1)\},

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle x^{2}(1-x)^{n}\{-x^{n-1}-x^{n-1}(\frac{C^{n}_{2n}}{n+1}-1)\},

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-x^{n+1}(1-x)^{n}\frac{C^{n}_{2n}}{n+1}.

(78)

In obtaining Eq.(
77
), we used the relations

\frac{n-2-j}{n}C_{n+j+1}^{j+2}=\frac{n-1-j}{n+1}C_{n+j+2}^{j+2}-\frac{n-j}{n+1}C_{n+j+1}^{j+1}
and

C_{n-1}^{j}-C_{n-1}^{j+2}=C_{n-1}^{j+1}+C_{n-1}^{j}-(C_{n-1}^{j+2}+C_{n-1}^{j+1})=C_{n}^{j+1}-C_{n}^{j+2}
.
Furthermore, for
x\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
, the following equation holds

\sum^{\infty}_{n=1}\frac{C^{n}_{2n}}{n+1}[x(1-x)]^{n}=\frac{x}{1-x}.

(79)

The proof is as follows. Let
x(1-x)=t
, and for
x\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
, we have
x=\frac{1-\sqrt{1-4t}}{2}
. Consequently, Eq. (
79
) becomes

\sum^{\infty}_{n=1}\frac{C^{n}_{2n}}{n+1}t^{n}=\frac{1-2t-\sqrt{1-4t}}{2t}.

(80)

Taylor expansion of
\sqrt{1-4t}
at
t=0
gives

\displaystyle\sqrt{1-4t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1-2t-2\sum^{\infty}_{n=2}\frac{(2n-2)!}{n!(n-1)!}t^{n},

(81)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1-2t-2\sum^{\infty}_{n=1}\frac{(2n)!}{n!(n+1)!}t^{n+1},

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1-2t-2\sum^{\infty}_{n=1}\frac{C^{n}_{2n}}{n+1}t^{n+1},

which is equivalent to Eq. (
80
), and thus we arrive at Eq. (
79
).

Therefore, for
x\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
,

\displaystyle I^{(n-1)}_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle I^{0}_{1}(x)+\sum^{n-1}_{n^{\prime}=1}I^{(n)}_{n+1}(x)-I^{(n-1)}_{n}(x),

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1-x-x\sum^{n-1}_{n^{\prime}=1}\frac{C^{n^{\prime}}_{2n^{\prime}}}{n^{\prime}+1}[x(1-x)]^{n^{\prime}},

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1-x-x(\frac{x}{1-x}-\sum^{\infty}_{n^{\prime}=n}\frac{C^{n^{\prime}}_{2n^{\prime}}}{n^{\prime}+1}[x(1-x)]^{n^{\prime}}),

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1-2x}{1-x}+x\delta_{n}(1-x).

For
n\gg 0
, by Stirling’s approximation
n!\approx\sqrt{2\pi n}(\frac{n}{e})^{n}
, we have

\displaystyle\delta_{n}(1-x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n^{\prime}=n}^{\infty}\frac{C_{2n^{\prime}}^{n^{\prime}}}{n^{\prime}+1}[x(1-x)]^{n^{\prime}},

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\sum_{n^{\prime}=n}^{\infty}\frac{\sqrt{2\pi(2n^{\prime})}}{2\pi n^{\prime}}\frac{(\frac{2n^{\prime}}{e})^{2n^{\prime}}}{(\frac{n^{\prime}}{e})^{2n^{\prime}}}\frac{1}{n^{\prime}+1}[x(1-x)]^{n^{\prime}},

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n^{\prime}=n}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi n^{\prime}}(n^{\prime}+1)}[4x(1-x)]^{n^{\prime}},

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle\sum_{n^{\prime}=n}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi n}(n+1)}[4x(1-x)]^{n^{\prime}}

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}n^{3/2}}\frac{[4x(1-x)]^{n}}{(2x-1)^{2}}=o[(4x(1-x))^{n}n^{-3/2}].

∎

Lemma 4
.

In the limit
n\to\infty
, the function
L_{n}^{(m)}(x)
approaches a discontinuous function as

\lim_{n\to\infty}L_{n}^{(m)}(x)=\left\{\begin{matrix}1,&x<\frac{m}{m+n-1},\\
0,&x>\frac{m}{m+n-1}.\end{matrix}\right.

(83)

Proof.

By definition, we have
L_{n}^{(m)}(0)=1
and
L_{n}^{(m)}(1)=0
,
\forall m,n
.

Now let
g^{(m)}_{n}(x):=-\frac{d}{dx}L_{n}^{(m)}(x)=nC_{n+m}^{m}x^{m}(1-x)^{n-1}
.

For
n\gg 0
and
m
being finite, or equivalently,
\frac{m}{m+n-1}\rightarrow 0_{+}
, we have

\displaystyle g^{(m)}_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{n}{m!}\frac{(n+m)!}{n!}x^{m}(1-x)^{n-1},

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\frac{n}{m!}\sqrt{\frac{n+m}{n}}\frac{(\frac{n+m}{e})^{m+n}}{(\frac{n}{e})^{n}}x^{m}(1-x)^{n-1},

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{n}{m!}\sqrt{\frac{n+m}{n}}(\frac{n+m}{e})^{m}(\frac{n+m}{n})^{n}x^{m}(1-x)^{n-1},

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\frac{n}{m!}\sqrt{\frac{n+m}{n}}(n+m)^{m}x^{m}(1-x)^{n-1}.

In the second line, we used Stirling’s formula, and in the fourth line, we used
(\frac{n+m}{n})^{n}\approx e^{m}
for
n\gg 0
. The above equation leads to
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}g^{(m)}_{n}(x)=0
,
\forall x\in(0,1]
. Therefore,

\lim_{n\to\infty}L_{n}^{(m)}(x)=\left\{\begin{matrix}1,&x=0,\\
0,&0<x\leq 1.\end{matrix}\right.

(84)

It means that Eq. (
83
) holds for
\frac{m}{m+n-1}\rightarrow 0_{+}
.

For
n\gg 0
and
\frac{m}{m+n-1}\neq 0
, which means that
m\gg 0
,

\displaystyle g^{(m)}_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(n+m)\frac{(n-1+m)!}{m!(n-1)!}x^{m}(1-x)^{n-1},

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\frac{n+m}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\sqrt{\frac{n-1+m}{m(n-1)}}\frac{(n-1+m)^{n-1+m}}{m^{m}(n-1)^{n-1}}x^{m}(1-x)^{n-1},

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{n+m}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\sqrt{\frac{n-1+m}{m(n-1)}}[\frac{n-1+m}{m}x]^{m}[\frac{n-1+m}{n-1}(1-x)]^{n-1},

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{n+m}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\sqrt{\frac{n-1+m}{m(n-1)}}[(1+\frac{1}{\mu})^{\mu}x^{\mu}(1+\mu)(1-x)]^{n-1}.

where
\mu=\frac{m}{n-1}\leq 1
.
Notice that
x^{\mu}(1-x)\leq[(1+\frac{1}{\mu})^{\mu}(1+\mu)]^{-1}
, and the equation holds if and only if
x=\frac{\mu}{1+\mu}=\frac{m}{m+n-1}
. Hence,

\lim_{n\to\infty}-\frac{d}{dx}L_{n}^{(m)}(x)=nC_{n+m}^{m}x^{m}(1-x)^{n-1}=\left\{\begin{matrix}\infty,&x=\frac{m}{m+n-1},\\
0,&otherwise.\end{matrix}\right.

(86)

Combining it with the fact that
L_{n}^{(m)}(0)=1
and
L_{n}^{(m)}(1)=0
, we arrive at Eq. (
83
).
∎

Definition 3
.

The function
I_{d}(x,y)
with
d
being a positive integer and
x,y\in[0,1]
is defined as

I_{d}(x,y):=\frac{(1-x)^{d}(1-y)^{d}}{d}\sum^{d-1}_{j=0}\sum^{d-j-1}_{k=0}(d-j-k)f^{(k)}_{d}x^{k}f^{(j)}_{d}y^{j}.

(87)

Lemma 5
.

In the limit
d\rightarrow\infty
, the function
I_{d}(x,y)
becomes

\lim_{d\to\infty}I_{d}(x,y)=\left\{\begin{matrix}1-\frac{x}{1-x}-\frac{y}{1-y},&\frac{y}{1-y}<1-\frac{x}{1-x},\\
0,&\frac{y}{1-y}\geq 1-\frac{x}{1-x}.\end{matrix}\right.

(88)

Proof.

Let
r_{d}^{(j)}(x):=\frac{(1-x)^{d}}{d}\sum^{d-j-1}_{k=0}(d-j-k)f^{(k)}_{d}x^{k}
, and then,

I_{d}(x,y)=(1-y)^{d}\sum^{d-1}_{j=0}r_{d}^{(j)}(x)f^{(j)}_{d}y^{j}.

(89)

By definition, the function
r_{d}^{(j)}(x)
is calculated as

\displaystyle r_{d}^{(j)}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{d-j}{d}L^{(d-j-1)}_{d}(x)-K^{(d-j-1)}_{d}(x),

(90)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\big(\frac{d-j}{d}-\frac{x}{1-x}\big)L^{(d-j-1)}_{d}(x).

In the limit of large
d
, by Lemma
4
, we have

\lim_{d\to\infty}r_{d}^{(j)}(x)=\left\{\begin{matrix}\frac{d-j}{d}-\frac{x}{1-x},&0\leq j<j_{x},\\
0,&j_{x}<j\leq d-1.\end{matrix}\right.

(91)

where
j_{x}=\frac{(d-1)(1-2x)}{1-x}
.

Submitting the above expression for
r_{d}^{(j)}(x)
into Eq. (
89
), we have for
d\rightarrow\infty
,

\displaystyle I_{d}(x,y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1-y)^{d}\sum^{d-1}_{j=0}\big(\frac{d-j}{d}-\frac{x}{1-x}\big)L^{(d-j-1)}_{d}(x)f^{(j)}_{d}y^{j},

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1-y)^{d}\sum^{\left\lfloor j_{x}\right\rfloor}_{j=0}\big(\frac{d-j}{d}-\frac{x}{1-x}\big)f^{(j)}_{d}y^{j},

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1-\frac{x}{1-x})L^{(\left\lfloor j_{x}\right\rfloor)}_{d}(y)-K^{(\left\lfloor j_{x}\right\rfloor)}_{d}(y),

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1-\frac{x}{1-x}-\frac{y}{1-y})L^{(\left\lfloor j_{x}\right\rfloor)}_{d}(y).

For

, or equivalently,
\frac{y}{1-y}<1-\frac{x}{1-x}
,
L^{(\left\lfloor j_{x}\right\rfloor)}_{d}(y)=1
, and otherwise,
L^{(\left\lfloor j_{x}\right\rfloor)}_{d}(y)=0
. Therefore, we arrive at Eq. (

Appendix B
QUBIT STATE TRANSFORMATIONS UNDER
\bm{\mathrm{MMTP}^{(d)}}

B.1
A motivating example

Here we consider a simplest example, where the target system
S
is a qubit with Hamiltonian
H_{S}=E|e\rangle\langle e|
and initially in state
[p_{0},1-p_{0}]
, and the memory is a qubit with Hamiltonian
H_{A}=0
and initially in state
[\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}]
.
The proposal for attaining the ground population
p^{(2)}
as in Eq. (
9
) goes as follows.
The initial state of
SA
can be expressed as a probability distribution on basis
\{|g0\rangle,|g1\rangle,|e0\rangle,|e1\rangle\}
, i.e.,

\bm{p}=\frac{1}{2}[p_{0},p_{0};1-p_{0},1-p_{0}].

(93)

The global MTP consist of two steps. In the first step, full elementary thermalizations are implemented subsequently between
|g0\rangle
and each of
|ej\rangle
(
j=1,2
), and the state becomes

\displaystyle\bm{p_{1}}=T^{g0,e1}T^{g0,e0}\bm{p}=\frac{1}{2}[\gamma(1-p_{0}+\gamma),p_{0};1-\gamma,(1-\gamma)(1-p_{0}+\gamma)].

(94)

Similarly, the second step consists of
T^{g1,e0}
and
T^{g1,e1}
in turn, and output state reads

\displaystyle\bm{p_{2}}=T^{g1,e1}T^{g1,e0}\bm{p_{1}}=[a_{2}^{(1)},a_{2}^{(2)};b_{1}^{(2)},b_{2}^{(2)}],

(95)

with

\displaystyle a_{2}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\gamma(1-p_{0}+\gamma),

\displaystyle a_{2}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\gamma[1+(2\gamma-1)(p_{0}-\gamma)],

\displaystyle b_{1}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(1-\gamma)[p_{0}+1-\gamma],

\displaystyle b_{2}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(1-\gamma)[1+(2\gamma-1)(p_{0}-\gamma)].

(96)

Then, full thermalization is applied to the auxiliary system
A
, leaving the target state in
\rho^{(2)}
with
p^{(2)}=a_{2}^{(1)}+a_{2}^{(2)}
, which equals to Eq. (
9
).

B.2
Simulation of
\beta
-swap by
\bm{\mathrm{MMTP}^{(d)}}

We propose a proposal for simulating
\beta
-swap via
\mathrm{MMTP}^{(d)}
. Our results show that, for
d
large enough, we can use
\mathrm{MMTP}^{(d)}
to approximate any qubit state transformation enabled by TP. Our proposal consists of
d^{2}
elementary full thermalizations and a full thermalization locally acting on
A
as a last step. Precisely, the protocol goes as follows.

Initially, the state of
SA
is prepared as

\rho=\big[p_{0}|g\rangle\langle g|+(1-p_{0})|e\rangle\langle e|\big]\otimes\frac{1}{d}\sum_{l=1}^{d}|l\rangle\langle l|.

(97)

On basis
\{|g1\rangle,\dots,|gd\rangle,|e1\rangle,\dots,|ed\rangle\}
, the probability distribution vector of the initial state is written as

\bm{p_{0d}}=[a_{0}^{(1)},\dots,a_{0}^{(d)};b_{1}^{(0)},\dots,b_{d}^{(0)}],

(98)

with

a_{0}^{(k)}=\frac{p_{0}}{d},\ b_{j}^{(0)}=\frac{1-p_{0}}{d}.

(99)

In the
k
th step (
k=1,\dots,d
), full elementary thermalizations are implemented subsequently between
|gk\rangle
and each of
|ej\rangle
(
j=1,\dots,d
). Then we have

\bm{p_{kj}}=\big\{\begin{array}[]{cc}T^{gk,e1}\bm{p_{k-1,d}},&j=1,\\
T^{gk,ej}\bm{p_{k,j-1}},&2\leq j\leq d,\end{array}

(100)

where

\displaystyle\bm{p_{kj}}=[a_{d}^{(1)},\dots,a_{d}^{(k-1)},a_{j}^{(k)},a_{0}^{(k+1)},\cdots,a_{0}^{(d)};b_{1}^{(k)},\dots,b_{j}^{(k)},b_{j+1}^{(k-1)},\dots,b_{d}^{(k-1)}].

(101)

Direct calculation leads to

\displaystyle a_{j}^{(k)}=\gamma(a_{j-1}^{(k)}+b_{j}^{(k-1)}),b_{j}^{(k)}=(1-\gamma)(a_{j-1}^{(k)}+b_{j}^{(k-1)}).

(102)

Therefore, we have

b_{j}^{(k)}=\frac{1-\gamma}{\gamma}a_{j}^{(k)},

(103)

and Eq. (
102
) becomes

\displaystyle a_{j}^{(k)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\gamma a_{j-1}^{(k)}+(1-\gamma)a_{j}^{(k-1)},

\displaystyle a_{0}^{(k)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{p_{0}}{d},\ a_{j}^{(0)}=\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}b_{j}^{(0)}=\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}\frac{1-p_{0}}{d}.

(104)

Now we make the substitution

a_{j}^{(k)}=\frac{1}{d}\gamma^{j}(1-\gamma)^{k-1}s_{j}^{(k)},

(105)

and the above problem is equivalent to

\displaystyle s_{j}^{(k)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle s_{j-1}^{(k)}+s_{j}^{(k-1)},

\displaystyle s_{0}^{(k)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle p_{0}(1-\gamma)^{-k+1},\ s_{j}^{(0)}=(1-p_{0})\gamma^{-j+1}.

(106)

This is in turn equivalent to

s_{j}^{(k)}=(1-\gamma)^{-k}[(1-p_{0})\gamma^{-j+1}-(\gamma-p_{0})\sum_{k^{\prime}=0}^{k-1}f_{j}^{(k^{\prime})}(1-\gamma)^{k^{\prime}}],

(107)

where
f_{j}^{(k)}:=\sum_{j^{\prime}=1}^{j}f_{j^{\prime}}^{(k-1)},\ f_{j}^{(0)}=1
, or equivalently,
f_{j}^{(k)}=C_{j+k-1}^{k}
.
Here
C_{n}^{m}:=\frac{n!}{m!(n-m)!}
.
The proof goes as follows.
For
k=0
, Eq. (
107
) reduces to
s_{j}^{(0)}=(1-p_{0})\gamma^{-j+1}
, which is equivalent to Eq. (
106
).
It means that Eq. (
107
) holds for the case with
k=0
and
j=1,\dots,d
.
Now assume it holds for the case with
k-1
and
j=1,\dots,d
, and we will prove it also holds for
k
.
From Eq. (
106
), we have
s_{j}^{(k)}-s_{j-1}^{(k)}=s_{j}^{(k-1)}
. Taking the summation over all
j
gives

\sum_{j^{\prime}=1}^{d}[s_{j}^{(k)}-s_{j-1}^{(k)}]=\sum_{j^{\prime}=1}^{d}s_{j}^{(k-1)}.

(108)

It follows that

\displaystyle s_{j}^{(k)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle s_{0}^{(k)}+(1-\gamma)^{1-k}\big[(1-p_{0})\sum_{j^{\prime}=1}^{j}\gamma^{1-j^{\prime}}-(\gamma-p_{0})\sum_{k^{\prime}=0}^{k-2}\sum_{j^{\prime}=1}^{j}f_{j^{\prime}}^{(k^{\prime})}(1-\gamma)^{k^{\prime}}\big]

(109)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle s_{0}^{(k)}+(1-\gamma)^{1-k}\big[(1-p_{0})\frac{1-\gamma^{-j}}{1-\gamma^{-1}}-(\gamma-p_{0})\sum_{k^{\prime}=1}^{k-1}f_{j^{\prime}}^{(k^{\prime})}(1-\gamma)^{k^{\prime}-1}\big]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1-\gamma)^{-k}[(1-p_{0})\gamma^{-j+1}-(\gamma-p_{0})\sum_{k^{\prime}=0}^{k-1}f_{j}^{(k^{\prime})}(1-\gamma)^{k^{\prime}}].

In the last line, we used Eq. (
106
).

After the
d^{2}
elementary full thermalizations, the state becomes

\displaystyle\bm{p_{dd}}=[a_{d}^{(1)},\dots,a_{d}^{(d)};b_{1}^{(d)},\dots,b_{d}^{(d)}].

(110)

It follows from Eq. (
107
) that

a_{d}^{(k)}=\frac{1}{d}\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}(1-p_{0})-\frac{\gamma-p_{0}}{d(1-\gamma)}\gamma^{d}\sum_{k^{\prime}=0}^{k-1}f_{d}^{(k^{\prime})}(1-\gamma)^{k^{\prime}}.

(111)

Then full thermalization is implemented locally on
A
, and the state of the qubit becomes
[p^{(d)},1-p^{(d)}]
with

p^{(d)}\equiv\sum_{k=1}^{d}a_{d}^{(k)}
. Direct calculations lead to

p^{(d)}=\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}(1-p_{0})-\frac{\gamma-p_{0}}{1-\gamma}I_{d}^{(d-1)}(1-\gamma),

(112)

where the function
I_{d}^{(d-1)}(x)
with
x\in(0,1)
is defined in Appendix
A
. It decreases with
d
and for finite
d
we have

I_{d}^{(d-1)}(1-\gamma)\in(\frac{2\gamma-1}{\gamma},\gamma]
. From Lemma
3
in Appendix
A
, we have

p^{(d)}=1-p_{0}e^{-\beta E}-(\gamma-p_{0})\delta_{d}(\gamma),

(113)

where
\delta_{d}(\gamma)<o[(4\gamma(1-\gamma))^{d}d^{-3/2}]
. Therefore,
\beta
-swap can be simulated by our protocol with error exponentially decreasing with the dimension of the assisted memory.

Appendix D
Details in the memory-assisted protocol for work extraction

The protocol is summarized in the following scheme

\displaystyle[0,0,1,0]\otimes[\overbrace{\frac{1}{d},...,\frac{1}{d}}^{d\ times}]

\displaystyle=[{0,...,0};0,...,0;\frac{1}{d},...,\frac{1}{d};0,...,0]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textbf{(I)}}}{{\longmapsto}}[0,...,0;a_{d}^{(1)},...,a_{d}^{(d)};b_{1}^{(d)},...,b_{d}^{(d)};0,...,0]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textbf{(II)}}}{{\longmapsto}}[0,...,0;a_{d}^{(1)},...,a_{d}^{(d)};\epsilon_{1},...,\epsilon_{d};c_{1}^{(d)},...,c_{d}^{(d)}].

Step I consists of
d
subroutines. In the
j
th subroutine, full elementary thermalizations are consequently implemented between
|e0j\rangle
and each of
|g1k\rangle
with
k=1,\dots,d
. This is equivalent to simulated
\beta
-swap between
|e0\rangle_{SW}
and
|g1\rangle_{SW}
. By putting Eqs. (
103
), (
105
) and (
107
) together, we obtain

b_{j}^{(d)}=\frac{1}{d}[1-\gamma_{\delta}^{j}\sum^{d-1}_{k=0}f^{(k)}_{j}(1-\gamma_{\delta})^{k}].

(114)

Further, because

\displaystyle\sum^{j-1}_{j^{\prime}=0}f^{(k)}_{j^{\prime}+1}\gamma_{\delta}^{j^{\prime}}-f^{(k+1)}_{j}\gamma_{\delta}^{j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f^{(k+1)}_{1}+\sum^{j-1}_{j^{\prime}=1}(f^{(k+1)}_{j^{\prime}+1}-f^{(k+1)}_{j^{\prime}})\gamma_{\delta}^{j^{\prime}}-f^{(k+1)}_{j}\gamma_{\delta}^{j}=\sum^{j-1}_{j^{\prime}=0}f^{(k+1)}_{j^{\prime}+1}\gamma_{\delta}^{j^{\prime}}-\sum^{j-1}_{j^{\prime}=0}f^{(k+1)}_{j^{\prime}+1}\gamma_{\delta}^{j^{\prime}+1}

(115)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1-\gamma_{\delta})\sum^{j-1}_{j^{\prime}=0}f^{(k+1)}_{j^{\prime}+1}\gamma_{\delta}^{j^{\prime}},

we arrive at

\displaystyle b^{(d)}_{j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{d}[(1-\gamma_{\delta}^{j})-\gamma_{\delta}^{j}\sum^{d-1}_{k=1}f^{(k)}_{j}(1-\gamma_{\delta})^{k}]=\frac{1-\gamma_{\delta}}{d}[\sum^{j-1}_{j^{\prime}=0}f^{(0)}_{j^{\prime}+1}\gamma_{\delta}^{j^{\prime}}-f^{(1)}_{j}\gamma_{\delta}^{j}-\gamma_{\delta}^{j}\sum^{d-1}_{k=2}f^{(k)}_{j}(1-\gamma_{\delta})^{k-1}]

(116)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{(1-\gamma_{\delta})^{2}}{d}[\sum^{j-1}_{j^{\prime}=0}f^{(1)}_{j^{\prime}+1}\gamma_{\delta}^{j^{\prime}}-f^{(2)}_{j}\gamma_{\delta}^{j}-\gamma_{\delta}^{j}\sum^{d-1}_{k=3}f^{(k)}_{j}(1-\gamma_{\delta})^{k-2}]=...=\frac{(1-\gamma_{\delta})^{d-1}}{d}[\sum^{j-1}_{j^{\prime}=0}f^{(d-2)}_{j^{\prime}+1}\gamma_{\delta}^{j^{\prime}}-f^{(d-1)}_{j}\gamma_{\delta}^{j}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{(1-\gamma_{\delta})^{d}}{d}\sum^{j-1}_{j^{\prime}=0}f^{(d-1)}_{j^{\prime}+1}\gamma_{\delta}^{j^{\prime}}.

Notice that
b^{(d)}_{j}
is increasing in
j
.

Step II also consists of
d
subroutines.
In the following, we will label the action of Step (II) as
\hat{\mathcal{T}}
, and thus,

[b_{1}^{(d)},...,b_{d}^{(d)};0,...,0]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\hat{\mathcal{T}}}}{{\longmapsto}}[\epsilon_{1},...,\epsilon_{d};c_{1}^{(d)},...,c_{d}^{(d)}].

(117)

Each subroutine of
\hat{\mathcal{T}}
is realized as
\hat{\mathcal{T}}^{(k)}\equiv T^{e0k,e1d}\dots T^{e0k,e11}
.
In order to decide the order of
\hat{\mathcal{T}}^{(k)}
in
\hat{\mathcal{T}}
, we first consider the following problem.

Let
H_{eff}=0|\alpha\rangle\langle\alpha|+W\sum_{j=1}^{d}|\beta j\rangle\langle\beta j|
be the effective Hamiltonian on a
(d+1)
-dimensional subspace spanned by
\{|\alpha\rangle;|\beta 1\rangle,\dots,|\beta d\rangle\}
, and
\mathcal{T}_{eff}=T^{\alpha,\beta d}\dots T^{\alpha,\beta 2}T^{\alpha,\beta 1}
be a Markovian thermal process acting on this subspace. We define the parameters
d_{k}
and
c_{j}^{(k)}
(
j,k=1,\dots,d
) as

\displaystyle[1;0,\dots,0]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{T}_{eff}}}{{\longmapsto}}[d_{1};c_{1}^{(1)},\dots,c_{d}^{(1)}],

(118)

\displaystyle[0;c_{1}^{(k-1)},\dots,c_{d}^{(k-1)}]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{T}_{eff}}}{{\longmapsto}}[d_{k};c_{1}^{(k)},\dots,c_{d}^{(k)}],\ k=2,\dots,d.

(119)

Then
d_{k}
is calculated as follows. Because

\displaystyle[1;\overbrace{0,...,0}^{d\ times}]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle T^{\alpha,\beta 1}}}{{\longmapsto}}[\gamma_{{}_{\tiny W}};1-\gamma_{{}_{\tiny W}},\overbrace{0,...,0}^{d-1\ times}]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle T^{\alpha,\beta 2}}}{{\longmapsto}}[\gamma_{{}_{\tiny W}}^{2};1-\gamma_{{}_{\tiny W}},\gamma_{{}_{\tiny W}}(1-\gamma_{{}_{\tiny W}}),\overbrace{0,...,0}^{d-2\ times}]\ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle}}{{\longmapsto}}\dots

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle T^{\alpha,\beta d}}}{{\longmapsto}}[\gamma_{{}_{\tiny W}}^{d};1-\gamma_{{}_{\tiny W}},\gamma_{{}_{\tiny W}}(1-\gamma_{{}_{\tiny W}}),...,(1-\gamma_{{}_{\tiny W}})\gamma_{{}_{\tiny W}}^{d-1}],

from Eq. (
118
), we have
d_{1}=\gamma_{{}_{\tiny W}}^{d}
,
c^{(1)}_{j}=(1-\gamma_{{}_{\tiny W}})\gamma_{{}_{\tiny W}}^{j-1}
.
Similarly, from Eq. (
119
), we have

\displaystyle d_{k+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\gamma_{{}_{\tiny W}}^{d}c_{1}^{(k)}+\gamma_{{}_{\tiny W}}^{d-1}c_{2}^{(k)}+...+\gamma_{{}_{\tiny W}}c_{d}^{(k)},

(120)

\displaystyle c^{(k+1)}_{j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1-\gamma_{{}_{\tiny W}})(\gamma^{j-1}_{{}_{\tiny W}}c^{(k)}_{1}+\gamma^{j-2}_{{}_{\tiny W}}c^{(k)}_{2}+...+c^{(k)}_{j}).

(121)

Now let
c^{(k)}_{j}=(1-\gamma_{{}_{\tiny W}})^{k}\gamma_{{}_{\tiny W}}^{j-1}\chi_{j}^{k}
, and we have
\chi_{j}^{k+1}=\sum^{j}_{j^{\prime}=1}\chi_{j^{\prime}}^{k}
, and

c^{(1)}_{j}=(1-\gamma_{{}_{\tiny W}})\gamma_{{}_{\tiny W}}^{j-1}=(1-\gamma_{{}_{\tiny W}})\gamma_{{}_{\tiny W}}^{j-1}\chi_{j}^{1}
.
Hence,
\chi_{j}^{k}=f_{j}^{(k-1)}
,
c^{(k)}_{j}=(1-\gamma_{{}_{\tiny W}})^{k}\gamma_{{}_{\tiny W}}^{j-1}f_{j}^{(k-1)}
. In turn, we get

d_{k+1}=\sum^{d}_{j=1}\gamma_{{}_{\tiny W}}^{d+1-j}c^{(k)}_{j}=\gamma_{{}_{\tiny W}}^{d}(1-\gamma_{{}_{\tiny W}})^{k}f^{k}_{d}
. Therefore, we arrive at

d_{k}=\gamma_{{}_{\tiny W}}^{d}(1-\gamma_{{}_{\tiny W}})^{k-1}f^{(k-1)}_{d}.

(122)

Now consider a
2d
-subspace spanned by
\{|\alpha 1\rangle,\dots,|\alpha d\rangle;|\beta 1\rangle,\dots,|\beta d\rangle\}
, and let
\vec{x}=[x_{1},x_{2},...,x_{d};0,...,0]
be a probability distribution in this subspace. (Notice that we do
not
require
\sum_{j=1}^{d}x_{j}=1
.) Here
|\alpha j\rangle
and
|\beta k\rangle
are energy eigenstates with energy
E_{\alpha}
and
E_{\beta}
, respectively, which satisfy
E_{\beta}-E_{\alpha}=W
.
We label
\mathcal{T}=\mathcal{T}^{(d)}\dots\mathcal{T}^{(1)}
, where
\mathcal{T}^{(k)}=T^{\alpha k,\beta d}\dots T^{\alpha k,\beta 1}
, and calculate the output probability distribution after the action of
\mathcal{T}
on
\vec{x}
.

The operator
\mathcal{T}^{(1)}
acts on the subspace spanned by
\{|\alpha 1\rangle;|\beta 1\rangle,\dots,|\beta d\rangle\}
. In this subspace, the action of
\mathcal{T}^{(1)}
is effectively the action of
\mathcal{T}_{eff}
, so the state transforms as

x_{1}[1;0,...,0]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{T}^{(1)}}}{{\longmapsto}}x_{1}[d_{1};c_{1}^{(1)},...,c_{d}^{(1)}].

(123)

Similarly,
\mathcal{T}^{(2)}
acts on the subspace spanned by
\{|\alpha 2\rangle;|\beta 1\rangle,\dots,|\beta d\rangle\}
, and also effectively equivalent to
\mathcal{T}_{eff}
. The input state of
\mathcal{T}^{(2)}
in this subspace reads

[x_{2};x_{1}c_{1}^{(1)},...,x_{1}c_{d}^{(1)}]=x_{2}[1;0,...,0]+x_{1}[0;c_{1}^{(1)},...,c_{d}^{(1)}].

(124)

It follows that

[x_{2};x_{1}c_{1}^{(1)},...,x_{1}c_{d}^{(1)}]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{T}^{(2)}}}{{\longmapsto}}x_{2}[d_{1};c_{1}^{(1)},...,c_{d}^{(1)}]+x_{1}[d_{2};c_{1}^{(2)},...,c_{d}^{(2)}].

(125)

Analogously,
\mathcal{T}^{(k)}
acts on the subspace spanned by
\{|\alpha k\rangle;|\beta 1\rangle,\dots,|\beta d\rangle\}
. The input state of
\mathcal{T}^{(k)}
in this subspace reads

x_{k}[1;0,...,0]+x_{k-1}[0;c_{1}^{(1)},...,c_{d}^{(1)}]+\dots+x_{1}[0;c_{1}^{(k-1)},...,c_{d}^{(k-1)}].

(126)

After the action of
\mathcal{T}^{(k)}
, the above state becomes

x_{k}[d_{1};c_{1}^{(1)},...,c_{d}^{(1)}]+x_{k-1}[d_{1};c_{1}^{(1)},...,c_{d}^{(1)}]+\dots+x_{1}[d_{k};c_{1}^{(k)},...,c_{d}^{(k)}].

(127)

Therefore, the population on
|\alpha k\rangle
after the action of
\mathcal{T}^{(k)}
reads

\epsilon_{k}=d_{1}x_{k}+d_{2}x_{k-1}+...+d_{k}x_{1},

(128)

which preserves during the actions of subsequent operations
\mathcal{T}^{(k^{\prime})}
with
k^{\prime}>k
. Finally, the total population on
|\alpha 1\rangle,\dots,|\alpha d\rangle
reads

\displaystyle\epsilon^{(d)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum^{d}_{k=1}\epsilon_{k}=d_{1}x_{d}+...+\sum_{k=1}^{d+1-i}d_{k}x_{i}+...+\sum_{k=1}^{d}d_{k}x_{1}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{d+1-i}d_{k}\right)x_{i}.

(129)

Because
d_{k}\geq 0
,
\epsilon^{(d)}
is minimized when
x_{i}
is increasing in
i
. Therefore, we choose
b_{j}^{(d)}=x_{j}
in the original problem as in Eq. (
117
), and the optimal order of
\hat{\mathcal{T}}^{(k)}
is
\hat{\mathcal{T}}=\hat{\mathcal{T}}^{(d)}\dots\hat{\mathcal{T}}^{(2)}\hat{\mathcal{T}}^{(1)}
.

Finally, the error of work extraction under our memory-assisted protocol is calculated as

\displaystyle\epsilon^{(d)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum^{d}_{j=1}\sum^{d-j+1}_{k=1}d_{k}b^{(d)}_{j}=\sum^{d}_{j=1}\sum^{d-j+1}_{k=1}\gamma_{{}_{\tiny W}}^{d}(1-\gamma_{{}_{\tiny W}})^{k-1}f^{(k-1)}_{d}\frac{(1-\gamma_{\delta})^{d}}{d}\sum^{j-1}_{j^{\prime}=0}f^{(d-1)}_{j^{\prime}+1}\gamma_{\delta}^{j^{\prime}}

(130)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{(\gamma_{{}_{W}}(1-\gamma_{\delta}))^{d}}{d}\sum^{d}_{j=1}\sum^{d-j}_{k=0}(1-\gamma_{{}_{\tiny W}})^{k}f^{(k)}_{d}\sum^{j-1}_{j^{\prime}=0}f^{(d-1)}_{j^{\prime}+1}\gamma_{\delta}^{j^{\prime}}.

where
f^{(d-1)}_{j^{\prime}+1}=C^{d-1}_{j^{\prime}+d-1}=C^{j^{\prime}}_{j^{\prime}+d-1}=f^{(j^{\prime})}_{d}
, and we have the following summation exchange

\displaystyle\sum^{d}_{j=1}\sum^{d-j}_{k=0}\sum^{j-1}_{j^{\prime}=0}\to\sum^{d-1}_{j^{\prime}=0}\sum^{d}_{j=j^{\prime}+1}\sum^{d-j}_{k=0}\to\sum^{d-1}_{j^{\prime}=0}\sum^{d-j^{\prime}-1}_{k=0}\sum^{d-k}_{j=j^{\prime}+1}

(131)

All the terms in the summation are independent of
j
, so the last summation
\sum^{d-k}_{j=j^{\prime}+1}
equivalent to multiplying each term by
(d-k-j^{\prime})
. Hence,

\displaystyle\epsilon^{(d)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{(1-\gamma_{\delta})^{d}}{d}\sum^{d-1}_{j^{\prime}=0}f^{(j^{\prime})}_{d}\gamma_{\delta}^{j^{\prime}}\gamma_{{}_{\tiny W}}^{d}\sum^{d-j^{\prime}-1}_{k=0}(d-k-j^{\prime})f^{(k)}_{d}(1-\gamma_{{}_{\tiny W}})^{k}

(132)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{(1-\gamma_{\delta})^{d}}{d}\sum^{d-1}_{j^{\prime}=0}(d-j^{\prime})f^{(j^{\prime})}_{d}(\gamma_{\delta}\gamma_{{}_{\tiny W}})^{j^{\prime}}\frac{\gamma^{n-j^{\prime}}_{{}_{\tiny W}}}{d-j^{\prime}}\sum^{d-j^{\prime}-1}_{k=0}(d-j^{\prime}-k)f^{(k)}_{d}(1-\gamma_{{}_{\tiny W}})^{k}=I_{d}(\gamma_{\delta},1-\gamma_{{}_{\tiny W}}).

Here
I_{d}(x,y)
is the function defined in Eq. (
88
). This completes the proof of Theorem
4
.
