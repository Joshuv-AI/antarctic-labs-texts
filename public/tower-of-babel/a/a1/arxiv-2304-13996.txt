A barrier for further approximating Sorting By Transpositions
Luiz Augusto G. da Silva, Luis Antonio B. Kowada, Maria Emília M. T. Walter

Abstract
The Transposition Distance Problem (TDP) is a classical problem in genome rearrangements which seeks to determine the minimum number of transpositions needed to transform a linear chromosome into another represented by the permutations π \pi and σ \sigma , respectively. This paper focuses on the equivalent problem of Sorting By Transpositions (SBT), where σ \sigma is the identity permutation ι \iota . Specifically, we investigate palisades, a family of permutations that are “hard” to sort, as they require numerous transpositions above the celebrated lower bound devised by Bafna and Pevzner. By determining the transposition distance of palisades, we were able to provide the exact transposition diameter for 3 3 -permutations (TD3), a special subset of the Symmetric Group S n S_{n} , essential for the study of approximate solutions for SBT using the simplification technique. The exact value for TD3 has remained unknown since Elias and Hartman showed an upper bound for it. Another consequence of determining the transposition distance of palisades is that, using as lower bound the one by Bafna and Pevzner, it is impossible to guarantee approximation ratios lower than 1.375 1.375 when approximating SBT. This finding has significant implications for the study of SBT, as this problem has been subject of intense research efforts for the past 25 years.

Introduction

Mutational events involving large portions of DNA in a chromosome are studied in the area of genome rearrangements. These are rare events and their study is crucial for understanding the evolutionary history of organisms
[
10
,
13
,
14
,
16
]
. One of these events is
transposition
, which informally corresponds to the exchange of positions of two adjacent blocks of genes in a linear chromosome. Transposition can also be seen as cutting out a gene block and then pasting it elsewhere (in the same chromosome). Then the question arises: what is the minimum number
t
of transpositions required to transform one linear chromosome, represented by a permutation
\pi
, into another one represented by
\sigma
, both consisting of the same set of genes? This classical problem is referred to as
Transposition Distance Problem
(TDP) and, if we consider the target chromosome
\sigma
as being the identity
\iota
, then TDP turns into the equivalent problem of
Sorting By Transpositions
(SBT). In this case, the number
t
is called the
transposition distance
of
\pi
and is denoted by
d_{t}(\pi)
.

SBT is a
\mathcal{NP}
-hard problem
[
3
]
and the first approximate algorithm for solving it was devised by Bafna and Pevzner
[
2
]
, with an approximation ratio of
1.5
. Later, Elias and Hartman
[
6
]
devised an
1.375
-approximation algorithm running in quadratic time, using a technique called
simplification
, which transforms an arbitrary permutation provided as input into a new one, seemingly easier to handle with. In 2022, Silva et al.
[
15
]
showed that a side effect of simplification could make the algorithm of Elias and Hartman
[
6
]
need one extra transposition above the approximation ratio of
1.375
and so they devised a new
1.375
-approximation algorithm, not using simplification, but with a high time complexity of
O(n^{6})
. Recently, Alexandrino et al.
[
1
]
published a result making it possible to approximate SBT with a
1.375
ratio, without extra transpositions, in
O(n^{5})
time.

In this work, we consider the palisades, a family of permutations which are “hard” to sort, in the sense that, to be sorted, numerous transpositions above the well-known lower bound established by Bafna and Pevzner
[
2
]
are required. By determining the transposition distance of palisades, we are able to determine the exact value for the transposition diameter for
3
-permutations (denoted by
TD3
), a special subset of the Symmetric Group
S_{n}
, essential for the study of approximate solutions for SBT using the simplification technique
[
9
,
8
]
. This problem has been open since Elias and Hartman
[
6
]
have shown an upper bound for it. Another surprising consequence of the result on the transposition distance of palisades, as we will show, is that, using as lower bound, the one by Bafna and Pevzner
[
2
]
, it is impossible to devise approximate solutions for SBT with approximation ratios lower than
1.375
.

We organise the text as follows. First, we briefly review the basic concepts of permutation groups needed to understand the algebraic formalism used in this work. Next, we will review SBT using the algebraic approach introduced by Silva et al.
[
15
]
. Subsequently, we will present our findings regarding palisades and, lastly, we include a brief discussion on the results and conclude. A very brief overview on the cycle graph

Permutation groups

A permutation group is a group of bijections from a set to itself (i.e., permutations), whose group operation is the usual function composition. The Symmetric Group
S_{n}
is the permutation group consisting of all permutations on a finite set
E
containing
n
symbols. Let
\alpha
and
\beta
be two permutations in
S_{n}
. The product
\alpha\beta
is obtained by composing the two permutation as functions. That is, the result of
\alpha\beta
is the permutation in which each element
x
of
E
is mapped to
\alpha(\beta(x))
.

A
cycle

\gamma
is a sequence
(c_{1}\;c_{2}\dots c_{\kappa})
of symbols of
E
such that
\alpha(c_{1})=c_{2}
,
\alpha(c_{2})=c_{3}
, …,
\alpha(c_{\kappa-1})=c_{\kappa}
,
\alpha(c_{\kappa})=c_{1}
. In other words, the symbols in
\gamma
are cyclically permuted by
\alpha
. Due to its cyclic nature,
\gamma
can be written starting by any of the symbols moved by it. If two cycles have no common symbols, then they commute, meaning that the order in which we compose them does not matter. In this case, these two cycles are said to be
disjoint
. A
disjoint cycle decomposition
of
\alpha
is a (unique) representation of
\alpha
as a product of disjoint cycles. Additionally, the number
\kappa
is the
length
of
\gamma
or, alternatively,
\gamma
is a
\kappa
-cycle. If
x\in E
is an element such that
\alpha(x)=x
, then
x
is said to be a
fixed element
. Fixed elements are sometimes omitted when representing a permutation as a product of cycles, but when necessary, a fixed element
x
is represented by the
1
-cycle
(x)
. The identity permutation
\iota
is the permutation which fixes all
E
elements.

Example 1

The permutation
(1\;2\;3)(5\;7)\in S_{7}
is the permutation which sends
1
to
2
,
2
to
3
,
3
to
1
,
5
to
7
and
7
to
5
, and leaves all the other symbols fixed.

It is well known that any permutation on
S_{n}
can be written as a (not unique) product of
2
-cycles. Furthermore, if a permutation can be written as a product of an even (odd) number of
2
-cycles, then it is impossible to write the same permutation using a product of an odd (even) number of
2
-cycles. Proofs for these results can be found in algebra textbooks
[
7
,
5
]
.

Example 2

The permutation
(1\;3\;2)(4\;5\;7)
can be written as a product of four
2
-cycle as
(1\;3)(3\;2)(4\;5)(5\;7)
. But this same permutation can be written as a product of
2
-cycles in infinitely many other ways.

Lastly, we highlight that many results in permutation groups consider the parity of the permutations, defined as follows. Suppose we can write
\alpha
as a product of
p

2
-cycles. In this case,
\alpha
is an
even (odd)
permutation if
p
is even (odd). An important result concerning the parity of permutations is that, assuming that
\alpha
and
\beta
have the same parity, then the product
\alpha\beta
is even
[
5
]
.

Preliminaries

The order in which a set of
n
genes appear along a linear chromosome, in the context of genome rearrangements, is usually represented by a permutation. For instance, given a set of
n
genes labelled
1
through
n
, a permutation of these genes can be represented as a sequence of integers
\pi=[\pi_{1}\;\pi_{2}\dots\pi_{n}]
, where each
\pi_{i}
,

, indicates the position of gene

in the chromosome. In our approach, the chromosome
\pi
is represented by the
(n+1)
-cycle
\bar{\pi}=(0\;\pi_{1}\;\pi_{2}\dots\pi_{n})
. In its turn, the identity permutation
\iota
is represented by
\bar{\iota}=(0\;1\;2\dots n)
. A transposition exchanging the positions of two adjacent blocks of genes is modelled by the multiplication of a
3
-cycle
\tau=(\pi_{i}\;\pi_{j}\;\pi_{k})
with
\bar{\pi}=(\pi_{0}\;\pi_{1}\dots\pi_{i-1}\;\pi_{i}\;\pi_{i+1}\dots\pi_{j-1}\;\pi_{j}\;\pi_{j+1}\dots\pi_{k-1}\;\pi_{k}\dots\pi_{n})
, resulting the
(n+1)
-cycle
(\pi_{0}\;\pi_{1}\dots\pi_{i-1}\;\pi_{j}\;\pi_{j+1}\dots\pi_{k-1}\;\pi_{i}\;\pi_{i+1}\dots\pi_{j-1}\;\pi_{k}\dots\pi_{n})
. When performing this multiplication, we can say that we are “applying”
\tau
on
\bar{\pi}
and, for this reason,
\tau
is
applicable
(to
\bar{\pi}
). However, we remark that there is a condition for a
3
-cycle
\tau=(\pi_{i}\;\pi_{j}\;\pi_{k})
to be applicable, which is that the symbols
\pi_{i}
,
\pi_{j}
, and
\pi_{k}
must be in the same cyclic order in both
\tau
and
\bar{\pi}
(as in the definition above). Otherwise, the product
\tau\bar{\pi}
will not be an
(n+1)
-cycle and consequently, will not model a chromosome.

Example 3

Let
\bar{\pi}=(0\;4\;5\;2\;3\;7\;6\;1\;8)
. Applying the
3
-cycle
\tau=(2\;6\;8)
to
\bar{\pi}
simulates a transposition as
\tau\bar{\pi}=(0\;4\;5\;6\;1\;2\;3\;7\;8)
. However, the
3
-cycle
\tau^{\prime}=(2\;4\;7)
cannot be applied to
\bar{\pi}
. This is because
\tau^{\prime}\bar{\pi}
does not correspond to a transposition in our approach, as
\tau^{\prime}\bar{\pi}=(0\;7\;8)(1\;4\;5\;6)(2\;3)
, which is not a
(n+1)
-cycle.

The problem of
Sorting By Transpositions
is therefore the problem of, given a
(n+1)
-cycle
\bar{\pi}
, finding the minimum
t
, denoted by
d_{t}(\bar{\pi})
, such that

\tau_{t}\dots\tau_{2}\tau_{1}\bar{\pi}=\bar{\iota},

(1)

where
\tau_{1}
is a
3
-cycle applicable to
\bar{\pi}
and each
\tau_{i}
,
i\in[2,t]
, is a
3
-cycle applicable to
\tau_{i-1}\dots\tau_{2}\tau_{1}\bar{\pi}
.

3.1
Lower bound for transposition distance

Mira and Meidanis
[
12
]
devised a measure for an even permutation
\alpha\in S_{n}
called
3
-norm, denoted as
\lvert\lvert\alpha\rvert\rvert 3
. The
3
-norm of
\alpha
is defined as the minimum
\ell
such that
\beta_{\ell}\dots\beta_{2}\beta_{1}=\alpha
, where each
\beta_{i}
,
i\in[1,\ell]
, is a
3
-cycle. Let
{c^{\circ}}_{odd}(\alpha)
denote the number of odd-length cycles (including
1
-cycles) in
\alpha
. Based on this, Mira and Meidanis
[
12
]
proved the following lemma:

Lemma 1
(Mira and Meidanis
[
12
]
)

\lvert\lvert\alpha\rvert\rvert_{3}=\frac{n-{c^{\circ}}_{odd}(\alpha)}{2}
.

Now let us exam Equation
1
. It can be rewritten as:

\tau_{t}\dots\tau_{2}\tau_{1}=\bar{\iota}\bar{\pi}^{-1}.

(2)

This means that the product of the
3
-cycles that transform
\bar{\pi}
into
\bar{\iota}
is equal to the product
\bar{\iota}\bar{\pi}^{-1}
. Furthermore, since
\bar{\pi}
and
\bar{\iota}
are both
(n+1)
-cycles, the product
\bar{\iota}{\bar{\pi}}^{-1}
is always even. As a result, we can state a lower bound for SBT as follows:

Lemma 2
(Mira and Meidanis
[
12
]
)

\displaystyle d_{t}(\bar{\pi})

\displaystyle\geq\lvert\lvert\bar{\iota}\bar{\pi}^{-1}\rvert\rvert 3

\displaystyle\geq\frac{n+1-{c^{\circ}}_{odd}(\bar{\iota}\bar{\pi}^{-1})}{2}.

Observe that the maximum number of cycles in
\bar{\iota}{\bar{\pi}}^{-1}
is achieved if and only if
\bar{\iota}{\bar{\pi}}^{-1}
is the identity permutation
\iota
. In this case,
\iota
has
n+1
cycles, all of which are odd-length (notably, they are all of length
1
).

Lastly, Silva et al.
[
15
]
have shown that there is a one-to-one mapping from
\bar{\iota}{\bar{\pi}}^{-1}
to the cycle graph
[
2
]
of
\pi
. Therefore, the lower bound stated above is equivalent to the well-known one devised by Bafna and Pevzner
[
2
]
, which is formalised in Appendix
0.A
(Theorem
0.A.1
).

Cycles of

While the concepts in this section apply to any

-cycle

, as previously defined by Silva et al.

4
Palisades and their transposition distance

This section introduces the concept of palisades, a family of permutations that are “hard” to sort, as they require a significant number of transpositions above the lower bound by Bafna and Pevzner (Theorem
0.A.1
) to be sorted. In this regard, this section aims to define their transposition distance.

A permutation
\bar{\pi}
is said to be a
palisade
if all components of
\bar{\iota}{\bar{\pi}}^{-1}
are unoriented interleaving pairs. If
\bar{\iota}{\bar{\pi}}^{-1}
consists of
\phi
unoriented interleaving pairs, then we refer to
\bar{\pi}
as a palisade of size
\phi
, or a
\phi
-palisade
.

Example 7

The permutations
(0\;5\;4\;3\;2\;1\;6\;11\;10\;9\;8\;7\;12\;17\;16\;15\;14\;13)
and
(0\;11\;16\;15\;14\;13\;12\;17\;10\;9\;8\;1\;6\;5\;4\;3\;2\;7)
are examples of palisades. Their cycle graphs
[
2
]
are depicted in Appendix
0.A
(figures
1
and
2
, respectively).

The next lemma can be demonstrated by observing that, for clearing two and three unoriented interleaving pairs, respectively, in the analysis carried out to prove the algorithms of Elias and Hartman
[
6
]
and of Silva et al.
[
15
]
, no
(4,3)
- and no
(8,6)
-sequence were found.

Lemma 3
(Elias and Hartman
[
6
]
, Silva et al.
[
15
]
)

There is no
(4,3)
- and
(8,6)
-sequence of transpositions able to clear two and three unoriented interleaving pairs on a palisade, respectively.

We can use the lower bound based on the counting of hurdles by Christie
[
4
]
to establish a tighter transposition distance lower bound for palisades, setting the initial conditions required for determining the transposition distance of palisades. A
hurdle
is a component of
\bar{\iota}{\bar{\pi}}^{-1}
consisting only of odd-length cycles that cannot be cleared using only
2
-moves. It is easy to see that the number of hurdles in a

\phi
. Let
\bar{\pi}
be a
\phi

Lemma 4
(Christie
[
4
]
)

d_{t}(\bar{\pi})\geq 2\phi+\lceil\frac{\phi}{2}\rceil
,

where
2\phi
is

-norm of
\bar{\iota}{\bar{\pi}}^{-1}
and
\lceil\frac{\phi}{2}\rceil
is the minimum number of

(i.e. one

Theorem 4.1

If
\bar{\pi}
is
\phi
-palisade, then
d_{t}(\bar{\pi})=\Bigl\lceil\frac{11\phi}{4}\Bigr\rceil=\Bigl\lceil\frac{11(n+1)}{24}\Bigr\rceil
.

Proof

Let us consider the lower bound established by Lemma
4
. To sort
\bar{\pi}
using as few as
2\phi+\lceil\frac{\phi}{2}\rceil
moves, it should be possible to clear two unoriented interleaving pairs using only one
0
-move (i.e., using a
(5,4)
-sequence, which includes a
(4,3)
-sequence). However, this is not possible due to Lemma
3
. Therefore, the lower bound can be raised to
2\phi+2\lfloor\frac{\phi}{3}\rfloor+\phi\bmod 3
, meaning that at least two
0
-moves are required to clear three unoriented interleaving pairs — the remaining ones require one
0
-move each. Nonetheless, this is not possible either, since Lemma
3
also states that there is no
(8,6)
-sequence to apply on
\bar{\pi}
clearing three unoriented interleaving pairs. As a result, the lower bound of
\phi
can be raised again, using similar reasoning, to
2\phi+3\lfloor\frac{\phi}{4}\rfloor+\phi\bmod 4
. Luckily, in this case,
(11,8)
-sequences
[
6
,
15
]
can be optimally applied clearing four pairs at once using three
0
-moves. Based on this, we can conclude that
d_{t}(\bar{\pi})=2\phi+3\lfloor\frac{\phi}{4}\rfloor+\phi\bmod 4
. Now, it remains to show the equality:

2\phi+3\Bigl\lfloor\frac{\phi}{4}\Bigr\rfloor+\phi\bmod 4=\Bigl\lceil\frac{11\phi}{4}\Bigr\rceil.

(3)

For this, we will examine the congruence of
\phi
modulo
4
, as follows.

1.

\phi\equiv 0\pmod{4}
. In this case,
\phi=4m
for some integer
m
. Thus,

\displaystyle 2\phi+3\Bigl\lfloor\frac{\phi}{4}\Bigr\rfloor+\phi\bmod 4

\displaystyle=2(4m)+3\Bigl\lfloor\frac{4m}{4}\Bigr\rfloor

\displaystyle=11m=\Bigl\lceil\frac{44m}{4}\Bigr\rceil=\Bigl\lceil\frac{11\phi}{4}\Bigr\rceil.

2.

\phi\equiv 1\pmod{4}
. In this case,
\phi=4m+1
for some integer
m
. Thus,

\displaystyle 2\phi+3\Bigl\lfloor\frac{\phi}{4}\Bigr\rfloor+\phi\bmod 4

\displaystyle=2(4m+1)+3\Bigl\lfloor\frac{4m+1}{4}\Bigr\rfloor+1

\displaystyle=11m+3

\displaystyle=\Bigl\lceil\frac{44m+11}{4}\Bigr\rceil

\displaystyle=\Bigl\lceil\frac{11(4m+1)}{4}\Bigr\rceil

\displaystyle=\Bigl\lceil\frac{11\phi}{4}\Bigr\rceil.

3.

\phi\equiv 2\pmod{4}
. In this case,
\phi=4m+2
for some integer
m
. Thus,

\displaystyle 2\phi+3\Bigl\lfloor\frac{\phi}{4}\Bigr\rfloor+\phi\bmod 4

\displaystyle=2(4m+2)+3\Bigl\lfloor\frac{4m+2}{4}\Bigr\rfloor+2

\displaystyle=11m+6

\displaystyle=\Bigl\lceil\frac{44m+22}{4}\Bigr\rceil

\displaystyle=\Bigl\lceil\frac{11(4m+2)}{4}\Bigr\rceil

\displaystyle=\Bigl\lceil\frac{11\phi}{4}\Bigr\rceil

4.

\phi\equiv 3\pmod{4}
. Finally, we have
\phi=4m+3
for some integer
m
. Thus,

\displaystyle 2\phi+3\Bigl\lfloor\frac{\phi}{4}\Bigr\rfloor+\phi\bmod 4

\displaystyle=2(4m+3)+3\Bigl\lfloor\frac{4m+3}{4}\Bigr\rfloor+3

\displaystyle=11m+9

\displaystyle=\Bigl\lceil\frac{44m+33}{4}\Bigr\rceil

\displaystyle=\Bigl\lceil\frac{11(4m+3)}{4}\Bigr\rceil

\displaystyle=\Bigl\lceil\frac{11\phi}{4}\Bigr\rceil.

Therefore, Equation
3
holds for all possible values of
\phi\bmod 4
, and hence for all
\phi
. To complete the proof, remark that a
\phi
-palisade is a permutation on
(n+1)\equiv 0\pmod{6}
symbols. Thus,
\lceil\frac{11\phi}{4}\rceil=\lceil\frac{11(n+1)}{24}\rceil
.

5
Transposition diameter for
3
-permutations

In this section, Theorem
4.1
is used to provide the exact value for
TD3(n)
.

Silva et al.
[
15
]
introduced a new upper bound for the transposition distance, valid for any
\bar{\pi}
, which can be stated as follows.

Theorem 5.1
(Silva et. al
[
15
]
)

d_{t}(\bar{\pi})\leq 11\Bigl\lfloor\frac{\lvert\lvert\bar{\iota}{\bar{\pi}}^{-1}\rvert\rvert_{3}}{8}\Bigr\rfloor+\Bigl\lfloor\frac{3(\lvert\lvert\bar{\iota}{\bar{\pi}}^{-1}\rvert\rvert_{3}\bmod 8)}{2}\Bigr\rfloor
.

Using this upper bound, we can devise a new upper bound for the transposition diameter for
3
-permutations (it should be noted that a
3
-permutation is a permutation on
(n+1)\equiv 0\pmod{3}
symbols).

Corollary 1

TD3(n)\leq 11\Bigl\lfloor\frac{n+1}{24}\Bigr\rfloor+\Bigl\lfloor\frac{(n+1)\bmod 24}{2}\Bigr\rfloor
.

Let
\bar{\pi}
be a
3
-permutation. We say
\bar{\pi}
is
diametral
if
d_{t}(\bar{\pi})
is equal to
TD3(n)
. One way to show that
\bar{\pi}
is diametral is by showing that
d_{t}(\bar{\pi})
is equal to the upper bound of the transposition diameter stated above. This observation is useful for proving the next theorem, which provides the exact value for
TD3(n)
.

Theorem 5.2

\displaystyle TD3(n)=

\displaystyle\Bigl\lceil\frac{11(n+1)}{24}\Bigr\rceil,(n+1)\equiv 0\pmod{6}

(4)

\displaystyle TD3(n)=

\displaystyle\Bigl\lceil\frac{11(n-2)}{24}\Bigr\rceil+1,(n+1)\equiv 3\pmod{6}

(5)

Proof

We divide the proof into two parts.

1.

If
(n+1)\equiv 0\pmod{6}
, then the palisades on
n+1
symbols are diametral. The proof is by showing that their transposition distances are equal to the upper bound established by Corollary
1
. That is, we have to show that

\Bigl\lceil\frac{11(n+1)}{24}\Bigr\rceil=11\Bigl\lfloor\frac{n+1}{24}\Bigr\rfloor+\Bigl\lfloor\frac{(n+1)\bmod 24}{2}\Bigr\rfloor.

(6)

We begin by noting that the fact
(n+1)\equiv 0\pmod{6}
implies
n+1=6m
, for some
m
integer. By replacing
n+1
with
6m
in the left-hand side of Equation
6
, we obtain:

\lceil\frac{11(n+1)}{24}\rceil=\lceil\frac{11m}{4}\rceil.

The same substitution made on the right-hand side yields:

\displaystyle 11\Bigl\lfloor\frac{n+1}{24}\Bigr\rfloor+\Bigl\lfloor\frac{(n+1)\bmod 24}{2}\Bigr\rfloor

\displaystyle=11\Bigl\lfloor\frac{6m}{24}\Bigr\rfloor+\Bigl\lfloor\frac{6m-24\Bigl\lfloor\frac{6m}{24}\Bigr\rfloor}{2}\Bigr\rfloor

\displaystyle=11\Bigl\lfloor\frac{m}{4}\Bigr\rfloor+\Bigl\lfloor 3m-12\Bigl\lfloor\frac{m}{4}\Bigr\rfloor\Bigr\rfloor

\displaystyle=11\Bigl\lfloor\frac{m}{4}\Bigr\rfloor+3m-12\Bigl\lfloor\frac{m}{4}\Bigr\rfloor

\displaystyle=3m-\Bigl\lfloor\frac{m}{4}\Bigr\rfloor.

Now it remains to show that the equality below holds:

3m-\Bigl\lfloor\frac{m}{4}\Bigr\rfloor=\Bigl\lceil\frac{11m}{4}\Bigr\rceil.

(7)

We will divide the analysis into two cases, next.

(a)

m\equiv 0\pmod{4}
. In this case,
m=4l
, for some integer
l
. Replacing
m
with
4l
in Equation
7
gives:

\displaystyle 12l-\Bigl\lfloor\frac{4l}{4}\Bigr\rfloor=\Bigl\lceil\frac{11(4l)}{4}\Bigr\rceil=11l.

(b)

m\not\equiv 0\pmod{4}
. In this case,
m=4l+r
for some integer
l
and
r\in[1,3]
. By rewriting the left-hand side of Equation
7
, we have:

3m-\Bigl\lfloor\frac{m}{4}\Bigr\rfloor=3(4l+r)-l=11l+3r.

On the right-hand side,

\Bigl\lceil\frac{11m}{4}\Bigr\rceil=\Bigl\lceil 11l+\frac{11r}{4}\Bigr\rceil=11l+\Bigl\lceil\frac{11r}{4}\Bigr\rceil.

Finally, note that
\lceil\frac{11r}{4}\rceil=3r
for all
r\in[1,3]
.

2.

Now we consider the case
(n+1)\equiv 3\pmod{6}
. In this scenario, we have identified another family of
3
-permutations that consists of
\frac{(n-2)}{6}
unoriented interleaving pairs and an oriented
3
-cycle not intersecting with any other cycle of
\bar{\iota}{\bar{\pi}}^{-1}
. The transposition distances of these permutations are
\lceil\frac{11(n-2)}{24}\rceil+1
, and we can prove that they are diametral (Figure
3
shows the cycle graph
[
2
]
of such a permutation). For this, we have to demonstrate the following equality:

\Bigl\lceil\frac{11(n-2)}{24}\Bigr\rceil+1=11\Bigl\lfloor\frac{n+1}{24}\Bigr\rfloor+\Bigl\lfloor\frac{(n+1)\bmod 24}{2}\Bigr\rfloor.

(8)

Note that if
(n+1)\equiv 3\pmod{6}
, then
n+1=6m+3
for some integer
m\geq 1
. By substituting this expression for
n+1
into the left-hand side of Equation
8
, we obtain:

\displaystyle\Bigl\lceil\frac{11(n-2)}{24}\Bigr\rceil+1

\displaystyle=\Bigl\lceil\frac{11m}{4}\Bigr\rceil+1.

The same substitution on right-hand side gives:

\displaystyle 11\Bigl\lfloor\frac{n+1}{24}\Bigr\rfloor+\Bigl\lfloor\frac{(n+1)\bmod 24}{2}\Bigr\rfloor

\displaystyle=11\Bigl\lfloor\frac{6m+3}{24}\Bigr\rfloor+\Bigl\lfloor\frac{6m+3-24\Bigl\lfloor\frac{6m+3}{24}\Bigr\rfloor}{2}\Bigr\rfloor

\displaystyle=11\Bigl\lfloor\frac{6m+3}{24}\Bigr\rfloor+\Bigl\lfloor\frac{6m+3}{2}\Bigr\rfloor-12\Bigl\lfloor\frac{6m+3}{24}\Bigr\rfloor

\displaystyle=\Bigl\lfloor\frac{6m+3}{2}\Bigr\rfloor-\Bigl\lfloor\frac{6m+3}{24}\Bigr\rfloor

\displaystyle=\Bigl\lfloor 3m+\frac{3}{2}\Bigr\rfloor-\Bigl\lfloor\frac{m}{4}+\frac{3}{24}\Bigr\rfloor

\displaystyle=3m-\Bigl\lfloor\frac{m}{4}+\frac{3}{24}\Bigr\rfloor+1

Our focus now is to show that:

3m-\Bigl\lfloor\frac{m}{4}+\frac{3}{24}\Bigr\rfloor+1=\Bigl\lceil\frac{11m}{4}\Bigr\rceil+1.

(9)

For this, let us analyse the congruence of
m
modulo
4
.

(a)

m\equiv 0\pmod{4}
. In this case,
m=4l
, for some integer
l
. By rewriting the terms, we have:

\displaystyle 3m-\Bigl\lfloor\frac{m}{4}+\frac{3}{24}\Bigr\rfloor+1

\displaystyle=12l+1-\Bigl\lfloor l+\frac{3}{24}\Bigr\rfloor

\displaystyle=11l+1

\displaystyle=\Bigl\lceil\frac{11m}{4}\Bigr\rceil+1.

Therefore, Equation
9
holds.

(b)

m\not\equiv 0\pmod{4}
. In this case,
m=4l+r
for some integer
l
and
r\in[1,3]
. Rewriting the left-hand side of Equation
9
gives:

\displaystyle\Bigl\lceil\frac{11m}{4}\Bigr\rceil+1

\displaystyle=\Bigl\lceil\frac{11(4l+r)}{4}\Bigr\rceil+1

\displaystyle=\Bigl\lceil 11l+\frac{11r}{4}\Bigr\rceil+1

\displaystyle=11l+\Bigl\lceil\frac{11r}{4}\Bigr\rceil+1.

On the right-hand side, we obtain:

\displaystyle 3m-\Bigl\lfloor\frac{m}{4}+\frac{3}{24}\Bigr\rfloor+1

\displaystyle=3(4l+r)-\Bigl\lfloor\frac{4l+r}{4}+\frac{3}{24}\Bigr\rfloor+1

\displaystyle=12l+3r-\Bigl\lfloor l+\frac{r}{4}+\frac{3}{24}\Bigr\rfloor+1

\displaystyle=12l+3r-(l+\Bigl\lfloor\frac{r}{4}+\frac{3}{24}\Bigr\rfloor)+1

\displaystyle=11l+3r+1.

Note that the term
\lfloor\frac{r}{4}+\frac{3}{24}\rfloor
in the expression above was cancelled since it always evaluates to
0
. The observation that
3r=\lceil\frac{11r}{4}\rceil
holds for all
r\in[1,3]
concludes the proof.

6
A barrier for approximating SBT

We observe that, when sorting a permutation
\bar{\pi}
by applying sequences of transpositions obtained by a polynomial-time routine, palisades of any sizes
\phi
,
0<2\phi\leq\lvert\lvert\bar{\iota}{\bar{\pi}}^{-1}\rvert\rvert_{3}
, may appear. The lowest achievable approximation ratio when sorting a palisade — using Bafna and Pevzner’s
[
2
]
as lower bound, is

\bigg(\Bigl\lceil\frac{11(n+1)}{24}\Bigr\rceil\mathbin{/}\frac{n+1}{3}\bigg)\geq\bigg(\frac{33}{24}=1.375\bigg),

where
\frac{n+1}{3}
is the
3
-norm of a palisade on
n+1
symbols. This remark has a striking implication that the palisades are an obstacle to approximating SBT with approximation ratios lower than
1.375
.

Corollary 2

It is impossible to guarantee an approximation ratio lower than
1.375
when sorting by transpositions using as lower bound, the one devised by Bafna and Pevzner
[
2
]
.

To illustrate Corollary
2
, we provide the following example: an arbitrary permutation in
S_{n}
on which we apply a sequence of
2
-moves resulting in a palisade. In this example, it is worth noting that, based only on the most apparent properties of the cycles of
\bar{\iota}{\bar{\pi}}^{-1}
(such as cycle parity), we cannot set a lower bound for
d_{t}(\bar{\pi})
that is tighter than the one proposed by Bafna and Pevzner
[
2
]
.

Example 8

Consider
\bar{\pi}=(0\;23\;22\;21\;1\;6\;5\;11\;20\;10\;9\;8\;13\;4\;3\;7\;12\;18\;2\;17\;16

\;15\;14\;19)
(cycle graph
[
2
]
depicted in Figure
4
). We can apply on
\bar{\pi}
a sequence of three
2
-moves, namely:
\tau_{1}=(1\;20\;10)
,
\tau_{2}=(2\;17\;7)
and
\tau_{3}=(4\;18\;11)
, giving
\tau_{3}\tau_{2}\tau_{1}\bar{\pi}=(0\;23\;22\;21\;20\;1\;6\;5\;4\;3\;2\;7\;12\;11\;10\;9\;8\;13\;18\;17\;16\;15\;14\;19)
, which is a
4
-palisade.

We highlight that attempting to determine in advance whether a sequence of transpositions will result in a palisade would be impractical. In this regard, we recall that, even for a
3
-permutation, deciding whether or not a single
0
-move is required for a sorting, is
\mathcal{NP}
-hard
[
3
]
.

7
Conclusions

This study focused on palisades, a family of permutations that are particularly “hard” to sort. We demonstrated that for
(n+1)\equiv 0\pmod{6}
, palisades qualify as diametral permutations for the
3
-permutations subset of
S_{n}
. Moreover, we have shown that there exists another family of diametral
3
-permutations for
(n+1)\equiv 3\pmod{6}
(a
3
-permutation is a permutation on
(n+1)\equiv 0\pmod{3}
). Elias and Hartman
[
6
]
had previously established an upper bound for the transposition diameter for
3
-permutations. In this work, we have provided the exact value.

Interestingly, our findings also indicate that palisades pose an obstacle to achieving approximation ratios lower than
1.375
for SBT. This partly answers a question raised by Bulteau, Fertin and Rusu
[
3
]
about the feasibility of devising a polynomial-time scheme for approximating SBT. Such a scheme is not possible using as lower bound, the well-known one by Bafna and Pevzner
[
2
]
. Therefore, for the study of approximate solutions for SBT to advance, it is crucial to find new lower bounds for the transposition distance.

Appendix 0.A
Cycle graph

The cycle graph is a commonly used graphical representation for permutations in the context of SBT
[
2
]
. To construct the cycle graph of
\pi=[\pi_{1}\;\pi_{2}\dots\pi_{n}]
, two extra symbols
\pi_{0}=0
and
\pi_{n+1}=n+1
are added to it. Thus,
cycle graph
of
\pi
, denoted by
G(\pi)
, is a directed graph consisting of the set of vertices
\{+0,\;-1,\;+1,\;-2,\;+2,\;\dots,

\;-n,\;+n,\;-(n+1)\}
and a set of coloured edges (which can be black or gray). The black edges connect
-\pi_{i}
to
+\pi_{i-1}
for all
i\in[1,n+1]
. The gray edges connect vertex
+i
to vertex
-(i+1)
for all
i\in[0,n]
. The black edges represent the current state of genes in the chromosome of
\pi
, while the gray edges indicate the desired order in the second permutation
\iota=[1\;2\dots n]
. Figures
1
,
2
and
3
provides examples of cycle graphs.

Each vertex in
G(\pi)
has in-degree and out-degree equal to
1
, with one black edge entering and one gray edge leaving. This induces a unique cycle decomposition in
G(\pi)
. A
\kappa
-cycle
is a cycle
C
in
G(\pi)
with
\kappa
black edges. If
\kappa
is
even (odd)
, we also say that
C
is an
even
(
odd
) cycle.

A transposition
\tau(i,j,k)
, with
1\leq i<j<k\leq n+1
, exchanges the block of symbols corresponding to interval
[\pi_{i},\pi_{j-1}]
with the block of symbols corresponding to interval
[\pi_{j},\pi_{k-1}]
. Thus, applying
\tau(i,j,k)
on
\pi
, denoted by
\tau(i,j,k)\cdot\pi
or just
\tau\cdot\pi
, yields the new permutation
[\pi_{1}\dots\pi_{i-1}\;\pi_{j}\dots\pi_{k-1}

\;\pi_{i}\dots\pi_{j-1}\;\pi_{k}\dots\pi_{n}]
. Let
c_{odd}(\pi)
be the number of odd cycles in
G(\pi)
and let
\Delta c_{odd}(\pi,\tau)=c_{odd}(\tau\cdot\pi)-c_{odd}(\pi)
denote the change in the number of odd cycles in
G(\pi)
after the application of a transposition
\tau
. The following lower bound, formulated by Bafna and Pevzner
[
2
]
, is the foundation of the most prevalent approximation algorithms for SBT.

Theorem 0.A.1
(Bafna and Pevzner
[
2
]
)

d(\pi)\geq\frac{n+1-c_{odd}(\pi)}{2}
.
