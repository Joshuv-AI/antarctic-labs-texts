Initial-boundary value problems for Poiseuille flow of nematic liquid crystal via full Ericksen-Leslie model
Geng Chen (∗) (*) Department of Mathematics, University of Kansas, Lawrence, KS, U.S.A. e-mail: gengchen@ku.edu, Yanbo Hu (∗∗) (**) Department of Mathematics, Hangzhou Normal University, Hangzhou, China e-mail: yanbo.hu@hotmail.com, Qingtian Zhang (∗∗∗) (***) Department of Mathematics, West Virginia University, Morgantown, WV 26501, U.S.A. e-mail: qingtian.zhang@mail.wvu.edu

Abstract
. In this paper, we study the initial-boundary value problem for the Poiseuille flow of hyperbolic-parabolic Ericksen-Leslie model of nematic liquid crystals in one space dimension. Due to the quasilinearity, the solution of this model in general forms cusp singularity. We prove the global existence of Hölder continuous solution, which may include cusp singularity, for initial-boundary value problems with different types of boundary conditions. KEYWORDS. Liquid crystal; Ericksen-Leslie; Poiseuille flow; global existence; Initial-boundary value problem. AMS subject classifications. 35M33; 35L53; 76D03.



1.
Introduction

The hydrodynamic theory of incompressible liquid crystals was established by Ericksen
[
14
,
15
,
16
]
and Leslie
[
27
]
.
The Ericksen-Leslie’s system is written as

(1.1)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\rho\dot{\mathbf{u}}+\nabla P=\nabla\cdot\sigma-\nabla(\frac{\partial W}{\partial\nabla\mathbf{n}}\otimes\nabla\mathbf{n}),\\
\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}=0,\\
\nu\ddot{\mathbf{n}}=\lambda\mathbf{n}-\frac{\partial W}{\partial\mathbf{n}}-\mathbf{g}+\nabla\cdot(\frac{\partial W}{\partial\nabla\mathbf{n}}),\\
|\mathbf{n}|=1,\end{array}\right.

where
\mathbf{u}
is the velocity,
\mathbf{n}
is the director field of the liquid crystal molecules,
\rho
is the constant density,
P
is the pressure,
\nu
is the inertia coefficient of the director
\mathbf{n}
.
\mathbf{g}
and
\sigma
are the kinematic transport and the viscous stress tensor, respectively, which satisfy

\displaystyle\mathbf{g}=\gamma_{1}N+\gamma_{2}D\mathbf{n},\quad N=\dot{\mathbf{n}}-\omega\mathbf{n},

\displaystyle D=\frac{1}{2}(\nabla\mathbf{u}+\nabla^{t}\mathbf{u}),\quad\omega=\frac{1}{2}(\nabla\mathbf{u}-\nabla^{t}\mathbf{u}),

\displaystyle\sigma=\alpha_{1}(\mathbf{n}^{t}D\mathbf{n})\mathbf{n}\otimes\mathbf{n}+\alpha_{2}N\otimes\mathbf{n}+\alpha_{3}\mathbf{n}\otimes N+\alpha_{4}D+\alpha_{5}(D\mathbf{n})\otimes\mathbf{n}+\alpha_{6}\mathbf{n}\otimes(D\mathbf{n}),

where
\alpha_{1},\cdots,\alpha_{6},\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}
are physical coefficients satisfying (see
[
27
,
8
]
)

\gamma_{1}=\alpha_{3}-\alpha_{2},\gamma_{2}=\alpha_{6}-\alpha_{5},\alpha_{2}+\alpha_{3}=\alpha_{6}-\alpha_{5},

\alpha_{4}>0,2\alpha_{1}+3\alpha_{4}+2\alpha_{5}+2\alpha_{6}>0,\gamma_{1}>0,

2\alpha_{4}+\alpha_{5}+\alpha_{6}>0,4\gamma_{1}(2\alpha_{4}+\alpha_{5}+\alpha_{6})>(\alpha_{2}+\alpha_{3}+\gamma_{2})^{2}.

If the orientation order parameters of nematic materials are treated as a unit vector
{\mathbf{n}}\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
, the director, then the Oseen-Frank energy density determines the macrostructure of the crystal structure (
[
27
]
)

\displaystyle\begin{split}2W(\mathbf{n},\nabla\mathbf{n})=&K_{1}(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{n})^{2}+K_{2}(\mathbf{n}\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf{n}))^{2}+K_{3}|\mathbf{n}\times(\nabla\times\mathbf{n})|^{2}\\
&+(K_{2}+K_{4})[\hbox{tr}(\nabla\mathbf{n})^{2}-(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{n})^{2}],\end{split}

where
K_{j}
,
j=1,2,3
, are the positive constants representing splay, twist, and bend effects respectively, with

K_{2}\geq|K_{4}|
,
2K_{1}\geq K_{2}+K_{4}
.

Successful theories with a wide range of interesting properties have been established for the equilibrium theory (the elliptic case on
\mathbf{n}
) and the evolutionary theory (the parabolic case on
\mathbf{n}
) when the inertial effect in (
1.1
) is neglected, i.e. when
\nu=0
. See a partial list of references in
[

However, there are very few studies on the full Ericksen-Leslie system, including the inertial effect, i.e. when
\nu=0
. Many fundamental problems including global wellposedness are still wide open. When one considers a special Oseen-Frank potential
W=|\nabla\mathbf{n}|^{2}
, which makes the wave equations on

essentially semilinear, for small data problem, Jiang and his collaborators obtained a series of existence results on regular solutions

In general, the solution of (

\mathbf{n}
. See
[

]
for the formation of finite time cusp singularity for the Poiseuille flow, and
[

for the formation of cusp singularity in multiple space dimension. For solutions in multiple space dimension, Chen-Huang-Xu in
[

found another type of singularity for (

In
[

[

for the 1-d Poiseuille flow, where the solution may include cusp singularity.

In this paper, we establish some global existence results for the initial-boundary value problem of the Poiseuille flow for nematic liquid crystals via the full Ericksen-Leslie model (
1.1
). More precisely, for the 1-d Poiseuille flow with
\mathbf{u}=(0,0,u)^{t},\mathbf{n}=(\sin\theta,0,\cos\theta)^{t}
, the system of
u
and
\theta
becomes

(1.2)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\rho u_{t}=(g(\theta)u_{x}+h(\theta)\theta_{t})_{x},\\
\nu\theta_{tt}+\gamma_{1}\theta_{t}=c(\theta)(c(\theta)\theta_{x})_{x}-h(\theta)u_{x},\end{array}\right.

where

\displaystyle g(\theta)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\alpha_{1}\sin^{2}\theta\cos^{2}\theta+\frac{\alpha_{5}-\alpha_{2}}{2}\sin^{2}\theta+\frac{\alpha_{3}+\alpha_{6}}{2}\cos^{2}\theta+\frac{\alpha_{4}}{2},

\displaystyle c(\theta)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sqrt{K_{1}\cos^{2}\theta+K_{3}\sin^{2}\theta},

\displaystyle h(\theta)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\alpha_{3}\cos^{2}\theta-\alpha_{2}\sin^{2}\theta=\frac{\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}\cos(2\theta)}{2}.

See the derivation of this model in
[
8
]
.
Notice that
g(\theta),c(\theta),h(\theta)
are smooth with respect to
\theta
, and
g(\theta),c(\theta),h(\theta),g^{\prime}(\theta),c^{\prime}(\theta),c^{\prime\prime}(\theta),h^{\prime}(\theta)
are uniformly bounded.

There are several physically interesting boundary conditions. For the velocity
u
, we have the following possible choices. We denote an important quantity

J=g(\theta)u_{x}+h(\theta)\theta_{t}.

•

Nonslip boundary condition. If the boundary is a solid wall, we can propose the nonslip boundary condition
u=0
on the boundary. If the boundary is moving, we can also propose
u=f(t)
on the boundary with
f(t)
is a given function with
t
. This corresponds to the Dirichlet boundary condition on
u
.

•

Stress-free boundary condition. On the boundary the shear stress is zero.
(1,0,0)D=(0,0,J)^{t}={\mathbf{0}}
, where
(1,0,0)
is the normal direction of the boundary. This boundary condition can be seen in the jet with a free boundary. Under the assumption of the Poiseuille flow, there is no in-flow or out-flow, so the boundary is fixed. This corresponds to the Neumann boundary condition on
u
.

•

Navier boundary condition. On the boundary, the shear stress is proportional to the tangential velocity, that is
J=-\gamma u
. This corresponds to the Robin boundary condition on
u
.

For the director
\mathbf{n}
, one can propose the following boundary conditions
[
35
]
.

•

Strong anchoring condition. On the boundary,
\mathbf{n}
is given. This corresponds to the Dirichlet boundary condition.

•

No anchoring condition.
\frac{\partial W}{\partial n_{i,j}}\nu_{j}=0
.

•

Weak anchoring condition.
\frac{\partial W_{F}}{\partial n_{i,j}}\nu_{j}+\frac{\partial W_{s}}{\partial n_{i}}=\gamma n_{i}
.

In this paper, we will consider all combinations of boundary conditions, excluding the Navier boundary condition.

1.1.
Main result

For simplicity, we only
consider a special case when
\alpha_{1}=0

\alpha_{5}-\alpha_{2}=\alpha_{3}+\alpha_{6}

\frac{\alpha_{3}+\alpha_{4}+\alpha_{6}}{2}

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}u_{t}=(u_{x}+\theta_{t})_{x},\\
\theta_{tt}+2\theta_{t}=c(\theta)(c(\theta)\theta_{x})_{x}-u_{x},\end{array}\right.

(1.4)

\displaystyle 0<C_{L}\leq c(\cdot)\leq C_{U}<\infty,\ \ |c^{\prime}(\cdot)|\leq C_{1}<\infty,

Our existence result on the initial boundary value problem also applies to the variational wave equation (

However, one cannot easily extend the result for the variational wave equation to (

For the initial-boundary value problem, we still use

The second step is to find a fixed point using the heat equation on
