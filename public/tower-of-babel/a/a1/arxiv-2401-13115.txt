Abstract
. Diffusion probabilistic models (DPMs) have emerged as a promising technique in generative modeling. The success of DPMs relies on two ingredients: time reversal of diffusion processes and score matching. In view of possibly unguaranteed score matching, we propose a new criterion – the contraction property of backward sampling in the design of DPMs, leading to a novel class of contractive DPMs (CDPMs). Our key insight is that, the contraction property can provably narrow score matching errors and discretization errors, thus our proposed CDPMs are robust to both sources of error. For practical use, we show that CDPM can leverage weights of pretrained DPMs by a simple transformation, and does not need retraining. We corroborated our approach by experiments on synthetic 1-dim examples, Swiss Roll, MNIST, CIFAR-10 32 × \times 32 and AFHQ 64 × \times 64 dataset. Notably, CDPM steadily improves the performance of baseline score-based diffusion models.



Key words
: Contraction, diffusion probabilistic models, discretization, generative models,
image synthesis, sampling, score matching, stochastic differential equations.

The concept of DPMs finds its roots in energy-based models

[

in an attempt to produce from noise new samples

See
[

DPMs relies on forward-backward Markov processes.

The forward process
starts with the target data distribution,
and runs for some time until the signal is destroyed –
this gives rise to
noise

The backward process
is then initiated with this prior,
and reverses the forward process in time to generate samples
whose distribution is close to the target distribution.
In

, DPMs are discrete time-indexed Markov chains;

Nevertheless,
there is no conceptual distinction between discrete and continuous DPMs,
as continuous DPMs can be viewed as the continuum limits of discrete DPMs,
and discrete DPMs are time discretizations of the latter.
In this paper, we adopt a
continuous-time
score-based perspective,
and algorithms are derived by discretization.

There has been great interest in improving diffusion models further in terms of image/distribution quality, training cost, and generation speed. In this work, we focus on understanding and improving the choice of drift and diffusion coefficients of the governed SDE in the forward process, which will affect the generation quality of the DPMs. Popular examples include
Ornstein-Uhlenbeck (OU) processes
[
13
]
,
variance exploding (VE) SDEs
[
50
]
,
variance preserving (VP) SDEs
[

]
,
and sub-variance preserving (subVP) SDEs
[
53
]
(see Table
1
for details). Despite the empirical success of diffusion models driven by these specific SDEs, theoretical understanding of these designs are limited and a fundamental question remains unanswered:

How to design parameters of a DPM for better generation quality?

There has been controversy around the best design of the diffusion model/SDE parameters, e.g. one should choose VP or VE for his own training of diffusion models. According to empirical studies, the design of exploding variance in VE could yield a better result for CIFAR10 and ImageNet synthesis, e.g. in EDM baseline
[
25
]
. However, the reason for this choice is highly empirical and with minimal theoretical justifications. Why VE SDE performs better than VP SDE? The purpose of this paper is to partly answer these questions theoretically and further improve the performance of diffusion models by our theorectical findings. Our proposal is, when facing score matching errors in score-based diffusion models:

The backward process of the DPM shall be
contractive
.

Here we explain the high-level idea of “contractive-ness".
For a diffusion process, “contractive-ness” is related to the (long-time) convergence of the process to stationarity. By letting
\mathcal{L}
be the infinitesimal generator, it is specified by the Lyapunov type condition:

\mathcal{L}f\leq-c
(or a stronger form
\mathcal{L}f\leq-cf
) outside a compact domain, for some test function
f
and
c>0
. When adapting to diffusion models, there are two tweaks:
(1) the Lyapunov condition is implicitly satisfied for some diffusion coefficients, and (2) there are score matching errors.
We further propose a simplified checkable necessary condition on diffusion coefficients to guarantee a contractive backward process, which we elaborate in Section 3.

We call DPMs that yield a contractive backward process as
contractive DPMs
(CDPMs).
At a high level,
the contraction of the backward process will prevent score matching errors,
which may be wild, from expanding over the time.
The contributions of this work are summarized as follows.

Methodology
:
We propose a new criterion for designing DPMs.
This naturally leads to a novel class of DPMs,
including contractive OU processes and contractive subVP SDEs.
The idea of requiring the backward process to be contractive
stems from sampling theory of SDEs,
so our methodology is theory-oriented.
To our best knowledge,
this is the first paper to integrate contraction into the design of DPMs,
with both theoretical guarantees
and good empirical performance.

Theory
:
We prove Wasserstein bounds between contractive DPM samplers
and the target data distribution.
While most previous work (e.g.,
[
10
,
13
,
32
]
)
focused on Kullback–Leibler (KL) or total variation bounds for OU processes,
we consider the Wasserstein metric because
it has shown to align with human judgment on image similarity
[
5
]
, and the standard evaluation metric – Fréchet inception distance (FID) is based on Wasserstein distance.
Early work
[
13
,
31
]
gave Wasserstein bounds for the existing DPMs (OU processes, VE and VP SDEs)
with exponential dependence on
T
.
This was improved in recent studies
[
10
,
33
,
20
]

under various assumptions of
p_{\scalebox{0.7}{data}}(\cdot)
,
where the bounds are typically of form:

\underbrace{(\small\mbox{noise inaccuracy})\cdot e^{-T}}_{\tiny\mbox{initialization error}}+\underbrace{(\small\mbox{score mismatch})\cdot\texttt{Poly}(T)}_{\tiny\mbox{score error}}+\underbrace{\texttt{Poly}(\small\mbox{step size})\cdot\texttt{Poly}(T)}_{\tiny\mbox{discretization error}},

with
\texttt{Poly}(\cdot)
referring to polynomial in the variable.
Our result gives a Wasserstein bound for CDPMs:

\displaystyle\underbrace{(\small\mbox{noise inaccuracy})\cdot e^{-T}}_{\tiny\mbox{initialization error}}+\underbrace{(\small\mbox{score mismatch})\cdot(1-e^{-T})}_{\tiny\mbox{score error}}+\underbrace{\texttt{Poly}(\small\mbox{step size})}_{\tiny\mbox{discretization error}}.

Score matching is often trained using blackbox function approximations,
and the errors incurred in this step may be large.
So CDPMs are designed to be robust to score mismatch
and discretization,
at the cost of possible initialization bias.

Experiments
: We apply the proposed CDPMs to both synthetic and real data. In dimension one, we compare contractive OU with OU by adding a fixed noise to the true score function, which yields the same score matching error. Our result shows that contractive OU consistently beats OU, and is robust to different error levels and time discretization.
We further compare the performance of different models via
Wasserstein-2 distance of the SWISS Roll dataset and FIDs of MNIST,
which show that CDPMs outperform other SDE models.
On the task of CIFAR-10 unconditional generation,
we obtain an FID score of 2.47 and an inception score of 10.18 for CDPM,
which requires no retraining by transforming the pretrained weights of VE-SDE in
[
53
]
, surpassing all other SDE models.

2.
Background on Score-based Diffusion Models

2.1.
Forward and Backward SDEs.

In this work, we explain the formulation of diffusion models based on the Stochastic Differential Equations (SDEs) presented by
[
53
]
.
Consider a fixed time horizon
T
, and an SDE-governed forward process
\{X_{t}\}_{t\in[0,T]}
that describes how data distribution evolves as:

\mathrm{d}X_{t}=b(t,X_{t})\mathrm{d}t+\sigma(t)\mathrm{d}B_{t},\quad\mbox{with }X_{0}\sim p_{\scalebox{0.7}{data}}(\cdot),

(2.1)

where drift coefficient

b:\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
diffusion coefficient
\sigma:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}

are hyper-parameters as part of the design space of diffusion models.
Theorectically, some conditions on
b(\cdot,\cdot)
and
\sigma(\cdot)
are required
so that the SDE (
2.1
) is at least well-defined (see
[
24
, Chapter 5]
,
[
55
, Section 3.1]
for details).

We further denote
p(t,\cdot)
be the probability density of
X_{t}
(then
p(0,\cdot)=p_{\scalebox{0.7}{data}}(\cdot)
is the data distribution).
Diffusion Models consider a time reversal
\overline{X}_{t}:=X_{T-t}
for
0\leq t\leq T
of the original process
X_{t}
for generative sampling. Existing literature on the time reversal of SDEs
[
1
,
21
]
have proved that
\overline{X}
also satisfies an SDE (see proof in
Appendix A.), such that

\mathrm{d}\overline{X}_{t}=\bar{b}(t,\overline{X}_{t})\mathrm{d}t+\bar{\sigma}(t)\mathrm{d}\overline{B}_{t},\quad\mbox{with }\overline{X}_{0}\sim p(T,\cdot),

(2.2)

in which the drift and diffusion coefficient yield a closed-form (we denote
\nabla
the gradient w.r.p.
x
):

\overline{\sigma}(t)=\sigma(T-t),\quad\overline{b}(t,x)=-b(T-t,x)+\sigma^{2}(T-t)\nabla\log p(T-t,x).

(2.3)

The term
\nabla\log p(\cdot,\cdot)
is also known as the Stein’s score function. However, scores are unknown, and the procedures or methods to estimate scores are referred as score matching.

2.2.
Score Matching

To estimate the scores, recently score-based diffusion models
[
50
,
53
]

proposed to learn
\nabla\log p(t,x)
by function approximations via deep neural networks.
More precisely,
denote by
\{s_{\theta}(t,x)\}_{\theta}
a family of functions on
\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}

parametrized by
\theta
, the scores are approximated by minimizing a weighted
L^{2}
loss across time:

\min_{\theta}\tilde{\mathcal{J}}_{\text{ESM}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{t\in\mathcal{U}(0,T)}\mathbb{E}_{p(t,\cdot)}\left[\lambda(t)|s_{\theta}(t,X)-\nabla\log p(t,X)|^{2}\right].

(2.4)

where
\mathcal{U}(0,T)
denotes a uniform distribution on
[0,T]
, and
\lambda:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
is a positive weighting function.
(
2.4
) is also known as the
explicit score matching
(ESM) objective. For efficiently solving (
2.4
) in which scores
\nabla\log p(t,X)
are still unknown, existing works in denoising diffusion models
[
53
,
25
]
solve an equivalent
denoising score matching
(DSM) objective

\tilde{\mathcal{J}}_{\text{DSM}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{t\sim\mathcal{U}(0,T)}\left\{\lambda(t)\mathbb{E}_{X_{0}\sim p_{data}(\cdot)}\mathbb{E}_{X_{t}\mid X_{0}}\left[\left|s_{\theta}(t,X(t))-\nabla_{X_{t}}\log p(t,X_{t}\mid X_{0})\right|^{2}\right]\right\}.

DSM objective has the advantage that the conditional distribution
p(t,X_{t}\mid X_{0})
is commonly tractable, and the scores yield a simple closed form when (
2.1
) is a linear SDE (which is a common choice in literature), i.e.
b(t,x)=b(t)\cdot x
for some function
b(t):\mathbb{R}_{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. We discuss this equivalence and other score matching methods such as sliced score matching
[
52
]
in Appendix C.

3.
Theory for contractive DPMs

In this section,
we formally introduce the idea of CDPMs, and present supportive theoretical results.
For a DPM specified by (
2.1
)-(
2.5
),
we consider the Euler-Maruyama discretization of its backward process
\overline{X}
.
Fix
\delta>0
as the step size,
and set
t_{k}:=k\delta
for
k=0,\ldots,N:=\frac{T}{\delta}
.
Let
\widehat{X}_{0}=\overline{X}_{0}
,
and

\displaystyle\widehat{X}_{k}:=\widehat{X}_{k-1}+(-b(T-t_{k},\widehat{X}_{k-1})+

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}(T-t_{k-1})s_{\theta}(T-t_{k-1},\widehat{X}_{k-1}))\delta

(3.1)

\displaystyle+\sigma(T-t_{k-1})(B_{t_{k}}-B_{t_{k-1}}),\quad\mbox{for }k=1,\ldots,N.

Our goal is to bound the Wasserstein-2 distance
W_{2}(p_{\scalebox{0.7}{data}}(\cdot),\widehat{X}_{N})
.
Clearly,

W_{2}(p_{\scalebox{0.7}{data}}(\cdot),\widehat{X}_{N})\leq W_{2}(p_{\scalebox{0.7}{data}}(\cdot),\overline{X}_{T})+\left(\mathbb{E}|\overline{X}_{T}-\widehat{X}_{N}|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}},

(3.2)

where the first term on the right side of (
3.2
) is
the sampling error at the continuous level,
and the second term is the discretization error.
We will study these two terms in the next two subsections.

3.1.
Sampling error in continuous time

We are concerned with the term
W_{2}(p_{\scalebox{0.7}{data}}(\cdot),\overline{X}_{T})
.
Existing work
[
10
,
33
,
13
]
established
W_{2}
bounds
for OU processes under a bounded support assumption.
Closer to our result (and proof) is the concurrent work
[
20
]
,
where a
W_{2}
bound is derived
for a class of DPMs with
b(t,x)
separable in
t
and
x
,
under a strongly log-concavity assumption.

Assumption 1
.

The following conditions hold:

(1)

There exists
r_{b}:[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}
such that

(x-x^{\prime})\cdot(b(t,x)-b(t,x^{\prime}))\geq r_{b}(t)|x-x^{\prime}|^{2}
for all
t
and
x,x^{\prime}
.

(2)

There exists
L>0
such that
|\nabla\log p(t,x)-\nabla\log p(t,x^{\prime})|\leq L|x-x^{\prime}|
for all
t
and
x,x^{\prime}
.

(3)

There exists
\varepsilon>0
such that

\mathbb{E}|s_{\theta}(t,\overline{X}_{T-t})-\nabla\log p(t,\overline{X}_{T-t})|^{2}\leq\varepsilon^{2}
for all
t
.

The condition (1) assumes the monotonicity of
b(t,\cdot)

and (2) assumes the Lipschitz property of the score functions.
In the previous examples,

b(t,x)
is linear in
x

so the density
p(t,\cdot)
is Gaussian-like,
and its score is almost affine.
Thus, it is reasonable to assume (2).
Conditions (1) and (2)
are used to quantify how a perturbation of the model parameters in an SDE
affects its distribution.
The condition (3) specifies how accurate Stein’s score is estimated by
a blackbox estimation.
There has been work (e.g.
[
7
,
29
,
39
]
) on the efficiency of score approximations.
So it is possible to replace the condition (3) with those score approximation bounds.

Theorem 2
.

Let Assumption
1
hold, and any
h>0
.
Define
\eta:=W_{2}(p(T,\cdot),p_{\scalebox{0.7}{noise}}(\cdot))
,
and

u(t):=\int_{T-t}^{T}\left(-2r_{b}(s)+(2L+2h)\sigma^{2}(s)\right)ds.

(3.3)

Then we have

W_{2}(p_{\scalebox{0.7}{data}}(\cdot),\overline{X}_{T})\leq\sqrt{\eta^{2}e^{u(T)}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2h}\int_{0}^{T}\sigma^{2}(t)e^{u(T)-u(T-t)}dt}.

(3.4)

The proof of Theorem
2
is deferred to Appendix D.
A similar result was given in
[
31
]

under an unconventional assumption that
the score matching functions
s_{\theta}(t,x)
,
rather than the score functions
\nabla p(t,x)
,
are Lipschitz.
In fact,
the (impractical) assumption that
the score matching functions are Lipschitz
is not needed at the continuous level,
and can be replaced with the Lipschitz condition on the score functions.
On the other hand,
the Lipschitz property of the score matching functions
are required, for technical purposes,
to bound the discretization error in Section
3.2
.

It is possible to establish sharper bounds under extra (structural) conditions on
(b(\cdot,\cdot),\sigma(\cdot))
,
and also specify the dependence in dimension
d
(e.g.
[
10
,
20
]
).
For instance, if we assume
b(t,x)
is separable in
t
and
x
and linear in
x
,
and
p_{\scalebox{0.7}{data}}(\cdot)
is strongly log-concave,
then the term
L\sigma^{2}(s)
in (
3.3
) will become
-L^{\prime}\sigma^{2}(s)
for some
L^{\prime}>0
. The dependence of
L
on the
b
and
\sigma
can also be readily relaxed given additional assumptions on the data distribution,we leave the full investigation of its theory to the future work.

Now we explain contractive DPMs.
Revisiting bound (
3.4
),
the sampling error
W_{2}(p_{\scalebox{0.7}{data}}(\cdot),\overline{X}_{T})

is linear in the score matching error
\varepsilon

and the initialization error
\eta
,
and these errors may be amplified in time
T

– in most aforementioned DPMs,

r_{b}(t)\leq 0
so
u(t)
is positive and at least linear.
As mentioned earlier,
it is problematic
if we don’t know how good a blackbox score matching
s_{\theta}(t,x)
is.
Our idea is simply
to make
u(t)
be negative, that is to set
r_{b}(t)>0
sufficiently large,
in order to prevent the score matching error from propagating in backward sampling.
This yields the class of CDPMs,
which is inherently different from existing DPMs in the sense that
these DPMs often have contractive forward processes,
while our proposal requires contractive backward processes.
Quantitatively, we can set for some
\alpha>0
,

\textbf{contractive cond.}\quad\inf_{0\leq t\leq T}\left(r_{b}(t)-(L+h)\sigma^{2}(t)\right)\geq\alpha.

(3.5)

In practice, it suffices to design
(b(\cdot,\cdot),\sigma(\cdot))
with a positive
r_{b}(t)
for ant
t\in[\epsilon,T]
, which we refer as a practical condition (actually a necessary condition) of contractiveness. We formally define
CDPMs
as diffusion models governed by SDEs that
satisfy this practical version of contractiveness
, i.e.
r_{b}(t)>0
. Notice that when
b(t,x)=b(t)\cdot x
, this is equivalent to
b(t)>0
.

is
\kappa
-strongly log-concave,
and
\mathbb{E}_{p_{\scalebox{0.7}{data}}(\cdot)}|x|^{2}<\infty
.
For
h<\min(\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{\beta_{\text{max}}T}\frac{\kappa}{1+\kappa})
, we have:

W^{2}_{2}(p_{\scalebox{0.7}{data}}(\cdot),\overline{X}_{T})\leq e^{-2\left(\frac{\kappa}{1+\kappa}-\beta_{\max}hT+\mathcal{O}(e^{-\beta_{\min}T})\right)}\mathbb{E}_{p_{\tiny\mbox{data}}(\cdot)}|x|^{2}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2h(1-2h)}.

(3.7)

The proof of Theorem
3
is given in Appendix E.
It is easy to see from the theorem that CVP (which follows similarly for other CDPMs)
allow to control the score matching error
\varepsilon
,
at the possible cost of initialization error coming from
\eta
.
Note that if
\beta_{\max}T
is asymptotically small,
this error is bounded.
This requires scaling the hyperparameters with respect to
T
in the model.
We observe in the experiment that tuning a moderate level
\beta
is important to let CDPM benefit from contraction while not suffer from the initialization error.
Also note that it is not necessary to send
T\to\infty
, and

T=1
is taken in
[
53
]
.

3.2.
Discretization error

We study the discretization error
\left(\mathbb{E}|\overline{X}_{T}-\widehat{X}_{N}|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

for CDPMs.
Classical SDE theory
[
28
]
indicates that
\left(\mathbb{E}|\overline{X}_{T}-\widehat{X}_{N}|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq C(T)\delta
,
with the constant
C(T)
exponential in
T
.
Here we show that by a proper choice of the pair
(b(\cdot,\cdot),\sigma(\cdot))
leading to CDPMs,
the constant
C(T)
can be made independent of
T
.
In other words, the discretization error will not expand over the time.
We need some technical assumptions.

Assumption 4
.

The following conditions hold:

(1)

There exists
L_{\sigma}>0
such that
|\sigma(t)-\sigma(t^{\prime})|\leq L_{\sigma}|t-t^{\prime}|
for all
t,t^{\prime}
.

(2)

There exists
R_{\sigma}>0
such that
\sigma(t)\leq R_{\sigma}
for all
t
.

(3)

There exists
L_{b}>0
such that
|b(t,x)-b(t^{\prime},x^{\prime})|\leq L_{b}(|t-t^{\prime}|+|x-x^{\prime}|)
for all
t,t^{\prime}
and
x,x^{\prime}
.

(4)

There exists
L_{s}>0
such that
|s_{\theta}(t,x)-s_{\theta}(t^{\prime},x^{\prime})|\leq L_{s}(|t-t^{\prime}|+|x-x^{\prime}|)
for all
t,t^{\prime}
and
x,x^{\prime}
.

(5)

There exists
R_{s}>0
such that
|s_{\theta}(T,x)|\leq R_{s}(1+|x|)
for all
x
.

Next we introduce a contractive assumption that is consistent with (
3.5
).

Assumption 5
.

There exists
\beta>0
such that

\int_{T-t}^{T}(r_{b}(s)-L_{s}\sigma^{2}(s))\,ds\geq\beta t,\quad\mbox{for all }t,

(3.8)

or simply

\beta:=\inf_{0\leq t\leq T}\left(r_{b}(t)-L_{s}\sigma^{2}(t)\right)>0.

(3.9)

Theorem 6
.

Let Assumptions
1
,
4
and
5
hold.
Then there exists
C>0
(independent of
\delta,T
) such that
for
\delta>0
sufficiently small,

\left(\mathbb{E}|\overline{X}_{T}-\widehat{X}_{N}|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq C\sqrt{\delta}.

(3.10)

The proof of Theorem
6
is given in Appendix F.

4.2.
CDPM can be transformed from VE

We show that CDPM, though different from existing DPMs,
can be derived from VE via a time/space change of variable,
so does not require pretraining the score matching objectives again.
Note that for COU, CVP and CsubVP,
the parameter
b(t,x)
is separate in
t
and
x
.
We follow
[
25
]
,
and define the perturbation kernel of CDPM as:

X_{t}\mid X_{0}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(f(t)X_{0},\,f(t)^{2}g(t)^{2}I\right),

where

f(t)=e^{\frac{t^{2}}{4T}(\beta_{\max}-\beta_{\min})+\frac{t}{2}\beta_{\min}}\quad\text{and}\quad g(t)=f(t)-f^{-1}(t).

(4.1)

Figure
2
plots
different perturbation kernels of SDE models:
existing models lead to either a constant or a decreasing kernel,
while we propose the kernel be increasing.
This yields CsubVP and EDM+,
which we will show in Section
5.3
.

Denote by
p_{\scalebox{0.7}{VE}}(t,\cdot)
and
p_{\scalebox{0.7}{CsubVP}}(t,\cdot)

the probability distribution of
X_{t}
following VE and CsubVP respectively.
Assume that we have access to a pretrained VE score matching

s_{pre}(t,x)\approx\nabla\log p_{VE}(t,x)
.
We can then compute the CDPM score by the following transformation,
which is read from
[
25
, Equation (12) and (19)]
.

Theorem 7
.

Assume that
\sigma^{2}_{\max}-\sigma^{2}_{\min}>g^{2}(T)
.
We have for
t\in[0,T]
,

p_{\scalebox{0.7}{CsubVP}}(t,x)=f(t)^{-d}p_{\scalebox{0.7}{VE}}(\tau(t),x/f(t)),

(4.2)

where

\tau(t)=\frac{T}{2}\frac{\log(1+\frac{g^{2}(t)}{\sigma^{2}_{\min}})}{\log(\sigma_{\max}/\sigma_{\min})}.

(4.3)

So it suffices to take
\nabla\log p_{\scalebox{0.7}{CsubVP}}(t,x)\approx s_{pre}(\tau(t),x/f(t))
,
meaning that we can exploit existing score matching neural nets,
or pretrained weights for CDPM sampling.

6.
Conclusion

In this paper,
we propose a new criterion –
the contraction of backward sampling
in the design of SDE-based DPMs.
This naturally leads to a novel class of contractive DPMs.
The main takeaway is that the contraction of the backward process
limits score matching errors from propagating,
and controls discretization error as well.
Our proposal is supported by theoretical considerations,
and is corroborated by experiments.
Notably, our proposed contractive subVP outperforms
other SDE-based DPMs on CIFAR 10 dataset.
Though the intention of this paper is not to beat SOTA diffusion models,
CDPMs show promising results
that we hope to trigger further research.

There are a few directions to extend this work.
First, we assume that score matching errors
are bounded in
L^{2}

as in
[
10
,
20
,
33
]
.
It is interesting to see whether this assumption
can be relaxed to more realistic conditions
given the application domain.
Second, it is desirable to establish sharp theory for CDPMs, with dimension dependence.
Finally, our formulation is based on SDEs,
and hence stochastic samplers.
We don’t look into ODE samplers as in

[
53
,
59
,
35
,
9
]
.
This leaves open the problems such as
whether the proposed CDPMs perform
well by ODE samplers,
and why the ODE samplers derived from SDEs
outperform those directly learnt,
as observed in previous studies.

A. Proof of SDE time reversal

The time reversal of SDEs
[
1
,
21
]
.

Theorem 8
.

Under suitable conditions on
b(\cdot,\cdot)
,
\sigma(\cdot)
and
\{p(t,\cdot)\}_{0\leq t\leq T}
,
we have

\overline{\sigma}(t)=\sigma(T-t),\quad\overline{b}(t,x)=-b(T-t,x)+\sigma^{2}(T-t)\nabla\log p(T-t,x),

(.1)

where the term
\nabla\log p(\cdot,\cdot)
is called Stein’s score function.

Proof.

Here we give a heuristic derivation of the time reversal formula (
.1
).
First, the infinitesimal generator of
X
is

\mathcal{L}:=\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}(t)\Delta+b\cdot\nabla
.
It is known that the density
p(t,x)
satisfies the the Fokker–Planck equation:

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}p(t,x)=\mathcal{L}^{*}p(t,x),

(.2)

where
\mathcal{L}^{*}:=\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}(t)\Delta-\nabla\cdot b
is the adjoint of
\mathcal{L}
.
Let
\overline{p}(t,x):=p(T-t,x)
be the probability density of the time reversal
\overline{X}
.
By (
.2
), we get

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\overline{p}(t,x)=-\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}(t)\Delta\overline{p}(t,x)+\nabla\cdot\left(b(T-t,x)\,\overline{p}(t,x)\right).

(.3)

On the other hand, we expect the generator of
\overline{X}
to be

\overline{\mathcal{L}}:=\frac{1}{2}\overline{\sigma}^{2}(t)\Delta+\overline{b}\cdot\nabla
.
The Fokker-Planck equation for
\overline{p}(t,x)
is

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\overline{p}(t,x)=\frac{1}{2}\overline{\sigma}^{2}(t)\Delta\overline{p}(t,x)-\nabla\cdot\left(\overline{b}(t,x)\,\overline{p}(t,x)\right).

(.4)

Comparing (
.3
) and (
.4
),
we set
\overline{\sigma}(t)=\sigma(T-t)
and then get

\left(b(T-t,x)+\overline{b}(t,x)\right)\overline{p}(t,x)=\sigma^{2}(T-t)\,\nabla\overline{p}(t,x).

This yields the desired result.
∎

Let’s comment on Theorem
8
.

[
21
]
proved the result by assuming that
b(\cdot,\cdot)
and
\sigma(\cdot,\cdot)
are globally Lipschitz, and
the density
p(t,x)
satisfies an a priori
H^{1}
bound.
The implicit condition on
p(t,x)
is guaranteed if
\partial_{t}+\mathcal{L}
is hypoelliptic.
These conditions were relaxed in
[
42
]
.
In another direction,
[
18
,
19
]
used an entropy argument to prove the time reversal formula in the case
\sigma(t)=\sigma
.
This approach was further developed in
[
6
]
which made connections to optimal transport.

\displaystyle d\overline{X}_{t}

\displaystyle=\bigg(\frac{1}{2}\beta(T-t)\overline{X}_{t}+\beta(T-t)\nabla\log p(T-t,\overline{X}_{t})\bigg)dt

C. Score matching

(a) Sliced score matching.
One way is that we further address the term
\nabla_{x}\cdot s_{\theta}(t,x)
by random projections. The method proposed in
[
52
]
is called sliced score matching.
Considering the Jacobian matrix
\nabla s_{\theta}(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
,
we have

\nabla\cdot s_{\theta}(t,x)=\operatorname{Tr}(\nabla s_{\theta}(t,x))=\mathbb{E}_{v\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)}\left[v^{\top}\nabla s_{\theta}(t,x)v\right].

We can then rewrite the training objective as:

\min_{\theta}\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}_{\text{SSM}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{t\in\mathcal{U}(0,T)}\mathbb{E}_{v_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)}\mathbb{E}_{p(t,\cdot)}\left[\lambda(t)\left(\|s_{\theta}(t,X)\|^{2}+2\,v^{\top}\nabla(v^{\top}s_{\theta}(t,x))\right)\right].

(.9)

which can be computed easily.
It requires only two times of back propagation, as
v^{\top}s_{\theta}(t,x)
can be seen as adding a layer of the inner product between
v
and
s_{\theta}
.

(b) Denoising score matching.
The second way is that we go back to the objective (
2.4
),
and use a nonparametric estimation.
The idea stems from
[
23
,
58
]
,
in which it was shown that

\mathcal{J}_{ESM}
is equivalent to the following denoising score matching (DSM) objective:

\tilde{\mathcal{J}}_{\text{DSM}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{t\sim\mathcal{U}(0,T)}\left\{\lambda(t)\mathbb{E}_{X_{0}\sim p_{data}(\cdot)}\mathbb{E}_{X_{t}\mid X_{0}}\left[\left|s_{\theta}(t,X(t))-\nabla_{X_{t}}\log p(t,X_{t}\mid X_{0})\right|^{2}\right]\right\}

Theorem 9
.

Let

\mathcal{J}_{\text{DSM}}(\theta):=\mathbb{E}_{X_{0}\sim p_{data}(\cdot)}\mathbb{E}_{X_{t}\mid X_{0}}\left[\left|s_{\theta}(t,X(t))-\nabla_{X_{t}}\log p(t,X_{t}\mid X_{0})\right|^{2}\right]
.
Under suitable conditions on
s_{\theta}
, we have

\mathcal{J}_{\text{DSM}}(\theta)=\mathcal{J}_{\text{ESM}}(\theta)+C
for some
C
independent of
\theta
.
Consequently, the minimum point of
\mathcal{J}_{\text{DSM}}
and that of
\mathcal{J}_{\text{ESM}}
coincide.

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{\text{ESM}}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{p(t,\cdot)}|s_{\theta}(t,X)-\nabla\log p(t,X)|^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{p(t,\cdot)}\left[|s_{\theta}(t,X)|^{2}-2s_{\theta}(t,X)^{\top}\nabla\log p(t,X)+|\nabla\log p(t,X)|^{2}\right].

Consider the inner product term, rewriting it as:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{p(t,\cdot)}\left[s_{\theta}(t,X)^{\top}\nabla\log p(t,X)\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{x}p(t,x)s_{\theta}(t,x)^{\top}\nabla\log p(t,x)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{x}s_{\theta}(t,x)^{\top}\nabla p(t,x)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{x}s_{\theta}(t,x)^{\top}\nabla\int_{x_{0}}p(0,x_{0})p(t,x|x_{0})\mathrm{d}x_{0}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{x_{0}}\int_{x}s_{\theta}(t,x)^{\top}p(0,x_{0})\nabla p(t,x|x_{0})\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}x_{0}

\displaystyle=\int_{x_{0}}p(0,x_{0})\int_{x}s_{\theta}(t,x)^{\top}p(t,x|x_{0})\nabla\log p(t,x|x_{0})\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}x_{0}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{X_{0}\sim p(0,\cdot)}\mathbb{E}_{X_{t}\mid X_{0}}\left[s_{\theta}(t,X(t))^{\top}\nabla_{X_{t}}\log p(t,X_{t}\mid X_{0})\right],

combining
\mathbb{E}_{X}|s_{\theta}(t,X)|^{2}=\mathbb{E}_{X_{0}}\mathbb{E}_{X\mid X_{0}}|s_{\theta}(t,X)|^{2}
concludes our proof.
∎

The intuition of DSM is that following the gradient
s_{\theta}
of the log density at some corrupted point
\tilde{x}
should ideally move us towards the clean sample
x
. The reason that the objective
\mathcal{J}_{DSM}
is comparatively easy to solve is that conditional distribution usually satisfies a good distribution, like Gaussian kernel, i.e.
p(X_{t}\mid X_{0})\sim N\left(X_{t};\mu_{t}(X_{0}),\sigma_{t}^{2}I\right)
, which is satisfied in many cases of DPM, we can explicitly compute that:

\nabla_{X_{t}}\log p(t,X_{t}\mid X_{0})=\frac{1}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}(\mu_{t}(X_{0})-X_{t}).

Direction
\frac{1}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}(X_{0}-\mu_{t}(X_{0}))
clearly corresponds to moving from
\tilde{x}
back towards clean sample
x
, and we want
s_{\theta}
to match that as best it can. Moreover, empirically validated by e.g.
[
53
]
, a good candidate of
\lambda(t)
is chosen as:

\lambda(t)\propto 1/\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\nabla_{X_{t}}\log p(t,X_{t}\mid X_{0})\right|^{2}\right]=\sigma_{t}^{2}

Thus, our final optimization objective is:

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{J}}_{\text{DSM}}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{t\sim\mathcal{U}(0,T)}\left\{\sigma_{t}^{2}\mathbb{E}_{X_{0}\sim p_{data}(\cdot)}\mathbb{E}_{X_{t}\mid X_{0}}\left[\left|s_{\theta}(t,X(t))-\nabla_{X_{t}}\log p(t,X_{t}\mid X_{0})\right|^{2}\right]\right\}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{t\sim\mathcal{U}(0,T)}\left\{\mathbb{E}_{X_{0}\sim p_{data}(\cdot)}\mathbb{E}_{X_{t}\mid X_{0}}\left[\left|\sigma_{t}s_{\theta}(t,X(t))+\frac{X_{t}-\mu_{t}(X_{0})}{\sigma_{t}}\right|^{2}\right]\right\}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{t\sim\mathcal{U}(0,T)}\left\{\mathbb{E}_{X_{0}\sim p_{data}(\cdot)}\mathbb{E}_{\epsilon_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)}\left[\left|\sigma_{t}s_{\theta}(t,\mu_{t}(X_{0})+\sigma_{t}\epsilon_{t})+\epsilon_{t}\right|^{2}\right]\right\}

where the second equality holds when
X_{t}\mid X_{0}
follows a conditionally normal and the third equality follows from a reparameterization/change of variables.

D. Proof of Theorem
2

The idea relies on coupling, which is similar to
[
61
, Lemma 4]
.
Consider the coupled SDEs:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lcl}dY_{t}=\left(-b(T-t,Y_{t})+\sigma^{2}(T-t)\nabla\log p(T-t,Y_{t})\right)dt+\sigma(T-t)dB_{t},\\
dZ_{t}=\left(-b(T-t,Z_{t})+\sigma^{2}(T-t)s_{\theta}(T-t,Z_{t})\right)dt+\sigma(T-t)dB_{t},\end{array}\right.

where
(Y_{0},Z_{0})
are coupled to achieve
W_{2}(p(T,\cdot),p_{\scalebox{0.7}{noise}}(\cdot))
,
i.e.
\mathbb{E}|Y_{0}-Z_{0}|^{2}=W_{2}(p(T,\cdot),p_{\scalebox{0.7}{noise}}(\cdot))
.
It is easy to see that

W_{2}^{2}(p_{\scalebox{0.7}{data}}(\cdot),\overline{X}_{T})\leq\mathbb{E}|Y_{T}-Z_{T}|^{2}.

(.10)

So the goal is to bound
\mathbb{E}|Y_{T}-Z_{T}|^{2}
.
By Itô’s formula, we get

d|Y_{t}-Z_{t}|^{2}=2(Y_{t}-Z_{t})\cdot(-b(T-t,Y_{t})+\sigma^{2}(T-t)\nabla\log p(T-t,Y_{t})\\
+b(T-t,Z_{t})-\sigma^{2}(T-t)s_{\theta}(T-t,Z_{t}))dt

which implies that

\frac{d\,\mathbb{E}|Y_{t}-Z_{t}|^{2}}{dt}=-2\underbrace{\mathbb{E}((Y_{t}-Z_{t})\cdot(b(T-t,Y_{t})-b(T-t,Z_{t}))}_{(a)})\\
+2\underbrace{\mathbb{E}((Y_{t}-Z_{t})\cdot\sigma^{2}(T-t)(\nabla\log p(T-t,Y_{t})-s_{\theta}(T-t,Z_{t})))}_{(b)}.

(.11)

By Assumption
1
(1), we get

(a)\geq r_{b}(T-t)\,\mathbb{E}|Y_{t}-Z_{t}|^{2}.

(.12)

Moreover,

\displaystyle(b)

\displaystyle=\sigma^{2}(T-t)\bigg(\mathbb{E}((Y_{t}-Z_{t})\cdot(\nabla\log p(T-t,Y_{t})-\nabla\log p(T-t,Z_{t})))

(.13)

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}((Y_{t}-Z_{t})\cdot(\nabla\log p(T-t,Z_{t})-s_{\theta}(T-t,Z_{t})))\bigg)

\displaystyle\leq\sigma^{2}(T-t)\left(L\,\mathbb{E}|Y_{t}-Z_{t}|^{2}+h\mathbb{E}|Y_{t}-Z_{t}|^{2}+\frac{1}{4h}\varepsilon^{2}\right),

where we use Assumption
1
(2)(3) in the last inequality.
Combining (
.11
), (
.12
) and (
.13
), we have

\frac{d\,\mathbb{E}|Y_{t}-Z_{t}|^{2}}{dt}\leq\left(-2r_{b}(T-t)+(2h+2L)\sigma^{2}(T-t)\right)\mathbb{E}|Y_{t}-Z_{t}|^{2}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2h}\sigma^{2}(T-t).

(.14)

Applying Grönwall’s inequality, we have:

\mathbb{E}|Y_{T}-Z_{T}|^{2}\leq e^{u(T)}\mathbb{E}|Y_{0}-Z_{0}|^{2}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2h}\int_{0}^{T}\sigma^{2}(T-t)e^{u(T)-u(t)}dt,

which combined with (
.10
) yields (
3.4
).

E. Proof of Theorem
3

Recall that
r_{b}(t)=\frac{1}{2}\beta(t)
,
\sigma(t)=\sqrt{\beta(t)}
and

p_{\tiny\mbox{noise}}(\cdot)\sim\mathcal{N}(0,(e^{\frac{T}{2}(\beta_{\max}+\beta_{\min})}-1)I)
.
By
[
20
, Proposition 10]
,
if
\log p_{\scalebox{0.7}{data}}(\cdot)
is
\kappa
-strongly log-concave,
then
\nabla\log p(T-t,\cdot)
is

\kappa\left(e^{\int_{0}^{T-t}\beta(s)ds}+\kappa\int_{0}^{T-t}e^{\int_{s}^{T-t}\beta(v)dv}\beta(s)ds\right)^{-1}
-strongly concave.
Thus, the term
\mathbb{E}((Y_{t}-Z_{t})\cdot(\nabla\log p(T-t,Y_{t})-\nabla\log p(T-t,Z_{t})))
in (
.13
) is bounded from above by

-\frac{\kappa}{e^{\int_{0}^{T-t}\beta(s)ds}+\kappa\int_{0}^{T-t}e^{\int_{s}^{T-t}\beta(v)dv}\beta(s)ds}\mathbb{E}|Y_{t}-Z_{t}|^{2},

instead of
L\mathbb{E}|Y_{t}-Z_{t}|^{2}
.
Consequently,
the bound (
3.4
) holds by replacing
u(t)
with

u_{\tiny\mbox{CVP}}(t):=\int_{T-t}^{T}\beta(s)\left(-1+2h-\frac{2\kappa}{e^{\int_{0}^{s}\beta(v)dv}+\kappa\int_{0}^{s}e^{\int_{v}^{s}\beta(u)du}\beta(v)dv}\right)ds.

(.15)

Note that

u_{\tiny\mbox{CVP}}(T)\leq-\int_{0}^{T}\beta(s)ds+2\beta_{\max}hT-\frac{2\kappa}{\kappa+1}\left(1-e^{-\beta_{\min}T}\right)

and

u_{\tiny\mbox{CVP}}(T)-u_{\tiny\mbox{CVP}}(T-t)\leq\beta_{\max}(2h-1)t
.
Moreover,

W_{2}^{2}(p(T,\cdot),p_{\tiny\mbox{noise}}(\cdot))\leq e^{\int_{0}^{T}\beta(s)ds}\mathbb{E}_{p_{\tiny\mbox{data}}(\cdot)}|x|^{2}
.
Combining (
3.4
) with the above estimates
yields (
3.7
).

F. Proof of Theorem
6

The analysis of the error
\left(\mathbb{E}|\overline{X}_{T}-\widehat{X}_{N}|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

relies on the following lemmas.
The lemma below proves the contraction of the backward SDE
\overline{X}
.

Lemma 10
.

Let
(\overline{X}^{x}_{t},\,0\leq t\leq T)

be defined by (
2.5
) with
\overline{X}^{x}_{0}=x
.
Let Assumptions
1
,
4
and
5
hold.
Then

\left(\mathbb{E}|\overline{X}^{x}_{t}-\overline{X}^{y}_{t}|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq\left(\mathbb{E}|x-y|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\exp(-2\beta t),\quad\mbox{for all }t,

(.16)

where
\overline{X}^{x}
and
\overline{X}^{y}
be coupled, i.e.
they are driven by the same Brownian motion with (different) initial values
x
and
y
respectively (
x
and

represent two random variables).

Proof.

Note that

d|\overline{X}^{x}_{s}-\overline{X}^{y}_{s}|^{2}=2\left(\overline{X}^{x}_{s}-\overline{X}^{y}_{s}\right)\cdot\bigg(-b(T-s,\overline{X}^{x}_{s})+\sigma^{2}(T-s)s_{\theta}(T-s,\overline{X}^{x}_{s})\\
+b(T-s,\overline{X}^{y}_{s})-\sigma^{2}(T-s)s_{\theta}(\overline{X}^{y}_{s})\bigg)ds.

Thus,

\displaystyle\frac{d}{ds}\mathbb{E}|\overline{X}^{x}_{s}-\overline{X}^{y}_{s}|^{2}=-2

\displaystyle\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\left[(\overline{X}^{x}_{s}-\overline{X}^{y}_{s})\cdot(b(T-s,\overline{X}^{x}_{s})-b(T-s,\overline{X}^{y}_{s}))\right]}_{(a)}

(.17)

\displaystyle+2\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\left[(\overline{X}^{x}_{s}-\overline{X}^{y}_{s})\sigma^{2}(T-s)(s_{\theta}(T-s,\overline{X}^{x}_{s})-s_{\theta}(T-s,\overline{X}^{y}_{s}))\right]}_{(b)}.

By Assumption
1
(1),
we get

(a)\geq r_{b}(T-s)\mathbb{E}|\overline{X}^{x}_{s}-\overline{X}^{y}_{s}|^{2}.

(.18)

By Assumption
4
(4), we obtain

(b)\leq L_{s}\sigma^{2}(T-s)\mathbb{E}|\overline{X}^{x}_{s}-\overline{X}^{y}_{s}|^{2}.

(.19)

Combining (
.17
), (
.18
)
and (
.19
) yields

\frac{d}{ds}\mathbb{E}|\overline{X}^{x}_{s}-\overline{X}^{y}_{s}|^{2}\leq-2(r_{b}(T-s)-L_{s}\sigma^{2}(T-s))\mathbb{E}|\overline{X}^{x}_{s}-\overline{X}^{y}_{s}|^{2}.

By Grönwall’s inequality, we have

\mathbb{E}|\overline{X}^{x}_{s}-\overline{X}^{y}_{s}|^{2}\leq\mathbb{E}|x-y|^{2}\exp\left(-2\int_{0}^{t}(r_{b}(T-s)-L_{s}\sigma^{2}(T-s))ds\right),

which, by the condition (
3.8
), yields (
.16
)
∎

Next we deal with the
local (one-step) discretization error
of the process
\overline{X}
.
Fixing
t_{\star}\leq T-\delta
,
the (one-step) discretization of
\overline{X}
starting at
\overline{X}_{t_{\star}}=x

is:

\displaystyle\widehat{X}^{t_{\star},x}_{1}=x+(-b(T-t_{\star},x)+\sigma^{2}(T-t_{\star})s_{\theta}(T-t_{\star},x))\delta+\sigma(T-t_{\star})(B_{t_{\star}+\delta}-B_{t_{\star}}).

(.20)

The following lemma provides an estimate of the local discretization error.

Lemma 11
.

Let
(\overline{X}_{t}^{t_{\star},x},\,t_{\star}\leq t\leq T)
be defined by (
2.5
) with
\overline{X}^{t_{\star},x}_{t_{\star}}=x
,
and
\widehat{X}^{t_{\star},x}_{1}
be given by (
.20
).
Let Assumption
4
hold.
Then for
\delta
sufficiently small (i.e.
\delta\leq\overline{\delta}
for some
\overline{\delta}<1
),
there exists
C_{1},C_{2}>0
independent of
\delta
and
x
such that

\left(\mathbb{E}|\overline{X}^{t_{\star},x}_{t_{\star}+\delta}-\widehat{X}^{t_{\star},x}_{1}|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq(C_{1}+C_{2}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}|x|^{2}})^{\frac{1}{2}}\delta^{\frac{3}{2}},

(.21)

|\mathbb{E}(\overline{X}^{t_{\star},x}_{t_{\star}+\delta}-\widehat{X}^{t_{\star},x}_{1})|\leq(C_{1}+C_{2}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}|x|^{2}})^{\frac{1}{2}}\delta^{\frac{3}{2}}.

(.22)

Proof.

For ease of presentation,
we write
\overline{X}_{t}
(resp.
\widehat{X}_{1}
)
for
\overline{X}^{t_{\star},x}_{t}
(resp.
\widehat{X}^{t_{\star},x}_{1}
).
Without loss of generality, set
t_{\star}=0
.
We have

\displaystyle\overline{X}_{\delta}=x+\int_{0}^{\delta}-b(T-t,\overline{X}_{t})+\sigma^{2}(T-t)s_{\theta}(T-t,\overline{X}_{t})dt+\int_{0}^{\delta}\sigma(T-t)dB_{t},

\displaystyle\widehat{X}_{1}=x+\int_{0}^{\delta}-b(T,x)+\sigma^{2}(T)s_{\theta}(T,x)dt+\int_{0}^{\delta}\sigma(T)dB_{t}.

So

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}|\overline{X}_{\delta}-\widehat{X}_{1}|^{2}

(.23)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigg|\int_{0}^{\delta}b(T,x)-b(T-t,\overline{X}_{t})dt+\int_{0}^{\delta}\sigma^{2}(T-t)s_{\theta}(T-t,\overline{X}_{t})-\sigma^{2}(T)s_{\theta}(T,x)dt

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{\delta}\sigma(T-t)-\sigma(T)dB_{t}\bigg|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 3\mathbb{E}\bigg(\left|\int_{0}^{\delta}b(T,x)-b(T-t,\overline{X}_{t})dt\right|^{2}+\left|\int_{0}^{\delta}\sigma^{2}(T-t)s_{\theta}(T-t,\overline{X}_{t})-\sigma^{2}(T)s_{\theta}(T,x)dt\right|^{2}

\displaystyle+\left|\int_{0}^{\delta}\sigma(T-t)-\sigma(T)dB_{t}\right|^{2}\bigg)

\displaystyle\leq 3\bigg(\delta\underbrace{\int_{0}^{\delta}\mathbb{E}|b(T,x)-b(T-t,\overline{X}_{t})|^{2}dt}_{(a)}+\delta\underbrace{\int_{0}^{\delta}\mathbb{E}|\sigma^{2}(T-t)s_{\theta}(T-t,\overline{X}_{t})-\sigma^{2}(T)s_{\theta}(T,x)|^{2}dt}_{(b)}

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\int_{0}^{\delta}|\sigma(T-t)-\sigma(T)|^{2}dt}_{(c)}\bigg),

where we use the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality and Itô’s isometry in the last inequality.
By Assumption
4
(1), we get

(c)\leq\int_{0}^{\delta}L_{\sigma}^{2}t^{2}dt=\frac{L_{\sigma}^{2}}{3}\delta^{3}.

(.24)

By Assumption
4
(3), we have

(a)\leq\int_{0}^{\delta}2L_{b}^{2}(t^{2}+\mathbb{E}|\overline{X}_{t}-x|^{2})dt=2L_{b}^{2}\left(\frac{\delta^{3}}{3}+\int_{0}^{\delta}\mathbb{E}|\overline{X}_{t}-x|^{2}dt\right).

According to
[
28
, Theorem 4.5.4]
,
we have
\mathbb{E}|\overline{X}_{t}-x|^{2}\leq C(1+\mathbb{E}|x|^{2})te^{Ct}

for some
C>0
(independent of
x
).
Consequently, for
t\leq\delta
sufficiently small (bounded by
\overline{\delta}<1
),

\mathbb{E}|\overline{X}_{t}-x|^{2}\leq C^{\prime}(1+\mathbb{E}|x|^{2})t,\quad\mbox{for some }C^{\prime}>0\mbox{ (independent of }\delta,x).

We then get

(a)\leq 2L_{b}^{2}\left(\frac{\delta^{3}}{3}+\frac{C^{\prime}(1+\mathbb{E}|x|^{2})}{2}\delta^{2}\right)\leq 2L_{b}^{2}\left(\frac{1}{3}+\frac{C^{\prime}}{2}+\frac{C^{\prime}}{2}\mathbb{E}|x|^{2}\right)\delta^{2}.

(.25)

Similarly, we obtain by Assumption
4
(1)(2)(4)(5):

(b)\leq C^{\prime\prime}(1+\mathbb{E}|x|^{2})\delta^{2},\quad\mbox{for some }C^{\prime\prime}>0\mbox{ (independent of }\delta,x).

(.26)

Combining (
.23
), (
.24
),
(
.25
) and (
.26
) yields
the estimate (
.21
).

Next we have

\displaystyle|\mathbb{E}(\overline{X}_{\delta}-\widehat{X}_{1})|

\displaystyle=\left|\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{\delta}b(T,x)-b(T-t,\overline{X}_{t})dt+\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{\delta}\sigma^{2}(T-t)s_{\theta}(T-t,\overline{X}_{t})-\sigma^{2}(T)s_{\theta}(T,x)dt\right|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{\delta}\mathbb{E}|b(T,x)-b(T-t,\overline{X}_{t})|dt+\int_{0}^{\delta}\mathbb{E}|\sigma^{2}(T-t)s_{\theta}(T-t,\overline{X}_{t})-\sigma^{2}(T)s_{\theta}(T,x)|dt

\displaystyle\leq C^{\prime\prime\prime}\int_{0}^{\delta}\left(t(1+\mathbb{E}|x|)+\mathbb{E}|\overline{X}_{t}-x|\right)dt

\displaystyle\leq C^{\prime\prime\prime\prime}(1+\sqrt{\mathbb{E}|x|^{2}})\delta^{\frac{3}{2}},\quad\mbox{for some }C^{\prime\prime\prime\prime}>0\mbox{ (independent of }\delta,x).

where the third inequality follows from Assumption

.22
).

Proof of Theorem
6
.

The proof is split into four steps.

Step 1
. Recall that
t_{k}=k\delta
for
k=0,\ldots,N
.
Denote
\overline{X}_{k}:=\overline{X}_{t_{k}}
,
and let

e_{k}:=\left(\mathbb{E}|\overline{X}_{k}-\widehat{X}_{k}|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

The idea is to build a recursion for the sequence
(e_{k})_{k=0,\ldots,N}
.
Also write
(\overline{X}^{t_{\star},x}_{t},\,t_{\star}\leq t\leq T)
to emphasize that
the reversed SDE (
2.5
) starts at
\overline{X}^{t_{\star},x}_{t_{\star}}=x
,
so
\overline{X}_{k+1}=\overline{X}^{t_{k},\overline{X}_{k}}_{t_{k+1}}
.
We have

\displaystyle e_{k+1}^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left|\overline{X}_{k+1}-\overline{X}_{t_{k+1}}^{t_{k},\widehat{X}_{k}}+\overline{X}_{t_{k+1}}^{t_{k},\widehat{X}_{k}}-\widehat{X}_{k+1}\right|^{2}

(.27)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\mathbb{E}|\overline{X}_{k+1}-\overline{X}_{t_{k+1}}^{t_{k},\widehat{X}_{k}}|^{2}}_{(a)}+\underbrace{\mathbb{E}|\overline{X}_{t_{k+1}}^{t_{k},\widehat{X}_{k}}-\widehat{X}_{k+1}|^{2}}_{(b)}+2\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\left[(\overline{X}_{k+1}-\overline{X}_{t_{k+1}}^{t_{k},\widehat{X}_{k}})(\overline{X}_{t_{k+1}}^{t_{k},\widehat{X}_{k}}-\widehat{X}_{k+1})\right]}_{(c)}.

Step 2
. We analyze the term (a) and (b).
By Lemma
10
(the contraction property),
we get

(a)=\mathbb{E}|\overline{X}_{t_{k+1}}^{t_{k},\overline{X}_{k}}-\overline{X}_{t_{k+1}}^{t_{k},\widehat{X}_{k}}|^{2}\leq e_{k}^{2}\exp(-2\beta\delta).

(.28)

By (
.21
) (in Lemma
11
), we have

(b)\leq\left(C_{1}+C_{2}\mathbb{E}|\widehat{X}_{k}|^{2}\right)\delta^{3}.

(.29)

Step 3
. We analyze the cross-product (c).
By splitting

\overline{X}_{k+1}-\overline{X}_{t_{k+1}}^{t_{k},\widehat{X}_{k}}=(\overline{X}_{k}-\widehat{X}_{k})+\underbrace{\left[(\overline{X}_{k+1}-\overline{X}_{k})-(\overline{X}_{t_{k+1}}^{t_{k},\widehat{X}_{k}}-\widehat{X}_{k})\right]}_{:=d_{\delta}(\overline{X}_{k},\widehat{X}_{k})},

we obtain

(c)=\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\left[(\overline{X}_{k}-\widehat{X}_{k})(\overline{X}_{t_{k+1}}^{t_{k},\widehat{X}_{k}}-\widehat{X}_{k+1})\right]}_{(d)}+\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\left[d_{\delta}(\overline{X}_{k},\widehat{X}_{k})(\overline{X}_{t_{k+1}}^{t_{k},\widehat{X}_{k}}-\widehat{X}_{k+1})\right]}_{(e)}.

(.30)

For the term (d), we have

\displaystyle(d)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[(\overline{X}_{k}-\widehat{X}_{k})\,\mathbb{E}(\overline{X}_{t_{k+1}}^{t_{k},\widehat{X}_{k}}-\widehat{X}_{k+1}|\mathcal{F}_{k})\right]

(.31)

\displaystyle\leq e_{k}\left(\mathbb{E}|\mathbb{E}(\overline{X}_{t_{k+1}}^{t_{k},\widehat{X}_{k}}-\widehat{X}_{k+1}|\mathcal{F}_{k})|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq e_{k}\left(C_{1}+C_{2}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}|\widehat{X}_{k}|^{2}}\right)\delta^{\frac{3}{2}},

where we use the tower property (of the conditional expectation) in the first equation, the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality in the second inequality, and (
.22
) in the final inequality.
According to
[
38
, Lemma 1.3]
, there exists
C_{0}>0
(independent of
\delta,\widehat{X}_{k}
) such that

\left(\mathbb{E}d^{2}_{\delta}(\overline{X}_{k},\widehat{X}_{k})\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq C_{0}e_{k}\sqrt{\delta}.

(.32)

Thus,

\displaystyle(e)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\mathbb{E}d^{2}_{\delta}(\overline{X}_{k},\widehat{X}_{k}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\mathbb{E}|\overline{X}_{t_{k+1}}^{t_{k},\widehat{X}_{k}}-\widehat{X}_{k+1}|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

(.33)

\displaystyle\leq C_{0}e_{k}\left(C_{1}+C_{2}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}|\widehat{X}_{k}|^{2}}\right)\delta^{2}.

where we use (
.21
) and (
.32
) in the last inequality.
Combining (
.30
), (
.31
) and (
.33
) yields

\delta

(c)\leq e_{k}\left(C^{\prime}_{1}+C^{\prime}_{2}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}|\widehat{X}_{k}|^{2}}\right)\delta^{\frac{3}{2}},\quad\mbox{for some }C^{\prime}_{1},C^{\prime}_{2}>0\mbox{ (independent of }\delta,\widehat{X}_{k}).

(.34)

Step 4
. Combining (
.27
) with (
.28
), (
.29
) and (
.34
) yields

10
(the contraction property) implies

\mathbb{E}|\overline{X}_{t}|^{2}\leq C

.
