Extensions of the Kahn–Saks inequality for posets of width two
Swee Hong Chan, Igor Pak, Greta Panova

Abstract
. The Kahn–Saks inequality is a classical result on the number of linear extensions of finite posets. We give a new proof of this inequality for posets of width two and both elements in the same chain using explicit injections of lattice paths. As a consequence we obtain a q q -analogue, a multivariate generalization and an equality condition in this case. We also discuss the equality conditions of the Kahn–Saks inequality for general posets and prove several implications between conditions conjectured to be equivalent.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Foreword

The study of linear extensions of finite posets is surprisingly rich as they
generalize permutations, combinations, standard Young tableaux, etc. By contrast,
the inequalities for the numbers of linear extensions are quite rare and difficult
to prove as they have to hold
for all
posets. Posets of width two serve
a useful middle ground as on the one hand there are sufficiently many of them
to retain the diversity of posets, and on the other hand they can be analyzed
by direct combinatorial tools.

In this paper, we study two classical results in the area:
the
Stanley inequality
(1981), and its generalization,
the
Kahn–Saks inequality
(1984).
Both inequalities were proved using the geometric
Alexandrov–Fenchel inequalities

and remain largely mysterious. Despite much effort, no combinatorial proof of these
inequalities has been found.

We give a new, fully combinatorial, proof of the Kahn–Saks inequality for posets of width two and both elements in the same chain.
In this case, linear extensions are in bijection with certain lattice paths, and we
prove the inequality by explicit injections. This is the approach first pioneered
in
[
CFG80
,
GYY80
]
and more recently extended by the authors in
[
CPP21a
]
.
In fact, Chung, Fishburn and Graham
[
CFG80
]
proved Stanley’s inequality
for width two posets and their conjecture paved a way to Stanley’s paper
[
Sta81
]
.
The details of our approach are somewhat different, but we do recover the

Chung–Fishburn–Graham
(CFG)
injection
as a special case. The construction in this
paper is quite a bit more technical and is heavily based on ideas in our previous
paper
[
CPP21a
]
, where we established the
cross-product conjecture

in the special case of width two posets.

Now, our approach allows us to obtain
q
-analogues
of both inequalities in the style
of the
q
-cross-product inequality
in
[
CPP21a
]
. More importantly, it is also
robust enough to imply
conditions for equality
of the Kahn–Saks inequalities for
the case of posets of width two and both elements in the same chain.
The corresponding result for the Stanley inequality
in the generality of all posets was obtained by Shenfeld and van Handel
[
SvH20+
]

using technology of geometric inequalities. Most recently, a completely different
proof was obtained by the first two authors
[
CP21
]
.
Although the equality condition in the special case of
the Kahn–Saks inequality is the main result of paper,
we start with a special case of the Stanley inequality as a stepping stone
to our main results.

1.2.
Two main inequalities

Let
P=(X,\prec)
be a finite poset.
A
linear extension
of
P
is a bijection
L:X\to[n]
, such that

L(x)<L(y)
for all
x\prec y
.
Denote by
\Ec(P)
the set of linear extensions of
P
,
and write
e(P):=|\Ec(P)|
. The following are two key results in the area:

Theorem 1.1
(
Stanley inequality

[
Sta81
, Thm 3.1]
)
.

Let
P=(X,\prec)
be a finite poset, and let
x\in X
.
Denote by
\textrm{N}(k)
the number of linear extensions
L\in\Ec(P)
,
such that
L(x)=k
. Then:

(1.1)

\textrm{N}(k)^{2}\,\hskip 1.70709pt\geq\,\hskip 1.70709pt\textrm{N}(k-1)\,\hskip 1.70709pt\textrm{N}(k+1)\quad\text{for all}\quad k\hskip 1.70709pt>\hskip 1.70709pt1\hskip 0.85355pt.

In other words, the distribution of value of linear extensions on
x
is
log-concave
.

Theorem 1.2
(
Kahn–Saks inequality

[
KS84
, Thm 2.5]
)
.

Let
x,y\in X
be distinct elements of a finite poset
P=(X,\prec)
.
Denote by
\textrm{F}(k)
the number of linear extensions
L\in\Ec(P)
,
such that
L(y)-L(x)=k
. Then:

(1.2)

\textrm{F}(k)^{2}\,\hskip 1.70709pt\geq\,\hskip 1.70709pt\textrm{F}(k-1)\,\hskip 1.70709pt\textrm{F}(k+1)\quad\text{for all}\quad k\hskip 1.70709pt>\hskip 1.70709pt1\hskip 0.85355pt.

Note that the Stanley inequality follows from the Kahn–Saks inequality by
adding the maximal element
\hskip 0.85355pt\widehat{1}
to the poset
P
, and
letting
y\leftarrow\widehat{1}
.

1.3.
The
q
-analogues

From this point on, we consider only posets
P
of
width two
. Fix a
partition of

\mathcal{C}_{1},\mathcal{C}_{2}\subset X
,

\mathcal{C}_{1}\cap\mathcal{C}_{2}=\varnothing

\mathcal{C}_{2}=\{\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{\emph{\textsf{b}}}\}

The

\wgt(L)\ :=\ \sum_{i=1}^{\emph{\textsf{a}}}\,L(\alpha_{i})\hskip 1.70709pt.

Note that the definition of the weight

Theorem 1.3
(

Let

Define

\textrm{N}_{q}(k)\,:=\,\sum_{L\in\Ec(P)\ :\ L(x)=k}\,q^{\wgt(L)}\hskip 1.70709pt.

Then:

(1.4)

\textrm{N}_{q}(k)^{2}\ \geqslant\ \textrm{N}_{q}(k-1)\,\textrm{N}_{q}(k+1)\quad\ \text{ for all}\ \quad k\hskip 1.70709pt>\hskip 1.70709pt1\hskip 0.85355pt,

where the inequality between polynomials in

The following result is a generalization, sice we can always assume that element

Theorem 1.4
(

Let

Suppose that either

\textrm{F}_{q}(k)^{2}\,\geqslant\,\textrm{F}_{q}(k-1)\hskip 1.70709pt\textrm{F}_{q}(k+1)\quad\ \text{for all}\ \quad k\hskip 1.70709pt>\hskip 1.70709pt1\hskip 0.85355pt,

In Section

We conclude:

\displaystyle\textrm{F}_{q}(2)^{2}\,-\,\textrm{F}_{q}(1)\,\textrm{F}_{q}(3)\ =\ q^{26}\hskip 1.70709pt-\hskip 1.70709ptq^{25}\ \not\geqslant\ 0\hskip 0.85355pt.

1.5.
Proof discussion

As we mentioned above, we start by
translating the problem into a natural question about directed lattice paths in a row/column
convex region in the grid (cf.
\lx@sectionsign
9.4
).
From this point on, we do not work with posets and the proof
becomes purely combinatorial enumeration of lattice paths.

While the geometric proofs in
[
KS84
,
Sta81
]
are quite powerful, the equality cases
of the Alexandrov–Fenchel inequality are yet to be fully understood. So proving the
equality conditions of poset inequalities is quite challenging, see
[
SvH20+
,
CP21
]

and
\lx@sectionsign
9.1
. This is why our direct combinatorial approach is so useful,
as the explicit injection becomes a bijection in the case of equality.

In the case of Stanley’s inequality the CFG injection is quite simple and elegant,
leading to a quick proof of the equality condition. For the Kahn–Saks inequality,
the direct injection is a large composition of smaller injections, each of which
is simple and either generalizes the CFG injection or of a different flavor, all
influenced by the noncrossing paths in the
Lindström–-Gessel–-Viennot

lemma

[
GV89
]
(see also
[
GJ83
,
\lx@sectionsign
5.4]
).
Consequently, the equality condition of the Kahn–Saks
inequality is substantially harder to obtain as one has to put together the
equalities for each component of the proof and do a careful case analysis.

In summary, our proof of the main result (Theorem
1.7
) is like
an elaborate but delicious dish: the individual ingredients are elegant and natural,
but the instruction on how they are put together is so involved the resulting
recipe may seem difficult and unapproachable.

3.1.
Criss-cross inequalities

Here we consider inequalities between sums of pairs of paths.

Lemma 3.1
(
Criss-cross lemma
)
.

Let
A,A^{\prime},B^{\prime},B\in\Reg(P)
be on the same vertical line, with

the highest and
B

In addition, let

be on another vertical line, with

the highest and
D
the lowest
points, and such that
\overrightarrow{CC^{\prime}}=-\overrightarrow{DD^{\prime}}=\overrightarrow{AA^{\prime}}=-\overrightarrow{BB^{\prime}}
.
Finally, let
\overrightarrow{AB}=\overrightarrow{CD}
. Then we have:

(3.1)

\begin{split}&\textrm{K}_{q}(A,C)\,\cdot\,\textrm{K}_{q}(B,D)\ +\ \textrm{K}_{q}(A^{\prime},C^{\prime})\,\cdot\,\textrm{K}_{q}(B^{\prime},D^{\prime})\\
&\qquad\geqslant\ \textrm{K}_{q}(A^{\prime},C)\,\cdot\,\textrm{K}_{q}(B^{\prime},D)\ +\ \textrm{K}_{q}(A,C^{\prime})\,\cdot\,\textrm{K}_{q}(B,D^{\prime}).\end{split}

Proof.

The idea is to consider the pairs of paths counted on each side, and show that each pair (after the necessary transformation) is counted less times on the RHS than on the LHS, where the number of times it could appear on each side is
0,1,2
.

To be precise, given two points

and
F
in
\Reg(P)
between the lines
AB
and
CD
, and paths
(\pi,\rho)
with endpoints
E
and
F

Here we have

\gamma\cap\gamma^{\prime}=\{E\}
and
\delta\cap\delta^{\prime}=\{F\}

to

as above, paths

to

\displaystyle\zeta_{1}\ :=\ \gamma\circ\pi\circ\delta,\qquad\zeta_{1}:A\to C\hskip 0.85355pt,\quad

\displaystyle\zeta_{2}\ :=\ \gamma^{\prime}\circ\pi\circ\delta^{\prime},\qquad\zeta_{2}:A^{\prime}\to C^{\prime}\hskip 0.85355pt,\quad

\displaystyle\eta_{2}\ :=\ (\gamma\circ\rho\circ\delta)+\vb,\qquad\eta_{2}:B^{\prime}\to D^{\prime}\hskip 0.85355pt,

\displaystyle\zeta_{3}\ :=\ \gamma\circ\pi\circ\delta^{\prime},\qquad\zeta_{3}:A\to C^{\prime}\hskip 0.85355pt,\quad

\displaystyle\eta_{3}\ :=\ (\gamma^{\prime}\circ\rho\circ\delta)+\vb,\qquad\eta_{3}:B\to D^{\prime}\hskip 0.85355pt,

\displaystyle\zeta_{4}\ :=\ \gamma^{\prime}\circ\pi\circ\delta,\qquad\zeta_{4}:A^{\prime}\to C\hskip 0.85355pt,\quad

7.
Multivariate generalization

The
q
-weights in the introduction can be refined as follows.
Let
\mathbf{q}:=(q_{1},\ldots,q_{\emph{\textsf{a}}})
be formal variables.
Define the
multivariate weight
of a linear extension
L\in\Ec(P)
as

\mathbf{q}^{L}\,:=\,\prod_{i=1}^{\emph{\textsf{a}}}\,q_{i}^{L(\alpha_{i})\hskip 0.85355pt-\hskip 0.85355ptL(\alpha_{i-1})},

where we set
L(\alpha_{0}):=0
. In the language of lattice paths we see that the
power of
q_{i}
is equal to one plus the number of vertical steps on the
vertical line passing through
(i-1,0)
.

Theorem 7.1
(
Multivariate Stanley inequality
)
.

Let
P=(X,\prec)
be a finite poset of width two,
let
(\mathcal{C}_{1},\mathcal{C}_{2})
be the chain partition of
P
, and let
x\in\mathcal{C}_{1}
.
Define

\textrm{N}_{\mathbf{q}}(k)\,:=\,\sum_{L\in\Ec(P)\ :\ L(x)=k}\,\mathbf{q}^{L}\hskip 1.70709pt.

Then:

(7.1)

\textrm{N}_{\mathbf{q}}(k)^{2}\ \geqslant\ \textrm{N}_{\mathbf{q}}(k-1)\,\textrm{N}_{\mathbf{q}}(k+1)\quad\ \text{ for all}\ \quad k\hskip 1.70709pt>\hskip 1.70709pt1\hskip 0.85355pt,

where the inequality between polynomials in the variables
\mathbf{q}=(q_{1},\ldots,q_{\emph{\textsf{a}}})
is coefficient-wise.

When
q_{1}=q_{2}=\ldots=q
, we obtain Theorem
1.3
.
Similarly, the following result generalizes both Theorem
1.4
and Theorem
7.1
.

Theorem 7.2
(
Multivariate Kahn–Saks inequality
)
.

Let
P=(X,\prec)
be a finite poset of width two,
let
(\mathcal{C}_{1},\mathcal{C}_{2})
be the chain partition of
P
, and let

x,y\in\mathcal{C}_{1}
be two distinct elements. Define:

\textrm{F}_{\mathbf{q}}(k)\ :=\ \sum_{L\in\Ec(P)\ :\ L(y)-L(x)=k}\,\mathbf{q}^{L}\hskip 1.70709pt.

Then:

(7.2)

\textrm{F}_{\mathbf{q}}(k)^{2}\,\geqslant\,\textrm{F}_{\mathbf{q}}(k-1)\hskip 1.70709pt\textrm{F}_{\mathbf{q}}(k+1)\quad\ \text{for all}\ \quad k\hskip 1.70709pt>\hskip 1.70709pt1\hskip 0.85355pt,

where the inequality between polynomials in the variables
\mathbf{q}=(q_{1},\ldots,q_{\emph{\textsf{a}}})
is coefficient-wise.

For the proof, note that in the case
x,y\in\mathcal{C}_{1}
,
the lattice paths lemmas in Subsections
2.3
and
3.1

rearrange and reassign pieces of paths via vertical translation. Thus, we preserve the total
number of vertical segments above each
(i,0)
in each pair of paths.
Therefore, the resulting injections preserve the multivariate weight
\mathbf{q}^{L}
, and
both theorems follow. We omit the details.

Remark 7.3
.

Note that, in general, this function is not quasi-symmetric in
q_{1},q_{2},\ldots
, much less symmetric.
This generalization is different from the
quasisymmetric functions
associated to
P
-partitions,
see e.g.
[
Sta81
, §7.19]
. Still, the multivariate polynomials in the theorems can be
expressed in terms of the (usual)
symmetric functions
in certain cases.

For example, let
P
be the parallel product of two chains
\mathcal{C}_{1}
and
\mathcal{C}_{2}
of sizes
a
and
b
, respectively. Clearly,
e(P)=\binom{\emph{\textsf{a}}+\emph{\textsf{b}}}{\emph{\textsf{a}}}
in this case. Fix
x=\alpha_{s}
and
y=\alpha_{r+s}
.
Then we have:

\textrm{F}_{\mathbf{q}}(k)\,=\,\sum_{j}\,h_{j}(q_{1},\ldots,q_{s})\,h_{k-r}(q_{s+1},\ldots,q_{s+r})\,h_{\emph{\textsf{b}}-k+r-j}(q_{r+s+1},\ldots,q_{\emph{\textsf{a}}}),

where
h_{i}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})
is the
homogeneous symmetric function
of degree
i
, see e.g.
[
Sta81
, §7.5]
.
Similarly, from Section
5
, we have:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\hskip 1.70709ptS(u;u+1)

\displaystyle=\,h_{u}(q_{1},\ldots,q_{s})\,h_{u+1}(q_{1},\ldots,q_{s})\,h_{k-1-r-u}(q_{s+r+1},\ldots,q_{\emph{\textsf{a}}})\,\times

\displaystyle\times\,h_{k-r-u}(q_{s+r+1},\ldots,q_{\emph{\textsf{a}}})\,s_{(k-r)^{2}}(q_{s+1},\ldots,q_{s+r}).

The
\Delta
terms involved in the other
S(u;w)
can be similarly expressed in terms of

Schur functions

s_{\lambda}
as in the formula above.
We leave the details to the reader.

8.2.
Vanishing conditions in the Kahn–Saks inequality

The following result is a natural generalization of Proposition
8.3
.

Theorem 8.5
.

Let
P=(X,\prec)
and let
x\prec y
, where
x,y\in X
.
Denote

h(x,y)\ :=\ \big|\{u\in X\hskip 1.70709pt{}:\hskip 1.70709pt{}x\prec u\prec y\}\big|\hskip 0.85355pt.

Then
\textrm{F}(k)>0
if and only if

h(x,y)\,<\,k\,<\,n\hskip 0.85355pt-\hskip 0.85355ptf(x)\hskip 0.85355pt-\hskip 0.85355ptg(y).

Proof.

For the
“only if”
direction, let
L\in\Ec(P)
be a linear extension such that

L(y)-L(x)=k
. By definition, we have
f(x)\leq L(x)-1
and

g(y)\leq n-L(y)
, which implies

f(x)+g(y)\,\leq\,L(x)-1+n-L(y)\,=\,n-k-1.

Furthermore, condition
L(y)-L(x)=k
implies
that
h(x,y)\leq k-1
, as desired.

For the
“if”
direction, let
c:=\min\{n-g(x),n-k-g(y)\}
.
Note that
g(x)\leq n-c
and
g(y)\leq n-c-k
.
We also have
f(x)\leq n-g(x)-1
by definition of upper and lower ideals,
and
f(x)\leq n-k-g(y)-1
by assumption.
Combining these two inequalities, we get
f(x)\leq c-1
.

Since
f(x)\leq c-1
and
g(x)\leq n-c
, by Proposition
8.3
,
there is a linear extension
L\in\Ec(P)
such that
L(x)=c
.
We are done if
L(y)=c+k
, so suppose that
L(y)\neq c+k
.
We split the proof into two cases.

(1)
Suppose that
L(y)<c+k
.
Since
g(y)\leq\,n-c-k
,
there exists
w\in X
such that
w\hskip 1.70709pt||\hskip 1.70709pty
and
L(w)>L(y)
.
Let
w
be such an element for which
L(w)
is minimal, let
a:=L(y)
and
b:=L(w)
.
The minimality assumption implies that every
u\in\{L^{-1}(a),\ldots,L^{-1}(b-1)\}
satisfies
u\succ y
,
which gives
u\hskip 1.70709pt||\hskip 1.70709ptw
.

Define a new linear extension
L^{\prime}\in\Ec(P)
, obtained from
L
by setting

L^{\prime}(w):=L(y),\quad L^{\prime}(y):=L(y)+1,\quad L^{\prime}(u)=L(u)+1\ \ \text{for all}\ \ u\in X\ \ \text{s.t.}\ \ a\leq L(u)\leq b-1,

and setting
L^{\prime}(v):=L(v)
for all other elements
v\in X
. Note that
L^{\prime}(x)=L(x)
by definition.

Denote by
\Phi:L\to L^{\prime}
the resulting map on
\Ec(P)
. From above,
\Phi
increases the difference

L(y)-L(x)
by one when defined. Iterate
\Phi
until we obtain a
linear extension
L^{\diamond}
that satisfies
L^{\diamond}(y)-L^{\diamond}(x)=(c+k)-c=k
, as desired.

(2)
Suppose that
L(y)>c+k
. This implies that
L(y)-L(x)>k
. Proceed analogously to
(1)
.
Since
h(x,y)<k
, there exists
w\in X
such that
L(x)<L(w)<L(y)
,
and either
w\hskip 1.70709pt{}||\hskip 1.70709pt{}x
or
w\hskip 1.70709pt{}||\hskip 1.70709pt{}y
. Assume that
w\hskip 1.70709pt||\hskip 1.70709ptx
, and let
w
be such an element for which
L(w)
is minimal.
Let
a:=L(x)
and
b:=L(w)
.
This minimality assumption implies that every

u\succ x
,
which gives
u\hskip 1.70709pt||\hskip 1.70709ptw
.

Define
L^{\prime}\in\Ec(P)
, obtained from

L^{\prime}(w):=L(x),\quad L^{\prime}(x):=L(x)+1,\quad L^{\prime}(u)=L(u)+1\ \ \text{for all}\ \ u\in X\ \ \text{s.t.}\ \ a\leq L(u)\leq b-1,

and setting
L^{\prime}(v):=L(v)
for all other elements
v\in X
. Note that
L^{\prime}(y)=L(y)
by definition.

Denote by
\Psi:L\to L^{\prime}
the resulting map on
\Ec(P)

The case

8.3.
Complete equality conditions in the Kahn–Saks inequality

As we discuss in the introduction, the equivalence (a)
\Rightarrow
(b)
in Theorem
1.7
does not extend to general posets.
However, the condition (b) which states
\textrm{F}(k)\hskip 0.85355pt=\hskip 0.85355pt\textrm{F}(k+1)\hskip 0.85355pt=\hskip 0.85355pt\textrm{F}(k-1)
is of independent interest and perhaps can be completely characterized.
Below we give some partial results in this direction.

First, observe that the equality condition (c) in
Theorem
8.1
is remarkably clean
when compared to our condition (e) in Theorem
1.7
.
This suggests the following natural generalization.

Let
P=(X,\prec)
and let
x,y\in X
.
We write
h(x,y):=|\{u\in X\hskip 1.70709pt{}:\hskip 1.70709pt{}x\prec u\prec y\}|
.
We say that
(x,y)
satisfies
k
-midway property
,
if

\circ

f(z)+g(y)\hskip 0.85355pt>\hskip 0.85355ptn-k
for every
z\in X
such that
x\prec z
and
z\not\succ y
,

\circ

h(z,y)\hskip 0.85355pt>\hskip 0.85355ptk
for every
z\prec x
,  and
f(y)\hskip 0.85355pt>\hskip 0.85355ptk
.

Note that the last condition
f(y)>k
is equivalent to
h(z,y)>k
for
z=\widehat{0}
, i.e. can be dropped when the element
\widehat{0}
is added to
P
.

Similarly, we say that
(x,y)
satisfies
dual
k
-midway property
, if:

\circ

g(z)+f(x)\hskip 0.85355pt>\hskip 0.85355ptn-k
for every
z\in X
such that
z\prec y
and
z\not\prec x
,

\circ

h(x,z)\hskip 0.85355pt>\hskip 0.85355ptk
for every
z\succ y
,  and
g(z)>k
.

By definition, pair
(x,y)
satisfies the
k
-midway property in the poset
P=(X,\prec)
,
if and only if pair
(y,x)
satisfies the dual
k
-midway property in the
dual poset

P^{\ast}=(X,\prec^{\ast})
,
obtained by reversing the partial order:
u\prec v

\Leftrightarrow

v\prec^{\ast}u
,
for all
u,v\in X
.

Conjecture 8.7
(
Complete equality condition for the Kahn–Saks inequality
).

Let
x,y\in X
be distinct elements of a finite poset
P=(X,\prec)
.
Denote by
\textrm{F}(k)
the number of linear extensions
L\in\Ec(P)
,
such that
L(y)-L(x)=k
. Suppose that
k\in\{2,\ldots,n-2\}
and
\textrm{F}(k)>0
. Then the following are equivalent:

(a)

\textrm{F}(k)\hskip 1.70709pt=\hskip 1.70709pt\textrm{F}(k+1)\hskip 1.70709pt=\hskip 1.70709pt\textrm{F}(k-1)
,

(b)

there is an element
z\in\{x,y\}
, such that
for every
L\in\Ec(P)
for which
L(y)-L(x)=k
,

there are elements
u,v\in X
which satisfy
u\hskip 1.70709pt||\hskip 1.70709ptz
,
v\hskip 1.70709pt||\hskip 1.70709ptz
, and
L(u)+1=L(z)=L(v)-1
,

(c)

the pair
(x,y)
satisfies either the
k
-midway or the dual
k
-midway property.

Below we prove three implications, which reduce the conjecture to the implications  (a)
\Rightarrow
(c).

Proposition 8.8
.

In the notation of Conjecture
8.7
, we have
(b)
\Rightarrow
(a).

Proof.

What follows is a variation on the argument in
\lx@sectionsign
6.1
.
Without loss of generality, assume that
z=x
. Denote
\mathcal{F}(i):=\{L\in\Ec(P)~:~L(y)-L(x)=i\}
,
so that
\textrm{F}(i)=|\mathcal{F}(i)|
.
Condition (b) implies that there is an injection
\mathcal{F}(k)\to\mathcal{F}(k+1)
given by relabeling
x\leftrightarrow u
,
so that
L(y)-L(x)=k+1
and
L(u)=L(x)+1
. Thus,
\textrm{F}(k)\leq\textrm{F}(k+1)
. Similarly, we obtain

\textrm{F}(k)\leq\textrm{F}(k-1)
by relabeling
x\leftrightarrow v
. However, by the Kahn–Saks
inequality (Theorem
1.2
), we have
\textrm{F}(k)^{2}\hskip 0.85355pt\geq\hskip 0.85355pt\textrm{F}(k-1)\hskip 1.70709pt\textrm{F}(k+1)
, implying
that all inequalities are in fact equalities.
∎

Theorem 8.9
.

In the notation of Conjecture
8.7
, we have
(b)
\Leftrightarrow
(c).

In other words, condition (b) in Conjecture
8.7
, which is the same as
condition (e) in Theorem
1.7
, can be viewed as a stepping stone towards
the structural condition (c) in the conjecture. We omit it from the introduction
for the sake of clarity.

Proof of Theorem
8.9
.

For  (c)
\Rightarrow
(b),
let
(x,y)
be a pair of elements which satisfies the
k
-midway property.
We prove (b) by setting
z\leftarrow x
.
Let
L\in\Ec(P)
such that
L(y)-L(x)=k
.
Note that
L(x)>1

as otherwise
L(y)=k+1
, which contradicts the assumption that
f(y)>k
.
Let
u\in X
be such that
L(u)=L(x)-1
.
Suppose to the contrary that
u\prec x
.
It then follows from
k
-midway property that
h(u,y)>k
.
On the other hand, since
L(u)=L(x)-1=L(y)-k-1
, we have
h(u,y)\leq k
, and gives us the desired contradiction.

Now, let
v\in X
be such that
L(v)=L(x)+1
.
We will again show that
v\hskip 1.70709pt||\hskip 1.70709ptx
.
Suppose to the contrary that
v\succ x
.
Note that

It then follows from

On the other hand, since

, which contradicts the fact that

.

Thus, the pair of elements

are as in (b), as desired.
The case when
(x,y)
satisfies the dual

z\leftarrow y
.

For  (b)
\Rightarrow

z=x
.
Now let

be such that
w\succ x
and

.
The proof is based on the following

Claim:

There exists a linear extension

, such that

Proof of Claim.
Since
\textrm{F}(k)>0

L\in\mathcal{F}(k)
, i.e. such that
L(y)-L(x)=k
.
The claim follows if
L(y)=n-g(y)
and

.
So suppose that
L(y)<n-g(y)
.

Then there exists
p\in X
such that
p\hskip 1.70709pt||\hskip 1.70709pty

,
and let
p
be such an element for which

and

.
This minimality assumption implies that every

,
which implies
q\hskip 1.70709pt||\hskip 1.70709ptp
.

Now, by (b) there exists

v\hskip 1.70709pt||\hskip 1.70709ptx

.
Define

by setting

\displaystyle L^{\prime}(p):=L(y),\quad L^{\prime}(y):=L(y)+1,\quad L^{\prime}(x):=L(x)+1,\quad L^{\prime}(v):=L(x),

.
Note that
L^{\prime}(y)-L^{\prime}(x)=k

Denote by

the resulting map on

\Omega

by one when defined. Iterate

.

We will now show that we can modify the current

to additionally satisfy

.
We are done if this is already the case, and since

9.
Final remarks and open problems

9.1.

Finding the equality conditions is an important problem for inequalities
across mathematics, see e.g.
[
BB65
]
, and throughout the sciences,
see e.g.
[
Dahl96
]
. Notably, for geometric inequalities,
such as the
isoperimetric inequalities
, these problems are classical
(see e.g.
[
BZ88
]
), and in many cases the equality conditions are equally
important and are substantially harder to prove. For example, in the

Brunn–Minkowski inequality
, the equality conditions
are crucially used in the proof of the
Minkowski theorem

on existence of a polytope with given normals
and facet volumes (see e.g.
\lx@sectionsign
7.7,
\lx@sectionsign
36.1 and
\lx@sectionsign
41.6 in
[
Pak09
]
).

For poset inequalities, the equality conditions have also been studied, see
e.g. an overview in
[
Win86
]
. In fact, Stanley’s original paper
[
Sta81
]

raises several versions of this question. In recent years, there were a number of key
advances on combinatorial inequalities using algebraic and analytic tools, see e.g.
[
CP21
,
Huh18
]
,
but the corresponding equality conditions are understood in only very few instances.

9.4.

The bijection in Lemma
2.1
, see also
Remark
2.2
, is natural
from both order theory and enumerative combinatorics points of view.
Indeed, the order ideals of a width two poset
P
with fixed chain partitions

(\mathcal{C}_{1},\mathcal{C}_{2})
are in natural bijection with lattice points in a
region
\Reg(P)\subset\mathbb{Z}^{2}
.
Now the
fundamental theorem for finite distributive lattices

(see e.g.
[
Sta99
, Thm 3.4.1]
), gives the same bijection between
\Ec(P)
and lattice paths
\zero\to(\emph{\textsf{a}},\emph{\textsf{b}})
in
\Reg(P)
.

9.8.

There is an indirect way to derive both Corollaries
8.4

and
8.6
without explicit combinatorial conditions for
vanishing of
\textrm{N}(k)
and
\textrm{F}(k)
, given in
Proposition
8.3
and Theorem
8.5
, respectively.
In fact, the vanishing problem is in
P
by the following general result:

Theorem 9.1
.

Let
P=(X,\prec)
be a finite poset with
|X|=n
elements, let
x_{1}\ldots,x_{k}\in X
be distinct poset elements, and let

a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
be distinct integers.
Finally, let
\textrm{N}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k})
be the number of linear extensions
L\in\Ec(P)
such that
L(x_{i})=a_{i}
for all
1\leq i\leq i
.
Then, deciding whether
\textrm{N}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k})=0
can be done
in
poly
(n)
time.

Proof.

It was shown by Stanley
[
Sta81
, Thm 3.2]
, that
\textrm{N}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k})/(n-k)!
is equal to the
mixed volume
of certain polytopes
\hskip 0.85355ptK_{i}
given by
explicit combinatorial inequalities. In the terminology of
[
DGH98
, p. 364]
, these
polytopes
\hskip 0.85355ptK_{i}
are
well-presented
, so by
[
DGH98
, Thm 8]

the vanishing of the mixed volume can be decided in polynomial time.
∎

To see the connection between the theorem and condition
\textrm{F}(k)=0
, note
that for every fixed
x,y\in X
, we have
\textrm{F}(k)=\textrm{N}(1,k+1)+\ldots+\textrm{N}(n-k,n)
.
In the same way, the vanishing
\textrm{F}(k,\ell)=0
in the cross–product inequality
(see
[
CPP21a
]
) can also be decided in polynomial time.

Note that the proof in
[
DGH98
, Thm 8]
involves a classical
but technically involved
matroid intersection algorithm
by Edmonds (1970).
Thus, finding an explicit combinatorial condition for vanishing
of
\textrm{N}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k})
is of independent interest.
