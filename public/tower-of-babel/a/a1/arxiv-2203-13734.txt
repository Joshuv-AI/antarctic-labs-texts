Fractional Integrable Nonlinear Soliton Equations
Mark J. Ablowitz, Joel B. Been, Lincoln D. Carr

Abstract
Nonlinear integrable equations serve as a foundation for nonlinear dynamics, and fractional equations are well known in anomalous diffusion. We connect these two fields by presenting the discovery of a new class of integrable fractional nonlinear evolution equations describing dispersive transport in fractional media. These equations can be constructed from nonlinear integrable equations using a widely generalizable mathematical process utilizing completeness relations, dispersion relations, and inverse scattering transform techniques. As examples, this general method is used to characterize fractional extensions to two physically relevant, pervasive integrable nonlinear equations: the Korteweg–deVries and nonlinear Schrödinger equations. These equations are shown to predict super-dispersive transport of non-dissipative solitons in fractional media.

In this Letter, we present a new class of integrable

Fractional media is “rough” or multiscale media that is neither regular nor random; it includes fractals but is more general as it need not be self-similar. We use the fractional NLS (fNLS) and fractional KdV (fKdV) equations as case studies. We show their integrability, demonstrate exact fractional soliton solutions, and make physical predictions about the speed of these localized waves. To date, to our knowledge, no nonlinear fractional evolution equation has been known to be integrable.

The building blocks of our demonstration are three mathematical ingredients. Two are familiar to physicists as they are well known concepts in physics. They are completeness and the dispersion relations. However, in our case the dispersion relation will use fractional, rather than integer, derivatives. The third building block is the fundamental ingredient of integrability, namely the inverse scattering transform (IST), well known to researchers in nonlinear dynamics.

The IST and anomalous dispersion relations
—
It is well known that linear evolution equations for

Physical Predictions
– The fKdV and the fNLS equations describe the transport of fluid and photons in multiscale fluid channels and laser fiberoptic systems, respectively. The multiscale characteristic of these materials represents a certain “roughness” which is averaged over in fKdV and fNLS. The solitonic solutions of these equations describe how localized waves of fluid/probability are transported in such systems.
Both fKdV and fNLS predict solitons with anomalous motion, that is, super-dispersive transport where speeds are larger than expected from regular or ordered systems (note that sub-dispersive transport can also be realized by modifying the dispersion relation). Specifically, the group velocity of fKdV and fNLS and the phase velocity of fNLS are

\displaystyle v_{K}(\epsilon,\kappa)

\displaystyle=\left(4\kappa^{2}\right)^{1+\epsilon}

(22)

\displaystyle v_{S}(\xi,\eta)

\displaystyle=2^{2+\epsilon}\xi(\xi^{2}+\eta^{2})^{\epsilon/2}

(23)

\displaystyle v_{\theta}(\xi,\eta)

\displaystyle=2^{1+\epsilon}\left(\xi^{2}-\eta^{2}\right)\left(\xi^{2}+\eta^{2}\right)^{\epsilon/2}/\xi

(24)

In a wave tank of height
5

Importantly, the physical properties of fractional solitons, besides the change in velocity described by equations (

An open question is to compare the solitons predicted by fKdV and fNLS to solitary waves predicted by other, non-integrable versions of these equations. This could be done by studying how the velocity of each equation varies with the fractional parameter
\epsilon

Conclusion
— We have demonstrated a new class of integrable equations, namely 1D fractional integrable nonlinear evolution equations, derivable from a general method. As ubiquitous examples of this class we presented integrability and solitonic solutions of the fractional nonlinear Schrödinger and Korteweg-deVries equations. We demonstrated the three basic mathematical ingredients of our procedure: completeness, dispersion relations, and inverse scattering transform techniques. We also gave fractional soliton solutions to these equations and demonstrated super-dispersive transport as a physical implication of the equations. Such fractional equations model multiscale materials and open new directions in integrable nonlinear dynamics for such systems, both artificial and naturally occurring. Our method provides a context for the discovery and understanding of 1D fractional nonlinear evolution equations generally, with integrability acting as a key signpost for fractional nonlinear dynamics.

\underline{v}=[v^{(1)},v^{(2)}]^{T}
. This is associated to the following set of integrable nonlinear equations

\displaystyle\sigma_{3}\partial_{t}\mathbf{u}+2A_{0}(\mathbf{L}^{A})\mathbf{u}=0,\quad\sigma_{3}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix}

(36)

where
\mathbf{u}=\left[r,q\right]^{T}
and the operator

\displaystyle\mathbf{L}^{A}\equiv\frac{1}{2i}\begin{pmatrix}\partial_{x}-2rI_{-}q&2rI_{-}r\\
-2qI_{-}q&-\partial_{x}+2qI_{-}r\end{pmatrix}

(37)

with
I_{-}=\int_{-\infty}^{x}dy
. With sufficient decay and smoothness, we define eigenfunctions for the AKNS system as solutions to Eqs. (
34
)-(
35
) satisfying the boundary conditions

\displaystyle\bm{\phi}(x,k,t)

\displaystyle\sim\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix}e^{-ikx},~

\displaystyle\overline{\bm{\phi}}(x,k,t)\sim\begin{pmatrix}0\\
1\end{pmatrix}e^{ikx},x\to-\infty,

(38)

\displaystyle\bm{\psi}(x,k,t)

\displaystyle\sim\begin{pmatrix}0\\
1\end{pmatrix}e^{ikx},~

\displaystyle\overline{\bm{\psi}}(x,k,t)\sim\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix}e^{-ikx},x\to\infty.

(39)

The eigenfunctions
\bm{\psi}
,
\overline{\bm{\psi}}
are linearly independent so that

\displaystyle\bm{\phi}(x,k,t)

\displaystyle=b(k,t)\bm{\psi}(x,k,t)+a(k,t)\overline{\bm{\psi}}(x,k,t),

(40)

\displaystyle\overline{\bm{\phi}}(x,k,t)

\displaystyle=\overline{a}(k,t)\bm{\psi}(x,k,t)+\overline{b}(k,t)\overline{\bm{\psi}}(x,k,t).

(41)

The scattering data is obtained from

\displaystyle a(k)

\displaystyle=W(\bm{\phi},\bm{\psi}),\quad\overline{a}(k)

\displaystyle=W(\overline{\bm{\psi}},\overline{\bm{\phi}}),

(42)

\displaystyle b(k)

\displaystyle=W(\overline{\bm{\psi}},\bm{\phi}),\quad\overline{b}(k)

\displaystyle=W(\overline{\bm{\phi}},\bm{\psi})

(43)

with the Wronskian given by
W(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})=u^{(1)}v^{(2)}-u^{(2)}v^{(1)}
. The transmission and reflection coefficients,
\tau
,
\overline{\tau}
,
\rho
, and
\overline{\rho}
, are defined analogously to Eq. (
32
).

The zeros of
a
and
\overline{a}
at
k_{j}=\xi_{j}+i\eta_{j}
,
\eta_{j}>0
,
j=1,2,...,J
and
\overline{k}_{j}=\overline{\xi}_{j}+i\overline{\eta}_{j}
,
\overline{\eta}_{j}<0
,
j=1,2,...,\overline{J}
are the eigenvalues. We assume the eigenvalues are ‘proper’: i.e. they are simple, not on the real
k
axis, and
J=\overline{J}
; cf. Ref.
Ablowitz et al. 2004
.
The bound state eigenfunctions are related by

\displaystyle\bm{\phi}_{j}(x,t)=b_{j}(t)\bm{\psi}_{j}(x,t),\quad\overline{\bm{\phi}}_{j}(x,t)=\overline{b}_{j}(t)\overline{\bm{\psi}}_{j}(x,t).

(44)

When
r=\mp q^{*}
, we have the symmetry reductions

\displaystyle\overline{\bm{\psi}}(x,k,t)

\displaystyle=\Sigma\bm{\psi}^{*}(x,k^{*},t),~~\overline{\bm{\phi}}(x,k,t)=\mp\Sigma\bm{\phi}^{*}(x,k^{*},t)

(45)

for the eigenfunctions where
\Sigma=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
\pm 1&0\end{pmatrix}
. We also have

\overline{a}(k)=a^{*}(k^{*})
and
\overline{b}(k)=\mp b^{*}(k^{*})
for the scattering data.

From the scattering eigenfunctions
\bm{\psi}
and
\bm{\phi}
, we can construct the eigenfunctions of the operator
\mathbf{L}
,
\mathbf{\Psi}
and
\overline{\mathbf{\Psi}}
, and its adjoint
\mathbf{L}^{A}
,
\mathbf{\Psi}^{A}
and
\overline{\mathbf{\Psi}}^{A}

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Psi}(x,k,t)

\displaystyle=\left[(\psi^{(1)}(x,k,t))^{2},(\psi^{(2)}(x,k,t))^{2}\right]^{T},

(46)

\displaystyle~\overline{\mathbf{\Psi}}(x,k,t)

\displaystyle=\left[(\overline{\psi}^{(1)}(x,k,t))^{2},(\overline{\psi}^{(2)}(x,k,t))^{2}\right]^{T},

(47)

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Psi}^{A}(x,k,t)

\displaystyle=\left[(\phi^{(2)}(x,k,t))^{2},-(\phi^{(1)}(x,k,t))^{2}\right]^{T},

(48)

\displaystyle\overline{\mathbf{\Psi}}^{A}(x,k,t)

\displaystyle=\left[(\overline{\phi}^{(2)}(x,k,t))^{2},-(\overline{\phi}^{(1)}(x,k,t))^{2}\right]^{T}.

(49)

We use these functions to define the fNLS equation.

.2
Direct Scattering

To solve fKdV and fNLS by the inverse scattering transform, we first map the initial condition into scattering space; this is analogous to taking the Fourier transform of a linear PDE. This process involves analyzing linear integral equations for the eigenfunctions, determining their analytic properties, and then obtaining the scattering data using Wronskian relations.

.2.1
Direct scattering for the time-independent Schrödinger equation

The eigenfunctions
\varphi
and
\psi
of the time-independent Schrödinger Eq. (
25
) solve linear integral equations which have uniformly convergent Neumann series for
q(x,0)
in
L_{2}^{1}

Ablowitz 2011
. This series can be used to construct the eigenfunctions explicitly. Then, the scattering data at
t=0
, that is
a(k,0)
,
b(k,0)
,
\tau(k,0)
, and
\rho(k,0)
, can be obtained from the Wronskian relations in Eq. (
31
) along with the definitions of the transmission and reflection coefficients in Eq. (
32
).

.2.2
Direct scattering for the AKNS system

Similarily, the eigenfunctions
\bm{\phi}
and
\bm{\psi}
of the AKNS system solve linear integral equations with convergent Neumann series
Ablowitz 2011
and the initial scattering data
a(k,0)
,
\overline{a}(k,0)
,
b(k,0)
, and
\overline{b}(k,0)
is constructed from the Wronskian relations in Eqs. (
42
) and (
43
).

.3
Time evolution of the scattering data

The scattering data evolve in time according to elementary differential equations.

.3.1
Time evolution for the time-independent Schrödinger equation

Following the procedure in cf. Ref.
Ablowitz 2011
the time dependence of the scattering data is given by

\displaystyle a(k,t)

\displaystyle=a(k,0),\quad b(k,t)=b(k,0)e^{-2ik\gamma(k^{2})t},

(50)

\displaystyle\rho(k,t)

\displaystyle=\rho(k,0)e^{\!-2ik\gamma(k^{2})t},~c_{j}(t)=c_{j}(0)e^{\kappa_{j}\gamma(-\kappa_{j}^{2})t}

(51)

where
c_{j}^{2}(t)=-ib_{j}(t)/a_{j}^{\prime}(t)
with
a_{j}^{\prime}(t)\equiv\partial_{k}a(k,t)|_{k=i\kappa_{j}}
and
\gamma
comes from the nonlinear evolution equation in Eq. (
26
). Notice that
a(k,t)
is a constant of motion.

.3.2
Time evolution for the AKNS system

For the general evolution equation associated with the AKNS system in Eq. (
36
), we find the time evolution to be
Ablowitz 2011

\displaystyle a(k,t)

\displaystyle=a(k,0),

\displaystyle\overline{a}(k,t)=\overline{a}(k,0),

(52)

\displaystyle b(k,t)

\displaystyle=b(k,0)e^{-2A_{0}(k)t},~

\displaystyle\overline{b}(k,t)=\overline{b}(k,0)e^{2A_{0}(k),t}

(53)

\displaystyle C_{j}(t)

\displaystyle=C_{j}(0)e^{-2A_{0}(k_{j})t},~

\displaystyle\overline{C}_{j}(t)=\overline{C}_{j}(0)e^{2A_{0}(\overline{k}_{j})t}\

(54)

where
C_{j}(t)=b_{j}(t)/a_{j}^{\prime}(t)
and
\overline{C}_{j}(t)=\overline{b}_{j}(t)/\overline{a}_{j}^{\prime}(t)
with
a_{j}^{\prime}(t)\equiv\partial_{k}a(k,t)|_{k=i\kappa_{j}}
and
\overline{a}_{j}^{\prime}(t)\equiv\partial_{k}\overline{a}(k,t)|_{k=i\overline{\kappa}_{j}}
. Recall that
A_{0}(k)
is related to the nonlinear evolution equation in Eq. (
36
).

.4
Inverse Scattering

Inverse scattering is analogous to the inverse Fourier transform, except evaluating an integral on the real line in the case of Fourier transforms is now replaced by solving a linear integral equation in the case of the IST.

.4.1
Inverse scattering for the time-independent Schrödinger equation

The inverse scattering and solution
q(x,t)
of fKdV can be constructed by solving the following Gel’fand-Levitan-Marchenko (GLM) integral equation for
K(x,y;t)
:

\displaystyle K(x,y;t)

\displaystyle+\tilde{F}(x+y;t)+\!\int_{x}^{\infty}\!dsK(x,s;t)\tilde{F}(s+y;t)=0,

(55)

\displaystyle\tilde{F}(x;t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{J}c_{j}^{2}(t)e^{-\kappa_{j}x}+\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dk\rho(k,t)e^{ikx}.

Here
y>x
and recall that
J
is the number of zeros of
a
or, equivalently, the number of solitons in the solution
q
. Here, the time dependence of
\rho(k,t)
and
c_{j}(t)
are given in Eq. (
51
). Then the solution of the fKdV is obtained from

\displaystyle q(x,t)=2\frac{\partial}{\partial x}K(x,x;t).

(56)

.4.2
Inverse scattering for the AKNS system

The solution of fNLS and the general fractional
q,r
system can be constructed by solving the following GLM-type integral equations

\displaystyle\mathbf{K}(x,y;t)

\displaystyle+\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix}\overline{F}(x+y;t)

(57)

\displaystyle+\int_{x}^{\infty}ds\,\overline{\mathbf{K}}(x,s;t)\overline{F}(s+y;t)=0,

\displaystyle\overline{\mathbf{K}}(x,y;t)

\displaystyle+\begin{pmatrix}0\\
1\end{pmatrix}F(x+y;t)

(58)

\displaystyle+\int_{x}^{\infty}ds\,\mathbf{K}(x,s;t)F(s+y;t)=0

where

\displaystyle F(x;t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dk\rho(k,t)e^{ikx}-i\sum_{j=1}^{J}C_{j}(t)e^{ik_{j}x},

(59)

\displaystyle\overline{F}(x;t)

\displaystyle=\!\frac{1}{2\pi}\!\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!dk\overline{\rho}(k,t)e^{\!-ikx}+i\sum_{j=1}^{\overline{J}}\overline{C}_{j}(t)e^{\!-i\overline{k}_{j}x}.

(60)

The time dependence of
\rho(k,t)=b(k,t)/a(k)
,
\overline{\rho}(k,t)=\overline{b}(k,t)/\overline{a}(k)
,
C_{j}(t)
, and
\overline{C}_{j}(t)
are given in Eqs. (
52
)-(
54
).

The solution of the fractional
q,r
system is obtained from

\displaystyle q(x,t)=-2K^{(1)}(x,x;t),~r(x,t)=-2\overline{K}^{(2)}(x,x;t)

(61)

where
K^{(n)}
and
\overline{K}^{(n)}
for
n=1,2
denote the
n
th component of the vectors
\mathbf{K}
and
\overline{\mathbf{K}}
respectively. If the symmetry
r=\mp q^{\ast}
holds then the GLM equations have the scalar reduction

\displaystyle\overline{F}(x;t)=\mp F^{\ast}(x;t)

and consequently

\displaystyle\overline{\mathbf{K}}(x,y;t)=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}K^{(2)}(x,y;t)\\
\mp K^{(1)}(x,y;t)\end{array}\right)^{\ast}.

In this case the inverse problem
reduces to

\displaystyle K^{(1)}(x,y;t)=\pm F^{\ast}(x+y;t)

(64)

\displaystyle\mp\int_{x}^{\infty}ds\int_{x}^{\infty}ds^{\prime}K^{(1)}(x,s^{\prime};t)F(s+s^{\prime};t)F^{\ast}(y+s;t)

and an equation for
K^{(2)}
which we do not write here. Then the solution to fNLS can be obtained from Eq. (
61
). We
also note that when
r=\mp q
with
q
real, then
F(x;t)
and
K^{(1)}(x,y;t)

are real.

.5
Alternative Representation of the fKdV operator

The operator
\gamma(L^{A})
acting on an arbitrary function
h(x)
may be represented by the spectral expansion

\displaystyle\gamma(L^{A})h(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Gamma_{\infty}}\!dk\gamma(k^{2})\frac{\tau^{2}(k)}{4\pi ik}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!dyG(x,y,k)h(y)

(65)

in terms of the eigenfunctions of the Schrödinger scattering problem where time
t
is suppressed. This expression can be evaluated using contour integration to give a representation of
\gamma(L^{A})
in terms of integrals along the real line and a sum over discrete values along the imaginary axis:

\displaystyle\gamma(L^{A})h(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dk\,\gamma(k^{2})\frac{\tau^{2}(k)}{4\pi ik}\,\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dy\,G_{c}(x,y,k)h(y)

\displaystyle+\sum_{j=1}^{J}\gamma(-\kappa_{j}^{2})\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dy\,G_{d,j}(x,y,k)h(y).

(66)

Here the continuous contribution
G_{c}
is defined by

\displaystyle G_{c}(x,y,k)=\partial_{x}\left(\psi^{2}(x,k)\varphi^{2}(y,k)-\varphi^{2}(x,k)\psi^{2}(y,k)\right),

(67)

and the discrete contribution, which comes from the poles of
\tau
at
k_{j}=i\kappa_{j}
,
j=1,2,...,J
, is given by

\displaystyle G_{d,j}\!=i\eta_{j}\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left[\psi^{2}(y)\psi(x)\partial_{k}\varphi(x)\!-\!\psi^{2}(y)\varphi(x)\partial_{k}\psi(x)\right]_{k=k_{j}}

\displaystyle-i\eta_{j}\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left[\psi^{2}(x)\psi(y)\partial_{k}\varphi(y)-\psi^{2}(x)\varphi(y)\partial_{k}\psi(y)\right]_{k=k_{j}}

(68)

where
\eta_{j}=\frac{b(k_{j})}{\kappa_{j}a^{\prime}(k_{j})^{2}}
. Note that we have suppressed
k
in the above expression, i.e.,
\psi(x)=\psi(x,k)
and
\varphi(x)=\varphi(x,k)
.

.6
Explicit form of the fNLS equation

The set of nonlinear evolution equations which may be associated with the AKNS scattering problem, Eqs. (
34
-
35
), is given in Eq. (
36
).
Further,
A_{0}(\mathbf{L}^{A})
may be represented as

\displaystyle A_{0}(\mathbf{L}^{A})\mathbf{v}(x)\!

\displaystyle=\!\!\sum_{n=1}^{2}\!\int_{\Gamma_{\infty}^{(n)}}\!\!\!dkA_{0}(k)f_{n}(k)\!\!\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!dy\mathbf{G}_{n}(x,\!y,\!k)\mathbf{v}(y).

(69)

We obtain fNLS by taking
A_{0}(\mathbf{L}^{A})=2i(\mathbf{L}^{A})^{2}|2\mathbf{L}^{A}|^{\epsilon}
and
r=\mp q^{*}
in Eq. (
36
). If we split off
2i(\mathbf{L}^{A})^{2}
and operate on
\mathbf{u}=\left(r,q\right)^{T}
, we find

\displaystyle 2i(\mathbf{L}^{A})^{2}\mathbf{u}=\frac{1}{2i}\begin{pmatrix}\mp q_{xx}^{*}-2(q^{*})^{2}q\\
q_{xx}\pm 2q^{2}q^{*}\end{pmatrix}.

(70)

Then, representing
|2\mathbf{L}^{A}|^{\epsilon}
with the spectral expansion in Eq. (
69
), we have

\displaystyle 2i(\mathbf{L}^{A})^{2}|2\mathbf{L}^{A}|^{\epsilon}\mathbf{u}=\sum_{n=1}^{2}\frac{1}{2i}\int_{\Gamma_{\infty}^{(n)}}\!\!dk|2k|^{\epsilon}f_{n}(k)

(71)

\displaystyle\times\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dy\mathbf{G}_{n}(x,y,k)\begin{pmatrix}\mp q_{yy}^{*}-2(q^{*})^{2}q\\
q_{yy}\pm 2q^{2}q^{*}\end{pmatrix}.

(72)

Putting Eq. (
72
) into Eq. (
36
), multiplying by
-i
, and taking the second component gives

\displaystyle iq_{t}=\sum_{n=1}^{2}\int_{\Gamma_{\infty}^{(n)}}\!\!dk|2k|^{\epsilon}f_{n}(k)\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dyF_{n}(x,y,k)

(73)

where

\displaystyle F_{1}(x,y,k)=\,

\displaystyle-\phi_{1}^{2}(x,k)\big[\psi_{1}^{2}(y,k)(\mp q_{yy}^{*}-2(q^{*})^{2}q)

(74)

\displaystyle+\psi_{2}^{2}(y,k)(q_{yy}\pm 2q^{2}q^{*})\big]

(75)

and
F_{2}
is the same, but with
\psi_{n}
replaced by
\overline{\psi}_{n}
and
\phi_{n}
replaced by
\overline{\phi}_{n}
for
n=1,2
where
\overline{\psi}_{n}
and
\overline{\phi}_{n}
are related to
\psi_{n}
and
\phi_{n}
by equation (
45
)

.7
Conserved Quantities

Like their integer counterparts, the fKdV and the fNLS equation also admit an infinite number of conserved quantities. In fact, the derivation of these using IST methods is independent of the exact form of
\gamma
and
A_{0}
, so their conserved quantities are the same as KdV and NLS; cf.
Ablowitz et al. 1991
. However, the fluxes associated with these conservation laws corresponding to these conserved quantities are not the same.

.8
Direct calculation for the fKdV soliton

It can be shown that the fractional soliton solution given by

\displaystyle q_{K}(x,t)

\displaystyle=2\kappa^{2}\text{sech}^{2}{w_{\epsilon}(x,t)}

(76)

solves the fKdV equation where
w_{\epsilon}(x,t)=\kappa[(x-x_{1})-(4\kappa^{2})^{1+\epsilon}t]
. This one soliton corresponds to
J=1
, i.e., one bound state solution of the time-independent Schrödinger equation, (
25
), at
k=i\kappa
and a reflectionless potential
\rho(k,t)=0
for real
k
. To show this, we verify that the fKdV equation

\displaystyle q_{t}+\!\int_{\Gamma_{\infty}}\!\!dk|4k^{2}|^{1+\epsilon}\frac{\tau^{2}(k)}{2\pi ik}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dy\,G(x,y,k)q_{y}\!=\!0

(77)

is satisfied when
q=q_{K}
.
For this case, the Schrödinger eigenfunctions — which are found by solving Eq. (
25
) with
q=q_{K}
— can be written as

\displaystyle\psi(x,k,t)

\displaystyle=v_{1}(x,k,t),

(78)

\displaystyle\varphi(x,k,t)

\displaystyle=v_{-1}(x,k,t)

(79)

where

\displaystyle v_{\sigma}(x,k,t)=e^{i\sigma kx}\left(\frac{k+\sigma i\kappa\tanh{w_{\epsilon}(x,t)}}{k+i\kappa}\right)

(80)

with
\sigma=\pm 1
. Further,
\partial_{x}q_{K}
is

\displaystyle\partial_{x}q_{K}(x,t)=-4\kappa^{3}\text{sech}^{2}{w_{\epsilon}(x,t)}\tanh{w_{\epsilon}(x,t)}.

(81)

To calculate
\gamma(L^{A})\partial_{x}q_{K}
, we compute the integrals
I_{c}\equiv\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dy\,G_{c}(x,y,k)\partial_{y}q_{K}(y,t)
and
I_{d}\equiv\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dy\,G_{d,1}(x,y,k)\partial_{y}q_{K}(y,t)
according to Eqs. (
66
)-(
68
). These two integral can be written as

\displaystyle I_{c}

\displaystyle=\partial_{x}(v_{1})^{2}I_{-1}^{(1)}+\partial_{x}(v_{-1})^{2}I_{1}^{(1)}|_{k=i\kappa},

(82)

\displaystyle I_{d}

\displaystyle=i\eta\partial_{x}\left(v_{1}\partial_{k}v_{-1}-v_{-1}\partial_{k}v_{1}\right)I_{1}^{(1)}|_{k=i\kappa}

(83)

\displaystyle-i\eta\partial_{x}(v_{1})^{2}\left(I^{(2)}_{1}-I^{(2)}_{-1}\right)|_{k=i\kappa}

where

\displaystyle I_{\sigma}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dy~v_{\sigma}^{2}(y,k,t)q_{y}(y,t),

(84)

\displaystyle I_{\sigma}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dy~v_{\sigma}(y,k,t)\partial_{k}v_{-\sigma}(y,k,t)q_{y}(y,t)

(85)

and
\eta=b(i\kappa,t)/\kappa(\partial_{k}a(k,t)|_{k=i\kappa})^{2}

Using the substitution
w=w_{\epsilon}(y,t)
, the integral
I_{\sigma}^{(1)}
can be shown to be proportional to
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dw~f_{\sigma}(w)
where

\displaystyle f_{\sigma}(w)=e^{2i\sigma kw/\kappa}\left(k+i\sigma\kappa\tanh{w}\right)^{2}\text{sech}^{2}{w}\tanh{w}.

(86)

If we consider the rectangular contour with corners at
w=-R
and
w=R+i\pi
and define the bottom, top, right, and left contour by
C_{B}
,
C_{T}
,
C_{R}
, and
C_{L}
respectively, we have

\displaystyle\left(\int_{C_{B}}dw+\int_{C_{T}}dw+\int_{C_{R}}dw+\int_{C_{L}}dw\right)f_{\sigma}(w)

(87)

\displaystyle=2\pi i\text{Res}\left(f_{\sigma},i\pi/2\right).

However, the contours along
C_{R}
and
C_{L}
vanish as
R\to\infty
and
C_{B}
may be written in terms of
C_{T}
as

\displaystyle\int_{C_{T}}dw~f_{\sigma}(w)=-e^{-2\sigma k\pi/\kappa}\int_{C_{B}}dw~f(w).

(88)

Because as
R\to\infty
,
\int_{C_{B}}dwf_{\sigma}(w)\to\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dwf_{\sigma}(w)
, equation (
87
) becomes

\displaystyle\left(1-e^{-2\sigma k\pi/\kappa}\right)\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dwf_{\sigma}(w)=2\pi i\text{Res}\left(f_{\sigma},i\pi/2\right).

(89)

But as the residue of
f_{\sigma}
vanishes when
\sigma=\pm 1
,
\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dwf_{\sigma}(w)=0
and, thus,
I_{\sigma}^{(1)}=0
. Therefore, the continuous contribution to
\gamma(L^{A})q_{x}
,
I_{c}
, vanishes. We also see that the first half of the discrete part,
I_{d}
given by Eq. (
83
), is zero. With these reductions, we can express
\gamma(L^{A})\partial_{x}q_{K}
as

\displaystyle\gamma(L^{A})\partial_{x}q_{K}=-i\eta\gamma(-\kappa^{2})\partial_{x}(v_{1})^{2}\left(I^{(2)}_{1}-I^{(2)}_{-1}\right)|_{k=i\kappa}.

(90)

By explicitly evaluating
\eta
and
\partial_{x}(v_{1})^{2}
, we find that

\displaystyle\eta\partial_{x}(v_{1})^{2}|_{k=i\kappa}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial_{x}q_{K}}{2\kappa}.

(91)

Then, the integral
I_{\sigma}^{(2)}
can be evaluated using the fundamental theorem of calculus to give

\displaystyle\left(I_{1}^{(2)}-I_{-1}^{(2)}\right)|_{k=i\kappa}=2i\kappa.

(92)

So,
\gamma(L^{A})\partial_{x}q_{K}
follows as

\displaystyle\gamma(L^{A})\partial_{x}q_{K}=-(2\kappa)^{2+2\epsilon}\partial_{x}q_{K}(x,t)

(93)

which is exactly
-\partial_{t}q_{K}(x,t)
. Thus, the soliton given in Eq. (
76
) solves the fKdV equation.
