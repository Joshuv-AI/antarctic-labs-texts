Nonequilibrium Phase Transition in a 2D Ferromagnetic Spins with Effective Interactions
Dagne Wordofa Tola, Chandan Dasgupta, Mulugeta Bekele, Email: mulugetabekele1@gmail.com (MB)

Abstract
We investigate nonequilibrium phase transitions in a 2D ferromagnetic Ising model on a square lattice with effective interactions using Monte Carlo-based computational algorithms. We verify the effective parameter h h by employing mean-field theory and derive self-consistent equations (SCEs) using two familiar dynamics: Metropolis and Glauber. For − 1 < h < 1 -1<h<1 , both dynamics are expected to estimate the same SCE. We find the relation between T c T_{c} and T c 0 T_{c}^{0} where T c T_{c} ( T c 0 T_{c}^{0} ) is the critical temperature of the model with (without) the effective interactions. Here, T c 0 T_{c}^{0} refers to the well-known analytical result of the equilibrium Ising model. We perform the simulations for different lattice sizes that enable us to measure physical quantities of interest. From numerical data, we determine T c T_{c} and relevant exponents for various values of h h by employing finite-size scaling (FSS). We find that the FSS result of T c T_{c} , which is quite different from T c 0 T_{c}^{0} , is in agreement with that of its analytical result, and the accuracy is good regardless of the types of model. The numerical results of the exponents are consistent with the analytical values of the equilibrium 2D Ising model, which belongs to the same universality class.

2.2
Monte Carlo simulation method

Consider a system that generates stochastic spin flips when in contact with a thermal reservoir
[
35
]
. In the equilibrium Ising model, the system achieves thermal equilibrium after a sufficiently long time, and the description of the steady-state distribution is subject to the Boltzmann distribution. This permits one to define transition rates and calculate the flipping probabilities. Nonequilibrium PT is discussed with emphasis on general features such as the role of breaking DBC in generating effective interactions
[
36
]
. In principle, DBC can be sufficient
-
but it is not necessary to ensure equilibration. For this work, our model violates the DBC and causes the system to come out of equilibrium based on the nature of the effective interaction in consideration (
h\neq 0

[
16

, there is a situation in which the system shows a disorder-order transition that is not similar to the usual equilibrium PT. As each spin flips from time to time due to the influence of thermal fluctuation, we use the master equation (ME) to develop this idea in terms of stochastic changes in its configurations. The intrinsic stochastic dynamics of the system allow us to compute its thermodynamic properties. In fact, most of our knowledge about equilibrium PT can be extended to the nonequilibrium case as well.

Denoting the spin configuration before the flip
\texttt{b}=\{S_{1},...,\pm S_{i},...,S_{N}\}
, the system’s state is described according to probability theory that the state has a configuration
b
at time
t

\mathcal{P}_{b}(h,t)
. There are
2^{N}
total configurations
-
thus we have an absolute stochastic form of the state at time

if we can describe all the
2^{N}
values of
\mathcal{P}_{b}(h,t)
for each
b
s. The ME justifies how such a set of probabilities evolves with

. Assuming that the configuration after the flip will be
\texttt{a}=\{S_{1},...,\mp S_{i},...,S_{N}\}
, the transition changes from state
b
to
a
with rate of transition probability
W(b\rightarrow a)
in a couple of time interval
\Delta t

[
34
]
. Consequently, the probability that the configuration of system being in state
b
decreases by
W(b\rightarrow a)\Delta t\mathcal{P}_{b}(h,t)
, since the system was in
b
with probability
\mathcal{P}_{b}(h,t)
and then has changed to
a
with rate of transition probability
W(b\rightarrow a)
in interval
\Delta t
. Correspondingly, the probability that it is in state
b
would be increased by

W(a\rightarrow b)\Delta t\mathcal{P}_{a}(h,t)
.
The ME is basically a linear partial differential equation determining probability fluxes (net flow away from and into a configuration). We shall use a discrete form of ME, as it is for the implementation of MC simulation in mind, and define the net change of probability per small time interval
\Delta t
as follows:

\frac{\Delta\mathcal{P}_{b}(h,t)}{\Delta t}=\sum_{a\neq b}W(a\rightarrow b)\mathcal{P}_{a}(h,t)-\sum_{a\neq b}W(b\rightarrow a)\mathcal{P}_{b}(h,t),

(2.8)

where
\Delta\mathcal{P}_{b}(h,t)=\mathcal{P}_{b}(h,t+\Delta t)-\mathcal{P}_{b}(h,t)
.
This ME (
2.8
) is an implicit idea of Markov processes where the characteristic change of
\mathcal{P}_{b}(h,t)
is completely described as an effective probability distribution at time
t
while
h
is fixed. Though a proposition that the dynamics are generated by heat baths secures that the rates (
2.8
) satisfy DB at some control parameters, we intended to deal with rates violating DB. In sufficiently long-time limit

, a stochastic dynamical system approaches a statistically stationary state where it evolves through certain configuration according to a distinct probability distribution that does not change with time and
\Delta\mathcal{P}_{b}(h,t\to\infty)\approx 0
. The key advantage of this concept is the appearance of a Hamiltonian (
2.1
) relating each state of configuration
\{S\}
with a certain effective energy (
2.4
). The ME is reduced to

(2.9)

which we refer to as global DBC for dynamical systems approaching thermal equilibrium. The global condition (
2.9

W(b\rightarrow a)\mathcal{P}_{b}(h)=W(a\rightarrow b)\mathcal{P}_{a}(h)\texttt{.}

(2.10)

For

However, an intrinsic microscopic irreversibility will essentially produce macroscopic nonequilibrium properties and we intended to study nonequilibrium PT occurring due to violation of local DB. Phase Transitions and Effective Parameter Explained

This section is devoted mainly to explaining the existence of effective parameter
h
and its verification with the occurrence of phase transition (PT). We treat the solution of the model in consideration using the mean-field (MF), also known as the Curie-Weiss molecular field approximation. In the context of this paper (and previous work), the occurrence of nonequilibrium PT solely relies on the existence of a parameter violating DBC. We discuss the computational algorithm under MF approximation (MFA) based on both Metropolis (
2.14
) and Glauber (
2.15
) dynamics to derive self-consistent equations (SCEs), and demonstrate the results quantitatively and qualitatively. Even though MF prediction is quantitatively not correct, the model’s qualitative behavior is outstandingly identical to the standard solution. In adopting and establishing nonequilibrium PTs, the effective values of parameter
h
must be determined and explained. If
h
exists and its value prescribes the violation of DBC, the transition should be regarded as nonequilibrium PT. Therefore, identifying and prescribing this parameter is very useful for actually describing the occurrence of PT, and also for the presentation and validation of the main results in Section
4
.

represents the possible number of neighbors for a given
d
-number of spatial dimensions. It follows that

\Delta E=2Jz\bar{m}(1+h)S_{i}\texttt{.}

(3.2)

Previously as introduced in Section
2
, the modified version of the energy difference would be convenient to describe the stochastic dynamical system that breaks the DBC. Straightforward from Eq. (
2.17
), this compatibility requires that

\Delta E=2Jz\bar{m}(S_{i}+h)\texttt{,}

(3.3)

and substitute this (
3.3
) in (
3.1
) to get the MF rate of transition for Metropolis dynamics

w=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\exp\left[-2\beta Jz\bar{m}(S_{i}+h)\right],&\hbox{}\Delta E\mathrm{\eqref{Eq:3.03}}>0;\\
1,&\hbox{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

(3.4)

Incorporating this MFA, let us now describe a SCE for
\bar{m}
and so it will be solved numerically. Let the spin variable at site
i
is
S_{i}=\{S_{\bullet},S_{\circ}\}
where
S_{\bullet}=1
and
S_{\circ}=-1
. Thus
w_{\bullet\rightarrow\circ}
denotes the probability of flipping from
S_{\bullet}
to
S_{\circ}
and, similarly,
w_{\circ\rightarrow\bullet}
denotes the probability of flipping from
S_{\circ}
to
S_{\bullet}
in the steady state of the system.
Defining
r
as the ratio (
2.11
) of these probabilities, which is a function of
\beta
,
m
, and
h
, it can be simplified as

r(\beta,\bar{m},h)=\frac{w_{\bullet\rightarrow\circ}}{w_{\circ\rightarrow\bullet}}\equiv\frac{\mathcal{P}_{\circ}}{\mathcal{P}_{\bullet}}\texttt{.}

(3.5)

Consequently, we can express MF magnetization
\bar{m}
in terms of ratio
r
to get the required SCE,

\bar{m}(h)=\frac{1}{Z}\sum_{\{s\}=\pm 1}\mathcal{P}_{\{s\}}S_{i}\texttt{,}\mathrm{\;where\;\;}Z=\mathrm{e}^{-\beta E_{\bullet}}+\mathrm{e}^{-\beta E_{\circ}}\texttt{,}

\bar{m}(h)=\frac{1-r(\beta,\bar{m},h)}{1+r(\beta,\bar{m},h)}\texttt{.}

(3.6)

This SCE can be solved merely for some values of
h
for which the DBC is satisfied.
Proceeding with equilibrium dynamics (
h=0
),
r(\beta,\bar{m},h=0)=\exp[-2\beta^{0}Jz\bar{m}]
where here
\beta^{0}=\beta^{0}(\textsc{MF})
denotes
1/T(h=0)
. Consequently

\bar{m}(h=0)=\frac{1-\exp[-2\beta^{0}Jz\bar{m}]}{1+\exp[-2\beta^{0}Jz\bar{m}]},

\bar{m}(h=0)=\tanh[\beta^{0}Jz\bar{m}]\texttt{ (Equilibrium SCE), }

(3.7)

which is the well-known SCE for the magnetization and we refer to this (
3.7
) as
original SCE
of the equilibrium Ising model.

Given the modified MF version of
\Delta E
(
3.3
), it is important to sort the following two statements:
(i) If
\Delta E
is always
nonnegative
, then
r=\exp[-4\beta Jz\bar{m}]
. Equation (
3.6
), therefore, satisfies a SCE of MF magnetization

\bar{m}(h\neq 0)=\frac{1-r}{1+r}=\tanh[2\beta Jz\bar{m}]\texttt{,}

(3.8)

which is independent of
h
.
(ii) If
\Delta E
is always
non-positive
, the ratio
r=1
and therefore it turn outs that

\bar{m}(h\neq 0)=0\texttt{.}

(3.9)

In contrast, the purpose of this paper is mainly intended to the case of
h
-dependent SCE where imposing the constraint
-1<h<1
implicitly admits establishing a microscopic irreversibility of the dynamics. This idea will be more clear in the following brief specification.

First consider the case for
\bar{m}<0
, and assume that
h
can take any real values (the restriction
-1<h<1
holds later). The dynamics defined in Eq. (
3.4
) now takes its explicit forms:

w_{\bullet\rightarrow\circ}^{(\bar{m}<0)}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\exp\left[-\beta(2Jz\bar{m}(1+h))\right],&\hbox{if }h<-1;\\
1,&\hbox{if }h\geq-1\end{array}\texttt{,}\right.

and

w_{\circ\rightarrow\bullet}^{(\bar{m}<0)}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\exp\left[-\beta(-2Jz\bar{m}(1-h))\right],&\hbox{if }h<1;\\
1,&\hbox{if }h\geq 1\end{array}\texttt{.}\right.

Looking for
h<-1
, this gives the ratio
r=\exp\left[-4\beta Jz\bar{m}\right]
and the next SCE for
\bar{m}
:

\bar{m}(h<-1)=\frac{1-r}{1+r}=\tanh\left[2\beta Jz\bar{m}\right]\texttt{.}

(3.10)

One may refer this (
3.10
) as a SCE model for
h<-1
though this is not of interest here.
But for
h>1
,
r=1
and
\bar{m}(h>1)=0
, indicating the negative
\bar{m}(h>1)
has no SCE.
For
-1<h<1
, we get the ratio
r=\exp\left[-2\beta Jz\bar{m}(1-h)\right]
and this gives

\bar{m}(-1<h<1)=\tanh\left[\beta Jz\bar{m}(1-h)\right]\texttt{.}

(3.11)

Second consider the case
\bar{m}\geq 0
in which is the transition probabilities would be,

w_{\bullet\rightarrow\circ}^{(\bar{m}\geq 0)}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\exp\left[-\beta(2Jz\bar{m}(1+h))\right],&\hbox{if }h>-1;\\
1,&\hbox{if }h\leq-1\texttt{,}\end{array}\right.

and

w_{\circ\rightarrow\bullet}^{(\bar{m}\geq 0)}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\exp\left[-\beta(-2Jz\bar{m}(1-h))\right],&\hbox{if }h>1;\\
1,&\hbox{if }h\leq 1\texttt{.}\end{array}\right.

For
h>1
, the ratio becomes
r=\exp\left[-4\beta Jzm\right]
and we get the following SCE for
\bar{m}
:

\bar{m}(h>1)=\frac{1-r}{1+r}=\tanh\left[2\beta Jz\bar{m}\right]\texttt{,}

(3.12)

which is identical to the SCE (
3.10
). This means that the magnitude of
\bar{m}(h<-1)
and that of
\bar{m}(h>1)
are the same, and
\bar{m}(|h|>1)
is independent of the parameter
h
. Comparing SCE (
3.12
) with SCE (
3.7
), for
h>1
, one can find the relation
2\beta_{c}=\beta_{c}^{0}
as long as
\bar{m}
is the same at critical. Hence, the critical temperature
T_{c}(h>1)\textsc{MF}=2T_{c}^{0}(\textsc{MF})
is the same for all
h>1
hence the PT is continuous as in the case of the equilibrium transitions. Intriguingly, we find
r=\exp\left[-2\beta Jz\bar{m}(1+h)\right]
for
-1<h<1
, and

\bar{m}(-1<h<1)=\tanh\left[\beta Jz\bar{m}(1+h)\right]\texttt{.}

(3.13)

Note from SCE (
3.11
) that its MF solution must obey
\bar{m}(T,h)=-\bar{m}(T,-h)
and hence
\tanh\left[\beta Jz\bar{m}(1-h)\right]=-\tanh\left[\beta Jz\bar{m}(1+h)\right]
. On the other hand, both
w_{\bullet\rightarrow\circ}
and
w_{\circ\rightarrow\bullet}
are equal to one and there is no SCE for
h\leq-1
where
\bar{m}(h\leq-1)=0
.

It has been noticed from SCEs (
3.10
) and (
3.12
) that
\bar{m}(|h|>1)
is independent of
h
. For the purpose of this paper, therefore, we proceed working with SCE (
3.13
) and compute
\bar{m}=\bar{m}(-1<h<1)
in the form of,

\bar{m}=\tanh[\beta Jz|\bar{m}|(1+h)],\texttt{(Metropolis SCE).}

(3.14)

Amazingly, the same SCE (
3.14
) can be obtained also using
\Delta E
(
3.2
) which means that the two different forms of
\Delta E
give the same results for
-1<h<1
.

3.1.2
Glauber dynamics:

The update rule that introduced in Eq. (
2.15
) is also of theoretical interest. To go any further, we expect that
Glauber SCE
should be the same as that of the
Metropolis SCE
for both
\Delta E
(
3.2
) and
\Delta E
(
3.3
). Make use of the former (
3.2
) now we can easily solve the Glauber algorithm with a dynamics of single spin flip

w=\frac{1}{2}\left(1-S_{i}\tanh[\beta Jz\bar{m}(1+h)]\frac{}{}\right)\texttt{.}

(3.15)

This result holds true also for
\Delta E
(

) luckily due to the fact that

and, therefore,

with

.
Straightforward to the Metropolis dynamics, this Glauber dynamics gives

w_{\circ\rightarrow\bullet}=\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\tanh[\beta Jz\bar{m}(1+h)]\right)

3.5
) now becomes

thus, one can find
\bar{m}
as in Eq. (

) providing a

:
\bar{m}(h)=\tanh[\beta Jz\bar{m}(1+h)]
. As expected, the Glauber SCE is the same as the Metropolis SCE (
3.14
) in the vicinity
-1<h<1
where the DBC does not hold. The DBC holds for
h=0
where
\bar{m}(h=0)=\tanh[\beta^{0}Jz\bar{m}]
, and for
h=1
where
\bar{m}(h=1)=\tanh[2\beta Jz\bar{m}]
. In practice, numerically the modified form of energy difference for

dynamics is therefore,

(3.16)

with the corresponding definition of magnetization SCEs (

4.1
Measurement of Physical Quantities

It has been previously argued
[

that physical quantities of interest such as the magnetization per site
m

\chi

\langle E\rangle/N
and the specific-heat
C=C_{V}
are defined in their usual forms;

\displaystyle m

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle M\rangle/N,\mathrm{\;\;\;from\;Equation~\eqref{Eq:207}}\texttt{,}

(4.1a)

\displaystyle\chi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left.\left(\langle M^{2}\rangle\frac{}{}-\langle M\rangle^{2}\right)\right/NT

(4.1b)

\displaystyle\langle E\rangle/N

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle E\rangle\mathrm{\;per\;site\;from\;Eq.~\eqref{Eq:204}}\texttt{,}

(4.1c)

\displaystyle C

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left.\left(\langle E^{2}\rangle\frac{}{}-\langle E\rangle^{2}\right)\right/NT^{2}\texttt{.}

(4.1d)

Here

is the total number of spins and the symbol

Making use of the modified algorithm, we perform Metropolis MC simulations of the model in consideration and measure all the required macroscopic quantities relevant to the investigation of PTs. In order to indicate the location of the transition temperature

Further, perusing the behavior of the system near its critical temperature is in fact a basic problem in the theory of PT. Noticeably, this behavior is well-marked as those physical quantities (
4.1
), and or their derivatives, possess singularities or discontinuities at the critical temperature. Here, the critical behaviors of
\chi
and
C
are common examples in the Ising model. In statistical physics, it is known that such singularities can be understood as a direct consequence of the fact that correlation length becomes infinitely large in the vicinity of the transition temperature. This indeed represents the correlation between spin positions in the lattice. As a result of such its infiniteness, the system becomes scale-invariance which means that the asymptotic behaviors of the relevant physical quantities
near
and/or
at
critical point are given by power laws. Continuous PTs are classified by their critical exponents, which characterize the behavior near/at the transition point. The most essential is
m\sim|T-T_{c}|^{\beta}
,

,

, and

\beta

,

\alpha=0
are known exactly. (Do not confuse the exponent
\beta

4

\displaystyle-\mathcal{X}(1-T/T_{c})L^{(1+\gamma)/\nu}\Longleftrightarrow\chi L^{-\gamma/\nu}\textsl{ vs }\tau L^{1/\nu}

5
Summary and Conclusions

We have investigated the nature of PTs in a 2D ferromagnetic Ising model on a square lattice (
d=2
) with effective interactions using a nonzero parameter
h
to account for all other interactions into consideration. An energy function describing a model system has been conceptually simplified to manage the computational algorithms, as we have briefly presented in Section
2
in which the importance of accounting for the effective interaction in establishing the nonequilibrium nature of the PT has been emphasized. Employing MF theory (Section
3
), SCEs are derived based on the two usually known dynamics, Metropolis and Glauber, and the existence of the effective
h
has verified to be
-1<h<1
in which our results qualitatively demonstrate that both dynamics estimate the same SCE. Here, the parameter
h
can be effective as long as the update rule has been modified in way that breaks the DBC. In the essence of qualifying the update rule, the restriction
|h|<1
can be appreciated from the following arguments.

•

The SCE is independent of
h
for
h\geq 1
, so
T_{c}
too.

•

For
h=0
, the well-known equilibrium Ising model is restored.

•

The Ising spin ferromagnet has lost its properties when
h<-1
as it is exhibiting anti-ferromagnet.

For
-1<h<1
, more interestingly, the nature of PTs with DBC is identical to that of without DBC, and the critical temperature satisfies Eq. (
3.20
). This suggests that equilibrium and nonequilibrium PTs exhibit similar critical phenomena regardless of the modification adopted in the update rule, which is likely unexpected.

In section
4
, the paper provided a numerical study of phases in the ferromagnetic system and their transitions in NESS of the prototypical 2D Ising model with effective interactions. Using the effective Metropolis update rule (
2.14
), we have performed standard MC simulations for finite systems of various lattice sizes with periodic boundary conditions. We measured physical quantities such as average magnetization per site (
4.1a
), magnetic susceptibility (
4.1b
) that computed from fluctuations of the magnetization, average energy per spin (
4.1c
), specific-heat (
4.1d
) that computed from fluctuations of the energy, and Binder cumulant (
4.2
), for various values of
h
in general, and two values of
h=\pm 0.25
in particular. Consequently, the transition temperature and relevant critical exponents have determined by employing the techniques of FSS as summarized in Table

.

A.1
Occurrence of Nonequilibrium Phase Transition

In equilibrium Ising model, the distribution in the steady state is given by Boltzmann distributions and the recognized (
h=0
) transition rate (
2.14
) can be written as

W=\texttt{MIN}\left[1,\texttt{e}^{-\beta^{0}\Delta E^{0}}\right],

(A.1)

where
W
is a rate of transition from a state
b
to other state
a
,

\Delta E^{0}=E_{\texttt{a}}^{0}-E_{\texttt{b}}^{0}
, is the change in energy due to this transition.
This algorithm satisfies DBC which means that the microscopic reversibility of each elementary process is balanced with its reverse processes (
2.9
). From Eq. (
2.10
), here the ratio
r(h=0)=\mathcal{P}_{\texttt{a}}^{0}/\mathcal{P}_{\texttt{b}}^{0}
becomes

r(h=0)=\texttt{e}^{-\beta^{0}\Delta E^{0}}.

(A.2)

We want to deliberately violate the DBC to cause the system to go out of equilibrium in which the system shows an order-disorder transition that is not similar to the usual equilibrium PT. Assuming
h\neq 0
denotes a parameter violating the DBC,
\Delta E^{0}
in Eq. (
A.1
) is substituted by

\Delta E=\Delta E^{0}+h|\Delta E^{0}|\equiv\Delta E^{0}+\varepsilon\mathrm{,}

(A.3)

where
\varepsilon=h|\Delta E^{0}|
. Thus the ratio
r(h\neq 0)
becomes

r(h\neq 0)=\mathrm{e}^{-\beta\Delta E}\equiv\mathrm{e}^{-\beta(\Delta E^{0}+\varepsilon)}.

(A.4)

It follows that
\Delta E>\Delta E^{0}
for positive
h
or (
\varepsilon>0
), and
\Delta E<\Delta E^{0}
for negative
h
(or
\varepsilon<0
).
Here we notice that the former doesn’t promote the flipping of spins while the latter becomes highly probable for spins to flip. Compared to spins with the usual Metropolis, spins under these flipping rates efficiently experience different temperatures. For
\varepsilon<0
(
\varepsilon>0
), the spins may be assumed as being coupled to a thermal bath at a higher(lower) effective temperature (
T_{\texttt{eff}}
) and this
T_{\texttt{eff}}
is not the same for all the spins in the system
[
16
]
.
Accordingly, this system is not in equilibrium and the PT would be a property of NESS of the system. Based on the modified Metropolis algorithm
[
17
]
, the transition rate for flipping a spin
S_{i}^{\texttt{b}}\rightarrow S_{i}^{\texttt{a}}
is

W(\pm S_{i}\rightarrow\mp S_{i})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\texttt{e}^{-\beta(\varepsilon\pm\Delta E^{0})},&\varepsilon\pm\Delta E>0;\\
1,&\hbox{otherwise,}\end{array}\right.

(A.5)

where
\Delta E^{0}=\{-8,-4,0,4,8\}
. This algorithm (
A.5
) still
respects
the DBC for
|h|\geq 1
(or
|\varepsilon|\geq 8
). For
h\geq 1
DBC is satisfied though at an effective temperature
T_{\mathrm{eff}}=T/2
and, therefore, the critical temperatures at which an
equilibrium
transition takes place is given by
T_{c}(h\geq 1)=2T_{c}^{0}
, where
T_{c}^{0}\equiv T_{c}(h=0)=2/\ln(1+\sqrt{2})

[
18
]
. However, this algorithm (
A.5
)
violates
the DBC for
-1<h<1
(with
h\neq 0
). Because, it is impossible to find a unique
T_{\mathrm{eff}}
for which the transition probabilities for all possible values of
\Delta E^{0}
satisfy DBC
[
17
]
. This realizes that the precise ratio of the transition probabilities depends upon the value of
\Delta E^{0}
(promisingly on
h
) and, therefore, it is impossible to define a unique
T_{\mathrm{eff}}
. If a PT takes place in this case, then
T_{c}
should fulfill

T_{c}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0<T_{c}<T_{c}^{0}&\hbox{for }-1<h<0,\;\mathrm{or\;}-8<\varepsilon<0;\\
T_{c}^{0}<T_{c}<2T_{c}^{0}&\hbox{for \; \; }0<h<1,\;\mathrm{or\;}0<\varepsilon<8\texttt{.}\end{array}\right.

(A.6)

Explicitly,
h
can take any real values within the range
-1<h<1
and so
-8<\varepsilon<8
though only some of its values are essentially considered as shown in Figure
3
. For these
h
values,
T_{c}(h,\Delta E^{0})
can be understood from the following argument. With positive
\Delta E^{0}=\{4,8\}
, the ratio (
A.4
) at a temperature
T=1/\beta
becomes
\exp[-\beta(\Delta E^{0}+\varepsilon)]
. Equating this with the ratio (
A.2
) at temperature
T^{0}=1/\beta^{0}
, we get
\beta^{0}\Delta E^{0}=\beta(\Delta E^{0}+\varepsilon)
. Similarly for negative
\Delta E^{0}=\{-8,-4\}
, we get
\beta^{0}\Delta E^{0}=\beta(\Delta E^{0}-\varepsilon)
.
Practically, it holds that,

\beta^{0}|\Delta E^{0}|=\beta(|\Delta E^{0}|+\varepsilon).

(A.7)

This Eq. (
A.7
) permits one to relate
T
(nonequilibrium model) to
T^{0}
(equilibrium model). Since this relation is different for different values of
\Delta E^{0}
, it is not possible to map the probability distribution in the NESS of this model to the equilibrium distributions at a unique temperature
T^{0}
. At the critical temperature
T_{c}\equiv 1/\beta_{c}
, Eq. (
A.7
) implies that
\beta^{0}_{c}|\Delta E^{0}|=\beta_{c}(|\Delta E^{0}|+\varepsilon)
, with
T^{0}_{c}=1/\beta^{0}_{c}
,

T_{c}(\varepsilon,\Delta E^{0})=\left(\frac{|\Delta E^{0}|+\varepsilon}{|\Delta E^{0}|}\right)T_{c}^{0}

(A.8)

If we would argue with
[
17
]
, finding a unique value of
T_{c}
requires to
average
over the different values of
T_{c}
obtained for the possible values of
\Delta E^{0}
. Incredibly, this step is not important for the present approach as we can replace
\varepsilon=h|\Delta E^{0}|
from (
A.3
) into (
A.8
) in which
|\Delta E^{0}|
can be canceled and that it is exempt. Therefore,
T_{c}=(1+h)T_{c}^{0}
get over the result recovered in (
3.19

A.2
FSS and critical exponents

Using the modified Metropolis update rule, we simulate 2D Ising model on a square lattice to study the nature of PT and critical behavior. Noticeably, this behavior is well-marked as various physical quantities of the system possess singularities or discontinuities at the critical temperature. Here, the critical behaviors of
\chi
and
C
are common examples in the Ising model. In statistical physics, it is known that such singularities can be understood as a direct consequence of the fact that correlation length becomes infinitely large in the vicinity of the transition temperature. This indeed represents the correlation between spin positions in the lattice. As a result of its infiniteness, the system becomes scale-invariance which means that the asymptotic behaviors of the relevant physical quantities near the critical point are given by power laws. That is;
M\sim(T-T_{c})^{\delta}
,
\chi\sim|T-T_{c}|^{-\gamma}
,
\xi\sim|T-T_{c}|^{-\nu}
, and
C\sim|T-T_{c}|^{-\alpha}
where
\delta
,
\gamma
,
\nu
,
\alpha
are critical exponents for magnetization, susceptibility, correlation length, and specific heat, respectively. If
\xi(T)
is less than linear size
L
, our MC data will be expected to yield results comparable to an infinite system. As it is known, however, the MC result is limited by finite-size effects for
T
close to
T_{c}
. Since we can only simulate finite systems, it is challenging to get the estimated values of critical exponents
\beta
,
\gamma
, and
\alpha
by varying
T
.

Qualitatively, the effects of finite size of the system can be treated using the following argument in which only essential length near
T_{c}
is considered to be the correlation length. Assume the case of
\chi
for example. If
\xi\gg 1
but
\xi\ll L
, a power law behavior holds. Conversely,
\xi
cannot change appreciably if it is comparable to
L
. As a result, the relation
\chi\sim|\tau|^{-\gamma}
becomes no longer applicable. Such a qualitative change in the behavior of
\chi
and other relevant quantities occurs for
\xi\sim L\sim|T-T_{c}|^{-\nu}
, and thus

|T_{c}-T_{c}(L)|\sim L^{-1/\nu}.

(A.11)

If
L
and
\xi
are considerably the same value, it follows that

\chi(T=T_{c})\approx\chi^{\mathrm{max}}\sim(L^{-1/\nu})^{-\gamma}\sim L^{\gamma/\nu},

(A.12)

which is consistent with the fact that the PT is defined only for systems of infinite size. This relation helps to find the ratio
\gamma/\nu
and such a method is known as FSS analysis, therefore,
\gamma
is determined using the theoretical value of
\nu\approx 1
. Similarly, the same reasoning can be used to find
\beta
. As an illustration, we calculate the exponent
\nu
based on the data of specific heat. The specific free energy

(A.13)

where
\delta=(\beta+\gamma)/\beta
,
\tau=(T-T_{c})/T_{c}
is a reduced temperature and
\tilde{h}
is a reduced magnetic field
3
