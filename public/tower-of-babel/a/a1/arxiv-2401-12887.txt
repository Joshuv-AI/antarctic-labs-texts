Abstract
. Many mathematical statements have the following form. If something is true for all finite subsets of an infinite set I I , then it is true for all of I I . This paper describes some old and new results on infinite sets of linear and polynomial equations with the property that solutions for all finite subsets of the set of equations implies the existence of a solution for the infinite set of equations.

2.
Equations over finite sets

Let
J
be a nonempty set. Let
X=\{x_{j}:j\in J\}
be a set of variables and
let
\Omega=\prod_{j\in J}\Omega_{j}
be the Cartesian product of a family
of nonempty sets. We denote by
f(X)
a statement about the variables

X=\{x_{j}:j\in J\}
.
Other, equally vague words, such as “sentence” or “proposition” or “assertion”,
might substitute for “statement”.
Let
\Lambda
be the set of
J
-tuples
U=(u_{j})_{j\in J}\in\Omega

for which the statement
f(U)
is true.

For example, the statement could be an equation of the form

f(X)=0

in the variables
X=\{x_{j}:j\in J\}
, and the

J
-tuples
U=(u_{j})_{j\in J}\in\Omega
for which the statement is true
are the solutions of the equation, that is, the
J
-tuples
U\in\Omega

such that
f(U)=0
.

Here is a simple result in the case of finite sets
\Omega_{j}
. We recall the

finite intersection property
of subsets of a compact set: If
\Lambda=\{\Lambda_{i}:i\in I\}

is an infinite set of closed subsets of a compact space and if the intersection
of every finite subset of
\Lambda
is nonempty, then the infinite intersection

\bigcap_{i\in I}\Lambda_{i}
is nonempty.

Theorem 1
.

Let
J
be a nonempty finite or infinite set.
Let
X=\{x_{j}:j\in J\}
be a set of variables
and let
\Omega=\prod_{j\in J}\Omega_{j}
be the Cartesian product of a family
of nonempty finite sets
\Omega_{j}
.
Let
I
be an infinite set, and, for all
i\in I
, let
f_{i}(X)

be a statement in which only finitely many variables
x_{j}\in X
occur.
The infinite set of statements
\{f_{i}(X):i\in I\}

has a solution in
\Omega
if and only if, for every finite subset
S
of
I
,
the set of statements
\{f_{i}(X):i\in S\}
has a solution in
\Omega
.

Proof.

If the infinite set of statements
\{f_{i}(X):i\in I\}
has a solution, then every finite subset
of
\{f_{i}(X):i\in I\}
has a solution.

Conversely, suppose that every finite subset of
S
of
I
,
the set of statements
\{f_{i}(X):i\in S\}
has a solution.
With the discrete topology, the finite sets
\Omega_{j}
are compact
and so
\Omega
is compact
with the usual product topology (Tikhonov’s theorem).
For each
i\in I
, let
\Lambda_{i}
be the set of solutions of the
statement
f_{i}(X)
in
\Omega
. Because the statement
f_{i}(X)

contains only finitely many variables
x_{j}\in X
and because the sets
\Omega_{j}

are finite, the solution set
\Lambda_{i}
is closed in
\Omega
.
Because every finite set of statements has a solution, the intersection
of a every finite set of the sets
\Lambda_{i}
is nonempty.
The finite intersection property
implies that
\bigcap_{i\in I}\Lambda_{i}
is nonempty, and so the infinite
set
\{f_{i}(X):i\in I\}
of statements has a solution in
\Omega
.
This completes the proof.
∎

3.
Quadratic equations that do not satisfy “finitely many implies infinitely many”

It is an important observation that there are sets of polynomial equations
such that every proper subset
of the set of equations has a common solution
but not the entire set. Here is a quadratic example due to Alexander Abian
[
3
]
.

Let
\mathbf{F}
be a field, let
J=\mathbf{F}\cup\{*\}
, and let
\mathbf{x}=\{x_{*}\}\cup\{x_{a}:a\in\mathbf{F}\}

be a set of variables. Consider the set of quadratic equations

Q_{\mathbf{F}}=\{(a-x_{*})x_{a}-1=0:a\in\mathbf{F}\}.

If
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbf{F}\times\mathbf{F}^{\mathbf{F}}
is a common solution
of these equations with
x_{*}=u\in\mathbf{F}
, then, for the equation
(u-x_{*})x_{u}-1=0
, we obtain

(u-u)x_{u}-1=0

which is absurd. Therefore, the set of equations
Q_{\mathbf{F}}
has no common solution.

Let
b\in\mathbf{F}
and consider the set of equations obtained by deleting from
Q_{\mathbf{F}}

the single equation
(b-x_{*})x_{b}-1=0
.
The set of equations

Q_{\mathbf{F}}\setminus\{(b-x_{*})x_{b}-1=0\}=\{(a-x_{*})x_{a}-1=0:a\in\mathbf{F}\text{ and }a\neq b\}

has the common solution
x_{*}=b
and
x_{a}=\frac{1}{a-b}
for all
a\neq b
.
In particular, if
\mathbf{F}
is an infinite field, then
every finite subset of the equations in
Q_{\mathbf{F}}
has a common solution
but the infinite set
Q_{\mathbf{F}}

has no common solution.
Thus, “finitely many implies infinitely many” does not necessarily hold for sets of polynomials.

We shall consider three classes of linear equations where the
“finitely many implies infinitely many” principle does hold.
First, we consider linear equations in finitely many variables, that is, expressions of the form

(1)

\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}x_{j}+b=0.

Next, we consider linear equations in infinitely many variables, that is,
expressions of the form

(2)

\sum_{j\in J}a_{j}x_{j}+b=0

where
J
is an infinite set and
a_{j}
is nonzero for only finitely many
j\in J
.
Finally, we discuss linear equations in countably infinitely many variables, that is,
expressions of the form

(3)

\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}a_{j}x_{j}+b=0.

in which there is no restriction on the number of nonzero coefficients
a_{j}
.

4.
Infinite sets of linear equations in
n
variables

Theorem 2
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be the set of linear equations in
n
variables
\mathbf{x}=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}

with coefficients in the field
\mathbf{F}
.
Let
I
be an infinite set and let
\mathcal{L}(I)=\{\ell_{i}:i\in I\}
be a subset of
\mathcal{L}
.
If every finite subset of
\mathcal{L}(I)
has a common solution, then the infinite set
\mathcal{L}(I)

has a common solution.

We shall prove the following stronger statement.

Let
\delta_{i,j}
be the Kronecker delta.

Theorem 3
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be the set of linear equations in
n
variables

\mathbf{x}=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}
with coefficients in the field
\mathbf{F}
.
Let
I
be a nonempty set and let
\mathcal{L}(I)=\{\ell_{i}:i\in I\}
be a subset of
\mathcal{L}
.
If every subset of
\mathcal{L}(I)
of cardinality at most
n+1

has a common solution, then the
\mathcal{L}(I)
has a common solution.

Proof.

We define addition and scalar multiplication of linear equations as follows.
If
\ell_{i}
is the equation
\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{i,j}x_{j}+b_{i}=0
for
i=1,2

and if
c_{1},c_{2}\in\mathbf{F}
, then
c_{1}\ell_{1}+c_{2}\ell_{2}
is the linear equation

\sum_{j=1}^{n}(c_{1}a_{1,j}+c_{2}a_{2,j})x_{j}+(b_{1}+b_{2})=0.

With these operations, the set
\mathcal{L}
is a vector space over the field
\mathbf{F}
of dimension
n+1
with basis

\{\mathbf{e}_{0},\mathbf{e}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{e}_{n}\}

where
\mathbf{e}_{i}
is the equation

\sum_{j=1}^{n}\delta_{i,j}x_{j}+0=0

for
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
and
\mathbf{e}_{0}
is the equation

\sum_{j=1}^{n}0x_{j}+1=0.

The linear equation
\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{i}x_{i}+b=0
is the unique linear combination

\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{i}\mathbf{e}_{j}+b\mathbf{e}_{0}.

Let
W
be the subspace of
\mathcal{L}
generated by
\mathcal{L}(I)

and let
\dim(W)=m
. We have
0\leq m\leq n+1
.
Let
\mathcal{B}=\{\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{m}\}
be a set of linear equations that is a basis for
W
,
and let
\mathbf{u}=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{n})
be a common solution of the
m
equations in
\mathcal{B}
.
Every equation in
S
is a linear combination of the
m
equations in
\mathcal{B}
,
and so
\mathbf{u}=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{n})
is a common solution of the equations in
S
.
This completes the proof.
∎

Let
\mathbf{F}
be an algebraically closed field.
Hilbert’s Nullstellensatz says that every set
\Phi
of polynomials in
\mathbf{F}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]

has a common solution in
\mathbf{F}
if and only if the ideal generated by
\Phi

is a proper ideal of
\mathbf{F}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
.
This is equivalent to the statement that
\Phi
has a common solution
if and only if every finite subset of
\Phi
has a common solution
(because, if every finite subset of
\Phi
has a common solution,
then the equation
\sum_{i=1}^{m}f_{i}g_{i}=1
with polynomials

f_{i}\in\Phi
and
g_{i}\in\mathbf{F}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
is impossible.

We can modify the proof of Theorem
3

to prove a version of the Nullstellensatz for polynomials
with coefficients in a Noetherian ring.

Let
\Phi
be a nonempty set of polynomials.
If every finite subset of
\Phi
has a common solution, then
the set
\Phi
generates a proper ideal of
R[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]

(because the ideal cannot contain 1) .

Theorem 4
.

Let
R
be a Noetherian ring. If
\Phi
is a set of polynomials in
R[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
such
that every finite subset of
\Phi
has a common solution, then
\Phi
has a common solution.

Proof.

By Hilbert’s basis theorem, the polynomial ring
R[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
is Noetherian.
Let
\mathcal{L}(\Phi)
be the ideal generated by
\Phi
, and let
\{\mathbf{e}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{e}_{m}\}

be a finite set of polynomials that generates
\mathcal{L}(\Phi)
.
For all
i\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
, there is a finite set of polynomials
\{f_{i,1},\ldots,f_{i,k_{i}}\}

in
\Phi
and a finite set of polynomials
\{g_{i,1},\ldots,g_{i,k_{i}}\}
in
\mathbf{R}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]

such that

\mathbf{e}_{i}=\sum_{r_{i}=1}^{k_{i}}f_{i,r_{i}}g_{i,r_{i}}.

The finite set of polynomials

\mathcal{F}=\{f_{i,r_{i}}:i\in\{1,\ldots,m\}\text{ and }r_{i}\in\{1,\ldots,k_{i}\}\}

has a common solution
\mathbf{u}=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{n})\in R^{n}
, and so
\mathbf{u}=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{n})

is a common solution of the polynomials in the generating set
\{\mathbf{e}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{e}_{m}\}
.
Every polynomial in
\Phi
, and, indeed, every polynomial in the ideal
\mathcal{L}(\Phi)
,
is of the form
\sum_{i=1}^{m}h_{i}\mathbf{e}_{i}
for polynomials
h_{i}\in R[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
,
and so
\mathbf{u}=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{n})

is a common solution of the polynomials in the
\mathcal{L}(\Phi)
.
This completes the proof.
∎

5.
Infinite sets of linear equations in infinitely many variables,
but with only finitely many summands

The following beautiful argument is due to Alexander Abian.

Theorem 5

(Abian
[
3
]
)
.

Let
\mathbf{F}
be a field and let
I
and
J
be infinite sets.
Let
\mathbf{x}=(x_{j})_{j\in J}
be a sequence of variables
and, for all
i\in I
, let

f_{i}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{j\in J}a_{i,j}x_{j}+b_{i}=0

be a linear equation with coefficients
a_{i,j}\in\mathbf{F}
,
b_{i}\in\mathbf{F}
,
and
a_{i,j}\neq 0
for only finitely many
j
.
Consider the infinite set of equations
\mathcal{L}(I)=\{f_{i}(\mathbf{x})=0:i\in I\}
.
If, for every finite subset
I_{0}
of
I
, the finite set of equations

\mathcal{L}(I_{0})=\{f_{i}(\mathbf{x})=0:i\in I_{0}\}
has a common solution,
then the infinite set
\mathcal{L}(I)
has a common solution.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be the infinite-dimensional vector space
whose vectors are linear equations of the form

f(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{j\in J}a_{j}x_{j}+b=0

where
a_{j}\in\mathbf{F}
,
b\in\mathbf{F}
, and
a_{j}\neq 0
for only finitely many
j\in J
.
Let

\mathcal{L}(I)=\left\{f_{i}(\mathbf{x})=0:i\in I\right\}=\left\{\sum_{j\in J}a_{i,j}x_{j}+b_{i}=0:i\in I\right\}

and let
W
be the subspace of
\mathcal{L}
generated by
\mathcal{L}(I)
.

Let
\mathbf{e}_{0}\in\mathcal{L}
be the equation

\sum_{j\in J}0x_{j}+1=0.

We shall prove that
\mathbf{e}_{0}\notin W
.

If
\mathbf{e}_{0}\in W
, then there is a finite subset
I_{0}
of
I
,
a finite set of equations

\mathcal{L}(I_{0})=\left\{\sum_{j\in J}a_{i,j}x_{j}+b_{i}=0:i\in I_{0}\right\}\subseteq\mathcal{L}(I)

and a finite set of scalars

\{\lambda_{i}:i\in I_{0}\}
such that

\mathbf{e}_{0}=\sum_{i\in I_{0}}\lambda_{i}\left(\sum_{j\in J}a_{i,j}x_{j}+b_{i}=0\right).

It follows that the linear equations

\sum_{j\in J}0x_{j}+1=0

and

\sum_{i\in I_{0}}\lambda_{i}\left(\sum_{j\in J}a_{i,j}x_{j}+b_{i}=0\right)

are the same equation.
Equivalently,

\sum_{j\in J}0x_{j}+1=0

and

\sum_{j\in J}\left(\sum_{i\in I_{0}}\lambda_{i}a_{i,j}\right)x_{j}+\sum_{i\in I_{0}}\lambda_{i}b_{i}=0

are the same equation.
Because the finite set of equations
\mathcal{L}(I_{0})
has a common solution,
there exist scalars
\mathbf{u}=(u_{j})_{j\in J}
such that

\sum_{j\in J}a_{i,j}u_{j}+b_{i}=0\qquad\text{for all $i\in I_{0}$}

and so

\sum_{j\in J}\left(\sum_{i\in I_{0}}\lambda_{i}a_{i,j}\right)u_{j}+\sum_{i\in I_{0}}\lambda_{i}b_{i}=0

It follows that

0=\sum_{j\in J}0u_{j}=1

which is absurd. Therefore,
\mathbf{e}_{0}\notin W
.

Let
\mathcal{B}_{W}
be a basis for the subspace
W

generated by
\mathcal{L}(I)
in the vector space
\mathcal{L}
.
Then
\mathcal{B}_{W}\cup\{\mathbf{e}_{0}\}
is a basis for the subspace

W^{\prime}
generated by
\mathcal{L}(I)\cup\{\mathbf{e}_{0}\}
.
We can extend the set
\mathcal{B}_{W}\cup\{\mathbf{e}_{0}\}
to a basis
\mathcal{B}

for the vector space
\mathcal{L}
.
Let
\alpha:\mathcal{L}\rightarrow\mathbf{F}
be the unique linear functional defined by

\alpha(\mathbf{v})=\begin{cases}0&\text{if $\mathbf{v}\in\mathcal{B}\setminus\{\mathbf{e}_{0}\}$}\\
1&\text{if $\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{e}_{0}$.}\end{cases}

It follows that the restriction of
\alpha
to
W
is 0,
and so the restriction of
\alpha
to the set
\mathcal{L}(I)
is 0.

For all
j\in J
, let
\mathbf{e}_{j}\in\mathcal{L}
be the linear equation
x_{j}=0

and let

u_{j}=\alpha(\mathbf{e}_{j})\in\mathbf{F}.

We shall prove that
\mathbf{u}=(u_{j})_{j\in J}
is a common solution of the equations in
\mathcal{L}(I)
.

For all
i\in I
, the linear equation
\sum_{j\in J}a_{i,j}x_{j}+b_{i}=0
in
\mathcal{L}(I)

can be written in the form

\sum_{j\in J}a_{i,j}\mathbf{e}_{j}+b_{i}\mathbf{e}_{0}

and so

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\alpha\left(\sum_{j\in J}a_{i,j}x_{j}+b_{i}=0\right)

\displaystyle=\alpha\left(\sum_{j\in J}a_{i,j}\mathbf{e}_{j}+b_{i}\mathbf{e}_{0}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in J}a_{i,j}\alpha\left(\mathbf{e}_{j}\right)+b_{i}\alpha\left(\mathbf{e}_{0}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in J}a_{i,j}u_{j}+b_{i}\alpha.

This completes the proof.
∎

6.
Infinite sets of linear equations in infinitely many variables
and with infinitely many summands

Let
p
and
q
satisfy
p>1
and

\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}=1.

For sequences
\mathbf{a}=(a_{j})_{j=1}^{\infty}\in\ell^{p}
and

\mathbf{u}=(u_{j})_{j=1}^{\infty}\in\ell^{q}
, we define the sequence

(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{u})=(a_{j}u_{j})_{j=1}^{\infty}.

By Hölder’s inequality,
(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{u})\in\ell^{1}

and

\|(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{u})\|_{1}=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}|a_{j}u_{j}|\leq\|\mathbf{a}\|_{p}\|\mathbf{u}\|_{q}.

Let
I
be an infinite set, and let

\mathbf{a}_{i}=(a_{i,j})_{j=1}^{\infty}\in\ell^{p}
and
b_{i}\in\mathbf{R}
for all
i\in I
.
For all
i\in I
we have the linear equation in infinitely many variables

(4)

(\mathbf{a}_{i},\mathbf{x})=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}a_{i,j}x_{j}=b_{i}.

Consider the set
\mathcal{L}(I)
of infinitely many linear equations in infinitely many variables

\mathcal{L}(I)=\left\{(\mathbf{a}_{i},\mathbf{x})=b_{i}:i\in I\right\}=\left\{\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}a_{i,j}x_{j}=b_{i}:i\in I\right\}.

A solution of the set
\mathcal{L}(I)
of equations is a sequence
\mathbf{u}\in\ell^{q}
such that

(\mathbf{a}_{i},\mathbf{u})=b_{i}
for all
i\in I
.

It is
not true
that every set of linear equations in infinitely many
variables satisfies the “finitely many implies infinitely many“ paradigm.
Here is a simple example of an infinite set of linear equations for which every finite subset
has a solution but the infinite set has no solution.
Let
\mathbf{N}=\{1,2,3,\ldots\}
be the set of positive integers.
For all
i\in\mathbf{N}
, let
\mathbf{a}_{i}=(a_{i,j})_{j=1}^{\infty}
be the sequence
defined by

a_{i,j}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if $j\leq i$}\\
0&\text{if $j>i$.}\end{cases}

For all
p\geq 1
we have
\mathbf{a}_{i}\in\ell^{p}
and

\|\mathbf{a}_{i}\|_{p}=i^{1/p}.

Let
b_{i}=i
for all
i\in\mathbf{N}
.
Consider the infinite set of linear equations

\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{N})=\{(\mathbf{a}_{i},\mathbf{x})=i:i\in\mathbf{N}\}=\left\{\sum_{j=1}^{i}x_{j}=i:i\in\mathbf{N}\right\}.

For all
n\in\mathbf{N}
, define the sequence
\mathbf{u}^{(n)}=(u_{j})_{j=1}^{\infty}\in\ell^{q}
by

u_{j}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if $j\leq n$}\\
0&\text{if $j>n$.}\end{cases}

For all
i\in\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
we have

(5)

(\mathbf{a}_{i},\mathbf{u}^{(n)})=\sum_{j=1}^{i}u_{j}=i

and so every finite subset of the infinite set
\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{N})
has a solution in
\ell^{q}
.

If a sequence
\mathbf{u}\in\ell^{q}
satisfies every equation
in the set
\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{N})
, then we have

i=(\mathbf{a}_{i},\mathbf{u})=\left\|(\mathbf{a}_{i},\mathbf{u})\right\|_{1}\leq\left\|\mathbf{a}_{i}\right\|_{p}\left\|\mathbf{u}\right\|_{q}=i^{1/p}\left\|\mathbf{u}\right\|_{q}

and so

i^{1/q}=i^{1-(1/p)}\leq\left\|\mathbf{u}\right\|_{q}<\infty

for all
i\in\mathbf{N}
, which is absurd.
Thus, the set
\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{N})
has no solution in
\ell^{q}
.

With an additional condition, one can prove that
sets of linear equations in infinitely many variables
do satisfy “finitely many implies infinitely many“.
The following theorem of Abian and Eslami
[
4
]

extends a classical result of F. Riesz
[
12
]
.

Let
M>0.

Extending a classical result of F. Riesz
[
12
]

(see also Banach
[
5
, p. 47]
),
Abian and Eslami
[
4
]
proved that if

\mathcal{L}(I)=\left\{(\mathbf{a}_{i},\mathbf{x})=b_{i}:i\in I\right\}
is an infinite set
of linear equations such that every finite subset
\mathcal{L}(I_{0})
has a solution

\mathbf{u}^{(0)}\in\ell^{q}

with
\|\mathbf{u}^{(0)}\|_{q}\leq M
, then the infinite set of equations has a solution

\mathbf{u}\in\ell^{q}
with
\|\mathbf{u}\|_{q}\leq M
.

Problem 1
.

Is there a condition weaker than that every finite subset of an infinite set
S
of
linear equations has a solution

\mathbf{x}
with
\ell_{q}
norm at most
M
that still guaranties a solution of every equation in
S
?

The linear equation (
4
) can be written in the form

\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}f_{i,j}(x_{j})=b_{i}

where

f_{i,j}(x_{j})=a_{i,j}x_{j}

is a linear polynomial with constant term 0.
Suppose we replace the linear polynomial with a polynomial of degree at most
d

and with constant term 0:

f_{i,j}(x_{j})=\sum_{k=1}^{d}a_{i,j,k}x_{j}^{k}.

For all
i\in\mathbf{N}
and
b_{i}\in\mathbf{R}
we obtain a polynomial equation in infinitely many variables

\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}f_{i,j}(x_{j})=b_{i}

Nathanson and Ross
[
9
]
proved the following.

Theorem 6
.

Let
d
be a positive integer and let
q>d
.
For all
i\in\mathbf{N}
and
k\in\{1,2,\ldots,d\}
, let

\left(a_{i,j,k}\right)_{j=1}^{\infty}\in\ell^{q/(q-k)}.

Define the polynomials

f_{i,j}(x_{j})=\sum_{k=1}^{d}a_{i,j,k}x_{j}^{k}

of degree at most
d
with constant term 0.
For every sequence of real numbers
(b_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}
,
consider the countably infinite set
\mathcal{S}
of polynomial equations
in infinitely many variables

\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{N})=\left\{\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}f_{i,j}(x_{j})=b_{i}:i\in\mathbf{N}\right\}

Let
M>0
.
There exists a sequence
\mathbf{u}=(u_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}\in\ell^{q}
such that
\|\mathbf{u}\|_{q}\leq M

and
\mathbf{u}
is a common solution of the equations in
\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{N})
if and only if
every finite subset of
\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{N})
has a common solution
\mathbf{u}^{(0)}\in\ell^{q}

with
\|\mathbf{u}^{(0)}\|_{q}\leq M
.

Appendix A
Theorems of Rado and de Bruijn-Erdős

Let
X
be a set.
Let
\mathcal{P}(X)
be the
power set
of
X
,
that is, the set of all subsets of
X
and let
\mathcal{F}^{*}(X)

be the set of all nonempty finite subsets of
X
.

Let
I
be a set and let
\{X_{i}:i\in I\}
be a sequence of nonempty subsets of
X
.
A
choice function
is a function

\varphi:I\rightarrow\mathcal{P}(X)
such that

\varphi(i)\in X_{i}
for all
i\in I
.

The following is
Rado’s selection lemma
.

Theorem 7

(Rado
[
10
]
)
.

Let
I
be an infinite set, and, for all
i\in I
,
let
X_{i}
be a nonempty finite set.
For all
A\in\mathcal{F}^{*}(I)
, let
\varphi_{A}:A\rightarrow\bigcup_{i\in I}X_{i}
be a choice function,
that is,
\varphi_{A}(a)\in X_{a}
for all
a\in A
.
There exists a choice function
\Phi:I\rightarrow X
such that, for all
A\in\mathcal{F}^{*}(I)
,
there exists
B\in\mathcal{F}^{*}(I)

with
A\subseteq B
such that
\varphi_{B}(a)=\Phi(a)
for all
a\in A
.

Proof.

The following proof is due to Gottschalk
[
7
]
.

With the discrete topology, the finite set
X_{i}
is compact for all
i\in I

and so the set
\Omega=\prod_{i\in I}X_{i}

with the usual product topology is compact (Tikhonov’s theorem).
The elements of
\Omega
are choice functions
\varphi:i\mapsto\Omega

with
\varphi(i)\in X_{i}
for all
i\in I
.

Let
A\in\mathcal{F}^{*}(I)
. For every set
B\in\mathcal{F}^{*}(I)
such that
A\subseteq B
,
the restriction of the choice function
\varphi_{B}
to the set
A
defines a choice function on
A
, that is, the
|A|
-tuple
(\varphi_{B}(a):a\in A)
in
\prod_{a\in A}X_{a}
.

Let
E_{A}
be the set of all
\varphi\in\Omega
such that there exists

B\in\mathcal{F}^{*}(I)
such that
A\subseteq B
and
\varphi(a)=\varphi_{B}(a)
for all
a\in A
.
There exists
\varphi\in\Omega
such that
\varphi(a)=\varphi_{A}(a)
for all
a\in A
,
and so
E_{a}
is nonempty.
There are only finitely many
|A|
-tuples in
\prod_{a\in A}X_{a}

that are restrictions of choice functions

\varphi_{B}
with
A\subseteq B
, and so
E_{A}
is a nonempty closed subset of
\Omega
.
Moreover, if
A,A^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}^{*}(I)
and
A\subseteq A^{\prime}
, then
\varphi\in E_{A^{\prime}}
implies there
exists
B^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}^{*}(I)
such that
A^{\prime}\subseteq B^{\prime}
and
\varphi(a^{\prime})=\varphi_{B^{\prime}}(a^{\prime})

for all
a^{\prime}\in A^{\prime}
. Because
A\subseteq A^{\prime}
, we have
\varphi(a)=\varphi_{B^{\prime}}(a)

for all
a\in A
, and so
\varphi\in E_{A}
. Thus,
E_{A^{\prime}}\subseteq E_{A}
.

Let
A_{1},\ldots,A_{n}\in\mathcal{F}^{*}(I)
and let
A=\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}A_{i}\in\mathcal{F}^{*}(I)
.
For all
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, the inclusion
A_{i}\subseteq A
implies
E_{A}\subseteq E_{A_{i}}
.
It follows that
E_{A}\subseteq\bigcap_{i=1}^{n}E_{A_{i}}
and so the closed set

\bigcap_{i=1}^{n}E_{A_{i}}
is nonempty.
Thus, the set of closed sets
\{E_{A}:A\in\mathcal{F}^{*}(I)
has the finite intersection property.
By compactness, there exists
\Phi\in\bigcap_{A\in\mathcal{F}^{*}(I)}E_{A}
.
This completes the proof.
∎

Corollary 2

(Gottschalk
[
7
]
)
.

Let
\{X_{i}:i\in I\}
be an infinite set of nonempty finite sets.
The set
X=\bigcup_{i\in I}X_{i}
has a one-to-one choice function if and only if
every finite subset has a one-to-one choice function.

A
k
-coloring
of the vertices of a graph
G=(V,E)
is a function

\chi:G\rightarrow\{1,2,\ldots,k\}

such that if
\{v_{1},v_{2}\}
is an edge, then
\chi(v_{1})\neq\chi(v_{2})
.
The graph
G=(V,E)
is
infinite
if its vertex set
V
is infinite.

Theorem 8

(de Bruijn and Erdős
[
6
]
)
.

Let
G
be an infinite graph. If every finite subgraph of
G
is
k
-colorable, then
G
is
k
-colorable.

Proof.

To every vertex
v\in V
we assign the set
I=\{1,2,\ldots,k\}
.
For every finite subset
W
of
V
, there is a
k
-coloring function
\chi_{W}
,
and
\chi_{W}
is a choice function.
Let
\chi:V\rightarrow I
be the choice function obtained from Rado’s selection lemma.
Let
e=\{v_{1},v_{2}\}

be an edge in the graph
G
. Then
e
is a 2-element subset of
V

and there is a finite set
W
of
V
such that
e\subseteq W
and
\chi(v_{1})=\chi_{W}(v_{1})

and
\chi(v_{2})=\chi_{W}(v_{2})
. Because
\chi_{W}
is a
k
-coloring of a finite subgraph, we have

\chi(v_{1})\neq\chi(v_{2}
and so
\chi
is a
k
-coloring of
G
.
This completes the proof.
∎

Appendix B
Helly’s theorem

Theorem 9

(Radon
[
11
]
)
.

Let
A
be a set of
n+2
distinct points in
\mathbf{R}^{n}
.
There is a partition of
A
into nonempty pairwise disjoint sets

A=A_{1}\cup A_{2}\qqand A_{1}\cap A_{2}=\emptyset

such that

\conv(A_{1})\cap\conv(A_{2})\neq\emptyset.

Proof.

Let
A=\{\mathbf{a}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{a}_{n+2}\}
, where

\mathbf{a}_{j}=\left(\begin{matrix}a_{1,j}\\
a_{2,j}\\
\vdots\\
a_{n,j}\end{matrix}\right)\in\mathbf{R}^{n}

for all
j\in\{1,2,\ldots,n+2\}
.
Every set of
n+1
homogeneous linear equations
in
n+2
variables has a nonzero solution. Thus,

(6)

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n+2}a_{i,j}x_{j}

\displaystyle=0\qquad\text{for $i\in\{1,2,\ldots,n\}$}

(7)

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n+2}x_{j}

\displaystyle=0.

has a nonzero solution
\mathbf{u}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}u_{1}\\
\vdots\\
u_{n+2}\end{smallmatrix}\right)
.
Let

A_{1}=\{\mathbf{a}_{j}\in A:u_{j}>0\}\qqand A_{2}=\{\mathbf{a}_{j}\in A:u_{j}\leq 0\}.

Equation (
7
) implies that the vector
\mathbf{u}

has both positive and negative coordinates, and so
the sets
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
are nonempty and partition
A
.
Moreover,

s=\sum_{\mathbf{a}_{j}\in A_{1}}u_{j}=\sum_{\mathbf{a}_{j}\in A_{2}}(-u_{j})>0.

Therefore,

\sum_{\mathbf{a}_{1}\in A_{1}}\frac{u_{j}}{s}=\sum_{\mathbf{a}_{j}\in A_{2}}\frac{-u_{j}}{s}=1.

The set of
n
linear equations (
6
)
is equivalent to the vector equation

\sum_{j=1}^{n+2}x_{j}\mathbf{a}_{j}=\mathbf{0}.

We obtain the vector relation

\sum_{\mathbf{a}_{j}\in A_{1}}u_{j}\mathbf{a}_{j}=\sum_{\mathbf{a}_{j}\in A_{2}}(-u_{j})\mathbf{a}_{j}.

Dividing by
s
gives the convex combinations

\sum_{\mathbf{a}_{j}\in A_{1}}\frac{u_{j}}{s}\mathbf{a}_{j}=\sum_{\mathbf{a}_{j}\in A_{2}}\frac{-u_{j}}{s}\mathbf{a}_{j}\in\conv(A_{1})\cap\conv(A_{2})

and so
\conv(A_{1})\cap\conv(A_{2})\neq\emptyset
.
This completes the proof.
∎

Theorem 10

(Helly
[
8
]
)
.

Let
m\geq n+2
and let
K_{1},\ldots,K_{m}
be convex subsets of
\mathbf{R}^{n}
.
If every
n+1
sets
K_{i}
have a nonempty intersection,
then
\bigcap_{i=1}^{m}K_{i}\neq\emptyset
.

Let
I
be an infinite set and let
\mathcal{K}=\{K_{i}:i\in I\}
be a set
of closed convex subsets of
\mathbf{R}^{n}
that contains at least one compact
convex set. If every
n+1
sets in
\mathcal{K}
have a nonempty intersection,
then
\bigcap_{i\in I}K_{i}
is nonempty.

Proof.

The proof is by induction on
m
. Let
m=n+2
.
By the induction hypothesis, for all
j\in\{1,\ldots,n+2\}
there is a
vector

\mathbf{a}_{j}\in\bigcap_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1\\
i\neq j\end{subarray}}^{n+2}K_{i}.

If
\mathbf{a}_{j}=\mathbf{a}_{j^{\prime}}
for some
j\neq j^{\prime}
, then

\mathbf{a}_{j}=\mathbf{a}_{j^{\prime}}\in\bigcap_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1\\
i\neq j^{\prime}\end{subarray}}^{n+2}K_{i}\subseteq K_{j}

and so
\mathbf{a}_{j}\in\bigcap_{i=1}^{n+2}K_{i}\neq\emptyset

and we are done.

If
\mathbf{a}_{j}\neq\mathbf{a}_{j^{\prime}}
for all
j\neq j^{\prime}
, then
A=\{\mathbf{a}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{a}_{n+2}\}

is a set of
n+2
distinct vectors in
\mathbf{R}^{n}
.
By Radon’s theorem (Theorem
9
),
there is a partition
A=A_{1}\cup A_{2}
and a vector
\mathbf{v}
such that

\mathbf{v}\in\conv(A_{1})\cap\conv(A_{2})
.
We shall prove that
\mathbf{v}\in\bigcap_{i=1}^{n+2}K_{i}
.

Let
j\in\{1,\ldots,n+2\}
.
If
\mathbf{a}_{j}\in A_{1}
, then
\mathbf{a}_{i}\in K_{j}
for all
i\neq j
.
It follows that
A_{2}\subseteq K_{j}
and, because the set
K_{j}
is convex, we have

\mathbf{v}\in\conv(A_{1})\cap\conv(A_{2})\subseteq\conv(A_{2})\subseteq K_{j}.

Similarly, if
\mathbf{a}_{j}\in A_{2}
, then
\mathbf{v}\in K_{j}
.
Thus,
\mathbf{v}\in K_{j}
for all
j\in\{1,\ldots,n+2\}
. This completes the proof for
m=n+2
.

Let
m\geq n+2
and assume that Helly’s theorem holds for
m
sets.
Given
m+1
convex sets
K_{1},\ldots,K_{m-1},K_{m},K_{m+1}
,
we simply apply Helly’s theorem to the
m
convex sets

K_{1},\ldots,K_{m-1},K^{\prime}_{m}
with
K^{\prime}_{m}=K_{m}\cap K_{m+1}
, and we are done.

Let
I
be an infinite set, and
let
\mathcal{K}=\{K_{i}:i\in I\}
be a set of closed convex sets
such that every
n+1
sets in
\mathcal{K}
have a nonempty intersection.
Let
K_{0}\in K
be compact.
Then
\mathcal{K}_{0}=\{K_{0}\cap K_{i}:i\in I\setminus\{0\}\}
is a set
of nonempty closed subsets of the compact set
K_{0}
.
The finite form of Helly’s theorem implies that every finite intersection
of sets in
\mathcal{K}_{0}
is nonempty.
The finite intersection property for closed compact sets implies that

\emptyset\neq\bigcap_{i\in I\setminus\{0\}}(K_{0}\cap K_{i})\subseteq\bigcap_{i\in I}K_{i}.

This completes the proof.
∎
