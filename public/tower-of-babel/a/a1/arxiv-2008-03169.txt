Critical exponent ν \nu of the Ising model in three dimensions with long-range correlated site disorder analyzed with Monte Carlo techniques Preprint: preprint number
Stanislav Kazmin, Wolfhard Janke

Abstract
We study the critical behavior of the Ising model in three dimensions on a lattice with site disorder by using Monte Carlo simulations. The disorder is either uncorrelated or long-range correlated with correlation function that decays according to a power-law r − a r^{-a} . We derive the critical exponent of the correlation length ν \nu and the confluent correction exponent ω \omega in dependence of a a by combining different concentrations of defects 0.05 ≤ p d ≤ 0.4 0.05\leq p_{d}\leq 0.4 into one global fit ansatz and applying finite-size scaling techniques. We simulate and study a wide range of different correlation exponents 1.5 ≤ a ≤ 3.5 1.5\leq a\leq 3.5 as well as the uncorrelated case a = ∞ a=\infty and are able to provide a global picture not yet known from previous works. Additionally, we perform a dedicated analysis of our long-range correlated disorder ensembles and provide estimates for the critical temperatures of the system in dependence of the correlation exponent a a and the concentrations of defects p d p_{d} . We compare our results to known results from other works and to the conjecture of Weinrib and Halperin: ν = 2 / a \nu=2/a and discuss the occurring deviations.



I
Introduction

The influence of quenched disorder on phase transition properties of a system is of great importance as many real-world materials show defects or impurities.
The simplest way to introduce the disorder is by assuming it to be point-wise and uncorrelated.
A prominent achievement in describing the critical behavior of such systems was done by
Harris 1974

Harris 1974
.
The result is known as
Harris criterion
.
It states that if the system has a negative specific heat exponent in the pure case (without disorder,
\alpha_{\text{pure}}<0
) the disorder does not influence the system’s universality class.
On the other hand, for
\alpha_{\text{pure}}>0
the disorder will change the system’s universality class.
This universality class will have new critical exponents which will not depend on the disorder concentration.
Various studies
Ballesteros et al. 1998a
;
Folk et al. 2003
;
Calabrese et al. 2003
;
Berche et al. 2004
;
Murtazaev et al. 2004
confirmed the change of the universality class of the three-dimensional Ising model for which
\alpha_{\text{pure}}>0
is true.

However, in nature the disorder usually comes with a certain structure.
One possible way to introduce such disorder to a model is by adding a spatial correlation to the disorder.
For a magnetic system this could be nonmagnetic lines or planes or clustered nonmagnetic impurities.
Other interesting areas are magnetic foams and magnetic elements in porous media
MacFarland et al. 1996
;
Paredes and Vásquez 2006
.
The correlated disorder in systems was intensively studied with the help of the renormalization group theory by
Weinrib and Halperin 1983

Weinrib and Halperin 1983
and the result is known as the
extended Harris criterion
.
It states that a system with long-range correlated disorder where the spatial disorder correlation follows a power-law
\propto r^{-a}
will change its universality class if
a<d
and otherwise the standard Harris criterion will be recovered.
Further, they claim that the critical exponent of the correlation length
\nu
in the long-range correlated three-dimensional Ising model is given by

\displaystyle\nu=\frac{2}{a}\;.

(1)

They argue, but do not prove rigorously, that this result is exact.
Several studies dealt with the Ising model with correlated disorder in two dimensions by applying Monte Carlo techniques
Chatelain 2014
;
Chatelain 2017
or renormalization group techniques
Dudka et al. 2016
.
In three dimensions Monte Carlo simulations were performed in Refs.
Ballesteros and Parisi 1999
;
Prudnikov et al. 2005
;
Ivaneyko et al. 2008
;
Herrmanns 1999
;
Ivaneyko et al. 2007
;
Marqués 2009
;
Wang et al. 2019
while renormalization group techniques were used in Refs.
Weinrib and Halperin 1983
;
Prudnikov et al. 2000
.
While it is generally accepted that the correlated disorder case belongs to a new universality class, the quantitative results and in particular the claim given in
Eq.

1
are controversially discussed.
One condition which is often overseen when assuming
Eq.

1
is that
d=4-\epsilon\approx 4
and
a=4-\delta\approx 4
is a necessary condition in Ref.
Weinrib and Halperin 1983
.
So it remains unclear which range of
a
values fulfills this requirement.
As a further reinforcement of the prediction given in
Eq.

1
,
Honkonen and Nalimov 1989

Honkonen and Nalimov 1989
claimed that
Eq.

1
is exact to all orders in the
\epsilon
-
\delta
-expansion.
This has been further analyzed in Refs.
Korzhenevskii et al. 1994
;
Korzhenevskii et al. 1995
.

We extensively analyzed a three-dimensional Ising lattice with power-law correlated site disorder by using Monte Carlo techniques.
In contrast to previous works we performed simulations for various different correlation strengths
a
and a wide range of disorder concentrations
p_{d}
.
We focused on the critical exponent of the correlation length
\nu
and the confluent correction exponent
\omega
and obtained a global picture of their behaviors in the long-range correlated cases and in the uncorrelated disorder case.
Additionally, we can present a rich palette of critical temperatures for various
a
and
p_{d}
.

The rest of the paper is structured as follows.
In Section
II
we specify our model and the details of the performed simulations.
In Section
III
we analyze the disorder realizations to confirm the desired power-law behavior.
The main analysis of the Monte Carlo simulations of the Ising model and the obtained results are contained in Section
IV
.
We present the derivation of the critical exponent
\nu
as well as the correction exponent
\omega
.
We compare our results to the Weinrib and Halperin conjecture,
\nu=2/a
, and to the known results.
Finally we obtain critical temperatures for different concentrations and correlation exponents.
A conclusion in Section
V
completes this work.

Model and Simulation Details

II.1
Ising Model with Site Disorder

We will not discuss the standard Ising model here and refer to
Landau and Binder 2009
;
Newman and Barkema 1999
as a good starting point for readers who need a deeper background.
For the rest of the paper we will deal with the Ising model with site disorder which we will refer to as
disordered Ising model

The Ising model with random couplings,
i.e.,
bond disorder, is also called
“disordered Ising model”
in the literature.
.
The Hamiltonian of the Ising model with site disorder has a very similar form to the standard Ising model

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}=-J\sum_{\langle xy\rangle}\eta_{x}\eta_{y}s_{x}s_{y}-h\sum_{x}\eta_{x}s_{x}\;,

(2)

where the spins can take the values
s_{x}=\pm 1
and the defect variables can be
\eta_{x}=1
when the spin is present at site
x
and
\eta_{x}=0
when the site
x
is empty (a defect).
The sum runs over all next-neighbors denoted by
\langle xy\rangle
.
The coupling constant is set to
J=1
on the whole lattice and we work without an external magnetic field,
i.e.,

h=0
.

We distinguish between two different disorder types.
The first type is the
uncorrelated disorder
or random disorder.
In this case the defects are chosen randomly according to the probability density

\displaystyle p\left(\eta\right)=p_{s}\delta(\eta)+p_{d}\delta(\eta-1)\;,

(3)

where
p_{s}
is the concentration of spins,
p_{d}=1-p_{s}
is the concentration of defects and
\delta
is the Dirac-delta distribution.

The second type is the
correlated disorder
.
In this case the probability density for the defects is again given by
Eq.

3
.
However, now additionally the spatial correlation between the defects decays according to a power-law

\displaystyle\langle\eta_{x}\eta_{y}\rangle\propto\frac{1}{r(x,y)^{a}}\;,

(4)

where
r(x,y)
is the distance between sites
x
and
y
and
a\geq 0
is the
correlation exponent
.
Note, that for both cases we work in the so-called grand-canonical approach where the desired concentration
p_{d}
is a mean value over a large number of realizations while for each separate realization
p_{d}
can vary.

According to the Harris criterion and the extended Harris criterion the disordered three-dimensional Ising model falls into three different universality classes in dependence of the correlation exponent
a
and the concentration of defects
p_{d}
.
The pure case where no defects are present (
p_{d}=0

a>d
and the correlated case for

II.2
Monte Carlo Simulation Details

We performed Monte Carlo simulations of the disordered Ising model and used the Swendsen-Wang multi-cluster update algorithm
Swendsen and Wang 1987
.
The linear lattice sizes of our cubic lattices were in the range between
L=8
and
L=256
and we chose periodic boundary conditions in each direction.
The correlation exponent values were
a=
1.5, 2.0, 2.5, 3.0, 3.5 and
\infty
which we will use symbolically for the uncorrelated case.
For each
a
value we simulated eight defect concentrations
p_{d}=
0.05, 0.1, 0.15, 0.2, 0.25, 0.3, 0.35 and 0.4.
After a thermalization period of
500
sweeps we performed
N=$10\,000$
measurement sweeps at each considered simulation point
\beta_{\text{sim}}=1/({k_{B}}T_{\text{sim}})
.
Throughout the paper we will refer to the inverse temperature defined by
\beta=1/({k_{B}}T)
simply as
“temperature”
.
The temperatures were first chosen in a wide range and with larger spacing for small lattices.
After the first analyses refined ranges (regions around the critical points for considered observables for finite lattice sizes
L
) were estimated and larger lattice sizes were simulated at less temperatures.
For each parameter tuple
(a,p_{d},L)
we simulated
N_{c}=$1000$
disorder realizations.
After each sweep we measured and stored the total energy
E

\displaystyle E=-J\sum_{\langle xy\rangle}\eta_{x}\eta_{y}s_{x}s_{y}\;,

(5)

and the total magnetization of the system
M

\displaystyle M=\sum_{x}\eta_{x}s_{x}\;.

(6)

At the end we had two-dimensional arrays of values
E_{i}^{c}
and
M_{i}^{c}
where
i=1,\dots,N
and
c=1,\dots,N_{c}
for each parameter tuple
(a,p_{d},L,\beta_{\text{sim}})
.
This was needed in order to apply the reweighting technique in later analysis.

III
Correlated Disorder Analysis

Before we move to the analysis of the Monte Carlo simulations of the Ising model we first take a look at the site disorder realization and analyze the generated ensembles.
It is a necessary step to gain control over the correlation exponents
a
of the disorder ensembles on which we will perform the simulations later on.

III.1
Disorder Generation

In this work we mainly study the Ising model on a lattice with uncorrelated and long-range correlated site disorder.
An important part is the generation of the site disorder for later Monte Carlo simulations.
The
uncorrelated disorder
case is realized by setting the defect variables
\eta_{x}
for each site
x
of the lattice according to

\displaystyle\eta_{x}=\begin{cases}0\quad\text{if }R_{x}\leq p_{d}\\
1\quad\text{else}\end{cases}\;,

(7)

where
0\leq R_{x}<1
is a uniform random number drawn for each site
x
.

For the case of
long-range correlated disorder
let us first define the correlation function
C_{\eta}
between two defects
\eta
at sites
x
and
y
at a distance
r=\left\lvert x-y\right\rvert

\displaystyle C_{\eta}(r)=\left.{\langle\eta(x)\eta(y)\rangle}\right|_{\left\lvert x-y\right\rvert=r}\;.

(8)

In this work we assume a
power-law decay
of the correlation function for large distances
r\gg 1

\displaystyle C_{\eta}(r)\propto r^{-a}\;.

(9)

We used a modified Fourier method by
Zierenberg et al. 2017

Zierenberg et al. 2017
for the generation of long-range correlated disorder.
Initially the Fourier method was introduced by
Makse et al. 1995

Makse et al. 1995
.
The code linked in
Zierenberg et al. 2017
was used in this work.
We will not discuss the details of the generation and only sketch the process:

1.

Generate uncorrelated, normally distributed random variables.

2.

Perform a Fourier transformation of these variables.

3.

Correlate the transformed variables by multiplying with a spectral density generated from a chosen correlation function
C_{0}
.

4.

Fourier transform the correlated variables back to real space.

5.

Truncate the final variables to
\{0,1\}
such that the mean concentration of zeros equals the desired concentration of defects
p_{d}
.

The resulting
\eta
variables are correlated and their correlation function is approximately given by
C_{0}
.
This approximation comes from the fact that the truncation in step 5 is not mathematically exact and introduces deviations from the desired function
C_{0}
.

In order to overcome the infinity at
r=0
in
Eq.

9
we used a slightly modified correlation function

\displaystyle C_{0}(r)\propto\left(1+r^{2}\right)^{-a/2}\;,

(10)

which asymptotically approaches
Eq.

9
for large distances,

\displaystyle C_{0}(r)\rightarrow r^{-a}\quad\text{for}\;\;r\rightarrow\infty\;.

(11)

We generated ensembles of disorder realizations by providing two parameters: the correlation decay exponent
a
and the concentration of defects
p_{d}
.
Because the step 5 is mathematically not exact and also because a modified correlation function was used, we verified both values
a
and
p_{d}
for each ensemble numerically.

III.3
Mean Correlation Exponent

The correlation function
C_{\eta}(r)
was calculated as a mean over all configurations for each parameter tuple (
a
,
p_{d}
,
L
).
It was measured for two different distance directions (along the
x
-axis and along the diagonal),

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{1}

\displaystyle=(1,0,0)^{T}\;,\quad\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{2}=(1,1,1)^{T}\;,

(12)

and all possible distances in the corresponding direction.
The correlation function was calculated by

\displaystyle C_{\eta}(r)=\langle\eta_{x}\eta_{y}\rangle=\left<\frac{\mathcal{C}}{N_{r}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y\\
y-x=r\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{i}\end{subarray}}(\eta_{x}-\overline{p}_{d})(\eta_{y}-\overline{p}_{d})\right>\;,

(13)

where
\mathcal{C}
is the normalization constant such that
C_{\eta}(0)=1
and
N_{r}
is the number of possible realizations of the distance
r
on the lattice.
From the chosen
\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{i}
vectors and from periodic boundary conditions it follows that

\displaystyle N_{r}=\begin{cases}V/2&\text{for }r=L/2\;\text{and}\;r=\sqrt{3}L/2\\
V&\text{else}\end{cases}\;.

(14)

The sum in
Eq.

13
runs over all site pairs
x
and
y
which have the vector distance
r\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{i}
where
i=1,2
.
The normalization constant turns out to be

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}=\frac{1}{\overline{p}_{d}(1-\overline{p}_{d})}\;.

(15)

Once the correlation functions defined through
Eq.

13
were measured for each disorder ensemble, we had to obtain the correlation exponent
a
.
We performed a fit to the linearized ansatz on a logarithmic scale corresponding to the asymptotic behavior of
Eq.

9

\displaystyle\ln C_{\eta}(r)=-a\ln r+B\;,

(16)

where
a
is the desired decay exponent.
We had to find a minimal distance
r_{\min}
included into the fits in order to obtain the correlation exponent for
r>r_{\min}\gg 1
where the assumption of a power-law decay is valid.
We used the condition that
r_{\min}
is the distance where the relative deviation between
C_{0}
and
C_{\eta}
became less than a threshold value of
\epsilon_{C}=0.05
for the first time,

\displaystyle\frac{C_{\eta}(r_{\min})-C_{0}(r_{\min})}{C_{\eta}(r_{\min})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{r_{\min}^{-a}-(1+r_{\min}^{2})^{-a/2}}{r_{\min}^{-a}}\leq\epsilon_{C}=0.05\;.

(17)

Note that the amplitudes for
C_{0}
and
C_{\eta}
were omitted as we assume them to be equal and to cancel in
Eq.

17
.

Eq.

17
leads to the condition

\displaystyle r_{\min}(a)\geq\left((1-\epsilon_{C})^{-2/a}-1\right)^{-1/2}\;.

(18)

Furthermore we had to set a maximum distance
r_{\max}
to exclude the noisy tail of the correlation function and possible finite-size effects.
Here we have chosen the distance
r_{\max}
where the absolute value of the measured correlation function
\left\lvert C_{\eta}\right\rvert
was below a minimal threshold value of
C_{\min}=10^{-5}
for the first time,

\displaystyle\left\lvert C_{\eta}\left(r_{\max}\right)\right\rvert\leq C_{\min}=10^{-5}\;.

(19)

For small lattices with
L\leq 20
and weak correlations (large
a
) sometimes the found
r_{\min}
and
r_{\max}
where too close together or even
r_{\min}>r_{\max}
.
Is such cases we reduced
r_{\min}
until a fit with 4 degrees of freedom was possible.

IV
Finite-Size Scaling Analysis

We will now discuss the extraction of the critical exponent of the correlation length
\nu
and the confluent correction exponent
\omega
.
For the finite-size scaling analysis we chose the derivative with respect to the inverse temperature
\beta=1/({k_{B}}T)
of the logarithm of the magnetization
\partial_{\beta}(\ln\left[\langle\left\lvert m\right\rvert\rangle\right])
.
It can be expressed in terms of expectation values as

\displaystyle\partial_{\beta}(\ln\left[\langle\left\lvert m\right\rvert\rangle\right])

\displaystyle=\frac{\frac{\partial}{\partial\beta}\left[\langle\left\lvert m\right\rvert\rangle\right]}{\left[\langle\left\lvert m\right\rvert\rangle\right]}

\displaystyle=V\frac{\left[\langle\left\lvert m\right\rvert e\rangle\right]-\left[\langle\left\lvert m\right\rvert\rangle\langle e\rangle\right]}{\left[\langle\left\lvert m\right\rvert\rangle\right]}\;,

(20)

where
\langle\cdot\rangle
denotes the thermal average and
\left[\cdot\right]
the disorder average and
e=E/V
,
m=M/V
are the normalized energy and magnetization, respectively.
Note, that we use the common convention of taking the absolute value of
m
to avoid the trivial averaging to zero in the low-temperature phase for finite lattice sizes.
For the sake of clarity, we will omit the average brackets for the rest of this work and simply write
\partial_{\beta}(\ln\left\lvert m\right\rvert)=\partial_{\beta}(\ln\left[\langle\left\lvert m\right\rvert\rangle\right])
.
The derivative of the logarithm of the magnetization
\partial_{\beta}(\ln\left\lvert m\right\rvert)
is known to diverge at the critical temperature in the thermodynamic limit
L\rightarrow\infty
. For finite system sizes it hence develops a minimum.
The finite-size scaling behavior up to the first-order correction reads

\displaystyle\partial_{\beta}(\ln\left\lvert m\right\rvert)_{\min}(L)=AL^{1/\nu}\left(1+BL^{-\omega}\right)\;,

(21)

where
\partial_{\beta}(\ln\left\lvert m\right\rvert)_{\min}(L)
is the (finite) minimum value of
\partial_{\beta}(\ln\left\lvert m\right\rvert)(\beta)
for a given lattice size
L
.
Fitting with this ansatz is difficult as it is a non-linear four-parameter fit.
Therefore, we first determined the correction exponent
\omega
separately and used it as a fixed parameter in the final estimation.

The whole finite-size scaling analysis can be split into three main steps.
In the first step we derive the peaks of
\partial_{\beta}(\ln\left\lvert m\right\rvert)
.
The second step is the extraction of the correction exponent
\omega
which is needed for the fits in the last step.
The last step is the fitting of
\partial_{\beta}(\ln\left\lvert m\right\rvert)_{\min}(L)
with fixed
\omega
and the extraction of
\nu
.

IV.1
Peaks of Observables

We start the analysis with the extraction of the peaks of the derivative of the logarithm of the magnetization
\partial_{\beta}(\ln\left\lvert m\right\rvert)_{\min}(L)
.
Out of all simulated temperatures for each parameter tuple
(a,p_{d},L)
we chose three temperatures
\beta_{\text{sim}}^{i}
with
i=1,2,3
where the derivative of the logarithm of the magnetization calculated at these temperatures
\partial_{\beta}(\ln\left\lvert m\right\rvert)(\beta_{\text{sim}}^{i})
was minimal.
For these three
\beta_{\text{sim}}^{i}
we performed a single histogram reweighting of
\partial_{\beta}(\ln\left\lvert m\right\rvert)
to find the minimum values
\partial_{\beta}(\ln\left\lvert m\right\rvert)^{i}_{\min}
and the corresponding temperatures
\beta_{\min}^{i}
.
The final
\partial_{\beta}(\ln\left\lvert m\right\rvert)_{\min}
was chosen to be the minimum of all three
\partial_{\beta}(\ln\left\lvert m\right\rvert)^{i}_{\min}
values.
A more detailed explanation of the reweighting and error estimation process through resampling is presented in
Appendix

A
.

An important issue was to ensure that the histogram reweighting results lay within the reweighting range.
This is an inevitable restriction coming from the limited statistics.
We used the reweighting range approximation as defined in
Janke 2008

\displaystyle\Delta\beta_{\text{rew}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\left[\langle E^{2}\rangle\right]-\left[\langle E\rangle^{2}\right]}}\;.

(22)

We looked at the ratios of the differences between the simulation temperatures
\beta_{\text{sim}}
and the found temperatures of the minimum values
\beta_{\min}
with respect to the reweighting range
\Delta\beta_{\text{rew}}

\displaystyle\frac{\left\lvert\beta_{\text{sim}}-\beta_{\min}\right\rvert}{\Delta\beta_{\text{rew}}}\;.

(23)

As can be seen in
Fig.

9
all obtained
\beta_{\min}
were close enough to the corresponding
\beta_{\text{sim}}
to assume that the use of the reweighting technique is valid.

The ratios of the differences between simulation temperatures

\Delta\beta_{\text{rew}}
.
Smaller points represent smaller lattice sizes
L
.
The majority of the ratios is

IV.2
Confluent Correction Exponent
\omega

The quotient method which we used for the determination of the confluent correction exponent
\omega
was successfully used in other works,
e.g.,

Ballesteros et al. 1998a
;
Ballesteros et al. 1998b
;
Fytas and Martín-Mayor 2016
.
Starting from an observable
\mathcal{O}
which has a peak at the critical temperature we build quotients of
\mathcal{O}
at different lattice sizes
L
and
sL

\displaystyle Q_{\mathcal{O}}(sL)=\frac{\mathcal{O}(sL,\beta_{c}(sL))}{\mathcal{O}(L,\beta_{c}(L))}\;,

(24)

where the observables are taken at the critical temperatures for the given lattice sizes
L
and
sL
, respectively, and
s
is an arbitrary positive (integer) factor.
For a dimensional observable,
e.g.,

\partial_{\beta}(\ln\left\lvert m\right\rvert)
, the finite-size scaling of
Q_{\mathcal{O}}
in leading order reads
Fytas and Martín-Mayor 2016

\displaystyle Q_{\mathcal{O}}(L)=s^{x_{\mathcal{O}}/\nu}+AL^{-\omega}\;,

(25)

where
x_{\mathcal{O}}
is the critical exponent of
\mathcal{O}
.

We calculated the quotients defined through
Eq.

24
for
\mathcal{O}=\partial_{\beta}(\ln\left\lvert m\right\rvert)
with
x_{\mathcal{O}}=1
and for
s=4
.
This allowed us to have 8 independent
Q
values without using the same lattice size twice.
We used the peak values
\partial_{\beta}(\ln\left\lvert m\right\rvert)_{\min}(L)
and performed a global fit to
Q_{\partial_{\beta}(\ln\left\lvert m\right\rvert)}(L,p_{d})
according to
Eq.

25
but using all
p_{d}
simultaneously

\displaystyle Q_{\partial_{\beta}(\ln\left\lvert m\right\rvert)}(L,p_{d})=C+A_{p_{d}}L^{-\omega}\;,

(26)

where we explicitly denote the dependence of the amplitudes
A_{p_{d}}
on the concentrations of defects with the index
p_{d}
and relate the constant
C
to
Eq.

25
with

\displaystyle C=s^{x_{\mathcal{O}}/\nu}\;.

(27)

In
Fig.

we present the
\omega
results and the qualities of the fits
\chi^{2}_{\text{red}}
for
p_{d}^{\max}=0.4
,
L_{\min}=20
and various
p_{d}^{\min}
while in
Fig.

11
the fits are shown.

p_{d}^{\min}
and
p_{d}^{\max}
denote the minimum and maximum concentrations of defects included in the fits, respectively.
We have checked the possibility of getting
\omega
from individual
p_{d}
values but the ratio data suffer from large error bars and the results were not representative.
This fact emphasizes the advantage of using a global fit by simulating at many different concentrations
p_{d}
.
Looking into
Fig.

p_{d}^{\min}\geq 0.1
are in a good region of

and therefore we took this value as the final values for all correlated cases

.
For the uncorrelated case we chose

.

V
Conclusions

We applied Monte Carlo simulation techniques to the three-dimensional Ising model on a lattice with long-ranged correlated site disorder.
The correlation of the disorder was assumed to be proportional to a power-law
\propto r^{-a}
with a correlation exponent
a
.
We provided a decent analysis of the disorder correlation in our disorder ensembles verifying the correlation exponent
a
numerically.

We found the critical exponents of the correlation length
\nu
and the confluent correction exponents
\omega
as well as the critical temperatures
\beta_{c}
of the system for various correlation exponents
1.5\leq a\leq 3.5
as well as for the uncorrelated case
a=\infty
.
Contrarily to other works we performed a global fit where we included different disorder concentrations into one simultaneous fit.
Such a study was not possible before because all known works only considered one particular correlation exponent choice
a=2.0
and only one or two different concentrations
p_{d}
whereas in this work we used a wide range of
a
and
p_{d}
values.

In
Fig.

we give a visual comparison of the critical exponents
\nu
obtained in this work, results known from other works and predictions by the extended Harris criterion.
We obtain a value
\nu=$0.6875(47)$
for the uncorrelated case which matches the results from other groups listed in
Table

1
and plotted in
Fig.

17
.
Also the correction exponent
\omega=$0.373(53)$
coincides with Ref.
Ballesteros and Parisi 1999
.

Appendix A
Estimation of Peaks of Observables

Suppose we performed simulations on
N_{c}
disorder realizations and did
N
measurements at a simulation temperature
\beta_{\text{sim}}
on each of them.
We are equipped with two-dimensional arrays of total energy
E_{i}^{c}
and total magnetization
M_{i}^{c}
for
i=1,\dots,N
and
c=1,\dots,N_{c}
.
Using these arrays we can calculate observables of the from

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{i}^{c}=(E_{i}^{c})^{k}(M_{i}^{c})^{l}\;,

(30)

where
k
and
l
are arbitrary powers.
We introduce the notation
\mathcal{O}^{c}
for an average over
\mathcal{O}_{i}^{c}
for one particular disorder realization
c

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}^{c}=\langle\mathcal{O}\rangle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathcal{O}_{i}^{c}\;.

(31)

The average over the disorder realizations is denoted by
\left[\cdot\right]
and the final estimate
\mathcal{O}
reads

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}=\left[\langle\mathcal{O}\rangle\right]=\left[\mathcal{O}^{c}\right]=\frac{1}{N_{c}}\sum_{c=1}^{N_{c}}\mathcal{O}^{c}\;.

(32)

For variables of the type of
Eq.

30
a histogram reweighting technique can be used to reweight the observable from the simulated temperature
\beta_{\text{sim}}
to a different temperature
\beta
.
We used the form given in
Janke 2008

\displaystyle\CUSTOMrew(\mathcal{O})^{c}(\beta)=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathcal{O}_{i}^{c}e^{-(\beta-\beta_{\text{sim}})E_{i}^{c}}}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}e^{-(\beta-\beta_{\text{sim}})E_{i}^{c}}}\;,

(33)

where the reweighting is performed separately for each disorder realization
c
and the final estimate at the temperature
\beta
is the disorder average

\displaystyle\CUSTOMrew(\mathcal{O})(\beta)=\left[\CUSTOMrew(\mathcal{O})^{c}\right]\;.

(34)

Not every observable of interest, in particular the derivative of the logarithm of the magnetization
\partial_{\beta}(\ln\left\lvert m\right\rvert)
has the form of
Eq.

30
.
Let
{\mathcal{P}\,}
denote a composite observable of the following form

\displaystyle{\mathcal{P}\,}=f(\mathcal{O}^{(1)},\mathcal{O}^{(2)},\dots)\;,

(35)

where each of
\mathcal{O}^{(k)}
fulfills the form of
Eq.

30
.
For this kind of composed observables we define the reweighting procedure by reweighting each component separately

\displaystyle\CUSTOMrew({\mathcal{P}\,})(\beta)=f(\CUSTOMrew(\mathcal{O}^{(1)})(\beta),\CUSTOMrew(\mathcal{O}^{(2)})(\beta),\dots)\;.

(36)

Let us summarize what we have achieved so far.
Starting with the arrays of raw observables
E
and
M
we are able to use the histogram reweighting technique to obtain practically any observable calculable from
E
and
M
as a function of
\beta
.

Let us now assume that the finite-size scaling analysis of
{\mathcal{P}\,}(\beta)
predicts a minimum
\check{\mathcal{P}}\,
at a certain temperature
\check{\beta}
.
Without loss of generality we assume a minimum of
{\mathcal{P}\,}(\beta)
, otherwise we transform
{\mathcal{P}\,}\rightarrow-{\mathcal{P}\,}
.
In the thermodynamic limit
L\rightarrow\infty
we expect
\check{\beta}\rightarrow\beta_{c}
.
We can apply an optimization routine by plugging in
\CUSTOMrew({\mathcal{P}\,})(\beta)
as the target function and obtain
\check{\mathcal{P}}\,
and
\check{\beta}
,

\displaystyle\check{\mathcal{P}}\,=\min_{\beta}\left(\CUSTOMrew({\mathcal{P}\,})(\beta)\right)\;.

(37)

However, we will not be able to estimate the errors
\epsilon{(\check{\mathcal{P}}\,)}
and
\epsilon{(\check{\beta})}
as only one final value is calculated through
Eq.

37
from all simulated data.
In order to overcome this problem, we can use a resampling technique.
We have chosen the
jackknife resampling
technique which is described,
e.g.,
in
Shao and Tu 1995
in full length.
We will only sketch the main steps applied in this work.
As our measurements were two-dimensional arrays consisting of time series
i=1,\dots,N
and disorder realizations
c=1,\dots,N_{c}
, we apply the resampling in both directions separately and combine the estimates at the end.
For each jackknife resampling step
j
in the time series direction we leave out a block
J^{j}\subset\{1,\dots,N\}
of measurements for each disorder realization
c
so that the thermal average defined through
Eq.

31
becomes

\displaystyle(\mathcal{O}^{c})^{j}=\frac{1}{N-\lvert J^{j}\rvert}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1\\
i\notin J^{j}\end{subarray}}^{N}\mathcal{O}_{i}^{c}\;,

(38)

where
\lvert J^{j}\rvert
is the number of left-out samples.
Analogously, for each resampling step
k
in the disorder direction we leave out a block
J^{k}\subset\{1,\dots,N_{c}\}
of disorder realizations so that the disorder average defined through
Eq.

32
becomes

\displaystyle(\mathcal{O})^{k}=\frac{1}{N_{c}-\lvert J^{k}\rvert}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}c=1\\
c\notin J^{k}\end{subarray}}^{N_{c}}\mathcal{O}^{c}\;.

(39)

where
\lvert J^{k}\rvert
is the number of left-out realizations.

Starting from the modified thermal averages
(\mathcal{O}^{c})^{j}
and disorder averages
(\mathcal{O})^{k}
respectively, all steps in the following analysis remain the same.
Let
\mathcal{A}
be a final estimate coming from a certain analysis,
e.g.,
minimum search as in
Eq.

37
.
By repeating the analysis for
N_{j}
different jackknife blocks in the time direction and
N_{k}
blocks in the disorder direction we are given two arrays of estimates
(\mathcal{A})^{j}
and
(\mathcal{A})^{k}
respectively.
We calculate two jackknife means
\overline{\mathcal{A}}^{j}
and
\overline{\mathcal{A}}^{k}

\displaystyle\overline{\mathcal{A}}^{a}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N_{a}}\sum_{a=1}^{N_{a}}(\mathcal{A})^{a}\quad\text{with}\;\;a=j,k\;,

(40)

and two corresponding jackknife errors
\epsilon{(\mathcal{A})}^{j}
and
\epsilon{(\mathcal{A})}^{k}

\displaystyle\epsilon{(\mathcal{A})}^{a}

\displaystyle=\frac{N_{a}-1}{N_{a}}\sum_{a=1}^{N_{a}}\left((\mathcal{A})^{a}-\overline{\mathcal{A}}^{a}\right)^{2}\quad\text{with}\;\;a=j,k\;.

(41)

As the last step we combine the two means and errors in a standard (uncorrelated) manner

\displaystyle\overline{\mathcal{A}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\overline{\mathcal{A}}^{j}+\overline{\mathcal{A}}^{k}\right)\;

(42)

\displaystyle\epsilon{(\mathcal{A})}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\left(\epsilon{(\mathcal{A})}^{j}\right)^{2}+\left(\epsilon{(\mathcal{A})}^{k}\right)^{2}}\;.

(43)

The mean
\overline{\mathcal{A}}
and the corresponding error
\epsilon{(\mathcal{A})}
are the final results for a given analysis after applying jackknife resampling.
