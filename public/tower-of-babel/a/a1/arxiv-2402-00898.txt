An Early Categorization of Prompt Injection Attacks on Large Language Models
Sippo Rossi, Alisia Marianne Michel, Raghava Rao Mukkamala, Jason Bennett Thatcher

Abstract
Large language models and AI chatbots have been at the forefront of democratizing artificial intelligence. However, the releases of ChatGPT and other similar tools have been followed by an increasing number of concerns regarding the difficulty of controlling large language models and their outputs. Currently, we are witnessing a cat-and-mouse game where users attempt to misuse the models with a novel attack called prompt injections while the developers attempt to discover the vulnerabilities and block the attacks simultaneously. In this paper, we provide an overview of this emergent threat and present a categorization of prompt injections, which can guide future research on prompt injections and act as a checklist of vulnerabilities in the development of LLM interfaces. Moreover, based on previous literature and our own empirical research, we discuss the implications of prompt injections to LLM end users, developers, and researchers.



1
Introduction

Research in Natural Language Processing (NLP) has made remarkable progress in recent years
(
Bubeck et al., 2023
)
. These advances have garnered widespread attention outside of academia as multiple companies and research labs have started to productize large language models (LLMs) through application programming interfaces (APIs) and more user friendly interfaces such as chatbots. These deep learning-based tools are capable of many tasks, from text generation, classification and summarization to generating scripts and fixing bugs in a wide range of programming languages. Examples of prominent LLMs include OpenAI’s GPT-4
(
OpenAI, 2023
)
, Meta’s LLaMA
(
Touvron et al., 2023
)
and Google’s Bard
(
Pichai, 2023
)
. The shift towards providing easy-to-use interfaces (such as chat prompts) for LLMs has helped democratize access to AI and given individuals and organizations opportunities to use powerful NLP tools, which previously required advanced skills in computer science and computational resources only available to the wealthiest organizations
(
Bommasani et al., 2021
)
.

LLMs have been trained with massive datasets consisting of texts scraped from the internet, Wikipedia, and books, as well as code from Stack Exchange
(
Brown et al., 2020
;
Touvron et al., 2023
)
. Curating such vast training data is difficult, and ultimately even after pre-processing, they contain content that represents the negative biases found in society, such as racism and sexism
(
Hao, 2021
;
Bender et al., 2021
)
. Consequently, LLMs are capable of generating undesirable content ranging such as hate speech, instructions for manufacturing drugs and even source code for malware. Even more benign examples, such as assigning traditional gender roles to men and women or associating stereotypical traits with a specific ethnicity, are problematic and common occurrences
(
Hao, 2021
;
Bender et al., 2021
)
.

These issues have resulted in the developers of the models attempting to control both inputs and outputs more rigorously by building different types of safeguards around the interfaces that should restrict the use of the LLM by making the interface refuse to act upon some prompts. However, users have not responded well to attempts to restrict the outputs and have started testing how to bypass these security features by probing for vulnerabilities with so-called prompt injections
(
Heikkilä, 2023
)
. The name "prompt injection" is derived from the common cyber security attack, SQL injections, where the attacker attempts to sneak a SQL command, for example, into a field in an online form that is connected to a database. When executed correctly, the attack can retrieve information that should not be visible or even damage the database by removing rows or entire tables. Similarly, in a prompt injection, the attacker attempts to get a chatbot or an LLM assisted tool to take some undesired action or produce some malicious output through creative formatting of the input (prompt) given to the model. A basic prompt injection is demonstrated in Table 1, where ChatGPT is asked to provide instructions on how to hot wire a car, which it would refuse to do if the prompt asked the same question directly.

The previous example is a straightforward, "direct" prompt injection where the injection is intentionally given to the LLM, but as LLMs are being incorporated into an increasing number of tools, such as plugins to browsers and email applications, this has opened a new attack vector. Early demonstrations have shown how it is possible to hide a prompt injection that is invisible to the human eye into a web page or email, with the goal being that an LLM tool that comes across it would then read the prompt and follow its instructions. A benign example of such an "indirect" prompt injection is hiding in white font on a white page a prompt that asks an LLM to reply only in emojis. Now if a user uses a tool such as Bing Chat to analyze the content on the webpage, this injection would give the LLM the new instruction and Bing Chat would respond accordingly
(
Burgess, 2023
)
. Malicious indirect prompt injections could have much more nefarious goals, such as data exfiltration from a browser or email to a location specified by the attacker
(
Burgess, 2023
)
.

Due to the recent emergence of the phenomenon of prompt injections, there were few peer-reviewed publications on prompt injections in late 2023. Most available information on the subject is in preprints as well as spread across online discussions and posts. The purpose and contribution of this paper is two-fold. First, in this exploratory study we aim to describe, document, and provide a comprehensive list of the known types of prompt injections. Second, our goal is to provide a checklist for developers and end users on what to expect and how to avoid potential threats when using LLMs and when building services that use LLMs by providing a categorization of prompt injections. Thus, our primary research question is what types of prompt injection attacks exist, and can they be grouped into categories?

The paper is organized as follows: we begin with a literature review that describes the state-of-the-art of LLMs and prompt injections and provides a brief history of prompt injections. Next, we describe the methodology used to find, group and test the prompt injections. This is followed by a summary of the results and a presentation of a proposed categorization of prompt injections. Lastly, we discuss the implications to users and developers of LLMs, as well as the ethics and limitations related to this study before concluding the paper.

2
Literature review

2.1
Large language models and AI chatbots

While there is no exact formal definition of what is a large language model and not merely a language model, the term has been used in relation to neural network models with a billion or more parameters and that have been trained on very large datasets
(
Radford et al., 2019
;
Carlini et al., 2021
)
. Most of the well-known LLMs at the time of writing are transformer models
(
Lee, 2023
;
Bubeck et al., 2023
)
. Due to the resources needed to train LLMs
(
Bommasani et al., 2021
)
, the most cited models have been developed by companies and private research organizations and are published in conferences
(
Brown et al., 2020
;
Devlin et al., 2019
)
or through arXiv as preprints
(
Touvron et al., 2023
;
OpenAI, 2023
)
. It should be noted that a large language model is not synonymous with an AI chatbot, since LLMs can be built for specific tasks and not all of them are designed for text generation or to work with chat based prompts. Moreover, while some LLMs such as LLaMA and its derivatives are capable of text generation
(
Touvron et al., 2023
)
, they still require a separate service built on top of the language model to be used for example through a chatbot interface or API.

There is a growing number of AI chatbot products that use LLMs, and some combine multiple generative AI models, to for example allow generating images from text prompts
(
Ramesh et al., 2021
)
. They can typically be accessed through a web browser interface where the user interacts with a chatbot that is connected to the LLM. Notable examples are ChatGPT, which uses a modified version of GPT-3.5 or GPT-4
(
OpenAI, 2022
)
, Bing AI, which uses GPT-4
(
OpenAI, 2023
)
and Google’s Bard
(
Pichai, 2023
)
, which uses LaMDA
(
Thoppilan et al., 2022
)
.

As result of this new way of using natural language to communicate with a language model, a practice called prompt engineering has emerged. Prompt engineering can be defined as the process of creating a prompt that achieves a desired goal
(
Liu and Chilton, 2022
)
. Prompt engineering is closely related to certain types of prompt injections, although in the latter the prompt is specifically designed to bypass some restrictions imposed by the chatbot or API of the LLM.

2.2
Academic research on prompt injections

Peer reviewed academic research on prompt injections is scarce due to the novelty of the subject. To identify papers, we conducted a literature review, going through all articles found via Google Scholar with the keyword "prompt injection" that were published after the discovery of prompt injections from May 2022 to September 2023 and that were published in English. We found 123 papers that discuss prompt injections as an adversarial activity or as a security threat and with a definition that is similar to ours, with a vast majority being arXiv preprints. We decided to focus our attention to papers that discuss prompt injections specifically and omitted works where they are only mentioned in passing. The identified papers explore prompt injections from very different perspectives, ranging from some that propose broader frameworks for studying the phenomenon and demonstrating prompt injections with a specific LLM
(
Perez and Ribeiro, 2022
)
, to more detailed investigations of individual prompt injection attack types
(
Greshake et al., 2023
)
.

Studies have already identified and discussed the two high-level categories for prompt injection attacks that were mentioned in the introduction; direct and indirect prompt injections
(
Greshake et al., 2023
)
. In a direct prompt attack, the attacker simply inputs a malicious prompt to the LLM, while in an indirect prompt injection the attacker’s goal can be to get the LLM to read for example a specific page where the malicious prompt is hidden in the text retrieved by the LLM, which it then executes
(
Greshake et al., 2023
;
Li et al., 2023a
)
, or to poison the training data of an LLM
(
Yan et al., 2023
)
. These will be described in detail and demonstrated in the findings section.

Early papers on prompt injections identified and described attacks, such as goal hijacking
(
Perez and Ribeiro, 2022
)
, payload splitting
(
Kang et al., 2023
)
, jailbreaking
(
Li et al., 2023a
;
Zhuo et al., 2023
)
, adversarial suffix attacks
(
Zou et al., 2023
)
and prompt leaking
(
Perez and Ribeiro, 2022
)
. In goal hijacking the attacker attempts to misalign the original goal of the prompt in a way that the chatbot outputs something that usually is filtered, such as a racist statement
(
Perez and Ribeiro, 2022
)
. Similarly, in payload splitting the attacker attempts to get the chatbot to output restricted text, by giving less malicious information in separate texts and then asking to combine them, resulting in for example racist or sexist outputs
(
Kang et al., 2023
)
. The third attack, jailbreaking is more loosely defined, but the goal of it is to get the chatbot with a prompt into a mode where the typical user restrictions that filter harmful content do not apply. It should be noted that jailbreaking has become somewhat synonymous with prompt injections, particularly in forums and non academic discourse on prompt injections in the internet. A categorization for different communities of jailbreak prompt injections has already been proposed previously by
Shen et al. (2023)
.

The fourth mentioned attack, adversarial suffix attacks, has a similar goal as the aforementioned three, which is to get the chatbot to produce a malicious output. However, it differs significantly in the way it is produced. Most prompt injection attacks require human labor and manual experimentation to craft, while adversarial suffix attacks are based on computationally generating and testing suffixes, until one is found which when added to a query allows bypassing security measures of the LLM interface and thus producing malicious outputs
(
Zou et al., 2023
)
. The suffix itself is a string of words and characters that do not have meaning when read by a human.

Lastly, in prompt leaking, the goal of the attacker is to get the chatbot to reveal its internal instructions or instructions given in previous prompts that are not visible to a regular user. This can reveal information that only the developers should have access to and give information to the attackers that can be used in future prompt injection attacks
(
Perez and Ribeiro, 2022
)
.

2.3
A brief history of prompt injections

As the last part of the literature review, we summarize the developments of the study and use of prompt injections using non-academic online sources such as private websites, blogs, social media posts and news articles. There are different claims on who discovered prompt injections and coined the term, and as early as in May 2022 researchers at Preamble, an AI service company focusing on safety, had discovered the vulnerability in GPT-3 and reported it privately to OpenAI
(
Preamble, 2022
)
. In September 2022, Riley Goodside published a demonstration of a prompt injection in a Tweet
(
Willison, 2022
)
, and this was possibly one of the early instances that made prompt injections known to a much wider audience as the Tweet became viral.

While the first examples of prompt injections were focusing on GPT-3 and ChatGPT, in early 2023 Microsoft published Bing Chat and soon after similar vulnerabilities were detected and successful prompt injection attack were shared on Twitter and other venues
(
Edwards, 2023
)
. Notably, a user was able to reveal the developer name "Sydney" of the chatbot and the rules governing what Bing AI should output, which is an example of the previously described prompt leaking.

At the time of writing there is a growing number of active online communities focused on discovering and sharing novel prompt injections as well as websites dedicated to the topic
(
Heikkilä, 2023
)
. Some examples include jailbreakchat.com, which contains a list of known ChatGPT vulnerabilities and a voting system and ratings of the quality of the prompt, and ChatGPTJailbreak, a Reddit community where people can share successful attacks. See appendix A for links to sources and a longer list of examples.

Many of the early posts demonstrating prompt injections have focused on tricking the LLM to produce comical or societally negative outputs such as immoral answers to a question or even hate speech. Some of the more recent and serious examples of negative ways to use prompt engineering and prompt injections have been cases where users have managed to make ChatGPT produce malicious and fully functioning code, such as SQL injections or even a script for malware that is difficult to detect even with modern malware detection systems, with relatively simple workarounds to get through the initial refusals of the language model to generate such outputs
(
Mulgrew, 2023
)
. Moreover, indirect prompt injections that could expose private data are also getting more attention as plugins using Bing AI and ChatGPT are emerging and security researchers are pointing out threats related to them as they can take actions for example on the web browser or email client without the user even seeing it
(
Burgess, 2023
)
.

Based on both academic and non-academic sources prompt injections are still an understudied and evolving threat. In the following sections we will discuss the aforementioned as well as other identified injection types and propose a categorization that will help with systematically reviewing and evaluating chatbots and LLM interfaces for weaknesses.

3
Methodology

In this section we specify the process used to gather, test and document different types of prompt injection attacks. As the main goal of this paper is to produce a categorization of the known types of prompt injection attacks, the empirical work for this paper consisted mainly of systematically reviewing different identified sources such as arXiv preprints and pages such as jailbreakchat.com. Moreover, due to the recentness of the topic and rapidly changing landscape as updates are being applied to the chatbots and APIs of LLMs, a heavy emphasis is based on non-academic sources.

To find the prompt injections we conducted keyword searches on Google, Google Scholar, arXiv, Github, Medium and Twitter (now X) searching with "prompt injection" and "jailbreak" to identify articles and posts related to the topic. Our approach to reviewing scholarly work was described in the first paragraph of section 2.2. To complement the academic sources, we went through the content available at jailbreakchat.com and the Reddit channels ChatGPTJailbreak and ChatGPT. The initial search of academic articles and websites was conducted from May to June 2023, and a second supplementary review was completed in September 2023. To address the issues related to a lack of peer review in the non-academic sources, we relied on a process where we first attempt to find multiple sources making the same claim with credible screenshots demonstrating how the prompt injection works. Secondly, we test the prompt injection in ChatGPT and GPT-3. Due to the rapid patching of these vulnerabilities, they do not always work without additional prompt engineering or in some cases they do not work at all anymore at the time of writing, making these vulnerabilities relevant only to future chatbot and LLM interface designs. As the primary objective of this paper is to document existing prompt injections based on secondary sources, we do not catalog or describe which prompt injections have been patched and which have not.

The prompt injections that were sufficiently documented and demonstrated previously in multiple sources, or verified by our own tests were stored. For each prompt injection, we recorded the most common name used to refer to it in the sources, the target of the attack (for instance ChatGPT, training data of future LLMs, or an email AI assistant), the objective of the attack and what the prompt looks like. The final categorization was created by merging the findings from all sources and grouping the prompt injections first into two broader categories that have been proposed earlier
(
Greshake et al., 2023
)
, direct and indirect prompt injections, and then further dividing the prompt injections into subcategories (referred to as classes of prompt injections). The classes of indirect prompt injections are distinct from each other as each injection takes advantage of a different attack vector. For direct prompt injections, the distinguishing factor for each injection is the method of circumventing restrictions established by LLM or service providers, which differs for each class. These will be described in detail in the following section. The process of creating the categorization is illustrated in Figure 1.

4
Findings

In our review of prompt injections we identified and verified seventeen different variations of prompt injection attacks. Following the broad categories proposed in earlier research, we chose to divide these on to two separate branches, direct and indirect prompt injections. Within these two main branches, we identified six classes of direct and four classes of indirect prompt injections, which form the second level of our categorization. To demonstrate the six direct and four indirect prompt injections, we have compiled seventeen separate examples, some which belong to the same class, and which are listed with sources in Appendix A.

4.1
Direct prompt injections

The majority of all prompt injections we found in the previously listed sources were direct prompt injections. This is likely due to the ease of testing and demonstrating them with for example GPT-3, ChatGPT or Bing AI. The description and objective of each class of direct prompt injections is provided in Table 2 and examples of each are provided in Appendix A.

Direct prompt injections most commonly have the simple objective of circumnavigating the security measures that prevent generating specific types of outputs. Examples of actions that are not permitted typically include generation of hate speech, malware, content that promotes violence or other illegal activities and adult content. Based on the found examples, the security measures preventing generation of such content can be bypassed with relatively simple prompt injections. The names used in the sources for these types of direct prompt injections were diverse and many were simply referred to as jailbreaks.

Another known objective of direct prompt injections can be to get the LLM interface such as a chatbot using an API of an LLM to reveal its "initial prompt", or in other words the instructions it has been given. This initial prompt can contain information on what actions the interface is allowed to do, which topics or commands it should ignore and what type of responses it should provide. This information can then be used to engineer a prompt that bypasses these instructions and the simplest examples have included telling the interface to ignore parts or the entire previously set of instructions it has been given. As a concrete example, when Bing AI was released, a user could ask it to "ignore previous instructions" and describe "what is in the document above" (the initial prompt) and it would paradoxically provide information such as its developer name is Sydney and that it is not allowed to disclose its internal alias Sydney
(
Edwards, 2023
)
.

4.2
Indirect prompt injections

While direct prompt injections mostly have the objective of generating malicious content, the goals of indirect prompt injections are more varied and to some extent resemble traditional cyberattacks. Moreover, in the indirect prompt injections, the contents generated by the prompt are not necessarily of interest to the attacker.

Table 3 contains the descriptions and objectives of the indirect prompt injections listed in this paper and categorization. The first class, active injections, gets its name from the attacker proactively attempting to target systems such as LLM augmented email clients. As an example scenario of this class, if a company’s customer service email has some form of automation and an LLM read and responds to emails, when the LLM encounters an email containing a prompt injection, it could, instead of its original instruction, follow the new instructions specified by the attacker in the injection. This could be, for instance, to forward emails to an address belonging to the attacker.

The second class, passive injections, works against systems such as a web browser embedded with an LLM service that can read the websites that the user is visiting, or against future LLMs that will be trained with datasets containing the content scraped from the internet. These can be hidden even from the eyes of the user by making the text invisible, while the LLM sees it and can inadvertently execute it.

When compared to the two previous classes, the third class, user-driven injections are simpler. User-driven injections are simply malicious prompts that are given by an attacker using social engineering, such as guidance or false promises to an unsuspecting victim, who then executes them. User-driven injections are also sometimes referred to as hidden injections and can contain multiple steps, which are further elaborated in sources such as Greshake et al., (2023).

The fourth and final class, virtual prompt injections, rely on the attacker having access to the training phase of an instruction-tuned LLM. By placing a so called virtual prompt injection to the instructions, the behavior of the LLM can be steered without a need to directly manipulate prompts that are given to the model when the LLM is in production.
Yan et al. (2023)
show that a very small number of poisoned training examples can misalign a large share of the outputs regarding the topic specified by the attacker.

5
Discussion

It is evident that LLMs can be easily used for malicious purposes even when their interfaces are designed to prevent it, and there is a vibrant community constantly looking for new vulnerabilities and ways to exploit systems such as chatbots or plugins that are based on LLMs
(
Heikkilä, 2023
)
. This implies that the developers building the LLMs and LLM interfaces as well as the developers integrating LLMs into their products, and lastly even the regular end-users wishing to use LLM-based services should be vigilant. In this section, we discuss the implication of direct and indirect prompt injections, and provide initial guidelines on how to avoid prompt injection attacks. Lastly, we discuss the ethics and limitations of our study as well as possible directions for future work.

5.1
Implications to the development of LMM interfaces and services

To clarify the terminology and concepts, a "raw" LLM will produce whatever it is asked to produce, including malicious content and hate speech, and the success in this depends on to what extent it has been exposed to such content in its training data. However, in most cases a user or developer does not directly interact with the raw LLM model, but instead with some interface such as an API or chatbot. The security systems which introduce limitations and filters to what inputs and outputs are allowed are in this interface layer. Prompt injections are a way that attackers will attempt to bypass these security measures.

Therefore, there is a significant difference in the amount of work needed to mitigate prompt injections depending on if the goal is to develop an interface or if building a service on top of an existing interface. In the first case, the developer of the interface will need to consider how to address all the previously listed direct prompt injections as well as some of the indirect prompt injections such as user-driven injections, which rely on design aspects of the interface. As even premier AI Labs and companies are having difficulties with detecting and blocking prompt injections, it can be argued that developing a fully safe LLM interface is difficult if not impossible
(
Heikkilä, 2023
)
, especially as computational approaches to creating new prompt injections become more mature and widespread
(
Zou et al., 2023
)
. One approach to could be to limit access to the interface to a much smaller and more carefully vetted audience, although as a related cautionary tale Meta’s LLaMA, which was made available to researcher was leaked soon after to the entire world
(
Vincent, 2023
)
.

Due to the difficulty of building a robust and safe interface, most consumer facing or otherwise publicly accessible applications and tools that are built on LLMs should opt to using an existing LLM interface, such as the API of GPT-3. However, as shown by previous research and this paper, these commercial solutions have vulnerabilities as well. For the developer of a service that uses another company’s LLM in the back-end, many of the direct prompt injections are not an immediate threat as long as the malicious outputs are not entered to another system through automation, such as a database or publicly visible part of the internet. Nevertheless, it is important to not have any sensitive information given to the LLM in the instruction prompts, due to the risk of an attacker using instruction manipulation to leak these. Moreover, the possibility of indirect prompt injections being used through the service should be considered and security measures set up to avoid an attacker compromising the users of the service.

5.2
Implications to the end users of LLMs

The main threat to regular end users of LLMs are indirect prompt injections, and thus we discuss the implications of them rather than those of direct prompt injections.

First, due to the existence of user-driven injections, end users are at risk of coming across malicious suggestions online and accidentally executing an injection themselves. Thus the same practice as with copying and executing code or scripts found online should be followed as it is not safe to run a script copied from another source without understanding more or less what each line of the code is doing. Similarly, with prompts copied from online sources, end users should carefully check that each instruction in the prompt makes sense for the given task.

The second implication is that users should thoroughly review the risks and verify the reliability of the developers before adopting tools such as LLM-using browser plugins or email assistants. Similarly with any software that is distributed online, it will possibly become a standard process to check whether the plugin is endorsed by a reputable company or can be downloaded from a web store that checks the safety before including it in the catalog.

We emphasize that we do not discourage the use of LLM-assisted tools such browser plugins, as they can genuinely be useful, but nevertheless the end user still needs to be cautious and aware of risks related to them. Overall, the implications to end users could be mitigated with good cyber hygiene
(
Vishwanath et al., 2020
)
.

5.3
Implications to research

Prompt injections have opened a new and rapidly expanding area of research. Many existing approaches to studying cyberthreats can be transferred to prompt injection research, as LLM-based applications can be thought of as a new class of software and their APIs, chatbot interfaces and content that they ingest and train on are the attack vectors used by threat actors. As the rapidly growing number of papers studying different types of prompt injections attacks suggests, there is much to discover merely by designing and testing new types of prompt injection attacks. Ideally researchers discover these before threat actors do and the most significant vulnerabilities can then be reported and patched.

Prompt injections can also be studied from a software development point of view. As LLMs and the software that uses them matures, best practices regarding safe guarding LLMs from prompt injections will eventually be developed and become part of software development practices, corporate training as well as NLP education. Thus research on the effectiveness of the newly developed best practices related to LLMs and prompt injections will be needed to evaluate whether they are effective or not. Even human factors can be studied, such why and how individuals attempt to circumnavigate safe guards by employing prompt injections, and how to effectively discourage users to not do so.

Besides being vulnerable to prompt injections, LLMs and AI chatbots have been criticized for racism and bias and in general the fairness of ML models has been of interest in recent years
(
Bender et al., 2021
;
Schramowski et al., 2022
)
. As a result, different metrics and tests have been been developed for evaluating the fairness of LLM models
(
Liang et al., 2021
;
Zhang et al., 2023
)
. Similarly, a new set of metrics and a standardized battery of tests will be needed to make the process of evaluating the effectiveness of safe guards and other defensive measures taken against prompt injections. As examples of this, the first benchmarks for evaluating text safety and output robustness of LLMs have already been proposed
(
Qiu et al., 2023
;
Li et al., 2023b
)
.

As new types of services and products are built that utilize LLMs, there will be also new potential attack vectors and approaches to creating prompt injections. Consequently, there will likely be a plethora of research opportunities beyond what was discussed above.

5.4
Ethics

One of the primary ethical concerns of studying prompt injections is that in-depth explanations of the methods could inadvertently act as instructions on how to use them. In this study, none of the presented prompt injections are novel and the exact prompts or scripts used to execute them can be found from many sources, and typically can be found almost immediately with a simple Google search using the keywords "prompt injection" or "ChatGPT jailbreak". Additionally, besides the example in Table 1, we refrained from providing the prompt texts for the various prompt injection classes directly. Thus, the information presented in this paper should have little risks.

Moreover, direct prompt injections could be tested safely without exposing others to the harmful content as they could be run in a sandbox environment, but with some of the indirect prompt injection classes it would be more difficult. For example, testing passive injections would require leaving the malicious or inaccurate content on a web page for an extended amount of time, while user-driven injections by definition would require experimenting with individuals who are not aware of the purpose of the prompt injection. By opting not to test the indirect prompt injections, such ethical concerns were avoided.

5.5
Limitations

The limitations of this study are primarily related to the difficulty of identifying and testing different prompt injections as well as on generalizing the findings related to one LLM interface to another. As the first limitation, our categorization is not exhaustive, as new approaches to prompt injection attacks are constantly being developed and since we did not include all of the examples found in the sources due to being unable to test or reliably verify their existence. However, we preferred omitting questionable or contested prompt injections that we found online, as many of them were backed by only one source and sometimes without proper documentation.

The other notable limitation is that most prompt injections have been demonstrated on only one or two LLM interfaces, with ChatGPT and GPT-3 or GPT-4 being by far the most common targets. With our categorization we attempted to counter this issue by describing the classes in general terms and without details that are related to specific AI chatbots such as ChatGPT. Moreover, we made the assumption that many of the prompt injection types could either directly or with moderate to significant altering be applied to other chatbots and language model interfaces, but while maintaining the same logic.

5.6
Future work

The purpose of this study was to produce an initial categorization of prompt injection attacks. Due to the recentness of the topic and fast evolving nature research and development on LLMs, it is likely that some prompt injection types were omitted from this study, and that some of the current approaches to writing prompt injection will change or even disappear as standard approaches to countering them are developed. Therefore, the categorization is likely to get revised as new classes are discovered or as existing ones become obsolete due to improvements to security measures or changes in attack patterns.

While this paper had very limited discussion on guidelines for developers and end users of LLMs, the defense against prompt injections or even individual prompt injection types merits much more in-depth investigation. Furthermore, existing streams of cyber security research on individual behavior could incorporate the study of how users are susceptible to scams and malware involving LLMs and how to train users to detect such.

Ultimately, we hope this stream of research will have two outcomes. First, as more prompt injection types become known and characterized, a standardized set of tests can be developed based on them to evaluate LLM interfaces for vulnerabilities in a systematic fashion. In concrete terms, this means compiling a list of prompt injections that are in a format that they can be easily used to test new LLM-based tools. This is particularly the case with direct prompt injections, which are more narrowly defined and easier to test. Secondly, we believe these can be used to create a set of best practices for developing LLM-based tools and services. Similarly as currently exists with database interfaces and SQL queries, where there are known ways to build a connection to a database without the risk of SQL injections, the same way for there is a need for design principles for LLM APIs and chat interfaces. By conducting research in these two areas, the end result would ideally be a well known list of vulnerabilities and then later a set of best practices to avoid the vulnerabilities, which would allow building resilient AI augmented tools.

6
Conclusion

By introducing conversation-based user interfaces, LLMs and AI chatbots opened the door for prompt injections and consequently brought an interesting new challenge for the developers of chatbots and other LLM-based services. Although initially some of the use cases for prompt injection attacks seemed trivial and more comedic than serious as seen in many of the direct prompt injection examples, the more sophisticated direct and indirect prompt injections now pose a serious cyber threat to both the end users of LLM-based services as well as the providers of those tools.

In this paper, we reviewed a wide range of academic and non-academic sources to compile a list of prompt injections and proposed a categorization to help scholars and practitioners understand the broad concepts behind this emergent threat. We identified two main branches of prompt injections, direct and indirect prompt injections and within them four and six separate classes of prompt injections respectively. Lastly, we provided initial recommendations on how future AI chatbots and services using LLMs could address prompt injections. The main contribution of this paper is the categories and the key information on each class of prompt injections which were summarized in tables 2 and 3.

Our goal was to provide an as broad as possible overview on prompt injections, but due to the rapid speed at which old vulnerabilities are removed and new vulnerabilities are discovered, the proposed categories will most likely evolve in the near future. Overall, we believe further research into different aspects of prompt injections is needed to fully understand this phenomenon, as at the time of writing there are only few studies on the subject.
