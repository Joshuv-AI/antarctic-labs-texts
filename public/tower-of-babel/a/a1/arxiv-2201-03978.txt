Kiera Kean, Xihui Xie, Shuxian Xu

Abstract
We develop, analyze and test adaptive penalty parameter methods. We prove unconditional stability for velocity when adapting the penalty parameter, ϵ , \epsilon, and stability of the velocity time derivative under a condition on the change of the penalty parameter, ϵ ⁡ ( t n + 1 ) − ϵ ⁡ ( t n ) \epsilon(t_{n+1})-\epsilon(t_{n}) . The analysis and tests show that adapting ϵ ⁡ ( t n + 1 ) \epsilon(t_{n+1}) in response to ∇ ⋅ u ⁡ ( t n ) \nabla\cdot u(t_{n}) removes the problem of picking ϵ \epsilon and yields good approximations for the velocity. We provide error analysis and numerical tests to support these results. We supplement the adaptive- ϵ \epsilon method by also adapting the time-step. The penalty parameter ϵ \epsilon and time-step are adapted independently. We further compare first, second and variable order time-step algorithms. Accurate recovery of pressure remains an open problem.

Introduction

The velocity and pressure of an incompressible, viscous fluid are given by the Navier-Stokes equations. Let
{\bf u}
denote the fluid velocity,
p
the pressure,
\nu
the kinematic viscosity and
f
an external force:

\displaystyle{\bf u}_{t}-\nu\Delta{\bf u}+{\bf u}\cdot\nabla{\bf u}+\nabla p=f,

\displaystyle\quad\nabla\cdot{\bf u}=0,\quad\forall({\bf x},t)\in\Omega\times(0,T].

(1)

The velocity and pressure are coupled together by the incompressibility constraint
\nabla\cdot{\bf u}=0
. Coupled systems require more memory to store and are more expensive to solve.
Penalty methods and artificial compression methods relax the incompressibility condition and result in a pseudo-compressible system. This allows us to uncouple velocity and pressure, which will reduce storage space and computational complexity.
Penalty methods that allow complete elimination of the pressure variable are the simplest and fastest, and will be studied herein.

Penalty methods replace
\nabla\cdot{\bf u}=0
with
\nabla\cdot{\bf u}+\epsilon p=0\,
where
0<\epsilon<<1
. The pressure can be eliminated using
\nabla p=-\nabla(1/\epsilon\nabla\cdot{\bf u})
. As the pressure is entirely eliminated from the system, we do not need to solve for it at every timestep, leading to further increases in speed. The accuracy of penalty methods is known to be very sensitive to the choice of
\epsilon
(see Fig. 6.1). This sensitivity suggests considering
\epsilon
a control and picking
\epsilon
through a self-adaptive algorithm.
This problem of determining
\epsilon
self-adaptively is considered herein.

When adapting the parameter
\epsilon
,
\|\nabla\cdot{\bf u}\|
is monitored and used to make adjustment to
\epsilon
.
The stability of the standard penalty method with variable
\epsilon
is examined in Section
3
. No condition on the rate of change of
\epsilon
is required for the stability of
\|{\bf u}\|
. However, stability of
\|{\bf u}_{t}\|
is not unconditional. There is no restriction on the increase of
\epsilon
, however decreasing
\epsilon
quickly will lead to growth in
\|{\bf u}\|
.
In Section 3.2, we derive condition (
21
)

(1-k\alpha)\epsilon_{n}\leq\epsilon_{n+1}\ \text{for some }\alpha>0,

where
k
is the step-size.
This condition is required for stability of
\|{\bf u}_{t}\|.

Figure

9
confirms that violating this condition leads to spikes of catastrophic growth in
\|{\bf u}_{t}\|
.

The utility of penalty methods lies in accurate velocity approximation at low cost by simple methods. Consistent with this intent, we couple the adaptive
\epsilon
algorithm with simple, low cost time stepping methods based on the backward Euler method. Simple time filters allow us to implement an effective variable order, variable time-step adaptive scheme, developing further an algorithm of
[
8
]
. The self-adaptive
\epsilon
penalty method can be easily implemented for both constant time-step and variable time-step methods. We develop, analyze and test these new algorithms that independently adapt the time-step
k
and the penalty parameter
\epsilon
.

In addition to adapting the time-step, we adapt the order of the method between first and second order. This variable time-step variable order (VSVO) method performed better than both first and second-order methods in our tests (see

1.3
Motivation For Choice of Estimator for
\epsilon

We choose an estimator to control the residual in the continuity equation,
\|\nabla\cdot{\bf u}_{\epsilon}\|
. The immediate choice is to adapt
\epsilon
based on the size of
\|\nabla\cdot{\bf u}_{\epsilon}\|
. However, controlling the relative, not the absolute error is a more logical choice.
Taking
L^{2}
inner product of (
4
) with
{\bf u}_{\epsilon}
, we get:

\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\|{\bf u}_{\epsilon}\|^{2}+\nu\|\nabla{\bf u}_{\epsilon}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{\epsilon}\|\nabla\cdot{\bf u}_{\epsilon}\|^{2}=(f,{\bf u}_{\epsilon}).

We aim to ensure
\nu\|\nabla{\bf u}_{\epsilon}\|^{2}
does not dominate
\frac{1}{\epsilon}\|\nabla\cdot{\bf u}_{\epsilon}\|^{2}
. This suggests an upper bound for
\epsilon
:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\epsilon}\|\nabla\cdot{\bf u}_{\epsilon}\|^{2}\geq\nu\|\nabla{\bf u}_{\epsilon}\|^{2}\quad\implies\epsilon\leq\frac{1}{\nu}\left(\frac{\|\nabla\cdot{\bf u}_{\epsilon}\|}{\|\nabla{\bf u}_{\epsilon}\|}\right)^{2}.

This motivates the choice of the estimator to be
\|\nabla\cdot{\bf u}_{\epsilon}\|/\|\nabla{\bf u}_{\epsilon}\|,
the relative residual. This has the additional benefits of being non-dimensional and independent of the size of
{\bf u}_{\epsilon}
. Since
\nu
is constant, scaling by
1/\nu
is just a change of adaptive tolerance.
The comparison of absolute and relative residual estimators is presented in Section 6.3.

e^{-A(t)}

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}[e^{-A(T)}\|\phi^{h}\|^{2}]+e^{-A(T)}\Big[\frac{\nu}{4}\|\nabla\phi^{h}\|^{2}+\epsilon(t)\|p^{h}-\lambda^{h}\|^{2}\Big]\leq

\displaystyle e^{-A(T)}\Big\{C(\nu)\Big[\|\eta_{t}\|_{-1}^{2}+\|\nabla{\bf u}_{\epsilon}\|^{2}\|\eta\|_{L_{4}}^{2}+\|{\bf u}_{\epsilon}^{h}\|_{L_{6}}^{2}\|\eta\|_{L_{3}}^{2}\Big]+\frac{1}{2}\|{\bf u}_{\epsilon}^{h}\|_{L_{6}}^{2}\|\nabla\eta\|_{L_{3}}^{2}+\epsilon(t)\|p_{\epsilon}-\lambda^{h}\|^{2}\Big\}.

Integrate over
[0,T]
and multiply through by
e^{A(T)}
gives

\displaystyle\|\phi^{h}(T)\|^{2}+\int_{0}^{T}\frac{\nu}{4}\|\nabla\phi^{h}\|^{2}+\epsilon(t)\|p^{h}-\lambda^{h}\|^{2}\ dt\leq e^{A(T)}\Big\{\|\phi^{h}(0)\|^{2}

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{T}C(\nu)\Big[\|\eta_{t}\|_{-1}^{2}+\|\nabla{\bf u}_{\epsilon}\|^{2}\|\eta\|_{L_{4}}^{2}+\|{\bf u}_{\epsilon}^{h}\|_{L_{6}}^{2}\|\eta\|_{L_{3}}^{2}\Big]+\frac{1}{2}\|{\bf u}_{\epsilon}^{h}\|_{L_{6}}^{2}\|\nabla\eta\|_{L_{3}}^{2}+\epsilon(t)\|p_{\epsilon}-\lambda^{h}\|^{2}\ dt\Big\}.

Applying Hölder’s inequality (
13
) gives

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\|{\bf u}_{\epsilon}^{h}\|_{L_{6}}^{2}\|\eta\|_{L_{3}}^{2}dt

\displaystyle\leq\|{\bf u}_{\epsilon}^{h}\|_{L^{6}(0,T;L^{6})}^{2}\|\eta\|_{L^{3}(0,T;L^{3})}^{2},

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\|\nabla{\bf u}_{\epsilon}\|^{2}\|\eta\|_{L_{4}}^{2}dt

\displaystyle\leq\|\nabla{\bf u}_{\epsilon}\|_{L^{4}(0,T;L^{2})}^{2}\|\eta\|_{L^{4}(0,T;L^{4})}^{2},

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\|{\bf u}_{\epsilon}^{h}\|_{L_{6}}^{2}\|\nabla\eta\|_{L_{3}}^{2}dt

\displaystyle\leq\|{\bf u}_{\epsilon}^{h}\|_{L^{6}(0,T;L^{6})}^{2}\|\nabla\eta\|_{L^{3}(0,T;L^{3})}^{2}.

\|{\bf u}_{\epsilon}^{h}\|_{L^{6}(0,T;L^{6})}
and
\|\nabla{\bf u}_{\epsilon}\|_{L^{4}(0,T;L^{2})}
are bounded by problem data by the stability bound. Using the Sobolev inequality (
16
),
L^{p}-L^{2}
type inverse inequality (
17
), the interpolation estimate (
10
) and the Poincaré-Friedrichs’ inequality (
11
)

\displaystyle\|\eta\|_{L^{3}}\leq C\|\eta\|^{1-d/6}\|\nabla\eta\|^{d/6},\quad\|\eta\|_{L^{4}}\leq C\|\eta\|^{1-d/4}\|\nabla\eta\|^{d/4},

\displaystyle\|\nabla\eta\|_{L^{3}}\leq Ch^{-d/6}\|\nabla\eta\|,\quad\quad\quad\|\eta_{t}\|_{-1}\leq Ch\|\eta_{t}\|\leq Ch\|\nabla\eta_{t}\|.

By Proposition 4.2 and (
25
)

\displaystyle\|\nabla({\bf u}_{\epsilon}-\tilde{{\bf u}})\|^{2}\leq C(\nu)[\inf_{v^{h}\in X^{h}}\|\nabla({\bf u}_{\epsilon}-v^{h})\|^{2}+\inf_{q^{h}\in Q^{h}}\|p_{\epsilon}-q^{h}\|^{2}],

\displaystyle\|{\bf u}_{\epsilon}-\tilde{{\bf u}}\|^{2}\leq C(\nu,\Omega)h^{2}[\inf_{v^{h}\in X^{h}}\|\nabla({\bf u}_{\epsilon}-v^{h})\|^{2}+\inf_{q^{h}\in Q^{h}}\|p_{\epsilon}-q^{h}\|^{2}],

\displaystyle\|\nabla({\bf u}_{\epsilon}-\tilde{{\bf u}})_{t}\|^{2}\leq C(\nu)[\inf_{v^{h}\in X^{h}}\|\nabla({\bf u}_{\epsilon}-v^{h})_{t}\|^{2}+\inf_{q^{h}\in Q^{h}}\|(p_{\epsilon}-q^{h})_{t}\|^{2}],

\displaystyle\|p_{\epsilon}-\lambda^{h}\|^{2}\leq C(\nu,\beta^{h})[\inf_{v^{h}\in X^{h}}\|\nabla({\bf u}_{\epsilon}-v^{h})\|^{2}+\inf_{q^{h}\in Q^{h}}\|p_{\epsilon}-q^{h}\|^{2}].

Thus,

\displaystyle\|\phi^{h}(T)\|^{2}+\int_{0}^{T}\frac{\nu}{4}\|\nabla\phi^{h}\|^{2}+\epsilon(t)\|p^{h}-\lambda^{h}\|^{2}\ dt\leq

\displaystyle e^{A(T)}\Big\{\|\phi^{h}(0)\|^{2}+\int_{0}^{T}C(\nu,\beta^{h})\epsilon(t)\left(\inf_{v^{h}\in X^{h}}\|\nabla({\bf u}_{\epsilon}-v^{h})\|^{2}+\inf_{q^{h}\in Q^{h}}\|p_{\epsilon}-q^{h}\|^{2}\right)\ dt

\displaystyle+C(\nu,\Omega)\Big[(h^{-d/3}+h^{2-d/3}+h^{2-d/2})\left(\inf_{v^{h}\in X^{h}}\|\nabla({\bf u}_{\epsilon}-v^{h})\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2})}^{2}+\inf_{q^{h}\in Q^{h}}\|p_{\epsilon}-q^{h}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2})}\right)

\displaystyle+h^{2}\left(\inf_{v^{h}\in X^{h}}\|\nabla({\bf u}_{\epsilon}-v^{h})_{t}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2})}+\inf_{q^{h}\in Q^{h}}\|(p_{\epsilon}-q^{h})_{t}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2})}\right)\Big]\Big\}.

Using the approximation properties (
8
) of the spaces
(X^{h},Q^{h})

\displaystyle\|\phi^{h}(T)\|^{2}+\int_{0}^{T}\frac{\nu}{4}\|\nabla\phi^{h}\|^{2}+\epsilon(t)\|p^{h}-\lambda^{h}\|^{2}\ dt\leq

\displaystyle e^{A(T)}\Big\{\|\phi^{h}(0)\|^{2}++\int_{0}^{T}C(\nu,\beta^{h})\epsilon(t)h^{2m}\left(\|{\bf u}_{\epsilon}\|^{2}_{H^{m+1}(\Omega)}+\|p_{\epsilon}\|^{2}_{H^{m}(\Omega)}\right)\ dt

\displaystyle+C(\nu,\Omega)\Big[(h^{-d/3}+h^{2-d/3}+h^{2-d/2})h^{2m}\left(\|{\bf u}_{\epsilon}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{m+1}(\Omega))}+\|p_{\epsilon}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{m}(\Omega))}\right)

\displaystyle+h^{2m+2}\left(\|{\bf u}_{\epsilon,t}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{m+1}(\Omega))}+\|p_{\epsilon,t}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{m}(\Omega))}\right)\Big]\Big\}.

Drop the pressure term on the left-hand side and apply triangle inequality, then we have the error estimate.

Next, we extend the algorithm to variable time-step methods based on the previous work by Guzel and Layton
[
8
]
and Layton and McLaughlin
[

]
. We summarize as follows.
In the variable time-step, the first-order and second-order method, the next time-step is adapted based on the following:

\displaystyle\text{first-order prediction}\ k_{new}=k_{old}\left(\frac{tTOL}{tEST_{1}}\right)^{1/2},

\displaystyle\text{second-order prediction}\ k_{new}=k_{old}\left(\frac{tTOL}{tEST_{2}}\right)^{1/3}.

Let
D_{2}
denote the difference

D_{2}(n+1)=\frac{2k_{n}}{k_{n}+k_{n+1}}{\bf u}_{n+1}^{1}-2{\bf u}_{n}+\frac{2k_{n+1}}{k_{n}+k_{n+1}}{\bf u}_{n-1}.

A simple estimate of the local truncation error in the first-order estimation is taken to be the difference between
{\bf u}_{n+1}
and
{\bf u}_{n+1}^{1}

\displaystyle\alpha_{1}=\frac{\tau(1+\tau)}{1+2\tau},

\displaystyle tEST_{1}=\|{\bf u}_{n+1}-{\bf u}_{n+1}^{1}\|=\frac{\alpha_{1}}{2}\|D_{2}(n+1)\|.

And the local truncation error of the second-order method is given by

\displaystyle\alpha_{2}=\frac{\tau_{n}(\tau_{n+1}\tau_{n}+\tau_{n}+1)(4\tau_{n+1}^{3}+5\tau_{n+1}^{2}+\tau_{n+1})}{3(\tau_{n}\tau_{n+1}^{2}+4\tau_{n}\tau_{n+1}+2\tau_{n+1}+\tau_{n}+1)},

\displaystyle tEST_{2}=\frac{\alpha_{2}}{6}\left\|\frac{3k_{n-1}}{k_{n+1}+k_{n}+k_{n-1}}D_{2}(n+1)-\frac{3k_{n+1}}{k_{n+1}+k_{n}+k_{n-1}}D_{2}(n)\right\|.

For both first-order and second-order variable time-step methods,
\epsilon
is still adapted independently using the same decision tree as in Algorithm 1.

Remark 19
.

The estimator
\|\nabla\cdot{\bf u}_{n+1}\|/\|\nabla{\bf u}_{n+1}\|
is chosen over
\|\nabla\cdot{\bf u}_{n+1}\|
as it is dimension free and removes dependence on the size of u.

Next, we consider the variable time-step variable order method. This algorithm computes two velocity approximations.
{\bf u}^{1}
is first-order, and
{\bf u}
is second-order by applying the time filter. The first-order variable time-step method, is unconditionally stable, while the second-order variable time-step method is A-stable, which would require a time-step condition for stability. Combining both first and second order methods by adapting the method order increases accuracy and efficiency.

7
Conclusions and open problems

This paper presents a stability and error analysis for the adaptive
\epsilon
penalty method. Also, four different algorithms for both constant and variable time-step were introduced. There remain open problems and algorithmic improvements possible in the future. In this paper, we introduced the adaptive
\epsilon
scheme with a condition from stability analysis which could ensure the stability of the result. It is unclear how sharp this bound is, or if the restriction (
21
) is necessary in all time-steps. Further, by rejecting and repeating steps to guarantee EST<TOL result in violating the restriction (
21
). For different time-step, the problem has a different optimal
\epsilon
value. An algorithm that adapts
\epsilon
and k independently may be inferior to one that relates the step size to the penalty parameter. However, it there is not an obvious relation between
\epsilon
and k, so further research may be necessary to find a more efficient doubly adaptive algorithm.
The pressure recovered directly from the continuity equation,
\nabla\cdot{\bf u}+\epsilon p=0
(
2
) is not good estimate to the pressure from coupled system. We can look into alternate ways to recover the pressure, such as using the Pressure Poisson equation (PPE) see Kean and Schneier
[
14
]
.
