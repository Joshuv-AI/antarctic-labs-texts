On the asymptotics of cubic fields ordered by general invariants
Arul Shankar, Frank Thorne

Abstract
In this article, we introduce a class of invariants of cubic fields termed “generalized discriminants”. We then obtain asymptotics for the families of cubic fields ordered by these invariants. In addition, we determine which of these families satisfy the Malle–Bhargava heuristic.

Introduction

A foundational result due to Davenport–Heilbronn
[
17
]
provides asymptotics for
the number of real and cubic fields, when these fields are ordered by
their discriminants. Specifically, the theorem states:

Theorem 1
(Davenport–Heilbronn)

Let
N^{\pm}_{\operatorname{Disc}}(X)
denote the number of cubic fields
K
, up to
isomorphism, that satisfy
|\operatorname{Disc}(K)|<X
and
\pm\operatorname{Disc}(K)>0
. Then

\begin{array}[]{rcccl}N^{+}_{\operatorname{Disc}}(X)&=&\displaystyle\frac{1}{12\zeta(3)}X&+&o(X);\\[14.45377pt]
N^{-}_{\operatorname{Disc}}(X)&=&\displaystyle\frac{1}{4\zeta(3)}X&+&o(X).\end{array}

The above theorem, its extensions, and the methods of their proofs, have had a host of applications. Among many other applications, they are used by Yang
[
38
]
to verify the Katz–Sarnak heuristics
[
18
]
for low-lying zeroes of Dedekind zeta
functions of cubic fields; by
Bhargava–Wood
[
11
]
, Belabas–Fouvry
[
3
]
and Wang
[
36
]
to prove Malle’s conjecture for various different Galois
groups; by Martin–Pollack
[
24
]
and Cho–Kim
[
12
]
to obtain
the average value of the smallest prime satisfying certain prescribed
splitting conditions; by AS–Södergren–Templier
[
30
]
to prove that the Dedekind
zeta functions of infinitely many
S_{3}
-cubic fields have negative central values.

Theorem
1
has also been generalized in numerous ways: Belabas–Bhargava–Pomerance
[
2
]
prove power saving error terms; Bhargava
[
4
,
6
]

determines the asymptotics of quartic and quintic fields, when
ordered by discriminant; Datskovsky–Wright
[
16
]
, Taniguchi
[
31
]
, and Bhargava–AS–Wang
[
9
]
count cubic extensions of number fields and function fields; Belabas–Fouvry
[
3
]
count subfamilies of cubic fields satisfying congruence conditions on their discriminants; Terr
[
34
]
proves that the “shapes” of cubic rings and fields are equidistributed (see also work of Bhargava–Harron
[
7
]
, who give a uniform proof that shapes of cubic, quartic, and quintic rings and fields are equidistributed); Taniguchi–FT
[
32
]
and Bhargava–AS–Tsimerman
[
8
]
compute secondary terms (of size
\asymp X^{5/6}
) for the asymptotics of
N^{\pm}_{\operatorname{Disc}}(X)
.

In this paper, we consider generalizations along a different direction: namely, we determine asymptotics for families of
cubic fields ordered by invariants more general than the
discriminant. Let
C(K)
be the radical of
|\operatorname{Disc}(K)|
. That is, we
have
C(K):=\prod_{p\mid\operatorname{Disc}(K)}p
. We then prove the following
result.

Theorem 2

Let
N^{\pm}_{C}(X)
denote the number of cubic fields
K
, up to
isomorphism, that satisfy
C(K)<X
and
\pm\operatorname{Disc}(K)>0
. Then

\begin{array}[]{rcl}N_{C}^{+}(X)&=&\displaystyle\frac{33}{120}\prod_{p}\Bigl(1+\frac{2}{p}\Bigr)\Bigl(1-\frac{1}{p}\Bigr)^{2}X\log X+o(X\log X);\\[14.45377pt]
N_{C}^{-}(X)&=&\displaystyle\left(\frac{3}{10}+\frac{33}{40}\right)\prod_{p}\Bigl(1+\frac{2}{p}\Bigr)\Bigl(1-\frac{1}{p}\Bigr)^{2}X\log X+o(X\log X).\end{array}

Note that we break up the main term in the asymptotics for
N^{-}_{C}(X)
into two summands; they correspond to what can be considered two disjoint subfamilies of cubic fields, namely, the family of pure cubic fields and the family of non-pure cubic fields.

Theorem
2
will be deduced as a special case of a more
general result that counts cubic fields ordered by various different
types of invariants.

Generalized discriminants of cubic fields

Let
M
be a Galois sextic field with Galois group
S_{3}
over
\mathbb{Q}
. Then
K
has three cubic
S_{3}
-subfields, which are conjugate to each other. One would therefore expect to be able to understand the family of sextic
S_{3}
-fields via the
family of cubic
S_{3}
-fields. Bhargava–Wood
[
11
]
and Belabas–Fouvry
[
3
]
independently use this philosophy to prove the
following result.

Theorem 3
(Belabas–Fouvry, Bhargava–Wood)

Let
N^{\pm}_{\Delta_{6}}(X)
denote the number of Galois sextic number
fields
M
with Galois group
S_{3}
, such that
|\operatorname{Disc}(M)|<X
and

\pm\operatorname{Disc}(M)>0
.

Then, we have

N^{\pm}_{\Delta_{6}}(X)=\frac{C^{\pm}}{12}\prod_{p}c_{p}\cdot X^{1/3}+o(X^{1/3}),

where
C^{+}=1,C^{-}=3
, the product is over all primes, and

c_{p}=\begin{cases}(1-p^{-1})(1+p^{-1}+p^{-4/3})&p\neq 3,\\
(1-\frac{1}{3})(\frac{4}{3}+\frac{1}{3^{5/3}}+\frac{2}{3^{7/3}})&p=3.\end{cases}

A power saving error term for the above quantity was obtained by work of Taniguchi–FT
[
33
]
. In this work, they also speculate about a possible secondary term, and discuss tensions between theoretical predictions and the data.

Similarly to
C(K)
, we will regard
|\operatorname{Disc}(M)|
as a “generalized
discriminant” of its cubic subfield. More specifically, let
K
be a non-Galois cubic field, and denote the Galois closure of
K
by
M
. Then
M
has a unique quadratic subfield, denoted
L
. We say that
L
is the
quadratic resolvent field
of
K
. Denote the discriminant of the quadratic resolvent
L
of
K
by
\mathrm{D}(K)
. Then

\mathrm{D}(K)\mid\operatorname{Disc}(K)
, and moreover,
\operatorname{Disc}(K)/\mathrm{D}(K)
is always a
perfect integer square. Denote its positive integer squareroot by

\mathrm{F}(K)
. We note that apart from a factor of a bounded power of
3
,
the quantity
\mathrm{F}(K)
is simply the product of primes that totally
ramify in
K
, where
p
is said to totally ramify in
K
if
p
splits as
p=\mathfrak{p}^{3}
. Similarly, up to a bounded power of
2
, the quantity
\mathrm{D}(K)
is the product of of primes that ramify, but not totally, in
K
. For a cubic
S_{3}
-field
K
, let
\Delta_{6}(K)
denote the
discriminant of the Galois closure
M
of
K
. Then
we have the decompositions

(1)

\operatorname{Disc}(K)=\mathrm{D}(K)\mathrm{F}(K)^{2},\quad\Delta_{6}(K)=\mathrm{D}(K)^{3}\mathrm{F}(K)^{4},\quad C(K)=|\mathrm{D}(K)|\mathrm{F}(K),

where the final equality is true up to bounded factors of
2

and
3
. For positive real numbers
\alpha
and
\beta
, we say that
the invariant
|\mathrm{D}|^{\alpha}\mathrm{F}^{\beta}
is a
generalized
discriminant
. This notion of generalized discriminant encompasses
all three invariants we have seen so far, namely,
\operatorname{Disc}(K)
,

\Delta_{6}(K)
, and
C(K)
.

When
K
is a cyclic cubic field, the invariant
\Delta_{6}(K)
has no special meaning
but an otherwise similar analysis holds with
\mathrm{D}(K):=1
. We also define the above
quantities analogously when
K
is a cubic étale extension of
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
.

\Sigma=(\Sigma_{v})_{v}
be a
collection of cubic

, where for each place

of
\mathbb{Q}

\Sigma_{v}
is the set of cubic étale extensions
of
\mathbb{Q}_{v}

The next result determines asymptotics for the family

Theorem 4

Fix positive real numbers
\alpha
and
\beta
, and let

I=|\mathrm{D}|^{\alpha}\mathrm{F}^{\beta}
be a generalized discriminant. Let
\Sigma

a finite collection of cubic splitting types. Then

(a)

When

N_{I}(\Sigma;X)=\frac{1}{2}\Bigl(\sum_{K\in\Sigma_{\infty}}\frac{1}{|\operatorname{Aut}(K)|}\Bigr)\prod_{p}\Bigl(\sum_{K\in\Sigma_{p}}\frac{|\mathrm{D}(K)|_{p}|\mathrm{F}(K)|_{p}^{\beta/\alpha}}{|\operatorname{Aut}(K)|}\Bigr)\Bigl(1-\frac{1}{p}\Bigr)\cdot X^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}+O_{\epsilon,I}\big((X^{\frac{2}{\alpha+\beta}+\epsilon}+X^{\frac{5}{6\alpha}})P_{\Sigma}^{2/3}\big).

(b)

When
\alpha>\beta

N_{I}(\Sigma;X)=\Bigl(\sum_{d\,{\rm fund.}\,{\rm disc}\neq-3}\frac{\operatorname{Res}_{s=1}\Phi_{\Sigma,d}(s)}{|d|^{\alpha/\beta}}\Bigr)\cdot X^{\frac{1}{\beta}}+O_{\epsilon,I}\big((X^{\frac{3}{2\alpha+\beta}+\epsilon}+X^{\frac{2}{3\beta}+\epsilon})P_{\Sigma}^{1/3}\big),

where

(c)

For the pure cubic fields, Cohen and Morra

(2)

\#\{K\in\mathcal{F}(\Sigma):\mathrm{D}(K)=-3,\mathrm{F}(K)<Z\}=C_{1}Z(\log(Z)+C_{2}-1)+O(Z^{2/3+\epsilon}),

The Malle–Bhargava heuristics

In
[
22
,
23
]
, Malle develops heuristics for asymptotics of the number of degree-
n
number fields with Galois group
G
and bounded discriminant, where
n>1
is any integer and
G
is a finite group with an action on a set with
n
elements. These heuristics are believed to be true in most cases. However, see
[
20
]
where Klüners demonstrates a counter example in the case
n=3
and
G=C_{3}\wr C_{2}
, and
[
35
]
, where Türkelli modifies Malle’s conjecture so that it holds in the above and similar cases. While Malle’s conjecture has been formulated only for families of fields ordered by discriminant, the same method applies to other orderings, in particular to the generalized discriminants that we work with.

Interestingly, the leading constants appearing in front of Malle’s heuristics are still shrouded with mystery. In the case of degree-
n

S_{n}
number fields ordered by discriminant, Bhargava
[
5
]
formulates a conjecture for the leading coefficients, using a general recipe which constructs these constants from mass formulae counting étale extensions of local fields. Once again, this recipe is quite general, applying to any family of number fields
constructed as follows: fix a degree
n>1
and a group
G
with a transitive action on the set
\{1,\ldots,n\}
. Then this recipe applies to the family of all degree-
n
number fields with Galois group
G
, satisfying any finite set of splitting conditions, ordered by any generalized discriminant. (See also work of Kedlaya
[
19
]
describing how these leading constants can be computed in the more general case of families of Galois representations.)
However, there are many instances where this prediction gives the incorrect leading constant. The prototypical example is the family of quartic
D_{4}
-fields ordered by discriminant, where the asymptotic constant determined by Cohen–Diaz y Diaz–Olivier
[
13
]
is not expected to equal the constant that this recipe would predict. On the other hand, when quartic
D_{4}
-fields are ordered by conductor, Altuğ–AS–Varma–Wilson
[
1
]
establish that the leading asymptotic constant does arise from the Malle–Bhargava recipe. This leads to the natural question, as
discussed by Bhargava in
[
5
]
, of which families of number fields ordered by which invariants satisfy this property.

We say that a family
F
of number fields, ordered by some generalized discriminant, satisfies the
Malle–Bhargava heuristic
if the asymptotics of every subfamily defined by prescribed splitting at finitely many primes are as predicted by the Malle–Bhargava recipe. (Despite our terminology,
we emphasize again that Bhargava conjectured this only for
S_{n}
, and did not predict that it should always hold.)

A necessary condition is that the splitting behaviour of primes is independent. We now precisely define this notion. Let
\mathcal{G}
be a family of number fields having the same degree

n
.
2
2

2

It is not entirely clear exactly what constitutes a
family
of number fields. Being the set of all number fields
having the same degree and the same Galois group is assumed to be a
sufficient though not a necessary condition. See
[
28

, where a similar question is discussed in detail.
Let
\Sigma=(\Sigma_{v})_{v}
be a
collection of degree-
n
splitting types
, where for each place
v
of
\mathbb{Q}
,

\Sigma_{v}
is the set of degree-
n
étale extensions of
\mathbb{Q}_{v}
satisfying specified inertial and ramification behaviour.
For
each place
v
, let
\Sigma_{v}^{\rm{all}}
denote the set of all degree-
n

étale extensions of
\mathbb{Q}_{v}
. Then
\Sigma
is said to be
finite

if
\Sigma_{p}=\Sigma_{p}^{\rm{all}}
for all sufficiently large primes
p
.
Let
h:\mathcal{G}\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}
be a height function (i.e., there are only
finitely many elements of
\mathcal{G}
having bounded height). Let

N_{h}(\mathcal{G}_{\Sigma};X)
denote the number of elements in
\mathcal{G}
satisfying
\Sigma

having height less than
X
. Then we say that the family
\mathcal{G}
ordered
by

satisfies
independence of primes
if the following is
true. For all places
v

\mathbb{Q}
, there exist functions

\sigma_{v}:\Sigma_{v}^{\rm{all}}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
with

\sum_{K_{v}\in\Sigma_{v}^{\rm{all}}}\sigma_{v}(K_{v})=1,

such that the following condition is satisfied. For each finite

\Sigma
, we have

N_{h}(\mathcal{G}_{\Sigma};X)\sim\Bigl(\prod_{v}\sum_{K_{v}\in\Sigma_{v}}\sigma_{v}(K_{v})\Bigr)\cdot N_{h}(\mathcal{G};X).

There are many known examples of families of number fields which do not satisfy independence of primes. See for example
[
37
]
, in which Wood studies families of number fields with any fixed abelian Galois group, and proves in many cases that, when ordered by discriminant, these families do not satisfy independence of primes. We note that the notion of satisfying independence of primes is a weaker notion than that of satisfying the Malle–Bhargava heuristic, when both these notions make sense. Moreover, independence of primes can be defined for a wider class of families, for example, this notion makes sense for the family of pure cubic fields, the family of monogenic degree-
n
fields, and many other families for which the Malle–Bhargava heuristics do not apply.

Next, we consider the family of all cubic fields. It is natural to partition this family into two subfamilies: the family of pure cubics and the family of non-pure cubics. The ordering on the family of pure cubic fields coming from any generalized discriminant is the same (since we have
\mathrm{D}(K)=-3
for every pure cubic field
K
). It follows from the method of Cohen–Morra
[
14
]
described in §2.2 that the family of pure cubic fields satisfies independence of primes.
For the family of non-pure cubic fields ordered by generalized
discriminants, we have the following result.

Theorem 6

Let
I=|\mathrm{D}|^{\alpha}\mathrm{F}^{\beta}
be a generalized discriminant. Then
the family of all non-pure cubic fields
ordered by
I
satisfies independence of primes and the Malle–Bhargava heuristic if and only if
\alpha\leq\beta
.

For the
\alpha\leq\beta
case, the above result is an immediate consequence of Theorem
4
. This
\alpha>\beta
case requires a bit more
work, since the residues of the Dirichlet series appearing in Part (b) of Theorem
4
are not explicit. We give a general proof which also applies to many different situations, such as the family of quartic
D_{4}
-fields ordered by discriminant.

Finally, our counting results also have implications towards families of Artin
L
-functions associated to cubic
S_{3}
-fields. Indeed, let
\rho:S_{3}\to\operatorname{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
be any representation of
S_{3}
. Given a cubic
S_{3}
-field
K
, with normal closure
M
, we obtain a Galois representation

\operatorname{Gal}(\overline{\mathbb{Q}}/\mathbb{Q})\to\operatorname{Gal}(M/\mathbb{Q})\cong S_{3}\to\operatorname{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{C}),

where the final map is
\rho
. We associate to this Galois representation its Artin
L
-function, denoted
L(s;\rho,K)
. Throughout, we assume that
\rho
contains at least one copy of the standard representation of
S_{3}
, which is necessary to ensure that different cubic fields give rise to different
L
-functions. Then, given a family
\mathcal{F}(\Sigma)
of cubic
S_{3}
-fields
K
, we obtain a family of Artin
L
-functions

that we denote by
{\mathcal{L}}(\rho,\Sigma)
. We order the
L
-functions in
{\mathcal{L}}(\rho,\Sigma)
by their conductors.

Ordering
{\mathcal{L}}(\rho,\Sigma)
by conductor corresponds to ordering
\mathcal{F}(\Sigma)
by a certain generalized discriminant
I=|\mathrm{D}|^{\alpha}\mathrm{F}^{\beta}
depending on
\rho
. Indeed, we have

(\alpha,\beta)=c_{1}(1,2)+c_{2}(1,0)
where
c_{1}\geq 1
and
c_{2}\geq 0
are the multiplicities of
the standard and sign representations respectively, so that
\alpha>0
and
\beta>0
.
A consequence of Theorem
4
is that the family
{\mathcal{L}}(\rho,\Sigma)
satisfies Sato–Tate equidistribution in the sence of
[
28
, Conjecture 1]
. Loosely speaking, a family of
L
-functions arising from number fields satisfies Sato–Tate equidistribution when the asymptotics of these number fields, ordered by the conductors of their
L
-functions, satisfy the Malle-Bhargava heuristics on average over primes
p
. Identically to the arguments in
[
29
, §3.1]
, when
\alpha\leq\beta
, this follows immediately from the shape of the leading constant in Parts (a) and (c) of Theorem
4
. When
\alpha>\beta
the situation is similar to the case of the family of Dedekind zeta functions of
D_{4}
-fields considered in
[
29
, §6.2]
. As there, we consider the family of cubic fields ordered by
I
to be a countable union of subfamilies, one for each fixed quadratic resolvent field. Since each of these subfamilies contributes a positive proportion to the full family, Sato–Tate equidistribution for the full family follows from Sato–Tate equidistribution for each subfamily. Thus, we have the following consequence to Theorem
4
:

Corollary 7

With notation as above, the families
{\mathcal{L}}(\rho,\Sigma)
satisfy Sato–Tate equidistribution.

It is interesting to note that despite independence of primes not always holding, Sato–Tate equidistribution is always satisfied for our families.

Organization of the paper

We begin in §2 by considering families of cubic fields with one fixed invariant. Invoking work of Bhargava–Taniguchi–FT
[
10
]
on the Davenport-Heilbronn theorem, we obtain asymptotics for families of cubic fields with fixed
\mathrm{F}
; using work of Cohen–Morra
[
14
]
and Cohen–FT
[
15
]
on a Kummer-theoretic approach, we deduce asymptotics for families of cubic fields with fixed
\mathrm{D}
. The leading constants appearing in the asymptotics for the latter family are somewhat inexplicit, but in §3 we prove that the average values of these constants have an explicit description given in terms of products of mass formulae.

The results of the previous two subsections allow us to determine asymptotics for
\mathcal{F}(\Sigma)
ordered by generalized discriminants. This is accomplished in §4, and we extract secondary terms and power saving error terms when possible. We then establish exactly when independence of primes holds, thereby proving Theorem
6
. Finally, we conclude in §5 by presenting some numerical data.

, which gives an expression for

of characters of

with the following modifications:

Step 3 – Interpretation in terms of field counting.

For

H_{\mathfrak{a}^{\prime}}:=\big(\textnormal{Cl}_{\mathfrak{a}^{\prime}}(\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-3d}))/\textnormal{Cl}_{\mathfrak{a}^{\prime}}^{3}(\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-3d}))\big)[1+\tau]

We turn now to the analytic consequences. Let
d\neq-3
be a fundamental discriminant and let
\Sigma

Define the quantity

Then we have the following consequence of Theorem

Theorem 13

Let
d
and
\Sigma
be as above.
Then we have

N(\mathcal{F}(\Sigma)_{d};Z)=\operatorname{Res}_{s=1}\bigl(\Phi_{\Sigma,d}(s)\bigr)\cdot Z+O_{\epsilon}\Bigl(|{\mathcal{L}}_{3}(P_{\Sigma},d)||d|^{1/6}P_{\Sigma}^{1/3}Z^{2/3+\epsilon}\Bigr).

Proof:

As this is standard, we give a brief account. Write the left side as

\#\bigl\{K\in\mathcal{F}(\Sigma)_{d}:\mathrm{F}(K)<Z\bigr\}=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{2-i\infty}^{2+i\infty}\Phi_{\Sigma,d}(s)\frac{Z^{s}}{s}ds.

We then write each Euler product in
\Phi_{\Sigma,d}(s)

as the product of a Dedekind zeta function
\zeta_{\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-3d})}(s)
or an irreducible degree
2
Artin
L
-function, times
a function holomorphic and bounded in any half plane
\Re(s)\geq\sigma_{0}>\frac{1}{2}
.

As all of the
L
-functions have conductor
\ll|d|P_{\Sigma}^{2}
, the convexity bound yields

\bigg|\Phi_{\Sigma,d}(s)-\frac{\operatorname{Res}_{s=1}\bigl(\Phi_{\Sigma,d}(s)\bigr)}{s-1}\bigg|\ll|{\mathcal{L}}_{3}(P_{\Sigma},d)|\cdot|d|^{1/4}\big((1+t)P_{\Sigma}\big)^{1/2},

uniformly in
\Re(s)=\sigma+it
with

1>\sigma\geq\sigma_{0}
.

Pick
T>1
to be optimized later. We shift the contour to the left, picking up one residue at
1
, ending up with a sum of the following integrals: from
1+\epsilon\pm iT
to

1+\epsilon\pm i\infty
; from
1+\epsilon\pm iT
to
\sigma_{0}\pm iT
; and from
\sigma_{0}-iT
to
\sigma_{0}+iT
. The residue gives the required main term, while the sum of these integrals is

\ll|{\mathcal{L}}_{3}(P_{\Sigma},d)|\cdot\left(\frac{Z^{1+\epsilon}}{T}+Z^{\sigma_{0}}T^{1/2}|d|^{1/4}P_{\Sigma}^{1/2}\right).

The result now follows by optimizing the value of
T
to be
Z^{1/3}/(d^{1/6}P_{\Sigma}^{1/3})
.

\Box

Remark 14

In the case

, we obtain the asymptotic (

this
generalizes to

(13)

\#\{K\in\mathcal{F}(\Sigma):\mathrm{D}(K)=-3,\mathrm{F}(K)<Z\}=C_{1}(\Sigma)Z(\log(Z)+C_{2}(\Sigma)-1)+O(P_{\Sigma}^{1/3+\epsilon}Z^{2/3+\epsilon}),

where

C_{1}(\Sigma):=C_{1}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\mid P_{\Sigma}\\
p\neq 3\end{subarray}}\frac{p}{3p+6}\cdot\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\mid P_{\Sigma}\\
p=3\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{7}

3
The asymptotics of cubic fields with bounded invariants

For a finite collection
\Sigma
of splitting types and positive real numbers
Y
and
Z
, define

N(\Sigma;Y,Z):=\bigl\{K\in\mathcal{F}(\Sigma):3\neq|\mathrm{D}(K)|<Y,\;\mathrm{F}(K)<Z\bigr\}.

In this section, we compute asymptotics for
N(\Sigma;Y,Z)
. We will handle the ‘large
Y
case’
(i.e., large
\frac{\log Y}{\log Z}
)
using Theorem
8
and the
‘small
Y
case’ using Theorem
13
. Our error terms are strong enough that these ranges of
Y
overlap, yielding
an asymptotic estimate for all
Y
and
Z
.
Indeed, we obtain asymptotic formulas with different expressions for the main terms, which we may then conclude are equal.

These results will be used in the proofs of Theorems
2
and

4
(c), where
\mathrm{D}
and
\mathrm{F}
are given equal weight. For parts (a) and (b)
of Theorem
4
we will instead use a more direct approach so as to optimize the error terms.
All of these proofs will be given
in Section
4
.

We begin with the following important uniformity estimate due to
Davenport–Heilbronn (see, e.g.,
[
2
, Lemma 3.3]
).

Lemma 15

The number of cubic fields
K
such that
|\operatorname{Disc}(K)|<X
and
\mathrm{F}(K)=f

is bounded by
O_{\epsilon}(X^{1+\epsilon}/f^{2})
.

The key result of this section is the following proposition:

Proposition 16

Let
\Sigma
be an finite collection of splitting types, and let
Y
and
Z
be positive real numbers. Then

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\displaystyle N(\Sigma;Y,Z)&=&\displaystyle\Bigl(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}f<Z\end{subarray}}C_{1}(\Sigma,f)\Bigr)\cdot Y+O_{\epsilon}(Y^{5/6}Z^{2/3}+Y^{2/3+\epsilon}Z^{4/3}P_{\Sigma}^{2/3});\\[18.06749pt]
\displaystyle N(\Sigma;Y,Z)&=&\displaystyle\Bigl(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|d|<Y\\
{\rm fund.disc}\neq-3\end{subarray}}\operatorname{Res}_{s=1}\Phi_{\Sigma,d}(s)\Bigr)\cdot Z+O_{\epsilon,\Sigma}\Bigl(Y^{7/6+\epsilon}Z^{2/3+\epsilon}P_{\Sigma}^{1/3+\epsilon}\Bigr).\end{array}

Proof:

To prove the first equality, we fiber by
\mathrm{F}(K)
and apply Theorem

8
(Davenport-Heilbronn), obtaining

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\displaystyle N(\Sigma;Y,Z)&=&\displaystyle\sum_{f<Z}N(\mathcal{F}(\Sigma)^{(f)};Y)\\[14.45377pt]
&=&\displaystyle\Bigl(\sum_{f<Z}C_{1}(\Sigma,f)\Bigr)\cdot Y+O\Bigl(\sum_{f<Z}(f^{-1/3}Y^{5/6}+E(Y;f,\Sigma))\Bigr)\\[14.45377pt]
&=&\displaystyle\Bigl(\sum_{f<Z}C_{1}(\Sigma,f)\Bigr)\cdot Y+O\Bigl(Y^{5/6}Z^{2/3}+Y^{2/3+\epsilon}Z^{4/3}P_{\Sigma}^{2/3}\Bigr),\end{array}

as necessary.
To prove the second equality, we fiber by
\mathrm{D}(K)
and
apply Theorem
13
(Cohen-Morra):

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\displaystyle N(\Sigma;Y,Z)&=&\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|d|<Y\\
{\rm fund.disc}\neq-3\end{subarray}}N(\mathcal{F}(\Sigma)_{d};Z)\\[14.45377pt]
&=&\displaystyle\Bigl(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|d|<Y\\
{\rm fund.disc}\neq-3\end{subarray}}\operatorname{Res}_{s=1}\Phi_{\Sigma,d}(s)\Bigr)\cdot Z+O_{\Sigma,\epsilon}\Bigl(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|d|<Y\\
{\rm fund.disc}\neq-3\end{subarray}}|{\mathcal{L}}_{3}(P_{\Sigma},d)||d|^{1/6}Z^{2/3+\epsilon}P_{\Sigma}^{1/3}\Bigr)\\[14.45377pt]
&=&\displaystyle\Bigl(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|d|<Y\\
{\rm fund.disc}\neq-3\end{subarray}}\operatorname{Res}_{s=1}\Phi_{\Sigma,d}(s)\Bigr)\cdot Z+O_{\Sigma,\epsilon}(Y^{7/6+\epsilon}Z^{2/3+\epsilon}P_{\Sigma}^{1/3+\epsilon}),\end{array}

where the bound on the sum over
d
of the sizes of
{\mathcal{L}}_{3}(P_{\Sigma},d)
follows from Lemma

15
.
This concludes the proof of the proposition.

\Box

Next, we estimate the leading constant in the right hand side of the
first equation of Proposition
16
.

Proposition 17

We have

\displaystyle\sum_{f<Z}C_{1}(\Sigma,f)=\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\Bigl(\sum_{K\in\Sigma_{\infty}}\frac{1}{|\operatorname{Aut}(K)|}\Bigr)\prod_{p}\Bigl(\sum_{K\in\Sigma_{p}}\frac{|\mathrm{D}(K)|_{p}|\mathrm{F}(K)|_{p}}{|\operatorname{Aut}(K)|}\Bigr)\Bigl(1-\frac{1}{p}\Bigr)^{2}\cdot Z+O_{\epsilon}(Z^{3/5+\epsilon});

Proof:

Recall the Dirichlet series
L_{1}(\Sigma,s):=\sum_{f}C_{1}(\Sigma,f)f^{-s}
from §2.1. It is easy to see that

L_{1}(\Sigma,s)
is holomorphic to the right of
\Re(s)>1/2
with a simple pole
at
s=1
. Indeed, the shape of
C_{1}(\Sigma,f)
described in (
6
) implies that
L_{1}(\Sigma,s)/\zeta(s)
converges absolutely and is bounded uniformly in
\Sigma
and
s

to the right of
\Re(s)=\sigma
for any
\sigma>1/2
. Pick a real number
T
to be optimized later. Following the proof of Theorem
13
, we have

(14)

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\displaystyle\sum_{f<Z}C_{1}(\Sigma,f)&=&\displaystyle\int_{\Re(s)=2}L_{1}(\Sigma,s)(s)\frac{Z^{s}}{s}ds\\[14.45377pt]
&=&\displaystyle\operatorname{Res}_{s=1}L_{1}(\Sigma,s)\cdot Z+O_{\epsilon}\Bigl(\frac{Z^{1+\epsilon}}{T}+Z^{1/2+\epsilon}T^{1/4}\Bigr),\end{array}

where we use the convex bound to estimate the growth of
\zeta(s)
(and therefore
L_{1}(\Sigma,s)
) on the line
\Re(s)=1/2+\epsilon
.
From the Euler product expansion of
L_{1}(\Sigma,s)
derived in Proposition
9
, it follows that the residue of
L_{1}(\Sigma,s)
at
s=1
is given by

(15)

\operatorname{Res}_{s=1}L_{1}(\Sigma,s)=\frac{1}{2}\Bigl(\sum_{K\in\Sigma_{\infty}}\frac{1}{|\operatorname{Aut}(K)|}\Bigr)\prod_{p}\Bigl(\sum_{K\in\Sigma_{p}}\frac{|\mathrm{D}(K)|_{p}|\mathrm{F}(K)|_{p}}{|\operatorname{Aut}(K)|}\Bigr)\Bigl(1-\frac{1}{p}\Bigr)^{2}.

The proposition follows by choosing
T=Z^{2/5}
.

\Box

The above two propositions have the following consequence:

Corollary 18

We have

\frac{1}{Y}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|d|<Y\\
{\rm fund.disc}\neq-3\end{subarray}}\operatorname{Res}_{s=1}\Phi_{\Sigma,d}(s)=\frac{1}{2}\prod_{p}\Bigl(\sum_{K\in\Sigma_{p}}\frac{|\mathrm{D}(K)|_{p}|\mathrm{F}(K)|_{p}}{|\operatorname{Aut}(K)|}\Bigr)\Bigl(1-\frac{1}{p}\Bigr)^{2}+O_{\epsilon,\Sigma}(Y^{-1/12+\epsilon}).

Proof:

The result follows from Propositions
16
and

17
by setting
Z=Y^{3/4}
.

\Box

In particular, the two estimates of Proposition
16
are asymptotic formulas
for
Y>Z^{1+\epsilon}
and
Y<Z^{2-\epsilon}
, respectively. Since these ranges overlap, we obtain
the following result.

Theorem 19

We have

N(\Sigma;Y,Z)=\frac{1}{2}\Bigl(\sum_{K\in\Sigma_{\infty}}\frac{1}{|\operatorname{Aut}(K)|}\Bigr)\prod_{p}\Bigl(\sum_{K\in\Sigma_{p}}\frac{|\mathrm{D}(K)|_{p}|\mathrm{F}(K)|_{p}}{|\operatorname{Aut}(K)|}\Bigr)\Bigl(1-\frac{1}{p}\Bigr)^{2}\cdot YZ+o_{\Sigma}(Y)Z+Yo_{\Sigma}(Z).

Proof:

Combining the above results yields the claimed result with an error term

\ll_{\epsilon,\Sigma}(YZ)^{\epsilon}\big(YZ^{3/5}+Y^{11/12}Z+\min(Y^{2/3}Z^{4/3},Y^{7/6}Z^{2/3})\big),

which is sufficiently small.

\Box

4
Ordering cubic fields by generalized discriminants

In this section we determine asymptotics for the number of cubic
fields with bounded generalized discriminant, thereby proving Theorem

4
. We also then determine which generalized discriminants
I

are such that the family of cubic fields ordered by

independence of primes, thus also proving Theorem
6
.

For a generalized discriminant
I=|\mathrm{D}|^{\alpha}\mathrm{F}^{\beta}
, after normalizing we may assume
that one of
\alpha
or
\beta
equals
1
and the other is
\geq 1
. We handle each of the three possible cases
in turn.

Proposition 20

For a finite collection
\Sigma
of cubic splitting types and a
real number
\beta>1
, we have

N_{|\mathrm{D}|F^{\beta}}(\Sigma;X)=L_{1}(\Sigma,\beta)\cdot X+L_{2}(\Sigma,5\beta/6)X^{5/6}+O_{\epsilon,\beta}\big((X^{\frac{2}{\beta+1}+\epsilon}+X^{\frac{2}{3}+\epsilon})P_{\Sigma}^{2/3}\big),

with
L_{1}(\Sigma,\beta)
and
L_{2}(\Sigma,5\beta/6)
as given in Proposition
9
.

Proof:

We fiber over
f\geq 1
and write

N_{|\mathrm{D}|F^{\beta}}(\Sigma,X)=\sum_{f}N(\mathcal{F}(\Sigma)^{(f)};f^{2-\beta}X)+O(X^{\frac{1}{\beta}}\log(X)),

where

N(\mathcal{F}(\Sigma)^{(f)};f^{2-\beta}X)
denotes the
number of cubic fields
K\in\mathcal{F}(\Sigma)^{(f)}
such that
|\operatorname{Disc}(K)|<f^{2-\beta}X
,
and the error term accounts for the pure cubic fields.
For any
1<Y\ll X^{1/\beta}
, by Lemma
15
we have

\displaystyle\sum_{f\geq Y}N(\mathcal{F}(\Sigma)^{(f)};f^{2-\beta}X)\ll_{\epsilon}\displaystyle\sum_{f\geq Y}\frac{f^{2-\beta+\epsilon}X^{1+\epsilon}}{f^{2}}\ll_{\epsilon}\ \frac{X^{1+\epsilon}}{Y^{\beta-1-\epsilon}}.

For
f<Y
, by Theorem
8
we have

\displaystyle\sum_{f<Y}N(\mathcal{F}(\Sigma)^{(f)};f^{2-\beta}X)=\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{f<Y}\Bigl(\frac{C_{1}(\Sigma,f)}{f^{\beta}}X+\frac{C_{2}(\Sigma,f)}{f^{5\beta/6}}X^{5/6}+O\bigl(E(X/f^{\beta},f,\Sigma)\bigr)\Bigr).

The error term is bounded by

\displaystyle\sum_{k<\log_{2}Y}\sum_{2^{k}\leq f<2^{k+1}}E(X/f^{\beta};f,\Sigma)\

\displaystyle\ll\sum_{k<\log_{2}Y}X^{2/3+\epsilon}(2^{k})^{4/3-2\beta/3+\epsilon}P_{\Sigma}^{2/3}

\displaystyle\ll\displaystyle X^{2/3+\epsilon}P_{\Sigma}^{2/3}\cdot\max(Y^{4/3-2\beta/3+\epsilon},1).

Meanwhile, the two main terms are:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\displaystyle\sum_{f<Y}\frac{C_{1}(\Sigma,f)}{f^{\beta}}&=&\displaystyle\sum_{f\geq 1}\frac{C_{1}(\Sigma,f)}{f^{\beta}}+O_{\beta}(Y^{1-\beta})\\[14.45377pt]
&=&\displaystyle L_{1}(\Sigma,\beta)+O_{\beta}(Y^{1-\beta});\\[3.61371pt]
\displaystyle\sum_{f<Y}\frac{C_{2}(\Sigma,f)}{f^{5\beta/6}}&=&\displaystyle\sum_{f\geq 1}\frac{C_{2}(\Sigma,f)}{f^{5\beta/6}}+O_{\beta}(Y^{1-5\beta/6})\\[14.45377pt]
&=&\displaystyle L_{2}(\Sigma,5\beta/6)+O_{\beta}(Y^{1-5\beta/6}).\end{array}

Optimizing (in
X
aspect), we
pick
Y=X^{\frac{1}{\beta+1}}
and obtain the result.

\Box

Proposition 21

For a finite collection
\Sigma
of cubic splitting types and a
real number
\alpha>1
, we have

N_{|\mathrm{D}|^{\alpha}\mathrm{F}}(\Sigma;X)=\Bigl(\sum_{d\,{\rm fund.}\,{\rm disc}\neq-3}\frac{\operatorname{Res}_{s=1}\Phi_{\Sigma,d}(s)}{|d|^{\alpha}}\Bigr)\cdot X+O_{\epsilon,\alpha}((X^{3/(2\alpha+1)+\epsilon}+X^{2/3+\epsilon})P_{\Sigma}^{1/3}).

We fiber over

To estimate the main term, we use Theorem

to write

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\,{\rm fund.}\,{\rm disc}\neq-3\\
|d|<Y\end{subarray}}N(\mathcal{F}(\Sigma)_{d};X/|d|^{\alpha})=\Bigl(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\,{\rm fund.}\,{\rm disc}\neq-3\\

We have

, we
obtain

\#\bigl\{K\in\mathcal{F}:C(K)<X,\,\mathrm{D}(K)\neq-3,\,\pm\operatorname{Disc}(K)>0\bigr\}=\frac{1}{2\sigma_{\pm}}\prod_{p}{\mathcal{C}}(p)\Bigl(1-\frac{1}{p}\Bigr)^{2}\cdot X\log X+o(X\log X),

where for any prime
p
, the quantity
{\mathcal{C}}(p)
is defined to be

{\mathcal{C}}(p):=\sum_{K\in\Sigma_{p}^{\rm{all}}}\frac{|{\rm rad}(|\mathrm{D}(K)|\mathrm{F}(K))|_{p}}{|\operatorname{Aut}(K)|}.

To compute these constants, we use the database of local fields available at
lmfdb.org

[
21
]
, which lists each
quadratic or cubic ramified extension of
\mathbb{Q}_{2}
and
\mathbb{Q}_{3}
with its Galois group; we obtain that

{\mathcal{C}}(2)=3
and

{\mathcal{C}}(3)=11/3
.

Finally, to count the contribution of the pure cubic fields, observe that we have

\sum_{\mathrm{D}(K)=-3}C(K)^{-s}=-\frac{1}{2}\cdot 3^{-s}+\frac{3}{2}\cdot 3^{-s}\prod_{p\neq 3}\big(1+\frac{2}{p^{s}}\big).

by
[
14
, Proposition 7.3]
. By an argument identical to that of
Theorem
13
or

[
14
, Proposition 7.4]
, we have

\#\bigl\{K\in\mathcal{F}:C(K)<X,\,\mathrm{D}(K)=-3,\,\pm\operatorname{Disc}(K)>0\bigr\}=\frac{3}{10}\prod_{p}\Bigl(1+\frac{2}{p}\Bigr)\Bigl(1-\frac{1}{p}\Bigr)^{2}\cdot X\log X+o(X\log X),

thereby completing the proof.
\Box

Proof of Theorem
6
:

For
\alpha\leq\beta
this follows immediately from the shape
of the leading asymptotics in parts (a) and (c) of Theorem
4
.
It remains to
prove the result when
\alpha>\beta
, and as before we may assume
that
\beta=1
. For fixed
\epsilon>0
let
N(\epsilon)
be
the smallest positive integer such that the following inequality is
satisfied:

(17)

\epsilon\cdot\frac{\operatorname{Res}_{s=1}\Phi_{\Sigma^{\rm{all}},5}}{5^{\alpha}}>\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|d|>N(\epsilon)\\
{\rm fund.disc.}\end{subarray}}\frac{\operatorname{Res}_{d=1}\Phi_{\Sigma^{\rm{all}},d}}{|d|^{\alpha}}.

Such an
N(\epsilon)
exists for each
\epsilon
since the sum of

\operatorname{Res}_{d=1}(\Phi_{\Sigma^{\rm{all}},d})/|d|^{\alpha}
is convergent, as can be seen from
Corollary
18
for example.

For
each fundamental discriminant
d
with
3,5\neq|d|\leq N(\epsilon)
, now let

p_{d}\neq 7
be a prime such that the splitting types of
p_{d}
at

\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{5})
and
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{d})
differ. (For
d=1
, we choose
p_{1}
to be inert in
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{5})
.)
Define

\Sigma_{p_{d}}^{(\epsilon)}
to be the set of all étale cubic
extensions of
\mathbb{Q}_{p_{d}}
whose quadratic resolvents
are equal to
\mathbb{Q}_{p_{d}}(\sqrt{5})\oplus\mathbb{Q}_{p_{d}}
.
We then define the collection
\Sigma^{(\epsilon)}
by taking

\Sigma_{p_{d}}^{(\epsilon)}
as above, and choosing

\Sigma_{p}^{(\epsilon)}=\Sigma_{p}^{\rm{all}}
for
p
not equal to any of the

p_{d}
.

Then (
17
) holds with
\Sigma^{{\rm{all}}}
replaced by
\Sigma^{\epsilon}
and

|d|>N(\epsilon)
replaced by
d\neq-3,5
, as
the newly imposed splitting conditions do not exclude any of the fields counted on the left,
nor do they include any of the fields added to the right.
Since
K\otimes\mathbb{Q}_{7}\not\cong\mathbb{Q}_{7}^{3}
for any
K
with resolvent
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{5})
, this implies that

0<\lim_{X\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\#\{K\in\mathcal{F}(\Sigma^{(\epsilon)}):\mathrm{D}(K)\neq-3,\,\mathrm{D}(K)^{\alpha}\mathrm{F}(K)<X,\,K\otimes\mathbb{Q}_{7}\cong\mathbb{Q}_{7}^{3}\}}{\#\{K\in\mathcal{F}(\Sigma^{(\epsilon)}):\mathrm{D}(K)\neq-3,\,\mathrm{D}(K)^{\alpha}\mathrm{F}(K)<X\}}<\frac{\epsilon}{1+\epsilon}<\epsilon.

In particular, since
7
does not split in
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{5})
, the probability of the prime
7
splitting
completely in
\mathcal{F}(\Sigma^{(\epsilon)})
goes to
0
as
\epsilon
tends to
0
.
Moreover, the probability that
7
splits completely in
\mathcal{F}(\Sigma^{\rm{all}})
is positive, since
7
splits completely a positive proportion of the time in cubic fields with resolvent, say,
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-19})
.
The result now follows from the fact that
\Sigma^{(\epsilon)}_{7}
is constant for all

\epsilon
.

\Box
