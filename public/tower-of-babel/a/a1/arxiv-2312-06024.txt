Thinking Assistants : LLM-Based Conversational Assistants that Help Users Think By Asking rather than Answering
Soya Park, Hari Subramonyam, Chinmay Kulkarni

Abstract
. Many AI systems focus solely on providing solutions or explaining outcomes. However, complex tasks like research and strategic thinking often benefit from a more comprehensive approach to augmenting the thinking process rather than passively getting information. We introduce the concept of “Thinking Assistants”, a new genre of assistants that help users improve decision-making with a combination of asking reflection questions based on expert knowledge. Through our lab study (N=80), these Large Language Model (LLM) based Thinking Assistants were better able to guide users to make important decisions, compared with conversational agents that only asked questions, provided advice, or neither. Based on the results, we develop a Thinking Assistant in academic career development, determining research trajectory or developing one’s unique research identity, which requires deliberation, reflection and experts’ advice accordingly. In a longitudinal deployment with 223 conversations, participants responded positively to approximately 65% of the responses. Our work proposes directions for developing more effective LLM agents. Rather than adhering to the prevailing authoritative approach of generating definitive answers, LLM agents aimed at assisting with cognitive enhancement should prioritize fostering reflection. They should initially provide responses designed to prompt thoughtful consideration through inquiring, followed by offering advice only after gaining a deeper understanding of the user’s context and needs.



1.
Introduction

Individuals often struggle with reflective thinking, especially when facing substantial decisions such as career transitions or evaluating competing job offers. This difficulty stems in part from the complexity of conducting comparisons along multiple dimensions, only some of which are readily apparent. Such decisions are frequently associated with rumination, i.e. conscious, repetitive thought, but rumination does not always lead to better or more creative decision-making
(
Cohen and Ferrari 2010
;
Treynor et al. 2003
)
. Critically, for better decision-making, individuals need to engage in productive self-reflection. i.e. repeated thinking that allows for elaboration of ideas, and one’s relationship to them
(
Verhaeghen et al. 2005
)
. For non-experts and newcomers (such as graduate students considering alternative research careers, or those transitioning careers), this is especially hard, as their lack of expert knowledge prevents elaboration and deliberation, and their outsider-status leads to fewer social connections that can scaffold well-informed self-reflection.

This paper introduces a genre of AI assistants,
Thinking Assistants
, that scaffold productive self-reflection. Thinking Assistants are inspired by two key observations: first,
in our everyday life, when we need help with complex cognition and manipulation tasks, we often turn to other people. We ask experts or colleagues (i.e. experts who know well about us) to think through together that we cannot answer ourselves – whether we hope to navigate careers or buy a car
(
Schotter 2003
)
, or improve our mental health
(
Davidson 2005
)
, we rely on others, not to make decisions for us but how to explore ourselves to reach the best decisions. By talking to individuals who are knowledgeable about the subject of concern, one can explore different perspectives and restructure their thoughts through articulation
(
Aakhus 2007
;
Schon 1984
)
. A second unfortunate observation is that engaging in a thorough discussion about one’s personal decision is often costly or inaccessible, accessible only to those with a robust network, not to those in a peripheral position (i.e., newcomers or emerging professionals)
(
Tamplin 2023
;
Rothschild et al. 2022
;
Aakhus 2007
)
.

Thinking Assistants build on both these observations. The key insight they leverage is that recent large-language models can engage in dialogue that encourages reflection, and also encode nuanced information about the world (so-called “world-knowledge”), but can be augmented with specific expertise. Specifically, we hypothesize that Large Language Model (LLM) agents with a basic knowledge of a domain, augmented by expert human knowledge, can support users’ reflective thinking through reflective inquiry. Through interactive discussions, these assistants engage users in exploring and deepening their understanding of various domains, facilitating a comprehensive exploration of the subject matter at hand.

Thinking Assistant is comprised of two main components:

(1)

Reflective inquiry: The primary objective is to probe users’ interests, emphasizing active engagement over passive reception of information; to promote reflective thinking through inquiry.

(2)

Expert knowledge: Inquiry alone is insufficient if it lacks grounding; effective inquiry requires more than merely posing questions for articulation
(
Aakhus 2007
)
. Thus, Thinking Assistant employs a framework where inquiry is constructed and guided by domain-specific expertise.

This paper makes two contributions: first, it demonstrates that for scaffolding productive self-reflection, Thinking Assistants must be designed with an
emphasis on inquiry
. This is in contrast to the traditional emphasis in the design of such agents; one of furnishing answers, either through their encoded knowledge, or through retrieving information from knowledge bases. A Thinking Assistant
poses thought-provoking questions and cultivates a dynamic exchange that stimulates user interest and encourages active participation
. Asking such questions requires domain knowledge, but their goal is to help users understand, refine, and develop their own interests, rather than to “transfer knowledge” (in this sense, Thinking Assistantsare constructivist learning aids
(
Cowan et al. 1969
)
.) Second, we demonstrate the effectiveness of Thinking Assistants. Through a controlled experiment (N=80) to show that participants engage in reflective thinking more frequently while using a Thinking Assistant than using assistants that either only provide expert advice or ask reflective questions not informed by domain expertise. Further, we evaluate a particular Thinking Assistant in a longitudinal deployment. This assistant,
Gradschool.chat
is designed to support students and junior researchers in choosing research directions. In this longitudinal study, users had 223 anonymous conversations about their graduate school choices. Most users naturally end up resorting to traditional conversational interactions with agents, expecting direct answers due to the prevalence of this interaction style in most existing agents. This expectation is shaped by the dominant conversation paradigm. However, users who remain aligned with this conventional approach, rather than embracing informed inquiry, often find the interaction less helpful. They tend to exhibit significantly lower levels of engagement – approximately half as much compared to those who adapt to or explore a more inquiry-driven conversational flow, which encourages deeper understanding and active participation.

2.
Background & Related Work

The scope of our work touches upon several aspects of learning at scale and conversational assistants that have been explored by prior researchers in three main areas. We first describe the previous approach to self-discovery and guidance on reflective thinking. We then review the methods of ensuring the safety of conversational agents, which are our means of scaling the thinking process especially relevant as we are focusing on reflective environments (e.g., graduate-application process). Lastly, we focus on previous work relevant to
Gradschool.chat
on scaling mentorship and guidance to students, especially in the case of lacking teachers and mentors.

2.1.
Systems for Self-Discovery

In open-ended domains where definitive answers are elusive, decisions are often guided by self-reflection and self-discovery (
Who am I? What do I like?
) by affirming their values and choices
(
Sherman and Cohen 2006
)
. Self-discovery is developing “a self-system that explains ourselves to ourselves, a system that persistently explains our behavior, and the world at large, so as to sustain a phenomenal experience of the self as adaptively and morally adequate”
(
Steele 1988
)
. These processes empower individuals to navigate ambiguity and chart their own course based on personal values, aspirations, and insights
(
Waterman 1984
;
Hinkle and Drew 2020
)
.

Self-reflection poses a significant challenge as it requires a deep analysis and examination of oneself
(
Berzonsky 2011
)
. Often, our own perspective may not fully illuminate aspects of ourselves that are apparent to others or essential for personal growth
(
Park and Kulkarni 2023
)
. This discrepancy can hinder our ability to gain a comprehensive understanding of our strengths, weaknesses, and areas for improvement
(
Robb 1967
)
.

Instead of tackling self-reflection in isolation, individuals frequently seek assistance from others to identify their strengths and weaknesses. Collaborative reflection allows individuals to gain valuable external viewpoints, which can shed light on aspects of themselves that they may overlook or undervalue
(
Aakhus 2007
)
. They often rely on colleagues for this purpose, drawing on their insights and perspectives
(
Park et al. 2024b
;
Evans and Chi 2008
)
. Alternatively, individuals may reach out to professionals, such as life coaches, who specialize in guiding individuals through the process of self-discovery and personal growth
(
Shaw and Glowacki-Dudka 2018
)
. They help individuals identify their core values, passions, and aspirations. They facilitate deep introspection to clarify what truly matters to the individual and what they want to achieve in various aspects of their life.

HCI communities have investigated various systems to support self-reflection through different interventions. These approaches include provoking thoughts about oneself by introducing different personas and having conversations with persona-driven chatbots
(
Lee et al. 2020
)
, encouraging users to think from different perspectives
(
Yeo et al. 2024
)
, collecting personal information to gain insights
(
Xu et al. 2024
)
, and sharing this information with others
(
Wang et al. 2022
)
. LLM-powered chatbots have emerged as a promising avenue for self-discovery, equipped with capabilities to provide interactive support, such as talking human-like (sharing experience or opinion on topics) and remembering essential user information
(
Lee et al. 2020
;
Jo et al. 2024
)
. While authentic interactions with chatbots allow users to feel more connected, they also increase the risk of over-reliance and frustration due to the inherent gap between human communication and chatbot capabilities
(
Ma et al. 2023
;
Jo et al. 2024
)
, as chatbots can not fully grasp the nuances of human interactions
(
Ma et al. 2024
)
. Instead of focusing on imitating the tone of experts, our approach leverages the power of LLMs to perform reflective inquiry, aiding in self-discovery and promoting reflective thinking through questions that experts might ask.

2.1.1.
Asking rather than Answering

Asking questions has long been a popular and effective method in educational settings, facilitating active learning and helping students elaborate and clarify their knowledge
(
Weinstein et al. 2018
;
Higgins and Rholes 1978
;
Dewey 2022
)
. By responding to questions posed by teachers, students engage more deeply with the material and develop a better understanding. Recent research indicates that answering questions generated by not just teacher, but LLMs can also foster robust logical thinking
(
Danry et al. 2023
)
. The effect of questions extends to self-discovery and personal growth as well. Engaging in reflective inquiry allows individuals to “conduct a crucial experiment in the form of a question”
(
Aakhus 2007
)
. Since life decisions are often open-ended and filled with tacit information, there are no definitive right or wrong answers. Instead, the process involves considering multiple questions and perspectives
(
Schon 1984
)
.

Asking contextual questions and personalizing advice is inherently resource-intensive because it requires a deep understanding of individual circumstances, preferences, and goals. This process often demands significant time, expertise, and computational power, especially when scaled across large populations
(
Bhatt et al. 2024
)
. Existing AI-assisted decision-making tools primarily focus on providing information, rather than fostering deeper thinking and reflection
(
Lai et al. 2021
)
. While these systems can efficiently deliver generic recommendations, understanding users’ intents and adapting advice accordingly demands significantly more resources and effort
(
Andukuri et al. 2024
)
. This challenge shapes the mental models of users, who may begin to view AI as primarily transactional and informational, rather than as a tool for deeper cognitive engagement
(
Luger and Sellen 2016
;
Zaib et al. 2022
)
.

Recent advancements in technology, such as LLMs, have demonstrated a promising ability to ask meaningful questions and gain a deep understanding of users. Leveraging these technologies, survey systems can interactively engage with users to elicit more thoughtful and accurate responses
(
Xiao et al. 2020
)
, while also capturing implicit signals such as users’ preferences
(
Andukuri et al. 2024
)
. Beyond merely gathering information, this process has been shown to benefit users by promoting logical thinking
(
Danry et al. 2023
)
. However, there remains limited research on how question-asking systems designed for open-ended domains can support self-discovery and reflection encouraging deeper engagement and fostering self-awareness.

Now there is an opportunity to enable scalable interactions that combine the strengths of informed inquiry with domain expertise. We propose leveraging a primary LLM conversation model focused on asking questions rather than solely providing answers. Our hypothesis is that LLM agents engaging in interaction models designed to ask expert-level questions can stimulate users’ deeper reflective thinking, fostering a more meaningful and thoughtful engagement process.

2.2.
Using Agents to Personalize Guidance

Prior work has leveraged machine-learning-enabled chatbots to create personalized interactions at scale, especially in situations where there is limited availability of experts. For instance, in healthcare, to mitigate the limited availability of medical professionals, researchers have developed chatbots capable of handling basic health inquiries
(
Bickmore et al. 2018
;
Xiao et al. 2023
)
. Often, these systems help triage questions, allowing experts to focus on the most challenging issues.
At the same time, such dialogue agents can also help in ways that humans cannot. For instance, in providing mental health support, such agents allow some participants to be more disinhibited in their conversation
(
Ma et al. 2023
)
. Thus, in addition to interpreting natural language queries and accessing knowledge bases, the non-human nature of these agents may be helpful in applications where people may be hesitant in asking others for help
(
Ma et al. 2023
;
Sharma et al. 2023
)
.

In educational settings, most chatbots are designed to teach new skills, enhance learning efficiency, motivate learners, and scale educational offerings
(
Wollny et al. 2021
)
. However, the support provided by these chatbots has primarily focused on delivering information rather than facilitating students’ reflective thinking or helping them find the right fit for their educational needs
(
Bhattacharjee et al. 2024
;
Kasneci et al. 2023
)
. For instance, while intelligent agents are adept at helping students navigate complex administrative logistics and academic requirements, they primarily serve as a substitute for limited administrative staff, focusing on information provision rather than advancing students’ academic exploration
(
Mitra et al. 2023
;
Lefdahl-Davis et al. 2018
)
.
Thinking Assistants share similarities with these existing educational systems in offering personalized guidance but distinguish themselves by focusing on reflective thinking through thought-provoking questions.

Our work is also motivated by prior work that finds that outsiders, such as junior researchers often find it stressful to interact with more knowledgeable community members, such as senior academics, leading to over-preparation for meetings
(
Park et al. 2024a
)
. By contrast, Thinking Assistants offer a more relaxed setting where users can be less inhibitied, and freely explore and ask questions without fear of embarrassment.

2.3.
Conversational Agent Safety

Ensuring the safety of conversational assistants, particularly those not designed for information seeking, remains a complex challenge. This is partly because the required safety measures vary depending on the assistant’s purpose
(
Kempt et al. 2023
)
. Most current measures focus on the accuracy of information provided by conversational assistants, which may not be entirely suitable for free-flowing conversations
(
Zubatiy et al. 2023
)
.
In contrast, our focus is on asking well-informed thoughtful questions. Even so, it is often necessary to provide some reflective and frequently asked information for users. This is challenging because of the limitations of LLMs, specifically the potential for hallucination
(
Dziri et al. 2022
;
Ji et al. 2023
)
).

Prior work has extensively explored various methods to improve factuality. One effective strategy involves structuring the conversation around expected domain-specific topics, and steering conversations away from other topics
(
Kempt et al. 2023
)
. For instance, an agent designed for booking flight tickets might predominantly navigate branches related to gathering travel itinerary details, selecting tickets, and processing payments. This allows for more precise shaping and testing of the conversation flow for chatbot developers
(
Klemmer et al. 2000
;
Choi et al. 2021
)
. Additionally, the incorporation of experts and inclusive datasets in training further bolsters the safety of conversational assistants
(
Aroyo et al. 2023
;
Xiao et al. 2023
)
.

While this prior work narrows the domains that the assistant deals with, we found that it is sometimes helpful to allow a Thinking Assistant to ask questions on broad areas such as the users’ interest. Instead of narrowing the topics of conversation, we introduce a ‘safety bot’ whose sole function is to verify the accuracy of the information provided by our virtual assistants in the narrow, targeted field of expertise. As such, our method allows the assistant to converse on a broad variety of topics, but only improves factuality in a narrow field.

3.
What kinds of assistant interactions lead to self-reflection?

Informed by prior work, we hypothesize that users engage in reflective thinking most effectively when they interact with assistants that ask thought-provoking questions supplemented with domain experts’ knowledge, in contrast to those that ask expertise-uninformed questions, or only offer expert advice. In our first lab study, we compared participants’ depth of reflective thinking in the presence of informed inquiry and/or advice. To allow quantitative comparison of the results of informed inquiry with or without expert guidance, we conducted subsequent dialog analysis with condition-blind crowd raters.

3.1.
Experiment on different interaction modes

3.1.1.
Participants

We posted this task on Prolific (https://www.prolific.com/). We restricted our recruitment to adults who are fluent in English. They were paid $5 per 20 minutes for their time and effort. We conducted a power analysis for a four-group ANCOVA study seeking a high power (0.8) with an alpha of 0.05 according to Cohen’s conventions, at medium to high effect size (0.38), yielding 20 participants per condition.

3.1.2.
Procedure

To explore different conversation modes of LLM agents in reflective thinking, we conducted a between-subject study with four conditions across two axes: whether participants are provided with question-asking and whether they are provided with information. The control condition (“diary” mode) lacked both question-asking and information, instead, users were given a blank text box to document their thinking process upon seeing the topic prompt (Fig.
1
Left). In the “ask” mode, conversation agents asked users clarifying questions to probe their characteristics. The “advice” condition provided information and suggestions to guide users’ thinking. Lastly, in the “informed inquiry” condition, agents asked questions and provided information, combining reflective inquiry and guidance. The example responses of the chatbot version is in Table
1
. For all four conditions, we used the same aesthetic style of interfaces to minimize response bias (Fig
1
). We used
gpt-o1-mini
of OpenAI for the chatbot condition. The specific system prompts for each condition are included in the Appendix.

Participants were asked to engage in a conversation about their decision regarding prioritizing work versus work-life balance. The procedures were as follows:

3.1.3.
Measure

We employed several measures to test the effect of different conditions. Mainly, we look into reflective-thinking effectiveness evaluated by external raters and the participants themselves.

External ratings of reflective-thinking effectiveness

To evaluate how different interventions effectively promoted reflective thinking, we rated their thinking process. Specifically, we measure employing two criteria: (1) how strong reasonings they have for their decisions and (2) how much users describe and analyze their experience
(
Schon 1984
;
Kolb 2014
;
Li et al. 2024
)
.

Through multiple iterations of discussion and review of the conversation logs in the research team, we consolidated coding guidelines to measure criteria. Two raters independently rated the experiment data, blind to condition. Raters were asked to rate diaries or conversations based on the following guidelines:

•

Reasoning: Is their decision profound and based on good reasoning? Count the number of reasoning statements or strategies to make decisions they discussed in the conversation.

Each strategy represents a separate actionable step that enhances the implementation of the discussed activity. The key is to identify and count these distinct strategies, even if they are related to the same overall activity or reasoning, based on their meaningful contribution to improving or facilitating that activity (not just repeating or paraphrasing).

•

Sharing about themselves: Are you able to capture the user’s experience, value, personality, or information (e.g., their job)? Count the number of traits that they mentioned in the conversation.

The independent ratings had an inter-rater reliability (IRR) of 71% using Cohen’s
\kappa
. We averaged the rating of the two raters to determine the final score.

Self-ratings of reflective-thinking effectiveness

We also asked participants to self-reported effectiveness in their reflective thinking through the following Likert-scale questions at the exit survey:

•

How much did you think deeply about your choices?

•

To what extent did the task help you generate new insights about your decision?

•

How effective was the task that made you reflect on different aspects of your decision?

•

Did the task help you become more aware of your own preferences, values, and priorities?

•

What aspects did you find difficult or easy? Please share any other thoughts or feelings you had while completing the task.

Conversation logs

To compare how they engage differently for different conditions, the users’ diaries and conversation logs during the tasks were coded and analyzed using the linguistic dictionary Linguistic Inquiry and Word Count (LIWC)
(
Pennebaker et al. 2001
)
.

We found significant differences in the reasoning between conditions (F(3, 72) = 13.25,

This discrepancy between observed reasoning differences and self-reported ratings aligns with previous research on reflection (e.g.
(
Park and Kulkarni 2023
)
). Studies have consistently shown weak to moderate correlations between self-assessments and external performance measures across various domains. For instance, previous work found an average correlation of only 0.29 between self-assessments and external standards
(
Karpen 2018
)
. The lack of significant differences in self-reported ratings, despite clear differences in observed reasoning, may be attributed to cognitive biases that affect self-perception. Individuals often struggle to accurately assess their own abilities, with a tendency towards overestimation. This phenomenon, known as the Dunning-Kruger effect, can lead to inflated self-ratings, particularly among less skilled individuals.

According to the LIWC analysis, participants using “informed inquiry” frequently discussed key topics related to career success and work-life balance. They significantly mentioned
Home
words—associated with staying home with family and working from home—more often than participants in the advice group (
p
=.01). Furthermore,
Leisure
words were more commonly used by “informed inquiry” participants compared to other groups: the advice group (
p
=.002), as well as the ask and diary groups (
p
=.05). Additionally, the term
Ingest

p

Several users in the “advice” group (8 out of 20) noted that their chatbot’s messages were excessively lengthy and overloaded with information, lacking guidance on reflective thinking. One user remarked, “It would be great if the chatbot challenged my thinking and encouraged me to consider various perspectives.” In contrast, fewer users in the “informed inquiry” group (3 out of 19) expressed concerns about the conversation length.

Participants in the “informed inquiry” group noted that the information provided by the chatbots was not only relevant and helpful, but to the extent “unusual for chatbots”. Six out of nineteen participants specifically mentioned that the chatbots’ messages were informative and offered valuable suggestions. One participant expressed, “I actually feel the chatbot gave me good advice that I can implement as I try to focus on balancing my work and life, while still working towards professional goals.” In contrast, only three participants from the “advice” group explicitly acknowledged the helpfulness of the information provided by the chatbots.

Users take considerations of more perspectives of their personal traits when they get reflective inquiry in addition to advice

There were significant differences in the amount of information shared by users among groups (F(3, 72) = 3.00,
p
=.04). The “informed inquiry” users share significantly more about themselves than the “advice” group (
p
=.02). There was no significant difference in self-rating of self-information disclosure.

Participants in the “ask” group noted that the questions posed by their chatbots were often difficult to answer and “weren’t really going anywhere or were heading away from what was helpful”. One user remarked, “The bot’s questions at times were difficult to interpret and I wasn’t sure how to give an answer that would help in the decision making process.” This feedback suggests that while the questions may prompt users to reflect and share more about themselves, they may not always steer the conversation in a relevant direction. To further investigate this, we decided to conduct a comparative study of the questions posed by the “ask” group and the “informed inquiry” group. Details of this follow-up study are outlined in the section below.

3.2.
Followup analysis on Reflective Inquiry

To further investigate and compare reflective inquiry between “ask” and “informed inquiry” groups, we recruited additional raters from the same crowd platform.

3.2.1.
Participants

We recruited participants in the same manner as the previous study. However, the participants of the previous study were excluded from the recruitment. New participants were paid $10 per 40 minutes for their time and effort. We recruited 12 raters to ensure that there is a chaining overlap between raters so we can compute IRR.

3.2.2.
Procedure

Each participant rated four conversations from the previous study – two of “ask” and the other two of “informed inquiry”. Raters were not informed about the presence of the different conditions and were blind to the condition. We also shuffled the order of conversation so that they were exposed to “ask” or “informed inquiry” in a randomized chance:

(1)

Training task : Participants are required to correctly rate two messages before they proceed to rate actual conversations.

(2)

Conversation analysis (x4): The rating interface presents a pair of the chatbot’s questions and the user’s response at a time.

(3)

Exit survey: Participants filled out a demographic survey asking about their gender and age.

3.2.3.
Measure

Raters analyzed the critical inquiry in different manners, such as categorizing the question and the users’ responses and their reaction and effort level.

Categories of critical inquiry and response

We categorized messages from our chatbot and users using Interaction Process Analysis (IPA)
(
Bales 1950
)
. Raters were asked to pick the most fitting code for each message. During the piloting of the original coding, due to the topic (i.e. life decision) of the conversation, raters frequently disagreed coding of “Asks for Information” and “Asks for Opinion”. After multiple iterations of improving our coding book, we finalized the coding book as described in Table
3
.

In the remainder of the paper, we present a case study to evaluate the utility of Thinking Assistant in real-world scenarios. Our lab study revealed that LLM agents are more effective at encouraging users to engage in critical thinking when they pose thought-provoking questions integrated with guidance, rather than focusing solely on questioning or providing guidance. These findings informed the design of an effective thinking-focused LLM companion, emphasizing the importance of striking a balance between questioning and guiding. The subsequent case study examines the exploration of research areas, a particularly relevant topic as it involves complex self-reflection and the application of tailored guidance to assess fit and direction.

4.
How do users interact with assistants to facilitate reflective inquiry?

We conducted an in-the-wild user study that consisted
of intervention deployment and follow-up interviews. Our main goal in this study was to investigate how Thinking Assistants are used in the wild. For our investigation, we implemented a new GPT-based assistant, which we call
Gradschool.chat
(See Fig.
3
), supporting junior researchers (e.g., prospective or graduate students) with reflective inquiry. The system focuses on fostering self-reflection and deeper intellectual engagement by posing targeted, thought-provoking questions that encourage users to critically evaluate their research concepts and aspirations. Each assistant within
Gradschool.chat
is built and trained based on a specific professor’s expertise and guidance, enabling the system to simulate tailored mentorship. While the system incorporates professor-related inquiries for better control over conversation flow and testing efficacy, its primary emphasis remains on guiding users through meaningful self-exploration of their research goals and academic fit, enriched by the perspective of domain-specific knowledge.

Figure 2.
Gradschool.chat
is a Thinking Assistant that helps junior researchers reflect on research interests. Based on the user’s question, the assistant chooses between a ‘probing’ mode, understanding user interests and prompting reflection, and an ‘answering’ mode that offers relevant information. Most conversational turns end with an encouraging follow-up question.

4.1.1.
Reflective Inquiry

Our primary goal is to engage users in exploring their research interests through probing. Adopting the ‘saying-is-believing’ approach
(
Aronson 1999
)
, our chatbot poses targeted questions that deepen user engagement with their research topics. This method actively involves individuals in reflecting on an idea rather than passively receiving information.
This strategy also sidesteps the stigma associated with passive receipt. Encouraging individuals to champion an idea increases its persuasive power, and by relating the idea to their personal experiences, users can personalize and internalize the concept, thereby making standardized materials resonate with a diverse audience
(
Aronson 1999
)
.
Gradschool.chat
engage in reflective inquiries to encourage users to thoroughly analyze their work, leading to tailored responses and academic recommendations.

Once the system has an initial understanding of the users’ research goals, it offers constructive feedback based on the Wise Feedback framework
(
Cohen et al. 1999
;
Yeager et al. 2014
)
. This feedback is twofold: firstly, it establishes the high standards used to evaluate the research and offers relevant advice; secondly, it affirms the users’ ability to meet these standards, buffering against losing confidence in their research endeavors due to critical feedback.

4.1.2.
Expert Knowledge

To make informed inquiry, we include each professor’s domain knowledge in the prompt. Capturing an expert’s knowledge comprehensively in a chatbot development is challenging
(
Xiao et al. 2023
)
. To ensure each professor is accurately represented, we’ve developed a method that allows professors to easily evaluate and verify the representation of their expertise in our system. Our strategy employs structured responses, which provide a clear, organized framework that parallels the expert’s knowledge. This approach simplifies the process for professors to assess and confirm the authenticity and accuracy of the information in the dataset.

We compile training data by sourcing information from their academic web pages and requesting they contribute additional materials. Professors have provided items such as recent talk summaries, lab manuals, and grant proposals.

We consolidated a questionnaire of six questions to capture each participating professor’s guidance based on literature in mentoring in high education
(
Nuis et al. 2024
;
of Graduate Studies 2015
)
.
Leveraging GPT-4, we draft responses to the following six questions, which act as “teaching data” for our assistants:

(1)

Research Evolution & Direction: How have your research areas evolved over the past few years, and where do you see them going in the near future?

(2)

Mentoring & Guidance: How do you structure your interactions with PhD students to ensure they receive adequate guidance and feedback?

(3)

Group Dynamics & Collaborations: Can you describe the nature of collaborations within your group and with external teams or departments?

(4)

Post-PhD Prospects: How has the research experience in your group equipped students for their post-PhD careers?

(5)

Supporting Students: How do you handle situations when a student is stuck or facing challenges in their research?

(6)

Key Qualities: What are the key qualities or attributes that have stood out in the most successful PhD students you’ve mentored?

After drafting answers to these questions, we shared the document with the professors for their review and endorsement. Once approved, we integrated the responses into a chatbot, which was then presented to the professors for a final evaluation. The professors interacted with and provided feedback on the virtual assistant prior to its official launch, with some inviting their students to participate in testing as well.

Participating professors received a general summary of interactions with their designated chatbots, such as common question themes by users, and answers provided. They retained the authority to discontinue our hosting of assistants related to their work at any time, at their discretion.

This domain knowledge is used to craft informed inquiry or provide answers to users when they ask.
For queries outside these areas, particularly regarding professors’ personal opinions, the chatbot clarifies its limitations. In situations where user questions diverge broadly from research-related topics, the chatbot redirects the conversation back to research-focused discussions, ensuring that the dialogue remains relevant and productive.

Where appropriate, it also attempts to retrieve relevant information based on model-pretraining data. For example, the chatbot assists users by explaining research terminology and addressing any follow-up questions related to their queries. This proactiveness during information-seeking also possibly improves users’ trust in our chatbot
(
Xiao et al. 2023
)
which could lead users to share more about their research interests with our chatbot.

4.1.3.
Safety through a Secondary Agent

Concerning sensitive information like professors’ recruitment and contact policies,
Gradschool.chat
is instructed to provide only verified facts. Considering the challenges of filtering real-time streaming messages from an LLM, we introduced a secondary virtual assistant. This assistant’s primary role is to review and correct any discrepancies in the chatbot’s messages, ensuring they align with the information provided by the professors, as depicted in Fig.
4
.

Our chatbot is instructed to refrain from making definitive statements about professors’ opinions or personal information unless such information is verifiably sourced. It is designed to acknowledge its lack of knowledge rather than provide uncertain or speculative information. To prevent potential misrepresentation concerns, we communicate to users that the chatbot is a virtual assistant with knowledge about the professors, not an entity impersonating them in multiple ways, including static descriptions on the webpage, and in conversational responses.

4.1.4.
Implementation

Gradschool.chat
is built using the Next.js framework. For our backend, we utilize OpenAI’s Streaming API with the
gpt-4
model for most functionalities. However, when users ask a question regarding the professor which requires higher token usage due to including information about professors’ publications — we employ the
gpt-3.5-turbo-16k
model. Please refer to Appendices for the actual prompts used in our system.

We store consenting users’ conversations occurring on our interface using Firebase, and track anonymous usage patterns using Google Analytics.

4.2.
Evaluation

We deployed
Gradschool.chat
and observed the types of conversations people had with our chatbot in a field deployment in November 2023.

4.2.1.
Procedure

Upon selecting a professor to initiate conversations in the interface, users were presented with a consent form and could only start conversations upon consenting.

Gradschool.chat
was deployed on November 3rd, 2023. Initially, there were chatbots for eight professors, but one was removed at the request of the respective professor. (The professor was concerned that students might receive erroneous information, when the chatbot incorrectly generated the professor’s email and lab name acronym, lacked information about their students, and provided incorrect information that the admission committee is in the university, but actually is in their department) The platform was advertised through social media post and word-of-mouth. A total of 173 users initiated conversations with at least one of our chatbots, resulting in 223 conversations between November 3rd and 10th, 2023. According to Google Analytics, 69% of them used our chatbot from the USA, 6% from India, 5% from Kenya, 4% from Canada, and 4% from Pakistan.

We prompted users to ask if they would like to participate in an exit interview when the user sent 8th message to one of our chatbots. Each interview is roughly 30 minutes guided by (but not limited to) the questionnaire in the Appendix. Interviewees were compensated with $10 Amazon gift cards for their time and effort.

We conducted an open-coding protocol to identify concepts and categories from our interview transcripts. Through multiple iterations along with periodic discussions with the rest of the research team, the coding led to six categories and 11 concepts, from which the major themes were selected. To yield concepts and themes, the authors discussed the codes through multiple iterations
(
McDonald et al. 2019
)
.

This deployment study is under the same IRB as our previous lab study.

4.2.2.
Measures

To understand the nature of student interactions with our chatbot and assess their satisfaction, we collected data on the following metrics:

•

Types of Questions: We analyzed the variety of questions posed by users to our chatbot to understand their primary concerns and interests.

•

Utterance Analysis: We evaluated the language patterns in conversations using the Linguistic Inquiry and Word Count (LIWC) tool
(
Pennebaker et al. 2001
)
, which provided insights into the linguistic characteristics of the dialogues.

•

User Rating: To obtain immediate user feedback, we asked users to rate their conversation after every three messages, using a thumbs-up or thumbs-down response system.

Experience difference in informed inquiry vs. answer seeking

Despite the instructions (e.g. Let’s get started! Tell us a little bit about your research interests, Discuss your research interest to discover alignment with the professor) on our interface, many users seek to find direct answers by asking questions about the professors, shaped by their past experience with conversational agents
(
Luger and Sellen 2016
;
Zaib et al. 2022
)
. Out of 223 conversations, 150 did not include personal information from the users, such as research interests or experiences. Conversely, 73 conversations involved users sharing such details.

This different conversational mode affects users’ engagement level. We observed differences in engagement levels based on whether students discussed their own work. This variance in self-disclosure is associated with a significant difference in engagement levels (t = -4.46,
p
< .001). When users did not share personal information, they typically asked a few questions about the professor and ended the conversation after an average of three queries. On the other hand, users who discussed their own work engaged more actively, with an average of six messages per conversation, which indicates a doubling in interaction levels.

Our analysis indicates that users are also more satisfied when the conversation centers on themselves rather than the professor. There is a significant difference in the messaging patterns of users and chatbots between conversations with positive and negative ratings. Users tend to rate conversations positively when they talk about themselves, as indicated by the use of
I
(
p
= .01). Notably, there is more than twice the usage of
Achieve
in negatively rated conversations (
p
= .02). Among these negative
Achieve
messages, most (78%) inquire about the professor’s achievements (e.g.,
“What is his latest work?”
). In contrast, in positively rated messages, 83% of
Achieve
references are about the users’ own achievements (e.g.,
“I am interested in working on [a research topic]”
). Similarly, when examining the chatbot’s responses, users tend to be more satisfied when the conversation evolves around the user’s work (
You
,
p
< .001) rather than the professor’s (
SheHe
,
p
= .06). An interviewee explained that sharing about their own work helps them understand the professor’s work better by relating to their own work:

“The chatbot at some point asked me [to] provide more details about my work. So I did. And then at some point, it actually gave me really interesting questions that I can start the conversation with or discuss. And I loved it. I actually took screenshots of this to reflect on it later. But yeah, I found the questions really interesting, like the kinds of connections that it makes between my work, the professor’s work, and the way it phrases the question.” [

Frustration with ambiguity

We observed user frustration when the chatbots did not provide definitive answers to their questions. Users often attempted to elicit ’yes or no’ responses by reformulating their queries, seeking to limit the chatbot’s replies (e.g.,
“Could you discuss the likelihood that [the professor] would be interested in working with me, ranging from very unlikely to very likely, based on his publications, research interests, and current student composition?”
). However, adhering to our guidelines, the chatbots did not provide the straightforward answers users sought.

The primary function of our system is to engage users through reflective/inquisitive dialogue, and users seem to generally enjoy learning by responding to it. Interviewees said: “[The chatbot] keeps asking me questions [..] that conversation is pretty fruitful to self-reflect” [
Researcher1
] and “getting the point [..] while answering these discussions” [
Researcher2
]. However, this approach sometimes led to dissatisfaction. For example, a user expressed exasperation in response to the chatbot’s series of questions:
“Why do you keep asking me questions? I want to know if [the professor’s] research interests are a good fit for me.”
This situation highlights the challenge of balancing the chatbot’s questioning strategy with users’ expectations for direct answers.

User frustration also arose regarding inaccurate paper recommendations made by the chatbot. Of the 146 paper recommendations, four in one conversation were either non-existent or incorrectly attributed to different authors. Analysis of these interactions revealed that a user requested papers not authored by the professors, a scenario our system was not designed to handle. During one conversation, after the chatbot had asked several probing questions, it began recommending papers by the professor. When the user requested additional papers from other researchers, they later discovered inaccuracies in the authors’ names of previously recommended papers. This incident underscored the need for more accurate tracking and presentation of publication data in our chatbot’s recommendation system. Although our chatbot was instructed to recommend only the professors’ recent publications, as detailed in the System prompt in the Appendix, it deviated from this instruction, reflecting known challenges in LLMs regarding the creation of fictitious paper titles and authors
(
Dziri et al. 2022
;
Ji et al. 2023
)
. Going forward, we plan to alert users about the potential inaccuracies of recommendations outside the professors’ publications and to enhance our safety bot’s verification of suggested papers.

Similarly, while not common, users occasionally inquired about other graduate students who worked with the professor. In one instance, a user commented through our feedback interface:
“It’s hallucinating about some of the PhD alumni of a faculty member.”
When asked about a professor’s students, our chatbot, which sources information from GPT, mistakenly provided names of collaborators, such as postdocs.
Despite the infrequency and uncertain relevance of such queries to graduate applications, we recognize the importance of further investigation.
Future work could integrate this information into the chatbot’s system prompt to ensure more accurate responses, especially if such information helps users’ self-reflection.

Conversely, the issue of providing incorrect professor email addresses did not occur during our deployment. This was preemptively addressed after a professor raised concerns about the dissemination of incorrect email addresses. We refined our prompts to include only the email addresses of professors who consented to be contacted, ensuring the accuracy of all email addresses mentioned by our chatbots since this modification.

An alternative but not a replacement to conversations with professors

Interviewees recognized that while our chatbot doesn’t replace real professors, it offers an alternative communication channel. Engaging directly with professors, when feasible, is unquestionably valuable for students. An interviewee said: “That really helps. Sort of like, give me confidence that what I’m working on is good, that people will appreciate the global south context, and that it’s actually fine [because] people [care]” [
Researcher1
].

Interviewees expressed their challenges and anxiety when it came to engaging with real professors, acknowledging the busy schedules and heavy workloads that professors often handle. One interviewee noted the infrequency of opportunities for such interactions, saying, “Definitely like the frequency of conversation, will be another [..] important issue, [..] I cannot meet with my graduate mentor [..] anytime, because [..] they’re pretty busy” [Undergrad2]. Another added, “We have to fight for his (advisor’s) time, because there are so many other students” [Grad1]. This awareness leads to hesitation in reaching out and setting high personal standards for preparedness, often resulting in increased anxiety. “I had a chance to talk to him last CSCW. But I [..] didn’t actively pursue it because [..] I was [..] avoiding him because of [..] anxiety. Because I know [..] he’s such a successful, important person” [Grad2].

In contrast, interacting with our chatbot offered other advantages. Its anytime availability is particularly appreciated, as one interviewee shared, “My whole family.. I’m the first person to go [to] a university. I’m the first one that everything happens to them. So this is so helpful for me. It’s [..] accessible. It’s like, right there” [Undergrad1].

The chatbot also provided a risk-free platform for students to navigate academic uncertainty, allowing them to ask questions freely without fear of judgment. “I can ask my questions in a stupid way, without properly phrasing them. Whatever I think I would just, you know, spit it out I mean, in conversations with professors usually want to have your questions polished” [Grad2]. Some interviewees also mentioned the ease of asking questions they would feel embarrassed to pose to a real professor. “I could not ask the question [..] to a real prof” [Researcher3]. The non-human aspect of the chatbot made it easier for students to share their concerns without guilt. “It is not a person, so [..] it doesn’t [..] show any emotion, so I can just like tell it my story. Sometimes when I tell people my story, I feel a little bit of guilt, cause I’m giving them [..] emotional stress [when] they’re not ready” [Undergrad1].

Moreover, conversations with the chatbot helped prepare students for future interactions with professors. “Getting out of this is a very polished version of what I wanna say and use that. So it kinda helped me. So like when I did this, my anxiety talking to [the professor] [..] actually decreased like, ’Okay, now, I wanna actually go and talk to this person”’ [Grad2]. Graduate students mentioned using the chatbot to prepare for meetings with advisors, aiding in brainstorming and refining ideas for more advanced discussions.

The chatbot was seen as a source of encouragement, particularly for undergraduates, boosting confidence in their research choices. “They kind of like recognize that both [of my projects] are important. And then that just makes me feel comfortable about my choices” [Undergrad2]. This indicates the chatbot’s significant role in positively influencing students’ academic journeys.

5.
Discussion

Our studies shed light on the new applications of LLM agents and the limitations of current LLM agents, particularly arising when discussing open-ended domains. Based on our observations, we outline design implications for enhancing the interaction design of LLM agents and safety and discuss future research directions and limitations of our studies.

5.1.
Implications

5.1.1.
New modality of AI agent interaction

We introduce Thinking Assistants, a new approach in which LLM agents are designed to focus on asking thought-provoking questions informed by domain expertise. In today’s increasingly automated environment, artificial intelligence is often perceived as a tool for delivering direct answers or completing tasks. However, there are many scenarios, such as research and strategic decision-making, where guidance and critical inquiry can be more valuable than straightforward solutions.

Our studies, including both lab studies and real-world deployment, demonstrate that Thinking Assistants effectively foster reflective thinking by prompting users to explore various perspectives and articulate their current thoughts. This method of inquiry not only stimulates deeper engagement but also enhances the overall conversational experience. Users have reported that they find the conversations with Thinking Assistants enjoyable and perceive a sense of enthusiasm from the agents, indicating a desire for continued interaction. This result is particularly intriguing as it mirrors the excitement typically associated with dynamic and engaging dialogues. We believe that this approach represents a significant shift in how AI agents can interact with users, offering a novel modality that opens up new possibilities for user interface design.

The in-the-wild deployment study also highlights a significant gap between Thinking Assistants and users’ mental models of conversational agents. Despite being instructed to share personal reflections through reflective inquiry, most users interacted with the system in the same way they would with traditional information retrieval chatbots. While conversational agents powered by LLMs are capable of supporting users’ cognitive processes on a deeper, more personalized level, users’ mental models still lean towards chatbots with more limited technical capabilities. This observation points to a new research agenda focused on bridging the gap between users’ expectations and the full potential of advanced conversational agents.

Future developers working on integrating LLMs into user interfaces can leverage the ask mode in their agent development. Instead of relying on assumptions or jumping to conclusions about what users might want, developers should consider using LLMs to actively learn about and explore users’ interests and preferences. By employing LLMs to engage in probing and exploratory conversations, developers can gain a deeper understanding of user needs and motivations. According to our results, this approach is likely to extend user engagement and facilitate a clearer expression of user intentions. When interfaces are designed with a focus on understanding and responding to these expressed intentions, they become more adept at aligning with users’ goals and preferences. This deeper alignment provides significant opportunities for customization, enabling the system to cater more effectively to individual user needs. By leveraging LLMs in this way, developers can move beyond generic solutions and create more nuanced and responsive interactions. This method not only enhances user satisfaction but also optimizes the effectiveness of the interface, leading to a more engaging and personalized experience.

Our research implicates important directions for HCI researchers exploring the integration of LLMs into user interfaces. It highlights the need to shift focus from LLMs as mere answer providers to their potential as facilitators of critical thinking and exploration, suggesting that researchers should investigate how different questioning strategies employed by LLMs can impact user engagement, reflection, and problem-solving abilities across various domains and user groups. The reported user enjoyment and perceived enthusiasm from Thinking Assistants agents opens up new research questions about how to design AI systems that can create and sustain engagement.

5.1.2.
Balancing Self-reflection and Information Retrieval in Academic Advising

Our findings suggest that many users had positive experiences interacting with our chatbot, especially when sharing and discussing their research interests, as opposed to merely getting domain knowledge.
Thinking Assistants recognize the importance of brainstorming research topics in aiding students to develop their ideas, especially as they often struggle to formulate research concepts independently
(
Schempp and Johnson 2006
)
.
Gradschool.chat
approach of deeply probing into students’ interests, by asking thought-provoking questions rather than passively delivering information
(
Clough 2007
)
, offers personalized research guidance. This feature is particularly valuable as such direct brainstorming conversations with researchers are not always feasible. Even when students have access to resources, they might feel intimidated about approaching senior researchers or hesitant to discuss their uncertainties about graduate school and research exploration
(
Park et al. 2024a
)
.

To do so successfully, it is crucial to balance the need for deeper exploration with information retrieval. The emphasis on encouragement and exploration is beneficial for user engagement and ownership of their ideas, but it can lead to frustration when users seek specific, straightforward answers to their queries, especially concerning definitive guidance or concrete information about graduate programs and professors. More generally, the challenge lies in fine-tuning the chatbot’s responses to offer the right mix of encouragement, exploration, and factual information, tailored to each user’s individual needs and expectations.
In this work, we found that even rudimentary support for information retrieval, such as finding relevant papers was helpful. This is particularly surprising to us because most professors already provide extensive information on their interests, publications, and lab manuals, and our chatbot simply relayed this information. This could be because users have already shared sufficient information and engaged deeply in the conversation, allowing the agent to gather enough context to provide recommendations that, while rudimentary, are
well situated
(
Andukuri et al. 2024
)
.

Finally, there was a notable preference among users for concrete answers, particularly for questions that related to the uncertainty in the process, such as the likelihood of success in graduate applications. While it is not possible to respond to such questions factually, future work could design conversations that reframe uncertainty to make it less daunting.

5.2.
AI Assistance in High-stakes Decision-making

A significant risk associated with Thinking Assistants is the potential for misleading users about important decisions. For example, the complexity of graduate admissions raises the possibility that the chatbot might inadvertently provide incorrect or oversimplified advice, stemming from limitations in its training data or misinterpretations of user queries. Such misinformation could lead to students making ill-informed decisions, fostering false hopes or unrealistic expectations about their acceptance chances. Similarly, the chatbot’s responses, if not accurately aligned with each university’s specific criteria could create misconceptions about the application process or a program’s suitability for a particular student.

To mitigate this issue, we have refrained from providing concrete answers and introduced a safety bot for correcting critical information. Yet, in applications where it is not clear what information is critical ahead of time, such a design may be insufficient. (For example, academic programs have specific deadlines which are known
a-priori
to be critical information. But for Thinking Assistants helping with healthy eating, for instance, what is critical may be less clearly defined.)

The introduction of AI assistants could also alter social relationships. For example, the existence of a
Gradschool.chat
might inadvertently convey the impression that professors are less accessible or approachable. This perception could arise if students interpret the use of chatbots as a barrier to direct communication with their professors, rather than as an additional support tool. Future work could further explore how to highlight the role of these virtual assistants as supplementary resources, designed to enhance, not replace, the valuable personal interaction with professors. Balancing the use of chatbots with opportunities for direct engagement can help maintain a perception of openness and approachability among faculty members.

5.3.
Limitations and Future Work

While our studies offer valuable insights into the potential utility of Thinking Assistants, several methodological limitations should be considered. First, our assessment methods relied heavily on self-reported data and immediate feedback, such as the binary rating system. This approach may have missed nuanced aspects of thinking and decision making. Further, we relied on linguistic patterns to assess reasoning and not extensively consider the quality or long-term impact of this reasoning on users’ decision-making processes. The study’s design, particularly the lab component comparing different interaction modes, may have introduced demand characteristics, potentially influencing participants’ responses and engagement levels. Additionally, the focus of the deployment study on specific academic fields, particularly computer science, may limit the applicability of our findings to other disciplines or decision-making contexts.

Finally, our approach may have overlooked specific nuances of self-reflection and critical thinking that are culturally specific (our participant pool was primarily North American, in the crowdsourced study, and comprised students that aspired to do research in north America in the field deployment). Further, the complex and deeply personal nature of self-reflection may not be fully captured in short-term interactions or through crowd-sourced evaluations. Future work should also consider more robust ways of studying self-reflection that account for individual differences, cultural contexts, and the long-term effects of AI-assisted guidance on career and academic decision-making.

This approach addresses a crucial gap in current online resources, which often prioritize immediate information delivery over fostering deeper cognitive processes.

Weasel words

Weasel words–phrases or words that sound good without conveying information–obscure precision.
I notice three kinds of weasel words in my students’ writing: (1) salt and pepper words, (2) beholder words and (3) lazy words.
Salt and pepper words
New grad students sprinkle in salt and pepper words for seasoning. These words look and feel like technical words, but convey nothing.
My favorite salt and pepper words/phrases are various, a number of, fairly, and quite. Sentences that cut these words out become stronger.
Bad: It is quite difficult to find untainted samples. Better: It is difficult to find untainted samples.
Bad: We used various methods to isolate four samples. Better: We isolated four samples.
Beholder words
Beholder words are those whose meaning is a function of the reader; for example: interestingly, surprisingly, remarkably, or clearly.
Peer reviewers don’t like judgments drawn for them.
Bad: False positives were surprisingly low. Better: To our surprise, false positives were low. Good: To our surprise, false positives were low (3Lazy words
Students insert lazy words in order to avoid making a quantitative characterization. They give the impression that the author has not yet conducted said characterization.
These words make the science feel unfirm and unfinished.
The two worst offenders in this category are the words very and extremely. These two adverbs are never excusable in technical writing. Never.
Other offenders include several, exceedingly, many, most, few, vast.
Bad: There is very close match between the two semantics. Better: There is a close match between the two semantics.
Adverbs
In technical writing, adverbs tend to come off as weasel words.
I’d even go so far as to say that the removal of all adverbs from any technical writing would be a net positive for my newest graduate students. (That is, new graduate students weaken a sentence when they insert adverbs more frequently than they strengthen it.)
Bad: We offer a completely different formulation of CFA. Better: We offer a different formulation of CFA.
Passive voice
There are times when the passive voice is acceptable in technical writing.
I also believe, as with adverbs, that removal of the passive voice would have been a net improvement for over half the technical writing I’ve edited. (That is, students abuse the passive voice more often than they use it well.)
Of course, I do not advocate dogmatic removal of the passive voice.
The passive voice is tough to shake. Even while writing this article, I caught myself defaulting to the passive in situations where the active was better.
The passive voice is bad when it hides relevant or explanatory information:
Bad: Termination is guaranteed on any input. Better: Termination is guaranteed on any input by a finite state-space. OK: A finite state-space guarantees termination on any input.
In the first sentence, the passive hides relevant information.
The second sentence includes the relevant information, but the passive misplaces the emphasis.
The third sentence contains all the relevant information, and it feels crisp.
There’s one case where I think the passive is preferrable in technical writing–when the subject is truly irrelevant:
OK: 4 mL HCl were added to the solution.
Even in this example, I personally don’t believe it’s egregious to use we:
OK (to me): We added 4 mL HCl to the solution.
In summary, for each use of the passive highlighted by my script, ask the following questions:
Is the agent relevant yet unclear?
Does the text read better with the sentence in the active?
If the answer to both questions is "yes," then change to the active.
If only the answer to the first question is "yes," then specify the agent.

Read the following text:
Many readers are not aware that the the brain will automatically ignore a second instance of the word "the" when it starts a new line.
Read that same text again, but with different line breaks:
Many readers are not aware that the the brain will automatically ignore a second instance of the word "the" when it starts a new line.
Duplicating words is a phenomenon of electronic composition.
They seem to happen as cut and paste accidents, and most frequently it’s with the word the.
Unfortunately, it can be difficult to proofread away duplicate words, because this lexical illusion prevents us from finding them.
No reviewer will shoot down a submission solely because it contains duplicate words, but when small mistakes like spelling errors and duplicate words pile up, they convey a lack of proofreading.
Reviewers will (rightfully) interpret inadequate proofreading as a lack of respect for their time and attention.
