An investigation of 𝒫𝒯 {\cal PT} -symmetry breaking in tight-binding chains
Jean-Marc Luck

Abstract
We consider non-Hermitian 𝒫𝒯 {\cal PT} -symmetric tight-binding chains where gain/loss optical potentials of equal magnitudes ± i ​ γ \pm{\rm i}\gamma are arbitrarily distributed over all sites. The main focus is on the threshold γ c \gamma_{c} beyond which 𝒫𝒯 {\cal PT} -symmetry is broken. This threshold generically falls off as a power of the chain length, whose exponent depends on the configuration of optical potentials, ranging between 1 (for balanced periodic chains) and 2 (for unbalanced periodic chains, where each half of the chain experiences a non-zero mean potential). For random sequences of optical potentials with zero average and finite variance, the threshold is itself a random variable, whose mean value decays with exponent 3/2 and whose fluctuations have a universal distribution. The chains yielding the most robust 𝒫𝒯 {\cal PT} -symmetric phase, i.e., the highest threshold at fixed chain length, are obtained by exact enumeration up to 48 sites. This optimal threshold exhibits an irregular dependence on the chain length, presumably decaying asymptotically with exponent 1, up to logarithmic corrections.



1
Introduction

Non-Hermitian Hamiltonians involving complex optical potentials
have been used in various guises for a long time,
in order to model either inelastic scattering or absorption,
in analogy with the use of complex refraction indices in optics.
Non-Hermitian operators have gained much attention lately,
both in classical and quantum physics
(see
[
1
]
for a recent comprehensive review).
Non-Hermitian Hamiltonians generically have complex spectra,
with a resulting non-unitary dynamics.
It was however emphasized by Bender and collaborators
[
2
,
3
]
,
generalizing an observation made by Bessis and Zinn-Justin
on the Lee-Yang Hamiltonian
{\cal H}=p^{2}+{\rm i}x^{3}
(see
[
4
]
),
that a whole class of non-Hermitian Hamiltonians may have real spectra.
These are the
{\cal PT}
-symmetric Hamiltonians,
which are invariant under the combined action
of spatial parity (
{\cal P}
) and time reversal (
{\cal T}
)
(see
[
5
]
for a full technical exposition,
and
[
6
,
7
]
for more accessible overviews).
The generic scenario is the following.
Let
{\cal H}
be a non-Hermitian
{\cal PT}
-symmetric Hamiltonian, obeying
[{\cal H},{\cal PT}]=0
.
If
{\cal H}
is close to being Hermitian,
in the sense that
\|{\cal H}^{\dagger}-{\cal H}\|
is small enough,

{\cal H}
and
{\cal PT}
have a complete set of common eigenvectors,
all energy eigenvalues of
{\cal H}
are real,
and the dynamics is unitary.
The system is in a
{\cal PT}
-symmetric phase, and
{\cal PT}
-symmetry is said to be unbroken.
If
\|{\cal H}^{\dagger}-{\cal H}\|
gets larger,
it may occur that some of the eigenvectors of
{\cal H}
are not eigenvectors of
{\cal PT}
any more.
This is possible since the
{\cal PT}
operator is not linear, but anti-linear
(see
[
5
]
for details).
The corresponding energy eigenvalues are complex,
so that the norm of a generic state vector blows up exponentially fast in time.

{\cal PT}
-symmetry is said to be broken.
The number of pairs of complex conjugate energy eigenvalues usually increases
with
\|{\cal H}^{\dagger}-{\cal H}\|
.
The threshold for
{\cal PT}
-symmetry breaking corresponds to the very first occurrence
of such a pair of complex eigenvalues.

Among the vast variety of
{\cal PT}
-symmetric classical and quantum systems
that have been investigated so far in more than 3,000 published papers,
tight-binding models are definitely among the easiest ones to grasp,
at least on the theoretical side.
Such models describe e.g. optical arrays consisting of coupled units,
some of them having gains and losses.
Most studies concern the one-dimensional geometry of
tight-binding chains
[
8
,
9
,
10
,
11
,
12
,
13
,
14
,
15
]
.
Various cases of finite chains consisting of
N=2M
sites, either pristine or disordered,
hosting one or several
{\cal PT}
-symmetric pairs of non-Hermitian impurities have been considered.
Scattering states and transport properties of
{\cal PT}
-symmetric chains
coupled to an environment through leads have also been considered
[
16
,
17
,
18
,
19
]
.
Most of these studies have dealt with the situation where all gains and losses
have the same magnitude
\gamma
, i.e.,
the optical potentials of all sites with gain (respectively, with loss)
read
+{\rm i}\gamma
(respectively,
-{\rm i}\gamma
).
The threshold for
{\cal PT}
-symmetry breaking is then a single number
\gamma_{c}
,
whose dependence on various model parameters has been explored in some detail
in various circumstances.
The presence of a single
{\cal PT}
-symmetric pair of sites with gain and loss
already yields a rich phenomenology
[
9
,
10
,
11
,
15
]
.
The situation where each site bears a random gain/loss optical potential
\pm{\rm i}\gamma

has also been considered
[
14
]
.
The dependence of the threshold on the chain length
has been found to strongly differ from one situation to another:
it reaches a finite limit in the case of a single pair of
{\cal PT}
-symmetric impurities
at the endpoints of a long pristine chain
[
9
,
11
]
,
whereas it is exponentially small in
M
in the insulating regime induced by Anderson localization,
i.e., when the on-site energies have strong enough random real parts
[
8
,
12
,
14
]
.
For
{\cal PT}
-symmetric gain/loss disorder alone,
numerical simulations have revealed
that the threshold has a smooth distribution
whose mean value falls off slowly as a function of the chain length
[
14
]
.

The aim of this paper is to put some of the findings recalled just above in a broader perspective,
by providing a systematic study of the threshold
of
{\cal PT}
-symmetric tight-binding chains of length
N=2M

where gain/loss optical potentials are distributed over all sites
in a periodic, random, or any other fashion.
For definiteness, we focus our attention onto a minimal setting,
where all gains and losses have the same magnitude
\gamma
,
whereas the chains are otherwise pristine,
in the sense that there are no other sources of inhomogeneity,
besides the optical potentials

{\rm i}\gamma\varepsilon_{n}
, with
\varepsilon_{n}=\pm 1
.
This model easily lends itself to an exact enumeration of all possible sequences
\varepsilon_{n}

of reduced optical potentials for a given chain size.
In this setting, the threshold for
{\cal PT}
-symmetry breaking is a single number
\gamma_{c}
,
depending only on the arrangement of the reduced optical potentials
\varepsilon_{n}
.
Right at this threshold, the spectrum
usually exhibits two symmetry-related exceptional points,
i.e., two (usually twofold) degenerate eigenvalues at opposite energies
(
\pm E_{c}
, with
E_{c}>0
),
or, albeit more rarely, a single exceptional point,
i.e., a single (again usually twofold) degenerate eigenvalue at zero energy.
More complex patterns of exceptional points may however occur at
\gamma_{c}
.
We shall meet two examples of such situations in the course of this work.
In Section
7
we provide an example with
M=6
,
where four twofold degeneracies take place simultaneously at non-zero energies given by (
7.3
).
In
B
it is shown that for
M=4
with generic optical potentials
there are four isolated points in the
\gamma_{1}
–
\gamma_{2}
plane
for which a fourfold degeneracy occurs at threshold at zero energy.

Throughout this work,
the main emphasis is on the scaling behavior of the threshold
\gamma_{c}
as a function of the chain length
for various classes of configurations of optical potentials.
Section
2
contains the theoretical framework of the model,
including the transfer-matrix approach and the discrete Riccati formalism.
General results are presented in Section
3
,
including explicit results for all chains up to
M=3
(Section
3.1
)
and an overview of the behavior of the threshold for various classes of sequences
(Section
3.2
).
More specific results for these classes of sequences are derived in the subsequent sections.
This detailed study includes diblock chains
and other unbalanced periodic chains (Section
4
),
alternating chains and other balanced periodic chains (Section
5
),
random chains (Section
6
),
and the chains yielding the most robust
{\cal PT}
-symmetric phases,
i.e., the largest threshold at fixed chain length (Section
7
).
Section
8
is devoted to a brief discussion.
Three appendices contain more technical matters.

2
Theoretical framework

2.1
The model

The focus of this work is on the time-independent

{\cal PT}
-symmetric tight-binding equation

\psi_{n-1}+\psi_{n+1}+{\rm i}\gamma\varepsilon_{n}\psi_{n}=E\psi_{n}

(2.1)

on a finite open chain of
N=2M
sites,
with Dirichlet boundary conditions

\psi_{0}=\psi_{2M+1}=0.

(2.2)

Each site bears a purely imaginary gain/loss optical potential of magnitude
\gamma
,
whereas
\varepsilon_{n}=\pm 1
is an arbitrary sequence of signs,
with
\varepsilon_{n}=+1
(abbreviated to
\varepsilon_{n}=+
) corresponding to gain,
and
\varepsilon_{n}=-1
(abbreviated to
\varepsilon_{n}=-
) to loss.
We always assume that the model is
{\cal PT}
-symmetric.
This translates to the constraint

\varepsilon_{2M+1-n}=-\varepsilon_{n},

(2.3)

imposing that the sequence of signs is of the form

\varepsilon_{1},\dots,\varepsilon_{2M}=\underbrace{\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2},\dots,\varepsilon_{M}},\underbrace{-\varepsilon_{M},\dots,-\varepsilon_{2},-\varepsilon_{1}}.

(2.4)

In the following,

(\varepsilon_{1},\dots,\varepsilon_{2M})
will be referred to as the sequence defining the chain,
and
(\varepsilon_{1},\dots,\varepsilon_{M})
as the corresponding half-sequence.

For a given
{\cal PT}
-symmetric sequence,
the spectrum of (
2.1
) consists of
2M
energy eigenvalues
E_{a}
,
which can either be real or occur in complex conjugate pairs
(see Section
2.3
for more details).
This spectrum is invariant under
{\cal P}
and
{\cal T}
separately,
so that one can choose
\gamma\geq 0
without loss of generality.
We are mainly interested in the threshold
\gamma_{c}
associated with the occurrence
of the first pair of complex conjugate eigenvalues,
and especially in the dependence of
\gamma_{c}
on the length
M
and on the nature
(periodic, random, etc.) of the half-sequence.
It is worth recalling here that only
{\cal PT}
-symmetric sequences give rise to a non-zero
\gamma_{c}
.
This can be shown by means of first-order perturbation theory
(see
[
14
]
, and
A
for a detailed proof).

2.2
Transfer-matrix approach

The transfer-matrix approach is a very useful tool
in the theory of one-dimensional disordered systems
[
20
,
21
,
22
,
23
,
24
]
.
In the present situation, the tight-binding equation (
2.1
) can be recast as

\pmatrix{\psi_{n+1}\cr\psi_{n}}=T_{n}\pmatrix{\psi_{n}\cr\psi_{n-1}},\qquad\pmatrix{\psi_{n-1}\cr\psi_{n}}=T_{n}\pmatrix{\psi_{n}\cr\psi_{n+1}},

(2.5)

where the transfer matrix
T_{n}
associated with site
n
reads

T_{n}=\pmatrix{E-{\rm i}\gamma\varepsilon_{n}&-1\cr 1&0}.

(2.6)

Iterating the recursion (
2.5
) along the half-sequence
and along its
{\cal PT}
-symmetric partner,
using the Dirichlet boundary conditions (
2.2
)
and the
{\cal PT}
-symmetry constraint (
2.3
),
we respectively obtain
1
1

1

Throughout this paper a star denotes complex conjugation.

\pmatrix{\psi_{M+1}\cr\psi_{M}}=U_{M}\pmatrix{\psi_{1}\cr 0},\qquad\pmatrix{\psi_{M}\cr\psi_{M+1}}=U^{\star}_{M}\pmatrix{\psi_{2M}\cr 0},

(2.7)

with

\displaystyle U_{M}=T_{M}\dots T_{1}=\pmatrix{a_{M}&b_{M}\cr c_{M}&d_{M}},

\displaystyle U^{\star}_{M}=T_{M+1}\dots T_{2M}=\pmatrix{a^{\star}_{M}&b^{\star}_{M}\cr c^{\star}_{M}&d^{\star}_{M}}.

(2.8)

We have therefore

\psi_{M}=c_{M}\psi_{1}=a^{\star}_{M}\psi_{2M},\qquad\psi_{M+1}=a_{M}\psi_{1}=c^{\star}_{M}\psi_{2M}.

(2.9)

The quantization condition which determines the spectrum of (
2.1
) therefore reads

a_{M}a^{\star}_{M}=c_{M}c^{\star}_{M},\qquad\hbox{i.e.,}\quad|a_{M}|=|c_{M}|.

(2.10)

We have
c_{n}=a_{n-1}
, whereas the entries
a_{n}
obey the linear recursion

a_{n}=(E-{\rm i}\gamma\varepsilon_{n})a_{n-1}-a_{n-2},

(2.11)

with
a_{0}=1
and
a_{-1}=0
.

2.3
Discrete Riccati formalism

Discrete Riccati variables are another classic tool
of the theory of one-dimensional disordered systems
[
20
,
21
,
22
,
23
,
24
]
.
In the present situation, they read

R_{n}=\frac{a_{n}}{a_{n-1}}.

(2.12)

These ratios obey the non-linear recursion

R_{n}=E-{\rm i}\gamma\varepsilon_{n}-\frac{1}{R_{n-1}},

(2.13)

known as a discrete Riccati equation, with initial value
R_{0}=\infty
.
In terms of these variables, the quantization condition (
2.10
) reads

R_{M}R^{\star}_{M}=1,\qquad\hbox{i.e.,}\quad|R_{M}|=1.

(2.14)

The above condition yields after reduction a polynomial equation
of the form
P(E,\gamma)=0
,
where the characteristic polynomial
P
has degree
2M
in
E
and in
\gamma
,
has real coefficients, and is even in each of the variables
E
and
\gamma

(see examples in Section
3.1
).
These properties have several consequences.

For a generic value of
\gamma
,
the spectrum of (
2.1
) consists of
2M
distinct energy eigenvalues,
which are the zeros of
P
.
If
E
is an eigenvalue, so are
E^{\star}
,
-E
and
-E^{\star}
.
These four numbers degenerate into two either if
E
is real (and so
E^{\star}=E
)
or if
E
is imaginary (and so
E^{\star}=-E
).
They degenerate into one if
E=0
.

At the threshold
\gamma_{c}
,
there are usually two (usually twofold) degenerate eigenvalues at opposite real energies
(
\pm E_{c}
, with
E_{c}>0
),
or, albeit more rarely, a single (usually twofold) degenerate eigenvalue at zero energy.
As announced in the Introduction,
more complex degeneracy patterns may take place at
\gamma_{c}
.

The recursion (
2.13
) implies

|R_{n}|+\frac{1}{|R_{n-1}|}\geq\sqrt{E^{2}+\gamma^{2}}

(2.15)

whenever
E
is real.
If the r.h.s. of the above inequality is larger than 2,
it can be shown by recursion that
|R_{n}|>1
for all
n
,
so that the quantization condition (
2.14
) cannot be satisfied.
In other words, all real eigenvalues of (
2.1
) obey

|E|\leq\sqrt{4-\gamma^{2}},

(2.16)

independently of the length
M
.
The real spectrum is therefore reduced
with respect to that of a free tight-binding particle (
|E|\leq 2
).
It shrinks down to zero as
\gamma\to 2
.
In particular, all sequences have
\gamma_{c}<2
.

3.2
Panoramic overview

We now turn to an overview of the behavior of the threshold
\gamma_{c}
for
{\cal PT}
-symmetry breaking
for various kinds of configurations (periodic, random, etc.) of the optical potentials.
We have run a computer routine which enumerates
all the
2^{M-1}
half-sequences of length
M
starting with
\varepsilon_{1}=+
,
up to a maximal length
M=24
, i.e.,
N=48
,
and determines for each sequence accurate numerical values of the threshold
and of the corresponding degenerate energy eigenvalues.
The most notable emerging features are illustrated in Figure
2
,
and will be studied in detail in the forthcoming sections.
In the regime of large chains,
thresholds fall off to zero, generically as a power law of the form

\gamma_{c}\sim\frac{1}{M^{\beta}}.

(3.23)

Different classes of sequences yield different decay exponents
\beta
.
The smallest of all
\gamma_{c}
, decaying with exponent
\beta=2
,
is reached for the diblock chains having a constant half-sequence
(see black dataset and Section
4.1
).
The same scaling holds for all unbalanced periodic sequences
with a non-zero mean value (see Section
4.2
).
Alternating half-sequences,
as well as all balanced periodic ones with zero mean value,
have thresholds falling off with exponent
\beta=1

(see green dataset and Section
5
).
The mean value of
\gamma_{c}
over all half-sequences of given length
scales with exponent
\beta=3/2
.
The same scaling holds for typical random sequences,
i.e., for almost all half-sequences
(see red dataset and Section
6
).
Finally, the largest threshold at fixed
M

has an irregular dependence on
M
and presumably also scales asymptotically as
1/M
,
up to logarithmic corrections
(see blue dataset and Section
7
).

4
Diblock and other unbalanced periodic chains

4.1
Diblock chains

This section is devoted to diblock chains,
which are the most unbalanced of all
{\cal PT}
-symmetric chains.
On a diblock chain of of length
N=2M
,
the
M
sites with gain are to the left,
and the
M
sites with loss are to the right.
In other terms, the half-sequence is constant, i.e.,
\varepsilon_{1}=\dots=\varepsilon_{M}=+
,
and so
\varepsilon_{M+1}=\dots=\varepsilon_{2M}=-
.
It is expected that the
{\cal PT}
-symmetric phase
is the most fragile on these diblock chains.
This is corroborated by the observation made in Section
3.2

that these chains yield the smallest threshold
at fixed chain length (see black dataset in Figure
2
).
The latter observation will now, in turn, be made quantitative by analytical means.

Instead of using a straightforward approach borrowed from elementary Quantum Mechanics,
i.e., looking for eigenfunctions of (
2.1
) as linear combinations of plane waves
with complex wavenumbers in each block,
we prefer to have recourse to the Riccati formalism introduced in Section
2.3
,
since the latter approach will prove more powerful in more complex situations.

The recursion (
2.13
) obeyed by the Riccati variables does not depend explicitly
on
n
and reads

R_{n}=2\cosh\mu-\frac{1}{R_{n-1}}\qquad(n=1,\dots,M),

(4.1)

where

\mu=\eta+{\rm i}q

(4.2)

is a complex variable such that

2\cosh\mu=E-{\rm i}\gamma,

(4.3)

i.e.,

E=2\cos q\;\cosh\eta,\qquad\gamma=-2\sin q\;\sinh\eta.

(4.4)

The fixed points of (
4.1
) are
R=\rme^{\pm\mu}
.
Setting

Y_{n}=\frac{R_{n}-\rme^{-\mu}}{R_{n}-\rme^{\mu}},

(4.5)

the recursion (
4.1
) boils down to the multiplication by a constant, i.e.,

Y_{n}=\rme^{2\mu}Y_{n-1}.

(4.6)

The initial value
R_{0}=\infty
yields
Y_{0}=1
, hence
Y_{n}=e^{2n\mu}
, and finally

R_{n}=\frac{\sinh(n+1)\mu}{\sinh n\mu}.

(4.7)

The quantization condition (
2.14
) therefore reads

\sinh(M+1)\mu\;\sinh(M+1)\mu^{\star}=\sinh M\mu\;\sinh M\mu^{\star},

(4.8)

i.e.,

\sin q\;\sin(2M+1)q+\sinh\eta\;\sinh(2M+1)\eta=0.

(4.9)

The above equations are exact for any finite
M
.

We are mainly interested in the scaling behavior of
\gamma_{c}
for long chains.
It turns out that the most unstable energy eigenvalues are close to the band edges
(
E\to\pm 2
).
Let us focus our attention onto the upper band edge (
E\to 2
, i.e.,
q\to 0
)
and introduce the scaling variables

x=(2M+1)q,\qquad g=\frac{(2M+1)^{2}}{4}\,\gamma.

(4.10)

We have then
(2M+1)\eta\approx(-2g/x)
,
and so the quantization condition (
4.9
) becomes

\sin x\approx-\frac{2g}{x^{2}}\sinh\frac{2g}{x},

(4.11)

up to corrections of relative order
1/(2M+1)^{2}
.

In the absence of optical potentials (
g=0
), the r.h.s. of (
4.11
) vanishes.
We thus obtain

x\approx a\pi\qquad(a=1,2,\dots),

(4.12)

in perfect agreement with (

).

g
of gains and losses increases,

especially so for the very first values of

,

.

) intersects the sine function at integer values of

(see (

)).
The green curve (

The red one, corresponding to

(4.13)

(4.14)

(blue symbol),
where the lowest pair of eigenvalues (

Other mergings between

and 4,
a=5
and 6, and so on,
take place at higher well-defined critical values of

.

In brief, for long diblock chains of length

-symmetry breaking scales as

4.2
Unbalanced periodic chains

We now turn to unbalanced periodic chains.
A
{\cal PT}
-symmetric chain is said to be periodic
if the half-sequence
\varepsilon_{1},\dots,\varepsilon_{M}
is periodic,
i.e.,
\varepsilon_{n}=\varepsilon_{n+L}
for some period
L\geq 1
and all
n=1,\dots,M-L
.
The motif
(\varepsilon_{1},\dots\varepsilon_{L})
is called the unit cell of the periodic sequence.
In this section we make the assumption that the periodic sequence
is unbalanced, i.e., its mean value
{\overline{\varepsilon}}

is non-zero.
2
2

2

Throughout this paper a bar denotes a spatial average.
If the sequence is disordered, e.g. with independent entries,
this amounts to averaging over disorder.

In the present case, we have

{\overline{\varepsilon}}=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{n=1}^{L}\varepsilon_{n}=\frac{L_{+}-L_{-}}{L},

(4.18)

where
L_{+}
and
L_{-}
are the numbers of plus and minus signs
(i.e., of sites with gain and loss) in the unit cell, with
L_{+}+L_{-}=L
.

For the time being, let us follow a heuristic line of thought.
If
{\overline{\varepsilon}}
is positive,
on a spatial scale much larger than the period
L
,
the left half of the chain has a roughly homogeneous gain,
whereas the right half has a roughly homogeneous loss, and vice versa if
{\overline{\varepsilon}}
is negative.
Any unbalanced periodic sequence therefore amounts to an effective diblock sequence,
with
\gamma
being replaced by the effective value
\gamma_{\rm eff}=\gamma{\overline{\varepsilon}}
.
This approach predicts that the threshold
of a long unbalanced periodic chain
is asymptotically given by the product of the expression (
4.15
)
for diblock chains and of an amplification factor

f=\frac{1}{|{\overline{\varepsilon}}|}=\frac{L}{|L_{+}-L_{-}|},

(4.19)

depending only on the contents of the unit cell of the sequence.
This reads

\gamma_{c}\approx\frac{4fg_{c}}{(2M+1)^{2}}\sim\frac{fg_{c}}{M^{2}}.

(4.20)

Consistently,
diblock chains themselves can be viewed as unbalanced periodic ones with
L=L_{+}=1
,
and so
f=1
.
Allowing larger and larger periods,
the amplification factor
f
may become
either arbitrarily close to unity or arbitrarily large:
if
L\geq 3
and
L_{\pm}=1
, we have
f=L/(L-2)
;
if
L
is odd and
L_{\pm}=(L-1)/2
, we have
f=L
.

The result (
4.20
) turns out to be asymptotically correct,
in spite of its heuristic derivation.
This will be shown in a broader setting in Section
6.1
,
by means of a continuum Riccati approach.

The non-trivial unbalanced chain with shortest period
has
L=3
and unit cell
(++-)
, hence
f=3
.
Figure
5
provides a quantitative check of the prediction (
4.20
)
on this example.
Here again, the combination
(2M+1)^{2}\gamma_{c}/4
is plotted against
1/(2M+1)^{2}
, up to
M=50
.
Data for finite chains converge to the predicted limit value
3g_{c}\approx 13.425

(horizontal blue line), oscillating with the period
L=3

of the underlying sequence of gains and losses.
Oscillations are damped linearly on the chosen scale.

5
Alternating and other balanced periodic chains

5.1
Alternating chains

In this section we consider alternating chains,
where sites with gains and losses alternate along the chain,
so that
\varepsilon_{n}=(-1)^{n}
.
If the chain length
N=2M
is even,
this configuration of optical potentials obeys
{\cal PT}
-symmetry.
Alternating chains are periodic with period
L=2
and balanced,
in the sense that
{\overline{\varepsilon}}=0
,
so that the analysis of Section
4.2
does not apply.

Our goal is to derive the exact threshold
\gamma_{c}
for any finite chain length
N=2M

(see (
5.11
)).
The analysis again relies on the Riccati formalism.
The recursion (
2.13
) takes two different forms according to the parity of
n
:

R_{2p+1}=E+{\rm i}\gamma-\frac{1}{R_{2p}},\qquad R_{2p+2}=E-{\rm i}\gamma-\frac{1}{R_{2p+1}}.

(5.1)

These two equations can be combined, yielding

R_{2p+2}=\frac{(E^{2}+\gamma^{2}-1)R_{2p}-E+{\rm i}\gamma}{(E+{\rm i}\gamma)R_{2p}-1}.

(5.2)

Introducing the parametrization

E+{\rm i}\gamma=2\cos q\,\rme^{{\rm i}\theta},

(5.3)

i.e.,

E=2\cos q\;\cos\theta,\qquad\gamma=2\cos q\;\sin\theta,

(5.4)

and so

E^{2}+\gamma^{2}=4\cos^{2}q,

(5.5)

the fixed points of (
5.2
) are
R=\rme^{-{\rm i}(\theta\pm q)}
.
Setting

Y_{n}=\frac{R_{n}-\rme^{-{\rm i}(\theta+q)}}{R_{n}-\rme^{-{\rm i}(\theta-q)}},

(5.6)

the recursion (
5.2
) boils down to

Y_{2p+2}=\rme^{4{\rm i}q}Y_{2p}.

(5.7)

The initial value
R_{0}=\infty
yields
Y_{0}=1
, hence
Y_{2p}=e^{4{\rm i}pq}
, and finally

R_{2p}=\rme^{-{\rm i}\theta}\;\frac{\sin(2p+1)q}{\sin 2pq},\qquad R_{2p+1}=\rme^{{\rm i}\theta}\;\frac{\sin(2p+2)q}{\sin(2p+1)q}.

(5.8)

The quantization condition (
2.14
) reads

\sin(2M+1)q=0,

(5.9)

irrespective of
\theta
and of the parity of
M
.
This equation is considerably simpler than its counterpart (
4.9
) for diblock chains.
It yields the explicit result

q=\frac{k\pi}{2M+1}\qquad(k=1,\dots,M).

(5.10)

For each quantized value of
q
, labelled by the integer
k
,
a pair of eigenvalues merges at zero energy as
\theta\to\pi/2
,
whereas
\gamma
reaches the critical value
\gamma_{k}=2\cos q_{k}
.
The pattern of real eigenvalues in the
E
–
\gamma
plane
therefore has the form of a rainbow of
M
nested arches
(see panels (1), (3) and (6) of Figure
1
).
The first merging event corresponds to the smallest critical value, i.e.,
k=M
.
We thus obtain the exact expression

\gamma_{c}=2\sin\frac{\pi}{2(2M+1)}

(5.11)

for the threshold of alternating
{\cal PT}
-symmetric chains of any finite length.
We recover known values of
\gamma_{c}
for the first values of
M
, namely

\gamma_{c}=2\sin\pi/6=1
for
M=1
(see (
3.2
)),

\gamma_{c}=2\sin\pi/10=(\sqrt{5}-1)/2
for
M=2
(see (
3.7
)),
and
\gamma_{c}=2\sin\pi/14
for
M=3
(see (
3.17
)).
For long alternating chains,
the threshold falls off as

\gamma_{c}\approx\frac{\pi}{2M}.

(5.12)

This law of decay with exponent
\beta=1
was announced in Section
3.2
.

5.2
One example with period four

We now turn to an example of balanced periodic chains
where pairs of sites with gains and losses form an alternating pattern.
These chains have period
L=4
and unit cell
(++--)
.
The analysis again relies on the Riccati formalism.
The recursion (
2.13
) reads

\displaystyle R_{4p+1}=E-{\rm i}\gamma-\frac{1}{R_{4p}},\qquad R_{4p+2}=E-{\rm i}\gamma-\frac{1}{R_{4p+1}},

\displaystyle R_{4p+3}=E+{\rm i}\gamma-\frac{1}{R_{4p+2}},\qquad R_{4p+4}=E+{\rm i}\gamma-\frac{1}{R_{4p+3}}.

(5.13)

These four equations can be combined into a recursion
expressing
R_{4p+4}
as a function of
R_{4p}
,
whose expression is more intricate than (
5.2
).
Anticipating that the threshold
corresponds to a merging of eigenvalues around
E_{c}=\sqrt{2}

(see the discussion below (
5.34
)),
we set
E=\sqrt{2}+a
, and simplify the recursion
in the scaling regime where both
a
and
\gamma
are small.
We thus obtain

R_{4p+4}=\frac{(1-2\sqrt{2}\,a)R_{4p}+2{\rm i}\gamma+4a}{1+2\sqrt{2}\,a+(2{\rm i}\gamma-4a)R_{4p}}.

(5.14)

Introducing parameters
w
,
\phi
and
\theta
such that

a={\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}}w\cos\phi,\qquad\gamma=w\sin\phi,\qquad\cos\theta=\frac{\cos\phi}{\sqrt{2}},

(5.15)

with
0<\phi<\pi
and
\pi/4<\theta<3\pi/4
,
the fixed points of (
5.14
) are
R=\rme^{{\rm i}(\phi\pm\theta)}
.
Setting

Y_{4p}=\frac{R_{4p}-\rme^{{\rm i}(\phi-\theta)}}{R_{4p}-\rme^{{\rm i}(\phi+\theta)}},

(5.16)

the recursion (
5.14
) boils down to

Y_{4p+4}=\rme^{-2{\rm i}\lambda}Y_{4p},

(5.17)

with

\tan\lambda=2w\sin\theta.

(5.18)

The initial value
R_{0}=\infty
yields
Y_{0}=1
, hence
Y_{4p}=e^{-2{\rm i}p\lambda}
, and finally

R_{4p}=\rme^{{\rm i}\phi}\;\frac{\sin(p\lambda-\theta)}{\sin p\lambda},\qquad R_{4p+1}=\sqrt{2}-\rme^{-{\rm i}\phi}\;\frac{\sin p\lambda}{\sin(p\lambda-\theta)}.

(5.19)

For
M=4p
(and also for
M=4p+2
),
the quantization condition (
2.14
) reads

\sin(p\lambda-\theta)=\pm\sin p\lambda,

(5.20)

yielding

\lambda=\frac{\theta+k\pi}{2p},

(5.21)

with
k
integer.
The threshold is expected to correspond to the smallest value of
\lambda
, i.e.,
k=0
.
It can be shown that the rescaled combinations

G=M\gamma,\qquad x=Ma=M(E-\sqrt{2}),

(5.22)

asymptotically only depend on
\theta
, according to

G=\frac{\theta\sqrt{-\cos 2\theta}}{\sin\theta},\qquad x=\frac{\theta\cot\theta}{\sqrt{2}}.

(5.23)

The threshold
\gamma_{c}
corresponds to the point in the
x
–
G
plane
where the curve given parametrically by (
5.23
)
has a horizontal tangent, as in Figure
1
,
i.e., to the maximum of the function
G(\theta)
.
As
\theta
is varied between
\pi/4
and
3\pi/4
,

G
reaches its maximum
G_{1}
for
\theta_{1}
such that
\theta_{1}=\cos 2\theta_{1}\tan\theta_{1}
,
i.e.,
\theta_{1}\approx 1.937\,183
, where we have

G_{1}\approx 1.788\,897,\qquad x_{1}\approx-0.525\,607.

(5.24)

For
M=4p+1
(and also for
M=4p+3
),
the quantization condition (
2.14
) reads

\sin^{2}(p\lambda-\theta)+\sin^{2}p\lambda-4\cos\theta\;\sin p\lambda\;\sin(p\lambda-\theta)=0,

(5.25)

whose smallest solution is

\lambda=\frac{\theta+\alpha}{2p},

(5.26)

with

\cos\alpha=\frac{\cos 2\theta}{\cos\theta}

(5.27)

and
0<\alpha<\pi
.
The quantities introduced in (
5.22
) now read

G=\frac{(\theta+\alpha)\sqrt{-\cos 2\theta}}{\sin\theta},\qquad x=\frac{(\theta+\alpha)\cot\theta}{\sqrt{2}}.

(5.28)

The threshold is obtained by the maximization procedure applied to (
5.23
).
As
\theta
is varied between
\pi/4
and
3\pi/4
,

G
reaches its maximum
G_{2}

for
\theta_{2}\approx 2.179\,171
, where we have

G_{2}\approx 2.223\,114,\qquad x_{2}\approx-1.525\,862.

(5.29)

To sum up,
for large chains with even
M
,
the threshold scales as

\gamma_{c}\approx\frac{G_{1}}{M},

(5.30)

and corresponds to two pairs of degenerate eigenvalues at
\pm E_{c}
, with

E_{c}\approx\sqrt{2}+\frac{x_{1}}{M},

(5.31)

where
G_{1}
and
x_{1}
are given in (
5.24
).
For large chains with odd
M
,
the threshold scales as

\gamma_{c}\approx\frac{G_{2}}{M},

(5.32)

and corresponds to two pairs of degenerate eigenvalues at
\pm E_{c}
, with

E_{c}\approx\sqrt{2}+\frac{x_{2}}{M},

(5.33)

where
G_{2}
and
x_{2}
are given in (
5.29
).

The product
M\gamma_{c}
is plotted against
1/M
, up to
M=100
.
The upper (resp. lower) dataset corresponds to odd (resp. even) values of
M
.
The blue lines have the theoretical intercepts
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
(see (
5.24
), (
5.29
)).

5.3
Balanced periodic chains

Let us now turn to balanced periodic chains
with even period
L
and
{\overline{\varepsilon}}=0
(see (
4.18
)).
Only a few general features can be stated in the present situation.
This is in strong contrast with unbalanced chains,
investigated in Section
4.2
,
where the quantitative prediction (
4.20
) has been derived in full generality.

The Fourier transform of a periodic chain with period
L

is supported by all integer multiples of the reciprocal period
2\pi/L
.
This singles out
L-1
resonant energies
inside the band of the free tight-binding chain, i.e.,

E_{a}=2\cos\frac{a\pi}{L}\qquad(a=1,\dots,L-1).

(5.34)

For long balanced periodic chains
(M\gg 1)
,
it is expected that the energy
E_{c}
at which the first merging of eigenvalues
takes place approaches one of those resonant energies.
The convention
E_{c}\geq 0
selects
a=1,\dots,L/2
, the last case yielding
E_{c}=0
.
For the alternating chain (see Section
5.1
),

L=2
and
a=1
correctly predict
E_{c}=0
.
For the chain studied in Section
5.2
,

L=4
and
a=1
correctly predict
E_{c}=\sqrt{2}
,
since
a=2
is ruled out by symmetry.
For chains with larger periods
L
,
there is no systematic way of predicting the resonant energy
E_{c}
.
There are actually examples where
E_{c}
keeps varying periodically with
M
mod
L
.

The threshold for
{\cal PT}
-symmetry breaking is expected to decay as

\gamma_{c}\approx\frac{G_{c}}{M}

(5.35)

for all balanced periodic chains.
For the alternating chain (see Section
5.1
),
we have
G_{c}=\pi/2
(see (
5.12
)).
For the chain with period four studied in Section
5.2
,
G_{c}
alternates
between the two numbers
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
,
according to the parity of
M
(see (
5.24
), (
5.29
)).
For chains with larger periods,
the amplitude
G_{c}
is generically expected to vary periodically with
M
mod
L
.
Many questions remain open,
including whether the value
\pi/2
obtained for alternating chains is a lower bound for
G_{c}

and whether
G_{c}
may become arbitrarily large.

6
Random chains

This section is devoted to random
{\cal PT}
-symmetric chains,
where each symbol of the half-sequence
(\varepsilon_{1},\dots,\varepsilon_{M})

is taken to be
\varepsilon_{n}=\pm
with equal probabilities.
Equivalently, the
2^{M}
different half-sequences are considered as equally probable.
The threshold
\gamma_{c}
for
{\cal PT}
-symmetry breaking is now a random variable,
as it depends on the chain under scrutiny.
This situation has been studied by numerical means in
[
14
]
.

6.1
Continuum Riccati formalism

The forthcoming analysis again relies on the Riccati formalism introduced in Section
2.3
.
We are mainly interested in the behavior of the threshold
\gamma_{c}
for long random chains.
In this regime,
\gamma_{c}
is typically small,
whereas the first mergings concern pairs
of eigenvalues close to the band edges (
E\to\pm 2
).

Let us focus for definiteness our attention onto the upper band edge (
E\to 2
).
In the vicinity of the latter, setting
E=2-q^{2}
,
in the presence of small optical potentials
{\rm i}\gamma_{n}={\rm i}\gamma\varepsilon_{n}
,
it is advantageous to consider the small variable

Z_{n}=R_{n}-1,

(6.1)

in terms of which the recursion (
2.13
) reads

Z_{n}=-q^{2}-{\rm i}\gamma_{n}+\frac{Z_{n-1}}{1+Z_{n-1}}=-q^{2}-{\rm i}\gamma_{n}+Z_{n-1}-Z_{n-1}^{2}+\cdots

(6.2)

This expansion suggests to use a continuum formalism,
considering
Z(n)
as a continuous function of the real variable
n
,
obeying the Riccati differential equation

\frac{{\rm d}Z}{{\rm d}n}=-q^{2}-{\rm i}\gamma(n)-Z^{2},

(6.3)

with initial condition

Z(0)=\infty.

(6.4)

In spite of this, the function
Z(n)
is small all over the relevant range.
In particular, if the optical potential is constant,
setting
\mu^{2}=-q^{2}-{\rm i}\gamma
,
the solution to (
6.3
) reads

Z=\mu\mathop{{\rm cotanh}}n\mu.

(6.5)

To leading order as
\mu\to 0
,
for fixed values of the product
n\mu
,
this is in agreement with (
4.7
),
which can be recast as

R_{n}=\cosh\mu+\sinh\mu\mathop{{\rm cotanh}}n\mu=1+\mu\mathop{{\rm cotanh}}n\mu+{\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}}\mu^{2}+\cdots

(6.6)

Before we pursue with random chains,
let us come back for a while to unbalanced periodic chains,
investigated in Section
4.2
.
If the sequence
\varepsilon_{n}
is periodic with period
L
and has a non-zero mean value
{\overline{\varepsilon}}
,
the continuous function
\gamma(n)
has the same period and mean value
{\overline{\gamma}}=\gamma{\overline{\varepsilon}}
.
In the situation of interest, namely sequences of length
M\gg L
,
the function
Z(n)
varies typically on a scale
M
.
It is therefore slowly varying at the scale of one period,
so that it is legitimate to replace
\gamma(n)
by its mean value
{\overline{\gamma}}
.
This puts the heuristic line of thought of Section
4.2
on a firmer basis.

6.2
Predictions for random chains

For long random
{\cal PT}
-symmetric chains,
the function
\gamma(n)
becomes in the continuum limit
a Gaussian white noise with covariance

{\overline{\gamma(m)\gamma(n)}}=\gamma^{2}\delta(m-n).

(6.7)

The continuum Riccati equation (
6.3
) for the function
Z(n)

has to be integrated from
n=0
, with initial condition (
6.4
),
to
n=M
, the length of the half-sequence,
where the quantization condition (
2.14
) translates to

\mathop{{\rm Re}}Z(M)=0.

(6.8)

The Riccati differential equation driven by a real Gaussian white noise, i.e.,

\frac{{\rm d}Z}{{\rm d}x}=-q^{2}-V(x)-Z^{2},

(6.9)

with

{\overline{V(x)V(y)}}=w^{2}\delta(x-y),

(6.10)

has a long history, dating back to pioneering studies in the 1960s
of Anderson localization in a one-dimensional white-noise potential
[
25
,
26
]
.
The Lyapunov exponent

\Gamma={\overline{Z(\infty)}},

(6.11)

representing the inverse localization length of the model on an infinite line,
obeys a scaling law of the form

\Gamma\approx\left({\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}}w^{2}\right)^{1/3}\,F(x),\qquad x=\left({\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}}w^{2}\right)^{-2/3}\,q^{2},

(6.12)

in the low-energy scaling regime where
q
and
w
are simultaneously small.
The scaling function
F(x)
is known in terms of Airy functions:

F(x)=\frac{{\rm Ai}(x){\rm Ai}^{\prime}(x)+{\rm Bi}(x){\rm Bi}^{\prime}(x)}{{\rm Ai}(x)^{2}+{\rm Bi}(x)^{2}}.

(6.13)

The result (
6.12
) is commonly referred to as anomalous band-edge scaling.
It has been rederived several times,
both in continuum and in lattice models
[
27
,
28
,
29
]
.
Several works have been devoted to the fluctuations of the process
Z(x)

and of closely related ones around their stationary mean value
[
30
,
31
,
32
]
.
The mathematics involved there is quite intricate.

Fortunately enough, predictions of interest concerning the statistics of the threshold
\gamma_{c}

of long random chains can be derived from the sole consideration of scale invariance.
The continuous Riccati equations (
6.3
), (
6.9
)
driven by noises obeying (
6.7
), (
6.10
)
are invariant under the scaling transformation

(n,x)\to\frac{1}{\lambda}\,(n,x),\quad(Z,q)\to\lambda(Z,q),\quad(\gamma,w)\to\lambda^{3/2}(\gamma,w).

(6.14)

For the problem of Anderson localization,
the scale invariance of the Riccati equation (
6.9
)
dictates the scaling form (
6.12
) of the Lyapunov exponent.

8
Discussion

This paper has been devoted to non-Hermitian
{\cal PT}
-symmetric
tight-binding chains of
N=2M
sites,
where gain/loss optical potentials of equal magnitudes,
of the form
{\rm i}\gamma\varepsilon_{n}
with
\varepsilon_{n}=\pm 1
,
are distributed over all sites in a periodic, random, or any other fashion.
We have provided a systematic study of the threshold
\gamma_{c}
for
{\cal PT}
-symmetry breaking,
with the main emphasis being on the dependence of
\gamma_{c}
on the chain length
for various classes of configurations of optical potentials.
Our main findings are summarized in the
panoramic overview of the asymptotic behavior of
\gamma_{c}
for various classes of chains
given in Section
3.2
(see Figure
2
).
The threshold generically falls off as a power of the chain length, i.e.,

\gamma_{c}\sim\frac{1}{M^{\beta}}.

(8.1)

The decay exponent
\beta
depends on the configuration of optical potentials.
The largest exponent
\beta=2
is reached for diblock chains,
and more generally for all unbalanced periodic chains,
i.e., periodic configurations of optical potentials
where each half of the chain has on average either gain or loss.
The smallest exponent
\beta=1

is attained for alternating chains, and more generally for all balanced periodic chains,
i.e., periodic configurations of optical potentials whose spatial average over one period vanishes.
For random sequences of optical potentials with zero average and finite variance,
the threshold
\gamma_{c}
is itself a random variable, whose mean value decays with exponent
\beta=3/2
,
whereas the fluctuations around this mean value obey a universal distribution
f(\xi)
.

These results have been corroborated and made quantitative
by detailed analytical studies performed in Sections
4
to
6
.
This analysis relies on concepts and techniques from the physics of one-dimensional disordered systems,
including the transfer-matrix approach and the discrete Riccati formalism.

The above results can be put in a broader context as follows.
Consider the sum

S=\varepsilon_{1}+\cdots+\varepsilon_{M}

(8.2)

of all reduced optical potentials over the left half of the chain,
and assume that this sum grows as

S\sim M^{\omega},

(8.3)

with a wandering exponent in the range
0\leq\omega\leq 1
.
The three classes of configurations of optical potentials recalled above
respectively yield
\beta=2
, 1 and 3/2,
whereas they clearly obey (
8.3
) with
\omega=1
, 0 and 1/2.
This observation suggests the hyperscaling relation

\beta=\omega+1.

(8.4)

This relation provides a quantitative form of the rule of thumb
according to which the more the sites with gain and loss are well mixed along the chain,
the more robust the corresponding
{\cal PT}
-symmetric phase.
A large class of deterministic aperiodic sequences have non-trivial wandering exponents
\omega
,
besides the three classical values recalled above
[
33
,
34
,
35
,
36
]
.
The relation (
8.4
) certainly holds in those cases as well,
thus predicting a whole zoo of non-trivial asymptotic decay exponents
\beta
.

The chains yielding the most robust
{\cal PT}
-symmetric phase,
i.e., the highest threshold
\gamma_{c}
at fixed chain length, have been investigated
by means of a systematic enumeration up to
M=24
, i.e.,
N=48
(see Section
7
).
Both the optimal
\gamma_{c}
itself and the sequences responsible for it
exhibit an irregular dependence on
M
.
The highest threshold presumably falls off asymptotically as
(\ln M)/M
.
These logarithmic corrections somehow represent an exception to the above rule of thumb.
The chains yielding the optimal thresholds indeed contain several domain walls,
i.e., pairs of consecutive sites with gain or loss.
They are therefore slightly less well mixed than pristine alternating ones,
whereas the corresponding thresholds are larger.

To close, it would certainly be interesting
to extend at least some aspects of the present study to more complex situations,
including e.g. a periodic, quasiperiodic or random modulation
of on-site energies and/or hopping rates,
or quasi-one-dimensional geometries such as ladders,
exhibiting either internal band gaps or flat bands.

Appendix A
First-order perturbation theory

In this appendix we derive within the framework of first-order perturbation theory
the spectrum of the tight-binding equation

\psi_{n-1}+\psi_{n+1}+{\rm i}\gamma_{n}\psi_{n}=E\psi_{n},

(1.1)

with Dirichlet boundary conditions,
for an arbitrary sequence of purely imaginary optical potentials
\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{2M}
.
Our goal is to show that all energy levels remain real within this framework
if and only if the sequence
\gamma_{n}
is
{\cal PT}
-symmetric.

In the absence of optical potentials,
the non-degenerate energy eigenvalues and normalized eigenstates read

\displaystyle E_{a}=2\cos\frac{a\pi}{2M+1}\qquad(a=1,\dots,2M),

(1.2)

\displaystyle\psi_{a,n}=\sqrt{\frac{2}{2M+1}}\sin\frac{an\pi}{2M+1}.

(1.3)

To first order in the optical potentials,
the energy eigenvalues read
E_{a}+{\rm i}\Gamma_{a}
, with

\displaystyle\Gamma_{a}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{2M}\gamma_{n}\psi_{a,n}^{2}

(1.4)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2}{2M+1}\sum_{n=1}^{M}\left(\gamma_{n}+\gamma_{2M+1-n}\right)\,\sin^{2}\!\frac{an\pi}{2M+1}.

If the sequence
\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{2M}
is
{\cal PT}
-symmetric, obeying

\gamma_{2M+1-n}=-\gamma_{n},

(1.5)

all the sums in the parentheses in the last line of (
1.4
) vanish,
and therefore all the first-order imaginary parts
\Gamma_{a}
vanish,
suggesting the existence of a non-zero threshold
\gamma_{c}
for
{\cal PT}
-symmetry breaking.

The proof of the reciprocal proceeds as follows.
The first-order imaginary parts (
1.4
) obey the mirror symmetry
\Gamma_{2M+1-a}=\Gamma_{a}
,
so that it is sufficient to consider
a=1,\dots,M
.
Requesting
\Gamma_{a}=0
for
a=1,\dots,M
yields a system of
M
linear equations
for the
M
unknown quantities
(\gamma_{n}+\gamma_{2M+1-n})
for
n=1,\dots,M
.
The corresponding determinant,

D_{M}=\det A,

(1.6)

with

A_{an}=\sin^{2}\!\frac{an\pi}{2M+1}\qquad(a,n=1,\dots,M),

(1.7)

is non-zero.
It can indeed be evaluated by using
D_{M}^{2}=(\det A)^{2}=\det(A^{2})
.
This is useful since the entries of the matrix
A^{2}
have simple expressions
[
37
,
38
]
.
Expanding the sines into complex exponentials and performing the sums,
we indeed obtain after some algebra

(A^{2})_{ab}=\frac{2M+1}{8}\left(1+{\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}}\delta_{ab}\right).

(1.8)

The determinant of a matrix of size
M\times M
with entries
\lambda+\mu\delta_{ab}

reads
\mu^{M-1}(M\lambda+\mu)
.
We thus obtain

|D_{M}|=\frac{(2M+1)^{(M+1)/2}}{2^{2M}}.

(1.9)

The sign of
D_{M}
is not predicted by this approach.
It depends periodically on
M
mod 4.

To sum up,

\Gamma_{a}=0
for all
a
implies
\gamma_{n}+\gamma_{2M+1-n}=0
for all
n
,
i.e.,
{\cal PT}
-symmetry.
This completes the proof.
