1.
introduction

Let
k
be a field. Recall that the generalized Kronecker quiver
K(n)
is just the quiver with two vertices
1
,
2
, and
n
arrows
\gamma_{1},\cdots,\gamma_{n}:

1\rightarrow 2
. Let
\mathcal{K}_{n}=kK(n)
, and let
\rep_{k}(K(n))
denote the finite-dimensional representations of
K(n)
. If we use
\modd\mathcal{K}_{n}
to denote the category of finite-dimensional modules of
\mathcal{K}_{n}
, then there exists an equivalence between categories
\rep_{k}(K(n))
and
\modd\mathcal{K}_{n}
. We will frequently identify these two subcategories.

Since the representation type of
K(n)
is wild
[
4
, 1.3]
, it is hopeless to classify all the indecomposable modules. Hence it is prevalent to investigate the module category of Kronecker quiver concerning the invariants. If we let
(T(n),\Omega)
be the universal covering of
K(n)
, then one practical way is to find invariants for those modules of
K(n)
which can be lifted to its covering
(T(n),\Omega)
, where
\Omega
is a fixed bipartite orientation (see (2.2)). In fact,
T(n)
is an
n
-regular tree. In general, we can take
k
-modules of the quiver
T(n)
as graded Kronecker modules (cf.
[
7
]
). We write
\modd(T(n),\Omega)
to denote the category of finite-dimensional graded Kronecker modules (or simply modules). There exists a reflection (or shift) functor
\sigma:\mod(T(n),\Omega)\rightarrow\mod(T(n),\sigma\Omega)
at all sinks, where
\sigma\Omega
is the orientation under the action of
\sigma
. Similarly, we have reflection functor
\sigma^{\prime}
for
\modd\mathcal{K}_{n}
. Using the push-down functor
\pi_{\lambda}:\modd(T(n),\Omega)\rightarrow\modd\mathcal{K}_{n}

[
3
, 3.2]
, we show that
\pi_{\lambda}\circ\sigma(M)=\sigma^{\prime}\circ\pi_{\lambda}(M)
for all
M\in\modd(T(n),\Omega)
. Let
M\in\modd(T(n),\Omega)
be an indecomposable module. We say that
M
is regular, provided
\sigma^{t}(M)\neq 0
for all
t\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Let
M\in\modd(T(n),\Omega)
be a regular indecomposable module, and let
T(M)
be the minimal tree containing the set
\{y\in T(n)_{0}\mid M_{y}\neq 0\}
. In 2018, Claus Michael Ringel introduces three types of modules:
sink module, source module
and
flow module
for
T(M)
and presents two invariants:
the smallest possible radius
and the
center path
in the
\sigma
-orbit of module
M
(see (2.5) and Section
3
). Moreover, a module
Y\in\modd(T(n),\Omega)
is said to be a sink (source, or flow) module if all its indecomposable direct summands are sink (source, or flow) modules with the same center. In the end of the paper
[
7
]
, Ringel asks readers a question: for an AR-sequence
0\rightarrow X\rightarrow Y\rightarrow Z\rightarrow 0
consisting of regular modules, what the precise relationship is between the middle term
Y
and
\sigma Z
. Since we know a little about the reflection functor
\sigma
in his paper
[
7
]
, it is hard to answer that question. In this article, we follow his work and build a bigger
ball
(see (2.5)) that could let us see clearly the properties of reflection functor
\sigma
and finally give a solution to Ringel’s problem. In particular, we can write down the functor
\sigma
individually for every indecomposable module.

We show that the functor
\sigma
induces an equivalence between
k
-linear full subcategories of
\modd(T(n),\Omega)
and
\modd(T(n),\sigma\Omega)
with quasi-inverse
\sigma^{-}
. This conclusion guarantees the indecomposability of regular modules of the category
\modd(T(n),\Omega)
under the action of
\sigma
. Furthermore, it allows us to consider AR-sequences in the regular components and give the clues to solve Ringel’s problem directly.

In Section
3
, we focus on a regular indecomposable module
M
of mod
(T(n),\Omega)
. It is also well-known that the regular components (or AR-components) of
K(n)
are components of type
\mathbb{Z}A_{\infty}
(cf.
[
6
]
), and it is the same for the covering
T(n)
. Suppose that
\mathcal{D}
is a regular component of mod
(T(n),\Omega)
. Then for an AR-sequence
0\rightarrow X\rightarrow Y\rightarrow Z\rightarrow 0
in
\mathcal{D}
, the module
Y
is a sink (source, or flow) module if and only if
\sigma Z
is a sink (source, or flow) module.

(2.2) Graphs
A
graph
is a pair
G=(G_{0},G_{1}):

G_{0}
(whose elements called the vertices) and
G_{1}
(whose elements called the edges). If
\{a,b\}
is an edge, then
a,b
are called
neighbours
.

For a graph
G
, there exsits a map
\Omega
, called
orientation

:G\rightarrow G_{0}\times G_{0}
, such that
\Omega(\{a,a^{\prime}\})
is either (

) or (

). We call

an

. We write

if
\Omega(\{a,a^{\prime}\})=(a,a^{\prime})
and call

the
start
,

the
target
. Meanwhile we call

a
sink
(or a
source
) if it is not a start (or the target, respectively) of any arrow. If any vertex is a sink or a source, then the orientation
\Omega
will be called
bipartite
. A
subgraph
of a graph
G=(G_{0},G_{1})
is a graph
G^{\prime}=(G^{\prime}_{0},G^{\prime}_{1})
such that
G^{\prime}_{0}\subseteq G_{0},G^{\prime}_{1}\subseteq G_{1}
.

A

of length

in a graph

is a finite sequence
(a_{0},a_{1},\cdots,a_{t})
of vertices such that
a_{i-1},a_{i}
are neighbours for

, and
a_{j-1}\neq a_{j+1}
for

. If

, then
a_{r}
is called its
center
and
r
its
radius

[
7
, 2.1]
. If
t=2r+1
, then
\{a_{r},a_{r+1}\}
is called its
center
and
r
its
radius
. Suppose that for any pair
a,b
of vertices in
G
, there always exists a path connecting
a
and
b
. Then
G
is said to be
connected
. We call a path of length
t\geq 0
a
cycle
whenever its source and target coincide. A cycle of length
1
is said to be a
loop
. A graph
G
is called

if it contains no cycles, and it is called

if
G_{0}
and
G_{1}
are finite sets.

A graph

is said to be a

if it is connected and paths connecting a vertex with itself are always length

. Let

be a tree. Then we define

and

to

. If

(2.3) Quivers
A

Let

. For every point

) : rep

{}_{k}(Q^{\prime})

middle terms of AR-sequences of T(n)

In this section, we discuss the middle terms of AR-sequences in the regular components of
\modd(T(n),\Omega)
. Now let
M\in\modd(T(n),\Omega)
be a regular indecomposable module. Combining with Theorem
2.1
, and sticking to its notation and conventions in
[
7
]
, we define

(a)

\pi(M):=(a_{0},\cdots,a_{b})
the
center path
of
M
,

(b)

p(M):=a_{0}
,
q(M):=a_{b}
.

Note that
p(M)
is the center of all sink modules in the
\sigma
-orbit of
M
and
q(M)
is the center of all its source modules, respectively. If, furthermore,
b(M)
is the number of flow modules in the
\sigma
-orbit of
M
, then
b(M)=d(p(M),q(M))
. Recall that
i=\iota(M)
. By Theorem
2.1
, we can get the following corollary.

Corollary 3.1
.

Let
M\in\modd(T(n),\Omega)
be a regular indecomposable module. Then

(a)

M
is a sink module if and only if
i\leq 0
,

(b)

M
is a flow module if and only if
0<i\leq b(M)
,

(c)

M
is a source module if and only if
i>b(M)
.

Let
\mathcal{D}
be a regular component of
\modd(T(n),\Omega)
, and let
M,M^{\prime}\in

\mathcal{D}
be two regular indecomposable modules. Suppose that the
\sigma
-orbit of
M
(or
M^{\prime}
) contains
b
(or
b^{\prime}
) flow modules. According to
[
7
, Section 7]
, we have
p(M)=p(M^{\prime})
,
q(M)=q(M^{\prime})
and
b=b^{\prime}
. This implies that for the
\sigma
-orbits of
M
and
M^{\prime}
, the centers of all the sink modules or source modules are the same. We also have
r_{0}(M)-ql(M)=r_{0}(M^{\prime})-ql(M^{\prime})
, where
r_{0}(M)=r(M_{0})
. Then we define

p(\mathcal{D})=p(M)
,

q(\mathcal{D})=q(M)
,

b(\mathcal{D})=d(p(M),q(M))
,

r(\mathcal{D})=r_{0}(M)-ql(M)
.

We have
p(M)=p(\sigma^{t}M)=p(M^{\prime})=p(\sigma^{t^{\prime}}M^{\prime})
and
q(M)=q(\sigma^{t}M)=q(M^{\prime})=q(\sigma^{t^{\prime}}M^{\prime})
for any
t,t^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}
. That is,
\pi(\sigma^{t}M)=\pi(\sigma^{t^{\prime}}M^{\prime})
and
b(M)=b(\sigma^{t}M)=b(M^{\prime})=b(\sigma^{t^{\prime}}M^{\prime})=b
.

We say that a module
Y\in
mod
(T(n),\Omega)
is a
sink
(
source
, or
flow
) module if all its indecomposable direct summands are sink (source, or flow) modules with the same center. Suppose that
Y=\oplus_{i\in I}Y^{i}
, where
Y^{i}
is an indecomposable sink (source, or flow) module for all
i
, and
C(Y^{i})=C(Y^{j}),i,j\in I
. Then we define
r(Y)=\max\{r(Y^{i})\mid i\in I\},C(Y)=C(Y^{i})
. Let
0\rightarrow X\rightarrow Y\rightarrow Z\rightarrow 0
be an AR-sequence in a regular component
\mathcal{D}
. According to
[
6
, Lemma 2.2]
, there exist at most two indecomposable direct summands in the middle term
Y
. Now suppose that
Y=Y^{1}\oplus Y^{2}
, where
Y^{1},Y^{2}\in\modd(T(n),\Omega)
are indecomposable and
ql(Y^{1})<ql(Y^{2})
. Then we have
b(Y^{1})=b(Y^{2})
and
\iota(Y^{1})=\iota(Y^{2})
[Theorem
2.2
]. In addition,
C(Y^{1})=C(Y^{2})
and
Y^{1},Y^{2}
are of the same type modules [Theorem
2.3
]. Hence we get
C(Y)=C(Y^{2})
,
r(Y)=r(Y^{2})
. We define

\iota(Y):=\iota(Y^{2})
and
b(Y):=b(Y^{1})=b(Y^{2})
.

In the following, we focus on such middle term

and let
Y=Y^{2}
when

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
X,Y,Z

\in\modd(T(n),\Omega)
be regular modules. Assume that

0\rightarrow X\rightarrow Y\rightarrow Z\rightarrow 0

is an AR-sequence. Then
\iota(Y)=\iota(\sigma Z)
. Moreover,
Y
is a sink (flow, or source) module if and only if
\sigma Z
is a sink (flow, or source) module.

Proof.

We already know that
\iota(Y^{1})=\iota(Y^{2})
by Theorem
2.3
. We just need to prove
\iota(Y^{2})=\iota(\sigma Z)
. By Theorem
2.2
, we get
\iota(Z)=\iota(Y^{2})-1
. Then
\iota(\sigma Z)=\iota(Y^{2})
. This also tells us that
Y
and
\sigma Z
keep the same type [Corollary

].

Proposition 3.5
.

Let

be regular modules. Suppose that

is an AR-sequence with
T(X)_{0}\cap T(Z)_{0}=\varnothing
. Then
Y
is a flow module.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.2
, it is enough to prove that
\sigma Z
is a flow module.

(a)

Suppose that
\sigma Z
is a sink module with
C(\sigma Z)=x_{0}
, where
x_{0}\in T(\sigma Z)_{0}
. According to Proposition
2.4
(1)
,
Z
is a sink module and
C(Z)=x_{0}
. For
\sigma^{2}Z=X
, we have
C(X)=C(\sigma Z)=x_{0}
when
X
is a source module [Proposition
2.4
(3)
], and
C(X)\cap C(Z)=x_{0}
when
X
is a flow module [Proposition
2.4
(2)
]. Hence we always have
C(Z)\cap C(X)=C(\sigma Z)\cap C(\sigma^{2}Z)\neq\varnothing
in this case. This contradicts our assumption
T(X)_{0}\cap T(Z)_{0}=\varnothing
.

(b)

Suppose that
\sigma Z
is a source module with
C(\sigma Z)=y_{0}
,
y_{0}\in T(\sigma Z)_{0}
. Then
\sigma^{2}Z=X
is a source module and
C(X)=C(\sigma Z)
[Proposition
2.6
]. Module
Z
has 3 possibilites: sink, flow or source. By Proposition
2.4
,
2.5
,
2.6
, we know that
C(Z)=C(\sigma Z)=y_{0}
when
Z
is a sink or source module. When

is a flow module, we have
C(Z)\cap C(\sigma Z)=y_{0}
, where
C(Z)=\{y^{\prime}_{0},y_{0}\},y^{\prime}_{0}\in T(n)_{0}
. Since
T(X)_{0}\cap T(Z)_{0}=\varnothing
and
C(X)\cap C(Z)=C(\sigma Z)\cap C(Z)\neq\varnothing
, this case also cannot happen.

Together with
(a)
and
(b)
, we can see that
\sigma Z
is a flow module.
