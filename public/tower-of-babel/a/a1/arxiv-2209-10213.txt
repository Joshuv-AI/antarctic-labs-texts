Scaling limits for Rudvalis card shuffles
P. Gonçalves, M. Jara, R. Marinho, D. Moreira

Abstract
. We consider the Rudvalis card shuffle and some of its variations that were introduced by Diaconis and Saloff-Coste in [ 3 ] , and we project them to some stochastic interacting particle system. For the latter, we derive the hydrodynamic limits and we study the equilibrium fluctuations. Our results show that, for these shuffles, when we consider an asymmetric variation of the Rudvalis shuffle, the hydrodynamic limit in Eulerian scale is given in terms of a transport equation with a constant that depends on the exchange rates of the system; while for symmetric and weakly asymmetric variations of this shuffle, in diffusive time scale, the evolution is given by the solution of a martingale problem.



1.
Introduction

Fluid dynamics are commonly modelled by stochastic interacting particle systems. With these models it may be possible to derive partial differential equations as the limits, in some sense, of some observables of the system that evolve both in space and time. Those are called
scaling limits

[
10
]
.

One of the most famous stochastic interacting particle systems is the symmetric simple exclusion process (SSEP), where particles perform symmetric nearest-neighbor random walks on a graph with the exclusion rule, which forbids more than one particle at the same vertex. When the graph is the finite cycle
{\mdmathbb Z}_{n}={\mdmathbb Z}/n{\mdmathbb Z}
, for instance, the total mass (number of particles) is conserved and one can recover the heat equation on the torus
{\mdmathbb T}=[0,1)
as the limit, in probability, of the
empirical measure
, which is a measure on
{\mdmathbb T}
obtained by re-scaling time diffusively as
n^{2}
, re-scaling space by
n^{-1}
, and assigning mass
n^{-1}
to each particle. The limit of the empirical measure is known as the
hydrodynamic limit
and it can be seen as a Law of Large Numbers for the density of particles. A more challenging task is to prove a central limit theorem from which one can observe the equilibrium fluctuations of the process.

An interesting fact is that the SSEP can be obtained as a projection of a very famous card shuffle known as the
interchange process
. In order to see this projection, one just has to color some cards in black, the remaining ones in red, and to associate black cards with particles and red cards with empty sites. This projection and its scaling limits were used in
[
11
]
and in
[
12
]
to study the mixing times of both processes, i.e. the time at which the total variation distance between the law of the process and the stationary measure is smaller than some threshold
\varepsilon>0
, both in the interval and in the cycle. Inspired by this, in this work we color the cards of a deck with black and red colours, but we study the corresponding stochastic interacting particle system obtained when we perform a peculiar card shuffle, the
Rudvalis shuffle
. This card shuffle was introduced by Arunas Rudvalis in
[
2
]
and it can be explained as follows: remove the card on the top of a deck and insert it back, uniformly at random, either at the bottom or at the second position from the bottom (see Figure
1
).

Figure 1.
Insertion of the top card at position
n-1
or
n
.

Lower and upper bounds on the mixing time for the Rudvalis card shuffle were obtained in
[
13
]
and in
[
6
]
. Matching bounds are still unknown. In the case of the interchange process, the knowledge about the hydrodynamic limit and the fluctuations was crucial to determine the lower and upper bounds of the mixing time. Motivated by this, in this work we investigate the hydrodynamic behavior and the equilibrium fluctuations of the Rudvalis card shuffle and some of its variations. In
[
3
]
for instance, Diaconis and Saloff-Coste introduced a symmetric version of the Rudvalis shuffle where one inserts the top card at the bottom of the deck with probability
1/4
, or inserts the bottom card at the top of the deck with probability
1/4
, or transposes the cards at positions
1
and
2
with probability
1/4
, or nothing is done with probability
1/4
. In order to obtain distinct hydrodynamic behaviours for the associated stochastic interacting particle systems, we introduce a continuous-time random walk on the symmetric group from which not only we can get the symmetric and asymmetric variations of the Rudvalis card shuffle, but also a weakly asymmetric one. We prove that, in the Eulerian time scale, the hydrodynamics and the equilibrium fluctuations of the projection of the asymmetric Rudvalis shuffle are driven by a transport equation with periodic boundary conditions. Furthermore, we show that, in the diffusive time scale, hydrodynamics and fluctuations for the symmetric and weakly asymmetric variations are driven by a solution of a non-trivial martingale problem. In
[
4
]
, Goel introduced a variation of the Rudvalis card shuffle where one puts the top card uniformly at random in the bottom-
k
positions of the deck. For this reason, this card shuffle is known as the Top to bottom-
k
shuffle. For the top-to-bottom
k
-shuffle, our methodology yields the hydrodynamic limits when
k=o(n)
and the equilibrium fluctuations when
k=o(\sqrt{n})
.

This work is organized as follows: in Section
2
we introduce the continuous-time card shuffle and we map it to a stochastic interacting particle systems. Moreover, in that section we state our main results, and to do so, we introduce some notation and definitions as Sobolev spaces, the empirical measure, the density fluctuation field, some weak solutions of partial differential equations and some martingale problems. In Section
3
we prove the hydrodynamic limits in the Eulerian and diffusive time scales, and in Section
4
we obtain the scaling limit of the equilibrium fluctuations in both time scales.
In Section
5
, we prove the uniqueness of the solution of the martingale problem obtained in diffusive time scale.

2.
Notation and results

2.1.
The Generalized Rudvalis shuffle

Let
n\in\mdmathbb N
be a scaling parameter and fix four sequences of real numbers
\{a_{n}\}_{n},\{b_{n}\}_{n},\{c_{n}\}_{n},\{d_{n}\}_{n}\in[0,1]
converging, as
n\to\infty
, respectively, to
a,b,c,d
.
Let
S_{n}
denote the symmetric group.
S_{n}
must be understood as the set of all possible permutations of a deck with
n
cards. Given a permutation
\sigma\in S_{n}
, we denote the card at position
x
by
\sigma(x)
. Given
\sigma\in S_{n}
and
x<y
, if we denote
\sigma=(\sigma(1),\sigma(2),\cdots,\sigma(x),\cdots,\sigma(y),\cdots,\sigma(n))\in S_{n}
, we define
\sigma^{x\rightarrow y}
as the permutation obtained from
\sigma
after we remove the card at position
x
from the deck and insert it at position
y
, i.e.

\sigma^{x\rightarrow y}=(\sigma(1),\sigma(2),\cdots,\sigma(x-1),\sigma(x+1),\cdots,\sigma(y),\sigma(x),\sigma(y+1),\cdots,\sigma(n)),

while for
x>y
and
\sigma=(\sigma(1),\sigma(2),\cdots,\sigma(y),\cdots,\sigma(x),\cdots,\sigma(n))
we define

\sigma^{x\rightarrow y}=(\sigma(1),\sigma(2),\cdots,\sigma(y-1),\sigma(y),\sigma(x),\sigma(y+1),\cdots,\sigma(x-1),\sigma(y+1),\cdots,\sigma(n)).

We also define
\sigma^{x\leftrightarrow y}
as the permutation obtained after we transpose the cards at positions
x
and
y
, i.e.
for
\sigma=(\sigma(1),\sigma(2),\cdots,\sigma(x-1),\sigma(x),\sigma(x+1),\cdots,\sigma(y-1),\sigma(y),\sigma(y+1),\cdots,\sigma(n))
, we define

\sigma^{x\leftrightarrow y}=\sigma=(\sigma(1),\sigma(2),\cdots,\sigma(x-1),\sigma(y),\sigma(x+1),\cdots,\sigma(y-1),\sigma(x),\sigma(y+1),\cdots,\sigma(n)).

We consider the continuous-time Markov chain
\{\sigma_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
with state space
S_{n}
and generator
{\mathcalboondox L}_{n}^{\spadesuit}
given on
f:S_{n}\to{\mdmathbb R}
and
\sigma\in S_{n}
by

(2.1)

\displaystyle{\mathcalboondox L}_{n}^{\spadesuit}f(\sigma)=a_{n}\left(f(\sigma^{1\rightarrow n-1})-f(\sigma)\right)+b_{n}\left(f(\sigma^{1\rightarrow n})-f(\sigma)\right)+c_{n}\left(f(\sigma^{n\rightarrow 1})-f(\sigma)\right)+d_{n}\left(f(\sigma^{1\leftrightarrow 2})-f(\sigma)\right)

and we call
\{\sigma_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
the
generalized Rudvalis card shuffle
.

Remark 2.1
.

We observe that for
a_{n}=b_{n}=1/2
and
c_{n}=d_{n}=0
we obtain the original Rudvalis shuffle introduced in
[
2
]
. Similarly, when
a_{n}=0
and
b_{n}=c_{n}=d_{n}=1/4
we obtain the symmetric version of the Rudvalis shuffle introduced in
[
3
]
. In
[
13
]
it is also considered the case
a_{n}\in(0,1)
,
b_{n}=1-a_{n}
and
c_{n}=d_{n}=0
.

Our aim is to project the generalized Rudvalis shuffle to a stochastic interacting particle system, where the latter is defined as a projection of the colours of the cards. Let
{\mdmathbb Z}_{n}={\mdmathbb Z}/n{\mdmathbb Z}
and let us colour each card of the deck in either red or black. For each permutation
\sigma
and each
x,y\in{\mdmathbb Z}_{n}
, we define the colouring
\eta=\eta(\sigma)
by

\eta(x)=\begin{cases}1&,\text{ if the card }\sigma(x)\text{ is black},\\
0&,\text{ if the card }\sigma(x)\text{ is red}.\end{cases}

Let
{}_{n}=\{0,1\}^{{\mdmathbb Z}_{n}}
. For each
x,y\in{\mdmathbb Z}_{n}
define
\eta^{x\rightarrow y}=\eta(\sigma^{x\rightarrow y})
and
\eta^{x\leftrightarrow y}=\eta(\sigma^{x\leftrightarrow y})
. The projection defined above allows mapping black cards to particles and red cards to holes. From (
2.1
), we see that
\{\eta_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
has an infinitesimal generator given on
f:{}_{n}\to{\mdmathbb R}
and
\eta\in{}_{n}
by

\displaystyle{\mathcalboondox L}_{n}f(\eta)=a_{n}\left(f(\eta^{1\rightarrow n-1})-f(\eta)\right)+b_{n}\left(f(\eta^{1\rightarrow n})-f(\eta)\right)+c_{n}\left(f(\eta^{n\rightarrow 1})-f(\eta)\right)+d_{n}\left(f(\eta^{1\leftrightarrow 2})-f(\eta)\right).

We will consider the process speeded up in the time scale
tn^{\beta}
with
\beta>0
and the generator of
\{\eta_{tn^{\beta}}\}_{t\geq 0}
is simply given by
n^{\beta}{\mathcalboondox L}_{n}
. We will consider two cases, the hyperbolic time scale, corresponding to the choice
\beta=1
and the diffusive time scale, corresponding to the choice
\beta=2
.

2.2.
Stationary measures

We assume that
a_{n},b_{n},c_{n},d_{n}
are chosen in such a way that the processes
\{\sigma_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
and
\{\eta_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
are irreducible and in those cases, the uniform measure on
S_{n}
will be the stationary measure of the card shuffle
\{\sigma_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
. Therefore, if the initial number of particles
\ell
is fixed, then the uniform measure on the hyperplane
{}_{n,\ell}:=\{\eta\in{}_{n};\sum_{x\in{\mdmathbb Z}_{n}}\eta(x)=\ell\}
is the stationary measure of the particle system.

For
\varrho\in[0,1]
, let
\nu_{\varrho}
be the Bernoulli product measure with parameter
\varrho
, that is, for each
\eta\in{}_{n}
,

(2.2)

\displaystyle\nu_{\varrho}(\eta)=\prod_{x\in{\mdmathbb Z}_{n}}\left\{\varrho\eta(x)+(1-\varrho)(1-\eta(x))\right\}.

It is straightforward to check that
\nu_{\varrho}
is stationary for any
\varrho\in[0,1]
.
In order to define the space where our functionals will act, we need to introduce the so-called Sobolev spaces.

2.3.
Sobolev Spaces

Let
{\mdmathbb T}
be the unit torus. For
f,g:{\mdmathbb T}\to{\mdmathbb C}
, let
\left<f,g\right>
denote the inner product in
L^{2}({\mdmathbb T})
, which is given by

\left<f,g\right>=\int_{{\mdmathbb T}}f(u)\overline{g(u)}du.

For each
k\in{\mdmathbb Z}
let
{}_{k}:{\mdmathbb T}\to{\mdmathbb C}
be defined by

(2.3)

\displaystyle{}_{k}(u)=\begin{cases}\sqrt{2}\sin(2\pi ku)&,\text{ if }k<0,\\
1&,\text{ if }k=0,\\
\sqrt{2}\cos(2\pi ku)&,\text{ if }k>0.\end{cases}

for any
u\in{\mdmathbb T}
. The family
\{{}_{k}\}_{k\in{\mdmathbb Z}}
forms an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}({\mdmathbb T})
. Note that the functions
k
are eigenfunctions of the positive symmetric linear operator
{\mathcalboondox L}=(1-\Delta)
, i.e. for each
k\in{\mdmathbb Z}
,

{\mathcalboondox L}{}_{k}={}_{k}{}_{k},

with
{}_{k}=1+4\pi^{2}k^{2}
.
Now we are ready to define Sobolev spaces. For each positive integer
m
, we denote by
{\mathcalboondox H}_{m}
the Hilbert space obtained as the completion of
C^{\infty}({\mdmathbb T})
endowed with the inner product
\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle_{m}
defined by

\left\langle f,g\right\rangle_{m}=\left\langle f,{\mathcalboondox L}^{m}g\right\rangle.

It is easy to verify that
{\mathcalboondox H}_{m}
is the subspace of
L^{2}({\mdmathbb T})
consisting of all functions
f
such that

\sum_{k\in{\mdmathbb Z}}|\left\langle f,{}_{k}\right\rangle|^{2}{}_{k}^{m}<\infty.

For each positive integer
m
, we denote by
{\mathcalboondox H}_{-m}
the dual of
{\mathcalboondox H}_{m}
with respect to the inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
. It is easy to check that
{\mathcalboondox H}_{-m}
consists of all sequences
\{\left\langle f,{}_{k}\right\rangle\}_{k\in{\mdmathbb Z}}
such that

\sum_{k\in{\mdmathbb Z}}|\left\langle f,{}_{k}\right\rangle|^{2}{}_{k}^{-m}<\infty

and that the inner product
\left\langle f,g\right\rangle_{-m}
of two functions
f,g\in{\mathcalboondox H}_{-m}
can be written as

(2.4)

\left\langle f,g\right\rangle_{-m}=\sum_{k\in{\mdmathbb Z}}\left\langle f,{}_{k}\right>\left\langle g,{}_{k}\right>{}_{k}^{-m}.

If we denote
L^{2}({\mdmathbb T})
by
{\mathcalboondox H}_{0}
, then
from the explicit characterization of
{\mathcalboondox H}_{-m}
, we obtain the following inclusion of spaces

\cdots\subset{\mathcalboondox H}_{2}\subset{\mathcalboondox H}_{1}\subset{\mathcalboondox H}_{0}\subset{\mathcalboondox H}_{-1}\subset{\mathcalboondox H}_{-2}\subset\cdots

2.4.
Empirical measure

We note that the number of particles (or the numbers of black cards) is conserved by the dynamics of
\{\eta_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
. Our first question is related to understanding how does the distribution of particles evolve in space and time. To properly answer this question we need to define the empirical measure associated to the conserved quantity, i.e. the number of particles. To that end,
let
{\mdmathbb T}
be the unit torus and fix a time horizon
[0,T]
, with
T>0
. We denote by
{\mathcalboondox M}
the space of positive measures on
{\mdmathbb T}
with total mass bounded by one, endowed with the weak topology.

For each
t\in[0,T]
, let
\pi_{t}^{n,\beta}\in{\mathcalboondox M}
be the empirical measure on
{\mdmathbb T}
obtained by rescaling space by
n^{-1}
, and assigning mass
n^{-1}
to each particle (black card), that is,

\displaystyle\pi_{t}^{n,\beta}(\eta,du):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{x\in{\mdmathbb Z}_{n}}\eta_{tn^{\beta}}(x)\delta_{\tfrac{x}{n}}\,(du),

where
\delta_{u}
is the Dirac measure concentrated on
u\in\mdmathbb T
. For convenience of notation, we denote the integral of
f:{\mdmathbb T}\to\mdmathbb R
with respect to
\pi_{t}^{n,\beta}
as

\langle\pi_{t}^{n,\beta},f\rangle
.
Let
\{\mu_{n}\}_{n\in{\mdmathbb N}}
be a sequence of probability measures in
n
. For
n\in{\mdmathbb N}
, let
\mdmathbb{P}_{\mu_{n}}
be the probability measure on the Skorokhod space
{\mathcalboondox D}([0,T],{}_{n})
induced by
\{\eta_{tn^{\beta}}\}_{t\geq 0}
and the initial measure
\mu_{n}
and let
\mdmathbb{E}_{\mu_{n}}
be the expectation with respect to
\mdmathbb{P}_{\mu_{n}}
. Let
\{{\mdmathbb Q}_{n,\beta}\}_{n\in{\mdmathbb N}}
be the sequence of probability measures on
{\mathcalboondox D}([0,T],{\mathcalboondox M})
induced by
\{\pi_{t}^{n,\beta}\}_{t\geq 0}
and
\mdmathbb{P}_{\mu_{n}}
.

Definition 1
.

Let
\mathfrak{h}:\mdmathbb{R}\rightarrow[0,1]
be a measurable function and
\{\mu_{n}\}_{n\in\ bbN}
a sequence of probability measures in
n
. We say that
\{\mu_{n}\}_{n\in{\mdmathbb N}}
is associated with
\mathfrak{h}
, if for any continuous function
G:\mdmathbb T\rightarrow\mdmathbb R
and any
\delta>0
,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mu_{n}\Big(\eta\in{}_{n}:\Big|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{x\in{\mdmathbb Z}_{n}}G(\tfrac{x}{n})\eta(x)-\int_{\mdmathbb{R}}G(u)\mathfrak{h}(u)\,du\,\Big|>\delta\Big)=0.

2.5.
Hydrodynamic limit

In order to state our first result, we introduce the notion of weak solution of a transport equation on
{\mdmathbb T}
:

Definition 2
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}:{\mdmathbb T}\to[0,1]
be a measurable function. Let
\theta\in{\mdmathbb R}
. We say that
\varrho^{\theta}:[0,T]\times{\mdmathbb T}\to[0,1]
is a weak solution of the transport equation

(2.5)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}\varrho^{\theta}(t,u)=\theta\nabla\varrho^{\theta}(t,u)&,\forall u\in{\mdmathbb T};\\
\varrho(0,u)=\mathfrak{g}(u)&,\forall u\in{\mdmathbb T}.\end{cases}

if for all
f\in C^{1}({\mdmathbb T})
and all
t\in[0,T]
we have

0=\left\langle\varrho^{\theta}_{t}-\mathfrak{g},f\right\rangle+\theta\int_{0}^{t}\langle\varrho_{s}^{\theta},\nabla f\rangle\,ds.

Equation (
2.5
) has a unique weak solution given by
\varrho_{t}^{\theta}(u)=\varrho_{0}^{\theta}(u+\theta t)
.
Now we state the
hydrodynamic limit
in the hyperbolic time scale (
\beta=1)
:

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
\beta=1
and
\varrho_{0}:{\mdmathbb T}\to[0,1]
be a measurable function. Let
\{\mu_{n}\}_{n\ \geq 1}
be a sequence of probability measures in
n
associated to the profile
\varrho_{0}
.
Assume that

(2.6)

\lim_{n\to+\infty}|a_{n}+b_{n}-c_{n}|=\kappa:=a+b-c\neq 0.

Then, for any
t\in[0,T]
, the sequence
\{{{\mdmathbb Q}}_{n,1}\}_{n\in{\mdmathbb N}}
converges, as
n\to+\infty
, with respect to the weak topology of
{\mathcalboondox D}([0,T],{\mathcalboondox H}_{-m})
, for
m>3/2
, to
{{\mdmathbb Q}}_{1}
, which is a Dirac measure on the trajectory
\pi^{1}_{t}(du)=\varrho^{\kappa}(t,u)du
, where
\varrho^{\kappa}
is the unique weak solution of (
2.5
) with
\theta=\kappa
.

We observe that since the limiting equation is a transport equation, we could perform a Galilean transformation at the level of the empirical measure and, in the hyperbolic time scale, we would have a trivial evolution (which also happens if
\kappa=0
). In that sense, we need to speed up the time scale in order to see a non-trivial limit. In fact, the trivial behavior goes up to
\beta<2
. Therefore, we consider now the diffusive time scale corresponding to
\beta=2
. In this regime the limit is not longer deterministic since the contribution of the martingale term is relevant and brings an additional term to the equation. Let us now define properly the notion of solution to the equation that we obtain in this regime.

Definition 3
.

Let
\nu,\sigma>0
and
{\mathcalboondox v}\in\mdmathbb R
. Fix
m>0
. We say that a process
\{X_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
with continuous trajectories in
{\mathcalboondox H}_{-m}
is a solution of the
martingale problem
associated to

(2.7)

\partial_{t}X_{t}=\nu\Delta X_{t}+{\mathcalboondox v}\nabla X_{t}+\sigma\nabla X_{t}d{\mathcal{B}}_{t},

where
\{{\mathcal{B}}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
is a standard Brownian motion,
if for every
f\in C^{\infty}({\mdmathbb T})
the process
\{{\mathcalboondox M}_{t}(f)\}_{t\geq 0}
given by

{\mathcalboondox M}_{t}(f):=X_{t}(f)-X_{0}(f)-\int_{0}^{t}X_{s}(\nu\Delta f-{\mathcalboondox v}\nabla f)ds

for every
t\geq 0
, is a martingale with respect to the filtration
\mathcal{F}_{t}:=\sigma\{X_{s}(g);\,\,s\leq t\text{ and }g\in C^{\infty}({\mdmathbb T})\}
whose quadratic variation is given by

(2.8)

\langle{\mathcalboondox M}(f)\rangle_{t}:=\int_{0}^{t}\sigma^{2}X_{s}(\nabla f)^{2}ds.

Since our proofs combine tightness and the unique characterization of the limit point, the next result is crucial in what follows. We did not find in the literature the proof of this result, therefore we present it in Section
5
.

Theorem 2.3
.

Any two solutions of the martingale problem associated to (
2.7
) starting from the same initial condition have the same law.

Now we state our second result.

Theorem 2.4
.

Let
\beta=2
and
\varrho_{0}:{\mdmathbb T}\to[0,1]
be a measurable function. Let
\{\mu_{n}\}_{n\in{\mdmathbb N}}
be a sequence of probability measures in

associated to the profile

.
Assume that
c>0
and that

. Then, the sequence

converges, as

) for

Now that we have established the hydrodynamic limit, which is a Law of Large Numbers for the empirical measure, we can analyse the fluctuations around that limit. That is the purpose of the next section, though we consider the process starting from the invariant state
\nu_{\varrho}
, since the non-equilibrium scenario is much more complicated and the equilibrium case already gives interesting results.

2.6.
Equilibrium density fluctuations

In the last subsection we proved the hydrodynamic limit in two different time scales: in the hyperbolic time scale the limit is deterministic, but in the diffusive time scale, the limit is random. Now we analyse the
corresponding central limit theorems starting from the stationary measure
\nu_{\varrho}
. To that end, fix
\varrho\in[0,1]
. We define the

on functions

\displaystyle{\mathcalboondox Y}_{t}^{n,\beta}(f)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{x\in{\mdmathbb Z}_{n}}\left(\eta_{tn^{\beta}}(x)-\varrho\right)f\left(\tfrac{x}{n}\right).

Now, we denote by
\widetilde{{\mdmathbb Q}}_{n,\beta}
the probability measures on the Skorohod space
{\mathcalboondox D}([0,T],{\mathcalboondox H}_{-m})

{\mdmathbb P}_{\nu_{\varrho}}
and the fluctuation field
\{{\mathcalboondox Y}_{t}^{n,\beta}\}_{t\geq 0}
. Let also
{\mathcalboondox C}([0,T],{\mathcalboondox H}_{-m})
be the space of continuous trajectories with values in
{\mathcalboondox H}_{-m}.
Recall the value of
\kappa
given in (

).

.

Let

. Assume that

, with

.

, let

3.
Hydrodynamic limits

In this section we prove Theorems
2.2
and
2.4
. Recall the definition of
{{\mdmathbb Q}}_{n,\beta}
given above Definition
1
. We first show that
\{{{\mdmathbb Q}}_{n,\beta}\}_{n\in{\mdmathbb N}}
is tight with respect to the weak topology of
{\mathcalboondox D}([0,T],{\mathcalboondox H}_{-m})
for any
m>3/2
when
\beta=1
and
m>5/2
when
\beta=2
. Then we characterize the limit points.

3.1.
Tightness

In order to prove tightness, we follow
[
5
]
and we invoke the following criterion.

Lemma 3.1
.

[
10
, Chapter 11, Lemma 3.2]

The sequence of probability measures
\{{\mdmathbb Q}_{n,\beta}\}_{n\in{\mdmathbb N}}
on
{\mathcalboondox D}([0,T],{\mathcalboondox H}_{-m})
is tight if

(1)

\lim_{A\to\infty}\limsup_{n\to\infty}{{\mdmathbb Q}}_{n,\beta}\left(\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|\pi_{t}^{n,\beta}\|_{-m}>A\right)=0
;

(2)

\lim_{\delta\to 0}\limsup_{n\to\infty}{{\mdmathbb Q}}_{n,\beta}\left(\sup_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle|s-t|\leq\delta}}{{0\leq s,t\leq T}}}\|\pi_{t}^{n,\beta}-\pi_{s}^{n,\beta}\|_{-m}\geq\varepsilon\right)=0
for every
\varepsilon>0
.

In order to prove the validity of (1) and (2) above, we use the martingale characterization of the empirical measure. We can recast (1) and (2) in terms of
\mdmathbb P_{\mu_{n}}
instead of
{{\mdmathbb Q}}_{n,\beta}
.
From Dynkin’s formula (see for instance
[
10
, Appendix 1, Lemma 5.1]
), for any
f\in C^{\infty}({\mdmathbb T})
,

(3.1)

\displaystyle{\mathcalboondox M}_{t}^{n,\beta}(f):=\langle\pi_{t}^{n,\beta},f\rangle-\langle\pi_{0}^{n,\beta},f\rangle-\int_{0}^{t}(\partial_{s}+n^{\beta}{\mathcalboondox L}_{n})\langle\pi_{s}^{n,\beta},f\rangle ds

(3.2)

\displaystyle{\mathcalboondox N}_{t}^{n,\beta}(f):=({\mathcalboondox M}_{t}^{n,\beta}(f))^{2}-\int_{0}^{t}n^{\beta}{\mathcalboondox L}_{n}(\langle\pi_{s}^{n,\beta},f\rangle)^{2}-2\langle\pi_{s}^{n,\beta},f\rangle{\mathcalboondox L}_{n}\langle\pi_{s}^{n,\beta},f\rangle ds

are mean-zero martingales with respect to the natural filtration of the process
\{\eta_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
.
A simple computation shows that

(3.3)

\begin{split}&{\mathcalboondox L}_{n}\eta(1)=(a_{n}+b_{n}+d_{n})(\eta(2)-\eta(1))+c_{n}(\eta(n)-\eta(1))\\
&{\mathcalboondox L}_{n}\eta(2)=(a_{n}+b_{n})(\eta(3)-\eta(2))+(c_{n}+d_{n})(\eta(1)-\eta(2))\\
&{\mathcalboondox L}_{n}\eta(n-1)=a_{n}(\eta(1)-\eta(n-1))++b_{n}(\eta(n)-\eta(n-1))+c_{n}(\eta(n-2)-\eta(n-1))\\
&{\mathcalboondox L}_{n}\eta(n)=b_{n}(\eta(1)-\eta(n))+c_{n}(\eta(n-1)-\eta(n))\end{split}

and
{\mathcalboondox L}_{n}\eta(x)=(a_{n}+b_{n})(\eta(x+1)-\eta(x))+c_{n}(\eta(x-1)-\eta(x))
for all
x\in\{3,4,\cdots,n-2\}
.
After long, but elementary computations, we get,

(3.4)

\begin{split}n^{\beta}{\mathcalboondox L}_{n}\langle\pi_{s}^{n,\beta},f\rangle&=-(a_{n}+b_{n})n^{\beta-2}\sum_{x\in{\mdmathbb Z}_{n}}\eta_{sn^{\beta}}(x)\nabla_{n}^{-}f(\tfrac{x}{n})+c_{n}n^{\beta-2}\sum_{x\in{\mdmathbb Z}_{n}}\eta_{sn^{\beta}}(x)\nabla_{n}^{+}f(\tfrac{x}{n})\\
&-a_{n}n^{\beta-2}\left(\eta_{sn^{\beta}}(1)-\eta_{sn^{\beta}}(n)\right)\nabla_{n}^{-}f\left(0\right)+d_{n}n^{\beta-2}\left(\eta_{sn^{\beta}}(1)-\eta_{sn^{\beta}}(2)\right)\nabla_{n}^{+}f(\tfrac{1}{n}).\end{split}

Above,
\nabla_{n}^{-}
,
\nabla_{n}^{+}
are discrete operators defined on
f:{\mdmathbb T}\to{\mdmathbb R}
and
x\in{\mdmathbb Z}_{n}
by

\nabla_{n}^{-}f(\tfrac{x}{n})=n(f(\tfrac{x}{n})-f(\tfrac{x-1}{n}))\quad\textrm{and}\quad\nabla_{n}^{+}f(\tfrac{x}{n})=\nabla_{n}^{-}f(\tfrac{x+1}{n}).

The quadratic variation of the martingale given in (
3.1
) is equal to

(3.5)

\displaystyle\left\langle{\mathcalboondox M}^{n,\beta}(f)\right\rangle_{t}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}(a_{n}+b_{n})n^{\beta-2}\left(\pi_{sn^{\beta}}^{n}(\nabla_{n}^{-}f)\right)^{2}ds+\int_{0}^{t}c_{n}n^{\beta-2}\left(\pi_{sn^{\beta}}^{n}(\nabla_{n}^{+}f)\right)^{2}ds

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}2a_{n}n^{\beta-3}(\eta_{sn^{\beta}}(1)-\eta_{sn^{\beta}}(n))\nabla_{n}^{-}f(0)\pi_{sn^{\beta}}^{n}(\nabla_{n}^{-}f)\,ds

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}a_{n}n^{\beta-4}(\eta_{sn^{\beta}}(1)-\eta_{sn^{\beta}}(n))^{2}\left(\nabla_{n}^{-}f(0)\right)^{2}ds

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}d_{n}n^{\beta-4}(\eta_{sn^{\beta}}(1)-\eta_{sn^{\beta}}(2))^{2}(\nabla_{n}^{+}f(\tfrac{1}{n}))^{2}ds.

Now we prove the validity of (1). Recall (
2.3
) and (
2.4
). From Markov’s inequality,

(3.6)

\displaystyle{\mdmathbb P}_{\mu_{n}}\Big(\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|\pi_{t}^{n,\beta}\|_{-m}>A\Big)\leq\frac{1}{A^{2}}\sum_{k\in{\mdmathbb Z}}{}_{k}^{-m}{\mdmathbb E}_{\mu_{n}}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\Big|\left\langle\pi_{t}^{n,\beta},{}_{k}\right\rangle\Big|^{2}\right].

To estimate last identity we analyse (
3.1
) for each time scale. Note that for
\beta=1
and from a Taylor’s expansion, we get

(3.7)

\displaystyle\langle\pi_{t}^{n,1},{}_{k}\rangle

\displaystyle={\mathcalboondox M}_{t}^{n,1}({}_{k})+\langle\pi_{0}^{n,1},{}_{k}\rangle-(a_{n}+b_{n}-c_{n})\int_{0}^{t}\langle\pi_{s}^{n,1},\nabla{}_{k}\rangle ds+{\mathcalboondox O}(n^{-1}),

while for
\beta=2
, we get

(3.8)

\displaystyle\langle\pi_{t}^{n,2},{}_{k}\rangle

\displaystyle={\mathcalboondox M}_{t}^{n,2}({}_{k})+\langle\pi_{0}^{n,2},{}_{k}\rangle+c_{n}\int_{0}^{t}\langle\pi_{s}^{n,2},\Delta_{n}{}_{k}\rangle ds+\left(c_{n}-(a_{n}+b_{n})\right)\int_{0}^{t}\langle\pi_{s}^{n,2},\nabla_{n}^{-}{}_{k}\rangle ds

\displaystyle-a_{n}\int_{0}^{t}(\eta_{sn^{2}}(1)-\eta_{sn^{2}}(n))\nabla_{n}^{-}{}_{k}(0)ds+d_{n}\int_{0}^{t}(\eta_{sn^{2}}(1)-\eta_{sn^{2}}(2))\nabla_{n}^{+}{}_{k}(\tfrac{1}{n})ds.

Above the discrete Laplacian operator is defined on
f:{\mdmathbb T}\to{\mdmathbb R}
and
x\in{\mdmathbb Z}_{n}
by

\Delta_{n}f(\tfrac{x}{n})=n^{2}(f(\tfrac{x+1}{n})+f(\tfrac{x-1}{n})-2f(\tfrac{x}{n})).

By the convexity inequality
|\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}x_{k}|^{2}\leq\ell\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}|x_{k}|^{2}
for
\ell\in{\mdmathbb N}
, we are left to estimate the contribution of each term in the previous identities (
3.7
) and (
3.8
). We note that the initial term can be estimated by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, as

(3.9)

\displaystyle{\mdmathbb E}_{\mu_{n}}\Bigg[|\langle\pi_{0}^{n,\beta},{}_{k}\rangle|^{2}\Bigg]\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{x\in{\mdmathbb Z}_{n}}|{}_{k}(\tfrac{x}{n})|^{2}.

Now we split the proof and we start with the hyperbolic case.

From Doob’s inequality, (
3.5
) for
\beta=1
and noting that
\eta(x)\leq 1
for all
x\in\mdmathbb Z_{n}
, we get

(3.10)

\displaystyle{\mdmathbb E}_{\mu_{n}}

\displaystyle\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|{\mathcalboondox M}_{t}^{n,1}({}_{k})|^{2}\right]\leq 4{\mdmathbb E}_{\mu_{n}}\Bigg[|{\mathcalboondox M}_{T}^{n,1}({}_{k})|^{2}\Bigg]\lesssim n^{-1}.

Now, it remains to estimate the third term on the right hand-side of (
3.7
).
By the definition of
k
and using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality twice, we get

(3.11)

\displaystyle{\mdmathbb E}_{\mu_{n}}\Bigg[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\Big|\int_{0}^{t}\langle\pi_{s}^{n,1},\nabla{}_{k}\rangle ds\Big|^{2}\Bigg]\leq T\,{\mdmathbb E}_{\mu_{n}}\Bigg[\int_{0}^{T}\Big|\langle\pi_{s}^{n,1},\nabla{}_{k}\rangle\Big|^{2}ds\Bigg]\leq\frac{T^{2}}{n}\sum_{x\in{\mdmathbb Z}_{n}}|\nabla{}_{k}(\tfrac{x}{n})|^{2}.

From the previous results and since
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{x\in{\mdmathbb Z}_{n}}|f(\tfrac{x}{n})|^{2}
converges to
\left\langle f,f\right\rangle
for any
f\in C^{\infty}({\mdmathbb T})
, we have

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}{\mdmathbb P}_{\mu_{n}}\left(\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|\pi_{t}^{n,1}\|_{-m}>A\right)

\displaystyle\lesssim{(T^{2}+1)}\,{A^{2}}\sum_{k\in{\mdmathbb Z}}{}_{k}^{-m}\Big\{\left\langle{}_{k},{}_{k}\right\rangle+\left\langle\nabla{}_{k},\nabla{}_{k}\right\rangle\Big\}

\displaystyle\lesssim{(T^{2}+1)}\,{A^{2}}\Big(1+2\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\Big\{1+4\pi^{2}k^{2}\Big\}^{1-m}\Big).

Therefore, (1) follows from the fact that the series above converges for
m>3/2
.

Now we focus on the case
\beta=2
.
From Doob’s inequality, (
3.5
) for
\beta=2
, noting that
\eta(x)\leq 1
for all
x\in\mdmathbb Z_{n}
, and by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we get

\displaystyle{\mdmathbb E}_{\mu_{n}}\Bigg[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|{\mathcalboondox M}_{t}^{n,2}({}_{k})|^{2}\Bigg]

\displaystyle\leq 4{\mdmathbb E}_{\mu_{n}}\Bigg[|{\mathcalboondox M}_{T}^{n,2}({}_{k})|^{2}\Bigg]

\displaystyle=4\int_{0}^{T}(a_{n}+b_{n}){\mdmathbb E}_{\mu_{n}}\Bigg[\langle\pi_{s}^{n,2},\nabla_{n}^{-}{}_{k}\rangle^{2}\Bigg]ds+4\int_{0}^{t}c_{n}{\mdmathbb E}_{\mu_{n}}\Bigg[\langle\pi_{s}^{n,2},\nabla_{n}^{+}{}_{k}\rangle^{2}\Bigg]ds+{\mathcalboondox O}(n^{-1})

(3.12)

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{(a_{n}+b_{n})T}{n}\sum_{x\in{\mdmathbb Z}_{n}}|\nabla_{n}^{-}{}_{k}(\tfrac{x}{n})|^{2}+\frac{c_{n}T}{n}\sum_{x\in{\mdmathbb Z}_{n}}|\nabla_{n}^{+}{}_{k}(\tfrac{x}{n})|^{2}.

With the same arguments as used in (
3.11
) we see that

(3.13)

\displaystyle{\mdmathbb E}_{\mu_{n}}\Bigg[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\Big|\int_{0}^{t}\langle\pi_{s}^{n,2},\Delta_{n}{}_{k}\rangle ds\Big|^{2}\Bigg]\leq\frac{T^{2}}{n}\sum_{x\in{\mdmathbb Z}_{n}}|\Delta_{n}{}_{k}(\tfrac{x}{n})|^{2},

(3.14)

\displaystyle{\mdmathbb E}_{\mu_{n}}\Bigg[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\Big|\int_{0}^{t}\langle\pi_{s}^{n,2},\nabla_{n}^{-}{}_{k}\rangle ds\Big|^{2}\Bigg]\leq\frac{T^{2}}{n}\sum_{x\in{\mdmathbb Z}_{n}}|\nabla_{n}^{-}{}_{k}(\tfrac{x}{n})|^{2}.

Finally, by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality we have

(3.15)

\displaystyle{\mdmathbb E}_{\mu_{n}}\Bigg[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\Big|a_{n}\int_{0}^{t}\left(\eta_{sn^{2}}(1)-\eta_{sn^{2}}(n)\right)\nabla_{n}^{-}{}_{k}\left(0\right)ds\Big|^{2}\Bigg]\leq|a_{n}|^{2}T|\nabla_{n}^{-}{}_{k}(0)|^{2},

(3.16)

\displaystyle{\mdmathbb E}_{\mu_{n}}\Bigg[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\Big|d_{n}\int_{0}^{t}\left(\eta_{sn^{2}}(1)-\eta_{sn^{2}}(2)\right)\nabla_{n}^{+}{}_{k}\left(\tfrac{1}{n}\right)ds\Big|^{2}\Bigg]\leq|d_{n}|^{2}T\left|\nabla_{n}^{+}{}_{k}(\tfrac{1}{n})\right|^{2}.

By (
3.9
), (
3.12
), (
3.13
), (
3.14
), (
3.15
) and (
3.16
), we get

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}

\displaystyle\,\,{\mdmathbb P}_{\mu_{n}}\Bigg(\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|\pi_{tn^{2}}^{n}\|_{-m}>A\Bigg)\leq\frac{1}{A^{2}}\sum_{k\in{\mdmathbb Z}}{}_{k}^{-m}\limsup_{n\to\infty}{\mdmathbb E}_{\mu_{n}}\Bigg[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|\pi_{tn^{2}}^{n}({}_{k})|^{2}\Bigg]

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{A^{2}}\sum_{k\in{\mdmathbb Z}}{}_{k}^{-m}\Big\{\left\langle{}_{k},{}_{k}\right\rangle+\left\langle\nabla{}_{k},\nabla{}_{k}\right\rangle+\left\langle\Delta{}_{k},\Delta{}_{k}\right\rangle\Big\}+\frac{(a^{2}+d^{2})T}{A^{2}}\sum_{k\in{\mdmathbb Z}}{}_{k}^{-m}|\nabla{}_{k}(0)|^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{A^{2}}+\frac{1}{A^{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\Big\{1+4\pi^{2}k^{2}+(2\pi k)^{4}\Big\}\left\{1+(2\pi k)^{2}\right\}^{-m}+\frac{\pi^{2}(a^{2}+d^{2})T}{A^{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}k^{2}\left\{1+\pi^{2}k^{2}\right\}^{-m}.

Observe that the series above converge if
m>5/2
. This concludes the proof of (1).

Now we prove (2). Observe that, arguing similarly, one can show that if
m>3/2
for
\beta=1
or
m>5/2
for
\beta=2
, then

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mdmathbb E_{\mu_{n}}\Bigg[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\,\,\sum_{|k|\geq N}|\langle\pi_{t}^{n,\beta},{}_{k}\rangle|^{2}{}_{k}^{-m}\Bigg]=0.

Thus, (2) follows if for any integer
N
and every
\varepsilon>0
,

\lim_{\delta\to 0}\limsup_{n\to\infty}{\mdmathbb P}_{\mu_{n}}\Bigg[\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}|s-t|\leq\delta\\
0\leq s,t\leq T\end{subarray}}\,\,\sum_{|k|\leq N}|\langle\pi_{t}^{n,\beta},{}_{k}\rangle-\langle\pi_{s}^{n,\beta},{}_{k}\rangle|^{2}{}_{k}^{-m}\geq\varepsilon\Bigg]=0.

Observe also that to prove the above limit it is sufficient to show that

(3.17)

\lim_{\delta\to 0}\limsup_{n\to\infty}{\mdmathbb P}_{\mu_{n}}\Bigg[\sup_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle|s-t|\leq\delta}}{{0\leq s,t\leq T}}}|\langle\pi_{t}^{n,\beta},{}_{k}\rangle-\langle\pi_{s}^{n,\beta},{}_{k}\rangle|^{2}\geq\varepsilon\Bigg]=0

for every
k\in{\mdmathbb Z}
. Now we split the proof and we start with the hyperbolic regime.
Recall (
3.5
) for
\beta=1
. Then the previous limit is a consequence of the next two results

\begin{split}&(A)\quad\quad\lim_{\delta\to 0}\limsup_{n\to\infty}{\mdmathbb P}_{\mu_{n}}\Bigg[\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}|s-t|\leq\delta\\
0\leq s,t\leq T\end{subarray}}\left|\int_{s}^{t}\langle\pi_{r}^{n,1},\nabla{}_{k}\rangle dr\right|>\varepsilon\Bigg]=0.\\
&(B)\quad\quad\lim_{\delta\to 0}\limsup_{n\to\infty}{\mdmathbb P}_{\mu_{n}}\Bigg[\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}|s-t|\leq\delta\\
0\leq s,t\leq T\end{subarray}}|{\mathcalboondox M}_{t}^{n,1}({}_{k})-{\mathcalboondox M}_{s}^{n,1}({}_{k})|>\varepsilon\Bigg]=0.\end{split}

We start by proving (A). Note that from Markov and Cauchy-Schwarz inequalities, and since
|t-s|\leq\delta
and
s,t\leq T
, we get

\displaystyle{\mdmathbb P}_{\mu_{n}}\Bigg[\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}|s-t|\leq\delta\\
0\leq s,t\leq T\end{subarray}}\Big|\int_{s}^{t}\langle\pi_{r}^{n,1},\nabla{}_{k}\rangle dr\Bigg|>\varepsilon\Big]\leq\frac{\delta T}{\varepsilon^{2}}{\mdmathbb E}_{\mu_{n}}\Bigg[\int_{0}^{T}|\langle\pi_{r}^{n,1},\nabla{}_{k}\rangle|^{2}dr\Bigg]\leq\frac{\delta T^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}n}\sum_{x\in{\mdmathbb Z}_{n}}|\nabla{}_{k}(\tfrac{x}{n})|^{2}.

Now we prove (B).
For
\beta>0
, define

(3.18)

\displaystyle\omega_{\delta}({\mathcalboondox M}^{n,\beta}(f))=\inf_{t_{i}}\max_{i\in[0,r)}\sup_{t_{i}\leq s<t<t_{i+1}}|{\mathcalboondox M}_{t}^{n,\beta}(f)-{\mathcalboondox M}_{s}^{n,\beta}(f)|,

where the first infimum is taken over all partitions of
[0,T]
such that

0=t_{0}<t_{1}<\cdots<t_{r}=T
and
t_{i+1}-t_{i}>\delta
for
0\leq i<r.

Since

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\Big|{\mathcalboondox M}_{t}^{n,\beta}(f)-{\mathcalboondox M}_{t-}^{n,\beta}(f)\Big|=\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\Big|\langle\pi_{t}^{n,\beta},{}_{k}\rangle-\langle\pi_{t^{-}}^{n,\beta},{}_{k}\rangle\Big|\leq\frac{2}{n}\|\nabla{}_{k}\|_{\infty}

and

\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}|s-t|\leq\delta\\
0\leq s,t\leq T\end{subarray}}\|{\mathcalboondox M}_{t}^{n,\beta}({}_{k})-{\mathcalboondox M}_{s}^{n,\beta}({}_{k})\|_{-m}\leq 2\omega_{\delta}({\mathcalboondox M}^{n,\beta}({}_{k}))+\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\Big|{\mathcalboondox M}_{t}^{n,\beta}({}_{k})-{\mathcalboondox M}_{t-}^{n,1}({}_{k})\Big|,

the proof ends (for
\beta=1
) if we show that

\lim_{\delta\to 0}\limsup_{n\to\infty}{\mdmathbb P}_{\mu_{n}}\Bigg[\omega_{\delta}({\mathcalboondox M}^{n,1}({}_{k}))>\varepsilon\Bigg]=0

for every
\varepsilon>0
. By
[
10
, Chapter 4, Proposition 1.6]
, it is enough to check that for every
\varepsilon>0

\lim_{\delta\to 0}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\sup_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\tau\in\mathcal{T}_{T}}}{{\theta\in[0,\delta]}}}{\mdmathbb P}_{\mu_{n}}\Bigg[\Big|{\mathcalboondox M}_{(\tau+\theta)\wedge T}^{n,1}({}_{k})-{\mathcalboondox M}_{\tau}^{n,1}({}_{k})\Big|>\varepsilon\Bigg]=0,

where
\mathcal{T}_{T}
stands for all stopping times bounded from above by
T
. From Markov’s inequality, since
{\mathcalboondox M}_{t}^{n,1}({}_{k})
is a martingale and since
\tau
is a bounded stopping time,

\displaystyle{\mdmathbb P}_{\mu_{n}}\Bigg[\Big|{\mathcalboondox M}_{\tau+\theta}^{n,1}(f)-{\mathcalboondox M}_{\tau}^{n,1}f)\Big|>\varepsilon\Bigg]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}{\mdmathbb E}_{\mu_{n}}\Bigg[\left\langle{\mathcalboondox M}^{n,1}(f)\right\rangle_{\tau+\theta}-\left\langle{\mathcalboondox M}^{n,1}(f)\right\rangle_{\tau}\Bigg]\lesssim Cn^{-1}.

And this ends the proof for the case
\beta=1
. For
\beta=2
, recall (
3.5
).
Then (
3.17
)
is a consequence of (A) (which also holds for
\beta=2
) and to prove (B), it is enough to note that

\displaystyle{\mdmathbb P}_{\mu_{n}}

\displaystyle\Bigg[\Big|{\mathcalboondox M}_{\tau+\theta}^{n,2}(f)-{\mathcalboondox M}_{\tau}^{n,2}(f)\Big|>\varepsilon\Bigg]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}{\mdmathbb E}_{\mu_{n}}\Bigg[\int_{\tau}^{\tau+\theta}\!\!\!\!(a_{n}+b_{n})(\langle\pi_{s}^{n,2},\nabla_{n}^{-}{}_{k}\rangle)^{2}ds+\int_{\tau}^{\tau+\theta}c_{n}(\langle\pi_{s}^{n,2},\nabla_{n}^{+}f\rangle)^{2}ds\Bigg]+{\mathcalboondox O}(n^{-1})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\delta}{\varepsilon^{2}}\Big[(a_{n}+b_{n})\|\nabla_{n}^{-}f\|_{\infty}^{2}+c_{n}\|\nabla_{n}^{+}f\|_{\infty}^{2}\Big]+{\mathcalboondox O}(n^{-1})

and last display vanishes by taking
n\to+\infty
and then
\delta\to 0.

This ends the proof of Lemma
3.1
for
\beta\in\{1,2\}
.

From last results, we know that
\{{\mdmathbb Q}_{n,\beta}\}_{n\in{\mdmathbb N}}
is tight in the weak topology of
{\mathcalboondox D}([0,T],{\mathcalboondox H}_{-m})
for any
m>3/2
when
\beta=1
and
m>5/2
for
\beta=2
. Now we characterize the limit point.

3.2.
Characterization of limit points

Now we complete the proof of Theorems
2.2
and
2.4
.
From the computations of last subsection, we know that there exists a limiting point
\mdmathbb Q_{\beta}
of the sequence
\{{\mdmathbb Q}_{n,\beta}\}_{n\in{\mdmathbb N}}
. Since in both cases of
\beta
we have
\eta(x)\leq 1
, then
\mdmathbb Q_{\beta}
is supported on trajectories of measures that are absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure:

{\mdmathbb Q}_{\beta}(\pi_{\cdot}:\forall t\in[0,T],\,\pi_{t}(du)=\varrho(t,u)du)=1.

Now we characterize the density.
We start with the hyperbolic case and we present an heuristic argument, from which one easily deduces the rigorous one.
Recall (
3.4
) with
\beta=1
.
From the previous section we know that the martingale (
3.1
) vanishes in
\mdmathbb L^{2}(\mdmathbb P_{\mu_{n}})
, as
n\to+\infty
. Since the limit
\pi^{1}_{t}(du)
satisfies
\pi^{1}_{t}(du)=\varrho^{\kappa}(t,u)du
and
\mu_{n}
is associated to
\varrho_{0}
, we get

0=\langle\varrho^{\kappa}_{t},f\rangle-\langle\varrho_{0},f\rangle+\int_{0}^{t}(a+b-c)\langle\varrho_{s}^{\kappa},\nabla f\rangle ds

which matches with the notion of weak solution given in Definition
2
with
\mathfrak{g}=\varrho_{0}
and
\kappa=a+b-c
.
Now we go to the diffusive case. Recall (
3.4
) with
\beta=2
. By reorganizing terms and using the fact that
a_{n}+b_{n}-c_{n}=\tfrac{\gamma}{n}
, we can rewrite

(3.19)

\displaystyle{\mathcalboondox M}_{t}^{n,2}(f)

\displaystyle=\langle\pi_{t}^{n,2},f\rangle-\langle\pi_{0}^{n,2},f\rangle-c_{n}\int_{0}^{t}\langle\pi_{s}^{n,2},\Delta_{n}f\rangle ds+\gamma\int_{0}^{t}\langle\pi_{s}^{n,2},\nabla_{n}^{-}f\rangle ds

\displaystyle+a_{n}\int_{0}^{t}(\eta_{sn^{2}}(1)-\eta_{sn^{2}}(n))\nabla_{n}^{-}f(0)ds-d_{n}\int_{0}^{t}(\eta_{sn^{2}}(1)-\eta_{sn^{2}}(2))\nabla_{n}^{+}f(\tfrac{1}{n})ds.

In this case, the argument is similar to one used in
\beta=1
, but now the martingale
{\mathcalboondox M}_{t}^{n,2}(f)
does not vanish, and showing that the terms in the last line of last display vanish is more complicated since we need to use the dynamics of the process. To overcome this problem we use the two lemmas below, which allows to conclude that in the limit, as
n\to+\infty
, we obtain

{\mathcalboondox M}^{2}_{t}(f):=\langle\pi^{2}_{t},f\rangle-\langle\pi^{2}_{0},f\rangle-c\int_{0}^{t}\langle\pi^{2}_{s},\Delta f\rangle ds+\gamma\int_{0}^{t}\langle\pi^{2}_{s},\nabla f\rangle ds.

The next lemma is necessary to control the boundary terms in (
3.19
).

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
r\in\{2,n\}
and assume that if
r=2
then
d_{n}>0
or if
r=n
then
b_{n}>0
. For any
t>0
and
\delta\in(0,1)

\displaystyle{\mdmathbb E}_{\mu_{n}}\Bigg[\Big|\int_{0}^{t}(\eta_{sn^{2}}(1)-\eta_{sn^{2}}(r))ds\Big|\Bigg]\lesssim t\max\Big\{\frac{1}{n^{\delta}},\frac{1}{\mathfrak{C}_{r}n^{1-\delta}}\Big\},

where
~\mathfrak{C}_{r}=d_{n}\textbf{1}_{r=2}+b_{n}\textbf{1}_{r=n}
.

The next lemma is necessary to control the martingale appearing in (
3.19
), which, contrarily to the case
\beta=1
does not vanish.

Lemma 3.3
.

The sequence of martingales
\{{\mathcalboondox M}_{t}^{n,2}(f);t\geq 0\}_{n\in{\mdmathbb N}}
introduced in (
3.1
) with
\beta=2
, converges, in distribution as
n\to+\infty
, to a mean-zero martingale
{\mathcalboondox M}^{2}_{t}(f)
with quadratic variation given by

\left\langle{\mathcalboondox M}^{2}(f)\right\rangle_{t}:=2c\int_{0}^{t}\langle\pi^{2}_{s},\nabla f\rangle^{2}ds.

Proof of Lemma
3.2
.

First observe that
\mu_{n}
is a general measure on
n
and by the entropy inequality we can pay a price to replace it by the stationary measure, the Bernoulli product measure
\nu_{\varrho}
. A simple computation shows that there exists a constant
C=C(\varrho)
such that

\displaystyle H(\mu|\nu_{\varrho})=\sum_{\eta\in{}_{n}}\mu(\eta)\log{\Big(\frac{\mu(\eta)}{\nu_{\varrho}(\eta)}\Big)}\leq C\,n.

This together with Jensen’s inequality, gives for any

that

\displaystyle{\mdmathbb E}_{\mu_{n}}\Bigg[\Big|\int_{0}^{t}(\eta_{sn^{2}}(1)-\eta_{sn^{2}}(r))ds\Big|\Bigg]\leq\frac{C}{A}+\frac{1}{A\,n}\log{\mdmathbb E}_{\nu_{\varrho}}\Bigg[\exp\Big\{A\,n\,\Big|\int_{0}^{t}(\eta_{sn^{2}}(1)-\eta_{sn^{2}}(r))ds\Big|\Big\}\Bigg].

Since
e^{|x|}\leq e^{x}+e^{-x}
and

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\log(B_{n}+C_{n})=\max\left\{\limsup_{n\to\infty}\log(B_{n})\,,\,\limsup_{n\to\infty}\log(C_{n})\right\},

we can remove the absolute value on the right-hand side of (
3.21
). From Feynman-Kac’s formula, the rightmost term of (
3.21
) is bounded from above by

(3.22)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\,\sup_{f}\,\left\{\int(\eta(1)-\eta(r))\,f(\eta)\,\nu_{\varrho}\,(d\eta)-\frac{n}{A}\langle{\mathcalboondox L}_{n}\sqrt{f},\sqrt{f}\rangle_{\nu_{\varrho}}\right\}ds,

where the supremum runs over all densities
f
with respect to
\nu_{\varrho}
. Now we introduce
the quadratic operator given on
g:{}_{n}\to{\mdmathbb R}
by

(3.23)

\displaystyle{\mathfrak{D}}_{n}\,(g;\nu_{\varrho})

\displaystyle=\frac{a_{n}}{2}\int\left(\sqrt{g(\eta^{1\rightarrow n-1})}-\sqrt{g(\eta)}\right)^{2}d\nu_{\varrho}+\frac{b_{n}}{2}\int\left(\sqrt{g(\eta^{1\rightarrow n})}-\sqrt{g(\eta)}\right)^{2}d\nu_{\varrho}

\displaystyle+\frac{c_{n}}{2}\int\left(\sqrt{g(\eta^{n\rightarrow 1})}-\sqrt{g(\eta)}\right)^{2}d\nu_{\varrho}+\frac{d_{n}}{2}\int\left(\sqrt{g(\eta^{1\leftrightarrow 2})}-\sqrt{g(\eta)}\right)^{2}d\nu_{\varrho}.

A simple computation shows that
the Dirichlet form associated with the generator
{\mathcalboondox L}_{n}
, i.e.
\langle{\mathcalboondox L}_{n}g,g\rangle_{\nu_{\varrho}}
is given by
{\mathfrak{D}}_{n}\,(g;\nu_{\varrho})
.
Now, we write the leftmost term in the supremum in (
3.22
) as one-half plus one-half of itself, and in one of the parcels we make the change of variables
\eta\mapsto\eta^{1\rightarrow r}
for

or
\eta\mapsto\eta^{1\leftrightarrow r}
for

\nu_{\varrho}

(x-y)=(\sqrt{x}-\sqrt{y})(\sqrt{x}+\sqrt{y})
and Young’s inequality, we can bound that term from above by

\displaystyle\frac{B}{2}\,\int\eta(1)\,\left(\sqrt{f(\eta^{1\rightarrow n})}+\sqrt{f(\eta)}\right)^{2}d\nu_{\varrho}+\frac{1}{2B}\,\int\left(\sqrt{f(\eta^{1\rightarrow n})}-\sqrt{f(\eta)}\right)^{2}d\nu_{\varrho},

for
r=n
and a similar expression for

. Above

.
Recalling (
3.23
), using the fact that

and choosing

\frac{2A}{b_{n}n}+\frac{n}{A}\,{\mathfrak{D}}_{n}\,(\sqrt{f};\nu_{\varrho}).

Taking

the proof ends. For
r=2

∎

Theorem 3.4
.

5.
Proof of Theorem
2.3

In order to prove the theorem, we show the uniqueness of strong solutions of

We define a strong solution to (
2.7
) in the following way.
We say that a process
\{X_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
in
{\mathcalboondox C}([0,T],{\mathcalboondox H}_{-m})
is a strong solution of (

) if for all
f\in{\mathcalboondox H}_{m}
it holds

(5.1)

d\langle X_{t},f\rangle=\langle X_{t},(\nu\Delta-{\mathcalboondox v}\nabla)f\rangle dt-\sigma\langle X_{t},\nabla f\rangle d{\mathcalboondox B}_{t}.

In order to prove uniqueness of strong solutions, first, recall that the functions
g_{k}(u)=e^{2\pi iku}
,
u\in{\mdmathbb T},k\in{\mdmathbb Z}
, form an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}({\mdmathbb T})
. For each
k\in{\mdmathbb Z}
, let
\widehat{f}(k)=\left\langle f,g_{k}\right\rangle
and let us rewrite
X_{t}
as a linear combination of the aforementioned eigenfunctions:

\displaystyle X_{t}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in{\mdmathbb Z}}\widehat{X}_{t}(k)\,g_{k}.

Observe that for each
k
the process
\widehat{X}_{t}(k)
is solution of

d\widehat{X}_{t}(k)=-\lambda_{k}\widehat{X}_{t}(k)dt+\theta i\widehat{X}_{t}(k)d{{\mathcalboondox B}}_{t},

where
\lambda_{k}=\nu(2\pi k)^{2}-2\pi{\mathcalboondox v}ki
and
\theta_{k}=2\pi k\sigma
. As a consequence, it is enough to show uniqueness of the strong solution of the previous equation. Let
Y_{t}(k):=\widehat{X}_{t}(k)e^{\lambda_{k}t}
. From the above identity, we obtain the Schrödinger equation

(5.2)

dY_{t}(k)=i\theta_{k}Y_{t}(k)d{{\mathcalboondox B}}_{t},

whose solutions are of the form

Y_{t}(k)=Y_{0}(k)+\int_{0}^{t}i\theta_{k}Y_{s}(k)d{{\mathcalboondox B}}_{t}.

Let us assume that there exist two strong solutions
Y_{t}^{1}(k)
and
Y_{t}^{2}(k)
of the above equation starting from the same initial condition. Define
\Phi_{t}(k):=Y_{t}^{1}(k)-Y_{t}^{2}(k)
and
\varphi_{t}(k)={\mdmathbb E}\left[\|\Phi_{t}(k)\|^{2}\right]
. Thus,

\displaystyle\varphi_{t}(k)={\mdmathbb E}\|\Phi_{t}(k)\|^{2}

\displaystyle={\mdmathbb E}\left[\Big\|\int_{0}^{t}i\theta_{k}\Phi_{s}(k)d{\mathcal{B}}_{s}\Big\|^{2}\right]=\theta_{k}^{2}\int_{0}^{t}\left\|\Phi_{s}(k)\right\|^{2}ds=\theta_{k}^{2}\int_{0}^{t}\varphi_{s}(k)ds.

Note that in the third identity above, we used the Itô’s isometry.
By Grönwall’s Lemma and since
\varphi_{0}(k)=Y_{0}^{1}(k)-Y_{0}^{2}(k)=0
, we conclude that
\varphi_{t}(k)=0
for any
t\geq 0
and any
k\in{\mdmathbb Z}
. Therefore, the strong solution of the Schrödinger equation (
5.2
) is unique and thus the strong solution of (
2.7
) is unique.

Now we prove that every solution of the martingale problem given in Definition
3
is a strong solution.
To that end, let
\{X_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
be a solution of that martingale problem. Observe that
\{{\mathcalboondox M}_{t}(f)\}_{t\geq 0}
is a continuous martingale, so, in particular, the representation theorem and the Itô’s formula hold for these martingales. Fix a countable subset
{\mathcalboondox D}
of
C^{\infty}({\mdmathbb T})
.
For each
f\in{\mathcalboondox D}
, let
\{B_{t}(f)\}_{t\geq 0}
be a Brownian motion such that

{\mathcalboondox M}_{t}(f)=\int_{0}^{t}\sigma X_{s}(\nabla f)dB_{s}(f)

for every
t\geq 0
. By the representation theorem, the Brownian motion
\{B_{t}(f)\}_{t\geq 0}
exists in an extended probability space. The importance of
{\mathcalboondox D}
to be countable is to guarantee that all the Brownian motions
\{B_{t}(f)\}_{t\geq 0,f\in{\mathcalboondox D}}
are defined on the same extended space. Our aim is to prove that we can choose
\{B_{t}(f)\}_{t\geq 0}
independently of
f
. For
f,g\in{\mathcalboondox D}
, from the polarization identity and (
2.8
), the joint quadratic variation of
\{{\mathcalboondox M}_{t}(f)\}_{t\geq 0}
and
\{{\mathcalboondox M}_{t}(g)\}_{t\geq 0}
can be computed as follows

(5.3)

\begin{split}\langle{\mathcalboondox M}(f),{\mathcalboondox M}(g)\rangle_{t}=&\frac{1}{2}\Big(\langle{\mathcalboondox M}(f)+{\mathcalboondox M}(g)\rangle_{t}-\langle{\mathcalboondox M}(f)\rangle_{t}-\langle{\mathcalboondox M}(g)\rangle_{t}\Big)\\
=&\int_{0}^{t}\sigma^{2}\Big(X_{s}(\nabla f)+X_{s}(\nabla g)\Big)^{2}ds-\int_{0}^{t}\sigma^{2}X_{s}(\nabla f)^{2}ds-\int_{0}^{t}\sigma^{2}X_{s}(\nabla g)^{2}ds\\
=&\int_{0}^{t}\sigma^{2}X_{s}(\nabla f)\,X_{s}(\nabla g)ds.\end{split}

On the other hand, from the representation theorem (see Theorem 3.4.2 of
[
9
]
), the joint quadratic variation is given by

(5.4)

\langle{\mathcalboondox M}(f),{\mathcalboondox M}(g)\rangle_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}\sigma^{2}X_{s}(\nabla f)X_{s}(\nabla g)\,d\langle B(f),B(g)\rangle_{s}.

Let us assume for a moment that
X_{t}(\sigma\nabla f)\,X_{t}(\sigma\nabla g)\neq 0
for every
t\geq 0
. If this is the case, then (
5.3
) and (
5.4
) imply that

\langle B(f),B(g)\rangle_{t}=t.

Another application of the polarization identity gives that
\langle B(f)-B(g)\rangle_{t}=0
, from where we conclude that
B_{t}(f)=B_{t}(g)
for every
t\geq 0
. However, we do not have any
a priori
reason to assume that
X_{t}(\sigma\nabla f)\,X_{t}(\sigma\nabla g)\neq 0
for every
t\geq 0
, nor we can derive this fact from the martingale property. Therefore, we will proceed more carefully. Let
\{f_{i}\}_{i\in{\mdmathbb N}}
be an enumeration of
{\mathcalboondox D}
and let
\{A_{i}\}_{i\in{\mdmathbb N}}
be given on
i\in\mdmathbb N
by

A_{i}:=\{t\geq 0;\,X_{t}(\sigma\nabla f_{i})=0\}.

The representation theorem states that
\{B_{t}(f)\}_{t\geq 0}
admits the representation

B_{t}(f_{i})=\int_{0}^{t}\Big(\mdmathbb{1}_{\{s\notin A_{i}\}}X_{s}(\sigma\nabla f_{i})^{-1}d{\mathcalboondox M}_{s}(f_{i})+\mdmathbb{1}_{\{s\in A_{i}\}}d{B}^{i}_{s}\Big),

where
\{B_{t}^{i}\}_{t\geq 0}
is an auxiliary Brownian motion. According to the representation theorem, we have the freedom to define this auxiliary Brownian motion using a Brownian motion defined in our original filtered space, using a Brownian motion independent of our original filtered space, or using a combination of both procedures. We adopt the latter option, as we explain below.

Let
A_{0}:=[0,\infty)
, and define the sets
\{C_{i}\}_{i\in{\mdmathbb N}}
as

C_{i}:=\bigcap_{j=0}^{i-1}A_{j}\setminus A_{i},

and let
C_{\infty}:=\cap_{i\in{\mdmathbb N}}A_{i}
. Observe that
C_{1}=A_{0}\setminus A_{1}
,
C_{2}=(A_{0}\cap A_{1})\setminus A_{2}
and so on. Note that
\{C_{i}\}_{i\in{\mdmathbb N}\cup\infty}
is a partition of
A_{0}
.
Let
\{W_{s}\}_{s\geq 0}
be a Brownian motion independent of
\{B_{s}^{i}\}_{s\geq 0}
and
\{{\mathcalboondox M}_{s}(f_{i})\}_{s\geq 0}
, for all
i\geq 1
. Define

\widetilde{B}_{t}(f_{1}):=\int_{0}^{t}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\mdmathbb{1}_{\{s\in C_{i}\}}X_{s}(\sigma\nabla f_{i})^{-1}d{\mathcalboondox M}_{s}(f_{i})+\mdmathbb{1}_{\{s\in C_{\infty}\}}d{W_{s}}\Big).

From the representation theorem, we have that

{\mathcalboondox M}_{t}(f_{1})=\int_{0}^{t}X_{s}(\sigma\nabla f_{1})d\widetilde{B}_{s}(f_{1}).

Recall that by (
5.3
) and (
5.4
),
dB_{t}(f_{1})=dB_{t}(f_{2})
for
t\notin A_{1}\cup A_{2}
. We also have that

d\widetilde{B}_{t}(f_{1})=d{B}_{t}(f_{1})\text{ for }t\notin A_{1}\cup A_{2},

d\widetilde{B}_{t}(f_{1})=dB_{t}(f_{2})\text{ for }t\in A_{1}\setminus A_{2},

from where we conclude that
d\widetilde{B}_{t}(f_{1})=dB_{t}(f_{2})
for
t\notin A_{2}
. Let us define

\widetilde{B}_{t}(f_{2}):=\int_{0}^{t}\Big(\mdmathbb{1}_{\{s\notin A_{2}\}}dB_{s}(f_{2})+\mdmathbb{1}_{\{s\in A_{2}\}}d\widetilde{B}_{s}(f_{1})\Big)

and observe that

{\mathcalboondox M}_{t}(f_{2})=\int_{0}^{t}X_{s}(\sigma\nabla f_{2})d\widetilde{B}_{s}(f_{2})

and
\widetilde{B}_{t}(f_{1})=\widetilde{B}_{t}(f_{2})
for every
t\geq 0
.
Inductively, we can define

\widetilde{B_{t}}(f_{\ell+1}):=\int_{0}^{t}\Big(\mdmathbb{1}_{\{s\notin A_{\ell+1}\}}dB_{s}(f_{\ell+1})+\mdmathbb{1}_{\{s\in A_{\ell+1}\}}d\widetilde{B}_{s}(f_{\ell})\Big)

and we can check that
\widetilde{B}_{t}(f_{\ell+1})=\widetilde{B}_{t}(f_{\ell})
for every
t\geq 0
. We conclude that
\widetilde{B}_{t}:=\widetilde{B}_{t}(f_{\ell})
does not depend on
\ell
and

{\mathcalboondox M}_{t}(f_{\ell})=\int_{0}^{t}X_{s}(\sigma\nabla f_{\ell})d\widetilde{B}_{s}(f_{\ell})

for every
\ell\in{\mdmathbb N}
. This shows that the solution of the martingale problem
\{X_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
of (
2.7
) satisfies

X_{t}(f)=X_{0}(f)+\int_{0}^{t}X_{s}(\nu\Delta f-\gamma\nabla f)ds+\int_{0}^{t}X_{s}(\sigma\nabla f)d\widetilde{B}_{s}

for every
f\in{\mathcalboondox D}
. Observe that in order to show uniqueness of strong solutions of (
2.7
), we only need to verify (
5.1
) for the trigonometric functions
\{g_{k}\}_{k\in{\mdmathbb Z}}
.
Choosing
{\mathcal{D}}=:\{\textrm{Re}(g_{k}),\textrm{Im}(g_{k})\}_{k\in{\mdmathbb Z}}
, we conclude that every martingale solution of (
2.7
) is also a strong solution of (
2.7
), from where uniqueness follows.
