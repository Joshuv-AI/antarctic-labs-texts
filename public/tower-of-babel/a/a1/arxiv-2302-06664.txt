Generalizations of the Muller-Schupp theorem and tree-like inverse graphs
Emanuele Rodaro

Abstract
. We extend the characterization of context-free groups of Muller and Schupp in two ways. We first show that for a quasi-transitive inverse graph Γ \Gamma , being quasi-isometric to a tree, or context-free (finitely many end-cones types), or having the automorphism group Aut ⁡ ( Γ ) \Aut(\Gamma) that is virtually free, are all equivalent conditions. Furthermore, we add to the previous equivalences a group theoretic analog to the representation theorem of Chomsky-Schützenberger that is fundamental in solving a weaker version of a conjecture of T. Brough which also extends Muller and Schupp’ result to the class of groups that are virtually finitely generated subgroups of direct product of free groups. We show that such groups are precisely those whose word problem is the intersection of a finite number of languages accepted by quasi-transitive, tree-like inverse graphs.



Mathematics Subject Classification (2020)
: 20F65, 05C75, 20F10, 68Q70, 20M18, 57M10.

Keywords
: group actions on inverse graphs, quasi-tree, word-problem, virtually free groups, context-free graphs and languages, subgroups of the direct product of free groups.

1.
Introduction

The word problem of a finitely generated group
G
presented by
\langle A|\mathcal{R}\rangle
consists of the set
WP(G;A)
o all words in
(A\cup A^{-1})^{*}
that represent the identity of
G
. One of the most interesting problems in this setting is the algebraic characterization of a group
G
in terms of the language theoretic properties of
WP(G;A)
. For example, Anisimov proved that a group is finite if and only if its word problem is a regular language
[
2
]
. A famous result in this area is the classification of the groups with context-free word problems (context-free groups) which states that a finitely generated group
G
has a context-free word problem if and only if
G
is virtually free; a result that follows from the work of Muller and Schupp
[
28
]
together with the work of Dunwoody
[
14
]
on the accessibility of finitely presented groups. Moreover, geometrically context-free groups are characterized by Cayley graphs which are quasi-isometric to trees
[
15
, Theorem 19 pp. 133]
. In this paper, we extend these results to the class of inverse graphs, i.e., graphs in the sense of Serre labeled on an involutive alphabet
A\cup A^{-1}
such that for every
a\in A\cup A^{-1}
and vertex
v
there is at most one edge
e
starting at
v
labeled by
a
, and consequently, the inverse edge
e^{-1}
has
v
as its terminal vertex and it is labeled by
a^{-1}
. These graphs often appear in several areas of mathematics, especially geometric group and semigroup theory via Cayley and Schreier graphs in the area of group theory, and Schützenberger graphs that are the analog of Cayley graphs in the area of inverse semigroup theory. An inverse graph
\Gamma
is called quasi-transitive if it has a finite number of orbits under the action of its automorphism group
\Aut(\Gamma)
.
One of the main results of the paper, Theorem
2
, stands at the intersection of graph theory, geometric group theory, and theoretical computer science:

Theorem
.

Let
\Gamma
be an infinite quasi-transitive inverse graph. T.F.A.E.

(1)

\Gamma
is context-free (it has finitely many end-cones types);

(2)

\Gamma
is quasi-isometric to a tree (it is tree-like);

(3)

\Aut(\Gamma)
is a finitely generated virtually free group;

(4)

there is a cover
\psi:\Gamma\to\Lambda
of inverse graphs with
\Lambda
finite, and a surjective morphism

\overline{\eta}:\pi_{1}(\Lambda,\psi(x_{0}))\to\mathbb{F}_{X}

from the fundamental group
\pi_{1}(\Lambda,\psi(x_{0}))
onto a free group
\mathbb{F}_{X}
of finite rank, with
\ker(\overline{\eta})
that is equal to the set of reduced words in the language
L(\Gamma,x_{0})
of the labels of circuits based at the root
x_{0}
.

In the same spirit of the previous theorem, in
[
22
]
the authors characterize quasi-transitive deterministic
A
-digraphs whose languages of the self-avoiding walks are context-free with respect to the size of the ends.
The generalization of the notion of context-freeness from groups to a generic inverse graph is not immediate at first, since there might be several ways to give such a definition. Since for a group the word problem corresponds to the set of labels of the walks starting and ending in a vertex of the Cayley graph, we can transfer this definition textually to an inverse graph.
In Section
4
we prove that this definition is equivalent to several others, including the more graph theoretic one of context-free graph given by Muller and Schupp in
[
29
]
as a labeled graph with finitely many labeled end-cones types. We also characterize such graphs as the configuration graphs of inverse pushdown automata, a variant of reversible pushdown automata introduced in
[
21
]
.

To prove the implication
(1)\Rightarrow(2)
of our characterization, in Section
5
we show that any context-free inverse graph is quasi-isometric to a tree. As a consequence, the converse of
[
16
, Theorem 4.13]
holds, providing a solution to
[
16
, Question 6.2]
and the following geometric characterization

Theorem
.

Let
M
be a finitely presented inverse monoid. Then, a Schützenberger graph of some element of
M
is context-free if and only if it is quasi-isometric to a tree.

We emphasize that also this result generalizes the analogous one for groups since a group is a special case of inverse monoid and the (unique) Schützenberger graph of a group coincides with its Cayley graph. While the equivalence
(2)\Leftrightarrow(3)
can be obtained directly from standard facts in literature, to close all the equivalences we pass from the technical condition
(4)
which is a group theoretic representation theorem à la Chomsky–Schützenberger. This condition is a key fact that it is used in the last section to shed some light on a conjecture regarding poly-context-free groups. As a natural extension of context-free groups, and thus to virtually free groups, Brough in
[
5
]
has introduced the concept of poly-context-free groups. A group is called poly-context-free if its word problem is the intersection of a finite number of context-free languages. Since the intersection of context-free languages is not in general context-free, a natural problem is the classification of such groups. In
[
5
]
the author has conjectured that the class of finitely generated poly-context-free groups coincides with the class of (finitely generated) groups which are virtually a finitely generated subgroup of a direct product of free groups. Using condition (4) in Section
7
we show the other main result of the paper which also offers another generalization of the theorem of Muller and Schupp.

Theorem
.

A group
G
has the word problem in the class of languages that are intersections
\bigcap_{i=1}^{k}L(\Gamma_{i},x_{i})
of finitely many languages accepted by quasi-transitive tree-like inverse graphs
\Gamma_{i}
, if and only if it is virtually a (finitely generated) subgroup of a direct product of free groups.

.
We say that

to the

An

The class of

the subgraph of

with set of vertices

and set of edges

From now on we will mainly consider digraphs on alphabets with an involution which is the main object of this paper.

Definition 1

(involutive and inverse graphs)
.

An
involutive

\widetilde{A}
-digraph is a directed graph
\Gamma
on the set of labels
\widetilde{A}
with an involution
\circ^{-1}:E\to E
such that

•

for any
e\in E
,
e^{-1}
is the opposite edge with
\iota(e^{-1})=\tau(e)
,
\tau(e^{-1})=\iota(e)
;

•

the involution is compatible with the labeling function:
\lambda(e^{-1})=\lambda(e)^{-1}
;

•

no edge is the inverse of itself
e\neq e^{-1}
.

\Gamma
is called
inverse
if, in addition, it is deterministic and connected, i.e., any two vertices are connected by a walk in
\Gamma
.

If
\Gamma
is an
A
-digraph, then by adding for each edge
e=x_{1}\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$a$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}x_{2}
in
\Gamma
a new edge
e^{-1}=x_{2}\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$a^{-1}$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}x_{1}
with label on the set of formal inverse
A^{-1}
, we obtain a new involutive graph on
\widetilde{A}
that we denote by
\Gamma^{-}
. It is obvious that if
\Gamma
is deterministic and co-deterministic, then
\Gamma^{-}
is deterministic.

The notions that we have previously considered for an
A
-digraph, transfer directly to involutive and inverse graphs by adding the compatibility with the involution on the edges. Thus, in the category of involutive (inverse) graphs a morphism
\psi:\Gamma_{1}\to\Gamma_{2}
of involutive (inverse) graphs is a morphism of
\widetilde{A}
-digraphs with the property that
\psi(e^{-1})=\psi(e)^{-1}
. In particular, considering the inclusion map, the subgraph of an involutive (inverse) graph is also involutive (inverse). In our context, a
tree
is a connected graph in which every circuit
p=e_{1}\ldots e_{k}
contains a subwalk of the form
ee^{-1}
for some edge
e
of
p
. A walk
p
not containing any subwalk of the form
ee^{-1}
is called
reduced
. So in a tree there are no reduced walks, except the empty one. Note that the associated simple graph of an involutive graph that is a tree
T
, is a simple graph that is also a tree as a simple graph, i.e., every two vertices are connected by a unique simple path.

Given a connected involutive graph
\Gamma
, we can equip
\Gamma
with a structure of a metric space by considering the usual distance given by

d(u,v)=\min\{n:\mbox{ such that }p=e_{1}\ldots e_{n}\mbox{ is a walk with }\iota(p)=u,\tau(p)=v\}

A walk

is a geodesic, if
p
has length exactly
d(\iota(p),\tau(p))
. The (induced) subgraph
D_{n}(p)
consisting of vertices whose length from a fixed vertex

is at most

is called the
disk of center
p
and radius
n
. For a connected subgraph

the diameter of

is given by
\delta_{\Gamma}(Y)=\max\{d(y_{1},y_{2}):y_{1},y_{2}\in V(Y)\}
.

2.1.
Basic facts about morphisms of inverse graphs

The condition of determinism and connectedness of an involutive graph has some consequences when we consider morphisms in the category of inverse graphs. For instance, a morphism
\psi:\Gamma\to\Lambda
is completely determined by the vertex map
\psi_{v}
. In particular,
\psi
is a monomorphism if for any
y\in V(\Lambda)
, the preimage
\psi_{v}^{-1}(y)
contains at most one element and checking if two morphisms are equal is just a matter of verifying if they agree on a vertex.

Lemma 2
.

Let
\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2}
be two morphisms from the inverse graph
\Gamma_{1}
to the inverse graph
\Gamma_{2}
, then
\varphi_{1}=\varphi_{2}
if and only if
\varphi_{1}(x)=\varphi_{2}(x)
for some
x\in V(\Gamma)
.

Proof.

Any walk
x\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$u$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}y
is mapped by
\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2}
into the two walks

\varphi_{1}(x)\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$u$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}\varphi_{1}(y)\mbox{ and }\varphi_{2}(x)\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$u$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}\varphi_{2}(y)

and so by the determinism of
\Gamma_{2}
and condition
\varphi_{1}(x)=\varphi_{2}(x)
we deduce
\varphi_{1}(y)=\varphi_{2}(y)
. Since
\Gamma_{1}
is connected, we have that
\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2}
agree on
V(\Gamma_{1})
, hence by the determinism of
\Gamma_{2}
, they also agree on
E(\Gamma_{1})
.
∎

The previous result is a particular case of a more general one since morphisms of inverse graphs are immersions of involutive graphs, the result follows from
[
23
, Proposition 2.1]
.
Another interesting feature of inverse graphs, and in particularly rooted inverse graphs, is the relationship between accepted languages and morphisms. This connection is contained in the following proposition belonging to folklore.

Proposition 1
.

Let
(\Gamma,x_{0}),(\Lambda,y_{0})
be two rooted inverse graphs, then
L(\Gamma,x_{0})\subseteq L(\Lambda,y_{0})
if and only if there is a morphism
\psi:\Gamma\rightarrow\Lambda
with
\psi(x_{0})=y_{0}
. Moreover,
L(\Gamma,x_{0})=L(\Lambda,y_{0})
if and only if there is an isomorphism
\psi:\Gamma\rightarrow\Lambda

Proof.

Suppose we have the inclusion
L(\Gamma,x_{0})\subseteq L(\Lambda,y_{0})
. Take a vertex
v\in V(\Gamma)
, since
\Gamma
is connected there is a walk
x_{0}\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$u$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}v
in
\Gamma
for some word
u\in\widetilde{A}^{*}
. Now, by the inclusion of the two languages, we have that
uu^{-1}\in L(\Lambda,y_{0})
, hence there is a walk
p
in
\Lambda
starting at
y_{0}
labeled by
uu^{-1}
. By the determinism of
\Lambda
we concluide that
p=y_{0}\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$u$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}z\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$u^{-1}$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}y_{0}
is actually a circuit. Define the map
\psi:\Gamma\to\Lambda
by putting
\psi(v)=z
. It is a well define map since if
x_{0}\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$w$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}v
is another walk connecting
x_{0}
with
v
, then
uw^{-1}\in L(\Gamma,x_{0})\subseteq L(\Lambda,y_{0})
which in turns implies that
y_{0}\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$u$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}z^{\prime}\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$w^{-1}$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}y_{0}
is a circuit in
\Lambda
. Hence, by the determinism of
\Lambda
, we conclude that
z^{\prime}=z
. By the way
\psi
is defined, it immediately follows that
\psi
is a morphism of inverse graphs with
\psi(x_{0})=y_{0}
. Conversely, any morphism
\psi:\Gamma\to\Lambda
with
\psi(x_{0})=y_{0}
implies that any word
w
labeling a circuit
p
centered at
x_{0}
, labels also the circuit
\psi(p)
centered at
y_{0}
.

The equality
L(\Gamma,x_{0})=L(\Lambda,y_{0})
implies that there are two morphisms
\psi:\Gamma\to\Lambda
,
\varphi:\Lambda\to\Gamma
with
\psi(x_{0})=y_{0}
,
\varphi(y_{0})=x_{0}
. Since the composition
\varphi\circ\psi:\Gamma\to\Gamma
is a morphism with
\varphi\circ\psi(x_{0})=x_{0}
by Lemma
2
we conclude that
\varphi\circ\psi
is the identity morphism on
\Gamma
. Similarly,
\psi\circ\varphi
is the identity morphism on
\Lambda
.
∎

Note that by the previous proposition we have that, up to isomorphism, there is a unique rooted inverse graph accepting the language
L(\Gamma,x_{0})
.
The set of automorphisms of the inverse graph
\Gamma
will be denoted by
\Aut(\Gamma)
. This is a group acting on
\Gamma
in a canonical way. In case the quotient graph
\Aut(\Gamma)\setminus\Gamma
is finite, we say that
\Gamma
is quasi-transitive. Note that this last condition is equivalent to the finiteness of
V(\Gamma)\setminus\Aut(\Gamma)
.

2.2.
Coverings of involutive and inverse graphs

In this section, we recall some basic topological facts of involutive graphs, and we derive some basic results for inverse graphs that will be used later in the proof of our characterizations.

Let
\Gamma
be an involutive graph, for any
v\in V(\Gamma)
, let
\Star(\Gamma,v)=\{e\in E(\Gamma):\iota(e)=v\}
be the star set of
\Gamma
at
v
. Any morphism
\varphi:\Gamma\to\Gamma^{\prime}
between two involutive graphs induces a map
\varphi:\Star(\Gamma,v)\to\Star(\Gamma^{\prime},\varphi(v))
between star sets in the obvious ways. Following Stallings
[
33
]
we say that a graph morphism
\varphi
is a
cover
(
immersion
) if the restrictions
\varphi:\Star(\Gamma,v)\to\Star(\Gamma^{\prime},\varphi(v))
are bijections (injections) for every
v\in V(\Gamma)
. In his paper
[
33
]
, Stallings made use of immersions between finite graphs to study finitely generated subgroups of free groups. Subsequently, Margolis and Meakin
[
25
]
showed how the theory of inverse monoids may be used to classify immersions between connected graphs, see
[
26
]
for further extensions to higher dimensional cell complexes. By Lemma
1
every morphism between two (complete) inverse
\widetilde{A}
-digraphs is a (covering) immersion.
One central feature of coverings is the possibility of lifting walks, this is contained in the following characterization.

Proposition 2
.

[
23
, Proposition 2.3]

Let
\varphi:\Gamma^{\prime}\to\Gamma
be an immersion between involutive connected graphs. Then,
\varphi
is a cover if and only if, for each vertex
v\in V(\Gamma)
and vertex
v^{\prime}\in\varphi^{-1}(v)
, every walk
p
in
\Gamma
with
\iota(p)=v
lifts to a unique walk
p^{\prime}
starting at
v^{\prime}
, i.e.,
\varphi(p^{\prime})=p
.

An immediate consequence of the previous proposition is that covers of inverse graphs are surjective. We now briefly summarize some basic facts linking group theory and covers of graphs. Following
[
33
]
two walks
p,q
of a connected involutive graph
\Gamma
are said to be homotopic equivalent (written
p\sim q
) if we may pass from
p
to
q
by a finite sequence of insertions or deletions of walks of the form
ee^{-1}
for some edges of
e\in E(\Gamma)
; clearly
\iota(p)=\iota(q)
,
\tau(p)=\tau(q)
. Let
[p]
denote the equivalence class (homotopy class) of the walk
p
in
\Gamma
. Fix a vertex
v\in V(\Gamma)
, the set
\pi_{1}(\Gamma,v)=\{[p]:\iota(p)=\tau(p)=v\}
together with the operation defined by
[p][q]=[pq]
is a group, called the
fundamental group of
\Gamma
based at
v
. If
\Gamma
is an inverse
\widetilde{A}
-digraph, then for any
u\in\widetilde{A}^{*}
, there is at most one walk starting at
v
with
u
as label. Thus, it is routine to check that the map
\omega:\pi_{1}(\Gamma,v)\to\mathbb{F}_{A}
sending
[p]
to the element
\overline{\lambda(p)}\in\mathbb{F}_{A}
is a well-defined map which is a monomorphism. With a slight abuse of notation we will denote by
\pi_{1}(\Gamma,v)
the isomorphic image
\omega\left(\pi_{1}(\Gamma,v)\right)
. In this way
\pi_{1}(\Gamma,v)
may be identified with the set
\overline{L(\Gamma,x_{0})}
of reduced words labeling a circuit at
x_{0}
. It is easily seen that the map
\theta:L(\Gamma,x_{0})\to\pi_{1}(\Gamma,x_{0})
defined by
\theta(u)=\overline{u}
is a (surjective) homomorphism of monoids. It is a well-known fact that the fundamental group of every connected involutive graph is free.
By Proposition 2.3 of
[
31
, Chapter 23]
and Proposition
1
, the Stalling automaton of
\pi_{1}(\Gamma,v)
is isomorphic to the
core
of
(\Gamma,v)
, i.e., the (induced) rooted inverse subgraph obtained from
(\Gamma,v)
by taking all the vertices belonging to a reduced circuit based at
v
. This operation is equivalent to removing all the “hanging trees” not containing
v
from
(\Gamma,v)
.

The notion of determinizing
V
-quotient, for short
DV
-quotient, of an inverse
\widetilde{A}
-digraph
\Gamma=(V,E,\iota,\tau,\lambda)
plays an important role in the proof of the characterization contained in Theorem
2
. This notion appears quite often in combinatorial group and inverse semigroup theory, for instance in the folding operation involved in the construction of a Stallings automaton, and the more general setting of Schützenberger automata, see
[
34
]
. Henceforth, we consider a very specific kind of
DV
-quotiens, the ones generated by a subset of vertices.

Definition 2

(
DV
-quotients generated by a subset of vertices)
.

Let
W\subseteq V(\Gamma)
be a subset of vertices and define the relations
\rho_{W}
on
V(\Gamma)
by
x\,\rho_{W}\,y
if there are walks
w_{1}\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$u$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}x
,
w_{2}\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$u$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}y
, for some
u\in\widetilde{A}^{*}
,
w_{1},w_{2}\in W
. This is clearly an equivalence relation on
V(\Gamma)
that is compatible with
\Gamma
in the following sense: if
x_{1}\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$v$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}x_{2}
,
y_{1}\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$v$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}y_{2}
,
v\in\widetilde{A}^{*}
, are walks in
\Gamma
with
x_{1}\,\rho_{W}\,y_{1}
, then
x_{2}\,\rho_{W}\,y_{2}
. The
DV
-quotient
\Gamma/\rho_{W}
is the
\widetilde{A}
-digraph with vertices the equivalence classes
V(\Gamma)/\rho_{W}
and edges
[x]_{\rho_{W}}\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$a$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}[y]_{\rho_{W}}
whenever
x_{1}\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$a$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}y_{1}
is and edge in
\Gamma
with
x_{1}\,\rho_{W}\,x
,
y_{1}\,\rho_{W}\,y
. We say that
W\subseteq V(\Gamma)
has
finite index
if the quotient graph
\Gamma/\rho_{W}
is finite.

The compatibility of
\rho_{W}
ensures that the determinism of
\Gamma
implies the determinism of
\Gamma/\rho_{W}
, hence

\Gamma/\rho_{W}
is also an inverse graph. Let
\pi:\Gamma\to\Gamma/\rho_{W}
be the morphism of inverse graphs defined by
\pi_{v}(x)=[x]_{\rho_{W}}
,
\pi_{e}(x\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$a$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}y)=[x]_{\rho_{W}}\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$a$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}[y]_{\rho_{W}}
. We have already remarked that the morphism
\pi:\Gamma\to\Gamma/\rho_{\rho_{W}}
is an immersion. However, it is a cover if one restricts to subsets of vertices of an
\Aut(\Gamma)
-orbit.

Lemma 3
.

Let
H\leq\Aut(\Gamma)
be a subgroup of
\Aut(\Gamma)
. If
W\subseteq H\cdot x_{0}
, then, the morphism
\pi:\Gamma\to\Gamma/\rho_{W}
is a cover of
\Gamma/\rho_{W}
. Moreover, if
H\cdot x_{0}=W
, then for any
x\in V(\Gamma)
we have
[x]_{\rho_{W}}=H\cdot x
.

Proof.

First note that if
x\,\rho_{W}\,y
then, there is an automorphism
\varphi\in H
with
\varphi(x)=y
. Indeed, by definition there are paths
w_{1}\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$u$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}x
,
w_{2}\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$u$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}y
with
w_{1},w_{2}\in W
, and since
W\subseteq H\cdot x_{0}
, there is an automorphism
\varphi\in H
with
\varphi(w_{1})=w_{2}
which in turn implies
\varphi(x)=y
. In particular, we have shown that
[x]_{\rho_{W}}\subseteq H\cdot x
. Thus, the fact that two
\rho_{W}
-related elements are in the same
H
-orbit, implies that
|\Star(x,\Gamma)|=|\Star([x]_{\rho_{W}},\Gamma/\rho_{W})|
, from which we conclude that
\pi:\Star(x,\Gamma)\to\Star([x]_{\rho_{W}},\Gamma/\rho_{W})
is a bijection.

For the second statement, we have to show the other inclusion
H\cdot x\subseteq[x]_{\rho_{W}}
. Let
y\in H\cdot x
then there is an automorphism
\varphi\in H
with
y=\varphi(x)
and so if
x_{0}\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$v$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}x
is a walk connecting
x_{0}
to
x
, this walk is sent into the walk
\varphi(x_{0})\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$v$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}\varphi(x)=y
, with
\varphi(x_{0})\in W
, i.e.,
x\,\rho_{W}\,y
.
∎

From this last Lemma
3
and Proposition
2
we immediately derive the following fact.

Lemma 4
.

Let
W=H\cdot x_{0}
, for some
x_{0}\in V(\Gamma)
and
H\leq\Aut(\Gamma)
. Then, any circuit
[w]_{\rho_{W}}\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$v$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}[w]_{\rho_{W}}
in
\Gamma/\rho_{W}
with
w\in W
, lifts uniquely to a walk
w\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$v$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}w_{1}
in
\Gamma
for some
w_{1}\in W
.

We have the following lemma.

Lemma 5
.

Let
W=H\cdot x_{0}
, for some
x_{0}\in V(\Gamma)
and
H\leq\Aut(\Gamma)
. Then,
H\leq\Aut(\Gamma)
is a finite index subgroups if and only if
\Gamma/\rho_{W}
is finite.

Proof.

By Lemma
2
we have
(H\varphi)\cdot x_{0}\cap(H\psi)\cdot x_{0}\neq\emptyset
if and only if
\varphi\psi^{-1}\in H
. Hence, by Lemma
3
we have
[\varphi(x_{0})]_{\rho_{W}}=[\psi(x_{0})]_{\rho_{W}}
if and only if
H\varphi=H\psi
. Thus, different right cosets give rise to different
\rho_{W}
-classes, and these are finitely many if and only if there are finitely many right cosets.
∎

Proposition 3
.

Let
W=H\cdot x_{0}
, for some
x_{0}\in V(\Gamma)
and
H\leq\Aut(\Gamma)
. Then, there is a surjective homomorphism of monoids:

\eta:L(\Gamma/\rho_{W},[x_{0}]_{\rho_{W}})\to H

such that
L(\Gamma,x_{0})=\eta^{-1}(\mathds{1}_{H})
, where
\mathds{1}_{H}
is the identity of
H
. Furthermore, there is a surjective homomorphism

\overline{\eta}:\pi_{1}(\Gamma/\rho_{W},[x_{0}]_{\rho_{W}})\to H

with
\overline{\eta}^{-1}(\mathds{1}_{H})=\overline{L(\Gamma,x_{0})}
.

Proof.

By Lemma
4
every circuit
[x_{0}]_{\rho_{W}}\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$u$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}[x_{0}]_{\rho_{W}}
in
\Gamma/\rho_{W}
may be uniquely lifted to a walk
x_{0}\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$u$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}w
for some
w\in W
. Since
W=H\cdot x_{0}
, there is a (unique by Lemma
2
) automorphism
\varphi_{u}\in H
sending
x_{0}
to
w
. Let us define the morphism
\eta
by putting
\eta(u)=\varphi_{u}
. By the uniqueness of the automorphism
\varphi_{u}
,
\eta
is a well-defined function. Let us show that it is also a homomorphism. Take two circuits

[x_{0}]_{\rho_{W}}\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$u$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}[x_{0}]_{\rho_{W}},\quad[x_{0}]_{\rho_{W}}\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$v$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}[x_{0}]_{\rho_{W}}\mbox{ in }\Gamma/\rho_{W}

and lift them to the walks
x_{0}\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$u$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}w_{1}
,
x_{0}\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$v$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}w_{2}
of
\Gamma
for some
w_{1},w_{2}\in W
and let
\varphi_{u},\varphi_{v}\in H
be two automorphisms with
\varphi_{u}(x_{0})=w_{1}
,
\varphi_{v}(x_{0})=w_{2}
. Similarly, the composition of the two circuits
[x_{0}]_{\rho_{W}}\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$uv$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}[x_{0}]_{\rho_{W}}
lifts uniquely to the walk
x_{0}\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$uv$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}w_{3}
for some
w_{3}\in W
and let
\varphi_{uv}\in H
be the corresponding automorphism with
\varphi_{uv}(x_{0})=w_{3}
. Now, by applying the automorphism
\varphi_{u}
to the walk
x_{0}\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$v$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}\varphi_{v}(x_{0})
we get the walk
\varphi_{u}(x_{0})\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$v$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}\varphi_{u}(\varphi_{v}(x_{0}))
. Hence, since
w_{1}=\varphi_{u}(x_{0})
in
\Gamma
there is the walk
x_{0}\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$uv$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}\varphi_{u}(\varphi_{v}(x_{0}))
. Therefore, by the determinism of
\Gamma
we deduce

\varphi_{u}(\varphi_{v}(x_{0}))=w_{3}=\varphi_{uv}(x_{0})

and so by Lemma
2

\varphi_{u}\circ\varphi_{v}=\varphi_{uv}
. From this last equation we conclude that
\eta
is a homomorphism
\eta(uv)=\varphi_{uv}=\varphi_{u}\circ\varphi_{v}=\eta(u)\circ\eta(v)
. Let us show that
\eta
is surjective. For any
\varphi\in H
take any walk
x_{0}\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$u$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}\varphi(x_{0})
, this walk maps onto a walk
[x_{0}]_{\rho_{W}}\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$u$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}[\varphi(x_{0})]_{\rho_{W}}
with
\eta(u)=\varphi
. The set
\eta^{-1}(\mathds{1}_{H})
is formed by the circuits
[x_{0}]_{\rho_{W}}\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$u$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}[x_{0}]_{\rho_{W}}
whose lifted walks
x_{0}\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$u$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}w
have the property that

, i.e.,
u\in L(\Gamma,x_{0})
. For the last statement of the proposition put
\overline{\eta}=\eta(\theta^{-1})
where the map
\theta:L(\Gamma/\rho_{W},[x_{0}]_{\rho_{W}})\to\pi_{1}(\Gamma/\rho_{W},[x_{0}]_{\rho_{W}})
is the surjective homomorphism defined by
\theta(u)=\overline{u}
. This is a well-defined homomorphism: take any two words
u_{1},u_{2}\in\theta^{-1}(g)
for some

. By definition of

there are two automorphisms
\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2}\in H
with
\eta(u_{1})=\varphi_{1}
,
\eta(u_{2})=\varphi_{2}
and two walks
x_{0}\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$u_{1}$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}\varphi_{1}(x_{0})
,
x_{0}\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$u_{2}$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}\varphi_{2}(x_{0})
. Now, since

is inverse by Lemma

we have that there are also the walks
x_{0}\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$\overline{u_{1}}$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}\varphi_{1}(x_{0})
,
x_{0}\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$\overline{u_{2}}$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}\varphi_{2}(x_{0})
. Hence, since

we conclude that

, i.e.,
\eta(u_{1})=\eta(u_{2})
.
∎

3.
Context-free grammars, pushdown automata and configurations graphs

There are several ways to define a context-free language, either via the notion of grammar, or using the notion of pushdown automaton. For the sake of completeness we briefly recall the main notions that are used in the paper, and we refer the reader to
[
19
]
for further details. A
context-free grammar
is a tuple
\mathcal{G}=(V,A,P,S)
where
V
is a finite set of non-terminal symbols (or variables),
A
is the finite alphabet of terminal symbols that is disjoint from
V
,
S\in V
is the starting symbol, and
P\subseteq V\times(V\cup A)^{*}
is a finite set called productions. Usually production are denoted by
X\Rightarrow_{\mathcal{G}}\alpha
,
\alpha\in(V\cup A)^{*},X\in V
. We extend
\Rightarrow_{\mathcal{G}}
by putting
\alpha X\gamma\Rightarrow_{\mathcal{G}}\alpha\beta\gamma
whenever
X\Rightarrow_{\mathcal{G}}\beta\in P
, and we denote by
\Rightarrow_{\mathcal{G}}^{*}
the reflexive and transitive closure of
\Rightarrow_{\mathcal{G}}
. For a variable
X\in V
the language generated by
X
is the set

L(X)=\{u\in A^{*}:X\Rightarrow_{\mathcal{G}}^{*}u\}

the language generated by the grammar
\mathcal{G}
is
L(S)
, and in this case we say that it is a context-free language. It is a well-known fact that every context-free grammar
\mathcal{G}
can be replaced by one in Chomsky normal form:

Proposition 4

(Theorems 4.5 and 4.3
[
19
]
)
.

Any context-free language
L
with
\varepsilon\notin L
is generated by a grammar
\mathcal{C}
in which all productions are of the form
X\Rightarrow_{\mathcal{C}}YZ
, with
Y,Z
variables different from the starting symbol, and
X\Rightarrow_{\mathcal{C}}a
, with
a\in A
terminal symbol. In case,
\varepsilon\in L
there is just one production involving
\varepsilon
which is
S\Rightarrow_{\mathcal{C}}\varepsilon
.

We call a rule of the form
X\Rightarrow_{\mathcal{C}}YZ

non-terminal
, while one of the form
X\Rightarrow_{\mathcal{C}}a
,
terminal
. Henceforth, we assume that all the variable of
\mathcal{G}
are useful, in the sense that any
X\in V
appears in some derivation
S\Rightarrow_{\mathcal{G}}^{*}w
for some
w\in L(\mathcal{G})
.

Remark 1
.

In the production
S\Rightarrow_{\mathcal{C}}w
of a word
w
it is not difficult to see that we may obtain the same word
w
by first applying all the non-terminal rules followed by the terminal ones.

An alternative way to describe such languages is via the notion of pushdown automaton (PDA). A PDA is a tuple
M=(Q,\Sigma,\Theta,\delta,q_{0},\perp,F)
, where
Q
is the set of states,
\Sigma
is the alphabet of the input tape,
\Theta
is the alphabet of the stack,
q_{0}
is the initial state,
\perp\in\Theta
is the initial symbol of the stack, and
F\subseteq Q
is the set of final states. The transition function
\delta
is a map from
Q\times(\Sigma\cup\{1\})\times\Theta
to subsets of
Q\times\Theta^{*}
. The interpretation of
\delta(q,a,x)=\{(p_{1},\gamma_{1}),\ldots,(p_{n},\gamma_{n})\}
is that when
M
is at the state
q
and is reading
a
in the input and
x
is the last symbol on the stack, it can nondeterministically choose to enter in state
p_{i}
and replace the letter
x
with the word
\gamma_{i}\in\Theta^{*}
for some
i\in[1,n]
; in case
\gamma_{i}=1
, we are removing the symbol
x
from the top of the stack (pop operation), otherwise we are pushing some word. Throughout the paper we will describe
\delta
by using the notation
(q,a,x)\mathrel{\hbox{\raisebox{3.44444pt}{$\rule[-6.45831pt]{0.47787pt}{12.91663pt}\rule[-0.23894pt]{12.37634pt}{0.47787pt}\hskip-12.37634pt\raisebox{-6.74446pt}{\makebox[12.37634pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{M}$}}}\hskip-12.37634pt\raisebox{1.96112pt}{\makebox[12.37634pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{}$}}}$}}}(p_{i},\gamma_{i})
to denote that
(p_{i},\gamma_{i})\in\delta(q,a,x)
. Transitions of the form
(q,1,x)\mathrel{\hbox{\raisebox{3.44444pt}{$\rule[-6.45831pt]{0.47787pt}{12.91663pt}\rule[-0.23894pt]{12.37634pt}{0.47787pt}\hskip-12.37634pt\raisebox{-6.74446pt}{\makebox[12.37634pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{M}$}}}\hskip-12.37634pt\raisebox{1.96112pt}{\makebox[12.37634pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{}$}}}$}}}(p_{i},\gamma_{i})
are called
1
-moves. The pushdown automaton
M
is called
deterministic
(DPDA) when for each
(q,a,x)\in Q\times\Sigma\times\Theta
there is at most one
(p,\gamma)\in Q\times\Theta^{*}
such that
(q,a,x)\mathrel{\hbox{\raisebox{3.44444pt}{$\rule[-6.45831pt]{0.47787pt}{12.91663pt}\rule[-0.23894pt]{12.37634pt}{0.47787pt}\hskip-12.37634pt\raisebox{-6.74446pt}{\makebox[12.37634pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{M}$}}}\hskip-12.37634pt\raisebox{1.96112pt}{\makebox[12.37634pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{}$}}}$}}}(p,\gamma)
is a transition, and if there is a
1
-move
(q,1,x)\mathrel{\hbox{\raisebox{3.44444pt}{$\rule[-6.45831pt]{0.47787pt}{12.91663pt}\rule[-0.23894pt]{12.37634pt}{0.47787pt}\hskip-12.37634pt\raisebox{-6.74446pt}{\makebox[12.37634pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{M}$}}}\hskip-12.37634pt\raisebox{1.96112pt}{\makebox[12.37634pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{}$}}}$}}}(p,\gamma)
then
\delta(q,a,x)
is empty for all
a\in\Sigma
.

The dynamics of
M
is described by the
instantaneous description
that defines how the configuration of the machine evolves at each step of the computation. We write
(q,\xi x)\mathrel{\hbox{\raisebox{3.44444pt}{$\rule[-6.45831pt]{0.47787pt}{12.91663pt}\rule[-1.23135pt]{12.37634pt}{0.47787pt}\hskip-12.37634pt\rule[0.75348pt]{12.37634pt}{0.47787pt}\hskip-12.37634pt\raisebox{-7.73688pt}{\makebox[12.37634pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{M}$}}}\hskip-12.37634pt\raisebox{2.95354pt}{\makebox[12.37634pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{a}$}}}$}}}(p,\xi\gamma)
(sometimes
(q,\xi x)\mathrel{\hbox{\raisebox{3.44444pt}{$\rule[-6.45831pt]{0.47787pt}{12.91663pt}\rule[-1.23135pt]{8.33759pt}{0.47787pt}\hskip-8.33759pt\rule[0.75348pt]{8.33759pt}{0.47787pt}\hskip-8.33759pt\raisebox{-7.81464pt}{\makebox[8.33759pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{\delta}$}}}\hskip-8.33759pt\raisebox{2.95354pt}{\makebox[8.33759pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{a}$}}}$}}}(p,\xi\gamma)
when we want to emphasize which transition function we are considering) if we have
(q,a,x)\mathrel{\hbox{\raisebox{3.44444pt}{$\rule[-6.45831pt]{0.47787pt}{12.91663pt}\rule[-0.23894pt]{12.37634pt}{0.47787pt}\hskip-12.37634pt\raisebox{-6.74446pt}{\makebox[12.37634pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{M}$}}}\hskip-12.37634pt\raisebox{1.96112pt}{\makebox[12.37634pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{}$}}}$}}}(p,\gamma)
. If there is a sequence

(q_{1},\xi_{1})\mathrel{\hbox{\raisebox{3.44444pt}{$\rule[-6.45831pt]{0.47787pt}{12.91663pt}\rule[-1.23135pt]{12.37634pt}{0.47787pt}\hskip-12.37634pt\rule[0.75348pt]{12.37634pt}{0.47787pt}\hskip-12.37634pt\raisebox{-7.73688pt}{\makebox[12.37634pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{M}$}}}\hskip-12.37634pt\raisebox{3.95354pt}{\makebox[12.37634pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{a_{1}}$}}}$}}}(q_{2},\xi_{2})\mathrel{\hbox{\raisebox{3.44444pt}{$\rule[-6.45831pt]{0.47787pt}{12.91663pt}\rule[-1.23135pt]{12.37634pt}{0.47787pt}\hskip-12.37634pt\rule[0.75348pt]{12.37634pt}{0.47787pt}\hskip-12.37634pt\raisebox{-7.73688pt}{\makebox[12.37634pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{M}$}}}\hskip-12.37634pt\raisebox{3.95354pt}{\makebox[12.37634pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{a_{2}}$}}}$}}}\ldots\mathrel{\hbox{\raisebox{3.44444pt}{$\rule[-6.45831pt]{0.47787pt}{12.91663pt}\rule[-1.23135pt]{13.24158pt}{0.47787pt}\hskip-13.24158pt\rule[0.75348pt]{13.24158pt}{0.47787pt}\hskip-13.24158pt\raisebox{-7.73688pt}{\makebox[13.24158pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{M}$}}}\hskip-13.24158pt\raisebox{3.95354pt}{\makebox[13.24158pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{a_{n}}$}}}$}}}(q_{n+1},\xi_{n+1})

for some
w=a_{1}\ldots a_{n}\in\Sigma^{*}
, we write
(q_{1},\xi_{1})\mathrel{\hbox{\raisebox{3.44444pt}{$\rule[-6.45831pt]{0.47787pt}{12.91663pt}\rule[-1.23135pt]{12.37634pt}{0.47787pt}\hskip-12.37634pt\rule[0.75348pt]{12.37634pt}{0.47787pt}\hskip-12.37634pt\raisebox{-7.73688pt}{\makebox[12.37634pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{M}$}}}\hskip-12.37634pt\raisebox{2.95354pt}{\makebox[12.37634pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{w}$}}}$}}}(q_{n+1},\xi_{n+1})
.
The
language accepted by

M
is the set
L(M)=\{w\in\Sigma^{*}:(q_{0},\perp)\mathrel{\hbox{\raisebox{3.44444pt}{$\rule[-6.45831pt]{0.47787pt}{12.91663pt}\rule[-1.23135pt]{12.37634pt}{0.47787pt}\hskip-12.37634pt\rule[0.75348pt]{12.37634pt}{0.47787pt}\hskip-12.37634pt\raisebox{-7.73688pt}{\makebox[12.37634pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{M}$}}}\hskip-12.37634pt\raisebox{2.95354pt}{\makebox[12.37634pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{w}$}}}$}}}(p,\xi)\mbox{ with }p\in F\}
. One may define the language accepted by
M
by requiring that the stack contains only the initial symbol
\perp
, so, in this case,
L_{e}(M)=\{w\in\Sigma^{*}:(q_{0},\perp)\mathrel{\hbox{\raisebox{3.44444pt}{$\rule[-6.45831pt]{0.47787pt}{12.91663pt}\rule[-1.23135pt]{12.37634pt}{0.47787pt}\hskip-12.37634pt\rule[0.75348pt]{12.37634pt}{0.47787pt}\hskip-12.37634pt\raisebox{-7.73688pt}{\makebox[12.37634pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{M}$}}}\hskip-12.37634pt\raisebox{2.95354pt}{\makebox[12.37634pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{w}$}}}$}}}(p,\perp)\}
. It is not difficult to see, by a standard argument similar to
[
19
, Theorem 5.1 and Theorem 5.2]
, that these two models of computation are equivalent in the sense that these two model of acceptance define the same class of languages. Throughout the paper we will use the first model, although the condition of the stack containing the initial symbol will be used in some later proofs.

Following
[
29
]
we may associate to
M
a
\Sigma\cup\{1\}
-directed graph
\Gamma(M)
, called the
configurations graph
, that is obtained as follows: the set of vertices
V(\Gamma(M))
is the subset of
Q\times\Theta^{*}
containing
(q_{0},\perp)
and all the pairs
(p,\xi)
for which there is a computation
(q_{0},\perp)\mathrel{\hbox{\raisebox{3.44444pt}{$\rule[-6.45831pt]{0.47787pt}{12.91663pt}\rule[-1.23135pt]{12.37634pt}{0.47787pt}\hskip-12.37634pt\rule[0.75348pt]{12.37634pt}{0.47787pt}\hskip-12.37634pt\raisebox{-7.73688pt}{\makebox[12.37634pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{M}$}}}\hskip-12.37634pt\raisebox{2.95354pt}{\makebox[12.37634pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{w}$}}}$}}}(p,\xi)
for some
w\in\Sigma^{*}
, and we draw an oriented edge
(q_{1},\xi_{1})\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$a$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}(q_{2},\xi_{2})
,
a\in\Sigma\cup\{1\}
between two vertices of
V(\Gamma(M))
whenever we have the computation
(q_{1},\xi_{1})\mathrel{\hbox{\raisebox{3.44444pt}{$\rule[-6.45831pt]{0.47787pt}{12.91663pt}\rule[-1.23135pt]{12.37634pt}{0.47787pt}\hskip-12.37634pt\rule[0.75348pt]{12.37634pt}{0.47787pt}\hskip-12.37634pt\raisebox{-7.73688pt}{\makebox[12.37634pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{M}$}}}\hskip-12.37634pt\raisebox{2.95354pt}{\makebox[12.37634pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{a}$}}}$}}}(q_{2},\xi_{2})
for some
a\in\Sigma\cup\{1\}
; note that we may have in general edges labeled with the empty word
1
. This graph may be used as an acceptor for the language accepted by
M
since
L(M)
is formed by all the words that label a walk connecting the initial configuration
(q_{0},\perp)
to a configuration
(p,\xi)
which is in a final state
p\in F
.

3.1.
Inverse configurations graphs, inverse pushdown automata, and inverse-context-free languages

In what follows, we will concentrate our attention on deterministic pushdown automata that are real-time, so without
1
-moves. Pushdown automata without
1
-moves have configurations graphs without edges labeled with the empty word
1
, so it is the natural framework when considering configurations graphs that are inverse
\Sigma
-directed graphs. It is clear that to have
\Gamma(M)
that is a deterministic
\Sigma
-directed graph, we need to assume
M
deterministic. The natural question is when
\Gamma(M)
is also co-deterministic, since in this case we may transform
\Gamma(M)
into an inverse graph
\Gamma(M)^{-}
by simply adding for every edge
(q,\xi x)\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$a$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}(p,\xi\gamma)
the reverse one
(p,\xi\gamma)\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$a^{-1}$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}(q,\xi x)
. In particular, it is of interest to see when
\Gamma(M)^{-}
is the configurations graph of some deterministic PDA. We shall see that this happens if
M
has a reverse transition function, so
M
is a reversible pushdown automaton, see
[
21
]
. Reversibility gives the possibility of stepping the computation back and forth, and it is a central notion in the theory of computation and quantum computing, for the specific case of reversible PDA we refer the reader to
[
21
]
. A reversible pushdown automaton
M=(Q,\Sigma,\Theta,\delta,q_{0},\perp,F)
is characterized by having a reverse transition function
\delta_{R}:Q\times\Sigma\times\Theta\to Q\times\Theta^{*}
such that is able to locally reverse the computation, i.e.,

(q_{1},\xi_{1})\mathrel{\hbox{\raisebox{3.44444pt}{$\rule[-6.45831pt]{0.47787pt}{12.91663pt}\rule[-1.23135pt]{8.33759pt}{0.47787pt}\hskip-8.33759pt\rule[0.75348pt]{8.33759pt}{0.47787pt}\hskip-8.33759pt\raisebox{-7.81464pt}{\makebox[8.33759pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{\delta}$}}}\hskip-8.33759pt\raisebox{2.95354pt}{\makebox[8.33759pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{a}$}}}$}}}(q_{2},\xi_{2})\text{ if and only if }(q_{2},\xi_{2})\mathrel{\hbox{\raisebox{3.44444pt}{$\rule[-6.45831pt]{0.47787pt}{12.91663pt}\rule[-1.23135pt]{13.29272pt}{0.47787pt}\hskip-13.29272pt\rule[0.75348pt]{13.29272pt}{0.47787pt}\hskip-13.29272pt\raisebox{-7.81464pt}{\makebox[13.29272pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{\delta_{R}}$}}}\hskip-13.29272pt\raisebox{2.95354pt}{\makebox[13.29272pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{a}$}}}$}}}(q_{1},\xi_{1}).

We remark that the initial assumption that
M
is real-time is not strict from the language point of view since by
[
21
, Theorem 5]
for every reversible pushdown automaton
N
there is a real-time reversible PDA
M
such that
L(M)=L(N)
. Reversible pushdown automata have a peculiar dynamics since by
[
21
, Fact 3]
no transition changes the length of the stack by more than one. Hence, each transition in
\delta
has one of the following form

(q,a,x)\mathrel{\hbox{\raisebox{3.44444pt}{$\rule[-6.45831pt]{0.47787pt}{12.91663pt}\rule[-0.23894pt]{12.37634pt}{0.47787pt}\hskip-12.37634pt\raisebox{-6.74446pt}{\makebox[12.37634pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{M}$}}}\hskip-12.37634pt\raisebox{1.96112pt}{\makebox[12.37634pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{}$}}}$}}}(p,xy),\,\text{or }(q,a,x)\mathrel{\hbox{\raisebox{3.44444pt}{$\rule[-6.45831pt]{0.47787pt}{12.91663pt}\rule[-0.23894pt]{12.37634pt}{0.47787pt}\hskip-12.37634pt\raisebox{-6.74446pt}{\makebox[12.37634pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{M}$}}}\hskip-12.37634pt\raisebox{1.96112pt}{\makebox[12.37634pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{}$}}}$}}}(p,x),\,\text{ or }(q,a,x)\mathrel{\hbox{\raisebox{3.44444pt}{$\rule[-6.45831pt]{0.47787pt}{12.91663pt}\rule[-0.23894pt]{12.37634pt}{0.47787pt}\hskip-12.37634pt\raisebox{-6.74446pt}{\makebox[12.37634pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{M}$}}}\hskip-12.37634pt\raisebox{1.96112pt}{\makebox[12.37634pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{}$}}}$}}}(p,1)

for some
x,y\in\Theta
, corresponding to the following reverse transitions

(p,a,y)\mathrel{\hbox{\raisebox{3.44444pt}{$\rule[-6.45831pt]{0.47787pt}{12.91663pt}\rule[-0.23894pt]{13.29272pt}{0.47787pt}\hskip-13.29272pt\raisebox{-6.82222pt}{\makebox[13.29272pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{\delta_{R}}$}}}\hskip-13.29272pt\raisebox{1.96112pt}{\makebox[13.29272pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{}$}}}$}}}(q,1),\,\text{or }(p,a,x)\mathrel{\hbox{\raisebox{3.44444pt}{$\rule[-6.45831pt]{0.47787pt}{12.91663pt}\rule[-0.23894pt]{13.29272pt}{0.47787pt}\hskip-13.29272pt\raisebox{-6.82222pt}{\makebox[13.29272pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{\delta_{R}}$}}}\hskip-13.29272pt\raisebox{1.96112pt}{\makebox[13.29272pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{}$}}}$}}}(q,x),\,\text{or }(p,a,z)\mathrel{\hbox{\raisebox{3.44444pt}{$\rule[-6.45831pt]{0.47787pt}{12.91663pt}\rule[-0.23894pt]{13.29272pt}{0.47787pt}\hskip-13.29272pt\raisebox{-6.82222pt}{\makebox[13.29272pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{\delta_{R}}$}}}\hskip-13.29272pt\raisebox{1.96112pt}{\makebox[13.29272pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{}$}}}$}}}(q,zx)

for some
x,y,z\in\Theta
.

To deal with inverse graphs, we need to introduce a new class of PDAs more suitable to consider languages on an involutive alphabet
\widetilde{\Sigma}
.

Definition 3

(Inverse PDA)
.

A real-time DPDA
M=(Q,\widetilde{\Sigma},\Theta,\delta,q_{0},\perp,F)
is called
inverse
if
\delta
may be partitioned into two functions:
\delta^{+}
which is the restriction of
\delta
to
Q\times\Sigma\times\Theta
and
\delta^{-}
which is the restriction of
\delta
to
Q\times\Sigma^{-1}\times\Theta
such that
\delta^{+}
is the reverse transition of
\delta^{-}
and vice versa in the following sense:

(q_{1},\xi_{1})\mathrel{\hbox{\raisebox{3.44444pt}{$\rule[-6.45831pt]{0.47787pt}{12.91663pt}\rule[-1.23135pt]{13.27365pt}{0.47787pt}\hskip-13.27365pt\rule[0.75348pt]{13.27365pt}{0.47787pt}\hskip-13.27365pt\raisebox{-9.3047pt}{\makebox[13.27365pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{\delta^{+}}$}}}\hskip-13.27365pt\raisebox{2.95354pt}{\makebox[13.27365pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{a}$}}}$}}}(q_{2},\xi_{2})\text{ if and only if }(q_{2},\xi_{2})\mathrel{\hbox{\raisebox{3.44444pt}{$\rule[-6.45831pt]{0.47787pt}{12.91663pt}\rule[-1.23135pt]{14.60156pt}{0.47787pt}\hskip-14.60156pt\rule[0.75348pt]{14.60156pt}{0.47787pt}\hskip-14.60156pt\raisebox{-8.12411pt}{\makebox[14.60156pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{\delta^{-}}$}}}\hskip-14.60156pt\raisebox{2.95354pt}{\makebox[14.60156pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{a^{-1}}$}}}$}}}(q_{1},\xi_{1}).

A language
L\subseteq\widetilde{\Sigma}^{*}
is called inverse-context-free if
L=L(M)
for some inverse PDA
M
on
\widetilde{\Sigma}
.

It is clear from the definition and the fact that
\delta^{+},\delta^{-}
are functions, that
\Gamma(M)
is inverse if and only if
M
is an inverse PDA. An important property of inverse PDAs that will be useful later, is the fact that in the configurations graph
\Gamma(M)
there are no configurations with the stack that is empty. Indeed, any computation
(p,x)\mathrel{\hbox{\raisebox{3.44444pt}{$\rule[-6.45831pt]{0.47787pt}{12.91663pt}\rule[-1.23135pt]{12.37634pt}{0.47787pt}\hskip-12.37634pt\rule[0.75348pt]{12.37634pt}{0.47787pt}\hskip-12.37634pt\raisebox{-7.73688pt}{\makebox[12.37634pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{M}$}}}\hskip-12.37634pt\raisebox{2.95354pt}{\makebox[12.37634pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{a}$}}}$}}}(q,1)
should have the reverse computation
(q,1)\mathrel{\hbox{\raisebox{3.44444pt}{$\rule[-6.45831pt]{0.47787pt}{12.91663pt}\rule[-1.23135pt]{14.60156pt}{0.47787pt}\hskip-14.60156pt\rule[0.75348pt]{14.60156pt}{0.47787pt}\hskip-14.60156pt\raisebox{-7.73688pt}{\makebox[14.60156pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{M}$}}}\hskip-14.60156pt\raisebox{2.95354pt}{\makebox[14.60156pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{a^{-1}}$}}}$}}}(p,x)
which is clearly not allowed in the definition. For an invertible PDA
M
let us set
M^{+}=(Q,\Sigma,\Theta,\delta^{+},q_{0},\perp,F)
.
There is a strict relationship between reversible and invertible PDAs. Indeed, any reversibile real-time PDA
N=(Q,\Sigma,\Theta,\delta,q_{0},\perp,F)
with reverse transition function
\delta_{R}
may be “extended” to an invertible PDA
\widetilde{N}=(Q,\widetilde{\Sigma},\Theta,\widetilde{\delta},q_{0},\perp,F)
on the involutive alphabet
\widetilde{\Sigma}
, by extending the map
\delta
to the map
\widetilde{\delta}
defined by
\widetilde{\delta}(q,a^{-1},x)=(p,\gamma)
whenever
\delta_{R}(q,a,x)=(p,\gamma)
. It immediately follows from the definitions that
\widetilde{N}
is inverse,
\widetilde{N}^{+}=N
, and
\Gamma(N)^{-}=\Gamma(\widetilde{N})
. By this last construction we deduce that a PDA
M
is inverse if and only if
M^{+}
is reversible. Moreover, it is evident from the language theoretic point of view, that inverse PDAs and reversible PDAs are essentially the same class of PDAs, since for any language
L
accepted by a real-time reversible PDA
N
, the associated inverse PDA
\widetilde{N}
has the property
L=L(\widetilde{N})\cap\Sigma^{*}
.

4.
Context-free inverse graphs

In their paper
[
28
]
, Muller and Schupp called a finitely generated group
G
presented by
\langle A\mid\mathcal{R}\rangle
context-free if the word problem
WP(G;A)
is a context-free language. With our notation, this language coincides with the set
L(\Cay(G;A),\mathds{1}_{G})
of words labeling a circuit starting and ending at the group identity, so the following definition is a natural generalization for a generic inverse graph.

Definition 4
.

A rooted inverse graph
(\Gamma,x_{0})
is called context-free if
L(\Gamma,x_{0})
is a context-free language.

The aim of this section is to show that for an inverse graph
\Gamma
being context-free is equivalent to be context-free in the sense of Muller-Schupp that is also equivalent to be the configurations graph of an inverse PDA.
To prove this characterization we first need to show that the rooted graph
(\Gamma,x_{0})
is a context-free graph in the sense of Muller and Schupp, a fact that does not immediately follow from the context-freeness of the language
L(\Gamma,x_{0})
. This fact has been already proved in
[
7
, Theorem 4.6]
in the complete case (fully deterministic in their notation), we generalize this fact for any context-free inverse graph. First we need to make some geometric considerations that arise from the fact that the language accepted by such a graph is generated by a context-free grammar
\mathcal{G}
. From now on we assume that the grammar
\mathcal{G}=(V,A,P,S)
generating
L(\Gamma,x_{0})
is in Chomsky normal form. To visualize the productions applied to the language
L(\Gamma,x_{0})
, it is useful to introduce the notion of
\mathcal{G}
-triangulation and
\mathcal{G}
-decoration of the inverse graph
\Gamma
. This is another more convenient way to adapt the notion of
K
-triangulation, introduced in
[
28
,
29
]
for Cayley graphs, to the general case of an inverse graph. Similar notions have been also considered in several other papers, see for instance
[
7
,
30
,
36
]
.

Figure 1.
The first step and the generic
i
-th step of the triangulation of a circuit
p=x_{0}\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$w$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}x_{0}
.

Definition 5

(
\mathcal{G}
-triangulation and
\mathcal{G}
-decoration)
.

Let
w\in L(\Gamma,x_{0})
, and let
p=x_{0}\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$w$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}x_{0}
be the corresponding circuit in
\Gamma
. By Remark
1
, any production
S\Rightarrow^{*}_{\mathcal{C}}w
can be obtained by first applying all the non-terminal rules followed by the terminal ones. A
\mathcal{G}
-triangulation of
p=x_{0}\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$w$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}x_{0}
associated to the production
S\Rightarrow^{*}_{\mathcal{C}}w
, is obtained by adding to
p
new edges with labels in the variable set
V
, let us call them
V
-edges. Suppose the first production of
S\Rightarrow^{*}_{\mathcal{C}}w
is
S\Rightarrow_{\mathcal{C}}XY
, where
X\Rightarrow^{*}_{\mathcal{C}}w_{1}
,
Y\Rightarrow^{*}_{\mathcal{C}}w_{2}
with
w=w_{1}w_{2}
. In the first step we add to
p
the two new edges
e_{1},g_{1}
with
\iota(e_{1})=\tau(g_{1})=x_{0}
,
\tau(e_{1})=\iota(g_{1})=x
where
x\in p
is the vertex for which
p
factorizes as
p=x_{0}\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$w_{1}$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}x\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$w_{2}$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}x_{0}
. We also label
e_{1},g_{1}
by
\lambda(e_{1})=X
,
\lambda(g_{1})=Y
and we say that the edges
e_{1},g_{1}
will give rise to the walks
x_{0}\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$w_{1}$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}x
,
x\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$w_{2}$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}x_{0}
, respectively, see Fig.
1
. Now, iteratively, at step
i
of the production

S\Rightarrow^{i-1}_{\mathcal{C}}\alpha_{i-1}X_{i-1}\beta_{i-1}\Rightarrow_{\mathcal{C}}\alpha_{i-1}WH\beta_{i-1}\Rightarrow_{\mathcal{C}}^{*}w

we have applied rule
X_{i-1}\Rightarrow_{\mathcal{C}}WH
, then in
p
we have already an edge
h_{i-1}
with
\lambda(h_{i-1})=X_{i-1}
which will give rise to the subwalk
q=\iota(h_{i-1})\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$u$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}\tau(h_{i-1})
of
p
and rule
X_{i-1}\Rightarrow_{\mathcal{C}}WH
corresponds to the factorization
\iota(h_{i-1})\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$u_{1}$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}y\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$u_{2}$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}\tau(h_{i-1})
of
q
given by the productions
W\Rightarrow_{\mathcal{C}}^{*}u_{1}
,
H\Rightarrow_{\mathcal{C}}^{*}u_{2}
,
u=u_{1}u_{2}
. Then, we create two new edges
e_{i},g_{i}
with
\iota(e_{i})=\iota(h_{i-1}),\tau(g_{i})=\tau(h_{i-1})
,
\tau(e_{i})=\iota(g_{i})=y
,
\lambda(e_{i})=W
,
\lambda(g_{i})=H
. We say that
e_{i},g_{i}
are generated from
h_{i-1}
. The process of triangulation stops when we apply the first terminal rule.
The
\mathcal{G}
-decoration of
\Gamma
is the
(\widetilde{A}\cup V)
-directed graph
\Gamma_{\mathcal{G}}
obtained from
\Gamma
by adding all the new
V
-edges involved in all the possible
\mathcal{G}
-triangulations of all the circuits starting at
x_{0}
.

Throughout the paper the following constant

K=\max\left\{\min\{|u|:u\in L(X),X\in V\}\right\}

will play a fundamental role. The following lemma can be found in
[
28
]
in the case of Cayley graphs of finitely generated groups, but also in
[
7
, Lemma 4.5]
with a similar spirit.

Lemma 6
.

Let
e
be a
V
-edge of
\Gamma_{\mathcal{G}}
with
\lambda(e)=X\in V
. Then, for any
z\in L(X)
, there is a walk
\iota(e)\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$z$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}\tau(e)
in
\Gamma
. In particular, the distance in
\Gamma
between the initial and terminal vertex of
e
satisfies the bound
d(\iota(e),\tau(e))\leq K
.

Proof.

By definition of
\mathcal{G}
-decoration there are derivations
S\Rightarrow_{\mathcal{C}}^{*}W_{1}XW_{2}
,
W_{1},W_{2}\in V^{*}
,
W_{1}\Rightarrow_{\mathcal{C}}^{*}w_{1},W_{2}\Rightarrow_{\mathcal{C}}^{*}w_{2},X\Rightarrow_{\mathcal{C}}^{*}u
,
w_{1},w_{2},u\in\widetilde{A}^{*}
, corresponding to a circuit
x_{0}\smash{\mathop{\relbar\joinrel\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}\limits^{w_{1}uw_{2}}}x_{0}
in
\Gamma
where
x_{0}\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$w_{1}$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}\iota(e)
,
\tau(e)\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$w_{2}$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}x_{0}
are two subwalks. Since
z\in L(X)
there is a derivation
X\Rightarrow_{\mathcal{C}}^{*}z
, and so also a derivation
S\Rightarrow_{\mathcal{C}}^{*}w_{1}zw_{2}
. Thus, since
\Gamma
is deterministic and
w_{1}zw_{2}\in L(\Gamma,x_{0})
we deduce that
\iota(e)\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$z$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}\tau(e)
is also a walk in
\Gamma
.

From this lemma we deduce that the decorated graph
\Gamma_{\mathcal{G}}
is deterministic: if

are two new edges with
\iota(e)=\iota(g)
and
\lambda(e)=\lambda(g)=X
, then for any

\iota(e)\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$z$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}\tau(e)
,
\iota(g)\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$z$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}\tau(g)
in
\Gamma
, hence the determinism and condition

yield
\tau(e)=\tau(g)
.
Henceforth,
d(x,y)
denotes the distance in the inverse graph
\Gamma
, and put
\|y\|_{x_{0}}=d(x_{0},v)
. In
[
29
]
the Muller and Schupp have introduced the central notion of end-cone together with the idea of end-isomorphism of such subgraphs. We briefly recall them. For a vertex
v
with
\|v\|_{x_{0}}=n
, the end-cone at
v
is the (inverse) subgraph
\Gamma(v,x_{0})
that is the connected component of
\Gamma\setminus D_{n-1}(x_{0})
containing
v
, and we denote by
\Delta(v,x_{0})=\{y\in\Gamma(v,x_{0}):\|y\|_{x_{0}}=n\}
the set of frontier points of
\Gamma(v,x_{0})
. An
end-isomorphism

\psi:\Gamma(v,x_{0})\to\Gamma(w,x_{0})
between the end-cones
\Gamma(v,x_{0}),\Gamma(w,x_{0})
is an isomorphism of inverse graphs with the property of preserving the frontier points:
\psi(\Delta(v,x_{0}))=\Delta(w,x_{0})
. The rooted graph
(\Gamma,x_{0})
is called
a context-free graph
if up to end-isomorphism there are finitely many end-cones. In
[
29
]
it is shown that the graph
(\Gamma(M),x_{0})
of the instantaneous descriptions of a pushdown automaton is a context-free graph, and conversely given a context free graph
(\Gamma,x_{0})
there is a pushdown automaton

such that
(\Gamma(M),x_{0})
is isomorphic to
(\Gamma,x_{0})
.

Now we show that an inverse graph
\Gamma
whose accepted language

is context-free, is also a context-free graph in the sense of Muller and Schupp. The strategy of the proof is to show that up to end-isomorphism there are finitely many end-cones
\Gamma(v,x_{0})
by showing that such a graph is completely determined by the finite decorated subgraph

\Delta^{C}(v,x_{0})=\langle x\in V(\Gamma_{\mathcal{G}}):\exists y\in\Delta(v,x_{0})\mbox{ with }d(x,y)\leq C\rangle_{\Gamma_{\mathcal{G}}}

of the decorated graph
\Gamma_{\mathcal{G}}
, where
C\geq 1
is a constant independent from
v
. To this end, we need to introduce some notations and concepts that are useful in the proof of such fact. We say that a walk

contained in
\Gamma(v,x_{0})
is

-external

is clear from the context, if the only vertices belonging to

are the endpoints

Lemma 7
.

Let

Consider a

\mathcal{T}
be the set of

\displaystyle x_{0}\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$v_{1}$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}x=x_{0}\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$a_{1}$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}x_{1}\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$a_{2}$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}x_{2}\ldots\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$a_{n}$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}x_{n}

\displaystyle x_{0}\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$v_{2}^{-1}$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}y=y_{0}\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$b_{1}$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}y_{1}\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$b_{2}$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}y_{2}\ldots\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$b_{n}$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}y_{n}

with

. In the first step of the
\mathcal{G}
-triangulation we have two
V

e_{1},g_{1}
giving rise to the subwalks
\ell(x_{0},z),\ell(z,x_{0})

s

\|s\|_{x_{0}}\geq n

z\notin p
, for if we would have
\|z\|_{x_{0}}\geq n

6

\ell
containing
p
. This implies that the set
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}

V

\mathcal{T}

\iota(e)\in\{x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}\}

\tau(e)\in\{y_{0},\ldots,y_{n}\}

\mathcal{T}^{\prime}

\hat{e}

\hat{e}

\mathcal{T}

y_{j^{\prime}}=\tau(e^{\prime})\in\{y_{0},\ldots,y_{n}\}

\hat{e}
.
Note that

\|\iota(\hat{e})\|_{x_{0}}<n-K

We say that a morphism of
(\widetilde{A}\cup V)
-directed graphs
\psi:\Delta^{C}(v,x_{0})\to\Delta^{C}(w,x_{0})
is
norm-preserving
if for any
z_{1},z_{2}\in\Delta^{C}(v,x_{0})
with
\|z_{1}\|_{x_{0}}\leq\|z_{2}\|_{x_{0}}
implies
\|\psi(z_{1})\|_{x_{0}}\leq\|\psi(z_{2})\|_{x_{0}}
. Note that we have that if
\|z_{1}\|_{x_{0}}=\|z_{2}\|_{x_{0}}
then
\|\psi(z_{1})\|_{x_{0}}=\|\psi(z_{2})\|_{x_{0}}
. In case
\psi:\Delta^{C}(v,x_{0})\to\Delta^{C}(w,x_{0})
is an isomorphism of

-directed graphs we require that also
\psi^{-1}
is norm-preserving. Therefore, in this case, since

is finite we may conclude that for any

, i.e.
\psi(\Delta(v,x_{0}))=\Delta(w,x_{0})
.

Lemma 8
.

Put
C\geq 2K
. Let
v,w
be two vertices with
\|v\|_{x_{0}},\|w\|_{x_{0}}>K
such that there is a norm-preserving isomorphism
\psi:\Delta^{C}(v,x_{0})\to\Delta^{C}(w,x_{0})
. Then, for any external walk
x^{\prime}\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$u$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}x^{\prime\prime}

\Gamma(v,x_{0})

\psi(x^{\prime})\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$u$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}\psi(x^{\prime\prime})

\Gamma(w,x_{0})

Proof.

To obtain a contradiction assume that there is an external walk
x^{\prime}\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$u$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}x^{\prime\prime}\subseteq\Gamma(v,x_{0})
for which there is no external walk
\psi(x^{\prime})\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$u$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}\psi(x^{\prime\prime})

\Gamma(w,x_{0})

\Gamma(w,x_{0})

\psi^{-1}(y^{\prime})\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$u^{\prime}$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}\psi^{-1}(y^{\prime\prime})

\Gamma(v,x_{0})

\pi_{1}=x_{1}\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$u$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}x_{k}=x_{1}\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$u_{1}$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}x_{2}\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$u_{2}$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}\ldots x_{k-1}\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$u_{k-1}$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}x_{k}

in
\Gamma(v,x_{0})
is external, but there is no external walk with endpoints

and labeled by

in
\Gamma(w,x_{0})

z\in\Delta(v,x_{0})

, then there is a subwalk
z^{\prime}\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$v^{\prime}$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}y\subseteq\Gamma(v,x_{0})

. Thus, from this observation it follows that there are two vertices
x,y\in\Delta(v,x_{0})

x\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$w_{1}$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}x_{1},x_{k}\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$w_{2}$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}y\subseteq\Gamma(v,x_{0})\cap\Delta^{C}(v,x_{0})

x_{0}\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$v_{1}$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}x
,
y\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$v_{2}$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}x_{0}

\ell=x_{0}\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$v_{1}$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}x\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$w_{1}$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}x_{1}\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$u$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}x_{k}\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$w_{2}$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}y\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$v_{2}$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}x_{0}

Now, by applying Lemma

\hat{e}=\iota(\hat{e})\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$X$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}\tau(\hat{e})

\ell(\iota(\hat{e}),\tau(\hat{e}))=\iota(\hat{e})\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$v_{1}^{\prime}$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}x\smash{\mathop{\relbar\joinrel\relbar\joinrel\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}\limits^{w_{1}uw_{2}}}y\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$v_{2}^{\prime}$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}\tau(\hat{e})

x\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$v_{1}^{\prime-1}$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}\iota(\hat{e})\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$X$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}\tau(\hat{e})\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$v_{2}^{\prime-1}$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}y

\Delta^{C}(v,x_{0})

\psi(x)\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$v_{1}^{\prime-1}$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}\psi(\iota(\hat{e}))\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$X$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}\psi(\tau(\hat{e}))\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$v_{2}^{\prime-1}$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}\psi(y)

Proposition 6
.

. Let
v,w
be two vertices with

\Gamma(v,x_{0}),\Gamma(\psi(v),x_{0})

Before proving the proposition, we first show that the following property holds

P)

\Gamma(v,x_{0})

\psi(v_{1})\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$w$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}\psi(v_{2})

\psi^{-1}(z_{1})\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$w$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}\psi^{-1}(z_{2})

\Gamma(v,x_{0})

\psi^{-1}(z_{1}),\psi^{-1}(z_{2})\in\Delta(v,x_{0})

accordingly as

(1)

p=v_{1}\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$w$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}v_{2}=\gamma_{1}\pi_{1}\gamma_{2}\pi_{2}\ldots\gamma_{k}\pi_{k}\gamma_{k+1}

\gamma_{i}

\Delta^{C}(v,x_{0})

2

P)
by induction on the number

p=\gamma_{1}

\Delta^{C}(v,x_{0})

\psi(p)

\Delta^{C}(\psi(v),x_{0})

\psi(v_{1}),\psi(v_{2})\in\Delta(\psi(v),x_{0})

\pi_{1}=x^{\prime}\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$u$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}x^{\prime\prime}

\psi(x^{\prime})\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$u$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}\psi(x^{\prime\prime})

\psi(\pi_{1})

\Gamma(\psi(v),x_{0})

p

p_{2}=\delta_{1}\gamma_{2}\pi_{2}\ldots\gamma_{k}\pi_{k}\gamma_{k+1}

\Gamma(v,x_{0})\cap\Delta^{C}(v,x_{0})

\iota(\delta_{1})\in\Delta(v,x_{0})

\tau(\delta_{1})=\iota(\gamma_{2})

\psi(p_{1})=\psi(\gamma_{1})\psi(\pi_{1})\psi(\delta_{1}^{-1})

\psi(p_{2})=\psi(\delta_{1})\psi(\pi_{2}\ldots\gamma_{k}\pi_{k}\gamma_{k+1})

\Gamma(\psi(v),x_{0})

\Delta(\psi(v),x_{0})

\Gamma(v,x_{0})

\Gamma(\psi(v),x_{0})
. Firstly note that
\psi
is a bijection between the frontier points
\Delta(v,x_{0}),\Delta(\psi(v),x_{0})
, so we define
\varphi:\Gamma(v,x_{0})\to\Gamma(\psi(v),x_{0})
to be equal to
\psi
on
\Delta(v,x_{0})
. Now, for any vertex
y\in\Gamma(v,x_{0})
let
v_{1}\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$u_{1}$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}y\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$u_{2}$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}v_{2}
in
\Gamma(v,x_{0})
be a walk with
v_{1},v_{2}\in\Delta(v,x_{0})
. By property
P)
there is walk
\psi(v_{1})\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$u_{1}$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}z\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$u_{1}$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}\psi(v_{2})
in
\Gamma(\psi(v),x_{0})
with
\psi(v_{1}),\psi(v_{2})\in\Delta(\psi(v),x_{0})
. Now, define
\varphi
by putting
\varphi(y)=z
. This is a well-defined morphism. Indeed, let
v_{1}^{\prime}\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$s_{1}$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}y\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$s_{2}$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}v_{2}^{\prime}
be another walk for some
v_{1}^{\prime},v_{2}^{\prime}\in\Delta(v,x_{0})
, then using property
P)
applied to the walk
v_{1}\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$u_{1}$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}y\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$s_{1}^{-1}$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}v_{1}^{\prime}
, we conclude that
\psi(v_{1})\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$u_{1}$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}z\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$s_{1}^{-1}$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}\psi(v_{1}^{\prime})
is also a walk in
\Gamma(\psi(v),x_{0})
, thus by the determinism of
\Gamma
we get that
\varphi(y)
does not depend on the chosen walk. It is now routine to check that
\varphi:\Gamma(v,x_{0})\to\Gamma(\psi(v),x_{0})
is a morphism of inverse graphs that is a bijection by the second part of property
P)
.
∎

We have the following characterization that extends and generalizes
[
7
, Theorem 4.6]
to the non-complete case.

Proposition 7
.

For an inverse graph
\Gamma
the following conditions are equivalent:

(1)

for any non-empty finite set
F\subseteq V(\Gamma)
the language
L(\Gamma,x_{0},F)
is context-free;

(2)

the language
L(\Gamma,x_{0})
is context-free;

(3)

the rooted graph
(\Gamma,x_{0})
is context-free in the sense of Muller-Schupp;

(4)

there exists an inverse PDA
M
with initial state
q_{0}
and initial stack symbol
\perp
such that the rooted configurations graph
(\Gamma(M),(q_{0},\perp))
is isomorphic to
(\Gamma,x_{0})
;

(5)

for any vertex
y_{0}\in V(\Gamma)
, the rooted graph
(\Gamma,y_{0})
is context-free in the sense of Muller-Schupp.

Proof.

(1)\Rightarrow(2)
. It follows by taking
F=\{x_{0}\}
.

(2)\Rightarrow(3)
. For a fixed constant
C\geq 2K
, up to norm-preserving isomorphism, there are finitely many decorated subgraphs
\Delta^{C}(v,x_{0})
of
\Gamma_{\mathcal{G}}
. Since there a finitely many end-cones
\Gamma(v,x_{0})
with
\|v\|_{x_{0}}\leq K
, then by Proposition
6
we deduce that there are finitely many end-cones up to end-isomorphism. Hence, the graph
(\Gamma,x_{0})
is context-free in the sense of Muller-Schupp.

(3)\Rightarrow(4)
. In the proof of
[
29
, Lemma 2.3]
the authors explain a way to associate a (canonical) pushdown automaton
M
to the context-free graph
(\Gamma,x_{0})
in such a way that the graph of transitions
\Gamma(M)
rooted at the initial configuration
(q_{0},\perp)
is isomorphic (as rooted
\widetilde{A}
-directed graphs) to
(\Gamma,x_{0})
. Since
\Gamma
is inverse we have that also
\Gamma(M)
is inverse, hence
M
is an inverse PDA.

(4)\Rightarrow(5)
. It follows from
[
29
, Lemma 2.5]
.

(5)\Rightarrow(3)
. Trivial.

(3)\Rightarrow(1)
. Since
[
7
, Theorem 4.2]
holds for any connected involutive graph that is context-free in the sense of Muller-Schupp, we have that for any
y\in F
the language
L(\Gamma,x_{0},y)
is context-free. Since the (finite) union of context-free languages is also context-free
[
19
]
, we conclude that

L(\Gamma,x_{0},F)=\bigcup_{y\in F}L(\Gamma,x_{0},y)

is also context-free.
∎

This characterization settles the name context-free for an inverse graph since such a graph can be either seen (rooted at any vertex) as an acceptor of a context-free language or as a configurations graph of an inverse PDA or, more geometrically, being context-free in the sense of Muller-Schupp.

5.
Context-free inverse graphs are quasi-isometric to a tree

We now show that context-free inverse graphs have a tree-like shape in the sense that they are quasi-isometric to a tree. We recall that a quasi-isometry between the two metric spaces
(X_{1},d_{1}),(X_{2},d_{2})
is a map
\psi:X_{1}\to X_{2}
for which there are positive constants
k,K,c,C
such that for all
x,y\in X_{1}
the following inequalities

kd_{1}(x,y)-c\leq d_{2}(\psi(x),\psi(y))\leq Kd_{1}(x,y)+C

hold, and
\psi
is almost surjective, in the sense that there exists a constant
N
such that for
z\in X_{2}
there is some
x\in X_{1}
for which
d(\psi(x),z)\leq N
holds. The idea of tree-like may be reformulated using several other notions, see for instance
[
16
, Proposition 3.1]
,
[
1
, Theorem 4.7]
. We briefly recall some of them and we add another equivalence that will be useful later.

A geodesic polygon
P
of
\Gamma
is a sequence
P=p_{1}\ldots p_{n}
,
n\geq 2
, of geodesics walks with
\iota(p_{i+1})=\tau(p_{i})
for
1\leq i\leq n-1
, and
\iota(p_{1})=\tau(p_{n})
. Each walk
p_{i}
forms one side of
P
. Let
\delta>0
be a constant, we say that
P
is
\delta
-thin if for all
i\in[1,n]
, and for all vertices
v\in p_{i}
, there exists a vertex
w\in p_{j}
for some
j\neq i
such that
d(w,v)\leq\delta
. In case all geodesic polygons of
\Gamma
are
\delta
-thin, we say that
\Gamma
is polygon
\delta
-hyperbolic, or simply polygon hyperbolic. This notion is a special case of Rips condition for
\delta
-hyperbolic spaces.

For a graph
\Gamma
and a partition
\mathcal{P}
of the vertex set
V(\Gamma)
, we may define the undirected graph
\Gamma/\mathcal{P}
as the simple graph having as set of vertices
\mathcal{P}
and two different subsets
S_{1},S_{2}\in\mathcal{P}
are connected by an edge if there are
u_{1}\in S_{1},u_{2}\in S_{2}
and an edge
u_{1}\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}u_{2}\in\Gamma
. In case there is some integer
N
such that
\delta_{\Gamma}(S)\leq N
for all
S\in\mathcal{P}
, we say that
\mathcal{P}
has
uniform diameter
. A partition
\mathcal{P}
such that
\Gamma/\mathcal{P}
is a tree, is called a
strong tree decomposition
.
The
cone
of a vertex
v
with respect to
x_{0}
is the induced subgraph obtained by all the vertices
x
that are in any geodesic connecting
x_{0}
with
x
and containing
v
, i.e.,

C(v,x_{0})=\left\langle x\in V(\Gamma):\|x\|_{x_{0}}=\|v\|_{x_{0}}+d(v,x)\right\rangle_{\Gamma}

A central notion for simple graphs is that of
tree decomposition
, see for instance
[
13
]
. For us a tree decomposition of
\Gamma
is a pair
(T,\mathcal{V})
where
T
is a simple graph that is a tree, and
\mathcal{V}=\{V_{t}\}_{t\in T}
is a collection of subsets
V_{t}\subseteq V(\Gamma)
indexed by the vertices of
T
satisfying the following properties:

T1)

\bigcup_{t\in T}V_{t}=V(\Gamma)
;

T2)

for every edge
e\in E(\Gamma)
, there is some
t\in T
such that
\iota(e),\tau(e)\in V_{t}
;

T3)

if
V_{t}
and
V_{s}
both contain a vertex
v
, then all nodes
V_{r}
of the tree in the (unique) path between
V_{t}
and
V_{s}
contain
v
as well.

If there is some uniform constant
N\geq 1
such that
\delta_{\Gamma}(V_{t})\leq N
for all
t\in T
, we say that
(T,\mathcal{V})
is a tree decomposition with
uniform diameter
. Since
\Gamma
is an inverse
\widetilde{A}
-graph with
A
finite, the out-degree of each vertex is finite, so any subgraph with finite diameter is finite, thus the condition that
(T,\mathcal{V})
has uniform diameter, implies that each subset
V_{t}
is uniformly bounded by some constant
C
. Hence, in the notation of
[
12
]
,
(T,\mathcal{V})
is a tree decomposition of treewidth
C
.
The following proposition characterizes tree-like inverse graphs in terms of all the previous definitions.

Proposition 8
.

For a connected inverse graph
\Gamma
, the following conditions are equivalent:

(1)

\Gamma
is quasi-isometric to a tree;

(2)

\Gamma
is polygon hyperbolic;

(3)

there is a strong tree decomposition
\mathcal{P}
of uniform diameter;

(4)

there exists a constant
\delta\geq 1
such that if
x_{0}\in V(\Gamma)\setminus D_{\delta}(v)
and
x\in C(v,x_{0})
, then there is no walk in
\Gamma\setminus D_{\delta}(v)
connecting
x_{0}
to
x
;

(5)

\Gamma
has a tree decomposition with uniform diameter;

Proof.

Conditions
(1)-(4)
are equivalent by
[
16
, Proposition 3.1]
.

(3)\Rightarrow(5)
. Take
T=\Gamma/\mathcal{P}
and for
t\in T
, let
S_{t}
be the corresponding subset of vertices. Clearly property T1) is already satisfied, however, to satisfy T2) it is enough to add to each
S_{t}\in\mathcal{P}
the adjacent vertices, so we augment each
S_{t}
by considering

S^{\prime}_{t}=S_{t}\cup\{v:\mbox{ there is an edge }e\in E(\Gamma)\mbox{ with }v=\tau(e),\mbox{ and }\iota(e)\in S_{t}\}

in this way properties T1) and T2) are both satisfied. Furthermore, for any two adjacent vertices
t_{1},t_{2}\in T
, the set
S^{\prime}_{t_{1}}\cap S^{\prime}_{t_{2}}
is formed by pairs of vertices
v_{1},v_{2}
such that there is an edge
v_{1}\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}v_{2}
connecting
v_{1}\in S_{t_{1}}
with
v_{2}\in S_{t_{2}}
. Hence, the only vertices
r\in T
such that
S^{\prime}_{t_{1}}\cap S^{\prime}_{t_{2}}\subseteq S^{\prime}_{r}
are
r=t_{1},t_{2}
, so T3) is satisfied, and this concludes the proof that
(T,\{S^{\prime}_{t}\}_{t\in T})
is a tree decomposition. Now, it is straightforward to verify that
\delta_{\Gamma}(S_{t})\leq N
for all
S\in\mathcal{P}
implies that
\delta_{\Gamma}(S_{t})\leq N+2
, hence
(T,\{S^{\prime}_{t}\}_{t\in T})
is a tree decomposition with uniform diameter.

(5)\Rightarrow(2)
. Let
(T,\{V_{t}\}_{t\in T})
be a tree decomposition with uniform diameter
N
and let
P=p_{1},\ldots,p_{n}
be a geodesic polygon of
\Gamma
. Let us show that
P
is
2N
-thin, so let
v\in p_{i}
be a fixed vertex, and let us show that the distance of
v
from the rest of the other walks is at most
2N
. For each vertex
v
of the walk
p_{i}
, let
T(v)=\{t\in T:v\in V_{t}\}
. By condition T3),
T(v)
has the following closure property: for any
x,y\in T(v)
all the vertices in
T
in the geodesic connecting
x
to
y
are contained in
T(v)
, hence
T(v)
may be visualized as a subtree of
T
. Now, note that if
v,w
are two adjacent vertices of
\Gamma
,
T(v)\cap T(w)\neq\emptyset
, thus the walk
p_{i}
induces a subtree
T(p_{i})
of
T
obtained by taking the union of
T(v)
for each
v\in p_{i}
. Let
\pi=p_{i+1}\ldots p_{n}p_{1}\ldots p_{i-1}
be the walk obtained by composing all the walks of
P
except
p_{i}
. Since
\pi
and
p_{i}
have the same endpoints
x=\iota(p_{i})=\tau(\pi)
,
y=\iota(\pi)=\tau(p_{i})
, then
T(\pi)
and
T(p_{i})
are two subtrees of
T
containing both
T(x),T(y)
. It is not difficult to show that we may choose a walk
q=t_{1}\ldots t_{s}\ldots t_{k}
in
T(p_{i})
that is compatible with
p_{i}
in the following sense: there is a map
f
from the vertices of
q
to the vertices of
p_{i}
such that
f(t_{1})=x\in V_{t_{1}}
,
f(t_{k})=y\in V_{t_{k}}
, and if
t_{s},t_{s+1}
are two consecutive vertices of
q

with
f(t_{s})\in V_{t_{s}}
, then
f(t_{s+1})\in V_{t_{s+1}}
and either
f(t_{s+1})=f(t_{s})
, or there is an edge
f(t_{s+1})\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}f(t_{s})
belonging to
p_{i}
. Note that
f
is surjective, and let
t_{j}\in q
be a vertex with
f(t_{j})=v\in V_{t_{j}}
. Since
T
is a tree,
q
contains (as a subgraph) the geodesic
q^{\prime}
connecting
t_{1}
with
t_{k}
and this is also contained in
T(\pi)
since it contains also the vertices
t_{1},t_{k}
. We have two cases: either
t_{j}
belongs to
q^{\prime}
, or it does not. In the first case since
q^{\prime}
is contained in both
T(p_{i})
and
T(\pi)
we deduce that there is a vertex
w\in\pi
such that
w\in V_{t_{j}}
, and since
v
is also contained in
V_{t_{j}}
and
\delta_{\Gamma}(V_{t_{j}})\leq N
we deduce that
d(v,w)\leq N
, so the distance of
v\in p_{i}
from
\pi
is at most
N
. In case
t_{j}
is not contained in the geodesic
q^{\prime}
, since
T
is a tree,
q
must contain a circuit
t^{\prime}\ldots t_{j}\ldots t^{\prime}
where
t^{\prime}
belongs to the geodesic
q^{\prime}
and the image of the circuit
t^{\prime}\ldots t_{j}\ldots t^{\prime}
through
f
is a subwalk
v_{1}\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}\ldots v\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}\ldots v_{2}
of
p_{i}
with
v_{1},v_{2}\in V_{t^{\prime}}
. Since
p_{i}
is a geodesic, then also the subwalk
v_{1}\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}\ldots v\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}\ldots v_{2}
is a geodesic, hence
d(v_{1},v)\leq d(v_{1},v_{2})\leq N
since
v_{1},v_{2}\in V_{t^{\prime}}
. Since
t^{\prime}
is in
q^{\prime}
by the same argument as before, we may find
w\in\pi
with
w\in V_{t^{\prime}}
. Hence, we deduce
d(v_{1},w)\leq N
which together with
d(v_{1},v)\leq N
yields
d(v,w)\leq 2N
and this concludes the proof.
∎

Context-free inverse graphs share all the properties stated in Proposition
8
, this is contained in the following proposition.

Proposition 9
.

Let
(\Gamma,x_{0})
be a rooted inverse graph that is context-free, then
\Gamma
is quasi-isometric to a tree.

Proof.

We show that condition
(4)
of Proposition
8
holds by taking
\delta=3K
. Now, note that
C(v,x_{0})
is a subgraph of
\Gamma(v,x_{0})
since by definition there is a geodesic

x_{0}=y_{1}\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}y_{2}\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}\ldots\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}y_{i}\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}\ldots\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}y_{k}=x

with
y_{i}=v
, for some
i
, moreover all the vertices
y_{j}
with
j\geq i
have the property
\|y_{j}\|_{x_{0}}\geq\|y_{i}\|_{x_{0}}=\|v\|_{x_{0}}
, and so
x
is connected to
v
by a walk contained in
\Gamma(v,x_{0})
, i.e.,
x\in\Gamma(v,x_{0})
. Therefore, any walk connecting
x_{0}
to some vertex
x\in C(v,x_{0})
passes from a frontier point in
\Delta(v,x_{0})
. By Proposition
5

\Delta(v,x_{0})\subseteq V(D_{\delta}(v))
, which implies that there is no walk in
\Gamma\setminus D_{\delta}(v)
connecting
x_{0}
to
x
.
∎

5.1.
A detour to inverse monoids and Schützenberger graphs

Schützenberger graphs are the natural generalization of Cayley graphs in the area of inverse semigroup theory. We know that for Cayley graphs being context-free and tree-like are equivalent properties (since they are transitive). Proposition
7
in combination with
[
16
, Theorem 4.13]
shows that the same property holds in the finitely presented case, answering to the open problem
[
16
, Question 6.2]
.

Theorem 1
.

Let
M=Inv\langle A\mid\mathcal{R}\rangle
be a finitely presented inverse monoid, and let
w\in\widetilde{A}^{*}
be a word. Then, the Schützenberger graph of
w
is tree-like if and only if the language accepted by the Schützenberger automaton of
w
is context-free.

Proof.

We refer the reader to
[
16
,
34
]
for more details on Schützenberger graphs, for our purpose it is enough to know that a Schützenberger automata
\mathcal{A}(A,\mathcal{R},w)
with respect to the presentation
\langle A\mid\mathcal{R}\rangle
and the word
w\in\widetilde{A}
is a birooted inverse graph
(\Gamma,x_{0},\{y_{0}\})
. One direction of the statement is
[
16
, Theorem 4.13]
. For the other direction, if the accepted language
L(\Gamma,x_{0},\{y_{0}\})
is context-free, then by Proposition
7

\Gamma
is context-free, and so by Proposition
9
it is also quasi-isometric to a tree.
∎

Since for groups the previous equivalence holds for any finitely generated group, the natural question arises as to whether we may extend the previous theorem to a generic finitely generated inverse monoid. We now show that this is not the case, and the counterexample is contained in
[
16
, Example 4.4]
. We first need the following fact.

Proposition 10
.

Let
(\Gamma,x_{0})
be a rooted inverse graph that is context-free. Then the language of the geodesics rooted at a vertex
x_{0}
, that is the set

\Geo(\Gamma,x_{0})=\{w\in\widetilde{A}^{*}:x_{0}\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$w$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}x\mbox{ is a geodesic}\}

is a regular language.

Proof.

Following the same strategy of the proof of
[
16
, Theorem 4.5]
, it is enough to show that there are finitely many cones
C(v,x_{0})
up to end-isomorphism, which in this context means an isomorphism
\psi:C(v,x_{0})\to C(w,x_{0})
with
\psi(v)=w
. Now, by Proposition
7

\Gamma
is a context-free graph in the sense of Muller-Schupp, so there are finitely many end-cones graphs
\Gamma(v,x_{0})
up to end-isomorphism (of end-cones). We have already seen in the proof of Proposition
9
that each cone
C(v,x_{0})
is contained in
\Gamma(v,x_{0})
. Let

\psi:\Gamma(v_{1},x_{0})\to\Gamma(v_{2},x_{0})

be an end-isomorphism. We claim that if
x_{0}\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$w_{1}$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}v_{1}\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$w_{2}$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}u
is a geodesic with
u\in C(v_{1},x_{0})
, then for any geodesic
x_{0}\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$s_{1}$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}\psi(v_{1})
, also the walk
x_{0}\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$s_{1}$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}\psi(v_{1})\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$w_{2}$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}\psi(u)
is a geodesic. Suppose by contradiction that there is a shorter walk
x_{0}\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$s_{1}^{\prime}$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}z\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$w_{2}^{\prime}$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}\psi(u)
where
z\in\Delta(v_{2},x_{0})
, since
\|z\|_{x_{0}}=\|\psi(v_{1})\|=\|v_{2}\|_{x_{0}}
we deduce that the length of the walk
z\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$w_{2}^{\prime}$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}\psi(u)
is shorter than
\psi(v_{1})\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$w_{2}$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}\psi(u)
. Thus, the walk
\psi^{-1}(z)\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$w_{2}^{\prime}$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}u
is shorter than
v_{1}\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$w_{2}$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}u
, and this contradicts the fact that
x_{0}\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$w_{1}$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}v_{1}\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$w_{2}$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}u
is a geodesic.
Hence,
\psi(u)\in C(\psi(v_{1}),x_{0})
and
\psi:C(v_{1},x_{0})\to C(\psi(v_{1}),x_{0})
is an end-isomorphism of cones. Since a cone
C(v_{1},x_{0})
is completely determined by the vertex
v_{1}
and the graph
\Gamma(v_{1},x_{0})
, and there are finitely many end-isomorphic classes of graphs
\Gamma(v,x_{0})
, and
\Delta(v,x_{0})
is uniformly upper bounded by Proposition
5
, then we deduce that there are also finitely many cones up to end-isomorphism.
∎

In
[
16
, Example 4.4]
it is described an inverse monoid on
A=\{a,b,c\}
that is not finitely presented, whose Schützenberger graph
\Gamma
of the element
aa^{-1}
is a tree, but the language of the geodesics rooted at
aa^{-1}
is not a regular language, see Fig.
3
. Hence, by the previous proposition, we conclude that
\Gamma
can not be a context-free graph. Therefore, if we remove the condition of being finitely presented the “only if” condition of the previous theorem is no longer valid.

aa^{-1}
of the inverse monoid with presentation
Inv\langle a,b,c\mid aa^{-1}b^{n^{2}}cc^{-1}b^{-n^{2}}=aa^{-1},n\geq 1\rangle
.

6.
Characterization of quasi-transitive context-free inverse graphs

In this section, we shall prove the characterization of context-free inverse graphs that are quasi-transitive, but first, we need to make some considerations on the language
L(\Gamma,x_{0})
of a rooted inverse graph that will be used in the proof.

For each vertex
v\in V(\Gamma)
we denote by
\mathcal{D}(\Gamma,v)
the set of symmetric Dyck words
u\in\widetilde{A}
labeling a reduced circuit
v\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$u$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}v
starting at
v
, i.e., a word
u\in\widetilde{A}
with

Remark 2

Note that to prove equivalences

The last condition may be seen as a more group theoretic restatement of the Chomsky-Schützenberger representation theorem for context-free languages

The previous theorem does not hold if we remove the hypothesis of being quasi-transitive. Indeed, consider the inverse graph

7.
A characterization of groups that are virtually finitely generated subgroups of the direct product of free groups

Differently from the regular case, the intersection of context-free languages is not, in general, a context-free language
[
19
]
. For this reason, it is natural to consider the closure of context-free languages under intersection. The intersection of
k
context-free languages is called a
k
-context-free language. A language is poly-context-free if it is
k
-context-free for some
k\in\mathbb{N}

[
5
]
. A group is called
k
-context-free if its word problem is a
k
-context-free language. A group whose word problem is a poly-context-free language is called a poly-context-free group. Such groups have been introduced in
[
5
]
as a natural generalization of virtually free groups, and they have been further explored in
[
6
]
, where it is proved that they have a multipass word problem. Since the class of 1-context-free groups coincides with the class of virtually free groups, it is a natural problem to generalize such result for a generic poly-context-free group. Brough has conjectured in
[
5
]
that the class of finitely generated poly-context-free groups coincides with the class of (finitely generated) groups which are virtually subgroups of a direct product of free groups. Note that, in general, subgroups of direct products of (two or more) free groups can behave quite wildly. For instance,
\mathbb{F}_{2}\times\mathbb{F}_{2}
contains as many non-isomorphic subgroups
[
3
]
, and for finitely generated subgroups they can even have unsolvable membership problem
[
27
]
.
In this section, we prove a weaker version of Brough’s conjecture by showing that the class of finitely generated groups which are virtually a finitely generated subgroup of a direct product of free groups coincides with the class of groups whose word problem is in the class of languages
PT-ICF
that are the intersection of finitely many inverse-context-free languages accepted by inverse graphs that are quasi-transitive. By Theorem
2
,
PT-ICF
is also the class of languages that is the intersection of finitely many languages accepted by quasi-transitive and tree-like inverse graphs.

Theorem 3
.

A group
G
has word problem in the class
PT-ICF
if and only if it is virtually a (finitely generated) subgroup of a direct product of free groups.

Remark 3
.

The class of groups having the word problem in
PT-ICF
is included in the class of co-context-free groups introduced in
[
18
]
. This follows from Proposition
7
since the language accepted by a context-free inverse graph, is, in particular, deterministic, and the fact that the union and complement of deterministic context-free languages is still context-free
[
19
, Theorem 10.1]
. Thus, by the previous theorem and
[
27
]
, we deduce that for the class of poly-context-free and co-context-free groups, the membership problem is undecidable.

We recall that a

product of groups

is a subgroup

which projects surjectively onto each factor. One direction of the proof of Theorem

there is a cover
\psi_{i}:\Gamma_{i}\to\Lambda_{i}
with
\Lambda_{i}
finite inverse graph and a surjective homomorphism
\overline{\eta}_{i}:\pi_{1}(\Lambda_{i},\psi(x_{i}))\to\mathbb{F}_{X_{i}}
onto a finite rank free group
\mathbb{F}_{X_{i}}
with
\overline{L(\Gamma_{i},x_{i})}=\ker(\overline{\eta_{i}})
. Note that since each
\Gamma_{i}
is complete,
\Lambda_{i}
is also complete. Hence,
(\Lambda_{i},\psi_{i}(x_{i}))
is the Stallings automaton of
\pi_{1}(\Lambda_{i},\psi_{i}(x_{i}))
which is a finite index subgroup of
\mathbb{F}_{A}
, being
\Lambda_{i}
complete, see for instance
[
31
, Chapter 23]
. Therefore,
\mathcal{H}=\cap_{i}\pi_{1}(\Lambda_{i},\psi_{i}(x_{i}))
is a finite index subgroup of
\mathbb{F}_{A}
. Consider the product homomorphism
\varphi:\mathcal{H}\to\Pi_{i}^{k}\mathbb{F}_{X_{i}}

defined by
\varphi(g)=(\overline{\eta}_{1}(g),\ldots,\overline{\eta}_{k}(g))
for all
g\in\mathcal{H}
. Clearly
\ker\varphi=\bigcap_{i=1}^{k}\overline{L(\Gamma_{i},x_{i})}=\theta(W)
, hence
\mathcal{H}/\theta(W)
is a finitely generated finite index subgroup of
G\simeq\mathbb{F}_{A}/\theta(W)
that is isomorphic to a subgroup of
\Pi_{i}^{k}\mathbb{F}_{X_{i}}
which projects surjectively onto each factor since each
\overline{\eta}_{i}
is surjective.
∎

To prove the other direction of Theorem
3
we need to recall the notion of inverse transducer. This notion has been firstly introduced in
[
32
]
as a tool to show that the fixed point subgroup of an endomorphism of a finitely generated virtually free group is finitely generated. A transducer which is also referred to as a generalized sequential machine is a finite
Y\times X^{*}
-digraph rooted at some vertex
p_{0}
. A generic edge may be graphically represented by
p_{1}\smash{\mathop{\relbar\joinrel\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}\limits^{y_{1}\mid u_{1}}}p_{2},y_{1}\in Y,u_{1}\in X^{*}
, while we may represent the walk

p_{1}\smash{\mathop{\relbar\joinrel\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}\limits^{y_{1}\mid u_{1}}}p_{2}\smash{\mathop{\relbar\joinrel\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}\limits^{y_{2}\mid u_{2}}}p_{3}\ldots p_{k}\smash{\mathop{\relbar\joinrel\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}\limits^{y_{k}\mid u_{k}}}p_{k+1}

shortly as
p_{1}\smash{\mathop{\relbar\joinrel\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}\limits^{s\mid h}}p_{k+1}
where
s=y_{1}y_{2}\ldots y_{k}
,
h=u_{1}u_{2}\ldots,u_{k}
. A transducer
\mathcal{A}
on the alphabet
\widetilde{Y}\times\widetilde{X}^{*}
is called
inverse
, if for any edge
p_{1}\smash{\mathop{\relbar\joinrel\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}\limits^{y\mid u}}p_{2}
,
y\in\widetilde{Y},u\in\widetilde{X}^{*}
, there is the reverse edge
p_{2}\smash{\mathop{\relbar\joinrel\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}\limits^{y^{-1}\mid u^{-1}}}p_{1}
, and
\mathcal{A}
is deterministic in the following sense: for any vertex
p
and
z\in\widetilde{Y}
there is at most one edge
p\smash{\mathop{\relbar\joinrel\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}\limits^{z\mid w}}p^{\prime}
in
\mathcal{A}
. In this way
\mathcal{A}
defines a partial function
\mathcal{A}:\widetilde{Y}^{*}\to\widetilde{X}^{*}
by putting
\mathcal{A}(s)=h
whenever in
\mathcal{A}
there is a circuit
p_{0}\smash{\mathop{\relbar\joinrel\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}\limits^{s\mid h}}p_{0}
.
Given an inverse
\widetilde{X}
-graph
\Gamma
and an inverse transducer
\mathcal{A}
on the alphabet
\widetilde{Y}\times\widetilde{X}^{*}
and root
p_{0}
, we may define the
\widetilde{Y}
-graph
\mathcal{A}\ltimes\Gamma
having as set of vertices
V(\mathcal{A})\times V(\Gamma)
and edges

(p_{1},q_{1})\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$y$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}(p_{2},q_{2})\mbox{ whenever }p_{1}\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$y\mid u$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}p_{2}\in\mathcal{A},\,q_{1}\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$u$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}q_{2}\mbox{ is a walk in }\Gamma\mbox{ for some }u\in\widetilde{X}^{*}.

It is straightforward to check that
\mathcal{A}\ltimes\Gamma
is an involutive
\widetilde{Y}
-digraph that is deterministic. In general, it is missing the condition to be connected to make this graph inverse. Thus, we may think of
\mathcal{A}\ltimes\Gamma
as a collection of disjoint union of its connected components each of which is an inverse graph. Note that by fixing a root
x_{0}
in
\Gamma
then the language
L(\mathcal{A}\ltimes\Gamma,(p_{0},x_{0}))
is the set of words
u\in\widetilde{Y}^{*}
such that
p_{0}\smash{\mathop{\relbar\joinrel\relbar\joinrel\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}\limits^{u\mid\mathcal{A}(u)}}p_{0}
is a circuit in
\mathcal{A}
with
\mathcal{A}(u)\in L(\Gamma,x_{0})
. Thus,
L(\mathcal{A}\ltimes\Gamma,(p_{0},x_{0}))=\mathcal{A}^{-1}(L(\Gamma,x_{0}))
is the inverse image under the partial map defined by the transducer of the language
L(\Gamma,x_{0})
. It is a well-known fact that context-free languages are closed under the inverse image of the partial map defined by a generic transducer, see for instance
[
19
, Theorem 11.2]
, a fact that will be used later.

Lemma 11
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be an inverse transducer on the alphabet
\widetilde{Y}\times\widetilde{X}^{*}
, and
(\Gamma,x_{0})
be a quasi-transitive inverse
\widetilde{X}
-graph. Then,
\mathcal{A}\ltimes\Gamma
is a quasi-transitive inverse graph. In particular, if
L(\Gamma,x_{0})
is an inverse context-free language, then
\mathcal{A}^{-1}(L(\Gamma,x_{0}))\in{\bf PT-ICF}
.

Proof.

For every automorphism
\varphi\in\Aut(\Gamma)
consider the map
\hat{\varphi}_{v}:V(\mathcal{A}\ltimes\Gamma)\rightarrow V(\mathcal{A}\ltimes\Gamma)
defined by putting
\hat{\varphi}(p_{1},q_{1})=(p_{1},\varphi(q_{1}))
. This map extends to a morphism of involutive graphs. Indeed,
(p_{1},q_{1})\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$y$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}(p_{2},q_{2})
is an edge in
\mathcal{A}\ltimes\Gamma
if and only if there is some
h\in\widetilde{X}^{*}
such that
p_{1}\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$y\mid h$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}p_{2}
is an edge in
\mathcal{A}
and
q_{1}\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$h$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}q_{2}
is a walk in
\Gamma
. Thus, by applying
\varphi
, in
\Gamma
we have also the walk
\varphi(q_{1})\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$h$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}\varphi(q_{2})
, hence
(p_{1},\varphi(q_{1}))\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$y$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}(p_{2},\varphi(q_{2}))
is an edge in
\mathcal{A}\ltimes\Gamma
. Thus, by putting

\hat{\varphi}_{e}((p_{1},q_{1})\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$y$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}(p_{2},q_{2}))=(p_{1},\varphi(q_{1}))\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$y$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}(p_{2},\varphi(q_{2}))

and by the determinism of
\mathcal{A}\ltimes\Gamma
, we have that
\hat{\varphi}:\mathcal{A}\ltimes\Gamma\to\mathcal{A}\ltimes\Gamma
is a morphism of involutive
\widetilde{X}
-graphs. Furthermore,
\hat{\varphi}
is an automorphism, since both
\hat{\varphi}_{v}
and
\hat{\varphi}_{e}
are bijective with inverses

\hat{\varphi}_{v}^{-1}(p_{1},q_{1})=(p_{1},\varphi^{-1}(q_{1})),\quad\hat{\varphi}^{-1}_{e}((p_{1},q_{1})\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$y$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}(p_{2},q_{2}))=(p_{1},\varphi^{-1}(q_{1}))\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{$y$}}}}{{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}}}(p_{2},\varphi^{-1}(q_{2}))

Thus, the map
\psi:\Aut(\Gamma)\to\Aut(\mathcal{A}\ltimes\Gamma)
defined by
\psi(\varphi)=\hat{\varphi}
is an homomorphism of groups which is also injective. Indeed,
\psi(\varphi_{1})=\psi(\varphi_{2})
implies that in each connected component
\Theta
of
\mathcal{A}\ltimes\Gamma
and for any
(p_{1},q_{1})\in\Theta
we have
\varphi_{1}(q_{1})=\varphi_{2}(q_{1})
, thus by Lemma
2

\left.\varphi_{1}\right|_{\Theta}=\left.\varphi_{2}\right|_{\Theta}
, i.e.,
\varphi_{1}=\varphi_{2}
. Since the action of
\Aut(\Gamma)
on
\Gamma
is quasi-transitive, the set
V(\Gamma)
decomposes into finitely many orbits
O_{1},\ldots,O_{k}
. Therefore, each
\Aut(\Gamma)
-orbit of
\mathcal{A}\ltimes\Gamma
is contained in
V(\mathcal{A})\times O_{i}
for some
i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
, thus
\Aut(\mathcal{A}\ltimes\Gamma)
acts quasi-transitively on
\mathcal{A}\ltimes\Gamma
. The last statement follows from the equality
L(\mathcal{A}\ltimes\Gamma,(p_{0},x_{0}))=\mathcal{A}^{-1}(L(\Gamma,x_{0}))
, which is a context-free language, being
L(\Gamma,x_{0})
context-free, which is also accepted by the quasi-transitive inverse graph
(\mathcal{A}\ltimes\Gamma,(p_{0},x_{0}))
, i.e.,
\mathcal{A}^{-1}(L(\Gamma,x_{0}))\in{\bf PT-ICF}
.
∎

Proposition 12
.

Let
G
be a group that is virtually a finitely generated subgroup of a direct product of free groups. Then, the word problem
W(G;Y)\in{\bf PT-ICF}
.

Proof.

Let
H
be the finite index subgroup of
G
that is isomorphic to a subgroup of the direct product
\Pi_{i=1}^{k}\mathbb{F}_{Z_{i}}
of the free groups
\mathbb{F}_{Z_{i}}
. Let
X
be a finite generating set of
H
, and let
T
be a right transversal with
\mathds{1}_{G}\in T
. Every element of
g\in G
may be represented as
g=_{G}ht
for some
h\in H
,
t\in T
. Put
Y=X\cup\{T\setminus\{\mathds{1}_{G}\}\}
as the generating set of
G
. For each
y\in\widetilde{Y}
and
t\in T
, fix a word
h_{t,y}\in\widetilde{X}^{*}
with
ty=_{G}h_{t,y}t^{\prime}
for some
t^{\prime}\in T
. As in
[
18
]
we construct a transducer
\mathcal{A}
in the following way. The set of vertices is
T
and for for any
y\in Y
and
t_{1}\in T
we have the edge

t_{1}\smash{\mathop{\relbar\joinrel\relbar\joinrel\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}\limits^{y\mid h_{t_{1},y}}}t_{2}\mbox{ whenever we have }t_{1}y=_{G}h_{t_{1},y}t_{2}

and take
\mathds{1}_{G}
as the root of
\mathcal{A}
. Note that
\mathcal{A}
is inverse. Indeed, it is involutive since

t_{1}\smash{\mathop{\relbar\joinrel\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}\limits^{y\mid h_{t_{1},y}}}t_{2}\in\mathcal{A}\mbox{ if and only if }t_{2}\smash{\mathop{\relbar\joinrel\relbar\joinrel\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}\limits^{y^{-1}\mid h_{t_{1},y}^{-1}}}t_{1}\in\mathcal{A}.

and it is also deterministic since
y_{t_{1},y}t_{2}=_{G}y_{t_{1},y}t^{\prime}_{2}
implies
t_{2}=t^{\prime}_{2}
. Now, it is straightforward to check that for any
t\in T
and
y\in\widetilde{Y}^{*}
, in
\mathcal{A}
we have a walk
t\smash{\mathop{\relbar\joinrel\longrightarrow}\limits^{y\mid u}}t^{\prime}
, for some
u\in\widetilde{X}^{*}
if and only if
ty=_{G}ut^{\prime}
. Let
\phi:\widetilde{Y}^{*}\to G
denote the natural projections, and let
\pi_{i}:H\to\Pi_{i}^{k}\mathbb{F}_{Z_{i}}
be the projection into the
i
-th component of the direct product. The group
H/\ker\pi_{i}
is a finitely generated free group whose word problem
W_{i}\subseteq\widetilde{X}^{*}
is a context-free language accepted by a quasi-transitive inverse graph
(\Gamma_{i},x_{i})
, namely the Cayley graph
\Cay(H/\ker\pi_{i};X)
. Note that any word
w\in W(G;Y)
belonging to the word problem is characterized by the conditions
\pi_{i}(\phi(w))=1
for
i=1,\ldots,k
, that is, for any
i=1,\ldots,k
there is some
h_{i}\in W_{i}=L(\Gamma_{i},x_{i})
such that
w=_{G}h_{i}
, i.e.,
\mathcal{A}(w)\in L(\Gamma_{i},x_{i})
. Hence, by Lemma
11
we conclude that the word problem

W(G;A)=\bigcap_{i=1}^{k}\mathcal{A}^{-1}(L(\Gamma_{i},x_{i}))\in{\bf PT-ICF}

∎
