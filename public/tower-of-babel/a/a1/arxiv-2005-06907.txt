Abstract
. We develop a systematic study of the superpositions of elliptic operators with different orders, mixing classical and fractional scenarios. For concreteness, we focus on the sum of the Laplacian and the fractional Laplacian, and we provide structural results, including existence, maximum principles (both for weak and classical solutions), interior Sobolev regularity and boundary regularity of Lipschitz type.

35A01, 35B65, 35R11

1.
Introduction

The goal of this paper is to develop a systematic study
of mixed operators. The word “mixed” refers here to
the differential (or pseudo-differential) order of the operator,
and to the type of the operator, which combines classical
and fractional features.

Though many of the techniques that we present here
are rather “general”, for the sake of concreteness,
and not to hide the main flow of ideas by technical complications,
we focus here on an operator which deals with the coexistence
of a Laplacian and a fractional Laplacian, given by

(1.1)

{\mathcal{L}}:=-\Delta+(-\Delta)^{s},\qquad{\mbox{ for some }}s\in(0,1).

Here,
(-\Delta)^{s}
is the nonlocal operator defined as

(1.2)

(-\Delta)^{s}u(x)=c_{N,s}\;\textrm{P.V.}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{u(x)-u(y)}{|x-y|^{N+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}y,

where
c_{N,s}
is a suitable normalizing constant, whose explicit expression is given by

c_{N,s}=\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{1-\cos(\zeta_{1})}{|\zeta|^{N+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}\zeta\bigg)^{-1},

and, as customary, “
P.V.
” stands for the Cauchy’s principal value,
see e.g. formulas (3.1) and (3.2) in
[
25
]
.

We recall that the literature presents
several
variants of the fractional Laplacian, including one that is defined in terms of the eigenbasis and spectrum of the
Dirichlet Laplacian on a bounded domain and one
in which the singular integral only extends to the given domain.
These are quite different operators from the one in (
1.2
), see e.g.
[
2
, Sections 2.1, 2.2. 2.3, 4.2 and 4.3]
for a list of similarities and differences between these fractional operators.

Operators as in (
1.2
) arise naturally
from the superposition of two stochastic processes
with different scales (namely, a classical random
walk and a Lévy flight): roughly speaking, when a particle can
follow either of these two processes according to a certain probability,
the associated limit diffusion equation is described by
an operator of the form described in (
1.1
):
see in particular the appendix in
[
28
]

for a
thorough discussion of this phenomenon and
[
30
]

for the description of a biological population in an ecological niche
modeled by a mixed operator.

In view of this motivation, we think that operators as
in (
1.1
) will enjoy a constantly rising popularity
in applied sciences, also to study the different impact
of “local” and “nonlocal” diffusions in concrete situations
(e.g. how different types of “regional” or “global”
restrictions may reduce the spreading of a pandemic disease, see e.g.
[
32
]
).
Other classical applications include
heat transport in magnetized
plasmas (see
[
13
]
).

The mathematical study of operators with different order
is not new in itself, and indeed the literature
already presents results concerning, among the others,
the theory of viscosity solutions
(see
[
37
,
38
,
6
,
11
,
21
,
4
,
5
]
),
the Aubry-Mather theory for sums of different
fractional Laplacians
(see Remark 5.6 in
[
41
]
),
regularization effects of Cahn-Hilliard equations
(see
[
18
]
), numerics (
[
12
]
),
probability
and stochastics (see
[
19
,
20
,
42
]
),
symmetry results
for mixed range phase transitions (see
[
16
]
),
porous medium equations
(see
[
24
]
),
decay estimates for parabolic equations (see
[
31
]
),
specific Liouville theorems for systems
of equations driven by sums of fractional Laplacians (see
[
40
,
3
]
),
fractional damping effects (see
[
23
]
),
and Bernstein-type regularity results (see
[
15
]
).

Though conceptually different,
the problems related to mixed order equations are closely
related in spirit to the ones of variable order equations
(see
[
39
,
8
,
9
,
47
]
), which have themselves concrete
significance in applied sciences (see e.g.
[
48
,
36
]
).

The main focus here
is on the operator in (
1.1
) with the aim
of obtaining a number of
structural results
(based on techniques
which we plan to extend to more general situations in future works).
These results deal with
distributional as well as classical solutions, and they
can be grouped into four categories: existence,
maximum principles,
interior regularity,
and boundary regularity.

Let us now describe the main results in further detail.
First of all, we will introduce a suitable notion of weak solution
associated to the operator in (
1.1
).
In light of the mixed nature of the problem, this step already presents
some caveats, due to the possible choices of including or excluding
the external data within the classical Sobolev spaces framework.
Our setting for weak solutions will be described in
Definition
2.1
, and then exploited
to obtain,
via the Lax-Milgram’s theorem,
the following existence result, of very
classical flavor. In all the forthcoming statements,
we tacitly understand that
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N}
is a
bounded open set with

C^{1}
boundary
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Given
f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
, there exists a unique

weak solution

u_{f}\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
of

\begin{cases}{\mathcal{L}}u=f&\text{in $\Omega$},\\
u\big|_{\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\Omega}=0.\end{cases}

Furthermore, there exists a
\mathbf{c}>0
such that

\|u_{f}\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}\leq\mathbf{c}\,\|f\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

We then focus on the maximum principles
associated to the operator in (
1.1
).
Their formulation is slightly different for weak
and classical solutions. More precisely, we
present a weak maximum principle for weak solutions, as follows:

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
u\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
weakly satisfy
{\mathcal{L}}u\geq 0
in
\Omega
.
If
u\geq 0
a.e. in
\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\Omega
, then

u\geq 0
a.e. on
\Omega
.

For classical solutions, this statement is strengthened in the following result:

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
u\in C(\mathbb{R}^{N},\mathbb{R})\cap C^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{R})
, with

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{|u(x)|}{1+|x|^{N+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}x<\infty.

Suppose that

\begin{cases}{\mathcal{L}}u\geq 0&\text{pointwise in $\Omega$},\\
u\geq 0&\text{in $\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\Omega$}.\end{cases}

Then

(1.3)

u\geq 0\quad{\mbox{ in }}\quad\Omega.

Furthermore,

(1.4)

if there exists
x_{0}\in\Omega
such that

u(x_{0})=0
, then

u\equiv 0
throughout
\mathbb{R}^{N}
.

As customary, the statement in (
1.3
) can be considered as a weak maximum principle
and the one in (
1.4
) as a strong maximum principle.
The difference between the weak maximum principle in Theorem
1.2

and that in (
1.3
) is in the assumptions required on
u

(the Sobolev setting being considered in Theorem
1.2
and the classical one in Theorem
1.3
).

Though these maximum principles are of classical
flavor, we remark that their validity depends sensibly on the
type of the operator and on the setting of the data.
In particular, we will show in
Appendix
A
that the maximum principle does not hold
if the external condition “
u\geq 0

in
\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\Omega
”
is replaced by “
u\geq 0

on
\partial\Omega
”: namely, classical boundary conditions
are not
enough to guarantee the validity of maximum principles for the operator
in (
1.1
), notwithstanding the presence
of the classical Laplacian in this operator.

Furthermore, we will show that these
maximum principles do not hold when one of the elliptic operators
in (
1.1
) has the “wrong sign”, e.g. for operators
of the type
\Delta+(-\Delta)^{s}
.

The fact that the weak maximum principles
in Theorem
1.2
and in (
1.3
) of Theorem
1.3

hold
for
{\mathcal{L}}
but not for similar operators
with the “wrong sign” is very reasonable, especially
in view of some potential-theoretic results of
the early ’60s (see, e.g.,
[
14
,
22
]
).
In fact, in the aforementioned papers is proved that: if

A:C_{0}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{N})\to C(\mathbb{R}^{N})

is a linear operator,
then the next two conditions are
equivalent
:

(i)

A
is continuous (with respect to suitable topologies) and

\begin{cases}u\in C_{0}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{N}),\\
\text{$u(x)=\inf_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}u\leq 0$}\end{cases}\,\,\Longrightarrow\,\,Au(x)\leq 0;

(ii)

for every
u\in C^{2}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
, we have

(1.5)

\begin{split}Au&=-\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}a_{i,j}(x)\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}(x)+\sum_{i=1}^{N}b_{i}(x)\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{i}}(x)+c(x)u(x)\\[2.84544pt]
&\qquad+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}s(x,\mathrm{d}y)\big[u(x)-u(y)-\mathbf{1}_{\{|x-y|\leq 1\}}\cdot\langle\nabla u(x),x-y\rangle\big],\end{split}

where
A(x)=\begin{pmatrix}a_{i,j}(x)\end{pmatrix}

is
positive definite
for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
,

c\leq 0
on the whole of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
and

s
is a measurable kernel on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
satisfying the properties

\bullet

s(x,\{x\})=0
;

\bullet

for every positive function
f\in C_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
, the map

x\mapsto\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}s(x,\mathrm{d}y)|y-x|^{2}f(y)\,\mathrm{d}y\quad\text{is measurable}.

In particular, property (i) (which shall be used in the proof of (
1.3
))
holds for

{\mathcal{L}}

but not for
similar operators with “wrong sign”. It is worth mentioning that
in the papers
[
14
,
22
]
it is not studied the validity
of a weak maximum principle as in (
1.4
) (i.e.,
the possibility of “propagating” the sign of
u
from
\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\Omega

into
\Omega
). On the other hand, the equivalence of (i) and (ii) is exploited
in the recent paper
[
3
]
to characterize all the operators
of the form (
1.5
) for which a Liouville-type theorem holds.

We devote part of this paper to the interior and boundary
regularity properties of solutions.
Though a variety of different directions
can be taken for this, we focus here on the interior regularity
theory in Sobolev spaces. The main result in this sense
goes as follows:

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
m\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
and

f\in H^{m}(\Omega)
. Assume that
u\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
is a weak solution
of

{\mathcal{L}}u=f\qquad\text{in $\Omega$}.

Then

u\in H^{m+2}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\Omega)
.

The proof of Theorem
1.4
requires some technical
improvements with respect to the
classical energy methods
and bootstrap arguments,
since the fractional operator prevents
the possibility of taking derivatives of the equation.
To overcome this difficulty, we will
exploit truncation arguments and difference quotients
in a suitable way.

As for the boundary regularity, for concreteness
we focus on the linear growth
and Lipschitz regularity for solutions in convex domain.
Our result can be summarized as follows:

Theorem 1.5
.

Assume that
\Omega
is strictly convex
and let
\bar{C}>0
. Let

u\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
be such that

(1.6)

\begin{cases}{\mathcal{L}}u\leq\bar{C}&\text{in $\Omega$},\\
u=0&\text{in $\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\Omega$},\\
u\leq\bar{C}&\text{in $\mathbb{R}^{N}$}.\end{cases}

Then, there exist
C
,
\ell>0
such that,
for every
p\in\partial\Omega
, we have that

(1.7)

u(x)\leq C\,\bar{C}\,|x-p|\qquad\text{for a.e.\,$x\in B(p,\ell)$.}

As customary, the notation
B(p,\ell)

here denotes the Euclidean ball centered at
p
with radius
\ell
.

Interestingly, the boundary regularity in Theorem
1.5

is stronger than in the case of the fractional Laplacian, in which the
solution is in general not better than
C^{s}
, see
[
43
]
.

As a byproduct of Theorem
1.5
, one obtains
also existence and regularity results, as given
by the following result:

Theorem 1.6
.

Assume that
\Omega
is strictly convex.
Let
m\geq\frac{N}{2}+3
and

f\in C^{m}(\Omega,\mathbb{R})\cap L^{\infty}(\Omega)
.
Then, there exists a unique classical solution

\mathfrak{u}_{f}\in C(\mathbb{R}^{N},\mathbb{R})\cap C^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{R})

of

(1.8)

\begin{cases}{\mathcal{L}}\mathfrak{u}_{f}=f&\text{in $\Omega$},\\
\mathfrak{u}_{f}\big|_{\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\Omega}=0,\end{cases}

with

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{|\mathfrak{u}_{f}(x)|}{1+|x|^{N+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}x<\infty.

Moreover, this solution
\mathfrak{u}_{f}
satisfies the following
additional properties:

(i)

\mathfrak{u}_{f}\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
;

(ii)

\|\mathfrak{u}_{f}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}\leq\mathbf{c}\,\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}
;

(iii)

for every
p\in\partial\Omega
there exists
\ell>0
such that

|\mathfrak{u}_{f}(x)|\leq\mathbf{c}\,\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\cdot|x-p|\qquad\text{for all $x\in\Omega\cap B(p,\ell)$}.

Here,
\mathbf{c}>0
is a constant independent of
\mathfrak{u}_{f}
.

Let us comment a bit about the “philosophy” of the regularity theory developed
in this paper. At first, in view of the classical regularity theory for the Laplacian,
one could believe that adding an
extra fractional Laplacian to an already elliptic problem would just produce “expected”
outcomes, as a lower order effect. For instance, one could argue that
interior regularity results (such as Theorem
1.4
, as well as the forthcoming Theorem
4.1
) could be a consequence of the classical theory.
Specifically: on the one hand, it is known that, if
g\in L^{2}(\Omega)
and
u\in H^{1}(\Omega)
are such that

(1.9)

\begin{cases}-\Delta u=g&{\mbox{ in }}\Omega,\\
u=0&{\mbox{ on }}\partial\Omega,\end{cases}

then
u\in H^{2}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\Omega)
and the corresponding norm is controlled, up to constants, by
\|u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|g\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
.

On the other hand, if we have a weak solution
u
to the
mixed problem, one can set
g:=f-(-\Delta)^{s}u
and we reduce the situation
to the classical problem as
formulated in (
1.9
).

These observations yield the regularity in class
H^{2}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\Omega)
with norm controlled
by
\|u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|(-\Delta)^{s}u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|f\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
,
leading to even better statements than the ones provided here
(e.g. in the forthcoming estimate (
4.1
)),
but only when
s
is below
1/2

(and above this threshold the norm in
H^{s}(\Omega)
would be too strong compared
with the initial regularity of the solution in
H^{1}(\Omega)
).

That is: for the low-range of
s
, the nonlocal part of the operator can be treated as a perturbation of the classical elliptic problem but for the high-range of fractional exponents these tricks seem to be unavailable
and the operator must be treated on its own terms (roughly speaking,
one can efficiently consider the fractional Laplacian as a lower order perturbation only when
the fractional exponent is “sufficiently small”).

This is perhaps not a merely technical aspect of the problem: for instance, a distinction of this
sort will pop up also in the construction of the barrier constructed to prove Theorem
1.5
,
since for
s
below
1/2
one would not need the iteration exploited to reabsorb the nonlocal contributions
and the proof would significantly simplify.

Similar occurrence of different phenomena according to the fractional threshold
1/2

appear in the literature in several descriptions of nonlocal problems, see e.g.
[
17
,
44
,
27
,
29
]

and the references therein.

For all these reasons, though in principle other approaches could be possible (relying e.g. on intermediate estimates to bootstrap, scaled norms and interpolation theory), we think it is useful and instructive
to develop a series of analytic tools which account for mixed operators in their whole complexity
rather than limiting our vision to perturbation methods from the classical cases.

we have received the very interesting preprint
[

,
which considered a nonlinear problem of mixed type.
The motivations, methodologies and results obtained are different
from the ones in this paper, but Theorem 1.1 in
[

]

is related to Theorem
1.6
here.
Complementary to this result,
we stress that Theorem 1.4 in
[
1
]
shows that
there exists no classical solutions of (
1.8
) when
f

is merely in
L^{\infty}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\Omega)
.

Finally, we mention that similar results could be obtained via the
probabilistic methods and the Green representation formulas dealt with
in
[
19
,
20
]
. For regularity results involving mixed order diffusive operators
in different directions see also
[
4
,
34
,

]
.

The proof of the boundary regularity result in Theorem
1.5
relies
on the introduction of an explicit barrier. To the best of our
knowledge, this barrier
is completely new in the literature, and its construction
is based on an iterative method
of introducing “correctors” to recursively
compensate the terms
produced by the action of the nonlocal operator.
We think that this technique of iteratively canceling “the nonlocal
tails” is interesting in itself and can produce other results
in greater generality.

Some of the methodologies and motivations
presented in this paper will be also exploited in
[
10
]

to analyze the qualitative properties of solutions in specific
problems.

2.
Existence of weak solutions

In this section we establish some basic facts on the existence
of weak solutions for the Dirichlet problem associated with
{\mathcal{L}}
, as defined in (
1.1
),
that is,

(2.1)

\begin{cases}{\mathcal{L}}u=f&\text{in $\Omega$},\\
u\big|_{\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\Omega}=0.\end{cases}

Throughout the sequel, we tacitly understand that
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N}
is a bounded open set with
C^{1}
boundary
.
Moreover,
s\in(0,1)
is a fixed parameter,
and
(-\Delta)^{s}
is as in (
1.2
).
In studying the solvability of (
2.1
),
a ‘natural’ space to consider is the following

(2.2)

\mathbb{X}(\Omega):=\big\{u\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N}):\,\text{$u\equiv 0$ in $\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\Omega$}\big\}.

We observe that, in view of the regularity assumption on
\Omega
,
the space
\mathbb{X}(\Omega)
is contained in
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
and
is isomorphic
to
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
via the ‘zero-extension’ map defined as

\mathcal{E}_{0}:H_{0}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})\to\mathbb{X}(\Omega),\qquad{\mbox{such that }}\quad\mathcal{E}_{0}(u):=u\cdot\chi_{\Omega}.

As a consequence,
\mathbb{X}(\Omega)
is endowed
with a structure of (real) Hilbert space by
the scalar product defined as follows

\langle u,v\rangle_{\mathbb{X}}:=\int_{\Omega}\langle\nabla u,\nabla v\rangle\,\mathrm{d}x\qquad\text{for all\penalty\ $u$, $v\in\mathbb{X}(\Omega)$}.

The norm associated with
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathbb{X}}
is

\|u\|_{\mathbb{X}}:=\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\qquad\text{for all\penalty\ $u\in\mathbb{X}(\Omega)$},

and
C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega,\mathbb{R})
is
dense
in
\mathbb{X}(\Omega)
.

Furthermore,
the classical Poincaré inequality holds in
\mathbb{X}
:
more precisely, there exists a constant
\mathbf{c}>0
such that

(2.3)

\|u\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}\leq\mathbf{c}\,\|u\|_{\mathbb{X}}\qquad\text{for all $u\in\mathbb{X}(\Omega)$}.

After all these preliminaries,
we can give the following definition.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
.
We say that a function
u\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
is a
weak solution
of the equation

(2.4)

{\mathcal{L}}u=f\qquad\text{in $\Omega$}

if, for every test function
\varphi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega,\mathbb{R})
, one has

(2.5)

\begin{split}\int_{\Omega}\langle\nabla u,\nabla\varphi\rangle\,\mathrm{d}x+\frac{c_{N,s}}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\mathbb{R}^{N}}\!\!\!\!\!\frac{(u(x)-u(y))(\varphi(x)-\varphi(y))}{|x-y|^{N+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y=\int_{\Omega}f\varphi\,\mathrm{d}x.\end{split}

Furthermore, one can more generally say that
a function
u\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
weakly satisfies
{\mathcal{L}}u\geq f

in
\Omega
if,
for every nonnegative
v\in\mathbb{X}(\Omega)
, one has

(2.6)

\begin{split}\int_{\Omega}\langle\nabla u,\nabla v\rangle\,\mathrm{d}x&+\frac{c_{N,s}}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\mathbb{R}^{N}}\!\!\!\!\!\frac{(u(x)-u(y))(v(x)-v(y))}{|x-y|^{N+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y\geq\int_{\Omega}fv\,\mathrm{d}x.\end{split}

One can say that
u
weakly satisfies
{\mathcal{L}}u\leq f
in
\Omega

if
v:=-u
weakly satisfies
{\mathcal{L}}v\geq f

in
\Omega
.

Finally, if
u
is a weak solution of (
2.4
) and

u\in\mathbb{X}(\Omega)
, we say that

u
is a
weak solution
of problem
(
2.1
).

Remark 2.2
.

We notice that, if
u\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
is any weak solution
of (
2.4
) (for some
f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
),
from the density of
C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega,\mathbb{R})
in
\mathbb{X}(\Omega)
we get

(2.7)

\begin{split}\int_{\Omega}\langle\nabla u,\nabla v\rangle\,\mathrm{d}x&+\frac{c_{N,s}}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{(u(x)-u(y))(v(x)-v(y))}{|x-y|^{N+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y\\
&\quad=\int_{\Omega}fv\,\mathrm{d}x\qquad\text{for all $v\in\mathbb{X}(\Omega)$}.\end{split}

In particular, if
u_{f}
is a solution of (
2.1
)
(so that
u_{f}\in\mathbb{X}(\Omega)
), we are entitled to use

u_{f}
as a test function in (
2.7
),
obtaining

\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u_{f}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+\frac{c_{N,s}}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{(u_{f}(x)-u_{f}(y))^{2}}{|x-y|^{N+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y=\int_{\Omega}fu_{f}\,\mathrm{d}x.

Having introduced the functional framework in which we work,
we now prove the basic existence result in Theorem
1.1
.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

We consider on the space
\mathbb{X}(\Omega)
the bilinear form

B
and the linear map
F
defined, respectively, as follows:

\displaystyle(\star)\,\,B(u,v):=\int_{\Omega}\langle\nabla u,\nabla v\rangle\,\mathrm{d}x+\frac{c_{N,s}}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\mathbb{R}^{N}}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\frac{(u(x)-u(y))(v(x)-v(y))}{|x-y|^{N+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y,

\displaystyle(\ast)\,\,F(u):=\int_{\Omega}fu\,\mathrm{d}x.

Using (
2.3
) and the fact that
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
is continuously
embedded into
H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
, we see that both
B
and
F

are (well-posed and) continuous with respect
to the Hilbert structure of
\mathbb{X}(\Omega)
.
Moreover, we have that

\displaystyle B(u,u)

\displaystyle=\|\nabla u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\frac{c_{N,s}}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{|u(x)-u(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{N+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y\geq\|\nabla u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=\|u\|^{2}_{\mathbb{X}(\Omega)},

for all
u\in\mathbb{X}(\Omega)
.
Hence, we are in the position to apply Lax-Milgram’s theorem,
ensuring the existence of
a unique function
u_{f}\in\mathbb{X}(\Omega)
such that

(2.8)

\text{$B(u_{f},v)=F(v)$ for every $v\in\mathbb{X}(\Omega)$},

further satisfying the estimate

\displaystyle\|u_{f}\|_{\mathbb{X}(\Omega)}\leq\mathbf{c}\,\|F\|_{(\mathbb{X}(\Omega))^{*}}=\mathbf{c}\,\sup_{\|v\|_{\mathbb{X}(\Omega)}=1}|F(v)|\leq\mathbf{c}\,\sup_{\|v\|_{\mathbb{X}(\Omega)}=1}\int_{\Omega}|f|\,|v|\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq\mathbf{c}\,\sup_{\|v\|_{\mathbb{X}(\Omega)}=1}\|f\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\|v\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq\mathbf{c}\,\|f\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)},

with the positive constant
\mathbf{c}
possibly varying from line to line
(actually, this argument shows that
\|u_{f}\|_{\mathbb{X}(\Omega)}\leq\mathbf{c}\|f\|_{H^{-1}(\Omega)}
).
From (
2.8
) we immediately conclude that
u_{f}
is a weak solution of (
2.4
) (according to Definition
2.1
),
and the proof is complete.
∎

Since one of the aims of this paper is to
prove regularity results for weak
solution of (
2.1
), it is convenient to fix
the following definition. We set

(2.9)

\mathcal{C}_{s}(\mathbb{R}^{N}):=\bigg\{u\in C(\mathbb{R}^{N},\mathbb{R}):\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{|u(x)|}{1+|x|^{N+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}x<\infty\bigg\}.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
f:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
. We say that a function

u:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
is a
classical solution

of (
2.1
) if it satisfies the following properties:

(1)

u\in\mathcal{C}_{s}(\mathbb{R}^{N})\cap C^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{R})
;

(2)

{\mathcal{L}}u=f
pointwise in
\Omega
;

(3)

u(x)=0
for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\Omega
.

If
u
fulfills only (1) and (2), we say that
u
is a
classical
solution
of (
2.4
).

We observe that, if
u\in\mathcal{C}_{s}(\mathbb{R}^{N})\cap C^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{R})

is a classical solution of (
2.1
),
it follows from (1) and (3) in Definition
2.3
that

(2.10)

u\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N}),\qquad\text{with\,\,\,$\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}=\max_{x\in\overline{\Omega}}|u(x)|$.}

Remark 2.4
.

As it is very well-known, the (linear) space

\mathcal{C}_{s}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
is ‘good’ for dealing with the fractional
Laplacian. In fact, if
u\in C^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{R})\cap\mathcal{C}_{s}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
, it is possible
to compute
(-\Delta)^{s}u(x)
pointwise for every
x\in\Omega
, and

\displaystyle(-\Delta)^{s}u(x)

\displaystyle=c_{N,s}\;\mathrm{P.V.}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{u(x)-u(y)}{|x-y|^{N+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=-\frac{c_{N,s}}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{u(x+z)+u(x-z)-2u(x)}{|z|^{N+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}z.

Moreover, it is easy to check that

(-\Delta)^{s}u\in L^{\infty}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\Omega)
.

The next remark describes the relation between weak and classical
solutions.

Remark 2.5
.

Let
f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
and let

u\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
be a weak solution of (
2.4
).
If we further assume that

u\in\mathcal{C}_{s}(\mathbb{R}^{N})\cap C^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{R})
, we can compute

{\mathcal{L}}u(x)=-\Delta u(x)+(-\Delta)^{s}u(x)\quad\text{for every
$x\in\Omega$}.

Then, it is not difficult to check that
{\mathcal{L}}u=f
pointwise in
\Omega
,
so that
u
is a classical solution of (
2.4
).
Conversely, if
u\in\mathcal{C}_{s}(\mathbb{R}^{N})\cap C^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{R})
is a classical
solution of (
2.4
) further satisfying

u\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
, then
u
is also a weak solution of (
2.4
).

A simple consequence of Theorem
1.1
is the solvability
of the non-homogeneous Dirichlet problem, as follows:

Corollary 2.6
.

Let
f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
and
g\in\mathcal{C}_{s}(\mathbb{R}^{N})\cap C^{2}(\overline{\Omega},\mathbb{R})\cap H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
.
Then, there exists a unique weak solution
u\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
of
the non-homogeneous Dirichlet problem

(2.11)

\begin{cases}{\mathcal{L}}u=f&\text{in $\Omega$},\\
u\big|_{\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\Omega}=g.\end{cases}

Proof.

We observe that, since
g\in\mathcal{C}_{s}(\mathbb{R}^{N})\cap C^{2}(\overline{\Omega},\mathbb{R})
, the function
{\mathcal{L}}g=-\Delta g+(-\Delta)^{s}g
can be computed pointwise in

\Omega
, and

{\mathcal{L}}g\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)\subseteq L^{2}(\Omega).

Hence, we can apply Theorem
1.1
to get the existence
of a unique weak solution
v\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})

of

\begin{cases}{\mathcal{L}}v=f-{\mathcal{L}}g&\text{in $\Omega$},\\
v\big|_{\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\Omega}=0.\end{cases}

Setting
u:=v+g
, it is then immediate to see that

u\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
solves (
2.11
), thus completing the proof of
Corollary
2.6
.
∎

Remark 2.7
.

Another functional framework naturally arising with
the mixed operator
{\mathcal{L}}
is given by the spaces
\mathcal{H}:=H^{1}(\Omega)\cap H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{N})

and

\mathcal{H}_{0}:=\big\{u\in\mathcal{H}:\,\text{$u\equiv 0$ a.e.\,in $\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\Omega$}\big\}.

In principle, one is tempted to define
a
\mathcal{H}
-weak solution of (
2.4
)

as a function
u\in\mathcal{H}
such that (
2.5
)
holds for every
\varphi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega,\mathbb{R})
, and
a
\mathcal{H}_{0}
-weak solution of (
2.1
)
as a
\mathcal{H}
-weak solution
of (
2.4
) which belongs to
\mathcal{H}_{0}
.

The use of Lax-Milgram’s theorem would provide the existence of
a unique
\mathcal{H}_{0}
-solution
v_{f}
of (
2.1
) (for
some
f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
). Nevertheless, we prefer to use the functional
setting in (
2.2
), since it possesses better density properties
for smooth compactly supported functions, allowing us to use the positive and negative parts
of the solution as a test function in (
2.5
).

This technical aspect is crucial for us in proving a weak maximum principle as in
Theorem
1.2
.

Another possible functional setting consists in requiring that,
for weak solutions,
identity (
2.5
) is fulfilled
for all
v\in\mathcal{H}_{0}
.
Nevertheless, this approach
causes a technical difficulty in the integration by parts formula, which is needed
to prove that a classical solution
of (
2.4
)-(
2.1
) is also a weak
solution, thus confirming that our choice in (
2.2
) is likely to be
structurally more robust to deal with the PDE properties of the solutions.

3.
Some maximum principles for
{\mathcal{L}}

In this section we establish some weak/strong maximum principles
for
{\mathcal{L}}
, as defined in (
1.1
).
To begin with, we prove the weak maximum principle for
weak solutions
in
Theorem
1.2
.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Arguing by contradiction, we assume that there exists
a set
E\subseteq\Omega
, with
positive Lebesgue measure
,
such that
u<0
a.e. on
E
. We then define

w:=u_{-}=\max\{-u,0\}

and we observe that, since
u\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
and
u\geq 0
a.e. in
\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\Omega
,
one has

(3.1)

\text{$w\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})$ and $w\equiv 0$ a.e.\,in $\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\Omega$}.

Moreover,

(3.2)

w=-u>0\qquad{\mbox{on $E$}}.

In particular, recalling formula (
2.2
),
from (
3.1
) we have that
w\in\mathbb{X}(\Omega)
.
Hence, according to formula (
2.7
)
in Remark
2.2
, we can use
w
as a test function
in (
2.6
), obtaining that

(3.3)

\begin{split}0&\leq\;\int_{\Omega}\langle\nabla u,\nabla w\rangle\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\mathbb{R}^{N}}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\frac{(u(x)-u(y))(w(x)-w(y))}{|x-y|^{N+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y\\
&=\;-\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u_{-}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\mathbb{R}^{N}}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\frac{(u(x)-u(y))(w(x)-w(y))}{|x-y|^{N+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y\\
&\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\mathbb{R}^{N}}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\frac{(u(x)-u(y))(w(x)-w(y))}{|x-y|^{N+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y.\end{split}

On the other hand,
denoting by
v
the positive part of
u
, that is
v:=u_{+}=\max\{u,0\}
,
we have that
u=v-w
. Therefore, utilizing (
3.1
) and (
3.2
), we have that

(3.4)

\begin{split}&\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{\big(u(x)-u(y)\big)\big(w(x)-w(y)\big)}{|x-y|^{N+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y\\
&\qquad=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{\big(v(x)-v(y)\big)\big(w(x)-w(y)\big)}{|x-y|^{N+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{|w(x)-w(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{N+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y\\
&\qquad\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{\big(v(x)-v(y)\big)\big(w(x)-w(y)\big)}{|x-y|^{N+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y-\int_{E\times(\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\Omega)}\frac{|w(x)-w(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{N+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y\\
&\qquad=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{\big(v(x)-v(y)\big)\big(w(x)-w(y)\big)}{|x-y|^{N+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y-\int_{E\times(\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\Omega)}\frac{|w(x)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{N+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y\\
&\qquad<\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{\big(v(x)-v(y)\big)\big(w(x)-w(y)\big)}{|x-y|^{N+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y.\end{split}

Also, a ‘case-by-case’ computation shows that

(v(x)-v(y))(w(x)-w(y))=(u_{+}(x)-u_{+}(y))(u_{-}(x)-u_{-}(y))\leq 0

for almost every
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
. Plugging this information into (
3.4
),
we obtain that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\mathbb{R}^{N}}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\frac{(u(x)-u(y))(w(x)-w(y))}{|x-y|^{N+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y<0.

This is in contradiction with (
3.3
), and thus
u\geq 0
a.e. on
\Omega
, as desired.
∎

As regards
classical solutions
, we now prove
Theorem
1.3
:

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

First of all, we establish the weak maximum principle in (
1.3
).
For this, we suppose by contradiction that there exists a point
\xi\in\Omega

such that
u(\xi)<0
.
Since
u\in C(\mathbb{R}^{N},\mathbb{R})
and

\overline{\Omega}
is compact, we can thus find

x_{0}\in\overline{\Omega}
such that

(3.5)

u(x_{0})=\min_{\overline{\Omega}}u<0.

Moreover, since
u\geq 0
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\Omega
, we necessarily
have that
x_{0}\in\Omega
, that is
x_{0}
in an
interior

minimum point for
u
in
\Omega
. Hence,

\Delta u(x_{0})\geq 0
and

(3.6)

(-\Delta)^{s}u(x_{0})={\mathcal{L}}u(x_{0})+\Delta u(x_{0})\geq\Delta u(x_{0})\geq 0.

Moreover, we have that
u(x_{0})\leq u(x)
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
.
Thus, we get

(3.7)

\begin{split}(-\Delta)^{s}u(x_{0})&=c_{N,s}\;\mathrm{P.V.}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{u(x_{0})-u(y)}{|x_{0}-y|^{N+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}y=c_{N,s}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{u(x_{0})-u(y)}{|x_{0}-y|^{N+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}y\leq 0.\end{split}

Owing to (
3.6
), and taking into account the fact
the integrand function in (
3.7
) is non-positive, we then conclude that

\text{$u\equiv u(x_{0})$ on $\mathbb{R}^{N}$}.

In particular, since
u\geq 0
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\Omega
,
we get
u(x_{0})\geq 0
, but this is clearly in contradiction with (
3.5
).
Hence,
u\geq 0
on
\Omega
, which establishes (
1.3
), as desired.

Now we prove the
strong maximum principle for classical solutions in (
1.4
).
To this end, we recall (
3.6
) and we note that

(3.8)

0\leq(-\Delta)^{s}u(x_{0})=c_{N,s}\;\mathrm{P.V.}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{u(x_{0})-u(y)}{|x_{0}-y|^{N+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}y=-c_{N,s}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{u(y)}{|x_{0}-y|^{N+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}y.

Additionally, by (
1.3
), we know that
u\geq 0
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
.
Comparing this with (
3.8
), we obtain that
u\equiv 0
throughout
\mathbb{R}^{N}
. This establishes (
1.4
) as desired.
∎

We shall see in Appendix
A
that,
due to the presence of the non-local term
(-\Delta)^{s}
,
a maximum principle analogous to that in (
1.4
)

does not hold
if the condition
u\geq 0
is satisfied only
on
\partial\Omega
(as in the classical case).
Furthermore, we shall also show that
maximum principles analogous to
Theorem
1.2
and in (
1.3
) of Theorem
1.3

do not hold

for

{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}:=\Delta+(-\Delta)^{s}.

As a simple consequence of the weak maximum principle in (
1.3
)
we have a uniqueness result, as follows:

Corollary 3.1
.

There exists
at most one
classical solution
of (
2.1
).

Proof.

Let
u_{1},u_{2}\in\mathcal{C}_{s}(\mathbb{R}^{N})\cap C^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{R})
be two classical
solutions of problem (
2.1
). Introducing the function

v:=u_{1}-u_{2}
, it is immediate to recognize that

(a)
v\in\mathcal{C}_{s}(\mathbb{R}^{N})\cap C^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{R})
;

(b)
v
is a solution of the Dirichlet problem

\begin{cases}{\mathcal{L}}u=-\Delta u+(-\Delta)^{s}u=0&\text{pointwise in $\Omega$},\\
u=0&\text{in $\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\Omega$}.\end{cases}

Thus, by the weak maximum principle in (
1.3
) we readily conclude that
v\equiv 0

on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, so that
u_{1}\equiv u_{2}
on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
.
This ends the proof.
∎

4.
Interior and boundary regularity for
{\mathcal{L}}

The main aim of this section is to prove
both interior and boundary regularity
for
{\mathcal{L}}
. To be more precise, we
first establish interior
H^{m}
-regularity
for the weak solutions of (
2.4
), that is
Theorem
1.4
;
then, we prove boundary regularity for
the solutions of (
2.1
),
that is Theorem
1.5
.

4.1.
Interior
H^{m}
-regularity and proof of Theorem
1.4

To begin with, we prove the following
H^{2}
-regularity
theorem, which in turn will serve as
the basic step to prove interior
H^{m}
-regularity in Theorem
1.4
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
and let
u\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
be a weak solution of
equation (
2.4
). Then,
u\in H^{2}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\Omega)
.
Furthermore, given any open set
V
with
\overline{V}\subseteq\Omega
, there exists a constant
\Lambda>0
, independent of

u
, such that

(4.1)

\|u\|_{H^{2}(V)}\leq\Lambda\Big(\|f\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|u\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}\Big).

Proof.

Let
V
be a fixed open set with
\overline{V}\subseteq\Omega
,
and let
\rho_{0}>0
be such that

V_{\rho}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}:\,\mathrm{dist}(x,V)<\rho\}\subseteq\Omega\quad\text{for every $\rho\in[0,2\rho_{0}]$}.

Moreover, let
\zeta\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N},\mathbb{R})

be a cut-off function
satisfying

(a)
\zeta\equiv 1
on
V
and

\mathrm{supp}(\zeta)\subseteq V_{\rho_{0}}
;

(b)
0\leq\zeta\leq 1
on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
.

Finally, for every fixed
k\in\{1,\ldots,N\}

and every
0<|h|<\rho_{0}
, we set

(4.2)

\varphi:=D_{k}^{-h}\big(\zeta^{2}\,D^{h}_{k}u\big),\qquad\text{where
$D^{h}_{k}w(x):=\frac{w(x+he_{k})-w(x)}{h}$}.

We notice that, since
u\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
, then
D^{h}_{k}u\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
.
Also, in light of (a), we have that
\zeta\in C_{0}^{\infty}(V_{\rho_{0}},\mathbb{R})
.
As a result,

\hat{\varphi}:=\zeta^{2}\,D_{k}^{h}u\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})\qquad\text{and}\qquad\text{supp}(\hat{\varphi})\subseteq V_{\rho_{0}}\subseteq\Omega.

As a consequence, by the definition of
\varphi
in (
4.2
),
we see that
\varphi\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})

and
\mathrm{supp}(\varphi)\subseteq V_{2\rho_{0}}\subseteq\Omega
,
which implies that
\varphi\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
.
Therefore, we are in the position of
using
\varphi

as a test function in (
2.7
), obtaining
(after a standard ‘integration by parts’
for difference quotients)

(4.3)

\begin{split}&\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{\Omega}\zeta^{2}\,|D^{h}_{k}(\partial_{x_{i}}u)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+\sum_{i=1}^{N}2\int_{\Omega}\zeta\,D^{h}_{k}u\,\partial_{x_{i}}\zeta\,D^{h}_{k}(\partial_{x_{i}}u)\,\mathrm{d}x\\[2.84544pt]
&\qquad+\frac{c_{N,s}}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{\big(D^{h}_{k}u(x)-D^{h}_{k}u(y)\big)\big(\zeta^{2}(x)D_{k}^{h}u(x)-\zeta^{2}(y)D_{k}^{h}u(y)\big)}{|x-y|^{N+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y\\[2.84544pt]
&\qquad=\int_{\Omega}f\varphi\,\mathrm{d}x.\end{split}

Now, by exploiting Cauchy-Swcharz’s inequality
and the classical Young inequality (with
\varepsilon=1/2
), we
obtain the following estimate

(4.4)

\begin{split}&\bigg|\sum_{i=1}^{N}2\int_{\Omega}\zeta\,D^{h}_{k}u\,\partial_{x_{i}}\zeta\,D^{h}_{k}(\partial_{x_{i}}u)\,\mathrm{d}x\bigg|\leq 2\int_{\Omega}\zeta\,|D^{h}_{k}u|\,|\nabla\zeta|\,|D_{k}^{h}(\nabla u)|\,\mathrm{d}x\\[2.84544pt]
&\qquad\leq\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\zeta^{2}\,|D_{k}^{h}(\nabla u)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+2\,\big(\sup_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|\nabla\zeta|\big)\cdot\int_{V_{\rho_{0}}}|D^{h}_{k}u|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\\[2.84544pt]
&\qquad\leq\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\zeta^{2}\,|D_{k}^{h}(\nabla u)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+C\,\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x,\end{split}

for some
C>0
,
where in the last inequality we have used the fact that

(4.5)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|D^{h}_{k}\omega|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\leq 4N^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|\nabla\omega|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x,\qquad\text{for every $\omega\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})$}

(see, e.g.,
[
33
, Theorem. 3, Chapter 5.8.2]
).
Gathering together estimates (
4.3
)
and (
4.4
), we get

(4.6)

\begin{split}&\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\zeta^{2}\,|D_{k}^{h}(\nabla u)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x-C\,\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\\[2.84544pt]
&\qquad+\frac{c_{N,s}}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{\big(D^{h}_{k}u(x)-D^{h}_{k}u(y)\big)\big(\zeta^{2}(x)D_{k}^{h}u(x)-\zeta^{2}(y)D_{k}^{h}u(y)\big)}{|x-y|^{N+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y\\[2.84544pt]
&\qquad\leq\int_{\Omega}f\varphi\,\mathrm{d}x.\end{split}

Now we estimate the integral in the right hand side
of (
4.6
). To this end we first observe that, recalling (
4.2
)
and exploiting once again (
4.5
), we have

\begin{split}&\int_{\Omega}\varphi^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\big|D^{-h}_{k}\big(\zeta^{2}D_{k}^{h}u\big)\big|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\\
&\qquad\leq 4N^{2}\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|\nabla(\zeta^{2}D^{h}_{k}u)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x=C\,\int_{V_{\rho_{0}}}|\nabla(\zeta^{2}D^{h}_{k}u)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\\
&\qquad\leq C\bigg(\int_{V_{\rho_{0}}}|D_{k}^{h}u|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}\zeta^{2}\,|D_{k}^{h}(\nabla u)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\bigg)\\
&\qquad\leq C\bigg(\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}\zeta^{2}\,|D_{k}^{h}(\nabla u)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\bigg),\end{split}

up to renaming
C>0
from line to line.
From this, using Young’s inequality (with
\varepsilon=1/(4C)
), we get

(4.7)

\begin{split}&\bigg|\int_{\Omega}f\varphi\,\mathrm{d}x\bigg|\leq\varepsilon\int_{\Omega}\varphi^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\Omega}f^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\\[2.84544pt]
&\qquad\leq\frac{1}{4}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+\frac{1}{4}\int_{\Omega}\zeta^{2}\,|D_{k}^{h}(\nabla u)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+4C\int_{\Omega}f^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x.\end{split}

By combining (
4.7
) with (
4.6
), we then obtain

(4.8)

\begin{split}&\frac{1}{4}\int_{\Omega}\zeta^{2}\,|D_{k}^{h}(\nabla u)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\\[2.84544pt]
&\qquad+\frac{c_{N,s}}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{\big(D^{h}_{k}u(x)-D^{h}_{k}u(y)\big)\big(\zeta^{2}(x)D_{k}^{h}u(x)-\zeta^{2}(y)D_{k}^{h}u(y)\big)}{|x-y|^{N+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y\\[2.84544pt]
&\qquad\leq C\bigg(\int_{\Omega}f^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\bigg),\end{split}

up to relabeling
C>0
.

We now provide a careful estimate of
the non-local term in the left hand side of (
4.8
), i.e,

(4.9)

J_{s}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{\big(D^{h}_{k}u(x)-D^{h}_{k}u(y)\big)\big(\zeta^{2}(x)D_{k}^{h}u(x)-\zeta^{2}(y)D_{k}^{h}u(y)\big)}{|x-y|^{N+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y.

To this end we first notice that, with obvious algebraic
manipulation, we can write

(4.10)

J_{s}=J_{0,s}+J_{1,s}+J_{2,s},

where

\begin{split}J_{0,s}&\;:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\mathbb{R}^{N}}\zeta^{2}(x)\cdot\frac{|D_{k}^{h}u(x)-D_{k}^{h}u(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{N+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y,\\
J_{1,s}&\,:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\mathbb{R}^{N}}D_{k}^{h}u(y)\,\zeta(x)\cdot\frac{\big(D^{h}_{k}u(x)-D^{h}_{k}u(y)\big)\big(\zeta(x)-\zeta(y)\big)}{|x-y|^{N+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y\\
{\mbox{and }}\quad J_{2,s}&\;:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\mathbb{R}^{N}}D_{k}^{h}u(y)\,\zeta(y)\cdot\frac{\big(D^{h}_{k}u(x)-D^{h}_{k}u(y)\big)\big(\zeta(x)-\zeta(y)\big)}{|x-y|^{N+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y.\end{split}

We notice that, by exchanging the variables
x
and
y
, we see that

(4.11)

\begin{split}J_{0,s}\;&=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\mathbb{R}^{N}}\zeta^{2}(y)\cdot\frac{|D_{k}^{h}u(y)-D_{k}^{h}u(x)|^{2}}{|y-x|^{N+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y\\
\;&=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\mathbb{R}^{N}}\zeta^{2}(y)\cdot\frac{|D_{k}^{h}u(x)-D_{k}^{h}u(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{N+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y.\end{split}

Moreover, since
\zeta\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N},\mathbb{R})
, for every
y\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
we have

(4.12)

\begin{split}&\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{|\zeta(x)-\zeta(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{N+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}x\\
\leq\;&\Big(\sup_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|\nabla\zeta|^{2}\Big)\cdot\int_{\{|x-y|\leq 1\}}\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{|x-y|^{N+2(s-1)}}+4\,\Big(\sup_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|\zeta|^{2}\Big)\cdot\int_{\{|x-y|>1\}}\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{|x-y|^{N+2s}}\\
=:\;&\mathbf{c}(\zeta,s).\end{split}

Using Young’s inequality once again, (
4.12
) and (
4.5
),
we can estimate
J_{1,s}
as follows:

(4.13)

\begin{split}|J_{1,s}|\leq\;&\frac{1}{4}\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\mathbb{R}^{N}}\zeta^{2}(x)\,\cdot\frac{|D_{k}^{h}u(x)-D_{k}^{h}u(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{N+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y\\
&\qquad+4\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\mathbb{R}^{N}}|D_{k}^{h}u(y)|^{2}\cdot\frac{|\zeta(x)-\zeta(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{N+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y\\
\leq\;&\frac{J_{0,s}}{4}+4\,\mathbf{c}(\zeta,s)\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|D_{k}^{h}u(y)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}y\\
\leq\;&\frac{J_{0,s}}{4}+4\,\mathbf{c}(\zeta,s)\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|\nabla u|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x.\end{split}

Similarly, making again use of Young’s inequality and (
4.5
),
and recalling (
4.11
),
we have the following estimate for
J_{2,s}
:

(4.14)

\begin{split}|J_{2,s}|\leq\;&\frac{1}{4}\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\mathbb{R}^{N}}\zeta^{2}(y)\,\cdot\frac{|D_{k}^{h}u(x)-D_{k}^{h}u(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{N+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y\\
&\qquad+4\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\mathbb{R}^{N}}|D_{k}^{h}u(y)|^{2}\cdot\frac{|\zeta(x)-\zeta(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{N+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y\\
\leq\;&\frac{J_{0,s}}{4}+4\,\mathbf{c}(\zeta,s)\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|D_{k}^{h}u(y)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}y\\
\leq\;&\frac{J_{0,s}}{4}+4\,\mathbf{c}(\zeta,s)\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|\nabla u|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x.\end{split}

Gathering together (
4.10
), (
4.13
) and (
4.14
), we obtain that

(4.15)

J_{s}\geq\frac{J_{0,s}}{2}-8\,\mathbf{c}(\zeta,s)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|\nabla u|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x.

Finally, by combining (
4.8
) and (
4.15
),
and recalling (
4.9
),
we derive

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}\int_{\Omega}\zeta^{2}\,|D_{k}^{h}(\nabla u)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+\frac{c_{N,s}}{2}\cdot\frac{J_{0,s}}{2}

\displaystyle\qquad\leq 4{c_{N,s}}\,\mathbf{c}(\zeta,s)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|\nabla u|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+C\bigg(\int_{\Omega}f^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\bigg)

\displaystyle\qquad\leq{C}\bigg(\int_{\Omega}f^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{{\mathbb{R}^{N}}}|\nabla u|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\bigg),

up to renaming
C>0
.
From this, since
J_{0,s}\geq 0
, we get

\begin{split}\int_{V}|D_{k}^{h}(\nabla u)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x&\leq\int_{\Omega}\zeta^{2}\,|D_{k}^{h}(\nabla u)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\leq{C}\bigg(\int_{\Omega}f^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|\nabla u|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\bigg),\end{split}

and thus, owing to
[
33
, Theorem 3, Chapter 5.8.2]
,
we conclude that

u\in H^{2}(V)
and

\|u\|_{H^{2}(V)}\leq\Lambda\Big(\|f\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|u\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}\Big),

for a suitable
\Lambda>0
. Finally, since
a careful inspection of the proof
shows that
\Lambda
does not depend on
u
,
we also obtain estimate (
4.1
).
∎

Starting from Theorem
4.1
, our next aim
it to establish Theorem
1.4
.
In contrast to the classical case, the proof of Theorem
1.4

is not merely a bootstrap argument based on Theorem
4.1
: in fact,
the presence of the non-local term
(-\Delta)^{s}
in
{\mathcal{L}}

prevents us to take derivatives of
equation (
2.4
).
To overcome this technical issue, we need to combine
a suitable truncation argument with the use of difference quotient.
This is done in the following preliminary lemmata.

In the sequel, we shall make use of the following notation:
given an arbitrary set
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N}
and a number
\delta>0
, we define

(4.16)

A_{\delta}:=\big\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}:\,\mathrm{dist}(x,A)<\delta\big\}.

Moreover, for any
\omega\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
, we set

(4.17)

\omega_{h}(x):=D^{h}_{k}\omega(x)=\frac{\omega(x+he_{k})-\omega(x)}{h},

with
h\in\mathbb{R}
and
k\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
u\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
be a solution of (
2.4
),
and let
\mathcal{O}
be an open set with

\overline{\mathcal{O}}\subseteq\Omega
.
Let
\rho:=\mathrm{dist}(\mathcal{O},\partial\Omega)>0
.
If
|h|<\rho
, then
u_{h}
solves

(4.18)

{\mathcal{L}}u_{h}=f_{h}\quad\text{in $\mathcal{O}$}.

Proof.

We observe that, for any
\varphi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathcal{O},\mathbb{R})
,
we have that

(4.19)

\begin{split}&\int_{\mathcal{O}}\langle\nabla u_{h},\nabla\varphi\rangle\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\langle\nabla u_{h},\nabla\varphi\rangle\,\mathrm{d}x=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\langle\nabla u,\nabla\psi_{-h}\rangle\,\mathrm{d}x,\end{split}

where we have used the notation

(4.20)

\psi_{-h}(x):=-\frac{\varphi(x-he_{k})-\varphi(x)}{h}.

Moreover, since
\mathrm{supp}(\varphi)\subseteq\mathcal{O}
, recalling the
notation in (
4.16
), we have that

\mathrm{supp}(\varphi(\cdot-he_{k}))\subseteq\mathcal{O}+he_{k}\subseteq\big\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}:\,\mathrm{dist}(x,\mathcal{O})<|h|\big\}=\mathcal{O}_{|h|}.

Hence, if
|h|<\rho
we obtain that

(a)
\psi_{-h}\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N},\mathbb{R})
;

(b)

\mathrm{supp}(\psi_{-h})\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{\rho}\subseteq\Omega
.

In particular,

(4.21)

\psi_{-h}\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathcal{O}_{\rho},\mathbb{R}).

As a consequence, identity (
4.19
) can be written as

(4.22)

\int_{\mathcal{O}}\langle\nabla u_{h},\nabla\varphi\rangle\,\mathrm{d}x=-\int_{\Omega}\langle\nabla u,\nabla\psi_{-h}\rangle\,\mathrm{d}x.

Furthermore, we observe that, for all
h\in\mathbb{R}
, we have

(4.23)

\begin{split}&\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{(u_{h}(x)-u_{h}(y))(\varphi(x)-\varphi(y))}{|x-y|^{N+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y\\[2.84544pt]
&\qquad=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{(u(x)-u(y))(\psi_{-h}(x)-\psi_{-h}(y))}{|x-y|^{N+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y.\end{split}

Thus, by combining (
4.22
) with (
4.23
),
and recalling (
4.21
) and the fact that
u
solves (
2.4
),
we get

(4.24)

\begin{split}&\int_{\mathcal{O}}\langle\nabla u_{h},\nabla\varphi\rangle\,\mathrm{d}x+\frac{c_{N,s}}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{(u_{h}(x)-u_{h}(y))(\varphi(x)-\varphi(y))}{|x-y|^{N+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y\\[2.84544pt]
&\quad=-\int_{\Omega}\langle\nabla u,\nabla\psi_{-h}\rangle\,\mathrm{d}x-\frac{c_{N,s}}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{(u(x)-u(y))(\psi_{-h}(x)-\psi_{-h}(y))}{|x-y|^{N+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y\\[2.84544pt]
&\qquad=-\int_{\mathcal{O}_{\rho}}f\,\psi_{-h}\,\mathrm{d}x.\end{split}

Now we observe that, if
|h|<\rho
,

\mathrm{supp}(\varphi)\subseteq\mathcal{O}\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{\rho}\cap(\mathcal{O}_{\rho}-he_{k}),

and therefore, recalling (
4.17
) and (
4.20
),
we have that

\displaystyle-\int_{\mathcal{O}_{\rho}}f\,\psi_{-h}\,\mathrm{d}x=\;

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{O}_{\rho}}f(x)\bigg(\frac{\varphi(x-he_{k})-\varphi(x)}{h}\bigg)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\;

\displaystyle\frac{1}{h}\bigg(\int_{\mathcal{O}_{\rho}-he_{k}}f(x+he_{k})\varphi(x)\,\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\mathcal{O}_{\rho}}f(x)\,\varphi(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\bigg)

\displaystyle=\;

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{O}}f_{h}\,\varphi\,\mathrm{d}x.

As a consequence of this and (
4.24
),

\int_{\mathcal{O}}\langle\nabla u_{h},\nabla\varphi\rangle\,\mathrm{d}x+\frac{c_{N,s}}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{(u_{h}(x)-u_{h}(y))(\varphi(x)-\varphi(y))}{|x-y|^{N+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y=\int_{\mathcal{O}}f_{h}\,\varphi\,\mathrm{d}x,

for any
\varphi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathcal{O},\mathbb{R})
.
Thus, recalling (
1.1
), this implies
that
u_{h}
solves (
4.18
), as desired.
∎

Thanks to Lemma
4.2
, we can prove a
‘weaker version’
of Theorem
1.4
.

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
m\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
and
f\in H^{m}(\Omega)
. If
u\in H^{m+1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
is any weak solution
of (
2.4
), then

u\in H^{m+2}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\Omega)
.

Furthermore, given any open set
V
with

\overline{V}\subseteq\Omega
, there exists a constant
\Lambda_{m}>0
, independent of
the function
u
, such that

(4.25)

\|u\|_{H^{m+2}(V)}\leq\Lambda_{m}\Big(\|f\|_{H^{m}(\Omega)}+\|u\|_{H^{m+1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}\Big).

Proof.

We proceed by induction on
m\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
.
First of all, the case
m=0
(that is,
f\in L^{2}(\Omega)

and
u\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
)
is given by Theorem
4.1
. Then, we assume that
Proposition
4.3
holds for some
m\geq 0
,
and we prove that it still holds for

m+1
.

Let
V
be a fixed open set with
\overline{V}\subseteq\Omega
,
and let
\mathcal{O}
be an open subset of
\Omega
satisfying
\overline{V}\subseteq\mathcal{O}

and
\overline{\mathcal{O}}\subseteq\Omega
.
Moreover, let
f\in H^{m+1}(\Omega)
and let

u\in H^{m+2}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
be a weak solution of (
2.4
).

Setting
\rho:=\mathrm{dist}(\mathcal{O},\partial\Omega)>0
,
and recalling the notation in (
4.17
), if
|h|<\rho

we know from Lemma
4.2
that

u_{h}
is a weak solution of the equation

\text{${\mathcal{L}}u_{h}=f_{h}$ in $\mathcal{O}$}.

Moreover, since
u\in H^{m+2}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
, we clearly
have that
u_{h}\in H^{m+1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
. As a result, we are in the position of applying
the inductive hypothesis to
u_{h}
, thus obtaining

(a)
u_{h}\in H^{m+2}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathcal{O})
and, in particular,

u_{h}\in H^{m+2}(V)
;

(b)  there exists a constant
\Lambda_{m}>0
, independent of
h
, such that

\|u_{h}\|_{H^{m+2}(V)}\leq\Lambda_{m}\Big(\|f_{h}\|_{H^{m}(\mathcal{O})}+\|u_{h}\|_{H^{m+1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}\Big)\qquad\text{for $|h|<\rho$}.

Furthermore, we observe that, since
u\in H^{m+2}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
, one has

\|u_{h}\|_{H^{m+1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}\leq\mathbf{c}\,\|u\|_{H^{m+2}(\mathbb{R}^{N})},

where
\mathbf{c}>0
is a suitable constant which
is independent of
h
(see, e.g.,
[
33
]
). Analogously,
since
f\in H^{m+1}(\Omega)
, we also have

\|f_{h}\|_{H^{m}(\mathcal{O})}\leq\mathbf{c}\,\|f\|_{H^{m+1}(\Omega)}.

Gathering together these facts, we obtain

\|u_{h}\|_{H^{m+2}(V)}\leq\mathbf{c}\,\Lambda_{m}\Big(\|f\|_{H^{m+1}(\Omega)}+\|u\|_{H^{m+2}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}\Big),

and this estimate is
uniform
with respect
to
h\in(-\rho,\rho)
. On account of
[
33
, Theorem. 3, Chapter 5.8.2]
,
we then easily conclude that

u\in H^{m+3}(V)
. Moreover,

\|u\|_{H^{m+3}(V)}\leq\mathbf{c}\,\Lambda_{m}\Big(\|f\|_{H^{m+1}(\Omega)}+\|u\|_{H^{m+2}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}\Big).

This is precisely estimate (
4.25
), and the proof is complete.
∎

To remove the assumption that
u\in H^{m+1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
in Proposition
4.3
,
we need to perform a “truncation” argument: this is described
in the next two lemmata.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
\mathcal{O}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N}
be open,

\delta>0
, and
\alpha>N
. Let
z\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
be such that

(4.26)

z\equiv 0
a.e. on
\mathcal{O}_{\delta}
,

with the notation introduced in (
4.16
). Then, the following facts hold.

(i)

for every fixed
x\in\mathcal{O}_{\delta/2}
, we have

(4.27)

y\mapsto\frac{z(y)}{|x-y|^{\alpha}}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N}).

(ii)

The function
\mathcal{I}_{\alpha}[z]
defined as

(4.28)

\mathcal{I}_{\alpha}[z](x):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{z(y)}{|x-y|^{\alpha}}\,\mathrm{d}y

is of class
C^{\infty}
on
\mathcal{O}_{\delta/2}
.

Proof.

(i)  Let
x\in\mathcal{O}_{\delta/2}
. From (
4.26
) and
Hölder’s inequality, we have the following estimate

(4.29)

\begin{split}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{|z(y)|}{|x-y|^{\alpha}}\,\mathrm{d}y&=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\mathcal{O}_{\delta}}\frac{|z(y)|}{|x-y|^{\alpha}}\,\mathrm{d}y\\[2.84544pt]
&\leq\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\mathcal{O}_{\delta}}\frac{|z(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{\alpha}}\,\mathrm{d}y\bigg)^{1/2}\cdot\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\mathcal{O}_{\delta}}\frac{1}{|x-y|^{\alpha}}\,\mathrm{d}y\bigg)^{1/2}.\end{split}

On the other hand, since
x\in\mathcal{O}_{\delta/2}
, it is immediate to check that

(4.30)

|x-y|\geq\frac{\delta}{2}\qquad\text{for every $y\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\mathcal{O}_{\delta}$}.

By exploiting (
4.30
), we get from (
4.29
) that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{|z(y)|}{|x-y|^{\alpha}}\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\bigg(\frac{2}{\delta}\bigg)^{\alpha/2}\bigg(\int_{\{|x-y|\geq\delta/2\}}\frac{1}{|x-y|^{\alpha}}\,\mathrm{d}y\bigg)^{1/2}\cdot\|z\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\bigg(\frac{2}{\delta}\bigg)^{\alpha/2}\bigg(\int_{\{|w|\geq\delta/2\}}\frac{1}{|w|^{\alpha}}\,\mathrm{d}w\bigg)^{1/2}\cdot\|z\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}.

From this, reminding that
\alpha>N
and that

z\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
, we obtain (
4.27
).

(ii)  First of all, owing to (i),
the function
\mathcal{I}_{\alpha}[z]
is well-posed on
\mathcal{O}_{\delta/2}
.
To prove its smoothness on
\mathcal{O}_{\delta/2}

we show that, for every
fixed
x\in\mathcal{O}_{\delta/2}
and every

N
-tuple
\gamma=(\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{N})
of non-negative integers, one has

(4.31)

\big(\partial_{x_{1}}\big)^{\gamma_{1}}\cdots\big(\partial_{x_{N}}\big)^{\gamma_{N}}\mathcal{I}_{\alpha}[z](x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\mathcal{O}_{\delta}}\big(\partial_{x_{1}}\big)^{\gamma_{1}}\cdots\big(\partial_{x_{N}}\big)^{\gamma_{N}}\bigg(\frac{z(y)}{|x-y|^{\alpha}}\bigg)\mathrm{d}y.

To this end we first observe that, setting

|\gamma|:=\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{N}\gamma_{k}
, one has

(4.32)

\bigg|\big(\partial_{x_{1}}\big)^{\gamma_{1}}\cdots\big(\partial_{x_{N}}\big)^{\gamma_{N}}\bigg(\frac{z(y)}{|x-y|^{\alpha}}\bigg)\bigg|\leq\kappa_{\alpha}\,\frac{|z(y)|}{|x-y|^{\alpha+|\gamma|}}

for every
x\neq y\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
(here,
\kappa_{\alpha}
is a positive
constant only depending on
\alpha
). Thus, by assertion (i)
(applied to
\alpha+|\gamma|
in place of
\alpha
), we derive that

y\mapsto\mathcal{D}_{x}(y):=\big(\partial_{x_{1}}\big)^{\gamma_{1}}\cdots\big(\partial_{x_{N}}\big)^{\gamma_{N}}\bigg(\frac{z(y)}{|x-y|^{\alpha}}\bigg)\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})

for every fixed
x\in\mathcal{O}_{\delta/2}
.
On account of this fact,
and owing to
classical results
on the regularity of
parameter-depending integrals,
to establish (
4.31
) it suffices
to prove the following fact:
for every

N
-tuple
\gamma=(\gamma_{1},\ldots\gamma_{N})
of non-negative integers
and every
x_{0}\in\mathcal{O}_{\delta/2}
, there exist
r>0

and a function

(4.33)

\Theta=\Theta_{\gamma,x_{0},r}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\mathcal{O}_{\delta})

such that

(a)

B(x_{0},r)\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{\delta/2}
;

(b)

for every
x\in B(x_{0},r)
and every

y\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\mathcal{O}_{\delta}
, one has

(4.34)

\bigg|\big(\partial_{x_{1}}\big)^{\gamma_{1}}\cdots\big(\partial_{x_{N}}\big)^{\gamma_{N}}\bigg(\frac{z(y)}{|x-y|^{\alpha}}\bigg)\bigg|\leq\Theta(y).

To prove this statement, let
x_{0}\in\mathcal{O}_{\delta/2}
, and

\gamma=(\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{N})\in(\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\})^{N}
.
Moreover, let
r>0
be such that
B(x_{0},r)\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{\delta/2}
.
We claim that there exists a constant
\mathbf{c}>0
,
only depending on
x_{0}
and
r
, such that

(4.35)

\frac{|x-y|}{|x_{0}-y|}\geq\mathbf{c}\qquad\text{for all
$x\in B(x_{0},r)$ and $y\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\mathcal{O}_{\delta}$}.

Indeed, recalling (
4.30
), if
x\in B(x_{0},r)\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{\delta/2}

and
y\in(\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\mathcal{O}_{\delta})\cap B(x_{0},2r)
, one has

\frac{|x-y|}{|x_{0}-y|}\geq\frac{\delta/2}{2r}=\frac{\delta}{4r}.

On the other hand, if
y\in\big(\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\mathcal{O}_{\delta}\big)\setminus B(x_{0},2r)
,
we have that

|x-x_{0}|<r<\tfrac{1}{2}|x_{0}-y|,

and therefore, by triangle inequality,

\displaystyle\frac{|x-y|}{|x_{0}-y|}\geq\frac{|x_{0}-y|-|x-x_{0}|}{|x_{0}-y|}=1-\frac{|x-x_{0}|}{|x_{0}-y|}\geq\frac{1}{2}.

Gathering together these facts, we obtain (
4.35
) with

\mathbf{c}:=\min\big\{\delta/(4r),1/2\big\}.

Now, by combining (
4.32
) with (
4.35
) we get

\displaystyle\bigg|\big(\partial_{x_{1}}\big)^{\gamma_{1}}\cdots\big(\partial_{x_{N}}\big)^{\gamma_{N}}\bigg(\frac{z(y)}{|x-y|^{\alpha}}\bigg)\bigg|\leq\frac{\kappa_{\alpha}}{\mathbf{c}^{\alpha+|\gamma|}}\cdot\frac{|z(y)|}{|x_{0}-y|^{\alpha+|\gamma|}}=:\Theta_{\gamma,x_{0},r}(y),

for
every

x\in B(x_{0},r)
and every
y\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\mathcal{O}_{\delta}
, and
this gives (
4.34
).
Moreover, since

Let

To begin with, we prove the following theorem.

Theorem 4.7
.

Assume that
1
1

1

We observe that when
N=1,2

the boundedness of the energy solutions follows directly by Sobolev Embedding.

N\geq 3
, and
let

Then,

Remark 4.8

We point out that, on account of Theorem

Proof of Theorem
4.7
.

The proof employs the classical method by Stampacchia,
as extended in the nonlocal setting, see e.g. the proof of Proposition 9
in
[
45
]
and of Theorem 2.3 in
[
26
]
.
We give full details for the reader’s convenience.

Let
\delta>0
to be conveniently chosen later on.
Assuming that
u_{f}
is not identically zero
(otherwise there is nothing to prove),
we set

(4.42)

\tilde{u}:=\frac{\delta\,{u}_{f}}{\|{u}_{f}\|_{L^{2^{*}}(\Omega)}+\|f\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}}\qquad{\mbox{and}}\qquad\tilde{f}:=\frac{\delta\,f}{\|{u}_{f}\|_{L^{2^{*}}(\Omega)}+\|f\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}},

where

2^{*}:=\frac{2N}{N-2}
.
In this way, we have that

(4.43)

\begin{cases}{\mathcal{L}}\tilde{u}=\tilde{f}&\text{in $\Omega$},\\
\tilde{u}=0&\text{in $\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\Omega$}.\end{cases}

Also, for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we define
C_{k}:=1-2^{-k}
and

v_{k}:=\tilde{u}-C_{k},\quad w_{k}:=(v_{k})_{+}:=\max\{v_{k},0\},\quad U_{k}:=\|w_{k}\|_{L^{2^{*}}(\Omega)}^{2}.

We point out that, by the Dominated
Convergence Theorem,

(4.44)

\lim_{k\to+\infty}U_{k}=\lim_{k\to+\infty}\|w_{k}\|^{2}_{L^{2^{*}}(\Omega)}=\|(\tilde{u}-1)_{+}\|^{2}_{L^{2^{*}}(\Omega)}.

Also, if we take
k:=0
, we see that

w_{0}=(v_{0})_{+}=(\tilde{u}-C_{0})_{+}=\tilde{u}_{+}
, and thus

(4.45)

U_{0}=\left(\int_{\Omega}w_{0}^{2^{*}}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\right)^{2/2^{*}}\leq\left(\int_{\Omega}\tilde{u}^{2^{*}}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\right)^{2/2^{*}}=\|\tilde{u}\|^{2}_{L^{2^{*}}(\Omega)}\leq\delta^{2},

which can be taken conveniently small in what follows.
In addition, in
\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\Omega
we have that

v_{k+1}=-C_{k+1}\leq 0
and thus

w_{k+1}=0.

We can then use
w_{k+1}
as test function
and deduce from (

) that

(4.46)

\begin{split}&\int_{\Omega}\nabla w_{k+1}(x)\cdot\nabla\tilde{u}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x+\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\mathbb{R}^{N}}\!\!\!\!\!\frac{(w_{k+1}(x)-w_{k+1}(y))(\tilde{u}(x)-\tilde{u}(y))}{|x-y|^{N+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y\\
&\qquad=\int_{\Omega}w_{k+1}(x)\,\tilde{f}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x.\end{split}

We also remark that, for a.e.

, we have (see, e.g.,
[
45

)

(4.47)

\begin{split}|w_{k+1}(x)-w_{k+1}(y)|^{2}&=|(v_{k+1})_{+}(x)-(v_{k+1})_{+}(y)|^{2}\\[5.69046pt]
&\leq((v_{k+1})_{+}(x)-(v_{k+1})_{+}(y))(v_{k+1}(x)-v_{k+1}(y))\\[5.69046pt]
&=(w_{k+1}(x)-w_{k+1}(y))(\tilde{u}(x)-\tilde{u}(y)).\end{split}

Moreover,

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\nabla w_{k+1}(x)\cdot\nabla\tilde{u}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega\cap\{\tilde{u}>C_{k}\}}\nabla v_{k+1}(x)\cdot\nabla\tilde{u}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}|\nabla w_{k+1}(x)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x.

From this, (
4.46
) and (
4.47
) we conclude that

\begin{split}&\int_{\Omega}|\nabla w_{k+1}(x)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\leq\int_{\Omega}w_{k+1}(x)\,\tilde{f}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x.\end{split}

Hence, by Sobolev Inequality,

(4.48)

U_{k+1}=\left(\int_{\Omega}|w_{k+1}(x)|^{2^{*}}\,\mathrm{d}x\right)^{2/2^{*}}\leq C\,\int_{\Omega}|\nabla w_{k+1}(x)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\leq C\,\int_{\Omega}w_{k+1}(x)\,|\tilde{f}(x)|\,\mathrm{d}x,

for some
C>0
. Also,
v_{k+1}\leq v_{k}
and therefore

(4.49)

w_{k+1}\leq w_{k}.

Moreover, we observe that

w_{k}=(\tilde{u}-C_{k})_{+}=\left(\tilde{u}-C_{k+1}+\frac{1}{2^{k+1}}\right)_{+}=\left(v_{k+1}+\frac{1}{2^{k+1}}\right)_{+},

and, as a result,

(4.50)

\{w_{k+1}>0\}=\{v_{k+1}>0\}\subseteq\left\{w_{k}>\frac{1}{2^{k+1}}\right\}.

We also observe that

2^{*}-\frac{2^{*}}{p}-1>2^{*}-\frac{2^{*}}{N/2}-1=\frac{2N}{N-2}-\frac{4}{N-2}-1=1.

Hence, we can define

(4.51)

q:=2^{*}\,\bigg(2^{*}-\frac{2^{*}}{p}-1\bigg)^{-1}<2^{*}.

We observe that

q>\frac{2^{*}}{2^{*}-1}>1.

In addition,

(4.52)

\frac{1}{2^{*}}+\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}=1.

From this, (
4.49
) and (
4.50
), using the Hölder Inequality
with exponents
2^{*}
,
p
and

(4.53)

\begin{split}&\int_{\Omega}w_{k+1}(x)\,|\tilde{f}(x)|\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega\cap\{w_{k+1}>0\}}w_{k+1}(x)\,|\tilde{f}(x)|\,\mathrm{d}x\\[5.69046pt]
&\qquad\leq\|\tilde{f}\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\,\|w_{k+1}\|_{L^{2^{*}}(\Omega)}\,|\Omega\cap\{w_{k+1}>0\}|^{1/q}\\[5.69046pt]
&\qquad\leq\|w_{k}\|_{L^{2^{*}}(\Omega)}\,\left|\Omega\cap\left\{w_{k}>\frac{1}{2^{k+1}}\right\}\right|^{1/q}\\[5.69046pt]

The first step towards the proof of Theorem
1.5

consists in an elementary computation on functions
which have a convex portion in their graphs.

Lemma 4.9
.

Let
s\in(0,1)
,
d>\ell>0
and
v:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
. Assume that

v\in C^{1,1}((-\infty,d))\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})

and that
v
is convex in
(-\infty,d)
.
Then, for every
x\in(0,\ell)
,

(-\Delta)^{s}v(x)\leq\frac{2c_{1,s}\,\|v\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}}{s\,(d-\ell)^{2s}}.

Proof.

In the principal value sense, we have that, for every
x\in(0,\ell)
,

\displaystyle\int_{2x-d}^{d}\frac{v(y)-v(x)}{|x-y|^{1+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}y=\int_{x-d}^{d-x}\frac{v(x+z)-v(x)}{|z|^{1+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}z=\int_{x-d}^{d-x}\frac{v(x+z)-v(x)-v^{\prime}(x)z}{|z|^{1+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}z\geq 0,

thanks to the convexity assumption. As a result,

\frac{(-\Delta)^{s}v(x)}{c_{1,s}}\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}\setminus(2x-d,d)}\frac{v(x)-v(y)}{|x-y|^{1+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}y=\int_{\mathbb{R}\setminus(x-d,d-x)}\frac{v(x)-v(x+z)}{|z|^{1+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}z.

We stress that, in the latter integral, we have that
|z|\geq d-x\geq d-\ell
.
Hence,

\frac{(-\Delta)^{s}v(x)}{c_{1,s}}\leq 2\|v\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}\int_{\{|z|\geq d-\ell\}}\frac{\mathrm{d}z}{|z|^{1+2s}}=\frac{2\|v\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}}{s\,(d-\ell)^{2s}},

and the proof is complete.
∎

Lemma 4.10
.

Let
s\in(0,1)

and

w_{\alpha}(x):=\begin{cases}x_{+}^{\alpha}&\text{if $x<2L$},\\
(2L)^{\alpha}&\text{if $x\geq 2L$}.\end{cases}

Then, there exists
C>0

,

and

|(-\Delta)^{s}w_{\alpha}(x)|\leq\begin{cases}C&\text{if $\alpha>2s$},\\
C\,\big(1+|\log x|\big)&\text{if $\alpha=2s$}.\end{cases}

Proof.

Up to scaling, it is not restrictive
to suppose that
L:=1
.
Given any point
x\in(0,1)
, we use the substitution

to see that

\displaystyle\frac{(-\Delta)^{s}w_{\alpha}(x)}{c_{1,s}}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{x^{\alpha}-\min\{y_{+}^{\alpha},2^{\alpha}\}}{|x-y|^{1+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}y=x^{\alpha-2s}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{1-\min\{z_{+}^{\alpha},(2/x)^{\alpha}\}}{|1-z|^{1+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}z,

where the principal value notation has been omitted for the sake of
shortness. Then, we observe that

\displaystyle A_{1}:=\left|\int_{-\infty}^{0}\frac{1-\min\{z_{+}^{\alpha},(2/x)^{\alpha}\}}{|1-z|^{1+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}z\right|=\int_{-\infty}^{0}\frac{\mathrm{d}z}{(1-z)^{1+2s}}=\int^{+\infty}_{1}\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t^{1+2s}}=\frac{1}{2s}.

Similarly,

\displaystyle A_{2}

\displaystyle:=\left|\int_{2}^{+\infty}\frac{1-\min\{z_{+}^{\alpha},(2/x)^{\alpha}\}}{|1-z|^{1+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}z\right|\leq\int_{2}^{+\infty}\frac{1+\min\{z_{+}^{\alpha},(2/x)^{\alpha}\}}{(z-1)^{1+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle=\int_{1}^{+\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t^{1+2s}}+\int_{2}^{2/x}\frac{z^{\alpha}}{(z-1)^{1+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}z+\left(\frac{2}{x}\right)^{\alpha}\int_{2/x}^{+\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}z}{(z-1)^{1+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}(1+x^{2s-\alpha}\ell(x)),

for some
C_{1}>0
, where

\ell(x):=\begin{cases}1+|\log x|&\text{if $\alpha=2s$},\\
1&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

In addition, using the principal value notation,

\displaystyle A_{3}

\displaystyle:=\left|\int_{0}^{2}\frac{1-\min\{z_{+}^{\alpha},(2/x)^{\alpha}\}}{|1-z|^{1+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}z\right|=\left|\int_{0}^{2}\frac{1-z^{\alpha}}{|1-z|^{1+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}z\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{1-(1+t)^{\alpha}}{|t|^{1+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}t\right|=\left|\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{(1+t)^{\alpha}-1-\alpha t}{|t|^{1+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}t\right|\leq C_{2},

for some
C_{2}>0
. All in all, we find that

\frac{|(-\Delta)^{s}w_{\alpha}(x)|}{c_{1,s}}\leq x^{\alpha-2s}(A_{1}+A_{2}+A_{3})\leq A_{1}+A_{3}+x^{\alpha-2s}A_{2}\leq C_{3}(1+\ell(x)),

for some
C_{3}>0
, yielding the desired result.
∎

From Lemma
4.10
, we obtain the following barrier.

Lemma 4.11
.

Let
s\in(0,1)
. There exist
a number
d>0
and a function

\beta\in\mathcal{C}_{s}(\mathbb{R})\cap C^{2}((0,d),\mathbb{R})
satisfying the following properties:

•

there exists
C_{0}>0
such that for all
x\geq d

(4.58)

\beta(x)\geq C_{0}\,;

•

for all
x\leq 0
,

(4.59)

\beta(x)=0\,;

•

there exists
C_{1}\geq 1
, independent of
d
, such that

(4.60)

\frac{x}{C_{1}}\leq\beta(x)\leq C_{1}x\qquad\text{for all $x\in(0,d)$};

•

there exists
C_{2}>0
, independent of
d
, such that

(4.61)

{\mathcal{L}}\beta(x)\geq-C_{2}\qquad\text{for all $x\in(0,d)$.}

Furthermore,
\beta\in H^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R})
.

Proof.

We distinguish two cases, according to the value of
s
.

Case I:

s\in(1/2,1)
.
In this case, we let

\rho(s):=\frac{2s-1}{2(1-s)}\qquad\text{and}\qquad J:=\begin{cases}[\rho(s)]&\text{if $\rho_{s}\notin\mathbb{N}$},\\
\rho(s)-1&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Also, for each
j\in\mathbb{N}
with
0\leq j\leq J+1
, we set
\alpha_{j}:=1+2j(1-s)
.
We observe that, for all
j\in\{0,\dots,J\}
, we have

\alpha_{j}\leq 1+2J(1-s)<1+(2s-1)=2s.

Therefore, for all
j\in\{0,\dots,J\}
,
we can define
(-\Delta)^{s}x_{+}^{\alpha_{j}}
and,
by homogeneity, we see that, for all
x>0
,

(-\Delta)^{s}x_{+}^{\alpha_{j}}=\kappa_{j}\,x_{+}^{\alpha_{j}-2s},

for a suitable
\kappa_{j}\in\mathbb{R}
.
As a matter of fact, since
\alpha_{j}\geq 1
, we have that
x_{+}^{\alpha_{j}}

is a convex function and therefore
(-\Delta)^{s}x_{+}^{\alpha_{j}}<0

in
(0,+\infty)
. From this, we get

(4.62)

\kappa_{j}<0\qquad\text{for every $j=0,\ldots,J$}.

We also point out that, for every
j\in\{0,\dots,J\}
,

\displaystyle\alpha_{j}-2s

\displaystyle=\alpha_{j}-2+2(1-s)=1+2j(1-s)-2+2(1-s)

\displaystyle=1+2(j+1)(1-s)-2=\alpha_{j+1}-2.

Now we define
\{c_{0},\dots,c_{J+1}\}
as follows. We let
c_{0}:=1
, and then,
recursively,
for every index
j\in\{1,\dots,J+1\}
,

(4.63)

c_{j}:=-\frac{\kappa_{j-1}\,c_{j-1}}{\alpha_{j}\,(\alpha_{j}-1)}.

We stress that this definition is well posed, since, if
j\in\{1,\dots,J\}
,

\alpha_{j}\geq 1+2(1-s)>1.

From this and (
4.62
), it follows that

(4.64)

c_{j}>0\qquad\text{for every $j=0,\ldots,J+1$}.

Hence, we consider the function
\tilde{\beta}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
defined as follows

(4.65)

\tilde{\beta}(x):=\sum_{j=0}^{J}c_{j}\,x^{\alpha_{j}}_{+}.

Since
\alpha_{j}<2s
for every
0\leq j\leq J
,
it is easy to recognize that

•

\tilde{\beta}(x)\geq 0
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
;

•

\tilde{\beta}\in\mathcal{C}_{s}(\mathbb{R})\cap C^{2}((0,\infty),\mathbb{R})

and
\tilde{\beta}\in H^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R})
.

Moreover, for every
x>0
we have

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}\tilde{\beta}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sum_{j=0}^{J}c_{j}\alpha_{j}(\alpha_{j}-1)\,x^{\alpha_{j}-2}+\sum_{j=0}^{J}c_{j}\kappa_{j}\,x^{\alpha_{j}-2s}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sum_{j=1}^{J}c_{j}\alpha_{j}(\alpha_{j}-1)\,x^{\alpha_{j}-2}+\sum_{j=1}^{J}c_{j-1}\kappa_{j-1}\,x^{\alpha_{j}-2}+c_{J}\kappa_{J}\,x^{\alpha_{J}-2s}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2\sum_{j=1}^{J}c_{j}\alpha_{j}(\alpha_{j}-1)\,x^{\alpha_{j}-2}+\,c_{J}\kappa_{J}\,x^{\alpha_{J}-2s},

where (
4.63
) was used in the latter line.
As a consequence, taking
d\in(0,1)
to be chosen conveniently small
in what follows,
employing the notation in (
4.56
) with
L:=1
,
and introducing the function

(4.66)

\beta_{\sharp}:=\tilde{\beta}+c_{J+1}w_{\alpha_{J+1}},

we obtain that
\beta_{\sharp}
satisfies the following properties:

•

\beta_{\sharp}\geq 0
on
\mathbb{R}
(as
c_{J+1}>0
and
w_{\alpha_{J+1}}\geq 0
on
\mathbb{R}
);

•

\beta_{\sharp}\in\mathcal{C}_{s}(\mathbb{R})\cap C^{2}((0,d),\mathbb{R})
and
\beta_{\sharp}\in H^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R})
.

Furthermore, if
x\in(0,d)
, we get

\begin{split}{\mathcal{L}}\beta_{\sharp}(x)=\;&-2\sum_{j=1}^{J}c_{j}\alpha_{j}(\alpha_{j}-1)\,x^{\alpha_{j}-2}+\,c_{J}\kappa_{J}\,x^{\alpha_{J}-2s}-c_{J+1}\Delta w_{\alpha_{J+1}}(x)+c_{J+1}(-\Delta)^{s}w_{\alpha_{J+1}}(x)\\[2.84544pt]
=\;&-2\sum_{j=1}^{J}c_{j}\alpha_{j}(\alpha_{j}-1)\,x^{\alpha_{j}-2}+\,c_{J}\kappa_{J}\,x^{\alpha_{J}-2s}-c_{J+1}\alpha_{J+1}(\alpha_{J+1}-1)x^{{\alpha_{J+1}-2}}+c_{J+1}(-\Delta)^{s}w_{\alpha_{J+1}}(x)\\
=\;&-2\sum_{j=1}^{J+1}c_{j}\alpha_{j}(\alpha_{j}-1)\,x^{\alpha_{j}-2}+\,c_{J+1}(-\Delta)^{s}w_{\alpha_{J+1}}(x),\end{split}

where (
4.63
) was used once again. From this
and (
4.57
), we obtain that,

(4.67)

{\mathcal{L}}\beta_{\sharp}(x)\geq-C_{\sharp}|\log x|-2\sum_{j=1}^{J+1}c_{j}\alpha_{j}(\alpha_{j}-1)\,x^{\alpha_{j}-2}=C_{\sharp}\log x-2\sum_{j=1}^{J+1}c_{j}\alpha_{j}(\alpha_{j}-1)\,x^{\alpha_{j}-2},

for all
x\in(0,d)
and for some
C_{\sharp}>0
.
Now, we let

\tilde{W}(x):=\frac{x_{+}^{2}}{4}(3-2\log x)_{+}+\frac{2}{C_{\sharp}}\sum_{j=1}^{J+1}c_{j}\,x_{+}^{\alpha_{j}}\qquad{\mbox{and}}\qquad S(d):=\max_{(-\infty,d]}\tilde{W}.

Notice that

\lim_{d\searrow 0}\frac{S(d)}{d}=0.

As a result, by possibly shrinking
d\in(0,1)
, we can
additionally suppose that

(4.68)

S(d)\leq\frac{d}{4C_{\sharp}}.

Then, we take a continuous function

(4.69)

W:\mathbb{R}\to[0,2S(d)]

satisfying the following properties:

(i)

W(x)=\tilde{W}(x)
for all
x\leq d
;

(ii)

W(x)=0
for all
x\geq 2d
;

(iii)

W\in C^{\infty}\left(\left(0,+\infty\right),\mathbb{R}\right)
.

We define

\beta(x):=\beta_{\sharp}(x)-C_{\sharp}W(x).

Notice that, by the regularity of
\beta_{\sharp}
and

W
,
we have that

\text{$\beta\in\mathcal{C}_{s}(\mathbb{R})\cap C^{2}((0,d),\mathbb{R})$ and $\beta\in H^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R})$}.

Moreover, if
d>0
is sufficiently small,

(4.70)

W(x)=\frac{x^{2}}{4}(3-2\log x)+\frac{2}{C_{\sharp}}\sum_{j=1}^{J+1}c_{j}\,x^{\alpha_{j}}\qquad{{\mbox{for all $x\in(0,d)$,}}}

and therefore

W^{\prime\prime}(x)=-\log x+\frac{2}{C_{\sharp}}\sum_{j=1}^{J+1}c_{j}\alpha_{j}(\alpha_{j}-1)\,x^{\alpha_{j}-2}.

In addition, for small
d>0
, we have that

\text{$W\in C^{1,a}(-1,2d)$ for some $a\in(0,1)$},

and thus
(-\Delta)^{s}W\in C^{{0,1+a-2s}}(-1,2d)
.
As a consequence (see, e.g.,
[
46
, Propositions 2.1.7 and 2.1.8]
),
we obtain that, for all
x\in(0,d)
,

|(-\Delta)^{s}W(x)|\leq\tilde{C},

for a suitable
\tilde{C}>0
only depending on
N,s
and
a
(hence,
in particular,
\tilde{C}
is independent of
d
).
Then, we deduce from (
4.67
) that, for every
x\in(0,d)
,

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}\beta(x)

\displaystyle\geq C_{\sharp}\log x-2\sum_{j=1}^{J+1}c_{j}\alpha_{j}(\alpha_{j}-1)\,x^{\alpha_{j}-2}-C_{\sharp}{\mathcal{L}}W(x)

\displaystyle=C_{\sharp}\log x-2\sum_{j=1}^{J+1}c_{j}\alpha_{j}(\alpha_{j}-1)\,x^{\alpha_{j}-2}-C_{\sharp}\big(\log x+(-\Delta)^{s}W(x)\big)+2\sum_{j=1}^{J+1}c_{j}\alpha_{j}(\alpha_{j}-1)\,x^{\alpha_{j}-2}

\displaystyle=-C_{\sharp}(-\Delta)^{s}W(x)

\displaystyle\geq-C_{\sharp}\,\tilde{C}.

This proves (
4.61
).
Also, (
4.59
) is obvious. To proceed
further we observe that,
for every
x\in\mathbb{R}
, we have the estimate

(4.71)

\sum_{j=0}^{J}c_{j}\,x^{\alpha_{j}}_{+}+c_{J+1}w_{\alpha_{J+1}}(x)\geq c_{0}\,x^{\alpha_{0}}_{+}=x_{+}\geq\min\{x_{+},d\}.

As a result, since
\alpha_{0}=1<\alpha_{1}\leq\alpha_{j}

for all
j\in\{2,\ldots,J+1\}
, we
have that, for every
x\in\mathbb{R}
,

(4.72)

\begin{split}&\beta(x)+C_{\sharp}W(x)\\[2.84544pt]
&\qquad=\sum_{j=0}^{J}c_{j}\,x^{\alpha_{j}}_{+}+c_{J+1}w_{\alpha_{J+1}}\in\Big[\min\{x_{+},d\},\;\bar{C}\,\max\{x_{+},x^{\alpha_{J+1}}_{+}\}\Big],\end{split}

for a suitable
\bar{C}>0
. From this, we conclude that

(4.73)

\beta(x)\geq\min\{x_{+},d\}-C_{\sharp}W(x).

Now, if
x\geq d
, we obtain from (
4.68
), (
4.69
)
and (
4.73
)
that

(4.74)

\beta(x)\geq d-C_{\sharp}W(x)\geq d-2C_{\sharp}\,S(d)\geq\frac{d}{2}.

If instead
x\in(-\infty,d)
, we deduce from (
4.73
)
that

(4.75)

\beta(x)\geq x_{+}-\frac{C_{\sharp}\,x_{+}^{2}}{4}(3-2\log x)_{+}-2\sum_{j=1}^{J+1}c_{j}\,x_{+}^{\alpha_{j}}\geq\frac{x_{+}}{2},

by possibly redefining
d>0
in a conveniently small way.

We notice that (
4.74
) implies (
4.58
),
as desired. In addition, (
4.75
) proves
the first inequality in (
4.60
).
Besides, from (
4.70
)
and (
4.72
) we obtain the second inequality in (
4.60
),
thus concluding the proof in the case
s>1/2
.

Case II:

s\in(0,1/2]
. This case is indeed simpler:
it suffices
to rerun the preceding argument starting from (
4.66
),
taking
\tilde{\beta}\equiv 0
and

\beta_{\sharp}:=w_{1}=\begin{cases}x_{+}&\text{if $x<2$},\\
2&\text{if $x\geq 2$}.\end{cases}

We omit any further detail.
∎

Corollary 4.12
.

Let
s\in(0,1)
be fixed. There exist a number
\ell>0

and
a non-negative function
\gamma\in\mathcal{C}_{s}(\mathbb{R})\cap C^{2}((0,\ell),\mathbb{R})
such that

•

for all
x\leq 0
,

(4.76)

\gamma(x)=0;

•

there exists
c\in(0,1)
such that for all
x\in(0,\ell)

(4.77)

cx\leq\gamma(x)\leq\frac{x}{c}\;;

•

for all
x\geq\ell
,

(4.78)

\gamma(x)\geq 1;

•

for all
x\in(0,\ell)
,

(4.79)

{\mathcal{L}}\gamma(x)\geq 1.

Furthermore,
\gamma\in H^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R})
.

Proof.

We let
\beta
as in Lemma
4.11

and, in the notation of Lemma
4.11
,
we will choose
\ell\in(0,d/2)
to be suitably small.
Given
M>0
, we define

\beta_{*}(x):=\begin{cases}0&\text{if $x\leq 0$},\\
C_{2}x^{2}&\text{if $x\in(0,\ell)$},\\
2C_{2}\ell x-C_{2}\ell^{2}&\text{if $x\in[\ell,d]$},\\
C_{2}\ell(2d-\ell)&\text{if $x\in(d,+\infty)$},\end{cases}\qquad{\mbox{and}}\qquad\gamma(x):=M\big(\beta(x)-\beta_{*}(x)\big),

where
C_{2}>0
is as in Lemma
4.11
.
We point out that, by taking into account the regularity of
\beta

and
\beta_{*}
, it is very easy to see that

\text{$\gamma\in\mathcal{C}_{s}(\mathbb{R})\cap C^{2}((0,\ell),\mathbb{R})$ and
$\gamma\in H^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R})$}.

Moreover, since
\beta_{*}
is convex
in
(-\infty,d)
, by Lemma
4.9

there exists a suitable constant
C_{3}>0
such that,
for every
x\in(0,\ell)
, we have

(-\Delta)^{s}\beta_{*}\leq\frac{C_{3}\ell(2d-\ell)}{(d-\ell)^{2s}}.

As a consequence, as long as
d
and
\ell
are sufficiently small we have

(-\Delta)^{s}\beta_{*}\leq\frac{C_{3}\ell(2d-\ell)}{(d-\ell)^{2s}}\leq\frac{2C_{3}\ell d}{(d/2)^{2s}}\leq 2^{2s+1}C_{3}\ell d^{1-2s}\leq 2^{2s}C_{3}d^{2-2s}\leq\frac{C_{2}}{2},

Therefore, for all
x\in(0,\ell)
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{M}\,{\mathcal{L}}\gamma(x)\geq{\mathcal{L}}\beta(x)+\Delta\beta_{*}(x)-\frac{C_{2}}{2}\geq-C_{2}+2C_{2}-\frac{C_{2}}{2}=\frac{C_{2}}{2},

thanks to (
4.61
). By choosing
M\geq\frac{2}{C_{2}}
, we obtain (
4.79
),
as desired.
Moreover, (
4.76
) follows from (
4.59
). In addition,
by (
4.60
), and taking

\ell\leq\frac{1}{2C_{1}C_{2}},

if
x\in(0,\ell)
we obtain

\gamma(x)\geq M\left(\frac{x}{C_{1}}-C_{2}x^{2}\right)\geq M\left(\frac{1}{C_{1}}-C_{2}\ell\right)x\geq\frac{Mx}{2C_{1}}.

Similarly, recalling (
4.60
), we have

\gamma(x)\leq MC_{1}x.

These considerations imply (
4.77
).
Furthermore, exploiting (
4.58
) and (
4.60
),
we see that, for every
x\geq\ell
, one has

(4.80)

\beta(x)\geq\min\left\{C_{0},\frac{\ell}{C_{1}}\right\}=\frac{\ell}{C_{1}},

as long as
\ell
is sufficiently small.
Moreover, if
x\in[\ell,d]
,

(4.81)

\beta_{*}(x)\leq 2C_{2}\ell x\leq 2C_{2}\ell d.

Similarly, if
x>d
,

\beta_{*}(x)\leq 2C_{2}\ell d.

This and (
4.81
) give that, for all
x\geq\ell
,

\beta_{*}(x)\leq 2C_{2}\ell d\leq\frac{\ell}{2C_{1}},

provided that
d
is chosen sufficiently small.
From this and (
4.80
), we get

\gamma(x)\geq M\left(\frac{\ell}{C_{1}}-\frac{\ell}{2C_{1}}\right)=\frac{M\ell}{2C_{1}}\geq 1,

as long as
M\geq\frac{2C_{1}}{\ell}
.
This gives (
4.78
), as desired.
∎

The function
\gamma
constructed in Corollary
4.12
would provide a ‘good’ barrier for
the proof of Theorem
1.5

if, in addition,
\gamma\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
. In fact,
since we aim to apply the weak maximum principle
in Theorem
1.2
to the function

\pm u-M\gamma

(for a suitable
M\in\mathbb{R}
), and since
u\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
, it is crucial
to have

(4.82)

\gamma\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}).

On the other hand, since
property (
4.78
) shows that (
4.82
) cannot hold,
we need to perform a suitable
truncation argument.
This is described in the next lemma.

Lemma 4.13
.

Let
\mathcal{O}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N}
be a
bounded

open set, and let

\rho>0
be such that
\mathcal{O}\subseteq B(0,\rho)
. Moreover,
let
f\in\mathcal{C}_{s}(\mathbb{R}^{N})\cap C^{2}(\mathcal{O},\mathbb{R})
.
Finally, let
R>4\rho
and let
\varphi=\varphi_{R}\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{N},\mathbb{R})
satisfy the properties

(i)

\varphi\equiv 1
on
B(0,R)
;

(ii)

0\leq\varphi\leq 1
on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
;

(iii)

\mathrm{supp}(\varphi)\subseteq B(0,2R)
.

Then, setting
f^{\star}:=f\varphi
, for all
x\in\mathcal{O}
we have

\big|{\mathcal{L}}f^{\star}(x)-{\mathcal{L}}f(x)\big|\leq C\,\left(\frac{|f(x)|}{R^{2s}}+\kappa(R)\right),

where
C>0
is a constant only depending on
N,s
and
\rho
, and

\kappa(R):=\int_{\{|y|\geq R\}}\frac{|f(y)|}{1+|y|^{N+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}y.

Proof.

We first observe that, since
R>4\rho
, we have

R-{\rho}\geq\frac{R}{2}+\rho\geq\frac{R}{2}.

As a consequence, if
x\in\mathcal{O}\subseteq B(0,\rho)
and
y\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus B(0,R)
, we have

|x-y|\geq|y|-|x|\geq R-\rho\geq\frac{R}{2}.

Moreover, one also has

\displaystyle|x-y|\geq|y|-|x|\geq\frac{|y|}{2}+\frac{R}{2}-\rho\geq\frac{\rho+|y|}{2}\geq c_{\rho}\cdot\frac{1+|y|}{2},

with
c_{\rho}:=\min\{\rho,1\}
.
Since
\varphi=1
in
B(0,R)\supseteq\mathcal{O}
, for every
x\in\mathcal{O}
we then get

(4.83)

\begin{split}&\bigg|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{(f(x)-f(y))(\varphi(x)-\varphi(y))}{|x-y|^{N+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}y\bigg|\\[5.69046pt]
&\quad=\left|\int_{\{|y|\geq R\}}\frac{(f(x)-f(y))(1-\varphi(y))}{|x-y|^{N+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}y\right|\leq\int_{\{|y|\geq R\}}\frac{|f(x)-f(y)|}{|x-y|^{N+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}y\\[5.69046pt]
&\quad\leq|f(x)|\int_{\{|y|\geq R\}}\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{|x-y|^{N+2s}}+\int_{\{|y|\geq R\}}\frac{|f(y)|}{|x-y|^{N+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}y\\[5.69046pt]
&\quad\leq|f(x)|\int_{\{|z|\geq R/2\}}\frac{\mathrm{d}z}{|z|^{N+2s}}+(2/c_{\rho})^{N+2s}\int_{\{|y|\geq R\}}\frac{|f(y)|}{({1}+|y|)^{N+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}y\\[5.69046pt]
&\quad\leq C\left(\frac{|f(x)|}{R^{2s}}+\kappa(R)\right),\end{split}

for some
C=C(N,s,\rho)>0
. Similarly, for every
x\in\mathcal{O}
we have

(4.84)

\begin{split}&\big|(-\Delta)^{s}\varphi(x)\big|=c_{N,s}\,\left|\int_{\{|y|\geq R\}}\frac{1-\varphi(y)}{|x-y|^{N+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}y\right|\\[2.84544pt]
&\qquad\leq c_{N,s}\,\int_{\{|y|\geq R\}}\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{|x-y|^{N+2s}}\leq c_{N,s}\,\int_{\{|z|\geq R/2\}}\frac{\mathrm{d}z}{|z|^{N+2s}}\leq\frac{C}{R^{2s}},\end{split}

up to renaming
C>0
.
Since, obviously,

\Delta f^{\star}=\Delta f
on
\mathcal{O}
(remind that
\varphi\equiv 1
on

B(0,R)\subseteq\mathcal{O}
), if
x\in\mathcal{O}
we obtain
(see, e.g.,
[
7
, formula (2.11)]
)

(4.85)

\begin{split}&\big|{\mathcal{L}}f^{\star}(x)-{\mathcal{L}}f(x)\big|\\[2.84544pt]
&\qquad=\Big|(-\Delta)^{s}f^{\star}(x)-(-\Delta)^{s}f(x)\Big|\\[2.84544pt]
&\qquad=\bigg|\varphi(x)(-\Delta)^{s}f(x)+f(x)(-\Delta)^{s}\varphi(x)\\[2.84544pt]
&\qquad\qquad\quad-c_{N,s}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{(f(x)-f(y))(\varphi(x)-\varphi(y))}{|x-y|^{N+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}y-(-\Delta)^{s}f(x)\bigg|\\[2.84544pt]
&\qquad=\left|f(x)(-\Delta)^{s}\varphi(x)-c_{N,s}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{(f(x)-f(y))(\varphi(x)-\varphi(y))}{|x-y|^{N+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}y\right|\\[2.84544pt]
&\qquad\leq|f(x)|\,\big|(-\Delta)^{s}\varphi(x)\big|+c_{N,s}\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{(f(x)-f(y))(\varphi(x)-\varphi(y))}{|x-y|^{N+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}y\right|.\end{split}

The desired result thus follows by inserting (
4.83
)
and (
4.84
)
into (
4.85
).
∎

We are finally ready to prove Theorem
1.5
.

Proof of Theorem
1.5
.

The gist is that the function
\gamma
belongs to the space
\mathcal{C}_{s}(\mathbb{R}^{N})

defined in (
2.9
) (because
\gamma(x_{1})

grows like
x_{1}^{\alpha_{J}}
with
\alpha_{J}<2s
as
x_{1}\to+\infty
,
the highest exponent coming from (
4.65
)) and therefore
the corresponding quantity
\kappa(R)
introduced in Lemma
4.13

goes to zero as
R\to+\infty
. As a general philosophy,
the main difficulty with all of the truncations in the fractional setting is to control the
errors developed by nonlocality:
in this argument these errors are accounted for by the quantity
\kappa(R)
, which becomes negligible for
R
large.
In this sense, taking care of the fact that the function
\gamma
grows slower than
x_{1}^{2s}
once we leave the “boundary layer”
\{x_{1}<\ell\}

pays off now, since it allows us to have full control of the “contributions coming from infinity”.

The technical details of the proof go as follows.
Up to a rigid motion, we can assume that
p=0

and that

(4.86)

\Omega\subset\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}:\,x_{1}>0\}.

Moreover, if
\ell>0
is as in Corollary
4.12
,
we define
\Omega_{*}:=\Omega\cap\{x_{1}<\ell\}
.
Notice that, on account of (
4.86
), we have

(4.87)

\Omega_{*}\subset\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}:\,x_{1}\in(0,\ell)\}.

We now choose

\rho>0
in such a way that
\Omega\subseteq B(0,\rho)
, and we
let
R>0
be such that
R>4\rho
. Moreover,
if
\gamma
is as in Corollary
4.12
, we define

\Theta(x):=\gamma(x_{1})\cdot\varphi(x)\qquad{\mbox{for any }}x\in\mathbb{R}^{N},

where
\varphi=\varphi_{R}\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N},\mathbb{R})

satisfies (i)-(ii)-(iii) in the statement of
Lemma
4.13
.
Taking into account that

\gamma\in\mathcal{C}_{s}(\mathbb{R})\cap C^{2}((0,\ell),\mathbb{R})
,
\varphi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N},\mathbb{R})

and
\varphi\equiv 1
on the ball
B(0,R)\supseteq\Omega_{*}
,
it is readily seen that

\Theta\in\mathcal{C}_{s}(\mathbb{R}^{N})\cap C^{2}(\Omega_{*},\mathbb{R}).

Moreover, since
\gamma\in H^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R})
and
\mathrm{supp}(\varphi)\subseteq B(0,2R)
, we also have

\Theta\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N}).

Finally, by combining (
4.79
),
(
4.77
) and Lemma
4.13
, we obtain

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}\Theta(x)\geq{\mathcal{L}}\gamma(x_{1})-C\left(\frac{|\gamma(x_{1})|}{R^{2s}}+\kappa(R)\right)\geq 1-C\left(\frac{\ell}{c}\cdot\frac{1}{R^{2s}}+\kappa(R)\right)

for every
x\in\Omega_{*}\subseteq\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}:\,x_{1}\in(0,\ell)\}
.
In view of this last computation, by enlarging
R>0
if necessary, we get

(4.88)

{\mathcal{L}}\Theta(x)\geq\frac{1}{2}\qquad\text{for
every $x\in\Omega_{*}$}.

We then turn to use
\Theta\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
as a barrier to prove (
1.7
). To this end, we consider
the function
v:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
defined as

v(x):=u(x)-4\bar{C}\,\Theta(x),

where
\bar{C}
is as in (
1.6
).
We observe that, by (
4.88
), we have

(4.89)

\begin{split}{\mathcal{L}}v={\mathcal{L}}u-4\,\bar{C}\,{\mathcal{L}}\Theta\leq\bar{C}-\frac{4\bar{C}}{2}\leq 0\end{split}

in
\Omega_{*}
. Now we claim that, for a.e.
x\in{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\setminus\Omega_{*}
, we have

(4.90)

v(x)\leq 0.

To check this, we observe that

{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\setminus\Omega_{*}\subseteq\big({\mathbb{R}^{N}}\setminus\Omega\big)\cup\{x\in\Omega:\,x_{1}\geq\ell\}.

Hence we distinguish two cases.

•

x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\Omega
. In this case, since
u\equiv 0
a.e. in
\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\Omega
, we have

v(x)=-4\,\bar{C}\,\Theta(x)=-4\,\bar{C}\,(\gamma(x_{1})\cdot\varphi(x));

from this, since

\gamma\geq 0
on
\mathbb{R}
(by Corollary
4.12
) and
0\leq\varphi\leq 1

(see (ii) in Lemma
4.13
), we derive that

v\leq 0
a.e. in
\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\Omega
.

•

x\in\{x\in\Omega:\,x_{1}\geq\ell\}
. In this case,
using (
4.78
) and the fact that

\varphi\equiv 1
on
B(0,R)\supseteq\Omega
, we can write

\displaystyle v(x)

\displaystyle=u(x)-4\,\bar{C}\,\gamma(x_{1})\leq u(x)-4\,\bar{C}\leq\bar{C}-4\,\bar{C}\leq 0,

and this concludes the proof of (
4.90
).

From (
4.89
), (
4.90
) and Theorem
1.2

(notice that
v\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
, since the same is true of
both
u
and
\Theta
), we conclude that

v(x)\leq 0
for a.e.
x\in{\mathbb{R}^{N}}
.
Hence, we obtain
(see (
4.77
))

u(x)\leq 4\,\bar{C}\,\Theta(x)=4\,\bar{C}\,\gamma(x_{1})\leq\frac{4\,\bar{C}\,x_{1}}{c}\leq\frac{4}{c}\cdot\bar{C}\,|x|

for a.e.
x\in\Omega\cap B(0,\ell)\subseteq\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}:\,x_{1}\in(0,\ell)\}
, and this establishes (
1.7
).
∎

From Theorem
1.5
, we immediately obtain the following result.

Corollary 4.14
.

Let
\Omega
be open and strictly convex, and let

.
Let

be the
(

)

).

Then, there exists

such that, for every

, we have that

(4.91)

Proof.

Formula (

)
follows from Theorem
4.7
and (
1.7
),
applied to both

∎

We point out that the term

in (

) can be actually reabsorbed into

,

.

As a byproduct of Theorem

, we also establish Theorem

:

.

First of all, since

, we know from

, such that

(b)

Appendix A
Failure of the maximum principle

A.1.
The case of
{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}:=\Delta+(-\Delta)^{s}

The following examples show that
the weak maximum principle contained in (
1.3
)
does not hold

for the operator

(A.1)

{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}:=\Delta+(-\Delta)^{s}.

Example A.1
.

Let
s\in(0,1/2)
be arbitrarily fixed, and let

f:\mathbb{R}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R},\qquad f(x):=\begin{cases}x^{2}-1,&\text{if $|x|\leq 1$},\\
0,&\text{if $|x|>1$}.\end{cases}

Clearly,
f\in C_{b}(\mathbb{R})
(as

|f|\leq 1
). Moreover, setting
\Omega_{0}:=(-1,1)
, we also
have that
f\in C^{2}(\Omega_{0})
.

We claim that,
for every fixed
x\in\mathbb{R}
, one has

(A.2)

y\mapsto\frac{f(x)-f(y)}{|x-y|^{1+2s}}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}).

In order to prove (
A.2
), we distinguish three cases:

(i)

x\in(-1,1)
. In this case, reminding that

s\in(0,1/2)
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{|f(x)-f(y)|}{|x-y|^{1+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\qquad=|f(x)|\,\int_{-\infty}^{-1}\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{|x-y|^{1+2s}}+|f(x)|\,\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{|x-y|^{1+2s}}+\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{|x^{2}-y^{2}|}{|x-y|^{1+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\int_{-\infty}^{-1}\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{|x-y|^{1+2s}}+\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{|x-y|^{1+2s}}+2\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{|x-y|^{2s}}<\infty.

(ii)

x=\pm 1
. We perform the computations when
x=1
,
being the case
x=-1
completely analogous.
In this case, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{|f(x)-f(y)|}{|1-y|^{1+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{1-y^{2}}{|1-y|^{1+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}y\leq 2\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{1}{|1-y|^{2s}}<\infty.

(iii)

x\notin[-1,1]
. In this case, since
|x-y|\geq|x|-1
if

y\in(-1,1)
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{|f(x)-f(y)|}{|x-y|^{1+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{1-y^{2}}{|x-y|^{1+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}y\leq\frac{1}{(|x|-1)^{1+2s}}\int_{-1}^{1}(1-y^{2})\,\mathrm{d}y<\infty.

Summing up, the claimed (
A.2
) is completely established.

Now, we observe that,
for any
x\in(-1,1)
,

\displaystyle\frac{|(-\Delta)^{s}f(x)|}{c_{1,s}}\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{|f(x)-f(y)|}{|x-y|^{1+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\qquad\leq(1-x^{2})\int_{-\infty}^{-1}\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{|x-y|^{1+2s}}+(1-x^{2})\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{|x-y|^{1+2s}}+\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{|x^{2}-y^{2}|}{|x-y|^{1+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\qquad\leq(1-x^{2})\int_{-\infty}^{-1}\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{|x-y|^{1+2s}}+(1-x^{2})\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{|x-y|^{1+2s}}+2\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{|x-y|^{2s}}

\displaystyle\qquad\leq 2^{2-2s}\cdot\frac{1-s}{s(1-2s)}.

As a consequence, if
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
and if

f_{\varepsilon}(x):=f(x/\varepsilon)
, we have

{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}f_{\varepsilon}(x)=\frac{2}{\varepsilon^{2}}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2s}}\big((-\Delta)^{s}f\big)(x/\varepsilon)\geq\frac{2}{\varepsilon^{2}}\bigg(1-\varepsilon^{2-2s}\cdot\frac{2^{1-2s}\,c_{1,s}\,(1-s)}{s(1-2s)}\bigg),

for every
x\in\mathbb{R}
with
|x|<\varepsilon
. If we choose
\varepsilon_{0}
so small that

1-\varepsilon_{0}^{2-2s}\cdot\frac{2^{1-2s}\,c_{1,s}\,(1-s)}{s(1-2s)}>0,

we thus see that
f_{\varepsilon_{0}}
enjoys the following properties:

(a)

f_{\varepsilon_{0}}\in C^{2}(\Omega_{\varepsilon_{0}})\cap C_{b}(\mathbb{R})
, where

\Omega_{\varepsilon_{0}}:=(-\varepsilon_{0},\varepsilon_{0})
;

(b)

the
s
-Laplacian of
f_{\varepsilon_{0}}
is pointwise defined on the whole of
\mathbb{R}
;

(c)

f_{\varepsilon_{0}}\equiv 0
on
\mathbb{R}\setminus\Omega_{\varepsilon_{0}}
and

{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}f_{\varepsilon_{0}}>0
on
\Omega_{\varepsilon_{0}}
.

Since, obviously,
f_{\varepsilon_{0}}<0
on
\Omega_{\varepsilon_{0}}
, we conclude that
a weak maximum principle as in (
1.3
) does not hold
for
{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}=\Delta+(-\Delta)^{s}
.

Example A.2
.

By dropping
the assumption
u\equiv 0
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\Omega
, it is possible
to show that
{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}
in (
A.1
)
violates the weak maximum principle
in (
1.3
)
for every
s\in(0,1)
.
Indeed, let
s\in(0,1)
and let

f:\mathbb{R}^{N}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R},\qquad f(x):=|x|^{2}-1.

Moreover, let
\varphi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N},[0,1])
be a cut-off function
such that

(A.3)

\text{$\varphi\equiv 1$ on $\Omega:=B(0,1)$ and
$\varphi\equiv 0$ on $\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus B(0,2)$}.

We then set
u:=f\varphi
. Obviously,

u\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})\subseteq C^{2}(\Omega)\cap C_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
. Moreover,
by taking into account the properties of
\varphi
in (
A.3
), we see that

\Delta u=\Delta f={2N}
in
\Omega
and
(-\Delta)^{s}u\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
.

As a consequence, if
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
and
u_{\varepsilon}:=u(x/\varepsilon)
, we have

{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}u_{\varepsilon}(x)=\frac{{2N}}{\varepsilon^{2}}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2s}}\big((-\Delta)^{s}u\big)(x/{\varepsilon})\geq\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\Big({2N}-\varepsilon^{2-2s}\,\|(-\Delta)^{s}u\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}\Big),

for all
x\in\Omega_{\varepsilon}:=B(0,\varepsilon)
. We now argue as in Example
A.1
:
if
\varepsilon_{0}
is so small that

{2N}-\varepsilon_{0}^{2-2s}\,\|(-\Delta)^{s}u\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}>0,

then the function
u_{\varepsilon_{0}}
enjoys the following properties:

(a)

u_{\varepsilon_{0}}\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})\subseteq C^{2}(\Omega_{\varepsilon_{0}})\cap C_{b}(\mathbb{R})
;

(b)

the
s
-Laplacian of
u_{\varepsilon_{0}}
is pointwise defined on the whole of
\mathbb{R}

(and it is globally bounded);

(c)

u_{\varepsilon_{0}}\geq 0
on
\mathbb{R}\setminus\Omega_{\varepsilon_{0}}
and

{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}u_{\varepsilon_{0}}>0
on
\Omega_{\varepsilon_{0}}
.

Since, obviously,
u_{\varepsilon_{0}}<0
on
\Omega_{\varepsilon_{0}}
, we conclude that
a weak maximum principle as in (
1.3
) does not hold
for
{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}=\Delta+(-\Delta)^{s}
, for any
s\in(0,1)
.

A.2.
The rôle of the ‘non-local boundary conditions’

Throughout the sequel, given any
R>0
, we adopt the simplified notation

B_{R}:=B(0,R).

Then, we claim that for every fixed
r>1
there exists

v\in C^{2}(B_{1},\mathbb{R})\cap\mathcal{C}_{s}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
such that

(A.4)

\begin{cases}{\mathcal{L}}v=0&\text{in $B_{1}$},\\
\displaystyle\inf_{B_{r}\setminus B_{1}}v>0,\\
\displaystyle\min_{B_{1}}v<0.\end{cases}

From this, since the continuity of
v
implies that

v\geq 0
on
\partial B_{1}
,
we deduce that the weak maximum principle in (
1.3
)

does not hold
if one requires
u\geq 0
only on
\partial\Omega
.

To prove the existence of such a function
v
,
we let

\phi\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{N},\mathbb{R})
be such that

(a)
\phi\equiv-1

in
B_{r+3}\setminus B_{r+2}
and
\phi\equiv 0
outside
B_{r+4}\setminus B_{r+1}
;

(b)
-1\leq\phi\leq 0
on the whole of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
.

Since
\phi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N},\mathbb{R})\subseteq\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
, we have that
f:=-{\mathcal{L}}\phi

can be computed pointwise in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
and
f\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
.

As a consequence, from Theorem
1.6
we know that there exists
a unique classical solution
\mathfrak{u}_{\phi}\in C^{2}(B_{1},\mathbb{R})\cap\mathcal{C}_{s}(\mathbb{R}^{N})

of the problem

(A.5)

\begin{cases}{\mathcal{L}}u=f=-{\mathcal{L}}\phi&\text{in $B_{1}$},\\
u\equiv 0&\text{on $\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus B_{1}$}.\end{cases}

We then set
w:=\mathfrak{u}_{\phi}+\phi

and we notice that, thanks to the regularity of
\mathfrak{u}_{\phi}
and
\phi
,
one has that
w\in C^{2}(B_{1},\mathbb{R})\cap\mathcal{C}_{s}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
. Furthermore, from (
A.5
)
we obtain that

(A.6)

\begin{cases}{\mathcal{L}}w=0&\text{in $B_{1}$},\\
w\equiv\phi&\text{in $\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus B_{1}.$}\end{cases}

We now claim that

(A.7)

m:=\inf_{B_{1}}w<0.

For this, we argue by contradiction and we suppose that

w(x)\geq 0\qquad\text{for every $x\in B_{1}$}.

In particular, since
w\in C(\mathbb{R}^{N},\mathbb{R})
and
w\equiv\phi\equiv 0
on
\partial B_{1}
,
we can find an
interior
maximum point
x_{0}\in B_{1}
for
w
such that

(A.8)

w(x_{0})\geq 0.

Thus,

\Delta w(x_{0})\leq 0
and therefore, using the exterior condition in (
A.6
),

(A.9)

\begin{split}&0=\frac{{\mathcal{L}}w(x_{0})}{c_{N,s}}=\frac{\big(-\Delta+(-\Delta)^{s}\big)w(x_{0})}{c_{N,s}}\geq\frac{(-\Delta)^{s}w(x_{0})}{c_{N,s}}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{w(x_{0})-w(y)}{|x_{0}-y|^{N+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}y\\[2.84544pt]
&\qquad=\int_{B_{1}}\frac{w(x_{0})-w(y)}{|x_{0}-y|^{N+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}y+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus B_{1}}\frac{w(x_{0})-\phi(y)}{|x_{0}-y|^{N+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}y\\[2.84544pt]
&\qquad\geq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus B_{1}}\frac{w(x_{0})-\phi(y)}{|x_{0}-y|^{N+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}y,\end{split}

where we used that fact that
w(x_{0})\geq w(y)
for all
y\in B_{1}
in the last line.
Moreover, by assumption (a), we know that
\phi\equiv-1

on
B_{r+3}\setminus B_{r+2}
. Accordingly, using also (
A.8
) and assumption (b)
on
\phi
, we conclude from (
A.9
) that

\displaystyle 0\geq\int_{B_{r+3}\setminus B_{r+2}}\frac{w(x_{0})+1}{|x_{0}-y|^{N+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}y+\int_{(B_{r+2}\setminus B_{1})\cup(\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus B_{r+3})}\frac{w(x_{0})-\phi(y)}{|x_{0}-y|^{N+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\qquad\geq\int_{B_{r+3}\setminus B_{r+2}}\frac{w(x_{0})+1}{|x_{0}-y|^{N+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}y>0.

This contradiction proves (
A.7
).
With (
A.7
) at hand, we define

u(x):=w(x)-m,

and we observe that, in view of the properties of
w
, one has

(1)
u\in C^{2}(B_{1},\mathbb{R})\cap\mathcal{C}_{s}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
(as the same is true of
w
);

(2)
{\mathcal{L}}u={\mathcal{L}}w=0
pointwise on
B_{1}
;

(3)
u=-m>0
on
B_{r}\setminus B_{1}

(as
w\equiv\phi\equiv 0
on
B_{r}\setminus B_{1}
);

(4)
\inf_{B_{1}}u=0
.

Thus, by making use of (1)–(4),
we easily conclude that the function

v(x):=2u(x)+m,

belongs to
C^{2}(B_{1},\mathbb{R})\cap\mathcal{C}_{s}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
and satisfies (
A.4
).
