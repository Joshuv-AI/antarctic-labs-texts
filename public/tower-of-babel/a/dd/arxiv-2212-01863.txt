Inverse semigroups of metrics on doubles related to certain subsets
V. Manuilov

Abstract
. Recently we have shown that the equivalence classes of metrics on the double of a metric space X X form an inverse semigroup. Here we define an inverse subsemigroup related to a family of isometric subspaces of X X , which is more computable. As a special case, we study this subsemigroup related to the family of geodesic rays starting from the basepoint, for Euclidean spaces and for trees.



1.
Introduction

Recently, in
[
3
]
, we have shown that the equivalence classes of metrics on the double of a metric space
X
form an inverse semigroup. Here we define an inverse subsemigroup related to a family of isometric subspaces of
X
, which is more computable. As a special case, we study this subsemigroup related to the family of geodesic rays starting from the basepoint, for Euclidean spaces and for trees.

Let
X=(X,d_{X})
be a metric space with the metric
d_{X}
.
We say that a metric
\rho
on
X\sqcup X
is
compatible
with
d_{X}
if both restrictions of
\rho
onto each copy of
X
coincide with
d_{X}
. Recall that metrics
d
and
d^{\prime}
on
Y
are coarsely equivalent (we write
d\sim_{c}d^{\prime}
) if there exists a monotone homeomorphism
\varphi
of
(0,\infty)
such that
d(y_{1},y_{2})\leq\varphi(d^{\prime}(y_{1},y_{2}))
and
d^{\prime}(y_{1},y_{2})\leq\varphi(d(y_{1},y_{2}))
for any
y_{1},y_{2}\in Y

[
5
]
. Let
M(X)
be the set of coarse equivalence classes of metrics on the double
X\sqcup X
of
X
compatible with
d_{X}
.

It was shown in
[
3
]
that
M(X)
has a natural structure of an inverse semigroup. Here we recall this structure.
For convenience, we identify
X\sqcup X
with
X\times\{0,1\}
and write
x
for
(x,0)
and
x^{\prime}
for
(x,1)
, where
x\in X
.
Composition of two metrics,
\rho
and
\sigma
, on
X\sqcup X
, compatible with
d_{X}
, was defined in
[
2
]
by

\rho\circ\sigma(x,y^{\prime})=\inf_{u\in X}[\sigma(x,u^{\prime})+1+\rho(u,y^{\prime})];

\rho\circ\sigma(x,y)=\rho\circ\sigma(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})=d_{X}(x,y),\qquad x,y\in X.

Clearly, if
\rho_{1}\sim_{c}\rho_{2}
,
\sigma_{1}\sim_{c}\sigma_{2}
then
\rho_{1}\circ\sigma_{1}\sim_{c}\rho_{2}\circ\sigma_{2}
.
We write
[\rho]
for the coarse equivalence class of a metric
\rho
. It was shown in
[
3
]
that
M(X)
is an inverse semigroup with respect to this composition, with the pseudoinverse element for
s=[\rho]\in M(X)
given by
s^{*}=[\rho^{*}]
, where
\rho^{*}
is a metric on
X\sqcup X
compatible with
d_{X}
such that
\rho^{*}(x,y^{\prime})=\rho(y,x^{\prime})
for any
x,y\in X
.

The deficiency of the inverse semigroup
M(X)
is that it is too great, as Lemma
1.1
below shows.
Recall that, given a subset
A\subset X
, there is a metric
\rho_{A}
compatible with
d_{X}
such that

\rho_{A}(x,y^{\prime})=\inf_{a\in A}[d_{X}(x,a)+1+d_{X}(a,y)],

(1.1)

and
[\rho_{A}]=[\rho_{B}]
iff the Hausdorff distance between
A
and
B
is finite
[
3
]
.

Lemma 1.1
.

Let
X=\mathbb{R}_{+}=[0,\infty)
be the set of non-negative reals with the standard metric. Then the cardinality of
M(X)
is at least continuum.

Proof.

Let
\lambda\in(1,\infty)
,
A_{\lambda}=\{\lambda^{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\}
. Clearly, the Hausdorff distance between
A_{\lambda}
and
A_{\mu}
is infinite when
\mu\neq\lambda
.

∎

Our aim is to define an inverse subsemigroup from metrics on the double of
X
that is substantially smaller and more computable than
M(X)
.

The idea is to find in
X
a family of isometric subsets and to find an inverse subsemigroup in
M(X)
that surjects onto the semigroup of partial bijections of this family. In Section 2 we describe the construction of such subsemigroup for a general inverse semigroup and a family of its idempotents. In Section 3 we show how this construction works for the semigroup
M(X)
. One of the most natural choices for a family of idempotent metrics is that related to geodesic rays, and in Section 4 we give some details for the case when the family of isometric subsets of a geodesic metric space
X
consists of geodesic rays starting at the fixed point
x_{0}\in X
. Sections 5 and 6 deal with the cases when
X
is a Euclidean space and a rooted tree, respectively. In these two cases we evaluate the image of this subsemigroup in the semigroup of partial bijections of the visual boundary of
X
.

2.
An inverse subsemigroup related to a set of idempotents

Let
S
be an inverse semigroup with
0
and
1
,
E(S)
its set of idempotents. Recall that a semigroup is inverse if any element
s\in S
has a unique pseudoinverse
t\in S
, which means that
sts=s
and
tst=t
. This pseudoinverse element is denoted by
s^{*}
. Any two elements in
E(S)
commute,
s^{*}s
is idempotent for any
s\in S
, and
E(S)
is partially ordered: for
e,f\in E(S)
,
e\leq f
if
ef=e
. Two idempotents,
e
and
f
are equivalent if there exists
s\in S
such that

ss^{*}=e
and
s^{*}s=f
. In this case
es=ss^{*}s=sf
.
Fix a set
\Phi\subset E(S)
of mutually equivalent non-zero idempotents. Our aim is to define an inverse subsemigroup in
S
that surjects onto the inverse semigroup of partial bijections of
\Phi
.

Recall that, given a set
X
, a partial bijection is a triple
(A,f,B)
, where
A,B\subset X
and
f:A\to B
is a bijection. The set
PB(X)
of all partial bijiections of
X
is an example of an inverse semigroup.

Consider the following subset
S(\Phi)\subset S
. Let
s\in S(\Phi)
if, for any
e\in\Phi
, one of the following holds:

•

either
es^{*}s=0
;

•

or
es^{*}s=e
and then there exists
f\in\Phi
such that

se=fs\quad\mbox{and}\quad fss^{*}=f.

(2.1)

Note that such
f
is unique. Indeed, if
f_{1},f_{2}\in\Phi
satisfy (
2.1
) then
f_{1}s=f_{2}s=se
. Multiplying this by
f_{1}
, we get
f_{1}s=f_{1}^{2}s=f_{1}f_{2}s
, hence
f_{1}=f_{1}ss^{*}=f_{1}f_{2}ss^{*}=f_{1}f_{2}
, which means that
f_{1}\leq f_{2}
. Similarly,
f_{2}\leq f_{1}
, hence
f_{1}=f_{2}
.

Put
S_{\Phi}=S(\Phi)\cap S(\Phi)^{*}
, where
R^{*}=\{s^{*}:s\in R\}
.

Lemma 2.1
.

S_{\Phi}
is an inverse semigroup.

Proof.

Let
s,t\in S(\Phi)
, and let
e\in\Phi
. If
et^{*}t=0
then, using that
s^{*}s\leq 1
for any
s\in S
, we have
et^{*}s^{*}st\leq et^{*}t=0
, hence
e(st)^{*}st=0
. If
et^{*}t\neq 0
then there exists
f\in\Phi
such that
te=ft
. Then
et^{*}=t^{*}f
, hence
e(st)^{*}st=t^{*}fs^{*}st
. Now, there are two possibilities. Either
fs^{*}s=0
, and then
e(st)^{*}st=t^{*}0t=0
, or
fs^{*}s=f
and there exists
g\in\Phi
such that
sf=gs
and
gss^{*}=g
. In the latter case, we have
(st)e=sft=g(st)
. We also have

e(st)^{*}st=et^{*}s^{*}st=t^{*}fs^{*}st=t^{*}ft=t^{*}te=e,

and, similarly,
g(st)(st)^{*}=g
.
This proves that
st\in S(\Phi)
.

By definition, if
s\in S_{\Phi}
then
s^{*}\in S_{\Phi}
, hence the semigroup
S_{\Phi}
is inverse.

∎

Let
s\in S_{\Phi}
. Set

A(s)=\{e\in\Phi:es^{*}s=e\}\subset\Phi.

(2.2)

Define
\alpha(s):A(s)\to\Phi
by
\alpha(e)=f
, where
f
satisfies (
2.1
). Then
\alpha(s)
is a bijection onto
B(s)=\alpha(A(s))\subset\Phi
(the argument about uniqueness of
f
works in the opposite direction, i.e. for
e
, too). Set
\alpha_{s}=(A(s),\alpha(s),B(s))
. Denote the set of all partial bijections on
\Phi
by
PB(\Phi)
. Then
\alpha_{s}\in PB(\Phi)
for any
s\in S_{\Phi}
.

Proposition 2.2
.

The map
s\mapsto\alpha_{s}
,
S_{\Phi}\to PB(\Phi)
, is a semigroup homomorphism.

Proof.

Obvious.

∎

Example 2.3
.

Let
S=PB(X_{2n})
, where
X_{n}=\{1,\ldots,n\}
, and let
\Phi=\{e,f\}
, where
e
(resp.,
f
) is the identity map of the set
Y_{1}=\{1,\ldots,n\}
(resp.,

). Let
A,B\subset X_{2n}
be two sets of the same cardinality, and let

be a partial bijection. Then
s\in S_{\Phi}
if
A
and

coincide with either

or

, hence

.

Example 2.4
.

Let
S=PB(X_{n})
,
k<n
, and let
\Phi=\{e\}
, where
e
is the identity map of the set
Y=\{1,\ldots,k\}
. Let
A,B\subset X_{n}
be two sets of the same cardinality, and let
s:A\to B
be a partial bijection. Then the condition
se=es
implies that
|A\cap Y|=|B\cap Y|
, hence
s\in S_{\Phi}
if
A
and
B
either contain
Y
or do not intersect
Y
. In the first case
s
is a permutation on
Y
and a partial bijection on
X_{n}\setminus Y
, and in the second case

is a partial bijection on
X_{n}\setminus Y
. Then

, where
\Sigma_{k}
denotes the transposition group on
k
elements.

3.
Inverse semigroup from metrics on the double restricted to a family of subsets

Let
X
,
Y
be metric spaces. A map
f:Y\to X
is almost isometric if there exists
C>0
such that
|d(f(x),f(y))-d(x,y)|<C
for any
x,y\in Y
. A self-map
h:X\to X
is almost identity if there exists
C>0
such that
d(x,h(x))<C
for any
x\in X
. The two spaces,
Y_{1}
and
Y_{2}
, are almost isometric if there exist almost isometries
f:Y_{1}\to Y_{2}
and
g:Y_{2}\to Y_{1}
such that
fg
and
gf
are almost identities for
Y_{2}
and
Y_{1}
, respectively.

Let
AI(Y,X)
denote the set of all almost isometric maps from
Y
to
X
. Let
\Psi\subset AI(Y,X)
,
\psi\in\Psi
, and let
A=\psi(Y)\subset X
. Let
\rho_{A}
be a metric on the double of
X
compatible with
d_{X}
defined by (
1.1
).

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\psi,\phi\in\Psi
,
A=\psi(Y)
,
B=\phi(Y)
. Then
[\rho_{A}]
and
[\rho_{B}]
are equivalent in
M(X)
.

Proof.

By assumption,
A
and
B
are almost isometric, i.e. there exists
C>0
and maps
f:A\to B
and
g:B\to A
such that
|d_{X}(f(x),f(y))-d_{X}(x,y)|<C
for any
x,y\in A
,
|d_{X}(g(x),g(y))-d_{X}(x,y)|<C
for any
x,y\in B
, and
d_{X}(x,f\circ g(x))<C
for any
x\in B
and
d_{X}(x,g\circ f(x))<C
for any
x\in A
. Let
\rho
be the metric on the double of
X
campatible with
d_{X}
defined by

\rho(x,y^{\prime})=\inf_{a\in A}d_{X}(x,a)+2C+d_{X}(f(a),y)

It is easy to check the triangle inequality for
\rho
, whence it is really a metric, hence it defines some
s=[\rho]\in M(X)
. We have to show that
s^{*}s=[\rho_{A}]
and
ss^{*}=[\rho_{B}]
.
Recall that by Proposition 3.2 in
[
3
]
, two idempotent metrics,
\rho
and
\sigma
, are equivalent if the two functions,
x\mapsto\rho(x,x^{\prime})
and
x\mapsto\sigma(x,x^{\prime})
are equivalent.
As

\displaystyle\rho^{*}\rho(x,x^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\inf_{y\in X}(2\inf_{a\in A}d_{X}(x,a)+2C+d_{X}(f(a),y)+1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\inf_{a\in A}d_{X}(x,a)+2C+1,

we have
s^{*}s=[\rho_{A}]
.
Similarly,

\displaystyle\rho\rho^{*}(x,x^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\inf_{y\in X}(2\inf_{a\in A}d_{X}(y,a)+2C+d_{X}(f(a),x)+1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\inf_{a\in A}d_{X}(x,f(a))+2C+1=2d_{X}(x,f(A))+2C+1.

As for any
b\in B
there exists
a\in A
such that
d_{X}(b,f(a))<C
, we have

|d_{X}(x,B)-d_{X}(f(A))|<C,

therefore,
|\rho\rho^{*}(x,x^{\prime})-2d_{X}(x,B)|<4C+1
, hence
ss^{*}=[\rho_{B}]
.

∎

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\Phi=\{\psi(Y):\psi\in\Psi\}
,
\psi\in\Psi
,
A=\psi(Y)
and
s\in M(X)_{\Phi}
. Suppose that
[\rho_{A}]s^{*}s\neq 0
. Then, by assumption, there exists
\phi\in\Psi
such that
s[\rho_{A}]=[\rho_{B}]s
and
[\rho_{B}]ss^{*}=[\rho_{B}]
, where
B=\phi(Y)
.
Let
\rho
be a metric on the double of
X
such that
[\rho]=s
. Then there exists
C>0
and almost isometries
f:A\to B
and
g:B\to A
such that
\rho(x,f(x)^{\prime})<C
for any
x\in A
and
\rho(x^{\prime},g(x))<C
for any
x\in B
.

Proof.

It follows from
se=fs
that
ses^{*}=fss^{*}=f
. so we have
[\rho][\rho_{A}][\rho^{*}]=[\rho_{B}]
, i.e.
\rho\rho_{A}\rho^{*}
is equivalent to
\rho_{B}
. This implies that there exists
C>0
such that
\rho\rho_{A}\rho^{*}(x,x^{\prime})<C/2
for any
x\in B
.
Let
x\in B
. As

\displaystyle\rho\rho_{A}\rho^{*}(x,x^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\inf_{u,v\in X}\rho^{*}(x,u^{\prime})+\rho_{A}(u,v^{\prime})+\rho(v,x^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\inf_{u,v\in X}\rho(x^{\prime},u)+\rho_{A}(u,v^{\prime})+\rho(v,x^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\inf_{u,v\in X,a\in A}\rho(x^{\prime},u)+d_{X}(u,a)+1+d_{X}(a,v)+\rho(v,x^{\prime})

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle C/2,

there exist
u\in X
,
a\in A
such that
\rho(x^{\prime},u)<C/2
and
d_{X}(u,a)<C/2
. Set
g(x)=a
. Then
\rho(x^{\prime},g(x))\leq\rho(x^{\prime},u)+d_{X}(u,a)<C
for any
x\in B
. Similarly, there is a map
f:A\to B
such that
\rho(x,f(x)^{\prime})<C
for any
x\in A
.

∎

Set
\Phi=\{\rho_{\psi(Y)}:\psi\in\Psi\}
. Then
M(X)_{\Phi}
is the inverse semigroup obtained from
M(X)
by the procedure described in Section
2
.

4.
Case of geodesic rays

For convenience, here we consider
pointed
metric spaces
X
with a basepoint
x_{0}\in X
. One of the most obvious choices for
Y
is the half-line
Y=[0,\infty)
with the natural metric, and with the basepoint 0. Let
\Psi
denote the set of all isometries
\psi:[0,\infty)\to X
with
\varphi(0)=x_{0}
. Then
\varphi([0,\infty))
is a geodesic ray, so if we want to have enough elements in
\Psi
then we should require that
X
should be a
geodesic
space. Set

\Phi=\{[\rho_{\psi([0,\infty))}]:\psi\in\Psi\}\subset E(M(X)).

We write
M(X)_{r}
for
M(X)_{\Phi}
with this choice for
\Phi
(the subscript
r
stands for ‘rays’).

Recall some standard notions from metric geometry
[
1
]
. A curve
\gamma:[0,1]\to X
is rectifiable if

L(\gamma)=\sup\bigl\{\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{n}d_{X}(\gamma(t_{i-1}),\gamma(t_{i})):n\in\mathbb{N},0\leq t_{0}\leq t_{1}\leq\cdots\leq t_{n}\leq 1\bigr\}

is finite. The metric
d_{X}
is inner if
d_{X}(x,y)=\inf L(\gamma)
, where the infimum is taken over all rectifiable curves
\gamma
from
x
to
y
, for any
x,y\in X
. If this infimum is attained on some
\gamma
then
\gamma
is a geodesic connecting
x
and
y
.
A geodesic ray from
x_{0}\in X
is an infinite length geodesic line that starts at
x_{0}
. A metric space is geodesic if any two points can be connected by a geodesic line.

Two geodesic rays,
r_{1}
and
r_{2}
, are equivalent if there exists
C>0
such that
d_{X}(r_{1}(t),r_{2}(t))<C
for any
t\in[0,\infty)
. The set of all geodesic rays starting at the basepoint can be endowed with the following topology: the sets
V_{R,\varepsilon}(r)
consisting of all geodesic rays
s
such that
d_{X}(s(t),r(t))<\varepsilon
for any
t\in[0,R)
form its base at the point
r
. The corresponding quotient topology (with respect the equivalence above) gives a topology on the set
\partial X
of equivalence classes of geodesic rays starting at the basepoint. The set
\partial X
is called the visual boundary of
X
.

Let us consider the case when each
r\in\partial X
is represented by a single geodesic ray. Then
\Phi=\{[\rho_{r}]:r\in\partial X\}
. In this case we can assign to each metric
\rho
on the double of
X
a function on
\partial X
as follows.
For a geodesic ray
r\in\partial X
, set

f_{\rho}(r)=\inf\{C:\lim\sup_{x\in r}\rho(x,X^{\prime})<C\}.

This gives a function
f_{\rho}:\partial X\to[1,\infty]
.

For
[\rho]\in M(X)_{r}
, consider the set
A=A([\rho])=f_{\rho}^{-1}([1,\infty))\subset\partial X
(cf. (
2.2
)).

Lemma 4.1
.

The set
A=f_{\rho}^{-1}([1,\infty))
is an
F_{\sigma}
set.

Proof.

Set
A_{n}=\{r\in\partial X:f_{\rho}(r)\leq n\}
. Let
r_{0}\in\overline{A}_{n}
, i.e. any neighborhood
V_{R,\varepsilon}(r_{0})
of
r_{0}
intersects
A_{n}
. Suppose that
f_{\rho}(r_{0})>n
. Then there exists
x\in r_{0}
such that
\rho(x,X^{\prime})>n
. Take
r\in A_{n}\cap V_{R,\varepsilon}(r_{0})
, where
R=d_{X}(x_{0},x)
and
\varepsilon=\frac{\rho(x,X^{\prime})-n}{2}
. Let
y\in r
,
d_{X}(x_{0},y)=R
, then
d_{X}(x,y)<\varepsilon
and
\rho(y,X^{\prime})\leq n
. By the triangle inequality,

\rho(x,X^{\prime})\leq d_{X}(x,y)+\rho(y,X^{\prime})\leq\varepsilon+n<\rho(x,X^{\prime}).

This contradiction shows that
x\in A_{n}
, hence
A_{n}
is closed. Clearly,
A=f_{\rho}^{-1}([1,\infty))=\cup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}A_{n}
.

∎

5.
Evaluation of
M(\mathbb{R}^{n})_{r}

Let
\mathbb{R}^{n}
be the euclidean space with the standard metric. Then the geodesic rays starting at the basepoint can be identified with the sphere
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}=\partial\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
s\in M(\mathbb{R}^{n})_{r}
,
[\rho]=s
, and let
r_{1},r_{2}
be two rays in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Let
e_{i}=[\rho_{r_{i}}]
,
i=1,2
. Suppose that
e_{i}s^{*}s\neq 0
,
i=1,2
, and let
f_{1},f_{2}\in E(M(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
satisfy
se_{i}=f_{i}s
and
f_{i}ss^{*}=f_{i}
,
i=1,2
. Let
t_{i}
be the ray such that
[\rho_{t_{i}}]=f_{i}
, which exists by Lemma
3.2
. Let
d_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}
be the standard metric on the sphere. Then
d_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}(t_{1},t_{2})=d_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}(r_{1},r_{2})
.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.2
, there exists
C>0
and an almost isometry
\varphi_{i}:r_{i}\to t_{i}
,
i=1,2
, such that
\rho(x,\varphi_{i}(x))<C
for any
x\in r_{i}
. For
Y=[0,\infty)
, any almost isometry is close to the identity map, so without loss of generality we may assume that
\varphi_{i}
is the bijective isometry between
r_{i}
and
t_{i}
.

Let
x_{i}\in r_{i}
,
d_{X}(x_{0},x_{i})=R
, and let
y_{i}=\varphi_{i}(x_{i})\in t_{i}
. Then
d_{X}(x_{0},y_{i})=R
. Let
\alpha
(resp.,
\beta
) be the angle between
r_{1}
and
r_{2}
(resp., between
t_{1}
and
t_{2}
). Then

d_{X}(x_{1},x_{2})=2R\sin\frac{\alpha}{2},\quad d_{X}(y_{1},y_{2})=2R\sin\frac{\beta}{2}.

As
\rho(x_{i},y^{\prime}_{i})<C
,
|d_{X}(y_{1},y_{2})-d_{X}(x_{1},x_{2})|<2C
, hence
R|\sin\frac{\alpha}{2}-\sin\frac{\beta}{2}|<C
. As
R
is arbitrary,
\alpha=\beta
.

∎

Let
PI(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})
be the set of pairs
(A,u|_{A})
, where
A\subset\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
is an
F_{\sigma}
set, and
u\in O(n)
is an orthogonal operator on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. This set has a natural injection to the inverse semigroup
PB(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})
of all partial bijections of the sphere: assign to a pair
(A,u|_{A})
the partial bijection
u|_{A}:A\to B
, where
B=u|_{A}(A)
. Thus,
PI(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})
is an inverse semigroup.

Theorem 5.2
.

There exists a split semigroup surjection
M(\mathbb{R}^{n})_{r}\to PI(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})
.

Proof.

Let
s\in M(\mathbb{R}^{n})_{r}
,
[\rho]=s
. Set
A=\{r\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}:f_{\rho}(r)<\infty\}
. For each
r\in A
there exists
t\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
such that
\rho(x,y^{\prime})
is uniformly bounded for any
x\in r
,
y\in t
with
d_{X}(x_{0},x)=d_{X}(x_{0},y)
. By Lemma
5.1
, the map
r\mapsto t
is an isometry of
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
, hence is given by some orthogonal operator (may be not unique, but with unique restriction to
A
). This gives a map
\psi:M(\mathbb{R}^{n})_{r}\to PI(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})
, which is well defined (i.e. does not depend on the representative
\rho
in
s
).

Let us construct a map in the opposite direction. Let
A=\cup_{m\in\mathbb{N}}A_{m}
, where
A_{m}
’s are closed subsets of
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
with
A_{m}\subset A_{m+1}
,
m\in\mathbb{N}
, let
u\in O(n)
, and let
B_{m}=u(A_{m})
. Let
E_{m}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:x\in r\mbox{\ for\ some\ }r\in A_{m}\}
. Set also
E_{0}=\{x_{0}\}
. For
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, set

\rho(x,y^{\prime})=\inf_{m\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}}\inf_{z\in E_{m}}[d_{X}(x,z)+m+1+d_{X}(u(z),y)].

It is easy to check the triangle inequality, so
\rho
determines a metric on the double of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
compatible with the Euclidean metric.

If
A=\cup_{m\in\mathbb{N}}A^{\prime}_{m}
with
A^{\prime}_{m}
closed and
A^{\prime}_{m}\subset A^{\prime}_{m+1}
,
m\in\mathbb{N}
, then, replacing
A^{\prime}_{m}
by
A_{m}\cap A^{\prime}_{m}
, we may assume that
A^{\prime}_{m}\subset A_{m}
, and as the sets
A_{m}
are compact, there exists a monotone map
\kappa:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
such that
A_{\kappa(m)}\subset A^{\prime}_{m}
and
\lim_{m\to\infty}\kappa(m)=\infty
. Let
E^{\prime}_{m}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:x\in r\mbox{\ for\ some\ }r\in A^{\prime}_{m}\}
, and let

\rho^{\prime}(x,y^{\prime})=\inf_{m\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}}\inf_{z\in E^{\prime}_{m}}[d_{X}(x,z)+m+1+d_{X}(u(z),y)].

Fix
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Take
\varepsilon>0
and find
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
z\in E_{m}
such that

[d_{X}(x,z)+m+1+d_{X}(u(z),y)]-\rho(x,y^{\prime})<\varepsilon.

Then
z\in E^{\prime}_{\tau(m)}
, where
\tau=\kappa^{-1}
, so there exists
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
m\leq k\leq\tau(m)
such that
z\in E^{\prime}_{k}\setminus E^{\prime}_{k-1}
, and

\rho^{\prime}(x,y^{\prime})\leq d_{X}(x,z)+k+1+d_{X}(z,y)<\rho(x,y^{\prime})+(k-n)+1+\varepsilon,

hence

\rho^{\prime}(x,y^{\prime})\leq\rho(x,y^{\prime})+\tau(m)-m<\rho(x,y^{\prime})+\tau(m).

We also have
m\leq\rho(x,y^{\prime})
, hence

\rho^{\prime}(x,y^{\prime})\leq\rho(x,y^{\prime})+\tau(m)\leq\varphi(\rho(x,y^{\prime})),

where
\varphi(t)=t+\tau(t)
.

Similarly, we get
\rho(x,y^{\prime})\leq\rho^{\prime}(x,y^{\prime})
for any
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, hence the metrics
\rho
and
\rho^{\prime}
represent the same element in
M(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Let us check that this element lies in
M(\mathbb{R}^{n})_{r}
. Let
r\notin A
. Set
E=\cup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}E_{n}
. As
A_{n}
is closed for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
E_{n}
is closed as well, hence for any
m\in\mathbb{N}
there exists
R>0
such that
d_{X}(x,E_{m})>m
for any
x\in r
with
d_{X}(x_{0},x)>R
. Then

\displaystyle\rho(x,X^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\inf_{y\in X}\inf_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\inf_{z\in E_{n}\setminus E_{n-1}}[d_{X}(x,z)+n+d_{X}(u(z),y)]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\inf_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\inf_{z\in E_{n}\setminus E_{n-1}}[d_{X}(x,z)+n].

If
k\leq m
and
z\in E_{k}
then
d_{X}(x,E_{k})\geq d_{X}(x,E_{m})>m
, hence
d_{X}(x,z)+k>m
. If
k>m
then
d_{X}(x,z)+k\geq n>m
. Thus,

\rho(x,X^{\prime})=\inf_{k\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}}\inf_{z\in E_{k}}[d_{X}(x,z)+k+1]>m\quad\mbox{when\ \ }d_{X}(x_{0},x)>R,

so in this case
[\rho_{r}\rho^{*}\rho]=0
. If
r\in A
then
r\in A_{k}
for some
k
. Set
t=u(r)
. If
x\in r
,
y\in t
and
d_{X}(x_{0},x)=d_{X}(x_{0},y)
then

\rho(x,y^{\prime})=\inf_{l\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}}\inf_{z\in E_{l}}[d_{X}(x,z)+l+1+d_{X}(u(z),u(x))]\leq l,

hence
[\rho_{r}\rho]=[\rho\rho_{t}]
and
[\rho_{t}\rho\rho^{*}]=[\rho_{t}]
, thus
[\rho]\in M(\mathbb{R}^{n})_{r}
.

Assigning
[\rho]
to
(A,u|_{A})\in PI(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})
, we obtain a map
\chi:PI(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})\to M(\mathbb{R}^{n})_{r}
.
Direct check shows that
\psi\circ\chi
is the identity map on
PI(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})
.

∎

6.
Evaluation of
M(X)_{r}
for trees

Let
X
be a rooted tree with a basepoint
x_{0}
(root), and let
d_{X}
be the intrinsic metric on
X
such that each edge has length one. A
ray
is a geodesic that starts at
x_{0}
and has infinite length. We assume that for any point
x\in X
there exists a ray that passes through
x
, i.e.
X
has no dead ends. Rays can be described in terms of infinite sequences of subsequent vertices,
x_{0},x_{1},x_{2},\ldots
with
d_{X}(x_{n},x_{n+1})=1
. Given a geodesic segment
\gamma
connecting
x_{0}
with a vertex
x
, denote by
U_{\gamma}
the set of all rays that begin with
\gamma
.

Recall that the visual boundary
\partial X
of
X
is the set of all rays, endowed with the topology determined by the base consisting of all sets of the form
U_{\gamma}
, where
\gamma
runs over the set of all geodesic segments starting at
x_{0}
.
The space
\partial X
is known to be compact and Hausdorff. Moreover, this topology is metrizable: given two rays,
\lambda
and
\mu
, with the common segment
\gamma
, set
d(\lambda,\mu)=e^{-|\gamma|}
, where
|\gamma|
denotes the length of
\gamma
.

Recall that a map
f:X\to Y
between two metric spaces is
Lipschitz
if there exists
K>1
such that
d_{Y}(f(x_{1}),f(x_{2}))\leq Kd_{X}(x_{1},x_{2})
. If
X
is locally compact,
X=\cup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}A_{n}
with compact
A_{n}
’s then a map
f:X\to Y
is
locally
Lipschitz if it is Lipschitz on each
A_{n}
(with possibly increasing Lipschitz constants). Clearly, if
X=\cup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}B_{n}
with compact
B_{n}
’s then a map is locally Lipschitz with respect to
X=\cup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}A_{n}
iff it is locally Lipschitz with respect to
X=\cup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}B_{n}
. A bijective map
f:X\to Y
between two locally compact metric spaces is locally
bi-
Lipschitz if both
f
and
f^{-1}
are locally Lipschitz.

For a metric space
Y
, a partial bijective locally bi-Lipschitz (PBLBL) map is a bijective locally bi-Lipschitz map
\varphi:A\to B
between two
F_{\sigma}
subspaces
A,B\subset Y
(note that an
F_{\sigma}
set is locally compact). It is easy to see that the set of all PBLBL maps has a natural structure of an inverse semigroup. Denote this inverse semigroup by
PBLBL(Y)
.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
s\in M(X)_{r}
,
[\rho]=s
, and let
r_{1},r_{2}
be two rays in
X
. Let
e_{i}=[\rho_{r_{i}}]
,
i=1,2
. Suppose that
e_{i}s^{*}s\neq 0
,
i=1,2
, and let
f_{1},f_{2}\in E(M(X))
satisfy
se_{i}=f_{i}s
and
f_{i}ss^{*}=f_{i}
,
i=1,2
. Let
t_{i}
be the ray such that
[\rho_{t_{i}}]=f_{i}
, which exists by Lemma
3.2
. Let
d_{\partial X}
be the metric on
\partial X
described above. Then there exists
K>0
such that
\frac{1}{K}<\frac{d_{\partial X}(t_{1},t_{2})}{d_{\partial X}(r_{1},r_{2})}<K
.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.2
, there exists
C>0
and an almost isometry
\varphi_{i}:r_{i}\to t_{i}
,
i=1,2
, such that
\rho(x,\varphi_{i}(x))<C
for any
x\in r_{i}
. Any almost isometry of
Y=[0,\infty)
is close to the identity map, so without loss of generality we may assume that
\varphi_{i}
is the bijective isometry between
r_{i}
and
t_{i}
.

Let
x_{i}\in r_{i}
,
d_{X}(x_{0},x_{i})=R
, and let
y_{i}=\varphi_{i}(x_{i})\in t_{i}
,
i=1,2
. Then
d_{X}(x_{0},y_{i})=R
. Let
L
(rep.,
M
) be the length of the common part of the geodesic rays
r_{1}
and
r_{2}
(resp.,
t_{1}
and
t_{2}
). We assume that
R>L,M
. Then
d_{X}(x_{1},x_{2})=2(R-L)
,
d_{X}(y_{1},y_{2})=2(R-M)
. As
|d_{X}(y_{1},y_{2})-d_{X}(x_{1},x_{2})|<2C
, we have
|R-L-(R-M)|=|M-L|<C
. This means that
\frac{d_{\partial X}(t_{1},t_{2})}{d_{\partial X}(r_{1},r_{2})}=\frac{e^{-M}}{e^{-L}}<e^{C}
. Similarly,
\frac{d_{\partial X}(t_{1},t_{2})}{d_{\partial X}(r_{1},r_{2})}>e^{-C}
.

∎

Theorem 6.2
.

Let
X
be a rooted tree without dead ends, with the intrinsic metric. Then there exists a split semigroup surjection
M(X)_{r}\to PBLBL(\partial X)
.

Proof.

Let
s\in M(X)_{r}
,
[\rho]=s
. Set
A_{m}=\{r\in\partial X:f_{\rho}(r)<m\}
,
A=\cup_{m\in\mathbb{N}}A_{m}
. For each
r\in A
there exists
t\in\partial X
such that
\rho(x,y^{\prime})
is uniformly bounded for any
x\in r
,
y\in t
with
d_{X}(x_{0},x)=d_{X}(x_{0},y)
. By Lemma
6.1
, the map
r\mapsto t
is a Lipschitz map on each
A_{m}
, hence a locally bi-Lipschitz map on
A\subset\partial X
. This gives a map
\psi:M(\mathbb{R}^{n})_{r}\to PBLBL(\partial X)
, which is well defined (i.e. does not depend on the representative
\rho
in
s
).

Let us construct a map in the opposite direction. Let
A=\cup_{m\in\mathbb{N}}A_{m}
, where
A_{m}
’s are closed subsets of
\partial X
with
A_{m}\subset A_{m+1}
,
m\in\mathbb{N}
, let
F:A\to\partial X
be a locally bi-Lipschitz map, and let
B_{m}=F(A_{m})
. Let
F|_{A_{m}}
is a bi-Lipschitz map with a bi-Lipschitz contant
K_{m}=e^{C_{m}}
. Let
E_{m}=\{x\in X:x\in r\mbox{\ for\ some\ }r\in A_{m}\}
,
E=\cup_{m\in\mathbb{N}}E_{m}
. Set also
E_{0}=\{x_{0}\}
.

For each
x\in X
choose a geodesic ray
r_{x}\in\partial X
such that
x\in r_{x}
. Let
x\in E_{m}
, and let
y\in F(r_{x})
satisfy
d_{X}(x_{0},x)=d_{X}(x_{0},y)
. Set
f(x)=y
.

Another choice of geodesic rays,
x\mapsto r^{\prime}_{x}
, gives rise to another map
f^{\prime}:E\to X
.

Lemma 6.3
.

One has

|d_{X}(f(x),f(y))-d_{X}(x,y)|<2C_{m}
for any
x,y\in E_{m}
, and
d_{X}(f(x),f^{\prime}(x))<2C_{m}
for any
x\in E_{m}
.

Proof.

Let
x,y\in E_{m}
.
Let
c
be the length of the common part of
r_{x}
and
r_{y}
, and let
a=d_{X}(x_{0},x)
,
b=d_{X}(x_{0},y)
. Then
d(r_{x},r_{y})=e^{-c}
, and
d_{X}(x,y)=a+b-2c
.
Let
K_{m}
be the Lipschitz constant on
A_{m}
,
C_{m}=\log K_{m}
, and let
t_{x}=F(r_{x})
,
t_{y}=F(r_{y})
. Then
\frac{e^{-c}}{K_{m}}<d(t_{x},t_{y})<e^{-c}K_{m}
, i.e. the length
d
of the common part of
t_{x}
and
t_{y}
lies in the interval
(c-K_{m},c+K_{m})
. Then
d_{X}(f(x),f(y))=a+b-2d
, hence

|d_{X}(f(x),f(y))-d_{X}(x,y)|=2|c-d|=2C_{m}.

If
x\in r_{x},r^{\prime}_{x}
then the length of the common part of
r_{x}
and
r^{\prime}_{x}
is not less than
c=d_{X}(x_{0},x)
, hence
d(r_{x},r^{\prime}_{x})\leq e^{-c}
. Let
t=F(r_{x})
,
t^{\prime}=F(r^{\prime}_{x})
. Then
d(t,t^{\prime})<K_{m}e^{-c}=e^{-(c-C_{m})}
, hence the length of the common part of
t
and
t^{\prime}
is greater than
c-C_{m}
. As
d_{X}(x_{0},f(x))=d_{X}(x_{0},f^{\prime}(x))=c
,
f(x)\in t
,
f^{\prime}(x)\in t^{\prime}
, we have
d_{X}(f(x),f^{\prime}(x))<2C_{m}
.

∎

For
x,y\in X
, set

\sigma(x,y^{\prime})=\inf_{m\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}}\inf_{z\in E_{m}}[d_{X}(x,z)+2C_{m}+d_{X}(u(z),y)].

This formula determines a metric on
X\sqcup X
(in fact, this is the intrinsic metric of the graph that is obtained from the two copies of the tree with added edges that connect any
x\in E_{m}
with
f(x)^{\prime}\in X^{\prime}
of length
2C_{m}
). Let us check that this metric is compatible with
d_{X}
. Let
x_{1},x_{2},y\in X
;
i=1,2
. Then

\displaystyle\sigma(x_{1},y^{\prime})+\sigma(x_{2},y^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\inf_{m_{i}\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}}\inf_{z_{i}\in E_{m_{i}}}d_{X}(x_{1},z_{1})+2C_{m_{1}}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle d_{X}(f(z_{1}),y)+d_{X}(x_{2},z_{2})+2C_{m_{2}}+d_{X}(f(z_{2}),y)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\inf_{m_{i}\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}}\inf_{z_{i}\in E_{m_{i}}}d_{X}(x_{1},z_{1})+2(C_{m_{1}}+C_{m_{2}})+d_{X}(f(z_{1}),f(z_{2}))

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\inf_{z_{i}\in E_{m_{i}}}d_{X}(x_{1},z_{1})+d_{X}(z_{1},z_{2})

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle d_{X}(x_{1},x_{2}),

and, similarly,
\sigma(x,y^{\prime}_{1})+\sigma(x,y^{\prime}_{2})\geq d_{X}(y_{1},y_{2})
for any
x,y_{1},y_{2}\in X
.

As for Euclidean spaces (Section
5
), it is easy to check that
[\sigma]\in M(X)_{r}
, that the map
\chi:PBLBL(\partial X)\to M(X)_{r}
,
\chi(A,F,B)=[\sigma]
, is well defined and that
\psi\circ\chi
is the identity map on
PBLBL(\partial X)
.

∎

Corollary 6.4
.

For
X=[0,\infty)
,
M(X)_{0}
consists of zero and unit. For
Y=\mathbb{R}
,
M(Y)_{0}
is the semigroup of partial bijections of the set
\{0,1\}
.

Proof.

The space
X
(resp.,
Y
) has one end (resp., two ends). Thus, the semigroup of partial bijective locally bi-Lipschitz maps of the set of ends is the semigroup of partial bijections of the set consisting of one element (resp., of two elements).

∎
