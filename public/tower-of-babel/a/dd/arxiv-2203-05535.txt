1.
Introduction

In 1914, Hardy and Littlewood [
References
, p 172] posed the question as to whether, when
\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}
,
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
\epsilon>0
is any positive number, there exists
\sigma>0
not depending on
\alpha
such that

(1.1)

\min_{1\leq x\leq X}\|\alpha x^{k}\|\leq X^{-\sigma+\epsilon},

provided that
X
is sufficiently large in terms of
k
and
\epsilon
. Here,
\|\cdot\|
denotes the distance to the nearest integer. Vinogradov [
References
] gave a positive answer to this question by providing a specific exponent
\sigma=k/(2^{k-1}k+1)
. This was quantitatively improved by Heilbronn [
References
], who replaced the exponent
\sigma=2/5
by
1/2
in the case
k=2
. Danicic [
References
] extended this conclusion to
\sigma=1/2^{k-1}
for all
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Subsequently, the exponent
1/2
in the case
k=2
was improved to
\sigma=4/7
by Zaharescu [
References
]. Sharper results for
k\geq 6
depend on recent progress Vinogradov’s mean value theorem, and thus Baker
[\ref{ref3}]
proved that the exponent
\sigma
can be replaced by
\sigma=1/(k(k-1))
. Furthermore, Wooley [
References
] showed that the exponent
\sigma
can be improved to
\sigma=1/(k\log k+O(k\log\log k))
. However, it is conjectured in [
References
] that the exponent
\sigma
should be improvable to
1.
Unfortunately, current techniques do not seem capable of achieving this conjecture.

Turning our attention to problems in many variables, one is led to ask analogous questions regarding the distribution mod
1
of polynomials
f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{s})\in{\mathbb{R}}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{s}]
. In [
References
, Theorem 10.2], Baker proved that when
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{s}\in{\mathbb{R}}
and
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, there exists
s_{0}
such that whenever
s\geq s_{0}
, one has

\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq\boldsymbol{x}\leq X\\
\boldsymbol{x}\neq\boldsymbol{0}\end{subarray}}\|\alpha_{1}x_{1}^{k}+\cdots+\alpha_{s}x_{s}^{k}\|\leq X^{-k+\epsilon}.

As a quantitative generalization, Baker [
References
,
References
] provided an explicit exponent
\sigma(s,k)
such that

\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq\boldsymbol{x}\leq X\\
\boldsymbol{x}\neq\boldsymbol{0}\end{subarray}}\|\alpha_{1}x_{1}^{k}+\cdots+\alpha_{s}x_{s}^{k}\|\leq X^{-\sigma(s,k)+\epsilon}.

For polynomials more general than additive forms, Schmidt [
References
] made progress on the analogous problem regarding forms of odd degree
k
. Specifically, for given
E>0
, there exist
s_{0}=s_{0}(E,k)
such that whenever
s>s_{0}
, one has

(1.2)

\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq|\boldsymbol{x}|\leq X\\
\boldsymbol{x}\neq\boldsymbol{0}\end{subarray}}\|f(\boldsymbol{x})\|<X^{-E}.

In the special case of cubic form
f(\underline{\mathbf{x}})
, Dietmann [
References
] provided explicit formula
\sigma(s)
such that

\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq|\boldsymbol{x}|\leq X\\
\boldsymbol{x}\neq\boldsymbol{0}\end{subarray}}\|f(\boldsymbol{x})\|<X^{-\sigma(s)}.

In this paper, we seek to make progress on making bounds of the shape
(\ref{2'})
explicit, concentrating for the present on the situation with
s=2.

Here, one may write
f(x_{1},x_{2})=\alpha_{k}x_{1}^{k}+\alpha_{k-1}x_{1}^{k-1}x_{2}+\cdots+\alpha_{0}x_{2}^{k}
with
\alpha_{k},\ldots,\alpha_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Hitherto, the only general strategy available has been a trivial one that reduces this problem in two variables, by specializing
x_{2}=0
for example, to a problem in one variable. In this direction, Wooley [
References
, Theorem 4] obtained a nontrivial exponent
\sigma
by restricting the polynomial to have the shape
\alpha\Phi(x_{1},x_{2})
where
\Phi(x_{1},x_{2})\in{\mathbb{Z}}[x_{1},x_{2}]
is a binary form of degree
k
exceeding
1
.
The main difficulties of the problem regarding general binary forms in
{\mathbb{R}}[x_{1},x_{2}]
arise from our relatively poor understanding concerning how the monomials
\alpha_{l}x_{1}^{l}x_{2}^{k-l}
, with
0\leq l\leq k
, combine to influence the fractional parts of
f(x_{1},x_{2})
. Our goal in this paper is to explore approaches to overcoming these difficulties and go beyond the trivial approach hitherto applied, concentrating on the class of binary forms. Our hope is that the arguments described here may be useful in studying fractional parts of polynomials in many variables in general.

Our first theorem provides bounds for binary forms of degree
k
useful for small
k
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
k
and
l
be natural numbers satisfying
1\leq l\leq k-2,
and consider real numbers
\alpha_{k}
,
\alpha_{j}
(
1\leq j\leq l
). Then, for any
\epsilon>0
, there exists a real number
X(k,\epsilon)
such that whenever
X\geq X(k,\epsilon)
one has

(1.3)

\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq x,y\leq X\\
(x,y)\neq(0,0)\end{subarray}}\|\alpha_{k}x^{k}+\alpha_{l}x^{l}y^{k-l}+\alpha_{l-1}x^{l-1}y^{k-l+1}+\cdots+\alpha_{0}y^{k}\|<X^{-\sigma+\epsilon},

where

\sigma=\frac{l+2}{l+1}2^{1-k}.

For comparison with earlier results, by incorporating the conclusion of Danicic [
References
] with the trivial strategy mentioned above, one obtains

\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq x,y\leq X\\
(x,y)\neq(0,0)\end{subarray}}\|\alpha_{k}x^{k}+\alpha_{l}x^{l}y^{k-l}+\alpha_{l-1}x^{l-1}y^{k-l+1}+\cdots+\alpha_{0}y^{k}\|\leq\min_{1\leq x\leq X}\|\alpha_{k}x^{k}\|\leq X^{-2^{1-k}+\epsilon}

The conclusion of Theorem 1.1 improves on the exponent
2^{1-k}
here by a factor which may be as large as
\frac{3}{2}
in the case
l=1.

Corollary 1.2
.

Let
k
be a natural number, and consider real numbers
\alpha_{k},\alpha_{j}\ (0\leq j\leq k-2)
. Let
\varphi(x,y)=c_{k}x^{k}+c_{k-1}x^{k-1}y+\cdots+c_{1}xy^{k-1}+c_{0}y^{k}
with
c_{j}\in{\mathbb{Z}}.
Then, for any
\epsilon>0
, there exist a real number
X(\epsilon,c_{k},c_{k-1})
such that whenever
X\geq X(\epsilon,c_{k},c_{k-1})
one has

\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq|x|,|y|\leq X\\
(x,y)\neq(0,0)\end{subarray}}\|\alpha_{k}\varphi(x,y)+\alpha_{k-2}x^{k-2}y^{2}+\cdots+\alpha_{0}y^{k}\|<X^{-\sigma+\epsilon},

where
\sigma=(k/(k-1))2^{1-k}.

In [
References
], Dietmann recorded that for sufficiently large
X,
one has

\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq|x|,|y|\leq X\\
(x,y)\neq(0,0)\end{subarray}}\|\alpha x^{3}+\beta x^{2}y+\gamma xy^{2}+\delta y^{3}\|<X^{-1/4+\epsilon},

which is obtained by specializing
y=0
and applying the earlier work of Danicic
[\ref{ref6}]
. In Corollary 1.2 with
k=3
, we obtain the superior exponent
\sigma=3/8
by imposing the condition that
\alpha
and
\beta
have a rational ratio. By further restricting
\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta
to have rational ratio to each other, Wooley [
References
, Theorem 4] proved that this can be improved to
\sigma=1/2.

Our next two theorems provide bounds useful when
k
is larger by exploiting recent progress on Vinogradov’s mean value theorem and earlier work applying smooth numbers. The statements of these results is facilitated by writing

\rho(k,l)=2^{2}(k-1)+2^{3}(k-2)+\cdots+2^{l+1}(k-l),

and

(1.4)

\displaystyle l_{0}=\max\{l\in{\mathbb{N}}|\ 7\rho(k,l)\leq k(k-1)\}

(1.5)

\displaystyle l_{1}=\max\{l\in{\mathbb{N}}|\ 7\rho(k,l)\leq k\log k\},

where the number 7 here may be replaced by smaller positive numbers.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
k
and
l
be natural numbers satisfying
1\leq l\leq l_{0}
and
k\geq 3
, and consider real numbers
\alpha_{k}
and
\alpha_{j}

(1\leq j\leq l)
. Then, for any
\epsilon>0
, there exists a real number
X(k,\epsilon)
such that whenever
X\geq X(k,\epsilon)
one has

(1.6)

\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq x,y\leq X\\
(x,y)\neq(0,0)\end{subarray}}\|\alpha_{k}x^{k}+\alpha_{l}x^{l}y^{k-l}+\alpha_{l-1}x^{l-1}y^{k-l+1}+\cdots+\alpha_{0}y^{k}\|<X^{-\sigma+\epsilon},

where

\sigma=\frac{2}{k(k-1)+\rho(k,l)}

For comparison with earlier results, by combining the earlier work of Baker [
References
, Theorem 3] and the trivial treatment mentioned above, one obtains

\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq x,y\leq X\\
(x,y)\neq(0,0)\end{subarray}}\|\alpha_{k}x^{k}+\alpha_{l}x^{l}y^{k-l}+\alpha_{l-1}x^{l-1}y^{k-l+1}+\cdots+\alpha_{0}y^{k}\|<\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq x\leq X\end{subarray}}\|\alpha x^{k}\|\leq X^{-\sigma+\epsilon},

where
\sigma=1/(k(k-1)).
Notice that for all
l
with
1\leq l\leq l_{0},
the conclusion of Theorem 1.3 is superior to this earlier result stemming from Baker [
References
, Theorem 3]. For instance, in the case
l=1
, the earlier result, which is
\sigma=1/(k(k-1))
, can be improved to

\sigma=\frac{2}{(k+2)(k-1)}.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
k
and
l
be natural numbers satisfying
1\leq l\leq l_{1}
and
k\geq 3
, and consider real numbers
\alpha_{k}
and
\alpha_{j}

(1\leq j\leq l)
. Then, for any
\epsilon>0
, there exist a real number
X(k,\epsilon)
such that whenever
X\geq X(k,\epsilon)
one has

(1.7)

\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq x,y\leq X\\
(x,y)\neq(0,0)\end{subarray}}\|\alpha_{k}x^{k}+\alpha_{l}x^{l}y^{k-l}+\alpha_{l-1}x^{l-1}y^{k-l+1}+\cdots+\alpha_{0}y^{k}\|<X^{-\sigma+\epsilon},

where

\sigma=\frac{2}{k\log k+\rho(k,l)+Ck\log\log k}

in which
C
is a positive constant which may not depend on
k
.

For comparison with earlier results, by combining the earlier work of Wooley
[\ref{ref18}
, Theorem 1.2] and the trivial method mentioned above, one obtains

\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq x,y\leq X\\
(x,y)\neq(0,0)\end{subarray}}\|\alpha_{k}x^{k}+\alpha_{l}x^{l}y^{k-l}+\alpha_{l-1}x^{l-1}y^{k-l+1}+\cdots+\alpha_{0}y^{k}\|<\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq x\leq X\end{subarray}}\|\alpha x^{k}\|\leq X^{-\sigma+\epsilon},

where
\sigma=1/(k\log k+Ck\log\log k)
in which
C
is a positive constant which may not depend on
k
. Notice that for all
l
with
1\leq l\leq l_{1},
the conclusion of Theorem 1.4 is superior to this earlier result stemming from Wooley [
References

The method underlying the proof of our main theorems is to exploit inductive arguments based on a classical argument widely used in studying fractional parts of polynomials. However, it is complicated by the need to obtain quantitative inequalities useful for each of the inductive steps. Thus, these and other technical complications may obscure the key ideas of our arguments, and so we sketch this inductive argument in Section 2. Then, in Section 3, we provide key lemmas about the quantitative inequalities required for the success of our arguments. In Sections 4, 5 and 6, by exploiting these key lemmas and the inductive arguments, we provide the proof of Theorem 1.1, 1.3 and 1.4, respectively. Throughout this paper, we use
\gg
and
\ll
to denote Vinogradov’s well-known notation, and write
e(z)
for
e^{2\pi iz}
. We adopt the convention that whenever
\epsilon
and
\delta
appear in a statement, then the statement holds for each
\epsilon,\delta>0
, with implicit constants depending on
\epsilon
and
\delta
, respectively.

2.
Outline of the proof

In this paper, we mainly follow a classical argument relating fractional parts of polynomials to lower bounds of associated exponential sums [
References
, Theorem 2.2]. We record here this theorem.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}
be real numbers. Suppose that
\|x_{n}\|\geq H^{-1}
for every
n
with
1\leq n\leq X
. Then,

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq h\leq H}\biggl|\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{N}e(hx_{n})\biggl|\geq N/6

Proof.

See [
References
, Theorem 2.2].
∎

Suppose that for all
x,y
with
x,y\in[1,X]\cap{\mathbb{Z}}
one has

(2.1)

\|\alpha_{k}x^{k}+\alpha_{l}x^{l}y^{k-l}+\alpha_{l-1}x^{l-1}y^{k-l+1}+\cdots+\alpha_{0}y^{k}\|>1/H.

Then, by Lemma 2.1, one obtains

(2.2)

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq h\leq H}|\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq x,y\leq X}e(h(\alpha_{k}x^{k}+\alpha_{l}x^{l}y^{k-l}+\alpha_{l-1}x^{l-1}y^{k-l+1}+\cdots+\alpha_{0}y^{k}))|\gg X^{2}.

Our first goal is to obtain information about Diophantine approximations to
\alpha_{l}
from the lower bound (
2.2
). In Section 3, we shall obtain upper bounds for the exponential sum on the left hand side of (
2.2
) in terms of
\alpha_{k}
and
\alpha_{l}
.
Combining these upper bound with the lower bound (
2.2
), one obtains the information concerning
\alpha_{l}.

The next goal is to reduce the problem of bounding the fractional parts on the left hand side of (
2.1
) to the corresponding diagonal problem through inductive arguments. Note that by the triangle inequality, one has

(2.3)

\|\alpha_{k}x^{k}+\alpha_{l}x^{l}y^{k-l}+\alpha_{l-1}x^{l-1}y^{k-l+1}+\cdots+\alpha_{0}y^{k}\|\\
\leq\|\alpha_{k}x^{k}+\alpha_{l-1}x^{l-1}y^{k-l+1}+\cdots+\alpha_{0}y^{k}\|+\|\alpha_{l}x^{l}y^{k-l}\|.

By putting
y=qy^{\prime}
where
q
is the denominator associated with a rational approximation to
\alpha_{l},
and again applying the triangle inequality, we see that the right hand side in (
2.3
) is bounded above by

(2.4)

\|\alpha_{k}x^{k}+\alpha_{l-1}x^{l-1}(qy^{\prime})^{k-l+1}+\cdots+\alpha_{0}(qy^{\prime})^{k}\|+x^{l}(qy^{\prime})^{k-l-1}y^{\prime}\|q\alpha_{l}\|.

From the information about
\alpha_{l}
obtained in the first step, one finds that
\|q\alpha_{l}\|
is small enough so that the second term in
(\ref{9})
is
\leq 1/(2H)
. Thus, the inequality (
2.1
) implies that for all
x,y^{\prime}
one has

\|\alpha_{k}x^{k}+\alpha_{l-1}x^{l-1}(qy^{\prime})^{k-l+1}+\cdots+\alpha_{0}(qy^{\prime})^{k}\|\gg 1/H.

By applying this argument inductively, we ultimately infer from (
2.1
) that for all
x
and
y_{0}
one has

\|\alpha_{k}x^{k}+\alpha_{0}^{\prime}y_{0}^{k}\|\gg 1/H

for all
x
and
y_{0}.

Experts will recognize that the diagonal polynomial here is accessible to classical methods.

3.
Exponential sums in two variables

Let
A
and
B
be subsets of
[1,X]\cap{\mathbb{Z}}.
Suppose that

\|\alpha_{k}x^{k}+\alpha_{l}x^{l}y^{k-l}+\alpha_{l-1}x^{l-1}y^{k-l+1}+\cdots+\alpha_{0}y^{k}\|\geq H^{-1}

for all
x\in A,\ y\in B.
Then, by Lemma 2.1, one finds that

(3.1)

\displaystyle\sum_{h\leq H}\biggl|\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in A\\
y\in B\end{subarray}}e(h(\alpha_{k}x^{k}+\alpha_{l}x^{l}y^{k-l}+\alpha_{l-1}x^{l-1}y^{k-l+1}+\cdots+\alpha_{0}y^{k}))\biggr|\gg|A||B|,

where
|A|
and
|B|
are cardinalities of the sets
A
and
B
, respectively.

Our goal in this section is to obtain upper bounds for the exponential sum above in terms of
\alpha_{k}
and
\alpha_{l}
. To obtain these upper bounds, we introduce a variant of conventional Weyl differencing applicable to exponential sums in many variables, and exploit an argument used in earlier works on exponential sums in many variables (See, for example, [
References
, Lemma 4.3]). To facilitate concision throughout this section, we define
g(x,y)=g(x,y;\boldsymbol{\alpha})\in{\mathbb{R}}[x,y]
by

g(x,y)=\alpha_{k}x^{k}+\alpha_{l}x^{l}y^{k-l}+\alpha_{l-1}x^{l-1}y^{k-l+1}+\cdots+\alpha_{0}y^{k}

with
l\leq k-2.
Furthermore, we define differencing operators
\Delta_{1}^{x}
and
\Delta_{1}^{y}
by

\Delta_{1}^{x}(g(x,y);h)=g(x+h,y)-g(x,y)

\Delta_{1}^{y}(g(x,y);h)=g(x,y+h)-g(x,y),

and so we define
\Delta_{j}^{x}
and
\Delta_{j}^{y}
for
j\geq 2
recursively by means of the relations

\Delta_{j}^{x}(g(x,y);\mathbf{h})=\Delta_{j}^{x}(g(x,y);h_{1},...,h_{j})=\Delta_{1}^{x}(\Delta_{j-1}^{x}(g(x,y);h_{1},...,h_{j-1});h_{j})

\Delta_{j}^{y}(g(x,y);\mathbf{h})=\Delta_{j}^{y}(g(x,y);h_{1},...,h_{j})=\Delta_{1}^{y}(\Delta_{j-1}^{y}(g(x,y);h_{1},...,h_{j-1});h_{j}).

The following lemma provides an upper bound for the exponential sum in (
3.1
) with
A=[1,X]\cap{\mathbb{Z}}
and
B=[1,Y]\cap{\mathbb{Z}}
. This upper bound is useful for small
k.
In advance of the statement of this lemma, we define the exponential sum
T(\boldsymbol{\alpha})=T(\boldsymbol{\alpha};H,X,Y)
by

T(\boldsymbol{\alpha})=\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq h\leq H}\biggl|\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq x\leq X}\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq y\leq Y}e(hg(x,y))\biggr|.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
k
and
l
be natural numbers satisfying
1\leq l\leq k-2
and
k\geq 3,
and consider real numbers
\alpha_{k}
and
\alpha_{j}\ (0\leq j\leq l)
. Suppose that
\alpha_{k}
and
\alpha_{l}
have rational approximations
|\alpha_{k}-a_{1}/q_{1}|\leq q_{1}^{-2}
and
|\alpha_{l}-a_{2}/q_{2}|<q_{2}^{-2}
with
(q_{1},a_{1})=(q_{2},a_{2})=1,
respectively. Suppose that
X
and
H
are positive real numbers sufficiently large in terms of
k
. Then, for each
\epsilon>0
, one has

(3.2)

T(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\ll H(XY)^{1+\epsilon}\left(\frac{1}{q_{1}}+\frac{1}{X}+\frac{q_{1}}{HX^{k}}\right)^{2^{1-k}}\left(\frac{1}{q_{2}}+\frac{1}{Y}+\frac{q_{2}}{HX^{l}Y^{k-l}}\right)^{2^{1-k}}

Proof.

By Hölder’s inequality, one obtains

T(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\ll\biggl(\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq h\leq H}\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq y\leq Y}1\biggr)^{1-2^{-l}}\biggl(\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq h\leq H}\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq y\leq Y}\biggl|\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq x\leq X}e(hg(x,y))\biggr|^{2^{l}}\biggr)^{2^{-l}}.

Then by Weyl differencing (see, for example, the proof of Vaughan [
References
, Lemma 2.4]), we obtain

\displaystyle T(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\ll(HY)^{1-2^{-l}}\biggl(X^{2^{l}-(l+1)}\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq h\leq H}\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq y\leq Y}\displaystyle\sum_{|h_{1}|\leq X}\cdots\displaystyle\sum_{|h_{l}|}\displaystyle\sum_{x\in I_{l}{(\boldsymbol{h}})}e(h\Delta_{l}^{x}(g(x,y),\mathbf{h}))\biggr)^{2^{-l}}

where
I_{l}({\boldsymbol{h}})
is an interval of integers contained in
[-X,X].
Note here that

\Delta_{l}^{x}(g(x,y);\mathbf{h})=\Delta_{l}^{x}(\alpha_{k}x^{k})+\Delta_{l}^{x}(\alpha_{l}x^{l}y^{k-l})

and that
\Delta_{l}^{x}(\alpha_{l}x^{l}y^{k-l})
does not depend on
x.
Hence, one obtains

(3.3)

\displaystyle T(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\ll(HY)^{1-2^{-l}}X^{1-(l+1)2^{-l}}V(\alpha_{k},\alpha_{l})^{2^{-l}},

where

V(\alpha_{k},\alpha_{l})=\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq h\leq H}\displaystyle\sum_{|h_{1}|\leq X}\cdots\displaystyle\sum_{|h_{l}|\leq X}|V_{1}(\alpha_{k};h,\mathbf{h})||V_{2}(\alpha_{l};h,\mathbf{h})|

in which

V_{1}(\alpha_{k};h,\mathbf{h})=\displaystyle\sum_{x\in I_{l}}e(h\Delta_{l}^{x}(\alpha_{k}x^{k},\mathbf{h}))

and

V_{2}(\alpha_{l};h,\mathbf{h})=\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq y\leq Y}e(h\Delta_{l}^{x}(\alpha_{l}x^{l}y^{k-l},\mathbf{h})).

By applying Hölder’s inequality again,

(3.4)

\displaystyle V(\alpha_{k},\alpha_{l})\ll\biggl(\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq h\leq H}\displaystyle\sum_{|h_{1}|\leq X}\cdots\displaystyle\sum_{|h_{l}|\leq X}1\biggr)^{1-2^{2-k+l}}W_{1}(\alpha_{k})^{2^{1-k+l}}W_{2}(\alpha_{l})^{2^{1-k+l}}

where

W_{1}(\alpha_{k})=\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq h\leq H}\displaystyle\sum_{|h_{1}|\leq X}\cdots\displaystyle\sum_{|h_{l}|\leq X}|V_{1}(\alpha_{k},h,\mathbf{h})|^{2^{k-l-1}},

W_{2}(\alpha_{l})=\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq h\leq H}\displaystyle\sum_{|h_{1}|\leq X}\cdots\displaystyle\sum_{|h_{l}|\leq X}|V_{2}(\alpha_{l};h,\mathbf{h})|^{2^{k-l-1}}.

By applying classical Weyl differencing arguments, one has

\displaystyle W_{1}(\alpha_{k})\ll X^{2^{k-l-1}-(k-l)}\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq h\leq H}\displaystyle\sum_{|h_{1}|\leq X}\cdots\displaystyle\sum_{|h_{k-1}|\leq X}\biggl|\displaystyle\sum_{x\in I_{k-1}(\boldsymbol{h})}e(c_{k}hh_{1}\cdots h_{k-1}x)\biggr|

where
c_{k}
is a constant depending on
k,
and
I_{k-1}(\boldsymbol{h})
is an interval of integers contained in
[-X,X]
. Thus, for each
\epsilon>0,
one has

\displaystyle W_{1}(\alpha_{k})\ll HX^{2^{k-l-1}+l+\epsilon}\left(\frac{1}{q_{1}}+\frac{1}{X}+\frac{q_{1}}{HX^{k}}\right).

Furthermore, one has

\displaystyle W_{2}(\alpha_{l})\ll Y^{2^{k-l-1}-(k-l)}\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq h\leq H}\displaystyle\sum_{|h_{1}|\leq X}\cdots\displaystyle\sum_{|h_{l}|\leq X}\displaystyle\sum_{|h_{l+1}|\leq Y}\cdots\displaystyle\sum_{|h_{k-1}|\leq Y}\biggl|\displaystyle\sum_{y\in J_{k-1}(\boldsymbol{h})}e(d_{k}hh_{1}\cdots h_{k-1}y)\biggr|

where
d_{k}
is a constant depending on
k,
and
J_{k-1}(\boldsymbol{h})

Since we have

. Then, by putting

which contradicts our assumption.

Suppose next that there exist
q\in{\mathbb{N}},\ a\in{\mathbb{Z}},\ h\in[1,H]\cap{\mathbb{Z}}

which contradicts our assumption.

Thus, on recalling the definition of
\mathfrak{m}_{\lambda}
in the statement of proposition 6.1. We may assume that for all
h\in[1,H]\cap{\mathbb{Z}},
one has
h\alpha,h\beta\in\mathfrak{m}_{\lambda}.
Meanwhile, it follows from our assumption by Lemma 2.1 that

XY\ll\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq h\leq H}\bigl|\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\mathcal{A}(X,R)\\

Therefore, on writing

\bigl|\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\mathcal{A}(X,R)\\

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq h\leq H}\bigl|\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\mathcal{A}(X,R)\\

which contradicts our assumption. Hence, we are forced to have

\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq x\leq X\\

∎

Appendix A. Estimations in the proof of Proposition 3.4

Let
\alpha,\ N
be positive real numbers, and
k
and
l
be natural numbers with
k-l\geq 2
. Then, one has following:

\displaystyle(1)\ \displaystyle\sum_{1\leq n\leq N}\frac{1}{1+\alpha n^{k-l}}\ll\frac{N}{(1+N^{k-l}\alpha)^{1/(k-l)}}

\displaystyle(2)\ \displaystyle\sum_{1\leq n\leq N}\frac{1}{(1+\alpha n^{k-l})^{1/(k-l)}}\ll\frac{N\log N}{(1+N^{k-l}\alpha)^{1/(k-l)}}

\displaystyle(3)\ \displaystyle\sum_{1\leq n\leq N}\frac{1}{(1+\alpha n)^{2/(k-l)}}\ll\frac{N\log N}{(1+N\alpha)^{2/(k-l)}}

Proof of (1).

We have

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq n\leq N}\frac{1}{1+\alpha n^{k-l}}\ll\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq n\leq N}\min\{1,\alpha^{-1}n^{-k+l}\}\ll N+\alpha^{-1/(k-l)},

where
N
and
\alpha^{-1/(k-l)}
are derived from the contributions
1
and
\alpha^{-1}n^{-k+l}
, respectively.
∎

Proof of (2).

We have

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq n\leq N}\frac{1}{1+\alpha n^{k-l}}\ll\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq n\leq N}\min\{1,\alpha^{-1/(k-l)}n^{-1}\}\ll N+\alpha^{-1/(k-l)}\log N,

where
N
and
\alpha^{-1/(k-l)}\log N
are derived from the contributions
1
and
\alpha^{-1/(k-l)}n^{-1}
, respectively.
∎

Proof of (3).

We have

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq n\leq N}\frac{1}{1+\alpha n^{k-l}}\ll\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq n\leq N}\min\{1,(\alpha n)^{-2/(k-l)}\}\ll N+\frac{N\log N}{\alpha^{2/(k-l)}N^{2/(k-l)}},

where
N
and
\frac{N\log N}{\alpha^{2/(k-l)}N^{2/(k-l)}}
are derived from the contributions
1
and
(\alpha n)^{-2/(k-l)}
, respectively.
∎
