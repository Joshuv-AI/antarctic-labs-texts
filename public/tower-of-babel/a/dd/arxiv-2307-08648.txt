Compression of metrological quantum information in the presence of noise Preprint: APS/123-QED
Flavio Salvati 1, Wilfred Salmon 2,3, Crispin H.W. Barnes 1, David R.M. Arvidsson-Shukur 1,3

Abstract
In quantum metrology, information about unknown parameters 𝜽 = ( θ 1 , … , θ M ) \bm{\theta}=(\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{M}) is accessed by measuring probe states ρ ^ 𝜽 \hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}} . In experimental settings where copies of ρ ^ 𝜽 \hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}} can be produced rapidly (e.g., in optics), the information-extraction bottleneck can stem from high post-processing costs or detector saturation. In these regimes, it is desirable to compress the information encoded in ρ ^ 𝜽 ⊗ n \hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}}\,^{\otimes n} into m < n m<n copies of a postselected state: ρ ^ 𝜽 ps ⊗ m {\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}}^{\textrm{ps}}}\,^{\otimes m} . Remarkably, recent works have shown that, in the absence of noise, compression can be lossless, for m / n m/n arbitrarily small. Here, we fully characterize the family of filters that enable lossless compression. Further, we study the effect of noise on quantum-metrological information amplification. Motivated by experiments, we consider a popular family of filters, which we show is optimal for qubit probes. Further, we show that, for the optimal filter in this family, compression is still lossless if noise acts after the filter. However, in the presence of depolarizing noise before filtering, compression is lossy. In both cases, information-extraction can be implemented significantly better than simply discarding a constant fraction of the states, even in the presence of strong noise.



I
Introduction

Quantum metrology is a promising application of quantum technologies. By using measurement probes made of quantum states, quantum metrology exploits nonclassical effects, such as entanglement and squeezing, to make high-resolution measurements of physical parameters. Using quantum resources, one can reduce errors in measurements compared to classical strategies
Giovannetti et al. 2006
;
Giovannetti et al. 2011
;
Grote et al. 2013
;
Tse et al. 2019
;
Acernese et al. 2019
;
Yonezawa et al. 2012
;
Wolfgramm et al. 2010
;
Li et al. 2018
;
Borregaard and Sørensen 2013
;
Pezzè and Smerzi 2020
;
Taylor et al. 2013
.

In metrology, one can improve signal-to-noise ratios by preparing and measuring an increasing number of probes. However, measurement devices often have limited sensitivity and suffer from a dead time, i.e. the time needed to reset a detector after triggering it. Moreover, each measurement might generate large overheads in terms of post-processing. Even if probes are “cheap” to produce, they may be expensive to measure.

These problems can be mitigated by compressing metrological information into fewer states, using a strategy known as
postselection
. Postselection is essentially the application of a filter. As recently demonstrated in a quantum-optics experiment
Lupu-Gladstein et al. 2022
, postselection of quantum probes can compress information beyond classically achievable limits
Arvidsson-Shukur et
al. 2020
. In particular, postselected quantum metrology can allow detectors to operate at lower intensities whilst retaining the vast majority of the information encoded in the original high-intensity beam of probe states. This can reduce saturation of sensitive components, as well as alleviate computational costs associated with post-processing. The most common instance of postselected metrology is
weak-value amplification

Aharonov et al. 1988
, which has found many applications
Hosten and Kwiat 2008
;
Dixon et al. 2009
;
Turner et al. 2011
;
Pfeifer and Fischer 2011
;
Starling et al. 2010a
;
Starling et al. 2010b
;
Xu et al. 2013
;
Magaña Loaiza et al. 2014
;
Strübi and Bruder 2013
;
Viza et al. 2013
;
Egan and Stone 2012
.

In recent papers
(
Jenne and Arvidsson-Shukur 2021
;
Lupu-Gladstein et al. 2022
)
Jenne, Arvidsson-Shukur and Lupu-Gladstein (JAL), et al., introduced a postselection filter that can compress information contained in
\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}}\,^{\otimes n}
into
{\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}}^{\textrm{ps}}}\,^{\otimes m}
, where
m\leq n
. Moreover,
m/n
can be made arbitrarily small, and the compression can happen without any loss of information. These remarkable properties of the JAL filter rely on the quantum metrology experiments’ being completely noise-free. However, noise in real experiments leads to natural limits on compression
(
Lupu-Gladstein et al. 2022
)
. Until now, there exists no thorough investigation of the effect of noise on general multi-paramater postselected quantum metrology.

In this paper, we provide such an investigation. First, we give a thorough review of the quantum-Fisher-information matrix (QFIM), which quantifies a metrology protocol’s ability to estimate multiple parameters; and we also review previous results in postselected metrology. Then, we find the family of optimal postselection filters for noiseless multi-parameter quantum metrology and show that it contains the JAL filter. Next, we analyze and quantify the effect of noise on postselected metrology protocols. We focus on the worst-case scenario of depolarizing noise, applied either before or after the probes have been postselected. We consider two regimes; firstly when post-processing costs are dominant and one wishes to maximize the information per measured probe, and secondly when detector saturation dominates, and one wishes to maximize the rate of information arriving at the detector. We analyze the performance of an experimentally motivated
(
Lupu-Gladstein et al. 2022
)
family of filters (including the JAL filter) in both of these regimes. This family, we show, is optimal for qubit probes. When noise acts after postselection, we find that the JAL filter remains optimal in all dimensions. Then, we provide an explicit example demonstrating that the JAL filter can be sub-optimal when noise acts before postselection. Finally, we provide a filter that always outperforms the naïve strategy of discarding a constant fraction of states, even in the presence of arbitrarily strong noise.

II
Preliminaries

II.1
Local Estimation Theory

A typical quantum estimation problem consists of recovering the value of
M
continuous parameters
\bm{\theta}:=(\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\dots,\theta_{M})
encoded in a parametrised quantum state
\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}}
. We focus on the well-established field of local estimation
Paris 2009
, where one considers small deviations in parameters, as opposed to global estimation
(
Smith et al. 2022
;
Salmon et al. 2023
)
where the entire parameter space is considered. A general scheme consists of three steps
(
Giovannetti et al. 2011
)
:

1.

Prepare a parameter-independent probe state
\hat{\rho}_{0}
.

2.

Evolve the state with a parameter-dependent unitary operation
\hat{U}(\bm{\theta})
:

\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}}=\hat{U}(\bm{\theta})\rho_{0}\hat{U}(\bm{\theta})^{\dagger}\,.

(1)

3.

Extract information by means of a suitable measurement. The most general measurement procedure is a positive-operator valued measure (POVM)
Giovannetti et al. 2006
, described by a collection
\big\{\hat{F}_{k}\big\}
of positive semi-definite operators
(\hat{F}_{k}\geq 0)
that sum to unity (
\sum_{k}\hat{F}_{k}=\hat{1}
). One then observes outcome
k
with probability:

p(k|\bm{\theta})=\text{Tr}\big[\hat{F}_{k}\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}}\big]\,.

(2)

All the information about the parameters
\bm{\theta}
is then encapsulated in the probability distribution
p(k|\bm{\theta})
.

The parameters are estimated through an estimator
\hat{\theta}(k):=(\hat{\theta}_{1},\hat{\theta}_{2},\dots\hat{\theta}_{M}\big)
, a map from the space of measurement outcomes to the space of possible values of the parameters. If we are in a limit of small deviations, we assume that the estimator
\hat{\bm{\theta}}
is locally unbiased, that is

\sum_{k}{\big[\bm{\theta}-\hat{\bm{\theta}}(k)\big]p(k|\bm{\theta})}=0\,\,\text{and}\,\,\sum_{k}{\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{i}(k)\partial_{j}p(k|\bm{\theta})}=\delta_{ij}\,,

(3)

where
i,j=1,\dots,M
and
\partial_{j}:=\dfrac{\partial}{\partial\bm{\theta}_{j}}
.

The accuracy of
\hat{\bm{\theta}}(k)
is quantified by its covariance matrix, given by

\text{Cov}\big(\hat{\bm{\theta}}\big):=\sum_{k}{\big[\bm{\theta}-\hat{\bm{\theta}}(k)\big]\big[\bm{\theta}-\hat{\bm{\theta}}(k)\big]^{\top}p(k|\bm{\theta})}\,.

(4)

The covariance matrix obeys the Cramér-Rao bound (CRB)
Cramer 1946
;
Rao 1992

\text{Cov}\big(\hat{\bm{\theta}}\big)\geq I(\bm{\theta})^{-1}\,,

(5)

where
I(\bm{\theta})
is the Fisher information matrix (FIM):

I(\bm{\theta})_{i,j}:=\sum_{k}{p(k|\bm{\theta})\big[\partial_{i}\log{p(k|\bm{\theta})}\big]\big[\partial_{j}\log{p(k|\bm{\theta})}\big]}\,.

(6)

In a quantum experiment, the choice of measurement in Step 3 affects the probabilities
p(k|\bm{\theta})
, and hence
I(\bm{\theta})
. The quantum Fisher information matrix (QFIM)
Braunstein and Caves 1994
;
Fujiwara and Nagaoka 1995
;
Liu et al. 2019
is defined by

\mathcal{I}(\bm{\theta}|\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}})_{i,j}=\text{Tr}\big[\hat{\Lambda}_{i}\,\partial_{j}\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}}\big]\,,

(7)

where
\hat{\Lambda}_{i}
is the symmetric logarithmic derivative, implicitly defined by
\partial_{i}\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}}=\dfrac{1}{2}(\hat{\Lambda}_{i}\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}}+\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}}\hat{\Lambda}_{i})

(
Helstrom 1969
)
. The inverse QFIM lower bounds the inverse classical Fisher information matrix:

I(\bm{\theta})^{-1}\geq\mathcal{I}(\bm{\theta}|\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}})^{-1},

(8)

for any choice of measurement. In general, Eq. (
8
) is not saturable. However, there is always a measurement that gets within a factor of 2 of the QFIM in the asymptotic limit: see Ref.
Albarelli et al. 2020
for details. The QFIM can thus replace the FIM in Eq. (
5
), leading to the quantum Cramér-Rao bound (QCRB):

\text{Cov}\big(\hat{\bm{\theta}}\big)\geq\mathcal{I}(\bm{\theta}|\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}})^{-1}\,.

(9)

In practice, one receives
N>1
copies of the quantum state
\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}}
and thus has access to the state
\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}}^{\otimes N}
. One finds that
\mathcal{I}(\bm{\theta}|\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}}^{\otimes N})=N\,\mathcal{I}(\bm{\theta}|\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}})
, implying that

\text{Cov}\big(\hat{\bm{\theta}}\big)\geq\frac{1}{N}\mathcal{I}(\bm{\theta}|\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}})^{-1}\,.

(10)

Hence, one can decrease the variance of the estimate by increasing the number of measurements
N
or by designing a setup that increases the quantum Fisher information
\mathcal{I}(\bm{\theta}|\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}})
.

Finally, we consider the choice of
\hat{\rho}_{0}
in step 1. Since
\mathcal{I}(\bm{\theta}|\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}})
is convex
Fujiwara 2001
, the maximum QFIM is always achieved by using pure probe states
(
Pang et al. 2014
;
Pang and Brun 2015
)
.

III
Optimal filter for noiseless postselection

In this section, we characterize the most general optimal filter for noiseless multi-parameter quantum metrology. Then, we show that the JAL filter is in the family of optimal filters; it is the canonical choice.

Suppose that we have a process that produces a state
\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}}^{(2)}
from a state
\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}}^{(1)}
with probability
P_{\bm{\theta}}^{\text{ps}}
, for example, by postselection. The process is said to uniformly amplify the information contained in
\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}}^{(1)}
if, for all
i
and
j
, the ratio

\mathcal{A}_{i,j}(\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}}^{\text{(2)}},\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}}^{\text{(1)}}):=\dfrac{\mathcal{I}_{i,j}(\bm{\theta}|{\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}}^{(2)}})}{\mathcal{I}_{i,j}(\bm{\theta}|\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}}^{(1)})}

(17)

is the same. In this case, we drop the
i,j
label in
\mathcal{A}
and call
\mathcal{A}(\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}}^{\text{(2)}},\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}}^{\text{(1)}})
the
information amplification
. We also define the
compression efficiency
(which appears in
(
Das et al. 2023
)
),

\eta(P_{\bm{\theta}}^{\text{ps}},\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}}^{\text{(2)}},\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}}^{\text{(1)}}):=P_{\bm{\theta}}^{\text{ps}}\,\mathcal{A}(\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}}^{\text{(2)}},\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}}^{\text{(1)}})\,.

(18)

The compression efficiency equals the ratio of the total expected information content before and after the process. If
\eta(P_{\bm{\theta}},\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}}^{\text{(2)}},\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}}^{\text{(1)}})=1
, the process is said to be lossless, otherwise it is said to be lossy. For a chosen postselection probability
P_{\bm{\theta}}^{\text{ps}}
, a filter
\hat{F}
is optimal if, in the limit as
\bm{\delta}\to 0
, postselection using
\hat{F}
is lossless. Because postselection cannot increase the average information, for a given postselection probability
P_{\bm{\theta}}^{\text{ps}}
, the optimal filter gives the maximum possible information amplification.

The simplest example of an optimal postselection filter is the JAL filter:

\hat{F}=(t^{2}-1)\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}}+\hat{1}\,.

(19)

Note that one does not need to know

Finally, let us discuss intuitively what makes a filter optimal. Provided that
\bm{\delta}
is small, the states
\ket{\psi_{\bm{\theta_{0}}}}
contribute vanishingly small information and should therefore be filtered away. This is achieved by setting

In this case, one wants to receive the most information from the smallest number of probe measurements, which corresponds to maximizing the information amplification [Eq. (
IV.2.1
)]. We work in the limit in which the quantum experiment is cheap to run, so that the postselection probability can be made arbitrarily small; one simply repeats the experiment as many times as is necessary to produce enough successfully postselected states to reduce the error below a set value.

Alternatively, suppose that detector saturation is the limiting factor. In this case, one fixes a maximum postselection probability
P_{\text{max}}
. Ideally, one would set
P_{\text{max}}
equal to the ratio between the maximum-probe-measurement rate and the maximum-probe-production rate. Then, one maximizes the compression efficiency [Eq. (
26
)] such that the postselection probability is no greater than
P_{\text{max}}
. This maximizes the rate of information arriving at the detector, whilst ensuring that it will not saturate.

In the first case,
\mathcal{A}(\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}}^{\text{n,ps}},\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}}^{\text{n}})
is maximized for
D=0
. In the second case, the compression efficiency is independent of
D
. Setting
D=0
gives the lowest postselection probability, which avoids detector saturation. Therefore, in both regimes the best choice is
D=0
. Denoting
p_{\bm{\theta}}/B=t^{2}
, Eq. (
IV.2.1
) becomes:

\displaystyle\hskip-5.69054pt\mathcal{A}(\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}}^{\text{n,ps}},\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}}^{\text{n}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hskip-28.45274pt\dfrac{\Big(1-\epsilon+2\dfrac{\epsilon}{d}\Big)t^{2}}{\Big[\Big(1-\epsilon+\dfrac{\epsilon}{d}\Big)t^{2}+\dfrac{\epsilon}{d}(u-1)\Big]\Big[\Big(1-\epsilon+\dfrac{\epsilon}{d}\Big)t^{2}+\dfrac{\epsilon}{d}\Big]}\,,

(27)

which is parameterized by
t^{2}
. We now proceed to maxmimize this expression.

IV.2.2
Post-processing is dominant

In the case where post-processing carries the largest overhead, we are interested in maximizing the information amplification, given by Eq. (
IV.2.1
). This expression attains a maximum at

t_{\text{pp}}^{2}=\dfrac{\sqrt{u-1}\,\epsilon}{d(1-\epsilon)+\epsilon}\,.

(28)

In Fig.
4(a)
, we plot
\mathcal{A}(\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}}^{\text{n,ps}},\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}}^{\text{n}})
vs
t^{2}
for different values of
\epsilon
and
d=u=2
(noting that
t_{\text{pp}}<1
). We see that the information amplification reaches a maximum at
t=t_{\text{pp}}
. The maximum of
\mathcal{A}
increases and
t_{\text{pp}}
moves towards zero as
\epsilon
decreases. Fig.
4(b)
shows the same plot, but for
d=10
,
u=5
. In this case, the optimal
t_{\text{pp}}
can be greater than
1
.

Substituting
t_{\text{pp}}^{2}
into Eq. (
28
), and taking the limit
d\rightarrow\infty
, with
u\sim d
, the maximum information amplification simplifies to
1/\epsilon
:

\lim_{d\rightarrow\infty}{\max\mathcal{A}(\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}}^{\text{n,ps}},\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}}^{\text{n}})}=\frac{1}{\epsilon}\,.

(29)

Any filter with
t=t_{\text{pp}}
will achieve the maximum information compression. Additionally, it is sensible to minimize the (expected) required number of probes, which corresponds to maximizing
P_{\bm{\theta}}^{\text{ps}}
for a fixed
\mathcal{A}(\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}}^{\text{n,ps}},\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}}^{\text{n}})
. Rewriting

P_{\bm{\theta}}^{\text{ps}}=B\left[(1-\epsilon+\frac{\epsilon}{d})t^{2}_{\text{pp}}+\frac{\epsilon}{d}(u-1)\right],

(30)

we see that maximizing
P_{\bm{\theta}}^{\text{ps}}
corresponds to setting
B
to its maximum possible value. Thus, we deduce the optimal form of
\hat{F}
to mitigate post-processing costs:

p_{\bm{\theta}}=\min\left[1,t^{2}_{\text{pp}}\right],\quad B=\min\left[1,\frac{1}{t^{2}_{\text{pp}}}\right].

(31)

In Fig.
4(c)

\epsilon
at different values of

.

When compared to an experiment with the same level of noise, our postselection filter can sometimes perform better for stronger depolarizing noise. As noise reduces the total available information, this does not mean that it would be beneficial to artificially increase
\epsilon

IV.2.3
Detector saturation is dominant

Here, we want to maximize the compression efficiency [Eq. (
26
)], subject to the constraint that the postselection probability
P_{\bm{\theta}}^{\text{ps}}
is no greater than
P_{\text{max}}\in[0,1]
. We calculate this maximum in Appendix
E
. For notational brevity, we define

b=\left(1-\epsilon+\dfrac{\epsilon}{d}\right),\quad c=\dfrac{\epsilon}{d}(u-1).

(32)

Mathematically, the optimal filter is most succinctly described by the following five (counting min and max) cases:

•

P_{\text{max}}\geq b+c
.

Then the optimal filter has
p_{\bm{\theta}}=B=1
.

•

P_{\text{max}}<b+c
.

Letting
t^{2}=p_{\bm{\theta}}/B
, the optimal filter has

\displaystyle p_{\bm{\theta}}=\frac{P_{\text{max}}}{b+c/t^{2}}\,,\hskip 28.45274ptB=\frac{P_{\text{max}}}{bt^{2}+c}.

(33)

There are 2 subcases depending on the value of
t_{\text{pp}}
.

*

t_{\text{pp}}\leq 1
. Then, the optimal filter has

t^{2}=\max\left[t_{\text{pp}}^{2},\frac{P_{\text{max}}-c}{b}\right].

(34)

*

t_{\text{pp}}\geq 1
. Then, the optimal filter has

t^{2}=\min\left[t_{\text{pp}}^{2},\left(\frac{c}{P_{\text{max}}-b}\right)^{+}\right],

(35)

where
a^{+}=a
if
a\geq 0
and
a^{+}=\infty
if
a<0
.

Physically, the optimal filter can fall into one of three distinct categories depending on the size of
P_{\max}
. The three categories arise from grouping the cases above into optimal filters with common behaviors. To describe the categories, we introduce
P_{*}
as the largest value of
P_{\text{max}}
for which the optimal filter has
t^{2}
equal to
t_{\text{pp}}^{2}
. We find that the three different categories are:

•

b+c=1-\epsilon+u\,\epsilon/d\leq P_{\max}
, whereupon
p_{\bm{\theta}}=B=1
. In this scenario, the filter simply blocks all states that can only arise from noise, and lets through all the states that carry information about the parameters. This regime is lossless (when compared to the information carried in
\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}}^{\text{n}}
).

•

P_{*}\leq P_{\max}<b+c=1-\epsilon+u\,\epsilon/d
. In this scenario, the filter compresses information. Since compression with noise is lossy, it does the smallest amount of compression possible, i.e. such that
P_{\bm{\theta}}^{\text{ps}}=P_{\max}
. It does this by maximizing the information amplification, which corresponds to setting
t
as close as possible to
t_{\text{pp}}
whilst still satisfying
P_{\bm{\theta}}^{\text{ps}}=P_{\max}
. In this regime, the filter incurs some loss due to noise.

•

0\leq P_{\max}<P_{*}
. When
P_{\max}
reaches
P_{*}
, it is no longer advantageous to compress information. Instead,
p_{\bm{\theta}}/B=t_{\text{pp}}^{2}
stays constant whilst
P_{\bm{\theta}}^{\text{ps}}=P_{\max}
, so that both
p_{\bm{\theta}}
and
B
decrease. The optimal filter can be decomposed as a compressive filter with
t=t_{\text{pp}}
followed by a filter that is proportional to
\hat{1}
. In this scenario, the filter compresses some of the information, but is also forced to blindly discard a constant fraction of the states.

We can compare our strategy with a näive classical strategy that would blindly discard a constant fraction of the states:

\hat{F}_{\text{nav}}=P_{\max}\,\hat{1}\,.

(36)

This filter gives a compression efficiency of
P_{\max}
. In Fig.

V
Conclusion

In this paper we derived the family of optimal postselection filters for noiseless multi-parameter quantum metrology. In the absence of noise, these filters are lossless: they compress information equally across all parameters, while decreasing the postselection probability by the same factor. We showed that the previously-proposed JAL filter
(
Jenne and Arvidsson-Shukur 2021
)
is contained wthin our family of optimal filters.

Noise in real experiments leads to natural limits on compression of information. The quantum optics experiment in Ref.
(
Lupu-Gladstein et al. 2022
)
was affected by errors in
t^{2}
, the filter transmission probability, and the unitary operation
\hat{U}(\bm{\theta})
, which bounded the maximum possible compression of information and made the filtering procedure somewhat lossy. This motivated our analysis of the effect of noise on postselected quantum metrology.

We focused our noise-analysis on the worst-case scenario of depolarizing noise of strength
\epsilon
. We considered situations where this noise was applied either before or after the probes have been postselected. When noise acts after the postselection, we found that the JAL filter remains optimal.

We also analyzed the JAL filter’s performance when noise acts before the postselection. In this case, we considered two regimes, whereby either post-processing or detector saturation is the main concern. To make the problem tractable, we considered a subset of experimentally sensible filters [Eq. (
94
)]. To alleviate post-processing costs, one wants to receive the most information in the smallest number of probes, which corresponds to maximizing the information amplification [Eq. (
IV.2.1
)]. To protect against detector saturation, we fixed a maximum postselection probability
P_{\text{max}}
and maximized the compression efficiency [Eq. (
26
)]. We showed that the JAL filter is not always optimal, but with slight modifications it performs well in both the post-processing and detector saturation regimes. Thus, if one can estimate the strength of noise in an experiment, one should implement a different filter compared to the case of no noise.

We showed that for qubit sensors (
u=d=2
) the family of filters in Eq. (
24
) is optimal. However, for finite
u,d\neq 2
, we show that this subset of filters is not always optimal. In fact, it can sometimes be advantageous for states corresponding to noise to be mixed with states corresponding to changes in
\bm{\theta}
.

Wit this work, we hope to extend the use cases of postselected metrology to non-optical systems.

Appendix A
Proof of optimal noiseless filter

In this Appendix we prove the main Theorem from section
III
.

From Eq. (
12
), we see that the QFIM only contains terms that depend on
\hat{F}_{u}
, and thus we deduce part 1 of the theorem.

We now focus on the diagonal entries of the QFIM:
\mathcal{I}_{j,j}({\bm{\theta}}|\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}}^{\text{ps}})
. Taking the derivative of
\innerproduct{\psi_{\bm{\theta}}}{\psi_{\bm{\theta}}}\equiv 1
, we see that:

\textrm{Re}(\innerproduct{\partial_j \psi_{\bm{\theta}}}{\psi_{\bm{\theta}}})=0\,.

(37)

Thus we can write

\ket{\partial_{j}\psi_{\bm{\theta}}}=ix_{j}\ket{\psi_{\bm{\theta}}}+\alpha_{j}\ket{{\psi_{\bm{\theta}}}^{\perp,j}}\,,

(38)

for
x_{j}\in\mathbb{R}
a
real
coefficient,
\alpha_{j}\in\mathbb{C}
and some normalized set of
\ket{\psi^{\perp,j}}
, orthogonal to
\ket{\psi_{\bm{\theta}}}
(note that they may not be orthogonal to each other). Define:

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{\hat{F}}{\psi_{\bm{\theta}}}

\displaystyle=P_{\bm{\theta}}^{\text{ps}}\in[0,1]\,,

(39)

\displaystyle\braket{{\psi_{\bm{\theta}}}^{\perp,j}|\hat{F}|{\psi_{\bm{\theta}}}^{\perp,j}}

\displaystyle=B_{j}\in[0,1]\,,

(40)

\displaystyle\braket{\psi_{\bm{\theta}}|\hat{F}|{\psi_{\bm{\theta}}}^{\perp,j}}

\displaystyle=C_{j}\in\mathbb{C}\,.

(41)

Substituting these expressions into Eq. (
12
), we find:

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{j,j}({\bm{\theta}}|\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}}^{\text{ps}})

\displaystyle=4\Bigg[\dfrac{1}{P_{\bm{\theta}}^{\text{ps}}}\Big(x_{j}^{2}P_{\bm{\theta}}^{\text{ps}}+|\alpha_{j}|^{2}B_{j}+ix_{j}(\alpha_{j}^{*}C_{j}^{*}-\alpha_{j}C_{j})\Big)-\dfrac{1}{({P_{\bm{\theta}}^{\text{ps}}})^{2}}|ix_{j}P_{\bm{\theta}}^{\text{ps}}+\alpha_{j}C_{j}|^{2}\Bigg]\,,

(42)

\displaystyle=4\Big(\dfrac{|\alpha_{j}|^{2}B_{j}}{P_{\bm{\theta}}^{\text{ps}}}-\dfrac{|\alpha_{j}C_{j}|^{2}}{({P_{\bm{\theta}}^{\text{ps}}})^{2}}\Big)\,.

(43)

Setting
\hat{F}=\hat{1}
, we have:

\mathcal{I}_{j,j}({\bm{\theta}}|\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}})=4|\alpha_{j}|^{2}.

(44)

It therefore follows that:

\mathcal{A}(\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}}^{\text{ps}},\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}}^{\text{}})=\Big(\dfrac{B_{j}}{P_{\bm{\theta}}^{\text{ps}}}-\dfrac{|C_{j}|^{2}}{\,({P_{\bm{\theta}}^{\text{ps}}})^{2}}\Big)\,.

(45)

In order for
\mathcal{A}(\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}}^{\text{ps}},\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}}^{\text{}})=1/P_{\bm{\theta}}^{\text{ps}}
, we require that
C_{j}=0
(by assumption
\mathcal{I}_{j,j}({\bm{\theta}}|\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}})\,,\alpha_{j}\neq 0
) and
B_{j}=1
. We must therefore have:

\hat{F}_{u}=(P_{\bm{\theta}}^{\text{ps}}-1)\outerproduct{\psi_{\bm{\theta}}}{\psi_{\bm{\theta}}}+\hat{\Pi}_{u}\,.

(46)

With this, we have deduced uniqueness of an optimal
\hat{F}_{u}
. In Ref.
(
Jenne and Arvidsson-Shukur 2021
)
it was shown that this
\hat{F}_{u}
is indeed lossless (which requires calculating the off diagonal terms).
\square

It may be that
\mathcal{U}
has a dimensionality less than
d
, the dimensionality of the unitary
U(\bm{\theta})
. For example, this will always happen if
M+1<d
, where
M
is the number of parameters. In general,
\mathcal{U}
can have dimension
u
, with
u\leq M+1
, and if
u<d
, the optimal filter is not fully determined. In an orthonormal basis, where
\ket{\psi_{\bm{\theta}}}
is the first basis vector, and the first
u
basis vectors span
\mathcal{U}
, we have that the optimal filter takes the form

\hat{F}=\begin{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}P_{\bm{\theta}}^{\text{ps}}&\dots&0\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&\dots&1\end{pmatrix}&\hat{C}\\
\hat{C}^{\dagger}&\hat{D}\end{pmatrix}\,,

(47)

where
\hat{C}
and
\hat{D}
are matrices such that
\hat{0}\leq\hat{F}\leq\hat{1}
.

The simplest example of an optimal postselection filter is for
\hat{C}=0
and
\hat{D}=\hat{1}
, in which case, setting
P_{\bm{\theta}}^{\text{ps}}=t^{2}
, we recover the JAL filter:

\hat{F}=(t^{2}-1)\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}}+\hat{1}\,.

(48)

Note that the JAL filter does not require knowledge of
\mathcal{U}
.

So far, we have assumed that our filter is constructed based on perfect knowledge of the parameters, i.e. our initial estimate
\bm{\theta}_{0}
is equal to the true value of the parameters
\bm{\theta}
. In practice, however, one does not know
\bm{\theta}
exactly. Instead, one has an estimate
\bm{\theta}_{0}=\bm{\theta-\delta}
and implements a filter using
\bm{\theta}_{0}
. In Ref.
Jenne and Arvidsson-Shukur 2021
, it was shown that if one implements the JAL filter using
\bm{\theta}_{0}
, then
\mathcal{A}(\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}}^{\text{ps}},\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}}^{\text{}})
and
P_{\bm{\theta}}^{\text{ps}}
are only perturbed at order
\bm{\delta}^{2}
- there is no order
\bm{\delta}
correction.

In Appendix
B
, we study the order
\bm{\delta}^{2}
correction when using the JAL filter, deriving the upper bound

\mathcal{A}(\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}}^{\text{ps}},\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}}^{\text{}})\leq\dfrac{1}{t^{2}}\Big[1-\dfrac{1-t^{2}}{t^{2}}\,\bm{\delta}^{T}\mathcal{I}(\bm{\theta}|\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}})\,\bm{\delta}\,\Big]\,.

(49)

Equation (
49
) provides a way to calculate the expected decrease in the information amplification to second order in
\bm{\delta}
. It allows one to understand which parameters in
\bm{\delta}
are responsible for the largest loss of information. In the trivial case when
\mathcal{I}(\bm{\theta}|\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}})
is diagonal, the parameters
\theta_{i}
with the largest QFI will also lead to the greatest loss.

Appendix B
Information amplification scaling for the JAL filter

In this Appendix, we study the second order corrections to the information amplification when
\bm{\delta}\neq\bm{0}
. Consider the perturbed JAL filter

\hat{F}=(t^{2}-1)\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}+\hat{1}\,.

(50)

We expand
\hat{U}(\bm{\theta}_{0})
to second order in
\bm{\delta}
:

\hat{U}(\bm{\theta}_{0})=\hat{U}(\bm{\theta})+\Big[\partial_{i}\hat{U}(\bm{\theta})\Big](\bm{\theta}_{0}-\bm{\theta})_{i}+\dfrac{1}{2}\Big[\partial^{2}_{i,j}\hat{U}(\bm{\theta})\Big](\bm{\theta}_{0}-\bm{\theta})_{i}(\bm{\theta}_{0}-\bm{\theta})_{j}\,.

(51)

The above equation can be written as

\displaystyle\hat{U}(\bm{\theta}_{0})=U(\bm{\theta})+\Big[\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\hat{U}(\bm{\theta})\Big]^{T}(\bm{\theta}_{0}-\bm{\theta})+\dfrac{1}{2}(\bm{\theta}_{0}-\bm{\theta})^{T}H(\bm{\theta})\,(\bm{\theta}_{0}-\bm{\theta})\,,

(52)

where
\hat{H}(\theta)=\partial^{2}_{i,j}U(\bm{\theta})
is the Hessian matrix. Hence

\displaystyle\hat{U}(\bm{\theta}_{0})=\hat{U}(\bm{\theta})-i\hat{U}(\bm{\theta})\hat{d}_{1}+\dfrac{i}{2}\hat{U}(\bm{\theta})\hat{d}_{2}+\mathcal{O}(\bm{\delta}^{3})\,,

(53)

where
\hat{d}_{1}
and
\hat{d}_{2}
are defined as

\displaystyle\hat{d}_{1}

\displaystyle=-i\,\hat{U}^{\dagger}(\bm{\theta})\Big[\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\hat{U}(\bm{\theta})\Big]^{T}\bm{\delta}\,,

(54)

\displaystyle\hat{d}_{2}

\displaystyle=-i\,\hat{U}^{\dagger}(\bm{\theta})\,\bm{\delta}^{T}\hat{H}(\bm{\theta})\,\bm{\delta}\,.

(55)

We want to find
\hat{d}^{\dagger}_{1}
and
\hat{d}^{\dagger}_{2}
. Consider

U(\bm{\theta}_{0})U(\bm{\theta}_{0})^{\dagger}=\hat{1}\,.

(56)

Inserting the Taylor expansion, we find

\displaystyle\hat{1}

\displaystyle=\Big[\hat{U}(\bm{\theta})-i\hat{U}(\bm{\theta})\hat{d}_{1}+\dfrac{i}{2}\hat{U}(\bm{\theta})\hat{d}_{2}\Big]\Big[\hat{U}(\bm{\theta})-i\hat{U}(\bm{\theta})\hat{d}_{1}+\dfrac{i}{2}\hat{U}(\bm{\theta})\hat{d}_{2}\Big]^{\dagger}

(57)

\displaystyle=\hat{U}(\bm{\theta})\hat{U}^{\dagger}(\bm{\theta})+i\hat{U}(\bm{\theta})\Big[\hat{d}_{1}-\hat{d}_{1}^{\dagger}\Big]\hat{U}^{\dagger}(\bm{\theta})+\dfrac{i}{2}\hat{U}(\bm{\theta})\Big[\hat{d}_{2}-\hat{d}_{2}^{\dagger}-2\,i\,\hat{d}_{1}\hat{d}^{\dagger}_{1}\Big]\hat{U}^{\dagger}(\bm{\theta})\,.

(58)

Looking at the term of order
\bm{\delta}
, we immediately see that
\hat{d}_{1}=\hat{d}^{\dagger}_{1}
. Instead, the second order term gives

\hat{d}_{2}^{\dagger}=\hat{d}_{2}-2\,i\,\hat{d}_{1}\hat{d}^{\dagger}_{1}\,.

(59)

Now, we can write
\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}
as

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}

\displaystyle=\hat{U}(\bm{\theta}_{0})\rho_{0}\hat{U}(\bm{\theta}_{0})^{\dagger}

(60)

\displaystyle=\Big[\hat{U}(\bm{\theta})-i\hat{U}(\bm{\theta})\hat{d}_{1}+\dfrac{i}{2}\hat{U}(\bm{\theta})\hat{d}_{2}\Big]\rho_{0}\Big[\hat{U}(\bm{\theta})^{\dagger}+i\hat{d}^{\dagger}_{1}\hat{U}(\bm{\theta})^{\dagger}-\dfrac{i}{2}\hat{d}^{\dagger}_{2}\hat{U}(\bm{\theta})^{\dagger}\Big]

(61)

\displaystyle=\hat{U}(\bm{\theta})\Big[1-i\hat{d}_{1}+\dfrac{i}{2}\hat{d}_{2}\Big]\rho_{0}\Big[1+i\hat{d}_{1}-\dfrac{i}{2}\hat{d}_{2}-\hat{d}^{2}_{1}\Big]\hat{U}(\bm{\theta})^{\dagger}\,,

(62)

\displaystyle=\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}}+i[\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}},\hat{D}_{1}]-\dfrac{i}{2}[\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}},\hat{D}_{2}]+\hat{D}_{1}\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}}\hat{D}_{1}-\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}}\hat{D}^{2}_{1}+\mathcal{O}(\bm{\delta}^{3})\,,

(63)

where we defined
\hat{D}_{1},\hat{D}_{2}
:

\displaystyle\hat{D}_{1}=U(\bm{\theta})\hat{d}_{1}\hat{U}(\bm{\theta})^{\dagger}\,,

(64)

\displaystyle\hat{D}_{2}=U(\bm{\theta})\hat{d}_{2}\hat{U}(\bm{\theta})^{\dagger}\,.

(65)

We can now calculate the postselection probability
P_{\bm{\theta}}^{\text{ps}}
:

\displaystyle P_{\bm{\theta}}^{\text{ps}}

\displaystyle=\text{Tr}[\hat{F}\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}}]\,,

(66)

\displaystyle=\braket{\psi_{\bm{\theta}}|\hat{F}|\psi_{\bm{\theta}}}\,,

(67)

\displaystyle=\braket{\psi_{\bm{\theta}}|\Big[(t^{2}-1)\Big(\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}}+i[\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}},\hat{D}_{1}]-\dfrac{i}{2}[\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}},\hat{D}_{2}]+\hat{D}_{1}\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}}\hat{D}_{1}-\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}}\hat{D}^{2}_{1}+\mathcal{O}(\bm{\delta}^{3})\Big)+\hat{1}\Big]|\psi_{\bm{\theta}}}+\mathcal{O}(\bm{\delta}^{3})\,,

(68)

\displaystyle=t^{2}-(1-t^{2})\Big[\braket{\psi_{\bm{\theta}}|\big(\hat{D}_{1}\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}}\hat{D}_{1}\big)|\psi_{\bm{\theta}}}-\braket{\psi_{\bm{\theta}}|\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}}\hat{D}^{2}_{1}|\psi_{\bm{\theta}}}\Big]+\mathcal{O}(\bm{\delta}^{3})\,,

(69)

\displaystyle=t^{2}+(1-t^{2})\Big[\braket{\psi_{\bm{\theta}}|\hat{D}^{2}_{1}|\psi_{\bm{\theta}}}-|\braket{\psi_{\bm{\theta}}|\hat{D}_{1}|\psi_{\bm{\theta}}}|^{2}\Big]+\mathcal{O}(\bm{\delta}^{3})\,,

(70)

\displaystyle=t^{2}+(1-t^{2})\Big[\braket{\psi_{0}|\hat{d}^{2}_{1}|\psi_{0}}-|\braket{\psi_{0}|\hat{d}_{1}|\psi_{0}}|^{2}\Big]+\mathcal{O}(\bm{\delta}^{3})\,,

(71)

\displaystyle=t^{2}+(1-t^{2})\sum_{i,j}{\bm{\delta}_{i}\bm{\delta}_{j}\Big[\braket{\partial_{i}\psi_{\bm{\theta}}|\partial_{j}\psi_{\bm{\theta}}}-\braket{\partial_{i}\psi_{\bm{\theta}}|\psi_{\bm{\theta}}}\braket{\psi_{\bm{\theta}}|\partial_{j}\psi_{\bm{\theta}}}}\Big]+\mathcal{O}(\bm{\delta}^{3})\,,

(72)

\displaystyle=t^{2}+(1-t^{2})\sum_{i,j}{\bm{\delta}_{i}\bm{\delta}_{j}\,\mathcal{I}(\bm{\theta}|\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}})_{i,j}}+\mathcal{O}(\bm{\delta}^{3})\,,

(73)

\displaystyle=t^{2}+(1-t^{2})\,\bm{\delta}^{T}\,\mathcal{I}(\bm{\theta}|\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}})\,\bm{\delta}+\mathcal{O}(\bm{\delta}^{3})\,.

(74)

Hence, we find:

P_{\bm{\theta}}^{\text{ps}}=t^{2}+(1-t^{2})\,\bm{\delta}^{T}\,\mathcal{I}(\bm{\theta}|\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}})\,\bm{\delta}+\mathcal{O}(\bm{\delta}^{3})\,.

(75)

Since the QFIM is positive semi-definite, the term of order
\mathcal{O}(|\bm{\delta}|^{2})
is always positive. Therefore, for
\bm{\delta}
small, the postselection probability increases with increasing
|\bm{\delta}|
.

We can now find an upper bound on
\mathcal{A}(\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}}^{\text{ps}},\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}}^{\text{}})
. From the discussion in Ref.
Jenne and Arvidsson-Shukur 2021
, we know that

\mathcal{I}(\bm{\theta}|\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}}^{\text{ps}})=\dfrac{1}{t^{2}}\Big[\mathcal{I}(\bm{\theta}|\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}})+\Delta\Big]\,,

(76)

where
\Delta
is a matrix of order
\mathcal{O}(|\bm{\delta}|^{2})
which we wish to bound. Because
\eta(P_{\bm{\theta}}^{\text{ps}},\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}}^{\text{ps}},\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}}^{\text{}})\leq 1
, we can write

P_{\bm{\theta}}^{\text{ps}}\,\mathcal{I}(\bm{\theta}|\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}}^{\text{ps}})\leq\mathcal{I}(\bm{\theta}|\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}})\,.

(77)

Substituting Eqs. (
75
) and Eq. (
76
) into Eq. (
77
), we find

\displaystyle\Big[t^{2}+(1-t^{2})\,\bm{\delta}^{T}\mathcal{I}(\bm{\theta}|\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}})\,\bm{\delta}+\mathcal{O}(\bm{\delta}^{3})\Big]\dfrac{1}{t^{2}}\Big[\mathcal{I}(\bm{\theta}|\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}})+\Delta\Big]\leq\mathcal{I}(\bm{\theta}|\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}})

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(\bm{\theta}|\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}})+\Delta+\dfrac{1-t^{2}}{t^{2}}\bm{\delta}^{T}\mathcal{I}(\bm{\theta}|\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}})\,\bm{\delta}\,\mathcal{I}(\bm{\theta}|\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}})\leq\mathcal{I}(\bm{\theta}|\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}})+\mathcal{O}(|\bm{\delta}|^{3})

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\Delta\leq-\dfrac{1-t^{2}}{t^{2}}\big[\bm{\delta}^{T}\mathcal{I}(\bm{\theta}|\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}})\,\bm{\delta}\big]\,\mathcal{I}(\bm{\theta}|\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}})\,+\mathcal{O}(|\bm{\delta}|^{3})\,.

Therefore, we reach the important result

\mathcal{I}(\bm{\theta}|\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}}^{\text{ps}})\leq\dfrac{1}{t^{2}}\mathcal{I}(\bm{\theta}|\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}})\Big[1-\dfrac{1-t^{2}}{t^{2}}\,\bm{\delta}^{T}\mathcal{I}(\bm{\theta}|\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}})\,\bm{\delta}\,\Big]\,,

(78)

which shows that any
\bm{\delta}\neq 0
decreases the information amplification.

Substituting

C.2
Noise before postselection

When noise acts before postselection, the resulting noisy postselected state is

\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}}^{\text{n, ps}}=\dfrac{1}{P_{\bm{\theta}}^{\text{ps}}}\Big[(1-\epsilon)\hat{K}\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}}\hat{K}^{\dagger}+\dfrac{\epsilon}{d}\,\hat{K}\hat{K}^{\dagger}\Big]\,,

Appendix D
Filter Optimization

In this Appendix, we treat noisy postselection explicitly, for some small values of
u
and
d

u=d=2

Since most of our states are non-normalized, it is first useful to explicitly deal with normalization. Consider the case of a single parameter:

\hat{\rho}_{\theta}=\dfrac{\hat{\rho}^{\prime}_{\theta}}{P_{\theta}^{\text{ps}}},

(125)

\hat{\Lambda}

\hat{\Lambda}=\hat{\Lambda}^{\prime}-\dfrac{\partial P_{\theta}^{\text{ps}}}{P_{\theta}^{\text{ps}}}\hat{1}.

(127)

It is then easy to see that

\dfrac{1}{2}\{\hat{\Lambda}^{\prime},\hat{\rho}^{\prime}_{\theta}\}=\partial\hat{\rho}^{\prime}_{\theta}.

(128)

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(\theta|\hat{\rho}_{\theta})

\displaystyle=\Tr(\partial\nr\nsld),

(129)

(130)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{P_{\theta}^{\text{ps}}}\left[\mathcal{I}(\theta|\hat{\rho}^{\prime}_{\theta})-\dfrac{\partial P_{\theta}^{\text{ps}}}{P_{\theta}^{\text{ps}}}\Tr(\nnr\nnsld)-\dfrac{\partial P_{\theta}^{\text{ps}}}{P_{\theta}^{\text{ps}}}\Tr(\partial\nnr)+\dfrac{(\partial P_{\theta}^{\text{ps}})^{2}}{P_{\theta}^{\text{ps}}}\right],

(131)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{P_{\theta}^{\text{ps}}}\left[\mathcal{I}(\theta|\hat{\rho}^{\prime}_{\theta})-\dfrac{(\Tr[\partial\hat{\rho}^{\prime}_{\theta}])^{2}}{P_{\theta}^{\text{ps}}}\right].

(132)

\ket{\partial\psi_{\bm{\theta}}}=ix\ket{\psi_{\bm{\theta}}}+\alpha\ket{\psi_{\perp}},

(133)

where
\ket{\psi_{\bm{\theta}}},\ket{\psi_{\perp}}
are an orthonormal basis of our Hilbert space
2
2

2

x
must be purely real.
. Choosing a Filter corresponds to picking a 2x2 matrix (with respect to the aforementioned basis)
\hat{K}
such that

0\leq\hat{K}^{\dagger}\hat{K}\leq\hat{1}

(134)

If one attempts to optimize directly over 2x2 matrices, constraint (
134
) is highly non-trivial, so we first parameterize
\hat{K}
to simplify (
134
).

Suppose that we have some choice of filter
\hat{K}
, we can write it in its singular value decomposition
\hat{K}=\hat{V}\hat{D}\hat{W}
, where
\hat{D}
is diagonal with non-negative real eigenvalues,
\hat{W}
is an SU(2) matrix and
\hat{V}
is a U(2) matrix. Let
\hat{K}^{\prime}=\hat{V}^{\dagger}\,\hat{K}
, then the postselected state is changed by a unitary and thus the QFI and postselection probabilities do not change. Therefore
\hat{K}^{\prime}
has exactly the same performance as
\hat{K}
and it is sufficient to consider filters of the form
\hat{D}\hat{W}
. Note that
\hat{K}^{\dagger}\hat{K}=\hat{W}^{\dagger}\hat{D}^{2}\hat{W}
and constraint (
134
) is equivalent to
0\leq\hat{D}\leq\hat{1}
. Using the general form of an SU(2) unitary, our filter is thus parameterised by

\hat{D}=\begin{pmatrix}a&0\\
0&b\end{pmatrix},\text{ where }0\leq a,b\leq 1,\quad\hat{W}=\begin{pmatrix}\gamma&-\beta^{*}\\
\beta&\gamma^{*}\end{pmatrix},\text{ where }\gamma,\beta\in\mathbb{C},\,|\gamma|^{2}+|\beta|^{2}=1\,,

(135)

and
\hat{K}=\hat{D}\hat{W}
. The postselected state is then given by

\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}}^{\text{n, ps}}=\dfrac{(1-\epsilon)\hat{D}\hat{W}\ket{\psi_{\theta}}\bra{\psi_{\theta}}\hat{W}^{\dagger}\hat{D}+\dfrac{\epsilon}{2}\hat{D}^{2}}{P_{\bm{\theta}}^{\text{ps}}}:=\dfrac{\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}}^{\prime}}{P_{\bm{\theta}}^{\text{ps}}}\,,

(136)

where
P_{\bm{\theta}}^{\text{ps}}=\Tr(\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}}')
is the probability of postselection.
One can calculate

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}a^{2}\epsilon+2(1-\epsilon)|\gamma|^{2}&2ab(1-\epsilon)\gamma\beta^{*}\\
2ab(1-\epsilon)\gamma^{*}\beta&b^{2}\epsilon+2(1-\epsilon)|\beta|^{2}\end{pmatrix}

(137)

\displaystyle\partial\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=(1-\epsilon)\begin{pmatrix}-a^{2}(\alpha\gamma^{*}\beta^{*}+\alpha^{*}\gamma\beta)&ab(\alpha^{*}\gamma^{2}-\alpha(\beta^{*})^{2})\\
ab(\alpha(\gamma^{*})^{2}-\alpha^{*}\beta^{2})&b^{2}(\alpha\gamma^{*}\beta^{*}+\alpha^{*}\gamma\beta)\end{pmatrix}\,.

(138)

We thus find

P_{\bm{\theta}}^{\text{ps}}=\Tr(\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}}')=\frac{1}{2}\left[(a^{2}+b^{2})\epsilon+2(1-\epsilon)(a^{2}|\gamma|^{2}+b^{2}|\beta|^{2})\right]\,.

(139)

By finding the symmetric logarithmic derivative, we use Eq. (
132
) to calculate

\mathcal{I}(\bm{\theta}|\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}}^{\text{n, ps}})=\frac{4a^{2}b^{2}(1-\epsilon)^{2}|\alpha|^{2}}{({P_{\bm{\theta}}^{\text{ps}}})^{2}}\,,

(140)

Recall that we wish to maximize
\mathcal{I}(\bm{\theta}|\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}}^{\text{n, ps}})
, whilst minimizing
P_{\bm{\theta}}^{\text{ps}}
. Consider the optimal values of
\gamma,\beta
for fixed values of
a,b
. Since
\gamma,\beta
only appear in
P_{\bm{\theta}}^{\text{ps}}
both of our goals are achieved by minimizing
P_{\bm{\theta}}^{\text{ps}}
subject to
|\gamma|^{2}+|\beta|^{2}=1
. This is equivalent to minimizing

f_{a,b}(\gamma)=a^{2}|\gamma|^{2}+b^{2}(1-|\gamma|^{2}).

(141)

If
a\geq b
this is clearly minimized for
|\gamma|=0
, otherwise if
a<b
it is clearly minimized for
|\gamma|=1
. Let

\hat{\Omega}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
-1&0\end{pmatrix}.

(142)

Then we can see that the best possible filter performance is attained by a filter with

\hat{W}=\begin{cases}\hat{1}_{2},\quad\text{for }a<b,\\
\hat{\Omega},\quad\text{for }a\geq b.\end{cases}

(143)

If
W=\hat{1}_{2}
then
\hat{K}
(and hence
\hat{F}
) are both diagonal. Otherwise note that
\hat{\Omega}^{T}\hat{K}
is a diagonal filter with the same filter performance. Hence in either case the best filter performance is attained by a diagonal filter.

Now consider the full multiparameter QFIM. Since
d=2
, we can express any state as a linear combination of
\{\ket{\psi_{\bm{\theta}}},\ket{\psi_{\perp}}\}
and thus the expansion in Eq. (
133
) will hold for any parameter derivative (for different values of
x
and
\alpha
). The optimum scaling for the diagonal terms of the QFIM is then achieved by a diagonal filter (as seen above) and thus if this filter also scales the off diagonal terms then it will be optimal. Indeed in Appendix
C.2
we show such a diagonal filter does scale the off diagonal terms, and thus the optimal filter in the multiparameter case is also diagonal.

D.2
Non-diagonal filters are better in u=2, d=3

For notational brevity, we consider the case of a single parameter
\theta
. We have some filter (with Kraus operator)
\hat{K}
, a pure state
\ket{\psi_{\theta}}
and a depolarizing noise rate
\epsilon
. For brevity, we will set
\epsilon=1/2
so that it can be absorbed into the normalisation constant. We then find

\hat{\rho}^{\prime}_{\theta}=\hat{K}\outerproduct{\psi_\theta}{\psi_\theta}\hat{K}^{\dagger}+\hat{K}\hat{K}^{\dagger}/d.

(144)

Fix some
\theta
, then we can decompose

\ket{\partial\psi_{\theta}}=ix\ket{\psi_{\theta}}+\alpha\ket{\psi_{\perp}},

(145)

where
\ket{\psi_{\theta}},\ket{\psi_{\perp}}
are orthonormal. Assume our Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
is 3-dimensional; the minimal dimension such that
\mathcal{H}
is not spanned by
\ket{\psi_{\theta}},\ket{\psi_{\perp}}
. Thus we can find a third normalised state
\ket{n}
orthogonal to
\ket{\psi_{\theta}}
and
\ket{\psi_{\perp}}
. Consider the representation of
\hat{K}
in this basis. Take the canonical choice
(t-1)\outerproduct{\psi_\theta}{\psi_\theta}+\hat{\Pi}_{u}
from the noiseless case, with the addition of a single off-diagonal term
b\in\mathbb{R}

\hat{K}=\begin{pmatrix}t&0&b\\
0&1&0\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix}.

(146)

We can calculate

\hat{F}=\hat{K}^{\dagger}\hat{K}=\begin{pmatrix}t^{2}&0&tb\\
0&1&0\\
tb&0&b^{2}\end{pmatrix}.

(147)

Note
\hat{F}
has eigenvalues
0,1,t^{2}+b^{2}
. Thus as long as
0\leq b\leq\sqrt{1-t^{2}}
, we have
0\leq\hat{F}\leq\hat{1}
as required. We then find

\hat{\rho}^{\prime}_{\theta}=\begin{pmatrix}(4/3)t^{2}&0&tb/3\\
0&1/3&0\\
tb/3&0&b^{2}/3\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{and}\quad\partial\hat{\rho}^{\prime}_{\theta}=\begin{pmatrix}0&\alpha^{*}t&0\\
\alpha t&0&0\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix}.

(148)

One can explicitly solve for
\hat{\Lambda}^{\prime}
:

\hat{\Lambda}^{\prime}=\begin{pmatrix}0&\dfrac{6(1+b^{2})t\alpha^{*}}{1+b^{2}+(4+3b^{2})t^{2}}&0\\
\dfrac{6(1+b^{2})t\alpha}{1+b^{2}+(4+3b^{2})t^{2}}&0&-\dfrac{6bt^{2}\alpha}{1+b^{2}+(4+3b^{2})t^{2}}\\
0&-\dfrac{6bt^{2}\alpha^{*}}{1+b^{2}+(4+3b^{2})t^{2}}&0\end{pmatrix}

(149)

Giving a quantum fisher information of

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(\theta|\hat{\rho}^{\prime}_{\theta})

\displaystyle=\dfrac{12|\alpha|^{2}(1+b^{2})t^{2}}{1+b^{2}+(4+3b^{2})t^{2}},

(150)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{12|\alpha|^{2}t^{2}}{1+\left(3+\dfrac{1}{1+b^{2}}\right)t^{2}},

(151)

\displaystyle=P_{\bm{\theta}}^{\text{ps}}\mathcal{I}(\theta|\hat{\rho}_{\theta})

(152)

using Eq. (
132
) and noting
\partial\hat{\rho}_{\theta}^{\prime}
is traceless. The information rate is manifestly increasing in
|b|
and thus it is maximized by setting
b=\sqrt{1-t^{2}}
, i.e. maximal mixing.

We can compare this with the canonical choice in the noiseless case:
(t-1)\outerproduct{\psi_\theta}{\psi_\theta}+\hat{1}
, which is diagonal in our basis. Consider a slightly more general filter
\hat{K}^{\prime}
, which we take to be diagonal:

\hat{K}^{\prime}=\begin{pmatrix}t&0&0\\
0&1&0\\
0&0&r\end{pmatrix},

(153)

where

controls the probability of transmitting an
\ket{n}
state (which can only come from noise).

In this case one can similarly calculate the information rate

P_{\bm{\theta}}^{\text{ps}}\mathcal{I}(\theta|\hat{\rho}_{\theta})=\dfrac{12|\alpha|^{2}t^{2}}{1+4t^{2}},

(154)

which is found to be independent of
r
.

Comparing equations (
150
) and (
154
), we see that any non-zero value of
b
increases the information rate. Thus mixing is beneficial and the optimal filter in the noiseless case is not optimal in the noisy case.

Appendix E
Optimal filter for detector saturation

In this Appendix we find the optimal values of
B
and
p_{\bm{\theta}}
for when detector saturation is the limiting factor. We recall from the main text [Eq. (
26
)] that we are attempting to solve the following optimization problem

maximise:

\displaystyle\eta(p_{\bm{\theta}},B)=\dfrac{\Big(1-\epsilon+2\dfrac{\epsilon}{d}\Big)p_{\bm{\theta}}}{\displaystyle\Big(1-\epsilon+\dfrac{\epsilon}{d}\Big)\frac{p_{\bm{\theta}}}{B}+\dfrac{\epsilon}{d}}\,,

(155)

subject to:

\displaystyle 0\leq p_{\bm{\theta}},B\leq 1,

\displaystyle(1-\epsilon+\frac{\epsilon}{d})p_{\bm{\theta}}+\frac{\epsilon}{d}(u-1)B\leq P_{\text{max}}.

For notational brevity, we define
x=p_{\bm{\theta}},y=B,b=(1-\epsilon+\frac{\epsilon}{d}),c=\frac{\epsilon}{d}(u-1),g=\frac{\epsilon}{d}
. In this notation, we see that the optimization problem (
155
) is equivalent to

maximise:

\displaystyle f(x,y)=\frac{(b+g)x}{b\dfrac{x}{y}+g}\,,

(156)

subject to:

\displaystyle 0\leq x,y\leq 1,

\displaystyle bx+cy\leq P_{\text{max}}.

We note that
f(x,y)
is a strictly increasing function of
x
and
y
(for
x,y\geq 0
) and thus the optimum point will be on the boundary of the feasible set. We deduce that if
(1,1)
is in the feasible set it will be the optimum point. This occurs iff.
b+c\leq P_{\text{max}}
.

Hence, we consider
b+c>P_{\text{max}}
, in which case the feasible set is the intersection of the unit square with a line of negative gradient, as depicted in Fig.
5
.

:

156
) as a subset of the unit square. The linear constraint intersects the unit square at
(1,y_{*})
and
(x_{*},1)
.

Since
f
is strictly increasing in the
x
and
y
directions, its maximum will be obtained on the linear constraint, i.e. it will satisfy
bx+cy=P_{\text{max}}
. As before, we introduce
t^{2}=x/y
, so that

x(t)=\dfrac{P_{\text{max}}}{b+c/t^{2}},\quad y(t)=\dfrac{P_{\text{max}}}{bt^{2}+c}.

(157)

We note that
f(x(t),y(t))=P_{\text{max}}\,\mathcal{A}(t)
, where
\mathcal{A}(t)
is the information amplification given by Eq. (
IV.2.1
):

\mathcal{A}(t)=\dfrac{(b+g)t^{2}}{(bt^{2}+c)(bt^{2}+g)}\,.

(158)

We thus wish to maximize
\mathcal{A}(t)
over the possible values of
t
. Let
x_{*}
be the smallest value of
x
attained on the line
bx+cy=P_{\text{max}}
whilst
0\leq x,y\leq 1
. If the line intersects the unit square on the boundary
y=1
, then
x_{*}>0
. Otherwise, the line intersects the square on the boundary
x=0
and
x_{*}=0
. We define
y_{*}
similarly. A graphical example is given in Fig.
5

t
is given by

x_{*}\leq t^{2}\leq 1/y_{*}.

(159)

Since
x_{*},y_{*}\leq 1

We find that
\mathcal{A}(t)\to 0
as

and
\mathcal{A}(t)
has a single stationary point, which therefore must be a maximum. As before, the maximum occurs at

, which can be bigger or smaller than 1, depending on

\epsilon

,

•

(160)

•

(161)

where

if

and
a^{+}=\infty
if
a<0
.

This solution is best described geometrically. Consider starting

at 1 and then decreasing it to zero. The maximum point of

then traces out a path

. If

until it reaches the point

. If

the behavior is mirrored, with the optimum point now moving along the y-axis to
(1,1/t_{\text{pp}}^{2})

Appendix F
Noisy information amplification scaling for the JAL filter

In this Appendix we show that, in the case of noisy postselection, the information amplification for the JAL filter is unchanged to first order in
\bm{\delta}
.

Referring back to the Taylor expansion in Appendix
B
, we now keep only the terms to first order in
\bm{\delta}
:

\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}=\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}}+i[\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}},\hat{D}]+\mathcal{O}(|\bm{\delta}|^{2})\,,

(162)

where
\hat{D}=-i[\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\hat{U}(\bm{\theta})]^{\text{T}}\bm{\delta}U(\bm{\theta})^{\dagger}
. The filter
\hat{F}
can then be written as

(163)

Denote the unperturbed eigenvectors (when
\bm{\delta}=0
) by
\ket{\lambda_{1}}=\hat{K}\ket{\psi_{\bm{\theta}}}/t
and
\ket{\lambda_{n}}=\hat{K}\ket{{\psi_{\bm{\theta}}}^{\perp,n}}
(for
2\leq n\leq d
),with corresponding eigenvalues

\lambda_{1}=\dfrac{t^{2}}{P_{\bm{\theta}}^{\text{ps}}}\Big(1-\epsilon+\dfrac{\epsilon}{d}\Big)\,,\hskip 28.45274pt\lambda_{n}=\dfrac{\epsilon}{dP_{\bm{\theta}}^{\text{ps}}}\hskip 8.5359pt\text{for}\hskip 8.5359ptn\geq 2\,,

(164)

We denote the true, perturbed eigenvectors and eigenvalues by
\ket{\lambda_{n}^{\prime}}
,
\lambda_{n}^{\prime}
. In the following derivation, we will find the leading order corrections in
\bm{\delta}
of the perturbed eigenvectors and eigenvalues. Using these corrections, we will then calculated the perturbed QFIM and show it is unchanged to order
\bm{\delta}
.

Looking at the action of
\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}}^{n,\text{ps}}
on
\ket{\lambda_{1}}=\hat{K}\ket{\psi_{\bm{\theta}}}/t
, we see that

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}}^{\text{n, ps}}\ket{\lambda_{1}}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{P_{\bm{\theta}}^{\text{ps}}}\Big[(1-\epsilon)\hat{K}\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}}\hat{K}^{\dagger}+\dfrac{\epsilon}{d}\hat{F}^{\dagger}\Big]\hat{K}\dfrac{\ket{\psi_{\bm{\theta}}}}{t},

(165)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{P_{\bm{\theta}}^{\text{ps}}}\Big[(1-\epsilon)\dfrac{\hat{K}\ket{\psi_{\bm{\theta}}}}{t}\braket{\psi_{\bm{\theta}}|\hat{F}|\psi_{\bm{\theta}}}+\dfrac{\epsilon}{d}\dfrac{\hat{K}\ket{\psi_{\bm{\theta}}}}{t}\braket{\psi_{\bm{\theta}}|\hat{F}|\psi_{\bm{\theta}}}+\dfrac{\epsilon}{d}\sum_{n}\dfrac{\hat{K}\ket{{\psi_{\bm{\theta}}}^{\perp,n}}}{t}\braket{{\psi_{\bm{\theta}}}^{\perp,n}|\hat{F}|\psi_{\bm{\theta}}}\Big],

(166)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{P_{\bm{\theta}}^{\text{ps}}}\Big[(1-\epsilon)t^{2}\ket{\lambda_{1}}+\dfrac{\epsilon}{d}t^{2}\ket{\lambda_{1}}+i\sum_{n}\dfrac{\epsilon}{d\,t}(t^{2}-1)\braket{{\psi_{\bm{\theta}}}^{\perp,n}|[\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}},\hat{D}]|\psi_{\bm{\theta}}}\ket{\lambda_{n}}\Big]+\mathcal{O}(\bm{\delta}^{2}),

(167)

(168)

Carrying out the same calculation for
\ket{\lambda_{n}}=\hat{K}\ket{{\psi_{\bm{\theta}}}^{\perp,n}}

\displaystyle\hskip-11.38092pt\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}}^{\text{n, ps}}\ket{\lambda_{n}}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{P_{\bm{\theta}}^{\text{ps}}}\Big[(1-\epsilon)\hat{K}\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\theta}}\hat{K}^{\dagger}+\dfrac{\epsilon}{d}\hat{F}^{\dagger}\Big]\hat{K}\ket{{\psi_{\bm{\theta}}}^{\perp,n}},

(169)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{P_{\bm{\theta}}^{\text{ps}}}\Big[(1-\epsilon)\dfrac{\hat{K}\ket{\psi_{\bm{\theta}}}}{t}t\braket{\psi_{\bm{\theta}}|\hat{F}|{\psi_{\bm{\theta}}}^{\perp,n}}+\dfrac{\epsilon}{d}\dfrac{\hat{K}\ket{\psi_{\bm{\theta}}}}{t}t\braket{\psi_{\bm{\theta}}|\hat{F}|{\psi_{\bm{\theta}}}^{\perp,n}}+\dfrac{\epsilon}{d}\sum_{m}\hat{K}\ket{{\psi_{\bm{\theta}}}^{\perp,m}}\braket{{\psi_{\bm{\theta}}}^{\perp,m}|\hat{F}|{\psi_{\bm{\theta}}}^{\perp,n}}\Big],

(170)

\displaystyle=i\dfrac{t}{P_{\bm{\theta}}^{\text{ps}}}\Big(1-\epsilon+\dfrac{\epsilon}{d}\Big)(t^{2}-1)\braket{\psi_{\bm{\theta}}|\hat{D}|{\psi_{\bm{\theta}}}^{\perp,n}}\ket{\lambda_{1}}+\dfrac{\epsilon}{dP_{\bm{\theta}}^{\text{ps}}}\ket{\lambda_{n}}+\mathcal{O}(\bm{\delta}^{2})\,.
