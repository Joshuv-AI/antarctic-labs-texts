Introduction

In this paper we shall generalize some results from long-established function theory of the unit disc to holomorphic functions of bfd-norm at most one on an elliptical domain.
Recall that the

\mathcal{S}

The notions of models and realizations of functions are useful for the understanding of the Schur class.
A

A closely related notion is a

\varphi(\lambda)=\alpha+\langle\lambda(1-D\lambda)^{-1}\gamma,\beta\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}\qquad\text{for all}\ \lambda\in\mathbb{D},

\begin{bmatrix}\alpha&1\otimes\beta\\

Remark 2.40
.

The Waring-Lagrange theorem: Any symmetric polynomial in
d
commuting variables can be expressed as a polynomial in the
d
elementary symmetric functions in the variables.

In an interesting historical survey
[
14
, pp. 364-365]
, H. G. Funkhauser attributes this fundamental fact about symmetric functions to Edward Waring, Lucasian Professor at Cambridge
[
20
]
in 1770 and independently to Joseph Lagrange
[
15
]
in 1798. Lagrange’s account is later, but is more explicit, so we shall call the statement the
Waring-Lagrange theorem
. It is likely that Euler had the result around the same time.

Although we have stated the Waring-Lagrange theorem only for symmetric polynomials, we actually invoke it for symmetric holomorphic functions on a symmetric open set. It is not difficult to deduce the result for symmetric holomorphic functions from the statement for polynomials, with the aid of the fact that a symmetric holomorphic function on a symmetric open set
U
can be approximated locally uniformly on
U
by symmetric polynomials.

In this section we show that the models described in Theorem

can also be expressed in slightly different forms. For this purpose we shall need some properties of the functions
\Phi_{\omega}:G\to\mathbb{C}
, where
\omega\in\mathbb{T}
, given by

\Phi_{\omega}(s,p)=\frac{2\omega p-s}{2-\omega s}\quad\text{for}\ (s,p)\in G.

By
[
6

,
\Phi_{\omega}(G)\subset\mathbb{D}
for any
\omega\in\mathbb{T}
. Note that, for any
\delta\in(0,1)
and
\lambda\in G_{\delta}

(\lambda,\delta)\in G
, and so we may define
\Psi_{\omega}:G_{\delta}\to\mathbb{D}
by

\Psi_{\omega}(\lambda)=\Phi_{\omega}(\lambda,\delta)\;\text{for}\;\lambda\in G_{\delta}.

(4.1)

The functions
\Psi_{\omega}
so defined are characterized by their mapping properties relative to the elliptical region
G_{\delta}
, as described in Proposition
4.2
below. To prove this proposition we
shall make use of the Žukovskii mapping

\check{Z}(z)=\frac{1}{z}+\delta z

which maps the annulus

A_{\delta}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}\{z\in\mathbb{C}:1<|z|<1/\delta\}

in a 2-to-1 manner to the elliptic region
G_{\delta}
, except for the two points
\pm 2\sqrt{\delta}\in G_{\delta}

\partial G_{\delta}
. The preimages in
A_{\delta}
under
\check{Z}
of the two foci are the singleton sets
\{\frac{1}{\sqrt{\delta}}

\{-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\delta}}

G_{\delta}

A_{\delta}

For any point
\omega\in\mathbb{T}

H_{\omega}
the closed supporting halfplane to the elliptical set
G_{\delta}
at the boundary point
\check{Z}(\omega)
and by
\ell_{\omega}
the tangent line to
G_{\delta}
at
\check{Z}(\omega)

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
0<\delta<1
and let
\omega\in\mathbb{T}
. The function
\Psi_{\omega}
defined in equation (
4.1
) is the unique linear fractional mapping that maps
H_{\omega}
onto
\mathbb{D}^{-}
, maps
0
to
\omega\delta
and maps
\check{Z}(\omega)
to
-\omega

Proof.

The function
\check{Z}
maps both bounding circles of
A_{\delta}
bijectively onto the ellipse
\partial G_{\delta}
, and so determines a parametrization of
\partial G_{\delta}

\check{Z}(e^{i\theta})=e^{-i\theta}+\delta e^{i\theta}=(1+\delta)\cos\theta-i(1-\delta)\sin\theta

for

. Note that as
\theta
increases from
0

\check{Z}(e^{i\theta})
describes the ellipse
\partial G_{\delta}
in a clockwise sense, that is, in a negative orientation. Hence

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\theta}\check{Z}(e^{i\theta})=ie^{i\theta}\check{Z}^{\prime}(e^{i\theta})=ie^{i\theta}(-e^{-2i\theta}+\delta)

is a tangent vector to the ellipse
\partial G_{\delta}
at the point
\check{Z}(e^{i\theta})
pointing in the negative orientation. It follows that an inward-pointing normal to the ellipse at the point

H_{\omega}
is bounded by the tangent line
\ell_{\omega}
to
G_{\delta}
at
\check{Z}(\omega)=\bar{\omega}+\delta\omega

H_{\omega}
, has direction
-i\bar{\omega}+i\delta\omega

\ell_{\omega}=\{\check{Z}(\omega)+ti(-\bar{\omega}+\delta\omega):t\in\mathbb{R}\}=\{\bar{\omega}+\delta\omega+ti(-\bar{\omega}+\delta\omega):t\in\mathbb{R}\}.

Thus
z\in\mathbb{C}

z=\bar{\omega}+\delta\omega+ti(-\bar{\omega}+\delta\omega)\ \text{for some}\ t\in\mathbb{R}.

(4.3)

\omega\in\mathbb{T}

(4.4)

Thus the linear fractional mapping
\Psi_{\omega}
maps
\ell_{\omega}

to the unit circle and the set
G_{\delta}
to
\mathbb{D}
. Hence it maps the closed

H_{\omega}
onto
\mathbb{D}^{-}
.

It is easy to check that
\Psi_{\omega}

maps
0
to
\omega\delta

\check{Z}(\omega)=\bar{\omega}+\omega\delta

Thus
\Psi_{\omega}
has the claimed mapping properties.
Furthermore, there is a
unique
linear fractional map of the plane that maps a prescribed line to a prescribed circle and satisfies interpolation conditions at two points. Hence
\Psi_{\omega}
is the unique linear fractional mapping that maps
H_{\omega}
onto
\mathbb{D}^{-}
, maps
0
to
\omega\delta
and maps
\check{Z}(\omega)
to
-\omega
.
