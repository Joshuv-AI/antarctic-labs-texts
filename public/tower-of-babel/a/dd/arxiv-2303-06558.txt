Main Result

This contribution is concerned with mathematical properties of the standard Gaussian kernel defined via the geodesic distance on Riemannian manifolds. Our main result is as follows:

Main Theorem
.

Let
{\mathcal{M}}
be any compact, non-simply-connected
{\mathscr{C}}^{\infty}
-manifold without boundary. For any Riemannian metric
g
on
{\mathcal{M}}
and any parameter
\lambda>0
, the Gaussian kernel
e^{-\lambda d_{g}^{2}(\bullet,\bullet)}:{\mathcal{M}}\times{\mathcal{M}}\to[0,\infty[
cannot be positive definite. Here
d_{g}
is the geodesic distance on
({\mathcal{M}},g)
.

The proof relies crucially on the recent nice work
[
9
]
by Da Costa–Mostajeran–Ortega, which proves the above theorem for
{\mathcal{M}}
= the round circle
\varrho{\mathbf{S}}^{1}
for any
\varrho>0
, and hence for spheres, real projective spaces, and real Grassmannians, etc. The arguments in
[
9
]
combined with classical calculus of variations techniques on manifolds will enable us to conclude the theorem.

For applications in machine learning (

Definition 1.1

k:(X,d)\times(X,d)\to\mathbb{R}

{\mathcal{G}}=\left\{{\mathcal{G}}_{ij}\right\}\in{\rm Mat}(N\times N;\mathbb{R})

This notion of positive definiteness of kernel functions was proposed by Mathias and Bochner. See

Positive definite kernels are also of considerable theoretical interest in functional analysis. A classical theorem of Schoenberg (

One thus finds that

contains copies of

Definition 1.2
.

Let

and
(X^{\prime},d^{\prime})
be metric spaces. A map

is said to be an isometric embedding if

For complete Riemannian manifolds

and

{\mathscr{C}}^{\infty}
-manifolds, and
\iota:\left({\mathcal{M}},d_{g}\right)\to\left({\mathcal{M}},d_{g^{\prime}}\right)
is an isometric embedding of metric spaces.

Remark 1.3

For Riemannian manifolds, an isometric embedding in the sense of metric spaces is very different from that in the sense of differential geometry. See Appendix
A

Non-positive definiteness of Gaussian kernel when

In this section we prove our main theorem, which is stated in §

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
{\mathcal{M}}
be an arbitrary closed (
i.e.
, compact and boundaryless) differentiable manifold such that
\pi_{1}({\mathcal{M}})\neq\{0\}

on
{\mathcal{M}}
and any parameter

e^{-\lambda d_{g}^{2}(\bullet,\bullet)}:{\mathcal{M}}\times{\mathcal{M}}\to[0,\infty[

That is, we prove for non-simply-connected closed manifolds

\displaystyle\Lambda_{+}({\mathcal{M}},g):=\left\{\lambda\in[0,\infty[:\,\text{$e^{-\lambda d_{g}^{2}(\bullet,\bullet)}:{\mathcal{M}}\times{\mathcal{M}}\to[0,\infty[$ is positive definite}\right\}=\emptyset.

The key to the proof is the following linear algebraic lemma, established by Da Costa–Mostajeran–Ortega

, pp.5–7]
by explicit computations:

Lemma 2.2

(See
[
9
]
)

Given any
\lambda>0
.
The circulant matrix

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}=\begin{bmatrix}1&\exp\left(-\lambda\left(\frac{2\pi}{N}\right)^{2}\right)&\exp\left(-\lambda\left(\frac{4\pi}{N}\right)^{2}\right)&\cdots&\exp\left(-\lambda\left(\frac{2\pi}{N}\right)^{2}\right)\\
\exp\left(-\lambda\left(\frac{2\pi}{N}\right)^{2}\right)&1&\cdots&\cdots&\exp\left(-\lambda\left(\frac{4\pi}{N}\right)^{2}\right)\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\exp\left(-\lambda\left(\frac{2\pi}{N}\right)^{2}\right)&\exp\left(-\lambda\left(\frac{4\pi}{N}\right)^{2}\right)&\exp\left(-\lambda\left(\frac{6\pi}{N}\right)^{2}\right)&\cdots&1\end{bmatrix}

has a negative eigenvalue for some sufficiently large natural number

2.2

to prove that the Gaussian kernel is never positive definite on any manifold admitting a metric space isometric embedding of the round circle

Proof of Theorem
2.1
.

Let
{\mathcal{M}}
be a closed, non-simply-connected
{\mathscr{C}}^{\infty}
-manifold. Fix a Riemannian metric
g
on
{\mathcal{M}}
and a positive number
\lambda
. Our arguments rely essentially on the classical theorem
à la
É. Cartan (
[
6
]
; see also do Carmo
[
5
, Theorem 12.2.2]
):

Theorem
.

For any Riemannian manifold
({\mathcal{M}},g)
with
\pi_{1}({\mathcal{M}})\neq\{0\}
, in each nontrivial
1
-homotopy class there is a
shortest
closed geodesic.

We want to pick “the shortest of all such shortest closed geodesics”. Note, however, that there may be infinitely many such geodesics, as
\pi_{1}({\mathcal{M}})
is only known to be finitely generated (but not finite) in general. To avoid complications due to non-uniqueness of generators, we resort to the following arguments, which allow us to select the “almost” shortest nontrivial closed geodesic on
({\mathcal{M}},g)
.

For any (piecewise differentiable) loop
\gamma
, we write

\displaystyle\Omega_{[\gamma]}:=\text{ the free homotopy class of loops containing $\gamma$.}

Then set

\displaystyle\kappa_{0}:=\inf\left\{{\rm Length}[\gamma]:\,\text{$\gamma$ is a shortest closed geodesic in $\Omega_{[\gamma]}$, with }\Omega_{[\gamma]}\neq\{0\}\text{ in $\pi_{1}({\mathcal{M}})$}\right\}.

The infimum is taken over all closed geodesics
\gamma
and then over all free homotopy classes
\Omega_{[\gamma]}
.

First, we claim that

\displaystyle\kappa_{0}>0.

To see this, if
\kappa_{0}=0
then there would be a closed geodesic
\gamma^{\prime}
, which is arbitrarily short, with
\Omega_{[\gamma^{\prime}]}\neq\{0\}
. But as
{\mathcal{M}}
is a compact Riemannian manifold, there is a positive number
\eta_{0}=\eta_{0}({\mathcal{M}})
such that any closed curve with length less than
\eta_{0}
must be null homotopic. Indeed, one may pick a finite open covering on
{\mathcal{M}}
consisting of geodesic normal charts, and take
\eta_{0}
to be the Lebesgue number of this covering. This leads to contradiction.

For an
\varepsilon\in\left]0,\frac{\kappa_{0}}{100}\right]
to be specified later, we pick
\gamma_{\star}
, a shortest closed geodesic in its own free homotopy class (which is non-trivial), such that

\displaystyle{\rm Length}\left[\gamma_{\star}\right]\leq\kappa_{0}+\varepsilon.

Then, for some big number
N\in\mathbb{N}
to be specified later, we pick
N
points
\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}\}\subset\gamma_{\star}
equidistributed with respect to the metric-induced distance
d_{g}

restricted to
\gamma_{\star}
. In formula, for any
1\leq j<i\leq N
we require that

\left[d_{g}\big|_{\gamma_{\star}}\right]\left(x_{i},x_{j}\right):=\begin{cases}\frac{2\pi}{N}(i-j)\qquad\text{ if }i-j\leq\frac{N}{2},\\
\frac{2\pi}{N}(j+N-i)\qquad\text{ if }i-j>\frac{N}{2}.\end{cases}

In other words,
\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}\}
on
\gamma_{\star}
satisfy the same distance relations — when considered with respect to
d_{g}
on
\gamma_{\star}
— as for
N
equidistributed points on the round circle
{\mathbf{S}}^{1}
. This can be done as
\gamma_{\star}
is diffeomorphic to
{\mathbf{S}}^{1}
by construction; in particular, the shortest condition implies the simplicity or primality of
\gamma_{\star}
.

To proceed, we claim that the distance relations of these
N
points are almost the same when considered with respect to
d_{g}
on the whole manifold
{\mathcal{M}}
. More precisely:

(
\spadesuit
)

0\leq\left[d_{g}\big|_{\gamma_{\star}}\right]\left(x_{i},x_{j}\right)-\left[d_{g}\big|_{{\mathcal{M}}}\right]\left(x_{i},x_{j}\right)\leq\varepsilon\qquad\text{for any }1\leq j<i\leq N.

Proof of Claim (
\spadesuit
).

The non-negativity of
\left[d_{g}\big|_{\gamma_{\star}}\right]\left(x_{i},x_{j}\right)-\left[d_{g}\big|_{{\mathcal{M}}}\right]\left(x_{i},x_{j}\right)
is clear, as distances are defined by taking infima.

To prove the other inequality, suppose for some
i,j
that the difference where greater than
\varepsilon
. Then there would be a curve
\sigma
(
s
igma for “
s
hort-cut”) with endpoints
x_{i}
,
x_{j}
whose concatenation with
\gamma_{\star}^{+}
is a piecewise-
{\mathscr{C}}^{\infty}
loop satisfying

\displaystyle{\rm Length}\left[\sigma\,\#\,\gamma^{+}_{\star}\right]<{\rm Length}\left[\gamma_{\star}\right]-\varepsilon.

Here and hereafter,
\gamma_{\star}^{+}
denotes the longer of the two geodesic segments on
\gamma_{\star}
with endpoints
x_{i}
and
x_{j}
, and
\gamma_{\star}^{-}
denotes the shorter one. (The choice is clear when the two segments have equal length, for which case we may safely omit the details.)

The above inequality implies

\displaystyle{\rm Length}\left[\sigma\,\#\,\gamma^{+}_{\star}\right]<\kappa_{0}.

Hence, the loop
\sigma\,\#\,\gamma^{+}_{\star}
must be null-homotopic, in view of the minimality condition in definition of
\kappa_{0}
. In this case, however, the loop
\sigma\,\#\,\gamma^{-}_{\star}
cannot be null-homotopic, for otherwise
\gamma_{\star}
must be null-homotopic, which can be seen by contracting

Therefore, from the shortness of

\displaystyle{\rm Length}\left[\sigma\,\#\,\gamma^{-}_{\star}\right]

\displaystyle<2\,{\rm Length}\left[\gamma^{-}_{\star}\right]-\varepsilon

\displaystyle\leq{\rm Length}\left[\gamma_{\star}^{+}\right]+{\rm Length}\left[\gamma_{\star}^{-}\right]-\varepsilon

\displaystyle={\rm Length}\left[\gamma_{\star}\right]-\varepsilon

\displaystyle\leq\kappa_{0}.

This contradicts the definition of
\kappa_{0}
. ∎

Now let us consider the Gram matrix

{\mathcal{G}}={\mathcal{G}}_{N}\in{\rm Mat}\left(N\times N;\mathbb{R}_{+}\right)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{G}}_{ij}=e^{-\lambda d_{g}^{2}(x_{i},x_{j})}\qquad\text{ for }i,j\in\{1,2,\ldots,N\},

The estimate (

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}=\begin{bmatrix}1&\exp\left(-\lambda\left(\frac{2\pi}{N}\right)^{2}\right)&\exp\left(-\lambda\left(\frac{4\pi}{N}\right)^{2}\right)&\cdots&\exp\left(-\lambda\left(\frac{2\pi}{N}\right)^{2}\right)\\

Let us comment on why the above proof fails for simply-connected Riemannian manifolds.

Although it remains true that there is a closed geodesic

3.
Remarks

In this paper, we have proved that for each parameter
\lambda>0
, the Gaussian kernel
e^{-\lambda d_{g}^{2}(\bullet,\bullet)}
on any non-simply-connected closed Riemannian manifold
({\mathcal{M}},g)
fails to be positive definite. Indeed, we establish —- relying crucially on the computations in

]
, especially Lemma

— the existence of a large number

such that for
N

{\mathcal{M}}
, the associated

for the Gaussian kernel is not positive semi-definite.

Nevertheless,
for any fixed
N

converges in the

-norm to the identity matrix as

. Hence, as the parameter
\lambda>0
increases, we need to take larger number
N\to\infty
of sample points to detect the failure of positive definiteness of the Gaussian kernel. See
[
9
, §5]
. We are thus led to propose the following

Conjecture/Question
.

On complete Riemannian manifolds the Gaussian kernel
e^{-\lambda d_{g}^{2}(\bullet,\bullet)}
is positive definite on coarser scales, while for compact manifolds of dimension
\geq 1
, positive definiteness is lost on finer scales. Formulate and prove a quantitative description of this phenomenon.

It should also be remarked (see
[
9
, §3]
) that one may construct finite metric spaces
{\mathcal{M}}^{0}
(which are
0
-dimensional manifolds) with complicated
\emptyset\varsubsetneq\Lambda_{+}\left({\mathcal{M}}^{0}\right)\varsubsetneq\,]0,\infty[
. Also, Sra
[
29
]
proved that for
X={\bf Sym}^{++}(n)
with S-divergence, it holds that
\Lambda_{+}(X)=\bigcup_{i=1}^{n-2}\left\{\frac{i}{2}\right\}\cup\left[\frac{n-1}{2},\infty\right[
.

A.
Two notions of isometric embeddings

An isometric embedding in the sense of metric spaces between Riemannian manifolds is defined in Definition
1.2
. Then, Remark
1.3
pointed out that this is very different from the usual (differential geometric) notion of isometric embedding of Riemannian manifolds. This appendix is devoted to elaborating on this issue. Recall that

Definition
.

Let
(X,d)
and
(X^{\prime},d^{\prime})
be metric spaces. A map
\iota:X\to X^{\prime}
is said to be an isometric embedding if
\iota
is one-to-one and
d^{\prime}\big(\iota(x),\iota(y)\big)=d(x,y)
for all
x,y\in X
.

Definition
.

Let
({\mathcal{M}},g)
and
({\mathcal{M}}^{\prime},g^{\prime})
be complete Riemannian manifolds. We say that
\iota:({\mathcal{M}},g)\hookrightarrow({\mathcal{M}}^{\prime},g^{\prime})
is a metric space isometric embedding if
\iota
is an embedding between
{\mathscr{C}}^{\infty}
-manifolds, and
\iota:\left({\mathcal{M}},d_{g}\right)\hookrightarrow\left({\mathcal{M}}^{\prime},d_{g^{\prime}}\right)
is a metric space isometric embedding.

The above definition is
non-local
in nature — instead of the pointwise coincidence of length of vectorfields in the differential geometric notion of isometric embedding, here one requires the coincidence of geodesic distance, which is defined via the length of curves.

In contrast, the differential geometric notion of isometric embeddings between Riemannian manifolds, as in the seminal works by Nash
[
23
,
24
]
(see the comprehensive survey
[
16
]
by Han–Hong), is as follows —
\iota:({\mathcal{M}},g)\hookrightarrow({\mathcal{M}}^{\prime},g^{\prime})
is an isometric embedding if
\iota
is an embedding of
{\mathscr{C}}^{\infty}
-manifolds and
g^{\prime}\big({\rm d}\iota(X),{\rm d}\iota(Y)\big)=g(X,Y)
for any vectorfields
X,Y
on
{\mathcal{M}}
. This is a
local
condition: it only requires the inner products induced by Riemannian metrics to coincide at each pair of points
\big(p,\iota(p)\big)\in{\mathcal{M}}\times{\mathcal{M}}^{\prime}
; or equivalently, the length of each pair of
\iota
-related vector
X\big|_{p}\in T_{p}{\mathcal{M}}
and
{\rm d}\iota(X)\big|_{\iota(p)}\in T_{\iota(p)}{\mathcal{M}}^{\prime}
to be identical, taken with respect to
g
and
g^{\prime}
.

It should be emphasised that these two notions of isometric embeddings between Riemannian manifolds are essentially distinctive. Consider the following simple example.

Example
.

The round circle
{\mathbf{S}}^{1}
is clearly isometric embeddable into the 2-dimensional Euclidean space in the sense of differential geometry, via the inclusion
\iota
of the unit circle in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. However,
\iota
is not an isometric embedding in the sense of metric spaces, since any pair of antipodal points
\{p_{+},p_{-}\}
on
{\mathbf{S}}^{1}
have intrinsic distance
\pi
, while their extrinsic distance (
i.e.
, with respect to the Euclidean distance on the ambient space) equals
2
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

One way to understand this phenomenon is that in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, the diameter segment between
p_{\pm}
serves as a “short-cut” relative to the geodesic distance on
{\mathbf{S}}^{1}
taken along the circumference.

More generally, by Nash’s theorem
[
24
]
, any Riemannian manifold
({\mathcal{M}},g)
can be isometrically embedded into some Euclidean space in the differential geometric sense. However, unless the manifold
({\mathcal{M}},g)
is itself Euclidean flat, such an isometric embedding would never be a metric space isometric embedding, as proved by Feragen–Lauze–Hauberg
[
12
]
.

Examples of Riemannian manifolds admitting metric space isometric embeddings of the round circle
\varrho{\mathbf{S}}^{1}
for some scaling factor
\varrho>0
include
{\mathbf{S}}^{n}
for
n\geq 1
,
\mathbb{R}{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
for
n>1
, and the real Grassmannians
{\bf Gr}(k,n)
for
1\leq k<n
. This, in fact, is how Theorem
1.4
was proved in
[
9
]
.

We conclude our clarification on the notions of isometric embeddings by the simple observation below, which sheds further lights to the examples above.

Theorem
.

Let
({\mathcal{M}},g)
and
({\mathcal{M}}^{\prime},g^{\prime})
be complete Riemannian manifolds.

(1)

If a submanifold
({\mathcal{M}},g)\subset({\mathcal{M}}^{\prime},g^{\prime})
(
i.e.
, the inclusion map
\iota
) is an isometric embedding in the sense of metric spaces, then
\iota
is necessarily totally geodesic, hence an isometric embedding in the sense of differential geometry.

(2)

Let
\pi:({\mathcal{M}},g)\to({\mathcal{M}},g)
be a surjective isometry from the metric space
({\mathcal{M}},d_{g})
to itself. Then
\pi
is isometric in the differential geometric sense. In particular, if
f:({\mathcal{M}},g)\to({\mathcal{M}},g)
is a metric space isometric embedding and is surjective, then it is an isometric embedding in the differential geometric sense.

Proof.

By Definition
1.2
, being a metric space isometric embedding,
\iota:({\mathcal{M}},g)\hookrightarrow({\mathcal{M}}^{\prime},g^{\prime})
preserves geodesic distances. In particular, it takes geodesics to geodesics. This proves the first statement. The second statement is the content of a theorem by Myers–Steenrod
[
8
]
. ∎
