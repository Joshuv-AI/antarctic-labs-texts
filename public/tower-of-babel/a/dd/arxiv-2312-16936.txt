A data-dependent regularization method based on the graph Laplacian
Davide Bianchi, Davide Evangelista, Stefano Aleotti, Marco Donatelli, Elena Loli Piccolomini, Wenbin Li

Abstract
We investigate a variational method for ill-posed problems, named graphLa+ ​ Ψ \texttt{graphLa+}\Psi , which embeds a graph Laplacian operator in the regularization term. The novelty of this method lies in constructing the graph Laplacian based on a preliminary approximation of the solution, which is obtained using any existing reconstruction method Ψ \Psi from the literature. As a result, the regularization term is both dependent on and adaptive to the observed data and noise. We demonstrate that graphLa+ ​ Ψ \texttt{graphLa+}\Psi is a regularization method and rigorously establish both its convergence and stability properties. We present selected numerical experiments in 2D computerized tomography, wherein we integrate the graphLa+ ​ Ψ \texttt{graphLa+}\Psi method with various reconstruction techniques Ψ \Psi , including Filter Back Projection ( graphLa+FBP ), standard Tikhonov ( graphLa+Tik ), Total Variation ( graphLa+TV ), and a trained deep neural network ( graphLa+Net ). The graphLa+ ​ Ψ \texttt{graphLa+}\Psi approach significantly enhances the quality of the approximated solutions for each method Ψ \Psi . Notably, graphLa+Net is outperforming, offering a robust and stable application of deep neural networks in solving inverse problems.



keywords

ill-posed problems; non-local operators; graph Laplacian; deep learning; deep neural networks; medical imaging

Introduction

We consider the model equation

K{\boldsymbol{x}}={\boldsymbol{y}}^{\delta},

(ME)

where
K\colon X\simeq\mathbb{R}^{n}\to Y\simeq\mathbb{R}^{m}
represents a discretized version of a linear operator that is inherently ill-posed. Given a fixed
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\textnormal{gt}}\in X
and
{\boldsymbol{y}}\coloneqq K{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\textnormal{gt}}
, we want to recover a good approximation of the ground-truth
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\textnormal{gt}}
from a noisy observation
{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\delta}
of
{\boldsymbol{y}}
, that is

{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\delta}\coloneqq{\boldsymbol{y}}+\boldsymbol{\eta},\qquad\|{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\delta}-{\boldsymbol{y}}\|\leq\delta,

(1)

where
\boldsymbol{\eta}
is a random perturbation, typically unavoidable in practical scenarios, and
\delta>0
is the noise intensity.

The ill-posedness of
K
and the presence of noise force the introduction of a regularization strategy to solve our model equation. A standard variational method for
eq.

ME
reads

{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\delta}_{\alpha}\in\underset{{\boldsymbol{x}}\in X}{\operatorname{argmin}}\left\{\frac{1}{2}\|K{\boldsymbol{x}}-{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\delta}\|_{2}^{2}+\alpha\|L{\boldsymbol{x}}\|_{1}\right\},

(2)

where
L
is a linear mapping, characterized by the property that
\ker(L)\cap\ker(K)=\{\mathbf{0}\}
, see
[
21
, Chapter 8]
and
[
43
]
. In this context, the term
\frac{1}{2}\|K\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}^{\delta}\|_{2}^{2}
quantifies the fidelity of the reconstruction, the
\|L\mathbf{x}\|_{1}
component serves as regularization term, and
\alpha>0
balances the trade-off between data fidelity and the regularization effect. Formulation (
2
) is a special case of generalized Tikhonov.

Typical choices for
L
include linear differential operators, especially for denoising applications involving signals that are nearly piecewise-constant, such as those encountered in imaging
[
29
]
.

In the majority of the cases,
L
is a discretization of Euclidean first or second order linear differential operators, but the last decade has been seen an increasing interest for models and techniques from graph theory. In
[
25
,
26
]
, the authors introduced for the first time graph-based non-local operators for image segmentation and denoising problems; in
[
10
,
14
,
7
,
39
,
51
]
this approach is further investigated in the context of image deblurring and computerized tomography problems, and in
[
45
,
4
]
it is applied to other different image processing inverse problems.

In essence, embedding a graph-based operator
L
in the regularization term acts as a guiding mechanism for the overall regularization process. This operator helps in identifying the “correct” neighborhood to concentrate the reconstruction efforts on, by capturing specific features that can be inferred from the observed signal
{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\delta}
.

Initially, a graph structure
G
is constructed from a discretized signal to incorporate features such as interfaces and discontinuities. This discrete space, which heavily depends on the signal itself, can provide more insights into the neighborhood where
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\textnormal{gt}}
resides than a flat manifold like the Euclidean space.

Then, by choosing a suitable graph operator
L
, the optimization process in Equation (
2
) is oriented towards the (supposed) neighborhood of
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\textnormal{gt}}
.

The key point is to construct the graph from a signal that closely approximates the primary features of
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\textnormal{gt}}
. In
[
39
]
, it was observed that generating the graph
G
directly from the observed and noisy data
{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\delta}
results in poor outcomes for imaging tasks such as deblurring or tomographic reconstruction. This is because
{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\delta}
exists in a different domain compared to
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\textnormal{gt}}
. To address this, the authors suggested an initial preprocessing step, transforming
{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\delta}
to
\Psi({\boldsymbol{y}}^{\delta})
, and subsequently constructing a graph from
\Psi({\boldsymbol{y}}^{\delta})
. This preprocessing step involves a reconstruction map
\Psi\colon Y\to X
, from the space of observations
Y
to the domain
X
where
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\textnormal{gt}}
lives. This can be achieved, for example, by employing a standard Tikhonov filter
[
21
]
or the Filter Back Projection (FBP) method
[
34
]
, depending on the inverse problem to handle. In the same spirit and independently, iterative schemes incorporating updates to graph weights were introduced in
[
45
,
51
,
4
]
.

Taking inspiration from
[
39
]
, we introduce the novel method
\texttt{graphLa+}\Psi
. The initial preprocessing step involves selecting a family of reconstructor maps

\Psi_{\Theta}\colon Y\to X,

where
\Theta=\Theta(\delta,{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\delta})
is a family of parameters that can depend on
\delta

{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\delta}
. It is important to note that the pair
(\Psi_{\Theta},\Theta)
is very general and may not be a convergent regularizing method.

Then, a graph

is built from

(3)

Properties of the graph Laplacian are the key ingredients. Informally speaking, it helps to “chain” the reconstructors

In this context, a very interesting choice for
\Psi_{\Theta}
is represented by the class of Deep Neural Networks (DNNs). They are highly nonlinear operators usually characterized by a paramount number of trainable parameters. Generally speaking, the set of parameters is trained by feeding the DNNs with a large number of data and minimizing a loss function. Thanks to the exponential increase of dedicated computational power, DNNs achieved state-of-the art performances in various applications. Particularly in recent years, they have been extensively studied in the field of inverse problems, see
[
5
]
and references therein.

However, applying DNNs to ill-posed inverse problems presents notable drawbacks, most importantly instability and their “black-box” nature. Firstly, DNNs are often sensitive to data perturbations and have a tendency to produce hallucinations, i.e. false yet realistic-looking artifacts, see
[
3
,
20
]
. Secondly, the complexity of their internal mechanisms, which involve millions of parameters and nonlinear mappings, makes them challenging to understand or explain
[
49
]
.

These shortcomings contribute to a general mistrust in DNNs, particularly in real-world situations where accuracy, stability, and reliability are paramount, such as in medical applications. To address these concerns, we propose the integration of the
\texttt{graphLa+}\Psi
method with a DNN, named
graphLa+Net
. This combination leverages the regularization properties of
\texttt{graphLa+}\Psi
, resulting in a stable, convergent, and mathematically robust regularization method that incorporates a DNN, effectively countering the aforementioned flaws. An initial demonstration of the potential of
graphLa+Net
is presented in
[
11
]
.

Our strategy with
graphLa+Net
follows other studies seeking to embed DNNs within a rigorous mathematical framework, see
[
38
,
48
,
44
,
12
]
. This approach is crucial for the reliable application of DNNs to ill-posed inverse problems.

While the methodology and theory we develop herein apply broadly and are not limited solely to ill-posed inverse problems in imaging, to keep the paper self-contained we will focus on 2D computerized tomography (CT) applications.

The model setting

Hereafter, we indicate in bold any finite dimensional (column) vector, i.e.
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in X\simeq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and for the
p
-component of
{\boldsymbol{x}}
we write
{\boldsymbol{x}}(p)
, for
p=1,\ldots,n
. We use the standard notation
\|\cdot\|_{r}
for an
\ell^{r}
-norm,
r\in[1,\infty]
.

As a baseline assumption we say that the unperturbed observation
{\boldsymbol{y}}\in Y\simeq\mathbb{R}^{m}
, which is given in
eq.

1
for
\delta=0
, is the realization of the action of
K
on an element
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\textnormal{gt}}\in X
. That is,

Hypothesis 1
.

There exists
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\textnormal{gt}}
such that
K{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\textnormal{gt}}={\boldsymbol{y}}
.

On the one hand, the goal is to obtain a reliable approximation of the ground truth signal
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\textnormal{gt}}
when we have access only to an observation
{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\delta}
corrupted by unknown noise. On the other hand, the unperturbed system
eq.

ME
for
\delta=0
can be underdetermined, the observed data
{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\delta}
can not belong to the range of
K
, or the (pseudo) inverse of
K
can be very sensitive even to small levels of
\delta>0
. For those reasons, we say that the inverse problem of finding a solution for
eq.

is ill-posed, and need to be regularized. For an overview of ill-posed inverse problems and regularization techniques, we refer the reader to
[
21
,
28
,
47
]
.

Our analysis will heavily depend on graph structures, and so in
Section

2.1
we provide the groundwork of Graph Theory, and in
Section

2.2
we explore the relationship between images and graphs. This relationship will be exploited in the numerical experiments of
Section

2.1
Graph Theory

Fix a finite set
P
. We indicate by
C(P)
the set of real-valued functions on
P
, that is,

C(P)\coloneqq\{{\boldsymbol{x}}\mid{\boldsymbol{x}}:P\to\mathbb{R}\}.

Assuming an implicit ordering of the elements in
P
, it is evident that if the cardinality of the set
P
is
n
then
C(P)\simeq\mathbb{R}^{n}\simeq X
. Hence, we use the same notation for finite-dimensional vectors, even when referring to elements
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in C(P)
. From
Section

3
we will often make the identifications
C(P)=\mathbb{R}^{n}=X
.

For a detailed introduction to the graph setting as presented here, see
[
35
]
.

Definition 2
.

A
graph
over a finite set
P
is a pair
G=(w,\mu)
given by:

•

A nonnegative
edge-weight
function
w\colon P\times P\to[0,\infty)
that satisfies

1.

Symmetry:
w(p,q)=w(q,p)
for every
p,q\in P
;

2.

No loops:
w(p,p)=0
for every
p\in P
.

•

A positive
node measure

\mu\colon P\to(0,\infty)
.

Two nodes are connected if
w(p,q)>0
, and in that case we write
p\sim q
. A finite
walk
is a finite sequence of nodes
\{p_{i}\}_{i=0}^{k}
such that
w(p_{i},p_{i+1})>0
for
i=0,\ldots,k-1
. A subset
Q\subseteq P
is
connected
if for every pair of nodes
p,q\in Q
there is a finite walk such that
p_{0}=p
,
p_{k}=q
, and each
p_{i}
belongs to
Q
. A connected subset
Q\subseteq P
is a
connected component
of
P
if it is maximal with respect to the ordering of inclusion.

Definition 3
(graph Laplacian).

The
graph Laplacian

\Delta\colon C(P)\to C(P)
associated to the graph
G=(w,\mu)
is defined by the action

\displaystyle\Delta{\boldsymbol{x}}(p)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{1}{\mu(p)}\sum_{q\in P}w(p,q)\left({\boldsymbol{x}}(p)-{\boldsymbol{x}}(q)\right).

Notice that
\Delta
does not depend on the ordering (or labeling) of the elements in
P
.

2.1.1
Construction of a graph from an element of
C(P)

Given a finite set
P=\{p\mid p=1,\ldots,n\}
, fix a distance
\operatorname{dist}(\cdot,\cdot)
on the set
P
and a nonnegative function
h_{\textnormal{d}}\colon\mathbb{R}\to[0,+\infty)
such that
h_{\textnormal{d}}(0)=0
.
Then it follows easily that

w_{\textnormal{d}}(p,q)\coloneqq h_{\textnormal{d}}(\operatorname{dist}(p,q))

is an edge-weight function on
P
, since
w_{\textnormal{d}}
satisfies
items

1
and
2
from
Definition

2
. It is based on the geometric properties of
P
induced by the distance
\operatorname{dist}(\cdot,\cdot)
. The magnitude of the connections between two nodes
p
and
q
is then regulated by
h_{\textnormal{d}}
.

Fix now an element
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in C(P)
, another nonnegative function
h_{\textnormal{i}}\colon\mathbb{R}\to[0,+\infty)
and finally define

w_{{\boldsymbol{x}}}(p,q)\coloneqq\underbrace{w_{\textnormal{d}}(p,q)}_{\text{geometry}}\cdot\underbrace{h_{\textnormal{i}}(|{\boldsymbol{x}}(p)-{\boldsymbol{x}}(q)|)}_{{\boldsymbol{x}}\text{ intensity}}.

(4)

The edge-weight function
eq.

4
between two nodes depends on both the “physical” distance, thanks to
w_{\textnormal{d}}
, and the “variation of intensity” of
{\boldsymbol{x}}
, thanks to
h_{\textnormal{i}}(|{\boldsymbol{x}}(p)-{\boldsymbol{x}}(q)|)
. This dual dependence has a twofold effect: it can separate nodes that reside in different and unrelated regions of the space, and it weights the magnitude of the connections depending on the difference of intensities of
{\boldsymbol{x}}
. For an example of application, see
Section

2.2
.

Now choose any strictly positive function, which may depend on
{\boldsymbol{x}}
,

\mu_{{\boldsymbol{x}}}(p)>0\quad\forall\;p\in P.

(5)

Therefore, following
Definition

2
, for any fixed
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in C(P)
, the pair
eq.

4
-
eq.

5
is a graph
G
, on the set
P
, induced by
{\boldsymbol{x}}
. We will write then
\Delta_{{\boldsymbol{x}}}
to indicate the associated graph Laplacian, as per
Definition

3
.

2.2
Images and graphs

We briefly describe here how images and graphs can be related, following the strategy and notation described in the previous
Section

2.1.1
, and provide a practical example.

First, any image is made by the union of several pixels
p\in P
disposed on a grid of dimension
d_{1}\times d_{2}
, where
d_{1},d_{2}\in\mathbb{N}
. It is then natural to identify them as ordered pairs
p=(i,j)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
with
i=1,\ldots,d_{1}
and
j=1,\ldots,d_{2}
.

As a consequence, a reasonable choice to connect two pixels by their “physical” distance is to set

\operatorname{dist}(p,q)\coloneqq\|p-q\|_{\infty}\quad\mbox{and}\quad h_{\textnormal{d}}(t)\coloneqq\mathds{1}_{(0,R]}(t),

where
\mathds{1}_{(0,R]}
is the indicator function of the set
(0,R]
and
R
is a parameter of control which tells the maximum distance allowed for two pixels to be neighbors. If
0<\|p-q\|_{\infty}\leq R
, then
p
and
q
are connected with an edge of magnitude
1
, viz.
w_{\textnormal{d}}(p,q)=1
.

Figure 2:
Simple outline of how to build a graph from an image
{\boldsymbol{x}}
. To be read from left to right. Left: a
7\times 7
pixels image made by orange-like and blue-like square pixels. The color intensity of each pixel is given by the pixel-wise evaluation of a function
{\boldsymbol{x}}
. Center-left: each pixel corresponds one node, represented by a black circle. Since the pixels are disposed on a grid, each node can be associated to an ordered pair in
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
. Center-right: the geometric edge-weight function
w_{\textnormal{d}}
in
eq.

4
is given by
\mathds{1}_{(0,1]}(\|p-q\|_{\infty})
, that is, two nodes
p,q
are connected if and only if
\|p-q\|_{\infty}=1
, and in that case the magnitude of the connection is one. Right: the magnitude of an edge between two nodes is then weighted by
h_{\textnormal{i}}(\|{\boldsymbol{x}}(p)-{\boldsymbol{x}}(q)\|)\in(0,1]
, where
h_{\textnormal{i}}
is the Gaussian function, see
eq.

6
. The role of
h_{\textnormal{i}}
is to measure the difference of intensity between two adjacent pixels, and it is close to zero when two pixels have very different color intensities. This is represented by the different thickness of the edges connecting two adjacent pixels, where a thick edge means very similar color intensity and a thin edge means a very different color intensity.

Second, a gray-scale image is given by the light intensities of its pixels. That is, a gray-scale image can be represented by a function
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in C(P)
such that
{\boldsymbol{x}}(p)\in[0,1]
, where
0
means black and
1
means white. A common choice to weight the connection of two different pixels by their light intensities is to use the Gaussian function, that is

h_{\textnormal{i}}(t)\coloneqq\rm{e}^{-\frac{t^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}},\qquad\sigma>0.

The reason lies on the relationship between the heat kernel and the discretization of the Laplacian on a manifold. See
[
13
]
for discussions about the Laplacian on discretized manifolds, and
[
17
]
for another application of the Gaussian function to define the weights of the graph Laplacian.

We arrive then at the definition of the edge-weight function in (
4
), applied to our case, that is

w_{{\boldsymbol{x}}}(p,q)=\mathds{1}_{(0,R]}(\|p-q\|_{\infty})\rm{e}^{-\frac{|{\boldsymbol{x}}(p)-{\boldsymbol{x}}(q)|^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}}.

(6)

For colored images, it is a little bit different. For example, in the RGB representation, the color of a pixel is given by the combination of the light intensities of three channels R(ed), G(reen) and B(lue). Therefore, in principle a colored image should be regarded as a function
{\boldsymbol{x}}:P\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
, where
{\boldsymbol{x}}(p)=({\boldsymbol{x}}_{R}(p),{\boldsymbol{x}}_{G}(p),{\boldsymbol{x}}_{B}(p))
is a vector-valued function whose elements represent the light intensities for each channel. However, it is common to assume that the ill-posed operator
K
acts independently on each channel, and therefore the regularization is made on each channel separately. If for some reasons this assumption can not be made, then we can simply modify the definition of (
4
) in the following way:

w_{{\boldsymbol{x}}}(p,q)\coloneqq w_{\textnormal{d}}(p,q)\cdot h_{\textnormal{i}}(\|{\boldsymbol{x}}(p)-{\boldsymbol{x}}(q)\|),

where
\|\cdot\|
is any appropriate norm in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. See Figure
2
for a simple example of building a graph from an image.

3
The
\texttt{graphLa+}\Psi
regularization method

In this section we introduce the
\texttt{graphLa+}\Psi
method and we show that it is a regularization method.

Let us begin by considering a family of operators
\{\Psi_{\Theta}:Y\rightarrow X\}
which we generally refer to as
reconstructors
.
\Psi_{\Theta}
s not necessarily linear, and
\Theta\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
denotes its parameters. This family of reconstructors is at the core of our
\texttt{graphLa+}\Psi
method. It has the very important role to give a first approximation of
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\textnormal{gt}}
, since upon this approximation the reconstruction step of our method is built .

We need to enforce some (weak) regularity on
\Psi_{\Theta}
. Assume that there exists an element
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}\in X
, a nonnegative function
g=g(\delta,{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0})
, and a parameter choice rule
\Theta=\Theta(\delta,{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\delta})
such that

Hypothesis 4
.

\|\Psi_{\Theta(\delta,{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\delta})}({\boldsymbol{y}}^{\delta})-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}\|_{2}\leq g(\delta,{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0})\to 0\qquad\mbox{as }\delta\to 0.

The above
4
will guarantee that the
\texttt{graphLa+}\Psi
method, which we will introduce properly in
eq.

7
, is a convergent regularization method. Let now
{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\delta}
and
\Theta\coloneqq\Theta(\delta,{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\delta})
be fixed, and
\{\delta_{k}\}
and
\{{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\delta_{k}}\}
be sequences such that
\delta_{k}\to\delta
and
{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\delta_{k}}\rightarrow{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\delta}
for
k\rightarrow\infty
. Write
\Theta_{k}\coloneqq\Theta(\delta_{k},{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\delta_{k}})
. The next hypothesis will guarantee stability for
eq.

7
.

Hypothesis 5
.

\Theta_{k}\to\Theta\quad\mbox{and}\quad\|\Psi_{\Theta_{k}}({\boldsymbol{y}}^{\delta_{k}})-\Psi_{\Theta}({\boldsymbol{y}}^{\delta})\|_{2}\leq f(k)\to 0\;\mbox{for }k\to\infty.

In the next two examples, we make clear that the above assumptions are pretty weak and they can be satisfied by several large classes of reconstructors. In particular, the pair
(\Psi_{\Theta},\Theta)
does not need to be a convergent regularization method.

Example 6
.

A simple example of a family of reconstructors that satisfies
4
is the case when we identify them with a single, (locally) Lipschitz continuous operator. That is, fix
\Theta\equiv\hat{\Theta}
for every
\delta
and
{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\delta}
, and choose an operator
\Psi_{\hat{\Theta}}
such that
\|\Psi_{\hat{\Theta}}({\boldsymbol{y}}_{1})-\Psi_{\hat{\Theta}}({\boldsymbol{y}}_{2})\|_{2}\leq c\|{\boldsymbol{y}}_{1}-{\boldsymbol{y}}_{2}\|_{2}
. Thanks to
eq.

1
and the Lipschitz condition,
4
is then trivially verified with
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}\coloneqq\Psi_{\hat{\Theta}}({\boldsymbol{y}})
and
g(\delta,{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0})=c\delta
. In the same way,
5
is verified by
f(k)=c\|{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\delta_{k}}-{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\delta}\|
.

The situation of the preceding example will occur later, when
\Psi_{\hat{\Theta}}
is implemented as a trained DNN, as discussed in
Sections

4
and
5.3
. Let us emphasize that DNNs are typically non-regularizing methods.

Example 7
.

A less trivial family of reconstructors that satisfy
4
is the one given by any typical regularization operators, as per
[
21
, Definition 3.1]
, for example. Let
\Theta\in(0,\infty)
and
\Psi_{\Theta}
be continuous, and
K^{\dagger}
be the usual Moore-Penrose pseudo-inverse of
K
. Fix
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}\coloneqq K^{\dagger}{\boldsymbol{y}}
and observe that
K{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}={\boldsymbol{y}}

The class of convergent regularization methods which fits

As detailed in
Section

, we can define a graph Laplacian induced by

\Psi_{\Theta}^{\delta}\coloneqq\Psi_{\Theta}({\boldsymbol{y}}^{\delta})\in X\simeq C(P),

and we will denoted it by
\Delta_{\Psi^{\delta}_{\Theta}}
. Consider then the following Tikhonov-type variational method for

{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\delta}_{\Psi^{\delta}_{\Theta},\alpha}\in\underset{{\boldsymbol{x}}\in X}{\operatorname{argmin}}\left\{\frac{1}{2}\|K{\boldsymbol{x}}-{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\delta}\|_{2}^{2}+\alpha\|\Delta_{\Psi^{\delta}_{\Theta}}{\boldsymbol{x}}\|_{1}\right\}.

(7)

We call

the
\texttt{graphLa+}\Psi
method.

Let us focus our attention on the regularization term
\mathcal{R}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\delta})\coloneqq\|\Delta_{\Psi^{\delta}_{\Theta}}{\boldsymbol{x}}\|_{1}
. As a first remark, notice that, unlike typical regularization methods,
\mathcal{R}
depends not only on
{\boldsymbol{x}}
but also on the data
{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\delta}
. This complicates any attempt at studying the convergence of
eq.

7
for
\delta\to 0
.

As a second remark, indicating with
w_{\Psi_{\Theta}^{\delta}}
the edge-weight function
eq.

4
with
{\boldsymbol{x}}
replaced by
\Psi_{\Theta}^{\delta}
, we have that minimizing
\mathcal{R}
means to force
{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\delta}_{\Psi^{\delta}_{\Theta},\alpha}
to be constant on the regions where
w_{\Psi_{\Theta}^{\delta}}(p,q)
is “large”. This is a direct consequence of
Definition

3
,

|\Delta_{\Psi^{\delta}_{\Theta}}{\boldsymbol{x}}(p)|=\frac{1}{\mu_{\Psi_{\Theta}^{\delta}}(p)}\left|\sum_{q\in P}w_{\Psi_{\Theta}^{\delta}}(p,q)({\boldsymbol{x}}(p)-{\boldsymbol{x}}(q))\right|.

For example, setting
w_{\Psi_{\Theta}^{\delta}}
as in
eq.

6
, then
w_{\Psi_{\Theta}^{\delta}}(p,q)
attains its maximum value of
1
if and only if
p\sim q
and
\Psi_{\Theta}^{\delta}(p)=\Psi_{\Theta}^{\delta}(q)
. Therefore,
\|\Delta_{\Psi^{\delta}_{\Theta}}{\boldsymbol{x}}\|_{1}\approx 0
if
{\boldsymbol{x}}
is constant on the regions where
\Psi_{\Theta}^{\delta}
is constant.

Put simply, we can say that
\mathcal{R}
helps to distinguish the regions of uniformity from the regions of interfaces. In intuition, once
\Psi_{\Theta}^{\delta}
reconstructs the region of interfaces in
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\textnormal{gt}}
, the final solution
{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\delta}_{\Psi^{\delta}_{\Theta},\alpha}
will be a good approximation of the ground truth
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\textnormal{gt}}
, even though
\Psi_{\Theta}^{\delta}
deviates from
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\textnormal{gt}}
in other aspects.

As a last comment, the
\ell^{1}
-norm in
\mathcal{R}
is introduced to enforce sparsity on the approximated solution
{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\delta}_{\Psi^{\delta}_{\Theta},\alpha}
. This is mainly in view of the imaging applications of Section
6
. All the theory developed here works with the
\ell^{1}
-norm replaced by any
\ell^{r}
-norm for
r>1
.

4
graphLa+Net

Among the possible choices for reconstructors
\Psi_{\Theta}
, we propose to use a Deep Neural Network (DNN). In this case, the set of parameters
\Theta
contains matrices and vectors, which are the building blocks of DNNs. For a mathematical introduction to neural networks we refer to
[
30
]
. Informally speaking, a DNN is a long chain of compositions of affine operators and nonlinear activation functions. The set of parameters
\Theta
is then trained by minimizing a loss function over a large number of data, see
Section

5.3
. When considering
\Psi_{\Theta}
as a DNN, we specify it by calling the method
graphLa+Net
. A first overview of
graphLa+Net
was proposed in
[
11
]
.

Note that, in principle, it is possible to make the network parameters
\Theta
dependent on the noise level
\delta
by training it multiple times for different values of
\delta
. However, this is rarely done in practice due to the significant amount of time and energy consumption it would require. This limitation has always been a crucial challenge in employing DNNs for regularizing ill-posed problems, as it necessitates the estimate of an optimal noise level
\delta
which is suitable for diverse applications. Additionally, opting for
\delta=0
is generally not a good choice due to the typical high sensitivity of DNNs to the noise.

Nonetheless, the regularizing property of
\texttt{graphLa+}\Psi
effectively addresses this issue. In the subsequent discussion, we will consider DNNs as reconstructors with a fixed
\hat{\Theta}
, where
\hat{\Theta}
has been trained over a
noiseless
dataset. As will be shown in
Sections

6.1
and
6.2
, the resulting
graphLa+Net
is not only regularizing and stable but also significantly superior in performance, despite the inherent instability of the original DNN.

4.1
The architecture

In our DNN model, we utilize a modified version of the U-net, as detailed in
[
46
]
. The structure of this modified U-net is illustrated in
Figure

3
. U-net is a widely recognized multi-scale Convolutional Neural Network architecture, known for its effectiveness in processing images with global artifacts. This fully convolutional network features a symmetrical encoder-decoder structure, employing strided convolutions to expand its receptive field. The encoder layers’ strides create distinct levels of resolution within the network. Each level comprises a set number of blocks, where a block consists of a convolutional layer with a fixed number of channels, followed by batch normalization and a ReLU activation function. The number of convolutional channels is doubled at each successive level, starting from a baseline number in the first layer. Specifically, our network is designed with four levels and a baseline of 64 convolutional channels.

As mentioned, the decoder mirrors the encoder but uses upsampling convolutional layers in place of strided convolutions. Furthermore, to preserve high-frequency details, skip connections link the final layer of each encoder level to the corresponding first layer of the decoder.

The network we consider in this paper is called
Residual U-net (ResU-net)
and it was proposed in
[
23
]
. This network is a variation of the original U-net architecture, modified through two key changes. First, we have reconfigured the skip connections to work as additions rather than concatenations, a strategy aimed at reducing the total number of parameters. Second, we introduce a residual connection that links the input and output layers directly, which implies that the network learns the residual mapping between the input and the expected output. The importance of the residual connection has been observed in
[
27
]
, where the authors proved that the residual manifold containing the artifacts is easier to learn than the true image manifold.

5
Experimental setup

In this section we describe the setting of the
\texttt{graphLa+}\Psi
method in our numerical experiment.

5.1
Sparse view Computed Tomography

X-rays Computed Tomography (CT) is a widespread imaging system particularly useful to detect injuries, tumors, internal bleeding, bone fractures, and various medical conditions. It is essentially constituted by a source rotating around an object and emitting X-rays beams from a fixed number of angles along its arc trajectory. Passing through the interior of the object, a quantity of radiation proportional to the density of the tissues gets absorbed and the resulting rays are measured by a detector. The collection of all the measurements at different projection angles is called the sinogram.

In this case, the matrix
K\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
represents the discrete Radon operator,
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the 2D object discretized in
n
pixels (voxels) and
{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\delta}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
is the sinogram. Here, the dimension
m
depends on the number of projection angles
n_{\alpha}
.
In medical applications, sparse view CT denotes the case where only a small number of angles
n_{\alpha}
is considered, in order to reduce the radiation dose absorbed by the patient. In this particular setting, the matrix
K
is under-determined (i.e.
m<n
). To faithfully simulate the real medical scenario in our experiments we model
K
as the fan beam Radon transform, modeling the spread of X-rays with a fan trajectory.

5.2
The datasets and the test problem

We evaluate the
\texttt{graphLa+}\Psi
algorithms using two distinct image datasets. The first is the COULE dataset, which comprises synthetic images with a resolution of 256x256 pixels. These images feature ellipses and lines of varying gray intensities against a dark background. This dataset is publicly available on Kaggle
[
22
]
. The second dataset is a subsampled version of the AAPM Low Dose CT Grand Challenge dataset, provided by the Mayo Clinic
[
40
]
. It contains real chest CT image acquisitions, each also at a resolution of 256x256 pixels.

To simulate the sinogram
{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\delta}
we consider
n_{\alpha}
different angles evenly distributed within the closed interval
[0,179]
, where
n_{\alpha}=120
in the experiments with COULE dataset and
n_{\alpha}=180
in the experiments with Mayo dataset. The sinograms are generated using the IRtools toolbox
[

.

5.3
Neural Network training

We randomly select 400 pairs of images from COULE and 3,305 pairs of images from Mayo as training sets, all of the form
({\boldsymbol{x}}_{\textnormal{gt}},{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\delta})
.
Following the discussion in
Section

4
, we train the DNN over the training sets in a supervised manner without extra noise, specifically by setting
\delta=0
. The process involves finding
\hat{\Theta}
that minimizes the Mean Squared Error between the predicted reconstruction
\Psi_{\Theta}({\boldsymbol{y}})
and the ground-truth solution
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\textnormal{gt}}
. Once the training process is completed, we set
\Theta\equiv\hat{\Theta}
.

Since the ResU-net architecture is fully convolutional, the input required by the network is an image. Hence, the input sinogram
{\boldsymbol{y}}
has to be pre-processed through a fast algorithm mapping the sinogram to a coarse reconstructed image, such as FBP
[
41
,
33
]
or a few iterations of a regularizing algorithm
[
42
,
23
]
. For those experiments, we choose the FBP.

The networks has been trained on an NVIDIA RTX A4000 GPU card with 16Gb of VRAM, for a total of 50 epochs and a batch size of 10. We use Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 0.001,
\beta_{1}=0.9
, and
\beta_{2}=0.999
, in all the experiments.

7
Conclusions

In this work we introduced and analyzed a novel regularization method which uses a graph Laplacian operator built upon a first approximation of the solution through a reconstructor
\Psi
. We demonstrated that under certain, albeit very weak, hypotheses on the recontructor
\Psi
,
\texttt{graphLa+}\Psi
is a convergent and stable regularization method. In all the proposed numerical examples,
\texttt{graphLa+}\Psi
greatly enhanced the quality of the reconstructions for every initial reconstructor
\Psi
.

Moreover, leveraging the regularization property of
\texttt{graphLa+}\Psi
, we suggested to use a DNN as first reconstructor
\Psi
. This new hybrid method, called
graphLa+Net
, merges the regularity of a standard variational method and the accuracy of a DNN, giving as a result a stable regularization method with very high accuracy.

In forthcoming works we will focus more on sharpening the choices for the edge-weight function
w_{\boldsymbol{x}}
such as building an automatic rule for estimating its parameters.

Proof.

A.1

\texttt{graphLa+}\Psi

Define

\Gamma({\boldsymbol{x}})\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\|K{\boldsymbol{x}}-{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\delta}\|_{2}^{2}+\alpha\|\Delta_{\Psi^{\delta}_{\Theta}}{\boldsymbol{x}}\|_{1}.

(12)

We recall that a (nonnegative) functional
\Gamma\colon X\to[0,\infty)
is
coercive
if
\Gamma({\boldsymbol{x}})\to\infty
for
\|{\boldsymbol{x}}\|\to\infty
, where
\|\cdot\|
can be any norm on
X\simeq\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Thanks to

eq.

is coercive, for every fixed
\alpha>0
and
\delta\geq 0
. Although this should certainly be well-known, for the convenience of the reader we include a short proof in the next lemma.

Lemma 19
.

\Gamma
is coercive for every fixed
\alpha>0
and
\delta\geq 0
.

Proof.

Let
V
be the invariant null space of
\Delta_{\Psi^{\delta}_{\Theta}}
from
Lemma

10
, and let us indicate with
\pi
and
\pi_{\perp}
the projection into
V
and
V^{\perp}
, respectively. In general, it holds that

\qquad\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\boldsymbol{u}}\in V^{\perp}\\
{\boldsymbol{u}}\neq\boldsymbol{0}\end{subarray}}\frac{\|\Delta_{\Psi^{\delta}_{\Theta}}{\boldsymbol{u}}\|_{1}}{\|{\boldsymbol{u}}\|_{1}}\geq\gamma_{1}>0.

(13)

Since
\ker(K)\cap V=\{\mathbf{0}\}
by
11
, then it holds that too

\qquad\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\boldsymbol{v}}\in V\\
{\boldsymbol{v}}\neq\boldsymbol{0}\end{subarray}}\frac{\|K{\boldsymbol{v}}\|_{2}}{\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\|_{2}}\geq\gamma_{2}>0.

(14)

Fix a sequence
\{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{j}\}
such that
\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{j}\|_{2}\to\infty
. We want to show that
\Gamma({\boldsymbol{x}}_{j})\to\infty
. Clearly, for

\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{j}\|_{2}\to\infty\quad\mbox{if and only if}\quad\|\pi{\boldsymbol{x}}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}+\|\pi_{\perp}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{j}\|_{1}\to\infty.

There are two cases:

1.

\lim_{j}\|\pi_{\perp}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{j}\|_{1}=\infty
;

2.

\liminf_{j}\|\pi_{\perp}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{j}\|_{1}\leq c<\infty
and

If we are in

\displaystyle\alpha\gamma_{1}\|\pi_{\perp}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{j}\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\alpha\|\Delta_{\Psi^{\delta}_{\Theta}}\pi_{\perp}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{j}\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\|K{\boldsymbol{x}}_{j}-{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\delta}\|_{2}^{2}+\alpha\|\Delta_{\Psi^{\delta}_{\Theta}}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{j}\|_{1}=\Gamma({\boldsymbol{x}}_{j}).

(15)

If we are in
2
, then by
eq.

14

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma_{2}}{4}\|\pi{\boldsymbol{x}}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{4}\|K\pi{\boldsymbol{x}}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\|K{\boldsymbol{x}}_{j}-{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\delta}\|_{2}^{2}+\alpha\|\Delta_{\Psi^{\delta}_{\Theta}}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{j}\|_{1}+\frac{1}{2}\|K\pi_{\perp}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{j}-{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\delta}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\Gamma({\boldsymbol{x}}_{j})+\frac{1}{2}\|K\pi_{\perp}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{j}-{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\delta}\|_{2}^{2}.

(16)

Notice that from
2
it follows that
\liminf_{j}\|K\pi_{\perp}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{j}-{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\delta}\|_{2}^{2}
is bounded.

Passing to the
\liminf
in both

and
16
we conclude.

The same result as in

19
applies when replacing

with
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}
in
\Gamma
, and the proof remains unchanged.

Proposition

13
.

Proof.

The existence relies on standard topological arguments, but we provide a comprehensive exposition

Let

which is well-defined thanks to

There are two possible cases:

Suppose now we are in case

Proof.

From

Lemma 21
.

Let
\Theta=\Theta(\delta,{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\delta})
,
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}
and

where

is a positive constant independent of
{\boldsymbol{x}}
. In particular, the convergence is uniform on every compact subset.

Proof.

We use the notations introduced in
Sections

2.1.1
and
3.1
. In particular,
w_{\Psi_{\Theta}^{\delta}}
and
w_{0}
are the edge-weight functions in
eq.

4

\Psi^{\delta}_{\Theta}\coloneqq\Psi_{\Theta}({\boldsymbol{y}}^{\delta})
and
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}
, respectively;
\mu_{\Psi_{\Theta}^{\delta}}
and
\mu_{0}
are the node measures in
eq.

5
induced by
\Psi^{\delta}_{\Theta}
and
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}
, respectively. Let us observe that, with this notation,
4
now reads

\|\Psi^{\delta}_{\Theta}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}\|_{2}\leq g(\delta,{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0})\quad\mbox{as }\delta\to 0.

Define

t_{\delta,p,q}\coloneqq|\Psi^{\delta}_{\Theta}(p)-\Psi^{\delta}_{\Theta}(q)|,\quad t_{0,p,q}\coloneqq|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}(p)-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}(q)|,\quad d\coloneqq\max_{p,q}\{w_{\textnormal{d}}(p,q)\}.

Then,

\displaystyle|\Delta_{\Psi^{\delta}_{\Theta}}{\boldsymbol{x}}(p)-\Delta_{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}}{\boldsymbol{x}}(p)|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{q\in P}\left|\frac{w_{\Psi_{\Theta}^{\delta}}(p,q)}{\mu_{\Psi_{\Theta}^{\delta}}(p)}-\frac{w_{0}(p,q)}{\mu_{0}(p)}\right||{\boldsymbol{x}}(p)-{\boldsymbol{x}}(q)|

\displaystyle=\sum_{q\in P}w_{\textnormal{d}}(p,q)\left|\frac{h_{\textnormal{i}}(t_{\delta,p,q})}{\mu_{\Psi_{\Theta}^{\delta}}(p)}-\frac{h_{\textnormal{i}}(t_{0,p,q})}{\mu_{0}(p)}\right||{\boldsymbol{x}}(p)-{\boldsymbol{x}}(q)|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2d\|{\boldsymbol{x}}\|_{\infty}}{\mu_{\Psi_{\Theta}^{\delta}}(p)\mu_{0}(p)}\sum_{q\in P}\left|\mu_{0}(p)h_{\textnormal{i}}(t_{\delta,p,q})-\mu_{\Psi_{\Theta}^{\delta}}(p)h_{\textnormal{i}}(t_{0,p,q})\right|.

(18)

We need to bound the right-hand side of the last inequality. Because of the locally Lipschitz continuity of the maps
h_{\textnormal{i}}
and
\Psi_{\Theta}^{\delta}\mapsto\mu_{\Psi_{\Theta}^{\delta}}(p)
, granted by
12
, then for
\delta
small enough there exists a positive constant
\bar{c}
, independent of
p
, such that

\frac{2d}{\mu_{\Psi_{\Theta}^{\delta}}(p)\mu_{0}(p)}\leq\bar{c},\quad|\mu_{\Psi_{\Theta}^{\delta}}(p)-\mu_{0}(p)|\leq\bar{c}\|\Psi_{\Theta}^{\delta}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}\|_{2},\quad\mbox{and}\quad|h_{\textnormal{i}}(t_{\delta,p,q})-h_{\textnormal{i}}(t_{0,p,q})|\leq\bar{c}|t_{\delta,p,q}-t_{0,p,q}|.

Therefore, by adding and subtracting the auxiliary term
\mu_{0}(p)h_{\textnormal{i}}(t_{0,p,q})
, it holds that

\begin{aligned} \left|\mu_{0}(p)h_{\textnormal{i}}(t_{\delta,p,q})-\mu_{\Psi_{\Theta}^{\delta}}(p)h_{\textnormal{i}}(t_{0,p,q})\right|&\leq\mu_{0}(p)|h_{\textnormal{i}}(t_{\delta,p,q})-h_{\textnormal{i}}(t_{0,p,q})|+|\mu_{\Psi_{\Theta}^{\delta}}(p)-\mu_{0}(p)|h_{\textnormal{i}}(t_{0,p,q})\\
&\leq\bar{c}\mu_{0}(p)|t_{\delta,p,q}-t_{0,p,q}|+\bar{c}\|\Psi_{\Theta}^{\delta}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}\|_{2}h_{\textnormal{i}}(t_{0,p,q})\\
&\leq\bar{c}\max_{p}\{\mu_{0}(p)\}|t_{\delta,p,q}-t_{0,p,q}|+\bar{c}\max_{p,q}\{h_{\textnormal{i}}(t_{0,p,q})\}g(\delta,{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}).\end{aligned}

(19)

Let us bound now
|t_{\delta,p,q}-t_{0,p,q}|
:

\displaystyle|t_{\delta,p,q}-t_{0,p,q}|

\displaystyle=\left||\Psi^{\delta}_{\Theta}(p)-\Psi^{\delta}_{\Theta}(q)|-|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}(p)-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}(q)|\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\left(\Psi^{\delta}_{\Theta}(p)-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}(p)\right)+\left({\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}(q)-\Psi^{\delta}_{\Theta}(q)\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\Psi^{\delta}_{\Theta}(p)-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}(p)\right|+\left|\Psi^{\delta}_{\Theta}(q)-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}(q)\right|

\displaystyle\leq 2\sqrt{n}\|\Psi^{\delta}_{\Theta}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}\|_{2}\leq 2\sqrt{n}g(\delta,{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}).

(20)

Combining
eqs.

18
,
19
, and
20
, we get that

|\Delta_{\Psi^{\delta}_{\Theta}}{\boldsymbol{x}}(p)-\Delta_{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}}{\boldsymbol{x}}(p)|\leq n\bar{c}^{2}\left(2\sqrt{n}\max_{p}\{\mu_{0}(p)\}+\max_{p,q}\{h_{\textnormal{i}}(t_{0,p,q})\}\right)\|{\boldsymbol{x}}\|_{\infty}g(\delta,{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}),

and then the thesis easily follows.

Let us point out that the constants that appears in the proof depends on the dimension
n
. Appropriate choices of
w_{\textnormal{d}}
,
h_{\textnormal{i}}
and
\mu_{\boldsymbol{x}}
, with respect to

, could make

independent of
n
itself. However, there is still the issue of the convergence of the (sequence of) graph Laplacians
\Delta_{\boldsymbol{x}}
as

, and this is beyond the scope of this work.
