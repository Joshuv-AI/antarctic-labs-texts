Abstract
. We establish a connection between the theory of Ulrich sheaves and 𝔸 1 {\mathbb{A}}^{1} -homotopy theory. For instance, we prove that the 𝔸 1 {\mathbb{A}}^{1} -degree of a morphism between projective varieties, that is relatively oriented by an Ulrich sheaf, is constant on the target even when it is not 𝔸 1 {\mathbb{A}}^{1} -chain connected or 𝔸 1 {\mathbb{A}}^{1} -connected. Further if an embedded projective variety is the support of a symmetric Ulrich sheaf of rank one, the 𝔸 1 {\mathbb{A}}^{1} -degree of all its linear projections can be read off in an explicit way from the free resolution of the Ulrich sheaf. Finally, we construct an Ulrich sheaf on the secant variety of a curve and use this to define an arithmetic version of Viro’s encomplexed writhe for curves in ℙ 3 \mathbb{P}^{3} . This can be considered to be an arithmetic analogue of a knot invariant. Namely, we define a notion of algebraic isotopy under which the arithmetic writhe is invariant. For rational curves of degree at most four in ℙ 3 \mathbb{P}^{3} we obtain a complete classification up to algebraic isotopies.

1.
Introduction

In the emerging field of
{\mathbb{A}}^{1}
-enumerative geometry
[
KW19
,
Lev20
,
KW21
]
, building upon the
{\mathbb{A}}^{1}
-homotopy theory developed by Morel and Voevodsky
[
MV99
]
, solutions to enumerative problems over a field
K
are counted in the Grothendieck–Witt group
\operatorname{GW}(K)
of this field in a way that the result does not depend on the chosen instance of the enumerative problem. Typical examples of enumerative problems whose solutions can be “arithmetically enriched” in this way include among others the 27 lines on a cubic surface
[
KW21
]
, Bézout’s theorem
[
McK21
]
or Gromov–Witten invariants
[
KLSW23
]
. Many results of this type are achieved by studying arithmetic versions of topological invariants like the Euler characteristic
[
Lev19
]
or the Brouwer degree
[
Mor12
,
KW19
]
. Here we focus on the latter, namely the
{\mathbb{A}}^{1}
-degree
of a morphism of smooth schemes as considered in
[
KLSW23
,
PW21
]
. This is defined for a finite and surjective morphism
f\colon X\to Y
of smooth
K
-varieties starting from a
relative orientation
of
f
, this is a line bundle
L
on
X
, with an isomorphism

\psi\colon L\otimes L\longrightarrow\operatorname{{\mathscr{H}om}}(\det{\mathcal{T}}_{X},f^{*}\det{\mathcal{T}}_{Y}).

With this, we can compute the
\mathbb{A}^{1}
-degree at a closed point
y\in Y
as a sum of local degrees on the fiber, as in the classical case; see Section
4
for more details. When this is independent of the point
y\in Y
, one says that the
{\mathbb{A}}^{1}
-degree of the map
f
is
well-defined
. This is not always the case. One important case, when this happens, is if
Y
is
{\mathbb{A}}^{1}
-chain connected. Our first result is a different condition for the well-definedness of the
{\mathbb{A}}^{1}
-degree in terms of
Ulrich sheaves
: a sheaf
\mathcal{F}
on
X
is called
f
-Ulrich
if its push-forward along
f
trivial, i.e.,
f_{*}\mathcal{F}\cong\mathcal{O}_{Y}^{\oplus N}
for
N\in\mathbb{N}
.

Theorem A
.

If the relative orientation
L
is
f
-Ulrich and if
H^{0}(Y,\mathcal{O}_{Y})=K
then the
\mathbb{A}^{1}
-degree of
f
is well-defined.

We refer to
4.17
for a precise statement of the result and for the proof. In particular, we consider the case of standard Ulrich sheaves on an embedded irreducible projective variety
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{n}
of dimension
\dim X=k
: these are sheaves
\mathcal{F}
on
X
which are
\pi
-Ulrich for any finite linear projection
\pi\colon X\to\mathbb{P}^{k}
. Ulrich sheaves were introduced by Eisenbud and Schreyer in
[
ES03
]
and they satisfy a plethora of nice properties. In particular, there has been ample interest in the question whether every closed subvariety of
\mathbb{P}^{n}
carries an Ulrich sheaf
[
ES03
,
Bea18
]
.

One of the main features of Ulrich sheaves is to give a determinantal representation of the
Chow form
of
X
. Recall that a finite linear projection
\pi\colon X\to\mathbb{P}^{k}
has the form
\pi=[s_{0},\dots,s_{k}]
, where
s_{0},\dots,s_{k}\in H^{0}(\mathbb{P}^{n},\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{n}}(1))
are linearly independent, and such that the linear space
\Pi=\{s_{0}=\dots=s_{k}=0\}\subset\mathbb{P}^{n}
does not intersect
X
. The
s_{0},\dots,s_{k}
satisfy these conditions if and only if
\mathscr{C}_{X}(s_{0}\wedge\dots\wedge s_{k})\neq 0
, for a homogeneous form on
\wedge^{k+1}H^{0}(\mathbb{P}^{n},\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{n}}(1))
called the Chow form of the embedded variety
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{n}
. One of the main results of
[
ES03
]
is that if
X
carries an Ulrich sheaf of rank one, then there is a matrix
\Lambda
of linear forms on
\wedge^{k+1}H^{0}(\mathbb{P}^{n},\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{n}}(1))
whose determinant gives the Chow form. Furthermore, there is a natural notion of
symmetry
for the Ulrich sheaf which guarantees that
\Lambda
can be taken to be symmetric. This matrix encodes the
{\mathbb{A}}^{1}
-degrees of all finite linear projections of
X
:

Theorem B
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a symmetric Ulrich sheaf of rank one on
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{n}
. Then this sheaf induces a relative orientation on each finite linear projection

[s_{0},\dots,s_{k}]\colon X\to\mathbb{P}^{k}

such that the resulting
{\mathbb{A}}^{1}
-degree is well-defined. Furthermore, there is a symmetric matrix
\Lambda
of linear forms on
\wedge^{k+1}H^{0}(\mathbb{P}^{n},\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{n}}(1))
whose determinant is the Chow form of
X
, and such that the class

[\Lambda(s_{0}\wedge\dots\wedge s_{k})]\in\operatorname{GW}(K)

is precisely the
{\mathbb{A}}^{1}
-degree of the linear projection
[s_{0},\dots,s_{k}]\colon X\to\mathbb{P}^{k}
, with respect to the above orientation.

We refer to
4.32
for a precise statement. We also note that the symmetric matrix
\Lambda
is explicitly computable from a resolution of the sheaf
\mathcal{F}
, for example via a computer algebra system such as Macaulay2
[
GS
]
or OSCAR
[
OSC24
]
, as already demonstrated in
[
ES03
]
.

With these results in hand, we pass to the second main theme of the paper, an
{\mathbb{A}}^{1}
-analogue of knot theory. Classical knots can be studied via
knot diagrams
. These are planar projections of the knot, which, for each point where two arcs cross, keeps track of the over- and under-passing arc, see e.g.
Figure

1
.
The
local writhe
of a crossing in a knot diagram is a number in
\{-1,+1\}
and is defined as in
Figure

2
.

Figure 2.
Local writhe numbers.

Note that one has to choose an orientation of the knot but the local writhe numbers are independent of this choice.
The
writhe
of a knot diagram is then the sum of the local writhe numbers of all its crossings. For example the writhe of the knot diagram in
Figure

1
is equal to
+3
.

Figure 3.
When applying a Reidemeister move of type I to a real algebraic curve, a crossing of two arcs becomes an isolated node.

While the writhe is unaffected by Reidemeister moves of type II and III, a Reidemeister move of type I changes the writhe by
\pm 1
. In particular, the writhe is only an invariant of the knot diagram but not of the knot itself. When the knot is the real part of an algebraic curve
C\subset\mathbb{P}^{3}
, this issue corresponds to the fact that some plane projections might have isolated real nodes, see
Figure

3
.
Viro
[
Vir01
]
showed that the problem can be circumvented by also assigning a local writhe to
isolated
real nodes of a planar projection of the algebraic curve: he defined the
encomplexed writhe number
as the sum over all local writhe numbers, including those at isolated nodes, and proved that this is indeed independent of the projection center, hence it is an invariant of the real curve
C\subset\mathbb{P}^{3}
. This invariant played a major role in the recent breakthrough on real algebraic links by Mikhalkin and Orevkov
[
MO19
]
.

We generalize Viro’s encomplexed writhe to any field
K
via Ulrich bundles on secant varieties.
This is natural, since the nodes of a linear projection
\pi\colon C\to\mathbb{P}^{2}
corresponds to the secant lines to
C
passing through the center of the projection. Let us be more precise here. If
\psi\colon C\hookrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{3}
is a non-degenerate embedding and
L=\psi^{*}\mathcal{O}_{C}(1)
the corresponding line bundle, then the embedding
\psi
is obtained from composing the embedding of the complete linear system
\varphi_{L}\colon C\hookrightarrow\mathbb{P}(H^{0}(C,L)^{\vee})=\mathbb{P}^{n}
with a linear projection
[s_{0},s_{1},s_{2},s_{3}]\colon\varphi_{L}(C)\to\mathbb{P}^{3}
. In order for
\psi
to be an embedding, the sections
s_{0},\dots,s_{3}\in H^{0}(C,L)
must be linearly independent, and the space
\Pi=\{s_{0}=\dots=s_{3}\}
should not intersect the secant variety
\Sigma=\Sigma(C,L)
of
\varphi_{L}(C)
in
\mathbb{P}^{n}
. In this setting, the induced projection
[s_{0},\dots,s_{3}]\colon\Sigma\to\mathbb{P}^{3}
is a finite and surjective map. Assume now that the secant variety
\Sigma
is
identifiable
in the sense that every point in
\Sigma\setminus\varphi_{L}(C)
is contained in a unique secant line. Then, by construction, the fiber of
[s_{0},\dots,s_{3}]\colon\Sigma\to\mathbb{P}^{3}
over a point
q\in\mathbb{P}^{3}
correspond to secant lines to
\psi(C)
passing through
q
.
Thus, if we can find a suitable orientation, the
{\mathbb{A}}^{1}
-degree of this map gives an arithmetic count of secant lines through
q
, or, equivalently, an arithmetic count of nodes of the projection of
\psi(C)
from
q
. We can actually find a suitable orientation via an Ulrich sheaf:

Theorem C
.

Let
C
be a smooth
K
-curve and
L
be a line bundle such that the secant variety
\Sigma
of
\varphi_{L}\colon C\hookrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{n}
is identifiable. Let also
\alpha
be a non-effective theta characteristic on
C
, i.e., a line bundle such that
\alpha\otimes\alpha\cong\omega_{C}
and
h^{0}(C,\alpha)=0
.
Then there is a symmetric Ulrich sheaf of rank one
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}
on
\Sigma
, depending on
\alpha
.

With such a symmetric Ulrich sheaf we can then use Theorem B, and give the following definition:

Definition

(Arithmetic writhe)
.

In the above setting, the
arithmetic writhe

\operatorname{w}(\psi(C),\alpha)\in\operatorname{GW}(K)

of the embedded curve
\psi\colon C\hookrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{3}
and of the theta characteristic
\alpha
, is the
{\mathbb{A}}^{1}
-degree of the map

[s_{0},\dots,s_{3}]\colon\Sigma\to\mathbb{P}^{3},

with respect to the relative orientation induced by
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}
. Here
[s_{0},\dots,s_{3}]
is such that
\psi=[s_{0},\dots,s_{3}]\circ\varphi_{L}
.

By the previous discussion, the arithmetic writhe is an arithmetically enriched count of the nodes of plane projections
\psi(C)\to\mathbb{P}^{2}
. The fact that the arithmetic writhe is well-defined as an
{\mathbb{A}}^{1}
-degree, means precisely that this does not depend on the center of the plane projection. We also provide an explicit local description of the arithmetic writhe which for real curves agrees with the local description of Viro’s encomplexed writhe. Hence, for real curves, the arithmetic writhe is equal to the encomplexed writhe.

Observe that the appearance of a non-effective theta characteristic
\alpha
on
C
is not surprising: indeed these correspond on the one hand to symmetric rank one Ulrich sheaves on the curve itself (see
[
ES03
, Theorem 4.3]
), and, on the other hand, if the curve is real, to classical orientations, see
4.5
. Over an arbitrary field, every smooth rational curve has a non-effective theta characteristic. We also remark that the condition of identifiability on the secant variety of
\varphi_{L}\colon C\hookrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{n}
is rather natural and general. For example, Riemann–Roch shows that this happens as soon as the degree
\deg(C)
is at least
2g(C)+3
, where
g(C)
is the genus of the curve. In particular, this is true for any smooth rational curve of degree at least
3
. Another important case are canonical curves of gonality at least
5
, such as any general real or complex curve in the moduli space
\mathcal{M}_{g},g\geq 7
.
We note that, in a similar spirit, Lemarié–Rieusset
[
LRar
]
has defined an arithmetic linking degree for two embeddings of
{\mathbb{A}}^{2}\setminus\{0\}
to
{\mathbb{A}}^{4}\setminus\{0\}
using motivic Seifert classes. On the other hand, to our knowledge, the arithmetic writhe is the first
knot
invariant defined in the
{\mathbb{A}}^{1}
-context.

In the last part of the paper, we define and study
algebraic isotopies
between embeddings of a smooth projective curve
C
in
3
-space in analogy with topological isotopies: They are morphisms

I\colon{\mathbb{A}}^{1}\times C\to\mathbb{P}^{3},\qquad(t,x)\mapsto I_{t}(x)

such that
I_{t}\colon C_{\kappa(t)}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{3}_{\kappa(t)}
is an embedding for all
t\in{\mathbb{A}}^{1}
. Two embeddings
\psi,\psi^{\prime}\colon C\hookrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{3}
are algebraically isotopic if they can be connected by a chain of algebraic isotopies. We extend our definition of the arithmetic writhe
\operatorname{w}(\psi(C),\alpha)
for an embedded curve
\psi\colon C\hookrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{3}
to a definition of the
arithmetic writhe

\operatorname{w}(\psi,\alpha)
for the embedding
\psi
itself, and we show that this is invariant under algebraic isotopies, see
8.3
. We use it to study algebraic isotopy classes of rational curves of low degree in
\mathbb{P}^{3}
.

Theorem D
.

Let
K
be a field of
\operatorname{char}(K)\neq 2
.

(1)

If
d\in\{1,2\}
, then any two embeddings
\psi,\psi^{\prime}\colon\mathbb{P}^{1}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{3}
over
K
of degree
d
are algebraically isotopic.

(2)

The set of algebraic isotopy classes of embeddings
\mathbb{P}^{1}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{3}
over
K
of degree
3
is in bijection to
K^{\times}/K^{\times 4}
.

(3)

Two embeddings
\psi,\psi^{\prime}\colon\mathbb{P}^{1}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{3}
over
K
of degree
4
are algebraically isotopic if and only if they have the same arithmetic writhe.

In the case
K={\mathbb{R}}
, Theorem D strengthens a result by Björklund
[
Bjö11
]
who proved that the encomplexed writhe characterizes real rational curves of degree at most four up to
rigid isotopy
, a (potentially strictly) coarser equivalence relation than algebraic isotopy. While Björklund’s result holds true in degree five as well, we do not know whether this is the case for our Theorem D. It was also shown in
[
Bjö11
]
that the encomplexed writhe is not enough to distinguish rigid isotopy classes of embeddings
\mathbb{P}^{1}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{3}
of degree at least six, hence the corresponding statement also fails for the arithmetic writhe and algebraic isotopies. However, Ulrich sheaves other than those produced in Theorem C can be used to give further isotopy invariants. In
5.18
, we construct a higher rank Ulrich sheaf on the secant variety of a rational normal curve. From this we obtain a new invariant for algebraic isotopy classes of rational curves of degree six, and we construct an example of two embeddings of the same arithmetic writhe for which the new invariant is different, see
8.8
. We do not know whether this is enough to distinguish all isotopy classes, nor do we have a local description as for the arithmetic writhe.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
3
, we collect some facts on Ulrich sheaves, and in Section
4
we show how they are connected to the
{\mathbb{A}}^{1}
-degree, proving Theorem A in
4.17
and Theorem B in both
4.28
and
4.32
. In Section
5
, we turn our attention on secants of curves and on Ulrich sheaves on them: we prove Theorem C in
5.16
and we also construct in
5.18
higher rank Ulrich sheaves on secants of rational normal curves. In Section
6
, we define the arithmetic writhe for an embedded curve, and we also show how to compute it via a sum of explicit local writhes, see Remark
6.2
. Finally, in Section
7
we collect some general results about isotopies for projective embeddings, which imply the first two parts of Theorem D. In Section
8
we focus on the case of curves in
3
-space. We define the arithmetic writhe of an embedding in
\mathbb{P}^{3}
and we prove that it is invariant under algebraic isotopies, see
8.3
. We then prove Theorem D in Theorem
8.4
and Corollary
8.5
, and we conclude with some remarks on rational curves of higher degree.

3.
Ulrich sheaves

We first recall the basic properties of Ulrich sheaves as introduced in
[
ES03
]
. Consider the projective space
\mathbb{P}^{n}
and the corresponding homogeneous coordinate ring
S=K[x_{0},\dots,x_{n}]
. Let
\mathcal{F}
be a coherent sheaf on
\mathbb{P}^{n}
with scheme-theoretic support
\iota\colon X\hookrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{n}
of pure dimension
k>0
and codimension
c=n-k
. Let also
\Gamma_{*}(\mathcal{F})=\oplus H^{0}(\mathcal{F}(q))
be the module of twisted global sections, seen as a graded
S
-module. The following important equivalence was proven in
[
ES03
, Theorem 2.1]
:

Definition 3.1

(Ulrich sheaf)
.

The sheaf
\mathcal{F}
is called an
Ulrich sheaf
if it satisfies one of the following equivalent conditions:

(i)

\Gamma_{*}(\mathcal{F})
has a linear minimal free resolution as an
S
-module:

\mathbf{F}:\qquad 0\longrightarrow F_{c}\overset{\varphi_{c}}{\longrightarrow}F_{c-1}\overset{\varphi_{c-1}}{\longrightarrow}\dots\overset{\varphi_{2}}{\longrightarrow}F_{1}\overset{\varphi_{1}}{\longrightarrow}F_{0}\longrightarrow\mathcal{F}\longrightarrow 0

where
F_{i}
is a direct sum of copies of
S(-i)
.

(ii)

H^{i}(X,{\mathcal{F}}(-i))=0
for
i>0
and
H^{i}(X,{\mathcal{F}}(-i-1)=0
for
i<k
.

(iii)

if
\pi\colon X\to\mathbb{P}^{k}
is a finite surjective linear projection then
\pi_{*}\iota^{*}{\mathcal{F}}\cong{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{P}^{k}}^{\oplus t}
for a certain
t>0
.

The resolution of
\mathcal{F}
can be used to compute a
Chow form
of
X
. Recall that this is a polynomial in the Plücker coordinates of the Grassmannian
{\mathbb{G}}(c-1,n)
that cuts out the locus of
(c-1)
-planes that intersect
X
. In the coordinate ring of
{\mathbb{G}}(c-1,n)
it is unique up to a scalar factor. A power of the Chow form can be written as the determinant of a matrix with entries linear forms in the Plücker coordinates
[
ES03
, §3]
. This is constructed from the resolution
\mathbf{F}
as follows: after choosing a basis of each
F_{i}
the maps
\varphi_{i}
are given by matrices
A_{i}
whose entries are linear forms in the variables
x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}
. We consider these linear forms as degree one elements of the tensor algebra
\textnormal{T}(K^{n+1})^{\vee}
and define
\gamma(\textbf{F})
to be the product
A_{1}\cdots A_{c}
over
\textnormal{T}(K^{n+1})^{\vee}
. The entries of
\gamma(\textbf{F})
are multilinear forms on
K^{n+1}
and since
\varphi_{i}\circ\varphi_{i+1}=0
these multilinear forms are alternating. Therefore, the entries of
\gamma(\textbf{F})
are elements of
\wedge^{c}(K^{n+1})^{\vee}
and thus linear in the Plücker coordinates of
{\mathbb{G}}(c-1,n)
. Evaluating
\gamma(\textbf{F})
at a
(c-1)
-plane
V\subset\mathbb{P}^{n}
gives a singular matrix if and only if
V
intersects
X
. This implies that the determinant of
\gamma(\textbf{F})
is the
\operatorname{rank}({\mathcal{F}})
-th power of the Chow form of
X
. It follows from the definition of the matrix
\gamma(\textbf{F})
that it actually depends only on the choice of bases of
F_{0}
and
F_{c}
. Thus
\gamma(\textbf{F})
is uniquely determined by
{\mathcal{F}}
up to multiplication from left and right by invertible matrices over
K
. Finally, if there exists a symmetric isomorphism
{\mathcal{F}}\to\operatorname{{\mathscr{E}xt}}^{c}({\mathcal{F}},{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{P}^{n}})(-c)
, then by choosing a suitable basis the determinantal representation can be made symmetric
[
ES03
, §3.1]
. We will come back to the symmetric case later.

4.
Ulrich sheaves and the
\mathbb{A}^{1}
-degree

In this section we discuss a connection of Ulrich sheaves and a recently developed notion of degree in the context of
{\mathbb{A}}^{1}
-enumerative geometry.

4.1.
Orientations and
\mathbb{A}^{1}
-degree

In this subsection we recall some preliminaries from
{\mathbb{A}}^{1}
-enumerative geometry, mostly following the exposition of
[
PW21
, §8]
. Let
K
always denote a field.

Definition 4.1

(Algebraic orientation)
.

Let
X
be a non-singular
K
-variety. An
algebraic orientation
of
X
is an isomorphism
L\otimes L\to\omega_{X}
where
L
is a line bundle on
X
and
\omega_{X}
the canonical sheaf. We also say that
X
is
oriented
by
L\otimes L\to\omega_{X}
. A
theta characteristic
of
X
is a line bundle
L
such that
L\otimes L
is isomorphic to
\omega_{X}
.

Example 4.2
.

If
X=\mathbb{P}^{n}
with
n=2h-1
odd, then
{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{P}^{n}}(-h)
is a theta characteristic of
X
. If
n
is even, then
\mathbb{P}^{n}
does not have a theta characteristic.

Definition 4.3
.

Let
X
be a non-singular
K
-variety and
L
a theta characteristic. Two algebraic orientations
\psi_{1},\psi_{2}\colon L\otimes L\to\omega_{X}
are
equivalent
if
\psi_{1}\circ\psi_{2}^{-1}
is multiplication by a square of a global section of
{\mathcal{O}}_{X}
.

Remark 4.4
.

Let
K={\mathbb{R}}
and
X
be a non-singular
{\mathbb{R}}
-variety of dimension
n
. Let
\psi\colon L\otimes L\to\omega_{X}
be an algebraic orientation. Let
f
some rational section of
L
and
\omega=\psi(f\otimes f)
. All zeros and poles of the rational differential
n
-form
\omega
on
X
are of even order. It can be shown that this implies the existence of a rational function
g
on
X
which is nonnegative wherever it is defined on
X({\mathbb{R}})
and such that
g\cdot\omega
does not have any zeros or poles on
X({\mathbb{R}})
. Thus
g\cdot\omega
defines an orientation on
X({\mathbb{R}})
in the classical topological sense. It is straight-forward to check that this does not depend on the choices we made and that two algebraic orientations that are equivalent in the sense of
4.3
induce the same classical orientation on
X({\mathbb{R}})
.

Example 4.5
.

Let
X
be a curve of genus
g
over
{\mathbb{R}}
. Then every topological orientation of
X({\mathbb{R}})
is induced by an algebraic orientation
L\otimes L\to\omega_{X}
. This follows for instance from
[
Gey77
, Satz 2.4.c]
or by the same argument as in
[
Kum19
, Corollary 2.4]
. However, this is not true in general. For instance, let
X
be a real K3 surface: since the Picard group of
X
is torsion-free, there is, up to a scalar multiple, only one algebraic orientation on
X
. Thus only two topological orientations on
X({\mathbb{R}})
arise in this way. However, if
X({\mathbb{R}})
is not connected, then there exist at least four different topological orientations of
X({\mathbb{R}})
.

There is a relative version of algebraic orientations for morphisms
[
PW21
, Definition 7]
:

Definition 4.6

(Relative orientation)
.

Let
f\colon X\to Y
be a finite surjective morphism of non-singular
K
-varieties. A
relative orientation
of
f
is an isomorphism

\psi\colon L\otimes L\to\operatorname{{\mathscr{H}om}}(\det{\mathcal{T}}_{X},f^{*}\det{\mathcal{T}}_{Y})

where
L
is a line bundle on
X
and
{\mathcal{T}}_{X},{\mathcal{T}}_{Y}
are the tangent bundles on
X
and
Y
respectively. Two relative orientations
\psi_{1},\psi_{2}
as above
are
equivalent
if
\psi_{1}\circ\psi_{2}^{-1}
is multiplication by a square of a global section of
{\mathcal{O}}_{X}
.

Remark 4.7
.

If
f\colon X\to Y
is a finite surjective morphism of non-singular
K
-varieties with algebraic orientations
\psi_{1}\colon L_{1}\otimes L_{1}\to\omega_{X}
and
\psi_{2}\colon L_{2}\otimes L_{2}\to\omega_{Y}
, then a relative orientation of
f
is given by the induced isomorphism

\psi\colon(L_{1}\otimes f^{*}L_{2}^{\vee})^{\otimes 2}\to\omega_{X}\otimes f^{*}\omega_{Y}^{\vee}.

Algebraic orientations
\psi_{1}^{\prime}
and
\psi_{2}^{\prime}
on
X
and
Y
that are equivalent to
\psi_{1}
and
\psi_{2}
induce a relative orientation
\psi^{\prime}
for
f
that is equivalent to
\psi
.

Recall the set of equivalence classes of non-degenerate symmetric bilinear forms on finite dimensional vector spaces over
K
form a monoid via the orthogonal sum. The Grothendieck–Witt group
\operatorname{GW}(K)
of
K
is the Grothendieck group of this monoid. It is generated by all equivalence classes
\langle a\rangle
of one-dimensional bilinear forms

K\times K\to K,\quad(x,y)\mapsto axy

for
a\in K^{\times}
. Note that
\langle a\rangle=\langle ab^{2}\rangle
for all
b\in K^{\times}
.

For a finite morphism
f\colon X\to Y
of non-singular oriented
K
-varieties of the same dimension one can define a notion of degree that takes its values in
\operatorname{GW}(K)
under appropriate
hypotheses. Actually it suffices to make the weaker assumption of requiring
f
to have a relative orientation
\psi\colon L\otimes L\to\operatorname{{\mathscr{H}om}}(\det{\mathcal{T}}_{X},f^{*}\det{\mathcal{T}}_{Y})
. In this case, let
x\in X
be a closed point where
f
is not ramified and set
y=f(x)\in Y
. Note that this in particular requires the field extension
\kappa(x)/\kappa(y)
to be separable. The differential of
f
defines a morphism
\operatorname{T}f\colon{\mathcal{T}}_{X}\to f^{*}{\mathcal{T}}_{Y}
and thus
\kappa(x)
-linear maps:

\operatorname{T}_{x}f\colon\operatorname{T}_{x}X\to\operatorname{T}_{y}Y\otimes_{\kappa(y)}\kappa(x),\,\operatorname{J}_{x}f=\det\operatorname{T}_{x}f\colon\det\operatorname{T}_{x}X\to\det\operatorname{T}_{y}Y\otimes_{\kappa(y)}\kappa(x)

Two bases of
\operatorname{T}_{x}X
and
\operatorname{T}_{y}Y
are called compatible with respect to the relative orientation if the determinant of the linear map that sends one base to the other is an element in the fiber of
\operatorname{{\mathscr{H}om}}(\det{\mathcal{T}}_{X},f^{*}\det{\mathcal{T}}_{Y})
at
x
which is the image of a square under
\psi

[
PW21
, Definition 8]
. After choosing such compatible bases, we can identify the determinant
\operatorname{J}_{x}f
with an element in
\kappa(x)
. Thanks to the requirement of compatibility, it is straight-forward to check that the class
\langle\operatorname{J}_{x}f\rangle\in\operatorname{GW}(\kappa(x))
does not depend on the bases. We can also define
\langle\operatorname{J}_{x}f\rangle
without mentioning bases as follows: taking the fiber of the orientation at
x
we see that
\operatorname{J}_{x}f=g\cdot\psi(t\otimes t)
, for a certain
t\in L_{x}
and
g\in\kappa(x)
. Then
\langle\operatorname{J}_{x}f\rangle=\langle g\rangle
.

Then the
local
{\mathbb{A}}^{1}
-degree
\deg_{x}^{{\mathbb{A}}^{1}}(f)
of
f
at
x
as the class in
\operatorname{GW}(\kappa(y))
of the symmetric
K
-bilinear form defined by

(1)

\operatorname{Tr}_{\kappa(x)/\kappa(y)}\langle\operatorname{J}_{x}f\rangle\colon\,\kappa(x)\times\kappa(x)\to\kappa(y),\,(a,b)\mapsto\operatorname{Tr}_{\kappa(x)/\kappa(y)}(\operatorname{J}_{x}ab).

Finally, for a closed point
y\in Y
outside the branch locus of
f
, one defines the
{\mathbb{A}}^{1}
-degree
\deg_{y}^{{\mathbb{A}}^{1}}(f)
of
f
at
y
as

\deg_{y}^{{\mathbb{A}}^{1}}(f)=\sum_{x\in f^{-1}(y)}\deg_{x}^{{\mathbb{A}}^{1}}(f)\in\operatorname{GW}(\kappa(y)).

Remark 4.8
.

One could argue that
Equation

1
is rather a
formula
for the local
{\mathbb{A}}^{1}
degree
[
Mor06
,
KW21
]
than a definition.

Remark 4.9
.

The (local)
{\mathbb{A}}^{1}
-degree only depends on the equivalence class of the relative orientation.

Remark 4.10
.

In the case
K={\mathbb{R}}
the signature of the
{\mathbb{A}}^{1}
-degree of
f\colon X\to Y
is the topological degree of the restriction of
f
to the real parts of
X
and
Y
. This is clear from the description of the local degree in
Equation

1
together with the observation that every scaled trace bilinear form
{\mathbb{C}}\times{\mathbb{C}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
has signature zero.

Definition 4.11
.

Let
f\colon X\to Y
be a relatively oriented finite surjective morphism of non-singular
K
-varieties. If there is an element
d\in\operatorname{GW}(K)
such that for every closed point
y\in Y
outside the branch locus we have that

\deg_{y}^{{\mathbb{A}}^{1}}(f)=d\otimes_{K}\kappa(y)\in\operatorname{GW}(\kappa(y)),

then we say that
\deg^{{\mathbb{A}}^{1}}(f)
is
well-defined
and we write
\deg^{{\mathbb{A}}^{1}}(f)=d
.

Remark 4.12
.

Note that in the situation of
4.11
writing
\deg^{{\mathbb{A}}^{1}}(f)=d
is a slight abuse of notation since there might be several different

satisfying the requirements from
4.11
— for instance in the case that
Y
has no
K
-rational points.

The degree of a morphism is not always well-defined. An important case where this happens is when the base of the morphism is
{\mathbb{A}}^{1}
chain-connected:

Example 4.13
.

Let
f\colon X\to Y
as in
4.11
.

(1)

If
Y
is
{\mathbb{A}}^{1}
-chain connected
in the sense of
[
PW21
, Definition 9]
, then
\deg^{{\mathbb{A}}^{1}}(f)
is well-defined by
[
PW21
, Theorem 8]
. This remains true under the potentially weaker hypothesis that

is
{\mathbb{A}}^{1}
-connected

[
KLSW23
, §2.5]
.

(2)

If

, then it suffices that

is connected in the classical topology because the (signature of the)

-degree is the Brouwer degree of the map

.

(3)

In general

is not necessarily well-defined, see for example
[

, Example 16]
.

4.2.
Relative Ulrich sheaves and the
\mathbb{A}^{1}
-degree

We now derive a further case when
\deg^{{\mathbb{A}}^{1}}(f)
is well-defined which is a condition on the relative orientation rather than on the target. Motivated by
3.1
(iii) one makes the following definition.

Definition 4.14
.

Let
f\colon X\to Y
a morphism of schemes. A coherent sheaf
{\mathcal{F}}
on
X
is called
f
-Ulrich
if there exists an integer
t>0
such that
f_{*}{\mathcal{F}}\cong{\mathcal{O}}_{Y}^{\oplus t}
.

We further recall the following construction of a trace map for differential forms.

Construction 4.15
.

[
Har83
, Exercise III.7.2]

Let
f\colon X\to Y
be a finite surjective morphism of non-singular varieties over
K
. We recall the construction of a natural trace map
t\colon f_{*}\omega_{X}\to\omega_{Y}
. First let
X=\operatorname{Spec}(B)
and
Y=\operatorname{Spec}(A)
be affine,
\dim(X)=\dim(Y)=n
and
E=\operatorname{Quot}(A)
,
F=\operatorname{Quot}(B)
the fields of fractions. Choose any nonzero
\omega\in\wedge^{n}\Omega_{A/K}
. Then, for any
\omega^{\prime}\in\wedge^{n}\Omega_{B/K}
, there is a
b\in F
such that
\omega^{\prime}=b\cdot\omega
. The trace map sends
\omega^{\prime}
to
t(\omega^{\prime})=\operatorname{tr}_{F/E}(b)\cdot\omega
. Note that while
b
is not necessarily in
B
, we always have that
t(\omega^{\prime})\in\wedge^{n}\Omega_{A/K}
. In the general, not necessarily affine case the map
t
is obtained by glueing.

Now let
f\colon X\to Y
be a finite surjective morphism of non-singular
K
-varieties, relatively oriented by the isomorphism

\psi\colon L\otimes L\to\operatorname{{\mathscr{H}om}}(\det{\mathcal{T}}_{X},f^{*}\det{\mathcal{T}}_{Y})=\omega_{X}\otimes f^{*}\omega_{Y}^{-1}.

By the projection formula the push-forward of
\psi
induces a map

f_{*}L\otimes f_{*}L\to f_{*}\omega_{X}\otimes\omega_{Y}^{-1}.

Tensoring the trace morphism from
4.15
by
\omega_{Y}^{-1}
we obtain a morphism
f_{*}\omega_{X}\otimes\omega_{Y}^{-1}\to{\mathcal{O}}_{Y}
which we precompose with the map above to obtain a symmetric
\mathcal{O}_{Y}
-bilinear form
\tilde{\psi}\colon f_{*}L\otimes f_{*}L\to{\mathcal{O}}_{Y}
. The following simple lemma can also be seen as a consequence of
[
KLSW23
, Corollary 3.10]
, but we give here a self-contained proof.

Lemma 4.16
.

Let
f\colon X\to Y
be a finite surjective morphism of non-singular
K
-varieties, relatively oriented by the isomorphism

\psi\colon L\otimes L\to\operatorname{{\mathscr{H}om}}(\det{\mathcal{T}}_{X},f^{*}\det{\mathcal{T}}_{Y}).

Let
y\in Y
a closed point outside the branch locus.
The class of the fiber of
\tilde{\psi}
at
y
in
\operatorname{GW}(\kappa(y))
is
\deg_{y}^{{\mathbb{A}}^{1}}(f)
.

Proof.

Let
U\subset Y
an open affine neighborhood of
y
and
V=f^{-1}(U)
. Then we have a finite ring extension
A\subset B
where
A={\mathcal{O}}_{Y}(U)
and
B={\mathcal{O}}_{X}(V)
. If we choose
U
sufficiently small, then
\Omega_{A/K}
is a free
A
-module and
\Omega_{B/K}
and
M=L(V)
are free
B
-modules. Since
y
is not in the branch locus of
f
we can, after further shrinking
U
if necessary, moreover assume that
\Omega_{B/K}=\Omega_{A/K}\otimes_{A}B
. Then there are
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\in A
such that
da_{1},\ldots,da_{n}
is an
A
-basis of
\Omega_{A/K}
and
da_{1}\otimes 1,\ldots,da_{n}\otimes 1
is a
B
-basis of
\Omega_{B/K}
. Let
v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}\in\Omega_{A/K}^{\vee}
and
w_{1},\ldots,w_{n}\in\Omega_{B/K}^{\vee}
the dual bases so that
\operatorname{T}_{x}f(w_{i})=v_{i}
for all
x\in V
, and let
t\in M
a generator of
M
. Then

\psi(t\otimes t)=g\cdot\left((da_{1}\otimes 1)\wedge\cdots\wedge(da_{n}\otimes 1)\right)\otimes(v_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge v_{n})

for some
g\in B^{\times}
. Then it follows that
\langle\operatorname{J}_{x_{i}}f\rangle=\langle g(x_{i})\rangle
.
For
s_{1},s_{2}\in M
we can write
s_{i}=h_{i}\cdot t
for
i=1,2
and
h_{i}\in B
. By the definition of
\tilde{\psi}
we then have

(2)

\tilde{\psi}(s_{1},s_{2})=\operatorname{Tr}_{B/A}(g\cdot h_{1}\cdot h_{2}).

Since
B\otimes_{A}\kappa(y)=\kappa(x_{1})\oplus\cdots\oplus\kappa(x_{r})
,
Equation

2
evaluated at
y
equals

\sum_{i=1}^{r}\operatorname{Tr}_{\kappa(x_{i})/\kappa(y)}(g(x_{i})\cdot h_{1}(x_{i})\cdot h_{2}(x_{i})).

Therefore, the class of the fiber of
\tilde{\psi}
at
y
equals

\sum_{i=1}^{r}\operatorname{Tr}_{\kappa(x_{i})/\kappa(y)}\langle g(x_{i})\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\operatorname{Tr}_{\kappa(x_{i})/\kappa(y)}\langle\operatorname{J}_{x_{i}}f\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\deg_{x_{i}}^{{\mathbb{A}}^{1}}(f)=\deg_{y}^{{\mathbb{A}}^{1}}(f)

where we use that
\langle g(x_{i})\rangle=\langle\operatorname{J}_{x_{i}}f\rangle
.
∎

Passing from
\tilde{\psi}\colon f_{*}L\otimes f_{*}L\to{\mathcal{O}}_{Y}
to global sections, we obtain a symmetric bilinear form

\bar{\psi}\colon H^{0}(Y,f_{*}L)\times H^{0}(Y,f_{*}L)\to H^{0}(Y,{\mathcal{O}}_{Y}).

In the case that
H^{0}(Y,{\mathcal{O}}_{Y})=K
, this is a symmetric
K
-valued bilinear form.

Theorem 4.17
.

Let
f\colon X\to Y
be a finite surjective morphism of non-singular
K
-varieties, relatively oriented by the isomorphism

\psi\colon L\otimes L\to\operatorname{{\mathscr{H}om}}(\det{\mathcal{T}}_{X},f^{*}\det{\mathcal{T}}_{Y}).

If
L
is
f
-Ulrich and
H^{0}(Y,{\mathcal{O}}_{Y})=K
, then the
K
-bilinear form

\bar{\psi}\colon H^{0}(Y,f_{*}L)\times H^{0}(Y,f_{*}L)\to K.

is non-degenerate. In this case
\deg^{{\mathbb{A}}^{1}}(f)
is well-defined and equals to the class of
\bar{\psi}
.

Proof.

At every
y\in Y
the fiber of a
K
-basis of
H^{0}(Y,f_{*}L)
is a
\kappa(y)
basis of the fiber of
f_{*}L
at
y
because
L
is
f
-Ulrich. Therefore, the fiber of
\tilde{\psi}
at
y
is isometric to
\bar{\psi}\otimes_{K}\kappa(y)
. Now the claim follows from
4.16
.
∎

Example 4.18
.

Let
X
be a smooth projective curve and
f\colon X\to\mathbb{P}^{1}
a finite surjective morphism. Every relative orientation of
f
is given by a line bundle of the form
L=M(1)
where
M
is a theta characteristic on
X
. Then
L
is
f
-Ulrich if and only if
h^{0}(M)=0
. In particular, for
X=\mathbb{P}^{1}
every relative orientation is given by an Ulrich line bundle.

One has the following converse of
4.16
.

Lemma 4.19
.

Let
f\colon X\to Y
be a finite surjective morphism of non-singular complete
K
-varieties, relatively oriented by the isomorphism

\psi\colon L\otimes L\to\operatorname{{\mathscr{H}om}}(\det{\mathcal{T}}_{X},f^{*}\det{\mathcal{T}}_{Y})

and assume that
Y
is geometrically irreducible. The following are equivalent:

(1)

L
is
f
-Ulrich.

(2)

\bar{\psi}
is non-degenerate and for every closed point
y\in Y
one has

\deg_{y}^{{\mathbb{A}}^{1}}(f)=\bar{\psi}\otimes_{K}\kappa(y)\in\operatorname{GW}(\kappa(y)).

Proof.

4.17
proves that
(1)
implies
(2)
. On the other hand, condition
(2)
implies that
\bar{\psi}
is non-degenerate and for
y\in Y

\dim(H^{0}(X,L))=\operatorname{rank}(\bar{\psi})=\operatorname{rank}(\deg_{y}^{{\mathbb{A}}^{1}}(f))=\deg(f).

Therefore, by
[
HK24
, Theorem 4.8]
the line bundle
L
is
f
-Ulrich.
∎

We conclude this section with two examples showing that the two sufficient conditions for the
{\mathbb{A}}^{1}
-degree being well-defined, that we have seen here, do not imply each other.

Example 4.20
.

Consider a smooth plane cubic curve
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{2}
and let
f\colon X\to\mathbb{P}^{1}
the linear projection from a point not on
X
. Then
f
is relatively oriented by

\psi\colon{\mathcal{O}}_{X}(1)\otimes{\mathcal{O}}_{X}(1)\to{\mathcal{O}}_{X}(2)\cong\operatorname{{\mathscr{H}om}}(\det{\mathcal{T}}_{X},f^{*}\det{\mathcal{T}}_{\mathbb{P}^{1}}).

However, the line bundle
{\mathcal{O}}_{X}(1)
is not
f
-Ulrich because it violates part (ii) of
3.1
. Since
\dim(H^{0}(X,{\mathcal{O}}_{X}(1))=3=\deg(f)
, this implies in particular that
\bar{\psi}
cannot be a non-degenerate bilinear form. On the other hand, the target
\mathbb{P}^{1}
is clearly
{\mathbb{A}}^{1}
-chain connected.

Example 4.21
.

Assume here that the characteristic of
K
is not
2
or
3
, and consider the smooth sextic curve
X
in
\mathbb{P}^{3}
defined over
{\mathbb{Q}}
by the two equations

Q=x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}-x_{3}^{2}\textnormal{ and }T=x_{0}^{3}+x_{1}^{3}+x_{2}^{3}

and the smooth cubic curve
Y
in
\mathbb{P}^{2}
defined by
T
. The linear projection from the point
[0,0,0,1]
defines a finite surjective morphism
f\colon X\to Y
of degree two.
The ramification divisor of
f
is the zero divisor of
x_{3}
on
X
. This shows that

\operatorname{{\mathscr{H}om}}(\det{\mathcal{T}}_{X},f^{*}\det{\mathcal{T}}_{Y})\cong{\mathcal{O}}_{X}(1).

Furthermore, the zero divisor of
x_{2}-x_{3}
on
X
is of the form
2D
where
D
is an effective divisor of degree three because this hyperplane intersects the singular quadric in
\mathbb{P}^{3}
defined by
Q
in a line with multiplicity two. Thus the corresponding line bundle
L
satisfies
L\otimes L\cong{\mathcal{O}}_{X}(1)
. In particular, we obtain a relative orientation

L\otimes L\to\operatorname{{\mathscr{H}om}}(\det{\mathcal{T}}_{X},f^{*}\det{\mathcal{T}}_{Y})

of
f
. A calculation with the computer algebra system
Macaulay2

[
GS
]
further shows that
f_{*}L={\mathcal{O}}_{Y}^{\oplus 2}
. Thus
\deg^{{\mathbb{A}}^{1}}(f)
is well-defined by
4.17
. On the other hand, by Lüroth’s theorem there is no non-constant morphism
{\mathbb{A}}^{1}\to Y
and therefore
Y
is not
{\mathbb{A}}^{1}
-chain connected (actually not even
{\mathbb{A}}^{1}
-connected
[
AM11
, Corollary 2.4.4]
).
Let us compute the degree of this map using the method of Theorem
4.17
. The quadratic form
\bar{\psi}
is given by the composition:

\displaystyle H^{0}(X,\mathcal{O}_{X}(D))\otimes H^{0}(X,\mathcal{O}_{X}(D))

\displaystyle\longrightarrow

\displaystyle H^{0}(X,\mathcal{O}_{X}(2D))

\displaystyle\overset{\sim}{\longrightarrow}

\displaystyle H^{0}(X,\mathcal{O}_{X}(R))

\displaystyle\longrightarrow

\displaystyle H^{0}(Y,\mathcal{O}_{Y})

where the first map is the multiplication map, the second map is given by the linear equivalence between
2D
and the ramification divisor
R=\{x_{3}=0\}
and the last map is the trace. A basis of
H^{0}(X,\mathcal{O}_{X}(D))
is given by the rational functions
1,\frac{x_{1}}{x_{2}-x_{3}}
and multiplying them together we obtain the rational functions
1,\frac{x_{1}}{x_{2}-x_{3}},\left(\frac{x_{1}}{x_{2}-x_{3}}\right)^{2}
. These get mapped to the elements
\frac{x_{2}-x_{3}}{x_{3}},\frac{x_{1}}{x_{3}},\frac{x_{1}^{2}}{(x_{2}-x_{3})x_{3}}
in
H^{0}(X,\mathcal{O}_{X}(R))
, and finally the traces of these are

\operatorname{Tr}\left(\frac{x_{2}-x_{3}}{x_{3}}\right)=\frac{x_{2}-x_{3}}{x_{3}}-\frac{x_{2}+x_{3}}{x_{3}}=\frac{-2x_{3}}{x_{3}}=-2,\quad\operatorname{Tr}\left(\frac{x_{1}}{x_{3}}\right)=\frac{x_{1}}{x_{3}}-\frac{x_{1}}{x_{3}}=0\\
\operatorname{Tr}\left(\frac{x_{1}^{2}}{(x_{2}-x_{3})x_{3}}\right)=\frac{x_{1}^{2}}{(x_{2}-x_{3})x_{3}}-\frac{x_{1}^{2}}{(x_{2}+x_{3})x_{3}}=\frac{2x_{1}^{2}}{x_{2}^{2}-x_{3}^{2}}=-2

where the last equality comes from the identity
x_{1}^{2}=x_{3}^{2}-x_{2}^{2}
on
X
. In conclusion, the quadratic form is given by
(\lambda,\mu)\mapsto-2\lambda^{2}-2\mu^{2}
so that the
\mathbb{A}^{1}
-degree is
\langle-2\rangle+\langle-2\rangle
.

4.3.
Symmetric Ulrich sheaves

Let us first set up some notation. We let
S=\Gamma_{*}({\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{P}^{n}})=\oplus_{j\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\Gamma(\mathbb{P}^{n},{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{P}^{n}}(j))
. If
M
is a graded
S
-module, we denote by
\tilde{M}
the sheaf associated to
M
on
\mathbb{P}^{n}
. If
\varphi\colon M\to N
is a homomorphism of graded
S
-modules, then we denote by
\tilde{\varphi}\colon\tilde{M}\to\tilde{N}
the associated morphism of quasi-coherent sheaves on
\mathbb{P}^{n}
. One has

{\mathcal{F}}=\widetilde{\Gamma_{*}({\mathcal{F}})}

for any quasi-coherent sheaf
{\mathcal{F}}
on
\mathbb{P}^{n}

[
Har83
, Proposition II.5.15]
. Conversely, if
M
is a finitely generated graded
S
-module of
0pt(M)\geq 2
, one also has
M=\Gamma_{*}(\tilde{M})

[
Eis05
, Corollary A.1.13]
. This applies in particular when
M
is Cohen–Macaulay of dimension at least
two.

Now let
{\mathcal{F}}
be an Ulrich sheaf on
\mathbb{P}^{n}
whose support is a closed subvariety
\iota\colon X\hookrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{n}
of dimension at least one. Let us denote the rank of
\iota^{*}{\mathcal{F}}
by
r
and by
M=\Gamma_{*}({\mathcal{F}})
the module of twisted global sections over
S
. As explained in
Section

3
we can construct from
{\mathcal{F}}
a matrix
\gamma(\textbf{F})
obtained from the free resolution
F
of
M
whose entries are linear forms in Plücker coordinates and whose determinant is the
r
-th power of the Chow form of
X
. We now recall from
[
ES03
, §3.1]
a condition for this matrix to be symmetric. Consider the contravariant functor

(3)

D\colon{\mathcal{G}}\mapsto\operatorname{{\mathscr{E}xt}}^{c}({\mathcal{G}},{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{P}^{n}}(-c))

where
c
is the codimension of

is Ulrich supported on

We will now recall a more explicit description of a minimal free resolution of

. To this end recall the following definition.

Definition 4.22
.

Let
A=(A_{1},\ldots,A_{s})
be a tuple of pairwise commuting

matrices over a ring

. We can define on

the structure of an

R[t_{1},\ldots,t_{s}]
-module

by letting

act on

via multiplication with the matrix
A_{i}
from the left.
Consider the complex

where
\operatorname{\bf K}(t)
is the Koszul complex of the sequence
t=(t_{1},\ldots,t_{s})
. We can view this complex as a complex of
R
-modules instead of
R[t_{1},\ldots,t_{s}]
-modules.
The resulting complex of free
R
-modules is called
the Koszul complex associated to the matrices

A_{1},\ldots,A_{s}

and we denote it by
\operatorname{\bf K}(A)
.
The maps of
\operatorname{\bf K}(A)
are obtained from the maps of the Koszul complex

by replacing every occurrence of

by

for

.

Let

be the dimension of

and

be sections that do not have a common zero on

. Then the morphism

defines a finite surjective morphism. Let
s_{0},\ldots,s_{n}
a basis of

and consider the inclusion

of graded polynomial rings. Because

is an Ulrich sheaf, it holds that
\pi_{*}\mathcal{F}\cong\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{k}}^{\oplus t}
, so that there is an isomorphism

\psi\colon M\to R^{m}

of
R
-modules where

. Under this identification multiplication by

can be represented by an

matrix

with entries from

for

. Let

Theorem 4.23
.

The Koszul complex
\operatorname{\bf K}(A)
associated to the matrices

is a minimal free resolution of

. In particular, we can describe

as the cokernel of the matrix
(A_{k+1}\cdots A_{n})
and
\operatorname{Ext}_{S}^{c}(M,S)(-c)
as the cokernel of
(A^{t}_{k+1}\cdots A^{t}_{n})
.

Proof.

See
[
Kum16
, Proof of Theorem 6.2.5]
or
[
KS20
, Remark 4.8]
.
∎

Remark 4.24
.

Note that, since the Koszul complex is self-dual,

also implies that a minimal free resolution of

Corollary 4.25

There is a natural isomorphism

\operatorname{Ext}_{S}^{c}(M,S)(-c)\cong\operatorname{Hom}_{R}(M,R)

of

-modules. Here the

-module structure on

and

Under the identification

∎

Lemma 4.26

(2)

5.
Secant varieties of curves

In this section we prove some cohomological statements on the secant variety that we will need later to construct Ulrich bundles. Furthermore, we define Viro frames on (the desingularization of) the secant variety.

5.1.
Symmetric products of curves

We recall some preliminaries on symmetric products of curves, their tautological bundles and the connections to secant varieties. Unless we give another reference, the statements made in this section can be found in
[
ENP20
, §3]
. If
C
is a curve, we will denote by
C_{n}
its
n
-th symmetric product. It can be considered as the quotient
\sigma\colon C^{n}\to C_{n}
with respect to the natural
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
-action. This is a non-singular projective variety of dimension
n
that parametrizes effective divisors of degree
n
on
C
. The addition map

\sigma\colon C\times C_{n-1}\to C_{n},\,(x,D)\mapsto x+D.

makes
C\times C_{n-1}
into the universal family over
C_{n}
: the fiber over
D\in C_{n}
is naturally isomorphic to the subscheme
D\subset C
. If
L
is a line bundle on
C
we can pull it back to the universal family
C\times C_{n-1}
and then pushforward to define the tautological bundle
E_{n,L}=\sigma_{*}pr_{C}^{*}L
. The sheaf
E_{n,L}
is a vector bundle of rank
n
on
C_{n}
whose fiber at
D
can identified with
H^{0}(C,L\otimes\mathcal{O}_{D})
. Here
{\mathcal{O}}_{D}
denotes the structure sheaf of
D
considered as subscheme of
C
.

Definition 5.1
.

A line bundle
L
on
C
is called
k
-very ample
if

h^{0}(C,L\otimes\mathcal{O}_{C}(-D))=h^{0}(C,L)-(k+1)

for all effective divisors
D
of degree
k+1
.

Remark 5.2
.

A line bundle
L
is
0
-very ample if and only if it is globally generated, and it is

A line bundle

We now define some line bundles on

Lemma 5.4

Letting

D(\varepsilon_{*}p^{*}F\otimes\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{r}}(t))\cong\varepsilon_{*}p^{*}(F^{\vee}\otimes A_{2,L\otimes\omega_{C}})\otimes\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{r}}(2-t).

Proof.

The hypothesis implies that
R^{1}\varepsilon_{*}(p^{*}F)=R^{1}\varepsilon_{*}(p^{*}F^{\vee}\otimes A_{2,\omega_{C}\otimes L})=0
as in the proof of
5.13
. Now we use Grothendieck duality: define
f\colon B\to\mathbb{P}^{r}
as the composition
B\to\Sigma\hookrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{r}
. If
\mathcal{G}
is a vector bundle on
B
, then

D(\varepsilon_{*}\mathcal{G})=\mathcal{E}xt^{r-3}_{\mathbb{P}^{r}}(f_{*}\mathcal{G},\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{r}}(3-r))

by definition. In particular, assume that
R^{1}f_{*}\mathcal{G}=0
, so that
f_{*}\mathcal{G}\cong Rf_{*}\mathcal{G}
in the derived category
D^{b}(\mathbb{P}^{n})
. Then Grothendieck duality
[
Huy06
, Theorem 3.34]
gives an isomorphism in
D^{b}(\mathbb{P}^{n})
:

\displaystyle R\mathcal{H}om_{\mathbb{P}^{r}}\left(f_{*}\mathcal{G},\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{r}}(3-r)\right)[r-3]

\displaystyle\cong Rf_{*}R\mathcal{H}om_{B}(\mathcal{G},f^{*}\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{r}}(3-r)\otimes\omega_{B}\otimes f^{*}\omega_{\mathbb{P}^{r}}^{\vee})

\displaystyle\cong Rf_{*}R\mathcal{H}om_{B}(\mathcal{G},\omega_{B}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{B}(4))

\displaystyle\cong Rf_{*}R\mathcal{H}om_{B}(\mathcal{G},p^{*}(S_{L\otimes\omega_{C}}(-\Delta))\otimes\mathcal{O}_{B}(2))

\displaystyle\cong Rf_{*}(\mathcal{G}^{\vee}\otimes p^{*}(S_{L\otimes\omega_{C}}(-\Delta))\otimes\mathcal{O}_{B}(2))

where we used the fact in
5.9
that
\omega_{B}\cong p^{*}(S_{L\otimes\omega_{C}}(-\Delta))\otimes\mathcal{O}_{B}(2)
. Since
R^{1}{\varepsilon}_{*}p^{*}F=0
, we can apply this reasoning to
\mathcal{G}=p^{*}F
, and we obtain

\displaystyle R\mathcal{H}om(f_{*}p^{*}F,\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{r}}(3-r))[r-3]

\displaystyle\cong Rf_{*}(p^{*}(F^{\vee}\otimes A_{2,L\otimes\omega_{C}}))\otimes\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{r}}(2)

\displaystyle\cong\varepsilon_{*}(p^{*}(F^{\vee}\otimes A_{2,L\otimes\omega_{C}}))\otimes\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{r}}(2)

where the last isomorphism comes from the fact that
R^{1}\varepsilon_{*}(p^{*}(F^{\vee}\otimes A_{2,L\otimes\omega_{C}}))=0
. If we tensor both sides by
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{r}}(-t)
, we get what we want.
∎

Now we can finally construct Ulrich sheaves on the secant variety:

Theorem 5.16
.

Let
\alpha
be a line bundle on
C
such that
H^{0}(C,\alpha)=H^{1}(C,\alpha)=0
. Then the coherent sheaf

\mathcal{F}=\varepsilon_{*}p^{*}A_{2,L\otimes\alpha}

is an Ulrich sheaf of rank one on
\Sigma
. If furthermore
\alpha
is a theta characteristic, meaning that
\alpha\otimes\alpha\cong\omega_{C}
, then this is a symmetric Ulrich sheaf of rank one on
\Sigma
.

Proof.

To prove that the sheaf is Ulrich, we check the conditions of
5.14
for
F=A_{2,L\otimes\alpha}
. The first condition
H^{1}(x+C,{A_{2,L\otimes\alpha}}_{|x+C})=0
for each
x\in C
becomes
H^{1}(C,\alpha\otimes L(-2x))=0
for all
x\in C
. Since
h^{0}(C,L)\geq 3
we see that
L(-2x)
is effective, and since
H^{1}(C,\alpha)=0
, it follows that
H^{1}(C,\alpha\otimes L(-2x))=0
as well. Observe that in the notation of
5.14
, we have
t=0
, so that the second condition is empty. For the third condition we have to check some cohomology vanishings, which in our case are :

\displaystyle H^{0}(C_{2},S_{2,\omega_{C}\otimes\alpha^{\vee}}\otimes E_{2,L})

\displaystyle=H^{1}(C_{2},S_{2,\omega_{C}\otimes\alpha^{\vee}}\otimes E_{2,L})=0.

\displaystyle H^{1}(C_{2},S_{2,\omega_{C}\otimes\alpha^{\vee}})

\displaystyle=H^{2}(C_{2},S_{2,\omega_{C}\otimes\alpha^{\vee}})=0

By Serre duality, we know that
H^{0}(C,\omega_{C}\otimes\alpha^{\vee})=H^{1}(C,\omega_{C}\otimes\alpha^{\vee})=0
and then the above vanishing follow from the formulas in
5.5
. This proves that
\mathcal{F}
is Ulrich. We can also compute
D(\mathcal{F})
using
5.15
: the other condition that we need to check is that
H^{1}(x+C,A_{2,L\otimes(\omega_{C}\otimes\alpha^{\vee})})=0
for all
x\in C
, but this can be proven as in the beginning of this proof. Then
5.15
shows that

D(\mathcal{F})\cong\varepsilon_{*}p^{*}S_{2,\omega_{C}\otimes\alpha^{\vee}}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{r}}(2)

To conclude, we will show that this is isomorphic to
\varepsilon_{*}p^{*}(A_{2,\omega_{c}\otimes\alpha^{\vee}})
, so that if
\alpha
is a theta characteristic, we are done thanks to Lemma
4.31
. We see that

\varepsilon_{*}p^{*}S_{2,\omega_{C}\otimes\alpha^{\vee}}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{r}}(2)\cong\varepsilon_{*}(p^{*}(S_{\omega\otimes\alpha^{\vee}})\otimes\mathcal{O}_{B}(2))\cong\varepsilon_{*}(p^{*}A_{2,L\otimes\omega_{C}\otimes\alpha^{\vee}}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{B}(E))

where the last isomorphism comes from
5.9
. Now consider the exact sequence on
B

(14)

0\to p^{*}A_{2,L\otimes\omega_{C}\otimes\alpha^{\vee}}\to p^{*}A_{L\otimes\omega_{C}\otimes\alpha^{\vee}}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{B}(E)\to p^{*}A_{L\otimes\omega_{C}\otimes\alpha^{\vee}}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{E}(E)\to 0

and observe that under the isomorphism
C\times C\to E
given by
(x,y)\mapsto(\iota(x),x+y)
it holds that

p^{*}A_{L\otimes\omega_{C}\otimes\alpha^{\vee}}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{E}(E)\cong p^{*}(S_{\omega_{C}\otimes\alpha^{\vee}})\otimes\mathcal{O}_{E}(2)\cong pr_{1}^{*}(L^{\otimes 2}\otimes\omega_{C}\otimes\alpha^{\vee})\otimes pr_{2}^{*}(\omega_{C}\otimes\alpha^{\vee})

and since the composition
C\times C\to E\overset{f}{\to}\mathbb{P}^{r}
is identified with the first projection
C\times C\to C
we see that

\displaystyle Rf_{*}(p^{*}A_{L\otimes\omega_{C}\otimes\alpha^{\vee}}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{E}(E))

\displaystyle\cong R{pr_{1}}_{*}(pr_{1}^{*}(L^{\otimes 2}\otimes\omega_{C}\otimes\alpha^{\vee})\otimes pr_{2}^{*}(\omega_{C}\otimes\alpha^{\vee}))

\displaystyle\cong(L^{\otimes 2}\otimes\omega_{C}\otimes\alpha^{\vee})\otimes R{pr_{1}}_{*}pr_{2}^{*}(\omega_{C}\otimes\alpha^{\vee})=0

where the last equality comes from Grauert’s theorem, together with the fact that
\omega_{C}\otimes\alpha^{\vee}
has no cohomology. At this point
Equation

14
shows that
\varepsilon_{*}(p^{*}A_{L\otimes\alpha^{\prime}}\otimes\mathcal{O}(E))\cong\varepsilon_{*}(p^{*}A_{L\otimes\alpha^{\prime}})
.
∎

Remark 5.17
.

Looking at the proofs in this section, we see that we do not need the full assumption of
3
-very ampleness. What is actually needed is that
R^{1}\varepsilon_{*}p^{*}(A_{L\otimes\alpha})=0
along the map
\varepsilon\colon B\to\Sigma
. For example, the proof of
5.13
shows that this holds whenever the map
\varepsilon\colon B\to\Sigma
has only zero-dimensional fibers over
\Sigma\setminus C
. Geometrically, this means that there are no infinitely secant lines passing through a point
x\in\mathbb{P}^{r}\setminus C
.

5.5.
Rational normal curves

In the case of rational normal curves, we can also find Ulrich sheaves of higher rank. Thus, we let now
C=\mathbb{P}^{1}
and
L=\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{1}}(n)
, with
n\geq 3
. Recall that in this case there is a natural identification of the second symmetric product
(\mathbb{P}^{1})_{2}
with a projective space
\mathbb{P}^{2}
, so that the divisors
x+C\subset C_{2}
are lines in
\mathbb{P}^{2}
and the locus of non-reduced divisors
\Delta\subset(\mathbb{P}^{1})_{2}
is a smooth conic. In particular, we see that
S_{2,\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{1}}(d)}\cong\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{2}}(d)
and
\mathcal{O}_{(\mathbb{P}^{1})_{2}}(\delta)\cong\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{2}}(1)
. We also recall that the quotient bundle
Q
on
\mathbb{P}^{2}
is defined by the Euler exact sequence

(15)

0\to\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{2}}(-1)\to\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{2}}^{\oplus 3}\to Q\to 0

and furthermore
Q\cong T_{\mathbb{P}^{2}}(-1)
.

Theorem 5.18
.

With the previous notation, the coherent sheaf

\mathcal{F}_{n}=\varepsilon_{*}p^{*}(S^{n-2}Q\otimes\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{2}}(-1))\otimes\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{n}}(1)

is a Ulrich sheaf of rank
n-1
on the secant
\Sigma
of the rational normal curve in
\mathbb{P}^{n}
. Furthermore
D(\mathcal{F}_{n})\cong\mathcal{F}_{n}
.

Proof.

To prove that the sheaf is Ulrich, we check the conditions of
5.14
for
F=S^{n-2}Q\otimes\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{2}}(-1)
. We first observe that in this case

(16)

F^{\vee}\otimes A_{2,\omega_{C}\otimes L}\cong S^{n-2}Q^{\vee}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{2}}(1)\otimes\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{2}}(n-4)\cong S^{n-2}Q\otimes\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{2}}(-1)\cong F

where the last isomorphism comes from the fact that
Q
is a rank two bundle of determinant
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{2}}(1)
, so that
Q^{\vee}\cong Q\otimes\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{2}}(-1)
. Now we check the conditions of
5.14
: for the first condition, we show that
H^{1}(\ell,F_{|\ell})=0
for every line in
\mathbb{P}^{2}
, and this follows from the fact that
Q_{|\ell}\cong\mathcal{O}_{\ell}\oplus\mathcal{O}_{\ell}(1)
for every line, so that
F_{|\ell}\cong\mathcal{O}_{\ell}(-1)\oplus\mathcal{O}_{\ell}\oplus\dots\oplus\mathcal{O}_{\ell}(n-3)
.

For the other conditions, we observe that in the notation of
5.14
, we have
t=1
, so that, thanks to
Equation

16
, we are reduced to the two vanishings

H^{0}(\mathbb{P}^{2},S^{n-2}Q(-1))=H^{1}(\mathbb{P}^{2},S^{n-2}Q(-1))=0

which hold because the bundle
S^{n-2}Q(-1)
has no cohomology. This last statement follows from Borel–Weil–Bott in characteristic zero, but there is an easier proof valid in all characteristics, for which we thank Claudiu Raicu: taking symmetric powers in the exact sequence
Equation

15
, we obtain another exact sequence

0\to S^{n-3}(\mathcal{O}^{\oplus_{3}}_{\mathbb{P}^{2}})\otimes\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{2}}(-2)\to S^{n-2}(\mathcal{O}^{\oplus_{3}}_{\mathbb{P}^{2}})\otimes\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{2}}(-1)\to S^{n-2}Q\otimes\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{2}}(-1)\to 0

and since the line bundles
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{2}}(-1)
and
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{2}}(-2)
have no cohomology, the statement follows. This proves that
\mathcal{F}_{n}
is Ulrich. The fact that
D(\mathcal{F}_{n})\cong\mathcal{F}_{n}
follows from

5.15
and
Equation

16
.
∎

6.
The arithmetic writhe

6.1.
An algebraic orientation on
B\setminus E

We use the notation from
Section

5.3
. Namely, let
L
be a
3
-very ample line bundle on the curve
C
over the field
K
and
\iota\colon C\hookrightarrow\mathbb{P}(H^{0}(C,L)^{\vee})=\mathbb{P}^{n}
be the corresponding embedding. We have the tautological projective bundle
p\colon B\to C_{2}
, i.e.,
B=\mathbb{P}(E_{2,L})
where
E_{2,L}
is the tautological bundle (see
Section

5
) on the second symmetric power
C_{2}
of
C
, and the projection
\varepsilon\colon B\to\Sigma
to the secant variety
\Sigma
of
C
. The latter is an isomorphism outside the preimage
E
of
C
.
Let
\alpha
be a theta characteristic on
C
such that
H^{0}(C,\alpha)=0
. We have seen in Theorem
5.16
that this defines a symmetric Ulrich sheaf of rank one on
\Sigma
, and consequently a relative orientation as in
4.28
. We will now make this concrete and explain how
\alpha
defines an algebraic orientation on
B\setminus E
, even when
\alpha
has section. To that end recall from
5.9
that the canonical bundle on
B
is given by

p^{*}(S_{2,\omega_{C}})\otimes{\mathcal{O}}_{B}(-E)\cong p^{*}(S_{2,\alpha})\otimes p^{*}(S_{2,\alpha})\otimes{\mathcal{O}}_{B}(-E).

This implies that
\omega_{B\setminus E}
is isomorphic to the restriction of
p^{*}(S_{2,\alpha})\otimes p^{*}(S_{2,\alpha})
. Thus
p^{*}(S_{2,\alpha})|_{B\setminus E}
is a theta characteristic on
B\setminus E
. In order to even define an algebraic orientation on
B\setminus E
, choose an isomorphism
\sigma\colon\alpha\otimes\alpha\to\omega_{C}
for now. Let
\tau
be a rational section of
\alpha
and let
\omega=\sigma(\tau\otimes\tau)
. Let
\rho
be a rational section of
p^{*}(S_{2,\alpha})
with divisor
S_{\operatorname{div}(\tau)}
. Then by
Equation

12
an isomorphism

\psi\colon p^{*}(S_{2,\alpha})|_{B\setminus E}\otimes p^{*}(S_{2,\alpha})|_{B\setminus E}\to\omega_{B\setminus E}

can be defined by mapping
\rho\otimes\rho
to the differential
3
-form
\Omega(\omega)
from
5.12
. Note that by
5.10
another choice of
\sigma
,
\tau
and
\rho
would lead to an equivalent algebraic orientation. Therefore, we can call
\psi
the
algebraic orientation induced by
\alpha
.

6.2.
An arithmetic count of secant lines

Let
x_{0},\ldots,x_{3}
be the homogeneous coordinates on
\mathbb{P}^{3}
and
x=\frac{x_{1}}{x_{0}}
,
y=\frac{x_{2}}{x_{0}}
and
z=\frac{x_{3}}{x_{0}}
. We consider on
\mathbb{P}^{3}
the algebraic orientation defined by the rational differential
3
-form
dx\wedge dy\wedge dz
(meaning that we choose an isomorphism for which this
3
-form is the image of a square).
Now let
\iota\colon C\hookrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{3}
be an embedded curve over
K
such that
L=\iota^{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}(1)
is
3
-very ample. Geometrically, this means that
C
is the isomorphic image via a linear projection of a curve
C\subset\mathbb{P}^{r}
such that no four points on
C
lie on a plane in
\mathbb{P}^{r}
.

Let
\alpha
be a theta characteristic on
C
and let
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{P}^{r}
be the secant variety to
C
. Composing the map
p\colon B\to\Sigma
with a suitable projection and restricting to
B\setminus E
, we obtain the finite surjective morphism
p^{\prime}\colon B\setminus E\to\mathbb{P}^{3}\setminus C
. The algebraic orientation on
B\setminus E
induced by
\alpha
together with our fixed algebraic orientation on
\mathbb{P}^{3}
define a relative orientation of
p^{\prime}
and since
\mathbb{P}^{3}
is
{\mathbb{A}}^{1}
-chain connected we have that
\deg^{{\mathbb{A}}^{1}}(p^{\prime})
is well-defined (see
[
PW21
, Theorem 9]
applied to the proper map
B\to\mathbb{P}^{3}
).

Definition 6.1
.

We define the
arithmetic writhe
of the
3
-very ample curve
C
semi-oriented by
\alpha
as
\operatorname{w}(C,\alpha):=\deg^{{\mathbb{A}}^{1}}(p^{\prime})
.

Remark 6.2
.

The arithmetic writhe is the result of an arithmetic count of secant lines
S
to
C
passing through a given point
q\in\mathbb{P}^{3}\setminus C
. Indeed, such secant lines are in bijection to points
q_{S}
in the preimage of
q
under
p^{\prime}
. We define the
local writhe

\operatorname{w}_{q,S}(C,\alpha)
at such a secant to be the local
{\mathbb{A}}^{1}
-degree
\deg_{q_{S}}^{{\mathbb{A}}^{1}}(p^{\prime})
. The sum of the local writhe over all secants that contain a point
q
is independent of
q
and equals
\operatorname{w}(C,\alpha)
. Note that
\operatorname{w}(C,\alpha)

does
however depend on the embedded curve
C
(and
\alpha
) as for example
6.6
shows.

We now describe how to compute the local writhe explicitly. By
5.12

\operatorname{w}_{q,S}(C,\alpha)
can be computed by evaluating
dx\wedge dy\wedge dz
(or any other differential
3
-form that differs from this by the square of a rational function) at the
\omega
-Viro frame at
q_{S}
where
\omega
is any differential on
C
whose divisor is of the form
2D
where
D
is a divisor whose line bundle is
\alpha
. More precisely, we choose tangent vectors
u\in\operatorname{T}_{q}S
,
v\in\operatorname{T}_{a}C
and
w\in\operatorname{T}_{b}C
as in
5.11
. Namely,
v\in\operatorname{T}_{a}C
and
w\in\operatorname{T}_{b}C
are chosen in a way that
\omega
evaluates to one in
v
and
w
. After identifying the secant
S
with
\mathbb{P}^{1}
sending
a
to
(1:0)
,
b
to
(0:1)
and
q
to
(1:1)
, the tangent vector
u
is chosen in a way that
dt
evaluates to one in
u
. Then write
u,v
and
w
as vectors
\tilde{u},\tilde{v}
and
\tilde{w}
with respect to the basis given by
dx
,
dy
and
dz
. The local writhe then equals

\operatorname{w}_{q,S}(C,\alpha)=\operatorname{Tr}_{F/K}\langle\det\begin{pmatrix}|&|&|\\
\tilde{v}&\tilde{u}&\tilde{w}\\
|&|&|\end{pmatrix}\rangle

where
F
is the field of definition of
S
.

Remark 6.3
.

The above description of the arithmetic writhe as a sum of local writhe numbers shows that in the case
K={\mathbb{R}}
it agrees with the encomplexed writhe number introduced by Viro in
[
Vir01
]
.
Realizing it as the degree of a morphism gives another proof that the encomplexed writhe number does not depend on the choice of the center of projection.

Example 6.4
.

Let
K
a field with
\operatorname{char}(K)\neq 2
and consider the rational curve
C\subset\mathbb{P}^{3}
of degree four defined as the image of

\varphi\colon\mathbb{P}^{1}\to\mathbb{P}^{3},\,(r:s)\mapsto(r^{4}:r^{3}s:rs^{3}:s^{4}).

In the following every (local) writhe number will be computed with respect to the unique theta characteristic on

given by
{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{P}^{1}}(-1)
.
In order to compute the local writhe of secants to

\sigma
on

dt=t^{2}d\bar{t}
where

and
\bar{t}=-\frac{r}{s}

The genus-degree formula implies that the projection from a general point has three nodes.
We will now compute the local writhe of all three secants that contain the point

.
For the secant

spanned by the points

and

and the coordinates

. In this chart the curve is parametrized by

Expressed in these coordinates our points

and

(

) and

\tilde{b}

\tilde{v}=(1,3,4)
and
\tilde{w}=(1,3,-4)
. For computing the vector
\tilde{u}
we have to consider the parametrization

\lambda\mapsto\left(\frac{1-\lambda}{1+\lambda},\frac{1-\lambda}{1+\lambda},1\right).

The tangent vector at
\lambda=1
equals
\tilde{u}=(-\frac{1}{2},-\frac{1}{2},0)
.
We can thus compute the local writhe as

\operatorname{w}_{p,L_{1}}(C)=\langle\det\begin{pmatrix}1&-\frac{1}{2}&1\\
3&-\frac{1}{2}&3\\

Let

a square root of
-1

For the secant
L_{2}
spanned by the points

and

and

is defined over

\tilde{a}=(i,-i,1)
(

\tilde{v}=(1,-3,-4i)

\tilde{b}=(-i,i,1)

\operatorname{w}_{p,L_{2}}(C)=\langle\det\begin{pmatrix}1&-\frac{i}{2}&1\\

For the third secant

spanned by

and

We have
\tilde{a}=(0,0,0)
(

Theorem 6.5

(2)

If the linear system spanned by the

7.
Algebraic isotopies

Let
C
be a curve over a field
K
of characteristic
\operatorname{char}(K)\neq 2
. In analogy to classical knot and link theory, we want to study embeddings of
C
into the three-dimensional space
\mathbb{P}^{3}
up to algebraic isotopies.
We first make some general definitions and observations.

Definition 7.1
.

Let
X
be a projective variety over
K
. An
algebraic isotopy
of two closed embeddings
\psi_{i}\colon X\to\mathbb{P}^{r}
,
i=0,1
, is a morphism

I\colon{\mathbb{A}}^{1}\times X\to\mathbb{P}^{r},\qquad(t,x)\mapsto I_{t}(x)

such that for all
t\in{\mathbb{A}}^{1}
the induced map
I_{t}\colon X_{\kappa(t)}\to Y_{\kappa(t)}
is an embedding and
I_{i}=\psi_{i}
for
i=0,1
.
Two embeddings
\psi,\psi^{\prime}
are
algebraically isotopic
if they are connected by a chain of algebraic isotopies. We denote by
[X,\mathbb{P}^{r}]^{\operatorname{I}}
the quotient of the set of embeddings
\operatorname{Emb}(X,\mathbb{P}^{r})(K)
by the equivalence relation generated by algebraic isotopies. We write
[\psi]
for the class of
\psi
in
[X,\mathbb{P}^{r}]^{\operatorname{I}}
and call it the
isotopy class
of
\psi
.

A first observation is that two isotopic embeddings have isomorphic underlying line bundles:

Lemma 7.2
.

Let
\psi,\psi^{\prime}\colon X\hookrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{r}
be two algebraically isotopic embeddings. Then
\psi^{*}\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{r}}(1)\cong\psi^{\prime*}\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{r}}(1)
.

Proof.

Any algebraic isotopy
I\colon{\mathbb{A}}^{1}\times X\to\mathbb{P}^{r}
induces a morphism
I\colon{\mathbb{A}}^{1}\to\operatorname{Pic}(X),t\mapsto I_{t}^{*}\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{r}}(1)
, which is constant, since any morphism from a rational curve to an abelian variety is constant. Alternatively, the map

\operatorname{pr}_{X}^{*}\colon\operatorname{Pic}(X)\longrightarrow\operatorname{Pic}({\mathbb{A}}^{1}\times X),\quad L\mapsto\operatorname{pr}_{X}^{*}L

is an isomorphism
[
Har83
, Proposition II.6.6]
, so that the isomorphism class of the line bundle
I^{*}\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{r}}(1)
is constant on the fibers
\{t\}\times X
.
∎

Definition 7.3
.

Let
X
be a projective variety over
K
and
L
a line bundle on
X
. We denote the set of embeddings
\psi\colon X\hookrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{r}
with
\psi^{*}\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{n}}(1)\cong L
by
\operatorname{Emb}_{L}(X,\mathbb{P}^{r})(K)
, and its quotient by chains of algebraic isotopies by
[X,\mathbb{P}^{r}]^{\operatorname{I}}_{L}
.

We now recall how to parametrize the set
\operatorname{Emb}_{L}(X,\mathbb{P}^{r})(K)
.
First of all, if this is nonempty, then
L
is very ample. In this case, consider the embedding given by the complete linear system
V=H^{0}(X,L)
:

\varphi_{L}\colon X\hookrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{n}=\mathbb{P}(V^{\vee})

and the secant variety
\Sigma=\Sigma(X,L)
of
X
in
\mathbb{P}(V^{\vee})
. Any embedding
\psi
from
\operatorname{Emb}_{L}(X,\mathbb{P}^{r})
is obtained by composing
\varphi_{L}
with a linear projection

[s_{0},\dots,s_{r}]\colon\varphi_{L}(X)\longrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{r},\qquad s_{i}\in V

and, in order for
\psi=[s_{0},\dots,s_{r}]\circ\varphi_{L}
to be an embedding, the condition is that the linear space
\Pi=\{s_{0}=\dots=s_{r}=0\}
does not intersect the secant variety
\Sigma
. Consider now the set

(18)

\mathcal{U}_{L,r}=\{(s_{0},\dots,s_{r})\in V^{\oplus(r+1)}\,|\,\{s_{0}=\dots=s_{r}=0\}\cap\Sigma=\emptyset\}

This is open: its complement in
V^{\oplus(r+1)}
is the image of the incidence correspondence

\mathcal{I}=\{((s_{0},\dots,s_{r}),p)\in V^{\oplus(r+1)}\times\Sigma\,|\,s_{0}(p)=\dots=s_{r}(p)=0\}

along the proper map
\operatorname{pr}_{V^{\oplus(r+1)}}\colon V^{\oplus(r+1)}\times\Sigma\to V^{\oplus(r+1)}
. The previous discussion shows that
\operatorname{Emb}_{L}(X,\mathbb{P}^{r})(K)
is the set of
K
-rational points of a (possibly empty) open subset of a projective space

(19)

\operatorname{Emb}_{L}(X,\mathbb{P}^{r})=\mathcal{U}_{L,n}/K^{\times}=\{[s_{0},\dots,s_{r}]\in\mathbb{P}(V^{\oplus(r+1)})\,|\,(s_{0},\dots,s_{r})\in\mathcal{U}_{L,r}\}.

This comes equipped with a universal embedding

\operatorname{Emb}_{L}(X,\mathbb{P}^{r})\times X\longrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{r},\qquad([s_{0},\dots,s_{r}],x)\mapsto[s_{0}(x),\dots,s_{r}(x)]

so that an algebraic isotopy between
\psi,\psi^{\prime}\in\operatorname{Emb}_{L}(X,\mathbb{P}^{r})(K)
is simply a map
{\mathbb{A}}^{1}\to\operatorname{Emb}_{L}(X,\mathbb{P}^{r})
whose image contains these two points. This leads naturally to the notion of naive connected components in
{\mathbb{A}}^{1}
-geometry as e.g. in
[
Caz12a
]
.

Definition 7.4
.

Let
X
be a scheme over the field
K
. The set
\pi_{0}^{\operatorname{N}}X(K)
of
naive connected components
of
X(K)
is the quotient of
X(K)
by the finest equivalence relation under which each two points
x_{0},x_{1}\in X(K)
for which there exists a morphism
f\colon{\mathbb{A}}^{1}\to X
with
f(i)=x_{i}
,
i=0,1
, are equivalent.

Remark 7.5
.

The previous discussion shows that the set of isotopy classes of embeddings with line bundle
L
can be considered as the set of naive connected components

[X,\mathbb{P}^{r}]^{\operatorname{I}}_{L}=\pi_{0}^{\operatorname{N}}\operatorname{Emb}_{L}(X,\mathbb{P}^{r})(K)

Note that we could also replace
\mathbb{P}^{r}
by any other projective variety
Y
by using Grothendieck’s Hom-scheme, although we will not consider this here.

Remark 7.6
.

Recall that a
K
-scheme
X
is
{\mathbb{A}}^{1}
-connected if and only if for any extension
K^{\prime}/K
, any two
K^{\prime}
-rational points in

can be connected by a chain of maps from
{\mathbb{A}}^{1}_{K^{\prime}}
. This means precisely that
\pi_{0}^{\operatorname{N}}\operatorname{Emb}_{L}(X_{K^{\prime}},\mathbb{P}^{r})(K^{\prime})
has at most one element for any extension

.

Example 7.7
.

The group scheme
\operatorname{{\mathbf{S}L}}_{r+1}
is
{\mathbb{A}}^{1}
-connected: indeed, for any field

, any matrix in
\operatorname{{\mathbf{S}L}}_{r+1}(K)
can be written as a product of upper or lower triangular elementary matrices with all ones on the diagonal. For such a matrix

connects

at

with the identity

at

Corollary 7.8
.

Let
\psi\colon X\hookrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{r}
be an embedding and let

. The two embeddings
\psi
and

are algebraically isotopic.

Proof.

Since
\operatorname{{\mathbf{S}L}}_{r+1}
is
{\mathbb{A}}^{1}
-connected, we can find a chain of algebraic isotopies connecting

with the identity

.
∎

Lemma 7.9
.

Let
\psi\colon X\hookrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{r}
be an embedding and let
A\in K^{(r+1)\times(r+1)}
of non-maximal rank, such that the composition of
\psi
with the rational map
A\colon\mathbb{P}^{r}\dashrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{r}
is still an embedding. Then
\psi
and
A\circ\psi
are algebraically isotopic.

Proof.

Let
\operatorname{rk}(A)=m+1
with
m<r
. Then there is an
M\in\operatorname{{\mathbf{G}L}}_{r+1}(K)
such that
MA
is in reduced row-echelon form:

M\cdot A=\begin{pmatrix}A^{\prime}\\
0\end{pmatrix}

where
A^{\prime}
is a
(m+1)\times(r+1)
matrix. If we let
M^{\prime}\in\operatorname{{\mathbf{G}L}}_{m-r}(K)
be any matrix with determinant
\det(M^{\prime})=\det(M)^{-1}
, we see that

N=\begin{pmatrix}I_{m+1}&0\\
0&M^{\prime}\end{pmatrix},\text{ satisfies }NM\in\operatorname{{\mathbf{S}L}}_{r+1}(K)\text{ and }NM\cdot A=\begin{pmatrix}A^{\prime}\\
0\end{pmatrix}

Thus, thanks to Corollary
7.8
we can assume that
A
has reduced row-echelon form.
For simplicity, let’s assume that the pivots of
A
are in the first
m+1
columns, so that

A=\begin{pmatrix}I_{m+1}&B\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}

for another
(m+1)\times(n-m)
matrix

, with the general case being similar. There is an algebraic isotopy

{\mathbb{A}}^{1}\times X\to\mathbb{P}^{n},\qquad(t,x)\mapsto\begin{pmatrix}I_{m+1}&B\\
0&tI_{n-m}\end{pmatrix}\cdot\psi(x)

between

at
t=0
and another embedding
\psi^{\prime}
at

. To conclude we need to show that
\psi^{\prime}
is algebraically isotopic to
\psi
. It is enough to observe that

\begin{pmatrix}I_{m+1}&-B\\
0&I_{n-m}\end{pmatrix}\cdot\begin{pmatrix}I_{m+1}&B\\
0&I_{n-m}\end{pmatrix}\cdot\psi(x)=\begin{pmatrix}I_{m+1}&0\\
0&I_{n-m}\end{pmatrix}\cdot\psi(x)=\psi(x)

so that we conclude by invoking Corollary
7.8
again.
∎

(2)

By the description in

(1)

Since

(2)

on

of

One always has

Now we want to strengthen

Since

S(t)\in\operatorname{{\mathbf{S}L}}_{\ell}(K[t])
and
T(t)\in\operatorname{{\mathbf{S}L}}_{d+1}(K[t])
such that

S(t)\cdot A^{\prime}(t)\cdot T(t)=\begin{pmatrix}D(t)\\
0\end{pmatrix}

where
D(t)
is a
(d+1)\times(d+1)
diagonal matrix with diagonal entries
p_{0},\ldots,p_{d}\in K[t]
. Let
w_{0}(t),\ldots,w_{d}(t)
be the first
d+1
columns of the matrix
S(t)^{-1}
. Then

Q(t)\cdot f(t)=p_{0}(t)\cdots p_{d}(t)\cdot(w_{0}(t)\wedge\cdots\wedge w_{d}(t)).

Since
S(t)^{-1}
is invertible over
K[t]
, the greatest common divisor of the Plücker coordinates of the right-hand side is
p_{0}(t)\cdots p_{d}(t)
. By construction, the greatest common divisor of the Plücker coordinates of the left-hand side is
G(t)\cdot Q(t)
. Thus, there is some
\lambda\in K^{\times}
such that

f(t)=\lambda\cdot G(t)\cdot(w_{0}(t)\wedge\cdots\wedge w_{d}(t)).

This proves the claim.
∎

Corollary 7.15
.

The map
\pi_{0}^{\operatorname{N}}\mathcal{U}_{L,b}(K)\to\pi_{0}^{\operatorname{N}}\mathcal{W}_{L,b}(K)
induced by
w
is a bijection.

Proof.

It is surjective because
w
is surjective on the level of
K
-points. For proving injectivity let
x,y\in\mathcal{U}_{L,b}(K)
such that
w(x)
and
w(y)
belong to the same equivalence class in
\pi_{0}^{\operatorname{N}}\mathcal{W}_{L,b}(K)
. By
7.14
there exists
y^{\prime}
in the equivalence class of
y
such that
w(y^{\prime})=w(x)
. The
K
-points of any fiber of
w
over a nonzero point form an
\operatorname{{\mathbf{S}L}}_{b+1}(K)
-orbit. Therefore, by
7.7
, the two points
x
and
y^{\prime}
and thus
x
and
y
belong to the same equivalence class in
\pi_{0}^{\operatorname{N}}\mathcal{U}_{L,b}(K)
.
∎

Corollary 7.16
.

Let
(s_{0},\ldots,s_{n}),(s_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,s_{n}^{\prime})\in\mathcal{U}_{b,L}(K)
. Then the embeddings

\psi,\psi^{\prime}\colon X\hookrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{b}

defined by the
s_{i}
and
s_{i}^{\prime}
, respectively, are algebraically isotopic if and only if there exists
\lambda\in K^{\times}
such that
s_{0}\wedge\cdots\wedge s_{b}
and
\lambda^{b+1}\cdot(s_{0}^{\prime}\wedge\cdots\wedge s_{b+1}^{\prime})
are in the same naive connected component of
\mathcal{W}_{b,L}(K)
. In other words, there is a natural bijection

\pi_{0}\mathcal{W}_{b,L}(K)/(K^{\times b+1})\overset{\sim}{\longrightarrow}[X,\mathbb{P}^{b}]^{\operatorname{I}}_{L}.

Proof.

Since
\operatorname{Emb}_{L}(X,\mathbb{P}^{r})(K)=\mathcal{U}_{L,b}(K)/K^{\times}
, we see that
\psi
and
\psi^{\prime}
are algebraically isotopic if and only if and only if there exists
\lambda\in K^{\times}
such that
(s_{0},\ldots,s_{b})
and
(\lambda s_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,\lambda s_{b}^{\prime})
are in the same naive connected component of
\mathcal{U}_{b,L}(K)
. By
7.15
this is the case if and only if
s_{0}\wedge\cdots\wedge s_{b}
and
\lambda^{b+1}\cdot(s_{0}\wedge\cdots\wedge s_{b+1})
are in the same naive connected component of
\mathcal{W}_{b,L}(K)
.
∎

The following corollary implies the second part of Theorem D.

Corollary 7.17
.

Assume that
h^{0}(X,L)=b+1
. There is a bijection

K^{\times}/K^{\times b+1}\to[X,\mathbb{P}^{b}]^{\operatorname{I}}_{L}.

Proof.

Since
\dim(V)=b+1
, we have that
\mathcal{W}\cong{\mathbb{A}}^{1}\setminus\{0\}
. This shows that every point of
\mathcal{W}_{L,b}(K)
is its own naive connected component and
\mathcal{W}_{L,b}(K)
can be identified with
K^{\times}
. The statement follows then from
7.16
.
∎

8.
Isotopic embeddings of curves in
3
-space

In the following
C
will be a smooth and connected projective curve over
K
. It follows from example from
7.11
that the secant variety of
C
has the expected dimension three if and only if
C
is not planar.
Thus, in the following we will consider embeddings

\psi\colon C\hookrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{3}

such that
L=\psi^{*}\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}(1)
has
h^{0}(C,L)\geq 4
and the isotopy classes in
[C,\mathbb{P}^{3}]^{\operatorname{I}}_{L}
, so that the results of the previous section apply, in particular
7.16
.

In the case of curves, we will prove that the arithmetic writhe is invariant under algebraic isotopies. We will make use of the following version of Harder’s theorem.

Theorem 8.1
.

Let
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
\mathscr{S}_{n}
the scheme of non-singular symmetric
n\times n
matrices over
K
. We consider the fiber product
\operatorname{GW}(K)\times_{K^{\times}/K^{\times 2}}K^{\times}
with respect to the canonical map
K^{\times}\to K^{\times}/K^{\times 2}
and the discriminant map
\operatorname{GW}(K)\to K^{\times}/K^{\times 2}
. There is a well-defined injection

(21)

\pi_{0}^{\operatorname{N}}\mathscr{S}_{n}(K)\to\operatorname{GW}(K)\times_{K^{\times}/K^{\times 2}}K^{\times}

that takes the naive connected component of a symmetric matrix to the pair of its class in
\operatorname{GW}(K)
and its determinant.

Proof.

See for example
[
Caz12b
, Proposition 3.9]
.
∎

Recall that the writhe is constructed as follows: let
\alpha
be a theta characteristic of
C
with
h^{0}(C,\alpha)=0
and let
\psi\colon C\hookrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{3}
be an embedding such that
L=\psi^{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}(1)
is
3
-very ample. The corresponding Ulrich sheaf
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}
of
6.5
provides a symmetric matrix
\Lambda
of linear forms on
\wedge^{4}V
. This defines a morphism
\mathcal{W}\to\mathscr{S}_{N}
where
N
is the degree of the secant variety. Taking the naive connected component, and using
Equation

21
this gives us a map

(22)

\operatorname{w}(-,\alpha):\pi_{0}^{\operatorname{N}}\mathcal{W}_{L,3}(K)\to\operatorname{GW}(K)\times_{K^{\times}/K^{\times 2}}(K^{\times}).

By
7.16
this induces a map

(23)

\operatorname{w}(-,\alpha)\colon[C,\mathbb{P}^{3}]^{\operatorname{I}}_{L}\to\operatorname{GW}(K)\times_{K^{\times}/K^{\times 2}}(K^{\times}/K^{\times 4N}).

Definition 8.2
.

In the above setting, we define the
arithmetic writhe
of the embedding
\psi
to be the image
\operatorname{w}([\psi],\alpha)
of the isotopy class of
\psi
under the map from
Equation

23
.

Remark 8.3
.

The arithmetic writhe of the embedding is automatically invariant under algebraic isotopies. Furthermore, The first component of
\operatorname{w}([\psi],\alpha)
is the arithmetic writhe of the embedded curve
\psi(C)
. In particular, this shows that if
\psi,\psi^{\prime}\colon C\hookrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{3}
are two algebraically isotopic embeddings, then the writhes of
\psi(C)
and
\psi^{\prime}(C)
are the same.

We also notice that, if
K
is an algebraically or real closed field, then we have
K^{\times 4N}=K^{\times 2}
which implies that the second factor of
\operatorname{w}([\psi],\alpha)
is superfluous: the arithmetic writhe of the embedding
\psi
coincides with the arithmetic writhe of the embedded curve
\psi(C)
.

8.1.
Rational curves of degree four

In this subsection we consider the case
C=\mathbb{P}^{1}
. As
\alpha={\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{P}^{1}}(-1)
is the only theta characteristic of
\mathbb{P}^{1}
, we denote
\operatorname{w}(-)=\operatorname{w}(-,\alpha)
. For
n\geq 3
we write
[\mathbb{P}^{1},\mathbb{P}^{3}]^{\operatorname{I}}_{n}=[\mathbb{P}^{1},\mathbb{P}^{3}]^{\operatorname{I}}_{{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{P}^{1}}(n)}
. Our goal is to prove that

\operatorname{w}\colon[\mathbb{P}^{1},\mathbb{P}^{3}]^{\operatorname{I}}_{4}\to\operatorname{GW}(K)\times_{K^{\times}/K^{\times 2}}(K^{\times}/K^{\times 12})

is injective in this case. In other words, the isotopy type of two embeddings of
\mathbb{P}^{1}
to
\mathbb{P}^{3}
of degree four is completely determined by the arithmetic writhe. To this end, we will prove that
\pi_{0}^{\operatorname{N}}\mathcal{W}_{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{1}}(4),3}(K)
is in bijection to the set of pointed naive homotopy classes of morphisms
\mathbb{P}^{1}\to\mathbb{P}^{1}
of degree three. These were characterized in
[
Caz12b
]
. We first have to introduce some notation. Following
[
Caz12b
, Definition 2.1]
we consider the scheme
\mathscr{T}_{n}
,
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, of pointed rational functions on
\mathbb{P}^{1}
, realized as the open subscheme of the affine space

{\mathbb{A}}^{2n}=\operatorname{Spec}K[a_{0},\ldots,a_{n-1},b_{0},\ldots,b_{n-1}]

where the resultant of the two polynomials
t^{n}+a_{n-1}t^{n-1}+\cdots+a_{0}
and
b_{n-1}t^{n-1}+\cdots+b_{0}
does not vanish. Hence
\mathscr{T}_{n}(K)
is the set of all pairs
(f,g)
of coprime polynomials
f,g\in K[t]
where
f
is monic of degree
n
and
g
has degree at most
n-1
. For such a pair one can write

\frac{g}{f}=h_{0}(f,g)\cdot t^{-1}+h_{1}(f,g)\cdot t^{-2}+h_{2}(f,g)\cdot t^{-3}+\cdots

for suitable
h_{i}(f,g)\in K
and we define the
Hankel matrix
of
f
and
g
as

\operatorname{H}_{n}(f,g)=(h_{i+j}(f,g))_{0\leq i,j\leq n-1}.

A related matrix is the
Bézout matrix
which is constructed as follows. The polynomial
f(x)g(y)-f(y)g(x)
is divisible by
x-y
so that we can write

\frac{f(x)g(y)-f(y)g(x)}{x-y}=\sum_{1\leq i,j\leq n}c_{ij}x^{i-1}y^{j-1}

for
c_{ij}\in K
. Then the Bézout matrix is defined as

\operatorname{B}_{n}(f,g)=(c_{ij})_{1\leq i,j\leq n}.

It is classical knowledge that
\operatorname{H}_{n}(f,g)
is equivalent as symmetric bilinear form to the Bézout matrix
\operatorname{B}_{n}(f,g)
and these two matrices have the same determinant, see for example
[
KS22a
, Proposition 1.9.7]
for a proof that, albeit formulated over real closed fields only, works over every field. In particular, the determinant of
\operatorname{H}_{n}(f,g)
is non-zero if and only if
f
and
g
are co-prime.

Theorem 8.4
.

The morphism
h\colon\mathscr{T}_{3}\to\mathcal{W}_{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{1}}(4),3}
that sends a pair
(f,g)
to

\varphi(f,g)=h_{4}(f,g)\cdot(t\wedge t^{2}\wedge t^{3}\wedge t^{4})+\cdots+h_{0}(f,g)\cdot(1\wedge t\wedge t^{2}\wedge t^{3})

is well-defined and surjective on
K
-points. It satisfies
\operatorname{H}_{3}=\Lambda\circ h
where
\Lambda
is the matrix from
6.5
.

Proof.

The property
\operatorname{H}_{3}=\Lambda\circ h
follows from the explicit expression of
\Lambda
is
Equation

17
. This also implies that
\varphi
is well-defined because the determinant of
\operatorname{H}_{3}
does not vanish on
\mathscr{T}_{3}
. For the surjectivity statement it suffices to show that every
h_{0},h_{1},h_{2},h_{3}\in K
whose Hankel matrix has rank three can be completed to the sequence of coefficients of the power series expansion of a suitable rational function. This is an easy linear algebra exercise.
∎

Corollary 8.5
.

The arithmetic writhe

\operatorname{w}\colon[\mathbb{P}^{1},\mathbb{P}^{3}]^{\operatorname{I}}_{4}\to\operatorname{GW}(K)\times_{K^{\times}/K^{\times 2}}(K^{\times}/K^{\times 12})

is injective.

Proof.

We consider the composition of maps

\pi_{0}^{\operatorname{N}}\mathscr{T}_{3}(K)\to\pi_{0}^{\operatorname{N}}\mathcal{W}_{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{1}}(4),3}(K)\to\operatorname{GW}(K)\times_{K^{\times}/K^{\times 2}}K^{\times}.

This is the map for which it was proven in
[
Caz12b
, §3.3]
that it is injective. Indeed, this follows from
\operatorname{H}_{3}=\Lambda\circ h
and from the fact that
\operatorname{H}_{3}(f,g)
and
\operatorname{B}_{3}(f,g)
have the same determinant and the same class in
\operatorname{GW}(K)
.
By
8.4
the first map is surjective which implies that the second map is injective. Now the claim follows from
7.16
.
∎

Remark 8.6
.

By the same argument as in
6.6
we can deduce that the image of the map of
8.5
consists of all pairs whose first component can be represented as
\sum_{i=1}^{3}\langle a_{i}\rangle
for
a_{i}\in K^{\times}
.

Remark 8.7
.

In the case
K={\mathbb{R}}
,
8.3
shows the arithmetic writhe of the embedding coincides with the arithmetic writhe of the embedded curve. Thus, we can see it as a map

\operatorname{w}\colon[\mathbb{P}^{1},\mathbb{P}^{3}]^{\operatorname{I}}_{n}\to\operatorname{GW}({\mathbb{R}})

As the rank of
\operatorname{w}([\psi])
is determined by
n
, we do not loose information when post-composing with the signature.
We can thus view
\operatorname{w}
as a map

\operatorname{w}\colon[\mathbb{P}^{1},\mathbb{P}^{3}]^{\operatorname{I}}_{n}\to{\mathbb{Z}}

which is the encomplexed writhe introduced by Viro in
[
Vir01
]
. In
[
Vir01
]
, it was shown that
\operatorname{w}
is an invariant under the possibly coarser equivalence relation of
rigid isotopy
. In other words, the map
\operatorname{w}
factors through the set
[\mathbb{P}^{1},\mathbb{P}^{3}]^{\rm rig}_{n}
of rigid isotopy classes:

[\mathbb{P}^{1},\mathbb{P}^{3}]^{\operatorname{I}}_{n}\to[\mathbb{P}^{1},\mathbb{P}^{3}]^{\rm rig}_{n}\to{\mathbb{Z}}.

It was shown in
[
Bjö11
, Remark 3.21]
that the map
[\mathbb{P}^{1},\mathbb{P}^{3}]^{\rm rig}_{6}\to{\mathbb{Z}}
is not injective. This implies that
\operatorname{w}\colon[\mathbb{P}^{1},\mathbb{P}^{3}]^{\operatorname{I}}_{6}\to\operatorname{GW}({\mathbb{R}})
is also not injective. On the other hand, by
[
Bjö11
, Theorem 1.2]
the map
[\mathbb{P}^{1},\mathbb{P}^{3}]^{\rm rig}_{5}\to{\mathbb{Z}}
is injective. We do not know whether this generalizes to our setup, i.e., whether
\operatorname{w}\colon[\mathbb{P}^{1},\mathbb{P}^{3}]^{\operatorname{I}}_{5}\to\operatorname{GW}({\mathbb{R}})
is injective or maybe even
\operatorname{w}\colon[\mathbb{P}^{1},\mathbb{P}^{3}]^{\operatorname{I}}_{5}\to\operatorname{GW}(K)\times_{K^{\times}/K^{\times 2}}(K^{\times}/K^{\times 4N})
is injective for every
K
.

Example 8.8
.

The
Macaulay2
code “invariants.m2”, attached as ancillary file to this arXiv submission, computes two Ulrich sheaves on the secant variety
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{P}^{6}
of a rational normal curve of degree
6
: the first one is the Ulrich sheaf
{\mathcal{F}}
of rank one coming from the theta characteristic
\alpha=\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{1}}(-1)
as in
5.16
and second is the sheaf
{\mathcal{F}}_{6}
of rank five coming from
5.16
. Then it computes for each of those a symmetric matrix of linear forms as in Theorem
4.17
. Note that, although we have not shown that the sheaf
{\mathcal{F}}_{6}
is a
symmetric
Ulrich sheaf, it turns out that the resulting matrix is symmetric. We denote these matrices by
\Lambda
and
\Lambda^{\prime}
respectively. The signature of
\Lambda
evaluated at a linear subspace
E
of dimension two in
\mathbb{P}^{6}
that is disjoint from the secant variety is the writhe of the projected curve in
\mathbb{P}^{3}
by
6.5
. The signature of
\Lambda^{\prime}
at such a space is a new invariant of the curve which also does not change along algebraic or rigid isotopies. Our code computes these two invariants for three different linear spaces
E
. Here we record the results:

(1)

The linear space
E
is the row-span of the matrix

\begin{pmatrix}2&9&3&1&8&7&3\\
1&6&5&1&9&6&8\\
7&8&1&1&2&6&9\end{pmatrix}.

Both invariants of the projected curve in
\mathbb{P}^{3}
are equal to
0
.

(2)

The linear space
E
is the row-span of the matrix

\begin{pmatrix}2&1&7&2&4&2&0\\
7&6&9&0&3&6&8\\
9&6&8&3&5&1&7\end{pmatrix}.

The writhe of the projected curve in
\mathbb{P}^{3}
is
0
while the new invariant is
4
.

(3)

The linear space
E
is the row-span of the matrix

\begin{pmatrix}0!&1!&2!&3!&4!&5!&6!\\
1!&2!&3!&4!&5!&6!&7!\\

The writhe of the projected curve in
\mathbb{P}^{3}
is

. Note that this is predicted by

KS22b

. The new invariant is
-2
.

In particular, these computations show that the new invariant can distinguish non-isotopic curves which cannot be distinguished by the writhe. It would be interesting to have a local description for this new invariant similar to the one of the writhe.
