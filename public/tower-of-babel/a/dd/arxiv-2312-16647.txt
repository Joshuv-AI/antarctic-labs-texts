Equivariance in Approximation by Compact Sets
Saugata Basu, Alison Rosenblum

Abstract
. We adapt a construction of Gabrielov and Vorobjov for use in the symmetric case. Gabrielov and Vorobjov had developed a means by which one may replace an arbitrary set S S definable in some o-minimal expansion of ℝ \mathbb{R} with a compact set T T . T T is constructed in such a way that for a given m > 0 m>0 we have epimorphisms from the first m m homotopy and homology groups of T T to those of S S . If S S is defined by a boolean combination of statements h ⁡ ( x ) = 0 h(x)=0 and h ⁡ ( x ) > 0 h(x)>0 for various h h in some finite collection of definable continuous functions, one may choose T T so that these maps are isomorphisms for 0 ≤ k ≤ m − 1 0\leq k\leq m-1 . In this case, T T is also defined by functions closely related to those defining S S . In this paper we study sets S S symmetric under the action of some finite reflection group G G . One may see that in the original construction, if S S is defined by functions symmetric relative to the action of G G , then T T will be as well. We show that there is an equivariant map T → S T\rightarrow S inducing the aforementioned epimorphisms and isomorphisms of homotopy and homology groups. We use this result to strengthen theorems of Basu and Riener concerning the multiplicities of Specht modules in the isotypic decomposition of the cohomology spaces of sets defined by polynomials symmetric relative to 𝔖 n \mathfrak{S}_{n} .



1.
Introduction

Fix an o-minimal structure expanding a real closed field
R
(on occasion, we will specify that this real closed field is
\mathbb{R}
; one may if desired assume as much throughout). We will take all sets, maps, etc. to be definable in this structure.

Gabrielov and Vorobjov in
[
9
]
develop a construction by which one may replace an arbitrary definable set
S
by a closed and bounded set
T
, such that we have epimorphisms (and in a special case isomorphisms) from the homotopy and homology groups of the approximating set to those of the original set. In this special case, termed the
constructible case
, we take
S
to be defined by a quantifier-free formula with atoms
h>0
or
h=0
for some finite number of continuous definable functions
h
. In this case, the procedure described in
[
9
]
gives an explicit description of, and control over the number of, the functions defining
T
. Gabrielov and Vorobjov’s paper describes the ramifications of this in calculating upper bounds on Betti numbers for sets definable in certain structures.

In
[
3
]
, Basu and Riener leverage properties of symmetry (particularly, symmetry relative to the standard action of
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
on
R^{n}
) to study the Betti numbers of semialgebraic sets defined by symmetric polynomials of bounded degree, developing algorithms with favorable complexity bounds. The paper makes use of the construction of Gabrielov and Vorobjov presented in
[
9
]
, replacing
S
with a set
T
defined by closed conditions and then applying various results requiring closedness in order to determine the structure of the cohomology spaces of
T
. From Gabrielov and Vorobjob’s results, we know that the cohomology spaces of
T
are isomorphic to those of
S
. If we assume
S
is defined by symmetric polynomials of degree bounded by some
d
at least 2, then the set produced by the Gabrielov-Vorobjov construction is also defined by symmetric polynomials of degree no more than
d
. However, it is not immediately clear whether the maps on the level of homotopy and homology are equivariant. Without equivariance, we cannot conclude that the cohomology spaces of
S
and
T
have the same structures as
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
-modules, meaning that results for
T
only translate back to
S
in part.

This paper establishes an equivariant version of the construction of Gabrielov and Vorobjov in
[
9
]
. We introduce a few pieces of notation in order to present our main theorem.

Notation 1.1
.

Let
S=\{\mathbf{x}\in R^{n}\mid\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{x})\}
be a set defined by a formula
\mathcal{F}
. Say
\mathcal{P}=\{h_{1},\ldots,h_{s}\}
is a finite collection of continuous definable functions
R^{n}\rightarrow R
. If
\mathcal{F}
is a boolean combination of statements of the form
h=0
and
h>0
for
h\in\mathcal{P}
, we call
\mathcal{F}
a
\mathcal{P}
-formula
and
S
a
\mathcal{P}
-set
. If
\mathcal{F}
is a monotone boolean combination (i.e. without negations) of statements of the form
h\geq 0
and
h\leq 0
for
h\in\mathcal{P}
, then we say that
\mathcal{F}
is a
\mathcal{P}
-closed formula
and
S
is a
\mathcal{P}
-closed set
.

Notation 1.2

(
[
9
]
Definition 1.7)
.

When we say a statement holds for

0<\varepsilon_{0}\ll\varepsilon_{1}\ll\cdots\ll\varepsilon_{m}\ll 1

we mean that for each
0\leq i\leq m
there is a definable function
f_{i}:(0,1)^{m-i}\rightarrow(0,1)
(i.e.
f_{m}
is a constant in
(0,1)
) such that the statement holds for all
\varepsilon_{0},\ldots,\varepsilon_{m}
which satisfy, for

, the condition that
0<\varepsilon_{i}<f_{i}(\varepsilon_{i+1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{m})
.

Our equivariant adaptation of the main theorem of Gabrielov and Vorobjov in
[
9
]
is then as follows.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
G
be a finite reflection group acting on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and let
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a definable set symmetric under the action of
G
. Choose an integer
m>0
.

(a)

(Definable Case) Say that
S
is represented in a compact symmetric set
A
by families
\{S_{\delta}\}_{\delta>0}
and
\{S_{\delta,\varepsilon}\}_{\delta,\varepsilon>0}
of compact sets (see Definition
3.1
), and that each
S_{\delta}
and
S_{\delta,\varepsilon}
is also symmetric under the action of
G
. Then for parameters
0<\varepsilon_{0},\delta_{0},\ldots,\varepsilon_{m},\delta_{m}<1
, there exists a compact symmetric set
T=T(\varepsilon_{0},\delta_{0},\ldots,\varepsilon_{m},\delta_{m})
such that, for
0<\delta_{0}\ll\varepsilon_{0}\ll\ldots\ll\varepsilon_{m}\ll\delta_{m}\ll 1
, we have an equivariant map
\psi:T\rightarrow S
inducing equivariant epimorphisms

\displaystyle\psi_{\#,k}

\displaystyle:\pi_{k}(T,*)\rightarrow\pi_{k}(S,*^{\prime})

\displaystyle\psi_{*,k}

\displaystyle:H_{k}(T)\rightarrow H_{k}(S)

for each
1\leq k\leq m
.

(b)

(Constructible Case) Say that
S
is a
\mathcal{P}
-set for
\mathcal{P}=\{h_{1},\ldots,h_{s}\}
a collection of continuous definable functions having the property that
h\circ g\in\mathcal{P}
for all
g\in G
and
h\in\mathcal{P}
. For parameters
r>0
and
0<\varepsilon_{0},\delta_{0},\ldots,\varepsilon_{m},\delta_{m}<1
, let

\mathcal{P}^{\prime}=\bigcup_{h\in\mathcal{P}\cup\{r^{2}-(X_{1}^{2}+\cdots+X_{n}^{2})\}}\bigcup_{j=0}^{m}\{h\pm\varepsilon_{j},h\pm\delta_{j}\}

Then there exists a
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
-closed and bounded set
T
such that for sufficiently large
r
and
0<\delta_{0}\ll\varepsilon_{0}\ll\ldots\ll\varepsilon_{m}\ll\delta_{m}
, there exists an equivariant map
\psi:T\rightarrow S
inducing equivariant homomorphisms

\displaystyle\psi_{\#,k}

\displaystyle:\pi_{k}(T,*)\rightarrow\pi_{k}(S,*^{\prime})

\displaystyle\psi_{*,k}

\displaystyle:H_{k}(T)\rightarrow H_{k}(S)

which are isomorphisms for
1\leq k\leq m-1
and epimorphisms for
k=m
. If
m\geq\dim(S)
, then
\psi
induces a homotopy equivalence
T\simeq S
. Note that in particular if all functions in
\mathcal{P}
are symmetric relative to
G
, then the same is true for
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
.

Parts (a) and (b) of this theorem are proved in Theorem
6.26
and Corollary
6.33
respectively. Our arguments follow the strategy of
[
9
]
, with adjustments to ensure equivariance. The maps composed to obtain
\psi
in the definable and constructible cases are displayed in the summary in Subsection
6.5
.

To construct the maps of homotopy and homology groups in
[
9
]
, Gabrielov and Vorobjov first consider a triangulation adapted to
S
of some larger compact set
A
. Thus, a major step in our paper concerns proving the existence of a triangulation with the needed symmetry and equivariance properties for a given symmetric, definable set. This is done in Theorem
4.7
. We also establish equivariant versions of a few other theorems as needed.

Section
2
contains background and definitions. Section
3
describes the construction of the approximating set
T
in the definable and constructible cases. The results relating to symmetric triangulation and equivariant versions of the other background theorems can be found in Sections
4
and
5
respectively. Finally, in Section
6
, we assemble our results to prove the existence of an equivariant map
T\rightarrow S
inducing the promised epimorphisms and isomorphisms of homotopy and homology groups. Section
7
discusses the ramifications for the results of Basu and Riener in
[
3
]
.

Background

Much of the information here is relatively standard. We include these definitions for completeness, and to establish a few conventions.

2.1.

Definition 2.1

Let

We extend the definition of a symmetric function, standard for the usual action of

and

.

.

2.2.
Reflection Groups

Let
V
be a finite dimensional real vector space equipped with an inner product, and let
\mathcal{O}(V)
denote the group of all orthogonal linear transformations of
V
. We will for the sake of clarity distinguish a
linear hyperplane
(a codimension
1
vector subspace, which must therefore contain
\mathbf{0}
) from an
affine hyperplane
(a codimension
1
affine subspace, which need not pass through the origin). If
P
is a hyperplane (in either sense), then the complement
V\setminus P
has two connected components, which are called (linear or affine, resp.) half-spaces.

Definition 2.5

(see
[
10
]
Chapter 3)
.

Let
G
be a finite subgroup of
\mathcal{O}(V)
. A subset
H\subset V
is a
fundamental region
of
V
relative to
G
provided

(1)

H
is an open subset of
V

(2)

H\cap g(H)=\emptyset
for all
e\neq g\in G

(3)

V=\bigcup_{g\in G}\overline{g(H)}

Theorem 2.6

(
[
10
]
Theorem 3.1.2)
.

Let
G
be a finite subgroup of
\mathcal{O}(V)
. Then there exists a finite collection
\{H_{1},\ldots,H_{k}\}
of linear half-spaces such that the region

H=\bigcap_{i=1}^{k}H_{i}

is a fundamental region of
V
relative to
G
.

We are interested in the case where
G
is a finite reflection group acting on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
(with the standard inner product), i.e.
G
is generated by elements
g
whose action on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
are given by
g(\mathbf{x})
reflecting
\mathbf{x}
across some linear hyperplane
P_{g}
. We will frequently use the fact that any affine hyperplane
P
in
R^{n}
coincides with some
\{\mathbf{x}\in R^{n}\mid L(\mathbf{x})=0\}
, where
L:R^{n}\rightarrow R
is of the form
L(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=a_{0}+a_{1}x_{1}+\cdots+a_{n}x_{n}
for some
a_{0},\ldots,a_{n}\in R
.
P
is a linear hyperplane iff
a_{0}=0
.

Say that
G
is a finite reflection group acting on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then, as detailed in
[
10
]
Chapter 4, we can select a subset
\{g_{1},\ldots,g_{k}\}
of non-identity elements of
G
such that, if
g_{i}
acts by reflecting through the hyperplane
P_{i}
, we can choose functions
L_{i}
with
P_{i}
given by
L_{i}(\mathbf{x})=0
so that

H=\bigcap_{i=1}^{k}\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid L_{i}(\mathbf{x})>0\}

is a fundamental region of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
relative to
G
. The sets of the form
(L_{1}\geq 0)\cap\ldots\cap(L_{i-1}\geq 0)\cap(L_{i}=0)\cap(L_{i+i}\geq 0)\cap\ldots\cap(L_{k}\geq 0)
are called the
\emph{walls}
of this fundamental region.

In this description, for
g\in G
we have that there exists a subset
\lambda_{g}
of
\{1,\ldots,k\}
such that

g(\overline{H})=\left(\bigcap_{i\in\lambda_{g}}L_{i}(\mathbf{x})\leq 0\right)\cap\left(\bigcap_{i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}\setminus\lambda_{g}}L_{i}(\mathbf{x})\geq 0\right)

Then
\overline{H}\cap g(\overline{H})
is given by

H_{\lambda_{g}}=\left(\bigcap_{i\in\lambda_{g}}L_{i}(\mathbf{x})=0\right)\cap\left(\bigcap_{i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}\setminus\lambda_{g}}L_{i}(\mathbf{x})\geq 0\right)

which is an intersection of walls of
H
.
H_{\lambda_{g}}
is also the set of points of
H
fixed by the action of
g
.

Example 2.7
.

In our primary example,
G=\mathfrak{S}_{n}
acts on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
via the standard action given by the permutation of coordinates, i.e. for
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
and
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
we have
\sigma(\mathbf{x})=(x_{\sigma(1)},\ldots,x_{\sigma(n)})
.
Here, we may take as a fundamental region the interior of the Weyl chamber

\mathcal{W}^{(n),\mathrm{o}}=\{(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid x_{1}<\ldots<x_{n}\}

(see
[
3
]
Notation 9). The walls of the Weyl chamber correspond to the adjacent transpositions
s_{i}=(i\;i+1)\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
(i.e. the standard Coxeter generators of
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
). Each
s_{i}
acts by reflecting through the hyperplane given by
x_{i}=x_{i+1}
, so we have that the walls of the Weyl chamber are the sets

\mathcal{W}^{(n)}_{s_{i}}=\{(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid x_{1}\leq\ldots\leq x_{i}=x_{i+1}\leq\ldots\leq x_{n}\}

for
1\leq i\leq n-1
(see
[
3
]
Notation 11).

2.3.
Simplicial Complexes

Our proofs make reference to both abstract and concrete simplicial complexes. Accordingly, we present both viewpoints here, beginning with the concrete setting.

Definition 2.8

(see
[
7
]
Section 4.2)
.

Let the points
v_{0},\ldots,v_{d}\in R^{n}
be affine independent. Then the
open simplex
(of dimension
d
) with vertices
v_{0},\ldots,v_{d}
is the set

\displaystyle\Delta(v_{0},\ldots,v_{d})=\{x

\displaystyle\in R^{n}\mid x=t_{1}v_{1}+\cdots+t_{d}v_{d}

\displaystyle\text{ for some }t_{1},\ldots,t_{d}\in(0,1]\text{ with }t_{1}+\cdots+t_{d}=1\}

The corresponding
closed simplex
is

\displaystyle\overline{\Delta}(v_{0},\ldots,v_{d})=\{x

\displaystyle\in R^{n}\mid x=t_{1}v_{1}+\cdots+t_{d}v_{d}

\displaystyle\text{ for some }t_{1},\ldots,t_{d}\in[0,1]\text{ with }t_{1}+\cdots+t_{d}=1\}

A
face
of a simplex
\overline{\Delta}(v_{0},\ldots,v_{d})
is a simplex
\overline{\Delta}(u_{0},\ldots,u_{d^{\prime}})
with vertex set
\{u_{0},\ldots,u_{d^{\prime}}\}\subset\{v_{0},\ldots,v_{d}\}
.

If we are drawing our vertices from an indexed set
\{v_{i}\}_{i\in I}
, we may simply refer to a simplex
\Delta(v_{i_{0}},\ldots,v_{i_{d}})
as
\Delta(i_{0},\ldots,i_{d})
.

Definition 2.9

(see
[
7
]
Section 4.2)
.

A (finite)
simplicial complex
in
R^{n}
is a finite collection
\Lambda
of open simplices in
R^{n}
with the following properties

(1)

If
\Delta\in\Lambda
, then for each face
\overline{\Delta}^{\prime}
of
\overline{\Delta}
, we have that
\Delta^{\prime}\in\Lambda

(2)

If
\Delta_{1},\Delta_{2}\in\Lambda
, then
\overline{\Delta}_{1}\cap\overline{\Delta}_{2}=\overline{\Delta}_{3}
for some
\Delta_{3}\in\Lambda
.

Given a simplicial complex
\Lambda
in
R^{n}
, we call
\bigcup_{\Delta\in\Lambda}\Delta\subset R^{n}
the
geometric realization
of
\Lambda
, and denote it
\lvert\Lambda\rvert
.

If not clear from context, we may include a subscript to indicate where the realization is taking place. For example, say
\Lambda
is a simplicial complex in
R^{n}
seen as a subset of
R^{n}\times R^{m}
. Then we may wish to distinguish between
\lvert\Lambda\rvert_{R^{n}}
and
\lvert\Lambda\rvert_{R^{n+m}}
, the realizations of
\Lambda
in
R^{n}
and
R^{n+m}
respectively.

Definition 2.10
.

Let
\Delta,\Delta^{\prime}\in\Lambda
for some simplicial complex
\Lambda
. Then
\Delta^{\prime}
is a
subsimplex
of
\Delta
if
\Delta^{\prime}\neq\Delta
and
\Delta^{\prime}\subset\overline{\Delta}
(in other words, if
\overline{\Delta}^{\prime}
is a proper face of
\overline{\Delta}
).

Definition 2.11
.

A
k
-flag
of cells in a CW complex (so in particular, of simplices in a simplicial complex) is a sequence
\sigma_{0},\ldots,\sigma_{k}
of cells such that
\sigma_{i}
is contained in the boundary of
\sigma_{i-1}
for each
1\leq i\leq k
.

When speaking of symmetry for any CW complex (and in particular the geometric realization of a simplicial complex
\Lambda
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
), we will in general consider the action of
G
on our decomposition to be that induced by a given action of
G
on our larger space:

Definition 2.12
.

Let
G
be a group acting on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. A CW complex
X
is
symmetric
with respect to the action of
G
if for each cell
\sigma\in\Lambda
and each
g\in G
,
g(\sigma)=\{g(x)\mid x\in\sigma\}
is again a cell of
X
.

In particular, this means that
X
is symmetric as a subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Now, we shift our attention to abstract simplicial complexes.

Definition 2.13

(from
[
14
]
)
.

An

is a set

of vertices and a set

of finite nonempty subsets of

which we consider to be simplices, having the properties

(1)

\{v\}\in\Lambda
for each

(2)

One can and often does ignore the distinction between an abstract simplicial complex and its set of simplices. An abstract simplicial complex also comes with a geometric realization, which we will explicitly describe for the sake of later use.

(see
[
14
]
)
.

Let
\Lambda
be a nonempty abstract simplicial complex. The

of

, denoted by

, is the space whose points are functions

(2)

[

).

.

(from
[

]

.

Let

. For

is a definable set.

The reader unfamiliar with o-minimal geometry is also encouraged to review the cell decomposition theorem (
[

]
Chapter 3 Section 2 or
[
7
]
Section 2.2) before approaching our equivariant triangulation proofs in Section

.

For a connected topological space
X
, we let
\pi_{k}(X)
denote the
k
th homotopy group. Let
H_{k}(X)
be the
k
th singular homology group with coefficients in some fixed Abelian group, and denote by
b_{k}(X)=\rank(H_{k}(X))
the
k
th Betti number of
X
. Throughout, we will use
\simeq
to denote homotopy equivalence and
\cong
to denote group isomorphism.

The following two theorems are used heavily in Gabrielov and Vorobjov’s proofs in
[
9
]
and referenced also in our own arguments. These do not require equivariant versions; if a map

is equivariant, the induced homomorphisms of homotopy and homology groups will be as well.

Theorem 2.18

(Whitehead Theorem on Weak Homotopy Equivalence,
[
14
]
7.6.24)
.

A map
f:X\rightarrow Y
between connected CW complexes is a weak homotopy equivalence (i.e. the induced homomorphism of homotopy groups
f_{\#,k}:\pi_{k}(X)\rightarrow\pi_{k}(Y)
is an isomorphism for each
k>0
) iff
f
is a homotopy equivalence.

Theorem 2.19

(Whitehead Theorem on Homotopy and Homology,
[
14
]
7.5.9)
.

Let
f:X\rightarrow Y
be a continuous map between path connected topological spaces. If there is a
k>0
such that the induced homomorphism of homotopy groups
f_{\#,j}:\pi_{j}(X)\rightarrow\pi_{j}(Y)
is an isomorphism for

and an epimorphism for

, then the induced homomorphism of homology groups
f_{*,j}:H_{j}(X)\rightarrow H_{j}(Y)
is an isomorphism for

and an epimorphism for
j=k
.

3.
The Gabrielov-Vorobjov Construction

In this section, we describe the construction of the approximating set
T
, as given by Gabrielov and Vorobjov in
[
9
]
. We also discuss the implications when symmetry is introduced to the construction.

In order to construct
T
, we begin with families of compact sets
\{S_{\delta}\}
and
\{S_{\delta,\varepsilon}\}
which represent
S
in some larger compact set
A
.

Definition 3.1

(
[
9
]
Definition 1.1)
.

Let
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be definable, and let
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a compact definable set with
S\subset A
. Let
\{S_{\delta}\}_{\delta>0}
and
\{S_{\delta,\varepsilon}\}_{\delta,\varepsilon>0}
be definable families of compact subsets of
A
. We say
S
is
represented
by
\{S_{\delta}\}_{\delta>0}
and
\{S_{\delta,\varepsilon}\}_{\delta,\varepsilon>0}
in
A
if we have that

(a)

for all
\delta^{\prime},\delta\in(0,1)
, if
\delta^{\prime}>\delta
, then
S_{\delta^{\prime}}\subset S_{\delta}

(b)

S=\bigcup_{\delta>0}S_{\delta}

and furthermore for each
\delta>0

(i)

for all
\varepsilon^{\prime},\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, if
\varepsilon^{\prime}>\varepsilon
, then
S_{\delta,\varepsilon}\subset S_{\delta,\varepsilon^{\prime}}

(ii)

S_{\delta}=\bigcap_{\varepsilon>0}S_{\delta,\varepsilon}

(iii)

for all
\delta^{\prime}
sufficiently smaller than
\delta
and for all
\varepsilon^{\prime}>0
there exists a set
U\subset A
with

open in
A
and
S_{\delta}\subset U\subset S_{\delta^{\prime},\varepsilon^{\prime}}

We then take

to be the union of a selection of finitely many of the sets

.

Definition 3.2

(
[
9
]
Definition 1.8)
.

For any nonnegative integer

and parameters
\varepsilon_{0},\delta_{0},\varepsilon_{1},\delta_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{m},\delta_{m}
, we denote

T=T(\varepsilon_{0},\delta_{0},\varepsilon_{1},\delta_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{m},\delta_{m}):=S_{\delta_{0},\varepsilon_{0}}\cup S_{\delta_{1},\varepsilon_{1}}\cup\cdots\cup S_{\delta_{m},\varepsilon_{m}}

Gabrielov and Vorobjov refer to the general case, in which
S
is an arbitrary definable set together with any representing families
\{S_{\delta}\}
and
\{S_{\delta,\varepsilon}\}
, as the
definable case
. If
S
is a
\mathcal{P}
-set for some collection
\mathcal{P}=\{h_{1},\ldots,h_{s}\}
of definable continuous functions
h_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, they prescribe particular choices of

,
\{S_{\delta}\}
, and
\{S_{\delta,\varepsilon}\}
(see Definition

), which grants an extra property (termed separability; see Definition
6.27
or
[
9
]
Section 5.2) that allows for the stronger version of the results. We call this case the
constructible case
. The choices in the constructible case also guarantee that our sets
S_{\delta}
and
S_{\delta,\varepsilon}
are described by definable functions closely related to the original functions
h_{i}
.

In these settings, Gabrielov and Vorobjov prove the following theorem. Note that they show (
[
9
]
Lemma 1.9) that provided
m
is at least
1
, there exists a one-to-one correspondence between the connected components of
S
and
T
. This allows Gabrielov and Vorobjov to consider each connected component individually and so ignore basepoint considerations, a luxury we do not have.

Theorem 3.3

(
[
9
]
Theorem 1.10)
.

(i)

(Definable Case) For
0<\varepsilon_{0}\ll\delta_{0}\ll\cdots\ll\varepsilon_{m}\ll\delta_{m}\ll 1
and every
1\leq k\leq m
, there are epimorphisms

\displaystyle\psi_{\#,k}

\displaystyle:\pi_{k}(T)\rightarrow\pi_{k}(S)

\displaystyle\psi_{*,k}

\displaystyle:H_{k}(T)\rightarrow H_{k}(S)

and in particular,
\rank(H_{k}(S))\leq\rank(H_{k}(T))

(ii)

(Constructible Case) In the constructible case, for
0<\varepsilon_{0}\ll\delta_{0}\ll\cdots\ll\varepsilon_{m}\ll\delta_{m}\ll 1
and every
1\leq k\leq m-1
,
\psi_{\#,k}
and
\psi_{*,k}
are isomorphisms. In particular,
\rank(H_{k}(S))=\rank(H_{k}(T))
. Moreover, if
m\geq\dim(S)
, then
T\simeq S
.

In applications, even in the definable case one may via this construction obtain improved upper bounds on the first
m
Betti numbers of a given set
S
. Our primary setting of interest, the one considered by Basu and Reiner in
[
3
]
, utilizes the constructible case.

Our main theorem (Theorem
1.3
) is an equivariant version of the above theorem. It is clear from the definition of
T
that, so long as each set in the family
\{S_{\delta,\varepsilon}\}
is symmetric relative to the action of some group
G
, then
T
will be as well. In the definable case, we need only assume that we have chosen our families to consist of symmetric sets. In the constructible case, we will show that if
S
is symmetric and the collection
\{h_{1},\ldots,h_{s}\}
is invariant under the action of
G
, our choices will produce symmetric sets. In subsequent sections, we show that for
G
a finite reflection group acting on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we can in fact construct an equivariant map
\psi:T\rightarrow S
which induces the desired isomorphisms and epimorphisms
\psi_{\#,k}
and
\psi_{*,k}
on the level of homotopy and homology. The remainder of this section is devoted to describing the sets
S_{\delta}
and
S_{\delta,\varepsilon}
in the constructible case.

3.1.
Constructible Case: Bounding
S

Let
S\subset R^{n}
be definable, and assume
S
is unbounded. Via the conical structure at infinity of definable sets, there exists an
r\in R
,
r>0
, such that
S
is (definably) homotopy equivalent to
S\cap\overline{B(0,r)}
. Basu, Pollack, and Roy in
[
1
]
show this for semialgebraic sets. However, their proof holds for any o-minimal structure in which addition and mulitpication are definable. The proof in
[
1
]
centers on the local conical structure specifically of semialgebraic sets, but this property (a consequence of Hardt triviality) holds for any o-minimal expansion of some real closed field (see
[
8
]
Chapter 9 Theorem 2.3). The remaining steps of their proof may be performed in any o-minimal structure provided it contains addition and mulitpication.

Proposition 3.4

(Conical Structure at Infinity, see
[
1
]
Proposition 5.49)
.

Let
A\subset R^{n}
be definable. Then there exists an
r\in R
,
r>0
, such that
A
is definably homotopy equivalent to
A\cap\overline{B(0,r)}
. Specifically, there exists a continuous definable function
h:[0,1]\times A\rightarrow A
with
h(0,-)=\id_{A}
,
h(1,-)
having image contained in
A\cap\overline{B(0,r)}
, and with
h(t,a)=a
for each
t\in[0,1]
and
a\in A\cap\overline{B(0,r)}
.

In our case, for any set
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
that is symmetric under the action of a finite reflection group
G
, certainly
S\cap\overline{B(0,r)}
is as well. The inclusion
S\cap\overline{B(0,r)}\hookrightarrow S
is also clearly equivariant, and so induces equivariant isomorphisms of homotopy and homology groups

\displaystyle\pi_{k}(S\cap\overline{B(0,r)},*)

\displaystyle\rightarrow\pi_{k}(S,*)

\displaystyle H_{k}(S\cap\overline{B(0,r)})

\displaystyle\rightarrow H_{k}(S)

for each
k\geq 0
.

We now replace
S
by
S\cap\overline{B(0,r)}
to assume henceforth that
S
is bounded. If
S
was a
\mathcal{P}
-set for some
\mathcal{P}=\{h_{1},\ldots,h_{s}\}
, we replace
\mathcal{P}
with
\mathcal{P}\cup\{r^{2}-(X_{1}^{2}+\cdots X_{n}^{2})\}
, and so increase
s
by one. Since
r^{2}-(X_{1}^{2}+\cdots+X_{n}^{2})
is symmetric relative to the action of any finite reflection group
G
, our collection
\mathcal{P}
remains invariant (and in fact if each
h_{i}\in\mathcal{P}
was symmetric, the same remains true after including this new function in our collection). Note also that in the case that each
h_{i}(x)
is a polynomial, the only instance in which we have increased the maximum degree among our collection is if each of
\{h_{1},\ldots,h_{s}\}
had degree one.

This is slightly different from the procedure employed by Gabrielov and Vorobjov in
[
9
]
, where the larger compact set
A
was taken as the definable one-point compactification of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. This choice aids certain results on Betti numbers in
[
9
]
, but we find our own method more convenient when tracking the group’s action. Since Gabrielov and Vorobjov bound their sets
\{S_{\delta}\}
and
\{S_{\delta,\varepsilon}\}
in the constructible case by intersecting with closed balls of radius
1/\delta
, our method does not stray too far from the intuition of the original. Our assumption will increase the number of equations needed to define
T
, but only slightly.

3.2.
Constructible Case: the Sets
\{S_{\delta}\}
and
\{S_{\delta,\varepsilon}\}

In the constructible case (with the assumption that
S
is bounded), we form families
\{S_{\delta}\}_{\delta>0}
and
\{S_{\delta,\varepsilon}\}_{\delta,\varepsilon>0}
by decomposing
S
into sign sets of the functions defining
S
, and then contracting inequalities and expanding equalities by a factor of
\delta
or
\varepsilon
respectively, in a manner we now proceed to describe.

Definition 3.5

(
[
9
]
Definition 1.5)
.

Let
\mathcal{P}=\{h_{1},\ldots,h_{s}\}
be a finite collection of functions with each
h_{i}:R^{n}\rightarrow R
. Let
\{I_{0},I_{+},I_{-}\}
be a partition of
\{1,\ldots,s\}
. Then the set
B_{\mathcal{P},(I_{0},I_{+},I_{-})}
given by

\{\mathbf{x}\in R^{n}\mid\bigwedge_{i\in I_{0}}(h_{i}(\mathbf{x})=0)\wedge\bigwedge_{i\in I_{+}}(h_{i}(\mathbf{x})>0)\wedge\bigwedge_{i\in I_{-}}(h_{i}(\mathbf{x})<0)\}

will be called the

of
\mathcal{P}
corresponding to the tuple
(I_{0},I_{+},I_{-})
.

Note that any two distinct sign sets of some
\mathcal{P}=\{h_{1},\ldots,h_{s}\}
are disjoint, and that for
S
a
\mathcal{P}
-set, we may write

as a union of sign sets of the functions in
\mathcal{P}
. Though for a given collection
\mathcal{P}
of functions, some tuples
(I_{0},I_{+},I_{-})
will produce empty sign sets, we will exclude these tuples from the sign set decomposition of any
\mathcal{P}
-set.

In the constructible case, we make the following choices for
A
,
\{S_{\delta}\}
and
\{S_{\delta,\varepsilon}\}
.

Definition 3.6

(from
[
9
]
)
.

Let
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a bounded
\mathcal{P}
-set for some collection
\mathcal{P}=\{h_{1},\ldots,h_{s}\}
of continuous definable functions
h:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. Let
\mathcal{B}
denote the set of tuples
(I_{0},I_{+},I_{-})
corresponding to elements in the sign set decomposition of
S
.

Let
r>0
be such that
S\subset\overline{B(0,r)}
. Then take
A=\overline{B(0,r^{\prime})}
for some
r^{\prime}
sufficiently larger than
r
. For each
\delta>0
, we let
S_{\delta}
be the union of sets defined by

\bigwedge_{i\in I_{0}}(h_{i}=0)\wedge\bigwedge_{i\in I_{+}}(h_{i}\geq\delta)\wedge\bigwedge_{i\in I_{-}}(h_{i}\leq-\delta)

over all tuples
(I_{0},I_{+},I_{-})\in\mathcal{B}
. For
\delta,\varepsilon>0
, we let
S_{\delta,\varepsilon}
be the union of the sets given by

\bigwedge_{i\in I_{0}}(-\varepsilon\leq h_{i}\leq\varepsilon)\wedge\bigwedge_{i\in I_{+}}(h_{i}\geq\delta)\wedge\bigwedge_{i\in I_{-}}(h_{i}\leq-\delta)

again over all tuples
(I_{0},I_{+},I_{-})\in\mathcal{B}
.

One may check that
S
is indeed represented by the families
\{S_{\delta}\}_{\delta>0}
and
\{S_{\delta,\varepsilon}\}_{\delta,\varepsilon>0}
in
A
. It remains to consider the conditions needed to ensure that the sets
S_{\delta}
and
S_{\delta,\varepsilon}
are symmetric.

Proposition 3.7
.

Assume we are in the constructible case. If
S
is symmetric under the action of
G
and furthermore we have that for each
g\in G
,
\{h_{1}\circ g,\ldots,h_{s}\circ g\}=\{h_{1},\ldots,h_{s}\}
, then every set in the families
\{S_{\delta}\}_{\delta>0}
and
\{S_{\delta,\varepsilon}\}_{\delta,\varepsilon>0}
is symmetric.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{P}=\{h_{1},\ldots,h_{s}\}
. Note that for a sign set
B=B_{\mathcal{P},(I_{0},I_{+},I_{-})}
and any
g\in G
,
g(B)=\{g(\mathbf{x})\mid\mathbf{x}\in B\}
is given by

\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid\bigwedge_{i\in I_{0}}(h_{i}(g^{-1}(\mathbf{x}))=0)\\
\wedge\bigwedge_{i\in I_{+}}(h_{i}(g^{-1}(\mathbf{x}))>0)\wedge\bigwedge_{i\in I_{-}}(h_{i}(g^{-1}(\mathbf{x}))<0)\}

For each
g\in G
, we define a function on the indices
\{1,\ldots,s\}\rightarrow\{1,\ldots,s\}
, given by
g(i)=j
if
h_{j}=h_{i}\circ g^{-1}
.

Let
\mathcal{B}
be the collection of tuples
(I_{0},I_{+},I_{-})
corresponding to the sign sets contained in
S
. We claim
\mathcal{B}
has the property that for each
g\in G
, if
(I_{0},I_{+},I_{-})\in\mathcal{B}
then
(g(I_{0}),g(I_{+}),g(I_{-}))\in\mathcal{B}
. Indeed, if
B
is the sign set corresponding to
(I_{0},I_{+},I_{-})
, we have that
B\subset S
and hence
g(B)\subset S
. But we have seen above that
g(B)
is the sign set corresponding to
(g(I_{0}),g(I_{+}),g(I_{-}))
. The symmetry of each
S_{\delta}
and each
S_{\delta,\varepsilon}
follows from this property and the descriptions of each
S_{\delta}
and
S_{\delta,\varepsilon}
given in Definition
3.6
.
∎

In particular, if each
h_{i}
is a symmetric function, then
h_{i}\circ g=h_{i}
, and so clearly
\{h_{1}\circ g,\ldots,h_{s}\circ g\}=\{h_{1},\ldots,h_{s}\}
for every
g\in G
.

3.3.
Connected Components

From the definitions (in both the definable and constructible cases), we see a clear correspondence between the connected components of
T
and those of
S
. Let
S
be represented in
A
by any compact families
\{S_{\delta}\}
and
\{S_{\delta,\varepsilon}\}
, and let
T=T(\varepsilon_{0},\delta_{0},\ldots,\varepsilon_{m},\delta_{m})
be as described in Definition
3.2
. For
0<\varepsilon_{0}\ll\delta_{0}\ll\cdots\ll\varepsilon_{m}\ll\delta_{m}\ll 1
, let
\mathbf{S}
and
\mathbf{T}
denote the sets of connected components of
S
and
T
respectively. Gabrielov and Vorobjov describe a map
C:\mathbf{T}\rightarrow\mathbf{S}
. If
S^{\prime}
is a connected component of
S
, then those elements of
\mathbf{T}
which map to
S^{\prime}
under
C
are exactly those components which would make up the approximating set defined relative to
S^{\prime}
alone. Thus, by the symmetry of
T
and
S
,
C
is equivariant. Gabrielov and Vorobjov demonstrate in
[
9
]
Lemma 1.9 that, provided
m>0
, we may choose the conditions upon the parameters in such a way that
C
is bijective. Hence, though we can’t simply reduce to the case of a connected set without risking a loss of symmetry, we may when needed use
C
to pair the connected components of
S
and
T
and then apply the needed theorems to each pair individually.

4.
Symmetric Triangulation

The construction of the maps in
[
9
]
relies upon triangulating the given set
S
inside the larger compact set
A
. Definable sets are triangulable (see for example
[
7
]
Theorem 4.4, quoted
below
). However, in order to ensure equivariance, we will need a triangulation that respects the action of our group. That is the aim of this section.

4.1.
Triangulation of Definable Sets

Definition 4.1

(
[
7
]
Section 4.3)
.

Let
A
be a closed bounded definable subset of
R^{n}
. Then a
triangulation

(\Lambda,\Phi)
of
A
is a finite simplicial complex
\Lambda
in
R
together with a definable homeomorphism
\Phi:\lvert\Lambda\rvert\rightarrow A
.

If we wish to decompose a set which is not necessarily closed into images of simplices, we must do so within some larger compact set. For
A\subset R^{n}
compact and
S\subset A
, we will say a triangulation
(\Lambda,\Phi)
of
A
is
adapted
to
S
if
S
is a union of images by
\Phi
of simplices of
\Lambda
. Triangulations of compact definable sets always exist, and may be adapted to any finite collection of subsets.

Theorem 4.2

(Triangulation of Definable Sets (
[
7
]
Theorem 4.4))
.

Let
A
be a closed and bounded definable subset of
R^{n}
, and let
S_{1},\ldots,S_{l}
be definable subsets of
A
. Then there exists a triangulation
(\Lambda,\Phi)
of
A
adapted to each of
S_{1},\ldots,S_{l}
. Furthermore, we may choose all vertices of
\Lambda
to be in
\mathbb{Q}^{n}
.

We need to triangulate the symmetric set
S
in a manner that retains symmetry relative to our group of interest. Specifically, we would like a triangulation with the following properties:

Definition 4.3
.

Let
G
be a finite reflection group acting on
R^{n}
and let
A\subset R^{n}
be a closed bounded definable set symmetric relative to
G
. A triangulation
(\Lambda,\Phi)
of
A
is said to be an
equivariant triangulation
if
\Lambda
is symmetric as a simplicial complex and the map
\Phi:\lvert\Lambda\rvert\rightarrow A
is equivariant.

Our general strategy for obtaining an equivariant triangulation of
A
adapted to
S
involves triangulating a fundamental region of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and then applying the action of
G
. This strategy has appeared in other contexts; see in particular
[
12
]
which proves such a result for smooth manifolds. We would like to establish a proof in the definable case. Furthermore, we would like our proof to follow the spirit of
[
7
]
Theorem 4.4, in which we create a concrete simplicial complex in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
symmetric relative to our existing action of
G
.

In order to make this approach feasible in the concrete setting, we must triangulate the portion of
A
lying in our fundamental region in such a way that the resulting simplicial complex lies within the same fundamental region of
R^{n}
, with points lying in its walls remaining as such. This motivates the following definition.

Definition 4.4
.

Let
B\subset R^{n}
. If a triangulation
(\Lambda,\Phi)
of
A\subset R^{n}
is adapted to
A\cap B
and furthermore we have
x\in A\cap B
if and only if
\Phi^{-1}(x)\in\lvert\Lambda\rvert\cap B
, then we say the triangulation
respects
the set
B
.

To show that we may find a triangulation respecting our fundamental region and its walls, we will in fact prove something slightly stronger: for an arrangement of affine hyperplanes given by some collection
\{L_{1}(\mathbf{x})=0,\ldots,L_{k}(\mathbf{x})=0\}
of linear functions, we can find a triangulation of
A
that respects any of the various half-spaces determined by our hyperplanes (for convenience, we will phrase this as respecting sign sets of
\{L_{1},\ldots,L_{k}\}
). We lose the property that all vertices lie in
\mathbb{Q}^{n}
, but the vertices of our triangulation will be rational expressions in the coefficients of
L_{1},\ldots,L_{k}
. Since our application does not in fact require any such condition on the vertices, this is not an issue here.

The reader may wish to study the proof of the following lemma in conjunction with Example
4.6
, in order to see concrete usage of the notation set forth in the proof.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
A\subset R^{n}
be closed, bounded, and definable, and let
S_{1},\ldots,S_{l}
be definable subsets of
A
. Let
\{L_{1},\ldots,L_{k}\}
be a collection of functions with each
L_{i}:R^{n}\rightarrow R
given by
L_{i}(\mathbf{x})=a_{i,0}+a_{i,1}x_{1}+\cdots+a_{i,n}x_{n}
for some
a_{i,j}\in R
. Then there exists a triangulation
(\Lambda,\Phi)
of
A
a which is adapted to each
S_{1},\ldots,S_{l}
and which respects all sign sets of
\{L_{1},\ldots,L_{k}\}
. Furthermore, we can choose this triangulation so that the coordinates of all vertices are in
\mathbb{Q}(\{a_{i,j}\mid 1\leq i\leq k,0\leq j\leq n\})^{n}
.

Proof.

We can obtain a triangulation with the desired properties by way of a few slight modifications to the proof of Theorem
4.2
presented in
[
7
]
. Specifically, the proof there chooses a number of points
b_{\nu}
which serve as vertices in the simplicial complex
\Lambda
. We must add the requirement that our points
b_{\nu}
belong to the correct sign sets, and confirm that our additional claims hold with this adjustment.

The proof in
[
7
]
proceeds by induction on dimension. In the case
n=1
, we have that
L_{i}=a_{i,0}+a_{i,1}x_{1}
for each
i
. We may assume without loss of generality that
a_{i,1}\neq 0
for every
i
. Set
c_{i}=-\frac{a_{i,0}}{a_{i,1}}
. Reorder and remove duplicates to assume we have points
c_{1}<\cdots<c_{k}\in R
. Then the sign sets we must respect are the points and intervals
(-\infty,c_{1})
,
\{c_{i}\}
for each
i
,
(c_{i},c_{i+1})
for
1\leq i\leq k-1
, and
(c_{k},\infty)
. As
[
7
]
describes, we choose points
\xi_{1}<\cdots<\xi_{p}\in R
so that each of
A
,
S_{1}\ldots,S_{l}
, and each nonempty intersection of
A
and a sign set of
\{L_{1},\ldots,L_{k}\}
can be written as a union of points and intervals
\{\xi_{\mu}\}
and
(\xi_{\mu},\xi_{\mu+1})
.

We define a map
\tau:\{\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{p}\}\rightarrow R
as follows:

•

If
k=0
, then let
\tau(\xi_{i})=i-1
for each
i
.

Otherwise,

•

If
\xi_{\mu}=c_{i}
for some
i
, let
\tau(\xi_{\mu})=c_{i}

•

Let

and all its walls. In particular,
\lvert\Lambda\rvert\subset\overline{H}
.

We now define our proposed triangulation of
S
. Let

\Lambda=\bigcup_{g\in G}\{g(\Delta)\mid\Delta\in\Lambda_{\id}\}

and let
\Phi:\lvert\Lambda\rvert\rightarrow A
be given by
\Phi(\mathbf{x})=g(\Phi_{\id}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}))
where
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in\lvert\Lambda_{\id}\rvert
and
g\in G
are such that
g(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})=\mathbf{x}
. We claim that (everything is well-defined and)
\Lambda
and
\Phi
give an equivariant triangulation of
A
adapted to
S_{1},\ldots,S_{l}
.

\Lambda
is a symmetric simplicial complex: We have that if
\Delta\in\Lambda_{\id}
and
g\in G
,
g(\Delta)
remains a simplex in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
by linearity of
g
, and so
\Lambda
is a collection of simplices. The symmetry property for
\Lambda
then holds by construction, so it remains to show that for
\Delta_{i},\Delta_{j}\in\Lambda
,
\overline{\Delta}_{i}\cap\overline{\Delta}_{j}
is the closure of some simplex in
\Lambda
. Without loss of generality, assume
\Delta_{1}\in\Lambda_{\id}
and
\Delta_{2}=g(\Delta_{2}^{\prime})
for
\Delta_{2}^{\prime}\in\Lambda_{\id}
and
g\in G
. This means
\overline{\Delta}_{1}\cap\overline{g(\Delta_{2}^{\prime})}\subset\overline{H}\cap g(\overline{H})
. As described in Subsection
2.1
,
\overline{H}\cap g(\overline{H})=H_{\lambda_{g}}
is a set of the form

H_{\lambda_{g}}=\left(\bigcap_{i\in\lambda_{g}}\{L_{i}(\mathbf{x})=0\}\right)\cap\left(\bigcap_{i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}\setminus\lambda_{g}}\{L_{i}(\mathbf{x})\geq 0\}\right)

which in particular is a sign set of
\{L_{1},\ldots,L_{k}\}
. Since
(\Lambda_{\id},\Phi_{\id})
is hence adapted to
A\cap H_{\lambda_{g}}
, we have that
\overline{\Delta}_{1}\cap H_{\lambda_{g}}
is also the closure of a simplex of
\Lambda_{\id}
. Since points of
H_{\lambda_{g}}
are fixed under the action of
g
,
\overline{\Delta}_{2}\cap H_{\lambda_{g}}=\overline{\Delta}_{2}^{\prime}\cap H_{\lambda_{g}}
is the closure of a simplex of
\Lambda_{\id}
as well. Then
\overline{\Delta}_{1}\cap\overline{\Delta}_{2}=(\overline{\Delta}_{1}\cap H_{\lambda_{g}})\cap(\overline{\Delta}_{2}\cap H_{\lambda_{g}})
is an intersection of closures of simplices of
\Lambda_{\id}
, and hence a common face of
\overline{\Delta}_{1}
and
\overline{\Delta}_{2}
.

Vertices of
\Lambda
are in
\mathbb{Q}(\{a_{i,j}\})^{n}
: We will show that, for
g\in G
and any point
\mathbf{x}
in
\mathbb{Q}(\{a_{i,j}\})^{n}
,
g(\mathbf{x})\in\mathbb{Q}(\{a_{i,j}\})^{n}
. Since any element
g
of
G
can be written as a product of those elements
g_{1},\ldots,g_{k}
, where the action of
g_{i}
is reflection through the linear hyperplane
L_{i}=0
, we may assume
g=g_{i}
for some
1\leq i\leq k
. The reflection of
\mathbf{x}
through
L_{i}=0
is given by

g_{i}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{x}-2\frac{(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{r}_{i})\mathbf{r}_{i}}{(\mathbf{r}_{i},\mathbf{r}_{i})}

where we take
\mathbf{r}_{i}
to be the vector
\left<a_{i,1},\ldots,a_{i,n}\right>
(which is perpendicular to the hyperplane
L_{i}=0
). Then since we have assumed that we are using the standard inner product on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the coordinates of
g_{i}(\mathbf{x})
are all also in
\mathbb{Q}(\{a_{i,j}\})
, as desired.

\Phi
is well-defined: Let
\mathbf{x}\in\lvert\Lambda\rvert
and say that
\mathbf{x}=g_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{1})=g_{2}(\mathbf{x}_{2})
for
\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2}\in\lvert\Lambda_{\id}\rvert
and
g_{1},g_{2}\in G
. We must show that
g_{1}(\Phi_{\id}(\mathbf{x}_{1}))=g_{2}(\Phi_{\id}(\mathbf{x}_{2}))
. Because
\mathbf{x}_{1}=g_{1}^{-1}(g_{2}(\mathbf{x}_{2}))
with
\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2}\in\lvert\Lambda_{\id}\rvert
, we have that
\mathbf{x}_{1}\in\lvert\Lambda_{\id}\rvert\cap g_{1}^{-1}\circ g_{2}(\lvert\Lambda_{\id}\rvert)\subset H_{\lambda_{g_{1}^{-1}g_{2}}}
. Since
g_{1}^{-1}\circ g_{2}
fixes the points of
H_{\lambda_{g_{1}^{-1}g_{2}}}
, we obtain that
\mathbf{x}_{1}=\mathbf{x}_{2}
. Since
\Phi_{\id}
carries
H_{\lambda_{g_{1}^{-1}g_{2}}}
to itself, we have that
\Phi_{\id}(\mathbf{x}_{1})\in H_{\lambda_{g_{1}^{-1}g_{2}}}
, as is
g_{1}^{-1}\circ g_{2}(\Phi_{\id}(\mathbf{x}_{1}))
, and so
\Phi_{\id}(\mathbf{x}_{1})=g_{1}^{-1}\circ g_{2}(\Phi_{\id}(\mathbf{x}_{1}))
, i.e. we have obtained that
g_{1}(\Phi_{\id}(\mathbf{x}_{1}))=g_{2}(\Phi_{\id}(\mathbf{x}_{2}))
. That the image of
\Phi
is
A
follows from the symmetry of
A=\{g(\mathbf{x})\mid\mathbf{x}\in A\cap\overline{H}\text{ and }g\in G\}
.

\Phi
is a homeomorphism: We have already established that the surjectivity of
\Phi
follows from the surjectivity of
\Phi_{\id}
onto
A\cap\overline{H}
. To show injectivity, say
\Phi(\mathbf{x}_{1})=\Phi(\mathbf{x}_{2})
for some
\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2}\in\lvert\Lambda\rvert
, i.e.
g_{1}(\Phi_{\id}(\mathbf{x}_{1}^{\prime}))=g_{2}(\Phi_{\id}(\mathbf{x}_{2}^{\prime}))
for some
\mathbf{x}_{1}^{\prime},\mathbf{x}_{2}^{\prime}\in\lvert\Lambda_{\id}\rvert
and
g_{1},g_{2}\in G
. Then since
\Phi_{\id}(\mathbf{x}_{1}^{\prime})=g_{1}^{-1}\circ g_{2}(\Phi_{\id}(\mathbf{x}_{2}^{\prime}))
, both are in
H_{\lambda_{g_{1}^{-1}g_{2}}}
, and so
\Phi_{\id}(\mathbf{x}_{1}^{\prime})=\Phi_{\id}(\mathbf{x}_{2}^{\prime})
. By the injectivity of
\Phi_{\id}
, this means
\mathbf{x}_{1}^{\prime}=\mathbf{x}_{2}^{\prime}
. Finally, since
\mathbf{x}_{1}^{\prime}=\mathbf{x}_{2}^{\prime}
is in
H_{\lambda_{g_{1}^{-1}g_{2}}}
,
\mathbf{x}_{1}^{\prime}=g_{1}^{-1}\circ g_{2}(\mathbf{x}_{2}^{\prime})
, so we have
\mathbf{x}_{1}=g_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{1}^{\prime})=g_{2}(\mathbf{x}_{2}^{\prime})=\mathbf{x}_{2}
. Continuity of
\Phi
follows from continuity on
g(\Lambda_{\id})
for each
g\in G
and agreement on the boundaries.

\Phi
is equivariant under the action of
G
by construction. The preimage under
\Phi
of each set
S_{i}
among
S_{1},\ldots,S_{l}
is the union of the simplices
g(\Delta)
for
g\in G
and
\Delta\in\Lambda_{\id}
such that
\Delta\subset\Phi_{\id}^{-1}(S_{i}\cap\overline{H})
. Hence,
(\Lambda,\Phi)
gives our desired triangulation.
∎

4.2.
Triangulation of Definable Functions

In the proofs in
[
9
]
, to ensure that the triangulation we use is properly compatible with the family of sets
\{S_{\delta}\}_{\delta>0}
, the authors invoke the triangulation of definable functions. The original theorem from
[
7
]
is below. We will proceed to prove a version for functions symmetric relative to the action of some finite reflection group
G
.

Theorem 4.8

(Triangulation of Definable Functions,
[
7
]
Theorem 4.5)
.

Let
A
be a closed and bounded definable subset of
R^{n}
and
f:A\rightarrow R
a continuous definable function. Then there exists a finite simplicial complex
\Lambda
in
R^{n+1}
and a definable homeomorphism
\rho:\lvert\Lambda\rvert\rightarrow A
such that
f\circ\rho
is an affine function on each simplex of
\Lambda
. Moreover, given
S_{1},\ldots,S_{l}
definable subsets of
A
, we may choose the triangulation
\rho:\lvert\Lambda\rvert\rightarrow A
to be adapted to the
S_{i}
.

Following
[
7
]
, we will prove a more general result concerning the triangulation of symmetric definable subsets of
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
, which we will apply to what is essentially the graph of our function
f
. Our procedure is similar to the one used for symmetric definable sets. Let
\pi:R\times R^{n}\rightarrow R
denote projection on the first coordinate.

Lemma 4.9

(ref
[
7
]
Proposition 4.6 and Proposition 4.8)
.

Let
A
be a closed, bounded, definable subset of
R\times R^{n}
, and let
S_{1},\ldots,S_{l}
be definable subsets of
A
. Let
\{L_{1},\ldots,L_{k}\}
be a collection of functions with
L_{i}:R\times R^{n}\rightarrow R
given by
L_{i}:(y,x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\mapsto a_{i,0}+a_{i,1}x_{1}+\cdots+a_{i,n}x_{n}
. Then there exists a triangulation
(\Lambda,\Phi)
of
A
adapted to
S_{1},\ldots,S_{l}
, respecting all sign sets of
\{L_{1},\ldots,L_{k}\}
, and having vertices of
\Lambda
in
\mathbb{Q}\times\mathbb{Q}(\{a_{i,j}\mid 1\leq i\leq k,0\leq j\leq n\})^{n}
, as well as a definable homeomorphism
\tau:R\rightarrow R
, such that
\tau\circ\pi\circ\Phi=\pi_{\restriction\lvert\Lambda\rvert}
.

Proof.

We induct on
n
. In the
n=0
case, since we are assuming all functions in our collection
\{L_{1},\ldots,L_{k}\}
to be independent of the first coordinate, we have nothing more to prove than
[
7
]
does, and so may as there choose a finite partition of
R
adapted to
A
and
S_{1},\ldots,S_{l}
. Letting
x_{1}<\cdots<x_{p}
be the points in
R
defining the partition, we take
\Lambda
to be the points
i\in\{1,\ldots,p\}
and intervals
[i,i+1]
such that
{x_{i}}\in A
or
[x_{i},x_{i+1}]\subset A
. We let
\tau(x_{i})=i
and extend piecewise affinely to a map
\tau:R\rightarrow R
.

Assume
n>0
and that the statement holds for
n-1
. We may again assume all
S_{i}
are closed (as described in the proof of
[
7
]
Theorem 4.4). We set
F^{\prime}_{0}
to be the boundary of
A
,
F^{\prime}_{i}
to be the boundary of
S_{i}
for each
i
, and
F^{\prime}=F^{\prime}_{0}\cup F^{\prime}_{1}\cup\ldots\cup F^{\prime}_{l}
. Since
F^{\prime}
is definable with dimension at most
n
, we have finitely many
c\in R
for which the set
\{\mathbf{x}\in R^{n}\mid(c,\mathbf{x})\in F^{\prime}\}
has dimension
n
. Let
C
be the set of all such
c
. For reasons of dimension, the proof in
[
7
]
considers sets
F_{i}
given by taking the union of
\overline{F^{\prime}_{i}\setminus(C\times R^{n})}
with the boundary of
F^{\prime}_{i}\cap(C\times R^{n})
in
C\times R^{n}
. We choose a cell decomposition of
R\times R^{n}
adapted to
F_{0},\ldots,F_{l}
, the sets
\{c\}\times R^{n}
for each
c\in C
, and all sign sets of
\{L_{1},\ldots,L_{k}\}
.

Let
p:R\times R^{n}\rightarrow R\times R^{n-1}
be the projection on the first
n
coordinates. Our cell decomposition partitions
p(A)
into definably connected subsets
X_{\alpha}
. By our inductive hypothesis, we have a triangulation
(\Lambda_{n-1},\Psi_{n-1})
of
R\times R^{n-1}
which is adapted to each
X_{\alpha}
, respects sign sets of
\{L_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,L_{m^{\prime}}^{\prime}\}
(where this collection is defined in a manner analogous to the proof of Lemma
4.5
), and with vertices of
\Lambda_{n-1}
in
\mathbb{Q}\times\mathbb{Q}(\{a_{i,j}^{\prime}\mid 1\leq i\leq m^{\prime},0\leq j\leq n-1\})^{n-1}=\mathbb{Q}\times\mathbb{Q}(\{a_{i,j}\mid 1\leq i\leq m,0\leq j\leq n\})^{n-1}
. We also have a map
\tau^{\prime}:R\rightarrow R
, having the property that
\tau^{\prime}\circ\pi_{n,1}\circ\Psi_{n-1}={\pi_{n,1}}\restriction_{\lvert\Lambda_{n-1}\rvert}
(where
\pi_{n,1}
is the projection
R\times R^{n-1}\rightarrow R
on the first coordinate). Now, if we follow the remaining steps in the proof of Lemma
4.5
, we obtain a triangulation
(\Lambda,\Phi)
of
A
which is adapted to
S_{1},\ldots,S_{l}
, respects sign sets of
\{L_{1},\ldots,L_{k}\}
, and has vertex coordinates in
\mathbb{Q}\times\mathbb{Q}(\{a_{i,j}\})^{n}
.
\Lambda
and
\Phi
are such that
p\circ\Phi=\Psi_{n-1}\circ p_{\restriction\lvert\Lambda\rvert}
. Taking
\tau=\tau^{\prime}
, this means that our property
\tau\circ\pi\circ\Phi=\pi_{\restriction\lvert\Lambda\rvert}
holds.
∎

Lemma 4.10

(ref
[
7
]
Proposition 4.6 and Proposition 4.8)
.

Let
A
be a closed, bounded, definable subset of
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
symmetric under the action of some finite reflection group
G
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
extended to
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and let
S_{1},\ldots,S_{l}
be definable symmetric subsets of
A
. Then there exists an equivariant triangulation
(\Lambda,\Phi)
of
A
adapted to
S_{1},\ldots,S_{l}
and a definable homeomorphism
\tau:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
having the property that
\tau\circ\pi\circ\Phi=\pi_{\restriction\lvert\Lambda\rvert}
.

Assume we have fixed a collection
\{L_{1},\ldots,L_{k}\}
of functions
L_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
given by
L_{i}(\mathbf{x})=a_{i,1}x_{1}+\cdots a_{i,n}x_{n}
, so that

H=\bigcap_{i=1}^{k}\{L_{i}(\mathbf{x})>0\}

is a fundamental region of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with respect to
G
. Then we may choose our triangulation so that vertices of
\Lambda
are in
\mathbb{Q}\times\mathbb{Q}(\{a_{i,j}\mid 1\leq i\leq k,1\leq j\leq n\})^{n}
.

Proof.

This is analogous to the proof of Theorem
4.7
. Let
H
be a fundamental region of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with respect to
G
. If not already specified, we take
\{L_{1},\ldots,L_{k}\}
with
L_{i}(\mathbf{x})=a_{i,1}x_{1}+\cdots+a_{i,n}x_{n}
to be a collection functions defining
H
. For each
i
, let
\tilde{L}_{i}:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
with
\tilde{L}_{i}(y,\mathbf{x})=L_{i}(\mathbf{x})
. We apply Lemma
4.11
to
A\cap(\mathbb{R}\times\overline{H})
, subsets
S_{1}\cap(\mathbb{R}\times\overline{H}),\ldots,S_{l}\cap(\mathbb{R}\times\overline{H})
, and linear functions
\{\tilde{L}_{1},\ldots,\tilde{L}_{k}\}
. We obtain a triangulation
(\Lambda_{\id},\Phi_{\id})
with
\lvert\Lambda_{\id}\rvert\subset\mathbb{R}\times\overline{H}
and coordinates of vertices of
\Lambda_{\id}
in
\mathbb{Q}\times\mathbb{Q}(\{a_{i,j}\})^{n}
, and
\Phi_{\id}:\lvert\Lambda_{\id}\rvert\rightarrow A\cap(\mathbb{R}\times\overline{H})
, and also obtain a map
\tau:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, having the property that
\tau\circ\pi\circ\Phi_{\id}=\pi_{\restriction\lvert\Lambda_{\id}\rvert}
. Again, we take

\Lambda=\bigcup_{g\in G}\{g(\Delta)\mid\Delta\in\Lambda_{\id}\}

and
\Phi:\lvert\Lambda\rvert\rightarrow A
given by
\Phi(\mathbf{x})=g(\Phi_{\id}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}))
, where
g\in G
and
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in\lvert\Lambda_{\id}\rvert
are such that
\mathbf{x}=g(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})
. As in the proof of Theorem
4.7
, this provides a symmetric triangulation of
A
adapted to
S_{1},\ldots,S_{l}
, with all vertices of
\Lambda
in
\mathbb{Q}\times\mathbb{Q}(\{a_{i,j}\})^{n}
.

It remains to show that
\tau\circ\pi\circ\Phi=\pi_{\restriction\lvert\Lambda\rvert}
. Take
\mathbf{x}\in\lvert\Lambda\rvert
. Then we have that
\mathbf{x}=g(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})
for some
g\in G
and
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in\lvert\Lambda_{\id}\rvert
. Note that by the definition of our action of
G
extended to
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we have that
\pi
is symmetric relative to this action. Then

\displaystyle\tau\circ\pi\circ\Phi(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\tau\circ\pi(g(\Phi_{\id}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})))

\displaystyle=\tau\circ\pi(\Phi_{\id}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}))

\displaystyle=\pi(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\pi(g(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}))=\pi(\mathbf{x})

as desired.
∎

Our theorem now follows, applying Lemma
4.10
to
A^{\prime}=\{(f(\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{x})\mid\mathbf{x}\in A\}
.

Theorem 4.11
.

Let
A
be a closed, bounded, definable subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
symmetric under the action of
a finite reflection group
G
, and let
f:A\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a continuous definable function symmetric relative to the action of
G
. Then there exists a finite symmetric simplicial complex
\Lambda
in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
and a definable equivariant homeomorphism
\rho:\lvert\Lambda\rvert\rightarrow A
such that
f\circ\rho
is an affine function on each simplex of
\Lambda
. Moreover, given
S_{1},\ldots,S_{l}
definable symmetric subsets of
A
, we may choose the triangulation
\rho:\lvert\Lambda\rvert\rightarrow A
to be adapted to the sets
S_{i}
.

Assume we have fixed a collection
\{L_{1},\ldots,L_{k}\}
of functions
L_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
given by
L_{i}(\mathbf{x})=a_{i,1}x_{1}+\cdots a_{i,n}x_{n}
, so that

H=\bigcap_{i=1}^{k}\{L_{i}(\mathbf{x})>0\}

is a fundamental region of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with respect to
G
. Then we may choose our simplicial complex
\Lambda
so that all vertices of
\Lambda
are in
\mathbb{Q}\times\mathbb{Q}(\{a_{i,j}\mid 1\leq i\leq k,1\leq j\leq n\})^{n}
.

Proof.

Consider the set
A^{\prime}=\{(f(\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{x})\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid\mathbf{x}\in A\}
. Since
f
is symmetric relative to the action of
G
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
A^{\prime}
is a symmetric set relative to the action induced by
G
on
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and projection on the last
n
coordinates gives an equivariant definable homeomorphism
p:A^{\prime}\rightarrow A
. By Lemma
4.10
, we have a symmetric triangulation
(\Lambda,\Phi)
of
A^{\prime}
which is adapted to the sets
S_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,S_{l}^{\prime}
(with
S_{i}^{\prime}=\{(f(\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{x})\mid\mathbf{x}\in S_{l}\}
) and has vertices in
\mathbb{Q}\times\mathbb{Q}(\{a_{i,j}\})^{n}
, and a definable function
\tau:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that
\tau\circ\pi\circ\Phi=\pi_{\restriction\lvert\Lambda\rvert}
(where
\pi:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is projection on the first coordinate). Applying
f
to
\mathbf{x}\in A
is equivalent to applying
\pi
to
(f(\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{x})\in A^{\prime}
, and so taking
\rho:\lvert\Lambda\rvert\rightarrow A
to be
p\circ\Phi
, we have that
f\circ\rho=\tau^{-1}\circ\pi_{\restriction\lvert\Lambda\rvert}
, which is an affine map on each simplex of
\Lambda
by construction.
∎

4.3.
Equivariance and Hardt Triviality

We will also want an equivariant version of Hardt Triviality for o-minimal sets. Because the proof uses the same sort of argument as equivariant triangulation, we include it within this section.

Definition 4.12

(
[
8
]
Chapter 9 Definitions 1.1 and 1.8)
.

Let
X\subset R^{n}
and
A\subset R^{m}
be definable sets and
f:X\rightarrow A
a continuous definable function. For
A^{\prime}\subset A
, we say that
f
is
definably trivial
over
A^{\prime}
if for any
a\in A^{\prime}
there is a definable homeomorphism
h:f^{-1}(A^{\prime})\rightarrow f^{-1}(a)\times A^{\prime}
such that the diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f^{-1}(A^{\prime})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f^{-1}(a)\times A^{\prime}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A^{\prime}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces h}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi}

commutes (where
\pi
is the projection on the second coordinate).

For
X_{1},\ldots,X_{l}
definable subsets of
X
, we say that the definable trivialization
h

respects

X_{1},\ldots,X_{l}
if
h
maps each
X_{j}\cap f^{-1}(A^{\prime})
homeomorphically to
(X_{j}\cap f^{-1}(a))\times A^{\prime}
.

Theorem 4.13

(
[
8
]
Chapter 9 Theorem 1.7)
.

Let
X\subset R^{n}
and
A\subset R^{m}
be definable sets,
f:X\rightarrow A
a continuous definable function, and
X_{1},\ldots,X_{l}
definable subsets of
X
. Then we may partition
A
into a finite number of definable subsets
A_{i}
such that
f
is definably trivial over each
A_{i}
in a manner that respects each of
X_{1},\ldots,X_{l}
.

We would like to show that, if
f
is a symmetric function, then the homeomorphisms guaranteed by the trivialization are equivariant.

Theorem 4.14
.

Let
G
be a finite reflection group acting on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and let
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
X_{1},\ldots,X_{l}\subset X
be symmetric relative to
G
. Say that
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
is definable and
f:X\rightarrow A
is a continuous, definable function symmetric relative to
G
. Then we may partition
A
into a finite number of definable subsets
A_{i}
such that for each
i
there is an equivariant homeomorphism
h_{i}:f^{-1}(A_{i})\rightarrow f^{-1}(a_{i})\times A_{i}
giving a definable trivialization of
f
over
A_{i}
(where
a_{i}
is any element of
A_{i}
, and the action on
f^{-1}(a_{i})\times A
is given by
g(x,y)=(g(x),y)
). Furthermore, each trivialization
h_{i}
respects the subsets
X_{1},\ldots,X_{l}
.

Proof.

Let
H
be a fundamental region of
\mathbb{R}
with respect to
G
. We apply Theorem
4.13
to
f_{\restriction\overline{H}}:\overline{H}\cap X\rightarrow A
, asking that our definable trivializations respect the sets
\overline{H}\cap X_{1},\ldots,\overline{H}\cap X_{l}
as well as each
H_{\lambda}\cap X
for
H_{\lambda}
an intersection of walls of
H
. We obtain a finite partition
\{A_{i}\}
of
A
, as well as homeomorphisms
h_{\id,i}:f^{-1}_{\restriction\overline{H}}(A_{i})\rightarrow f^{-1}_{\restriction\overline{H}}(a_{i})\times A_{i}
which respect both the portions of each
X_{j}
within
\overline{H}
and the intersections of
X
with the walls of
H
.

Since
f
is a symmetric function, we know that for
a_{i}\in A_{i}
,

f^{-1}(a_{i})=\bigcup_{g\in G}g(f^{-1}_{\restriction\overline{H}}(a_{i}))

and that the same holds true for
f^{-1}(A_{i})
. We define
h_{i}:f^{-1}(A_{i})\rightarrow f^{-1}(a_{i})\times A_{i}
by
h_{i}(\mathbf{x})=g(h_{\id,i}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}))
, where
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in f^{-1}(A_{i})\cap\overline{H}
and
g\in G
are such that
g(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})=\mathbf{x}
. We must show that
h_{i}
is a definable trivialization of
f
.

We may use arguments similar to those of Theorem
4.7
to establish that
h_{i}
is a homeomorphism from

to
f^{-1}(a_{i})\times A_{i}
. The map
h_{i}
is equivariant by construction, and

sends each

homeomorphically to
(X_{j}\cap f^{-1}(a_{i}))\times A_{i}
by construction and the symmetry of each

. Say that
\mathbf{x}\in f^{-1}(A_{i})
with

. Then if
\mathbf{x}=g(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})
for some

and
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in f^{-1}(A_{i})\cap\overline{H}
, by symmetry we know that

as well, and so
h(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})=(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{y})
for some

. Then
h(\mathbf{x})=g(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{y})=(g(\mathbf{z}),\mathbf{y})
, so

is indeed a definable trivialization of

over

.
∎

5.
Equivariant Versions of some Topological Results

We address some topological aspects of the construction in this section. Specifically, for
X
a regular CW complex whose cells are convex polyhedra (so specifically, for
X
a simplicial complex), we explicitly describe certain maps between
X
and the order complex of the face poset of
X
, in aid of demonstrating equivariance. We also establish an equivariant version of a Nerve Theorem due to Björner.

Definition 5.1

(
[
5
]
Appendix A2)
.

Let
P
be a partially ordered set. The
order complex
of
P
,
\Delta(P)
, is the abstract simplicial complex with vertex set the elements of
P
and simplices given by finite chains
x_{0}<\cdots<x_{d}
of elements of
P
.

The order complex of the face poset of a simplicial complex coincides with the first barycentric subdivision of that simplicial complex. For a regular CW complex, then, taking the order complex of the face poset serves as a generalized barycentric subdivision:

Theorem 5.2

(
[
13
]
Theorem 1.7)
.

Let
X
be a regular CW complex. Then
\Delta(\mathscr{F}(X))
is homeomorphic to
X
.

The proof involves choosing a point in the interior of each cell
\sigma
to act as the barycenter of the cell, and sending the vertex of
\Delta(\mathscr{F}(X))
corresponding to
\sigma
to that barycenter. Elsewhere, the homeomorphism arises from the fact that the characteristic maps used to assemble
X
as a regular CW complex endow each cell with the structure of a cone with the barycenter as vertex and boundary as base. Unfortunately, this construction as it stands is not specific enough to ensure equivariance without some sort of equivariance condition on the characteristic maps. However, if we know that each cell of
X
is, for example, a convex polyhedron, we may make our choices in a manner sufficiently canonical to ensure equivariance. Note that by polyhedron in this context we mean a bounded subset of
R^{n}
obtained by intersecting a finite number of affine half planes.

Definition 5.3
.

Let
X\subset R^{n}
be a regular CW complex in which each cell is a convex polyhedron. Then we will refer to the homeomorphism
\Psi
described below as the
centroidal
homeomorphism
\lvert\Delta(\mathscr{F}(X))\rvert\rightarrow X
.

We define
\Psi
inductively. Let
X^{k}
denote the
k
-skeleton of
X
. In the
k=0
case, let
\Psi^{0}:\lvert\Delta(\mathscr{F}(X^{0}))\rvert\rightarrow X^{0}
send the vertex
v_{\sigma}
of
\Delta(\mathscr{F}(X^{0}))
corresponding to the
0
-dimensional cell
\sigma=\{x\}
to the point
x\in X
.

Now say
k\geq 1
. Assume we have defined
\Psi^{k-1}:\lvert\Delta(\mathscr{F}(X^{k-1}))\rvert\rightarrow X^{k-1}
and established that
\Psi^{k-1}
is a homeomorphism. We may identify
\Delta(\mathscr{F}(X^{k-1}))
with the subset of
\Delta(\mathscr{F}(X^{k}))
consisting of simplices whose vertices correspond to cells of
X
of dimension less than
k
; on this subset, let
\Psi^{k}
agree with
\Psi^{k-1}
. Now, assume
\sigma
is a cell of dimension
k
, and let
v_{\sigma}
be the vertex of
\Delta(\mathscr{F}(X^{k}))
corresponding to
\sigma
. Define
\Psi^{k}(v_{\sigma})=b_{\sigma}
, where
b_{\sigma}
is the centroid of the cell
\sigma
(which is in the interior of
\sigma
by convexity). Say
x\in\lvert\Delta(\mathscr{F}(X^{k}))\rvert
. Then we may uniquely write
x=tv_{\sigma}+(1-t)x^{\prime}
, where
\sigma
is a cell of
X
of dimension
k
,
x^{\prime}
is in the realization of some simplex of
\Delta(\mathscr{F}(X^{k-1}))
, and
t\in[0,1]
. Set
\Psi^{k}(x)=tb_{\sigma}+(1-t)\Psi^{k-1}(x^{\prime})
. Since each cell
\sigma
of
X
can be seen as a cone with base
\partial\sigma
and vertex
b_{\sigma}
, this gives a well-defined homeomorphism to
X^{k}
. Since
X=\bigcup_{k}X^{k}
, we inductively obtain our desired homeomorphism
\Psi:\lvert\Delta(\mathscr{F}(X))\rvert\rightarrow X
.

When we refer to the barycentric subdivision of a polyhedral CW complex
X\subset R^{n}
, we will mean the simplicial complex in
R^{n}
whose geometric realization is equal as a set to
X
and whose cell structure is inherited from this map.

Remark 5.4
.

Let
X\subset R^{n}
be a regular CW complex whose cells are all convex polyhedra. If
G
is a group acting linearly on
X
such that
X
is symmetric as a CW complex under the action of
G
, then the action of
G
on
X
induces an action of
G
on
\Delta(\mathscr{F}(X))
under which
\Delta(\mathscr{F}(X))
is symmetric, and the homeomorphism
\Psi:\lvert\Delta(\mathscr{F}(X))\rvert\rightarrow X
of Definition
5.3
is equivariant.

We will also want to consider subsets of simplicial or polyhedral CW complexes which are not full subcomplexes. Let
X
be polyhedral CW complex and let
Y
be a union of cells of
X
. Gabrielov and Vorobjov in
[
9
]
Remark 2.12 describe how we may consider only those cells of the barycentric subdivision of
X
which are contained with their closures in
Y
. This gives us a subcomplex of the barycentric subdivision of
X
which is homotopy equivalent to
Y
, and which may replace
Y
in our applications. Though this procedure is relatively standard, we will describe the contraction explicitly so that we may ensure equivariance.

Definition 5.5
.

Let
X\subset R^{n}
be a regular CW complex whose cells are all convex polyhedra, and let
Y
be a union of cells of
X
. Let
\widehat{X}
be the barycentric subdivision of
X
, and let
\widehat{Y}=\{\Delta\in\widehat{X}\mid\Delta\subset Y\}
. Define the
barycentric retraction
of
Y
to be

\br(Y)=\{\Delta\in\widehat{X}\mid\overline{\Delta}\subset Y\}

Proposition 5.6
.

Say
Y
is a union of cells of some regular CW complex
X\subset R^{n}
whose cells are all convex polyhedra. Then
\br(Y)
is homotopy equivalent to
Y

Proof.

We construct a homotopy
h_{\br}:[0,1]\times Y\rightarrow Y
, which we will term the
barycentric retracting map
.

Observe that
\br(Y)\subset Y
is a subcomplex of
\widehat{X}
, whose vertex set is precisely those
0
-simplices of
\widehat{X}
corresponding to cells of
Y
. In fact, the simplices of
\br(Y)
correspond precisely to flags of cells in
Y
. This means that
Y=\emptyset
iff
\br(Y)=\emptyset
.

Let
\Delta=\Delta(v_{0},\ldots,v_{d})
be a simplex of
\widehat{Y}
, and define the set
J_{\Delta}=\{v_{i_{0}},\ldots,v_{i_{d^{\prime}}}\}=\{v_{i}\in\{v_{0},\ldots,v_{d}\}\mid v_{i}\in\br(Y)\}
. Then if we let
\Delta^{\prime}=\Delta(v_{i_{0}},\ldots,v_{i_{d^{\prime}}})
, it is clear from the correspondence of simplices of
\br(Y)
to flags of cells in
Y
that
J_{\Delta}
is nonempty and that
\overline{\Delta}\cap\lvert\br(Y)\rvert=\overline{\Delta}^{\prime}
. Say that
x\in Y
with
x\in\overline{\Delta}
. Then we may write
x
uniquely as
x=\sum_{i=0}^{d}t_{v_{i}}v_{i}
, where
0\leq t_{v_{i}}\leq 1
for each
i
and
\sum t_{v_{i}}=1
. Then, for
t\in[0,1]
, let

h_{\br}(t,x)=t\sum_{v_{i}\not\in J_{\Delta}}t_{v_{i}}v_{i}+\frac{1-t\sum_{v_{i}\not\in J_{\Delta}}t_{v_{i}}}{\sum_{v_{i}\in J_{\Delta}}t_{v_{i}}}\sum_{v_{i}\in J_{\Delta}}t_{v_{i}}v_{i}

From this, we obtain our desired map
h_{\br}:[0,1]\times Y\rightarrow\lvert\widehat{Y}\rvert
, noting that the definition of
h_{\br}
agrees on the boundaries of simplices. Observe that
h_{\br}(0,-):Y\rightarrow\lvert\br(Y)\rvert
serves as homotopy inverse to the inclusion
\lvert\br(Y)\rvert\hookrightarrow Y
.
∎

Proposition 5.7
.

Let
X\subset R^{n}
be a regular CW complex whose cells are all convex polyhedra, and let
Y
be a union of cells of
X
. If
X
is symmetric as a CW complex under the action of some group
G
acting linearly on
X
and
Y
is also symmetric relative to
G
, then
h_{\br}:[0,1]\times Y\rightarrow Y
is equivariant (where the action of
G
on
[0,1]\times Y
is given by
g(t,x)=(t,g(x))
for all
g\in G
).

Proof.

From the symmetry of
X
and
Y
, we have that
\widehat{X}
,
\widehat{Y}
, and
\br(Y)
are all symmetric. Hence, for
\Delta\in\widehat{Y}
,
g(J_{\Delta})=J_{g(\Delta)}
. Equivariance of
h_{\br}
then follows from the linearity of the action of
G
.
∎

Even when
Y
is not a full subcomplex of
X
, we may make sense of the simplicial complex
\Delta(\mathscr{F}(Y))
. As described in Definition
5.5
, we may identify
\Delta(\mathscr{F}(Y))
with

. Furthermore, if

and

are both symmetric, then the homeomorphism
\lvert\Delta(\mathscr{F}(Y))\rvert\rightarrow\lvert\br(Y)\rvert
is equivariant.

We conclude by addressing equivariance in Börner’s Nerve Theorem, which will be used in Section

.

Definition 5.8

(
[
9
]
Definition 2.9)
.

Let
\{X_{i}\}_{i\in I}
be a family of sets. The
nerve
of
\{X_{i}\}_{i\in I}
is the abstract simplicial complex
\mathcal{N}
having vertex set
I
and simplices given by the finite subsets
\sigma
of
I
such that

\bigcap_{i\in\sigma}X_{i}\neq\emptyset

A variety of theorems exist which relate a space covered by a family of sets to the nerve of that cover. Björner’s Nerve Lemma has the advantage of only requiring the triviality of sufficiently many homotopy groups of intersections of sets in the cover (with a slightly weaker conclusion in exchange).

Theorem 5.9

(Nerve Theorem,
[
4
]
Theorem 6 and Remark 7)
.

Let
X
be a regular connected CW complex and
\{X_{i}\}_{i\in I}
a family of subcomplexes with
X=\bigcup_{i\in I}X_{i}
. Let
\mathcal{N}
be the nerve of
\{X_{i}\}

(i)

Say that every finite nonempty intersection
X_{i_{1}}\cap\ldots\cap X_{i_{t}}
is
(k-t+1)
-connected. Then there is a map
f:X\rightarrow\lvert\mathcal{N}\rvert
such that the induced homomorphism
f_{\#,j}:\pi_{j}(X)\rightarrow\pi_{j}(\lvert\mathcal{N}\rvert)
is an isomorphism for all
j\leq k
and an epimorphism for
j=k+1
.

(ii)

If every finite nonempty intersection
X_{i_{1}}\cap\ldots\cap X_{i_{t}}
is contractible, then
f
gives a homotopy equivalence
X\simeq\lvert\mathcal{N}\rvert
.

A version of Björner’s nerve lemma sufficiently equivariant for our purposes reads thus:

Theorem 5.10
.

Let
X
be a regular connected CW complex which is a subset of a real vector space, and whose cells are all convex polyhedra. Let
G
act linearly on
X
in such a way that
X
is symmetric as a CW complex under the action of
G
. Let
\{X_{i}\}_{i\in I}
be a family of subcomplexes with
X=\bigcup_{i\in I}X_{i}
, and say that for each

and

g(X_{i})\in\{X_{i}\}_{i\in I}
. Then if
\mathcal{N}
is the nerve of
\{X_{i}\}_{i\in I}
, we have the following:

(i)

Say every finite nonempty intersection
X_{i_{1}}\cap\ldots\cap X_{i_{t}}
is
(k-t+1)
-connected. Then there is an equivariant map
f:X\rightarrow\lvert\mathcal{N}\rvert
such that the induced (equivariant) homomorphism
f_{\#,j}:\pi_{j}(X,*)\rightarrow\pi_{j}(\lvert\mathcal{N}\rvert,f(*))
is an isomorphism for all
j\leq k
and an epimorphism for
j=k+1
.

(ii)

If every finite nonempty intersection
X_{i_{1}}\cap\ldots\cap X_{i_{t}}
is contractible, then
f
gives a homotopy equivalence
X\simeq\lvert\mathcal{N}\rvert
.

Proof.

(i): We claim that we may construct an equivariant map in the manner described in the proof in
[
4
]
. By Proposition
5.4
, we have an equivariant homeomorphism
X\rightarrow\lvert\Delta(\mathscr{F}(X))\rvert
. Let
\varphi:\mathscr{F}(X)\rightarrow\mathscr{F}(\mathcal{N})
be given by
\varphi(\sigma)=\{i\in I\mid\sigma\in X_{i}\}
for each cell
\sigma
of
X
. This is an order-reversing map of posets. It is also equivariant, since for a given cell
\sigma
,
\{g(i)\mid i\in I\text{ with }\sigma\in X_{i}\}=\{i\in I\mid g(\sigma)\in X_{i}\}
. Then
\varphi
induces an equivariant continuous function
\lvert\Delta(\mathscr{F}(X))\rvert\rightarrow\lvert\Delta(\mathscr{F}(\mathcal{N}))\rvert
. Since Remark
5.4
gives an equivariant homeomorphism
\lvert\Delta(\mathscr{F}(\mathcal{N}))\rvert\rightarrow\lvert\mathcal{N}\rvert
, composing gives us an equivariant continuous function

f:X\rightarrow\lvert\Delta(\mathscr{F}(X))\rvert\rightarrow\lvert\Delta(\mathscr{F}(\mathcal{N}))\rvert\rightarrow\lvert\mathcal{N}\rvert

The equivariance of
f
gives the equivariance of each induced
f_{\#,j}:\pi_{j}(X)\rightarrow\pi_{j}(\lvert\mathcal{N}\rvert)
.

Since our equivariant map
f
is the map from the original proof of the nerve theorem, part (ii) follows automatically.
∎

Note that though, if following the proof for (ii), one would obtain a map
g:\lvert\mathcal{N}\rvert\rightarrow X
which serves as a homotopy inverse to

, we have not guaranteed the equivariance of this or any map in the opposite direction. Thus there is more work to be done before we may term this a true equivariant version of Björner’s nerve theorem.

The barycentric retracting map described in Definition

allows us to apply this version of the nerve theorem to coverings by sets open in our larger space. This version of the nerve theorem is used in

.

.

From

6.2.
Equivariance of the map

The existence of homomorphisms

Lemma 6.11
.

Fix a simplex

of some simplicial complex
\Lambda
, let

be a subset of the vertex set of

, and let

Proof.

Each cell of our collection is a convex polyhedron more or less by definition, and hence a regular cell in a fairly immediate manner. We have that for each simplex

,
\mathcal{C}_{K^{\prime},I_{K^{\prime}},\varepsilon}

It remains to show that for a cell

is contained in a union of cells of lower dimension. Say

(i.e. where the vertex set of

is not disjoint from I). The case of

\{\br(\widetilde{B})\}_{B\in\widehat{S}}
. Then there exists an equivariant map
\psi_{\widehat{S}}:\lvert\mathcal{N}_{\br(\widehat{S})}\rvert\rightarrow S
which induces a homotopy equivalence.

Proof.

Observe that
\br(\widehat{S})
is a full simplicial complex, each
\br(\widetilde{B})
is a subcomplex of
\br(\widehat{S})
, and that the family
\{\br(\widetilde{B})\}_{B\in\widehat{S}}
is invariant under the action of
G
. Furthermore, any nonempty intersection
\br(\widetilde{B}_{1})\cap\ldots\cap\br(\widetilde{B}_{l})
is equal to
\br(\widetilde{B}_{0})
for some
B_{0}\in\widehat{S}
.

Let
v_{B_{0}}
be the vertex of the second barycentric subdivision of
\Lambda
corresponding to the simplex
B_{0}\in\widehat{S}
. For
\Delta
a simplex of the second barycentric subdivision of
\Lambda
having
v_{B_{0}}
as one of its vertices, we may define a map
h_{\restriction\overline{\Delta}}:[0,1]\times\overline{\Delta}\rightarrow\overline{\Delta}
by

h_{\restriction\overline{\Delta}}(t,x)=t\sum_{v_{i}\neq v_{B_{0}}}t_{v_{i}}v_{i}+\left(1-t\sum_{v_{i}\neq v_{B_{0}}}t_{v_{i}}\right)v_{B_{0}}

where

. Since we have agreement on the boundaries, and since
\br(\widetilde{B}_{0})
is the union of all such
\overline{\Delta}
which are contained entirely in

, we may extend to obtain a map
h:[0,1]\times\lvert\br(\widetilde{B}_{0})\rvert\rightarrow\lvert\br(\widetilde{B}_{0})\rvert
. The map

has the property that

and

, and by linearity together with the fact that for such

, we see that

Thus by Theorem
5.13
, we obtain an equivariant map

which induces a homotopy equivalence. However, as described in Definition

\lvert\br(\widehat{S})\rvert\hookrightarrow\lvert\widehat{S}\rvert=\Phi^{-1}(S)
induces a homotopy equivalence (and is equivariant). Composing gives us the desired equivariant map

.
∎

7.
Application to Cohomology of Symmetric Semialgebraic sets

Basu and Riener in
[
3
]
investigate the cohomology of semialgebraic sets defined by symmetric polynomials of bounded degree. They develop an algorithm for computing the first
l
Betti numbers of such a set
S
, with complexity polynomially bounded in dimension,
n
, and number of symmetric polynomials,
s
. To accomplish this, Basu and Riener develop results on the structure of the cohomology groups of
\mathcal{P}
-closed semialgebraic sets, which allow for a significant reduction in the number of computations which must be performed. To extend these results to an arbitrary semialgebraic set, they apply the Gabrielov-Vorobjov construction. From the isomorphisms of homology groups, it follows that
S
and its approximating set have the same Betti numbers (at least up to a chosen degree). However, the structural results rely on decomposing the cohomology spaces as
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
-modules. The Gabrielov-Vorobjov construction alone does not guarantee that the cohomology spaces of
S
and its compact approximation have the same
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
-module structure, but our equivariant version does. Utilizing our new equivariance results, we strengthen a few results of Basu and Riener in
[
3
]
.

Let
\mathbb{R}[X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}]^{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}_{\leq d}
denote the space of symmetric polynomials over
\mathbb{R}
of degree at most
d
(see
[
3
]
Notation 4). By
H^{k}(S)
we mean the
k
th cohomology space (with rational coefficients) of
S
. If
S
is a symmetric semialgebraic set, then the action of
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
on
S
induces an action of
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
on
H^{*}(S)
, giving
H^{*}(S)
the structure of a finite dimensional
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
-module. As such, each
H^{k}(S)
admits a decomposition into a direct sum of irreducible
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
-modules

H^{k}(S)\cong_{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}\bigoplus_{\lambda\vdash n}m_{k,\lambda}(S)\;\mathbb{S}^{\lambda}

where the sum is taken over all partitions
\lambda
of the integer
n
and
\mathbb{S}^{\lambda}
is the particular irreducible
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
-module (the Specht module) corresponding to
\lambda
. The integer
m_{k,\lambda}(S)\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
is called the mulitplicity of
\mathbb{S}^{\lambda}
in
H_{k}(S)
. The dimension of each
\mathbb{S}^{\lambda}
may be computed (via what is known as the hook length formula), and so by this isotypic decomposition, we have reduced the task of computing the
k
th Betti number
b_{k}(S)=\dim(H^{k}(S))
to that of computing the various mulitpicities
m_{k,\lambda}(S)
. Appendix 6 of
[
3
]
summarizes the classical results from the represention theory of finite groups pertaining to the above decomposition. For
S
a
\mathcal{P}
-closed set, Basu and Riener in
[
3
]
Theorem 4 prove that the mulitpicities corresponding to partitions whose lengths are too long or too short must be zero. This dramatically reduces the number of multiplicities one needs to compute.

Remark 7.1
.

Though
[
3
]
defines the Betti number
b_{k}(S)
in terms of cohomology spaces rather than homology groups, in this setting it holds that
H_{k}(S)\cong_{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}H^{k}(S)
, and so the distinction may be set aside (see
[
2
]
Remark 1).

Let
S
be a
\mathcal{P}
-semialgebraic set for some finite
\mathcal{P}\subset\mathbb{R}[X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}]^{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}_{\leq d}
, where
d\geq 2
. For algorithmic reasons, Basu and Riener reset the Gabrielov-Vorobjov results in terms of Puiseux series. Let
\mathbb{R}\left<\varepsilon\right>
be the real closed field of algebraic Puiseux series in
\varepsilon
with coefficients in
\mathbb{R}
, and let

\mathbb{R}\left<\varepsilon_{m},\varepsilon_{m-1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{0}\right>=\mathbb{R}\left<\varepsilon_{m}\right>\left<\varepsilon_{m-1}\right>\cdots\left<\varepsilon_{0}\right>

(see
[
3
]
Notation 15). Then in the unique ordering on
\mathbb{R}\left<\varepsilon_{m},\ldots,\varepsilon_{0}\right>
, we have
0<\varepsilon_{0}\ll\varepsilon_{1}\ll\ldots\ll\varepsilon_{m}
(where here
\ll
indeed denotes ‘infinitesimally smaller than’). In this phrasing, the equivariant Gabrielov-Vorobjov construction (Theorem
1.3
part (ii)) gives us a
\mathcal{P^{\prime}}
-closed and bounded semialgebraic set

S^{\prime}_{m}\subset\mathbb{R}\left<\delta_{m},\varepsilon_{m},\delta_{m-1},\varepsilon_{m-1},\ldots,\delta_{0},\varepsilon_{0}\right>^{n}

and equivariant homomorphisms
\psi_{\#,k}:\pi_{k}(S^{\prime}_{m},*)\rightarrow\pi_{k}(S,*^{\prime})
and
\psi_{*,k}:H_{k}(S^{\prime}_{m})\rightarrow H_{k}(S)
which are isomorphisms for
0\leq k\leq m-1
and epimorphisms for
k=m
. By construction (see Section
3
) we know that

\mathcal{P}^{\prime}\subset\mathbb{R}\left<\delta_{m},\varepsilon_{m},\ldots,\delta_{0},\varepsilon_{0}\right>[X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}]^{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}_{\leq d}

and if
\mathcal{P}
has cardinality
s
,
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
has cardinality
4m(s+1)
. See
[
3
]
Section 5.2 for more details on this rephrasing.

Because we have an isomorphism
H^{k}(S_{m}^{\prime})\rightarrow H^{k}(S)
for any
0\leq k\leq m-1
which is equivariant relative to
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
, we know that these spaces have the same
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
-module structure and hence the same isotypic decomposition:

\bigoplus_{\lambda\vdash n}m_{k,\lambda}(S)\mathbb{S}^{\lambda}\cong_{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}H^{k}(S)\cong_{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}H^{k}(S_{m}^{\prime})\cong_{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}\bigoplus_{\lambda\vdash n}m_{k,\lambda}(S_{m}^{\prime})\mathbb{S}^{\lambda}

In particular, for each
0\leq k\leq m-1
and
\lambda\vdash n
,
m_{k,\lambda}(S)=m_{k,\lambda}(S_{m}^{\prime})
. This allows us to make the following strengthenings of two of Basu and Riener’s theorems in
[
3
]
.

Let
\Parr_{k}({S})=\{\lambda\vdash n\mid m_{k,\lambda}(S)\neq 0\}
(see
[
3
]
Notation 5).
