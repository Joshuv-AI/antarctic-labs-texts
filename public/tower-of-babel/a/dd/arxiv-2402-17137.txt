Abstract
. A set of points S S in Euclidean space ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} is called Ramsey if any finite partition of ℝ ∞ \mathbb{R}^{\infty} yields a monochromatic copy of S S . While characterization of Ramsey set remains a major open problem in the area, a stronger “density” concept was considered in [ 4 ] : If S S is a d d -dimensional simplex, then for any μ > 0 \mu>0 there is an integer d := d ⁡ ( S , μ ) d:=d(S,\mu) and finite configuration X ⊆ ℝ d X\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d} such that any subconfiguration Y ⊆ X Y\subseteq X with | Y | ⩾ μ ​ | X | |Y|\geqslant\mu|X| contains a copy of S S . Complementing this, here we show the existence of μ := μ ⁡ ( S ) \mu:=\mu(S) and of an infinite configuration X ⊆ ℝ ∞ X\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{\infty} with the property that any finite coloring of X X yields a monochromatic copy of S S , yet for any finite set of points Y ⊆ X Y\subseteq X contains a subset Z ⊆ Y Z\subseteq Y of size | Z | ⩾ μ ​ | Y | |Z|\geqslant\mu|Y| without a copy of S S .

1.
Introduction

We will find convenient to present our discussion in the framework of
\mathbb{R}^{\infty}
, by which we understand a subspace of
\ell^{2}
consisting of infinite sequences of real numbers with all but finitely many nonzero entries and with
\mathbb{R}^{\infty}
equipped by the usual euclidean metric. In other words, we can view
\mathbb{R}^{\infty}
as the infinite union
\mathbb{R}^{\infty}=\bigcup_{d=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where we understand that the copies of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
are being included in one another.

For two configuration of points
A,B\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{\infty}
we will write

\displaystyle A\rightarrow(B)_{r}

to denote the fact that any
r
-coloring of
A
yields a monochromatic copy of
B
. By a (congruent) copy of
B
, we mean a subconfiguration
B^{\prime}\subseteq A
that is isometric to
B
, i.e., that exists a bijective map
\varphi:B\rightarrow B^{\prime}
such that

\displaystyle||b_{1}-b_{2}||=||\varphi(b_{1})-\varphi(b_{2})||

for every
b_{1},b_{2}\in B
. Given two configurations
A,B
we say that
B
is contained in
A
, and denote
B\subseteq A
, if there exists a copy
A^{\prime}
of
A
such that
B\subseteq A^{\prime}
(in the set theoretical sense).

A finite configuration
S
is said to be
Ramsey
if
\mathbb{R}^{\infty}\rightarrow(S)_{r}
for every integer
r\geqslant 1
. The concept was introduced in
[
2
]
by Erdős, Graham, Montgomery, Rothschild, Spencer and Strauss, who proved that the vertex set of every brick (rectangular parallelepiped) of arbitrary finite dimension is Ramsey. The list of Ramsey configurations was extended by a few more configurations in
[
4
,
6
,
10
,
11
]
. On the other hand, the authors of
[
2
]
also proved that any Ramsey set is spherical, i.e., all points of
S
lie on some finite dimensional sphere. They asked if the opposte implication is also true: If any spherical set is Ramsey. In
[
9
]
Ron Graham offered
\$1000
dollars for deciding if this implication holds as well. Based on the evidence coming from known Ramsey configurations Leader, Russel and Walters
[
12
]
proposed an alternative conjecture. Calling a finite set transitive if its symmetry group is transitive, i.e., if all points play the same role, their conjecture states that Ramsey sets are precisely the transitive sets together with their subsets.

While the progress on these conjectures was very small, some alternative concepts were considered in
[
7
,
8
,
13
,
4
,
5
,
1
]
. In this paper we will introduce another concept related to the Euclidean Ramsey problems. A
d
-dimensional simplex
S
is a configuration consisting of
d+1
affinely independent points in
\mathbb{R}^{\infty}
. In
[
4
]
it was proved that all simplices are Ramsey. One interesting feature of their proof is that they actually show the following stronger statement.

Theorem 1.1

(
[
4
]
)
.

Let
S\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{\infty}
be a
d
-dimensional simplex and
0<\mu<1
real number. Then there exists finite configuration
Y\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{\infty}
such that any subconfiguration
Z\subseteq Y
of size
|Z|\geqslant\mu|Y|
contains a copy of
S
.

In other words, Theorem
1.1
not only finds a configuration
Y
such that
Y\rightarrow(S)_{r}
, but also with the extra property that any subset of positive density contains a copy of
S
. One of the goals of this paper is to show an alternative construction of the fact that simplices are Ramsey where our set
Y
does not have the density property. The following definition is central for our exposition.

Definition 1.2
.

A finite configuration
X\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{\infty}
is called
P-Ramsey
if there exists a configuration
Y\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{\infty}
and a real number
\mu>0
such that the following holds:

(i)

Y\rightarrow(X)_{r}
holds for every integer
r\geqslant 1
.

(ii)

Every finite subconfiguration
Y^{\prime}\subseteq Y
contains a configuration
Z\subseteq Y^{\prime}
with
|Z|\geqslant\mu|Y^{\prime}|
such that
Z
is
X
-free

The definition is motivated by a problem formulated by Pisier in
[
15
]
(see also
[
3
,
17
,
14
]
). Note that statement
(ii)
of the P-Ramsey definition is in contrast with the density statement introduced in Theorem
1.1
, since it says that every finite subconfiguration contains a large set without a copy of
X
.

Clearly, if
X
is P-Ramsey, then
X
is Ramsey. However, the converse is not so clear. In this paper, we start the study of P-Ramsey configurations by showing the following two results.

Theorem 1.3
.

All simplices are P-Ramsey.

We say that a configuration
B\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{\infty}
is a
d
-dimensional brick if there exists positive real numbers
a_{1},\ldots,a_{d}\in\mathbb{R}
such that
B
is congruent to the set

\displaystyle\left\{(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}):\>x_{i}=0\text{ or }x_{i}=a_{i},\,1\leqslant i\leqslant d\right\}.

Theorem 1.4
.

All bricks are P-Ramsey.

The paper is organized as follows: In Section
2
we prove that segments are P-Ramsey. We introduce our main technical result in Section
3
, a variation of the product theorem for P-Ramsey configurations, and prove Theorem
1.4
. Section
4
is devoted to the proof of Theorem
1.3
.

2.
Segments are P-Ramsey

We prove in this section that segments are P-Ramsey. In fact, we will prove a sligthly stronger statement. Recall that a weight vector
\mathbf{w}:X\rightarrow[0,1]
is
stochastical
if
\sum_{x\in X}\mathbf{w}(x)=1
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
A
be a segment of distance
a
and
\gamma>0
be a real number. Then there exists a countable configuration
Y_{A}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{\infty}
satisfying the following:

(i)

The set of squares of all distances of points in
Y_{A}
is

\displaystyle\left\{a^{2},\frac{a^{2}}{1+\gamma+\gamma^{2}},\frac{(1+\gamma^{2})a^{2}}{1+\gamma+\gamma^{2}},\frac{\gamma^{2}a^{2}}{1+\gamma+\gamma^{2}}\right\}

(ii)

Y_{A}\rightarrow(C)_{r}
holds for every
r\geqslant 1
and finite configuration
C\subseteq Y_{A}
.

(iii)

For every finite subconfiguration
Y^{\prime}\subseteq Y_{A}
and stochastic weight vector
\mathbf{w}:Y^{\prime}\rightarrow[0,1]
, there exists a configuration
Z\subseteq Y^{\prime}
with no segments of lenght
a
such that
\sum_{z\in Z}\mathbf{w}(z)\geqslant\frac{1}{4}
.

(iv)

Y_{A}
does not contain an equilateral triangle of sides of lenght
a
.

The proof of Lemma
2.1
uses the following result about independent sets of a shift graph. The shift graph
\Sh(2,\mathbb{N})
is the graph with vertex set
V(\Sh(2,\mathbb{N}))=\mathbb{N}^{(2)}
, i.e., the pairs of natural numbers, and edge set

\displaystyle E(\Sh(2,\mathbb{N}))=\left\{\left\{\{x,y\},\{y,z\}\right\}:\>x<y<z\right\}.

Claim 2.2
.

Let
\Sh(2,\mathbb{N})
be the shift graph on the pairs of
\mathbb{N}
. Then for every finite subset
X\subseteq V(\Sh(2,\mathbb{N}))
and stochastical weight vector
\mathbf{w}:X\rightarrow[0,1]
, there exists an independent set
I\subseteq X
such that

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in I}\mathbf{w}(i)\geqslant\frac{1}{4}.

Proof.

Let
X\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{(2)}
be a finite subset of vertices of
\Sh(2,\mathbb{N})
and let
\mathbf{w}:X\rightarrow[0,1]
be a stochastic weight vector. Consider a random coloring
c:\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
, where each integer
n
is colored independently with probability

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(c(n)=0)=\frac{1}{2}.

Let
X_{0,1}
be the random set defined by

\displaystyle X_{0,1}=\left\{\{x,y\}\in X:\>x<y\text{ and }c(x)=0,\,c(y)=1\right\}.

That is,
X_{0,1}
are the ordered pairs of
X
such that the first integer is of color
0
and the last one of color
1
. One can see that
X_{0,1}
is an independent set in
\Sh(2,\mathbb{N})
. Moreover, by letting

\displaystyle Z_{0,1}=\sum_{x\in X_{0,1}}\mathbf{w}(x).

we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(Z_{0,1})=\sum_{\{x,y\}\in X}\mathbb{P}\big(\{c(x)=0\}\wedge\{c(y)=1\}\big)\mathbf{w}(\{x,y\})=\sum_{\{x,y\}\in X}\frac{1}{4}\mathbf{w}(\{x,y\})=\frac{1}{4}.

Thus, by the first moment, with positive probability there is a coloring
c
such that
X_{0,1}
is an independent set satisfying the statement of the claim.
∎

Proof of Lemma
2.1
.

Let
\{e_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
be the standard basis of
\mathbb{R}^{\infty}
. We construct a configuration
Y_{A}=\{y_{e}\}_{e\in\mathbb{N}^{(2)}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{\infty}
by associating to each pair
e=\{i,j\}\in\mathbb{N}^{(2)}
,
i<j
, the point

\displaystyle y_{e}=\beta e_{i}-\beta\gamma e_{j},

where
\beta=\frac{a}{\sqrt{2(1+\gamma+\gamma^{2})}}
. We claim that the configuration
Y_{A}
satisfies properties
(i)
,
(ii)
,
(iii)
and
(iv)
of Lemma
2.1
.

Property
(i)
comes from the fact that given two pairs
e=\{i,j\}
,
e^{\prime}=\{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}\}\in\mathbb{N}^{(2)}
the square of the distance between
y_{e}
and
y_{e^{\prime}}
can assume the following values

\displaystyle||y_{e}-y_{e^{\prime}}||^{2}=\begin{cases}2\beta^{2}\gamma^{2},\quad&\text{if }i=i^{\prime}\\
2\beta^{2},\quad&\text{if }j=j^{\prime}\\
2\beta^{2}(1+\gamma+\gamma^{2}),\quad&\text{if }i=j^{\prime}\text{ or }i^{\prime}=j\\
2\beta^{2}(1+\gamma^{2}),\quad&\text{if }\{i,j\}\cap\{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}\}=\emptyset\end{cases}

By plugging
\beta=\frac{a}{\sqrt{2(1+\gamma+\gamma^{2})}}
we obtain the set of distances of the statement. Moreover, another important consequence of the computation is that
||y_{e}-y_{e^{\prime}}||=a
if and only if
e\sim e^{\prime}
in
\Sh(2,\mathbb{N})
.

In order to prove
(ii)
, consider a finite configuration
C\subseteq Y_{A}
. Naturally
C
can be written as
C=\{y_{e}\}_{e\in E}
for some
E\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{(2)}
. Since
E
is finite, there exists an integer
n
such that
E\subseteq[n]^{(2)}
. An
r
-coloring of
Y_{A}
corresponds to an
r
-coloring of
\mathbb{N}^{(2)}
. By Ramsey theorem
[
16
]
, there exists a set
W\subseteq\mathbb{N}
of size
n
such that
W^{(2)}
is monochromatic. Hence, this configuration
C^{\prime}=\{y_{e}\}_{e\in W^{(2)}}
is monochromatic. This implies property
(i)
, since
C^{\prime}
contains a copy of
C
.

To check property
(iii)
, let
Y^{\prime}\subseteq Y_{A}
be a finite subconfiguration of
Y_{A}
. By our construction, this corresponds to a finite set
X\subseteq V(\Sh(2,\mathbb{N}))
. Let
\mathbf{w}^{\prime}:X\rightarrow[0,1]
be the stochastic weight vector given by
\mathbf{w}^{\prime}(x)=\mathbf{w}(y)
, where
y\in Y^{\prime}
is the corresponding point to
x\in X
. Claim
2.2
applied to the vector
\mathbf{w}^{\prime}
gives us an independent set
I\subseteq X
in
\Sh(2,\mathbb{N})
such that
\sum_{i\in I}\mathbf{w}^{\prime}(i)\geqslant\frac{1}{4}
. This corresponds to a subconfiguration
Z\subseteq Y^{\prime}
with no segments of length
a
and such that

\displaystyle\sum_{z\in Z}\mathbf{w}(z)\geqslant\frac{1}{4}.

Finally, property
(iv)
follows from the fact that an equilateral triangle of sides of length
a
corresponds to a triangle in
\Sh(2,\mathbb{N})
and
\Sh(2,\mathbb{N})
is triangle free.
∎

3.
Robust configurations

One of the main techniques developed in
[
2
]
to prove that a configuration is Ramsey is the product theorem

Theorem 3.1

(
[
2
]
, Theorem 20)
.

Let
A
and
B
be finite configurations which are Ramsey and
X,Y\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{\infty}
be such that
X\rightarrow(A)_{r}
and
Y\rightarrow(B)_{r}
for every
r\geqslant 1
. Then
X\times Y\rightarrow(C)_{r}
for
C\subseteq A\times B
for every
r\geqslant 1
.

Unfortunately, it is not clear if a similar statement holds for P-Ramsey configurations. The goal of this sections is to develop a partial version of the product theorem that will enable us to prove Theorems
1.3
and
1.4
.

Definition 3.2
.

We say that a countable configuration
Y
is
robust
if for every finite configuration
C
with
C\subseteq Y
we have that
Y\rightarrow(C)_{r}
for every
r\geqslant 1
.

Note for instance, that by property
(i)
of Lemma
2.1
we have the following.

Corollary 3.3
.

Let
Y_{A}
be the configuration obtained by Lemma
2.1
. Then
Y_{A}
is a robust configuration.

The following is our main result in the section. Recall that by
B\subseteq A
we understand that there exists
A^{\prime}
copy of
A
such that
B\subseteq A
.

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
B
be a brick and
Y
be a robust configuration. If
F\subseteq B\times Y
and
F\not\subseteq Y
, then
F
is P-Ramsey.

Theorem
3.4
is a consequence of the following lemma.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
Y
be a robust configuration,
A
be a segment and
F
a finite configuration with
|F|>1
. Then the following holds:

(a)

If
F\subseteq A\times Y
and
F\not\subseteq Y
, then
F
is P-Ramsey.

(b)

If
F\not\subseteq A\times Y
, then there exists a robust configuration
\tilde{Y}
such that
A\times Y\subseteq\tilde{Y}
and
F\not\subseteq\tilde{Y}
.

Proof.

Let
a
be the length of the segment
A
, also let
D_{Y}
be the set of all distances in
Y
and let
D_{F}
be the set of all distances in
F
. Consider the field extension
L=\mathbb{Q}(a,D_{Y},D_{F})
of
\mathbb{Q}
, where
\mathbb{Q}(a,D_{Y},D_{F})
is the minimal field containing
a
,
D_{Y}
,
D_{F}
and
\mathbb{Q}
. Since
D_{Y}\cup D_{F}\cup\{a\}
is countable, we have that
L
is a countable extension of
\mathbb{Q}
and consequently
L\neq\mathbb{R}
. Let
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
be a transcedental number over
L
, i.e.,

there is no polynomial
p\in L[x]
such that
p(\gamma)=0

(1)

Let
Y_{A}
be the configuration obtained by Lemma
2.1
with parameters
a
and
\gamma
. By property
(iii)
the set of all square distances is given by

\displaystyle\left\{a^{2},\,\frac{a^{2}}{1+\gamma+\gamma^{2}},\,\frac{(1+\gamma^{2})a^{2}}{1+\gamma+\gamma^{2}},\,\frac{\gamma^{2}a^{2}}{1+\gamma+\gamma^{2}}\right\}

Note that while
a^{2}\in L
, due to the fact that
\gamma
is transcedental, the other three distances are not in
L
. Indeed, to illustrate, assume for example that
\frac{a^{2}}{1+\gamma+\gamma^{2}}\in L
. Then there exists
b\in L
such that

\displaystyle\frac{a^{2}}{1+\gamma+\gamma^{2}}=b.

This implies that
\gamma
is a root of the polynomial
p\in L[x]
given by
p(x)=bx^{2}+bx+b-a^{2}
, which contradicts the assumption that
\gamma
is transcedental over
L
.

Before we address statements
(a)
and
(b)
of Lemma
3.5
, we will prove the following claim. Let
\pi_{A}:Y_{A}\times Y\rightarrow Y_{A}
and
\pi_{Y}:Y_{A}\times Y\rightarrow Y
be the projection maps of
Y_{A}\times Y
on
Y_{A}
and
Y
, respectively.

Claim 3.6
.

Let
F\subseteq Y_{A}\times Y
. Then either
F\subseteq Y
or
\pi_{A}(F)
is a copy of

.

Proof.

If
\pi_{A}(F)
is a single point, then

and there is nothing to do. Thus, we may assume that
|\pi_{A}(F)|\geqslant 2
. Let
p,q
be two points of

such that

are distinct. We claim that
||p^{\prime}-q^{\prime}||=a
. Let
p^{\prime\prime}=\pi_{Y}(p)
and

. Since all distances from points of

and

are in

, we have that

. Thus, by Pythagoras theorem we have

\displaystyle||p^{\prime}-q^{\prime}||^{2}=||p-q||^{2}-||p^{\prime\prime}-q^{\prime\prime}||^{2}\in L.

(2)

On the other hand, by Lemma
2.1
we have that

\displaystyle||p^{\prime}-q^{\prime}||^{2}\in\left\{a^{2},\,\frac{a^{2}}{1+\gamma+\gamma^{2}},\,\frac{(1+\gamma^{2})a^{2}}{1+\gamma+\gamma^{2}},\,\frac{\gamma^{2}a^{2}}{1+\gamma+\gamma^{2}}\right\}.

Due to our choice of
\gamma
, the value

is the only one of the four values in the field

. Hence, due to (

||p^{\prime}-q^{\prime}||=a
.

Suppose that
|\pi_{A}(F)|\geqslant 3
. Then by the previous paragraph, there is an equilateral triangle of sides of length

in

, which contradicts property

of Lemma

. Therefore,

is a segment of length

.
∎

Now we prove statement
(a)
of Lemma
3.5
. Let
F
be a finite configuration,

, such that

and
F\not\subseteq Y
for a segment

and a robust configuration

. By Corollary

, the configuration

satisfying (

. We claim that

.

In order to prove property

Consider the configuration

Proof of Theorem
3.4
.

Let
B

of robust configurations with the property that

, for

. Suppose that we already constructed

. If

, then we stop the process and set

. Otherwise, by statement

of Lemma

. This implies that

and
F\not\subseteq Y_{\ell}
and by statement
(a)
of Lemma
3.5
, we have that
F
is P-Ramsey.
∎

A corollary of Theorem
3.4
is that bricks are P-Ramsey. In fact, we prove the slighter stronger statement that in particular implies Theorem
1.4
.

Corollary 3.7
.

Let
B
be a brick and
F\subseteq B
be a subconfiguration with
|F|>1
. Then
F
is P-Ramsey.

Proof.

Suppose that
B
is
d
-dimensional brick and write
B=A_{1}\times\ldots\times A_{d}
, where
A_{i}
is a segment of length
a_{i}
and
a_{1}\geqslant\ldots\geqslant a_{d}
. Let
\gamma>0
be an arbitrary real number and let
Y_{A_{d}}
be the configuration obtained by Lemma
2.1
with parameters
\gamma
and
a_{d}
. Suppose that
F\subseteq Y_{A_{d}}
. By the minimality of the segment
A_{d}
, we have that the minimum distance between two points in
F
is at least
a_{d}
. Moreover, by property
(iii)
of Lemma
2.1
, the diameter of
Y_{A_{d}}
is exactly
a_{d}
. Hence, any two points of
F
has distance
a_{d}
. If
|F|\geqslant 3
, then
Y_{A_{d}}
contains an equilateral triangle of sides
a_{d}
. This contradicts property
(iv)
of Lemma
2.1
. Thus,
F
is a copy of the segment
A_{d}
and in this case
F
is P-Ramsey by property
(ii)
and
(iii)
of Lemma
2.1
.

Now suppose that
F\not\subseteq Y_{A_{d}}
. Since
A_{d}\subseteq Y_{A_{d}}
, then
F\subseteq A_{1}\times\ldots\times A_{d}\subseteq A_{1}\times\ldots\times A_{d-1}\times Y_{A_{d}}
. Therefore,
F
satisfies the hypothesis of Theorem
3.4
and we obtain that
F
is P-Ramsey.
∎

4.
Simplices are P-Ramsey

In this section we prove Theorem
1.3
. The proof follows the ideas from
[
4
,
13
]
. First, we will introduce the terminology and auxiliary results from those papers. The main idea will be to prove that any simplex
S
can be embedded in a product
B\times Y
, where
B
is a brick and
Y
is a robust configuration.

To address the robust configuration consider the following definition: Let
\{e_{i}\}_{i\geqslant 1}
be the standard basis of
\mathbb{R}^{\infty}
. Given an integer
k
, a vector
c=(c_{1},\ldots,c_{k})\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
and a
k
-tuple
J=(j_{1},\ldots,j_{k})\in\mathbb{N}^{(k)}
, we define the point
\spread(c,J)\in\mathbb{R}^{\infty}
as

\displaystyle\spread(c,J)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}c_{\ell}e_{j_{\ell}}

Given a subset of integers
A\subseteq\mathbb{N}
, one can then define the configuration
\Spread(c,A)
as

\displaystyle\Spread(c,A)=\{\spread(c,J):\>J\in A^{(k)}\}.

The reason why spread configurations are interesting for us is twofold: One is that those configurations approximate simplices very well. The second is that it fits well in the context of P-Ramseyeness (see Claim
4.3
below) The next result was proven in
[
13
]
. Given real number
\rho>0
, we denote by
S_{\rho}(\mathbb{R}^{\infty})
the sphere of radius
\rho
in
\mathbb{R}^{\infty}
. For a linear subspace
Z\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{\infty}
, let
S_{\rho}(Z)=S_{\rho}(\mathbb{R}^{\infty})\cap Z
.

Proposition 4.1

(
[
13
]
)
.

For every
\delta>0
and every integer
m
, there exist integer
n
,
k
, a
k
-dimensional vector
c=(c_{1},\ldots,c_{k})\in\mathbb{R}^{\infty}
with
||c||=\rho
and an
m
-dimensional subspace
Z\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{\infty}
such that the following holds. For every
z\in Z
, there is a point
y\in\Spread(c,[n])
such that
||z-y||<\delta
.

Since any
d
-dimensional simplex can be embedded in any
d
-dimensional vector space, we obtain the following corollary from Proposition
4.1
.

Corollary 4.2
.

For
\delta<\rho/2
and
d
-dimensional simplex
S=\{y_{0},\ldots,y_{d}\}
of circumradius
\rho(S)=\rho
, there exist integers
n
,
k
, a
k
-dimensional vector
c=(c_{1},\ldots,c_{k})\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
with
||c||=\rho
and a
d
-dimensional simplex
S^{\prime}=\{z_{0},\ldots,z_{d}\}\subseteq\Spread(c,[n])
such that
||y_{i}-z_{i}||<\delta
for
0\leqslant i\leqslant d
.

The second reason is that Spread configurations are robust.

Claim 4.3
.

\Spread(c,\mathbb{N})
is robust.

Proof.

Let
X\subseteq\Spread(c,\mathbb{N})
be a finite configuration. Then there exist
N
and
\mathcal{J}=\{J_{1},\ldots,J_{t}\}\subseteq[N]^{(k)}
such that
X=\{\spread(c,J):J\in\mathcal{J}\}
. Note that there exists a bijective map
\varphi
from
\Spread(c,\mathbb{N})
to
\mathbb{N}^{(k)}
given by
\varphi(\spread(c,J))=J
. Thus, for any finite coloring of
\Spread(c,\mathbb{N})
there is a corresponding coloring of
\mathbb{N}^{(k)}
. By Ramsey’s theorem, there exist set
A\subseteq\mathbb{N}
of size
N
such that
A^{(k)}
is monochromatic. Therefore, the configuration
\Spread(c,A)
is monochromatic. The result follows now since
X\subseteq\Spread(c,A)
.
∎

Another important result for our proof is the next characterization of configurations of points in an Euclidean space. Let
M=(m_{ij})_{0\leqslant i,j\leqslant d}
be a symmetric real matrix with zero entries on the main diagonal. We say that the matrix
M
is of
negative type
if

\displaystyle\sum_{0\leqslant i<j\leqslant d}m_{ij}\lambda_{i}\lambda_{j}\leqslant 0

(4)

holds for all choices of
\lambda_{0},\ldots,\lambda_{d}
with
\lambda_{0}+\ldots+\lambda_{d}=0
and
\lambda_{0}^{2}+\ldots+\lambda_{d}^{2}=1.

Theorem 4.4

(
[
18
]
)
.

A finite configuration
X=\{x_{0},\ldots,x_{d}\}
with distances
d_{ij}=||x_{i}-x_{j}||
can be embedded in the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
if and only if the matrix
M=(m_{ij})_{0\leqslant i,j\leqslant d}
given by
m_{ij}=d_{ij}^{2}
is of negative type. Moreover,
X
is a
d
-dimensional simplex if and only if the inequality in (
4
) is strict for all choices of
\lambda_{0},\ldots,\lambda_{d}
.

As a consequence of Theorem
4.4
, we can show that all almost regular simplices are realizable.

Corollary 4.5
.

Let
d
be an integer and
\beta,\varepsilon>0
be real numbers such that
\varepsilon<\frac{\beta}{64d^{2}}
. For any symmetric matrix of distances
D=\{d_{ij}\}_{0\leqslant i,j\leqslant d}
satisfying

\displaystyle\beta-\varepsilon\leqslant d_{ij}\leqslant\beta+\varepsilon,

(5)

there exists a
d
-dimensional simplex
S=\{x_{0},\ldots,x_{d}\}
such that
||x_{i}-x_{j}||=d_{ij}
for every
0\leqslant i<j\leqslant d
.

Proof.

Let
M=(m_{ij})_{0\leqslant i,j\leqslant d}
be the symmetric matrix with zero entries in the main diagonal given by
m_{ij}=d_{ij}^{2}
for
i\neq j
. For real numbers
\lambda_{0},\ldots,\lambda_{d}
satisfying
\lambda_{0}+\ldots+\lambda_{d}=0
and
\lambda_{0}^{2}+\ldots+\lambda_{d}^{2}=1
we have that

\displaystyle 0=\left(\sum_{i=0}^{d}\lambda_{i}\right)^{2}=1+2\sum_{0\leqslant i<j\leqslant d}\lambda_{i}\lambda_{j}.

Hence,

\displaystyle\sum_{0\leqslant i<j\leqslant d}\lambda_{i}\lambda_{j}=-\frac{1}{2}

(6)

Thus, by (
5
) and (
6
) we have

\displaystyle\left|\left|\sum_{0\leqslant i<j\leqslant d}m_{ij}\lambda_{i}\lambda_{j}+\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}\right|\right|=\left|\left|\sum_{0\leqslant i<j\leqslant d}(m_{ij}-\beta^{2})\lambda_{i}\lambda_{j}\right|\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{0\leqslant i<j\leqslant d}||m_{ij}-\beta^{2}||

\displaystyle\leqslant(d+1)^{2}(2\varepsilon\beta+\varepsilon^{2})<\frac{\beta^{2}}{4}.

This implies that
\sum_{0\leqslant i<j\leqslant d}m_{ij}\lambda_{i}\lambda_{j}<-\frac{\beta^{2}}{4}
and
M
is strictly of negative type. Therefore, by Theorem
4.4
there exists a simplex
S=\{x_{0},\ldots,x_{d}\}
with
||x_{i}-x_{j}||=d_{ij}
for
0\leqslant i<j\leqslant d
.
∎

Finally, the last auxiliary result shows that any almost regular simplex can be embedded in a brick.

Theorem 4.6

(
[
4
,
13
]
)
.

For every
\beta,d>0
, there exists a real number
\eta:=\eta(\beta,d)
such that the following holds. For any simplex
S=\{w_{0},\ldots,w_{d}\}
satisfying

\displaystyle\beta-\eta\leqslant||w_{i}-w_{j}||\leqslant\beta+\eta

for
0\leqslant i<j\leqslant d
, there exists a
\binom{d+1}{2}
-dimensional brick
B
with
S\subseteq B
.

We are now ready to prove Theorem
1.3
.

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

To prove that a simplex is P-Ramsey we will apply again Theorem
3.4
, now combined with ideas from
[
4
,
13
]
. We find convenient to divide the proof in the next four steps:

Step 1:
For a simplex
S=\{x_{0},\ldots,x_{d}\}
with circumradius
\rho(S)=\rho
, we will find a simplex
S_{1}=\{y_{0},\ldots,y_{d}\}
a small positive real number
\beta
such that

\displaystyle||y_{i}-y_{j}||^{2}=||x_{i}-x_{j}||^{2}-\beta

for all
0\leqslant i\neq j\leqslant d
. This implies that the circumradius
\rho(S_{1})=\rho^{\prime}<\rho
.

Let
M=(m_{ij})_{0\leqslant i,j\leqslant d}
be the matrix given by
m_{ij}=||x_{i}-x_{j}||^{2}
. Since
S
is a simplex, by Theorem
4.4
there exists
\gamma>0
such that

\displaystyle\sum_{0\leqslant i<j\leqslant d}m_{ij}\lambda_{i}\lambda_{j}\leqslant-\gamma

for all choices of
\lambda_{0},\ldots,\lambda_{d}
with
\lambda_{0}+\ldots+\lambda_{d}=0
and
\lambda_{0}^{2}+\ldots+\lambda_{d}^{2}=1
. Set
\beta=\frac{\gamma}{8d^{2}}
and let
M^{\prime}=(m^{\prime}_{ij})_{0\leqslant i,j\leqslant d}
be the matrix defined by
m^{\prime}_{ij}=m_{ij}-\beta
for
i\neq j
and zero entries in the main diagonal. Since
\beta(d+1)^{2}\leqslant 4\beta d^{2}<\gamma/2
, then

\displaystyle\sum_{0\leqslant i<j\leqslant d}m^{\prime}_{ij}\lambda_{i}\lambda_{j}\leqslant-\beta\sum_{0\leqslant i<j\leqslant d}\lambda_{i}\lambda_{j}-\gamma\leqslant\beta(d+1)^{2}-\gamma<-\frac{\gamma}{2}<0.

Consequently,
M^{\prime}
is strictly negative, which implies that there exists simplex
S_{!}=\{y_{0},\ldots,y_{d}\}
such that

\displaystyle||y_{i}-y_{j}||^{2}=m^{\prime}_{ij}=||x_{i}-x_{j}||^{2}-\beta

(7)

for
0\leqslant i<j\leqslant d
.

Step 2:
For
\delta\ll\beta
, we find
k
-dimensional vector
c=(c_{1},\ldots,c_{k})\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
and
S_{2}=\{z_{0},\ldots,z_{d}\}\subseteq\Spread(c,[n])
with
||z_{i}-y_{i}||<\delta
for
0\leqslant i\leqslant d
. Moreover,

\displaystyle||z_{i}-z_{j}||^{2}=||x_{i}-x_{j}||^{2}-\beta\pm\varepsilon

where
\varepsilon:=\varepsilon(\beta,d)\rightarrow 0
as
\delta\rightarrow 0
.

Let
\eta:=\eta(\beta,d)>0
be the positive real number given by Theorem
4.6
, let
\varepsilon<\min\{\beta/64d^{2},\eta\}
and let
\delta:=\delta(\eta,\rho)
be sufficiently small. By Corollary
4.2
, there exist integers
n
,
k
, a
k
-dimensional vector
c=(c_{1},\ldots,c_{k})\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
with
||c||=\rho^{\prime}
and a simplex
S_{2}=\{z_{0},\ldots,z_{d}\}\subseteq\Spread(c,[n])
such that
||y_{i}-z_{i}||<\delta
for
0\leqslant i\leqslant d
. Thus, a triangle inequality gives us that

\displaystyle||y_{i}-y_{j}||-2\delta<||z_{i}-z_{j}||<||y_{i}-y_{j}||+2\delta.

(8)

Hence, by combining (
7
) and (
8
)

\displaystyle||x_{i}-x_{j}||^{2}-\beta+4\delta^{2}-4\delta||y_{i}-y_{j}||<||z_{i}-z_{j}||^{2}<||x_{i}-x_{j}||^{2}-\beta+4\delta^{2}+4\delta||y_{i}-y_{j}||.

Since
||y_{i}-y_{j}||<2\rho^{\prime}
and
4\delta^{2}+8\delta\rho^{\prime}<\varepsilon
for sufficiently small
\delta
, we have that

\displaystyle||x_{i}-x_{j}||^{2}-\beta-\varepsilon<||z_{i}-z_{j}||^{2}<||x_{i}-x_{j}||^{2}-\beta+\varepsilon

Step 3:
We find an almost regular simplex
S_{3}=\{w_{0},\ldots,w_{d}\}
satisfying

\displaystyle||w_{i}-w_{j}||^{2}=||x_{i}-x_{j}||^{2}-||z_{i}-z_{j}||^{2}=\beta\pm\varepsilon

for all
0\leqslant i\neq j\leqslant d
. Furthermore, there exists a brick
B
such that
S_{3}\subseteq B
.

This is an easy consequence of our preliminary results. By our choice of
\varepsilon
, Corollary
4.5
guarantees that there exists simplex
S_{3}=\{w_{0},\ldots,w_{d}\}
such that

\displaystyle||w_{i}-w_{j}||^{2}=||x_{i}-x_{j}||^{2}-||z_{i}-z_{j}||^{2}.

Moreover, by Theorem
4.6
, there exists a
\binom{d+1}{2}
-dimensional brick
B
such that
W\subseteq B
.

Step 4:
We construct a simplex
F\cong S
such that
F\subseteq B\times\Spread(c,[n])
and
F\not\subseteq\Spread(c,[n])
and apply Theorem
3.4

Let
F=\{f_{0},\ldots,f_{d}\}
be the simplex defined by

\displaystyle f_{i}=w_{i}*z_{i},

where the symbol
*
stands for the usual concatenation, i.e., if
a=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{r})
and
b=(b_{1},\ldots,b_{s})
, then
a*b=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{r},b_{1},\ldots,b_{s})
. Hence,

\displaystyle||f_{i}-f_{j}||^{2}=||w_{i}-w_{j}||^{2}+||z_{i}-z_{j}||^{2}=||x_{i}-x_{j}||^{2}

for every
0\leqslant i,j\leqslant d
. Thus, the configuration
F
is a copy of
S
. Furthermore, by the construction of
F
we have that

\displaystyle F\subseteq W\times Z\subseteq B\times\Spread(c,\mathbb{N}),

where
B
is a
\binom{d+1}{2}
-dimensional brick and
\Spread(c,\mathbb{N})
is a robust configuration (Claim
4.3
). Since
\rho(\Spread(c,\mathbb{N}))=\rho^{\prime}<\rho=\rho(F)
, we obtain that
F\not\subseteq\Spread(c,\mathbb{N})
and by Theorem
3.4
the configuration
F\cong S
is P-Ramsey.
∎

5.
Concluding remarks

The list of known Ramsey configurations is quite limited. Apart of simplices and bricks, the most significant result is due to Kříž
[
10
]
who proved that regular polygons are Ramsey. Unfortunately, our method of robust configurations does not apply here. This leaves us with the following question:

Question 5.1
.

Are regular polygons P-Ramsey?

Differently from Theorem
1.1
, the proof in
[
10
]
do not provide a density result for regular polygons. Another interesting question would be to determine if such result exists.

Question 5.2
.

Let
F
be a regular polygon. For every
\mu>0
, is there a configuration
Y:=Y(F,\mu)
such that any set
Z\subseteq Y
of size
|Z|\geqslant\mu|Y|
contains a copy of
F
?

Lastly, another direction of research would be to obtain sharp bounds for the real number
\mu
in the P-Ramsey definition. It is not hard to show that for a configuration
X
with
k
points we cannot take
\mu>\frac{k-1}{k}
. However, our proofs of Theorem
1.3
and
1.4
only gives
\mu=\frac{1}{4}
. It would be interesting to close the gap for simplices.

Question 5.3
.

Let
S
be a
d
-dimensional simplex. What is the largest value of
\mu>0
such that there exists a configuration
Y
satisfying properties
(i)
and
(ii)
of Definition
1.2
?
