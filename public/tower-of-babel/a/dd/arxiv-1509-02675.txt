Orbital effect of magnetic field on the Majorana phase diagram
Bas Nijholt, Anton R. Akhmerov

Abstract
Studies of Majorana bound states in semiconducting nanowires frequently neglect the orbital effect of a magnetic field. Systematically studying its role leads us to several conclusions for designing Majoranas in this system. Specifically, we show that for experimentally relevant parameter values the orbital effect of a magnetic field has a stronger impact on the dispersion relation than the Zeeman effect. While Majoranas do not require the presence of only one dispersion subband, we observe that the size of the Majoranas becomes unpractically large, and the band gap unpractically small, when more than one subband is filled. Since the orbital effect of a magnetic field breaks several symmetries of the Hamiltonian, it leads to the appearance of large regions in parameter space with no band gap whenever the magnetic field is not aligned with the wire axis. The reflection symmetry of the Hamiltonian with respect to the plane perpendicular to the wire axis guarantees that the wire stays gapped in the topologically nontrivial region as long as the field is aligned with the wire.

I
Introduction

The search for Majorana bound states, the simplest non-Abelian particles, is fueled by their suitability for fault-tolerant quantum computation
Alicea 2012
;
Beenakker 2013
.
A large fraction of the experimental effort
Mourik et al. 2012
;
Das et al. 2012
;
Deng et al. 2012
;
Churchill et al. 2013
;
Deng et al. 2014
is focused on creating Majoranas in semiconducting nanowires with proximity superconductivity, spin-orbit coupling, and magnetic field.
The theoretical foundation for this platform was initially developed for a single one-dimensional spinful band with intrinsic superconducting pairing
Lutchyn et al. 2010
;
Oreg et al. 2010
.
Due to its compactness this model can be solved analytically, and it predicts that Majorana bound states appear when
E_{\textrm{Z}}^{2}>\mu^{2}+\Delta^{2}
, when the Zeeman energy becomes larger than the harmonic mean of the superconducting gap and the chemical potential.

The single-mode model is minimalistic and neglects many physical phenomena that are crucial for understanding the properties of the Majorana bound states.
The existing extensions of this model study multimode wires
Potter and Lee 2010
, better modeling of the induced gap
Liu et al. 2012
;
Stanescu et al. 2014
, the role of electrostatics
Vuik et al. 2016
, disorder
Potter and Lee 2012
;
Pientka et al. 2012
;
Adagideli et al. 2014
, and the
k\cdot p
-model
Stanescu and Tewari 2013
.
The orbital effect of a magnetic field was analyzed both in planar wires
Osca and Serra 2015
;
Lim et al. 2012
and on the surface of a cylinder
Lim et al. 2013
.

We systematically study the influence of the orbital effect of a magnetic field on the symmetries of the Hamiltonian and the topological phase diagram for a three-dimensional (3D) nanowire.
The orbital effect of a magnetic field perpendicular to the wire induces a skipping orbit motion of the electrons.
The cyclotron radius becomes comparable to the typical wire diameters
d\sim$100\text{\,}\mathrm{nm}$
already at the field of
0.3\text{\,}\mathrm{T}
, and at chemical potential corresponding to the optimal topological band gap.
In addition, a field parallel to the wire shifts the energies of each band due to the effect of magnetic flux.
We expect the shift of the energies to be comparable to the level spacing when the flux through the wire diameter is of the order of a flux quantum.
Our findings are very different from those of Refs.
Osca and Serra 2015
;
Lim et al. 2013
;
Lim et al. 2012
because we do not limit our analysis to a Hamiltonian with an artificially high spatial symmetry, or low dimensionality.

III
Symmetry analysis

The Majorana bound states are protected by the combination of the band gap and the particle-hole symmetry of the Hamiltonian
\mathcal{P}H\left(k\right)\mathcal{P}^{-1}=-H\left(-k\right)
.
In the basis of Eq. (
1
) this symmetry has the form
\mathcal{P}=\sigma_{y}\tau_{y}\mathcal{K}
, with
\mathcal{K}
the complex conjugation.
In general there are no additional symmetries and the Hamiltonian belongs to symmetry class D
Altland and Zirnbauer 1997
.
Particle-hole symmetry only requires that the energy
E_{n}(k)
of
n
-th band at momentum
k
is
E_{n}(k)=-E_{m}(-k)
of some other
m
-th band; at the same time
\mathcal{P}
puts no constraints on
E_{n}
itself.
This means that whenever
E_{n}
changes sign at a certain momentum, the band structure becomes gapless.
This tilting of the band structure
Rex and Sudbø 2014
[shown in the middle panels in Fig.
2
, where
E_{n}(k)\neq-E_{m}(k)
] is a strong effect that does not vanish with superconducting gap or spin-orbit, and can easily become larger than the induced gap, rendering the creation of Majoranas impossible.

The tilting of the band structure is absent if the Hamiltonian has an extra chiral symmetry alongside
\mathcal{P}
.
It has been shown that the Hamiltonian has an approximate chiral symmetry
\mathcal{C}H\left(k\right)\mathcal{C}^{-1}=-H\left(k\right)
,
\mathcal{C}=\sigma_{y}\tau_{y}
, valid when the wire diameter
d
is smaller than the spin-orbit length
l_{so}=\hbar^{2}/m^{*}\alpha

Tewari and Sau 2012
;
Diez et al. 2012
, and
B_{y}=0
.
Then the
p_{y}\sigma_{x}\tau_{z}
term, associated with the transverse motion in Eq. (
1
), is negligible.
Without the tilting, the system is gapped in every region of parameter space, except at the topological phase boundaries.
However, for relevant experimental parameters
Mourik et al. 2012
, the orbital terms break this symmetry more strongly than the spin-orbit term, bringing the system back to symmetry class D.

We perform a systematic search of symmetries that the Hamiltonian (
1
) may have.
Rosdahl and Akhmerov

We find the reflection symmetry with respect to the

-plane
\mathcal{R}_{x}H\left(k\right)\mathcal{R}_{x}^{-1}=H\left(-k\right)
,
\mathcal{R}_{x}=\sigma_{x}\delta(x+x^{\prime})
.
It is independent of the wire geometry and spin-orbit strength and guarantees the absence of tilting whenever the field is aligned with the

-axis.
The combined symmetry
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}=\mathcal{R}_{x}\mathcal{P}

\mathcal{P}^{\prime}H(k)\mathcal{P}^{\prime-1}=-H(k)
.

Additionally, we find a chiral symmetry

\mathcal{C}^{\prime}H\left(k\right)\mathcal{C}^{\prime-1}=-H\left(k\right)

the reflection with respect to the

This chiral symmetry holds when the magnetic field lies in the

1

\mathcal{R}_{y}

1

When present,

guarantees the absence of band structure tilting just like

This symmetry is present in most theoretical models, and in particular it is obeyed by the Hamiltonians used in Refs.
Osca and Serra 2015
;
Lim et al. 2013
;
Lim et al. 2012
.
A finite

breaks both

and
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
therefore, the bands can tilt and close the topological gap.

V
Results

We use realistic parameters of an InSb nanowire
Mourik et al. 2012
:
\alpha=$20\text{\,}\mathrm{meV}\text{\,}\mathrm{nm}$
,
m^{*}=0.015m_{e}
,
\Delta=$0.250\text{\,}\mathrm{meV}$
,
d=$100\text{\,}\mathrm{nm}$
, and

.
At the high fields that are typically used in experiment (
B\apprge$1\text{\,}\mathrm{T}$
), we find that the Zeeman effect of the magnetic field has a lower impact on the phase boundaries than the orbital effect of the magnetic field (see Fig.
4
).
We verify that the band gap is protected by
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
as long as
B_{y}=0
, despite that the orbital effect of the magnetic field reduces
E_{\textrm{gap}}
.

In agreement with our expectations a finite
B_{y}\lesssim$0.1\text{\,}\mathrm{T}$
leads to the closing of the band gap (see Figs.
2
and
5
).
The maximum tolerable

becomes smaller with increasing
\mu
.
The narrow regions with suppressed
E_{\textrm{gap}}
visible in Figs.
4
(c) and
4
(d) are the consequence of Dirac cones appearing in
(k_{x},B)
-space and are protected by
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
.
Breaking

breaks
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
and removes these Dirac cones.

We now turn to study the system shown in Fig.
1

\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
strongly broken and only
\mathcal{R}_{x}
and
\mathcal{P}

Since the induced superconducting gap
\Delta_{\textrm{ind}}\approx$250\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro eV}$

In the long junction limit the induced gap equals
\Delta_{\textrm{ind}}\approx\hbar Tv_{\textrm{F}}/d
, where

is the transparency of the tunnel barrier, and

the Fermi velocity.
In the absence of the orbital effect of a magnetic field, this means that the Zeeman energy has to exceed
\Delta_{\textrm{ind}}
and therefore the critical value of the magnetic field at which the gap closes strongly depends on

6

With the orbital effect of the magnetic field flux, penetration through the quasiparticle trajectory changes the interference phases, which suppresses the induced gap and causes the topological phase transitions to occur at a value of

6

The Majorana decay lengths

7

Specifically, the mode of the distribution of

changes by a factor of
\sim 4
in the parameter range we consider (see histograms in Fig.

However, the minimum values of

\approx$200\text{\,}\mathrm{nm}$

Therefore,

To investigate the effect of the spin-orbit coupling on the Majorana properties in the presence of an orbital field, we have repeated the calculations shown in Figs.

Therefore, increasing spin-orbit strength has a positive but not very strong effect on the topological band gap.

VI
Discussion and Conclusions

We have shown that the orbital effect of a magnetic field complicates the creation of Majoranas in nanowires.
Orbital terms break the chiral symmetry
\mathcal{C}
and prevent the appearance of Majoranas whenever the magnetic field is not aligned with the wire axis.
When the field does point along the
x
-axis, we find that the reflection symmetry
\mathcal{R}_{x}
in combination with particle-hole symmetry
\mathcal{P}
protects the band gap from closing everywhere in
(B,\mu)
-space, except at the topological phase boundaries.
At experimentally relevant values of magnetic field, the orbital effect has a stronger impact on the dispersion relation than the Zeeman effect.
Furthermore, the orbital effect suppresses
E_{\textrm{gap}}
and increases
\xi
.
However, the maximum value of the
E_{\textrm{gap}}
in the topologically nontrivial region does not change as drastically (from
0.21\text{\,}\mathrm{meV}
to
0.14\text{\,}\mathrm{meV}
) and the minimal decay length changes even less (from
201\text{\,}\mathrm{nm}
to
210\text{\,}\mathrm{nm}
).
The reflection symmetry
\mathcal{R}_{x}
of the Hamiltonian that we consider is respected by any Rashba spin-orbit interaction.
Dresselhaus spin-orbit coupling breaks
\mathcal{R}_{x}
; however, it is expected to be weak in the nanowires.

Our simulations can be made more complete by complementing them with self-consistent electrostatics and magnetic field screening by the superconductor.
An additional extension of our work is to go beyond the effective mass approximation and to use the
k\cdot p
model.
A separate topic of study is the interplay between the orbital effect of the magnetic field and disorder.
We expect that the sensitivity to disorder will increase by taking the orbital effect of the magnetic field into account.

Our results suggest that keeping the chemical potential low is required to obtain Majoranas with reasonable length and energy scales.
Furthermore, our findings reveal a complication in realizing more sophisticated Majorana setups, such as a T-junction required for braiding.
This is because of the requirement that the field should be aligned with the nanowire.
A possible strategy to reduce the undesirable orbital effect of the magnetic field is to use nanowires with smaller diameters at a cost of reduced electric field effect and increased disorder sensitivity.
