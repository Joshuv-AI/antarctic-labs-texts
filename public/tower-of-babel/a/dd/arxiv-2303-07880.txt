Run-and-tumble motion in a linear ratchet potential: Analytic solution, power extraction, and first-passage properties Preprint: APS/123-QED
Connor Roberts, Zigan Zhen

Abstract
We explore the properties of run-and-tumble particles moving in a piecewise-linear “ratchet” potential by deriving analytic results for the system’s steady-state probability density, current, entropy production rate, extractable power, and thermodynamic efficiency. The ratchet’s broken spatial symmetry rectifies the particles’ self-propelled motion, resulting in a positive current that peaks at finite values of the diffusion strength, ratchet height, and particle self-propulsion speed. Similar nonmonotonic behaviour is also observed for the extractable power and efficiency. We find the optimal apex position for generating maximum current varies with diffusion, and that entropy production can have nonmonotonic dependence on diffusion. In particular, for vanishing diffusion, entropy production remains finite when particle self-propulsion is weaker than the ratchet force. Furthermore, power extraction with near-perfect efficiency is achievable in certain parameter regimes due to the simplifications afforded by modelling “dry” active particles. In the final part, we derive mean first-passage times and splitting probabilities for different boundary and initial conditions. This work connects the study of work extraction from active matter with exactly solvable active particle models and will therefore facilitate the design of active engines through these analytic results.



I
Introduction

Active matter is composed of agents that consume energy from their environment to exert mechanical forces
Marchetti et al. 2013
. These systems break detailed balance and are thus impervious to the tools of equilibrium statistical mechanics
Tailleur and Cates 2008
. Because of the rich phenomenology they capture, even at the single-particle scale
Elgeti et al. 2015
, active matter models have become the most favoured route for exploring the broader field of nonequilibrium systems
Gompper et al. 2020
.

A paradigmatic active matter model is the run-and-tumble (RnT) particle, which idealises the piecewise-ballistic locomotion of
E. coli
bacteria
Schnitzer 1993
. RnT particles are arguably the simplest of the canonical active particle models, which also includes active Ornstein-Uhlenbeck particles
Fodor et al. 2016
;
Martin et al. 2021
;
Maggi et al. 2015
and active Brownian particles
Wagner et al. 2017
;
Das et al. 2018
;
Caraglio and Franosch 2022
. This has led to extensive study of their nonequilibrium steady-state distributions in confining potentials
Tailleur and Cates 2009
;
Zhen and Pruessner 2022
;
Roberts and Pruessner 2022
;
Angelani 2017
;
Garcia-Millan and Pruessner 2021
;
Razin 2020
;
Dhar et al. 2019
;
Basu et al. 2020
;
Frydel 2022a
;
Ezhilan et al. 2015
;
Elgeti and Gompper 2015
. Notably, most results for these models are obtained through simulations or under approximations
Maggi et al. 2015
;
Ezhilan et al. 2015
;
Elgeti and Gompper 2015
;
Fodor et al. 2016
;
Martin et al. 2021
;
Fox 1986
;

;
Marconi et al. 2016
;
Jung and Hänggi 1987
;
Marconi and Maggi 2015
, including recent work concerning RnT particles subject to a differentiable ratchet potential
Derivaux et al. 2022

Angelani 2017
;

When immersed in asymmetric environments, active particles display unidirectional motion
Doering and Gadoua 1992
;
Astumian and Bier 1994
;
Pavliotis 2005
;
Galajda et al. 2007
;
Wan et al. 2008
;
Angelani et al. 2009
;
Di Leonardo et al. 2010
;

;
Reichhardt and Reichhardt 2017
;
Zhen and Pruessner 2022
;
Koumakis et al. 2014
;
Muhsin and Sahoo 2023
. The emergence of such rectified motion has generated significant interest in active engines, whose constituent active agents perform work on an external load to store potential energy
Jülicher et al. 1997
;
Parrondo et al. 1998
;
Pietzonka et al. 2019
;
Sekimoto 1997
. The net current observed in these systems is the hallmark of broken time-reversal symmetry, indicating a system is operating far from equilibrium. Exactly “how far” is quantified by the entropy production rate

;
Garcia-Millan and Pruessner 2021
;
Cocconi et al. 2020
;
Martin et al. 2021
, which was recently calculated for RnT particles confined to an infinite-square well

, but has yet to be calculated for asymmetric potentials.

The nonequilibrium behaviour of active matter results in first-passage properties that are significantly different to that of equilibrium systems. For instance, the mean escape time of a Brownian particle from a potential well depends only on the ratio of the relative height of the potential barrier to the particle’s thermal energy
Kramers 1940
. In contrast, the mean escape time of active particles can depend on the detailed shape of the potential
Woillez et al. 2019
. Studying the first-passage properties of active matter is particularly relevant to biology, which is often concerned with the time it takes motile agents to reach certain locations, such as in chemotaxis
Angelani et al. 2014
. For the specific case of RnT particles, first-passage properties have been studied both with
Malakar et al. 2018
and without
Angelani et al. 2014
;
Le Doussal et al. 2019
;
Angelani 2015
;
Angelani 2017
the presence of additive diffusive noise. The noiseless case allows analytic results for run-and-tumble particles to be obtained in the case of partially absorbing boundaries
Angelani et al. 2014
;
Angelani 2017
, encounter-based absorption
Bressloff 2022
;
Bressloff 2023
, and in the presence of symmetric confining potentials
Dhar et al. 2019
. However, even for the more general case of nonnegligible diffusive noise, it is possible to obtain analytic expressions for the first-passage time density on a semi-infinite line, as well as the splitting probabilities and mean first-passage times on a finite interval
Malakar et al. 2018
. It is therefore of interest to see how the combination of asymmetric potentials and diffusion, both of which will be considered here, generalise these known results.

In the present work, we obtain exact analytic results for RnT particles in the presence of an external piecewise-linear “ratchet” potential on a one-dimensional ring. In addition, we consider the application of an external counterforce, assuming the role of an external load, to the resulting rectified motion. The counterforce allows for the study of work extraction and is therefore relevant to the operation of active engines. This model of RnT motion is implemented through the addition of active telegraphic noise to the motion of an overdamped Brownian particle in a ratchet. This can describe several physically equivalent nonequilibrium systems, discussed further in Sec.
II
.

In what follows, the derivation and discussion of new results begin in Sec.
III
with a calculation of the state-dependent probability densities at stationarity, which are obtained by solving the governing coupled Fokker-Planck equations of the system and then confirmed through Monte Carlo simulations of the Langevin equation. From these densities, in Sec.
IV
, we obtain the corresponding currents and observe nonmonotonic dependencies on the system parameters, similarly to other nonequilibrium systems
Semeraro et al. 2023
;
Rizkallah et al. 2023
;
Bijnens and Maes 2021
;
Ai and Li 2017
. Then, following the formalism outlined in Ref.
Seifert 2005
, we derive the steady-state entropy production rate in Sec.
V
, which paves the way for the study of the power output and thermodynamic efficiency in Sec.
VI
. Finally, in Sec.
VII

Given the large number of quantities of interest, the aim of this work is not to provide a full characterisation of the high-dimensional phase space in each case, but to give an overview of the most interesting and relevant features.

III
Probability densities

The probability densities at stationarity
P_{R,L}^{[i]}(x)\equiv\lim\limits_{t\rightarrow\infty}P_{R,L}^{[i]}(x,t)
are derived from solving the coupled Fokker-Planck equations (
3
) with the left-hand sides set to zero, i.e.
\partial_{t}P_{R}^{[i]}(x)=\partial_{t}P_{L}^{[i]}(x)=0
. For the specific case of
f=0
, the steady-state solution to Eq. (
3
) has been derived in the context of molecular motors
Astumian and Bier 1994
, and was used principally to investigate how the steady-state current varies with the switching rate (equivalent to
\gamma
here) of the potential. However, this solution has never been studied in the context of RnT motion. The current of an RnT particle moving in a piecewise-linear potential has been studied for the less general case of
D=0

Angelani et al. 2011
. However, to the best of our knowledge, a detailed study of the more general
D>0
solution for RnT particles does not exist in the literature. Furthermore, we believe our subsequent derivations of the entropy production, power output, thermodynamic efficiency and first-passage properties, as well as the generalisation to
f\neq 0
, to all be novel results.

To obtain the steady-state probability densities, we make the ansatz
P_{R,L}^{[i]}(x)=\mathcal{Z}_{R,L}^{[i]}\exp(\lambda^{[i]}x)

Astumian and Bier 1994
. Substituting this ansatz into Eq. (
3
) for
\partial_{t}P_{R,L}(x,t)=0
results in four linearly independent solutions for each of the particle species, right movers and left movers, in each section of the ratchet,
i=1
and
i=2
, totalling 16 degrees of freedom. Each set of eigenvalues
\lambda^{[i]}
,
i\in\{1,2\}
, is found by solving a quartic characteristic equation. One of the eigenvalues in each set vanishes and so the probability densities have the general solution

P_{R,L}^{[i]}(x)=\mathcal{A}_{R,L}^{[i]}e^{\lambda_{\mathcal{A}}^{[i]}x}+\mathcal{B}_{R,L}^{[i]}e^{\lambda_{\mathcal{B}}^{[i]}x}+\mathcal{C}_{R,L}^{[i]}e^{\lambda_{\mathcal{C}}^{[i]}x}+\mathcal{D}_{R,L}^{[i]},

(4)

with the 16 coefficients
\mathcal{A}_{R}^{[1]},\mathcal{A}_{L}^{[1]},\mathcal{A}_{R}^{[2]},\dots,\mathcal{D}_{L}^{[2]}
to be determined.

These coefficients are fixed by demanding each linearly independent term in Eq. (
4
) individually satisfies Eq. (
3
) in the steady state, i.e. the resulting prefactors after substitution of each
\exp(\lambda^{[i]}x)
must be zero
Astumian and Bier 1994
. This fixes half of the 16 degrees of freedom in Eq. (
4
). The remaining half is fixed through continuity of the probability densities and currents for each species across
x=0
and
x=a
, as well as the normalisation condition
\int_{0}^{\ell}\mathrm{d}x~P(x)=1
, where
P(x)=P_{R}(x)+P_{L}(x)
is the total probability density. Hence, in principle, the steady-state densities for RnT particles in a piecewise-linear ratchet potential can be obtained in closed form. However, solving 16 coupled equations unsurprisingly results in unwieldy expressions for the coefficients
\mathcal{A}_{R}^{[1]},\mathcal{A}_{L}^{[1]},\mathcal{A}_{R}^{[2]},\dots,\mathcal{D}_{L}^{[2]}
, so the bulk of the calculations was performed using Mathematica. The resulting solutions are too long to conveniently display as equations and so most of our results are displayed as graphs alongside descriptions of their qualitative behaviour in the main text. Nevertheless, we emphasise that all results presented in this paper originate from exact expressions. In fact, any observable that can be written exclusively in terms of the system parameters and the coefficients
\mathcal{A}_{R}^{[1]},\mathcal{A}_{L}^{[1]},\mathcal{A}_{R}^{[2]},\dots,\mathcal{D}_{L}^{[2]}
, such as the steady-state current, Eq. (
8
), and the steady-state entropy production rate, Eq. (
21
), can be written in closed form. The full details of how the expressions for the densities were obtained are left to Appendix
A
. We also derive the steady-state probability densities for the nontrivial
D=0
case in Appendix
B
. This simplification ultimately leads to closed-form expressions for the corresponding particle current that are compact enough to be written down, see Eqs. (
46
)-(
49

IV
Currents

The currents for right movers and left movers are, respectively,

\displaystyle J_{R}^{[i]}(x,t)

\displaystyle=\left(v-f-U^{\prime[i]}\right)P^{[i]}_{R}(x,t)-D\frac{\partial P^{[i]}_{R}(x,t)}{\partial x},

(5a)

\displaystyle J_{L}^{[i]}(x,t)

\displaystyle=\left(-v-f-U^{\prime[i]}\right)P^{[i]}_{L}(x,t)-D\frac{\partial P^{[i]}_{L}(x,t)}{\partial x},

(5b)

where, again, we define separate contributions for the different regions of the ratchet (denoted by the superscript
[i]
) and we recall the
U^{\prime[i]}
are constant, see Sec.
II
. The Fokker-Planck equation (
3
) can be rewritten as a continuity equation in terms of the currents defined in Eq. (
5
),

\displaystyle\frac{\partial P^{[i]}_{R}(x,t)}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial J^{[i]}_{R}(x,t)}{\partial x}-\mathcal{J}_{L\rightarrow R}^{[i]}(x,t)

\displaystyle=0,

(6a)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial P^{[i]}_{L}(x,t)}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial J^{[i]}_{L}(x,t)}{\partial x}+\mathcal{J}_{L\rightarrow R}^{[i]}(x,t)

\displaystyle=0,

(6b)

where we have also defined the flux density of transitions from the left-moving state to the right-moving state as

\mathcal{J}_{L\rightarrow R}^{[i]}(x,t)\equiv\gamma\left(P^{[i]}_{L}(x,t)-P^{[i]}_{R}(x,t)\right)=-\mathcal{J}_{R\rightarrow L}^{[i]}(x,t).

(7)

The overall current is given by
J(x,t)=J_{R}(x,t)+J_{L}(x,t)
. By summing Eqs. (
6a
) and (
6b
), it can be seen that the stationary condition
\partial_{t}P^{[i]}(x)=0
implies the overall current at stationarity is constant across space since this leads to
\partial_{x}J^{[i]}=0
. Hence,
J
can be calculated from the probability densities on just a single section
[i]
of the ratchet since the other section must have the same current. If we choose
i=1
, corresponding to
x\in[0,a)
, then the resulting expression for
J=J_{R}^{[1]}(x)+J_{L}^{[1]}(x)
can be simplified significantly by setting
x=0
, yielding a closed expression in terms of the known coefficients
\mathcal{A}_{R}^{[1]},\mathcal{A}_{L}^{[1]},\mathcal{A}_{R}^{[2]},\dots,\mathcal{D}_{L}^{[2]}
from Eq. (
4
),

J=\sum_{\mathcal{Z}=\mathcal{A}}^{\mathcal{C}}\left[v\mathcal{Z}_{-}^{[1]}-\left(f+\frac{h}{a}+D\lambda_{\mathcal{Z}}^{[1]}\right)\mathcal{Z}_{+}^{[1]}\right]-\left(f+\frac{h}{a}\right)\mathcal{D}^{[i]},

(8)

where we have defined
\mathcal{Z}_{\pm}^{[i]}\equiv\mathcal{Z}_{R}^{[i]}\pm\mathcal{Z}_{L}^{[i]}
and have used that
\mathcal{D}_{R}^{[i]}=\mathcal{D}_{L}^{[i]}\equiv\mathcal{D}^{[i]}
, see Appendix
A
.

Based on the mechanism described above and in Fig.

\sim\gamma

V
Entropy production

The entropy production rate
\dot{S}_{\mathrm{int}}
quantifies the degree to which time-reversal symmetry is broken and therefore distinguishes between nonequilibrium,
\dot{S}_{\mathrm{int}}>0
, and equilibrium,
\dot{S}_{\mathrm{int}}=0
, systems. In the present system, there are several sources of symmetry breaking, such as asymmetric overall drift speeds due to the external force
f
, and that particles tend to run faster down the sides of the ratchet potential
U(x)
, Eq. (
1
), when the force due to the potential is aligned with the particle’s own self-propulsion speed
v
, similarly to RnT particles in a harmonic potential
Garcia-Millan and Pruessner 2021
. Starting from the Gibbs-Shannon entropy
Shannon 1948
, we take a well-established approach
Seifert 2005
;
Alston et al. 2022
;
Razin 2020
to obtain an exact expression for the entropy production rate at stationarity, Eq. (
21
).

The Gibbs-Shannon entropy for the present system is defined as

S(t)=-\sum_{k=R,L}\int_{0}^{\ell}\mathrm{d}x~P_{k}(x,t)\log\left(\frac{P_{k}(x,t)}{\bar{P}}\right),

(10)

where
\bar{P}
, with
\left[\bar{P}\right]=1/\ell
, is for dimensional consistency in the logarithm but will drop out of the final expression for
\dot{S}_{\mathrm{int}}
. The time derivative of Eq. (
10
) is

\dot{S}(t)=-\sum_{k=R,L}\int_{0}^{\ell}\mathrm{d}x~\frac{\partial P_{k}(x,t)}{\partial t}\left(\log\left(\frac{P_{k}(x,t)}{\bar{P}}\right)+1\right).

(11)

After relating
\partial_{t}P_{k}(x,t)
in Eq. (
11
) to the currents
J_{k}(x,t)
and
\mathcal{J}_{L\rightarrow R}(x,t)
through the continuity equations, Eq. (
6
), integrating by parts, and dropping boundary terms due to the periodic boundary conditions, we eventually obtain

\begin{split}\dot{S}(t)=-\int_{0}^{\ell}\mathrm{d}x\Bigg(&\sum_{k=R,L}\frac{J_{k}(x,t)}{P_{k}(x,t)}\frac{\partial P_{k}(x,t)}{\partial x}\\
&+\mathcal{J}_{L\rightarrow R}(x,t)\log\left(\frac{P_{R}(x,t)}{P_{L}(x,t)}\right)\Bigg).\end{split}

(12)

Using the definition of the currents, Eq. (
5
),
\partial_{x}P_{k}(x,t)
in Eq. (
12
) can be rewritten to yield

\begin{split}&\dot{S}(t)=\int_{0}^{\ell}\mathrm{d}x\Bigg[\sum_{k=R,L}\frac{J_{k}^{2}(x,t)}{DP_{k}(x,t)}+\frac{f+U^{\prime}(x)}{D}J(x,t)\\
&-\frac{v}{D}\left(J_{R}(x,t)-J_{L}(x,t)\right)+\mathcal{J}_{L\rightarrow R}(x,t)\log\left(\frac{P_{L}(x,t)}{P_{R}(x,t)}\right)\Bigg],\end{split}

(13)

where we have used that
J_{R}(x,t)+J_{L}(x,t)=J(x,t)
.

At this point, we separate the time derivative of the entropy into the contributions
\dot{S}(t)=\dot{S}_{\mathrm{int}}(t)-\dot{S}_{\mathrm{ext}}(t)
and identify the positive-definite terms in Eq. (
13
) as those corresponding to the internal entropy production rate of the system
\dot{S}_{\mathrm{int}}(t)

Cocconi et al. 2020
;
Alston et al. 2022
;
Razin 2020
. We thus have

\dot{S}_{\mathrm{int}}(t)=\dot{S}_{R}(t)+\dot{S}_{L}(t)+\dot{S}_{R\leftrightarrow L}(t)

(14)

where

\dot{S}_{R/L}(t)\equiv\int_{0}^{\ell}\mathrm{d}x~\dot{s}_{R/L}(x,t)\equiv\int_{0}^{\ell}\mathrm{d}x~\frac{J_{R/L}^{2}(x,t)}{DP_{R/L}(x,t)}

(15)

is the entropy production of right/left movers and
\dot{s}_{R/L}(x,t)\equiv J_{R/L}^{2}(x,t)/(DP_{R/L}(x,t))
is the local entropy production
density
of right/left movers at position
x
and time
t

Razin 2020
;
Garcia-Millan and Pruessner 2021
. Similarly,

\begin{split}\dot{S}_{R\leftrightarrow L}(t)&\equiv\int_{0}^{\ell}\mathrm{d}x~\dot{s}_{R\leftrightarrow L}(x,t)\\
&\equiv\int_{0}^{\ell}\mathrm{d}x~\mathcal{J}_{L\rightarrow R}(x,t)\log\left(\frac{P_{L}(x,t)}{P_{R}(x,t)}\right)\end{split}

(16)

is the entropy production due to transitions between right movers and left movers, with
\dot{s}_{R\leftrightarrow L}(x,t)=\mathcal{J}_{L\rightarrow R}(x,t)\log\left(P_{L}(x,t)/P_{R}(x,t)\right)
its local density. Note that
\dot{S}_{R\leftrightarrow L}(t)\geq 0
for all
t
since
\mathcal{J}_{L\rightarrow R}(x,t)
, Eq. (
7
), and
\log\left(P_{L}(x,t)/P_{R}(x,t)\right)
are both positive when
P_{L}(x,t)>P_{R}(x,t)
and both negative when
P_{L}(x,t)<P_{R}(x,t)
.

12

For

is a monotonically increasing function of the external force

v-f<U^{\prime[1]}
and
v+f<|U^{\prime[2]}|

a>\ell/2

For

\dot{S}_{\text{int}}

VIII
Conclusion

We have studied in detail the motion of run-and-tumble particles in a piecewise-linear “ratchet” potential
U(x)
, Eq. (
1
), that are also subject to an external counterforce
f
for the purpose of extracting useful work. We derived the stationary probability densities
P_{R,L}(x)
, Eq. (
4
), in Sec.
III
and Appendix
A
for both right-moving and left-moving particle species, Fig.
3
(a), and confirmed the numerically simulated time-dependent densities converge to these expressions as
t\rightarrow\infty
, Fig.
4
.

From the densities
P_{R,L}(x)
, we calculated the corresponding steady-state currents
J_{R}(x)
,
J_{L}(x)
, and
J=J_{R}(x)+J_{L}(x)
, Eq. (
8
), in Sec.
IV
and analysed how they depend on the system’s parameters. We found the current
J
peaks at a finite value of the ratchet potential height
h
and decays to zero as
h\to\infty
, Fig.
8
. The current
J
displays similar nonmonotonic dependencies on the magnitude of the particle’s self-propulsion speed
v
and the translational diffusion
D
. Depending on the strength of the diffusion
D
, the position of the apex
a
resulting in maximum current
J
occurs between
a=\ell-h/(v+f)
for
D=0
and
a\rightarrow\ell^{-}
for large
D
, Fig.
7
. As
D\rightarrow 0
, the current
J
either remains finite or vanishes depending on whether the particle is confined, Fig.
9
, i.e. whether
v-f<h/a
and
v+f<h/(\ell-a)
, representing confinement conditions for right movers and left movers respectively. Simple closed-form expressions were obtained for the
D=0
current in both the unconfined, Eq. (
46
), and confined, Eq. (
48
), cases in Appendix
B
. The currents for each particle species,
J_{R}(x)
and
J_{L}(x)
, are greater in magnitude close to the potential minimum at
x=0
due to particles that tumble before crossing the potential barrier, see Fig.
3
(b). This phenomenon is still present even when particles are confined because of weaker “effective” potentials that particles experience as a result of their self-propulsion, Fig.
5
. While many ratchet models exhibit current reversals
Reimann and Elston 1996
, where particles preferentially flow against the steeper slope of the potential, we do not observe any such phenomenon in the present system. This is in agreement with the findings of Ref.
Ai and Li 2017
, where there is no current reversal for overdamped active particles moving in a piecewise-linear potential, but there is for underdamped active particles. Hence, introducing inertia into the present system could result in a current reversal and thus allow power extraction from a “negative” load,
f<0
, i.e. an external force applied in the opposite direction to that considered here, which could be explored in future work.

In Sec.
V
, we derived the steady-state entropy production rate
\dot{S}_{\mathrm{int}}
, Eq. (
21
), and entropy production densities
\dot{s}_{R}(x)
,
\dot{s}_{L}(x)
, and
\dot{s}_{R\leftrightarrow L}(x)
, Eqs. (
15
) and (
16
), for each particle species. The total entropy production density
\dot{s}_{\mathrm{int}}(x)=\dot{s}_{R}(x)+\dot{s}_{L}(x)+\dot{s}_{R\leftrightarrow L}(x)
is greatest at the apex
x=a
of the ratchet where the mean local velocity of particles is greatest, Fig.
10
. The steady-state entropy production
\dot{S}_{\mathrm{int}}
was found to decrease monotonically with the height
h
of the potential, Fig.
11
. Remarkably, the entropy production
\dot{S}_{\mathrm{int}}
can have a nonmonotonic dependence on
D
and, in the limit
D\rightarrow 0
, remains finite when the particle is confined due to the resulting scaling of the currents for each particle species, Fig.
12
. We would expect to see similar behaviour for the entropy production of run-and-tumble particles in confining potentials of the form
U_{p}(x)\sim|x|^{p}
for
p=1
, studied in Ref.
Dhar et al. 2019
for the case
D=0
. Therefore, studying the entropy production of run-and-tumble particles subject to potentials of the form
U_{p}(x)\sim|x|^{p}
for
D>0
would be an interesting avenue for further research. It would also be interesting to consider smooth ratchet potentials, where particles can become confined to a finite region rather than a single point, to verify whether entropy production diverges in the confined case as
D\rightarrow 0
. Furthermore, throughout this paper we have calculated average values for our observables of interest. A study of higher-order moments, such as fluctuations of the entropy production, would be interesting to consider for future work.

The system studied in this work can be interpreted as an autonomous engine extracting work
W
from a run-and-tumble particle. For such an “active engine” we studied the extractable power
\dot{W}
, Eq. (
23
), and the thermodynamic efficiency
\eta
in Sec.
VI
. Poor efficiencies
\eta<1\%
are observed in the case where the particle is confined by the potential
U(x)
, Eq. (
1
), and therefore able to generate a current
J
only via diffusive fluctuations, Fig.
13
. However, efficiencies
\eta
arbitrarily close to
100\%
are possible for
\gamma\rightarrow 0
and
D\rightarrow 0
in the case where right movers are unconfined,
v-f>U^{\prime[1]}
, but left movers are confined,
v+f<|U^{\prime[2]}|
, as this prevents left movers from dissipating wasted energy (at least when particles are modelled as “dry” active matter). It would be interesting to calculate the maximum efficiency
\eta
of this system in the case of “wet” active matter where momentum is conserved through hydrodynamic interactions.

Finally, in Sec.
VII
and Appendix
C
, we derived the mean first-passage times
\tau_{R,L}(x)
, Eq. (
25
), and splitting probabilities
\Pi_{R,L}(x)
, Eq. (
27
), for this system under different boundary conditions and explored how they depend on the system’s parameters, Figs.
16
and
17
.

Appendix A
Derivation of steady-state probability densities
P_{R,L}^{[i]}(x)

To solve the coupled Fokker-Planck equations,
Eq. (
3
), for the steady-state probability densities
P_{R,L}^{[i]}(x)
, we rewrite Eq. (
3
) as a four-dimensional first-order ODE system for both
i=1
and
i=2
. Introducing
\eta^{[i]}_{R,L}(x)\equiv P^{\prime[i]}_{R,L}(x)
, the steady-state Fokker-Planck equations, i.e. Eq. (
3
) with
\partial_{t}P_{R,L}^{[i]}=0
, can be rewritten as

\mathbf{y}^{\prime}(x)=\mathsf{A}\mathbf{y}(x),

(28)

where
\mathbf{y}^{\prime}
is the derivative of
\mathbf{y}
with respect to
x
, and we have defined

\mathbf{y}(x)=\begin{pmatrix}P_{R}^{[i]}(x)\\
\eta_{R}^{[i]}(x)\\
P_{L}^{[i]}(x)\\
\eta_{L}^{[i]}(x)\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathsf{A}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0&0\\
\frac{\gamma}{D}&\frac{v-f-U^{\prime[i]}}{D}&-\frac{\gamma}{D}&0\\
0&0&0&1\\
-\frac{\gamma}{D}&0&\frac{\gamma}{D}&\frac{-v-f-U^{\prime[i]}}{D}\end{pmatrix}.

(29)

As Eq. (
28
) is linear and homogeneous for both
i=1
and
i=2
, we make the ansatz
P_{R,L}^{[i]}(x)=\mathcal{Z}_{R,L}^{[i]}\exp(\lambda^{[i]}x)
, where the
\lambda^{[i]}
are the eigenvalues of
\mathsf{A}
. After substituting this ansatz into Eq. (
28
), the eigenvalues
\lambda^{[i]}
are found to satisfy a quartic characteristic equation (arising from two coupled quadratic equations),

D^{2}(\lambda^{[i]})^{4}+(\lambda^{[i]})^{3}\left(2Df+2DU^{\prime[i]}\right)+(\lambda^{[i]})^{2}\left(-2\gamma D+f^{2}+2fU^{\prime[i]}+\left(U^{\prime[i]}\right)^{2}-v^{2}\right)-\lambda^{[i]}\left(2\gamma f+2\gamma U^{\prime[i]}\right)=0.

(30)

The solutions
\lambda^{[i]}
to Eq. (
30
) are too long to display conveniently as equations. However, one of the solutions to Eq. (
30
) is
\lambda^{[i]}=0
for both
i\in\{1,2\}
, and so the general solution to the matrix differential equation (
28
) is

P_{R,L}^{[i]}(x)=\mathcal{A}_{R,L}^{[i]}e^{\lambda_{\mathcal{A}}^{[i]}x}+\mathcal{B}_{R,L}^{[i]}e^{\lambda_{\mathcal{B}}^{[i]}x}+\mathcal{C}_{R,L}^{[i]}e^{\lambda_{\mathcal{C}}^{[i]}x}+\mathcal{D}_{R,L}^{[i]},

(31)

where
\lambda_{\mathcal{A}}^{[i]},\lambda_{\mathcal{B}}^{[i]},\lambda_{\mathcal{C}}^{[i]}
are the other three nonzero solutions (for a given
[i]
) to the quartic equation (
30
).

It remains to calculate the 16 coefficients
\mathcal{A}_{R}^{[1]},\mathcal{A}_{L}^{[1]},\mathcal{A}_{R}^{[2]},\dots,\mathcal{D}_{L}^{[2]}
in Eq. (
31
). First, the ratios of the coefficients
\mathcal{A}_{R}^{[i]}/\mathcal{A}_{L}^{[i]}
, representing half of the degrees of freedom, are fixed from substituting Eq. (
31
) into Eq. (
28
) and requiring the resulting prefactor of each linearly independent
\exp(\lambda^{[i]}x)
term to vanish. This results in

\frac{\mathcal{Z}_{R}^{[i]}}{\mathcal{Z}_{L}^{[i]}}=\frac{\gamma}{\gamma+\left(v-f-U^{\prime[i]}\right)\lambda_{\mathcal{Z}}^{[i]}-D(\lambda_{\mathcal{Z}}^{[i]})^{2}},\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{D}_{R}^{[i]}=\mathcal{D}_{L}^{[i]},

(32)

where
\mathcal{Z}\in\{\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B},\mathcal{C}
}. The remaining eight degrees of freedom are fixed through normalisation as well as continuity of the probability densities
P_{R,L}^{[i]}(x)
and currents
J_{R,L}^{[i]}(x)
, Eq. (
5
), for each species across
x=0
and
x=a
. Specifically, the continuity conditions are

\displaystyle P_{R,L}^{[1]}(0)

\displaystyle=P_{R,L}^{[2]}(\ell),

(33a)

\displaystyle J_{R,L}^{[1]}(0)

\displaystyle=J_{R,L}^{[2]}(\ell),

(33b)

\displaystyle P_{R,L}^{[1]}(a)

\displaystyle=P_{R,L}^{[2]}(a),

(33c)

\displaystyle J_{R,L}^{[1]}(a)

\displaystyle=J_{R,L}^{[2]}(a).

(33d)

Finally, we also have the normalisation condition

\int_{0}^{a}\mathrm{d}x\left(P_{R}^{[1]}(x)+P_{L}^{[1]}(x)\right)+\int_{a}^{\ell}\mathrm{d}x\left(P_{R}^{[2]}(x)+P_{L}^{[2]}(x)\right)=1.

(34)

Only 15 of the resulting 16 equations from Eqs. (
32
) and (
33
) are linearly independent. After removing any one of these equations, the steady-state densities
P_{R,L}^{[i]}(x)
for RnT particles moving in a piecewise-linear ratchet potential
U(x)
, Eq. (
1
), can be obtained in closed form by solving the remaining Eqs. (
32
), (
33
) and (
34
) for the coefficients
\mathcal{A}_{R}^{[1]},\mathcal{A}_{L}^{[1]},\mathcal{A}_{R}^{[2]},\dots,\mathcal{D}_{L}^{[2]}
.

Appendix B
Derivation of
D=0
steady-state probability densities
p_{R,L}^{[i]}(x)
and currents
j_{R,L}^{[i]}(x)

Here, we derive the steady-state probability densities
p_{R,L}^{[i]}(x)
and currents
j_{R,L}^{[i]}(x)
for
D=0
. We set
D=0
in Eq. (
3
) so that the coupled Fokker-Planck equations in the absence of diffusion become

\displaystyle\frac{\partial p^{[i]}_{R}(x,t)}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=-\left(v-f-U^{\prime[i]}\right)\frac{\partial p^{[i]}_{R}(x,t)}{\partial x}-\gamma\left(p^{[i]}_{R}(x,t)-p^{[i]}_{L}(x,t)\right),

(35a)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial p^{[i]}_{L}(x,t)}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=-\left(-v-f-U^{\prime[i]}\right)\frac{\partial p^{[i]}_{L}(x,t)}{\partial x}-\gamma\left(p^{[i]}_{L}(x,t)-p^{[i]}_{R}(x,t)\right).

(35b)

The currents for right movers and left movers then become, respectively,

\displaystyle j_{R}^{[i]}(x,t)

\displaystyle=\left(v-f-U^{\prime[i]}\right)p^{[i]}_{R}(x,t),

(36a)

\displaystyle j_{L}^{[i]}(x,t)

\displaystyle=\left(-v-f-U^{\prime[i]}\right)p^{[i]}_{L}(x,t).

(36b)

To solve the coupled Fokker-Planck equations (
35
) in the steady state,
\partial_{t}p_{R,L}^{[i]}=0
, we again rewrite them in a matrix form as a two-dimensional system of first-order ODEs for both
i=1
and
i=2
, i.e.

\hat{\mathbf{y}}^{\prime}(x)=\hat{\mathsf{A}}\hat{\mathbf{y}}(x),

(37)

where

\hat{\mathbf{y}}(x)=\begin{pmatrix}p_{R}^{[i]}(x)\\
p_{L}^{[i]}(x)\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{and}\quad\hat{\mathsf{A}}=\begin{pmatrix}-\frac{\gamma}{v-f-U^{\prime[i]}(x)}&\frac{\gamma}{v-f-U^{\prime[i]}(x)}\\
\frac{\gamma}{-v-f-U^{\prime[i]}(x)}&-\frac{\gamma}{-v-f-U^{\prime[i]}(x)}\end{pmatrix}.

(38)

As Eq. (
37
) is homogenous, we again make the ansatz
p_{R,L}^{[i]}(x)=\hat{\mathcal{Z}}_{R,L}^{[i]}\exp(\hat{\lambda}^{[i]}x)
, where
\hat{\lambda}^{[i]}
are the eigenvalues of
\hat{\mathsf{A}}
. The two eigenvalues are found to be

\hat{\lambda}_{\mathcal{A}}^{[i]}=\frac{2\gamma~(f+U^{\prime[i]})}{(-v+f+U^{\prime[i]})(v+f+U^{\prime[i]})}\quad\text{and}\quad\hat{\lambda}_{\mathcal{B}}^{[i]}=0.

(39)

The general solutions to the matrix ODE (
37
) are then

\displaystyle p_{R,L}^{[1]}(x)

\displaystyle=\hat{\mathcal{A}}_{R,L}^{[1]}e^{\hat{\lambda}_{\mathcal{A}}^{[1]}x}+\hat{\mathcal{B}}_{R,L}^{[1]}+\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{R,L}\delta(x),

(40a)

\displaystyle p_{R,L}^{[2]}(x)

\displaystyle=\hat{\mathcal{A}}_{R,L}^{[2]}e^{\hat{\lambda}_{\mathcal{A}}^{[2]}x}+\hat{\mathcal{B}}_{R,L}^{[2]},

(40b)

where the
\delta(x)
term is due to the possibility of particles becoming trapped at
x=0
in the absence of diffusion. There are 10 coefficients
\hat{\mathcal{A}}_{R}^{[1]},\hat{\mathcal{A}}_{L}^{[1]},\hat{\mathcal{A}}_{R}^{[2]},\dots,\hat{\mathcal{B}}_{L}^{[2]},\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{R},\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{L}
in Eq. (
40
), but only 9 of these need to be fixed at any given time since
\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{R}
and
\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{L}
are both nonzero
only
in the case where the particle is completely confined, i.e.
v-f<U^{\prime[1]}

and

v+f<|U^{\prime[2]}|
. In this case, the solutions are trivially given by
p_{R,L}(x)=\delta(x)/2
. Therefore, to fix the 9 coefficients that are nonzero, we first substitute Eq. (
40
) into the Fokker-Planck equation (
35
), and require the resulting prefactor of each linearly independent
\exp(\hat{\lambda}^{[i]}x)
to vanish, resulting in

\frac{\hat{\mathcal{A}}_{R}^{[i]}}{\hat{\mathcal{A}}_{L}^{[i]}}=\frac{\gamma}{\gamma+(v-f-U^{\prime[i]}(x))\hat{\lambda}_{\mathcal{A}}^{[i]}}\quad\text{and}\quad\hat{\mathcal{B}}_{R}^{[i]}=\hat{\mathcal{B}}_{L}^{[i]},

(41)

and leaving 5 degrees of freedom to fix.

Continuity in the densities
p_{R,L}^{[i]}(x)
is no longer a viable condition to fix the degrees of freedom since the absence of diffusion ceases to smooth out the solutions to Eq. (
37
), resulting in the densities becoming discontinuous at
x=0
and
x=a
. The form of the solutions depends on the relations between the self-propulsion
v
and the slopes of the potential,
U^{\prime[1]}=h/a
and
U^{\prime[2]}=-h/(\ell-a)
, in particular whether or not the particle is confined. For instance, the particle could climb over both slopes of the potential, i.e.
v-f>U^{\prime[1]}

and

v+f>|U^{\prime[2]}|
, only one slope,
v-f>U^{\prime[1]}

or

v+f>|U^{\prime[2]}|
, or be completely confined,
v-f<U^{\prime[1]}

and

v+f<|U^{\prime[2]}|
. At
x=0
, if the particle is confined by the barrier to the left,
v+f<|U^{\prime[2]}|
, then there is a
\delta(x)
contribution to the left-moving density
p_{L}^{[1]}(x)
with prefactor
\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{L}
. A tumbling event from a left mover to a right mover thus results in an instantaneous contribution
\gamma\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{L}
to the right-moving current
j^{[1]}_{R}(x)
at
x=0
. Similarly, if the particle were instead confined to the right,
v-f<U^{\prime[1]}
, then there would be a
\delta(x)
contribution to the right-moving density
p_{R}^{[1]}(x)
with prefactor
\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{R}
, and therefore a contribution
-\gamma\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{R}
to the left-moving current
j^{[1]}_{L}(x)
at
x=0
. Hence, in the case where the particle is confined only to the left,
v+f<|U^{\prime[2]}|

and

v-f>U^{\prime[1]}
, the continuity conditions are

\displaystyle j^{[1]}_{R}(0)

\displaystyle=j^{[2]}_{R}(\ell)+\gamma\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{L},

(42a)

\displaystyle j_{R}^{[1]}(a)

\displaystyle=j_{R}^{[2]}(a),

(42b)

\displaystyle p_{L}^{[1]}(a)

\displaystyle=0,

(42c)

\displaystyle p_{L}^{[2]}(a)

\displaystyle=0,

(42d)

where, since the particle cannot cross the left barrier, there is no contribution to the left-moving probability density
p_{L}(x)
at
x=a
. Similarly, in the case where the particle is instead confined only to the right,
v+f>|U^{\prime[2]}|

and

v-f<U^{\prime[1]}
, the continuity conditions are

\displaystyle j^{[1]}_{L}(0)

\displaystyle=j^{[2]}_{L}(\ell)-\gamma\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{R},

(43a)

\displaystyle j_{L}^{[1]}(a)

\displaystyle=j_{L}^{[2]}(a),

(43b)

\displaystyle p_{R}^{[1]}(a)

\displaystyle=0,

(43c)

\displaystyle p_{R}^{[2]}(a)

\displaystyle=0.

(43d)

Finally, if the particle can overcome both barriers,
v-f>U^{\prime[1]}

and

v+f>|U^{\prime[2]}|
, then the continuity conditions are

\displaystyle j^{[1]}_{R,L}(0)

\displaystyle=j^{[2]}_{R,L}(\ell),

(44a)

\displaystyle j_{R,L}^{[1]}(a)

\displaystyle=j_{R,L}^{[2]}(a).

(44b)

The remaining degree of freedom is then fixed through the normalisation condition,

\int_{0}^{a}\mathrm{d}x\left(p_{R}^{[1]}(x)+p_{L}^{[1]}(x)\right)+\int_{a}^{\ell}\mathrm{d}x\left(p_{R}^{[2]}(x)+p_{L}^{[2]}(x)\right)=1.

(45)

The overall current
j=j_{R}(x)+j_{L}(x)
has a closed-form expression that is compact enough to be written down. In the case where the particle is unconfined and therefore able to cross both barriers,
v+f>|U^{\prime[2]}|

and

v-f>U^{\prime[1]}
, the current is given by

j_{\leftrightarrow}=\frac{2\gamma(af+h)^{2}(af-f\ell+h)^{2}\left(e^{\kappa+\delta\ell}-e^{\delta a}\right)}{\theta(e^{\delta a}-e^{\kappa+\delta\ell})+h^{2}\ell^{2}v^{2}(e^{\kappa}-1)(e^{\delta a}-e^{\delta\ell})},

(46)

where

\displaystyle\delta

\displaystyle=\frac{2\gamma(a-\ell)(af-f\ell+h)}{(af-f\ell+h)^{2}-v^{2}(a-\ell)^{2}},

(47a)

\displaystyle\kappa

\displaystyle=\frac{2\gamma a^{2}(af+h)}{(af+h)^{2}-a^{2}v^{2}},

(47b)

\displaystyle\theta

\displaystyle=2\gamma\ell(af+h)(af-f\ell+h)(a(af+2h)-\ell(af+h)).

(47c)

However, when the particle is confined only to the left,
v+f<|U^{\prime[2]}|

and

v-f>U^{\prime[1]}
, the current is given by

j_{\not\leftarrow}=\frac{2\gamma(af+h)^{2}(af-f\ell+h)^{2}}{\tilde{\theta}_{1}e^{-\kappa}+\tilde{\theta}_{2}e^{\delta(\ell-a)}+\tilde{\theta}_{3}-\theta},

(48)

where

\displaystyle\tilde{\theta}_{1}

\displaystyle=(af-f\ell+h)^{2}(a(f+v)+h)^{2},

(49a)

\displaystyle\tilde{\theta}_{2}

\displaystyle=-(af+h)^{2}(h+(a-\ell)(f+v))^{2},

(49b)

\displaystyle\tilde{\theta}_{3}

\displaystyle=h\ell^{2}v^{2}(2af+h)-2h\ell v(af+h)(af+h-f\ell)-2h\ell av^{2}(af+h).

(49c)

Finally, the overall current
j_{\not\rightarrow}
in the case where the particle is confined only to the right,
v+f>|U^{\prime[2]}|

and

v-f<U^{\prime[1]}
, can be found by inverting the overall sign in Eq. (
48
) and replacing
a\rightarrow\ell-a
in Eqs. (
48
) and (
49
) above.

Appendix C

C.1
Mean first-passage times
\tau_{R,L}^{[i]}(x)

As the coupled ODEs for the mean first-passage time
\tau_{R,L}^{[i]}(x)
, Eq. (
24
), are similar to the coupled Fokker-Planck equations (
3
) for the steady-state probability density
P_{R,L}^{[i]}(x)
, the procedure for solving Eq. (
24
) follows similarly to solving Eq. (
3
) in Appendix
A
. However, Eq. (
24
) is nonhomogeneous and so we must also find a particular solution satisfying the equation. Again, Eq. (
24
) can be rewritten as a four-dimensional system of first-order ODEs for both
i=1
and
i=2
. As in the previous appendices, introducing
\tilde{\eta}^{[i]}_{R,L}(x)=\tau_{R,L}^{\prime[i]}(x)
allows
Eq. (
24
) to be written as

\tilde{\mathbf{y}}^{\prime}(x)=\tilde{\mathsf{A}}\tilde{\mathbf{y}}(x)+\tilde{\mathbf{b}}

(50)

where

\tilde{\mathbf{y}}(x)=\begin{pmatrix}\tau_{R}^{[i]}(x)\\
\tilde{\eta}_{R}^{[i]}(x)\\
\tau_{L}^{[i]}(x)\\
\tilde{\eta}_{L}^{[i]}(x)\end{pmatrix},\quad\tilde{\mathsf{A}}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0&0\\
\frac{\gamma}{D}&\frac{-v+f+U^{\prime[i]}}{D}&-\frac{\gamma}{D}&0\\
0&0&0&1\\
-\frac{\gamma}{D}&0&\frac{\gamma}{D}&\frac{v+f+U^{\prime[i]}}{D}\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{and}\quad\tilde{\mathbf{b}}=\begin{pmatrix}-1\\
0\\
-1\\
0\end{pmatrix}.

(51)

First, the solution to the homogeneous equation is found by substituting the ansatz
\tau_{R,L}^{[i]}(x)=\tilde{\mathcal{Z}}_{R,L}^{[i]}\exp(\tilde{\lambda}^{[i]}x)
into Eq. (
50
) to obtain a quartic equation for the eigenvalues
\tilde{\lambda}^{[i]}
,

D^{2}(\tilde{\lambda}^{[i]})^{4}-(\tilde{\lambda}^{[i]})^{3}\left(2Df+2DU^{\prime[i]}\right)+(\tilde{\lambda}^{[i]})^{2}\left(-2\gamma D+f^{2}+2fU^{\prime[i]}+\left(U^{\prime[i]}\right)^{2}-v^{2}\right)+\tilde{\lambda}^{[i]}\left(2\gamma f+2\gamma U^{\prime[i]}\right)=0,

which differs to Eq. (
30
) only in the sign of the eigenvalues, i.e.
\lambda^{[i]}\rightarrow-\tilde{\lambda}^{[i]}
. As in Appendix
A
, one eigenvalue for each of
i=1
and
i=2
vanishes and so the solution to the homogeneous part of Eq. (
50
) has the same form as for
P_{R,L}^{[i]}(x)
in Eq. (
31
), i.e.

\tau_{R,L}^{[i]}\Big|_{\text{H}}(x)=\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{R,L}^{[i]}e^{\tilde{\lambda}_{\mathcal{A}}^{[i]}x}+\tilde{\mathcal{B}}_{R,L}^{[i]}e^{\tilde{\lambda}_{\mathcal{B}}^{[i]}x}+\tilde{\mathcal{C}}_{R,L}^{[i]}e^{\tilde{\lambda}_{\mathcal{C}}^{[i]}x}+\tilde{\mathcal{D}}_{R,L}^{[i]}.

(53)

Next, it is easily verified that the particular solution satisfies

\tau_{R,L}^{[i]}\Big|_{\text{P}}(x)=\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{R,L}^{[i]}x+\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{R,L}^{[i]},

(54)

and after substituting Eq. (
54
) into Eq. (
50
), we obtain

\displaystyle(v-f-U^{\prime[i]})\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{R}^{[i]}-\gamma(\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{R}^{[i]}x+\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{R}^{[i]}-\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{L}^{[i]}x-\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{L}^{[i]})

\displaystyle=0,

(55a)

\displaystyle(-v-f-U^{\prime[i]})\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{L}^{[i]}-\gamma({\mathcal{E}}_{L}^{[i]}x+\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{L}^{[i]}-\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{R}^{[i]}x-\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{R}^{[i]})

\displaystyle=0.

(55b)

As Eqs. (
55a
) and (
55b
) are valid for all
x\in[0,\ell]
, then we have that

\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{R}^{[i]}=\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{L}^{[i]}

(56)

and

\displaystyle(v-f-U^{\prime[i]})\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{R}^{[i]}-\gamma(\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{R}^{[i]}-\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{L}^{[i]})

\displaystyle=0,

(57a)

\displaystyle(-v-f-U^{\prime[i]})\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{L}^{[i]}-\gamma({\mathcal{F}}_{L}^{[i]}-\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{R}^{[i]})

\displaystyle=0.

(57b)

Adding Eqs. (
57a
) and (
57b
), then combining with Eq. (
56
), yields

\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{R}^{[i]}=\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{L}^{[i]}=\frac{1}{f+U^{\prime[i]}}.

(58)

Absorbing the constant
\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{R,L}^{[i]}
into
\tilde{\mathcal{D}}_{R,L}^{[i]}
, the full solution to Eq. (
50
) is therefore

\tau_{R,L}^{[i]}(x)=\tau_{R,L}^{[i]}\Big|_{\text{H}}(x)+\tau_{R,L}^{[i]}\Big|_{\text{P}}(x)=\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{R,L}^{[i]}e^{\tilde{\lambda}_{\mathcal{A}}^{[i]}x}+\tilde{\mathcal{B}}_{R,L}^{[i]}e^{\tilde{\lambda}_{\mathcal{B}}^{[i]}x}+\tilde{\mathcal{C}}_{R,L}^{[i]}e^{\tilde{\lambda}_{\mathcal{C}}^{[i]}x}+\tilde{\mathcal{D}}_{R,L}^{[i]}+\frac{x}{f+U^{\prime[i]}}.

(59)

As for the probability densities
P_{R,L}^{[i]}(x)
in Appendix
A
, the ratios
\tilde{\mathcal{Z}}_{R}^{[i]}/\tilde{\mathcal{Z}}_{L}^{[i]}
, where
\tilde{\mathcal{Z}}\in\{\tilde{\mathcal{A}},\tilde{\mathcal{B}},\tilde{\mathcal{C}},\tilde{\mathcal{D}}\}
, are fixed by requiring each linearly independent term in Eq. (
59
) to satisfy Eq. (
50
), resulting in

\frac{\tilde{\mathcal{Z}}_{R}^{[i]}}{\tilde{\mathcal{Z}}_{L}^{[i]}}=\frac{\gamma}{\gamma-\left(v-f-U^{\prime[i]}\right)\tilde{\lambda}_{\mathcal{Z}}^{[i]}-D(\tilde{\lambda}_{\mathcal{Z}}^{[i]})^{2}},\quad\text{and}\quad\tilde{\mathcal{D}}_{R}^{[i]}=\tilde{\mathcal{D}}_{L}^{[i]}.

(60)

The remaining degrees of freedom are then fixed from the boundary conditions and continuity conditions,

\displaystyle\tau_{R,L}^{[1]}(0)

\displaystyle=0,

(61a)

\displaystyle\tau_{R,L}^{[1]}(a)

\displaystyle=\tau_{R,L}^{[2]}(a),

(61b)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\tau_{R,L}^{[1]}(x)}{\mathrm{d}x}\Big|_{x=a}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{d}\tau_{R,L}^{[2]}(x)}{\mathrm{d}x}\Big|_{x=a},

(61c)

and

\text{(i)}\quad\tau_{R,L}^{[2]}(\ell)=0,\quad\text{or}\quad\text{(ii)}\quad\frac{\mathrm{d}\tau_{R,L}^{[2]}(x)}{\mathrm{d}x}\Big|_{x=\ell}=0,

(61d)

depending on whether the system has (i) an absorbing boundary or (ii) a reflecting boundary at
x=\ell
(see, for instance, Ref.
Ahmad et al. 2022
).

C.2
Splitting probabilities
\Pi_{R,L}^{[i]}(x)

To derive the splitting probabilities
\Pi_{R,L}^{[i]}(x)
, we define
\check{\eta}^{[i]}_{R,L}(x)=\Pi_{R,L}^{\prime[i]}(x)
and
\check{\mathbf{y}}(x)=(\pi_{R}^{[i]}(x),\check{\eta}_{R}^{[i]}(x),\pi_{L}^{[i]}(x),\check{\eta}_{L}^{[i]}(x))
such that the governing equations (
26
) for the splitting probabilities
\Pi_{R,L}^{[i]}(x)
can be written as a four-dimensional system of first-order ODEs,
\check{\mathbf{y}}^{\prime}(x)=\tilde{\mathsf{A}}\breve{\mathbf{y}}(x)
, where
\tilde{\mathsf{A}}
is the same operator acting on the mean first-passage times
\tau_{R,L}^{[i]}(x)
above, Eq. (
51
). Thus, the splitting probabilities
\Pi_{R,L}^{[i]}(x)
obey the same (backward) equations as for that of the mean first-passage times
\tau_{R,L}^{[i]}(x)
, Eqs. (
50
)-(
51
), aside from being homogeneous, i.e.
\tilde{\mathbf{b}}=\mathbf{0}
. Hence, the splitting probabilities have the same form of general solution as for the homogeneous part to the mean first-passage times, Eq. (
53
), but with different coefficients, i.e.

\Pi_{R,L}^{[i]}(x)=\check{\mathcal{A}}_{R,L}^{[i]}e^{\tilde{\lambda}_{\mathcal{A}}^{[i]}x}+\check{\mathcal{B}}_{R,L}^{[i]}e^{\tilde{\lambda}_{\mathcal{B}}^{[i]}x}+\check{\mathcal{C}}_{R,L}^{[i]}e^{\tilde{\lambda}_{\mathcal{C}}^{[i]}x}+\check{\mathcal{D}}_{R,L}^{[i]},

(62)

where the eigenvalues
\tilde{\lambda}^{[i]}
are the same as those obtained through solving Eq. (
52
) and the ratios
\check{\mathcal{Z}}_{R}^{[i]}/\check{\mathcal{Z}}_{L}^{[i]}
, where
\check{\mathcal{Z}}\in\{\check{\mathcal{A}},\check{\mathcal{B}},\check{\mathcal{C}},\check{\mathcal{D}}\}
, also satisfy equations analogous to that of the mean first-passage times
\tau_{R,L}^{[i]}(x)
, i.e. Eq. (
60
). The remaining degrees of freedom in the general solution for the splitting probabilities
\Pi_{R,L}^{[i]}(x)
, Eq. (
62
), are fixed from the boundary conditions and continuity conditions,

\displaystyle\Pi_{R,L}^{[1]}(0)

\displaystyle=1,

(63a)

\displaystyle\Pi_{R,L}^{[2]}(\ell)

\displaystyle=0,

(63b)

\displaystyle\Pi_{R,L}^{[1]}(a)

\displaystyle=\Pi_{R,L}^{[2]}(a),

(63c)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\Pi_{R,L}^{[1]}(x)}{\mathrm{d}x}\Big|_{x=a}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{d}\Pi_{R,L}^{[2]}(x)}{\mathrm{d}x}\Big|_{x=a},

(63d)

recalling that we define
\Pi_{R/L}(x)
to be the probabilities for a right/left mover to exit at the
left
-hand boundary of the system at
x=0
.

C.3
Conditional splitting probabilities
\pi_{X|Y}^{[i]}(x)

The splitting probabilities
\Pi_{R,L}^{[i]}(x)
calculated above can be generalised to be conditioned on the particle’s self-propulsion state as it exits the interval. Following the calculation in Ref.
Cocconi et al. 2022
, we define
\pi_{X|Y}(x_{0})
as the “conditional splitting probability” for a particle initialised in state
Y\in\{R,L\}
at
x=x_{0}
to leave through the left-hand boundary at
x=0
in the self-propulsion state
X\in\{R,L\}
. Hence, there are four conditional splitting probabilities that exist for each boundary, obtained from every possible permutation of
X,Y\in\{R,L\}
. Introducing
\breve{\eta}^{[i]}_{X|Y}(x)=\pi_{X|Y}^{\prime[i]}(x)
, the conditional splitting probabilities satisfy
\breve{\mathbf{y}}^{\prime}_{R,L}(x)=\tilde{\mathsf{A}}\breve{\mathbf{y}}_{R,L}(x)
where
\tilde{\mathsf{A}}
is the same operator, defined in Eq. (
51
), acting on the “unconditional” splitting probabilities
\Pi_{R,L}^{[i]}(x)
and mean first-passage times
\tau_{R,L}^{[i]}(x)
above, and
\breve{\mathbf{y}}_{R,L}(x)=(\pi_{R,L|R}^{[i]}(x),\breve{\eta}_{R,L|R}^{[i]}(x),\pi_{R,L|L}^{[i]}(x),\breve{\eta}_{R,L|L}^{[i]}(x))
. Hence, the conditional splitting probabilities satisfy two different sets of coupled ODEs, one for each conditional exit state. As these ODEs are the same as for that of the unconditional splitting probabilities, Eq. (
26
), they thus have the same general solution given in Eq. (
62
), i.e.

\pi_{X|Y}^{[i]}(x)=\breve{\mathcal{A}}_{X|Y}^{[i]}e^{\tilde{\lambda}_{\mathcal{A}}^{[i]}x}+\breve{\mathcal{B}}_{X|Y}^{[i]}e^{\tilde{\lambda}_{\mathcal{B}}^{[i]}x}+\breve{\mathcal{C}}_{X|Y}^{[i]}e^{\tilde{\lambda}_{\mathcal{C}}^{[i]}x}+\breve{\mathcal{D}}_{X|Y}^{[i]},

(64)

where
X,Y\in\{R,L\}
and the eigenvalues
\tilde{\lambda}^{[i]}
are found through solving Eq. (
52
). The coefficients
\breve{\mathcal{A}}_{X|Y}^{[1]},\breve{\mathcal{A}}_{X|Y}^{[2]},\dots,\breve{\mathcal{D}}_{X|Y}^{[2]}
are fixed in the same manner as for the mean first-passage times
\tau_{R,L}^{[i]}(x)
and splitting probabilities
\Pi_{R,L}^{[i]}(x)
above, by requiring each linearly independent term to satisfy the ODEs before applying boundary conditions and continuity conditions. The boundary conditions of the ODEs are

\displaystyle\pi^{[1]}_{R|R}(0)=\pi^{[1]}_{L|L}(0)

\displaystyle=1,

(65a)

\displaystyle\pi^{[1]}_{R|L}(0)=\pi^{[1]}_{L|R}(0)

\displaystyle=0,

(65b)

\displaystyle\pi^{[2]}_{R|R}(\ell)=\pi^{[2]}_{L|L}(\ell)

\displaystyle=0,

(65c)

\displaystyle\pi^{[2]}_{R|L}(\ell)=\pi^{[2]}_{L|R}(\ell)

\displaystyle=0,

(65d)

and the continuity conditions are analogous to that of the unconditional splitting probabilities in Eq. (
63
), i.e.

\displaystyle\pi^{[1]}_{R,L|R}(a)

\displaystyle=\pi^{[2]}_{R,L|R}(a),

(66a)

\displaystyle\pi^{[1]}_{R,L|L}(a)

\displaystyle=\pi^{[2]}_{R,L|L}(a),

(66b)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\pi_{R,L|R}^{[1]}(x)}{\mathrm{d}x}\Big|_{x=a}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{d}\pi_{R,L|R}^{[2]}(x)}{\mathrm{d}x}\Big|_{x=a},

(66c)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\pi_{R,L|L}^{[1]}(x)}{\mathrm{d}x}\Big|_{x=a}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{d}\pi_{R,L|L}^{[2]}(x)}{\mathrm{d}x}\Big|_{x=a}.

(66d)
