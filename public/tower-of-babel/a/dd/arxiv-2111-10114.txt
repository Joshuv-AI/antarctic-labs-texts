Tautological bases of CoHA modules
Hans Franzen, Sergey Mozgovoy

Abstract
. Given a quiver, we consider its cohomological Hall algebra (CoHA) as well as CoHA modules built of cohomology groups of non-commutative Hilbert schemes. We investigate cell decompositions of non-commutative Hilbert schemes and the corresponding (non-canonical) bases of CoHA modules. We construct canonical bases of CoHA modules, which consist of products of Chern classes of tautological vector bundles over non-commutative Hilbert schemes. This result generalizes classical results for Grassmannians and (partial) flag varieties.



1.
Introduction

Cohomological Hall algebras (abbreviated as CoHAs) were introduced in
[
7
]
as a mathematical incarnation of the algebras of BPS states proposed in string theory.
The definition of CoHAs goes through the same lines as the definition of conventional Hall algebras
[
11
]
.
The benefit of the former is that cohomology groups used in the definition of CoHAs are smaller and easier to describe explicitly than the spaces of functions on the groupoids of objects used in the definition of conventional Hall algebras.

In particular, for the category of representations of a quiver
Q
,
the corresponding CoHA
\mathcal{H}
was described explicitly in
[
7
]
as a shuffle algebra.
More precisely, let
Q
have the set of vertices
I
.
We can represent
\mathcal{H}
as a graded vector space

\mathcal{H}=\bigoplus_{\mathbf{d}\in\mathbb{N}^{I}}\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{d}},\qquad\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{d}}=\bigotimes_{i\in I}\Lambda_{\mathbf{d}_{i}}\simeq\mathbb{Q}[x_{i,k}\col i\in I,1\leq k\leq\mathbf{d}_{i}]^{\Sigma_{\mathbf{d}}},

where
\Lambda_{\mathbf{d}_{i}}
denotes the ring of symmetric polynomials in
\mathbf{d}_{i}
variables and
\Sigma_{\mathbf{d}}=\prod_{i\in I}\Sigma_{\mathbf{d}_{i}}
is the product of symmetric groups.
The shuffle product on
\mathcal{H}
is encoded by the Euler form of
Q
.

The next natural question is a study of modules over the CoHA
\mathcal{H}

[
12
,
2
,
3
,
4
,
13
]
.
A structure of an
\mathcal{H}
-module can be introduced
on the cohomology of the moduli spaces of stable framed representations of
Q
, similar to the action of quantum affine algebras on (equivariant) cohomology of Nakajima quiver varieties
[
8
,
9
]
.
More precisely, given vectors
\mathbf{f}\in\mathbb{N}^{I}

and
\mathbf{d}\in\mathbb{N}^{I}
(called
framing
and
dimension
vectors respectively),
we consider the moduli space
\mathcal{N}_{\mathbf{d},\mathbf{f}}

that parametrizes pairs
(M,s)
, where
M
is a
Q
-representation having dimension vector
\mathbf{d}
and
s\in\bigoplus_{i\in I}\operatorname{Hom}(\mathbb{C}^{\mathbf{f}_{i}},M_{i})
is such that its image generates
M
as a
Q
-representation.
This moduli space is called a
non-commutative Hilbert scheme
.
For example, for the quiver having one vertex and no loops and natural numbers
d\leq w
,
the moduli space
\mathcal{N}_{d,w}
can be identified with the Grassmannian
\operatorname{Gr}(w-d,w)
.
Similarly, for the quiver
1\to 2\to\dots\to n

and vectors
\mathbf{f}=(w,0,\dots,0)
,
\mathbf{d}=(\mathbf{d}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{d}_{n})
with
w\geq\mathbf{d}_{1}\geq\dots\geq\mathbf{d}_{n}\geq 0
,
the moduli space
\mathcal{N}_{\mathbf{d},\mathbf{f}}
can be identified with the space of (partial) flags.

The graded vector space

\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{f}}=\bigoplus_{\mathbf{d}\in\mathbb{N}^{I}}H^{*}(\mathcal{N}_{\mathbf{d},\mathbf{f}})

can be naturally equipped with the structure of a module over the CoHA
\mathcal{H}

[
12
,
2
]
.
In
[
4
,
2
]
one constructed an explicit epimorphism
\mathcal{H}\twoheadrightarrow\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{f}}

between
\mathcal{H}
-modules and determined its kernel in terms of the shuffle product on
\mathcal{H}
.
This gives, in principle, a description of cohomologies
H^{*}(\mathcal{N}_{\mathbf{d},\mathbf{f}})
of non-commutative Hilbert schemes.

The epimorphism
\mathcal{H}\twoheadrightarrow\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{f}}

is given for the degree
\mathbf{d}
components by

\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{d}}\to H^{*}(\mathcal{N}_{\mathbf{d},\mathbf{f}}),\qquad\prod_{{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in I\\
1\leq k\leq\mathbf{d}_{i}\end{subarray}}}e_{i,k}^{n_{i,k}}\mapsto\prod_{{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in I\\
1\leq k\leq\mathbf{d}_{i}\end{subarray}}}c_{k}(\mathcal{U}_{i})^{n_{i,k}},

where
e_{i,k}\in\Lambda_{\mathbf{d}_{i}}
is the
k
-th elementary symmetric polynomial and
c_{k}(\mathcal{U}_{i})
is the
k
-th Chern class of the tautological vector bundle
\mathcal{U}_{i}
over
\mathcal{N}_{\mathbf{d},\mathbf{f}}
at the vertex
i\in I
.
This implies that for appropriate choices of the powers
(n_{i,k})
we can obtain a basis of
H^{*}(\mathcal{N}_{\mathbf{d},\mathbf{f}})
.

On the other hand, it was proved in
[
10
,
1
]

that non-commutative Hilbert schemes
\mathcal{N}_{\mathbf{d},\mathbf{f}}
possess a cell decomposition, parametrized by subtrees of the tree of paths
\mathcal{P}^{\mathbf{f}}
in the framed quiver
Q^{\mathbf{f}}
.
While the set of parameters (subtrees of the tree of paths) is independent of any choices, the corresponding cells (and the classes of their closures) generally depend on a (non-canonical) choice of a total order on the tree of paths.
Taking duals of the cycles of cell closures, we obtain a (non-canonical) basis of
H^{*}(\mathcal{N}_{\mathbf{d},\mathbf{f}})
.

In this paper we relate the above two approaches to cohomology of non-commutative Hilbert schemes and,
as a result,
we describe a canonical basis of
H^{*}(\mathcal{N}_{\mathbf{d},\mathbf{f}})
in terms of the Chern classes of tautological vector bundles over
\mathcal{N}_{\mathbf{d},\mathbf{f}}
.
There exists a bijection between the set of subtrees of
\mathcal{P}^{\mathbf{f}}
and the set of certain multi-partitions
[
1
, §8]
,
which is obtained by applying a modification of a depth-first search algorithm for plane trees.
This bijection
plays a fundamental role in our approach, as we parametrize products of Chern classes of tautological bundles by multi-partitions and relate them to the classes of cell closures parametrized by subtrees of
\mathcal{P}^{\mathbf{f}}
.
Our main result is (see Theorem
6.4
)

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\mathcal{S}^{\mathbf{f}}(\mathbf{d})
denote the set of multi-partitions

\lambda=(\lambda^{(i)})_{i\in I},\qquad\lambda^{(i)}=(\lambda^{(i)}_{1}\geq\dots\geq\lambda^{(i)}_{\mathbf{d}_{i}}\geq 0),\qquad i\in I,

satisfying condition (
22
).
Then the classes

\prod_{i\in I}\prod_{k\geq 1}c_{k}(\mathcal{U}_{i})^{\lambda^{(i)}_{k}-\lambda^{(i)}_{k+1}}\in H^{2\left\lvert\lambda\right\rvert}(\mathcal{N}_{\mathbf{d},\mathbf{f}}),\qquad\lambda\in\mathcal{S}^{\mathbf{f}}(\mathbf{d}),

form a basis of
H^{*}(\mathcal{N}_{\mathbf{d},\mathbf{f}})
,
where
\left\lvert\lambda\right\rvert=\sum_{i\in I}\left\lvert\lambda^{(i)}\right\rvert
.

2.
Cell decomposition of non-commutative Hilbert schemes

2.1.
Non-commutative Hilbert schemes

Let
Q
be a quiver with the set of vertices
I=Q_{0}
and the set of arrows
Q_{1}
.
The bilinear form
\chi=\chi_{Q}\colon\mathbb{Z}^{I}\times\mathbb{Z}^{I}\to\mathbb{Z}
defined by

(1)

\chi(\mathbf{d},\mathbf{e})=\sum_{i\in I}\mathbf{d}_{i}\mathbf{e}_{i}-\sum_{(a\colon i\to j)\in Q_{1}}\mathbf{d}_{i}\mathbf{e}_{j}

is called the
Euler form
of
Q
.
Given a vector
\mathbf{f}\in\mathbb{N}^{I}
, called a
framing vector
,
we define a new (framed) quiver
Q^{\mathbf{f}}
by adding a new vertex
\infty
and
\mathbf{f}_{i}
arrows
\infty\to i
, for all
i\in I
.
We define a
framed representation

M
to be a representation of
Q^{\mathbf{f}}
such that
\dim M_{\infty}=1
.
We call it
stable
if
M
is generated, as a
Q^{\mathbf{f}}
-representation, by the subspace
M_{\infty}
.

For any dimension vector
\mathbf{d}\in\mathbb{N}^{I}
,
we define

(2)

\displaystyle R(Q,\mathbf{d})

\displaystyle=\bigoplus_{(a\colon i\to j)\in Q_{1}}\operatorname{Hom}(\mathbb{C}^{\mathbf{d}_{i}},\mathbb{C}^{\mathbf{d}_{j}}),

(3)

\displaystyle R^{\mathbf{f}}(Q,\mathbf{d})

\displaystyle=R(Q,\mathbf{d})\oplus\bigoplus_{i\in I}\operatorname{Hom}(\mathbb{C}^{\mathbf{f}_{i}},\mathbb{C}^{\mathbf{d}_{i}}).

An element
M\in R^{\mathbf{f}}(Q,\mathbf{d})
can be interpreted as a framed representation.
Let
R^{\mathbf{f},\mathrm{st}}(Q,\mathbf{d})\subset R^{\mathbf{f}}(Q,\mathbf{d})
be the open subspace of stable framed representations.
The space
R^{\mathbf{f}}(Q,\mathbf{d})
is equipped with the action of the group
G_{\mathbf{d}}=\prod_{i\in I}\operatorname{GL}_{\mathbf{d}_{i}}(\mathbb{C})
.
The induced action of
G_{\mathbf{d}}
on

R^{\mathbf{f},\mathrm{st}}(Q,\mathbf{d})
is free and there exists a smooth algebraic variety

(4)

\mathcal{N}_{\mathbf{d}}=\mathcal{N}_{\mathbf{d},\mathbf{f}}=R^{\mathbf{f},\mathrm{st}}(Q,\mathbf{d})/G_{\mathbf{d}}

parameterizing stable framed representations up to isomorphism,
called the
non-commutative Hilbert scheme
.
If non-empty, it has dimension

(5)

\dim\mathcal{N}_{\mathbf{d}}=\mathbf{f}\cdot\mathbf{d}-\chi(\mathbf{d},\mathbf{d}).

Every representation
M\in\mathcal{N}_{\mathbf{d}}
is equipped with a nonzero element
m_{*}\in M_{\infty}
that generates
M
as a representation of
Q^{\mathbf{f}}
.
Let
A=\mathbb{C}Q^{\mathbf{f}}
be the path algebra of the quiver
Q^{\mathbf{f}}
and let
P=Ae_{\infty}
be the projective
A
-module
corresponding to the idempotent

e_{\infty}\in A
(the trivial path at
\infty
).
For any
M\in\mathcal{N}_{\mathbf{d}}
, we have an epimorphism

P\to M
,
u\mapsto um_{*}
.
The moduli space
\mathcal{N}_{\mathbf{d}}
parametrizes such epimorphisms up to an automorphism of
M
.

The projective module
P=Ae_{\infty}
has a basis consisting of paths in
Q^{\mathbf{f}}
that start at
\infty
.
This set of paths has a tree structure which will be important in our analysis of stratifications of the moduli space
\mathcal{N}_{\mathbf{d}}
.
Therefore we will discuss these combinatorial structures in more detail in the next sections.

2.2.
Posets and trees

Let
(\mathcal{P},\preceq)
be a partially ordered set (poset).
We will write
u\prec v
if
u\preceq v
and
u\neq v
, for
u,v\in\mathcal{P}
.
For any
v\in\mathcal{P}
, we define

(6)

\mathcal{P}_{\preceq v}=\left\{\left.u\in\mathcal{P}\ \right|u\preceq v\right\}\qquad\mathcal{P}_{\prec v}=\left\{\left.u\in\mathcal{P}\ \right|u\prec v\right\}.

The elements of
\mathcal{P}_{\prec v}
are called
predecessors
of
v
.
A subset
S\subset\mathcal{P}
is called a
lower set
if
\mathcal{P}_{\preceq v}\subset S
, for all
v\in S
.

We define a
(rooted) tree
to be a poset
(\mathcal{P},\preceq)
such that

(1)

There is a unique minimal element
*\in\mathcal{P}
, called
the root
.

(2)

For any
v\in\mathcal{P}\backslash\left\{*\right\}
,
the poset
\mathcal{P}_{\prec v}
is a finite chain (totally ordered set).
Its unique maximal element is denoted by
p(v)
, called the
parent
of
v
.

We define a
subtree
of a tree
\mathcal{P}
to be a non-empty lower set
S\subset\mathcal{P}
.
In what follows we will consider only finite subtrees, unless otherwise stated.
For any subtree
S\subset\mathcal{P}
,
we define its
critical set
to be

(7)

C(S)=\min\nolimits_{\preceq}(\mathcal{P}\backslash S)=\left\{\left.v\in\mathcal{P}\backslash S\ \right|u\prec v\implies u\in S\right\}.

Note that if
v\in C(S)
, then
S\cup\left\{v\right\}
is again a tree.

2.3.
Path poset

For any path
u
in
Q^{\mathbf{f}}
, we denote by
s(u)
and
t(u)
its source and target vertices, respectively.
Let
\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{P}^{\mathbf{f}}
be the set of paths in
Q^{\mathbf{f}}
that start at
\infty
.
It is a basis of the module
P=Ae_{\infty}
considered earlier.
We define the partial order on
\mathcal{P}
given by

(8)

u\preceq v\quad\text{ if }\quad v=wu

for some path
w
with
s(w)=t(u)
.
The poset
\mathcal{P}
is a tree with the root
*=e_{\infty}
.
For any non-trivial path
u=a_{n}\dots a_{1}\in\mathcal{P}
(where
a_{i}
are arrows with
s(a_{i})=t(a_{i-1})
, for
1<i\leq n
), the parent of
u
is
a_{n-1}\dots a_{1}\in\mathcal{P}
.

For any (finite) subset
S\subset\mathcal{P}
, we define its dimension vector (note that we omit the vertex
\infty
here)

(9)

\operatorname{\mathbf{dim}}S=(\#S_{i})_{i\in I},\qquad S_{i}=\left\{\left.u\in S\ \right|t(u)=i\right\}.

For any
\mathbf{d}\in\mathbb{N}^{I}
, let
\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{d})
denote the set of trees
S\subset\mathcal{P}
with
\operatorname{\mathbf{dim}}S=\mathbf{d}
.
This set is finite.
Indeed, for any
S\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{d})
and
v\in S
, the length of the path
v
is
\leq\#S-1=\left\lvert\mathbf{d}\right\rvert=\sum_{i\in I}\mathbf{d}_{i}
and the number of paths in
\mathcal{P}
having a given length is finite.

2.4.
Properties of stable framed representations

Recall that every
M\in\mathcal{N}_{\mathbf{d}}
is equipped with a vector
m_{*}\in M_{\infty}
that generates
M
as a
Q^{\mathbf{f}}
-representation.
For any path
u\in\mathcal{P}
, we define

(10)

m_{u}=um_{*}\in M_{t(u)}.

These vectors generate
M
as a vector space.
For any subset
S\subset\mathcal{P}
, we define the subspace

(11)

M_{S}=\left\langle m_{u}\col u\in S\right\rangle\subset M.

Note that if
(m_{u})_{u\in S}
is a basis of
M
, then

\operatorname{\mathbf{dim}}S=\mathbf{d}
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
S\subset\mathcal{P}
be a tree such that
m_{v}\in M_{S}
for all
v\in C(S)
. Then
M_{S}=M
.

Proof.

It is enough to show that
M_{S}\subset M
is a subrepresentation.
Let
u\in S
and
a
be an arrow with
s(a)=t(u)
.
Then
au\in S
or
au\in C(S)
.
If
au\in S
, then
am_{u}=m_{au}\in M_{S}
by the definition of
M_{S}
.
If
au\in C(S)
, then
am_{u}=m_{au}\in M_{S}
by assumption.
∎

Corollary 2.2
.

Let
S\subset\mathcal{P}
be a tree, such that
(m_{u})_{u\in S}
are linearly independent.
Then there exists a tree
S\subset S^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{P}

such that
(m_{u})_{u\in S^{\prime}}
is a basis of
M
.

Proof.

If
M_{S}=M
, then we are done.
Otherwise, by the previous lemma, there exists
v\in C(S)
such that
m_{v}\notin M_{S}
.
Then
S^{\prime}=S\sqcup\left\{v\right\}
is a tree and
(m_{u})_{u\in S^{\prime}}
are linearly independent.
Then we proceed by induction.
∎

2.5.
The cells

To define a cell decomposition of the non-commutative Hilbert scheme
\mathcal{N}_{\mathbf{d}}
, we need to fix a total order on the set of paths
\mathcal{P}
.
We will say that a total order
\leq
on
\mathcal{P}
is
admissible
if it extends the partial order
\preceq

on
\mathcal{P}
.
We will say that a total order
\leq
on
\mathcal{P}
is
monomial
if

(1)

\leq
extends the partial order
\preceq
on
\mathcal{P}
.

(2)

If
u<v
, then
au<av
for any compatible arrow
a
.

(3)

\leq
is a well-order.

Example 2.3

(Shortlex order)
.

Assume that the set of arrows
Q^{\mathbf{f}}_{1}

is totally ordered.
Then, for two paths in
\mathcal{P}

(12)

u=a_{m}\dots a_{1},\qquad v=b_{n}\dots b_{1},

we say that
u<v
if
m<n
or if
m=n
and
a_{k}<b_{k}
for the minimal
k
with
a_{k}\neq b_{k}
.
This is a monomial order.

Example 2.4

(Weighted shortlex order)
.

Let
Q^{\mathbf{f}}_{1}
be totally ordered and let
\operatorname{wt}\colon Q^{\mathbf{f}}_{1}\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}
be a map.
For any path
u=a_{m}\dots a_{1}
, we define its weight
\operatorname{wt}(u)=\sum_{i}\operatorname{wt}(a_{i})
.
Given two paths
u,v
(
12
), we say that
u<v
if
\operatorname{wt}(u)<\operatorname{wt}(v)
or if
\operatorname{wt}(u)=\operatorname{wt}(v)
and
u<v
with respect to the shortlex order.
This is a monomial order.

Example 2.5

(Lex order)
.

As before, let
Q^{\mathbf{f}}_{1}
be totally ordered.
Given two paths
u,v
(
12
), we say that
u<v

if
a_{k}<b_{k}
for the minimal
k
with
a_{k}\neq b_{k}
(if such
k
exists)
or if
m<n
and
a_{k}=b_{k}
for all
1\leq k\leq m
.
This is the order used in

[
10
,
1
]
.
It is admissible, but not monomial.
For example, for the quiver
Q^{\mathbf{f}}
with arrows
f\colon\infty\to 0
and
a,b\colon 0\to 0
and the order on arrows
f<a<b
,
we have
af<a^{2}f
, but
baf>ba^{2}f
.

In what follows, we fix an admissible order on
\mathcal{P}
.
For any tree
S\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{d})
, we define the sets

(1)

U_{S}\subset\mathcal{N}_{\mathbf{d}}
that consists of
M\in\mathcal{N}_{\mathbf{d}}
such that the tuple

(m_{u})_{u\in S}
is a basis of
M
.

(2)

Z_{S}\subset U_{S}
that consists of
M\in U_{S}
such that,
for every
v\in C(S)
, we have

m_{v}\in M_{S_{<v}}
.

Remark 2.6
.

Note that
U_{S}
is independent of the choice of the total order on
\mathcal{P}
, while
Z_{S}
depends on it.
It follows from Corollary
2.2
that
\mathcal{N}_{\mathbf{d}}
is covered by the sets
U_{S}
.

The following result was proved in
[
10
, Lemma 3.4]
for the
m
-loop quiver. The proof for a general quiver is analogous. We give it for completeness.

Lemma 2.7
.

For any tree
S\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{d})
,
the set
U_{S}\subset\mathcal{N}_{\mathbf{d}}
is open.
It is an affine space of dimension

\#\bigcup_{i\in I}\left(S_{i}\times C(S)_{i}\right)
.

Proof.

The condition
M\in U_{S}
means that the vectors
(m_{u})_{u\in S}
are linearly independent and this is an open condition. We may express
m_{v}
in a unique way as a linear combination
m_{v}=\sum_{u\in S_{i}}c_{u,v}m_{u}
for every
v\in C(S)_{i}
and every
i\in I
. The scalars
c_{u,v}
provide a morphism
U_{S}\to\mathbb{A}^{N}
, where

N=\#\bigcup_{i\in I}S_{i}\times C(S)_{i}
.

Conversely, let us define a map
\mathbb{A}^{N}\to U_{S}
. Fix a basis
(m_{u})_{u\in S_{i}}
of each vector space
M_{i}
for
i\in Q^{\mathbf{f}}_{0}
. To a tuple
(c_{u,v})
of scalars with
(u,v)\in\bigcup_{i\in I}S_{i}\times C(S)_{i}
, we assign a representation
M\in U_{S}
as follows.
To specify a representation, we need to specify the maps
a\colon M_{i}\to M_{j}
for all arrows
a\colon i\to j
in
Q^{\mathbf{f}}
.
This means that, for every
u\in S_{i}
, we need to specify
am_{u}\in M_{j}
.
If
au\in S_{j}
, then we set
am_{u}=m_{au}
.
If
au\in C(S)_{j}
, then we define
am_{u}=\sum_{u^{\prime}\in S_{j}}c_{u^{\prime},au}m_{u^{\prime}}
. The thus obtained representation
M
lies in
U_{S}
.
The two maps are mutually inverse.
∎

Lemma 2.8
.

For any tree
S\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{d})
,
the set
Z_{S}\subset\mathcal{N}_{\mathbf{d}}
is locally-closed.
It is an affine space of dimension

\#\bigcup_{i\in I}\left\{\left.(u,v)\in S_{i}\times C(S)_{i}\ \right|u<v\right\}
.

Proof.

For any
M\in U_{S}
, the condition
m_{v}\in M_{S_{<v}}
for all
v\in C(S)_{i}
is a closed condition: when writing
m_{v}=\sum_{u\in S_{i}}c_{u,v}m_{u}
as in the proof of Lemma
2.7
, it is given by the vanishing of

c_{u,v}
for all
u\in S_{i}
with
u>v
.
This also shows that
Z_{S}
is a linear subspace of
U_{S}
of the required dimension.
∎

Lemma
2.8
implies that
Z_{S}\subset\mathcal{N}_{\mathbf{d}}
has dimension

(13)

d(S)=\sum_{v\in C(S)}k_{v},\qquad k_{v}=\#\left\{\left.u\in S_{t(v)}\ \right|u<v\right\}.

Lemma
2.7
implies that

(14)

\dim\mathcal{N}_{\mathbf{d}}=\dim U_{S}=\operatorname{\mathbf{dim}}C(S)\cdot\mathbf{d}.

On the other hand, we have
\dim\mathcal{N}_{\mathbf{d}}=\mathbf{f}\cdot\mathbf{d}-\chi(\mathbf{d},\mathbf{d})

(
5
).
We can prove more precisely

Lemma 2.9
.

For any tree
S\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{d})
, the dimension vector of the critical set
C(S)
is

(15)

\operatorname{\mathbf{dim}}C(S)=\mathbf{f}-\chi(\mathbf{d},-),

meaning that
\operatorname{\mathbf{dim}}C(S)\cdot\mathbf{e}=\mathbf{f}\cdot\mathbf{e}-\chi(\mathbf{d},\mathbf{e})
, for all
\mathbf{e}\in\mathbb{Z}^{I}
.

Proof.

The statement is clear for
S=\left\{*\right\}
.
Assume that the statement is true for a tree
S
and let
v\in C(S)_{i}
and
S^{\prime}=S\cup\left\{v\right\}
.
Then
C(S^{\prime})=C(S)\backslash\left\{v\right\}\cup\operatorname{ch}(v)
, where
\operatorname{ch}(v)=\left\{\left.u\in\mathcal{P}\ \right|p(u)=v\right\}
is the set of children of
v
(they correspond to arrows that start at
i
).
Therefore

\operatorname{\mathbf{dim}}C(S^{\prime})=\operatorname{\mathbf{dim}}C(S)-e_{i}+\operatorname{\mathbf{dim}}\operatorname{ch}(v)=\operatorname{\mathbf{dim}}C(S)-\chi(e_{i},-).

If
\mathbf{d}=\operatorname{\mathbf{dim}}S
, then
\mathbf{d}^{\prime}=\operatorname{\mathbf{dim}}S^{\prime}=\mathbf{d}+e_{i}
, hence

\operatorname{\mathbf{dim}}C(S^{\prime})=\mathbf{f}-\chi(\mathbf{d},-)-\chi(e_{i},-)=\mathbf{f}-\chi(\mathbf{d}^{\prime},-)

and we proceed by induction.
∎

2.6.
Properties of monomial orders

In this section we will assume that
\mathcal{P}
is equipped with a monomial order.

Lemma 2.10
.

Let
M\in\mathcal{N}_{\mathbf{d}}
and
S\subset\mathcal{P}
be a tree.
If
v\in\mathcal{P}
is such that

m_{u}\in M_{S_{\leq u}}
, for all
u\in C(S)
with
u\leq v
,
then
m_{v}\in M_{S_{\leq v}}
.

Proof.

We can assume by induction that
m_{w}\in M_{S_{\leq w}}
for all
w<v
.
Let
v=au
for some arrow
a
and
u\in\mathcal{P}
.
If
u\in S
, then
v\in S\cup C(S)
and we are done.
If
u\notin S
, then
m_{u}\in M_{S_{<u}}
,
hence
m_{u}=\sum_{w\in S_{<u}}c_{w}m_{w}
for some scalars
c_{w}
.
For any
w\in S_{<u}
, we have
aw<au=v
, hence
m_{aw}\in M_{S_{\leq aw}}\subset M_{S_{<v}}
.
We conclude that

m_{v}=am_{u}=\sum_{w\in S_{<u}}c_{w}m_{aw}\in M_{S_{<v}}
.
∎

Corollary 2.11
.

Let
M\in\mathcal{N}_{\mathbf{d}}
and
S\subset\mathcal{P}
be a tree such that

m_{v}\in M_{S_{\leq v}}
, for all
v\in C(S)
.
Then the same is true for all
v\in\mathcal{P}
.

We will see in Lemma
2.13
that the cells
Z_{S}\subset\mathcal{N}_{\mathbf{d}}
, for
S\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{d})
, are disjoint.
The following result gives an algorithm that, for any
M\in\mathcal{N}_{\mathbf{d}}
, produces a tree
S\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{d})
such that
M\in Z_{S}
.
It implies (in the case of monomial orders) that
\mathcal{N}_{\mathbf{d}}
is a disjoint union of the cells
Z_{S}
(cf. Theorem
2.15
, where this statement is proved for admissible orders).

Theorem 2.12
.

Given
M\in\mathcal{N}_{\mathbf{d}}
, consider the maximal sequence
(v_{1},\dots,v_{n})
such that

v_{k}=\min\left\{\left.v\in\mathcal{P}\ \right|m_{v}\notin\left\langle m_{v_{i}}\col i<k\right\rangle\right\},\qquad 1\leq k\leq n.

Then
S=(v_{1}<\dots<v_{n})
is a tree and
M\in Z_{S}
.

Proof.

We have

m_{v_{k+1}}\notin\left\langle m_{v_{i}}\col i<k\right\rangle
,
hence
v_{k}<v_{k+1}
by the minimality of
v_{k}
.

Let us assume by induction that
S^{\prime}=(v_{1}<\dots<v_{k})
is a tree.
If
u\in\mathcal{P}
is such that
m_{u}\notin M_{S^{\prime}_{\leq u}}
,
then
S^{\prime}_{\leq u}=S^{\prime}
and
m_{u}\notin M_{S^{\prime}}
.
Indeed, otherwise
S^{\prime}_{\leq u}=(v_{1}<\dots<v_{i})
for some
i<k
.
But then
v_{i+1}\leq u
by the minimality of
v_{i+1}
.
Therefore
v_{i+1}\in S^{\prime}_{\leq u}
, a contradiction.

Let
v=v_{k+1}\in\mathcal{P}
be the minimal element such that
m_{v}\notin M_{S^{\prime}}
.
For any
u<v
, we have
m_{u}\in M_{S^{\prime}}
, hence
m_{u}\in M_{S^{\prime}_{\leq u}}
by the previous statement.
If
v\notin C(S^{\prime})
, then we conclude from Lemma
2.10
that
m_{v}\in M_{S^{\prime}_{\leq v}}
, a contradiction.
Therefore
v\in C(S^{\prime})
and
S^{\prime}\cup\left\{v\right\}
is a tree.

By the maximality of the sequence, we conclude that
m_{v}\in M_{S}
for all
v\in\mathcal{P}
, hence
(m_{u})_{u\in S}
is a basis of
M
.
If
M\notin Z_{S}
, then there exists an element
v\in\mathcal{P}
such that
m_{v}\notin M_{S_{\leq v}}
.
But then
m_{v}\notin M_{S}
by the statement we proved.
This is a contradiction.
∎

2.7.
Cell decomposition

In this section we give slightly simplified proofs of some results from
[
1
,
10
]
.
As before, we assume that
\mathcal{P}
is equipped with an admissible order.
We define a total order on the set of trees
\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{d})
as follows.
Given trees
S,S^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{d})
we write them in the form

(16)

S=(v_{1}<\dots<v_{k}),\qquad S^{\prime}=(v^{\prime}_{1}<\dots<v^{\prime}_{k})

and define
S^{\prime}<S
if, for the minimal

with
v^{\prime}_{i}\neq v_{i}
, we have

.

Lemma 2.13
.

If
S^{\prime}<S
, then
Z_{S}\cap U_{S^{\prime}}=\varnothing
.
In particular, if
S\neq S^{\prime}
, then
Z_{S}\cap Z_{S^{\prime}}=\varnothing
.

Proof.

If
M\in Z_{S}\cap U_{S^{\prime}}
, then
k=\dim M=\#S=\#{S^{\prime}}
.
Let

S=(v_{1}<\dots<v_{k}),\qquad S^{\prime}=(v^{\prime}_{1}<\dots<v^{\prime}_{k})

and let
1\leq i\leq k
be minimal such that
v_{i}\neq v^{\prime}_{i}
.
Then
v^{\prime}_{i}<v_{i}
and
v^{\prime}_{i}\notin S
(if
v^{\prime}_{i}\in S
, then
v^{\prime}_{i}=v_{j}
for some
j<i
, hence
v^{\prime}_{j}<v^{\prime}_{i}=v_{j}
, a contradiction).
Note that
v_{1}=v^{\prime}_{1}=*
, hence
v^{\prime}_{i}\neq*
.
Let
v^{\prime}_{i}=av
, where
a
is an arrow.
Then
v<v^{\prime}_{i}
and
v\in S^{\prime}
, hence
v=v^{\prime}_{j}=v_{j}\in S
for some
j<i
.
This implies that
v^{\prime}_{i}=av\in C(S)
.
By the assumption

, we have

m_{v^{\prime}_{i}}\in M_{S_{<v^{\prime}_{i}}}
.
If
u\in S
and
u<v^{\prime}_{i}<v_{i}
, then
u=v_{j}=v^{\prime}_{j}\in S^{\prime}
for some
j<i
.
This implies
m_{v^{\prime}_{i}}\in M_{S^{\prime}_{<v^{\prime}_{i}}}
.
A contradiction to

.
∎

Lemma 2.14
.

For any tree
S\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{d})
, we have
U_{S}\subset Z_{S}\cup\left(\bigcup_{S^{\prime}<S}U_{S^{\prime}}\right)
.

Proof.

We need to show that

U_{S}\backslash Z_{S}\subset\bigcup_{S^{\prime}<S}U_{S^{\prime}}
.
Let
M\in U_{S}\backslash Z_{S}

and let
v\in C(S)
be minimal such that
m_{v}\notin M_{S_{<v}}
.
Consider the tree

\bar{S}=\left\{\left.u\in S\ \right|u<v\right\}\cup\left\{v\right\}.

Then the vectors
(m_{u})_{u\in\bar{S}}
are linearly independent and there exists a tree
\bar{S}\subset S^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{P}
such that
M\in U_{S^{\prime}}
by Corollary
2.2
.
We will prove that
S^{\prime}<S
.
We can write

\bar{S}=(v_{1}<\dots<v_{i-1}<v),\qquad S=(v_{1}<\dots<v_{i-1}<v_{i}<\dots),

where
v<v_{i}
.
We claim that for all
w\in S^{\prime}\backslash\bar{S}
, we have
w>v
, hence

S^{\prime}=(v_{1}<\dots<v_{i-1}<v<\dots)

and therefore
S^{\prime}<S
.

If our claim is not true, choose the minimal
w\in S^{\prime}\backslash\bar{S}
with
w<v
.
Then
w\in C(\bar{S}\backslash\left\{v\right\})
,
hence either
w\in S
(but then
w\in\bar{S}
, a contradiction) or
w\in C(S)
.
We have
m_{w}\notin M_{S^{\prime}_{<w}}
as
w\in S^{\prime}
and
M\in U_{S^{\prime}}
.
Note that if
u\in S
and
u<w<v
, then
u\in\bar{S}\subset S^{\prime}
.
Therefore
M_{S_{<w}}\subset M_{S^{\prime}_{<w}}
,
hence
m_{w}\notin M_{S_{<w}}
, contradicting to the minimality of

.
∎

We arrive at the following result (cf.
[
10
, Theorem 7.7]
).

Theorem 2.15
.

For any tree
S\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{d})
, define the open set
V_{S}=\bigcup_{S^{\prime}\leq S}U_{S^{\prime}}
.
Then
V_{S}=\bigsqcup_{S^{\prime}\leq S}Z_{S^{\prime}}
.
In particular,

\mathcal{N}_{\mathbf{d}}=\bigsqcup_{S\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{d})}Z_{S}.

Proof.

Recall that
\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{d})
is a finite set.
We conclude from Lemma
2.14
by induction
that
V_{S}\subset\bigcup_{S^{\prime}\leq S}Z_{S}
.
This union is disjoint by Lemma
2.13
.
Moreover, for any
S^{\prime}\leq S
, we have
Z_{S^{\prime}}\subset U_{S^{\prime}}\subset V_{S}
. Therefore
V_{S}=\bigsqcup_{S^{\prime}\leq S}Z_{S^{\prime}}
.
Taking the maximal tree
S\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{d})
, we obtain the last statement.
∎

Corollary 2.16

(Cell decomposition)
.

The moduli space
\mathcal{N}_{\mathbf{d}}
admits a cell decomposition, meaning that it admits a filtration by open subvarieties

\varnothing=V_{0}\subset V_{1}\subset\dots\subset V_{r}=\mathcal{N}_{\mathbf{d}}

such that
V_{k}\backslash V_{k-1}
are affine spaces, for all
1\leq k\leq r
.

Proof.

Let us order the trees in
\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{d})

as
S_{1}<\dots<S_{r}
.
Then
V_{k}=V_{S_{k}}=\bigcup_{i\leq k}U_{S_{i}}=\bigsqcup_{i\leq k}Z_{S_{i}}
are open and satisfy

V_{k}\backslash V_{k-1}=Z_{S_{k}}
.
∎

For any subset

to be the set of representations

such that the vectors
(m_{u})_{u\in S}
in

For any tree
S\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{d})
and

(18)

S(v)=\left\{\left.u\in S_{t(v)}\ \right|u<v\right\}\cup\left\{v\right\}.

S
to be

(19)

D_{S}=\bigcap_{v\in C(S)}D(S(v)).

Lemma 2.19
.

We have
D_{S}\subset\bigsqcup_{S^{\prime}\geq S}Z_{S^{\prime}}

and

D_{S}\cap U_{S}=Z_{S}
.

Proof.

We note that
\bigsqcup_{S^{\prime}<S}Z_{S^{\prime}}=\bigcup_{S^{\prime}<S}U_{S^{\prime}}
.
Therefore to show that
D_{S}\subset\bigsqcup_{S^{\prime}\geq S}Z_{S^{\prime}}
it is enough to show that
D_{S}\cap U_{S^{\prime}}=\varnothing
for
S^{\prime}<S
.
The proof of this statement goes through the same lines as the proof of Lemma
2.13
.

We have
Z_{S}\subset D_{S}\cap U_{S}
by definition.
If

m_{v}\in M_{S_{<v}}
, for all

, hence

.
Therefore

.

2.10.
Varieties having cell decomposition

The following result is folklore.

Theorem 2.21
.

Assume that an algebraic variety
X
admits a cell decomposition, meaning that it admits a filtration by open subvarieties

\varnothing=V_{0}\subset V_{1}\subset\dots\subset V_{r}=X

such that
Z_{k}=V_{k}\backslash V_{k-1}
are affine spaces, for all
1\leq k\leq r
.
Then

A_{*}(X)=\bigoplus_{k}\mathbb{Z}[\bar{Z}_{k}].

Moreover,
H^{\mathrm{BM}}_{i}(X,\mathbb{Z})=0
for odd
i
and the cycle map
A_{i}(X)\to H^{\mathrm{BM}}_{2i}(X,\mathbb{Z})
is an isomorphism for
i\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Proof.

We have
V_{k}=\bigsqcup_{i\leq k}Z_{i}
.
Define closed subsets

D_{k}=\bigsqcup_{i\geq k}Z_{i}=X\backslash V_{k-1}
.
Then
Z_{k}
is open in
D_{k}
and its complement is
D_{k+1}
.
We obtain a long exact sequence of BM homology

\dots\to H^{\mathrm{BM}}_{i}(D_{k+1})\to H^{\mathrm{BM}}_{i}(D_{k})\to H^{\mathrm{BM}}_{i}(Z_{k})\to\dots.

Note that
H^{\mathrm{BM}}_{i}(Z_{k})=0
for odd
i
and we obtain by induction (on decreasing
k
) that
H^{\mathrm{BM}}_{i}(D_{k})=0
for odd
i
.
This implies that we have a commutative diagram with exact rows

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A_{i}(D_{k+1})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A_{i}(D_{k})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A_{i}(Z_{k})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H^{\mathrm{BM}}_{2i}(D_{k+1})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H^{\mathrm{BM}}_{2i}(D_{k})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H^{\mathrm{BM}}_{2i}(Z_{k})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0.}

The right vertical map is an isomorphism as
Z_{k}
is an affine space.
We can assume by induction (on decreasing
k
) that the left vertical map is also an isomorphism.
Then by the snake lemma we conclude that the middle vertical arrow is also an isomorphism.
Therefore we have an exact sequence

0\to A_{*}(D_{k+1})\to A_{*}(D_{k})\to A_{*}(Z_{k})\to 0.

Note that
A_{*}(Z_{k})=\mathbb{Z}[Z_{k}]
and the above exact sequence splits canonically, where we map

to

.
Therefore we obtain

A_{*}(D_{k})\simeq\mathbb{Z}[\bar{Z}_{k}]\oplus A_{*}(D_{k+1}).

By induction this implies

A_{*}(X)=A_{*}(D_{1})\simeq\bigoplus_{k=1}^{r}\mathbb{Z}[\bar{Z}_{k}].

∎

\#\left\{\left.v\in C(S)_{i}\ \right|v<u_{i,\mathbf{e}_{i}+1}\right\}=\#\left\{\left.v\in S_{i}\cup C(S)_{i}\ \right|v\leq u_{i,\mathbf{e}_{i}+1}\right\}-\mathbf{e}_{i}-1

Every
v\in S_{i}\cup C(S)_{i}
satisfying
v\leq u_{i,\mathbf{e}_{i}+1}

can be written in the form

v=a\bar{v}
for some

\bar{v}\in S_{j}
,
j\in I\cup\left\{\infty\right\}
, and some arrow
a\colon j\to i
in
Q^{\mathbf{f}}
.

If
j\in I
, then
\bar{v}<v\leq u_{i,\mathbf{e}_{i}+1}\leq u_{j,\mathbf{e}_{j}+1}
, whenever
\mathbf{e}_{j}<\mathbf{d}_{j}
.
The number of such
\bar{v}\in S_{j}
is bounded by
\mathbf{e}_{j}
(this also includes the case
\mathbf{e}_{j}=\mathbf{d}_{j}
).
Therefore the number of
v
of the required form
is bounded by
\sum_{j}a_{ji}\mathbf{e}_{j}
, where
a_{ji}
is the number of arrows
j\to i
in
Q^{\mathbf{f}}
.
If
j=\infty
, then
\bar{v}=e_{\infty}
and the number of
v
of the required form is bounded by
\mathbf{f}_{i}
.
We conclude that

\#\left\{\left.v\in C(S)_{i}\ \right|v<u_{i,\mathbf{e}_{i}+1}\right\}\leq\mathbf{f}_{i}+\sum_{j}a_{ji}\mathbf{e}_{j}-\mathbf{e}_{i}-1=\mathbf{f}_{i}-\chi(\mathbf{e},e_{i})-1<c(\mathbf{e})_{i}

and this contradicts assumption (
28
).

Now let us construct the inverse map
\mathcal{S}(\mathbf{d})\to\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{d})
.
Given a multi-partition
\lambda\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbf{d})
, we define the numbers
m^{(i)}_{k}
as in (
27
).
We need to construct a tree

S=(u_{0}<u_{1}<\dots)

in
\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{d})
with

S_{i}=(u_{i,1}<\dots<u_{i,\mathbf{d}_{i}}),\qquad i\in I,

such that

\#\left\{\left.v\in C(S)_{i}\ \right|v<u_{i,k+1}\right\}=m^{(i)}_{k},\qquad 0\leq k<\mathbf{d}_{i}.

We construct this tree inductively as follows.
We set
u_{0}=e_{\infty}
.
Assume that a tree
S^{\prime}=(u_{0}<\dots<u_{r})

is constructed
and satisfies the conditions

(29)

\#\left\{\left.v\in C(S^{\prime})_{i}\ \right|v<u_{i,k+1}\right\}=m^{(i)}_{k},\qquad i\in I,\,0\leq k<\mathbf{e}_{i}.

(30)

\#\left\{\left.v\in C(S^{\prime})_{i}\ \right|v<u\right\}\leq m^{(i)}_{\mathbf{e}_{i}}\qquad i\in I,\,u\in S^{\prime}.

where
\mathbf{e}=\operatorname{\mathbf{dim}}S^{\prime}\leq\mathbf{d}
and

S^{\prime}_{i}=(u_{i,1}<\dots<u_{i,\mathbf{e}_{i}}),\qquad i\in I.

Assuming that
\mathbf{e}<\mathbf{d}
, we will construct a tree
S^{\prime\prime}=S^{\prime}\cup\left\{u_{r+1}\right\}
satisfying the same conditions.

By the assumption on
\lambda
,
there exists
i\in I
such that

m^{(i)}_{\mathbf{e}_{i}}<c(\mathbf{e})_{i}.

We order the elements of
C(S^{\prime})_{i}
and denote the
m^{(i)}_{\mathbf{e}_{i}}+1
-st element by
v_{i}
.
We claim that
v_{i}
is the maximal element in

S^{\prime}\cup\left\{v_{i}\right\}
.
Indeed, we have

\#\left\{\left.v\in C(S^{\prime})_{i}\ \right|v\leq v_{i}\right\}=m^{(i)}_{\mathbf{e}_{i}}+1

while by condition (
30
)

\#\left\{\left.v\in C(S^{\prime})_{i}\ \right|v\leq u\right\}\leq m^{(i)}_{\mathbf{e}_{i}},\qquad u\in S^{\prime}.

This implies that
u<v_{i}
for all
u\in S^{\prime}
.

Let
j\in I
be such that
v_{j}\in C(S^{\prime})_{j}
is minimal
and let
S^{\prime\prime}=S^{\prime}\cup\left\{v_{j}\right\}
.
Then

C(S^{\prime\prime})=C(S^{\prime})\backslash\left\{v_{j}\right\}\cup\operatorname{ch}(v_{j}),

where
\operatorname{ch}(v)=\min_{\preceq}(\mathcal{P}_{\succ v})
denotes the set of children of
v\in\mathcal{P}
.
As
v_{j}
is the maximal element in
S^{\prime\prime}
, we have
S^{\prime\prime}_{i}=S^{\prime}_{i}
for
i\neq j
and

S^{\prime\prime}_{j}=(u_{j,1}<\dots<u_{j,\mathbf{e}_{j}}<v_{j}=\colon u_{j,\mathbf{e}_{j}+1}).

We will show that conditions (
29
) and (
30
) are satisfied for the tree
S^{\prime\prime}
.
For
i\in I
and
0\leq k<\mathbf{e}_{i}
, we have

\#\left\{\left.v\in C(S^{\prime\prime})_{i}\ \right|v<u_{i,k+1}\right\}=\#\left\{\left.v\in C(S^{\prime})_{i}\ \right|v<u_{i,k+1}\right\}=m^{(i)}_{k}

by condition (
29
) for
S^{\prime}
.
On the other hand

\#\left\{\left.v\in C(S^{\prime\prime})_{j}\ \right|v<v_{j}\right\}=\#\left\{\left.v\in C(S^{\prime})_{j}\ \right|v<v_{j}\right\}=m^{(j)}_{\mathbf{e}_{j}+1}.

Therefore condition (
29
) for the tree
S^{\prime\prime}
is satisfied.
To prove condition (
30
) for
S^{\prime\prime}
it is enough to consider the maximal element
u=v_{j}
of the tree.

If
i\in I
is such that
m^{(i)}_{\mathbf{e}_{i}}<c(\mathbf{e})_{i}
, then

v_{j}\leq v_{i}
by the minimality of
v_{j}
, hence

\#\left\{\left.v\in C(S^{\prime})_{i}\ \right|v<v_{j}\right\}\leq m^{(i)}_{\mathbf{e}_{i}}.

If
i\in I
is such that

, then we also obtain

\#\left\{\left.v\in C(S^{\prime})_{i}\ \right|v<v_{j}\right\}\leq\#C(S^{\prime})_{i}=c(\mathbf{e})_{i}\leq m^{(i)}_{\mathbf{e}_{i}}.

We conclude that for all
i\in I

\#\left\{\left.v\in C(S^{\prime\prime})_{i}\ \right|v<v_{j}\right\}=\#\left\{\left.v\in C(S^{\prime})_{i}\ \right|v<v_{j}\right\}\leq m^{(i)}_{\mathbf{e}_{i}}

which implies condition (
30
) for the tree
S^{\prime\prime}
.
∎

3.2.
Total order on the set of multi-partitions

In the previous section we constructed a bijection
\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{d})\simeq\mathcal{S}(\mathbf{d})
which depends on an admissible order on
\mathcal{P}
.
On the other hand,
in §
2.7
we constructed a total order on the set of trees
\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{d})
.
This implies that we have the induced total order on the set of multi-partitions
\mathcal{S}(\mathbf{d})
, which a priori may depend on an admissible total order on
\mathcal{P}
.
In this section we will show that, for a quiver
Q
having only one vertex, the induced total order on
\mathcal{S}(\mathbf{d})
is independent of an admissible order on
\mathcal{P}
.

Let us assume that
Q
has one vertex.
Recall that,
for
d\in\mathbb{N}\simeq\mathbb{N}^{I}
and a tree

S=(*<u_{1}<\dots<u_{d})
in
\mathcal{P}(d)
,
the corresponding partition
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{d})\in\mathcal{S}(d)

is defined by (
24
)

(31)

\lambda_{d-k}=\#\left\{\left.v\in C(S)\ \right|v<u_{k+1}\right\},\qquad 0\leq k<d.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
S,S^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}(d)
and
\lambda,\mu\in\mathcal{S}(d)
be the corresponding partitions.
Then
S<S^{\prime}
if and only if
\lambda_{k}<\mu_{k}
for the maximal
1\leq k\leq d
with
\lambda_{k}\neq\mu_{k}
.
In particular, the induced total order on
\mathcal{S}(d)
is independent of the choice of an admissible order on
\mathcal{P}
.

Proof.

It is enough to show that if partitions
\lambda,\mu
satisfy the above property, then
S<S^{\prime}
.
Let
m_{k}=\lambda_{d-k}
and
m^{\prime}_{k}=\mu_{d-k}
for
0\leq k<d
.
By the proof of Theorem
3.1
the tree
S=(*<u_{1}<\dots<u_{d})
has the property that
u_{k+1}
is the
(m_{k}+1)
-st element in
C(\bar{S})
, where
\bar{S}
is the tree
\bar{S}=(*<u_{1}<\dots<u_{k})
.
Similarly for the tree
S^{\prime}=(*<u^{\prime}_{1}<\dots<u^{\prime}_{d})
.

By assumption, we have
m_{k+1}<m^{\prime}_{k+1}
for the minimal
k
with
m_{k+1}\neq m^{\prime}_{k+1}
.
This implies that
\bar{S}=(*<u_{1}<\dots<u_{k})=(*<u^{\prime}_{1}<\dots<u^{\prime}_{k})

and

.
Therefore

.
∎

3.3.
Cells parametrized by multi-partitions

Theorem
3.1
implies that we can parametrize the cells
Z_{S}\subset\mathcal{N}_{\mathbf{d}}
, for
S\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{d})
, by multi-partitions.
Note that the number of

with

is equal to
\lambda^{(i)}_{k}-\lambda^{(i)}_{k+1}
for

,
where we use now

\lambda^{(i)}_{0}=\#C(S)_{i}=\mathbf{f}_{i}-\chi(\mathbf{d},i)
.
Therefore, the dimension of the cell

is

(32)

\dim Z_{S}=\sum_{v\in C(S)}k_{v}=\sum_{i\in I}\sum_{k\geq 0}(\mathbf{d}_{i}-k)(\lambda^{(i)}_{k}-\lambda^{(i)}_{k+1})=\mathbf{f}\cdot\mathbf{d}-\chi(\mathbf{d},\mathbf{d})-\left\lvert\lambda\right\rvert,

.
This implies that the motivic class of

can be written in the form

[
1
, Theorem 6.2]

(33)

[\mathcal{N}_{\mathbf{d}}]=\mathbb{L}^{\mathbf{f}\cdot\mathbf{d}-\chi(\mathbf{d},\mathbf{d})}\sum_{\lambda\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbf{d})}\mathbb{L}^{-\left\lvert\lambda\right\rvert}.

It follows from Theorem
3.1
that,
for an admissible total order on
\mathcal{P}
,
we have a bijection
\mathcal{S}(\mathbf{d})\to\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{d})
,
\lambda\mapsto S_{\lambda}
.
We will see later that the class

.

4.
Grassmannians and non-commutative Hilbert schemes

Let
0\leq d\leq w
be two integers,
r=w-d
and
W=\mathbb{C}^{w}
.
We define the Grassmannian

(34)

\operatorname{Gr}^{d}(W)=\operatorname{Gr}_{r}(W)=\operatorname{Gr}(r,w)

to be the algebraic variety parameterizing
surjective linear maps
M\colon W\to\mathbb{C}^{d}
(up to the action of
\operatorname{GL}_{d}(\mathbb{C})
) or, equivalently,
subspaces
U=\ker(M)\subset W
of dimension
r
.

We can interpret it as a non-commutative Hilbert scheme as follows.
Let
Q
be the quiver having one vertex
0
and no arrows,
and let
Q^{w}
be the framed quiver obtained from
Q
by adding one vertex
\infty
and
w
arrows
\infty\to 0
.
The non-commutative Hilbert scheme
\mathcal{N}_{d}=\mathcal{N}_{d,w}
parametrizes representations
M
of
Q^{w}
having dimension vector
(d,1)
(with the coordinate
1
at the vertex
\infty
), generated by
M_{\infty}
.
Such representations can be identified with surjective linear maps
M\colon\mathbb{C}^{w}\to\mathbb{C}^{d}

(up to the action of
\operatorname{GL}_{d}(\mathbb{C})
), hence

(35)

\mathcal{N}_{d}=\operatorname{Gr}^{d}(W)=\operatorname{Gr}_{r}(W).

This is a smooth projective variety of dimension
d(w-d)
.

In the previous sections we established a cell decomposition of
\mathcal{N}_{d}
parametrized by trees or partitions.
On the other hand, Grassmannians possess a decomposition by Schubert cells parametrized by partitions.
In this section we will establish a precise relationship between these decompositions.
We will also discuss if the properties satisfied by cell decompositions of Grassmannians can be generalized to other quivers.

4.1.
Trees and cells

Recall that the framed quiver
Q^{w}
consists of two vertices
\infty,\,0
and
w
arrows
\infty\to 0
.
Let us define
[1,w]=\left\{1,\dots,w\right\}
.
Then

(1)

The path poset
\mathcal{P}
can be identified with the set
\left\{*\right\}\cup[1,w]
.

(2)

A subtree
S\in\mathcal{P}(d)
can be identified with a subset
S_{0}\subset[1,w]
having
d
elements.

(3)

The critical set
C(S)
can be identified with
S^{\prime}=[1,w]\backslash S_{0}
having
r=w-d
elements.

In what follows we will write
S
for
S_{0}\subset[1,w]
and still call it a tree.
We order the elements of
\mathcal{P}
as

*<1<\dots<w.

A framed representation
M\in\mathcal{N}_{d}

can be identified with a surjective linear map
M\colon\mathbb{C}^{w}\to\mathbb{C}^{d}
.
We define

m_{i}=M(e_{i})\in\mathbb{C}^{d},\qquad i\in[1,w].

Then the cell
Z_{S}
, for
S\subset[1,w]
, consists of such
M
that

(1)

(m_{i})_{i\in S}
are linearly independent.

(2)

m_{j}\in\left\langle m_{i}\col i\in S_{<j}\right\rangle
for all
j\in S^{\prime}=[1,w]\backslash S
.

Earlier we defined the set
\mathcal{S}(d)=\mathcal{S}^{w}(d)
to be the set of partitions (
21
)

(36)

\mu=(\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{d}),\qquad\mu_{1}\leq w-d.

In Theorem
3.1
we described a bijection

\mathcal{P}(d)\to\mathcal{S}(d)
.
For every tree

S=(u_{1}<\dots<u_{d})\subset[1,w]

we define the corresponding partition
\mu\in\mathcal{S}(d)
by

(37)

\mu_{d-k}=\#\left\{\left.j\in[1,w]\backslash S\ \right|j<u_{k+1}\right\},\qquad 0\leq k<d.

We denote the cell
Z_{S}
also by
Z_{\mu}
.

4.2.
Schubert cells

As before, we consider
0\leq d\leq w
and
r=w-d
.
Consider a full flag

0=F_{0}\subset F_{1}\subset\dots\subset F_{w}=W=\mathbb{C}^{w}.

Given a partition

(38)

\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{r}),\qquad\lambda_{1}\leq\lambda_{0}\col=d,

we define
the Schubert variety
[
5
, §9.4]

(39)

\Omega_{\lambda}=\left\{\left.U\in\operatorname{Gr}^{d}(W)\ \right|\dim(U\cap F_{d+i-\lambda_{i}})\geq i,\,1\leq i\leq r\right\}

and the Schubert cell

(40)

\Omega_{\lambda}^{\circ}=\left\{\left.U\in\operatorname{Gr}^{d}(W)\ \right|\dim(U\cap F_{k})=i\text{ for }d+i-\lambda_{i}\leq k\leq d+i-\lambda_{i+1},\,0\leq i\leq r\right\}.

It is known that

(1)

\Omega_{\lambda}^{\circ}
is an affine space.

(2)

\operatorname{Gr}^{d}(W)=\bigsqcup_{\lambda}\Omega_{\lambda}^{\circ}
.

(3)

\Omega_{\lambda}
is the closure of
\Omega_{\lambda}^{\circ}
in
\operatorname{Gr}_{r}(W)
.

(4)

\Omega_{\lambda}=\bigsqcup_{\nu\supseteq\lambda}\Omega_{\nu}^{\circ}
,
where
\nu\supseteq\lambda
if
\nu_{i}\geq\lambda_{i}
for all
i
.

Proposition 4.1
.

Assume that the flag on
\mathbb{C}^{w}
is given by
F_{i}=\left\langle e_{1},\dots,e_{i}\right\rangle
, for
1\leq i\leq w
.
Then, for any partition
\lambda
as in (
38
), we have

(41)

\Omega_{\lambda}^{\circ}=Z_{S},

where
S=[1,w]\backslash S^{\prime}
with

(42)

S^{\prime}=\left\{v_{1},\dots,v_{r}\right\},\qquad v_{i}=d+i-\lambda_{i},\qquad 1\leq i\leq r.

Proof.

We have

0=v_{0}<v_{1}<\dots<v_{r}\leq d+r=w

so that
S^{\prime}
consists of
r
elements and
S
consists of
d
elements.

Given
U\subset\operatorname{Gr}_{r}(\mathbb{C}^{w})
, we consider the quotient

M\colon\mathbb{C}^{w}\to\mathbb{C}^{w}/U\simeq\mathbb{C}^{d}.

Then the condition
\dim U\cap F_{k}=i
, means that
\dim\ker(M\colon F_{k}\to\mathbb{C}^{d})=i
, hence

(43)

\operatorname{rk}\left\langle m_{1},\dots,m_{k}\right\rangle=k-i,\qquad m_{i}=M(e_{i})\in\mathbb{C}^{d}.

The condition that
U\in\Omega_{\lambda}^{\circ}
means that we have the above equality for
v_{i}\leq k<v_{i+1}
.
This means that the rank in (
43
) increases by
1
if
v_{i}<k<v_{i+1}
(hence
k\in S
) and doesn’t increase if
k=v_{i+1}
(hence
k\in S^{\prime}
).
This can be reformulated as the condition that

(m_{i})_{i\in S}
are linearly independent and that

m_{j}\in\left\langle m_{i}\col i\in S_{<j}\right\rangle
for all
j\in S^{\prime}
.
This is exactly the definition of the cell
Z_{S}
.
∎

4.3.
Relation between partitions

In Prop.
4.1
, for any partition
\lambda
as in (
38
), we constructed the sets

S=(u_{1}<\dots<u_{d})\subset[1,w],\qquad S^{\prime}=[1,w]\backslash S=(v_{1}<\dots<v_{r}),

with
v_{i}=d+i-\lambda_{i}
.
Conversely, we can reconstruct the partition
\lambda
from

by

\lambda_{i}=d+i-v_{i},\qquad 1\leq i\leq r.

On the other hand, we associated with

a partition
\mu
with (

\mu_{k}=\#\left\{\left.i\geq 1\ \right|v_{i}<u_{d-k+1}\right\},\qquad 1\leq k\leq d,

Lemma 4.2
.

The partitions
\mu
and
\lambda
are conjugate to each other, meaning that

\mu_{k}=\#\left\{\left.i\geq 1\ \right|\lambda_{i}\geq k\right\},\qquad k\geq 1.

Proof.

We need to show that

\lambda_{i}\geq k\iff v_{i}<u_{d-k+1}.

The first inequality can be written in the form

v_{i}\leq d-k+i.

In this case the number of
j
with
u_{j}<v_{i}
is
\leq d-k
, implying that
v_{i}<u_{d-k+1}
.

If
v_{i}<u_{d-k+1}
, then the number of
j
with
u_{j}<v_{i}
is
\leq d-k
.
Therefore
v_{i}
is bounded by

(the number of
u_{j}
on the left of
v_{i}
) plus
i
(the number of
v_{j}
on the left of
v_{i}
), hence
v_{i}\leq d-k+i
.
∎

We conclude from the previous lemma that

(44)

Z_{\mu}=\Omega_{\mu^{\prime}}^{\circ},\qquad\bar{Z}_{\mu}=\Omega_{\mu^{\prime}}.

where
\mu^{\prime}
denotes the conjugate partition.
Schubert varieties have a decomposition

(45)

\Omega_{\lambda}=\bigsqcup_{\nu\supseteq\lambda}\Omega_{\nu}^{\circ},

where
\nu\supseteq\lambda
if
\nu_{i}\geq\lambda_{i}
for all
i
.
This implies that

(46)

\bar{Z}_{\mu}=\bigsqcup_{\nu\supseteq\mu}Z_{\nu}.

On the other hand, we have seen in §
2.8
that the closure of a cell is not a disjoint union of cells for more general quivers.
The class of a Schubert variety
[\Omega_{\mu^{\prime}}]\in A_{*}(\operatorname{Gr}^{d}(W))
is independent of a flag.
Therefore the class
[\bar{Z}_{\mu}]
is independent of the choice of a total order on
\mathcal{P}
.
On the other hand, we will see in §
6.5
that this is not the case for more general quivers.

5.2.
Shuffle algebra description

Let us recall an explicit shuffle product description of the multiplication of CoHA from
[
7
]
.
For any
\mathbf{d}\in\mathbb{N}^{I}
, the group
G_{\mathbf{d}}
is linearly reductive.
The subgroup
T_{\mathbf{d}}\subset G_{\mathbf{d}}
consisting of tuples of invertible diagonal matrices is a maximal torus. The corresponding Weyl group is isomorphic to the product of symmetric groups
\Sigma_{\mathbf{d}}=\prod_{i\in I}\Sigma_{\mathbf{d}_{i}}
. As the space
R_{\mathbf{d}}
is

G_{\mathbf{d}}
-equivariantly contractible, we obtain

(49)

\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{d}}=H_{G_{\mathbf{d}}}^{*}(R_{\mathbf{d}})\simeq H_{G_{\mathbf{d}}}^{*}(\mathbf{pt})\simeq H_{T_{\mathbf{d}}}^{*}(\mathbf{pt})^{\Sigma_{\mathbf{d}}}\simeq\bigotimes_{i\in I}\mathbb{Q}[x_{i,1},\ldots,x_{i,\mathbf{d}_{i}}]^{\Sigma_{\mathbf{d}_{i}}}.

The last isomorphism is provided by the isomorphism
H_{T_{\mathbf{d}}}^{*}(\mathbf{pt})\simeq S(X^{*}(T_{\mathbf{d}})\otimes\mathbb{Q})
, where
X^{*}(T_{\mathbf{d}})
is the group of characters of
T_{\mathbf{d}}
. This is the free abelian group generated by the characters
x_{i,k}\colon T_{\mathbf{d}}\to\mathbb{C}^{\times}
which selects the
k
th
diagonal entry from the
i
th
matrix.

Modules over the Cohomological Hall algebra that come from framed stable representations were introduced in
[

Given a framing vector
\mathbf{f}\in\mathbb{N}^{I}

where

Let

Q
.
We consider

(52)

N

Given a morphism

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X^{\prime}\times_{Y}Y^{\prime}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X^{\prime}\times Y^{\prime}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X\times Y}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\gamma_{f}}

is the graph morphism of

(note that

where

Lemma 6.1
.

Let

be a morphism of smooth algebraic varieties (note that

is automatically an l.c.i. morphism),

be a subvariety and

.
Then we have

f^{!}(y_{1}\cdot y_{2})=f^{!}(y_{1})\cdot f^{!}(y_{2})\in A_{*}(X\times_{Y}(Y_{1}\cap Y_{2})),

where
y_{1}\cdot y_{2}\in A_{*}(Y_{1}\cap Y_{2})
and
f^{!}(y_{i})\in A_{*}(X\times_{Y}Y_{i})
, for

.

Proof.

We obtain from associativity
[
6
, Prop. 8.1.1(a)]
applied to

that

[X]\cdot_{f}(y_{1}\cdot y_{2})=([X]\cdot_{f}y_{1})\cdot_{f}y_{2}.

By
[

, Prop. 8.1.2]
we have

for

.
Therefore

f^{!}(y_{1}\cdot y_{2})=f^{!}(y_{1})\cdot_{f}y_{2}

and we just need to show that

for

.
But we have

6.3.
Coefficient lemma

Let
Y
be a separated algebraic scheme over
\mathbb{C}
.
Let
Z\subset Y
be an irreducible component of dimension
d
, equipped with the reduced subscheme structure, and let
Y^{\prime}
be the closure of
Y\backslash Z
(it is the union of all other irreducible components of
Y
),
also equipped with the reduced subscheme structure.
There is an exact sequence

A_{d}(Z\cap Y^{\prime})\to A_{d}(Z)\oplus A_{d}(Y^{\prime})\to A_{d}(Y)\to 0

(see
[
6
, Ex. 1.3.1]
). As
\dim Z\cap Y^{\prime}<\dim Z=d
, we obtain

A_{d}(Y)\simeq A_{d}(Z)\oplus A_{d}(Y^{\prime})=\mathbb{Z}[Z]\oplus A_{d}(Y^{\prime}).

Therefore every cycle
y\in A_{d}(Y)
can be written in a unique way as

y=m\cdot[Z]+y^{\prime},\qquad m\in\mathbb{Z},\,y^{\prime}\in A_{d}(Y^{\prime}).

We call
m
the
coefficient
of
[Z]
in
y
.

Lemma 6.2
.

Using notation from §
6.1
,
let
U\subset X
be an open subset such that

(1)

Z\col=D\cap U
is irreducible and reduced of dimension
d
,

(2)

D(\sigma_{i})\cap U
has pure dimension
N-r_{i}+k_{i}
, for all
1\leq i\leq n
.

Then the closure
\bar{Z}\subset D
is an irreducible component of
D
and, if we equip
\bar{Z}
with the reduced subscheme structure, the coefficient of
[\bar{Z}]
in the refined product

\mathbb{D}=\prod_{i}\mathbb{D}(\sigma_{i})\in A_{d}(D)
is
1
.

Proof.

We first show that
\bar{Z}
is an irreducible component of
D
.
It is irreducible being the closure of an irreducible set.
Therefore it is contained in some irreducible component
C
of
D
.
If
\bar{Z}\neq C
then
\dim C>d
.
But
C
intersects
U
and thus
\dim C\cap U=\dim C>d=\dim Z
,
while
Z=D\cap U\supset C\cap U
.
A contradiction.

Now we will show that the coefficient of
[\bar{Z}]
in
\mathbb{D}
is one.
We can write

D=\bar{Z}\cup D^{\prime}
, where
D^{\prime}
is the union of all other irreducible components of
D
.
Then

\mathbb{D}=m\cdot[\bar{Z}]+x,\qquad m\in\mathbb{Z},\,x\in A_{d}(D^{\prime}).

Consider the open embedding

j\colon U\hookrightarrow X
.
Then

j^{*}\mathbb{D}=m\cdot j^{*}[\bar{Z}]+j^{*}x=m\cdot[Z]+j^{*}x.

(Note that
j^{*}[\bar{Z}]=[Z]
as both
Z
and
\bar{Z}
are reduced, so
\bar{Z}\cap U=Z
also scheme-theoretically.)
But
D\cap U=Z\subset\bar{Z}
, hence

D^{\prime}\cap U\subset D^{\prime}\cap\bar{Z}
.
Therefore
\dim D^{\prime}\cap U\leq\dim D^{\prime}\cap\bar{Z}<d

and thus
A_{d}(D^{\prime}\cap U)=0
and
j^{*}x=0
.
We conclude that

j^{*}\mathbb{D}=m\cdot[Z]

and we need to show that the coefficient of
[Z]
in
j^{*}\mathbb{D}
is one.
The degeneracy locus
of the homomorphism

j^{*}\sigma_{i}\colon\mathcal{O}_{U}^{k_{i}+1}\to E_{i}|_{U}

is
D(j^{*}\sigma_{i})=D(\sigma_{i})\cap U
.
As degeneracy classes are compatible with flat pull-backs
[
6
, Thm. 14.4(d)]
,
we obtain

j^{*}\mathbb{D}(\sigma_{i})=\mathbb{D}(j^{*}\sigma_{i})\in A_{N-r_{i}+k_{i}}(D(\sigma_{i})\cap U),

where, by abuse of notation, we use the letter
j
also for the embedding
D(\sigma_{i})\cap U\hookrightarrow D(\sigma_{i})
.
As refined products are compatible with pull-backs by Lemma
6.1
, we obtain

j^{*}\mathbb{D}=\prod_{i}j^{*}\mathbb{D}(\sigma_{i})=\prod_{i}\mathbb{D}(j^{*}\sigma_{i})\in A_{d}(D\cap U)=A_{d}(Z)=\mathbb{Z}[Z].

By assumption (2), the scheme

V_{i}=D(\sigma_{i})\cap U=D(j^{*}\sigma_{i}),\qquad 1\leq i\leq n,

has pure dimension
N-r_{i}+k_{i}
.
Therefore by
[
6
, Thm. 14.4(c)]
, we get

\mathbb{D}(j^{*}\sigma_{i})=[D(j^{*}\sigma_{i})]=[V_{i}],\qquad 1\leq i\leq n.

This implies that (see
[
6
, Ex. 8.2.1]
)

j^{*}\mathbb{D}=\prod_{i}\mathbb{D}(j^{*}\sigma_{i})=\prod_{i}[V_{i}]=i(Z,V_{1}\cdot\ldots\cdot V_{n},U)\cdot[Z]\in A_{d}(Z).

We know by
[
6
, Prop. 8.2]
that

m=i(Z,V_{1}\cdot\ldots\cdot V_{n},U)

satisfies

1\leq m\leq l(\mathcal{O}_{D\cap U,Z})
, where
\mathcal{O}_{D\cap U,Z}
is the local ring of
D\cap U=\cap_{i}V_{i}
at the generic point of the irreducible component
Z
.
But as
D\cap U=Z
(which is reduced and irreducible), the length of this artinian local ring is one, hence
m=1
.
∎
