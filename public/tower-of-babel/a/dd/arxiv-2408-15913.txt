A simulation platform for slender, semiflexible, and inextensible fibers with Brownian hydrodynamics and steric repulsion
Ondrej Maxian, Aleksandar Donev

Abstract
The last few years have witnessed an explosion of new numerical methods for filament hydrodynamics. Aside from their ubiquity in biology, physics, and engineering, filaments present unique challenges from an applied-mathematical point of view. Their slenderness, inextensibility, semiflexibility, and meso-scale nature all require numerical methods that can handle multiple lengthscales in the presence of constraints. Accounting for Brownian motion while keeping the dynamics in detailed balance and on the constraint is difficult, as is including a background solvent, which couples the dynamics of multiple filaments together in a suspension. In this paper, we present a simulation platform for deterministic and Brownian inextensible filament dynamics which includes nonlocal fluid dynamics and steric repulsion. For nonlocal hydrodynamics , we define the mobility on a single filament using line integrals of Rotne-Prager-Yamakawa regularized singularities, and numerically preserve the symmetric positive definite property by using a thicker regularization width for the nonlocal integrals than for the self term. For steric repulsion, we introduce a soft local repulsive potential defined as a double-integral over two filaments, then present a scheme to identify and evaluate the nonzero components of the integrand. Using a temporal integrator developed in previous work, we demonstrate that Langevin dynamics sample from the equilibrium distribution of free filament shapes, and that the modeling error in using the thicker regularization is small. We conclude with two examples, sedimenting filaments and cross-linked fiber networks, in which nonlocal hydrodynamics does and does not generate long-range flow fields, respectively. In the latter case, we show that the effect of hydrodynamics can be accounted for through steric repulsion.

Even prior to the recent flurry of activity, the literature in the area of filament dynamics was quite vast, and the reader will pardon any over-simplifications on our part. Painting with a broad brush, there seem to be two main sub-areas: first, there is the question of how to resolve the

Beginning with the first part of the literature, there is by now a long history of using immersed boundary methods to simulate the dynamics of filaments. In the classical immersed boundary method as formulated by Peskin
[
15
,
22

, the fiber has a Lagrangian representation, on which the internal forces can be computed and “spread” to a background fluid grid. Solving the fluid equations on the grid and “interpolating” the result back to the filament yields a velocity of the structure, allowing for an update in its position. The cornerstone of the immersed boundary method is the regularized delta function which is used to spread the force onto the grid, thereby imparting a thickness
(called the hydrodynamic radius)
on which the fluid “feels” the fiber
[
23
]
. In the classical IB method, the width of the regularized delta (or “blob”) function is tied to the width of the Eulerian fluid grid. Other extensions based on the same idea eliminate the fluid grid entirely by using a clever choice of delta function. For example, the force coupling method
[

,
25
,
3

[

29

(notably omitting the inerpolation step). Even though these methods lack a fluid grid, they retain the physics of the blob function representing the thickness of the fiber. As such, modeling slender fibers requires a blob function with width

30
,
31
,
32
,
33

blobs, making even the most optimized methods impractical.

Because of this, filament representations based on line-integrals of regularized singularities have become more popular

,
36
]
. In the case when the line integrals are discretized with direct quadrature, this of course has no advantage, but in the cases when special quadrature (or analytical) techniques are available to integrate the kernel, the spatial discretization can be decoupled from the width of the regularization function. For example, if the integral can be computed analytically for straight lines, a fiber can be discretized into a series of rigid segments

37

,
39
,

A related approach to filament hydrodynamics, which is also based on integral equations, is to use
slender body theory (SBT)
[
16
,
17
,
40
,
41
,
42

to asymptotically remove the smallest lengthscale in the problem. The original result, which was based on singularities and matched asymptotic expansions
[

, can be recast as an asymptotic reduction of the three-dimensional boundary integral equations on the filament surface, whereby the forces and velocities are expanded in a Fourier representation (over the cross section), and only the constant mode is kept

43

and expresses the velocity due to force concentrated

from the point of interest, plus an

,
45
,
1

. Yet methods based on SBT are plagued by a number of issues, foremost among which is the nonlocal finite part integral, which causes the mobility to have negative eigenvalues when the filament is over-resolved

. Approaches to deal with this include regularizing the integrand

,

and putting discretization nodes on the cross sections to avoid singularities

This is especially the case for Brownian dynamics, where the square root of the (symmetric positive definite) mobility is required.

Once the method for the hydrodynamics is specified, it remains to deal with the inextensibility constraint. Most immersed boundary methods use penalty terms for the purposes of treating constraints

,
49
,
50
]
. To eliminate the potentially stiff timescales associated with these forces, recent studies have explicitly solved for the forces required to keep the dynamics on the constraint

[
38
]
for two dimensions).

Returning to our second broad area of the literature, there are a number of methods for Brownian filament fluctuations, but these do not treat the hydrodynamics between filament pieces or distinct filaments. Typically, these methods take the form of a set of beads connected by springs, which are governed by stretching and bending energies and are therefore constraint free
[
21
,
51
,
52
]
. Similar versions of these models fix the distance between beads, which confines the dynamics to a certain constrained manifold

19
,
54

Separate from the chain representation is the matter of Brownian hydrodynamics. Here the slender body theory problem rears its ugly head once again. If the mobility is not guaranteed to be symmetric positive definite (as is the case also in regularized Stokeslets because of a lack of symmetry in the spread/interpolate operations), it is impossible to define its square root, which is necessary for fluctuation-dissipation balance. Thus, the available methods that attempt Brownian motion with a faithful representation of hydrodynamics either approximate the hydrodynamic interactions (not using the full SBT)
[
19
,
55
]
, or use regularized singularities (IB methods)
[
49
]
. Indeed, while the spread/interpolate symmetry in regularized singularity methods gives an automatically SPD mobility,
for slender filaments the number of regularized singularities required to accurately resolve the hydrodynamics is still prohibitively large.

All of this literature gently points us to a path that harmonizes regularized singularities (with their SPD properties) with slender body theory (to remove the smallest lengthscale), combined with constrained dynamics (to remove stiff penalty terms) and efficient handling of stochastic drift terms (to integrate the equations of Brownian dynamics)
[
54
]
. This is more or less the path we have followed over the past few years. First, we showed that slender body theory is asymptotically equivalent to a line integral of Rotne-Prager-Yamakawa (RPY) singularities
[
26
,
56
]
. Second, we developed an efficient quadrature scheme for the self RPY integral, fully decoupling the degrees of freedom from the fiber aspect ratio
[
57
]
. And third, we showed how to implement the special quadrature scheme in a constrained Brownian framework for a single filament
[
58
]
. Until now, however, we were not able to formulate a method for Brownian dynamics of multiple filaments; that is, a method with Brownian fluctuations
and
inter-fiber hydrodynamic interactions that has cost independent of the fiber aspect ratio. The primary purpose of this paper is to present such a method, while collecting, in one readable place, all of the main equations and numerical methods that were developed in previous work and are preserved in this final implementation. Finally, to complete the simulation framework, in this paper we present a novel method for steric repulsion (to keep the fibers well separated). While this method is based on penalty forces rather than newer constraint-based ideas
[
59
,
60
,
6
,
61
]
, our tests show that it is effective at keeping the fibers apart while reducing the required time step size by at most a factor of ten.

The paper is therefore laid out as follows: in Section
2
, we introduce the continuum and discrete equations of motion for deterministic dynamics. Here we leave the mobility
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}
(force-velocity relationship) general, and focus more on the inextensibility constraint. Once a discrete evolution equation is obtained, it becomes straightforward to introduce Brownian motion in a manner consistent with detailed balance
[

5

7

The sedimentation example in particular reveals the benefits of the new “fat-corrected” mobility formulation, while the cross linking fiber example shows the importance of resolving steric interactions.

2
Equations of motion

In this section, we lay out the governing equations for the discrete spatial variables
\boldsymbol{X}
which describe the filament centerlines. This material is entirely a review of previous publications
[
56
,
57
,
58
]
, but it appears here for completeness, and in an effort to collect all of the key information in one place. In that spirit, we begin with a continuum formulation, which only makes sense in the deterministic context. After the continuum formulation, we present the discretization in space, which uses a spectral collocation method and carefully handles the nonlinearities by using two different grids for the tangent vectors and collocation nodes. The Langevin equation for Brownian dynamics follows from the deterministic dynamics and detailed balance.

2.1
Continuum formulation for deterministic dynamics

Let us begin with a continuous curve
\mathbb{X}(s)
which describes the centerline of a filament. In this paper, the filaments are inextensible with constant length
L
, and so
s\in[0,L]
is an arclength parameterization. The filament shapes have an associated bending energy density

\mathcal{E}^{(\kappa)}\left[\mathbb{X}\right]=\frac{\kappa}{2}\int_{0}^{L}\partial_{s}^{2}\mathbb{X}(s)\cdot\partial_{s}^{2}\mathbb{X}(s)\,ds.

(1)

Using the principle of virtual work, the pointwise force density on the filament can be found by taking a variational derivative of the energy (
1
)

\boldsymbol{f}^{(\kappa)}=-\frac{\delta\mathcal{E}^{(\kappa)}}{\delta\mathbb{X}}=-\kappa\partial_{s}^{4}\mathbb{X}.

(2)

The same procedure also gives the free-fiber boundary conditions
[
63
, Sec. 2.1]

\partial_{s}^{2}\mathbb{X}(s=0,L)=\boldsymbol{0}\qquad\partial_{s}^{3}\mathbb{X}(s=0,L)=\boldsymbol{0}.

(3)

Since these “natural” boundary conditions come from differentiating the energy functional in continuum, they can be implemented in the discrete setting by discretizing the energy (
1
) directly and differentiating the result (see Section
2.2.2
).

To obtain velocity from forces, we introduce the linear mobility operator
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}\left[\mathbb{X}\right]
, and set the velocity of the filament centerline
\boldsymbol{U}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}\boldsymbol{f}
. Note that because the power dissipated in the fluid is always positive,

\langle\boldsymbol{U},\boldsymbol{f}\rangle_{L^{2}}=\langle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}\boldsymbol{f},\boldsymbol{f}\rangle_{L^{2}}>0,

(4)

the operator
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}
is symmetric positive definite with respect to the
L^{2}
inner product. Denoting any external force density (e.g., gravity) by
\boldsymbol{f}^{\text{(ext)}}
, the equation of motion for the fiber centerline is

\partial_{t}\mathbb{X}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}\left[\mathbb{X}\right]\left(\boldsymbol{f}^{(\kappa)}+\boldsymbol{\lambda}+\boldsymbol{f}^{\text{(ext)}}\right).

(5)

Here
\boldsymbol{\lambda}(s)
is a constraint force which enforces the inextensibility constraint,

\mathbb{T}(s)\cdot\mathbb{T}(s)=1,

(6)

where
\mathbb{T}=\partial_{s}\mathbb{X}
.

2.1.1
Closing the equations: the kinematic operator
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}

To close the equation of motion, we differentiate the constraint (
6
) with respect to time to yield

\partial_{t}\mathbb{T}\cdot\mathbb{T}=0\rightarrow\partial_{t}\mathbb{T}=\boldsymbol{\Omega}\times\mathbb{T},

(7)

where
\boldsymbol{\Omega}(s)
denotes the rotation rate of the tangent vectors on the unit sphere. Integrating (
7
), we obtain a representation of all inextensible motions

\partial_{t}\mathbb{X}(s)=\boldsymbol{U}_{\text{MP}}+\int_{L/2}^{s}\left(\boldsymbol{\Omega}\left(s^{\prime}\right)\times\mathbb{T}\left(s^{\prime}\right)\right)\,ds^{\prime}:=\left(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}\boldsymbol{\alpha}\right)(s),

(8)

in terms of the degrees of freedom
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=\left(\boldsymbol{\Omega},\boldsymbol{U}_{\text{MP}}\right)
, which are the tangent vector rotation rates
\boldsymbol{\Omega}

and velocity of the fiber midpoint
\boldsymbol{U}_{\text{MP}}
. Equation (
8
) defines the
kinematic
operator
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}\left[\mathbb{X}\right]
.

While the representation of inextensible motions (
8
) reduces the number of degrees of freedom (removing rotations parallel to the tangent vector), it does not close the dynamics (
5
) because it does not constrain the forces
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
. This needs to be done by imposing the principle of virtual work, which states that the constraint forces
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
dissipate no power in the fluid with respect to all possible inextensible motions
[
64
,
56
]

\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}=\langle\boldsymbol{\lambda},\boldsymbol{U}\rangle_{L^{2}}=\langle\boldsymbol{\lambda},\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}\boldsymbol{\alpha}\rangle_{L^{2}}=\langle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}^{*}\boldsymbol{\lambda},\boldsymbol{\alpha}\rangle=0\rightarrow\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}^{*}\boldsymbol{\lambda}=\boldsymbol{0}.

(9)

The last equality defines
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}^{*}[\mathbb{X}]
as the
L^{2}
adjoint of
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}
. In numerical methods, this definition is sufficient, as we will form
\boldsymbol{K}
as a matrix, then apply an
L^{2}
weights matrix to
\boldsymbol{K}^{T}
to obtain a representation of
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}^{*}
.

In previous formulations of inextensibility based on Euler beam theory
[
44
]
, the equations of motion are closed by differentiating the constraint with respect to time to obtain
\partial_{s}\left(\partial_{t}\mathbb{X}\right)\cdot\mathbb{T}=0
, then substituting
\boldsymbol{\lambda}=\partial_{s}\left(T\mathbb{T}\right)
to obtain a
line tension equation
for the tensions
T
, which is solved simultaneously with the equation of motion (
5
). We choose to close the formulation differently for two main reasons: first, solving for the rotation rates
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
is advantageous in numerical methods, since we can rotate the tangent vectors by those rates to keep the dynamics exactly on the constraint. Second, the kinematic constraint
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}^{*}\boldsymbol{\lambda}=0
allows us to eliminate
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
and write a closed-form equation for
\mathbb{X}
, which will allow us to write the Langevin equation for thermal fluctuations. Nevertheless, it is instructive to insert the definition of
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}
in (
8
) into the virtual work constraint (
9
) to show that the two formulations are equivalent,

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{L}\boldsymbol{\lambda}(s)\cdot\boldsymbol{U}_{\text{MP}}+\int_{0}^{L}\boldsymbol{\lambda}(s)\cdot\left(\int_{0}^{s}\left(\boldsymbol{\Omega}\left(s^{\prime}\right)\times\mathbb{T}\left(s^{\prime}\right)\right)\,ds^{\prime}\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{L}\boldsymbol{\lambda}(s)\cdot\boldsymbol{U}_{\text{MP}}+\int_{0}^{L}\left(\boldsymbol{\Omega}\left(s^{\prime}\right)\times\mathbb{T}\left(s^{\prime}\right)\right)\cdot\left(\int_{s^{\prime}}^{L}\boldsymbol{\lambda}(s)\,ds\right)\,ds^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{L}\boldsymbol{\lambda}(s)\cdot\boldsymbol{U}_{\text{MP}}+\int_{0}^{L}\boldsymbol{\Omega}\left(s^{\prime}\right)\cdot\left(\mathbb{T}\left(s^{\prime}\right)\times\left(\int_{s^{\prime}}^{L}\boldsymbol{\lambda}(s)\,ds\right)\right)\,ds^{\prime}=0.

(10)

Since (
10
) must hold for all
\boldsymbol{U}_{\text{MP}}
and
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
, we have that

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}^{*}\boldsymbol{\lambda}=\begin{pmatrix}\displaystyle{\mathbb{T}\left(s\right)\times\left(\int_{s}^{L}\boldsymbol{\lambda}(s^{\prime})\,ds^{\prime}\right)}\\[10.0pt]
\displaystyle{\int_{0}^{L}\boldsymbol{\lambda}(s)}\end{pmatrix}=\boldsymbol{0}.

(11)

If the fiber is not straight, the first condition implies that
\boldsymbol{\lambda}(s)=\partial_{s}\left(T(s)\mathbb{T}(s)\right)
for some scalar function
T(s)
with
T(L)=0
, while the second equation gives
T(0)=0
. In the case of a straight fiber, we have
\boldsymbol{\lambda}(s)=\left(\partial_{s}T(s)\right)\mathbb{T}
, and we can only recover
T(L)=T(0)
, which is logical since tension is only defined up to a constant if the filament is straight. This demonstrates that our kinematic formulation is equivalent to the more standard line tension equation with free fiber boundary conditions
T(0)=T(L)=0

[
56
, Sec. 3]
.

2.1.2
Continuum saddle point system

To conclude the continuum formulation, we insert
the kinematic equations (
8
) and (
9
) into the mobility equation (
5
) to obtain a saddle point system in the constraint forces
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
and degrees of freedom
\boldsymbol{\alpha}

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}&\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}\\
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}^{*}&\boldsymbol{0}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\lambda}\\
\boldsymbol{\alpha}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}\left(-\delta\mathcal{E}^{(\kappa)}/\delta\mathbb{X}+\boldsymbol{f}^{\text{(ext)}}\right)\\
\boldsymbol{0}\end{pmatrix}.

(12)

This system is written over a single fiber, where
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}
is the kinematic operator (
8
), and
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
and
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
are the constraint forces and motions over that fiber. But it can also be extended to multiple fibers by slight abuse of notation, in which case
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}
is a block diagonal operator whose diagonal entries are the
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}
operators for each fiber, and
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
and
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
are the list of all constraint forces and kinematic motions. The only distinction between these two systems comes when we include nonlocal hydrodynamics. In that case the mobility operator
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}
is
not
block diagonal, and couples the fibers together.

2.2
Spatial discretization

Figure 1:
Spectral discretization. The discrete degrees of freedom
\boldsymbol{\tau}
and
\boldsymbol{X}_{\text{MP}}
define a set of Chebyshev points
\boldsymbol{X}
and continuum interpolant
\mathbb{X}(s)
.

The first decision that has to be made when discretizing in space is the representation of the fiber centerline
\mathbb{X}(s)
. For slender filaments, the mobility
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}
is typically written as an integral over the fiber centerline. This leads us down the path of a spectral discretization
[
1
]
, whereby we can use a set of collocation nodes to define a representation of
\mathbb{X}(s)
and develop quadrature schemes for integral equations. The smoothness of the fiber shapes we are interested in reinforces this idea, as spectral methods allow us to represent fibers with only a few modes, removing the need to resolve (irrelevant) high-order bending modes. However, it leaves inextensibility unclear: in spectral methods, the fiber shapes are ultimately polynomials, and it is impossible for the polynomial
\partial_{s}\mathbb{X}\cdot\partial_{s}\mathbb{X}-1
to be zero
everywhere
without being identically zero. And, if the space of polynomials to work in is unclear, how will we write an overdamped Langevin equation over a continuous constraint we do not understand?

Our discretization philosophy is to take advantage of the dual nature of a spectral collocation discretization: on the one hand, we have a set of discrete collocation points and tangent vectors which are the degrees of freedom in the simulation. But on the other, these points give us access to a continuum interpolant
\mathbb{X}(s)
which we can use to design efficient quadrature schemes for the mobility of slender filaments. Concretely, as shown in Fig.
1
, the fiber is defined by a set of interior tangent vectors
\boldsymbol{\tau}
on a
type 1
(not including the endpoints) Chebyshev grid of size
N
, together with the fiber midpoint
\boldsymbol{X}_{\text{MP}}
. From this, the positions of the fiber
\boldsymbol{X}
can be obtained
exactly
(no integration error) on a grid of size
N_{x}=N+1
via integration

\boldsymbol{X}=\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}}\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\tau}\\
\boldsymbol{X}_{\text{MP}}\end{pmatrix}.

(13)

The exact expression for
\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}}
in terms of the constituent matrices is given in
[
58
, Sec. 2.1.1]
; here it suffices to note that
\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}}
is an invertible matrix which maps the constrained degrees of freedom
\boldsymbol{\tau}
to the fiber position (with the additional constant of the fiber midpoint).

2.2.1
L^{2}
inner products and the mobility

While the continuum equations (
12
) are formulated in terms of force densities
\boldsymbol{f}
, in discrete variables we always need to work with
forces

\boldsymbol{F}
, and the matrix
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}
which maps forces to velocity. To understand how to relate force to force density, we consider the
L^{2}
inner product for power dissipated in the fluid

\mathcal{P}=\langle\boldsymbol{f},\boldsymbol{U}\rangle_{L^{2}}=\boldsymbol{f}^{T}\boldsymbol{E}_{N_{x}\rightarrow 2N_{x}}^{T}\boldsymbol{W}_{2N_{x}}\boldsymbol{E}_{N_{x}\rightarrow 2N_{x}}\boldsymbol{U}:=\boldsymbol{f}^{T}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{W}}\boldsymbol{U}:=\boldsymbol{F}^{T}\boldsymbol{U}

(14)

In the case when
\boldsymbol{f}
and
\boldsymbol{U}
are polynomials defined on a Chebyshev grid of size
N_{x}
, the computation (
14
) gives the power
exactly
by first upsampling both
\boldsymbol{f}
and
\boldsymbol{U}
to a grid of size
2N_{x}
.
The upsampling, which is denoted by the extension matrix
\boldsymbol{E}_{N_{x}\rightarrow 2N_{x}}
, transforms the data from a coarse to fine grid by using the coarse grid values to compute a Chebyshev polynomial, which is evaluated pointwise on the finer grid to give the upsampled representation. After upsampling, integration is performed on the finer grid
by applying the integration weights (diagonal matrix
\boldsymbol{W}_{2N_{x}}
). The last two equalities define the (symmetric positive definite)
L^{2}
inner product weights matrix
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{W}}=\widetilde{\boldsymbol{W}}^{T}
, which we use to define a force
\boldsymbol{F}=\widetilde{\boldsymbol{W}}\boldsymbol{f}
from force density.

Let us now reconsider the power equation with the mobility (
4
). Letting
\boldsymbol{M}
be the discretization of the matrix which maps force
densities
to forces, the inner product (
4
) can be written in terms of forces as

\mathcal{P}=\langle\boldsymbol{f},\boldsymbol{M}\boldsymbol{f}\rangle_{L^{2}}=\langle\widetilde{\boldsymbol{W}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{F},\boldsymbol{M}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{W}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{F}\rangle_{L^{2}}=\boldsymbol{F}^{T}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{W}}^{-1}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{W}}\boldsymbol{M}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{W}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{F}:=\boldsymbol{F}^{T}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}\boldsymbol{F}>0,

(15)

which defines the SPD matrix
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}=\boldsymbol{M}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{W}}^{-1}
that acts on
forces
to give velocity.

2.2.2
Bending force

Once we define the
L^{2}
inner product, discretization of the bending energy (
1
) is straightforward. We compute the bending energy exactly via the
L^{2}
inner product

\mathcal{E}^{(\kappa)}\left[\boldsymbol{X}\right]=\frac{\kappa}{2}\boldsymbol{X}^{T}\left(\boldsymbol{D}_{N_{x}}^{2}\right)^{T}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{W}}\boldsymbol{D}_{N_{x}}^{2}\boldsymbol{X}:=\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{X}^{T}\boldsymbol{L}\boldsymbol{X}.

(16)

The bending
force
is now the derivative of the energy with respect to
\boldsymbol{X}
,

\boldsymbol{F}^{(\kappa)}=-\frac{\partial\mathcal{E}^{(\kappa)}}{\partial\boldsymbol{X}}=-\boldsymbol{L}\boldsymbol{X}.

(17)

This type of discretization, which is common in immersed boundary methods
[
22
,
65
]
, enforces the boundary conditions naturally, in essence because minimizing the energy forces the boundary terms to be zero.

2.2.3
Kinematic matrices

The discretization of the kinematic operator
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}
is straightforward once we have a representation for the filament and
L^{2}
inner product. We recall the definition of
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}
in (
8
) and the mapping from the tangent vectors to the nodes (
13
). Letting
\boldsymbol{C}\left[\boldsymbol{\tau}\right]
be a matrix encoding
\boldsymbol{C}\boldsymbol{\Omega}=\boldsymbol{\tau}\times\boldsymbol{\Omega}
at each point, the product
\boldsymbol{K}\boldsymbol{\alpha}
is discretized as

\boldsymbol{K}\boldsymbol{\alpha}=\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}}\begin{pmatrix}-\boldsymbol{C}&\boldsymbol{0}\\
\boldsymbol{0}&\boldsymbol{I}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\Omega}\\
\boldsymbol{U}_{\text{MP}}\end{pmatrix},

(18)

where now
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
represents a
3N
vector of rotation rates for each point. The matrix
\boldsymbol{K}
is a square
3N_{x}\times 3(N+1)
matrix, with a null space of size
N
which contains the tangent vectors at each of the
N
tangent vector nodes.

The constraint on the
forces

\boldsymbol{\Lambda}:=\widetilde{\boldsymbol{W}}\boldsymbol{\lambda}
follows from the virtual work principle (
9
)

P_{\lambda}=\langle\boldsymbol{\lambda},\boldsymbol{K}\boldsymbol{\alpha}\rangle_{L^{2}}=\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{T}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{W}}\boldsymbol{K}\boldsymbol{\alpha}=\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{T}\boldsymbol{K}^{T}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{W}}\boldsymbol{\lambda}=\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{T}\boldsymbol{K}^{T}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}=\boldsymbol{0}.

(19)

Since this must hold for all
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
, we have the simple equation
\boldsymbol{K}^{T}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}=\boldsymbol{0}
.

2.2.4
Discrete saddle point system

Summing up, the discrete saddle point system is the continuum saddle point system (
12
), but rewritten in terms of
forces
which contain the
L^{2}
inner product weights,

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}&\boldsymbol{K}\\
\boldsymbol{K}^{T}&\boldsymbol{0}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\\
\boldsymbol{\alpha}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}\left(-\boldsymbol{L}\boldsymbol{X}+\boldsymbol{F}^{\text{(ext)}}\right)\\
\boldsymbol{0}\end{pmatrix},

(20)

and the deterministic dynamics can be obtained by eliminating
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
to obtain

\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{X}=-\widehat{\boldsymbol{N}}\boldsymbol{L}\boldsymbol{X},\qquad\widehat{\boldsymbol{N}}=\boldsymbol{K}\left(\boldsymbol{K}^{T}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{K}\right)^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{K}^{T}.

(21)

2.3
Langevin equation for Brownian fluctuations

Once the deterministic dynamics are known, we can formulate an overdamped Langevin equation which is in detailed balance with respect to the energy (
16
), constrained on the inextensibility of the tangent vectors. This requires two pieces: first, in order to satisfy fluctuation-dissipation balance, the covariance of the noise must be proportional to
\widehat{\boldsymbol{N}}
. In other words, coefficients of the noise should be proportional to
\widehat{\boldsymbol{N}}^{1/2}
, where
\widehat{\boldsymbol{N}}^{1/2}
(which is not necessarily unique) satisfies

\widehat{\boldsymbol{N}}^{1/2}\left(\widehat{\boldsymbol{N}}^{1/2}\right)^{T}=\widehat{\boldsymbol{N}}.

(22)

Secondly, because the mobility
\widehat{\boldsymbol{N}}
depends on the fiber positions/tangent vectors, the noise is multiplicative. Thus, when the SDE for the motion of the filament collocation points is formulated in an Ito sense, stochastic drift terms are required to ensure detailed equilibrium
[
54
, Sec. II.B.2]
. The Ito Langevin equation governing the evolution of the fiber centerline is given by
[
58
, Eq. (35)]

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{X}

\displaystyle=-\widehat{\boldsymbol{N}}\boldsymbol{L}\boldsymbol{X}+k_{B}T\left(\partial_{\boldsymbol{X}}\cdot\widehat{\boldsymbol{N}}\right)+\sqrt{2k_{B}T}\widehat{\boldsymbol{N}}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{W}}

(23a)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{N}}\boldsymbol{L}\boldsymbol{X}+\sqrt{2k_{B}T}\widehat{\boldsymbol{N}}\circ\widehat{\boldsymbol{N}}^{-1/2}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{W}},

(23b)

where
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{W}}
is a collection of standard i.i.d. Gaussian white noise processes (the formal derivatives of Brownian motion), the divergence of a matrix is defined as

\left(\partial_{\boldsymbol{X}}\cdot\boldsymbol{A}\right)_{j}:=\sum_{k}\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{A}_{jk}}{\partial\boldsymbol{X}_{k}}

(24)

and
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}
denotes equality in distributions of trajectories. The second equation (
23b
) is the split Stratonovich-Ito
[
54
]
or kinetic
[
66
]
form, where the terms before the
\circ
are evaluated
at the mid-point of the time interval (Stratanovich interpretation), while those after the
\circ
are evaluated at the beginning of the time interval (Ito interpreation). When we present our temporal integrator for (
23b
), which was developed in
[
58
]
and shown to be consistent with (
23a
) therein, it will be helpful to keep (
23b
) in mind.

At first glance, computing
\widehat{\boldsymbol{N}}^{1/2}
is quite difficult, since (
21
) suggests it requires the square root of a resistance solve. In fact, however, solving the saddle point system

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}&\boldsymbol{K}\\
\boldsymbol{K}^{T}&\boldsymbol{0}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\\
\boldsymbol{\alpha}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{\eta}\\
\boldsymbol{0}\end{pmatrix},

(25)

where
\boldsymbol{\eta}
is an i.i.d. vector of standard normal random variables, gives

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{X}=\boldsymbol{K}\boldsymbol{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\widehat{\boldsymbol{N}}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}^{-1/2}\boldsymbol{\eta}=\widehat{\boldsymbol{N}}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{\eta},

where the last equality holds because

\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{N}}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}^{-1/2}\left(\widehat{\boldsymbol{N}}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}^{-1/2}\right)^{T}

\displaystyle=\widehat{\boldsymbol{N}}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}^{-1}\widehat{\boldsymbol{N}}=\boldsymbol{K}\left(\boldsymbol{K}^{T}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{K}\right)^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{K}^{T}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{K}\left(\boldsymbol{K}^{T}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{K}\right)^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{K}^{T}=\widehat{\boldsymbol{N}}.

Thus, solving a saddle point system with right hand side
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{\eta}
generates the noise
\widehat{\boldsymbol{N}}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{\eta}
, and only a single resistance solve is necessary
[
54
, Sec. II(B)]
.

In
[
58
]
, we showed that the Langevin equation (
23
) samples from the equilibrium Gibbs-Boltzmann distribution

\displaystyle P_{\text{eq}}\left(\bar{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\right)

\displaystyle=Z^{-1}\exp{\left(-\mathcal{E}^{(\kappa)}(\bar{\boldsymbol{\tau}})/k_{B}T\right)}\prod_{p=1}^{N}\delta\left(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\{p\}}^{T}\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\{p\}}-1\right),

(26)

where the discrete energy
\mathcal{E}^{(\kappa)}
is defined in (
16
),
Z
is a normalization constant, and the product of
\delta
functions encodes the constraint that the tangent vectors
\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\{p\}}
are independently uniformly distributed on the unit sphere for
p=1,\dots N
. This distribution is actually a postulate more than a fact; for worm-like chains, the tangent vectors are all equally spaced and thus independent. But for the spectral discretization shown in Fig.
1
, the tangent vectors near the fiber endpoints are quite close together, and it is doubtful that they are truly independent. Nevertheless, we showed in
[
58
]
that
Markov Chain Monte Carlo
(MCMC) samples from this equilibrium distribution converge to the theoretical end-to-end distribution of a free filaments as
N
increases.

3
Mobility

This section discusses how to compute the action of the mobility
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}
and its square root
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}^{1/2}
, both of which are necessary in Brownian dynamics simulations. To do this, we first define the velocity on filament
i
as the sum of regularized Rotne-Prager-Yamakawa singularities over all other filaments. Then, we introduce a simple SPD “reference” mobility matrix which is based on global oversampling to compute the integrals accurately. We demonstrate through numerical experiments that the accuracy of the reference mobility is limited by the self term; some
1/\epsilon
points are required to resolve it accurately. It therefore becomes impractical for many filaments. As a result, the third part of this section seeks an SPD mobility where the self term is separated from the nonlocal terms, i.e., where the mobility can be written as

\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}=\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}_{1}+\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}_{2},

(27)

where both
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}_{1}
and
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}_{2}
are SPD matrices, and
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}_{1}
is a local operation while
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}_{2}
is a global operation. When the mobility is split in this way, the expectation of the covariance of the noise
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}_{1}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{W}}_{1}+\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}_{2}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{W}}_{2}
(where
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{W}}_{1}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{W}}_{2}
are independent white noise processes) is equal to
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}
, as required to satisfy fluctuation-dissipation balance
[
67
,
32
]
. We demonstrate that a combination of special quadrature for the local mobility, plus a “fattening” the nonlocal part of the mobility, gives a splitting with the desired properties and a cost independent of the fiber slenderness.

3.1
The definition of the mobility

In
[
57
]
, we motivated our choice of mobility (for a single fiber) by appealing to the immersed boundary or regularized singularity literature
[
15
,
28
,
24
,
26
,
27
]
. In our approach, the centerline of the fiber is modeled as a series of infinitely-many regularized delta functions (“blobs”), which in the Rotne-Prager-Yamakawa (RPY) case are surface delta functions on spheres of radius
{\hat{a}}
. For two blobs, a regularized kernel
\boldsymbol{M}_{\text{RPY}}
is obtained by solving the Stokes equations with forcing given by the regularized delta function centered at a point
\boldsymbol{y}
, then averaging the resulting fluid velocity field at another surface delta function centered at a point
\boldsymbol{x}

[
26
,
27
]

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{M}_{\text{RPY}}\left(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y};{\hat{a}}\right)=\frac{1}{8\pi\mu}\begin{cases}\left(\dfrac{\boldsymbol{I}+\widehat{\boldsymbol{R}}\widehat{\boldsymbol{R}}}{R}+\dfrac{2{\hat{a}}^{2}}{3}\dfrac{\boldsymbol{I}-3\widehat{\boldsymbol{R}}\widehat{\boldsymbol{R}}}{R^{3}}\right)&R>2{\hat{a}}\\[8.0pt]
\left(\left(\dfrac{4}{3{\hat{a}}}-\dfrac{3R}{8{\hat{a}}^{2}}\right)\boldsymbol{I}+\dfrac{R}{8{\hat{a}}^{2}}\widehat{\boldsymbol{R}}\widehat{\boldsymbol{R}}\right)&R\leq 2{\hat{a}}\end{cases}.

(28)

When the blobs are well separated (
R>2{\hat{a}}
), the kernel is the sum of a Stokeslet and an
\mathcal{O}({\hat{a}}^{2})
multiple of the doublet, while when the blobs overlap there is a nonsingular correction which tends to the classical Stokes drag mobility
1/(6\pi{\hat{a}}\mu)
as
\boldsymbol{x}\rightarrow\boldsymbol{y}
. Because of the symmetric nature of the “spreading” and “averaging” of force, the grand mobility matrix for a series of blobs is symmetric positive definite, which is a vital property for thermal fluctuations.

Once the regularized kernel for two blobs is defined, the velocity on fiber
i
is defined as an integral of the regularized kernel

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{U}}^{(i)}(s)=\int_{0}^{L}\boldsymbol{M}_{\text{RPY}}\left({\mathbb{X}}^{(i)}(s),{\mathbb{X}}^{(i)}(s^{\prime});{\hat{a}}\right){\boldsymbol{f}}^{(i)}(s^{\prime})\,ds^{\prime}

(29)

\displaystyle+\sum_{j\neq i}\int_{0}^{L}\boldsymbol{M}_{\text{RPY}}\left({\mathbb{X}}^{(i)}(s),{\mathbb{X}}^{(j)}(s^{\prime});{\hat{a}}^{*}\right){\boldsymbol{f}}^{(j)}(s^{\prime})\,ds^{\prime},

where
the superscript
(i)
indexes the fibers
, and we have used a distinct radius (
{\hat{a}}
) for the self term than for the nonlocal terms (
{\hat{a}}^{*}
) for the sake of generality. While we will typically assume
{\hat{a}}={\hat{a}}^{*}
, we will later see that it is numerically useful to set
{\hat{a}}^{*}>{\hat{a}}
. Indeed, for fibers that are well separated, the radius enters only in the doublet part of the regularized singularity (
28
), and as such has a negligible effect.

In the case of a single fiber (first line of (
29
))
with radius
a
, we previously showed
[
56
, Appendix A]
through asymptotic analysis that the choice of regularized radius

{\hat{a}}=\frac{e^{3/2}}{4}a

(30)

gives the same mobility as slender body theory
[
17
,
40
,
41
,
44
]
to
\mathcal{O}({\hat{a}})
. Unlike slender body theory, however, which suffers from ill-posedness on lengthscales less than
{\hat{a}}
(leading to negative eigenvalues), the RPY formulation has the advantage of giving a well-posed SPD mobility with a well-defined square root. Thus, using the RPY integral with the radius (
30
) imparts all the benefits of slender body theory (in terms of asymptotic accuracy to the true three-dimensional Stokes equations
[
68
]
), without the associated ill-posedness. In our notation, we will often switch back and forth between the
true
fiber radius
a
and aspect ratio
\epsilon=a/L
, and the RPY radius
{\hat{a}}
and aspect ratio
\hat{\epsilon}={\hat{a}}/L
.

3.2
Upsampled mobility

We first define a naive, obviously SPD, discretization of the mobility which will facilitate comparisons with more efficient methods. Let us set
{\hat{a}}^{*}={\hat{a}}
in (
29
) and consider a simple way to
compute
the velocity at a point on fiber
i
. Given a set of forces
\boldsymbol{F}
defined at Chebyshev grid points (either on one or many fibers), we form a reference mobility by applying the following steps

1.

Obtain the force density
\boldsymbol{f}=\widetilde{\boldsymbol{W}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{F}
.

2.

Extend the force density to an upsampled grid with
N_{u}
points by resampling the Chebyshev interpolant,
\boldsymbol{f}_{u}=\boldsymbol{E}_{u}\boldsymbol{f}
.

3.

Convert these force densities to forces on the upsampled grid
\boldsymbol{F}_{u}=\boldsymbol{W}_{u}\boldsymbol{f}_{u}

4.

Apply the grand RPY mobility matrix to obtain velocities on the upsampled grid
\boldsymbol{U}_{u}=\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}_{\text{RPY},u}\boldsymbol{F}_{u}
. If there are multiple fibers, this is the step which encodes the nonlocal interactions.

5.

Perform a least-squares projection in
L^{2}
to obtain the velocity on the original
N
point grid,
\boldsymbol{U}=\widetilde{\boldsymbol{W}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{E}_{u}^{T}\boldsymbol{W}_{u}\boldsymbol{U}_{u}
.
Here the
L^{2}
weights matrix
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{W}}
, which is computed in (
14
) by going through a grid of size
2N_{x}
, is the same when computed on a grid of larger size, since any grid of size
N_{u}\geq 2N_{x}
eliminates aliasing errors. Since least-squares projection in
L^{2}
would use the matrix computed on a grid of size
N_{u}
, this step implies the restriction
N_{u}\geq 2N_{x}
.

Putting these steps together, we arrive at a
reference oversampled mobility
matrix

\displaystyle\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}=\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}_{\text{os}}:=\widetilde{\boldsymbol{W}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{E}_{u}^{T}\boldsymbol{W}_{u}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}_{\text{RPY},u}\boldsymbol{W}_{u}\boldsymbol{E}_{u}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{W}}^{-1},

(31)

which has the obvious Cholesky factor

\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}_{\text{os}}^{1/2}=\widetilde{\boldsymbol{W}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{E}_{u}^{T}\boldsymbol{W}_{u}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}_{\text{RPY},u}^{1/2}.

(32)

The most expensive part of applying the mobility is the application
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}_{\text{RPY},u}
on the upsampled grid, which can be done in linear time on periodic domains using the positively-split Ewald method
[
32
]
(as we do here), or in free space using the fast multipole method
[
69
,
70
]
. Likewise, we apply the square root of the mobility (
32
) by using the positively-split Ewald method implemented in the UAMMD GPU library
[
71
]
. This applies
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}_{\text{RPY},u}^{1/2}
on the
upsampled
grid, with the premultipliers in (
32
) downsampling to the original Chebyshev grid.

The mobility (
31
) is a robust, SPD mobility which can be applied in linear time. However, as we demonstrate in Appendix
A.2
, the number of oversampling points required to resolve the
self
interaction to a given accuracy scales as
1/\epsilon
, while the number required to resolve the
nonlocal
interactions is
\mathcal{O}(1)
. Consequently, making the mobility (
31
) practical for slender filament suspensions requires replacing the block diagonal entries with a more efficient method.

3.3
Special quadrature for the self term

We previously developed
[
57
]
a special quadrature scheme for the self-RPY integral, which is summarized in Appendix
A
. This scheme takes the force density
\boldsymbol{f}
on the Chebyshev grid as input, and computes a velocity
\boldsymbol{U}=\boldsymbol{M}_{\text{SQ}}\boldsymbol{f}=\boldsymbol{M}_{\text{SQ}}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{W}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{F}
. Thus, the special quadrature mobility matrix can be written as
\boldsymbol{M}_{\text{SQ}}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{W}}^{-1}
, but this matrix is not even symmetric, much less positive definite. However, because it is localized to a single fiber, we can perform dense linear algebra operations on it, such as symmetrizing it and truncating its eigenvalues, thus resulting in

\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}_{\text{SQS}}=\left[\frac{1}{2}\left(\boldsymbol{M}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{W}}^{-1}+\widetilde{\boldsymbol{W}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{M}^{T}\right)\right]_{\lambda^{*}}

(33)

where
\left[\cdot\right]_{\lambda^{*}}
denotes the operation of computing an eigenvalue decomposition, and setting all eigenvalues less than
{\lambda^{*}}
as equal to
{\lambda^{*}}
. As shown in Appendix
A.2
, truncating the eigenvalues causes a loss of accuracy, but only at low tolerances. While we previously used (
31
) as a basis to set the eigenvalue threshold
[
58
]
, here we find smaller tolerance to give higher accuracy without compromising robustness. We therefore use
\lambda^{*}=10^{-3}
throughout this paper.

As shown in Appendix
A.2
, the special quadrature scheme can correctly resolve the self velocity with a cost independent of
\epsilon
. In fact, for moderate numbers (
N_{x}\approx 25
) of collocation points, we find that the special quadrature scheme is as accurate as using
N_{u}=2/\epsilon
oversampling points in the reference mobility (
31
). Thus, replacing the block diagonal terms of (
31
) with the special quadrature matrix (
33
) allows for
\mathcal{O}(1)
oversampling points
(which are now only required for nonlocal terms)
as
\epsilon
decreases.

3.4
Nonlocal mobility

Therefore, a first guess for an
\epsilon
-independent nonlocal mobility is a replacement of the block diagonal parts of (
31
) with special quadrature

\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}=\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}_{\text{os}}-\text{BDiag}\left\{\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}_{\text{os}}\right\}+\text{BDiag}\left\{\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}_{\text{SQS}}\right\}.

(34)

Because the oversampled mobility
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}_{\text{os}}
is only used for the nonlocal velocity and not the self term, a given accuracy could be obtained using
N_{u}=\mathcal{O}(1)
points (with respect to
\hat{\epsilon}
). The special quadrature, which has cost independent of
\hat{\epsilon}
, then gives the self velocity contributions. Thus the mobility (
34
) has cost independent of
\hat{\epsilon}
.

While this is an appealing property, for the purposes of fluctuations we need to split the mobility into a sum of two SPD matrices (see (
27
) and the discussion there). While
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}_{\text{os}}
is SPD by construction, the block diagonal correction
\text{BDiag}\left\{\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}_{\text{SQS}}\right\}-\text{BDiag}\left\{\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}_{\text{os}}\right\}
is not SPD; in fact, the left panel of Fig.
2
shows that it has mostly negative eigenvalues. If we conceptualize the special quadrature as the limit
N_{u}\rightarrow\infty
, then we can think of this result as a consequence of how the fluid “sees” the fiber. In the extreme limit
N_{u}=1
, the mobility becomes that of a sphere,
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}_{\text{os}}\sim 1/\hat{\epsilon}
, while in the limit
N_{u}\rightarrow\infty
, the mobility becomes filamentous,
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}_{\text{SQS}}\sim\log{\hat{\epsilon}}
. Thus, the difference matrix
\text{BDiag}\left\{\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}_{\text{SQS}}\right\}-\text{BDiag}\left\{\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}_{\text{os}}\right\}
will have almost exclusively negative eigenvalues (an example for a filament with
N_{x}=25
is shown in Fig.
2
).

Figure 2:
Eigenvalues of possible corrections to the nonlocal mobility for
\hat{\epsilon}=10^{-3}
. Left panel: correcting the mobility via (
34
), by subtracting oversampled quadrature (with number of oversampled points
N_{u}
) and adding the special quadrature, gives a correction matrix with negative eigenvalues. Right panel: using a fatter radius
{\hat{a}}^{*}
for the nonlocal mobility via (
35
), then using the correction matrix
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}_{\text{SQS}}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}_{\text{SQS}}^{(*)}
, always gives a symmetric positive definite correction. For these plots,
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}_{\text{SQS}}
is symmetrized, but the eigenvalues are not truncated (truncating them does not change the plots by eye). These plots are generated with a specific fiber (
N_{x}=25
and
\hat{\epsilon}=10^{-3}
), but are unchanged when we vary the shape.

We propose counteracting this drop in the eigenvalues via a smoothing of the oversampled mobility matrix. Returning to the generalized mobility (
29
), we set a larger
(“fatter”)
RPY radius
{\hat{a}}^{*}>{\hat{a}}
, which is independent of the true radius
{\hat{a}}
. We then propose changing the
nonlocal
kernel to use this larger radius, denoting this by
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}_{\text{os}}^{(*)}
. That is,
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}_{\text{os}}^{(*)}
is the reference mobility (
31
), but with the larger radius
{\hat{a}}^{*}
. We then set the total mobility

\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}=\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}_{\text{os}}^{(*)}-\text{BDiag}\left\{\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}_{\text{SQS}}^{(*)}\right\}+\text{BDiag}\left\{\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}_{\text{SQS}}\right\},

(35)

i.e., we compute the oversampled fattened mobility for the nonlocal velocity, subtract special quadrature on the fattened mobility (to remove the incorrect self velocity), then add the special quadrature with the correct value of
{\hat{a}}
(to add the correct self velocity). This formulation relies on three key ideas:

1.

The correct aspect ratio is only important for the self velocity, and not the nonlocal term. As discussed previously, for well-separated fibers, the nonlocal integrals take the form of a Stokeslet +
\mathcal{O}({\hat{a}}^{2})\times
the doublet. Thus, for small aspect ratios, the thickness of the fiber is secondary to the line of Stokeslets, and artificially thickening the fibers in the nonlocal velocity calculation makes little difference for the error (see Fig.
14
in Appendix
A.2
). This was recognized previously in methods that only used lines of Stokeslets for the nonlocal velocity
[
1
,
7
]
. Our approach introduces an
\mathcal{O}(\left(\hat{\epsilon}^{*}\right)^{2})
asymptotic error for well-separated filaments, where
\hat{\epsilon}^{*}={\hat{a}}^{*}/L
is the thickened aspect ratio.

2.

Because
\hat{\epsilon}^{*}
will be relatively large, we can afford a larger number of oversampled points to compute the fattened mobility,
N_{u}=1/\hat{\epsilon}^{*}
. As a result, special quadrature on the fattened kernel will be a good approximation to the block diagonal terms of
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}_{\text{os}}^{(*)}
, and the difference
\text{BDiag}\left\{\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}_{\text{os}}^{(*)}\right\}-\text{BDiag}\left\{\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}_{\text{SQS}}^{(*)}\right\}
is near zero. As shown in Fig.
15
, the addition and subtraction of two different numerical representations of the self mobility on the fattened fibers contributes an error of at most 1%, and can be controlled by increasing the number of upsampled points.

3.

Since the difference
\text{BDiag}\left\{\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}_{\text{SQS}}\right\}-\text{BDiag}\left\{\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}_{\text{SQS}}^{(*)}\right\}
represents the same numerical scheme with two different values of
{\hat{a}}
, the eigenvalues of the correction matrix should be positive if the correct radius
{\hat{a}}<{\hat{a}}^{*}
. This is demonstrated in the right panel of Fig.
2
, where we plot the eigenvalues of the correction matrix
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}_{\text{SQS}}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}_{\text{SQS}}^{(*)}
for a single fiber with difference choices of
{\hat{a}}^{*}
(they approach zero as
{\hat{a}}^{*}\rightarrow{\hat{a}}
).

Putting these observations together, the mobility (
35
) gives the self mobility to numerical precision, while approximating the nonlocal mobility in a controllable way (approximations can be checked by decreasing
{\hat{a}}^{*}
towards
{\hat{a}}
), and, most importantly, is the sum of two SPD components. Thus, we can generate
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{W}}
by taking (c.f. (
27
))

\left(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}_{\text{os}}^{(*)}\right)^{1/2}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{W}}_{1}+\left(\text{BDiag}\left\{\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}_{\text{SQS}}\right\}-\text{BDiag}\left\{\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}_{\text{SQS}}^{(*)}\right\}\right)^{1/2}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{W}}_{2}.

(36)

The first square root is applied using (
32
) and the positively-split Ewald method, while the second is localized to each fiber and is computed by dense linear algebra.

In practice, we find the correction matrix
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}_{\text{SQS}}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}_{\text{SQS}}^{(*)}
can occasionally have negative eigenvalues when the time step size does not correctly resolve thermal fluctuations. While we should never be running in this regime, we prevent our code from crashing in this case by truncating the eigenvalues at
\lambda^{*}=10^{-3}
, as discussed in (
33
).

4
Steric repulsion

The change in the RPY mobility (
28
) on
R\leq 2{\hat{a}}
is unphysical, since fibers immersed in a common medium should never overlap one another. Of course, this happens quite a lot in our simulations, since the filament geometries are slender and underresolved. This section offers a way to keep filaments well-separated by using a soft Gaussian force between nearly-touching fibers. This approach is in no way superior to the recently-developed (implicit) methods that guarantee no overlap between particles
[
59
,
60
,
6
,
61
]
, but it is more practical for our purposes, since it uses an explicit method (one
force calculation
per time step) and does not become singular when fibers overlap (which will happen randomly due to Brownian motion). Simulations in Section
7
show that our approach is effective in keeping the filaments apart without introducing a strong restriction on the time step size. The focus then becomes how to efficiently determine near-contacts and evaluate forces.

As in
[
72
]
, we propose a double-integral steric interaction energy between two fibers
i
and
j
,

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}^{(ij)}=\int_{0}^{L}\int_{0}^{L}\hat{\mathcal{E}}\left(r\left({s}^{(i)},{s}^{(j)}\right)\right)\,d{s}^{(i)}\,d{s}^{(j)},

(37)

\displaystyle r\left({s}^{(i)},{s}^{(j)}\right)=\left\|{\mathbb{X}}^{(i)}\left({s}^{(i)}\right)-{\mathbb{X}}^{(j)}\left({s}^{(j)}\right)\right\|,

where
\hat{\mathcal{E}}
is the potential density function (units energy/area), which we assume is compactly supported on
0\leq r\leq r_{\text{max}}
. For the potential density function, we use an error function so that the force will ultimately be a Gaussian with standard deviation
\delta

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{E}}(r)=\frac{{\mathcal{E}}_{0}}{a}\text{erf}\left(r/(\delta\sqrt{2})\right)

(38)

\displaystyle\frac{d\hat{\mathcal{E}}}{dr}=\frac{{\mathcal{E}}_{0}}{a\delta}\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\exp{\left(-r^{2}/\left(2\delta^{2}\right)\right)}

In this potential density, we have two free parameters:
\delta
, which is the scale on which the force decays, and
{\mathcal{E}}_{0}
, which controls the magnitude of the forces (and therefore the temporal stiffness) and has units of energy per length (force). We set
\delta=a
to make the steric repulsion short ranged, and truncate the Gaussian at four standard deviations,
r_{\text{max}}=4\delta
. Because the force
d\hat{\mathcal{E}}/dr
is typically multiplied by weights on both interacting filaments with size
\mathcal{O}(a)
, choosing
{\mathcal{E}}_{0}
constant with
\delta\sim a
gives a force which is
\mathcal{O}(1)
with respect to the fiber aspect ratio, thus ensuring a relatively constant required time step size with decreasing aspect ratio. Based on these considerations, we set
\mathcal{E}_{0}=Bk_{B}T
, with
B
a simulation-dependent constant which controls the magnitude of the force.

While it is possible to differentiate the energy (
37
) in continuum first, this results in a force density on fiber
i
that is a
single integral
over fiber
j
. In this case, we could only detect contacts close to the chosen Chebyshev discretization points on fiber
i
. Rather, we will discretize the energy first, thus ensuring we actually compute a double integral, detecting contacts between all pairs of fiber regions. We then differentiate the discrete energy expression to get force. The details are as follows:
if the Chebyshev points used to discretize
\mathbb{X}(s)
are given by
\boldsymbol{X}
, then the upsampled points will be denoted by
\boldsymbol{X}^{(u)}=\boldsymbol{E}\boldsymbol{X}
. The double integral can then be evaluated and differentiated via

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}=

\displaystyle\sum_{k}\sum_{j}\hat{\mathcal{E}}\left(\left\|\boldsymbol{X}^{(u)}_{{\{k\}}}-\boldsymbol{X}^{(u)}_{{\{j\}}}\right\|\right)w_{k}w_{j}

(39)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k}\sum_{j}\hat{\mathcal{E}}\left(\left\|\boldsymbol{E}_{kp}\boldsymbol{X}_{\{p\}}-\boldsymbol{E}_{jq}\boldsymbol{X}_{{\{q\}}}\right\|\right)w_{k}w_{j}

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{F}}^{(i)}_{{\{a\}}}=-\frac{\partial\mathcal{E}}{\partial{\boldsymbol{X}}^{(i)}_{{\{a\}}}}=-

\displaystyle\sum_{k}\sum_{j}\frac{\partial\hat{\mathcal{E}}}{\partial r}\left(r_{kj}\right)\widehat{\boldsymbol{r}}_{kj}\boldsymbol{E}_{ka}w_{k}w_{j},

(40)

where
\boldsymbol{r}_{kj}=\boldsymbol{X}^{(u)}_{{\{k\}}}-\boldsymbol{X}^{(u)}_{{\{j\}}}
. The last equation gives the force at Chebyshev node
a
on fiber
i
, and is a function of the integration weights
w_{k}
and
w_{j}
of points
k
and
j
on the upsampled grid.

In our algorithm, we will
always
use (
40
) to compute forces. The freedom we have is how to choose the oversampled points
\boldsymbol{X}^{(u)}
(and therefore the matrix entry
\boldsymbol{E}_{ka}
in (
40
)). We present two possibilities for doing this: global upsampling (guaranteed accuracy, but inefficient in the limit
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
), as well as a second algorithm based on selective upsampling for pieces of the fibers that are nearly in contact. This second algorithm achieves our goal of a cost independent of
\epsilon
. In Appendix
B
, we analyze the differences between the two algorithms, demonstrating that the accuracy of the segment-based algorithm is comparable to the uniform-point-based algorithm with
1/\epsilon
points.

4.1
Global uniform point resampling

Let us suppose that we resample the fiber
\mathbb{X}(s)
at
N_{u}
uniform points to form the vector
\boldsymbol{X}^{(u)}
. We will typically use
N_{u}=1/\epsilon=1/\delta
, but more upsampling might be necessary to achieve higher accuracy. Thus the points we choose are at arclength coordinates
\boldsymbol{s}=0,\Delta s_{u},\dots,L
, where
\Delta s_{u}=L/(N_{u}-1)
is the spacing. The corresponding weights are
\boldsymbol{w}=\Delta s_{u}/2,\Delta s_{u},\dots\Delta s_{u},\Delta s_{u}/2
, so that the first and last point have a weight of 1/2, in accordance with the trapezoid rule. Then a simple algorithm to evaluate (
40
) is as follows:

1.

Resample every fiber at
N_{u}
points.

2.

Perform a (linear-cost) neighbor search to determine pairs of points for which the potential function
\partial\hat{\mathcal{E}}/\partial r
is nonzero to within a certain tolerance, i.e., find pairs of points a distance
r_{\text{max}}
or less apart
(excluding points on the same fiber that are closer than
r_{\text{max}}
when the fiber is straight)
.

3.

For each pair of points with
\left\|\boldsymbol{X}^{(u)}_{\{k\}}-\boldsymbol{X}^{(u)}_{\{j\}}\right\|<r_{\text{max}}
(determined in step 2), compute the corresponding term in the sum (
40
) and add it to obtain the forces on the Chebyshev nodes.

This is obviously an extremely simple algorithm, but it can become costly as the fibers get more slender, since resolving all of the potential contacts requires a large number uniform points (the quadratures are also only second-order accurate, which could limit accuracy). In the next section, we aim for an algorithm which is independent of
\epsilon
.

4.2
Segment-based algorithm

Figure 3:
Schematic of segment-based steric interaction algorithm. (a) We divide the fiber into pieces and use the midpoint of each piece (black circles) to identify possible close segments. (b) We then approximate each piece using a straight segment (dashed lines), find the minimum distance between the two segments, and use that as an initial guess for Newton’s method. (c) Newton’s method finds the local minimizer of the distance on the two fibers, around which we draw quadrature intervals (thick lines).

The following algorithm theoretically achieves a cost that is linear in the number of fibers (at constant density) and independent of
\delta\sim a
. It is based on dividing the fiber into
N_{\text{seg}}
(curved) fiber pieces of length
\ell_{s}
, then approximating those pieces as straight segments for the purposes of a cheap distance estimation. If the segments are close enough, we perform a nonlinear Newton solve to obtain the closest points on the corresponding fibers. The number of fiber pieces in this algorithm is chosen to minimize computational cost, and should be independent of
\epsilon
. A schematic of the algorithm is shown in Fig.
3
, and a more detailed explanation of the steps is as follows.

1.

We first sample each fiber at
N_{\text{seg}}
points, corresponding to the midpoints of the fiber pieces (circles in Fig.
3
(a)). Then, we perform a neighbor search over all the midpoints using the cutoff distance
r_{\text{cut}}=\ell_{s}+r_{\text{max}}
. This gives a list of potentially interacting segments, from which we exclude the self-interaction (there is no steric force between a segment and itself).

2.

For each pair of fiber pieces, we approximate curved fiber pieces as straight segments (dashed lines in Fig.
3
(b)) and solve a quadratic equation to determine the closest point of interaction between the straight segments. Let us denote this distance
{d}^{(ij)}_{\text{segs}}
, with the corresponding closest points on the fibers given by
{s}^{(i)}_{\text{seg}}
and
{s}^{(j)}_{\text{seg}}
.

3.

We next determine if
{d}^{(ij)}_{\text{segs}}<r_{\text{max}}+{\Delta h}^{(i)}+{\Delta h}^{(j)}
, where
{\Delta h}^{(i)}
represents the distance the fiber piece
{\mathbb{X}}^{(i)}(s)
curves from the straight segment we use to approximate it (see Section
B.1
). If
d_{\text{segs}}\geq r_{\text{max}}+{\Delta h}^{(i)}+{\Delta h}^{(j)}
, then we exit, as the corresponding fiber pieces could never be close enough to interact.

4.

We now proceed to the case when the fiber pieces could be close enough to interact. In this case, we solve the minimization problem

\left({s}^{(i)}_{*},{s}^{(j)}_{*}\right)=\argmin_{\begin{matrix}0\leq{s}^{(i)}\leq L\\
0\leq{s}^{(j)}\leq L\end{matrix}}\left\|{\mathbb{X}}^{(i)}\left({s}^{(i)}\right)-{\mathbb{X}}^{(j)}\left({s}^{(j)}\right)\right\|^{2},

(41)

with a modified projected Newton’s method, with initial guess
{s}^{(i)}={s}^{(i)}_{\text{seg}}
and
{s}^{(j)}={s}^{(j)}_{\text{seg}}
. Our methodology is described in Appendix
B.2
, and is based on the projected Newton methods discussed in
[
73
,
74
]
.

5.

We then approximate the fibers as quadratic curves around the local minimum, and solve
\left\|{\mathbb{X}}^{(i)}\left({s}^{(i)}_{*}+{\Delta s}^{(i)}\right)-{\mathbb{X}}^{(j)}\left({s}^{(j)}_{*}+{\Delta s}^{(j)}\right)\right\|^{2}=r_{\text{max}}^{2}
to obtain intervals
{S}^{(i)}
and
{S}^{(j)}
on which we need to integrate the energy (
37
).

6.

Because there are multiple segments on each fiber, it is possible that the interval associated with a particular minimum might overlap with another interval for the same fiber pair. Because of that, we assemble a list of fiber pairs
i
and
j
and intervals
{S}^{(i)}
and
{S}^{(j)}
from step 5, over which we do the integral (
40
) by taking a kind of “union.” Because we are in two dimensions, this procedure is not entirely straightforward, and so we describe details of it in Appendix
B.4
.

7.

Finally, we put a grid of Gauss-Legendre points over each interval. Letting
L_{\text{int}}
be the length of an interval, the number of points we use is

N_{\text{GL}}=\left\lfloor N_{\delta}\frac{L_{\text{int}}}{\delta}\right\rfloor+1,

(42)

where
\delta
is the standard deviation of the Gaussian potential (
38
) and
N_{\delta}
is a constant which gives the number of Gauss-Legendre points we use per standard deviation. We then apply the formula (
40
) over the two Gauss-Legendre grids. To implement this step efficiently, we precompute a maximum number of Gauss-Legendre points by setting
L_{\text{int}}=L
in (
42
). We then pretabulate all possible points and weights on
[-1,1]
for
N_{\text{GL}}=1,\dots N_{\text{GL}}^{\text{(max)}}
. Then, for a given
L_{\text{int}}
, we compute
N_{\text{GL}}
using (
42
), and look up the points and weights in the table, scaling appropriately by
L_{\text{int}}
.

To make the cost of the algorithm formally independent of
\epsilon
, the neighbor search in step 1 must be on a number of segments that is independent of
\epsilon
. The cost of step 2 is then independent of
\epsilon
because we are minimizing segment distances. Steps 3–5 are the most difficult, but have cost independent of
\epsilon
because we use nonlinear solves to obtain the closest points. And because
r_{\text{max}}\sim\delta\sim a
, the grid in steps 5–7 will have a thickness that scales with
\epsilon
, so the cost of the quadrature in step 7 is independent of
\epsilon
.

Ultimately, the efficiency of the algorithm (and its favorability over the one presented in Section
4.1
) comes down to the expense of the neighbor search vs. root finding and quadrature. If the root finding is expensive, then it makes sense to just use
1/\epsilon
fiber pieces (which become spheres), in which case the algorithm becomes equivalent to Section
4.1
. If the root finding and quadrature are cheaper
(which is typically the case as
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
)
, then we can use less points in the neighbor search and this segment-based algorithm might be more efficient than the uniform-point algorithm.

5
Temporal integration

Now that we have reviewed the spatial equations, it remains to discretize in time. Because the applications for these methods are mainly biological, the focus of temporal integration is on robustness, and not necessarily accuracy. For this reason, the temporal integrators we present here are first-order accurate (see
[
56
]
for a second-order version in the deterministic case). The deterministic integrator is straightforward, and amounts to a first-order backward Euler discretization of (
20
), with the caveat that the final update to the fiber is a (nonlinear) rotation of the tangent vectors to keep the dynamics on the constraint, followed by integration to obtain the new fiber positions. The integrator for Brownian fluctuations is based on using the Brownian fluctuations to generate a trial step to the midpoint, then solving the equations using the midpoint configuration. This procedure captures a key part of the drift term (
23a
), precisely because it is consistent with the midpoint interpretation in (
23b
).

5.1
Deterministic methods

Considering the linear system (
20
), which must be solved at every time step, let us introduce the time step index
n
and approximate

\boldsymbol{X}_{n+1,*}=\boldsymbol{X}_{n}+\Delta t\boldsymbol{K}_{n}\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{n}.

(43)

Because bending forces are stiff, we treat them implicitly using the linearized backward Euler method. This gives the linear system

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}&\boldsymbol{K}\\
\boldsymbol{K}^{T}&\boldsymbol{0}\end{pmatrix}_{n}\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\\
\boldsymbol{\alpha}\end{pmatrix}_{n}=\begin{pmatrix}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}_{n}\left(-\boldsymbol{L}\boldsymbol{X}_{n+1,*}+\boldsymbol{F}^{\text{(ext)}}_{n}\right)\\
\boldsymbol{0}\end{pmatrix},

(44)

Substituting (
43
) gives a linear system for
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
and
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
in terms of
\boldsymbol{X}_{n}
.

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}&\boldsymbol{K}+\Delta t\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}\boldsymbol{L}\boldsymbol{K}\\
\boldsymbol{K}^{T}&\boldsymbol{0}\end{pmatrix}_{n}\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\\
\boldsymbol{\alpha}\end{pmatrix}_{n}=\begin{pmatrix}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}_{n}\left(-\boldsymbol{L}\boldsymbol{X}_{n}+\boldsymbol{F}^{\text{(ext)}}_{n}\right)\\
\boldsymbol{0}\end{pmatrix}.

(45)

Now, depending on the form of the mobility, the solution strategies for this system vary. If
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}
is a block diagonal matrix (localized to each fiber without any inter-fiber interactions), this system can be solved using dense linear algebra (i.e., by forming
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}
as a dense matrix and inverting it directly). If, however,
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}
is a dense matrix, encoding interactions of all fibers with all other fibers, iterative methods are required. In this case, we split the mobility matrix into a local and nonlocal part,

\displaystyle\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}=\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}^{\text{L}}+

\displaystyle\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}^{\text{NL}}

(46)

\displaystyle=\text{BDiag}\left\{\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}\right\}+

\displaystyle\left(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}-\text{BDiag}\left\{\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}\right\}\right).

(47)

We then solve the system (
45
) using a block diagonal preconditioner,

\boldsymbol{P}=\begin{pmatrix}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}^{\text{L}}&\boldsymbol{K}+\Delta t\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}^{\text{L}}\boldsymbol{L}\boldsymbol{K}\\[2.0pt]
\boldsymbol{K}^{T}&\boldsymbol{0}\end{pmatrix},

(48)

This preconditioner ought to be effective since
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}^{\text{L}}
typically dominates
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}^{\text{NL}}
for slender filaments. Note that while other options for the mobility splitting are possible, including making
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}^{\text{L}}
itself diagonal, here we will restrict to the case of (
47
), where the local mobility on fiber
i
is just the
i
th diagonal block of the matrix
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}
.

5.1.1
Time-lagging the nonlocal forces

For most fiber suspensions, the local behavior dominates the dynamics, and so we can actually obtain similar stability behavior by
time-lagging
the non-local parts of the mobility. In particular, we can only treat the bending and constraint forces implicitly for the
local
parts of the mobility, which gives rise to the linear system

\displaystyle\small\begin{pmatrix}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}^{\text{L}}&\boldsymbol{K}+\Delta t\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}^{\text{L}}\boldsymbol{L}\boldsymbol{K}\\
\boldsymbol{K}^{T}&\boldsymbol{0}\end{pmatrix}_{n}\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\\
\boldsymbol{\alpha}\end{pmatrix}_{n}=\begin{pmatrix}\left(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}^{\text{L}}_{n}+\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}^{\text{NL}}_{n}\right)\left(-\boldsymbol{L}\boldsymbol{X}_{n}+\boldsymbol{F}^{\text{(ext)}}_{n}\right)+\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}^{\text{NL}}_{n}\left(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{n-1}\right)\\
\boldsymbol{0}\end{pmatrix}.

(49)

To the extent that this approach is stable, it significantly reduces the cost, since only one nonlocal hydrodynamic evaluation is required per time step. When the suspension is dense enough, the dynamics are unstable, but we can use the solutions above, denoted by
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}_{n}
and
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{n}
, to obtain a modified system of equations. The key is to rewrite the locally implicit system (
49
) as

\boldsymbol{K}_{n}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{n}=\left(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}^{\text{L}}_{n}+\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}^{\text{NL}}_{n}\right)\left(-\boldsymbol{L}\boldsymbol{X}_{n}-\Delta t\boldsymbol{L}\boldsymbol{K}_{n}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{n}+\boldsymbol{F}^{\text{(ext)}}+\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}_{n}\right)+\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}^{\text{NL}}_{n}\left(\Delta t\boldsymbol{L}\boldsymbol{K}_{n}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{n}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}_{n}+\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{n-1}\right),

then subtract this from the fully implicit system (
45
), which gives the residual form of the saddle-point system

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}&\boldsymbol{K}+\Delta t\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}\boldsymbol{L}\boldsymbol{K}\\[2.0pt]
\boldsymbol{K}^{T}&\boldsymbol{0}\end{pmatrix}_{n}\begin{pmatrix}\Delta\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\\[2.0pt]
\Delta\boldsymbol{\alpha}\end{pmatrix}_{n}=\begin{pmatrix}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}^{\text{NL}}_{n}\left(-\Delta t\boldsymbol{L}\boldsymbol{K}_{n}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{n}+\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}_{n}-\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{n-1}\right)\\[2.0pt]
\boldsymbol{0}\end{pmatrix}

(50)

to be solved using GMRES for the perturbations
\Delta\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{n}=\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{n}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}_{n}
and
\Delta\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{n}=\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{n}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{n}
.

There are two caveats here. First, obviously the time-lagging procedure does not work at
t=0
, so we must solve the fully implicit system (
45
) there. Second, the solution of the residual system (
50
) is only required for
stability
, and not accuracy. As such, we do not have to solve the system to a low tolerance. Instead, we empirically set a maximum number of GMRES iterations, increasing this number until we obtain stability.

5.1.2
Nonlinear update

No matter the precise linear system being solved, once we solve for
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{n}=\left(\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{n},{\boldsymbol{U}_{\text{MP}}}_{,n}\right)
, we have access to the discrete tangent vector rotation rates
\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{n}
. As such, we update the fibers by rotating the tangent vectors

\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n+1}=\text{rotate}\left(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{n},\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{n}\Delta t\right),

(51)

using the Rodrigues rotation formula
[
56
, Eq. (111)]
. We then update the midpoint
\boldsymbol{X}_{\text{MP}}
via
{\boldsymbol{X}_{\text{MP}}}_{,n+1}={\boldsymbol{X}_{\text{MP}}}_{,n}+\Delta t{\boldsymbol{U}_{\text{MP}}}_{,n}
, and obtain
\boldsymbol{X}_{n+1}
by applying the matrix
\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}}
defined in (
13
). We will denote this nonlinear update, which preserves the length of the tangent vectors exactly, as
\text{RotateAndIntegrate}\left(\boldsymbol{X}_{n},\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{n}\Delta t\right)
.

5.2
Brownian fluctuations

The temporal integrator for Brownian fluctuations requires additional terms in the saddle point system (
20
) to account for the Brownian velocity and drift terms in the Langevin equation (
23a
). In
[
58
]
, we broke the drift term into three pieces, showing that one piece can be obtained by rotating the tangent vectors at the end of the time step, while the second piece can be obtained by solving the saddle point system (
20
) at a kind of “midpoint” configutation (consistent with the kinetic formulation (
23b
)). The third piece is obtained by adding a drift of the mobility
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}
to the right hand side, computed using a random finite difference
[
75
]
.

The full temporal integration scheme for Brownian filaments is as follows
[
58
]

1.

Generate a Brownian velocity increment

\boldsymbol{U}^{(B)}_{n}=\sqrt{\frac{2k_{B}T}{\Delta t}}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{n},

(52)

where
\boldsymbol{\eta}_{n}
is a vector of i.i.d. standard Gaussian
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
random variables.

2.

Use the Brownian velocity increment to propose a rotation rate for the tangent vectors

\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{n,*}=\boldsymbol{K}_{n}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{U}^{(B)}_{n}

(53)

3.

Use the proposed rotation rates to generate a “midpoint” configuration of the filaments

\boldsymbol{X}_{n+1/2,*}=\text{RotateAndIntegrate}\left(\boldsymbol{X}_{n},\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{n.*}\Delta t/2\right)

(54)

4.

Compute an additional drift velocity (“mobility drift,” MD) in one of two ways. If the mobility is local and can be formed as a dense matrix, set

\boldsymbol{U}_{n}^{\text{(MD)}}=\sqrt{\frac{2k_{B}T}{\Delta t}}\left(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}_{n+1/2,*}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}_{n}\right)\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}_{n}^{-T/2}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{n}.

(55)

This term might be impractical for large systems because it is based on solving a resistance problem to obtain
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}^{-1/2}
. An alternative expression
(used for nonlocal hydrodynamics)
that generates the same velocity in expectation is to use the random finite difference
[
75
]

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{X}^{\text{(RFD)}}=\text{RotateAndIntegrate}\left(\boldsymbol{X}_{n},\delta L\boldsymbol{K}_{n}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{\eta}^{\text{(RFD)}}\right)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{U}_{n}^{\text{(MD)}}=\frac{k_{B}T}{\delta L}\left(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}\left(\boldsymbol{X}^{\text{(RFD)}}\right)-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}_{n}\right)\boldsymbol{\eta}^{\text{(RFD)}},

(56)

where
\boldsymbol{\eta}^{\text{(RFD)}}
is a vector of i.i.d. standard Gaussian random variables (independent of
\boldsymbol{\eta}_{n}
), and
\delta=10^{-5}
is a small number.

5.

Compute an additional velocity to modify the backward Euler method for more accurate estimation of the covariance at a finite time step size
[
58
, Sec. 3.1]

\boldsymbol{U}^{\text{(MBE)}}_{n}=\sqrt{k_{B}T}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}_{n}\boldsymbol{L}^{1/2}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{n}.

(57)

6.

Solve the saddle point system

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}&\boldsymbol{K}+\Delta t\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}\boldsymbol{L}\boldsymbol{K}\\
\boldsymbol{K}^{T}&\boldsymbol{0}\end{pmatrix}_{n+1/2,*}\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\\
\boldsymbol{\alpha}\end{pmatrix}_{n+1/2}=\begin{pmatrix}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}_{n+1/2,*}\boldsymbol{L}\boldsymbol{X}_{n}+\boldsymbol{U}^{\text{(ext)}}_{n}\\
\boldsymbol{0}\end{pmatrix}

(58)

\displaystyle\text{where}\qquad\boldsymbol{U}^{\text{(ext)}}_{n}=\boldsymbol{U}^{(B)}_{n}+\boldsymbol{U}^{\text{(MBE)}}_{n}+\boldsymbol{U}_{n}^{\text{(MD)}},

for
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{n+1/2}
and
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{n+1/2}
.

7.

Update the fiber via

\boldsymbol{X}_{n+1}=\text{RotateAndIntegrate}\left(\boldsymbol{X}_{n},\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{n+1/2}\Delta t\right)

(59)

The temporal integrator has a certain synergy with the split Stratonovich-Ito form (
23b
), in the sense that the Brownian velocity is evaluated at time
n
(Ito interpretation), while the saddle point solve (which generates the action of
\widehat{\boldsymbol{N}}
) is done at the midpoint (Stratanovich intepretation).

6
Numerical tests

In this section, we examine the mobility (
35
) in both steady state and dynamic numerical tests, and demonstrate how changing between local and nonlocal mobilities can yield insights into the physics of deterministic and Brownian filament suspensions. We first consider the equilibrium bending fluctuations of filaments, where we compare the steady state end-to-end distance distribution to that obtained by MCMC using the energy (
26
). We then use a simple example of filaments under gravity to demonstrate how the mobility (
35
) can accurately resolve nonlocal hydrodynamics while treating the local mobility in an efficient way. After testing the accuracy of the mobility in a deterministic context, we perform simulations of two and four hundred Brownian filaments under gravity, all interacting hydrodynamically. Our simulations demonstrate anew the role of hydrodynamic interactions in constricting Brownian fluctuations
[
55
,
52
]
.

6.1
Equilibrium fluctuations

) changes the time step size required to successfully sample from the end-to-end distance distribution of free fibers at equilibrium. To do this, we consider a suspension of

also revealed a time step size of order

for local hydrodynamics only, so the nonlocal hydrodynamics with oversampling does not appear to change the required time step size.

We now consider what happens when we switch to the fat-corrected mobility (

\hat{\epsilon}^{*}=10^{-2}
, so that the nonlocal hydrodynamics can be resolved using only

\Delta t_{f}=10^{-4}

\Delta t_{f}=5\times 10^{-4}
(red) and
\Delta t_{f}=2.5\times 10^{-4}
(yellow) are acceptable for our purposes, meaning that the fat-corrected mobility requires a time step size two-fold lower than the oversampled one.

The remaining four panels in Fig.

show how the required time step size with the fat-corrected mobility changes with the parameters. We observe that halving the mesh size (the middle left plot has
\ell_{m}=0.1

\Delta t_{f}=10^{-3}

1/k^{4}

[

[

[

In addition to the required time step size, another important part of the mobility cost is the number of GMRES iterations required to solve (
58
) (tolerance
10^{-3}
) for each time step size. Here we have a very convenient result: as shown in the insets of Fig.
4
, when we correctly resolve the equilibrium distribution, the number of GMRES iterations is three with a frequency of 99%. Exceptions to this include larger persistence length, for which there are at most four iterations with frequency 99.9%, and a smaller mesh size, in which the fewest number of iterations is four (this is unsurprising because nonlocal hydrodynamics makes a larger contribution). Likewise, when the fibers are more slender, so that nonlocal hydrodynamics makes a relatively smaller contribution, we see less iterations (compare the top right and middle right plots in Fig.
4
). Overall, the iteration counts give us a clear litmus test for the equilibrium distribution: when the number of iterations exceeds five with frequency larger than 1%, we are not correctly simulating the equilibrium distribution. Furthermore, the iteration counts that we obtain when converged to the equilibrium distribution (three or four iterations) are the same as those obtained for oversampled quadrature, in which each iteration can be five to ten times more expensive. Thus, the only possible additional cost in the fat-corrected mobility is the small change in required time step size.

7.1
Model set up

In our model of a cross-linked fiber network, the cross linkers are virtual entities which exert time-dependent external forces
\boldsymbol{F}^{\text{(CL)}}(t)
on the filaments. The details of the algorithm for transient cross linking are discussed in
[
86
,
87
]
; here we give a brief summary in the interest of completeness. At each time, the “cross-linked network” is defined by a list of pairs of points (on distinct filaments) which are connected by a cross linker (CL). To define the list of potential points, we sample each filament
{\mathbb{X}}^{(i)}(s)
at
N_{\text{cl}}
uniformly-spaced points
s^{*}_{i}
with spacing
\Delta s_{\text{cl}}=L/(N_{\text{cl}}-1)
. At a given time, a CL can have one end bound to a binding site (“singly-bound”), or both ends bound to binding sites on distinct filaments (“doubly-bound”). Each singly- or doubly-bound CL has a rate of unbinding: the rate at which a singly-bound CL unbinds is
k_{\text{off}}
, while a doubly bound CL can unbind from either binding site with rate
k_{\text{off,s}}
(making the total rate
2k_{\text{off,s}}
).

We model the process of CL diffusion and binding by an effective attachment rate for one CL end, given at each site by
k_{\text{on}}\Delta s_{\text{cl}}
. If a binding site on a nearby filament
{\mathbb{X}}^{(j)}(s)
is a distance
\ell=\left\|{\mathbb{X}}^{(i)}\left(s_{p}^{*}\right)-{\mathbb{X}}^{(j)}(s_{q}^{*})\right\|
away from the docking site of the first end, the energy associated with binding to that site is given by

\mathcal{E}^{\text{(CL)}}=\frac{K_{c}}{2}\left(\left\|{\mathbb{X}}^{(i)}\left(s_{p}^{*}\right)-{\mathbb{X}}^{(j)}\left(s_{q}^{*}\right)\right\|-\ell_{c}\right)^{2}=\frac{K_{c}}{2}\left(\ell-\ell_{c}\right)^{2},

(62)

and the rate of binding of the second end to the nearby binding site is given by

k_{\text{on,s}}\left(\ell\right)=k_{\text{on,s}}^{0}\exp{\left(-\frac{\mathcal{E}^{\text{(CL)}}}{k_{B}T}\right)},

(63)

where
\ell_{c}
is the rest length of the CL, and
K_{c}
is its stiffness. In
[
87
]
, we derived the relationship (
63
) by assuming that the fluctuations in CL length are in equilibrium with respect to the energy (
62
), thus ensuring that the links are in detailed balance and therefore passive. Because the binding rate (
63
) is compactly-supported (to finite precision), we perform a neighbor search to obtain a list of all uniform point pairs within two standard deviations of the Gaussian (
63
); that is, all uniform point pairs separated by a distance
\ell_{c}+2\sqrt{k_{B}T/K_{c}}
. This defines a list of the possible pairs of points to which new CLs can bind, and completes a list of four possible reactions which we simulate using a standard stochastic simulation / Gillespie algorithm
[
88
,
89
,
86
]
.

We use a first-order splitting algorithm to update the cross linkers and filaments. At each time step, we first take a step of size
\Delta t
in the Markov chain. This produces a list of fibers and uniform points which are connected by cross linkers. To compute forces, we denote the discrete uniform points as
\hbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 2.15277pt\hbox{\kern-1.00006pt$\boldsymbol{X}$\kern-1.00006pt}}}=\boldsymbol{R}^{(u)}\boldsymbol{X}
, so that the
p
th uniform point on fiber
i
can be written as
{\hbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 2.15277pt\hbox{\kern-1.00006pt$\boldsymbol{X}$\kern-1.00006pt}}}}^{(i)}_{{\{p\}}}=\boldsymbol{R}^{(u)}_{{\{p,:\}}}\boldsymbol{X}
, and the energy (
62
) can be written in terms of
\boldsymbol{\ell}={\hbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 2.15277pt\hbox{\kern-1.00006pt$\boldsymbol{X}$\kern-1.00006pt}}}}^{(i)}_{{\{p\}}}-{\hbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 2.15277pt\hbox{\kern-1.00006pt$\boldsymbol{X}$\kern-1.00006pt}}}}^{(j)}_{{\{q\}}}
.
In a similar way to the steric force calculation (
40
), the
CL force on each (Chebyshev) fiber point
{\boldsymbol{X}}^{(i)}_{{\{a\}}}
can be obtained by differentiating the energy (
62
),

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{F}}^{(i)}_{{\{a\}}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\partial\mathcal{E}^{\text{(CL)}}}{\partial\ell}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\ell}}\boldsymbol{R}^{(u)}_{{\{p,a\}}}\qquad\qquad{\boldsymbol{F}}^{(j)}_{{\{b\}}}=\frac{\partial\mathcal{E}^{\text{(CL)}}}{\partial\ell}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\ell}}\boldsymbol{R}^{(u)}_{{\{q,b\}}}.

(64)

Adding up the forces (
64
) for all pairs of points gives the external force
\boldsymbol{F}^{\text{(CL)}}
which is added as an external force to the right-hand side of the saddle-point system (
58
). We note that the CL forces can be nonsmooth; in the special case when the two uniform points are Chebyshev points, we get
\boldsymbol{R}^{(u)}_{{\{p,a\}}}=\delta_{ap}
and
\boldsymbol{R}^{(u)}_{{\{q,b\}}}=\delta_{qb}
, and the resulting force is a delta function between the two points on the Chebyshev grid.

The role of hydrodynamic interactions in the higher aspect ratio suspensions is intertwined with the role of steric interactions. If we examine the number of contacts in the simulation, there appears to be a slight but noticeable reduction when nonlocal hydrodynamics is included (compare blue and red curves in right panels of Fig.

This effect is natural given the nature of flows: when two fibers move towards each other, they create flows which tend to push them apart, or make it more difficult for them to come together. These flows are extremely short-ranged, however, as the inclusion of steric interactions keeps the fibers far enough apart so as to eliminate their effects (compare solid blue and red curves in the top panel of Fig.

Because the cross linkers generate additional force on the filaments, the filament shapes will no longer be samples from the equilibrium distribution (

8
Conclusion

This paper represents the end of a series of works
[
56
,
57
,
58
]
on slender filament hydrodynamics. Our purpose here was two fold: first, we summarized the (often tinkered-with) governing equations and spatial discretization that came out of previous work, so that the reader can have one reference instead of four. This summary revealed an important missing piece that represents the key contribution of this paper: formulating a
nonlocal
mobility that is symmetric positive definite and has cost independent of fiber aspect ratio. Combining this with a novel method for handling steric interactions between filaments, we presented a simulation package which can account for filament slenderness, inextensibility, hydrodynamics, and Brownian motion. We used this package to simulate fibers under gravity (a common example where nonlocal flows are integral to the dynamics) and cross-linked fiber networks (an example from biology which is dominated more by
local
interactions).

Designing an SPD nonlocal mobility turned out to be a winding journey which alternated between discrete and continuum pictures. At first glance, a continuum mobility based on RPY integrals (
29
) ought to generate a discrete SPD mobility, and indeed it does if all of the integrals are resolved with the same number of points. This is where the main problem arises: if all of the integrals must use the same number of points, then the number of points is defined by the self integral, which requires
\mathcal{O}(1/\epsilon)
discretization points to resolve accurately. Attempts to circumvent this difficulty (by using a special quadrature scheme for the self term) destroy the SPD property of the mobility. Using previous methodology, we therefore are able to simulate
either
Brownian motion
or
nonlocal interactions, but not both.

In this paper, we circumvented this difficulty by making two key observations: first, for a given numerical scheme (oversampling or special quadrature), the eigenvalues of the mobility decrease as the
fibers get fatter
. Second, for a given aspect ratio, the eigenvalues of the mobility decrease as the accuracy of integration increases. Putting these together, we coupled a more accurate quadrature scheme for the self term with
an artificially-fattened fiber radius
. This generated an SPD mobility, which we split into an oversampling step
(for the fattened aspect ratio)
, plus a local correction, which is the special quadrature scheme on the correct aspect ratio minus the special quadrature scheme on the thicker aspect ratio. While this approximation, which comes out of numerical and not theoretical necessity, introduces an error into the dynamics, we demonstrated in both static (Appendix
A.2
) and dynamic (Sec.
6.2
) examples that this error is about the same order of magnitude as using
1/\epsilon
points in the self integrals, making it relatively small. In fact, for well-separated filaments the RPY kernel takes the form of a Stokeslet +
\mathcal{O}({\hat{a}}^{2})\times
the doublet, and so changing from
{\hat{a}}
to
{\hat{a}}^{*}
introduces an asymptotic error of order
(\hat{\epsilon}^{*})^{2}
. The thickened aspect ratio
\hat{\epsilon}^{*}
represents a degree of freedom in the dynamics; we fixed
\hat{\epsilon}^{*}=10^{-2}
, believing that
1/\hat{\epsilon}^{*}=100
oversampling points per filament would be a good ceiling, but it can drop even further if computationally feasible. Of course, if the filaments are not too slender (
\epsilon\approx 10^{-2}
), then the oversampled mobility is sufficient to resolve the dynamics without incurring a large oversampling expense.

In addition to a new formulation of the mobility, we also designed a novel algorithm to treat steric (excluded volume) interactions in filaments discretized with Chebyshev collocation points. The key was to formulate the energy as a double integral, similar to what was done in
[
72
]
for straight cylinders. However, in
[
72
]
, a singular energy density is chosen, which allows for analytic integration over straight segments. Because our simulations include Brownian fluctuations, fibers might overlap regardless of the strength of the steric potential, and so we chose an energy density that is Gaussian (nonsingular and smooth) in nature, and consequently not possible to integrate analytically. As such, we needed to design efficient quadrature schemes to integrate the density in the limit
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
.

We considered two different quadrature schemes to integrate the steric interaction potential and obtain the steric forces at the Chebyshev collocation points. In the first scheme, we simply resampled the fiber at a set of uniform points, then performed a neighbor search to find pairs of interactions that contribute to the sum. This scheme does not scale well in the limit
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
, so we developed a second scheme based on breaking the fibers into
\mathcal{O}(1)
pieces, then finding and progressively zeroing in on pieces that could be interacting. Similar to the mobility, we validated our more efficient segment-based algorithm by comparing the steric forces it generated to those obtained using oversampling with
1/\epsilon
points (Appendix
B.5
).

We chose to treat steric forces explicitly in our temporal update. This leads to a reduction of a factor of roughly 5 to 10 in the allowable time step size, with our original time step leading to unstable simulations when sterics is included. It is tempting in this case to consider an implicit approach to achieve stability at larger time step sizes, such as the complementarity formulation used in
[
59
,
60
,
6
,
61
]
, whereby an optimization problem is solved to obtain a configuration that is contact-free while minimizing the contact forces between particles. While these methods do indeed increase the time step size, the method used to solve the optimization problem requires multiple applications of the hydrodynamic mobility, and as such makes each time step at least 10 times more expensive than when contacts are not resolved. Thus employing a more complex contact algorithm would make our simulations just as expensive as treating the steric force explicitly. Furthermore, the complementarity formulation introduces new constraints into the problem, which leads to new drift terms in the Ito Langevin equation that describes the fiber dynamics. Formulating and accounting for these drift terms will be a challenge for any method that attempts to combine Brownian dynamics with contact resolution.

Explicit time integration is more efficient in our case because our volume-exclusion potential is “soft,” in the sense that we allow some particle overlaps to keep the time step relatively large. If we were to stiffen up the potential to prevent more overlaps, then we likely would arrive at a point where contact-based algorithms
would
be more efficient. It remains unclear, however, how to extend these algorithms from rigid particles
[
60
,
61
,
59
]
to flexible fibers, as the most recent works of Yan et al.
[
60
,
6
]
treat flexible fibers via a series of rigid rods. The most straightforward way would be to keep the segment-based fiber representation, but replace our special quadrature scheme with segment-based quadrature,
such as that previously developed for regularized Stokeslets

[
37
,
38
]
. However, as discussed in the introduction, regularized Stokeslets suffer from a lack of symmetry which destroys the SPD-property of the mobility, so a different regularized singularity (e.g., RPY or FCM) would be required.

We ended this paper by applying our simulation algorithm to two very different examples of fiber dynamics. In the example of fibers under gravity, there were nonlocal fluid flows which entrained Brownian motion; the local and nonlocal mobilities generated qualitatively different dynamics. For cross-linked fiber networks, the only effect of hydrodynamics was to keep the fibers apart, something that we could also account for via steric repulsion. Since our interests are in the latter system, it was a disappointment to see flows making such little difference, but it is no surprise given that the
in vitro
experimental dynamics never demonstrated any large-scale flows
[
84
]
. Indeed, in biology, examples of large-scale fluid flows typically occur when molecular motors are around to actively contract or expand filament networks
[
92
,
93
,
94
]
. It would be straightforward to expand the cross-linking algorithm presented here (Sec.
7.1
) to account for motors, which are essentially moving cross linkers
[
95
,
96
]
. Doing so will allow for a more thorough study of the hydrodynamics of the cytoskeleton, including how actin architecture affects fluid flows
[
97
]
.

The platform presented here could also be used to study stress relaxation in cross-linked networks, where previous theories have posited a relationship between cross link activity and stress relaxtion timescales
[
91
,
90
,
98
,
99
]
. However, the system stress, which is the quantity of interest in rheology, includes a contribution from Brownian motion
[
100
, Eq (3.169)]
. Classical work showed that the Brownian stress can be computed as a divergence of the matrix mapping velocity to stress, which, when done naively, would require additional resistance solves
[
101
]
, significantly increasing the cost of the algorithm presented here. Developing numerical methods for efficient calculation of Brownian stress is consequently an important future direction that would enable large-scale rheological studies.

Regarding fiber sedimentation, an avenue for future exploration is to study how a bottom wall impacts the hydrodynamic interactions between fibers. Previous studies on colloidal particles
[
102
]
have shown how proximity to the wall can impact agglomeration and layering of active colloids (magnetic rollers). Extending such a study to filaments would require efficient evaluation of the hydrodynamic mobility for filaments above a wall. While the corrections to the RPY kernel in this case are known
[
103
]
, fast (linear-scaling) methods to compute them remain under development
[
104
,
105
,
106
]
. Extending these methods to filaments would again require the development of special quadrature schemes that preserve the overall SPD properties of the RPY kernel. These, and extensions to other non-periodic geometries, represent a challenge for future work.

Appendix B
Steric interactions

In this appendix, we give more details on the steric interaction algorithm we presented in Section
4.2
. There are four main points to clarify: the errors in approximating curved fiber pieces as straight segments, the Newton solver for curved segments, the approximation of the fibers as quadratic curves to obtain the integration domains, and the union of integration domains on nearly interacting fibers. We conclude by studying the accuracy of the steric forces in the case of bundling fibers, finding that our segment-based algorithm gives the same accuracy as oversampling in estimating the steric forces.

B.1
Errors from straight segments

One difficult part of the segment-based algorithm is step 3, where we need to estimate the curvature of the fibers and use Newton’s method to obtain the closest points on the two segments. This step is actually strictly necessary to preserve independence from
\epsilon
. If we use segments (dashed lines in Fig.
3
(b)), we will have some error in estimating the distance between curved fiber pieces. Since
r_{\text{max}}\sim a
, this error will matter more when
\epsilon
is smaller, and so there will be more erroneous pairs of fiber pieces as
\epsilon
decreases (assuming the curvature of the fibers does not change with
\epsilon
). Thus we need to eliminate errors by performing the nonlinear optimization (
41
).

This section deals with how we estimate the curvature of the fiber segments relative to their straight backbones. Figure
3
(b) shows what we mean by this: the pieces of the fibers
\mathbb{X}(s)
that we are concerned with are shown as solid lines, while the straight segment approximations are shown as dotted lines. We define a distance
\Delta h(s)
that is simply the distance between the straight segment and the curved fiber. Our goal is to get an estimate for the maximum
\Delta h
along the segment. We expect this distance to depend on the number of segments we put in each persistence length, which is the ratio
\ell_{p}/\ell_{s}
.

Figure 16:
Error in estimating
\Delta h
using its value at the midpoint of the segment. When there are at least 2 panels per persistence length the error is typically 5% of the segment length or less.

There are two possible ways to do this. First, we can pretabulate, for each

, the expected deviation from straightness, and use that as

The simplest thing we can do is sample the fiber at the segment midpoint, and then use this to estimate the deviation from straight. For fibers sampled from the equilibrium distribution, Fig.
16
shows that we can use segments of size
\ell_{p}
and still make an error of no more than 10% in doing this. Therefore, we just sample the segment at the midpoint, compute
\Delta h_{\text{mp}}
(at the fiber midpoint), and set

in step 3.
