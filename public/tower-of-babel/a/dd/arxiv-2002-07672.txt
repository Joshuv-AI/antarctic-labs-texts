Structural Invariants for the Verification of Systems with Parameterized Architectures Institute of Engineering Univ. Grenoble Alpes
Marius Bozga, Javier Esparza, Radu Iosif, Joseph Sifakis, and Christoph Welzel

Abstract




We consider parameterized concurrent systems consisting of a finite
but unknown number of components, obtained by replicating a given set
of finite state automata. Components communicate by executing atomic
interactions whose participants update their states simultaneously. We
introduce an interaction logic to specify both the type of
interactions (e.g. rendez-vous, broadcast) and the topology of the
system (e.g. pipeline, ring). The logic can be easily embedded in
monadic second order logic of
\kappa\geq 1
successors (
\mathsf{WS}\kappa\mathsf{S}
),
and is therefore decidable.

Proving safety properties of such a parameterized system, like
deadlock freedom or mutual exclusion, requires to infer an
inductive invariant that contains all reachable states of all system instances, and
no unsafe state. We present a method to automatically synthesize
inductive invariants directly from the formula describing the interactions,
without costly fixed point iterations. We experimentally prove that
this invariant is strong enough to verify safety properties of a large number
of systems including textbook examples (dining philosophers,
synchronization schemes), classical mutual exclusion algorithms,
cache-coherence protocols and self-stabilization algorithms, for an arbitrary number of components.

Our Contributions.

The novelty of the approach described in this paper is three-fold:

1.

The architecture of the system is not fixed a priori, but given
as a parameter of the verification problem. In fact, we describe
parameterized systems using the Behavior-Interaction-Priorities (BIP)
framework
[
9
]
, in which processes are instances of
finite-state
component types
, whose interfaces are sets of

ports
, labeling transitions between local states, and
interactions are sets of strongly synchronizing ports, described by
formulae of an
interaction logic
. An interaction formula
captures the architecture of the interactions (pipeline, ring,
clique, tree) and the communication scheme (rendez-vous, broadcast),
which are not hardcoded, but rather specified by the
designer of the system.

2.

We synthesize parameterized invariants directly from the
interaction formula of a system, without iterating its transition
relation. Such invariants depend only on the structure (and not on
the operational semantics) of an infinite family of Petri Nets, one
for each instance of the system, being thus
structural

invariants. Essentially, the invariants we infer use the

traps

2

verification problem (proving the emptiness of the intersection

3.

We refine the approach by considering another type of

cheaply from the interaction formula of the system. We show that

Comparison to related work.

Trap invariants have been very successfully used in the verification

succeeds in numerous cases. It is ideal as preprocessing step, that

\displaystyle\mathit{deadlock}(X_{w},X_{e},X_{f},X_{b})=\forall i~.~

\displaystyle[\neg X_{w}(i)\vee\neg X_{f}(i)\vee\neg X_{f}(\mathrm{succ}(i))]\;\wedge

\displaystyle[\neg X_{e}(i)\vee\neg X_{b}(i)\vee\neg X_{b}(\mathrm{succ}(i))]

\displaystyle[X_{w}(i)\vee X_{f}(i)\vee X_{f}(\mathrm{succ}(i))]\;\;\leftrightarrow

Parameterized Component-based Systems

A
component type
is a tuple
\mathcal{C}=\langle\mathsf{P},\mathsf{S},{s_{0}},\Delta\rangle
, where
\mathsf{P}=\{p,q,r,\ldots\}
is a finite set of
ports
,
\mathsf{S}
is a
finite set of
states
,
{s_{0}}\in\mathsf{S}
is an initial
state and
\Delta\subseteq\mathsf{S}\times\mathsf{P}\times\mathsf{S}
is a
set of
transitions
denoted
s\xrightarrow{{\scriptscriptstyle p}}s^{\prime}
, for
s,s^{\prime}\in\mathsf{S}
and
p\in\mathsf{P}
. We assume there are no two different transitions with the
same port.

A
component-based system

\mathcal{S}=\langle{\mathcal{C}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{{1}}},\ldots,{\mathcal{C}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{{N}}},\Gamma\rangle
consists of a fixed number

N\geq 1
of component types
{\mathcal{C}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{{k}}}=\langle{\mathsf{P}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{{k}}},{\mathsf{S}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{{k}}},{{s_{0}}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{{k}}},{\Delta}^{\scriptscriptstyle{{k}}}\rangle
and an

interaction formula

\Gamma
. In the dining philosophers
there are two component types,
\mathsf{Philosopher}
and

\mathsf{Fork}
, each with two states and two transitions, as shown in
Fig.
1
. We assume that
{\mathsf{P}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{{i}}}\cap{\mathsf{P}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{{j}}}=\emptyset
and
{\mathsf{S}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{{i}}}\cap{\mathsf{S}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{{j}}}=\emptyset
, for all
1\leq i<j\leq N
. We
denote the component type of a port
p
or a state
s
by
\mathit{type}({p})

and
\mathit{type}({s})
, respectively. For instance, in
Fig.
1
we have
\mathit{type}({p})=\mathit{type}({g})=\mathit{type}({w})=\mathit{type}({e})=\mathsf{Philosopher}
and
\mathit{type}({t})=\mathit{type}({\ell})=\mathit{type}({f})=\mathit{type}({b})=\mathsf{Fork}
.

The interaction formula
\Gamma
determines the family of systems
we can construct out of these components. It does so by specifying,
for each possible number of replicated instances (for example, 3
philosophers and 3 forks), which are the possible interactions between
them. An interaction consists of a set of transitions that are
executed simultaneously. For example, in an interaction philosopher 3
executes a
g
(et) transition simultaneously with
t
(ake) transitions
of the forks 2 and 3. Before formalizing this, we introduce the syntax
and semantics of Interaction Logic.

Interaction Logic.

For a constant
\kappa\geq 1
, fixed throughout the paper,
the
Interaction Logic

\mathsf{IL}{\kappa}
is built on top of a
countably infinite set
\mathsf{Var}
of variables, the set
\mathsf{Pred}=\bigcup_{k=1}^{N}{\mathsf{P}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{{k}}}
of monadic predicate symbols
ranged over by
\mathsf{pr}
(i.e. the logic has a predicate symbol for
each port), the binary predicate
\leq
, and the
successor

functions
\mathrm{succ}_{0},\ldots,\mathrm{succ}_{\kappa-1}
, of arity one. The
formulae of
\mathsf{IL}{\kappa}
are generated by the syntax

\begin{array}[]{rclr}t&:=&i\in\mathsf{Var}\mid\mathrm{succ}_{0}(t)\mid\ldots\mid\mathrm{succ}_{\kappa-1}(t)&\text{ terms}\\
\phi&:=&t_{1}\leq t_{2}\mid\mathsf{pr}(t)\mid\phi_{1}\wedge\phi_{2}\mid\neg\phi_{1}\mid\exists i~.~\phi_{1}&\text{ formulae}\end{array}

Abbreviations like
t_{1}=t_{2}
,
t_{1}<t_{2}
,
\phi_{1}\vee\phi_{2}
,

\phi_{1}\leftrightarrow\phi_{2}
, and
\forall i~.~\phi
are defined
as usual.
\mathsf{IL}{\kappa}
is interpreted over finite ranked trees of
arity
\kappa
, which we identify with a prefix-closed language of
words, also called
nodes
, over the alphabet
\{0,\ldots,\kappa-1\}
. The root of the tree is the empty word
\epsilon
, and
the children of
w
are
w0,w1,\ldots,w{(\kappa-1)}
. Formally, an

interpretation
or
structure
is a pair
\mathcal{I}=(\mathfrak{U},\iota)
,
where the universe
\mathfrak{U}
is a tree and
\iota
assigns a node to each
variable and a set of nodes to each predicate in
\mathsf{Pred}
. The
predicate
\leq
and the functions
\mathrm{succ}_{0},\ldots,\mathrm{succ}_{k-1}
have
the usual fixed interpretations: If
t
and
t^{\prime}
are interpreted as

w
and
w^{\prime}
, then
t_{1}\leq t_{2}
holds iff

is a prefix of

,
and
\mathrm{succ}_{i}(t)
is interpreted as the node

, and
as the root

functions wrap around to the root.

When
\kappa=1
, formulae are interpreted on languages
\{\epsilon,0,00,\ldots,0^{n-1}\}
for some number
n
. To simplify notation,
in this case we assume that they are interpreted over the set
\{0,1,\ldots,n-1\}
, and
\mathrm{succ}_{0}
is the usual successor function on
numbers, modulo
n
.

Intuitively, a universe
\mathfrak{U}
determines an instance of the
component-based system, with one instance of each component for each

w\in\mathfrak{U}
. So, for example, for
\kappa=1
and
\mathfrak{U}=\{0,1,2,\ldots,n-1\}
we have in our running example philosophers

and forks

. Generally, with

Interaction formulae.

A formula of
\mathsf{IL}{\kappa}
is an

if it is
the conjunction of the following formula:

\begin{array}[]{c}\forall i\forall j~.~\bigwedge_{\begin{array}[]{c}\scriptstyle{p,q\in\mathsf{Pred}}\\[-5.69054pt]
\scriptstyle{\mathit{type}({p})=\mathit{type}({q})}\end{array}}p(i)\wedge q(j)\rightarrow i\neq j\end{array}

(4)

with a finite disjunction of formulae of the form:

\begin{array}[]{c}\mathfrak{C}(i_{1},\ldots,i_{\ell})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle{\mathsf{def}}}}{{=}}\;\;\varphi\;\;\wedge\;\;\bigwedge_{j=1}^{\ell}p_{j}(i_{j})\;\;\wedge\;\;\bigwedge_{j=1}^{m}\forall k~.~\psi_{j}\rightarrow q_{j}(k)\end{array}

where
\varphi,\psi_{1},\ldots,\psi_{m}
are

2.1
Execution Semantics of Component-based Systems

The semantics of a component-based system
\mathcal{S}=\langle{\mathcal{C}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{{1}}},\ldots,{\mathcal{C}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{{N}}},\Gamma\rangle
is an infinite
family of Petri Nets, one for each universe of
\Gamma
.
The reachable markings and actions of the Petri Net characterize the reachable global
states and transitions of the system, respectively. To fix notations,
we recall several basic definitions.

Preliminaries: Petri Nets.

A
Petri Net
(PN) is a tuple
\mathsf{N}=\langle S,T,E\rangle
, where
S
is a set of

places
,
T
is a set of
transitions
,
S\cap T=\emptyset
, and

is a set of
edges
. The elements of

S\cup T
are called
nodes
. Given nodes

, we write
E(x,y)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle{\mathsf{def}}}}{{=}}1
if
(x,y)\in E
and

E(x,y)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle{\mathsf{def}}}}{{=}}0
, otherwise. For a node
x
, let
{\vphantom{\text{${x}$}}}{}^{\bullet}\text{${x}$}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle{\mathsf{def}}}}{{=}}\{y\in S\cup T\mid E(y,x)=1\}
,
{x}^{\bullet}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle{\mathsf{def}}}}{{=}}\{y\in S\cup T\mid E(x,y)=1\}
and lift these definitions to
sets of nodes.

A
marking
of
\mathsf{N}
is a function
\mathrm{m}:S\rightarrow{\bf\mathbb{N}}
. A transition
t
is
enabled
in
\mathrm{m}
if
and only if
\mathrm{m}(s)>0
for each place
s\in{\vphantom{\text{${t}$}}}^{\bullet}\text{${t}$}
.
For all markings
\mathrm{m}
,
\mathrm{m}^{\prime}
and transitions
t
, we write
\mathrm{m}\xrightarrow{{\scriptscriptstyle t}}\mathrm{m}^{\prime}
whenever
t
is enabled in
\mathrm{m}
and

\mathrm{m}^{\prime}(s)=\mathrm{m}(s)-E(s,t)+E(t,s)
, for all
s\in S
. Given two markings
\mathrm{m}
and
\mathrm{m}^{\prime}
, a finite sequence
of transitions
\sigma=t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}
is a
firing sequence
,
written
\mathrm{m}\xrightarrow{{\scriptscriptstyle\sigma}}\mathrm{m}^{\prime}
if and only if

(i)

and
\mathrm{m}=\mathrm{m}^{\prime}
, or

(ii)

and there exist markings
\mathrm{m}_{1},\ldots,\mathrm{m}_{n-1}
such that
\mathrm{m}\xrightarrow{{\scriptscriptstyle t_{1}}}\mathrm{m}_{1}\ldots\mathrm{m}_{n-1}\xrightarrow{{\scriptscriptstyle t_{n}}}\mathrm{m}^{\prime}
.

A
marked Petri Net
is a pair
\mathcal{N}=(\mathsf{N},\mathrm{m}_{0})
,
where
\mathrm{m}_{0}
is the

of

. A marking

\mathrm{m}
is

if there exists a firing

\sigma
such that
\mathrm{m}_{0}\xrightarrow{{\scriptscriptstyle\sigma}}\mathrm{m}
. We
denote by
\mathcal{R}({\mathcal{N}})
the set of reachable markings of

\mathcal{N}
. A marked PN

is
1
-safe
if

\mathrm{m}(s)\leq 1
, for each
s\in S
and
\mathrm{m}\in\mathcal{R}({\mathcal{N}})
. All PNs considered in the following will be
1-safe and we shall silently blur the distinction between a marking

\mathrm{m}:S\rightarrow\{0,1\}
and the boolean valuation

\beta_{\mathrm{m}}:S\rightarrow\{\bot,\top\}
defined as

\beta_{\mathrm{m}}(s)=\top\iff\mathrm{m}(s)=1
. A set of markings

\mathcal{M}
is an
inductive invariant
of

\mathcal{N}=(\mathsf{N},\mathrm{m}_{0})
if and only if
\mathrm{m}_{0}\in\mathcal{M}
and for each
\mathrm{m}\xrightarrow{{\scriptscriptstyle t}}\mathrm{m}^{\prime}
such that

\mathrm{m}\in\mathcal{M}
, we have
\mathrm{m}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}

Petri Net Semantics of Component-Based Systems.

intersection with the trap invariant. For self-completeness, we
briefly discuss the computation of the trap invariant for a given
marked Petri Net of fixed size, before explaining how this can be done
for the infinite family of marked Petri Nets defining the executions
of parameterized systems.

is viewed as a propositional variable.

From

We briefly recall the syntax and semantics of

\begin{array}[]{rclr}t&:=&\overline{\epsilon}\mid x\mid\mathrm{succ}_{0}(t)\mid\ldots\mid\mathrm{succ}_{\kappa}(t)&\text{ terms}\\

\begin{array}[]{c}x\leq y\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle{\mathsf{def}}}}{{=}}\forall X~.~\mathit{closed}(X)\wedge X(x)\rightarrow X(y)\hskip 14.22636pt\mathit{closed}(X)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle{\mathsf{def}}}}{{=}}\forall x~.~X(x)\rightarrow\bigwedge_{i=0}^{\kappa-1}X(\mathrm{succ}_{i}(x))\end{array}

Like
\mathsf{IL}{\kappa}

\mathsf{WS}\kappa\mathsf{S}
are interpreted on

\kappa
. The models of
\mathsf{WS}\kappa\mathsf{S}
are

, where

, defined recursively on the structure of

\mathit{Tr}(\mathrm{succ}_{i}(x)=y)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle{\mathsf{def}}}}{{=}}(\neg\max(x)\wedge\mathrm{succ}_{i}(x)=y)\vee(\max(x)\wedge y=\overline{\epsilon})

We show that a formula

Lemma 1

Defining Parameterized Trap Invariants in

encodes a set of places, and vice versa. So, abusing language, we can
speak of the set of places
\overline{X}
.

We define auxiliary predicates that capture the intersection of the
set of places

with the pre and postset of a

\small{\begin{array}[]{l}\intersectspre(\overline{X},x_{1},\dots,x_{\ell})=\bigvee_{j=1}^{\ell}X_{{\vphantom{\text{${p_{j}}$}}}{}^{\bullet}\text{${p_{j}}$}~}(x_{j})\vee\bigvee_{j=\ell+1}^{\ell+m}\exists x_{j}~.~\mathit{Tr}(\psi_{j})\wedge X_{{\vphantom{\text{${p_{j}}$}}}{}^{\bullet}\text{${p_{j}}$}~}(x_{j})\text{ and}\\

\begin{array}[]{r}\small{\forall x_{1}\ldots\forall x_{\ell}~.~\left[\mathit{Tr}(\varphi)\wedge\intersectspre(\overline{X},x_{1},\ldots,x_{\ell})\right]}\\

Lemma 2

\mathcal{S}=\langle{\mathcal{C}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{{1}}},\ldots,{\mathcal{C}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{{N}}},\Gamma\rangle

, where

, the set

Parameterized Trap Invariants in
\mathsf{WS}\kappa\mathsf{S}
.

Loosely speaking, the intended meaning of

\trappred(\overline{X})
is “the set of places

\overline{X}
is a trap”. Our goal is to construct a
formula stating: “the marking
m
marks all initially marked traps”.

Recall that the Petri Nets obtained from component-based systems are
always 1-safe, and so a marking is also a set of places. Recall,
however, that all reachable markings have the property that they place
exactly one token in the set of places modeling the set of states of a
component (loosely speaking, the set of places of the
k
-th
philosopher is
(w,k)
and
(e,k)
, and there is always one token in
the one or the other). So we define a formula

\marking(\overline{X})
with intended meaning “the set of
places
\overline{X}
is a legal marking”, and another one,

\trapconstraintpred(\overline{X})
with intended meaning
“the set of places
\overline{X}
marks every initially
marked trap (IMT)”.

In addition to the tuple of set variables
\overline{X}

defined above, we consider now the “copy” tuple

\overline{X^{\prime}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle{\mathsf{def}}}}{{=}}\langle X_{s}^{\prime}\rangle_{s\in{\mathsf{S}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{{i}}},1\leq i\leq N}
. Intuitively,
\overline{X}
and

\overline{X^{\prime}}
represent one set of places each.

We define a (
1
-safe) marking as a set of places that marks exactly one state of each copy of each
component:

\marking(\overline{X})=\forall x~.~\bigwedge\limits_{1\leq i\leq N}\bigvee\limits_{s\in{S}^{\scriptscriptstyle{{i}}}}\left(X_{s}(x)\wedge\bigwedge\limits_{s^{\prime}\in{S}^{\scriptscriptstyle{{i}}}~\setminus~\{s\}}\neg X_{s^{\prime}}(x)\right).

Secondly, we give a formula describing the intersection of two sets of places:

\intersection(\overline{X},\overline{X^{\prime}})=\exists x~.~\bigvee\limits_{s\in\bigcup_{1\leq i\leq N}{\mathsf{S}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{{i}}}}(X_{s}(x)\wedge X_{s}^{\prime}(x)).

Finally, to actually capture IMTs we need to determine if a trap is initially
marked. However, this can be easily described by the formula:

\initially(\overline{X})=\exists x~.~\bigvee\limits_{1\leq i\leq N}X_{{{s_{0}}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{{i}}}}(x).

and so we can define the
trap-invariant
by the
\mathsf{WS}\kappa\mathsf{S}
formula:

\begin{array}[]{r}\trapconstraintpred(\overline{X})=\forall\overline{X^{\prime}}~.~\left[\trappred(\overline{X^{\prime}})\wedge\initially(\overline{X^{\prime}})\right]\\
\rightarrow\intersection(\overline{X},\overline{X^{\prime}}).\end{array}

(7)

Relying on Lemma
2
we are assured that the set
represented by
\overline{X}
intersects all IMTs. Further,
let
\varphi(\overline{X})
be any formula that defines a
set of
good
global states of the component-based systems (or,
equivalently, a good set of markings of their corresponding Petri
nets), with the intuition that, at any moment during execution, the
current global state of the component-based system should be good. We
can now state the following theorem, that captures the soundness of
the verification method based on trap invariants:

Theorem 3.1

Given a component-based system
\mathcal{S}
and a
\mathsf{WS}\kappa\mathsf{S}
formula

\varphi(\overline{X})
, if the formula

\exists\overline{X}~.~\marking(\overline{X})\wedge\trapconstraintpred(\overline{X})\wedge\neg\varphi(\overline{X})

(8)

is unsatisfiable, then for every universe
\mathfrak{U}
, the property defined
by the formula
\varphi(\overline{X})
holds in every
reachable marking of
\mathcal{N}^{\mathfrak{U}}_{\mathcal{S}}
.

In the light of the above theorem, verifying the correctness of a
component-based system with any number of active components boils down
to deciding the satisfiability of a
\mathsf{WS}\kappa\mathsf{S}
formula. The latter
problem is known to be decidable, albeit in general, with
non-elementary recursive complexity. A closer look at the verification
conditions of the form (
8
) generated by our
method suffices to see that the quantifier alternation is finite,
which implies that the time needed to decide the (un)satisfiability of
(
8
) is elementary recursive. Moreover, our
experiments show that these checks are very fast (less than 1 second
on an average machine) for a non-trivial set of examples.

Proposition 1

Consider an instance of the alternating dining philosophers system
in Fig.
3

\mathsf{Fork}(0)

\mathsf{Philosopher}_{rl}(0)
,

\mathsf{Fork}(1)
,
\mathsf{Philosopher}_{lr}(1)
,

\mathsf{Fork}(2)
and
\mathsf{Philosopher}_{lr}(2)

\langle b,0\rangle,\langle h,0\rangle,\langle b,1\rangle,\langle w,1\rangle,\langle f,2\rangle

\langle f,i\rangle
and

\langle w,i\rangle

configuration, each fork is in state

neighboring philosophers is in state

deadlock freedom, one must learn this and other similar
constraints. Next, we present a heuristic method for strengthening the
trap invariant, that infers such universal constraints, involving a
fixed set of components.

4.1
One Invariants

As shown by the example above, trap constraints do sometimes fail to
prove interesting properties. Hence, it is desirable to refine the
overapproximation of viable markings to exclude more spurious
counterexamples. In order to do so, we consider a special class of

linear invariants
, called
1
-invariants
in the
following. Although linear invariants are not structural and rely on
the set of reachable markings of a marked Petri Net, the set of

1
-invariants can be sufficiently under-approximated by structural
conditions.

Definition 1

Given a marked PN
\mathcal{N}=((S,T,E),\mathrm{m}_{0})
, with
S=\{s_{1},\ldots,s_{n}\}
, a
vector
\mathbf{a}=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})\in\{0,1\}^{n}
is a

1
-invariant

\mathcal{N}
if and only if, for each
reachable marking
\mathrm{m}\in\mathcal{R}({\mathcal{N}})
, we have

There are tasks which can be either waiting,

The first observation is that the satisfiability checks often can be done

6
Conclusions

We have shown that the trap technique used
in
[
11
,
26
,
13
]
for the verification of single
systems can be extended to parameterized systems with sophisticated
communication structures, like pipelines, token rings and trees. Our
extension constructs a parameterized trap invariant, a formula of

\mathsf{WS}\kappa\mathsf{S}
satisfied by the reachable global states of all instances
of the system. The core of the approach is a purely syntactic,
automatic derivation of the trap invariant from the interaction
formula describing the possible transitions of the system. When the
set of safe global states can also be expressed in
\mathsf{WS}\kappa\mathsf{S}
, which
is usually the case, we check using the Mona tool whether the trap
invariant implies the safety formula. The technique proves correctness
of systems that do not produce well-structured transition systems in
the sense of
[
1
,
27
]
, and of systems with
broadcast communication, for which, to the best of our knowledge,
cut-off results have not been obtained yet.

Our experiments demonstrate that trap invariants can be very effective
in finding proofs of correctness (inductive invariants) of common
benchmark examples. In practice, the technique is very cheap, since it
avoids costly fixpoint computations. This suggests incorporating it
into other verifiers as a cheap preprocessing step.
