Quantum general covariance
Christian Gaß, José M. Gracia-Bondía, Karl-Henning Rehren, [6pt] Department of Mathematical Methods in Physics, Faculty of Physics,University of Warsaw, Pasteura 5, 02-093 Warszawa, Poland.

Abstract
The structure of quantum interactions with fields of helicity two (“gravitons”) is strongly constrained by three principles: positivity (Hilbert space), covariance, and locality of observables. To fulfil them simultaneously, some (non-observable) fields need to be non-local. We work with string-localized fields. The results then follow from the condition that entities closely related to observables, like the 𝕊 \mathbb{S} -matrix, be local and string-independent. They in particular reproduce the interactions dictated by general covariance in classical field theory. Graviton-matter couplings are consistent only when the graviton self-interaction is taken into account as well.

guise of the
string-independence

perceived inconsistency between general relativity and quantum field
theory. The main aim of SQFT is to avoid the difficulties associated
with “canonical quantization” of gauge theories, notably the use of
state spaces with indefinite metrics, by working with interaction
densities which are operators on a physical Hilbert space throughout
– so that observables are elements of an operator

string-localized

The dependence of the interaction density on the “string” is a total
derivative, so that the action (an integral over the density) is
string-independent, and so string-localization does not spoil locality
of a perturbative QFT model, at least in first order. The latter
property of the action appears to be a rather restrictive one;
however, it is satisfied by all interactions among particles of the
Standard Model. Here we intend to show that it can be satisfied also
both for the self-interaction of gravitons and for the interactions
between gravitons and matter.

On the face of it, the total-derivative property is sufficient to
ensure SI of the action only at first order of perturbation theory. At
higher orders, there will occur ‘‘obstructions’’ to SI. These must be
well-localized, so that they have a chance to be canceled by
higher-order interactions -- then we say ‘‘the obstruction is
resolved’’. Both the conditions on the interaction at the lower order
ensuring that resolution, and the form of the higher-order
interactions themselves, are therefore predictions of the approach.
They do not refer to an underlying classical theory, nor do they
assume gauge or diffeomorphism invariance, but arise as consistency
conditions for local and covariant quantum field models on
Hilbert space.

1.2
Quantum general covariance

Remarkably, for all the interactions of interest, one finds oneself in
the “lucky” situation of well-localized obstructions. In other
words, quantum field theory in the SQFT dispensation has the power to
predict on its own the structure of consistent interactions. In the
presence of interacting massive vector bosons, like in the Standard
Model (SM) that structure includes chirality of the weak interaction

[
22
]
, the Lie-algebra makeup of cubic self-couplings of
several vector bosons and the quartic self-coupling familiar from
Yang–Mills theory
[
19
]
, the need of a scalar field with its
potential terms
[
30
]
– without ever invoking “gauge
invariance” nor classical “Lagrangians”.

The above is shown here to hold also true for gravity: the unique
self-interactions obtained from the expansion of the classical
diffeomorphism invariant action of general relativity at first and
second order in terms of the Newton constant are coincident with the
self-interactions of our
h^{\mu\nu}(x;c)
. So we reproduce the
uniqueness of graviton self-interactions which is the general
conclusion of the various approaches presented in the beginning. The
main new feature in SQFT is the nature of the argument, totally based
on quantum principles.

We find a second, even more remarkable result, concerning the
couplings to matter. Free matter fields in a flat spacetime have
conserved stress-energy tensors
[
27
,
28
]
. In
perturbation theory, their time-ordered products fail to be conserved,
i.e., Ward identities are violated. Now, the coupling of matter to a
field of helicity 2 turns out to have an obstruction proportional to
the violation of the Ward identity. This obstruction cannot be
resolved on its own. However, on including the self-interaction of the
graviton field, one finds an interference term yielding another
obstruction. And both obstructions together can be resolved by a
higher-order matter coupling. That the latter coincide with the
second-order term of the expansion of the generally covariant matter
coupling is in the vein of the above: consistency of the quantum
couplings “predicts” general covariance.

This outcome (at second order of perturbation theory) reveals a kind
of “predestined match” between matter and gravity. On the one hand,
the violation of the matter’s Ward identity knows nothing about
gravity. On the other hand, the self-interaction of gravitons knows
nothing about matter. Yet, the two taken together produce a sum of
non-resolvable obstructions that together can be resolved – like a
lock and a key. Consistency is only achieved when the key and the
lock, apparently ignorant of each other, do meet. We are tempted to
call this mechanism, which does not require classical covariance:

“Quantum general covariance”
. The latter holds for matter
consisting of an arbitrary number of scalar, photon and Dirac fields.
We prove these facts here in due course.

1.3
Strategy of the paper

In order to arrive at our results, the overall strategy schematically
is: (i) Start with a first order input interaction and compute its
obstruction to SI. (ii) Make sure that the obstruction is resolvable.
This is separately the case for the self-interaction of gravitons, but
not for the coupling to matter. It is again true if both are taken
together. (iii) Determine the respective second-order interactions that
do the job. If necessary, continue to the next order.

We next elaborate a bit on how SQFT enters this strategy in the
subject at hand – leaving the filling in of the technical details and
proofs for the main body of the paper.

1.4
Final introductory comments

An extra word is in order concerning renormalizability. The field
strength
F
has scaling dimension 3, the string-localized field
h

-- as any other boson potential in SQFT -- has scaling dimension 1. In
spite of the latter’s good behaviour, the first-order interaction has
dimension 5, as we shall see. Thus it is not UV power-counting
renormalizable.

Our path to perturbative QG in a Minkowski background does not branch
from standard gauge theory in the first place; rather it has to be
regarded as an alternative to it. In the former, the BRST method is a
crucial tool to eliminate unphysical degrees of freedom after the

\displaystyle\delta_{c}\big(h_{\mu\nu}\big)=\delta_{c}(I_{c}^{\kappa}I_{c}^{\lambda})F_{[\mu\kappa][\nu\lambda]}=(\delta_{c}I_{c}^{\kappa})\partial_{[\mu}h_{\kappa]\nu}+(\mu\leftrightarrow\nu)=(\delta_{c}I_{c}^{\kappa})\partial_{\mu}h_{\kappa\nu}+(\mu\leftrightarrow\nu)=:\partial_{\mu}w_{\nu}+\partial_{\nu}w_{\mu}.

If
\delta c(e)
is compactly supported, then one may choose
b(e)
to
be compactly supported, and
w_{\mu}
is string-localized in the convex
hull of
\mathbb{R}_{+}\cdot\bigl(\supp(b)\cup\supp(c)\bigr)
.

By (
2.6
), the field
w_{\mu}
is divergenceless:

\displaystyle\partial^{\mu}w_{\mu}=0.

(2.8)

That the string variation (
2.7
) be a derivative is
essential in order to construct
\mathbb{S}
-matrices
T\exp\bigl(i\int d^{4}x\,L_{\rm int}(x;c)\bigr)
that do not depend on the profile
function
c
, from the interactions satisfying (
1.5
).

2.2
The
L
-
Q
pair formalism and the
condition of string-independence

In string-localized QFT, the perturbative
\mathbb{S}
-matrix can be defined
as a formal power series

\displaystyle\mathbb{S}[g;c]=1+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n!}\int d^{4}x_{1}\dots\int d^{4}x_{n}\,g(x_{1})\dots g(x_{n})\,S_{n}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n};c),

(2.9)

where
g
is a test function over Minkowski space and the
S_{n}
are
time-ordered products of a normal-ordered interaction density,
possibly plus local terms supported on the “small diagonal”:

\Delta_{n}:=(x_{1}=x_{2}=\dots=x_{n})
. Such local terms supported on

\Delta_{n}
either correspond to new Wick-polynomials in the fields or
to corrections to already existent ones. We call the new
Wick-polynomials
induced interactions
, while the corrections to
already existent ones correspond to a renormalization of their
prefactor (that is, they are
local counterterms
).

The expressions
S_{n}
depend on the string-smearing function
c
via
the contained string-localized fields. The first order term
S_{1}(x;c)

is related to the
\mathcal{O}(g)
-part of the interaction density,

\displaystyle S_{1}(x;c)\equiv iL_{1}(x;c).

(2.10)

At higher orders, the situation becomes more complicated. In general, we
will have

\displaystyle S_{2}(x_{1},x_{2};c)=i^{2}\,T_{\textup{ren}}[L_{1}(x_{1};c)L_{1}(x_{2};c)]+iL_{2}(x_{1};c)\delta(x_{1}-x_{2}),

(2.11)

where the renormalized time-ordered product
T_{\textup{ren}}[L_{1}(x_{1};c)L_{1}(x_{2};c)]
contains the local counterterms and
L_{2}(x_{1};c)
is an
induced interaction. Then the third order contribution will be a
time-ordered product

\displaystyle S_{3}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3};c)

\displaystyle=iT_{\textup{ren}}[S_{2}(x_{1},x_{2};c)S_{1}(x_{3};c)]+\textup{symmetric}

\displaystyle\quad+iL_{3}(x_{1};c)\delta(x_{1}-x_{2})\delta(x_{2}-x_{3}),

(2.12)

and so on. The full (unrenormalized) interaction density of the model
would then be:

\displaystyle L[g;c](x):=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{g(x)^{k}}{k!}L_{k}(x;c),

(2.13)

with normal-ordered induced interactions
L_{k}
. For the interactions
of the Standard Model,
L_{k}=0
for all
k>2
, and so one does not have
to look for induced couplings after third order. For graviton
interactions, it is expected that the series does not terminate.

The dependence of scattering amplitudes on the test function
c
(or
the existence of a preferred direction, if the support of
c
is very
narrow) is not observed in scattering experiments. Hence, one requires
that the
\mathbb{S}
-matrix be independent of the choice of
c
in the

adiabatic limit

g(x)\uparrow\mathrm{const}
, where the test
function
g
becomes a true coupling constant. That is,

\displaystyle\lim_{g\uparrow\mathrm{const}}\delta_{c}\big(\mathbb{S}[g;c]\big)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle!}}{{=}}0.

(2.14)

Requirement (
2.14
) is what we call the
SI
condition
for the
\mathbb{S}
-matrix. Provided that the adiabatic limit can
be performed, it is fulfilled if

\displaystyle\delta_{c}\big(S_{n}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n};c)\big)=i^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{k}^{\mu}}\,Q_{n}^{\mu}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n};c),\quad\forall n\in\mathbb{N},

(2.15)

so that after integration by parts, one finds

\displaystyle\delta_{c}\big(\mathbb{S}\big)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{i^{n}}{n!}\int d^{4}x_{1}\dots\int d^{4}x_{n}\,\sum_{k=1}^{n}g(x_{1})\dots(\partial_{\mu}g)(x_{k})\dots g(x_{n})\,Q_{n}^{\mu}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle g\uparrow\mathrm{const}}}{{\longrightarrow}}0.

As already indicated in (
1.5
), at first order in perturbation
theory the SI condition implies the
L
-
Q
-pair condition
\delta_{c}\big(L_{1}(x;c)\big)=\partial_{\mu}Q_{1}^{\mu}(x;c)
. That is, the string
variation of the interaction density must be a total divergence. In
this paper we only consider tree graph contributions up to second
order of perturbation theory.
In particular, we will not have to deal with local counterterms, but
only with induced interactions. By Wick’s theorem, modulo loop graphs
we have:

\displaystyle T_{\textup{ren}}[L_{1}(x_{1};c)L_{1}(x_{2};c)]=\,\mathopen{:}L_{1}(x_{1};c)L_{1}(x_{2};c)\mathclose{:}\,+\,\mathopen{:}\underbracket{L_{1}(x_{1};c)L_{1}}(x_{2};c)\mathclose{:}\,+\dots,\qquad

(2.16)

\displaystyle\quad\text{where}\quad\,\mathopen{:}\underbracket{L_{1}(x_{1};c)L_{1}}(x_{2};c)\mathclose{:}\,\,:=\sum_{\varphi,\chi}\,\mathopen{:}\frac{\partial L_{1}}{\partial\varphi}(x_{1};c)\langle\!\langle T_{\textup{ren}}\varphi(x_{1};c)\chi(x_{2};c)\rangle\!\rangle\frac{\partial L_{1}}{\partial\chi}(x_{2};c)\mathclose{:}\,

(2.17)

is the sum of all terms with one contraction. The fields

\varphi,\chi
may depend on the string-smearing function
c
, and

\langle\!\langle T_{\textup{ren}}\varphi(x_{1})\chi(x_{2})\rangle\!\rangle
denotes the renormalized
time-ordered two-point function of
\varphi
and
\chi
, whose precise
meaning is explained in the next subsection
2.3
.
Under application of
\delta_{c}
, the first term on the right-hand side
of (
2.16
) is a total divergence by virtue of the

L
-
Q
-pair condition. Then the SI condition is satisfied at second-order
tree level if

\displaystyle\delta_{c}\bigl(\,\mathopen{:}\underbracket{L_{1}(x_{1};c)L_{1}}(x_{2};c)\mathclose{:}\,\bigr)=\sum_{k=1}^{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{k}^{\mu}}\,Q_{2,\textup{tree}}^{\mu}(x_{1},x_{2};c)+i\delta_{c}\big(L_{2,\textup{tree}}(x_{1})\big)\delta(x_{1}-x_{2}).

(2.18)

For our present aims, the precise form of
Q_{n}^{\mu}
,

Q_{n,\textup{tree}}^{\mu}
and other total divergences is not required.

2.3
Two-point functions and propagators

All two-point functions and propagators (i.e. time-ordered two-point
functions) can be respectively expressed in terms of the two-point
functions and propagators of scalar fields
\phi
of mass
m\geq 0
:

\displaystyle\langle\!\langle\phi(x)\phi(x^{\prime})\rangle\!\rangle=\int\frac{d^{4}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}\,\delta(k^{2}-m^{2})\theta(k^{0})\,e^{-ik(x-x^{\prime})}=:

\displaystyle~W_{m}(x-x^{\prime}),

(2.19)

\displaystyle\langle\!\langle T\phi(x)\phi(x^{\prime})\rangle\!\rangle=i\int\frac{d^{4}k}{(2\pi)^{4}}\frac{e^{-ik(x-x^{\prime})}}{k^{2}-m^{2}+i\varepsilon}=:

\displaystyle~D_{m,F}(x-x^{\prime}).

(2.20)

The two-point function for the Maxwell field strength tensor (of
helicity 1) is

\displaystyle\langle\!\langle F_{\mu\nu}(x)F_{\kappa\lambda}(x^{\prime})\rangle\!\rangle=-\eta_{\mu\kappa}\partial_{\nu}\partial^{\prime}_{\lambda}W_{0}(x-x^{\prime})-(\mu\leftrightarrow\nu)-(\kappa\leftrightarrow\lambda).

(2.21)

The two-point function for the field strength tensor of helicity 2 was
given in (
1
). It is of course consistent with the
relations (
2.1
) and the wave equation.
Likewise, the “kinematic” propagator is obtained by replacing the
massless two-point function
W_{0}
by the massless propagator

D_{0,F}
.

Similarly, the kinematic propagator for
h_{\mu\nu}
is defined by
applying the string integrations as in (
2.2
). The result
is:

\displaystyle\langle\!\langle T_{0}h_{\mu\nu}(x)h_{\rho\sigma}(x^{\prime})\rangle\!\rangle=

\displaystyle~\hbox{\large{$\frac{1}{2}$}}\bigl[E^{\prime}_{\mu\rho}E^{\prime}_{\nu\sigma}+E^{\prime}_{\mu\sigma}E^{\prime}_{\nu\rho}-E_{\mu\nu}E^{\prime\prime}_{\rho\sigma}\bigr]D_{0,F}(x-x^{\prime}),

(2.22)

where (with
I_{c}^{\prime}
the string integrations w.r.t.
x^{\prime}
):

\displaystyle E_{\mu\nu}:=~

\displaystyle\eta_{\mu\nu}+I_{c,\mu}\partial_{\nu}+I_{c,\nu}\partial_{\mu}+I^{2}_{c}\partial_{\mu}\partial_{\nu},

\displaystyle E^{\prime\prime}_{\rho\sigma}:=~

\displaystyle\eta_{\rho\sigma}+I^{\prime}_{c,\rho}\partial^{\prime}_{\sigma}+I^{\prime}_{c,\sigma}\partial^{\prime}_{\rho}+I^{\prime 2}_{c}\partial^{\prime}_{\rho}\partial^{\prime}_{\sigma},

\displaystyle E^{\prime}_{\mu\rho}:=~

\displaystyle\eta_{\mu\rho}+I^{\prime}_{c,\mu}\partial^{\prime}_{\rho}+I_{c,\rho}\partial_{\mu}+(I_{c}I_{c}^{\prime})\partial_{\mu}\partial^{\prime}_{\rho},

(2.23)

cf.
[
28
]
in this respect. Kinematic propagators of
derivatives of
h_{\mu\nu}
are obtained by applying such
derivatives in Eq. (
2.22
).

The kinematic propagator (
2.22
) and its derivatives respect
the symmetry of
h_{\mu\nu}
, but because of

\displaystyle\square D_{0,F}(x-x^{\prime})=-i\delta(x-x^{\prime})

(2.24)

they do not respect the trace condition in (
2.6
) –
consult (
B.10
) in this respect. This would seem to
imply that the propagators reflect a non-existent trace degree of
freedom – an unacceptable feature in a Hilbert space theory.
Fortunately, the linear dependency among field components can be
restored by exploiting the freedom of renormalization of propagators
(see again App.
B
). The matter generally consists in
adding string-integrals of derivatives of
\delta(x-x^{\prime})
:

\displaystyle\langle\!\langle T_{\rm ren}\phi(x)\chi(x^{\prime})\rangle\!\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\!\langle T_{0}\phi(x)\chi(x^{\prime})\rangle\!\rangle+\langle\!\langle T_{r}\phi(x)\chi(x^{\prime})\rangle\!\rangle,

\displaystyle\quad\text{where}\quad\quad\langle\!\langle T_{r}\phi(x)\chi(x^{\prime})\rangle\!\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{\underline{\mu},\underline{\nu},\underline{\lambda}}iC^{\phi,\chi}_{\underline{\mu},\underline{\nu},\underline{\lambda}}I_{c}^{\underline{\mu}}{I^{\prime}}_{c}^{\underline{\nu}}\,\partial^{\underline{\lambda}}\,\delta(x-x^{\prime}),

(2.25)

with numerical coefficients
C
.
\underline{\lambda}
etc. are Lorentz multi-indices. The lengths
|\underline{\mu}|
and

|\underline{\nu}|
give the numbers of string-integrations, and

4+|\underline{\lambda}|-|\underline{\mu}|-|\underline{\nu}|
is the scaling dimension of the
renormalization term. By the standard power counting argument, these
numbers cannot exceed those of
\langle\!\langle T_{0}\phi\chi^{\prime}\rangle\!\rangle
. Since we do not
want to break scale invariance of a massless theory already at the
tree level, we shall actually demand
equality
of the scaling
dimensions.

The coefficients in (
2.25
) can be adjusted so that
the trace conditions are restored. Further constraints on these
coefficients will arise from the condition that the second-order
obstruction has the form (
1.8
) without string-integrated

\delta
-functions. For the details, see App.
B
.

The renormalized propagators determine the obstructions to SI.
Propagators for matter fields and their renormalizations are studied
in Sect.
3.1
.

3
Graviton couplings on Hilbert space

We set out now to derive up to second order of perturbation theory in
the framework of SQFT the structure of graviton couplings to the
stress-energy tensors of the
scalar, Maxwell and Dirac

“matter” fields, as well as the structure of the graviton
self-coupling.

As already indicated, to describe spin one-half fermions and scalars
the SQFT construction is unnecessary, their stress-energy tensors thus
being point-localized from the outset. In the case of Maxwell fields,
one might envisage coupling the string-localized graviton potential to
a string-localized stress-energy tensor for helicity 1. It turns out
however
[
17
]
that the
L
-
Q
-pair condition (
1.5
) can only
hold when the string-localized graviton is coupled to the well-known

point-localized
stress energy-tensor of the Maxwell field.

Notation 3.1
.

To simplify notation in what follows, we drop the colon-notation for
Wick-ordering and write
A\equiv A(x)
and
A^{\prime}\equiv A(x^{\prime})
for
generic fields
A
(possibly Wick polynomials), and
\varphi
,
\chi^{\prime}

for generic linear fields. It will be also convenient to write

\displaystyle(AB)_{\mu\nu}:=A_{\mu\kappa}B^{\kappa}_{\nu},\quad(\!(AB)\!):=A_{\kappa\lambda}B^{\kappa\lambda}

for contractions of symmetric tensors
A
and
B
, and similarly for
just one tensor or products of more than two tensors: i.e.,

(\!(A)\!)
is the trace of
A
. We will write
A\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textup{div}}}{{=}}B
when
expressions
A
and
B
differ by a total divergence.

3.1
Graviton coupling to stress-energy tensors

For the moment, let
\Theta^{\mu\nu}_{\textup{mat}}
denote the (symmetric
conserved, point-localized) stress-energy tensor associated with a
generic “matter” field, and consider the coupling:

\displaystyle L_{1,\textup{mat}}:=\hbox{\large{$\frac{1}{2}$}}(\!(h\Theta_{\textup{mat}})\!).

(3.1)

In view of (
2.7
), clearly
L_{1,\textup{mat}}
of above
satisfies the
L
-
Q
-pair condition:

\displaystyle\delta_{c}\big(L_{1,\textup{mat}}\big)=\partial_{\mu}Q_{1,\textup{mat}}^{\mu}\quad\text{with}\quad Q_{1,\textup{mat}}^{\mu}=w_{\nu}\,\Theta^{\mu\nu}_{\textup{mat}}.

(3.2)

From Eq. (
2.17
), the renormalized second order tree
graph can be expanded as

\displaystyle\underbracket{L_{1,\textup{mat}}L}\!_{1,\textup{mat}}

\displaystyle=\hbox{\large{$\frac{1}{4}$}}\Theta^{\mu\nu}_{\textup{mat}}\langle\!\langle T_{\textup{ren}}h_{\mu\nu}h^{\prime}_{\rho\sigma}\rangle\!\rangle{\Theta^{\prime}}^{\rho\sigma}_{\textup{mat}}+\hbox{\large{$\frac{1}{4}$}}h_{\mu\nu}h^{\prime}_{\rho\sigma}\sum_{\varphi,\chi^{\prime}}\frac{\partial\Theta^{\mu\nu}_{\textup{mat}}}{\partial\varphi}\langle\!\langle T_{\textup{ren}}\varphi\chi^{\prime}\rangle\!\rangle\frac{\partial{\Theta^{\prime}}^{\rho\sigma}_{\textup{mat}}}{\partial\chi^{\prime}}.

(3.3)

We next have to keep an eye on the renormalization of the graviton
propagators in App.
B
. By inspection of (
2.22
)
and (
2.23
), we see that all string-dependent parts of the
kinematic propagator contain uncontracted derivatives. Because the
stress-energy tensors are conserved, this means that they only
contribute divergences to the first term in (
3.3
). To
ensure the same for the renormalized propagator
\langle\!\langle T_{r}hh^{\prime}\rangle\!\rangle
, as
well it must contain only terms with uncontracted derivatives, or
string-independent terms. The latter cannot occur by the bounds
discussed around Eq. (
2.25
). Thus a necessary
condition for the string variation of expression (
3.3
)
to satisfy Eq. (
1.8
) is to impose the form (
B.11
) as a
renormalization condition on
\langle\!\langle T_{r}hh^{\prime}\rangle\!\rangle
.

In summary, the string variation of the first term in
(
3.3
) is a total divergence and we find:

\displaystyle\delta_{c}\Big(\underbracket{L_{1,\textup{mat}}L}\!_{1,\textup{mat}}\Big)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textup{div}}}{{=}}-\hbox{\large{$\frac{1}{2}$}}h^{\prime}_{\rho\sigma}w_{\nu}\,\partial_{\mu}\sum_{\varphi,\chi^{\prime}}\frac{\partial\Theta^{\mu\nu}_{\textup{mat}}}{\partial\varphi}\langle\!\langle T_{\textup{ren}}\varphi\chi^{\prime}\rangle\!\rangle\frac{\partial{\Theta^{\prime}}^{\rho\sigma}_{\textup{mat}}}{\partial\chi^{\prime}}+(x\leftrightarrow x^{\prime}).

That is to say, obstructions to second-order SI at tree level can only
be caused by a violation of the
Ward identity
associated to
the (point-localized) matter stress-energy tensors, that is:

\displaystyle\partial_{\mu}T_{\textup{ren}}\Theta^{\mu\nu}_{\textup{mat}}{\Theta^{\prime}}^{\rho\sigma}_{\textup{mat}}\Big|_{\rm tree}=\partial_{\mu}\sum_{\varphi,\chi^{\prime}}\frac{\partial\Theta^{\mu\nu}_{\textup{mat}}}{\partial\varphi}\langle\!\langle T_{\textup{ren}}\varphi\chi^{\prime}\rangle\!\rangle\frac{\partial{\Theta^{\prime}}^{\rho\sigma}_{\textup{mat}}}{\partial\chi^{\prime}}\neq 0.

(3.4)

We compute the obstructions to string-independence at second order
tree level corresponding to the possible violation of the Ward
identities for the following stress-energy tensors:

\displaystyle\Theta^{\mu\nu}_{\phi}

\displaystyle=\partial^{\mu}\phi\partial^{\nu}\phi-\tfrac{1}{2}\eta^{\mu\nu}\big(\partial_{\kappa}\phi\partial^{\kappa}\phi-m^{2}\phi^{2}\big)

\displaystyle\text{of a scalar field }\phi(x);

(3.5)

\displaystyle\Theta^{\mu\nu}_{F}

\displaystyle=-F^{\mu\kappa}{F^{\nu}}_{\kappa}+\tfrac{1}{4}\eta^{\mu\nu}F^{\kappa\lambda}F_{\kappa\lambda}

\displaystyle\text{of the Maxwell tensor}~F_{\mu\nu}(x);

(3.6)

\displaystyle\Theta^{\mu\nu}_{\psi}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{i}{4}\Bigl(\overline{\psi}\gamma^{\mu}\overset{\leftrightarrow}{\partial^{\nu}}\psi+\overline{\psi}\gamma^{\nu}\overset{\leftrightarrow}{\partial^{\mu}}\psi\Bigr)

\displaystyle\text{of a Dirac field }\psi(x).

(3.7)

Notice first that the trace parts of the stress-energy tensors do not
contribute to the tree graph, because
h_{\mu\nu}
is traceless and
the renormalizations are determined such that

\displaystyle\eta^{\mu\nu}\langle\!\langle T_{\textup{ren}}h_{\mu\nu}h^{\prime}_{\rho\sigma}\rangle\!\rangle=0=\eta^{\rho\sigma}\langle\!\langle T_{\textup{ren}}h_{\mu\nu}h^{\prime}_{\rho\sigma}\rangle\!\rangle.

We are going to show:

Proposition 3.2
.

For the matter couplings
L_{1,\rm mat}=\frac{1}{2}h_{\mu\nu}\Theta^{\mu\nu}_{\rm mat}
with either of
(
3.5
)–(
3.7
), there is a
non-resolvable obstruction of the interaction
L_{1,\rm mat}
, of
the universal form:

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{2,\rm mat}(x,x^{\prime})=-i\,\Theta^{\mu\nu}_{\rm mat}\,w^{\kappa}\big(\partial_{\kappa}h_{\mu\nu}-\partial_{\mu}h_{\kappa\nu}-\partial_{\nu}h_{\kappa\mu}\big)\,\delta(x-x^{\prime}).

(3.8)

The proof proceeds by a case-by-case analysis in the next three
subsections. The cases are mutually dissimilar enough that one is
allowed to conjecture universality of the above form.

3.1.1
Graviton coupling to scalar fields

Having seen that the contractions
\langle\!\langle T_{\textup{ren}}hh^{\prime}\rangle\!\rangle
in

\underbracket{L_{1,\phi}L^{\prime}}\!\!_{1,\phi}
do not contribute to the
obstruction, we study the contractions of the scalar fields. Since the
string-localized graviton potential is traceless, in view of
Eq. (
3.3
) those are:

\displaystyle\hbox{\large{$\frac{1}{4}$}}h_{\mu\nu}h^{\prime}_{\rho\sigma}\sum_{\varphi,\chi^{\prime}}\frac{\partial\Theta^{\mu\nu}_{\phi}}{\partial\varphi}\langle\!\langle T_{\textup{ren}}\varphi\chi^{\prime}\rangle\!\rangle\frac{\partial{\Theta^{\prime}_{\phi}}^{\rho\sigma}}{\partial\chi^{\prime}}=h_{\mu\nu}\partial^{\mu}\phi\langle\!\langle T_{\textup{ren}}\,\partial^{\nu}\phi\,{\partial^{\prime}}^{\sigma}\phi^{\prime}\rangle\!\rangle h^{\prime}_{\rho\sigma}{\partial^{\prime}}^{\rho}\phi^{\prime}.

(3.9)

Because the scaling dimension of the renormalized propagator on the
right-hand side of (
3.9
) equals the
spacetime dimension, it admits one free renormalization parameter:

\displaystyle\langle\!\langle T_{\textup{ren}}\partial^{\nu}\phi\,{\partial^{\prime}}^{\sigma}\phi^{\prime}\rangle\!\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\!\langle T_{0}\,\partial^{\nu}\phi\,{\partial^{\prime}}^{\sigma}\phi^{\prime}\rangle\!\rangle+\langle\!\langle T_{r}\partial^{\nu}\phi\,{\partial^{\prime}}^{\sigma}\phi^{\prime}\rangle\!\rangle

(3.10)

\displaystyle=\langle\!\langle T_{0}\partial^{\nu}\phi{\partial^{\prime}}^{\sigma}\phi^{\prime}\rangle\!\rangle+ic_{\phi}\eta^{\nu\sigma}\delta(x-x^{\prime}).

The non-kinematic part of (
3.9
) can
always be absorbed into an
induced interaction
:

\displaystyle L_{2,r,\phi}:=c_{\phi}(hh)^{\mu\nu}\partial_{\mu}\phi{\partial}_{\nu}\phi=c_{\phi}\big((\!(hh\Theta_{\phi})\!)+(\!(hh)\!)L_{0,\phi}\big),

where
L_{0,\phi}
is the quadratic (free) scalar “Lagrangian”
visible in (
3.5
), so that

\displaystyle iL_{2,r,\phi}\delta(x-x^{\prime})+i^{2}h_{\mu\nu}\partial^{\mu}\phi\,\langle\!\langle T_{r}\partial^{\nu}\phi{\partial^{\prime}}^{\sigma}\phi^{\prime}\rangle\!\rangle\,h^{\prime}_{\rho\sigma}{\partial^{\prime}}^{\rho}\phi^{\prime}=0.

In conclusion,
only the kinematic part
can contribute a
possible obstruction to SI. Since one has for it:

\displaystyle\partial_{\mu}\langle\!\langle T_{0}\partial^{\mu}\phi{\partial^{\prime}}^{\sigma}\phi^{\prime}\rangle\!\rangle=\square{\partial^{\prime}}^{\sigma}D_{m,F}(x-x^{\prime})=-m^{2}\langle\!\langle T_{0}\,\phi{\partial^{\prime}}^{\sigma}\phi^{\prime}\rangle\!\rangle-i{\partial^{\prime}}^{\sigma}\delta(x-x^{\prime}),

we find

\displaystyle\delta_{c}\Big(\underbracket{L_{1,\phi}L^{\prime}}\!\!_{1,\phi}\big|_{T_{0}}\Big)=\delta_{c}\big(h_{\mu\nu}\partial^{\mu}\phi\,\langle\!\langle T_{0}\partial^{\nu}\phi{\partial^{\prime}}^{\sigma}\phi^{\prime}\rangle\!\rangle\,h^{\prime}_{\rho\sigma}{\partial^{\prime}}^{\rho}\phi^{\prime}\big)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textup{div}}}{{=}}w_{\mu}\partial^{\mu}\phi\,h^{\prime}_{\rho\sigma}{\partial^{\prime}}^{\rho}\phi^{\prime}{\partial^{\prime}}^{\sigma}\cdot i\delta(x-x^{\prime})+(x\leftrightarrow x^{\prime})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textup{div}}}{{=}}:i\delta_{c}\big(L_{2,0,\phi}\big)\delta(x-x^{\prime})+\mathcal{O}_{2,\phi}(x,x^{\prime}),

with the
induced interaction
density:

\displaystyle L_{2,0,\phi}=(hh)_{\mu\nu}\Theta_{\phi}^{\mu\nu}+\hbox{\large{$\frac{1}{4}$}}(\!(hh)\!)\big((\partial\phi\partial\phi)-m^{2}\phi^{2}\big)=(\!(hh\Theta_{\phi})\!)+\hbox{\large{$\frac{1}{2}$}}(\!(hh)\!)L_{0,\phi},

plus the remaining
obstruction
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{2,\phi}(x,x^{\prime})=\Theta_{\phi}^{\mu\nu}w^{\kappa}\big(\partial_{\mu}h_{\kappa\nu}+\partial_{\nu}h_{\kappa\mu}-i\partial_{\kappa}h_{\mu\nu}\big)\delta(x-x^{\prime}),

(3.11)

which cannot be resolved by adding an induced term with field content

(\phi,\phi,h,h)
. In total, we collect:

\displaystyle\delta_{c}\Bigl(\underbracket{L_{1,\phi}L^{\prime}}\!\!_{1,\phi}\Bigr)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textup{div}}}{{=}}\delta_{c}\bigl((1+c_{\phi})(\!(hh\Theta_{\phi})\!)+i\big(\hbox{\large{$\frac{1}{2}$}}+c_{\phi}\bigr)(\!(hh)\!)L_{0,\phi}\bigr)\delta(x-x^{\prime})+\mathcal{O}_{2,\phi}(x,x^{\prime}).

3.1.2
Graviton coupling to electromagnetic fields

Keeping in mind that that contractions
\langle\!\langle T_{\textup{ren}}hh^{\prime}\rangle\!\rangle
in

\underbracket{L_{1,\phi}L^{\prime}}\!\!_{1,\phi}
did not contribute to the
obstruction, we now study the contractions for electromagnetic fields:

\displaystyle\quad\hbox{\large{$\frac{1}{4}$}}h_{\mu\nu}h^{\prime}_{\rho\sigma}\sum_{\varphi,\chi^{\prime}}\frac{\partial\Theta^{\mu\nu}_{F}}{\partial\varphi}\langle\!\langle T_{\textup{ren}}\varphi\chi^{\prime}\rangle\!\rangle\frac{\partial{\Theta^{\prime}_{F}}^{\rho\sigma}}{\partial\chi^{\prime}}=h_{\mu\nu}{F^{\mu}}_{\kappa}\langle\!\langle T_{\textup{ren}}F^{\nu\kappa}{F^{\prime}}^{\sigma\lambda}\rangle\!\rangle h^{\prime}_{\rho\sigma}{{F^{\prime}}^{\rho}}_{\lambda}.

(3.12)

Similarly to the scalar case, the renormalized propagator of the Maxwell
field has an ambiguity:

\displaystyle\langle\!\langle T_{\textup{ren}}F^{\nu\kappa}{F^{\prime}}^{\sigma\lambda}\rangle\!\rangle=\langle\!\langle T_{0}F^{\nu\kappa}{F^{\prime}}^{\sigma\lambda}\rangle\!\rangle+\langle\!\langle T_{r}F^{\nu\kappa}{F^{\prime}}^{\sigma\lambda}\rangle\!\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\!\langle T_{0}F^{\nu\kappa}{F^{\prime}}^{\sigma\lambda}\rangle\!\rangle+ic_{F}\bigl(\eta^{\nu\sigma}\eta^{\kappa\lambda}-\eta^{\nu\lambda}\eta^{\kappa\sigma}\bigr)\delta(x-x^{\prime}),

with a free parameter
c_{F}
. As before, the non-kinematic part can
be absorbed into an induced interaction

\displaystyle L_{2,r,F}:=

4
Conclusion and further discussion

For perturbative QFT of helicity 2 on a Minkowski background we have
established here a remarkable coincidence between the structure of
quantum interactions – dictated by the quantum principles:

Hilbert space (positivity), covariance, locality of observables
– and of

classical
“Lagrangians” – dictated by classical general
covariance. It pertains not only to the self-interaction of particles
of spin 2 (“gravitons”), but also to matter couplings that go
necessarily through the stress-energy tensor.

Now, the quantum principles are well-known to exclude each other for
helicity
\geq 1
in standard axiomatic setups of QFT. This
long-standing barrier has been conquered by admitting string-localized
free fields and interactions, and imposing the condition of
string-independence on the
\mathbb{S}
-matrix.

There are of course fundamental differences between classical
Lagrangians and interaction densities in Quantum Field Theory. The
former are the starting point of a variational principle to derive the
equations of motion. One may freely add terms that are total
divergences
without using the equations of motion
. E.g., the
classical expansion of the Einstein Lagrangian as a power series in
the metric deviation field – see
[
35
]
. In contrast, the
(perturbative)
\mathbb{S}
-matrix of QFT is defined as the time-ordered
exponential of the action, i.e., the integral over the
interaction density

\displaystyle\int d^{4}x\,L_{\rm int}(x),

(4.1)

not including a free Lagrangian. This interaction density is a
functional of the free fields, that are autonomously defined on the
Fock space over Wigner’s unitary representations of the Poincaré
group, automatically operating on a Hilbert space. The equations of
motion of the free fields are consequences of their construction.
(There is a similar split in the path integral approach: the “free
Lagrangian” only defines the integration measure, while the integrand
contains only the interaction Lagrangian.) Thus one may freely add
terms to the interaction density that are total derivatives as quantum
operators, i.e., when the free equations of motion are used,
without changing the action (
4.1
). The last term in
(
3.23
) is an example. It must not be discarded in classical
field theory.

Now, adding a divergence does affect the
\mathbb{S}
-matrix, because the
time-ordered exponential of the action is changed. A thorough analysis
can be found in
[
30
]
. There, the point was to add
divergences as to turn a non-renormalizable point-localized
interaction into a renormalizable string-localized one, or a
gauge-theoretic interaction on an indefinite Fock space into a
string-localized interaction in an embedded Hilbert space, as in BRST
theory.

The analysis is not restricted to such cases. The general tenor is
that if two interactions
L_{1}=K_{1}+\partial_{\mu}V_{1}^{\mu}
differ by a
divergence, then the induced interactions will differ by
L_{2}-K_{2}
,
such that

\displaystyle\hbox{\large{$\frac{1}{2}$}}\bigl(\partial_{\mu}TV_{1}^{\mu}(L^{\prime}_{1}+K^{\prime}_{1})-T\partial_{\mu}V_{1}^{\mu}(L^{\prime}_{1}+K^{\prime}_{1})\bigr)+(x\leftrightarrow x^{\prime})=i(L_{2}-K_{2})(x)\delta(x-x^{\prime}).

(4.2)

In other words, the effect of adding a divergence in first order is
compensated by an induced term in second order, and so on, which
itself is not a divergence. It is then established that the resulting

\mathbb{S}
-matrices (including the induced higher-order interactions) will
be the same. Thus, physics cannot tell the difference between those
different choices of the interaction density.

To be sure, the story is a bit subtler. Formal expressions like
\int d^{4}x\,L(x)
exist as quantum operators only with a cutoff function

g(x)
, and the “adiabatic limit”
g(x)\uparrow 1
is particularly
laborious in theories with massless fields. It is therefore important
that the equality of
\mathbb{S}
-matrices hold for arbitrary cutoff
functions – as shown in
[
30
]
– before the limit is taken.

\displaystyle~\frac{\sqrt{-g}}{2}\big(g^{\mu\nu}\partial_{\mu}\chi\partial_{\nu}\chi-m^{2}\chi^{2})

(C.9)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~L_{0,\phi}+\hbox{\large{$\frac{\kappa}{2}$}}(\!(h\Theta_{\phi})\!)+\hbox{\large{$\frac{\kappa^{2}}{2}$}}\Bigl[\hbox{\large{$\frac{1}{2}$}}(\!(hh)\!)L_{0,\phi}+\hbox{\large{$\frac{1}{4}$}}(\!(hh)\!)(\!(\Theta_{\phi})\!)\Bigr]+\dots

and of the generally covariant Maxwell Lagrangian:

\displaystyle L_{g,F}=

(C.10)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~L_{0,F}+\hbox{\large{$\frac{\kappa}{2}$}}(\!(h\Theta_{F})\!)+\hbox{\large{$\frac{\kappa^{2}}{2}$}}\big[\hbox{\large{$\frac{1}{2}$}}(\!(hh)\!)L_{0,F}-\hbox{\large{$\frac{1}{2}$}}h^{\mu\nu}h^{\kappa\lambda}F_{\mu\kappa}F_{\nu\lambda}\big]+\dots

The case of the Dirac field is more intricate. The generally covariant Lagrangian is

\displaystyle L_{g,\psi}=\sqrt{-g}\cdot\overline{\psi}\big(\hbox{\large{$\frac{i}{2}$}}(\gamma_{g}^{\mu}D_{\mu}-\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\leftarrow}}{{D}}_{\mu}\!\!\gamma_{g}^{\mu})-m\big)\psi

(C.11)

where the covariant
\gamma
-matrices
satisfy
\gamma_{g}^{\mu}\gamma_{g}^{\nu}+\gamma_{g}^{\nu}\gamma_{g}^{\mu}=2g^{\mu\nu}
,
and the covariant derivative is defined with the spin
connection. These are defined in terms of a real tetrad
field
e^{\mu}_{\alpha}(x)
such that

\displaystyle g^{\mu\nu}(x)=e^{\mu}_{\alpha}(x)e^{\nu}_{\beta}(x)\eta^{\alpha\beta}.

(C.12)

The tetrad gives
\gamma_{g}^{\mu}
in terms
of “flat” Dirac matrices satisfying

\gamma^{\alpha}\gamma^{\beta}+\gamma^{\beta}\gamma^{\alpha}=2\eta^{\alpha\beta}
:

\displaystyle\gamma_{g}^{\mu}:=e^{\mu}_{\alpha}\gamma^{\alpha}

(C.13)

as well as the
coefficients of the spin connection:

\displaystyle\omega_{\mu;\alpha\beta}=-\omega_{\mu;\beta\alpha}:=g_{\kappa\lambda}\,e^{\kappa}_{\alpha}D_{\mu}e^{\lambda}_{\beta}=g_{\kappa\lambda}\,e^{\kappa}_{\alpha}\big(\partial_{\mu}e^{\lambda}_{\beta}+\Gamma^{\lambda}_{\mu\nu}e^{\nu}_{\beta}\big).

(C.14)

Then, the covariant derivatives of the spinors are

\displaystyle D_{\mu}\psi=\partial_{\mu}\psi+\hbox{\large{$\frac{1}{8}$}}\omega_{\mu;\alpha\beta}\,[\gamma^{\alpha},\gamma^{\beta}]\psi,\qquad D_{\mu}\overline{\psi}=\partial_{\mu}\overline{\psi}-\hbox{\large{$\frac{1}{8}$}}\omega_{\mu;\alpha\beta}\,\overline{\psi}[\gamma^{\alpha},\gamma^{\beta}].

(C.15)

Now, the tetrad as given by
[
33
]
is the “square root” of

g^{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle e^{\mu}_{\alpha}=\delta^{\mu}_{\alpha}+\hbox{\large{$\frac{\kappa}{2}$}}h^{\mu}_{\alpha}+\hbox{\large{$\frac{\kappa^{2}}{8}$}}\big((\!(hh)\!)\delta^{\mu}_{\alpha}-(hh)^{\mu}_{\alpha}\big)+O(\kappa^{3}),

(C.16)

from which one obtains

\displaystyle\omega_{\mu;\alpha\beta}=\hbox{\large{$\frac{\kappa}{2}$}}\cdot\partial_{[\alpha}h_{\beta]\mu}+O(\kappa^{2}).

(C.17)

The first-order term of
L_{g,\psi}
has contributions from the kinetic terms involving
the derivatives of the Dirac field, and from

\omega_{\mu;\alpha\beta}
. The latter contribution vanishes
by (
C.17

\gamma

h_{\beta\mu}

.
L_{2,\psi}

\sqrt{-g}

.

to second order,
requires a lengthy computation, with the result

\displaystyle\hbox{\large{$\frac{i}{8}$}}\big[e^{\mu}_{\nu}\omega_{\mu;\alpha\beta}\big]^{(2)}\cdot\overline{\psi}(\gamma^{\nu}\gamma^{\alpha}\gamma^{\beta}+\gamma^{\alpha}\gamma^{\beta}\gamma^{\nu})\psi\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textup{div}}}{{=}}\hbox{\large{$\frac{i}{32}$}}\cdot(h\partial_{\nu}h)_{\beta\alpha}\cdot\overline{\psi}(\gamma^{\alpha}\gamma^{\beta}\gamma^{\nu}-\gamma^{\nu}\gamma^{\beta}\gamma^{\alpha})\psi.

Thus, to second order and modulo derivatives,

\displaystyle L_{g,\psi}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textup{div}}}{{=}}\,

\displaystyle L_{0,\psi}+\hbox{\large{$\frac{\kappa}{2}$}}(\!(h\Theta_{\psi})\!)+\hbox{\large{$\frac{\kappa^{2}}{2}$}}\big[-\hbox{\large{$\frac{1}{2}$}}(\!(hh)\!)L_{0,\psi}

(C.18)

\displaystyle+\hbox{\large{$\frac{1}{4}$}}(\!(hh)\!)(\!(\Theta_{\psi})\!)-\hbox{\large{$\frac{1}{4}$}}(\!(hh\Theta_{\psi})\!)+\hbox{\large{$\frac{i}{16}$}}(h\partial_{\nu}h)_{\beta\alpha}\overline{\psi}(\gamma^{\alpha}\gamma^{\beta}\gamma^{\nu}-\gamma^{\nu}\gamma^{\beta}\gamma^{\alpha})\psi\big)\big].

Notice that
