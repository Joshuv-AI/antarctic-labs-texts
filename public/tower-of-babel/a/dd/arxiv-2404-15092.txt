Geometric measures of uniaxial solids of revolution in ℝ 4 \mathbb{R}^{4} and their relation to the second virial coefficient
Markus Kulossa, *

Abstract
We provide analytical expressions for the second virial coefficients of hard, convex, monoaxial solids of revolution in ℝ 4 \mathbb{R}^{4} . The excluded volume per particle and thus the second virial coefficient is calculated using quermassintegrals and rotationally invariant mixed volumes based on the Brunn-Minkowski theorem. We derive analytical expressions for the mutual excluded volume of four-dimensional hard solids of revolution in dependence on their aspect ratio ν \nu including the limits of infinitely thin oblate and infinitely long prolate geometries. Using reduced second virial coefficients B 2 ∗ = B 2 / V P B_{2}^{*}=B_{2}/V_{\mathrm{P}} as size-independent quantities with V P V_{\mathrm{P}} denoting the D D -dimensional particle volume, the influence of the particle geometry to the mutual excluded volume is analyzed for various shapes. Beyond the aspect ratio ν \nu , the detailed particle shape influences the reduced second virial coefficients B 2 ∗ B_{2}^{*} . We prove that for D D -dimensional spherocylinders in arbitrary-dimensional Euclidean spaces ℝ D \mathbb{R}^{D} their excluded volume solely depends on at most three intrinsic volumes, whereas for different convex geometries D D intrinsic volumes are required. For D D -dimensional ellipsoids of revolution, the general parity B 2 ∗ ​ ( ν ) = B 2 ∗ ​ ( ν − 1 ) B_{2}^{*}(\nu)=B_{2}^{*}(\nu^{-1}) is proven.

Introduction

Place two identical coins on a table and roll one coin completely around the second coin’s rim: The moving coin completes two revolutions during the turn around the
first coin which is known as the coin rotation paradox.
To analyze this phenomenon, in addition to a cardioid curve generated by a point on the perimeter of the second coin, the excluded volume between two rigid bodies can be used:
The excluded volume is the volume inaccessible for the second body in the vicinity of the first one. The perimeter of the excluded volume is the curve generated
by the center of the second body during its turn around the first one preserving contact distance.
As visualized in Fig.
1

r_{0}

per circle
2\pi r_{0}^{2}
and thus twice the two-dimensional volume of one circle. Hence, the number of revolutions of the turning coin is the ratio of the excluded volume’s perimeter
to the coin’s perimeter.

While this problem can be generalized to coins with unequal radii, also rolling a disk around other, possibly anisotropic shapes is feasible.
When both objects are anisotropic, the excluded area additionally depends on the relative orientation of these bodies as shown in Fig.

Here, an orientation-averaged, two-dimensional excluded volume results which can be generalized to arbitrary, convex hard bodies in Euclidean spaces
\mathbb{R}^{D}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{n}\left(\mathbf{c},\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}\right)

\displaystyle=-\mathbf{n}\left(\mathbf{c},\mathbf{b},\mathbf{a}\right).

(46)

A uniaxial solid’s of revolution second fundamental form using the normal field

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{n}}=\dfrac{\mathbf{n}}{\|\mathbf{n}\|}=\dfrac{1}{\left[1+\dot{r}^{2}(z)\right]^{1/2}}\begin{pmatrix}-\sin\chi\cos\varphi\\
-\sin\chi\sin\varphi\\
-\cos\chi\\
\dot{r}(z)\\
\end{pmatrix}

(47)

reads as

\displaystyle\mathbf{\underline{II}}_{\mathrm{P}}=\dfrac{1}{\left[1+\dot{r}^{2}(z)\right]^{1/2}}\begin{bmatrix}r(z)\sin^{2}\chi&0&0\\
0&r(z)&0\\
0&0&-\ddot{r}(z)\\
\end{bmatrix}\,.

(48)

From both fundamental forms, the Weingarten map

\displaystyle\underline{\mathcal{W}}=\begin{pmatrix}\dfrac{1}{r(z)\left[1+\dot{r}^{2}(z)\right]^{1/2}}&0&0\\
0&\dfrac{1}{r(z)\left[1+\dot{r}^{2}(z)\right]^{1/2}}&0\\
0&0&-\dfrac{\ddot{r}(z)}{\left[1+\dot{r}^{2}(z)\right]^{3/2}}\\
\end{pmatrix}

(49)

is obtained as a main diagonal matrix. Hence, the eigenvalues are directly accessible from the diagonal elements indicating the principal curvatures.
The mean curvature thus can be written with the trace of the Weingarten map as

\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{P}}=\dfrac{1}{D-1}\mathrm{Tr}\left(\underline{\mathcal{W}}\right)=\dfrac{1}{D-1}\mathrm{Tr}\left(\mathbf{\underline{I}}_{\mathrm{P}}^{-1}\mathbf{\underline{II}}_{\mathrm{P}}\right)\,.

(50)

Hence, for a uniaxial solid of revolution in
\mathbb{R}^{4}
three principal radii of curvature

\displaystyle R_{1}(z)

\displaystyle=R_{2}(z)=r(z)\left[1+\dot{r}^{2}(z)\right]^{1/2}

(51)

\displaystyle R_{3}(z)

\displaystyle=-\dfrac{1}{\ddot{r}(z)}\left[1+\dot{r}^{2}(z)\right]^{3/2}

(52)

result as inverses of the principal curvatures where two of them are identical.

Combining Eqs. (
51
), (
52
), and (
29
), the mean curvature can be written as

\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{P}}=\dfrac{1}{3}\left[\dfrac{1}{R_{1}(z)}+\dfrac{1}{R_{2}(z)}+\dfrac{1}{R_{3}(z)}\right]

(53)

in dependence on the meridian curve
r(z)
. Using the surface element
\mathrm{d}^{3}S=r^{2}(z)\sin\chi\,\mathrm{d}\varphi\,\mathrm{d}\chi\,\mathrm{d}s
, the second quermassintegral in
\mathbb{R}^{4}
with Eqs. (
28
) and (
34
), performing the integration over the angular coordinates
\varphi
and
\chi
, finally reads as

\displaystyle W_{2}(K)=\dfrac{\pi}{3}\int\limits_{z_{\rm min}}^{z_{\rm max}}r^{2}(z)\left[\dfrac{1}{R_{1}(z)}+\dfrac{1}{R_{2}(z)}+\dfrac{1}{R_{3}(z)}\right]\left[1+\dot{r}^{2}(z)\right]^{1/2}\,\mathrm{d}z

(54)

for convex solids with continuous surface curvatures.

The mean radius of curvature of a convex solid with continuous surface curvature can, in general, be calculated from its principal radii of curvature as

\displaystyle\tilde{R}_{\mathrm{P}}=\dfrac{1}{\beta_{D}}\oint\dfrac{1}{D-1}\left[\sum\limits_{i=1}^{D-1}R_{i}(\phi_{0},\phi_{1},\dots\phi_{D-2})\right]\prod\limits_{j=0}^{D-2}\sin^{j}\left(\phi_{j}\right)\,\mathrm{d}\phi_{j}

(55)

normalized to the surface area of a
D
-dimensional unit sphere.
In
\mathbb{R}^{4}
,

\displaystyle\tilde{R}_{\mathrm{P}}(K)=\dfrac{1}{6\pi^{2}}\int\limits_{0}^{\pi}\int\limits_{0}^{\pi}\int\limits_{0}^{2\pi}\left[R_{1}(\vartheta,\chi,\varphi)+R_{2}(\vartheta,\chi,\varphi)+R_{3}(\vartheta,\chi,\varphi)\right]\sin^{2}\vartheta\sin\chi\,\mathrm{d}\varphi\,\mathrm{d}\chi\,\mathrm{d}\vartheta

(56)

is obtained. Employing the surface element

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}^{3}S=R_{1}R_{2}R_{3}\sin^{2}\vartheta\sin\chi\,\mathrm{d}\varphi\,\mathrm{d}\chi\,\mathrm{d}\vartheta=r^{2}(z)\sin\chi\left[1+\dot{r}^{2}(z)\right]^{1/2}\,\mathrm{d}\varphi\,\mathrm{d}\chi\,\mathrm{d}z\,,

(57)

the angular integral can be written as a surface integral

\displaystyle\tilde{R}_{\mathrm{P}}(K)=\dfrac{2}{3\pi}\int\limits_{z_{\rm min}}^{z_{\rm max}}r^{2}(z)\left[\dfrac{1}{R_{2}(z)R_{3}(z)}+\dfrac{1}{R_{1}(z)R_{3}(z)}+\dfrac{1}{R_{1}(z)R_{2}(z)}\right]\left[1+\dot{r}^{2}(z)\right]^{1/2}\,\mathrm{d}z

(58)

where again the integration over the angular coordinates
\varphi
and
\chi
results in a factor
4\pi
.

Using the relations to the other quermassintegrals in Eq. (
24
), analytical expressions for them can be summarized as

\displaystyle W_{0}(K)

\displaystyle=V_{\mathrm{P}}(K)

(59a)

\displaystyle W_{1}(K)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{4}\oint\,\mathrm{d}^{3}S=\dfrac{1}{4}S_{\mathrm{P}}(K)

(59b)

\displaystyle W_{2}(K)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{4}\oint\dfrac{1}{3}\left[\dfrac{1}{R_{1}}+\dfrac{1}{R_{2}}+\dfrac{1}{R_{3}}\right]\mathrm{d}^{3}S

(59c)

\displaystyle W_{3}(K)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{4}\oint\dfrac{1}{3}\left[\dfrac{1}{R_{1}R_{2}}+\dfrac{1}{R_{1}R_{3}}+\dfrac{1}{R_{2}R_{3}}\right]\mathrm{d}^{3}S=\dfrac{1}{4}\tilde{R}_{\mathrm{P}}\beta_{4}=\tilde{R}_{\mathrm{P}}\kappa_{4}

(59d)

\displaystyle W_{4}(K)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{4}\oint\dfrac{1}{R_{1}R_{2}R_{3}}\,\mathrm{d}^{3}S=\dfrac{1}{4}\beta_{4}=\kappa_{4}

(59e)

for convex solids with continuous surface curvature in
\mathbb{R}^{4}
.

For arbitrary Euclidean spaces
\mathbb{R}^{D}
, this can, in general, be written as

\displaystyle W_{i}(K)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lcl}V_{\mathrm{P}}(K)&:&i=0\\
\dfrac{1}{D}\mathop{\text{\Large$\oint$}}\nolimits\,\mathrm{d}^{D-1}S&:&i=1\\
\dfrac{1}{D}\mathop{\text{\Large$\oint$}}\nolimits\left[\begin{pmatrix}D-1\\
i-1\end{pmatrix}\right]^{-1}\left[\sum\limits_{j=1}^{D-i+1}a_{D-i+1,j}\right]\,\mathrm{d}^{D-1}S&:&2\leq i\leq D\end{array}\right.

with

\displaystyle a_{ij}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lcl}\dfrac{1}{R_{j}}&:&i=D-1\\
\dfrac{1}{R_{j}}\sum\limits_{k=j+1}^{D-1}\dfrac{1}{R_{k}}&:&i=D-2\\
\dfrac{1}{R_{j}}\sum\limits_{k=j+1}^{i+1}a_{i+1,k}&:&i<D-2\end{array}\right.

and

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}^{D}S=\left[\,\prod\limits_{i=1}^{D}R_{i}\right]\left[\prod\limits_{j=0}^{D-1}\sin^{j}\left(\phi_{j}\right)\,\mathrm{d}\phi_{j}\right]

(66)

for the surface element.

For convex solids with discontinuities of their surface curvature, additional contributions to quermassintegrals
W_{i}(K)

exist for uniaxial solids of revolution in

4.1.1
Hypersphere

The simplest solid of revolution in the four-dimensional Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{4}

Placing the center of a hypersphere in the coordinate system’s origin, the meridian curve

of the hypersphere reads as

\displaystyle r(z)=\left(r_{0}^{2}-z^{2}\right)^{1/2}

being the radius of the hypersphere and

Using Eq. (
32

\displaystyle V_{\mathrm{P}}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{4}{3}\pi\int\limits_{-r_{0}}^{r_{0}}\left(r_{0}^{2}-z^{2}\right)^{3/2}\,\mathrm{d}z=\dfrac{1}{2}\pi^{2}r_{0}^{4}\,

(68)

which is identical to
V_{\mathrm{P}}=\kappa_{4}r_{0}^{4}
using Eq. (
16

The total area

of a surface without singularities is simply the lateral surface area
S_{\mathrm{P}}^{\prime}
.
The lateral surface area
S_{\mathrm{P}}^{\prime}
of a hypersphere can be calculated with Eqs. (
35

67
)

\displaystyle S_{\mathrm{P}}

\displaystyle=S_{\mathrm{P}}^{\prime}=4\pi r_{0}\int\limits_{-r_{0}}^{r_{0}}\left(r_{0}^{2}-z^{2}\right)^{1/2}\,\mathrm{d}z=2\pi^{2}r_{0}^{3}

(69)

as the expected results, identical to
S_{\mathrm{P}}=\beta_{4}r_{0}^{3}
using Eq. (
13
) with
D=4
.

For the second quermassintegral
W_{2}
and the mean radius of curvature
\tilde{R}_{\mathrm{P}}
of a four-dimensional, convex solids of revolution the principal radii of curvature
R_{1}
,
R_{2}
, and

are required [Eqs. (
54

These can be calculated in dependence on the meridian curve as

\displaystyle R_{1}=R_{2}=R_{3}=r_{0}

(70)

for a hypersphere employing Eqs. (

) and (

).
Its principal radii of curvature are simply the radius of the hypersphere
r_{0}
.
For the second quermassintegral
W_{2}
, Eq. (
54
) leads to

\displaystyle W_{2}

\displaystyle=\pi\int\limits_{-r_{0}}^{r_{0}}\left(r_{0}^{2}-z^{2}\right)^{1/2}\,\mathrm{d}z=\dfrac{1}{2}\pi^{2}r_{0}^{2}

(71)

and the mean radius of curvature from Eq. (
58

\displaystyle\tilde{R}_{\mathrm{P}}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{2}{\pi r_{0}}\int\limits_{-r_{0}}^{r_{0}}\left(r_{0}^{2}-z^{2}\right)^{1/2}\,\mathrm{d}z=r_{0}

is again simply the radius of the hypersphere

Using the relations in Eq. (
24
), the quermassintegrals
W_{i}
of a four-dimensional hypersphere can be written as

\displaystyle W_{i}=\dfrac{1}{2}\pi^{2}r_{0}^{4-i},

agreeing with the general solution for

(74)

from the literature

37
]

4.1.2
Hyperellipsoid

A uniaxial hyperellipsoid of revolution is the affine transformation of a hypersphere with radius
r_{\mathrm{eq}}
in
z
-direction by a factor
\nu
.
The meridian curve
r(z)
of this hyperellipsoid of revolution with the center in the origin of the coordinate system reads as

\displaystyle r(z)=\left[r_{\mathrm{eq}}^{2}-\left(\dfrac{z}{\nu}\right)^{2}\right]^{1/2}\,.

(75)

For the aspect ratio
\nu=1
, a hypersphere results. Hyperellipsoids of revolution with
\nu>1
are prolate and with
\nu<1
are oblate solids of revolution.
The two-dimensional sections of such geometries are shown in Fig.
3
.

Figure 3:
Two-dimensional sections of prolate and oblate uniaxial hyperellipsoids of revolution with radius
r_{\mathrm{eq}}
, aspect ratio
\nu=2

\nu=1/2

For the volume of a four-dimensional hyperellipsoid of revolution, Eq. (

\displaystyle V_{\mathrm{P}}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{4}{3}\pi\int\limits_{-\nu r_{\mathrm{eq}}}^{\nu r_{\mathrm{eq}}}\left(r_{\mathrm{eq}}^{2}-\dfrac{z^{2}}{\nu^{2}}\right)^{3/2}\,\mathrm{d}z=\dfrac{1}{2}\pi^{2}\nu r_{\mathrm{eq}}^{4}

(76)

for prolate and oblate hyperellipsoids.
The total surface area
S_{\mathrm{P}}
for this geometry is the lateral surface area
S_{\mathrm{P}}^{\prime}
which can with Eq. (
35
) be written as

\displaystyle S_{\mathrm{P}}

\displaystyle=S_{\mathrm{P}}^{\prime}=4\pi\int\limits_{-\nu r_{\mathrm{eq}}}^{\nu r_{\mathrm{eq}}}\left(r_{\mathrm{eq}}^{2}-\dfrac{z^{2}}{\nu^{2}}\right)\left[\dfrac{\nu^{4}r_{\mathrm{eq}}^{2}+z^{2}\left(1-\nu^{2}\right)}{\nu^{2}\left(\nu^{2}r_{\mathrm{eq}}^{2}-z^{2}\right)}\right]^{1/2}\,\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle=2\pi^{2}\nu^{2}r_{\mathrm{eq}}^{3}\,{}_{2}\mathcal{F}_{1}\left(\dfrac{5}{2},\dfrac{1}{2},2\mathchar 24635\relax\;1-\nu^{2}\right)

(77)

where
{}_{2}\mathcal{F}_{1}
is the Gauss hypergeometric function defined as

{}_{2}\mathcal{F}_{1}\left(a,b,c\mathchar 24635\relax\;z\right)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\Gamma(c)}{\Gamma(b)\Gamma(c-b)}\int\limits_{0}^{1}t^{b-1}\left(1-t\right)^{c-b-1}\left(1-zt\right)^{-a}\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}\dfrac{\left(a\right)_{n}\left(b\right)_{n}}{\left(c\right)_{n}}\dfrac{z^{n}}{n!}

(78)

and

\displaystyle\left(q\right)_{n}=\prod_{k=0}^{n-1}\left(q+k\right)=\dfrac{\Gamma(q+n)}{\Gamma(q)}

(79)

the Pochhammer symbol.

Using linear transformation and identities of the hypergeometric function
[
48
]
, the total surface area can also be denoted as

\displaystyle S_{\mathrm{P}}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccl}\dfrac{8\pi\nu r_{\mathrm{eq}}^{3}}{3(\nu^{2}-1)}\left[\left(2\nu^{2}-1\right)\mathcal{E}\left(\dfrac{\sqrt{\nu^{2}-1}}{\nu}\right)-\mathcal{K}\left(\dfrac{\sqrt{\nu^{2}-1}}{\nu}\right)\right]&:&\nu>1\\
\dfrac{8\pi\nu r_{\mathrm{eq}}^{3}}{3(1-\nu^{2})}\left[\left(\dfrac{1-2\nu^{2}}{\nu}\right)\mathcal{E}\left(\sqrt{1-\nu^{2}}\right)+\nu\mathcal{K}\left(\sqrt{1-\nu^{2}}\right)\right]&:&\nu<1\end{array}\right.

with the complete elliptic integral of the first kind

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}(k)=\dfrac{\pi}{2}\,_{2}\mathcal{F}_{1}\left(\dfrac{1}{2},\dfrac{1}{2},1\mathchar 24635\relax\;k^{2}\right)

(82)

and the complete elliptic integral of the second kind

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(k)=\dfrac{\pi}{2}\,_{2}\mathcal{F}_{1}\left(-\dfrac{1}{2},\dfrac{1}{2},1\mathchar 24635\relax\;k^{2}\right)\,.

(83)

For the limiting case of a hypersphere with
\nu\to 1

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{\nu\to 1^{+}}S_{\mathrm{P}}(\nu)=\lim\limits_{\nu\to 1^{-}}S_{\mathrm{P}}(\nu)=2\pi^{2}r_{\mathrm{eq}}^{3}

results which agrees with the expression in Eq. (
69

For infinitely thin hyperellipsoids of revolution

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{\nu\to 0}S_{\mathrm{P}}(\nu)=\dfrac{8}{3}\pi r_{\mathrm{eq}}^{3}

(85)

results.

For the second quermassintegral
W_{2}
and the mean radius of curvature

,

Using Eqs. (

\displaystyle R_{1}(z)=R_{2}(z)=\dfrac{1}{\nu^{2}}\left[\nu^{4}r_{\mathrm{eq}}^{2}+z^{2}\left(1-\nu^{2}\right)\right]^{1/2}

(86)

and

\displaystyle R_{3}(z)=\dfrac{1}{\nu^{4}r_{\mathrm{eq}}^{2}}\left[\nu^{4}r_{\mathrm{eq}}^{2}+z^{2}\left(1-\nu^{2}\right)\right]^{3/2}

(87)

are obtained.
Then the second quermassintegral is, using Eq. (
54

\displaystyle W_{2}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\pi}{3\nu^{3}}\int\limits_{-\nu r_{\mathrm{eq}}}^{\nu r_{\mathrm{eq}}}\left(\nu^{2}r_{\mathrm{eq}}^{2}-z^{2}\right)^{1/2}\left[\dfrac{\nu^{4}r_{\mathrm{eq}}^{2}}{\nu^{4}r_{\mathrm{eq}}^{2}+z^{2}\left(1-\nu^{2}\right)}+2\nu^{2}\right]\,\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{3}\pi^{2}r_{\mathrm{eq}}^{2}\left(\dfrac{1-\nu^{3}}{1-\nu^{2}}\right)\,,

(88)

both for prolate and oblate hyperellipsoids of revolution. In the limit of a four-dimensional hypersphere

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{\nu\to 1^{+}}W_{2}\left(\nu\right)=\lim\limits_{\nu\to 1^{-}}W_{2}\left(\nu\right)=\dfrac{1}{2}\pi^{2}r_{\mathrm{eq}}^{2}

(89)

is obtained while

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{\nu\to 0}W_{2}\left(\nu\right)=\dfrac{1}{3}\pi^{2}r_{\mathrm{eq}}^{2}

(90)

results for infinitely thin hyperellipsoids of revolution. Eq. (
89

71

Finally, the mean radius of curvature
\tilde{R}_{\mathrm{P}}
for hyperellipsoids of revolution can be written as

\displaystyle\tilde{R}_{\mathrm{P}}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{2}{3\pi\nu^{3}}\int\limits_{-\nu r_{\mathrm{eq}}}^{\nu r_{\mathrm{eq}}}\left(\nu^{2}r_{\mathrm{eq}}^{2}-z^{2}\right)^{1/2}\left\{\dfrac{2\nu^{6}r_{\mathrm{eq}}^{2}+\nu^{4}\left[\nu^{4}r_{\mathrm{eq}}^{2}+z^{2}\left(1-\nu^{2}\right)\right]}{\left[\nu^{4}r_{\mathrm{eq}}^{2}+z^{2}\left(1-\nu^{2}\right)\right]^{3/2}}\right\}\,\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle=\nu^{4}r_{\mathrm{eq}}\,{}_{2}\mathcal{F}_{1}\left(\dfrac{5}{2},\dfrac{3}{2},2\mathchar 24635\relax\;1-\nu^{2}\right)

(91)

using Eq. (
58

Using complete elliptic integrals [Eqs. (
82
) and (
83
)], it can alternatively be denoted as

\displaystyle\tilde{R}_{\mathrm{P}}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lcc}\dfrac{4}{3\pi}\dfrac{\nu r_{\mathrm{eq}}}{\nu^{2}-1}\left[\left(\nu^{2}-2\right)\mathcal{E}\left(\dfrac{\sqrt{\nu^{2}-1}}{\nu}\right)+\mathcal{K}\left(\dfrac{\sqrt{\nu^{2}-1}}{\nu}\right)\right]&:&\nu>1\\
\dfrac{4}{3\pi}\dfrac{r_{\mathrm{eq}}}{1-\nu^{2}}\left[\left(2-\nu^{2}\right)\mathcal{E}\left(\sqrt{1-\nu^{2}}\right)-\nu^{2}\mathcal{K}\left(\sqrt{1-\nu^{2}}\right)\right]&:&\nu<1\\
\end{array}\right.

for prolate and oblate hyperellipsoids of revolution. In the limit of a hypersphere,

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{\nu\to 1^{+}}\tilde{R}_{\mathrm{P}}\left(\nu\right)=\lim\limits_{\nu\to 1^{-}}\tilde{R}_{\mathrm{P}}\left(\nu\right)=r_{\mathrm{eq}}

(94)

results [Eq. (

Using Eqs. (
24

\displaystyle W_{i}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{2}\pi^{2}\nu^{i+1}r_{\mathrm{eq}}^{4-i}\,{}_{2}\mathcal{F}_{1}\left(\dfrac{5}{2},\dfrac{i}{2},2\mathchar 24635\relax\;1-\nu^{2}\right)\,,

(96)

with

(97)

{}_{2}\mathcal{F}_{1}\left(\dfrac{5}{2},1,2\mathchar 24635\relax\;1-\nu^{2}\right)=\dfrac{2}{3\nu^{3}}\,\dfrac{1-\nu^{3}}{1-\nu^{2}}

4.1.3
Hyperspherocylinder

4.2.1
Spherical hyperplates

The spherical hyperplate as an infinitely thin hyperellipsoid is the limit of a hyperellipsoid with aspect ratio
\nu\to 0

r_{0}

It is a
(D-1)
-dimensional sphere with radius
r_{0}

While in

This can also be seen from Eq. (

Despite for this geometry the

Therefore, the total surface area

of a

-dimensional spherical hyperplate is two times the volume of a
(D-1)
-dimensional sphere
and reads as

(117)

in Euclidean spaces
\mathbb{R}^{D}
. The second quermassintegral

for a spherical hyperplate in
\mathbb{R}^{4}

\displaystyle W_{2}=\dfrac{1}{3}\pi^{2}r_{0}^{2}

(118)

[Eq. (
90

\displaystyle\tilde{R}_{\mathrm{P}}=\dfrac{8}{3\pi}r_{0}

(119)

results from Eq. (
95

Using Eqs. (
24
), the obtained results agree with the general formulation of quermassintegrals
W_{i}
for
(D-1)
-dimensional spheres in
\mathbb{R}^{D}

\displaystyle W_{i}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lcc}0&:&i=0\\
\dfrac{\kappa_{D-1}}{\kappa_{i-1}}\dfrac{i}{D}\kappa_{i}r_{0}^{D-i}&:&i\geq 1\\
\end{array}\right.

as provided in the literature
[
37
]

4.2.5
Hyperspherical cap

A hyperspherical cap is a hypersphere cut off by a single hyperplane resulting in half of a spherical hyperplate as a singularity on the surface of this geometry.
The hyperspherical cap can be defined by the radius of the hypersphere
r_{0}
and the critical angle
\theta_{\mathrm{c}}
(Fig.

With these parameters, the height

\displaystyle h=r_{0}\left[1-\cos\left(\dfrac{\theta_{\mathrm{c}}}{2}\right)\right]

(185)

\displaystyle\nu=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}\dfrac{h}{2r_{\mathrm{cap}}}&:&0\leq\theta_{\mathrm{c}}\leq\pi\\

4.2.6
Hyperdoublecone

A hyperdoublecone is a uniaxial solid of revolution with inversion symmetry whose meridian curve can be written as

\displaystyle r(z)=r_{\mathrm{eq}}-\dfrac{|z|}{\nu}

(215)

r_{\mathrm{eq}}

\nu

(\nu\leq 1)
and prolate
(\nu\geq 1)

for
\nu\to 0

\mathbb{R}^{4}

[
34

The two-dimensional section of a hyperdoublecone with aspect ratio

\theta_{\mathrm{c}}

\displaystyle\cos\left(\theta_{\mathrm{c}}\right)=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1+\nu^{2}}}\,,

(216)

similar to hyperlenses.
Additionally, two apical singularities exist at

\displaystyle R_{1}(z)=R_{2}(z)=\left(r_{\mathrm{eq}}-\dfrac{|z|}{\nu}\right)\dfrac{\sqrt{1+\nu^{2}}}{\nu}

5
Results

Using the geometric measures volume
V_{\mathrm{P}}
, total surface area
S_{\mathrm{P}}
, second quermassintegral
W_{2}
, and mean radius of curvature
\tilde{R}_{\mathrm{P}}
, the second virial coefficient
B_{2}
, representing the excluded volume per particle for the four-dimensional, convex solids of revolution provided in Sec.
4
, can be obtained using Eq. (
27
).
To investigate the influence of the specific particle shape in dependence on the aspect ratio
\nu
, these virial coefficients can be normalized to the particle volume
V_{\mathrm{P}}
using

\displaystyle B_{2}^{\ast}=\dfrac{B_{2}}{V_{\mathrm{P}}}=1+\dfrac{S_{\mathrm{P}}\tilde{R}_{\mathrm{P}}}{V_{\mathrm{P}}}+\dfrac{6}{\pi^{2}}\dfrac{W_{2}^{2}}{V_{\mathrm{P}}}

(264)

as reduced second virial coefficients
B_{2}^{\ast}
.
The resulting reduced virial coefficients, independent of the particle size, allow an expansion of the real gas factor
Z
in powers of the packing fraction
\eta
[Eq. (
2
)]
and thus a comparison of different geometric shapes.

6
Summary and Outlook

In this work, analytical expressions for the geometric measures volume
V_{\mathrm{P}}
, surface area
S_{\mathrm{P}}
, second quermassintegrals
W_{2}
and mean radius of curvature
\tilde{R}_{\mathrm{P}}
are derived for selected convex solids of revolution in
\mathbb{R}^{4}
.
The results are summarized in Tables
3
,
4
, and
5
.
Using Eqs. (
24
), analytical expressions for so far unknown quermassintegrals
W_{i}
are obtained.
Employing these quantities, with Eq. (
20
) the intrinsic volumes
\upsilon_{i}
of these convex solids
of revolution are analytically accessible, too.

In addition to hyperspheres, hyperellipsoids of revolution, hyperspherocylinders, hypercylinders, and spherical hyperplates with already known quermassintegrals (Table
3
),
analytical expression for so far unknown quermassintegrals of hyperspindles, hyperlenses, hyperdoublecones, hypercones, truncated hypercones, and hyperspherical caps are
provided (Tables
4
and
5
). The latter geometries possess removable singularities in their surface curvature. While apical, zero-dimensional singularities do not contribute to
quermassintegrals, higher-dimensional singularities contribute as exemplarily shown in detail for hyperlenses. This contribution, in general depends on the critical angle enclosed within the
\epsilon
-vicinity
of the respective singularity. For uniaxial solids of revolution with infinitely large aspect ratios in
\mathbb{R}^{4}
, a general proportionality
B_{2}^{\ast}\propto\nu
of reduced second virial coefficients to the aspect ratio arises whereas for infinitely thin oblate geometries, the general proportionality
B_{2}^{\ast}\propto\nu^{-1}
results.

With known principal radii of curvature, the quermassintegrals are accessible for arbitrary convex solids in
\mathbb{R}^{4}
[Eqs. (
59
)]. The generalization to dimensions
D>4
for geometries with continuous surfaces curvature [Eq. (
3
)] is straight forward. Possible contributions of singularities can
be determined analogously as demonstrated in
\mathbb{R}^{3}

[
34
]
and
\mathbb{R}^{4}
(this work).

For selected geometries, general expressions for their quermassintegrals are compiled in Table
6
. Using such general expressions, the parity
B_{2}^{\ast}(\nu)=B_{2}^{\ast}(\nu^{-1})
for uniaxial
D
-dimensional ellipsoids is proven. Additionally,
for
D
-dimensional, uniaxial spherocylinders the dependence of the reduced second virial coefficient
B_{2}^{\ast}
on at most three quermassintegrals
W_{0}
,
W_{1}
, and
W_{D-1}
is proven, too.

A remaining task is the derivation of a general expression for quermassintegrals of
D
-dimensional doublecones in
\mathbb{R}^{D}
needed to prove or disprove the conjecture that in
even dimensions
D
reduced second virial coefficients of cylinders and doublecones are identical at aspect ratio
\nu=1
.
