Dynamical properties of coarse-grained linear SDEs
Thomas Hudson, Xingjie Helen Li

Abstract
Coarse-graining or model reduction is a term describing a range of approaches used to extend the time-scale of molecular simulations by reducing the number of degrees of freedom. In the context of molecular simulation, standard coarse-graining approaches approximate the potential of mean force and use this to drive an effective Markovian model. To gain insight into this process, the simple case of a quadratic energy is studied in an overdamped setting. A hierarchy of reduced models is derived and analysed, and the merits of these different coarse-graining approaches are discussed. In particular, while standard recipes for model reduction accurately capture static equilibrium statistics, it is shown that dynamical statistics such as the mean-squared displacement display systematic error, even when a system exhibits large time-scale separation. In the linear setting studied, it is demonstrated both analytically and numerically that such models can be augmented in a simple way to better capture dynamical statistics.



Keywords:
Reduced-order modelling, Markovian approximate dynamics, Autocovariance error, Progressive coarse-graining

60H10, 34F05

Introduction

The behaviour of a system of interest as it evolves in time is often modelled by a large number of interacting degrees of freedom. While such detailed models provide excellent accuracy in theory, their high resolution often renders the cost of simulating them repeatedly over long time scales prohibitive. This limits the applicability of such models, and as a result, the answers to many scientific questions lie outside the realms of what is computationally feasible. To remedy this, various coarse-graining (or model reduction) strategies have been developed, aiming to create cheap-to-simulate effective models which govern a smaller number of degrees of freedom. Such models significantly expand the possible applications of the computational resources available to theoretical scientists.

Coarse-graining is generally believed to be an effective approach when simulating a dynamical system which exhibits a significant time-scale separation. When present, such separation can be exploited in classical asymptotic approaches such as the method of multiple scales and stochastic averaging
[
1
,
2
]
. These methods tend to assume that it is possible to assess and classify variables
a priori
according to the time-scales on which they evolve. For example, in statistical physics a separation of timescales provides the motivation for the use of stochastic models through the Mori-Zwanzig formalism
[
3
,
4
,
5
]
, and a range of approximate methods have been developed:

1.

When the relaxation of the coarse-grained (CG) and unresolved variables exhibit significant timescale separation which is spatially homogeneous, one can approximate complex time correlated effects by a local-in-time fluctuations and dissipation, rendering the effective dynamics Markovian
[
6
,
7
]
. There are numerous recent works concerning error estimates and the properties of this Markovian approach; see for example
[

,

,

,

,

,

,

]
.

2.

In case where there is no significant timescale separation between CG and unresolved variables, a Markovian approach may at best provide satisfactory approximation of equilibrium
[
15
,
16
,
17
,
18
]
. A remedy in the case of spatially homogeneous effects is to approximate the memory term in a non-Markovian fashion, and fluctuations are treated as colored noised. Examples of the range of approaches available include
[

.

The settings where CG strategies would have the most impact are in cases where the dynamical systems are extremely high-dimensional in nature, and so it is usually unclear which variables are slow and which are fast. As such, all CG methods require intuition or theoretical input in determining what an appropriate reduction for a system should be. In the case of molecular dynamics, this problem is widely recognised, and the development of new methods to better select CG variables or reaction coordinates remains a problem of significant interest.

Motivated by this broader problem of effectively coarse-graining models in statistical physics, we seek to advance the rigorous analysis of the accuracy of CG models. Here, we focus on simple case where there is sufficient structure to develop an instructive mathematical theory which can assess the strengths and weaknesses of different CG approaches. Starting from the linear overdamped Langevin equation with a linear coarse-graining map, we derive a hierarchy of approximate coarse-grained models. We then assess the accuracy of these approaches on the basis of their ability to capture observables of interest to molecular dynamics practitioners. The first of these observables is the Gibbs distribution of the reduced model, which captures the static equilibrium properties of the coarse-grained model. The second observable is the equilibrium autocovariance of the coarse-grained evolutions, representing the dynamical statistics of the model.
While the model we study is evidently much simpler than many molecular dynamics models, a particular case where our analysis and the methodology of constructing higher-order approximations in time could already find direct applications includes the normal mode analysis of proteins
[
27
,
28
]
, where coarse-graining can be performed at the level of residues.

The derivation of the hierarchy of approaches we study is based on a reformulation of the dynamics of the CG variables as an integro-differential equation, similar to the derivation of Generalised Langevin Equations via the Mori-Zwanzig formalism
[
3
,
29
]
. The integrals present in the resulting equations are then approximated via the truncation of a series expansion based upon formal asymptotic methods. In this expansion, an approach consistent with other CG strategies naturally appears at leading order, and a Markovian correction similar to that observed in
[
30
]
appears at first-order.
In particular, the first-order approach can be seen as a form of predictor-corrector strategy in the time evolution, and as we show in our rigorous results, enables us to improve the time approximation error.

After deriving our CG approaches, we proceed to study them both analytically and numerically, focusing in particular on their ability to accurately capture the dynamical statistics for coarse-grained models. This particular focus lies in contrast to estimates obtained in previous rigorous mathematical work
[
8
,
10
,
11
,
13
]
. We note that while pathwise error estimates were presented in
[
11
,
13
]
, in
[
30
]
we observed that these estimates are highly accurate for short time predictions, but may not necessarily be accurate over longer timescales. To the best of our knowledge, our results therefore provide the first rigorous analysis of the dynamical statistics for CG approaches. Our analysis proceeds via a direct approach, and we derive comprehensive short and long-time autocovariance error estimates for a two-dimensional case, quantifying the error in terms of the deviation from an optimal choice of CG variables and the extent of timescale separation present in the system. Intriguingly, we find that our first-order approach better captures autocovariance properties of the CG system overall, particular in the case of large time-scale separation, but that the leading-order approach better captures autocovariances over very short time-lags than our first-order approach.

To conclude our study, we explore a series of numerical examples both to validate our analytical results and to establish evidence regarding the validity of our conclusions for higher-dimensional systems. In higher-dimensional examples, we find consistently that our new first-order approach provides greater accuracy for the dynamical properties of the system, while maintaining accuracy on static equilibrium statistics, and we discuss further directions for exploration.

2
Setting

Throughout this paper, we use bold capital letters for matrices, e.g.
\mathbf{A}
, bold lower-case letters for vectors, e.g.
\mathbf{q}
, and italics for scalars, e.g.
t
. For matrices, we write the transpose as
\mathbf{A}^{*}
. All vectors and numbers used are real.
To quantify the size of matrices, we use the Frobenius norm, which is defined to be

\|\mathbf{A}\|_{F}:=\sqrt{\tr(\mathbf{A}^{*}\mathbf{A})}.

2.1
Linear overdamped Langevin equation

We consider the first-order stochastically-forced linear system of SDEs

(2.1)

\mathrm{d}\mathbf{q}_{t}=-\mathbf{A}\mathbf{q}_{t}\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2\beta^{-1}}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{w}_{t}.

Since we consider a diffusion independent of position, we note that this system of SDEs can be viewed in either the Itô or Stratonovich sense, and our results hold independently of the precise choice of interpretation.
We assume that
\mathbf{A}
is symmetric and strictly positive definite, which entails that the system is ergodic with respect to the invariant measure corresponding to the Boltzmann-Gibbs equilibrium distribution. In this case, the equilibrium distribution takes the form

\mu(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{q})=\frac{1}{Z}\exp\big(-\tfrac{1}{2}\beta\mathbf{q}\cdot(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{q})\big),

i.e. a mean-zero Gaussian distribution
\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\beta^{-1}\mathbf{A}^{-1})
. We note that while it is possible to consider more general linear systems than (
2.1
) for our analysis, we focus on this choice since we are motivated by applications in statistical physics
[
31
]
.

The general solution to (
2.1
) can be expressed using the matrix exponential and stochastic integral as

(2.2)

\mathbf{q}_{t}=\mathrm{e}^{-\mathbf{A}t}\mathbf{q}_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{e}^{\mathbf{A}(s-t)}\sqrt{2\beta^{-1}}\,\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{w}_{s}.

If we suppose that the system is at thermal equilibrium at initial time, so that the initial conditions are distributed according to the equilibrium distribution,
\mathbf{q}_{0}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\beta^{-1}\mathbf{A}^{-1})
, then it follows that
\mathbf{q}_{t}
is stationary, i.e. it has an identical distribution for any
t
. Moreover, using the treatment of linear SDEs considered in §3.7 of
[
32
]
, we can establish that the autocovariance of the solution (irrespective of the initial distribution) is

(2.3)

\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{q}_{t}\otimes\mathbf{q}_{s}]=\beta^{-1}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathrm{e}^{-|t-s|\mathbf{A}}+\mathrm{e}^{-t\mathbf{A}}\Big(\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{q}_{0}\otimes\mathbf{q}_{0}]-\beta^{-1}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\Big)\mathrm{e}^{-s\mathbf{A}}.

We note that the latter term vanishes if
\mathbf{q}_{0}
is distributed according to the equilibrium distribution.

2.2
Coarse-grained variables

We suppose that (
2.1
) represents an accurate physical system of interest which we would like to simplify for the purposes of generating predictions. In order to do so, we seek to reduce the number of degrees of freedom in the model. In particular, we suppose that there is a linear map
\widetilde{\Phi}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{R}^{N};\mathbb{R}^{n})
with
n<N
which selects a collection of variables of interest. Selecting a linear combination of variables (even for a nonlinear model) is a very standard coarse-graining approach, since it tends to enhance the interpretability of the coarse-grained model.

In a slight abuse of notation, we will identify the linear operator
\widetilde{\Phi}
with a matrix
\widetilde{\Phi}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times N}
defined with respect to fixed orthonormal coordinate bases for
\mathbb{R}^{N}
and
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. We will assume that
\widetilde{\Phi}
has full rank, which ensures there are no ‘redundant’ coarse-grained variables. In cases where
\widetilde{\Phi}
is not of full rank, it is always possible to reduce to the case we consider by redefining
\widetilde{\Phi}
with a smaller
n
by selecting a maximal collection of linearly independent rows.

Using the matrix
\widetilde{\Phi}
, we denote the coarse-grained variables
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}}:=\widetilde{\Phi}\mathbf{q}
. We introduce the
matrix

(2.4)

\Phi=\big(\widetilde{\Phi}\widetilde{\Phi}^{*}\big)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\widetilde{\Phi},

and corresponding variables
\boldsymbol{\xi}:=\Phi\mathbf{q}=\big(\widetilde{\Phi}\widetilde{\Phi}^{*}\big)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
. It is straightforward to verify that

\mathbf{P}:=\Phi^{*}\Phi=\widetilde{\Phi}^{*}\big(\widetilde{\Phi}\widetilde{\Phi}^{*}\big)^{-1}\widetilde{\Phi}

is an orthogonal projection, and so we will assume that we can partition the identity of
\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
as
\mathbf{I}=\mathbf{P}+\Psi^{*}\Psi
, where
\Psi\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times N}
is also a matrix of full rank with orthonormal rows, and
m:=N-n>0
.

Now defining
\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{t}:=\Psi\mathbf{q}_{t}
, we can write the original SDE system in block format as

(2.5)

\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}\\
\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{t}\end{array}\right)=-\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\mathbf{A}_{0}&\boldsymbol{\alpha}\\
\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{*}&\mathbf{A}_{1}\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}\\
\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{t}\end{array}\right)\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2\beta^{-1}}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{w}_{0,t}\\
\mathrm{d}\mathbf{w}_{1,t}\end{array}\right),

where we have set:

(2.6)

\begin{gathered}\mathbf{A}_{0}:=\Phi\mathbf{A}\Phi^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n},\quad\boldsymbol{\alpha}:=\Phi\mathbf{A}\Psi^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m},\quad\mathbf{A}_{1}:=\Psi\mathbf{A}\Psi^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m},\\
\mathbf{w}_{0,t}:=\Phi\mathbf{w}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbf{w}_{1,t}:=\Psi\mathbf{w}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}.\end{gathered}

2.3
Solution of coarse-grained model

Following similar steps to those used to derive the general solution (
2.2
) to (
2.1
), we can express the general solution to the equation for
\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{t}
in the block system (
2.5
) as

(2.7)

\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{t}=\mathrm{e}^{-\mathbf{A}_{1}t}\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{0}-\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{e}^{\mathbf{A}_{1}(s-t)}\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{*}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{s}\,\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{e}^{\mathbf{A}_{1}(s-t)}\sqrt{2\beta^{-1}}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{w}_{1,s}.

We note that the equation satisfied by
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}
in the block system (
2.5
) is

(2.8)

\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}=-\mathbf{A}_{0}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}\mathrm{d}t-\boldsymbol{\alpha}\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{t}\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2\beta^{-1}}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{w}_{0,t}.

By substituting the expression (
2.7
) into the second term on the right-hand side of this SDE, we have

(2.9)

\begin{split}\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}=-\bigg(\mathbf{A}_{0}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}+\boldsymbol{\alpha}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathbf{A}_{1}t}\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{0}-\int_{0}^{t}\!\!\boldsymbol{\alpha}\mathrm{e}^{\mathbf{A}_{1}(s-t)}\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{*}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{s}\,\mathrm{d}s\bigg)\mathrm{d}t\qquad\qquad\\
+\bigg(\int_{0}^{t}\!\!\boldsymbol{\alpha}\mathrm{e}^{\mathbf{A}_{1}(s-t)}\sqrt{2\beta^{-1}}\cdot\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{w}_{1,s}\bigg)\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2\beta^{-1}}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{w}_{0,t}.\end{split}

In this equation, we have obtained a closed equation for
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}
, up to prescribing an initial condition for
\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{0}
.

The derivation we have just performed can be viewed as a form of the Mori-Zwanzig approach to model reduction
[
3
,
4
]
, as we have expressed the evolution of the coarse-grained variables as a self-consistent evolution equation. However, the price for this transformation is that the equation has become non-local in time due to the two integral terms which now appear on the right-hand side of the equation. In the following section, we will approximate these integral terms, to derive coarse-grained models which are local in time.

3
Markovian approximate dynamics

In this section, we use the integrodifferential equation obtained in (
2.9
) to derive Markovian equations which approximate its solution by replacing the integral terms which require evaluation at time
t
only. We proceed formally to motivate the rigorous approximation results which follow.

3.1
Approximation of integral terms

First, we consider the first integral term on the right-hand side of (
2.9
). By formally expanding
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{s}
about
s=t
, exchanging the order of summation and integration, and rearranging, we obtain:

\displaystyle I

\displaystyle:=\int_{0}^{t}\!\!\boldsymbol{\alpha}\mathrm{e}^{\mathbf{A}_{1}(s-t)}\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{*}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{s}\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\!\!\boldsymbol{\alpha}\mathrm{e}^{\mathbf{A}_{1}(s-t)}\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{*}\bigg(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}+(s-t)\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}+\sum_{j=2}^{\infty}\frac{(s-t)^{j}}{j!}\mathrm{d}^{j}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}\bigg)\,\mathrm{d}s.

Truncating the series, we have the approximation

I\approx\underbrace{\bigg(\int_{0}^{t}\!\!\boldsymbol{\alpha}\mathrm{e}^{\mathbf{A}_{1}(s-t)}\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{*}\mathrm{d}s\bigg)\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}}_{=:I_{1}}+\underbrace{\bigg(\int_{0}^{t}\!\!\boldsymbol{\alpha}\mathrm{e}^{\mathbf{A}_{1}(s-t)}\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{*}(s-t)\,\mathrm{d}s\bigg)\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}}_{=:I_{2}}.

For values of
t
which are sufficiently large, the integrals
\{I_{j}\}_{j=1}^{2}
may be approximated by integrating over the interval
(-\infty,t)
. This leads to the further approximations

\displaystyle I_{1}

\displaystyle\approx\bigg(\int_{-\infty}^{t}\boldsymbol{\alpha}e^{\mathbf{A}_{1}(s-t)}\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{*}\,\mathrm{d}s\bigg)\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}=\boldsymbol{\alpha}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{*}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t},

\displaystyle\quad\text{and}\quad I_{2}

\displaystyle\approx\bigg(\int_{-\infty}^{t}\boldsymbol{\alpha}e^{\mathbf{A}_{1}(s-t)}\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{*}(s-t)\,\mathrm{d}s\bigg)\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}=-\boldsymbol{\alpha}\mathbf{A}_{1}^{-2}\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{*}\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}.

To handle the stochastic integral term on the right-hand side of (
2.9
), we apply Itô’s formula.
Recall that for any function
\mathbf{u}:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{k}\to\mathbb{R}^{l}
which is continuously differentiable in its first argument, and twice continuously differentiable in its second argument, we have

\mathbf{u}(t,\mathbf{w}_{t})-\mathbf{u}(0,\mathbf{0})=\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\partial\mathbf{u}}{\partial s}(s,\mathbf{w}_{s})\,\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}\nabla\mathbf{u}(s,\mathbf{w}_{s})\cdot\mathrm{d}\mathbf{w}_{s}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\Delta\mathbf{u}(s,\mathbf{w}_{s})\mathrm{d}s.

In this formula, the differential operators
\nabla
and
\Delta
are the usual
k
–dimensional gradient and Laplacian, which act component-wise on the vector argument of
\mathbf{u}
and the differentiation is performed with respect to the second variable.
In order to use this result in our case, we set
\mathbf{u}(s,\mathbf{x})=\boldsymbol{\alpha}\mathrm{e}^{\mathbf{A}_{1}(s-t)}\mathbf{x}
for any
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, which allows us to write

(3.1)

\int_{0}^{t}\boldsymbol{\alpha}\mathrm{e}^{\mathbf{A}_{1}(s-t)}\cdot\mathrm{d}\mathbf{w}_{1,s}=\boldsymbol{\alpha}\mathbf{w}_{1,t}-\int_{0}^{t}\boldsymbol{\alpha}\mathbf{A}_{1}\mathrm{e}^{\mathbf{A}_{1}(s-t)}\mathbf{w}_{1,s}\,\mathrm{d}s.

We now once again formally Taylor expand
\mathbf{w}_{1,s}
about
s=t
on the right-hand side, and switch the order of summation and integration, and truncate the series, leading to the approximation

\displaystyle J:

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\boldsymbol{\alpha}\mathbf{A}_{1}\mathrm{e}^{\mathbf{A}_{1}(s-t)}\mathbf{w}_{1,s}\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\boldsymbol{\alpha}\mathbf{A}_{1}\mathrm{e}^{\mathbf{A}_{1}(s-t)}\Big(\mathbf{w}_{1,t}+(s-t)\mathrm{d}\mathbf{w}_{1,t}+\sum_{j=2}^{\infty}\frac{(s-t)^{j}}{j!}\mathrm{d}^{j}\mathbf{w}_{1,t}\Big)\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\approx\underbrace{\int_{0}^{t}\boldsymbol{\alpha}\mathbf{A}_{1}\mathrm{e}^{\mathbf{A}_{1}(s-t)}\mathrm{d}s\,\mathbf{w}_{1,t}}_{=:J_{1}}+\underbrace{\int_{0}^{t}\boldsymbol{\alpha}\mathbf{A}_{1}\mathrm{e}^{\mathbf{A}_{1}(s-t)}(s-t)\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{w}_{1,t}}_{=:J_{2}}.

As in the case of
I_{1}
and
I_{2}
, we may approximate the deterministic integral factors by extending the regime of integration to
(-\infty,t)
and by doing so, we obtain

J_{1}\approx\boldsymbol{\alpha}\mathbf{w}_{1,t}\quad\text{and}\quad J_{2}\approx-\boldsymbol{\alpha}\mathbf{A}_{1}^{-1}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{w}_{1,t}.

Note that
J_{1}
cancels the first term on the right hand side of (
3.1
).

3.2
Markovian approximate equations

The formal arguments made above lead to a series possible approximations to the coarse-grained equation (
2.9
), depending on our choice of leading order approximation for the integral terms
I
and
J
. Natural choices are to include zero, one or two terms in each of the approximations. In order to state these alternative effective dynamics in a concise form, we define

(3.2)

\mathbf{B}:=\mathbf{A}_{0}-\boldsymbol{\alpha}\mathbf{A}_{1}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{*}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbf{C}:=(\mathbf{I}+\boldsymbol{\alpha}\mathbf{A}_{1}^{-2}\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{*})^{-1},

where we recall the definitions of the submatrices
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
,
\mathbf{A}_{0}
and
\mathbf{A}_{1}
made in (
2.6
). The resulting equations are then:

(3.3)

Approach 0:

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}=

\displaystyle-\Big(\mathbf{A}_{0}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}-\boldsymbol{\alpha}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathbf{A}_{1}t}\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{0}\Big)\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2\beta^{-1}}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{w}_{0,t}

(3.4)

Approach 1:

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}=

\displaystyle-\Big(\mathbf{B}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}-\boldsymbol{\alpha}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathbf{A}_{1}t}\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{0}\Big)\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2\beta^{-1}}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{w}_{0,t}

(3.5)

Approach 2:

\displaystyle\mathbf{C}^{-1}\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}=

\displaystyle-\Big(\mathbf{B}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}-\boldsymbol{\alpha}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathbf{A}_{1}t}\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{0}\Big)\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2\beta^{-1}}\Big(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{w}_{0,t}+\boldsymbol{\alpha}\mathbf{A}_{1}^{-1}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{w}_{1,t}\Big).

Approach 0 approximates the integral terms by neglecting them, i.e. by setting
I\approx 0
and
J\approx 0
. Approach 1 includes the approximation of
I_{1}
and
J_{1}
, and Approach 2 includes the approximation of
I_{1}
,
I_{2}
,
J_{1}
and
J_{2}
.

Note that in the final case where we introduce
\mathrm{d}\mathbf{w}_{1,t}
, we could replace the sum of two independent Brownian motions with a single Brownian motion

\sqrt{2\beta^{-1}}\Big(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{w}_{0,t}+\boldsymbol{\alpha}\mathbf{A}_{1}^{-1}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{w}_{1,t}\Big)\sim\sqrt{2\beta^{-1}}\Big(\mathbf{I}+\boldsymbol{\alpha}\mathbf{A}_{1}^{-2}\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{*}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{w}_{0,t}=\sqrt{2\beta^{-1}}\mathbf{C}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{w}_{0,t},

where the RHS has identical statistics to the sum of Brownian motions on the left due to the additive properties of the variance of Gaussian distributions. This means we can replace Approach 2 by

(3.6)

\text{Approach $2$: }\quad\begin{aligned} \mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}=&-\mathbf{C}\Big(\mathbf{B}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}-\boldsymbol{\alpha}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathbf{A}_{1}t}\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{0}\Big)\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2\beta^{-1}\mathbf{C}}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{w}_{0,t}.\end{aligned}

Solutions of this SDE are statistically equivalent to (
3.5
), and we will use this definition from now on.

Remark 1.

An alternative way to derive Approach 2 is to first integrate Approach 1 to get an approximation of
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}
and then plug it into (
2.9
). This type of strategy is widely used when constructing predictor-corrector numerical integrators
[
33
]
. In fact, we can continue this Predict–Evaluate–Correct (PEC) strategy to further improve the approximation of
\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}
if the corrector method is convergent.
∎

Remark 2.

We note that we chose to truncate the formal series approximating
I
and
J
at first-order, but we could have retained further terms. Doing so would have resulted in an SDE system to approximate the coarse-grained which was higher-order in time, requiring an increased number of variables to describe properly. This idea is closely related to the approximation approach studied in
[
34
,
35
,
36
]
.
∎

Remark 3.
Note that if the dimensionality of the original model
N
is very large, directly computing and storing
\mathbf{A}_{1}^{-1}
will be computationally infeasible. In such cases, it follows that assembling the matrices
\mathbf{B}
and
\mathbf{C}
directly may not be possible, and hence they must be approximated in practice (see for example
[
37
]
). On the other hand, our derivation does elucidate the right structural choice for the coarse-grained dynamics, and this seems a promising way to inform data-driven approaches to fitting coarse-grained models in future.
∎

4
Statistical properties of dynamics

We now consider the various dynamical approaches we have proposed as Markovian approximations to the true coarse-grained dynamics given in (
2.9
), and derive and compare various statistical properties of these alternatives with those of the true dynamics. In particular, we study the equilibrium statistics for the very long-time property and the mean-squared displacement and autocovariance for the short and medium-time properties.

4.1
Equilibrium statistics

Independently of the initial conditions for the true dynamics, we note that as
t\to\infty
, the distribution of
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}
evolving under the original (
2.9
) tends to a Gaussian equilibrium distribution,
\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\beta^{-1}\Phi\mathbf{A}^{-1}\Phi^{*})
, i.e. a multivariate normal distribution with mean
\mathbf{0}
and covariance matrix
\Phi\mathbf{A}^{-1}\Phi^{*}
. Using the theory of Schur complements
[
38
]
, we have that

(4.1)

\Phi\mathbf{A}^{-1}\Phi^{*}=(\mathbf{A}_{0}-\boldsymbol{\alpha}\mathbf{A}_{1}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{*})^{-1}=\mathbf{B}^{-1}.

For
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}
evolving under Approaches 1 and 2, it can be checked that the resulting equilibrium distribution for is identical. On the other hand, the equilibrium distribution for Approach 0 has covariance
\mathbf{A}_{0}^{-1}
. These facts are encoded in the following proposition:

Proposition 1.

Approaches 0, 1 and 2 all have uniquely-defined equilibrium distributions, which are Gaussian with mean
\mathbf{0}
and covariances
\beta^{-1}\mathbf{A}_{0}^{-1}
,
\beta^{-1}\mathbf{B}^{-1}
and
\beta^{-1}\mathbf{B}^{-1}
respectively. In each case, the dynamics is ergodic with respect to this distribution.

As a consequence, the equilibrium distributions for Approaches 1 and 2 always agree with the true equilibrium distribution of
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}
evolving under the full dynamics. The equilibrium distribution for
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}
evolving under Approach 0 agrees with the true equilibrium distribution if and only if the
n
rows of
\Phi
span a subspace generated by
n
linearly independent eigenvectors of
\mathbf{A}
.

The first part of this result is a direct corollary of Proposition 4.2 in
[
32
]
applied to each of the equations, so we omit a proof. The latter part of this result is implied by the following lemma, which provides conditions under which the covariance of of the equilibrium distribution for Approach 0 agrees with the true covariance: a detailed proof is provided in Appendix
A.1
.

Lemma 2.

Assuming the definitions given in (
2.6
) and (
3.2
), the following statements are equivalent:

•

\mathbf{A}_{0}^{-1}=\mathbf{B}^{-1}
;

•

\boldsymbol{\alpha}=\Phi\mathbf{A}\Psi^{*}=\mathbf{0}
; and

•

The
n
rows of
\Phi
span a subspace generated by
n
linearly independent eigenvectors of
\mathbf{A}
.

We note further that in the case where the rows of
\Phi
span a collection of eigenspaces
\mathbf{A}
, we have the stronger result that the equations (
3.3
)–(
3.5
) defining Approaches 0–2 are identical, since the result of Lemma

guarantees that
\mathbf{B}=\mathbf{A}_{0}
, and moreover

\mathbf{C}=(\mathbf{I}+\boldsymbol{\alpha}\mathbf{A}_{1}^{-2}\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{*})^{-1}=\mathbf{I}.

In other words, if the row-space of the coarse-graining map
\Phi
is exactly a span of eigenvectors of the matrix
\mathbf{A}
, then the coarse-grained system completely decouples from the remaining variables.

In a practical coarse-graining setting, managing to accurately capture a large collection of eigenspaces of the dynamics via the coarse-graining map without doing so intentionally is highly improbable. The fact that the approaches do collapse in this way does however suggest that, under the simple choice of dynamics (
2.1
), designing a coarse-graining map which captures eigenspaces of
\mathbf{A}
will lead to accurate effective dynamics, regardless of the sophistication of the approach chosen.

4.2
Mean-squared displacement and covariance

Above, we have summarised the equilibrium statistics for the different coarse-graining approaches we proposed in (
3.3
)-(
3.5
). Similar properties have been studied extensively in previous works, and so the focus of our work here is on the dynamical statistical properties of the system.
We note that Proposition
4.1
implies that in general, Approach 0 incorrectly predicts the equilibrium statistics of the coarse-grained variables, and for this reason, we will ignore this approach from now on, considering only Approaches 1 and 2.

In many applications in statistical physics, and particularly in the simulation of molecular diffusion in solutes, a natural dynamical statistical quantity of interest is the mean-squared displacement of a particle. Mathematically, this quantity is closely related to the autocovariance of the evolution, as we discuss here. In particular, the mean-squared displacement of the stochastic process
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}
is defined to be

(4.2)

D(t):=\mathbb{E}\big[\|\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}-\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0}\|^{2}\big],

where
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0}
is a deterministic initial condition for the evolution of
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}
.
We note that in general, this definition is dependent upon the choice of
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0}
, but we suppress this dependence in our notation. We note that we can rewrite
D(t)
as

D(t)=\tr\Big(\mathbb{E}\big[(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}-\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0})\otimes(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}-\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0})\big]\Big),

where
\otimes
is the usual dyadic product.
Then, by adding and subtracting
\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}]
and
\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0}]
and using standard properties of expectations, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}-\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0})\otimes(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}-\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0})\big]

\displaystyle\qquad=\mathbb{E}\big[(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}-\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}]+\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}-\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0}]+\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0}]-\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0})\otimes(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}-\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}]+\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}-\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0}]+\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0}]-\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0})\big],

\displaystyle\qquad=\Cov(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t})+\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}-\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0}]\otimes\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}-\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0}]+\Cov(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0})-\Cov(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t})-\Cov(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0}),

where the covariance matrix for two multivariate random variables is

\Cov(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}):=\mathbb{E}\Big[\big(\mathbf{u}-\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{u}]\big)\otimes\big(\mathbf{v}-\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{v}]\big)\Big].

Taking the trace, we have

D(t)=\big\|\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}-\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0}]\big\|^{2}+\tr[\Cov(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}-\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}-\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0})].

We hence see that the mean-squared displacement is the sum of two contributions: the squared mean of the displacement and the trace of the covariance matrix of displacement at time
t
relative to the position at initial time.

In the case where the mean of the initial condition is zero and remains zero for all time, so that
\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0}]=\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}]=\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}-\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0}]=\mathbf{0}
, the mean-squared displacement is completely determined by the displacement covariance matrix,
\Cov(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}-\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}-\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0})
.
Moreover, if we further assume that the initial condition is deterministic with
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0}=\mathbf{0}
, then
\Cov(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0})
and
\tr[\Cov(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t})]
are zeros, and hence
D(t)
is exactly the trace of
\Cov(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t})
. As we will see below, different coarse-graining approaches provide a range of approximations to the covariance matrix.

4.3
Autocovariance of full model

In order to compare the statistics of interest, we first compute the autocovariance of
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}
evolving under the full model, (
2.9
), where the initial conditions
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0}=\Phi\mathbf{q}_{0}
and
\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{0}=\Psi\mathbf{q}_{0}
are assumed to be fully deterministic. In this case, it is staightforward to show that the mean and covariance of
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}
are

\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}]=\Phi\mathrm{e}^{-t\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{q}_{0},\quad\text{and}\quad\Cov(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{s},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t})=\beta^{-1}\Phi\mathbf{A}^{-1}\big(\mathrm{e}^{-|s-t|\mathbf{A}}-\mathrm{e}^{-(s+t)\mathbf{A}}\big)\Phi^{*}.

If
s=t
, the latter becomes

\Cov(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t})=\beta^{-1}\Phi\mathbf{A}^{-1}\big(\mathbf{I}-\mathrm{e}^{-2t\mathbf{A}}\big)\Phi^{*}
, and we note that the autocovariance at initial time is zero, so the mean-squared displacement is

\displaystyle D(t)

\displaystyle=\tr[\Cov(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t})]+\|\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}-\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0}]\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\beta^{-1}\tr\big[\Phi\mathbf{A}^{-1}\big(\mathbf{I}-\mathrm{e}^{-2t\mathbf{A}}\big)\Phi^{*}\big]+\|\Phi(\mathrm{e}^{-t\mathbf{A}}-\mathbf{I})\mathbf{q}_{0}\|^{2}.

As noted above, if
\mathbf{q}_{0}=\mathbf{0}
, the mean-squared displacement is completely determined by the covariance of the evolution, becoming

D(t)=\beta^{-1}\tr\big[\Phi\mathbf{A}^{-1}\big(\mathbf{I}-\mathrm{e}^{-2t\mathbf{A}}\big)\Phi^{*}\big].

Although we have assumed a deterministic initial condition here, we note that the dynamics converges to a unique equilibrium regardless of the precise initial distribution, and indeed as
s,t\to\infty
with
|s-t|
bounded, the autocovariance in both of the above cases tends towards

\Cov(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{s},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t})\to\beta^{-1}\Phi\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathrm{e}^{-|s-t|\mathbf{A}}\Phi^{*}.

To this end, we define the equilibrium autocovariance function
\mathbf{R}:[0,+\infty)\to\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
to be

(4.3)

\mathbf{R}(\tau)=\beta^{-1}\Phi\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathrm{e}^{-\tau\mathbf{A}}\Phi^{*}.

4.5
A matrix form of Jensen’s inequality

In order to provide global bounds on the autocovariance error we will use a technical result, which is a version of Jensen’s inequality for matrices. The form of the result is an adaptation of aspects of the results of
[
39
]
or
[
40
, Theorem 2.1]
.

In order to state this result, we introduce the Löwner partial ordering on matrices
[
41
]
, which will be used throughout the remainder of this work. We write
\mathbf{A}\leq\mathbf{B}
if and only if
\mathbf{B}-\mathbf{A}
is positive definite, and for any
x\in\mathbb{R}
, we write
x\leq\mathbf{A}
to mean that
x\mathbf{I}\leq\mathbf{A}
. In particular,
0\leq\mathbf{A}
means that
\mathbf{A}
is positive definite. With this notation in place, we may state and prove the following result.

Theorem 3.

Suppose that
f:(0,+\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
is monotone and convex, and extend the action of this function to real, positive definite matrices via the standard identification

f(\mathbf{A})=f(\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{Q}^{*})=\mathbf{Q}f(\mathbf{D})\mathbf{Q}^{*}:=\mathbf{Q}\,\mathrm{diag}\big[f(\mathbf{D}_{11}),\dots,f(\mathbf{D}_{NN})\big]\mathbf{Q}^{*},

where
\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{Q}^{*}
is the diagonalisation of
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbf{R}^{N\times N}
, so
\mathbf{Q}
is an orthogonal matrix and
\mathbf{D}
is a diagonal matrix.
Then, if
\Phi\in\mathbf{R}^{n\times N}
satisfies
\Phi\Phi^{*}=\mathbf{I}_{n}
, it holds that

f(\Phi\mathbf{A}\Phi^{*})\leq\Phi f(\mathbf{A})\Phi^{*}

for all symmetric positive definite matrices
\mathbf{A}
. For a more general matrix
\boldsymbol{\Phi}
, if we further define
\mathbf{\Sigma}=\sqrt{\Phi\Phi^{*}}
then

\mathbf{\Sigma}^{*}\Big(f\big(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\Phi\mathbf{A}\Phi^{*}(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{*})^{-1}\big)\Big)\mathbf{\Sigma}\leq\Phi f(\mathbf{A})\Phi^{*}.

Proof.

As stated above, this is a direct consequence of results in
[
39
]
. In particular, the map

\phi:\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}\to\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
defined by

\phi(\mathbf{A}):=\Phi\mathbf{A}\Phi^{*}

is positive (order–preserving) and unital (identity–preserving), and so conclusion (1) of Proposition 5.2 in
[
39
]
implies the result. The latter conclusion is a simple consequence of applying the order-preserving mapping
\psi(\mathbf{B}):=\mathbf{\Sigma}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{\Sigma}
to both sides of the inequality.
∎

4.6
Global pointwise-in-time error bounds

We now employ the result above to prove the following global bounds on the difference between the autocovariance matrices. Full proofs of the global bounds which follow are postponed to Appendix
A.2
.

Theorem 4.

For all
\tau>0
, we have that the difference between the true equilibrium autocovariance and the equilibrium autocovariance for Approach 1 can be bounded above and below using the Löwner partial order as follows:

0\leq\mathbf{R}(\tau)-\mathbf{R}_{1}(\tau)\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\beta^{-1}\tau^{2}(\mathbf{A}_{0}-\mathbf{B})=\tfrac{1}{2}\beta^{-1}\tau^{2}\boldsymbol{\alpha}\mathbf{A}_{1}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{*}.

Likewise, for all
\tau>0
, the difference between the true equilibrium autocovariance and the autocovariance
of Approach 2 can be bounded above and below as follows:

\beta^{-1}\tau(\mathbf{C}-\mathbf{I})\leq\mathbf{R}(\tau)-\mathbf{R}_{2}(\tau)\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\beta^{-1}\tau^{2}(\mathbf{A}_{0}-\mathbf{C}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{C})+\beta^{-1}\tau(\mathbf{C}-\mathbf{I}).

As a particular consequence of the latter bound, there exists
\tau^{*}>0
such that

\beta^{-1}\tau(\mathbf{C}-\mathbf{I})\leq\mathbf{R}(\tau)-\mathbf{R}_{2}(\tau)\leq\beta^{-1}\tau(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{C})

for all
\tau\in[0,\tau^{*}]
.

While the bounds provided by this result are global, it is clear that they do not accurately reflect the error for large values of
\tau
, since the autocovariance functions all decay exponentially. Nevertheless, performing Taylor expansions of the autocovariance functions at
\tau=0
demonstrate that these bounds are highly accurate for
\tau
small; a short calculation demonstrates that, as
\tau\to 0
, we have

(4.6)

\displaystyle\beta\mathbf{R}(\tau)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{B}^{-1}-\tau\mathbf{I}+\tfrac{1}{2}\tau^{2}\mathbf{A}_{0}+O(\tau^{3}),

\displaystyle\beta\mathbf{R}_{1}(\tau)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{B}^{-1}-\tau\mathbf{I}+\tfrac{1}{2}\tau^{2}\mathbf{B}+O(\tau^{3})={\mathbf{R}(\tau)+O(\tau^{2})},

\displaystyle\beta\mathbf{R}_{2}(\tau)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{B}^{-1}-\tau\mathbf{C}+\tfrac{1}{2}\tau^{2}\mathbf{C}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{C}+O(\tau^{3})={\mathbf{R}(\tau)+O(\tau)}.

We see directly that the comparison of these expansions would give us the same results for sufficiently small
\tau
.

We note that the first bounds provided by Theorem
4.6
demonstrate that the autocovariance of Approach 1 is always an underestimate of the true autocovariance. Connecting this result with the discussion of the mean-squared displacement in Section
4.2
, we see that coarse-graining via Approach 1 will always predict a faster rate of self-diffusion than the full dynamics. Our result therefore agrees with the widely-observed fact that coarse-grained models are often less stiff than their fine-grained counterparts. While this property may be desirable for certain problems where shortening equilibriation times can lead to accelerated mixing
[
42
,
43
]
, for problems where better estimates of dynamical statistics are required (see for instance
[
44
]
), Approach 1 demonstrates a systematic bias.

On the other hand, inspecting the Taylor expansion for Approach 2 provided in (
4.6
), we see that this approach systematically overestimates the true autocovariance for short lag times. This overestimation does not persist: indeed, we will confirm this both analytically and numerically in subsequent sections, where we will show that while Approach 1 provides an excellent approximation to the autocovariance for short lags
\tau
, Approach 2 provides a better global approximation, particularly when considering the long-time tail behaviour.

4.7
Progressive coarse-graining

In addition to the bounds obtained in Theorem
4.6
, we now argue that the application of Jensen’s inequality in our analysis has an interesting consequence for the comparison of models obtained by progressively coarse-graining.

Corollary 5.

Suppose that
\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}
and
\Phi\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times N}
with
d<n
are coarse-graining maps which satisfy

\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{*}=\mathbf{I}\quad\text{and}\quad\Phi\Phi^{*}=\mathbf{I},

and let
\mathbf{Y}=\mathbf{X}\Phi\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times N}
be their composition. Suppose further that, applying approximation Approach 1, we have that:

•

\mathbf{q}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
solves the full dynamics,

•

\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
solves the approximate dynamics derived using the map
\Phi
, and

•

\mathbf{x}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
solves the approximate dynamics derived using the map
\mathbf{Y}
.

Then we have the following estimates relating the equilibrium covariances:

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}

\displaystyle\Big\|\Cov(\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{q}_{t},\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{q}_{t+\tau})-\Cov(\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t},\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t+\tau})\Big\|_{F}

(4.7)

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\lim_{t\to\infty}\Big\|\Cov(\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{q}_{t},\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{q}_{t+\tau})-\Cov(\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{x}_{t+\tau})\Big\|_{F},

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}

\displaystyle\Big\|\Cov(\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t},\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t+\tau})-\Cov(\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{x}_{t+\tau})\Big\|_{F}

(4.8)

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\lim_{t\to\infty}\Big\|\Cov(\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{q}_{t},\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{q}_{t+\tau})-\Cov(\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{x}_{t+\tau})\Big\|_{F},

where
\|\cdot\|_{F}
is the Frobenius norm.

We may interpret these estimates as saying that the covariance error necessarily increases with the degree of coarse-graining, and differences in autocovariance between coarse and intermediately coarse-grained models provide guaranteed lower bounds on the error between the finest and coarsest models. A full proof of the result is given in Section
A.3
, and employs Theorem
4.5
.

As a concrete example to illustrate the application of the above result, let us consider taking
\Phi
to be a map onto the first
n
variables, and
\mathbf{X}
a further reduction onto the first
d
variables with
d<n
, so that

\Phi=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\mathbf{I}_{n}&\mathbf{0}_{n\times(N-n)}\end{array}\right)\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbf{X}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\mathbf{I}_{d}&\mathbf{0}_{d\times(n-d)}\end{array}\right).

Here,
\mathbf{I}_{n}
and
\mathbf{I}_{d}
are identity submatrices, and
\mathbf{0}_{n\times(N-n)}
and
\mathbf{0}_{d\times(n-d)}
are submatrices of zeros.
It follows that the composition of these maps,
\mathbf{Y}:=\mathbf{X}\Phi\in\mathbf{R}^{d\times N}
is a ‘coarser’ coarse-graining map satisfying
\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{Y}^{*}=\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\Phi}\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{*}\mathbf{X}^{*}=\mathbf{I}
. In the particular example case given above, we have that

\mathbf{Y}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\mathbf{I}_{d}&\mathbf{0}_{d\times(N-d)}\end{array}\right).

In this instance, this result compares the autocovariances of the first
d
variables under the approximate coarse-graining approaches laid out in Section
3
.

Remark 4.
The first estimate in the statement of Corollary
4.7
now says that if we consider the difference between the autocovariance of the first
d
variables in the full model and the first
d
variables in the
n
-dimensional coarse-grained model with
d<n
, then the error will be smaller than that committed in the
d
-dimensional coarse-grained model.

The second estimate says that the error in the autocovariance between the first
d
variables in the
n
-dimensional model and the autocovariance of the
d
-dimensional model provides a lower bound on the autocovariance error to the full model. As such, comparing the autocovariance of a partially coarse-grained model with a further coarse-grained model can be used as an practical assessment of its potential inaccuracy.

We note that we do not prove a similar analytic result for Approach 2. When the time-scale separation (i.e., the spectral gap) within the system is large, the numerical results in Section 6.2 suggest that Approach 2 also possesses this property; but when the spectral gap is not notable, as seen in some examples of Section 6.3, the conclusion of Corollary
4.7
will fail for Approach 2.

As a consequence, we will study a two-dimensional system in detail in the next section as the 2D error estimates provide error bounds and insights for higher-dimensional systems.
∎

5
Coarse-graining of two-dimensional systems

We next perform a detailed analytical comparison of both approaches for two-dimensional systems. In particular, throughout this section we will assume
N=2
and
n=m=1
, so that the coarse graining map reduces a two-dimensional system to a one-dimensional one. In this case, we obtain improved versions of the error estimates established in Theorem
4.6
which hold for longer time-scales. In doing so, we elucidate various features of coarse-graining which we expect to still be present in higher-dimensional cases, which are studied numerically in Section
6
.

In the two-dimensional case, we may describe all possible coarse-grained systems of the form (
2.5
) using only two parameters
(\lambda,\,\theta)
.
Choosing coordinates such that
\mathbf{A}
is diagonal, and rescaling such that the lowest eigenvalue of
\mathbf{A}
is
1
, we may assume that

(5.1)

\mathbf{A}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&0\\
0&\lambda\end{array}\right),

where
1\leq\lambda
. In general, any coarse-graining map satisfying the assumptions outlined in Section
2.2
can be expressed in terms of a single angle
\theta\in(-\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{\pi}{2})
between the coarse-grained variable and the eigenspace of
\mathbf{A}
corresponding to the eigenvalue

(5.2)

\Phi:=(\cos\theta\;\sin\theta)\quad\text{and}\quad\Psi=(-\sin\theta\;\cos\theta).

In this context, the parameter
\lambda
therefore provides us with a way to measure the timescale separation in the system, while the angle parameter

provides with a way to explore the alignment of the CG variable with the eigenspace corresponding to the slowest timescale. In particular, we will be interested in comparing the dynamical properties of our CG approaches in the asymptotic regime where

and

, so that timescale separation is large.

5.1
Measuring autocovariance error

In order to quantify the accuracy of the approximate approaches proposed in Section

\displaystyle:=\|\mathbf{R}_{i}(\tau)-\mathbf{R}(\tau)\|_{F},

\displaystyle:=\frac{\|\mathbf{R}_{i}(\tau)-\mathbf{R}(\tau)\|_{F}}{\|\mathbf{R}(\tau)\|_{F}},

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{\tau}\int_{0}^{\tau}\big\|\mathbf{R}_{i}(t)-\mathbf{R}(t)\big\|_{F}\,\mathrm{d}t,

7
Conclusion

We have derived a framework to construct higher-order Markovian approximation of SDEs by expanding the integral representations of SDEs and hierarchically truncating them at various orders. We then analytically studied the long-time equilibrium properties and the short-time dynamical statistics of proposed approximations in two and higher dimensions. In particular, the dependence of the error in the dynamical autocovariance predicted by our reduced models is quantified in terms of both the timescale separation in the dynamics and the choice of coarse-grained variables, and the asymptotic dependence of the errors were explicitly specified in two-dimensional systems. For high-dimensional systems, we proved that autocovariance errors increase monotonically as the system is progressively coarse-grained using approximation Approach 1. While these features have been observed in numerical simulations in the past, our results provide the first theoretical confirmation that this must always happen.

Our analysis identified the conditions under which autocovariance errors are likely to increase more slowly with system timescale separation; the choice of coarse-grained variables; and the time-scale of simulation interest.

In future work, we aim to extend the current approximation strategy and analysis to other linear systems which act as prototypes for a range of more realistic dynamics for applications in statistical physics such as the underdamped Langevin system. We also plan to study nonlinear systems through a systematic expansion along the low-dimensional structure such as a slow manifold.

A.1
Proof of Lemma
4.1

To prove Lemma
4.1
, we note that the equivalence of the first two statements is easy to verify using the definition
\mathbf{B}=\mathbf{A}_{0}-\boldsymbol{\alpha}\mathbf{A}_{1}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{*}
and the fact that
\mathbf{A}_{1}
is always a positive definite symmetric matrix under our standing assumptions on
\mathbf{A}
,
\Phi
and
\Psi
.

To prove equivalence between the second and third statements, we first suppose that
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=\mathbf{0}
. Let
\mathbf{U}
be the square matrix

\mathbf{U}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\Phi\\
\Psi\end{array}\right),

so that

\mathbf{U}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{U}^{*}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\mathbf{A}_{0}&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{A}_{1}\end{array}\right).

From this block representation, we see that if
\mathbf{v}=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{N})
is an eigenvector of
\mathbf{U}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{U}
, then so too are the vectors
\mathbf{v}_{0}=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{n},0\ldots,0)
and
\mathbf{v}_{1}=(0,\ldots,0,v_{n+1},\ldots,v_{N})
. In particular, since
\mathbf{U}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{U}^{*}
is symmetric and strictly positive definite, there must be
n
linearly independent eigenvectors of
\mathbf{U}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{U}^{*}
with
v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}
which are not all zero. Moreover, taking a corresponding
\mathbf{v}_{0}
vector, we have

\lambda\mathbf{v}_{0}=\mathbf{U}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{U}^{*}\mathbf{v}_{0}\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\lambda\mathbf{U}^{*}\mathbf{v}_{0}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{U}^{*}\mathbf{v}_{0}\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\lambda\Phi^{*}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}v_{1}\\
\vdots\\
v_{n}\end{array}\right)=\mathbf{A}\Phi^{*}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}v_{1}\\
\vdots\\
v_{n}\end{array}\right).

It follows that
\Phi^{*}\mathbf{v}_{0}
is an eigenvector of
\mathbf{A}
, and hence there are
n
distinct eigenvectors of
\mathbf{A}
spanned by the rows of
\Phi
.

To prove the other implication, we note that by assumption, there exists a basis of

n
vectors
\mathbf{v}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that

\lambda\Phi^{*}\mathbf{v}_{0}=\mathbf{A}\Phi^{*}\mathbf{v}_{0}.

As
\Psi\Phi^{*}=\mathbf{0}
due to the orthogonality of projections. we may apply
\Psi
on the left to see that

\mathbf{0}=\Psi\mathbf{A}\Phi^{*}\mathbf{v}_{0}=\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{*}\mathbf{v}_{0}.

Since
\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
vanishes on a basis of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, it must be that
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=\mathbf{0}
, completing the proof.

A.2
Proof of Theorem
4.6

In this section, we provide a full proof of Theorem
4.6
. We approach each inequality in turn.

Approach 1 lower bound.
We note that
f(x):=x\mathrm{e}^{-\tau/x}
is a monotone convex function for any
\tau\geq 0
, which can be verified directly by differentiating. Applying Theorem
4.5
, we have that

\mathbf{R}_{1}(\tau)=(\Phi\mathbf{A}^{-1}\Phi^{*})\mathrm{e}^{-\tau(\Phi\mathbf{A}^{-1}\Phi^{*})^{-1}}=f\big(\Phi\mathbf{A}^{-1}\Phi^{*}\big)\leq\Phi f(\mathbf{A}^{-1})\Phi^{*}=\Phi\Big(\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathrm{e}^{-\tau\mathbf{A}}\Big)\Phi^{*}=\mathbf{R}(\tau),

implying the lower bound.

Approach 1 upper bound.

Consider the function
g(x):=\tfrac{1}{2}\tau^{2}/x-x\mathrm{e}^{-\tau/x}
, which is again a monotone convex function for any
\tau\geq 0
. Applying Theorem
4.5
, we have

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{2}t^{2}\mathbf{B}-\mathbf{R}_{1}(\tau)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\tau^{2}(\Phi\mathbf{A}^{-1}\Phi^{*})^{-1}-(\Phi\mathbf{A}^{-1}\Phi^{*})^{-1}\mathrm{e}^{-\tau(\Phi\mathbf{A}^{-1}\Phi^{*})}

\displaystyle\leq\Phi\Big(\tfrac{1}{2}\tau^{2}\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathrm{e}^{-\tau\mathbf{A}}\Big)\Phi^{*}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\tau^{2}\mathbf{A}_{0}-\mathbf{R}(\tau),

which, after rearranging, implies the desired upper bound.

Approach 2 lower bound.

To prove Approach 2 bounds, we begin by defining

\mathbf{\Omega}:=\mathbf{C}^{\frac{1}{2}}\Phi\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times N},

Now, using the second part of the statement in Theorem
4.5
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbf{R}_{2}(\tau)+\tau\mathbf{C}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{B}^{-1}\mathrm{e}^{-t\mathbf{B}\mathbf{C}}+\tau\mathbf{C}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{C}^{\frac{1}{2}}\Big(f(\mathbf{\Omega}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{\Omega}^{*})+\tau\mathbf{I}\Big)\mathbf{C}^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbf{\Omega}\Big(f(\mathbf{A}^{-1})+\tau\mathbf{I}\Big)\mathbf{\Omega}^{*}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{C}^{\frac{1}{2}}\Big(\mathbf{R}(\tau)+\tau\mathbf{I}\Big)\mathbf{C}^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbf{R}(\tau)+\tau\mathbf{I}.

Rearranging the final inequality yields the desired result.

Approach 2 upper bound.

In this case, we consider

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{2}\tau^{2}\mathbf{C}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{C}-\tau\mathbf{C}-\mathbf{R}_{2}(\tau)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{C}^{\frac{1}{2}}\Big(g(\mathbf{\Omega}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{\Omega}^{*})-\tau\mathbf{I}\Big)\mathbf{C}^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbf{\Omega}g(\mathbf{A}^{-1})\mathbf{\Omega}^{*}-\tau\mathbf{C}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{C}^{\frac{1}{2}}\Big(\tfrac{1}{2}\tau^{2}\mathbf{A}_{0}-\mathbf{R}(\tau)-\tau\mathbf{I}\Big)\mathbf{C}^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\tau^{2}\mathbf{A}_{0}-\mathbf{R}(\tau)-\tau\mathbf{I},

and hence

\mathbf{R}(\tau)-\mathbf{R}_{2}(\tau)\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\tau^{2}(\mathbf{A}_{0}-\mathbf{C}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{C})+\tau(\mathbf{C}-\mathbf{I}).

Now, since
\mathbf{C}\leq\mathbf{I}
and
\mathbf{A}_{0}\geq\mathbf{B}
, it follows that the operators on the right-hand side of this inequality are negative for small enough
\tau
, and hence there exists
\tau^{*}>0
(in particular, taking the value of the first
\tau>0
for which
0
is an eigenvalue of the matrix on the right-hand side) such that

\mathbf{R}(\tau)-\mathbf{R}_{2}(\tau)\leq 0\quad\text{for all }0\leq\tau\leq\tau^{*}.

This completes the proof of the second upper bound, and hence of the Theorem.

A.3
Proof of Corollary
4.7

Here, we give a proof of Corollary
4.7
.

Approach 1 estimates.

We note that for any fixed
t\geq 0
,
f(x):(0,+\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
defined via
f(x):=x\mathrm{e}^{-t/x}
is a monotone and convex function.
Applying Theorem
4.5
, we have that

\displaystyle\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathrm{e}^{-t\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{Y}^{*}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{X}\bigg(\boldsymbol{\Phi}\big(\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathrm{e}^{-t\mathbf{A}}\big)\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{*}\bigg)\mathbf{X}^{*}

\displaystyle\geq\mathbf{X}(\Phi\mathbf{A}^{-1}\Phi^{*})\mathrm{e}^{-t(\Phi\mathbf{A}^{-1}\Phi^{*})^{-1}}\mathbf{X}^{*}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{X}\Big((\Phi\mathbf{A}^{-1}\Phi^{*})\mathrm{e}^{-t(\Phi\mathbf{A}^{-1}\Phi^{*})^{-1}}\Big)\mathbf{X}^{*}

\displaystyle\geq\big(\mathbf{X}\Phi\mathbf{A}^{-1}\Phi^{*}\mathbf{X}^{*}\big)\mathrm{e}^{-t(\mathbf{X}\Phi\mathbf{A}^{-1}\Phi^{*}\mathbf{X}^{*})^{-1}}

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{Y}^{*})\mathrm{e}^{-t(\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{Y}^{*})^{-1}}\geq 0,

which entails that

\mathbf{Y}\Cov(\mathbf{q}_{t},\,\mathbf{q}_{t+\tau})\mathbf{Y}^{*}\geq\mathbf{X}\Cov(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t},\,\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t+\tau})\mathbf{X}^{*}\geq\Cov(\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{x}_{t+\tau})\geq 0.

Next, we note that we can rearrange this inequality to deduce that

\begin{split}0&\leq\mathbf{Y}\Cov(\mathbf{q}_{t},\mathbf{q}_{t+\tau})\mathbf{Y}^{*}-\mathbf{X}\Cov(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t+\tau})\mathbf{X}^{*}\leq\mathbf{Y}\Cov(\mathbf{q}_{t},\mathbf{q}_{t+\tau})\mathbf{Y}^{*}-\Cov(\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{x}_{t+\tau}),\end{split}

and

\mathbf{Y}\Cov(\mathbf{q}_{t},\mathbf{q}_{t+\tau})\mathbf{Y}-\Cov(\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{x}_{t+\tau})\geq\mathbf{X}\Cov(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t+\tau})\mathbf{X}^{*}-\Cov(\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{x}_{t+\tau})\geq 0.

Taking the Frobenius norm, which preserves the Löwner ordering on symmetric positive definite matrices, we obtain the stated estimates.

A.4
Proof of Theorem
5.2

Here, we provide a proof of the results on the asymptotic error for systems with large time-scale separations in two dimensions.

Using the definitions of
\mathbf{A}
,
\Phi
and
\Psi
in this setting and all relevant submatrices, we note that

(A.1)

B=\frac{\lambda}{\lambda\cos^{2}\theta+\sin^{2}\theta}\quad\text{and}\quad C=\frac{(\lambda\cos^{2}\theta+\sin^{2}\theta)^{2}}{\lambda^{2}\cos^{2}\theta+\sin^{2}\theta}.

As such, we have that

B^{-1}=\cos^{2}\theta+\lambda^{-1}\sin^{2}\theta\quad\text{and}\quad B=\sec^{2}\theta+O(\lambda^{-1})

as
\lambda\to+\infty
. This entails that

\beta R_{1}(\tau)=B^{-1}\mathrm{e}^{-\tau B}=\cos^{2}\theta\,\mathrm{e}^{-\tau\sec^{2}\theta}+O(\mathrm{e}^{-\tau\lambda})+O(\lambda^{-1}).

Similarly, we find that

\beta R(\tau)=\mathrm{e}^{-\tau}\cos^{2}\theta+\lambda^{-1}\mathrm{e}^{-\tau\lambda}\sin^{2}\theta,

and so for fixed
\tau>0
, the absolute error satisfies

|R(\tau)-R_{1}(\tau)|=\beta^{-1}\big(\mathrm{e}^{-\tau}-\mathrm{e}^{-\tau\sec^{2}\theta}\big)\cos^{2}\theta+O(\lambda^{-1})

as
\lambda\to+\infty
. The relative error bound follows upon noting that for fixed
\tau
,

\frac{1}{R(\tau)}=\beta\mathrm{e}^{\tau}\sec^{2}\theta+O(\lambda^{-1}),

so multiplying the expansions together, we obtain the desired result.

Turning to Approach 2, we note that

BC=\frac{\lambda\sin^{2}\theta+\lambda^{2}\cos^{2}\theta}{\sin^{2}\theta+\lambda^{2}\cos^{2}\theta}=1+\frac{\tan^{2}\theta}{\lambda}-\frac{\tan^{2}\theta}{\lambda^{2}}+O(\lambda^{-3}).

Substituting this expansion and Taylor expanding, we find

\displaystyle\beta R_{2}(\tau)=B^{-1}\mathrm{e}^{-\tau BC}

\displaystyle=\big(\cos^{2}\theta+\lambda^{-1}\sin^{2}\theta\big)\exp\Big(-\tau-\frac{\tau}{\lambda}\tan^{2}\theta+\frac{\tau}{\lambda^{2}}\tan^{2}\theta+O(\lambda^{-3})\Big)

\displaystyle=\big(\cos^{2}\theta+\lambda^{-1}\sin^{2}\theta\big)\mathrm{e}^{-\tau}\Big(1-\frac{\tau}{\lambda}\tan^{2}\theta+O(\lambda^{-2})\Big)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}^{-\tau}\cos^{2}\theta+\frac{1-\tau}{\lambda}\mathrm{e}^{-\tau}\sin^{2}\theta+\tau\mathrm{e}^{-\tau}\sin^{2}\theta\frac{1+(\tau-\frac{1}{2})\tan^{2}\theta}{\lambda^{2}}+O(\lambda^{-3}).

This expansion and those constructed for
R(\tau)
and
R(\tau)^{-1}
above immediately yield the result sought.
The relative error bound at
\tau=1
follows from the higher-order expansion of
R_{2}(\tau)
made above.

A.5
Proof of Theorem
5.2

To prove this result, we use various expressions computed in the proof of Theorem
5.2
, given in Section
A.4
above.

We note that if
\tau\ll\lambda^{-1}\ll 1
, then Taylor expanding about
\tau=0
gives

\displaystyle\beta R(\tau)

\displaystyle=B^{-1}-\tau+\tfrac{1}{2}\big(1+(\lambda-1)\sin^{2}\theta\big)\tau^{2}+O(\lambda^{2}\tau^{3})

\displaystyle\beta R_{1}(\tau)

\displaystyle=B^{-1}-\tau+\tfrac{1}{2}\tau^{2}B+O(\tau^{3})

\displaystyle\beta R_{2}(\tau)

\displaystyle=B^{-1}-C\tau+O(\tau^{2})

This yields

\displaystyle|R(\tau)-R_{1}(\tau)|

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\tau^{2}\beta^{-1}\big(1+(\lambda-1)\sin^{2}\theta-B\big)+O(\lambda^{2}\tau^{3})

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\tau^{2}\beta^{-1}(\lambda-1)\sin^{2}\theta+O(\tau^{2})+O(\lambda^{2}\tau^{3})

and noting that
R(\tau)^{-1}=\beta\sec^{2}\theta+O(\tau)+O(\lambda\tau^{2})
, we also have

\frac{|R_{1}(\tau)-R(\tau)|}{R(\tau)}=\tfrac{1}{2}\tau^{2}(\lambda-1)\tan^{2}\theta+O(\tau^{2})+O(\lambda^{3}\tau^{3}).

For Approach 2, we find

\displaystyle|R_{2}(\tau)-R(\tau)|

\displaystyle=\beta^{-1}(1-C)\tau+O(\lambda\tau^{2})

\displaystyle=\beta^{-1}\tau\sin^{2}\theta+O(\tau/\lambda)+O(\lambda\tau^{2}),

and so again using the asymptotic properties of
1/R(\tau)
in this case, we have

\frac{|R_{2}(\tau)-R(\tau)|}{R(\tau)}=\tau\tan^{2}\theta+O(\tau/\lambda)+O(\lambda\tau^{2}).

This completes the proof.
