Communication Cost Reduction for Subgraph Counting under Local Differential Privacy via Hash Functions
Quentin Hillebrand, Vorapong Suppakitpaisarn, Tetsuo Shibuya

Abstract
We suggest the use of hash functions to cut down the communication costs when counting subgraphs under edge local differential privacy. While various algorithms exist for computing graph statistics, including the count of subgraphs, under the edge local differential privacy, many suffer with high communication costs, making them less efficient for large graphs. Though data compression is a typical approach in differential privacy, its application in local differential privacy requires a form of compression that every node can reproduce. In our study, we introduce linear congruence hashing. With a sampling rate of s s , our method can cut communication costs by a factor of s 2 s^{2} , albeit at the cost of increasing variance in the published graph statistic by a factor of s s . The experimental results indicate that, when matched for communication costs, our method achieves a reduction in the ℓ 2 \ell_{2} -error for triangle counts by up to 1000 times compared to the performance of leading algorithms.



I
Introduction

Differential privacy

[
1
,
2
]
has emerged as a benchmark for protecting user data. To meet this standard, it is necessary to obfuscate the publication results. This can be achieved by adding small noise
[
3
]
or altering the publication outcomes with small probability
[
4
]
.

In differential privacy, it is typically assumed that there is a complete dataset available. After statistical analyses are done, obfuscation is applied to the results. However, there can be leaks of user information during the data collection or storage phases. To address these concerns, a variant of differential privacy, termed
local differential privacy

[
5
,
6
]
, has been introduced. Here, rather than starting with a full dataset, each user is prompted to disguise their data before sharing it. As a result, the received dataset is not perfect. Numerous studies, like those referenced in
[
7
,
8
]
, have aimed to extract accurate statistical insights from these imperfect datasets. The local differential privacy has been practically employed by several companies to guarantee the privacy of their users’ information. Those companies include Apple, Microsoft, and Google
[
9
,
10
,
11
]
.

Many studies on local differential privacy focus on tabular datasets, but there is also significant research dedicated to publishing graph statistics
[
12
,
13
]
. For graph-based inputs, such as social networks, the prevalent privacy standard is
edge local differential privacy

[
14
]
. In this framework, users are asked to share a disguised version of their adjacency vectors. These vectors, essentially bit sequences, signify mutual connections within the network. For instance, in a social network comprising
n
users, user
v_{i}
would provide their adjacency vector, denoted as
a_{i}=[a_{i,1},\dots,a_{i,n}]\in\{0,1\}^{n}
. Here,
a_{i,j}=1
implies that users
v_{i}
and
v_{j}
are connected, while
a_{i,j}=0
means there is no connection between them.

One widely adopted method for obfuscation is the
randomized response

[
15
,
16
,
17
]
. In this approach, users are prompted to invert each bit in their adjacency vector, denoted as
a_{i,j}
, based on a specific probability. However, while the method is straightforward, its application to real-world social networks presents challenges. Typically, in practical social networks, an individual may have at most a few thousand friends, implying that the majority of
a_{i,j}
values are zeroes. When employing the randomized response technique and flipping each bit of
a_{i,j}
based on the designated probability, a significant number of zero bits get inverted to one. This phenomenon can distort the resulting graph statistics considerably
[
18
,
19
]
.

Beyond the publication of full graphs via randomized rounding, there is a growing interest in subgraph counting queries under local differential privacy. The challenge was first highlighted by
[
20
]
, who proposed two key algorithms for
k
-star and triangle counting. Building on the
k
-star counting,
[
21
]
later introduced an unbiased and refined algorithm. For triangle counting, several noteworthy contributions include
[
22
]
, which addressed the communication cost,
[
23
]
, which delved into scenarios where users have access to a 2-hop graph view, and
[
24
]
, which studies the lower bound of the additive error. Moving to the shuffle model,
[
25
]
gave algorithms for both triangle and 4-cycle counting. To conclude,
[
26
]
gave an approximation method for identifying the densest subgraph, rooted in locally adjustable algorithms.

Most of the works mentioned in the previous paragraph use the two-step mechanism
[
20
,
22
,
25
]
. In this mechanism, users are required to download the adjacency vectors of all other participants. They then compute graph statistics locally using their genuine adjacency vector and the obfuscated vectors from their peers. While this technique substantially boosts the accuracy for various graph statistics, such as the count of triangles, it comes with the drawback of demanding a vast amount of data download. Each user incurs a communication cost of
\Theta(n^{2})
. Given that the user count,
n
, can reach several billion in real-world scenarios, this download demand is not affordable for the majority.

Instead of using adjacency vectors, users might consider exchanging adjacency lists. This approach involves each user transmitting and receiving only a list of node pairs that are adjacent. If we assume that a user can have a limited, constant number of friends, then the bit-download requirement for the original graph becomes
\Theta(n\log n)
. However, given the surge in edge count to
\Theta(n^{2})
due to the randomized response, the communication cost associated with downloading the obfuscated adjacency list shoots up to
\Theta(n^{2}\log n)
. This means that, post-obfuscation, the adjacency list fails to offer any communication efficiency benefits over the adjacency vector.

There are several techniques proposed to reduce the communication cost for the two-step mechanism such as the asymmetric randomized response
[
22
]
, the degree-preserving randomized response
[
27
]
, the degree-preserving exponential mechanism
[
28
]
, or technique based on the compressive sensing
[
29
]
. However, we strongly believe that the communication cost could be further reduced both in theory and practice.

I-A

Our Contributions

To mitigate the communication overhead, we suggest employing a
compression
technique on the adjacency vector or list prior to its transmission to the central server.

The concept of database compression is not novel within the realm of differential privacy. Numerous mechanisms have been developed to enhance the precision of statistics derived from tabular data through database compression using sampling. One of the most prominent among these is the SmallDB algorithm
[
30
]
. There have also been initiatives that harness the Kronecker graph to refine the accuracy of published graph statistics
[
31
]
.

On the contrary, implementing database compression for the two-step mechanism under edge local differential privacy poses greater challenges. Imagine we compress the adjacency list
[a_{i,1},\dots,a_{i,n}]^{t}
down to
[a_{i,c_{1}},\dots,a_{i,c_{m}}]^{t}
, with
m\ll n
, via sampling, and then disseminate this compressed list to all users. When other users utilize this compressed list for local calculations in the second step, they must be able to replicate the sampling outcome. We therefore need a function which can be replicated with small communication cost. This requirement inspired our proposition to utilize
linear congruence hashing

[
32
,
33
]
for the sampling process.

Using linear congruence hashing, we can evenly partition the node set
\{v_{1},\dots,v_{n}\}
into
m
subsets. Let us denote these subsets as
S_{1},\dots,S_{m}
, each having a size of
s
. The index
c_{i}
is derived from
S_{i}
with a uniform probability of
1/s
. Since all data is transmitted from the users with a probability of
1/s
, we can leverage the theorem on amplification by sub-sampling
[
34
]
to diminish the bit-flipping probability in the randomized response mechanism by a factor of
1/s
. Consequently, the count of non-zero entries in the bit vector
[a_{i,c_{1}},\dots,a_{i,c_{m}}]^{t}
is roughly
m/s
, which is approximately equal to
n/s^{2}
. By employing a sampling rate of
s
, the communication overhead can be reduced by a factor of
s^{2}
.

While our publication’s variance might increase by up to a factor of
s
for certain graph statistics, our experimental results demonstrate that, for a fixed download cost, our algorithm can reduce the
\ell_{2}
-error in triangle counts by a factor of 1000.

A valid inquiry might be whether we would obtain a comparable outcome by deterministically choosing the same disjoint sets
S_{1},\dots,S_{m}
for every user
v_{i}
. While this approach could simplify the algorithm by eliminating the need for hash functions, our findings indicate that deterministic set selection leads to a significant variance in the estimations during the mechanism’s second step. Hence, using the linear congruence hashing is vital for our compression.

II
Preliminaries

II-A

Hash functions

Hash functions (c.f.
[
35
]
) map keys from a specified key space to a designated hash space. In this paper, unless mentioned differently, the key space is represented by
[|0,n-1|]
and the hash space by
[|0,m-1|]
.

Each value in the hash space is referred to as a bin. Two keys,
\mathsf{x}
and
\mathsf{y}
, are said to be in the same bin or to ‘collide’ if they have identical hash values. In other words, for a hash function
h
, a collision occurs if
h(\mathsf{x})=h(\mathsf{y})
. Hash functions are crafted to minimize the likelihood of collisions. Universal hashing, detailed in the subsequent definition, is a primary method employed in the design of these functions.

Definition 1

(Universal Hashing Scheme
[
36
]
)
.

A family of hash functions
\mathcal{H}
is universal if, for all
\mathsf{x},\mathsf{y}\in[|0,n-1|]
such that
\mathsf{x}\neq\mathsf{y}
, we have
|\{h\in\mathcal{H}|h(\mathsf{x})=h(\mathsf{y})\}|\leq\frac{|\mathcal{H}|}{m}
.

It is shown in
[
36
]
that a scheme called linear congruence hashing
[
32
,
33
]
is universal. The scheme can be defined as
\mathcal{H}=\{h_{\mathsf{a},\mathsf{b}}|\mathsf{a}\in[|1,\mathsf{p}-1|],\mathsf{b}\in[|0,\mathsf{p}-1|]\}
when
\mathsf{p}
is a prime number greater than
n
and

h_{\mathsf{a},\mathsf{b}}(\mathsf{x})=((\mathsf{a}\mathsf{x}+\mathsf{b})\text{ mod }\mathsf{p})\text{ mod }m
. Notice that, for all
\mathsf{a,b}
and
\mathsf{k}\in[|0,m-1|]
, we have
\left\lfloor\frac{n}{m}\right\rfloor\leq|\{\mathsf{x}:h_{\mathsf{a,b}}(\mathsf{x})=k\}|\leq\left\lceil\frac{n}{m}\right\rceil
.
In other words, the number of key in a given bin is either
\left\lfloor\frac{n}{m}\right\rfloor
or
\left\lceil\frac{n}{m}\right\rceil
.

II-B

Edge Local Differential Privacy

Let the set of users be
\{v_{1},\dots,v_{n}\}
. Each user
v_{i}
has own adjacency list
a_{i}=[a_{i,1},\dots,a_{i,n}]^{t}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
where
a_{i,j}=1
if
\{i,j\}\in E
and
a_{i,j}=0
otherwise. For any
a_{i},a_{i}^{\prime}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
, let
d(a_{i},a_{i}^{\prime})
be the
\ell_{1}
-distance between the two vectors. The edge local differential privacy can be explained as in the following definition:

Definition 2

(Edge Local Differential Privacy
[
14
]
)
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
, let
\mathcal{R}:\{0,1\}^{n}\rightarrow R
be a randomized function, and let
\mathcal{A}:R^{n}\rightarrow\mathcal{S}
be a function called aggregator. The mechanism
\mathcal{M}
which outputs
\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{R}(a_{1}),\ldots,\mathcal{R}(a_{n}))
is said to be
\varepsilon
-edge local differentially private if, for any
a_{i},a_{i}^{\prime}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
such that
d(a_{i},a_{i}^{\prime})=1
and any
\mathsf{R}\subset R
, we have

\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{R}(a_{i})\in\mathsf{R}\right)\leq e^{\varepsilon}\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{R}(a_{i}^{\prime})\in\mathsf{R}\right)
.

In this setting,
\varepsilon
represents the degree of privacy protection and is termed the privacy budget. A lower privacy budget value indicates a stricter privacy limitation on published data. Each user possesses an adjacency list denoted by
a_{i}
. Before transmitting the result to the central server, they apply the randomized function
\mathcal{R}
. Subsequently, the central server aggregates this data using the aggregator
\mathcal{A}
. According to Definition 2, if a mechanism
\mathcal{M}
is
\varepsilon
-edge differentially private, significant information of
a_{i}
cannot be obtained from
\mathcal{R}(a_{i})
once it is transferred and stored on the central server.

One of the most important properties of the edge local differential privacy is the composition theorem.

Theorem 1

(Composition Theorem
[
37
]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{M}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{M}_{p}
be edge local differentially private mechanism with privacy budget
\varepsilon_{1},\dots,\varepsilon_{p}
. Then, the mechanism
\mathcal{M}_{p}\circ\cdots\circ\mathcal{M}_{1}
is
(\varepsilon_{1}+\cdots+\varepsilon_{p})
-edge local differentially private.

II-D

Smooth Sensitivity

The traditional Laplacian Mechanism adjusts noise based on the global sensitivity, which is determined from the worst possible outcome for a selected function. It does not consider the specific adjacency list. Therefore, in certain situations, the global sensitivity might be much greater than the function’s changes due to slight update in the real adjacency list. Noticing this,
[
40
]
created a mechanism where the noise relates to the smooth sensitivity, which varies depending on the instance. But before we define smooth sensitivity, we first need to explain local sensitivity and sensitivity at a distance
k
.

Definition 5

(Local Sensitivity from Definition 1.4 of
[
40
]
)
.

For
f:\{0,1\}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, the local sensitivity of
f
on
a\in\{0,1\}^{n}
is

\text{LS}_{f}(a)=\max\limits_{a^{\prime}:d(a,a^{\prime})=1}\lVert f(a)-f(a^{\prime})\rVert_{1}
.

Simply put, the local sensitivity of
f
on
\{0,1\}^{n}
measures the largest change in
f
due to a single edge alteration when starting with the adjacency list
a
. The sensitivity of
f
at distance
k
expands on this idea by looking at the changes resulting from a single alteration in the adjacency lists that are within
k
modifications from
a
.

Definition 6

(Sensitivity at distance
k
from Definition 3.1 of
[
40
]
)
.

For
f:\{0,1\}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, the sensitivity of
f
at distance
k
at
a
is

A^{(k)}(a)=\max\limits_{a^{\prime}:d(a,a^{\prime})\leq k}\text{LS}_{f}(a^{\prime})
.

We are now ready to define the smooth sensitivity.

Definition 7

(Smooth Sensitivity from Definition 2.2 of
[
40
]
)
.

For
f
a function and
\beta>0
, the
\beta
-smooth sensitivity of
f
at
a
is

S^{*}_{f,\beta}(a)=\max\limits_{k}e^{-\beta k}A^{(k)}(a)

We are now ready to define the mechanism based on the smooth sensitivity. It is shown that the mechanism is
\varepsilon
-edge local differential private.

Definition 8

(Smooth Sensitivity mechanism from Lemma 2.5 of
[
40
]
)
.

Let
\gamma>1
and
h(z)
be a probability distribution proportional to
1/(1+|z|^{\gamma})
. We will denote
Z_{\gamma}
the random variable drawn from
h
.
For
\varepsilon>0
and
\gamma>1
, the smooth sensitivity mechanism for
f
, denoted by
\mathcal{M}
, is a mechanism with the randomized function
\mathcal{R}(a)=f(a)+\frac{4\gamma}{\varepsilon}S^{*}_{f,\varepsilon/\gamma}(a)\cdot Z_{\gamma}
.

This result was later improved in
[
41
]
who proved that
\mathcal{R}(a)
could be changed to
f(a)+\frac{2(\gamma-1)}{\varepsilon}S^{*}_{f,\varepsilon/2(\gamma-1)}(a)\cdot Z_{\gamma}
.

The mechanism operates effectively for all values of
\gamma>1
. Nonetheless, it is important to highlight that when
\gamma\leq 3
, we do not know a way to calculate the variance of the random variable
Z_{\gamma}
. Consequently, we will adopt
\gamma=4
for this article, where the variance of
Z_{\gamma}
is one.

III
Previous Works on Subgraph Countings under Edge Local Differential Privacy

Let us suppose the the original graph is
G=(V,E)
when
V=\{v_{1},\dots,v_{n}\}
is a set of nodes or users and
E\subseteq\{\{u,v\}:u,v\in V\}
is a set of edges or relationships. We can publish
G
using the randomized response mechanism defined in the previous section.
Given that
a^{\prime}_{i,j}=RR(a_{i,j})
obtained from the randomized response, we can define a graph
G^{\prime}=(V,E^{\prime})
with its adjacency matrix given by
A^{\prime}=(a^{\prime}_{i,j})_{1\leq i,j\leq n}
. While one can release the count of subgraphs by counting them in
G^{\prime}
, such a release is known to introduce a significant error
[
22
]
.

III-A

Two-Step Mechanism

To mitigate the error, Imola, Murakami, and Chaudhuri
[
20
]
introduced a two-step mechanism to release the count of triangles. A tuple of three nodes, represented as
(v_{i},v_{j},v_{k})
where
i>j>k
is a triangle if
\{v_{i},v_{j}\},\{v_{i},v_{k}\},\{v_{j},v_{k}\}\in E
. We denote the set of triangles in
G
by
\Delta_{G}
.
Furthermore, for tuples in
\Delta_{G}
where the initial element is
v_{i}
, we denote them as
\Delta_{i}
. We have that
|\Delta_{G}|=\sum\limits_{i}|\Delta_{i}|
.

The first step, represented by
\mathcal{M}_{1}
, involves releasing the obscured graph
A^{\prime}
. In the second step, represented by
\mathcal{M}_{2}
, every user
v_{i}
provides an estimated value for
|\Delta_{i}|
that is based on both the real adjacency vector
a_{i}
and the distorted graph
G^{\prime}
. Let
\Delta^{\prime}_{i}=\{(v_{j},v_{k}):\{v_{i},v_{j}\},\{v_{i},v_{k}\}\in G,\{v_{j},v_{k}\}\in G^{\prime},\text{ and }i>j>k\}
. Consider a function
f
defined as
f(a_{i})=|\Delta^{\prime}_{i}|
. The randomized funtion of
\mathcal{M}_{2}
, represented as
\mathcal{R}_{2}
, aligns with what is used in the edge local Laplacian mechanism. Specifically, users are asked to add a Laplacian noise into
f(a_{i})
. The mechanism’s aggregator then sums the outcomes derived from these randomized functions.

The two-step mechanism can significantly increase the precision of the triangle counting. However, it has a large variance. Consider
i>i^{\prime}>j>k
such that
\{v_{i},v_{j}\},\{v_{i},v_{k}\},\{v_{i^{\prime}},v_{j}\},\{v_{i^{\prime}},v_{k}\}\in E
and
\{v_{j},v_{k}\}\notin E
. If, by the randomized response,
\{v_{j},v_{k}\}\in E^{\prime}
, that would contribute two to the number of triangles. When the input graph has a large number of
C_{4}
, the variance becomes large. The authors of
[
22
]
propose a technique called four-cycle trick to reduce the variance. Define
\Delta_{i}^{\prime\prime}:=\{(v_{j},v_{k})\in\Delta_{i}^{\prime}:\{v_{i},v_{k}\}\in G^{\prime}\}
. Instead of having
f(a_{i})=|\Delta_{i}^{\prime}|
, they use
f(a_{i})=|\Delta_{i}^{\prime\prime}|
.

III-B

Communication Cost

In many real-world social networks, each user is typically connected to a constant number of other users
[
42
]
. This means the number of connections (or ‘ones’ in the adjacency list
a_{i}
) is a lot fewer than
n
. So, instead of sending their entire adjacency list
a_{i}
to the aggregator, user
v_{i}
can just share the list of those directly connected to them. This approach reduces the communication cost from
n
bits down to
\Theta(\log n)
.

However, when using randomized response mechanisms, every bit in
a_{i}
has a constant probability of being flipped. The resulting adjacency list
a_{i}^{\prime}
will have roughly
\Theta(n)
ones. So, the communication-saving technique mentioned earlier will not work here.

The situation gets more difficult with the two-step mechanism discussed earlier. In this approach, each user needs to access the entire distorted graph
A^{\prime}
. The user
v_{i}
cannot choose to access only the edge
\{v_{j},v_{k}\}
such that
\{v_{i},v_{j}\},\{v_{i},v_{k}\}\in E
as that will reveal the existence of those two edges. This means they have to download the whole distorted graph, which requires about
\Theta(n^{2})
bits. Such a cost becomes unrealistic for social networks with millions of nodes.

The four-cycle trick mentioned in the previous section can help reducing the communication cost. As a triangle
(v_{i},v_{j},v_{k})\in\Delta_{i}^{\prime\prime}
only if
\{v_{i},v_{k}\}\in E^{\prime}
, user
v_{i}
can calculate
|\Delta_{i}^{\prime\prime}|
without accessing the information about whether
\{v_{j},v_{k}\}\in E^{\prime}
or not when
\{v_{i},v_{k}\}\notin E^{\prime}
. Still, we think that the savings from the trick are not substantial enough to make the two-step method viable for big graphs.

IV
Group Randomized Response

In this section, we introduce a mechanism designed for publishing graphs in a distributed environment under the edge local differential privacy. We have termed this approach the
group randomized response
(GRR). The mechanism is a four-step process:

1.

Degree Sharing Step
: In this step, each user share their degree protected by the Laplacian mechanism.

2.

Grouping Step
: In this step, nodes and edges are categorized into groups using the linear congruence hashing function.

3.

Randomized Response Step
: Here, obfuscation is applied solely to one randomly chosen representative from each group. Only edges corresponding to the representatives are sent to the aggregator.

4.

Counting Step:
Subgraphs are counted based on the information from the representatives.

Thanks to the sampling created by the grouping, this mechanism achieves a notably reduced communication cost. Moreover, the privacy budget amplification from the sampling
[
34
]
further decreases the probabilities of bit-flipping during the randomized response phase, thus further reduces the cost.

IV-A

Degree Sharing Step

During this step, each user
v_{i}
calculates the count of their connections, specifically
|\{j<i:\{i,j\}\in E\}|
. They then share this count using the Laplacian mechanism with the privacy budget of
\varepsilon_{0}
. The shared count, denoted by
\tilde{d}_{i}
, will be used only for adjusting bias in the fourth step. If there are a lot of users (
n
is large), any errors from this count are minor even if a large Laplacian noise is added. Thus, we can choose a relatively small value for
\varepsilon_{0}
.

IV-B

Grouping Step

In this step, users do not publish any information. The aim here is to form random groups of edges that are of similar sizes. It is crucial that this randomization can be reproduced, and that the division is easily shareable among all participants. To achieve such a grouping that meets these criteria, we employ the linear congruence hashing, which is discussed in Section
II
.

The central server calculates
p\in\mathbb{N}
, which represents the smallest prime number greater than
n
, the graph’s size. For each node
v_{i}
when
i\in[|1,n|]
, the server randomly generates two values:
\theta_{i}
from the range
[|1,p-1|]
and
\phi_{i}
from the range
[|0,p-1|]
. These values act as coefficients for the linear congruence hashing. After generating these coefficients, the server shares them, along with
p
, with all users.

Let
s
be a parameter called
sampling rate
,
m
be an integer such that
m=\lceil p/s\rceil
, and let
h_{i}(j)=h_{\theta_{i},\phi_{i}}(j)=((\theta_{i}\cdot j+\phi_{i})\text{ mod }p)\text{ mod }m
.
Each user
v_{i}
classifies the set of users
\{v_{1},\dots,v_{n}\}
into
m
disjoint groups, denoted by
S_{i,1},\dots,S_{i,m}
. The group
S_{i,t}
can be defined as
\{v_{j}:h_{i}(j)=t\}
. By the property of the linear congruence hashing, we have that
s-1\leq|S_{i,t}|\leq s
. To make sure that all the sets have size
s
, we add a dummy element to all
S_{i,t}
with size
s-1
. There is no edge between
v_{i}
and the dummy node.

To ensure each edge of the input undirected graph is published only once, node
v_{i}
takes on the duty of publishing only for its adjacency information with node
v_{j}
such that
j<i
. However, the grouping is consistently done using the same
p
value for all nodes. This is equivalent to a setting where node
v_{i}
assumes
a_{i,j}=0
for any
j
in the range
[|i,p|]
.

We then choose a representative for each group,
S_{i,t}
. Every member of the group has an equal probability of being selected, which is
1/s
. Let us call the chosen member from group
S_{i,t}
as
v_{c_{t}}
. When
c_{t}
is chosen from the group containing
k
, we will employ the notation
a_{i,h_{i}(k)}=a_{i,c_{t}}
for convenience.

IV-C

Randomized Response Step

Each user then sends the values
a_{i,c_{1}},\dots,a_{i,c_{m}}
to the aggregator using the randomized response mechanism with a privacy budget of
\varepsilon^{\prime}=\ln(1+s(e^{\varepsilon_{1}}-1))
. It is noteworthy that this privacy budget,
\varepsilon^{\prime}
, is considerably greater than the
\varepsilon_{1}
budget used in the traditional method. This results in a much smaller probability of flipping bits in the adjacency vector.

The obfuscated values sent from
v_{i}
to the central system are denoted as
a^{\prime}_{i,c_{1}},\dots,a^{\prime}_{i,c_{m}}\in\{0,1\}
. The aggregator then releases these values, making them available for each user in the mechanism’s final step. In the earlier approach, users had to know the list of
v_{j}
such that
a^{\prime}_{i,j}=1
. Now, they only need to know the list of
t
such that
a^{\prime}_{i,c_{t}}=1.
This significant reduces the number of bits users have to download from the central server. We will conduct the analysis on the number of bits which each user needs to download in the next section.

IV-D

Counting Step

Recall that
d_{i}
is the number of connections with nodes of smaller indexes of user
v_{i}
, which will be called low degree from here after. Let
p_{\text{p},i}:=\mathbb{P}\left(a_{j,h_{j}(k)}=1|a_{j,k}=1\right)
, i.e.

p_{\text{p},i}=\frac{s-1}{s}\cdot\frac{d_{i}-1}{ms-1}+\frac{1}{s},

and let
p^{\prime}_{\text{p},i}:=\mathbb{P}\left(a^{\prime}_{j,h_{j}(k)}=1|a_{j,k}=1\right)
, i.e.

p^{\prime}_{\text{p},i}=\frac{e^{\varepsilon^{\prime}}}{1+e^{\varepsilon^{\prime}}}\cdot p_{\text{p},i}+\frac{1-p_{\text{p},i}}{1+e^{\varepsilon^{\prime}}}.

Also, let
p_{\text{a},i}:=\mathbb{P}\left(a_{j,h_{j}(k)}=1|a_{j,k}=0\right)
, i.e.

p_{\text{a},i}=\frac{s-1}{s}\cdot\frac{d_{i}}{ms-1},

and let
p^{\prime}_{\text{a},i}:=\mathbb{P}\left(a^{\prime}_{j,h_{j}(k)}=1|a_{j,k}=0\right)
, i.e.

p^{\prime}_{\text{a},i}=\frac{e^{\varepsilon^{\prime}}}{1+e^{\varepsilon^{\prime}}}\cdot p_{\text{a},i}+\frac{1-p_{\text{a},i}}{1+e^{\varepsilon^{\prime}}}.

Define

\displaystyle\omega_{i}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p_{\text{p},i}^{\prime}-p_{\text{a},i}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1/\left(\frac{e^{\varepsilon^{\prime}}-1}{e^{\varepsilon^{\prime}}+1}(p_{\text{p},i}-p_{\text{a},i})\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1/\left(\frac{e^{\varepsilon^{\prime}}-1}{e^{\varepsilon^{\prime}}+1}\left(\frac{1}{s}-\frac{1}{ms-1}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{e^{\varepsilon^{\prime}}+1}{e^{\varepsilon^{\prime}}-1}\cdot\frac{ms^{2}-s}{ms-s-1}.

Also, define

\displaystyle\sigma_{i}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\frac{p_{\text{a},i}^{\prime}}{p_{\text{p},i}^{\prime}-p_{\text{a},i}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{e^{\varepsilon^{\prime}}-1}{e^{\varepsilon^{\prime}}+1}\cdot p_{\text{a},i}+\frac{1}{1+e^{\varepsilon^{\prime}}}\right)\cdot\frac{e^{\varepsilon^{\prime}}+1}{e^{\varepsilon^{\prime}}-1}\cdot\frac{ms^{2}-s}{ms-s-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle p_{\text{a},i}\cdot\frac{ms^{2}-s}{ms-s-1}+\frac{1}{e^{\varepsilon^{\prime}}-1}\cdot\frac{ms^{2}-s}{ms-s-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{s-1}{ms-s-1}d_{i}+\frac{1}{e^{\varepsilon^{\prime}}-1}\cdot\frac{ms^{2}-s}{ms-s-1}.

Recall that
\tilde{d}_{i}
is the low degree estimation of user
v_{i}
published in the first step of our framework. Define

\displaystyle\tilde{\sigma}_{i}:=\frac{s-1}{ms-s-1}\tilde{d}_{i}+\frac{1}{e^{\varepsilon^{\prime}}-1}\cdot\frac{ms^{2}-s}{ms-s-1}.

We have that
\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\sigma}_{i}]=\sigma_{i}
. For all
i,t
, denote

\tilde{a}_{i,c_{t}}:=\omega_{i}a_{i,c_{t}}^{\prime}-\tilde{\sigma}_{i}.

In the previous work, subgraphs are counted based on the value of
a_{j,k}^{\prime}\in\{0,1\}
obtained from the randomized mechanism. In GRR, we replace
a_{j,k}^{\prime}
with
\tilde{a}_{j,c_{t}}
when
t=h_{j}(k)
. Let
W_{i}:=\{(j,k):j<k<i\text{ and }\{v_{i},v_{j}\},\{v_{i},v_{k}\}\in E\}
.
The estimated number of triangles (denoted by
|\Delta_{i}|
was
\sum\limits_{(j,k)\in W_{i}}a^{\prime}_{j,k}
in the previous work. In this work, the estimated number of triangle corresponding to
v_{i}
, denoted by
|\tilde{\Delta}_{i}|
, is
\sum\limits_{(j,k)\in W_{i}}\tilde{a}_{j,h_{j}(k)}
.

Similar to the previous method, each user calculate the number of subgraphs corresponding to their nodes. They then report the number using the Laplacian mechanism or the smooth sensitivity mechanism with the privacy budget
\varepsilon_{2}
. The estimated number of subgraphs is then equal to the sums of those reported from users.

V
Theoretical Analysis of Our Mechanism

V-A

Privacy

The privacy of our mechanism, discussed in the next theorem, is a direct result of the theorem on amplification by sub-sampling
[
34
]
.

Theorem 2
.

Let
\mathcal{S}:\{0,1\}^{n}\rightarrow\{0,1\}^{m}
be our mechanism mapping from
[a_{i,1},\dots,a_{i,n}]^{t}
to
[a_{i,c_{1}}\dots,a_{i,c_{m}}]^{t}
in the grouping step, and
let
\mathcal{M}_{1}
be a
\varepsilon
-edge local differential private mechanism. Then,
\mathcal{M}_{1}\circ\mathcal{S}
is
\varepsilon_{\text{s}}
-edge local differential private with
\varepsilon_{\text{s}}=\ln(1+(e^{\varepsilon}-1)/s)
.

As the randomized response step has a privacy budget of
\varepsilon^{\prime}=\ln(1+s(e^{\epsilon_{1}}-1))
, the release of
a_{i,c_{1}},\dots,a_{i,c_{m}}
in the first two steps has a privacy budget of
\ln(1+(e^{\varepsilon^{\prime}}-1)/s)=\ln(1+(1+s(e^{\varepsilon_{1}}-1)-1)/s)=\varepsilon_{1}
.

Since users do not release any information during the grouping step, the privacy budget of our four-step mechanism is then equal to
\varepsilon_{0}+\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}
by the composition theorem.

V-B

Expected Value of
\tilde{a}_{i,c_{t}}

In the subsequent theorem, we discuss the expected value of
\tilde{a}_{i,c_{t}}
. The theorem reveals that when an estimate from the counting phase is a linear composition of
\tilde{a}_{i,c_{t}}
, it remains unbiased. The triangle count estimation in Section 4.3 is an example of these estimations.

Theorem 3
.

For all
j,k\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, the expected value of
\tilde{a}_{j,h_{j}(k)}
equals
a_{j,k}
.

Proof.

When
a_{j,k}=0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\tilde{a}_{j,h_{j}(k)}|a_{j,k}=0\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\omega_{j}-\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\sigma}_{j}])\cdot p^{\prime}_{\text{a},j}-\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\sigma}_{j}]\cdot(1-p^{\prime}_{\text{a},j})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\omega_{j}p_{\text{a},j}^{\prime}-\sigma_{j}=0.

When
a_{j,k}=1
, we have that:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\tilde{a}_{j,h_{j}(k)}|a_{j,k}=1\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\omega_{j}-\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\sigma}_{j}])\cdot p^{\prime}_{\text{p},j}-\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\sigma}_{j}]\cdot(1-p^{\prime}_{\text{p},j})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\omega_{j}p^{\prime}_{\text{p},j}-\sigma_{j}=1.

∎

V-C

Variance of
\tilde{a}_{i,c_{t}}

The previous section demonstrated that our mechanism gives no bias for certain specific publications. In this subsection, we show that the variance of our mechanism is also quite minimal.

We use the following lemmas in the analysis.

Lemma 1
.

For any
j,k
,
\mathbb{P}\left(a^{\prime}_{j,h_{j}(k)}=1\right)=\frac{a_{j,k}+\sigma_{j}}{\omega_{j}}.

Proof.

When
a_{j,k}=0
, we have that

\mathbb{P}\left(a^{\prime}_{j,h_{j}(k)}=1\right)=p^{\prime}_{\text{a},j}=\frac{\sigma_{j}}{\omega_{j}}.

When
a_{j,k}=1
, we have that

\mathbb{P}\left(a^{\prime}_{j,h_{j}(k)}=1\right)=p^{\prime}_{\text{p},j}=\frac{1+\sigma_{j}}{\omega_{j}}.

∎

Lemma 2
.

For all
i
such that
d_{i}<m
,
\omega_{i}=O\left(\frac{s}{e^{\varepsilon_{1}}-1}\right)
and
\sigma_{i}=O\left(\frac{1}{e^{\varepsilon_{1}}-1}\right)
.

Proof.

By
\varepsilon^{\prime}=\ln(1+s(e^{\varepsilon_{1}}-1))
, we have

\displaystyle e^{\varepsilon^{\prime}}-1

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle s(e^{\varepsilon_{1}}-1)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{e^{\varepsilon^{\prime}}-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{s(e^{\varepsilon_{1}}-1)}.

Hence,

\displaystyle\omega_{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2+s(e^{\varepsilon_{1}}-1)}{s(e^{\varepsilon_{1}}-1)}\cdot\frac{ms^{2}-s}{ms-s-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle O\left(\frac{s}{e^{\varepsilon_{1}}-1}\right).

By the assumption that
d_{i}<m
, we have

\displaystyle\sigma_{i}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{e^{\varepsilon^{\prime}}-1}\cdot\frac{ms^{2}-s}{ms-s-1}+1

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{s(e^{\varepsilon_{1}}-1)}\cdot\frac{ms^{2}-s}{ms-s-1}+1

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 1+\frac{m}{m-1}\cdot\frac{1}{e^{\varepsilon_{1}}-1}=O\left(\frac{1}{e^{\varepsilon_{1}}-1}\right).

∎

We consider the variance of the variable
\tilde{a}_{j,h_{j}(k)}
in the next theorem.

Theorem 4
.

For all
j
such that
d_{j}<m
,
the variance of
\tilde{a}_{j,h_{j}(k)}
is
O\left(\frac{s}{(e^{\varepsilon_{1}}-1)^{2}}+\frac{s^{2}}{n^{2}\varepsilon_{0}^{2}}\right)
.

Proof.

We first consider the variance of the variable
a^{\prime}_{j,h_{j}(k)}
. Because the variable is binary, by Lemma
1
, we obtain that

\displaystyle\text{Var}\left(a^{\prime}_{j,h_{j}(k)}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(a^{\prime}_{j,h_{j}(k)}=1\right)\cdot\left(1-\mathbb{P}\left(a^{\prime}_{j,h_{j}(k)}=1\right)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{a_{j,k}+\sigma_{j}}{\omega_{j}}\right)\cdot\left(1-\frac{a_{j,k}+\sigma_{j}}{\omega_{j}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1+\sigma_{j}}{\omega_{j}}.

Then, by Lemma
2
,

\displaystyle\text{Var}\left(\tilde{a}_{j,h_{j}(k)}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\text{Var}\left(\omega_{j}a^{\prime}_{j,h_{j}(k)}+\tilde{\sigma}_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\omega_{j}^{2}\text{Var}\left(a^{\prime}_{j,h_{j}(k)}\right)+\text{Var}\left(\tilde{\sigma}_{i}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\omega_{j}(1+\sigma_{j})+\left(\frac{s-1}{ms-s-1}\right)^{2}\frac{2}{\varepsilon_{0}^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle O\left(\frac{s}{(e^{\varepsilon_{1}}-1)^{2}}+\frac{s^{2}}{n^{2}\varepsilon_{0}^{2}}\right).

∎

In the following subsection, we will elaborate that
\tilde{a}_{j,h_{j}(k)}
can cut the communication cost by a factor of
s^{2}
in contrast to using the direct randomized response results
a^{\prime}_{j,k}
. Given that the variance of
a^{\prime}_{j,k}
is known to be
\Theta\left(\frac{1}{(e^{\epsilon_{1}}-1)^{2}}\right)
, the previous theorem suggests that, while we manage to decrease the communication cost by a factor of
s^{2}
, this is at the expense of amplifying the variance by a factor of
s
. In the upcoming section, we will demonstrate that, for a set communication cost, this mechanism yields a considerably reduced variance.

In situations where the goal is to sum up specific estimators, like in subgraph counting, a covariance emerges between these estimators. The size of this covariance is addressed in the subsequent theorem:

Theorem 5
.

Assume
d_{i}<m
for all
i
. The covariance of the estimators
\tilde{a}_{j,h_{j}(k)}
and
\tilde{a}_{j^{\prime},h_{j^{\prime}}(k^{\prime})}
is in
\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{s^{2}}{n(e^{\varepsilon_{1}}-1)^{2}}\right)
when
j=j^{\prime}
and
k,k^{\prime}<j
. Otherwise, the covariance of the two estimators is zero.

Proof.

When
j\neq j^{\prime}
, the two estimators are sampled from different sets of variables. They are thus independent.

When
j=j^{\prime}
, the two estimators are identical when
k
and
k^{\prime}
are in the same bin, i.e.
h_{j}(k)=h_{j}(k^{\prime})
. This event, that we will denote
C^{(j)}_{k,k^{\prime}}
, happens with a probability of
\frac{s-1}{ms-1}
. As a result, for
k,k^{\prime}<j
:

\displaystyle\text{Cov}\left(\tilde{a}_{j,h_{j}(k)},\tilde{a}_{j,h_{j}(k^{\prime})}\right)

\displaystyle=\

\displaystyle\omega_{j}^{2}\cdot\text{Cov}\left(a^{\prime}_{j,h_{j}(k)},a^{\prime}_{j,h_{j}(k^{\prime})}\right)

\displaystyle=\

\displaystyle\omega_{j}^{2}\cdot\left(\mathbb{E}\left[a^{\prime}_{j,h_{j}(k)}a^{\prime}_{j,h_{j}(k^{\prime})}\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[a^{\prime}_{j,h_{j}(k)}\right]\mathbb{E}\left[a^{\prime}_{j,h_{j}(k^{\prime})}\right]\right).

Using those notations, we will consider the values of
\mathbb{E}\left[a^{\prime}_{j,h_{j}(k)}a^{\prime}_{j,h_{j}(k^{\prime})}\mid C^{(j)}_{k,k^{\prime}}\right]
and
\mathbb{E}\left[a^{\prime}_{j,h_{j}(k)}a^{\prime}_{j,h_{j}(k^{\prime})}\mid\overline{C^{(j)}_{k,k^{\prime}}}\right]
.

Recall from the previous section that
\mathbb{P}\left(a_{j,h_{j}(k)}=1\right)=\frac{s-1}{s}\cdot\frac{d_{j}}{ms-1}+a_{j,k}\left[\frac{1}{s}-\frac{1}{ms-1}\right]
. We have that:

\displaystyle\frac{ms-1}{ms-2}\mathbb{P}\left(a_{j,h_{j}(k)}=1\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{s-1}{s}\cdot\frac{d_{i}}{ms-2}+a_{j,k}\left[\frac{ms-1}{s(ms-2)}-\frac{1}{ms-2}\right]

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{s-1}{s}\cdot\frac{d_{i}-a_{j,k}}{ms-2}+\frac{a_{j,k}}{s}.

For
\mathbb{E}\left[a^{\prime}_{j,h_{j}(k)}a^{\prime}_{j,h_{j}(k^{\prime})}\mid\overline{C^{(j)}_{k,k^{\prime}}}\right]
, let us denote
\mathbb{P}\left(a_{j,h_{j}(k)}=1\mid\overline{C^{(j)}_{k,k^{\prime}}}\right)
as
p_{k}
.
We obtain that:

\displaystyle p_{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{a_{j,k}}{s}+\frac{s-1}{s}\cdot\frac{d_{j}-a_{j,k}-a_{j,k^{\prime}}}{ms-2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{ms-1}{ms-2}\cdot\mathbb{P}\left(a_{j,h_{j}(k)}=1\right).

Using those notations, we have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(a^{\prime}_{j,h_{j}(k)}=1\text{ and }a^{\prime}_{j,h_{j}(k^{\prime})}=1\mid\overline{C^{(j)}_{k,k^{\prime}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\

\displaystyle\left(p_{k}\frac{e^{\varepsilon^{\prime}}-1}{e^{\varepsilon^{\prime}}+1}+\frac{1-p_{k}}{e^{\varepsilon^{\prime}}+1}\right)\left(p_{k^{\prime}}\frac{e^{\varepsilon^{\prime}}-1}{e^{\varepsilon^{\prime}}+1}+\frac{1-p_{k^{\prime}}}{e^{\varepsilon^{\prime}}+1}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\

\displaystyle\left(\frac{ms-1}{ms-2}\right)^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[a^{\prime}_{j,h_{j}(k)}\right]\mathbb{E}\left[a^{\prime}_{j,h_{j}(k^{\prime})}\right].

In consequence, as long as
s
is larger than 3, which will always be the case in practice, we have that:

\mathbb{P}\left(\overline{C^{(j)}_{k,k^{\prime}}}\right)\mathbb{E}\left[a^{\prime}_{j,h_{j}(k)}a^{\prime}_{j,h_{j}(k^{\prime})}\mid\overline{C^{(j)}_{k,k^{\prime}}}\right]\\
-\mathbb{E}\left[a^{\prime}_{j,h_{j}(k)}\right]\mathbb{E}\left[a^{\prime}_{j,h_{j}(k^{\prime})}\right]\leq 0

This simplifies the computation as we now have that:

\text{Cov}\left(\tilde{a}_{j,h_{j}(k)},\tilde{a}_{j,h_{j}(k^{\prime})}\right)\\
\leq\omega_{j}^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[a^{\prime}_{j,h_{j}(k)}a^{\prime}_{j,h_{j}(k^{\prime})}\mid C^{(j)}_{k,k^{\prime}}\right]\mathbb{P}\left(C^{(j)}_{k,k^{\prime}}\right)

Now, let us denote
\mathbb{P}\left(a_{j,h_{j}(k)}=1\mid C^{(j)}_{k,k^{\prime}}\right)
as
p^{\prime}_{k}
. We have that:

\displaystyle p_{k}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{a_{j,k}+a_{j,k^{\prime}}}{s}+\frac{s-2}{s}\cdot\frac{d_{j}-a_{j,k}-a_{j,k^{\prime}}}{ms-2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(a_{j,h_{j}(k)}=1\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(a_{j,h_{j}(k^{\prime})}=1\right).

Using the same argument as in the previous paragraph, we obtain that:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[a^{\prime}_{j,h_{j}(k)}a^{\prime}_{j,h_{j}(k^{\prime})}\mid C^{(j)}_{k,k^{\prime}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[a^{\prime}_{j,h_{j}(k)}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[a^{\prime}_{j,h_{j}(k^{\prime})}\right].

By Lemma
1
and the fact that
\mathbb{P}\left(C_{k,k^{\prime}}^{(j)}\right)=\frac{s-1}{ms-1}
, we get:

\displaystyle\text{Cov}\left(\tilde{a}_{j,h_{j}(k)},\tilde{a}_{j,h_{j}(k^{\prime})}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\frac{s-1}{ms-1}\omega_{j}(1+\sigma_{j})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle O\left(\frac{s^{2}}{n(e^{\varepsilon_{1}}-1)^{2}}\right).

∎

It can be noticed that, when
s\ll n
, the covariance in the previous theorem is much smaller than the variance calculated in Theorem
4
. We thus believe that the variance dominates almost all the loss we have found in the experiment.

V-D

Communication Cost

The final aspect we will examine regarding the mechanism’s efficiency is the communication needed for graph aggregation and distribution to each user. We will particularly look at two metrics: the “download cost” (the average number of bits the server transmits to each user) and the “upload cost” (the average number of bits each user sends to the server). Our analysis will offer approximate values under the assumption that the graph is sparse, meaning the number of edges (
|E|
) is much less than the square of the number of vertices (
|V|^{2}
).

From our discussion in the previous section, it is evident that the majority of the upload cost arises from the randomized response step, while the download cost primarily stems from the counting step. Therefore, in this analysis, we will disregard the costs associated with the other steps.

Theorem 6
.

The upload cost of user
v_{i}
in our mechanism is
\mathcal{O}\left(\log{\frac{n}{s}}\left(\frac{d_{i}}{s}+\frac{n}{s^{2}(e^{\varepsilon_{1}}-1)}\right)\right)
.

Proof.

Recall that, in the randomized response step, each user
v_{i}
submit the vector
[a^{\prime}_{i,c_{1}},\dots,a^{\prime}_{i,c_{m}}]\in\{0,1\}^{m}
for
m\approx n/s
. To save the communication cost, the user can send the index set
\{c_{t}:a^{\prime}_{i,c_{t}}=1\}\subseteq\{1,\dots,m\}
. If
\ell
elements of the vector are one, the cost is
\ell\cdot\log m\approx\ell\cdot\log n/s
.

Now, we discuss the expected value of
\ell
. As each
a^{\prime}_{i,j}=1
is sampled in the vector with probability
1/s
and
e^{\varepsilon^{\prime}}-1=s(e^{\varepsilon_{1}}-1)
, we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\ell]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{e^{\varepsilon^{\prime}}}{1+e^{\varepsilon^{\prime}}}d_{i}+\frac{1}{1+e^{\varepsilon^{\prime}}}(n-d_{i})\right)/s

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{d_{i}}{s}+\frac{1}{s^{2}(e^{\varepsilon_{1}}-1)}(n-d_{i})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle O\left(\frac{d_{i}}{s}+\frac{n}{s^{2}(e^{\varepsilon_{1}}-1)}\right).

∎

Next, we consider the download cost for our mechanism.

Corollary 1
.

The download cost for all users is
\mathcal{O}\left(\log{\frac{n}{s}}\left(\frac{|E|}{s}+\frac{n}{s^{2}(e^{\varepsilon_{1}}-1)}\right)\right)
.

Proof.

As users have to download information from all others, the download cost is then:

\displaystyle\sum_{i}\mathcal{O}\left(\log{\frac{n}{s}}\left(\frac{d_{i}}{s}+\frac{n}{s^{2}(e^{\varepsilon_{1}}-1)}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}\left(\log{\frac{n}{s}}\left(\frac{|E|}{s}+\frac{n^{2}}{s^{2}(e^{\varepsilon_{1}}-1)}\right)\right).

∎

Application: Triangle Counting

While our GRR mechanism is adaptable for counting various subgraphs, the prevalent focus in existing literature, including
[
20
,
22
,
24
]
, has been on triangle counting under local differential privacy. Therefore, in this section, we focus on triangle counting as well, enabling a direct comparison with these studies.

VI-A

Sensitivity and Loss of Our Mechanism

Recall from the previous section that, in the counting step of our mechanism, we ask each user
v_{i}
to estimate
|\Delta_{i}|
when
\Delta_{i}=\{(v_{i},v_{j},v_{k}):i<j<k,\{v_{i},v_{j}\},\{v_{i},v_{k}\},\{v_{j},v_{k}\}\in E\}
. The number of triangles in the graph
G
, denoted by
|\Delta_{G}|
is then the sum of all
|\Delta_{i}|
. The estimation of
|\Delta_{i}|
in our mechanism is
\tilde{\Delta}_{i}:=\sum\limits_{j,k:\{i,j\},\{i,k\}\in E}\tilde{a}_{j,h_{j}(k)}
added with the Laplacian noise when the variable
\tilde{a}_{j,h_{j}(k)}
is defined in the previous section.

The magnitude of the Laplacian noise, dictated by the sensitivity of
\tilde{\Delta}_{i}
, is capped at
d_{i}\cdot\omega_{i}
, where
d_{i}
is the degree of node
v_{i}
and
\omega_{i}
is the unbiased factor outlined in the previous section. If the degree
d_{i}
is publicly known, we can directly set the sensitivity to
d_{i}\cdot\omega_{i}
. In scenarios where
d_{i}
is not public, the sensitivity must be adjusted to the maximum degree,
d_{\text{max}}=\max_{i}d_{i}
. This maximum degree can be estimated as
\tilde{d}_{\text{max}}=C+\max_{i}\tilde{d}_{i}
, with
C
being a positive integer and
\tilde{d}_{i}
representing the degree announced in the degree sharing step. It is important to note, however, that this publication could potentially underestimate the actual maximum degree, leading to some bias in our publication. If a node
i
has a degree
d_{i}
that exceeds
\tilde{d}_{\text{max}}
, it is required to eliminate edges from its local data.

From now, let us suppose that
d_{i}
is publicly known and
Lap_{i}
is a noise sampled from the Laplacian distribution with parameter
\omega_{i}\cdot d_{i}/\varepsilon_{2}
. Consequently, the estimation of user
v_{i}
is
\tilde{\Delta}_{i}+Lap_{i}
.
In the preceding section, we established that
\tilde{\Delta}_{i}+Lap_{i}
serves as an unbiased estimate of
|\Delta_{i}|
. The primary source of estimation loss arises from the variance of
\sum_{i}(\tilde{\Delta}_{i}+Lap_{i})
, which we consider in the subsequent theorem.

Theorem 7
.

Let
S_{2}
,
C_{4}
, and
W_{4}
are the number of two-star, four-cycle, and walks of length four in the graph
G
.

We believe that the variance detailed in Theorem

Remember that through the GRR mechanism, the server acquires the set

Smooth Sensitivity for Triangle Counting

As mentioned in the preceding subsection, the clipping method can be employed to diminish the magnitude of Laplacian noise introduced in the final stage of our mechanism. However, this method of clipping incurs a certain degree of bias in our estimator. To remove this bias, we suggest adopting the smooth sensitivity approach, which is introduced in Section
II

We now calculate the sensitivity at distance
k

6

5

When

is the adjacent vector of user

is the number of ones in the vector, the number of ones in the vector

VII
Conclusion

Our work introduces a private graph publishing mechanism that provides unbiased estimations of all graph edges while maintaining low communication costs. The effectiveness of this method stems from integrating two key components: the application of universal hashing functions to achieve uniform edge partitions and the amplification of budget efficiency through sampling within each group. This synergy leads to a significant reduction in download costs, quantifiable as a factor of
\mathcal{O}(s^{3})
, where
s
represents the size of the groups.

We then demonstrated its utility in accurately estimating the number of triangles in a graph. Furthermore, we elaborated on the application of smooth sensitivity to these problems, ensuring that the resulting estimations remain unbiased. Our experiments have demonstrated that these methods offer substantial improvements in precision over existing state-of-the-art approaches for the counting.

Our future work is focused on enhancing the scalability of subgraph counting under local differential privacy. We have observed that the computation time required for each user in all mechanisms proposed to date is considerable. This computation time often increases for mechanisms involving multiple steps. Our aim is to explore how sampling methods can not only improve precision but also significantly boost the scalability of the counting process.
