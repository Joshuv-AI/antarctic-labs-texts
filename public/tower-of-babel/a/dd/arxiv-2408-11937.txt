Abstract
. This paper presents a first-order distributed algorithm for solving a convex semi-infinite program (SIP) over a time-varying network. In this setting, the objective function associated with the optimization problem is a summation of a set of functions, each held by one node in a network. The semi-infinite constraint, on the other hand, is known to all agents. The nodes collectively aim to solve the problem using local data about the objective and limited communication capabilities depending on the network topology. Our algorithm is built on three key ingredients: consensus step, gradient descent in the local objective, and local gradient descent iterations in the constraint at a node when the estimate violates the semi-infinite constraint. The algorithm is constructed, and its parameters are prescribed in such a way that the iterates held by each agent provably converge to an optimizer. That is, as the algorithm progresses, the estimates achieve consensus, and the constraint violation and the error in the optimal value are bounded above by vanishing terms. Simulation examples illustrate our results.



1.
Introduction

A semi-infinite program (SIP) is a mathematical optimization problem featuring an objective function and a feasible set described by infinitely many inequalities. The presence of an infinite number of constraints makes the task of numerically solving SIPs challenging. These problems are encountered in a variety of fields, see
[
1
]
for a detailed discussion. Applications include, for example, robotics and control
[
2
]
, finance
[
3
]
, and other areas
[
4
,
5
]
. These problems are particularly challenging to solve due to the presence of possibly uncountable number of constraints. Several numerical methods are proposed to solve SIPs exactly or approximately, see surveys
[
6
,
7
,
8
]
for detailed expositions. A majority of the work on numerical solutions to SIPs focuses on centralized approaches. However, advancements in communication technologies as well as increased emphasis on large-scale network-centric systems have facilitated the need for distributed algorithms for solving optimization problems
[
9
]
. Naturally, applications exist for distributed frameworks where there are an infinite number of constraints. For example, robust distributed control problems
[
10
,
11
]
can be reformulated as distributed SIPs. Similarly, the authors in
[
12
]
study the distributionally robust optimization over a network as a distributed SIP. In such settings, the difficulties associated with distributed computing and the infinite number of constraints compound each other, thus demanding specialized algorithms. Our objective in this article is to design a distributed algorithm for solving semi-infinite programs (SIPs) with convex data, where the objective function is a sum of local functions, each privately known to its corresponding agent in a network, while the semi-infinite constraint is shared globally among all agents.

1.1.
Related works

Distributed SIPs have appeared in few works and existing methods can be mainly categorized as cutting-plane methods, and scenario-based methods. In this article, we introduce a third category based on first-order methods. Below we briefly review representative works in each category and highlight the distinctions relative to our approach.

Cutting-plane methods construct outer approximations of the feasible set of the problem; this set may be defined by infinitely many constraints. A distributed cutting-plane approach was developed in
[
13
]
for optimization problems with a common linear objective and node-specific semi-infinite constraints. A combination of cutting-plane ideas and distributed ADMM was proposed in
[
12
]
for a related problem structure. In both cases, convergence guarantees are asymptotic with no known explicit convergence rates. Moreover, the number of constraints maintained at each agent can grow with iterations, leading to significant computational and memory burdens. In contrast, our approach offers explicit convergence rate guarantees for both objective suboptimality and feasibility violation while requiring each agent to store only local data and a current estimate. A cutting-plane-based scheme with convergence rate guarantees comparable to our method was presented in
[
14
]
, along with finite-time termination using zeroth-order stopping conditions. Unlike
[
14
]
, which requires potentially expensive projection operations onto constraint sets, our method circumvents this challenge by relying on first-order information of the constraint function to maintain feasibility.

Scenario-based
[
1
]
methods, such as those explored in
[
15
,
16
,
17
]
, rely on random sampling from the constraint index set to create a finitely described outer approximation of the feasible set. Convergence guarantees for these methods are probabilistic and typically asymptotic, and the absence of convergence rates at the same level of granularity limits their applicability. Furthermore, achieving feasibility with high-probability requires a large number of samples especially in high dimensions, resulting in substantial memory and computation costs. Finally,
[
18
]
developed a finite-time convergence scheme for distributed SIPs based on a distributed upper and lower bounding procedure. While this method achieves finite-time convergence guarantees, but it requires solving two finitely constrained distributed optimization problems at each iteration, which is challenging.

In summary, compared to existing approaches, our method provides explicit convergence guarantees, maintains low memory and computational overhead, and utilizes only first-order information without requiring projection based on the constraint function. We note that random projection methods, introduced in
[
19
,
20
]
, could be adapted to solve SIPs. However, such an adaptation would require computing expensive projections at each iteration and would only provide asymptotic convergence, without any guarantees on the rate of convergence for either optimality or feasibility.

1.2.
Contributions

Our approach combines the standard first-order distributed optimization framework
[
21
]
with the alternating gradient descent schemes for constrained optimization problems
[
22
,
23
]
, where the latter set of algorithms employ gradient descent either for the objective or the constraint function depending on the feasibility or lack of it, respectively, of the iterate. Our algorithm is built on three blocks: (a) a consensus step to bring iterates close to each other; (b) a gradient step in the objective; and (c) several gradient steps in the constraint when faced with infeasibility of the iterates. Our algorithm overcomes the challenge of projection onto a set defined by an infinite number of constraints in this way. We show that the iterates held by each node in the algorithm get close to each other as iterations progress and furthermore, two properties hold. First, the feasibility violation of the estimate at the
K
-th iteration is of the order
\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{K}}}}\right)
, and second, the error in the objective value is also of the order
\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{K}}}}\right)
, and thus, decays asymptotically to zero as
K\to\infty
. A numerical experiment is provided to illustrate the performance of this framework and to compare it with two standard methods from the literature, namely, the distributed cutting-surface ADMM
[
12
]
and the distributed scenario approach
[
16
]
.

An informal description of Algorithm
1

Each node holds and updates an estimate of an optimizer of (
1
), this process is elaborated in detail below:

•

The algorithm is written from the perspective of an arbitrary node
i\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[V}}\right]
, but requires all nodes to agree a priori on the number of iterations

, the feasibility tolerances
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\eta_{k}}}\right\}_{k\geqslant 1}

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{t_{k}}}\right\}_{k\geqslant 1}

•

The estimate of the optimizer at node

and the nodes exchange their estimates with their neighbors in Step 2.

•

In Step 3, a cumulative estimate

of the network as weighting factors.

•

Projected subgradient descent is performed at the cumulative estimate in Step 4, we term this as the outer descent.

•

Since the outer descent step is not guaranteed to result in a feasible estimate, Steps 6 to 13 are designed to ensure feasibility through a sequence of gradient steps on the constraint function.

•

In Step 6, the inner loop counter

are copied to

•

A closed Euclidean ball

is defined around the estimate

in Step 7, and is used as the feasible set for the projected gradient descent in the inner loop.

•

Step 8 checks the feasibility of the current estimate by verifying whether
f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\bar{z}_{i}^{k,\ell},\bar{u}_{i}^{k,\ell}}}\right)>\eta_{k}
. If the estimate is feasible, the computations at the node loop to Step 2; otherwise, local computations based on gradient descent are performed on the constraint function, using the gradient

•

The stepsize for these computations follows the Polyak stepsize rule

\bar{t}^{k,\ell}_{i}\leftarrow\frac{f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\bar{z}_{i}^{k,\ell},\bar{u}_{i}^{k,\ell}}}\right)}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert\nabla_{x}f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\bar{z}_{i}^{k,\ell},\bar{u}_{i}^{k,\ell}}}\right)}}\right\rVert^{2}}

•

The inner descent aims to obtain a feasible estimate at the node. For every outer iteration

3.2.
Convergence analysis of Algorithm
1

In this section, we present the convergence guarantees of the main algorithm. We derive an upper bound on the suboptimality gap, measured by the difference between the objective value corresponding to the returned estimate at each node and the global optimal value. We also establish guarantees on the feasibility of these estimates. To build toward the main result stated in Theorem
12

8

Due to their relevance in subsequent analysis, we introduce the following notations:
\theta_{ik}
represents the number of inner iterations required by the node

at the

Note that Assumption

such that
\min_{x\in\mathbb{L}_{0}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(f}}\right)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert\nabla_{x}f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,u^{*}(x)}}\right)}}\right\rVert\geqslant G_{0}
, for all
u^{*}(x)\in\mathbb{U}^{*}(x):=\argmax_{u\in\mathbb{U}}f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,u}}\right)
.

Proof.

Denote
\bar{f}(x):=\max_{u\in\mathbb{U}}f(x,u)
and let
\bar{x}\in\mathbb{F}
be the point satisfying
\bar{f}(\bar{x})<0
. By Danskin’s theorem
[
25
, Proposition B.25]
, we deduce that
\bar{f}
is convex and

(2)

\displaystyle\partial\bar{f}(x)=\mathrm{conv}\{\nabla_{x}f(x,u^{*}(x))\,:\,u^{*}(x)\in\mathbb{U}^{*}(x)\}

for any
x\in\mathbb{X}
. Now pick any point
y\in\mathbb{L}_{0}(f)
and any
\zeta_{y}\in\partial\bar{f}(y)
. By convexity and the definition of the subgradient, we get
\bar{f}(y)+\zeta_{y}^{\top}(\bar{x}-y)\leqslant\bar{f}(\bar{x})<0
. This implies that
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert\zeta_{y}}}\right\rVert\not=0
and since
\zeta_{y}
was arbitrarily picked, we conclude from (
2
) that for any
y\in\mathbb{L}_{0}(f)
, we have

(3)

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert\nabla_{x}f(y,u^{*}(y))}}\right\rVert\not=0,\text{ for all }u^{*}(y)\in\mathbb{U}^{*}(y).

Having established the above fact, we proceed to show the lower bound on this gradient over the set
\mathbb{L}_{0}(f)
. Firstly, note that
\mathbb{L}_{0}(f)
is compact as
\mathbb{L}_{0}(f)
is closed and is a subset of a compact set
\mathbb{X}
. Further, from
[
26
, Proposition 4.4]
, the map
x\mapsto\mathbb{U}^{*}(x)
is upper semicontinuous. This means that for any sequence
\{(x_{k},u_{k})\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{U}
with
u_{k}\in\mathbb{U}^{*}(x_{k})
for all
k
, if
x_{k}\to x^{\prime}
, then all accumulation points of
\{u_{k}\}
belong to
\mathbb{U}^{*}(x^{\prime})
. We will use these properties to infer that the map
y\mapsto\kappa(y):=\min_{u^{*}(y)\in\mathbb{U}^{*}(y)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert\nabla_{x}f(y,u^{*}(y))}}\right\rVert
is lower semicontinuous. To see the reasoning, note that since
f
is continuously differentiable, the value
\kappa(y)
is attained at all
y
. Thus, for any sequence
\{y_{k}\}
, there exists a sequence
\{u_{k}\in\mathbb{U}^{*}(y_{k})\}
such that
\kappa(y_{k})=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert\nabla_{x}f(y_{k},u_{k})}}\right\rVert
for all
k
.
Let
y_{k}\to y^{\prime}
and evaluating the limit we get
\lim_{k\to\infty}\kappa(y_{k})=\lim_{k\to\infty}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert\nabla_{x}f(y_{k},u_{k})}}\right\rVert=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert\nabla_{x}f(y^{\prime},u^{\prime})}}\right\rVert
for some
u^{\prime}\in\mathbb{U}^{*}(y^{\prime})
. The existence of such a
u^{\prime}
is possible since the sequence belongs to a compact set. Thus, we get
\lim_{k\to\infty}\kappa(y_{k})\geqslant\kappa(y^{\prime})
showing lower semicontinuity of
\kappa
. Finally using this property, the fact (
3
), and compactness of
\mathbb{L}_{0}(f)
, we conclude the result.
∎

We henceforth define
G_{0}
as the lower bound established in the above result.
The following lemma establishes an upper bound on the distance between the current weighted average
y^{k}_{i}
and the next estimate

at a node
i\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[V}}\right]

Lemma 6
.

(Maximum distance between the weighted average and the next estimate at a node):

At any node
i\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[V}}\right]
, and the
k^{\text{th}}
outer iteration of Algorithm
1
we have

(4)

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert y^{k}_{i}-z^{k}_{i}}}\right\rVert\leqslant t_{k}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert\zeta_{i,y^{k}_{i}}}}\right\rVert_{*}.

Proof.

The projected subgradient descent iteration at Step 4 of Algorithm
1
can be equivalently written as,

z^{k}_{i}=\argmin_{x\in\mathbb{X}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{F_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(y^{k}_{i}}}\right)+\langle\zeta_{i,y^{k}_{i}},x-y^{k}_{i}\rangle+\frac{1}{2t_{k}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert x-y^{k}_{i}}}\right\rVert^{2}}}\right\}.

Then, by the first-order optimality condition we have,

0\in t_{k}\zeta_{i,y^{k}_{i}}+z^{k}_{i}-y^{k}_{i}+\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{X}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(z^{k}_{i}}}\right),

where
\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{X}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(z^{k}_{i}}}\right)

\mathbb{X}
at

implying,

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert y^{k}_{i}-z^{k}_{i}}}\right\rVert\leqslant t_{k}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert\zeta_{i,y^{k}_{i}}}}\right\rVert_{*}.

As stated earlier, the role of the inner descent is to provide a feasible estimate when a node encounters infeasibility. We next proceed to establish that the inner descent loop terminates after a finite number of iterations. To this end, we first show that the closed ball used to define the inner descent step has a nonempty intersection with the feasible set of problem (
1
).

Lemma 7
.

(Significance of the ball around an infeasible estimate):

For an arbitrary iteration

of Algorithm
1

\mathbb{B}^{k}_{i}\cap\mathbb{F}\not=\emptyset

\mathbb{B}^{k}_{i}

Proof.

Let
\bar{f}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right):=\max_{u{}\in\mathbb{U}}f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,u}}\right)

\mathbb{U}
is compact and

is convex and continuously differentiable for a fixed second variable, by Danskin’s theorem
[
25

we deduce that
\bar{f}
is convex and subdifferentiable. Using this fact, we deduce that
\mathbb{F}
is closed and convex. Consider
\hat{y}^{k}_{i}:=P_{\mathbb{F}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(y^{k}_{i}}}\right)

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert c}}\right\rVert\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert y^{k}_{i}-\hat{y}^{k}_{i}}}\right\rVert\leqslant\bar{f}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(y^{k}_{i}}}\right)-\bar{f}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\hat{y}^{k}_{i}}}\right)

\mathbb{F}
, and the lower bound on

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert y^{k}_{i}-\hat{y}^{k}_{i}}}\right\rVert\leqslant\frac{\eta_{k}}{G_{0}}

and Lemma

we have
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert y^{k}_{i}-z^{k}_{i}}}\right\rVert\leqslant t_{k}F_{\mathbb{X}}

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert\hat{y}^{k}_{i}-z^{k}_{i}}}\right\rVert\leqslant\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert\hat{y}^{k}_{i}-y^{k}_{i}}}\right\rVert+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert y^{k}_{i}-z^{k}_{i}}}\right\rVert\leqslant t_{k}F_{\mathbb{X}}+\frac{\eta_{k}}{G_{0}}

centered at

.
∎

The following proposition establishes an upper bound on the number of inner iterations required at each node.

\displaystyle=2\sum_{k\in\mathbb{K}}t_{k}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2t_{k}F_{\mathbb{X}}+\frac{\eta_{k}}{G_{0}}}}\right)

\displaystyle=2R\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2RF_{\mathbb{X}}+\frac{1}{G_{0}}}}\right)\sum_{k\in\mathbb{K}}\frac{1}{k}

\displaystyle\leqslant 4R\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2RF_{\mathbb{X}}+\frac{1}{G_{0}}}}\right)\ln{2},

where, the bound follow via substitution and upper bounding the sum as done previously. Using these we establish,

\displaystyle C

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{VR\Gamma}{1-\gamma}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2RF_{\mathbb{X}}+\frac{1}{G_{0}}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{1-\gamma}+2\ln{2}}}\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt+\Gamma\frac{R}{1-\gamma}\sum_{j\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[V}}\right]}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert x^{1}_{j}}}\right\rVert+4R\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2RF_{\mathbb{X}}+\frac{1}{G_{0}}}}\right)\ln{2}

Also, note that
D=G/2
, therefore

\displaystyle D\leqslant 2\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2R^{2}F_{\mathbb{X}}+\frac{R}{G_{0}}}}\right)\ln{2}

By using the bounds for the terms A, B, C and D we get

\displaystyle F\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\bar{x}^{K}_{i}}}\right)-F\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x^{*}}}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{V}{\sum_{k\in\mathbb{K}}t_{k}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{R}{2}+H_{\mathbb{X}}^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(R^{2}+\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\eta_{k}+t_{k}F_{\mathbb{X}}G_{0}}}\right)^{2}}{G_{0}^{4}}}}\right)\ln{2}}}\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt+\frac{2F_{\mathbb{X}}V}{\sum_{k\in\mathbb{K}}t_{k}}\frac{VR\Gamma}{1-\gamma}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2RF_{\mathbb{X}}+\frac{1}{G_{0}}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{1-\gamma}+2\ln{2}}}\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt+\frac{2F_{\mathbb{X}}V}{\sum_{k\in\mathbb{K}}t_{k}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Gamma\frac{R}{1-\gamma}\sum_{j\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[V}}\right]}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert x^{1}_{j}}}\right\rVert+4R\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2RF_{\mathbb{X}}+\frac{1}{G_{0}}}}\right)\ln{2}}}\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt+\frac{2F_{\mathbb{X}}V}{\sum_{k\in\mathbb{K}}t_{k}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2R^{2}F_{\mathbb{X}}+\frac{R}{G_{0}}}}\right)\ln{2}

Now by substituting with the expression for
t_{k}
and using the lower bound
\sum_{k\in\mathbb{K}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}}\geqslant(2-\sqrt{2})\sqrt{K}
in the above inequality and collecting all the constant term together as
\overline{C}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma,\Gamma,F_{\mathbb{X}},G_{0},G_{\mathbb{X}},H_{\mathbb{X}},R,V,\sum_{j\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[V}}\right]}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert x^{1}_{j}}}\right\rVert}}\right)
, we get the required bound.

(iii)

For the constraint function we have,

\displaystyle\max_{u\in\mathbb{U}}f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\bar{x}^{K}_{i},u}}\right)

\displaystyle=\max_{u\in\mathbb{U}}f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{\sum_{k\in\mathbb{K}}t_{k}}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{K}}t_{k}x^{k+1}_{i},u}}\right)

\displaystyle\overset{(p)}{\leqslant}\max_{u\in\mathbb{U}}\frac{1}{\sum_{k\in\mathbb{K}}t_{k}}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{K}}t_{k}f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x^{k+1}_{i},u}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sum_{k\in\mathbb{K}}t_{k}}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{K}}t_{k}\max_{u\in\mathbb{U}}f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x^{k+1}_{i},u}}\right)

\displaystyle\overset{(q)}{\leqslant}\frac{1}{\sum_{k\in\mathbb{K}}t_{k}}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{K}}t_{k}\eta_{k+1}

where (p) is due to the convexity of
f
in the first variable and (q) follows from the algorithm design which ensures that
\sup_{u\in\mathbb{U}}f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x^{k+1}_{i},u}}\right)\leqslant\eta_{k+1}
. Now by substituting
t_{k}=\frac{R}{\sqrt{k}}
and
\eta_{k}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}}
, we have

\displaystyle\sup_{u\in\mathbb{U}}f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\bar{x}^{K}_{i},u}}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{\sum_{k\in\mathbb{K}}t_{k}}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{K}}\frac{R}{\sqrt{k}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{k+1}}\leqslant\frac{R}{\sum_{k\in\mathbb{K}}t_{k}}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{K}}\frac{1}{k},

now, by using the inequalities:
\sum_{k\in\mathbb{K}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}}\geqslant(2-\sqrt{2})\sqrt{K}
and
\sum_{k\in\mathbb{K}}\frac{1}{k}\leqslant 2\ln{2}
, we get the required bound.

∎

The above results establish both optimality and feasibility guarantees for Algorithm
1
, and confirm that the algorithm terminates (i.e., the inner descent terminates finitely). Specifically, the suboptimality is of the order
\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{K}}}}\right)
and so vanishes asymptotically with
K
. Similarly, the constraint violation also decreases with the number of outer iterations, with the worst-case infeasibility being of the same order,
\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{K}}}}\right)
. Note that, we have not established convergence to an optimizer in Theorem
12
.

4.2.
Comparison results

We compared Algorithm
1
against two other distributed methods that can handle semi-infinite constraints: the Distributed Cutting-Surface ADMM (DCSA) method
[
12
]
, and the Distributed Scenario-based Algorithm (DSA)
[
16
]
. The comparison is conducted in the context of problem (
15
), where the network topology is the cycle digraph. In DCSA, each agent constructs progressively tighter outer approximations of the semi-infinite constraint by incrementally adding more constraints (termed cuts) sampled from the set
\mathbb{U}
, as the iterations progress. These approximations are then used in combination with a distributed ADMM method to solve the optimization problem (
1
). Increasing the number of cuts improves the feasibility and optimality guarantee of the obtained solution. On the other hand, DSC relies on a scenario-based approximation, where the constraints are sampled from
\mathbb{U}
before the iterations start and remain fixed throughout the execution of the distribution algorithm. The distributed algorithm can be any method designed to solve a deterministic distributed optimization problem. Naturally, the feasibility guarantee in this case depends on the number of initial samples and is therefore probabilistic in nature. The comparison provided here with our method focuses on numerical performance in terms of feasibility, optimality, and computational efficiency.

Fig.
2
shows the optimality and feasibility comparison. We plot the objective and constraint function values for the average of estimates
\widehat{x}^{k}
defined in (
10
) for Algorithm
1
, DCSA, and three instances of DSA with 50, 500, and 5000 scenarios distributed evenly among the ten nodes. While the DSA iterates initially converge faster toward optimality, DCSA and Algorithm
1
begin to show comparable performance at around the
100^{\text{th}}
iteration. As the iterations progress, both our method and DCSA outperform DSA and continue to approach the optimal value. This is consistent with the fact that both methods provably reach the optimal value asymptotically. The feasibility plot further supports this fact. Since the constraint samples in DSA are fixed from the start, constraint violations (if any) do not vanish as iterations progress; they only improve with the number of initially drawn samples. In contrast, both Algorithm
1
and DCSA reduce the constraint violation as iterations progress, achieving comparable levels of feasibility.

Fig.
3
compares the per-iteration computation time across all methods. Rather than plotting the raw per-iteration times, which exhibit significant variability, we report the cumulative average per-iteration time to obtain smoother and more interpretable plots. Let
\overline{T}_{i}^{k}
be the time taken by node
i
during the
k^{\text{th}}
iteration of an approach. Then the per-iteration time is the average time taken by all nodes at the
k^{\text{th}}
iteration, that is,
\overline{T}_{\mathrm{avg}}^{k}:=V^{-1}\sum_{i\in[V]}\overline{T}_{i}^{k}
. The cumulative average per-iteration time at
k^{\text{th}}
iteration is computed as
\overline{T}^{k}:=k^{-1}\sum_{s=1}^{k}\overline{T}_{\mathrm{avg}}^{k}
.

This running average provides a stable representation of computational effort per iteration across the distributed system. As shown in Fig.
3
, the per-iteration computation time for DCSA increases over the course of execution. This is due to the cumulative growth in the number of cutting planes, which leads to progressively larger subproblems. In contrast, the DSA methods exhibit nearly constant per-iteration times, as their computational complexity is determined by the fixed number of scenarios specified in advance. Algorithm
1
maintains a nearly constant and significantly lower per-iteration computation time throughout. This efficiency arises from the first-order nature of the method and the finite termination of its inner loop. Summarizing the above outlined results, we conclude that Algorithm
1
combines both features: asymptotic optimality as well as low per-iteration computation cost.

4.3.
Robust meta control design

Consider the following parameter dependent discrete-time linear system:

(16)

\mathbbm{x}^{t+1}=A(\mathbbm{w})\mathbbm{x}^{t}+B\mathbbm{u}^{t},\;t\in\{0\}\cup[99],

where, for a time-step
t
,
\mathbbm{x}^{t}:=(\mathbbm{x}_{1}^{t}\;\mathbbm{x}_{2}^{t})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
is the state,
\mathbbm{u}^{t}\in\mathbb{R}
is the control input, and
\mathbbm{w}\in\mathbb{W}:=[10,20]
is an uncertain parameter, drawn i.i.d under uniform distribution on the set
\mathbb{W}
. The system matrices are of the form:

A(\mathbbm{w})=\begin{bmatrix}1&0.01\\
-0.01\mathbbm{w}&0.99\end{bmatrix},\quad B=\begin{bmatrix}0.0\\
0.01\end{bmatrix}.

We consider a scenario where four realizations of the above system are present at four different nodes of a network in cycle configuration. These realization are:

\displaystyle A_{1}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}1&0.01\\
-0.1&0.99\end{bmatrix},

\displaystyle\hskip-71.13188ptA_{2}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0.01\\
-0.12&0.99\end{bmatrix},

\displaystyle A_{3}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}1&0.01\\
-0.15&0.99\end{bmatrix},

\displaystyle\hskip-71.13188ptA_{4}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0.01\\
-0.2&0.99\end{bmatrix}.

Our objective is to determine a control sequence
\{\mathbbm{u}^{t}\}_{t=0}^{99}
that (a) minimizes the cumulative cost associated with these four realizations and (b) satisfies a terminal constraint when this control sequence is applied to any possible realization of the parameter dependent system (
16
). This formulation is motivated from meta learning frameworks
[
31
,
32
]
for linear systems where the objective is to find control inputs that can be easily adapted to unseen realizations of an uncertain system. This objective is encoded in the control design as a terminal state constraint that needs to be satisfied for all possible realizations of the parametric uncertainty. This meta control design may be used to safely initialize a system with unknown dynamics and collect data before estimating the same.

Formally, the agents must agree on a common control sequence
\{\mathbbm{u}^{t}\}_{t=0}^{99}
that minimizes the sum of their individual cost functions, given a prespecified common initial state
\mathbbm{x}^{0}=\bar{\mathbbm{x}}
, while ensuring that the following semi-infinite constraint is satisfied by the terminal state:

(17)

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert A(\mathbbm{w})^{100}\mathbbm{x}^{0}+\sum_{t=0}^{99}A(\mathbbm{w})^{99-t}B\mathbbm{u}_{i}^{t}}}\right\rVert^{2}\leqslant 1.5,\text{for all }\mathbbm{w}\in\mathbb{W}.

The above description is captured in the following robust meta control problem:

\displaystyle\min_{(\mathbbm{u}^{t})_{t=0}^{99}}

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[4}}\right]}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\langle\mathbbm{x}_{i}^{100},P_{i}\mathbbm{x}_{i}^{100}\rangle+\sum_{t=0}^{99}\langle\mathbbm{x}_{i}^{t},Q_{i}\mathbbm{x}_{i}^{t}\rangle+\langle{\mathbbm{u}^{t}},R_{i}\mathbbm{u}^{t}\rangle}}\right)

(18)

s.t.

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\mathbbm{x}_{i}^{t+1}=A_{i}\mathbbm{x}_{i}^{t}+B\mathbbm{u}^{t},\;t\in\{0\}\cup[99],\\
\mathbbm{x}_{i}^{0}=(0.5\;0),\;\text{for all }i\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[4}}\right],\\
\text{the constraint~\eqref{eq:sim:discon:con}},\end{cases}

where the cost matrices are given by:

\displaystyle(Q_{1},P_{1},R_{1})

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\begin{bmatrix}1.0&0.0\\
0.0&2.0\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}1.0&0.0\\
0.0&2.0\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}0.1\end{bmatrix}}}\right),

\displaystyle(Q_{2},P_{2},R_{2})

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\begin{bmatrix}2.0&0.0\\
0.0&1.5\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}2.0&0.0\\
0.0&1.5\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}1.0\end{bmatrix}}}\right),

\displaystyle(Q_{3},P_{3},R_{3})

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\begin{bmatrix}1.5&0.0\\
0.0&2.0\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}1.5&0.0\\
0.0&2.0\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}1.0\end{bmatrix}}}\right),

\displaystyle(Q_{4},P_{4},R_{4})

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\begin{bmatrix}1.0&0.0\\
0.0&1.0\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}1.0&0.0\\
0.0&1.0\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}0.1\end{bmatrix}}}\right).

We used Algorithm
1
to solve the robust meta control problem (
18
). The meta control sequence obtained is depicted is depicted in Fig.
4(a)
. Using this control sequence we plot in Fig.

1000 state trajectories corresponding to the dynamics (
16
)for 1000 different realizations of the uncertain parameter
\mathbbm{w}
(generated by uniformly randomly sampling on the set
\mathbb{W}
). As illustrated in Fig.
4
, the robust meta control sequence successfully ensures constraint satisfaction across all realizations, demonstrating its effectiveness even on previously unseen instances of the system.

5.
Conclusion

We established a distributed algorithm for solving a convex semi-infinite program over a time-varying network. Algorithm
1
developed here is a first-order distributed method based on CoMirror descent for constrained optimization. We established its convergence guarantee, showing that the network achieves consensus and the optimal value and constraint violation for the estimates held by each node decay asymptotically to zero. We also established the rate of this decay. The numerical experiments corroborate the algorithm’s performance guarantees. In future work, we plan to extend our approach to distributed problems where the constraint functions are heterogeneous across nodes and they change with time. Moreover, we aim to refine the convergence rate for special class of objective functions.
