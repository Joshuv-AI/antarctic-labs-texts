Monotonic Alpha-divergence Minimisation for Variational Inference
Kamélia Daudel, Randal Douc, François Roueff

Abstract
In this paper, we introduce a novel family of iterative algorithms which carry out α \alpha -divergence minimisation in a Variational Inference context. They do so by ensuring a systematic decrease at each step in the α \alpha -divergence between the variational and the posterior distributions. In its most general form, the variational distribution is a mixture model and our framework allows us to simultaneously optimise the weights and components parameters of this mixture model. Our approach permits us to build on various methods previously proposed for α \alpha -divergence minimisation such as Gradient or Power Descent schemes and we also shed a new light on an integrated Expectation Maximization algorithm. Lastly, we provide empirical evidence that our methodology yields improved results on several multimodal target distributions and on a real data example.



1  Introduction

Bayesian inference tasks often induce intractable and hard-to-compute posterior densities which need to be approximated. Among the class of approximating methods, Variational inference methods
(
Jordan et al. 1999
;
James Beal 2003
, for example Variational Bayes)
have attracted a lot of attention as they have empirically been shown to be widely applicable to many high-dimensional machine-learning problems
(
Hoffman et al. 2013
;
Ranganath et al. 2014
;
Kingma and Welling 2014
)
.

These optimisation-based methods introduce a simpler variational family
\mathcal{Q}
and find the best approximation to the unknown posterior density among this family in terms of a certain measure of dissimilarity, the most common choice of measure of dissimilarity being the exclusive Kullback-Leibler divergence
(
Blei et al. 2017
;
Zhang et al. 2019
)
.

However, the exclusive Kullback-Leibler divergence is known to have some drawbacks. Indeed, its
zero-forcing
behavior is responsible for returning variational approximations with light tails that severely underestimate the posterior covariance and that are unable to capture multimodality within the posterior density
(
Minka 2005
;
Jerfel et al. 2021
;
Prangle 2021
)
. This is especially inconvenient when the variational family
\mathcal{Q}
does not exactly match the posterior density
(
Yao et al. 2018
;
Campbell and Li 2019
)
and even more so if one wants to appeal to Importance Sampling methods to approximate integrals of interest in a Bayesian Inference setting
(
Jerfel et al. 2021
;
Prangle 2021
)
.

To avoid this hurdle, advances in Variational Inference turned to more general families of divergences such as the
\alpha
-divergence
(
Zhu and Rohwer 1995b
;
Zhu and Rohwer 1995a
)
and Rényi’s
\alpha
-divergence
(
Rényi 1961
;
van Erven and Harremoes 2014
)
. These families of divergences both recover the exclusive Kullback-Leibler when
\alpha\to 1
and thanks to the hyperparameter
\alpha
, they provide a more flexible framework that can bypass the difficulties associated to the exclusive Kullback-Leibler divergence by choosing
\alpha<1

(
Minka 2005
)
. For this reason, they have notably been used in
Minka 2004
;
Hernandez-Lobato et al. 2016
;
Li and Turner 2016
;
Dieng et al. 2017
;
Wang et al. 2018
;
Daudel et al. 2021
;
Daudel and Douc 2021
.

In the spirit of Variational Inference methods based on the
\alpha
-divergence, we propose in this paper to build a framework for
\alpha
-divergence minimisation in a Variational Inference context. The particularity of our work is that our algorithms will ensure a monotonic decrease at each step in the
\alpha
-divergence between the variational and the posterior distributions. In addition, our work will apply to variational families as wide as the class of mixture models. The paper is then organised as follows:

2  Notation and Optimisation Problem

Let
(\mathsf{Y},\mathcal{Y},\nu)
be a measured space, where
\nu
is a
\sigma
-finite measure on
(\mathsf{Y},\mathcal{Y})
. Assume that we have access to some observed variables
\mathscr{D}
generated from a probabilistic model
p(\cdot|y)
parameterised by a hidden random variable
y\in\mathsf{Y}
that is drawn from a certain prior
p_{0}
. The posterior density of the latent variable
y
given the data
\mathscr{D}
is then given by

p(y|\mathscr{D})=\frac{p(y,\mathscr{D})}{p(\mathscr{D})}=\frac{p_{0}(y)p(\mathscr{D}|y)}{p(\mathscr{D})}\;,

where
p(\mathscr{D})=\int_{\mathsf{Y}}p_{0}(y)p(\mathscr{D}|y)\nu(\mathrm{d}y)
is called the
marginal likelihood
or
model evidence
. For many interesting choices of models, sampling directly from the posterior density is impossible and the marginal likelihood is also unknown or too costly to compute.

To address this difficulty, Variational Inference methods approximate the posterior density by a simpler probability density belonging to a given variational family
\mathcal{Q}
(from which it is easy to sample from). They select the best probability density in
\mathcal{Q}
by solving an optimisation problem that involves a certain measure of dissimilarity, which is chosen to be the
\alpha
-divergence in this paper due to its advantages compared to the more traditional exclusive Kullback-Leibler divergence
(
Minka 2005
, see for example)
.

More precisely, let us denote by
\mathbb{P}
the probability measure on
(\mathsf{Y},\mathcal{Y})
with corresponding density
p(\cdot|\mathscr{D})
with respect to
\nu
. As for the variational family, let us denote by
\mathbb{Q}
the probability measure on
(\mathsf{Y},\mathcal{Y})
with associated density
q\in\mathcal{Q}
with respect to
\nu
. We let
f_{\alpha}
be the convex function on
(0,+\infty)
defined by
f_{0}(u)=-\log(u)
,
f_{1}(u)=u\log u
and
f_{\alpha}(u)=\frac{1}{\alpha(\alpha-1)}\left[u^{\alpha}-1\right]
for all
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\left\{0,1\right\}
. Then, assuming that both
\mathbb{Q}
and
\mathbb{P}
are absolutely continuous w.r.t.
\nu
, the
\alpha
-divergence between
\mathbb{Q}
and
\mathbb{P}

(
Cichocki and Amari 2010
, extended by continuity to the cases
\alpha=0
and
\alpha=1
as for example done in)
is given by

\displaystyle D_{\alpha}(\mathbb{Q}||\mathbb{P})=\int_{\mathsf{Y}}f_{\alpha}\left(\frac{q(y)}{p(y|\mathscr{D})}\right)p(y|\mathscr{D})\nu(\mathrm{d}y)\;,

(1)

and the Variational Inference optimisation problem we aim at solving is

\inf_{q\in\mathcal{Q}}D_{\alpha}(\mathbb{Q}||\mathbb{P})
. Notably, it can easily be proven that this optimisation problem is equivalent to solving

\displaystyle\inf_{q\in\mathcal{Q}}\Psi_{\alpha}(q;p)\;\quad\mbox{with}\quad p(y)=p(y,\mathscr{D})\quad\mbox{for all }y\in\mathsf{Y}\;,

(2)

where, for all measurable non-negative function
p
on
(\mathsf{Y},\mathcal{Y})
and for all
q\in\mathcal{Q}
, we have set

\displaystyle\Psi_{\alpha}(q;p)=\int_{\mathsf{Y}}f_{\alpha}\left(\frac{q(y)}{p(y)}\right)p(y)\nu(\mathrm{d}y)\;.

As the unknown constant
p(\mathscr{D})
does not appear anymore in the optimisation problem (
2
), this formulation is often preferred. Therefore, we consider the general optimisation problem

\displaystyle\inf_{q\in\mathcal{Q}}\Psi_{\alpha}(q;p)\;,

(3)

where
p
is any measurable positive function on
(\mathsf{Y},\mathcal{Y})
. Note that we may drop the dependency on
p
in
\Psi_{\alpha}
for notational ease and when no ambiguity occurs.

At this stage, we are left with the choice of the variational family
\mathcal{Q}
appearing in the optimisation problem (
3
). The natural idea in Variational Inference and the starting point of our approach is then to work within a parametric family : letting
({\mathsf{T}},\mathcal{T})
be a measurable space,
K:(\theta,A)\mapsto\int_{A}k(\theta,y)\nu(\mathrm{d}y)
be a Markov transition kernel on
{\mathsf{T}}\times\mathcal{Y}
with kernel density
k
defined on
{\mathsf{T}}\times\mathsf{Y}
, we consider a parametric family of the form

\mathcal{Q}=\left\{q:y\mapsto k(\theta,y)\;:\;\theta\in{\mathsf{T}}\right\}\;.

3  An Iterative Algorithm for Optimising
\Psi_{\alpha}(k(\theta,\cdot);p)

In this section, our goal is to define iterative procedures which optimise
\Psi_{\alpha}(k(\theta,\cdot);p)
with respect to
\theta
and which are such that they ensure a
systematic decrease
in
\Psi_{\alpha}
at each step. For this purpose, we start by introducing some mild conditions on
k
,
p
and
\nu
that will be used throughout the paper.

(A1)

The kernel density
k
on
{\mathsf{T}}\times\mathsf{Y}
, the function
p
on
\mathsf{Y}
and
the
\sigma
-finite measure
\nu
on
(\mathsf{Y},\mathcal{Y})
satisfy, for all

(\theta,y)\in{\mathsf{T}}\times\mathsf{Y}
,
k(\theta,y)>0
,
p(y)\geqslant 0
and
0<\int_{\mathsf{Y}}p(y)\nu(\mathrm{d}y)<\infty
.

From this point onwards, and as announced in the previous section, we will drop the dependency on
p
in
\Psi_{\alpha}
for notational convenience. Let us now construct a sequence
(\theta_{n})_{n\geqslant 1}
valued in
{\mathsf{T}}
such that the sequence
(\Psi_{\alpha}(k(\theta_{n},\cdot)))_{n\geqslant 1}
is decreasing. The core idea of our approach will rely on the following proposition.

Proposition 1
.

Assume
(A1)
. For all
\alpha\in[0,1)
and all
\theta,\theta^{\prime}\in{\mathsf{T}}
such that
\Psi_{\alpha}(k(\theta^{\prime},\cdot))<\infty
, it holds that

\displaystyle\Psi_{\alpha}(k(\theta,\cdot))

\displaystyle\leqslant\int_{\mathsf{Y}}\frac{k(\theta^{\prime},y)^{\alpha}p(y)^{1-\alpha}}{\alpha-1}\log\left(\frac{k(\theta,y)}{k(\theta^{\prime},y)}\right)\nu(\mathrm{d}y)+\Psi_{\alpha}(k(\theta^{\prime},\cdot))\;.

(4)

Moreover, if
\alpha=0
, this inequality is an equality and, if
\alpha\in(0,1)
, equality in (
4
) is equivalent to having
k(\theta,y)=k(\theta^{\prime},y)
for
\nu
-a.e.
y\in\{p>0\}
.

Proof.

We treat the two cases
\alpha=0
and
\alpha\in(0,1)
separately.

(a)

Case
\alpha=0
with
f_{0}(u)=-\log(u)
for all
u>0
. This case is immediate since

\displaystyle\Psi_{0}(k(\theta,\cdot))=-\int_{\mathsf{Y}}p(y)\log\left(\frac{k(\theta,y)}{k(\theta^{\prime},y)}\right)\nu(\mathrm{d}y)+\Psi_{0}(k(\theta^{\prime},\cdot))\;.

(b)

Case
\alpha\in(0,1)
with
f_{\alpha}(u)=\frac{1}{\alpha(\alpha-1)}\left[u^{\alpha}-1\right]
for all
u>0
. We have that

\displaystyle\Psi_{\alpha}(k(\theta,\cdot))

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathsf{Y}}\frac{\left[\left(\frac{k(\theta,y)}{p(y)}\right)^{\alpha}-1\right]}{\alpha(\alpha-1)}p(y)\nu(\mathrm{d}y)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathsf{Y}}\left(\frac{k(\theta^{\prime},y)}{p(y)}\right)^{\alpha}\frac{\left[\left(\frac{k(\theta,y)}{k(\theta^{\prime},y)}\right)^{\alpha}-1\right]}{\alpha(\alpha-1)}p(y)\nu(\mathrm{d}y)+\Psi_{\alpha}(k(\theta^{\prime},\cdot))

Furthermore, using that
\log(u^{\alpha})\leqslant u^{\alpha}-1
for
all
u>0
with equality only if
u=1
, and since
\alpha\in(0,1)
, this inequality becomes

\frac{u^{\alpha}-1}{\alpha(\alpha-1)}\leqslant\frac{\log(u^{\alpha})}{\alpha(\alpha-1)}=\frac{\log(u)}{\alpha-1}\;.

Applying this with
u=k(\theta,y)/k(\theta^{\prime},y)
in the previous
expression, we get that

\displaystyle\Psi_{\alpha}(k(\theta,\cdot))

\displaystyle\leqslant\int_{\mathsf{Y}}\frac{k(\theta^{\prime},y)^{\alpha}p(y)^{1-\alpha}}{\alpha-1}\log\left(\frac{k(\theta,y)}{k(\theta^{\prime},y)}\right)\nu(\mathrm{d}y)+\Psi_{\alpha}(k(\theta^{\prime},\cdot))

which is exactly (
4
). Moreover, the
equality holds if
k(\theta,y)/k(\theta^{\prime},y)=1
for
\nu
-a.e.
y

such that
k(\theta^{\prime},y)^{\alpha}p(y)^{1-\alpha}>0
, which,
by
(A1)
, only happens if
p(y)>0
.

∎

This result then allows us to deduce
1
below.

Theorem 1
.

Assume
(A1)
. Let
\alpha\in[0,1)
and starting from an initial
\theta_{1}\in{\mathsf{T}}
, let
(\theta_{n})_{n\geqslant 1}
be defined iteratively such that for all
n\geqslant 1
,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathsf{Y}}\frac{k(\theta_{n},y)^{\alpha}p(y)^{1-\alpha}}{\alpha-1}\log\left(\frac{k(\theta_{n+1},y)}{k(\theta_{n},y)}\right)\nu(\mathrm{d}y)\leqslant 0\;.

(5)

Further assume that
\Psi_{\alpha}(k(\theta_{1},\cdot))<\infty
. Then,

\Psi_{\alpha}(k(\theta_{n+1},\cdot))\leqslant\Psi_{\alpha}(k(\theta_{n},\cdot))
for
all
n\geqslant 1
with an equality occurring only
if (
5
) is also an equality.

Proof.

Applying

1
iteratively for
n=1,2,\dots
with
\theta=\theta_{n+1}
and

\theta^{\prime}=\theta_{n}
in (
4
) combined with
(
5
), we get that
\Psi_{\alpha}(k(\theta_{n},\cdot))<\infty
and

\Psi_{\alpha}(k(\theta_{n+1},\cdot))\leqslant\int_{\mathsf{Y}}\frac{k(\theta_{n},y)^{\alpha}p(y)^{1-\alpha}}{\alpha-1}\log\left(\frac{k(\theta_{n+1},y)}{k(\theta_{n},y)}\right)\nu(\mathrm{d}y)+\Psi_{\alpha}(k(\theta_{n},\cdot))\leqslant\Psi_{\alpha}(k(\theta_{n},\cdot))\;.

Suppose now that

\Psi_{\alpha}(k(\theta_{n+1},\cdot))=\Psi_{\alpha}(k(\theta_{n},\cdot))
for
some
n\geqslant 1
. Then we have equalities in the previous display and
thus an equality in (
5
) and
in (
4
) with
\theta=\theta_{n+1}
and

\theta^{\prime}=\theta_{n}
.
∎

At this point, we seek to find iterative schemes satisfying (
5
). To do so, for all
\alpha\in[0,1)
, all
n\geqslant 1
and all
y\in\mathsf{Y}
, we introduce the notation:

\displaystyle\ratio(y)={k(\theta_{n},y)^{\alpha}p(y)^{1-\alpha}}

(6)

\displaystyle\check{\varphi}_{n}^{(\alpha)}=\frac{\ratio}{\int\ratio\;\mathrm{d}\nu}\;.

Note that under
(A1)
, the normalisation in
\check{\varphi}_{n}^{(\alpha)}
satisfies
0<\int\ratio\;\mathrm{d}\nu<\infty
, where the right inequality in particular follows from Jensen’s inequality applied to the strictly concave function
u\mapsto u^{1-\alpha}
. We then state our first corollary.

Corollary 1

(Maximisation approach)
.

Assume
(A1)
. Let
\alpha\in[0,1)
and let
(b_{n})_{n\geqslant 1}
be a non-negative sequence. Starting from an initial
\theta_{1}\in{\mathsf{T}}
, let
(\theta_{n})_{n\geqslant 1}
be defined iteratively as follows

\displaystyle\theta_{n+1}

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathrm{argmax}}_{\theta\in{\mathsf{T}}}\int_{\mathsf{Y}}\left[\ratio(y)+b_{n}k(\theta_{n},y)\right]\log\left(\frac{k(\theta,y)}{k(\theta_{n},y)}\right)\nu(\mathrm{d}y)\;,\quad n\geqslant 1\;{{,}}

(7)

where we assume that this argmax is uniquely defined at each
step. Then (
5
) holds at all times
n\geqslant 1

and we can apply
1
.

Proof.

Under
(A1)
and using that
\alpha\in[0,1)
, we can rewrite (
5
) as

\displaystyle\int_{\mathsf{Y}}\ratio(y)\log\left(\frac{k(\theta_{n+1},y)}{k(\theta_{n},y)}\right)\nu(\mathrm{d}y)\geqslant 0\;.

Since
\int_{\mathsf{Y}}k(\theta_{n},y)\log\left(\frac{k(\theta_{n+1},y)}{k(\theta_{n},y)}\right)\nu(\mathrm{d}y)=-D_{1}(K(\theta_{n},\cdot)||K(\theta_{n+1},\cdot))\leqslant 0
, we also obtain that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathsf{Y}}\ratio(y)\log\left(\frac{k(\theta_{n+1},y)}{k(\theta_{n},y)}\right)\nu(\mathrm{d}y)\geqslant\int_{\mathsf{Y}}\left[\ratio(y)+b_{n}k(\theta_{n},y)\right]\log\left(\frac{k(\theta_{n+1},y)}{k(\theta_{n},y)}\right)\nu(\mathrm{d}y)\;.

Thus, (
5
) holds at all times
n
if the following condition is satisfied:

\displaystyle\int_{\mathsf{Y}}\left[\ratio(y)+b_{n}k(\theta_{n},y)\right]\log\left(\frac{k(\theta_{n+1},y)}{k(\theta_{n},y)}\right)\nu(\mathrm{d}y)\geqslant 0\;,\quad n\geqslant 1\;,

which is implied by the definition of
\theta_{n+1}
in (
7
).
∎

We now make three comments regarding
1
.

•

The r.h.s. of (
7
) can be simplified, yielding the equivalent iterative scheme

\displaystyle\theta_{n+1}

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathrm{argmax}}_{\theta\in{\mathsf{T}}}\int_{\mathsf{Y}}\left[\ratio(y)+b_{n}k(\theta_{n},y)\right]\log k(\theta,y)\nu(\mathrm{d}y)\;,\quad n\geqslant 1\;,

provided that
\int_{\mathsf{Y}}\left[\ratio(y)+b_{n}k(\theta_{n},y)\right]|\log k(\theta_{n},y)|\nu(\mathrm{d}y)<\infty
for all
n\geqslant 1
.

•

While it suffices to find any
\theta_{n+1}
so that (
5
) holds to obtain a systematic decrease in
\Psi_{\alpha}
, defining
\theta_{n+1}
as in (
7
) enables us to solve this argmax problem for notable choices of kernel density
k
. A remarkable aspect is indeed that (
7
) is written as a maximisation problem involving the logarithm of the ratio
k(\theta,y)/k(\theta_{n},y)
whereas
\Psi_{\alpha}
is not directly expressed in terms of logarithm of this ratio for
\alpha\notin\{0,1\}
. As a result, we can use the
maximisation approach
of
1
to derive simple update rules for
(\theta_{n})_{n\geqslant 1}
. An example and a lemma are provided thereafter to illustrate this fact and more general results regarding exponential families will follow later in
Section

6
.

•

The sequence
(b_{n})_{n\geqslant 1}
appearing in (
7
) is arbitrary and can as a result be chosen by the practitioner. It is responsible for introducing a regularisation term in the argmax problem (
7
) so that the larger
b_{n}
is, the closer
\theta_{n+1}
is to
\theta_{n}
.

We next provide a motivating example where the maximisation approach is applicable.

Example 1

(Maximisation approach for a Gaussian density)
.

We consider a
d
-dimen-sional Gaussian density, in which case
\mathsf{Y}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\nu
is the

d
-dimensional Lebesgue measure and
k(\theta,y)=\mathcal{N}(y;m,\Sigma)
, where

\theta=(m,\Sigma)\in{\mathsf{T}}
denotes the mean and covariance
matrix of the Gaussian density. We let the parameter set
{\mathsf{T}}
include all possible means
m
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and all possible positive-definite covariance matrices
\Sigma
. Finally, denoting by
\left\|\cdot\right\|
the Euclidean norm, we assume that the non-negative
function
p
defined on
\mathsf{Y}
satisfies

\displaystyle 0<\int_{\mathsf{Y}}\left(1+\|y\|^{2/(1-\alpha)}\right)\,p(y)\;\mathrm{d}y<\infty\;.

(8)

Then
(A1)
holds. Furthermore, starting from any

\theta_{1}=(m_{1},\Sigma_{1})\in{\mathsf{T}}
so that
\Psi_{\alpha}(k(\theta_{1},\cdot))<\infty
and denoting

\theta_{n}=(m_{n},\Sigma_{n})
for all
n\geqslant 1
, it holds that: for all
n\geqslant 1
and all
\gamma_{n}\in(0,1]
, there exists
b_{n}\geqslant 0
such that the argmax problem (
7
) has a unique solution defined by

\displaystyle\begin{split}&m_{n+1}=\gamma_{n}\,\hat{m}_{n}+(1-\gamma_{n})\,m_{n}\;,\\
&\Sigma_{n+1}=\gamma_{n}\,\widehat{\Sigma}_{n}+(1-\gamma_{n})\,\Sigma_{n}+\gamma_{n}(1-\gamma_{n})\,\left(\widehat{m}_{n}-m_{n}\right)\left(\widehat{m}_{n}-m_{n}\right)^{T}\;,\end{split}

(9)

where, using the definition of
\check{\varphi}_{n}^{(\alpha)}
in (
6
), we set

\displaystyle\widehat{m}_{n}=\int_{\mathsf{Y}}y\,\check{\varphi}_{n}^{(\alpha)}(y)\;\nu(\mathrm{d}y)\;,

\displaystyle\widehat{\Sigma}_{n}=\int_{\mathsf{Y}}yy^{T}\,\check{\varphi}_{n}^{(\alpha)}(y)\;\nu(\mathrm{d}y)-\widehat{m}_{n}\widehat{m}_{n}^{T}\;.

Here, the detailed derivation of (
9
) is deferred to
Section

6.2
as we focus on interpreting this result in light of
1
(in particular we will see in
6
that the case
\gamma_{n}=1
corresponding to
b_{n}=0
will require an additional non-degenerate condition on
p
).
By
1
, the systematic decrease in
(\Psi_{\alpha}(k(\theta_{n},\cdot)))_{n\geqslant 1}
holds for any choice of sequence
(\gamma_{n})_{n\geqslant 1}
valued in
(0,1]
in the updates (
9
). In addition,
\gamma_{n}
permits us to build a tradeoff at time
n\geqslant 1
between selecting an update close to the current parameter (that is, taking
\gamma_{n}
close to zero) and choosing the Gaussian density with exactly the same mean and covariance matrix as
\check{\varphi}_{n}^{(\alpha)}
(that is, setting
\gamma_{n}=1
). This is a key idea that is linked to the regularisation term appearing in (
7
) and that we will revisit several times throughout the paper.

The maximisation approach of
1
is also applicable to the commonly-used
mean-field
variational family, which approximates the true density by a density with independent components parameterised separately. The following lemma indeed states that the global argmax problem can be separated into component-wise argmax problems in that case.

Lemma 1

(Maximisation approach for the mean-field family)
.

A generic member of the mean-field variational family is given by
k(\theta,y)=\prod_{\ell=1}^{L}k^{(\ell)}(\theta^{(\ell)},y^{(\ell)})
with
\theta=(\theta^{(1)},\ldots,\theta^{(L)})\in{\mathsf{T}}
and
y=(y^{(1)},\ldots,y^{(L)})\in\mathsf{Y}
. Then, starting from
\theta_{1}\in{\mathsf{T}}
and denoting
\theta_{n}=(\theta_{n}^{(1)},\ldots,\theta_{n}^{(L)})
for all
n\geqslant 1
, solving (
7
) yields the following update formulas:

\displaystyle\theta^{(\ell)}_{n+1}

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathrm{argmax}}_{\theta^{(\ell)}}\int_{\mathsf{Y}}\left[\ratio(y)+b_{n}k(\theta_{n},y)\right]\log\left(\frac{k^{(\ell)}(\theta^{(\ell)},y^{(\ell)})}{k^{(\ell)}(\theta_{n}^{(\ell)},y^{(\ell)})}\right)\nu(\mathrm{d}y)\;,\quad{{\ell=1\ldots L}},\quad n\geqslant 1\;.

The maximisation approach is not the only way to satisfy (
5
). Indeed, this can also be achieved by taking a
gradient-based
approach and relying on additional smoothness conditions (see
Section

A.1
for the definition of
\beta
-smoothness), as written in
2
.

Corollary 2

(Gradient-based approach)
.

Assume
(A1)
. Let
{\mathsf{T}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a
convex set, let
\alpha\in[0,1)
and let
(\gamma_{n})_{n\geqslant 1}

be valued in
(0,1]
. Starting from an initial
\theta_{1}\in{\mathsf{T}}
, let
(\theta_{n})_{n\geqslant 1}
be defined iteratively as follows

\displaystyle\theta_{n+1}

\displaystyle=\theta_{n}-\frac{\gamma_{n}}{\beta_{n}}\nabla g_{n}(\theta_{n})\;,\quad n\geqslant 1\;,

(10)

where
(g_{n})_{n\geqslant 1}
is the sequence of functions defined by: for all
n\geqslant 1
and all
\theta\in{\mathsf{T}}
,

\displaystyle g_{n}(\theta)=\int_{\mathsf{Y}}\frac{{{\ratio(y)}}}{\alpha-1}\log\left(\frac{k(\theta,y)}{k(\theta_{n},y)}\right)\nu(\mathrm{d}y)\;

(11)

and
g_{n}
is assumed to be
\beta_{n}
-smooth. Then (
5
) holds for all
n\geqslant 1
and we can apply
1
.

Proof.

Since
\gamma_{n}\in(0,1]
and
g_{n}
is a
\beta_{n}
-smooth function
by assumption, we can apply
2
and we obtain
that for all
n\geqslant 1
,

\displaystyle g_{n}(\theta_{n})-g_{n}\left(\theta_{n}-\frac{\gamma_{n}}{\beta_{n}}\nabla g_{n}(\theta_{n})\right)\geqslant\frac{\gamma_{n}}{2\beta_{n}}\left\|\nabla g_{n}(\theta_{n})\right\|^{2}\;.

Thus, by definition of
\theta_{n+1}
in (
10
), we have

0=g_{n}(\theta_{n})\geqslant g_{n}(\theta_{n+1})
, which in turn implies (
5
) and the proof is concluded.
∎

Let us now reflect on the implications of
2
. Under common differentiability assumptions, we can write: for all
n\geqslant 1
and all
{{\theta^{\prime}}}\in{\mathsf{T}}

\displaystyle\nabla g_{n}(\theta^{\prime})=\int_{\mathsf{Y}}\frac{{{\ratio(y)}}}{\alpha-1}\left.\frac{\partial\log k(\theta,y)}{\partial\theta}\right|_{(\theta,y)=(\theta^{\prime},y)}\nu(\mathrm{d}y)\;.

Then, (
10
) becomes

\displaystyle\theta_{n+1}

\displaystyle=\theta_{n}-\frac{\gamma_{n}}{\beta_{n}}\int_{\mathsf{Y}}\frac{{{\ratio(y)}}}{\alpha-1}\left.\frac{\partial\log k(\theta,y)}{\partial\theta}\right|_{(\theta,y)=(\theta_{n},y)}\nu(\mathrm{d}y)\;,\quad n\geqslant 1\;.

(12)

Under this form, the iterative scheme (
12
) bears similarities with Gradient Descent iterations for
\alpha
-divergence and Rényi’s
\alpha
-divergence minimisation. Indeed, given a learning rate policy
(r_{n})_{n\geqslant 1}
and setting
p=p(\cdot,\mathscr{D})
, such Gradient Descent iterations are given respectively by

\displaystyle\theta_{n+1}=\theta_{n}-r_{n}\int_{\mathsf{Y}}\frac{\ratio(y)}{\alpha-1}\left.\frac{\partial\log k(\theta,y)}{\partial\theta}\right|_{(\theta,y)=(\theta_{n},y)}\nu(\mathrm{d}y)\;,\quad n\geqslant 1

\displaystyle\theta_{n+1}=\theta_{n}-r_{n}\int_{\mathsf{Y}}\frac{\check{\varphi}_{n}^{(\alpha)}(y)}{\alpha-1}\left.\frac{\partial\log k(\theta,y)}{\partial\theta}\right|_{(\theta,y)=(\theta_{n},y)}\nu(\mathrm{d}y)\;,\quad n\geqslant 1

(we refer to
Section

A.2
for details regarding how these updates are obtained). Building on this comment, let us now give an example where the conditions on
(g_{n})_{n\geqslant 1}
from
2
are satisfied and show how Gradient Descent steps (in that case for Rényi’s
\alpha
-divergence minimisation) can originate from our gradient-based approach.

Example 2

(Gradient-based approach for a Gaussian density)
.

We consider the case of a
d
-dimensional Gaussian density with
k(\theta,y)=\mathcal{N}(y;m,\Sigma)
, where this time
\theta=m
. Further assume that
{\mathsf{T}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a convex subset and that
\Sigma=\sigma^{2}\boldsymbol{I_{d}}
, where
\sigma^{2}>0
and
\boldsymbol{I_{d}}
denotes the
d
-dimensional identity matrix. Finally, denoting by
\left\|\cdot\right\|
the Euclidean norm, we assume that the non-negative function
p
defined on
\mathsf{Y}
satisfies

\displaystyle 0<\int_{\mathsf{Y}}\left(1+\|y\|^{1/(1-\alpha)}\right)p(y)\mathrm{d}y<\infty\;.

(13)

Then,
(A1)
holds.
Furthermore, denoting
\theta_{n}=m_{n}
and setting
\beta_{n}=\sigma^{-2}(1-\alpha)^{-1}\int\ratio\mathrm{d}\nu
for all
n\geqslant 1
, the conditions on
(g_{n})_{n\geqslant 1}
from
2
are satisfied so that the mean of the Gaussian density
\mathcal{N}(y;m,\Sigma)
can be optimised as follows: for all
n\geqslant 1
and all
\gamma_{n}\in[0,1)

\displaystyle{{m_{n+1}}}

\displaystyle=\gamma_{n}\hat{m}_{n}+\left(1-\gamma_{n}\right)m_{n}\;,

(14)

where
\hat{m}_{n}=\int_{\mathsf{Y}}y\penalty\ \check{\varphi}_{n}^{(\alpha)}(y)\penalty\ \nu(\mathrm{d}y)
(we refer to
8
for the detailed derivation). Setting
p=p(\cdot,\mathscr{D})
, the update (
14
) can notably be seen as a Gradient Descent step for Rényi’s
\alpha
-divergence minimisation with a learning rate
r_{n}=\sigma^{2}(1-\alpha)\gamma_{n}
, where
\gamma_{n}\in[0,1)
.

We now make two important comments.

•

2
sheds light on the links between our approach and the more traditional Gradient Descent methodology for optimising objective functions based on the
\alpha
-divergence in Variational Inference
(
Li and Turner 2016
;
Dieng et al. 2017
)
.

Unlike the usual Gradient Descent methodology,
2
requires a smoothness condition on
g_{n}
. Smoothness conditions are tools that are often used to obtain stronger convergence guarantees for Variational Inference algorithms
(
Buchholz et al. 2018
;
Alquier and Ridgway 2020
, see for example)
. Yet, it can be difficult to satisfy these smoothness assumptions in practice, even if some results have been derived for specific variational families when using the exclusive Kullback-Leibler divergence as the objective function
(
Domke 2020
)
.

To the best of our knowledge, no such results have been proven for
\alpha
-divergence minimisation. In our case, we obtain that a smoothness condition on
g_{n}
translates into a systematic decrease in
\Psi_{\alpha}
. As we shall see in the forthcoming section, being able to establish monotonicity results on
(\theta_{n})_{n\geqslant 1}
will come in handy as we try to go beyond the framework considered in
Section

3
.

•

To put things into perspective with the maximisation approach from earlier, observe that the updates on the means in Examples
1
and
2
coincide. The difference between the two examples is due to the fact that the former provides an update for the covariance matrix as well,
without sacrificing the monotonicity of the overall algorithm
. It is in fact hard to derive a covariance matrix update in the Gaussian case using the gradient-based approach (see
Section

6.3
for details). In that sense, the maximisation approach provides an interesting alternative that can bypass this difficult smoothness assumption on
g_{n}
. We will further delve into this aspect as we reach
Section

6
.

So far, the
\log
function appearing in
1
considerably eased the derivation of iterative update formulas for well-chosen kernel densities
k
that can exploit this log structure (Examples
1
-
2
and
1
). Yet, these choices of kernel densities can be too restrictive to fully capture complex and multimodal posterior densities. Since working with a larger variational family might lead to more accurate approximations of the posterior density, an idea is then to investigate whether the iterative update formulas from
Section

3
can be generalised to the mixture model variational family (for example whether we can extend the updates for a Gaussian kernel from
1
to Gaussian Mixture Models).

As we shall see in the next section, further theoretical developments will be required in order to derive valid iterative schemes that optimise both the mixture weights and the mixture components parameters of a given mixture model.

4  Extension to Mixture Models

Let us first formally define the class of mixture
models we are going to be working with. Given
J\in\mathbb{N}^{\star}
, we introduce the simplex of
\mathbb{R}^{J}
:

\mathcal{S}_{J}=\left\{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{J})\in\mathbb{R}^{J}\;:\;\forall j\in\left\{1,\ldots,J\right\},\;\lambda_{j}\geqslant 0\;\mbox{and}\;\sum_{j=1}^{J}\lambda_{j}=1\right\}\;,

we define
\mathcal{S}_{J}^{+}=\left\{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\in\mathcal{S}_{J}\;:\;\forall j\in\left\{1,\ldots,J\right\},\;\lambda_{j}>0\right\}
and we denote
\Theta=(\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{J})\in{\mathsf{T}}^{J}
. We consider the mixture model variational family given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}=\left\{q:y\mapsto\mu_{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}},\Theta}k(y):=\sum_{j=1}^{J}\lambda_{j}k(\theta_{j},y)\;:\;{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\in\mathcal{S}_{J},\Theta\in{\mathsf{T}}^{J}\right\}

(15)

that is, we are interested in solving the optimisation problem

\displaystyle\inf_{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\in\mathcal{S}_{J},\Theta\in{\mathsf{T}}^{J}}\Psi_{\alpha}(\mu_{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}},\Theta}k)\;,

with
J>1
. Let us next denote
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{n}}=(\lambda_{j,n})_{1\leqslant j\leqslant J}
and
\Theta_{n}=(\theta_{j,n})_{1\leqslant j\leqslant J}
for all
n\geqslant 1
. For convenience, we also introduce the shorthand notation
\mu_{n}k=\mu_{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{n}},\Theta_{n}}k
and

\displaystyle\varphi_{j,n}^{(\alpha)}(y)=k(\theta_{j,n},y)\left(\frac{\mu_{n}k(y)}{p(y)}\right)^{\alpha-1}

(16)

\displaystyle\check{\varphi}_{j,n}^{(\alpha)}=\frac{\varphi_{j,n}^{(\alpha)}}{\int\varphi_{j,n}^{(\alpha)}\mathrm{d}\nu}

for all
\alpha\in[0,1)
, all
j=1\ldots J
, all
n\geqslant 1
and all
y\in\mathsf{Y}
.

Our goal in this section is to derive iterative schemes for the mixture weights and the mixture components parameters
({\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{n}},\Theta_{n})_{n\geqslant 1}
ensuring that the sequence
(\Psi_{\alpha}(\mu_{n}k))_{n\geqslant 1}
is decreasing. As
1
holds for any choice of parametric family, a first idea is to apply
1
to the variational family (
15
), which gives
3
below.

Corollary 3
.

Assume
(A1)
. Let
J\in\mathbb{N}^{\star}
, let
\alpha\in[0,1)
and starting from an initial parameter set
({\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{1}},\Theta_{1})\in\mathcal{S}_{J}^{+}\times{\mathsf{T}}^{J}
, let
({\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{n}},\Theta_{n})_{n\geqslant 1}
be defined iteratively such that for all
n\geqslant 1
,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathsf{Y}}\frac{(\mu_{n}k(y))^{\alpha}p(y)^{1-\alpha}}{\alpha-1}\log\left(\frac{\mu_{n+1}k(y)}{\mu_{n}k(y)}\right)\nu(\mathrm{d}y)\leqslant 0\;.

(17)

Further assume that
\Psi_{\alpha}(\mu_{1}k)<\infty
. Then,
\Psi_{\alpha}(\mu_{n+1}k)\leqslant\Psi_{\alpha}(\mu_{n}k)
for all
n\geqslant 1
.

Observe that we are in a less favourable situation in
3
with
J>1
compared to the cases we previously studied in
Section

3
. Indeed, we now have a ratio of sums inside the
\log
function in (
17
), meaning that the approach from
1
to derive simple iterative schemes does not directly transfer to the variational family (
15
) for choices of kernel densities
k
identified in Examples
1
-
2
and
1
. However, by carefully exploiting the condition (
17
), we are able to overcome this difficulty in our second main theorem below.

Theorem 2
.

Assume
(A1)
. Let
J\in\mathbb{N}^{\star}
,
\alpha\in[0,1)
and starting from an initial parameter set
({\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{1}},\Theta_{1})\in\mathcal{S}_{J}^{+}\times{\mathsf{T}}^{J}
, let
({\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{n}},\Theta_{n})_{n\geqslant 1}
be defined iteratively such that for all
n\geqslant 1
,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathsf{Y}}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\lambda_{j,n}\frac{\varphi_{j,n}^{(\alpha)}(y)}{\alpha-1}\log\left(\frac{\lambda_{j,n+1}}{\lambda_{j,n}}\right)\nu(\mathrm{d}y)\leqslant 0

(18)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathsf{Y}}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\lambda_{j,n}\frac{\varphi_{j,n}^{(\alpha)}(y)}{\alpha-1}\log\left(\frac{k(\theta_{j,n+1},y)}{k(\theta_{j,n},y)}\right)\nu(\mathrm{d}y)\leqslant 0\;.

(19)

Further assume that
\Psi_{\alpha}(\mu_{1}k)<\infty
. Then,
\Psi_{\alpha}(\mu_{n+1}k)\leqslant\Psi_{\alpha}(\mu_{n}k)
for all
n\geqslant 1
.

Proof.

By
3
, we can conclude if we show that (
18
) and (
19
) together imply (
17
).
To show this, first observe that since the function
u\mapsto\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\log(u)
is convex and
\alpha\in[0,1)
, Jensen’s inequality implies that: for all
y\in\mathsf{Y}
and all
n\geqslant 1
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\log\left(\frac{\mu_{n+1}k(y)}{\mu_{n}k(y)}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\log\left(\sum_{j=1}^{J}\frac{\lambda_{j,n}k(\theta_{j,n},y)}{\sum_{\ell=1}^{J}\lambda_{\ell,n}k(\theta_{\ell,n},y)}\frac{\lambda_{j,n+1}k(\theta_{j,n+1},y)}{\lambda_{j,n}k(\theta_{j,n},y)}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{j=1}^{J}\frac{\lambda_{j,n}k(\theta_{j,n},y)}{\sum_{\ell=1}^{J}\lambda_{\ell,n}k(\theta_{\ell,n},y)}\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\log\left(\frac{\lambda_{j,n+1}k(\theta_{j,n+1},y)}{\lambda_{j,n}k(\theta_{j,n},y)}\right)

that is:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\log\left(\frac{\mu_{n+1}k(y)}{\mu_{n}k(y)}\right)\leqslant\sum_{j=1}^{J}\frac{\lambda_{j,n}}{\alpha-1}\frac{k(\theta_{j,n},y)}{\mu_{n}k(y)}\log\left(\frac{\lambda_{j,n+1}k(\theta_{j,n+1},y)}{\lambda_{j,n}k(\theta_{j,n},y)}\right)\;.

Multiplying by
(\mu_{n}k(y))^{\alpha}p(y)^{1-\alpha}
on both sides, integrating with respect to
\nu(\mathrm{d}y)
and using the definition of
\varphi_{j,n}^{(\alpha)}(y)
in (
16
), this in turn implies that: for all
n\geqslant 1
,

\int_{\mathsf{Y}}\frac{(\mu_{n}k(y))^{\alpha}p(y)^{1-\alpha}}{\alpha-1}\log\left(\frac{\mu_{n+1}k(y)}{\mu_{n}k(y)}\right)\nu(\mathrm{d}y)\\
\leqslant\int_{\mathsf{Y}}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\lambda_{j,n}\frac{\varphi_{j,n}^{(\alpha)}(y)}{\alpha-1}\log\left(\frac{\lambda_{j,n+1}k(\theta_{j,n+1},y)}{\lambda_{j,n}k(\theta_{j,n},y)}\right)\nu(\mathrm{d}y)\;.

As a consequence, the condition

\displaystyle\int_{\mathsf{Y}}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\lambda_{j,n}\frac{\varphi_{j,n}^{(\alpha)}(y)}{\alpha-1}\log\left(\frac{\lambda_{j,n+1}k(\theta_{j,n+1},y)}{\lambda_{j,n}k(\theta_{j,n},y)}\right)\nu(\mathrm{d}y)\leqslant 0

(20)

implies (
17
). The condition (
20
) in itself is then straightforwardly implied by (
18
) and (
19
) and the proof is concluded.
∎

Strikingly, (
18
) does not depend on
\Theta_{n+1}
nor does (
19
) depend on
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{n+1}}
in
2
. This means that we can treat these two conditions separately and thus that the weights and components parameters of the mixture can be optimised simultaneously. This result was far from immediate by looking at the initial condition (
17
) and, as we shall see laterly in
Section

8
, will lead to reduced computational power in practice.

In addition, we have recovered in (
19
) the key property used in
Section

3
: compared to the condition (
17
) which involved the
\log
of weighted sums of kernel densities, (
19
) considers a sum of logs of kernel densities. This suggests that we can extend the updates derived in
Section

3
for well-chosen kernel densities
k
to the more general case of mixture models.

Observe finally that the dependency in
\lambda_{j,n+1}
appearing in (
18
) is simpler than the dependency in
\theta_{j,n+1}
appearing in (
19
) and that is expressed through the kernel density
k
. As a result, we will first study (
18
), in the hope of deriving iterative update formulas for the mixture weights that do not require a specific choice of kernel density
k
. Interestingly, while the natural idea is to perform direct optimisation of the left-hand side of (
18
), we will derive a more general expression for the mixture weights update, which shall induce numerical advantages later illustrated in
Section

8
.

4.1  Choice of
({\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{n}})_{n\geqslant 1}

In the following theorem, we identify an update formula which satisfies (
18
), regardless of the choice of the kernel density
k
.

Theorem 3
.

Assume
(A1)
. Let
\alpha\in[0,1)
, let
(\eta_{n})_{n\geqslant 1}
be valued in
(0,1]
, let
(\kappa_{n})_{n\geqslant 1}
be such that
(\alpha-1)\kappa_{n}\geqslant 0
at all times
n\geqslant 1
and let
(\Theta_{n})_{n\geqslant 1}
be any sequence valued in
{\mathsf{T}}^{J}
. Starting from an initial
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{1}}\in\mathcal{S}_{J}^{+}
, let
({\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{n}})_{n\geqslant 1}
be defined iteratively such that for all
n\geqslant 1

\displaystyle\lambda_{j,n+1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda_{j,n}\left[\int_{\mathsf{Y}}\varphi_{j,n}^{(\alpha)}(y)\nu(\mathrm{d}y)+(\alpha-1)\kappa_{n}\right]^{\eta_{n}}}{\sum_{\ell=1}^{J}\lambda_{\ell,n}\left[\int_{\mathsf{Y}}\varphi_{\ell,n}^{(\alpha)}(y)\nu(\mathrm{d}y)+(\alpha-1)\kappa_{n}\right]^{\eta_{n}}}\;,\quad j=1\ldots J\;.

(21)

Then (
18
) holds.

Proof.

We first check that the integrals appearing
in (
21
) are finite:

\int_{\mathsf{Y}}\varphi_{j,n}^{(\alpha)}(y)\;\nu(\mathrm{d}y)<\infty\;,\qquad j=1,\dots,J\,,\;n\geqslant 1\;.

(22)

Using (
16
), that

\lambda_{j,n}>0
, that
\lambda_{j,n}k(\theta_{j,n},y)\leqslant\mu_{n}k(y)
and Jensen’s inequality applied to the concave
function
u\mapsto u^{1-\alpha}
, we have,
for any
j=1,\dots,J
and
n\geqslant 1
,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathsf{Y}}\varphi_{j,n}^{(\alpha)}(y)\;\nu(\mathrm{d}y)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathsf{Y}}k(\theta_{j,n},y)\left(\frac{\mu_{n}k(y)}{p(y)}\right)^{\alpha-1}\nu(\mathrm{d}y)

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{\lambda_{j,n}}\int_{\mathsf{Y}}\mu_{n}k(y)\left(\frac{p(y)}{\mu_{n}k(y)}\right)^{1-\alpha}\nu(\mathrm{d}y)

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{\lambda_{j,n}}\left(\int_{\mathsf{Y}}p(y)\nu(\mathrm{d}y)\right)^{1-\alpha}\;.

The bound (
22
) follows by
(A1)
.
Now, to prove (
18
), we treat the cases

\eta_{n}=1
and
\eta_{n}\in(0,1)
separately.

(a)

Case
\eta_{n}=1
. Since
(\alpha-1)\kappa_{n}\geqslant 0
with
\alpha\in(0,1)
, we have that

\kappa_{n}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\lambda_{j,n}\log(\lambda_{j}/\lambda_{j,n})\geqslant 0

where we have used that
\sum_{j=1}^{J}\lambda_{j,n}\log(\lambda_{j}/\lambda_{j,n})\leqslant\sum_{j=1}^{J}\lambda_{j,n}(\lambda_{j}/\lambda_{j,n}-1)=0
. In other words, to obtain (
18
) in the particular case
\eta_{n}=1
, it is enough to show

\displaystyle\int_{\mathsf{Y}}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\lambda_{j,n}\frac{\varphi_{j,n}^{(\alpha)}(y)}{\alpha-1}\log\left(\frac{\lambda_{j,n+1}}{\lambda_{j,n}}\right)\nu(\mathrm{d}y)+\kappa_{n}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\lambda_{j,n}\log\left(\frac{\lambda_{j,n+1}}{\lambda_{j,n}}\right)\leqslant 0

that is, since (
22
) holds,

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{J}\lambda_{j,n}\left[\int_{\mathsf{Y}}\frac{\varphi_{j,n}^{(\alpha)}(y)}{\alpha-1}\nu(\mathrm{d}y)+\kappa_{n}\right]\log\left(\frac{\lambda_{j,n+1}}{\lambda_{j,n}}\right)\leqslant 0\;.

(23)

Notice then that by definition of
(\lambda_{j,n+1})_{1\leqslant j\leqslant J}
when
\eta_{n}=1
, we can write

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{n+1}}=\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\in\mathcal{S}_{J}^{+}}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\lambda_{j,n}\left[\int_{\mathsf{Y}}\frac{\varphi_{j,n}^{(\alpha)}(y)}{\alpha-1}\nu(\mathrm{d}y)+\kappa_{n}\right]\log\left(\frac{\lambda_{j}}{\lambda_{j,n}}\right)\;.

[Indeed, setting
\beta_{j}=\lambda_{j,n}\left[\int_{\mathsf{Y}}\varphi_{j,n}^{(\alpha)}(y)\nu(\mathrm{d}y)+(\alpha-1)\kappa_{n}\right]
and
\bar{\beta}_{j}=\beta_{j}/\sum_{\ell=1}^{J}\beta_{\ell}
for all
j=1\ldots J
, we have that
\sum_{j=1}^{J}\bar{\beta}_{j}\log\left(\bar{\beta}_{j}/\lambda_{j}\right)\geqslant 0

and that this quantity is minimal when
\lambda_{j}=\bar{\beta}_{j}
for
j=1\ldots J
.] This implies (
23
) and settles the case
\eta_{n}=1
.

(b)

For the particular case
\eta_{n}\in(0,1)
, we will use that for all
\epsilon>0
and all
u>0
,

\log(u)=\frac{1}{\epsilon}\log(u^{\epsilon})\geqslant\frac{1}{\epsilon}\left(1-\frac{1}{u^{\epsilon}}\right)\;.

Indeed, since
\int_{\mathsf{Y}}\frac{\varphi_{j,n}^{(\alpha)}(y)}{\alpha-1}\nu(\mathrm{d}y)+\kappa_{n}\leqslant 0
for all
j=1\ldots J
, we can then write that for all
\epsilon>0
,

\sum_{j=1}^{J}\lambda_{j,n}\left[\int_{\mathsf{Y}}\frac{\varphi_{j,n}^{(\alpha)}(y)}{\alpha-1}\nu(\mathrm{d}y)+\kappa_{n}\right]\log\left(\frac{\lambda_{j,n+1}}{\lambda_{j,n}}\right)\\
\leqslant\frac{1}{\epsilon}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\lambda_{j,n}\left[\int_{\mathsf{Y}}\frac{\varphi_{j,n}^{(\alpha)}(y)}{\alpha-1}\nu(\mathrm{d}y)+\kappa_{n}\right]\left[1-\left(\frac{\lambda_{j,n}}{\lambda_{j,n+1}}\right)^{\epsilon}\right]\;.

(24)

Now notice that by definition of
(\lambda_{j,n+1})_{1\leqslant j\leqslant J}
we can write

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{n+1}}=\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\in\mathcal{S}_{J}^{+}}\frac{1}{\epsilon}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\lambda_{j,n}\left[\int_{\mathsf{Y}}\frac{\varphi_{j,n}^{(\alpha)}(y)}{\alpha-1}\nu(\mathrm{d}y)+\kappa_{n}\right]\left[1-\left(\frac{\lambda_{j,n}}{\lambda_{j}}\right)^{\epsilon}\right]

when
\epsilon
satisfies
\eta_{n}=\frac{1}{1+\epsilon}
. [Indeed setting
\beta_{j}=\lambda_{j,n}\left[\int_{\mathsf{Y}}\varphi_{j,n}^{(\alpha)}(y)\nu(\mathrm{d}y)+(\alpha-1)\kappa_{n}\right]^{\frac{1}{1+\epsilon}}
and
\bar{\beta}_{j}=\beta_{j}/\sum_{\ell=1}^{J}\beta_{\ell}
for all
j=1\ldots J
, we have by convexity of the function
u\mapsto u^{1+\epsilon}
that
\sum_{j=1}^{J}\left(\bar{\beta}_{j}/\lambda_{j}\right)^{1+\epsilon}\lambda_{j}\geqslant(\sum_{j=1}^{J}{\bar{\beta}_{j}})^{1+\epsilon}
and that this quantity is minimal when
\lambda_{j}=\bar{\beta}_{j}
for
j=1\ldots J
.] We then deduce that taking
\epsilon=\eta_{n}^{-1}-1
(it is always possible since
\eta_{n}\in(0,1)
by assumption) yields

\frac{1}{\epsilon}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\lambda_{j,n}\left[\int_{\mathsf{Y}}\frac{\varphi_{j,n}^{(\alpha)}(y)}{\alpha-1}\nu(\mathrm{d}y)+\kappa_{n}\right]\left[1-\left(\frac{\lambda_{j,n}}{\lambda_{j,n+1}}\right)^{\epsilon}\right]\leqslant 0

which in turn yields (
18
) [since combined with (
24
) it implies (
23
) which itself implies (
18
) as seen in the case
\eta_{n}=1
]. This settles the case
\eta_{n}\in(0,1)
.

∎

Notice that as a byproduct of the proof of

5.1  Rényi Divergence Variational Inference
(

In

Contrary to us, the work from

Yet, a parallel can be drawn in the GMM case between

and our approach by observing that the gradient-based updates on the means in
4
each coincide with a Gradient Ascent step on the objective function (

is proportional to the gradient of Rényi’s
\alpha
-divergence with a factor

Section

4.2.2
regarding Gradient Descent steps for Rényi’s
\alpha
-divergence minimisation in the GMM case).

Our work hence provides a theoretical framework which enables simultaneous optimisation of the mixture weights
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
and of the mixture components parameters
\Theta
. In addition, beyond the gradient-based updates, we propose the novel maximisation updates and we allow for covariance matrices optimisation. We also emphasise that our maximisation approach will apply to well-chosen kernels

beyond the Gaussian case, as we will detail in
Section

(

The Power Descent is a

\displaystyle={\int_{\mathsf{Y}}}k(\theta,y)\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\left[\left(\dfrac{\mu k(y)}{p(y)}\right)^{\alpha-1}-1\right]\nu(\mathrm{d}y)

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\alpha}(\mu)(\mathrm{d}\theta)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\mu(\mathrm{d}\theta)\penalty\ [(\alpha-1)({b_{\mu,\alpha}}(\theta)+\kappa)+1]^{\frac{\eta}{1-\alpha}}}{\mu([(\alpha-1)({b_{\mu,\alpha}}+\kappa)+1]^{\frac{\eta}{1-\alpha}})}\;.

\upsilon(\theta)=\Sigma^{-1}\theta
and define the canonical kernel

with

,

and

Then (

and
(ii)

lead to the the following gradient-based updates, respectively,

\displaystyle\theta=\theta_{0}-\frac{\gamma}{\beta_{0}}\Sigma\left(\theta_{0}-\int y\penalty\ \check{\varphi}(y)\;\mathrm{d}y\right)

(46)

\displaystyle\theta=\theta_{0}-\frac{\gamma}{\beta}\Sigma^{-1}\,\left(\theta_{0}-\int y\check{\varphi}(y)\;\mathrm{d}y\right)

where
\beta_{0}
is the largest eigenvalue of
\Sigma
and
\beta
is
the largest eigenvalue of
\Sigma^{-1}
. They correspond to the
smoothness indices of

and
g
over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, respectively.

8
illustrates how, even in a
simple framework, the gradient-based updates strongly depend
on the parameterisation chosen. They in fact coincide only if

\Sigma
is scalar in the above corollary, in which case

and

\Sigma^{-1}/\beta

matrix.

Following the reasoning of
Section

6.2
,
we can then apply

with
\check{\varphi}=\check{\varphi}_{n}^{(\alpha)}
(and more generally with
\check{\varphi}=\check{\varphi}_{j,n}^{(\alpha)}
). As (

(
13

in

Section

C.2
and that
here
S(y)=y
), this enables us to deduce the updates in
Examples
2
and
4

introduce a convex subset

C_{0}
in

(this is due to the fact that
the smoothness index is constant over the whole parameter space for
both gradient-based updates in that case), which is why

C_{0}
does not appear in Examples
2
and

4
.

Let us next put into perspective
6
, in which we considered a Gaussian density with varying mean vector and covariance matrix, and
8
, where the covariance matrix is assumed to be known. While
8
kept the computations straightforward and provided gradient-based updates that do not require to introduce a convex subset

, this is no longer the case if we take the same exact setting as in

. Indeed, when

and under the condition (
34
), the gradient of

is given by

\displaystyle\nabla g(\theta)

\displaystyle=\left(\Sigma^{-1}\left(m-\int y\check{\varphi}(y)\;\mathrm{d}y\right),\frac{1}{2}\Sigma^{-1}\left(\int yy^{T}\check{\varphi}(y)\;\mathrm{d}y-\Sigma+mm^{T}\right)\Sigma^{-1}\right)\;.

The smoothness index
\beta
is now no longer constant here, which makes it much more involved to choose a convenient convex subset
C_{0}
and to bound the smoothness index over it.

At this stage, we can solve the maximisation and gradient-based approaches when the kernel
k
belongs to the exponential family for variational families as large as (finite) mixture models. The maximisation approach appears to be preferable to the gradient-based one as it does not require a smoothness assumption and does not depend on the parameterisation.

In the next section, we investigate how our maximisation approach may further generalise to include extensions of the exponential family distribution such as Student’s
t
distributions, hence further motivating the maximisation approach over the gradient-based approach.

6.4  Extension to Linear Mixture (LM) Models

The goal of this section is to examine what becomes of the
maximisation approach for a specific extension of the exponential
family distribution, which we will refer to as
exponential linear mixture
(ELM) family. The ELM family will in
particular encompass the Student’s
t
distribution with mean
m
,
covariance matrix
\Sigma
and
a
degrees of freedom which can be
defined as the continuous mixture

where
\check{\tau}_{a}
denotes the
\chi^{2}
distribution with

a
degrees of freedom. As a result, building a
maximisation approach within the ELM family will lead to new
iterative schemes for variational families that go beyond the cases
considered thus far. To this end, let us first provide a precise definition of the ELM family we want to study and introduce the main assumptions of this section. All the proofs from
Section

6.4
are deferred to
Section

C.4
.

6.4.1

Let

Here, Assumption

6.4.3
The Maximisation Approach for the ELM Family

Let us show that we can solve an argmax of the form (

an adequate choice of

(C3)

(C4)

For all

\displaystyle m_{j,n+1}=\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}}z\,\psi_{j,n}(z,y)\;\mathrm{d}y\,\check{\tau}_{a_{j,n}}(\mathrm{d}z)\right)^{-1}\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}}y\,z\,\psi_{j,n}(z,y)\;\mathrm{d}y\,\check{\tau}_{a_{j,n}}(\mathrm{d}z)\;,

(69)

\displaystyle a_{j,n+1}=2\,\kappa^{-1}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}}(z-\ln(z))\,\varphi_{j,n}(z,y)\;\mathrm{d}y\,\check{\tau}_{a_{j,n}}(\mathrm{d}z)\right)\;,

(70)

where, defining

\displaystyle\check{\tau}_{a}(\mathrm{d}z)=\mathbbm{1}_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}(z)\,\frac{(a/2)^{a/2}}{\Gamma(a/2)}z^{a/2-1}\mathrm{e}^{-za}\;\mathrm{d}z\quad\text{for any $a>0$}\;,

.

In the setting of

We have stated results that solve our maximisation and gradient-based approaches when the variational family is based on the exponential family. However, all the update formulas we have obtained so far involve intractable integrals. In the next section, we focus on the case of Gaussian Mixture Models optimisation seen in Examples

7  Gaussian Mixture Models (GMMs) Optimisation

We consider
d
-dimensional Gaussian mixture densities with
k(\theta_{j},y)=\mathcal{N}(y;m_{j},\Sigma_{j})
, where
\theta_{j}=(m_{j},\Sigma_{j})\in{\mathsf{T}}
denotes the mean and covariance matrix of the
j
-th Gaussian component density. By
2
, a valid update formula at time
n\geqslant 1
for
({\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{n}})_{n\geqslant 1}
is

\displaystyle\lambda_{j,n+1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda_{j,n}\left[\int_{\mathsf{Y}}\varphi_{j,n}^{(\alpha)}(y)\nu(\mathrm{d}y)+(\alpha-1)\kappa_{n}\right]^{\eta_{n}}}{\sum_{\ell=1}^{J}\lambda_{\ell,n}\left[\int_{\mathsf{Y}}\varphi_{\ell,n}^{(\alpha)}(y)\nu(\mathrm{d}y)+(\alpha-1)\kappa_{n}\right]^{\eta_{n}}}\;,\quad j=1\ldots J\;.

(74)

Furthermore,
as underlined in
3
, a valid update at time
n\geqslant 1
for the means
(m_{j,n+1})_{1\leqslant j\leqslant J}
and the covariances matrices
(\Sigma_{j,n+1})_{1\leqslant j\leqslant J}
is given by

\displaystyle\forall j=1\ldots J,\penalty\ m_{j,n+1}

\displaystyle=(1-\gamma_{j,n})m_{j,n}+\gamma_{j,n}\widehat{m}_{j,n}

(75)

\displaystyle\Sigma_{j,n+1}

\displaystyle=(1-\gamma_{j,n})\Sigma_{j,n}+\gamma_{j,n}\widehat{\Sigma}_{j,n}+\gamma_{j,n}(1-\gamma_{j,n})\left(\widehat{m}_{j,n}-m_{j,n}\right)\left(\widehat{m}_{j,n}-m_{j,n}\right)^{T}

where
\widehat{m}_{j,n}=\int_{\mathsf{Y}}y\,\check{\varphi}_{j,n}^{(\alpha)}(y)\;\nu(\mathrm{d}y)
,
\widehat{\Sigma}_{j,n}=\int_{\mathsf{Y}}yy^{T}\,\check{\varphi}_{j,n}^{(\alpha)}(y)\;\nu(\mathrm{d}y)-\widehat{m}_{j,n}\widehat{m}_{j,n}^{T}
with
\check{\varphi}_{j,n}^{(\alpha)}=\varphi_{j,n}^{(\alpha)}/\int\varphi_{j,n}^{(\alpha)}\nu(\mathrm{d}y)
and
\gamma_{j,n}\in(0,1]
. In addition, another possible update for the means is

\displaystyle m_{j,n+1}

\displaystyle=m_{j,n}+\gamma_{j,n}\frac{\int_{\mathsf{Y}}\lambda_{j,n}\varphi_{j,n}^{(\alpha)}(y)(y-m_{j,n})\nu(\mathrm{d}y)}{\int_{\mathsf{Y}}{\mu_{n}k(y)^{\alpha}p(y)^{1-\alpha}}\nu(\mathrm{d}y)}\;,\quad j=1\ldots J\;.

(76)

By virtue of
4
, this update can be used in lieu of the means update written in (
75
) with
\gamma_{j,n}
still in
(0,1]
. Since the update (
76
) is linked to Gradient Descent steps for Rényi’s
\alpha
-divergence minimisation (see
4
), we will refer to this update as the
Rényi Gradient Descent
(RGD) update in the following. One the other hand, the update on the means written in (
75
) will be referred to as the
Maximisation Gradient
(MG) update.

Notice that the updates (
74
), (
75
) and (
76
) all involve intractable integrals. However, we can resort to approximate update rules and take advantage of the fact that the integrals appearing in these update formulas are of the form

\displaystyle\int_{\mathsf{Y}}\varphi_{j,n}^{(\alpha)}(y)g(y)\nu(\mathrm{d}y)\;,

(77)

where
g
is a well-chosen function defined on
\mathsf{Y}
and where
\varphi_{j,n}^{(\alpha)}
is defined in (
16
) by

\varphi_{j,n}^{(\alpha)}(y)=k(\theta_{j,n},y)\left({\mu_{n}k(y)}/{p(y)}\right)^{\alpha-1}
for all
y\in\mathsf{Y}
. Many choices are indeed possible to approximate (
77
) and for simplicity we restrict ourselves to typical Importance Sampling estimation. Looking at the definition of
\varphi_{j,n}^{(\alpha)}
, a first idea would be to sample
M
i.i.d. random variables
(Y_{m,n}^{(j)})_{1\leqslant m\leqslant M}
according to
k(\theta_{j,n},\cdot)
for
j=1\ldots J
and to use the unbiased estimator of (
77

\displaystyle\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\left(\frac{\mu_{n}k(Y_{m,n}^{(j)})}{p(Y_{m,n}^{(j)})}\right)^{\alpha-1}g(Y_{m,n}^{(j)})\;.

As this sampler depends on
j
, this option becomes very computationally heavy as
J
and
M
increase due to the
sampling cost
(
J\times M
) and to the
evaluation cost
(
\mu_{n}k
and
p
need to be evaluated
M\times J
times each, where
\mu_{n}k
is a sum over
J
). To reduce this computational bottleneck, it is then preferable to consider sequences of samplers
(q_{n})_{n\geqslant n}
that do not depend on
j
at time
n
. More specifically, we discuss two possibilities.

(i)

Best sampler at time
n
(IS-n).
Approximated update rules can be obtained by sampling
M
i.i.d. random variables
(Y_{m,n})_{1\leqslant m\leqslant M}
according to the best approximation of
p
we have a time
n
, that is
q_{n}=\mu_{n}k
. Then, the samples are shared throughout the
J
mixture weights and mixture components parameters updates. This eases the computational burden, with both a sampling and an evaluation costs of
M
, as an unbiased estimator of (
77
) is

\displaystyle\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\hat{\varphi}_{j,n}^{(\alpha)}(Y_{m,n})g(Y_{m,n})\penalty\ \penalty\ \mbox{where}\penalty\ \penalty\ \hat{\varphi}_{j,n}^{(\alpha)}(y)=\frac{k(\theta_{j,n},y)}{q_{n}(y)}\left(\frac{\mu_{n}k(y)}{p(y)}\right)^{\alpha-1},\penalty\ y\in\mathsf{Y}\;.

(78)

(ii)

Uniform sampler (IS-unif).
Approximated update rules can be obtained by sampling according to the uniform sampler
q_{n}=J^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{J}k(\theta_{j,n},\cdot)
and using the unbiased estimator (
78
). That way, the sampling and evaluation costs are also
M
each. Contrary to IS-n, this sampler ensures a fair sampling among all components, as it entails sampling an index vector
[i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{M}]
uniformly from
\{1,\dots,J\}
whereas IS-n samples an index vector
[i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{M}]
according to the mixture weights
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{n}}
.

Remark 2
.

Approximated update rules for GMMs can be obtained by sampling according to
q_{n}=\mathcal{N}(\cdot;\boldsymbol{0}_{d},\boldsymbol{I}_{d})
, where
\boldsymbol{0}_{d}
is the null vector of dimension

,

if

with
\varepsilon_{m,n}\sim q_{n}
and by observing that an unbiased estimator of (

M^{-1}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\left({\mu_{n}k(Y_{m,n}^{(j)})}/{p(Y_{m,n}^{(j)})}\right)^{\alpha-1}g(Y_{m,n}^{(j)})
.
This choice of
q_{n}
relies on the existence of a reparameterisation and while it incurs a gain in terms of sampling cost as we only need
M
samples, the evaluation cost remains
M\times J
.

We thus have access to tractable algorithms that iteratively update both the weights and components parameters of a GMM by optimising the
\alpha
-divergence between the mixture distribution and the targeted distribution. Our framework also allows the use of learning rates
\gamma_{j,n}
that are dependent on

according to Algorithm

and move on to presenting our numerical experiments.

9  Conclusion

We introduced a novel methodology to build algorithms ensuring a monotonic decrease in the
\alpha
-divergence at each step. Our methodology enabled simultaneous updates for both the weights and components parameters of a given mixture model, making it suitable for capturing complex multimodal target densities. Our work also connected and improved on different approaches: Gradient Descent for
\alpha
-divergence minimisation, Power Descent and an Integrated EM algorithm. By investigating variational families based on the exponential family, we applied our framework to important classes of models such as Gaussian Mixture Models and Student’s
t
Mixture Models. Finally, we provided empirical evidence that our methodology can be used to enhance the aformentioned existing algorithms.

To conclude, we state several directions to extend our work. Now that we have established a systematic decrease for our iterative schemes, the next step could be to derive convergence results and to compare them with those obtained using typical Gradient Descent schemes. Based on
2
, another direction is to generalise the monotonicity property from
3
beyond the case
\alpha\in[0,1)
. Lastly, much like it is already the case in traditional Black-Box Variational Inference, we expect that fine-tuning our hyperparameters and resorting to more advanced Monte Carlo methods in the estimation of the intractable integrals appearing in our ideal algorithms will lead to further improved numerical results.
