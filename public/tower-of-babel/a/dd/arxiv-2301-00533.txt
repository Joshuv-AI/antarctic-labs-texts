Analytical comparison between X ⁡ ( 3 ) X(3) and X ⁡ ( 5 ) X(5) models of the Bohr Hamiltonian


Abstract
The 3-D Bohr-Mottelson Hamiltonian for γ \gamma -rigid prolate isotopes, known as X ⁡ ( 3 ) X(3) , is solved via inverse square potential having only one free parameter, β 0 \beta_{0} . The exact form of the wave functions and the energy spectra are obtained as a function of the free parameter of the potential that determines the changes in the spectra ratios and the B ⁡ ( E ​ 2 ) B(E2) . Since X ⁡ ( 3 ) X(3) is an exactly separable γ \gamma -rigid version of X ⁡ ( 5 ) X(5) , the solutions are compared with the X ⁡ ( 5 ) X(5) model and some new set of equations that show the relationships between the two models are stated. In other to show the dynamical symmetry nature of the solutions, the entire solutions from β 0 = 0 \beta_{0}=0 to β 0 = ∞ \beta_{0}=\infty are compared with U ⁡ ( 5 ) U(5) , X ⁡ ( 5 ) X(5) and S ​ U ​ ( 3 ) SU(3) . The solutions spread from the region around U ⁡ ( 5 ) U(5) over X ⁡ ( 5 ) X(5) and approach S ​ U ​ ( 3 ) SU(3) at β 0 = ∞ \beta_{0}=\infty . The exact solutions obtained via variational procedure are compared favourably with some existing X ⁡ ( 3 ) X(3) models found in the literature. The strong agreement between the present model and X ⁡ ( 3 ) X(3) via infinite square well potential is discussed. Twelve best critical point isotopes, 102 Mo, 104-108 Ru, 120-126 Xe, 148 Nd, 184-188 Pt are chosen for experimental realization of the model and moderate agreements are recorded. An excellent agreement which appears in the first β \beta -excited state in the comparison of the present model with three N = 90 N=90 isotones: 150 Nd, 154 Gd, and 156 Dy, known to be X ⁡ ( 5 ) X(5) candidates, suggests that the present model compensates the X ⁡ ( 5 ) X(5) models whose predictions are excellent in the ground states but moderately bad in the first β \beta -excited states.

Introduction

The establishment of the critical point symmetries by Iachello
1,2

1,2

Methodology of

It has been briefly stated, in the introductory section, that the standard Bohr Hamiltonian

(6)

The eigenvalue equation as regards to the Hamiltonian is

\hat{H}\Psi(\beta,\theta,\phi)=E\Psi(\beta,\theta,\phi).

(7)

By the usual method of separation of variable employed in some of our quantum texts

\Psi(\beta,\theta,\phi)=\chi(\beta)Y_{L,M}(\theta,\phi),

(8)

where

is the spherical harmonics and

-\left(\dfrac{1}{\sin\theta}\dfrac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\sin\theta\dfrac{\partial}{\partial\theta}+\dfrac{1}{\sin^{2}\theta}\dfrac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\phi^{2}}\right)Y_{L,M}(\theta,\phi)=L(L+1)Y_{L,M}(\theta,\phi),

(9)

where
L
is the angular momentum quantum number. After few steps, the simplified form of the radial part equation reads

\dfrac{d^{2}}{d\beta^{2}}\chi(\beta)+\dfrac{2}{\beta}\dfrac{d}{d\beta}\chi(\beta)-\dfrac{[L(L+1)+\beta_{0}]}{3\beta^{2}}\chi(\beta)+\epsilon\chi(\beta)=0,

(10)

where
\dfrac{2B}{\hbar^{2}}\dfrac{\beta_{0}}{\beta^{2}}=v(\beta)
has been considered as the reduced potential during the simplification and
\epsilon=\dfrac{2B}{\hbar^{2}}E
is the reduced energy.
