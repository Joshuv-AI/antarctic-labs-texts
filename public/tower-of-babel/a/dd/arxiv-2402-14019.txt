Maxentropy completion and properties of some partially defined Stationary Markov chains
Pierre Collet, Servet Martínez

Abstract
We consider a stationary Markovian evolution with values on a finite disjointly partitioned set space I ⊔ ℰ I\sqcup{\cal E} . The evolution is visible (in the sense of knowing the transition probabilities) on the states in I I but not for the states in ℰ {\cal E} . One only knows some partial information on the transition probabilities on ℰ {\cal E} , the input and output transition probabilities and some constraints of the transition probabilities on ℰ {\cal E} . Under some conditions we supply the transition probabilities on ℰ {\cal E} that satisfies the maximum entropy principle.



Keywords:
Hidden states, maximal entropy principle,
stationarity, incomplete Markov chain.

1
Introduction

Discrete time Markov chains on a finite state space are completely
described by their matrix of transitions probabilities. We consider a situation
where only a subset of the transition probabilities is known.
More precisely we consider an evolution on a set of states
I\sqcup{\cal E}

(disjoint union).
The states in
I
are visible and the set
{\cal E}
is called a
labyrinth (composed of invisible states).
In a first stage the states in
{\cal E}
act
as absorbing states but their persistence or reappearance
lead to consider these states in a different way.
The transition probabilities within the labyrinth are unknown.
One only knows some basic relations: the input and output
transition probabilities and eventually communication constraints in the
labyrinth. The transition probabilities between the states in
I
are
known and those between
I
and
{\cal E}
also.
We assume
I
and
{\cal E}
are nonempty sets
(there
are two mentions to the case
I=\emptyset
, in Remarks
3.2
) and
3.4
).

A natural question is to complete the description of the chain,
i.e. to find the
transition probabilities into the labyrinth so that the matrix of
transition probabilities is Markovian. There are many possible
solutions to this problem and
we propose a maximum entropy approach.

This partially defined process is an extremely simplified
model for some phenomena:

•

Disease epidemics that emerge and disappear
in time. A disease may emerge at some location due to human
contact with a biological reservoir of some microorganism,
diffuse in some geographical area, and when finished
retreat unseen to some biological reservoir again.
In this case the observable states represent the sizes of
the healthy and infected populations in geographic areas
while the labyrinth corresponds to the
biological reservoirs of the microorganism. A transition from
the labyrinth to an observable state is an outbreak;

•

Underground rivers, that can disappear at some sinkholes,
continues underground and reemerge further downstream. There can
be a cave system having an underground water dynamics. At a first
stage the caves can be considered absorbing states, but this
does not describe the whole phenomenon because the river reemerges
after an underground water evolution.

One can conceive many other concrete examples of partially
defined Markov processes.

We assume a stationary behavior of the
total process, not only on the visible states
I

but also for the evolution on the states
{\cal E}

(which is unseen). Given the
data (some transition probabilities associated with the states in
I
),
we try to reconstruct
the transition probabilities of the process in the unseen part by
using the maximum entropy principle. Our problem falls quite naturally
in this context. Some average quantities (probability of two successive
states) are known and we asked for some other similar quantities. As
described by Jaynes, ”…in making inference on the basis of partial
information we must use that probability distribution which has the
maximum entropy to whatever is known. This is the only unbiased
assignment we can make…”
[
11
]
p.623. See also

[
12
]
, and
[
16
]
Section 5.

Therefore, we assume we are given some transition probabilities
and the proposal for the remaining unknown ones is based upon
the maximum entropy principle.
We emphasize that we do not assume
sample paths of the Markov chain are available,
in other words we do not consider observations of a process
and no estimation is made.
There is a large literature devoted to estimate a transition matrix that fits
incomplete observation data, by using the maximum likelihood
criterion, like in
[
8
]
, or by some Bayesian approach, where
there is a prior of the transition matrix
and given the data one seeks to compute the posterior distribution,
see
[
15
]
.

The paper is organized as follows. In section
2
we describe
the set of known transition probabilities and introduce some more
hypothesis. We then derive the maximum entropy
solution satisfying the Markovian constraints.
We are aware that the hypothesis
(H1)
and
(H2)
are very strong, but up no now
they turn to be necessary for describing the
maxentropy behavior inside the labyrinth.

Section
3
is devoted to
the more general case
where a communication matrix inside the labyrinth is imposed.
If all the states communicate the maximum entropy solution is Bernoulli. But the
problem turns harder when the communication matrix is not trivial. In fact
there could be no matrix that satisfies the constraints. In Section
3.2

the constraints are written in a linear way and in Section
3.3
we
use Farkas’ lemma to give a criterion for the existence of a solution. In Proposition
3.6

we give a simple sufficient condition for the existence of solution.
In Section
3.4
we study the case when there are several labyrinths
that are connected only by the visible states, and show that all the computations related to
maximum entropy matrices are reduced to the case when there is a unique labyrinth.

In section
4
we relate our problem and hypothesis to the
theory of quasi stationary distributions. We show that under our
hypotheses the chain
can be reconstructed
from the evolution on the visible states and
in the labyrinth
separately, using families of independent geometric random variables
that mark the times of restarting.

As explained above we assume
only a subset of the
transition probabilities is known and we look to complete these transitions
probabilities by using the maximal entropy criterion. This makes part
of the problem where the transition matrix is known to belong to some
class of matrices, and one seeks the one that satisfies a certain
optimization criterion. This is the case in
[
10
]
, but the
optimization is on an infinite horizon reward on all the states.
In
[
3
]
the
unknown is a transition matrix that satisfies some general order
constraints on the coefficients and its stationary vector, which is
unknown, maximizes some linear functional. But, in our case the set of
constraints is more specific allowing to handle the optimization of
the entropy.

In
[
14
]
the inverse problem for partially observed Markov
chains with restart has been considered in a parametric setting for the unknown
transition probabilities and with an objective (cost) function different from
ours.
More precisely,
there is a transition probability kernel
p(\cdot,\cdot)
among the states and a distribution
{\tilde{p}}

on the states, and at each time the chain evolves with the kernel
p(\cdot,\cdot)
with probability

(1-\beta)
or it restarts with
{\tilde{p}}
with probability
\beta\in(0,1)
.
The process is assumed
to be ergodic and the authors look for for a stationary
probability. This search is based upon the knowledge of the stationary
vector up to a constant and the hitting probabilities prior to
restart, in a fixed region. The optimization is made on the parameters
guiding the evolution. There are some differences with our setting,
one of them
is that the optimization functions are different.

2
Partially defined Markov chains, hypothesis
and Maxentropy.

Our starting point is an irreducible stationary Markov chain,

X=(X_{n}:n\geq\mathbb{Z})
with values in the finite set
I\sqcup{\cal E}
. Its transition
matrix is denoted by
P=(P(a,b):a,b\in I\sqcup{\cal E})
and
\pi
denotes
stationary distribution, so
\pi^{t}P=\pi^{t}
.
The irreducibility of
P
implies
\pi>0
.
We will assume that the restricted transition matrix
P_{I,I}=(P(a,b):a,b\in I)
is also an
irreducible matrix.

By vectors we always mean
column vectors and we add the transposition superscript
t

to denote the associated row vector. We also denote

P_{J\times K}=(P(a,b):i\in J,k\in J)
the restricted submatrix
when
J,K\subseteq I\sqcup{\cal E}
.

When the chain
X
takes values in
I
, this is
visible. The states in the labyrinth
{\cal E}
can be distinguished and
visible only when there
is a nonzero probability of emerging from
{\cal E}
to
I
or when there
is a nonzero probability of transition from
some state in
I
to
{\cal E}
.
But once the chain is inside and before leaving the labyrinth
this is not visible.

Remark 2.1
.

With this notation, the visible process

A necessary condition in order that the pair

is

is that: for all

,
c\in I\sqcup{\cal E}\sqcup\{\mathfrak{e}\}
,

a_{k}\in I\sqcup{\cal E}
,

, one has:

\mathbb{P}({\cal O}_{n}=c\,|\,X_{k}=a_{k},k\leq n)=\mathbb{P}({\cal O}_{n}=c\,|\,X_{n}=a_{n}).

(1)

But, this condition

,
a_{n-1}\in I
,

. In fact,
since

then the visible state at coordinate

is

{\cal O}_{n}=X_{n}=a_{n}\in{\cal E}
which is different from
\mathfrak{e}
, and so

\mathbb{P}({\cal O}_{n}=\mathfrak{e}|X_{k}=a_{k},k\leq n)=0
. But
we can have

\mathbb{P}({\cal O}_{n}=\mathfrak{e}|X_{n}=a_{n})=\mathbb{P}(X_{n-1}\in{\cal E},X_{n}\in{\cal E},X_{n+1}\in{\cal E}|X_{n}=a_{n})>0
. This
is fulfilled in our case
when using
the hypothesis
(H2)
and the condition
\pi_{\cal E}>0
stated below.
Also, when it exists,
the maximum entropy matrix
P_{{\cal E}\times{\cal E}}
fulfills this condition, because
the labyrinth network is assumed to be irreducible (See Theorem
3.3
).

, relation

in Section 2, the above

) is part of

For the definition and applications of Hidden Markov models for

.

Let
\pi_{I}=(\pi(i):i\in I)

and
\pi_{\cal E}=(\pi(d):d\in{\cal E})
denote
the restrictions of
\pi
to
I
and
{\cal E}
respectively.

Summarizing, we assume
that the transition submatrices
P_{I\times I}
,
P_{I\times{\cal E}}
and

P_{{\cal E}\times I}
are known. In particular

P_{I\times(I\sqcup{\cal E})}
is known. We also assume that
\pi_{I}
is
known. These are the only available data.

If a large number of observations of the
dynamics of the chain is available, these data can be estimated
by averaging using the Law of Large Numbers
when the chain is in
I
and when
it enters from
I
to
{\cal E}
or emerges from
{\cal E}
to
I
.

We denote by
\pi(a)
the weight of
a\in I\sqcup{\cal E}
and by

\pi(A)=\sum_{a\in A}\pi(a)
the weight of
A\subseteq I\sqcup{\cal E}
.

We assume the existence of some states
i\in I

such that
P(i,{\cal E})>0
(where
P(i,{\cal E})=\sum_{d\in{\cal E}}P(i,d)
),
hence the restricted matrix
P_{I\times I}

is a substochastic kernel.

The first assumption we make is the following one:
when the chain emerges from the labyrinth
{\cal E}

to the visible states
I
, it is always with the stationary law

\pi_{I}
, so we assume

(H1)\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad P_{{\cal E}\times I}={{\bf{1}}}_{{\cal E}}\pi^{t}_{I}

where
{{\bf{1}}}_{{\cal E}}
is the unit vector of dimension the cardinal
number
|{\cal E}|
. This hypothesis means that the law of the outbreak on

I
has distribution
\pi^{t}_{I}
. In the case where a sample of the chain
is available
one could look for elaborating a test
that permits to analyze if hypothesis
(H1)
is satisfied.

Now we give a context for the next assumption.
Since
\pi
is stationary one has

\pi_{I}^{t}=\pi_{I}^{t}P_{I\times I}+\pi_{{\cal E}}^{t}P_{{\cal E}\times I}
and
by using
(H1)
one gets

\pi_{I}^{t}=\pi_{I}^{t}P_{I\times I}+\pi_{{\cal E}}^{t}{{\bf{1}}}_{{\cal E}}\pi^{t}_{I}=\pi_{I}^{t}P_{I\times I}+\pi({\cal E})\pi^{t}_{I}.

(Recall
\pi({\cal E})=\sum_{d\in{\cal E}}\pi(d)
,
\pi(I)=\sum_{i\in I}\pi(i)
).
Since
1-\pi({\cal E})=\pi(I)
, then a consequence
of
(H1)
is

\pi_{I}^{t}P_{I\times I}=\pi(I)\pi_{I}^{t},

(2)

and so
\pi_{I}
is the left Perron-Frobenius eigenvector
of
P_{I\times I}
with Perron-Frobenius eigenvalue
\pi(I)
.

We have only partial information on the transition matrix

P_{{\cal E}\times{\cal E}}
.
First, one has that all sums of the rows of
P_{{\cal E}\times{\cal E}}
are
constant because
P
is Markov and so from
(H1)
,

{{\bf{1}}}_{{\cal E}}=P_{{\cal E}\times I}{{\bf{1}}}_{I}+P_{{\cal E}\times{\cal E}}{{\bf{1}}}_{{\cal E}}={{\bf{1}}}_{{\cal E}}\pi_{I}^{t}{{\bf{1}}}_{I}+P_{{\cal E}\times{\cal E}}{{\bf{1}}}_{{\cal E}}=\pi(I){{\bf{1}}}_{\cal E}+P_{{\cal E}\times{\cal E}}{{\bf{1}}}_{{\cal E}}.

Then,

P_{{\cal E}\times{\cal E}}{{\bf{1}}}_{{\cal E}}=\pi({\cal E}){{\bf{1}}}_{\cal E}.

(3)

Therefore, a consequence of
(H1)
is that

\pi({\cal E})^{-1}P_{{\cal E}\times{\cal E}}
is a stochastic matrix.
We have that
\pi({\cal E})^{-1}\pi_{{\cal E}}
is a probability vector.
We assume the following property:

\pi({\cal E})^{-1}\pi_{{\cal E}}
is a stationary distribution for

\pi({\cal E})^{-1}P_{{\cal E}\times{\cal E}}
. This is equivalent to,

(H2)\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\pi({\cal E})\pi_{\cal E}^{t}=\pi_{\cal E}^{t}P_{{\cal E}\times{\cal E}}.

So,
\pi_{\cal E}
is the left Perron-Frobenius eigenvector of

P_{{\cal E}\times{\cal E}}
with Perron-Frobenius eigenvalue
\pi({\cal E})
.

Note that the hypothesis
(H1)
can be tested from observing
the transitions from the labyrinth to the visible states,
but this is not the case of
(H2)
.

From (
3
) and
(H2)
we have

\pi_{\cal E}^{t}P_{{\cal E}\times{\cal E}}{{\bf{1}}}_{\cal E}=\pi({\cal E})^{2}
.
The stationarity of
\pi
implies

\pi_{\cal E}^{t}=\pi_{I}^{t}P_{I\times{\cal E}}+\pi_{{\cal E}}^{t}P_{{\cal E}\times{\cal E}}

and so from
(H2)
one gets

\pi_{I}^{t}P_{I\times{\cal E}}=\pi_{{\cal E}}^{t}-\pi_{\cal E}^{t}P_{{\cal E}\times{\cal E}}=(1-\pi({\cal E}))\pi_{\cal E}=\pi(I)\pi_{\cal E}.

and then

\pi_{{\cal E}}=\pi(I)^{-1}\pi_{I}^{t}P_{I\times{\cal E}},

(4)

that is
\pi(e)=\pi(I)^{-1}\sum_{i\in I}\pi(i)P(i,e)
for
e\in{\cal E}
.

Hence, from hypotheses
(H1)
and
(H2)
one knows

\pi_{\cal E}
. As said, we have
\pi_{I}
and
P_{I\times{\cal E}}
from
the visible evolution. But, based upon observation of a sample, we cannot
test that
\pi({\cal E})^{-1}\pi_{\cal E}
is the stationary distribution for

\pi({\cal E})^{-1}P_{{\cal E}\times{\cal E}}
, which gives the
dynamics conditioned to be in
{\cal E}
. This occurs even if
(\pi_{I},\pi_{\cal E})

is the stationary distribution for the dynamics on
I\sqcup{\cal E}
.
This is why the hypothesis
(H2)
cannot be tested.

The entropy of the stationary Markov chain
X=(X_{n})

is given by,

h(X)=-\sum_{a\in I\sqcup{\cal E}}\pi(a)\sum_{b\in I\sqcup{\cal E}}P(a,b)\log P(a,b),

so, using (H1)

\displaystyle h(X)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sum_{d\in{\cal E}}\pi(d)\sum_{b\in I\sqcup{\cal E}}P(d,b)\log P(d,b)-\sum_{i\in I}\pi(i)\sum_{b\in I\sqcup{\cal E}}P(i,b)\log P(i,b)

(5)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sum_{i\in I}\pi(i)\sum_{b\in I\sqcup{\cal E}}P(i,b)\log P(i,b)-\sum_{d\in{\cal E}}\pi(d)\sum_{j\in I}\pi(j)\log\pi(j)

\displaystyle\;-\sum_{d\in{\cal E}}\pi(d)\sum_{e\in{\cal E}}P(d,e)\log P(d,e).

According to Jaynes maximal entropy principle
we look for maximizing
h(X)
, with
the following knowledge:

\pi_{I},P_{I\times(I\cup{\cal E})},P_{{\cal E}\times I}.

So, the first term of the (
5
) is fixed.
Now, from assumptions
(H1)
and
(H2)
we also know
\pi_{\cal E}
.
So, the second term in (
5
) is also fixed. Hence,
the maximization of
h(X)
is equivalent to maximize

H^{\prime}(P_{{\cal E}\times{\cal E}})=-\sum_{d\in{\cal E}}\pi(d)\sum_{e\in{\cal E}}P(d,e)\log P(d,e),

with
\pi_{\cal E}=\pi(I)^{-1}\pi_{I}^{t}P_{I\times{\cal E}}
known. So, one
seeks to
maximize
H^{\prime}(P_{{\cal E}\times{\cal E}})
with the matrix
P_{{\cal E}\times{\cal E}}

subject to (
3
) and
(H2)
.

3
Partially defined Markov chains with communication constraints.

This section will be mainly devoted to the case where there are some communications constraints
among the states of the labyrinth. Firstly we will consider the case when there
is no constraint of this type, in other words the states in
{\cal E}
communicate among themselves.

3.1
When all states in the labyrinth communicate

Theorem 3.1
.

When the unique constraints are (
3
) and
(H2)
,
the maximum entropy completion of the chain is given by

P_{{\cal E}\times{\cal E}}={{\bf{1}}}_{\cal E}\pi_{\cal E}^{t}
,
that is the transitions are Bernoulli:
P(d,e)=\pi(e)
,
d,e\in{\cal E}
.

Proof.

When the unique constraints are (
3
) and
(H2)
,
the maximum of
H^{\prime}(P_{{\cal E}\times{\cal E}})
is obtained with

P_{{\cal E}\times{\cal E}}={{\bf{1}}}_{\cal E}\pi_{\cal E}^{t}
.
This is well-known and can be proved as follows,

H^{\prime}(P_{{\cal E}\times{\cal E}})=\sum_{d\in{\cal E}}\pi(d)\sum_{e\in{\cal E}}P(d,e)\log(\pi(e)/P(d,e))-\sum_{d\in{\cal E}}\pi(d)\sum_{e\in{\cal E}}P(d,e)\log\pi(e).

By using
\log(x)\leq x-1
and (
3
) we get,

\displaystyle\sum_{d\in{\cal E}}\pi(d)\sum_{e\in{\cal E}}P(d,e)\log(\pi(e)/P(d,e))\leq\sum_{d\in{\cal E}}\pi(d)\sum_{e\in{\cal E}}P(d,e)(\pi(e)/P(d,e)-1)

\displaystyle\;\leq\sum_{d\in{\cal E}}\pi(d)\sum_{e\in{\cal E}}\pi(e)-\sum_{d\in{\cal E}}\pi(d)\sum_{e\in{\cal E}}P(d,e)=\pi({\cal E})^{2}-\pi({\cal E})^{2}=0.

Hence,

\displaystyle H^{\prime}(P_{{\cal E}\times{\cal E}})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle-\sum_{d\in{\cal E}}\pi(d)\sum_{e\in{\cal E}}P(d,e)\log(\pi(e))=-\sum_{e\in{\cal E}}\log(\pi(e))\sum_{d\in{\cal E}}\pi(d)P(d,e)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\pi({\cal E})\sum_{e\in{\cal E}}\pi(e)\log(\pi(e)),

where
(H2)
was used to establish the last inequality.
This inequality is an equality
for the transitions
P(d,e)=\pi(e)
with
d,e\in{\cal E}
.
Hence, in the absence of other constraint the maximum of the entropy
is realized with a Bernoulli.
∎

3.2
Constraints on the communication
matrix in the labyrinth.

Let us define
{\widehat{\pi}}
on

the normalized restrictions
to
I
and
{\cal E}
,
that is
{\widehat{\pi}}={\widehat{\pi}}_{I}
on

and
{\widehat{\pi}}={\widehat{\pi}}_{\cal E}
on

,
where

{\widehat{\pi}}_{I}=(\pi(I))^{-1}\pi_{I}\,\hbox{ and }\,{\widehat{\pi}}_{\cal E}=(\pi({\cal E}))^{-1}\pi_{\cal E}.

These are probability vectors on

and
{\cal E}

We define

From (

We denote by

\displaystyle H^{\prime}(P_{{\cal E}\times{\cal E}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sum_{d\in{\cal E}}\pi(d)\sum_{e\in{\cal E}}P(d,e)\log P(d,e)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\pi({\cal E})^{2}\sum_{d\in{\cal E}}{\widehat{\pi}}(d)\sum_{e\in{\cal E}}{\widehat{P}}(d,e)\log(\pi({\cal E}){\widehat{P}}(d,e))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\pi({\cal E})^{2}h(Z)+\pi({\cal E})^{2}\log(\pi({\cal E})).

is known and (

of the Markov shift

,

We will assume one also knows the communication matrix

L=(L(e,d):e,d\in{\cal E})
in the labyrinth. This information

means the state

with

In other words
for all

there is a path
d_{0}=d,..,d_{k}=e
such that
L(d_{i},d_{i+1})=1

for

. For

we define,

L_{d}=\{e\in{\cal E}:L(d,e)=1\}\hbox{ and }L^{d}=\{e\in{\cal E}:L(e,d)=1\},

which are the set of states that follow

and the set of states
that precede

, respectively. By definition,

if and only if

. We assume that the transition

{\widehat{P}}
satisfies

(H3)\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad L(d,e)=0\Rightarrow{\widehat{P}}(d,e)=0.

So, we must maximize

h(Z)=-\sum_{d\in{\cal E}}{\widehat{\pi}}(d)\sum_{e\in L_{d}}{\widehat{P}}(d.e)\log{\widehat{P}}(d,e),

subject to the set constraints

\displaystyle\forall d\in{\cal E}:\;\sum_{e\in L_{d}}{\widehat{P}}(d,e)=1\,;

(6)

\displaystyle\forall e\in{\cal E}:\;\sum_{d\in L^{e}}{\widehat{\pi}}(d){\widehat{P}}(d,e)={\widehat{\pi}}(e)\,.

(7)

Theorem 3.3
.

Assuming that the constraints (
6
) and (
7
) are
feasible, the maximum entropy completion of the chain satisfying

is given by

{\widehat{P}}(d,e)=\alpha(d)\beta(e){\bf 1}(e\in L_{d})\;,

where

\forall e\in{\cal E}:\;\beta(e)=\frac{{\widehat{\pi}}(e)}{\sum_{d\in L^{e}}{\widehat{\pi}}(d)\frac{1}{\sum_{c\in L_{d}}\beta(c)}}\;,

3.3
Associated linear problem

The hypothesis
(H3)
and constraints (
6
),
(
7
), can be put in the
following form for the matrix
{\widehat{P}}\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\forall d\in{\cal E}:\;\sum_{e\in{\cal E}}{\widehat{P}}(d,e)=1\,;

(13)

\displaystyle\forall e\in{\cal E}:\;\sum_{d\in{\cal E}}{\widehat{\pi}}(d){\widehat{P}}(d,e)={\widehat{\pi}}(e)\,;

(14)

\displaystyle\forall d,e\in{\cal E}:\;L(d,e)=0\Rightarrow{\widehat{P}}(d,e)=0.

(15)

Let us put the whole problem, in particular these constraints,
in a vector form.

Let
\ell=|{\cal E}|
. We assume

{\cal E}=\{1,\dots,\ell\}
and so
{\widehat{P}}=\{{\widehat{P}}(i,j):i,j=1,\dots,\ell)
.
We define a column vector
{\vec{p}}=(p(k);k=1,\cdots,\ell^{2})

representing the matrix
{\widehat{P}}
. We set

p(i+(j-1)\ell)={\widehat{P}}(i,j),i,j=1,\dots,\ell,

so we group sequentially the columns of
{\widehat{P}}
, in fact

(p(i+(j-1)\ell):i=1,\cdots,\ell)
is the
j-
the column of

{\widehat{P}}
. Since
{\widehat{P}}\geq 0
one has
{\vec{p}}\geq 0
. We also write

{\widehat{\pi}}_{\cal E}=({\widehat{\pi}}(i):i=1,\dots,\ell)
. Consider the Kronecker function

\delta(a,b)=\begin{cases}1&\hbox{ if }a=b\,;\\
0&\hbox{ if }a\neq b.\end{cases}

Define the following three
matrices
A
,
B
,
C
, of dimension
\ell\times\ell^{2}
.
For
r,s,t\in\{1,\dots,\ell\}
set,

\displaystyle A(r,t+(s-1)\ell)=\delta(t,r);

\displaystyle B(r,t+(s-1)\ell)={\widehat{\pi}}(t)\delta(s,r);

\displaystyle C(r,t+(s-1)\ell)=(1-L(t,r))\delta(s,r).

We have

\sum_{t=1}^{\ell}\sum_{s=1}^{\ell}A(r,t+(s-1)\ell)p(t+(s-1)\ell)=\sum_{s=1}^{\ell}p(r+(s-1)\ell)=\sum_{s=1}^{\ell}{\widehat{P}}(r,s).

Then, the restriction
{\widehat{P}}{{\bf{1}}}_{\cal E}={{\bf{1}}}_{\cal E}
(
13
),
is equivalent to
A{\vec{p}}={{\bf{1}}}
with
{{\bf{1}}}
the unit vector of size
\ell
.
On the other hand

\sum_{t=1}^{\ell}\sum_{s=1}^{\ell}B(r,t+(s-1)\ell)p(t+(s-1)\ell)=\sum_{t=1}^{\ell}{\widehat{\pi}}(t)p(t+(r-1)\ell)=\sum_{t=1}^{\ell}{\widehat{\pi}}(t){\widehat{P}}(t,r).

Then, the restriction
{\widehat{\pi}}^{t}{\widehat{P}}={\widehat{\pi}}^{t}
(
14
) is equivalent to

B{\vec{p}}={\widehat{\pi}}
. Finally, since
L(\cdot,\cdot)
is
0-1
valued
we get,

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{\ell}\sum_{s=1}^{\ell}C(r,t+(s-1)\ell)p(t+(s-1)\ell)=\sum_{t=1}^{\ell}(1-L(t,r))p(t+(r-1)\ell)

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{\ell}(1-L(t,r)){\widehat{P}}(t,r)=\sum_{t\in\{1,..,\ell\}:L(t,r)=0}{\widehat{P}}(t,r).

Since the restriction (
15
)
is equivalent to
L(t,r)=0
implies
{\widehat{P}}(t,r)=0
for all
t,r
, then
it is equivalent to
C{\vec{p}}={\bf 0}
, the zero vector of size
\ell
.

We have proven that there exists

{\widehat{P}}\geq 0
such that the conditions (
13
), (
14
),
(
15
), are fulfilled if and only if we have,

D{\vec{p}}={\vec{b}},\;{\vec{p}}\geq 0,

where

D=(D(r,t+(s-1)\ell):r=1,..,3\ell,t,s=1,..,\ell)

is a
3\ell\times\ell^{2}
matrix whose coefficients are given by

D(r,t+(s-1)\ell)=\begin{cases}A(r,t+(s-1)\ell)&\hbox{ if }1\leq r\leq\ell,\\
B(r-\ell,t+(s-1)\ell)&\hbox{ if }\ell+1\leq r\leq 2\ell,\\
C(r-2\ell,t+(s-1)\ell)&\hbox{ if }2\ell+1\leq r\leq 3\ell\,;\end{cases}

and
{\vec{b}}
is a
3\ell-
dimensional vector given by

b(r)=\begin{cases}1&\hbox{ if }1\leq r\leq\ell,\\
{\widehat{\pi}}(r-\ell)&\hbox{ if }\ell+1\leq r\leq 2\ell,\\
0&\hbox{ if }2\ell+1\leq r\leq 3\ell\,.\end{cases}

By Farkas’ Lemma (see Proposition 3.2.1 p. 170 in
[
1
]
)
there exists
{\vec{p}}\geq 0
satisfying
D{\vec{p}}={\vec{b}}

or there exists some
y\in\mathbb{R}^{3\ell}
satisfying

D^{t}y\geq 0
and
b^{t}y<0
. Let us set
y^{t}=(u^{t},v^{t},w^{t})
with

be
\ell-
dimensional vector. Then we have,

b^{t}y=\sum_{r=1}^{\ell}\big(u(r)+v(r){\widehat{\pi}}(r)\big)

and so the condition

is equivalent to

\sum_{r=1}^{\ell}(u(r)+{\widehat{\pi}}(r)v(r))<0.

(16)

We have that,

(y^{t}D)(t+(s-1)\ell)=u(t)+v(s){\widehat{\pi}}(t)+w(s)(1-L(t,s)).

Then, the condition
D^{t}y\geq 0
is equivalent to

\forall t,s=1,\dots,\ell:\quad u(t)+v(s){\widehat{\pi}}(t)+w(s)(1-L(t,s))\geq 0.

(17)

Proposition 3.5
.

The conditions (
16
) and (
17
) are
equivalent to (
16
) and (
18
), with

\forall t,s\hbox{ such that }L(t,s)=1:\quad u(t)+v(s){\widehat{\pi}}(t)\geq 0.

Proof.

If (

) is fulfilled then the choice

) and (

3.4
Non-connected labyrinths

When there is a set of disjoint labyrinths that are only
connected through the states in
I
the analysis is analogous.
Let us briefly sketch it. As before,
X
is an irreducible stationary
Markov chain,
with values in the set

with
{\cal E}=\bigsqcup_{m\in M}{\cal E}_{m}

The transitions among the labyrinths

{\cal E}_{m}
and
{\cal E}_{m^{\prime}}
are only connected through
I
.
So, for all

Let
\pi_{I}=(\pi(i):i\in I)

\pi_{{\cal E}_{m}}=(\pi(d):d\in{\cal E}_{m})
denote
the restrictions of
\pi
to
I
and
{\cal E}_{m}
respectively, for
m\in M
.
We assume
that the transition submatrices
P_{I\times I}
,
P_{I\times{\cal E}}
,

P_{{\cal E}\times I}
and the vector
\pi_{I}
are known.

The assumption
(H1)
now reads:

P_{{\cal E}_{m}\times I}={{\bf{1}}}_{{\cal E}_{m}}\pi^{t}_{I}
for all

, so
from all the labyrinths the law of the outbreak on

has distribution
\pi^{t}_{I}
.

Equation (
2
),
\pi_{I}^{t}P_{I\times I}=\pi(I)\pi^{t}

is also deduced from
(H1)
. From
(H1)
we also get,

{{\bf{1}}}_{{\cal E}_{m}}=P_{{\cal E}_{m}\times I}{{\bf{1}}}_{I}+\sum_{m^{\prime}\neq m}P_{{\cal E}_{m}\times{\cal E}_{m^{\prime}}}{{\bf{1}}}_{{\cal E}_{m^{\prime}}}+P_{{\cal E}_{m}\times{\cal E}_{m}}{{\bf{1}}}_{{\cal E}_{m}}=\pi(I){{\bf{1}}}_{{\cal E}_{m}}+P_{{\cal E}_{m}\times{\cal E}_{m}}{{\bf{1}}}_{{\cal E}_{m}}.

Since
\pi({\cal E})=1-\pi(I)
, (
3

P_{{\cal E}_{m}\times{\cal E}_{m}}{{\bf{1}}}_{{\cal E}_{m}}=\pi({\cal E}){{\bf{1}}}_{{\cal E}_{m}}
.
We assume that

\pi({\cal E}_{m})^{-1}\pi_{{\cal E}_{m}}
is a stationary distribution for

\pi({\cal E})^{-1}P_{{\cal E}\times{\cal E}}
. So
(H2)
becomes

\pi({\cal E})\pi_{{\cal E}_{m}}^{t}=\pi_{{\cal E}_{m}}^{t}P_{{\cal E}_{m}\times{\cal E}_{m}}

m\in M
.

The same arguments show that (
4

\pi_{{\cal E}_{m}}=\pi(I)^{-1}\pi_{I}^{t}P_{I\times{\cal E}_{m}}
for all
m\in M
.
Hence, all the vectors
\pi_{{\cal E}_{m}}
are known.
The entropy of the stationary Markov chain

is,

\displaystyle h(X)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sum_{i\in I}\pi(i)\sum_{a\in I\sqcup{\cal E}}P(i,a)\log P(i,a)-\sum_{d\in{\cal E}}\pi(d)\sum_{j\in I}\pi(j)\log\pi(j)

\displaystyle\;-\sum_{d\in{\cal E}}\pi(d)\sum_{e\in{\cal E}}P(d,e)\log P(d,e).

Since all
\pi_{{\cal E}_{m}}=\pi(I)^{-1}\pi_{I}^{t}P_{I\times{\cal E}_{m}}

is equivalent to maximize

H^{\prime}(P_{{\cal E}\times{\cal E}})=-\sum_{d\in{\cal E}}\pi(d)\sum_{e\in{\cal E}}P(d,e)\log P(d,e)

Since

So, it is equivalent to
maximize
H^{\prime}(P_{{\cal E}_{m}\times{\cal E}_{m}})
with
P_{{\cal E}_{m}\times{\cal E}_{m}}

subject to
P_{{\cal E}_{m}\times{\cal E}_{m}}{{\bf{1}}}_{{\cal E}_{m}}\!=\!\pi({\cal E}){{\bf{1}}}_{{\cal E}_{m}}
and

\pi_{{\cal E}_{m}}\!=\!\pi(I)^{-1}\pi_{I}^{t}P_{I\times{\cal E}_{m}}
for

.
Then, the analysis is the same as before, but for each one of the matrices

.

4.1
Quasi-stationarity

We can use the quasi-stationary theory, see
[
4
]
, to
illustrate how the chain
X
restarts
its evolution when it exits from
I
or from
{\cal E}
.

When starting from
X_{0}\in I
, let
\tau_{\cal E}=\inf\{n>0:X_{n}\in{\cal E}\}
be the hitting time of
{\cal E}
.
By iterating (
2
)
we get
{\widehat{\pi}}_{I}^{t}P^{n}_{I\times I}=\pi(I)^{n}{\widehat{\pi}}_{I}^{t}
,
and so
\mathbb{P}_{\widehat{\pi}}(\tau_{\cal E}>n)=\pi(I)^{n}
, Then,

\mathbb{P}_{{\widehat{\pi}}_{I}}(X_{n}=j\,|\,\tau_{\cal E}>n)={\widehat{\pi}}_{I}(j),j\in I,n\geq 0,

that is
{\widehat{\pi}}_{I}
is a quasi-stationary distribution (q.s.d.)
of the Markov chain
X^{(I)}=(X_{n}:n<\tau_{\cal E})
with states
in
I
and killed at
\tau_{\cal E}
. So, When
starting from
{\widehat{\pi}}_{I}
the hitting time of
{\cal E}
is geometrically distributed,

\tau_{\cal E}\sim
Geometric
(\pi({\cal E}))
(firstly shown in
[
6
]
for q.s.d.’s).

Starting with
{\widehat{\pi}}_{I}
the exit distribution from
I
is
given by

\displaystyle\forall d\!\in\!{\cal E}\!:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{{\widehat{\pi}}_{I}}(X_{\tau_{\cal E}}\!=\!d)\!=\!\sum_{n\geq 1}\mathbb{P}(X_{n}=d,\tau_{\cal E}\!=\!n)\!=\!\sum_{n\geq 1}\mathbb{P}_{{\widehat{\pi}}_{I}}(X_{n}=d,\tau_{\cal E}\!>\!n\!-\!1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 1}\mathbb{P}(X_{n}=d|\tau_{\cal E}\!>\!n-1)\mathbb{P}_{{\widehat{\pi}}_{I}}(\tau_{\cal E}\!>\!n\!-\!1)\!=\!\sum_{n\geq 1}(\sum_{i\in I}{\widehat{\pi}}(i)P(i,d))\pi(I)^{n-1}.

From (
4
) we get
\pi(d)\!=\!\sum_{i\in I}{\widehat{\pi}}(i)P(i,d)
, and so

\mathbb{P}_{{\widehat{\pi}}_{I}}(X_{{\cal E}}\!=\!d)\!=\!\pi(d)(1\!-\!\pi(I))^{-1}
. Then, the exit law from
I
is
{\widehat{\pi}}_{\cal E}
:

\forall d\in{\cal E}:\quad\mathbb{P}_{{\widehat{\pi}}_{I}}(X_{\tau_{\cal E}}=d)={\widehat{\pi}}(d).

(19)

Similar computations give that when starting from
{\widehat{\pi}}_{I}
,
X_{\tau_{\cal E}}
and
\tau_{\cal E}
are independent,

\forall d\in{\cal E},n\geq 1:\quad\mathbb{P}_{{\widehat{\pi}}_{I}}(X_{\tau_{\cal E}}=d,\tau_{\cal E}=n)={\widehat{\pi}}(d)\mathbb{P}_{{\widehat{\pi}}_{I}}(\tau_{\cal E}=n).

Similarly, let

\tau_{I}=\inf\{n>0:X_{n}\in I\}
be the hitting time of
I
. Then, the hypothesis
(H2)

implies that
{\widehat{\pi}}_{\cal E}
is a q.s.d. of the Markov

with states in
{\cal E}
killed at

\tau_{I}
. So,
\mathbb{P}_{{\widehat{\pi}}_{\cal E}}(X_{n}=d\,|\,\tau_{I}>n)={\widehat{\pi}}_{\cal E}(d),d\in{\cal E},n\geq 0
,
and
\tau_{I}\sim
Geometric
(\pi(I))
.
Let us see that when starting with
{\widehat{\pi}}_{\cal E}
, the exit distribution from
{\cal E}
is
{\widehat{\pi}}_{I}
. For every

\mathbb{P}_{{\widehat{\pi}}_{\cal E}}(X_{\tau_{I}}\!=\!i)\!=\!\!\sum_{n\geq 1}\mathbb{P}_{{\widehat{\pi}}_{\cal E}}(X_{n}=i|\tau_{I}\!>\!n\!-\!1)\mathbb{P}_{{\widehat{\pi}}_{\cal E}}(\tau_{I}\!>\!n\!-\!1)\!\!=\!\sum_{n\geq 1}\!\pi({\cal E})^{n-1}(\sum_{e\in{\cal E}}\!{\widehat{\pi}}(e)P(e,i)).

(H1)

Also

and

(X_{0},..,X_{k},\tau_{I}>k)

]

Z^{n}_{0}=d
. Hence
Z^{n}_{0}(d)=d
, it evolves with kernel
P

and its killing time is
\tau^{n}(d)=\tau^{n}
.

To construct a copy

W=(W_{t}:t\in\mathbb{Z})
of
X
we cut the trajectory

Y
at some random places
and insert copies of the killed trajectories on
{\cal E}
. This will define a process
W

with states in
I\cup{\cal E}
with the same conditional probabilities as
X
.
The initial distribution of
W
will satisfy
W_{0}\sim\pi
,
then the processes
W
and
X
will be equally distributed.

Let
{\cal G}=(G_{n}(i,j):i\in I,j\in I,n\geq 0)
be an array of independent Bernoulli random variables with

G_{n}(i,j)\sim G(i,j)
for all
n\geq 0
and such that
\mathbb{P}(G(i,j)=1)=\theta(i,j)
,
\mathbb{P}(G(i,j)=0)=1-\theta(i,j)
,
with

\theta(i,j)\!=\!\frac{P(i,j)}{Q(i,j)},\;\;(1-\theta(i,j))=\frac{P(i,{\cal E}){\widehat{\pi}}(j)}{Q(i,j)}

(26)

Then,
Q(i,j)\theta(i,j)=P(i,j)
and

\sum_{j\in I}Q(i,j)(1-\theta(i,j))\frac{P(i,d)}{P(i,{\cal E})}=\sum_{j\in I}{\widehat{\pi}}(j)P(i,d)=\pi(d),

where the last equality follows from (
4
).
The array
{\cal G}
and a part of the construction that follows was already considered in
[
13
]
.

We define recursively a process
(W_{t}:t\in\mathbb{Z})

and a sequence of random times
(S_{n}:n\in\mathbb{Z})
which will be the times of presence of the process

W

in
I
and the random variables
{\cal G}
will help us to describe the switching of
W
from
I
to
{\cal E}
.

Firstly, let
Z^{\prime}=(Z^{\prime}_{k}:0\leq k<\tau^{\prime})
be a copy of
Z
, independent of
(Z^{n}:n\geq 0)
with
killing time
\tau^{\prime}\sim\tau_{I}
. We define,

W_{t}=Z^{\prime}_{t+1}\hbox{ for }0\leq t<\tau^{\prime}-1,\;W_{\tau^{\prime}-1}\sim{\widehat{\pi}}_{I}\hbox{ and }S_{0}=\tau^{\prime}-1.

Therefore
S_{0}\sim
Geometric
(\pi(I))-1

and we have

S_{0}=0
if and only if
\tau^{\prime}=1
. So,
\mathbb{P}(S_{0}=0)=\mathbb{P}_{{\widehat{\pi}}_{\cal E}}(\tau_{I}=1)=\pi(I)
.
On the other hand we have, when
S_{0}=0
then
W_{0}\sim\pi_{I}
and when
S_{0}>0

we have
W_{0}=Z^{\prime}_{1}\sim{\widehat{\pi}}_{\cal E}
.
We conclude that

W_{0}\sim\pi(I){\widehat{\pi}}_{i}+\pi({\cal E}){\widehat{\pi}}_{\cal E}=\pi.

which is the initial distribution of
X
. The variable
Z^{\prime}_{0}\sim{\widehat{\pi}}_{\cal E}
is fixed later.

\bullet
Let
n\geq 0
and assume
we have defined
(S_{l}:0\leq l\leq n)
, then we continue with step
n+1
.

If
G_{n}(Y_{n},Y_{n+1})=1
then we put
S_{n+1}=S_{n}+1
,
W_{S_{n+1}}=Y_{n+1}
and we
continue with step
n+2
.

If
G_{n}(Y_{n},Y_{n+1})=0
then we put
S_{n+1}=S_{n}+\tau^{n}
,

W_{S_{n}+t}=Z^{n}_{t-1}(U_{n}(Y_{n}))
for
1\leq t\leq\tau^{n}-1
and

W_{S_{n+1}}=Y_{n+1}
. In particular

W_{S_{n}+1}=Z^{n}_{0}(U_{n}(Y_{n}))=U_{n}(Y_{n})
. We continue with step
n+2
.

This construction for
n=0
is visualized in Figures
1
and
2
.

We define
S_{-1}=-\tau^{-1}
and
W_{S_{-1}}=Y_{-1}
.
If
S_{-1}=-1
we take
Z^{\prime}_{0}\sim{\widehat{\pi}}_{\cal E}

and
Z^{\prime}
is chosen independent of the sequence
(Z^{n}:n\in\mathbb{Z})
.
If
-S_{-1}\geq 2
, we take
W_{S_{-1}+t}=Z^{-1}_{t-1}(U_{-1}(Y_{-1}))
for
1\leq t\leq\tau^{-1}-1
.
In this case,
in the definition of
S_{0}

we set
Z^{\prime}_{0}=Z^{-1}_{\tau^{-1}-2}(U_{-1}(Y_{-1}))
.

\bullet
Let
n\leq-1
and assume we have already defined

(S_{l}:n\leq l\leq-1)
, then we continue with step
n-1
.

If
G_{n-1}(Y_{n-1},Y_{n})=1
then we put
S_{n-1}=S_{n}-1
,
W_{S_{n-1}}=Y_{n}
and we
continue with step
n-2
.

If
G_{n-1}(Y_{n-1},Y_{n})=0
then we put
S_{n-1}=S_{n}-\tau^{n-1}
,

W_{S_{n-1}+t}=Z^{n}_{t-1}(U_{n-1}(Y_{n-1}))
for
1\leq t\leq\tau^{n-1}-1
and

W_{S_{n-1}}=Y_{n-1}
. In particular

W_{S_{n-1}+1}=U_{n-1}(Y_{n-1})
. We continue with step
n-2
.

Hence, we have defined
(W_{t}:t\in\mathbb{Z})
and
{\mathfrak{S}}=\{S_{n}:n\in\mathbb{Z}\}
. Note that

\mathbb{P}(\lim_{n\to\infty}S_{n}=\infty,\;\lim_{n\to-\infty}S_{n}=-\infty)=1.

(27)

Theorem 4.1
.

The processes
W
and
X
have the same law.

Proof.

From definition and since
W_{S_{0}}\sim{\widehat{\pi}}_{I}
, we have
(W_{S_{n}}:n\in\mathbb{Z})=(Y_{n}:n\in\mathbb{Z})
. Then,

\forall n\in\mathbb{Z}:W_{S_{n}}\sim{\widehat{\pi}}_{I}.

(28)

Let us show that

\forall n\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}:S_{n+1}-S_{n}\sim\hbox{Geometric}(\pi(I)).

(29)

From the construction, (
28
) and (
2
), we have,

\mathbb{P}(S_{n+1}\!-\!S_{n}=1)\!=\!\sum_{i\in I}\sum_{j\in I}{\widehat{\pi}}(i)Q(i,j)\theta(i,j)\!=\!\sum_{j\in I}\sum_{i\in I}{\widehat{\pi}}(j)P(i,j)\!=\!\sum_{i\in I}\pi(j)=\pi(I).

From the construction we have for all
n\geq 2
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(S_{n+1}-S_{n}=n)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in I}\sum_{d\in{\cal E}}\sum_{j\in I}{\widehat{\pi}}(i)Q(i,j)(1-\theta(i,j))\frac{P(i,d)}{P(i,{\cal E})}\pi({\cal E})^{n-2}\pi(I)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in I}\sum_{i\in I}\sum_{d\in{\cal E}}{\widehat{\pi}}(i){\widehat{\pi}}(j)P(i,d)\pi({\cal E})^{n-2}\pi(I)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\sum_{j\in I}{\widehat{\pi}}(j))(\sum_{d\in{\cal E}}\sum_{i\in I}{\widehat{\pi}}(i)P(i,d))\pi({\cal E})^{n-2}\pi(I)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\pi(I)\pi({\cal E})^{n-1},

where we used
\sum_{j\in I}{\widehat{\pi}}(j)P(j,d)=\pi(d)
that follows from (
4
).
Since
S_{0}\sim\hbox{Geometric}(\pi(I))-1
and
-S_{-1}\sim\hbox{Geometric}(\pi(I))
, the sequence

(S_{n}:n\in\mathbb{Z})
is a stationary renewal sequence in
\mathbb{Z}
,
see (
21
), (
22
), (
23
) and (
24
).
Then,

\forall t\geq 0:\mathbb{P}(t\in{\mathfrak{S}})=\pi(I).

(30)

Now define
\mathfrak{N}=\{n\in\mathbb{Z}:S_{n+1}-S_{n}\geq 2\}
that index
the nonempty connected components of
\mathbb{Z}\setminus{\mathfrak{S}}
. When
n\neq 0
, we have
n\in\mathfrak{N}
if

\tau^{n}\geq 2
. From the construction
the class of sequences

\left((W_{t}:t\!=\!S_{n}\!+\!1,...,S_{n+1}\!-\!1):n\!\in\!\mathfrak{N}\right)
are i.i.d. and

\forall n\!\in\!\mathfrak{N},n\neq 0:(W_{t}:t\!=\!S_{n}\!+\!1,...,S_{n+1}\!-\!1)\!\sim\!(Z_{k}:k\!=\!0,...,\tau-2).

(31)

On the other hand if
S_{0}-S_{-1}>1
we have,

(W_{t}:t=S_{-1}+1,...,{S_{0}}-1)=(Z^{-1}_{0},...Z^{-1}_{\tau^{-1}-2},Z^{\prime}_{1},...,Z^{\prime}_{\tau^{\prime}-1}),

(32)

where
Z^{-1}\sim Z(U_{-1}(Y_{-1}))
and
Z^{\prime}=(Z^{\prime}_{0}=Z^{-1}_{\tau^{-1}-2},...,Z^{\prime}_{\tau^{\prime}-1})\sim Z
.

It is left to show that
W
is a Markov chain with the same transition
probabilities as
X
. Let

m\in\mathbb{Z}
. Assume
W_{m}=a,W_{m+1}=b
.

Let
a,b\in I
, or
a,b\in{\cal E}
, or
a\in I,b\in{\cal E}
. From
the definition of
W
, for all
a_{-l}\in I\cup{\cal E}
,
l\geq 1
, and
for any
m\in\mathbb{Z}
one has

\mathbb{P}(W_{m+1}\!=\!b|W_{m}\!=\!a,W_{m-l}\!=\!a_{-l},l\geq 1)\!=\!\mathbb{P}(W_{m+1}\!=\!b|W_{m}\!=\!a).

Also for each one of these couples
a,b
we have
\mathbb{P}(W_{m+1}=b|W_{m}=a)=P(a,b)
. In fact, when
a,b\in I

this follows from
\mathbb{P}(W_{m+1}=b|W_{m}=a)=Q(a,b)\theta(a,b)
. If

a,b\in{\cal E}
this follows from (
31
) and (
32
). Let us show it for
a\in I,b\in{\cal E}
.
From the construction,
(
26
) and (
25
), we get,

\mathbb{P}(W_{m+1}=b|W_{m}=a)\!=\!\sum_{j\in I}Q(a,j)(1-\theta(a,j))\frac{P(a,b)}{P(a,{\cal E})}=\sum_{j\in I}{\widehat{\pi}}(j)P(a,b)=P(a,b).

(33)

Let us now take
a\in{\cal E}
,
b\in I
. We set
a_{0}=a
. Notice that

\mathbb{P}(W_{m+1}\!=\!b|W_{m-l}\!=\!a_{-l},l\!\geq\!0)\!=\!\mathbb{P}(W_{m+1}\!=\!b|W_{m-l}\!=\!a_{-l},l\!=\!0,..,k),

where
k
is the first
k\geq 1
such that
a_{-k}\in I
.
(From (
27
) we can assume
k
to be finite).
From (
30
) and (
28
) we have
\mathbb{P}(W_{m-k}=a_{-k})=\pi(a_{-k})
,
Now, we use (
26
) and (
31
)
to get

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(W_{m+1}\!=\!b,W_{m-l}\!=\!a_{-l},l\!=\!0,..,k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\!\!\pi(a_{-k})Q(a_{-k},b)(1-\theta(a_{-k},b))\frac{P(a_{-k},a_{-k+1})}{P(a_{-k},{\cal E})}\left(\!\prod_{l=1}^{k-1}\!\!P(a_{-k+l},a_{-k+l+1}\right)\pi(I)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\!\!\pi(a_{-k}){\widehat{\pi}}(b)\pi(I)\!\prod_{l=0}^{k-1}P(a_{-k+l},a_{-k+l+1})=\pi(a_{-k})\pi(b)\!\prod_{l=0}^{k-1}P(a_{-k+l},a_{-k+l+1}).

By summing on
b\in I
we find
\mathbb{P}(W_{m+1}\in I,W_{m-l}\!=\!a_{-l},l\!=\!0,..,k)=\!\pi(I)\pi(a_{-k})\prod_{l=0}^{k-1}P(a_{-k+l},a_{-k+l+1})
. On the other hand when
W_{m+1}\in{\cal E}
we
have

\mathbb{P}(W_{m+1}\!\in\!{\cal E},W_{m-l}\!=\!a_{-l},l\!=\!0,..,k)=\!\pi({\cal E})\pi(a_{-k})\prod_{l=0}^{k-1}P(a_{-k+l},a_{-k+l+1}).

Hence

\mathbb{P}(W_{m-l}\!=\!a_{-l},l\!=\!0,..,k)=\!\pi(a_{-k})\prod_{l=0}^{k-1}P(a_{-k+l},a_{-k+l+1})
. So, we get

\mathbb{P}(W_{m+1}\!=\!b|W_{m-l}\!=\!a_{-l},l\!=\!0,..,k)=\pi(b).

Since
(H1)
gives
P(a,b)=\pi(b)
, we have proven

\mathbb{P}(W_{m+1}\!=\!b|W_{m}\!=\!a,W_{m-l}\!=\!a_{-l},l=1,..,k)=P(a,b)
.
Hence,

W
is a Markov chain having the same transition probabilities as
X
. Then, the proof of Theorem
4.1
is
complete.
