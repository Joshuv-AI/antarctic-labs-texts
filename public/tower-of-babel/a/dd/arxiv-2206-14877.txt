Configurational density of states and melting of simple solids Journal: Physica A
Sergio Davis, Claudia Loyola, Joaquín Peralta

Abstract
We analyze the behavior of the microcanonical and canonical caloric curves for a piecewise model of the configurational density of states of simple solids, in the context of melting from the superheated state, as realized numerically in the Z-method via atomistic molecular dynamics. A first-order phase transition with metastable regions is reproduced by the model, being therefore useful to describe aspects of the melting transition. Within this model, transcendental equations connecting the superheating limit, the melting point, and the specific heat of each phase are presented and numerically solved. Our results suggest that the essential elements of the microcanonical Z curves can be extracted from simple modeling of the configurational density of states.

2
Configurational density of states and thermodynamics

For a classical system with Hamiltonian

H(\bm{r}_{1},\ldots,\bm{r}_{N},\bm{p}_{1},\ldots,\bm{p}_{N})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{{\bm{p}_{i}}^{2}}{2m_{i}}+\Phi(\bm{r}_{1},\ldots,\bm{r}_{N}),

(1)

we will define the configurational density of states (CDOS) as the multidimensional integral

\mathcal{D}(\phi)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\int d\bm{r}_{1}\ldots d\bm{r}_{N}\>\delta(\Phi(\bm{r}_{1},\ldots,\bm{r}_{N})-\phi),

(2)

where
\Phi(\bm{r}_{1},\ldots,\bm{r}_{N})
is the potential energy describing the interaction between particles. Using the definition of
\mathcal{D}(\phi)

in (
2
) is possible to rewrite any configurational integral of the form

I=\int d\bm{r}_{1}\ldots d\bm{r}_{N}\>G(\Phi(\bm{r}_{1},\ldots,\bm{r}_{N}))

(3)

as a one-dimensional integral over
\phi
. In fact, taking
I
and introducing a factor of 1 as an integral over a Dirac delta function, we have

\begin{split}I&=\int d\bm{r}_{1}\ldots d\bm{r}_{N}\>G(\Phi(\bm{r}_{1},\ldots,\bm{r}_{N}))\\
&=\int d\bm{r}_{1}\ldots d\bm{r}_{N}\left[\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\phi\delta(\phi-\Phi(\bm{r}_{1},\ldots,\bm{r}_{N}))\right]\>G(\Phi(\bm{r}_{1},\ldots,\bm{r}_{N}))\\
&=\int_{0}^{\infty}d\phi\left\{\int d\bm{r}_{1}\ldots d\bm{r}_{N}\delta(\phi-\Phi(\bm{r}_{1},\ldots,\bm{r}_{N}))\right\}\>G(\phi)\\
&=\int_{0}^{\infty}d\phi\mathcal{D}(\phi)G(\phi).\end{split}

(4)

where we have assumed that
\Phi
has its global minimum at
\Phi=0
. One such integral of particular importance is the canonical partition function

Z(\beta)
, defined as

Z(\beta)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\int d\bm{\Gamma}\exp\big(-\beta H(\bm{\Gamma})\big),

(5)

where
\bm{\Gamma}=(\bm{r}_{1},\ldots,\bm{r}_{N},\bm{p}_{1},\ldots,\bm{p}_{N})
, and which can be computed, following (
4
), as

\begin{split}Z(\beta)=&\int d\bm{p}_{1}\ldots d\bm{p}_{N}\exp\Big(-\beta\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\bm{p}^{2}_{i}}{2m_{i}}\Big)\times\int d\bm{r}_{1}\ldots d\bm{r}_{N}\>\exp\Big(-\beta\Phi(\bm{r}_{1},\ldots,\bm{r}_{N})\Big)\\
=&\>Z_{0}(N)\>\beta^{-3N/2}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\phi\mathcal{D}(\phi)\exp(-\beta\phi)\\
=&\>Z_{0}(N)\>\beta^{-3N/2}Z_{c}(\beta),\end{split}

(6)

with
Z_{0}(N)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\prod_{i=1}^{N}(\sqrt{2\pi m_{i}})^{3}
a constant only dependent on
N
and the masses of the particles, and where

Z_{c}(\beta)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\int_{0}^{\infty}d\phi\mathcal{D}(\phi)\exp(-\beta\phi)

(7)

is the configurational partition function. Similarly, the full density of states,

\Omega(E)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\int d\bm{\Gamma}\delta(E-H(\bm{\Gamma}))

(8)

can also be computed from the CDOS, by a convolution with the density of states
\Omega_{K}
of the ideal gas
[
16
]
, that is,

\Omega(E)=\int d\phi\mathcal{D}(\phi)\Omega_{K}(E-\phi),

(9)

where

\Omega_{K}(E)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\int d\bm{p}_{1}\ldots d\bm{p}_{N}\delta\Big(\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\bm{p}^{2}_{i}}{2m_{i}}-E\Big)=\Omega_{0}(N)\Theta(E)E^{\frac{3N}{2}-1}.

(10)

In order to obtain the result in (
9
), we replace the integral over the momenta in
\Omega(E)
by using
(
10
),

\begin{split}\Omega(E)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=&\int d\bm{r}_{1}\ldots d\bm{r}_{N}\>\left[\int d\bm{p}_{1}\ldots d\bm{p}_{N}\>\delta\Big(\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\bm{p}^{2}_{i}}{2m_{i}}+\Phi(\bm{r}_{1},\ldots,\bm{r}_{N})-E\Big)\right]\\
=&\int d\bm{r}_{1}\ldots d\bm{r}_{N}\>\Omega_{K}(E-\Phi(\bm{r}_{1},\ldots,\bm{r}_{N}))\\
=&\int_{0}^{\infty}d\phi\mathcal{D}(\phi)\Omega_{K}(E-\phi),\end{split}

(11)

where in the last equality we have used (
4
). Finally we have

\Omega(E)=\Omega_{0}(N)\int_{0}^{E}d\phi\mathcal{D}(\phi)(E-\phi)^{\frac{3N}{2}-1}=\Omega_{0}(N)\eta(E)

(12)

where
\Omega_{0}(N)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=Z_{0}(N)/\Gamma\big(3N/2\big)
is a function only of the size
N
of the system, and

\eta(E)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\int_{0}^{E}d\phi\mathcal{D}(\phi)\big(E-\phi\big)^{\frac{3N}{2}-1}.

(13)

Now we have all the elements needed for the computation of the caloric curves and the transition energy (or temperature) for a given model of CDOS. In the canonical
ensemble, the probability of observing a value
\phi
of potential energy at inverse temperature
\beta
is given by

P(\phi|\beta)=\frac{1}{Z_{c}(\beta)}\exp(-\beta\phi)\mathcal{D}(\phi)

(14)

and, using this, we can determine the caloric curve (internal energy as a function of inverse temperature) as

\big<H\big>_{\beta}=-\frac{\partial}{\partial\beta}\ln Z(\beta)=\frac{3N}{2\beta}+\big<\phi\big>_{\beta},

(15)

where

\big<\phi\big>_{\beta}=\frac{1}{Z_{c}(\beta)}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\phi\mathcal{D}(\phi)\exp(-\beta\phi)\phi=-\frac{\partial}{\partial\beta}\ln Z_{c}(\beta).

(16)

On the other hand, in the microcanonical ensemble the probability of having potential energy
\phi
at total energy
E
is given by
[
17
,
18
,
19
,
20
]

P(\phi|E)=\frac{1}{\eta(E)}(E-\phi)^{\frac{3N}{2}-1}\mathcal{D}(\phi).

(17)

The microcanonical caloric curve (inverse temperature as a function of internal energy) is given by

\beta(E)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\frac{\partial}{\partial E}\ln\Omega(E),

(18)

which can also be rewritten as an expectation as follows. Replacing
\Omega(E)
in terms of
\eta(E)
in (
18
) and using (
17
), we
can write

\begin{split}\beta(E)&=\frac{1}{\eta(E)}\frac{\partial}{\partial E}\int_{0}^{E}d\phi\mathcal{D}(\phi)(E-\phi)^{\frac{3N}{2}-1}\\
&=\frac{1}{\eta(E)}\int_{0}^{E}d\phi\mathcal{D}(\phi)\big(E-\phi)^{\frac{3N}{2}-1}\left[\frac{3N-2}{2(E-\phi)}\right]=\big<\hat{\beta}_{K}\big>_{E}.\end{split}

(19)

where
\hat{\beta}_{K}
is the kinetic inverse temperature estimator

\hat{\beta}_{K}(\phi;E)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\frac{3N-2}{2(E-\phi)}.

(20)

3
Model for the CDOS

In the following sections we will use, for the configurational density of states, the model presented in Ref.
[
14
]
which is defined piecewise, as

\mathcal{D}(\phi)=\begin{cases}d_{S}(\phi-\phi_{S})^{\alpha_{S}}\qquad\text{for}\;\phi<\phi_{c},\\[15.0pt]
d_{L}(\phi-\phi_{L})^{\alpha_{L}}\qquad\text{for}\;\phi\geq\phi_{c}.\end{cases}

(21)

This model represents two segments, one for the solid phase which, by definition, will have potential energies
\phi<\phi_{c}
, and one for the liquid phase where

\phi\geq\phi_{c}
. That is, our main assumption is that the potential energy landscape is effectively divided into solid and liquid states by a surface

\Phi(\bm{r}_{1},\ldots,\bm{r}_{N})=\phi_{c}

in configurational space. By imposing continuity of the CDOS at
\phi=\phi_{c}
, we must have

d_{S}(\phi_{c}-\phi_{S})^{\alpha_{S}}=d_{L}(\phi_{c}-\phi_{L})^{\alpha_{L}}.

(22)

Here
\phi_{S}
represents the potential energy minimum of the ideal solid, that can be set to zero without loss of generality provided that all energies are
measured with respect to this value. We can also set
d_{S}
=1 and express the potential energy in units of
\phi_{c}
, and then we have

\mathcal{D}(\phi)=\begin{cases}\phi^{\alpha_{S}}\qquad\text{for}\;\phi<1,\\[15.0pt]
\displaystyle\Big(\frac{\phi-\gamma}{1-\gamma}\Big)^{\alpha_{L}}\qquad\text{for}\;\phi\geq 1,\end{cases}

(23)

where we have defined the dimensionless parameter

\gamma\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\frac{\phi_{L}}{\phi_{c}}.

In this way, the model for the CDOS has only three free parameters, namely

\alpha_{S}
,
\alpha_{L}
and
\gamma
.

4
The caloric curve in the canonical ensemble

The configurational partition function
Z_{c}(\beta)
associated to the model for the CDOS in (
23
) can be obtained by piecewise integration as

\begin{split}Z_{c}(\beta)&=\int_{0}^{\infty}d\phi\mathcal{D}(\phi)\exp(-\beta\phi)\\
&=\int_{0}^{1}d\phi\phi^{\alpha_{S}}\exp(-\beta\phi)+\frac{1}{(1-\gamma)^{\alpha_{L}}}\int_{1}^{\infty}d\phi(\phi-\gamma)^{\alpha_{L}}\exp(-\beta\phi)\\
&=\beta^{-(\alpha_{S}+1)}\int_{0}^{\beta}duu^{\alpha_{S}}\exp(-u)+\frac{\beta^{-(\alpha_{L}+1)}}{(1-\gamma)^{\alpha_{L}}}\int_{\beta}^{\infty}du(u-\beta\gamma)^{\alpha_{L}}\exp(-u)\\
&=\beta^{-(\alpha_{S}+1)}\int_{0}^{\beta}duu^{\alpha_{S}}\exp(-u)+\frac{\beta^{-(\alpha_{L}+1)}}{(1-\gamma)^{\alpha_{L}}}\exp(-\beta\gamma)\int_{\beta(1-\gamma)}^{\infty}dww^{\alpha_{L}}\exp(-w).\end{split}

(24)

Finally we obtain

Z_{c}(\beta)=\beta^{-(\alpha_{S}+1)}G_{S}(0\rightarrow\beta)+\frac{\beta^{-(\alpha_{L}+1)}}{(1-\gamma)^{\alpha_{L}}}\exp(-\beta\gamma)G_{L}(\beta(1-\gamma)\rightarrow\infty)

(25)

where we have defined, for convenience, the auxiliary functions

G_{\nu}(a\rightarrow b)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\int_{a}^{b}dt\exp(-t)t^{\alpha_{\nu}}=\Gamma(\alpha_{\nu}+1;b)-\Gamma(\alpha_{\nu}+1;a)

(26)

for
\nu=S,L
, where
\Gamma(k;x)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\int_{0}^{x}dt\exp(-t)t^{k-1}
is the lower incomplete Gamma function.

4.1
Low and high-temperature limits

By taking the limit
\beta\rightarrow\infty
of (
25
) we see that the second term vanishes, and also
G_{S}(0\rightarrow\infty)=\Gamma(\alpha_{S}+1)
so
for low temperatures we can approximate

Z_{c}(\beta)\approx\beta^{-(\alpha_{S}+1)}\Gamma(\alpha_{S}+1).

(27)

By replacing (
27
) in (
16
) we have

\big<\phi\big>_{\beta}=-\frac{\partial}{\partial\beta}\ln Z_{c}(\beta)\approx\frac{\alpha_{S}+1}{\beta}

(28)

and then the solid branch of the canonical caloric curve is given by a straight line,

E_{S}(T)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\big<H\big>_{T,S}=\frac{3N}{2}k_{B}T+\big<\Phi\big>_{T}=\big(\textstyle{\frac{3N}{2}}+\alpha_{S}+1\big)k_{B}T

(29)

as expected. Here we can verify that
E_{S}(T)\rightarrow 0
as
T\rightarrow 0
, because we have fixed
\phi_{S}=0
, and moreover, we learn that the value of the
specific heat for the solid phase is

C_{S}=\frac{d}{dT}E_{S}(T)=\big(\textstyle{\frac{3N}{2}}+\alpha_{S}+1\big)k_{B}.

(30)

On the other hand, for high temperatures (i.e. in the limit
\beta\rightarrow 0
) we can approximate

Z_{c}(\beta)\approx\left[\frac{\beta^{-(\alpha_{L}+1)}\exp(-\beta\gamma)}{(1-\gamma)^{\alpha_{L}}}\right]\Gamma(\alpha_{L}+1),

(31)

obtaining from (
16
) that

\big<\phi\big>_{\beta}=-\frac{\partial}{\partial\beta}\ln Z_{c}(\beta)\approx\frac{\alpha_{L}+1}{\beta}+\gamma

(32)

hence the liquid branch is also a straight line, given by

E_{L}(T)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\big<H\big>_{T,L}=\frac{3N}{2}k_{B}T+\big<\Phi\big>_{T}\approx\gamma+\big(\textstyle{\frac{3N}{2}}+\alpha_{L}+1\big)k_{B}T.

(33)

This allows us to interpret
\gamma
as the extrapolation of the liquid branch towards
T=0
, that is,
\phi_{L}
in units of
\phi_{c}
is the potential energy of a perfectly frozen
liquid at
T=0
,

E_{L}(T=0)=\gamma=\frac{\phi_{L}}{\phi_{c}}.

(34)

Moreover, we obtain that the specific heat of the liquid phase is

C_{L}=\frac{d}{dT}E_{L}(T)=\big(\textstyle{\frac{3N}{2}}+\alpha_{L}+1\big)k_{B}.

(35)

In order for the energy to be extensive in both branches as
N\rightarrow\infty
, it must hold true that the parameters
\alpha_{\nu}
are proportional to
N
, and it follows
that

\frac{C_{\nu}}{k_{B}}=\alpha_{\nu}+\frac{3N}{2}

(36)

with
\nu=S,L
. Using these definitions we can write
E_{S}
and
E_{L}
in terms of
\beta
more compactly, as

\displaystyle E_{S}(\beta)

\displaystyle=\frac{C_{S}}{\beta},

(37a)

\displaystyle E_{L}(\beta)

\displaystyle=\gamma+\frac{C_{L}}{\beta}.

(37b)

4.2
Melting temperature

On account of the assumption that all solid states have
\phi<\phi_{c}
and for the liquid states
\phi>\phi_{c}
, we will define the probabilities
P(S|\beta)
of being
in the solid phase, and
P(L|\beta)
of being in the liquid phase as

\displaystyle P(S|\beta)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=P(\phi<1|\beta)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Z_{c}(\beta)}\int_{0}^{1}d\phi\exp(-\beta\phi)\phi^{\alpha_{S}}=\frac{\beta^{-(\alpha_{S}+1)}G_{S}(0\rightarrow\beta)}{Z_{c}(\beta)},

(38)

\displaystyle P(L|\beta)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=P(\phi\geq 1|\beta)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Z_{c}(\beta)}\int_{1}^{\infty}d\phi\exp(-\beta\phi)\frac{(\phi-\gamma)^{\alpha_{L}}}{(1-\gamma)^{\alpha_{L}}}=\frac{\beta^{-(\alpha_{L}+1)}\exp(-\beta\gamma)}{Z_{c}(\beta)(1-\gamma)^{\alpha_{L}}}G_{L}(\beta(1-\gamma)\rightarrow\infty)

(39)

respectively, such that

P(S|\beta)+P(L|\beta)=1

for all values of
\beta
. The probability of solid is shown as a function of
T
in Fig.
1
.
The melting temperature
T_{m}
is such that both probabilities are equal
[
21
]
, that is,

P(S|\beta_{m})=P(L|\beta_{m})=\frac{1}{2},

(40)

with
\beta_{m}=1/(k_{B}T_{m})
, which is then the solution of the transcendental equation

(\beta_{m})^{\alpha_{L}-\alpha_{S}}G_{S}(0\rightarrow\beta_{m})=\frac{\exp(-\beta_{m}\gamma)}{(1-\gamma)^{\alpha_{L}}}G_{L}(\beta_{m}(1-\gamma)\rightarrow\infty).

(41)

Using
P(S|\beta)
and
P(L|\beta)
we can write the canonical caloric curve for any
\beta
as the sum of three contributions, namely

\big<H\big>_{\beta}=\frac{3N}{2\beta}-\frac{\partial}{\partial\beta}\ln Z_{c}(\beta)=E_{S}(\beta)P(S|\beta)+E_{L}(\beta)\big(1-P(S|\beta)\big)+E_{\text{res}}(\beta),

(42)

where the first and second terms account for the solid and liquid branches, respectively, and
E_{\text{res}}
is equal to

E_{\text{res}}(\beta)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=-\frac{\gamma\beta^{-1}\exp(-\beta)}{Z_{c}(\beta)}.

(43)

By comparing (
42
) with the low and high temperature limits, namely
E_{S}
and
E_{L}
, and noting that
P(S|\beta)\rightarrow 1
for low temperatures
and
P(S|\beta)\rightarrow 0
for high temperatures, we see that
E_{\text{res}}
must vanish on both limits, as can be verified by using (
27
) and
(
31
). Therefore, this term is only relevant near the transition region. If we replace (
41
) into the configurational partition
function in (
25
) we obtain

Z_{c}(\beta_{m})=2(\beta_{m})^{-(\alpha_{S}+1)}G_{S}(0\rightarrow\beta_{m})

(44)

and we can determine the melting energy
E^{*}
as

E^{*}=\frac{3N}{2\beta_{m}}+\frac{1}{2}\left[N\gamma+\frac{\alpha_{S}+\alpha_{L}+2}{\beta_{m}}\right]-\frac{\gamma\exp(-\beta_{m})}{2G_{S}(0\rightarrow\beta_{m})}(\beta_{m})^{\alpha_{S}}.

(45)

Because
\alpha_{S}\propto N
, in the thermodynamic limit we can approximate

\begin{split}G_{S}(0\rightarrow\beta_{m})&=\int_{0}^{\beta_{m}}dt\exp(-t)t^{\alpha_{S}}\\
&=\int_{0}^{\beta_{m}}dt\exp(-t+\alpha_{S}\ln t)\\
&\approx\int_{0}^{\beta_{m}}dt\exp(\alpha_{S}\ln t)=\frac{\beta_{m}^{\alpha_{S}+1}}{\alpha_{S}+1},\end{split}

(46)

and then we have, for the melting energy per particle
\varepsilon^{*}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=E^{*}/N
in the original energy units, that

\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\varepsilon^{*}=\varphi_{S}+\frac{\gamma}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{c_{S}+c_{L}}{k_{B}}-\gamma a_{S}\exp\Big(-\frac{\phi_{c}}{k_{B}T_{m}}\Big)\right)k_{B}T_{m},

(47)

with
\varphi_{S}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\phi_{S}/N
,
c_{\nu}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=C_{\nu}/N
and
a_{\nu}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\alpha_{\nu}/N
. This becomes a linear relation between
E^{*}
and
T_{m}
for
\gamma\approx 0
, because

\lim_{\gamma\rightarrow 0}\varepsilon^{*}=\varphi_{s}+\left(\frac{c_{s}+c_{L}}{2}\right)T_{m},

(48)

and also for
k_{B}T_{m}\ll\phi_{c}
. Fig.
2
shows the dimensionless intensive quantity

\zeta\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\frac{\varepsilon^{*}-\varphi_{S}}{k_{B}T_{m}}

as a function of

\gamma
, using the approximation in (
47
) and the exact value in (
45
).

5
The caloric curve in the microcanonical ensemble

Just as we used the configurational partition function in Section
4
to compute the canonical caloric curve, we can use the full density of states

\Omega(E)
to obtain the microcanonical caloric curve, being given in terms of the CDOS as

\begin{split}\eta(E)&=\int_{0}^{E}d\phi\mathcal{D}(\phi)\big(E-\phi\big)^{\frac{3N}{2}-1}=E^{\frac{3N}{2}-1}\int_{0}^{E}d\phi\mathcal{D}(\phi)\Big(1-\frac{\phi}{E}\Big)^{\frac{3N}{2}-1}\\
&=E^{\frac{3N}{2}-1}\left[\int_{0}^{\min(E,1)}\hskip-7.0ptd\phi\Big(1-\frac{\phi}{E}\Big)^{\frac{3N}{2}-1}\phi^{\alpha_{S}}+\frac{\Theta(E-1)}{(1-\gamma)^{\alpha_{L}}}\int_{1}^{E}d\phi\Big(1-\frac{\phi}{E}\Big)^{\frac{3N}{2}-1}(\phi-\gamma)^{\alpha_{L}}\right]\\
&=E^{C_{S}}B_{S}\Big(\textstyle 0\rightarrow\min(1,\frac{1}{E})\Big)+\displaystyle\frac{\Theta(E-1)}{(1-\gamma)^{\alpha_{L}}}(E-\gamma)^{C_{L}}B_{L}\Big(\lambda(E)\rightarrow 1\Big)\end{split}

(49)

where we have defined the auxiliary functions

\lambda(E)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\frac{1-\gamma}{E-\gamma}

(50)

and

B_{\nu}(a\rightarrow b)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\int_{a}^{b}dt\;t^{\alpha_{\nu}+1}(1-t)^{\frac{3N}{2}-1}

(51)

for convenience of notation, where
\nu=S,L
and
a,b\in[0,1]
. Replacing in (
49
) we obtain
\eta(E)
as a piecewise function,

\eta(E)=\begin{cases}E^{C_{S}}B(\alpha_{S}+1,\textstyle\frac{3N}{2})\;\text{for}\;E\leq 1\\[15.0pt]
\displaystyle E^{C_{S}}B_{S}(0\rightarrow\textstyle\frac{1}{E})\displaystyle+\frac{(E-\gamma)^{C_{L}}}{(1-\gamma)^{\alpha_{L}}}B_{L}(\lambda(E)\rightarrow 1)\;\text{for}\;E>1.\end{cases}

(52)

We can see that the microcanonical inverse temperature for the branch with
E\leq 1
is simply given by

\beta_{\text{low}}(E)=\frac{\partial}{\partial E}\ln(E^{C_{S}})=\frac{C_{S}}{E},

(53)

that is
E
as a function of
T(E)
is a straight line with slope
C_{S}
, in agreement with the low temperature approximation
E_{S}(T)
of the canonical caloric curve in
(
29
). This means the non-monotonic behavior, i.e. the
van der Waals
loop and the microcanonical melting energy
E_{m}
must ocurr above
E=1
.
Just as we did for the canonical ensemble, we will define the probabilities of solid and liquid,
P(S|E)
and
P(L|E)
respectively, at an energy
E\geq 1
, according to

\displaystyle P(S|E)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\frac{1}{\eta(E)}\int_{0}^{1}d\phi\mathcal{D}(\phi)(E-\phi)^{\frac{3N}{2}-1}=\frac{E^{C_{S}}B_{S}(0\rightarrow 1/E)}{\eta(E)},

(54)

\displaystyle P(L|E)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\frac{1}{\eta(E)}\int_{1}^{E}d\phi\mathcal{D}(\phi)(E-\phi)^{\frac{3N}{2}-1}=\frac{(E-\gamma)^{C_{L}}}{(1-\gamma)^{\alpha_{L}}}\frac{B_{L}(\lambda(E)\rightarrow 1)}{\eta(E)}.

(55)

The probability of solid is shown, as a function of energy, in Fig.
3
. We will define the microcanonical melting energy
E_{m}
as the value of
E

such that
P(S|E_{m})=P(L|E_{m})
, being then the solution of the transcendental equation

(E_{m})^{C_{S}}(1-\gamma)^{\alpha_{L}}B_{S}(0\rightarrow 1/E_{m})=(E_{m}-\gamma)^{C_{L}}B_{L}(\lambda(E_{m})\rightarrow 1).

(56)

Using the derivatives

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial E}B_{S}(0\rightarrow 1/E)

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial E}\int_{0}^{1}dt\>t^{\alpha_{S}+1}(1-t)^{\frac{3N}{2}-1}\Theta(1/E-t)=-E^{-(C_{S}+2)}(E-1)^{\frac{3N}{2}-1}

(57)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial E}B_{L}(\lambda(E)\rightarrow 1)

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial E}\int_{0}^{1}dt\>t^{\alpha_{L}+1}(1-t)^{\frac{3N}{2}-1}\Theta(t-\lambda(E))=\frac{1}{1-\gamma}\left[\lambda(E)^{\alpha_{L}+3}-\lambda(E)^{C_{L}+2}\right],

(58)

we can write

\eta^{\prime}(E)=\eta(E)\left(\frac{C_{S}}{E}P(S|E)+\frac{C_{L}}{E-\gamma}P(L|E)\right)+\lambda(E)^{2}\left[(E-\gamma)^{\frac{3N}{2}}-(1-\gamma)^{\frac{3N}{2}}\right]-E^{-2}(E-1)^{\frac{3N}{2}-1}

(59)

and then

\beta_{\text{high}}(E)=\left(\frac{C_{S}}{E}\right)P(S|E)+\left(\frac{C_{L}}{E-\gamma}\right)\big(1-P(S|E)\big)+\beta_{\text{res}}(E)

(60)

where

\beta_{\text{res}}(E)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\frac{1}{\eta(E)}\left(\lambda(E)^{2}\left[(E-\gamma)^{\frac{3N}{2}}-(1-\gamma)^{\frac{3N}{2}}\right]-\frac{1}{E^{2}}(E-1)^{\frac{3N}{2}-1}\right)

(61)

from which we see that the (inverse) temperature is also continuous at
E=1
. Similarly to
E_{\text{res}}(\beta)
in the canonical ensemble, we can also see that

\beta_{\text{res}}(E)
vanishes for both
E\rightarrow 1
and
E\rightarrow\infty
. By using the Stirling approximation on the representation of the Beta function
written in terms of
\Gamma
-functions,

B(x,y)=\frac{\Gamma(x)\Gamma(y)}{\Gamma(x+y)}\approx\sqrt{2\pi}\>\left[\frac{x^{x-1/2}y^{y-1/2}}{(x+y)^{x+y-1/2}}\right]

(62)

for large
x
and
y
we have

B_{\nu}(a\rightarrow b)\approx\begin{cases}K_{\nu}\;\text{if}\;\alpha_{\nu}/C_{\nu}\in[a,b]\\[10.0pt]
0\;\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(63)

where

K_{\nu}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\sqrt{2\pi}\>\left[\frac{(\alpha_{\nu}+2)^{\alpha_{\nu}+3/2}{\Big(\tfrac{3N}{2}\Big)}^{3N/2-1/2}}{(C_{\nu}+1)^{C_{\nu}-1/2}}\right].

(64)

The probability of solid phase
P(S|E)
is then approximated by

P(S|E)\approx\frac{E^{C_{S}}(1-\gamma)^{\alpha_{L}}K_{S}\mathrm{Q}[E\leq c_{S}/a_{S}]}{E^{C_{S}}(1-\gamma)^{\alpha_{L}}K_{S}\mathrm{Q}[E\leq c_{S}/a_{S}]+(E-\gamma)^{C_{L}}K_{L}\mathrm{Q}[E\geq\gamma+(c_{L}/a_{L})(1-\gamma)]}

(65)

where
\mathrm{Q}(A)
is the indicator function
[
22
]
of the proposition
A
, defined as

\mathrm{Q}(A)=\begin{cases}1\;\;\text{if}\;A\;\text{is true},\\
0\;\;\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(66)

Therefore, in this approximation the transition energy
E_{m}
, such that
P(S|E_{m})=1/2
, must be the solution of

E_{m}^{C_{S}}(1-\gamma)^{\alpha_{L}}K_{S}=(E_{m}-\gamma)^{C_{L}}K_{L}

(67)

provided that no indicator function vanishes, that is, it must hold that

\gamma+\frac{c_{L}}{a_{L}}(1-\gamma)\leq E_{m}\leq\frac{c_{S}}{a_{S}}.

(68)

These inequalities impose a lower limit on
\gamma
, namely

\gamma\geq\frac{a_{L}}{a_{S}}-1,

(69)

which however is only relevant if
a_{L}>a_{S}
, as it prevents
\gamma
for reaching zero. In order to determine the solutions
E^{*}
corresponding to the maximum
and minimum of microcanonical temperature, we impose

\frac{\partial}{\partial E}\beta(E)\Big|_{E=E^{*}}=\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial E^{2}}\ln\eta(E)\Big|_{E=E^{*}}=\frac{\eta^{\prime\prime}(E^{*})}{\eta(E^{*})}-\left(\frac{\eta^{\prime}(E^{*})}{\eta(E^{*})}\right)^{2}=0,

(70)

therefore

\eta^{\prime\prime}(E^{*})\cdot\eta(E^{*})=\eta^{\prime}(E^{*})^{2}.

(71)

The exact conditions under which (
71
) has exactly two solutions remain to be explored. Nevertheless, we have verified this fact numerically, and in Fig.

4
the green diamonds show the numerical solutions of (
71
) using the expression (
52
) for
\eta(E)
in the case
E>1
, together
with the canonical and microcanonical caloric curves. The ensemble inequivalence expected in small systems
[
23
]
is clearly seen, and a rather remarkable agreement
of the microcanonical curves with the usual shape of the Z curves in the literature is found. The degree of superheating increases with
\gamma
, and the van der Waals loop
typically found in microcanonical curves of small systems
[
24
,
25
,
26
,
27
,
28
,
29
,
30
]
gradually
becomes sharper. However, the lower inflection point in the microcanonical curve does not coincide with the value of
T_{m}
obtained from the canonical curve, suggesting that the
model for the CDOS could be improved by adding additional parameters.

6
Concluding remarks

We have presented a study of the microcanonical and canonical melting curves for a simple solid, based on the recently proposed model in Ref.
[
14
]
. Our
results show that this model is sufficient to reproduce the existence of superheating and recover the so-called Z curves of microcanonical melting, in which the Z-method
by Belonoshko
et al

[
1
]
is based. This is a first step for an explanation of the foundations of the Z-method in terms of ensemble inequivalence
for small systems.
