Speed limits to fluctuation dynamics
Ryusuke Hamazaki

Abstract
Fluctuation dynamics of an experimentally measured observable offer a primary signal for nonequilibrium systems, along with dynamics of the mean. While universal speed limits for the mean have actively been studied recently, constraints for the speed of the fluctuation have been elusive. Here, we develop a theory concerning rigorous limits to the rate of fluctuation growth. We find a principle that the speed of an observable’s fluctuation is upper bounded by the fluctuation of an appropriate observable describing velocity, which also indicates a tradeoff relation between the changes for the mean and fluctuation. We demonstrate the advantages of our inequalities for processes with non-negligible dispersion of observables, quantum work extraction, and the entanglement growth in free fermionic systems. Our results open an avenue toward a quantitative theory of fluctuation dynamics in various non-equilibrium systems encompassing quantum many-body systems and nonlinear population dynamics.

Introduction

Many physical systems involve randomness caused by stochastic noises due to external environment or intrinsic quantum uncertainty, which have been central objects in nonequilibrium statistical mechanics

Two primary quantities that characterize such fluctuating systems are the mean and the fluctuations of an experimentally measured observable.
When the system is out of equilibrium, the distribution of the fluctuating observable changes in time, which causes the time evolution for the mean and the fluctuation.
To understand nonequilibrium dynamics, rigorous bounds concerning the rate of the change for the mean value of the observable have intensively been studied recently

;

;
del Campo et al. 2013
;

;

;

;

;

;

;

;

” dates back more than a half-century ago by Mandelstam and Tamm
Mandelstam and Tamm 1945
.
For example, the speed (i.e., the time derivative) of the mean is known to be bounded by a product of the standard deviation and the Fisher information
Ito and Dechant 2020
;
Nicholson et al. 2020
;
García-Pintos et al. 2022

Such speed limits for observables can provide more experimentally relevant and even tighter constraints than those for the entire states
García-Pintos et al. 2022
;
Hamazaki 2022

Although the speed of the mean value has been investigated so far, previous speed limits cannot make a clear prediction to fluctuations of an observable, e.g., the standard deviation, which are not simply given as a mean of a state-independent observable (see Ref.
Hamazaki 2022
for an exception).
Such fluctuation growth plays an equally (or, sometimes even more) important role compared with the mean in characterizing out-of-equilibrium dynamics.
For example, the spreading of particles should be characterized by the fluctuation dynamics of the particles’ position as well as the mean displacement
Schneider et al. 2012
;
Ronzheimer et al. 2013

As another example, fluctuation dynamics of macroscopic observables can relate with dynamics of entanglement
Fujimoto et al. 2021
;
Zhao et al. 2020
and coherence
Shimizu and Morimae 2005
in certain quantum systems, which are not understood from the mean value alone.
It is thus a pivotal open question to elucidate the dynamical behavior of fluctuations of observables.

Results

Speed limits to fluctuation dynamics.

We argue that for certain dynamics, the speed of the standard deviation of an observable
A
is bounded by the standard deviation of the appropriately defined velocity observable
\mathcal{V}_{A}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}{\sigma}_{A}}{\mathrm{d}t}\right|\leq c\>\sigma_{\mathcal{V}_{A}}.

(1)

Here,
\sigma_{A}
denotes the standard deviation of
A
,
and the velocity observable is chosen such that
\frac{\mathrm{d}\braket{{A}}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\braket{\mathcal{V}_{A}}
is satisfied, where
\braket{\cdots}
denotes the mean.
The constant
c
is determined by the dynamics and the choice of
\mathcal{V}_{A}
, and we will obtain
c=1
or
c=1/2
in this manuscript.
In addition, this inequality indicates a tradeoff relation between the speeds of the mean and the standard deviation.
That is, two quantities cannot be simultaneously fast, which we concisely represent as (Fig.
1
b
)

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}\braket{{A}}}{\mathrm{d}t}\right)^{2}+\frac{1}{c^{2}}\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}{\sigma}_{A}}{\mathrm{d}t}\right)^{2}\leq\braket{\mathcal{V}_{A}^{2}}=:C_{A}.

(2)

We call the equivalent inequalities (
1
) and (
2
), as well as their underlying equality (
3
) below, as our main results.
Note that an inappropriate choice of
\mathcal{V}_{A}
, say
\mathcal{V}_{A}=\frac{\mathrm{d}\braket{{A}}}{\mathrm{d}t}1
, breaks Eqs. (
1
) and (
2
).
Nonetheless, we will show that we can choose appropriate velocity observables where Eqs. (
1
) and (
2
) hold for various physically relevant setups.

When
C_{A}
is evaluated easily (see, e.g., unitary quantum dynamics with (
12
)), (
2
) is already practically useful because we can upper bound the speeds of the mean and fluctuation.
Moreover, as another nontrivial result of ours, we discover that

can be further upper bounded using other physical quantities for certain setups, such as the kinetic energy in classical and quantum hydrodynamics and the entropy production rate for thermodynamic processes.

General proof and the required condition.

We first provide a general proof of our main results (
1
)
and (
2
). We especially identify nontrivial sufficient conditions for our inequalities to hold, with discussing how to choose the velocity observable.
For this purpose, we first show the following important equality for the variance of
A
,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\braket{\delta A^{2}}}{\mathrm{d}t}=2c\braket{\delta A,\delta\mathcal{V}_{A}},

(3)

where
\delta X=X-\braket{X}
is the fluctuation observable and
\braket{X,Y}=\frac{1}{2}\braket{\{X,Y\}}
is the correlation between
X
and
Y
.
Here, we have introduced the anti-commutator
\{X,Y\}=XY+YX
to include the quantum case with non-commutativity between
X
and
Y
(
\{X,Y\}=2XY
for classical cases).
Note that the right-hand side in Eq. (
3
) is given as the covariance between
A
and
\mathcal{V}_{A}
.
If we obtain (
3
), (
1
) can be shown by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality
|\braket{\delta A,\delta\mathcal{V}_{A}}|\leq\sigma_{A}\sigma_{\mathcal{V}_{A}}
and
\frac{\mathrm{d}\braket{\delta A^{2}}}{\mathrm{d}t}=2\sigma_{A}\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma_{A}}{\mathrm{d}t}
.

We now discuss conditions for Eq. (
3
) to hold and ansatz to choose
\mathcal{V}_{A}
. For this purpose, we introduce a notation where the expectation value of an observable
A
is given by some inner product between
A
and some probability (density)
\rho
as
\braket{A}=(A|\rho)
.
For example, for continuous classical systems we have a probability density
\rho=\{\rho(\mathbf{x},t)\}
and
(A|\rho)=\int d\mathbf{x}A(\mathbf{x},t)\rho(\mathbf{x},t)
; for classical discrete systems we have a probability distribution
\rho=\{p_{i}(t)\}
and

(A|\rho)=\sum_{i}A_{i}(t)p_{i}(t)
; for quantum systems, we have a density matrix

\rho=\hat{\rho}(t)
and

(A|\rho)=\mathrm{Tr}[\hat{A}(t)\hat{\rho}(t)]
.
Now, we assume that the time evolution of the probability is given by

\frac{\mathrm{d}\rho}{\mathrm{d}t}=\mathcal{L}[{\rho}],

where the map
\mathcal{L}
is not unique and can depend on
\rho
.
We can also define the dual map
\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}
of
\mathcal{L}
satisfying the relation

(A|\dot{\rho})=(A|\mathcal{L}[\rho])=(\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}[A]|\rho),

which is required to hold for an arbitrary observable
A
.

The above definition for
\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}
leads to the natural way to find the velocity observable. Indeed, if we define

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{A}=\dot{A}+\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}[A],

(4)

\mathcal{V}_{A}
satisfies the requirement for the velocity observable because

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\braket{A}}{\mathrm{d}t}=(\dot{A}|\rho)+(A|\dot{\rho})=(\mathcal{V}_{A}|\rho)=\braket{\mathcal{V}_{A}},

(5)

where
\dot{X}=\frac{\mathrm{d}X}{\mathrm{d}t}
(see
Methods
for the possible freedom of choosing velocity observables). As discussed later, Eq. (
5
) with (
4
) has often been used to derive speed limits for the mean of
A

Nicholson et al. 2020
;
Ito and Dechant 2020
;
García-Pintos et al. 2022
;
Hamazaki 2022
. Indeed, by finding the further upper bound for
|\braket{\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}[A]}|
, one obtains the speed limit for
\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}\braket{A}}{\mathrm{d}t}-\dot{A}\right|
in terms of, e.g., Fisher information
Nicholson et al. 2020
;
Ito and Dechant 2020
;
García-Pintos et al. 2022
.
However, these previous results cannot lead to Eq. (
3
) and our main bounds (
1
) and (
2
).

To derive Eq. (
3
) under the choice in (
4
), we need an additional nontrivial condition. Indeed, a straightforward calculation leads to

\frac{\mathrm{d}\braket{\delta A^{2}}}{\mathrm{d}t}=(\{\delta A,\dot{A}\}+\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}[A^{2}]-2\braket{A}\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}[A]|\rho)
.
We thus need

\displaystyle\braket{\{\delta A,\dot{A}\}+\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}[A^{2}]-2\braket{A}\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}[A]}=c\braket{\{\delta A,\mathcal{V}_{A}\}}

(6)

to obtain (
3
) (note that
\braket{\{\delta A,\mathcal{V}_{A}\}}=\braket{\{\delta A,\delta\mathcal{V}_{A}\}}
). We stress that this condition is unique to our analysis for fluctuation dynamics, which goes beyond previous speed limits for the mean

;
Ito and Dechant 2020
;
García-Pintos et al. 2022
;
Hamazaki 2022
.

We find two different situations (
7
) and (
22
), each of which ensures (
6
).
The first situation corresponds to

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}[A^{2}]=\{A,\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}[A]\},

(7)

which leads to Eq. (
6
) with
c=1
.
Importantly, if
\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}
represents a derivative in an abstract sense, i.e.,

\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}

\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}[AB]=A\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}[B]+\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}[A]B

6

Limits from the local conservation law.

There are several class of dynamics satisfying (
7
). As a first class, let us consider continuous dynamics under local conservation law of some normalized distribution
\rho=\{\rho(\mathbf{x},t)\}
with
\int d\mathbf{x}\rho(\mathbf{x},t)=1
.
It is then natural to define the mean and the standard deviation of a space-dependent observable
A
coupled to
\rho
as
(A|\rho)=\braket{A(t)}=\int d\mathbf{x}A(\mathbf{x})\rho(\mathbf{x},t)

and
\sigma_{A}(t)=\sqrt{\int d\mathbf{x}(A(\mathbf{x})-\braket{A(t)})^{2}\rho(\mathbf{x},t)}
, respectively.

We assume that the continuity equation
\partial_{t}\rho(\mathbf{x},t)=\mathcal{L}[\rho]=-\nabla\cdot(\rho(\mathbf{x},t)\mathbf{V}(\mathbf{x},t))

holds by introducing the normalized current
\mathbf{V}(\mathbf{x},t)
, and that
\rho\mathbf{V}
vanishes in the limit
|\mathbf{x}|\rightarrow\infty
.
Using the integration by parts, we can find
\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}[A]=\nabla A\cdot\mathbf{V}
and thus

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{A}(\mathbf{x},t)=\nabla A(\mathbf{x},t)\cdot\mathbf{V}(\mathbf{x},t)+\dot{A}(\mathbf{x},t).

(8)

by using Eq. (
4
).

Now, since
\nabla A^{2}=2A\nabla A
, Eq. (
7
) holds for
\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}
, and thus Eq. (
6
) and Eq. (
3
) do.
Therefore, our main bounds on the fluctuation dynamics, (
1
) and (
2
), are obtained for the velocity observable (
8
) with
c=1
.

Now, if we consider time-independent observables, we can further find the upper bound of
C_{A}=\braket{(\nabla A\cdot\mathbf{V})^{2}}
by virtue of, e.g., the Hölder’s inequality:

\displaystyle C_{A}\leq\|\nabla A\|_{\infty}^{2}\cdot\int d\mathbf{x}\rho(\mathbf{x},t)|\mathbf{V}(\mathbf{x},t)|^{2}.

(9)

Here,
\|\nabla A\|_{\infty}=\max_{\mathbf{x}}|\nabla A(\mathbf{x})|
, which is calculated easily for a given
A
.
We stress that this result cannot be obtained from the previous speed limit for the mean despite the appearance of a similar factor.
Indeed, the previous approach
Dechant and Sasa 2018a
;
Hamazaki 2022
uses the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality to obtain
\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}\braket{A}}{\mathrm{d}t}\right|^{2}=|\braket{\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}[A]}|^{2}=|\braket{\nabla A\cdot\mathbf{V}}|^{2}\leq\braket{(\nabla A)^{2}}\int d\mathbf{x}\rho(\mathbf{x},t)|\mathbf{V}(\mathbf{x},t)|^{2}
, where we do not need the condition (
7
) for the proof.

Let us provide some examples of our general argument above:

Example 1: Irreversible thermodynamics.

Let us consider thermodynamic systems of Brownian particles described by the overdamped Fokker-Planck equation
Seifert 2012
.
In this case, the probability density
\rho(\mathbf{x},t)
satisfies the continuity equation as
\partial_{t}\rho=-\nabla\cdot(\rho\mathbf{v})=-\nabla\cdot(\rho\mu(\mathbf{F}-T\nabla\ln\rho))
, where
\mathbf{v}
is the local velocity for the probability current,
\mu
is the mobility,
T
is the temperature, and
\mathbf{F}
is the force.
Then, our results Eqs. (
1
)-(
3
) hold for any function
A(\mathbf{x},t)
with
\mathcal{V}_{A}=\nabla A\cdot\mathbf{v}+\dot{A}
.
For time-independent observables,
C_{A}
is bounded by the total entropy production rate
Seifert 2012

\dot{\Sigma}=\frac{1}{\mu T}\int d\mathbf{x}\rho|\mathbf{v}|^{2}
as
C_{A}\leq\mu T\|\nabla A\|_{\infty}^{2}\dot{\Sigma}
.
Thus, to change the mean and the fluctuation quickly, the irreversibility of the system should be significant; note that the previous classical speed limits only considered the speed of the mean
Dechant and Sasa 2018a
;
Dechant and Sasa 2018b
;
Shiraishi et al. 2018
.

Our bounds can become advantageous over previous ones if we consider a transition with non-negligible dispersion of observables as shown in Fig.
2
a
.
In this case, we typically have
\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}\braket{A}}{\mathrm{d}t}\right|\sim\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma_{A}}{\mathrm{d}t}\right|
or
\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}\braket{A}}{\mathrm{d}t}\right|\ll\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma_{A}}{\mathrm{d}t}\right|
at an instantaneous time.
Then, the previous works on the speed limit for the mean of observables can be problematic in understanding such a transition, i.e., they can provide rather loose bounds.
In contrast, our inequality (
2
) succeeds in resolving this problem by considering the degree of dispersion of the distribution.

As a simple demonstration, let us consider
an example of a classical particle obeying the Fokker-Planck equation and take the distance of the particle from the origin,
A(\mathbf{x})=|\mathbf{x}|
, as an observable.
We consider a localized initial state at the origin, for which
\braket{A(0)}
and
\sigma_{A}(0)
are very small.
Due to the time evolution,
\braket{A}
and
\sigma_{A}
change, but since we assume that the diffusion is non-negligible, the rate
\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma_{A}}{\mathrm{d}t}\right|
is comparable to

\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}\braket{A}}{\mathrm{d}t}\right|
.
Now, the previously known inequality
Ito 2023
leads to

\mathfrak{S}_{\mathrm{mean}}:=\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}\braket{A}}{\mathrm{d}t}\right)^{2}\leq\mu T\dot{\Sigma}.

Moreover, using the method in our previous paper
Hamazaki 2022
, we also find

\mathfrak{S}_{\mathrm{std}}:=\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma_{A}}{\mathrm{d}t}\right)^{2}\leq\mu T\dot{\Sigma},

where we have used the fact
\|\nabla A\|_{\infty}=1
.
However, these inequalities are far from tight, as numerically demonstrated in the red and orange curves for a one-dimensional system in Fig.
2
b
.

Example 2: Classical and quantum hydrodynamics.

Consider the classical hydrodynamics described by, e.g., the nonlinear Navier-Stokes equation.
For the density
\rho(\mathbf{x},t)
of the fluid, we generally have a continuity equation
\partial_{t}\rho=-\nabla\cdot(\rho\mathbf{u})
, where
\mathbf{u}
is the current of the fluid.
Similarly, we can consider quantum hydrodynamics
Tsubota et al. 2013
that is described by the nonlinear Schrödinger equation. While the quantum fluid is described by the complex amplitude
\Psi(\mathbf{x},t)
, its density
\rho(\mathbf{x},t)=|\Psi(\mathbf{x},t)|^{2}
obeys the continuity equation
\partial_{t}\rho=-\nabla\cdot(\rho\mathbf{u})
.
Then, in both cases, our results Eqs. (
1
)-(
3
) hold for any function given by
A(\mathbf{x},t)
with
\mathcal{V}_{A}=\nabla A\cdot\mathbf{u}+\dot{A}
.
For time-independent observables,
C_{A}
is bounded by the kinetic energy
E_{\mathrm{kin}}=\frac{M}{2}\int d\mathbf{x}\rho|\mathbf{u}|^{2}
as
C_{A}\leq\frac{2\|\nabla A\|_{\infty}^{2}E_{\mathrm{kin}}}{M}
, where
M
is the total mass.

Our bound has a physical meaning that the speed of mean and fluctuation becomes small simultaneously if the kinetic energy
E_{\mathrm{kin}}
is small, irrespective of potential and interaction energies. In addition, our bound again becomes much tighter than that in
Hamazaki 2022
when we prepare a situation satisfying
\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}\braket{A}}{\mathrm{d}t}\right|\sim\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma_{A}}{\mathrm{d}t}\right|
, in a manner similar to
Example 1
.

n=2
.
Second, we note that
the bound on
C_{A}
, (
9
), is related to the Wasserstein geometry in the optimal transport theory
Villani 2009
.
Indeed, by minimizing the right-hand side of (
9
) in terms of
\mathbf{V}
appearing in the continuity equation, we find
Benamou and Brenier 2000
;
Nakazato and Ito 2021
that
C_{A}
is further upper bounded by a factor involving the order-2 Wasserstein distance.
However, our general results in (

) and (

)
are tighter and not directly understood from the Wasserstein geometry.
Third, though we have considered continuous systems, a similar structure appears for discrete systems that satisfy the discrete version of the local conservation law of probability. While the details are given in
Methods

Hamazaki 2022
;
Van Vu and Saito 2023
(see

in

and Supplementary Note 1).

Limits in unitary quantum dynamics.

Our main results in (
1
), (
2
), and (
3
) with
c=1
also hold for a completely different situation, i.e., unitary quantum dynamics, described by the von Neumann equation of the density matrix as
\frac{\mathrm{d}\hat{\rho}}{\mathrm{d}t}=-\frac{i}{\hbar}[\hat{H},\hat{\rho}]
.
Indeed, we can take
\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}[\hat{A}]=\frac{i}{\hbar}[\hat{H},\hat{A}]
, which satisfies (
7
) and thus (
6
).
Therefore, our main results apply under the velocity observable

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{V}}_{A}=\frac{i}{\hbar}[\hat{H},\hat{A}]+\dot{\hat{A}}.

(11)

For example, for time-independent
\hat{A}
, (
2
) reads

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}\braket{\hat{A}}}{\mathrm{d}t}\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma_{A}}{\mathrm{d}t}\right)^{2}\leq C_{A}=-\frac{\braket{[\hat{H},\hat{A}]^{2}}}{\hbar^{2}},

(12)

where
C_{A}
is often easy to evaluate (see the time-dependent case later). This inequality is meaningful for the semi-classical limit
\hbar\rightarrow 0
, in stark contrast with the Mandelstam-Tamm bound
Mandelstam and Tamm 1945
(see
Methods
).
Note that
\mathcal{\hat{V}}_{A}
is the Schrodinger picture of the time derivative of the observable in the Heisenberg representation.
Indeed, its Heisenberg picture satisfies
\mathcal{\hat{V}}_{A}^{\mathrm{H}}(t)=\frac{\mathrm{d}\hat{A}^{\mathrm{H}}(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}
with
\hat{X}^{\mathrm{H}}(t)=U^{\dagger}(t)\hat{X}(t)U(t)
and
U(t)=\mathbb{T}e^{-i\int_{0}^{t}\hat{H}(s)ds}
(
\mathbb{T}
denotes the time ordering).

Example 3: Dynamics of spin systems.

We first consider
\hat{H}=g\hat{s}^{x}
and
\hat{A}=\hat{s}^{z}
, where
\hat{s}^{s,y,z}
is the spin-1/2 spin operator.
Let us take an initial state given by
\ket{\psi_{0}}=\cos(\theta/2)\ket{\uparrow}+i\sin(\theta/2)\ket{\downarrow}
with arbitrary
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
, where
\ket{\uparrow}\>(\ket{\downarrow})
is the eigenstate of
\hat{s}^{z}
with eigenvalue
+1\>(-1)
.
We then find that
\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}\braket{{\hat{A}}}}{\mathrm{d}t}\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma_{A}}{\mathrm{d}t}\right)^{2}=-\braket{[\hat{H},\hat{A}]^{2}}/\hbar^{2}
holds for arbitrary time, which means that inequalities (
1
) and (
2
) become equalities.

Our results are useful even for quantum many-body systems.
For example, let us consider a spin-
S
system whose Hamiltonian reads

\hat{H}=\sum_{ij}J_{ij}(\hat{S}_{i}^{x}\hat{S}_{j}^{x}+\hat{S}_{i}^{y}\hat{S}_{j}^{y})+\Delta_{ij}\hat{S}_{i}^{z}\hat{S}_{j}^{z}+\sum_{i}h_{i}\hat{S}_{i}^{z}+g\hat{S}_{i}^{x}
, where
J_{ij},h_{i}
and
g
are arbitrary (
\hat{S}_{i}^{x,y,z}
is the spin-
S
spin operator at site
i
).
Then, if we take
\hat{A}=\hat{M}_{z}=\sum_{i}\hat{S}_{i}^{z}
, we have

\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}{\sigma}_{M_{z}}}{\mathrm{d}t}\right|\leq|g|\sigma_{M_{y}}
, where
\hat{M}_{y}=\sum_{i}\hat{S}_{i}^{y}
.
Therefore, fluctuation dynamics of magnetization in
z
-direction is simply bounded by the fluctuation of that in
y
-direction (or vice versa).
We numerically demonstrate our limits to fluctuation dynamics for quantum many-body systems in Supplementary Note 2.
There, we also show that our bound leads to the tighter estimation of the speed of the mean than the Mandelstam-Tamm bound.

Example 4: Amount of work done.

Importantly, our bounds are also applicable to time-dependent observables. This fact leads to a speed limit for the amount of work done in time-dependent unitary dynamics.
Specifically, let us assume the initial state
\hat{\rho}(0)
that is diagonal in the energy eigenbasis of
\hat{H}(0)
.
In this case, the instantaneous averaged work done on the system at time
t
is represented by
\frac{\mathrm{d}{W}(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}=\frac{\mathrm{d}\braket{\hat{H}(t)}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\mathrm{Tr}\left[\hat{\rho}(t)\frac{\mathrm{d}\hat{H}(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}\right]
, and the total work is given by
W(t_{\mathrm{fin}})=\mathrm{Tr}[\hat{\rho}(t_{\mathrm{fin}})\hat{H}(t_{\mathrm{fin}})]-\mathrm{Tr}[\hat{\rho}(0)\hat{H}(0)]

Deffner et al. 2016
.
We also find that
\hat{\mathcal{V}}_{A}=\frac{\mathrm{d}\hat{H}(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}
. Then, our inequality leads to

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}{W}}{\mathrm{d}t}\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma_{H(t)}}{\mathrm{d}t}\right)^{2}\leq C_{H(t)}=\left\langle\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}\hat{H}(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}\right)^{2}\right\rangle.

(13)

This inequality indicates that, given
C_{H(t)}
, both the instantaneous work and the fluctuation change are upper-bounded.
Note that we often consider
\hat{H}(t)=\hat{H}_{1}+\lambda(t)\hat{H}_{2}
, where
\hat{H}_{2}
takes much simpler form than
\hat{H}_{1}
(e.g.,
\hat{H}_{1}
has a complicated interaction but
\hat{H}_{2}
is non-interacting).
In such cases,
C_{H(t)}
is easier to access experimentally than the instantaneous work and energy fluctuation.
Moreover, since
C_{H(t)}\leq\left|\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}\hat{H}(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}\right|\right|_{\infty}^{2}
, the above inequality states that the instantaneous work and the fluctuation change cannot be large when the control cost of changing the Hamiltonian
\left|\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}\hat{H}(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}\right|\right|_{\infty}
is given.

Furthermore, integrating over the entire interval and using the triangle inequality, we have

\displaystyle{W({t_{\mathrm{fin}}})^{2}+(\sigma_{H(t_{\mathrm{fin}})}-\sigma_{H(0)})^{2}}\leq t_{\mathrm{fin}}^{2}{\overline{C_{H(t)}}},

(14)

where
\overline{X}=\frac{1}{t_{\mathrm{fin}}}\int_{0}^{t_{\mathrm{fin}}}Xdt
.
There are some applications of these inequalities. For example, if the work and its fluctuation are difficult to measure because of complicated form of
\hat{H}
, we can use (
14
) to evaluate it by
\overline{C_{H}(t)}
, which is often easy to measure (see the previous paragraph).
As another implication, let us assume
that we want to perform a state transition from initial to final states with the given amount of work
W_{\mathrm{fin}}
and the energy-fluctuation difference
\Delta\sigma_{\mathrm{fin}}
.
Then, (
14
) immediately indicates the lower bound for the transition time in terms of
\overline{C_{H}(t)}
, which quantifies the average magnitude of the Hamiltonian change, as

t_{\mathrm{fin}}\geq\sqrt{\frac{W_{\mathrm{fin}}^{2}+\Delta\sigma_{\mathrm{fin}}^{2}}{\overline{C_{H(t)}}}}.

We note that even a weaker version of this inequality,
t_{\mathrm{fin}}\geq\frac{|W_{\mathrm{fin}}|}{\sqrt{\overline{C_{H(t)}}}},

has not been obtained before, while this is a fundamental speed limit concerning the work.
We also note that several
recent works discussed similar bounds on the speed of the erergy fluctuations, which set the quantum acceleration limits for the projective Hilbert space
Pati 2023
;
Cafaro et al. 2024
.

Limits from the classical information theory.

So far, we have considered situations where our results with
c=1
hold with the condition (
7
).
Now let us discuss another scenario, where Eq. (
6
) and thus our main results hold for
c=1/2
.
Although the theory applies to both continuous and discrete systems, we here present the case with discrete systems,
\rho=\{p_{i}\}
.
For an observable
A=\{A_{i}\}
, we have
the average
(A|\rho)=\braket{A}=\sum_{i}p_{i}A_{i}
and
(A|\mathcal{L}[\rho])=\sum_{i}p_{i}\delta A_{i}\dot{p}_{i}/p_{i}=(\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}|\rho)
, where we have defined

(\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}[A])_{i}=\delta A_{i}\dot{p}_{i}/p_{i}=-\delta A_{i}\dot{I}_{i}
, with
{I}_{i}=-\ln p_{i}
called the surprisal
Nicholson et al. 2020
.
Then,
\frac{\mathrm{d}\braket{A}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\braket{\mathcal{V}_{A}}
with Eq. (
4
) leads to the continuous-time Price equation
Price 1972
,
\frac{\mathrm{d}\braket{A}}{\mathrm{d}t}-\dot{A}=-\braket{\delta A\>\dot{I}}=\mathrm{cov}(A,-\dot{I})
, where
\mathrm{cov}(X,Y)=\braket{XY}-\braket{X}\braket{Y}
is the covariance.
Using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we find the speed limit for the mean
Nicholson et al. 2020
,
\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}\braket{A}}{\mathrm{d}t}-\dot{A}\right|\leq\sigma_{A}\sqrt{\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{C}}}
, where
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{C}}=\sigma_{\dot{I}}=\sum_{i}\frac{\dot{p}_{i}^{2}}{p_{i}}
is the classical Fisher information.

In contrast, if we consider the fluctuation, we cannot necessarily obtain our main results (
1
)-(
3
) for
\mathcal{V}_{A}=\dot{A}+\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}[A]
since Eq. (
6
) does not hold.
For example,
\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}
does not satisfy Eq. (
7
) in general.
To proceed, we focus on the time-independent observable
\dot{A}=0
in the following.
Then, while Eq. (
7
) is not still satisfied, we find that
Eq. (
6
) with
c=1/2
holds for this case, by noticing that
\braket{\braket{A^{2}}\dot{I}}=\braket{\braket{A}\dot{I}}=0
.
Therefore, for time-independent observables
A
, we have
\frac{\mathrm{d}\braket{\delta A^{2}}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\braket{\delta A\>\delta\mathcal{V}_{A}}=\mathrm{cov}(A,\mathcal{V}_{A})
,
\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma_{A}}{\mathrm{d}t}\right|\leq\frac{\sigma_{\mathcal{V}_{A}}}{2}
, and

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}\braket{A}}{\mathrm{d}t}\right)^{2}+4\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma_{A}}{\mathrm{d}t}\right)^{2}\leq C_{A}

(21)

with

\displaystyle(\mathcal{V}_{A})_{i}=(\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}[A])_{i}=-\delta A_{i}\dot{I}_{i}.

(22)

Now, using the Hölder inequality (instead of the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality as done for the mean
Nicholson et al. 2020
;
Ito and Dechant 2020
),
C_{A}=\braket{\mathcal{V}_{A}^{2}}
is upper bounded as

\displaystyle C_{A}\leq\|\delta A\|_{\infty}^{2}\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{C}}.

(23)

Here,
\|\delta A\|_{\infty}=\max_{i}|\delta A_{i}|\leq\Delta_{A}
, where
\Delta_{A}
is the difference between the maximum and minimum values of
A
and often easily computed.
Our inequalities mean that, as in the conventional speed limit only for the mean
Nicholson et al. 2020
;
Ito and Dechant 2020
, the sum of the squared standard deviation and the mean obeys the time-information uncertainty relation, i.e., the times for the change of those fundamental quantities and the Fisher information (the metric of information geometry) cannot be simultaneously small.

However, we again stress that these results for the fluctuation cannot be inferred from previous results for the mean
Nicholson et al. 2020
;
Ito and Dechant 2020
, which are obtained without the nontrivial condition in Eq. (
6
) [see also
Methods
for the distinctions from
Nicholson et al. 2020
;
Ito and Dechant 2020
;
García-Pintos et al. 2022
].

Example 5: Nonlinear population dynamics.

Let us consider nonlinear population dynamics, where the number
N_{i}
of some types (such as species in an ecological system) labeled by
i=1,\cdots,f
obeys some dynamical equation,
\dot{N}_{i}=F_{i}(N_{1},\cdots,N_{f},t)
. Note that
F
is nonlinear in general.
While the total number of the types
N_{\mathrm{tot}}=\sum_{i=1}^{f}N_{i}
changes in time, the proportion for each type
i
, i.e.,
p_{i}=N_{i}/N_{\mathrm{tot}}
, satisfies
\sum_{i=1}^{f}p_{i}=1
.
We can then apply our general discussion above.
We stress that our fluctuation relations
\frac{\mathrm{d}\braket{\delta A^{2}}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\mathrm{cov}(A,\mathcal{V}_{A})
and
\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma_{A}}{\mathrm{d}t}\right|\leq\frac{\sigma_{\mathcal{V}_{A}}}{2}
are fundamentally distinct from the Price equation,
\frac{\mathrm{d}\braket{A}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\braket{\mathcal{V}_{A}}=-\mathrm{cov}(A,\dot{I})

Price 1972
and the speed limit of the mean
Adachi et al. 2022
;
García-Pintos 2024
;
Hoshino et al. 2023
.

We especially take
F_{i}=s_{i}N_{i}
, which describes the evolutionary dynamics without mutation, where
s_{i}
is the growth rate for type
i
.
The dynamical equation for
p_{i}
then reads
\dot{p}_{i}=\delta s_{i}\>p_{i}
, where
\delta s_{i}=s_{i}-\braket{s}
.
In this case, we can obtain
(\mathcal{V}_{A})_{i}=\delta A_{i}\delta s_{i}

and
\frac{\mathrm{d}\braket{\delta A^{2}}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\braket{\delta A^{2}\delta s}
, which leads to
\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}\braket{A}}{\mathrm{d}t}\right)^{2}+4\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma_{A}}{\mathrm{d}t}\right)^{2}\leq C_{A}=\braket{{\delta A^{2}\delta s^{2}}}\leq\|\delta A\|_{\infty}^{2}\sigma_{s}^{2}
.
Thus, the
C_{A}
is bounded using the variance of the growth rate,
\sigma_{s}^{2}
.

As the simplest case, let us consider
A=s
, for which we have
\frac{\mathrm{d}\braket{{s}}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\braket{\mathcal{V}_{s}}=\sigma_{s}^{2}
, which is known as the Fisher’s fundamental theorem of natural selection
Fisher 1999
. In this case, we furthermore find a nontrivial higher-order equality
\frac{\mathrm{d}\braket{\delta s^{2}}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\braket{\delta s^{3}}
, meaning that the change of the variance of the growth rate is related to that of the skewness.

Limits in arbitrary quantum dynamics.

Finally, let us discuss arbitrary quantum dynamics, which may include dissipation and thus non-unitarity.
Because of the non-commutative nature of quantum theory,

we have not found a suitable velocity observable for which (
1
)-(
3
) hold.
For example, since the general dynamics is given by
\frac{\mathrm{d}\hat{\rho}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\frac{1}{2}(\hat{\rho}\hat{L}+\hat{L}\hat{\rho})
using the symmetric-logarithmic derivative
\hat{L}

García-Pintos et al. 2022
, it is natural to take
\mathcal{\hat{V}}_{A}=\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}[\hat{A}]+\dot{\hat{A}}
with

\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}[\hat{A}]=\frac{1}{2}\{\hat{A},\hat{L}\}
.
Indeed, this leads to the quantum speed limit for the mean in Ref.
García-Pintos et al. 2022
with the help of the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality.
However, the above
\mathcal{\hat{V}}_{A}
does
not
satisfy Eq. (
6
) (even when
\dot{\hat{A}}=0
), and thus (
1
)-(
3
) cannot be proved. For another example, if we assume the Lindlad equation for the dynamics, it is natural to expect
\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}[\hat{A}]=i[\hat{H},\hat{A}]+\sum_{\mu}\hat{l}_{\mu}^{\dagger}\hat{A}\hat{l}_{\mu}-\frac{1}{2}\{\hat{A},\hat{l}_{\mu}^{\dagger}\hat{l}_{\mu}\}
, where
\hat{l}_{\mu}
is the Lindblad jump operator. However, this does not satisfy Eq. (
6
), either.

While our results, e.g., Eq. (
1
), may break down for general quantum systems,
we can still find an information-theoretical bound for the tradeoff relation between the changes of the mean and the standard deviation for time-independent observables (see Supplementary Note 4):

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}\braket{{\hat{A}}}}{\mathrm{d}t}\right)^{2}+4\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma_{A}}{\mathrm{d}t}\right)^{2}\leq\braket{\hat{L}\delta\hat{A}^{2}\hat{L}}\leq\|\delta\hat{A}\|_{\infty}^{2}\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{Q}},

(24)

where

\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{Q}}=\braket{\hat{L}^{2}}
is the quantum Fisher information
Liu et al. 2019
.
We recover inequality (
23
) in the classical limit.
We note that inequality (
24
) is fundamentally distinct from the (generalized) quantum Cramér-Rao inequality
\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}\braket{{\hat{A}}}}{\mathrm{d}t}\right|\leq\sigma_{A}\sqrt{\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{Q}}}
since (
24
) tells us the upper bound on the speed of the fluctuation
\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma_{A}}{\mathrm{d}t}
.

By considering the unitary dynamics and pure state, (
24
) becomes

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}\braket{\hat{A}}}{\mathrm{d}t}\right)^{2}+4\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma_{A}}{\mathrm{d}t}\right)^{2}\leq\frac{4\braket{\delta\hat{H}\delta\hat{A}^{2}\delta\hat{H}}}{\hbar^{2}}\leq\frac{4\|\delta\hat{A}\|_{\infty}^{2}\Delta E^{2}}{\hbar^{2}},

(25)

which is the inequality different from (
12
).
This means that the energy fluctuation can be the upper bound on the change of the standard deviations, similar to the spirit of conventional speed limits in isolated quantum systems.
We stress that our result addresses such an energy-fluctuation-based bound on the fluctuation of observables, not mean.
We also note that this inequality is true even for mixed states, as shown in Supplementary Note 4.

Discussion

We here discuss the ditinctions of our work from previous results.

Our previous work
Hamazaki 2022
also
considered speed limits for the fluctuation of observables for a specific situation.
However, the inequalities (
1
) and (
2
) are tighter and more general.
For tightness, the previous results in
Hamazaki 2022
lack the term
\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}\braket{A}}{\mathrm{d}t}\right)^{2}
in (
2
).
Consequently, the results in
Hamazaki 2022
cannot suggest crucial physical implications found in our present manuscript, e.g., the tradeoff relation between the speeds of the mean and the standard deviation.
For generality, Ref.
Hamazaki 2022
only treated bounds based on the classical continuity equation of probability; in contrast, we can treat, e.g., bounds in unitary quantum systems for general observables (
12
) and information-theoretical bounds (
21
), which are not obtained from the method in Ref.
Hamazaki 2022
.
We also note that the results in Ref.
Martinez-Azcona et al. 2023
focused on the variance
concerning different quantum trajectories of noisy systems, while we consider variance concerning the quantum fluctuation of the state.
Thus, the quantities of interest are different (e.g., only the latter includes the non-linearity with respect to the state), and the meanings of the obtained inequalities in Ref.
Martinez-Azcona et al. 2023
and our present paper are conceptually distinct.

In addition, some previous works studied quantum speed limits for quantum scrambling and correlation functions, which are related to fluctuations of dynamics
Parker et al. 2019
;
Hörnedal et al. 2022
;
Mohan and Pati 2022
;
Carabba et al. 2022
;
Hörnedal et al. 2023
.
Our approach of directly considering the fluctuation of observables (e.g., standard deviation)
is beneficial because it can primarily characterize the probability distribution of observables along with the mean.
Another striking consequence of our approach is the discovery of the concise inequalities (
1
) and (
2
), which lead to profound implications, e.g., a nontrivial tradeoff relation between the speeds of the mean and fluctuation.

Conclusion

To conclude, we discover a law in far-from-equilibrium dynamics,
stating that the speed of the fluctuation dynamics is always smaller than the fluctuation of the velocity observable.
This can also be rewritten as the tradeoff relation that the sum of the squares of the speeds of the mean and the standard deviation cannot exceed some quantity
C_{A}
, which is further bounded by physical quantities (e.g., entropy production) or information-theoretical quantity (i.e., the Fisher information).
We stress that our bounds can indicate the
upper
bound for the change of the fluctuation,
while the conventional speed limit based on the Cramér-Rao bound only leads to the
lower
bound on the fluctuation.

Our results are extended to cases with multiple parameters
\vec{\lambda}=(\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{Z})
, instead of the actual time
t
.
We can show limits for the change of the fluctuation of an observable when these parameters are varied; in this case, the bounds are generalized to matrix inequalities (see Supplementary Note 5).
As the Cramér-Rao inequalities for multiple parameters turn out to be useful in the field of (quantum) metrology, our inequalities
may also be applied to such metrological purposes, as well as non-equilibrium statistical mechanics.

Our fluctuation relations can apply to continuous-time classical and unitary quantum systems, deterministic and stochastic systems, and even nonlinear and many-body systems.
In contrast, we have not known yet whether we can extend our theory to broader classes of dynamics, e.g., general quantum systems (which are partially done as in (
24
)) and discrete-time dynamics.
It is also a crucial future task to harness these relations to control complicated systems at the level of fluctuations.

Extension of the current-based bound to discrete systems.

While we have mainly discussed the bounds based on the local conservation law for continuous systems in the main text, a similar structure appears for discrete systems.
To see this, let us consider a graph

Due to the existence of two types of averages,
\braket{\cdots}

,
\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}\braket{A}}{\mathrm{d}t}\right|\leq\sigma_{A}\sqrt{\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{C}}}
.

In contrast, our limits to fluctuation dynamics for the above
\mathcal{V}_{A}
are obtained by considering Eq. (
3
) with
c=1/2
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\braket{\delta{A}^{2}}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\braket{\delta A\delta\mathcal{V}_{A}}=\mathrm{cov}(A,\mathcal{V}_{A}),

(53)

which has not been obtained before.
We stress that this cannot be trivially obtained from Eq. (
52
).
Indeed, while it seems that (
53
) would be obtained by substituting
A
in Eq. (
52
) with
F(\delta A)\delta A
(
F
is some function) as

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\braket{F(\delta A)\delta{A}}}{\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle\overset{?}{=}\braket{F(\delta A)\delta A\delta(-\dot{I})}

\displaystyle=\braket{F(\delta A)\delta\mathcal{V}_{A}}=\mathrm{cov}(F(\delta A),\mathcal{V}_{A})

(54)

and taking
F(\delta A)=\delta A
, the first equation is incorrect in general because of the non-trivial dependence of
F(\delta A)
on
t
and
\{p_{i}\}
.
Note that while the equation happens to be correct for
F(\delta A)=\delta A
, this is a coincidence, where we carefully need to check the condition (
6
).
For example, a naive extension to the higher-order fluctuation cannot hold for this case,
\frac{\mathrm{d}\braket{\delta{A}^{3}}}{\mathrm{d}t}\neq\mathrm{cov}(\delta A^{3},-\dot{I})=\mathrm{cov}(\delta A^{2},\mathcal{V}_{A})
.
Moreover, the fact that (
53
) does not trivially follow from (
52
) is known from their quantum generalization; while quantum extension of Eq. (
52
) holds as discussed in Ref.
García-Pintos et al. 2022
, the naive quantum extension in (
53
) fails for general open quantum systems.
