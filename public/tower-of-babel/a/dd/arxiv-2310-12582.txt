Empirical Risk Minimization over Artificial Neural Networks Overcomes the Curse of Dimensionality in the Numerical Approximation of Linear Kolmogorov Partial Differential Equations with Unbounded Initial Functions
Jichang Xiao, Xiaoqun Wang

Abstract
Deep learning algorithms have been successfully applied to numerically solve linear Kolmogorov partial differential equations (PDEs). A recent research shows that the empirical risk minimization (ERM) over deep artificial neural networks overcomes the curse of dimensionality in the numerical approximation of linear Kolmogorov PDEs with bounded initial functions. However, the initial functions may be unbounded in many applications such as the Black Scholes PDEs in pricing call options. In this paper, we extend this result to the cases involving unbounded initial functions. We prove that for d d -dimensional linear Kolmogorov PDEs with unbounded initial functions, under suitable assumptions, the number of training data and the size of the artificial neural network required to achieve an accuracy ε \varepsilon for the ERM grow polynomially in both d d and ε − 1 \varepsilon^{-1} . Moreover, we verify that the required assumptions hold for Black-Scholes PDEs and heat equations which are two important cases of linear Kolmogorov PDEs.

Introduction

Partial differential equations (PDEs) have been widely used in modeling problems in physics, finance and engineering. Traditional numerical methods for solving PDEs such as finite differences
[
26
]
and finite elements
[
6
]
suffer from the curse of dimensionality, which implies that the computational cost grows exponentially as the dimension increases.
Recently, many deep learning-based algorithms have been proposed for different classes of PDEs, see
[
2
,
9
,
15
,
21
,
22
,
23
,
25
]
.

In this paper, we focus on the numerical approximation of linear Kolmogorov PDEs, with a special attention to heat equations and the Black-Scholes PDEs. Beck et al.
[
2
]
first proposed a deep learning algorithm to approximate the solution over a full hypercube by reformulating the approximation problem into an optimization problem. Berner et al.
[
4
]
further developed the deep learning algorithm to solve parametric Kolmogorov PDEs. Richter et al.
[
23
]
introduced different formulations of the risk functional to enhance the robustness of the deep learning algorithm. The numerical results in
[
2
,
4
,
23
]
demonstrated the effectiveness of the deep learning algorithms even in high dimensions. These findings suggest that deep learning algorithms for solving linear Kolmogorov PDEs do not suffer from curse of dimensionality.

One theoretical explanation for this phenomenon is that the solutions of Kolmogorov PDEs can be approximated by deep artificial neural networks in which the number of parameters grows polynomially in both the prescribed accuracy
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
and the dimension
d
, see
[
10
,
13
,
17
,
18
]
. Besides the analysis of the approximation error for deep artificial neural networks, Berner et al.
[
5
]
proved that under suitable conditions, the generalization error resulted from empirical risk minimization (ERM) also break the curse of dimensionality.

Our work are inspired by Berner et al.
[
5
]
. We notice that the generalization results in
[
5
]
are established only for linear Kolmogorov PDEs with bounded initial functions. There are many linear Kolmogorov PDEs in which the initial functions are in fact unbounded , for example, the Black-Sholes PDEs in the problems of the option pricing of basket call and call on max options. In this paper, we prove that for linear Kolmogorov PDEs with unbounded initial function, the generalization error still does not suffer from the curse of dimensionality if some assumptions are satisfied. For Black-Scholes PDEs and heat equations, we prove that these assumptions always hold. For the approximation error, we extend the theoretical results in
[
13
]
and establish approximation properties of clipped neural networks for solutions of linear Kolmogorov PDEs with unbounded initial functions, affine drift function and affine diffusion function. Summarizing the results of the generalization error and the approximation error, we show that the ERM indeed overcomes the curse of dimensionality for linear Kolmogorov PDEs with unbounded initial functions.

This paper is organised as follows. In Section
2
, we introduce the background knowledge related to the optimization problem and the ERM principle, we also introduce two important linear Kolmogorov PDEs that are the heat equation and Black-Scholes PDEs. In Section
3
, for linear Kolmogorov PDEs with unbounded initial functions, we establish theoretical results of the generalization error and the approximation error, respectively. By combining the two parts, we prove that the ERM over clipped neural networks overcomes the curse of dimensionality. Section
4
concludes the paper.

Background Knowledge

2.1
Deep learning and empirical risk minimization

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
,
T\in(0,\infty)
and let
f_{d}(x,t)\in C(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,T],\mathbb{R})
be the solution of the linear Kolmogorv PDE which satisfies for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
t\in(0,T)
that
f_{d}(x,0)=\varphi_{d}(x)
and

(\nabla_{t}f_{d})(x,t)=\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Trace}\left(\sigma_{d}(x)[\sigma_{d}(x)]^{*}(\nabla_{x}^{2}f_{d})(x,t)\right)+\left<\mu_{d}(x),(\nabla_{x}f_{d})(x,t)\right>_{\mathbb{R}^{d}},

(1)

where
\mu_{d}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\sigma_{d}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
are Lipschitz continuous,
\varphi_{d}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is the initial function,
\left[\sigma_{d}(x)\right]^{*}
is the transpose of matrix
\sigma_{d}(x)
.

Let the interval
[u,v]\subset\mathbb{R}
. Throughout this paper, we focus on the numerical approximation of the endpoint solution
f_{d}(\cdot,T)
on
[u,v]^{d}
. Beck et al.
[
2
]
first proposed a novel deep learning algorithm to approximate
f_{d}(x,T),x\in[u,v]^{d}
by artificial neural networks. The key idea is to reformulate the solutions of Kolmogorov PDEs as the solutions of infinite dimensional optimization problems, see
[
2
, Corollary 2.4]
for details. The fundamental theory in
[
2
]
considers the strong solution
f_{d}(x,t)\in C^{2,1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,T],\mathbb{R})
. For the viscosity solution
[
14
]
, especially for the solution of Black-Scholes PDEs, the optimization problem is still valid under some regularity conditions, see
[
5
, Lemma 3.2]
and
[
4
, Theorem A.1]
. The optimization problem is stated without proof in the following setting.

Setting 1
(Optimization problem associated with linear Kolmogorov PDEs).

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
,
[u,v]\subset\mathbb{R}
, let
\mu_{d}
,
\sigma_{d}
,
\varphi_{d}
be functions in linear Kolmogorov PDEs (
1
). Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
be a probability space with a normal filtration
\left(\mathcal{F}_{t}\right)_{t\in[0,T]}
, let
\left\{B_{t},\mathcal{F}_{t}\mid 0\leq t\leq T\right\}
be the standard
d
-dimensional Brownian motion, and
X_{d}:\Omega\to[u,v]^{d}
be uniformly distributed and
\mathcal{F}_{0}
-measurable. Let
\left\{S_{t},\mathcal{F}_{t}\mid 0\leq t\leq T\right\}
be the continuous stochastic process satisfying that for every
t\in[0,T]
it holds
\mathbb{P}
-a.s.

S_{t}=X_{d}+\int_{0}^{t}\mu_{d}(S_{u})du+\int_{0}^{t}\sigma_{d}(S_{u})dB_{u}.

(2)

Denote
Y_{d}=S_{T}
, define the risk functional

\mathcal{E}_{d}(f)=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(f(X_{d})-\varphi_{d}(Y_{d})\right)^{2}\right]

for every
f\in\mathcal{L}^{2}([u,v]^{d})
. The optimization problem is given by

\min_{f\in\mathcal{L}^{2}([u,v]^{d})}\mathcal{E}_{d}(f).

Then it holds that

f_{d}(\cdot,T)=\underset{f\in\mathcal{L}^{2}([u,v]^{d})}{\mathrm{argmin}}\mathcal{E}_{d}(f),

(3)

where
f_{d}(x,t)\in C(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,T],\mathbb{R})
is the solution of the linear Kolmogorov PDE (
1
).

Remark 2
.

In this paper, we assume that the solution of the optimization problem is indeed
f_{d}(x,T),x\in[u,v]^{d}
. It is beyond the scope of this paper to study on the regularity conditions under which the solution
f_{d}(\cdot,T)
solves the optimization problem. It has been proved that (
3
) holds for heat equations and Black-Sholes PDEs, we refer the readers to
[
2
,
4
,
5
]
for the rigorous proof.

We cannot directly compute the risk
\mathcal{E}
due to its dependence on the unknown random variable
S_{T}
. In matching learning theory, the ERM principle solves the optimization problem by minimizing the empirical risk over the hypothesis class
\mathcal{H}\subseteq C([u,v]^{d},\mathbb{R})
.

Setting 3
(Empirical Risk Minimization).

Assume Setting
1
, let

\left\{\bm{x}_{i},\bm{y}_{i}\mid 1\leq i<\infty\right\}
be the independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) samples simulated from population
(X_{d},Y_{d})
. Let
m\in\mathbb{N}
, define the empirical risk
\mathcal{E}_{d,m}
by

\mathcal{E}_{d,m}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(f(\bm{x}_{i})-\varphi_{d}(\bm{y}_{i})\right)^{2}.

Let
C([u,v]^{d})
be the Banach space of continuous functions with norm
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\infty}
defined by

\left\|f\right\|_{\infty}=\underset{x\in[u,v]^{d}}{\max}\left|f(x)\right|.

Let the hypothesis class
\mathcal{H}\subseteq C([u,v]^{d})
be compact, the optimal estimator
f_{d,\mathcal{H}}
on
\mathcal{H}
is defined by

f_{d,\mathcal{H}}=\underset{f\in\mathcal{H}}{\mathrm{argmin}}\mathcal{E}_{d}(f),

the estimator obtained by ERM is defined by

f_{d,m,\mathcal{H}}=\underset{f\in\mathcal{H}}{\mathrm{argmin}}\mathcal{E}_{d,m}(f).

Remark 4
.

In classical learning theory
[
1
,
8
,
19
,
24
]
, the output
\varphi_{d}(Y_{d})
is usually assumed to be bounded, i.e.,
\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\varphi_{d}(y)\right|<\infty
. In this paper, the output is actually unbounded. The existence of
f_{d,\mathcal{H}}
and
f_{d,m,\mathcal{H}}
is guaranteed by the compactness of
\mathcal{H}
(see Proposition
5
below), the measurability of
f_{d,m,\mathcal{H}}:\Omega\to\mathcal{H}
is proved in
[
5
, Section S.1]
.

Proposition 5
.

Assume that
\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\varphi_{d}(Y_{d})\right|\right]<\infty
, then
f_{d,\mathcal{H}}
and
f_{d,m,\mathcal{H}}
in Setting
3
exist.

Proof.

For every
d\in\mathbb{N}
,
f_{1},f_{2}\in\mathcal{H}
, we have

\displaystyle\left|\mathcal{E}(f_{1})-\mathcal{E}(f_{2})\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left|f_{1}(X_{d})-f_{2}(X_{d})\right|\left|f_{1}(X_{d})+f_{2}(X_{d})-2\varphi_{d}(Y_{d})\right|\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\left|f_{1}(X_{d})\right|+\left|f_{2}(X_{d})\right|+2\left|\varphi_{d}(Y_{d})\right|\right]\right)\left\|f_{1}-f_{2}\right\|_{\infty},

by compactness of
\mathcal{H}
, the risk
\mathcal{E}:\mathcal{H}\to\mathbb{R}
is Lipschitz continuous on
\mathcal{H}
, thus
f_{d,\mathcal{H}}
exists. For every
\omega\in\Omega
,
f_{d,m,\mathcal{H}}(\omega)
exists in the same way.

In deep learning, a variety of artificial neural networks are designed for different application tasks. Here we take the widely-used feedforward architecture with the ReLU activation function as the hypothesis class
\mathcal{H}
, we call it ReLU neural networks.

Definition 6
(ReLU neural networks).

Let
L\in\mathbb{N}
,
N_{0},N_{1},\dots,N_{L}\in\mathbb{N}
, let
A_{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{l-1}\times N_{l}}
and
B_{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{l-1}}
for
l=1,2,\dots,L
. Denote the collection of
A_{l},B_{l}
by

\theta=\left((A_{1},B_{1}),\dots,(A_{L},B_{L})\right).

The ReLU neural network
\Phi_{\theta}:\mathbb{R}^{N_{0}}\to\mathbb{R}^{N_{L}}
with parameters
\theta
is defined by

\Phi_{\theta}(x)=W_{L}\circ\rho\circ W_{L-1}\circ\rho\cdots\circ\rho\circ W_{1}(x),

where
\left\{W_{l}(x)=A_{l}x+B_{l},1\leq l\leq L\right\}
are affine transformations and
\rho(x)
is the ReLU activation function applied component-wisely, i.e.,

\rho(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})=\left(\max\left\{x_{1},0\right\},\dots,\max\left\{x_{N},0\right\}\right).

Let
R\in(0,\infty)
,
a=\left(d,N_{1},\dots,N_{L-1},1\right)\in\mathbb{N}^{L+1}
, define the function class
\mathcal{N}_{a,R}
of ReLU neural networks with architecture
a
and parameter bound
R
by

\mathcal{N}_{a,R}=\left\{\Phi_{\theta}|_{[u,v]^{d}}:\left\|\theta\right\|_{\infty}\leq R\right\},

where
\left\|\theta\right\|_{\infty}=\max_{1\leq l\leq L}\max\left\{\left\|A_{l}\right\|_{\infty},\left\|B_{l}\right\|_{\infty}\right\}
. Define the depth
L(a)
, the width
W(a)
and the number of parameters
P(a)
of the neural networks respectively by

\displaystyle L(a)=L,\ \ W(a)=\left\|a\right\|_{\infty},\ \ P(a)=\sum_{l=1}^{L}N_{l}(N_{l-1}+1).

We consider the restriction of neural networks on
[u,v]^{d}
since we only intend to approximate the solution on the hypercube
[u,v]^{d}
. The function class
\mathcal{N}_{a,R}
is indeed a compact subspace of
C([u,v]^{d})
, which can be proved with the Lipschitz continuity (
[
5
, Theorem 2.6]
) of the map
\theta\mapsto\Phi_{\theta}
and the compactness of
\left\{\left\|\theta\right\|_{\infty}\leq R\right\}
, here we equivalent
\theta
to a vector on
\mathbb{R}^{P(a)}
. The number of parameters
P(a)
and the parameter bound
R
are two important quantities that measure the complexity of the function class
\mathcal{N}_{a,R}
.

2.2
Estimation error of Empirical Risk Minimization

We evaluate the estimator
f_{d,m,\mathcal{H}}
obtained by ERM with the estimation error defined by

\mathbb{E}\left[\left(f_{d,m,\mathcal{H}}(X_{d})-f_{d}(X_{d},T)\right)^{2}\right]=\frac{1}{(v-u)^{d}}\left\|f_{d,m,\mathcal{H}}-f_{d}(\cdot,T)\right\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}([u,v]^{d})}^{2}.

The following lemma shows that the estimation error can be expressed in terms of the risk.

Lemma 7
.

Assume Settings
1
and
3
, for every
f\in C([a,b]^{d},\mathbb{R})
, we have

\mathbb{E}\left[\left(f(X_{d})-f_{d}(X_{d},T)\right)^{2}\right]=\mathcal{E}_{d}(f)-\mathcal{E}_{d}\left(f_{d}(\cdot,T)\right)

Proof.

Define
q(t)=\mathcal{E}_{d}\left(tf+(1-t)f_{d}(\cdot,T)\right)
. The function
q(t)
is a quadratic function and takes the minimum at
t=0
. Thus
q(1)-q(0)
equal to the coefficient of the term
t^{2}
which is exactly
\mathbb{E}\left[\left(f(X_{d})-f_{d}(X_{d},T)\right)^{2}\right]
. Hence we have

\mathcal{E}_{d}(f)-\mathcal{E}_{d}\left(f_{d}(\cdot,T)\right)=q(1)-q(0)=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(f(X_{d})-f_{d}(X_{d},T)\right)^{2}\right].

The proof is completed.

Based on Lemma
7
, we can derive the so-called bias-variance decomposition.

Corollary 8
.

For the estimation error, we have the decomposition

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left(f_{d,m,\mathcal{H}}(X_{d})-f_{d}(X_{d},T)\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\underbrace{\mathcal{E}_{d}(f_{d,m,\mathcal{H}})-\mathcal{E}_{d}(f_{d,\mathcal{H}})}_{\text{generalization error}}+\underbrace{(v-u)^{-d}\left\|f_{d,\mathcal{H}}-f_{d}(\cdot,T)\right\|_{L^{2}([u,v]^{d})}^{2}}_{\text{approximation error}}.

Proof.

By Lemma
7
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left(f_{d,m,\mathcal{H}}(X_{d})-f_{d}(X_{d},T)\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{d}(f_{d,m,\mathcal{H}})-\mathcal{E}_{d}\left(f_{d}(\cdot,T)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{d}(f_{d,m,\mathcal{H}})-\mathcal{E}_{d}\left(f_{d,\mathcal{H}}\right)+\mathcal{E}_{d}(f_{d,\mathcal{H}})-\mathcal{E}_{d}\left(f_{d}(\cdot,T)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{d}(f_{d,m,\mathcal{H}})-\mathcal{E}_{d}\left(f_{d,\mathcal{H}}\right)+\mathbb{E}\left[\left(f_{d,\mathcal{H}}(X_{d})-f_{d}(X_{d},T)\right)^{2}\right].

Note that
X_{d}
is uniformly distributed on
[u,v]^{d}
, the proof is completed.

We follow
[
3
,
5
]
to refer
\mathcal{E}_{d}(f_{d,m,\mathcal{H}})-\mathcal{E}_{d}(f_{d,\mathcal{H}})
as the generalization error. The classical learning theory attempts to establish bounds on the sample size
m
in terms of complexity measures of the hypothesis class
\mathcal{H}
such that the generalization error is smaller than
\varepsilon
with confidence greater than
1-\varrho
, i.e.,

\mathbb{P}\left[\mathcal{E}_{d}(f_{d,m,\mathcal{H}})-\mathcal{E}_{d}(f_{d,\mathcal{H}})\leq\varepsilon\right]\geq 1-\varrho,

see
[
1
,
8
,
19
,
24
]
for details. The approximation error measures how accurately the target
f_{d}(\cdot,T)
can be approximated by functions in
\mathcal{H}
, and it is independent of data
(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{y}_{i})
. There are many results on the approximation properties of artificial neural networks, for examples
[
7
,
13
,
16
,
20
,
11
]
. For linear kolmogorov PDEs, Berner et al.
[
5
]
prove that the ERM over clipped ReLU neural networks (see Definition
9
below) overcomes the curse of dimensionality. More precisely, they establish polynomial bounds on both
m
and
(P(a),R)
in terms of
(d,\varepsilon^{-1})
to ensure that

\mathbb{P}\left[\frac{1}{(v-u)^{d}}\left\|f_{d,m,\mathcal{H}}-f_{d}(\cdot,T)\right\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}([u,v]^{d})}^{2}\leq\varepsilon\right]\geq 1-\varrho.

(4)

Such theoretical results hold for linear Kolmogorov PDEs with affine drift functions
\mu_{d}
, affine diffusion functions
\sigma_{d}
and bounded initial functions
\varphi_{d}
satisfying

\sup_{d\in\mathcal{N}}\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\varphi_{d}(y)\right|\leq D<\infty.

The clipped ReLU neural networks introduced in
[
5
]
are truncation of the standard ReLU neural networks and uniformly bounded by a constant
D
.

Definition 9
.

Let
D\in(0,\infty)
, define function class
\mathcal{N}_{a,R,D}
of clipped ReLU neural networks with architecture
a
and weight bound
R
by

\mathcal{N}_{a,R,D}=\left\{\mathrm{Clip}_{D}\circ\Phi\mid\Phi\in\mathcal{N}_{a,R}\right\},

where the clipping function
\mathrm{Clip}_{D}
is given by

\mathrm{Clip}_{D}(x)=\max\left\{x,D\right\}\mathrm{sgn}(x).

In Section
3
, we extend the results (
4
) and prove that ERM over clipped neural networks also overcomes the curse of dimensionality if initial functions are unbounded.

2.3
Examples of linear Kolmogorov PDEs with unbounded initials

In this section, we introduce two important linear Kolmogorov PDEs: heat equations and Black-Scholes PDEs of which the initial functions are unbounded.

Setting 10
(Heat equation).

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
, denote
d
-dimensional identity matrix by
I_{d}
. Assume that drift function
\mu_{d}
and diffusion function
\sigma_{d}
satisfy

\mu_{d}(x)=0,\ \ \sigma_{d}(x)=\sqrt{2}I_{d},

(5)

then linear Kolmogorov PDE (
1
) becomes the heat equation defined by

(\nabla_{t}f_{d})(t,x)=(\Delta_{x}f_{d})(t,x).

For the heat equation on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, if the initial function
\varphi_{d}
is globally bounded and continuous, then the solution
f_{d}
exists and can be expressed as

f_{d}(x,t)=\frac{1}{\left(4\pi t\right)^{n/2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-\frac{\left\|x-y\right\|_{2}^{2}}{4t}}\varphi_{d}(y)dy,

(6)

moreover we have
f_{d}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,\infty))
, see
[
12
, Chapter 2.3]
for rigorous proof. The theoretical results of the generalization error in
[
5
]
hold for heat equations with bounded initial functions. However, for unbounded initial functions such as polynomials, the solution may also exists. In this paper, we pay more attention to the following example.

Example 11
(Heat equation with polynomial initial functions).

Assume Setting
10
, let
d,k\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\left\{c_{d,i},1\leq i\leq d\right\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
satisfy that
\sup_{d\in\mathbb{N},i=1,\dots,d}\left|c_{d,i}\right|<\infty
. The initial function
\varphi_{d}
is given by

\varphi_{d}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{d}c_{d,i}x_{i}^{k}.

The expectation form of the formula (
6
) is

f_{d}(x,T)=\mathbb{E}\left[\varphi_{d}\left(x+\sqrt{2T}Z\right)\right],

(7)

where
Z
is the
d
-dimensional standard normal variable. It is easy to prove that the endpoint solution
f_{d}(\cdot,T)
of Example
11
is still a polynomial of degree

.

Next, we consider the Black-Scholes PDE which governs the price evolution of European options under the Black–Scholes model.

Setting 12

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
, in accordance with reality, assume
[u,v]\subseteq(0,\infty)
. Let
(\alpha_{d,i})_{i=1}^{d}
,
(\beta_{d,i})_{i=1}^{d}\subseteq\mathbb{R}

\sup_{d\in\mathbb{N},1\leq i\leq d}\left(\left|\alpha_{d,i}\right|+\left|\beta_{d,i}\right|\right)<\infty,

let
\Sigma_{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
satisfy
\left\|\Sigma_{i,d}\right\|_{2}=1
,

, where

is the

. Assume drift function
\mu_{d}

\sigma_{d}
satisfy that

\mu_{d}(x)=\left(\alpha_{d,1}x_{1},\dots,\alpha_{d,d}x_{d}\right),\ \ \sigma_{d}(x)=\mathrm{diag}\left(\beta_{d,1}x_{1},\dots,\beta_{d,d}x_{d}\right)\Sigma_{d}.

For different choice of initial functions

, Black-Scholes PDEs model the pricing problem for different types of European options. We take basket call options and call on max options in
[
13
]
for examples since the corresponding initial functions are unbounded.

Example 13
(Basket call options).

Assume Setting
12
, let
\left(c_{d,i}\right)_{i=1}^{d}\subseteq[0,1]

\sum_{i=1}^{d}c_{d,i}=1
, let
K_{d}\in(0,\infty)
satisfy
\sup_{d\in\mathbb{N}}K_{d}<\infty
. The initial function
\varphi_{d}
is given by

\varphi_{d}(x)=\max\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{d}c_{d,i}x_{i}-K_{d},0\right\}.

(8)

(Call on max options).

Assume Setting

, let
\left(c_{d,i}\right)_{i=1}^{d}\subseteq[0,\infty)
satisfying

satisfying
\sup_{d\in\mathbb{N}}K_{d}<\infty
. The initial function
\varphi_{d}

(9)

3
Main Results

3.1
Generalization error

In this section, we establish the generalization error bound for unbounded initial functions. To deal with unbounded
\varphi_{d}
, we introduce the truncated function as well as the truncated risk and the truncated empirical risk. Throughout this section, we have
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{N}_{a,R,D}
.

Definition 15
.

Let
1\left\{\cdot\right\}
be the indicator function, for
K\in(0,\infty)
, the truncated function
\varphi_{d}^{(K)}(\cdot)
is defined by

\varphi_{d}^{(K)}(x)=1\left\{\left\|x\right\|_{\infty}\leq K\right\}\varphi_{d}(x),

the truncated risk
\mathcal{E}_{d}^{(K)}\left(\cdot\right)
is defined by

\mathcal{E}_{d}^{(K)}\left(f\right)=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(f(X_{d})-\varphi_{d}^{(K)}(Y_{d})\right)^{2}\right],

and the truncated empirical risk
\mathcal{E}_{d,m}^{(K)}\left(\cdot\right)
is defined by

\mathcal{E}_{d,m}^{(K)}\left(f\right)=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left[f(\bm{x}_{i})-\varphi_{d}^{(K)}(\bm{y}_{i})\right]^{2}.

The next lemma gives the upper bound of the generalization error in terms of the truncated risk and the truncated empirical risk.

Lemma 16
.

Assume Setting
3
. Let
K\in(0,\infty)
, then for every
\omega\in\Omega
it holds that

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{d}(f_{d,m,\mathcal{H}}(\omega))-\mathcal{E}_{d}(f_{d,\mathcal{H}})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\sup_{f\in\mathcal{H}}\left|\mathcal{E}_{d}(f)-\mathcal{E}_{d,m}(f)\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2(G_{1}+G_{2}+G_{3}),

where
G_{1},G_{2},G_{3}
are defined by

\displaystyle G_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup_{f\in\mathcal{H}}\left|\mathcal{E}_{d}(f)-\mathcal{E}_{d}^{(K)}(f)\right|,

\displaystyle G_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup_{f\in\mathcal{H}}\left|\mathcal{E}_{d}^{(K)}(f)-\mathcal{E}^{(K)}_{d,m}(f)\right|,

\displaystyle G_{3}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup_{f\in\mathcal{H}}\left|\mathcal{E}_{d,m}^{(K)}(f)-\mathcal{E}_{d,m}(f)\right|.

Proof.

For every
w\in\Omega
, by definition we have

\mathcal{E}_{d,m}\left(f_{d,m,\mathcal{H}}(\omega)\right)(\omega)\leq\mathcal{E}_{d,m}\left(f_{d,\mathcal{H}}\right)(\omega),

thus

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{d}\left(f_{d,m,\mathcal{H}}(\omega)\right)-\mathcal{E}_{d}\left(f_{d,\mathcal{H}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{d}\left(f_{d,m,\mathcal{H}}(\omega)\right)-\mathcal{E}_{d,m}\left(f_{d,m,\mathcal{H}}(\omega)\right)(\omega)+\mathcal{E}_{d,m}\left(f_{d,\mathcal{H}}\right)(\omega)-\mathcal{E}_{d}\left(f_{d,\mathcal{H}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\sup\limits_{f\in\mathcal{H}}\left|\mathcal{E}_{d}\left(f\right)-\mathcal{E}_{d,m}\left(f\right)(\omega)\right|\leq 2(G_{1}+G_{2}+G_{3}).

The last inequality follows from the triangle inequality.

By Lemma
16
, it suffices to derive conditions such that

\mathbb{P}\left[G_{i}\leq\varepsilon\right]\geq 1-\varrho,\ \ i=1,2,3.

The next assumption is necessary for subsequent analysis of
G_{i}
.

Assumption 17
(Basic assumptions for the output).

Let
Y_{d}=\left(Y_{d,1},\dots,Y_{d,d}\right)
. For the output
\varphi_{d}(Y_{d})
, assume that

(i)

Tail probability condition: There exists constant
c_{1}\in[0,\infty)
such that for every
d\in\mathbb{N}
,
t\in[1,\infty)
and
i\in\left\{1,2,\dots,d\right\}
it holds that

\mathbb{P}\left[\left|Y_{d,i}\right|\geq t\right]\leq 2\exp\left\{-c_{1}\left(\log t\right)^{2}\right\}.

(ii)

Polynomial growth condition: There exist constant
c_{2}\in(0,\infty)
and
\lambda\in[2,\infty)
such that for every
d\in\mathbb{N}
and
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
it holds that

\left|\varphi_{d}\left(y\right)\right|\leq c_{2}\left(1+\left\|y\right\|_{2}^{\lambda}\right).

Remark 18
.

The polynomial growth condition of the initial function
\varphi_{d}
typically holds for applications of the linear Kolmogorov PDE, whereas the tail probability condition for
Y=S_{T}
often does not. However, for important applications such as the heat equation and the Black-Scholes PDE, the tail probability condition always holds, we prove this statement in Proposition
24
.

The next lemma is a direct consequence of Assumption
17
.

Lemma 19
.

Assume that the output
\varphi_{d}(Y_{d})
satisfies the conditions in Assumption
17
. For every
m\in[0,\infty)
, we have

\sup_{d\in\mathbb{N},i=1,\dots,d}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|Y_{d,i}\right|^{m}\right]<\infty.

(10)

Moreover, for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

M_{k,d}\triangleq\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\varphi_{d}(Y_{d})\right|^{k}\right]=O(d^{\frac{k\lambda}{2}}).

Proof.

Apply the expectation formula in terms of tail probability, we have

\displaystyle\sup_{d\in\mathbb{N},i=1,\dots,d}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|Y_{d,i}\right|^{m}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup_{d\in\mathbb{N},i=1,\dots,d}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left[\left|Y_{d,i}\right|^{m}\geq t\right]dt\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sup_{d\in\mathbb{N},i=1,\dots,d}\left[1+\int_{1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left[\left|Y_{d,i}\right|^{m}\geq t\right]dt\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 1+\sup_{d\in\mathbb{N},i=1,\dots,d}\int_{0}^{\infty}2\exp\left\{-c_{1}\left(\frac{1}{m}\log t\right)^{2}\right\}dt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1+2\int_{1}^{\infty}\exp\left\{-c_{1}\left(\frac{1}{m}\log t\right)^{2}\right\}dt<\infty,

where the second inequality follows from the tail probability condition of
Y_{d}
. Notice that for
k=0
, obviously
M_{k,d}=1=O(1)
. Assume
k\geq 1
. From the polynomial growth condition of function
\varphi_{d}(\cdot)
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\varphi_{d}(Y_{d})\right|^{k}\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle c_{2}^{k}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(1+\left\|Y_{d}\right\|^{\lambda}\right)^{k}\right].

(11)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle c_{2}^{k}\mathbb{E}\left[C_{k}\left(1+\left\|Y_{d}\right\|^{k\lambda}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle c_{2}^{k}C_{k}+c_{2}^{k}C_{k}\mathbb{E}\left[d^{\frac{k\lambda}{2}-1}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left|Y_{d,i}\right|^{k\lambda}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle O(d^{\frac{k\lambda}{2}}),

where the constant
C_{k}
satisfies for every

it holds that

(1+x)^{k}\leq C_{k}(1+x^{k}),

and the third inequality in (
11
) follows from the Jensen’s inequality of the convex function
f(x)=x^{k\lambda/2}
on
[0,\infty)
, the last equality in (
11
) follows from (
10
), the proof is completed.

Proposition 20

Under Assumption

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\mathcal{E}_{d}(f_{d,m,\mathcal{H}})-\mathcal{E}_{d}(f_{d,\mathcal{H}})>\varepsilon\right]

\displaystyle\leq

Proposition 24
.

For the heat equation and the Black-Sholes PDE (see Settings
10
and
12
), the tail probability condition in Assumption
17
always hold. For initial functions of Examples
11
,
13
,
14
, the polynomial growth condition in Assumption

holds.

Proof.

For the heat equation, we can solve the stochastic differential equation (
2
) explicitly

Y_{d}=S_{T}=X_{d}+\sqrt{2}B_{T}.

Note that
X_{d,i}\in[u,v]
is bounded and
B_{T,i}
is normally distributed random variable
N(0,\sqrt{T})
. From the concentration theory
[
27
, Section 3.5]
,
X_{d,i}
and

are independent sub-Gaussian random variables, hence
Y_{d,i}=X_{d,i}+\sqrt{2}B_{T,i}
is also sub-Gaussian random variable. By sub-Gaussian properties
[
27
, Proposition 2.5.2]
, there exists a parameter
K
depending only on on

such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\left|Y_{d,i}\right|\geq t\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\exp\left\{-\frac{t^{2}}{K}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\exp\left\{-\frac{\left(\log t\right)^{2}}{K}\right\},

where the second inequality follows from
0\leq\log t\leq t
for
t\in[1,\infty)
, hence the tail probability condition is satisfied.

For the Black-Scholes PDE, we can also solve the SDE explicitly

\displaystyle Y_{d,i}=S_{T,i}=X_{d,i}\exp\left\{\left(\alpha_{d,i}-\frac{\left\|\beta_{d,i}\Sigma_{d,i}\right\|^{2}_{2}}{2}\right)T+\beta_{d,i}\left\langle\Sigma_{d,i},B_{T}\right\rangle\right\},

where
\Sigma_{d,i}
is the
i

\Sigma_{d}
and
\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle
is the inner product in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. For every
d\in\mathbb{N}
,

,

, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\left|Y_{d,i}\right|\geq t\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\log\left|Y_{d,i}\right|\geq\log t\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\log\left|X_{d,i}\right|+\left(\alpha_{d,i}-\frac{\left\|\beta_{d,i}\Sigma_{d,i}\right\|^{2}_{2}}{2}\right)T+\beta_{d,i}\left\langle\Sigma_{d,i},B_{T}\right\rangle\geq\log t\right].

Note that in Setting
12
, we assume that

0<u<v,\ \ \sup_{d\in\mathbb{N},1\leq i\leq d}\left(\left|\alpha_{d,i}\right|+\left|\beta_{d,i}\right|\right)<\infty,

and
\left\|\Sigma_{d,i}\right\|=1
for every

. Hence the random variable

\log\left|X_{d,i}\right|+\left(\alpha_{d,i}-\frac{\left\|\beta_{d,i}\Sigma_{d,i}\right\|_{2}^{2}}{2}\right)T

. In summary, the random variable

is the sum of two independent sub-Gaussian random variables, hence there exists a parameter
K
independent of

such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\left|Y_{d,i}\right|\geq t\right]\leq 2\exp\left\{-\frac{\left(\log t\right)^{2}}{K}\right\}.

It is obvious that the initial functions in Examples

,
13
,
14
satisfy the polynomial growth condition, the proof is completed.

Proposition 26

be the Hilbert-Schmidt norm on
\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
, let

, assume that for every
\varepsilon\in(0,1)

\left|\Psi_{d,\varepsilon}(x)\right|\leq C_{1}(1+\left\|x\right\|_{2}^{\lambda})\ \ \mathrm{and}\ \ \left|\Psi_{d,\varepsilon}(x)-\varphi_{d}(x)\right|\leq\varepsilon(1+\left\|x\right\|_{2}^{\lambda}).

Let

M_{d}=(p-1)^{1/2}\exp\left(3\lambda\left(1+L^{2}T\left(\sqrt{T}+\lambda p\right)^{2}\right)\right)\left(1+\left[\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\|x\right\|_{2}^{p\lambda}u_{d}(dx)\right]^{1/p}\right).

Then there exist neural networks
\Phi_{d,\varepsilon}
with parameters
\theta_{d,\varepsilon}

\left[\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|f_{d}(x,T)-\Phi_{d,\varepsilon}(x)\right|^{p}u_{d}(dx)\right]^{1/p}\leq\varepsilon,

\mathcal{P}(\Phi_{d,\varepsilon})\leq CM_{d}^{4}\varepsilon^{-4}\mathcal{P}(\Psi_{d,\varepsilon})
and
\left\|\theta_{d,\varepsilon}\right\|_{\infty}\leq Cd^{3/2}M_{d}^{2}\varepsilon^{-2}\left\|\eta_{d,\varepsilon}\right\|_{\infty},
where
C\in(0,\infty)
is a constant,
\mathcal{P}(\Phi_{d,\varepsilon})
denotes the number of parameters of
\Phi_{d,\varepsilon}

Proof.

Let
n=\lceil C_{1}^{2}\varepsilon^{-2}\rceil
and

C_{2}=2^{1/2}\left(1+LT+2L\sqrt{T}\right)^{p}\exp\left(L^{2}T\left(\sqrt{T}+2\right)^{2}\right).

Let
\mathcal{A}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
,
\mathcal{B}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
be the same random variables as in the proof of
[
13
, Proposition 3.4]
such that for every

f_{d}(x,T)=\mathbb{E}\left[\varphi_{d}\left(\mathcal{A}x+\mathcal{B}\right)\right].

Let
\left(\mathcal{A}_{i},\mathcal{B}_{i}\right)_{i=1}^{n}
be i.i.d. random variables with the same distribution as
\left(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}\right)
, define

\displaystyle G

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(4M_{d}\varepsilon)^{-1}\left[\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|f_{d}(x,T)-\frac{1}{n}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Psi_{d,\varepsilon}\circ\left(\mathcal{A}_{i}x+\mathcal{B}_{i}\right)\right]\right|^{p}u_{d}(dx)\right]^{1/p}

\displaystyle+(6C_{2}dn)^{-1}\max_{i=1}^{n}\left(\left\|\mathcal{A}_{i}\right\|_{2}+\left\|\mathcal{B}_{i}\right\|_{2}\right).

From the proof of
[
5
, Theorem3.3]
and
[

, Proposition 3.4 and Corollary 3.5]
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[G\right]\leq(4M_{d}\varepsilon)^{-1}(\varepsilon+n^{-1/2}C_{1})M_{d}+(6C_{2}dn)^{-1}3C_{2}dn.

Note that
n\geq C_{1}^{2}\varepsilon^{-2}
, we can prove that
\mathbb{E}\left[G\right]\leq 1
. Hence there exists
\omega\in\Omega
such that

, consequently we have

\displaystyle\left[\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|f_{d}(x,T)-\frac{1}{n}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Psi_{d,\varepsilon}\circ\left(\mathcal{A}_{i}(\omega)x+\mathcal{B}_{i}(\omega)\right)\right]\right|^{p}u_{d}(dx)\right]^{1/p}\leq 4M_{d}\varepsilon,

\displaystyle\max_{i=1}^{n}\left(\left\|\mathcal{A}_{i}(\omega)\right\|_{2}+\left\|\mathcal{B}_{i}(\omega)\right\|_{2}\right)\leq 6C_{2}dn.

By
[
5
, Lemma A.4]
, there exist ReLU neural networks
\Phi_{d,\varepsilon}
with parameter
\theta_{d,\varepsilon}
such that

\Phi_{d,\varepsilon}(x)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Psi_{d,\varepsilon}\circ\left(\mathcal{A}_{i}(\omega)x+\mathcal{B}_{i}(\omega)\right),

\mathcal{P}(\Phi_{d,\varepsilon})\leq n^{2}\mathcal{P}(\Psi_{d,\varepsilon})=\left(\lceil C_{1}^{2}\varepsilon^{-2}\rceil\right)^{2}\mathcal{P}(\Psi_{d,\varepsilon}),

and

\displaystyle\left\|\theta_{d,\varepsilon}\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sqrt{d}\max_{i=1}^{n}\left(\left\|\mathcal{A}_{i}(\omega)\right\|_{2}+\left\|\mathcal{B}_{i}(\omega)\right\|_{2}+1\right)\left\|\eta_{d,\varepsilon}\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sqrt{d}\left(6C_{2}d\lceil C_{1}^{2}\varepsilon^{-2}\rceil+1\right)\left\|\eta_{d,\varepsilon}\right\|_{\infty},

For
\Phi_{d,\varepsilon}
, we have

\left[\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|f_{d}(x,T)-\Phi_{d,\varepsilon}(x)\right|^{p}u_{d}(dx)\right]^{1/p}\leq 4M_{d}\varepsilon.

Substituting
\varepsilon
with
(4M_{d})^{-1}\varepsilon
, the proof is completed.

Proposition
26
shows that if

grows polynomially in

and the initial functions
\varphi_{d}
can be approximated by neural networks
\Psi_{d,\varepsilon}
with
\mathcal{P}(\Psi_{d,\varepsilon})
and

growing polynomially in the accuracy

and the dimension

, then the solutions of linear Kolmogorov PDEs with affine drift and diffusion can be approximated by ReLU neural networks without curse of dimensionality. Since we only consider the uniform probability measure on

,

indeed grows polynomially in

.

Corollary 27
.

Under the setting of Proposition
26
, let
p=2
,
u_{d}:\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to[0,1]
be the uniform probability measure over
[u,v]^{d}
. Then
M_{d}
satisfies

M_{d}\leq C_{3}(1+d^{\lambda/2})

for a constant
C_{3}\in(0,\infty)
independent of
d
. Moreover, there exist neural networks
\Phi_{d,\varepsilon}
with parameter
\theta_{d,\varepsilon}
such that

\frac{1}{(v-u)^{d}}\int_{[u,v]^{d}}\left|f_{d}(x,T)-\Phi_{d,\varepsilon}(x)\right|^{2}dx\leq\varepsilon,

\mathcal{P}(\Phi_{d,\varepsilon})\leq Cd^{2\lambda}\varepsilon^{-2}\mathcal{P}(\Psi_{d,\varepsilon})
and
\left\|\theta_{d,\varepsilon}\right\|_{\infty}\leq Cd^{\lambda+3/2}\varepsilon^{-1}\left\|\eta_{d,\varepsilon}\right\|_{\infty},
where
C\in(0,\infty)
is a constant.

Proof.

Let
p=2
then we have

\displaystyle M_{d}=\exp\left(3\lambda\left(1+L^{2}T\left(\sqrt{T}+2\lambda\right)^{2}\right)\right)\left(1+\left[\int_{[u,v]^{d}}\left\|x\right\|_{2}^{2\lambda}u_{d}(dx)\right]^{1/2}\right),

note that
\left\|x\right\|_{2}^{2}\leq d\max\left\{u^{2},v^{2}\right\}
for every
x\in[u,v]^{d}
, thus

\left[\int_{[u,v]^{d}}\left\|x\right\|_{2}^{2\lambda}u_{d}(dx)\right]^{1/2}\leq d^{\lambda/2}\max\left\{\left|u\right|^{\lambda},\left|v\right|^{\lambda}\right\}.

It is obvious that
M_{d}=O(d^{\lambda/2})
. Substituting
M_{d}
with
O(d^{\lambda/2})
and
\varepsilon
with
\varepsilon^{2}
in the claims of
\Phi_{d,\varepsilon}
in Proposition
26
, the proof is completed.

Corollary 28
.

Under the setting of Proposition
26
, let
p=2
,

be the uniform probability measure over

,

and

is a constant.

Proof.

For every
x\in[u,v]^{d}
,
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
,

\displaystyle\left|\varphi_{d}(x)\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left|\Psi_{d,\varepsilon}(x)-\varphi_{d}(x)\right|+\left|\Psi_{d,\varepsilon}(x)\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(C_{1}+\varepsilon)(1+\left\|x\right\|_{2}^{\lambda}).

Note that
f_{d}(x,T)=\mathbb{E}\left[\varphi_{d}\left(\mathcal{A}x+\mathcal{B}\right)\right]
, we have

\displaystyle\left|f_{d}(x,T)\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(C_{1}+1)\mathbb{E}\left[1+\left\|\mathcal{A}x+\mathcal{B}\right\|_{2}^{\lambda}\right].

By
[
13
, Proposition 2.15]
and
[
5
, Lemma A.3]
, for every

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\mathcal{A}x+\mathcal{B}\right\|_{2}^{\lambda}\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2^{\lambda/2}\left(\left\|x\right\|_{2}+LT+\lambda L\sqrt{T}\right)^{\lambda}\exp\left(\lambda L^{2}T\left(\sqrt{T}+\lambda\right)^{2}\right).

D=\sup_{x\in[u,v]^{d}}\left|f_{d}(x,T)\right|
, then

. Suppose
\Phi_{d,\varepsilon}
are the desired neural networks with parameter
\theta_{d,\varepsilon}

-1\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}0\\

Overall estimation error

In the section, we combine the theoretical results of the generalization and approximation errors in Sections

Theorem 30
.

Assume Setting
3
, suppose that Assumptions
17
and
25
hold. Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
,
\varepsilon,\varrho\in(0,1)

p
and
q
such that the following holds: if sample size

satisfies

m\geq p(d,\varepsilon^{-1},\varrho^{-1}),\\

there exists a class of clipped ReLU neural networks
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{N}_{a,R,D}

\mathbb{P}\left[\frac{1}{(v-u)^{d}}\left\|f_{d,m,\mathcal{H}}-f_{d}(\cdot,T)\right\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}([u,v]^{d})}^{2}\leq\varepsilon\right]\geq 1-\varrho,

and
\max\left\{P(a),R,D\right\}\leq q(d,\varepsilon^{-1})
.

Proof.

By Corollary
8
and the definition of

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(v-u)^{d}}\left\|f_{d,m,\mathcal{H}}-f_{d}\right\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}([u,v]^{d})}^{2}

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}\left(f_{d,m,\mathcal{H}}\right)-\mathcal{E}\left(f_{d,\mathcal{H}}\right)+\frac{1}{(v-u)^{d}}\left\|f_{d,\mathcal{H}}-f_{d}\right\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}([u,v]^{d})}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}\left(f_{d,m,\mathcal{H}}\right)-\mathcal{E}\left(f_{d,\mathcal{H}}\right)+\frac{1}{(v-u)^{d}}\left\|g_{d,\varepsilon}-f_{d}\right\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}([u,v]^{d})}^{2},

4
Conclusion

In this paper, we extend the result (
4
) of
[
5
]
to the cases involving unbounded initial functions. We prove that for the ERM over deep artificial neural networks in solving linear Kolmogorov PDEs with unbounded initial functions, if Assumptions
17
and
25
hold, the number of training data and the size of the clipped ReLU neural network grow polynomially in both the dimension
d
and the reciprocal of the prescribed accuracy
\varepsilon^{-1}
, see Theorem
30
. For Black-Scholes PDEs (Setting
12
) and heat equations (Setting
10
), we prove that the two assumptions are indeed satisfied if the initial function can be approximated by ReLU neural networks without the curse of dimensionality, see Corollary
31
. Most initial functions can indeed be approximated by the the artificial neural networks without the curse of dimensionality. Specifically for Examples
11
,
13
,
14
, we prove that the deep learning algorithms based on the ERM over clipped ReLU neural networks break the curse of dimensionality, see Corollary
32
.
