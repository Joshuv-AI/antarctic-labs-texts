Abstract
In this paper, we investigate joint unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV) formation and resource allocation optimization for communication-assisted three-dimensional (3D) synthetic aperture radar (SAR) sensing. We consider a system consisting of two UAVs that perform bistatic interferometric SAR (InSAR) sensing for generation of a digital elevation model (DEM) and transmit the radar raw data to a ground station (GS) in real time. To account for practical 3D sensing requirements, we use non-conventional sensing performance metrics, such as the interferometric coherence, i.e., the local cross-correlation between the two co-registered UAV SAR images, the point-to-point InSAR relative height error, and the height of ambiguity, which together characterize the accuracy with which the InSAR system can determine the height of ground targets. Our objective is to jointly optimize the UAV formation, speed, and communication power allocation for maximization of the InSAR coverage while satisfying energy, communication, and InSAR-specific sensing constraints. To solve the formulated non-smooth and non-convex optimization problem, we divide it into three sub-problems and propose a novel alternating optimization (AO) framework that is based on classical, monotonic, and stochastic optimization techniques. The effectiveness of the proposed algorithm is validated through extensive simulations and compared to several benchmark schemes. Furthermore, our simulation results highlight the impact of the UAV-GS communication link on the flying formation and sensing performance and show that the DEM of a large area of interest can be mapped and offloaded to ground successfully, while the ground topography can be estimated with centimeter-scale precision. Lastly, we demonstrate that a low UAV velocity is preferable for InSAR applications as it leads to better sensing accuracy.

Introduction

The widespread use of unmanned aerial vehicles has revolutionized modern technology, impacting fields like remote sensing, communication, and disaster monitoring
[

. Specifically, unmanned aerial vehicles excel in remote sensing applications using a variety of technologies, such as cameras, lidars, and radars. Among these technologies, radar sensing has attracted particular attention due to its capacity to operate day and night under all weather conditions. Radar sensing applications can be classified into three distinct categories based on the dimensionality of the extracted information. Firstly, one-dimensional sensing includes applications such as ranging and detection and necessitates at least one radar antenna. Secondly, two-dimensional sensing, also known as radar imaging, can be realized using, e.g., multiple-input multiple-output radar systems employing multiple antennas
[
3

or by moving a single radar antenna or a multiple-input multiple-output array along a trajectory to create a synthetic aperture radar or multiple-input multiple-output-synthetic aperture radar system, and can provide a high-resolution image of the illuminated area
[
4

. Lastly, three-dimensional sensing provides an additional dimension besides the two-dimensional spatial location of a target, such as its altitude above ground. Here, an interesting remote sensing technique that can be used to generate accurate three-dimensional digital elevation models is across-track interferometric SAR
[
4
]
. In the following subsection, we discuss one-dimensional and two-dimensional unmanned aerial vehicle-based sensing applications, focusing on the existing trajectory and resource optimization frameworks. Then, in Subsection

I-B

unmanned aerial vehicle-based three-dimensional Sensing via interferometric SAR

Across-track interferometric SAR is an interesting three-dimensional remote sensing technique that requires the use of more than one radar antenna. At least two radar antennas are moved to illuminate a given target area from different angles, such that at least two synthetic aperture radar images can be formed. All possible image pairs are first co-registered
[
19
]
, i.e., aligned such that each ground point corresponds to the same pixel in both images, and based on the phase difference, an interferogram is created to extract information about the topography of the target area
[
19
,
20
]
.
interferometric SAR-based three-dimensional sensing is fundamentally different from one-dimensional and two-dimensional sensing. In particular, to assess the performance of one-dimensional sensing, conventional metrics, such as signal-to-noise ratio
[
5
]
, beam pattern gain
[
7
,
6
]
, and probability of detection and false alarm
[
21
,
22
]
can be adopted. For two-dimensional sensing, image performance metrics can be used, including synthetic aperture radar signal-to-noise ratio
[
14
]
, spatial resolution
[
9
]
, and integrated sidelobe ratio and peak sidelobe ratio
[
23
]
. However, these performance metrics are not sufficient for three-dimensional sensing applications, such as generating an accurate digital elevation model based on across-track interferometry. In fact, the key performance metric for estimating interferometric performance is coherence, which is a function of the correlation between the pair of co-registered synthetic aperture radar images
[
20
]
. Additional relevant performance metrics are the height of ambiguity, which is a proportionality constant between the interferometric phase and the terrain height and is thus related to the sensitivity of the radar to the ground topography
[
20
]
, and the relative height accuracy, which represents the accuracy with which the altitude of ground objects (i.e., the ground topography) can be estimated. In this context, an interesting trade-off arises; while a large inter-unmanned aerial vehicle separation distance, i.e., a large interferometric baseline, leads to a small height of ambiguity, which in general improves the height accuracy of digital elevation models, it also leads to a degradation of the image coherence
[
18
]
. Furthermore, three-dimensional interferometric SAR sensing is different from cooperative sensing
[
21
,
24
]
for two main reasons. In particular, for three-dimensional interferometric SAR (i) the coverage is defined by the region where the ground footprints of the two radar antennas overlap and (ii) the sensing performance is highly dependent on the unmanned aerial vehicle formation. Moreover, compared to one-dimensional and two-dimensional sensing, three-dimensional sensing is more challenging due to the use of multiple sensors and the need for considering the aforementioned dedicated performance metrics. Specifically, relying solely on beam pattern design, signal-to-noise ratio, or resolution is not sufficient for three-dimensional applications. Therefore, based on the above discussion, existing results for unmanned aerial vehicle-based one-dimensional and two-dimensional sensing are not applicable to interferometric three-dimensional sensing.

I-C

Main Contributions

In this paper, we consider a system of two unmanned aerial vehicles that perform synthetic aperture radar sensing of a given area from different angles, such that the difference of the sensors’ phases allows for estimating the height of ground objects based on interferometric SAR techniques. For this system, we maximize the sensing coverage by jointly optimizing the formation, velocity, and communication transmit power of both unmanned aerial vehicles while satisfying practical energy, communication, and sensing constraints. Our main contributions can be summarized as follows:

•

Different from existing works targeting one-dimensional and two-dimensional sensing applications, we consider interferometric SAR-specific sensing performance metrics, such as interferometric SAR coverage, coherence, height of ambiguity, and relative height accuracy, that can capture the quality of digital elevation models.

•

A joint unmanned aerial vehicle formation and resource allocation optimization problem is formulated to maximize the sensing coverage while satisfying practical energy, communication, and sensing constraints.

•

A novel alternating optimization-based algorithm, which incorporates classical, monotonic, and stochastic optimization techniques, is proposed to solve the formulated challenging non-smooth and non-convex optimization problem.

•

Our simulations show the superior performance of the proposed solution compared to a conventional alternating optimization algorithm and other benchmark schemes and unveil the impact of the interferometric SAR-specific sensing requirements on communication and vice versa.

We note that this article extends a corresponding conference version
[
1
]
. In contrast to this paper, in
[
1
]
, the velocity of both the master and slave unmanned aerial vehicles was fixed and the radar look angle of the slave drone was also not optimized. Furthermore, the minimum sensing data rate was assumed to be fixed, i.e., independent of the imageable area. Lastly, the relative point-to-point height accuracy was not accounted for in
[
1
]
.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. In Section II, we present the considered system. In Section III, the formation and resource allocation problem is formulated, and the proposed solution is presented in Section IV. In Section V, the performance of the proposed scheme is assessed via simulations, and finally, conclusions are drawn in Section VI.

II-B

In practical interferometric SAR missions, such as TanDEM-X and SRTM

The height accuracy, i.e., the precision with which the ground topography can be estimated, depends on several intermediate performance metrics, specifically the coherence and the height of ambiguity. In the following, we introduce these key three-dimensional sensing performance metrics and highlight their impact on the overall interferometric SAR performance.

III
Problem Formulation

In this paper, we aim to maximize the interferometric SAR coverage by jointly optimizing the unmanned aerial vehicle formation
\{\mathbf{q}_{1},\mathbf{q}_{2}\}
, the instantaneous communication transmit powers
\{\mathbf{P}_{\mathrm{com,1}},\mathbf{P}_{\mathrm{com,2}}\}
, and the instantaneous unmanned aerial vehicle velocity
\mathbf{v}_{y}
, while satisfying energy, communication, and interferometric quality-of-service constraints. To this end, we formulate the following optimization problem:

\displaystyle(\mathrm{P.1}):\max_{\mathbf{q}_{1},\mathbf{q}_{2},\mathbf{P}_{\mathrm{com},1},\mathbf{P}_{\mathrm{com},2},\mathbf{v}_{y}}\hskip 8.53581ptC_{N}(\mathbf{q}_{1},\mathbf{q}_{2},\mathbf{v}_{y})

s.t.

\displaystyle\mathrm{C1:}\;z_{\mathrm{min}}\leq z_{i}\leq z_{\mathrm{max}},\forall i\in\{1,2\},

\displaystyle\mathrm{C2}:\;x_{1}=x_{t}-z_{1}\tan(\theta_{1}),

\displaystyle\mathrm{C3}:\;r_{2}(\mathbf{q}_{2})\leq r_{1}(\mathbf{q}_{1}),

\displaystyle\mathrm{C4}:\;x_{2}\leq x_{t},

\displaystyle\mathrm{C5}:b(\mathbf{q}_{1},\mathbf{q}_{2})\geq b_{\mathrm{min}},

\displaystyle\mathrm{C6}:\gamma_{\mathrm{SNR},n}(\mathbf{q}_{1},\mathbf{q}_{2},\mathbf{v}_{y})\geq\gamma_{\mathrm{SNR}}^{\mathrm{min}},\forall n,

\displaystyle\mathrm{C7}:\gamma_{\mathrm{Rg}}(\mathbf{q}_{2})\geq\gamma_{\mathrm{Rg}}^{\mathrm{min}},

\displaystyle\mathrm{C8}:\;h_{\mathrm{amb}}(\mathbf{q}_{1},\mathbf{q}_{2})\geq h_{\mathrm{amb}}^{\mathrm{min}},

\displaystyle\mathrm{C9}:\;\Delta h_{90\%}^{\rm worst}(\mathbf{q}_{1},\mathbf{q}_{2})\leq\Delta h^{\mathrm{max}},

\displaystyle\mathrm{C10}:0\leq P_{\mathrm{com},i}[n]\leq P_{\mathrm{com}}^{\mathrm{max}},\forall\;i\in\{1,2\},\forall n,

\displaystyle\mathrm{C11}:R_{i,n}(\mathbf{q}_{i},\mathbf{P}_{\mathrm{com},i},\mathbf{v}_{y})\geq R_{\mathrm{min},i}(\mathbf{q}_{i}),\forall\;i\in\{1,2\},\forall n,

\displaystyle\mathrm{C12}:E_{i,N}(\mathbf{P}_{\mathrm{com},i},\mathbf{v}_{y})\leq E_{\mathrm{max},i},\forall\;i\in\{1,2\},

\displaystyle\mathrm{C13}:v_{\mathrm{min}}\leq v_{y}[n]\leq v_{\mathrm{max}},\forall n,

\displaystyle\mathrm{C14}:\theta_{\mathrm{min}}\leq\theta_{2}(\mathbf{q}_{2})\leq\theta_{\mathrm{max}},

\displaystyle\mathrm{C15}:\theta_{2}(\mathbf{q}_{2})=\arctan\left(\frac{x_{2}-x_{t}}{z_{2}}\right).

Constraint
\mathrm{C1}
specifies the maximum and minimum allowed flying altitude, denoted by
z_{\mathrm{max}}
and
z_{\mathrm{min}}
, respectively. Constraints
\mathrm{C2}
and
\mathrm{C3}
ensure maximum overlap between the beam footprint of the master drone and the area of interest. Constraint
\mathrm{C4}
is imposed because a side-looking synthetic aperture radar is assumed. Constraint
\mathrm{C5}
ensures a minimum separation distance between the unmanned aerial vehicles, denoted by
b_{\mathrm{min}}
. Constraints
\mathrm{C6}
and
\mathrm{C7}
ensure minimum required thresholds for the sensing
\mathrm{SNR}
and baseline decorrelation, denoted by
\gamma_{\mathrm{SNR}}^{\mathrm{min}}
and
\gamma_{\mathrm{Rg}}^{\mathrm{min}}
, respectively. Constraint
\mathrm{C8}
imposes a minimum height of ambiguity, denoted by

, that satisfies phase unwrapping requirements
[
20
]
. Constraint
\mathrm{C9}
ensures that the worst-case point-to-point 90% relative height error of unmanned aerial vehicle formation
\{\mathbf{q}_{1},\mathbf{q}_{2}\}
, defined as
\Delta h_{90\%}^{\rm worst}(\mathbf{q}_{1},\mathbf{q}_{2})=\Delta h_{90\%}(\mathbf{q}_{1},\mathbf{q}_{2},\gamma_{\rm SNR}^{\rm min}\gamma_{\rm Rg}^{\rm min}\gamma_{\rm other})
, does not exceed the maximum allowed height error, denoted by

. The definition of

ensures that the achievable throughput of drone

to its maximum battery capacity, denoted by

as some variables, such as

Problem
\mathrm{(P.1)}
is a non-smooth non-convex optimization problem for the following reasons. First, the use of a variable slave look angle as well as
\max
and
\min
functions causes the objective function
C_{N}
to be non-smooth and non-convex with respect to
\mathbf{q}_{2}
. Second, the coupling between different optimization variables and the presence of trigonometric functions in the expressions for the
\mathrm{SNR}
decorrelation, baseline decorrelation, and height of ambiguity (see (
9
), (
11
), and (
12
)), make constraints
\mathrm{C6}
,
\mathrm{C7}
,
\mathrm{C8}
, and
\mathrm{C9}
non-convex. Third, the use of variable minimum sensing data rates causes
\mathrm{C11}
to be neither monotonic nor convex. Fourth, constraints
\mathrm{C3}
and
\mathrm{C5}
involve lower bounds on Euclidean distances, and thus, they are also non-convex. Lastly, the unmanned aerial vehicle propulsion power, given in (
18
), causes constraint
\mathrm{C12}
to be non-convex. In short, problem
\mathrm{(P.1)}
involves interesting performance trade-offs, which do not allow for a trivial solution. For instance, achieving extensive coverage requires a high speed, which reduces the sensing signal-to-noise ratios, leading to degraded coherence and, consequently, increased estimation errors. Furthermore, choosing a close flying formation for the unmanned aerial vehicles can improve the coverage (due to the increased overlap of the corresponding individual images), however, it also causes the baseline to be small, which leads to high height of ambiguity, and consequently, large estimation errors. In summary, it is very difficult and challenging to find a globally optimal solution to problem
\mathrm{(P.1)}
. To overcome this challenge, in the next section, we divide problem
\mathrm{(P.1)}
into several more tractable and easier-to-solve sub-problems and tackle them in an alternating manner.

IV
Solution of the Optimization Problem

VI
Conclusion

In this paper, we investigated joint formation and resource allocation optimization for bistatic unmanned aerial vehicle-based three-dimensional interferometric SAR sensing. To assess the three-dimensional sensing performance, the interferometric SAR coverage, interferometric coherence, height of ambiguity, and relative height accuracy were introduced as relevant interferometric SAR-specific performance metrics. A non-convex non-smooth optimization problem was formulated and solved for the maximization of the bistatic interferometric SAR ground coverage while enforcing energy, communication, and sensing performance constraints. Our simulation results confirmed the superior performance of the proposed algorithm compared to several benchmark schemes and emphasized the relevance of optimizing the velocity of both radar platforms and the look angle of the slave unmanned aerial vehicle for maximum interferometric SAR coverage. We revealed the importance of low unmanned aerial vehicle speed for high interferometric SAR performance and showed that large areas can be mapped and offloaded to ground in real time while ground object heights can be estimated with high precision. Lastly, we highlighted the significant impact of the unmanned aerial vehicle-ground station communication link on the unmanned aerial vehicle formation, and therefore, sensing performance.

Potential topics for future work include the consideration of non-linear trajectories. Additionally, extending current results to the case of more than two unmanned aerial vehicles case is a promising research direction, for which, different communication schemes and sensing performance metrics can be used. Finally, considering a priori knowledge about the imaged scene in the optimization framework, such as the terrain type, moisture, or roughness, can also be explored.
