Asymptotic quasinormal modes of string-theoretical 𝒅 d -dimensional black holes arXiv: 2105.02616
Filipe Moura, João Rodrigues

Abstract
We compute the quasinormal frequencies of d d -dimensional spherically symmetric black holes with leading string α ′ \alpha^{\prime} corrections for tensorial gravitational perturbations in the highly damped regime. We solve perturbatively the master differential equation and we compute the monodromies of the master perturbation variable (analytically continued to the complex plane) in different contours, in order to obtain the quasinormal mode spectra. We proceed analogously for the quasinormal modes of test scalar fields. Differently than in Einstein gravity, we obtain distinct results for the two cases.



1
Introduction

Black hole quasinormal modes (QNMs) are associated to perturbations that can be related either to the black hole metric (gravitational perturbations) or to external fields. They represent resonances in black hole scattering problems that are purely ingoing at the event horizon and purely outgoing at infinity. These boundary conditions imply that the associated frequencies must be complex. The quasinormal ringing frequencies carry unique information about parameters of the black hole in the ringdown phase resulting from a black hole collision; they can be directly measured by the gravitational wave detectors. This feature turns QNMs into preferential probes for testing theories of gravity beyond Einstein, since the ringing frequencies represent a universal part of the gravitational wave signals. These frequencies do not depend on what drives the perturbations: they are given exclusively in terms of intrinsic physical quantities of the black hole such as mass, charge or spin, and eventually (beyond Einstein gravity) some other parameters of the theory. With the advent of gravitational wave astronomy, therefore, interest in the study of black hole QNMs has raised.
Literature concerning QNMs is vast and rich; many excellent references can be provided, such as the seminal works

;

Asymptotic quasinormal modes of tensorial gravitational perturbations to the Callan-Myers-Perry black hole

3.1
Quasinormal modes, boundary conditions and the monodromy method

Quasinormal modes of tensorial gravitational perturbations are solutions to the corresponding master equation (
5
) subject to the boundary conditions

\displaystyle\psi\propto e^{-i\omega x}\hskip 3.0pt,\hskip 3.0ptr\to+\infty;

(14)

\displaystyle\psi\propto e^{i\omega x}\hskip 3.0pt,\hskip 3.0ptr\to R_{H}.

(15)

In terms of the tortoise coordinate
x
, these boundary conditions are written as

\displaystyle\psi\propto e^{-i\omega x}\hskip 3.0pt,\hskip 3.0ptx\to+\infty;

(16)

\displaystyle\psi\propto e^{i\omega x}\hskip 3.0pt,\hskip 3.0ptx\to-\infty.

(17)

It should be clear that any method devised to compute quasinormal frequencies will have to make use of these defining boundary conditions. Because quasinormal frequencies are complex and
r
and
x
are real, gathering information of these boundary conditions amounts to distinguish between an exponentially vanishing and an exponentially growing term. Clearly, a numerical approach will face problems with such task. Moreover, any kind of analytical approximate approach will also fail to some extent. Indeed, many lower order terms of the approximation are needed in order to make sense of the exponentially decreasing term, otherwise this term might be much smaller than the approximation error and consequently needs to be disregarded.

An elegant solution to half of this issue emerges from the moment we allow
r
(and
x
) to take complex values and consequently assume an analytic continuation of functions of
r
to the complex plane. Indeed, if one takes the contour
\mathop{\rm Im}\left(\omega x\right)=0
in the complex
r
plane, then

|e^{\pm i\omega x}|=1,
the asymptotic behavior of
e^{\pm i\omega x}
is always oscillatory and there will be no problems with exponentially growing versus exponentially vanishing terms. Thus if one considers the Stokes lines
\mathop{\rm Im}\left(\omega x\right)=0
, imposing the boundary condition (
16
) in the complex
r
plane no longer poses a challenge to an approximate analytical method.

Near the event horizon, we can distinguish the two exponential terms by computing the respective monodromies around it. As we will see in the next section, these monodromies are non trivial because
x
has a branch point in the event horizon. As it turns out, the boundary condition (
17
) can be set as a monodromy condition.

The monodromy method we will be using was introduced in
Motl:2003cd
. In order to apply it one needs two contours, and the solutions to the master equation in the respective regions, to impose the appropriate boundary conditions. The general idea goes as follows:

•

We pick two closed homotopic contours on the complex
r
-plane. Both these contours enclose only the physical horizon: none of them encloses the origin of the complex
r
-plane nor any fictitious horizon.

•

One of these contours, which we designate as the big contour, seeks to encode information of the boundary condition (
16
) on the monodromy of
\psi
associated with a full loop around it.

•

The other contour, which we designate as the small contour, seeks to encode information of the boundary condition (
17
) on the monodromy of
\psi
associated with a full loop around it.

•

As both contours are homotopic, the monodromy theorem asserts that the respective monodromies must be the same. Thus, equating them hopefully yields a restriction on the values of the quasinormal frequencies
\omega
, from the complex plane to an infinite but countable subset.

We restrict our analysis in this article to the highly damped regime of quasinormal modes defined by the condition

\mathop{\rm Im}\left(\omega\right)\gg\mathop{\rm Re}\left(\omega\right).

(18)

This condition is equivalent to
\omega
being approximately imaginary. The definition of a Stokes line comes thus as

\mathop{\rm Im}\left(\omega x\right)=0\Rightarrow\mathop{\rm Re}\left(x\right)=0.

(19)

\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}\psi_{0}}{dz^{2}}+\left(\omega^{2}-V_{0}\right)\psi_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,

(39)

\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}\psi_{1}}{dz^{2}}+\left(\omega^{2}-V_{0}\right)\psi_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\xi,

(40)

with the function
\xi
given by

\displaystyle\xi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\xi_{1}+\xi_{2}+\xi_{3},

(41)

\displaystyle\xi_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2\delta f(r)\left(\frac{d^{2}\psi_{0}}{dz^{2}}\right),

(42)

\displaystyle\xi_{2}

\displaystyle=

(43)

\displaystyle\xi_{3}

\displaystyle V_{1}(r)\psi_{0}.

(44)

The boundary conditions at spatial infinity of the differential equations (

The big contour

3.5
Solution in a neighborhood of the origin and asymptotic behavior

We will now compute the solutions to the differential equations (
39
) and (
40
) in a neighborhood of the origin of the complex
r-
plane. We then proceed to study the asymptotic behavior of these solutions in the portion of the big contour that follows the Stokes lines extending to complex infinity.

We start by solving (
39
), the differential equation for
\psi_{0}
. This is precisely the same equation describing the same quasinormal modes in Einstein gravity. The resolution of this equation has been studied in detail in
Motl:2003cd
;
Natario:2004jd
; we will review the main steps.

In an arbitrarily small neighborhood of the origin, the leading term of the potential
V_{0}(r)
is given by

V_{0}(r)\sim-\left(\frac{d-2}{2}\right)^{2}\frac{R_{H}^{2d-6}}{r^{2d-4}}.

(45)

Replacing
z
given by (
24
) in
V_{0}(r)
, we obtain for the leading term
V_{0}(z)\sim-\frac{1}{4z^{2}}
. We can then write the differential equation (
39
), in this neighborhood, as

\frac{d^{2}\psi_{0}}{dz^{2}}+\left(\omega^{2}-\frac{j^{2}-1}{4z^{2}}\right)\psi_{0}=0

(46)

for
j=0
. Following the procedure of
Motl:2003cd
;
Natario:2004jd
we will consider the general solution, for arbitrary
j
, of the above differential equation, and at the end take the limit
j\rightarrow 0
. Such solution is given by

\psi_{0}(z)=A_{+}\sqrt{2\pi}\sqrt{\omega z}J_{\frac{j}{2}}(\omega z)+A_{-}\sqrt{2\pi}\sqrt{\omega z}J_{-\frac{j}{2}}(\omega z),

(47)

where
J_{\pm\frac{j}{2}}(\omega z)
are Bessel functions of the first kind and
A_{+},A_{-}
arbitrary constants.

Bessel functions verify the asymptotic condition (for
|\omega z|\gg 1
)

\displaystyle\sqrt{2\pi}\sqrt{\omega z}J_{\pm\frac{j}{2}}(\omega z)\sim 2\cos\left(\omega z-\alpha_{\pm}\right),

\displaystyle\alpha_{\pm}=\frac{\pi}{4}\left(1\pm j\right).

(48)

The condition
|\omega z|\gg 1
holds in the portion of the big contour following the Stokes lines everywhere except near the origin. Therefore, in this portion of the contour extending to complex infinity
\psi_{0}
will be a linear combination of plane waves:

\displaystyle\psi_{0}(z)

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle 2A_{+}\cos\left(\omega z-\alpha_{+}\right)+2A_{-}\cos\left(\omega z-\alpha_{-}\right)

(49)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(A_{+}e^{-i\alpha_{+}}+A_{-}e^{-i\alpha_{-}}\right)e^{i\omega z}+\left(A_{+}e^{i\alpha_{+}}+A_{-}e^{i\alpha_{-}}\right)e^{-i\omega z}.

Imposing the boundary condition (
28
) to
\psi_{0}
, we get a linear system of equations for the coefficients
A_{+},A_{-}
(with
c\in\mathbb{C}
):

\begin{cases}A_{+}e^{i\alpha_{+}}+A_{-}e^{i\alpha_{-}}=c,\\
A_{+}e^{-i\alpha_{+}}+A_{-}e^{-i\alpha_{-}}=0,\end{cases}

(50)

which we can solve:

\begin{cases}A_{-}=\frac{ce^{-i\alpha_{+}}}{2i\sin(\alpha_{-}-\alpha_{+})},\\
A_{+}=-\frac{ce^{-i\alpha_{-}}}{2i\sin(\alpha_{-}-\alpha_{+})}.\end{cases}

(51)

We now turn to the solution of the differential equation (
40
), the nonhomogeneous differential equation for
\psi_{1}
. In order to obtain a particular solution to it we use the method of variation of parameters to write

\psi_{1}(z)=2\pi\sqrt{\omega z}J_{-\frac{j}{2}}(\omega z)\int\sqrt{\omega z}J_{\frac{j}{2}}(\omega z)\frac{\xi(z)}{W}dz-2\pi\sqrt{\omega z}J_{\frac{j}{2}}(\omega z)\int\sqrt{\omega z}J_{-\frac{j}{2}}(\omega z)\frac{\xi(z)}{W}dz,

(52)

where
W
denotes the wronskian of the basis of solutions of (
46
)

W=\frac{d}{dz}\left(\sqrt{2\pi}J_{-\frac{j}{2}}(\omega z)\right)\sqrt{2\pi}J_{\frac{j}{2}}-\frac{d}{dz}\left(\sqrt{2\pi}J_{\frac{j}{2}}(\omega z)\right)\sqrt{2\pi}J_{-\frac{j}{2}}=-4\omega\sin\left(\frac{\pi j}{2}\right).

(53)

The extra function
\xi
depends on
\psi_{0}
, given by (
47
), and its derivatives, given by

\displaystyle\frac{d\psi_{0}}{dz}(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}\sqrt{\frac{\omega}{z}}\left(A_{+}J_{\frac{j}{2}}(z\omega)+A_{+}\omega zJ_{\frac{j}{2}-1}(z\omega)-A_{+}\omega zJ_{\frac{j}{2}+1}(z\omega)\right)

(54)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}\sqrt{\frac{\omega}{z}}\left(A_{-}J_{-\frac{j}{2}}(z\omega)+A_{-}\omega zJ_{-\frac{j}{2}-1}(z\omega)-A_{-}\omega zJ_{1-\frac{j}{2}}(z\omega)\right),

\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}\psi_{0}}{dz^{2}}(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{8}}\sqrt{\omega}z^{-\frac{3}{2}}\left(j^{2}-4\omega^{2}z^{2}-1\right)\left(A_{+}J_{\frac{j}{2}}(z\omega)+A_{-}J_{-\frac{j}{2}}(z\omega)\right).

(55)

From (
41
) we see that
\xi
also depends on three other quantities, whose leading terms in an arbitrarily small neighborhood around the origin are given by

\displaystyle V_{1}(r)

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}(d-4)(d(d-5)(2d-7)-22)\frac{R_{H}^{3d-7}}{r^{3d-5}},

(56)

\displaystyle f(r)\left[\delta f(r)\right]^{\prime}

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle-\frac{(d-4)(d-3)(d-1)}{2}\frac{R_{H}^{2d-4}}{r^{2d-3}},

(57)

\displaystyle 2\delta f(r)

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle-(d-3)(d-4)\left(\frac{R_{H}}{r}\right)^{d-1}.

(58)

Expressed in terms of
z
through (
24
), the same leading terms are given by

\displaystyle 2\delta f(r)

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle\Upsilon_{1}\left(\frac{R_{H}}{z}\right)^{-\rho-2},

(59)

\displaystyle f(r)\left[\delta f(r)\right]^{\prime}

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle\Upsilon_{2}z^{\rho+1}R_{H}^{-\rho-2},

(60)

\displaystyle V_{1}(r)

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle\Upsilon_{3}z^{\rho}R_{H}^{-\rho-2},

(61)

with the definitions

\displaystyle\rho

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle-2-\frac{d-1}{d-2},

(62)

\displaystyle\Upsilon_{1}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle(-1)^{\rho+2}(d-2)^{\rho+2}(d-4)(d-3),

(63)

\displaystyle\Upsilon_{2}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle(-1)^{\rho+1}(d-2)^{\rho+1}\frac{(d-4)(d-3)(d-1)}{2},

(64)

\displaystyle\Upsilon_{3}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}(-1)^{\rho}(d-2)^{\rho}(d-4)((d-5)d(2d-7)-22).

(65)

Analogously to (
41
), we decompose the particular solution to the nonhomogeneous differential equation (
40
) into a sum of three terms, each one corresponding to one term of
\xi

\displaystyle\psi_{1}(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{3}\phi_{i}(z),

\displaystyle\phi_{i}(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\pi\sqrt{\omega z}J_{-\frac{j}{2}}(\omega z)\int\sqrt{\omega z}J_{\frac{j}{2}}(\omega z)\frac{\xi_{i}(z)}{W}dz-2\pi\sqrt{\omega z}J_{\frac{j}{2}}(\omega z)\int\sqrt{\omega z}J_{-\frac{j}{2}}(\omega z)\frac{\xi_{i}(z)}{W}dz.

In appendix

. Based on these results, we can write the asymptotic behavior of

in the limit

\psi_{1}(z)\sim\left(\Lambda_{I}^{+}e^{-i\alpha_{+}}+\Lambda_{I}^{-}e^{-i\alpha_{-}}\right)e^{i\omega z}+\left(\Lambda_{I}^{+}e^{i\alpha_{+}}+\Lambda_{I}^{-}e^{i\alpha_{-}}\right)e^{-i\omega z},

with the definition

\Lambda_{I}^{\pm}(d,j,\omega,R_{H})=\left(R_{H}\omega\right)^{\frac{d-1}{d-2}}\sum_{k=1}^{3}\Theta_{k}^{\pm}(d,j,1,1)

(68)

and the coefficients

(also depending on the constants

We see that the above asymptotic behavior (

28

\psi^{*}
of the homogeneous equation associated with the differential equation (

for some

\begin{cases}C_{+}e^{i\alpha_{+}}+C_{-}e^{i\alpha_{-}}=c,\\

The second equation in (

term in (
67
) with the redefinition (

). Similarly to this redefinition, we may decompose the coefficients

in (

) as

with the coefficients

satisfying (

) and

satisfying

whose solution is given by

\begin{cases}B_{-}=\frac{e^{i\alpha_{+}}\left(\Lambda_{I}^{+}e^{-i\alpha_{+}}+\Lambda_{I}^{-}e^{-i\alpha_{-}}\right)}{2i\sin\left(\alpha_{-}-\alpha_{+}\right)},\\
B_{+}=-\frac{e^{i\alpha_{-}}\left(\Lambda_{I}^{+}e^{-i\alpha_{+}}+\Lambda_{I}^{-}e^{-i\alpha_{-}}\right)}{2i\sin\left(\alpha_{-}-\alpha_{+}\right)}.\end{cases}

One can immediately see that, by adding the systems of equations (

in (

); having in mind (

. Therefore, without loss of generality one can define

as in (

), but with coefficients

satisfying (

) (i.e. just by the second part of the splitting).

We get finally
\psi_{1}(z)\sim\left(\Lambda_{I}^{+}e^{i\alpha_{+}}+\Lambda_{I}^{-}e^{i\alpha_{-}}\right)e^{-i\omega z}
(for the function after the redefinition (

)) and, from (

49
), (
50
), we obtain asymptotically as

\begin{split}\psi(z)\sim\left(A_{+}e^{i\alpha_{+}}+A_{-}e^{i\alpha_{-}}\right)e^{-i\omega z}+\lambda^{\prime}\left(\Lambda_{I}^{+}e^{i\alpha_{+}}+\Lambda_{I}^{-}e^{i\alpha_{-}}\right)e^{-i\omega z}\\
=\left(A_{+}e^{i\alpha_{+}}+A_{-}e^{i\alpha_{-}}\right)\left(1+\lambda^{\prime}\left(\frac{\Lambda_{I}^{+}e^{i\alpha_{+}}+\Lambda_{I}^{-}e^{i\alpha_{-}}}{A_{+}e^{i\alpha_{+}}+A_{-}e^{i\alpha_{-}}}\right)\right)e^{-i\omega z}.\end{split}

(75)

3.6
Computation of the total monodromy in the big contour

We now proceed with the analysis of the behavior of
\psi
around the big contour described in section
3.4
and depicted in figure
2
.

In the contour we consider, we start from region
D
in the lower part of the complex
r
-plane (close to
r=-i\infty
for
d=5
and in the fourth quadrant for
d>5
), where
\psi(z)
is given by (
75
). We follow the Stokes line towards the origin. In a neighborhood of the origin, we make a
\frac{3\pi}{d-2}
rotation around the origin; we present the detailed study of the behavior of
\psi=\psi_{0}+\lambda^{\prime}\psi_{1}
under such rotation in appendix
B
. We then follow again a portion of the contour coincident with a Stokes line and all the way to the upper portion of the contour back to complex infinity (region
U
), where the asymptotic values of
\psi_{0},\psi_{1}
are given by (
127
) and (
133
) respectively. We notice that both asymptotic values contain terms proportional to
e^{i\omega z}
and to
e^{-i\omega z}
. This is to be expected from the WKB approximation.

Now we want to see how
\psi
behaves in the large arc shaped portion of the big contour. We notice that this portion of the contour no longer coincides
with a Stokes line of the WKB approximation of the master equation (
39
). Because of the condition

\mathop{\rm Im}(\omega z)\gg 0,

the term proportional to
e^{i\omega z}
gives only an exponentially small contribution to
\psi
in this region of the complex
r-
plane; therefore, as we abandon
U
and start following this portion of the big contour this term can be modified by the small correction terms arising from the WKB approximation that have been neglected in the plane wave approximation. This way, we cannot trust the coefficient multiplying
e^{i\omega z}
, as we cannot expect it to stay the same after closing the big contour. However, even not knowing the coefficient of this term, we know it is exponentially small in the large arc, and can be discarded there.
Thus near
D
, after closing the big contour we will then have (from (
127
) and (
133
), with coefficients
\Lambda_{F}^{\pm}(d,j,\omega,R_{H})
given by (
132
), and discarding the
e^{i\omega z}
term)

\psi(z)\sim\left(A_{+}e^{5i\alpha_{+}}+A_{-}e^{5i\alpha_{-}}\right)e^{-i\omega z}+\lambda^{\prime}\left(\Lambda_{F}^{+}e^{5i\alpha_{+}}+\Lambda_{F}^{-}e^{5i\alpha_{-}}\right)e^{-i\omega z}.

(76)

This way, we use the plane wave approximation to obtain the coefficient multiplying
e^{-i\omega z}
in
\psi
, and we assume that the corresponding monodromy is equivalent to the monodromy of
\psi
, i.e. that the monodromy of
\psi
is multiplicative. This assumption will be fully justified when we compute the monodromy of
\psi
in the small contour, in section
3.7
.

Before computing the total monodromy of
\psi
, we must consider one further detail. As we mentioned in section
3.2
, as a function
z(r)
has a branch point in each zero of
f_{0}(r)
: the real and the “fictitious” horizons (
26
). From the discussion in section
3.2.3
, the big contour does not enclose any of these “fictitious” horizons but, since it encloses the real horizon, a full loop around it is bound to cross a branch cut somewhere. Thus the expression (
76
) above is written with respect to a variable
z
, defined on a branch, in the Riemann surface of
z
, different from the branch in which the variable
z
used in (
75
) is defined. In order to relate these two variables we simply need to compute the monodromy of
z
associated with one clockwise loop around the event horizon
r=R_{H}
.

Close to the horizon, from (
22
) we write (ignoring an irrelevant integration constant)

z=\int\frac{dr}{f_{0}(r)}\approx\frac{1}{f^{\prime}_{0}(R_{H})}\ln\left(1-\frac{r}{R_{H}}\right)=\frac{R_{H}}{d-3}\ln\left(1-\frac{r}{R_{H}}\right).

(77)

From the parametrization
1-\frac{r}{R_{H}}=\rho e^{i\theta}
for
\rho\geq 0
and

, we can rewrite the expansion (
77
) near

as
z(r)\sim\frac{R_{H}}{d-3}\left(\ln\left(\rho\right)+i\theta\right).
Following a full clockwise loop around

is equivalent to letting
\theta
run from

to
0
. Therefore, the monodromy of

, associated with a full clockwise loop around

, is

(78)

Using this monodromy, we can relate the previously mentioned
z
variables by redefining the one used in (
76
) as

We can then consider the monodromy of

around the big contour and rewrite (

) near

\psi(z)\sim\left(A_{+}e^{5i\alpha_{+}}+A_{-}e^{5i\alpha_{-}}\right)e^{-i\omega m}e^{-i\omega z}\left[1+\lambda^{\prime}\left(\frac{\Lambda_{F}^{+}e^{5i\alpha_{+}}+\Lambda_{F}^{-}e^{5i\alpha_{-}}}{A_{+}e^{5i\alpha_{+}}+A_{-}e^{5i\alpha_{-}}}\right)\right].

Comparing (
75
) and (

where, to first order in

We recall that
\alpha_{\pm}
are given by (

);
A_{\pm}
are given by (

);
\Lambda_{I}^{\pm}(d,j,\omega,R_{H})
are given by (

) with coefficients

defined in appendix

;

) with coefficients

) and

Also in the limit

, and after a lot of algebraic manipulations, we get for (

)

\displaystyle\delta\mathcal{M}_{1}

\displaystyle=

.

. The monodromy

of
x
, associated with a full clockwise loop around the small contour, is related to the equivalent monodromy
m
of
z
, given by (
78
), as

n:=\left(1-\frac{\lambda}{R_{H}^{2}}\delta f(R_{H})\right)m=\left(1+\frac{\lambda^{\prime}}{2}(d-4)(d-1)\right)m.

(88)

From (
86
), the monodromy of
\psi
associated with a full clockwise loop around the small contour is multiplicative and given by

\mathcal{M}_{2}:=e^{i\omega n}.

(89)

The monodromy theorem tells us that this monodromy of
\psi
is the same for every contour that is homotopic to the small contour, namely any larger contour around the horizon
R_{H}
, where
\psi
is given by a combination of
e^{-i\omega z}
and
e^{i\omega z}
. Thus the monodromy of (
86
), i.e. the monodromy of
e^{i\omega x}
, is indeed the monodromy of
\psi
. The multiplicativity of this monodromy is therefore a general property. The assumption of multiplicativity of the monodromy around the big contour is indeed justified, and so is its calculation in section
3.6
.

3.8
Equating monodromies

Now, we want to relate the monodromies
\mathcal{M}_{1}
and
\mathcal{M}_{2}
. To this end, we start by noticing that the big contour is homotopic to the small one. This is so, because one can continuously deform the big contour into the small one. Thus, using the monodromy theorem, we know that the monodromies of
\psi
, associated with the full clockwise loops around the big and the small contour, are the same.
Hence, the equation

\mathcal{M}_{1}=\mathcal{M}_{2}

(90)

must hold. From (
84
) and (
89
) we can rewrite the equation above, to first order in
\lambda^{\prime}
, as

-3=e^{i\omega(m+n)}(1-\lambda^{\prime}\delta\mathcal{M}_{1}).

(91)

Taking the logarithm on both sides of (
91
) we get, to first order in
\lambda^{\prime}
,

\ln(-3)-i\omega(m+n)+\lambda^{\prime}\delta\mathcal{M}_{1}=0.

(92)

From (
78
) and (
88
), we can write

m+n=-\left(2+\lambda^{\prime}\frac{(d-4)(d-1)}{2}\right)\frac{2\pi i}{d-3}R_{H}.

(93)

Using (
12
) we can rewrite
m+n
, to first order in
\lambda^{\prime}
, as a function of the black hole temperature
T_{\mathcal{H}}
:

m+n=-\frac{i}{T_{\mathcal{H}}}\left(1-\lambda^{\prime}\frac{(d-1)(d-4)}{4}\right).

(94)

Similarly, from (
12
) we can also write, to first order in
\lambda^{\prime}
,

\lambda^{\prime}\left(\frac{R_{H}\omega}{d-2}\right)^{\frac{d-1}{d-2}}=\lambda^{\prime}\left[\frac{d-3}{d-2}\frac{\omega}{4\pi T_{\mathcal{H}}}\right]^{\frac{d-1}{d-2}}.

(95)

Taking (
94
) for
m+n
, (
83
) for
\delta\mathcal{M}_{1}
and using (
95
), we can rewrite (
92
) as

\ln(3)+(2k+1)\pi i=\frac{\omega}{T_{\mathcal{H}}}\left(1-\lambda^{\prime}\frac{(d-1)(d-4)}{4}-\lambda^{\prime}\left[\frac{d-3}{d-2}\right]^{\frac{d-1}{d-2}}\left[\frac{\omega}{4\pi T_{\mathcal{H}}}\right]^{\frac{1}{d-2}}\frac{\Pi_{\textsf{T}}(d)}{4\pi}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{2}{d-2}\pi i}\right)

(96)

for
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Here we take
k>0
in order to get
\mathop{\rm Im}(\omega)>0
in the Einstein limit
\lambda^{\prime}\to 0
, and also considering condition (
18
). We obtain this way a transcendental equation that restricts the possible values of the quasinormal frequencies
\omega
from the complex plane to an infinite but countable (for every value of the mode number
k
) subset. In the limit of Einstein gravity, we recover the results of
Natario:2004jd
, as we should:

\left.\frac{\omega}{T_{\mathcal{H}}}\right|_{\alpha^{\prime}=0}=\ln(3)+(2k+1)\pi i,\,k\in\mathbb{N}.

(97)

Since we are working perturbatively in
\lambda^{\prime}
, we can simply consider this value of
\frac{\omega}{T_{\mathcal{H}}}
for the
\lambda^{\prime}
correction in (
96
) and solve this equation for
\frac{\omega}{T_{\mathcal{H}}}
, obtaining

\frac{\omega}{T_{\mathcal{H}}}=\left(\ln(3)+(2k+1)\pi i\right)\left(1+\lambda^{\prime}\frac{(d-1)(d-4)}{4}+\lambda^{\prime}\left[\frac{d-3}{d-2}\right]^{\frac{d-1}{d-2}}\left[\frac{\ln(3)+(2k+1)\pi i}{4\pi}\right]^{\frac{1}{d-2}}\frac{\Pi_{\textsf{T}}(d)}{4\pi}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{2}{d-2}\pi i}\right).

(98)

With this replacement, from (
98
) it is not easy to evaluate the real and imaginary parts of
\omega
. One should keep in mind that we have been working in the highly damped limit (
18
), which allows us to consider
\left.\frac{\omega}{T_{\mathcal{H}}}\right|_{\alpha^{\prime}=0}\approx(2k+1)\pi i
in the
\lambda^{\prime}
correction in (
98
). We also make the dependence on the temperature more explicit by writing
\lambda^{\prime}=\lambda\frac{16\pi^{2}}{(d-3)^{2}}T_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}
, according to (
13
). This way we obtain for the
\alpha^{\prime}
-corrected asymptotic quasinormal spectra:

\frac{\omega}{T_{\mathcal{H}}}=\left(\ln(3)+(2k+1)\pi i\right)\left[1+\lambda\frac{16\pi^{2}}{(d-3)^{2}}T_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}\left(\frac{(d-1)(d-4)}{4}+\left[\frac{d-3}{d-2}\right]^{\frac{d-1}{d-2}}\left[\frac{2k+1}{4}\right]^{\frac{1}{d-2}}\frac{\Pi_{\textsf{T}}(d)}{4\pi}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{3\pi i}{2(d-2)}}\right)\right],

(99)

with
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
\Pi_{\textsf{T}}(d)
given by (
83
).

Written in this form, for each value of
d
one can evaluate the real and imaginary parts of the frequencies
\omega
. We notice that because of the
\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{3\pi i}{2(d-2)}}
term the
\alpha^{\prime}
correction is complex, which means it will affect both the real and the imaginary parts of
\omega
. The real part of the asymptotic limit of
\frac{\omega}{T_{\mathcal{H}}}
, therefore, is no longer equal to the universal value
\ln(3)
: because of the
\alpha^{\prime}
-correction, it now also depends on the spacetime dimension
d
and on the mode number
k
.

It is interesting to study the magnitudes of the different contributions to the
\lambda^{\prime}
correction in (
99
). For that purpose, we have
evaluated
\left[\frac{d-3}{d-2}\right]^{\frac{d-1}{d-2}}\frac{\Pi_{\textsf{T}}(d)}{4\pi}
numerically for the relevant values of
d
. This factor grows monotonically with
d
, varying from approximately 0.19 (corresponding to
d=5
) to approximately 13.5 (corresponding to
d=10
). Just for comparison,
\frac{(d-1)(d-4)}{4}
varies between 1 and
13.5
on the same range. For values of
k
that are not very large (although verifying the condition
(2k+1)\pi\gg\ln(3)
defining the highly damped limit (
18
)), the two terms in the
\lambda^{\prime}
correction in (
99
) are of comparable orders of magnitude. For these “intermediate” values of
k
one must consider those two terms and, therefore, (
99
) cannot be simplified.

For fixed values of
\omega
, we notice our results may loose precision as
d
increases. Indeed, as we discussed in appendix
A
, while computing the monodromy of
\psi
around the big contour we made an approximation in the asymptotic expansion (
116
) by discarding terms of order at most
\left(\frac{\omega}{T_{\mathcal{H}}}\right)^{-\frac{1}{d-2}}.
This is acceptable for the theoretical asymptotic limit, in which we assume that
|\omega|\to+\infty
. However, one must take care, when applying our results for very large, but not arbitrary so, values of
|\omega|
. Indeed, suppose one uses our transcendental equations to compute values of
\omega
such that

\Big|\frac{\omega}{T_{\mathcal{H}}}\Big|\sim 10^{8}.

(100)

Then, those values were computed assuming that
10^{-\frac{8}{d-2}}
is negligible. However, taking
d=5,7,9
yields

10^{-\frac{8}{3}}\approx 0.002,\hskip 20.0pt10^{-\frac{8}{5}}\approx 0.03,\hskip 20.0pt10^{-\frac{8}{7}}\approx 0.07,

(101)

respectively. As we can see, the approximation grows worst as we increase
d
. Thus, our results for progressively higher dimensions only apply to progressively higher values of
\Big|\frac{\omega}{T_{\mathcal{H}}}\Big|
.

3.9
The asymptotic limit

The decomposition of
\omega
into its real and imaginary parts is simpler to obtain if one takes the asymptotic limit (i.e. the limit of large
k
) in (
99
).

One should keep in mind that we have been assuming in our calculations the highly damped limit (
18
). Assuming the result of Einstein gravity (
97
), the highly damped limit (
18
) is simply equivalent to having
(2k+1)\pi\gg\ln(3)
; a very large value of
k
implies then the highly damped limit, but the converse does not need to be true. One may now ask the question: considering the
\alpha^{\prime}
correction to
\omega
, does the large
k
limit still imply the highly damped limit (
18
)?

Throughout this work we have been taking a perturbative expansion in
\lambda
(or
\alpha^{\prime}
). With such expansion, we have taken only terms of first order in
\lambda
, because that was the order considered in the lagrangian (
7
): terms of higher order are meaningless unless they had been included in the lagrangian since the beginning, changing the field equations and their solutions like (
9
). These are only approximate and subject to even higher order corrections in
\lambda
, and the same is true for the perturbation potential
V_{\textsf{T}}[f(r)]
given by (
8
). But once the order of perturbation is fixed and
V_{\textsf{T}}[f(r)]
is given, the master equation (
5
) is also fixed and can be solved exactly, like we did. The only reason we took the expansions (
20
) of the tortoise coordinate and (
37
), (
38
) of the perturbation function and potential was the fact that the
\lambda-
corrected master differential equation is nonhomogeneous, and the corresponding homogeneous equation is precisely the
\lambda=0
master equation. The motivation for considering (
37
), (
38
) was not a perturbative expansion, but the (exact) method of variation of parameters to solve nonhomogeneous differential equations. This way, our solution to the master equation is exact to first order in
\lambda
, and so are the mathematical consequences that we can extract from it.

In general, a perturbative expansion means that a higher order term is expected to be negligible when compared to a lower order term, the reason being that the perturbative parameter is supposed to be small. That is certainly the case for our parameter
\lambda
. But that does not mean at all that
\lambda
can be taken as arbitrarily small, and therefore it may happen that a higher order term of a perturbative expansion in
\lambda
is not negligible if such term is made arbitrarily large.

But from equation (
125
) in appendix
A
and from (
67
) and (
68
) we see that
\lambda^{\prime}\psi_{1}
has an overall factor

\left(R_{H}\omega\right)^{\frac{d-1}{d-2}}
in the asymptotic limit when
|\omega z|\gg 1
. Such overall factor is also present in the
\lambda
corrections
\delta\mathcal{M}_{1}
to the monodromy
\mathcal{M}_{1}
in (
83
) and to the result for
\frac{\omega}{T_{\mathcal{H}}}
in eq. (
96
) (in this case after having
R_{H}
expressed in terms of
T_{\mathcal{H}}
in (
95
)). One expects the perturbative parameter
\lambda^{\prime}
to be small, but if the absolute value
|\omega|
is very large none of these
\lambda^{\prime}
corrections to
\psi
and
\mathcal{M}_{1}
is negligible, and nor is the one of
\omega
itself. This is what happens in the asymptotic limit, with very large mode number
k
and very large
\mathop{\rm Im}(\omega)
. One may argue that
\lambda^{\prime}
is a small parameter, and therefore the larger the order in
\lambda^{\prime}
, the smaller the (relative) magnitude of the
\lambda^{\prime}
correction, even if their (absolute) magnitude is large. That argument may be true in our concrete discussion for large but finite
k
, but not in the asymptotic limit of very large
k
, say
k\to\infty
. For such limit the first order
\lambda^{\prime}
correction becomes too large, as one can see from (
98
) or from (
99
), and it cannot be neglected in any circumstance, contrarily to what one expects from a perturbative correction. Presumably the same is true for higher order
\lambda^{\prime}
corrections, which means in this limit the perturbative expansion breaks down. Therefore, strictly speaking, the asymptotic limit cannot be inferred from our result. The condition (
18
) we requested for the validity of our method should not be understood as “arbitrarily large
\mathop{\rm Im}(\omega)
(or
k
)”:
\mathop{\rm Im}(\omega)
and
k
can be very large, but not so large to break down the perturbative expansion. Other works computing asymptotic quasinormal modes with higher derivative corrections, such as
Daghigh:2006xg
, require a similar interpretation for (
18
).

We suppose now that
k
is large enough so that we can neglect the whole term multiplied by
\ln 3
and also the term
\frac{(d-1)(d-4)}{4}
in the
\lambda^{\prime}
correction in (
99
). If that is the case, we obtain then the simpler result that we were looking for in the beginning of this section:

\frac{\omega}{T_{\mathcal{H}}}=\ln(3)+(2k+1)\pi i+\lambda\left(\frac{4\pi}{d-3}\right)^{2}T_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}\left[\frac{d-3}{d-2}\frac{(2k+1)}{4}\right]^{\frac{d-1}{d-2}}\Pi_{\textsf{T}}(d)\,\mathrm{e}^{\frac{d-5}{2(d-2)}\pi i}

(102)

Similarly to (
99
), because of the
\mathrm{e}^{\frac{d-5}{2(d-2)}\pi i}
term the
\alpha^{\prime}
correction is complex, which means it will affect both the real and the imaginary parts of
\omega
. We have evaluated this term numerically for the relevant values of
d
. While
\cos\left(\frac{d-5}{2(d-2)}\pi\right)
varies between 1 (corresponding to
d=5
) and approximately 0.56 (corresponding to
d=10
),
\sin\left(\frac{d-5}{2(d-2)}\pi\right)
varies on the same range from 0 to approximately 0.83. For
d=5
, therefore, in this limit only the real part of
\omega
gets an
\alpha^{\prime}
correction. For other relevant values of
d
, (first order)
\alpha^{\prime}
corrections to the real and the imaginary parts of
\omega
are both positive and have similar orders of magnitude in this limit (for
d=8
they are actually equal).

4
Asymptotic quasinormal modes of test scalar fields

A minimally coupled test scalar field propagating in the background of a black hole of the form (
1
) can be expanded as

\Phi(t,r,\theta)=e^{i\omega t}\sum_{\ell}\Phi_{\ell,\omega}(r)Y_{\ell}(\theta)\,,

(103)

where
\omega
is the wave frequency,
\ell
is the angular quantum number associated with the polar angle
\theta
and
Y_{\ell}(\theta)
are the usual spherical harmonics defined over the
(d-2)
unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{d-2}
. Each component
\Phi_{\ell,\omega}(r)
obeys a field equation like (
5
), with precisely the same potential of the tensorial perturbations in Einstein gravity, given
hep-th/0206084
by
V_{\textsf{min}}[f_{0}(r)]
in (
6
), with
f_{0}(r)
given by (
10
). In the presence of
\alpha^{\prime}
corrections this field equation remains the same (since the scalar field is minimally coupled); the only effect of the
\alpha^{\prime}
corrections in it is indirect, through the metric. The potential corresponding to such equation is now given by
V_{\textsf{min}}[f(r)]
, with
f(r)
corresponding to the
\alpha^{\prime}
-corrected metric (in our case given by (
9
)).

Naturally, if the field equation is the same, so is the spectrum of quasinormal modes. In Einstein gravity, therefore, test scalar fields and tensorial gravitational perturbations share the same spectra of quasinormal modes. For an
\alpha^{\prime}
-corrected metric, because of the difference in the potentials that is no longer true: the two spectra are indeed different. In
Moura:2021eln
we verified this fact by computing the quasinormal modes in the eikonal limit for both cases.

In this section we address the calculation of the quasinormal modes corresponding to test scalar fields in the highly damped regime, like we previous did for tensorial gravitational perturbations, in the background of the
\alpha^{\prime}
-corrected black hole given by (
9
). The calculation is totally analogous to the one described in section
3
; we just have to consider the effects of changing the potential.

This change in the potential does not affect the monodromy
\mathcal{M}_{2}
around the small contour, since close to the horizon the general potential
V_{\textsf{T}}[f(r)]
vanishes, and so does the potential
V_{\textsf{min}}[f(r)]
we are now considering. The same is true at infinity.

The result affected by the change in the potential, as one could expect, is the monodromy
\mathcal{M}_{1}
around the big contour. The procedure we took in subsections
3.3
to
3.6
to compute this monodromy, with a perturbative solution to the master equation, remains valid; it is actually totally analogous. The only change is in the expansion (
38
) of the potential:
V_{0}(r)
remains given by
V_{\textsf{min}}[f_{0}(r)]
, but because
V(r)
is now different, so is
V_{1}(r)
.

Close to the origin,
V_{1}(r)
is given by (
61
), but with
\Upsilon_{3}
in (
65
) replaced by the new value

\Upsilon_{3}\mapsto\Upsilon_{3}=\frac{1}{4}(-1)^{\rho}(d-2)^{\rho+1}(d-4)(d-3)(2d-3).

(104)

This change in the value of
\Upsilon_{3}
implies a change in the values of the coefficients
\Omega_{3}^{\pm}(d,j,\omega,R_{H}),\,\Theta_{3}^{\pm}(d,j,\omega,R_{H})

\Xi_{3}^{\pm}(d,j,\omega,R_{H})

\Lambda_{I}^{\pm}(d,j,\omega,R_{H})
,
\Lambda_{F}^{\pm}(d,j,\omega,R_{H})

\lambda
correction
\delta\mathcal{M}_{1}
in the monodromy
\mathcal{M}_{1}
around the big contour. This correction is now given by

\displaystyle\delta\mathcal{M}_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{R_{H}\omega}{d-2}\right)^{\frac{d-1}{d-2}}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{2\pi i}{d-2}}\Pi_{\textsf{S}}(d),

(105)

\displaystyle\Pi_{\textsf{S}}(d)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\frac{8}{3}\frac{(d-4)(d-3)(d-2)}{d-1}\frac{\pi^{2}}{2^{\frac{1}{d-2}}}\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{2(d-2)}\right)\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{d-2}\right)}{\left[\Gamma\left(\frac{d-1}{2d-4}\right)\right]^{4}}.

\Pi_{\textsf{T}}(d)

\Pi_{\textsf{T}}(d)

\Pi_{\textsf{S}}(d)

\frac{\omega}{T_{\mathcal{H}}}=\left(\ln(3)+(2k+1)\pi i\right)\left[1+\lambda\frac{16\pi^{2}}{(d-3)^{2}}T_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}\left(\frac{(d-1)(d-4)}{4}+\left[\frac{d-3}{d-2}\right]^{\frac{d-1}{d-2}}\left[\frac{2k+1}{4}\right]^{\frac{1}{d-2}}\frac{\Pi_{\textsf{S}}(d)}{4\pi}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{3\pi i}{2(d-2)}}\right)\right],

(106)

with

and
\Pi_{\textsf{S}}(d)
given by (
105

We have evaluated
\left[\frac{d-3}{d-2}\right]^{\frac{d-1}{d-2}}\frac{\Pi_{\textsf{S}}(d)}{4\pi}

\frac{\omega}{T_{\mathcal{H}}}=\ln(3)+(2k+1)\pi i+\lambda\left(\frac{4\pi}{d-3}\right)^{2}T_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}\left[\frac{d-3}{d-2}\frac{(2k+1)}{4}\right]^{\frac{d-1}{d-2}}\Pi_{\textsf{S}}(d)\,\mathrm{e}^{\frac{d-5}{2(d-2)}\pi i}.

5
Conclusions and future directions

In this work we have computed analytically the quasinormal frequencies in the highly damped limit corresponding to tensorial gravitational perturbations and scalar test fields for the simplest case of a
d-
dimensional spherically symmetric black hole solution with leading string
\alpha^{\prime}
corrections given by the Callan-Myers-Perry black hole (
9
). In both cases, we have obtained conditions that restrict the quasinormal frequencies
\omega
to an infinite but countable set.

\alpha^{\prime}
corrections are expressed through a transcendental (in
\omega
) term in those conditions, that we may solve perturbatively in
\omega
, obtaining a closed form of the final result. In the Einstein limit, both the real and the imaginary parts of the quasinormal frequencies are independent of the spacetime dimension
d
, but the
\alpha^{\prime}
corrections we obtained depend strongly on
d
. We checked that the magnitude of these
\alpha^{\prime}
corrections increases with
d
.

The
\alpha^{\prime}
corrections we obtained grow with the mode number
k
, which means the allowed values of
k
should be large (in order to verify the highly damped limit we have assumed), but cannot be taken arbitrarily large, or the perturbative expansion will break down. Taking arbitrarily large values of
k
does not have an immediate practical relevance; we considered that asymptotic limit only as a partial analysis. Assuming the higher order in
\alpha^{\prime}
corrections also to grow with the mode number
k
, a complete analysis of the asymptotic limit should include the complete contributions from all such orders.

Since the value of
k
is large but not arbitrarily large, corrections in powers of
1/k
to the highly damped quasinormal frequencies could also be included. Corrections of that type have been studied in
Musiri:2003bv
;
Cardoso:2003vt
, and those results should be considered together with the ones we obtain in this article.

In the highly damped limit, the
\alpha^{\prime}
correction terms we obtained are different for quasinormal frequencies of tensorial gravitational perturbations and quasinormal frequencies of scalar test fields. Therefore, under the stringy correction considered, the highly damped limits of the associated quasinormal spectra are different. This situation is similar to the eikonal limit, where we also found different results for quasinormal frequencies of tensorial gravitational perturbations and scalar test fields
Moura:2021eln
. However, in Einstein gravity all quasinormal frequencies (for tensorial, vectorial and scalar gravitational perturbations and for scalar test fields as well) are equal in the eikonal and in the highly damped limits. Stringy
\alpha^{\prime}
corrections, therefore, allow us to distinguish between the quasinormal spectra of tensorial gravitational perturbations and scalar test fields in the eikonal and highly damped limits. It would be very interesting to check if vectorial and scalar gravitational perturbations also exhibit a similar behaviour in the stringy solution we considered. That is, should the quasinormal spectra associated with these perturbations be affected under the stringy correction as well and, if so, should they differ from the spectra associated with the remaining perturbations in the eikonal and highly damped limits?

In order to answer these questions, one must study vectorial and scalar gravitational perturbations in the Callan-Myers-Perry black hole and then obtain their quasinormal spectra in the highly damped and eikonal limits. These are topics for future works.
