A thin film model for meniscus evolution
Amrita Ghosh, Juan J.L. Velázquez

Abstract
In this paper, we discuss a particular model arising from sinking of a rigid solid into a thin film of liquid, i.e. a liquid contained between two solid surfaces and part of the liquid surface is in contact with the air. The liquid is governed by Navier-Stokes equation, while the contact point, i.e. where the gas, liquid and solid meet, is assumed to be given by a constant, non-zero contact angle. We consider a scaling limit of the liquid thickness (lubrication approximation) and the contact angle between the liquid-solid and the liquid-gas interfaces is close to π \pi . This resulting model is a free boundary problem for the equation h t + ( h 3 ​ h x ​ x ​ x ) x = 0 h_{t}+(h^{3}h_{xxx})_{x}=0 , for which we have h > 0 h>0 at the contact point (different from the usual thin film equation with h = 0 h=0 at the contact point). We show that this fourth order quasilinear (non-degenerate) parabolic equation, together with the so-called partial wetting condition at the contact point, is well-posed. Furthermore, the contact point in our thin film equation can actually move, contrary to the classical thin film equation for a droplet arising from the no-slip condition. Additionally, we show the global stability of steady state solutions in a periodic setting.

Introduction

In this paper, we investigate a particular thin film model, where a rigid solid enters a liquid film (cf. Figure
1
), leading to movement of the contact point (where the gas, liquid and solid meet) and the formation of a meniscus, as the initial state is out of equilibrium. This free boundary problem gives rise to the following fourth order non-linear parabolic equation, in one dimension,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}h+\partial_{x}(h^{3}\partial^{3}_{x}h)=0\qquad\text{ in }x>\Lambda,t>0,

(1.1a)

along with the boundary conditions,

h=g,\qquad\partial_{x}h=\partial_{x}g-k,\qquad h\;\partial_{x}^{3}h=-\frac{2\Lambda\;\partial_{t}g}{g^{2}}\quad\qquad\text{ at }x=\Lambda,t>0.

(1.1b)

:
General meniscus formation

Here,

•

h=h(x,t)
is the height of the liquid film;

•

\Lambda=\Lambda(t)
is the contact point (which is a free boundary in one dimension);

•

k
is the contact angle (rescaled), i.e. the angle between the liquid-solid and the liquid-gas interfaces; and

•

g=g(x,t)
is the profile of the rigid solid, with a vertically downward motion only.

The above condition on solid means that
\partial_{xt}g=0
for all
x,t
.

Formal derivation of the above system is elaborated in Section
2
. Here the situation in one spatial dimension is studied only, although one may consider general dimension as well (physically relevant cases correspond to
d=1
or
2
). The study of fluid problems involving free boundary evolution mostly concerns with an interface between two phases (cf.
[
26
]
), whereas the interest of the current paper is the situation where three phases of matter meet (typically, liquid-solid-air), generating a
contact line/point
(also known as
triple junction
). The main differences between the model considered here with other classical triple junction models is pointed out later.

Equation (
1.1a
):

Let us first discuss equation
(
1.1a
) which can be viewed as a particular case of the general class of thin film equations

\partial_{t}h+\partial_{x}(f(h)\partial_{x}^{3}h)=0\quad\text{ in }\{h>0\}.

(1.2)

Equations of the form (
1.2
) are typically obtained by lubrication approximation of the motion of a two-dimensional viscous liquid droplet, spreading over a solid substrate by the effect of surface tension (cf.
[
3
]
). Classical macroscopic fluid mechanics to model such phenomenon imposes the no-slip boundary condition at the liquid-solid interface, that is the velocity of the liquid must be equal to that of the solid substrate in contact with. This corresponds to the case
f(h)=h^{3}
. However, this condition cannot hold at the moving contact point/triple junction where the solid, liquid and gas, three phases meet. It is known to give rise to a non-integrable singularity at the contact point (cf.
[
15
]
) which further implies that boundary of the film (i.e. the triple junction) cannot move in time (
no slip paradox
). On other hand, Navier slip condition (corresponding to the case
f(h)=h^{2}
), proposed by Navier
[
21
]
, which allows the liquid to slip over the solid surface (characterized by a slip parameter), resolves such paradox (cf.
[
22
]
for a discussion in this direction).

Note that equation (
1.2
) can also be viewed as a fourth order version for the elliptic part of the porous medium equation (with different sign)

\partial_{t}h-\partial_{x}(h^{n}\partial_{x}h)=0,\quad n>0.

However, the standard techniques used for such second order degenerate parabolic equations (such as a maximum principle) are not available for the higher order counterpart which makes the analysis more difficult for the latter (cf.
[
10
]
).

Equation (
1.1b
):

Next let us discuss the boundary conditions in (
1.1b
). The first condition gives the position of the free boundary, whereas the second one determines the slope at the free boundary (contact angle condition). The case where the (equilibrium) contact angle
k
is given by a non-zero constant, determined by
Young’s law
(balance between interfacial energies) (cf.
[
6
]
), is known as
partial wetting
, whereas the zero contact angle is referred as
complete wetting
. This work takes into account both possibilities. There are also notions of dynamic/apparent contact angle (cf.
[
14
,
23
]
). The third boundary condition comes from a matching condition with the internal region (i.e. the liquid region bounded between solid parts), while in the standard setting of a droplet, a third condition is deduced from matching the velocity of the contact point. This would lead to an over-determined problem for a fourth order operator with fixed boundary, but not for the free boundary problem where the boundary is an unknown.

State of the art.
All the literature available in the context of the thin film equation for contact angle problems consider either compactly supported initial data (droplet case) or an unbounded support with single free boundary. Thus the standard condition
h=0
of the vanishing liquid height at the free boundary makes the equation
\partial_{t}h+\partial_{x}(h^{n}\partial^{3}_{x}h)=0
singular/degenerate. Following the pioneering work of Bernis and Friedman
[
2
]
, several results
have been established for (
1.2
), considering different forms of
f
, for example in
[
10
]
,
[
9
]
-
[

]
,
[

]
-
[

]
,
[

]
. We also point out the work
[

]
where stability of a global solution for the general contact line problem for the full Stokes equation has been discussed. Furthermore, the works
[
1
,
5
,
12
,
16
]
discuss water wave problems for floating bodies (more generally, wave-structure interactions) which primarily concern the inviscid fluid governed by Euler equation, and driven by the inertial forces; Whereas the dominant force for the system in the current paper is the viscosity, modelled by Navier-Stokes equation. As a result, the water wave models are hyperbolic in nature while the contact angle problem considered in the current work is parabolic. On the other hand, rigorous derivation of thin film approximations of free boundary problems for the Stokes equation has been studied by several people, e.g. in
[
4
]
. The above references are not claimed to be exhaustive.

The current work is concerned with a different thin film model, deduced from the no-slip boundary condition assumption at the liquid-solid interface. The film height being
h>0
at the contact point makes our model non-degenerate, unlike the standard thin film equation as discussed above. The result shows well-posedness as well as the possibility of a
moving
contact point. Moreover, no paradox arises in spite that the contact point moves.
The reason of such behavior in this case is
that the contact angle is very close to
\pi
. As pointed out by Solonnikov in
[
27
]
, the paradox gets resolved in the case of contact angle
\pi
. The main contribution of the paper is to bring forward this particular interesting yet simple situation involving the evolution of triple junction and the discussion of different behavior in contrast to the known cases.
This observation intrigued our curiosity to analyze the model in detail.

Structure of the paper.
We first deduce a general thin film model describing the above particular situation of meniscus formation under the classical approach in Section
2
. The arguments used in this derivation are standard. Still we write this derivation in detail, since we did not find any suitable reference for such a model. In Section
3
, the local well-posedness result is established, for the particular case of a no-slip boundary condition. The system being non-degenerate, standard theory for fourth order parabolic quasilinear equations can be employed. Nevertheless, obtaining suitable regularity (in time) in order to closing the final fixed point argument is not straight-forward (cf. Remark
3.1
). Finally we obtain the asymptotic behavior of a solution, in the periodic setting, in Section
4
. By looking at perturbations of a steady state of (
1.1a
)-(
1.1b
), existence of a unique global solution is obtained with the help of a priori energy estimates. As long as the initial data remains close (in a certain norm) to the stationary solution, the local solution of the thin film model converges to the steady state for all time. Such stability result of the so-called propagating fronts in approximated version of compressible Navier-Stokes equations have been considered in
[
5
]
.

1.1
Main results

Our first result concerns a formal derivation of the lubrication model of meniscus formation and contact point evolution as a solid body immerses into a thin film of incompressible, viscous liquid, surrounded by gas. Refer to Section
2
and beginning of Subsection
3.2
for the details.

Theorem 1.1

The following system of thin film equations with moving contact point and fixed contact angle (including the complete wetting case) is obtained from the classical fluid dynamical equations, coupled with rigid solid and gas, under no-slip boundary condition at the liquid-solid interface, as

\displaystyle\partial_{t}h+\partial_{x}(h^{3}\partial^{3}_{x}h)=0

\displaystyle\text{ in }x>\Lambda(t),t>0,

(1.3)

\displaystyle h=g,\quad\partial_{x}h=\partial_{x}g-k,\quad h\;\partial_{x}^{3}h=-2\Lambda\frac{\partial_{t}g}{g^{2}}

\displaystyle\text{ at }x=\Lambda(t),t>0,

\displaystyle h\to 1

\displaystyle\text{ as }\ x\to\infty,t>0,

\displaystyle h=h_{0}

\displaystyle\text{ at }\ t=0,x>\Lambda_{0}.

Here
\Lambda_{0}=\Lambda(0)
denotes the initial position of the contact point.

Next, existence of a local in time solution to (
1.3
) is deduced.

Theorem 1.2
(Heuristic version of Theorem
3.5
)

For any initial data
(h_{0},\Lambda_{0})
, satisfying compatibility conditions, there exists a unique strong solution
(h,\Lambda)
, for short times, of the free boundary problem (
1.3
).

A rigorous statement and proof of the above theorem is given in Section
3
.
Next we consider the system (
1.3
) over an interval
(0,2L)
(in order to have a bounded domain), symmetric with respect to
x=L
and with periodic boundary conditions (cf. the beginning of Section
4
for further discussion on the choice of such domain). Precisely,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}h+\partial_{x}(h^{3}\partial^{3}_{x}h)=0

\displaystyle\text{ in }\ x\in(\Lambda,L),t>0,

(1.4)

\displaystyle h=g,\quad\partial_{x}h=\partial_{x}g-k,\quad h\;\partial_{x}^{3}h=0

\displaystyle\text{ at }\ x=\Lambda,t>0,

\displaystyle\partial_{x}h=0

\displaystyle\text{ at }\ x=L,t>0,

\displaystyle h=h_{0}

\displaystyle\text{ at }\ t=0,x\in(\Lambda_{0},L),

\displaystyle h(x,t)=h(x+2L,t),\quad g(x,t)=g(x+2L,t),

\displaystyle x,t>0.

The free interface
h(x,t)
is defined over the domain
(\Lambda,2L-\Lambda)
in this case. Also we assume here that the solid is not moving, i.e.

We refer to (

) for an explicit description of the steady state.

Theorem 1.3
(Heuristic version of Theorem

For initial data close enough to the steady state of (

2
Lubrication approximation

In this section, we elaborate the thin film approximation of the original three phases free boundary problem in consideration.

Original model
:
Let us consider a horizontal, two-dimensional thin film of viscous, incompressible Newtonian liquid, of thickness
H
at the initial time and a rigid
solid (locally convex) touching the film (cf. Figure
2
). As the solid enters the liquid, the contact point moves and makes an angle
(\pi-\theta)
,
\theta>0
, between the liquid-solid and the liquid-gas interface. The liquid-gas interface is described by
y=h(x,t)
and the solid bottom is parametrized by
y=g(x,t)
.
One may think of particular cases, for example
g=H\left(1-\left(\frac{t}{t_{0}}\right)^{n}\right)
with
n>0
where
t_{0}
is the time scale that characterises the motion of the solid moving down. Here
n=1
corresponds to the solid moving with constant velocity, while
n=2
corresponds to the case of constant acceleration. A particular case of wedge-shaped solid (in the form
g(x,t)=\tilde{h}(t)+c|x|
) has been mentioned in
[
25
, Fig 6]
in a context of a correct modelling approach describing the creation of a contact angle.
The free interface makes an angle
\tilde{\theta}
with the horizontal plane (cf. Figure
2
). Therefore,
\tan\tilde{\theta}=-\partial_{x}h
and
\theta=-\arctan(\partial_{x}h)+\arctan(\partial_{x}g)
. To obtain the lubrication approximation, we need to assume that the two angles
\theta
and
\tilde{\theta}
are very small and of the same order of magnitude, which means that the contact angle
(\pi-\theta)
is close to
\pi
in this setting. The expected thin film model is obtained when the small parameter
\theta
vanishes. Note that the thin film approximation would not be valid if
\tilde{\theta}=O(1)
. The contact points are given by
s_{i}=(\Lambda_{i}(t),g(\Lambda_{i}(t),t))
.
Finally, we assume the far field condition
h\to H
as
|x|\to\infty
.

Let
\mathbf{v}^{L}=(u,v)
be the velocity of the liquid, with constant density
\varrho^{L}
, viscosity
\mu^{L}
and pressure
p^{L}
, governed by the Navier-Stokes equations, while the solid motion is governed by the translational velocity
\mathbf{v}^{S}(t)
only, pointing vertically downward. Let us consider the bottom part of the liquid domain as a reference configuration, i.e. at
y=0
.

In the framework of classical fluid mechanics, conditions at the triple junction are considered only at equilibrium, i.e. in the situations where the contact point is not moving with respect to the bulk phases. Furthermore, we consider here the Navier slip condition at the liquid-solid interface, for a general framework, which covers both the no-slip and the full-slip condition. 3
Local well-posedness for no-slip condition

3.1
Notations and functional settings

Let
C_{0}[0,\infty)
denote the Banach space of continuous functions on
[0,\infty)
vanishing at infinity, together with the supremum norm on
[0,\infty)
. The space
C^{k}[0,\infty),k\in\mathbb{N}
of functions with
k
times continuous and bounded derivatives is endowed with the standard norm

\|v\|_{C^{k}[0,\infty)}:=\sum_{m=0}^{k}\|\partial^{m}v\|_{C[0,\infty)}.

We write
C[0,\infty)\equiv C^{0}[0,\infty)
for the space of all continuous, bounded functions on
[0,\infty)
.
The Banach space of
\rho
-Hölder continuous functions
C^{k+\rho}[0,\infty)
for
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}:=\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\},\rho\in(0,1)
, is defined by

C^{k+\rho}[0,\infty):=\{v\in C^{k}[0,\infty):[\partial^{k}v]_{\rho}:=\sup_{\underset{x\neq y}{x,y\in[0,\infty)}}\frac{|\partial^{k}v(x)-\partial^{k}v(y)|}{|x-y|^{\rho}}<\infty\},

together with the norm

\|v\|_{C^{k+\rho}[0,\infty)}:=\|v\|_{C^{k}[0,\infty)}+[\partial^{k}v]_{\rho}.

For
\rho=1
, the above notions coincide with the class of Lipschitz functions which we denote by
C^{1-}[0,\infty)
.
Sometimes we omit the underlying space
[0,\infty)
below which should not cause any confusion.

3.2
Reduction to a fixed domain

Recall that we are interested to study the specific situation of the no-slip boundary condition at the liquid-solid interface
and will show the local and global well-posedness for this problem. As mentioned in the introduction, such result is different from the standard analysis for the thin film equation for droplet with no-slip condition.

Using the time scaling

1/3

)-(

\displaystyle I

\displaystyle:=\int\displaylimits_{0}^{t}\int\displaylimits_{0}^{\infty}{G(0,t,y,\tau)(\dot{\Lambda}\psi_{2}\;\xi^{2}_{\delta})(y,\tau)\;\mathrm{d}y\;\mathrm{d}\tau}

\displaystyle=\int\displaylimits_{0}^{t}{(\dot{\Lambda}\psi_{2})(\tau)}\int\displaylimits_{0}^{\infty}{G(0,t,y,\tau)\;\mathrm{d}y\;\mathrm{d}\tau}-\int\displaylimits_{0}^{t}\int\displaylimits_{0}^{\infty}{G(0,t,y,\tau)(\dot{\Lambda}\psi_{2})(\tau)(1-\xi^{2}_{\delta})(y)\;\mathrm{d}y\;\mathrm{d}\tau}

\displaystyle=\int\displaylimits_{0}^{t}{\;\dot{\Lambda}(\tau)\;\psi_{2}(\Lambda(\tau),\tau)\;\mathrm{d}\tau}-\int\displaylimits_{0}^{t}\int\displaylimits_{0}^{\infty}{G(0,t,y,\tau)(\dot{\Lambda}\psi_{2})(\tau)(1-\xi^{2}_{\delta})(y)\;\mathrm{d}y\;\mathrm{d}\tau}

\displaystyle=:I_{1}+I_{2}.

(3.15)

At this point, let us denote
\Psi(\Lambda,t)=\int\displaylimits_{0}^{\Lambda}{\psi_{2}(y,t)\;\mathrm{d}y}
, which gives,

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\Psi=\int\displaylimits_{0}^{\Lambda}{\partial_{t}\psi_{2}(y,t)\;\mathrm{d}y}+\psi_{2}(\Lambda(t),t)\;\dot{\Lambda}(t).

Plugging this into (
3.15
), we get

\displaystyle I_{1}

\displaystyle=\int\displaylimits_{0}^{t}{\bigg[\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}\Psi(\Lambda(\tau),\tau)-\int\displaylimits_{0}^{\Lambda(\tau)}{\partial_{\tau}\psi_{2}(y,\tau)\;\mathrm{d}y}\bigg]\mathrm{d}\tau}

\displaystyle=\left[\Psi(\Lambda(t),t)-\Psi(0,0)\right]-\int\displaylimits_{0}^{t}{\int\displaylimits_{0}^{\Lambda(\tau)}{\partial_{\tau}\psi_{2}(y,\tau)\;\mathrm{d}y}\;\mathrm{d}\tau}.

Using the definition of
\Psi
and
\psi_{2}
, one further obtains, after some integration by parts with respect to
y
,

\displaystyle I_{1}

\displaystyle=\int\displaylimits_{0}^{\Lambda}{(\partial_{y}g(y,t)-k)\;\mathrm{d}y}-\int\displaylimits_{0}^{t}{(\partial_{\tau}g(\Lambda(\tau),\tau)-\partial_{\tau}g(0,\tau))\;\mathrm{d}\tau}

\displaystyle=\left[(g(\Lambda,t)-g(0,t))-k\Lambda\right]-\int\displaylimits_{0}^{t}{\partial_{\tau}g(\Lambda(\tau),\tau)\;\mathrm{d}\tau}+\left[g(0,t)-g(0,0)\right]

\displaystyle=g(\Lambda,t)-g(0,0)-k\;\Lambda(t).

(3.16)

Therefore, combining the relations (
3.14
), (
3.15
) and (
3.16
), one gets, by the definition of
\psi_{1}
in (
3.4
),

0=-g(0,0)-k\Lambda(t)+I_{2}+II+III+IV+V.

In other words, as
k>0
,

\displaystyle\Lambda(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k}\left[-g(0,0)+I_{2}+II+III+IV+V\right].

(3.17)

Next we need to estimate the different terms of (
3.17
), in particular to show that the above relation is a contraction map for
\Lambda
.

Estimate of
I_{2}
:

Using the estimate (
3.11
) for the fundamental solution, we have

\displaystyle|I_{2}|

\displaystyle=\bigg|\int\displaylimits_{0}^{t}{\dot{\Lambda}(\tau)\;\psi_{2}(\Lambda(\tau),\tau)\int\displaylimits_{0}^{\infty}{(\xi^{2}_{\delta}(y)-1)\;G(0,t,y,\tau)\;\mathrm{d}y}\;\mathrm{d}\tau}\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq c\int\displaylimits_{0}^{t}{|\dot{\Lambda}(\tau)\;\psi_{2}(\Lambda(\tau),\tau)|\int\displaylimits_{\delta}^{\infty}{(t-\tau)^{-1/4}\exp\left(-c\;\frac{y}{(t-\tau)^{1/4}}\right)\;\mathrm{d}y}\;\mathrm{d}\tau}

\displaystyle\leq c\int\displaylimits_{0}^{t}{|\dot{\Lambda}(\tau)\;\psi_{2}(\Lambda(\tau),\tau)|\;\exp\left(-c\;\tfrac{\delta}{(t-\tau)^{1/4}}\right)\;\mathrm{d}\tau}\;\leq c_{\delta}\int\displaylimits_{0}^{t}{|\dot{\Lambda}(\tau)|\;\mathrm{d}\tau}.

Due to the definition of
\psi_{2}
in (
3.4
), it is a bounded quantity given by the solid
g
. The above constant
c_{\delta}>0
can be made small by choosing
\delta
and in turn
T
suitably which makes the exponential term small enough.

Estimate of
II
:

Recalling the definition of
F_{2}
from (
3.8
), as it comprises several terms of the form
\dot{\Lambda}\;\partial^{k}_{x}U\;\xi^{\prime}_{\delta},k\in[0,3],
passing the derivatives onto
G
by integration by parts in

) for the fundamental solution, one obtains the following

\displaystyle|II|=\bigg|\int\displaylimits_{0}^{t}\int\displaylimits_{0}^{\infty}{G(0,t,y,\tau)F_{2}(y,\tau)\;\mathrm{d}y\;\mathrm{d}\tau}\bigg|\leq c_{\delta}\;\|U\|_{C((0,T_{0}),C[0,\infty))}\int\displaylimits_{0}^{t}{|\dot{\Lambda}(\tau)|(t-\tau)^{-\beta/4}\;\mathrm{d}\tau},

for some
\beta\in(0,4)
. Here we have used the crucial fact that

. As before, choosing

suitably, the constant in the above integral can be made small.

Estimate of
III
:

All the terms in (

) are small. For example,

\displaystyle\quad\bigg|\int\displaylimits_{0}^{t}\int\displaylimits_{0}^{\infty}{G(0,t,y,\tau)\;[\dot{\Lambda}\;3x^{2}\psi_{3}\;\xi^{2}_{\delta}](y,\tau)\;\mathrm{d}y\;\mathrm{d}\tau}\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq c\;\delta^{2}\int\displaylimits_{0}^{t}|\dot{\Lambda}(\tau)||\psi_{3}(\Lambda(\tau),\tau)|\int\displaylimits_{0}^{\delta}|G(0,t,y,\tau)|\;\mathrm{d}y\;\mathrm{d}\tau\leq c_{\delta}\;\|\Lambda\|_{C^{1+\frac{\alpha}{4}}[0,T]}.

Similarly, the other two terms can be estimated as,

\displaystyle\quad\left|\int\displaylimits_{0}^{t}\int\displaylimits_{0}^{\infty}{G(0,t,y,\tau)\;[6(1-\Lambda\xi^{\prime}_{\delta})^{4}\;\partial_{x}((a\xi_{\delta}+(1-\xi_{\delta})+U)^{3}\;\psi_{3}\;x^{3}\;\xi_{\delta})-\partial_{t}a\;\xi_{\delta}](y,\tau)\;\mathrm{d}y\;\mathrm{d}\tau}\right|

\displaystyle\leq C_{\delta}(\|\psi_{2}\|,\|\psi_{3}\|,\|U(t)\|_{C[0,\infty)})\;(T+\|\Lambda\|_{C^{1+\frac{\alpha}{4}}[0,T]}).

Due to the expressions in (
3.4

.

:

By the same argument as for estimating

\displaystyle|IV|\leq c_{\delta}(\|U\|_{C((0,T_{0}),C[0,\infty))})\;(T+\|\Lambda\|_{C^{1+\frac{\alpha}{4}}[0,T]}).

One can prove the Lipschitz estimates for each term in (

) by the same argument as above. Note that the fundamental solution

Therefore, the map (

Theorem 3.5

Let
\Lambda_{0},h_{0}>0
and
h_{0}\in C^{4+\alpha}[\Lambda_{0},\infty),\alpha\in(0,1)
with

as

, with the regularity

4
Stability analysis

In this section, we discuss steady state/equilibrium solutions of the model (
1.3
) and asymptotic stability of the non-linear problem around a steady state.

For the standard thin film equation on an unbounded domain (with slip condition), the conservation of mass usually does not hold.
Therefore, with our current approach, we consider a periodic setting, or in other words, consecutive solid objects immersed in the liquid. Precisely, we assume that the initial thin film is spatially periodic, i.e. for some
L>0
,

h_{0}(x)=h_{0}(x+2L)\qquad\text{ for all }\ x\in\mathbb{R}.

(4.1)

Note that the periodicity assumption is primarily for mathematical simplification
in order to have a bounded domain (and in turn finite mass). It is also possible to consider a fixed lateral boundary, but only with small angle between the free interface and the lateral boundary for the lubrication approximation to be valid. Furthermore, it is enough to assume that the configuration is symmetric with respect to
y=L
.
Thus, we add the condition

\partial_{x}h=0\qquad\text{ at }\quad y=L

and hence, problem (
1.4
).

4.1
Conservation of mass

When the solid is stationary i.e.
\partial_{t}g=0
, observe that
(h\partial_{x}^{3}h)|_{x=\Lambda}=0
in
(\ref{25})_{2}
. Therefore we have, from the system (
1.4
),

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int\displaylimits_{\Lambda(t)}^{L}{h(x,t)\;\mathrm{d}x}=\int\displaylimits_{\Lambda(t)}^{L}{\partial_{t}h\;\mathrm{d}x}-\dot{\Lambda}\;h|_{x=\Lambda}

\displaystyle=-\int\displaylimits_{\Lambda(t)}^{L}{\partial_{x}(h^{3}\partial_{x}^{3}h)\;\mathrm{d}x}-\dot{\Lambda}\;h|_{x=\Lambda}

\displaystyle=-(h^{3}\partial_{x}^{3}h)|_{x=\Lambda}-\dot{\Lambda}\;h|_{x=\Lambda}=-\dot{\Lambda}\;h|_{x=\Lambda}

and

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int\displaylimits_{0}^{\Lambda(t)}{g(x,t)\;\mathrm{d}x}=\int\displaylimits_{0}^{\Lambda(t)}{\partial_{t}g\;\mathrm{d}x}+\dot{\Lambda}\;g|_{x=\Lambda}=\dot{\Lambda}\;g|_{x=\Lambda}

which shows due to
(\ref{25})_{2}
that the mass is conserved, i.e.

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\bigg(\int\displaylimits_{0}^{\Lambda(t)}{g(x,t)\;\mathrm{d}x}+\int\displaylimits_{\Lambda(t)}^{L}{h(x,t)\;\mathrm{d}x}\bigg)=\dot{\Lambda}\;g|_{x=\Lambda}-\dot{\Lambda}\;h|_{x=\Lambda}=0.

(4.2)

4.2
Equilibrium solution

In this subsection, we first compute the equilibrium solution of the system (
1.4
) for a given liquid volume (which happens to be the same as the steady state), as a constrained minimization problem using the Lagrange multipliers method. Subsequently, we deduce the energy dissipation relation.

For a fixed volume of the liquid

V_{0}=\int\displaylimits_{0}^{\Lambda(t)}{g\;\mathrm{d}x}+\int\displaylimits_{\Lambda(t)}^{L}{h\;\mathrm{d}x},

(4.3)

an equilibrium solution
(\overline{h},\overline{\Lambda})
of (
1.4
) is a minimizer of the total energy
E
, given by

E(t):=a\int\displaylimits_{0}^{\Lambda(t)}{|\partial_{x}g|^{2}\mathrm{d}x}+b\int\displaylimits_{\Lambda(t)}^{L}{|\partial_{x}h|^{2}\mathrm{d}x}+c\int\displaylimits_{\Lambda(t)}^{L}{|\partial_{x}g|^{2}\mathrm{d}x},

(4.4)

where
a,b,c
are non-negative constants with
b>0
, corresponding to the energy for the liquid-solid, liquid-gas and gas-solid interfaces respectively. Note that energy of the solid-gas interface must be included in the total energy. The total energy of the considered system (
1.4
) comprises of the kinetic energy of the bulk phases and the surface energies of the three interfaces. The former being negligible compared to the latter one, it reduces to (
4.4
).

One can compute the new energy for small changes
(\overline{h}+\delta h,\overline{\Lambda}+\delta\Lambda)
of the equilibrium state as

\displaystyle\ \ E+\delta E

\displaystyle=a\int\displaylimits_{0}^{\overline{\Lambda}+\delta\Lambda}{|\partial_{x}g|^{2}\mathrm{d}x}+b\int\displaylimits_{\overline{\Lambda}+\delta\Lambda}^{L}{|\partial_{x}(\overline{h}+\delta h)|^{2}\mathrm{d}x}+c\int\displaylimits_{\overline{\Lambda}+\delta\Lambda}^{L}{|\partial_{x}g|^{2}\mathrm{d}x}

\displaystyle=E+(a-c)\delta\Lambda|\partial_{x}g|^{2}|_{x=\overline{\Lambda}}-b\;\delta\Lambda|\partial_{x}(\overline{h}+\delta h)|^{2}|_{x=\overline{\Lambda}}+b\int\displaylimits_{\overline{\Lambda}}^{L}{(2\partial_{x}\overline{h}\;\partial_{x}(\delta h)+|\partial_{x}(\delta h)|^{2})\;\mathrm{d}x}.

Neglecting the small quadratic terms, one obtains,

\displaystyle\delta E=(a-c)\;\delta\Lambda|\partial_{x}g|^{2}|_{x=\overline{\Lambda}}-b\;\delta\Lambda|\partial_{x}\overline{h}|^{2}|_{x=\overline{\Lambda}}+2b\int\displaylimits_{\overline{\Lambda}}^{L}{\partial_{x}\overline{h}\;\partial_{x}(\delta h)\;\mathrm{d}x}.

Similarly, the new volume becomes,

V+\delta V=\int\displaylimits_{0}^{\overline{\Lambda}+\delta\Lambda}{g\;\mathrm{d}x}+\int\displaylimits_{\overline{\Lambda}+\delta\Lambda}^{L}{(\overline{h}+\delta h)\;\mathrm{d}x}=V+\int\displaylimits_{\overline{\Lambda}}^{L}{\delta h\;\mathrm{d}x}.

Hence,

\delta V=\int\displaylimits_{\overline{\Lambda}}^{L}{\delta h\;\mathrm{d}x}.

Therefore, we need to find a unique Lagrange multiplier
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\delta E=\lambda\;\delta V
, i.e. after integrating by parts,

(a-c)\;\delta\Lambda|\partial_{x}g|^{2}|_{x=\overline{\Lambda}}-b(\partial_{x}g|_{x=\overline{\Lambda}}-k)^{2}\delta\Lambda-2b\int\displaylimits_{\overline{\Lambda}}^{L}{\partial^{2}_{x}\overline{h}\;\delta h\;\mathrm{d}x}-2b(\partial_{x}\overline{h}\;\delta h)|_{x=\overline{\Lambda}}=\lambda\int\displaylimits_{\overline{\Lambda}}^{L}{\delta h\;\mathrm{d}x}.

(4.5)

Here we used the relation
(\ref{25})_{2}
since
(\overline{h},\overline{\Lambda})
solves the system (
1.4
).
Choosing
\delta\Lambda=0
and
\delta h
having compact support, one then obtains,

-2b\int\displaylimits_{\overline{\Lambda}}^{L}{\partial^{2}_{x}\overline{h}\;\delta h\;\mathrm{d}x}=\lambda\int\displaylimits_{\overline{\Lambda}}^{L}{\delta h\;\mathrm{d}x}\qquad\text{ which implies }\quad\partial^{2}_{x}\overline{h}=-\frac{\lambda}{2b},\quad x\in(\overline{\Lambda},L).

(4.6)

Also we have the continuity relation satisfied by the perturbed solution,

(\overline{h}+\delta h)|_{x=\overline{\Lambda}+\delta\Lambda}=g|_{x=\overline{\Lambda}+\delta\Lambda},

which gives, neglecting small terms,

\overline{h}|_{x=\overline{\Lambda}}+\partial_{x}\overline{h}|_{x=\overline{\Lambda}}\;\delta\Lambda+\delta h|_{x=\overline{\Lambda}}=g|_{x=\overline{\Lambda}}+\partial_{x}g|_{x=\overline{\Lambda}}\;\delta\Lambda

implying, by using
(\ref{25})_{2}
,

\delta h|_{x=\overline{\Lambda}}=(\partial_{x}g-\partial_{x}\overline{h})|_{x=\overline{\Lambda}}\;\delta\Lambda=k\;\delta\Lambda.

Inserting (
4.6
) into equation (
4.5
), we get a relation determining the contact angle,

b\;k^{2}+|\partial_{x}g|^{2}(a-b-c)=0,\qquad\text{or},\qquad k=\sqrt{\left(1-\tfrac{a-c}{b}\right)}\;\partial_{x}g.

(4.7)

Here we assume the condition
(b+c-a)/b>0
so that the above relation is well defined. Also recall that
k>0
(by construction).
This is a form of Young’s condition which says that the contact angle between the liquid-gas and the liquid-solid interfaces depends only on the slope of the solid and the energies of the interfaces, in other words, only on the material properties, as mentioned in the Introduction under paragraph "Equation (
1.1b
)".

From relation (
4.6
) and the boundary conditions
(\ref{25})_{2}
together with
\partial_{x}\overline{h}|_{x=L}=0
(due to the symmetry assumption), we can further determine the equilibrium solution completely,

\overline{h}=\tfrac{(\partial_{x}g-k)|_{x=\overline{\Lambda}}}{2(\overline{\Lambda}-L)}(x-L)^{2}+g|_{x=\overline{\Lambda}}-\tfrac{(\partial_{x}g-k)|_{x=\overline{\Lambda}}}{2}(\overline{\Lambda}-L),\qquad x\in(\overline{\Lambda},L),

(4.8)

and also the Lagrange multiplier, in terms of
\overline{\Lambda}
, as

\lambda=2b\tfrac{(\partial_{x}g-k)}{(L-\overline{\Lambda})}.

Finally, from the volume constraint (
4.3
), the contact point at equilibrium
\overline{\Lambda}
can be determined.

Energy dissipation:

The main idea in order to perform the stability analysis in Section
4.3
is to derive the energy dissipation formula. The existence of a classical solution in Theorem
3.5
in case of the half line, which also applies in case of periodic boundary conditions, allows us to make all the following calculations in a rigorous manner.

When the solid is stationary, it holds from
(\ref{25})_{2}
that
h|_{x=\Lambda}=g|_{x=\Lambda}
for
t>0
and in turn, we obtain the following relation by differentiating in time

\partial_{t}h+\dot{\Lambda}\;\partial_{x}h=\dot{\Lambda}\;\partial_{x}g\qquad\text{ which implies }\quad\partial_{t}h=k\dot{\Lambda}\quad\text{ at }x=\Lambda.

(4.9)

Therefore, one obtains the following energy dissipation relation from (
4.4
) as

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}E

\displaystyle=2b\int\displaylimits_{\Lambda(t)}^{L}{\partial_{x}h\;\partial_{xt}h\;\mathrm{d}x}-b\dot{\Lambda}|\partial_{x}h|^{2}|_{x=\Lambda}+(a-c)\dot{\Lambda}|\partial_{x}g|^{2}|_{x=\Lambda}

\displaystyle=-2b\int\displaylimits_{\Lambda(t)}^{L}{\partial_{x}h\;\partial^{2}_{x}(h^{3}\partial_{x}^{3}h)\;\mathrm{d}x}+\dot{\Lambda}((a-c)|\partial_{x}g|^{2}-b|\partial_{x}h|^{2})|_{x=\Lambda}\hskip 42.67912pt[\text{using }(\ref{25})_{1}]

\displaystyle=-2b\int\displaylimits_{\Lambda(t)}^{L}{h^{3}|\partial^{3}_{x}h|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x}+2b\;(\partial_{x}h\;\partial_{x}(h^{3}\partial_{x}^{3}h))|_{x=\Lambda}+\dot{\Lambda}((a-c)|\partial_{x}g|^{2}-b|\partial_{x}h|^{2})|_{x=\Lambda}

\displaystyle=-2b\int\displaylimits_{\Lambda(t)}^{L}{h^{3}|\partial^{3}_{x}h|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x}+\dot{\Lambda}((a-c)|\partial_{x}g|^{2}-b(\partial_{x}g-k)^{2}-2kb(\partial_{x}g-k))|_{x=\Lambda}

\displaystyle=-2b\int\displaylimits_{\Lambda(t)}^{L}{h^{3}|\partial^{3}_{x}h|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x}-\dot{\Lambda}((b+c-a)|\partial_{x}g|^{2}|_{x=\Lambda}-bk^{2})

\displaystyle=-2b\int\displaylimits_{\Lambda(t)}^{L}{h^{3}|\partial^{3}_{x}h|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x}.

(4.10)

We have used relations
(\ref{25})_{1}
, (
4.9
) and
(\ref{25})_{2}
to obtain the fourth equality whereas Young’s condition (
4.7
) is used at the last line. Furthermore, the condition
\partial_{x}h|_{x=L}=0
(due to the symmetry assumption) has been used in the above deduction.

Steady state:

For the stationary solution of (
1.4
), as the time derivative vanishes,
(\ref{25})_{1}
reduces to

\partial_{x}(h^{3}\partial_{x}^{3}h)=0\qquad\text{ in }\ (\Lambda,L),

which means two possibilities: either the thin film is given by a constant height or, a parabola (below we give a complete description, cf. Remark
4.1
). The constant thin film corresponds to the case where the slope of the thin film is zero, i.e.
\tilde{\theta}=0
(cf. Figure
2
) which means
\partial_{x}g=k
at
x=\Lambda
. If
\partial_{x}g\neq k
, then the shape of the thin film is given by a parabola. This indicates that the liquid film might rupture in finite time if the volume of the liquid is small.

Recall that the steady state of the classical thin film equation
\partial_{t}h+\partial_{x}(h^{2}\partial_{x}^{3}h)=0
on a bounded domain (with slip boundary condition) is indeed given by a parabola.

Remark 4.1

The above energy dissipation relation (
4.10
) shows that
the equilibrium solution
(\overline{h},\overline{\Lambda})
, given by (
4.8
), is also a steady state of (
1.4
) since it satisfies
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}E|_{(\overline{h},\overline{\Lambda})}=0
.

Remark 4.2

From the relation (
4.7
) and
(\ref{25})_{2}
, one obtains that
\partial_{x}g-\partial_{x}\overline{h}=\gamma\partial_{x}g
at
x=\overline{\Lambda}
where
\gamma=\sqrt{(b+c-a)/b}
. Depending on
\gamma\geq 1
or
\gamma<1
, the steady state has a (locally) convex or concave profile, respectively.

4.3
Asymptotic stability of steady state

Finally we are in a situation to study the stability of the stationary solution
(\overline{h},\overline{\Lambda})
of (
1.4
), given by (
4.8
).
To this end, we introduce the following linear transformation

\overline{x}=\tfrac{x(L-\overline{\Lambda})+L(\overline{\Lambda}-\Lambda)}{(L-\Lambda)}

which maps the domain
(\Lambda,L)
to the fixed domain
(\overline{\Lambda},L)
.
Differentiating, we get

\partial_{x}\overline{x}=\tfrac{(L-\overline{\Lambda})}{(L-\Lambda)},\qquad\partial_{t}\overline{x}=-\dot{\Lambda}\tfrac{(L-x)(L-\overline{\Lambda})}{(L-\Lambda)^{2}}.

In this new coordinate, let us denote
H(\overline{x},t)=h(x,t)
. Recall that
g
is independent of
t
here since we assume that the solid is stationary.

Now consider a small perturbation of the steady state as
H=\overline{h}+\varphi
where
|\varphi|\ll 1
.
First of all, existence of a unique solution of (
1.4
), for some small time
T_{0}
, in the periodic setting can be obtained as in Theorem
3.5
. Considering spaces on bounded spatial interval
(\overline{\Lambda},L)
instead of
(0,\infty)
in Theorem
3.4
does not pose any extra difficulty.
Therefore,
\varphi
satisfies the same regularity as in Theorem
3.5
, for suitable initial data. Note that
\varphi
has vanishing mean due to the mass conservation property (
4.2
),

\langle\varphi\rangle:=\int\displaylimits_{\overline{\Lambda}}^{L}{\varphi\;\mathrm{d}\overline{x}}=0.

(4.11)

Inserting the above expression of
H
, the perturbation
\varphi
satisfies,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\varphi+\left(\tfrac{L-\overline{\Lambda}}{L-\Lambda}\right)^{4}\partial_{\overline{x}}((\overline{h}+\varphi)^{3}\partial^{3}_{\overline{x}}\varphi)=\dot{\Lambda}\tfrac{(L-\overline{x})}{(L-\Lambda)}(\partial_{\overline{x}}\overline{h}+\partial_{\overline{x}}\varphi)

\displaystyle\text{ in }\ \overline{x}\in(\overline{\Lambda},L),

(4.12)

\displaystyle\varphi=\psi_{1},\qquad\partial_{\overline{x}}\varphi=\psi_{2},\qquad\partial_{\overline{x}}^{3}\varphi=0

\displaystyle\text{ at }\ \overline{x}=\overline{\Lambda},

\displaystyle\partial_{\overline{x}}\varphi=0

\displaystyle\text{ at }\ \overline{x}=L,

\displaystyle\varphi=\tilde{h}_{0}

\displaystyle\text{ at }\ t=0,

where
\tilde{h}_{0}(\overline{x})\equiv h_{0}(x)
and

\psi_{1}(t)\equiv g(\Lambda)-g(\overline{\Lambda}),\qquad\psi_{2}(t)\equiv\left(\tfrac{L-\Lambda}{L-\overline{\Lambda}}\right)(\partial_{x}g(\Lambda)-k)-(\partial_{x}g(\overline{\Lambda})-k).

Furthermore, with help of the energy dissipation relation for the equilibrium solution,

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}E|_{(\overline{h},\overline{\Lambda})}=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\bigg[a\int\displaylimits_{0}^{\overline{\Lambda}}{|\partial_{\overline{x}}g|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}\overline{x}}+b\int\displaylimits_{\overline{\Lambda}}^{L}{|\partial_{\overline{x}}\overline{h}|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}\overline{x}}+c\int\displaylimits_{\overline{\Lambda}}^{L}{|\partial_{\overline{x}}g|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}\overline{x}}\bigg]=0,

the energy equation (
4.10
) results into, (plugging in the change of variable
\overline{x}
and the expression of
H
)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}E_{\varphi}(t)

\displaystyle:=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\bigg[b\int\displaylimits_{\overline{\Lambda}}^{L}{|\partial_{\overline{x}}\varphi|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}\overline{x}+\frac{{\widetilde{\Lambda}}^{2}}{(L-\overline{\Lambda})}(|\partial_{\overline{x}}g|^{2}|_{\overline{x}=\overline{\Lambda}}-k^{2})+O({\widetilde{\Lambda}}^{3})}\bigg]

(4.13)

\displaystyle=-2b(1+O(\widetilde{\Lambda}))\int\displaylimits_{\overline{\Lambda}}^{L}{(\overline{h}+\varphi)^{3}|\partial_{\overline{x}}^{3}\varphi|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}\overline{x}},

where
\widetilde{\Lambda}\equiv\widetilde{\Lambda}(t):=(\Lambda(t)-\overline{\Lambda})
. Note that in the above expression,
(1+O(\widetilde{\Lambda}))>0
which is important for subsequent analysis.

Next let us prove an priori estimate which is essential to prove the stability result.
The ideas used here, in particular in Lemma
4.3
and Corollary
4.5
, are similar to that of
[
17
, Section 4, 5]
.

Lemma 4.3

Any function
\varphi\in H^{3}(\overline{\Lambda},L)
, satisfying the boundary conditions
\partial_{x}\varphi=\frac{(k_{1}-k_{2})}{k_{1}(L-\overline{\Lambda})}\varphi
at
x=\overline{\Lambda}
and
\partial_{x}\varphi=0
at
x=L
where
k_{1},k_{2}
are some non-zero constants, and with zero mean value
\langle\varphi\rangle=0
,
satisfies the following estimates,

\int\displaylimits_{\overline{\Lambda}}^{L}{|\partial_{x}\varphi|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x}\leq C\int\displaylimits_{\overline{\Lambda}}^{L}{|\partial_{x}^{3}\varphi|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x},

(4.14)

and

|\varphi(\overline{\Lambda})|^{2}\leq C\int\displaylimits_{\overline{\Lambda}}^{L}{|\partial_{x}^{3}\varphi|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x}.

(4.15)

Remark 4.4

Note that the boundary condition
\partial_{x}\varphi=\frac{(k_{1}-k_{2})}{k_{1}(L-\overline{\Lambda})}\varphi
at
x=\overline{\Lambda}
is crucial for estimate (
4.14
) to hold. It is possible to construct a function (e.g. a quadratic polynomial) with zero mean value and symmetric with respect to
x=L
whose first derivative does not vanish, that is the right hand side of (
4.14
) vanishes while not the left hand side. On the other hand, the only function which satisfies all the conditions stated in the above lemma is the null function.

Proof.
(i) Let us first note that since
\varphi\in H^{1}(\overline{\Lambda},L)
, it is absolutely continuous, in particular, it holds that

|\varphi(\overline{\Lambda})|^{2}\leq C\int\displaylimits_{\overline{\Lambda}}^{L}{|\partial_{x}\varphi|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x}.

Thus, (
4.15
) is a consequence of (
4.14
).

(ii) We prove the inequality (
4.14
) by a contradiction argument. Suppose that for each
m\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists
\varphi_{m}\in H^{3}(\overline{\Lambda},L)
with the conditions

\partial_{x}\varphi_{m}|_{x=\overline{\Lambda}}=\frac{\lambda}{2k_{2}b}\varphi_{m}\big|_{x=\overline{\Lambda}},\qquad\partial_{x}\varphi_{m}|_{x=L}=0,\quad\text{ and }\quad\langle\varphi_{m}\rangle=0,

such that

\int\displaylimits_{\overline{\Lambda}}^{L}{|\partial_{x}\varphi_{m}|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x}\geq m\int\displaylimits_{\overline{\Lambda}}^{L}{|\partial_{x}^{3}\varphi_{m}|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x}.

(4.16)

Further we may renormalize the sequence as
\|\partial_{x}\varphi_{m}\|_{L^{2}(\overline{\Lambda},L)}=1
.

Relation (
4.16
) shows that
\{\varphi_{m}\}
is a bounded sequence in
H^{3}(\overline{\Lambda},L)
. In fact,
\partial_{x}^{3}\varphi_{m}\to 0
in
L^{2}(\overline{\Lambda},L)
. Thus, there exists a subsequence, still denoted by
\{\varphi_{m}\}
, and a function
\varphi
such that
\varphi_{m}\rightharpoonup\varphi
in
H^{3}(\overline{\Lambda},L)
. Due to the compactness of
H^{3}(\overline{\Lambda},L)\hookrightarrow H^{2}(\overline{\Lambda},L)
, one further has that
\varphi_{m}\to\varphi
strongly in
H^{2}(\overline{\Lambda},L)
. Therefore,
\varphi
satisfies,

\partial_{x}\varphi|_{x=\overline{\Lambda}}=\frac{\lambda}{2k_{2}b}\varphi\big|_{x=\overline{\Lambda}},\qquad\partial_{x}\varphi|_{x=L}=0,\quad\quad\langle\varphi\rangle=0,

(4.17)

and

\|\partial_{x}\varphi_{m}\|_{L^{2}(\overline{\Lambda},L)}=1.

(4.18)

On the other hand, one has from (
4.16
) that
\partial_{x}^{3}\varphi=0
in
L^{2}(\overline{\Lambda},L)
. This implies, together with the boundary conditions and the vanishing mean (
4.17
), that
\varphi\equiv 0
in
(\overline{\Lambda},L)
which is a contradiction to (
4.18
).

Now we can establish the asymptotic stability of the energy corresponding to (
1.4
).

Corollary 4.5

Let
(\overline{h},\overline{\Lambda})
be a steady state of (
1.4
), given by (
4.8
).
There exists
\varepsilon>0
such that for any initial data
\tilde{h}_{0}\in C^{4+\alpha}(\overline{\Lambda},L),\alpha\in(0,1)
and
\Lambda_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
with
|\Lambda_{0}-\overline{\Lambda}|+\|\tilde{h}_{0}-\overline{h}\|_{C^{1}(\overline{\Lambda},L)}\leq\varepsilon
, a solution
(h,\Lambda)
of (
1.4
) satisfies, for
t\in(0,T_{0})
,

|\Lambda-\overline{\Lambda}|\leq Ce^{-\omega t}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\|H-\overline{h}\|_{H^{1}(\overline{\Lambda},L)}\leq Ce^{-\omega t}\quad\text{ for some }\omega>0.

(4.19)

Proof.
The right hand side of (
4.13
) can be estimated, since
|\varphi|\ll 1
, as

2b(1+O(\widetilde{\Lambda}))\int\displaylimits_{\overline{\Lambda}}^{L}{(\overline{h}+\varphi)^{3}|\partial_{\overline{x}}^{3}\varphi|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}\overline{x}}\geq C\int\displaylimits_{\overline{\Lambda}}^{L}{\left(\frac{\overline{h}}{2}\right)^{3}|\partial_{\overline{x}}^{3}\varphi|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}\overline{x}}\geq C\;\min_{\overline{x}}\overline{h}\;\int\displaylimits_{\overline{\Lambda}}^{L}{|\partial_{\overline{x}}^{3}\varphi|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}\overline{x}}.

Recall that in the above expression,
(1+O(\widetilde{\Lambda}))>0
as obtained in the deduction of (
4.13
). Combining with the estimates in Lemma
4.3
, we get that

2b(1+O(\widetilde{\Lambda}))\int\displaylimits_{\overline{\Lambda}}^{L}{(\overline{h}+\varphi)^{3}|\partial_{\overline{x}}^{3}\varphi|^{2}\mathrm{d}\overline{x}}\geq CE_{\varphi}(t),

(4.20)

where the energy for the perturbation
E_{\varphi}
is defined in (
4.13
). Therefore (
4.20
) yields that

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}E_{\varphi}\leq-CE_{\varphi}\qquad\text{ for }\quad 0\leq t\leq T_{0}

which finally gives,

E_{\varphi}(t)\leq\exp(-Ct)E_{\varphi}(0)\qquad\text{ for }\quad 0\leq t\leq T_{0}.

In particular, one gets for the full energy defined by (
4.4
),

E(t)\leq\exp(-Ct)E(0)\qquad\text{ for }\quad 0\leq t\leq T^{*}.

This completes the proof.

Next, to obtain the global in time existence result, we follow the path as in Section
3.3
. To begin with, a suitable estimate on the fundamental solution for the linear problem corresponding to (
4.12
) can be obtained as before. Here onwards, the bar over
x
is omitted.

Lemma 4.6

Let
\tilde{h}_{0}\in C^{4+\alpha}(\overline{\Lambda},L)
for
\alpha\in(0,1)
. There exists a fundamental solution
G(x,t,\xi,\tau)
of the linear problem

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\varphi+\left(\tfrac{L-\overline{\Lambda}}{L-\Lambda}\right)^{4}\partial_{x}(\overline{h}^{3}\partial^{3}_{x}\varphi)-\dot{\Lambda}\tfrac{(L-x)}{(L-\Lambda)}\partial_{x}\varphi=0

\displaystyle\text{ in }\ x\in(\overline{\Lambda},L),t>0,

(4.21)

\displaystyle\partial_{x}\varphi=\psi_{2},\qquad\partial_{x}^{3}\varphi=0

\displaystyle\text{ at }\ x=\overline{\Lambda},t>0,

\displaystyle\partial_{x}\varphi=0

\displaystyle\text{ at }\ x=L,t>0,

\displaystyle\varphi=\tilde{h}_{0}

\displaystyle\text{ at }\ t=0,x\in(\overline{\Lambda},L),

satisfying the estimate

|\partial^{m}G(x,t,\xi,\tau)|\leq c_{m}(t-\tau)^{-\frac{m+1}{4}}\exp\left\{-c\left(\tfrac{|x-y|}{(t-\tau)^{1/4}}\right)^{4/3}\right\},\quad t\in[0,T].

(4.22)

Proof.
The result follows from
[
7
, Chapter IV.2, Theorem 3.4]
, as in Lemma
3.2
.
As per the notations in
[
7
]
, for our system (
4.21
),
N=1=n,b=2,r_{1}=1,r_{2}=3
and the boundary conditions read as
B\equiv(\partial_{x},\partial_{x}^{3})u|_{x=\overline{\Lambda}}=f\equiv(\psi_{2},0)
. In
(\ref{36})_{1}
, the leading order coefficient
a_{4}(x,t)=\left(\frac{L-\overline{\Lambda}}{L-\Lambda}\right)^{4}\overline{h}^{3}
is Hölder continuous in both
t\in(0,T_{0})
and
x\in(0,L)
, since Theorem
3.5
gives that
\Lambda\in C^{1+\frac{\alpha}{4}}[0,T_{0}]
and
\overline{h}
is smooth. Furthermore, the lower order coefficients
a_{3}(x,t)=\left(\frac{L-\overline{\Lambda}}{L-\Lambda}\right)^{4}\partial_{x}(\overline{h}^{3})
and
a_{1}(x,t)=\dot{\Lambda}\left(\frac{L-x}{L-\Lambda}\right)
are Hölder continuous in
x
as well. Hence, all conditions of
[
7
, Chapter IV.2, Theorem 3.4]
are satisfied.

Theorem 4.7

Let
(\overline{h},\overline{\Lambda})
be a steady state of (
1.4
) and
\tilde{h}_{0}\in C^{4+\alpha}(\overline{\Lambda},L),\alpha\in(0,1)
and
\Lambda_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
. There exists
\varepsilon>0
such that for any initial data satisfying
|\Lambda_{0}-\overline{\Lambda}|+\|\tilde{h}_{0}-\overline{h}\|_{C^{4+\alpha}(\overline{\Lambda},L)}\leq\varepsilon
, a unique global solution
(h,\Lambda)
of the problem (
1.4
) exists, with regularity given by Theorem
3.5
, and satisfies, for all
t>0
,

|\Lambda-\overline{\Lambda}|\leq Ce^{-\omega t}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\|H-\overline{h}\|_{C^{4+\alpha}(\overline{\Lambda},L)}\leq Ce^{-\omega t}\quad\text{ for some }\omega>0.

Proof.
Theorem
3.5
provides existence of
\varphi
as a unique solution of the quasilinear problem

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\varphi+\left(\tfrac{L-\overline{\Lambda}}{L-\Lambda}\right)^{4}\partial_{x}((\overline{h}+\varphi)^{3}\partial^{3}_{x}\varphi)=\dot{\Lambda}\tfrac{(L-x)}{(L-\Lambda)}(\partial_{x}\overline{h}+\partial_{x}\varphi)

\displaystyle\text{ in }\ x\in(\overline{\Lambda},L),t>0,

(4.23)

\displaystyle\partial_{x}\varphi=\psi_{2},\quad\partial_{x}^{3}\varphi=0

\displaystyle\text{ at }\ x=\overline{\Lambda},t>0,

\displaystyle\partial_{x}\varphi=0

\displaystyle\text{ at }\ x=L,t>0,

\displaystyle\varphi=\tilde{h}_{0}

\displaystyle\text{ at }\ t=0,x\in(\overline{\Lambda},L),

in some interval
[0,T_{0}]
, which in addition matches the boundary condition
\varphi|_{x=\overline{\Lambda}}=\psi_{1}
, and is given by the following integro-differential form,

\varphi(x,t)=\int\displaylimits_{0}^{t}\int\displaylimits_{\overline{\Lambda}}^{L}G(x,t,y,\tau)\left[F_{1}+F_{2}\right](y,\tau)\;\mathrm{d}y\;\mathrm{d}\tau.

(4.24)

Here
G
is the fundamental solution of the linear problem given in Lemma
4.6
and

F_{1}=\left(\tfrac{L-\overline{\Lambda}}{L-\Lambda}\right)^{4}\partial_{x}((\varphi^{3}+3\overline{h}\;\varphi\;(\overline{h}+\varphi))\;\partial_{x}^{3}\varphi),\qquad F_{2}=\dot{\Lambda}\;\tfrac{(L-x)}{(L-\Lambda)}\;\partial_{x}\overline{h}.

Since the assumptions of the local existence result (eg.
[
7
, Chapter III.4, Theorem 8.3]
) are satisfied, there exists a unique solution
\varphi_{1}
of the Cauchy problem
\varphi_{1}|_{t=T_{0}}=\varphi(x,T_{0})
for equation (
4.23
), which is defined for
t\in(T_{0},T_{1})
where
T_{1}>T_{0}
and belongs to
C^{4+\alpha}(\overline{\Lambda},L)
. In turn, also
\Lambda\in C^{1+\frac{\alpha}{4}}[T_{0},T_{1}]
. The solution can be continued in this way.

Now observe that,

\displaystyle I

\displaystyle=\bigg|\int\displaylimits_{0}^{t}\int\displaylimits_{\overline{\Lambda}}^{L}G(x,t,y,\tau)F_{1}(y,\tau)\;\mathrm{d}y\;\mathrm{d}\tau\bigg|

\displaystyle=\bigg|\int\displaylimits_{0}^{t}{\left(\tfrac{L-\overline{\Lambda}}{L-\Lambda}\right)^{4}(\tau)}\int\displaylimits_{\overline{\Lambda}}^{L}G(x,t,y,\tau)\partial_{x}((\varphi^{3}+3\overline{h}\;\varphi\;(\overline{h}+\varphi))\;\partial_{x}^{3}\varphi)(y,\tau)\;\mathrm{d}y\;\mathrm{d}\tau\bigg|

\displaystyle=\bigg|\int\displaylimits_{0}^{t}{\left(\tfrac{L-\overline{\Lambda}}{L-\Lambda}\right)^{4}(\tau)}\int\displaylimits_{\overline{\Lambda}}^{L}\partial_{x}G(x,t,y,\tau)(\varphi^{3}+3\overline{h}\;\varphi\;(\overline{h}+\varphi))\;\partial_{x}^{3}\varphi(y,\tau)\;\mathrm{d}y\;\mathrm{d}\tau\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq c(\|\varphi\|_{C((0,t),C^{\frac{1}{2}}(\overline{\Lambda},L))})\int\displaylimits_{0}^{t}{\left(\tfrac{L-\overline{\Lambda}}{L-\Lambda}\right)^{4}(\tau)}\int\displaylimits_{\overline{\Lambda}}^{L}|\partial_{x}G(x,t,y,\tau)|\;\mathrm{d}y\;\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle\leq c(\|\varphi\|_{C((0,t),C^{\frac{1}{2}}(\overline{\Lambda},L))})\int\displaylimits_{0}^{t}{\left(\tfrac{L-\overline{\Lambda}}{L-\Lambda}\right)^{4}(\tau)(t-\tau)^{-\frac{1}{4}}\;\mathrm{d}\tau}.

In the above we used integration by parts to pass all the derivatives over
G
and then use estimate (
4.22
) for
m=1
and the regularity of
\overline{h}
and
\varphi
. Similarly, we have

\displaystyle II

\displaystyle=\bigg|\int\displaylimits_{0}^{t}\int\displaylimits_{\overline{\Lambda}}^{L}G(x,t,y,\tau)F_{2}(y,\tau)\;\mathrm{d}y\;\mathrm{d}\tau\bigg|

\displaystyle=\bigg|\int\displaylimits_{0}^{t}{\tfrac{\dot{\Lambda}}{(L-\Lambda)}(\tau)}\int\displaylimits_{\overline{\Lambda}}^{L}G(x,t,y,\tau)\;(L-y)\;\partial_{x}\overline{h}(y)\;\mathrm{d}y\;\mathrm{d}\tau\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq c\int\displaylimits_{0}^{t}{\left|\dot{\Lambda}\tfrac{(L-\overline{\Lambda})}{(L-\Lambda)}(\tau)\right|}\int\displaylimits_{\overline{\Lambda}}^{L}|G(x,t,y,\tau)|\;\mathrm{d}y\;\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle\leq c\int\displaylimits_{0}^{t}{|\dot{\Lambda}(\tau)|\tfrac{(L-\overline{\Lambda})}{(L-\Lambda)}\;\mathrm{d}\tau}\leq c\int\displaylimits_{0}^{t}{|\dot{\Lambda}(\tau)|(1+\tfrac{\widetilde{\Lambda}}{(L-\overline{\Lambda})}+O({\widetilde{\Lambda}}^{2}))\;\mathrm{d}\tau}.

Therefore, from expression (
4.24
), we can conclude

\|\varphi\|_{C^{4+\alpha}(\overline{\Lambda},L)}\leq C\left(|\widetilde{\Lambda}(t)|+\|\varphi\|_{C^{\frac{1}{2}}(\overline{\Lambda},L)}\right)\leq C\left(|\Lambda-\overline{\Lambda}|+\|\varphi\|_{H^{1}(\overline{\Lambda},L)}\right).

Thus, the local solution can be continued up to time
T=\infty
in the solution space, as long as the initial data stays close enough. This concludes the proof.
