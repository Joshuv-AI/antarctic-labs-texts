The first uniformly finite homology group with coefficients in ℤ {\mathbb{Z}} and a characterisation of its vanishing in the transitive case
Rémi Bottinelli, Tom Kaiser

Abstract
We study the first uniformly finite homology group of Block and Weinberger for uniformly locally finite graphs, with coefficients in ℤ \mathbb{Z} and ℤ 2 \mathbb{Z}_{2} . When the graph is a tree, or coefficients are in ℤ 2 \mathbb{Z}_{2} , a characterisation of the group is obtained. In the general case, we describe three phenomena that entail non-vanishing of the group; their disjunction is shown to also be necessary for non-vanishing in the case of transitive graphs.



1
Introduction

Uniformly finite homology, introduced in
[
1
]
, is a coarse invariant of well-behaved metric spaces.
In
[
1
]
, it is shown that vanishing of zeroth uniformly finite homology with coefficients in
{\mathbb{Z}}
or
{\mathbb{R}}
is equivalent to expansion (non-amenability) of the space at hand.
Higher uniformly finite homology groups don’t enjoy, as far as we know, such clear cut descriptions.
Partial results have been obtained, e.g. in
[
2
,
3
]
.

In these notes, we focus on the first homology group, with coefficients in
{\mathbb{Z}}
of (tame enough) graphs:
H^{\mathrm{uf}}_{1}({\cdot},{\mathbb{Z}})
.
Informally, three relatively independent phenomena are responsible for the appearance of homology classes in dimension one:

1.

Graph theoretical ends.

2.

The existence of circuits of arbitrarily large girth.
More precisely, the non-existence of a uniform constant
R
such that any circuit can be written as a (possibly infinite) sum of circuits of length
{\leq}R
.

3.

A lack of
2
-simplices in the clique complex.
This should be understood quantitatively, and likened to amenability.

After recalling the necessary definitions in
Section

2
, we proceed in four parts.

In
Section

3
, we study the first of the above phenomena.
Starting with a classification of the (first) homology of trees (
Theorem

3.6
) in terms of their ends, we then show that the homology of an arbitrary graph is bounded from below by that of an embedded tree that “realises the ends” (
Proposition

3.11
).

In
Section

4
, motivated by the observation that there is a surjection:

H^{\mathrm{uf}}_{1}({\cdot},{\mathbb{Z}})\rightarrow H^{\mathrm{uf}}_{1}({\cdot},{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}),

we study
H^{\mathrm{uf}}_{1}({\cdot},{\mathbb{Z}}_{2})
(note that the “uniformly finite” condition becomes vacuous in
{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
— this may sound like a bad omen, but turns out to clarify a lot of constructions).
In particular, we get, in
Proposition

4.2
, a full description of
H^{\mathrm{uf}}_{1}({\cdot},{\mathbb{Z}}_{2})
as a direct sum of two factors corresponding to exactly the first and second phenomena, that is, ends and “large circuits” respectively.
Moreover, it is shown in
Theorem

4.8
that the existence of “large circuits” actually implies infinite-dimensionality of
H^{\mathrm{uf}}_{1}({\cdot},{\mathbb{Z}}_{2})
.

In
Section

5
, again in
\mathbb{Z}
, we make precise the claim that “large circuits” imply non-zero homology.
Since “large circuits” is a notion which depends on the coefficient rings, this result is
not
a consequence of the previous section.

In
Section

6
, inspired by a construction of
[
4
]
, we define a notion of “expansion” in higher dimension, which aims at capturing the third phenomenon.
It is easily verified (
Lemma

6.3
) that our “expansion” implies vanishing of homology.
As a (very partial) converse, we show (
Theorem

6.6
) that in dimension one, and for vertex transitive graphs, vanishing implies “expansion”.

Finally, refining the notion of expansion, we reach, for (infinite) transitive graphs (or more generally, infinite graphs with a cocompact action by their automorphism group), a characterisation of the non-vanishing of homology in terms of the three phenomena of (the refined) non-expansion, ends, and large circuits.

We follow the conventions of

If

Uniformly Contractible

\forall r>0\ \exists s(r)>0

2.2

If

\mathbb{R}
,

where

-simplices of

.
and the boundary map:

\displaystyle\partial:C^{\mathrm{suf}}_{n+1}(X)

\displaystyle\rightarrow C^{\mathrm{suf}}_{n}(X)

\displaystyle f

\displaystyle\mapsto[\partial f:\tau\mapsto\sum_{\tau<\sigma}i(\tau:\sigma)f(\sigma)],

where

takes care of alternating signs, after choosing an orientation for

.

Then, the

of

is defined as

H^{\mathrm{suf}}_{*}(X)=H(C^{\mathrm{suf}}_{*}(X)).

In the following, we will use

2.4
“Hands-on” definition of
H^{\mathrm{uf}}_{1}

The definition of uniformly finite homology is rather abstract; the goal in this section is to find more concrete description of
H^{\mathrm{uf}}_{1}
.

Let
X
be a ULF graph, and
A
either
\mathbb{Z}
or
{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
.

An element of
Z^{\mathrm{suf}}_{1}(X,A)
can be seen as a (uniformly bounded)
flow
on
X
; that is, each (directed) edge takes a certain value, which we view as flowing through the edge.
The condition of being a homological cycle states that at any vertex, the sum of flows of edges directed to the vertex is the same as the flow of edges directed out of the vertex (“in=out”).
The two building blocks for such flows are circuits and bi-infinite paths (which we will call “bips”):
Indeed, any oriented, graph theoretical circuit in
X
defines an element of
Z^{\mathrm{suf}}_{1}(X,A)
by taking the sum of its (oriented) edges.
Similarly, a bi-infinite path (that is, morphism of graphs from
\mathbb{Z}
to
X
, injective on edges for convenience) also defines an element of
Z^{\mathrm{suf}}_{1}(X,A)
.
Now, for
A
either
\mathbb{Z}
or
{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
, any element of
f\in Z^{\mathrm{suf}}_{1}(X,A)
can be decomposed into a sum of circuits and bips by “following paths”, in such a way that there is a uniform bound on the number of circuits/bips passing over any given edge (Take a maximal multiset of circuits “below” f and subtract it; Take a maximal multiset of bips “below” the result and subtract it; Nothing remains).
From now on, we will not distinguish circuits and bips as paths in
X
from their representatives in homology.

If a (possibly infinite) family
(c_{i})_{i\in I}
of circuits is given, along with coefficients
({\lambda}_{i}\in A)_{i\in I}
, then we say they define a
thin
sum if the family of circuits is locally finite (see
[
6
]
for a motivation of “thinness”).

We can then define the following objects:

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{r}(X,A)

\displaystyle:=\text{``sums of circuits of length $\leq r$ with uniformly bounded coefficients in $A$''}

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\infty}(X,A)

\displaystyle:=\cup_{r}\mathcal{C}_{r}(X,A)

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(X,A)

\displaystyle:=\text{``thin sums of circuits with coefficients in $A$ such that the result is uniformly bounded''}

It is easy to see that all of those objects are actually vector subspaces of
Z^{\mathrm{suf}}_{1}(X,A)
.
Note that in the case
A=\mathbb{Z}_{2}
,
\mathcal{C}_{r}
can be described simply as the space of sums of circuits of length
\leq r
, and
\mathcal{C}
as the space of thin sums of circuits.

Note finally that, letting
\mathfrak{C}_{r}
stand for the set of circuits of length
\leq r
, and

\mathfrak{e}:l^{\infty}(\mathfrak{C}_{r})\rightarrow l^{\infty}(EX)=C^{\mathrm{suf}}_{1}(X),

extended linearly from
c\mapsto\text{“its edges”}
, we have
\mathcal{C}_{r}=\mathfrak{e}\left(l^{\infty}(\mathfrak{C}_{r})\right)
, and
\mathfrak{e}
is continuous w.r.t. the pointwise topologies.

We start with a description of
\mathcal{C}_{\infty}
in terms of our chain complexes.

Lemma 2.3
.

If
X
is a ULF graph, then, for any
r>0
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{r}(X,A)

\displaystyle\subseteq Z^{\mathrm{suf}}_{1}(X,A)\cap B^{\mathrm{suf}}_{1}(R_{r/2+1}X,A)

\displaystyle Z^{\mathrm{suf}}_{1}(X,A)\cap B^{\mathrm{suf}}_{1}(R_{r}X,A)

\displaystyle\subseteq\mathcal{C}_{3r}(X,A).

Consequently:

\mathcal{C}_{\infty}(X,A)=Z^{\mathrm{suf}}_{1}(X,A)\cap B^{\mathrm{uf}}_{1}(X,A).

Proof.

Drop the
A
s.

If
f\in\mathcal{C}_{r}(X)
,
f=\mathfrak{e}\phi
for some
\phi:\mathfrak{C}_{r}\rightarrow A
uniformly bounded.
Obviously,
f\in Z^{\mathrm{suf}}_{1}(X)
.
For any
c\in\supp\phi
, one can “triangulate”
c
: that is, if
c
is the circuit
(v_{1},…,v_{s})
with
s\leq r
, we can consider the sum

\Delta c:=\sum_{i=1}^{s}(v_{1},v_{i},v_{i+1})\in C^{\mathrm{suf}}_{2}(R_{2r+1}X),

with addition
\mod s
.
One sees that
\partial(\Delta c)=\mathfrak{e}c
, and letting

\Delta\phi:=\sum_{c}g(c)\cdot(\Delta c),

we get
\Delta\phi\in C^{\mathrm{suf}}_{2}(R_{r/2+1}X)
by ULF, and
\partial(\Delta\phi)=\mathfrak{e}\phi=f
.
Thus,
f\in B^{\mathrm{suf}}_{2}(R_{r/2+1}X)
.

Conversely, assume
f\in Z^{\mathrm{suf}}_{1}(X)\cap B^{\mathrm{suf}}_{1}(R_{r}X)
.
Let
g\in C^{\mathrm{suf}}_{2}(R_{r}X)
such that
\partial g=f
.

For any two vertices
u,v
at distance
\leq r
, fix a shortest path
p_{u,v}=(u=u_{0},u_{1},…u_{s}=v)
from
u
to
v
.
If
t:=(u,v,w)
is a triangle in
R_{r}X
, consider the circuit
Ot
obtained by concatenating the paths
p_{u,v},p_{v,w},p_{w,u}
.
By construction,
Ot\in\mathfrak{C}_{3r}
.
Let also

Og:=\sum_{t\in(R_{r}X)_{(3)}}g(t)\cdot(Ot)

Then
Og\in l^{\infty}(\mathfrak{C}_{3r})
, again by ULF, and by our assumption that
\partial g\in Z^{\mathrm{suf}}_{1}(X)
, we get:

\mathfrak{e}(Og)=\partial g.

It follows that
f=\mathfrak{e}(Og)\in\mathcal{C}_{3r}
.

The last equality of the lemma follows simply by taking the directed unions.

∎

We can now describe
H^{\mathrm{uf}}_{1}(X)
in a way that only deals with circuits and paths, and does not deal with Rips complexes:

Proposition 2.4
.

If
X
is a ULF graph, then, for any
r>0
, the composite homomorphism:

{\Phi}_{r}:Z^{\mathrm{suf}}_{1}(X,A)\hookrightarrow Z^{\mathrm{suf}}_{1}(R_{r}X,A)\twoheadrightarrow H^{\mathrm{suf}}_{1}(R_{r}X,A)

is surjective and its kernel satisfies:

\mathcal{C}_{2r}\leq\ker{\Phi}_{r}\leq\mathcal{C}_{3r}.

Consequently, the composite homomorphism

{\Phi}_{\infty}:Z^{\mathrm{suf}}_{1}(X,A)\hookrightarrow Z^{\mathrm{uf}}_{1}(X,A)\twoheadrightarrow H^{\mathrm{uf}}_{1}(X,A)

is surjective and has kernel

\ker{\Phi}_{\infty}=\mathcal{C}_{\infty}(X,A).

In particular:

H^{\mathrm{uf}}_{1}(X,A)\cong\frac{Z^{\mathrm{suf}}_{1}(X,A)}{\mathcal{C}_{\infty}(X,A)}.

Proof.

Drop the
A
s.

Fix
r>0
.
We first verify surjectivity of
{\Phi}_{r}
.
Fix any
f\in Z^{\mathrm{suf}}_{1}(R_{r}X)
.
Choose for any two
u,v
in
X
, a unique shortest path
p_{u,v}=(u=u_{0},u_{1},…,u_{s}=v)
from
u
to
v
, and let:

\tilde{f}:=\sum_{(u,v)\in(R_{r}X)_{(2)}}f(u,v)\cdot p_{u,v},

that is,
\tilde{f}
is obtained by replacing “virtual edges” (i.e. edges in
(R_{r}X)_{(2)}
) by paths.
By ULF,
\tilde{f}\in C^{\mathrm{suf}}_{1}(X)
and
\partial\tilde{f}=\partial f
, so that
\partial\tilde{f}=0
, hence
\tilde{f}\in Z^{\mathrm{suf}}_{1}(X)
.
We claim that
\tilde{f}-f\in B^{\mathrm{suf}}_{1}(R_{r}X)
, which will clearly imply surjectivity of
{\Phi}_{r}
.
Indeed, if for any
(u,v)
at distance
\leq r
, one considers:

\Delta(u,v):=\sum_{i=1}^{s-1}(u_{0},u_{i},u_{i+1})\in C^{\mathrm{suf}}_{2}(R_{r}X)

where
(u=u_{0},u_{1},…,u_{s}=v)
is a path from
u
to
v
, then
\partial\Delta(u,v)=(u,v)-p_{u,v}
.
Summing over the virtual edges
(u,v)
in the support of
f
, we get:

\partial\left(\sum_{(u,v)\in(R_{r}X)_{(1)}}f(u,v)\cdot\Delta(u,v)\right)=f-\tilde{f}.

This shows that, indeed,
{\Phi}_{r}
is surjective, assuming the sum in the parentheses is in
C^{\mathrm{suf}}_{2}(R_{r}X)
which holds, as usual, by ULF.
Now, the inclusions related to
\ker{\Phi}_{r}
remain, but these follow at once from the first part of
Lemma

2.3
.

The properties of
{\Phi}_{\infty}
follow from the second part of
Lemma

2.3
and the fact that:

H^{\mathrm{uf}}_{1}(X)=\frac{\bigcup_{r}Z^{\mathrm{suf}}_{1}(R_{r}X)}{\bigcup_{r}B^{\mathrm{suf}}_{1}(R_{r}X)}.

∎

Corollary 2.5
.

Let
X
a ULF graph and assume that
\mathcal{C}_{r}(X,A)=\mathcal{C}_{\infty}(X,A)

.
Then, for any

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\frac{Z^{\mathrm{suf}}_{1}(X,A)}{\mathcal{C}_{r}(X,A)}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H^{\mathrm{suf}}_{1}(R_{s}X,A)}

From now on, by a trivalent tree, we mean a tree with vertices of degree either

We first verify that in terms of uniformly finite homology, leaves (that is, vertices of degree

Lemma 3.1
.

Take

H^{\mathrm{suf}}_{1}(T,A)=H^{\mathrm{suf}}_{1}(T^{\prime},A)

3.1
Homology of Trees

We want to better describe the first uniformly finite homology of trees.
Recall that we essentially have two kinds of flows on a graph
X
: circuits and bips.
In case
X

The “building blocks” for a flow on

We will extract from

a specific set

.
The construction of

will actually be used.

In order to ease notation we consider the following construction of a trivalent tree (with no leaves).

•

Start with a single vertex
v_{0}
, and call that graph
T_{0}
.

•

Let

4.3
Large circuits and dimensions

Our goal in this section is to show that, intuitively, if a graph has circuits of increasing length that can not be decomposed into smaller ones, then
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-homology cannot vanish, and will in fact be infinite dimensional.

Recall that a collection of elements of
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{EX}
is said to be
thin
if it is locally finite.
The sum of the collection (taken pointwise) is then well-defined, and we already defined:

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}

\displaystyle:=\{\text{``thin sum of circuits''}\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{R}

\displaystyle:=\{\text{``thin sum of circuits of length $\leq R$''}\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\infty}

\displaystyle:=\cup_{R}\mathcal{C}_{R}.

The following are equivalent:

1.

There exists an
R>0
such that any circuit can be written as a thin-sum sum of circuits of length
\leq R
.

2.

\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}_{R}
.

3.

For all

,
\mathcal{C}_{R^{\prime}}=\mathcal{C}_{R}
.

If those do not hold, we say that

has

.

For the sake of clarity, we will settle on the following notation:

•

A thin-sum is a well-defined sum of a potentially infinite thin collection
A\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{EX}
.

•

There is an obvious notion of being closed under thin-sums, and a vector subspace of
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{EX}
is said to be a thin-sum space if it is closed under thin-sums.

•

The thin-sum-span of a collection
A
is the set:

\{\sum B\ |\ B\text{ a thin subset of }A\}.

By compactness, the thin-sum span of a collection is closed under thin-sums (see
[
6
]
).

•

A collection
A
is thin-sum-independent if no two distinct thin subcollections of
A
yield the same sum; equivalently, no thin subcollection has sum
0
.

•

A thin-sum basis is a collection that is thin-sum-independent and a thin-sum basis for a given thin-sum space.
Note that we do not require a thin-sum basis
A
for a thin-sum space
\mathcal{W}
to be thin itself, but if it is, we get a bijection

\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{A}\leftrightarrow\mathcal{W}.

With those clarifications in mind, we see that each
\mathcal{C}_{R}
is a thin-sum space, as is
\mathcal{C}
(as, respectively, the thin-sum span of circuits of length
\leq R
, and all circuits), but
\mathcal{C}_{\infty}
is not necessarily.
Finally, all the concepts above still make sense if we replace
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{EX}
by
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{W}
for any countable set
W
.

We will show that as soon as

has large circuits, then

\mathcal{C}
.

Well-order the set

in a way that respects the subsets

, i.e.

W=\{v_{\alpha}\ |\ \alpha<\kappa\},

with
\kappa
the cardinal of
W
and such that there exist increasing ordinals

with

V_{i}=\{v_{\alpha}\in W\ |\ \alpha<{\alpha}_{i+1}\}.

We show that we can construct
\mathcal{B}_{i}
(

) and

satisfying these requirements.
We define the “Gaussian elimination” map

\nu:\kappa{\times}\kappa{\times}\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{2}

inductively as follows:

\displaystyle{\nu}_{0,\alpha}

\displaystyle=v_{\alpha},

\displaystyle{\nu}_{\tau,\alpha}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\lim_{\sigma<\tau}{\nu}_{\sigma,\alpha}&\text{if $\tau$ is a limit ordinal},\\
{\nu}_{\alpha,\alpha}&\text{otherwise and $\tau>\alpha$},\\
{\nu}_{\tau-1,\alpha}-{\nu}_{\tau-1,\alpha}(l({\nu}_{\tau-1,\tau-1}))\cdot{\nu}_{\tau-1,\tau-1}&\text{otherwise and $\tau\leq\alpha$},\\
\end{cases}

(1)

where the limit is a pointwise limit (which means that at any coordinate
r
, the transfinite sequence, evaluated at that coordinate, is eventually constant).

From now on, we will write
{\nu}_{\alpha}
for the diagonal element
{\nu}_{\alpha,\alpha}
.

The interpretation of the map
\nu
is as follows.
An element of
W
is uniquely determined by a map
\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{2}
(a row).
Thus, the set
W
, once ordered, can be represented as a map
\kappa{\times}\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{2}
(a matrix).
Gaussian elimination, in the finite case, is an iterative process where one iterates over the rows of a matrix, each the “pivot”, while modifying the matrix at each step, hence a map
\kappa{\times}\kappa{\times}\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{2}
.
In short, the first coordinate represents the iteration/pivot, the second the element in question, and the third its value at a given base coordinate.

At time zero, the matrix has not been modified.
At time
\tau
, if
\tau
is a successor ordinal, we pivot around the element
{\nu}_{\tau-1,\tau-1}
. Note that if the row
\alpha
“lies before the pivot” or “is the pivot ” (i.e.
\alpha<\tau
), it is not changed.
If row
\alpha
“lies after the pivot” (
\alpha\geq\tau
), we must subtract the pivot to what row
\alpha
“just was”.
In case
\tau
is a limit ordinal, then we will just re-iterate all changes that happened before time
\tau
and restart pivoting in its successor.

We start by showing by induction on
\tau
that:

1.

For any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the number of ordinals
\sigma{\leq}\tau
such that

is finite.

2.

The limit in
Section

4.3
is actually well-defined.

3.

For any
\sigma<\tau,\alpha
,
{\nu}_{\tau,\alpha}(l({\nu}_{\sigma}))=0
.

4.

The value of
{\nu}_{\tau,\alpha}
is

{\nu}_{\tau,\alpha}={\nu}_{0,\alpha}+\sum_{\sigma<\min\{\alpha,\tau\}}{\nu}_{\sigma,\alpha}(l({\nu}_{\sigma}))\cdot{\nu}_{\sigma}.

(2)

Indeed, assume the above holds for any
\theta<\tau
.

If
\tau=0
:

Then
Items

1
,
2
,

and
4

If

Item

For

, we see that if

If

Finally,

. One sees that if

, and:

\displaystyle{\nu}_{\tau,\alpha}

\displaystyle={\nu}_{\tau-1,\alpha}+{\nu}_{\tau-1,\alpha}(l({\nu}_{\tau-1,\tau-1}))\cdot{\nu}_{\tau-1,\tau-1}

\displaystyle={\nu}_{0,\alpha}+\sum_{\sigma<\tau-1}{\nu}_{\sigma,\alpha}(l({\nu}_{\sigma}))\cdot{\nu}_{\sigma}+{\nu}_{\tau-1,\alpha}(l({\nu}_{\tau-1,\tau-1}))\cdot{\nu}_{\tau-1,\tau-1},

by definition of

. If
\tau>\alpha

\displaystyle{\nu}_{\tau,\alpha}={\nu}_{\tau-1,\alpha}

\displaystyle={\nu}_{0,\alpha}+\sum_{\sigma<\min\{\alpha,\tau-1\}}{\nu}_{\sigma,\alpha}(l({\nu}_{\sigma}))\cdot{\nu}_{\sigma}

\displaystyle={\nu}_{0,\alpha}+\sum_{\sigma<\min\{\alpha,\tau\}}{\nu}_{\sigma,\alpha}(l({\nu}_{\sigma}))\cdot{\nu}_{\sigma}

Now moving on to the limit case.

If
\tau
is limit:

We first check

1
.
It suffices to show that for any

If

for some

Assume then that there exists some

We now check

Theorem 4.8
.

If

has large circuits, then

Proof.

Let

Choose then, for each

We claim that the family

We can actually show more:

In

However, in

, only

Thus, the passage to a quotient involves a major loss of information. We suggest a very simple approach that remedies this problem in our ad hoc situation. It makes sense to define a thin quotient of thin vector spaces. There might be a more abstract setting about which one could say something.

Definition 4.9
.

Fix a thin-sum space
V\leq\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{X}
, and a sub-vector space
W\leq V
(not necessarily closed under thin-sum).
In the quotient
V/W
, we say that a potentially infinite sum of elements

is

iff there is a collection of preimages
\tilde{a}_{i}\in a_{i}
such that the collection
\{\tilde{a}_{i}\}_{i}
is thin (in particular, abusing notation, any

appears as at most a finite number of

), and all such thin choices of representatives result in the same element in

\sum\tilde{a}_{i}-\sum\tilde{b}_{i}\in W,

are two choices of representatives.

In order to have a cleaner theorem we also introduce the following “thin” sum.

Theorem 4.10
.

The following are equivalent:

1.

In
H^{\mathrm{uf}}_{1}(X,\mathbb{Z}_{2})
, the infinite sum
\{[0]\}_{i=0}^{\infty}
of the zero-class is consistent.

2.

X
has no large circuits.

3.

Any sum of cosets that has a thin representative sum is consistent.

Proof.

Clearly 3 implies 1.

We show 1 implies 2. Suppose
X
has large circuits, then there exists an infinite thin sum of large circuits that is non-zero in homology. However every individual circuit is finite and thus zero in homology. On the other hand the repeated infinite sum of
0
gives
0
. So we see that the infinite sum of the zero class has two representing thin sums that give a different solution. Hence it is inconsistent.

We show 2 implies 1. Consider an infinite representing thin sum
\sum\limits_{i=1}^{\infty}\tilde{x}_{i}
of the infinite zero class sum. Since there are no large circuits, there is some
R>0
such that each
\tilde{x}_{i}
can be written as a sum of
R
-circuits. More specifically there is a
g_{i}
such that
\partial g_{i}=\tilde{x}_{i}
. Now take some accumulation point
\lambda
of the sequence
\left\{\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}g_{i}\right\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
. Then
\partial\lambda=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{\infty}\tilde{x}_{i}
. The infinite zero-class sum is as such consistent.

We show 1 implies 3. Suppose there is some inconsistent thin sum. So two representing sums
\sum\limits_{i=1}^{\infty}\tilde{x}_{i}
and
\sum\limits_{i=1}^{\infty}\tilde{y}_{i}
, such that
[\tilde{x}_{i}]=[\tilde{y}_{i}]
. Then each
[\tilde{x}_{i}-\tilde{y}_{i}]=[0]
. Hence by assumption
\left[\sum\limits_{i=1}^{\infty}\tilde{x}_{i}-\tilde{y}_{i}\right]=[0]
. Which shows that
\left[\sum\limits_{i=1}^{\infty}\tilde{x}_{i}\right]=\left[\sum\limits_{i=1}^{\infty}\tilde{y}_{i}\right]
, because finite sums are always well defined.
∎

Corollary 4.11

If

Direct consequence of Theorem

.
∎

Theorem 4.12
.

•

\dim_{\text{thin}}(H^{\mathrm{uf}}_{1}(X,\mathbb{Z}_{2}))
is countable if and only if

has no large circuits. In this case it is equal to the number of ends

•

First part.
If there are no large circuits, then by

6
Expansion

In this section, we follow a different trail in our search for a description of vanishing
H^{\mathrm{uf}}_{1}(\cdot,\mathbb{Z})
.
Our motivation is twofold:
On the one hand, after playing a bit with
H^{\mathrm{uf}}_{1}(\cdot,\mathbb{Z})
, one comes to the conclusion that, ends and “large circuits” aside, the main ingredient in non-vanishing of homology is a lack of triangles, compared to edges.
The typical example here is (a triangulated)
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
: Any infinite sum of parallel bips going in one direction will define a non-zero class in homology, since there are not enough triangles to kill all bips at the same time.
On the other hand, (classical) graph expansion can be interpreted as the ability, given any finite subset of vertices
U
, and element
f\in Z^{\mathrm{suf}}_{0}(U)
, to push
f
out of
U
in a bounded way; that is, there exists some
g\in C^{\mathrm{suf}}_{1}(X)
with
\partial g|U=f
and
\|g\|\leq{\varepsilon}^{-1}\|f\|
, where
\varepsilon
is the Cheeger constant for
X
.
This section can then be described as an effort to try and mimic the “homological interpretation” of expansion in higher dimension (we call this
H^{\mathrm{suf}}_{n}
-expansion), and fine-tune it to isolate the phenomenon of “lack of triangles” from “ends” and “large circuits” (thus getting
pure

H^{\mathrm{suf}}_{n}
-expansion).

Recall that a (ULF) graph
X
is said to be
amenable
if it satisfies the following condition:

\forall\epsilon>0\ \exists U{\subseteq}_{f}VX\ :\ |\partial U|<\varepsilon|U|.

If
X
is non-amenable, the least
\varepsilon>0
satisfying
\forall U{\subseteq}_{f}VX
,
|\partial U|\geq{\varepsilon}|U|
is called the Cheeger constant for
X
.

[

]
:

Proposition 6.1

(Part of
[
1
, Theorem 3.1]
)
.

A ULF graph

6.1
Basics

Let
X
be a ULF simplicial complex.
If
U\subseteq VX
is a set of vertices, we also use
U
for the induced simplicial subcomplex.
We then write
\partial U
for the simplices of
X
adjacent to a vertex not in
U
.

Definition 6.2

(
H^{\mathrm{suf}}
-Expansion)
.

We say that
X
has
n
-dimensional
H^{\mathrm{suf}}
-expansion if:
There exists a function
K:\mathbb{N}{\rightarrow}\mathbb{N}
satisfying the following.
Fix any finite set of vertices
U{\subseteq}_{f}VX
, and any
f\in C^{\mathrm{suf}}_{n}(U,\mathbb{Z})
satisfying:

1.

\partial f
has support contained in
\partial U
.

2.

f
can be extended to some
\hat{f}\in Z^{\mathrm{suf}}_{n}(X,\mathbb{Z})
with
\|\hat{f}\|=\|f\|
(by extended, we mean
\hat{f}|U=f
).

Then: There exists
g\in C^{\mathrm{suf}}_{n+1}(X,\mathbb{Z})
such that
\partial g|_{U}=f
and
\|g\|\leq K(\|f\|)
.

We say that a a function
K
as above is a certificate to
H^{\mathrm{suf}}_{n}
-expansion.

Finally, if
Item

2
in the definition is replaced by

3.

f
can be extended to some
\hat{f}\in B^{\mathrm{suf}}_{n}(X,\mathbb{Z})
with
\|\hat{f}\|=\|f\|
.

and the rest is leaved as-is, then the obtained condition is called
pure

H^{\mathrm{suf}}_{n}
-expansion.

Intuitively, expansion can be interpreted as homological cycles locally being homological boundaries, with control.
In other words, one can always kill “pieces of cycles” with “pieces of boundaries” in a bounded way.
We will see that this property
always
implies that simplicial uniformly finite homology vanishes, at the corresponding dimension.
Pure expansion is slightly harder to describe, but the core idea is that it only takes into account those
f
that “can actually be killed” by some boundary.

Note that

\text{$H^{\mathrm{suf}}_{n}$-expansion}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\text{pure $H^{\mathrm{suf}}_{n}$-expansion}.

Lemma 6.3
.

If
X
has
H^{\mathrm{suf}}_{n}
-expansion, then
H^{\mathrm{suf}}_{n}(X,\mathbb{Z})=0
.

Proof.

Assume that
K
is a certificate.
Fix
f\in Z^{\mathrm{suf}}_{n}(X,\mathbb{Z})
, and
\langle U_{n}\subseteq VX{\rangle}_{n}
a nested sequence of finite subsets exhausting
VX
.
For each
n
, let
f_{n}
the restriction of
f
to
U_{n}
, and assume that
\|f_{n}\|=\|f\|
for each
n
(achievable by starting with
U_{0}
large enough).
Since
\|f\|=\|f_{n}\|
, let
g_{n}\in C^{\mathrm{uf}}_{n+1}(X,\mathbb{Z})
satisfying
\partial g_{n}|U_{n}=f_{n}
and
\|g_{n}\|\leq K(\|f\|)
, as given by
H^{\mathrm{suf}}_{n}
-expansion.

Then the sequence
\langle g_{n}{\rangle}_{n}
has an accumulation point, say
g
, since its elements are uniformly bounded (by
K(\|f\|)
).
Since
\partial g
is also an accumulation point of
(\partial g_{n})_{n}
, which converges to
f
, we conclude that
\partial g=f
.

Thus,
f\in B^{\mathrm{uf}}_{n}(X,\mathbb{Z})
and
H^{\mathrm{suf}}_{n}(X,\mathbb{Z})=0
.
∎

Remark 6.4
.

Consider the following condition on
X
:
There exists some constant
N
such that for any
f\in Z^{\mathrm{suf}}_{n}(X,\mathbb{Z})
, one can write
f=\sum_{i=1}^{N\|f\|}f_{i}
, with each
f_{i}
of norm
1
and in
Z^{\mathrm{suf}}_{n}(X,\mathbb{Z})
.
If this condition held, we could always take the function
K:\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
in
H^{\mathrm{suf}}_{n}
-expansion to be linear.
We do not know whether it holds, even in dimension
1
(that is: can one decompose any flow of norm
k
into a sum of
Nk
flows of norm
1
, for some universal
N
?)

In the zero-dimensional case,
H^{\mathrm{suf}}
-expansion is actually equivalent to non-amenability. Moreover since
B_{0}^{suf}=Z_{0}^{suf}
, also pure
H^{\mathrm{suf}}_{0}
expansion coincides with
H^{\mathrm{suf}}_{0}
expansion.

Proposition 6.5

(
H^{\mathrm{suf}}_{0}
-expansion is just expansion.)
.

Let
X
be a ULF graph.
Then
X
has
H^{\mathrm{suf}}_{0}
-expansion iff
X
is non-amenable.

Proof.

By
Proposition

6.1
and
Lemma

6.3
, we get
H^{\mathrm{suf}}_{0}
-expansion
\Rightarrow
non-amenability.

Now for the converse:
By decomposing such an
f:U\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}
into a sum of
0/1
-valued function, the condition reduces to:

•

There exists
K\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that for all
U{\subseteq}_{f}VX
and
W\subseteq U
, there exists
g:EX\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}
with
\partial g|U={\chi}_{W}
and
\|g\|\leq K
.

Assume now that
X
is non-amenable, with Cheeger constant
\varepsilon
.
By adapting the proof of
[
4
, Lemma 2.1]
(more precisely, their construction of a flow), we see that given
U{\subseteq}_{f}VX
and
W\subseteq U
, one can construct a
g
satisfying
\partial g|U={\chi}_{W}
.
Since in their argument, the “flux” (=excess flow) at vertices in
W
is
\varepsilon
and the flow at edges at most
1
, scaling by
{\varepsilon}^{-1}
yields the bound of
K:={\varepsilon}^{-1}
for
g
.

∎

6.2
Dimension
1

Theorem 6.6
.

If
X
is an infinite, vertex transitive ULF simplicial complex and
H^{\mathrm{suf}}_{1}(X,\mathbb{Z})=0
, then
X
has
H^{\mathrm{suf}}_{1}
-expansion.

Proof.

Suppose
X
does not have
H^{\mathrm{suf}}_{1}
-expansion but
H^{\mathrm{suf}}_{1}(X,\mathbb{Z})=0
.
Then, there exist sequences
\langle U_{i}{\subseteq}_{f}VX\rangle
and
\langle f_{i}\in C^{\mathrm{suf}}_{1}(X,\mathbb{Z}){\rangle}_{i}
as in the definition, with
\|f_{i}\|\leq C
for some
C
, but such that if
\partial g_{i}|U_{i}=f_{i}
, then
\|g_{i}\|\geq K_{i}
, with
K_{i}\rightarrow\infty

(the sequence
\langle f_{i}{\rangle}_{i}
becomes “harder” to kill with
i
increasing).

Let
f_{i}^{\prime}\in Z^{\mathrm{suf}}_{1}(X,\mathbb{Z})
be an extension of
f_{i}
, as in the definition, but with finite support (their existence is proven in
Lemma

6.7
).
Since
X
is infinite and transitive, one can assume that the support of all
f_{i}^{\prime}
s are pairwise disjoint.

Let then
F=\sum_{i}f_{i}^{\prime}
.

F\in Z^{\mathrm{suf}}_{1}(X,\mathbb{Z})
implies the existence of some
G\in C^{\mathrm{uf}}_{2}(X,\mathbb{Z})
with
\partial G=F
.
In particular,
\partial G|U_{i}=f_{i}
, and since
\|G\|\leq K_{i}
for large enough
i
, we get a contradiction.
∎

Note that we do not actually need as much as transitivity, but merely being able to move finite pieces of the graphs sufficiently far, so that, e.g. having a cocompact action by the automorphism group is enough.

Lemma 6.7
.

Assume
X
is ULF and one-ended.
Let
U{\subseteq}_{f}VX
and
f\in C^{\mathrm{suf}}_{1}(U,\mathbb{Z})
satisfying
\partial f\subseteq\partial U
and extensible to
\hat{f}\in Z^{\mathrm{suf}}_{1}(X,\mathbb{Z})
with
\|\hat{f}\|=\|f\|
.
Then
\hat{f}
can be taken with finite support.

Proof.

That
f
can be extended to
\hat{f}\in Z^{\mathrm{suf}}_{1}(X,\mathbb{Z})
implies the existence of a finite number of vertices
v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}\in\partial U
, and integers
m_{1},\ldots,m_{n}
such that
\partial f=\sum m_{i}v_{i}
.
Since
\partial f\subseteq\partial U
, we must have
\sum m_{i}=0
.
Note that the values at these vertices must be killed. To do this, one can either connect two vertices of opposite sign outside of
U
, or add an infinite ray. Because both the number of
v_{i}
as their coefficients are finite, there are at most finitely many rays. Consider a ball enveloping the finite structure of
\hat{f}
, such that only the rays are escaping. Take any ray coming from a positive vertex and any ray coming from a negative vertex. Since
X
is one-ended, both rays can be connected outside of the chosen ball by a path
p
. If the path does not cross any other rays, we can reconnect locally. If it crosses other rays we can see this as sequence of basis vertices starting in one with a positive coefficient, ending in a negative one. Hence there exist two consecutive rays such that their vertices have opposite sign. This ends the proof.
∎

The above “finite extension” property for dimension
1
is crucial in our proof that for transitive infinite graphs, vanishing of
H^{\mathrm{suf}}_{1}
is equivalent to
H^{\mathrm{suf}}_{1}
-expansion.
Were it to also hold for higher dimension, the proof of
Theorem

6.6
would obviously generalise.

6.2.1
H^{\mathrm{uf}}

In the above, we focused on
H^{\mathrm{suf}}
, since
H^{\mathrm{suf}}
-expansion is defined in terms of the simplicial complex at hand.
In the (coarse) setting of
H^{\mathrm{uf}}
, this is obviously problematic.
Our aim now is essentially to coarsify (pure)
H^{\mathrm{suf}}
-expansion.

Definition 6.8

(
H^{\mathrm{uf}}_{1}
-expansion)
.

A ULF graph
X
is said to have (pure)
H^{\mathrm{uf}}_{1}
-expansion iff its Rips complexes eventually have (pure)
H^{\mathrm{suf}}_{1}
-expansion.

The following proposition justifies our interpretation of non-vanishing of
H^{\mathrm{uf}}_{1}(X,\mathbb{Z})
in terms of the three phenomena of ends, large circuits and expansion:

Proposition 6.9
.

If
X
has
\mathbb{Z}
-small circuits (i.e.
\mathcal{C}_{r}(X,\mathbb{Z})=\mathcal{C}_{\infty}(X,\mathbb{Z})
for some
r
) and is one-ended, then
H^{\mathrm{uf}}_{1}(X,\mathbb{Z})\neq 0
implies that
X
does not have pure
H^{\mathrm{uf}}_{1}
-expansion.

In other words, if
H^{\mathrm{uf}}_{1}(X,\mathbb{Z})\neq 0
then at least one of the three phenomena is responsible for it.
In the non-transitive case, it is not clear that the converse holds, that is, that
H^{\mathrm{uf}}_{1}(X,\mathbb{Z})=0
would imply at least one of: ends, large circuits, expansion.

Proof.

Assume
H^{\mathrm{uf}}_{1}(X,\mathbb{Z})\neq 0
,
X
is one-ended and for some
r
,
\mathcal{C}_{r}(X,\mathbb{Z})=\mathcal{C}_{\infty}(X,\mathbb{Z})
.
Then, by
Corollary

2.5
, for
s
big enough,
H^{\mathrm{suf}}_{1}(R_{s}X,\mathbb{Z})\neq 0
.
By
Lemma

6.3
, it follows that
R_{s}X
does not have expansion, and it remains to check that it dos not have
pure
expansion either.

Fix
(U,f)
with
f\in C^{\mathrm{suf}}_{1}(R_{s}U,\mathbb{Z})
with
\partial f\subseteq\partial U
and
f
extensible.

Since
R_{s}X
does not have expansion, it suffices to check that there exists some
g\in C^{\mathrm{suf}}_{2}(R_{s}X,\mathbb{Z})
with
\partial g|{R_{s}U}=f
and
\|\partial g\|=\|f\|
.
Let
\hat{f}
a finite extension of
f
, as given by
Lemma

6.7
.
By “tracing virtual edges”,
\hat{f}
is, up to a finite sum of boundaries of triangles in
R_{s}X
, an element of
Z^{\mathrm{suf}}_{1}(X,\mathbb{Z})
, of finite support.
In particular, up to a finite sum of boundaries of triangles,
\hat{f}
lies in
\mathcal{C}_{\infty}(X,\mathbb{Z})=\mathcal{C}_{r}(X,\mathbb{Z})
, and can be decomposed into a sum of circuits of length
\leq r
.
Now, if
s
is large enough, each of those circuits can itself be decomposed as a sum of boundaries of triangles in
R_{s}X
, so that
\hat{f}
can be written as a sum of boundaries of triangles of
R_{s}X
.
In other terms,
\hat{f}=\partial g
for some
g\in C^{\mathrm{suf}}_{2}(R_{s}X,\mathbb{Z})
.

∎

Theorem 6.10

(
H^{\mathrm{uf}}_{1}
triad)
.

Let
X
a transitive graph, and consider the following three conditions:

1.

X
has more than one end.

2.

X
has
\mathbb{Z}
-large circuits; that is,
\mathcal{C}_{r}(X,\mathbb{Z})\neq\mathcal{C}_{\infty}(X,\mathbb{Z})
for any
r
.

3.

X
does not have pure
H^{\mathrm{uf}}_{1}
-expansion.

Then
H^{\mathrm{uf}}_{1}(X,\mathbb{Z})\neq 0
iff either of the above holds.

Proof.

By the results in
Section

3
, know already that
Item

1
implies non-zero homology.
Similarly, the results in
Section

5
give that
Item

2
implies non-zero homology.

Finally, assume that
Item

3
holds.
If any of
Items

1
and
2
hold, then obviously
H^{\mathrm{uf}}_{1}(X,\mathbb{Z})\neq 0
by what we already know.
So, one may assume now that for some
r
large enough,
\mathcal{C}_{r}(X,\mathbb{Z})=\mathcal{C}_{\infty}(X,\mathbb{Z})
.
Since
R_{r}X
does not have (pure) expansion (
r
large enough),
H^{\mathrm{suf}}_{1}(R_{r}X,\mathbb{Z})\neq 0
, and by
Corollary

2.5
,
H^{\mathrm{uf}}_{1}(X,\mathbb{Z})\neq 0
either.

For the converse, it suffices to see that if
H^{\mathrm{uf}}_{1}(X,\mathbb{Z})\neq 0
, but
X
has one end and
\mathbb{Z}
-small circuits (i.e. neither of

1
and
2
hold), then we can apply
Proposition

6.9

∎
