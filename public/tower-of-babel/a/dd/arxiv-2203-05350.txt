Introduction

A semi-infinite Jacobi (tridiagonal) matrix will be written in the
form

\mathcal{J}=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccccccc}\beta_{0}&\alpha_{0}\\
\alpha_{0}&\beta_{1}&\alpha_{1}\\
&\alpha_{1}&\beta_{2}&\alpha_{2}\\
&&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots\\
&&&\ddots&\ddots\\
\\
\end{array}\right)\!.

(1)

\mathcal{J}
is always assumed to be real and non-decomposable,
i.e.
\alpha_{n}\neq 0
for all
n\geq 0
. Suppose we are given a
sequence of positive numbers
\{a_{n};\,n\geq 0\}
such that

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{a_{n}}<\infty,

(2)

and a parameter
k\in(0,1)
. We will focus on sequences
\{\alpha_{n}\}

and
\{\beta_{n}\}
defined as

\alpha_{n}:=ka_{n},\ \beta_{n}:=a_{n}+k^{2}a_{n-1}\ \,\text{for}\ n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}

(3)

(with
\mathbb{Z}_{+}
standing for non-negative integers).

We aim to study the orthonormal polynomial sequence
\{P_{n}(x);\,n\geq 0\}

defined by the three-term recurrence with initial data:

\displaystyle\alpha_{0}P_{1}(x)+(\beta_{0}-x)P_{0}(x)=0,

\displaystyle\alpha_{n}P_{n+1}(x)+(\beta_{n}-z)P_{n}(x)+\alpha_{n-1}P_{n-1}(x)=0\ \,\text{for}\ n\geq 1,

(4)

\displaystyle P_{0}(x)=1.

Our goal is derivation of a formula for the polynomials
P_{n}(x)

and a description of the respective orthogonality measure. This task
is closely related to analysis of spectral properties of an operator
in the Hilbert space
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}_{+})
represented by the
Jacobi matrix (
1
), (
3
).

A short remark concerning the matrix operator is worthwhile. Let us
denote the canonical basis in
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}_{+})
as
\{\boldsymbol{e}_{n};\,n\geq 0\}
.
The matrix (
1
) clearly represents a symmetric operator in

\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}_{+})
, with the domain being equal to the linear
hull of
\{\boldsymbol{e}_{n}\}
. The symmetric operator will be called

\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{{\ }}_{\circ}}}{{J}}
. The deficiency indices of
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{{\ }}_{\circ}}}{{J}}

are either
(0,0)
or
(1,1)
. As is well known, this happens if
and only if the Hamburger moment problem for
\{P_{n}(x)\}
is or
is not determinate, respectively
[
1
]
. Under our assumptions
it will turn out that
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{{\ }}_{\circ}}}{{J}}
is essentially
self-adjoint. We will denote its self-adjoint closure by the symbol

J
.

In order to formulate the result we introduce two complex functions

\displaystyle\mathfrak{F}(z)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle 1+\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{m}

(5)

\displaystyle\times\bigg(\,\sum_{0\leq j_{1}<j_{2}<\ldots<j_{m}<\infty}\frac{(1-k^{2(j_{1}+1)})(1-k^{2(j_{2}-j_{1})})\cdots\left(1-k^{2(j_{m}-j_{m-1})}\right)}{(1-k^{2})^{m}a_{j_{1}}a_{j_{2}}\cdots a_{j_{m}}}\bigg)z^{m}

and

\displaystyle\mathfrak{W}(z)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{m}

(6)

\displaystyle\hskip-13.99995pt\times\bigg(\,\sum_{0\leq j_{0}<j_{1}<\ldots<j_{m}<\infty}\frac{k^{2j_{0}}(1-k^{2(j_{1}-j_{0})})(1-k^{2(j_{2}-j_{1})})\cdots(1-k^{2(j_{m}-j_{m-1})})}{(1-k^{2})^{m}a_{j_{0}}a_{j_{1}}a_{j_{2}}\ldots a_{j_{m}}}\bigg)z^{m}

With the above assumptions it is readily seen that the functions are
both entire. Moreover, we extend definition (
6
) by defining
a countable family of entire functions, indexed by

,

\displaystyle\mathfrak{W}_{n}(z)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{m}

(7)

\displaystyle\hskip-18.00005pt\times\bigg(\,\sum_{n\leq j_{0}<j_{1}<\ldots<j_{m}<\infty}\frac{k^{2j_{0}}(1-k^{2(j_{1}-j_{0})})(1-k^{2(j_{2}-j_{1})})\cdots(1-k^{2(j_{m}-j_{m-1})})}{(1-k^{2})^{m}a_{j_{0}}a_{j_{1}}a_{j_{2}}\ldots a_{j_{m}}}\bigg)z^{m}.

Hence

. It is straightforward
to derive the estimate

|\mathfrak{W}_{n}(z)|\leq\frac{k^{2n}}{\min\{a_{j};\,j\geq n\}}\,\exp\!\bigg(\,\frac{|z|}{1-k^{2}}\,\sum_{j=n+1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{a_{j}}\bigg).

.

Let

Preliminaries

It can be shown that if there exists exactly one self-adjoint operator

J
whose matrix in the canonical basis equals
\mathcal{J}
then
a similar assertion is true for the associated Jacobi matrix
\mathcal{J}^{(1)}

as well
[
9
]
. The self-adjoint operator corresponding
to
\mathcal{J}^{(1)}
will be denoted
J^{(1)}
.

Proposition 4
.

Assume that there exists
exactly one self-adjoint operator
J
in
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}_{+})

whose matrix in the canonical basis equals a Jacobi matrix
\mathcal{J}
.
Assume further that
J
is bounded below by a positive constant and

J^{-1}
belongs to the trace class. Then the same is true for the
self-adjoint operator
J^{(1)}
corresponding to the associated Jacobi
matrix
\mathcal{J}^{(1)}
.

Proof.

The proof is computational and is outlined below. From
the fact that the matrix of
J
in the canonical basis is tridiagonal
it follows that

\displaystyle\beta_{0}\langle e_{0},J^{-1}\boldsymbol{e}_{0}\rangle+\alpha_{0}\langle\boldsymbol{e}_{1},J^{-1}\boldsymbol{e}_{0}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1,

\displaystyle\beta_{0}^{\,2}\,\langle\boldsymbol{e}_{0},J^{-1}\boldsymbol{e}_{0}\rangle-\alpha_{0}^{\,2}\,\langle\boldsymbol{e}_{1},J^{-1}\boldsymbol{e}_{1}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\beta_{0}.

Using these relations it is straightforward to show that for

\displaystyle M

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&0\\
0&1\end{array}\right)-\alpha_{0}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\langle\boldsymbol{e}_{1},J^{-1}\boldsymbol{e}_{0}\rangle&\langle\boldsymbol{e}_{1},J^{-1}\boldsymbol{e}_{1}\rangle\\
\langle\boldsymbol{e}_{0},J^{-1}\boldsymbol{e}_{0}\rangle&\langle\boldsymbol{e}_{1},J^{-1}\boldsymbol{e}_{0}\rangle\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\beta_{0}\,\langle\boldsymbol{e}_{0},J^{-1}\boldsymbol{e}_{0}\rangle&-\alpha_{0}\,\langle\boldsymbol{e}_{1},J^{-1}\boldsymbol{e}_{1}\rangle\\
-\alpha_{0}\,\langle\boldsymbol{e}_{0},J^{-1}\boldsymbol{e}_{0}\rangle&\beta_{0}\,\langle\boldsymbol{e}_{0},J^{-1}\boldsymbol{e}_{0}\rangle\end{array}\right)

we have

\det(M)=\beta_{0}\,\langle\boldsymbol{e}_{0},J^{-1}\boldsymbol{e}_{0}\rangle.

Denote as
\beta_{0}\oplus J^{(1)}
the block diagonal matrix with
blocks
(\beta_{0})
and
J^{(1)}
. Equation

(\beta_{0}\oplus J^{(1)})\!\bigg(J^{-1}+J^{-1}\bigg(\,\sum_{s=0}^{1}\sum_{t=0}^{1}x_{s,t}\,\boldsymbol{e}_{s}\boldsymbol{e}_{t}{}^{T}\bigg)J^{-1}\bigg)\!=I

results in four equations for four unknowns
x_{s,t}
,
0\leq s,t\leq 1
,
written in the matrix form

\alpha_{0}\!\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&1\\
1&0\end{array}\right)=\left(\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&0\\
0&1\end{array}\right)-\alpha_{0}\!\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\left\langle\boldsymbol{e}_{1},J^{-1}\boldsymbol{e}_{0}\right\rangle&\left\langle\boldsymbol{e}_{1},J^{-1}\boldsymbol{e}_{1}\right\rangle\\
\left\langle\boldsymbol{e}_{0},J^{-1}\boldsymbol{e}_{0}\right\rangle&\left\langle\boldsymbol{e}_{1},J^{-1}\boldsymbol{e}_{0}\right\rangle\end{array}\right)\right)\!\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}x_{0,0}&x_{0,1}\\
x_{1,0}&x_{1,1}\end{array}\right)\!.

A unique solution is readily obtained. Since the inverse is unique
we have

(\beta_{0}\oplus J^{(1)})^{-1}=J^{-1}+J^{-1}\bigg(\,\sum_{s=0}^{1}\sum_{t=0}^{1}x_{s,t}\,\boldsymbol{e}_{s}\boldsymbol{e}_{t}{}^{T}\bigg)J^{-1}

where

x_{0,0}=\frac{\alpha_{0}^{\,2}\langle\boldsymbol{e}_{1},J^{-1}\boldsymbol{e}_{1}\rangle}{\beta_{0}\langle\boldsymbol{e}_{0},J^{-1}\boldsymbol{e}_{0}\rangle}\,,\ x_{0,1}=x_{1,0}=\alpha_{0},\ x_{1,1}=\frac{\alpha_{0}^{\,2}}{\beta_{0}}\,.

Whence

\mathop{\mathrm{}}{tr}\nolimits\big((J^{(1)})^{-1}\big)=\mathop{\mathrm{}}{tr}\nolimits(J^{-1})+\sum_{s=0}^{1}\sum_{t=0}^{1}x_{s,t}\langle\boldsymbol{e}_{s},J^{-2}\,\boldsymbol{e}_{t}\rangle-\frac{1}{\beta_{0}}.

This shows the proposition. ∎

We still assume that
J
is a unique self-adjoint operator in
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}_{+})

whose matrix in the canonical basis equals
\mathcal{J}
, as given
in (
1
). Let
\mu
be the unique Borel probability measure
on
\mathbb{R}
which is an orthogonality measure for the orthonormal
polynomial sequence
\{P_{n}(x)\}
defined in (
4
).
Furthermore, let
w_{n}(z)
,
n\geq 0
, be the so called functions
of the second kind
[
11
]
,

\forall z\in\varrho(J),\,\forall n\geq 0,\ w_{n}(z):=\int\frac{P_{n}(\lambda)}{\lambda-z}\,\text{d}\mu(\lambda).

(16)

Here
\varrho(J)
denotes the resolvent set of
J
. In particular,

w(z)\equiv w_{0}(z)=\int\frac{\text{d}\mu(\lambda)}{\lambda-z}

(17)

is the Weyl function of
J
. Thus the Weyl function is the Stieltjes
transformation of the measure
\mu
.

One can check that
[
9
]

\forall z\in\varrho(J),\,\forall n\geq 0,\ w_{n}(z)P_{n}(z)=\langle\boldsymbol{e}_{n},(J-z)^{-1}\boldsymbol{e}_{n}\rangle.

Particularly, if

is bounded below by a positive constant
\gamma

and so

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\langle\boldsymbol{e}_{n},J^{-1}\boldsymbol{e}_{n}\rangle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}w_{n}(0)P_{n}(0).

(18)

Hence

belongs to the trace class if and only if this sum

\mathop{\mathrm{}}{tr}\nolimits J^{-1}
. Moreover, the

.
Necessarily, all roots of the polynomials

are contained in this interval, too

. We have

\forall z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus[\,\gamma,\infty),\,\forall n\geq 0,\ w_{n}(z)=-\bigg(\sum_{j=n}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\alpha_{j}P_{j}(z)P_{j+1}(z)}\bigg)P_{n}(z).

(19)

In addition, as also proven in

\mathfrak{F}_{\text{char}}(z)=\prod_{j=0}^{\infty}\bigg(1-\frac{z}{\lambda_{j}}\bigg)\ \text{where}\ \mathop{\mathrm{}}{spec}\nolimits(J)=\mathop{\mathrm{}}{spec}\nolimits_{p}(J)=\{\lambda_{j};\,j\geq 0\}.

Proposition 5
.

The symmetric operator

\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{{\ }}_{\circ}}}{{J}}
in
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}_{+})
,
with the matrix
\mathcal{J}
given in (
1
), is essentially
self-adjoint, and the Hamburger moment problem for
\{P_{n}(x)\}

is determinate. The closure of
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{{\ }}_{\circ}}}{{J}}
,
i.e. the self-adjoint operator
J
, is positive definite and
J^{-1}

belongs to the trace class.

Proof.

From (
3
), (
4
)
we infer that the three-term recurrence in case
x=0
can be reduced
to a two-term recurrence, namely we get

ka_{n}\big(P_{n+1}(0)+k^{-1}P_{n}(0)\big)+k^{2}a_{n-1}\big(P_{n}(0)+k^{-1}P_{n-1}(0)\big)=0\ \,\text{for}\ n\geq 1.

Taking into account the initial data we have

P_{n}(0)=(-1)^{n}k^{-n}\ \,\text{for}\ n\geq 0.

(21)

Thus the sum
\sum_{n}P_{n}(0)^{2}
is divergent and the Hamburger
moment problem is determinate
[
1
]
. This in turn means
that the symmetric operator
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{{\ }}_{\circ}}}{{J}}
is
essentially self-adjoint
[
1
]
.

The matrix
\mathcal{J}
can be decomposed,

\mathcal{J}=(I+kE)J_{0}(I+kE^{T}),

where
I
is the unite matrix,
J_{0}:=\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diag}\nolimits(a_{0},a_{1},a_{2},\ldots)

and
E
is a strictly lower triangular matrix whose only nonzero
elements are
E_{n+1,n}=1
,
n\geq 0
. Since all factors are band
matrices the product is well defined. It follows that

\forall\boldsymbol{f}\in\mathop{\mathrm{}}{span}\nolimits\{\boldsymbol{e}_{n}\},\ \langle\boldsymbol{f},J\boldsymbol{f}\rangle\geq a_{\text{min}}\,\|(I+kE^{T})\boldsymbol{f}\|^{2}\geq a_{\text{min}}\,(1-k)^{2}\,\|\boldsymbol{f}\|^{2}

where

a_{\text{min}}:=\min\{a_{n};\,n\geq 0\}.

Since
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{span}\nolimits\{\boldsymbol{e}_{n}\}
is a core for
J
we have

J\geq\gamma\ \,\text{where}\ \gamma:=a_{\text{min}}\,(1-k)^{2}>0.

In view of (
21
) and recalling (
19
), (
18
)
we get

w_{n}(0)=(-1)^{n}\sum_{j=n}^{\infty}\frac{k^{2j-n}}{a_{j}}=(-1)^{n}k^{n}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{k^{2j}}{a_{n+j}}

(22)

and

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{}}{tr}\nolimits J^{-1}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}w_{n}(0)P_{n}(0)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}P_{n}(0)^{2}\sum_{j=n}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\alpha_{j}P_{j}(0)P_{j+1}(0)}\,=\,\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}k^{-2n}\sum_{j=n}^{\infty}\frac{k^{2j}}{a_{j}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{a_{j}}\sum_{n=0}^{j}k^{2(j-n)}\,=\,\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{1-k^{2j+2}}{(1-k^{2})a_{j}}\,<\infty.

Hence
J^{-1}
belongs to the trace class ∎

Remark 6

In view of (

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hskip-60.00009pt\times\sum_{0\leq j_{1}<j_{2}<\ldots<j_{m-1}<n}\frac{(1-k^{2(j_{1}+1)})(1-k^{2(j_{2}-j_{1})})\cdots(1-k^{2(j_{m-1}-j_{m-2})})(1-k^{2(n-j_{m-1})})}{(1-k^{2})^{m}a_{j_{1}}a_{j_{2}}\cdots a_{j_{m-1}}a_{n}}.

This can be rewritten as

\displaystyle a_{n}\big(\pi_{n+1}(x)-\pi_{n}(x)\big)

Remark 15
.

We shall need the identity

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{q^{rn}}{(q^{n}w;q)_{r+1}}=\frac{1}{(1-q^{r})(w;q)_{r}},\ \forall r\in\mathbb{N}.

(33)

The both sides are regarded as meromorphic functions in

The identity is a straightforward consequence of a well known formula
for

\,{}_{1}\phi_{0}(q^{m};;q,w)
telling us that
[
3
,
7
]

\frac{1}{(w;q)_{m}}=\sum_{s=0}^{\infty}\frac{(q^{m};q)_{s}}{(q;q)_{s}}\,w^{s}.

(34)

Here
w\in\mathbb{C}
,
|w|<1
, and
m\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
. In fact,
it suffices to show (
33
) for
|w|<1
. Then, in view
of (
34
), the LHS of (

) equals

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}q^{rn}\sum_{s=0}^{\infty}\frac{(q^{r+1};q)_{s}}{(q;q)_{s}}\,q^{ns}w^{s}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{s=0}^{\infty}\frac{(q^{r+1};q)_{s}}{(1-q^{r+s})(q;q)_{s}}\,w^{s}\,=\,\frac{1}{1-q^{r}}\,\sum_{s=0}^{\infty}\frac{(q^{r};q)_{s}}{(q;q)_{s}}\,w^{s}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(1-q^{r})(w;q)_{r}}.

Lemma 16
.

For every
w\in\mathbb{C}
,
|w|<1
,
and
m\in\mathbb{N}
,

(1-q^{m})(w;q)_{m}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{q^{(m+1)j}w^{j}}{1-q^{j+m}}\,\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{q^{(j+m+1)n}}{\left(q^{n}w;q\right){}_{m+1}}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{q^{mj}w^{j}}{1-q^{j+m+1}}.

Proof.

We can express the LHS of (
35
) as

\displaystyle\frac{(w;q)_{m}}{w}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{q^{(m+1)j}w^{j}}{1-q^{j+m}}\,\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}q^{(j+m)n}\bigg(\frac{1}{(q^{n}w;q)_{m}}-\frac{1}{(q^{n+1}w;q)_{m}}\bigg)

\displaystyle=\,\frac{(w;q)_{m}}{w}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{q^{(m+1)j}w^{j}}{1-q^{j+m}}\bigg(\frac{1}{(w;q)_{m}}+\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(q^{j+m}-1)q^{(j+m)n}}{(q^{n+1}w;q)_{m}}\bigg)

\displaystyle=\,\frac{1}{w}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{q^{(m+1)j}w^{j}}{1-q^{j+m}}-\frac{(w;q)_{m}}{w}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{q^{mn}}{(q^{n+1}w;q)_{m+1}}.

Referring to (
33
), this expression can be further
simplified and we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{w}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{q^{(m+1)j}w^{j}}{1-q^{j+m}}-\frac{(w;q)_{m}}{w(1-q^{m})(qw;q)_{m}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{w}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{q^{(m+1)j}w^{j}}{1-q^{j+m}}-\frac{1-w}{w\,(1-q^{m})(1-q^{m}w)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1-q^{m}w}+\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{q^{(m+1)(j+1)}w^{j}}{1-q^{j+m+1}}.

Now one can readily check that the last expression actually equals
the RHS of (
35
). ∎

Proposition 21
.

Let

,

, and

\displaystyle\hskip-10.00002pt\sum_{0\leq n_{m}\leq\ldots\leq n_{2}\leq n_{1}<\infty}q^{s_{1}n_{1}+\ldots+s_{m}n_{m}}\bigg((q^{n_{m}+a};q)_{s_{m}+1}(q^{n_{m-1}+s_{m}+a+1};q)_{s_{m-1}+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(1-q^{s_{1}})(1-q^{s_{1}+s_{2}})\cdots(1-q^{s_{1}+\ldots+s_{m}})(q^{a};q)_{s_{1}+\ldots+s_{m}}}.

Remark 22

In particular,

)
as

. For

the equation reduces to (

For

we have

\displaystyle\hskip-13.99995ptS_{m}(s_{1},\ldots,s_{m};a)

\displaystyle\hskip-13.99995pt=\,\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{q^{(s_{1}+\ldots+s_{m-1}+s_{m})n}}{(q^{n+a};q)_{s_{m}+1}}\Bigg(\sum_{0\leq n_{m-1}\leq\ldots\leq n_{2}\leq n_{1}<\infty}q^{s_{1}n_{1}+\ldots+s_{m-1}n_{m-1}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad\ \times\big((q^{n_{m-1}+s_{m}+n+a+1};q)_{s_{m-1}+1}(q^{n_{m-2}+s_{m-1}+s_{m}+n+a+2};q)_{s_{m-2}+1}

\displaystyle\hskip-10.00002pt=\,\frac{1}{(1-q^{s_{1}})(1-q^{s_{1}+s_{2}})\cdots(1-q^{s_{1}+\ldots+s_{m-1}})(1-q^{s_{1}+\ldots+s_{m-1}+s_{m}})(q^{a};q)_{s_{1}+\ldots+s_{m-1}+s_{m}}}.

-Laguerre polynomials

We are going to consider a particular case of (

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\beta_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle q^{-2n-2}(1-q^{n+1})+q^{-2n+1}(1-q^{n})=q^{-2(n+1)}\big(1-q^{n+1}+q^{3}(1-q^{n})\big).

.

Recall that the

-Laguerre polynomials are defined as follows

,

It is known that

\underset{q\to 1}{\text{lim}}\,L_{n}^{(a)}\big((1-q)x;q\big)=L_{n}^{(a)}(x).

Recall, too, that the Jackson
q
-Bessel functions of the second

,

]

particularly,

It is known that all the roots of

are real and simple with the only cluster point at infinity
[
4
]
.

Here we propose a modification of the
q
-Laguerre polynomials though
this is done for the parameter

only.

Definition 23
.

The modified
q
-Laguerre polynomials

are introduced by the equation

Comparing (

) to (

) one finds
that, for all

. Clearly,

\underset{q\to 1}{\text{lim}}\,\tilde{L}_{n}\big((1-q)x;q\big)=L_{n}^{(0)}(x).

Lemma 24

It holds true that

q^{n}L_{n}^{(0)}(x;q)+L_{n-1}^{(1)}(x;q)-L_{n}^{(1)}(x;q)=0,\ \forall n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}.

(47)

Proof.

Verification is straightforward. It is sufficient to
use definition (

) and expand the basic hypergeometric

Proposition 25

The modified

-Laguerre

\displaystyle(1-q^{n+1})\tilde{L}_{n+1}(x;q)-\big(1-q^{n+1}+q^{3}(1-q^{n})\big)\tilde{L}_{n}(x;q)+q^{3}(1-q^{n})\tilde{L}_{n-1}(x;q)

(
\tilde{L}_{-1}(x;q)

First note that
\tilde{L}_{0}(x;q)=\tilde{L}_{0}(0;q)=1
.

The

-Laguerre polynomials are known to obey the three-term recurrence

[
7
]

\displaystyle-q^{2n+1}xL_{n}^{(0)}(x;q)

\displaystyle(1-q^{n+1})\big(L_{n+1}^{(0)}(x;q)-L_{n}^{(0)}(x;q)\big)

\displaystyle-\,q(1-q^{n})\big(L_{n}^{(0)}(x;q)-L_{n-1}^{(0)}(x;q)\big)

\displaystyle-q^{2n+2}xL_{n}^{(1)}(x;q)

Taking an appropriate linear combination of (
