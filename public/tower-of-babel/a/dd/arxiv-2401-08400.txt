Well-posedness of a bulk-surface convective Cahn–Hilliard system with dynamic boundary conditions
Patrik Knopf, Jonas Stange

Abstract

Abstract

We consider a general class of bulk-surface convective Cahn–Hilliard systems with dynamic boundary conditions. In contrast to classical Neumann boundary conditions, the dynamic boundary conditions of Cahn–Hilliard type allow for dynamic changes of the contact angle between the diffuse interface and the boundary, a convection-induced motion of the contact line as well as absorption of material by the boundary. The coupling conditions for bulk and surface quantities involve parameters
K,L\in[0,\infty]
, whose choice declares whether these conditions are of Dirichlet, Robin or Neumann type. We first prove the existence of a weak solution to our model in the case
K,L\in(0,\infty)
by means of a Faedo–Galerkin approach. For all other cases, the existence of a weak solution is then shown by means of the asymptotic limits, where
K
and
L
are sent to zero or to infinity, respectively. Eventually, we establish higher regularity for the phase-fields, and we prove the uniqueness of weak solutions given that the mobility functions are constant.

Keywords:
convective Cahn–Hilliard equation, bulk-surface interaction, dynamic boundary conditions, dynamic contact angle, moving contact line.

Mathematics Subject Classification:

35K35, 35D30, 35A01, 35A02, 35Q92.

1  Introduction

In recent times, the description of immiscible
two-phase flows
in a bounded domain by diffuse-interface models has become very popular. This is especially because those systems can usually be handled more easily in terms of mathematical analysis than their sharp-interface counterparts.
In such models, the location of the two fluids inside the container is represented by an order parameter, the so-called
phase-field
. The time evolution of this phase-field function is often described by a
convective Cahn–Hilliard equation
. There, the velocity field of the mixture enters via the material derivative of the phase-field. The time evolution of the velocity field is usually described by a fluid equation, e.g., the
Navier–Stokes equation
. Very frequently used
Navier–Stokes–Cahn–Hilliard
models for two-phase flows are the
Model H
(see
[
25
,
27
]
), which covers the case where both fluids have the same individual density, and the
Abels–Garcke–Grün model (AGG model)
(see
[
1
]
), which is even capable of describing the situation where both fluids have different (i.e.,
unmatched
) densities.

For the Model H and the AGG model, the standard choice are homogeneous Neumann boundary conditions for the Cahn–Hilliard quantities (i.e., the phase-field and the chemical potential) as well as a no-slip boundary condition for the velocity field. However, these choices lead to some crucial limitations of these models:

•

The contact angle between the diffuse interface and the boundary of the domain is fixed at ninety degrees at all times, which is unrealistic for many real-world applications.

•

The motion of the contact line, where the diffuse interface intersects the boundary of the domain, is driven only by diffusion. This means that a motion of the contact line caused by convection is not taken into account.

•

No transfer of material between the bulk and the boundary of the domain is allowed. Therefore, any absorption of material by the boundary cannot be described.

For a more detailed discussion of these issues, we refer the reader to
[
23
]
.

To overcome these limitations, a new Navier–Stokes–Cahn–Hilliard model with dynamic boundary conditions was derived in
[
23
]
. It can be regarded as an extension of the AGG model and is capable of describing two-phase flows with unmatched densities. In the case of matched densities, some first analytic results (namely the existence of weak solution and their uniqueness under certain additional assumptions) were presented in
[
23
]
. The case of unmatched densities, however, is much more involved. This is mainly because in the Navier–Stokes equation, the density function then depends on the phase-field, and a further flux term related to the interfacial motion will appear.
In the case of unmatched densities, at least for certain parameter choices in the dynamic boundary conditions, the existence of a weak solution to the model introduced in
[
23
]
was shown in
[
17
]
by an implicit time discretization scheme.
A different strategy to prove the existence of weak solutions to the model proposed in
[
23
]
, in a unified framework for all parameter choices in the dynamic boundary conditions, is to first analyze the underlying bulk-surface convective Cahn–Hilliard subsystem separately. Using this information, a weak solution to the full Navier–Stokes–Cahn–Hilliard system is then to be constructed by means of a suitable fixed point argument.

This motivates us to investigate the following
bulk-surface convective Cahn–Hilliard system with dynamic boundary conditions
:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\phi+\textnormal{div}(\phi\boldsymbol{v})=\textnormal{div}(m_{\Omega}(\phi)\nabla\mu)

\displaystyle\text{in}\ Q,

(1.1a)

\displaystyle\mu=-\epsilon\Delta\phi+\epsilon^{-1}F^{\prime}(\phi)

\displaystyle\text{in}\ Q,

(1.1b)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\psi+\textnormal{div}_{\Gamma}(\psi\boldsymbol{w})=\textnormal{div}_{\Gamma}(m_{\Gamma}(\psi)\nabla_{\Gamma}\theta)-\beta m_{\Omega}(\phi)\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\mu

\displaystyle\text{on}\ \Sigma,

(1.1c)

\displaystyle\theta=-\epsilon_{\Gamma}\kappa\Delta_{\Gamma}\psi+\epsilon_{\Gamma}^{-1}G^{\prime}(\psi)+\alpha\epsilon\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\phi

\displaystyle\text{on}\ \Sigma,

(1.1d)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\epsilon K\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\phi=\alpha\psi-\phi&\text{if}\ K\in[0,\infty),\\
\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\phi=0&\text{if}\ K=\infty\end{cases}

\displaystyle\text{on}\ \Sigma,

(1.1e)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}Lm_{\Omega}(\phi)\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\mu=\beta\theta-\mu&\text{if}\ L\in[0,\infty),\\
m_{\Omega}(\phi)\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\mu=0&\text{if}\ L=\infty\end{cases}

\displaystyle\text{on}\ \Sigma,

(1.1f)

\displaystyle\phi|_{t=0}=\phi_{0}

\displaystyle\text{in}\ \Omega,

(1.1g)

\displaystyle\psi|_{t=0}=\psi_{0}

\displaystyle\text{on}\ \Gamma.

(1.1h)

Here,
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
d\in\{2,3\}
is a bounded domain with boundary
\Gamma\coloneqq\partial\Omega
,
T>0
is a prescribed final time, and we set
Q\coloneqq\Omega\times(0,T)
and
\Sigma\coloneqq\Gamma\times(0,T)
.
The outward unit normal vector field on
\Gamma
is denoted by
\mathbf{n}
.
Moreover,
\nabla_{\Gamma}
,
\textnormal{div}_{\Gamma}
and
\Delta_{\Gamma}
denote the surface gradient, the surface divergence, and the Laplace-Beltrami operator on
\Gamma
, respectively.

The function
\phi:Q\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is the bulk phase-field. It is an order parameter, which represents the distribution of the two immiscible materials within the domain
\Omega
. Similarly, the surface phase-field
\psi:\Sigma\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
represents the distribution of two materials on the surface.
The functions
F
and
G
are double-well potentials, which lead to the effect that
\phi(t)
and
\psi(t)
will attain values close to
\pm 1
in most parts of
\Omega
and
\Gamma
(which correspond to the pure phases of the materials). In the remaining intermediate regions, which are called diffuse interfaces,
\phi
and
\psi
are expected to exhibit a continuous transition between the values
-1
and
1
.
The functions
\mu:Q\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and
\theta:\Sigma\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
represent the bulk chemical potential and the surface chemical potential, respectively. The non-negative, scalar functions
m_{\Omega}(\phi)
and
m_{\Gamma}(\psi)
are called mobilities. They depend on the phase-fields and describe where diffusion processes occur. Moreover, the functions
\boldsymbol{v}:Q\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\boldsymbol{w}:\Sigma\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
are prescribed velocity fields that correspond to the flow of the two materials in the bulk and on the surface, respectively.
In this paper, we always assume that
\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\mathbf{n}=0
and
\boldsymbol{w}\cdot\mathbf{n}=0
on
\Sigma
.
We point out that in many cases (e.g., in the model derived in
[
23
]
),
\boldsymbol{v}
and
\boldsymbol{w}
are related by the condition
\boldsymbol{w}=\boldsymbol{v}|_{\Sigma}
on
\Sigma
.
However, as this relation will not have any impact on our mathematical analysis, we consider
\boldsymbol{v}
and
\boldsymbol{w}
as independent functions to keep our model as general as possible.
Furthermore,
\epsilon>0
is a parameter related to the thickness of the diffuse interface in the bulk, whereas
\epsilon_{\Gamma}>0
corresponds to the width of the diffuse interface on the boundary. The parameter
\kappa>0
acts as a weight for the surface Dirichlet energy
\psi\mapsto\int_{\Gamma}\left|\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi\right|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}\Gamma
, which has a smoothing effect on the phase separation at the boundary.

The time evolution of
(\phi,\mu)
is determined by the
bulk convective Cahn–Hilliard subsystem

\big(\eqref{CH:1},\eqref{CH:2}\big)
, whereas the evolution of
(\psi,\theta)
is described by the
surface convective Cahn–Hilliard subsystem

\big(\eqref{CH:3},\eqref{CH:4}\big)
, which is coupled to the bulk by expressions involving the normal derivatives
\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\phi
and
\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\mu
. The bulk and surface quantities are further coupled by the boundary conditions (
1.1e
) and (
1.1f
), which depend on parameters
K,L\in[0,\infty]
and
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
.

In (
1.1e
) and (
1.1f
), the parameters
K,L\in[0,\infty]
are used to distinguish different cases, each corresponding to a certain solution behaviour related to a physical phenomenon. The case that has been studied most extensively in the literature is
K=0
. In this case, (
1.1e
) is to be interpreted as the Dirichlet type boundary condition
\phi=\alpha\psi
on
\Sigma
.
This choice makes particular sense along with
\alpha=1
, if the materials on the boundary are simply considered as an extension of those in the bulk. Originally, the boundary condition (
1.1f
) was introduced in the
non-convective case
(i.e.,
\boldsymbol{v}\equiv 0
and
\boldsymbol{w}\equiv 0
) in the following literature:

•

The choice
L=0
was proposed in
[
14
]
and
[
24
]
. Then, (
1.1f
) can be restated as the Dirichlet condition
\mu=\beta\theta
on
\Sigma
, which means that the chemical potentials
\mu
and
\theta
are always in a chemical equilibrium. In this case, a rapid transfer of material between bulk and boundary can be expected (see, e.g.,
[
29
]
).

•

The choice
L=\infty
was introduced in
[
32
]
. In this case, (
1.1f
) is a homogeneous Neumann boundary condition on
\mu
, which means that the mass flux between bulk and surface is zero. Consequently, no transfer of material between bulk and surface will occur.

•

The choice
L\in(0,\infty)
was first used in
[
29
]
to interpolate between the extreme cases
L=0
and
L=\infty
. In this case, a transfer of material between bulk and surface will occur, and the number
L^{-1}
is related to the rate of absorption of bulk material by the boundary (cf.
[
29
]
).

In the
convective case
(i.e., with non-trivial velocity fields
\boldsymbol{v}
and
\boldsymbol{w}
), the boundary condition (
1.1f
) was also used in the Navier–Stokes–Cahn–Hilliard model derived in
[
23
]
and in the Cahn–Hilliard–Brinkman model studied in
[
12
]
. Furthermore, the model derivation in
[
23
]
shows that the parameter
\beta
acts as a weight for the mass flux between bulk and surface. This means that
\beta
can also be negative and therefore, we simply assume
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
.

However, we also want to cover the case, where the materials on the boundary are not the same as those in the bulk. For instance, this is the case if the bulk materials are transformed at the boundary by a chemical reaction. In this case, the surface phase-field might not be proportional to the trace of the bulk phase-field, so the choice
K=0
would not be appropriate. In our model, this is taken into account by the choice
K\in(0,\infty]
. In the case
K=\infty
, (
1.1f
) degenerates to a homogeneous Neumann boundary condition for the phase-field. For most applications, this might not be the preferred choice as it is known that such a Neumann boundary condition enforces the diffuse interface to always intersect the boundary at a perfect angle of ninety degrees. In fact, this is one of the aforementioned limitations we usually want to overcome by the usage of dynamic boundary conditions. However, we include the case
K=\infty
for the sake of completeness. In the case
K\in(0,\infty)
, (
1.1f
) can be regarded as a Robin type boundary condition. It is suitable to describe a scenario, where
\psi
and the trace of
\phi
are not proportional, but it still allows for dynamical changes of the contact angle between the diffuse interface and the boundary. In the context of bulk-surface Cahn–Hilliard equations, condition (
1.1f
) with
K\in(0,\infty)
was used in
[
28
]
(in the non-convective case) and in
[
12
]
(in the convective case). In
[
28
]
and
[
12
]
, it was further shown that the limit
K\to 0
can be used to recover the boundary condition (
1.1f
) with
K=0
.
Especially in the case that the materials on the boundary differ from those in the bulk, the parameter
\alpha
could be any real number (even with a negative sign). Therefore, to keep the model as general as possible, we allow for
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
. However, for our mathematical analysis, we will need the additional relation
\alpha\beta\left|\Omega\right|+\left|\Gamma\right|\neq 0
. Of course, this is trivially satisfied if
\alpha
and
\beta
have the same sign.

We further want to point out that including the case
K\in(0,\infty)
also helps our mathematical analysis. This is because the existence of a weak solution to (
1.1
) can be shown by a suitable Faedo–Galerkin scheme only in the cases, where the boundary conditions are of the same type (i.e.,
K=L=0
,
K=L=\infty
or
0<K,L<\infty
), as otherwise, the spaces of admissible test functions in the weak formulation do not match. In this paper, we will first construct a weak solution of (
1.1
) in the case
0<K,L<\infty
. Then, we prove the existence of a weak solution in the remaining cases by sending the parameters
K
and
L
to zero or to infinity, respectively.

Let us now discuss some important properties of our model.
The system (
1.1
) is associated with the total energy

\displaystyle\begin{split}E_{K}(\phi,\psi)&=\int_{\Omega}\frac{\epsilon}{2}\left|\nabla\phi\right|^{2}+\epsilon^{-1}F(\phi)\;\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Gamma}\frac{\epsilon_{\Gamma}\kappa}{2}\left|\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi\right|^{2}+\epsilon_{\Gamma}^{-1}G(\psi)\;\mathrm{d}\Gamma\\
&\quad+\mathds{h}(K)\int_{\Gamma}\frac{1}{2}\left|\alpha\psi-\phi\right|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}\Gamma,\end{split}

(1.2)

where the function

\displaystyle\mathds{h}(K)\coloneqq\begin{cases}K^{-1},&\text{if }K\in(0,\infty),\\
0,&\text{if }K\in\{0,\infty\}\end{cases}

is used to distinguish the different cases corresponding to the choice of
K
. Sufficiently regular solutions of the system (
1.1
) satisfy the
mass conversation law

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\beta\int_{\Omega}\phi(t)\;\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Gamma}\psi(t)\;\mathrm{d}\Gamma=\beta\int_{\Omega}\phi_{0}\;\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Gamma}\psi_{0}\;\mathrm{d}\Gamma,&\textnormal{if }L\in[0,\infty),\\
\int_{\Omega}\phi(t)\;\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}\phi_{0}\;\mathrm{d}x\quad\textnormal{and}\quad\int_{\Gamma}\psi(t)\;\mathrm{d}\Gamma=\int_{\Gamma}\psi_{0}\;\mathrm{d}\Gamma,&\textnormal{if }L=\infty\end{cases}

(1.3)

for all
t\in[0,T]
.
As mentioned above, this means that a transfer of material between bulk and surface is allowed only in the cases
L\in[0,\infty)
.
Moreover, sufficiently regular solutions satisfy the
energy identity

\displaystyle\begin{split}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}E_{K}(\phi,\psi)&=\int_{\Omega}\phi\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\nabla\mu\;\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Gamma}\psi\boldsymbol{w}\cdot\nabla_{\Gamma}\theta\;\mathrm{d}\Gamma-\int_{\Omega}m_{\Omega}(\phi)\left|\nabla\mu\right|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x\\
&\qquad-\int_{\Gamma}m_{\Gamma}(\psi)\left|\nabla_{\Gamma}\theta\right|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}\Gamma-\mathds{h}(L)\int_{\Gamma}(\beta\theta-\mu)^{2}\;\mathrm{d}\Gamma\\
\end{split}

(1.4)

for all
t\in[0,T]
. We point out that in the non-convective case (i.e.,
\boldsymbol{v}=0
and
\boldsymbol{w}=0
), the right-hand side of (
1.4
) is clearly non-positive. This means that the energy dissipates over the course of time and the terms on the right-hand side of (
1.4
) can be interpreted as the dissipation rate. In this case, the system (
1.1
) can be derived as an
H^{-1}
type gradient flow of the free energy
E_{K}
subject to the inner product
\left(\cdot,\cdot\right)_{L,\beta,*}
that will be introduced in Section
2
,
(P5)
(cf.
[
29
, Remark 2.2]
and
[
28
, Section 3]
). Moreover, if
L=\infty
, the system (
1.1
) can also be derived by an energetic variational approach that combines the least action principle and Onsager’s principle of maximum energy dissipation (cf.
[
32
, Section 2]
and
[
28
, Appendix]
).

In the convective case (i.e.,
\boldsymbol{v}
and
\boldsymbol{w}
are non-trivial), the energy identity (
1.4
) does, in general, not imply dissipation of the energy. However, if the velocity field is not just prescribed but determined by a Navier–Stokes equation (cf.
[
23
]
) or a Brinkman/Stokes equation (cf.
[
12
]
), an energy dissipation law for the corresponding total energy can be obtained.

Structure of this paper.

In Section
2
we collect some notation, assumptions, preliminaries and important tools. After introducing the notion of weak solutions of (
1.1
), our main results are stated in Section
3
. In Section
4
, we construct a weak solution in the case

via a Faedo–Galerkin approach. Afterwards, in Section

,

2.2  Assumptions

We make the following general assumptions.

(A1)

\Omega
is a non-empty, bounded Lipschitz domain in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
d\in\{2,3\}
, whose boundary is denoted by
\Gamma\coloneqq\partial\Omega
. Moreover,
T>0
denotes an arbitrary final time and for brevity, we use the notation

\displaystyle Q\coloneqq\Omega\times(0,T),\hskip 10.00002pt\Sigma\coloneqq\Gamma\times(0,T).

(A2)

The constants in system (
1.1
) satisfy
\epsilon,\epsilon_{\Gamma},\kappa>0
, and
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
with
\alpha\beta\left|\Omega\right|+\left|\Gamma\right|\neq 0
. (The latter condition is required to apply a certain bulk-surface Poincaré inequality, see
(P6)
.) Since the choice of
\epsilon_{\Gamma},\epsilon
and
\kappa
has no impact on the mathematical analysis, we will simply set (without loss of generality)
\epsilon=\epsilon_{\Gamma}=\kappa=1
in the remainder of this paper.

(A3)

The mobility functions
m_{\Omega}:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and
m_{\Gamma}:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
are bounded, continuous and uniformly positive. This means that there exist constants
m_{\Omega}^{\ast},M_{\Omega}^{\ast},m_{\Gamma}^{\ast},M_{\Gamma}^{\ast}>0
such that

\displaystyle 0<m_{\Omega}^{\ast}\leq m_{\Omega}(s)\leq M_{\Omega}^{\ast}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt0<m_{\Gamma}^{\ast}\leq m_{\Gamma}(s)\leq M_{\Gamma}^{\ast}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for all $s\in\mathbb{R}$}.

(A4)

The potentials
F:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow[0,\infty)
and
G:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow[0,\infty)
are continuously differentiable and there exist exponents
p
and
q
satisfying

\displaystyle p\in\begin{cases}[2,\infty),&\text{if }d=2,\\
[2,6],&\text{if }d=3,\end{cases}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002ptq\in[2,\infty)

as well as constants
c_{F^{\prime}},c_{G^{\prime}}\geq 0
such that the first-order derivatives satisfy the growth conditions

\displaystyle\left|F^{\prime}(s)\right|

\displaystyle\leq c_{F^{\prime}}(1+\left|s\right|^{p-1}),

(2.1)

\displaystyle\left|G^{\prime}(s)\right|

\displaystyle\leq c_{G^{\prime}}(1+\left|s\right|^{q-1})

(2.2)

for all
s\in\mathbb{R}
.
These assumptions already imply the existence of constants
c_{F},c_{G}\geq 0
such that
F
and
G
satisfy the growth conditions

\displaystyle F(s)

\displaystyle\leq c_{F}(1+\left|s\right|^{p}),

(2.3)

\displaystyle G(s)

\displaystyle\leq c_{G}(1+\left|s\right|^{q})

(2.4)

for all
s\in\mathbb{R}
.

(A5)

There exist constants
a_{F},a_{G}>0
and
b_{F},b_{G}\geq 0
such that the potentials
F
and
G
introduced in
(A4)
satisfy

\displaystyle F(s)

\displaystyle\geq a_{F}\left|s\right|^{2}-b_{F},

(2.5)

\displaystyle G(s)

\displaystyle\geq a_{G}\left|s\right|^{2}-b_{G}

(2.6)

for all
s\in\mathbb{R}
.

(A6)

The potentials
F
and
G
introduced in
(A4)
are twice continuously differentiable, and there exist constants
c_{F^{\prime\prime}},c_{G^{\prime\prime}}\geq 0
such that the second-order derivatives of
F
and
G
satisfy the growth conditions

\displaystyle\left|F^{\prime\prime}(s)\right|

\displaystyle\leq c_{F^{\prime\prime}}(1+\left|s\right|^{p-2}),

(2.7)

\displaystyle\left|G^{\prime\prime}(s)\right|

\displaystyle\leq c_{G^{\prime\prime}}(1+\left|s\right|^{q-2})

(2.8)

for all
s\in\mathbb{R}
.
We point out that these assumptions already imply the growth conditions (
2.1
)–(
2.4
) stated in
(A4)
.

Remark 2.1
.

A standard choice for
F
and
G
is the polynomial double-well potential

W:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R},\hskip 10.00002ptW(s)=\tfrac{1}{4}(s^{2}-1)^{2}.

It satisfies the assumptions
(A4)
–
(A6)
with
p=q=4
. However, singular potentials such as the logarithmic Flory–Huggins potential or the double-obstacle potential are not admissible as they do not satisfy any polynomial growth condition.
Nevertheless, the investigation of system (
1.1
) with singular potentials is an interesting topic for future research.

2.3  Preliminaries

(P1)

For any real numbers
s\geq 0
and
p\in[1,\infty]
, we set

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{p}\coloneqq L^{p}(\Omega)\times L^{p}(\Gamma),\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\mathcal{H}^{s}\coloneqq H^{s}(\Omega)\times H^{s}(\Gamma),

provided that the boundary is sufficiently regular.
As usual, we identify
\mathcal{L}^{2}
with
\mathcal{H}^{0}
. Note that
\mathcal{H}^{s}
is a Hilbert space with respect to the inner product

\displaystyle\big(\left(\phi,\psi\right),\left(\zeta,\xi\right)\big)_{\mathcal{H}^{s}}\coloneqq\left(\phi,\zeta\right)_{H^{s}(\Omega)}+\left(\psi,\xi\right)_{H^{s}(\Gamma)}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for all }\left(\phi,\psi\right),\left(\zeta,\xi\right)\in\mathcal{H}^{s}

and its induced norm
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{H}^{s}}\coloneqq\left(\cdot,\cdot\right)_{\mathcal{H}^{s}}^{1/2}
. We recall that the duality pairing can be expressed as

\displaystyle\langle\left(\phi,\psi\right),\left(\zeta,\xi\right)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}^{1}}=\left(\phi,\zeta\right)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\left(\psi,\xi\right)_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}

if
\left(\phi,\psi\right)\in\mathcal{L}^{2}
and
\left(\zeta,\xi\right)\in\mathcal{H}^{1}
.

(P2)

Let
L\in[0,\infty]
and
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
. We introduce the closed linear subspace

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\beta}\coloneqq\{(\phi,\psi)\in\mathcal{H}^{1}:\phi=\beta\psi\text{ a.e.\penalty\ on }\Gamma\}\subset\mathcal{H}^{1}

and define

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{L,\beta}^{1}\coloneqq\begin{cases}\mathcal{H}^{1},&\text{if }L\in(0,\infty],\\
\mathcal{D}_{\beta},&\text{if }L=0.\end{cases}

Endowed with the inner product
\left(\cdot,\cdot\right)_{\mathcal{H}_{L,\beta}^{1}}\coloneqq\left(\cdot,\cdot\right)_{\mathcal{H}^{1}}
and its induced norm, the space
\mathcal{H}_{L,\beta}^{1}
is a Hilbert space. Moreover, we define the product

\displaystyle\langle\left(\phi,\psi\right),\left(\zeta,\xi\right)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{L,\beta}^{1}}\coloneqq\left(\phi,\zeta\right)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\left(\psi,\xi\right)_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}

for all
\left(\phi,\psi\right),\left(\zeta,\xi\right)\in\mathcal{L}^{2}
. By means of the Riesz representation theorem, this product can be extended to a duality pairing on
(\mathcal{H}_{L,\beta}^{1})^{\prime}\times\mathcal{H}_{L,\beta}^{1}
, which will also be denoted as
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{L,\beta}^{1}}
.

(P3)

Let
L\in[0,\infty]
and
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
. We define the closed linear subspace

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{L,\beta}^{1}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\begin{cases}\{\left(\phi,\psi\right)\in\mathcal{H}^{1}_{L,\beta}:\beta\left|\Omega\right|{\langle\phi\rangle}_{\Omega}+\left|\Gamma\right|{\langle\psi\rangle}_{\Gamma}=0\},&\text{if\penalty\ }L\in[0,\infty),\\
\{\left(\phi,\psi\right)\in\mathcal{H}^{1}:{\langle\phi\rangle}_{\Omega}={\langle\psi\rangle}_{\Gamma}=0\},&\text{if\penalty\ }L=\infty.\end{cases}

Note that
\mathcal{V}_{L,\beta}^{1}
is a Hilbert space with respect to the inner product
\left(\cdot,\cdot\right)_{\mathcal{H}^{1}}
and its induced norm.

(P4)

Let
L\in[0,\infty]
and
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
. We set

\displaystyle\mathds{h}(L)\coloneqq\begin{cases}L^{-1},&\text{if }L\in(0,\infty),\\
0,&\text{if }L\in\{0,\infty\},\end{cases}

and we define a bilinear form on
\mathcal{H}^{1}\times\mathcal{H}^{1}
by

\displaystyle\big(\left(\phi,\psi\right),\left(\zeta,\xi\right)\big)_{L,\beta}\coloneqq

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\nabla\phi\cdot\nabla\zeta\;\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Gamma}\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi\cdot\nabla_{\Gamma}\xi\;\mathrm{d}\Gamma

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt+\mathds{h}(L)\int_{\Gamma}(\beta\psi-\phi)(\beta\xi-\zeta)\;\mathrm{d}\Gamma

for all
\left(\phi,\psi\right),\left(\zeta,\xi\right)\in\mathcal{H}^{1}
. Moreover, we set

\displaystyle\|\left(\psi,\phi\right)\|_{L,\beta}\coloneqq\big(\left(\phi,\psi\right),\left(\phi,\psi\right)\big)_{L,\beta}^{1/2}

for all
\left(\phi,\psi\right)\in\mathcal{H}^{1}
. The bilinear form
\left(\cdot,\cdot\right)_{L,\beta}
defines an inner product on
\mathcal{V}^{1}_{L,\beta}
, and
\|\cdot\|_{L,\beta}
defines a norm on
\mathcal{V}^{1}_{L,\beta}
, that is equivalent to the norm
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{H}^{1}}
(see
[
30
, Corollary A.2]
). Moreover, the space
\big(\mathcal{V}^{1}_{L,\beta},\left(\cdot,\cdot\right)_{L,\beta},\|\cdot\|_{L,\beta}\big)
is a Hilbert space.

(P5)

For any
L\in[0,\infty]
and
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
, we define the space

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{L,\beta}^{-1}\coloneqq\begin{cases}\{\left(\phi,\psi\right)\in(\mathcal{H}^{1}_{L,\beta})^{\prime}:\beta\left|\Omega\right|{\langle\phi\rangle}_{\Omega}+\left|\Gamma\right|{\langle\psi\rangle}_{\Gamma}=0\},&\text{if\penalty\ }L\in[0,\infty),\\
\{\left(\phi,\psi\right)\in(\mathcal{H}^{1})^{\prime}:{\langle\phi\rangle}_{\Omega}={\langle\psi\rangle}_{\Gamma}=0\},&\text{if\penalty\ }L=\infty.\end{cases}

Using the Lax-Milgram theorem, one can show that for any
\left(\phi,\psi\right)\in\mathcal{V}_{L,\beta}^{-1}
, there exists a unique weak solution
\mathcal{S}_{L,\beta}(\phi,\psi)=\big(\mathcal{S}_{L,\beta}^{\Omega}(\phi,\psi),\mathcal{S}_{L,\beta}^{\Gamma}(\phi,\psi)\big)\in\mathcal{V}^{1}_{L,\beta}
to the following elliptic problem with bulk-surface coupling:

\displaystyle-\Delta\mathcal{S}_{L,\beta}^{\Omega}

\displaystyle=-\phi\hskip 20.00003pt

\displaystyle\text{in }\Omega,

(2.9a)

\displaystyle-\Delta_{\Gamma}\mathcal{S}_{L,\beta}^{\Gamma}+\beta\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\mathcal{S}_{L,\beta}^{\Omega}

\displaystyle=-\psi

\displaystyle\text{on }\Gamma,

(2.9b)

\displaystyle L\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\mathcal{S}_{L,\beta}^{\Omega}

\displaystyle=\beta\mathcal{S}_{L,\beta}^{\Gamma}-\mathcal{S}_{L,\beta}^{\Omega}\hskip 20.00003pt

\displaystyle\text{on }\Gamma.

(2.9c)

This means that
\mathcal{S}_{L,\beta}(\phi,\psi)
satisfies the weak formulation

\displaystyle\big(S_{L,\beta}(\phi,\psi),\left(\zeta,\xi\right)\big)_{L,\beta}=-\langle\left(\phi,\psi\right),\left(\zeta,\xi\right)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}^{1}_{L,\beta}}

for all test functions
\left(\xi,\zeta\right)\in\mathcal{H}^{1}_{L,\beta}
. Consequently, we have

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{S}_{L,\beta}(\phi,\psi)\|_{\mathcal{H}^{1}}\leq C\|\left(\phi,\psi\right)\|_{(\mathcal{H}^{1}_{L,\beta})^{\prime}}

(2.10)

for all
\left(\phi,\psi\right)\in\mathcal{V}^{-1}_{L,\beta}
, for a constant
C\geq 0
depending only on
\Omega
,
L
and
\beta
. We can thus define the solution operator

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{L,\beta}:\mathcal{V}_{L,\beta}^{-1}\rightarrow\mathcal{V}^{1}_{L,\beta},\hskip 10.00002pt\left(\phi,\psi\right)\mapsto\mathcal{S}_{L,\beta}(\phi,\psi)=\big(\mathcal{S}_{L,\beta}^{\Omega}(\phi,\psi),\mathcal{S}_{L,\beta}^{\Gamma}(\phi,\psi)\big),

as well as an inner product and its induced norm on
\mathcal{V}^{-1}_{L,\beta}
by

\displaystyle\big(\left(\phi,\psi\right),\left(\zeta,\xi\right)\big)_{L,\beta,\ast}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\big(\mathcal{S}_{L,\beta}(\phi,\psi),\mathcal{S}_{L,\beta}(\zeta,\xi)\big)_{L,\beta},

\displaystyle\|\left(\phi,\psi\right)\|_{L,\beta,\ast}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\big(\left(\phi,\psi\right),\left(\phi,\psi\right)\big)_{L,\beta,\ast}^{1/2},

for
\left(\phi,\psi\right),\left(\zeta,\xi\right)\in\mathcal{V}_{L,\beta}^{-1}
. This norm is equivalent to the norm
\|\cdot\|_{(\mathcal{H}^{1}_{L,\beta})^{\prime}}
on
\mathcal{V}_{L,\beta}^{-1}
. For the case
L\in(0,\infty)
, we refer the reader to
[
30
, Theorem 3.3 and Corollary 3.5]
for a proof of these statements. In the other cases, the results can be proven analogously.

(P6)

We further recall the following
bulk-surface Poincaré inequalitiy
, which has been established in
[
30
, Lemma A.1]
:

Let
K\in[0,\infty)
and
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
with
\alpha\beta\left|\Omega\right|+\left|\Gamma\right|\neq 0
be arbitrary. Then there exists a constant
C_{P}>0
depending only on
K,\alpha,\beta
and
\Omega
such that

\displaystyle\|\left(\phi,\psi\right)\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}}\leq C_{P}\|\left(\phi,\psi\right)\|_{K,\alpha}

(2.11)

for all
\left(\phi,\psi\right)\in\mathcal{H}^{1}_{K,\alpha}
satisfying
\beta\left|\Omega\right|{\langle\phi\rangle}_{\Omega}+\left|\Gamma\right|{\langle\psi\rangle}_{\Gamma}=0
.

We conclude this section by presenting a simple inequality that will be frequently used in our mathematical analysis.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
K\in[0,\infty]
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
. Then, it holds

\displaystyle\|\left(\zeta,\xi\right)\|^{2}_{\mathcal{L}^{2}}\leq 2\|\left(\zeta,\xi\right)\|_{(\mathcal{H}^{1}_{K,\alpha})^{\prime}}\|\left(\nabla\zeta,\nabla_{\Gamma}\xi\right)\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}}+\|\left(\zeta,\xi\right)\|_{(\mathcal{H}^{1}_{K,\alpha})^{\prime}}^{2}.

for all
\left(\zeta,\xi\right)\in\mathcal{H}^{1}_{K,\alpha}
.

Let
(\zeta,\xi)\in\mathcal{H}^{1}_{K,\alpha}
be arbitrary. Recalling the definition of
\mathcal{H}^{1}_{K,\alpha}
(see
(P2)
), we have

\|(\zeta,\xi)\|_{\mathcal{H}^{1}_{K,\alpha}}=\|(\zeta,\xi)\|_{\mathcal{H}^{1}}\leq\|(\zeta,\xi)\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}}+\|(\nabla\zeta,\nabla_{\Gamma}\xi)\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}}.

Using Young’s inequality, we deduce

\displaystyle\|\left(\zeta,\xi\right)\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\langle\left(\zeta,\xi\right),\left(\zeta,\xi\right)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}^{1}_{K,\alpha}}

\displaystyle\leq\|\left(\zeta,\xi\right)\|_{(\mathcal{H}^{1}_{K,\alpha})^{\prime}}\|\left(\zeta,\xi\right)\|_{\mathcal{H}^{1}_{K,\alpha}}

\displaystyle\leq\|\left(\zeta,\xi\right)\|_{(\mathcal{H}^{1}_{K,\alpha})^{\prime}}\|\left(\zeta,\xi\right)\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}}+\|\left(\zeta,\xi\right)\|_{(\mathcal{H}^{1}_{K,\alpha})^{\prime}}\|\left(\nabla\zeta,\nabla_{\Gamma}\xi\right)\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\|\left(\zeta,\xi\right)\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}}^{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}\|\left(\zeta,\xi\right)\|_{(\mathcal{H}^{1}_{K,\alpha})^{\prime}}^{2}+\|\left(\zeta,\xi\right)\|_{(\mathcal{H}^{1}_{K,\alpha})^{\prime}}\|\left(\nabla\zeta,\nabla_{\Gamma}\xi\right)\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}}.

Hence, the claim directly follows.
∎

(3.1g)

\displaystyle\|\left(\mu_{m},\theta_{m}\right)\|_{L^{2}(0,T_{m};\mathcal{H}^{1})}\leq C.

(4.16)

Lastly, we derive a uniform estimate of the time derivatives. Therefore, we consider again
\left(\zeta,\xi\right)\coloneqq\left(\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{A}_{m}}(\zeta^{\ast}),\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{B}_{m}}(\xi^{\ast})\right)
for an arbitrary
\left(\zeta^{\ast},\xi^{\ast}\right)\in\mathcal{H}^{1}
. Recalling that the mobility functions
m_{\Omega}
and
m_{\Gamma}
are bounded (see
(A3)
), we use Hölder’s inequality as well as the continuous embeddings
H^{1}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow L^{6}(\Omega)
and
H^{1}(\Gamma)\hookrightarrow L^{6}(\Gamma)
to infer that

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left|\langle\left(\partial_{t}\phi_{m},\partial_{t}\psi_{m}\right),\left(\zeta^{\ast},\xi^{\ast}\right)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}^{1}}\right|&=\left|\langle\left(\partial_{t}\phi_{m},\partial_{t}\psi_{m}\right),\left(\zeta,\xi\right)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}^{1}}\right|\\
&\leq\|\phi_{m}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{L^{3}(\Omega)}\|\zeta\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}+C\|\mu_{m}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\|\zeta\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\\
&\quad+\|\psi_{m}\|_{H^{1}(\Gamma)}\|\boldsymbol{w}\|_{L^{3}(\Gamma)}\|\xi\|_{H^{1}(\Gamma)}+C\|\theta_{m}\|_{H^{1}(\Gamma)}\|\xi\|_{H^{1}(\Gamma)}\\
&\quad+L^{-1}\|\beta\theta_{m}-\mu_{m}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}\|\beta\xi-\zeta\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}\\
\end{split}

on
[0,T_{m}]
. Hence, after taking the supremum over all
\left(\zeta^{\ast},\xi^{\ast}\right)\in\mathcal{H}^{1}
satisfying
\|\left(\zeta^{\ast},\xi^{\ast}\right)\|_{\mathcal{H}^{1}}\leq 1
, taking the square of the resulting inequality and integrating in time over
[0,T_{m}]
, we deduce that

\displaystyle\|\left(\partial_{t}\phi_{m},\partial_{t}\psi_{m}\right)\|_{L^{2}(0,T_{m};(\mathcal{H}^{1})^{\prime})}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C\|\phi_{m}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T_{m};H^{1}(\Omega))}^{2}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{L^{2}(0,T_{m};L^{3}(\Omega))}^{2}

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt+C\|\psi_{m}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T_{m};H^{1}(\Gamma))}^{2}\|\boldsymbol{w}\|_{L^{2}(0,T_{m};L^{3}(\Gamma))}^{2}

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt+C\|\left(\nabla\mu_{m},\nabla_{\Gamma}\theta_{m}\right)\|_{L^{2}(0,T_{m};\mathcal{L}^{2})}^{2}

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt+C\|\beta\theta_{m}-\mu_{m}\|_{L^{2}(0,T_{m};L^{2}(\Gamma))}^{2}.

Exploiting the uniform estimates (
4.10
) and (
4.16
) we conclude

\displaystyle\|\left(\partial_{t}\phi_{m},\partial_{t}\psi_{m}\right)\|_{L^{2}(0,T_{m};(\mathcal{H}^{1})^{\prime})}\leq C.

(4.17)

Step 3: Extension of the approximate solution onto

[0,T]
. Using the definition of the approximate solution (
4.1
) and the uniform estimate (
4.10
), we obtain for any
T_{m}\in[0,T_{m}^{\ast})
, all
t\in[0,T_{m}]
and
j\in\{1,\ldots,m\}

\displaystyle\left|a_{j}^{m}(t)\right|+\left|b_{j}^{m}(t)\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\left(\phi_{m}(t),\zeta_{j}\right)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\right|+\left|\left(\psi_{m}(t),\xi_{j}\right)_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\|\phi_{m}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T_{m};L^{2}(\Omega))}+\|\psi_{m}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T_{m};L^{2}(\Gamma))}\leq C.

This shows that the solution
(\mathbf{a}^{m},\mathbf{b}^{m})^{\top}
is bounded on the time interval
[0,T_{m}^{\ast})
by a constant that is independent of
m
and
T_{m}^{\ast}
. Hence, by classical ODE theory, this allows us to extend the solution beyond the time
T_{m}^{\ast}
. However, as
\left(\mathbf{a}^{m},\mathbf{b}^{m}\right)^{\top}
was assumed to be non-extendable, this is a contradiction. We thus infer that the solution
\left(\mathbf{a}^{m},\mathbf{b}^{m}\right)^{\top}
actually exists on
[0,T]
. Since the coefficients
\left(\mathbf{c}^{m},\mathbf{d}^{m}\right)^{\top}
can be reconstructed from
(\mathbf{a}^{m},\mathbf{b}^{m})^{\top}
, we further conclude that the coefficients
(\mathbf{c}^{m},\mathbf{d}^{m})^{\top}
also exist on
[0,T]
. This automatically entails that the approximate solution
(\phi_{m},\psi_{m},\mu_{m},\theta_{m})
exists on
[0,T]
and satisfies the discretized weak formulation (
4.2
) on
[0,T]
for all
m\in\mathbb{N}
. Additionally, as the particular choice of
T_{m}
did not play any role in the derivation of the uniform estimates established in Step 2, we infer that the estimates (
4.10
), (
4.11
), (
4.15
), (
4.16
) and (
4.17
) remain true after replacing
T_{m}
with
T
. In summary, we conclude that for each
m\in\mathbb{N}
, the approximate solution
(\phi_{m},\psi_{m},\mu_{m},\theta_{m})
satisfies the uniform estimate

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\|\left(\partial_{t}\phi_{m},\partial_{t}\psi_{m}\right)\|_{L^{2}(0,T;(\mathcal{H}^{1})^{\prime})}+\|\left(\phi_{m},\psi_{m}\right)\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;\mathcal{H}^{1})}+\|\alpha\psi_{m}-\phi_{m}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Gamma))}\\
&\;\;+\|\left(\mu_{m},\theta_{m}\right)\|_{L^{2}(0,T;\mathcal{H}^{1})}+\|\left(\mu_{m},\theta_{m}\right)\|_{L^{4}(0,T;\mathcal{L}^{2})}+\|\beta\theta_{m}-\mu_{m}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Gamma))}\leq C.\end{split}

(4.18)

\phi,\psi,\mu
and
\theta

\displaystyle\left(\partial_{t}\phi_{m},\partial_{t}\psi_{m}\right)

\displaystyle\rightarrow\left(\partial_{t}\phi,\partial_{t}\psi\right)\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002pt

\displaystyle\text{weakly in }L^{2}(0,T;(\mathcal{H}^{1})^{\prime}),

(4.19)

\displaystyle\left(\phi_{m},\psi_{m}\right)

\displaystyle\rightarrow\left(\phi,\psi\right)

\displaystyle\text{weakly-star in }L^{\infty}(0,T;\mathcal{H}^{1}),

\displaystyle\text{strongly in }C([0,T];\mathcal{H}^{s})\text{ for all }s\in[0,1),

(4.20)

\displaystyle\left(\mu_{m},\theta_{m}\right)

\displaystyle\rightarrow\left(\mu,\theta\right)

\displaystyle\text{weakly in }L^{4}(0,T;\mathcal{L}^{2})\cap L^{2}(0,T;\mathcal{H}^{1}),

(4.21)

\displaystyle\beta\theta_{m}-\mu_{m}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\beta\theta-\mu

\displaystyle\text{weakly in }L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Gamma)),

(4.22)

\displaystyle\alpha\psi_{m}-\phi_{m}

\displaystyle\text{weakly-star in }L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Gamma)),

(4.23)

as

Using Sobolev’s embedding theorem, we infer from (

\displaystyle\phi_{m}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\phi\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002pt

\displaystyle\text{strongly in }C([0,T];L^{r}(\Omega))\text{ for all }r\in[2,6),\text{ and a.e.\penalty\ in }Q,

(4.24)

\displaystyle\psi_{m}

as

as

due to (

\displaystyle F^{\prime}(\phi_{m})\rightarrow F^{\prime}(\phi)\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002pt\text{weakly in }L^{6/5}(Q)\text{ and a.e.\penalty\ in }Q

(4.27)

as

. Recalling the growth conditions on

), the convergence in (

\displaystyle G(\psi_{m})

\displaystyle\rightarrow G(\psi)\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002pt

\displaystyle\text{strongly in }L^{1}(\Sigma),\text{ and a.e.\penalty\ on }\Sigma,

(4.28)

\displaystyle G^{\prime}(\psi_{m})

\displaystyle\rightarrow G^{\prime}(\psi)

\displaystyle\text{strongly in }L^{2}(\Sigma),\text{ and a.e.\penalty\ on }\Sigma,

(4.29)

as
m\rightarrow\infty
.
Furthermore, by means of Lebesgue’s dominated convergence theorem, it follows from (
4.24
), (

) and

\displaystyle m_{\Omega}(\phi_{m})

\displaystyle\rightarrow m_{\Omega}(\phi)\hskip 10.00002pt

\displaystyle\text{strongly in }L^{r}(Q),\text{ and a.e.\penalty\ in Q},

(4.30)

\displaystyle m_{\Gamma}(\psi_{m})

\displaystyle\rightarrow m_{\Gamma}(\psi)

\displaystyle\text{strongly in }L^{r}(\Sigma),\text{ and a.e.\penalty\ on }\Sigma,

(4.31)

for all

\displaystyle\sqrt{m_{\Omega}(\phi_{m})}\nabla\mu_{m}

\displaystyle\text{weakly in }L^{2}(Q),

(4.32)

\displaystyle\sqrt{m_{\Gamma}(\psi_{m})}\nabla_{\Gamma}\theta_{m}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\sqrt{m_{\Gamma}(\psi)}\nabla_{\Gamma}\theta

\displaystyle\text{weakly in }L^{2}(\Sigma),

(4.33)

\displaystyle m_{\Omega}(\phi_{m})\nabla\mu_{m}

\displaystyle\rightarrow m_{\Omega}(\phi)\nabla\mu

\displaystyle\text{weakly in }L^{2}(Q),

(4.34)

\displaystyle m_{\Gamma}(\psi_{m})\nabla_{\Gamma}\theta_{m}

\displaystyle\rightarrow m_{\Gamma}(\psi)\nabla_{\Gamma}\theta

\displaystyle\text{weakly in }L^{2}(\Sigma),

(4.35)

as
m\rightarrow\infty.

Lastly, exploiting the convergences (

), (
4.24
) and (
4.25
), and using

\displaystyle\phi_{m}\,\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\nabla\mu_{m}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\phi\,\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\nabla\mu\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002pt

\displaystyle\text{strongly in }L^{1}(Q),\text{ and a.e.\penalty\ in }Q,

(4.36)

\displaystyle\psi_{m}\,\boldsymbol{w}\cdot\nabla_{\Gamma}\theta_{m}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\psi\,\boldsymbol{w}\cdot\nabla_{\Gamma}\theta

\displaystyle\text{strongly in }L^{1}(\Sigma),\text{ and a.e.\penalty\ on }\Sigma,

as

.

hold for all

and all test functions
\sigma\in C_{c}^{\infty}([0,T])
. Since
\text{span}\{\left(\zeta_{j},\xi_{j}\right):j\in\mathbb{N}\}

, this proves that the quadruplet

satisfies the weak formulation (
3.4
) for all test functions
\left(\zeta,\xi\right)\in\mathcal{H}^{1}
. We thus conclude that Definition
3.1(iii)
is fulfilled.

Since the approximate solutions
\left(\phi_{m},\psi_{m}\right)
satisfy the discrete mass conservation law (see (
4.8
)) for all
m\in\mathbb{N}
, we can use the convergence (
4.20
) to deduce that
\left(\phi,\psi\right)
satisfies the mass conservation law (
3.5
) as well.
Thus, Definition
3.1(iv)
is fulfilled.

Concerning the initial conditions, we infer from (
4.3
), due to the convergence properties of the projections, that

\displaystyle\left(\phi_{m}(0),\psi_{m}(0)\right)\rightarrow\left(\phi_{0},\psi_{0}\right)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{strongly in }\mathcal{L}^{2}

as
m\rightarrow\infty
. Additionally, the convergences (
4.24
) and (
4.25
) yield

\displaystyle\left(\phi_{m}(0),\psi_{m}(0)\right)\rightarrow\left(\phi(0),\psi(0)\right)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{strongly in }\mathcal{L}^{2}

as

. We deduce that
\phi(0)=\phi_{0}
a.e. in
\Omega
and
\psi(0)=\psi_{0}
a.e. on
\Gamma
. This proves that Definition
3.1(ii)
is fulfilled.

To verify the energy inequality (
3.6
), we consider an arbitrary non-negative test function
\sigma\in C_{c}^{\infty}(0,T)
. Multiplying (
4.4
) with
\sigma
and integrating in time from
0
to
T
yields

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\int_{0}^{T}E_{K}(\phi_{m}(t),\psi_{m}(t))\,\sigma(t)\;\mathrm{d}t\\
&\quad\quad+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\left(m_{\Omega}(\phi_{m})\left|\nabla\mu_{m}\right|^{2}-\phi_{m}\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\nabla\mu_{m}\right)\sigma(t)\;\mathrm{d}x\;\mathrm{d}s\;\mathrm{d}t\\
&\quad\quad+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Gamma}\left(m_{\Gamma}(\psi_{m})\left|\nabla_{\Gamma}\theta_{m}\right|^{2}-\psi_{m}\boldsymbol{w}\cdot\nabla_{\Gamma}\theta_{m}\right)\sigma(t)\;\mathrm{d}\Gamma\;\mathrm{d}s\;\mathrm{d}t\\
&\quad\quad+L^{-1}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Gamma}(\beta\theta_{m}-\mu_{m})^{2}\sigma(t)\;\mathrm{d}\Gamma\;\mathrm{d}s\;\mathrm{d}t\\
&\ =\int_{0}^{T}E_{K}(\phi_{m}(0),\psi_{m}(0))\,\sigma(t)\;\mathrm{d}t.\end{split}

(4.39)

As
m\rightarrow\infty
, we infer from (
4.28
) that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\left(\int_{\Gamma}G(\psi_{m})\;\mathrm{d}\Gamma\right)\sigma(t)\;\mathrm{d}t\rightarrow\int_{0}^{T}\left(\int_{\Gamma}G(\psi)\;\mathrm{d}\Gamma\right)\sigma(t)\;\mathrm{d}t.

(4.40)

Additionally, using (
4.24
) and
(A4)
, Fatou’s lemma implies

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\left(\int_{\Omega}F(\phi)\;\mathrm{d}x\right)\sigma(t)\;\mathrm{d}t\leq\liminf_{m\rightarrow\infty}\int_{0}^{T}\left(\int_{\Omega}F(\phi_{m})\;\mathrm{d}x\right)\sigma(t)\;\mathrm{d}t.

(4.41)

Moreover, from the strong convergence in (
4.26
) we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\left(\int_{\Gamma}\left|\alpha\psi_{m}-\phi_{m}\right|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}\Gamma\right)\sigma(t)\;\mathrm{d}t\rightarrow\int_{0}^{T}\left(\int_{\Gamma}\left|\alpha\psi-\phi\right|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}\Gamma\right)\sigma(t)\;\mathrm{d}t,

(4.42)

as
m\rightarrow\infty
. Lastly, in view of (
4.20
) and the weak lower semicontinuity of norms, we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\int_{0}^{T}\left(\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla\phi\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}^{2}\right)\sigma(t)\;\mathrm{d}t\\
&\quad\leq\liminf_{m\rightarrow\infty}\int_{0}^{T}\left(\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla\phi_{m}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi_{m}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}\right)\sigma(t)\;\mathrm{d}t.\end{split}

(4.43)

Combining (
4.40
)–(
4.43
) yields

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}E_{K}(\phi(t),\psi(t))\,\sigma(t)\;\mathrm{d}t\leq\liminf_{m\rightarrow\infty}\int_{0}^{T}E_{K}(\phi_{m}(t),\psi_{m}(t))\,\sigma(t)\;\mathrm{d}t.

(4.44)

As a consequence of (
4.32
), (
4.33
) and the weak lower semicontinuity of norms with respect to the weak convergence, another application of Fatou’s lemma entails that

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}m_{\Omega}(\phi)\left|\nabla\mu\right|^{2}\,\sigma(t)\;\mathrm{d}x\;\mathrm{d}s\;\mathrm{d}t+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Gamma}m_{\Gamma}(\psi)\left|\nabla_{\Gamma}\theta\right|^{2}\,\sigma(t)\;\mathrm{d}\Gamma\;\mathrm{d}s\;\mathrm{d}t\\
&\leq\liminf_{m\rightarrow\infty}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}m_{\Omega}(\phi_{m})\left|\nabla\mu_{m}\right|^{2}\,\sigma(t)\;\mathrm{d}x\;\mathrm{d}s\;\mathrm{d}t\right.\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\left.+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Gamma}m_{\Gamma}(\psi_{m})\left|\nabla_{\Gamma}\theta_{m}\right|^{2}\,\sigma(t)\;\mathrm{d}\Gamma\;\mathrm{d}s\;\mathrm{d}t\right].\end{split}

(4.45)

Further, due to the convergences (
4.36
) and (
4.37
), we find that

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\phi_{m}\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\nabla\mu_{m}\,\sigma(t)\;\mathrm{d}x\;\mathrm{d}s\;\mathrm{d}t+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Gamma}\psi_{m}\boldsymbol{w}\cdot\nabla_{\Gamma}\theta_{m}\,\sigma(t)\;\mathrm{d}\Gamma\;\mathrm{d}s\;\mathrm{d}t\\
&\quad\longrightarrow\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\phi\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\nabla\mu\,\sigma(t)\;\mathrm{d}x\;\mathrm{d}s\;\mathrm{d}t+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Gamma}\psi\boldsymbol{w}\cdot\nabla_{\Gamma}\theta\,\sigma(t)\;\mathrm{d}\Gamma\;\mathrm{d}s\;\mathrm{d}t\end{split}

(4.46)

as
m\rightarrow\infty
.
Additionally, as convex and continuous functionals are weakly lower semicontinuous, we conclude from (

) that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Gamma}(\beta\theta-\mu)^{2}\,\sigma(t)\;\mathrm{d}\Gamma\;\mathrm{d}s\;\mathrm{d}t\leq\liminf_{m\rightarrow\infty}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Gamma}(\beta\theta_{m}-\mu_{m})^{2}\,\sigma(t)\;\mathrm{d}\Gamma\;\mathrm{d}s\;\mathrm{d}t.

(4.47)

Lastly, recalling the growth conditions on
F
and
G
(see
(A4)
), we use the initial conditions (
4.3
) as well as the convergence properties of the projections
\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{A}_{m}}
and
\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{B}_{m}}

\displaystyle\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}E_{K}(\phi_{m}(0),\psi_{m}(0))=E_{K}(\phi_{0},\psi_{0}).

(4.48)

Collecting (
4.45

4.48

\displaystyle\begin{split}&E_{K}(\phi(t),\psi(t))+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}m_{\Omega}(\phi)\left|\nabla\mu\right|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x\;\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Gamma}m_{\Gamma}(\psi)\left|\nabla_{\Gamma}\theta\right|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}\Gamma\;\mathrm{d}s\\
&\quad-\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\phi\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\nabla\mu\;\mathrm{d}x\;\mathrm{d}s-\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Gamma}\psi\boldsymbol{w}\cdot\nabla_{\Gamma}\theta\;\mathrm{d}\Gamma\;\mathrm{d}s\\
&\quad+L^{-1}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Gamma}(\beta\theta-\mu)^{2}\;\mathrm{d}\Gamma\;\mathrm{d}s\\
&\leq E_{K}(\phi_{0},\psi_{0})\end{split}

(4.49)

for almost every
t\in[0,T]
. To prove that (
4.49
) holds for every
t\in[0,T]
, first note that all integral terms in (
4.49
) depend continuously on time.
Furthermore, due to
(\phi,\psi)\in C([0,T];\mathcal{L}^{2})
, we deduce that the following functions

\displaystyle t\mapsto\|\nabla\phi(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2},\hskip 10.00002ptt\mapsto\|\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}^{2},\hskip 10.00002ptt\mapsto\int_{\Omega}F(\phi(t))\;\mathrm{d}x,\hskip 10.00002ptt\mapsto\int_{\Gamma}G(\psi(t))\;\mathrm{d}\Gamma

are lower semicontinuous on
[0,T]
. For the first two functions, this is a consequence of the lower semicontinuity of the respective norms, while for the last two functions, it follows from Fatou’s lemma. This already entails that the weak solution
(\phi,\psi,\mu,\theta)
satisfies the energy inequality for
all
times
t\in[0,T]
. Thus, Definition
3.1(v)
is fulfilled.

We have therefore shown that the quadruplet
(\phi,\psi,\mu,\theta)
is a weak solution of system (
3.1
) in the sense of Definition
3.1
.

Also note that, while we do not have the bulk-surface Poincaré inequality at our disposal anymore, we can use the standard Poincaré inequality for functions with vanishing mean, since the approximate solutions satisfy the mass conservation law (

5  Asymptotic limits and existence of weak solutions to the limit models

In this section, we investigate the asymptotic limits

and

and

Remark 5.1
.

In this section, we will need the additional assumption

when approaching the limit cases

and

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\left|\nabla\phi_{K_{m}}\right|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}F(\phi_{K_{m}})\;\mathrm{d}x+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Gamma}\left|\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi_{K_{m}}\right|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}\Gamma+\int_{\Gamma}G(\psi_{K_{m}})\;\mathrm{d}\Gamma\\

) for

\displaystyle\|\left(\mu_{K_{m}},\theta_{K_{m}}\right)\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}^{\prime})}\leq C.

\displaystyle\|\left(\mu_{K_{m}},\theta_{K_{m}}\right)\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}}^{2}\leq 2\|\left(\mu_{K_{m}},\theta_{K_{m}}\right)\|_{\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}^{\prime}}\|\left(\nabla\mu_{K_{m}},\nabla_{\Gamma}\theta_{K_{m}}\right)\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}}+\|\left(\mu_{K_{m}},\theta_{K_{m}}\right)\|_{\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}^{\prime}}^{2}.

Lastly, proceeding similarly as in the derivation of (

) and estimate (

) that

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\|\left(\partial_{t}\phi_{K_{m}},\partial_{t}\psi_{K_{m}}\right)\|_{L^{2}(0,T;(\mathcal{H}^{1})^{\prime})}+\|\left(\phi_{K_{m}},\psi_{K_{m}}\right)\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;\mathcal{H}^{1})}\\

Using the Banach–Alaoglu theorem and the Aubin–Lions–Simon lemma, we deduce the existence of functions

\displaystyle\left(\partial_{t}\phi_{K_{m}},\partial_{t}\psi_{K_{m}}\right)

\displaystyle\rightarrow\left(\partial_{t}\phi_{\ast},\partial_{t}\psi_{\ast}\right)\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002pt

\displaystyle\text{weakly in }L^{2}(0,T;(\mathcal{H}^{1})^{\prime}),

(5.8)

\displaystyle\left(\phi_{K_{m}},\psi_{K_{m}}\right)

\displaystyle\rightarrow\left(\phi_{\ast},\psi_{\ast}\right)

\displaystyle\text{weakly-star in }L^{\infty}(0,T;\mathcal{H}^{1}),

\displaystyle\text{strongly in }C([0,T];\mathcal{H}^{s})\text{ for all }s\in[0,1),

and the fact that
\left(\phi,\psi\right)\in L^{\infty}(0,T;\mathcal{H}^{1}_{K,\alpha})
to derive the estimate

\displaystyle\|F^{\prime}(\phi(t))\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq C+C\|\phi(t)\|^{p-1}_{L^{2(p-1)}(\Omega)}\leq C,

(6.11)

as well as

\displaystyle\|G^{\prime}(\psi(t))\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}\leq C+C\|\psi(t)\|^{q-1}_{L^{2(q-1)}(\Gamma)}\leq C,

(6.12)

for almost all
t\in[0,T]
. In particular, the estimates (
6.11
) and (
6.12
) imply
\left(f(t),g(t)\right)\in\mathcal{L}^{2}
with

\displaystyle\|f(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq C+\|\mu(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)},

(6.13)

\displaystyle\|g(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}

\displaystyle\leq C+\|\theta(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma)},

(6.14)

for almost all
t\in[0,T]
.

We first consider the case
k=2
. In the case
K\in[0,\infty)
, we deduce from regularity theory for elliptic problems with bulk-surface coupling (see
[
30
, Theorem 3.3]
) that
\left(\phi(t),\psi(t)\right)\in\mathcal{H}^{2}
with

\displaystyle\begin{split}\|\left(\phi(t),\psi(t)\right)\|_{\mathcal{H}^{2}}^{2}&\leq C\|\left(f(t),g(t)\right)\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}}^{2}\\
&\leq C\Big(\|\mu(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|\theta(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}^{2}+\|F^{\prime}(\phi(t))\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|G^{\prime}(\psi(t))\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}^{2}\Big)\\
&\leq C+C\|\mu(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+C\|\theta(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}^{2}\end{split}

for almost all
t\in[0,T]
. Integrating this inequality in time from
0
to
T
, we use the regularity of
\left(\mu,\theta\right)
to infer that
\left(\phi,\psi\right)\in L^{2}(0,T;\mathcal{H}^{2})
. In the case
K\in(0,\infty)
, we even obtain
\left(\phi,\psi\right)\in L^{4}(0,T;\mathcal{H}^{2})
since
\left(\mu,\theta\right)\in L^{4}(0,T;\mathcal{L}^{2})
.

If
K=\infty
, we may choose

\left(\eta,\vartheta\right)=\left(1,0\right)\in\mathcal{H}^{1}
as a test function (
6.7
), which yields

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}f(t)\;\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}\mu(t)-F^{\prime}(\phi(t))\;\mathrm{d}x=0

for almost all
t\in[0,T]
. This allows us to use regularity theory for Poisson’s equations with homogeneous Neumann boundary condition (see, e.g.
[
40
, s.5, Proposition 7.7]
) to infer that
\phi(t)\in H^{2}(\Omega)
with

\displaystyle\|\phi(t)\|_{H^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C\|f(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+C\|\phi(t)\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}

for almost all
t\in[0,T]
. In combination with (
6.13
) we deduce that

\displaystyle\|\phi(t)\|_{H^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C+C\|\mu(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

(6.15)

for almost all
t\in[0,T]
.
Next, due to (
6.14
), we can apply regularity theory for elliptic equations on submanifolds (see, e.g.,
[
40
, s.5, Theorem 1.3]
) for (
6.10
) and infer that
\psi(t)\in H^{2}(\Gamma)
with

\displaystyle\|\psi(t)\|_{H^{2}(\Gamma)}\leq C\|g(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}+C\|\psi(t)\|_{H^{1}(\Gamma)}\leq C+C\|\theta(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}

(6.16)

for almost all
t\in[0,T]
. Squaring the equations (
6.15
) and (
6.16
) and integrating in time over
[0,T]
yields
\left(\phi,\psi\right)\in L^{2}(0,T;\mathcal{H}^{2})
which proves the assertion in the case
K=\infty
. Additionally, as
\left(\mu,\theta\right)\in L^{4}(0,T;\mathcal{L}^{2})
, we obtain
\left(\phi,\psi\right)\in L^{4}(0,T;\mathcal{H}^{2})
.

Let us now consider the case
k=3
. Since
p\leq 4
, we use Hölder’s inequality and the Sobolev embedding
H^{1}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow L^{6}(\Omega)
to derive the estimate

\displaystyle\begin{split}\|F^{\prime\prime}(\phi(t))\nabla\phi(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}&\leq C\|\nabla\phi(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+C\|\left|\phi(t)\right|^{4}\nabla\phi(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\\
&\leq C\|\nabla\phi(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+C\|\phi(t)\|_{L^{6}(\Omega)}^{2}\|\nabla\phi(t)\|_{L^{6}(\Omega)}\\
&\leq C+C\|\phi(t)\|_{H^{2}(\Omega)}.\end{split}

for almost all
t\in[0,T]
. In combination with (
6.11
) this yields

\displaystyle\|F^{\prime}(\phi(t))\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\leq\|F^{\prime}(\phi(t))\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|F^{\prime\prime}(\phi(t))\nabla\phi(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C+C\|\phi(t)\|_{H^{2}(\Omega)}

(6.17)

for almost all
t\in[0,T]
. Proceeding similarly, we derive the estimate

\displaystyle\|G^{\prime\prime}(\psi(t))\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}\leq C+C\|\psi(t)\|_{H^{2}(\Gamma)},

which leads to

\displaystyle\|G^{\prime}(\psi(t))\|_{H^{1}(\Gamma)}\leq C+C\|\psi(t)\|_{H^{2}(\Gamma)}

(6.18)

for almost all
t\in[0,T]
. We thus obtain
\left(f(t),g(t)\right)\in\mathcal{H}^{1}
. If
K\in[0,\infty)
we may use again regularity theory for elliptic problems with bulk-surface coupling (see
[
30
, Theorem 3.3]
) to infer
\left(\phi(t),\psi(t)\right)\in\mathcal{H}^{3}
with

\displaystyle\begin{split}\|\left(\phi(t),\psi(t)\right)\|_{\mathcal{H}^{3}}^{2}&\leq C\|\left(f(t),g(t)\right)\|_{\mathcal{H}^{1}}^{2}\\
&\leq C\|\mu(t)\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}+C\|\theta(t)\|_{H^{1}(\Gamma)}^{2}+C\|F^{\prime}(\phi(t))\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}+C\|G^{\prime}(\psi(t))\|_{H^{1}(\Gamma)}^{2}\\
&\leq C+C\|\mu(t)\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}+C\|\theta(t)\|_{H^{1}(\Gamma)}^{2}+C\|\phi(t)\|_{H^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+C\|\psi(t)\|_{H^{2}(\Gamma)}^{2}\end{split}

for almost all
t\in[0,T]
. Integrating this inequality in time over
[0,T]
yields
\left(\phi,\psi\right)\in L^{2}(0,T;\mathcal{H}^{3})
.

If
K=\infty
, the result can be established analogously to the case
k=2
as the regularity results cited above hold true for any integer
k\geq 0
. We again find that

\displaystyle\|\left(\phi(t),\psi(t)\right)\|_{\mathcal{H}^{3}}\leq C\|\left(f(t),g(t)\right)\|_{\mathcal{H}^{1}}

for almost all
t\in[0,T]
, where the right-hand side is bounded due to (
6.17
) and (
6.18
).

This means that (
6.1
) and (
6.2
) are established. If
(K,L)\in[0,\infty]\times(0,\infty]
and
k=3
, the additional continuity property (
6.3
) follows from Proposition
A.1
. Therefore, the proof is complete.
∎

Remark 6.2
.

In the other cases, where the boundary conditions involving
K
and
L
are of the same type (i.e.,
K=L=0
or
K=L=\infty
), it should also be possible to obtain the regularities (
6.1
) and (
6.2
) even for
d=3
and
p\in(4,6)
. This is because in these cases, the system (
1.1
) can be discretized by a Faedo–Galerkin scheme (cf. Remark
4.2
) and therefore, the higher order regularity estimates can be performed on the level of the approximate solutions.
In the case
K=L=0
, we refer the reader to the proof of
[
23
, Theorem 3.3]
, where this line of argument is carried out in detail.

6.2  Continuous dependence and uniqueness

As the functions
m_{\Omega}
and
m_{\Gamma}
are constant, we assume, without loss of generality, that
m_{\Omega}\equiv 1
and
m_{\Gamma}\equiv 1
. In the following, we use the letter
C
to denote generic positive constants depending only on
\Omega
, the parameters of the system (
3.1
), and the initial data and the prescribed velocity field.

We consider two weak solutions
(\phi_{1},\psi_{1},\mu_{1},\theta_{1})
and
(\phi_{2},\psi_{2},\mu_{2},\theta_{2})
corresponding to the initial data
\left(\phi_{0}^{1},\psi_{0}^{1}\right),\left(\phi_{0}^{2},\psi_{0}^{2}\right)\in\mathcal{H}^{1}_{K,\alpha}
, the bulk velocity fields
\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\boldsymbol{v}_{2}\in L^{2}(0,T;\mathbf{L}_{\textnormal{div}}^{3}(\Omega))
and the surface velocity fields
\boldsymbol{w}_{1},\boldsymbol{w}_{2}\in L^{2}(0,T;\mathbf{L}^{3}(\Gamma))
, respectively, and set

\displaystyle(\phi,\psi,\mu,\theta,\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w})\coloneqq(\phi_{2}-\phi_{1},\psi_{2}-\psi_{1},\mu_{2}-\mu_{1},\theta_{2}-\theta_{1},\boldsymbol{v}_{2}-\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\boldsymbol{w}_{2}-\boldsymbol{w}_{1}).

Then, due to their respective weak formulations (
3.4
), the quadruplet
(\phi,\psi,\mu,\theta)
satisfies

\displaystyle\langle\left(\partial_{t}\phi,\partial_{t}\psi\right),\left(\zeta,\xi\right)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{L,\beta}^{1}}-\int_{\Omega}\phi_{2}\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\nabla\zeta\;\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\Omega}\phi\boldsymbol{v}_{1}\cdot\nabla\zeta\;\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt-\int_{\Gamma}\psi_{2}\boldsymbol{w}\cdot\nabla_{\Gamma}\xi\;\mathrm{d}\Gamma-\int_{\Gamma}\psi\boldsymbol{w}_{1}\cdot\nabla_{\Gamma}\xi\;\mathrm{d}\Gamma

(6.19a)

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt=-\int_{\Omega}\nabla\mu\cdot\nabla\zeta\;\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\Gamma}\nabla_{\Gamma}\theta\cdot\nabla_{\Gamma}\xi\;\mathrm{d}\Gamma-\mathds{h}(L)\int_{\Gamma}(\beta\theta-\mu)(\beta\xi-\zeta)\;\mathrm{d}\Gamma,

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\int_{\Omega}\mu\,\eta\;\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Gamma}\theta\,\vartheta\;\mathrm{d}\Gamma-\int_{\Omega}\big[F^{\prime}(\phi_{2})-F^{\prime}(\phi_{1})\big]\eta\;\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\Gamma}\big[G^{\prime}(\psi_{2})-G^{\prime}(\psi_{1})\big]\vartheta\;\mathrm{d}\Gamma\\
&\quad=\int_{\Omega}\nabla\phi\cdot\nabla\eta\;\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Gamma}\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi\cdot\nabla_{\Gamma}\vartheta\;\mathrm{d}\Gamma+\mathds{h}(K)\int_{\Gamma}(\alpha\psi-\phi)(\alpha\vartheta-\eta)\;\mathrm{d}\Gamma,\end{split}

(6.19b)

a.e. on
[0,T]
for all
\left(\zeta,\xi\right)\in\mathcal{H}^{1}_{L,\beta}
and
\left(\eta,\vartheta\right)\in\mathcal{H}^{1}_{K,\alpha}
.
Now, due to (
3.11
), we have

\displaystyle\beta\left|\Omega\right|{\langle\phi\rangle}_{\Omega}+\left|\Gamma\right|{\langle\psi\rangle}_{\Gamma}=0\hskip 10.00002pt\text{a.e.\penalty\ on\penalty\ }[0,T].

This shows that the pair
\left(\phi(t),\psi(t)\right)
is an admissible right-hand side in the elliptic problem with bulk-surface coupling (
2.9
) for almost all
t\in[0,T]
. Further, testing the weak formulation (
6.19
) with
\left(\zeta,\xi\right)=\left(\beta,1\right)\in\mathcal{H}^{1}_{L,\beta}
, we infer that

\displaystyle\beta\left|\Omega\right|{\langle\partial_{t}\phi\rangle}_{\Omega}+\left|\Gamma\right|{\langle\partial_{t}\psi\rangle}_{\Gamma}=0\hskip 10.00002pt\text{a.e.\penalty\ on\penalty\ }[0,T].

Hence, the pair
\left(\partial_{t}\phi(t),\partial_{t}\psi(t)\right)
is an admissible right-hand side in (
2.9
) for almost all
t\in[0,T]
as well.
Due to the linearity of the operator
\mathcal{S}_{L,\beta}
, we deduce that

\partial_{t}\mathcal{S}_{L,\beta}(\phi,\psi)=\mathcal{S}_{L,\beta}(\partial_{t}\phi,\partial_{t}\psi)
in
\mathcal{H}^{1}_{L,\beta}
for almost all
[0,T]
.
Thus, choosing
\left(\zeta,\xi\right)=\mathcal{S}_{L,\beta}(\phi,\psi)\in\mathcal{H}^{1}_{L,\beta}
in (
6.19
), we derive the identity

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\frac{1}{2}\|\left(\phi,\psi\right)\|_{L,\beta,\ast}^{2}=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\frac{1}{2}\|\mathcal{S}_{L,\beta}(\phi,\psi)\|_{L,\beta}^{2}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt=\left(\mathcal{S}_{L,\beta}(\partial_{t}\phi,\partial_{t}\psi),\mathcal{S}_{L,\beta}(\phi,\psi)\right)_{L,\beta}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt=-\langle\left(\partial_{t}\phi,\partial_{t}\psi\right),\mathcal{S}_{L,\beta}(\phi,\psi)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}^{1}_{L,\beta}}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt=\big(\left(\mu,\theta\right),\mathcal{S}_{L,\beta}(\phi,\psi)\big)_{L,\beta}

(6.20)

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt-\big(\left(\phi_{2}\boldsymbol{v}+\phi\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\psi_{2}\boldsymbol{w}+\psi\boldsymbol{w}_{1}\right),\left(\nabla\mathcal{S}_{L,\beta}^{\Omega}(\phi,\psi),\nabla_{\Gamma}\mathcal{S}_{L,\beta}^{\Gamma}(\phi,\psi)\right)\big)_{\mathcal{L}^{2}}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt=-\big(\left(\mu,\theta\right),\left(\phi,\psi\right)\big)_{\mathcal{L}^{2}}

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt-\big(\left(\phi_{2}\boldsymbol{v}+\phi\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\psi_{2}\boldsymbol{w}+\psi\boldsymbol{w}_{1}\right),\left(\nabla\mathcal{S}_{L,\beta}^{\Omega}(\phi,\psi),\nabla_{\Gamma}\mathcal{S}_{L,\beta}^{\Gamma}(\phi,\psi)\right)\big)_{\mathcal{L}^{2}}

a.e. on
[0,T]
. Here, the second term on the right-hand side can be bounded by

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\big(\left(\phi_{2}\boldsymbol{v}+\phi\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\psi_{2}\boldsymbol{w}+\psi\boldsymbol{w}_{1}\right),\left(\nabla\mathcal{S}_{L,\beta}^{\Omega}(\phi,\psi),\nabla_{\Gamma}\mathcal{S}_{L,\beta}^{\Gamma}(\phi,\psi)\right)\big)_{\mathcal{L}^{2}}\\
&\leq\|\left(\phi_{2}\boldsymbol{v}+\phi\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\psi_{2}\boldsymbol{w}+\psi\boldsymbol{w}_{1}\right)\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}}\|\left(\nabla\mathcal{S}_{L,\beta}^{\Omega}(\phi,\psi),\nabla_{\Gamma}\mathcal{S}_{L,\beta}^{\Gamma}(\phi,\psi)\right)\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}}\\
&\leq\Big(\|\left(\phi_{2}\boldsymbol{v},\psi_{2}\boldsymbol{w}\right)\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}}+\|\left(\phi\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\psi\boldsymbol{w}_{1}\right)\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}}\Big)\|\mathcal{S}_{L,\beta}(\phi,\psi)\|_{L,\beta}\\
&\leq\Big(C\|\left(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w}\right)\|_{\mathcal{L}^{3}}+\|\left(\phi,\psi\right)\|_{\mathcal{L}^{6}}\|\left(\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\boldsymbol{w}_{1}\right)\|_{\mathcal{L}^{3}}\Big)\|\left(\phi,\psi\right)\|_{L,\beta,\ast}\end{split}

(6.21)

a.e. on
[0,T]
. Using Young’s inequality in combination with the Sobolev embedding
\mathcal{H}^{1}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{L}^{6}
and the Poincaré inequality, we obtain

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\|\left(\phi,\psi\right)\|_{\mathcal{L}^{6}}\|\left(\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\boldsymbol{w}_{1}\right)\|_{\mathcal{L}^{3}}\|\left(\phi,\psi\right)\|_{L,\beta,\ast}\\
&\leq\varepsilon\|\left(\phi,\psi\right)\|^{2}_{K,\alpha}+C_{\varepsilon}\|\left(\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\boldsymbol{w}_{1}\right)\|_{\mathcal{L}^{3}}^{2}\|\left(\phi,\psi\right)\|_{L,\beta,\ast}^{2}\end{split}

(6.22)

a.e. on
[0,T]
for all
\varepsilon>0
and a constant
C_{\varepsilon}
. Another application of Young’s inequality yields

\displaystyle\begin{split}C\|\left(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w}\right)\|_{\mathcal{L}^{3}}\|\left(\phi,\psi\right)\|_{L,\beta,\ast}\leq\frac{1}{2}\|\left(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w}\right)\|_{\mathcal{L}^{3}}^{2}+C\|\left(\phi,\psi\right)\|_{L,\beta,\ast}^{2}\end{split}

(6.23)

a.e. on
[0,T]
. Plugging (
6.22
) and (
6.23
) back into (
6.21
), we infer

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\big(\left(\phi_{2}\boldsymbol{v}+\phi\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\psi_{2}\boldsymbol{w}+\psi\boldsymbol{w}_{1}\right),\left(\nabla\mathcal{S}_{L,\beta}^{\Omega}(\phi,\psi),\nabla_{\Gamma}\mathcal{S}_{L,\beta}^{\Gamma}(\phi,\psi)\right)\big)_{\mathcal{L}^{2}}\\
&\leq\frac{1}{2}\|\left(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w}\right)\|_{\mathcal{L}^{3}}^{2}+\varepsilon\|\left(\phi,\psi\right)\|_{K,\alpha}^{2}+C\|\left(\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\boldsymbol{w}_{1}\right)\|_{\mathcal{L}^{3}}^{2}\|\left(\phi,\psi\right)\|_{L,\beta,\ast}^{2}\end{split}

(6.24)

a.e. on
[0,T]
. For the first term on the right-hand side of (
6.20
) we find

\displaystyle\begin{split}-\big(\left(\mu,\theta\right),\left(\phi,\psi\right)\big)_{\mathcal{L}^{2}}&=-\|\left(\phi,\psi\right)\|_{K,\alpha}^{2}-\int_{\Omega}[F^{\prime}(\phi_{2})-F^{\prime}(\phi_{1})]\phi\;\mathrm{d}x\\
&\qquad-\int_{\Gamma}[G^{\prime}(\psi_{2})-G^{\prime}(\psi_{1})]\psi\;\mathrm{d}\Gamma\end{split}

(6.25)

a.e. on
[0,T]
. Now, exploiting the growth condition on
F^{\prime\prime}
(see
(A4)
) with
p<6
and using the fundamental theorem of calculus as well as Hölder’s inequality, we compute

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\left|\int_{\Omega}[F^{\prime}(\phi_{2})-F^{\prime}(\phi_{1})]\phi\;\mathrm{d}x\right|=\left|\int_{\Omega}\int_{0}^{1}F^{\prime\prime}\big(\tau\phi_{2}+(1-\tau)\phi_{1}\big)\;\mathrm{d}\tau(\phi_{2}-\phi_{1})\phi\;\mathrm{d}x\right|\\
&\quad\leq C\int_{\Omega}\left(1+\left|\phi_{1}\right|^{p-2}+\left|\phi_{2}\right|^{p-2}\right)\left|\phi\right|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x\\
&\quad\leq C\big(1+\|\phi_{1}\|_{L^{6}(\Omega)}^{p-2}+\|\phi_{2}\|_{L^{6}(\Omega)}^{p-2}\big)\|\phi\|_{L^{12/(8-p)}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq C\|\phi\|_{L^{12/(8-p)}(\Omega)}^{2}\end{split}

(6.26)

a.e. on
[0,T]
. Recalling
H^{1}(\Gamma)\hookrightarrow L^{q}(\Gamma)
, we analogously infer

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\Gamma}[G^{\prime}(\psi_{2})-G^{\prime}(\psi_{1})]\psi\;\mathrm{d}\Gamma\right|

\displaystyle\leq C\|\psi\|_{L^{12/(8-p)}(\Gamma)}^{2}

(6.27)

a.e. on
[0,T]
.
Combining (
6.26
)–(
6.27
) yields

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\Omega}[F^{\prime}(\phi_{2})-F^{\prime}(\phi_{1})]\phi\;\mathrm{d}x\right|+\left|\int_{\Gamma}[G^{\prime}(\psi_{2})-G^{\prime}(\psi_{1})]\psi\;\mathrm{d}\Gamma\right|\leq C\|\left(\phi,\psi\right)\|_{\mathcal{L}^{12/(8-p)}}^{2}

a.e. on
[0,T]
.
Next, recalling again that
p<6
, which entails the compact embedding
\mathcal{H}^{1}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{L}^{12/(8-p)}
, we deduce with Ehrling’s lemma that

\displaystyle\|\left(\phi,\psi\right)\|_{\mathcal{L}^{12/(8-p)}}^{2}\leq\varepsilon\|\left(\phi,\psi\right)\|_{K,\alpha}^{2}+C_{\varepsilon}\|\left(\phi,\psi\right)\|_{L,\beta,\ast}^{2}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\frac{1}{2}\|\left(\phi,\psi\right)\|_{L,\beta,\ast}^{2}+(1-C\varepsilon)\|\left(\phi,\psi\right)\|_{K,\alpha}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{2}\|\left(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w}\right)\|_{\mathcal{L}^{3}}^{2}+C\mathcal{F}\|\left(\phi,\psi\right)\|_{L,\beta,\ast}^{2}\end{split}

a.e. on
[0,T]
, where
\mathcal{F}\coloneqq\|\left(\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\boldsymbol{w}_{1}\right)\|_{\mathcal{L}^{3}}^{2}
.
Choosing
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small, we conclude

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\frac{1}{2}\|\left(\phi,\psi\right)\|_{L,\beta,\ast}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{2}\|\left(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w}\right)\|_{\mathcal{L}^{3}}^{2}+C\mathcal{F}\|\left(\phi,\psi\right)\|_{L,\beta,\ast}^{2}.

As
\mathcal{F}\in L^{1}(0,T)
, Gronwall’s lemma directly implies

\displaystyle\|\left(\phi(t),\psi(t)\right)\|^{2}_{L,\beta,\ast}

\displaystyle\leq\|\left(\phi(0),\psi(0)\right)\|^{2}_{L,\beta,\ast}\exp\left(C\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{F}(\tau)\;\mathrm{d}\tau\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt+\int_{0}^{t}\|\left(\boldsymbol{v}(s),\boldsymbol{w}(s)\right)\|^{2}_{\mathcal{L}^{3}}\exp\left(C\int_{s}^{t}\mathcal{F}(\tau)\;\mathrm{d}\tau\right)\;\mathrm{d}s

for almost all
t\in[0,T]
, which proves (
3.12
). As a consequence, if
(\phi_{0}^{1},\psi_{0}^{1})=(\phi_{0}^{2},\psi_{0}^{2})
a.e. in
\Omega\times\Gamma
,
\boldsymbol{v}_{1}=\boldsymbol{v}_{2}
a.e. in
Q
and
\boldsymbol{w}_{1}=\boldsymbol{w}_{2}
a.e. on
\Sigma
, the uniqueness of the corresponding weak solution immediately follows. Thus, the proof is complete.
∎

Appendix: Some calculus for bulk-surface function spaces

Proposition A.1
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
d\in\{2,3\}
be a non-empty, bounded domain with Lipschitz boundary
\Gamma\coloneqq\partial\Omega
. Moreover, let
T>0
,
K,L\in[0,\infty]
and
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
be arbitrary.
Let
\left(u,v\right)\in C([0,T];\mathcal{L}^{2})\cap L^{2}(0,T;\mathcal{H}^{3})
with
\left(-\Delta u,-\Delta_{\Gamma}v+\alpha\partial_{\mathbf{n}}u\right)\in\mathcal{H}^{1}_{L,\beta}
and

\displaystyle\begin{cases}K\partial_{\mathbf{n}}u=\alpha v-u&\text{if}\ K\in[0,\infty),\\
\partial_{\mathbf{n}}u=0&\text{if}\ K=\infty\end{cases}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{a.e.\penalty\ on }\Sigma.

(A.1)

We further suppose that their weak time derivative satisfies
\left(\partial_{t}u,\partial_{t}v\right)\in L^{2}(0,T;(\mathcal{H}^{1}_{L,\beta})^{\prime})
. Then, the continuity property
\left(u,v\right)\in C([0,T];\mathcal{H}^{1})
holds, the mapping

\displaystyle t\mapsto\|\left(u(t),v(t)\right)\|^{2}_{K,\alpha}

is absolutely continuous on
[0,T]
, and the chain rule formula

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\|\left(u(t),v(t)\right)\|^{2}_{K,\alpha}=2\big<\left(\partial_{t}u,\partial_{t}v\right)(t),\left(-\Delta u,-\Delta_{\Gamma}v+\alpha\partial_{\mathbf{n}}u\right)(t)\big>_{\mathcal{H}^{1}_{L,\beta}}

(A.2)

holds for almost all
t\in[0,T]
.

In the cases
(K,L)\in[0,\infty)\times\{\infty\}
, a proof was already given in
[
12
, Proposition A.1.(b)]
. There, the claim was established by approximating the occurring functions by a sequence of time-mollified functions, and eventually passing to the limit. For all other choices of
(K,L)
, the proof can be carried out analogously.
