Simpler foundations for the hyperbolic plane
John Bamberg, Tim Penttila

Abstract
. H. L. Skala (1992) gave the first elegant first-order axiom system for hyperbolic geometry by replacing Menger’s axiom involving projectivities with the theorems of Pappus and Desargues for the hyperbolic plane. In so doing, Skala showed that hyperbolic geometry is incidence geometry. We improve upon Skala’s formulation by doing away with Pappus and Desargues altogether, by substituting for them two simpler axioms.

1.
Introduction

The independent discovery by Lobachevsky and Bolyai of hyperbolic geometry in the 1830’s was
followed by slow acceptance of the subject from the 1860’s on, with the publications of relevant parts of the
correspondence of Gauss. A new phase was entered from 1903, when Hilbert
[
12
]
, in his work introducing
the
calculus of ends
, introduced an axiomatisation for hyperbolic plane geometry by adding a hyperbolic parallel
axiom to the axioms for plane absolute geometry. (A detailed survey of axiomatisations for hyperbolic geometry
is given in
[
24
]
).

In 1938, Menger
[
18
]
made the important discovery that in hyperbolic geometry the concepts of
betweenness and equidistance can be defined in terms of point-line incidence. Since an axiom system obtained by replacing all occurrences of betweenness and equidistance with their definitions in terms of incidence would look highly unnatural, Menger and his students looked for a more natural axiom system.

“This research, which is still in progress, will result, I believe, in a system of fairly simple assumptions in terms of the projective operations for the whole Bolyai-Lobachevsky geometry.”

Karl Menger, 1940
[
19
]
.

This task was carried out by Menger
[
18
,
19
,
20
,
21
]
, and by his students Abbott
[
1
,
2
,
4
]
, DeBaggis
[
8
,

]
and Jenks
[
14
,
15
,

]
, but even their most polished axiom system contained a statement on projectivities that was not reducible to a first-order statement. In 1992, Menger’s student Skala
[
29
]
showed that the
statement on projectivities can be replaced by the axioms of Pappus and Desargues for the hyperbolic plane, thereby producing the first elegant first-order axiom system for hyperbolic geometry based on incidence alone. Since one of the Menger school’s other axioms was ‘Pascal’s Theorem’ on the rimpoints, this axiom system looks a little excessive. In 1905, Hessenberg
[
11
]
had shown that, in a projective plane, the axiom of Pappus implies
the axiom of Desargues. In 1966, Buekenhout
[
7
]
had shown that, in a projective plane, the axiom of Pascal for an oval
implies the axiom of Pappus for the plane. This context suggests that the axiom of Pascal for the rimpoints should
be sufficient without the need for the axioms of Pappus and Desargues. This paper establishes that suggestion to be valid, fulfilling Menger’s dream, but for
two additional axioms that ensures that midpoints exist, and that perpendicularity of lines is well-defined.

Some notation and definitions

In this paper, we will often encounter standard concepts in incidence geometry and group theory.
An
incidence structure
is a triple
(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{B},\mathrm{I})
with
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{B}

disjoint, non-empty sets, and
\mathrm{I}
a relation between them; the
incidence relation
. An
isomorphism
from the incidence structure
(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{B},\mathrm{I})
to
the incidence structure
(\mathcal{P^{\prime}},\mathcal{B^{\prime}},\mathrm{I^{\prime}})
is a bijection
\phi\colon\mathcal{P}\cup\mathcal{B}\to\mathcal{P^{\prime}}\cup\mathcal{B^{\prime}}
such that
\mathcal{P}^{\phi}=\mathcal{P^{\prime}}
,
\mathcal{B}^{\phi}=\mathcal{B^{\prime}}
, and for all
P\in\mathcal{P}
and
B\in\mathcal{B}
:

P\,\mathrm{I}\,B\Leftrightarrow P^{\phi}\,\mathrm{I^{\prime}}\,B^{\phi}.

An
automorphism
of an incidence structure
\mathcal{S}=(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{B},I)
is an isomorphism to itself. We denote the set of all automorphisms of
\mathcal{S}
by
\mathsf{Aut}(\mathcal{S})
. Moreover,
\mathsf{Aut}(\mathcal{S})
is a group under composition, called the
automorphism group
of
\mathcal{S}
.
If
G
is a group acting on a set
\Omega
, and
H
is a group acting on a set
\Gamma
, we say that these two actions
are
permutationally isomorphic
, and we write
(\Omega,G)\cong(\Gamma,H)
, if there exists a group isomorphism
\varphi:G\to H
and a bijection
\beta:\Omega\to\Gamma
such
that

\beta(\omega^{g})=\beta(\omega)^{\varphi(g)}

for all
\omega\in\Omega
and
g\in G
. The pair
(\beta,\varphi)
is a
permutational isomorphism
from
(\Omega,G)
to
(\Gamma,H)
, and permutational isomorphism
is an equivalence relation on group actions.

lying on the other side of
\ell
to
X
. So

meets

in a finite point

, and by definition of an order-preserving axial map,

lies on
\ell
. Consider the hexagon

. Then the diagonal points are

XX^{P}\cap YY^{P}=P

, and

.

to

as

.

,
and hence
X^{P}X^{\alpha}\cap Y^{P}Y^{\alpha}=Q

Y^{\alpha}=Y^{PQ}
. So we have shown that

is equal to
PQ

on the rimpoints lying on the side of

opposite to

. By a similar argument, and consideration of a rimpoint

on the opposite
of
\ell
to
X

Q^{\prime}
incident with
\ell
such that
\alpha
is equal to
PQ^{\prime}
on the

\ell
as
X
. However, we also know, by definition of

,

fixes

,

is the identity permutation and

.

and (i) holds.

Suppose
\alpha
and
\beta
are two order-preserving axial maps with axis

(where

and

Let

be a point incident with

, there exist points

∎

Remark 3.20

Lemma 3.21
.

An order-preserving axial map cannot interchange two rimpoints.

Proof.

Let
\alpha
be an order-preserving axial map and suppose it interchanges two rimpoints
X
and
Y
. By Lemma
3.19
,
there exist two finite points incident with the axis of
\alpha
such that
\alpha=MN
.
Then
\{X,X^{M}\}
separates
\{Y,Y^{M}\}
and hence
XY^{M}
does not meet
YX^{M}

(by (S3));
a contradiction as

is the point of intersection of these two lines.
∎

Lemma 3.22
.

Given a line
\ell
and rimpoints
U
,
V
on the same side of
\ell
there is a unique order-preserving axial mapping
\alpha
with axis
\ell
such that
U^{\alpha}=V
.

Proof.

Let
P
be a finite point on
\ell
and let
W:=U^{P}
. Then
WV
intersects
\ell
, as
V
,
W
are on opposite sides of
\ell
; let

\alpha:=PQ

.
∎

Lemma 3.23
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a cyclic order compatible with
//
, and let
\alpha
be an order-preserving axial mapping with axis
\ell
.
Then for every rimpoint
X
not incident with
\ell
, we have either

\mathcal{C}(X^{\alpha},X,X^{\alpha^{-1}})
or
\mathcal{C}(X^{\alpha^{-1}},X,X^{\alpha})
.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.18
, there exist finite point
P
and
Q
incident with
\ell
such that
\alpha=PQ
.
Let
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
be the rimpoints of
\ell
, such that we have the ordering
X_{1}\,P\,Q\,X_{2}
.
Let
X
be a rimpoint not incident with
\ell
. If (without loss of generality)
\mathcal{C}(X_{1},X,X_{2})
, then we have the ordering,

X_{2}\,X^{Q}\,X^{P}\,X_{1}
(via projection). Therefore,
\mathcal{C}(X^{Q},X^{P},X_{1})

and hence
\mathcal{C}(X,X^{PQ},X_{2})
(by Lemma
3.14
). By a similar argument,

\mathcal{C}(X_{1},X,X^{QP},X)
. Therefore,
\mathcal{C}(X^{\alpha^{-1}},X,X^{\alpha})
.
If
P
and

are situated such that we have the ordering

∎

Lemma 3.24
.

Two order-preserving axial maps with the same axis commute.

Proof.

Let
\alpha
and
\alpha^{\prime}
be two order-preserving axial maps with common axis
\ell
. By Lemma
3.19
, there exist three finite points
P
,
P^{\prime}
,
Q
incident with
\ell

such that
\alpha=PQ
and
\alpha^{\prime}=QP^{\prime}
. By Theorem
3.7
, there exists a finite point
Q^{\prime}
incident with
\ell
such that

P^{\prime}PQ=Q^{\prime}
and hence
P^{\prime}PQ=(P^{\prime}PQ)^{-1}=QPP^{\prime}
. Therefore,

\alpha^{\prime}\alpha=QP^{\prime}PQ=Q(P^{\prime}PQ)=Q(QPP^{\prime})=PP^{\prime}=\alpha\alpha^{\prime}

and we have proved our result.
∎

The following two lemmas will be used later to define

of lines of

.
Later we will see that Lemma

).

Lemma 3.25
.

Every order-preserving axial mapping is the square of an order-preserving axial mapping (with the same axis).

Proof.

Let
\alpha
be an order-preserving axial mapping with axis
\ell
. By Lemma
3.19
,
there exist finite points
P
and
Q
incident with
\ell
such that
\alpha=PQ
.
By Lemma
3.15
, there exists a finite point
M
incident with
PQ
such that

P^{M}=Q
(i.e.,
M
is the
midpoint
of the segment
\overline{PQ}
).
Then
(PM)^{2}=PMPM=PP^{M}=PQ=\alpha
, and we know that
PM
is an order-preserving axial map by Lemma
3.18
,
with axis
\ell
.
Therefore,
\alpha
is the square of an order-preserving axial mapping.
∎

Lemma 3.26
.

Let
X_{1}
,
X_{2}
,
X_{3}
,
X_{4}
be rimpoints such that
X_{1}X_{2}
and
X_{3}X_{4}
are non-intersecting non-parallel lines. Then
there is a unique order-preserving axial map

with axis
X_{1}X_{2}
such that
X_{4}^{g^{2}}=X_{3}
.

Proof.

Let
\ell
be the line
X_{1}X_{2}
.
By Lemma
3.22
, there exists an order-preserving axial map
\alpha
with axis
\ell
such that
X_{4}^{\alpha}=X_{3}
.
By Lemma
3.25
, there is an order-preserving axial map
g
with axis
\ell
such that
\alpha=g^{2}
.
So we know that an order-preserving axial map

with axis
\ell
exists such that
X_{4}^{g^{2}}=X_{3}
.
By Lemma
3.24
and Lemma

, the order-preserving axial mappings with axis
\ell
form an Abelian group

,
and so the squaring map

is a homomorphism on

.
There are no elements of order

.
∎

4.
Line-reflections and perpendicularity

Throughout this section, we will suppose that
\mathcal{I}
is an incidence structure satisfying
axioms (A1) through (A7), plus (A10) and (A11).
Suppose
\ell
and
m
are non-parallel lines, and suppose
\ell
has rimpoints
\{L_{1},L_{2}\}

and
m
has rimpoints
\{M_{1},M_{2}\}
such that
L_{1}M_{2}//L_{2}M_{1}
. We define
\ell\bowtie m
to be the finite
point
L_{1}M_{2}\cap L_{2}M_{1}
. In this notation, Axiom (A11) states that if
X
is a rimpoint not on a line

,
then there is a unique point
R
on
\ell
such that for every finite point
S
on
\ell

.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\alpha
and
\beta
be order-preserving axial maps with a common axis
\ell
. Let
A
be a rimpoint not incident
with
\ell
. Then
A
,
\ell\bowtie A^{\alpha}A^{\alpha^{-1}}
,
\ell\bowtie A^{\beta}A^{\beta^{-1}}

are collinear.

Proof.

By (A11), there is a unique finite point
R
on
\ell
such that for every finite point
S
on
\ell
,

\ell\bowtie A^{SR}A^{RS}
is incident with
AR
. By Lemma
3.19
,
there exist finite points
M
and
N
on
\ell
such that
\alpha=RM
and
\beta=RN
.
Therefore,
\ell\bowtie A^{\alpha}A^{\alpha^{-1}}=\ell\bowtie A^{RM}A^{MR}

and
\ell\bowtie A^{RM}A^{MR}
is incident with
AR
. Likewise,

\ell\bowtie A^{\beta}A^{\beta^{-1}}
is incident with
AR
, and so

A
,
\ell\bowtie A^{\alpha}A^{\alpha^{-1}}
,
\ell\bowtie A^{\beta}A^{\beta^{-1}}

all lie on
AR
.
∎

Definition 4.2
.

Let
\ell
be a line and let
A
be a rimpoint not incident with
\ell
. Let
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
be the rimpoints on
\ell
and let
\alpha
be an
order-preserving axial map with axis
\ell
. Let
O:=\ell\bowtie A^{\alpha}A^{\alpha^{-1}}
.
Then
AA^{O}
is the
perpendicular line
to
\ell
incident with
A
.

Axiom (A11) (see Lemma
4.1
)
implies that the definition (in Definition
4.2
) of the perpendicular line to
\ell
incident with
A

is independent of the choice of
\alpha
, and hence well-defined.
Therefore, given a line
\ell
, there is a function
\sigma_{\ell}
on the rimpoints defined as follows:

\sigma_{\ell}
fixes the rimpoints of
\ell
, and for a rimpoint
A
not incident with
\ell
,
we define
A^{\sigma_{\ell}}
to be the rimpoint
A^{O}
given in Definition
4.2
.
By Lemma
4.4
(below),
\sigma_{\ell}
is a permutation of the rimpoints and we will refer to this map as the
reflection in the line
\ell
.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
\ell
be a line and let
A
be a rimpoint not incident with
\ell
. Then the line
AA^{\sigma_{\ell}}
meets
\ell
in a finite point.
Therefore,
\sigma_{\ell}
interchanges the sides of
\ell
.

Proof.

Let
\alpha
be an order-preserving axial map with axis
\ell
, and let
\{X_{1},X_{2}\}
be the rimpoints of
\ell
. Let
\mathcal{C}
be a cyclic order
on the rimpoints compatible with the separation relation
//
such that
\mathcal{C}(X_{1},A,X_{2})
.
Now
A^{\alpha}
and
A^{\alpha^{-1}}
lie on the same side of
\ell
as
A
(by Lemma
3.2
), and either

\mathcal{C}(A^{\alpha},A,A^{\alpha^{-1}})
or
\mathcal{C}(A^{\alpha^{-1}},A,A^{\alpha})
(by Lemma
3.23
).
Suppose, without loss of generality, that
\mathcal{C}(X_{1},A^{\alpha},A)
and
\mathcal{C}(A,A^{\alpha^{-1}},X_{2})
.
Let
O:=\ell\bowtie A^{\alpha}A^{\alpha^{-1}}
. In particular, from our ordering, we have
X_{1}^{O}=A^{\alpha^{-1}}
and
X_{2}^{O}=A^{\alpha}
.
Now the half-turn
O
preverses the cyclic order
\mathcal{C}
, and hence

\mathcal{C}(X_{1}^{O},A^{\alpha O},A^{O})
and
\mathcal{C}(A^{O},A^{\alpha^{-1}O},X_{2}^{O})
.
So
\mathcal{C}(A^{\alpha^{-1}},X_{2},A^{O})
and
\mathcal{C}(A^{O},X_{1},A^{\alpha})
,
and it follows that
\mathcal{C}(X_{2},A^{O},X_{1})
. In particular,
A^{\sigma_{\ell}}=A^{O}
lies on the opposite side of
\ell
as
A
.
∎

Lemma 4.4
.

For any line
\ell
, we have
\sigma_{\ell}^{2}=1
.

Proof.

Let
A
be a rimpoint not incident with
\ell
, and let
\alpha
be an order-preserving axial map with axis
\ell
.
By definition,
A^{\sigma_{\ell}}=A^{O}
where
O=\ell\bowtie A^{\alpha}A^{\alpha^{-1}}
.
Let
O^{\prime}:=\ell\bowtie(A^{O})^{\alpha}(A^{O})^{\alpha^{-1}}
.
By Lemma
4.3
,
\ell
meets
AO
in a finite point
R
.
Write
\alpha=PQ
for two finite points
P
and
Q
incident with
\ell
(see Lemma
3.19
).
Since
P
,
Q
,
R
are collinear, we have from Theorem
3.7
that
PQR=RQP
and
QPR=RPQ
. So

\displaystyle\left((A^{O})^{\alpha}\right)^{R}

\displaystyle=A^{O(PQR)}=A^{O(RQP)}=(A^{OR})^{QP}=A^{QP}=A^{\alpha^{-1}},

\displaystyle\left((A^{O})^{\alpha^{-1}}\right)^{R}

\displaystyle=A^{O(QPR)}=A^{O(RPQ)}=(A^{OR})^{PQ}=A^{PQ}=A^{\alpha}.

Therefore, since half-turns are automorphisms by Theorem
3.8
, we have

(O^{\prime})^{R}=(\ell\bowtie(A^{O})^{\alpha}(A^{O})^{\alpha^{-1}})^{R}=\ell\bowtie A^{\alpha^{-1}}A^{\alpha}=O

as
\ell^{R}=\ell
. So
O^{\prime}
lies on the line
RO=AO
and therefore,

\left(A^{\sigma_{\ell}}\right)^{\sigma_{\ell}}=(A^{\sigma_{\ell}})^{O^{\prime}}=A^{OO^{\prime}}=A
.
Thus we have shown that
\sigma_{\ell}^{2}
fixes every rimpoint, and so is the identity.
∎

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
\ell
and
m
be two lines meeting in a finite point. Then the following are equivalent:

(1)

\sigma_{\ell}
interchanges the rimpoints of
m
;

(2)

\sigma_{m}
interchanges the rimpoints of
\ell
;

Proof.

Suppose
\sigma_{\ell}
interchanges the rimpoints
\{M_{1},M_{2}\}
of
m
.
Let
\alpha
be an order-preserving axial map with axis
\ell
.
Let
\{X_{1},X_{2}\}
be the rimpoints of
\ell
, written in such a way that
\{X_{1},M_{1}^{\alpha^{-1}}\}
separates
\{X_{2},M_{1}^{\alpha}\}
.
Let
T:=X_{1}M_{1}^{\alpha^{-1}}\cap X_{2}M_{1}^{\alpha}
and
U:=\ell\cap m
. By assumption,
M_{1}^{\sigma_{\ell}}=M_{2}

and hence
M_{2}=M_{1}^{T}
. Therefore,
T
is incident with
m
and

m=M_{1}T
is the perpendicular line to
\ell
through
M_{1}
.
Let
\beta:=UT
. Then

\displaystyle X_{1}^{\beta}

\displaystyle=(X_{1}^{U})^{T}=X_{2}^{T}=M_{1}^{\alpha},

\displaystyle X_{1}^{\beta^{-1}}

\displaystyle=(X_{1}^{T})^{U}=M_{1}^{\alpha^{-1}U},

and so
X_{1}P
is the perpendicular to
m
through
X_{1}
where
P:=M_{1}^{\alpha^{-1}U}M_{1}\cap M_{2}M_{1}^{\alpha}
.
We will show that
P
is incident with
\ell
.
Now
PUTP
is axial with axis
m^{P}=M_{1}^{\alpha^{-1}U}M_{1}^{\alpha}
(see Remark
3.20
), and

M_{1}^{(PUTP)^{2}}=M_{1}^{PUTUTP}=(M_{1}^{\alpha^{-1}})^{TUTP}=X_{1}^{UTP}=X_{2}^{TP}=M_{1}^{\alpha P}=M_{2}.

So by Lemma
3.22
,
PUTP=T^{\prime}U^{\prime}
where
U^{\prime}=\ell\cap M_{1}^{\alpha}M_{1}^{\alpha^{-1}U}
and
T^{\prime}=X_{1}M_{1}\cap X_{2}M_{2}^{U^{\prime}}
.
(Note:
M_{1}^{(T^{\prime}U^{\prime})^{2}}=M_{1}^{T^{\prime}U^{\prime}T^{\prime}U^{\prime}}=X_{1}^{U^{\prime}T^{\prime}U^{\prime}}=X_{2}^{T^{\prime}U^{\prime}}=M_{2}
). Therefore,

X_{1}^{P}=(M_{1}^{\alpha^{-1}})^{TP}=(M_{1}^{\alpha^{-1}U})^{UTP}=M_{1}^{PUTP}=M_{1}^{T^{\prime}U^{\prime}}=X_{1}^{U^{\prime}}=X_{2}.

Hence
P
lies on
\ell
and
X_{1}P=\ell
. It follows that
\sigma_{m}
interchanges the rimpoints of
\ell
.
Interchanging the roles of
\ell
and
m
in this argument shows that the converse also holds.
∎

Lemma
4.5
allows us to define perpendicularity of lines. We say that a line
\ell
and a line

are

, and we write
\ell\perp m
, if
\ell
and

are concurrent and

\sigma_{\ell}
interchanges the rimpoints of

implies that

is a symmetric relation on lines.
Recall from Theorem

that half-turns are automorphisms of

Lemma 4.6
.

Half-turns preserve perpendicularity. That is, if
\ell\perp m
and

is a finite point, then
\ell^{P}\perp m^{P}
.

Proof.

Suppose
\ell\perp m
and let
P
be a finite point. Let
M
and
M^{\prime}
be the rimpoints of
m
, and
let
\alpha
be an axial map with axis
\ell
. Then,
m=MO
where
O=\ell\bowtie M^{\alpha}M^{\alpha^{-1}}
.
Let
\beta:=P\alpha P
.
By Remark
3.20
,
\beta
is axial with axis
\ell^{P}
.
Moreover, by Lemma
3.8
, we have

\displaystyle O^{P}

\displaystyle=\ell^{P}\bowtie M^{\alpha P}M^{\alpha^{-1}P}

\displaystyle=\ell^{P}\bowtie M^{P\beta}M^{P\beta^{-1}}

\displaystyle=\ell^{P}\bowtie(M^{P})^{\beta}(M^{P})^{\beta^{-1}}

.
∎

Lemma 4.7
.

Suppose
\ell
and
m
are two non-parallel non-intersecting lines. Then there exists a unique line

perpendicular to both

\ell
and
m
. Moreover, there are unique order-preserving axial maps

and

such that (i)

has axis
\ell
, (ii)

has axis
m
, (iii)
X_{4}^{g^{2}}=X_{3}
, (iv)
X_{2}^{h^{2}}=X_{1}
. The line

is equal to
X_{4}^{g}X_{2}^{h}
and is the

of
\ell
and

.

Proof.

Let
\{X_{1},X_{2}\}
be the rimpoints of
\ell
and let
\{X_{3},X_{4}\}
be the rimpoints of
m
such that
X_{1}X_{4}//X_{2}X_{3}
.
By Lemma
3.26
, there is a unique order-preserving axial map
g
with axis
\ell
such that
g^{2}
maps
X_{4}
to
X_{3}
.
Likewise, there is a unique order-preserving axial map

with axis

such that
h^{2}
maps
X_{2}
to
X_{1}
.
Let
A:=X_{4}^{g}
. Then

A^{g}=X_{3}\text{ and }A^{g^{-1}}=X_{4}.

Let
O:=X_{1}X_{4}\cap X_{2}X_{4}
. By definition, the perpendicular line to
\ell

is

.
Now consider the map

. First, by Lemma

,

is an order-preserving axial map as it is the product of two half-turns: if

,

), and so

. Also,

Therefore, by Lemma

,

.
Let

. Then

and so the perpendicular line to

through

is

. On the other hand,

and therefore

and

. So we have shown that

is perpendicular to
\ell
and

.

It remains to prove uniqueness of the line

.
Let

be another line perpendicular to both
\ell
and

. (The following argument is similar
to that given in
[

Let the rimpoints of

and

be

and

, and

and

, respectively, arranged so
that
U
and
U^{\prime}
lie on the same side of
\ell
and
m
. So there exist finite points

and
Q_{2}

such that

and let

.
Let

and
V_{2}^{\prime}
be the other rimpoints on

and
UQ_{2}
, respectively. Since
\ell
is perpendicular to

, and the half-turn about

maps

to

and

to

,

is perpendicular to

is unique and hence

. Therefore,

. Similarly,
using the fact that

is perpendicular to

is the perpendicular line
to

, and so

. Thus

is on

6.
A proof of Theorem
1.2
using a theorem of F. Bachmann

A
metric plane
is an incidence structure
\mathcal{M}
of points and lines equipped with a symmetric binary relation of
perpendicularity
on the lines of
\mathcal{M}
, satisfying the following axioms. In this context, a
reflection

in a line
a
is a collineation of
\mathcal{M}
that preserves the perpendicularity relation
and fixes
a
pointwise. We call
a
the
axis
of the reflection.

(I1)

Every pair of distinct points is incident with a unique line.

(I2)

Every line is incident with at least three points.

(I3)

There exist three points with the property that no line is incident with all of them.

(P1)

For any point
P
and any line
a
, there is a line
b
incident with
P
and
perpendicular to
a
. If
P
is incident with
a
, then
b
is unique.

(P2)

If
a
is perpendicular to
b
, then there is a unique point incident with both
a
and
b
.

(M1)

Every line is the axis of at least one reflection.

(M2)

Reflections are involutions (on the points).

(M3)

If three lines
a
,
b
,
c
are concurrent, then a composition of reflections in the lines
a
,
b
and
c
is equal to a reflection in another line
d
.

(M4)

If three lines
a
,
b
,
c
have a common perpendicular, then a composition of reflections in the lines
a
,
b
and
c
is equal to a reflection in another line
d
.

These axioms are due to Bachmann
[
5
, §2.3]
, except for (I3), which is due to Ewald
[
10
]

and simply ensures non-degeneracy. One consequence of these axioms is that for every line
\ell
, there is at most one
reflection with axis
\ell
(see
[
5
, p.27, Satz 3]
).

The main result of our paper centres around the creation of a perpendicularity relation
\perp
for an incidence structure
that gives rise to reflections yielding a metric plane.
Now (A1), (A2), (A3) imply (I1), (I2), (I3), and (M1), (M2) follow from the definition of a reflection.
So the important results will be the affirmations of (M3) and (M4).

such that for every

An abstract oval gives rise to an incidence structure

as follows:

Points:

the elements of

Lines:

incident with

. An abstract oval is said to be Pascalian if every line of its associated incidence structure is Pascalian.
A conic
\mathcal{K}
of a Pappian projective plane

gives rise to a Pascalian abstract oval as follows.
Given a point

to be the other point of
\mathcal{K}

is tangent to

. Each point

Then

. The incidence structure

Concluding remarks

(1)

A metric plane is said to have
free mobility
if its group of motions
\Gamma
(i.e., automorphisms that preserve perpendicularity)
acts transitively on flags; pairs
(P,\ell)
where
P
is a point incident with
\ell
. It turns out (see
[
5
, Satz 2, p.126]
)
that free mobility is equivalent to each of the following three properties:

(a)

\Gamma
is transitive on points and lines;

(b)

Every two points have a midpoint, and every two lines have a bisector;

(c)

Every element of
\Gamma
that has a fixed line or fixed point is a square.

Note that the third condition above resembles the fundamental property of order-preserving axial maps (Lemma
3.25
) that enables us to define perpendicularity
of lines. The bisectors in the second condition are in analogy with midpoints for half-turns. That is, if
\ell
and
m
are lines, then
n
is a bisector of

\ell
and
m
if
\sigma_{\ell}\sigma_{n}=\sigma_{n}\sigma_{m}
.
We can show directly that the incidence structure
\mathcal{I}
studied in this paper is a metric plane with free mobility:

Lemma 8.1

(Bisectors exist)
.

Every two distinct lines
\ell
and
m
have a (unique) bisector.

Proof.

Take the rimpoints
X
,
Y
of
\ell
and
X^{\prime}
,
Y^{\prime}
of
m
. We may suppose that we have labelled our rimpoints so that one of the following holds:
(i)
XX^{\prime}
and
YY^{\prime}
do not meet and are not parallel, (ii)
X=X^{\prime}
or (iii)
Y=Y^{\prime}
.
In all cases, thee is no half-turn interchanging
X
and
X^{\prime}
, and
Y
and
Y^{\prime}
.
Since the half-turns and the reflections form the set of permutations of an abstract oval (Theorem
7.1
),
there is a reflection
\sigma_{n}
interchanging
X
and
X^{\prime}
and
Y
and
Y^{\prime}
. Note that this is valid in cases (ii) and (iii).
Hence, by Lemma
4.13
,
\ell^{\sigma_{n}}=m
and hence
\sigma_{n}\sigma_{\ell}\sigma_{n}=\sigma_{m}
(by Lemma

Therefore,
\sigma_{\ell}\sigma_{n}=\sigma_{n}\sigma_{m}
and

is a bisector of

and

.
∎

(2)

We show that we can use Skala’s Theorem for a fourth proof of Theorem
1.2
.
In each of the three proofs that we have given, we have an intermediate step that shows that
our incidence structure
\mathcal{I}
can be embedded into a Pappian projective plane
