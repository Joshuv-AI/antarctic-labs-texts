Abstract
. We prove a Bennett-type concentration bound for suprema of empirical processes based on sampling without replacement and a corresponding bound in the case of an arbitrary Hoeffding statistics. We improve on the previous results of such type, providing a sharper concentration profile.



Keywords
: concentration of measure, sampling, empirical processes, Hoeffding statistics.

AMS Classification
: 60E15, 60C05

1.
Preliminaries

In this short note we investigate concentration properties of particular functionals of uniform random permutations.
Namely, we focus on the suprema of empirical processes when sampling without replacement.
Such processes can be seen as Hoeffding statistics for matrices of a special form with repeated rows.
We also obtain corresponding bounds for a single Hoeffding statistics for general underlying matrix.
Such bounds were considered extensively in the literature, cf., e,g,
[
2
,
5
,
15
]
, and they play an important role in various applications, e.g., in transductive learning
[
16
]
, or statistical testing
[
1
]
.

Organization of this paper

In the rest of this section we introduce some core notation.
In Section
2
we present our results concerning concentration for suprema of empirical processes when sampling without replacement.
In Section
3
we present analogous results for a single Hoeffding statistic.
We provide remaining proofs of our concentration estimates in Section
4
.

1.2.
Basic notation

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, consider the symmetric group
S_{n}
of permutations of the set
[n]:=\{1,\ldots,n\}
equipped with the uniform probability measure
\pi_{n}
.
It is the stationary distribution of the
interchange process
defined via its generator
L
given by the formula

Lf(\sigma)=\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\big(f(\sigma\circ\tau_{ij})-f(\sigma)\big)=\frac{2}{n(n-1)}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\big(f(\sigma\circ\tau_{ij})-f(\sigma)\big),

where
\tau_{ij}
stands for the transposition of elements
i
and
j
.
By
\EE
, we denote the expectation w.r.t.
\pi_{n}
.
Moreover, for a function
f\colon S_{n}\to\mathbb{R}
, denote

f_{ij}(\cdot)=f(\cdot\circ\tau_{ij})

for short.
The corresponding Dirichlet form is then expressed as

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(f,g)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2n(n-1)}\EE\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}(f_{ij}-f)(g_{ij}-g)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\EE\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}(g_{ij}-g)(f_{ij}-f).

If
f
and
g
have the same monotonicity, then by the reversibility of
L
we also have

\mathcal{E}(f,g)=\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\EE\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}(g_{ij}-g)_{+}(f_{ij}-f)_{+}.

We say that the
modified log-Sobolev
inequality is satisfied with constant
\rho_{0}>0
if

(1.1)

\rho_{0}\Ent_{\mu}(f)\leq\mathcal{E}(f,\log f)

for all positive functions
f
, where
\Ent_{\mu}(f)=\int f\log f\,d\mu-\int f\,d\mu\log(\int f\,d\mu)
is the entropy functional.
For this process,
\rho_{0}\geq\frac{1}{n-1}
was obtained independently by Gao–Quastel
[
8
]
and Bobkov–Tetali
[
3
]
(note that the normalization of the generator
L
differs across various references – we provide here scaled constants matching our setting).

2.
Sampling without replacement – concentration for suprema

Consider a set of vectors
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Let
I_{1},\ldots,I_{n}
be a uniform sample without replacement and
J_{1},\ldots,J_{n}
be a sample with replacement from the set
[n]
.
For
m\leq n
, define

(2.1)

Z=\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\sum_{k=1}^{m}x_{I_{k}},\qquad Z^{\prime}=\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\sum_{k=1}^{m}x_{J_{k}}

so that
Z^{\prime}
can be considered a supremum of the empirical process in independent random variables
J_{k}
.
Tails of
Z^{\prime}
have been extensively studied beginning with the work of Talagrand
[
15
]
.

To analyze the tails of
Z
, it is often convenient to represent it as a supremum of Hoeffding statistics over a family of matrices.
Namely, for
x\in\mathcal{X}
, denote
a^{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
to be such that the first
m
rows of
a
consist of copies of vector
x
and the remaining rows have zero entries only, i.e.,
a_{ij}=x_{j}
for
i\leq m
,
j\in[n]
and
a_{ij}=0
for
i>m
,
j\in[n]
.
Then

Z=\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}a^{x}_{k\sigma_{k}},

where
\sigma=(I_{1},I_{2},\ldots,I_{n})\sim\pi_{n}
.
Moreover, denote
\sigma_{ij}=\sigma\circ\tau_{ij}
for any
i,j\in[n]
and

Z_{ij}=\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}a^{x}_{k\sigma_{ij}(k)},

so that the modified log-Sobolev inequality (
1.1
) applied to the Laplace transform of
Z
reads

\Ent(e^{\lambda Z})\leq\frac{\lambda}{n}\EE e^{\lambda Z}\sum_{ij}(1-e^{-\lambda(Z-Z_{ij})})_{+}(Z-Z_{ij})_{+}.

In the sequel, we express our concentration results for
Z
using the following quantities

\Sigma^{2}=\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\sum_{k=1}^{m}x_{I_{k}}^{2},\qquad\widetilde{\Sigma}^{2}=\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\sum_{k=1}^{m}x_{J_{k}}^{2}.

As pointed out in
[
9
]
, it follows from an argument due to
Hoeffding
[
11
]
(cf. also
[
14
]
) that if
E
is a normed space and

g\colon[n]\to E
, then for any convex function

\Psi\colon E\to\mathbb{R}
,

(2.2)

\EE\Psi\Bigl(\sum_{k=1}^{m}g({I_{k}})\Bigr)\leq\EE\Psi\Bigl(\sum_{k=1}^{m}g({J_{k}})\Bigr).

The meaning of (
2.2
) in terms of
Z
and
Z^{\prime}
and related quantities is explained in the following lemma, which in particular implies that

\EE Z\leq\EE Z^{\prime}
and

\EE\Sigma^{2}\leq\EE\widetilde{\Sigma}^{2}
.
We provide its proof for completeness in Appendix
A
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\phi\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be convex and increasing, and let
Z,Z^{\prime}
be given by (
2.1
).
Then

\EE\phi(Z)\leq\EE\phi(Z^{\prime}).

Our main result regarding concentration of
Z
is the theorem below providing a Bennett-type bound.

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
Z
be given by (
2.1
) and assume
\mathcal{X}\subset[-1,1]^{n}
.
Then, for some absolute constants
C_{1},C_{2}>0
,

\forall\;t\geq 0\qquad\PP(Z\geq\EE Z+t)\leq 2\exp\Bigl(-\frac{t}{C_{1}}\log\Bigl(1+\frac{t}{C_{2}\EE\widetilde{\Sigma}^{2}}\Bigr)\Bigr),

where

\widetilde{\Sigma}^{2}=\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\sum_{k=1}^{m}x_{J_{k}}^{2}
.
One can take
C_{1}=36
,
C_{2}=46
.

Remark 2.3
.

Assume that

\mathcal{X}\subset\{\,x\in[-1,1]^{n}\colon\sum_{i}x_{i}=0\,\}

and denote
v=m\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\Var(x_{J_{1}})+2\EE Z^{\prime}
.
Then, Tolstikhin–Blanchard–Kloft
[
16
, Theorem 2]
proved that

(2.3)

\forall\;t\geq 0\qquad\PP(Z\geq\EE Z^{\prime}+t)\leq\exp\Bigl(-t\log\bigl(1+\frac{t}{v}\bigr)+t-v\log\bigl(1+\frac{t}{v}\bigr)\Bigr).

Recall that by Hoeffding’s argument (
2.2
), cf. Lemma
2.1
,
\EE Z\leq\EE Z^{\prime}
and in many situations the latter quantity can be significantly larger.
Using symmetrization and Talagrand’s contraction principle for Rademacher averages, cf., e.g.,
[
13
]
, we can estimate

\EE\widetilde{\Sigma}^{2}\leq m\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\Var(x_{J_{1}})+8\EE\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\varepsilon_{k}x_{J_{k}},

where
\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{m}
are i.i.d. Rademacher variables independent of
J_{1},\ldots,J_{m}
.
Thus, in the case when the set
\mathcal{X}
is symmetric with respect to the origin we obtain that

\EE\widetilde{\Sigma}^{2}\leq m\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\Var(x_{J_{1}})+16\EE Z^{\prime}\leq 8v

and consequently our estimate of Theorem
2.2
, in contrast to (
2.3
), provides a bound on deviations around the "proper" mean, while having no worse scaling behavior in the exponent (up to numerical constants).

In the general case however, it does not need to hold that
\EE\widetilde{\Sigma}^{2}=\mathcal{O}(v)
, whence the bound (
2.3
) and our bound of Theorem
2.2
are not directly comparable.
It is also worth noting that Authors of
[
16
]
provide a bound
\EE Z^{\prime}\leq\EE Z+2\frac{m^{3}}{n}
which shows that one can replace
\EE Z^{\prime}
with
\EE Z
under the probability estimate without losing much for small values of
m
.
In Appendix
D
, we provide an example illustrating a situation in which our estimate still improves upon (
2.3
) in such a general case of non-symmetric set
\mathcal{X}
.

To prove the Bennett-type inequality of Theorem
2.2
, we need the following estimate due to Ledoux
[
12
]
.
We provide the proof for completeness in Appendix
B
.

Lemma 2.4

(Proof of Theorem 2.4 in
[
12
]
)
.

Let
Z^{\prime}
be given by (
2.1
) and assume
\mathcal{X}\subset[0,1]^{n}
.
Then

\forall\;\lambda\geq 1/4\qquad\log\EE e^{\lambda Z^{\prime}}\leq\frac{1}{16}e^{8\lambda}\EE Z^{\prime}.

We also need the following proposition providing the Bernstein inequality for
Z
.
We defer its proof to Section
4
.

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
Z
be given by (
2.1
) and assume

\mathcal{X}\subset[-1,1]^{n}
.
Then

\forall\;t\geq 0\qquad\PP(Z\geq\EE Z+t)\leq\exp\Bigl(-\min\Bigl(\frac{t}{32},\frac{t^{2}}{128\EE{\Sigma}^{2}}\Bigr)\Bigr),

where

{\Sigma}^{2}=\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\sum_{k}x_{I_{k}}^{2}
.

Proof of Theorem
2.2
.

If

32t<C_{1}C_{2}\EE\widetilde{\Sigma}^{2}
,
then we apply Proposition
2.5
and estimate

\log(1+x)\leq x

to get that as long as

128\leq C_{1}C_{2}
,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\PP(Z\geq\EE Z+t)&\leq\exp\Bigl(-\min\Bigl(\frac{t}{32},\frac{t^{2}}{128\EE\widetilde{\Sigma}^{2}}\Bigr)\Bigr),\\
&\leq\exp\Bigl(-\min\Bigl(\frac{t}{32},\frac{t^{2}}{C_{1}C_{2}\EE\widetilde{\Sigma}^{2}}\Bigr)\Bigr)\\
&=\exp\Bigl(-\frac{t^{2}}{C_{1}C_{2}\EE\widetilde{\Sigma}^{2}}\Bigr)\leq\exp\Bigl(-\frac{t}{C_{1}}\log\Bigl(1+\frac{t}{C_{2}\EE\widetilde{\Sigma}^{2}}\Bigr)\Bigr)\end{split}

and the result follows in this case.

If
32t\geq C_{1}C_{2}\EE\widetilde{\Sigma}^{2}
, then set

\rho^{-1}=\alpha\log\bigl(1+\beta\frac{t}{\EE\widetilde{\Sigma}^{2}}\bigr)

for some
\alpha,\beta>0
(to be fixed later)
and denote

Z^{\downarrow}=\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\sum_{k=1}^{m}x_{I_{k}}{\bf 1}_{\{|x_{I_{k}}|\leq\rho\}}

and

Z^{\uparrow}=\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\sum_{k=1}^{m}|x_{I_{k}}|{\bf 1}_{\{|x_{I_{k}}|>\rho\}}

so that
Z\leq Z^{\downarrow}+Z^{\uparrow}
.
We estimate the tail probabilities for
Z^{\downarrow}
and
Z^{\uparrow}
.

By the estimate

\log(1+x)\leq x
,
by the definition of
\rho
and as long as

\alpha\beta\leq 1/4
,

\frac{t}{32\rho}\leq\alpha\beta\cdot\frac{t^{2}}{32\EE\widetilde{\Sigma}^{2}}\leq\frac{t^{2}}{128\EE\widetilde{\Sigma}^{2}}\leq\frac{t^{2}}{128\EE{\Sigma}^{2}},

whence, by Proposition
2.5
applied to
Z^{\downarrow}/\rho
,

(2.4)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\PP(Z^{\downarrow}\geq\EE Z^{\downarrow}+t)&\leq\exp\Bigl(-\min\Bigl(\frac{t}{32\rho},\frac{t^{2}}{128\EE{\Sigma}^{2}}\Bigr)\Bigr)\\
&=\exp\Bigl(-\frac{t}{32\rho}\Bigr)=\exp\Bigl(-\frac{\alpha t}{32}\log\bigl(1+\beta\frac{t}{\EE\widetilde{\Sigma}^{2}}\bigr)\Bigr).\end{split}

We turn to the tails of
Z^{\uparrow}
.
Denote

Z^{\prime}_{\rho}=\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\sum_{k=1}^{m}|x_{J_{k}}|{\bf 1}_{\{|x_{J_{k}}|>\rho\}}.

Lemma
2.1
applied with

\{\,(|x_{i}|{\bf 1}_{\{|x_{i}|>\rho\}})_{i=1}^{n}\;\colon\;x\in\mathcal{X}\,\}

in place of
\mathcal{X}

together with Lemma
2.4
applied to
Z^{\prime}_{\rho}
yield

(2.5)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\log\EE e^{\lambda Z^{\uparrow}}\leq\log\EE e^{\lambda Z^{\prime}_{\rho}}\leq\frac{1}{16}e^{8\lambda}\EE Z^{\prime}_{\rho}\end{split}

for all
\lambda\geq 1/4
.
Choose

\lambda^{\ast}=\frac{1}{8}\log\Bigl(1+\beta\frac{t}{\EE\widetilde{\Sigma}^{2}}\Bigr).

Since
32t\geq C_{1}C_{2}\EE\widetilde{\Sigma}^{2}
by assumption,
then

\lambda^{\ast}\geq\frac{1}{8}\log(1+\frac{\beta C_{1}C_{2}}{32})\geq\frac{1}{4}
,
as long as
\beta C_{1}C_{2}\geq 32(e^{2}-1)
.
Moreover, note that

\EE Z_{\rho}^{\prime}\leq\rho^{-1}\EE\widetilde{\Sigma}^{2}\leq\frac{32t\rho^{-1}}{C_{1}C_{1}}.

Consequently, by the Chernoff bound combined with (
2.5
),

(2.6)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\PP(Z^{\uparrow}\geq t)&\leq\exp\Bigl(-t\lambda^{\ast}+\frac{e^{8\lambda^{\ast}}}{16}\EE Z^{\prime}_{\rho}\Bigr)\\
&=\exp\Bigl(-\frac{t}{8}\log\Bigl(1+\beta\frac{t}{\EE\widetilde{\Sigma}^{2}}\Bigr)+\frac{1}{16}\Bigl(\EE Z^{\prime}_{\rho}+\frac{\EE Z^{\prime}_{\rho}}{\EE\widetilde{\Sigma}^{2}}t\beta\Bigr)\Bigr)\\
&\leq\exp\Bigl(-\frac{t}{8}\log\Bigl(1+\beta\frac{t}{\EE\widetilde{\Sigma}^{2}}\Bigr)+\frac{1}{16}\Bigl(\frac{32t\rho^{-1}}{C_{1}C_{1}}+{t\beta\rho^{-1}}\Bigr)\Bigr)\\
&=\exp\Bigl(-t\log\Bigl(1+\beta\frac{t}{\EE\widetilde{\Sigma}^{2}}\Bigr)\cdot\Bigl(\frac{1}{8}-\frac{2\alpha}{C_{1}C_{2}}-\frac{\alpha\beta}{16}\Bigr)\Bigr).\end{split}

Using the estimate
\log(1+x)\leq x
we obtain that

(2.7)

|\EE Z^{\downarrow}-\EE Z|\leq\EE Z^{\uparrow}\leq\frac{\EE\widetilde{\Sigma}^{2}}{\rho}\leq\alpha\beta t.

Thus, combining (
2.4
), (
2.6
) and (
2.7
) and as long as

\alpha\beta\leq 1/4

and

\beta C_{1}C_{2}\geq 32(e^{2}-1)
,
we arrive at

\displaystyle\PP(Z\geq\EE Z+2t+\alpha\beta t)

\displaystyle\leq\PP(Z^{\uparrow}+Z^{\downarrow}\geq\EE Z+2t+\alpha\beta t)

\displaystyle\leq\PP(Z^{\uparrow}+Z^{\downarrow}\geq\EE Z^{\downarrow}-|\EE Z-\EE Z^{\downarrow}|+2t+\alpha\beta t)

\displaystyle\leq\PP(Z^{\uparrow}+Z^{\downarrow}\geq\EE Z^{\downarrow}+2t)

\displaystyle\leq\PP(Z^{\uparrow}\geq t)+\PP(Z^{\downarrow}\geq\EE Z^{\downarrow}+t)

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp\Bigl(-\min\Bigl(\frac{\alpha}{32},\frac{1}{8}-\frac{\alpha\beta}{16}-\frac{2\alpha}{C_{1}C_{2}}\Bigr)\cdot t\log\Bigl(1+\beta\frac{t}{\EE\widetilde{\Sigma}^{2}}\Bigr)\Bigr).

Substituting

t\leftarrow(2+\alpha\beta)^{-1}t

and estimating

\frac{1}{2+\alpha\beta}\geq\frac{4}{9}

yields

\displaystyle\begin{split}\PP(Z\geq\EE Z+t)&\leq 2\exp\Bigl(-\frac{1}{2+\alpha\beta}\min\Bigl(\frac{\alpha}{32},\frac{1}{8}-\frac{\alpha\beta}{16}-\frac{2\alpha}{C_{1}C_{2}}\Bigr)\cdot t\log\Bigl(1+\frac{\beta}{2+\alpha\beta}\frac{t}{\EE\widetilde{\Sigma}^{2}}\Bigr)\Bigr)\\
&\leq 2\exp\Bigl(-\frac{4}{9}\min\Bigl(\frac{\alpha}{32},\frac{1}{8}-\frac{\alpha\beta}{16}-\frac{2\alpha}{C_{1}C_{2}}\Bigr)\cdot t\log\Bigl(1+\frac{4\beta}{9}\frac{t}{\EE\widetilde{\Sigma}^{2}}\Bigr)\Bigr)\\
&\leq 2\exp\Bigl(-\frac{4}{9}\min\Bigl(\frac{\alpha}{32},\frac{1}{8}-\frac{\alpha\beta}{16}-\frac{2\alpha}{C_{1}C_{2}}\Bigr)\cdot t\log\Bigl(1+\frac{t}{C_{2}\EE\widetilde{\Sigma}^{2}}\Bigr)\Bigr).\end{split}

as long as

\alpha\beta\leq 1/4
,

\beta C_{1}C_{2}\geq 32(e^{2}-1)

and

4\beta C_{2}\geq 9
.
Setting

\alpha=2
and
\beta=\frac{1}{8}

yields the result with

C_{1}=36
and
C_{2}=46
.
∎

3.
Concentration for a single Hoeffding statistic

In this section, we provide concentration bounds for single Hoeffding statistics, extending the results of Chatterjee
[
6
]
, Bercu–Deylon–Rio
[
2
]
and Albert
[
1
]
.
In the sequel,
f
denotes some Hoeffding statistics, i.e.,

(3.1)

f(\sigma)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k\sigma(k)},

where
(a_{ij})_{i,j=1}^{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is some real matrix.
The main result of this section is the following theorem.
To the best of our knowledge, this is the first result that captures both the subgaussian and Poisson behaviors of Hoeffding statistics.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
f
be given by (
3.1
).
If
a_{ij}\in[-1,1]
for all
i,j
and

\sum_{ij}a_{ij}=0
, then for some absolute constants
C_{1},C_{2}>0
,

\forall\;t\geq 0\qquad\PP(f\geq t)\leq 2\exp\Bigl(-\frac{t}{C_{1}}\log\Bigl(1+\frac{t}{C_{2}\EE\Sigma^{2}}\Bigr)\Bigr),

where

\Sigma^{2}=\sum_{k}a_{k\sigma_{k}}^{2}

so that

\EE\Sigma^{2}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{ij}a_{ij}^{2}

.
One can take
C_{1}=C_{2}=36

Remark 3.2
.

As in Bercu–Deylon–Rio
[
2
]
, note that setting

d_{ij}=a_{ij}-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\bigl(a_{ik}+a_{kj}\bigr)+\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{k,l=1}^{n}a_{kl}

yields

\Var(f)=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{ij}d_{ij}^{2}

and

f-\EE f=\sum_{k=1}^{n}d_{k\sigma(k)}.

Therefore, an application of Theorem
3.1
to
(f-\EE f)/2
in place of
f
(note that
\sum_{ij}d_{ij}=0
, while
a_{ij}\in[-1,1]
are arbitrary) provides that

(3.2)

\forall\;t\geq 0\qquad\PP(f\geq\EE f+t)\leq 2\exp\Bigl(-\frac{t}{2C_{1}}\log\Bigl(1+\frac{t}{2C_{2}\Var(f)}\Bigr)\Bigr).

As shown by Hoeffding in
[
10
]
(cf. also Bolthausen
[
4
]
for a Stein method based approach), as soon as

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\max_{i,j\in[n]}d_{ij}}{\Var(S_{n})}=0,

then
f
verifies the CLT, i.e.,

\frac{f-\EE f}{\sqrt{\Var(f)}}\overset{n\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}\mathcal{N}(0,1)

in law.
Clearly, the bound from (
3.2
) becomes subgaussian for small values of
t
and whence matches the CLT behavior described above (up to numerical constants).
Similarly, if one chooses
a_{ij}={\bf 1}_{\{i=j\}}
, then
f
becomes the number of fixed points of a random permutation
\sigma
.
The exact tail distribution of
f
in such case is well known, cf.
[
7
, Section IV.4]
, and is of order
\exp(-Ct\log t)
for
t
big and some
C>0
, which agrees with the bound (
3.2
).
This shows that Theorem
3.1
is optimal up to the numerical constants.

To prove the Bennett inequality of Theorem
3.1
, we first derive it for non-negative statistics in the theorem below.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
f
be given by (
3.1
).
If
a_{ij}\in[0,1]
for all
i,j
, then

\forall\;t\geq 0\qquad\PP(f>\EE f+t)\leq\exp\Bigl(-\frac{t}{4}\log\Bigl(1+\frac{t}{4\EE f}\Bigr)\Bigr).

Remark 3.4
.

Theorem
3.3
already improves (up to numerical constants in the exponent) upon a Bernstein-type bound

\forall\;t\geq 0\qquad\PP(f>\EE f+t)\leq\exp\Bigl(-\frac{t^{2}}{4\EE f+2t}\Bigr)

obtained by Chatterjee
[
6
, Proposition 1.1]
.

Proof of Theorem
3.3
.

Since
a_{ij}\in[0,1]
, then for any
i,j
,

(3.3)

\sum_{ij}(f_{ij}-f)_{+}=\sum_{ij}(a_{i\sigma_{j}}+a_{j\sigma_{i}}-a_{i\sigma_{i}}-a_{j\sigma_{j}})_{+}\leq\sum_{ij}(a_{i\sigma_{j}}+a_{j\sigma_{i}})=2\sum_{ij}a_{ij}=2n\EE f.

By the modified log-Sobolev inequality, using (
3.3
) and convexity of
x\mapsto e^{2x}
, we arrive at

\displaystyle\Ent(e^{\lambda f})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\lambda}{n}\EE e^{\lambda f}\sum_{ij}(e^{\lambda(f_{ij}-f)_{+}}-1)(f_{ij}-f)_{+}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\lambda}{n}(e^{2\lambda}-1)\EE e^{\lambda f}\sum_{ij}(f_{ij}-f)_{+}

\displaystyle\leq 2\lambda(e^{2\lambda}-1)\EE f\EE e^{\lambda f}

\displaystyle\leq 4\lambda^{2}e^{2\lambda}\EE f\EE e^{\lambda f}

for all
\lambda\geq 0
.
Hence, using Proposition
C.1
with
a=4\EE f
,
b=2
gives the conclusion.
∎

Finally, to prove Theorem
3.1
, we need the following proposition.
We defer its proof to Section
4
.

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
f
be given by (
3.1
).
If
a_{ij}\in[-1,1]
for all
i,j
, then

\forall\;t\geq 0\qquad\PP(f\geq\EE f+t)\leq\exp\Bigl(-\min\Bigl(\frac{t}{32},\frac{t^{2}}{128\EE{\Sigma}^{2}}\Bigr)\Bigr),

where
\Sigma^{2}=\sum_{k}a_{k\sigma_{k}}^{2}
so that

\EE\Sigma^{2}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{ij}a_{ij}^{2}
.

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

For a fixed
t>0
, set

\rho^{-1}=2\log\bigl(1+\frac{t}{16\EE\Sigma^{2}}\bigr)

and denote

f^{\downarrow}(\sigma)=\sum_{i}a_{i\sigma_{i}}{\bf 1}_{\{|a_{i\sigma_{i}}|\leq\rho\}}

and

f^{\uparrow}(\sigma)=\sum_{i}|a_{i\sigma_{i}}|{\bf 1}_{\{|a_{i\sigma_{i}}|>\rho\}}

so that
f\leq f^{\downarrow}+f^{\uparrow}
.
We estimate the tail probabilities for
f^{\downarrow}
and
f^{\uparrow}
.

By the estimate
\log(1+x)\leq x
and by the definition of
\rho
,

\frac{t}{32\rho}\leq\frac{t^{2}}{256\EE\Sigma^{2}}\leq\frac{t^{2}}{128\EE\Sigma^{2}},

whence by Proposition
3.5
applied to
f^{\downarrow}/\rho
,

(3.4)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\PP(f^{\downarrow}\geq\EE f^{\downarrow}+t)&\leq\exp\Bigl(-\min\Bigl(\frac{t}{32\rho},\frac{t^{2}}{128\EE\Sigma^{2}}\Bigr)\Bigr)\\
&=\exp\Bigl(-\frac{t}{32\rho}\Bigr)=\exp\Bigl(-\frac{t}{16}\log\bigl(1+\frac{t}{16\EE\Sigma^{2}}\bigr)\Bigr).\end{split}

By the definitions of
f^{\uparrow},\rho
and estimate
\log(1+x)\leq 2\log(1+\sqrt{x})\leq 2\sqrt{x}
,

\EE f^{\uparrow}\leq\frac{\EE\Sigma^{2}}{\rho}=2(\EE\Sigma^{2})\log\Bigl(1+\frac{t}{16\EE\Sigma^{2}}\Bigr)\leq\sqrt{t\EE\Sigma^{2}},

whence by Theorem
3.3
applied to
f^{\uparrow}
,

(3.5)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\PP(f^{\uparrow}\geq\EE f^{\uparrow}+t)&\leq\exp\Bigl(-\frac{t}{4}\log\Bigl(1+\frac{t}{4\EE f^{\uparrow}}\Bigr)\Bigr)\\
&\leq\exp\Bigl(-\frac{t}{4}\log\Bigl(1+\frac{1}{4}\sqrt{\frac{t}{\EE\Sigma^{2}}}\Bigr)\Bigr)\\
&\leq\exp\Bigl(-\frac{t}{8}\log\Bigl(1+\frac{t}{16\EE\Sigma^{2}}\Bigr)\Bigr),\end{split}

where in the last step we have used again the estimate
2\log(1+\sqrt{x})\geq\log(1+x)
.
Using the assumption
\EE f=0
, triangle inequality and estimating
\log(1+x)\leq x
, we obtain

(3.6)

|\EE f^{\downarrow}|=|\EE f^{\downarrow}-\EE f|\leq\EE f^{\uparrow}\leq\frac{\EE\Sigma^{2}}{\rho}\leq\frac{1}{8}t.

By combining (
3.4
), (
3.5
) and (
3.6
) we arrive at

\displaystyle\PP(f\geq 9t/4)

\displaystyle\leq\PP(f^{\downarrow}\geq 9t/8)+\PP(f^{\uparrow}\geq 9t/8)

\displaystyle\leq\PP(f^{\downarrow}\geq\EE f^{\downarrow}+t)+\PP(f^{\uparrow}\geq\EE f^{\uparrow}+t)

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp\Bigl(-\frac{t}{16}\log\Bigl(1+\frac{t}{16\EE\Sigma^{2}}\Bigr)\Bigr).

Substituting
t\leftarrow 4t/9
yields the result.
∎

Proof of Propositions
2.5
and
3.5

Both propositions are special cases of a more general result for suprema of Hoeffding statistics which we provide below.
Let
R\subset\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be a set of real matrices.
Denote

(4.1)

S=\sup_{r\in R}\sum_{k=1}^{n}r_{k\sigma_{k}}.

The main result of this section is the following estimate.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
S
be given by (
4.1
) and assume

R\subset[-1,1]^{n\times n}
.
Then

\forall\;t\geq 0\qquad\PP(S\geq\EE S+t)\leq\exp\Bigl(-\min\Bigl(\frac{t}{32},\frac{t^{2}}{128\EE\Sigma_{R}^{2}}\Bigr)\Bigr),

where

\Sigma_{R}^{2}=\sup_{r\in R}\sum_{k}r_{k\sigma_{k}}^{2}
.

Propositions
2.5
and
3.5
are special cases of Proposition
4.1
as illustrated below.

Proof of Proposition
2.5
.

Apply Proposition
4.1
with
R=\{\,a^{x}\colon x\in\mathcal{X}\,\}
(recall the definition of the matrix
a^{x}
introduced at the beginning of Section
2
).
∎

Proof of Propositoin
3.5
.

Apply Proposition
4.1
with
R=\{a\}
.
∎

To prove Proposition
4.1
, let us first state the modified log-Sobolev inequality (
1.1
) for the Laplace transform of
S
.
For any
i,j\in[n]
, denote

S_{ij}=\sup_{r\in R}\sum_{k=1}^{n}r_{k\sigma_{ij}(k)}.

Then, the modified log-Sobolev inequality (
1.1
) implies that

\Ent(e^{\lambda S})\leq\frac{\lambda}{n}\EE\Bigl[e^{\lambda S}\sum_{ij}(1-e^{-\lambda(S-S_{ij})})_{+}(S-S_{ij})_{+}\Bigr],

which after estimating
1-e^{-x}\leq x
can be further specialized to

(4.2)

\Ent(e^{\lambda S})\leq\frac{\lambda}{n}\EE\Bigl[e^{\lambda S}\sum_{ij}(S-S_{ij})_{+}^{2}\Bigr].

We need also the following auxiliary fact.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
S
be given by (
4.1
) and assume
R\subset[0,1]^{n\times n}
.
Then

\forall\;\lambda\in[0,1/4]\qquad\log\EE e^{\lambda S}\leq 2\lambda\EE S.

Proof.

Assume w.l.o.g. that
R
is finite.
Let
\hat{r}
be a random matrix taking values in
R
such that
S=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\hat{r}_{k\sigma_{k}}
.
We have

(4.3)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\sum_{ij}(S-S_{ij})_{+}^{2}&\leq\sum_{ij}(\hat{r}_{i\sigma_{i}}+\hat{r}_{j\sigma_{j}}-\hat{r}_{i\sigma_{j}}-\hat{r}_{j\sigma_{i}})_{+}^{2}\\
&\leq\sum_{ij}(\hat{r}_{i\sigma_{i}}+\hat{r}_{j\sigma_{j}})^{2}\leq 2n\sum_{i}(\hat{r}_{i\sigma_{i}})^{2}\leq 2nS,\end{split}

where in the last inequality we have used that
R\in[0,1]^{n\times n}
.

By the modified log-Sobolev inequality (
4.2
) combined with (
4.3
), we arrive at

\displaystyle\Ent(e^{\lambda S})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\lambda^{2}}{n}\EE\Bigl[e^{\lambda S}\sum_{ij}(S-S_{ij})_{+}^{2}\Bigr]\leq 2\lambda^{2}\EE[e^{\lambda S}S]

for all
\lambda\geq 0
.
Applying Proposition
C.2
with
a=2
,
b=0
results in

(1-2\lambda)\log\EE e^{\lambda S}\leq\lambda\EE S,

for all
\lambda\geq 0
, which yields the conclusion.
∎

We are in position to prove Proposition
4.1
.

Proof of Proposition
4.1
.

Let
\hat{r}
be a random matrix taking values in
R
such that
S=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\hat{r}_{k\sigma_{k}}
.
By the triangle inequality in
\ell^{2}
,

(4.4)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\sum_{ij}(S-S_{ij})_{+}^{2}&\leq\sum_{ij}(\hat{r}_{i\sigma_{i}}+\hat{r}_{j\sigma_{j}}-\hat{r}_{i\sigma_{j}}-\hat{r}_{j\sigma_{i}})_{+}^{2}\\
&\leq 8\sum_{ij}\hat{r}_{i\sigma_{i}}^{2}+8\sum_{ij}\hat{r}_{i\sigma_{j}}^{2}\leq 8n\Sigma_{R}^{2}+8\sum_{ij}\hat{r}_{i\sigma_{j}}^{2}.\end{split}

Note that

\sum_{ij}\hat{r}_{i\sigma_{j}}^{2}=\sum_{ij}\hat{r}_{ij}^{2}=n\EE\sum_{i}\hat{r}_{i\sigma_{i}}^{2}\leq n\EE\sup_{r\in R}\sum_{i}r_{i\sigma_{i}}^{2}=n\EE\Sigma_{R}^{2},

whence (
4.4
) can be further specialized to

(4.5)

\sum_{ij}(S-S_{ij})_{+}^{2}\leq 8n(\Sigma_{R}^{2}+\EE\Sigma_{R}^{2}).

By the modified log-Sobolev inequality (
4.2
) combined with (
4.5
), we arrive at

(4.6)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Ent(e^{\lambda S})&\leq\frac{\lambda^{2}}{n}\EE\Bigl[e^{\lambda S}\sum_{ij}(S-S_{ij})_{+}^{2}\Bigr]\leq 8\lambda^{2}\bigl((\EE e^{\lambda S})(\EE{\Sigma}_{R}^{2})+\EE[e^{\lambda S}\Sigma_{R}^{2}]\bigr).\end{split}

Recall the variational formula for entropy
\Ent(h)=\sup\bigl\{\,\EE hg\colon\EE e^{g}\leq 1\,\bigr\}
, from which it follows that for any
h,g

(4.7)

\EE hg\leq\Ent(h)+(\EE h)\log(\EE e^{g}).

Applying first (
4.7
) with
h=e^{\lambda S}
,
g=\Sigma_{R}^{2}/4
and then Lemma
4.2
yields

\EE\bigl[e^{\lambda S}\Sigma_{R}^{2}\bigr]\leq 4\Ent(e^{\lambda S})+4\bigl(\EE e^{\lambda S}\bigr)\bigl(\log\EE e^{\Sigma_{R}^{2}/4}\bigr)\leq 4\Ent(e^{\lambda S})+2\bigl(\EE e^{\lambda S}\bigr)\bigl(\EE\Sigma_{R}^{2}\bigr),

which combined with (
4.6
) results in

\displaystyle(1-32\lambda^{2})\Ent(e^{\lambda S})\leq 24\lambda^{2}\bigl(\EE{\Sigma_{R}}^{2}\bigr)\bigl(\EE e^{\lambda S}\bigr)

for all
\lambda\geq 0
, so that

\displaystyle\Ent(e^{\lambda S})\leq\frac{192}{7}\lambda^{2}\bigl(\EE{\Sigma_{R}}^{2}\bigr)\bigl(\EE e^{\lambda S}\bigr)\leq 32\lambda^{2}\bigl(\EE{\Sigma_{R}}^{2}\bigr)\bigl(\EE e^{\lambda S}\bigr)

for all
\lambda\in[0,1/16]
.
We conclude by applying Proposition
C.3
with
\varepsilon=\frac{1}{16}
and
b=32\EE{\Sigma}^{2}_{R}
.
∎
