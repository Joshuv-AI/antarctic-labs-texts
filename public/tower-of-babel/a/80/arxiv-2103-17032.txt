Weighted SPICE Algorithms for Range-Doppler Imaging Using One-Bit Automotive Radar
Xiaolei Shang, Jian Li, Petre Stoica

Abstract
We consider the problem of range-Doppler imaging using one-bit automotive LFMCW 1 1 1 All abbreviations used in this paper are explained at the end of the Introduction. or PMCW radar that utilizes one-bit ADC sampling with time-varying thresholds at the receiver. The one-bit sampling technique can significantly reduce the cost as well as the power consumption of automotive radar systems. We formulate the one-bit LFMCW/PMCW radar range-Doppler imaging problem as one-bit sparse parameter estimation. The recently proposed hyperparameter-free (and hence user friendly) weighted SPICE algorithms, including SPICE, LIKES, SLIM and IAA, achieve excellent parameter estimation performance for data sampled with high precision. However, these algorithms cannot be used directly for one-bit data. In this paper we first present a regularized minimization algorithm, referred to as 1bSLIM, for accurate range-Doppler imaging using one-bit radar systems. Then, we describe how to extend the SPICE, LIKES and IAA algorithms to the one-bit data case, and refer to these extensions as 1bSPICE, 1bLIKES and 1bIAA. These one-bit hyperparameter-free algorithms are unified within the one-bit weighted SPICE framework. Moreover, efficient implementations of the aforementioned algorithms are investigated that rely heavily on the use of FFTs. Finally, both simulated and experimental examples are provided to demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed algorithms for range-Doppler imaging using one-bit automotive radar systems.



I
Introduction

Millimeter wave radar is widely used in diverse applications including advanced driver assistance automotive systems and fully autonomous vehicles
[
1
]
. Compared with other sensing systems, such as cameras, lidars and ultrasonics, radar systems can provide better performance, especially in poor lighting or adverse weather conditions
[
2
]
.

The most commonly used probing waveform in existing commercial automotive radar systems is the LFMCW waveform, which reduces the system cost by allowing low-rate ADCs at the radar receivers. An LFMCW radar system transmits a series of chirps, which are reflected by the targets and mixed with the transmitted chirp at the receiver. The range or range-Doppler estimation problem is converted to a 1-D or 2-D complex sinusoidal parameter estimation problem
[
3
,
4
]
. However, mutual interferences among LFMCW automotive radars become more severe as these radar systems become more widely used, since they occupy the same 77-81 GHz band. PMCW waveforms can be used to mitigate the interference problems. To reduce costs, binary sequences with desirable auto- and cross-correlation properties have been designed for PMCW automotive radars (see, e.g.,
[
5
]
and the references therein). The flexibility and diversity offered by binary sequences allow the PMCW radars to mitigate mutual interferences similar to the CDMA communication systems
[
6
]
. Also, near orthogonality can be realized in the coding domain, which is advantageous for MIMO operations
[
7
,
8
]
.

To improve the range resolution of automotive radars, the fundamental approach is to increase the bandwidth of the transmitted signal, which can increase the ADC cost and power consumption. For example, a PMCW radar usually performs demodulation in the digital domain
[
9
]
. A PMCW radar with a chip rate of 2 GHz requires a sampling rate of at least 2 GHz
[
10
]
. However, an ADC with high precision quantization and high sampling rate is both expensive and power hungry. Yet low cost and low power consumption are crucial for commercial radar systems such as automotive radar.

One-bit sampling is a promising technique to mitigate the aforementioned ADC problems because of its low cost and low power consumption advantages. Due to these attractive properties, one-bit sampling has been considered for radar sensing
[
11
,
12
]
, spectral estimation
[
13
,
14
,
15
]
, as well as massive MIMO communications
[
16
,
17
,
18
]
. The conventional one-bit sampling considered in, e.g.,
[
19
,
20
,
21
]
, compares the original signal with a zero threshold, which results in a loss of signal amplitude information. Recently, time-varying thresholds have been considered for one-bit sampling to achieve accurate amplitude estimation
[
22
,
15
]
. In such a case, signed measurements are obtained by comparing the received signal with a known time-varying threshold.

In this paper, we consider one-bit sampling with time-varying thresholds for automotive radars to reduce cost and power consumption. We formulate the one-bit range-Doppler estimation problem as a one-bit sparse parameter estimation problem. Sparse parameter estimation using high-precision data has attracted much attention in the last two decades. Many algorithms have been developed with the goal of obtaining accurate sparse parameter estimates. Among them, the unified weighted SPICE algorithms
[
23
]
, including SPICE
[
24
]
, LIKES
[
25
]
, SLIM
[
26
]
and IAA
[
27
,
28
,
29
]
, do not require the tuning of any user-parameter and hence are easy to use in practice. These algorithms yield excellent estimation performance, in particular they possess high resolution and low sidelobe properties. Furthermore, IAA works well in various environments and the robustness of IAA has been ranked high in a recent review article
[
30
]
. However, these algorithms, together with the conventional FFT, cannot be used directly to solve one-bit sparse parameter estimation problems. Herein, we first present a regularized minimization algorithm, referred to as 1bSLIM, for accurate range-Doppler imaging using one-bit automotive radar. Additionally, we show that 1bSLIM, devised by means of a majorization-minimization approach
[
31
,
32
,
33
,
34
,
35
]
, can be interpreted as applying the conventional SLIM algorithm to certain modified high-precision data. Inspired by this relationship, we describe how to extend SPICE, LIKES and IAA to the one-bit case, and refer to these extensions as 1bSPICE, 1bLIKES and 1bIAA. Each of the algorithms we present herein has its own merits and may be preferred in a specific application. Similar to the original weighted SPICE algorithms, which unify SPICE, LIKES, SLIM and IAA under the same weighted SPICE umbrella, the aforementioned one-bit algorithms are unified within the one-bit weighted SPICE framework. Moreover, these four one-bit algorithms are all hyperparameter-free and thus are easy to use in practice. Finally, simulated and experimental examples are provided to demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed algorithms for range-Doppler imaging using one-bit automotive radars.

II
Problem Formulation

In this section, we first briefly describe the signal models for the LFMCW and PMCW radars. Then, using the signed measurements obtained by one-bit sampling with time-varying thresholds, we formulate the range-Doppler imaging problem as a sparse parameter estimation problem.

II-A

LFMCW Radar

Without loss of generality, we consider an LFMCW single-input single-output radar. A simplified model for the received signal (after dechirping and pre-processing) is as follows (see e.g.,
[
11
]
):

y(n_{1},n_{2})=\sum_{k_{\rm d}=1}^{K_{\rm d}}\sum_{k_{\rm r}=1}^{K_{\rm r}}\gamma_{k_{\rm r},k_{\rm d}}e^{j(\omega_{k_{\rm r}}n_{1}+\bar{\omega}_{k_{\rm d}}n_{2})}+e(n_{1},n_{2}),

(1)

where
K_{\rm r}
and
K_{\rm d}
are the numbers of the grid points in the range and Doppler domains, respectively; the fast-time index
n_{1},n_{1}=1,2,\dots,N_{1}
, corresponds to the
n_{1}
th sample within a chirp; the slow-time index
n_{2},n_{2}=1,2,\dots,N_{2}
, is for the
n_{2}
th chirp;
\gamma_{k_{\rm r},k_{\rm d}}
represents the reflection coefficient of a scatterer corresponding to the frequency pair
(\omega_{k_{\rm r}},\bar{\omega}_{k_{\rm d}})
; and
e(n_{1},n_{2})
is the unknown additive noise. The frequency pair
(\omega_{k_{\rm r}},\bar{\omega}_{k_{\rm d}})
is approximately related to the range-Doppler pair
(R_{k_{\rm r}},v_{k_{\rm d}})
through the following equations:

\omega_{k_{\rm r}}=2\pi\left(\frac{4\mu R_{k_{\rm r}}}{cf_{\rm s}}+\frac{2f_{0}v_{k_{\rm d}}}{cf_{\rm s}}\right),\quad\bar{\omega}_{k_{\rm d}}=\frac{4\pi f_{0}v_{k_{\rm d}}T_{\rm c}}{c},

(2)

where
2\mu
is the chirp rate;
c
is the speed of light;
f_{\rm s}
is the sampling frequency;
f_{0}
denotes the carrier frequency; and
T_{\rm c}
is the chirp duration. The range-Doppler information can therefore be recovered from the frequency pair
(\omega_{k_{\rm r}},\bar{\omega}_{k_{\rm d}})
by using Equation (
2
). The range-Doppler estimation problem associated with (
1
) for an LFMCW radar is equivalent to a 2-D complex-valued sinusoidal parameter estimation problem. Let the measurement vector be denoted
\boldsymbol{y}=\begin{bmatrix}y(1,1),y(2,1),\dots,y(N_{1},N_{2})\end{bmatrix}^{T}
and let
\boldsymbol{e}
denote the unknown noise vector. Let the unknown parameter vector be denoted
\boldsymbol{\gamma}=\begin{bmatrix}\gamma_{1,1},\gamma_{2,1},\dots,\gamma_{K_{\rm r},K_{\rm d}}\end{bmatrix}^{T}
. Equation (
1
) can then be reformulated as:

\boldsymbol{y}=\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{\gamma}+\boldsymbol{e}\in\mathbb{C}^{N},\quad\boldsymbol{B}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times M},\quad M\gg N,

(3)

where
N=N_{1}N_{2}
and
M=K_{\rm r}K_{\rm d}
.
K_{\rm r}\gg N_{1}
and
K_{\rm d}\gg N_{2}
are typical assumptions used by sparse parameter estimation algorithms to form a sufficiently fine grid, which enables us to obtain high resolution spectral estimates and excellent results even when the true target frequencies do not lie on the grid. Let
\boldsymbol{\psi}_{N}(\omega)=\begin{bmatrix}1,\ e^{j\omega},\dots,e^{j(N-1)\omega}\end{bmatrix}^{T}
. Then, the columns of
\boldsymbol{B}
are given by
\{\boldsymbol{\psi}_{N_{1},N_{2}}\left(\omega_{k_{\rm r}},\bar{\omega}_{k_{\rm d}}\right)\}_{k_{\rm r}=1,k_{\rm d}=1}^{K_{\rm r},K_{\rm d}}
with
\boldsymbol{\psi}_{N_{1},N_{2}}(\omega_{k_{\rm r}},\bar{\omega}_{k_{\rm d}})=\boldsymbol{\psi}_{N_{2}}(\bar{\omega}_{k_{\rm d}})\otimes\boldsymbol{\psi}_{N_{1}}(\omega_{k_{\rm r}})
.

For radar range-Doppler imaging applications, the number of potential scatterers of interest in a scene is usually much smaller than the number of grid points, which means that most of the elements in
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
will be zero. Hence, the LFMCW radar range-Doppler imaging problem in (
3
) can be treated as a sparse parameter estimation problem.

II-B

PMCW Radar

The PMCW radar periodically transmits a binary sequence
\boldsymbol{s}\!=\begin{bmatrix}s(0),\ s(1),\dots,s(N_{1}-1)\end{bmatrix}^{T}
, where
N_{1}
is the sequence length and each element of
\boldsymbol{s}
is either
1
or
-1
, i.e.,
s(n_{1})\in\{1,-1\}
. Then, the transmitted baseband signal can be written as:

x(t)=\sum_{n_{2}=-\infty}^{\infty}\sum_{n_{1}=0}^{N_{1}-1}s(n_{1})p(t-n_{1}T_{\rm s}-n_{2}T_{\rm p}),

(4)

where
T_{\rm s}
is the so-called chip duration;
T_{\rm p}=N_{1}T_{\rm s}
is the PRI; and
p(t)
is the impulse response of a pulse-shaping filter which, without loss of generality, is chosen as a raised-cosine with a roll-off factor of 0.25. For the convenience of the subsequent signal processing, the pulse shaping filter is assumed to be non-zero in the interval
\left(-5T_{\rm s},5T_{\rm s}\right]
. Let the range and Doppler domains within the CPI be grided into
K_{\rm r}
and
K_{\rm d}
grid points, respectively. Then, at the receiver, we have:

y(t)=\sum_{k_{\rm r}=1}^{K_{\rm r}}\sum_{k_{\rm d}=1}^{K_{\rm d}}\gamma_{k_{\rm r},k_{\rm d}}x(t-\tau_{k_{\rm r}})e^{j2\pi f_{k_{\rm d}}t}+e(t),

(5)

where
\tau_{k_{\rm r}}
,
f_{k_{\rm d}}
, and
\gamma_{k_{\rm r},k_{\rm d}}
represent the round-trip time delay, the Doppler frequency, and the corresponding reflection coefficient, respectively; and
e(t)
is the unknown additive noise. For a real-valued time delay
\tau_{k_{r}}
, we can split it into the integer part and the fractional part, i.e.,
\tau_{k_{\rm r}}=l_{k_{\rm r}}T_{\rm s}+\bar{\tau}_{k_{\rm r}}
, where
l_{k_{\rm r}}=\lfloor\tau_{k_{\rm r}}/T_{\rm s}\rfloor
with
\lfloor\cdot\rfloor
being the floor operation. Note that the echo sequence with time delay
\tau_{k_{\rm r}}=l_{k_{\rm r}}T_{\rm s}+\bar{\tau}_{k_{\rm r}}
circularly shifts the transmitted sequence by
l_{k_{r}}
positions, as shown in Fig.
1
. The echo signal with a real-valued time delay
\tau_{k_{\rm r}}
for the
n_{2}
th period (i.e.,
n_{2}T_{\rm p}\leq t<(n_{2}+1)T_{\rm p}
) can be written as:

\displaystyle x(t-\tau_{k_{\rm r}})=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=-5}^{N_{1}+4}s\left(({N_{1}}+i-l_{k_{\rm r}})\ {\rm mod}\ N_{1}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\cdot p\left(t-n_{2}T_{\rm p}-iT_{\rm s}-\bar{\tau}_{k_{\rm r}}\right).

(6)

We assume that there are
N_{2}
periods during one CPI and the Doppler shift within one period is so small that it can be neglected
[
10
,
9
]
. Then, the received baseband signal for the
n_{2}
th period can be written as:

\displaystyle y_{n_{2}}(t)=\sum_{k_{\rm r},k_{\rm d}}\sum_{i=-5}^{N_{1}+4}\gamma_{k_{\rm r},k_{\rm d}}s\left(({N_{1}}+i-l_{k_{r}})\ {\rm mod}\ N_{1}\right)

\displaystyle\cdot p\left(t-n_{2}T_{\rm p}-iT_{\rm s}-\bar{\tau}_{k_{r}}\right)e^{j\bar{\omega}_{k_{\rm d}}n_{2}}+e(t),

(7)

where
\bar{\omega}_{k_{\rm d}}=2\pi f_{k_{\rm d}}T_{\rm p}
. We insert
t=n_{2}T_{\rm p}+n_{1}T_{\rm s}
into
x(t-\tau_{k_{\rm r}})
and define:

\displaystyle\bar{s}(n_{1},\tau_{k_{r}})

\displaystyle\triangleq x(n_{2}T_{p}+n_{1}T_{s}-\tau_{k_{r}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=-5}^{4}s\left((N_{1}+n_{1}+i-l_{k_{\rm r}})\ {\rm mod}\ N_{1}\right)\cdot p(iT_{\rm s}+\bar{\tau}_{k_{r}}).

(8)

Note that
p(iT_{\rm s})=0
,
i\neq 0
. If
\bar{\tau}_{k_{\rm r}}
is equal to 0, then
\bar{s}(n_{1},\tau_{k_{\rm r}})
becomes
s((N_{1}+n_{1}-l_{k_{\rm r}})\ {\rm mod}\ N_{1})
.
Next, assume that
N_{1}
samples are captured at instants
n_{2}T_{\rm p},\dots,n_{2}T_{\rm p}+(N_{1}-1)T_{\rm s}
and let
\boldsymbol{y}_{n_{2}}=\begin{bmatrix}y(n_{2}T_{\rm p}),\dots,y(n_{2}T_{\rm p}+(N_{1}-1)T_{\rm s})\end{bmatrix}^{T}
. Then,
\boldsymbol{y}_{n_{2}}
can be written as:

\boldsymbol{y}_{n_{2}}=\boldsymbol{S}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{n_{2}}+\boldsymbol{e}_{n_{2}},

(9)

where the reflection coefficient matrix
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}
and the steering vector
\boldsymbol{\phi}_{n_{2}}
are given by:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Gamma}

\displaystyle=\left[\begin{matrix}\gamma_{1,1}&\gamma_{1,2}&\dots&\gamma_{1,K_{\rm d}}\\
\gamma_{2,1}&\gamma_{2,2}&\dots&\gamma_{2,K_{\rm d}}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\gamma_{K_{\rm r},1}&\gamma_{K_{\rm r},2}&\dots&\gamma_{K_{\rm r},K_{\rm d}}\end{matrix}\right]\in\mathbb{C}^{K_{\rm r}\times K_{\rm d}},

(10)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\phi}_{n_{2}}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}e^{j\bar{\omega}_{1}n_{2}},&e^{j\bar{\omega}_{2}n_{2}},\dots,&e^{j\bar{\omega}_{K_{\rm d}}n_{2}}\end{bmatrix}^{T},

(11)

and
\boldsymbol{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{1}\times K_{\rm r}}
is defined as:

\boldsymbol{S}=\left[\begin{matrix}\bar{s}(0,\tau_{1})&\bar{s}(0,\tau_{2})&\dots&\bar{s}(0,\tau_{K_{\rm r}})\\
\bar{s}(1,\tau_{1})&\bar{s}(1,\tau_{2})&\dots&\bar{s}(1,\tau_{K_{\rm r}})\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\bar{s}(N_{1}-1,\tau_{1})&\bar{s}(N_{1}-1,\tau_{2})&\dots&\bar{s}(N_{1}-1,\tau_{K_{\rm r}})\\
\end{matrix}\right].

(12)

Let

\boldsymbol{Y}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{y}_{0},\boldsymbol{y}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{y}_{N_{2}-1}\end{bmatrix},

(13)

\boldsymbol{\Phi}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{1},\boldsymbol{\phi}_{2},\dots,\boldsymbol{\phi}_{N_{2}}\end{bmatrix}^{T},

(14)

and

\boldsymbol{E}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{e}_{1},\boldsymbol{e}_{2},\dots,\boldsymbol{e}_{N_{2}}\end{bmatrix}.

(15)

Then, the observed data matrix can be expressed compactly as follows:

\boldsymbol{Y}=\boldsymbol{S}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{T}+\boldsymbol{E}.

(16)

Let
\boldsymbol{\gamma}={\rm vec}(\boldsymbol{\Gamma})
,
\boldsymbol{y}={\rm vec}(\boldsymbol{Y})
and
\boldsymbol{e}={\rm vec}(\boldsymbol{E})
.
Then, the data model in (
16
) can be rewritten as:

\boldsymbol{y}=\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{\gamma}+\boldsymbol{e}\in\mathbb{C}^{N},\ \boldsymbol{B}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times M},\quad M\gg N,

(17)

where
\boldsymbol{B}=\boldsymbol{\Phi}\otimes\boldsymbol{S}
is given,
N=N_{1}N_{2}
and
M=K_{\rm r}K_{\rm d}
. Similar to the LFMCW radar case, the range-Doppler imaging problem for PMCW radar can also be treated as a sparse parameter estimation problem with the unknown parameter vector
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
being sparse.

II-C

One-Bit Quantization

Applying one-bit quantization at the receivers of both LFMCW and PMCW radars, we obtain the signed measurement vector
\boldsymbol{z}=\boldsymbol{z}_{\rm R}+j\boldsymbol{z}_{\rm I}
by comparing the unquantized received signal
\boldsymbol{y}
with a known time-varying threshold
\boldsymbol{h}\triangleq\boldsymbol{h}_{\rm R}+j\boldsymbol{h}_{\rm I}
:

\boldsymbol{z}={\rm signc}\left(\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{\gamma}-\boldsymbol{h}+\boldsymbol{e}\right),

(18)

where
{\rm signc}(x)={\rm sign}\left({\rm Re}[x]\right)+j{\rm sign}\left({\rm Im}[x]\right)
and

\displaystyle{\rm sign}(x)=\begin{cases}\ 1&x\geqslant 0,\\
-1&x<0.\end{cases}

(19)

The problem of interest herein is to find an accurate sparse estimate of
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
given the signed measurement vector
\boldsymbol{z}
. In the following sections, we present several approaches to solving the range-Doppler imaging problem for one-bit automotive radars.

III
Overview of the Weighted SPICE framework

We begin with revisiting the SPICE algorithm for the standard high-precision data model in (
3
) or (
17
). To iteratively solve the optimization problem associated with SPICE, a cyclic approach was employed in
[
24
]
and a gradient approach was used in
[
23
]
. We show that the same weighted
{\rm SPICE\textsubscript{a}}
algorithm in
[
23
]
can be derived using an MM approach. The MM derivations of the weighted SPICE algorithms are needed later on when extending them to the one-bit data case. Additionally, we briefly review the basic idea of MM in Appendix
A
.

For notational convenience, we let
\{\gamma_{k}\}
denote the elements of the vector
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
in (
3
) or (
17
).
The SPICE algorithm makes the assumption that

E\left[\boldsymbol{e}\boldsymbol{e}^{H}\right]=\left[\begin{matrix}p_{M+1}&0&\dots&0\\
0&p_{M+2}&\dots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&\dots&\dots&p_{M+N}\end{matrix}\right],

(20)

and that the phases of
\{\gamma_{k}\}
are independently and uniformly
distributed in
[0,2\pi]
(see
[
24
]
for a discussion showing that these assumptions have only a marginal effect on the performance of the algorithm). Then, the covariance matrix of the data vector
\boldsymbol{y}
has the following form:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{R}

\displaystyle=E\{\boldsymbol{y}\boldsymbol{y}^{H}\}=\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{P}_{1}\boldsymbol{B}^{H}+\boldsymbol{P}_{2}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{P}\boldsymbol{A}^{H},

(21)

where
\boldsymbol{P}_{1}={\rm diag}(\boldsymbol{p}_{1})
, with
\boldsymbol{p}_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}p_{1},\dots,p_{M}\end{bmatrix}^{T}
and
p_{k}=|\gamma_{k}|^{2}
;
\boldsymbol{P}_{2}={\rm diag}(\boldsymbol{p}_{2})
, with
\boldsymbol{p}_{2}=\begin{bmatrix}p_{M+1},\dots,p_{M+N}\end{bmatrix}^{T}
; and where

\boldsymbol{A}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{B}&\boldsymbol{I}\end{bmatrix},\quad\boldsymbol{P}={\rm diag}(\boldsymbol{p}),\quad\boldsymbol{p}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{p}_{1}^{T}&\boldsymbol{p}_{2}^{T}\end{bmatrix}^{T}.

(22)

For later use, let
\boldsymbol{A}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{a}_{1},\boldsymbol{a}_{2},\dots,\boldsymbol{a}_{M+N}\end{bmatrix}
.

III-A

SPICE

SPICE minimizes the following covariance fitting criterion
[
24
]
:

\mathop{\min}_{\boldsymbol{p}}\|\boldsymbol{R}^{-1/2}\left(\boldsymbol{y}\boldsymbol{y}^{H}-\boldsymbol{R}\right)\|_{F}^{2}.

(23)

After simple calculations, the SPICE criterion can be reformulated as
[
23
]
:

\displaystyle\mathop{\min}_{\vec{p}}f_{1}\left(\vec{p}\right)

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{y}^{H}\boldsymbol{R}^{-1}\boldsymbol{y}+{\rm tr}\{\boldsymbol{R}\}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{y}^{H}\vec{R}^{-1}\vec{y}+\sum_{k=1}^{M+N}w_{k}p_{k},

(24)

where
w_{k}=\|\boldsymbol{a}_{k}\|^{2}
. While the optimization problem of minimizing (
24
) is a convex semidefinite program (SDP), the use of a generic SDP solver would be computationally intensive
[
24
]
. Using the MM technique, the optimization problem in (
24
) can be efficiently solved iteratively with closed-form solutions at each iteration. The following inequality provides an upper bound for
\boldsymbol{R}^{-1}
:

\vec{R}^{-1}\preceq(\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}^{t})^{-1}\vec{A}\hat{\boldsymbol{P}}^{t}\vec{P}^{-1}\hat{\boldsymbol{P}}^{t}\boldsymbol{A}^{H}(\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}^{t})^{-1},

(25)

where
\hat{\boldsymbol{P}}^{t}
and
\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}^{t}=\boldsymbol{A}\hat{\boldsymbol{P}}^{t}\boldsymbol{A}^{H}
denote the estimates of
\boldsymbol{P}
and
\boldsymbol{R}
at the
t
th iteration; and the equality is achieved at
\boldsymbol{P}=\hat{\boldsymbol{P}}^{t}
. While this property follows from
[
36
]
, we include a simple proof of it in Appendix
B
to make the paper self-contained. Making use of (
25
), we can obtain a majorizing function for
f_{1}\left(\vec{p}\right)
as follows:

f_{1}(\boldsymbol{p})\leq g_{1}(\vec{p}|\hat{\boldsymbol{p}}^{t})=(\bar{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{(t+1)})^{H}\boldsymbol{P}^{-1}\bar{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{(t+1)}+\sum_{k=1}^{M+N}w_{k}p_{k},

(26)

where

\displaystyle\bar{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{(t+1)}

\displaystyle=\hat{\boldsymbol{P}}^{t}\boldsymbol{A}^{H}(\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}^{t})^{-1}\boldsymbol{y}

\displaystyle\triangleq\begin{bmatrix}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{(t+1)})^{T},\bar{\gamma}^{(t+1)}_{M+1},\dots,\bar{\gamma}^{(t+1)}_{M+N}\end{bmatrix}^{T}.

(27)

Note that
\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{(t+1)}=\hat{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}^{t}\vec{B}^{H}(\hat{\vec{R}}^{t})^{-1}\vec{y}
is the LMMSE estimate of
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
corresponding to the estimate
\hat{\boldsymbol{p}}^{t}_{1}

[
37
,
23
]
. The majorizing function
g_{1}(\boldsymbol{p}|\hat{\boldsymbol{p}}^{t})
is easily minimized since

\displaystyle g_{1}(\boldsymbol{p}|\hat{\boldsymbol{p}}^{t})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{M+N}\frac{\left|\bar{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{(t+1)}_{k}\right|^{2}}{p_{k}}+w_{k}p_{k}

\displaystyle\geqslant\sum_{k=1}^{M+N}2\sqrt{w_{k}}\left|\bar{\gamma}^{(t+1)}_{k}\right|,

(28)

with the equality achieved for

\hat{p}^{(t+1)}_{k}=\left|\bar{\gamma}^{(t+1)}_{k}\right|/\sqrt{w_{k}}.

(29)

Inserting
\bar{\gamma}_{k}^{(t+1)}
into (
29
) gives:

\hat{p}_{k}^{(t+1)}=\hat{p}_{k}^{t}\left|\boldsymbol{a}_{k}^{H}(\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}^{t})^{-1}\vec{y}\right|/w_{k}^{1/2}.

(30)

Given
\hat{\boldsymbol{p}}^{(t+1)}
, SPICE updates the convariance matrix via
\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}^{(t+1)}=\boldsymbol{A}\hat{\boldsymbol{P}}^{(t+1)}\boldsymbol{A}^{H}
. Although derived differently, the updating scheme in (
30
) coincides with that of the SPICE
a
algorithm in
[
23
]
.
The global convergence of the algorithm is guaranteed by the convexity of the optimization problem and the monotonically decreasing property of the MM technique.

Once
\hat{\boldsymbol{p}}
is obtained, we can estimate
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
via the well-known LMMSE estimator:

\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}=\hat{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}\boldsymbol{B}^{H}\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{y}.

(31)

III-B

LIKES

LIKES estimates
\boldsymbol{p}
via minimizing the negative log-likelihood function:

f_{2}(\vec{p})=\boldsymbol{y}^{H}\boldsymbol{R}^{-1}\vec{y}+\ln\left|\vec{R}\right|.

(32)

Note that
\ln\left|\vec{R}\right|
is a concave function of
\boldsymbol{p}
, and hence the minimization of (
32
) is a difficult non-convex optimization problem. We again use the MM technique to decrease (
32
) efficiently at each iteration. A majorizing function for
\ln\left|\vec{R}\right|
can be easily constructed by its first-order Taylor expansion:

\ln\left|\boldsymbol{R}\right|\leq\sum_{k=1}^{M+N}\left(\boldsymbol{a}_{k}^{H}(\hat{\vec{R}}^{t})^{-1}\vec{a}_{k}\right)p_{k}+{\rm const}.

(33)

Using (
33
) yields the following majorizing function for
f_{2}(\boldsymbol{p})
:

f_{2}(\vec{p})\leq g_{2}(\vec{p}|\hat{\boldsymbol{p}}^{t})=\boldsymbol{y}^{H}\boldsymbol{R}^{-1}\vec{y}+\sum_{k=1}^{M+N}w_{k}p_{k},

(34)

with

w_{k}=\boldsymbol{a}_{k}^{H}(\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}^{t})^{-1}\boldsymbol{a}_{k}.

(35)

Different from the constant weights of the SPICE algorithm, the weights of LIKES are updated during the MM iterations. For fixed weights at the
(t+1)
th iteration, the majorizing function
g_{2}(\boldsymbol{p}|\hat{\boldsymbol{p}}^{t})
has the same form as the SPICE criterion (see (
24
)). Hence the decrease or minimization of
g_{2}(\boldsymbol{p}|\hat{\boldsymbol{p}}^{t})
can be achieved via the SPICE solver presented in the previous subsection. LIKES consists of the inner and outer iterations. At the
(t+1)
th outer iteration,

\displaystyle\hat{p}_{l+1,k}^{(t+1)}

\displaystyle=\hat{p}_{l,k}^{(t+1)}\left|\boldsymbol{a}_{k}^{H}\left(\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}_{l}^{(t+1)}\right)^{-1}\boldsymbol{y}\right|/\left(\vec{a}_{k}^{H}(\hat{\vec{R}}^{t})^{-1}\vec{a}_{k}\right)^{1/2},

\displaystyle\quad l

\displaystyle=0,\dots,L-1,

(36)

where
\hat{p}_{l,k}^{(t+1)}
and
\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}_{l}^{(t+1)}
denote the estimates of
p_{k}
and
\boldsymbol{R}
, respectively, at the
l
th inner iteration and the
(t+1)
th outer iteration, with
\hat{p}_{0,k}^{(t+1)}=\hat{p}^{t}_{k}
and
\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}^{(t+1)}_{0}=\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}^{t}
; and
L
denotes the number of inner iterations. In practice,
L
can be a small number or even just
1
. After
L
inner iterations, we let
\hat{p}_{k}^{(t+1)}=\hat{p}_{L,k}^{(t+1)}
and
\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}^{(t+1)}=\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}_{L}^{(t+1)}
, and update the weights
\{w_{k}\}
according to (
35
) and continue with the next outer iteration. LIKES converges locally, but the global convergence of the algorithm is difficult to analyze due to the fact that (
32
) is a non-convex function of
\boldsymbol{p}
.

III-C

SLIM

By making use of a slightly different form of the sparsity enforcing term of LIKES, the criterion optimized by SLIM has the following form:

f_{3}(\vec{\vec{p}})=\vec{y}^{H}\vec{R}^{-1}\vec{y}+\ln\left|\vec{P}\right|.

(37)

Again,
\ln\left|\vec{P}\right|
is a concave function of
\boldsymbol{p}
and a majorizing function can be constructed by a first-order Taylor expansion:

\ln\left|\vec{P}\right|=\sum_{k=1}^{M+N}\ln\left|p_{k}\right|\leq\sum_{k=1}^{M+N}\frac{p_{k}}{\hat{p}^{t}_{k}}+{\rm const}.

(38)

Using (
25
) and (
38
) yields the following majorizing function for
f_{3}(\vec{p})
:

f_{3}(\vec{p})\leq g_{3}(\vec{p}|\hat{\boldsymbol{p}}^{t})=(\bar{\vec{\gamma}}^{(t+1)})^{H}\vec{P}^{-1}\bar{\vec{\gamma}}^{(t+1)}+\sum_{k=1}^{M+N}w_{k}p_{k},

(39)

where
w_{k}=1/{\hat{p}_{k}^{t}}
. Minimizing
g_{3}(\boldsymbol{p}|\hat{\boldsymbol{p}}^{t})
yields the
(t+1)
th updating formula of SLIM:

\hat{p}^{(t+1)}_{k}=(\hat{p}_{k}^{t})^{\frac{3}{2}}\left|\boldsymbol{a}^{H}(\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}^{t})^{-1}\vec{y}\right|.

(40)

Similar to LIKES, SLIM monotonically decreases the objective function in (
37
) and converges locally.

III-D

IAA

There is no known objective function-based derivation of IAA that is similar to (
24
), (
32
) or (
37
) above. According to the approximate interpretation of this algorithm in
[
27
,
28
]
, the weight of IAA is given by:

w_{k}=\hat{p}_{k}^{t}\left(\boldsymbol{a}^{H}_{k}(\hat{\vec{R}}^{t})^{-1}\boldsymbol{a}_{k}\right)^{2}.

(41)

Inserting (
41
) in (
30
) yields the IAA updating formula:

\hat{p}_{k}^{(t+1)}=(\hat{p}_{k}^{t})^{1/2}\left|\boldsymbol{a}_{k}^{H}(\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}^{t})^{-1}\vec{y}\right|/\left(\vec{a}_{k}^{H}(\hat{\vec{R}}^{t})^{-1}\vec{a}_{k}\right).

(42)

All four algorithms above can be unified under a weighted SPICE framework (as mentioned above, IAA can be cast in this framework only approximately):

\mathop{\min}_{\boldsymbol{p}}\boldsymbol{y}^{H}\boldsymbol{R}^{-1}\boldsymbol{y}+\sum_{k=1}^{M+N}w_{k}p_{k}.

(43)

The different choices of the weights
\{w_{k}\}
in (
24
), (
35
), (
39
) and (
41
) lead to four different hyperparameter-free algorithms. SPICE has constant weights, while LIKES, SLIM and IAA use adaptive weights and can be interpreted as adaptively reweighted SPICE methods.

IV
One-bit SLIM

In this section, we present a regularized minimization approach, referred to as 1bSLIM, for the one-bit case. After one-bit quantization, the linear model (
3
) or (
17
) becomes the nonlinear model (
18
). The problem is to estimate
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
from the one-bit quantized signal
\boldsymbol{z}
, making use of the fact that the time-varying threshold
\boldsymbol{h}
is known.

Assume that
\boldsymbol{e}
is i.i.d. circularly symmetric complex-valued white Gaussian noise with zero-mean and unknown variance
\sigma^{2}
, i.e.,
{\rm Re}\left[e(n)\right]\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2})
and
{\rm Im}\left[e(n)\right]\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2})
. Then, the negative log-likelihood function of the signed measurement vector is given by
[
38
,
22
]
:

\displaystyle L(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\sigma)=-\sum_{n=1}^{N}\ln\left(\Phi\left(z_{\rm R}(n)\frac{{\rm Re}\left[\boldsymbol{b}_{n}^{T}\boldsymbol{\gamma}\right]-h_{\rm R}(n)}{\sigma/\sqrt{2}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle-\sum_{n=1}^{N}\ln\left(\Phi\left(z_{\rm I}(n)\frac{{\rm Im}\left[\boldsymbol{b}_{n}^{T}\boldsymbol{\gamma}\right]-{h}_{\rm I}(n)}{\sigma/\sqrt{2}}\right)\right),

(44)

where
\Phi(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{x}e^{-\frac{t^{2}}{2}}dt
is the cumulative density function of the standard Gaussian distribution, and
\boldsymbol{b}_{n}
is the
n
th column of
\boldsymbol{B}^{T}
. For convenience, we reparameterize the negative log-likelihood function by defining
\eta=\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\sigma}
and
\boldsymbol{\beta}=\eta\boldsymbol{\gamma}
. Then, (
44
) can be reformulated as:

\displaystyle L(\boldsymbol{\beta},\eta)=

\displaystyle-\sum_{n=1}^{N}\ln\left(\Phi\left(z_{\rm R}(n)\left({\rm Re}\left[\boldsymbol{b}_{n}^{T}\boldsymbol{\beta}\right]-\eta h_{\rm R}(n)\right)\right)\right)

\displaystyle-\sum_{n=1}^{N}\ln\left(\Phi\left(z_{\rm I}(n)\left({\rm Im}\left[\boldsymbol{b}_{n}^{T}\boldsymbol{\beta}\right]-\eta{h}_{\rm I}(n)\right)\right)\right).

(45)

Consider minimizing the following regularized negative log-likelihood function for sparse parameter estimation:

G(\boldsymbol{\beta},\eta)=L(\boldsymbol{\beta},\eta)+\sum_{k=1}^{M}{\ln{(|\beta_{k}|^{2}+\varepsilon)}},

(46)

where
\varepsilon>0
is a small positive number that makes sure that the function has a finite lower bound. The first term of
G(\boldsymbol{\beta},\eta)
(i.e.,
L(\boldsymbol{\beta},\eta)
) is the fitting term and the second term (i.e.,
\sum_{k=1}^{M}{\ln{(|\beta_{k}|^{2}+\varepsilon)}}
) is a sparsity enforcing term. Similar to the original SLIM algorithm, 1bSLIM can be interpreted as a MAP approach. Consider the following Bayesian model:

\displaystyle p(\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{\beta},\eta)

\displaystyle=\prod_{n=1}^{N}\Phi\left(z_{\rm R}(n)\left({\rm Re}\left[\boldsymbol{b}_{n}^{T}\boldsymbol{\beta}\right]-\eta h_{\rm R}(n)\right)\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\quad\Phi\left(z_{\rm I}(n)\left({\rm Im}\left[\boldsymbol{b}_{n}^{T}\boldsymbol{\beta}\right]-\eta{h}_{\rm I}(n)\right)\right),

\displaystyle p(\boldsymbol{\beta})

\displaystyle\propto\prod_{k=1}^{M}\frac{1}{|\beta_{k}|^{2}+\varepsilon},\ p(\eta)\propto 1,

(47)

where
p(\boldsymbol{\beta})\propto\prod_{k=1}^{M}\frac{1}{|\beta_{k}|^{2}+\varepsilon}
is a sparsity promoting prior, and
p(\eta)\propto 1
is a noninformative prior
[
39
]
. We can estimate
\boldsymbol{\beta}
and
\eta
via the MAP approach:

\left(\boldsymbol{\beta},\eta\right)=\arg\mathop{\max}_{\boldsymbol{\beta},\eta}p(\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{\beta},\eta)p(\boldsymbol{\beta})p(\eta).

(48)

Note that after taking the negative logarithm of the above expression, (
48
) becomes equivalent to (
46
).

To efficiently minimize the complicated and non-convex objective function in (
46
), we utilize the MM approach once again. Specifically, we first derive a majorizing function for
L(\boldsymbol{\beta},\eta)
. After that, a closed-form updating formula for each MM iteration is obtained.

IV-A

Majorizing Function for
L(\boldsymbol{\beta},\eta)

Let
f(x)=-\ln\left(\Phi(x)\right)
. The following inequality holds for any
u,x\in\mathbb{R}

[
22
]
:

f(x)\leq f(u)+f^{\prime}(u)(x-u)+\frac{1}{2}(x-u)^{2}.

(49)

Let

\displaystyle x_{\rm R}(n)

\displaystyle=z_{\rm R}(n)\left({\rm Re}\left[\boldsymbol{b}_{n}^{T}\boldsymbol{\beta}\right]-\eta h_{\rm R}(n)\right),

\displaystyle x_{\rm I}(n)

\displaystyle=z_{\rm I}(n)\left({\rm Im}\left[\boldsymbol{b}_{n}^{T}\boldsymbol{\beta}\right]-\eta h_{\rm I}(n)\right),

\displaystyle f^{\prime}(x)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\phi(x)}{\Phi(x)},

where
\phi(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{2}}
is the standard normal probability density function. Then, the negative log-likelihood function
L(\boldsymbol{\beta},\eta)
in (
45
) can be written as:

L(\boldsymbol{\beta},\eta)=\sum_{n=1}^{N}f\left(x_{\rm R}(n)\right)+\sum_{n=1}^{N}f\left(x_{\rm I}(n)\right).

(50)

Assuming that
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{t}
and
\hat{\eta}^{t}
are available from the
t
th iteration, we can obtain a majorizing function for
L(\boldsymbol{\beta},\eta)
by making use of (
49
):

\displaystyle L(\boldsymbol{\beta},\eta)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{1}{2}x_{\rm R}^{2}(n)+\frac{1}{2}x_{\rm I}^{2}(n)

\displaystyle-u^{t}_{\rm R}(n)x_{\rm R}(n)-u^{t}_{\rm I}(n)x_{\rm I}(n)+{\rm const},

(51)

where
u^{t}_{\rm R/I}(n)
is given by:

u_{\rm R/I}^{t}(n)=x_{\rm R/I}^{t}(n)-f^{\prime}\left(x_{\rm R/I}^{t}(n)\right).

(52)

A simple calculation shows that (
51
) can be re-written as follows:

L(\boldsymbol{\beta},\eta)\leq\frac{1}{2}\|\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{\beta}-\left(\eta\boldsymbol{h}+\boldsymbol{g}^{t}\right)\|^{2}+{\rm const},

(53)

where
\boldsymbol{g}^{t}\!=\!\begin{bmatrix}g^{t}(1),\dots,g^{t}(N)\end{bmatrix}^{T}
, with

g^{t}(n)=z_{\rm R}(n)u_{\rm R}^{t}(n)+jz_{\rm I}(n)u_{\rm I}^{t}(n).

(54)

IV-B

Updating Formula

Making use of (
53
) and (
38
) and ignoring the terms independent of
\boldsymbol{\beta}
and
\eta
yield the following majorizing function for the objective function
G(\boldsymbol{\beta},\eta)
at the
(t+1)
th iteration:

Q(\boldsymbol{\beta},\eta|\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{t},\hat{\eta}^{t})=\frac{1}{2}\|\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{\beta}-\left(\eta\boldsymbol{h}+\boldsymbol{g}^{t}\right)\|^{2}+\boldsymbol{\beta}^{H}\left(\hat{\boldsymbol{P}}^{t}\right)^{-1}\boldsymbol{\beta},

(55)

where
\hat{\boldsymbol{P}}^{t}={\rm diag}\{\hat{\boldsymbol{p}}^{t}\}
with
\hat{\boldsymbol{p}}^{t}=\begin{bmatrix}\hat{p}^{t}_{1},\hat{p}^{t}_{2},\dots,\hat{p}_{M}^{t}\end{bmatrix}
and

\hat{p}^{t}_{k}=|\hat{\beta}_{k}^{t}|^{2}+\varepsilon.

(56)

Note that minimizing
Q\left(\boldsymbol{\beta},\eta|\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{t},\hat{\eta}^{t}\right)
with respect to
\boldsymbol{\beta}
and
\eta
is an unconstrained quadratic optimization problem, and we can set the complex derivatives
\left(d/d\boldsymbol{\beta}^{H}\right)Q(\boldsymbol{\beta},\eta|\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{t},\hat{\eta}^{t})
and
\left(d/d\eta\right)Q(\boldsymbol{\beta},\eta|\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{t},\hat{\eta}^{t})
to zeros to obtain
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(t+1)}
and
\hat{\eta}^{(t+1)}
(ignoring, for a moment, the fact that
\eta\geqslant 0
). This approach leads to the following linear equations:

\displaystyle\left(\boldsymbol{B}^{H}\boldsymbol{B}+2\left(\hat{\boldsymbol{P}}^{t}\right)^{-1}\right)\boldsymbol{\beta}-\eta\boldsymbol{B}^{H}\boldsymbol{h}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{B}^{H}\boldsymbol{g}^{t},

(57)

\displaystyle-{\rm Re}\left[\boldsymbol{h}^{H}\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{\beta}\right]+\eta\boldsymbol{h}^{H}\boldsymbol{h}

\displaystyle=-{\rm Re}\left[\boldsymbol{h}^{H}\boldsymbol{g}^{t}\right].

(58)

Since the matrix
\boldsymbol{B}^{H}\boldsymbol{B}+2\left(\hat{\boldsymbol{P}}^{t}\right)^{-1}
is positive definite, we can obtain
\boldsymbol{\beta}
from Equation (
57
) as follows:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\beta}

\displaystyle=\left[\boldsymbol{B}^{H}\boldsymbol{B}+2\left(\hat{\boldsymbol{P}}^{t}\right)^{-1}\right]^{-1}\boldsymbol{B}^{H}(\eta\boldsymbol{h}+\boldsymbol{g}^{t}),

\displaystyle=\hat{\boldsymbol{P}}^{t}\boldsymbol{B}^{H}(\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}^{t})^{-1}\left(\eta\boldsymbol{h}+\boldsymbol{g}^{t}\right),

(59)

where

\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}^{t}=\boldsymbol{B}\hat{\boldsymbol{P}}^{t}\boldsymbol{B}^{H}+2\boldsymbol{I}.

(60)

Substituting
\boldsymbol{\beta}
from (
59
) into (
58
), we obtain the solution for
\hat{\eta}^{\left(t+1\right)}
:

\hat{\eta}^{(t+1)}=\max\left(0,-\frac{{\rm Re}\left[\boldsymbol{h}^{H}(\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}^{t})^{-1}\boldsymbol{g}^{t}\right]}{\boldsymbol{h}^{H}(\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}^{t})^{-1}\boldsymbol{h}}\right).

(61)

Finally, substituting
\hat{\eta}^{(t+1)}
into (
59
) we get:

\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(t+1)}=\hat{\boldsymbol{P}}^{t}\boldsymbol{B}^{H}(\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}^{t})^{-1}\left(\hat{\eta}^{(t+1)}\boldsymbol{h}+\boldsymbol{g}^{t}\right).

(62)

By making use of the MM technique, the majorizing function (
55
), which we derived above, is much easier to minimize than the original objective function (
46
). The objective function
G(\boldsymbol{\beta},\eta)
is guaranteed to decrease monotonically due to the MM methodology used to derive the algorithm, and hence 1bSLIM converges to a local minimum of the cost function in (
46
).

V
one-bit weighted SPICE framework

In this section, we first discuss the relationship between the proposed 1bSLIM for one-bit data and the original SLIM algorithm for high-precision data. Using the MM approach, each iteration of the 1bSLIM can be interpreted as a step of the original SLIM applied to a modified high-precision data, and has an updating formula similar to that of the original SLIM. Inspired by this connection and the MM derivations of the weighted SPICE algorithms, we also extend SPICE, LIKES and IAA to the one-bit data case.

V-A

The Connection Between 1bSLIM and SLIM

Consider the following cost function:

\bar{G}(\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{p},\eta)=L(\boldsymbol{\beta},\eta)+\sum_{k=1}^{M}\left|\beta_{k}\right|^{2}/p_{k}+\sum_{k=1}^{M}\ln\left(p_{k}+\varepsilon\right).

(63)

Note that minimizing
\bar{G}(\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{p},\eta)
with respect to
\boldsymbol{p}
(for
\varepsilon=0
) gives
p_{k}=|\beta_{k}|^{2}
and hence (
63
) reduces to (
46
). This fact shows that (
63
) is an augmented form of (
46
) and hence the minimization of (
63
) can be conveniently done by means of minimizing (
46
) with respect to
\boldsymbol{\beta}
and
\eta
.

In this section, we use (
63
) to discuss the connection between 1bSLIM and SLIM. Making use of the inequality (
38
) and (
53
) and ignoring the terms independent of
\boldsymbol{\beta}
,
\boldsymbol{p}
and
\eta
yield the following majorizing function for the objective function
\bar{G}(\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{p},\eta)
:

\bar{Q}(\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{p},\eta)=\frac{1}{2}\|\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{\beta}-\left(\eta\boldsymbol{h}+\boldsymbol{g}^{t}\right)\|^{2}+\boldsymbol{\beta}^{H}\boldsymbol{P}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\beta}+\sum_{k=1}^{M}w_{k}p_{k},

(64)

where

\boldsymbol{P}={\rm diag}\{\boldsymbol{p}\}\ {\rm and}\ w_{k}=1/\left(\hat{p}_{k}^{t}+\varepsilon\right).

(65)

Let

\bar{\boldsymbol{y}}^{(t+1)}=\hat{\eta}^{(t+1)}\boldsymbol{h}+\boldsymbol{g}^{t}.

(66)

Then,
\bar{Q}\left(\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{p},\hat{\eta}^{(t+1)}\right)
becomes

\bar{Q}\left(\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{p},\hat{\eta}^{(t+1)}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\|\bar{\boldsymbol{y}}^{(t+1)}-\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{\beta}\|^{2}+\sum_{k=1}^{M}\frac{\left|\beta_{k}\right|^{2}}{p_{k}}+\sum_{k=1}^{M}w_{k}p_{k}.

(67)

The minimization of
\bar{Q}\left(\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{p},\hat{\eta}^{(t+1)}\right)
with respect to
\boldsymbol{\beta}
yields:

\min_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\bar{Q}\left(\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{p},\hat{\eta}^{(t+1)}\right)=\left(\bar{\boldsymbol{y}}^{(t+1)}\right)^{H}\boldsymbol{R}^{-1}\bar{\boldsymbol{y}}^{(t+1)}+\sum_{k=1}^{M}w_{k}p_{k},

(68)

where
\boldsymbol{R}=\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{P}\boldsymbol{B}^{H}+2\boldsymbol{I}
, and the minimum is achieved at:

\boldsymbol{\beta}=\boldsymbol{P}\boldsymbol{B}^{H}\boldsymbol{R}^{-1}\bar{\boldsymbol{y}}^{(t+1)}.

(69)

Hence,
\bar{Q}(\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{p},\hat{\eta}^{(t+1)})
is an augmented function for the weighted SPICE criterion in (
68
). By interpreting
\bar{\boldsymbol{y}}^{(t+1)}
as a modified high-precision data, the weighted SPICE criterion in (
68
) coincides with that of the original SLIM.

In sum, by using the MM technique the sparse parameter estimation problem for the nonlinear model in (
18
) can be approximately solved by making use of a linear model with a high-precision data
\bar{\boldsymbol{y}}^{(t+1)}
given by:

\bar{\boldsymbol{y}}^{(t+1)}=\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{\beta}+\bar{\boldsymbol{e}},

(70)

where the mean and covariance matrix of the noise vector
\bar{\boldsymbol{e}}
are
0
and
2\boldsymbol{I}
, respectively. Then, the amplitude
\boldsymbol{\beta}
and power
\boldsymbol{p}
of 1bSLIM can be equivalently obtained by applying SLIM to
\bar{\boldsymbol{y}}^{(t+1)}
in (
70
).

V-B

Extending SPICE, LIKES and IAA to the One-Bit Case

Section
III
showed that four different choices of the weight of the weighted SPICE algorithm correspond to four different sparsity enforcing terms and they lead to four different hyperparameter-free sparse parameter estimation algorithms.
We now use the sparsity enforcing terms of SPICE, LIKES and IAA to obtain extensions of these algorithms to the one-bit data case.

V-B
1
1bSPICE

Replacing the sparsity enforcing term in (
63
) by
{\rm tr}\{\boldsymbol{R}\}
, we get the following optimization criterion:

G_{1}(\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{p},\eta)=L(\boldsymbol{\beta},\eta)+\sum_{k=1}^{M}\left|\beta_{k}\right|^{2}/p_{k}+{\rm tr}\{\boldsymbol{R}\}.

(71)

Making use of (
53
) yields the following majorizing function for (
71
):

\bar{Q}_{1}(\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{p},\eta)=\frac{1}{2}\|\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{\beta}-\left(\eta\boldsymbol{h}+\boldsymbol{g}^{t}\right)\|^{2}+\boldsymbol{\beta}^{H}\boldsymbol{P}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\beta}+\sum_{k=1}^{M}w_{k}p_{k},

(72)

where
w_{k}=\|\boldsymbol{b}_{k}\|^{2}
. The minimization of (
72
) can be achieved by a blockwise cyclic algorithm, which alternatingly minimizes (
72
) with respect to
\{\vec{\beta},\eta\}
for fixed
\boldsymbol{p}
and with respect to
\boldsymbol{p}
for fixed
\{\boldsymbol{\beta},\eta\}
. That is,
\{\vec{\beta},\eta\}
and
\boldsymbol{p}
are updated iteratively by solving the following subproblems:

\displaystyle\{\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(t+1)},\hat{\eta}^{(t+1)}\}=\arg\mathop{\min}_{\vec{\beta},\eta}\bar{Q}_{1}\left(\vec{\beta},\hat{\boldsymbol{p}}^{t},\eta\right),

(73)

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{p}}^{(t+1)}=\arg\mathop{\min}_{\boldsymbol{p}}\bar{Q}_{1}\left(\hat{\vec{\beta}}^{(t+1)},\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{p}},\hat{\eta}^{(t+1)}\right).

(74)

Note that the optimization problem in (
73
) is similar to the one in (
55
). Hence the updating formulas for
\hat{\eta}^{(t+1)}
and
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(t+1)}
are similar to those in (
61
) and (
62
), respectively. The solution to the optimization problem in (
74
) is:

\hat{p}^{(t+1)}_{k}=\left|\hat{\beta}^{(t+1)}_{k}\right|/\sqrt{w_{k}}.

(75)

Extensive numerical examples showed that decreasing (
72
) by iterating (
73
) and (
74
) only once can typically provide a similar performance to that corresponding to multiple iterations of these two equations, but at a reduced overall computational cost. The monotonicity property of the blockwise cyclic algorithm is guaranteed since

\displaystyle\bar{Q}_{1}\left(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{t},\hat{\boldsymbol{p}}^{t},\hat{\eta}^{t}\right)

\displaystyle\geqslant\bar{Q}_{1}\left(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(t+1)},\hat{\boldsymbol{p}}^{t},\hat{\eta}^{(t+1)}\right)

\displaystyle\geqslant\bar{Q}_{1}\left(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(t+1)},\hat{\boldsymbol{p}}^{(t+1)},\hat{\eta}^{(t+1)}\right).

The first inequality comes from the minimization of
\bar{Q}_{1}(\boldsymbol{\beta},\hat{\boldsymbol{p}}^{t},\eta)
with respect to
\{\boldsymbol{\beta},\eta\}
, and the second inequality can be guaranteed via
decreasing
\bar{Q}_{1}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(t+1)},\boldsymbol{p},\hat{\eta}^{(t+1)})
with respect to
\boldsymbol{p}
.

We refer to the above algorithm as 1bSPICE due to its relationship to the SPICE algorithm. 1bSPICE decreases (
71
) at each iteration and converges to a local minimum.

V-B
2
1bLIKES

Replacing the sparsity enforcing term in (
63
) by
\ln{|\boldsymbol{R}|}
, we obtain the following criterion:

G_{2}(\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{p},\eta)=L(\boldsymbol{\beta},\eta)+\sum_{k=1}^{M}\left|\beta_{k}\right|^{2}/p_{k}+\ln{\left|\boldsymbol{R}\right|}.

(76)

By making use of (
33

VII
Conclusions

We have considered range-Doppler imaging via one-bit automotive LFMCW/PMCW radar systems, which use one-bit sampling with time-varying thresholds at the receiver to obtain signed measurements. We have formulated the range-Doppler imaging problem as one-bit sparse-parameter estimation. We have first presented a user parameter-free regularized minimization approach, namely 1bSLIM, to obtain accurate range-Doppler estimates. Inspired by the relationship between 1bSLIM and the original SLIM for high-precision data, we have also extended the conventional SPICE, LIKES and IAA to the one-bit data case, referred to as 1bSPICE, 1bLIKES and 1bIAA. These four hyperparameter-free algorithms have been unified under the one-bit weighted SPICE umbrella. Computationally efficient implementations of the algorithms have been investigated as well. Finally, in the simulated and experimental examples, we have compared the performance of the one-bit weighted SPICE algorithms to that of the one-bit periodogram and the ADMM log-norm approach and we have shown that the proposed one-bit weighted SPICE methods are not only more accurate but also much faster.

\displaystyle g(\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t}|\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t})

\displaystyle=f(\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t}),
