Where do (random) trees grow leaves?
Alessandra Caraceni, Nicolas Curien, & Robin Stephenson

Abstract
We study a model of random binary trees grown “by the leaves" in the style of Luczak and Winkler [ LW04 ] . If τ n \tau_{n} is a uniform plane binary tree of size n n , Luczak and Winkler, and later explicitly Caraceni and Stauffer, constructed a measure ν τ n \nu_{\tau_{n}} such that the tree obtained by adding a cherry on a leaf sampled according to ν τ n \nu_{\tau_{n}} is still uniformly distributed on the set of all plane binary trees with size n + 1 n+1 . It turns out that the measure ν τ n \nu_{\tau_{n}} , which we call the leaf-growth measure, is noticeably different from the uniform measure on the leaves of the tree τ n \tau_{n} . In fact we prove that as n → ∞ n\to\infty , with high probability it is almost entirely supported by a subset of only n^3 ( 2 - 3 )+o(1) ≈n^0.8038… leaves . In the continuous setting, we construct the scaling limit of this measure, which is a probability measure on the Brownian Continuum Random Tree supported by a fractal set of dimension 6 ​ ( 2 − 3 ) 6(2-\sqrt{3}) . We also compute the full (discrete) multifractal spectrum. This work is a first step towards understanding the diffusion limit of the discrete leaf-growth procedure.

Towards a diffusion on real trees.

The detailed analysis of the leaf-growth measure both in the discrete and continuous setting performed in this work can be seen as a first step in the ambitious programme of understanding the scaling limit of the leaf-growth process itself. Indeed, it is natural to postulate that the rescaled leaf-growth dynamics would converge towards an appropriate continuous Markov process with values in real trees whose invariant measure is the Brownian CRT (such a dynamic should be non-trivial by Proposition
5.1
). Several such dynamics have been the object of intense study in recent years: from the Rémy dynamic, which almost surely converges in the GHP sense
[
CH13
]
, to the Aldous move on cladograms converging to the Aldous diffusion
[
LMW20
,
FPRW18
,
FPRW20
,
FPRW23
]
. We hope to pursue those investigations in future works.

Building schemes for trees: leaf-growth measures

In this section, we set up some notation and give some context for what we are about to discuss, i.e., how to “uniformly grow” trees “from their leaves”. For completeness, we shall state some known results in their general form, which involves

LW04
]

of binary trees, which forms the basis of the current work. The procedure in the latter case, with motivation coming from mixing times of flip chains, was made completely explicit by Caraceni and Stauffer
[
CS20
]
.

In the case

Definition 2.1

Let

A

2.2
The leaf-growth measure for binary trees

We shall henceforth only deal with what we will simply call
binary trees
, that is, trees in
\mathsf{T}^{(2)}
. We will drop
d
from all notations, writing
\mathsf{T}
for
\mathsf{T}^{(2)}
and
\mathsf{T}_{n}
for
\mathsf{T}_{n}^{(2)}
. Given
t\in\mathsf{T}
such that
|t|\geqslant 1
, we shall write
t^{L}
and
t^{R}
for the trees
t^{1}
and
t^{2}
, identifying them as
the left subtree
and
the right subtree
. Moreover, we have the well-known result that binary trees are counted by Catalan numbers, i.e., that

|\mathsf{T}_{n}|=\operatorname{Cat}(n)=\frac{1}{n+1}\binom{2n}{n}.

This implies that, given a profile
(a,b)\in\mathbb{N}^{2}
and a uniform tree
\tau_{a+b+1}
from
\mathsf{T}_{a+b+1}
, we have

\Pr(\underline{x}(\tau_{a+b+1})=(a,b))=\frac{\operatorname{Cat}(a)\operatorname{Cat}(b)}{\operatorname{Cat}(a+b+1)}.

We shall need a compact notation for this expression, which from now on we will simply write as
P(a,b)
.

Now suppose we were to look for a recursive building scheme
f_{\nu}
for binary trees, with the additional natural property of being
symmetric
, that is, that
f_{\nu}(1;(a,b))=f_{\nu}(2;(b,a))
. In this case,
f_{\nu}
can be computed explicitly and takes a very simple form. Indeed, as shown within the proof of
[
CS20
, Proposition 5.1]
, we have

Theorem 2.4
.

The function

C(a,b)=\frac{(a+1)(2a+1)(a+3b+3)}{(a+b+1)(a+b+2)(2(a+b)+3)}

(4)

is the only solution to the equations

C(a,0)=1;

C(a,b)+C(b,a)=1;

P(a+1,b+1)=P(a,b+1)C(a,b+1)+P(a+1,b)(1-C(a+1,b))

for all
a,b\geqslant 0
.

Therefore, the only symmetric recursive building scheme for binary trees is the family of measures
(\nu_{t})_{t\in\mathsf{T}}
, where
\nu_{t}
is the probability measure on the leaves of
t
satisfying the recursion

\nu_{t}(\ell)=1_{\ell\in t^{L}}C(|t^{L}|,|t^{R}|)\nu_{t^{L}}(\ell)+1_{\ell\in t^{R}}(1-C(|t^{L}|,|t^{R}|))\nu_{t^{R}}(\ell).

From now on, we shall write
(\nu_{t})_{t\in\mathsf{T}}
for the unique symmetric recursive building scheme for binary trees; we will call
\nu_{t}
the
leaf-growth measure
on
t
. Notice that the equations in the statement above are precisely the requirement (
3
) in order for
\nu_{t}
to be a building scheme, with the added requirement of symmetry.

Remark that the uniqueness of the solution is clear, since one can inductively compute
C(a,b)
, for
a>b
, by using equations of the third type to reduce the value of
b
until it becomes
0
(while obviously
C(a,a)=\frac{1}{2}
). The fact that the expression (
4
) is a solution can be easily checked by induction.

Remark 2.5

(A sport question)
.

The expression (
4
) for
C(a,b)
has an interesting combinatorial interpretation. Imagine a match played between
L
(“left”) and
R
(“right”), with
L
initially having
2a+1

R
having
2b+1
. Each player has probability of winning the match proportional to their number of tokens: for the first match,
L
wins with probability
\frac{2a+1}{2a+2b+2}
and
R
wins with probability
\frac{2b+1}{2a+2b+2}
. After a match is played, the winner gains one token. Any subsequent matches have updated outcome probabilities, but are played independently.

The quantity
C(a,b)
is precisely the probability that
L
wins a majority of three successive matches, that is,

C(a,b)=3\frac{(2a+1)(2a+2)(2b+1)}{(2a+2b+2)(2a+2b+3)(2a+2b+4)}+\frac{(2a+1)(2a+2)(2a+3)}{(2a+2b+2)(2a+2b+3)(2a+2b+4)}.

We do not have a direct combinatorial proof of the link between the tree growth measure and this presentation in terms of best-of-three winning probabilities, but this interpretation can be used to give alternative descriptions of the recursive symmetric building scheme, such as the one that follows.

.

Given
n\geqslant 1
and

, place
n+1

to
n+1
on the leaves of

•

v
with tokens such that its descendants have no tokens (initially, one can pick any leaf of
t
); move all the tokens of
v
to the parent of
v
;

•

if a vertex
w
contains
n_{a}>0

a
and
n_{b}>0

, play a best-of-three game on the vertex according to the rules described in Remark
2.5
with one player having
n_{a}
starting tokens and the other having
n_{b}
. After all three matches are played, three tokens have been added and the winner has been determined. Relabel all tokens at

with
a
or

, according to which player was the winner, and destroy two tokens (thus leaving

•

unless all tokens have the same label, perform another step.

The leaf corresponding to the final surviving label is distributed according to
\nu_{t}
.

x\in[0,1]
,

\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\to\infty\\
a/n\to x\end{subarray}}C(a,b)=x^{2}(3-2x)=:\mathsf{c}(x).

(6)

The more compact notation
\mathsf{p}(x)
and
\mathsf{c}(x)
for the functions
x^{-3/2}(1-x)^{-3/2}
and
x^{2}(3-2x)
(see Figure
5
) that is being introduced here will be used frequently in future sections.

Figure 5:
A plot of the function
x\mapsto\mathsf{c}(x)
. Notice that it is symmetric with respect to
(1/2,1/2)
and below the first bisector when
x\in[0,1/2]
.

Over the course of the paper we will encounter several Riemann sums (of improper integrals) of the form

\sum_{a=1}^{n-1}f(\frac{a}{n})P(a,n-1-a)C(a,n-1-a)^{k}
for some integer
k\in\{0,1,2,...\}
. Under some mild conditions on
f
and
k
, they exhibit the expected behaviour:

Proposition 2.7
.

Let
f
be a continuous function on
(0,1),
and
k\in\{0,1,2,...\}.
Assume that
|f(x)|\leqslant K(x\wedge(1-x))^{1-2k}
for some
K>0,
and if
k>0
, assume further that
\sup\{f(\frac{a}{n}),a\in\{1,\ldots,n-1\}\}=o(\sqrt{n}).
We then have

\underset{n\to\infty}{\lim}\sqrt{n}\sum_{a=1}^{n-1}f{\left(\frac{a}{n}\right)}P(a,n-1-a)\,C(a,n-a-1)^{k}=\frac{1}{4\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{0}^{1}f(x)\mathsf{c}(x)^{k}\mathsf{p}(x)\mathrm{d}x.

(7)

Note that, while the
k=0
case is essentially a variant of
[
HM12
, Proposition 39]
, we prefer giving a self-contained proof here.

Proof.

The following uniform bounds on
C
and
P
are easily checked: there exists a constant
K^{\prime}>0
such that

n^{3/2}P(a,b)\leqslant\frac{K^{\prime}}{(\frac{a}{n})^{3/2}(\frac{b}{n})^{3/2}},

(8)

and

\left|C(a,b)-\mathsf{c}{\left(\frac{a}{n}\right)}\right|\leqslant\frac{1}{n}.

(9)

We start with the
k=0
case. Specifically, we are going to show

\sqrt{n}\sum_{a=2}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}f{\left(\frac{a}{n}\right)}P(a,n-1-a)\longrightarrow\frac{1}{4\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{0}^{1/2}f(x)\mathsf{p}(x)\mathrm{d}x.

This is enough conclude by first exploiting the natural symmetry around
1/2
, then observing that
P(\lfloor n/2\rfloor,\lfloor n/2\rfloor)=O(n^{-3/2})
so overcounting middle terms is not an issue, and finally noting that
f(1/n)P(1,n-2)=O(1/n),
so removing the first term is also not an issue.

Write
n^{1/2}\sum_{a=2}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}f(\frac{a}{n})P(a,n-1-a)
as
\int_{0}^{1/2}g_{n}(x)\mathrm{d}x
, where
g_{n}
is the function defined by
g_{n}(x)=n^{3/2}f(a/n)P(a,n-1-a)
for
x\in[a-1/n,a/n)
and
a\in\{2,\ldots,\lfloor n/2\rfloor\},
and
g_{n}(x)=0
otherwise. It is readily checked that
g_{n}(x)
converges pointwise to
\frac{1}{4\sqrt{\pi}}f(x)\mathsf{p}(x),
and we just need to check that we can apply the dominated convergence theorem. To do this, notice that, if
x\in[a-1/n,a/n)
for
2\leqslant a\leqslant\lfloor n/2\rfloor
, then
x+1/n\leqslant 2x,
while
n-1-a\geqslant n/3
. Hence, by (
8
), we have

\displaystyle g_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=n^{3/2}f(a/n)P(a,n-1-a)

\displaystyle\leqslant KK^{\prime}\frac{a}{n}\left(\frac{a}{n}\right)^{-3/2}\left(\frac{n-1-a}{n}\right)^{-3/2}

\displaystyle\leqslant 3^{-3/2}KK^{\prime}\left(x+\frac{1}{n}\right)x^{-3/2}

\displaystyle\leqslant 2\,3^{-3/2}KK^{\prime}x^{-1/2},

which ends the proof for
k=0
.

Next we treat the
k>0
case. Notice first that, since
|C(a,n-1-a)-\mathsf{c}(\frac{a}{n})|\leqslant\frac{1}{n}
and both terms are less than one, we have
|C(a,n-1-a)^{k}-\mathsf{c}(\frac{a}{n})^{k}|\leqslant\frac{k}{n}
. Hence we can write

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}\sum_{a=1}^{n-1}f{\left(\frac{a}{n}\right)}P(a,n-1-a)\,C(a,n-a-1)^{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}\sum_{a=1}^{n-1}f{\left(\frac{a}{n}\right)}P(a,n-1-a)\,\mathsf{c}{\left(\frac{a}{n}\right)}^{k}+O\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{a=1}^{n}f{\left(\frac{a}{n}\right)}P(a,n-1-a)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}\sum_{a=1}^{n-1}f{\left(\frac{a}{n}\right)}P(a,n-1-a)\,\mathsf{c}{\left(\frac{a}{n}\right)}^{k}+O\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sup\left\{f{\left(\frac{a}{n}\right)},a\in\{1,\ldots,n-1\}\right\}\sum_{a=1}^{n-1}P(n,n-1-a)\right)

By assumption, the second term tends to
0
. We are then reduced to the
k=0
case, with the function
f(x)\mathsf{c}(x)^{k}
replacing
f(x)
, and its sole assumption is verified since
\mathsf{c}(x)\leqslant 3(x\wedge(1-x))^{2}
.
∎

3
The leaf-growth measure on the Brownian CRT

In this section we construct the leaf-growth measure
\nu_{\mathcal{T}}
on the Brownian CRT and prove that it is the scaling limit for the Gromov–Hausdorff–Prokhorov metric of the discrete model. Our main tool is an explicit description of the spine towards a typical
\nu_{\mathcal{T}}
-point.

3.1
Preliminary notation

The Brownian Continuum Random Tree (CRT) is the random real tree
(\mathcal{T},d)
coded by a standard Brownian excursion. We refer to
[
LG05
]
for its standard properties, some of which are recalled below. It is given with a distinguished point
\rho
called the
root
of the tree. The degree of a point
x\in\mathcal{T}
is the number of connected components of
\mathcal{T}\backslash\{x\}
. When the degree is equal to
1
we say
x
is a
leaf
; we call
x
a
point of the skeleton
when its degree is
2
and a
branch point
when its degree is
3
(there are almost surely no points with degree
4
or more in the Brownian CRT). For
x\in\mathcal{T},
we call
height
of
x
its distance to
\rho
and denote it by
\mathrm{ht}(x)
. We say that
x
is a
descendant
of another point
y\in\mathcal{T}
(and equivalently that
y
is an
ancestor
of
x
) if
y
lies on the segment between
\rho
and
x
, and we call
\mathcal{T}_{x}
the subset of
\mathcal{T}
formed by
x
and all its descendants. Finally, for
x\in\mathcal{T}
and
t\geqslant 0
we define for the point
[x]_{t}
as:

•

the unique ancestor of
x
with height
t
, if
t<\mathrm{ht}(x)

•

x
itself, if
t\geqslant\mathrm{ht}(x).

In particular,
\mathcal{T}_{[x]_{t}}
denotes the subtree above height
t
which contains the point
x
. When
[x]_{t}
is a branch point and if
t<\mathrm{ht}(x)
, then
\mathcal{T}_{[x]_{t}}\backslash\{[x]_{t}\}
has two components and we denote by
\mathcal{T}_{x[t+]}
the closure of component that contains
x
.

The Brownian CRT is also endowed with its uniform mass measure
\mu
, which can either be seen as the projection of the Lebesgue measure on
[0,1]
in the coding by the Brownian excursion, or more intrinsically as its natural Hausdorff measure, see
[
DG05
]
.

In our work, another description of
\mathcal{T}
will be of key importance. Namely, the Brownian CRT can (with this normalisation) be seen as a fragmentation tree in the sense of Haas and Miermont
[
HM04
]
with self-similarity index
\alpha=-\frac{1}{2}
for the dislocation measure

\displaystyle\mathsf{Dis}:=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\int_{1/2}^{1}\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{(x(1-x))^{3/2}}\delta_{(x,(1-x))}.

(10)

Heuristically, this means that it encodes the genealogy of a system of particles starting from a single particle of mass
1
, where particles of mass
m
evolve independently and split into two particles of mass
mx
and
m(1-x)
at rate
m^{-1/2}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\mathsf{p}(x).
Following Bertoin
[
Ber02
]
, those particles alive at time
t
correspond to the subtrees of
\mathcal{T}
above height
t
and their masses correspond to the
\mu
-masses of those subtrees.

3.2
Definition of
\nu

Conditionally on the CRT
\mathcal{T}
, the leaf-growth measure
\nu_{\mathcal{T}}
, which throughout this section we will also denote by
\nu
, rendering the deopendence on
\mathcal{T}
implicit, will be defined by specifying the value
\nu(\mathcal{T}_{x})
for all non-leaf
x\in\mathcal{T}
. Proposition 1 of
[
Ste13
]
will then guarantee its existence and uniqueness. The definition will be analogous to the discrete case: there, the relative masses given to the subtrees in an
(a,b)
split were
C(a,b)
and
C(b,a),
and here the relative masses given to the subtrees in a
(x,1-x)
split will be
\mathsf{c}(x)
and
1-\mathsf{c}(x)=\mathsf{c}(1-x).

More precisely if
x\in\mathcal{T}
, recall from above the notation
\mathcal{T}_{[x]_{t}}
and
\mathcal{T}_{[x]_{t+}}
respectively for the subtree above height
t
which contains
x
, and in the case of a branch point, the closure of one of the two components after removing
[x]_{t}
which contains
x
. Finally we set

u_{x}(t):=\frac{\mu\big(\mathcal{T}_{[x]_{t+}}\big)}{\mu\big(\mathcal{T}_{[x]_{t}}\big)}\in[0,1].

In particular, by standard properties of the CRT, for any
x\in\mathcal{T}
we have
u_{x}(t)<1
only for a countable number of heights
t\leqslant\mathrm{ht}(x)
. Notice also that, by the very definition of
\mu
, for all
x\in\mathcal{T}
we have

\mu\big(\mathcal{T}_{x}\big)=\prod_{0\leqslant t<\mathrm{ht}(x)}u_{x}(t).

Proposition 3.1

(Definition of
\nu
)
.

Almost surely for
\mathcal{T}
, there exists a unique Borel measure
\nu
on
\mathcal{T}
specified by

\displaystyle\nu(\mathcal{T}_{x}):=\prod_{0\leqslant t<\mathrm{ht}(x)}\mathsf{c}(u_{x}(t)).

(11)

Proof.

Let, for
x\in\mathcal{T},

m(x)=\prod_{0\leqslant t<\mathrm{ht}(x)}\mathsf{c}(u_{x}(t)).
One can straightforwardly check the following:

•

m
is a well-defined and nonnegative function, which is equal to
0
exactly on the leaves of
\mathcal{T}.

•

m
is nonincreasing, in the sense that, if
x
is an ancestor of
y
, then
m(x)\geqslant m(y).

•

m
is “left-continuous", in the sense that
\displaystyle m(x)=\lim_{s\to(\mathrm{ht}(x))^{-}}m([x]_{s}).

•

m
is “additively right-continuous": if
x
is not a branchpoint, then
m
is continuous at
x
, while if
x
is a branchpoint, then, letting
y
and
z
be two descendants of
x
not in the same one of the two subtrees originating at
x
, then
m(x)=\displaystyle\lim_{s\to(\mathrm{ht}(x))^{+}}m([y]_{s})+m([z]_{s}).

These facts, combined, let us use
[
Ste13
, Proposition 1]
, which then tells us there exists a unique measure
\nu
such that
\nu(\mathcal{T}_{a})=m(a)
for
a
, and that it has no atoms.
∎

3.3
Path towards a typical
\nu
-leaf and spine decomposition

Recalling the definition of the Brownian dislocation measure in (
10
), we now build two Lévy measures
\pi_{\mu}
and
\pi_{\nu}
on
(0,\infty)
defined by

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\pi_{\mu}(s)\ \phi(s)

\displaystyle:=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\int_{1/2}^{1}\left(\mathsf{c}(x)\phi(-\log(x))+\mathsf{c}(1-x)\phi(-\log(1-x))\right)\mathsf{p}(x)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\int_{0}^{1}\mathsf{c}(x)\phi\left(-\log(x)\right)\mathsf{p}(x)\mathrm{d}x

and

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\pi_{\nu}(s)\ \phi(s)

\displaystyle:=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\int_{1/2}^{1}\left(\mathsf{c}(x)\phi(-\log(\mathsf{c}(x)))+\mathsf{c}(1-x)\phi(-\log(\mathsf{c}(1-x)))\right)\mathsf{p}(x)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\int_{0}^{1}\mathsf{c}(x)\phi\left(-\log(\mathsf{c}(x))\right)\mathsf{p}(x)\mathrm{d}x.

Consider then
\Pi_{\mu}
a Poisson point process (P.p.p.) on
\mathbb{R}_{-}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}
with intensity
\mathrm{d}\pi_{\mu}\otimes\mathrm{d}t
. By replacing each atom
(p,t)
of
\Pi_{\mu}
by the atom
(-\log(\mathsf{c}(e^{-p})),t)
we obtain another Poisson point process with intensity
\mathrm{d}\pi_{\nu}\otimes\mathrm{d}t
. We can then define two coupled subordinators
\xi_{\mu}
and
\xi_{\nu}
started from
0
and obtained by summing the atoms of the respective P.p.p., i.e.,

\xi_{\mu}(t)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(p,s)\in\Pi_{\mu}\\
s\leqslant t\end{subarray}}p\quad\mbox{and}\quad\xi_{\nu}(t)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(p,s)\in\Pi_{\nu}\\
s\leqslant t\end{subarray}}p=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(p,s)\in\Pi_{\mu}\\
s\leqslant t\end{subarray}}-\log(\mathsf{c}(\exp(-p))).

Then we perform the Lamperti transformation with index
1/2
using
\xi_{\mu}:
we let

\theta(t)=\inf\left\{u\geqslant 0:\int_{0}^{u}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\xi_{\mu}(r)}\mathrm{d}r>t\right\},

and build two processes
X_{\mu}
(a so-called positive self-similar Markov process, see
[
Lam62
]
) and
X_{\nu}
(which is not Markovian) defined for
t\geqslant 0
by

(X_{\mu}(t),X_{\nu}(t)):=(e^{-\xi_{\mu}(\theta(t))},e^{-\xi_{\nu}(\theta(t))}).

Both of those processes are pure jump and non-increasing from
1
to
0
, and are absorbed at
0
at time

\displaystyle I:=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\xi_{\mu}(t)}\mathrm{d}t

(12)

which is almost surely finite since
\xi_{\mu}
drifts to
-\infty
at linear speed.

Theorem 3.2

(Spine decomposition)
.

Conditionally on
\mathcal{T}
, let
L
be a
\nu
-distributed random point of
\mathcal{T}.
Then the bivariate process

(\mu(\mathcal{T}_{[L]_{t+}}),\nu(\mathcal{T}_{[L]_{t+}}))_{t\geqslant 0}

is distributed as
(X_{\mu},X_{\nu}).

As a consequence,
L
is almost surely a leaf, and its height has the law of
I
.

Moreover, let
(t_{i},i\in\mathbb{N})
be the times at which
u_{L}(\cdot)\in(0,1)
, and for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
let
\mathcal{T}_{i}
be the subtrees which branch off
[\hskip-1.00006pt[\rho,L]\hskip-1.00006pt]
at those times (ranked, say in decreasing order of their
\mu
-masses). Then, conditionally on the whole process
(\mu(\mathcal{T}_{[L]_{t}}),t\geqslant 0),
the trees

\big(u_{L}(t_{i})\cdot\mu(\mathcal{T}_{L(t_{i})})\big)^{-1/2}\cdot\mathcal{T}_{i},\quad i\geqslant 1

are independent Brownian CRT’s.

Proof.

The main idea is that the path to
L
encounters binary splits at the same rate as the path to a uniform leaf, but crosses those splits differently, using the probability
\mathsf{c}.
This is formalised in
[
WP13
]
by the notion of
bifurcator
: if
L^{\prime}
is a leaf with distribution
\mu,
then at an
(x,1-x)
split, if the path to
L^{\prime}
follows
x
then the path to
L
will follow
(1-x)
with probability
\mathsf{c}(1-x)
(known as the “switching probability" in
[
WP13
]
). While the process
(Y_{t},t\geqslant 0)
defined by
Y(t)=\mu(\mathcal{T}_{[L^{\prime}]_{t+}})
is well-known
[
Ber02
, Section 4]
to be the Lamperti transform of a subordinator with Lévy measure
\pi_{\mathrm{unif}},
defined by

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\pi_{\mathrm{unif}}(s)\ \phi(s)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\int_{1/2}^{1}\left(x\phi(-\log(x))+(1-x)\phi(-\log(1-x))\right)\mathsf{p}(x)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\int_{0}^{1}x\phi(-\log(x))\mathsf{p}(x)\mathrm{d}x,

Proposition 1 from
[
WP13
]
states then that the process
\mu(\mathcal{T}_{[L]_{t+}}),t\geqslant 0
will be the Lamperti transform of a subordinator where
x
in the integrand of the above is replaced by
\mathsf{c}(x)
, giving us
\pi_{\mu}.

That
(\nu(\mathcal{T}_{[L]_{t+}}),t\geqslant 0)
has the appropriate paired distribution comes from the fact that its multiplicative jumps are obtained by applying
\mathsf{c}
to those of
(\mu(\mathcal{T}_{[L]_{t+}}),t\geqslant 0),
which is how
X_{\nu}
is derived from
X_{\mu}.

Finally, the spinal decomposition itself is again a consequence of
[
WP13
]
, this time Lemma 21.
∎

3.4
Applications: height and dimension

Let us see two direct applications of Theorem
3.2
. The first one computes the law of the height of a random
\nu
-leaf (under the annealed law), the second one computes the almost sure Hausdorff dimension of
\nu
.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
\mathcal{T}
be a Brownian CRT, and conditionally on
\mathcal{T}
, let
L\in\mathcal{T}
be sampled according to
\nu
. Then

\mathrm{ht}(L)\overset{(d)}{=}8x^{3}e^{-2x^{2}}\mathrm{d}x\mathbf{1}_{x>0}.

Remark 3.4
.

An integration by parts shows that this dominates the Rayleigh distribution (
4xe^{-2x^{2}}\mathrm{d}x,
which is the distribution of the height of a uniform leaf) stochastically. This is expected, as the path to
L
favours large subtrees more than the path to a uniform leaf.

Proof.

We know that
\mathrm{ht}(L)
has the same distribution as
I=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\xi_{\mu}(t)}\mathrm{d}t.

The results of
[
CPY97
]
show that the integer moments of this distribution can be linked to the Laplace exponent of
\xi_{\mu}
, which we call
\Phi
, by the following formula:

\mathbb{E}[(\mathrm{ht}(L))^{k}]=\frac{k!}{\prod_{i=1}^{k}\Phi(\frac{i}{2})}.

However, in our setting, we have for
\alpha\geqslant 0

\displaystyle\Phi(\alpha)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\int_{1/2}^{1}\left(\mathsf{c}(x)(1-x^{\alpha})+\mathsf{c}(1-x)(1-(1-x))^{\alpha}\right)\mathsf{p}(x)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\int_{1/2}^{1}\left(1-\mathsf{c}(x)x^{\alpha}-\mathsf{c}(1-x)(1-x)^{\alpha}\right)\mathsf{p}(x)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{0}^{1}\left(1-\mathsf{c}(x)x^{\alpha}-\mathsf{c}(1-x)(1-x)^{\alpha}\right)\mathsf{p}(x)\mathrm{d}x.

(13)

The latter integral can be computed (see Appendix
Appendix A: computing integral (
)
), and we obtain
\Phi(\alpha)=2\sqrt{2}\alpha\frac{\Gamma(3/2+\alpha)}{\Gamma(2+\alpha)}.
Hence

\mathbb{E}[(\mathrm{ht}(L))^{k}]=\frac{\Gamma(\frac{4+k}{2})}{2^{k/2}}.

A straightforward induction shows that
\frac{\Gamma(\frac{4+k}{2})}{2^{k/2}}=\int_{0}^{\infty}8x^{3+k}e^{-2x^{2}}\mathrm{d}x.
Given that the probability distribution with density
8x^{3}e^{-2x^{2}}
is determined by its moments (since its moment generating function has positive radius of convergence), this ends the proof.
∎

Hausdorff dimension.

The fact that the Hausdorff dimension of the measure
\nu
is
2\gamma
follows from the coming proposition together with standard results about Hausdorff dimension (see e.g.
[
LPP95
, Lemma 4.1]
):

Proposition 3.5
.

Almost surely for
\mathcal{T}
and a.e. for
\nu(\mathrm{d}\ell)
we have

\lim_{r\to 0}\frac{\log\nu\big(B_{r}(\ell)\big)}{\log r}=2\gamma.

Proof.

Let us compute the first moments of the Lévy processes
\xi_{\mu}
and
\xi_{\nu}:
we have

\mathbb{E}[\xi_{\mu}(t)]=t\int_{0}^{\infty}s\ \mathrm{d}\pi_{\mu}(s)=2(\sqrt{2\pi})\quad\mbox{and}\quad\mathbb{E}[\xi_{\nu}(t)]=t\int_{0}^{\infty}s\ \mathrm{d}\pi_{\nu}(s)=(2\gamma)\sqrt{2\pi}.

Since the Lévy measure integrates the identity function, by the law of large numbers we have
t^{-1}\xi_{\mu}(t)\to 2(\sqrt{2\pi})
and
t^{-1}\xi_{\nu}(t)\to(2\gamma)\sqrt{2\pi}
as
t\to\infty
almost surely. Through the Lamperti transformation, if
I
is the (random) extinction time of the processes
X_{\mu}
and
X_{\nu}
this entails that

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{\log X_{\mu}(I-\varepsilon)}{\log\varepsilon}=2\quad\mbox{and}\quad\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{\log X_{\nu}(I-\varepsilon)}{\log\varepsilon}=2\gamma,

also almost surely. Now, by the first item of the spine decomposition (Theorem
3.2
), we clearly have
\nu\big(B_{\varepsilon}(L)\big)\leqslant X_{\nu}(I-\varepsilon)
as a process in
\varepsilon
and together with the last display this entails that

\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{\log\nu\big(B_{\varepsilon}(L)\big)}{\log\varepsilon}\geqslant 2\gamma,\quad\mbox{a.s.}.

Let us now prove the lower bound. For this, we concentrate on jumps of
\xi_{\mu}
of size at least
\log 2
. Remember that thanks to the spine decomposition, conditionally on such a jump
s
appearing at time
t
, the subtree left on the side is an independent Brownian CRT with
\mu
-mass

(1-\mathrm{e}^{-s})\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{1}{2}\xi_{\mu}(t-)}\geqslant\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{1}{2}\xi_{\mu}(t-)}.

Notice two things: first the
\nu
-mass of this subtree is at least
\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{1}{2}\xi_{\nu}(t-)}/2
(because
\mathsf{c}(x)\leqslant x
for
x\in[0,1/2]
) and it has a probability equal to
\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{ht}(\mathcal{T})\leqslant 1)
of being of height less than the square root of its
\mu
-mass. If those assumptions are satisfied, we can ensure that

\nu\big(B_{\varepsilon}(L)\big)\geqslant\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{1}{2}\xi_{\nu}(t-)}\quad\mbox{as soon as }\quad\varepsilon\geqslant\sqrt{\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{1}{2}\xi_{\mu}(t-)}}+\int_{t}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{1}{2}\xi_{\mu}(s)}\mathrm{d}s.

Since the intensity of such events is positive, by easy estimates, one can almost surely find a sequence of jump
(s_{i},t_{i})
satisfying the above assumptions and where we have

0<t_{1}<t_{2}<\cdots<t_{i}\xrightarrow[i\to\infty]{}\infty\quad\mbox{and}\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{t_{i+1}}{t_{i}}=1.

Combining this with the above controls yields

\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{\log\nu\big(B_{\varepsilon}(L)\big)}{\log\varepsilon}\leqslant 2\gamma+\eta,\quad\mbox{a.s.},

for any
\eta>0
, and this completes the proof. ∎

3.5
Scaling limits

Let us now prove Theorem
1.3
. We refer to
[
ADH13
,
Khe20
]
for background on the (pointed) Gromov–Hausdorff–Prokhorov topology. It is very well-known that we have the convergence in distribution

(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\tau_{n},\rho_{n},\mu_{n})\underset{n\to\infty}{\overset{(d)}{\longrightarrow}}(2\sqrt{2}\mathcal{T},\rho,\mu),

(14)

for the pointed Gromov–Hausdorff–Prokhorov metric, where
\mu_{n}
is the uniform measure on the vertices of
\tau_{n}
. By analogy with this notation, we shall also use the notation
\nu_{n}
for the measure
\nu_{\tau_{n}}
introduced in Section
2
. We saw in Section
2
and in Proposition
3.1
that the measures
\nu_{n}
and
\nu
are obtained as measurable functions of
(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\tau_{n},\rho_{n},\mu_{n})
and
(2\sqrt{2}\mathcal{T},\rho,\mu)
respectively and it turns out that those functions are almost “continuous", letting us adjoin
\nu_{n}
and
\nu
to (
14
) using deterministic arguments.

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.
Let us use the Skorokhod representation theorem and suppose that on some probability space we have built versions of our random variables so that (
14
) holds almost surely.
Let
z
be a point on the skeleton of
\mathcal{T}
(which makes
\mathcal{T}_{z}
a continuity set for both measures
\mu
and
\nu
). By (
14
), there exists a sequence
(\mathcal{R}^{(n)})
of correspondences with vanishing distortions and a sequence
(z^{(n)})
of points in
\tau_{n}
with
z^{(n)}\mathcal{R}^{(n)}z
for all
n
such that

\mu(\mathcal{T}_{z})=\lim_{n\to\infty}\mu_{n}((\tau_{n})_{z^{(n)}}).

Our goal is to prove that

\nu(\mathcal{T}_{z})=\lim_{n\to\infty}\nu_{n}((\tau_{n})_{z^{(n)}}),

which guarantees via the Portmanteau theorem that any subsequential limit of
(\nu_{n})
(of which there are some since
\mathcal{T}
is compact) is equal to
\nu.

Let us denote by
h_{n}
the height of
x^{(n)}
and by
(a^{(n)}_{i},b^{(n)}_{i})
the sizes of the two subtrees above the ancestor of
z^{(n)}
at height
0\leqslant i<h_{n}
, with the convention that
a^{(n)}_{i}
is the size of the subtree containing
z^{(n)}
. Then we have, as mentioned in the introduction,

\displaystyle\nu_{n}((\tau_{n})_{z^{(n)}})=\prod_{i=0}^{h_{n}-1}C(a_{i}^{(n)},b_{i}^{(n)})

(15)

and

\mu_{n}((\tau_{n})_{z^{(n)}})=\prod_{i=0}^{h_{n}-1}\frac{a_{i}^{(n)}}{a_{i}^{(n)}+b_{i}^{(n)}+1}.

Similarly, let us write
h
for the height of
x
and denote by
(a_{t},b_{t})
the
\mu
-mass of the subtrees above
[x]_{t}\in\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
with the same convention that
a_{t}
is the mass of the subtree containing
x
(with
b_{t}=0
when
[x]_{t}
is not a branch point). Then by Proposition
3.1
we have

\displaystyle\nu(x)=\prod_{t<h}\mathsf{c}(\frac{a_{t}}{a_{t}+b_{t}}),

(16)

as well as, by definition,

\displaystyle\mu(x)=\prod_{t<h}\frac{a_{t}}{a_{t}+b_{t}}.

(17)

The heuristic reason why
\nu_{n}((\tau_{n})_{z^{(n)}})
converges to
\nu(\tau_{z})
is that the only source of irregularity in the infinite products would be from the accumulation of terms which are close to
1
, however since
C(\cdot,\cdot)
and
\mathsf{c}
are very regular for large fragments (specifically,
1-\mathsf{c}(1-h)
is of order
h^{2}
when
h
is close to
0
, though we will only use
\mathsf{c}(x)\geqslant x
for
x\geqslant 1/2
, and the equivalent for
C
), the fact that the
\mu
products behave well implies that the
\nu
products also do. The rest of this proof formalises this.

Let
(x_{i}^{(n)},i\in\mathbb{N})
be the sequence
(\frac{a_{i}^{(n)}}{a_{i}^{(n)}+b_{i}^{(n)}+1},i\leqslant h_{n}-1),
sorted in increasing order, and completed by an infinite repeat of
1
, and let
(y_{i}^{(n)},i\in\mathbb{N})
be the sequence
(C(a_{i}^{(n)},b_{i}^{(n)},n\in\mathbb{N}))
sorted the same way, also with an infinite repeat of
1
. Let
(x_{i},i\in\mathbb{N})
the increasingly sorted sequence of the non-
1
members of
(\frac{a_{t}}{a_{t}+b_{t}},t\geqslant 0),
and then
y_{t}=\mathsf{c}(x_{t}).
By (
14
), we have that
x_{i}^{(n)}
converges to
x_{i}
for all
i
and that
\prod x_{i}^{(n)}
converges to
\prod x_{i}.

Let
\varepsilon>0,

K\in\mathbb{N}
be such that
\prod_{i=K+1}^{\infty}x_{i}\geqslant 1-\varepsilon,
and take
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that, for
n\geqslant N
,

1-\varepsilon\leqslant\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}x_{i}^{(n)}}{\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}x_{i}}\leqslant 1+\varepsilon,

as well as
(1-\varepsilon)x_{i}\leqslant|x_{i}^{(n)}|\leqslant(1+\varepsilon)x_{i}
.
For
n\geqslant N,
we then also have

\displaystyle\prod_{i={K+1}}^{\infty}x_{i}^{(n)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}x_{i}^{(n)}}{\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}x_{i}}\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{K}x_{i}}{\prod_{i={1}}^{K}x_{i}^{(n)}}\prod_{i=K+1}^{\infty}x_{i}

\displaystyle\geqslant(1-\varepsilon)^{2}(1+\varepsilon)^{-K}

\displaystyle\geqslant(1-\varepsilon)

up to changing
\varepsilon.
From this we deduce
x_{i}^{(n)}\geqslant 1-\varepsilon\geqslant 1/2
for all
n\geqslant N
and
i\geqslant K+1,
and therefore, since
C(a,b)\geqslant\frac{a}{a+b+1}
for
a\geqslant b,
we also have
y_{i}^{(n)}\geqslant x_{i}^{(n)}.
Now write

\displaystyle\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}y_{i}^{(n)}}{\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}y_{i}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{K}y_{i}^{(n)}}{\prod_{i=1}^{K}y_{i}}\frac{\prod_{i=K+1}^{\infty}y_{i}^{(n)}}{\prod_{i=K+1}^{\infty}y_{i}}

The first fraction in the RHS can be made close to
1
as each
y_{i}^{(n)}
converges to
y_{i}.
For the second, we have
\frac{\prod_{i=K+1}^{\infty}y_{i}^{(n)}}{\prod_{i=K+1}^{\infty}y_{i}}\geqslant\prod_{i=K+1}^{\infty}x_{i}^{(n)}\geqslant 1-\varepsilon,
and, up to increasing
K
again,
\prod_{i=K+1}^{\infty}y_{i}\geqslant 1-\varepsilon,
hence

1-\varepsilon\leqslant\frac{\prod_{i=K+1}^{\infty}y_{i}^{(n)}}{\prod_{i=K+1}^{\infty}y_{i}}\leqslant\frac{1}{1-\varepsilon}.

From this we conclude that
\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}y_{i}^{(n)}
, which is
\nu_{n}((\tau_{n})_{z_{n}}),
converges to
\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}y_{i},
which is
\nu(\mathcal{T}_{z}).
∎

4
Discrete fractal properties of leaf-growth measures

In this section, we study the discrete fractal properties of
\nu_{\tau_{n}}
and focus in particular on Theorem
1.2
and Theorem
1.1
. Those results will be proved by investigating the asymptotic behaviour of the random variable
M_{n}=\nu_{\tau_{n}}(L_{n})
, where
\tau_{n}
is uniform in
\mathsf{T}_{n}
and
L_{n}
is, conditionally on
\tau_{n}
, a random leaf of
\tau_{n}
sampled according to the leaf-growth measure
\nu_{\tau_{n}}
. This notation will be used throughout the section.

4.1
Typical exponent of the leaf-growth mass of a random leaf

Theorem
1.1
is a straightforward consequence of the following:

Proposition 4.1
.

Setting
\gamma=3(2-\sqrt{3})
, we have the following convergence in probability:

\frac{-\log M_{n}}{\log n}\underset{n\to\infty}{\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\longrightarrow}}\gamma.

Proof.

For
k\in\mathbb{N},
let
L_{n}
be a
\nu_{\tau_{n}}
-distributed random leaf of
\tau_{n}.
Define two processes
X^{(n)}_{\mu}(\cdot)
and
X^{(n)}_{\nu}(\cdot)
as follows:

•

For
k\leqslant\mathrm{ht}(L_{n})
, let
x
be the ancestor of
V_{n}
in
\tau_{n}
with height
k
. Define
X^{(n)}_{\mu}(k)
to be the number of inner vertices in the subtree of
\tau_{n}
rooted at
x
, and
X^{(n)}_{\nu}(k)
to be the
\nu_{\tau_{n}}
-mass of that subtree.

•

For
k>\mathrm{ht}(L_{n}),
let
X^{(n)}_{\mu}(k)=X^{(n)}_{\nu}(k)=0
.

It is possible to show that the scaling limit of
X^{(n)}_{\mu}
is
X_{\mu},
however here we are interested in the large-time behaviour of
X^{(n)}_{\mu}
and
X^{(n)}_{\nu}.
Specifically, note then that
\mathrm{ht}(L_{n})
is the time at which the process
X^{(n)}_{\mu}
hits the value
\frac{1}{n}
, and that
M_{n}=X^{(n)}_{\nu}(\mathrm{ht}(L_{n})).

Setup.

The proof of the proposition will be mainly based on showing weak LLN-type behaviour for both processes
X^{(n)}_{\mu}
and
X^{(n)}_{\nu}
at large times. Taking inspiration from
[
BK16
]
, we first switch to continuous time by using a “Poissonization" technique: let
\mathcal{P}_{n}(t)
be a Poisson process with local rate given by
\sqrt{nX^{(n)}_{\mu}(\mathcal{P}_{n}(t))},
and set
\mathcal{X}^{(n)}_{\mu}(t)=-\log X^{(n)}_{\mu}(\mathcal{P}_{n}(t))
. The latter is a Markov process: when at position
\log n-\log m
for
m\geqslant 2
, it waits an amount of time which is exponentially distributed with parameter
m^{1/2}
and then jumps to
\log n-\log a,
for
a\in\{1,\ldots,m-1\},
with probability
2P(a,b)C(a,b).
Notice that
\mathcal{X}^{(n)}_{\mu}
grows from
0
to
\log(n),
since
X^{(n)}_{\mu}
decreased from 1 to
1/n
. Similarly, set
\mathcal{X}^{(n)}_{\nu}(t)=-\log X^{(n)}_{\nu}(\mathcal{P}_{n}(t))
.

Letting
\theta_{n}=\inf\{t:\mathcal{X}^{(n)}_{\mu}(t)=\log n\}
, our aim is to show that

\frac{\mathcal{X}^{(n)}_{\nu}(\theta_{n})}{\log n}\underset{n\to\infty}{\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\longrightarrow}}\gamma

(18)

To do this, we will first consider
\theta^{\prime}_{n}=\inf\{t:\mathcal{X}^{(n)}_{\mu}(t)=\log n-K\}
for a large
K
that will be chosen later. Notice that, for any value of
K
, the distribution of
\theta_{n}-\theta^{\prime}_{n}
is tight as
n
tends to infinity, because it is a mixture of the distributions of
(\theta_{i})
for
i
in the finite set
\{1,\ldots,\lfloor e^{K}\rfloor\}.
Similarly, the distribution of
\mathcal{X}^{(n)}_{\nu}(\theta_{n})-\mathcal{X}^{(n)}_{\nu}(\theta^{\prime}_{n})
is also tight, as it is a mixture of the distributions of
\mathcal{X}^{(i)}(\theta_{i})
for
i\in\{1,\ldots,\lfloor e^{K}\rfloor\}
. This implies that we only need to prove Equation (
18
) with
\theta^{\prime}_{n}
replacing
\theta_{n}
:

\frac{\mathcal{X}^{(n)}_{\nu}(\theta^{\prime}_{n})}{\log n}\underset{n\to\infty}{\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\longrightarrow}}\gamma.

(19)

We set out to prove (
19
).

Analysis of
\mathcal{X}^{(n)}_{\mu}
and estimate of
\theta_{n}
.

Since
\mathcal{X}^{(n)}_{\mu}
is a pure jump process, we can compute its predictable compensator. It is given by integrating the local drift
D_{\mu}(t)=\frac{1}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{X}^{(n)}_{\mu}(t+\mathrm{d}t)-\mathcal{X}^{(n)}_{\mu}(t)\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}]
which is equal to the rate of jumps, times the expectation of the next jump, that is

D_{\mu}(t)=m^{1/2}\sum_{a=1}^{m-1}-\log(a/m)2P(a,m-1-a)C(a,m-1-a),

with
m
such that
\mathcal{X}^{(n)}_{\mu}(t)=\log n-\log m.

We can apply Proposition
2.7
, part
(ii)
, to this sum (since
\log(1/m)=o(\sqrt{m})
and
x\log(x)
tends to
0
as
x
tends to
0
), obtaining that it converges as
m
tends to infinity to

\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{0}^{1}-\log x\,\mathsf{c}(x)\mathsf{p}(x)\mathrm{d}x=\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2}.

The fact that this is a limit as
m
tends to infinity is useful because the drift will be close to that constant at any
t
, as long as
m
is big (i.e., as long as
\mathcal{X}_{n}(t)<\log(n)-K
for large
K
). By definition of the predictable compensator, the process
(M^{(n)}_{\mu}(t),t\geqslant 0)
defined by

M^{(n)}_{\mu}(t)=\mathcal{X}^{(n)}_{\mu}(t)-\int_{0}^{t}D_{\mu}(t)\mathrm{d}t,

is a
(\mathcal{F}_{t}:t\geqslant 0)
-martingale. Its quadratic variation is given by integrating the rate of jumps, times the expectation of the square of the next jump, which is

V_{\mu}(t)=m^{1/2}\sum_{a=1}^{m-1}(\log(a/m))^{2}2P(a,m-1-a)C(a,m-1-a)<\infty

Again by Proposition
2.7
, this converges as
m\to\infty
to

\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{0}^{1}(\log x)^{2}\mathsf{c}(x)\mathsf{p}(x)\mathrm{d}x.

In particular, this means that that
V^{(n)}_{\mu}(t)\leqslant B
uniformly for some
B>0.

Since
M^{(n)}_{\mu}(0)=0
, we have

\mathbb{E}[M^{(n)}_{\mu}(t)^{2}]=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{t}V_{\mu}(t)\mathrm{d}t\right]\leqslant Bt,

implying via Doob’s inequality that

\mathbb{P}[\underset{u\leqslant t}{\sup}\,M^{(n)}_{\mu}(u)>t^{0.6}]\leqslant\frac{Bt}{t^{1.2}}\underset{t\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}0,

uniformly in
n
.

Let now
\varepsilon
be a positive real and choose
K
large enough to have
|D_{\mu}(t)-\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2}|\leqslant\frac{\varepsilon}{2}
whenever
\mathcal{X}^{(n)}_{\mu}(t)\leqslant\log n-K.
Consider
t_{n}=\frac{2(1-\varepsilon)\log n}{\sqrt{\pi}}
and let
E_{n}
be the event that
\underset{t\leqslant t_{n}}{\sup}\,M^{(n)}_{\mu}(t)\leqslant t_{n}^{0.6}
. We claim that, for
n
large enough, on
E_{n}
, we have
\theta^{\prime}_{n}\geqslant t_{n}.
To see this, notice that, on
E_{n}
, we have for all
t\leqslant t_{n}

\mathcal{X}^{(n)}_{\mu}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}D^{(n)}_{\mu}(s)\mathrm{d}s+O(\log(n)^{0.6})

However, if
\theta^{\prime}_{n}\leqslant t_{n}
, then for
n
large enough we would have

\displaystyle\log(n)-K

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathcal{X}^{(n)}_{\mu}(\theta^{\prime}_{n})

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2}(1+\frac{\varepsilon}{2})\theta^{\prime}_{n}+O(\log(n)^{0.6})

\displaystyle\leqslant(1+\frac{\varepsilon}{2})(1-\varepsilon)\log n+O(\log(n)^{0.6}),

which eventually gives a contradiction. Put together, this means that
\mathbb{P}[\theta^{\prime}_{n}\geqslant\frac{(1-\varepsilon)\log n}{A}]\geqslant\mathbb{P}[E_{n}]\to 1.

Let now
u_{n}=\frac{2(1+\varepsilon)\log n}{\sqrt{\pi}},
and this time let
F_{n}
be the event that
\underset{t\leqslant t_{n}}{\sup}M^{(n)}_{\mu}(t)\leqslant u_{n}^{0.6},
whose probability tends to
1
. Let us now check that, on
F_{n}
,
\theta^{\prime}_{n}\leqslant u_{n}.
Similarly to before, observe that, on
F_{n}
, we have for all
t\leqslant u_{n}
that

\mathcal{X}^{(n)}_{\mu}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}D^{(n)}_{\mu}(s)\mathrm{d}s+O(\log(n)^{0.6}).

Thus, if we had
\mathcal{X}^{(n)}_{\mu}(u_{n})<\log(n)-K,

then we would have
V^{(n)}_{\mu}(s)\geqslant A(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{2})
for all
s\leqslant u_{n},
and in particular

\log n\geqslant\mathcal{X}^{(n)}_{\mu}(u_{n})\geqslant\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2}(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{2})\frac{2(1+\varepsilon)\log n}{\sqrt{\pi}}+O((\log n)^{0.6}),

a contradiction for
n
large enough. Hence
\mathbb{P}[\theta^{\prime}_{n}\leqslant\frac{2(1+\varepsilon)\log n}{\sqrt{\pi}}]\geqslant\mathbb{P}[F_{n}]\rightarrow 1.

Analysis of
\mathcal{X}^{(n)}_{\nu}
and conclusion.

The same arguments hold for
\mathcal{X}^{(n)}_{\nu}
. Keeping the notation
\mathcal{X}^{(n)}_{\mu}(t)=\log n-\log m
, we have that
\mathcal{X}^{(n)}_{\nu}
jumps at rate
m^{1/2},
and if
\mathcal{X}^{(n)}_{\mu}
jumps to
a
, then the value of
\mathcal{X}^{(n)}_{\nu}
increases by
C(a,b)
. Hence

D_{\nu}(t)=m^{1/2}\sum_{a=1}^{m-1}C(a,b)2P(a,b)C(a,b),

the limit of which is

\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{0}^{1}\mathsf{c}(x)^{2}\mathsf{p}(x)\mathrm{d}x=\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2}3(2-\sqrt{3}).

Similarly, the local variance is then

V_{\nu}(t)=m^{1/2}\sum_{a=1}^{m-1}C(a,b)^{2}2P(a,b)C(a,b),

the limit of which is

\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{0}^{1}\mathsf{c}(x)^{3}\mathsf{p}(x)\mathrm{d}x,

hence we have
V_{\nu}(t)\leqslant B
for all
t
, up to changing
B.

We consider the martingale
(M^{(n)}_{\nu}(t),t\geqslant 0)
defined by

M^{(n)}_{\nu}(t)=\mathcal{X}^{(n)}_{\mu}(t)-\int_{0}^{t}D_{\nu}(t)\mathrm{d}t.

The same arguments as for
M^{(n)}_{\nu}
show that, if
G_{n}
is the event that
\underset{t\leqslant u_{n}}{\sup}M^{(n)}_{\nu}(t)\leqslant u_{n}^{0.6},
then
\mathbb{P}[G_{n}]
tends to
1
. Up to increasing
K
, we can moreover assume that
|D_{\nu}(t)-\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2}3(2-\sqrt{3})|\leqslant\varepsilon
for
t\leqslant\theta^{\prime}_{n}.
Then on the event
G_{n}\cap\{|\theta^{\prime}_{n}-\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\log(n)|\leqslant\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\varepsilon\log(n)\},
which has probability tending to
1
, we have

|\mathcal{X}^{(n)}_{\nu}(\theta^{\prime}_{n})-\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2}3(2-\sqrt{3})\theta^{\prime}_{n}|\leqslant\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\theta^{\prime}_{n}+O((\log n)^{0.6}),

from which (
19
) readily follows.
∎

4.2
Moments of the leaf-growth mass of a random leaf: the multifractal spectrum

In contrast to the result of the previous section, we now highlight the multifractality phenomenon governing the behaviour of the random variable
M_{n}
and proceed to compute the full discrete multifractal spectrum of the measure
\nu_{\tau_{n}}
.

Our aim within this section will be to estimate all moments
\mathbb{E}[M_{n}^{\alpha}]
for
\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}
; by the end, we shall prove Theorem
1.2
. Before we do, let us give a brief insight into the appearance of the integral
I(\alpha,\beta)
from (
2
); we shall then show that, given
\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}
, there is a unique
\beta(\alpha)
such that
I(\alpha,\beta(\alpha))=0
. Finally, we dive into a formal proof of Theorem
1.2
.

In order to compute
\mathbb{E}[M_{n}^{\alpha}]
, we can disintegrate according to the profile of
\tau_{n}
; indeed, letting
\tau_{n}^{L}
and
\tau_{n}^{R}
be the left and right subtrees, conditionally on
|\tau_{n}^{L}|=a
, the leaf
L_{n}
is distributed according to
\nu_{\tau_{n}^{L}}
among the
a+1
leaves of
\tau_{n}^{L}
with probability
C(a,b)
(where
b=n-1-a
), and distributed according to
\nu_{\tau_{n}^{R}}
among the
b
leaves of
\tau_{n}^{R}
with probability
1-C(a,b)
. In the first case, the mass
M_{n}
has the distribution of
C(a,b)M_{a}
, and in the second case it is distributed as
C(b,a)M_{b}
. This yields

\mathbb{E}[M_{n}^{\alpha}]=\sum_{a=1}^{n-1}P(a,b)\left(C(a,b)^{\alpha+1}\mathbb{E}[M_{a}^{\alpha}]+C(b,a)^{\alpha+1}\mathbb{E}[M_{b}^{\alpha}]\right).

Since we have
\sum_{a=1}^{n-1}P(a,b)=1
, we can rewrite this as

\sum_{a=1}^{n-1}P(a,b)\left(C(a,b)^{\alpha+1}\mathbb{E}[M_{a}^{\alpha}]+C(b,a)^{\alpha+1}\mathbb{E}[M_{b}^{\alpha}]-\mathbb{E}[M_{n}^{\alpha}]\right)=0.

Now, if we informally assume an asymptotic of the form
\mathbb{E}[M_{n}^{\alpha}]\approx n^{-\beta}
, where
\beta
is some positive real number, and recall that
C(a,b)
is close to
\mathsf{c}(\frac{a}{n}),
we obtain that

\sum_{a=1}^{n-1}P(a,b)\left(\mathsf{c}(a/n)^{\alpha+1}(a/n)^{-\beta}+\left(1-\mathsf{c}(a/n)\right)^{\alpha+1}\left(b/n\right)^{-\beta}-1\right)

should be close to
0
. However, similarly to Proposition
2.7
, we expect to this sum converge to
\frac{1}{4\sqrt{\pi}}I(\alpha,\beta),
hence if
\mathbb{E}[M_{n}^{\alpha}]\approx n^{-\beta}
then we should have
I(\alpha,\beta)=0
.

Let us now consider the problem of determining
\beta(\alpha)
as a function of
\alpha
:

Lemma 4.2
.

For
\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}
, the quantity

I(\alpha,\beta)=\int_{0}^{1}\left(\mathsf{c}(x)^{\alpha+1}x^{-\beta}+\mathsf{c}(1-x)^{\alpha+1}(1-x)^{-\beta}-1\right)\mathsf{p}(x)\mathrm{d}x

is finite for all
\beta\in(-\infty,2\alpha+\frac{3}{2}).
It is continuous and strictly increasing as a function of
\beta
, with limits

\lim_{\beta\to-\infty}I(\alpha,\beta)=-\infty\mbox{ and }\lim_{\beta\to 2\alpha+\frac{3}{2}^{-}}I(\alpha,\beta)=+\infty.

Proof.

By symmetry, we only need to check what happens when
x
is close to
0
. Since
\mathsf{c}(1)=1
and
\mathsf{c}
is differentiable, we have
\mathsf{c}(1-x)^{\alpha+1}(1-x)^{-\beta}-1\sim Ax
for some
A\in{\mathbb{R}}
, hence
(x(1-x))^{-3/2}(\mathsf{c}(1-x)^{\alpha+1}(1-x)^{-\beta}-1)
is integrable, while since
\mathsf{c}(x)\sim x^{2},

(x(1-x))^{-3/2}\mathsf{c}(x)^{\alpha+1}x^{-\beta}\sim x^{2\alpha-\beta+1/2}
is integrable only if
\beta<2\alpha+\frac{3}{2}.

Monotonicity, continuity and limit properties are direct consequences of the same properties for the integrand and the monotone convergence theorem.
∎

It follows that, for each
\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}
, there is a unique
\beta(\alpha)
such that
I(\alpha,\beta(\alpha))=0
.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Fix
\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}
. We will prove that, for all
\varepsilon>0,

e_{n}=O(n^{-\beta+\varepsilon})
and
e_{n}=\Omega(n^{-\beta-\varepsilon}),
from which the result follows straightforwardly.

We start with the upper bound. For
n\in\mathbb{N},
let
C_{n}=\underset{0\leqslant k\leqslant n}{\sup}\,e_{k}(k+1)^{\beta-\varepsilon};
we aim to show by induction that
C_{n}
stays bounded as
n
tends to infinity. Recall the equation

e_{n}=\sum_{a=1}^{n-1}P(a,b)\left(C(a,b)^{\alpha+1}e_{a}+C(b,a)^{\alpha+1}e_{b}\right)

where
b
is implicitly always equal to
n-1-a.
We then have

e_{n}\leqslant C_{n-1}\sum_{a=1}^{n-1}P(a,b)\left(C(a,b)^{\alpha+1}(a+1)^{-\beta+\varepsilon}+C(b,a)^{\alpha+1}(b+1)^{-\beta+\varepsilon}\right).

Using the fact that
\sum_{a=1}^{n-1}P(a,b)=1,
we can now write

\frac{e_{n}}{C_{n-1}(n+1)^{-\beta+\varepsilon}}\leqslant 1+\sum_{a=1}^{n-1}P(a,b)\left(C(a,b)^{\alpha+1}\left(\frac{a+1}{n+1}\right)^{-\beta+\varepsilon}+C(b,a)^{\alpha+1}\left(\frac{b+1}{n+1}\right)^{-\beta+\varepsilon}-1\right).

While the sum on the RHS is not of the type featured in Proposition
2.7
, it is reasonable to expect that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\sqrt{n}\sum_{a=1}^{n-1}P(a,b)\left(C(a,b)^{\alpha+1}\left(\frac{a+1}{n+1}\right)^{-\beta+\varepsilon}+C(b,a)^{\alpha+1}\left(\frac{b+1}{n+1}\right)^{-\beta+\varepsilon}-1\right)=\frac{1}{4\sqrt{\pi}}I(\alpha,\beta-\varepsilon).

(20)

We postpone checking (
20
) to the end of this proof.

Now, noting that
I(\alpha,\beta-\varepsilon)<0,
we then get that, for
n
large enough,

\frac{e_{n}}{C_{n-1}n^{-\beta+\varepsilon}}\leqslant 1-\frac{A}{\sqrt{n}}

for some
A>0,
hence

e_{n}\leqslant C_{n-1}(1-\frac{A}{\sqrt{n}})(n+1)^{-\beta+\varepsilon}\leqslant C_{n-1}(n+1)^{-\beta+\varepsilon}.

This shows that
C_{n+1}=C_{n},
and so the sequence
(C_{n},n\in\mathbb{N})
is eventually constant.

The lower bound is proved the same way: letting
c_{n}=\underset{k\leqslant n}{\inf}\,e_{k}(k+1)^{\beta+\varepsilon},
we obtain

\frac{e_{n}}{c_{n-1}(n+1)^{-\beta-\varepsilon}}\geqslant 1+\frac{1}{4\sqrt{\pi n}}I(\alpha,\beta+\varepsilon)+o(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}),

and since
I(\alpha,\beta+\varepsilon)>0
this implies that the sequence
(c_{n},n\in\mathbb{N})
is eventually constant, completing our proof.

We now prove statement (
20
). We will use bounds (
8
) and (
9
) as well as

\frac{1}{3}\mathsf{c}{\left(\frac{a}{n}\right)}\leqslant C(a,b)\leqslant 3\mathsf{c}{\left(\frac{a}{n}\right)},

for
a+b=n-1.
As with Proposition
2.7
, it is sufficient to only consider

\sqrt{n}\sum_{a=2}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}P(a,b)\left(C(a,b)^{\alpha+1}\left(\frac{a+1}{n+1}\right)^{-\beta+\varepsilon}+C(b,a)^{\alpha+1}\left(\frac{b+1}{n+1}\right)^{-\beta+\varepsilon}-1\right).

This sum is equal to
\int_{0}^{1/2}g_{n}(x)\mathrm{d}x,
where

g_{n}(x)=n^{3/2}P(a,b)\left(C(a,b)^{\alpha+1}\left(\frac{a+1}{n+1}\right)^{-\beta+\varepsilon}+C(b,a)^{\alpha+1}\left(\frac{b+1}{n+1}\right)^{-\beta+\varepsilon}-1\right)

if
x\in[\frac{a-1}{n},\frac{a}{n})
with
a\in\{2,\ldots,\lfloor n/2\rfloor\},
and
g_{n}(x)=0
otherwise. It is readily checked that
g_{n}(x)
converges pointwise towards
\frac{1}{4\sqrt{\pi}}\mathsf{p}(x)\left(\mathsf{c}(x)^{\alpha+1}x^{-\beta}+\mathsf{c}(1-x)^{\alpha+1}(1-x)^{-\beta}-1\right),

and we need to check that the convergence is dominated. Notice the following, where the constant
K>0
may vary from line to line:

•

Since
n^{3/2}P(a,b)\left(\frac{a}{n}\right)^{3/2}\left(\frac{b}{n}\right)^{3/2}
is uniformly bounded in
a
and
n
, and
x<\frac{1}{2},
we have
n^{3/2}P(a,b)\leqslant Kx^{-3/2}.

•

Since
\frac{1}{3}\mathsf{c}(\frac{a}{n})\leqslant C(a,b)\leqslant 3\mathsf{c}(\frac{a}{n})
and
x\leqslant\frac{a}{n}\leqslant 2x,
we have
\frac{1}{3}\mathsf{c}(x)\leqslant C(a,b)\leqslant 3\mathsf{c}(2x).
Since moreover
x\leqslant\frac{a+1}{n+1}\leqslant 3x,
we deduce that
C(a,b)^{\alpha+1}\left(\frac{a+1}{n+1}\right)^{-\beta+\epsilon}\leqslant Kx^{2(\alpha+1)}x^{-\beta+\varepsilon},
independently of the signs of
\alpha+1
and
\beta.

•

Similarly, we have
|1-C(b,a)|\leqslant Kx^{2},
and
\left|1-\frac{b+1}{n+1}\right|\leqslant Kx
hence
\left|C(b,a)^{\alpha+1}\left(\frac{b+1}{n+1}\right)^{-\beta+\epsilon}-1\right|\leqslant Kx.

Combining these together yields a constant K such that

|g_{n}(x)|\leqslant\frac{K}{x}^{-3/2}\left(x^{2\alpha+2-\beta+\varepsilon}+x\right),

which is a uniform integrable bound on
(0,1/2).

∎

5
Towards a diffusion limit

In this work we studied in details the leaf-growth measure on large binary trees and their continuous limit. This is the first step towards understanding the possible Markov process on real trees that one would obtain by passing the discrete Luczak–Winkler growth procedure to the limit. In the following we heuristically call “CRT dynamic" any Markov process with values in compact real trees with invariant measure given by the Brownian CRT.

Stationary dynamics.

A natural way to define a CRT dynamic is to take a scaling limit of a Markov chain
(T^{(n)}_{k}:k\geqslant 1)
on (say) the space of binary trees with fixed size
n
with uniform invariant distribution. There are many such chains: the flip dynamic
[
MT97
]
, Aldous’ move on cladograms
[
LMW20
,
FPRW23
]
, subtree pruning and re-graft
[
EW06
]
… Passing those dynamics to the scaling limit as
n\to\infty
requires first to understand the appropriate scaling in time which is related to the mixing time of the chain: we want to find
f(n)
so that

\left(T^{(n)}_{[f(n)t]}:t\geqslant 0\right)

converges towards a CRT dynamic. When the Markov chain is itself reversible, the dynamic can even be extended to
\mathbb{R}
. Finding
f(n)
is usually a hard problem and has been the subject of many investigations in recent years.

Growing dynamics.

Another route is to consider a growing chain
(T_{n}:n\geqslant 1)
on (say) the set of binary trees so that
T_{n}
is uniformly distributed on plane binary trees of size
n
for each
n\geqslant 1
fixed. Those chains do not require to understand the mixing time since the time and size are intimately tight and the limit CRT-dynamic should be the scaling limit as
n\to\infty
of

\displaystyle\left(\frac{T_{[n\mathrm{e}^{s}]}}{\sqrt{n\mathrm{e}^{s}}}\right)_{s\in\mathbb{R}},

(21)

where we performed the time change
\mathrm{e}^{s}=t
to get a two-sided dynamic.

The most well-known growth procedure is perhaps Rémy’s algorithm
[
Rém85
]
, where iteratively, a uniform edge of
T_{n}
is split in its middle to yield
T_{n+1}
. When the choices of edges are made independently for each
n
, the resulting Markov chain converges almost surely towards a Brownian CRT
[
CH13
]
, so that the limiting CRT dynamic obtained in (
21
) is constant (not so interesting). However, still in the Rémy growth, there is a less well-known way of coupling the choices of edges, due to Bacher, Bodini and Jacquot
[
BBJ17
]
, which yields a different Markov chain, whose growth is more “local". The scaling limits of this chain given in (
21
) is under current investigation and is a CRT dynamic with “local and continuous growth".

The Luczak–Winkler leaf-growth mechanism studied in these pages is totally different from Rémy’s algorithm. We conjecture that if
(T^{lw}_{n}:n\geqslant 1)
is a sequence of uniform binary trees so that conditionally on
(T^{lw}_{k}:1\leqslant k\leqslant n)
, the tree
T^{lw}_{n+1}
is obtained by growing a cherry on point sample according to the leaf-growth measure on
T^{lw}_{n}
, then (
21
) yields a non-trivial CRT-dynamic
(\mathcal{LW}_{t}:t\in\mathbb{R})
. This growth should be non-local and be dictated by the fractal leaf-growth measure studied in this work. The following proposition indicates that the limiting CRT dynamic should be mixing:

Proposition 5.1
.

Consider a sequence
(m_{n},n\in\mathbb{N})
which tends to
\infty
such that
m_{n}=o(n)
. We abuse notation and skip the
n
index in the sequel. We have

\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}T^{lw}_{m},\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}T^{lw}_{n}\right)\underset{n\to\infty}{\overset{(d)}{\longrightarrow}}(2\sqrt{2}\,\mathcal{T},2\sqrt{2}\,\mathcal{T}^{\prime}),

where
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
is an independent copy of
\mathcal{T}
.

Proof.

By construction,
T^{lw}_{n}
can be seen as a tree obtained from
T^{lw}_{m}
by grafting trees at each of the
m+1
leaves of
T^{lw}_{m}.
More precisely, if we denote by
S_{1}(n,m),\ldots,S_{m+1}(n,m)
the sizes of the subtrees
(\theta_{1}(n,m),\ldots,\theta_{m+1}(n,m))
grafted on the leaves of
T^{lw}_{m}
to get
T^{lw}_{n}
, it should be clear from the dynamics that conditionally on the vector
(S_{1}(n,m),\ldots,S_{m+1}(n,m))
and on
T^{lw}_{m}
, the random trees
(\theta_{1}(n,m),\ldots,\theta_{m+1}(n,m))
are independent and uniform binary trees of sizes
(S_{1}(n,m),\ldots,S_{m+1}(n,m))
. Since the random tree
T^{lw}_{m}
has diameter of order
\sqrt{m}=o_{\mathbb{P}}(\sqrt{n})
, the root of the
m+1
trees
\theta_{1}(n,m),\ldots,

\theta_{m+1}(n,m)
are almost identified in
T^{lw}_{n}
. Since the roots of the Brownian CRT is almost surely a point of degree
1
, it follows that as
n,m\to\infty
satisfying
\frac{n}{m}\to\infty
, with high probability there exists a unique index
1\leqslant I(n,m)\leqslant m+1
such that

n^{-1}\cdot S_{I(n,m)}(n,m)\to 1\quad\mbox{and}\quad n^{-1/2}\cdot\max_{i\neq I(n,m)}\mathrm{Height}(\theta_{i}(n,m))\to 0,

in probability. In particular, the geometry of
n^{-1/2}\cdot T^{lw}_{n}
is close to that of
S_{I(n,m)}(n,m)^{-1/2}\cdot\theta_{I(n,m)}(n,m)
which converges towards a Brownian CRT, while being independent of
T^{lw}_{m}
, and hence the respective scaling limits are also independent. ∎

Let us conclude this paper with the following remark: as in
[
BBJ17
]
for the Rémy growth, it seems possible in our case to
couple
the sampling of the leaves in the leaf-growth mechanism to get even more “local" growth process
(\tilde{T}_{n}^{lw}:n\geqslant 0)
whose dynamical scaling limits seem easier to establish than that
({T}_{n}^{lw}:n\geqslant 0)
. We hope to address those questions in future work.

Appendix A: computing integral (
13
)

We are going to prove that, for all real
\alpha\geqslant 0
,

\Phi(\alpha)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{0}^{1}\left(1-x^{\alpha}\mathsf{c}(x)-(1-x)^{\alpha}\mathsf{c}(1-x)\right)\mathsf{p}(x)\mathrm{d}x=2\sqrt{2}\alpha\frac{\Gamma(\frac{3}{2}+\alpha)}{\Gamma(2+\alpha)}.

(22)

Recall that for
x,y\in\mathbb{C}
with
\Re x,\Re y>0
the Beta function is defined by

B(x,y)=\int_{0}^{1}t^{x-1}(1-t)^{y-1}\mathrm{d}t.

and satisfies the fundamental equality

B(x,y)=\frac{\Gamma(x)\Gamma(y)}{\Gamma(x+y)}.

Note that, since
\Gamma(1/2)=\sqrt{\pi}
, what we are claiming is that
\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}\Phi(\alpha)=2\alpha B(\frac{3}{2}+\alpha,\frac{1}{2})
. In order to show this, one can use the following lemma:

Lemma 5.2
.

For
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}
such that
\Re\alpha>\frac{1}{2}
, we have

\int_{0}^{1}(1-x^{\alpha}-(1-x)^{\alpha})(x(1-x))^{-3/2}\mathrm{d}x=4\alpha\left(B\left(\alpha+\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}\right)-B\left(\frac{3}{2},\alpha-\frac{1}{2}\right)\right).

Proof.

Let
F(x)=1-x^{\alpha}-(1-x)^{\alpha}
,
g(x)=(x(1-x))^{-3/2}
and

G(x)=\frac{2(2x-1)}{\sqrt{x(1-x)}}.

One can immediately check that
G(x)
is a primitive of
g(x)
; thus, integrating by parts and noticing that the boundary term
F(x)G(x)
has limit equal to
0
both as
x\to 0
and
x\to 1
, we find that the integral in the statement is equal to

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{1}F^{\prime}(x)G(x)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\left(\alpha x^{\alpha-1}-\alpha(1-x)^{\alpha-1}\right)\frac{2(2x-1)}{\sqrt{x(1-x)}}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=2\alpha\int_{0}^{1}\left(2x^{\alpha}-x^{\alpha-1}-2x(1-x)^{\alpha-1}+(1-x)^{\alpha-1}\right)\frac{1}{\sqrt{x(1-x)}}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=2\alpha\left(2B\left(\alpha+\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}\right)-B\left(\alpha-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}\right)-2B\left(\frac{3}{2},\alpha-\frac{1}{2}\right)+B\left(\frac{1}{2},\alpha-\frac{1}{2}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=2\alpha\left(2B\left(\alpha+\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}\right)-2B\left(\frac{3}{2},\alpha-\frac{1}{2}\right)\right),

where we have used the symmetry
B\left(\frac{1}{2},\alpha-\frac{1}{2}\right)=B\left(\alpha-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}\right)
.
∎

Now, in order to compute (
22
), we can just express
f_{3}(x)
as
2x^{2}(1-x)+x^{2}
, thus decomposing
2\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}\Phi(\alpha)
as the sum of three integrals
I_{1}(\alpha),I_{2}(\alpha),I_{3}(\alpha)
:

I_{1}(\alpha):=\int_{0}^{1}-x^{\alpha}2x^{2}(1-x)x^{-3/2}(1-x)^{-3/2}\mathrm{d}x=-2B\left(\frac{3}{2}+\alpha,\frac{1}{2}\right).

I_{2}(\alpha):=\int_{0}^{1}-(1-x)^{\alpha}2(1-x)^{2}xx^{-3/2}(1-x)^{-3/2}\mathrm{d}x=-2B\left(\frac{1}{2},\frac{3}{2}+\alpha\right).

I_{3}(\alpha)=\int_{0}^{1}(1-x^{\alpha+2}-(1-x)^{\alpha+2})(x(1-x))^{-3/2}\mathrm{d}x.

Using Lemma
5.2
with parameter
\alpha+2
(thus merely requiring the condition that
\Re\alpha>-3/2
), the quantity
I_{3}(\alpha)
can be rewritten as

I_{3}(\alpha)=2(2\alpha+4)\left(B\left(\frac{5}{2}+\alpha,\frac{1}{2}\right)-B\left(\frac{3}{2},\frac{3}{2}+\alpha\right)\right).

On the other hand, one has

B\left(\frac{5}{2}+\alpha,\frac{1}{2}\right)=\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{5}{2}+\alpha\right)\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(3+\alpha\right)}=\frac{(\frac{3}{2}+\alpha)\Gamma\left(\frac{3}{2}+\alpha\right)\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)}{(2+\alpha)\Gamma\left(2+\alpha\right)}=\frac{2\alpha+3}{2\alpha+4}B\left(\frac{3}{2}+\alpha,\frac{1}{2}\right)

and

B\left(\frac{3}{2},\frac{3}{2}+\alpha\right)=\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{3}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(\frac{3}{2}+\alpha\right)}{\Gamma\left(3+\alpha\right)}=\frac{\frac{1}{2}\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(\frac{3}{2}+\alpha\right)}{(2+\alpha)\Gamma\left(2+\alpha\right)}=\frac{1}{2\alpha+4}B\left(\frac{3}{2}+\alpha,\frac{1}{2}\right).

Setting for simplicity
B:=B\left(\frac{3}{2}+\alpha,\frac{1}{2}\right)
, we have obtained

2\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}\Phi(\alpha)=I_{1}(\alpha)+I_{2}(\alpha)+I_{3}(\alpha)=-4B+2(2\alpha+4)\left(\frac{2\alpha+3}{2\alpha+4}B-\frac{1}{2\alpha+4}B\right)=-4B+2(2\alpha+2)B=4\alpha B,

as desired.
