Indicatrix of Invariant metrics, Maximal circularity and Scaling of domains
Huda Alzaki and Jae-Cheon Joo

Abstract
. We investigate how to use the indicatrix of an invariant metric to rescale a sequence of biholomorphic maps and to ensure the convergence of the rescaled sequence. We also use the indicatrix of the Kobayashi-Royden metric to define the maximal circularity function which indicates how the domain is close to a circular domain.

32H02, 32M05

1.
Introduction

This paper consists of several observations on the celebrated work
[
5
]
by S. Frankel which states that a convex hyperbolic domain in complex Euclidean space admitting a discrete and cocompact subgroup of automorphisms which acts freely is biholomorphic to a bounded symmetric domain. As a key ingredient, Frankel proved that if
\Omega
is a convex hyperbolic domain, then for any sequence
\{\varphi_{j}\}
of holomorphic automorphisms and any
p\in\Omega
, the
scaled
sequence

(1.1)

\tau_{j}=[d\varphi_{j}(p)]^{-1}\circ(\varphi_{j}(\cdot)-\varphi_{j}(p)),

has a convergent subsequence. In this paper, we investigate the sequence (
1.1
) about the construction, the convergence and the symmetry of limits in a geometric point of view on invariant metrics.

Roughly speaking, by the method of scaling we mean a way of renomalizing maps in a sequence by compositions with affine linear maps to make the new sequence subseuentially convergent. The linear map which is composed for the scalng is usually called the
stretching factor
. Although the idea of scaling is used in a wide range of mathematics, two specific constructions are usually cited when we deal with sequences of holomorphic automorphisms of a domain: Frankel’s sequence (
1.1
) and Pinchuk’s one (
[
19
]
). In contrast with (
1.1
), Pinchuk’s construction of the stretching factor is more complicated and not completely known in general cases of domain. On the other hand, Pinchuk’s construction has a merit that the stretching factor is completely determined by the geometry of domain, while the stretching factor of (
1.1
) is
(d\varphi_{j}(p))^{-1}
and it depends on the map. The independence of stretching factor from the map in Pinchuk’s constuction turned out to be quite useful in dealing with invariants of domain, and it has been asked if or when two constuctions are equivalent. There are some partial results about the question (
[
8
,
12
]
), however it is not answered yet in general cases. Instead, we construct a new strecthing factor which is fully detemined by the indicatrix of an invariant metric and prove that the scaled sequence with the new stretching factor is indeed equivalent with (
1.1
) as the first result (Theorm
2.2
).

The second observation is on a condition for the convergence of (
1.1
). As mentioned above, Frankel proved that the sequence is subsequentially convergent if
\Omega
is convex and hyperbolic by obtaining local uniform estimates of derivatives for automorphisms up to the second order. In view of Theorem
2.2
, it is also natural to describe a geometric condition on invariant metrics for the convergence. The condition is given by a relation between the indicatrix of an invariant metric and the ball of the induced distance. We call the condition the
uniform domination
of the domain by the given metric (Definition
2.4
). After observing the convergence of (
1.1
) under the uniform domination condition, we will prove that any convex hyperbolic domain is uniformly dominated by the Kobayashi-Royden metric in Theorem
2.8
in order to recover the case of
[
5
]
.

The last result is about a symmetry of the image of a subsequential limit of (
1.1
). In
[
5
]
, the author chooses a sequence of automorphisms which drags an interior point to a
regular
boundary point, and proves the image of the limit (and hence the given domain as well) admits a
1
-parameter family of automorphisms. Let us recall that if
q
is a regular boundary point of a bounded symmetric domain which is not biholomorphic to the ball, then there is a nontrivial analytic variety in the boundary which passes through
q
. On the other hand, a boundary point
q
of a domain is called a
minimal
boundary point if the boundary of domain does not contain any nontrivial analytic variety passing through
q
. As seen in some examples of domain of piecewise smooth boundary (
[
11
,
13
,
14
,
21
]
), the image of the limit of the scaled sequence is usually more canonical in case the given sequence of automorphisms drags an interior point to a minimal boundary point rather than to a non-minimal boundary point. With this observation, it is natural to ask
what kind of symmetry the limit of (
1.1
) has, in case
\Omega
is bounded and convex, and
\varphi_{j}(p)
tends to a minimal boundary point.
Another question is
where the circularity of the domain comes from.
Recall that the circularity is a signature property of bounded symmetric domains in the set of bounded homogeneous domains. We believe that these two questions are indeed same and can be combined to the following one.

Question.
Let
\Omega
be a bounded convex domain in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
. Suppose that there exists a sequence
\{\varphi_{j}\}
of holomorphic automorphisms and
p\in\Omega
such that
\varphi_{j}(p)
tends to a minimal boundary point of
\Omega
. Then is
\Omega
biholomorphic to a completely circular domain?

In order to study this problem in more geometric point of view, we define in Section 3 an invariant function
c_{\Omega}
on
\Omega
which we call the
maximal circularity
. We will see that
0<c_{\Omega}(x)\leq 1
for any
x\in\Omega
and
c_{\Omega}(x)=1
if and only if
\Omega
is biholomorphic to the indicatrix of the Kobayashi-Royden metric of
\Omega
at
x
. With this, we can ask a more general question.

Question.
Let
\Omega
be a bounded convex domain in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
. Does
c_{\Omega}(x)
tend to
1
as
x
tends to a minimal boundary point?

We do not know yet whether or not the answers to the questions are all yes. We will only prove in Section 3 that the answer to the last question is affirmative in case
\Omega
is either strongly pseudoconvex (not necessarily convex) or a convex analytic polyhedral domain.

2.
Construction and Convergence of a scaling sequence

Let
\Omega
be a domain in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
. By a
complex Finsler metric
or simply a
metric
on
\Omega
, we mean an upper semicontinuous (usc) function
F
on
T\Omega=\Omega\times\mathbb{C}^{n}
such that

(i)

F(x;v)\geq 0
for any
x\in\Omega
and
v\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
and
F(x;v)=0
only if
v=0
, and

(ii)

F(x;cv)=|c|F(x;v)
for any
(x,v)\in\Omega\times\mathbb{C}^{n}
and
c\in\mathbb{C}
.

The
indicatrix
of
F
at
x\in\Omega
is the set

I_{\Omega,F}(x):=\{v\in\mathbb{C}^{n}:F(x;v)<1\}.

We say a metric on
\Omega
is
convex
if the indicatrix of
F
is a convex domain in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
at any point of
\Omega
.

Example 2.1
.

Obviously, any Hermitian metric on
\Omega
is a convex metric. In particular, the Bergman metric or the Kähler-Einstein metric are convex metrics which are invariant under biholomorphic maps. Another example of invariant metric on a domain is the Kobayashi-Royden metric which is defined by

K_{\Omega}(x;v)=\inf\{\lambda>0:\exists f\in\mathcal{O}(\Delta,\Omega),\,\,f(0)=x,\,\,\lambda f^{\prime}(0)=v\},

where
\mathcal{O}(\Delta,\Omega)
represents the space of holomorphic mappings from the unit disc
\Delta
into
\Omega
. Indeed,
K_{\Omega}
is not necessarily a metric, since
K_{\Omega}(x;v)
may be
0
for a nonzero vector
v
, however, it turns out that it is a metric for instance if
\Omega
is bounded. We call a domain (or more generally a complex manifold) a
hyperbolic domain
if
K_{\Omega}
is a metric. It turns out that
K_{\Omega}
is convex if
\Omega
is convex however it is not true for general hyperbolic domains. To make it convex, one can consider a new invariant metric
\widehat{K}_{\Omega}
whose indicatrix is the convex hull of the indicatrix of
K_{\Omega}
at each point. The metric is called the
Kobayashi-Buseman metric.
See for instance
[
7
,
16
]
for more details about invariant metrics.

Let
F
be a metric on a domain
\Omega
and
x\in\Omega
. By the upper semicontnuity, we can find
u_{1}=u_{1}(x)\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
with
|u_{1}|=1
such that

\mu_{1}(x):=F(x;u_{1})=\max\{F(x;v):v\in\mathbb{C}^{n},\,\,|v|=1\}.

Inductively, if we have chosen
u_{1},\ldots,u_{\alpha}
and
\mu_{\beta}=F(x;u_{\beta})
for
1\leq\beta\leq\alpha\leq n-1
, then a unit vector
u_{\alpha+1}
and
\mu_{\alpha+1}
are defined by

\mu_{\alpha+1}(x):=F(x;u_{\alpha+1})=\max\{F(x;v):v\in V_{\alpha},\,\,|v|=1\},

where
V_{\alpha}
is the orthogonal complement of
\{u_{1}\ldots,u_{\alpha}\}
in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
.
Let
L_{x}
be the linear transform on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
defined by

(2.1)

L_{x}(u_{\alpha})=\mu_{\alpha}(x)e_{\alpha},\quad\alpha=1,\ldots,n,

where
\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}\}
is the standard Euclidean basis for
\mathbb{C}^{n}
. We call
L_{x}
a
stretching factor
of
\Omega
at
x
induced by the metric
F
.

For a sequence
\varphi_{j}\in\mathrm{Aut}(\Omega)
, the group of holomorphic automorphisms of
\Omega
, S. Frankel (
[
5
]
) defined a sequence

(2.2)

\tau_{j}=[d\varphi_{j}(p)]^{-1}\circ(\varphi_{j}(\cdot)-p_{j}),

where
p_{j}=\varphi_{j}(p)
and
d\varphi_{j}(p)
represents the complex Jacobian matrix of
\varphi_{j}
at
p
. Frankel proved that the sequence
\{\tau_{j}\}
has a convergent subsequence if
\Omega
is hyperbolic and convex. We prove that the linear transform
[d\varphi_{j}(p)]^{-1}
can be replaced by a stretching factor induced by an invariant metric.

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
\Omega
be a domain in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
,
p\in\Omega
and
\{\varphi_{j}\}
be a sequence of holomorphic automorphisms. Then the sequence (
2.2
) converges subsequentially if and only if

(2.3)

\sigma_{j}:=L_{j}\circ(\varphi_{j}(\cdot)-p_{j})

converges subsequentially, where
L_{j}=L_{p_{j}}
is a stretching factor induced by any convex invariant metric.

Proof.

Let
F
be a convex invariant metric on
\Omega
. Suppose that
u_{\alpha}^{j}
and
\mu_{\alpha}^{j}
were chosen to construct
L_{j}
by (
2.1
) at
p_{j}=\varphi_{j}(p)
and let

\tilde{u}^{j}_{\alpha}=d\varphi_{j}(p)^{-1}(u^{j}_{\alpha}),\quad\alpha=1,\ldots,n.

Since
\varphi_{j}
preserves
F
, we see that
F(p;\tilde{u}^{j}_{\alpha})=\mu^{j}_{\alpha}
. Therefore,
the vectors
\tilde{u}^{j}_{\alpha}/\mu^{j}_{\alpha}\in\partial I_{\Omega,F}(p),
where

I_{\Omega,F}(q)=\{v\in\mathbb{C}^{n}:F(q;v)<1\},

the indicatrix of
F
at
q\in\Omega
. Notice that the indicatrix is always convex for any
q\in\Omega
. Let
A_{j}
be the linear map on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
defined by

A_{j}\left(\frac{\tilde{u}^{j}_{\alpha}}{\mu^{j}_{\alpha}}\right)=e_{\alpha},\quad\alpha=1,\ldots,n.

Then

L_{j}=A_{j}\circ d\varphi_{j}(p)^{-1}

by definition. Therefore, we only need to show that

(2.4)

C_{1}|v|\leq|A_{j}v|\leq C_{2}|v|,\quad\forall v\in\mathbb{C}^{n}

for some constants
C_{1}>0
and
C_{2}>0
which are independent of
j
, in order to show the equivalence of the subsequential convergence of
\{\sigma_{j}\}
and
\{\tau_{j}\}
.

The lower estimate is equivalent to the upper bound of
A_{j}^{-1}
. This is obvious since

|A_{j}^{-1}v|=\left|A_{j}^{-1}\left(\sum_{\alpha}a_{\alpha}e_{\alpha}\right)\right|=\left|\sum_{\alpha}a_{\alpha}\left(\frac{\tilde{u}^{j}_{\alpha}}{\mu^{j}_{\alpha}}\right)\right|\leq nM|v|

for any
v=\sum_{\alpha}a_{\alpha}e_{\alpha}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
, where
M=\sup\{|w|:w\in\partial I_{\Omega,F}(p)\}
.

This lower estimate and Cramer’s formula of inverse transform also implies that the upper estimate in (
2.4
) follows from an estimate of the determinant of
A_{j}
, that is, the upper estimate is obtained if we prove

(2.5)

|\det A_{j}|\leq C

for some constant
C>0
independent of
j
. The definition of
L_{j}
implies

(2.6)

|\det L_{j}|=\prod_{\alpha}\mu^{j}_{\alpha}.

Let
\det_{\mathbb{R}}
be the determinant of real linear operator on
\mathbb{R}^{2n}=\mathbb{C}^{n}
. Then

(2.7)

{\det}_{\mathbb{R}}d\varphi_{j}(p)=|\det d\varphi_{j}(p)|^{2}=\frac{\mathrm{Vol}(I_{\Omega,F}(p_{j}))}{\mathrm{Vol}(I_{\Omega,F}(p))}=C\,\mathrm{Vol}(I_{\Omega,F}(p_{j}))

where
\mathrm{Vol}(E)
represents the Euclidean volume of a given region
E
and
C^{-1}=\mathrm{Vol}(I_{\Omega,F}(p))
, since
d\varphi_{j}(p)
maps
I_{\Omega}(p)
onto
I_{\Omega}(p_{j})
. Identifying the vector
\sum_{\alpha}a_{\alpha}u^{j}_{\alpha}
with the
n
-tuple
(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})
, we regard
I_{\Omega,F}(p_{j})
as a balanced convex set in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
. Regarding
\mathbb{C}^{n}=\mathbb{R}^{n}+\sqrt{-1}\mathbb{R}^{n}
, let

R_{j}=I_{\Omega,F}(p_{j})\cap\mathbb{R}^{n}=\{(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})\in I_{\Omega,F}(p_{j}):a_{\alpha}\mbox{'s are real}\}.

Since
I_{\Omega,F}(p_{j})
is completely circular, it is a circular region generated by
R_{j}
and
R_{j}
is symmetric with respect to the origin. Notice that
R_{j}
is also a convex region in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
since
I_{\Omega,F}(p_{j})
is convex. In order to prove the estimate (
2.5
), we need the following lemma.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
R
be a bounded convex domain in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
which is symmetric with respect to the origin. Let

r_{1}=\min\{|x|:x\in\partial R\}.

Suppose that
(r_{1},0,\ldots,0)\in\partial R
(this means the minimum distance to the boundary is attained at the
x_{1}
-intercept of
\partial R
). Let

\widetilde{R}=\{(x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}:(0,x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})\in R\}.

Suppose

\widetilde{R}\subset\widetilde{B}:=[-r_{2},r_{2}]\times\cdots\times[-r_{n},r_{n}]

for some
r_{2},\ldots,r_{n}>0
. Then

R\subset B:=[-r_{1},r_{1}]\times 2\widetilde{B}=[-r_{1},r_{1}]\times[-2r_{2},2r_{2}]\times\cdots\times[-2r_{n},2r_{n}].

Proof of Lemma
2.3
.

We notice first that the supporting hyperplanes at
(\pm r_{1},0,\ldots,0)
are

x_{1}=\pm r_{1},

since
r_{1}
is the minimum distance from the origin to
\partial R
. Therefore

x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in R\Longrightarrow|x_{1}|\leq r_{1}.

We also observe that we may assume
\widetilde{R}=\widetilde{B}
. Otherwise, we only need to replace
R
by the convex hull of
\widetilde{B}\cup R
. Since
\partial\widetilde{R}=\partial\widetilde{B}
contains boxes
\{\pm r_{2}\}\times[-r_{3},r_{3}]\times\cdots\times[-r_{n},r_{n}]
, the supporting hyperplanes of
R
at
(0,\pm r_{2},0,\ldots,0)\in\partial R
also contains those boxes. This means if

x_{2}=\alpha_{1}x_{1}+\alpha_{3}x_{3}+\cdots+\alpha_{n}x_{n}\pm r_{2}

are the equation of the supporting hyperplanes, then
\alpha_{3}=\cdots=\alpha_{n}=0.
Then from the condition that the distance from the origin to the supporting hyperplanes are greater than equal to
r_{1}
, we have

\frac{r_{2}}{\sqrt{\alpha_{1}^{2}+1}}\geq r_{1}.

Thereore,

|\alpha_{1}|\leq\sqrt{\left(\frac{r_{2}}{r_{1}}\right)^{2}-1}.

Since
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
lies between two support hyperplanes
x_{2}=\alpha_{1}x_{1}\pm r_{2}
and
|x_{1}|\leq r_{1}
if
x\in R
, we conclude that

|x_{2}|\leq\sqrt{r_{2}^{2}-r_{1}^{2}}+r_{2}\leq 2r_{2}.

Similarly, we can also show that
|x_{\alpha}|\leq 2r_{\alpha}
for any
\alpha=2,\ldots,n
whenever
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in R
. This yields the conclusion.
∎

We resume the proof of Theorem
2.2
. Let

R_{j,1}=\{(x_{n-1},x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:(0,\ldots,0,x_{n-1},x_{n})\in R_{j}\}.

Applying Lemma
2.3
, we see that

R_{j,1}\subset B_{j,1}:=[-1/\mu^{j}_{n-1},1/\mu^{j}_{n-1}]\times[-2/\mu^{j}_{n},2/\mu^{j}_{n}],

since

\widetilde{R}_{j,1}=[-1/\mu^{j}_{n},1/\mu^{j}_{n}].
Applying the lemma to

R_{j,2}=\{(x_{n-2},x_{n-1},x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:(0,\ldots,0,x_{n-2},x_{n-1},x_{n})\in R_{j}\},

we see that

R_{j,1}\subset B_{j,2}:=[-1/\mu^{j}_{n-2},1/\mu^{j}_{n-2}]\times[-2/\mu^{j}_{n-1},2/\mu^{j}_{n-1}]\times[-2^{2}/\mu^{j}_{n},2^{2}/\mu^{j}_{n}],

since
\widetilde{R}_{j,2}=R_{j,1}\subset B_{j,1}.
Repeating this procedure, we conclude that

R_{j}\subset B_{j}:=\prod_{\alpha=1}^{n}[-2^{\alpha-1}/\mu^{j}_{\alpha},2^{\alpha-1}/\mu^{j}_{\alpha}].

Therefore, the indicatrix
I_{\Omega,F}(p_{j})
is a subset of the circular region in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
generated by
B_{j}
which is the polydisc

P_{j}:=\{z=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n})\in\mathbb{C}^{n}:|z_{\alpha}|\leq 2^{\alpha-1}/\mu^{j}_{\alpha},\,\,\alpha=1,\ldots,n\}.

This implies that

\mathrm{Vol}(I_{\Omega,F}(p_{j}))\leq\mathrm{Vol}(P_{j})\leq C\prod_{\alpha}\left(\frac{1}{\mu^{j}_{\alpha}}\right)^{2}

for some constant
C>0
independent of
j
. Then (
2.6
) and (
2.7
) yields the desired estimate (
2.5
).
∎

We will now investigate a condition of metric under that the Frankel sequence (
2.2
) has a convergent subsequence. Let
F
be a metric on a domain
\Omega
. We denote by
d_{\Omega,F}
the induced distance function, that is,

d_{\Omega,F}(x,y)=\inf\left\{\int_{0}^{1}F(c(t);c^{\prime}(t))\,dt:c\in C(x,y)\right\}

where
C(x,y)
denotes the space of all piecewise differentiable (real) curve in
\Omega
joining
x
and
y
. For
r>0
, we denote the
d_{\Omega,F}
-ball of radius
r
by
B_{\Omega,F}(x;r)
, that is,

B_{\Omega,F}(x;r)=\{y\in\Omega:d_{\Omega,F}(x,y)<r\}.

Definition 2.4
.

Let
\Omega
be a domain in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
. We say that
\Omega
is
uniformly dominated
by a metric
F
on
\Omega
if

•

F
is complete (i.e.
d_{\Omega,F}
is Cauchy complete),

•

For each
r>0
, there is
\lambda(r)>0
such that

(2.8)

B_{\Omega,F}(x;r)-x\subset\lambda(r)I_{\Omega,F}(x)

for any
x\in\Omega
, where
B_{\Omega,F}(x;r)-x=\{y-x:y\in B_{\Omega,F}(x;r)\}
, the Euclidean translation.

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
\Omega
be a domain and
p\in\Omega
fixed. Assume that
\Omega
is uniformly dominated by an invariant metric
F
. Then

\mathcal{S}=\{\tau_{\varphi}:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{n}:\varphi\in\mathrm{Aut}(\Omega)\}

is a normal family, where

\tau_{\varphi}=[d\varphi(p)]^{-1}\circ(\varphi(\cdot)-\varphi(p))
,

Proof.

For any
r>0
and
\varphi\in\mathrm{Aut}(\Omega)
,

\displaystyle\tau_{\varphi}(B_{\Omega,F}(p;r))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle[d\varphi(p)]^{-1}(B_{\Omega,F}(\varphi(p);r)-\varphi(p))

\displaystyle\subset

\displaystyle\lambda(r)[d\varphi(p)]^{-1}(I_{\Omega,F}(\varphi(p)))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda(r)I_{\Omega,F}(p)

by (
2.8
), since

is invariant. Therefore, the family
\mathcal{S}
is uniformly bounded on
B_{\Omega,F}(p;r)
. Since

is complete, this implies that
\mathcal{S}
is uniformly bounded on each compact subset of

. The Montel theorem yields the conclusion.
∎

Example 2.6
.

Let
\mathcal{H}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\mathrm{Im}\,z>0\}
the upper half plane. Let

be the Poincaré metric on

which coincides with the Kobayashi-Royden metric

F(z;v)=\frac{|v|}{2\mathrm{Im}\,z},\quad d_{\mathcal{H},F}(z_{1},z_{2})=2\tanh^{-1}\left|\frac{z_{2}-z_{1}}{z_{2}-\bar{z}_{1}}\right|.

We will show (
2.8
) for a suitable
\lambda
at every
x=a+\sqrt{-1}b.
By the translation invariance of

, we assume without loss of generality

. Therefore, the indicatrix

is the disc

Remark 2.7
.

It may be necessary to point out that the uniform domination condition of a metric is not holomorphic invariant. More precisely, even if
\Omega
is uniformly dominated by a metric
F
and
\varphi:\widehat{\Omega}\rightarrow\Omega
is a biholomorphic map,
\widehat{\Omega}
is not necessarily uniformly dominated by
\varphi^{*}F
. The reason is that the definition of the uniform domination depends on a Euclidean translation of sets, which is not preserved by biholomorphic maps in general. Therefore, the uniform domination condition is just invariant under affine transforms. This is not an unexpected result, since the convergence of Frankel’s scaling sequence is not a holomorphic invariant property, either. It is also an affine invariant one.

It would be an interesting question to ask about conditions of invariant metrics and domains which imply the uniform domination. The following theorem is an answer to this question in case of the Kobayshi-Royden metric. Together with Proposition
2.5
, it provides a geometric proof of the convergence of Frankel’s scaling sequence on convex domains.

In order to simplify symbols, we denote the indicatrix and the distance ball by
I_{\Omega}(x)
and
B_{\Omega}(x;r)
, respectively, if the metric is the Kobayashi-Royden metric.

Theorem 2.8
.

Let
\Omega
be a hyperbolic convex domain in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
. Then
\Omega
is uniformly dominated by the Kobayashi-Royden metric
K_{\Omega}
. Indeed,

B_{\Omega}(x;r)-x\subset 2\lambda_{\mathcal{H}}(r)I_{\Omega}(x)

for each
r>0
and
x\in\Omega
, where
\lambda_{\mathcal{H}}
is the function defined as (
2.10
)

Proof.

Let
x\in\Omega
be a point and
v\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
be arbitrary nonzero vector. We denote by
l_{v}
the complex line defined by

l_{v}=\{\zeta v:\zeta\in\mathbb{C}\}

and
l_{v}+x=\{x+\zeta v:\zeta\in\mathbb{C}\}
by
l_{x,v}
. Let
\Omega_{x,v}=\Omega\cap l_{x,v}
. Since
\Omega
is convex and hyperbolic,
\Omega_{x,v}
is also a convex hyperbolic planar domain. Let
p=p(x,v)
be a point on
\partial\Omega_{x,v}
such that

d:=|x-p|=\mathrm{dist}(x,\partial\Omega_{x,v})

where
\mathrm{dist}
represents the Euclidean distance. Then since
\Omega_{x,v}
contains the disc centered at
x
of radius
d
,

(2.11)

K_{\Omega}(x;v)\leq K_{\Omega_{x,v}}(x;v)\leq\frac{|v|}{d}=2K_{\mathcal{H}}(\sqrt{-1}d;v).

By a unitary change which map
(x-p)/|x-p|
to
e_{1}
and the translation
z\rightarrow z-p
, we may first assume that

•

p
is the origin,

•

l_{x,v}
is the
z_{1}
-plane,

•

\Omega_{x,v}
lies on the upper half plane in
z_{1}
-plane and the real axis is the supporting line of
\Omega_{x,v}
,

•

x=\sqrt{-1}d
.

Applying a linear transform which leaves
z_{1}
-plane invariant, we can also assume that

•

the supporting hyperplane of
\Omega
at
p=O
is
\Pi=\{\mathrm{Im}\,z_{1}=0\}
and as a result
\Omega\subset{\mathcal{H}}\times\mathbb{C}^{n-1}
.

It can be achieved by the linear map

e_{1}\rightarrow e_{1},\,\,v_{2}\rightarrow e_{2},\ldots,v_{n}\rightarrow e_{n},

where
v_{2},\ldots,v_{n}
are linearly independent complex vectors which are tangent to
\Pi\cap\sqrt{-1}\Pi
. Recall that the Kobayashi distance decreases under holomorphic map, and if
p_{1},p_{2}\in X
and
q_{1},q_{2}\in Y
for two complex manifolds
X
and
Y
, then

d_{X\times Y}((p_{1},q_{1}),(p_{2},q_{2}))=\max\{d_{X}(p_{1},p_{2}),d_{Y}(q_{1},q_{2})\}.

Therefore, if
y\in\Omega_{x,v}\subset\mathcal{H}\times\{O\}
, then

(2.12)

d_{\Omega}(x,y)\geq d_{\mathcal{H}\times\mathbb{C}^{n-1}}(x,y)=d_{\mathcal{H}}(x,y),

by identiying
\mathcal{H}\times\{O\}
with
\mathcal{H}
. Combining (
2.11
) and (
2.12
), we conclude that

(B_{\Omega}(x;r)-x)\cap l_{v}\subset 2\lambda_{\mathcal{H}}(I_{\Omega}(x)\cap l_{v}).

The conclusion follows since
x\in\Omega
and
v\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
are arbitrary.
∎

3.
Maximal circularity function

Let
\Omega
be a bounded domain in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
. For
x\in\Omega
, let

\mathcal{B}_{\Omega}(x)
be the class of bounded holomorphic embeddings
\varphi:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{n}
such that
\varphi(x)=0
. Let

(3.1)

\displaystyle c_{\Omega}(x)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\sup\left\{\frac{r}{R}:\exists\varphi\in\mathcal{B}_{\Omega}(x),R>0,r>0\mbox{ such that }\right.

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt\left.rI_{\varphi(\Omega)}(0)\subset\varphi(\Omega)\subset RI_{\varphi(\Omega)}(0)\right\},

where
I_{\Omega}(x)
represents the Kobayashi indicatrix of
\Omega
at
x\in\Omega
. Obiously,
0<c_{\Omega}(x)\leq 1
, and if
\Omega
is a bounded complete circular domain, then
\Omega
is same with its Kobayashi indicatrix at the origin (
[
1
]
) and hence
c_{\Omega}(0)=1
. With ths reason, we call
c_{\Omega}
the
maximal circularity function
or simply the
maximal circularity
of
\Omega
. The first observation is that the converse is also true.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
\Omega
be a bounded domain in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
. Suppose that
c_{\Omega}(x)=1
at a point
x\in\Omega
. Then
\Omega
is biholomorphic to a complete circular domain.

Proof.

Suppose
\varphi_{j}\in\mathcal{B}_{\Omega}(x)
such that

(3.2)

r_{j}I_{j}\subset\varphi_{j}(\Omega)\subset R_{j}I_{j}

for some
R_{j}\geq r_{j}>0
and
r_{j}/R_{j}\rightarrow 1
as
j\rightarrow\infty
, where

I_{j}:=I_{\varphi_{j}(\Omega)}(0)=d\varphi_{j}(x)(I_{\Omega}(x)).

Let

\psi_{j}=[d\varphi_{j}(x)]^{-1}\circ\varphi_{j}.

Then

\psi_{j}\in\mathcal{B}_{\Omega}(x)
, and acting
[d\varphi_{j}(x)]^{-1}
to (
3.2
), we have

r_{j}I_{\Omega}(x)\subset\psi_{j}(\Omega)\subset R_{j}I_{\Omega}(x).

Notice that
R_{j}<R
for some
R>0
. Otherwise, we may assume
R_{j}\rightarrow\infty
as
j\rightarrow\infty
. Then
r_{j}\rightarrow\infty
as well. Let
\epsilon>0
be chosen small enough that
B_{\epsilon}\subset I_{\Omega}(x)
, where
B_{\epsilon}
is the Euclidean
\epsilon
-ball centered at the origin. Let
v\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
be a nonzero vector. Then since
d\psi_{j}(x)=Id
and
\Omega
is hyperbolic,

0<K_{\Omega}(x;v)=K_{\psi_{j}(\Omega)}(0;v)\leq K_{r_{j}B_{\epsilon}}(0;v)=\frac{|v|}{r_{j}\epsilon}\rightarrow 0

as
j\rightarrow\infty
. This is a contradiction. Choosing a subsequence, we may assume that
\psi_{j}
is convergent by Montel’s theorem and
r_{j}\rightarrow R_{0}
,
R_{j}\rightarrow R_{0}
for some
R_{0}\geq 0
. Since
d\psi_{j}(x)=Id
, the limit
\psi
of the sequence
\psi_{j}
is also a 1-1 holomorphic mapping and
\psi(\Omega)=R_{0}I_{\Omega}(x)
. Therefore,
R_{0}>0
and
\psi:\Omega\rightarrow R_{0}I_{\Omega}(x)
is a biholomorphic mapping.
∎

Notice that the defintion of
c_{\Omega}
is similar to that of the squeezing function defined in
[
2
]
(cf.
[
3
,
9
,
15
]
) which is motivated by
[
17
,
18
,
20
]
. The defintion of the squeezing function can be generalized to the case of more arbitrary circular models as follows (see also
[
6
]
). Let
D
be a bounded complete circular domain in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
. Then the
squeezing function

s^{D}_{\Omega}(x)
of
\Omega
at
x\in\Omega
modelled on
D
is defined by

(3.3)

\displaystyle s_{\Omega}^{D}(x)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\sup\left\{\frac{r}{R}:\exists\varphi\in\mathcal{B}_{\Omega}(x),R>0,r>0\mbox{ such that }\right.

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt\left.rD\subset\varphi(\Omega)\subset RD\right\}.

Thanks to the similarity of definitions (
3.1
) and (
3.3
), the following natural comparison between
c_{\Omega}
and
s^{D}_{\Omega}
can be verified.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
\Omega
be a bounded domain in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
and
x\in\Omega
. Then for any bounded complete circular domain
D\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
,

c_{\Omega}(x)\geq\left(s^{D}_{\Omega}(x)\right)^{2}.

Proof.

Let
\varphi\in\mathcal{B}_{\Omega}(x)
such that
\varphi(\Omega)\subset D.
and
r>0
be chosen that
rD\subset\varphi(\Omega).
Since
D
and
rD
are completely circular, Barth’s theorem (
[
1
]
) implies that

I_{D}(0)=D,\quad I_{rD}(0)=rD.

Then we have

rD=I_{rD}(0)\subset I_{\varphi(\Omega)}(0)\subset I_{D}(0)=D

from the decreasing property of the Kobayashi metric. Therefore,

rI_{\varphi(\Omega)}(0)\subset rD\subset\varphi(\Omega)\subset D\subset\frac{1}{r}I_{\varphi(\Omega)}(0).

This implies
c_{\Omega}(x)\geq r^{2}.
Taking the supremum for
r
, we get the conclusion.
∎

If
\Omega
is strongly pseudoconvex, then it turned out that the squeezing function
s_{\Omega}(x)=s^{B^{n}}_{\Omega}(x)
modelled on the unit ball tends to
1
as
x
goes to a boundary point (
[
4
,
15
]
). Therefore, we have immediately the following theorem as a corollary.

Theorem 3.3
.

If
\Omega
is a bounded domain in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
with
C^{2}
-smooth strongly pseudoconvex boundary, then
c_{\Omega}(x)\rightarrow 1
as
x\rightarrow p\in\partial\Omega
.

Another example of domains is
polyhedral domains
. Recall that a bounded domain
\Omega
in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
is called a polyhedral domain if there are smooth real-valued functions
\rho_{1},\ldots,\rho_{N}
defined on a neighborhood of
\overline{\Omega}
such that

•

\Omega=\{z\in\mathbb{C}^{n}:\rho_{1}(z)<0,\ldots,\rho_{N}(z)<0\}
,

•

on
\partial_{\alpha}\Omega:=\{z\in\partial\Omega:\rho_{\alpha}(z)=0\}
for
\alpha=1,\ldots,N
,
\nabla\rho_{\alpha}
never vanishes,

•

each
\partial_{\alpha}\Omega
is Levi flat.

\Omega
is said to be
normal
if it satisfies in addition

•

on each
\partial_{\alpha_{1}\cdots\alpha_{k}}\Omega:=\{z\in\partial\Omega:\rho_{\alpha_{1}}(z)=\cdots=\rho_{\alpha_{k}}(z)=0\}
,
\nabla\rho_{\alpha_{1}}
,…,
\nabla\rho_{\alpha_{k}}
are linearly independent over
\mathbb{C}
.

Imitating the idea of
[
10
,
11
]
about the scaling of convex normal polyhedral domains, we can prove the following.

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
\Omega=\{z\in\mathbb{C}^{n}:\rho_{1}(z)<0,\ldots,\rho_{N}(z)<0\}
be a convex normal polyhedral domain in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
. Let

q\in\partial_{\alpha_{1}\cdots\alpha_{n}}\Omega
for some distinct indices
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}.
Then
c_{\Omega}(x)\rightarrow 1
as
x\rightarrow q
.

Proof.

Reordering indices, we may assume that
\{\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}\}=\{1,\ldots,n\}
without loss of generality. We can also assume that
|\nabla\rho_{\alpha}|\equiv 1
on
\partial_{j}\Omega
for each
\alpha=1,\ldots,N
. By Proposition
3.2
, we only need to prove that

\sigma(x):=s^{\Delta^{n}}_{\Omega}(x)\rightarrow 1\quad\mbox{as $x\rightarrow q$},

where
\Delta^{n}
represents the unit polydisc in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
.
Notice that
\partial_{\alpha}\Omega
is foliated by analytic varieties since it is Levi flat, and each of them is indeed a part of affine hyperplane because
\Omega
is convex. Therefore, there are
n
hyperplanes
H_{1},\ldots,H_{n}
such that

•

H_{1}\cap\cdots\cap H_{n}=\{q\}
,

•

V_{\alpha}:=H_{\alpha}\cap\partial\Omega
is the maximal subvariety in
\partial_{j}\Omega
containing
q
.

Taking a linear affine change of coordinates, we assume that

•

q=O=(0,\ldots,0)
the origin,

•

H_{\alpha}=\{z_{\alpha}=0\}
,

•

\{\mathrm{Re}\,z_{\alpha}=0\}
is a supporting (real) hyperplanes of
\Omega
for
\alpha=1,\ldots,n
,

•

\Omega\subset\{\mathrm{Re}\,z_{1}<0,\ldots\mathrm{Re}\,z_{n}<0\}=\mathcal{H}^{n}
,

where
\mathcal{H}=\{\zeta\in\mathbb{C}:\mathrm{Re}\,\zeta<0\}
, the left half plane in
\mathbb{C}
. Let us suppose that
x\in\Omega
is sufficiently close to
O
. Then among all affine hyperplanes which are tangent to
\partial_{\alpha}\Omega
, there is a unique
h_{\alpha}=h_{\alpha}(x)
which is closest to
x
,
\alpha=1,\ldots,n
. Let
p=p(x)\in\partial_{12\cdots n}\Omega
be the intersection point of those hyperplanes, that is,

h_{1}\cap\cdots\cap h_{n}=\{p\}.

Let

v_{\alpha}=v_{\alpha}(x)=\nabla\rho_{\alpha}(p),

and let
A_{x}
be the affine transform defined by

•

A_{x}(p)=0
,

•

dA_{x}(v_{\alpha})=e_{\alpha}
for
\alpha=1,\ldots,n
.

It is obvious that
A_{x}\rightarrow Id
locally uniformly on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
as
x\rightarrow O
, since
p\rightarrow O
and
v_{\alpha}\rightarrow e_{\alpha}
as
x\rightarrow O
by the construction. Notice also that
A_{x}(\Omega)\subset\mathcal{H}^{n}
and
A_{x}(x)=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
where
x_{\alpha}<0
and
x_{\alpha}\rightarrow 0
as
x\rightarrow O
for all
\alpha=1,\ldots,n
. Let
\Phi:\mathcal{H}^{n}\rightarrow\Delta^{n}
be the Cayley transform such that
\Phi(O)=(1,\ldots,1)
and
\Phi(-1,\ldots,-1)=O=(0,\ldots,0)
. Then
\Phi(A_{x}(x))=(1-r_{1},\ldots,1-r_{n})
for some
r_{\alpha}>0
which tends to
0
as
x\rightarrow O
. The normality condition implies that
\partial_{1\cdots n}\Omega
is a smooth real
n
-dimensional manifold and for each
\alpha=1,\ldots,n
,
\pi_{\alpha}(\Phi(A_{x}(\Omega)))
has a smooth boundary near
1
, where
\pi_{\alpha}
denotes the natural projection
\mathbb{C}^{n}\ni(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n})\rightarrow z_{\alpha}\in\mathbb{C}
. Therefore, there is
\epsilon>0
such that
\Delta_{\epsilon}^{n}\subset\Phi(A_{x}(\Omega))
for all
x\in\Omega
which is sufficiently close to
O
, where
\Delta_{\epsilon}
represents the disc in
\mathbb{C}
centered at
1-\epsilon
of radius
\epsilon
. Let
\Psi_{x}\in\mathrm{Aut}(\Delta^{n})
such that

\Psi_{x}(1,\ldots,1)=(1,\ldots,1)\quad\mbox{and}\quad\Psi_{x}(1-r_{1},\ldots,1-r_{n})=(0,\ldots,0)

.
For each
0<r<1
, then

r\Delta^{n}\subset\Psi_{x}(\Delta_{\epsilon}^{n})\subset\Psi_{x}\circ\Phi\circ A_{x}(\Omega)

for any
x\in\Omega
which is suficiently close to
O
. Therefore,

\liminf_{\Omega\ni x\rightarrow O}s^{\Delta^{n}}_{\Omega}(x)\geq r

for each
0<r<1
, and this yields the conclusion.
∎
