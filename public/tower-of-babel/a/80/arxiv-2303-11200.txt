Parent Hamiltonian reconstruction via inverse quantum annealing Preprint: APS/123-QED
Davide Rattacaso, Gianluca Passarelli, Angelo Russomanno, Procolo Lucignano, Giuseppe E. Santoro, Rosario Fazio

Abstract
Finding a local Hamiltonian ℋ ^ \hat{\mathcal{H}} having a given many-body wavefunction | ψ ⟩ |\psi\rangle as its ground state, i.e. a parent Hamiltonian , is a challenge of fundamental importance in quantum technologies. Here we introduce a numerical method, inspired by quantum annealing, that efficiently performs this task through an artificial inverse dynamics: a slow deformation of the states | ψ ⁡ ( λ ⁡ ( t ) ) ⟩ |\psi(\lambda(t))\rangle , starting from a simple state | ψ 0 ⟩ |\psi_{0}\rangle with a known ℋ ^ 0 \hat{\mathcal{H}}_{0} , generates an adiabatic evolution of the corresponding Hamiltonian. We name this approach inverse quantum annealing . The method, implemented through a projection onto a set of local operators, only requires the knowledge of local expectation values, and, for long annealing times, leads to an approximate parent Hamiltonian whose degree of locality depends on the correlations built up by the states | ψ ⁡ ( λ ) ⟩ |\psi(\lambda)\rangle . We illustrate the method on two paradigmatic models: the Kitaev fermionic chain and a quantum Ising chain in longitudinal and transverse fields.

;

Here we introduce a method for obtaining a PH, referred to as
inverse quantum annealing
(IQA), which is inspired by
quantum annealing
Kadowaki and Nishimori 1998
;
Farhi et al. 2001
;
Santoro et al. 2002
;
Hauke et al. 2020
;
Albash and Lidar 2018
,
but with the role of states and Hamiltonians swapped.
Given a state
|\psi_{1}\rangle
, whose PH
\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{1}
we wish to construct, and starting from a simple state

|\psi_{0}\rangle
with a well-known PH
\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{0}
, we construct a path
|\psi(\lambda(t))\rangle
, connecting

|\psi_{0}\rangle
to
|\psi_{1}\rangle
. We then write down an artificial dynamics for the Hamiltonian, inspired by von Neumann’s equation for the density matrix, which is amenable of a well-defined adiabatic limit, and, more importantly, can be approximately solved with a space of local Hamiltonians. In the adiabatic limit we obtain a Hamiltonian having the target state as the ground state.
We will illustrate the main ideas of the method with two paradigmatic examples:
1) the “exactly solvable” Kitaev fermionic chain, where
|\psi(\lambda)\rangle
crosses a second-order transition point, and
2) a quantum Ising chain in the presence of a longitudinal field, where
|\psi(\lambda)\rangle
crosses a first-order transition point.
In case 1) power-law correlations emerge, making the local approximation harder,
while in case 2) correlations are exponentially decreasing and the local approximation is excellent.

Inverse quantum annealing protocol.—

Given a (many-body) state
|\psi_{1}\rangle
, the task is to find a (local) Hamiltonian
\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{1}

for which
|\psi_{1}\rangle
is the ground state.
In many cases, this problem has more than one solution

1

1

For instance, the standard state
|\psi_{0}\rangle=|+\rangle^{\otimes N}
has a non-local parent Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{\mathrm{aux}}=-|\psi_{0}\rangle\langle\psi_{0}|
, but also a quite simple 1-local PH
\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{0}=-\sum_{j}\hat{\sigma}^{x}_{j}
.
.
To find a solution, we introduce a method inspired by Quantum Annealing
Kadowaki and Nishimori 1998
;
Farhi et al. 2001
;
Santoro et al. 2002
,

alias
Adiabatic Quantum Computation
Albash and Lidar 2018
.
The first step is to introduce a family of states
|\psi(\lambda)\rangle
, depending on a parameter
\lambda
, with
0\leq\lambda\leq 1
, such that:
i)
|\psi(1)\rangle\equiv|\psi_{1}\rangle
is the quantum state whose PH
\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{1}
we wish to determine, and ii)
|\psi(0)\rangle\equiv|\psi_{0}\rangle
is a simple initial state whose PH
\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{0}
is known.
Then we seek a dynamics that, by changing
\lambda(t)
with time, in the adiabatic limit
\dot{\lambda}(t)\to 0
, leads to the desired
\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{1}
, starting from
\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{0}
.

The idea behind this artificial Hamiltonian dynamics is the following.
Consider the projector
\hat{\Pi}_{\psi(\lambda)}
on the selected state path
|\psi(\lambda)\rangle
, suitably redefined as follows:

\hat{\Pi}_{\psi(\lambda)}=-J|\psi(\lambda)\rangle\langle\psi(\lambda)|\;,

(1)

where
J
is an arbitrary energy scale which we use as our unit, setting
J=1
.

\hat{\Pi}_{\psi(\lambda)}
has
|\psi(\lambda)\rangle
as its unique ground state, at energy
-J
, while all other states are degenerate, at energy
0
.

Regard now
\hat{\Pi}_{\psi(\lambda(t))}
as a
(pseudo-)Hamiltonian
— in general, non-local — generating a Schrödinger dynamics associated to the evolution operator

\hat{U}(t)=\mathrm{T}\!\!-\!\!\exp\Big(-\textstyle{\frac{i}{\hbar}\int_{0}^{t}\!\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}}\;\hat{\Pi}_{\psi(\lambda(t^{\prime}))}\Big)\;.

(2)

By assumption a local PH
\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{0}
for
|\psi_{0}\rangle
exists.
Consider now an “auxiliary Hamiltonian”
\hat{H}_{\mathrm{aux}}(t)=\hat{U}(t)\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{0}\hat{U}^{\dagger}(t)
.
It will satisfy von Neumann’s equation:

\partial_{t}\hat{H}_{\mathrm{aux}}(t)=-\frac{i}{\hbar}\big[\hat{\Pi}_{\psi(\lambda(t))},\hat{H}_{\mathrm{aux}}(t)\big]\;,

(3)

with the boundary condition
\hat{H}_{\mathrm{aux}}(0)=\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{0}
.
The presence of the spectral gap
J
in
\hat{\Pi}_{\psi(\lambda)}
guarantees that,
in the adiabatic limit
\dot{\lambda}\to 0
, the time-evolved state
\hat{U}(t)|\psi_{0}\rangle
will be closer and closer to the desired path of states
|\psi(\lambda(t))\rangle
, and, correspondingly, the “Hamiltonian”
\hat{H}_{\mathrm{aux}}(t)
will approximate a PH
\hat{\mathcal{H}}(\lambda(t))
.
The non-trivial issue with such an adiabatically-inspired solution for the PH problem is the possible
non-locality

of the Hamiltonian determined.
We need to devise a further
local approximation
for the PH problem to guarantee that the solution found is actually a physical
local
PH.

Before tackling the locality issue, let us rewrite our equation using a fixed basis of Hermitian operators
\mathcal{P}=\{\hat{P}_{\mathrm{j}}\}
acting on a system of
N
particles.
As an example, think of a system made of
N
spin-1/2, where
\hat{P}_{\mathrm{j}}
are all possible Pauli string operators made by an arbitrary number of Pauli matrices
\hat{\sigma}_{i}^{x,y,z}

at sites
i
.
Without loss of generality, we can assume that the normalization of the operators is such that

\tr(\hat{P}_{\mathrm{j}}\hat{P}_{\mathrm{j}^{\prime}})=\delta_{\mathrm{j},\mathrm{j}^{\prime}}

sup
.
For any finite
N
, the total number of elements in
\mathcal{P}
is finite,
\mathcal{N}=\big|\mathcal{P}\big|
.
Any arbitrary Hermitian operator can be expanded in the basis
\mathcal{P}
,
for instance,
\hat{H}_{\mathrm{aux}}(t)=\sum_{\mathrm{j}}h_{j}(t)\,\hat{P}_{\mathrm{j}}
.
By substituting in Eq. (
3
), after simple algebra, see
sup
, we
can rewrite
(
3
) as

\partial_{t}h_{\rm j}(t)=\sum_{\mathrm{j}^{\prime}=1}^{\mathcal{N}}K_{\mathrm{j},\mathrm{j}^{\prime}}[\psi(\lambda(t))]\,h_{\mathrm{j}^{\prime}}(t)\;,

(4)

where

K_{\mathrm{j},\mathrm{j}^{\prime}}[\psi]\equiv-i(J/\hbar)\langle\psi|\big[\hat{P}_{\mathrm{j}},\hat{P}_{\mathrm{j}^{\prime}}\big]|\psi\rangle

is a skew-symmetric
commutator matrix
.

The space of
l
-local Hamiltonians is formed by linear combinations of a subset

\mathcal{L}^{(l)}\subset\mathcal{P}
consisting of all Hermitian operators connecting particles within maximum distance
l
on a lattice, whose number we denote as
\mathcal{N}_{l}
. (Let us call the maximum coupling distance of an operator as its coupling length.)
As an example, a
1
-local Hamiltonian contains only single-particle terms, while
a
2
-local Hamiltonian will also contain two adjacent particles interactions. In both cases,
\mathcal{N}_{l}
scales linearly in
N
.
Any
l
-local Hamiltonian is written as
\hat{\mathcal{H}}^{(l)}=\sum_{\mathrm{j}}h_{\mathrm{j}}\hat{L}_{\mathrm{j}}^{(l)}
.
We call a Hamiltonian
local
if its locality range
l
does not depend
on the system size
N
.

To find an optimal
l
-local PH from the adiabatic solution of Eq. (
3
),
we now use a time-dependent variational principle (TDVP)
Haegeman et al. 2011
,
which allows us to determine the Hamiltonian couplings
h_{\mathrm{j}}(t)
, by projecting the right-hand side of Eq. (
3
) on the space
\mathcal{L}^{(l)}=\{\hat{L}_{\mathrm{j}}^{(l)}\}
, through the Hilbert-Schmidt distance
d(\hat{A},\hat{B})=\sqrt{\tr(\hat{A}-\hat{B})^{2}}
, a natural Euclidean structure in the space of Hermitian operators.
As detailed in
sup
, we can write the resulting projected evolution as:

\partial_{t}\hat{\mathcal{H}}^{(l)}={\mathds{P}}_{l}\Big(-\frac{i}{\hbar}\big[\hat{\Pi}_{\psi(\lambda(t))},\hat{\mathcal{H}}^{(l)}(t)\big]\Big)\;,

(5)

where the projector
{\mathds{P}}_{l}(\hat{A})
defines the closest
l
-local operator
\hat{B}
to a given
\hat{A}
.
This leads to the following equation for the coefficients
h_{\mathrm{j}}(t)
of the
l
-local Hamiltonian
\hat{\mathcal{H}}^{(l)}(t)
:

\partial_{t}h_{\mathrm{j}}(t)=\sum_{\mathrm{j}^{\prime}=1}^{\mathcal{N}_{l}}K_{\mathrm{j},\mathrm{j}^{\prime}}^{(l)}[\psi(\lambda(t))]\,h_{\mathrm{j}^{\prime}}(t)\;,

(6)

where
K_{\mathrm{j},\mathrm{j}^{\prime}}^{(l)}[\psi]\equiv-i(J/\hbar)\langle\psi|\big[\hat{L}_{\mathrm{j}}^{(l)},\hat{L}_{\mathrm{j}^{\prime}}^{(l)}\big]|\psi\rangle
has a size that scales polynomially with the system size
N
. Let us note that Eq. (
6
) is a truncated version of Eq. (
4
), with the commutator matrix restricted to the space of
l
-local operators.

Equation (
6
) is the central result of this work.
In the adiabatic regime, it allows us to construct an
l
-local PH
\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{1}

for the final state
|\psi_{1}\rangle
, by integrating the differential equations from
t=0
, with initial condition set by the expansion coefficients
h_{\mathrm{j}}(0)
of
\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{0}
, up to a suitably large annealing time
T
.
While the non-projected adiabatic scheme behind Eqs. (
1
)-(
3
) is, by construction, protected by a gap
J
,
there is no guarantee that the projection on the space of
l
-local Hamiltonians will not bring in components of higher states.

The error introduced by the
l
-local approximation is inherently related to the nature of the
path of states
|\psi(\lambda)\rangle
.
Our physical expectation is that if
|\psi(\lambda)\rangle
has a finite correlation length, then the adiabatic solution of Eq. (
3
) is a local Hamiltonian.
In this case the matrix
K_{\mathrm{j},\mathrm{j}^{\prime}}^{(l)}
connects operators with similar coupling length, and the truncation leading from Eq. (
4
) to Eq. (
6
) gives rise to a small error (akin to truncating a short-range Hamiltonian — we discuss this point in
sup
).
In this case, we can truncate to a coupling length
l
, independent of the system size, such that the
error introduced by the TDVP projection is arbitrarily small. The opposite occurs if there is a
\lambda_{c}
where the correlation length of
|\psi(\lambda)\rangle
diverges:
K_{\mathrm{j},\mathrm{j}^{\prime}}^{(l)}
connects operators with very different coupling lengths, and the truncation gives rise to a large error.

The commutator matrix
K_{\mathrm{j},\mathrm{j}^{\prime}}^{(l)}
has been used in previous works to reconstruct local Hamiltonians from their eigenstates
Bairey et al. 2019
.
Previous methods, however, could not guarantee to find Hamiltonians having
|\psi(\lambda)\rangle
as the
ground state
.
The IQA, implemented through Eq. (
6
), can select a PH, without the need of an explicit diagonalization for checking the solution.

We now illustrate our method on two paradigmatic examples:
1) the integrable quantum Ising chain in a transverse field, where we will exploit the Jordan-Wigner mapping to
to the 1D Kitaev chain,
illustrating the case of a diverging correlation length for
|\psi(\lambda)\rangle
, at a second-order transition;
2) the non-integrable quantum Ising chain in a transverse and longitudinal field, where the path
|\psi(\lambda)\rangle
crosses a first-order transition, with a finite correlation length.

F(\lambda)
between the target state and the ground state of the IQA Hamiltonian, as a function of the control parameter
\lambda
for different annealing times. We see that before the phase transition at
\lambda_{c}
, a large fidelity is reached also for short annealing times.
Near
\lambda_{c}
the fidelity drops, but, differently from the second order transition case, we observe a large recovery after
\lambda_{c}
.

In Fig.

1-F(\lambda)

the infidelity decreases polynomially with the annealing time and becomes independent on the system size. Remarkably, a small interaction range

Discussion and conclusions —
Quantum annealing represents one of the major examples of the computational potential of quantum many-body systems. In this work, exploiting a combination of adiabatic approximation and TDVP, we introduced an annealing technique for approximating the
l
-local parent Hamiltonian of a target many-body state. We exemplified our method by reconstructing PHs for the Kitaev fermionic chain and a quantum Ising chain in longitudinal and transverse fields. The IQA allows for efficient reconstruction of PHs for paths of many-body states with finite correlation lengths, independently of the integrability of the model or the emergence of a first-order phase transition. The next step is to use IQA to design Hamiltonians that would allow for the experimental preparation of quantum states relevant in many-body physics and quantum information, directly from their wavefunctions. Remarkable examples include quantum spin liquid variational wavefunctions
Zhou et al. 2017
.
Finally, IQA only relies on the knowledge of local expectation values. This can be relevant for applications to the quantum marginal problem
Xin et al. 2019
;
Linden and Wootters 2002
;
Klyachko 2006
;
Eisert et al. 2008
;
Linden et al. 2002
;
Wyderka et al. 2017
;
Yu et al. 2021
.

A.1
The
l
-local projection and the time-dependent variational principle

Let us consider basis of Hermitian operators
\mathcal{P}=\{\hat{P}_{\mathrm{j}}\}
acting on a system of
N
particles. We choose the elements in
\mathcal{P}
end their normalization such that

\tr[\hat{P}_{\mathrm{j}}\hat{P}_{\mathrm{j}^{\prime}}]=\delta_{\mathrm{j},\mathrm{j}^{\prime}}\;,

(S1)

As an example, for a system made of
N
spin-1/2, we can define the
\hat{P}_{\mathrm{j}}
as the set of all possible Pauli string operators
\hat{P}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^{N}}}\,\hat{\sigma}^{\alpha_{1}}_{1}\,\hat{\sigma}^{\alpha_{2}}_{2}\,\cdots\,\hat{\sigma}^{\alpha_{n}}_{n}
where
\alpha_{j}=x,y,z
for the ordinary Pauli matrices
\hat{\sigma}_{j}^{x,y,z}
at site
j
, while
\alpha_{j}=0
denotes the identity,
\hat{\sigma}_{j}^{0}={\mathds{1}}_{j}
.

A
N
-particles operator on a lattice is termed
l
-local
if it acts differently that the identity only on particles within a maximum lattice distance
l
. For example, the first-neighbouring interaction
\hat{P}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^{N}}}\,\hat{\sigma}^{x}_{1}\,\hat{\sigma}^{y}_{2}\,{\mathds{1}}_{3}\,\cdots\,{\mathds{1}}_{3}
on a spin lattice is a
2
-local operator.

Any Hermitian operator
\hat{A}
can be expanded in terms of elements of
\mathcal{P}
.
Given a value of
l<n
, you could ask what is the
l
-local operator
\hat{B}\in\textrm{span}(\mathcal{L}^{(l)})
,
that is closest to
\hat{A}
according to some distance
d(\hat{A},\hat{B})
.
We consider here the trace distance
d(\hat{A},\hat{B})=\sqrt{\tr[(\hat{A}-\hat{B})^{2}]}
.
By conveniently organizing the indices, the expansion of
\hat{A}
in terms of a set of real coefficients

a_{\mathrm{j}}
can be equivalently expressed as:

\hat{A}=\sum_{\mathrm{j}=1}^{\mathcal{N}}a_{\mathrm{j}}\,\hat{P}_{\mathrm{j}}=\sum_{\mathrm{j}=1}^{\mathcal{N}_{l}}a_{\mathrm{j}}\,\hat{L}^{(l)}_{\mathrm{j}}+\sum_{\mathrm{j}>\mathcal{N}_{l}}^{\mathcal{N}}a_{\mathrm{j}}\,\hat{P}_{\mathrm{j}}\;.

(S2)

By expressing the desired
l
-local approximation
\hat{B}
as

\hat{B}=\sum_{\mathrm{j}=1}^{\mathcal{N}_{l}}b_{\mathrm{j}}\,\hat{L}_{\mathrm{j}}^{(l)}\;,

and imposing a vanishing gradient for
\tr[(\hat{A}-\hat{B})^{2}]
, we deduce that:

\displaystyle 0=\frac{\partial}{\partial b_{\mathrm{j}}}\tr[(\hat{A}-\hat{B})^{2}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial b_{\mathrm{j}}}\Big(\tr\big[\hat{A}^{2}\big]-2\sum_{\mathrm{j}=1}^{\mathcal{N}_{l}}b_{\mathrm{j}}\tr\big[\hat{A}\,\hat{L}^{(l)}_{\mathrm{j}}\big]+\sum_{\mathrm{j}=1}^{\mathcal{N}_{l}}\sum_{\mathrm{j}^{\prime}=1}^{\mathcal{N}_{l}}b_{\mathrm{j}}b_{\mathrm{j}^{\prime}}\tr\big[\hat{L}^{(l)}_{\mathrm{j}}\hat{L}^{(l)}_{\mathrm{j}^{\prime}}\big]\Big)

(S3)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\Big(\sum_{\mathrm{j}^{\prime}=1}^{\mathcal{N}_{l}}b_{\mathrm{j}^{\prime}}\tr\big[\hat{L}^{(l)}_{\mathrm{j}}\hat{L}^{(l)}_{\mathrm{j}^{\prime}}\big]-\tr\big[\hat{A}\,\hat{L}^{(l)}_{\mathrm{j}}\big]\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\Big(b_{\mathrm{j}}-\tr\big[\hat{A}\,\hat{L}^{(l)}_{\mathrm{j}}\big]\Big)\hskip 28.45274pt\Longleftrightarrow\hskip 28.45274ptb_{\mathrm{j}}=\tr\big[\hat{A}\,\hat{L}^{(l)}_{\mathrm{j}}\big]\equiv a_{\mathrm{j}}\;,

where we used the orthonormality condition (
S1
) for the operators in
\mathcal{L}^{(l)}
,
and the expansion of
\hat{A}
in Eq. (
S2
). We deduced a simple result: in words, the
l
-local operator
\hat{B}
which is closest in trace-distance to a given operator
\hat{A}
is simply obtained by setting to zero all the coefficients
a_{\mathrm{j}}
of Pauli string operators

\hat{P}_{\mathrm{j}}
not belonging to the desired
l
-local set
\mathcal{L}^{(l)}
,
while
b_{\mathrm{j}}=a_{\mathrm{j}}
for the
l
-local terms.
This is essentially, a
projection
on the space of
l
-local operators spanned by
\mathcal{L}^{(l)}
, which we
denote as

{\mathds{P}}_{l}(\hat{A})=\sum_{\mathrm{j}=1}^{\mathcal{N}_{l}}a_{\mathrm{j}}\,\hat{L}^{(l)}_{\mathrm{j}}\;.

(S4)

Now, take the “Hamiltonian”
\hat{H}_{\mathrm{aux}}(t)

considered in the main text.
We can project it on the space of
l
-local operators
\mathcal{L}^{(l)}
:

{\mathds{P}}_{l}\big(\hat{H}_{\mathrm{aux}}(t)\big)=\sum_{\mathrm{j}=1}^{\mathcal{N}_{l}}h_{\mathrm{j}}(t)\,\hat{L}^{(l)}_{\mathrm{j}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{def}}}{{=}}\hat{\mathcal{H}}^{(l)}(t)\;,

(S5)

with appropriate real coefficients
h_{\mathrm{j}}(t)
.
The dynamics imposed by the von Neumann’s equation associated to the projector
\hat{\Pi}_{\psi}

on the state
|\psi(\lambda)\rangle
, however, brings the
l
-local projected
\hat{\mathcal{H}}^{(l)}(t)

out
of the
desired space spanned by
\mathcal{L}^{(l)}
.
In a way that is reminiscent of the time-dependent variational principle (TDVP) idea
Haegeman et al. 2011
,
we can project again the result on the
l
-local space, obtaining an
l
-local projected dynamics of the
form:

\partial_{t}\hat{\mathcal{H}}^{(l)}(t)=-\frac{i}{\hbar}{\mathds{P}}_{l}\Big(\big[\hat{\Pi}_{\psi(\lambda(t))},\hat{\mathcal{H}}^{(l)}(t)\big]\Big)\;.

(S6)

To determine the differential equations for the coefficients
h_{\mathrm{j}}(t)
, let us project the commutator

-\frac{i}{\hbar}\big[\hat{\Pi}_{\psi(\lambda(t))},\hat{\mathcal{H}}^{(l)}(t)\big]=-\frac{i}{\hbar}\sum_{\mathrm{j}^{\prime}=1}^{\mathcal{N}_{l}}h_{\mathrm{j}^{\prime}}(t)\,\big[\hat{\Pi}_{\psi(\lambda(t))},\hat{L}^{(l)}_{\mathrm{j}^{\prime}}\big]

on the subspace spanned by
\mathcal{L}^{(l)}
, by multiplying by
\hat{L}^{(l)}_{\mathrm{j}}
and taking the trace:

\displaystyle-\frac{i}{\hbar}\sum_{\mathrm{j}^{\prime}=1}^{\mathcal{N}_{l}}h_{\mathrm{j}^{\prime}}(t)\,\tr\Big(\hat{L}^{(l)}_{\mathrm{j}}\big[\hat{\Pi}_{\psi(\lambda(t))},\hat{L}^{(l)}_{\mathrm{j}^{\prime}}\big]\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{i}{\hbar}\sum_{\mathrm{j}^{\prime}=1}^{\mathcal{N}_{l}}\tr\Big(\big[\hat{L}^{(l)}_{\mathrm{j}},\hat{L}^{(l)}_{\mathrm{j}^{\prime}}\big]\hat{\Pi}_{\psi(\lambda(t))}\Big)\,h_{\mathrm{j}^{\prime}}(t)

(S7)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{iJ}{\hbar}\sum_{\mathrm{j}^{\prime}=1}^{\mathcal{N}_{l}}\Big(\langle\psi(\lambda(t))|\big[\hat{L}^{(l)}_{\mathrm{j}},\hat{L}^{(l)}_{\mathrm{j}^{\prime}}\big]|\psi(\lambda(t))\rangle\Big)\,h_{\mathrm{j}^{\prime}}(t)\;.

Summarizing, Eq. (
S6
) translates into the following differential equations for the local Hamiltonian coefficients
h_{\mathrm{j}}(t)
:

\partial_{t}h_{\mathrm{j}}(t)=\sum_{\mathrm{j}^{\prime}=1}^{\mathcal{N}_{l}}K_{\mathrm{j},\mathrm{j}^{\prime}}^{(l)}[\psi(\lambda(t))]\,h_{\mathrm{j}^{\prime}}(t)\;,

(S8)

where the truncated
\mathcal{N}_{l}\times\mathcal{N}_{l}
commutator matrix is given by:

K_{\mathrm{j},\mathrm{j}^{\prime}}^{(l)}[\psi]=-\frac{iJ}{\hbar}\langle\psi|\big[\hat{L}^{(l)}_{\mathrm{j}},\hat{L}^{(l)}_{\mathrm{j}^{\prime}}\big]|\psi\rangle\;.

(S9)

When no
l
-local projection is performed, i.e., for
l=n
, then Eq. (
S8
) reduces
to Eq. (4) of the main text, and Eq. (
S9
) to the associated full commutator matrix.

A.2
IQA with fermionic Gaussian states: basis of quadratic fermionic Hamiltonians

We define the basis
\mathcal{L}^{(l)}
of a space of translation and reflection invariant interactions of range
l
as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{(l<N/2)}\equiv\{\Sigma_{0}^{z}/\sqrt{2},\Sigma_{1}^{x},\Sigma_{1}^{y},\Sigma_{1}^{z},\dots,\Sigma_{l}^{x},\Sigma_{l}^{y},\Sigma_{l}^{z}\},

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{(N/2)}\equiv\mathcal{L}^{(N/2-1)}\cup\{\Sigma_{N/2}^{x}/\sqrt{2},\Sigma_{N/2}^{y}/\sqrt{2}\},

where

\displaystyle\Sigma_{m}^{x}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{N}}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left(\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{j}\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{j+m}-\hat{c}^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{j}\hat{c}^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{j+m}\right)

\displaystyle\Sigma_{m}^{y}

\displaystyle=\frac{i}{2\sqrt{N}}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left(\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{j}\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{j+m}+\hat{c}^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{j}\hat{c}^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{j+m}\right)

\displaystyle\Sigma_{m}^{z}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{N}}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left(\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{j}\hat{c}^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{j+m}-\hat{c}^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{j}\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{j+m}\right)

(S10)

with
\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{j}
and
\hat{c}^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{j}
fermionic creation and annihilation operators at position
j
on the lattice, and antiperiodic boundary conditions
\hat{c}^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{N+m}\equiv-\hat{c}^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{m}
.

The operators in
\mathcal{L}^{(l)}
allow for a common block-diagonal representation
Mbeng et al. 2020
. As a first step in this direction, we introduce the fermionic operators
\hat{c}^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{k}
in reciprocal space:

\displaystyle\hat{c}^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{-i\pi/4}}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{j=1}^{N}e^{-ikj}\hat{c}^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{j}\;.

The antiperiodic boundary conditions
\hat{c}^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{N+m}=-\hat{c}^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{m}
imply that
\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}}e^{ik(N+m)}\hat{c}^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{k}=-\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}}e^{ikm}\hat{c}^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{k}
, hence

k\in\mathcal{K}\equiv\Big\{k=\pm\frac{(2n-1)\pi}{N},\text{ with }n\in\{1,\dots,\textstyle{\frac{N}{2}}\}\Big\}.

Now the operators in
\mathcal{L}^{(l)}
can be written as:

\displaystyle\Sigma_{m}^{x}

\displaystyle=-\frac{i}{2\sqrt{N}}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}}(e^{imk}\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{k}\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{-k}-e^{-imk}\hat{c}^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{-k}\hat{c}^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{k})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}}\sin(mk)(\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{k}\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{-k}+\hat{c}^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{-k}\hat{c}^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{k})

\displaystyle\Sigma_{m}^{y}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{N}}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}}(e^{imk}\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{k}\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{-k}+e^{-imk}\hat{c}^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{-k}\hat{c}^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{k})=\frac{i}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}}\sin(mk)(\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{k}\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{-k}-\hat{c}^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{-k}\hat{c}^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{k})

\displaystyle\Sigma_{m}^{z}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{N}}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}}e^{imk}(\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{k}\hat{c}^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{k}-\hat{c}^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{-k}\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{-k})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}}\cos(mk)(\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{k}\hat{c}^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{k}-\hat{c}^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{-k}\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{-k})\;,

(S11)

where we have used the canonical anti-commutation relations.

Finally, let us define the pseudospin operators

\tilde{\sigma}_{k}^{x}=(\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{k}\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{-k}+\hat{c}^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{-k}\hat{c}^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{k})
,
\tilde{\sigma}_{k}^{y}=-i(\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{k}\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{-k}-\hat{c}^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{-k}\hat{c}^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{k})
, and

\tilde{\sigma}_{k}^{z}=(\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{k}\hat{c}^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{k}-\hat{c}^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{-k}\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{-k})
. These operators form, within the subspace of empty and paired fermionic states, a representation of the spin algebra on the lattice of momenta, i. e.,

\{\tilde{\sigma}_{k}^{\mu},\tilde{\sigma}_{l}^{\nu}\}=2\mathds{1}\delta_{kl}\delta_{\mu\nu}

and
[\tilde{\sigma}_{k}^{\mu},\tilde{\sigma}_{l}^{\nu}]=2i\varepsilon_{\mu\nu\gamma}\delta_{kl}\tilde{\sigma}_{k}^{\gamma}
, where
\varepsilon_{\mu\nu\gamma}
is the Levi-Civita symbol.
In terms of pseudospins, the operators in
\mathcal{L}^{(l)}
read

\displaystyle\Sigma_{m}^{x}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}^{+}}\sin(mk)\,\tilde{\sigma}_{k}^{x}

\displaystyle\Sigma_{m}^{y}

\displaystyle=\frac{-2}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}^{+}}\sin(mk)\,\tilde{\sigma}_{k}^{y}

\displaystyle\Sigma_{m}^{z}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}^{+}}\cos(mk)\,\tilde{\sigma}_{k}^{z},

(S12)

where
\mathcal{K}^{+}\equiv\Big\{k=(2n-1)\pi/N,\text{ with }n\in\{1,\dots,N/2\}\Big\}
.

Now, we show that the operators in
\mathcal{L}^{(N/2)}
are orthogonal and that they have the same norm.
First, let us remark that
\tr(\tilde{\sigma}_{k}^{\mu}\tilde{\sigma}_{k^{\prime}}^{\nu})=\tr(\{\tilde{\sigma}_{k}^{\mu},\tilde{\sigma}_{k^{\prime}}^{\nu}\})/2=\tr(\mathds{1})\delta_{kk^{\prime}}\delta_{\mu\nu}
. It follows that the scalar products of the operators
\Sigma_{m}^{\mu}
are

\tr\left(\Sigma_{m}^{\mu}\Sigma_{l}^{\nu}\right)=\frac{4}{N}\sum_{k}\sum_{k^{\prime}}\tr(\tilde{\sigma}_{k}^{\mu}\tilde{\sigma}_{k^{\prime}}^{\nu})f^{\mu}(km)f^{\nu}(k^{\prime}l)=\frac{4}{N}\delta_{\mu\nu}\tr(\mathds{1})\sum_{k}f^{\mu}(km)f^{\mu}(kl),

(S13)

where
f^{\mu}(x)\equiv(\sin(x),-\sin(x),\cos(x))
. Considering the orthogonality of the Fourier basis, the latter equation becomes

\displaystyle\tr\left(\Sigma_{m}^{\mu}\Sigma_{l}^{\nu}\right)

\displaystyle=\delta_{\mu\nu}\delta_{ml}\frac{1}{2}\tr(\mathds{1})\qquad\text{for}\quad 0<m<N/2

\displaystyle\tr\left(\Sigma_{m}^{\mu}\Sigma_{l}^{\nu}\right)

\displaystyle=\delta_{\mu\nu}\delta_{ml}\tr(\mathds{1})\qquad\text{for}\quad m\in\{0,N/2\}\;.

(S14)

Therefore,

\mathcal{L}^{(N/2)}\equiv\{\Sigma_{0}^{z}/\sqrt{2},\Sigma_{1}^{x},\Sigma_{1}^{y},\Sigma_{1}^{z},\dots,\Sigma_{N/2-1}^{x},\Sigma_{N/2-1}^{y},\Sigma_{N/2-1}^{z},\Sigma_{N/2}^{x}/\sqrt{2},\Sigma_{N/2}^{y}/\sqrt{2}\},

(S15)

is a set of
3N/2
orthogonal vectors with the same norm.

A.3
IQA with fermionic Gaussian states: Hamiltonian diagonalization and commutator matrix

Here we calculate the commutator matrix

K^{(l)}_{\mathrm{j},\mathrm{j}^{\prime}}(\lambda)
, i.e., more explicitly

K^{(l)}_{\{n,\nu\}\{m,\mu\}}(\lambda)\equiv\langle\psi(\lambda)|-i[\Sigma_{m}^{\mu},\Sigma_{n}^{\nu}]|\psi(\lambda)\rangle
. These are the commutators expectation values of the operators in
\mathcal{L}^{(l)}
for the ground states
|\psi(\lambda)\rangle
of

\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\text{K}}(\lambda)=\sin\big(\lambda\frac{\pi}{2}\big)\sum_{j=1}^{N}\big(\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{j}\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{j+1}+\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{j}\hat{c}^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{j+1}+\text{h.\,c.}\big)+\cos\big(\lambda\frac{\pi}{2}\big)\sum_{j=1}^{N}\big(\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{j}\hat{c}^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{j}-\hat{c}^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{j}\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{j}\big)\;,

(S16)

Exploiting Eqs. (
A.2
), this Hamiltonian can be written in pseudospin form as

\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\text{K}}(\lambda)=-\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}^{+}}\epsilon_{k}\left(v_{k}^{x}\tilde{\sigma}_{k}^{x}+v_{k}^{z}\tilde{\sigma}_{k}^{z}\right),

(S17)

where

\displaystyle\epsilon_{k}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{1+2\sin\left(\lambda\pi/2\right)\cos\left(\lambda\pi/2\right)\cos(k)}

\displaystyle v_{k}^{x}

\displaystyle=-\sin\left(\lambda\pi/2\right)\sin(k)/\epsilon_{k}

(S18)

\displaystyle v_{k}^{z}

\displaystyle=-\left(\cos\left(\lambda\pi/2\right)+\sin\left(\lambda\pi/2\right)\cos(k)\right)/\epsilon_{k}\;.

The density matrix of the ground states of
H^{\text{K}}(\lambda)
is

\rho(\lambda)\equiv\bigotimes_{k\in\mathcal{K}^{+}}\frac{1}{2}\left(v_{k}^{x}\tilde{\sigma}_{k}^{x}+v_{k}^{z}\tilde{\sigma}_{k}^{z}+\mathds{1}\right)\,.

(S19)

We will exploit this density matrix representation of the state to evaluate the commutator matrix. The corresponding state
|\psi(\lambda)\rangle
is the
+1
eigenvector of
\rho(\lambda)
:

|\psi(\lambda)\rangle\equiv\prod_{k\in\mathcal{K}^{+}}\left(\cos(\theta_{k}(\lambda))\,\tilde{\sigma}_{k}^{x}+\sin(\theta_{k}(\lambda))\,\mathds{1}\right)|\downarrow_{k}\rangle,

(S20)

where

\theta_{k}(\lambda)=\frac{1}{2}\arctan\left(\frac{\sin\left(\lambda\pi/2\right)\sin(k)}{\cos\left(\lambda\pi/2\right)+\sin\left(\lambda\pi/2\right)\cos(k)}\right).

(S21)

In the standard fermionic formalism, this reads:

|\psi(\lambda)\rangle\equiv\prod_{k\in\mathcal{K}^{+}}\left(\cos(\theta_{k}(\lambda))\,\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{k}\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{-k}+\sin(\theta_{k}(\lambda))\right)|0\rangle\,,

(S22)

where
|0\rangle
is the vacuum state. Thanks to a Fourier transform, we can represent the ground states in position space as

|\psi(\lambda)\rangle\equiv\prod_{k\in\mathcal{K}^{+}}\left(\frac{i}{N}\cos(\theta_{k})\sum_{j,j^{\prime}}e^{ik(j-j^{\prime})}\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{j}\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{j^{\prime}}+\sin(\theta_{k})\right)|0\rangle\;.

(S23)

The commutator matrix is related to the pseudospin commutator matrix

K^{\prime}_{\{k,\alpha\}\{k^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime}\}}(\lambda)\equiv\langle\psi(\lambda)|-i[\tilde{\sigma}_{k^{\prime}}^{\alpha^{\prime}},\tilde{\sigma}_{k}^{\alpha}]|\psi(\lambda)\rangle

as follows:

K^{(l)}_{\{n,\nu\}\{m,\mu\}}=\langle\psi(\lambda)|-i[\Sigma_{m}^{\mu},\Sigma_{n}^{\nu}]|\psi(\lambda)\rangle=\sum_{k,k^{\prime}}^{\mathcal{K}^{+}}\sum_{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}}^{x,y,z}\mathcal{F}_{\{n,\nu\}\{k,\alpha\}}^{(l)}\mathcal{F}_{\{m,\mu\}\{k^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime}\}}^{(l)}K^{\prime}_{\{k,\alpha\}\{k^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime}\}}\;,

(S24)

where
\mathcal{F}_{\{n,\nu\}\{k,\alpha\}}^{(l)}
is a matrix Fourier transform. In particular, for
l<N/2
we have:

\mathcal{F}^{(l)}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\begin{pmatrix}0&0&1/\sqrt{2}&0&0&1/\sqrt{2}&\dots\\
\sin(1k_{1})&0&0&\sin(1k_{2})&0&0&\dots\\
0&-\sin(1k_{1})&0&0&-\sin(1k_{2})&0&\dots\\
0&0&\cos(1k_{1})&0&0&\cos(1k_{2})&\dots\\
\dots&\dots&\dots&\dots&\dots&\dots&\dots\\
\sin(lk_{1})&0&0&\sin(lk_{2})&0&0&\dots\\
0&-\sin(lk_{1})&0&0&-\sin(lk_{2})&0&\dots\\
0&0&\cos(lk_{1})&0&0&\cos(lk_{2})&\dots\end{pmatrix},

(S25)

and for
l=N/2
:

\mathcal{F}^{(N/2)}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\begin{pmatrix}0&0&1/\sqrt{2}&0&0&1/\sqrt{2}&\dots\\
-\sin(1k_{1})&0&0&-\sin(1k_{2})&0&0&\dots\\
0&\sin(1k_{1})&0&0&\sin(1k_{2})&0&\dots\\
0&0&\cos(1k_{1})&0&0&\cos(1k_{2})&\dots\\
\dots&\dots&\dots&\dots&\dots&\dots&\dots\\
\sin((\frac{N}{2}-1)k_{1})&0&0&\sin((\frac{N}{2}-1)k_{2})&0&0&\dots\\
0&-\sin((\frac{N}{2}-1)k_{1})&0&0&-\sin(\frac{N}{2}k_{2})&0&\dots\\
0&0&\cos((\frac{N}{2}-1)k_{1})&0&0&\cos((\frac{N}{2}-1)k_{2})&\dots\\
\sin(Nk_{1}/2)/\sqrt{2}&0&0&\sin(Nk_{2}/2)/\sqrt{2}&0&0&\dots\\
0&-\sin(Nk_{1}/2)/\sqrt{2}&0&0&-\sin(Nk_{2}/2)/\sqrt{2}&0&\dots\end{pmatrix}.

(S26)

The last step consists of calculating the elements of the matrix
K^{\prime}(\lambda)
. Thanks to the simple commutation rules between pseudospins, this is a block-diagonal matrix:

K^{\prime}(\lambda)=\begin{pmatrix}K_{k_{1}}(\lambda)&0&\dots\\
0&K_{k_{2}}(\lambda)&\dots\\
\dots&\dots&\dots\\
\end{pmatrix},

(S27)

where the blocks are labelled by the elements of
\mathcal{K}^{+}
. Exploiting Eq. (
S19
), we obtain the explicit form of the submatrices:

K_{k}(\lambda)=\begin{pmatrix}0&v_{k}^{z}(\lambda)&0\\
-v_{k}^{z}(\lambda)&0&v_{k}^{x}(\lambda)\\
0&-v_{k}^{x}(\lambda)&0\end{pmatrix}.

(S28)

Now, the commutator matrix can be easily calculated through the Fourier transform of
K^{\prime}(\lambda)
:

K^{(l)}(\lambda)=\big(\mathcal{F}^{(l)}\big)\big(K^{\prime}(\lambda)\big)\big(\mathcal{F}^{(l)}\big)^{\mathrm{T}}\;.

(S29)

A.4
IQA with fermionic Gaussian states: relationship with spin systems

Here we show that, thanks to the Jordan-Wigner transformations
Mbeng et al. 2020
, all the operators in
\mathcal{L}^{(l)}
are equivalent to a set of spin strings spanning over a space of Hamiltonians that encloses several important physical models, such as the Ising model in transverse field
Pfeuty 1970
and the XY model
Lieb et al. 1961
. As a consequence, our results about Gaussian states can be extended to a large class of spin systems.

We consider the operators in

Here, we exploit Gaussian states to study the IQA for large system sizes without the TDVP. In this way, we test our claim that the non-projected dynamics of Eq. (4) generates
l
-local PHs for states with finite correlation length, making negligible the approximation induced by the TDVP.

We refer to the adiabatic solution to Eq. (4) as
non-projected
adiabatic Hamiltonian. Becouse of the definition of
\mathcal{L}^{(l)}
for this many-body system, the evolution generated by
K_{\mathrm{j},\mathrm{j}^{\prime}}^{(N/2)}
spans over the space of all translation-invariant quadratic fermionic Hamiltonians with antiperiodic boundary conditions, therefore it is equivalent to the non-projected evolution in Eq. (4). We study the link between the (effective) interaction range of the non-projected Hamiltonian and the correlation length of
|\psi(\lambda)\rangle
, showing that the effective range of adiabatic Hamiltonian obtained through the non-projected dynamics scales with the correlation range of the ground state and therefore increases with the system size at the phase transition.

In Fig.
S1
, we plot the total norm

\lVert h_{r}\rVert=\sqrt{\sum_{i:\text{range}(L_{i}^{l})=r}h_{i}^{2}}

of the couplings of range
r
of this Hamiltonian at different values of
\lambda
, for a system of
40
(a) and
60
(b) sites. We observe that this norm exponentially decays with
r
for
\lambda\neq\lambda_{c}=1/2
with a decay rate that does not depend on the system size. We can confirm that Eq. (4) generates a local PH, but only for non-critical states.

We can define an effective interaction range as

r_{\text{avg}}(h)\equiv\frac{\sum_{r}r\lVert h_{r}\rVert}{\sum_{r}\lVert h_{r}\rVert}.

This range is represented in Fig.
S2
(a). We can see that, for sufficiently large
N
, it does not depend on the system size for non-critical states. However, it diverges linearly with
N
at
\lambda=\lambda_{c}
. This scaling is reminiscent of
|\psi(\lambda)\rangle
correlation length.

To understand how the scaling behavior originated in the equation of motion, we look at the commutator matrix
K_{\mathrm{j},\mathrm{j}^{\prime}}^{(N/2)}
. We define a correlation range for this matrix

r_{\text{avg}}(K)\equiv\frac{\sum_{\mathrm{j},\mathrm{j}^{\prime}}\lvert\mathrm{j}-\mathrm{j}^{\prime}\rvert\;\lvert K_{\mathrm{j},\mathrm{j}^{\prime}}\rvert}{\sum_{\mathrm{j},\mathrm{j}^{\prime}}\lvert K_{\mathrm{j},\mathrm{j}^{\prime}}\rvert}\,,

which measures the decay of non-diagonal elements. It is depicted in Fig.
S2
(b), and follows the same scaling behavior as the previously analyzed functions. We can conclude that, when the correlation length of states is finite, the non-projected dynamics weakly couples the local and non-local operators and the Hamiltonian does not delocalize, and so the truncation at finite

in Eq. (6) is a good approximation far from the critical point.
