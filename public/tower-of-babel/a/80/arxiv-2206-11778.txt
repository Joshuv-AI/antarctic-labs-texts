Abstract
. For each prime number p p one can associate a Fekete polynomial with coefficients − 1 -1 or 1 1 except the constant term, which is 0. These are classical polynomials that have been studied extensively in the framework of analytic number theory. In a recent paper, we showed that these polynomials also encode interesting arithmetic information. In this paper, we define generalized Fekete polynomials associated with quadratic characters whose conductors could be composite numbers. We then investigate the appearance of cyclotomic factors in these generalized Fekete polynomials. Based on this investigation, we introduce a compact version of Fekete polynomials as well as their trace polynomials. We then study the Galois groups of these Fekete polynomials using modular techniques. In particular, we discover some surprising extra symmetries which imply some restrictions on the corresponding Galois groups. Finally, based on both theoretical and numerical data, we propose a precise conjecture on the structure of these Galois groups.

1.
Introduction

Fekete polynomials are classical polynomials whose history can be traced to the 19th century in relation to the studies of Dirichlet
L
-functions. In fact, as explained very nicely in
[
20
, Chapter 10, Section 3]
these polynomials already played a significant role in Gauss’s original sixth proof of the quadratic reciprocity law.

We recall that for each prime
p
, the Fekete polynomial
F_{p}(x)
associated with
p
is defined by

F_{p}(x)=\sum_{a=1}^{p-1}\left(\frac{a}{p}\right)x^{a}.

Here
\left(\dfrac{a}{p}\right)
is the Legendre symbol which is equal to, for
1\leq a\leq p-1
, the value
1
if
a
is a quadratic residue modulo
p
and the value -1 if
a
is not a quadratic residue modulo
p
.

In our previous work
[
23
]
, we have studied the arithmetic of these Fekete polynomials. We also described briefly some early history of Fekete polynomials and their role in the studies of some
L
-functions. Additionally, we also introduced their cousin which we called reduced Fekete polynomials
g_{p}(x)
. Our main theorems in
[
23
]
show that these polynomials contain interesting arithmetic information such as the class numbers or the orders of certain
K
-groups. Furthermore, our experimental data suggests that the Galois group of
f_{p}(x)
and
g_{p}(x)
are as large as possible (see
[
23
, Conjecture 4.9, Conjecture 4.13]
).

F. Lemmermeyer in
[
20
, Page 231]
naturally extended Fekete polynomials for general Dirichlet characters
\chi.
When
\chi
is a primitive quadratic Dirichlet character with conductor equals to a prime number
p
, Lemmermeyer’s definition specializes to
F_{p}(x)
that we have recalled above. Note that when
p=2
,
F_{2}(x)=x
and hence we can restrict our attention to
p
odd. We remark also that these Fekete polynomials also appear implicitly in the work of Baker and Montgomery about quadratic
L
-functions (see
[
3
, Page 24]
).

In this paper, we adopt Lemmermeyer’s approach. We shall call these polynomials generalized Fekete polynomials
F_{\Delta}(x)
. These are polynomials associated with a (quadratic) character
\chi=\chi_{\Delta}
where the conductor
D=|\Delta|
of
\chi
is not necessarily a prime number (see
3.2
for the precise definition). We will study the factorization of
F_{\Delta}(x)
, which leads to the definitions of the generalized Fekete polynomial
f_{\Delta}(x)
and their reduced version
g_{\Delta}(x)
. In this present article, we will mostly focus on the precise determination of
f_{\Delta}(x)
and
g_{\Delta}(x)
and their Galois groups. Further arithmetical properties of
f_{\Delta}(x)
and
g_{\Delta}(x)
will be discussed in a separate paper in preparation. We shall see that studying these polynomials presents a rich opportunity to extend considerably arithmetic and Galois theoretic considerations of a new class of polynomials with interesting properties. We make considerable effort to make our article as self-contained as possible and we provide all key definitions and motivations. Some of our proofs are involved and intricate and we attempt to explain them with all details in a lucid way. We hope that our article opens a new interesting area of number theory and related areas. In particular, our investigation in the last section about the conjectural Galois groups of Fekete’s polynomials suggests that it would be interesting to study the irreducibility of these polynomials using their special values. For some related references regarding this approach see
[
5
,
12
,
13
,
25
]
.

The structure of this article is as follows. In
Section

2
, we recall some basic definitions in algebraic number theory including the Legendre, Jacobi, and Kronecker symbols and their relations with quadratic fields. We will also prove some lemmas about
{\mathbb{Q}}
-bases of cyclotomic extensions. In
Section

3
, we introduce the generalized Fekete polynomial
F_{\Delta}(x)
and their modified version when the conductor is even. Using the theory of Gauss sums, we discuss some cyclotomic factors of the generalized Fekete polynomials.
Section

4
will focus on the multiplicity of
x=1
and
x=-1
in
F_{\Delta}(x).

Section

5
and
Section

6
will discuss some partial results about the appearance of cyclotomic factors in
F_{\Delta}(x).
We will then focus exclusively on the case where
\Delta
has a few prime factors. More specifically, we will discuss the case
\Delta\in\{4p,-4p,3p,-3p\}
in Section 7-10. This is done by an extensive study of cyclotomic factors of
F_{\Delta}(x).
The calculations in these sections lead to the precise definition of the Fekete polynomial
f_{\Delta}(x)
and its trace polynomial
g_{\Delta}(x)
. In the final section, we provide some numerical data for the Galois groups of
f_{\Delta}(x)
and
g_{\Delta}(x).
In particular, we discuss some cases where a surprising extra symmetry appears, hence putting some restrictions on the Galois group of
f_{\Delta}(x)
. Additionally, we discuss some tests to detect the Galois group of a reciprocal polynomial, which might be of independent interest. Finally, we propose a precise conjecture on the structure of the Galois groups of
f_{\Delta}(x)
and
g_{\Delta}(x)
based on our experimental data.

2.
Background in algebraic number theory

2.1.
Legendre symbol, Jacobi symbol, and Kronecker symbol

In this section, we recall the definition and basic properties of the Legendre, Jacobi, and Kronecker symbols. Our main references for this section are
[
19
]
and
[
22
, Chapter 9]
.

We first recall the definition of the Legendre symbol. Let
a
be an integer and
p
a prime number. The Legendre symbol
\left(\dfrac{a}{p}\right)
is defined as follows.

\left(\frac{a}{p}\right)=\begin{cases}0&\text{if $p|a$ }\\
1&\text{if $a$ is a square modulo $p$ }\\
-1&\text{else. }\end{cases}

The Legendre symbol is multiplicative. In fact, it is a Dirichlet character with conductor
p
. The Jacobi symbol
\left(\frac{a}{b}\right)
, where
b
is an odd positive integer, is a generalization of the Legendre symbol. Specifically, suppose that
b
has the following prime factorization
b=p_{1}^{e_{1}}p_{2}^{e_{2}}\ldots p_{r}^{e_{r}}.
Then the Jacobi symbol
\left(\dfrac{a}{b}\right)
is defined as follow

\left(\frac{a}{b}\right)=\left(\frac{a}{p_{1}}\right)^{e_{1}}\left(\frac{a}{p_{2}}\right)^{e_{2}}\ldots\left(\frac{a}{p_{r}}\right)^{e_{r}},

where
\left(\dfrac{a}{p_{i}}\right)
is the Legendre symbol. Finally, we recall the Kronecker symbol, which generalizes both the Legendre and the Jacobi symbols. To do so, we first define the following conventions

\left(\frac{a}{-1}\right)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }a\geq 0\\
-1&\text{if }a<0\end{cases},\;\left(\frac{a}{2}\right)=\begin{cases}0&\text{if $2|a$ }\\
1&\text{if }a\equiv\pm{1}\pmod{8}\\
-1&\text{if }a\equiv\pm{3}\pmod{8}\end{cases},\;\left(\frac{a}{0}\right)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }a=\pm{1}\\
0&\text{otherwise. }\end{cases}

Let
a,n
be integers. Suppose that
n
has the following factorization into the product of distinct prime numbers

n=\text{sgn}(n)p_{1}^{e_{1}}p_{2}^{e_{2}}\ldots p_{r}^{e_{r}}.

Here
\text{sgn}(n)
is the sign of
n
, which is
1
if
n>0
and
-1
otherwise. Then, the Kronecker symbol
\left(\frac{a}{n}\right)
is defined as

\left(\frac{a}{n}\right)=\left(\frac{a}{\text{sgn}(n)}\right)\left(\frac{a}{p_{1}}\right)^{e_{1}}\left(\frac{a}{p_{2}}\right)^{e_{2}}\ldots\left(\frac{a}{p_{r}}\right)^{e_{r}}.

We note that the Kronecker symbol is defined for all integers
a,n
.

2.2.
Quadratic characters

Let
d
be a squarefree integer. Let
\Delta
be the discriminant of the quadratic extension
{\mathbb{Q}}(\sqrt{d})/{\mathbb{Q}}
, which is given by

\Delta=\begin{cases}d&\text{if }d\equiv 1\pmod{4}\\
4d&\text{if }d\equiv 2,3\pmod{4}.\end{cases}

From this definition, we can see that
\Delta
necessarily determines the quadratic extension
{\mathbb{Q}}(\sqrt{d})/{\mathbb{Q}}.
We have the following theorem.

Theorem 2.1
.

(
[
22
, Theorem 9.13]
)
Let
\chi_{\Delta}:{\mathbb{Z}}\to\mathbb{C}^{\times}
be the function given by

\chi_{\Delta}(a)=\left(\frac{\Delta}{a}\right),

where
\left(\frac{\Delta}{a}\right)
is the Kronecker symbol introduced in the previous section. Then
\chi_{\Delta}
is a primitive quadratic character of conductor
D=|\Delta|.
Furthermore, every primitive quadratic character is given uniquely this way.

2.3.
Cyclotomic polynomials

We recall some properties of cyclotomic polynomials that we will use throughout this article. For a more detailed treatment, interested readers are encouraged to see
[
26
, Sections 46 and 48]
. For a more recent survey on both coefficients and higher-order derivatives of cyclotomic polynomials, we recommend
[
15
]
.

Let
n
be a positive integer. The
n
-th cyclotomic polynomial
\Phi_{n}(x.)
is defined as

\Phi_{n}(x)=\prod_{\zeta}(x-\zeta),

where
\zeta
runs over the set of all primitive
n
-roots of unity. It is known that
\Phi_{n}(x)
is a polynomial of degree
\varphi(n)
where
\varphi(n)
is the Euler totient function. Additionally, we have the following factorization

x^{n}-1=\prod_{d|n}\Phi_{d}(x).

We have the following identities.

Proposition 2.2
.

(
[
26
, Section 46]
)

Let
n
be a positive integer and
p
a prime number. We have the following statements.

(1)

If
\gcd(p,n)=1
, then

\Phi_{np}(x)\Phi_{n}(x)=\Phi_{n}(x^{p}).

(2)

If
p|n
then
\Phi_{np}(x)=\Phi_{n}(x^{p}).

(3)

If
n
is odd and
n>1
then
\Phi_{2n}(x)=\Phi_{n}(-x),
and
\Phi_{4n}(x)=\Phi_{2n}(x^{2})=\Phi_{n}(-x^{2}).

(4)

Additionally

\Phi_{4}(x)=x^{2}+1=\Phi_{2}(x^{2})=-\Phi_{1}(-x^{2}).

Here are some examples of cyclotomic polynomials.

Example 2.3
.

\Phi_{1}(x)=x-1,\Phi_{2}(x)=x+1.

If
p
is an odd prime number then

\Phi_{p}(x)=\frac{x^{p}-1}{x-1}=\sum_{k=0}^{p-1}x^{k},\Phi_{2p}(x)=\Phi_{p}(-x),\Phi_{4p}(x)=\Phi_{p}(-x^{2}),

We have the following observation.

Lemma 2.4
.

\Phi_{1}(x)^{2}
and
\Phi_{n}(x)
, for
n\geq 2
, are reciprocal polynomials.

Proof.

For the first statement, we note that
\Phi_{1}(x)^{2}=(x-1)^{2}=x^{2}-2x+1
. This is clearly a reciprocal polynomial. If
n=2
then

\Phi_{2}(x)=x+1
. This is also clearly a reciprocal polynomial. Let us assume now that
n>2
. By definition, we have

\Phi_{n}(x)=\prod_{\zeta}(x-\zeta),

where
\zeta
runs over the set of all primitive
n
-root of unity. Therefore

\displaystyle x^{\varphi(n)}\Phi_{n}\left(\frac{1}{x}\right)

\displaystyle=x^{\varphi(n)}\prod_{\zeta}(\frac{1}{x}-\zeta)=\prod_{\zeta}(1-x\zeta)=(-1)^{\varphi(n)}(\prod_{\zeta}\zeta)\prod_{\zeta}(x-\zeta^{-1}).

Note that
\zeta
is a primitive
n
-root of unity if and only if
\zeta^{-1}
is. Furthermore, as long as
n>2
,
\zeta\neq\zeta^{-1}.
Therefore, we can partition the set of all primitive
n
-root of unity into pairs
\{\zeta,\zeta^{-1}\}.
This shows that
\varphi(n)
is even,
\prod_{\zeta}\zeta=1
, and

\prod_{\zeta}(x-\zeta)=\prod_{\zeta}(x-\zeta^{-1}).

We then conclude that

x^{\varphi(n)}\Phi_{n}\left(\frac{1}{x}\right)=\Phi_{n}(x).

In other words,
\Phi_{n}(x)
is a reciprocal polynomial.
∎

Next, we discuss some results about
{\mathbb{Q}}
-bases of the cyclotomic extension
{\mathbb{Q}}(\zeta_{n})/{\mathbb{Q}}.
We first have the following classical theorem.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
n
be a squarefree positive integer. Then the set of all primitive
n
-roots of unity, namely
\{\zeta_{n}^{k}|\gcd(k,n)=1\}
, is
{\mathbb{Q}}
-linear independent.

A proof of this lemma can be found in
[
18
]
. Interested readers are encouraged to look at the thread
[
8
]
on mathoverflow for another proof provided by Keith Conrad as well as some interesting historical discussions. We will use Conrad’s argument to prove the following lemmas.

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
p
be an odd prime. Then the set
\{\zeta_{4p}^{k}\mid 1\leq k\leq 2p,(k,2p)=1\}
is
{\mathbb{Q}}
-linearly independent.

Proof.

We have
\phi(4p)=2(p-1)
and the set
S=\{\zeta_{4p}^{k}\mid 1\leq k\leq 2p-2\}
is a
{\mathbb{Q}}
-basis of
{\mathbb{Q}}(\zeta_{4p})
. Since
\Phi_{4p}(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{p-1}(-1)^{j}x^{2j}
, one has

\sum_{j=0}^{p-1}(-1)^{j}\zeta_{4p}^{2j+1}=\zeta_{4p}\Phi_{4p}(\zeta_{4p})=0.

Hence
\zeta_{4p}^{p}
is a linear combination of
\{\zeta_{4p}^{2j+1}\mid j=0,\ldots,p-1\text{ and }2j+1\not=p\}
. So if we replace
\zeta_{4p}^{p}
in the set
S
by
\zeta_{4p}^{2p-1}
, we still have a
{\mathbb{Q}}
-basis of
{\mathbb{Q}}(\zeta_{4p})
. In other words,
\{\zeta_{4p}^{k}\mid 1\leq k\leq 2p-1,k\not=p\}
is a
{\mathbb{Q}}
-basis of
{\mathbb{Q}}(\zeta_{4p})
. In particular, the set
\{\zeta_{4p}^{k}\mid 1\leq k\leq 2p,(k,2p)=1,1\leq j\leq p,(j,p)=1\}
is
{\mathbb{Q}}
-linearly independent.
∎

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
m
be an odd squarefree positive integer. Then the set
\{\zeta_{4m}^{k}\mid 1\leq k\leq 2m,(k,2m)=1\}
is
{\mathbb{Q}}
-linearly independent.

Proof.

We proceed by induction on the number of prime factors of
m
. If
m
is prime, we are done by the previous lemma. Suppose that the statement is true for an odd squarefree integer
m
. We show that the statement is true for
mp
, where
p
is an odd prime number coprime to
m
. We know that
\{\zeta_{4m}^{k}\mid(k,2m)=1,1\leq k\leq 2m\}
is
{\mathbb{Q}}
-linearly independent,
\{\zeta^{j}_{p}\mid 1\leq j\leq p,(j,p)=1\}
is a
{\mathbb{Q}}
-basis of
{\mathbb{Q}}(\zeta_{p})
. Since
{\mathbb{Q}}(\zeta_{4m})\cap{\mathbb{Q}}(\zeta_{p})={\mathbb{Q}}
, the set

S=\{\zeta_{4m}^{k}\zeta_{p}^{j}\mid 1\leq k\leq 2m,(k,2m)=1,1\leq j\leq p,(j,p)=1\}

is
{\mathbb{Q}}
-linearly independent.
Inside
{\mathbb{Q}}(\zeta_{4mp})
we can use
\zeta_{4m}=\zeta_{4mp}^{p}
and
\zeta_{p}=\zeta_{4mp}^{4m}
. Then
\zeta_{4m}^{k}\zeta_{p}^{j}=\zeta_{4mp}^{kp+4mj}
. By taking modulo
4m
and
p
, we see that
kp+4mj
is coprime to
4mp
.
Let
l
be the residue of
kp+4mj
modulo
4mp
. Then
l
is coprime to
2mp
and
\zeta_{4mp}^{kp+4mj}=\zeta_{4p}^{l}
. If
l>2mp
then
l^{\prime}=l-2mp
is coprime to
2mp
,
1\leq l^{\prime}\leq 2mp
and
\zeta_{4mp}^{kp+4mj}=-\zeta_{4p}^{l^{\prime}}
.

Note that if
kp+4mj\equiv k^{\prime}p+4mj^{\prime}\pmod{4mp}
, then
k\equiv k^{\prime}\pmod{2m}
and
j\equiv j\pmod{p}
. Hence the cardinality of
S
is
\phi(2m)\phi(p)
, which is equality to the set

\{\zeta_{4mp}^{l}\mid 1\leq l\leq 2mp,(k,2mp)=1\}
. This implies that the latter set is
{\mathbb{Q}}
-linearly independent.
∎

3.
Generalized Fekete polynomials

In this section, we introduce the generalized Fekete polynomials (see also
[
20
, Page 231]
and
[
3
, Page 24]
.)

Definition 3.1
.

Let
\chi:({\mathbb{Z}}/D)^{\times}\to\mathbb{C}^{\times}
be a primitive Dirichlet character of conductor
D
. The generalized Fekete polynomial associated with
\chi
is given by

F_{\chi}(x)=\sum_{a=1}^{D-1}\chi(a)x^{a}.

In this article, we will focus on the case of real primitive Dirichlet characters.

Definition 3.2
.

Let
d
be a squarefree integer. Let
\Delta
be the fundamental discriminant of the quadratic extension
{\mathbb{Q}}(\sqrt{d})/{\mathbb{Q}}
and
\chi_{\Delta}
the associated quadratic character. The generalized Fekete polynomial associated with
{\mathbb{Q}}(\sqrt{d})/{\mathbb{Q}}
is given by

F_{\Delta}(x)=F_{\chi_{\Delta}}(x)=\sum_{a=1}^{D-1}\chi_{\Delta}(a)x^{a}=\sum_{a=1}^{D-1}\left(\frac{\Delta}{a}\right)x^{a}.

Note that in the above definition, we index
F
by
\Delta
instead of
D
because
{\mathbb{Q}}(\sqrt{d})/{\mathbb{Q}}
is determined by
\Delta
and not necessarily by
D
. For example,
{\mathbb{Q}}(\sqrt{2})
and
{\mathbb{Q}}(\sqrt{-2})
both have
D=8.
More generally, if
2m
is a squarefree number, then
{\mathbb{Q}}(\sqrt{2m})
and
{\mathbb{Q}}(\sqrt{-2m})
both have
D=|\Delta|=8m.

We recall that the
L
-function associated with a primitive Dirichlet character
\chi
with conductor
D
is defined as

L(\chi,s)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\chi(n)}{n^{s}}.

There is a direct relationship between the generalized Fekete polynomial
F_{\chi}(x)
and values of the
L
-function associated with
\chi
. More precisely

Proposition 3.3
.

\Gamma(s)L(\chi,s)=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{(-\log(t))^{s-1}}{t}\frac{F_{\chi}(t)}{1-t^{D}}dt,

where
\Gamma(s)
is the Gamma function.

Proof.

For
0<m\leq 1
, we recall that the Hurwitz zeta function
\zeta(s,m)
is defined by

\zeta(s,m)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(n+m)^{s}}.

By
[
1
, Section 12.1]
, we have

(1)

L(s,\chi)=\frac{1}{D^{s}}\sum_{a=1}^{D-1}\chi(a)\zeta(s,\frac{a}{D}).

Furthermore, by
[
1
, Theorem 12.2]
, the Hurwitz zeta function
\zeta(s,m)
has the following integral representation

\Gamma(s)\zeta(s,m)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{x^{s-1}e^{-mx}}{1-e^{-x}}dx.

Apply this formula for
m=\frac{a}{D}
we have

\Gamma(s)\zeta(s,\frac{a}{D})=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{x^{s-1}e^{-\frac{a}{D}x}}{1-e^{-x}}dx.

Let
t=e^{-\frac{1}{D}x}.
Then
x=-D\log(t)
and
dx=\frac{-D}{t}dt.
We then have

(2)

\Gamma(s)\zeta(s,\frac{a}{D})=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{D^{s}(-\log(t))^{s-1}t^{a}}{t(1-t^{D})}dt.

Combining Equation
1
and Equation
2
we have

\Gamma(s)L(s,\chi)=\sum_{a=1}^{D-1}\chi(a)\int_{0}^{1}\frac{D^{s}(-\log(t))^{s-1}t^{a}}{t(1-t^{D})}dt=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{(-\log(t))^{s-1}}{t}\frac{F_{\chi}(t)}{1-t^{D}}dt\qed.

Remark 3.4
.

For some further discussions regarding real zeroes of
F_{\Delta}(x)
over the interval
(0,1)
and complex zeroes of
F_{\Delta}(x)
on the unit circle, we refer the readers to
[
3
,
9
]
.

Our numerical data suggests that some care must be taken when
\Delta
is even. More precisely, when
\Delta
is even,
\chi_{\Delta}(a)=0
if
a
is even. Consequently,
F_{\Delta}(x)/x
is a polynomial in
x^{2}.
In order to define and study the Fekete polynomials
f_{\Delta}(x)
and the reduced Fekete polynomial
g_{\Delta}(x)
associated with
\Delta
(see
[
23
]
in the case
D=|\Delta|
is a prime number), we introduce the following modification.

Definition 3.5
.

Suppose that
\Delta
is an even number. The modified Fekete polynomial
\tilde{F}_{\Delta}(x)
associated with
\Delta
is given by

F_{\Delta}(x)=x\tilde{F}_{\Delta}(x^{2}).

Concretely

\tilde{F}_{\Delta}(x)=\sum_{a=0}^{D/2-1}\left(\frac{\Delta}{2a+1}\right)x^{a}.

Remark 3.6
.

We motivate this definition by a concrete example; namely when
\Delta=4\times 11
. In this case,
F_{\Delta}(x)
has the following factorization

\displaystyle F_{\Delta}(x)

\displaystyle=x(x-1)^{2}(x+1)^{2}\Phi_{4}(x)\Phi_{11}(x)\Phi_{22}(x)(x^{16}-x^{14}+2x^{12}+3x^{8}+2x^{4}-x^{2}+1)

\displaystyle=x\Phi_{1}(x^{2})^{2}\Phi_{2}(x^{2})\Phi_{11}(x^{2})f_{\Delta}(x^{2}),

where
f_{\Delta}(x)=x^{8}-x^{7}+2x^{6}+3x^{4}+2x^{2}-x+1.

By definition, we have

\tilde{F}_{\Delta}(x)=\Phi_{1}(x)^{2}\Phi_{2}(x)\Phi_{11}(x)f_{\Delta}(x).

Philosophically speaking, we want to define the Fekete polynomial
f_{\Delta}(x)
in such a way that
f_{\Delta}(x)
is as simple as possible (and that
f_{\Delta}(x)
contains all essential information about
F_{\Delta}(x)
and hence
\tilde{F}_{\Delta}(x)
.) This shows that, we should define

f_{\Delta}(x)=x^{8}-x^{7}+2x^{6}+3x^{4}+2x^{2}-x+1.

Note that
f_{\Delta}(x)
is a reciprocal polynomial and its trace polynomial is given by (see
[
23
, Section 3]
for the definition)

g_{\Delta}(x)=x^{4}-x^{3}-2x^{2}+3x+1.

The polynomials
f_{\Delta}(x)
and
g_{\Delta}(x)
will be the main subjects of investigation in our work.

Finally, we also observe that
F_{\Delta}(x)
satisfies the following symmetry.

Proposition 3.7
.

Let
\chi=\chi_{\Delta}
be a quadratic Dirichlet character with conductor
D
. Then we have

x^{D}F_{\Delta}\left(\frac{1}{x}\right)=\chi(-1)F_{\Delta}(x).

If
D
is even then

x^{\frac{D}{2}-1}{\tilde{F}_{\Delta}}\left(\frac{1}{x}\right)=\chi(-1){\tilde{F}_{\Delta}}(x).

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle x^{D}F_{\chi}\left(\frac{1}{x}\right)

\displaystyle=x^{D}\sum_{a=1}^{D}\chi(a)\left(\frac{1}{x}\right)^{a}=\sum_{a=1}^{D-1}\chi(a)x^{D-a}

\displaystyle=\sum_{u=1}^{D-1}\chi(D-u)x^{u}\quad=\chi(-1)\sum_{u=1}^{D-1}\chi(u)x^{u}=\chi(-1)F_{\chi}(x).

When
D
is even, the second equality follows directly from the above equation and the definition
{\tilde{F}_{\Delta}}(x).

∎

3.1.
Gauss sums and zeros of generalized Fekete polynomials

Let
\chi_{\Delta}
be a quadratic Dirichlet character of conductor
D
and
b
an integer. Let
\zeta_{D}=\exp\left(\frac{2\pi i}{D}\right)
be a primitive
D
-root of unity.

Definition 3.8
.

(
[
2
, Chapter V]
)
The Gauss sum
G(b,\chi_{\Delta})
is defined as follow

G(b,\chi_{\Delta})=\sum_{a=1}^{D-1}\chi_{\Delta}(a)\zeta_{D}^{ab}=F_{\Delta}(\zeta_{D}^{b}).

We have the following fundamental property
[
2

We also note that when

Lemma 3.10
.

Let
F\in{\mathbb{Z}}[x]
be a polynomial in
x^{2}
; namely
F(x)=\tilde{F}(x^{2})
with
F(x)\in{\mathbb{Z}}[x]
. Suppose that
p(x)
is an irreducible factor of
F(x)
with multiplicity
m
.

(1)

If
p(x)\neq p(-x)
, then
p(-x)
is also a factor of
F(x)
with the same multiplicity.

(2)

Assume that
p(x)=p(-x)
and
p(x)=\tilde{p}(x^{2})
. Then the multiplicity of
p(x)
in
F(x)
is equal to the multiplicity of
\tilde{p}(x)
in
\tilde{F}(x).

Proof.

Because
p(x)
is a factor of
F(x)
, we can write
F(x)=p(x)q(x).
Take
x=-x
and use the fact that
F(x)=F(-x)

F(x)=p(-x)q(-x).
This shows that
p(-x)
is a factor of
F(x).

Let us first consider the case
p(x)\neq p(-x)
. In this case,
p(x)
and
p(-x)
are distinct factors of
F(x).
Let us write

F(x)=p(x)^{m_{1}}p(-x)^{m_{2}}h(x),
where
h(x)\in{\mathbb{Z}}[x]
such that
\gcd(h(x),p(x)p(-x))=1.
Take
x=-x
again, we have

F(x)=F(-x)=p(-x)^{m_{1}}p(x)^{m_{2}}h(-x).

Consequently
p(x)^{m_{1}}p(-x)^{m_{2}}h(x)=p(-x)^{m_{1}}p(x)^{m_{2}}h(-x).
We conclude that
m_{1}=m_{2}.

Let us now consider the second case, namely
p(x)=\tilde{p}(x^{2}).
Let
m
be the multiplicity of
p(x)
in
F(x).
We can write
\tilde{F}(x^{2})=F(x)=p(x)^{m}q(x)=\tilde{p}(x^{2})^{m}q(x).

Here
q(x)\in{\mathbb{Z}}[x]
and
\gcd(q(x),p(x))=1
. From this equation, we see that
q(x)=\tilde{q}(x^{2})
for some
\tilde{q}(x)\in{\mathbb{Z}}[x]
; and hence
\tilde{F}(x)=\tilde{p}(x)^{m}\tilde{q}(x)
and
\gcd(\tilde{q}(x),\tilde{p}(x))=1.
This shows that the multiplicity of
\tilde{p}(x)
in
\tilde{F}(x)
is
m
as well.
∎

\Phi_{n}(x)\Phi_{2n}(x)=\Phi_{n}(x^{2}).

(2)

\Phi_{n}(x)
in
\tilde{F}_{\Delta}(x)

(3)

For any positive integer

r_{\Delta}(\Phi_{4m})=\tilde{r}_{\Delta}(\Phi_{2m}).

Proof.

Let
m
be the multiplicity of
\Phi_{n}(x)
in

Then by Lemma

,

in

Here

We also note that the quotient of two polynomials in

From this equation, we see that the multiplicity of

One can also define the generalized Bernoulli numbers

Proof of Lemma
4.1
.

Let
\chi

\chi_{\Delta}
. One has (
[
16
, page 10]
)

(3)

Substituting

\displaystyle B_{1,\chi}

\displaystyle=\sum_{a=1}^{D-1}\chi(a)B_{1}\left(\dfrac{a}{D}-1\right)=\sum_{a=1}^{D-1}\chi(a)\left(\dfrac{a}{D}-\dfrac{1}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{D}\sum_{a=1}^{D-1}\chi(a)a-\dfrac{1}{2}\sum_{a=1}^{D-1}\chi(a)=\dfrac{1}{D}\sum_{a=1}^{D-1}\chi(a)a.

Substituting

[

In this section, we discuss some general results about

for big

Theorem 5.1
.

Suppose that
\Delta=dn
is odd and
n>d>1.
Then
\zeta_{n}
is a simple root of
F_{\Delta}(x).
In other words,
r_{\Delta}(\Phi_{n})=1.

Proof.

Recall that

F_{\Delta}(x)=\sum\limits_{a=1}^{\Delta-1}\left(\frac{\Delta}{a}\right)x^{a}.

We already know that
F_{\Delta}(\zeta_{n})=0.
We need to show that
F^{\prime}_{\Delta}(\zeta_{n})\neq 0
. We have

F^{\prime}_{\Delta}(x)=\sum\limits_{a=1}^{\Delta-1}a\left(\frac{\Delta}{a}\right)x^{a-1}.

We then see that

\displaystyle\zeta_{n}F^{\prime}_{\Delta}(\zeta_{n})

\displaystyle=\sum_{a=1}^{\Delta-1}\left(\frac{\Delta}{a}\right)a\zeta_{n}^{a}=\sum_{m=1}^{n}\left[\sum_{a\equiv m\pmod{n}}\left(\frac{\Delta}{a}\right)a\right]\zeta_{n}^{k}=\sum_{m=1}^{n}A_{m}\zeta_{n}^{k},

where

A_{m}=\sum_{a\equiv m\pmod{n}}\left(\frac{\Delta}{a}\right)a,\quad 1\leq m\leq n.

We know that
A_{m}=0
for every
m
with
(m,n)>1
. Now suppose that
F^{\prime}_{\Delta}(\zeta_{n})=0
. Because
n
is squarefree, the set
\{\zeta_{n}^{m}\mid(m,n)=1,1\leq m\leq n\}
is a
{\mathbb{Q}}
-linearly independent (see
2.5
). Hence
A_{m}=0
for every
m
with
(m,n)=1
.

We observe that we can rewrite
A_{m}
as follows

\displaystyle A_{m}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{d-1}(kn+m)\left(\frac{\Delta}{kn+m}\right)=\sum_{k=0}^{d-1}(kn+m)\left(\frac{kn+m}{dn}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{m}{n}\right)\sum_{k=0}^{d-1}(kn+m)\left(\frac{kn+m}{d}\right)=m\left(\frac{m}{n}\right)\sum_{k=0}^{d-1}\left(\frac{kn+m}{d}\right)+n\left(\frac{m}{n}\right)\sum_{k=0}^{d-1}\left(\frac{kn+m}{d}\right)k.

We note that
\{kn+m\}_{k=0}^{d-1}
is a complete system of residues modulo
d
. Therefore

\sum_{k=0}^{d-1}\left(\frac{kn+m}{d}\right)=0.

Consequently, we have

E_{m}=\sum_{k=0}^{d-1}\left(\frac{kn+m}{d}\right)k=\sum_{k=1}^{d-1}\left(\frac{kn+m}{d}\right)k=0,\forall 1\leq m\leq n-1.

Let
\widetilde{m}
be the unique number in
\{0,1,\ldots,d-1\}
such that
\widetilde{m}n\equiv m\pmod{d}
(in other words
\widetilde{m}=n^{-1}m
modulo
d
). We then have

E_{m}=\left(\frac{n}{m}\right)\sum_{k=0}^{d-1}\left(\frac{k+\widetilde{m}}{d}\right)k=\left(\frac{n}{m}\right)\sum_{k=0}^{d-1}\left(\frac{k+\widetilde{m}}{d}\right)(k+\widetilde{m}).

The last inequality follows from the fact that
\sum_{k=0}^{d-1}\left(\frac{k+\widetilde{m}}{d}\right)=0.
Let

C_{\widetilde{m}}=\sum_{k=0}^{d-1}\left(\frac{k+\widetilde{m}}{d}\right)(k+\widetilde{m}).

Then
C_{\widetilde{m}}=0
by our assumption (note that
\widetilde{m}
depends on
m
.) Under the assumption that
n>d
,
let us consider
m=n-d.
Then
\tilde{m}=1.
We have

\displaystyle C_{1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{d-1}\left(\frac{k+1}{d}\right)(k+1)=\sum_{u=1}^{d-1}\left(\frac{u}{d}\right)u=dB_{1,\chi_{d^{*}}}.

Here

If
d^{*}<0
then
C_{1}\neq 0.
In this case, we are done. Now suppose
d^{*}>0.

We have the following small lemma.

Lemma 5.2
.

There exists
m
such that
1\leq m<n
,
\gcd(m,n)=1
and
2n\equiv m\pmod{d}.

For this
m
,
\tilde{m}=2.

Proof.

Let us write
n=qd+r
, with
1\leq r\leq d-1
. Under the condition,
n>d
, we have
q\geq 1.
We will look for of the form
m=hd+2r
for some
0\leq h\leq q-1
.
Note that for this choice of
m
, conditions
(1)
and
(3)
are satisfied. We will look for an
h
such that
m
satisfies condition (2) as well.
We have

\displaystyle\gcd(n,m)

\displaystyle=\gcd(n,hd+2r)=\gcd(n,2n-(hd+2r))

\displaystyle=\gcd(n,(2q-h)d)=\gcd(n,2q-h).

Note that
q+1\leq 2q-h\leq 2q.

We claim that there exists an integer
t
such that
q+1\leq 2^{t}\leq 2q.

In fact, let

be the largest integer such that
2^{s}\leq q+1.
If
q+1=2^{s}
, we can take

. Otherwise,
2^{s}<q+1.
Hence

From this, we can easily see that

In this case, we can take

Now, let
0\leq h\leq q-1
be the number such that

Our argument shows that

.

For this choice of

,
\tilde{m}=2

\displaystyle C_{2}

This completes the proof.

We then see that

\displaystyle\zeta_{n}F^{\prime}_{\Delta}(\zeta_{n})

\displaystyle=\sum_{a=1}^{\Delta-1}\left(\frac{\Delta}{a}\right)a\zeta_{n}^{a}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left[\sum_{a\equiv k\pmod{n}}\left(\frac{\Delta}{a}\right)a\right]\zeta_{n}^{k}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}A_{k}\zeta_{n}^{k},

where

A_{k}=\sum_{a\equiv k\pmod{n}}\left(\frac{\Delta}{a}\right)a,\quad 1\leq k\leq n.

We know that
A_{k}=0
for every
k
with
(k,n)>1
. Now suppose that
F^{\prime}_{\Delta}(\zeta_{n})=0
. Because
n
is squarefree, the set
\{\zeta_{n}^{k}\mid(k,n)=1,1\leq k\leq n\}
is a
{\mathbb{Q}}
-basis of
{\mathbb{Q}}(\zeta_{n})
. Hence
A_{k}=0
for every
k
with
(k,n)=1
. This implies that

F^{\prime}_{\Delta}(1)=\sum\limits_{m=1}^{n}A_{m}=0.

This contradicts to

4.2
. Therefore
F^{\prime}_{\Delta}(\zeta_{n})
must be nonzero, as required.
∎

When
\Delta
is negative and even, we have the following partial result.

Theorem 5.4
.

If
\Delta=-4d<0
, where
d
is odd squarefree and
d\equiv 1\pmod{4}
. Assume that
d=mq
where
q
is a prime number and
m
is a positive number. Let
S_{m}=\{1\leq k\leq 2m|\gcd(2m,k)=1\}.
Let us assume further that
S_{m}
contains at least
q/2
distinct residues modulo
q.
Then
\zeta_{4m}
is a simple root of
F_{\Delta}(x).

Proof.

Let
n=4m
. We already know that
F_{\Delta}(\zeta_{n})=0.
We need to show that
F^{\prime}_{\Delta}(\zeta_{n})\neq 0
. We have

\displaystyle\zeta_{n}F^{\prime}_{\Delta}(\zeta_{n})

\displaystyle=\sum_{a=1}^{\Delta-1}\left(\frac{\Delta}{a}\right)a\zeta_{n}^{a}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left[\sum_{a\equiv k\pmod{n}}\left(\frac{\Delta}{a}\right)a\right]\zeta_{n}^{k}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}A_{k}\zeta_{n}^{k},

where

A_{k}=\sum_{a\equiv k\pmod{n}}\left(\frac{\Delta}{a}\right)a,\quad 1\leq k\leq n.

Note that
\zeta_{n}^{k}=-\zeta_{n}^{k+2m}
. Hence
\zeta_{n}F^{\prime}_{\Delta}(\zeta_{n})=\sum_{k=1}^{2m}(A_{k}-A_{k+2m})\zeta_{n}^{k}.
We know that
A_{k}=0
for every
k
with
(k,n)>1
. Now suppose that
F^{\prime}_{\Delta}(\zeta_{n})=0
. By
2.7
, the set
\{\zeta_{n}^{k}\mid(k,2m)=1,1\leq k\leq 2m\}
is
{\mathbb{Q}}
-linearly independent in
{\mathbb{Q}}(\zeta_{n})
. Hence
A_{k}=A_{k+2m}
for every
k
with
(k,2m)=1
and
1\leq k\leq 2m
.

From our assumption, we have
d=mq
. We then have

\displaystyle A_{k}-A_{k+2m}

\displaystyle=\sum_{a\equiv k\pmod{4m}}\left[\left(\frac{-4mq}{a}\right)a-\left(\frac{-4mq}{a+2m}\right)(a+2m)\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{a\equiv k\pmod{4m}}\left[\left(\frac{-4mq}{a}\right)-\left(\frac{-4mq}{a+2m}\right)\right]a-2m\sum_{a\equiv k\pmod{4m}}\left(\frac{-4mq}{a+2m}\right).

Let us write
a=k+4mt
for
0\leq t\leq q-1
. We have

\displaystyle\left(\frac{-4mq}{a}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{-4mq}{k+4mt}\right)=\left(\frac{-qm}{k+4mt}\right)=(-1)^{\frac{k-1}{2}}\left(\frac{k+4mt}{qm}\right)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{\frac{k-1}{2}}\left(\frac{k+4mt}{q}\right)\left(\frac{k+4mt}{m}\right)=(-1)^{\frac{k-1}{2}}\left(\frac{k+4mt}{q}\right)\left(\frac{k}{m}\right).

Similarly

\displaystyle\left(\frac{-4mq}{a+2m}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{-4mq}{k+2m+4mt}\right)=(-1)^{\frac{k-1}{2}+m}\left(\frac{k+2m+4mt}{q}\right)\left(\frac{k+2m}{m}\right)

\displaystyle=-(-1)^{\frac{k-1}{2}}\left(\frac{k}{m}\right)\left(\frac{k+2m+4mt}{q}\right).

Because
\{k+2m+4mt\}_{t=0}^{q-1}
is a complete residue system modulo
q
, we have

\sum_{a\equiv k\pmod{4m}}\left(\frac{-4mq}{a+2m}\right)=-(-1)^{\frac{k-1}{2}}\left(\frac{k}{m}\right)\sum_{0\leq t\leq q-1}\left(\frac{k+2m+4mt}{q}\right)=0.

Therefore, we conclude that

\displaystyle A_{k}-A_{k+2m}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{\frac{k-1}{2}}\left(\frac{k}{m}\right)\sum_{0\leq t\leq q-1}\left[\left(\frac{k+4mt}{q}\right)+\left(\frac{k+2m+4mt}{q}\right)\right](k+4mt)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{\frac{k-1}{2}}\left(\frac{k}{m}\right)4m\sum_{0\leq t\leq q-1}\left[\left(\frac{k+4mt}{q}\right)+\left(\frac{k+2m+4mt}{q}\right)\right]t.

The second equality follows from the fact that

\sum_{0\leq t\leq q-1}\left(\frac{k+4mt}{q}\right)=\sum_{0\leq t\leq q-1}\left(\frac{k+2m+4mt}{q}\right)=0.

By our assumption,
A_{k}-A_{k+2m}=0
. Hence

\displaystyle\sum_{0\leq t\leq q-1}\left[\left(\frac{k+4mt}{q}\right)+\left(\frac{k+2m+4mt}{q}\right)\right]t=0.

Note that all terms in the above sum are even, except the case where either
\left(\frac{k+4mt}{q}\right)
or
\left(\frac{k+2m+4mt}{q}\right)
is zero. Let
t_{1,k}^{*}
(respectively
t_{2,k}^{*}
) be the unique integer in
\{0,1,2,\ldots,q-1\}
such that
q|4mt_{1,k}^{*}+k
(respectively
q|4mt_{2,k}^{*}+2m+k
). Then

\displaystyle 0=\sum_{0\leq t\leq q-1}\left[\left(\frac{k+4mt}{q}\right)+\left(\frac{k+2m+4mt}{q}\right)\right]t

\displaystyle\equiv t_{1,k}^{*}-t_{2,k}^{*}\pmod{2}.

We remark that by our definition
q|4m(t_{1,k}^{*}-t_{2,k}^{*})-2m.
Equivalently
q|2(t_{1,k}^{*}-t_{2,k}^{*})-1.
We conclude that either
t_{1,k}^{*}-t_{2,k}^{*}=\frac{q+1}{2}
or
t_{1,k}^{*}-t_{2,k}^{*}=\frac{1-q}{2}.
Furthermore, the first case happens if
t_{1,k}^{*}>q/2
and the second case happens otherwise. Under the assumption that
S_{m}
contains at least
q/2
distinct residues modulo
q
, we know that there exist
k_{1},k_{2}\in S_{m}
such that
t_{1,k_{1}}^{*}>q/2
and
t_{1,k_{2}}^{*}<q/2.
In the first case

0\equiv t_{1,k_{1}}^{*}-t_{2,k_{1}}^{*}\equiv\frac{q+1}{2}\pmod{2}.

In the second case

0\equiv t_{1,k_{2}}^{*}-t_{2,k_{2}}^{*}\equiv\frac{1-q}{2}=1+\frac{q+1}{2}\pmod{2}.

This is a contradiction.
∎

We provide below a concrete example where the condition on
S_{m}
is satisfied.

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
m=p_{1}\ldots p_{r}
. Assume that
\min\{p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{r}\}>q
. Then
S_{m}
contains at least
q/2
residues modulo
q.

Proof.

Let us consider the following set

S=\{1,3,\ldots,q\}=\{2k+1|1\leq 2k+1\leq q\}.

We can see that
|S|=\frac{q+1}{2}>q/2
. Furthermore, by our assumption
S\subset S_{m}
. Consequently,
|S_{m}|>q/2.

∎

Lemma 6.1
.

Let

and

.
∎

Proposition 6.2
.

Let
D=|\Delta|=p
be a prime number and
m
an positive integer. If
\gcd(m,p-1)<\phi(m)
, then

.

Proof.

One has

F_{\Delta}(x)\equiv x+x^{2}+\cdots+x^{p-1}=x\frac{x^{p-1}-1}{x-1}\pmod{2}.

Suppose that
F_{\Delta}(\zeta_{m})=0
. Then one has

\zeta_{m}^{p-1}-1\in I,

where
I=2{\mathbb{Z}}[\zeta_{m}]
.
Clearly,
\zeta_{m}^{m}-1=0\in I
. By previous lemma,
\zeta_{m}^{d}-1\in I
, where

. Therefore

\zeta_{m}^{d}=1+2(a_{0}+a_{1}\zeta_{m}+\cdots+a_{\varphi(m)}\zeta_{m}^{\varphi(m)-1}).

is a

∎

Proposition 6.3
.

Let
D=|\Delta|=p
be a prime number and
m
an positive integer. If
\dfrac{m}{\gcd(m,p-1)}

. In particular, if

Proof.

Let

and let

. Suppose that

.
As in the previous proof,
\zeta_{m}^{d}-1\in I:=2{\mathbb{Z}}[\zeta_{m}]
. Write

, for some

One has

∎

It turns out that the values

for

can often be expressed in terms of Bernoulli numbers and hence special values of

-functions. Using this relationship, we obtain information about the vanishing and non-vanishing of

7.1.
Cyclotomic factors of
{\tilde{F}_{\Delta}}(x)

By Corollary
4.3
in Section
4
, we know that

r_{\Delta}(\Phi_{1})=r_{\Delta}(\Phi_{2})=\tilde{r}_{\Delta}(\Phi_{1})=2.

We next determine the multiplicity of
\Phi_{p}(x),\Phi_{2p}(x)
in
F_{\Delta}(x).
We first recall the definition of
{\tilde{F}_{\Delta}}(x)

{\tilde{F}_{\Delta}}(x)=\sum_{a=0}^{2p-1}\left(\frac{4p}{2a+1}\right)x^{a}.

By the reciprocity law, we have

\left(\frac{4p}{2a+1}\right)=\left(\frac{p}{2a+1}\right)=\left(\frac{2a+1}{p}\right)(-1)^{\frac{a(p-1)}{2}}=(-1)^{a}\left(\frac{2a+1}{p}\right).

Note also that

\left(\dfrac{2a+1}{p}\right)=\left(\dfrac{2}{p}\right)\left(\dfrac{a+\frac{p+1}{2}}{p}\right).

Combining these facts, we have

\displaystyle{\tilde{F}_{\Delta}}(x)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{2}{p}\right)\sum_{a=0}^{2p-1}\left(\frac{a+\frac{p+1}{2}}{p}\right)(-1)^{a}x^{a}=\left(\frac{2}{p}\right)\sum_{a=0}^{p-1}\left(\frac{a+\frac{p+1}{2}}{p}\right)[(-1)^{a}x^{a}-(-1)^{a}x^{a+p}].

Proposition 7.1
.

r_{\Delta}(\Phi_{p})=r_{\Delta}(\Phi_{2p})=\tilde{r}_{\Delta}(\Phi_{p})=1.

Proof.

We have

{\tilde{F}_{\Delta}}^{\prime}(x)=\left(\dfrac{2}{p}\right)\sum\limits_{a=1}^{p-1}\left(\frac{a+\frac{p+1}{2}}{p}\right)[(-1)^{a}ax^{a-1}-(-1)^{a}(a+p)x^{a+p-1}].

Hence

\displaystyle{\tilde{F}_{\Delta}}^{\prime}(\zeta_{p})

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{2}{p}\right)\sum_{a=1}^{p-1}\left(\frac{a+\frac{p+1}{2}}{p}\right)[(-1)^{a}a\zeta_{p}^{a-1}-(-1)^{a}(a+p)\zeta_{p}^{a+p-1}]

\displaystyle=-\left(\frac{2}{p}\right)p\sum_{a=1}^{p-1}\left(\frac{a+\frac{p+1}{2}}{p}\right)(-1)^{a}\zeta_{p}^{a-1}.

By Lemma
2.5
we know that
\{\zeta_{p},\zeta_{p}^{2},\ldots,\zeta_{p}^{p-1}\}
is
{\mathbb{Q}}
-linearly independent. Therefore, we can conclude that
{\tilde{F}_{\Delta}}^{\prime}(\zeta_{p})\neq 0
and the above statement holds.
∎

For the multiplicity of
\Phi_{4}(x)
, we have the following.

Proposition 7.2
.

r_{\Delta}(\Phi_{4})=\tilde{r}_{\Delta}(\Phi_{2})=\begin{cases}3&\mbox{if }p\equiv 7\pmod{8}\\
1&\mbox{if }p\equiv 3\pmod{8}.\end{cases}

Proof.

We have

{\tilde{F}_{\Delta}}(-1)=2\left(\dfrac{2}{p}\right)\sum\limits_{a=0}^{p-1}\left(\dfrac{a+\frac{p+1}{2}}{p}\right)=0.

We also have

\displaystyle\left(\frac{2}{p}\right){\tilde{F}_{\Delta}}^{\prime}(-1)

\displaystyle=\sum_{a=0}^{p-1}\left(\frac{a+\frac{p+1}{2}}{p}\right)(-a-(a+p))=-2\sum_{a=0}^{p-1}\left(\frac{a+\frac{p+1}{2}}{p}\right)a-p\sum_{a=0}^{p-1}\left(\frac{a+\frac{p+1}{2}}{p}\right)

\displaystyle=-2\sum_{a=0}^{p-1}\left(\frac{a+\frac{p+1}{2}}{p}\right)a=-2\sum_{a=0}^{\frac{p-1}{2}}\left(\frac{a+\frac{p+1}{2}}{p}\right)a-2\sum_{a=\frac{p+1}{2}}^{p-1}\left(\frac{a+\frac{p+1}{2}}{p}\right)a

\displaystyle=-2\sum_{u=\frac{p+1}{2}}^{p}\left(\frac{u}{p}\right)(u-\frac{p+1}{2})-2\sum_{u=1}^{\frac{p-1}{2}}\left(\frac{u}{p}\right)(u+\frac{p-1}{2})

\displaystyle=-2\sum_{u=1}^{p-1}\left(\frac{u}{p}\right)u+(p+1)\sum_{u=\frac{p+1}{2}}^{p}\left(\frac{u}{p}\right)-(p-1)\sum_{u=1}^{\frac{p-1}{2}}\left(\frac{u}{p}\right)

\displaystyle=-2\sum_{u=1}^{p-1}\left(\frac{u}{p}\right)u-2p\sum_{u=1}^{\frac{p-1}{2}}\left(\frac{u}{p}\right)=2ph(-p)-2p\left(2-\left(\dfrac{2}{p}\right)\right)h(-p)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}0&\text{ if }p\equiv 7\pmod{8}\\
-4ph(-p)&\text{ if }p\equiv 3\pmod{8}.\end{cases}

For the second to the last equality we use the fact that for
p\equiv 3\pmod{4}
), one has

\displaystyle\sum_{u=1}^{p-1}\left(\frac{u}{p}u\right)

\displaystyle=-ph(-p)

\displaystyle(\text{see \cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{[Girstmair]}{}{}, Equation 3]}})

\displaystyle\sum_{u=1}^{\frac{p-1}{2}}\left(\frac{u}{p}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(2-\left(\dfrac{2}{p}\right)\right)h(-p)

\displaystyle(\text{see \cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{[Berndt]}{}{}, Corollary 3.4]}}).

Now we suppose that
p\equiv 7\pmod{8}
. We have

\displaystyle\left(\frac{2}{p}\right){\tilde{F}_{\Delta}}^{\prime\prime}(-1)

\displaystyle=2\sum_{a=0}^{p-1}\left(\frac{a+\frac{p+1}{2}}{p}\right)\left(a(a-1)+(a+p)(a+p-1)\right)

\displaystyle=2\sum_{a=0}^{p-1}\left(\frac{a+\frac{p+1}{2}}{p}\right)(a^{2}+(a+p)^{2})

\displaystyle=4\sum_{a=0}^{p-1}\left(\frac{a+\frac{p+1}{2}}{p}\right)a^{2}+4p\sum_{a=0}^{p-1}\left(\frac{a+\frac{p+1}{2}}{p}\right)a+2p^{2}\sum_{a=0}^{p-1}\left(\frac{a+\frac{p+1}{2}}{p}\right)

\displaystyle=4\sum_{a=0}^{p-1}\left(\frac{a+\frac{p+1}{2}}{p}\right)a^{2}=4\sum_{u=\frac{p+1}{2}}^{p}\left(\frac{u}{p}\right)(u-\frac{p+1}{2})^{2}+4\sum_{u=1}^{\frac{p-1}{2}}\left(\frac{u}{p}\right)(u+\frac{p-1}{2})^{2}

\displaystyle=4\sum_{u=0}^{p-1}\left(\frac{u}{p}\right)u^{2}-4(p+1)\sum_{u=\frac{p+1}{2}}^{p}\left(\frac{u}{p}\right)u+4(p-1)\sum_{u=1}^{\frac{p-1}{2}}\left(\frac{u}{p}\right)u

\displaystyle+(p+1)^{2}\sum_{u=\frac{p+1}{2}}^{p}\left(\frac{u}{p}\right)+(p-1)^{2}\sum_{u=1}^{\frac{p-1}{2}}\left(\frac{u}{p}\right)

\displaystyle=4\sum_{u=0}^{p-1}\left(\frac{u}{p}\right)u^{2}+4p(p+1)h(-p)-4ph(-p)

\displaystyle=4\sum_{u=0}^{p-1}\left(\frac{u}{p}\right)u^{2}+4p^{2}h(-p)=0.

Here, we use (for
p\equiv 7\pmod{8}
):

\begin{aligned} \sum_{u=1}^{\frac{p-1}{2}}\left(\frac{u}{p}\right)u&=0\quad(\text{\cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{[Berndt]}{}{}, Corollary 13.4]}}),\sum_{u=1}^{p-1}\left(\frac{u}{p}\right)u=-ph(-p)\quad(\text{see \cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{[Girstmair]}{}{}, Equation 3]}}),\\
\sum_{u=1}^{\frac{p-1}{2}}\left(\frac{u}{p}\right)&=h(-p)\quad(\text{\cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{[Berndt]}{}{}, Corollary 3.4]}}),\\
\sum_{u=0}^{p-1}\left(\frac{u}{p}\right)u^{2}&=\frac{-\sqrt{p}p^{2}}{\pi}L(1,\chi_{p})=-p^{2}h(-p)\;(\text{\cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{[Berndt]}{}{}, Theorem 13.15]}}),\end{aligned}.

Now we consider
{\tilde{F}_{\Delta}}^{\prime\prime\prime}(-1)
. We have

\displaystyle\left(\frac{2}{p}\right){\tilde{F}_{\Delta}}^{\prime\prime\prime}(-1)=2\sum_{a=0}^{p-1}\left(\frac{a+\frac{p+1}{2}}{p}\right)\left(-a(a-1)(a-2)-(a+p)(a+p-1)(a+p-2)\right)

\displaystyle=-2\sum_{a=0}^{p-1}\left(\frac{a+\frac{p+1}{2}}{p}\right)(a^{3}+(a+p)^{3})=-4\sum_{a=0}^{p-1}\left(\frac{a+\frac{p+1}{2}}{p}\right)a^{3}

\displaystyle=-4\sum_{u=\frac{p+1}{2}}^{p}\left(\frac{u}{p}\right)(u-\frac{p+1}{2})^{3}-4\sum_{u=1}^{\frac{p-1}{2}}\left(\frac{u}{p}\right)(u+\frac{p-1}{2})^{3}

\displaystyle=-4\sum_{u=0}^{p-1}\left(\frac{u}{p}\right)u^{3}+6(p+1)\sum_{u=\frac{p+1}{2}}^{p}\left(\frac{u}{p}\right)u^{2}-3(p+1)^{2}\sum_{u=\frac{p+1}{2}}^{p}\left(\frac{u}{p}\right)u+\frac{(p+1)^{3}}{2}\sum_{u=\frac{p+1}{2}}^{p}\left(\frac{u}{p}\right)

\displaystyle-6(p-1)\sum_{u=1}^{\frac{p-1}{2}}\left(\frac{u}{p}\right)u^{2}-3(p-1)^{2}\sum_{u=1}^{\frac{p-1}{2}}\left(\frac{u}{p}\right)u-\frac{(p-1)^{3}}{2}\sum_{u=1}^{\frac{p-1}{2}}\left(\frac{u}{p}\right).

We have (for
p\equiv 7\pmod{8}
)

\displaystyle L(3,\chi_{p})

\displaystyle=\dfrac{i\sqrt{p}}{2i}\left(\dfrac{2\pi}{p}\right)^{3}\dfrac{L(-2,\chi_{p})}{\Gamma(3)\cos(\pi)}\quad\text{(see \cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{[Iwasawa]}{}{}, page 5]})}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{-2\sqrt{p}\pi^{3}}{p^{3}}L(-2,\chi_{p})=\dfrac{2\sqrt{p}\pi^{3}}{3p^{3}}B_{3,\chi}\quad\text{(see \cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{[Iwasawa]}{}{}, Theorem 1]})}.

Hence, by
[
4
, Theorem 13.15]
,

\sum\limits_{u=1}^{\frac{p-1}{2}}\left(\dfrac{u}{p}\right)u^{2}=\dfrac{-7\sqrt{p}p^{2}}{8\pi^{3}}L(3,\chi_{p})=-\dfrac{7}{12}B_{3,\chi},

and

\sum\limits_{u=\frac{p+1}{2}}^{p}\left(\dfrac{u}{p}\right)u^{2}=-p^{2}h(-p)+\dfrac{7}{12}B_{3,\chi}.

Thus

6(p+1)\sum_{u=\frac{p+1}{2}}^{p}\left(\frac{u}{p}\right)u^{2}-6(p-1)\sum_{u=1}^{\frac{p-1}{2}}\left(\frac{u}{p}\right)u^{2}=-6(p+1)p^{2}h(-p)+7pB_{3,\chi}.

One also has

\displaystyle-3(p+1)^{2}\sum_{u=\frac{p+1}{2}}^{p}\left(\frac{u}{p}\right)u+\frac{(p+1)^{3}}{2}\sum_{u=\frac{p+1}{2}}^{p}\left(\frac{u}{p}\right)-3(p-1)^{2}\sum_{u=1}^{\frac{p-1}{2}}\left(\frac{u}{p}\right)u-\frac{(p-1)^{3}}{2}\sum_{u=1}^{\frac{p-1}{2}}\left(\frac{u}{p}\right)

\displaystyle=3(p+1)^{2}ph(-p)-(p^{3}+3p)h(-p)=(2p^{3}+6p^{2})h(-p).

On the other hand, by
[
16
, page 10]

\displaystyle B_{3,\chi_{p}}

\displaystyle=p^{2}\sum_{u=1}^{p-1}\left(\dfrac{a}{p}\right)B_{3}(\frac{a}{p}-1)=p^{2}\left[\sum_{u=1}^{p-1}\left(\dfrac{a}{p}\right)\frac{a^{3}}{p^{3}}-\dfrac{3}{2}\sum_{u=1}^{p-1}\left(\dfrac{a}{p}\right)\frac{a^{2}}{p^{2}}+\dfrac{1}{2}\sum_{u=1}^{p-1}\left(\dfrac{a}{p}\right)\frac{a}{p}\right].

Thus

\displaystyle\sum_{u=1}^{p-1}\left(\dfrac{a}{p}\right)a^{3}

\displaystyle=pB_{3,\chi}+\dfrac{3}{2}p\sum_{u=1}^{p-1}\left(\dfrac{a}{p}\right)a^{2}-\dfrac{1}{2}p^{2}\sum_{u=1}^{p-1}\left(\dfrac{a}{p}\right)a

(k\;a_{k}k)
is a
2
-cycle and its signature is -1. In any case,
{\rm sgn}((k\;a_{k}k))=a_{k}
if
k\geq 3
.

If
a_{1}=a_{2}=1
then one has two 2-cycles
(1\;2)
and
(-1\;-2)
and the product of their signature is
1=a_{1}a_{2}
.
If
a_{1}=a_{2}=-1
then one has two 2-cycles
(1\;-2)
and
(-1\;2)
and the product of their signature is
1=a_{1}a_{2}
.
If
a_{1}=1,a_{2}=-1
then one has one 4-cycle
(1\;-2\;-1\;2)
and its signature is
-1=a_{1}a_{2}
.
If
a_{1}=-1,a_{2}=1
then one has one 4-cycle
(1\;2\;-1\;2)
and its signature is
-1=a_{1}a_{2}
.

Thus
{\rm sgn}(\tilde{\sigma})=a_{1}a_{2}a_{3}\cdots a_{n}
, as desired.
∎

A direct corollary of this lemma is the following.

Corollary 11.2
.

Suppose that the discriminant
\text{disc}(f_{\Delta})
of
f_{\Delta}
is a non-zero perfect square. Then the Galois group of
f_{\Delta}
is contained in the kernel of the map

\Sigma:({\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}})^{h_{\Delta}}\rtimes S_{h_{\Delta}}\to{\mathbb{Z}}/2.

Note further that the kernel is isomorphic to to
\ker(\Sigma^{\prime})\rtimes S_{h_{\Delta}}
, where
\Sigma^{\prime}
is the summation map

\Sigma^{\prime}:({\mathbb{Z}}/2)^{h_{\Delta}}\to{\mathbb{Z}}/2.

Proof.

Let
u_{1},u_{2},\ldots,u_{2h_{\Delta}}
be all the roots of
f_{\Delta}.
Let

A=\sqrt{\text{disc}(f)}=\prod_{i<j}(u_{j}-u_{i}).

For
\sigma\in{\rm Gal}({\mathbb{Q}}(f_{\Delta})/{\mathbb{Q}})
, we have
\sigma(A)=\text{sgn}(\sigma)A.
By our assumption,
A\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, so
\sigma
must be an even permutation. By Lemma
11.1
, we conclude that
\sigma\in\ker(\Sigma)
.
∎

Remark 11.3
.

A quite interesting consequence of this corollary is that when
\text{dics}(f)
is a perfect square, even though
f_{\Delta}
is expected to be irreducible over
{\mathbb{Z}}
, it is reducible over
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
for all prime
q.
In fact, if
f_{\Delta}
is irreducible modulo
q
then the Galois group of
f_{\Delta}
must contain a
2h_{\Delta}
-cycle. Since an
2h_{\Delta}
-cycle is an odd permutation, this contradicts the fact that all elements of the Galois group of
f_{\Delta}
are even.

Regarding the discriminant of
f_{\Delta}(x)
, we recall the following proposition.

Proposition 11.4
.

(
[
23
, Proposition 4.4]
)
Let
f
be a reciprocal polynomial of even degree
2n
over a field of characteristics different from
2
. Let
g
be the trace polynomial associated with
f
. Let
s=(-1)^{n}f(1)f(-1)
. Then

{\rm disc}(f)=s\times{\rm disc}(g)^{2}.

Consequently, if
s
is a perfect square then so is
{\rm disc}(f).

In
[
23
]
, we conjectured that the Galois group of
f_{\Delta}(x)
and
g_{\Delta}(x)
are as large as possible when
D=|\Delta|
is a prime number (see
[

); namely the Galois group of

is
S_{h_{\Delta}}
and the Galois group of
f_{\Delta}
is
({\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}})^{h_{\Delta}}\rtimes S_{h_{\Delta}}
. It is tempting to make a similar conjecture for all discriminant
\Delta
. Unfortunately, in our investigation, we found that it is not always the case. After some further investigation, we realize that this issue arises from the fact that, occasionally, there is an extra symmetry that puts some constraints on the Galois group of
f_{\Delta}
. More specifically, we discover some cases where the discriminant of
f_{\Delta}
is a perfect square. By Corollary
11.2
, the Galois group of

is contained in the kernel of the map

On the other hand, we also find that within the range of our data, it is always the case that the Galois group of
g_{\Delta}(x)
is
S_{h_{\Delta}}.
In this section, we provide some extensive data about the Galois groups of
f_{\Delta}(x)
and
g_{\Delta}(x).
As in
[
23
]
, when the Galois group of
f_{\Delta}=({\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}})^{h_{\Delta}}\rtimes S_{h_{\Delta}}
, we can detect it by using
[

. Similarly, when the Galois group of

is

, we can detect it by using

, Proposition 4.10]
.

To deal with the case where the discriminant of
f
is a square as discussed in Corollary
11.2
, we need the following proposition which is a variant of
[
23
, Proposition 4.14]
. We first introduce a lemma.

Lemma 11.5
.

Let
H
be a subgroup of
({\mathbb{Z}}/2)^{n}\rtimes S_{n}\subset S_{2n}
such that

(1)

The natural projection map
H\to S_{n}
is surjective.

(2)

H
contains a product of a
2
-cycle and another
4
-cycle (these two cycles are disjoint).

Then
H
contains
\ker(\Sigma^{\prime})\rtimes S_{n}
where
\Sigma^{\prime}
is the summation map

\Sigma^{\prime}:({\mathbb{Z}}/2)^{n}\to{\mathbb{Z}}/2.

Proof.

We we recall that we consider
({\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}})^{n}\rtimes S_{n}
as a subgroup of the symmetric group of
\{-n,-(n-1),\ldots,-1,1,\ldots,(n-1),n\}
by the following rules: an element
((a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}),\sigma)
of
({\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}})^{n}\rtimes S_{n}
is considered as a permutation
\tilde{\sigma}
which is defined by

\displaystyle\tilde{\sigma}(k)=a_{\sigma(k)}\sigma(k),1\leq k\leq n,\text{ and }\tilde{\sigma}(-k)=-a_{\sigma(k)}\sigma(k),1\leq k\leq n.

We can see that for all
k
,

(6)

\tilde{\sigma}(-k)=-\tilde{\sigma}(k).

By the second assumption, we can assume that, up to an ordering,
H
contains an element
\tilde{\sigma}=(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{n},\sigma)
of the form

\tilde{\sigma}=(n,-n)(k_{1},k_{2},k_{3},k_{4}),

where
k_{i}
are distinct and
|k_{i}|\in\{1,\ldots,n-1\}
for
1\leq i\leq 4.
By Equation
6
the 4-cycle
(-k_{1},-k_{2},-k_{3},-k_{4})
is factor of
\tilde{\sigma}
as well. Therefore, we must have

(k_{1},k_{2},k_{3},k_{4})=(-k_{1},-k_{2},-k_{3},-k_{4}).

We remark that
|k_{1}|\neq|k_{2}|
; otherwise
k_{1}\to k_{2}\to k_{1}
. Therefore, either
k_{3}=-k_{1}
or
k_{4}=-k_{1}.
The second case cannot happen because it will force
k_{2}=-k_{2}.
So,
k_{3}=-k_{1}
and consequently
k_{4}=-k_{2}.
In other words,
\tilde{\sigma}
is of the form

\tilde{\sigma}=(n,-n)(k_{1},k_{2},-k_{1},-k_{2}).

We then have

\tilde{\sigma}^{2}=(k_{1},-k_{1})(k_{2},-k_{2})\in H.

Let
1\leq i<j\leq n
. By the first assumption, we can find an element
\tilde{\tau}=(b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{n},\tau)\in H
such that

\tau=(k_{1},i)(k_{2},j).

We can see that

\tilde{\tau}\tilde{\sigma}^{2}\tilde{\tau}^{-1}=(\tilde{\tau}(k_{1}),\tilde{\tau}(-k_{1}))(\tilde{\tau}(k_{2}),\tilde{\tau}(-k_{2})=(i,-i)(j,-j).

This shows that the element
(c_{1},c_{2},\ldots,c_{n},1)\in H
where
c_{i}=c_{j}=-1
and
c_{k}=0
if
k\not\in\{i,j\}.
Consequently,
H
contains
\ker(\Sigma^{\prime})\times{1}.
By our assumption, the natural map
H\to S_{n}
is surjective, we can conclude that
H
contains
\ker(\Sigma^{\prime})\rtimes S_{n}.

∎

We then have the following proposition.

Proposition 11.6
.

Let
f(x)
be a monic reciprocal polynomial with integer coefficients of even degree
2n
. Let
g
be the trace polynomial of
f
. Assume that

(1)

The Galois group of
g
is
S_{n}.

(2)

There exists a prime number
q
such that
f(x)
has the following factorization in
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}(x)

f(x)=p_{2}(x)p_{4}(x)h(x),

where
p_{2}(x)
is an irreducible polynomial of degree
2
,
p_{4}(x)
is an irreducible polynomial of degree
4
, and
h(x)
is a product of distinct irreducible polynomials of odd degrees.

Then the Galois group of
f
contains
\ker(\Sigma^{\prime})\rtimes S_{n}.
Furthermore

(1)

If the discriminant of
f
is a perfect square then the Galois group of
f
is
\ker(\Sigma^{\prime})\rtimes S_{n}.

(2)

If the discriminant of
f
is not a perfect square then the Galois group of
f
is
({\mathbb{Z}}/2)^{n}\rtimes S_{n}
.

Proof.

Let
H
be the Galois group of
f
. The first condition and the second condition imply that
H
satisfies the hypothesis described in Lemma
11.5
. Therefore,
H
contains
\ker(\Sigma^{\prime})\rtimes S_{n}.
If the discriminant of
f
is a perfect square then Corollary
11.2
implies the Galois group of
f
is contained in
\ker(\Sigma^{\prime})\rtimes S_{n}.
Since we have inclusion on both ways, we conclude that
H=\ker(\Sigma^{\prime})\rtimes S_{n}.
If the discrimant of
f
is not a prefect square then
H
is strictly larger than
\ker(\Sigma^{\prime})\rtimes S_{n}
. Therefore, it must be
({\mathbb{Z}}/2)^{n}\rtimes S_{n}
.
∎

Corollary 11.7
.

Assume that
f
satisfies the hypotheses described in Proposition
11.6
. Then
f
is irreducible.

Proof.

First, suppose that
f
is a product of two reciprocal polynomials, say
f_{1}f_{2}
of degree
m_{1},m_{2}
respectively. We can assume that
m_{1},m_{2}
are both even. In fact, we have

(-1)^{m_{1}}f_{1}(-1)=f_{1}(-1).
If
m_{1}
is odd, then
f(-1)=0
. Consequently, we can rewrite
f(x)=h_{1}(x)h_{2}(x)
,
where
h_{1}(x)=\frac{h_{1}(x)}{x+1}
and
h_{2}(x)=(x+1)f_{2}(x)
. We can see that both
h_{1}(x)
and
h_{2}(x)
are reciprocal polynomials of even degrees.

Now assume that both
m_{1},m_{2}
are even. Let
g(x),g_{1}(x),g_{2}(x)
be the reciprocal polynomials of
f,f_{1},f_{2}
respectively. Then we have
g(x)=g_{1}(x)g_{2}(x).

This is a contradiction because as the Galois group of
g
is
S_{n}
,
g
must be irreducible. Now, suppose that
f
is not a product of two reciprocal polynomials. Then by
[
7
, Lemma 6]
, either
f
is irreducible over
{\mathbb{Z}}[x]
or
f
is of the form
f=ax^{n}h(x)h(\frac{1}{x})
, where
h\in{\mathbb{Q}}[x]
and
a\in{\mathbb{Q}}^{\times}.
However, this implies that the splitting field of
f
is the same as the splitting field of
h
. Consequently, the Galois group of
f
is at most
S_{n}.
This contradicts the fact that the Galois group of
f
is
\ker(\Sigma^{\prime})\rtimes S_{n}
as shown in Proposition
11.6
.
∎

We remark that by a similar argument, we have the following lemma.

Lemma 11.8
.

Let
H
be a subgroup of
({\mathbb{Z}}/2)^{n}\rtimes S_{n}\subset S_{2n}
such that

(1)

The natural projection map
H\to S_{n}
is surjective,

(2)

H
contains a
2
-cycle.

Then
H=({\mathbb{Z}}/2)^{n}\rtimes S_{n}
.

Proof.

By the second assumption, we can assume that, up to an ordering,
H
contains an element
\tilde{\sigma}
of the form

\tilde{\sigma}=(n,-n).

Let
1\leq i\leq n
. By the first assumption, we can find an element
\tilde{\tau}=(b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{n},\tau)\in H
such that

\tau=(n,i).

We can see that

\tilde{\tau}\tilde{\sigma}\tilde{\tau}^{-1}=(i,-i).

This shows that the element
(c_{1},c_{2},\ldots,c_{n},1)\in H
, where
c_{i}=-1
and
c_{k}=0
if
k\not\in\{i\}.
Consequently,
H
contains
({\mathbb{Z}}/2)^{n}\times{1}.
By our assumption, the natural map
H\to S_{n}
is surjective, we can conclude that
H
equals
({\mathbb{Z}}/2)^{n}\rtimes S_{n}.

∎

As a consequence, we have another test to detect the Galois group of
f_{\Delta}
when its Galois group is

Proposition 11.9
.

Let
f(x)
be a monic reciprocal polynomial with integer coefficients of even degree
2n
. Let
g
be the trace polynomial of
f

(1)

The Galois group of
g
is

(2)

There exists a prime number

Proposition 11.12

If

Proof.

One has

xf_{\Delta}(x)=\dfrac{F_{\Delta}(x)}{\Phi_{1}(x)}\frac{1}{\Phi_{3}(x)\Phi_{p}(x)}.

Taking the limit when

f_{\Delta}(1)=F^{\prime}_{\Delta}(1)\dfrac{1}{\Phi_{3}(1)\Phi_{p}(1)}=\dfrac{F^{\prime}_{\Delta}(1)}{D}=B_{1,\chi}.

Here, the last equality follows from Equation (
4

One has

where

On the other hand,
