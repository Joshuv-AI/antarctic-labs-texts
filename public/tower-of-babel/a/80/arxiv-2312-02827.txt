Computing k k -Crossing Visibility through k k -levels
Frank Duque

Abstract
Let 𝒜 \mathcal{A} be a set of straight lines in the plane (or planes in ℝ 3 \mathbb{R}^{3} ). The k k -crossing visibility of a point p p on 𝒜 \mathcal{A} is the set Q Q of points in the elements of 𝒜 \mathcal{A} such that the segment p ​ q pq , where q ∈ Q q\in Q , intersects at most k k elements of 𝒜 \mathcal{A} . In this paper, we present algorithms for computing the k k -crossing visibility. Specifically, we provide O ⁡ ( n ​ log ⁡ n + k ​ n ) O(n\log n+kn) and O ⁡ ( n ​ log ⁡ n + k 2 ​ n ) O(n\log n+k^{2}n) time algorithms for sets of n n lines in the plane and arrangements of n n planes in ℝ 3 \mathbb{R}^{3} , which are optimal for k = Ω ⁡ ( log ⁡ n ) k=\Omega(\log n) and k = Ω ⁡ ( log ⁡ n ) k=\Omega(\sqrt{\log n}) , respectively. We also introduce an algorithm for computing k k -crossing visibilities on polygons, which achieves the same asymptotic time complexity as the one presented by Bahoo et al. The techniques proposed in this paper can be easily adapted for computing k k -crossing visibilities on other instances where the ( ≤ k ) (\leq k) -level is known.

Let

3
Results in
\mathbb{R}^{3}

Let
\mathcal{D}
be the set of points
\left(x,y,z\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
such that
z\neq 0
. Throughout this section,
O
denotes the point
\left(0,0,0\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
and
\mathcal{T}
denotes the transformation
\mathcal{T}:\mathcal{D}\rightarrow\mathcal{D}
defined by

\mathcal{T}\left(\left(x,y,z\right)\right)=\left(x/z,y/z,1/z\right).

The proofs of Proposition
3.1
, Proposition
3.2
, Proposition
3.3
, Proposition
3.4
, Proposition
3.5
, and Lemma
3.6
can be obtained as in Section
2
.

Proposition 3.1
.

\mathcal{T}
is self-inverse.

Proof.

Let
\left(x,y,z\right)\in\mathcal{D}
, then

\mathcal{T}\circ\mathcal{T}(\left(x,y,z\right))=\mathcal{T}(\left(x/z,y/z,1/z\right))=\left(x,y,z\right)
.
∎

Proposition 3.2
.

\mathcal{T}
maps planes to planes. More precisely, if
\pi
is the plane in
\mathcal{D}
with equation
ax+by+cz+d=0
then
\mathcal{T}\left[\pi\right]
is the plane in
\mathcal{D}
with equation
ax+by+dz+c=0
.

Proof.

Let
\pi^{\prime}
be the plane with equation
ax+by+dz+c=0
. If
\left(x_{0},y_{0},z_{0}\right)\in\mathcal{D}
is in
\pi
then
ax_{0}+by_{0}+cz_{0}+d=0
; thus, as
a\frac{x_{0}}{z_{0}}+b\frac{y_{0}}{z_{0}}+d\frac{1}{z_{0}}+c=0
, then
\mathcal{T}\left(x_{0},y_{0},z_{0}\right)
is in
\pi^{\prime}
. Similarly, if
\left(x_{0},y_{0},z_{0}\right)\in\mathcal{D}
is in
\pi^{\prime}
then
\mathcal{T}^{-1}\left(x_{0},y_{0},z_{0}\right)=\mathcal{T}\left(x_{0},y_{0},z_{0}\right)
is in
\pi
.
∎

Proposition 3.3
.

\mathcal{T}
preserves incidences between points and planes. More precisely, the point
p
is on the plane
\pi
in
\mathcal{D}
if and only if
\mathcal{T}(p)
is on the plane
\mathcal{T}\left[\pi\right]
in
\mathcal{D}
.

Proof.

Let
p=(x_{0},y_{0},z_{0})
be a point in
\mathcal{D}
and let
\pi:ax+by+cz+d=0
be a plane in
\mathcal{D}
. This proof follows from the fact that
(x_{0},y_{0},z_{0})
satisfies
ax+by+cz+d=0
if and only if
\left(\frac{x_{0}}{z_{0}},\frac{y_{0}}{z_{0}},\frac{1}{z_{0}}\right)
satisfies
\mathcal{T}\left[\pi\right]:ax+by+dz+c=0
.
∎

Given a plane
\pi
in
\mathcal{D}
, we denote by
\pi^{+}
the set of points in
\pi
whose third coordinate is greater than zero, and we denote by
\pi^{-}
the set of points in
\pi
whose third coordinate is less than zero.

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
\pi
be a plane in
\mathcal{D}
. Then
\mathcal{T}\left[\pi^{+}\right]=\mathcal{T}\left[\pi\right]^{+}
and
\mathcal{T}\left[\pi^{-}\right]=\mathcal{T}\left[\pi\right]^{-}
. Moreover, if
p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{k}
are in
\pi^{+}
(or they are in
\pi^{-}
) ordered by their distance to the plane
z=0
from the closest to the furthest, then
\mathcal{T}(p_{1}),\mathcal{T}(p_{2}),\ldots,\mathcal{T}(p_{k})
are in
\mathcal{T}\left[\pi^{+}\right]
(or they are in
\mathcal{T}\left[\pi^{-}\right]
, respectively), ordered by their distance to the plane
z=0
from the furthest to the closest.

Proof.

As
\mathcal{T}
maps planes to planes and it does not change the sign of the third coordinate, then
\mathcal{T}\left[\pi^{+}\right]=\mathcal{T}\left[\pi\right]^{+}
and
\mathcal{T}\left[\pi^{-}\right]=\mathcal{T}\left[\pi\right]^{-}
. If the third coordinates of
p_{i}
and
p_{j}
are
z_{i}
and
z_{j}
, respectively, then the third coordinates of
\mathcal{T}(p_{i})
and
\mathcal{T}(p_{j})
are
1/z_{i}
and
1/z_{j}
, respectively. This proof follows from the fact that
|z_{i}|<|z_{j}|
if and only if
|1/z_{i}|>|1/z_{j}|
.
∎

Given a set
D\subset\mathcal{D}
, we denote by
\overline{D}
the closure of
D
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
L
be a straight line in
\mathcal{D}
. Then
O\in\overline{L}
if and only if
\mathcal{T}\left[L\right]
is a vertical line.

Proof.

As
\mathcal{T}\left(td_{1},td_{2},td_{3}\right)=\left(\frac{d_{1}}{d_{3}},\frac{d_{2}}{d_{3}},\frac{1}{td_{3}}\right)
,
\mathcal{T}^{-1}\left(a,b,t\right)=\mathcal{T}\left(a,b,t\right)=\frac{1}{t}\left(a,b,1\right)
,
\mathcal{T}
is self-inverse and
O\in\overline{L}
if and only if
\mathcal{T}\left[L\right]
is a vertical line.
∎

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a set of planes. Then:

1.

\mathcal{V}_{k}^{+}\left(O,\mathcal{A}\right)=\mathcal{T}\left[{(\leq k)level^{+}}\left(\mathcal{T}\left[\mathcal{A}\right]\right)\right]
.

2.

\mathcal{V}_{k}^{-}\left(O,\mathcal{A}\right)=\mathcal{T}\left[{(\leq k)level^{-}}\left(\mathcal{T}\left[\mathcal{A}\right]\right)\right]
.

Proof.

Similar to the proof of Lemma
2.9
.
∎
