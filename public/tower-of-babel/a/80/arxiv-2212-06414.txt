Hong-Yan Zhang, Fei Liu, Yu Zhou, Man Liang

Abstract
Quaternion kinematical differential equation (QKDE) plays a key role in navigation, control and guidance systems. Although explicit symplectic geometric algorithms (ESGA) for this problem are available, there is a lack of a unified way for constructing high order symplectic difference schemes with configurable order parameter and the fractional interval sampling problem should be treated carefully. We present even order explicit symplectic geometric algorithms to solve the QKDE with diagonal Padé approximation via a four-step strategy. Firstly, the Padé-Cayley lemma is proved and used to simplify the symplectic Padé approximation for the linear Hamiltonian system with infinitesimal symplectic structure. Secondly, both parallel and alternative iterative methods are proposed to construct the symplectic difference schemes with even order accuracy. Thirdly, the symplecity, orthogonality and invertibility of the single-step transition matrices are proved rigorously. Finally, the explicit symplectic geometric algorithms are designed for both the linear time-invariant and linear time-varying QKDE. The maximum absolute error for solving the QKDE is 𝒪 ⁡ ( ( t f − t 0 ) ​ τ 2 ​ ℓ ) \mathcal{O}((t_{f}-t_{0})\tau^{2\ell}) where τ \tau is the time step, ℓ \ell is the order parameter and [ t 0 , t f ] [t_{0},t_{f}] is the time span. The linear time complexity and constant space complexity of computation as well as the simple algorithmic structure show that our algorithms are appropriate for real-time applications in aeronautics, astronautics, robotics and so on. The performance of the proposed algorithms are verified and validated by mathematical analysis and numerical simulation. Keywords : Inertia measurement unit (IMU); Guidance navigation and control; Quaternion kinematical differential equation (QKDE); Symplectic geometric algorithm (SGA); Symplectic difference scheme (SDS); Diagonal Padé approximation; Mirrored Cayley transform

For non-negative integers

\left\{\begin{aligned} N_{\ell m}(x)&=\sum^{m}_{k=0}\frac{(\ell+m-k)!m!}{(\ell+m)!k!(m-k)!}x^{k},\\

(7)

The

\exp(x)=\frac{N_{\ell\ell}(x)}{D_{\ell\ell}(x)}+\mathcal{O}(\left|x\right|^{2\ell+1})

\left\{\begin{aligned} N_{\ell\ell}(x)&=\sum^{\ell}_{k=0}\frac{(2\ell-k)!\ell!}{(2\ell)!k!(\ell-k)!}x^{k}\\
D_{\ell\ell}(x)&=\sum^{\ell}_{k=0}\frac{(2\ell-k)!\ell!}{(2\ell)!k!(\ell-k)!}(-x)^{k}.\end{aligned}\right.

Let

P_{\ell}(x)=\sum^{\ell}_{k=0}\dfrac{(2\ell-k)!}{(2\ell)!}\cdot\dfrac{\ell!}{(\ell-k)!}\cdot\dfrac{x^{k}}{k!},

(10)

then both the nominator

and denominator

can be expressed by the

N_{\ell\ell}(x)=P_{\ell}(x),D_{\ell\ell}(x)=P_{\ell}(-x).

Simple algebraic operations on factorials show that

\begin{split}P_{\ell}(x)&=1+\sum^{\ell}_{k=1}\frac{(2\ell-k)!}{(2\ell)!}\cdot\frac{\ell!}{(\ell-k)!}\cdot\frac{x^{k}}{k!}\\
&=1+\sum^{\ell-1}_{k=0}x^{k+1}\prod^{k}_{r=0}\frac{\ell-r}{(2\ell-r)(r+1)}\end{split}

2.3
Cayley Transform and Euler’s Formula

We now cite two lemmas about mirrored Cayley transform and Euler’s formula proved in
[
35
]
.

Lemma 2

(
Generalized Euler’s formula
)
.

Suppose matrix
\bm{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{{n}\times{n}}
is skew-symmetric, i.e.,
{\bm{M}}^{\textsf{T}}=-\bm{M}
, and there exists
a positive constant
\gamma
such that
\bm{M}^{2}=-\gamma^{2}\bm{I}
. Let
\hat{\bm{M}}={\gamma}^{-1}\bm{M}
, then
\hat{\bm{M}}^{2}=-\bm{I}
.
For any
y\in\mathbb{R}
, we have

\mathrm{e}^{y\bm{M}}=\cos(y\gamma)\bm{I}+\sin(y\gamma)\hat{\bm{M}}.

(22)

Lemma 3

(
Cayley-Euler formula
)
.

Suppose matrix
\bm{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{{n}\times{n}}
is skew-symmetric, i.e.,
{\bm{M}}^{\textsf{T}}=-\bm{M}
, and there exists
a positive constant
\gamma
such that
\bm{M}^{2}=-\gamma^{2}\bm{I}
. Let
\hat{\bm{M}}={\gamma}^{-1}\bm{M}
, then
\hat{\bm{M}}^{2}=-\bm{I}
.
Let

{\varphi}_{\mathrm{mct}}(\lambda)=\frac{1+\lambda}{1-\lambda}=(1+\lambda)(1-\lambda)^{-1}

(23)

be the
mirrored Cayley transform, then for any
y\in\mathbb{R}
the Cayley-Euler formula

\begin{split}{\varphi}_{\mathrm{mct}}(y\bm{M})&=\frac{\bm{I}+y\bm{M}}{\bm{I}-y\bm{M}}=\frac{1}{1+\alpha}[(1-\alpha)\bm{I}+2y\bm{M}]\\
&=\cos\delta(y,\gamma)\bm{I}+\sin\delta(y,\gamma)\hat{\bm{M}}\\
&=\mathrm{e}^{\delta(y,\gamma)\bm{M}}\end{split}

(24)

holds, in which

\begin{cases}\delta=\delta(y,\gamma)=2\cdot\arctan(y\gamma)\\
\alpha=\alpha(y,\gamma)=y^{2}\gamma^{2}\end{cases}

(25)

Furthermore,
{\varphi}_{\mathrm{mct}}(y\bm{M})
is an orthogonal matrix.

3.1
Relation of Padé Approximation and Cayley Transform

Generally, the Padé approximation involves the ratio of polynomials with the same order. It is interesting that the expression of Padé approximation can be simplified into the mirrored Cayley transform for solving the QKDE.
We now prove some interesting lemmas and theorems for constructing the SDS for LTI-QKDE.

Lemma 4

(
Padé-Cayley
)
.

If there exists some positive constant
c
such that

, then each diagonal Padé approximation
{g}_{\mathrm{DPA}}^{\ell}(x)

{g}_{\mathrm{DPA}}^{\ell}(x)=\frac{1+\beta(\ell,c)x}{1-\beta(\ell,c)x}={\varphi}_{\mathrm{mct}}\left(\beta(\ell,c)x\right),

(26)

where
\beta(\ell,c)

is a constant determined by
\ell
and

.

: Let

\left\lfloor{x}\right\rfloor=m
in which
\exists m\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
m\leq x<m+1
, and

s_{1}=\left\lfloor{(\ell-1)/2}\right\rfloor,\quad s_{2}=\left\lfloor{\ell/2}\right\rfloor,

(27)

be two integer parameters, we immediately have

s_{2}=s_{1}+1-\mod(\ell,2)=\begin{cases}s_{1},&\quad 2\nmid\ell;\\
s_{1}+1,&\quad 2\mid\ell.\end{cases}

(28)

When

, equation (
12

\begin{split}P_{\ell}(x)&=1+\sum^{\ell-1}_{k=0}x^{k+1}\prod^{k}_{r=0}\eta^{\ell}_{r}\\
&=1+\sum^{s_{1}}_{j=0}x^{2j+1}\prod^{2j}_{r=0}\eta^{\ell}_{r}+\sum^{s_{2}}_{j=1}x^{2j}\prod^{2j-1}_{r=0}\eta^{\ell}_{r}\\
&=1+\sum^{s_{2}}_{j=1}(-c)^{j}\prod^{2j-1}_{r=0}\eta^{\ell}_{r}+x\sum^{s_{1}}_{j=0}(-c)^{j}\prod^{2j}_{r=0}\eta^{\ell}_{r}.\end{split}

(29)

Let

\left\{\begin{split}a^{\ell}_{j}&=\prod^{2j}_{r=0}\eta^{\ell}_{r},\quad 0\leq j\leq s_{1}\\

b^{\ell}_{0}&=1,\end{split}\right.

(30)

and

\left\{\begin{aligned} n(s_{1},x)&=\sum^{s_{1}}_{j=0}a^{\ell}_{j}x^{j}\\
d(s_{2},x)&=\sum^{s_{2}}_{j=0}b^{\ell}_{j}x^{j}\end{aligned}\right.

By substituting (

Obviously, (

(33)

by substituting (

(

\displaystyle{g}_{\mathrm{DPA}}^{\ell}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{P_{\ell}(x)}{P_{\ell}(-x)}=\frac{d(s_{2},-c)+x\cdot n(s_{1},-c)}{d(s_{2},-c)-x\cdot n(s_{1},-c)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1+\beta(\ell,c)x}{1-\beta(\ell,c)x}={\varphi}_{\mathrm{mct}}\left(\beta(\ell,c)x\right).

according to (

Lemma 5
.

The diagonal Padé approximation of order
\ell
for the transition matrix for the LTI-QKDE is

{g}_{\mathrm{DPA}}^{\ell}\left(\frac{\tau}{2}\bm{\Omega}\right)={\varphi}_{\mathrm{mct}}\left(\beta(\ell,c)\frac{\tau}{2}\bm{\Omega}\right)=\frac{\bm{I}+\beta(\ell,c)\frac{\tau}{2}\bm{\Omega}}{\bm{I}-\beta(\ell,c)\frac{\tau}{2}\bm{\Omega}}

(38)

where

c=\left\lVert\bm{\omega}\right\rVert^{2}\tau^{2}/4.

(39)

Proof
: Equation (
39
) implies that

\left(\tau\bm{\Omega}/2\right)^{2}=-c\bm{I}
. Thus by Lemma
4
we can find that (
38
) holds.

Theorem 6
.

For any time step
\tau
and time-invariant vector
\bm{\omega}
, the transition matrix for the LTI-QKDE is

\begin{split}\bm{G}(\ell,\tau)&={g}_{\mathrm{DPA}}^{\ell}\left(\frac{\tau}{2}\bm{\Omega}\right)\\
&=\frac{1}{1+\alpha(\ell,c)}\left[\left(1-\alpha(\ell,c)\right)\bm{I}+\tau\beta(\ell,c)\bm{\Omega}\right]\\
&=\cos\delta(\ell,\bm{\omega},\tau)\bm{I}+\sin\delta(\ell,\bm{\omega},\tau)\hat{\bm{\Omega}},\end{split}

(40)

in which

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\alpha(\ell,c)=c[\beta(\ell,c)]^{2},\\
&\delta(\ell,\bm{\omega},\tau)=2\arctan(\beta(\ell,c)\left\lVert\bm{\omega}\right\rVert\tau/2),\\
&\hat{\bm{\Omega}}=\bm{\Omega}/\left\lVert\bm{\omega}\right\rVert,\\
&\hat{\bm{\Omega}}^{2}=-\bm{I}.\end{aligned}\right.

(41)

Proof
: Let

\displaystyle y=\tau\beta(\ell,c)/2,\quad\gamma=\left\lVert\bm{\omega}\right\rVert,\quad\delta=2\arctan(y\gamma),

then
\bm{\Omega}^{2}=-\gamma^{2}\bm{I}
and

\alpha(\ell,c)=y^{2}\gamma^{2}=c[\beta(\ell,c)]^{2}.

Let
\bm{M}=\bm{\Omega}
, then this theorem follows from the
Lemma
3
directly. Q.E.D.

Theorem 7
.

For any
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
, constant
\bm{\omega}\in\mathbb{R}^{{3}\times{1}}
and any
\tau\in\mathbb{R}
, the transition matrix
\bm{G}(\ell,\tau)

is an orthogonal, invertible and symplectic transformation with
2\ell
-th order accuracy, i.e.,

\bm{G}(\ell,\tau)^{-1}=\bm{G}(\ell,-\tau)={\bm{G}(\ell,\tau)}^{\textsf{T}}

(42)

for the LTI-QKDE.

Proof
: For a time-invariant vector
\bm{\omega}
, the function
\delta(\ell,\bm{\omega},\tau)
is an odd function of
\tau
. With the help of
\hat{\bm{\Omega}}^{2}=-\bm{I}
and
{\hat{\bm{\Omega}}}^{\textsf{T}}=-\hat{\bm{\Omega}}
, we immediately obtain

\displaystyle{\bm{G}(\ell,\tau)}^{\textsf{T}}

\displaystyle={[\cos\delta(\ell,\bm{\omega},\tau)\bm{I}+\sin\delta(\ell,\bm{\omega},\tau)\hat{\bm{\Omega}}]}^{\textsf{T}}

\displaystyle=\cos(-\delta(\ell,\bm{\omega},\tau))\bm{I}+\sin(-\delta(\ell,\bm{\omega},\tau))\hat{\bm{\Omega}}

\displaystyle=\bm{G}(\ell,-\tau),

which implies that the transition matrix is invertible. Moreover,
\bm{G}(\ell,\tau)
is orthogonal by
Lemma
3
. In consequence,
{\bm{G}(\ell,\tau)}^{\textsf{T}}=\bm{G}(\ell,-\tau)=\bm{G}(\ell,\tau)^{-1}
.

Since the
\bm{G}(\ell,\tau)
is constructed from the Padé approximation
{g}_{\mathrm{DPA}}^{\ell}(\cdot)
directly according to Theorem
1
, it must be a SDS with
2\ell
-th order accuracy. Q.E.D.

3.5
2\ell
-th order SDS for LTV-QKDE

For the time-varying vector
\bm{\omega}(t)
, the LTV-QKDE is a non-autonomous Hamiltonian system essentially and the corresponding SGA can be obtained via the concept of extended phase space and time-centered difference scheme (See
[
44
]
, Chapter 5).
Let
\bar{t}_{k}=t[k]+\tau/2,\bm{\omega}_{k}=\bm{\omega}(\bar{t}_{k}),\bm{\Omega}_{k}=\bm{\Omega}(\bm{\omega}_{k})
with the similar procedure as we do
in finding the transition matrix for SDS of the LTI-QKDE.
However, we have to treat the discrete time sequence
t_{0},t_{0}+\frac{\tau}{2},t_{0}+\frac{3\tau}{2},\cdots,t_{0}+\frac{(2k+1)\tau}{2},t_{0}+\frac{(2k+3)\tau}{2},\cdots

is not equal to that of
[t_{0}+\frac{(2k+1)\tau}{2},t_{0}+\frac{(2k+3)\tau}{2}]
. In order to avoid this phenomena and the fractional sampling problem, we use the following modified version of the discrete vectors
\bm{\omega}_{k}
and matrices
\bm{\Omega}_{k}

\bm{\omega}_{k}=\bm{\omega}(t[k]),\quad\bm{\Omega}_{k}=\bm{\Omega}(\bm{\omega}_{k}),

(43)

thus we can obtain the
k
-th transition matrix at time

\begin{split}\bm{G}^{k+1}_{k}(\ell,\tau)&=g_{\ell}\left(\tau\bm{\Omega}_{k}/2\right)=\phi\left(\beta(\ell,c_{k})\tau\bm{\Omega}_{k}/2\right)\end{split}

(44)

for the LTV-QKDE. By Lemma
3
, it is easy to prove the following theorem.

Theorem 8
.

For the LTV-QKDE
\dfrac{\dif\mathbbm{q}}{\dif t}=\frac{1}{2}\bm{\Omega}[\bm{\omega}(t)]\mathbbm{q}
, let

\left\{\begin{aligned} c_{k}&=\tau^{2}\left\lVert\bm{\omega}_{k}\right\rVert^{2}/4\\

which generates a SDS with

-th order accuracy for

4.5
Analysis of Computational Complexity

Let

\timer_{*}{(\texttt{expr})}
and

\timer_{+}{(\texttt{expr})}

be the times of multiplication and addition in some operation expression
expr
. The
time complexity vector of computation
(TCVC) for
expr
is defined by

\timer(\texttt{expr})=[\timer_{*}{(\texttt{expr})},\timer_{+}{(\texttt{expr})}].

(47)

Note that we just list two components of
\timer(\texttt{expr})
here since for the problems and algorithms which contain massive matrix-vector operations, the multiplication and addition operations for real numbers are fundamental and essential.

Similarly, we use
T(\texttt{expr})
to denote the computation time for
expr
. We also use
T_{*}(\texttt{expr})
and
T_{+}(\texttt{expr})
to represent the computation time for the multiplications and additions involved in
expr
.
Suppose that the time units for multiplication and addition are
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
respectively. Let

\bm{u}=[u_{1},u_{2}]

(48)

be the vector of time units. Then the time for computing
expr
will be

\begin{split}T(\texttt{expr})&=\langle\timer(\texttt{expr})|\bm{u}\rangle\\
&=\timer_{*}{(\texttt{expr})}u_{1}+\timer_{+}{(\texttt{expr})}u_{2}\\
&=T_{*}(\texttt{expr})+T_{+}(\texttt{expr})\end{split}

(49)

if only multiplication and addition are essential for the total time consumed.

For an algorithm named with
Alg
, its TCVC is defined by

\begin{split}\timer{(\textsc{Alg})}&=\sum_{\texttt{expr}\in\textsc{Alg}}\timer{(\texttt{expr})}\\
&=[\timer_{*}{(\textsc{Alg})},\timer_{+}{(\textsc{Alg})}].\end{split}

(50)

The time needed for algorithm
Alg
can be measured by

T(\textsc{Alg})=\langle\timer{(\textsc{Alg})}|\bm{u}\rangle=\timer_{*}{(\textsc{Alg})}u_{1}+\timer_{+}{(\textsc{Alg})}u_{2}

(51)

theoretically with acceptable accuracy.

It is easy to check that the TCVC for Algorithm
4.1
for computing
\eta^{\ell}_{k}=\textsc{Eta}(\ell,k)
is

\timer{(\textsc{Eta})}=\sum_{\texttt{expr}\in\textsc{Eta}}\timer{(\texttt{expr})}=[3,3].

(52)

The TCVC for computing the polynomial
\displaystyle p_{s}(x)=\sum^{s}_{i=0}c_{i}x^{i}
via Horne’s rule is

\timer{(\textsc{Polynomial})}=\sum_{\texttt{expr}\in\textsc{Polynomial}}\timer{(\texttt{expr})}=[s,s]

(53)

Simple algebraic operations show that The TCVC for

4.1
and
Algorithm

&=s_{1}[11,9]+s_{2}[11,8]+[5,3]\\
&=[11(s_{1}+s_{2})+5,9s_{1}+8s_{2}+3]\\

E_{0}(\ell,\tau)=\left\lVert{\mathbbm{q}}^{\mathrm{AS}}[0]-{\mathbbm{q}}^{\mathrm{NS}}[0]\right\rVert=0

because of
{\mathbbm{q}}^{\mathrm{AS}}[0]={\mathbbm{q}}^{\mathrm{NS}}[0]=\mathbbm{q}[0]
.
In consequence,

\begin{split}E_{k}(\ell,\tau)&=\left\lVert{\mathbbm{q}}^{\mathrm{AS}}[k]-{\mathbbm{q}}^{\mathrm{NS}}[k]\right\rVert\\
&\leq\left\lVert\bm{E}_{\mathcal{G}}\right\rVert+E_{k-1}(\ell,\tau)\\
&\leq k\left\lVert\bm{E}_{\mathcal{G}}\right\rVert\end{split}

(75)

for any
k
by induction. Therefore,

\begin{split}E_{max}(\ell,\tau)&\leq 2k_{max}(\left|f_{1}(\ell,x)\right|+\left|f_{2}(\ell,x)\right|)\\
&=2\left\lfloor{\frac{t_{f}-t_{0}}{\tau}}\right\rfloor(\left|f_{1}(\ell,x)\right|+\left|f_{2}(\ell,x)\right|)\\
&=\mathcal{O}((t_{f}-t_{0})\tau^{2\ell}).\end{split}

(76)

This implies that the maximum absolute error
E_{max}(\ell,\tau)
has an upper bound with order
\mathcal{O}(\tau^{2\ell})
for fixed value of
t_{f}-t_{0}
.

5.4
Discussion

For the purpose of verification and validation, our emphasis is put on
numerical simulation and system simulation since strict mathematical proofs and analysis are provided in this paper. For the readers who cares about real-world or benchmark problems, please see
[
11
]
,
[
12
]
and so on. This work is a big extension of
[
35
]
, thus the comparison of SDS and traditional difference scheme is omitted. We remark that the adaptive step size method
[
49
]
is not considered since it increases the complexity when implementing the SDS with hardware due to the fixed sampling frequency of IMU and it is not symplectic.

Our work is based on the single step numerical method driven by the real-time computation and hardware implementation. The multiple steps method is not considered in this paper. The typical multiple steps method is the
triple-jump composition
method popularized by
Creutz
[
50
]
, Yosida
[
51
]
and Suzuki
[
52
]
. Furthermore, Yosida’s symplectic method is based on the separability of the Hamiltonian which is not satisfied for the QKDE, neither Suzuki’s method nor Creutz’s method is symplectic.

We also remark that the computational costs of division operation and multiplication are not the same in general. However, the analysis of computational cost with the time complexity vector of computation
\bm{u}=[u_{+},u_{*},u_{\div}]=[u_{1},u_{2},u_{3}]
instead of
\bm{u}=[u_{+},u_{*}]=[u_{1},u_{2}]
will lead to the same computational complexity. In consequence, it is enough to consider the addition and multiplication for the operations of float numbers. The essence lies in whether there are nested loops or not in the algorithms instead of the difference of division and multiplication.

6
Conclusions

The essential observations for solving the QKDE with high order ESGA include four aspects:

•

the general LTV-QKDE can be modeled by the linear Hamiltonian system with infinitesimal symplectic structure;

•

the symplectic Padé approximation can be used to construct the SDS for QKDE with
2\ell
-th order accuracy;

•

the symplectic transform (single-step transition operator) can be simplified by the Padé-Cayley lemma;

•

the parameters for the SDS can be obtained with parallel and alternative iterative methods;

The performance of algorithms —
EoEsgaQkdeLTV
and
EoEsgaQkdeLTI
— are attractive due to the following reasons:

•

there are no accumulative computational errors in the sense of long term time since they are symplectic;

•

the order parameter
\ell
is configurable, which benefits the designer of practical engineering systems such as aircraft, spacecrafts and so on according to the acceptable angular velocity;

•

the time complexity of computation for the algorithms proposed is linear while the space complexity of computation is constant, thus our algorithms are appropriate for real-time applications;

•

the maximum absolute error when solving the LTI-QKDE is controlled by the time span
t_{f}-t_{0}
, time step
\tau
and the order parameter
\ell
through the relation
E_{max}(\ell,\tau)=\mathcal{O}((t_{f}-t_{0})\tau^{2\ell})
, and the simulation result shows that this relation also holds for some LTV-QKDE.

Since the LTI-QKDE is a special case of LTV-QKDE and the
EoEsgaQkdeLTV
surpasses the algorithms ESGA-I and ESGA-II proposed in
[
35
]
, it is enough for us to use the
EoEsgaQkdeLTV
algorithm for solving QKDE. Generally,
\ell=2
is enough (which means fourth order precision) for the applications with low speed and
\ell=5
is enough (which means tenth order precision) for the applications with high speed.
