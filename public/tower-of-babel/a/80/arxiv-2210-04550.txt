Almost perfect linear Lee codes of packing radius 2 2 only exist for small dimensions
Zijian Zhou 1, Yue Zhou 1, † \dagger

Abstract
. It is conjectured by Golomb and Welch around half a century ago that there is no perfect Lee codes C C of packing radius r r in ℤ n \mathbb{Z}^{n} for r ≥ 2 r\geq 2 and n ≥ 3 n\geq 3 . Recently, Leung and the second author proved this conjecture for linear Lee codes with r = 2 r=2 . A natural question is whether it is possible to classify the second best, i.e., almost perfect linear Lee codes of packing radius 2 2 . We show that if such codes exist in ℤ n \mathbb{Z}^{n} , then n n must be 1 , 2 , 11 , 29 , 47 , 56 , 67 , 79 , 104 , 121 , 134 1,2,11,29,47,56,67,79,104,121,134 or 191 191 .



1.
Introduction

Let
\mathbb{V}
be a metric space. A code
\mathcal{C}
of
\mathbb{V}
is just a subset of points in
\mathbb{V}
. The code
\mathcal{C}
is a
perfect code
with radius
r
, if for any point
x
in
\mathbb{V}
, there is exactly one point in
\mathcal{C}
with distance at most
r
from
x
. Usually, perfect codes yields beautiful mathematical structures and they also have very important applications in the associated metric spaces.

In this paper, we are interested in perfect and almost perfect codes with respect to the Lee metric. For other interesting topics on perfect codes, we refer to the monograph
[
4
]
by Etzion. Let
\mathbb{Z}
denote the ring of integers. For
x=(x_{1},\cdots,x_{n})
and
y=(y_{1},\cdots,y_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
, the
Lee metric
(also known as
\ell_{1}
-norm, taxicab metric, rectilinear distance or Manhattan distance) between them is defined by

d_{L}(x,y)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}|x_{i}-y_{i}|\text{ for }x,y\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}.

A Lee code
\mathcal{C}
is called
linear
, if
\mathcal{C}
is a lattice in
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
. The
minimum distance

d(\mathcal{C})
of a Lee code
\mathcal{C}
containing at least two elements is defined to be
\min\{d_{L}(x,y):x,y\in\mathcal{C},x\neq y\}
.

Lee codes have many practical applications such as interleaving schemes
[
2
]
, constrained and partial-response channels
[
13
]
, and flash memory
[
14
]
.

By definition, a Lee code
\mathcal{C}
is perfect if and only if

\mathbb{Z}^{n}=\dot{\bigcup}_{c\in\mathcal{C}}(S(n,r)+c),

where

S(n,r):=\left\{(x_{1},\cdots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}:\sum_{i=1}^{n}|x_{i}|\leq r\right\}

is the Lee sphere of radius
r
centered at the origin and
S(n,r)+c:=\{v+c:v\in S(n,r)\}
. In another word, the existence of a perfect Lee code is equivalent to a tiling of
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
by Lee spheres of radius
r
. The cardinality of
S(n,r)
has been determined in
[
5
,
15
]
as follows

|S(n,r)|=\sum_{i=0}^{\text{min}\{n,r\}}2^{i}\binom{n}{i}\binom{r}{i}.

It is worth pointing out that a perfect linear Lee code is also equivalent to an abelian Cayley graph of degree
2n
and diameter
r
whose number of vertices meets the so-called
abelian Cayley Moore bound
; see
[
3
,
19
]
. For more results about the degree-diameter problems in graph theory, we refer to the survey
[
11
]
.

It has been showed by Golomb and Welch
[
5
]
that perfect Lee codes exist for
n=1,2
and every positive integer
r
, and for
n\geq 3
and
r=1
. In the same paper, Golomb and Welch also proposed the conjecture that there are no more perfect Lee codes for other choices of
n
and
r
. To the best of our knowledge, this conjecture is still far from being solved, despite many efforts and various approaches applied on it. For more history and results about this conjecture, we refer to the survey
[
8
]
and the references therein.

In recent years, there are some important progress on the existence of perfect linear Lee code for small
r
. For
r=2
, Leung and the second author
[
10
]

proved that perfect linear Lee codes only exist for
n=1,2
. For
r\geq 2
, Zhang and Ge
[
18
]
, and Qureshi
[
12
]
have obtained many nonexistence result by extending the symmetric polynomial method introduced by Kim
[
9
]
for
r=2
. It is worth pointing out that by
[
16
]
the linearity assumption of a nonexistence result for perfect linear Lee codes of radius
r
in
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
can be removed provided that the size of Lee sphere
S(n,r)
is a prime number.

Obviously, a Lee code
\mathcal{C}
is perfect implies that the packing of
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
by
S(n,r)
with centers consisting of all the elements in
\mathcal{C}
is of packing density
1
.
As there is no perfect linear Lee code for
r=2
and
n\geq 3
by
[
10
]
, one natural question is whether the second best is possible. More precisely, one may ask the following question for any
r\geq 2
.

Question 1.1
.

Does there exist a lattice packing of
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
by
S(n,r)
with density
\frac{|S(n,r)|}{|S(n,r)|+1}
?

If such a lattice packing exists, we call the associated linear Lee code
almost perfect
. For convenience, we abbreviate the term almost perfect linear Lee code to
APLL
code throughout the rest of this paper.

The
packing radius
(
covering radius
, resp.) of a Lee code
C
is defined to be the largest (smallest resp.) integer
r^{\prime}
such that for any element
w\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
there exists at most (at least, resp.) one codeword
c\in C
with
d_{L}(w,c)\leq r^{\prime}
. It is clear that a Lee code is perfect if and only if its packing radius and covering radius are the same.
For an APLL code with packing density
\frac{|S(n,r)|}{|S(n,r)|+1}
, its
packing radius and covering radius are
r
and
r+1
, respectively.

It is easy to see that
\mathcal{C}=\{x(2r+2):x\in\mathbb{Z}\}
is the unique APLL code of packing radius
r
in
\mathbb{Z}
for any
r
.

Figure 1.
Packing of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
by
L(2,2)
associated with an APLL code

Let
L(n,r)
denote the union of
n
-cubes centered at each point of
S(n,r)
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. It is clear that each packing of
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
by
S(n,r)
corresponds to a packing of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
by
L(n,r)
. Figure
1
depicts the lattice packing of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
by
L(2,2)
associated with a lattice generated by
\{(1,4),(3,-2)\}
in
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
. Clearly the packing density is
\frac{|S(2,2)|}{|S(2,2)|+1}=\frac{13}{14}
. Therefore this lattice is an APLL code in
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
. It is not difficult to show that the minimum distance of an APLL code in
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
is
2r+1
for
n>1
and
2r+2
for
n=1
.

In
[
17
]
, the following result on the nonexistence of APLL codes of packing radius
2
has been obtained.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
n
be a positive integer. If
n\equiv 0,3,4\pmod{6}
,
then there exists no almost perfect linear Lee code of packing radius
2
.

In this paper, we consider the rest cases with
n\equiv 1,2,5\pmod{6}
. Together with Theorem
1.2
, we can prove the following result.

Theorem 1.3
.

There is no almost perfect linear Lee code of packing radius
2
in
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
for any positive integer
n
except for
n=1,2,11,29,47,56,67,79,104,121,134,191
.

The rest part of this paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we recall some necessary and sufficient conditions of the existence of APLL codes of packing radius
2
in terms of group ring equations. In Section
3
, we recall how to prove Theorem
1.2
in
[
17
]
and provide an outline of the proof of Theorem
1.3
. As this proof is very long, we separate it into several lemmas and theorems in Sections
4
–
7
, which tell us that
n
is bounded if there exist APLL codes of packing radius
2
in
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
for
n\equiv 1,2,5\pmod{6}
. In Section
8
, by two nonexistence results from
[
6
]
and
[
7
]
, we can exclude most of the rest small values of
n
and finish the proof of Theorem
1.3
. In Section
9
, we conclude this paper with a conjecture and several remarks.

2.
Preliminaries

Let
G
be a finite group. Let
\mathbb{Z}[G]
denote the set of formal sums
\sum_{g\in G}a_{g}g
, where
a_{g}\in\mathbb{Z}
and
G
is a finite multiplicative group. The addition and the multiplication on
\mathbb{Z}[G]
are defined by

\sum_{g\in G}a_{g}g+\sum_{g\in G}b_{g}g:=\sum_{g\in G}(a_{g}+b_{g})g,

and

(\sum_{g\in G}a_{g}g)\cdot(\sum_{g\in G}b_{g}g):=\sum_{g\in G}(\sum_{h\in G}a_{h}b_{h^{-1}g})\cdot g,

for
\sum_{g\in G}a_{g}g,\sum_{g\in G}b_{g}g\in\mathbb{Z}[G]
. Moreover,

\lambda\cdot(\sum_{g\in G}a_{g}g):=\sum_{g\in G}(\lambda a_{g})g

for
\lambda\in\mathbb{Z}
and
\sum_{g\in G}a_{g}g\in\mathbb{Z}[G]
.

For an element
A=\sum_{g\in G}a_{g}g\in\mathbb{Z}[G]
and
t\in\mathbb{Z}
, we define

A^{(t)}:=\sum_{g\in G}a_{g}g^{t}.

A multi-subset
D
of
G
can be viewed as an element
\sum_{g\in D}g\in\mathbb{Z}[G]
. In the rest of this paper, by abuse of notation, we will use the same symbol to denote a multi-subset in
G
and the associated element in
\mathbb{Z}[G]
. Moreover,
|D|
denotes the number of distinct elements in
D
. In another word,
|D|
is the cardinality of the
support
of
D
which is defined by
\mathrm{Supp}(D):=\{g\in G:\text{ the coefficient of $g$ in $D$ is positive}\}
.

In
[
17
, Section 3]
, Xu and the second author have proved the equivalence between the existence of an APLL code of packing radius
2
and some group ring equations.

Lemma 2.1
.

There exists an APLL code of packing radius
2
in
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
if and only if there is an abelian group
H
of order
n^{2}+n+1
containing two inverse-closed subsets
T_{0}
and
T_{1}
such that the identity element
e\in T_{0}
, and the following two group ring equations in
\mathbb{Z}[H]
holds.

(1)

\displaystyle T_{0}T_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle H-e,

(2)

\displaystyle T_{0}^{2}+T_{1}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2H-T_{0}^{(2)}-T_{1}^{(2)}+2ne.

Example 2.2
.

The following examples of
T_{0}
and
T_{1}
satisfy (
1
) and (
2
).

(a)

For
n=1
,
H=\langle h\rangle\cong C_{3}
. Let
T_{0}=\{e\}
and
T_{1}=\{h,h^{2}\}
.

(b)

For
n=2
,
H=\langle h\rangle\cong C_{7}
. Define

T_{0}=\{e,h,h^{6}\}\text{ and }T_{1}=\{h^{2},h^{5}\}.

The following result is a collection of necessary conditions for
T_{0}
and
T_{1}
, which is also proved in
[
17
]
.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
H
be an abelian group of order
n^{2}+n+1
. Suppose
T_{0},T_{1}
are subsets of
H
satisfying
e\in T_{0}
,
T_{i}^{(-1)}=T_{i}
for
i=0,1
, (
1
) and (
2
). Then the following statements hold.

(a)

e\in T_{0}
,
e\notin T_{1}
;

(b)

T_{0}\cap T_{1}=\emptyset
and
T_{0}^{(2)}\cap T_{1}^{(2)}=\emptyset
;

(c)

T_{0}\cap(T_{0}^{(2)}\setminus\{e\})=T_{0}\cap T_{1}^{(2)}=\emptyset
;

(d)

\{ab:a\neq b,a,b\in T_{0}\}\cap T_{0}^{(2)}=\{e\}
;

(e)

When
n
is odd,
|T_{0}|=n
and
|T_{1}|=n+1
;

(f)

When
n
is even,
|T_{0}|=n+1
and
|T_{1}|=n
;

(g)

There is no common non-identity element in
T_{0}^{2}
and
T_{1}^{2}
;

(h)

T_{0}\cap T_{0}^{(3)}=\{e\}
.

3.
Outline of the method

First, let us take a look at the sketch of the proof of the nonexistence of APLL codes of packing radius
2
with
n\equiv 0,3,4\pmod{6}
in
[
17
]
.

By Lemma
2.1
, we only have to show there is no
T_{0}
and
T_{1}
in
H
satisfying the necessary and sufficient conditions in it. Define
\hat{T}=T_{0}+T_{1}\in\mathbb{Z}[H]
,
k_{0}=|T_{0}|
and
k_{1}=|T_{1}|
.

Multiplying
T_{0}
and
T_{1}
on both sides of
(\ref{eq:T0^2+T1^2})
, we get

(3)

\displaystyle T_{0}^{3}=(2k_{0}-k_{1})H+2nT_{0}+T_{1}-T_{0}\hat{T}^{(2)}\,,

(4)

\displaystyle T_{1}^{3}=(2k_{1}-k_{0})H+2nT_{1}+T_{0}-T_{1}\hat{T}^{(2)}\,.

Consider the above two equations modulo
3

\displaystyle\hat{T}^{(2)}T_{0}

\displaystyle\equiv(2k_{0}-k_{1})H+T_{1}-T_{0}^{(3)}+2nT_{0}\pmod{3},

\displaystyle\hat{T}^{(2)}T_{1}

\displaystyle\equiv(2k_{1}-k_{0})H+T_{0}-T_{1}^{(3)}+2nT_{1}\pmod{3}.

Note that
T_{i}^{3}\equiv T_{i}^{(3)}\pmod{3}
for
i=0,1
. If
3\nmid|H|
, then
T_{0}^{(3)}
and
T_{1}^{(3)}
are both subsets of
H
; for
3\mid|H|
, we can also show that there are at most 2 elements in
T_{i}^{(3)}
appearing twice for
i=0,1
; see Lemma
4.1
. Hence, by calculation, we can derive some strong restrictions on the coefficients of most of the elements in the right-hand side of the above two equations. For instance, when
n\equiv 1\pmod{3}
and
n
is odd, the first equation becomes

\hat{T}^{(2)}T_{0}\equiv T_{1}-T_{0}^{(3)}+2T_{0}\pmod{3}.

As
T_{1}
,
T_{0}^{(3)}
and
T_{0}
are approximately of size
n
, most of the elements in
H
appear in
\hat{T}^{(2)}T_{0}
for
3k
times,
k=0,1,\cdots
.

Let
X_{i}
(
Y_{i}
, resp.) be the subset of elements of
H
appearing in
\hat{T}^{(2)}T_{0}
(
\hat{T}^{(2)}T_{1}
, resp.) exactly
i
times for
i=0,1,\cdots,M_{0}
(
M_{1}
, resp.), which means
X_{i}
’s (
Y_{i}
’s, resp.) form a partition of the group
H
. In particular, we define
M_{0}
and
M_{1}
to be the largest integers such that
X_{M_{0}}
and
Y_{M_{1}}
are non-empty sets. Then

(5)

\displaystyle\hat{T}^{(2)}T_{0}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{M_{0}}iX_{i},

(6)

\displaystyle\hat{T}^{(2)}T_{1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{M_{1}}iY_{i}.

By (
5
) and (
6
), we have the following three conditions on the value of
|X_{i}|
’s and
|Y_{i}|
’s:

(7)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{M_{0}}i|X_{i}|

\displaystyle=(2n+1)k_{0},

(8)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{M_{1}}i|Y_{i}|

\displaystyle=(2n+1)k_{1},

(9)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{M_{0}}|X_{i}|=\sum_{i=0}^{M_{1}}|Y_{i}|

\displaystyle=n^{2}+n+1.

Some extra conditions can also be derived:

(10)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{M_{0}}|X_{i}|=

\displaystyle(2n+1)k_{0}-2(k_{0}-1)k_{0}+\theta_{0}+\sum_{s\geq 3}\frac{(s-1)(s-2)}{2}|X_{s}|,

(11)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{M_{1}}|Y_{i}|=

\displaystyle(2n+1)k_{1}-2(k_{1}-1)k_{1}+\theta_{1}+\sum_{s\geq 3}\frac{(s-1)(s-2)}{2}|Y_{s}|,

where

(12)

\theta_{0}=|(T_{0}^{2}\setminus T_{0}^{(2)})\cap\hat{T}^{(4)}|,

and

(13)

\theta_{1}=\frac{|T_{1}\cap\hat{T}^{(2)}|}{2}+|(T_{1}^{2}\setminus(T_{1}^{(2)}\cup\{e\}))\cap\hat{T}^{(4)}|;

see
[
17
, Lemma 3.1]
.

Note that
|X_{i}|
’s and
|Y_{i}|
’s are nonnegative integers. Our main approach in
[
17
]
is to use the 5 equations (
7
) , (
8
) , (
9
), (
10
) and (
11
) together with
\hat{T}^{(2)}T_{0}
and
\hat{T}^{(2)}T_{1}\pmod{3}
to find contradictions on the value of
|X_{i}|
’s and
|Y_{i}|
’s provided that
n
is not too small.

However, for
n\equiv 1,2,5\pmod{6}
, the above approach does not lead to any contradiction. Instead, we can only determine the value or the ranges of
|X_{i}|
’s and
|Y_{i}|
’s. These results are provided in Section
4
.

To obtain further nonexistence results, one possible strategy is to look at
\hat{T}^{(4)}T_{0}
and
\hat{T}^{(4)}T_{1}
modulo
5
. To be precise, we concentrate on the necessary conditions

(14)

\displaystyle\hat{T}^{(4)}T_{0}=(5k_{0}+(2k_{0}-k_{1})(k_{0}^{2}-1))H+(4n^{2}+2n-3)T_{0}+2nT_{1}-4n\hat{T}^{(2)}T_{0}-\hat{T}^{(2)}T_{1}-T_{0}^{5},

(15)

\displaystyle\hat{T}^{(4)}T_{1}=(5k_{1}+(2k_{1}-k_{0})(k_{1}^{2}-1))H+(4n^{2}+2n-3)T_{1}+2nT_{0}-4n\hat{T}^{(2)}T_{1}-\hat{T}^{(2)}T_{0}-T_{1}^{5};

which will be proved in Lemma

.

As

is always relatively prime to
5
,
T_{0}^{(5)}
and
T_{1}^{(5)}
are both subsets of

. The above two equations modulo

provide us more conditions. For instance, when
n
satisfies

,
n\equiv 1\pmod{6}
and

, the equation (

\hat{T}^{(4)}T_{1}=4H+4T_{1}+3T_{0}+4\sum_{i=1}^{M_{0}}iX_{i}+4\sum_{i=1}^{M_{1}}iY_{i}-T_{1}^{(5)}\pmod{5}.

Similar to (

) and (
6
), we set

(16)

\hat{T}^{(4)}T_{0}=\sum_{i=0}^{N_{0}}iU_{i},

and

(17)

\hat{T}^{(4)}T_{1}=\sum_{i=0}^{N_{1}}iV_{i}\,

where

and

are subsets of
H
such that

(resp.
V_{i}
) consists of the elements appearing in
\hat{T}^{(4)}T_{0}
(resp.

) exactly

By combining the known restrictions on

’s and

’s with the group ring equations on

and
\hat{T}^{(4)}T_{1}

, we can prove that

; see Theorems

and

. However the computation is quite complicated, because we have to separate the proof into 5 different cases depending on the value of

4.
Restrictions on
|X_{i}|
’s and

When

,

always divides
|H|
. Hence

and

are not necessarily subsets of

, i.e., it is possible that some elements in them appear more than once. In

]
, the following result has been proved.

Lemma 4.1
.

When
n\equiv 1\pmod{3}
,

(a)

there is no element in

appearing more than
2
times for

,

(b)

e
appears only once in
T_{0}^{(3)}
, and

(c)

there are

elements appearing twice in
T_{0}^{(3)}
, and there are at most
2
elements appearing twice in

.

Let
\Delta_{i}
be the set of elements appearing twice in
T_{i}^{(3)}
for

. By Lemma

,

and there is no element appearing more than 2 times in
T_{i}^{(3)}
for

.

.

For
n\equiv 1\pmod{6}
, suppose that there exist inverse-closed subsets
T_{0}
and

with

and

satisfying (

) and (

). Then the following results hold.

,

,
|X_{3}|=\frac{2}{3}(n-1)^{2},|X_{i}|=0
for

.

(b)

T_{1}\cap T_{0}^{(3)}=\emptyset
,
\theta_{0}=0
and
\Delta_{0}\subseteq T_{1}
.

(c)

Set
u_{0}=|T_{1}^{(3)}\cap T_{0}|
,

,
\Delta_{1}^{0}=\Delta_{1}\cap T_{0}
,

\frac{2n^{2}-5n-4+\theta_{1}}{3}+2u_{0}+u_{1}+2|\Delta_{1}^{1}|+|\Delta_{1}^{2}|\leq|Y_{3}|\leq\frac{2n^{2}-2n-3}{3}+u_{0}+u_{1}+|\Delta_{1}^{1}|+|\Delta_{1}^{2}|,

k_{1}=|T_{1}|
. For

and
V_{i}
defined in (

(59)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N_{1}}|V_{i}|=

\displaystyle(2n+1)k_{1}-2(k_{1}-1)k_{1}+\eta+\sum_{s\geq 3}\frac{(s-1)(s-2)}{2}|V_{s}|,

where
\eta
is a nonnegative integer. When
n
is odd,

(60)

2|V_{1}|+3|V_{2}|+3|V_{3}|+2|V_{4}|\geq 2n^{2}+4n+2.

Proof.

By the inclusion–exclusion principle, we count the distinct elements in
\hat{T}^{(4)}T_{1}
,

(61)

|\hat{T}^{(4)}T_{1}|=\sum_{i=0}^{2n}|b_{i}T_{1}|-\sum_{i<j}|b_{i}T_{1}\cap b_{j}T_{1}|+\sum_{r\geq 3}(-1)^{r-1}|b_{i_{1}}T_{1}\cap b_{i_{2}}T_{1}\cap\cdots\cap b_{i_{r}}T_{1}|,

where
i_{1}<i_{2}<\cdots<i_{r}
cover all the possible values. By the definition of
V_{i}
’s, the left-hand side of (
61
) equals
\sum_{i=1}^{N_{1}}|V_{i}|
.

Now we determine the value of the different sums in (
61
). First,
\sum_{i=0}^{2n}|b_{i}T_{1}|=(2n+1)k_{1}
.

An element
b_{i}x=b_{j}y\in b_{i}T_{k}\cap b_{j}T_{k}
for some
x,y\in T_{k}
if and only if

b_{i}b_{j}^{-1}=x^{-1}y\in T_{k}^{2}.

By (
2
), there exist at most two possible choices of
(x,y)
. Moreover, if
(x,y)
is such that
b_{i}x=b_{j}y
, then
b_{i}y^{-1}=b_{j}x^{-1}
which also provides a solution
(y^{-1},x^{-1})
. Thus
|b_{i}T_{k}\cap b_{j}T_{k}|=1
if and only if
x=y^{-1}
, which is equivalent to
b_{i}b_{j}^{-1}\in T_{k}^{(2)}
. Then for any
i<j
and
k=0,1
, we have

|b_{i}T_{k}\cap b_{j}T_{k}|\in\{0,1,2\}.

This implies

\sum_{i<j}|b_{i}T_{1}\cap b_{j}T_{1}|\leq 2\cdot|T_{1}|\cdot(|T_{1}|-1)=2(k_{1}-1)k_{1}.

Moreover, we set
\eta=2(k_{1}-1)k_{1}-\sum_{i<j}|b_{i}T_{1}\cap b_{j}T_{1}|
.

Finally, suppose that
g\in b_{i_{1}}T_{1}\cap b_{i_{2}}T_{1}\cap\cdots\cap b_{i_{r}}T_{1}
with
r\geq 3
. It means that
g\in V_{s}
for some
s\geq 3
. Then the contribution for
g
in the sum
\sum_{r\geq 3}(-1)^{r-1}|b_{i_{1}}T_{1}\cap b_{i_{2}}T_{1}\cap\cdots\cap b_{i_{r}}T_{1}|
is

(-1)^{3-1}\binom{s}{3}+(-1)^{4-1}\binom{s}{4}+\cdots=\binom{s}{2}-\binom{s}{1}+\binom{s}{0}=\frac{(s-1)(s-2)}{2}.

Therefore,

\sum_{r\geq 3}(-1)^{r-1}|b_{i_{1}}T_{1}\cap b_{i_{2}}T_{1}\cap\cdots\cap b_{i_{r}}T_{1}|=\sum_{s\geq 3}|V_{s}|\frac{(s-1)(s-2)}{2}.

Plugging them all into (
61
), we get (
59
).

By the definition of
V_{i}
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N_{1}}i|V_{i}|=|\hat{T}^{(4)}T_{1}|=(2n+1)(n+1)\,.

For odd
