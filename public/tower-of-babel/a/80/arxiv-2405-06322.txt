Laser-assisted radiative recombination beyond the dipole approximation
Deeksha Kanti 1, M. M. Majczak 1, J. Z. Kamiński 1, Liang-You Peng 2,3,4, K. Krajewska 1

Abstract
A comprehensive theoretical approach to describe the electron-ion radiative recombination in the presence of intense, short laser pulses, which accounts for nondipole corrections is presented. It is based on the relativistic Coulomb-Volkov solution describing an electron in a combined Coulomb potential and a laser field, which is systematically expanded in powers of 1 / c 1/c . Thus, it allows us to trace the origin of nondipole effects observed in the spectrum of emitted radiation. Hence, as we demonstrate for high-frequency pulses assisting the process, a significant extension of the cutoff and asymmetry in angular distributions of the emitted radiation can be attributed to the electron recoil off the laser pulse. In addition, we investigate a possibility of enhancing the efficiency of the generated high-energy radiation by chirping the pulse.



I
Introduction

There exist different regimes of laser-matter interactions. For instance, the focus of strong-field physics is to describe laser-matter interactions
in the nonrelativistic regime, in which the strength of the laser field is comparable to that binding electrons in atoms. For
near optical fields, this concerns the intensity range of roughly
10^{13}
-
10^{17}
W/cm
2
. Beyond that, the electron dynamics in the
laser field becomes fully relativistic, which is already the subject of high-field physics. While the latter treats the laser field as
a propagating electromagnetic wave, with a full account for its temporal and spatial dependence, in strong-field physics it is traditionally
described as a homogeneous time-dependent electric field. This being the essence of the dipole approximation. In more physical terms,
in the dipole approximation the momentum transfer from the laser field and, hence, the radiation pressure
experienced by the electron are neglected. Finally, it is crucial to realize that while the strong-field physics relies on the time-dependent
Schrödinger equation, the correct framework for relativistic investigations is set by either the Dirac or the Klein-Gordon
equation. Hence, going beyond the standard strong-field physics framework leads to our better understanding of
processes at the crossover of both regimes.

II
Theoretical formulation

In this section, we present the theoretical formulation of the laser-assisted electron-ion radiative recombination that is followed by a formation
of a hydrogen-like atom and emission of a photon. Our objective is to introduce a systematic approach which accounts for nondipole corrections
of the order
1/c
. For this purpose, starting with the Klein-Gordon equation, we shall derive the Coulomb-Volkov states that describe the electron
in a laser field. This relativistic solution will then be expanded in powers of
1/c
, thus providing the leading nondipole contributions to
the probability amplitude of LARR.

Consider recombination of an electron with an asymptotic momentum
{\bm{p}}
by a Coulomb potential
V({\bm{r}})
in the presence of a laser field;
thus, resulting in emission of a photon characterized by the wave vector
{\bm{K}}
, energy
\omega_{\bm{K}}
, and linear polarization vector

{\bm{\varepsilon}}_{\bm{K}}
. The probability amplitude of the process takes the form,

{\cal A}({\bm{p}})=-\mathrm{i}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t\,\langle\psi_{B}(t);1_{{\bm{K}}}|\hat{H}_{I}(t)|\psi_{\bm{p}}^{(+)}(t);0_{{\bm{K}}}\rangle,

(1)

where
|\psi_{\bm{p}}^{(+)}(t);0_{{\bm{K}}}\rangle
is the initial state of the system representing the electron in the scattering state

\psi_{\bm{p}}^{(+)}({\bm{r}},t)
and no photons, whereas
|\psi_{B}(t);1_{{\bm{K}}}\rangle
is the final state of the system describing asymptotically the electron
in the bound state
\psi_{B}({\bm{r}},t)
of energy
E_{B}
and an emitted photon. Moreover,
\hat{H}_{I}(t)
represents the interaction Hamiltonian that causes
the transition.

Note that both wave functions
\psi_{\bm{p}}^{(+)}({\bm{r}},t)
and
\psi_{B}({\bm{r}},t)
are, in principle, the exact electronic
wave functions. However, as we will argue below, for the initial wave function
\psi_{\bm{p}}^{(+)}({\bm{r}},t)
it is justified to use the
Volkov-Coulomb wave with nondipole corrections. Also, as we are dealing with short laser pulses, we shall assume that the final electron state

\psi_{B}({\bm{r}},t)
is field-free.

II.1
Volkov-Coulomb wave function

In this section, we derive the Coulomb-Volkov solution of the Schrödinger equation with the leading nondipole corrections of the order
1/c
.
Our starting point is the Klein-Gordon equation for an electron coupled to an electromagnetic field,

\left[\frac{1}{c^{2}}(\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}-V)^{2}-(\hat{\bm{p}}-e{\bm{A}})^{2}-(m_{\rm e}c)^{2}\right]\psi_{\bm{p}}({\bm{r}},t)=0.

(2)

Thus, we neglect the electron spin.
Here,
V=V({\bm{r}})
is the Coulomb potential, whereas
{\bm{A}}={\bm{A}}(t-{\bm{n}}\cdot{\bm{r}}/c)
represents a vector potential
describing a laser field. For the transverse laser field, that propagates in the direction
{\bm{n}}
, we assume that
{\bm{n}}\cdot{\bm{A}}=0
.
We look for solutions of this equation in the form,

\psi_{\bm{p}}({\bm{r}},t)=\mathrm{e}^{{\mathrm{i}F}({\bm{r}},t)}\phi_{\bm{p}}({\bm{r}},t),

(3)

with the electron asymptotic momentum
{\bm{p}}
and the phase depending on time and position such that
F({\bm{r}},t)=F(t-{\bm{n}}\cdot{\bm{r}}/c)
.
Substituting the above solution to Eq. (
2
), we obtain the following differential equation for unknown functions
F({\bm{r}},t)
and

\phi_{\bm{p}}({\bm{r}},t)
,

\displaystyle\biggl[\frac{1}{c^{2}}(\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}

\displaystyle-V)^{2}-\hat{\bm{p}}^{2}-(m_{\rm e}c)^{2}-\frac{2}{c^{2}}F^{\prime}(\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}-V)

\displaystyle+2\left(e{\bm{A}}+\frac{1}{c}F^{\prime}{\bm{n}}\right)\cdot\hat{\bm{p}}-e^{2}{\bm{A}}^{2}\biggr]\phi_{\bm{p}}({\bm{r}},t)=0.

(4)

Here,
F^{\prime}
means the derivative of the phase
F
with respect to the retarded time,
t_{\rm R}=t-{\bm{n}}\cdot{\bm{r}}/c
. In order to simplify Eq. (
4
), we further assume that

2\left(e{\bm{A}}+\frac{1}{c}F^{\prime}{\bm{n}}\right)\cdot{\bm{p}}-e^{2}{\bm{A}}^{2}-\frac{2E_{\bm{p}}}{c^{2}}F^{\prime}=0,

(5)

where
E_{\bm{p}}
is the electron initial energy. Note that this condition can be rewritten in a relativistically invariant form by introducing the
four-vector notation. Taking
p=(E_{\bm{p}}/c,{\bm{p}})
,
n=(1,{\bm{n}})
, and
A=(0,{\bm{A}})
, Eq. (
5
) becomes

F^{\prime}=-c\frac{eA\cdot p}{n\cdot p}+c\frac{e^{2}A^{2}}{2n\cdot p},

(6)

or, after integrating it,

F({\bm{r}},t)=\int^{t-{\bm{n}}\cdot{\bm{r}}/c}\mathrm{d}\phi\left[-c\frac{eA(\phi)\cdot p}{n\cdot p}+c\frac{e^{2}A^{2}(\phi)}{2n\cdot p}\right],

(7)

which is
the relativistic Volkov phase

[
67
]
. Next, by accounting for Eq. (
5
), Eq. (
4
) takes the form

\displaystyle\biggl[\frac{1}{c^{2}}(\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}

\displaystyle-V)^{2}-\hat{\bm{p}}^{2}-(m_{\rm e}c)^{2}-\frac{2}{c^{2}}F^{\prime}(\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}-V-E_{\bm{p}})

\displaystyle+2\left(e{\bm{A}}+\frac{1}{c}F^{\prime}{\bm{n}}\right)\cdot(\hat{\bm{p}}-{\bm{p}})\biggr]\phi_{\bm{p}}({\bm{r}},t)=0,

(8)

where one has to keep in mind that
F^{\prime}
satisfies Eq. (
6
). In the next step, we take care of rest-mass oscillations by replacing

\phi_{\bm{p}}({\bm{r}},t)
by

\phi_{\bm{p}}({\bm{r}},t)=\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}m_{\rm e}c^{2}t}\,\bar{\phi}_{\bm{p}}({\bm{r}},t).

(9)

After the relativistic reduction, Eq. (
8
) can be conveniently rewritten in the form resembling the Schrödinger equation,

\left(\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}-V-\frac{\hat{\bm{p}}^{2}}{2m_{\rm e}}+\hat{h}\right)\bar{\phi}_{\bm{p}}({\bm{r}},t)=0,

(10)

where the operator
\hat{h}
has been introduced,

\displaystyle\hat{h}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m_{\rm e}}\left(e{\bm{A}}+\frac{1}{c}F^{\prime}{\bm{n}}\right)\cdot(\hat{\bm{p}}-{\bm{p}})

(11)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2m_{\rm e}c^{2}}\left[(\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}-V)^{2}-2F^{\prime}(\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}-V+m_{\rm e}c^{2}-E_{\bm{p}})\right].

Note that
\hat{h}
can be treated as a perturbation to the field-free nonrelativistic problem. Specifically, for highly energetic electrons, the first
term in Eq. (
11
) represents a correction of the order of
(\hat{\bm{p}}-{\bm{p}})
(see, Ref.
[
68
]
). The second term, on the other hand, represents a relativistic
correction of the order of
1/c^{2}
. In that case, expanding the wave function
\bar{\phi}_{\bm{p}}({\bm{r}},t)
as

\bar{\phi}_{\bm{p}}({\bm{r}},t)=\bar{\phi}_{\bm{p}}^{(0)}({\bm{r}},t)+\bar{\phi}_{\bm{p}}^{(1)}({\bm{r}},t)+...,

(12)

we arrive at the corresponding lowest-order differential equations,

\displaystyle\left(\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}-V-\frac{\hat{\bm{p}}^{2}}{2m_{\rm e}}\right)\bar{\phi}_{\bm{p}}^{(0)}({\bm{r}},t)

\displaystyle=0,

(13)

\displaystyle\left(\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}-V-\frac{\hat{\bm{p}}^{2}}{2m_{\rm e}}\right)\bar{\phi}_{\bm{p}}^{(1)}({\bm{r}},t)

\displaystyle=-\hat{h}\bar{\phi}_{\bm{p}}^{(0)}({\bm{r}},t).

(14)

Hence,
\bar{\phi}_{\bm{p}}^{(0)}({\bm{r}},t)
is the field-free solution of the nonrelativistic wave equation, whereas

\bar{\phi}_{\bm{p}}^{(1)}({\bm{r}},t)
represents its first-order correction. Thus, for energetic electrons
and as long as we are interested in leading nondipole corrections, the scattering wave function of the electron can be represented in a product form,

\psi_{\bm{p}}({\bm{r}},t)=\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}m_{\rm e}c^{2}t+\mathrm{i}F({\bm{r}},t)}\bar{\phi}_{\bm{p}}^{(0)}({\bm{r}},t),

(15)

known as
the Coulomb-Volkov wave function
. We recall that
\bar{\phi}_{\bm{p}}^{(0)}({\bm{r}},t)
is the scattering solution of the nonrelativistic
Coulomb problem [Eq. (
13
)], whereas the Volkov phase
F({\bm{r}},t)
in its full relativistic form is given by Eq. (
7
). For completeness,
following Ref.
[
69
]
, we write down that

\displaystyle\bar{\phi}_{\bm{p}}^{(0)}({\bm{r}},t)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}^{\pi\nu/2}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}{\bm{p}}\cdot{\bm{r}}-\mathrm{i}{\bm{p}}^{2}t/(2m_{\rm e})}\Gamma(1-\mathrm{i}\nu)

\displaystyle\times{}_{1}F_{1}(\mathrm{i}\nu,1,\mathrm{i}(|{\bm{p}}|\,|{\bm{r}}|-{\bm{p}}\cdot{\bm{r}})),

(16)

where
\nu={\cal Z}/(a_{0}|{\bm{p}}|)
, whereas
{}_{1}F_{1}(a,b,z)
is the confluent hypergeometric function of the first kind.

For our further purpose, we transform the Coulomb-Volkov wave into the length gauge. In that case,
Eq. (
15
) becomes

\psi_{\bm{p}}^{(L)}({\bm{r}},t)=\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}m_{\rm e}c^{2}t-\mathrm{i}e{\bm{A}}({\bm{r}},t)\cdot{\bm{r}}+\mathrm{i}F({\bm{r}},t)}\bar{\phi}_{\bm{p}}^{(0)}({\bm{r}},t),

(17)

where we extract now the leading nondipole terms. Since the laser field depends on the retarded time

t_{\rm R}=t-{\bm{n}}\cdot{\bm{r}}/c
, we account for the retardation correction by expanding the vector potential in powers of
1/c
such that

{\bm{A}}({\bm{r}},t)={\bm{A}}(t-{\bm{n}}\cdot{\bm{r}}/c)\approx{\bm{A}}(t)+\frac{{\bm{n}}\cdot{\bm{r}}}{c}\,{\bm{\mathcal{E}}}(t),

(18)

where
{\bm{\mathcal{E}}}(t)=-\partial_{t}{\bm{A}}(t)
is the electric field describing the laser light in the dipole approximation.
Then, the gauge transformation phase factor in Eq. (
17
) can be replaced by

\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}e{\bm{A}}({\bm{r}},t)\cdot{\bm{r}}}\approx\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}e{\bm{A}}(t)\cdot{\bm{r}}}\left[1-\mathrm{i}e{\bm{\mathcal{E}}}(t)\cdot{\bm{r}}\,\frac{{\bm{n}}\cdot{\bm{r}}}{c}\right],

(19)

which we will relate to as
the gauge transformation correction
.
The second
retardation correction
follows from the integration limit in Eq. (
7
). Hence, up to the first order in
1/c
,

\displaystyle F({\bm{r}},t)

\displaystyle=\frac{c}{n\cdot p}\int^{t-{\bm{n}}\cdot{\bm{r}}/c}\mathrm{d}\phi\left[e{\bm{A}}(\phi)\cdot{\bm{p}}-\frac{1}{2}e^{2}{\bm{A}}^{2}(\phi)\right]

\displaystyle\approx\frac{c}{n\cdot p}\int^{t}\mathrm{d}\phi\left[e{\bm{A}}(\phi)\cdot{\bm{p}}-\frac{1}{2}e^{2}{\bm{A}}^{2}(\phi)\right]

\displaystyle-\frac{{\bm{n}}\cdot{\bm{r}}}{n\cdot p}\,\left[e{\bm{A}}(t)\cdot{\bm{p}}-\frac{1}{2}e^{2}{\bm{A}}^{2}(t)\right].

(20)

Finally, we shall account for the correction originating from the term
1/(n\cdot p)
present in the definition of
F({\bm{r}},t)

above. In the leading order in
1/c
, we obtain that

\frac{1}{n\cdot p}\approx\frac{1}{m_{\rm e}c}\left(1+\frac{{\bm{n}}\cdot{\bm{p}}}{m_{\rm e}c}\right),

(21)

which is known as the
Nordsieck correction

[
70
]
. As it was argued in Ref.
[
24
]
in the context of ionization,
this term can be interpreted as arising from an effective momentum-dependent mass,

m_{\rm eff}=m_{\rm e}-{\bm{n}}\cdot{\bm{p}}/c,

(22)

and is responsible for the electron recoil when interacting with the laser field. Thus, it can be attributed to the radiation pressure
exerted on the electron by the field. Hence, combining Eqs. (
20
) and (
21
), we obtain in the leading order in
1/c
that the relativistic
Volkov phase can be approximated as

\displaystyle F({\bm{r}},t)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{m_{\rm e}}\left(1+\frac{{\bm{n}}\cdot{\bm{p}}}{m_{\rm e}c}\right)\int^{t}\mathrm{d}\phi\left[e{\bm{A}}(\phi)\cdot{\bm{p}}-\frac{1}{2}e^{2}{\bm{A}}^{2}(\phi)\right]

\displaystyle-\frac{{\bm{n}}\cdot{\bm{r}}}{m_{\rm e}c}\left[e{\bm{A}}(t)\cdot{\bm{p}}-\frac{1}{2}e^{2}{\bm{A}}^{2}(t)\right].

(23)

This together with Eq. (
16
) define the scattering wave function of the initial electron [Eq. (
15
)]. It will be used next
to derive the formula for the probability amplitude of LARR beyond the dipole approximation.

II.2
Probability amplitude of LARR

II.2.1
Monochromatic electron wave

Consider an electron of momentum
{\bm{p}}
recombining to the ground state of a hydrogen-like atom with the atomic number
{\cal Z}
.
Starting with the Klein-Gordon equation, one can show through the relativistic reduction that, in the leading order in
1/c
, for the final electron state
one can take

\psi_{B}({\bm{r}},t)=\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}m_{\rm e}c^{2}t-\mathrm{i}E_{B}t}\psi_{B}({\bm{r}}),

(24)

where

\psi_{B}({\bm{r}})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\biggl(\frac{\cal Z}{a_{0}}\biggr)^{3/2}\mathrm{e}^{-{\cal Z}r/a_{0}},

(25)

whereas for the bounding energy,
E_{B}=-{\cal Z}^{2}E_{0}/2
. Similarly, one can identify in the leading order, that the interaction Hamiltonian in the length gauge is

\hat{H}_{I}({\bm{r}},t)=-e\hat{\bm{\mathcal{E}}}_{\bm{K}}(\hat{\bm{r}},t)\cdot\hat{\bm{r}},

(26)

where the quantum field operator
\hat{\bm{\mathcal{E}}}_{\bm{K}}(\hat{\bm{r}},t)
represents the radiated photon. More specifically,

\hat{\bm{\mathcal{E}}}_{\bm{K}}(\hat{\bm{r}},t)=\hat{\bm{\mathcal{E}}}_{\bm{K}}^{(-)}(\hat{\bm{r}},t)+h.c.,

(27)

with

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{\mathcal{E}}}_{\bm{K}}^{(-)}(\hat{\bm{r}},t)=-\mathrm{i}{\bm{\varepsilon}}_{\bm{K}}\sqrt{\frac{\omega_{\bm{K}}}{2\epsilon_{0}V}}\,\hat{a}_{\bm{K}}^{\dagger}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}(\omega_{\bm{K}}t-{\bm{K}}\cdot\hat{\bm{r}})},

(28)

where
V
is the quantization volume and
\hat{a}_{\bm{K}}^{\dagger}
is the creation operator. Note that in Eq. (
27
)
there is also the hermitian conjugate of
\hat{\bm{\mathcal{E}}}_{\bm{K}}^{(-)}(\hat{\bm{r}},t)
.

Substituting now Eqs. (
17
), (
24
), and (
26
) into the definition of the probability amplitude of LARR [Eq. (
1
)], we obtain

\displaystyle{\cal A}({\bm{p}})

\displaystyle=e\sqrt{\frac{\omega_{\bm{K}}}{2\varepsilon_{0}V}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t\int\mathrm{d}^{3}r\,\psi_{B}^{*}({\bm{r}})({\bm{\varepsilon}}_{\bm{K}}\cdot{\bm{r}})\psi_{\bm{p}}^{(0)}({\bm{r}},t)

\displaystyle\times\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}(E_{B}+\omega_{\bm{K}})t-\mathrm{i}[{\bm{K}}+e{\bm{A}}({\bm{r}},t)]\cdot{\bm{r}}-\mathrm{i}F({\bm{r}},t)},

(29)

where
F({\bm{r}},t)
is given by Eq. (
23
), whereas
\psi_{\bm{p}}^{(0)}({\bm{r}},t)
by Eq. (
16
). Taking into account those definitions,
we introduce now the following abbreviations,

\displaystyle Q

\displaystyle=E_{B}+\omega_{\bm{K}}-\frac{{\bm{p}}^{2}}{2m_{\rm e}},

(30)

\displaystyle{\bm{q}}(t)

\displaystyle={\bm{p}}-e{\bm{A}}(t)-{\bm{K}}

\displaystyle-\frac{{\bm{n}}}{m_{\rm e}c}\bigl[e{\bm{A}}(t)\cdot{\bm{p}}-\frac{1}{2}e^{2}{\bm{A}}^{2}(t)\bigr],

(31)

\displaystyle H(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m_{\rm e}}\Bigl(1+\frac{{\bm{n}}\cdot{\bm{p}}}{m_{\rm e}c}\Bigr)

\displaystyle\times\int^{t}\mathrm{d}\tau\bigl[e{\bm{A}}(\tau)\cdot{\bm{p}}-\frac{1}{2}e^{2}{\bm{A}}^{2}(\tau)\bigr].

(32)

Then, the LARR probability amplitude which includes nondipole first-order corrections (
29
) takes the form,

\displaystyle{\cal A}({\bm{p}})

\displaystyle=\frac{{\cal Z}e}{Va_{0}}\sqrt{\frac{{\cal Z}\omega_{\bm{K}}}{2\pi\varepsilon_{0}a_{0}}}\mathrm{e}^{\pi\nu/2}\Gamma(1-\mathrm{i}\nu)\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}Qt+\mathrm{i}H(t)}

\displaystyle\times\int\mathrm{d}^{3}r\,({\bm{\varepsilon}}_{\bm{K}}\cdot{\bm{r}})\Bigl[1-\mathrm{i}\left(e{\bm{\mathcal{E}}}(t)\cdot{\bm{r}}\right)\frac{{\bm{n}}\cdot{\bm{r}}}{c}\Bigr]

\displaystyle\times{}_{1}F_{1}(\mathrm{i}\nu,1,\mathrm{i}(|{\bm{p}}|\,|{\bm{r}}|-{\bm{p}}\cdot{\bm{r}}))\,\mathrm{e}^{-{\cal Z}r/a_{0}+\mathrm{i}{\bm{q}}(t)\cdot{\bm{r}}},

(33)

where we have inserted the scattering Coulomb state [Eq. (
16
)] and the bound state wave function [Eq. (
25
)].

Note that Eq. (
33
) can be represented as

{\cal A}({\bm{p}})={\cal N}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}Qt+\mathrm{i}H(t)}\Bigl[{\cal B}(t)-\frac{1}{c}{\cal C}(t)\Bigr],

(34)

where
{\cal N}=\frac{{\cal Z}e}{Va_{0}}\sqrt{\frac{{\cal Z}\omega_{\bm{K}}}{2\pi\varepsilon_{0}a_{0}}}\mathrm{e}^{\pi\nu/2}\Gamma(1-\mathrm{i}\nu)
and where we have introduced
the following functions,

\displaystyle{\cal B}(t)

\displaystyle=\int\mathrm{d}^{3}r\,({\bm{\varepsilon}}_{\bm{K}}\cdot{\bm{r}})\mathrm{e}^{-{\cal Z}r/a_{0}+\mathrm{i}{\bm{q}}(t)\cdot{\bm{r}}}

\displaystyle\times{}_{1}F_{1}(\mathrm{i}\nu,1,\mathrm{i}(|{\bm{p}}|\,|{\bm{r}}|-{\bm{p}}\cdot{\bm{r}})),

(35)

\displaystyle{\cal C}(t)

\displaystyle=\int\mathrm{d}^{3}r\,({\bm{\varepsilon}}_{\bm{K}}\cdot{\bm{r}})(e\bm{\mathcal{E}}(t)\cdot{\bm{r}})({\bm{n}}\cdot{\bm{r}})\mathrm{e}^{-{\cal Z}r/a_{0}+\mathrm{i}{\bm{q}}(t)\cdot{\bm{r}}}

\displaystyle\times{}_{1}F_{1}(\mathrm{i}\nu,1,\mathrm{i}(|{\bm{p}}|\,|{\bm{r}}|-{\bm{p}}\cdot{\bm{r}})).

(36)

Each of these functions can be calculated with the help of the so-called
Nordsieck integral
, which is defined as

f(\nu,\lambda,{\bm{q}},{\bm{p}})=\int\mathrm{d}^{3}r\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda r}}{r}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}{\bm{q}}\cdot{\bm{r}}}{}_{1}F_{1}(\mathrm{i}\nu,1,\mathrm{i}(|{\bm{p}}|\,|{\bm{r}}|-{\bm{p}}\cdot{\bm{r}})).

(37)

Because the above integral can be solved analytically, leading to

f(\nu,\lambda,{\bm{q}},{\bm{p}})=4\pi\zeta(1+\xi)^{-\mathrm{i}\nu},

(38)

with

\zeta=\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}+{\bm{q}}^{2}},\quad\xi=-2\zeta\left({\bm{p}}\cdot{\bm{q}}+\mathrm{i}\lambda|{\bm{p}}|\right),

(39)

one can derive the explicit formulas for the functions (
35
) and (
36
). This is done using the following relations,

\displaystyle{\cal B}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda}({\bm{\varepsilon}}_{\bm{K}}\cdot{\bm{\nabla}}_{\bm{q}})f(\nu,\lambda,{\bm{q}},{\bm{p}}),

(40)

\displaystyle{\cal C}(t)

\displaystyle=-\mathrm{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda}({\bm{\varepsilon}}_{\bm{K}}\cdot{\bm{\nabla}}_{\bm{q}})(e\bm{\mathcal{E}}(t)\cdot{\bm{\nabla}}_{\bm{q}})({\bm{n}}\cdot{\bm{\nabla}}_{\bm{q}})f(\nu,\lambda,{\bm{q}},{\bm{p}}),

(41)

where
\lambda={\cal Z}/a_{0}
and
{\bm{q}}={\bm{q}}(t)
is given by Eq. (
31
).

Let us consider now the radiative recombination which is assisted by a short laser pulse, lasting from 0 to
T_{p}
. In that case, the probability amplitude
of LARR (
34
) is divergent due to the presence of term
{\cal B}(t)
. However, as we have elaborated in Ref.
[
65
]
, such kind of divergence can
be treated using the Boca-Florescu transformation
[
71
]
. Hence, following the procedure described in Appendix
A
, the LARR probability amplitude (
34
) becomes

\displaystyle{\cal A}({\bm{p}})

\displaystyle={\cal N}\biggl\{2\pi\delta(Q){\cal B}(0)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}H(T_{p})/2}\cos(H(T_{p})/2)

\displaystyle+\mathrm{i}{\cal P}\left(\frac{1}{Q}\right)\int_{0}^{T_{p}}\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}Qt+\mathrm{i}H(t)}\Bigl[\dot{\cal B}(t)+\mathrm{i}\dot{H}(t){\cal B}(t)\Bigr]

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{c}\int_{0}^{T_{p}}\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}Qt+\mathrm{i}H(t)}{\cal C}(t)\biggr\},

(42)

where the dot stands for the time derivative.
Note that, compared to Eq. (
34
), we have restricted the integration limit in the last integral as
{\cal C}(t)
takes nonzero values only
for
t\in[0,T_{p}]
.

At this point, it is worth noting that the probability amplitude of LARR (
42
) contains a contribution from the field-free
process. This follows from the presence of the Dirac
\delta
function, which determines the energy conservation condition,
Q=0
[Eq. (
30
)].
Based on this condition, we expect to observe a peak in the spectrum of LARR at energy,

\omega_{\bm{K}}=\frac{{\bm{p}}^{2}}{2m_{\rm e}}+|E_{B}|.

(43)

The appearance of this peak was also predicted in Ref.
[
65
]
for the laser-assisted radiative attachment, in the framework based on the dipole
approximation. Hence, it appears as a rather universal feature. As we have already argued in Ref.
[
65
]
, the reason for the field-free peak
is that laser-assisted processes occur even in the absence of the laser field. Thus, the corresponding field-free spectrum is embedded in the laser-induced
one. As one can see from Eq. (
42
), there is a significant contribution from the latter, corresponding to
Q\neq 0
. Thus, we predict to observe
a broad spectrum of the induced radiation along with the peak at
Q=0
.

For our further purposes, we rewrite Eq. (
42
) in a more transparent way. Namely,

{\cal A}({\bm{p}})={\cal N}{\cal R}({\bm{p}}),

(44)

where

{\cal R}({\bm{p}})={\cal R}_{0}({\bm{p}})\delta(Q)+{\cal R}_{1}({\bm{p}}){\cal P}\left(\frac{1}{Q}\right)+{\cal R}_{2}({\bm{p}}),

(45)

with the partial LARR contributions defined as

\displaystyle{\cal R}_{0}({\bm{p}})

\displaystyle=2\pi{\cal B}(0)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}H(T_{p})/2}\cos(H(T_{p})/2),

(46)

\displaystyle{\cal R}_{1}({\bm{p}})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{i}\int_{0}^{T_{p}}\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}Qt+\mathrm{i}H(t)}\Bigl[\dot{\cal B}(t)+\mathrm{i}\dot{H}(t){\cal B}(t)\Bigr],

(47)

\displaystyle{\cal R}_{2}({\bm{p}})

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{c}\int_{0}^{T_{p}}\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}Qt+\mathrm{i}H(t)}{\cal C}(t).

(48)

As one can see, in order to calculate the probability amplitude (
44
), we need to perform the remaining integrals in Eqs. (
47
)
and (
48
). In actual computations, we define time-dependent functions,

\displaystyle{\cal R}_{1}({\bm{p}},t)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{i}\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}\tau\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}Q\tau+\mathrm{i}H(\tau)}\Bigl[\dot{\cal B}(\tau)+\mathrm{i}\dot{H}(\tau){\cal B}(\tau)\Bigr],

(49)

\displaystyle{\cal R}_{2}({\bm{p}},t)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{c}\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}\tau\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}Q\tau+\mathrm{i}H(\tau)}{\cal C}(\tau).

(50)

Then, we solve the following system of differential equations,

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl}\displaystyle\dot{\bm{A}}(t)&=&-{\bm{\mathcal{E}}}(t),\\
\dot{H}(t)&=&\frac{1}{m_{\rm e}}\Bigl(1+\frac{{\bm{n}}\cdot{\bm{p}}}{m_{\rm e}c}\Bigr)\bigl[e{\bm{A}}(t)\cdot{\bm{p}}-\frac{1}{2}e^{2}{\bm{A}}^{2}(t)\bigr],\\
\dot{\cal R}_{1}({\bm{p}},t)&=&\mathrm{i}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}Qt+\mathrm{i}H(t)}\left[\dot{\cal B}(t)+\mathrm{i}\dot{H}(t){\cal B}(t)\right],\\
\dot{\cal R}_{2}({\bm{p}},t)&=&-\frac{1}{c}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}Qt+\mathrm{i}H(t)}{\cal C}(t),\end{array}\right.

(51)

which we integrate from
t=0
to
t=T_{p}
. This is provided that we know the explicit form of the electric field,
{\bm{\mathcal{E}}}(t)
.
As a result, we obtain the values of
H(T_{p})
,
{\cal R}_{1}({\bm{p}},T_{p})={\cal R}_{1}({\bm{p}})
, and
{\cal R}_{2}({\bm{p}},T_{p})={\cal R}_{2}({\bm{p}})
,
which allow us to calculate the probability amplitude of LARR, given by Eq. (
44
).

In deriving the above formulas, we have assumed a monochromatic electron wave
\psi_{\bm{p}}({\bm{r}},t)
[Eq. (
15
)] impinging on an ion in the presence of a laser pulsed field. This has led
to appearance of singular distributions
\delta(Q)
and
{\cal P}(\frac{1}{Q})
in Eq. (
45
). As it follows from our previous work
[
65
]
, those singularities
can be removed provided that, instead of a monochromatic electron wave, we take a coherent superposition of such waves. This is elaborated on in the next section.

II.2.2
Coherent electron wave packet

Now we consider a coherent electron wave packet that recombines with an ion in the presence of a laser pulsed field. The initial electron wave packet

\psi_{\bm{p}}[{\bm{r}},t|f_{\bm{p}}]
is defined as a weighted superposition of monochromatic electron waves
\psi_{\bm{p}}({\bm{r}},t)
[Eq. (
15
)],

\psi_{\bm{p}}[{\bm{r}},t|f_{\bm{p}}]=\int\mathrm{d}^{3}{\bm{\kappa}}\,\psi_{\bm{\kappa}}({\bm{r}},t)f_{\bm{p}}({\bm{\kappa}}).

(52)

Here, we assume that the wave packet profile
f_{\bm{p}}({\bm{\kappa}})
is such that

f_{\bm{p}}({\bm{\kappa}})\approx\delta^{(3)}({\bm{\kappa}}-{\bm{p}}),

(53)

i.e., it describes a nearly monochromatic electron beam.
Consequently, the LARR probability amplitude (
29
) integrated over the initial electron momentum profile equals

\langle{\cal A}({\bm{p}})\rangle=\int\mathrm{d}^{3}{\bm{\kappa}}\,{\cal A}({\bm{\kappa}})f_{\bm{p}}({\bm{\kappa}}).

(54)

Substituting here Eq. (
44
), we obtain

\displaystyle\langle{\cal A}({\bm{p}})\rangle

\displaystyle={\cal N}\langle{\cal R}({\bm{p}})\rangle

(55)

\displaystyle={\cal N}\biggl[{\cal R}_{0}({\bm{p}})\langle\delta(Q)\rangle+{\cal R}_{1}({\bm{p}})\left\langle{\cal P}\left(\frac{1}{Q}\right)\right\rangle+{\cal R}_{2}({\bm{p}})\biggr],

which implicitly defines
\langle{\cal R}({\bm{p}})\rangle
and
where each contribution
{\cal N}{\cal R}_{j}({\bm{p}})
(for
j=0,1,2
) [Eqs. (
46
), (
47
), and (
48
)] is slowly
varying function of
{\bm{p}}
. Note that this is in contrast to the singular distributions
\delta(Q)
and
{\cal P}(\frac{1}{Q})
,
which are averaged in Eq. (
55
) over the initial electron momentum spread. In doing so, we define

\left\langle\frac{1}{Q+\mathrm{i}\epsilon}\right\rangle=\int\mathrm{d}^{3}{\bm{\kappa}}\,\frac{f_{\bm{p}}({\bm{\kappa}})}{Q_{\bm{\kappa}}+\mathrm{i}\epsilon},

(56)

with
Q_{\bm{\kappa}}=E_{B}+\omega_{\bm{K}}-{\bm{\kappa}}^{2}/(2m_{\rm e})
. Following the Sokhotski-Plemelj formula (
87
), this allows us to calculate

\displaystyle\langle\delta(Q)\rangle

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\pi}\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}{\rm Im}\left\langle\frac{1}{Q+\mathrm{i}\varepsilon}\right\rangle,

(57)

\displaystyle\left\langle{\cal P}\left(\frac{1}{Q}\right)\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}{\rm Re}\left\langle\frac{1}{Q+\mathrm{i}\epsilon}\right\rangle.

(58)

Thus, the field-free singularity at
Q=0
is smeared out by the initial electron momentum profile.
Note also that Eq. (
55
) accounts coherently for LARR contributions arising from different wave components of the initial electron wave packet.

As an example, we shall assume that the initial electron wave packet is well-collimated such that it is represented by the following
momentum distribution,

f_{\bm{p}}({\bm{\kappa}})\approx\frac{1}{\pi}\frac{\Delta|{\bm{p}}|}{(\kappa_{\|}-|{\bm{p}}|)^{2}+(\Delta|{\bm{p}}|)^{2}}\delta^{(2)}({\bm{\kappa}}_{\perp}).

(59)

Here, the contributing longitudinal
\kappa_{\|}
and transverse
{\bm{\kappa}}_{\perp}
momentum components are defined with respect to the direction of the central
electron momentum
{\bm{p}}
,

\kappa_{\|}=\frac{{\bm{\kappa}}\cdot{\bm{p}}}{|{\bm{p}}|},\qquad{\bm{\kappa}}_{\perp}={\bm{\kappa}}-\kappa_{\|}\frac{{\bm{p}}}{|{\bm{p}}|}.

(60)

Thus, while we neglect the electron momentum spread in the transverse direction, we assume the Lorentzian profile of the longitudinal
momenta forming the initial electron wave packet. Note that the latter is centered at
\kappa_{\|}=|{\bm{p}}|
and has the half-width at half maximum
(HWHM)
\Delta|{\bm{p}}|
. Hence, the average (
56
)
equals

\left\langle\frac{1}{Q+\mathrm{i}\varepsilon}\right\rangle=\frac{1}{E_{B}+\omega_{\bm{K}}-\frac{{\bm{p}}^{2}}{2m_{\rm e}}+\mathrm{i}\frac{\kappa_{0}}{m_{\rm e}}\Delta|{\bm{p}}|},

(61)

where
\kappa_{0}=\sqrt{2m_{\rm e}(E_{B}+\omega_{\bm{K}})}
and where the limit
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}
was taken. Following Eqs. (
57
)
and (
58
), we obtain

\displaystyle\langle\delta(Q)\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}\frac{\frac{\kappa_{0}}{m_{\rm e}}\Delta|{\bm{p}}|}{(E_{B}+\omega_{\bm{K}}-\frac{{\bm{p}}^{2}}{2m_{\rm e}})^{2}+(\frac{\kappa_{0}}{m_{\rm e}}\Delta|{\bm{p}}|)^{2}},

(62)

\displaystyle\left\langle{\cal P}\left(\frac{1}{Q}\right)\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{E_{B}+\omega_{\bm{K}}-\frac{{\bm{p}}^{2}}{2m_{\rm e}}}{(E_{B}+\omega_{\bm{K}}-\frac{{\bm{p}}^{2}}{2m_{\rm e}})^{2}+(\frac{\kappa_{0}}{m_{\rm e}}\Delta|{\bm{p}}|)^{2}},

(63)

which determine the probability amplitude of LARR integrated over the initial electron momentum distribution, Eq. (
55
).
Keeping this in mind, we shall define below the corresponding energy distribution of LARR.

II.3
Energy probability distribution of LARR

The total energy [per the initial electron flux
j_{\rm e}({\bm{p}})
] irradiated due to the interaction
of an electron wave packet with an ion in the presence of a pulsed laser field equals

E_{\bm{K}}({\bm{p}})=\frac{1}{j_{\rm e}({\bm{p}})}\int\frac{V\mathrm{d}^{3}{\bm{K}}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\,\omega_{\bm{K}}|\langle{\cal A}({\bm{p}})\rangle|^{2},

(64)

where
\langle{\cal A}({\bm{p}})\rangle
is given by Eq. (
55
). Here, the integration is over the density of final radiation states,

V\mathrm{d}^{3}{\bm{K}}/(2\pi)^{3}
. Moreover, using the relation
|{\bm{K}}|=\omega_{\bm{K}}/c
we derive that

\mathrm{d}^{3}{\bm{K}}=\omega_{\bm{K}}^{2}\mathrm{d}\omega_{\bm{K}}\mathrm{d}^{2}\Omega_{\bm{K}}/c^{3}
, where
\mathrm{d}^{2}\Omega_{\bm{K}}
is the solid angle of emitted photons.
The initial electron flux is

j_{\rm e}({\bm{p}})=\frac{|{\bm{p}}|}{m_{\rm e}}\frac{1}{V}.

(65)

Hence, we obtain

\displaystyle E_{\bm{K}}({\bm{p}})

\displaystyle=\frac{\nu^{4}\mathrm{e}^{\pi\nu}}{\sinh(\pi\nu)}\frac{\alpha m_{\rm e}|{\bm{p}}|^{2}}{(2\pi)^{2}c^{2}}

\displaystyle\times\int\mathrm{d}^{2}\Omega_{\bm{K}}\int\mathrm{d}\omega_{\bm{K}}\,\omega_{\bm{K}}^{4}|\langle{\cal R}({\bm{p}})\rangle|^{2},

(66)

where we have used the following property:
|\Gamma(1-\mathrm{i}\nu)|^{2}=\pi\nu/\sinh(\pi\nu)
.
Rewriting this formula as

E_{\bm{K}}({\bm{p}})=\int\mathrm{d}^{2}\Omega_{\bm{K}}\int\mathrm{d}\omega_{\bm{K}}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}^{3}E_{\bm{K}}({\bm{p}})}{\mathrm{d}\omega_{\bm{K}}\mathrm{d}^{2}\Omega_{\bm{K}}},

(67)

we conclude that the triply differential energy distribution (per the initial electron flux) of photons emitted
in the solid angle
\mathrm{d}^{2}\Omega_{\bm{K}}
and having energy within the interval
(\omega_{\bm{K}},\omega_{\bm{K}}+\mathrm{d}\omega_{\bm{K}})
equals

\frac{\mathrm{d}^{3}E_{\bm{K}}({\bm{p}})}{\mathrm{d}\omega_{\bm{K}}\mathrm{d}^{2}\Omega_{\bm{K}}}=\frac{\nu^{4}\mathrm{e}^{\pi\nu}}{\sinh(\pi\nu)}\frac{\alpha m_{\rm e}|{\bm{p}}|^{2}}{(2\pi)^{2}c^{2}}\,\omega_{\bm{K}}^{4}|\langle{\cal R}({\bm{p}})\rangle|^{2},

(68)

where
\langle{\cal R}({\bm{p}})\rangle
has been defined in Eq. (
55
).

While the above derivations are quite general, for numerical illustrations we need to specify certain models of the initial electron
wave packet and the laser field. This is done in the following section, along with an analysis of properties of emitted radiation.

III
Numerical illustrations

Following the theoretical framework introduced above, we are going to analyze in this section possibilities for either extending the range
of the LARR energy spectrum or enhancing it; both being of potential practical interest. In doing so, we shall use tailored laser pulses
which allow for a great control over LARR energy and angular distributions. In this context, we shall investigate nondipole effects in
LARR along with a control of LARR radiation by chirping the assisting laser pulse.

IV
Conclusions

We have demonstrated a comprehensive theoretical description of electron-atom radiative recombination in the presence of a laser pulse
that accounts for nondipole corrections. The latter were derived systematically by the relativistic reduction, enabling to follow the origin
of various nondipole contributions. As it followed from our numerical analysis, the most important of them originates from the electron
recoil off the laser pulse. We have recognized that, for the parameters considered in the paper, it results in extension of the LARR high-energy
plateau and is a cause of an asymmetry of energy distributions of generated radiation with respect to the polar angle of recombining electron.
The remaining nondipole corrections of the leading order in
1/c
turned out to be less significant.

In the present paper, we have analyzed ways to control the LARR radiation using external laser pulses. Despite the aforementioned nondipole
effects which arise entirely from the presence of the laser field, we have also investigated a possibility of manipulating the intensity
of the cutoff portion of LARR by chirping the accompanying laser pulse. More specifically, we have been able to shape the laser pulse such that
the recombining electron acquires maximum energy from the field for an extended period of time. This has allowed us to maximize the intensity of the high-energy
LARR radiation, which can be of interest while synthesizing ultrashort laser pulses out of LARR.

\varepsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}

(80)

\displaystyle H(t)=

\displaystyle 0,\;\;

\displaystyle{\rm for}\;\;t<0,

\displaystyle H(t)=

\displaystyle\,H(T_{p}),\;\;

\displaystyle{\rm for}\;\;t>T_{p},

\displaystyle f(t)=

\displaystyle f(0),\;\;

\displaystyle{\rm for}\;\;t<0\;\;{\rm and}\;\;t>T_{p}.

(81)

In our case,

corresponds to the laser field duration and

is the field-free portion of
f(t)
. Taking into account
the above conditions, one can rewrite Eq. (
79

(82)

where the integral in Eq. (
79
) was divided into two intervals,
(-\infty,0]
and
[0,\infty)
, and the former one was calculated
by parts. Now, in order to calculate the remaining integral, we use the identity,

\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t\,\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left[\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}(Q+\mathrm{i}\varepsilon)t}F(t)\right]=-F(0),

(83)

which leads to

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t\,

\displaystyle\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}(Q+\mathrm{i}\varepsilon)t}F(t)=-\frac{F(0)}{\mathrm{i}(Q+\mathrm{i}\varepsilon)}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\mathrm{i}(Q+\mathrm{i}\varepsilon)}\int_{0}^{T_{p}}\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}(Q+\mathrm{i}\varepsilon)t}\dot{F}(t).

(84)

one obtains therefore

\displaystyle I_{\varepsilon}=\frac{F(0)}{\mathrm{i}(Q-\mathrm{i}\varepsilon)}

\displaystyle-\frac{F(0)}{\mathrm{i}(Q+\mathrm{i}\varepsilon)}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\mathrm{i}(Q+\mathrm{i}\varepsilon)}\int_{0}^{T_{p}}\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}(Q+\mathrm{i}\varepsilon)t}\dot{F}(t).

(85)

\displaystyle=\pi\delta(Q)[F(0)+F(T_{p})]

(86)

As argued in Sec.
II.2

Appendix B
Classical considerations

The aim of this Appendix is to derive the formula for the kinetic energy of an electron moving in an electromagnetic field, within the framework of classical electrodynamics. We consider an electron of momentum
{\bm{p}}

moving in an electromagnetic field that propagates in the direction
{\bm{n}}
. The latter represents a finite laser pulse that is described by the electric and magnetic field components,

{\bm{\mathcal{E}}}({\bm{r}},t)
and
{\bm{\mathcal{B}}}({\bm{r}},t)
, respectively. Note that for a transverse field it holds that
{\bm{n}}\cdot{\bm{\mathcal{E}}}({\bm{r}},t)=0

and
{\bm{n}}\cdot{\bm{\mathcal{B}}}({\bm{r}},t)=0
. Hence, we proceed with the Newton-Lorentz equation for the electron motion,

\dot{\bm{\pi}}=e{\bm{\mathcal{E}}}({\bm{r}},t)+\frac{e}{m_{\rm e}}{\bm{\pi}}\times{\bm{\mathcal{B}}}({\bm{r}},t),

(90)

where
{\bm{\pi}}
is the electron kinetic momentum such that

m_{\rm e}\dot{\bm{r}}={\bm{\pi}}

(91)

and

{\bm{\mathcal{B}}}({\bm{r}},t)=\frac{1}{c}{\bm{n}}\times{\bm{\mathcal{E}}}({\bm{r}},t).

(92)

Plugging the above relation into Eq. (
90
) and using the triple product formula, we obtain

\dot{\bm{\pi}}=e{\bm{\mathcal{E}}}({\bm{r}},t)+\frac{e}{m_{\rm e}c}{\bm{n}}\left[{\bm{\pi}}\cdot{\bm{\mathcal{E}}}({\bm{r}},t)\right]-\frac{e}{m_{\rm e}c}{\bm{\mathcal{E}}}({\bm{r}},t)({\bm{n}}\cdot{\bm{\pi}}).

(93)

Next, we expand Eq. (
93
) in powers of
1/c
, leaving only the leading order terms,

\dot{\bm{\pi}}=e{\bm{\mathcal{E}}}(t)-\frac{{\bm{n}}\cdot{\bm{r}}}{c}e\dot{\bm{\mathcal{E}}}(t)+\frac{e}{m_{\rm e}c}{\bm{n}}\left[{\bm{\pi}}\cdot{\bm{\mathcal{E}}}(t)\right]-\frac{e}{m_{\rm e}c}{\bm{\mathcal{E}}}(t)({\bm{n}}\cdot{\bm{\pi}}).

(94)

We look for solutions of this equation in the form

{\bm{\pi}}={\bm{p}}-e{\bm{A}}(t)+\delta{\bm{\pi}}(t),

(95)

where
{\bm{p}}
is the dynamical electron momentum that is defined asymptotically, whereas
{\bm{A}}(t)
denotes the vector potential describing
the electromagnetic field in the dipole approximation such that
{\bm{\mathcal{E}}}(t)=-\dot{\bm{A}}(t)
. Moreover,
\delta{\bm{\pi}}(t)
represents
the leading order correction (of the order of
1/c
) to the electron kinetic momentum in the field,
{\bm{p}}-e{\bm{A}}(t)
. Inserting
Eq. (
95
) into Eq. (
94
), we obtain up to the first order in
1/c
that

\displaystyle\delta\dot{\bm{\pi}}(t)=

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\frac{{\bm{n}}\cdot{\bm{r}}}{c}e\dot{\bm{\mathcal{E}}}(t)+\frac{e}{m_{\rm e}c}{\bm{n}}\cdot\left[({\bm{p}}-e{\bm{A}}(t))\cdot{\bm{\mathcal{E}}}(t)\right]

(96)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\frac{e}{m_{\rm e}c}{\bm{\mathcal{E}}}(t)\cdot({\bm{n}}\cdot{\bm{p}}).

Here, we have used the fact that
{\bm{n}}\cdot{\bm{A}}(t)=0
for a transverse field. The solution to this equation has the form,

\delta{\bm{\pi}}(t)=-\frac{{\bm{n}}\cdot{\bm{r}}}{c}e{\bm{\mathcal{E}}}(t)-\frac{e}{m_{\rm e}c}{\bm{n}}\cdot\left[{\bm{p}}\cdot{\bm{A}}(t)\right]+\frac{e^{2}{\bm{A}}^{2}(t)}{2m_{\rm e}c}{\bm{n}}.

(97)

Together with Eq. (
95
), it allows us to derive the classical formula for the kinetic energy of an electron in an electromagnetic field,
which accounts for the leading nondipole corrections. Namely,

\displaystyle\frac{{\bm{\pi}}^{2}}{2m_{\rm e}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\left[{\bm{p}}-e{\bm{A}}(t)\right]^{2}}{2m_{\rm e}}-\frac{{\bm{n}}\cdot{\bm{p}}}{m_{\rm e}^{2}c}\Big[e\bm{A}(t)\cdot\bm{p}-\frac{1}{2}e^{2}\bm{A}^{2}(t)\Big]

(98)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{{\bm{n}}\cdot{\bm{r}}}{m_{\rm e}c}\left[({\bm{p}}-e{\bm{A}}(t))\cdot e{\bm{\mathcal{E}}}(t)\right]+{\cal O}\Big(\frac{1}{c^{2}}\Big).

While the second term in Eq. (
98
) corresponds to the electron recoil, the third term is the reminiscence of retardation.
We note that for the electron in an electromagnetic field,
{\bm{n}}\cdot{\bm{r}}\sim{\bm{n}}\cdot[{\bm{p}}-e{\bm{A}}(t)]/m_{\rm e}={\bm{n}}\cdot{\bm{p}}/m_{\rm e}
,
which follows from Eqs. (
91
) and (
95
) for a transverse field.
Therefore, this correction is negligible for as long as the electron is impinging nearly perpendicularly to the field propagation direction,
when
{\bm{n}}\cdot{\bm{p}}\approx 0
. This is in agreement with our results and their interpretation, as presented in Sec.
III.1
.

Appendix C
Spectrogram

In order to calculate the spectrogram
S(t,\omega_{\bm{K}})
of a signal
{\cal A}(\omega)
, the latter is first truncated to a finite interval

\omega\in[\omega_{1},\omega_{2}]
. This is to avoid the Gibbs effect, as we have discussed in Ref.
[
65
]
. Then, we define the truncated signal

{\cal A}_{T}(\omega)
as

{\cal A}_{T}(\omega)={\cal A}(\omega)f_{T}\left(\frac{\omega-\omega_{1}}{\omega_{2}-\omega_{1}},\xi_{T}(\omega_{2}-\omega_{1})\right),

(99)

where
\xi_{T}
is a small parameter and

f_{T}(x,\Delta x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0,&\textrm{for $x\leqslant 0$},\\
\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\pi x}{2\Delta x}\right),&\textrm{for $0<x<\Delta x$},\\
1,&\textrm{for $\Delta x\leqslant x\leqslant 1-\Delta x$},\\
\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\pi(1-x)}{2\Delta x}\right),&\textrm{for $1-\Delta x<x<1$},\\
0,&\textrm{for $x\geqslant 1$}.\end{array}\right.

(100)

The short-time Fourier transform of the truncated signal is defined as

{\cal A}_{ST}(t,\omega_{\bm{K}})=\int_{\omega_{1}}^{\omega_{2}}\mathrm{d}\omega{\cal A}_{T}(\omega)W(\omega-\omega_{\bm{K}},\xi_{W}(\omega_{2}-\omega_{1}))\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\omega t},

(101)

where
\xi_{W}
is a parameter determining the width of the window function
W(x,\Delta x)
. Moreover, for calculations presented in this paper
we shall use the Gaussian window,

W(x,\Delta x)=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-(x/\Delta x)^{2}}}{\sqrt{\pi}\Delta x},

(102)

satisfying the condition,

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}xW(x,\Delta x)=1.

(103)

Finally, we define the spectrogram as
[
65
]

S(t,\omega_{\bm{K}})=|{\cal A}_{ST}(t,\omega_{\bm{K}})|^{2},

(104)

that provides information about the frequency spectrum of the signal while it varies in time.
