Abstract
. In this paper we show that the spherical cap discrepancy of the point set given by centers of pixels in the HEALPix tessellation (short for Hierarchical, Equal Area and iso-Latitude Pixelation) of the unit 2 2 -sphere is lower and upper bounded by order square root of the number of points, and compute explicit constants. This adds to the known collection of explicitly constructed sets whose discrepancy converges with order N − 1 / 2 N^{-1/2} , matching the asymptotic order for i.i.d. random point sets. We describe the HEALPix framework in more detail and give explicit formulas for the boundaries and pixel centers. We then introduce the notion of an n n -convex curve and prove an upper bound on how many fundamental domains are intersected by such curves, and in particular we show that boundaries of spherical caps have this property. Lastly, we mention briefly that a jittered sampling technique works in the HEALPix framework as well.



1.
Introduction

HEALPix, short for “Hierarchical, Equal Area and iso-Latitude Pixelation,” is an algorithm introduced in
[
14
]
to discretize
\mathbb{S}^{2}
and has been widely used in cosmology and astrophysics. Given
\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, the algorithm produces a tessellation starting with 12
base pixels
followed by a further binary partition up to resolution
\ell
. In this paper we study the spherical cap discrepancy of the point set given by the centers of the HEALPix pixels. We discuss the tessellation algorithm in more detail in §
2
and give a precise definition of
\Gamma
, a projection of the sphere onto the plane, in §
3.1
.

Definition 1
.

Let
N=12\cdot 4^{\ell}
and let
\Gamma
be as defined in §
3.1
. Then the HEALPix points
H_{N}
on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
are defined as the preimage under
\Gamma
of the centers of squares obtained by the image of the HEALPix tessellation under
\Gamma
.

In order to quantify how well-distributed HEALPix points are on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
, we use the spherical cap discrepancy. We refer for other notions of discrepancy to the texts
[
17
]
and
[
11
]
and for their applications to the surveys
[
8
]
and
[
16
]
. There are other ways to quantify uniform distribution. For example, one can consider the
Riesz
s
-energy
of a point set: see
[
9
]
for a comprehensive monograph on these topics.

Let
\mathbb{S}^{2}=\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:\|z\|=1\}
denote the unit sphere in the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, where
\|\cdot\|
is induced by the standard inner product
\langle x,y\rangle
. A
spherical cap
with center
w\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
and height
t\in[-1,1]
is given by the set

C(w,t)=\{x\in\mathbb{S}^{2}\ :\ \langle x,w\rangle\geq t\}.

Definition 2

(Local spherical cap discrepancy)
.

Let
Z_{N}=\{z_{1},\ldots,z_{N}\}\subset\mathbb{S}^{2}
.
The local spherical cap discrepancy of
Z_{N}
with respect to a cap
C(w,t)
is

\mathcal{D}_{C(w,t)}(Z_{N})=\Bigl\lvert\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathds{1}_{C(w,t)}(z_{j})-\sigma(C(w,t))\Bigr\rvert,

where
\sigma
is the normalized uniform measure on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
.

Definition 3

(Spherical cap discrepancy)
.

Let
Z_{N}=\{z_{1},\ldots,z_{N}\}\subset\mathbb{S}^{2}
.
The spherical cap discrepancy of
Z_{N}
is

\mathcal{D}(Z_{N})=\sup_{w\in\mathbb{S}^{2}}\sup_{-1\leq t\leq 1}\mathcal{D}_{C(w,t)}(Z_{N}).

Lubotzky, Phillips and Sarnak in
[
18
]
were the first to achieve an upper bound for the spherical cap discrepancy of a deterministic point set, of the order
N^{-1/3}\log^{2/3}N
. Aistleitner, Brauchart, and Dick showed in
[
1
]
that for i.i.d. random point sets, spherical digital nets, and spherical Fibonacci lattices, the spherical cap discrepancy is at most of order
N^{-1/2}
. The latter two point sets are deterministic, and a further such example is given by the Diamond ensemble introduced first in
[
6
]
, which has cap discrepancy of order
N^{-1/2}
as shown in
[
12
]
. Beck showed in
[
3
]
that there exist point sets
\omega_{N}^{\star}
which achieve even lower order discrepancy:
there are constants
c,C>0
independent of
N
such that

(1)

cN^{-3/4}\leq\mathcal{D}(\omega_{N}^{\star})\leq CN^{-3/4}\sqrt{\log N}

where the lower bound holds for all
N
-point sets. Numerical results indicate that Fibonacci lattices achieve discrepancy of an order
close to Beck’s upper bound. On the other hand, probabilistic point sets with discrepancy matching this upper bound with high probability were obtained in
[
2
]
and later in
[
7
]
.

Our main result is as follows and extends Proposition 2.4 in
[
15
]
, where
H_{N}
are shown to be asymptotically uniformly distributed.

Theorem 1
.

Let
H_{N}
be the set of centers of pixels in a HEALPix tessellation of
\mathbb{S}^{2}
with
N
pixels. Then

\frac{2}{\sqrt{3}}N^{-1/2}\leq\mathcal{D}(H_{N})\leq\frac{2(5+\sqrt{2})}{\sqrt{3}}N^{-1/2}+1000N^{-1}.

To obtain the coefficient of the leading term in the upper bound, we bound the maximum number
I_{H_{N}}
of pixels of the HEALPix tessellation that are intersected by spherical caps. We believe that the constant could be improved to
4/\sqrt{3}
, which would make the HEALPix points the deterministic algorithm with the lowest-known constant in the discrepancy upper bound. This would follow from the proof technique used later and from the following conjecture.

Conjecture 1
.

I_{H_{N}}=2^{3+\ell}
, where
N=12\cdot 4^{\ell}
.

Much of our paper is devoted to showing that
I_{H_{N}}\leq 4(5+\sqrt{2})2^{\ell}+1000
. We do so by projecting the boundaries of spherical caps under the HEALPix projection
\Gamma
, bounding their lengths, and employing a lemma that relates the length of a curve to the number of intersections with the projected subpixels of the HEALPix tessellation.

In §
2
of the paper, we describe the HEALPix tessellation in more detail and give explicit formulas for the boundaries. (The appendix includes a more detailed indexing of the pixel centers for the reader’s convenience.) We also parametrize the boundaries of spherical caps which are used throughout the text. §
3
introduces the projection
\Gamma:\mathbb{S}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
of HEALPix pixels into the plane and some key properties. The main theorem is proved in §
4
. For the upper bound of the discrepancy, we use a bound on the length of
\Gamma(\partial C)
for spherical caps
C
, and then employ a classical approach. The lower bound of the same order is then established by considering a particular spherical cap. Lastly, we mention that a jittered sampling technique in the HEALPix lattice yields Beck’s upper bound (
1
). In §
5
we will introduce the notion of an
n
-convex curve and prove an upper bound on how many fundamental domains are intersected by such curves, as recently studied by the first-named author in
[
13
]
. This upper bound depends directly on the length of the curve, so we then show a length bound for boundaries of spherical caps. The last part of the section is the final ingredient to show that the boundaries of spherical caps are
n
-convex.

2.
Boundaries of the HEALPix pixels and spherical caps

Equal area partitions of the sphere give rise to point sets with good distribution properties; examples of such partitions are given in
[
10
]
and
[
19
]
. The HEALPix tessellation is another such example. The HEALPix algorithm introduces a division of the sphere into 12 spherical rectangles (
base pixels
) of equal area, each of which allows a dyadic decomposition into smaller rectangles and satisfies the following three properties.

(1)

Hierarchical structure of the database.
The resolution of a particular tessellation increases by dividing each pixel into four smaller ones. The hierarchical nature of the HEALPix algorithm allows us to easily define the center and boundaries of each pixel.

(2)

Equal areas of pixels.
Ensuring equal area of pixels in a HEALPix tessellation of any resolution is advantageous in a number of physical contexts.

(3)

Iso-Latitude distribution of pixels.
As in item 1. above, this property is useful in allowing us to precisely define the centers and boundaries of the pixels.

We introduce below the general formulas for the boundaries of pixels, following
[
14
]
.

2.1.
HEALPix tessellation algorithm

The sphere is separated into three regions: the north pole cap, the equatorial belt and the south pole cap. The lines of separation between the regions are given via intersection of
\mathbb{S}^{2}
with
\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\{\pm\frac{2}{3}\}
(see Figure
4
).

We refer to the 12 base pixels, which form the basis of the subdivision algorithm, as the level 0 HEALPix tessellation
\mathcal{T}_{0}
: shown in Figure
1
below. Note that four of the base pixels lie entirely within the equatorial belt; four pixels share a vertex at the north pole; and the southern hemisphere is a copy of the northern with negated
z
-value.

The level
\ell
tessellation
\mathcal{T}_{\ell}
takes a dyadic decomposition of each base pixel into
4^{\ell}
subpixels of equal area. In total there are
12\cdot 4^{\ell}
many pixels in
\mathcal{T}_{\ell}
. We refer to
\ell
as the
resolution parameter
or
level
of a tessellation.

2.2.
Boundaries of pixels in equatorial belt

First consider the base pixels that are entirely contained within the equatorial belt. Suppose the resolution parameter of the HEALPix tesselation is
\ell
, i.e. each base pixel is divided into
4^{\ell}
rectangular shapes of equal area and similar diameter. Let
L=2^{\ell}
. Then we define for
j\in\{0,1,\ldots,4L-1\}

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\Phi^{\ell}_{j}:I_{j}^{\ell}&\rightarrow&\big[0,\frac{\pi}{2}\big]\\
\phi&\mapsto&\phi-\frac{j}{L}\frac{\pi}{2}\end{array}\hskip 14.22636pt\mbox{with}\hskip 14.22636ptI_{j}^{\ell}:=\Big[\frac{j}{L}\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{j+L}{L}\frac{\pi}{2}\Big].

The pixel boundaries of resolution parameter
\ell
are obtained via curves
m_{j,\ell}^{e}
from north to south, and curves
p_{j,\ell}^{e}
from south to north, implicitly given by the lines

m_{j,\ell}^{e}\sim\cos(\theta)=\frac{2}{3}-\frac{8}{3\pi}\Phi_{j}^{\ell}(\phi)\hskip 7.11317pt\text{ and }\hskip 7.11317ptp_{j,\ell}^{e}\sim\cos(\theta)=-\frac{2}{3}+\frac{8}{3\pi}\Phi_{j}^{\ell}(\phi),

meaning that
m_{j,\ell}^{e}=\big(\cos(\phi)\sin(\theta),\sin(\phi)\sin(\theta),\cos(\theta)\big)
for
\phi\in I_{j}^{\ell}
and
\theta=\theta(\ell,j)
.

2.3.
Boundaries of pixels in north and south pole caps

We will only describe the boundaries for pixels in the north pole cap, as those on the south pole cap are obtained by changing the sign of the
z
-value. The boundaries for the base pixels are given by a fixed
\phi\in\{0,\frac{\pi}{2},\pi,\frac{3\pi}{2}\}
and all
0\leq\theta\leq\arccos(\frac{2}{3})
. We index the four base pixels with vertex at the north pole by
k\in\{0,1,2,3\}
. If
\ell\geq 1
let
L=2^{\ell}
; we define for
k
and
j\in\{1,\ldots,L-1\}
:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\Phi^{\ell,k}_{j}:E_{j}^{\ell,k}&\rightarrow&\big[\frac{j}{L}\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{\pi}{2}\big]\\
\phi&\mapsto&(k+1)\frac{\pi}{2}-\phi\end{array}\hskip 14.22636pt\mbox{with}\hskip 14.22636ptE_{j}^{\ell,k}:=\Big[k\frac{\pi}{2},(k+1)\frac{\pi}{2}-\frac{j}{L}\frac{\pi}{2}\Big];

and further

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\psi^{\ell,k}_{j}:A_{j}^{\ell,k}&\rightarrow&\big[\frac{j}{L}\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{\pi}{2}\big)\\
\phi&\mapsto&\phi-k\frac{\pi}{2}\end{array}\hskip 14.22636pt\mbox{with}\hskip 14.22636ptA_{j}^{\ell,k}:=\Big[k\frac{\pi}{2}+\frac{j}{L}\frac{\pi}{2},(k+1)\frac{\pi}{2}\Big).

The pixel boundaries of resolution parameter
\ell
are obtained via curves
m^{n}_{j,k,\ell}
from north to south, and curves
p^{n}_{j,k,\ell}
from south to north, implicitly given by the lines (where
\theta=\theta(j,k,\ell)
):

\displaystyle m^{n}_{j,k,\ell}\sim\cos(\theta)=1-\frac{j^{2}}{3L^{2}}\Big(\frac{\pi}{2\Phi_{j}^{k,\ell}(\phi)}\Big)^{2}\hskip 5.69046pt\text{ and }

\displaystyle p^{n}_{j,k,\ell}\sim\cos(\theta)=1-\frac{j^{2}}{3L^{2}}\Big(\frac{\pi}{2\psi_{j}^{\ell,k}(\phi)}\Big)^{2}.

2.4.
Parametrizations of spherical cap boundaries

In all that follows,
\gamma:[0,2\pi)\times(0,\pi)\rightarrow\mathbb{S}^{2}_{p}
is the standard parametrization of the (punctured) sphere defined below:

(2)

\begin{array}[]{rcl}(\phi,\theta)&\mapsto&\gamma(\phi,\theta)=\left(\begin{array}[]{l}\cos(\phi)\sin(\theta)\\
\sin(\phi)\sin(\theta)\\
\cos(\theta)\end{array}\right).\end{array}

(We exclude the poles to obtain an injection, but in what follows we sometimes abuse notation and plug in points of the form
(\phi,0)
and
(\phi,\pi)
.) Let
C=C(w,t)
be a spherical cap with boundary
\partial C
, and
\gamma(\phi_{w},\theta_{w})=w\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
and
t\in[0,1)
, then we have by the trigonometric angle sum formulas

(3)

\partial C=\Big\{\gamma(\phi,\theta)\ :\ \sin(\theta)\sin(\theta_{w})\cos(\phi-\phi_{w})+\cos(\theta)\cos(\theta_{w})=t\Big\}.

Let
c_{w}:=\cos(\theta_{w})
and
s_{w}:=\sin(\theta_{w})
. If
s_{w}=0
, then
w
is one of the poles
p_{n}
or
p_{s}
and

\partial C(p_{n,s})=\Big\{\gamma(\phi,\arccos\big(\tfrac{t}{c_{w}}\big))\ :\ \phi\in[0,2\pi)\Big\}.

In all other cases we can write

(4)

\displaystyle\phi

\displaystyle=\phi_{w}+\arccos\Big(\frac{t-\cos(\theta)c_{w}}{\sin(\theta)s_{w}}\Big)\mbox{ and/or }

(5)

\displaystyle\phi

\displaystyle=\phi_{w}-\arccos\Big(\frac{t-\cos(\theta)c_{w}}{\sin(\theta)s_{w}}\Big),

since the cosine from (
3
) depends on
\lvert\phi-\phi_{w}\rvert
, and where

\theta\in\big[\lvert\arccos(t)-\theta_{w}\rvert,\arccos(t)+\theta_{w}\big]

with
t\in[0,1)
and
\theta_{w}\in[0,\frac{\pi}{2}]
.

As the curvature and other properties for both parametrizations behave similarly, we will be using only parametrization (
4
) in the curvature calculations in §
5.3
and elsewhere.

If the north pole is in
\partial C
but the south pole is not, then
c_{w}=t
, and since
1-\cos(x)=\tan\big(\frac{x}{2}\big)\sin(x)
, we obtain from (
4
)

(6)

\phi=\arccos\Big(\frac{t}{\sqrt{1-t^{2}}}\tan\big(\tfrac{\theta}{2}\big)\Big)+\phi_{w}.

If the south pole is in
\partial C
but the north pole is not, then
c_{w}=-t
, and since
1+\cos(x)=\cot\big(\frac{x}{2}\big)\sin(x)
we obtain

(7)

\phi=\arccos\Big(\frac{t}{\sqrt{1-t^{2}}}\cot\big(\tfrac{\theta}{2}\big)\Big)+\phi_{w}.

If the north and south poles are in
\partial C
, then clearly
\theta_{w}=\frac{\pi}{2}
and (
3
) is equivalent to

\sin(\theta)\cos(\phi-\phi_{w})=t=0,

and the only possibility for a circle satisfying this relation and going through the poles is to have

\partial C=\gamma\Big(\{\phi_{w}+\frac{\pi}{2},\phi_{w}+\frac{3\pi}{2}\}\times[0,\pi]\Big).

3.
Projection into the Plane

In this section we define the projection map
\Gamma
of the HEALPix tessellation into the plane, as defined in
[
14
]
, show that it preserves area up to scaling, and study boundaries of spherical caps under
\Gamma
.

Each of the eight base pixels which have one vertex at a pole are simply rotated copies of the others. The remaining four base pixels have centers on the equatorial belt, and again each of these four base pixels are rotated copies of the others. Thus we have some rotational symmetry.

3.1.
Definition of
\Gamma

The projection map

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\Gamma:\mathbb{S}^{2}_{p}&\rightarrow&R\\
x&\mapsto&\Gamma(x)\end{array}

from the sphere to
R:=[0,2)\times(-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2})
is defined in the following fashion, where
\gamma
is as in (
2
).

•

If
\lvert\cos(\theta)\rvert\leq\frac{2}{3}
, then

\begin{array}[]{rcl}x=\gamma(\phi,\theta)&\mapsto&\Gamma(x)=\left(\begin{array}[]{l}\phi/\pi\\
3/8\cos(\theta)\end{array}\right).\end{array}

•

If
\cos(\theta)>\frac{2}{3}
, then

\begin{array}[]{rcl}x=\gamma(\phi,\theta)&\mapsto&\Gamma(x)=\dfrac{1}{\pi}\left(\begin{array}[]{l}\phi-\big(1-\sqrt{1-\cos(\theta)}\sqrt{3}\big)\cdot(\phi\hskip-5.69046pt\mod\frac{\pi}{2}-\frac{\pi}{4})\\
\frac{\pi}{4}\big(2-\sqrt{1-\cos(\theta)}\sqrt{3}\big)\end{array}\right).\end{array}

•

If
\cos(\theta)<-\frac{2}{3}
, then

\begin{array}[]{rcl}x=\gamma(\phi,\theta)&\mapsto&\Gamma(x)=\dfrac{1}{\pi}\left(\begin{array}[]{l}\phi-\big(1-\sqrt{1-\lvert\cos(\theta)\rvert}\sqrt{3}\big)\cdot(\phi\hskip-5.69046pt\mod\frac{\pi}{2}-\frac{\pi}{4})\\
\frac{\pi}{4}\big(\sqrt{1-\lvert\cos(\theta)\rvert}\sqrt{3}-2\big)\end{array}\right).\end{array}

Remark 1

(See
[
14
]
)
.

Pixel boundaries are mapped under
\Gamma
to straight line segments.

Each pixel is mapped to a square in
R
, as the segments
\Gamma(m^{e}_{j,\ell})
are parallel to each other and seperated by the same distance. The same is true for
\Gamma(p^{e}_{j,\ell})
, which are orthogonal to
\Gamma(m^{e}_{j,\ell})
. The same applies to
\Gamma(m^{n}_{j,k,\ell})
and
\Gamma(p^{n}_{j,k,\ell})
. The resulting lattice (see Fig.
2
) of pixel centers is the
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
lattice scaled and rotated.

We use the following fact, which follows from the definition of
\Gamma
, implicitly in further calculations.

Remark 2
.

Boundaries of hemispheres going through the poles are mapped to simple polygons by
\Gamma
. For most other caps
C
, if
\Gamma(\partial C)
intersects such polygons, it does so at most twice, hence restricting the geometry of
\Gamma(\partial C)
.

3.2.
Preservation of area up to scaling

It is shown in
[
14
]
that pixels of the same resolution
\ell
have the same area. Let
B_{1},\cdots,B_{12}
denote the 12 base pixels on the sphere. Then
\Gamma(B_{1}),\Gamma(B_{2}),\cdots,\Gamma(B_{12})
are squares in
R
(if we think of
R
as a cylinder), and they are all of equal area. The tiling in a base pixel with resolution parameter
\ell
is mapped to a tiling in the associated square of the same resolution, and each of the subpixels at resolution
\ell
has equal area. Any open subset
A
of a base pixel
B_{i}
can now be approximated from inside and outside via a cover of subpixels of ever increasing resolution; thus we obtain the following lemma.

Lemma 1
.

Given a base pixel
B
and an open set
A\subset B
, then

\frac{\mathrm{area}(A)}{\mathrm{area}(B)}=\frac{\mathrm{area}(\Gamma(A))}{\mathrm{area}(\Gamma(B))}.

4.
Discrepancy bounds

In this section we prove the main theorem, using ideas about intersections of curves with lattice boxes that go back to Gauss (e.g. the Gauss circle problem). We leave the proofs of some necessarily lemmas to §
5
.

4.1.
Proof of upper bound of Theorem
1

The next proposition, whose proof is in §
5
, bounds the length of projected spherical cap boundaries under
\Gamma
.

Proposition 1
.

Let
w\in\mathbb{S}^{2},t\in(-1,1)
be chosen and let
\Gamma:\mathbb{S}^{2}\rightarrow R
be as in §
3
. Set
\beta=\Gamma(\partial C(w,t))
, then

\mbox{length}(\beta)\leq 5+\sqrt{2}<6.42.

The length bound translates directly to the error coefficient in the discrepancy, as we see below. Let
H_{N}
be the HEALPix points, i.e., the centers for some tessellation with a total amount of
N
pixels.

Lemma 2
.

For any spherical cap
C=C(w,t)
the boundary of
C
intersects at most
\frac{2(5+\sqrt{2})}{\sqrt{3}}\sqrt{N}+1000
(sub)-pixels.

The proof of Lemma
2
is also left to §
5
. We now show the upper bound of Theorem
1
:

D(H_{N})\leq\frac{2(5+\sqrt{2})}{\sqrt{3}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}+\frac{1000}{N}.

Proof.

Let
C
be an arbitrary spherical cap.
We fix some ordering of the HEALPix points,
H_{N}=\{p_{1},\dots,p_{N}\}
.
To each (mid) point
p_{i}
there corresponds a pixel which we denote by
P_{i}
.
For neighbouring pixels we assign the boundary between them to exactly one pixel.
In this way we have that pixels are disjoint, while still satisfying
\sigma(P_{i})=\sigma(P_{j})
for any
1\leq i,j\leq N
.

By disjointness
\sigma(C)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sigma(C\cap P_{i})
and consequently

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathds{1}_{C}(p_{i})-\sigma(C)\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\frac{1}{N}\mathds{1}_{C}(p_{i})-\sigma(C\cap P_{i})\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left|\frac{1}{N}\mathds{1}_{C}(p_{i})-\sigma(C\cap P_{i})\right|.

If either
C\cap P_{i}=P_{i}
, or
C\cap P_{i}=\varnothing
, then
|\frac{1}{N}\mathds{1}_{C}(p_{i})-\sigma(C\cap P_{i})|=0
, since HEALPix provides an equal area partition.
Otherwise, i.e., when
\partial C
intersects
P_{i}
, one has
|\frac{1}{N}\mathds{1}_{C}(p_{i})-\sigma(C\cap P_{i})|\leq\frac{1}{N}
.
So by Lemma
2
we can bound the local discrepancy as follows

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left|\frac{1}{N}\mathds{1}_{C}(p_{i})-\sigma(C\cap P_{i})\right|\leq\frac{2(5+\sqrt{2})}{\sqrt{3}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}+\frac{1000}{N}.

Since this estimate holds for arbitrary spherical caps the result follows.
∎

4.2.
Proof of lower bound of Theorem
1

The lower bound for
\mathcal{D}(H_{N})
is easily achieved by considering the specific spherical cap
C
that is centered at the north pole
p_{n}
and has height
t=-\frac{1}{N}
: that is, it covers slightly more than half of the surface area of the sphere.

\lvert H_{N}\rvert=N=12\cdot 4^{\ell}
, so in each of the 4 base pixels on the equatorial belt, there are
4^{\ell}
subpixels. Hence there are
2^{\ell}
subpixels on the “diagonal” of each base pixel, and their centers are on the equator. Multiplying by 4, we obtain that the equator contains
\sqrt{\frac{4N}{3}}
points in
H_{N}
. Thus, the local cap discrepancy satisfies

\mathcal{D}_{C\big(p_{n},-\tfrac{1}{N}\big)}(H_{N})=\frac{2}{\sqrt{3}}N^{-1/2}+O(N^{-1}).

4.3.
Remark on jittered sampling in HEALPix pixels

We remark that if the point set
H_{N}
of centers of HEALPix pixels is replaced by a point set
J_{N}
obtained by choosing a point at random from each pixel, as in jittered sampling methods, we obtain the following upper bound matching Beck’s:

\mathcal{D}(J_{N})\leq CN^{-3/4}\sqrt{\log N}.

This can be seen roughly as follows. Replace the class
\mathcal{C}
of all spherical caps
C(w,t)
with those who have rational
w
and
t
: call this class
\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{Q}}
. The discrepancy with respect to
\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{Q}}
is the same as the discrepancy with respect to
\mathcal{C}
, but has finite VC dimension (see
[
1
, Proposition 8]
).

We can now apply the jittered sampling upper bound for discs given in
[
17

5.1.
Intersection of convex curves with fundamental domains

Most of the contents of this section also appear verbatim in a work of the first-named author

Definition 4
.

A continuous curve
\beta:[a,b]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
(
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
,
a<b
) is
n
-convex for
n\in\mathbb{N}
, if there are points
a=t_{0}<t_{1}<\ldots<t_{n-1}<t_{n}=b
with the property that
\beta\big([t_{j},t_{j+1}]\big)
is a convex curve for each
0\leq j<n
, i.e. there exist convex sets
A_{1},\ldots,A_{n}
with
\beta\big([t_{j},t_{j+1}]\big)\subset\partial A_{j}
.

A circle is
1
-convex, as is a straight line segment. We will give a characterization of
n
-convex regular
\mathcal{C}^{2}
-curves in Lemma
3
in terms of sub-spirals, which are defined next.

Definition 5
.

A sub-spiral
S
is a
\mathcal{C}^{2}
-curve with compact domain, no self-intersections, and such that the curvature exists, is bounded, and does not change sign.

A spiral is a sub-spiral, but a circle is not, as it intersects itself at one point. Recall that a curve
\beta
is called regular if
\|\dot{\beta}(t)\|>0
everywhere on its domain, where the derivative at boundary points is defined via one-sided limits.

Lemma 3
.

A regular
\mathcal{C}^{2}
-curve
\beta
is
n
-convex if and only if it consists of finitely many sub-spirals.

Proof.

If
\beta
is
n
-convex, take arbitrary
c_{0},\ldots,c_{n}
with
t_{j}<c_{j}<t_{j+1}
(the
t_{j}
as in Definition
4

\beta\big([t_{j},c_{j}]\big)
and
\beta\big([c_{j},t_{j+1}]\big)
is a sub-spiral.

To show the other implication, let us first assume
\beta:[a,b]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
is a sub-spiral itself and we define for each
\tau\in[a,b]

\dot{\beta}^{-}(\tau)=\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{\beta(\tau)-\beta(\tau+\epsilon)}{\epsilon}.

By regularity we have
\|\dot{\beta}^{-}(\tau)\|>0
, and we define the half-line

L(\tau)=\big\{T(\rho,\beta(\tau))\ \mid\ \rho\geq 0\big\}\hskip 8.5359pt\mbox{where}\hskip 8.5359ptT(\rho,\beta(\tau))=\beta(\tau)+\rho\dot{\beta}^{-}(\tau).

We will now introduce an algorithm to find all necessary
t_{j}
’s:

Loop 1.
If
L(a)
doesn’t intersect
\beta
, proceed to Loop 2. Otherwise let

\rho_{1}=\min_{\rho}\{T(\rho,\beta(a))\cap\beta\big([a,b]\big)\neq\emptyset\}.

If
T(\rho_{1},\beta(a))=\beta(b)
, proceed to Loop 2. Otherwise define
t_{1}
so that
\beta(t_{1})=T(\rho_{1},\beta(a))
. If

L(t_{1})
doesn’t intersect
\beta
, proceed to Loop 2. Else set

\rho_{2}=\min_{\rho}\{T(\rho,\beta(t_{1}))\cap\beta\big([a,b]\big)\neq\emptyset\},

and
t_{2}
so that
\beta(t_{2})=T(\rho_{2},\beta(t_{1}))
. By construction,

\beta([t_{1},t_{2}])
does not intersect the line segment
T\big([0,\rho_{1}],\beta(a)\big)
, and thus it coils around
\beta(a)
.
If
\beta(t_{2})=\beta(b)
, proceed to Loop 2. Otherwise regard
L(t_{2})
and define
\rho_{3},t_{3}
similarly or proceed to Loop 2. It then follows from constant sign of curvature, no self-intersection and choices of
\rho_{s}
that

coils around
\beta(a)
and
\beta(t_{1})
. Continuing in this fashion we see that this process has to terminate since
\beta
has finite length, and we proceed to Loop 2.

Loop 2.
Let
\{t_{1},\ldots,t_{k}\}
be the values generated in Loop 1 (potentially empty). We now reverse the parametrization:
\beta^{\leftarrow}:[a,b]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
with
\beta^{\leftarrow}(\tau)=\beta(a+b-\tau)
, and proceed as in Loop 1 for the curve
\beta^{\leftarrow}
, where we replace the command ”proceed to Loop 2” by “terminate algorithm”.

This way potentially further points are generated, say
\{t_{k+1},\ldots,t_{k+m}\}
, where
t_{k+m}=b
, and we see that
\beta
is
(k+m)
-convex.
If
\beta
is the concatenation of sub-spirals
S_{k}
, then we apply the algorithm above to each
S_{k}
, where we collect all the values
t_{j}
in one list and extend it by the points of separation between

and

if necessary.
∎

To see if a
\mathcal{C}^{2}
-curve is
n
-convex it is hence enough to find the points of self-intersection and zeros of curvature where the sign changes – the arcs between these points then are sub-spirals, and the algorithm above will find
n
.

The images

5.3.
Curvature calculations

For the proof of Corollary
1
we will now calculate the signed curvature
\kappa
of the planar curve
\Gamma(\partial C)\subset R
and give a universal bound on the number of zeros. Here
x
and
y
are the coordinates of
\Gamma\circ\gamma\circ(\text{param.}\eqref{parametrization1})
and
x^{\prime}
and
y^{\prime}
denote the derivatives of
x
and
y
with respect to
\theta
:

\kappa=\frac{x^{\prime}y^{\prime\prime}-y^{\prime}x^{\prime\prime}}{\big((x^{\prime})^{2}+(y^{\prime})^{2}\big)^{3/2}}.

We see immediately that if
w
is a pole, then
\kappa(\theta)\equiv 0
since the
y
value is constant. Similarly, if
\partial C
goes through both poles, then
\kappa(\theta)\equiv 0
since the
x
value is constant. These caps are treated separately in Corollary
1
, and hence we assume in the subsequent calculations that
\theta_{w}\in(0,\frac{\pi}{2}]
, and
t>0
in case
\theta_{w}=\frac{\pi}{2}
.

5.3.1.
Equatorial belt region

From the definition of
\Gamma
it follows that the part of
\Gamma(\partial C)
that belongs to the equatorial belt has the following parametrization (note that
\sqrt{5}/3\leq\sin(\theta)\leq 1
):

x(\theta)=\frac{1}{\pi}\arccos\Big(\frac{t-\cos(\theta)c_{w}}{\sin(\theta)s_{w}}\Big)+\frac{\phi_{w}}{\pi},

and

y(\theta)=\frac{3}{8}\cos(\theta).

The derivatives needed for the curvature are as follows

\displaystyle x^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}\frac{-\sin(\theta)s_{w}}{\sqrt{\sin(\theta)^{2}s_{w}^{2}-(t-\cos(\theta)c_{w})^{2}}}\frac{\sin(\theta)^{2}s_{w}c_{w}-(t-\cos(\theta)c_{w})\cos(\theta)s_{w}}{\sin(\theta)^{2}s_{w}^{2}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\pi A}\frac{c_{w}-t\cos(\theta)}{\sin(\theta)},

where we use the abbreviation

A=\sqrt{\sin(\theta)^{2}s_{w}^{2}-(t-\cos(\theta)c_{w})^{2}}.

Further

\displaystyle\pi x^{\prime\prime}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sin(\theta)\cos(\theta)s_{w}^{2}-(t-\cos(\theta)c_{w})\sin(\theta)c_{w}}{\big(\sin(\theta)^{2}s_{w}^{2}-(t-\cos(\theta)c_{w})^{2}\big)^{3/2}}\frac{c_{w}-t\cos(\theta)}{\sin(\theta)}

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt-\frac{t\sin(\theta)^{2}-(c_{w}-t\cos(\theta))\cos(\theta)}{\sqrt{\sin(\theta)^{2}s_{w}^{2}-(t-\cos(\theta)c_{w})^{2}}\sin(\theta)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\big(\cos(\theta)-tc_{w}\big)\big(c_{w}-t\cos(\theta)\big)}{A^{3}}-\frac{t-c_{w}\cos(\theta)}{A\sin(\theta)^{2}}

and

\displaystyle y^{\prime}(\theta)=-\frac{3}{8}\sin(\theta)\hskip 14.22636pt\text{and}\hskip 14.22636pty^{\prime\prime}(\theta)=-\frac{3}{8}\cos(\theta).

Thus
x^{\prime}y^{\prime\prime}-y^{\prime}x^{\prime\prime}=

\displaystyle\frac{3}{\pi 8A}\Big(\big(c_{w}-t\cos(\theta)\big)\big(\cot(\theta)+\big(\cos(\theta)-tc_{w}\big)\frac{\sin(\theta)}{A^{2}}\big)-\frac{t-c_{w}\cos(\theta)}{\sin(\theta)}\Big)

\displaystyle=\frac{3}{\pi 8A}\Big(\cot(\theta)2c_{w}+\big(\cos(\theta)-tc_{w}\big)\big(c_{w}-t\cos(\theta)\big)\frac{\sin(\theta)}{A^{2}}-\frac{t(1+\cos(\theta)^{2})}{\sin(\theta)}\Big),

and

x^{\prime 2}+y^{\prime 2}=\frac{1}{\pi^{2}}\frac{1}{A^{2}}\frac{\big(c_{w}-t\cos(\theta)\big)^{2}}{\sin(\theta)^{2}}+\frac{9}{64}\sin(\theta)^{2}.

Assuming
A\neq 0
, we obtain

\kappa(\theta)=\frac{3A^{2}\Big(\cot(\theta)2c_{w}+\frac{1}{A^{2}}\big(\cos(\theta)-tc_{w}\big)\big(c_{w}-t\cos(\theta)\big)\sin(\theta)-\frac{t(1+\cos(\theta)^{2})}{\sin(\theta)}\Big)}{\pi 8\Big(\frac{1}{\pi^{2}}\frac{\big(c_{w}-t\cos(\theta)\big)^{2}}{\sin(\theta)^{2}}+A^{2}\frac{9}{64}\sin(\theta)^{2}\Big)^{\frac{3}{2}}.}

This equation can be continuously extended to
A=0
. By the trigonometric angle sum formula,

\displaystyle A^{2}

\displaystyle=\big[\sin(\theta)s_{w}-t+\cos(\theta)c_{w}\big]\big[\sin(\theta)s_{w}+t-\cos(\theta)c_{w}\big]

\displaystyle=\big[\cos(\theta-\theta_{w})-t\big]\big[t-\cos(\theta+\theta_{w})\big]

is zero only for
\theta\in[\arccos(\tfrac{2}{3}),\arccos(-\tfrac{2}{3})]\cap\{\arccos(t)+\theta_{w},\lvert\arccos(t)-\theta_{w}\rvert\}.
We also make sure that
c_{w}-t\cos(\theta)
is not zero when
A
is, thus plugging in
\theta=\arccos(t)+\theta_{w}
and
\theta=\lvert\arccos(t)-\theta_{w}\rvert
:

\displaystyle c_{w}-t(c_{w}t-s_{w}\sqrt{1-t^{2}})

\displaystyle=c_{w}(1-t^{2})+s_{w}t\sqrt{1-t^{2}},

\displaystyle c_{w}-t(c_{w}t+s_{w}\sqrt{1-t^{2}})

\displaystyle=\sqrt{1-t^{2}}\sin\big(\arccos(t)-\theta_{w}\big).

Since
0\leq t<1
, the first value above is always positive, while the second is not zero since
\theta=\arccos(t)-\theta_{w}\in\{0,\pi\}
is not in the domain of the parametrization.

Thus the question if the curvature changes sign is reduced to finding
\theta
such that

A^{2}\cos(\theta)2c_{w}+\sin(\theta)^{2}\big(\cos(\theta)-tc_{w}\big)\big(c_{w}-t\cos(\theta)\big)-A^{2}t(1+\cos(\theta)^{2})=0

which can be expanded in the notation
\sin(\theta)=s_{\theta}
,
\cos(\theta)=c_{\theta}
as

\displaystyle 2c_{\theta}c_{w}s_{\theta}^{2}s_{w}^{2}-2c_{\theta}c_{w}t^{2}+4tc_{\theta}^{2}c_{w}^{2}-2c_{\theta}^{3}c_{w}^{3}+s_{\theta}^{2}c_{\theta}c_{w}-tc_{\theta}^{2}s_{\theta}^{2}-tc_{w}^{2}s_{\theta}^{2}+t^{2}c_{\theta}c_{w}s_{\theta}^{2}

\displaystyle-ts_{\theta}^{2}s_{w}^{2}+t^{3}-2t^{2}c_{\theta}c_{w}+tc_{\theta}^{2}c_{w}^{2}-tc_{\theta}^{2}s_{\theta}^{2}s_{w}^{2}+t^{3}c_{\theta}^{2}-2t^{2}c_{\theta}^{3}c_{w}+tc_{\theta}^{4}c_{w}^{2}=0.

Using that
s_{\theta}^{2}=1-c_{\theta}^{2}
, we see that
this is a fourth degree polynomial in
c_{\theta}
and has at most 4 roots for
c_{\theta}\in[-\tfrac{2}{3},\tfrac{2}{3}]
. Therefore, there are at most
8
zeros of curvature.

5.3.2.
North and south pole caps

Here we discuss the curvature of
\Gamma(\partial C)
in the north and south pole caps.
From the definition of
\Gamma
it follows that the part of
\Gamma(\partial C)
that falls in the north pole cap can be parametrized as follows. (The parametrization of the south pole cap is analogous, so we just focus on the north pole cap.)

x(\theta)=\frac{\phi}{\pi}-\frac{1}{\pi}\big(1-\sqrt{1-\cos(\theta)}\sqrt{3}\big)\cdot(\phi\hskip-5.69046pt\mod\tfrac{\pi}{2}-\tfrac{\pi}{4}),

and by trigonometric half-angle identities

y(\theta)=\frac{1}{4}\big(2-\sqrt{1-\cos(\theta)}\sqrt{3}\big)=\frac{1}{2}-\sin\Big(\frac{\theta}{2}\Big)\frac{\sqrt{6}}{4}.

It follows that

\displaystyle y^{\prime}(\theta)=-\cos\Big(\frac{\theta}{2}\Big)\frac{\sqrt{6}}{8}\hskip 14.22636pt\text{and}\hskip 14.22636pty^{\prime\prime}(\theta)=\sin\Big(\frac{\theta}{2}\Big)\frac{\sqrt{6}}{16}.

As for the derivative of
x
, note that the factor
\phi\hskip-5.69046pt\mod\frac{\pi}{2}
can be written as
\phi(\theta)-j\frac{\pi}{2}
for appropriate
j
and
\theta
, thus in the derivative we can ignore that part:

\displaystyle x^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\frac{\phi^{\prime}}{\pi}\Big(1-1+\sqrt{1-\cos(\theta)}\sqrt{3}\Big)-\frac{4y^{\prime}}{\pi}\big(\phi\hskip-5.69046pt\mod\tfrac{\pi}{2}-\tfrac{\pi}{4}\big)

\displaystyle=16\frac{\phi^{\prime}y^{\prime\prime}}{\pi}-\frac{4y^{\prime}}{\pi}\big(\phi\hskip-5.69046pt\mod\tfrac{\pi}{2}-\tfrac{\pi}{4}\big)

and thus, making use of the fact that
y^{\prime\prime\prime}=\frac{1}{4}y^{\prime}
,

\displaystyle x^{\prime\prime}

\displaystyle=16\frac{\phi^{\prime\prime}y^{\prime\prime}-\phi^{\prime}y^{\prime}/4}{\pi}-\frac{4y^{\prime\prime}}{\pi}\big(\phi\hskip-5.69046pt\mod\tfrac{\pi}{2}-\tfrac{\pi}{4}\big)-\frac{4y^{\prime}\phi^{\prime}}{\pi}

\displaystyle=\frac{16}{\pi}\phi^{\prime\prime}y^{\prime\prime}-\frac{4y^{\prime\prime}}{\pi}\big(\phi\hskip-5.69046pt\mod\tfrac{\pi}{2}-\tfrac{\pi}{4}\big)-\frac{8}{\pi}y^{\prime}\phi^{\prime}.

Note that the term

(y^{\prime})^{2}=\frac{3}{8^{2}}(1+\cos(\theta))

is zero only if
\theta=\pi
, but since we restricted ourselves to the case
0\leq\theta_{w}\leq\frac{\pi}{2}
and
0\leq t<1
due to symmetry, this would mean that
t=0
and
\theta_{w}=\frac{\pi}{2}
, but these caps can be disregarded here because they are dealt with separately in Corollary
1
. Thus

(8)

\kappa(\theta)=\frac{16\frac{\phi^{\prime}(y^{\prime\prime})^{2}}{\pi}-\frac{16}{\pi}\phi^{\prime\prime}y^{\prime\prime}y^{\prime}+\frac{8}{\pi}(y^{\prime})^{2}\phi^{\prime}}{\Big[\Big(16\frac{\phi^{\prime}y^{\prime\prime}}{\pi}-\frac{4y^{\prime}}{\pi}\big(\phi\hskip-5.69046pt\mod\tfrac{\pi}{2}-\tfrac{\pi}{4}\big)\Big)^{2}+\frac{3}{8^{2}}(1+\cos(\theta))\Big]^{3/2}}.

To show that the curvature is well defined, we proceed as in the previous section - with one exception: if the north pole is in
\partial C
, then
\theta=0
is in the domain of parametrization. If this case occurs, then we can use the parametrization (
6
)
and obtain subsequently with
\tau=\frac{t}{\sqrt{1-t^{2}}}
(note that
t=0
is excluded, because
\theta_{w}=\frac{\pi}{2}
in that case):

\phi^{\prime}=-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\tau+\tau\tan\big(\tfrac{\theta}{2}\big)^{2}}{\sqrt{1-\tau^{2}\tan\big(\tfrac{\theta}{2}\big)^{2}}}

and using the product formula for the derivative,

\displaystyle\phi^{\prime\prime}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\tau}{4}\frac{\tau^{2}\tan\big(\tfrac{\theta}{2}\big)\big(1+\tan\big(\tfrac{\theta}{2}\big)^{2}\big)}{\Big(1-\tau^{2}\tan\big(\tfrac{\theta}{2}\big)^{2}\Big)^{3/2}}\big(1+\tan\big(\tfrac{\theta}{2}\big)^{2}\big)-\frac{\tau}{2}\frac{\tan\big(\tfrac{\theta}{2}\big)\big(1+\tan\big(\tfrac{\theta}{2}\big)^{2}\big)}{\sqrt{1-\tau^{2}\tan\big(\tfrac{\theta}{2}\big)^{2}}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\tau}{2}\frac{\tan\big(\tfrac{\theta}{2}\big)\big(1+\tan\big(\tfrac{\theta}{2}\big)^{2}\big)}{\sqrt{1-\tau^{2}\tan\big(\tfrac{\theta}{2}\big)^{2}}}\left(\frac{\tau^{2}}{2}\frac{\big(1+\tan\big(\tfrac{\theta}{2}\big)^{2}\big)}{1-\tau^{2}\tan\big(\tfrac{\theta}{2}\big)^{2}}+1\right).

Thus the curvature is hence well defined for
0<\theta<2\arctan\big(\frac{\sqrt{1-t^{2}}}{t}\big)
and can be continuously extended to zero by

\kappa(0)=\frac{16\frac{\phi^{\prime}\cdot 0}{\pi}-\frac{16}{\pi}\cdot 0\cdot y^{\prime\prime}y^{\prime}-\frac{8}{\pi}\frac{6}{8^{2}}\frac{\tau}{2}}{\Big[8^{2}\tau^{2}\frac{\phi^{\prime}\cdot 0}{\pi}+\frac{6}{4\pi^{2}}\big(\phi_{w}\hskip-5.69046pt\mod\tfrac{\pi}{2}-\tfrac{\pi}{4}\big)^{2}+\frac{6}{8^{2}}\Big]^{3/2}}\leq-\frac{2^{3}}{\pi\sqrt{3}}\frac{t}{\sqrt{1-t^{2}}}.

A similar calculation, using the parametrization (
7
), yields that
\theta=\pi
can be treated in the same fashion. We hence also know that the curvature is not zero for
\theta\in\{0,\pi\}
, and by continuity, also not in an neighborhood of these values.

The curvature
\kappa(\theta)
thus changes sign, if at all, at

\frac{16\phi^{\prime}}{\pi}(y^{\prime\prime})^{2}-\frac{16}{\pi}\phi^{\prime\prime}y^{\prime\prime}y^{\prime}+\frac{8\phi^{\prime}}{\pi}(y^{\prime})^{2}=0.

We note that

y^{\prime\prime}y^{\prime}=-\sin(\theta)\frac{3}{128}\hskip 14.22636pt\mbox{and}\hskip 14.22636pt(y^{\prime\prime})^{2}=\frac{3}{16^{2}}(1-\cos(\theta)),

thus we equivalently want, letting
\xi=\frac{\phi}{\pi}
and multiplying by
\sin(\theta)A^{3}
,

\sin(\theta)A^{3}\left(\xi^{\prime}\frac{3}{16}(1-\cos(\theta))+\frac{3\sin(\theta)}{8}\xi^{\prime\prime}+\frac{3}{8}(1+\cos(\theta))\xi^{\prime}\right)=0;

or if we multiply by
\frac{16}{3}

\sin(\theta)A^{3}\Big(2\sin(\theta)\xi^{\prime\prime}+(3+\cos(\theta))\xi^{\prime}\Big)=0.

Next we use the expansion of
\xi^{\prime}=x^{\prime}(\theta)
(this refers to the
x
in §
5.3.1
) to obtain a polynomial in
c_{\theta}
of degree at most five
which has at most 5 roots for
c_{\theta}\in[\tfrac{2}{3},1]
.

5.3.3.
Curvature for the other parametrization

If we had chosen to work with parametrization (
5
) instead, then in the equatorial belt region only the sign of
\kappa
would have changed, as the derivatives of the
x
-values flip signs. In the polar cap regions the curvatures of the two parametrizations have opposing signs as can be seen from formula (
8
), and are zero for the same values
\theta_{0}
. In either region, a zero for the curvature of one paramatrization is also a zero for the other. Since the
y
-values are the same for both parametrizations, such zeros have the same
y
-value.

Note that the value of 36 in Corollary
1
is obtained by summing the total number of zeros of curvature for one parametrization, and then doubling it.

Here we detail (for the reader’s convenience) an indexing of pixels in the HEALPix tessellation at an arbitrary level
\ell
, and give formulas for their centers, in other words the HEALPix point set. This is an alternative to the indexing presented in
[
14
]
and may be useful for programming.

As before, we use
\theta\in[0,\pi)
to denote angle with the north pole, so
z=\cos(\theta)
.
\phi\in[0,2\pi)
denotes the angle with the
x
axis on the
xy
-plane. We define a pixel by
(s,k,\ell,r,c)
. Each index is described below.

•

s\in\{-1,0,1\}
denotes whether the pixel is one of the north base pixels, one of the equatorial base pixels, or one of the south base pixels (
s=1
,
s=0
,
s=-1
, respectively).

•

k\in\{0,1,2,3\}
defines which of the four base pixels on level
s
the pixel is in. For
s=\pm 1
,
k=0
corresponds to the base pixel whose boundary touches
z=\pm\frac{2}{3}
at the points
\phi=0
and
\phi=\frac{\pi}{2}
. For
s=0
,
k=0
corresponds to the base equatorial pixel centered on the arc
\phi=0
. For
k>0
, this value of

indicates a rotation by
\frac{k\pi}{2}
radians in
\phi
.

•

\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
denotes the resolution of the pixelation. In each base pixel, there are
4^{\ell}
pixels of resolution
\ell
, in a grid with
2^{\ell}
rows and
2^{\ell}
columns.

•

r\in\{1,\cdots,2^{\ell}\}
indexes the
2^{\ell}
parallels to the northeast boundary in the grid of subpixels of the base pixel. In the north pole cap and equatorial belt, the indexing starts on the northeastern side of the base pixel and ends at the southwestern side;
r=1
denotes the pixels that have the northeastern boundary of the base pixel as their boundary. In the south pole cap, the indexing starts on the southeastern side of the base pixel and ends at the northwestern side.

•

c\in\{1,\cdots,2^{\ell}\}
indexes the
2^{\ell}
parallels to the northwest boundary in the grid of subpixels of the base pixel. In the north pole cap and equatorial belt, the indexing starts on the northwestern side of the base pixel;

denotes the pixels that have the northwestern boundary of the base pixel as their boundary. In the south pole cap, the indexing starts on the southwestern side of the base pixel and ends at the northeastern side.

p_{(2c-1),k,\ell+1}
, which is at

\phi=\frac{\pi(r-c)}{4L}+\frac{(2k+1)\pi}{4}

and

z=\frac{4}{3}-\frac{2(r+c-1)}{3L}.

6.2.
Pixels contained in north and south caps or on their boundaries

We concern ourselves only with the pixel centers that lie in the north pole cap or on its boundary, which occurs when
r+c\leq L+1
(again, the centers for pixels in the south pole cap are obtained by changing the sign of the
z
-value).

The center of pixel
(1,k,\ell,r,c)
is given by the intersection of the curves
p_{2c-1,k,\ell}^{n}
and
m_{2r-1,k,\ell}^{n}
, which occurs at

\phi=k\frac{\pi}{2}+\frac{\pi(2c-1)}{4(r+c-1)}

and

z=1-\frac{(r+c-1)^{2}}{3L^{2}}.
