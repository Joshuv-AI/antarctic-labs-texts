Abstract
If this article, an elementary kernel-cokernel exact sequence is introduced for A → f B → g C A\stackrel{{\scriptstyle f}}{{\to}}B\stackrel{{\scriptstyle g}}{{\to}}C . Some relative sequences and applications are discussed. This result can simplify some proofs—the indices of Frodholm operators, Harada and Sai theorem, and the derived couples of exact couples.

Index of Fredholm operator or Herbrand quotient

Let
\mathcal{C}
be an abelian category, if there is a well-defined length function
\ell

over some Serre subcategory (closed under kernel and cokernel),
say
\mathcal{C}_{\ell<\infty}
such that for any exact sequence in
\mathcal{C}_{\ell<\infty}
,

0\to A^{\prime}\to A\to A^{\prime\prime}\to 0.

we have
\ell(A^{\prime})+\ell(A^{\prime\prime})=\ell(A)
.
Or abstractly,
\ell
is an element in Grothendieck group of
\mathcal{C}_{\ell<\infty}
.
We say an morphism
A\stackrel{{\scriptstyle f}}{{\to}}B
is Fredholm, if
\ker f
and
\operatorname{cok}f
are in
\mathcal{C}_{\ell<\infty}
.

For any Fredholm morphism
A\stackrel{{\scriptstyle f}}{{\to}}B
, we can consider the index (Herbrand quotient)

\operatorname{ind}(f)=\ell(\operatorname{cok}f)-\ell(\ker f).

Using our exact sequence, and the euler characteristic we have

Theorem 4

If
f
,
g
and
gf
are all Fredholm morphisms, then

\operatorname{ind}(gf)=\operatorname{ind}f+\operatorname{ind}g.

Note that the proof that the index of composition of Fredholm operators is the sum of indices is really a hard work in standard books.
In functional analysis, we define the Fredholm operator to be the bounded linear operator between Banach space with closed image and
having finite dimensional kernel and cokernel. the index of Fredholm operator
A
is defined to be the

\operatorname{ind}A=\dim\ker A-\dim\ker A^{*},

which is exactly
\dim\ker A-\dim\operatorname{cok}A
, see
[
2
]
Page 354.
But the proof of
\operatorname{ind}(BA)=\operatorname{ind}A+\operatorname{ind}B
there is really hard.
When we proved the composition of Fredholm operator is still Fredholm operator (this part is easy),
the proof can be direct by our more general consequence.

It also works well for the (co)homology of finite groups,
since the (co)homology groups are finite for finite generated module.
Here it is efficient to take
h(f)=\frac{\#(\operatorname{cok}f)}{\#(\ker f)}
rather than additive.
Let
M
be an
G
-module, consider the norm map

N_{G}^{M}\quad:\quad M_{G}\longrightarrow M^{G}\qquad x\longmapsto\sum gx

we can define

h(M)=h(N_{G})=\frac{\#\tilde{H}^{0}(G;M)}{\#\tilde{H}_{-1}(G,M)}.

Here
\tilde{H}^{\bullet}(G;M)
is Tate-cohomology group.
In particular, it works very well for cyclic groups, since now Tate group is cyclic, now we have
h(M)=h(M^{\prime})+h(M^{\prime\prime})
for exact sequence

0\to M^{\prime}\to M\to M^{\prime\prime}\to 0.

A simpler proof of Harada and Sai theorem

In representation of associative algebras,
we have the following theorem by Harada and Sai, which is useful in proving the first Brauer–Thrall conjecture
c.f.
[
4
]
volume one page 139.
The proof there is standard, not difficult, but I will give a direct and simpler proof.

Theorem 5
(Harada and Sai)

Let
M_{1},\ldots,M_{2^{n}}
be indecomposable module of length no more than
n
,
if we have a sequence

M_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle f_{1}}}{{\to}}M_{2}\to\cdots\to M_{2^{n}-1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle f_{2^{n}-1}}}{{\to}}M_{2^{n}}

if each of
f_{i}
is not isomorphism, then the composition
f_{{2^{n}}-1}\cdots f_{1}=0
.

We prove by induction that the image of
f_{{2^{m}}-1}\cdots f_{1}=0
them are of length no more than
n-m
.
For
m=1
, this is trivial, since it is not an isomorphism.
For general case, consider the half
M_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle g}}{{\to}}M_{2^{m-1}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle h}}{{\to}}M_{2^{m}}
. We have

0\to\ker g\stackrel{{\scriptstyle*}}{{\to}}\ker hg\to\ker h\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\delta}}{{\to}}\operatorname{cok}g\to\operatorname{cok}hg\stackrel{{\scriptstyle**}}{{\to}}\operatorname{cok}h\to 0

•

If
(*)
is not isomorphism, then
\operatorname{im}hg=M_{1}/\ker hg
whose length is strictly less than
\operatorname{im}g=M_{1}/\ker g
, so it follows by induction hypothesis.

•

If
(**)
is not isomorphism, then
\operatorname{im}hg
whose length is strictly less than
\operatorname{im}h
, so it follows by induction hypothesis.

•

If
\delta
is isomorphism, then
\ker h\to M_{2^{m-1}}\to\operatorname{im}g
is an isomorphism, but
M_{2^{m-1}}
is assumed to be indecomposable,
so
\ker h=0
and
\operatorname{im}g=0
. Now
M_{2^{m-1}}
is of less length of
M_{2^{n}}
—otherwise they are isomorphic.
