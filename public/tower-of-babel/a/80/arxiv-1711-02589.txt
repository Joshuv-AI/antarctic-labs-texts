Gene flow across geographical barriers - scaling limits of random walks with obstacles
Raphaël Forien

Abstract
We study a class of random walks which behave like simple random walks outside of a bounded region around the origin and which are subject to a partial reflection near the origin. We obtain a non trivial scaling limit which behaves like reflected Brownian motion until its local time at zero reaches an exponential variable. It then follows reflected Brownian motion on the other side of the origin until its local time at zero reaches another exponential level, etc. These random walks are used in population genetics to trace the position of ancestors in the past near geographical barriers. Keywords: generalised Brownian motions;random walks with obstacles;partial reflection;barriers to gene flow. 2010 MSC: 60G99, 60F99, 60J65, 60J70



Introduction

Barriers to gene flow are physical obstacles to migration.
Examples include mountain ranges, highways, political borders and the Great Wall of China
[
28
]
.
All these geographical features leave traces in the genetic composition of populations living on both sides of the barrier.
Geneticists try to use these traces to detect barriers to gene flow and to quantify their effect on migration.

A naive approach to this problem would be to compute a measure of genetic differentiation (
e.g.
Wright’s F
st

[
29
]
which measures the level of inbreeding in the population resulting from its structure
[
24
]
) between the two populations on each side of the candidate barrier, and to say that the latter acts as a barrier to gene flow if two individuals living on the same side are more related to each other on average than two indivudals living on different sides of the barrier.

This method assumes that the two subpopulations on each side of the obstacle are well mixed.
This may not always be a reasonable assumption and in some cases it is preferable to take into account the finer scale geographic structure of the sampled population.

Mathematical models for spatially extended populations with barriers to gene flow already exist in the literature
[
13
,
27
]
, but most assume a discrete space and finding analytical formulae in this framework is challenging at best.
Such formulae are particularly useful for inference purposes, where computational power is limiting.
This paper is a step towards a rigorous mathematical framework to model genetic isolation by distance with barriers to gene flow in a continuous space.

Stepping stone model with a barrier at the origin

Nagylaki and his co-authors proposed the following model for the evolution of a spatially structured population with a barrier to gene flow
[

,
15

17

Consider a population living in a discrete linear space, with colonies (or demes) at locations
\{\ldots,-2,-1,1,2,\ldots\}
.
Each deme contains
N

An individual in deme
i\notin\{-1,1\}
has its parent in the previous generation in deme
i-1
or
i+1
with probability
m/2
for some
m\in(0,1)
, otherwise its parent is drawn from deme
i
.
Individuals in deme
1
have their parent in deme
2
with probability
m/2
and in deme
-1
with probability
cm/2
, with
c\in(0,1)
, and likewise individuals in deme
-1
have their parent in deme
1
with probability
cm/2
.
Migration probabilities are depicted in Figure

.
Properties of this model and applications to various settings were studied in a sequence of papers
[

,
14
,
19
,
16
,
20
]
.

In this model, two individuals sampled at a given distance from each other will be more related if they are sampled on the same side of the origin than if they are not.
This can be seen by assuming that each new individual mutates to a type never seen before with some probability
\mu\in(0,1)
.
Relatedness between individuals can then be measured by the probability that two sampled individuals are of the same type.
This probability is called the probability of identity by descent, and it is given by the probability generating function of the age of the most recent common ancestor of these individuals.
Properties of this function were studied in this setting in
[
2
]
.

Also of interest is the evolution of the frequency of a given type (or allele) in the population, denoted by
(p^{N}(t,x),x\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\},t\geq 0)

Ignoring mutations and setting

\bar{p}(t,x)=\mathbb{E}\left[p^{N}(t,x)\right],

\bar{p}

, Nagylaki
[

showed that

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}p(t,x)=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\partial_{xx}p(t,x)\qquad\text{ for }x\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\},\\
\partial_{x}p(t,0^{-})=\partial_{x}p(t,0^{+})=\gamma(p(t,0^{+})-p(t,0^{-}))\end{cases}

(1)

where
\sigma^{2}=m
, see Figure

In
[
14
]
(see also
[

]
), Nagylaki showed a similar approximation for the probability of identity by descent.

Duality

An alternative way to study this model from the forwards in time evolution of types is to look back in time for the position of one’s ancestor some number of generations in the past.
If
\xi^{x}_{t}
denotes the position of the ancestor
t
generations ago of an individual sampled at
x\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
, then
(\xi^{x}_{t},t\geq 0)
is a random walk with transition probabilities given by the migration rates in Figure
1
.
In the absence of mutations, the proportion of individuals carrying a given allele at location
x
is the proportion of those individuals whose ancestor
t
generations ago carried the same allele.
As a result, for
x\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
and
t\geq 0
,

\bar{p}(t,x)=\mathbb{E}\left[p^{N}(0,\xi^{x}_{t})\right].

Likewise, the probability of identity by descent can be expressed with the help of the coalescence time of two random walks
\xi^{x},\xi^{y}
,
i.e.
the first time that the two ancestors have the same parent.

Scaling limits of random walks with obstacles

In this paper, we present a result on the scaling limits of a class of random walks with obstacles which includes
(\xi^{x}_{t},t\geq 0)
.
For
n\geq 1
, if we set

X_{n}(t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\xi_{nt}^{\lfloor\sqrt{n}x\rfloor},

and if
c
is of order
n^{-1/2}
, we show that
X_{n}
converges in distribution to a continuous stochastic process.
This process resembles Brownian motion everywhere except near the origin where it has a singular behaviour.
More precisely, this process behaves like reflected Brownian motion until its local time at the origin reaches an exponential random variable, after which it becomes reflected Brownian motion on the other side of the origin, until its local time reaches another exponential variable, and so on.
We call this process partially reflected Brownian motion.
It generalises elastic Brownian motion considered for example in
[
5
]
.

The same process was obtained as a limit of one dimensional diffusions in
[
12
]
.
For
\varepsilon>0
, they consider
(X_{\varepsilon}(t),t\geq 0)
, solution to

dX_{\varepsilon}(t)=\frac{L_{\varepsilon}}{\varepsilon}a\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}X_{\varepsilon}(t)\right)dt+dB_{t},

where
B
is standard Brownian motion,
L_{\varepsilon}\to\infty
as
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
and
\operatorname{sign}(x)a(x)\geq 0
,
supp(a)\subset[-1,1]
, and they give conditions on
L_{\varepsilon}
under which
X_{\varepsilon}
converges to partially reflected Brownian motion (which they call Brownian motion with a hard membrane).

This process also appears in
[
10
]
under the name
snapping out Brownian motion
and is obtained as a limit of one dimensional diffusions with a small diffusivity in a thin layer around the origin.
Lejay also gives another construction by piecing together a sequence of elastic Brownian motions, choosing their sign at random each time the process is killed and reborn.

In addition, we give a different construction of partially reflected Brownian motion inspired by the speed and scale construction of one dimensional diffusions.
Starting with standard Brownian motion, we glue together its excursions above level
x>0
and below level
-x
and we show that the result is the same process as the one described above.

Moreover, we provide a martingale problem characterisation of partially reflected Brownian motion, where equation (
1
) can be seen as the action of the semigroup of partially reflected Brownian motion on the initial allele frequency.
In particular, the domain of the infinitesimal generator associated to partially reflected Brownian motion is precisely the space of twice continuously differentiable functions on
\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
satisfying

\dfrac{dp}{dx}(0^{+})=\dfrac{dp}{dx}(0^{-})=\gamma(p(0^{+})-p(0^{-}))

for some
\gamma>0
.

We also provide an explicit formula for the transition density of partially reflected Brownian motion in Corollary
1.6
below.
It turns out that this transition density has already been in use in the field of diffusion in porous media
[
18
,
6
]
, but without mention of the underlying stochastic process.

More recently, this process has been used in
[
22
]
to detect barriers to gene flow in genetic samples and to measure their strength from the resulting distortion in isolation by distance patterns.

This paper is laid out as follows.
In Section
1
, we present our main results: partially reflected Brownian motion is defined as the solution to a martingale problem and two constructions of this process are given, we also state the convergence of a class of random walks to partially reflected Brownian motion.
In Section
2
, we prove that the martingale problem which charaterizes partially reflected Brownian motion is well posed and we show that the two constructions in Section
1
provide solutions to this martingale problem.
Finally in Section
3
, we prove the convergence in distribution of a sequence of random walks to partially reflected Brownian motion.

Main results

1.1
Definition and constructions of partially reflected Brownian motion

We first give a definition of partially reflected Brownian motion as a solution to a martingale problem on a space consisting of the disjoint union of the positive and negative half lines.
We will show that this martingale problem is well posed.
We then give two constructions of this process.

Definition

Let
\ddot{\mathbb{R}}
be the disjoint union of the positive and negative half lines,

\ddot{\mathbb{R}}=(-\infty,0^{-}]\cup[0^{+},+\infty).

It is endowed with the metric
d
defined by

\forall x,y\in\ddot{\mathbb{R}},\quad d(x,y)=\left|x-y\right|+\mathds{1}_{\left\{xy\leq 0\right\}}.

Let
\hat{C}(\ddot{\mathbb{R}})
be the set of continuous real-valued functions on
\ddot{\mathbb{R}}
which vanish at infinity.
For
\gamma\in[0,+\infty]
, let
\mathcal{D}^{\gamma}
be the subspace of functions
f\in\hat{C}(\ddot{\mathbb{R}})
which are twice continuously differentiable on each half line and satisfy

\dfrac{df}{dx}(0^{-})=\dfrac{df}{dx}(0^{+})=\gamma(f(0^{+})-f(0^{-})).

(2)

(For
\gamma=+\infty
, (
2
) becomes
f^{\prime}(0^{-})=f^{\prime}(0^{+})
and
f(0^{-})=f(0^{+})
.)
Fix
\sigma>0
and let us define a linear operator
L^{\gamma}
on
\mathcal{D}^{\gamma}
by

L^{\gamma}f=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\dfrac{d^{2}f}{dx^{2}},\quad\forall f\in\mathcal{D}^{\gamma}.

(3)

Let
D(\mathbb{R}_{+},\ddot{\mathbb{R}})
denote the space of càdlàg functions from
\mathbb{R}_{+}
to
\ddot{\mathbb{R}}
.

Definition 1.1

(partially reflected Brownian motion)
.

Let
\left(X_{t}\right)_{t\geq 0}
be a càdlàg,
\ddot{\mathbb{R}}
-valued Markov process on a probability space
\left(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\mathbb{P}\right)
, and call
\mathbb{P}^{X}
its law on
D(\mathbb{R}_{+},\ddot{\mathbb{R}})
.
The process
\left(X_{t}\right)_{t\geq 0}
(resp. its law
\mathbb{P}^{X}
) is said to be (the law of) partially reflected Brownian motion if it is a solution to the martingale problem associated with
L^{\gamma}
for some
\gamma\in[0,+\infty]
, i.e. if for any
f\in\mathcal{D}^{\gamma}
, the process

f(X_{t})-\int_{0}^{t}L^{\gamma}f(X_{s})ds

(4)

is a martingale with respect to the filtration generated by
\left(X_{t}\right)_{t\geq 0}
.
We call
\gamma
the permeability of the barrier.

Naturally, we say that
\left(X_{t}\right)_{t\geq 0}
is partially reflected Brownian motion with initial distribution
\mu
if it is a solution to the martingale problem associated with
(L^{\gamma},\mu)
,
i.e.
if (
4
) is a martingale for all
f\in\mathcal{D}^{\gamma}
and if
\mathbb{P}^{X}\left(X_{0}\in\cdot\right)=\mu(\cdot)
.

The operator
L^{\gamma}
is thus the generator of partially reflected Brownian motion.
This definition does not seem to provide much information about possible solutions to this martingale problem.
It does not even tell us if such solutions exist or if they are unique (in distribution).
This is the subject of the next proposition.
Below, we also give two ways to construct solutions to this martingale problem.

It should be noted that for
\gamma=0
(impermeable barrier), the operator
L^{\gamma}
is the generator of reflected Brownian motion (see for example Exercice VII.1.23 in
[
23
]
in the case
\alpha=1
), while for
\gamma=+\infty
(completely permeable barrier),
L^{\gamma}
is the generator of Brownian motion.

Proposition 1.2
.

For any
\gamma\in[0,+\infty]
, the martingale problem associated with
L^{\gamma}
has at most one
D(\mathbb{R}_{+},\ddot{\mathbb{R}})
valued solution.

Proof.

The operator
L^{\gamma}
satisfies the positive maximum principle on
\mathcal{D}^{\gamma}
,
i.e.
whenever
f\in\mathcal{D}^{\gamma}
and
\sup_{x\in\ddot{\mathbb{R}}}f(x)=f(x_{0})\geq 0
, we have
L^{\gamma}f(x_{0})\leq 0
.
By Lemma 4.2.1 of
[
4
]
,
L^{\gamma}
is thus dissipative on
\mathcal{D}^{\gamma}
(recall that we require the functions in
\mathcal{D}^{\gamma}
to vanish at infinity).

Let us now show that for any positive
\lambda
, the range of
\lambda-L^{\gamma}
contains the space
\hat{C}(\ddot{\mathbb{R}})
of continuous functions vanishing at infinity.
We do it in the case
\sigma^{2}=2
, but the general case is similar.
Let
f\in\hat{C}(\ddot{\mathbb{R}})
be such a function and define

g(x)=\left\{\begin{aligned} &e^{-\sqrt{\lambda}x}\int_{0}^{x}\dfrac{e^{\sqrt{\lambda}y}}{2\sqrt{\lambda}}f(y)dy+e^{\sqrt{\lambda}x}\int_{x}^{+\infty}\dfrac{e^{-\sqrt{\lambda}y}}{2\sqrt{\lambda}}f(y)dy+Ae^{-\sqrt{\lambda}x}&\text{ if }x\geq 0^{+},\\
&e^{-\sqrt{\lambda}x}\int_{-\infty}^{x}\dfrac{e^{\sqrt{\lambda}y}}{2\sqrt{\lambda}}f(y)dy+e^{\sqrt{\lambda}x}\int_{x}^{0}\dfrac{e^{-\sqrt{\lambda}y}}{2\sqrt{\lambda}}f(y)dy+Be^{\sqrt{\lambda}x}&\text{ if }x\leq 0^{-},\end{aligned}\right.

for some
A,B\in\mathbb{R}
.
Then
g
is twice continuously differentiable on
\ddot{\mathbb{R}}
, vanishes at infinity and satisfies

\dfrac{d^{2}g}{dx^{2}}(x)=\lambda g(x)-f(x)

for all
x\in\ddot{\mathbb{R}}
.
The constants
A
and
B
can then be chosen so that
g
also satisfies (
2
).
As a result we have found a function
g
in
\mathcal{D}^{\gamma}
such that
\lambda g-L^{\gamma}g=f
for any
f\in\hat{C}(\ddot{\mathbb{R}})
.

In particular, since
\mathcal{D}^{\gamma}
is a subset of
\hat{C}(\ddot{\mathbb{R}})
, it is in the range of
\lambda-L^{\gamma}
for any
\lambda>0
.
Furthermore
\hat{C}(\ddot{\mathbb{R}})
is separating in the sense of Section 3.4 in
[
4
]
.
Proposition
1.2
then follows from Corollary 4.4.4 in
[
4
]
.
∎

"Speed and scale" construction of partially reflected Brownian motion

We now present a way to construct partially reflected Brownian motion from Brownian motion, via an analogy with the speed and scale construction of one dimensional diffusions.
This will give us a better sense of what "typical" trajectories of this process look like.
Indeed, we show that the excursions of partially reflected Brownian motion outside the origin are given by the sequence of excursions of a Brownian motion outside a macroscopic region of length
\frac{1}{\gamma}

For any

Construction involving the local time at the origin

From the previous construction, one is led to think that
\left(\left|X_{t}\right|\right)_{t\geq 0}
has the law of reflected Brownian motion.
It is then natural to ask if partially reflected Brownian motion can be constructed by randomly "flipping" the excursions of reflected Brownian motion.
The next proposition provides such a construction.
It turns out that the crossing times of the origin are the times at which the local time at the origin of the process reaches the arrival times of an independent Poisson process with parameter
\gamma

Let

Let

Set

X_{t}=\operatorname{sign}(x)(-1)^{N(L^{0}_{t}(W))}W_{t},

X_{n}(t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\xi_{n}(nt)
.
We now state conditions under which the rescaled random walk
X_{n}=(X_{n}(t),t\geq 0)
converges to partially reflected Brownian motion.
We equip the space of càdlàg functions from
\mathbb{R}_{+}
to
\mathbb{R}
(denoted by
\mathrm{D}\left(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{R}\right)
) with the topology of Skorokhod convergence on compact time intervals.
If
d_{sko}^{T}(\cdot,\cdot)
is a metric for the Skorokhod convergence on
\mathrm{D}\left({[0,T]},\mathbb{R}\right)
for
T>0
, then

d_{sko}(f,g)=\int_{0}^{+\infty}e^{-t}(d_{sko}^{t}(f,g)\wedge 1)dt

(8)

is a metric for Skorokhod convergence on compact time intervals.

Theorem 1
.

Suppose
\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}x_{n}^{0}\underset{n\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}x^{0}
with
x^{0}\neq 0
and
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\sqrt{n}}{K}c_{n}=\gamma\in[0,+\infty]
.
Then as
n\to\infty
, the sequence of real-valued processes
\left(X_{n}(t),t\geq 0\right)
converges in distribution in
(\mathrm{D}\left(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{R}\right),d_{sko})
to a continuous real-valued process
(X(t),t\geq 0)
which is (a projection on
\mathbb{R}
of) a solution to the martingale problem associated with
\left(L^{\gamma},\delta_{x^{0}}\right)
, with
\sigma^{2}=m
.

In other words, if
\sqrt{n}c_{n}\to+\infty
,
X_{n}
converges to Brownian motion, if
\sqrt{n}c_{n}\to 0
,
X_{n}
converges to reflected Brownian motion, while if
\frac{\sqrt{n}}{K}c_{n}\to\gamma\in(0,\infty)
,
X_{n}
converges to (the projection of) partially reflected Brownian motion (recall that the latter takes values in
\ddot{\mathbb{R}}
, its projection is obtained by identifying
0^{+}
and
0^{-}
with
0
).

Remark
.

In the case
x^{0}=0
, the convergence still holds provided the probability of first exiting the set
[-K/2,K/2]
on the right converges as
n\to\infty
.
The initial distribution is then a convex combination of
\delta_{0^{+}}
and
\delta_{0^{-}}
, given by the limits of the exit probabilities.

Theorem
1
is proved in Section
3
in the case
K=2
.
The generalisation to other values of
K
is straighforward, and the case
K=1
introduces some simplifications, which makes the case
K=2
more representative of the general case.

Note that in Nagylaki’s model presented in Figure
1
, ancestral lineages are distributed as the random walk of Definition
1.7
with
K=1
.

2
Constructions of partially reflected Brownian motion

2.1
Speed and scale construction

Here we prove that the process
X_{t}=r(B_{\tau(t)})
defined in Subsection
1.1
is a solution to the martingale problem associated with
L^{\gamma}
.
This proof will require the following lemma, proved in Subsection
2.3
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Set
W_{t}=\left|X_{t}\right|
.
Then
\left(W_{t}\right)_{t\geq 0}
is distributed as reflected Brownian motion.

Proof of Proposition
1.3
.

Recall that
B
is standard Brownian motion started at
r^{-1}(x)
, hence
X_{0}=x
almost surely.
Let
\left(\mathcal{F}^{B}_{t}\right)_{t\geq 0}
denote the natural filtration of
\left(B_{t}\right)_{t\geq 0}
, and let
\mathcal{F}_{t}=\mathcal{F}^{B}_{\tau(t)}
.
Then
\left(\mathcal{F}_{t}\right)_{t\geq 0}
is a filtration,
\left(X_{t}\right)_{t\geq 0}
is
\left(\mathcal{F}_{t}\right)_{t\geq 0}
adapted and, for
s,t\geq 0
and
f:\ddot{\mathbb{R}}\to\mathbb{R}
bounded and continuous,

\mathbb{E}\left[\left.f(X_{t+s})\>\right|\>\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]=\mathbb{E}_{r^{-1}(X_{t})}\left[f(r(B_{\tau(s)}))\right].

Hence
(X_{t},t\geq 0)
is a Markov process with respect to
(\mathcal{F}_{t},t\geq 0)
.
Now let
\left(\mathcal{F}^{X}_{t}\right)_{t\geq 0}
be the filtration generated by
\left(X_{t}\right)_{t\geq 0}
.
Since
\mathcal{F}^{X}_{t}\subset\mathcal{F}_{t}
,
\left(X_{t}\right)_{t\geq 0}
is a Markov process with respect to
(\mathcal{F}^{X}_{t},t\geq 0)
.

Suppose now that for any
x\in\ddot{\mathbb{R}}
and
f\in\mathcal{D}^{\gamma}
,

\lim_{t\downarrow 0}\dfrac{1}{t}\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[f(X_{t})-f(X_{0})\right]=\dfrac{1}{2}\dfrac{d^{2}f}{dx^{2}}(x).

(9)

(Recall that we assumed
\sigma^{2}=1
for simplicity.)
Then, by Proposition 4.1.7 in
[
4
]
, (
4
) is an
\mathcal{F}^{X}
-martingale for all
f\in\mathcal{D}^{\gamma}
(
X
is progressive since it is right-continuous).
It follows that
\left(X_{t}\right)_{t\geq 0}
is a solution to the martingale problem associated with
L^{\gamma}
.

Let us now show (
9
).
Since
X
behaves as standard Brownian motion until the first time it hits the origin, (
9
) clearly holds for all
x\in\ddot{\mathbb{R}}\setminus\{0^{+},0^{-}\}
.
By symmetry, we can restrict the proof to
x=0^{+}
.
For any
t\geq 0
,

\mathbb{E}_{0^{+}}\left[f(X_{t})\right]=\mathbb{E}_{0^{+}}\left[\left(f(X_{t})-f(0^{+})\right)\mathds{1}_{\left\{X_{t}\geq 0^{+}\right\}}+\left(f(X_{t})-f(0^{-})\right)\mathds{1}_{\left\{X_{t}\leq 0^{-}\right\}}\right]\\
+\mathbb{E}_{0^{+}}\left[f(0^{+})\mathds{1}_{\left\{X_{t}\geq 0^{+}\right\}}+f(0^{-})\mathds{1}_{\left\{X_{t}\leq 0^{-}\right\}}\right].

Subtracting
f(0^{+})
on both sides we obtain

\mathbb{E}_{0^{+}}\left[f(X_{t})-f(0^{+})\right]=\mathbb{E}_{0^{+}}\left[\left(f(X_{t})-f(0^{+})\right)\mathds{1}_{\left\{X_{t}\geq 0^{+}\right\}}+\left(f(X_{t})-f(0^{-})\right)\mathds{1}_{\left\{X_{t}\leq 0^{-}\right\}}\right]\\
+\mathbb{E}_{0^{+}}\left[\left(f(0^{-})-f(0^{+})\right)\mathds{1}_{\left\{X_{t}\leq 0^{-}\right\}}\right].

(10)

Since
f
is twice continuously differentiable on
[0^{+},+\infty)
, for any
y
in
[0^{+},+\infty)
, there exists
h(y)\in[0^{+},y]
such that

f(y)-f(0^{+})=\dfrac{df}{dx}(0^{+})y+\frac{1}{2}\dfrac{d^{2}f}{dx^{2}}(h(y))y^{2}.

Replacing
y
by
X_{t}
, we write, for any
r>0
,

(f(X_{t})-f(0^{+}))\mathds{1}_{\left\{X_{t}\geq 0^{+}\right\}}=\left(\dfrac{df}{dx}(0^{+})X_{t}+\frac{1}{2}\dfrac{d^{2}f}{dx^{2}}(h(X_{t}))X_{t}^{2}\right)\mathds{1}_{\left\{0^{+}\leq X_{t}\leq r\right\}}\\
+(f(X_{t})-f(0^{+}))\mathds{1}_{\left\{X_{t}>r\right\}}.

By the Markov inequality and Lemma
2.1
,

\mathbb{P}\left(\left|X_{t}\right|>r\right)\leq 3\frac{t^{2}}{r^{4}}.

(11)

As a result, since
f
is bounded,

\mathbb{E}_{0^{+}}\left[(f(X_{t})-f(0^{+}))\mathds{1}_{\left\{X_{t}>r\right\}}\right]\leq 6\left\|f\right\|_{\infty}\frac{t^{2}}{r^{4}}.

(12)

In addition,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{0^{+}}\left[\dfrac{df}{dx}(0^{+})X_{t}\mathds{1}_{\left\{0^{+}\leq X_{t}\leq r\right\}}\right]=\dfrac{df}{dx}(0^{+})\mathbb{E}_{0^{+}}\left[X_{t}\mathds{1}_{\left\{X_{t}\geq 0^{+}\right\}}\right]-\dfrac{df}{dx}(0^{+})\mathbb{E}_{0^{+}}\left[X_{t}\mathds{1}_{\left\{X_{t}>r\right\}}\right],

(13)

and by the Cauchy-Schwartz inequality, Lemma
2.1
and (
11
),

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}_{0^{+}}\left[X_{t}\mathds{1}_{\left\{X_{t}>r\right\}}\right]\right|

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{0^{+}}\left[X_{t}^{2}\right]^{1/2}\mathbb{P}_{0^{+}}\left(X_{t}>r\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq t^{1/2}\sqrt{3}\frac{t}{r^{2}}.

(14)

Moreover, since
f^{\prime\prime}
is continuous on
[0^{+},+\infty)
, it is uniformly continuous on compact sets and there exists
C_{r}>0
such that

\forall x,y\in[0^{+},r],\qquad\left|\dfrac{d^{2}f}{dx^{2}}(y)-\dfrac{d^{2}f}{dx^{2}}(x)\right|\leq C_{r}\left|x-y\right|.

As a result,

\left|\mathbb{E}_{0^{+}}\left[\dfrac{d^{2}f}{dx^{2}}(h(X_{t}))X_{t}^{2}\mathds{1}_{\left\{0^{+}\leq X_{t}\leq r\right\}}\right]-\mathbb{E}_{0^{+}}\left[\dfrac{d^{2}f}{dx^{2}}(0^{+})X_{t}^{2}\mathds{1}_{\left\{0\leq X_{t}\leq r\right\}}\right]\right|\leq C_{r}\mathbb{E}_{0^{+}}\left[\left|X_{t}\right|^{3}\right],

(15)

and by Lemma
2.1
,
\mathbb{E}_{0^{+}}\left[\left|X_{t}\right|^{3}\right]=\mathcal{O}\left(t^{3/2}\right)
.
Proceeding as for (
14
), we also have

\mathbb{E}_{0^{+}}\left[\frac{1}{2}\dfrac{d^{2}f}{dx^{2}}(0^{+})X_{t}^{2}\mathds{1}_{\left\{0^{+}\leq X_{t}\leq r\right\}}\right]=\frac{1}{2}\dfrac{d^{2}f}{dx^{2}}(0^{+})\mathbb{E}_{0^{+}}\left[X_{t}^{2}\mathds{1}_{\left\{X_{t}\geq 0^{+}\right\}}\right]+\mathcal{O}\left(t^{3/2}\right).

(16)

Putting together (
13
), (
15
) and (
16
), we obtain

\mathbb{E}_{0^{+}}\left[(f(X_{t})-f(0^{+}))\mathds{1}_{\left\{X_{t}\geq 0^{+}\right\}}\right]=\dfrac{df}{dx}(0^{+})\mathbb{E}_{0^{+}}\left[X_{t}\mathds{1}_{\left\{X_{t}\geq 0^{+}\right\}}\right]\\
+\frac{1}{2}\dfrac{d^{2}f}{dx^{2}}(0^{+})\mathbb{E}_{0^{+}}\left[X_{t}^{2}\mathds{1}_{\left\{X_{t}\geq 0^{+}\right\}}\right]+o\left(t\right).

Likewise, we have

\mathbb{E}_{0^{+}}\left[(f(X_{t})-f(0^{-}))\mathds{1}_{\left\{X_{t}\leq 0^{-}\right\}}\right]=\dfrac{df}{dx}(0^{-})\mathbb{E}_{0^{+}}\left[X_{t}\mathds{1}_{\left\{X_{t}\leq 0^{-}\right\}}\right]\\
+\frac{1}{2}\dfrac{d^{2}f}{dx^{2}}(0^{-})\mathbb{E}_{0^{+}}\left[X_{t}^{2}\mathds{1}_{\left\{X_{t}\leq 0^{-}\right\}}\right]+o\left(t\right).

Plugging these two equations in (
10
) and using the fact that
f^{\prime}(0^{-})=f^{\prime}(0^{+})
, we obtain

\mathbb{E}_{0^{+}}\left[f(X_{t})-f(X_{0})\right]=\dfrac{df}{dx}(0^{\pm})\mathbb{E}_{0^{+}}\left[X_{t}\right]+\dfrac{1}{2}\dfrac{d^{2}f}{dx^{2}}(0^{+})\mathbb{E}_{0^{+}}\left[X_{t}^{2}\right]\\
+\dfrac{1}{2}\left(\dfrac{d^{2}f}{dx^{2}}(0^{-})-\dfrac{d^{2}f}{dx^{2}}(0^{+})\right)\mathbb{E}_{0^{+}}\left[X_{t}^{2}\mathds{1}_{\left\{X_{t}\leq 0^{-}\right\}}\right]\\
+\left(f(0^{-})-f(0^{+})\right)\mathbb{P}_{0^{+}}\left(X_{t}\leq 0^{-}\right)+o\left(t\right).

(17)

Moreover, by the construction of
X_{t}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{0^{+}}\left[X_{t}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\frac{1}{2\gamma}}\left[r(B_{\tau(t)})\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\frac{1}{2\gamma}}\left[\left(B_{\tau(t)}-\dfrac{1}{2\gamma}\right)\mathds{1}_{\left\{B_{\tau(t)}\geq\frac{1}{2\gamma}\right\}}+\left(B_{\tau(t)}+\dfrac{1}{2\gamma}\right)\mathds{1}_{\left\{B_{\tau(t)}\leq-\frac{1}{2\gamma}\right\}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\frac{1}{2\gamma}}\left[B_{\tau(t)}\right]+\dfrac{1}{2\gamma}\mathbb{E}_{\frac{1}{2\gamma}}\left[\mathds{1}_{\left\{B_{\tau(t)}\leq-\frac{1}{2\gamma}\right\}}-\mathds{1}_{\left\{B_{\tau(t)}\geq\frac{1}{2\gamma}\right\}}\right].

(18)

Note that
\tau(t)
is an
\mathcal{F}^{B}_{t}
-stopping time.
Furthermore, for any given
t\geq 0
, the martingale
(B_{s\wedge\tau(t)},s\geq 0)
is uniformly integrable.
To see this, write

\sup_{s\geq 0}\left|B_{s\wedge\tau(t)}\right|\leq\frac{1}{2\gamma}+\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}W_{s},

and note that the right-hand-side is integrable by Lemma
2.1
and Doob’s maximal inequality.
Hence, by the Optional Stopping Theorem,
\mathbb{E}_{\frac{1}{2\gamma}}\left[B_{\tau(t)}\right]=\frac{1}{2\gamma}
.
As a result, returning to (
18
),

\mathbb{E}_{0^{+}}\left[X_{t}\right]=\frac{1}{\gamma}\mathbb{P}_{0^{+}}\left(X_{t}\leq 0^{-}\right).

Since
f\in\mathcal{D}^{\gamma}
, the first term in (
17
) cancels with the last one.
By Lemma
2.1
,
\mathbb{E}_{0^{+}}\left[X_{t}^{2}\right]=t
.
Also note that by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality,

\mathbb{E}_{0^{+}}\left[X_{t}^{2}\mathds{1}_{\left\{X_{t}\leq 0^{-}\right\}}\right]\leq\sqrt{3}\,t\,\mathbb{P}_{0^{+}}\left(X_{t}\leq 0^{-}\right)^{1/2}.

(We have used Lemma
2.1
to compute the fourth moment of
X_{t}
.)
Furthermore,

\mathbb{P}_{0^{+}}\left(X_{t}\leq 0^{-}\right)=\mathbb{P}_{\frac{1}{2\gamma}}\left(B_{\tau(t)}\leq-\frac{1}{2\gamma}\right)\underset{t\to 0}{\longrightarrow}0.

Coming back to (
17
), dividing both sides by
t
and letting
t\downarrow 0
, we obtain

\lim_{t\downarrow 0}\frac{1}{t}\mathbb{E}_{0^{+}}\left[f(X_{t})-f(X_{0})\right]=\frac{1}{2}\dfrac{d^{2}f}{dx^{2}}(0^{+}).

The proof of Proposition
1.3
is now complete.
∎

2.2
Construction involving the local time at the origin

Let
\left(B_{t}\right)_{t\geq 0}
be standard Brownian motion and let
X_{t}=r(B_{\tau(t)})
be partially reflected Brownian motion constructed as before.
Set
W_{t}=\left|X_{t}\right|
and

\displaystyle T_{0}=0,

\displaystyle T_{i+1}=\inf\{t>T_{i}:X_{T_{i}}X_{t}<0\},\quad i\geq 0.

For
i\geq 1
set

E_{i}=L^{0}_{T_{i}}(X)-L^{0}_{T_{i-1}}(X)

and for
t\geq 0
,

N(t)=\max\left\{n\in\mathbb{N}:\sum_{i=0}^{n}E_{i}\leq t\right\}.

Then, for all
t\geq 0
,

X_{t}=\operatorname{sign}(X_{0})(-1)^{N(L^{0}_{t}(W))}W_{t}.

We know from Lemma
2.1
that
\left(W_{t}\right)_{t\geq 0}
is distributed as reflected Brownian motion.
Proposition
1.5
will be proven if we show that
(N(t),t\geq 0)
is a Poisson process with rate
\gamma
and that it is independent of
\left(W_{t}\right)_{t\geq 0}
.

The fact that
N
and
W
are independent might seem implausible at first sight as they are both constructed from
\left(B_{t}\right)_{t\geq 0}
.
However, the
E_{i}
(and hence
N
) only depend on the amount of local time that
B
accumulates at
\pm\frac{1}{2\gamma}
between successive crossings of
[-\frac{1}{2\gamma},\frac{1}{2\gamma}]
, and those crossing times cannot be determined by observing
\left(W_{t}\right)_{t\geq 0}
.
To prove this, we construct two independent processes in such a way that
\left(W_{t}\right)_{t\geq 0}
is a function of the former and
(N(t),t\geq 0)
is a function of the latter.
Set

\theta(t)=\inf\left\{\theta>0:\int_{0}^{\theta}\mathds{1}_{\left\{\left|B_{s}\right|\leq\frac{1}{2\gamma}\right\}}ds>t\right\}.

We prove the following in Subsection
2.3
.

Lemma 2.2
.

The processes
(\left|B_{\tau(t)}\right|,t\geq 0)
and
(B_{\theta(t)},t\geq 0)
are independent.

Proof of Proposition
1.5
.

Note that the left (resp. right) local time accumulated by
X
at the origin up to time
t
is the local time accumulated by
B
at
-\frac{1}{2\gamma}
(resp.
\frac{1}{2\gamma}
) up to time
\tau(t)
.
Indeed, by the Tanaka formula
[
23
, Theorem VI.1.2]
, letting
x^{+}=\max(x,0)
,

\frac{1}{2}L^{0^{+}}_{t}(X)=X_{t}^{+}-X_{0}^{+}-\int_{0}^{t}\mathds{1}_{\left\{X_{s}>0\right\}}dX_{s}

and

\frac{1}{2}L^{1/2\gamma}_{\tau(t)}(B)=(B_{\tau(t)}-\frac{1}{2\gamma})^{+}-(B_{0}-\frac{1}{2\gamma})^{+}-\int_{0}^{\tau(t)}\mathds{1}_{\left\{B_{s}>\frac{1}{2\gamma}\right\}}dB_{s}.

(For Brownian motion, considering the right, the left or the symmetric local time makes no difference.)
By the construction of
X
,
X_{t}^{+}=(B_{\tau(t)}-\frac{1}{2\gamma})^{+}
and, since
\mathds{1}_{\left\{B_{s}>\frac{1}{2\gamma}\right\}}=0
when
s\in(\tau(t^{-}),\tau(t))
,

\int_{0}^{\tau(t)}\mathds{1}_{\left\{B_{s}>\frac{1}{2\gamma}\right\}}dB_{s}=\int_{0}^{t}\mathds{1}_{\left\{X_{s}>0\right\}}dX_{s}.

As a result,

L^{0^{+}}_{t}(X)=L^{1/2\gamma}_{\tau(t)}(B)

(19)

and likewise,
L^{0^{-}}_{t}(X)=L^{-1/2\gamma}_{\tau(t)}(B)
.

For
a\in\mathbb{R}
, set
\mathds{T}_{a}=\inf\{t>0:B_{t}=a\}
.
Assuming without loss of generality that
X_{0}>0
,
\tau(T_{1})=\mathds{T}_{-1/2\gamma}
.
Then,

\displaystyle E_{1}=L^{0}_{T_{1}}(X)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(L^{0^{+}}_{T_{1}}(X)+L^{0^{-}}_{T_{1}}(X)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}L^{1/2\gamma}_{\mathds{T}_{-1/2\gamma}}(B).

By the Ray-Knight theorem
[
25
, Theorem 6.4.7]
,

L^{1/2\gamma}_{\mathds{T}_{-1/2\gamma}}(B)

is an exponential random variable with parameter
\frac{\gamma}{2}
.
Hence
E_{1}
is exponential with parameter
\gamma
.

Further, the strong Markov property of
\left(B_{t}\right)_{t\geq 0}
and its symmetry imply that the
E_{i}
are independent and identically distributed.
As a result
(N(t),t\geq 0)
is a Poisson process with rate
\gamma
.
It remains to show that it is independent of
\left(W_{t}\right)_{t\geq 0}
.

Define

\displaystyle S_{0}=0,

\displaystyle S_{i}=\inf\left\{t>S_{i-1}:B_{\theta(t)}=\frac{(-1)^{i}}{2\gamma}\right\}.

By the same argument as above,

L^{0}_{T_{i}}(W)=L^{1/2\gamma}_{S_{i}}(B_{\theta})+L^{-1/2\gamma}_{S_{i}}(B_{\theta}).

As a result, the
E_{i}
, and
(N(t),t\geq 0)
, are measurable with respect to the sigma field generated by
(B_{\theta}(t),t\geq 0)
.
Since
W_{t}=\left|B_{\tau(t)}\right|-\frac{1}{2\gamma}
, Lemma
2.2
implies the independence of
N
and
W
.
This concludes the proof of Proposition
1.5
.
∎

2.3
The absolute value of partially reflected Brownian motion

Let us start by recalling the following lemma, due to Skorokhod
[
26
]
(also Lemma 3.6.14 in
[
25
]
).

Lemma 2.3

(
[
26
]
)
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}
be a continuous function with
f(0)\geq 0
.
There exists a unique continuous function
l:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}
such that

i)

X(t):=l(t)+f(t)
is non negative for all
t\geq 0
,

ii)

l(0)=0
and
t\mapsto l(t)
is non decreasing,

iii)

\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathds{1}_{\left\{X(t)>0\right\}}dl(t)=0
.

The function
l
is then called the solution of the Skorokhod problem for
f
and it is given by

l(t)=\inf_{0\leq s\leq t}(f(s))^{-}.

The following generalisation can be found in
[
7
, Proposition 2.4.6]
.

Lemma 2.4

(
[
7
]
)
.

Fix
a<b\in\mathbb{R}
and let
f:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}
be a continuous function such that
f(0)\in[a,b]
.
There exists a unique pair of continuous functions
(l,u)
from
\mathbb{R}_{+}
to
\mathbb{R}
such that

i)

X(t):=f(t)+l(t)-u(t)\in[a,b]
for all
t\geq 0
,

ii)

l(0)=u(0)=0
and
l
and
u
are non decreasing,

iii)

\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathds{1}_{\left\{X(t)>a\right\}}dl(t)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathds{1}_{\left\{X(t)<b\right\}}du(t)=0
.

The pair
(l,u)
is called the two-sided regulator of
f
.

For
t\geq 0
, set

\displaystyle I^{1}(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\mathds{1}_{\left\{B_{s}>\frac{1}{2\gamma}\right\}}dB_{s}-\int_{0}^{t}\mathds{1}_{\left\{B_{s}<-\frac{1}{2\gamma}\right\}}dB_{s},

\displaystyle I^{2}(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\mathds{1}_{\left\{\left|B_{s}\right|\leq\frac{1}{2\gamma}\right\}}dB_{s}.

Both
I^{1}
and
I^{2}
are continuous
\mathcal{F}^{B}_{t}
martingales with

\displaystyle\left\langle I^{1}\right\rangle_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}\mathds{1}_{\left\{\left|B_{s}\right|>\frac{1}{2\gamma}\right\}}ds

\displaystyle\left\langle I^{2}\right\rangle_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}\mathds{1}_{\left\{\left|B_{s}\right|\leq\frac{1}{2\gamma}\right\}}ds

\displaystyle\left\langle I^{1},I^{2}\right\rangle_{t}=0.

By F. B. Knight’s theorem
[
9
]
(also Theorem 3.4.13 in
[
25
]
), the processes

\displaystyle\tilde{B}^{1}_{t}=W_{0}+I^{1}(\tau(t)),

\displaystyle\tilde{B}^{2}_{t}=B_{\theta(0)}+I^{2}(\theta(t)),

are independent standard Brownian motions.

Proof of Lemma
2.1
.

By the Tanaka formula
[
23
, Theorem VI.1.2]
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}L^{1/2\gamma}_{t}(B)=\left(B_{t}-\tfrac{1}{2\gamma}\right)^{+}-\left(B_{0}-\tfrac{1}{2\gamma}\right)^{+}-\int_{0}^{t}\mathds{1}_{\left\{B_{s}>\frac{1}{2\gamma}\right\}}dB_{s},

(20)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}L^{-1/2\gamma}_{t}(B)=\left(B_{t}+\tfrac{1}{2\gamma}\right)^{-}-\left(B_{0}+\tfrac{1}{2\gamma}\right)^{-}+\int_{0}^{t}\mathds{1}_{\left\{B_{s}<-\frac{1}{2\gamma}\right\}}dB_{s}.

(21)

On the other hand, from the construction of
X_{t}
,

W_{t}=\left|X_{t}\right|=(B_{\tau(t)}-\frac{1}{2\gamma})^{+}+(B_{\tau(t)}+\frac{1}{2\gamma})^{-}

and from (
19
),

L^{0}_{t}(W)=L^{0}_{t}(X)=\frac{1}{2}\left(L^{1/2\gamma}_{\tau(t)}(B)+L^{-1/2\gamma}_{\tau(t)}(B)\right).

Adding (
20
) and (
21
) and replacing
t
by
\tau(t)
, we obtain

\tilde{B}^{1}_{t}=W_{t}-L^{0}_{t}(W).

Since
\tilde{B}^{1}
is standard Brownian motion,
W
is reflected Brownian motion
[
23
, VI.2]
.
∎

Proof of Lemma
2.2
.

Since
\tilde{B}^{1}_{t}=W_{t}-L^{0}_{t}(W)
, the function
t\mapsto L^{0}_{t}(W)
is a solution of the Skorokhod problem for
t\mapsto\tilde{B}^{1}_{t}
, and by Lemma
2.3
,

W_{t}=\tilde{B}^{1}_{t}+\inf_{s\leq t}(\tilde{B}^{1}_{s})^{-}.

On the other hand,
B_{\theta(t)}
is a function of
(\tilde{B}^{2}_{t},t\geq 0)
.
To see this, note that since
B_{\theta(t)}\in[-1/2\gamma,1/2\gamma]
,

B_{\theta(t)}=\left(B_{\theta(t)}+\frac{1}{2\gamma}\right)^{+}-\left(B_{\theta(t)}-\frac{1}{2\gamma}\right)^{+}-\frac{1}{2\gamma}.

By the Tanaka formula,

\displaystyle\left(B_{t}+\frac{1}{2\gamma}\right)^{+}

\displaystyle=\left(B_{0}+\frac{1}{2\gamma}\right)^{+}+\int_{0}^{t}\mathds{1}_{\left\{B_{s}>-\frac{1}{2\gamma}\right\}}dB_{s}+L^{-\frac{1}{2\gamma}}_{t}(B),

\displaystyle\left(B_{t}-\frac{1}{2\gamma}\right)^{+}

\displaystyle=\left(B_{0}-\frac{1}{2\gamma}\right)^{+}+\int_{0}^{t}\mathds{1}_{\left\{B_{s}>\frac{1}{2\gamma}\right\}}dB_{s}+L^{\frac{1}{2\gamma}}_{t}(B).

Subtracting these equations with
t
replaced by
\theta(t)
, and noting that
\mathds{1}_{\left\{B_{s}\geq-1/2\gamma\right\}}-\mathds{1}_{\left\{B_{s}>1/2\gamma\right\}}=\mathds{1}_{\left\{\left|B_{s}\right|\leq 1/2\gamma\right\}}
, we obtain

B_{\theta(t)}=\tilde{B}^{2}_{t}+L^{-\frac{1}{2\gamma}}_{\theta(t)}(B)-L^{\frac{1}{2\gamma}}_{\theta(t)}(B).

From this equation, we see that
(L^{-\frac{1}{2\gamma}}_{\theta(\cdot)}(B),L^{\frac{1}{2\gamma}}_{\theta(\cdot)}(B))
is the two-sided regulator of
\tilde{B}^{2}
with reflection at
\pm 1/2\gamma
.
By Lemma
2.4
,
(B_{\theta(t)},t\geq 0)
is then uniquely determined by
(\tilde{B}^{2}_{t},t\geq 0)
.

Since
\left|B_{\tau(t)}\right|=W_{t}+\frac{1}{2\gamma}
is a function of
\tilde{B}^{1}
,
B_{\theta(t)}
is a function of
\tilde{B}^{2}
, and
\tilde{B}^{1}
is independent of
\tilde{B}^{2}
,
\left(\left|B_{\tau(t)}\right|,t\geq 0\right)
and
\left(B_{\theta(t)},t\geq 0\right)
are independent.
∎

2.4
Transition density of partially reflected Brownian motion

Proof of Corollary
1.6
.

Recall that
X_{t}
was defined as

X_{t}=\operatorname{sign}(x)(-1)^{N(L^{0}_{t}(W))}W_{t},

where
W
is reflected Brownian motion started from
\left|x\right|
and
\left(N_{t}\right)_{t\geq 0}
is an independent Poisson process with rate
\gamma
.
Hence, summing over all possible values of
L^{0}_{t}(W)
,

\mathbb{P}_{x}\left(X_{t}\in dy\right)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(N(l)\equiv\operatorname{sign}(x)-\operatorname{sign}(y)\>(\mathrm{mod}\,2)\right)\mathbb{P}_{\left|x\right|}\left(W_{t}\in d\left|y\right|,L^{0}_{t}(W)\in dl\right).

Since
N(l)
is a Poisson random variable with parameter
\gamma l
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(N(l)\equiv 0\>(\mathrm{mod}\,2)\right)=\frac{1+e^{-2\gamma l}}{2},

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(N(l)\equiv 1\>(\mathrm{mod}\,2)\right)=\frac{1-e^{-2\gamma l}}{2}.

In addition, taking
\alpha=1/2
in Corollary 3.3 of
[
1
]
, we obtain, for
x,y\geq 0
,

\mathbb{P}_{x}\left(W_{t}\in dy,L^{0}_{t}(W)\in dl\right)=\left(G_{t}(x-y)-G_{t}(x+y)\right)dy\delta_{0}(dl)-2\partial_{x}G_{t}(x+y+l)dydl.

As a result, if
xy\geq 0^{+}
,

\mathbb{P}_{x}\left(X_{t}\in dy\right)=\left(G_{t}(x-y)-G_{t}(x+y)\right)dy-2\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1+e^{-2\gamma l}}{2}\partial_{x}G_{t}(\left|x\right|+\left|y\right|+l)dldy.

Integrating by parts yields

\frac{\mathbb{P}_{x}\left(X_{t}\in dy\right)}{dy}=G_{t}(x-y)+G_{t}(x+y)-2\gamma\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-2\gamma l}G_{t}(\left|x\right|+\left|y\right|+l)dl.

Likewise if
xy\leq 0^{-}
,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{P}_{x}\left(X_{t}\in dy\right)}{dy}

\displaystyle=-2\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1-e^{-2\gamma l}}{2}\partial_{x}G_{t}(\left|x\right|+\left|y\right|+l)dl

\displaystyle=2\gamma\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-2\gamma l}G_{t}(\left|x\right|+\left|y\right|+l)dl.

The proof of Corollary
1.6
is now complete.
∎

3.1
The absolute value of

To prove that the absolute value of any possible limit point of

is reflected Brownian motion, we write
\left|X_{n}\right|
as the sum of a martingale term and a non-decreasing term.
We then show that the martingale term converges to Brownian motion while the non-decreasing term converges to the opposite of the running minimum of this Brownian motion.
The conclusion follows from a classical result on reflected Brownian motion
[
23
, VI.2]
.

Set

\tilde{X}_{n}(t)=\left|X_{n}(t)\right|\mathds{1}_{\left\{\left|X_{n}(t)\right|\geq\frac{2}{\sqrt{n}}\right\}}

and, for
i\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\sigma_{0}^{n}=0,

\displaystyle\tau_{i}^{n}=\inf\{t>\sigma_{i}^{n}:\left|X_{n}(t)\right|\leq\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\},

\displaystyle\sigma_{i+1}^{n}=\inf\{t>\tau_{i}^{n}:\left|X_{n}(t)\right|>\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\}.

The process
\tilde{X}_{n}
can then be decomposed as follows
[
8
]

\tilde{X}_{n}(t)=M_{n}(t)+L_{n}(t)-\sum_{i\geq 0}\left|X_{n}(\tau_{i}^{n})\right|\mathds{1}_{\left\{\tau_{i}^{n}\leq t<\sigma_{i+1}^{n}\right\}},

(23)

with

M_{n}(t)=\left|X_{n}(0)\right|+\int_{0}^{t}\mathds{1}_{\left\{\left|X_{n}(s)\right|>\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right\}}d\left|X_{n}\right|(s)

and

\displaystyle L_{n}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\geq 0}\left(\left|X_{n}(\sigma_{i+1}^{n})\right|-\left|X_{n}(\tau_{i}^{n})\right|\right)\mathds{1}_{\left\{\sigma_{i+1}^{n}\leq t\right\}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i\geq 0}\mathds{1}_{\left\{\sigma_{i+1}^{n}\leq t\right\}}.

(24)

The term
M_{n}
is a martingale, while
L_{n}
counts the number of visits (in fact of exits) of
[-\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}},\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}]
.

Define the running minimum

of the martingale part as

V_{n}(t)=\sup_{s\leq t}\left(\tfrac{2}{\sqrt{n}}-M_{n}(s)\right)^{+}

(25)

and note that

first becomes positive when

first reaches
\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}
,
i.e.

\inf\{t\geq 0:V_{n}(t)\geq\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\}=\inf\{t\geq 0:M_{n}(t)\leq\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\}.

Since up to that time the other terms on the right-hand-side of (
23
) are zero, we get

\inf\{t\geq 0:V_{n}(t)\geq\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\}=\tau_{0}^{n}.

The next time
V_{n}
increases is

\inf\{t\geq 0:V_{n}(t)\geq\tfrac{2}{\sqrt{n}}\}=\inf\{t\geq 0:M_{n}(t)\leq 0\}.

By (
23
), this is also
\tau_{1}^{n}
.
By induction,

V_{n}(t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i\geq 0}\mathds{1}_{\left\{\tau_{i}^{n}\leq t\right\}}.

(26)

This translates the fact that the excursions of
M_{n}
above its running minimum are given by the excursions of
\left|X_{n}\right|
above
\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}
, see also Figure
8
.
Returning to (
23
), we have shown

\tilde{X}_{n}(t)=M_{n}(t)+V_{n}(t).

(27)

3.2
Local time accumulated between crossings

To prove that the local time accumulated by
X_{\infty}
at the origin between crossings is a sequence of exponential variables, we show that the number of visits of the random walk
X_{n}
to
[-\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}},\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}]
before the first time it reaches
-\frac{2}{\sqrt{n}}
is a geometric random variable.

Let
(T_{i}^{n},i\geq 0)
be the sequence of crossing times of
[-1/\sqrt{n},1/\sqrt{n}]
by
X_{n}
,
i.e.
for
n\geq 0
, set
T_{0}^{n}=0
and

T_{i+1}^{n}=\inf\{t>T_{i}^{n}:\operatorname{sign}(X_{n}(T_{i}^{n}))X_{n}(t)<-\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\}.

Recall also the definition of
(T_{i},i\geq 0)
in (
22
).

Lemma 3.7
.

As
n
tends to infinity,

\left(X_{n},L_{n},\left(T_{i}^{n}\right)_{i\geq 0}\right)\overset{d}{\underset{n\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}}\left(X_{\infty},L^{0}(X_{\infty}),\left(T_{i}\right)_{i\geq 0}\right)

in
\mathrm{D}\left([0,T]^{2},\mathbb{R}^{2}\right)\times\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}
.

The proof of Lemma
3.7
is given in Subsection
3.5
.

Proof of Lemma
3.3
.

Let
Y_{n}
be the number of visits to
[-\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}},\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}]
up to the first crossing time,

Y_{n}=\sum_{i\geq 1}\mathds{1}_{\left\{\sigma_{i}^{n}\leq T_{1}^{n}\right\}}.

By the Markov property,
Y_{n}
is a geometric random variable with parameter

p_{n}=\mathbb{P}_{\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{n}}}\left(X_{n}(\sigma_{1}^{n})=-\tfrac{2}{\sqrt{n}}\right).

For
K=2
,
p_{n}=\frac{c_{n}}{2(1+c_{n})}
(and in the general case,
p_{n}\sim\frac{c_{n}}{K}
as
n\to\infty
).
Since
\frac{\sqrt{n}}{2}c_{n}\to\gamma\in(0,\infty)
,

L_{n}(T_{1}^{n})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}Y_{n}

converges in distribution to an exponential random variable with parameter
\gamma
.
Set
E^{n}_{i}=L_{n}(T^{n}_{i+1})-L_{n}(T^{n}_{i})
.
The random variables
E_{0}^{n},E_{1}^{n},\ldots
are independent and identically distributed by the strong Markov property and by symmetry.
As a result,
\left(E^{n}_{i}\right)_{i\geq 0}
converges in distribution as
n
tends to infinity to a sequence
\left(E_{i}\right)_{i\geq 0}
of independent and identically distributed exponential random variables with parameter
\gamma
.
By Lemma
3.7
, this limit coincides with
(L^{0}_{T_{i+1}}(X_{\infty})-L^{0}_{T_{i}}(X_{\infty}))_{i\geq 0}
(also note that
t\mapsto L^{0}_{t}(X_{\infty})
is continuous almost surely).

We would like to show that the sequence
\left(E_{i}^{n}\right)_{i\geq 0}
is independent of
\tilde{X}_{n}
, but this fails when
K\geq 2
.
To circumvent this issue, we tweak
\tilde{X}_{n}
so that it “forgets” the amount of time
X_{n}
spends in
[-\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}},\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}]
.
We do this via a time change.
Set

\delta^{n}_{i}=\inf\{t>\tau_{i}^{n}:X_{n}(t)\neq X_{n}(t^{-})\}

and

\theta^{n}(t)=\inf\left\{\theta>0:\int_{0}^{\theta}\sum_{i\geq 0}\mathds{1}_{\left\{s\notin[\delta_{i}^{n},\sigma_{i+1}^{n}[\right\}}ds>t\right\}.

Then
(\tilde{X}_{n}(\theta^{n}(t)),t\geq 0)
and
(L_{n}(T_{i+1}^{n})-L_{n}(T_{i}^{n}))_{i\geq 0}
are independent.
Furthermore, for
t\geq 0
,

\left|\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{i\geq 0}\mathds{1}_{\left\{s\notin[\delta_{i}^{n},\sigma_{i+1}^{n}[\right\}}ds-t\right|\leq\nu^{n}(t).

Moreover
t\mapsto\nu^{n}(t)
is nondecreasing, hence, by Lemma
3.5
,
(\nu^{n}(t),t\geq 0)
converges to 0 uniformly on compact sets in

.
It follows that
\theta^{n}(t)\to t

As a result,
\tilde{X}_{n}\circ\theta^{n}
converges in the Skorokhod topology to
\left|X_{\infty}\right|
(also in probability).
We can thus conclude that
(L^{0}_{T_{i+1}}(X_{\infty})-L^{0}_{T_{i}}(X_{\infty}))_{i\geq 0}

\left|X_{\infty}\right|
.
∎

3.3
Tightness

Proof of Lemma
3.1
.

Tightness of the sequence
X_{n}
follows from the convergence in distribution of
M_{n}
(recall the decomposition (
23
)).
Reasoning as in
[

]
(Proof of Lemma 2.1), we show below that for any

\sup_{\left|s-t\right|<\delta}\left|X_{n}(t)-X_{n}(s)\right|\leq\tfrac{3}{\sqrt{n}}+2\sup_{\left|s-t\right|<\delta}\left|M_{n}(t)-M_{n}(s)\right|.

(30)

We can thus write, for
T>0
and
\varepsilon>0

\lim_{\delta\downarrow 0}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\Bigg(\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\left|t-s\right|<\delta\\
s,t\in[0,T]\end{subarray}}\left|X_{n}(s)-X_{n}(t)\right|>\varepsilon\Bigg)\\
\leq\lim_{\delta\downarrow 0}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\Bigg(\tfrac{3}{\sqrt{n}}+2\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\left|t-s\right|<\delta\\
s,t\in[0,T]\end{subarray}}\left|M_{n}(t)-M_{n}(s)\right|>\varepsilon\Bigg).

The right-hand-side is zero because the sequence
M_{n}
converges in distribution in the space
\mathrm{D}\left({[0,T]},\mathbb{R}\right)
, and tightness of
X_{n}
in
\mathrm{D}\left({[0,T]},\mathbb{R}\right)
follows
[
3
, Theorem 7.3]
.
Since
X_{n}
is tight in
\mathrm{D}\left({[0,T]},\mathbb{R}\right)
for all
T>0
, it is tight in
(\mathrm{D}\left(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{R}\right),d)
.

Let us now prove (
30
).
Fix
0\leq s\leq t
.
If
\left|X_{n}(u)\right|>\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}
for all
u\in[s,t]
, then

X_{n}(t)-X_{n}(s)=M_{n}(t)-M_{n}(s).

Otherwise, let

\displaystyle\alpha

\displaystyle=\inf\{u>s:\left|X_{n}(u)\right|\leq\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\},

\displaystyle\beta

\displaystyle=\sup\{u<t:\left|X_{n}(u)\right|\leq\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\},

and note that

\displaystyle\left|X_{n}(t)-X_{n}(s)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|X_{n}(t)-X_{n}(\beta)\right|+\left|X_{n}(\beta)-X_{n}(\alpha)\right|+\left|X_{n}(\alpha)-X_{n}(s)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\tfrac{3}{\sqrt{n}}+\left|M_{n}(t)-M_{n}(\beta)\right|+\left|M_{n}(s)-M_{n}(\alpha)\right|.

Inequality (
30
) thus holds and the proof of Lemma
3.1
is complete.

3.4
Occupation time of the barrier

Proof of Lemma
3.5
.

The bound on the expected time spent inside
[-\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}},\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{n}}]
follows after showing that the expected length of a visit in this set is of order
\frac{1}{n}
while the expected number of those visits is of order
\sqrt{n}
.
By the definition of
\nu^{n}(t)
,

\displaystyle\nu^{n}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\geq 0}\left(\sigma_{i+1}^{n}\wedge t-\tau_{i}^{n}\wedge t\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i\geq 0}\left(\sigma_{i+1}^{n}-\tau_{i}^{n}\right)\mathds{1}_{\left\{\tau_{i}^{n}\leq t\right\}}.

By the strong Markov property,

\mathbb{E}\left[\nu^{n}(t)\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i\geq 0}h^{n}(X_{n}(\tau_{i}^{n}))\mathds{1}_{\left\{\tau_{i}^{n}\leq t\right\}}\right]

where
h^{n}(x)=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\inf\{t>0:\left|X_{n}(t)\right|>\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\}\right]
.
By the Markov property, for
i\in\{-1,0,1\}
,

n\sum_{j\in E}q_{n}(i,j)\left(h^{n}(j/\sqrt{n})-h^{n}(i/\sqrt{n})\right)=-1.

Also
h^{n}(x)=0
when
\left|x\right|>\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}
.
Solving these equations for
K=2
yields

\displaystyle h^{n}\left(\pm\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right)=\frac{3}{mn},

\displaystyle h^{n}(0)=\frac{3}{mn}+\frac{1}{c_{n}mn}.

(In the general case,
h^{n}\left(\lfloor\frac{K+1}{2}\rfloor/\sqrt{n}\right)=\frac{K+1}{mn}
.)
For
i\geq 1
,
X_{n}(\tau_{i}^{n})=\pm\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}
, hence

\mathbb{E}\left[\nu^{n}(t)\right]\leq\frac{1}{c_{n}mn}+\frac{3}{mn}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i\geq 0}\mathds{1}_{\left\{\tau_{i}^{n}\leq t\right\}}\right].

But the number of visits of
X_{n}
to
[-\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}},\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{n}}]
before time
t
is less than the number of excursions outside
[-\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}},\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{n}}]
before the first excursion of length longer than
t
.
By the Markov property, the latter is a geometric random variable with parameter

\mathbb{P}_{\tfrac{2}{\sqrt{n}}}\left(\tau_{0}^{n}>t\right).

But, for
t>0
, there exists
c\in(0,\infty)
such that
[
11
, Proposition 4.2.4]

\lim_{n\to\infty}\sqrt{n}\mathbb{P}_{\tfrac{2}{\sqrt{n}}}\left(\tau_{0}^{n}>t\right)=c.

Hence, since
\sqrt{n}c_{n}\to K\gamma\in(0,\infty)
,

\mathbb{E}\left[\nu^{n}(t)\right]\leq\frac{1}{c_{n}nm}+\frac{3}{m\sqrt{n}}\left(\sqrt{n}\mathbb{P}_{\tfrac{2}{\sqrt{n}}}\left(\tau_{0}^{n}>t\right)\right)^{-1}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right).

This concludes the proof of Lemma
3.5
.
∎

3.5
Convergence of the crossing times

Proof of Lemma
3.7
.

From Lemma
3.6
, we already know that

(X_{n},L_{n})\overset{d}{\underset{n\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}}(X_{\infty},L^{0}(X_{\infty})).

Furthermore, for all
i\geq 0
,

T_{i}^{n}=L_{n}^{-1}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{i}E_{k}^{n}\right),

where
t\mapsto L_{n}^{-1}(t)
is the right continuous inverse of
L_{n}
.
Since
\left(E_{i}^{n}\right)_{i\geq 0}
converges in distribution to
\left(E_{i}\right)_{i\geq 0}
and
L_{n}
converges in distribution to
L^{0}(X_{\infty})
, the sequence
\left(T_{i}^{n}\right)_{n\geq 1}
is tight in
\mathbb{R}
for all
i\geq 0
.

As a result the sequence of random variables
(X_{n},L_{n},\left(T_{i}^{n}\right)_{i\geq 0})
is tight in
\mathrm{D}([0,T]^{2}
,
\mathbb{R}^{2})\times\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}
, where this space is endowed with the product topology.
Let
(X_{\infty}
,
L^{0}(X_{\infty})
,
(\tilde{T}_{i})_{i\geq 0})
be a possible limit point of this subsequence.
By the Skorokhod embedding theorem, we can assume that there exists (a version of) a subsequence which converges to (a version of) this limit point almost surely.
For ease of notation we denote this subsequence by
(X_{n},L_{n},\left(T_{i}^{n}\right)_{i\geq 0})
.

Let
\mathcal{N}\subset\Omega
be the negligible set on which this convergence fails, and suppose that there exists
\omega\in\Omega\setminus\mathcal{N}
such that
\tilde{T}_{1}(\omega)<T_{1}(\omega)
.
We show that for this to happen, one of two very improbable things must occur: either
\tilde{T}_{1}(\omega)=\tilde{T}_{2}(\omega)
(but remember that
L_{n}(T^{n}_{2})-L_{n}(T^{n}_{1})
is asymptotically exponentially distributed) or
X_{\infty}
must remain equal to zero for a positive amount of time after
\tilde{T}_{1}
.

Assume without loss of generality that
X_{\infty}(0)>0
and that
X_{n}(0)>0
for all
n\geq 1
.
Then take
\varepsilon>0
such that
\tilde{T}_{1}+\varepsilon<T_{1}
(
\omega
is kept fixed in the remainder of the proof).
Since
X_{n}\Rightarrow X_{\infty}
,

\inf\{X_{n}(s),T_{1}^{n}\leq s\leq T_{1}^{n}+\varepsilon\}\underset{n\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}\inf\{X_{\infty}(s),\tilde{T}_{1}\leq s\leq\tilde{T}_{1}+\varepsilon\}.

Since
X_{\infty}(s)\geq 0
for
s<T_{1}
, the right-hand-side is non-negative while the left-hand-side is non-positive because
X_{n}(T_{1}^{n})=-\frac{2}{\sqrt{n}}
.
As a result

\lim_{n\to\infty}\inf\{X_{n}(s),T_{1}^{n}\leq s\leq T_{1}^{n}+\varepsilon\}=0.

Also note that

\sup\{\left|X_{n}(s)\right|,T_{1}^{n}\leq s\leq T_{2}^{n}\wedge(T_{1}^{n}+\varepsilon)\}\leq\left|\inf\{X_{n}(s),T_{1}^{n}\leq s\leq T_{1}^{n}+\varepsilon\}\right|.

Moreover the left-hand-side converges to

\sup\{\left|X_{\infty}(s)\right|,\tilde{T}_{1}\leq s\leq\tilde{T}_{2}\wedge(\tilde{T}_{1}+\varepsilon)\}.

The latter must then be zero.
Hence either
\tilde{T}_{1}=\tilde{T}_{2}
or there exists
\eta>0
such that
\left|X_{\infty}(s)\right|=0
for all
\tilde{T}_{1}\leq s\leq\tilde{T}_{1}+\eta
.
Since
L_{n}(T_{1}^{n})-L_{n}(T_{2}^{n})
converges to an exponential random variable with parameter
\gamma\in(0,\infty)
and
\left|X_{\infty}\right|
is distributed as reflected Brownian motion, both these events have probability zero.

Suppose now that
\tilde{T}_{1}(\omega)>T_{1}(\omega)
for some
\omega\in\Omega\setminus\mathcal{N}
.
By the definition of
T_{1}
, there exists
t\in(T_{1},\tilde{T}_{1})
such that
X_{\infty}(t)<0
.
Since
T_{1}\to\tilde{T}_{1}>t
, there exists
n_{0}
large enough that
T_{1}^{n}>t
for all
n\geq n_{0}
.
Then for all
n\geq n_{0}
,
X_{n}(t)\geq-\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}
, but at the same time
X_{n}(t)\to X_{\infty}(t)<0
, leading to a contradiction.

We have thus shown that
\tilde{T}_{1}=T_{1}
almost surely.
By induction one shows that
\tilde{T}_{i}=T_{i}
almost surely for all
i\geq 0
.
It follows that
(X_{\infty},L_{\infty},\left(T_{i}\right)_{i\geq 0})
is the only possible limit point of the sequence
(X_{n},L_{n},\left(T_{i}^{n}\right)_{i\geq 0})
.
Together with the tightness of this sequence, this concludes the proof of Lemma
3.7
.
∎
