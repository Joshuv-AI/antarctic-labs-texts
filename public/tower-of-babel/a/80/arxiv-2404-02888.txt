Branching Brownian motion versus Random Energy Model in the supercritical phase: overlap distribution and temperature susceptibility
Benjamin Bonnefont, Michel Pain and Olivier Zindy

Abstract
In comparison with Derrida’s REM, we investigate the influence of the so-called decoration processes arising in the limiting extremal processes of numerous log-correlated Gaussian fields. In particular, we focus on the branching Brownian motion and two specific quantities from statistical physics in the vicinity of the critical temperature. The first one is the two-temperature overlap, whose behavior at criticality is smoothened by the decoration process—unlike the one-temperature overlap which is identical—and the second one is the temperature susceptibility, as introduced by Sales and Bouchaud, which is strictly larger in the presence of decorations and diverges, close to the critical temperature, at the same speed as for the REM but with a different multiplicative constant. We also study some general decorated cases in order to highlight the fact that the BBM has a critical behavior in some sense to be made precise.

Introduction and results

1.1
Motivations

In order to shed some light on the mysteries of the Parisi theory for mean field spin glasses, Derrida introduced in the
80’s the
random energy models
(REMs)
[
29
]
, where the Gaussian energy levels are assumed to be independent, and its generalizations, the
generalized random energy models
(GREMs)
[
30
]
, whose correlations are given by a tree structure of finite depth. One question of central interest in spin glass theory is to understand the structure of pure states, which translates into the analysis of the extremal process in the language of extreme value theory of stochastic processes.

These two tractable models have been extensively studied and allowed, in particular, to investigate the phenomenon of
replica symmetry breaking
. Indeed the REM seems to be the foremost representative of a universality class: in spin glass terminology one may call this the
1-step replica symmetry breaking
(1-RSB) class, or REM-class. More precisely, a spin glass model displays a 1-RSB if there exists some critical
\beta_{c}>0
such that, asymptotically, the overlap between two points chosen independently according to the Gibbs measure at inverse temperature
\beta>0
is concentrated at 0 for
\beta\leq\beta_{c}
, but is supported by 0 and 1, when
\beta>\beta_{c}
. This phenomenon is a consequence of the fact that the REM-class undergo what physicists refer to as the
REM-freezing transition
: there is a phase transition for the free energy at some
\beta_{c}>0
meaning that, for
\beta\leq\beta_{c}
, there is an exponentially large number of configurations, with energy level strictly less than the extremes, contributing to free energy and the Gibbs measure is roughly uniformly distributed among such configurations while, for
\beta>\beta_{c}
, the relevant configurations are the ones with the largest energies and the free energy becomes dominated by a relatively small set of configurations.
Another striking fact characterizing the REM-class is that, for
\beta>\beta_{c}
, the ordered weights of the pure states under the Gibbs measure at inverse temperature
\beta>0
follow asymptotically a
Poisson–Dirichlet distribution
of parameter
\beta_{c}/\beta
.
We refer to Bolthausen

Finally, let us mention that, despite the simplicity of the freezing transition, rather sophisticated models are known, or conjectured, to belong to the REM-class, such is the case for the extremes of the Riemann zeta-function along the critical line, see Arguin
[
2

Natural hierarchical models with an infinite number of levels are the branching Brownian motion (BBM) and the
branching random walk (BRW), see e.g. the seminal paper by Derrida and Spohn

\log
-REMs

A statistical physics approach.

In statistical physics, it is common to consider first the
partition function

Z_{t,{\mathrm{d}}}(\beta)
of the model, here the BBM (
\beta
stands for the inverse-temperature):

Z_{t,{\mathrm{d}}}(\beta)\coloneqq\sum_{x\in L_{t}}\mathrm{e}^{\beta h_{t}(x)},\qquad\forall\,\beta>0,

and the
free energy

f_{t,{\mathrm{d}}}(\beta)\coloneqq\frac{1}{t}\,\log Z_{t,{\mathrm{d}}}(\beta),\qquad\forall\,\beta>0.

The subscript “d” stands for “decorated” and is here to highlight the difference with the REM (for which similar quantities are written without the subscript).
It is well known, see
[
32
]
, that the BBM exhibits a phase transition at the level of the free energy and that this latter is the same as for the REM, namely

f_{\mathrm{d}}(\beta)\coloneqq\lim_{t\to\infty}f_{t,{\mathrm{d}}}(\beta)=\begin{cases}\frac{1+\beta^{2}}{2},&{\rm if}\ \beta<\beta_{c}\coloneqq 1,\\
\beta,&{\rm if}\ \beta\geq\beta_{c},\end{cases}\qquad\text{ a.s. and in $L^{1}$.}

In particular, the model undergoes
freezing
above
\beta_{c}
in the sense that the quantity
f_{\mathrm{d}}(\beta)/\beta
is constant.
More importantly, one considers the
Gibbs measure
; this is the random probability measure
{\cal G}_{\beta,t,{\mathrm{d}}}
on
L_{t}
defined, at inverse temperature
\beta>0
, by

{\cal G}_{\beta,t,{\mathrm{d}}}(x)\coloneqq\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\beta h_{t}(x)}}{Z_{t,{\mathrm{d}}}(\beta)},\qquad\forall\,x\in L_{t}.

By design, the Gibbs measure concentrates at low temperature, i.e. when
\beta>\beta_{c}
, on the high points of the Gaussian field. With spin glasses in mind, one also considers the normalized covariance or
overlap
between two particles
x,y\in L_{t}
by

q_{t,{\mathrm{d}}}(x,y)\coloneqq\frac{1}{t}\,\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[h_{t}(x)h_{t}(y)}}\right]=\frac{1}{t}\sup\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{s\geq 0\,:\,x,y\text{ share a common ancestor in }L_{s}}}\right\}\,.

A fundamental object, that records the correlations of high points, is the
distribution function of the overlap
sampled from the Gibbs measure, i.e.
\mathcal{G}_{\beta,t,{\mathrm{d}}}\otimes\mathcal{G}_{\beta^{\prime},t,{\mathrm{d}}}(q_{t,{\mathrm{d}}}(u,v)\geq a)
for any
a\in(0,1)
, where

(respectively

) is sampled according to
\mathcal{G}_{\beta,t,{\mathrm{d}}}
(respectively
\mathcal{G}_{\beta^{\prime},t,{\mathrm{d}}}
). Bonnefont
[
14
]
recently proved that, if
\beta\leq\beta_{c}
or
\beta^{\prime}\leq\beta_{c}

\mathcal{G}_{\beta,t,{\mathrm{d}}}\otimes\mathcal{G}_{\beta^{\prime},t,{\mathrm{d}}}(q_{t,{\mathrm{d}}}(u,v)\geq a)
tends to

1.3
Main results

Near-critical two-temperature overlap.

Our first results concern the behavior of the mean overlap
\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[Q(\beta,\beta^{\prime})}}\right]
or
\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[Q_{\mathrm{d}}(\beta,\beta^{\prime})}}\right]
at two supercritical inverse temperatures
\beta
and
\beta^{\prime}
.
Recall from Equation (
1.4
) that it has already been proved that
\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[Q_{\mathrm{d}}(\beta,\beta^{\prime})}}\right]<\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[Q(\beta,\beta^{\prime})}}\right]
.
Our goal here is to quantify this difference in the regime where
\beta^{\prime}
is fixed but
\beta
approaches the critical inverse temperature
\beta_{c}=1
.

We start with the REM case.
It is not hard to see that the function
\beta\mapsto\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[Q(\beta,\beta^{\prime})}}\right]
has an infinite right-derivative at 1, see Remark
3.10
. We establish a sharper estimate in the following result.

Theorem 1.1

(REM case)
.

For any
\beta^{\prime}>\beta_{c}=1
, as
\beta\downarrow 1
,

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[Q(\beta,\beta^{\prime})}}\right]=(\beta-1)\log\frac{1}{\beta-1}+O(\beta-1).

We compare this behavior to the one arising for the BBM.
Surprisingly, the decorations have the effect of smoothing the function
\beta\mapsto\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[Q_{\mathrm{d}}(\beta,\beta^{\prime})}}\right]
at criticality: more precisely,
\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[Q_{\mathrm{d}}(\beta,\beta^{\prime})}}\right]
is of order
(\beta-1)
as
\beta\downarrow 1
, as shown in the following result.
It would be interesting to obtain an asymptotic equivalent instead, which would prove that
\beta\mapsto\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[Q_{\mathrm{d}}(\beta,\beta^{\prime})}}\right]
is right-differentiable at
\beta=1
, but this seems out of reach with the techniques used here.

Theorem 1.2

(BBM case)
.

For any
\beta^{\prime}>\beta_{c}=1
,

0<\liminf_{\beta\downarrow 1}\,\frac{\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[Q_{\mathrm{d}}(\beta,\beta^{\prime})}}\right]}{\beta-1}\leq\limsup_{\beta\downarrow 1}\,\frac{\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[Q_{\mathrm{d}}(\beta,\beta^{\prime})}}\right]}{\beta-1}<+\infty.

In order to shed some light on these different behaviors, we study in Section
3.1
the case of general decoration processes
\mathcal{D}=\sum_{k\geq 0}\delta_{d_{k}}
.
We do not state these results precisely here, some of them having very technical assumptions, but rather give an informal description of some of the conclusions:

•

If the sum
\sum_{k\geq 0}\mathrm{e}^{\beta d_{k}}
has bounded
(1+\varepsilon)
-moment as
\beta\downarrow 1
, then the behavior of
\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[Q_{\mathrm{d}}(\beta,\beta^{\prime})}}\right]
is the same as for the REM in Theorem
1.1
, see Corollary
3.2
.

•

If the
\gamma
-th moment of the sum
\sum_{k\geq 0}\mathrm{e}^{\beta d_{k}}
explodes like
(\beta-1)^{-\psi(\gamma)}
as
\beta\downarrow 1
, then different behaviors appear depending on the value of
\psi^{\prime}(1)-\psi(1)
: the larger this value—in other words, the more moments of
\sum_{k\geq 0}\mathrm{e}^{\beta d_{k}}
are governed by rare events—the faster
\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[Q_{\mathrm{d}}(\beta,\beta^{\prime})}}\right]
decreases as
\beta\downarrow 1
.
More precisely, when
\psi^{\prime}(1)-\psi(1)<1
,
\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[Q_{\mathrm{d}}(\beta,\beta^{\prime})}}\right]
is still of order
(\beta-1)\log\frac{1}{\beta-1}
like in the REM case, but possibly with another multiplicative constant.
At the critical value
\psi^{\prime}(1)-\psi(1)=1
, the first order constant vanishes and
\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[Q_{\mathrm{d}}(\beta,\beta^{\prime})}}\right]
becomes of order
(\beta-1)
.
Above the critical value,
\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[Q_{\mathrm{d}}(\beta,\beta^{\prime})}}\right]
decreases faster than
(\beta-1)^{1+\eta}
for
\eta>0
in some explicit interval,
see Corollary
3.5
for a precise statement.

It turns out that the BBM corresponds exactly to the critical case described in the second point. This fact is a consequence of some fine properties of the decoration of the BBM, proved by building upon results by Cortines, Hartung and Louidor
[
26
,
27
]
, who showed that the mean of level sets of the decoration process is driven by rare events in which the decoration is much bigger than its typical behavior.
We believe that this should be the case of other models falling in the log-correlated fields universality class, such as general branching random walks or the 2D discrete Gaussian free field.

We conclude this discussion by two open questions.
Firstly, we wonder if the limit in Theorem
1.2
actually exists, or in other words:

Open question 1.3

(BBM case)
.

For any
\beta^{\prime}>1
, is the function
\beta\in(1,\infty)\mapsto\mathbb{E}[Q_{\mathrm{d}}(\beta,\beta^{\prime})]
right-differentiable at
1
?

Our results compare two 1-RSB systems, but, as suggested by an anonymous referee, it would be interesting to compare systems with more levels of replica symmetry breaking.
More precisely, a
k
-GREM (that is with a tree structure of height
k+1
), where each vertex has
\mathrm{e}^{t/(2k)}
children and with energy increments
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{\ell}^{2}t)
at level
\ell
, is a
k
-RSB system if
\sigma_{1}>\dots>\sigma_{k}
, see
[
16
]
for details.
It can be compared to a
k
-speed BBM, where particles move with variance
\sigma_{\ell}^{2}
on
[(\ell-1)t,\ell t]
for a given time horizon
t
, see
[
15
]
for a description of its extremal process when
k=2
.
With these definitions, the one-temperature overlap distribution is the same for both models in the limit
t\to\infty
, but the two-temperature overlap distribution should be different.
It could be compared in an abstract manner in the spirit of
[
48
,
14
]
, or more quantitatively in the near-critical regime as in this paper.

Open question 1.4
.

Compare the two-temperature overlap distributions of the
k
-GREM and of the
k
-speed BBM.

Temperature susceptibility.

We now turn to results concerning the temperature susceptibility.
We first compute it explicitly for the REM in the supercritical phase and study its asymptotic behavior close to the critical temperature, as well as in the low temperature regime.

Theorem 1.5

(REM case)
.

For any
\beta>\beta_{c}=1
,

\displaystyle\kappa(\beta)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{\beta^{2}-1}+\frac{6}{\pi^{2}\beta^{3}(\beta+1)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\Gamma^{\prime\prime}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\beta-1}{\beta}}}\right)}{\Gamma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\beta-1}{\beta}}}\right)}-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\Gamma^{\prime}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\beta-1}{\beta}}}\right)}{\Gamma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\beta-1}{\beta}}}\right)}}}\right)^{2}}}\right)-\frac{\beta^{2}}{(\beta^{2}-1)^{2}}}}\right)\,,

and

\kappa(\beta)\,\underset{\beta\downarrow 1}{\sim}\,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{3}{2\pi^{2}}-\frac{1}{8}}}\right)\frac{1}{(\beta-1)^{2}}\,,\qquad\qquad\kappa(\beta)\underset{\beta\rightarrow+\infty}{\sim}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{6}{\pi^{2}}\zeta(3)-\frac{1}{2}}}\right)\frac{1}{\beta^{5}}\,.

Remark 1.6
.

This question has already been studied in the physics literature by Sales and Bouchaud
[
52
]
in the case of the REM with exponentially distributed energies.
The formula they obtain for
\kappa(\beta)
(see their appendix) does not seem to match the one above, but we are not able to find the source of this discrepancy. In particular, the exponents appearing as
\beta\downarrow 1
and
\beta\to\infty
are different.

Then, we study the temperature susceptibility of the BBM.
The following results shows that it is strictly larger than the one of the REM and gives the behavior of the temperature susceptibility close to the critical temperature, showing that it diverges at the same speed as for the REM but with a different multiplicative constant. We do not investigate the behavior at low temperature.

Theorem 1.7

(BBM case)
.

For any
\beta>\beta_{c}=1
, one has
\kappa_{\mathrm{d}}(\beta)>\kappa(\beta)
. Moreover, we have

\frac{3}{2\pi^{2}}-\frac{1}{8}<\liminf_{\beta\downarrow 1}\,(\beta-1)^{2}\,\kappa_{\mathrm{d}}(\beta)\leq\limsup_{\beta\downarrow 1}\,(\beta-1)^{2}\,\kappa_{\mathrm{d}}(\beta)\leq\frac{3}{\pi^{2}}-\frac{1}{8}.

As for the two-temperature overlap, we also investigate the general decorated case. Proposition
4.5
shows that
\kappa_{\mathrm{d}}(\beta)
can be written as the sum of
\kappa(\beta)
and a nonnegative term, which is positive for most non-deterministic decoration processes.
Showing that this additional term is growing like
(\beta-1)^{-2}
as
\beta\downarrow 1
for the BBM, and in particular the lower bound, is the main task in the proof of Theorem
1.7
.
In general, this term can be negligible w.r.t.
(\beta-1)^{-2}
for “small enough” decorations, and it can be much larger than
(\beta-1)^{-2}
for “big enough” decorations, see Example
4.6
. So again, the BBM seems to belong to a critical window, even if here this critical window seems to include more models than for the two-temperature overlap, see e.g. the first family of decorations considered in Example
4.6
.

Finally we state two open questions similar to the ones for the two-temperature overlap.

Open question 1.8

(BBM case)
.

Does the limit of
(\beta-1)^{2}\,\kappa_{\mathrm{d}}(\beta)
as
\beta\downarrow 1
exists?

In the context of susceptibility, the number of levels of replica symmetry breaking does not really matter. However, one can expect that the susceptibility is also explicit for the GREM and it would be interesting to compare it with the one for the REM.

Open question 1.9
.

Find the susceptibility for the GREM.

2.1
Properties of
Z(\beta)

In this section, we collect some basic facts on the partition function of the REM in the supercritical phase
\beta>1
that will be useful in the following sections. We work directly with the limiting extremal process in the spirit of Ruelle’s reformulation of the REM
[
51
]
(see also
[
31
]
by Derrida and Mottishaw where this model is called the Poisson REM) : the partition function is given by
Z(\beta)=\sum\mathrm{e}^{\beta\xi_{k}}
where
(\xi_{k})_{k\geq 1}
are the atoms ranked in non-increasing order of a PPP(
\mathrm{e}^{-x}\mathrm{d}x
). The fact that this is the limit of the properly renormalized partition function of the finite size REM is proved by Bovier, Kurkova and Löwe
[
18
, Theorem 1.5]
.

It will be convenient to use the notation
\eta_{k}\coloneqq\mathrm{e}^{-\xi_{k}}
because these are the atoms of a homogeneous Poisson point process on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
and allows us to use the law of large numbers.
Hence
Z(\beta)
, can be rewritten

Z(\beta)=\sum_{k\geq 1}\eta_{k}^{-\beta},\qquad\forall\,\beta>1.

(2.1)

We first show that
Z(\beta)
has a stable distribution. We use the following convention: the stable distribution
{\rm Stable}_{\alpha}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(c,s,\mu}}\right)
3
3

3

Here we do not use
\beta
for the skewness parameter because the letter is used for the inverse temperature.
has a characteristic function given by

t\mapsto\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{it\mu-|ct|^{\alpha}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-is\,\mathrm{sgn}(t)\Phi}}\right)}}\right\},\quad\text{where}\quad\Phi=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\tan\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\pi\alpha}{2}}}\right),&\mbox{if }\alpha\neq 1,\\
-\frac{2}{\pi}\log|t|,&\mbox{if }\alpha=1.\end{array}}}\right.

Lemma 2.1

(REM case)
.

The Laplace transform of
Z(\beta)
is given, for any
t\geq 0
, by

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathrm{e}^{-tZ(\beta)}}}\right]=\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{-\Gamma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\beta-1}{\beta}}}\right)t^{\frac{1}{\beta}}}}\right\}.

In other words, the distribution of
Z(\beta)
is
{\rm Stable}_{1/\beta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\big(\Gamma(1-\frac{1}{\beta})\cos\frac{\pi}{2\beta}\big)^{\beta},1,0}}\right)
.

Proof.

The formula for Laplace transforms of Poisson point processes applied to Equation (
2.1
) gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathrm{e}^{-tZ(\beta)}}}\right]

\displaystyle=\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{-\int_{0}^{\infty}(1-\mathrm{e}^{-tx^{-\beta}})\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}x}}\right\}=\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{-\Gamma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\beta-1}{\beta}}}\right)t^{\frac{1}{\beta}}}}\right\}\,,

which is the Laplace transform of the desired stable law.
∎

Remark 2.2
.

The fact that the partition function has a stable distribution appears as a consequence of
[
6
, Theorem 2.3]
by Ben Arous, Bogachev and Molchanov, where the authors study the limiting distributions of exponential sums of i.i.d. random variables.

Lemma 2.3

(REM case)
.

For any
\beta>1
and any
\alpha>-\frac{1}{\beta}
,

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[Z(\beta)^{-\alpha}}}\right]=\frac{\Gamma(\alpha\beta+1)}{\Gamma(\alpha+1)\Gamma(\frac{\beta-1}{\beta})^{\alpha\beta}}\,.

Proof.

For
\alpha>0
, this is proved by taking the expectation of

Z(\beta)^{-\alpha}=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{\alpha-1}\mathrm{e}^{-tZ(\beta)}\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}t,

and then applying Lemma
2.1
.
For
-1/\beta<\alpha<0
, we use instead the following representation

Z(\beta)^{-\alpha}=\frac{1}{\Gamma(1+\alpha)}\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{\alpha}Z(\beta)\,\mathrm{e}^{-tZ(\beta)}\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}t,

and note that
\mathbb{E}[Z(\beta)\mathrm{e}^{-tZ(\beta)}]
is obtained by differentiating
\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{e}^{-tZ(\beta)}]
with respect to
t
.
∎

The formula established in the previous lemma yields to the following moment estimates in the regime
\beta\downarrow 1
, which are used throughout the paper.

Corollary 2.4

(REM case)
.

Let
\alpha>-1
. Then, as
\beta\downarrow 1
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[Z(\beta)^{-\alpha}}}\right]

\displaystyle=(\beta-1)^{\alpha}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left((\beta-1)\log\frac{1}{\beta-1}}}\right)}}\right)\,,

(2.2)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[Z(\beta)^{-\alpha/\beta}}}\right]

\displaystyle=(\beta-1)^{\alpha}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+O(\beta-1)}}\right)\,,

(2.3)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[Z(\beta)^{-1/\beta}\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(Z(\beta)^{1/\beta}}}\right)}}\right]

\displaystyle=(\beta-1)\log\frac{1}{\beta-1}+O(\beta-1).

(2.4)

Proof.

The first two expansions are obtained by expanding the Gamma function in the formula given by Lemma
2.3
.
For the third one, note that, for any
\beta>1
and
\alpha>-1/\beta
,
\frac{\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}\alpha}\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[Z(\beta)^{-\alpha}}}\right]=-\alpha\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[Z(\beta)^{-\alpha}\log Z(\beta)}}\right]
.
∎

The expansions in the previous corollary are governed by the fact that
Z(\beta)
concentrates around
1/(\beta-1)
as
\beta\downarrow 1
. More precisely, we have surprisingly the following almost sure expansion.

Lemma 2.5

(REM case)
.

When
\beta\downarrow 1
, we have

Z(\beta)=\frac{1}{\beta-1}+W+o(1),\quad\text{almost surely,}

where
W\sim{\rm Stable}_{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\pi}{2},1,1-\gamma}}\right)

and
\gamma
is the Euler constant.

Proof.

With the Riemann
\zeta
function defined by
\zeta(\beta)=\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{1}{k^{\beta}}
, one has

Z(\beta)-\zeta(\beta)=\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{k^{\beta}-\eta_{k}^{\beta}}{k^{\beta}\eta_{k}^{\beta}}.

If
\beta\leq 2
, one has

\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|k^{\beta}-\eta_{k}^{\beta}}}\right|}{k^{\beta}\eta_{k}^{\beta}}\leq\frac{\beta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\eta_{k}^{\beta-1}+k^{\beta-1}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\eta_{k}-k}}\right|}{k^{\beta}\eta_{k}^{\beta}}\leq 5\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\eta_{k}-k}}\right|}{k^{2}},

for
k
large enough. This last term is summable since
\eta_{k}=k+O(k^{\frac{3}{4}})
a.s.
Hence, the dominated convergence theorem (for series) implies that
Z(\beta)-\zeta(\beta)
has an a.s. limit when
\beta
tends to
1
.
The almost sure expansion follows from the fact that
\zeta(\beta)=\frac{1}{\beta-1}+\gamma+o(1)
when
\beta\downarrow 1
. Then, computing the characteristic function enables to identify the limiting law: if
t>0
, one has

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathrm{e}^{itZ(\beta)}}}\right]

\displaystyle=\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{-\Gamma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\beta-1}{\beta}}}\right)\cos\frac{\pi}{2\beta}\,t^{\frac{1}{\beta}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-i\tan\frac{\pi}{2\beta}}}\right)}}\right\}

\displaystyle=\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{-\Gamma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\beta-1}{\beta}}}\right)t^{\frac{1}{\beta}}\mathrm{e}^{-i\frac{\pi}{2\beta}}}}\right\}

\displaystyle=\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{it\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{\beta-1}-\log(t)+1-\gamma+i\frac{\pi}{2}}}\right)+O(\beta-1)}}\right\},

as
\beta\downarrow 1
, therefore

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathrm{e}^{it\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(Z(\beta)-\frac{1}{\beta-1}}}\right)}}}\right]

\displaystyle\underset{\beta\downarrow 1}{\longrightarrow}\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{it(1-\gamma)-\frac{\pi}{2}t\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+i\frac{2}{\pi}\log t}}\right)}}\right\},

which is the expected characteristic function. The case
t<0
is similar.
∎

Remark 2.6
.

These 1-stable fluctuations are reminiscent of some recent results on the BBM: such fluctuations appear for the critical Gibbs measure
[
43
,
44
]
, as well as for the limiting extremal process, when re-centered around a low position
[
47
]
. The result above shows that 1-stable fluctuations already appear in the much simpler context of the extremal process of the REM.
Moreover, it has a consequence for the BBM stated in Remark
2.14
.

2.2
Change of measure in the general decorated case

In the paper, we sometimes consider the
general decorated case
.
This means that we work with a limiting extremal process which is of the form

\sum_{i\geq 1}\sum_{k\geq 0}\delta_{\xi_{i}+d_{ik}},

where the
(\xi_{i})_{i\geq 1}
are the atoms of a PPP(
\mathrm{e}^{-x}\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}x
) independent of
(\sum_{k\geq 0}\delta_{d_{ik}})_{i\geq 1}
, which are i.i.d. copies of a point process
\mathcal{D}=\sum_{k\geq 0}\delta_{d_{k}}
on
(-\infty,0]
which has a.s. an atom at 0,
but we do not assume that
\mathcal{D}
is the decoration process of the BBM.
In particular, for
\beta,\beta^{\prime}>\beta_{c}=1
the limiting partition function
Z_{\mathrm{d}}(\beta)
is defined as in Equation (
1.5
) and the limiting mass at 1 of the two-temperature overlap
Q_{\mathrm{d}}(\beta,\beta^{\prime})
is defined as in Equation (
1.3
).

Moreover, let us introduce the partition functions associated to the decoration processes
4
4

4

Note that we do not add a subscript “d” in this notation for the sake of readability in the proofs where the quantity
S_{\beta}
is ubiquitous. There is no confusion possible with the REM for which the quantity
S_{\beta}
does not appear (it is equal to 1 in that case).
:

S_{\beta}\coloneqq\sum_{k\geq 0}\mathrm{e}^{\beta d_{k}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad S_{\beta,i}\coloneqq\sum_{k\geq 0}\mathrm{e}^{\beta d_{ik}},\qquad\forall\,i\geq 1.

For
\beta>1
, assuming
\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[S_{\beta}^{1/\beta}}}\right]<\infty
, we introduce a new probability measure
\mathbb{P}_{\beta}
such that

•

the distribution of
\mathcal{D}
under
\mathbb{P}_{\beta}
is characterized by

\mathbb{E}_{\beta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[F(\mathcal{D})}}\right]=\frac{\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[S_{\beta}^{1/\beta}F(\mathcal{D})}}\right]}{\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[S_{\beta}^{1/\beta}}}\right]},

(2.5)

for any measurable bounded function
F
from the space of Radon measures on
\mathbb{R}
to
\mathbb{R}
;

•

under
\mathbb{P}_{\beta}
,
the
(\xi_{i})_{i\geq 1}
are still the atoms of a Poisson point process on
\mathbb{R}
with intensity measure
\mathrm{e}^{-x}\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}x
independent of
(\sum_{k\geq 0}\delta_{d_{ik}})_{i\geq 1}
, which are i.i.d. copies of
\mathcal{D}
under
\mathbb{P}_{\beta}
.

With this definition in hand, the following fact holds.

Lemma 2.7

(General decorated case)
.

Let
\beta>1
. Assume
\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[S_{\beta}^{1/\beta}}}\right]<\infty
.
Then, with
c_{\beta}\coloneqq\log\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[S_{\beta}^{1/\beta}}}\right]
,

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\,\sum_{i\geq 0}\delta_{\xi_{i}+\frac{1}{\beta}\log S_{\beta,i}},\bigg(\sum_{k\geq 0}\delta_{d_{ik}}\bigg)_{i\geq 1}\,}}\right)\;\mathrm{under}\;\mathbb{P}\;\;\overset{\mathrm{(d)}}{=}\;\;\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\,\sum_{i\geq 0}\delta_{\xi_{i}+c_{\beta}},\bigg(\sum_{k\geq 0}\delta_{d_{ik}}\bigg)_{i\geq 1}\,}}\right)\;\mathrm{under}\;\mathbb{P}_{\beta}\,.

Proof.

This can be obtained via a direct Laplace transform calculation, or as a consequence of
[
49
, Lemma 2.1]
applied with

u_{i}=\mathrm{e}^{\beta\xi_{i}}
,

m=1/\beta
,

X_{i}=S_{\beta,i}

and
Y_{i}=\sum_{k\geq 0}\delta_{d_{ik}}
.
∎

As a consequence, we get the following simple distribution for the decorated partition function

Z_{\mathrm{d}}(\beta)\coloneqq\sum_{i\geq 1}\sum_{k\geq 0}\mathrm{e}^{\beta(\xi_{i}+d_{ik})}=\sum_{i\geq 1}\mathrm{e}^{\beta\xi_{i}}S_{\beta,i}.

However, note that one cannot relate the joint distribution of
(Z_{\mathrm{d}}(\beta),Z_{\mathrm{d}}(\beta^{\prime}))
with the one of
(Z(\beta),Z(\beta^{\prime}))
in such a way (otherwise the two-temperature overlap distribution would not depend on the decoration).

Corollary 2.8

(General decorated case)
.

Let
\beta>1
. Assume
\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[S_{\beta}^{1/\beta}}}\right]<\infty
. Then, under
\mathbb{P}
,

Z_{\mathrm{d}}(\beta)\overset{(\mathrm{d})}{=}\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[S_{\beta}^{1/\beta}}}\right]^{\beta}Z(\beta).

Proof.

By Lemma
2.7
,
Z_{\mathrm{d}}(\beta)
under
\mathbb{P}
has the same distribution as
\mathrm{e}^{\beta c_{\beta}}Z(\beta)
under
\mathbb{P}_{\beta}
. But
Z(\beta)
has the same law under
\mathbb{P}
and under
\mathbb{P}_{\beta}
, so the result follows.
∎

Remark 2.9
.

When
\mathbb{E}[S_{\beta}^{1/\beta}]=\infty
, one can show that
Z_{\mathrm{d}}(\beta)=\infty
a.s.
This follows for example from the previous result applied to
S_{\beta}\wedge M
and then letting
M\to\infty
. Therefore, the assumption that
\mathbb{E}[S_{\beta}^{1/\beta}]<\infty
for any
\beta>1
is a very minimalist one when working with the decorated partition function.

2.3
The decoration of the BBM

A description of the decoration.

In this section, we recall some results on the law of the decoration point process

\mathcal{D}=\sum_{k\geq 0}\delta_{d_{k}}\,,

appearing in the limit of the extremal process of the BBM.
As mentioned in the introduction (see Equation (
1.1
)), convergence of this extremal process has been established in
[
1
]
and
[
3
]
, which give two different descriptions of the law of
\mathcal{D}
.
However, we use here a third description obtained in
[
26
, Lemma 5.1]
, as well as several other results shown in this paper and its sequel
[
27
]
.
Note that the authors work with a BBM with branching rate 1 (instead of
1/2
for us here), but that both processes have the same distribution up to a time-space scaling.
In particular, one can check that the decoration process
\mathcal{C}
appearing in this case (branching rate 1) can be related to
\mathcal{D}
as follows:

\mathcal{C}=\sum_{k\geq 0}\delta_{d_{k}/\sqrt{2}}\,.

For the sake of clarity, we give below the description of the law of
\mathcal{C}
(instead of
\mathcal{D}
) so that we can work exactly in the same setting as
[
26
]
when we adapt some of their proofs.

We consider a BBM with a spine defined under a new probability measure
\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}
as follows. It starts with one particle at 0 at time 0 which is part of the spine. Particles along the spine branch at rate 2 and move according to a standard Brownian motion. When they branch into two particles, one of them, chosen uniformly at random, is part of the spine and the other one starts a standard BBM with branching rate 1.
We denote by
L_{t}
the set of particles alive at time
t
, by
X_{t}
the particle at time
t
which is part of the spine, and by
h_{t}(x)
for
x\in L_{t}
the position of particle
x
at time
t
.

Moreover, we set

m_{t}\coloneqq\sqrt{2}t-\frac{3}{2\sqrt{2}}\log t\,,

which is the position of the maximum of the BBM (with branching rate 1) at time
t
up to
O(1)
fluctuations,
and, for
0<r\leq t
and
x\in L_{t}
,

\mathcal{C}_{t,r}(x)\coloneqq\sum_{y\in L_{t}\,:\,d(x,y)<r}\delta_{h_{t}(y)-h_{t}(x)}\,,

where
d(x,y):=\inf\{s\geq 0:x\text{ and }y\text{ share a common ancestor in }L_{t-s}\}
.
For brevity, we introduce the new probability measure

\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}_{t}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\cdot}}\right)\coloneqq\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\,\cdot\,\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}h_{t}(X_{t})=\max_{x\in L_{t}}h_{t}(x)=m_{t}}}\right),

(2.6)

as well as

\mathcal{C}_{t,r_{t}}^{*}\coloneqq\mathcal{C}_{t,r_{t}}(X_{t}).

Then, for any positive function
t\mapsto r_{t}
such that both
r_{t}
and
t-r_{t}
tend to
\infty
as
t\to\infty
,
[
26
, Lemma 5.1]
with
u=0
establishes that, for the vague convergence on the set of Radon measures on
\mathbb{R}
,

\mathcal{C}_{t,r_{t}}^{*}\text{ under }\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}_{t}\quad\xrightarrow[t\to\infty]{\text{(d)}}\quad\mathcal{C}\text{ under }\mathbb{P}.

(2.7)

In their papers
[
26
,
27
]
, Cortines, Hartung and Louidor develop tools to study
\mathcal{C}_{t,r_{t}}^{*}
under
\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}_{t}
and therefore obtain results on the distribution of
\mathcal{C}
.

Level sets of the decoration.

Some of the main results in
[
26
]
concern the level sets of the decoration point process itself. Recall that
\mathcal{C}
is supported on
(-\infty,0]
and has a.s. an atom at 0.
In
[
26
, Proposition 1.5]
, they prove the existence of constants
C_{\star},C>0
such that

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathcal{D}([-\sqrt{2}x,0])}}\right]=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathcal{C}([-x,0])}}\right]\sim C_{\star}\,\mathrm{e}^{\sqrt{2}x},\qquad\text{as }x\to\infty,

(2.8)

(2.10)

As a consequence, Cortines, Hartung and Louidor deduce a law of large numbers for large level sets of the whole limiting extremal process.
This law of large numbers, as well as the 1-stable fluctuations appearing at the next order, have been subsequently obtained via PDE techniques by Mytnik, Roquejoffre and Ryzhik

Proposition 2.11

(BBM case)

Similarly, applying Fubini’s theorem and then Cauchy-Schwarz inequality together with Equation (

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[S_{\beta}^{2}}}\right]

Corollary 2.13

(BBM case)
.

Let

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[S_{\beta}^{1/\beta}}}\right]\sim\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[S_{\beta}^{2-1/\beta}}}\right]\sim\frac{C_{\star}}{\beta-1}.

3
Overlap distribution at two temperatures

3.1
Results in the general decorated case

We study here the influence of the decorations on the behavior of
\mathbb{E}[Q_{\mathrm{d}}(\beta,\beta^{\prime})]
, when
\beta^{\prime}>\beta_{c}=1
is fixed and
\beta\downarrow 1
and show that they can change the leading order drastically.
We work in the general decorated case (see Section
2.2
) in order to highlight the fact that the BBM has a critical behavior in some sense to be made precise below.

We assume that

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[S_{\beta}^{1/\beta}}}\right]<\infty,\qquad\forall\,\beta>1.

(3.1)

Recall from Corollary
2.8
and Remark
2.9
that it is a necessary and sufficient condition to have
Z_{\mathrm{d}}(\beta)<\infty
almost surely.
In particular, this ensures that
Q_{\mathrm{d}}(\beta,\beta^{\prime})
, introduced in Equation (
1.3
) and which can be written as

Q_{\mathrm{d}}(\beta,\beta^{\prime})=\frac{\sum_{i}\mathrm{e}^{(\beta+\beta^{\prime})\xi_{i}}S_{\beta,i}S_{\beta^{\prime},i}}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{i}\mathrm{e}^{\beta\xi_{i}}S_{\beta,i}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{i}\mathrm{e}^{\beta^{\prime}\xi_{i}}S_{\beta^{\prime},i}}}\right)},

(3.2)

is well-defined, and henceforth
\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[Q_{\mathrm{d}}(\beta,\beta^{\prime})}}\right]
as well.

In the following results, assumptions are stated in terms of the following random variables

T_{\beta}\coloneqq\frac{S_{\beta}^{1/\beta}}{\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[S_{\beta}^{1/\beta}}}\right]},\qquad\forall\,\beta>1.

Note that
\mathbb{E}[T_{\beta}]=1
.
The fact that the assumptions of the following results are stated in terms of
T_{\beta}
shows that the behavior of
\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[Q_{\mathrm{d}}(\beta,\beta^{\prime})}}\right]
as
\beta\downarrow 1
is not governed by the growth rate of
\mathbb{E}[S_{\beta}^{1/\beta}]
as
\beta\downarrow 1
, but rather by how much
S_{\beta}^{1/\beta}
is fluctuating around its mean.
In the forthcoming assumptions, these fluctuations are controlled in terms of how fast
\mathbb{E}[T_{\beta}^{1+\varepsilon}]
explodes or
\mathbb{E}[T_{\beta}^{1-\varepsilon}]
vanishes.

The first result concerns the case of weakly fluctuating decorations in which
\mathbb{E}[T_{\beta}^{1+\varepsilon}]
does not explode too fast.

Theorem 3.1

(General decorated case)
.

Assume (
3.1
) and that the decoration process
\mathcal{D}
satisfies the following properties:

(i)

There exists
\alpha\in[0,1]
such that

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[T_{\beta}\log T_{\beta}}}\right]=(\alpha+o(1))\log\frac{1}{\beta-1}
, as
\beta\downarrow 1
.

(ii)

There exists
\varepsilon>0
such that

\mathbb{E}[T_{\beta}^{1+\varepsilon}]=O((\beta-1)^{-\varepsilon})
, as
\beta\downarrow 1
.

(iii)

For any
\beta^{\prime}>1
,
\mathbb{E}[T_{\beta}\log T_{\beta^{\prime}}]=O(1)
, as
\beta\downarrow 1
.

Then, for any
\beta^{\prime}>1
, as
\beta\downarrow 1
,

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[Q_{\mathrm{d}}(\beta,\beta^{\prime})}}\right]=(1-\alpha+o(1))(\beta-1)\log\frac{1}{\beta-1}.

If moreover, we assume

(i’)

There exists
\alpha\in[0,1]
such that

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[T_{\beta}\log T_{\beta}}}\right]=\alpha\log\frac{1}{\beta-1}+O(1)
, as
\beta\downarrow 1
,

\beta^{\prime}>1
, as
\beta\downarrow 1

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[Q_{\mathrm{d}}(\beta,\beta^{\prime})}}\right]=(1-\alpha)(\beta-1)\log\frac{1}{\beta-1}+O(\beta-1).

(General decorated case)
.

Noting that

Corollary 3.5

(General decorated case)

Assume there exist
\gamma_{-}<1<\gamma_{+}

Remark 3.10
.

It follows from Lemma
2.5
that a.s., as
\beta\downarrow 1
,

Q(\beta,\beta^{\prime})=\frac{Z(\beta+\beta^{\prime})}{Z(\beta)Z(\beta^{\prime})}\sim\frac{Z(1+\beta^{\prime})}{Z(\beta^{\prime})}(\beta-1),

proving that the function
Q(\,\cdot\,,\beta^{\prime})
is almost surely right differentiable at
1
.
On the other hand, Theorem
1.1
implies that
\mathbb{E}[Q(\,\cdot\,,\beta^{\prime})]
has an infinite right-derivative at
1
.
There is however a simple way to show this, without aiming for the first order of
\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[Q(\beta,\beta^{\prime})}}\right]
as
\beta\downarrow 1
: indeed, using Fatou’s lemma,

\liminf\limits_{\beta\downarrow 1}\frac{\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[Q(\beta,\beta^{\prime})}}\right]}{\beta-1}\geq\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\liminf\limits_{\beta\downarrow 1}\frac{Q(\beta,\beta^{\prime})}{\beta-1}}}\right]=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{Z(1+\beta^{\prime})}{Z(\beta^{\prime})}}}\right],

and it is not hard to see that this last expectation is infinite using Palm formula.

We have seen in the previous remark that, as
\beta\downarrow 1
,
Q(\beta,\beta^{\prime})
is of order
\beta-1
, but Theorem
1.1
shows that
\mathbb{E}[Q(\beta,\beta^{\prime})]
is much larger.
Hence, the expectation is dominated by an unlikely event, in which the highest point in the extremal process is exceptionally high, more precisely at a position of order
\log\frac{1}{\beta-1}
, but not higher than
\log\frac{1}{\beta-1}
, as proved in the following proposition.

Proposition 3.11

(REM case)
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
and

E_{\beta}\coloneqq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\xi_{1}\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\varepsilon\log\frac{1}{\beta-1},\log\frac{1}{\beta-1}}}\right]}}\right\}.

On the one hand, noting that

, we have

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[Q(\beta,\beta^{\prime})\mathbbm{1}_{\{\xi_{1}>\log\frac{1}{\beta-1}\}}}}\right]\leq\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\xi_{1}>\log\frac{1}{\beta-1}}}\right)=1-\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{-\int_{\log\frac{1}{\beta-1}}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-x}\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}x}}\right\}=O(\beta-1).

On the other hand, recalling that

for all

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[Q(\beta,\beta^{\prime})\mathbbm{1}_{\{\xi_{1}<\varepsilon\log\frac{1}{\beta-1}\}}}}\right]

4
Temperature susceptibility

4.1
A first formula for
\kappa_{\mathrm{d}}

We work here in the general decorated case (see Section
2.2
) to prove a first formula for the susceptibility, which is useful for both the REM and the BBM.
Recall that we consider

\mathbf{C}_{\mathrm{d}}(\beta,\beta+h)=\frac{\Cov\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\log Z_{\mathrm{d}}(\beta),\log Z_{\mathrm{d}}(\beta+h)}}\right)}{\sigma(\log Z_{\mathrm{d}}(\beta))\,\sigma(\log Z_{\mathrm{d}}(\beta+h))}

(4.1)

and define the susceptibility in temperature, if it exists, as the coefficient
\kappa_{\mathrm{d}}(\beta)
such that

\mathbf{C}_{\mathrm{d}}(\beta,\beta+h)=1-\kappa_{\mathrm{d}}(\beta)\,h^{2}+o(h^{2}),\qquad h\rightarrow 0.

(4.2)

The main technical tool to prove existence of
\kappa_{\mathrm{d}}(\beta)
is the following lemma.

Lemma 4.1

(General decorated case)
.

Assume that
\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[S_{\beta}}}\right]<\infty
, for every
\beta>1
. Then, for any
\beta^{\prime}>1
, the functions

\beta\in(1,\infty)\mapsto f_{1}(\beta)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\log Z_{\mathrm{d}}(\beta^{\prime})\log Z_{\mathrm{d}}(\beta)}}\right],

\beta\in(1,\infty)\mapsto f_{2}(\beta)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\log^{2}Z_{\mathrm{d}}(\beta)}}\right],

\beta\in(1,\infty)\mapsto f_{3}(\beta)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\log Z_{\mathrm{d}}(\beta)}}\right],

are of class
C^{2}
and their first and second derivatives are given by taking the derivative under
\mathbb{E}
.

Proof.

Let us write

Z_{\mathrm{d}}(\beta)=\mathrm{e}^{\beta\xi_{1}}\sum_{k\geq 1}\mathrm{e}^{\beta E_{k}},

where
(E_{k})_{k\geq 1}
is the decreasing reordering of
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\xi_{i}+d_{i,j}-\xi_{1}\,;\,i,j}}\right\}
. Note that
E_{1}=0
and rewrite
f_{1}
as

f_{1}(\beta)=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\log Z_{\mathrm{d}}(\beta^{\prime})\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathrm{e}^{\beta\xi_{1}}\sum\mathrm{e}^{\beta E_{k}}}}\right)}}\right]=\beta\,\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\xi_{1}\log Z_{\mathrm{d}}(\beta^{\prime})}}\right]+\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\log Z_{\mathrm{d}}(\beta^{\prime})\log\sum\mathrm{e}^{\beta E_{k}}}}\right].

Thus
f_{1}
is
C^{1}
with

f_{1}^{\prime}(\beta)=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\xi_{1}\log Z_{\mathrm{d}}(\beta^{\prime})}}\right]+\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\log Z_{\mathrm{d}}(\beta^{\prime})\frac{\sum E_{k}\,\mathrm{e}^{\beta E_{k}}}{\sum\mathrm{e}^{\beta E_{k}}}}}\right],

since, for any given
\beta_{1}>1
, one has, for all
\beta>\beta_{1}
,

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\log Z_{\mathrm{d}}(\beta^{\prime})\,\frac{\sum E_{k}\,\mathrm{e}^{\beta E_{k}}}{\sum\mathrm{e}^{\beta E_{k}}}}}\right\rvert\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\log Z_{\mathrm{d}}(\beta^{\prime})}}\right\rvert\frac{\sum\,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert E_{k}}}\right\rvert\,\mathrm{e}^{\beta_{1}E_{k}}}{\sum\mathrm{e}^{\beta_{1}E_{k}}}\,,

and those two last terms have finite second moments. The first one because
\log Z_{\mathrm{d}}(\beta^{\prime})
has the same law as
\log Z(\beta)
up to translation and the second one since

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\sum|E_{k}|\,\mathrm{e}^{\beta_{1}E_{k}}}{\sum\mathrm{e}^{\beta_{1}E_{k}}}}}\right)^{2}\leq\frac{\sum E_{k}^{2}\,\mathrm{e}^{\beta_{1}E_{k}}}{\sum\mathrm{e}^{\beta_{1}E_{k}}}\leq\sum E_{k}^{2}\,\mathrm{e}^{\beta_{1}E_{k}}\leq C\sum\mathrm{e}^{\beta_{2}E_{k}},

for some
\beta_{2}\in(1,\beta_{1})
and
C>0
.
For the second derivative, one has

f_{1}^{\prime\prime}(\beta)=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\log Z_{\mathrm{d}}(\beta^{\prime})\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\sum E_{k}^{2}\,\mathrm{e}^{\beta E_{k}}}{\sum\mathrm{e}^{\beta E_{k}}}-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\sum E_{k}\,\mathrm{e}^{\beta E_{k}}}{\sum\mathrm{e}^{\beta E_{k}}}}}\right)^{2}}}\right)}}\right],

since, for any given
\beta_{1}>1
, one has, for all
\beta>\beta_{1}
,

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\log Z_{\mathrm{d}}(\beta^{\prime})\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\sum E_{k}^{2}\,\mathrm{e}^{\beta E_{k}}}{\sum\mathrm{e}^{\beta E_{k}}}-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\sum E_{k}\,\mathrm{e}^{\beta E_{k}}}{\sum\mathrm{e}^{\beta E_{k}}}}}\right)^{2}}}\right)}}\right\rvert\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\log Z_{\mathrm{d}}(\beta^{\prime})}}\right\rvert\frac{\sum E_{k}^{2}\,\mathrm{e}^{\beta_{1}E_{k}}}{\sum\mathrm{e}^{\beta_{1}E_{k}}}\,,

which has again finite expectation with the same reasoning as above. One can then check that the first and second derivatives are the same than the ones obtained by derivating under
\mathbb{E}
. The computations are very similar for
f_{2}
and
f_{3}
, the only extra argument one needs for
f_{2}
is

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\log\sum\mathrm{e}^{\beta E_{k}}}}\right\rvert\,\frac{\sum E_{k}^{2}\,\mathrm{e}^{\beta E_{k}}}{\sum\mathrm{e}^{\beta E_{k}}}\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\log\sum\mathrm{e}^{\beta_{1}E_{k}}}}\right\rvert\,\frac{\sum E_{k}^{2}\,\mathrm{e}^{\beta_{1}E_{k}}}{\sum\mathrm{e}^{\beta_{1}E_{k}}},

for
\beta>\beta_{1}>1
using the fact
\beta\mapsto\log\sum\mathrm{e}^{\beta E_{k}}
is decreasing and positive.
∎

We can now deduce the main result of this section.

Corollary 4.2

(General decorated case)
.

, for every

. Then, for any

,

is well-defined and given by

\kappa_{\mathrm{d}}(\beta)=\frac{1}{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\Var\,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{Z_{\mathrm{d}}^{\prime}(\beta)}{Z_{\mathrm{d}}(\beta)}}}\right)}{\Var\,(\log Z_{\mathrm{d}}(\beta))}-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\Cov\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\log Z_{\mathrm{d}}(\beta),\frac{Z_{\mathrm{d}}^{\prime}(\beta)}{Z_{\mathrm{d}}(\beta)}}}\right)}{\Var\,(\log Z_{\mathrm{d}}(\beta))}}}\right)^{2}}}\right).

Proof.

We expand
\mathbf{C}_{\mathrm{d}}(\beta,\beta+h)
as

, starting from Equation (
4.1
) and using Lemma
4.1
, writing

instead of

for brevity,

\displaystyle\mathbf{C}_{\mathrm{d}}(\beta,\beta+h)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Cov\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\log Z_{\mathrm{d}},\log Z_{\mathrm{d}}+\frac{Z_{\mathrm{d}}^{\prime}}{Z_{\mathrm{d}}}\,h+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{Z_{\mathrm{d}}^{\prime\prime}}{Z_{\mathrm{d}}}-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{Z_{\mathrm{d}}^{\prime}}{Z_{\mathrm{d}}}}}\right)^{2}}}\right)\,h^{2}}}\right)}{\sigma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\log Z_{\mathrm{d}}}}\right)\,\sigma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\log Z_{\mathrm{d}}+\frac{Z_{\mathrm{d}}^{\prime}}{Z_{\mathrm{d}}}\,h+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{Z_{\mathrm{d}}^{\prime\prime}}{Z_{\mathrm{d}}}-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{Z_{\mathrm{d}}^{\prime}}{Z_{\mathrm{d}}}}}\right)^{2}}}\right)\,h^{2}}}\right)}+o\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(h^{2}}}\right)

\displaystyle=1-\frac{1}{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\Var\frac{Z_{\mathrm{d}}^{\prime}}{Z_{\mathrm{d}}}}{\Var\log Z_{\mathrm{d}}}-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\Cov\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\log Z_{\mathrm{d}},\frac{Z_{\mathrm{d}}^{\prime}}{Z_{\mathrm{d}}}}}\right)}{\Var\log Z_{\mathrm{d}}}}}\right)^{2}}}\right)h^{2}+o\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(h^{2}}}\right)\,,

\mathbb{E}[\log Z(\beta)]
, and differentiating once more, we get

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\log^{2}Z(\beta)}}\right]=\frac{\pi^{2}}{6}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\beta^{2}-1}}\right)+\gamma^{2}(\beta-1)^{2}+\beta\log\Gamma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\frac{1}{\beta}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2\gamma(\beta-1)+\beta\log\Gamma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\frac{1}{\beta}}}\right)}}\right),

which yields the formula for the variance.
∎

The following formula is a bit more tricky to obtain.

Lemma 4.4

(REM case)
.

For
\beta>1
, we have

\Var\,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{Z^{\prime}(\beta)}{Z(\beta)}}}\right)=\frac{\pi^{2}}{6}+\frac{\beta-1}{\beta^{3}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\Gamma^{\prime\prime}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\beta-1}{\beta}}}\right)}{\Gamma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\beta-1}{\beta}}}\right)}-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\Gamma^{\prime}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\beta-1}{\beta}}}\right)}{\Gamma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\beta-1}{\beta}}}\right)}}}\right)^{2}}}\right)\,.

Proof.

To lighten notations, let us denote
\Gamma=\Gamma(\frac{\beta-1}{\beta})
and similarly
\Gamma^{\prime}=\Gamma^{\prime}(\frac{\beta-1}{\beta})
,
\Gamma^{\prime\prime}=\Gamma^{\prime\prime}(\frac{\beta-1}{\beta})
.
Differentiating once Equation (
4.3
) gives

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{Z^{\prime}(\beta)}{Z(\beta)}}}\right]=\log\Gamma+\frac{1}{\beta}\frac{\Gamma^{\prime}}{\Gamma}+\gamma\,,

(4.5)

and differentiating once more yields

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{Z^{\prime\prime}(\beta)}{Z(\beta)}-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{Z^{\prime}(\beta)}{Z(\beta)}}}\right)^{2}}}\right]=\frac{1}{\beta^{3}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\Gamma^{\prime\prime}}{\Gamma}-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\Gamma^{\prime}}{\Gamma}}}\right)^{2}}}\right)\,.

(4.6)

Therefore, we now aim at computing
\mathbb{E}[Z^{\prime\prime}(\beta)/Z(\beta)]
. Applying Palm formula (see Proposition
3.8
), we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{Z^{\prime\prime}(\beta)}{Z(\beta)}}}\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\log^{2}(x)\,\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{1}{1+x^{\beta}Z(\beta)}}}\right]\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}x\,\log^{2}(x)\int_{0}^{\infty}\,\mathrm{e}^{-t}\,\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathrm{e}^{-tx^{\beta}Z(\beta)}}}\right]\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}t\mathrm{e}^{-t}\int_{0}^{\infty}\log^{2}(x)\mathrm{e}^{-\Gamma\,t^{1/\beta}x}\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Gamma}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}t\,t^{-1/\beta}\,\mathrm{e}^{-t}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\log u-\log t^{1/\beta}\,\Gamma}}\right)^{2}\,\mathrm{e}^{-u}\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Gamma}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}t\,t^{-1/\beta}\,\,\mathrm{e}^{-t}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Gamma^{\prime\prime}(1)-2\,t^{1/\beta}\,\Gamma^{\prime}(1)\log\Gamma\,+t^{1/\beta}\,\log^{2}\Gamma}}\right)

\displaystyle=\gamma^{2}+\frac{\pi^{2}}{6}+2\gamma\log\Gamma+\log^{2}\Gamma+\frac{2\gamma}{\beta}\frac{\Gamma^{\prime}}{\Gamma}+\frac{2}{\beta}\frac{\Gamma^{\prime}}{\Gamma}\log\Gamma+\frac{1}{\beta^{2}}\frac{\Gamma^{\prime\prime}}{\Gamma}\,.

Combining this with Equation (
4.6
) gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{Z^{\prime}(\beta)}{Z(\beta)}}}\right)^{2}}}\right]

\displaystyle=\gamma^{2}+\frac{\pi^{2}}{6}+2\gamma\log\Gamma+\log^{2}\Gamma+\frac{2\gamma}{\beta}\,\frac{\Gamma^{\prime}}{\Gamma}+\frac{2}{\beta}\frac{\Gamma^{\prime}}{\Gamma}\log\Gamma+\frac{\beta-1}{\beta^{3}}\frac{\Gamma^{\prime\prime}}{\Gamma}+\frac{1}{\beta^{3}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\Gamma^{\prime}}{\Gamma}}}\right)^{2}\,,

which together with Equation (
4.5
) yields the result.
∎

We can now conclude the section by proving Theorem
1.5
concerning the susceptibility for the REM.

Proof of Theorem
1.5
.

We start from the formula given by Corollary
4.2
.
Differentiating Equation (
4.4
) shows that

\Cov\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\log Z(\beta),\frac{Z^{\prime}(\beta)}{Z(\beta)}}}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}\beta}\Var(\log Z(\beta))=\frac{\pi^{2}}{6}\beta.

Together, with Equation (
4.4
) and Lemma
4.4
, this yields

\displaystyle\kappa(\beta)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{\beta^{2}-1}+\frac{6}{\pi^{2}\beta^{3}(\beta+1)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\Gamma^{\prime\prime}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\beta-1}{\beta}}}\right)}{\Gamma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\beta-1}{\beta}}}\right)}-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\Gamma^{\prime}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\beta-1}{\beta}}}\right)}{\Gamma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\beta-1}{\beta}}}\right)}}}\right)^{2}}}\right)-\frac{\beta^{2}}{(\beta^{2}-1)^{2}}}}\right)\,,

and using the asymptotic expansion of
\Gamma
at
0
and at
1
together with extra computations yield the desired asymptotic equivalents of
\kappa(\beta)
as
\beta\downarrow 1
and
\beta\to\infty
.
∎

4.3
Comparison wih the REM in the general decorated case

In this section, we come back to the general decorated case.
Our aim is to prove the following proposition which compares the temperature susceptibility
\kappa_{\mathrm{d}}(\beta)
with the one of the REM.

Proposition 4.5

(General decorated case)
.

Assume that
\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[S_{\beta}}}\right]<\infty
, for every
\beta>1
.
Then, for any
\beta>1
,

\kappa_{\mathrm{d}}(\beta)=\kappa(\beta)+\frac{3}{\pi^{2}\beta(\beta+1)}\Var_{\beta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{\beta}\log S_{\beta}-\frac{S^{\prime}_{\beta}}{S_{\beta}}}}\right),

where
\Var_{\beta}
denotes the variance under
\mathbb{P}_{\beta}
, which was introduced in Subsection
2.2
, and
S_{\beta}^{\prime}=\sum_{k\geq 0}d_{k}\mathrm{e}^{\beta d_{k}}
.

Proof.

We start from the expression given by Corollary
4.2
.
By Corollary
2.8
, we have

\Var\,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\log Z_{\mathrm{d}}(\beta)}}\right)=\Var\,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\log\Big(\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[S_{\beta}^{1/\beta}}}\right]^{\beta}Z(\beta)\Big)}}\right)=\Var\,(\log Z(\beta))=\frac{\pi^{2}}{6}(\beta^{2}-1)

and, by differentiating with respect to
\beta
,

\Cov\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\log Z_{\mathrm{d}}(\beta),\frac{Z_{\mathrm{d}}^{\prime}(\beta)}{Z_{\mathrm{d}}(\beta)}}}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}\beta}\Var\,(\log Z_{\mathrm{d}}(\beta))=\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}\beta}\Var\,(\log Z(\beta))=\Cov\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\log Z(\beta),\frac{Z^{\prime}(\beta)}{Z(\beta)}}}\right).

Therefore, we get

\kappa_{\mathrm{d}}(\beta)=\kappa(\beta)+\frac{3}{\pi^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\beta^{2}-1}}\right)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Var\,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{Z_{\mathrm{d}}^{\prime}(\beta)}{Z_{\mathrm{d}}(\beta)}}}\right)-\Var\,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{Z^{\prime}(\beta)}{Z(\beta)}}}\right)}}\right).

(4.7)

We shall now focus on
\Var\,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{Z_{\mathrm{d}}^{\prime}(\beta)}{Z_{\mathrm{d}}(\beta)}}}\right)
.
Notice first that

Z_{\mathrm{d}}^{\prime}(\beta)=\sum_{i,k}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\xi_{i}+d_{ik}}}\right)\mathrm{e}^{\beta(\xi_{i}+d_{ik})}.

Therefore, it follows from Lemma
2.7
that

\frac{Z_{\mathrm{d}}^{\prime}(\beta)}{Z_{\mathrm{d}}(\beta)}\;\text{ under }\mathbb{P}\;\overset{({\rm d})}{=}\;\frac{\sum_{i,k}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\xi_{i}+c_{\beta}-\frac{1}{\beta}\log S_{\beta,i}+d_{ik}}}\right)\mathrm{e}^{\beta(\xi_{i}-\frac{1}{\beta}\log S_{\beta,i}+d_{ik})}}{\sum_{i}\mathrm{e}^{\beta\xi_{i}}}\;\text{ under }\mathbb{P}_{\beta}.

Moreover, the quantity on the right-hand side of the last equation equals

c_{\beta}+\frac{\sum_{i}\xi_{i}\mathrm{e}^{\beta\xi_{i}}}{\sum_{i}\mathrm{e}^{\beta\xi_{i}}}+\frac{\sum_{i}\mathrm{e}^{\beta\xi_{i}}(S_{\beta,i}^{\prime}/S_{\beta,i}-\frac{1}{\beta}\log S_{\beta,i})}{\sum_{i}\mathrm{e}^{\beta\xi_{i}}}\,.

Hence, using the law of total variance, we get

\displaystyle\Var\,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{Z_{\mathrm{d}}^{\prime}(\beta)}{Z_{\mathrm{d}}(\beta)}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\Var_{\beta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbb{E}_{\beta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{\sum_{i}\xi_{i}\mathrm{e}^{\beta\xi_{i}}}{\sum_{i}\mathrm{e}^{\beta\xi_{i}}}+\frac{\sum_{i}\mathrm{e}^{\beta\xi_{i}}(S_{\beta,i}^{\prime}/S_{\beta,i}-\frac{1}{\beta}\log S_{\beta,i})}{\sum_{i}\mathrm{e}^{\beta\xi_{i}}}\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}\xi}}\right]}}\right)

\displaystyle\quad{}+\mathbb{E}_{\beta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\Var_{\beta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\sum_{i}\xi_{i}\mathrm{e}^{\beta\xi_{i}}}{\sum_{i}\mathrm{e}^{\beta\xi_{i}}}+\frac{\sum_{i}\mathrm{e}^{\beta\xi_{i}}(S_{\beta,i}^{\prime}/S_{\beta,i}-\frac{1}{\beta}\log S_{\beta,i})}{\sum_{i}\mathrm{e}^{\beta\xi_{i}}}\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}\xi}}\right)}}\right].

Then, using the fact that the
(S_{\beta,i},S_{\beta,i}^{\prime})_{i\geq 0}
are i.i.d. and independent of
\xi
, we get

\displaystyle\Var\,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{Z_{\mathrm{d}}^{\prime}(\beta)}{Z_{\mathrm{d}}(\beta)}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\Var_{\beta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\sum_{i}\xi_{i}\mathrm{e}^{\beta\xi_{i}}}{\sum_{i}\mathrm{e}^{\beta\xi_{i}}}}}\right)+\mathbb{E}_{\beta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{\sum_{i}\mathrm{e}^{2\beta\xi_{i}}}{(\sum_{i}\mathrm{e}^{\beta\xi_{i}})^{2}}}}\right]\Var_{\beta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{S_{\beta}^{\prime}}{S_{\beta}}-\frac{1}{\beta}\log S_{\beta}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\Var\,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{Z^{\prime}(\beta)}{Z(\beta)}}}\right)+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\frac{1}{\beta}}}\right)\Var_{\beta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{S_{\beta}^{\prime}}{S_{\beta}}-\frac{1}{\beta}\log S_{\beta}}}\right).

Coming back to Equation (
4.7
), this concludes the proof.
∎

Example 4.6
.

We investigate here the behavior of the temperature susceptibility for the decoration processes
\mathcal{D}=\delta_{0}+f(X)\delta_{-X}
, where
X
is a positive r.v. with density
p
and
f\colon(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{N}^{*}
a measurable function. We specify these parameters afterwards to obtain different behaviors of the susceptibility as
\beta\downarrow 1
.

By Proposition
4.5
, one has

\kappa_{\mathrm{d}}(\beta)=\kappa(\beta)+\frac{3}{\pi^{2}\beta(\beta+1)}\Var_{\beta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{\beta}\log S_{\beta}-\frac{S^{\prime}_{\beta}}{S_{\beta}}}}\right).

(4.8)

In the cases which follow, our strategy to find the behavior of
\kappa_{\mathrm{d}}(\beta)-\kappa(\beta)
is to estimate precisely

\Var_{\beta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{S^{\prime}_{\beta}}{S_{\beta}}}}\right)=\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[S_{\beta}^{1/\beta}}}\right]}\cdot\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{(S_{\beta}^{\prime})^{2}}{S_{\beta}^{2-1/\beta}}}}\right]-\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[S_{\beta}^{1/\beta}}}\right]^{2}}\cdot\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{S_{\beta}^{\prime}}{S_{\beta}^{1-1/\beta}}}}\right]^{2},

(4.9)

where we used the definition of
\mathbb{P}_{\beta}
, and then to control
\Var_{\beta}(\log S_{\beta})
to show that its contribution to the variance in Equation (
4.8
) is negligible.
Hence, we now aim at estimating the expectations appearing on the right-hand side of Equation (
4.9
). Note that
S_{\beta}=1+f(X)e^{-\beta X}
and
S_{\beta}^{\prime}=-Xf(X)e^{-\beta X}
.
If
\mathbb{E}[X^{2}]<\infty
, we get the following estimates

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[S_{\beta}^{1/\beta}}}\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+f(x)\mathrm{e}^{-\beta x}}}\right)^{1/\beta}p(x)\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}x=\int_{0}^{\infty}f(x)^{1/\beta}\mathrm{e}^{-x}p(x)\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}x+O(1),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{S_{\beta}^{\prime}}{S_{\beta}^{1-1/\beta}}}}\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{-xf(x)\mathrm{e}^{-\beta x}}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+f(x)\mathrm{e}^{-\beta x}}}\right)^{1-1/\beta}}p(x)\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}x=-\int_{0}^{\infty}xf(x)^{1/\beta}\mathrm{e}^{-x}p(x)\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}x+O(1),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{(S_{\beta}^{\prime})^{2}}{S_{\beta}^{2-1/\beta}}}}\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{(xf(x)\mathrm{e}^{-\beta x})^{2}}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+f(x)\mathrm{e}^{-\beta x}}}\right)^{2-1/\beta}}p(x)\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}x=\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{2}f(x)^{1/\beta}\mathrm{e}^{-x}p(x)\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}x+O(1),

(4.10)

using respectively

0\leq(1+a)^{1/\beta}-a^{1/\beta}\leq 1
,

0\leq a^{1/\beta-1}-(1+a)^{1/\beta-1}\leq(1-1/\beta)a^{-1}
and

0\leq a^{1/\beta-2}-(1+a)^{1/\beta-2}\leq(2-1/\beta)a^{-2}

for any
a>0
and
\beta>1
.

First family of examples.
We specify
p(x)=(x+1)^{-4}
and
f(x)=\lceil(x+1)^{b}\mathrm{e}^{x}\rceil
with
b\in\mathbb{R}
.
Then, noting that the ceiling function can be removed up to another
O(1)
term,
one gets, for any
k\geq 0
, as
\beta\downarrow 1
,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{k}f(x)^{1/\beta}\mathrm{e}^{-x}p(x)\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{k}(x+1)^{(b/\beta)-4}\mathrm{e}^{-(\beta-1)x/\beta}\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}x+O(1)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}O(1),&\text{if }k+b<3,\\
\log\dfrac{1}{\beta-1}+O(1),&\text{if }k+b=3,\\
\dfrac{\Gamma(b+k-3)}{(2(\beta-1))^{b+k-3}}(1+o(1)),&\text{if }k+b>3.\end{cases}

Therefore, combining this with Equations (
4.9
) and (
4.10
), we get (also note that
\mathbb{E}[S_{\beta}^{1/\beta}]\geq 1
), as
\beta\downarrow 1
,

\Var_{\beta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{S^{\prime}_{\beta}}{S_{\beta}}}}\right)\,\begin{cases}=O(1),&\text{if }b<1,\\
\asymp\log\dfrac{1}{\beta-1},&\text{if }b=1,\\
\sim\dfrac{\Gamma(b-1)}{(2(\beta-1))^{b-1}},&\text{if }b\in(1,3),\\
\sim\dfrac{1}{4(\beta-1)^{2}\log\frac{1}{\beta-1}},&\text{if }b=3,\\
\sim\dfrac{(b-3)}{4(\beta-1)^{2}},&\text{if }b>3.\end{cases}

We now write
\Var_{\beta}(\log S_{\beta})\leq\mathbb{E}_{\beta}[\log^{2}S_{\beta}]
and hence bound, proceeding initially as in Equation (
4.10
),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[S_{\beta}^{1/\beta}\log^{2}S_{\beta}}}\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\log^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+f(x)\mathrm{e}^{-\beta x}}}\right)f(x)^{1/\beta}\mathrm{e}^{-x}p(x)\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}x+O(1)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}\log^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+(1+x)^{b}}}\right)(x+1)^{(b/\beta)-4}\mathrm{e}^{-(\beta-1)x/\beta}\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}x+O(1)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}O(1),&\text{if }b<3,\\
O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[S_{\beta}^{1/\beta}}}\right]\cdot\log^{2}\dfrac{1}{\beta-1}}}\right),&\text{if }b\geq 3.\end{cases}

It follows that
\Var_{\beta}(\log S_{\beta})
is either a
O(1)
if
b<3
, or a
o(\Var_{\beta}(S_{\beta}^{\prime}/S_{\beta}))
otherwise.
Controlling
\Cov_{\beta}(\log S_{\beta},S_{\beta}^{\prime}/S_{\beta})
by Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and coming back to Equation (
4.8
), we get

\kappa_{\mathrm{d}}(\beta)-\kappa(\beta)\,\begin{cases}=O(1),&\text{if }b<1,\\
\asymp\log\dfrac{1}{\beta-1},&\text{if }b=1,\\
\sim\dfrac{3\Gamma(b-1)}{2^{b}\pi^{2}(\beta-1)^{b-1}},&\text{if }b\in(1,3),\\
\sim\dfrac{3}{8\pi^{2}(\beta-1)^{2}\log\frac{1}{\beta-1}},&\text{if }b=3,\\
\sim\dfrac{3(b-3)}{8\pi^{2}(\beta-1)^{2}},&\text{if }b>3.\end{cases}

Note that, for this first family of examples, the difference
\kappa_{\mathrm{d}}(\beta)-\kappa(\beta)
grows at most like
(\beta-1)^{-2}
, which is the order of magnitude of
\kappa(\beta)
as
\beta\downarrow 1
. More precisely, if
b\leq 3
, then
\kappa_{\mathrm{d}}(\beta)\sim\kappa(\beta)
, and if
b>3
, then both susceptibilities grow at the same speed but with different multiplicative constants.

In the case
b>3
, observe that the expectations in Equation (
4.10
) are all dominated by the event where
X\asymp(\beta-1)^{-1}
, so, on this event,
S_{\beta}^{\prime}/S_{\beta}\asymp(\beta-1)^{-1}
which yields eventually a variance
\Var_{\beta}(S_{\beta}^{\prime}/S_{\beta})
of order
(\beta-1)^{-2}
.
This observation is the idea motivating the next family of examples: in order to construct cases where
\kappa_{\mathrm{d}}(\beta)
grows much faster than
\kappa(\beta)
, we want the expectations in Equation (
4.10
) to be dominated by the event where
X\asymp(\beta-1)^{-\alpha}
for some
\alpha>1
.

Second family of examples.

We now take
p(x)=\mathrm{e}^{-x^{\gamma}}
and
f(x)=\lceil\mathrm{e}^{x+2x^{\gamma}}\rceil
for some
\gamma\in(0,1)
.
First note that, getting rid of the ceiling function and replacing
x
by
(\beta/(2-\beta))^{1/\gamma}x
, we have

\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{k}f(x)^{1/\beta}\mathrm{e}^{-x}p(x)\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}x=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\beta}{2-\beta}}}\right)^{(k+1)/\gamma}\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{k}\mathrm{e}^{x^{\gamma}-\varepsilon x}\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}x+O(1),

(4.11)

with
\varepsilon\coloneqq(2-\beta)^{-1/\gamma}\beta^{(1-\gamma)/\gamma}(\beta-1)
.
One can check that this last integral is dominated, as
\varepsilon\to 0
, by the part
x\sim(\gamma/\varepsilon)^{1/(1-\gamma)}
, which means that the expectations in Equation (
4.10
) are dominated by the event where
X\sim(\gamma/(\beta-1))^{1/(1-\gamma)}
. This implies that the two terms on the right-hand side of Equation (
4.9
) are of order
(\beta-1)^{-2/(1-\gamma)}
, however the first order terms cancel out and
\Var_{\beta}(S_{\beta}^{\prime}/S_{\beta})
is of a smaller order.
Hence, we have to push the calculation to the second order term: we prove in Appendix
B
that, for
k\in\{0,1,2\}
, as
\varepsilon\to 0
,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{k}\mathrm{e}^{x^{\gamma}-\varepsilon x}\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{2\pi}{1-\gamma}}}\right)^{1/2}\gamma^{\frac{1}{2(1-\gamma)}}\varepsilon^{-\frac{2-\gamma}{2(1-\gamma)}}\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left((1-\gamma)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\gamma}{\varepsilon}}}\right)^{\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}}}}\right)

\displaystyle\quad{}\lx@nobreakspace\times\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\gamma}{\varepsilon}}}\right)^{\frac{k}{1-\gamma}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+c_{k}\varepsilon^{\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}}+O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\varepsilon^{\frac{3\gamma}{2(1-\gamma)}}}}\right)}}\right),

(4.12)

where the constants
c_{0},c_{1},c_{2}
explicitly depend on
\gamma
and satisfy
c_{2}+c_{0}-2c_{1}=\frac{1}{1-\gamma}\gamma^{-\frac{1}{1-\gamma}}
.
Coming back to Equation (
4.9
), we get

\displaystyle\Var_{\beta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{S^{\prime}_{\beta}}{S_{\beta}}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\beta}{2-\beta}}}\right)^{2/\gamma}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\gamma}{\varepsilon}}}\right)^{\frac{2}{1-\gamma}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left((c_{2}+c_{0}-2c_{1})\varepsilon^{\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}}+O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\varepsilon^{\frac{3\gamma}{2(1-\gamma)}}}}\right)}}\right)\sim\frac{\gamma^{\frac{1}{1-\gamma}}}{(1-\gamma)}(\beta-1)^{-\frac{2-\gamma}{1-\gamma}},

using the definition of
\varepsilon
.
We now aim at bounding

\Var_{\beta}(\log S_{\beta})=\frac{\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[S_{\beta}^{1/\beta}\log^{2}S_{\beta}}}\right]}{\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[S_{\beta}^{1/\beta}}}\right]}-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[S_{\beta}^{1/\beta}\log S_{\beta}}}\right]}{\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[S_{\beta}^{1/\beta}}}\right]}}}\right)^{2}.

(4.13)

For
k\in\{0,1,2\}
, proceeding in the first equality as in Equations (
4.10
) and (
4.11
), we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[S_{\beta}^{1/\beta}\log^{k}S_{\beta}}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\beta}{2-\beta}}}\right)^{1/\gamma}\int_{0}^{\infty}\log^{k}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+\mathrm{e}^{\frac{2\beta}{2-\beta}x^{\gamma}-\varepsilon\beta x}}}\right)\mathrm{e}^{x^{\gamma}-\varepsilon x}\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}x+O(1)

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\beta}{2-\beta}}}\right)^{1/\gamma}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{2\pi}{1-\gamma}}}\right)^{1/2}\gamma^{\frac{1}{2(1-\gamma)}}\varepsilon^{-\frac{2-\gamma}{2(1-\gamma)}}\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left((1-\gamma)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\gamma}{\varepsilon}}}\right)^{\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}}}}\right)

\displaystyle\quad{}\lx@nobreakspace\times\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{2\beta}{2-\beta}-\beta\gamma}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\gamma}{\varepsilon}}}\right)^{\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}}}}\right)^{k}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\varepsilon^{\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}}}}\right)}}\right),

(4.14)

where the last equality is proved in Appendix
B
.
Plugging this in (
4.13
), we get
\Var_{\beta}(\log S_{\beta})=O(\varepsilon^{-2\gamma/(1-\gamma)}\cdot\varepsilon^{\gamma/(1-\gamma)})=o(\Var_{\beta}(S^{\prime}_{\beta}/S_{\beta}))
because
\gamma<2-\gamma
.
Controlling
\Cov_{\beta}(\log S_{\beta},S^{\prime}_{\beta}/S_{\beta})
by Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and coming back to (
4.8
), we get

\kappa_{\mathrm{d}}(\beta)-\kappa(\beta)\sim\frac{3\gamma^{\frac{1}{1-\gamma}}}{2\pi^{2}(1-\gamma)}(\beta-1)^{-\frac{2-\gamma}{1-\gamma}}.

Note that the exponent
\frac{2-\gamma}{1-\gamma}
is always larger than 2, so that
\kappa_{\mathrm{d}}(\beta)
grows faster than
\kappa(\beta)
, and this exponent can be made arbitrarily large by taking
\gamma
close to 1.

4.4
The BBM case

In this section, our goal is to prove Theorem
1.7
which concerns the BBM.

Lemma 4.7

(BBM case)
.

For the decoration arising in the BBM and the constant
C_{\star}
appearing in Equation (
2.8
), we have, as
\beta\downarrow 1
,

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[S_{\beta}^{\prime}}}\right]\sim-\frac{C_{\star}}{(\beta-1)^{2}}\qquad\textrm{and}\qquad\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[S_{\beta}^{\prime\prime}}}\right]\sim\frac{2C_{\star}}{(\beta-1)^{3}}\,.

Proof.

This follows from Equation (
2.8
) in a similar way as the proof of Proposition
2.11
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.7
.

We first prove that
\kappa_{\mathrm{d}}(\beta)>\kappa(\beta)
, when
\beta>1
is fixed.
By Proposition
4.5
, it is sufficient to show that

\Var_{\beta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{\beta}\log S_{\beta}-\frac{S^{\prime}_{\beta}}{S_{\beta}}}}\right)>0.

(4.15)

We proceed by contradiction and assume that this variance equals zero.
Then, there exists
c\in\mathbb{R}
such that,
\mathbb{P}_{\beta}
-a.s.,

\frac{1}{\beta}\log S_{\beta}=\frac{S_{\beta}^{\prime}}{S_{\beta}}+c,

(4.16)

But
\mathbb{P}
is absolutely continuous w.r.t.
\mathbb{P}_{\beta}
such that Equation (
4.16
) holds also
\mathbb{P}
-a.s.
On the one hand, using
S_{\beta}=\sum_{k\geq 0}\mathrm{e}^{\beta d_{k}}
, we have

S_{\beta}\log S_{\beta}>\sum_{k\geq 0}\mathrm{e}^{\beta d_{k}}\beta d_{k}=\beta S_{\beta}^{\prime}.

Thus, we necessarily have
c>0
.
On the other hand, fix some
\beta^{\prime}\in(1,\beta)
.
Then, there exists
C>0
such that

\forall x\leq 0,\qquad\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1\vee\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert x}}\right\rvert}}\right)\,\mathrm{e}^{\beta x}\leq C\,\mathrm{e}^{\beta^{\prime}x}.

(4.17)

Now, for some
\varepsilon>0
, consider the event

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\sum_{k\geq 1}\mathrm{e}^{\beta^{\prime}d_{k}}\leq\varepsilon}}\right\}.

This event has positive
\mathbb{P}
-probability by
[
14
, Proposition 3.4]
.
So, on this event intersected with the one where Equation (
4.16
) holds (this intersection being non-empty), we have

c=\frac{1}{\beta}\log S_{\beta}-\frac{S^{\prime}_{\beta}}{S_{\beta}}\leq\frac{1}{\beta}\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+\sum_{k\geq 1}\mathrm{e}^{\beta d_{k}}}}\right)+\sum_{k\geq 1}d_{k}\mathrm{e}^{\beta d_{k}}\leq\frac{1}{\beta}\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+C\varepsilon}}\right)+C\varepsilon,

using Equation (
4.17
).
Letting
\varepsilon\to 0
shows
c\leq 0
, which contradicts
c>0
and concludes our proof of Equation (
4.15
).

We study now the regime
\beta\downarrow 1
.
Since the asymptotics of
\kappa(\beta)
are given by Theorem
1.5
, it remains to study the behavior of

\Var_{\beta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{\beta}\log S_{\beta}-\frac{S^{\prime}_{\beta}}{S_{\beta}}}}\right)=\frac{1}{\beta^{2}}\Var_{\beta}\,(\log S_{\beta})-\frac{2}{\beta}\Cov_{\beta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\log S_{\beta},\frac{S^{\prime}_{\beta}}{S_{\beta}}}}\right)+\Var_{\beta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{S^{\prime}_{\beta}}{S_{\beta}}}}\right).

(4.18)

As we will see, the main term is the third one, which is of order
(\beta-1)^{-2}
, while the other terms are negligible.

For the first term on the right-hand side of Equation (
4.18
), observe that

\Var_{\beta}\,(\log S_{\beta})\leq\mathbb{E}_{\beta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\log^{2}S_{\beta}}}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}_{\beta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\log^{2}(\mathrm{e}+S_{\beta})}}\right]\leq\log^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathrm{e}+\mathbb{E}_{\beta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[S_{\beta}}}\right]}}\right),

by Jensen’s inequality.
Then, by definition of
\mathbb{E}_{\beta}
, we get

\mathbb{E}_{\beta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[S_{\beta}}}\right]=\frac{\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[S_{\beta}^{1+1/\beta}}}\right]}{\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[S_{\beta}^{1/\beta}}}\right]}\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[S_{\beta}^{2}}}\right]}{\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[S_{\beta}^{1/\beta}}}\right]}=O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{(\beta-1)^{2}}}}\right),

as
\beta\downarrow 1
by Equation (
2.13
) and Corollary
2.13
.
This proves

\Var_{\beta}\,(\log S_{\beta})=O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\log^{2}\frac{1}{\beta-1}}}\right).

(4.19)

We now consider the third term on the right-hand side of Equation (
4.18
).
Using the definition of
\mathbb{E}_{\beta}
and then that
S_{\beta}\geq 1
, we get

\mathbb{E}_{\beta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{S^{\prime}_{\beta}}{S_{\beta}}}}\right]=\frac{\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[S_{\beta}^{1/\beta-1}S^{\prime}_{\beta}}}\right]}{\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[S_{\beta}^{1/\beta}}}\right]}\sim-\frac{1}{\beta-1},

(4.20)

as
\beta\downarrow 1
by Corollary
2.13
and the fact that
\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[S_{\beta}^{1/\beta-1}(-S^{\prime}_{\beta})}}\right]\sim C_{\star}(\beta-1)^{-2}
as a consequence of the first part of Equation (
2.14
) (with
X=S_{\beta}
and
Y=-S_{\beta}^{\prime}
) together with Lemma
4.7
.
On the other hand, it follows from Cauchy–Schwarz inequality that

\frac{S_{\beta}^{\prime 2}}{S_{\beta}}=S_{\beta}\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{k\geq 0}d_{k}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\beta d_{k}}}{S_{\beta}}}}\right)^{2}\leq S_{\beta}\cdot\sum_{k\geq 0}d_{k}^{2}\,\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\beta d_{k}}}{S_{\beta}}=S_{\beta}^{\prime\prime}\,,

and therefore

\mathbb{E}_{\beta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{S^{\prime}_{\beta}}{S_{\beta}}}}\right)^{2}}}\right]=\frac{\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[S_{\beta}^{1/\beta-2}S^{\prime 2}_{\beta}}}\right]}{\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[S_{\beta}^{1/\beta}}}\right]}\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[S^{\prime 2}_{\beta}/S_{\beta}}}\right]}{\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[S_{\beta}^{1/\beta}}}\right]}\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[S_{\beta}^{\prime\prime}}}\right]}{\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[S_{\beta}^{1/\beta}}}\right]}\sim\frac{2}{(\beta-1)^{2}},

(4.21)

using Lemma
4.7
and Corollary
2.13
.
For the lower bound, using the first part of Equation (
2.14
) with
X=S_{\beta}
and
Y=(S_{\beta}^{\prime})^{2}/S_{\beta}
, we get

\mathbb{E}_{\beta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{S^{\prime}_{\beta}}{S_{\beta}}}}\right)^{2}}}\right]=\frac{\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[S_{\beta}^{1/\beta-2}S^{\prime 2}_{\beta}}}\right]}{\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[S_{\beta}^{1/\beta}}}\right]}\geq\frac{\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[S^{\prime 2}_{\beta}/S_{\beta}}}\right]^{2-1/\beta}}{\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[S_{\beta}^{1/\beta}}}\right]\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[S_{\beta}}}\right]^{1-1/\beta}}.

Hence, applying Lemma
4.8
, Proposition
2.11
and Corollary
2.13
, we get

\liminf_{\beta\downarrow 1}\,(\beta-1)^{2}\,\mathbb{E}_{\beta}\,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{S^{\prime}_{\beta}}{S_{\beta}}}}\right)^{2}}}\right]>1.

(4.22)

Combining Equations (
4.20
), (
4.21
) and (
4.22
) yields

0<\liminf_{\beta\downarrow 1}(\beta-1)^{2}\Var_{\beta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{S^{\prime}_{\beta}}{S_{\beta}}}}\right)\leq\limsup_{\beta\downarrow 1}(\beta-1)^{2}\Var_{\beta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{S^{\prime}_{\beta}}{S_{\beta}}}}\right)\leq 1.

(4.23)

Finally, for the second term on the right-hand side of Equation (
4.18
), by Cauchy–Schwarz inequality and then Equations (
4.19
) and (
4.23
), we get

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\Cov_{\beta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\log S_{\beta},\frac{S^{\prime}_{\beta}}{S_{\beta}}}}\right)}}\right\rvert\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Var_{\beta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\log S_{\beta}}}\right)\Var_{\beta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{S^{\prime}_{\beta}}{S_{\beta}}}}\right)}}\right)^{1/2}=O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\log\,\frac{1}{\beta-1}}{\beta-1}}}\right),

which proves that this term is negligible in Equation (
4.18
) and concludes the proof.
∎

Lemma 4.8

(BBM case)
.

We have

\liminf_{\beta\downarrow 1}\,(\beta-1)^{3}\,\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{(S^{\prime}_{\beta})^{2}}{S_{\beta}}}}\right]>C_{\star}.

Remark 4.9
.

A lower bound with a weak inequality could be easily obtained via Cauchy–Schwarz inequality:

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{(S^{\prime}_{\beta})^{2}}{S_{\beta}}}}\right]\geq\frac{\mathbb{E}[S_{\beta}^{\prime}]^{2}}{\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[S_{\beta}}}\right]}\sim\frac{C_{\star}}{(\beta-1)^{3}},

using Lemma
4.7
and Proposition
2.11
. Equality at the first order in this inequality would suggest that
S^{\prime}_{\beta}
and
S_{\beta}
are colinear at first order (on the events that are significant for the first moment).
Therefore, the idea of the proof below is to find an event such that the first moments of
S^{\prime}_{\beta}
and
S_{\beta}
given this event have a different ratio than the one for the non-conditional first moments.

Proof.

Step 1: Working at finite
t
.
Fix some
\beta>1
.
Setting
f_{\beta}(x)\coloneqq\mathrm{e}^{\beta\sqrt{2}x}
and
\partial_{\beta}f_{\beta}(x)\coloneqq\sqrt{2}x\mathrm{e}^{\beta\sqrt{2}x}
, note that

S_{\beta}=\mathcal{C}(f_{\beta})
and
S_{\beta}^{\prime}=\mathcal{C}(\partial_{\beta}f_{\beta})
.
Our first aim in this step is to show

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{(S^{\prime}_{\beta})^{2}}{S_{\beta}}}}\right]=\lim_{t\to\infty}\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{t}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{(\mathcal{C}_{t,r_{t}}^{*}(\partial_{\beta}f_{\beta}))^{2}}{\mathcal{C}_{t,r_{t}}^{*}(f_{\beta})}}}\right].

(4.24)

For
K>0
, let
\chi_{K}
denote a continuous function such that
\mathbbm{1}_{[-K,K]}\leq\chi_{K}\leq\mathbbm{1}_{[-(K+1),K+1]}
.
It follows from the vague convergence stated in Equation (
2.7
) that

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{C}_{t,r_{t}}^{*}(\chi_{K}f_{\beta}),\mathcal{C}_{t,r_{t}}^{*}(\chi_{K}\partial_{\beta}f_{\beta})}}\right)\text{ under }\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}_{t}\quad\xrightarrow[t\to\infty]{\text{(d)}}\quad\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{C}(\chi_{K}f_{\beta}),\mathcal{C}(\chi_{K}\partial_{\beta}f_{\beta})}}\right)\,\text{ under }\mathbb{P}.

Moreover, recalling
\mathcal{C}_{t,r_{t}}^{*}
is supported on
(-\infty,0]
under
\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}_{t}
we get

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\frac{\mathcal{C}_{t,r_{t}}^{*}(\chi_{K}\partial_{\beta}f_{\beta})^{2}}{\mathcal{C}_{t,r_{t}}^{*}(\chi_{K}f_{\beta})}}}\right\rvert\leq 2(K+1)^{2}\mathcal{C}_{t,r_{t}}^{*}(\chi_{K}f_{\beta})\leq 2(K+1)^{2}\mathcal{C}_{t,r_{t}}^{*}([-(K+1),0])\,,

which is bounded in
L^{2}
by Lemma
2.16
. Hence,
(\mathcal{C}_{t,r_{t}}^{*}(\chi_{K}\partial_{\beta}f_{\beta})^{2}/\mathcal{C}_{t,r_{t}}^{*}(\chi_{K}f_{\beta}))_{t}
is uniformly integrable and we get

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{\mathcal{C}(\chi_{K}\partial_{\beta}f_{\beta})^{2}}{\mathcal{C}(\chi_{K}f_{\beta})}}}\right]=\lim_{t\to\infty}\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{t}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{\mathcal{C}_{t,r_{t}}^{*}(\chi_{K}\partial_{\beta}f_{\beta})^{2}}{\mathcal{C}_{t,r_{t}}^{*}(\chi_{K}f_{\beta})}}}\right].

(4.25)

We now control the differences between the expectations in Equation (
4.24
) and Equation (
4.25
) and show they are small when
K
is large.
We have

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\frac{\mathcal{C}(\partial_{\beta}f_{\beta})^{2}}{\mathcal{C}(f_{\beta})}-\frac{\mathcal{C}(\chi_{K}\partial_{\beta}f_{\beta})^{2}}{\mathcal{C}(\chi_{K}f_{\beta})}}}\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\mathcal{C}(\partial_{\beta}f_{\beta})^{2}-\mathcal{C}(\chi_{K}\partial_{\beta}f_{\beta})^{2}}}\right\rvert}{\mathcal{C}(f_{\beta})}+\mathcal{C}(\chi_{K}\partial_{\beta}f_{\beta})^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\frac{1}{\mathcal{C}(f_{\beta})}-\frac{1}{\mathcal{C}(\chi_{K}f_{\beta})}}}\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq 2\,\mathcal{C}(\partial_{\beta}f_{\beta})\,\mathcal{C}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbbm{1}_{(-\infty,-K]}\,\partial_{\beta}f_{\beta}}}\right)+(K+1)^{2}\mathcal{C}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbbm{1}_{(-\infty,-K]}\,\partial_{\beta}f_{\beta}}}\right),

(4.26)

where, for the first term, we used
\mathcal{C}(f_{\beta})\geq 1
and the fact that
\partial_{\beta}f_{\beta}
is of constant sign on
(-\infty,0]
which is the support of
\mathcal{C}
and, for the second term, we used
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\mathcal{C}(\chi_{K}\partial_{\beta}f_{\beta})}}\right\rvert/\mathcal{C}(f_{\beta})\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\mathcal{C}(\chi_{K}\partial_{\beta}f_{\beta})}}\right\rvert/\mathcal{C}(\chi_{K}f_{\beta})\leq K+1
.
Then, writing
\partial_{\beta}f_{\beta}(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{x}\sqrt{2}(\beta\sqrt{2}y+1)\mathrm{e}^{\beta\sqrt{2}y}\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}y
and using Fubini’s theorem, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathcal{C}(\partial_{\beta}f_{\beta})\mathcal{C}(\mathbbm{1}_{(-\infty,-K]}\partial_{\beta}f_{\beta})}}\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{0}\int_{-\infty}^{-K}\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathcal{C}([y,0])\,\mathcal{C}([z,-K])}}\right]\,2(\beta\sqrt{2}z+1)\,\mathrm{e}^{\beta\sqrt{2}z}(\beta\sqrt{2}y+1)\mathrm{e}^{\beta\sqrt{2}y}\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}z\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\leq C(\beta)(K+1)^{3/2}\mathrm{e}^{-(\beta-1)\sqrt{2}K},

where we bounded the last expectation by
(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert y}}\right\rvert+1)^{1/2}(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert z}}\right\rvert+1)^{1/2}\mathrm{e}^{-\sqrt{2}(y+z)}
using Cauchy–Schwarz inequality and Equation (
2.9
), and where
C(\beta)
denotes a constant depending only on
\beta
and which can change from line to line.
Similarly, we have
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{C}(\mathbbm{1}_{(-\infty,-K]}\partial_{\beta}f_{\beta})]\leq C(\beta)(K+1)\mathrm{e}^{-(\beta-1)\sqrt{2}K}
by using Equation (
2.10
).
Coming back to Equation (
4.26
), we get

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{\mathcal{C}(\partial_{\beta}f_{\beta})^{2}}{\mathcal{C}(f_{\beta})}}}\right]-\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{\mathcal{C}(\chi_{K}\partial_{\beta}f_{\beta})^{2}}{\mathcal{C}(\chi_{K}f_{\beta})}}}\right]}}\right\rvert\leq C(\beta)(K+1)^{3}\mathrm{e}^{-(\beta-1)\sqrt{2}K},

and the same holds true for
\mathcal{C}_{t,r_{t}}^{*}
instead of
\mathcal{C}
, uniformly in
t\geq 1
, by replacing Equations (
2.9
) and (
2.10
) by Lemma
2.16
in the proof.
Therefore, letting
K\to\infty
in Equation (
4.25
) yields Equation (
4.24
).

Step 2: Using Cauchy–Schwarz inequality conditionally on a well-chosen event.
We fix some parameters
a>0
and
0<b<B
.
For any
\beta>1
, letting
r=r(\beta)\coloneqq a(\beta-1)^{-2}
, and for any

, we introduce the event

B_{r,t}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{h_{t-r}(X_{t-r})-m_{t}+m_{r}\in[-B\sqrt{r},-b\sqrt{r}]}}\right\}.

(4.27)

Then, we use Cauchy–Schwarz inequality given
B_{r,t}
or given
B_{r,t}^{c}
to get

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{t}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{(\mathcal{C}_{t,r_{t}}^{*}(\partial_{\beta}f_{\beta}))^{2}}{\mathcal{C}_{t,r_{t}}^{*}(f_{\beta})}}}\right]

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{t}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{(\mathcal{C}_{t,r_{t}}^{*}(\partial_{\beta}f_{\beta}))^{2}}{\mathcal{C}_{t,r_{t}}^{*}(f_{\beta})}\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}B_{r,t}}}\right]\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}_{t}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(B_{r,t}}}\right)+\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{t}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{(\mathcal{C}_{t,r_{t}}^{*}(\partial_{\beta}f_{\beta}))^{2}}{\mathcal{C}_{t,r_{t}}^{*}(f_{\beta})}\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}B_{r,t}^{c}}}\right]\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}_{t}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(B_{r,t}^{c}}}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{t}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathcal{C}_{t,r_{t}}^{*}(\partial_{\beta}f_{\beta})\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}B_{r,t}}}\right]^{2}}{\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{t}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathcal{C}_{t,r_{t}}^{*}(f_{\beta})\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}B_{r,t}}}\right]}\,\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}_{t}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(B_{r,t}}}\right)+\frac{\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{t}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathcal{C}_{t,r_{t}}^{*}(\partial_{\beta}f_{\beta})\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}B_{r,t}^{c}}}\right]^{2}}{\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{t}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathcal{C}_{t,r_{t}}^{*}(f_{\beta})\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}B_{r,t}^{c}}}\right]}\,\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}_{t}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(B_{r,t}^{c}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{t}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathcal{C}_{t,r_{t}}^{*}(\partial_{\beta}f_{\beta})\mathbbm{1}_{B_{r,t}}}}\right]^{2}}{\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{t}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathcal{C}_{t,r_{t}}^{*}(f_{\beta})\mathbbm{1}_{B_{r,t}}}}\right]}+\frac{\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{t}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathcal{C}_{t,r_{t}}^{*}(\partial_{\beta}f_{\beta})\mathbbm{1}_{B_{r,t}^{c}}}}\right]^{2}}{\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{t}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathcal{C}_{t,r_{t}}^{*}(f_{\beta})\mathbbm{1}_{B_{r,t}^{c}}}}\right]},

which yields a lower bound for the right-hand side of Equation (

), that we now have to estimate.

Step 3: Estimating the expectations.
Let
\varepsilon>0

\beta>1
close enough to 1, there exists

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert(\beta-1)\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{t}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathcal{C}_{t,r_{t}}^{*}(f_{\beta})\mathbbm{1}_{B_{r,t}}}}\right]-C_{\star}\kappa}}\right\rvert\leq\varepsilon,

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert(\beta-1)^{2}\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{t}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathcal{C}_{t,r_{t}}^{*}(\partial_{\beta}f_{\beta})\mathbbm{1}_{B_{r,t}}}}\right]+C_{\star}\kappa^{\prime}}}\right\rvert\leq\varepsilon,

where

\displaystyle\kappa

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\varphi_{b,B}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{a\vee w}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert a-w}}\right\rvert}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}u\mathrm{e}^{-u-u^{2}/(4w)}\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}u}}\right)\frac{\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}w}{w^{3/2}}\,,

4.10
for some

introduced earlier to estimate the part

Therefore, for

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{t}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathcal{C}_{t,r_{t}}^{*}(f_{\beta})\mathbbm{1}_{B_{r,t}}}}\right]-C_{\star}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}\varphi_{b,B}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{r\vee s}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert r-s}}\right\rvert}}}\right)\frac{x\mathrm{e}^{-x^{2}/(2s)}}{\sqrt{2\pi}s^{3/2}}\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}s}}\right)\beta\sqrt{2}\,\mathrm{e}^{-(\beta-1)\sqrt{2}x}\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}x}}\right\rvert

This proves the first inequality in Equation (

\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{t}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathcal{C}_{t,r_{t}}^{*}(f_{\beta})\mathbbm{1}_{B_{r,t}}}}\right]=-\int_{0}^{\infty}\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{t}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathcal{C}_{t,r_{t}}^{*}([-x,0])\mathbbm{1}_{B_{r,t}}}}\right]\sqrt{2}(\beta\sqrt{2}x-1)\mathrm{e}^{-\beta\sqrt{2}x}\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}x

).

Step 4: Conclusion.

We first claim that for
\theta\in(0,1)
and
\epsilon>0
, there exists
r_{0}>0

Lemma A.2

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C}{t}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(s+1)\sqrt{s}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left((s+1)\wedge(\beta-1)^{-2}}}\right)^{\gamma}\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}s

Appendix B
Asymptotic expansion of an integral

Proof of Equation (
4.12
).

Write
h_{\varepsilon}(x)=x^{\gamma}-\varepsilon x
for any
x\geq 0
.
Recall we aim at estimating

I_{k}\coloneqq\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{k}\mathrm{e}^{h_{\varepsilon}(x)}\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}x,

for
k\in\{0,1,2\}
.
The function
h_{\varepsilon}
is maximized at
x_{0}\coloneqq(\gamma/\varepsilon)^{1/(1-\gamma)}
, and we can rewrite, for
x\geq-x_{0}
,

h_{\varepsilon}(x_{0}+x)-h_{\varepsilon}(x_{0})=-x_{0}^{\gamma}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+\gamma\frac{x}{x_{0}}-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+\frac{x}{x_{0}}}}\right)^{\gamma}}}\right).

Let
\delta\in(0,1)
.
Setting
f\colon t\in[-1,\infty)\mapsto 1+\gamma t-(1+t)^{\gamma}
, there exists
c>0
depending only on
\gamma
such that
f(\pm\delta)\geq c\delta^{2}
. Together with convexity of
f
, this proves that
f(t)\geq c\delta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert t}}\right\rvert
for any
t\in[-1,\infty)\setminus(-\delta,\delta)
.
From this inequality, choosing
\delta=x_{0}^{-\theta}
for some
\theta\in(0,\gamma/2)
so that
\delta\to 0
and
\delta^{2}x_{0}^{\gamma}\to\infty
as
\varepsilon\to 0
, we deduce that, up to a modification of the constant
c
,

\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{k}\mathrm{e}^{h_{\varepsilon}(x)}\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}x=\mathrm{e}^{h_{\varepsilon}(x_{0})}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\int_{-\delta x_{0}}^{\delta x_{0}}(x_{0}+x)^{k}\mathrm{e}^{h_{\varepsilon}(x_{0}+x)-h_{\varepsilon}(x_{0})}\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}x+O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathrm{e}^{-c\delta^{2}x_{0}^{\gamma}}}}\right)}}\right).

(B.1)

Uniformly in
x\in[-\delta x_{0},\delta x_{0}]
, we can expand

h_{\varepsilon}(x_{0}+x)-h_{\varepsilon}(x_{0})=-\frac{x^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}+a_{3}\frac{x^{3}}{x_{0}^{3-\gamma}}-a_{4}\frac{x^{4}}{x_{0}^{4-\gamma}}+O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{x^{5}}{x_{0}^{5-\gamma}}}}\right),

(B.2)

where

\gamma

\theta>\gamma/3

uniformly in

to get that (

\displaystyle I_{1}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma x_{0}\mathrm{e}^{h_{\varepsilon}(x_{0})}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+\frac{3(a_{3}-a_{4})}{\gamma(\gamma-1)}\frac{\sigma^{2}}{x_{0}^{2}}+O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\sigma^{3}}{x_{0}^{3}}}}\right)}}\right),

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma x_{0}^{2}\mathrm{e}^{h_{\varepsilon}(x_{0})}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{3(2a_{3}-a_{4})}{\gamma(\gamma-1)}+1}}\right)\frac{\sigma^{2}}{x_{0}^{2}}+O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\sigma^{3}}{x_{0}^{3}}}}\right)}}\right),

4.12
).
∎
