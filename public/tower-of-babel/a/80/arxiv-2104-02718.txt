Abstract




\proofmodefalse

The attractor equations for an arbitrary one-parameter family of Calabi-Yau manifolds are studied in the large complex structure region. These equations are solved iteratively, generating what we term an
N
-expansion, which is a power series in the Gromov-Witten invariants of the manifold. The coefficients of this series are associated with integer partitions. In important cases we are able to find closed-form expressions for the general term of this expansion. To our knowledge, these are the first generic solutions to attractor equations that incorporate instanton contributions. In particular, we find a simple closed-form formula for the entropy associated to rank two attractor points, including those recently discovered. The applications of our solutions are briefly discussed. Most importantly, we are able to give an expression for the Wald entropy of black holes that includes all genus 0 instanton corrections.

Introduction

1.1
Preamble

Ever since the discovery of the attractor mechanism
[
1
]
, the study of supersymmetric black holes in
\mathcal{N}=2
theories in four dimensions has been intimately linked to the study of attractor points on Calabi-Yau manifolds. This has led to many interesting conjectures and results, such as the intricate connections to number theory, discussed by Moore in
[
2
,
3
]
and the BPS state existence conjecture
[
4
]
, which links the existence of certain type of attractor points, in the
d=4,\,\mathcal{N}=2
supergravity theories, to the existence of BPS states in the quantum theory of gravity.

While the attractor mechanism applies to any four-dimensional
\mathcal{N}=2
supergravity theory coupled to vector multiplets, an especially interesting class of examples is provided by compactifying 10-dimensional type IIA and IIB supergravities on Calabi-Yau threefolds (see
[
5
,
6
]
and references cited therein). In this case, special geometry is used to describe the complex structure or complexified Kähler moduli space. The scalars in
\mathcal{N}=2
vector multiplets can be interpreted as coordinates on the moduli space, and the attractor mechanism can be viewed as specifying, on the black hole horizon, a particular Calabi-Yau manifold in the moduli space as a function of the charges.

In this paper, we study attractor equations in
d=4,\,\mathcal{N}=2
supergravity theories that have been obtained by compactifying a type IIA or IIB supergravity on a one-parameter family of Calabi-Yau manifolds. We concentrate on the region of the moduli space that is near the large complex structure point. In this region, it is possible to solve the instanton-corrected attractor equations using perturbative methods.

What is even more surprising than the existence of this kind of solution is the fact that for some important special cases, we have been able to find a closed-form for the instanton corrections. With the help of these solutions, we are able to give interesting formulae for locations of attractor points, among other physical quantities of interest. For example, we are able to give a formula for the Wald entropy of a black hole that, in the large charge limit, is fully quantum- and instanton-corrected.

Our aim in this paper is to develop what we term
N
-expansions
, by which we mean expansions in terms of the Gromov-Witten invariants
N^{\mbox{\tiny GW}}_{k}
. These include all perturbative and non-perturbative terms. The prototypical quantity that exhibits such an
N
-expansion is the prepotential, which is a key ingredient in formulating the attractor equations. Recall that the prepotential is introduced by first choosing a homology basis
A^{a},B_{b}\in H_{3}(X,\mathbbl{Z})

\alpha_{a},\beta^{b}\in H^{3}(X,\mathbbl{Z})

\displaystyle\Omega\;=\;z^{a}\alpha_{a}-\mathcal{F}_{b}\beta^{b}~,\qquad a,b\;=\;0,\dots,h^{2,1}(X)~,

where

It is believed that near the large complex structure point there is a choice of a symplectic basis such that, in the one-parameter case, the prepotential

The large complex structure region

We use the term
large complex structure region
to refer to a region in the Calabi-Yau moduli space where our solutions, such as (
1.6
), converge. We will later in appendix
F
argue that such a region does exist and contains an open neighbourhood of the point of maximal unipotent monodromy which we will refer to simply as the
large complex structure point
.

There are also at least two other closely related, but ultimately different, regions in the moduli space that could reasonably be called large complex structure regions. One is the region where the instanton sum
\mathcal{I}(t)
converges. This is determined by the asymptotic growth of the Gromov-Witten invariants (see for example
[
10
,
26
,
27
,
28
]

\displaystyle\log N^{\text{\tiny{GW}}}_{k}\;\sim\;2\pi y_{\text{sing}}k~,

where
y_{\text{sing}}
is the coordinate of the singularity nearest to the large complex structure point. Thus the instanton sum
\mathcal{I}(t)
converges if and only if
\text{Im}\,t>y_{\text{sing}}
.

It is also possible to study this region using the mirror symmetry relation to the complex structure moduli space on the mirror manifold. On the complex structure moduli space, the coordinates can be chosen so that the point of maximal unipotent monodromy is located at
\varphi=0
. The period vector is most conveniently expressed in terms of a basis of solutions
\widehat{\varpi}_{i}
to a Picard-Fuchs equation around
\varphi=0

\widehat{\varpi}
, has asymptotics

\displaystyle\widehat{\varpi}\;\sim\;\left(\begin{matrix}1\\
t\\

t^{3}\end{matrix}\right)~.

After identifying
(z_{0},z_{1}){\;=\;}(\widehat{\varpi}_{0},\widehat{\varpi}_{1})

\displaystyle t\;=\;\frac{\widehat{\varpi}_{1}(\varphi)}{\widehat{\varpi}_{0}(\varphi)}\;=\;\frac{1}{2\pi\text{i}}\log\varphi+\mathcal{O}(\varphi)~,

(1.11)

with the last equality following from the specifics of the Picard-Fuchs equation
[

\displaystyle\Pi\;=\;-\left(\begin{matrix}\frac{1}{3}Y_{000}&\frac{1}{2}Y_{100}&0&-\frac{1}{6}Y_{111}\\[2.0pt]

The attractor mechanism

In
[
30
,
1
]
, the authors studied a four-dimensional
\mathcal{N}{\;=\;}2
supergravity coupled to
\mathcal{N}{\;=\;}2
vector multiplets, which can be conveniently formulated in terms of special geometry
[
31
]
. For special geometry itself, see
[
32
,
7
]
, and for a short summary in the style of the present paper, see
[
15
]
. For a pedagogical introduction to the attractor mechanism, see for example
[
33
,
34
,
35
]
. It was noticed that, for the supersymmetric black hole solutions in these theories, the special geometry moduli have to satisfy a pair of relatively simple differential equations. These equations can be interpreted as requiring that the central charge of the supergravity theory has a critical point at the horizon of the black hole. In this section, we will look at things from the IIB point-of-view.

If the black hole solution in question is spherically symmetric and static, we can write the line element

\text{d}s^{2}\;=\;e^{2U(r)}\text{d}t^{2}-e^{-2U(r)}(\text{d}r^{2}+r^{2}\text{d}\Omega^{2}),

where the radial coordinate
r
is zero on the horizon. A key quantity is the central charge
\mathcal{Z}
of the superymmetry algebra, which is related to the mass and entropy of the black hole.

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}(Q)\;=\;\frac{Q^{T}\,\Sigma\,\Pi}{\sqrt{-\text{i}\Pi^{\dagger}\,\Sigma\,\Pi}}~,

(1.12)

The intersection matrix
\Sigma
appearing here is given in our chosen basis by

\displaystyle\Sigma\;=\;\begin{pmatrix}\phantom{-}0&\mathbbm{1}\\
-\mathbbm{1}&0\end{pmatrix}.

The dynamics of the scalars
z^{a}
and the function
U
can be written in terms of a new coordinate
\rho=1/r
:

\displaystyle\frac{dU(\rho)}{d\rho}

\displaystyle\;=\;-e^{U(\rho)}|\mathcal{Z}|~,\qquad\frac{dz^{a}(\rho)}{d\rho}\;=\;-2e^{U(\rho)}g^{a\,\bar{b}}\partial_{\bar{b}}|\mathcal{Z}|~,

(1.13)

where
g_{a\,\bar{b}}
is the special geometry metric on the moduli space
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbbl{C}}
and
z^{a}
are the scalars of the
\mathcal{N}{\;=\;}2
vector multiplets, which are viewed as coordinates on the moduli space
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbbl{C}}
. These equations describe flows of scalar field values from
\rho=0
at spatial infinity to
\rho=\infty
at the black hole horizon. The terminology “attractor mechanism” owes to the fact that the values of
z^{a}
are fixed at the black hole horizon at
r=0
(
\rho=\infty
) by the black hole charges, and so are independent of the values they take at
r=\infty
(
\rho=0
), at least for sufficiently small variations of boundary values.

The second equation, (
1.13
), can be used to find a gradient flow equation:

\frac{d|\mathcal{Z}|}{d\rho}\;=\;-4e^{U(\rho)}g^{a\bar{b}}\partial_{a}|\mathcal{Z}|\partial_{\bar{b}}|\mathcal{Z}|~,

revealing that the end points of attractor flows are critical values of
|\mathcal{Z}|
, since the metric
g_{a\bar{b}}
is positive definite. One can also see that
|\mathcal{Z}|
decreases along attractor flows.

It was pointed out early on by Strominger
[
36
]
that at an attractor point where the central charge is not vanishing, the period vector of the Calabi-Yau manifold satisfies a vector relation that we shall refer to as the
alignment equations

\displaystyle\text{Im}\left[C\Pi\right]

\displaystyle\;=\;Q~,

(1.14)

where
\Pi
is the period vector of the Calabi-Yau manifold,
Q
is the charge vector of the corresponding black hole, and

\displaystyle C\;=\;2\text{e}^{-K/2}\overline{\mathcal{Z}},\qquad\text{where}\qquad\text{e}^{-K}\;\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits^{\rm def}}\;\text{i}\int_{X}\Omega\wedge\overline{\Omega}~.

In the following, we often choose a gauge in which
z^{0}=1
. The equation (
1.14
) determines the relation between the charges and the prepotential.

If, on the other hand, the central charge vanishes, then the corresponding relation is

\displaystyle Q^{T}\,\Sigma\,\Pi\;=\;0~,

(1.15)

which we will refer to as the
orthogonality equation
. This determines the prepotential in such a case.

The one-parameter attractor equations for a certain classes of Calabi-Yau manifolds were studied for example in
[
37
,
38
,
39
]
.

The alignment equations

It is convenient to write
C=a+\text{i}b
and express the alignment equation (
1.14
) in the form

\displaystyle a\,\text{Im}\Pi+b\,\text{Re}\Pi\;=\;Q~.

(1.16)

This equation can also be understood in terms of cohomology, and amounts to the condition that

\displaystyle\Gamma\in H^{(3,0)}(X,\mathbbl{Z})\oplus H^{(0,3)}(X,\mathbbl{Z})~.

We can think of the period vector
\Pi
as defining a 2-plane over the reals, spanned by
\text{Re}\Pi
and
\text{Im}\Pi
. This two plane varies as we move in the moduli space giving a family of 2-planes in
\mathbbl{R}^{4}
, parametrised by the moduli-space coordinate
t
.

In order to satisfy the alignment equations, the plane

\displaystyle V\;=\;\text{Span}(\text{Re}\Pi,\text{Im}\Pi)

must contain a non-zero point of the charge lattice
H^{3}(X,\mathbbl{Z})\simeq\mathbbl{Z}^{4}
, and so a lattice line consisting of integer multiples of smallest such point. If the intersection is a lattice line, the attractor is said to be of rank one. Exceptionally, the plane
V
will intersect the charge lattice in two linearly independent points, and so in a lattice plane. Then the attractor point is said to be of rank two. If this is so, the alignment equations are satisfied for two linearly independent vectors
Q
.

The orthogonality equation

In addition to the attractor points solving the alignment equations, there are attractor points where the central charge vanishes, since these points are necessarily local minima of the nonnegative quantity
|\mathcal{Z}(Q)|
. In this case

\displaystyle\Gamma\in H^{(2,1)}(X,\mathbbl{Z})\oplus H^{(1,2)}(X,\mathbbl{Z}).

There is again an interpretation of this equation in terms of planes and lattices. The charge vector
\Gamma
lies in the plane
V^{\perp}
that is orthogonal to
V
under the symplectic inner product, corresponding to
\Sigma
. For there to be an attractor point, the plane
V^{\perp}
must also intersect the charge lattice
H^{3}(X,\mathbbl{Z})
, and this intersection can also be of rank one, or exceptionally of rank two.

Solutions to the alignment equations and the orthogonality equation are related. The fact that
V
is to some extent rational with respect to the charge lattice has consequences for the rationality of the orthogonal plane
V^{\perp}
. In particular, if
V
intersects the lattice in a lattice plane, then
V^{\perp}

V

V{\;=\;}\text{Span}(Q_{3},Q_{4})

\displaystyle\text{Span}(\text{Re}\Pi,\text{Im}\Pi)\;=\;\text{Span}(Q_{1},Q_{2})^{\phantom{\perp}}=\;\text{Span}(Q_{3},Q_{4})^{\perp}~.

Likewise, the orthogonality equation can be written as

\displaystyle\text{Span}(\text{Re}\Pi,\text{Im}\Pi)\;=\;\text{Span}(Q_{1},Q_{2})^{\perp}=\;\text{Span}(Q_{3},Q_{4})^{\phantom{\perp}}~.

Thus alignment equations can be, in this case, written as an orthogonality equation and vice versa. In the case that

intersects the lattice in a lattice line, the situation is more complicated, and is explored in more detail in §

.

We wish to be clear about an issue of terminology. The term “attractor point” is often used to refer exclusively with regards to massive black holes, and so to solutions of the alignment equations, ignoring possible solutions to the orthogonality equation. In the following, we will understand an attractor point to be a point that is either a solution of the alignment equations or a solution of the orthogonality equation. By a rank two attractor point, we will continue to mean that there are two independent solutions to either the alignment or the orthogonality equation, and hence that either
V
or
V^{\perp}
intersects the charge lattice in a plane.

Interpretation

The solutions to the alignment and orthogonality equations can be given very different physical interpretations, owing to their relation to the BPS state existence conjecture and the nature of the corresponding attractor flows. The BPS state existence conjecture
[
4
,
40
,
41
,
3
,
2
]
asserts that, in the full quantum theory of gravity, the existence of BPS states with certain charge vectors is determined by the nature of the attractor point where the flow ends. If we leave aside the possibility of a limiting cycle, there are three different types of flows:

1.

The attractor flow ends at a point where
|\mathcal{Z}|{\,\neq\,}0
.

2.

The attractor flow ends at a point where
|\mathcal{Z}|{\,=\,}0
, and the point is a regular point on the moduli space of the Calabi-Yau manifold.

3.

The attractor flow ends at a point where
|\mathcal{Z}|{\,=\,}0
, and the point is a singular point on the moduli space of the Calabi-Yau manifold.

The BPS state existence conjecture asserts that BPS states always exist in the first case, and never in the second case, while in the third case BPS states may or may not exist, and this question cannot be determined from the attractor flow data alone
[
3
,
2
]
. Thus the conjecture asserts that the solutions to the alignment equations should have an associated BPS state in the full theory, while the solutions to the orthogonality equation do not correspond to such states.

Even though the BPS state existence conjecture asserts that solutions to the orthogonality equation do not correspond to BPS states, this does not mean that the solutions are not significant. First of all, as we have seen, the alignment and orthogonality equations are intimately connected: in some cases, solutions to the alignment equations can be found from solutions to the orthogonality equation. In addition, the orthogonality equation has a relation to flux compactifications
[
42
,
43
]
(see also section 4 of
[
15
]
). Finally, it has been suggested
[
15
]
that, at least at rank two attractor points, the existence of solutions to the orthogonality equation may indicate supersymmetry enhancement.

The central charge and entropy

In the case of Calabi-Yau compactifications, the attractor equations not only make it possible to find the complex or complexified Kähler structure of the internal manifold, but also can be used to compute the central charge (
1.12
) of the supergravity theory, which in turn is related to the Bekenstein-Hawking entropy of the black hole associated to the attractor point.

\displaystyle S_{\mbox{\tiny BH}}\;=\;\frac{A}{4}\;=\;\pi|\mathcal{Z}|^{2}.

Solutions for the attractor equations in the approximation where the instanton corrections to the prepotential are ignored, but the genus 1 perturbative contribution is included, were found in
[
9
,
22
]
and used to give an approximate formula for the Bekenstein-Hawking entropy in any Calabi-Yau compactification, where the internal manifold has a large complex structure. For black holes for which
p^{0}=0
but the other charges are large,

\displaystyle S_{\mbox{\tiny BH}}=\pi\sqrt{-\frac{2}{3}Y(\widetilde{p}^{3}+c_{2}\widetilde{p})\left(\widetilde{q}_{0}-\frac{1}{2\widetilde{p}Y}\widetilde{q}^{2}\right)}~.

(1.17)

Here
\widetilde{p}
,
\widetilde{q}
and
\widetilde{q}_{0}
are the microscopic charges, that is numbers of D4-, D2-, and D0-branes. The relation to the macroscopic charges
p
,
q
and
q_{0}
can be found in appendix
C
.

In the calculation of this formula
Y_{000}
and
Y_{110}
cancel out, and
Y_{100}
and
Y_{111}
appear via
c_{2}
and
Y
, respectively. The existence of such a result, which bears a clear resemblance to Cardy growth
[
44
]
, raises the question whether there is a modular form related to the microstate counting, whose asymptotics reproduce the above formula in a manner similar to the celebrated results of Strominger and Vafa
[
16
]
. This question was first tackled in
[
17
]
, where the authors showed that this entropy can be reproduced, under some mild assumptions, by counting massless degrees of freedom in an M-theory scenario with fivebranes wrapping a four-cycle on
X
and the M-theory circle, together with momentum
q_{0}
around the circle. This setup gives rise to a two-dimensional
\mathcal{N}=(4,0)
conformal field theory on the fivebrane world-volume. It is possible to define a
modified elliptic genus
, which is a supersymmetric index that it is invariant under continuous deformations of the theory, at least for small enough deformations. This modified elliptic genus can be expressed in terms of a vector-valued modular form. The coefficients of this modular form contain information about the number of microstates. The asymptotic growth of the number of microstates is then given by the Cardy formula
[
44
]
with the central charges

\displaystyle\begin{split}c_{R}&=Y\left(\widetilde{p}^{3}+c_{2}\widetilde{p}\right),\qquad c_{L}=Y\left(\widetilde{p}^{3}+\frac{1}{2}c_{2}\widetilde{p}\right).\end{split}

In the large charge limit
\widetilde{p}\to\infty
, this correctly reproduces (
1.17
).

For cases where the charge
\widetilde{p}
is small, the corresponding modified elliptic genera have been found explicitly, for some simple but interesting Calabi-Yau manifolds in
[
45
,
46
]
. To do this, one can consider the geometry of divisors, curves, and points on the manifold. The fivebranes are understood as divisors, and the lower-dimensional branes as curves or points on these. The coefficients in the modular form that appears in the modified elliptic genus can then be found by studying the moduli spaces of various such configurations of branes and computing their Witten indices.

There is, however, an alternative method, which uses
split attractor flows

[
40
,
47
,
41
]
to compute elliptic genera. This was used in
[
48
]
to repeat the calculation for the Calabi-Yau manifolds referred to above. This technique is based on the observation that at least some of the coefficients count microscopic degrees of freedom in situations where the black hole can be viewed as a bound state of a D6 and an anti-D6 brane
[
4
]
. Such microstate indices can be expressed in terms of Donaldson-Thomas invariants
[
49
,
50
]
, which in turn can be expressed in terms of the Gromov-Witten invariants. Interestingly, these invariants also appear in our macroscopic expressions, such as (
1.6
). Unfortunately, the modified elliptic genus is not currently known for the manifold AESZ34, which we use as the main example of a one-parameter Calabi-Yau manifold in this paper owing to that fact that it has rank two attractor points that are known explicitly. We hope to revisit this issue elsewhere.

It can also be shown, as we will review in §
7.2
, that the central charge can be viewed as the leading-order contribution to the Wald entropy
[
51
]
of the black hole in the large charge limit
[
19
]
. The Wald entropy can be obtained from an analogue of the attractor equations, known as the
stabilisation equations
, where the period vector
\Pi
is replaced by a more general vector that includes the higher-genus prepotentials. However, as we will see in §
7.2
, in the large charge limit the stabilisation equations reduce to the alignment equations (
1.14
).

One of the aims of the present work is to extend these results by including the contributions of instantons that have not been included in the approximation used in
[
9
,
24
,
17
,
8
]
. We set up an iterative procedure that generates the
N
-expansions, which have a universal form that depends on the manifold through the perturbative coefficients of the prepotential together with the instanton numbers. A simple example of such an expansion has been given in (
1.6
).

The black hole charge determines the location of the attractor point, and so also the period vector. The value of the central charge therefore depends in a detailed way on the particular brane system under consideration. Nevertheless, we close this subsection with some general remarks, which we will later refine in order to obtain explicit expressions for different brane systems.

From the alignment equations it follows that at an attractor point the charge vector can be expressed as a linear combination of real and imaginary parts of the period vector. Substituting (
1.16
) into the definition of the central charge, the square of the central charge can be simplified

\displaystyle|\mathcal{Z}|^{2}

\displaystyle\;=\;\frac{a^{2}+b^{2}}{2}\left|\text{Re}\Pi^{T}\,\Sigma\;\text{Im}\Pi\right|.

Without yet specialising to any particular case, we can expand this near the large complex structure limit, so for large
y
, in the form

\displaystyle\frac{|\mathcal{Z}|^{2}}{a^{2}+b^{2}}

\displaystyle\;=\;\frac{1}{3}y^{3}Y+\frac{1}{6}Y\sigma+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{(2\pi ky+1)\cos(2\pi kx)}{(2\pi k)^{3}}N^{\text{GW}}_{k}\text{e}^{-2\pi ky}~.

This formula, however, is deceptively simple. It depends on the location of the attractor point
x+\text{i}y
, which in turn depends on the charge vector
Q
. Our aim is to solve this dependence explicitly and so find a formula for the central charge that depends only on the black hole charges and the internal geometry of the manifold. An interesting aspect of this expression is the appearance of the Gromov-Witten invariants, or equivalently the instanton numbers, since these hint at counting of microstate contributions. Since
x
and
y
also have expansions in terms of the Gromov-Witten invariants, this feature is preserved when we eliminate
x
and
y
from the expression.

Rank two attractor points

A particularly interesting examples of attractor points are provided by
rank two attractor points
where two linearly independent charges solve the alignment equation (
1.14
) at the same point in the moduli space. These are much rarer than rank one attractor points, as the period vector has to satisfy much more stringent conditions. However, precisely due to these restrictions, we also have more control over various quantities associated to these points.

As first emphasised by Moore
[
3
,
2
]
, rank two attractor point also have interesting connections to number theory. The explicit examples of rank two attractor points found for the manifold AESZ34 in
[
15
]
display some intriguing connections between attractor point coordinates, central charge and L-functions. For example, it was conjectured (supported by extensive numerical evidence) that there is a rank two attractor point at
\varphi=33-8\sqrt{17}
whose location in the complexified Kähler moduli space is

\displaystyle t\;=\;\text{i}\frac{5}{16\cdot 17}(9+\sqrt{17})\frac{\pi\lambda_{4}(1)}{\lambda_{4}(2)}~,

where
\lambda_{4}(s)
are the real parts of an L-function
L(s)
for the group
\Gamma_{1}(34)
with the LMFDB designation
34.4.b.a

[
52
]
. In this paper, we will be able to rederive some of these results using a complementary point of view that emphasises the relation of the attractor point coordinate and the charge vector, whereas the method utilised in
[
15
]
uses zeta-function factorisation to find the location attractor point in terms of the mirror coordinate
\varphi
. As a result of our different approach, we are able to derive some interesting identities between the L-function values
\lambda_{4}(1)
,
\lambda_{4}(2)
and Gromov-Witten invariants. Identities like this were already anticipated by Moore
[
3
]
.

1.2
Outline of the paper

In this paper, our aim is to solve the attractor equations for an arbitrary one-parameter family of Calabi-Yau manifolds by developing
N
-expansions for the attractor point coordinate
t
. Although we consider an arbitrary manifold, we specialise the charge vector for simplicity and concentrate on two important special cases that correspond to the D6-D2-D0 and D4-D2-D0 brane systems. The main reason for this choice is that restricting to a case where one of the charges vanishes makes the resulting equations much more tractable. In addition, these systems are of particular interest with microstate counting in mind, as their microstate indices can be viewed either as Donaldson-Thomas invariants or as Witten indices of divisor moduli spaces
[
48
,
45
,
46
]
.

In §
2
, prior to studying the two special cases separately, we discuss the general method that will be used to derive the
N
-expansions. We use this section to set notation and find expressions that we will later specialise.

In §
3
, we review some basic facts concerning partitions. While this material is standard, we take the opportunity to set the notation that is used extensively in the rest of the paper. We also define operations on partitions that are useful for expressing various functions as perturbative expansions.

Following these preliminaries, in §
5
and §
4
we are able to calculate fully instanton-corrected solutions to the attractor equations for the general D6-D2-D0 and D4-D2-D0 configurations. The alignment and orthogonality equations are considered separately. The solutions are separated into a perturbative part and instanton corrections.

Our strategy is to express the instanton corrections as series in
q_{y}=\text{e}^{-2\pi y_{0}}
. These series have coefficients which are polynomials in the perturbative coordinates
x_{0}
,
y_{0}
, as well as the related quantities
\sin 2\pi x_{0}
and
\cos 2\pi x_{0}
. The polynomials are most conveniently expressed as solutions to recurrence relations. These relations can be quite complicated, but in §
6
we see that in some important cases there exist closed-form expressions. In particular, the polynomials appearing in the solutions to the D4-D2-D0 orthogonality equation and a special case of the D6-D2-D0 alignment equations can be written in terms of modified spherical Bessel functions.

This special case of the D6-D2-D0 alignment equations is of particular interest since the rank two attractor points of AESZ34, found in
[
15
]
, are included in this case. The highly constrained nature of the rank two attractor points allows one, for example, to write the entropy in the particularly simple form (
1.8
). Combining the solutions obtained here to the formulae in
[
15
]
, where the entropy at rank two attractor points was related to L-function values, we are able to derive an intriguing formula relating L-function values to Gromov-Witten invariants and Bessel functions.

In §
7
we discuss some applications of our expressions. First the attractor points in the large complex structure region are divided according to whether or not the black hole central charge vanishes: an important distinction for the physical interpretation of the solutions. We are able, based on consideration of the black hole charge only, to determine whether solutions to the alignment or orthogonality equations exist near the large complex structure point.

To conclude this section, we consider our entropy formulae from the perspective of microstate counting and verify that in leading order our expressions agree with those in
[
17
]
and with modular form asymptotics. We are, however, considering a limit that is different from that in
[
17
]
and so differences appear in subleading orders. Building on the work of
[
22
,
23
,
24
,
18
,
19
,
20
,
21
]
(for a review, see
[
6
]
), we show that the proper interpretation of our entropy formula is that it gives the leading-order Wald entropy of the black hole in the large charge limit. In this sense, our results extend those in
[
9
,
17
,
18
]
.

Matters that would otherwise disrupt the narrative are relegated to four appendices. In appendix
A
, we discuss the action on our formulae of a
\mathbbl{Z}^{3}
subgroup of
\text{Sp}(4,\mathbbl{Z})
, and identify certain combinations of the Yukawa couplings and the charges which are invariant under this group. In appendix
B
, we examine the role of the monodromy transformations around the large complex structure limit, showing that these can either be interpreted as a symmetry of the solutions or used to extend our solutions to cases where the D4 brane charge divides the D6 charge. Appendix
C
sets out the relation of the charge vector
Q
to the various D-brane charges. In appendices
D
and
E
, we list a set of polynomials which can be used to express the perturbative solutions to the D6-D2-D0 attractor equations. Finally, in appendix
F
, we discuss the asymptotics and convergence of solutions we find.

2
Solving the Attractor Equations: a General Overview

Our strategy for solving the attractor equations relies on using the expression (
1.9
) for the period vector
\Pi
to express both the alignment equations (
1.16
) and the orthogonality equation (
1.15
) explicitly as a set of equalities involving geometric data (the topological quantities
Y_{abc}
and Gromov-Witten invariants of the Calabi-Yau manifold) and the black hole charges.

In the following sections §
4
and §
5
, we solve these equations perturbatively in the instanton corrections. We first find the zeroth-order solution corresponding to what we shall refer to as the
perturbative
part of the prepotential, meaning the
Y_{abc}
-terms, corresponding to ignoring the contributions of the instanton sums. This gives us a pair of polynomial equations of degree at most four. Explicit solutions of such polynomial equations are of course known since the Renaissance, but we dedicate some discussion to giving the most concise solutions. We show that the solutions can, in most cases, be given by simple trigonometric expressions.

Near the large complex structure point the instanton corrections are exponentially small and we expand in terms of the quantity
q_{y}{\;=\;}\text{e}^{-2\pi y_{0}}
, where
y_{0}
is the perturbative value of the attractor coordinate, found by using the perturbative equations as outlined above. We find that the instanton corrections take the form of a series in
q_{y}
,

\displaystyle x\;=\;\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}x_{j}q_{y}^{j}~,\qquad y\;=\;\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}y_{j}q_{y}^{j}~,

(2.1)

with coefficients
x_{j}
and
y_{j}
that are rational functions of
x_{0}
,
y_{0}
,
\sin 2\pi x_{0}
, and
\cos 2\pi x_{0}
.

\displaystyle x_{j}

\displaystyle\;=\;\sum_{\mathfrak{p}\in\text{pt}(j)}\hskip-5.0ptN_{\mathfrak{p}}R^{x_{\,}}_{\mathfrak{p}}(x_{0},y_{0}),\qquad y_{j}\;=\;\sum_{\mathfrak{p}\in\text{pt}(j)}\hskip-5.0ptN_{\mathfrak{p}}R^{y_{\,}}_{\mathfrak{p}}(x_{0},y_{0}),

(2.2)

where
\mathfrak{p}
is a partition of
j{\,\in\,}\mathbbl{Z}^{+}
,
R^{x_{\,}}_{\mathfrak{p}}(x_{0},y_{0})
and
R^{y_{\,}}_{\mathfrak{p}}(x_{0},y_{0})
are the rational functions of
x_{0}
,
y_{0}
,
\sin(2\pi x_{0})
and
\cos(2\pi x_{0})
and are independent of the Gromov-Witten invariants. The
N_{\mathfrak{p}}
are combinations of the scaled Gromov-Witten invariants as in (
1.7
). We give recurrence relations for the
R_{\mathfrak{p}}^{x}
and
R_{\mathfrak{p}}^{y}
, which are amenable to rapid computer calculation. Since the individual terms in the sum are exponentially small the first few terms suffice for many practical purposes.

A pleasant surprise is that even though the recurrence relations are somewhat complicated in the general case, we are able to give simple closed-form solutions in important special cases. In particular, for the D4-D2-D0 orthogonality equation and a special case of D6-D2-D0 alignment equations, we will find in §
6
interesting expressions for the location of the attractor point which curiously involve spherical Bessel functions.

2.1
Particular brane systems

We study extremal charged black holes that, in the string theory picture, arise from wrappings of
D-branes
on cycles on the internal Calabi-Yau manifold. We restrict our attention to arrangements containing either D6- or D4-branes but not both. We attach to these some D2- and D0-branes. Recalling the form of a generic charge vector (
1.10
), this means that we consider charge vectors of the form

Q_{\text{D4}}=\kappa\left(\begin{matrix}\Lambda\\
\Upsilon\\
0\\
1\end{matrix}\right),\hskip 56.9055ptQ_{\text{D6}}=\kappa\left(\begin{matrix}\Lambda\\
\Upsilon\\
1\\
0\end{matrix}\right).

(2.3)

The physical charge vectors have integral components, so
\Lambda
and
\Upsilon
are rational numbers, and

is a suitable integer. The charge vectors are written in this way partly to simplify later expressions since the locations of attractor points depend only on the
charge ratios

\Upsilon
and
\Lambda

large charge limit
, where
{|\kappa|\to\infty}
and the
large charge ratio limits
, where either
|\Lambda|
or
|\Upsilon|
is large.

In
[
15
]
, where rank two attractor points for one-parameter families were first explicitly identified, the associated charge vectors were of this type, and charge vectors of this form have received much attention in the literature. Microscopic state counting has been performed both for black holes of
Q_{\text{D4}}
charge using elliptic genera
[
45
,
46
]
and D6-D0 bound states using Donaldson-Thomas invariants
[
4
,
48

.

By utilising the monodromy around the large complex structure point we can extend our solutions to any brane system where the D6-charge divides the D4-charge, as we will show in detail in appendix
B

Setting up the iteration

To set up the perturbative problem, we will separate, in the period vector, those terms that do not contain instanton corrections from those that do.

\begin{gathered}\Pi=\Pi_{0}+\Delta_{\mathcal{I}}\Pi,\\[15.0pt]

As stated above, we wish to treat the instanton terms as small corrections, and so in all cases we first solve the zeroth-order

. These are obtained from the full alignment or orthogonality equations by simply replacing the full period vector
\Pi

\Pi_{0}

\displaystyle\text{Im}[C\Pi_{0}]\;=\;Q~,\qquad\qquad\Pi_{0}^{T}\Sigma Q\;=\;0~.

(2.6)

x_{0}
and

, which depends on
\sigma
. This solution is usually most conveniently expressed in terms of trigonometric functions. We can also give

and

\sigma
. Thus, in addition to the perturbative coordinates
x_{0}
and
y_{0}
, we define quantities
x_{00}
and

as the

The utility of this expansion will be seen in §

Solving the orthogonality equation proceeds in a very similar manner. The principal difference is that, as the orthogonality equation (

3
Partitions

Since many of the formulae we will come across while solving the attractor equations involve partitions, we find it useful to introduce some terminology and notation from the domain of enumerative combinatorics and provide a rudimentary introduction to the theory of partitions. This serves to fix our notation and give some of the basic results that will be extensively used in the following. A more thorough treatment can be found in, for example,
[
53
]
, whose notation we largely follow.

3.1
Basic definitions and notation

A
partition

\mathfrak{p}
of a positive integer
k\in\mathbbl{Z}_{>0}
is a sequence of positive integers

0<p_{1}\leqslant p_{2}\leqslant\ldots\leqslant p_{m}\quad\text{such that}\quad\sum_{i=1}^{m}p_{i}\;=\;k~.

If
\mathfrak{p}
is a partition of
k
, we write
|\mathfrak{p}|{\;=\;}k
and refer to the terms
p_{i}
as the
parts
of
\mathfrak{p}
. The number of parts is called the
length
of the partition, which is denoted by
\bm{\ell}(\mathfrak{p})
. If
\mu_{i}
denotes the number of parts equal to
i
, we can write partitions by means of a multiset notation
\mathfrak{p}{\;=\;}\{1^{\mu_{1}},2^{\mu_{2}},...\}
, where only a finite number of
\mu_{i}
are non-zero.

The partitions of an integer
k
can be also viewed as the different ways of expressing
k
as a sum of positive integers without distinguishing the order of the summands. In virtue of this, partitions are often also written using a summation notation:

\mathfrak{p}\;=\;\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\mu_{i}i~.

Every integer
k
is a partition of itself, so we can regard integers as special cases of partitions.

Although partitions are defined as sequences of positive numbers, it is sometimes convenient to generalise the notion of partition to include
partitions with zeros
. A partition with zeros
\mathfrak{p}
of
k{\,\in\,}\mathbbl{Z}_{>0}
is a sequence of non-negative integers

0\leqslant p_{1}\leqslant p_{2}\leqslant...\leqslant p_{m}\quad\text{such that}\quad\sum_{i=1}^{m}p_{i}\;=\;k~.

The definitions of
\bm{\ell}(\mathfrak{p})
and
|\mathfrak{p}|
are as before.

There is an important special case where the partition corresponds to an empty multiset
\mathfrak{p}=\emptyset
. This is the
empty partition
, and we take
\bm{\ell}(\emptyset)=0
and
|\emptyset|=0
. It is important to distinguish the empty partition
\emptyset
from the partition with zeros
\{0^{n}\}
. For example, we have
\bm{\ell}(\emptyset)=0
, while
\bm{\ell}(\{0^{n}\})=n
.

3.2
Operations on partitions

In this subsection

\displaystyle\mathfrak{p}\;=\;\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\mu_{i}i\;=\;\{0^{\mu_{0}},1^{\mu_{1}},2^{\mu_{2}},...\}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathfrak{q}\;=\;\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\nu_{i}i\;=\;\{0^{\nu_{0}},1^{\nu_{1}},2^{\nu_{2}},...\}

are either partitions or partitions with zeros, for which we use the multiset or sum notation, as convenient.

We write
\mathfrak{p}\subseteq\mathfrak{q}
if, as multisets,
\mathfrak{p}\subseteq\mathfrak{q}
, which means that
\mu_{i}\leqslant\nu_{i}
for all
i
. We use
\subset
when
\mu_{i}<\nu_{i}
.

We can always define a sum of two partitions as a union of the partitions:
\mathfrak{p}+\mathfrak{q}{\;=\;}\mathfrak{p}\cup\mathfrak{q}
, where in writing
\mathfrak{p}\cup\mathfrak{q}
we understand
\mathfrak{p}
and
\mathfrak{q}
as multisets. Alternatively, in the sum notation, we can write

\mathfrak{p}+\mathfrak{q}\;=\;\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}(\mu_{i}+\nu_{i})\,i~.

It is clear that this is always a partition, and that
|\mathfrak{p}+\mathfrak{q}|{\;=\;}|\mathfrak{p}|+|\mathfrak{q}|
, and
\bm{\ell}(\mathfrak{p}+\mathfrak{q}){\;=\;}\bm{\ell}(\mathfrak{p})+\bm{\ell}(\mathfrak{q})
.

When
\mathfrak{p}\subseteq\mathfrak{q}
, we can define also the difference of these partitions as
\mathfrak{q}-\mathfrak{p}{\;=\;}\mathfrak{q}\setminus\mathfrak{p}
or, slightly informally, using the sum notation, as

\mathfrak{q}-\mathfrak{p}\;=\;\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}(\nu_{i}-\mu_{i})\,i~.

Note that there is a potential source of confusion here with the treatment of zeros. We emphasise that this sum is just an informal way of writing the difference, and the unambiguous definition is given via the multiset difference. Similarly to the sum, we have that
|\mathfrak{q}-\mathfrak{p}|{\;=\;}|\mathfrak{q}|-|\mathfrak{p}|
and
\bm{\ell}(\mathfrak{q}-\mathfrak{p}){\;=\;}\bm{\ell}(\mathfrak{q})-\bm{\ell}(\mathfrak{p})
. In the special case
\mathfrak{p}=\mathfrak{q}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathfrak{p}-\mathfrak{p}\;=\;\emptyset,

which follows unambiguously by taking the multiset difference.

It is useful also to set notation for some important sets of partitions. We denote the set of all partitions of
k
, of any length, by

\text{pt}(k)\;\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits^{\rm def}}\;\left\{\,\mathfrak{p}~\big|~|\mathfrak{p}|=k\right\}~.

(3.1)

The set of all partitions of
k
with length
s
is denoted by

\text{pt}_{s}(k)\;\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits^{\rm def}}\;\{\,\mathfrak{p}~\big|~|\mathfrak{p}|=k~~\text{and}~~\bm{\ell}(\mathfrak{p})=s\}~.

(3.2)

In addition, we define some unions of the above sets:

\text{pt}_{{\scriptscriptstyle\leqslant}\,s}(k)\;\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits^{\rm def}}\;\bigcup_{r=1}^{s}\text{pt}_{r}(k)\,,~~\text{and}~~\text{pt}_{{\scriptscriptstyle<}\,s}(k)\;\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits^{\rm def}}\;\bigcup_{r=1}^{s-1}\text{pt}_{r}(k)~,

with the sets
\text{pt}_{{\scriptscriptstyle\geqslant}\,s}(k)
and
\text{pt}_{{\scriptscriptstyle>}\,s}(k)
defined similarly.

The set of partitions of
k
with zeros is denoted by
\text{pt}^{0}(k)
, and the definitions of the variants such as
\text{pt}^{0}_{{\scriptscriptstyle\leqslant}\,s}(k)
are given straightforwardly, as in the above. To avoid any confusion arising from the somewhat subtle issue of the empty partition, we take
\text{pt}(0)=\{\emptyset\}
, and
\emptyset\notin\text{pt}^{0}_{i}(k)
for any
i
or
k
.

It is sometimes convenient to use a partition
\mathfrak{p}
as a multi-index, specifying a product. This is understood exclusively for the following five quantities:

x_{\mathfrak{p}}\;=\;\prod_{j=0}^{\infty}x_{j}^{\mu_{j}}~,\qquad y_{\mathfrak{p}}\;=\;\prod_{j=0}^{\infty}y_{j}^{\mu_{j}}~,\qquad t_{\mathfrak{p}}\;=\;\prod_{j=0}^{\infty}t_{j}^{\mu_{j}}~,\qquad N_{\mathfrak{p}}\;=\;\prod_{j=0}^{\infty}N_{j}^{\mu_{j}}~,\qquad\bm{m}_{\mathfrak{p}}\;=\;\prod_{j=0}^{\infty}\bm{m}_{j}^{\mu_{j}}~.

In all other cases where
\mathfrak{p}
appears in a subscript, such as the later
\mathcal{E}_{j,\mathfrak{p}}
, it is meant as an index that associates a quantity to a partition in some other way specific to that symbol. In other words, said symbol is a different function of
\mathfrak{p}
.

Functions arise that depend on partitions and also Gromov-Witten invariants. We often expand these functions as power series in the rescaled Gromov-Witten invariants
N_{k}
. Using the partition notation, the resulting series can be written as

F\;=\;\sum_{\mathfrak{p}}N_{\mathfrak{p}}F_{\mathfrak{p}}~.

We shall refer to such an expansion as an
N
-expansion
.

We can think of the coefficients
F_{\mathfrak{p}}
as functions of the partitions
\mathfrak{p}
. For two such functions
F_{\mathfrak{p}}
and
G_{\mathfrak{p}}
, there is a natural convolution-like product

(F\hskip 1.0pt{*}\hskip 1.0ptG)_{\mathfrak{p}}\,\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits^{\rm def}}\,\sum_{\mathfrak{q}\subseteq\mathfrak{p}}F_{\mathfrak{p}-\mathfrak{q}}\,G_{\mathfrak{q}}~.

(3.3)

This product is clearly commutative and associative, so we write

F\hskip 1.0pt{*}\hskip 1.0pt(G\hskip 1.0pt{*}\hskip 1.0ptH)\;=\;(F\hskip 1.0pt{*}\hskip 1.0ptG)\hskip 1.0pt{*}\hskip 1.0ptH\;=\;F\hskip 1.0pt{*}\hskip 1.0ptG\hskip 1.0pt{*}\hskip 1.0ptH~.

The main utility of this product is that if

\displaystyle F(j)\;=\;\hskip-3.0pt\sum_{\mathfrak{p}\in\text{pt}(j)}\hskip-5.0ptF_{\mathfrak{p}}\,N_{\mathfrak{p}}~,\qquad\text{and}\qquad G(j)\;=\;\hskip-3.0pt\sum_{\mathfrak{p}\in\text{pt}(j)}\hskip-5.0ptG_{\mathfrak{p}}\,N_{\mathfrak{p}}~,

then we have the following identity

\sum_{j=0}^{k}F(j)\,G(k-j)\;=\sum_{\mathfrak{p}\in\text{pt}(k)}(F\hskip 1.0pt{*}\hskip 1.0ptG)_{\mathfrak{p}}\,N_{\mathfrak{p}}~.

We will find frequent use for this identity in formulating different recurrence relations.

The sum in the convolution product includes the cases
\mathfrak{q}{\;=\;}\mathfrak{p}
and
\mathfrak{q}{\;=\;}\emptyset
, which both correspond to the empty partition. It is sometimes useful to consider a convolution product which does not include these two terms. We denote this product by
\circ
.

(F{\circ}G)_{\mathfrak{p}}\,\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits^{\rm def}}\hskip-3.0pt\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathfrak{q}\subset\mathfrak{p},\mathfrak{q}\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}F_{\mathfrak{p}-\mathfrak{q}}\,G_{\mathfrak{q}}\;=\;(F\hskip 1.0pt{*}\hskip 1.0ptG)_{\mathfrak{p}}-F_{\emptyset}G_{\mathfrak{p}}-F_{\mathfrak{p}}G_{\emptyset}~.

3.3
Series expansions

The main utility of the theory of partitions to the discussion of the attractor equations is that the generating functions of many series that arise may be expressed in terms of the quantities defined above. The prototypical quantities where sums or series can be written with the help of partitions are the powers of sums. For example

\left(\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}x_{j}q^{j}\right)^{n}\;=\;\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}q^{k}\sum_{\mathfrak{p}\in\text{pt}_{n}(k)}\hskip-5.0ptc_{\mathfrak{p}}x_{\mathfrak{p}}~,

(3.4)

where
c_{\mathfrak{p}}
is the multinomial coefficient associated to the partition
\mathfrak{p}{\;=\;}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\mu_{j}j

c_{\mathfrak{p}}\;=\;\frac{\left(\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\mu_{j}\right)!}{\prod_{j=1}^{\infty}\mu_{j}!}~.

(3.5)

Expressions like this will be appear repeatedly in what follows. In this way, we are led to series expansions for

\displaystyle x\;=\;\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}x_{i}\,q_{y}^{i}~,\quad y\;=\;\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}y_{i}\,q_{y}^{i}~,\quad t\;=\;\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}t_{i}\,q_{t}^{i}~,

and the following closely related quantities. These expressions follow straightforwardly from (
3.4
).

\displaystyle x^{n}

\displaystyle=\;\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}q_{y}^{k}\sum_{\mathfrak{p}\in\text{pt}^{0}_{n}(k)}\hskip-5.0ptc_{\mathfrak{p}}x_{\mathfrak{p}}~,\qquad n>0~,

(3.6)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{y}

\displaystyle=\;\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\eta_{k}\,q_{y}^{k}~,\qquad\qquad\qquad\text{where}\;\qquad\qquad\eta_{k}\;\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits^{\rm def}}\;\sum_{j=0}^{k}\frac{(-1)^{j}}{y_{0}^{j+1}}\sum_{\mathfrak{p}\in\text{pt}_{j}(k)}\hskip-5.0ptc_{\mathfrak{p}}y_{\mathfrak{p}}~,

\displaystyle\text{e}^{-2\pi my}

\displaystyle=\;\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\text{e}_{k}^{y_{\,}}(-m)\,q_{y}^{k+m}~,\;\,\qquad\text{where}\qquad\;\,\text{e}_{k}^{y_{\,}}(m)\;\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits^{\rm def}}\;\sum_{j=0}^{k}\frac{(2\pi m)^{j}}{j!}\sum_{\mathfrak{p}\in\text{pt}_{j}(k)}\hskip-5.0ptc_{\mathfrak{p}}y_{\mathfrak{p}}~,

\displaystyle\text{e}^{2\pi\text{i}mx}

\displaystyle=\;\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\text{e}_{k}^{x_{\,}}(\text{i}m)\,q_{y}^{k}q_{x}^{m}~,\;\;\;\,\qquad\text{where}\qquad\;\,\text{e}_{k}^{x_{\,}}(m)\;\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits^{\rm def}}\;\sum_{j=0}^{k}\frac{(2\pi m)^{j}}{j!}\sum_{\mathfrak{p}\in\text{pt}_{j}(k)}\hskip-5.0ptc_{\mathfrak{p}}x_{\mathfrak{p}}~,

\displaystyle\text{e}^{2\pi\text{i}mt}

\displaystyle=\;\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\text{e}_{k}^{t_{\,}}(\text{i}m)\,q_{t}^{k+m}~,\;\;\;\,\qquad\text{where}\qquad\;\,\text{e}_{k}^{t_{\,}}(m)\;\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits^{\rm def}}\;\sum_{j=0}^{k}\frac{(2\pi m)^{j}}{j!}\sum_{\mathfrak{p}\in\text{pt}_{j}(k)}\hskip-5.0ptc_{\mathfrak{p}}t_{\mathfrak{p}}~.

It is also useful to express these in terms of the quantities in (
2.2
) and (
2.8
). Substituting these for
x
and
y
, we find a set of expressions listed below. In the intermediate expressions, the following quantity appears often:

\displaystyle r^{x_{\,}}_{(m,n),\mathfrak{p}}

\displaystyle\;\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits^{\rm def}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathfrak{p}_{j}=\mathfrak{p}\\
\mathfrak{p}_{1},...,\mathfrak{p}_{m}\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}c\Bigl(\hskip-2.0pt\left\{\mathfrak{p}_{j}\right\}_{j=1}^{n}\Bigr)\prod_{j=1}^{n}R^{x_{\,}}_{\mathfrak{p}_{j}}~.

(3.7)

The sum here is over all partitions such that their sum is equal to
\mathfrak{p}
, with the condition that the partitions
\mathfrak{p}_{1},...,\mathfrak{p}_{m}
are non-empty (equivalently, there are between 0 and
n-m
empty partitions). The coefficients
c(\{\mathfrak{p}_{j}\})
are given by
c_{\mathfrak{q}}
, where
\mathfrak{q}
is the partition
\sum_{j=1}^{n}q_{j}
formed out of the multiplicities
q_{j}
of distinct partitions in the set
\{\mathfrak{p}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n}
. We define functions
r^{y}_{(m,n),\mathfrak{p}}
and
r^{t}_{(m,n),\mathfrak{p}}
similarly, with the only difference being that
R^{x_{\,}}
in replaced by
R^{y_{\,}}
or
R^{t_{\,}}
.

With the help of these quantities, we can express various quantities appearing in (
3.6
), in a form that is convenient for later computations, for example:

\displaystyle\eta_{k}

\displaystyle\;=\;\sum_{\mathfrak{p}\in\text{pt}(k)}\hskip-3.0ptN_{\mathfrak{p}}\sum_{j=1}^{l(\mathfrak{p})}\frac{(-1)^{j}}{y_{0}^{j+1}}r^{y_{\,}}_{(j,j),\mathfrak{p}}\,

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits^{\rm def}}\,\sum_{\mathfrak{p}\in\text{pt}(k)}\bm{\eta}_{\mathfrak{p}}N_{\mathfrak{p}}~,

\displaystyle\eta^{\prime}_{k}

\displaystyle\;=\;\sum_{\mathfrak{p}\in\text{pt}(k)}\hskip-3.0ptN_{\mathfrak{p}}\sum_{j=2}^{l(\mathfrak{p})}\frac{(-1)^{j}}{y_{0}^{j+1}}r^{y_{\,}}_{(j,j),\mathfrak{p}}\,

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits^{\rm def}}\,\sum_{\mathfrak{p}\in\text{pt}(k)}\bm{\eta^{\prime}}_{\mathfrak{p}}N_{\mathfrak{p}}~,

(3.8)

\displaystyle\;=\;\sum_{\mathfrak{p}\in\text{pt}(k)}\hskip-3.0ptN_{\mathfrak{p}}\sum_{j=0}^{l(\mathfrak{p})}\frac{(2\pi\alpha)^{j}}{j!}r^{x_{\,}}_{(j,j),\mathfrak{p}}\,

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits^{\rm def}}\,\sum_{\mathfrak{p}\in\text{pt}(k)}\text{{\boldmath e}}_{\mathfrak{p}}^{x_{\,}}(\alpha)\,N_{\mathfrak{p}}~,

with similar expressions for

. When

\displaystyle\bm{\eta}_{\emptyset}\;=\;\frac{1}{y_{0}}~,\qquad\bm{\eta}^{\prime}_{\emptyset}\;=\;1~.

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{C}_{j}(k)&\;=\;\hskip-3.0pt\sum_{\mathfrak{p}\in\text{pt}(k)}N_{\mathfrak{p}}\,\mathcal{C}_{j,\mathfrak{p}}~,\qquad\mathcal{S}_{j}(k)\;=\;\hskip-3.0pt\sum_{\mathfrak{p}\in\text{pt}(k)}N_{\mathfrak{p}}\mathcal{S}_{j,\mathfrak{p}}~,\qquad\mathcal{E}_{j}(k)\;=\;\hskip-3.0pt\sum_{\mathfrak{p}\in\text{pt}(k)}N_{\mathfrak{p}}\mathcal{E}_{j,\mathfrak{p}}~,\\[5.0pt]

where the sums over

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{I}_{1}&\;=\;-Y\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}q_{y}^{k}\,\mathcal{S}_{3}(k)~,\qquad\mathcal{I}_{2}\;=\;\phantom{-}Y\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}q_{y}^{k}\,\mathcal{C}_{3}(k)~,\\[5.0pt]

4
The D4-D2-D0 System

In this and the following section, we use the methods outlined previously to find the perturbative solutions to the zeroth-order attractor equations and the recurrence relations for the instanton corrections. We will consider the orthogonality and alignment equations separately.

We start with the case where the charge vector takes the following form, corresponding to the D4-D2-D0 system:

\displaystyle Q_{\text{D4}}=\kappa\begin{pmatrix}\Lambda\\
\Upsilon\\
0\\
1\end{pmatrix}.

The orthogonality equation for a charge vector of this form turns out to be the simplest among the four different cases we study in this paper. Consequently, this is one of the cases where we are able to find a closed-form solution for the instanton corrections, as will be shown in detail in §
6
.

With regards to microstate counting, the alignment equations in this case are particularly interesting. For example in
[
45
,
46
]
the microstates of black holes with these charges are counted by the coefficients of modular forms. Using these results, it is possible to express the supergravity entropy in terms of the perturbative corrections corresponding to
\sigma
as well as the instanton corrections. This can be used to compare the macroscopic expression to the asymptotics of modular forms, a subject to which we intend to return elsewhere. We discuss this in a little more detail in §
7.2
, where we give the relation of our results to the Wald entropy of the black hole associated to the attractor point we find here. We show that the leading-order asymptotics of the coefficients of the modular forms agree with the formulae in this section.

4.1
The orthogonality equation

The orthogonality equation depends only on the complex period
{\Pi}
and not on the real and imaginary parts separately. Therefore it is most natural to write the orthogonality equation as a condition on the complex coordinate
t
instead of separating this into the real and imaginary parts
x
and
y
as we are obliged to do for the non-holomorphic alignment equations later.

The orthogonality equation
\Pi^{T}\,\Sigma\,Q_{\text{D4}}=0
can be written as

\displaystyle t^{2}-2t\alpha\widetilde{\beta}+\widetilde{\beta}^{2}+\frac{2}{Y}\,\mathcal{I}^{\prime}\;=\;0~.

(4.1)

The perturbative equations

Ignoring the instanton corrections, the equation is just a quadratic. Moreover, it is independent of
\sigma
. Of the two solutions, the solution that can, for suitable values of
\Lambda
and
\Upsilon
, lie near the large complex structure point is

\displaystyle t_{0}\;=\;\widetilde{\beta}\left(\alpha+\sqrt{\alpha^{2}-1}\right).

(4.2)

For this to correspond to a solution that lies in the large complex structure region,
t_{0}
needs to have a large imaginary part. Noting that
\alpha\widetilde{\beta}=-\frac{Y_{110}+\Upsilon}{Y}
is always real and
\widetilde{\beta}
has a phase
0
or
\frac{\pi}{2}
, we have that the pair
\alpha
and
\widetilde{\beta}
are either both real or both purely imaginary. In fact, in the large complex structure region they cannot both be imaginary, for then
t_{0}
would be real. Thus we require that
\widetilde{\beta}>0
and
-1<\alpha<1
, in order for
t_{0}
to have a positive imaginary part. We will discuss the conditions for existence of perturbative solutions near the large complex structure point in more detail in §
7.1
. This said, the real and imaginary parts
x_{0}
and
y_{0}
can be written as

\displaystyle x_{0}\;=\;\widetilde{\beta}\alpha~,\qquad y_{0}\;=\;\widetilde{\beta}\sqrt{1-\alpha^{2}}~.

(4.3)

Instanton corrections

The instanton corrections to the solutions of the orthogonality equation are included as a series in
q_{t}
by writing
t=\sum_{j}t_{j}q_{t}^{j}
. To solve the attractor equations, we require that the coefficient of every power of
q_{t}
vanishes. This requirement can be written as

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\;=\;\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\mathfrak{p}\in\text{pt}^{0}_{2}(k)}\hskip-6.0ptc_{\mathfrak{p}}t_{\mathfrak{p}}-\alpha\widetilde{\beta}t_{k}+\mathcal{E}_{2}(k)~.

It is simple to solve this equation for
t_{k}
in terms of
t_{i}
with
i<k
. As
\mathcal{E}_{2}(k)
is independent of
t_{k}
, the above equation contains
t_{k}
multiplied by the factor
(t_{0}-\alpha\widetilde{\beta})
. Thus
t_{k}
is given by

\displaystyle t_{k}

\displaystyle\;=\;\frac{1}{\alpha\widetilde{\beta}-t_{0}}\left(\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\mathfrak{p}\in\text{pt}_{2}(k)}\hskip-6.0ptc_{\mathfrak{p}}t_{\mathfrak{p}}+\mathcal{E}_{2}(k)\right).

We now express
t_{k}
in terms of the Gromov-Witten invariants in the form (
2.8
). The rational functions
R^{t}_{\mathfrak{p}}
are chosen precisely to be themselves independent of the scaled Gromov-Witten invariants
N_{k}
. This leads to a recurrence relation

\displaystyle R^{t}_{\mathfrak{p}}(t_{0})

\displaystyle\;=\;\frac{1}{\alpha\widetilde{\beta}-t_{0}}\left(\frac{1}{2}\left(R^{t}{\circ}R^{t}\right)_{\mathfrak{p}}+\mathcal{E}_{2,\mathfrak{p}}\right).

(4.4)

From this we see that the
R^{t}_{\mathfrak{p}}
are rational functions of
t_{0}
. The denominators of these rational functions are due to the overall prefactor
1/(\alpha\widetilde{\beta}-t_{0})
, and can be written explicitly. We can concentrate on the numerators by introducing polynomials
P^{t}_{\mathfrak{p}}

\displaystyle P^{t}_{\mathfrak{p}}(w)\;=\;-\frac{\text{i}\pi\mathbbl{\Delta}(w)^{\bm{\ell}(\mathfrak{p})}}{w}R^{t}_{\mathfrak{p}}\left(\alpha\widetilde{\beta}+\text{i}\frac{w}{\pi}\right),

where in this subsection we set

\displaystyle\mathbbl{\Delta}(w)

\displaystyle\;=\;-4Yw^{2}~.

This formula differs somewhat from the relation between the polynomials and rational functions in the other cases considered later, since here the argument of
R^{t}_{\mathfrak{p}}
is shifted by
\alpha\widetilde{\beta}
, while in the other cases we just multiply the argument
v
by an overall factor. The reason for the shift in this case, as will soon become apparent, is that with this definition we can later substitute
v=y_{0}
to re-obtain the rational functions
R_{\mathfrak{p}}^{t}(t_{0})
satisfying the recurrence relation (
4.4
). This is the consequence of the fact that the instanton corrections depend essentially on
y_{0}
only.

It is easy to write down the first few polynomials. These have a simple form, and will later play an important role in finding solutions to the attractor equations in closed form.

\displaystyle\begin{split}P_{\{1\}}^{t}(w)&\;=\;\phantom{-}\thinspace 1~,\\[3.0pt]
P_{\{2\}}^{t}(w)&\;=\;\phantom{-}\thinspace\frac{1}{4}~,\\[3.0pt]
P_{\{1^{2}\}}^{t}(w)&\;=\;-\thinspace\frac{1}{2}\hskip 3.0pt\left(1+4w\right),\\[3.0pt]
P_{\{1,2\}}^{t}(w)&\;=\;-\thinspace\frac{1}{4}\hskip 3.0pt\left(1+6w\right),\\[3.0pt]
P_{\{1^{3}\}}^{t}(w)&\;=\;\phantom{-}\thinspace\frac{1}{2}\hskip 3.0pt\left(1+6w+12w^{2}\right),\\[3.0pt]
P_{\{1^{2},2\}}^{t}(w)&\;=\;\phantom{-}\thinspace\frac{1}{8}\hskip 3.0pt\left(3+24w+64w^{2}\right),\\[3.0pt]
P_{\{1^{4}\}}^{t}(w)&\;=\;-\frac{1}{24}\left(15+120w+384w^{2}+512w^{3}\right).\end{split}

Combining these observations, and recalling from (
4.3
) that
\alpha\widetilde{\beta}-t_{0}=-\text{i}y_{0}
, we are able to write the coordinate
t
in the form

\displaystyle\begin{split}t&\;=\;t_{0}+\text{i}y_{0}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\text{e}^{2\pi\text{i}kt_{0}}\!\sum_{\mathfrak{p}\in\text{pt}(k)}\frac{N_{\mathfrak{p}}}{\mathbbl{\Delta}\left(\pi y_{0}\right)^{\bm{\ell}(\mathfrak{p})}}P^{t}_{\mathfrak{p}}\left(\pi y_{0}\right).\end{split}

Let us also consider the real and imaginary parts of
t
. The polynomials
P^{t}_{\mathfrak{p}}(v)
are real for real
v
, as can be seen from the recurrence (
4.4
), so the real and imaginary parts of
t
are given by

\displaystyle\begin{split}x\;=\;x_{0}-y_{0}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\text{e}^{-2\pi ky_{0}}\sin(2\pi kx_{0})\sum_{\mathfrak{p}\in\text{pt}(k)}\frac{N_{\mathfrak{p}}}{\mathbbl{\Delta}\left(\pi y_{0}\right)^{\bm{\ell}(\mathfrak{p})}}P^{t}_{\mathfrak{p}}\left(\pi y_{0}\right),\\[5.0pt]

(4.5)

4.2
The alignment equations

The alignment equations are most conveniently expressed in terms of the real coordinates
x
and
y
. After replacing
a
and
b
in the equations (
2.4
) via (
2.5

\displaystyle\begin{split}(3x^{2}-y^{2})y-2\sigma+\frac{6}{Y}\left(x\mathcal{I}_{2}^{\prime}-2\mathcal{I}_{2}+y\mathcal{I}_{1}^{\prime}\right)-3\widetilde{\beta}^{2}y&\;=\;0~,\\[10.0pt]
x+\frac{1}{Yy}\mathcal{I}_{2}^{\prime}-\alpha\widetilde{\beta}&\;=\;0~.\end{split}

(4.6)

The perturbative equations

Neglecting the instanton contributions, the attractor equations become

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left(3x_{0}^{2}-y_{0}^{2}\right)y_{0}-2\sigma-3\widetilde{\beta}^{2}y_{0}&\;=\;0~,\qquad x_{0}-\alpha\widetilde{\beta}\;=\;0~.\end{split}

(4.7)

y_{0}

\displaystyle y_{0}^{3}-3\widetilde{\beta}^{2}(\alpha^{2}-1)y_{0}+2\sigma\;=\;0~.

This makes it possible to give a simple solution for

\displaystyle-4\sin^{3}\theta+3\sin\theta\;=\;\frac{\sigma}{\widetilde{\beta}^{3}(\alpha^{2}-1)^{3/2}}~,

\displaystyle\sin 3\theta\;=\;\frac{\sigma}{\widetilde{\beta}^{3}(\alpha^{2}-1)^{3/2}}~.

(4.9)

Having written the alignment equations in terms of

Using the

2.1
) for
x
and

) becomes

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}q_{y}^{k}\left(x_{k}-\sum_{j=1}^{k}\eta_{k-j}\,\mathcal{S}_{2}(j)\right)\;=\;0~.

Demanding that the coefficient of

\displaystyle x_{k}\;=\;\sum_{j=1}^{k}\eta_{k-j}\,\mathcal{S}_{2}(j)~.

This lends itself to an iterative solution as the left-hand side only involves
x_{j}
and
y_{j}
with

Next we use the

) to give

,

, and

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathfrak{p}\in\text{pt}(k)}N_{\mathfrak{p}}R^{x_{\,}}_{\mathfrak{p}}\;=\;\sum_{\mathfrak{p}\in\text{pt}(k)}N_{\mathfrak{p}}(\bm{\eta}\hskip 1.0pt{*}\hskip 1.0pt\mathcal{S}_{2})_{\mathfrak{p}}~.

and
R^{y_{\,}}_{\mathfrak{q}}

\displaystyle R^{x_{\,}}_{\mathfrak{p}}

The alignment equations

As discussed in §

\displaystyle\begin{split}x(x^{2}+y^{2})-\frac{\sigma x}{y}+\frac{3}{Y}\left(2\mathcal{I}_{1}-\frac{2x}{y}\mathcal{I}_{2}+\frac{x^{2}+y^{2}}{y}\mathcal{I}_{2}^{\prime}\right)-\beta^{3}\gamma&\;=\;0~,\\[10.0pt]

The perturbative equations

In this case, solving the perturbative equations reduces to finding the roots of a quartic recorded in (

It is optimal to work with the rescaled parameter

. We define also the rescaled coordinates

\widehat{x}\;=\;\frac{x_{0}}{\beta}~,\hskip 30.0pt\widehat{y}\;=\;\frac{y_{0}}{\beta}~.

\widehat{x}\widehat{y}-\gamma\widehat{y}-\widehat{\sigma}\widehat{x}\;=\;0~,\hskip 30.0pt\widehat{x}^{2}+\widehat{y}^{2}\;=\;1~.

(5.8)

The first equation above provides an expression for

\widehat{x}\;=\;\frac{\gamma\widehat{y}}{\widehat{y}-\widehat{\sigma}}~.

(5.9)

On substituting (

(\widehat{y}^{2}-1)\left(\widehat{y}-\widehat{\sigma}\right)^{2}+\widehat{y}^{2}\gamma^{2}\;=\;0~.

(5.10)

Some general remarks about this equation are in order. The highest term is
\widehat{y}^{4}
, and the constant term is
-\widehat{\sigma}^{2}
, so the product of the four roots of this equation is
-\widehat{\sigma}^{2}\leqslant 0
.

We are interested in solutions of (
5.10
) with positive positive imaginary part, which is necessary for them to lie in the large complex structure region, and how the number of these solutions varies as a function of
\widehat{\sigma}
. To any quartic polynomial with real coefficients is associated a discriminant
D_{4}
, which for our polynomial is

D_{4}\;=\;16\,\gamma^{2}\widehat{\sigma}^{2}\left(\left(1-\gamma^{2}-\widehat{\sigma}^{2}\right)^{3}-27\gamma^{2}\widehat{\sigma}^{2}\right).

When
D_{4}
is positive, the quartic has either four or no real roots, while when
D_{4}
is negative the quartic has precisely two real solutions.

When
|\gamma|\geqslant 1
, this discriminant is negative for all
\widehat{\sigma}
. So when
|\gamma|\geqslant 1
there are two real solutions to (
5.10
), and by our earlier observation that the product of the roots is
-\widehat{\sigma}^{2}
, precisely one of these is positive. So in the following we will restrict our attention to the case
|\gamma|=\cos\phi_{00}<1
. When
\widehat{\sigma}=0
, the quartic has four real roots. These are
\pm\sin\phi_{00}
and
0
, the latter with multiplicity two. When
\widehat{\sigma}
is small but non-vanishing, the solutions
\pm\sin\phi_{00}
are perturbed slightly and both of the previously vanishing solutions become nonzero.

Since a root can only vanish when
\widehat{\sigma}=0
, these new nonzero roots cannot change sign as
|\widehat{\sigma}|
increases. Furthermore, the four roots multiply together to give a negative number. Since there is always one positive and one negative root, this forces both newly nonzero roots to have the same sign. Solving to leading order in
\widehat{\sigma}
, these solutions are

\widehat{y}\;=\;\frac{\widehat{\sigma}}{1-\gamma}+\mathcal{O}\left(\widehat{\sigma}^{2}\right)~,\ \ \ \ \ \widehat{y}\;=\;\frac{\widehat{\sigma}}{1+\gamma}+\mathcal{O}\left(\widehat{\sigma}^{2}\right)~.

So, irrespective of the value of
\phi_{00}
, the newly nonzero solutions are both of the same sign as
\widehat{\sigma}
.

The sign of
D_{4}
is determined by the cubic

d(\phi_{00},\xi)\;\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits^{\rm def}}\;\left.\frac{D_{4}}{16\gamma^{2}\widehat{\sigma}^{2}}\right|_{\widehat{\sigma}^{2}=\xi}\;=\;\left(\sin^{2}\phi_{00}-\xi\right)^{3}-27\cos^{2}\phi_{00}\,\xi~.

For all
\phi_{00}\,{\in}\,(0,\pi)
, this quantity

is monotonically decreasing as a function of

. Such a monotonic cubic has one real root, which here is located at

\left(1-|\gamma|^{2/3}\right)^{3}\;\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits^{\rm def}}\;\Xi(\gamma)^{2}.

The sign of
D_{4}
changes when
\widehat{\sigma}^{2}=\Xi(\gamma)^{2}
.

When
\widehat{\sigma}\,{=}\,\left(1-|\gamma|^{2/3}\right)^{3/2}
, the solution flowing from
\sin\phi_{00}
meets the larger solution that vanishes at
\widehat{\sigma}=0
. For larger

they both become nonreal, and so lose relevance for us. The solution flowing from

\widehat{\sigma}
.

6
Solutions

In the previous sections, we have introduced the general formalism for solving attractor equations, which we will use in this section to study some important special cases for which this formalism undergoes considerable simplification. In particular, we find simple solutions to the orthogonality equation in the case of the D4-D2-D0 system, and for a special case of the alignment equations for the D6-D2-D0 system with
x=0
.

We first consider the D6-D2-D0 alignment equations in the case
x=0
. We find that the perturbative equations (
5.2
) do not, in fact, involve
\sigma
, so
y_{0}=\beta
, which is a great simplification compared to the analysis of §
5.2
. The recurrence relations for
R^{y}_{\mathfrak{p}}
have also a relatively simple form.

The slightly more involved D4-D2-D0 orthogonality equation is also studied. We can solve the attractor equations in this case without any additional assumptions. These solutions have a similar form to those of the D6-D2-D0 case, which can be understood by examining intersections of linear subspaces with the charge lattice.

One of the most interesting consequences of these solutions is that we are able to give a systematic expansion, including the instanton corrections, for the central charge and thus for the entropy of the black holes corresponding to the aforementioned systems.

Although our results are not limited to rank two attractor points, an important class of concrete examples are provided by the rank two attractor points found in
[
15
]
. We briefly review some results on these points before verifying that our method reproduces these from a different point of view. The intriguing number theoretic properties of the rank two attractor points provide us with an interesting identity (
6.10
) connecting Bessel functions and Gromov-Witten invariants to L-functions.

\displaystyle f_{s}(z)\;=\;\sum_{j=0}^{s}\frac{(2s-j)!}{2^{s-j}(s-j)!j!}\,z^{j}~.

\displaystyle f_{s}(y)

\displaystyle f_{s}(z)

\displaystyle\;=\;z^{2}f_{s-2}(z)+(2s-1)f_{s-1}(z)~.

(6.6)

(6.7)

We can explain the striking similarity of the situation we have just discussed to the alignment equations in the D6-D2-D0 case by inspecting the period vector in the special case

7
Applications

To conclude, we give here two simple applications of the formulae we have found and take the opportunity to explore some of their qualitative properties. The first application is to classify the attractor points into those with vanishing and non-vanishing central charges, based on the values of the black hole charge ratios
\Upsilon
and
\Lambda
. These two types of attractor points have distinct physical interpretations: It is conjectured that only the attractor points with
|\mathcal{Z}|\neq 0
have corresponding BPS states in the full quantum theory of gravity
[
4
,
40
,
41
,
3
,
2
]
, while the attractor points with
|\mathcal{Z}|=0
are related to supersymmetric flux compactifications
[
42
,
43
]
. In addition, knowing which charges have an attractor point with vanishing central charge can be useful for analysing split attractor flows
[
4
,
48
]
.

Another, more speculative, application arises from comparing the entropy formulae derived in the previous sections to the counting of microstates. We argue that the correct interpretation is to consider them the leading order contributions in the large charge expansion. The large complex structure limit is interesting for analysing the microscopic description of the black holes, as in this region the black holes can be thought of as a bound state of D-branes
[
56
]
. These branes, in turn, can be realised as divisors, curves or points on the Calabi-Yau manifold. The microstate indices can then be given by the Witten indices of moduli spaces of these curves, which appear as coefficients of a modified elliptic genus
[
45
,
46
]
.

Here we show that our formulae, which converge rapidly in the appropriate large charge ratio limits, also reproduce the correct leading order expressions in the large charge limit, so reproducing the well-known results of
[
17
,
9
]
in the regime where these results are simultaneously valid. More interesting, however, is the fact that our expressions provide a systematic expansion that includes the perturbative corrections in
\sigma
and the exponentially small instanton contributions. It is intriguing to ponder on how this expansion relates to the celebrated Rademacher expansion
[
57
]
, which provides expressions for the coefficients of modular forms from which asymptotics can be read off. We hope to return elsewhere to the relation between these expansions, which we do not attempt to match here.

7.1
Classification of attractor points

We have been considering the
N
-expansions relating to the D6-D2-D0 and D4-D2-D0 systems, and have assumed that the value of
y_{0}
is large enough for the
N
-expansions to converge. Recall that each such a solution devolves from either a solution of the alignment or orthogonality equations. Moreover, every solution to the perturbative equations, with large enough
y_{0}
, leads to an associated solution to the fully instanton-corrected equations, having
t_{0}
as a zeroth-order term. From these considerations, we can draw a conclusion: the central charge vanishes if and only if
t_{0}
is a perturbative solution to the orthogonality equation.

The classification of attractor points into those with vanishing and non-vanishing central charges is of central importance if the BPS state existence conjecture holds
[
4
,
40
,
41
,
3
,
2
]
. As discussed in greater detail in §
1.1
, the conjecture states that the existence of BPS states in the full quantum theory of gravity can be deduced from the existence of attractor flows.

We concentrate on the case where at least one of the charge ratios
\Lambda
or
\Upsilon
is large, and further
\big||\Upsilon|-|\Lambda|\big|
is also large. In this regime we inevitably approach the large complex structure limit, which allows us to make statements that are true for any one-parameter family. We are then able to derive simple conditions for existence of attractor points with large complex structure. However, the fact that we are limited to the large complex structure region has the consequence that we cannot make meaningful statements about flows that terminate elsewhere.

We should point out an important complication due to split attractor flows. These are flows which split into multiple branches
[
47
,
40
,
41
,
58
,
4
]
, due to the existence of multi-centred black holes. Owing to both the existence of split flows and the possible existence of different basins of attraction for single attractor flows, the results here do not completely determine the BPS spectrum of one-parameter Calabi-Yau manifolds, even in the large charge ratio limits. For example, even if an attractor point with a non-vanishing central charge exists near the large complex structure point, the basin of attraction for this point is not necessarily the whole large complex structure region; there could be another basin of attraction that flows to an attractor point outside the large complex structure region. Such a point does not enter our classification here. Even within the basin of attraction of an attractor point included in the classification below, there can exist split flows with at least one branch ending at an attractor point that does not lie within the large complex structure region.

7.2
Comparison to microscopic entropy

The attractor points we have considered are related to solutions to the two-derivative
\mathcal{N}{\;=\;}2
,
d{\;=\;}4
supergravity action, with the prepotential appearing in the supergravity theory identified with the prepotential of the Calabi-Yau manifold. However, in general, the effective actions of string- and M-theories have an infinite series of higher-derivative terms. A subset of these corrections
[
59
]
are determined by a holomorphic function
\mathcal{F}(z,W^{2})
, called the
all-genus prepotential
, which depends on not only on the vector multiplet scalars
z
, but also on the Weyl multiplet, which is a chiral multiplet

(for details, see for example
[
24
]
).

The function
\mathpzc F(z,W^{2})
can be expanded as a power series in
W^{2}
:

\displaystyle\mathpzc F(z,W^{2})

\displaystyle\;=\;\sum_{g=0}^{\infty}\mathcal{F}^{(g)}(z)W^{2g}~,

(7.3)

with
\mathcal{F}^{(g)}(z)
identified as the
genus
g
prepotential
. In particular, the genus 0 term
\mathcal{F}^{(0)}(z)
is the prepotential
\mathcal{F}(z)
that we have been using thus far.

An important point is that the all-genus prepotential has the homogeneity property

\displaystyle\mathpzc F(\lambda z,\lambda^{2}W^{2})\;=\;\lambda^{2}\mathpzc F(z,W^{2})~.

(7.4)

From the homogeneity property (
7.4
) of the all-genus prepotential and the expansion (
7.3
), it follows that

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{(g)}(\lambda z)\;=\;\lambda^{2-2g}\mathcal{F}^{(g)}(z)~.

In
[
24
]
, it is argued that to take the higher-genus corrections into account, the alignment equations in (
1.16
) have to be modified to take the form

\displaystyle Q\;=\;\left(\begin{array}[]{c}q_{0}\\
q_{\phantom{0}}\\
p^{0}\\
p^{\phantom{0}}\end{array}\right)\;=\;\text{Im}\left[C\begin{pmatrix}\partial_{0}\mathpzc F(z,W^{2})\\
\partial_{1}\mathpzc F(z,W^{2})\\
z^{0}\\
z^{1}\end{pmatrix}\right],

where

\displaystyle C\;=\;2\text{e}^{\mathpzc K/2}\,\overline{\mathcal{Z}}~,

with
\mathpzc K
an all-genus Kähler potential, which generalises the genus zero Kähler potential of special geometry:

\displaystyle\text{e}^{-\mathpzc K}\;\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits^{\rm def}}\;\text{i}\left(\overline{z}^{a}\partial_{a}\mathpzc F(z,W^{2})-z^{a}\overline{\partial_{a}\mathpzc F(z,W^{2})}\right).

The authors of
[

show that by defining the rescaled variables

\displaystyle\widetilde{Z}^{a}\;=\;Cz^{a},\qquad\widetilde{W}^{2}\;=\;C^{2}W^{2},

the equations (

) become simply

\displaystyle Q\;=\;2\,\text{Im}\begin{pmatrix}\partial_{0}\mathpzc F(\widetilde{Z},\widetilde{W}^{2})\\[3.0pt]
\partial_{1}\mathpzc F(\widetilde{Z},\widetilde{W}^{2})\\[3.0pt]
\widetilde{Z}^{0}\\[3.0pt]
\widetilde{Z}^{1}\end{pmatrix},\qquad\widetilde{W}^{2}\;=\;-64~.

From this it follows that if we make replacement

\text{Im}\,\widetilde{Z}^{a}\to\lambda\,\text{Im}\,\widetilde{Z}^{a}
to preserve equality. The real parts must be chosen to satisfy the two remaining equations. By using the explicit expressions for the polynomial part of
\mathcal{F}
, it can be shown that for large real

\text{Re}\,\widetilde{Z}^{a}\to\lambda\,\text{Re}\,\widetilde{Z}^{a}

\displaystyle\mathpzc F(\lambda\widetilde{Z},\widetilde{W}^{2})\;=\;\sum_{g=0}^{\infty}\lambda^{2-2g}\mathcal{F}(\widetilde{Z})\widetilde{W}^{2g}~,

Using this and going back to the original unscaled variables, the stabilisation equations can be written in the large charge limit as

\displaystyle Q\;=\;\text{Im}\left[C\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\partial}{\partial z^{0}}\mathcal{F}^{(0)}\\[5.0pt]

This observation is important not only for the consistency, but also a key to relating our results for the central charge to the microscopic entropy of a black hole. In
[

\displaystyle S_{\text{Wald}}\;=\;\pi|\mathcal{Z}|^{2}-256\pi\,\text{Im}\left[\frac{\partial\mathpzc F(Z,W^{2})}{\partial W^{2}}\right],\qquad\text{with}\qquad W^{2}=-64\overline{\mathcal{Z}}^{-2}\text{e}^{-\mathpzc K}~.

,
5
]

\displaystyle S_{\text{Wald}}\;\sim\;\pi|\mathcal{Z}|^{2}~.

If we assume that the microscopic entropy should be compared to the Wald entropy, as computed in supergravity, an assumption supported by
[

\sigma

The monodromy matrix M is symplectic and the central charge depends on the period and charge vectors only through the symplectic invariant inner product, so the central charge is invariant under the monodromy. It follows that if
t
is an attractor point corresponding to a charge vector
Q^{(0)}
, then
t+n
is the coordinate of an attractor point corresponding to the charge vector
Q^{(n)}
. In virtue of this, the entire discussion of the D6 system in §
5
can be extended to include a restricted set of D4 charges.

B.1
D4-D2-D0 system

The monodromy preserves
p^{0}
, and so in particular the condition
p^{0}=0
. In this case the monodromy can be thought of as acting on the charge ratios as

\displaystyle\Lambda^{(n)}\;=\;\Lambda^{(0)}+\frac{n^{2}}{2}Y-n\left(Y_{110}+\Upsilon^{(0)}\right),\qquad\Upsilon^{(n)}\;=\;\Upsilon^{(0)}-nY~.

Since we have in §
4.2
found an expression for the coordinates
x,y
of the attractor point in terms of these charges, it is interesting to follow how this transformation gives rise to the coordinate transformation
x\to x+n
,
y\to y
. First note that it follows from the transformation above that the quantities
\alpha
and
\widetilde{\beta}
transform so that

\displaystyle\widetilde{\beta}\;\to\;\sqrt{\widetilde{\beta}^{2}+n^{2}+2n\alpha\widetilde{\beta}}~,\qquad\alpha\widetilde{\beta}\;\to\;\alpha\widetilde{\beta}+n~.

It follows that
\widetilde{\beta}\sqrt{\alpha^{2}-1}
is invariant. Thus the perturbative coordinates transform as

\displaystyle x_{0}\;=\;\alpha\widetilde{\beta}\;\to\;\alpha\widetilde{\beta}+n\;=\;x_{0}+n~,\qquad y_{0}\;=\;2\widetilde{\beta}\sqrt{\alpha^{2}-1}\sin\theta\;\to\;y_{0}~.

To see that
\theta
is invariant under the monodromy, note that this is required by the definition (
4.9
), together with the above transformation laws.

The instanton corrections are not expected to modify these transformations and this is indeed the case as
R_{\mathfrak{p}}^{x_{\,}}
and
R_{\mathfrak{p}}^{y_{\,}}
depend on
x_{0}
only via
\cos(2m\pi x_{0})
or
\sin(2m\pi x_{0})
with
m\in\mathbbl{Z}
.

B.2
D6-D2-D0 system

A charge vector with
p\;=\;0
maps in general to a vector
p\;\neq\;0
. The charge ratios transform as

\displaystyle\Lambda^{(n)}\;=\;\Lambda^{(0)}+\frac{n^{3}}{6}Y-n(\Upsilon^{(0)}-Y_{100})~,\qquad\Upsilon^{(n)}\;=\;\Upsilon^{(0)}-\frac{n^{2}}{2}Y-nY_{110}~.

Thus, by choosing
\Lambda^{(0)}
and
\Upsilon^{(0)}
suitably, we can always have arbitrary values for the first two components. The third component is kept fixed, and the last component is an integer multiple of
p^{0}
, the value of the third component. Conversely, given any charge of this form, we can always find a monodromy that transforms it into the form where

. So the solutions in §
5
relate to charge vectors of the form

\displaystyle Q\;=\;\left(\begin{matrix}q_{0}\\
q\\
p^{0}\\
p\end{matrix}\right),\qquad\text{with}\enskip p^{0}|p\hskip 3.0pt\text{ or }p^{0}\;=\;0~.

Appendix C
Microscopic Charge Vector

The discussion above has been cast in terms of the charge vector
Q
(defined by (
1.10

\displaystyle\widetilde{Q}

\displaystyle\;=\;\begin{pmatrix}\phantom{-}\widetilde{q}_{0}\\
-\widetilde{q}_{\phantom{0}}\\
\phantom{-}\widetilde{p}^{0}\\
-\widetilde{p}^{\phantom{0}}\end{pmatrix}.

The charges that appear here correspond to the couplings to the RR-potentials in the D-brane world-volume action. Consider first the D6 brane with a world-volume flux
F
, the 2-form field
B
and RR-potentials

,

\displaystyle S_{\text{WZ}}

\displaystyle\;=\;\int_{X}C\wedge\text{e}^{F-B}\wedge\sqrt{\widehat{A}(\widetilde{X})}~,

where

is a multi-form built out of the RR-potentials,

. We combine the world-volume flux and B-field into a single tensor
\mathcal{F}=F-B

\displaystyle\frac{\delta S_{\text{WZ}}}{\delta C^{2n+1}}

\displaystyle\;=\;\left[\;\text{e}^{\mathcal{F}}\wedge\sqrt{\widehat{A}(\widetilde{X})}\;\right]_{6-2n}\hskip-5.0pt=\quad\left[\left(1+\mathcal{F}+\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{F}^{2}+\frac{1}{6}\mathcal{F}^{3}\right)\wedge\left(1+\frac{c_{2}(\widetilde{X})}{24}\right)\right]_{6-2n},

denotes the
(6-2n)
-form part of the multiform. We can combine all these parts into a microscopic charge multiform

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Gamma}

\displaystyle\;=\;\sum_{n=0}^{3}\frac{\delta S_{\text{WZ}}}{\delta C^{2n+1}}\;=\;\text{e}^{\mathcal{F}}\wedge\sqrt{\widehat{A}(\widetilde{X})}~.

To write this in a convenient form, we set
\mathcal{F}=fe_{1}
, where

is a scalar, and
e_{1}
generates

. Thus we can write the charge form as

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Gamma}

\displaystyle\;=\;1+fe_{1}+\left(\frac{1}{2}f^{2}+\frac{c_{2}}{24}\right)e_{1}^{2}+\left(\frac{1}{6}f^{3}+\frac{c_{2}}{24}f\right)e_{1}^{3}~.

\displaystyle\mho\;=\;\text{e}^{B+\text{i}J}\;=\;\text{e}^{te_{1}}\;=\;1+t\,e_{1}+\frac{1}{2}t^{2}\,e_{1}^{2}+\frac{1}{6}t^{3}\,e_{1}^{3}~.

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Gamma}\cdot\mho\;=\;\int_{\widetilde{X}}\widetilde{\Gamma}\wedge\mho^{*}~,

\mho^{*}\;=\;\text{e}^{-te_{1}}

Alternatively, we can identify the components of the vectors
\widetilde{Q}
and
\widetilde{\Pi}

and

\displaystyle\widetilde{Q}\;=\;\begin{pmatrix}\phantom{-}\,\,\,\,\frac{1}{6}Yf^{3}-\frac{Y_{100}}{2}f\,\\[3.0pt]

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Gamma}\cdot\mho\;=\;\widetilde{Q}^{T}\Sigma\widetilde{\Pi}~.

As a simple consistency check, we note that this is invariant under the monodromy

and

as then

. Note that this is the action of the monodromy despite the fact that

To find the relation between the microscopic charge vector

\displaystyle\Pi_{0}\big|_{\sigma=0}\;=\;\text{T}_{\Pi}\widetilde{\Pi}~,\qquad\text{with}\qquad\text{T}_{\Pi}\;=\;\begin{pmatrix}1&\phantom{-}0&0&-\frac{1}{2}Y_{100}\\[3.0pt]

\displaystyle\widetilde{Q}\;=\;\text{T}^{-1}_{Q}Q~,\qquad\text{with}\qquad\text{T}_{Q}\;=\;\left(\Sigma^{-1}\text{T}_{\Pi}^{-1}\Sigma\right)^{T}\;=\;\begin{pmatrix}1&\phantom{-}0&0&\frac{1}{2}Y_{100}\\[3.0pt]

into sums over partitions of different length. This gives a further bound

\displaystyle S(y_{0})\;\leqslant\;\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\sum_{l=1}^{j}|\text{pt}_{l}(j)|\max\left(a_{j,l}\right)\left(\frac{j}{2\pi y_{0}Y}\right)^{l}\bm{k}_{l-1}(2\pi jy_{0})\,\text{e}^{\frac{j}{w}\log N_{w}}~,

where
\max\left(a_{j,l}\right)
is the maximal value
a_{\mathfrak{p}}
takes over
\text{pt}_{l}(j)
, and
|\text{pt}_{l}(j)|
is the number of partitions of
j
of length
l
. Ramanujan’s asymptotic formula for
|\text{pt}(j)|
tells us that

\displaystyle|\text{pt}_{l}(j)|\;\leqslant|\text{pt}(j)|\;<\text{e}^{\pi\sqrt{\frac{2}{3}j}}~.

This gives

\displaystyle S(y_{0})\;\leqslant\;\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\sum_{l=1}^{j}\max\left(a_{j,l}\right)\left(\frac{j}{2\pi y_{0}Y}\right)^{l}\bm{k}_{l-1}(2\pi jy_{0})\text{e}^{\frac{j}{w}\log N_{w}+\pi\sqrt{\frac{2}{3}j}}~.

For
j>L
, we break the sum over
l
into two parts, one from
1
to
L
and the other from
L+1
to
j
. We can then apply the bounds (
F.2
) and (
F.3
), allowing us to give another upper bound:

\displaystyle S(y_{0})\;\leqslant\;C_{2}+\sum_{j=L+1}^{\infty}\left(P(j)+C_{1}\sum_{l=L+1}^{j}\left(\frac{\text{e}\pi j(1+\sqrt{2})}{12y_{0}Yl}\right)^{l}\right)\text{e}^{-j\left(2\pi y_{0}-\frac{1}{w}\log N_{w}\right)+\pi\sqrt{\frac{2}{3}j}}\;,

where
P(j)
is a polynomial of degree
L
in
j
and
C_{2}
is a constant corresponding to a finite sum over
j
from
1
to
L
. To understand the appearance of
P(j)
, recall the relation between
\bm{k}
and the polynomials
f
as in (
6.4
). The summand in the second sum over
l
is maximised when

\displaystyle l\;=\;j\frac{\pi(1+\sqrt{2})}{12y_{0}Y}~.

At this point the summand takes the value

\displaystyle\text{e}^{j\frac{\pi(1+\sqrt{2})}{12y_{0}Y}}~.

Therefore we have an upper bound

\displaystyle S(y_{0})\;\leqslant\;C_{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\left(P(j)+C_{1}j\text{e}^{j\frac{\pi(1+\sqrt{2})}{12y_{0}Y}}\right)\text{e}^{-j\left(2\pi y_{0}-\frac{1}{w}\log N_{w}\right)+\pi\sqrt{\frac{2}{3}j}}\;,

and the sum converges when

\displaystyle 2\pi y_{0}-\frac{1}{w}\log N_{w}-\frac{\pi(1+\sqrt{2})}{12Yy_{0}}\;>\;0~.

This gives a sufficient condition for the sums in our solutions, such as (
6.6
), to converge. However, this bound can be improved.

For the case of AESZ34, the above bound is
y_{0}>0.4442
, while the numerical value of
y_{0}
at the rank two attractor point is
y_{0}=\frac{\sqrt{15}}{6}\approx 0.64
.

F.1
Bound for
a_{\mathfrak{p}}

We seek a sufficiently strong bound on
a_{\mathfrak{p}}
, for partitions
\mathfrak{p}
of a fixed length
l
. To this end, we begin with the definition

\displaystyle\frac{1}{a_{\mathfrak{p}}}\;=\;\prod_{j=1}^{\infty}\mu_{j}!\;j^{2\mu_{j}}~.

For positive integers
n
, one has that
n!\geq\sqrt{2\pi}n^{n+1/2}\text{e}^{-n}
. Hence

\displaystyle\frac{1}{a_{\mathfrak{p}}}\;\geqslant\;\prod_{j|\mu_{j}\neq 0}\sqrt{2\pi}\mu_{j}^{\mu_{j}+1/2}\text{e}^{-\mu_{j}}\;j^{2\mu_{j}}\;=\;\text{e}^{-l}\prod_{j|\mu_{j}\neq 0}\sqrt{2\pi}\mu_{j}^{\mu_{j}+1/2}\;j^{2\mu_{j}}~,

wherein we have used that
\sum_{j}\mu_{j}=l
. Because the
\mu_{j}
are integers, we can write

\displaystyle\frac{1}{a_{\mathfrak{p}}}\;\geqslant\;\text{e}^{-l}\prod_{j=1}^{l}\mu_{j}^{\mu_{j}}\;j^{2\mu_{j}}~,

where
0^{0}
is to be interpreted as
1
. We need only concern ourselves with
\mu_{j}
where
j\in\{1,...,l\}
, since
\mu_{k}
must vanish for
k>l
. On the space
\{\mu_{j}\geq 0,\;\sum_{j}\mu_{j}=l\}
, the above function has a single global minimum. One can determine the minimising
\mu_{j}
by using a Lagrange multiplier
\lambda
:

\displaystyle\partial_{\mu_{i}}\left[\left(\prod_{j=1}^{l}\mu_{j}^{\mu_{j}}\;j^{2\mu_{j}}\right)-\lambda\sum_{j=1}^{l}\mu_{j}\right]\;=\;0~.

These relations are solved by
\mu_{j}=Cj^{-2}
, where
C
is a constant. Of course, these extremising
\mu_{j}
are not necessarily integers, but the result suffices for deriving a bound. Taking a logarithm,

\displaystyle\log\frac{1}{a_{\mathfrak{p}}}\;\geqslant\;-l+\sum_{j=1}^{l}\,\left(\frac{C}{j^{2}}\,\log\frac{C}{j^{2}}+2\,\frac{C}{j^{2}}\,\log j\right)\;=\;-l+(\log C)\sum_{j=1}^{l}\frac{C}{j^{2}}\;=\;-l+l\,\log C~,

where the relation
\sum_{j}Cj^{-2}=l
was employed. Upon recognising the Basel sum, we can write
C>\frac{6l}{\pi^{2}}
. Therefore

\displaystyle\log\frac{1}{a_{\mathfrak{p}}}\;\geqslant\;l\left(-1+\log\frac{6}{\pi^{2}}\right)+l\log l~.

And so, as was required,

\displaystyle a_{\mathfrak{p}}\;\leqslant\;\left(\frac{\,\text{e}\pi^{2}}{6l}\right)^{l}~.
