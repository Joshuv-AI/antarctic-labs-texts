Discontinuous stationary solutions to certain reaction-diffusion systems
Szymon Cygan ID, Anna Marciniak-Czochra ID, Grzegorz Karch ID

Abstract
. Systems consisting of a single ordinary differential equation coupled with one reaction-diffusion equation in a bounded domain and with the Neumann boundary conditions are studied in the case of particular nonlinearities from the Brusselator model, the Gray-Scott model, the Oregonator model and a certain predator-prey model. It is shown that the considered systems have the both smooth and discontinuous stationary solutions, however, only discontinuous ones can be stable.

35K57; 35B35; 35B36; 92C15

1.
Introduction

We discuss stationary solutions to certain particular systems consisting of a single ordinary differential equation coupled with one reaction-diffusion equation:

(1.1)

\displaystyle u_{t}

\displaystyle=f(u,v),

\displaystyle x\in\overline{\Omega},\quad t>0,

\displaystyle v_{t}

\displaystyle=\gamma\Delta v+g(u,v),

\displaystyle x\in\Omega,\quad t>0,

with unknown functions
u=u(x,t)
and
v=v(x,t)
.
System (
1.1
) is considered in a bounded, open domain

\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
with
the

C^{2}
-boundary
\partial\Omega
.
Moreover, the reaction-diffusion equation in (
1.1
) is
supplemented with the Neumann boundary condition

(1.2)

\displaystyle\partial_{\nu}v=0\qquad\text{for}\quad x\in\partial\Omega,\quad t>0,

where
\partial_{\nu}=\nu\cdot\nabla
with
\nu
denoting the unit
outer normal vector to
\partial\Omega
. The letter
\gamma>0
is a constant diffusion coefficient.
In the following, we denote by
\Delta_{\nu}
the Laplacian operator together with the Neumann boundary condition (
1.2
).

Particular versions of such a system have been studied,
e.g.
, in the works

[
18
,
17
,
23
,
12
,
24
,
21
,
11
,
20
,
22
,
8
,
16
,
13
,
25
,
30
,
31
,
35
]
where the following two types of stationary solutions
\big(U(x),V(x)\big)
were identified:

(1)

Regular stationary solutions
, where

U(x)=k\big(V(x)\big)
for one
C^{2}
-function
k
and all
x\in\overline{\Omega}
.

(2)

Jump-discontinuous stationary solutions
with
U(x)
obtained using different branches of solutions to the equation
f(U,V)=0
with respect to
U
.

Regular stationary solutions to general problem (
1.1
)-(
1.2
)
have been investigated recently in the paper
[
2
]
showing that
they
all
are unstable. The second work
[
3
]
is devoted to the second type stationary solutions with jump-discontinuities and provides sufficient conditions for their existence and stability.
The theory developed in both papers
[
2
,
3
]

can be applied to several reaction-diffusion-ODE models in spatially homogeneous environments arising from applications as
e.g.
studied in

[
11
,

,

,

,

,

,

,

,

,

,

]
.

In this work, we explain how to apply general results from the papers
[
2
,
3
]
to systems of
type
(
1.1
) with particular well-known nonlinearities which appear in Turing models. Recall that a system of reaction-diffusion equations

(1.3)

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}u_{t}=\varepsilon\Delta u+f(u,v),\qquad v_{t}=\gamma\Delta v+g(u,v)}

(in a bounded domain and with the Neumann boundary condition) exhibits a Turing instability if it has a constant stationary solution which is stable if
\varepsilon=\gamma=0
and unstable for
\varepsilon>0
and
\gamma>0
.
In his work
[
32
]
,
Turing showed that this kind of instability may occurs in some particular reaction-diffusion systems with
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small. Due to different nonzero diffusion
rates, the system gives rise spontaneously to stationary pattern formation with a characteristic
length scale from an initial configuration (see
e.g.

[
34
]
and the references therein).

In this work, we apply
the
theory from the papers
[
2
,
3
]
to study nonconstant stationary solutions of some reaction-diffusion models with the Turing instability in the diffusion degenerated case, namely, when
\varepsilon=0
and
\gamma>0
.
In Section
3
, we consider the diffusion-degenerated Gray-Scott model

(1.4)

\displaystyle u_{t}=u^{2}v-\alpha u,\qquad v_{t}=\gamma\Delta_{\nu}v-u^{2}v+\beta(1-v)

and the diffusion-degenerated Brusselator system

(1.5)

\displaystyle u_{t}=\alpha+u^{2}v-(\beta+1)u,\qquad v_{t}=\gamma\Delta_{\nu}v+\beta u-u^{2}v.

By applying results from
[
2
,
3
]
, we obtain these two
systems
(considered in bounded domains) have stationary solutions of both types: regular and discontinuous.
However, their all nonconstant stationary solutions are unstable.

Next, in Section
4
, we discuss two other models: the diffusion-degenerated Oregonator

(1.6)

\displaystyle u_{t}=u-u^{2}+\alpha v\frac{\beta-u}{\beta+u},\qquad v_{t}=\gamma\Delta_{\nu}v+u-v

and a certain kind of predator-prey system

(1.7)

\displaystyle u_{t}=u\left(\frac{u^{2}}{u^{3}+1}-v\right),\qquad v_{t}=\gamma\Delta_{\nu}v+v(\alpha u-v-\beta).

In the following, we show how to use theory from the work
[
3
]
to construct
stable discontinuous stationary solutions
of these two systems.

2.
General results

Here, we review briefly results from
[
2
,
3
]
formulating them in a form which is suitable to deal with a system consisting of one ODE coupled with one reaction-diffusion equation.

We deal with
a weak solution

(U,V)=\big(U(x),V(x)\big)

to the boundary value problem

(2.1)

\displaystyle f(U,V)

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle x\in\overline{\Omega},

\displaystyle\gamma\Delta_{\nu}V+g(U,V)

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle x\in\Omega,

with arbitrary
C^{2}
-functions
f
and
g
, with constant
\gamma>0
, and
in an
open bounded domain
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N}
with a
C^{2}
-boundary.
Recall (
cf.

[
2
,
3
]
) that a weak solution has the following properties:
U\in{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}
,
V\in{W^{1,2}(\Omega)}
, first equation in (
2.1
) holds true almost everywhere and the second equation is satisfied in
the
usual weak sense in the
Sobolev space

{W^{1,2}(\Omega)}
.

We require that problem (
2.1
) has a constant solution, namely, a
constant vector

(2.2)

\displaystyle(\overline{U},\overline{V})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\quad\text{such that}\quad f(\overline{U},\overline{V})=0\quad\text{and}\quad g(\overline{U},\overline{V})=0.

In this work, we use the following notation

(2.3)

\displaystyle a_{0}=f_{u}\left(\overline{U},\overline{V}\right),\quad b_{0}=f_{v}\left(\overline{U},\overline{V}\right),\quad c_{0}=g_{u}\left(\overline{U},\overline{V}\right),\quad d_{0}=g_{v}\left(\overline{U},\overline{V}\right)

and we always impose the following assumption.

Assumption 2.1
.

We assume that
a_{0}=f_{u}(\overline{U},\overline{V})\neq 0
.

2.1.
Regular stationary solutions

A solution
\big(U(x),V(x)\big)
of system (
2.1
) is called regular if there exists a
C^{2}
-function
k:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
such that
U(x)=k(V(x))
for all
x\in\Omega
and

(2.4)

\displaystyle f\big(U(x),V(x)\big)=f\big(k(V(x)),V(x)\big)=0\quad\text{for all}\quad x\in\Omega,

where
V=V(x)
is a solution of the elliptic Neumann problem

(2.5)

\displaystyle\gamma\Delta_{\nu}V+h(V)=0\qquad\text{for}\quad x\in\Omega

with
h(V)=g\big(k(V),V\big)
.
Now,
we recall a result from work
[
2
]
on the existence of regular stationary solutions in the case of one ODE coupled with one reaction-diffusion equation.

Proposition 2.2

(
[
3
, Prop. 2.5]
)
.

Let
N\leqslant 6
and let
(\overline{U},\overline{V})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a constant solution of problem (
2.1
) satisfying Assumption
2.1
. If

(2.6)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{a_{0}}\det\begin{pmatrix}a_{0}&b_{0}\\
c_{0}&d_{0}\end{pmatrix}=\gamma\mu_{k}>0,

for some
\mu_{k}
eigenvalues of
-\Delta_{\nu}
, then there exists a sequence of real numbers
d_{\ell}\to 1
such that the following “perturbed” problem

(2.7)

\displaystyle f(U,V)=0,

\displaystyle x\in\overline{\Omega},

\displaystyle d_{\ell}\Delta_{\nu}V+(1-d_{\ell})(V-\overline{V})+g(U,V)=0,

\displaystyle x\in\Omega

has a non-constant regular solution.

All regular stationary solutions are
unstable,
except the degenerate case when
f_{u}\big(U(x),V(x)\big)\leqslant 0
for all
x\in\overline{\Omega}
and
f_{u}\big(U(x_{0}),V(x_{0})\big)=0
for some
x_{0}\in\overline{\Omega}
, see the following theorem.

Theorem 2.3

(
[
2
, Thm. 2.7 and Thm. 2.8]
)
.

Let
(U,V)=\big(U(x),V(x)\big)
be a non-constant regular stationary solution of problem (
1.1
)-(
1.2
) such that

•

either
f_{u}\big(U(x_{0}),\,V(x_{0})\big)>0
for some
x_{0}\in\overline{\Omega}
,

•

or
f_{u}\big(U(x),V(x)\big)<0
for all
x\in\overline{\Omega}
and the domain
\Omega
is convex.

Then
(U,V)
is nonlinearly unstable.

2.2.
Discontinuous solutions

Now, we impose additional assumptions
on a constant stationary solution using notation (
2.3
).

Assumption 2.4
.

The constant solution
(\overline{U},\overline{V})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
of problem (
2.1
) satisfies

(2.8)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{a_{0}}\det\begin{pmatrix}a_{0}&b_{0}\\
c_{0}&d_{0}\end{pmatrix}\neq\gamma\mu_{k},

for each eigenvalue
\mu_{k}
of
-\Delta_{\nu}
.

Notice that if
a_{0}=f_{u}\left(\overline{U},\overline{V}\right)\neq 0
, by the Implicit mapping theorem, there exist an open neighborhood
\mathcal{V}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
of
\overline{V}
and a function
k\in C^{2}(\mathcal{V},\mathbb{R})
such that

(2.9)

\displaystyle k(\overline{V})=\overline{U}\quad\text{and}\quad f(k(w),w)=0\quad\text{for all }w\in\mathcal{V}.

In the following assumption, the equation
f(U,V)=0
is required to have
two different branches of solutions
with respect to
U
on a common domain
\mathcal{V}
.

Assumption 2.5
.

We assume that there exists an open set
\mathcal{V}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
and
k_{1},k_{2}\in C^{2}(\mathcal{V},\mathbb{R})
such that

•

\overline{V}\in\mathcal{V}
,

•

\overline{U}=k_{1}(\overline{V}),\quad k_{1}(\overline{V})\neq k_{2}(\overline{V})
,

•

f\big(k_{1}(w),w)\big)=f\big(k_{2}(w),w)\big)=0\ \text{for all}\ w\in\mathcal{V}.

Such two branches
k_{1},k_{2}
allow us to construct discontinuous solution of problem (
2.1
).

Theorem 2.6

(Existence of discontinuous stationary solutions
[
3
, Thm. 2.6]
)
.

Assume that

•

problem (
2.1
) has a constant solution
(\overline{U},\overline{V})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
satisfying
Assumptions

2.1
and
2.4
,

•

Assumption

2.5
holds true,

•

for an arbitrary open set
\Omega_{1}\subset\Omega
we put
\Omega_{2}=\Omega\setminus\overline{\Omega}_{1}

and the sets
\Omega_{1}
and
\Omega_{2}
have nonzero Lesbegue measures.

There is
\varepsilon_{0}>0
such that for all
\varepsilon\in(0,\,\varepsilon_{0})
there exists
\delta>0
such that if
|\Omega_{2}|<\delta

then problem (
2.1
) has a weak solution with the following properties:

•

(U,V)\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)\times{W^{2,p}_{\nu}(\Omega)}
for each
p\in[2,\infty)
,

•

V=V(x)
is a weak solution to problem

(2.10)

\displaystyle\gamma\Delta_{\nu}V+g(U,V)=0\quad\text{for}\quad x\in\Omega,

where

(2.11)

\displaystyle U(x)=\begin{cases}k_{1}\big(V(x)\big),\quad x\in\Omega_{1},\\
k_{2}\big(V(x)\big),\quad x\in\Omega_{2},\end{cases}

satisfies
f\big(U(x),V(x)\big)=0
for almost all
x\in\overline{\Omega}
.

•

the solution
(U,V)
stays around the points
(\overline{U},\overline{V})
and

\big(k_{2}(\overline{V}),\overline{V}\big)
in the following sense

(2.12)

\displaystyle\|V-\overline{V}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}<\varepsilon\quad\text{and}\quad\|U-\overline{U}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega_{1})}+\|U-k_{2}(\overline{V})\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega_{2})}<C\varepsilon

for a constant
C=C(f,g)
.

Next, we discuss a stability of discontinuous stationary solutions by the linearization procedure. Here, we say that
(U,V)
is
linearly stable (unstable)
if the zero solution to the following system

(2.13)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\begin{pmatrix}\varphi\\
\psi\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
\gamma\Delta_{\nu}\psi\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\varphi\\
\psi\end{pmatrix}\equiv\mathcal{L}_{p}\begin{pmatrix}\varphi\\
\psi\end{pmatrix}

with the bounded (and possibly
x
-dependent) coefficients

(2.14)

\displaystyle a=f_{u}\left({U},{V}\right),\quad b=f_{v}\left({U},{V}\right),\quad c=g_{u}\left({U},{V}\right),\quad d=g_{v}\left({U},{V}\right),

is stable (unstable).

Theorem 2.7

(Instability of stationary solutions
[
2
,
23
]
)
.

Let
(U,V)
be an arbitrary weak stationary solution to problem (
1.1
)-(
1.2
). If

(2.15)

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}f_{u}\big(U(x),\,V(x)\big)>0}\qquad\text{for $x$ from a set of a positive measure}

then
(U,V)
is linearly unstable in
{L^{p}(\Omega)}^{2}
for each
p\in(1,\infty)
.

The assumption (
2.15
) is called the
autocatalysis condition
in the work
[
23
]
. It appears in models with the Turing instability and it leads to an instability of the both: constant and nonconstant stationary solutions. Theorem
2.7
is an immediate consequence of the results in
[
23
, Thm. 4.5]
and

[
2
, Thm. 4.6]
, where the spectrum of the operator

\mathcal{L}_{p}
defined in (
2.13
) and acting on the space
L^{p}(\Omega)^{2}
is described.

Next, we discuss a stability of discontinuous stationary solutions and we require
from the constant solution
(\overline{U},\overline{V})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
used in Theorem
2.6
to have the following
additional properties
.

Assumption 2.8
.

For the numbers defined in (
2.3
),
assume that the following inequalities
hold
true

(2.16)

\displaystyle a_{0}<0,\quad d_{0}<0\quad\text{and the matrix }\quad\begin{pmatrix}a_{0}&b_{0}\\
c_{0}&d_{0},\end{pmatrix}

has both eigenvalues with strictly negative real parts.

Theorem 2.9

(Stability of discontinuous stationary solutions
[
3
]
)
.

Let the assumptions of Theorem
2.6

hold
true. If, moreover,

•

Assumption
2.8
is valid,

•

\varepsilon>0
and
|\Omega_{2}|>0
are small enough in Theorem
2.6
,

•

the following inequalities are satisfied

(2.17)

\displaystyle f_{u}(k_{2}(\overline{V}),\,\overline{V}\big)<0\quad\text{ and }\quad g_{v}\big(k_{2}(\overline{V}),\overline{V}\big)<0.

then the stationary solution
(U,V)
constructed in Theorem
2.6
is exponentially stable in
{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}
.

Theorem
2.9
can be obtained from
results in
[
3
]
in the following way. First, we apply
[
3
, Prop. 4.2]
to linear system (
2.13
) to show that the stationary solution is linearly exponentially stable in
{L^{p}(\Omega)}
. Here, our system consists of one ODE coupled with one PDE, thus Assumption
2.8
and the condition

imply that
[

, Assumptions 2.8 and 2.9]
are satisfied by
[

. Next, the nonlinear stability in

is a direct consequence of
[

which proof does not require
from

Remark 2.10
.

Other discontinuous stationary solutions can be constructed under
the
following more general version of Assumption
2.5
, where we postulate an existence of a set
\mathcal{V}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
and different branches
k_{1},\cdots,k_{J}\in C(\mathcal{V},\mathbb{R})
of solutions to

. Then for
an
arbitrary decomposition

(2.18)

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Omega\subseteq\overline{\bigcup_{i\in\{1,\cdots,J\}}\Omega_{i}}},\quad\Omega_{i}\cap\Omega_{j}=\emptyset\ \ \text{for}\ \ i\neq j\quad\text{and}\quad|\Omega_{i}|<\delta\ \ \text{for}\ \ i\in\{2,\cdots,J\},

\displaystyle U(x)=\begin{cases}k_{1}\big(V(x)\big),\quad x\in\Omega_{1},\\
\qquad\vdots\\

using the same reasoning as in Theorem

. If

(2.20)

\displaystyle f_{u}\big(k_{i}(\overline{V}),\,\overline{V}\big)<0\quad\text{and}\quad g_{v}\big(k_{i}(\overline{V}),\overline{V}\big)<0\quad\text{for each}\quad i\in\{1,\cdots,J\}

3.
Unstable stationary solutions

We apply results from Section
2

to particular reaction-diffusion-ODE systems. The following two models with classical nonlinearities have stationary solutions
(U,V)
, the both regular and discontinuous. For each stationary solution the
autocatalysis condition (
2.15
) is satisfied,
hence such solutions are
unstable.

3.1.
Gray-Scott type model

First, we deal with the reaction-diffusion-ODE model with the Gray-Scott nonlinearities

(3.1)

\displaystyle u_{t}

\displaystyle=u^{2}v-\alpha u,

\displaystyle x\in\overline{\Omega},

\displaystyle t>0,

\displaystyle v_{t}

\displaystyle={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma\Delta_{\nu}v-u^{2}v+\beta(1-v)},

\displaystyle\quad x\in{\Omega},

\displaystyle t>0,

with arbitrary constants
\alpha>0
,
\beta>0
and the diffusion coefficient
\gamma>0
. This is the diffusion degenerate case of the classical Gray-Scott model introduced in
[
9
]
and studied in
e.g.

[
15
,
4
]
and in the references therein.

Notice that problem (
3.1
) has the constant
stationary
solution
(\overline{U}_{1},\overline{V}_{1})=(0,1)
. Two
other
constant solutions
(\overline{U}_{2},\overline{V}_{2})
,
(\overline{U}_{3},\overline{V}_{3})
satisfy the relations

(3.2)

\displaystyle\overline{U}

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha}{\overline{V}}\quad\text{and}\quad 0

\displaystyle=\overline{V}^{2}-\overline{V}+\frac{\alpha^{2}}{\beta}

and they exist if
\alpha^{2}/\beta<1/4
.

Theorem 3.1
.

For a certain choice of coefficients
\alpha,\beta>0
and for a discrete sequence of diffusion coefficients
\gamma>0
problem (
3.1
) has a regular stationary solution. All regular stationary solutions
(
not only those obtained via Proposition
2.2
)
are nonlinearly unstable.

Proof.

We choose
\alpha,\beta>0
such that
\alpha^{2}/\beta<1/4
and consider a solution
\overline{V}_{3}
of the quadratic equation in (
3.2
) satisfying
\overline{V}_{3}<1/2
.
We apply Proposition
2.2
with the constant stationary solution
(\overline{U},\overline{V})=(\overline{U}_{3},\overline{V}_{3})
, where
\overline{U}_{3}=\alpha/\overline{V}_{3}
. Assumption
2.1
holds true, because
a_{0}=f_{u}(\overline{U}_{3},\overline{V}_{3})=\alpha>0
. Moreover, for the numbers
a_{0},b_{0},c_{0},d_{0}
as in (
2.3
), we have

(3.3)

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\det\begin{pmatrix}a_{0}&b_{0}\\
c_{0}&d_{0}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{vmatrix}\alpha&(\alpha/\overline{V}_{3})^{2}\\
-2\alpha&-(\alpha/\overline{V}_{3})^{2}-\beta\end{vmatrix}=\alpha\left((\alpha/\overline{V}_{3})^{2}-{\beta}\right)>0},

where the last inequality follows
from
an explicit formula for
\overline{V}_{3}
. Thus, we may choose
\gamma>0
to satisfy equation (
2.6
) for some eigenvalue
\mu_{k}>0
.

Next, we show that every stationary solution satisfies
f_{u}\big(U(x),V(x)\big)\neq 0
for each
x\in\Omega
. Indeed, notice that either
U(x)=0
for all
x\in\overline{\Omega}
or
U(x)=\alpha/V(x)
for all
x\in\overline{\Omega}
. Thus, since,
f_{u}(U,V)=2UV-\alpha
we have either
f_{u}\big(U(x),V(x)\big)=\alpha
or
f_{u}\big(U(x),V(x)\big)=-\alpha
.

Hence,
by
Theorem
2.3
,
we conclude that all regular solutions to problem (
3.1
) are unstable.
∎

Theorem 3.2
.

For arbitrary
\alpha>0
and
\beta>0
and for each diffusion
coefficient
\gamma>0
,
except of a discrete set, there exists a family of discontinuous stationary solutions to problem (
3.1
). All discontinuous stationary solutions
(
not only those obtained via Theorem
2.6
)
are linearly unstable in
{L^{p}(\Omega)}^{2}
for each
p\in(1,\infty)
.

Proof.

First, we apply Theorem
2.6
to construct discontinuous stationary solutions to problem (
3.1
). Assumption
2.4
is satisfied for
all stationary points

(\overline{U}_{1},\overline{V}_{1})
,
(\overline{U}_{2},\overline{V}_{2})
,
(\overline{U}_{3},\overline{V}_{3})

and for all diffusion coefficients
\gamma>0
, possibly except of a discrete set
. To check Assumption
2.5
, we notice that the equation

{U}(UV-\alpha)=0
has
the following
two branches of solutions

(3.4)

\displaystyle U=k_{1}(V)=0\qquad\text{and}\qquad U=k_{2}(V)=\frac{\alpha}{V}\quad\text{for all}\quad V\neq 0.

Thus, for an arbitrary open set
\Omega_{1}\subset\Omega
, with
\Omega_{2}=\Omega\setminus\overline{\Omega}_{1}
and such that the measure
|\Omega_{2}|>0
is sufficiently small, there exist a family of discontinuous stationary solutions around
the points either
(\overline{U}_{1},\overline{V}_{1})
or
(\overline{U}_{2},\overline{V}_{2})
or
(\overline{U}_{3},\overline{V}_{3})
as stated in Theorem
2.6
.

Each discontinuous stationary solution
(U,V)
satisfies
k_{2}\big(V(x)\big)=\alpha/V(x)
for
x
from a set of a positive measure. Thus,
f_{u}\big(k_{2}(V(x)),V(x)\big)=\alpha>0
and this solution is linearly unstable in
{L^{p}(\Omega)}^{2}
for each
p\in(1,\infty)
by Theorem
2.7
.
∎

3.2.
Brusselator type model

Next, we deal with the reaction-diffusion-ODE model with the nonlinearity as in the Brusselator system

(3.5)

\displaystyle u_{t}

\displaystyle=\alpha+u^{2}v-(\beta+1)u,

\displaystyle x\in\overline{\Omega},

\displaystyle t>0,

\displaystyle v_{t}

\displaystyle=\gamma\Delta_{\nu}v+\beta u-u^{2}v,

\displaystyle x\in\Omega,

\displaystyle t>0,

with arbitrary constants
\alpha>0
,
\beta>0
and the diffusion coefficient
\gamma>0
. This is the diffusion-degenerate case of the classical Brusselator model
which was introduced in
[
29
]
as a model for
an autocatalytic oscillating chemical reaction.
It has been
shown (
e.g.

in
[
5
,
14
,
26
]
and in
references therein) that the Brusselator system with the both nonzero diffusion coefficients (as in (
1.3
))
exhibits the Turing instability.

Notice that a constant stationary solution
(\overline{U},\overline{V})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
of problem (
3.5
) satisfies the equations

(3.6)

\displaystyle 0=\alpha+\overline{U}^{2}\overline{V}-(\beta+1)\overline{U},\qquad 0=\beta\overline{U}-\overline{U}^{2}\overline{V}

with the only solution

(3.7)

\displaystyle(\overline{U}_{1},\overline{V}_{1})=(\alpha,\beta/\alpha).

Theorem 3.3
.

For each
\alpha>0
and
\beta>1
and for a discrete sequence of
the
diffusion coefficients
\gamma>0
problem (
3.5
) has a regular stationary solution. All regular stationary solutions
(
not only those obtained via Proposition
2.2
)
are nonlinearly unstable.

Proof.

We use the notation from (
2.3
) with the constant stationary solution
(\overline{U}_{1},\overline{V}_{1})
and we notice that

(3.8)

\displaystyle a_{0}=\beta-1\quad\text{and}\quad\det\begin{pmatrix}a_{0}&b_{0}\\
c_{0}&d_{0}\end{pmatrix}=\alpha^{2}.

Thus, for

\beta>1
,
the proof of
the existence of regular stationary solutions,
may be completed in the analogous way as the proof of Theorem
3.1
.
All regular stationary solutions are unstable by Theorem
2.3
.

∎

Theorem 3.4
.

For
some

\alpha>0
,
\beta>0
,
\beta\neq 1
, and for each diffusion coefficient
\gamma>0
except of a discrete set, there exists a family of discontinuous stationary solutions to problem (
3.5
). All discontinuous stationary solutions
(
not only those obtained via Theorem
2.6
)
are linearly unstable in
{L^{p}(\Omega)}^{2}
for each
p\in(1,\infty)
.

Proof.

We choose
the
coefficient
\alpha>0
,
\beta>0
and
\gamma>0

in such a way that
Assumptions
2.1
and
2.4
are satisfied (notice that
a_{0}=\beta-1\neq 0
). The equation

(3.9)

\displaystyle f(U,V)=\alpha+U^{2}V-(\beta+1)U=0

has two different branches of solutions given by the explicit formulas

(3.10)

\displaystyle U=k_{1}(V)=\frac{\beta+1+\sqrt{(\beta+1)^{2}-4\alpha V}}{2V},\quad U=k_{2}(V)=\frac{\beta+1-\sqrt{(\beta+1)^{2}-4\alpha V}}{2V}

for
V<(\beta+1)^{2}/(4\alpha)
. Note that

(3.11)

\displaystyle\overline{V}_{1}=\beta/\alpha<(\beta+1)^{2}/(4\alpha)\quad\text{and}\quad\overline{U}_{1}=k_{1}(\overline{V}_{1}).

Thus, for an open set
\Omega_{1}\subset\Omega
with
\Omega_{2}=\Omega\setminus\overline{\Omega}_{2}
and such that the measure
|\Omega_{2}|>0
is
sufficiently small, there exist a family of discontinuous stationary solutions around
points

(\overline{U}_{1},\overline{V}_{1})
and
(k_{2}(\overline{V}_{1}),\overline{V}_{1})
as stated in Theorem
2.6
.

Each discontinuous stationary solution
(U,V)
satisfies
U(x)=k_{1}\big(V(x)\big)
for
x
from a set of positive measure. Thus,

(3.12)

\displaystyle f_{u}\big(k_{1}(V(x)),V(x)\big)=\sqrt{(\beta+1)^{2}-4\alpha V(x)}>0

and this solution is linearly unstable in
{L^{p}(\Omega)}^{2}
for each
p\in(1,\infty)
by Theorem
2.7
.
∎

4.
Stable discontinuous stationary solutions

Now, we discuss reaction-diffusion-ODE models with stable discontinuous stationary solutions.

4.1.
Oregonator type model

The following reaction-diffusion-ODE model

(4.1)

\displaystyle u_{t}

\displaystyle=u-u^{2}+\alpha v\frac{\beta-u}{\beta+u},

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}x\in\overline{\Omega},}

\displaystyle t>0,

\displaystyle v_{t}

\displaystyle=\gamma\Delta_{\nu}v+u-v,

\displaystyle x\in\Omega,

\displaystyle t>0,

contains the nonlinearities as in the classical Oregonator system with the parameters
\alpha>0
,
\beta>0
and the diffusion coefficient
\gamma>0
. A general version of this model was introduced in
[
33
,
7
,
6
]
to describe the FKN-Belousov reaction. It is known that system (
4.1
) may exhibit the Turing instability for a certain choice of parameters and diffusion coefficients, see
e.g.

[
36
,
27
]
and the references therein.

System (
4.1
) has the zero constant solution
(\overline{U}_{1},\overline{V}_{1})=(0,0)
and two other constant solutions have the form

(4.2)

\displaystyle(\overline{U}_{2},\overline{V}_{2})=(\overline{U}_{2},\overline{U}_{2})\qquad(\overline{U}_{3},\overline{V}_{3})=(\overline{U}_{3},\overline{U}_{3}),

where
the numbers

\overline{U}_{2}
and
\overline{U}_{3}
satisfy the quadratic equation

(4.3)

\displaystyle\overline{U}^{2}+\overline{U}(\beta+\alpha-1)-\beta(\alpha+1)=0.

Fig.
4.1
presents a location of the nullclines defined by the following equation for
\beta>1
and for sufficiently small
\alpha>0
:

(4.4)

\displaystyle f(U,V)=U-U^{2}+\alpha V\frac{\beta-U}{\beta+U}=0\quad\text{and}\quad g(U,V)=U-V=0.

Figure 4.1.
The nullclines (
4.4
) for the Oregonator model with
\beta=2
and sufficiently small
\alpha>0
. The point
(-2,0)
is excluded because it does not satisfy the equation
f(U,V)=0
.

Theorem 4.1
.

For each
\beta>1
and sufficiently small
\alpha>0
there exist a family of discontinuous stationary solutions to problem (
4.1
).

Proof.

Fix
\beta>1
and choose sufficiently small
\alpha>0
to have the nullclines as in Fig.
4.1
. In such a case, we have an open subset
\mathcal{V}\subset\mathbb{R}
satisfying
V_{1},V_{2},V_{3}\in\mathcal{V}
and three branches
k_{1},k_{2},k_{3}
defined on
\mathcal{V}
.

Here, we check assumptions of Theorem
2.6
for the stationary points either
(\overline{U}_{1},\overline{V}_{1})
or
(\overline{U}_{2},\overline{V}_{2})
or
(\overline{U}_{3},\overline{V}_{3})
in the cases shown in Fig.
4.1
.
First,
we notice that for
a branch

k\in\{k_{1},k_{2},k_{3}\}
, we have

(4.5)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dV}f\big(k(V),V\big)=f_{u}\big(k(V),V\big)k^{\prime}(V)+f_{v}\big(k(V),V\big)=0.

Since

f_{v}(U,V)=\frac{\alpha(\beta-U)}{\beta+U},

choosing
the
constant stationary solution

(\overline{U}_{i},\overline{V}_{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
satisfying
\overline{U}\neq\beta
and
k^{\prime}(\overline{V})\neq 0
we obtain
a_{0}=f_{u}(\overline{U},\overline{V})\neq 0
as required in Assumption
2.1
. This is the case of all constant stationary solutions
(\overline{U}_{1},\overline{V}_{1})
,
(\overline{U}_{2},\overline{V}_{2})
and
(\overline{U}_{3},\overline{V}_{3})
shown in Fig.
4.1
.

To apply Theorem
2.6
, we need a constant stationary solution
(\overline{U}_{i},\overline{V}_{i})
, an open set
\mathcal{V}\subset\mathbb{R}
with
V_{i}\in\mathcal{V}
and two branches of solutions
k_{i},k_{j}\in C(\mathcal{V},\mathbb{R})
to equation
f(U,V)=0
. On Fig
4.1
, we may choose each constant stationary solution with corresponding branches, namely,
the point

(\overline{U}_{1},\overline{V}_{1})
with the branches
k_{1}
and
k_{2}
, the point
(\overline{U}_{2},\overline{V}_{2})
with the branches
k_{2}
and either
k_{1}
or
k_{3}
as well as
the point

(\overline{U}_{3},\overline{V}_{3})
with the branches
k_{3}
and either
k_{1}
or
k_{2}
. We do not choose the point
(\overline{U}_{1},\overline{V}_{1})
with the branch
k_{3}
in order to avoid the singular point
(-\beta,0)
.

Thus, for
\gamma>0
satisfying Assumption
2.4
,
an arbitrary open set
\Omega_{1}\subset\Omega
with
\Omega_{2}=\Omega\setminus\overline{\Omega}_{1}
and
|\Omega_{2}|>0
sufficiently small, there exist a family of discontinuous stationary solutions around
the points

(\overline{U},\overline{V})=(k_{i}(\overline{V}),\overline{V})
and
(k_{j}(\overline{V}),\overline{V})
as stated in Theorem
2.6
.

By Remark
2.10
, we
can also construct
discontinuous stationary solutions with all three branches
k_{1}
,
k_{2}
,
k_{3}
and
with an
arbitrary partition
\Omega_{1}
,
\Omega_{2}
,
\Omega_{3}
of
\Omega
.
∎

Theorem 4.2
.

For each
\beta>1
and for sufficiently small
\alpha>0
there exist discontinuous stationary solutions constructed around the points
(\overline{U}_{2},\overline{V}_{2})
and
\big(k_{3}(\overline{V}_{2}),\overline{V}_{2}\big)
which are stable.

Proof.

As in the proof of Theorem
4.1
, we have an open subset
\mathcal{V}\subset\mathbb{R}
satisfying
V_{1},V_{2},V_{3}\in\mathcal{V}
and three branches
k_{1},k_{2},k_{3}
defined on
\mathcal{V}
, see Fig.
4.1
. Let
(U,V)
be a discontinuous stationary solution constructed around points
(\overline{U}_{2},\overline{V}_{2})
and
(k_{3}(\overline{V}_{2}),\overline{V}_{2})
via Theorem
2.6
.

First, we check Assumption
2.8
. Since
|\overline{U}_{2}|<\beta
and
since

k_{2}(V)
is increasing in the neighbourhood of
\overline{V}_{2}
(see Fig.
4.1
) we have
a_{0}<0
by
equation
(
4.5
). In order to show that the matrix
\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}a_{0}&b_{0}\\
c_{0}&d_{0}\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)
has both eigenvalues with negative real parts, it suffices to show that

(4.6)

\displaystyle a_{0}+d_{0}<0\quad\text{and}\quad a_{0}d_{0}-b_{0}c_{0}>0.

Since
c_{0}=1
and
d_{0}=-1
, first inequality is immediately satisfied. Moreover, since

k_{2}^{\prime}\big(\overline{V}_{2})\big)<1
, by identity (
4.5
), we have

(4.7)

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}a_{0}d_{0}-b_{0}c_{0}=-a_{0}\big(1-k_{2}^{\prime}(\overline{V}_{2})\big)>0}.

Finally,
|k_{3}\big(\overline{V}_{2}\big)|<\beta
(see Fig.
4.1
) and the function
k_{3}(V)
is increasing in
a
neighbourhood of
\overline{V}_{2}
which implies that
f_{u}\big(k_{3}(\overline{V}_{2}),\overline{V}_{2}\big)<0
.

By Theorem
2.9
, the discontinuous stationary solution
(U,V)
is stable provided
\varepsilon>0
and
|\Omega_{2}|>0
are small enough.
∎

Remark 4.3
.

On the other hand, all discontinuous stationary solutions constructed in Theorem
4.1
around the points
(\overline{U}_{1},\overline{V}_{1})
and
(\overline{U}_{3},\overline{V}_{3})
are unstable. Indeed, analogously as in the proof of Theorem
4.1
, we analyse nullclines (
4.4
) presented in Fig.
4.1
. Following the calculations from equation (
4.5
) we obtain, that
the number

f_{u}\big(k(\overline{V}),\overline{V})
is positive if either

(4.8)

\displaystyle|\overline{U}|<\beta\quad\text{and}\quad k^{\prime}(\overline{V})<0\qquad\text{or}\qquad|\overline{U}|>\beta\quad\text{and}\quad k^{\prime}(\overline{V})>0.

Since the constant solutions given by explicit formulas satisfy
\overline{U}_{1}=0
,
\overline{U}_{3}<-\beta
and
since
the function
k(V)
is decreasing in
a
neighbourhood of
\overline{U}_{1}
and increasing in a neighbourhood of
\overline{U}_{3}
(see Fig.
4.1
) we obtain that every solution constructed around points
(\overline{U}_{1},\overline{V}_{1})
and
(\overline{U}_{3},\overline{V}_{3})
is linearly unstable by Theorem
2.7
.

4.2.
Predator-prey model

Finally, we discuss stationary solutions to the following model of predator-prey interactions which is a particular case of a system considered in
[
25
]
:

(4.9)

\displaystyle u_{t}

\displaystyle=u\left(\frac{u^{2}}{u^{3}+1}-v\right),

\displaystyle x\in\overline{\Omega},

\displaystyle t>0

\displaystyle v_{t}

\displaystyle=\gamma\Delta_{\nu}v+v(\alpha u-v-\beta)

\displaystyle x\in\Omega,

\displaystyle t>0.

which
\alpha>0
and
\beta>0
. Here, the couple
(\overline{U}_{2},\overline{V}_{2})=(0,0)
is the constant stationary solution and the second constant stationary solution
(\overline{U}_{1},\overline{V}_{1})
satisfies the system

(4.10)

\displaystyle f(U,V)=\frac{U^{2}}{U^{3}+1}-V=0\quad\text{and}\quad g(U,V)=\alpha U-V-\beta=0.

{U^{2}}/({U^{3}+1})
attains a maximum for

at some point which we denoted by

Theorem 4.4
.

Assume that
\alpha>0
and
\beta>0
are such that the constant stationary solution

(\overline{U}_{1},\overline{V}_{1})
satisfies

\overline{U}_{1}\neq U_{m}
. Then there exist a family of discontinuous stationary solutions to problem (
4.9
).

Proof.

It is sufficient to apply Theorem
2.6
in the same way as
in the proof of
Theorem
4.1
with the stationary point

(\overline{U}_{1},\overline{V}_{1})
with the branch

k_{1}
and the branches either

k_{2}
or
k_{3}
(or the both, see Remark
2.10
).
∎

Theorem 4.5
.

Assume that
\alpha>0
and
\beta>0
are such that the constant stationary solution

(\overline{U}_{1},\overline{V}_{1})
satisfies

\overline{U}_{1}>U_{m}
. Then there exist stable discontinuous stationary solutions to problem (
4.9
).

Proof.

Following the reasoning
from
Theorem
4.2
we obtain that the
number
f_{u}(\overline{U}_{1},\overline{V}_{1})
is negative because the function

k_{1}(V)
is decreasing in a neighbourhood of

\overline{V}_{1}
(see Fig.
4.2
). Moreover, since
b_{0}<0
,
c_{0}>0
and
d_{0}<0
(with the notation from (
2.3
)) we obtain that

(4.11)

\displaystyle a_{0}+d_{0}<0\quad\text{and}\quad a_{0}d_{0}-b_{0}c_{d}>0

and Assumption
2.8
holds true. By a direct calculation, we obtain that

(4.12)

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}f_{u}(0,\overline{V}_{1})=-\overline{V}_{1}<0\quad\text{and}\quad g_{v}(0,\overline{V}_{1})=-2\overline{V}_{1}-\beta<0}.

Thus,
the solution is stable by Theorem
2.9
.
∎
