The BMO-discrepancy suffers from the curse of dimensionality
Friedrich Pillichshammer

Abstract
We show that the minimal discrepancy of a point set in the d d -dimensional unit cube with respect to the BMO seminorm suffers from the curse of dimensionality.



Keywords:
Discrepancy, BMO seminorm, tractability, quasi-Monte Carlo

MSC 2010:
11K38, 65C05, 65Y20

The discrepancy is a quantitative measure for the irregularity of distribution of an
N
-element point set
\mathcal{P}=\{\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\boldsymbol{x}_{2},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}_{N}\}
in the unit cube
[0,1)^{d}
(see, e.g.,
[
1
,
13
,
17
,
18
]
). This concept has important applications in numerical analysis, where so-called Koksma-Hlawka inequalities establish a deep connection between norms of the discrepancy and worst-case errors of quasi-Monte Carlo rules (see, e.g,
[
11
,
12
,
19
,
21
]
).

For a measurable subset
B\subseteq[0,1)^{d}
the
local discrepancy

\Delta_{\mathcal{P}}
defined as

\Delta_{\mathcal{P}}(B):=\frac{|\{j\in\{1,2,\ldots,N\}\ :\ \boldsymbol{x}_{j}\in B\}|}{N}-\lambda_{d}(B),

where
\lambda_{d}
stands for the
d
-dimensional Lebesgue measure. Choosing a suitable class of “test sets”
B
and taking a norm
\|\cdot\|_{\bullet}
of the local discrepancy with respect to the considered test sets leads to a discrepancy
\|\Delta_{\mathcal{P}}\|_{\bullet}
of the point set
\mathcal{P}
. An important choice is the class of subintervals of
[0,1)^{d}
that are anchored in the origin, i.e., intervals of the form
[\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{t})=[0,t_{1})\times\ldots\times[0,t_{d})
, where
\boldsymbol{t}=(t_{1},\ldots,t_{d})\in[0,1]^{d}
. Taking the
L_{p}
-norm of the function
\boldsymbol{t}\mapsto\Delta_{\mathcal{P}}([\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{t}))
gives the usual
(star)
L_{p}
-discrepancy

L_{p,N}(\mathcal{P}):=\|\Delta_{\mathcal{P}}([\boldsymbol{0},\cdot))\|_{L_{p}}=\left(\int_{[0,1]^{d}}|\Delta_{\mathcal{P}}([\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{t}))|^{p}\,{\rm d}\boldsymbol{t}\right)^{1/p}\quad\mbox{for $1\leq p<\infty$}

with the usual adaptions for
p=\infty
, i.e.,

L_{\infty,N}(\mathcal{P}):=\|\Delta_{\mathcal{P}}([\boldsymbol{0},\cdot))\|_{L_{\infty}}=\sup_{\boldsymbol{t}\in[0,1]^{d}}|\Delta_{\mathcal{P}}([\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{t}))|.

The star
L_{\infty}
-discrepancy is often simply called
star-discrepancy
and is denoted by
D_{N}^{\ast}
.

Another example are arbitrary subintervals of
[0,1)^{d}
as test sets of the form
[\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})=[x_{1},y_{1})\times\ldots\times[x_{d},y_{d})
with
\boldsymbol{x}\leq\boldsymbol{y}
, where
\boldsymbol{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})
and
\boldsymbol{y}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{d})
in
[0,1]^{d}
and where
\boldsymbol{x}\leq\boldsymbol{y}
means
x_{i}\leq y_{i}
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
. Again, taking the
L_{p}
-norm with respect to
\boldsymbol{x}\leq\boldsymbol{y}
leads to the so-called
extreme (or unanchored)
L_{p}
-discrepancy

L_{p,N}^{\mathrm{extr}}(\mathcal{P}):=\left(\int_{[0,1]^{d}}\int_{[0,1]^{d},\,\boldsymbol{x}\leq\boldsymbol{y}}|\Delta_{\mathcal{P}}([\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}))|^{p}\,{\rm d}\boldsymbol{x}\,{\rm d}\boldsymbol{y}\right)^{1/p},

(1)

again with the usual adaptions if
p=\infty
. Choosing so-called “periodic boxes” as test sets leads to the notion of periodic
L_{p}
-discrepancy (or diaphony in the special case
p=2
). See
[
15
,
16
]
for more details.

Besides intervals as test sets and the
L_{p}
-norms also other norms of the discrepancy function are considered and studied (see, e.g.,
[
1
,
18
,
21
,
24
]
). In this context the exponential Orlicz norms and the BMO (semi)norm attracted much attention in recent years (see
[
3
,
4
,
5
,
8
,
10
]
).

Often discrepancies are directly related to the worst-case integration error of quasi-Monte Carlo rules for a suitable class of integrands (see, e.g.,
[
21
, Chapter 9]
). For this reason, point sets with low discrepancy have an important application in numerical analysis. This motivates the study of the
N
-th minimal discrepancy
with respect to the norm
\|\cdot\|_{\bullet}
in dimension
d
which is the best possible discrepancy over all
N
-point sets in
[0,1]^{d}
, i.e.,

\mathrm{disc}_{\bullet}(N,d):=\inf_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{P}\subseteq[0,1]^{d}}}{{|\mathcal{P}|=N}}}\|\Delta_{\mathcal{P}}\|_{\bullet}.

In many cases, for fixed dimension
d
, asymptotically optimal bounds on
\mathrm{disc}_{\bullet}(N,d)
in
N
are available. However, in practical integration problems, the dimension
d
may be very large and then these optimal bounds are often useless, since they yield no useful information within the pre-asymptotic regime of
N
. This problem is studied in the discipline “information based complexity”.

The so-called
initial discrepancy
is defined as the discrepancy of the empty point set
\|\Delta_{\emptyset}\|_{\bullet}
. Since this quantity may depend on the dimension, it is used to normalize the
N
-th minimal discrepancy when the dependence of
\mathrm{disc}_{\bullet}(N,d)
on
d
(and some error threshold
\varepsilon
) is studied. Therefore the
inverse of the
N
-th minimal discrepancy
in dimension
d
is defined as the number
N_{\bullet}(\varepsilon,d)
to be the smallest number
N
such that a point set with
N
points exists that reduces the initial discrepancy at least by a factor of
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, i.e.,

N_{\bullet}(\varepsilon,d):=\min\big\{N\in\mathbb{N}\,:\,\mathrm{disc}_{\bullet}(N,d)\leq\varepsilon\|\Delta_{\emptyset}\|_{\bullet}\big\}.

Now a discrepancy is said to
suffer from the curse of dimensionality
if it has the unfavourable property that its inverse grows exponentially with the dimension
d
, i.e., if there exist numbers
C,\tau\in(0,\infty)
and
\varepsilon_{0}\in(0,1)
such that

N_{\bullet}(\varepsilon,d)\geq C(1+\tau)^{d}\quad\mbox{for all $\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon_{0})$ and infinitely many $d\in\mathbb{N}$.}

Otherwise, if this is not the case, one says that the discrepancy is
tractable
. There are many notions of tractability, which characterize the growth rate of
N_{\bullet}(\varepsilon,d)
when
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
and
d\rightarrow\infty
, which in any case has to be sub-exponential. An important notion is
polynomial tractability
, which holds whenever there are numbers
C,\tau,\sigma\in(0,\infty)
such that

N_{\bullet}(\varepsilon,d)\leq C\,d^{\tau}\varepsilon^{-\sigma}\quad\mbox{for all }\varepsilon\in(0,1)\mbox{ and all }d\in\mathbb{N}.

Another, weaker notion is
weak tractability
, which means that

\lim_{d+\varepsilon^{-1}\to\infty}\frac{\log N_{\bullet}(\varepsilon,d)}{d+\varepsilon^{-1}}=0.

The subject of tractability of multivariate problems and in particular of discrepancy is a very popular and active area of research. We refer to the books
[
20
,
21
,
22
]
by Novak and Woźniakowski for an introduction and an exhaustive exposition.

It is known from a famous result by Heinrich, Novak, Wasilkowski, and Woźniakowski
[
14
]
that the star
L_{\infty}
-discrepancy is polynomially tractable. On the other hand, the star
L_{2}
-discrepancy is known to suffer from the curse of dimensionality, as shown by Woźniakowski
[
25
]
(see also
[
21
]
). Also the extreme- (see
[
21
, Section 10.5.3, p. 94]
) and the periodic- (see
[
9
]
)
L_{2}
-discrepancy suffer from the curse of dimensionality. The behavior of the
L_{p}
-discrepancies in between, where
p\notin\{2,\infty\}
, seems to be unknown.

It is now a natural and instructive question to ask what happens in intermediate spaces “close” to
L_{\infty}
. Standard examples of such spaces are the space
{\rm BMO}
, which stands for
bounded mean oscillation
, or exponential Orlicz spaces. The study of these spaces in discrepancy theory with the aim to understand the precise nature of the kink that occurs at the passage from the
L_{p}
with finite
p
to the
L_{\infty}
norm was initiated by Bilyk, Lacey, Parissis, and Vagharshakyan in
[
4
]
. Other papers in this context followed, we refer to
[
3
,
4
,
5
,
8
,
10
]
.

The dependence of the inverse of discrepancy with respect to Orlicz norms on the dimension has been studied recently in
[
10
]
with quite positive results showing that the discrepancy with respect to suitable Orlicz norms can exhibit both polynomial and weak tractability (see
[
10
, Theorem 1 and 2]
). In this note we supplement the BMO case.

For the definition we need the concept of Haar functions: Let
\mathbb{N}_{0}=\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
and
\mathbb{N}_{-1}=\mathbb{N}_{0}\cup\{-1\}
. Let
\mathbb{D}_{j}=\{0,1,\ldots,2^{j}-1\}
for
j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
\mathbb{D}_{-1}=\{0\}
. For
\boldsymbol{j}=(j_{1},\dots,j_{d})\in\mathbb{N}_{-1}^{d}
let
\mathbb{D}_{\boldsymbol{j}}=\mathbb{D}_{j_{1}}\times\ldots\times\mathbb{D}_{j_{d}}
. For
\boldsymbol{j}\in\mathbb{N}_{-1}^{d}
we write
|\boldsymbol{j}|=\max(j_{1},0)+\cdots+\max(j_{d},0)
.

For
j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
m\in\mathbb{D}_{j}
we call the interval
I_{j,m}=\big[2^{-j}m,2^{-j}(m+1)\big)
the
m
-th dyadic interval in
[0,1)
on level
j
. We put
I_{-1,0}=[0,1)
and call it the
0
-th dyadic interval in
[0,1)
on level
-1
. Let
I_{j,m}^{+}=I_{j+1,2m}
and
I_{j,m}^{-}=I_{j+1,2m+1}
be the left and right half of
I_{j,m}
, respectively. For
\boldsymbol{j}\in\mathbb{N}_{-1}^{d}
and
\boldsymbol{m}=(m_{1},\ldots,m_{d})\in\mathbb{D}_{\boldsymbol{j}}
we call
I_{\boldsymbol{j},\boldsymbol{m}}=I_{j_{1},m_{1}}\times\ldots\times I_{j_{d},m_{d}}
the
\boldsymbol{m}
-th dyadic interval in
[0,1)^{d}
on level
\boldsymbol{j}
. We call the number
|\boldsymbol{j}|
the order of the dyadic interval
I_{\boldsymbol{j},\boldsymbol{m}}
. Its volume is
2^{-|\boldsymbol{j}|}
.

Let
j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
m\in\mathbb{D}_{j}
. Let
h_{j,m}
be the function on
[0,1)
with support in
I_{j,m}
and the constant values
1
on
I_{j,m}^{+}
and
-1
on
I_{j,m}^{-}
. We put
h_{-1,0}=\mathbf{1}_{I_{-1,0}}
on
[0,1)
(where
\mathbf{1}_{A}
denotes the indicator function of a set
A
). The function
h_{j,m}
is called the
m
-th dyadic Haar function on level
j
.

Let
\boldsymbol{j}\in\mathbb{N}_{-1}^{d}
and
\boldsymbol{m}\in\mathbb{D}_{\boldsymbol{j}}
. The function
h_{\boldsymbol{j},\boldsymbol{m}}
given as the tensor product

h_{\boldsymbol{j},\boldsymbol{m}}(\boldsymbol{x})=h_{j_{1},m_{1}}(x_{1})\cdots h_{j_{d},m_{d}}(x_{d})

for
\boldsymbol{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})\in[0,1)^{d}
is called a
dyadic Haar function
on
[0,1)^{d}
. The system of dyadic Haar functions
h_{\boldsymbol{j},\boldsymbol{m}}
for
\boldsymbol{j}\in\mathbb{N}_{-1}^{d},\,\boldsymbol{m}\in\mathbb{D}_{\boldsymbol{j}}
is called
dyadic Haar basis on
[0,1)^{d}
.

It is well known that the system

\left\{2^{\frac{|\boldsymbol{j}|}{2}}h_{\boldsymbol{j},\boldsymbol{m}}\,:\,\boldsymbol{j}\in\mathbb{N}_{-1}^{d},\,\boldsymbol{m}\in\mathbb{D}_{\boldsymbol{j}}\right\}

is an orthonormal basis of
L_{2}([0,1)^{d})
. For any function
f\in L_{2}([0,1)^{d})
we have Parseval’s identity

\|f\|_{L_{2}}^{2}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{j}\in\mathbb{N}_{-1}^{d}}2^{|\boldsymbol{j}|}\sum_{\boldsymbol{m}\in\mathbb{D}_{\boldsymbol{j}}}|\langle f,h_{\boldsymbol{j},\boldsymbol{m}}\rangle|^{2},

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes the usual
L_{2}
-inner product, i.e.,
\langle f,g\rangle=\int_{[0,1]^{d}}f(\boldsymbol{x})g(\boldsymbol{x})\,{\rm d}\boldsymbol{x}
. The terms
\langle f,h_{\boldsymbol{j},\boldsymbol{m}}\rangle
are called the
Haar coefficients
of the function
f
.

For an integrable function
f:[0,1]^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
define

\|f\|^{2}_{{\rm BMO}}:=\sup_{U\subseteq[0,1)^{d}}\frac{1}{\lambda_{d}(U)}\sum_{\boldsymbol{j}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}}2^{|\boldsymbol{j}|}\sum_{\boldsymbol{m}\in\mathbb{D}_{\boldsymbol{j}}\atop I_{\boldsymbol{j},\boldsymbol{m}}\subseteq U}|\langle f,h_{\boldsymbol{j},\boldsymbol{m}}\rangle|^{2},

(2)

where the supremum is taken over all measurable sets
U\subseteq[0,1)^{d}
.

In order to give an intuition, consider
d=1
and assume that
U\subseteq[0,1)
is a dyadic interval. Let
\langle f\rangle_{U}:=\lambda_{1}(U)^{-1}\int_{U}f(x)\,{\rm d}x
be the mean of
f
over
U
. Then it is easy to show that
\langle(f-\langle f\rangle_{U}){\bf 1}_{U},h_{-1,0}\rangle=0
and, for
j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
m\in\mathbb{D}_{j}
,

\langle(f-\langle f\rangle_{U}){\bf 1}_{U},h_{j,m}\rangle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\langle f,h_{j,m}\rangle&\mbox{if $I_{j,m}\subseteq U$,}\\
0&\mbox{otherwise,}\end{array}\right.

where
{\bf 1}_{U}
is the indicator function of
U
. Hence we have by Parseval’s identity, that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}(U)}\int_{U}|f(x)-\langle f\rangle_{U}|^{2}\,{\rm d}x

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}(U)}\int_{0}^{1}|(f(x)-\langle f\rangle_{U}){\bf 1}_{U}(x)|^{2}\,{\rm d}x

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}(U)}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}2^{j}\sum_{m\in\mathbb{D}_{j}\atop I_{j,m}\subseteq U}|\langle f,h_{j,m}\rangle|^{2},

where the right hand side is exactly the term that appears in right hand side of (
2
).

The BMO space contains all integrable functions
f
with finite norm
\|f\|_{{\rm BMO}}
. Note that strictly speaking
\|f\|_{{\rm BMO}}
is only a seminorm, since it vanishes on linear combinations of functions which are constant in one or more coordinate directions. This means that formally we need to consider a factor space over such functions. We also note that there are different definitions of BMO spaces in the multivariate case. The setting introduced here is the so-called dyadic product BMO as introduced by Bernard

Theorem 1
.

For
d\in\mathbb{N}
and
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
we have

N_{{\rm BMO}}(\varepsilon,d)\geq\left(\frac{4}{3}\right)^{d}(1-\varepsilon^{2}).

In particular, the BMO-discrepancy suffers from the curse of dimensionality.

So the inverse of BMO-discrepancy behaves like the inverse of the classical (star)
L_{2}
-discrepancy rather than the inverse of the star
L_{\infty}
-discrepancy (for which we have polynomial tractability according to
[
14
]
). The proof of Theorem
1
is not a big deal because we can fall back on powerful auxiliary results. In particular, we use a relation between the BMO-discrepancy and the extreme
L_{2}
-discrepancy which is interesting on its own and which follows from a recent Haar series expansion of the extreme
L_{2}
-discrepancy. But first we compute the initial BMO-discrepancy.

Lemma 1
.

The initial BMO-discrepancy in dimension
d
is
\|\Delta_{\emptyset}\|_{{\rm BMO}}=12^{-d/2}
.

Proof.

For
\boldsymbol{j}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
and
\boldsymbol{m}\in\mathbb{D}_{\boldsymbol{j}}
we have

\displaystyle\langle\Delta_{\emptyset},h_{\boldsymbol{j},\boldsymbol{m}}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{d}\int_{0}^{1}x\,h_{j_{i},m_{i}}(x)\,{\rm d}x

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{d}\left(\int_{m_{i}/2^{j_{i}}}^{(m_{i}+1/2)/2^{j_{i}}}x\,{\rm d}x-\int_{(m_{i}+1/2)/2^{j_{i}}}^{(m_{i}+1)/2^{j_{i}}}x\,{\rm d}x\right)=\frac{(-1)^{d}}{2^{2(d+|\boldsymbol{j}|)}}.

Hence, considering
U=[0,1)^{d}
, we find that

\displaystyle\|\Delta_{\emptyset}\|_{{\rm BMO}}^{2}\geq\sum_{\boldsymbol{j}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}}2^{|\boldsymbol{j}|}\sum_{\boldsymbol{m}\in\mathbb{D}_{\boldsymbol{j}}}\frac{1}{2^{4(d+|\boldsymbol{j}|)}}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{j}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}}\frac{2^{2|\boldsymbol{j}|}}{2^{4(d+|\boldsymbol{j}|)}}=\left(\frac{1}{16}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{4^{j}}\right)^{d}=\frac{1}{12^{d}}.

On the other hand, it is clear that for each
U\subseteq[0,1)^{d}
and
\boldsymbol{j}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
there are at most
2^{|\boldsymbol{j}|}\lambda_{d}(U)
values of
\boldsymbol{m}\in\mathbb{D}_{\boldsymbol{j}}
such that
I_{\boldsymbol{j},\boldsymbol{m}}\subseteq U
. Hence we have

\displaystyle\|\Delta_{\emptyset}\|_{{\rm BMO}}^{2}\leq\sup_{U\subseteq[0,1)^{d}}\frac{1}{\lambda_{d}(U)}\sum_{\boldsymbol{j}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}}2^{|\boldsymbol{j}|}2^{|\boldsymbol{j}|}\lambda_{d}(U)\ \frac{1}{2^{4(d+|\boldsymbol{j}|)}}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{j}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}}\frac{2^{2|\boldsymbol{j}|}}{2^{4(d+|\boldsymbol{j}|)}}=\frac{1}{12^{d}}

and the result follows.
∎

Lemma 2
.

For every
N
-element point set
\mathcal{P}
in
[0,1)^{d}
we have

(L_{2,N}^{\mathrm{extr}}(\mathcal{P}))^{2}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{j}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}}2^{|\boldsymbol{j}|}\sum_{\boldsymbol{m}\in\mathbb{D}_{\boldsymbol{j}}}|\langle\Delta_{\mathcal{P}}([\boldsymbol{0},\cdot)),h_{\boldsymbol{j},\boldsymbol{m}}\rangle|^{2}.

(3)

Note that in formula (
3
) the Haar coefficients of the local discrepancy of
anchored
intervals appear. Comparing formula (
3
) with the corresponding formula for the star
L_{2}
-discrepancy, which is

(L_{2,N}^{\mathrm{star}}(\mathcal{P}))^{2}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{j}\in\mathbb{N}_{-1}^{d}}2^{|\boldsymbol{j}|}\sum_{\boldsymbol{m}\in\mathbb{D}_{\boldsymbol{j}}}|\langle\Delta_{\mathcal{P}}([\boldsymbol{0},\cdot)),h_{\boldsymbol{j},\boldsymbol{m}}\rangle|^{2},

it can be observed that the only difference is that in the case of extreme
L_{2}
-discrepancy the terms of order
-1
are not present. This also shows that the extreme
L_{2}
-discrepancy is dominated by the star
L_{2}
-discrepancy.

For us, however, the following is important. Comparing (
3

2
) for the local discrepancy and taking
U=[0,1)^{d}
immediately implies the following lemma, which is interesting on its own and which is the key to the proof of Theorem

.

Lemma 3
.

For every
N
-element point set
\mathcal{P}
in
[0,1)^{d}
we have

\|\Delta_{\mathcal{P}}\|_{{\rm BMO}}\geq L_{2,N}^{\mathrm{extr}}(\mathcal{P}).

Now we can give the proof of Theorem
1
.

Proof of Theorem
1
.

It is easily shown (or see
[
21
, p. 33]
) that also the initial extreme
L_{2}
-discrepancy equals
12^{-d/2}
. This shows that
\|\Delta_{\emptyset}\|_{{\rm BMO}}=L_{2,0}^{\mathrm{extr}}(\emptyset)
. Hence, using Lemma
3
,

\displaystyle N_{{\rm BMO}}(\varepsilon,d)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\min\{N\in\mathbb{N}\ :\ {\rm disc}_{{\rm BMO}}(N,d)\leq\varepsilon\|\Delta_{\emptyset}\|_{{\rm BMO}}\}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\min\{N\in\mathbb{N}\ :\ {\rm disc}_{L_{2}^{{\rm extr}}}(N,d)\leq\varepsilon L_{2,0}^{\mathrm{extr}}(\emptyset)\}=N_{L_{2}^{{\rm extr}}}(\varepsilon,d).

For the latter quantity however it follows from
[
21
, Section 10.5.3, p. 94]
that

N_{L_{2}^{{\rm extr}}}(\varepsilon,d)\geq\left(\frac{9}{4}\right)^{d}(1-\varepsilon^{2}).

Hence the result follows.

Remark 1
.

Another interesting consequence of Lemma
3
is the following. From
[
15
, Theorem 6]
(or also
[
16
, Corollary 4]
) it is known that for every
d\in\mathbb{N}
there exists a positive real
c_{d}
with the property that for every
N
-element point set
\mathcal{P}
in
[0,1)^{d}
we have

L_{2,N}^{{\rm extr}}(\mathcal{P})\geq c_{d}\frac{(1+\log N)^{\frac{d-1}{2}}}{N}.

Now from Lemma
3
it follows that the same lower bound applies to the BMO-discrepancy for every
N
-element point set in
[0,1)^{d}
. Thus, both discrepancies satisfy a Roth-type lower bound like the usual (star)
L_{2}
-discrepancy (see
[
23
]
). The Roth-type lower bound for the BMO-discrepancy has been proved earlier by Bilyk, Lacey, Parissis and Vagharshakyan
[
4
, Theorem 1.6]
for
d=2
and by Bilyk and Markhasin
[
5
, Theorem 1.2]
for
d\geq 3
.
