Scale Invariant Scattering and Bernoulli Numbers
Thomas L. CURTRIGHT

Abstract
Non-relativistic quantum mechanical scattering from an inverse square potential in two spatial dimensions leads to a novel representation of the Bernoulli numbers.

Introduction

In this note, I summarize some observations about Bernoulli numbers as
obtained in the context of computing the scattering amplitude for a scale
invariant potential.

In two spatial dimensions non-relativistic scattering by a repulsive
potential
V=\kappa/r^{2}
, with
{\kappa>0}
, results in a surprisingly
simple form for the integrated cross section,
\sigma=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\left(\frac{{\rm d}\sigma}{{\rm d}\theta}\right){\rm d}\theta
, when computed using quantum
mechanics. The result for a mono-energetic beam of mass
m
particles is

[
2
,
3
]

\sigma=\frac{2\pi^{2}m\kappa}{\hbar^{2}k}

where the incident energy is
E=\hbar^{2}k^{2}/(2m)
. This
result follows from a straightforward application of phase-shift analysis
for the potential
V=\kappa/r^{2}
upon realizing a remarkable identity
involving the sinc function,
\operatorname{sinc}(z)\equiv\sin(z)/z
, as shown in
[
2
]
. For real
x
, a succinct form of the identity in question is
1
1

1

A selection of other identities of this
type, but not exactly (
1.1
) so far as I can tell, can be found in
[
5
]
.

1=\frac{\sin(\pi x)}{\pi x}+2\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{l}\sin\bigl(\pi\sqrt{l^{2}+x^{2}}\bigr)}{\pi\sqrt{l^{2}+x^{2}}}.

(1.1)

All higher powers of
x
cancel when terms on the right-hand side are expanded as series
in
x^{2}
, as a consequence of familiar
\zeta(2n)
exact
values for integer
n>0
.

2
Bessel functions and Bernoulli numbers

Upon expressing the sinc function in terms of
spherical Bessel functions
,

\displaystyle j_{n}(z)=\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2z}}J_{n+1/2}(z)=(-z)^{n}\left(\frac{1}{z}\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}z}\right)^{n}\frac{\sin z}{z}

one may expand the summand of (
1.1
) in powers of
x^{2}
to obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\sin\bigl(\pi\sqrt{l^{2}+x^{2}}\bigr)}{\pi\sqrt{l^{2}+x^{2}}}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\bigl(-\pi x^{2}\bigr)^{n}}{n!2^{n}}\frac{1}{l^{n}}\sqrt{\frac{1}{2l}}J_{n+1/2}(\pi l).

Performing the sum over
l
in (
1.1
) before the sum over
n
then
leads to
2
2

2

Truncating the right-hand side of (
2.1
) to
\sum_{l=1}^{N}
produces an
O(1/N)
error which is largest at

n=1
.

\frac{\pi^{n}}{\sqrt{2}}=-\frac{(2n+1)!}{2^{n}n!}\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{l}}{l^{n+1/2}}~J_{n+1/2}(\pi l)\qquad\text{for integer}\quad n\geq 1.

(2.1)

Note the prefactor on the right-hand side can be expressed in terms of the double
factorial:
(2n+1)!!=(2n+1)!/\big(2^{n}n!\big)
.

Implicit in (
2.1
) is an identity involving the Bernoulli numbers, as
follows from using series representations for the Bessel functions
[
1
, Chapter 10, in particular, equation (10.1.8)]
and interchanging summations. For even
n
,

\displaystyle\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{l}}{l^{n+1/2}}J_{n+1/2}(l\pi)

\displaystyle\qquad=(-1)^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor(n-1)/2\rfloor}(-1)^{k}\frac{\sqrt{2}(2\lfloor n/2\rfloor+2k+1)!}{(2k+1)!\Gamma(2\lfloor n/2\rfloor-2k)}\frac{\zeta(2k+2\lfloor n/2\rfloor+2)}{2^{2k+1}\pi^{2k+2}},

while for odd
n

\displaystyle\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{l}}{l^{n+1/2}}J_{n+1/2}(l\pi)

\displaystyle\qquad=-(-1)^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor(n-1)/2\rfloor}(-1)^{k}\frac{\sqrt{2}(2\lfloor n/2\rfloor+2k+1)!}{(2k)!\Gamma(2\lfloor n/2\rfloor+2-2k)}\frac{\zeta(2k+2\lfloor n/2\rfloor+2)}{2^{2k}\pi^{2k+1}}.

But then
B_{2n+1}=0
for
n=1,2,\dots
and
\zeta(2n)=\frac{(2\pi)^{2n}}{2(2n)!}|B_{2n}|
for
n=1,2,\dots

with the usual phases given by
B_{2n}=(-1)^{n+1}|B_{2n}|
. Thus from (
2.1
) we are led to another, rather
remarkable identity

1=(-1)^{n+1}(4n+2)\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{(2n)!}{n!k!(n-k)!}\left(\frac{B_{n+k+1}}{n+k+1}\right)\qquad\text{for integer}\quad n\geq 1.

(2.2)

Here the sum involves
trinomial coefficients
as well as
divided Bernoulli numbers
,
\beta_{m}\equiv B_{m}/m
. It is not
difficult to check the validity of (
2.2
) using various expressions of
the Bernoulli numbers as finite,
alternating
sums, e.g., as
sums of Worpitzky numbers weighted by the harmonic sequence
.

3
A novel representation of Bernoulli numbers

If encountered as graffiti on the stones of a bridge, e.g., in Ireland,
either (
1.1
) or its companion identity (
2.2
) might cause
nothing more than a raised eyebrow in passing. Perhaps justifiably so.

However, upon inverting the linear relations in (
2.2
) to obtain
expressions for each individual Bernoulli number, the results are more
striking: The unsigned Bernoulli numbers
|B_{2n}|

for
n\geq 2
are given by interesting sums of
n-1
monotonically decreasing
positive rational numbers. Unlike many other such representations
[
11
]
, here the terms in the finite sums that represent
B_{2n}
do

not
alternate in sign.

For example,

\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\left|B_{2}\right|\\[2.84526pt]
\left|B_{4}\right|\\[2.84526pt]
\left|B_{6}\right|\\[2.84526pt]
\left|B_{8}\right|\\[2.84526pt]
\left|B_{10}\right|\\[2.84526pt]
\left|B_{12}\right|\\[2.84526pt]
\left|B_{14}\right|\\[2.84526pt]
\left|B_{16}\right|\\[2.84526pt]
\left|B_{18}\right|\\[2.84526pt]
\left|B_{20}\right|\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\frac{1}{6}\\[5.69054pt]
\frac{1}{30}\\[5.69054pt]
\frac{1}{42}\\[5.69054pt]
\frac{1}{30}\\[5.69054pt]
\frac{5}{66}\\[5.69054pt]
\frac{691}{2730}\\[5.69054pt]
\frac{7}{6}\\[5.69054pt]
\frac{3617}{510}\\[5.69054pt]
\frac{43867}{798}\\[5.69054pt]
\frac{174611}{330}\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{l}\frac{1}{6}\\[5.69054pt]
\frac{1}{30}\\[5.69054pt]
\frac{1}{60}+\frac{1}{140}\\[5.69054pt]
\frac{1}{45}+\frac{1}{105}+\frac{1}{630}\\[5.69054pt]
\frac{1}{20}+\frac{3}{140}+\frac{1}{252}+\frac{1}{2772}\\[5.69054pt]
\frac{1}{6}+\frac{1}{14}+\frac{17}{1260}+\frac{1}{693}+\frac{1}{12012}\\[5.69054pt]
\frac{691}{900}+\frac{691}{2100}+\frac{59}{945}+\frac{41}{5940}+\frac{5}{10296}+\frac{1}{51480}\\[5.69054pt]
\frac{14}{3}+2+\frac{359}{945}+\frac{8}{189}+\frac{4}{1287}+\frac{1}{6435}+\frac{1}{218790}\\[5.69054pt]
\frac{3617}{100}+\frac{10851}{700}+\frac{1237}{420}+\frac{217}{660}+\frac{293}{12012}+\frac{1}{780}\\[2.84526pt]

\hphantom{\frac{43867}{126}}{}+\frac{217}{437580}+\frac{2}{138567}+\frac{1}{3879876}\end{array}\right).

(3.1)

For these examples, in the finite sequence of terms that sum to give
|B_{2n}|
obviously the second number in the sequence
is just
3/7
times the first. Less obviously, each term in the sequence
for
|B_{2n}|
is greater than the subtotal of all the
smaller terms in that same sequence.

The general result for
|B_{2n}|
is obtained by
writing (
2.2
) as an infinite matrix equation,
\boldsymbol{1}=\boldsymbol{M\cdot B}
, where
\boldsymbol{B}
is an infinite column of the
even index Bernoulli numbers,
\boldsymbol{1}
is an infinite column of
1

s, and
\boldsymbol{M}_{m,n}=2(-1)^{m+1}\binom{2n-1}{m}\binom{2m+1}{2n}
. Computing the inverse for the triangular matrix
\boldsymbol{M}
then gives
\boldsymbol{B=M}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\cdot 1}
. The ordered terms
in the sums of (
3.1
) are just the unsigned entries in the
corresponding columns of
\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}
for the
n
-th row. All
terms in a given row of
\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}
have the same sign. Some
straightforward algebra then leads to

|B_{2n}|=\frac{(n!)^{2}}{(2n+1)!}+\sum_{k=2}^{n-1}n!~q_{n-k-1}(n)\frac{k!(k-1)}{(2k+1)!},

where the
l
-th order polynomials
q_{l}(n)
, with
l\geq 0
and

n\geq l+3
, may be obtained sequentially from

q_{l}(n)=\frac{(-1)^{l}(n-l-3)!}{(2l+3)!(n-2l-3)!}+\sum_{j=0}^{l-1}\frac{(-1)^{l+j+1}(n-l-1+j)!}{(2l+1-2j)!(n-2l-1+2j)!}~q_{j}(n+j-l).

For example,

q_{0}(n)=\frac{1}{6},\qquad q_{1}(n)=\frac{7}{360}n-\frac{1}{45},\qquad q_{2}(n)=\frac{31}{15120}n^{2}-\frac{89}{15120}~n+\frac{1}{315},\qquad\text{etc}.

4
Conclusions

Many things can be said about the entries in
\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}
, such as
those along the diagonal, i.e.,

\bigl|\boldsymbol{M}_{n,n}^{-1}\bigr|=\frac{(n!)^{2}}{(2n+1)!},

the first
sub-diagonal, i.e.,

\bigr|\boldsymbol{M}_{n\geq 2,n-1}^{-1}\bigr|=\frac{1}{6}(n-2)\frac{n!(n-1)!}{(2n-1)!},

the second sub-diagonal, i.e.,

\bigl|\boldsymbol{M}_{n\geq 3,n-2}^{-1}\bigr|=\frac{7}{360}\left(n-\frac{8}{7}\right)(n-3)\frac{n!(n-2)!}{(2n-3)!},

etc. But those things remain to be said later, and not here.

Some additional details have been worked out
explicitly in T.S. Van Kortryk, On Bernoulli numbers (private communication).

It is also possible to relate the terms in the finite monotonic series for
|B_{2n}|
to various partitions of the
infinite

monotonic series obtain from

|B_{2n}|=\frac{2(2n)!}{(2\pi)^{2n}}\zeta(2n)=\frac{2(2n)!}{(2\pi)^{2n}}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}1/k^{2n}.

But
that too remains to be discussed later, and not here.

Suffice it to say here that this monotonic finite series representation of
B_{2n}=(-1)^{n+1}|B_{2n}|
clearly gives a
series of progressively better bounds on
B_{2n}
. Such a series of
constraints on
B_{2n}
might be useful to establish bounds on functions
defined as infinite series whose coefficients involve the Bernoulli numbers

[
10
]
. But that remains to be shown.
Finally, as suggested by an anonymous reviewer, the identity (
2.2
)
may have an implicit connection to various multi-linear identities for
Bernoulli numbers
[
6
,
9
]
, perhaps due in part to the fact that such
“convolution” identities also appear in
the context of theoretical physics models
[
4
]
.

These pending developments notwithstanding, in closing it seems appropriate
to note the results presented here have already led to the addition of three
entries to the
Online Encyclopedia of Integer
Sequences
