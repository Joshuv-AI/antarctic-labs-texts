Velocity-vorticity-pressure formulation for the Oseen problem with variable viscosity Thanks: Updated: August 9, 2026.
V. Anaya, R. Caraballo, B. Gómez-Vargas, D. Mora, R. Ruiz-Baier

Abstract
We propose and analyse an augmented mixed finite element method for the Oseen equations written in terms of velocity, vorticity, and pressure with non-constant viscosity and homogeneous Dirichlet boundary condition for the velocity. The weak formulation includes least-squares terms arising from the constitutive equation and from the incompressibility condition, and we show that it satisfies the hypotheses of the Babuška-Brezzi theory. Repeating the arguments of the continuous analysis, the stability and solvability of the discrete problem are established. The method is suited for any Stokes inf-sup stable finite element pair for velocity and pressure, while for vorticity any generic discrete space (of arbitrary order) can be used. A priori and a posteriori error estimates are derived using two specific families of discrete subspaces. Finally, we provide a set of numerical tests illustrating the behaviour of the scheme, verifying the theoretical convergence rates, and showing the performance of the adaptive algorithm guided by residual a posteriori error estimation.



keywords
Oseen equations, velocity-vorticity-pressure formulation,
mixed finite element methods, variable viscosity, a priori and a posteriori
error analysis, adaptive mesh refinement.

Introduction

Using vorticity as additional field in the formulation of incompressible flow
equations can be advantageous in a number of applicative problems
[
47
]
.
Starting from the seminal works
[
25
,
26
]
that focused on Stokes equations and where vorticity was sought in
\mathrm{H}(\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits,\Omega)
,
several different problems including Brinkman, Navier-Stokes, and related flow problems written in terms of vorticity have been studied from the viewpoint of
numerical analysis of
finite volume and mixed finite element methods exhibiting diverse properties
and specific features. Some of these contributions include

[
3
,
2
,
4
,
7
,
23
,
12
,
45
,
46
]
.

The starting point is the Oseen equations
in the case of variable viscosity, and written
in terms of velocity
\boldsymbol{u}
and pressure
p
, as follows (see
[
35
]
):

(1.1a)

\displaystyle\sigma\boldsymbol{u}-2\boldsymbol{\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits}(\nu\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u}))+(\boldsymbol{\beta}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{u}+\nabla p

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{f}

\displaystyle\mbox{ in }\Omega,

(1.1b)

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{u}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\mbox{ in }\Omega,

(1.1c)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{0}

\displaystyle\mbox{ on }\Gamma,

(1.1d)

\displaystyle(p,1)_{0,\Omega}

\displaystyle=0,

where
\sigma>0
is inversely proportional to the time-step,

\boldsymbol{f}\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)^{d}
is a force density,

\boldsymbol{\beta}\in\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)^{d}
is the convecting velocity
field (not necessarily divergence-free),
and
\nu\in W^{1,\infty}(\Omega)
is the kinematic viscosity of the fluid,
satisfying

(1.2)

0<\nu_{0}\leq\nu\leq\nu_{1}.

Such a set of equations will appear, for instance,
in the linearisation of non-Newtonian flow problems,
as well as in applications where viscosity may depend
on temperature, concentration or volume fractions,
or other fields where the fluid flow patterns depend on
marked spatial distributions of viscosity

[
37
,
41
,
42
,
44
]
.
The specific literature related to the analysis of numerical schemes for
the Oseen equations in terms of vorticity includes the non-conforming
exponentially accurate least-squares spectral method
proposed in
[
40
]
, least-squares methods proposed
in
[
48
]
for Oseen and Navier-Stokes equations with velocity
boundary conditions,
the family of vorticity-based first-order Oseen-type systems studied in
[
21
]
,
the enhanced accuracy formulation in terms of velocity-vorticity-helicity
investigated in
[
11
]
, and the recent mixed (exactly divergence-free)
and DG discretisations for Oseen’s problem in velocity-vorticity-pressure
form given in
[
5
]
.
However, in most of these references, the derivation of the variational formulations
depends on the viscosity being constant. This is attributed to the fact that the usual vorticity-based weak formulation results from exploiting the following identity

(1.3)

\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits(\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{v})=-\Delta\boldsymbol{v}+\nabla(\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{v}),

applied to the viscous term.
However for a more general
friction term of the form
-\boldsymbol{\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits}(\nu\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u}))
,
where
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u})

is the strain rate tensor, the decomposition gives other additional terms that
do not permit the direct recasting of the coupled system as done in the
cited references above.

Extensions to cover the case of variable viscosity
do exist in the literature. For instance,
[
27
]

addresses the well-posedness of the vorticity–velocity
formulation of the Stokes problem with varying density
and viscosity, and the equivalence of the vorticity–velocity
and velocity–pressure formulations in appropriate functional
spaces is proved. More recently, in
[
6
]

we have taken a different approach and employed an
augmented vorticity-velocity-pressure formulation for Brinkman
equations with variable viscosity.
Here we extend that analysis to the
generalised Oseen equations with variable viscosity, and address in particular
how to deal with the additional challenges posed by the
presence of the convective term that did not appear in the Brinkman
momentum equation.

We will employ the so-called augmented formulations (also known as Galerkin
least-squares methods), which can be regarded as a stabilisation technique
where some terms are added to the variational formulation. Augmented finite
elements have been considered in several
works with applications in fluid mechanics
(see, e.g.,
[
8
,
9
,
13
,
20
,
17
,
18
,
34
,
43
]

and the references therein). These methods enjoy appealing advantages as those described in length in, e.g.,
[
14
,
16
]
, and reformulations of the set of equations following this approach are also of great importance in the design of block preconditioners (see
[
10
,
31
]
for an application in Oseen and Navier-Stokes equations in primal form,
[
30
]
for stress-velocity-pressure formulations for non-Newtonian flows, or
[
19
,
29
]
for stress-displacement-pressure mixed formulations for hyperelasticity).
In the particular context of our mixed formulation for Oseen equations, the augmentation assists us in deriving the Babuška-Brezzi property of ellipticity on the kernel needed for the top-left diagonal block.

The formulation that we employ is non-symmetric, and the augmentation terms
appear from least-squares contributions associated with the constitutive equation and
the incompressibility constraint. The mixed variational formulation is
shown to be well-posed under a condition on the viscosity bounds (a generalisation of the
usual condition needed in Oseen equations, (cf. Theorem
2.4
and Remark
2.6
).
Then we establish the well-posedness of
the discrete problem for generic inf-sup stable finite elements (for velocity
and pressure) in combination with a generic space for vorticity approximation.
We obtain error estimates for two stable families of finite elements.
We also derive a reliable and efficient residual-based
a posteriori error estimator for the mixed problem,
which can be fully computed locally.
In summary, the advantages of the proposed method
are the possibility to obtain directly
the vorticity field
with optimal accuracy and without the need of postprocessing;
moreover, different from many existing finite element methods
with vorticity field as unknown, the present contribution
supports variable viscosity and no-slip boundary
condition in a natural way.

The contents of the paper have been structured as follows. Functional
spaces and recurrent notation is collected in the remainder of
this section. Section
2
presents the governing equations
in terms of velocity, vorticity and pressure;
we state an augmented formulation,
and we perform the solvability analysis invoking
the Babuška–Brezzi theory. The finite element discretisation
is introduced in Section
3
, where we also derive the
stability analysis and optimal error estimates for two families of stable elements.
In Section
4
, we
develop the a posteriori error analysis.
Several numerical tests illustrating the convergence of the proposed
method under different scenarios are reported in Section
5
.

Preliminaries.

Let
\Omega
be a bounded domain of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d=2,3
, with Lipschitz boundary
\Gamma=\partial\Omega
.
For any
s\geq 0
, the notation
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{s,\Omega}
stands
for the norm of the Hilbertian Sobolev spaces
\mathrm{H}^{s}(\Omega)
or

\mathrm{H}^{s}(\Omega)^{d}
, with the usual convention
\mathrm{H}^{0}(\Omega):=\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)
.

Moreover,
c
and

C
, with or without subscripts, tildes, or hats, will represent a generic constant
independent of the mesh parameter
h
, assuming different values
in different occurrences. In addition,
for any vector field
\boldsymbol{v}=(v_{i})_{i=1}^{3}

and any scalar field
q
we recall the notation:

\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{v}=\sum_{i=1}^{3}\partial_{i}v_{i},\ \mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{v}=\begin{pmatrix}\partial_{2}v_{3}-\partial_{3}v_{2}\\
\partial_{3}v_{1}-\partial_{1}v_{3}\\
\partial_{1}v_{2}-\partial_{2}v_{1}\end{pmatrix},\ \nabla q=\begin{pmatrix}\partial_{1}q\\
\partial_{2}q\\
\partial_{3}q\end{pmatrix},

whereas for dimension
d=2
, the curl of a vector
\boldsymbol{v}
and a scalar
q
are
scalar function
\partial_{1}v_{2}-\partial_{2}v_{1}

and the vector
\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits q=(\partial_{2}q,\partial_{1}q)^{t}
, respectively.

Recall that, according to
[
35
, Theorem 2.11]
,
for a generic domain
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{3}
, the relevant
integration by parts formula corresponds to

\int_{\Omega}\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}=\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{v}+\langle\boldsymbol{\omega}\times\boldsymbol{n},\boldsymbol{v}\rangle_{\Gamma},

which in 2D reads as

(1.4)

\int_{\Omega}\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits\omega\cdot\boldsymbol{v}=\int_{\Omega}\omega\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{v}-\langle\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{t},\omega\rangle_{\Gamma}.

2
Vorticity-based formulation

With the aim of proposing
a vorticity-based formulation for (
1.1
), we consider
the following identities

\displaystyle-2\boldsymbol{\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits}(\nu\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u}))

\displaystyle=-2\nu\boldsymbol{\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits}(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u}))-2\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u})\nabla\nu=-\nu\Delta\boldsymbol{u}-2\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u})\nabla\nu

\displaystyle=\nu\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits(\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{u})-\nu\nabla(\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{u})-2\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u})\nabla\nu.

Therefore, problem (
1.1
) rewrites as

(2.5a)

\displaystyle\sigma\boldsymbol{u}+\nu\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{\omega}-2\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u})\nabla\nu+(\boldsymbol{\beta}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{u}+\nabla p

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{f}

\displaystyle\mbox{ in }\Omega,

(2.5b)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\omega}-\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{u}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{0}

\displaystyle\mbox{ in }\Omega,

(2.5c)

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{u}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\mbox{ in }\Omega,

(2.5d)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{0}

\displaystyle\mbox{ on }\Gamma,

(2.5e)

\displaystyle(p,1)_{0,\Omega}

\displaystyle=0,

where we have considered the definition of the vorticity
and have applied the incompressibility condition. The equations
state, respectively, the momentum conservation, the constitutive
relation, the mass balance, the no-slip boundary condition, and
the pressure closure condition.

2.1
Variational formulation for the Oseen equations
with non-constant viscosity

In this section, we propose a mixed variational formulation
of system (
2.5a
)-(
2.5e
). First,
we endow the space
\mathrm{H}_{0}^{1}(\Omega)^{d}
with the following norm:

|\!|\!|\boldsymbol{v}|\!|\!|_{1,\Omega}^{2}:=\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}+\|\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{v}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}+\|\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{v}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0},}

and note that for
\mathrm{H}_{0}^{1}(\Omega)^{d}
the above norm is equivalent
to the usual norm. In particular, we have that there
exists a positive constant
C_{pf}
such that:

\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{1,\Omega}^{2}\leq C_{pf}(\|\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{v}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}+\|\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{v}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2})\qquad\forall\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathrm{H}_{0}^{1}(\Omega)^{d},

where the above inequality is a consequence of the identity

(2.6)

\|\nabla\boldsymbol{v}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}=\|\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{v}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}+\|\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{v}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2},

which follows from (
1.3
)
and the Poincaré inequality. Moreover, in order to establish a weak formulation for (
2.5
),
we will use the following identity:

(2.7)

\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits(\phi\boldsymbol{v})=\nabla\phi\times\boldsymbol{v}+\phi\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{v},

valid for any vector field
\boldsymbol{v}

and any scalar field
\phi
.

After testing each equation of (
2.5a
)-(
2.5d
)
against adequate functions, using (
2.7
), and imposing
the boundary conditions, we end up with the following system:

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}(\sigma\boldsymbol{u}+(\boldsymbol{\beta}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{u})\cdot\boldsymbol{v}-2\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u})\nabla\nu\cdot\boldsymbol{v}+\int_{\Omega}\nu\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{v}+\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot(\nabla\nu\times\boldsymbol{v})-\int_{\Omega}p\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{v}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{f}\cdot\boldsymbol{v},

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\nu\boldsymbol{\theta}\cdot\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{u}-\int_{\Omega}\nu\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\boldsymbol{\theta}

\displaystyle=\,0,

\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega}q\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{u}

\displaystyle=\,0,

for all
(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{\theta},q)\in\mathrm{H}_{0}^{1}(\Omega)^{d}\times\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)^{d(d-1)/2}\times\mathrm{L}_{0}^{2}(\Omega)
,
where
\mathrm{L}_{0}^{2}(\Omega)\,:=\,\{q\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega):\ (q,1)_{0,\Omega}\,=\,0\}
.

Contrary to what is usually found in the the standard velocity-pressure mixed
formulation, the ellipticity on the kernel condition for
the Babuška-Brezzi theory is not straightforward
in the above mixed formulation. Here is where the augmentation
contributes to simplify the analysis. We introduce
the following residual terms arising from equations
(
2.5b
) and (
2.5c
):

\kappa_{1}\int_{\Omega}(\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{\omega})\cdot\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{v}=0,\qquad\kappa_{2}\int_{\Omega}\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{u}\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{v}=0\qquad\forall\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathrm{H}_{0}^{1}(\Omega)^{d},

where
\kappa_{1}
and
\kappa_{2}
are positive
parameters to be specified later on.

In this way, we propose the following augmented variational formulation
for (
2.5
):

Find
((\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{\omega}),p)\in(\mathrm{H}_{0}^{1}(\Omega)^{d}\times\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)^{d(d-1)/2})\times\mathrm{L}_{0}^{2}(\Omega)
such that

(2.8a)

\displaystyle A((\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{\omega}),(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{\theta}))+B((\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{\theta}),p)=

\displaystyle\;F(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle\qquad\forall(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{\theta})\in\mathrm{H}_{0}^{1}(\Omega)^{d}\times\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)^{d(d-1)/2},

(2.8b)

\displaystyle B((\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{\omega}),q)=

\displaystyle\;0

\displaystyle\qquad\forall q\in\mathrm{L}_{0}^{2}(\Omega),

where the bilinear forms and the linear functional are defined by

\displaystyle A((\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{\omega}),(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{\theta}))

\displaystyle:=\int_{\Omega}(\sigma\boldsymbol{u}+(\boldsymbol{\beta}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{u})\cdot\boldsymbol{v}+\int_{\Omega}\nu\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\boldsymbol{\theta}+\int_{\Omega}\nu\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{v}-\int_{\Omega}\nu\boldsymbol{\theta}\cdot\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{u}

\displaystyle\quad+\kappa_{1}\int_{\Omega}\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{v}+\kappa_{2}\int_{\Omega}\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{u}\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{v}-\kappa_{1}\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{v}

(2.9a)

\displaystyle\quad-2\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u})\nabla\nu\cdot\boldsymbol{v}+\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot(\nabla\nu\times\boldsymbol{v}),

(2.9b)

\displaystyle B((\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{\theta}),q)

\displaystyle:=-\int_{\Omega}q\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{v},

(2.9c)

\displaystyle F(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle:=\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{f}\cdot\boldsymbol{v},

for all
(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{\omega}),(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{\theta})\in\mathrm{H}_{0}^{1}(\Omega)^{d}\times\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)^{d(d-1)/2}
, and

q\in\mathrm{L}_{0}^{2}(\Omega)
.

As we will address in full detail in the next section,
the augmented mixed formulation
will permit us to analyse the problem directly
under the classical Babuška-Brezzi theory
[
16
]
.

2.2
Well-posedness analysis

In this section, we will address the well-posedness
of the proposed weak formulation (
2.8
).

In our analysis, we will need to invoke the following inequality, which
is a consequence of the Sobolev embedding
\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow\mathrm{L}^{4}(\Omega)

(2.10)

\left|\int_{\Omega}\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{\beta}(\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{v})\right|\leq\widehat{C}\|\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{0,\Omega}|\!|\!|\boldsymbol{u}|\!|\!|_{1,\Omega}|\!|\!|\boldsymbol{v}|\!|\!|_{1,\Omega}.

We will also make use of the following identity
(cf.
[
35
, Lemma 2.2]
)

(2.11)

\int_{\Omega}[(\boldsymbol{\beta}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{u}]\cdot\boldsymbol{v}+\int_{\Omega}[(\boldsymbol{\beta}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{v}]\cdot\boldsymbol{u}=-\int_{\Omega}\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{\beta}(\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}).

The continuity of the bilinear forms and the linear functional
(cf. (
2.9
)-(
2.9c
)), will be a consequence of the
following lemma, whose proof follows standard arguments in combination
with (
1.2
).

Lemma 2.1
.

The following estimates hold

\displaystyle\left|\sigma\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}\right|\leq\sigma\|\boldsymbol{u}\|_{0,\Omega}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{0,\Omega},\qquad\left|\int_{\Omega}\nu\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\boldsymbol{\theta}\right|\leq\nu_{1}\|\boldsymbol{\omega}\|_{0,\Omega}\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|_{0,\Omega},

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\Omega}[(\boldsymbol{\beta}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{u}]\cdot\boldsymbol{v}\right|\leq\widehat{C}|\!|\!|\boldsymbol{\beta}|\!|\!|_{1,\Omega}\|\nabla\boldsymbol{u}\|_{0,\Omega}|\!|\!|\boldsymbol{v}|\!|\!|_{1,\Omega},

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\Omega}\nu\boldsymbol{\theta}\cdot\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{v}\right|\leq\nu_{1}\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|_{0,\Omega}|\!|\!|\boldsymbol{v}|\!|\!|_{1,\Omega},\qquad\left|\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u})\nabla\nu\cdot\boldsymbol{v}\right|\leq\|\nabla\nu\|_{\infty,\Omega}\|\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u})\|_{0,\Omega}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{0,\Omega},

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\theta}\cdot(\nabla\nu\times\boldsymbol{v})\right|\leq 2\|\nabla\nu\|_{\infty,\Omega}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{0,\Omega}\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|_{0,\Omega},\qquad|F(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{\theta})|\leq\|\boldsymbol{f}\|_{0,\Omega}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{0,\Omega}.

As a consequence of the above lemma, there
exist constants
C_{1},C_{2},C_{3}>0
such that

\displaystyle|A((\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{\omega}),(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{\theta}))|\leq C_{1}\|(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{\omega})\|\|(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{\theta})\|,\qquad|B((\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{\theta}),q)|\leq C_{2}\|(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{\theta})\|\|q\|_{0,\Omega},

\displaystyle|F(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{\theta})|\leq C_{3}\|(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{\theta})\|,

with the product space norm defined as

\|(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{\theta})\|^{2}:=|\!|\!|\boldsymbol{v}|\!|\!|_{1,\Omega}^{2}+\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}.

The following lemma states the ellipticity of the bilinear form
A(\cdot,\cdot)
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Assume that

(2.12)

\sigma>\frac{9\|\nabla\nu\|_{\infty,\Omega}^{2}}{\nu_{0}}\quad\mbox{ and }\quad\widehat{C}\|\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{0,\Omega}<\min\left\{\sigma-\frac{9\|\nabla\nu\|_{\infty,\Omega}^{2}}{\nu_{0}},\dfrac{\nu_{0}}{12}\right\}.

Then, if we choose
\kappa_{1}=\frac{2}{3}\nu_{0}
and
\kappa_{2}>\dfrac{\nu_{0}}{3}
,
there exists a constant
\alpha>0
such that

A((\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{\theta}),(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{\theta}))\geq\alpha\|(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{\theta})\|^{2}\qquad\forall(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{\theta})\in\mathrm{H}_{0}^{1}(\Omega)^{d}\times\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)^{d(d-1)/2}.

Proof.

Let
(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{\theta})\in\mathrm{H}_{0}^{1}(\Omega)^{d}\times\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)^{d(d-1)/2}
.
As a consequence of Lemma
2.1
, we have that

\displaystyle\left|2\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{v})\nabla\nu\cdot\boldsymbol{v}\right|

\displaystyle\leq 2\|\nabla\nu\|_{\infty,\Omega}\left(\dfrac{\nu_{0}}{12\|\nabla\nu\|_{\infty,\Omega}}\|\nabla\boldsymbol{v}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}+\dfrac{3\|\nabla\nu\|_{\infty,\Omega}}{\nu_{0}}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}\right)

(2.13)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\nu_{0}}{6}(\|\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{v}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}+\|\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{v}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2})+\dfrac{6\|\nabla\nu\|_{\infty,\Omega}^{2}}{\nu_{0}}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2},

where we have used (
2.6
). Moreover, using that

\|(\nabla\nu\times\boldsymbol{v})\|_{0,\Omega}\leq 2\|\nabla\nu\|_{\infty,\Omega}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{0,\Omega}
, we get

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\theta}\cdot(\nabla\nu\times\boldsymbol{v})\right|

\displaystyle\leq 2\|\nabla\nu\|_{\infty,\Omega}\left(\dfrac{\nu_{0}}{6\|\nabla\nu\|_{\infty,\Omega}}\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}+\dfrac{3\|\nabla\nu\|_{\infty,\Omega}}{2\nu_{0}}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\nu_{0}}{3}\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}+\dfrac{3\|\nabla\nu\|_{\infty,\Omega}^{2}}{\nu_{0}}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2},

\displaystyle\left|\kappa_{1}\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\theta}\cdot\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{v}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\kappa_{1}\left(\dfrac{\nu_{0}}{3\kappa_{1}}\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}+\dfrac{3\kappa_{1}}{4\nu_{0}}\|\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{v}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}\right)

(2.14)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\nu_{0}}{3}\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}+\dfrac{3\kappa_{1}^{2}}{4\nu_{0}}\|\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{v}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}.

Thus, using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality,
(
2.2
)-(
2.14
), (
2.11
) and (
2.10
), we obtain

\displaystyle A((\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{\theta}),(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{\theta}))\geq

\displaystyle\sigma\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}+\int_{\Omega}[(\boldsymbol{\beta}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{v}]\cdot\boldsymbol{v}+\int_{\Omega}\nu|\boldsymbol{\theta}|^{2}+\kappa_{1}\|\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{v}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}+\kappa_{2}\|\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{v}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}

\displaystyle-\kappa_{1}\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\theta}\cdot\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{v}-2\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{v})\nabla\nu\cdot\boldsymbol{v}+\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\theta}\cdot(\nabla\nu\times\boldsymbol{v})

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\sigma\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}-\widehat{C}\|\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{0,\Omega}{|\!|\!|\boldsymbol{v}|\!|\!|_{1,\Omega}^{2}}+\nu_{0}\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}+\kappa_{1}\|\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{v}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}+\kappa_{2}\|\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{v}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}

\displaystyle-\dfrac{\nu_{0}}{3}\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}-\dfrac{3\kappa_{1}^{2}}{4\nu_{0}}\|\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{v}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}-\dfrac{\nu_{0}}{6}(\|\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{v}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}+\|\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{v}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2})

\displaystyle-\dfrac{6\|\nabla\nu\|_{\infty,\Omega}^{2}}{\nu_{0}}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}-\dfrac{\nu_{0}}{3}\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}-\dfrac{3\|\nabla\nu\|_{\infty,\Omega}^{2}}{\nu_{0}}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{\nu_{0}}{3}\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}+\left(\dfrac{\nu_{0}}{6}-\widehat{C}\|\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{0,\Omega}\right)\|\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{v}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}

\displaystyle+\left(\kappa_{2}-\dfrac{\nu_{0}}{6}-\widehat{C}\|\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{0,\Omega}\right)\|\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{v}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}

\displaystyle+\left(\sigma-\dfrac{9\|\nabla\nu\|_{\infty,\Omega}^{2}}{\nu_{0}}-\widehat{C}\|\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{0,\Omega}\right)\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}.

Now, using assumption (
2.12
), we have

A((\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{\theta}),(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{\theta}))\geq\alpha\|(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{\theta})\|^{2},

where

\alpha:=\min\left\{{\dfrac{\nu_{0}}{3}},\dfrac{\nu_{0}}{6}-\widehat{C}\|\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{0,\Omega},\kappa_{2}-\dfrac{\nu_{0}}{6}-\widehat{C}\|\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{0,\Omega},\sigma-\dfrac{9\|\nabla\nu\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}}{\nu_{0}}-\widehat{C}\|\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{0,\Omega}\right\},

which is clearly positive according to (
2.12
) and the assumptions on
\kappa_{1}

and
\kappa_{2}
.

Now we recall the following result related to the inf-sup condition: There exists
C>0
, depending only
on
\Omega
, such that (cf.
[
33
]
)

\sup_{0\neq\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathrm{H}_{0}^{1}(\Omega)^{d}}\frac{\left|\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}q\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{v}\right|}{\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{1,\Omega}}\geq C\|q\|_{0,\Omega}\quad\forall q\in\mathrm{L}_{0}^{2}(\Omega).

As a consequence, we immediately have the following lemma.

Lemma 2.3
.

There exists
\gamma>0
, independent of
\nu
, such that

\sup_{0\neq(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{\theta})\in\mathrm{H}_{0}^{1}(\Omega)^{d}\times\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)^{d(d-1)/2}}\frac{|B((\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{\theta}),q)|}{\|(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{\theta})\|}\geq\gamma\|q\|_{0,\Omega}\quad\forall q\in\mathrm{L}_{0}^{2}(\Omega).

We state the well-posedness of problem (
2.8
)
in the next theorem.

Theorem 2.4
.

Assume that the hypotheses of Lemma
2.2
hold true.
Then, there exists a unique solution

((\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{\omega}),p)\in(\mathrm{H}_{0}^{1}(\Omega)^{d}\times\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)^{d(d-1)/2})\times\mathrm{L}_{0}^{2}(\Omega)

to problem (
2.8
). Moreover, there exists
C>0

such that

\|(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{\omega})\|+\|p\|_{0,\Omega}\leq C\|\boldsymbol{f}\|_{0,\Omega}.

Proof.

The proof follows from Lemmas
2.2
and

2.3
, and a direct consequence of the Babuška-Brezzi
Theorem (
[
16
, Theorem II.1.1]
).

Remark 2.5
.

The unique solution of problem (
2.8
) also solves (
2.5a
)-(
2.5e
).
The equivalence follows essentially from applying integration by parts backwardly in
(
2.8
) and using suitable test functions.
This is employed in Section
4

to prove the efficiency of the a posteriori error estimator.

Remark 2.6
.

If
the convective velocity
\boldsymbol{\beta}\in\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)^{d}
is solenoidal
(i.e.,
\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{\beta}=0
in
\Omega
),
then problem (
2.8
) is well-posed after choosing
\kappa_{1}=\frac{2}{3}\nu_{0}
,
\kappa_{2}>\dfrac{\nu_{0}}{3}
, and assuming

(2.15)

\sigma\nu_{0}>{9\|\nabla\nu\|_{\infty,\Omega}^{2}}.

3
Numerical discretisation

Let
\{\mathcal{T}_{h}(\Omega)\}_{h>0}
be a shape-regular
family of partitions of the polygonal/polyhedral region

\bar{\Omega}
, by triangles/tetrahedrons
T
of diameter
h_{T}
,
with the meshsize defined as
h:=\max\{h_{T}:\,T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}(\Omega)\}
.
In what follows, given an integer
k\geq 0
and a subset

S
of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\mathbb{P}_{k}(S)
denotes the space of polynomial functions defined on
S
and being of degree
\leq

k
.

Now, we consider generic finite dimensional subspaces

\mathbf{V}_{h}\subseteq\mathrm{H}_{0}^{1}(\Omega)^{d}
,
\mathbf{W}_{h}\subseteq\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)^{d(d-1)/2}

and
Q_{h}\subseteq\mathrm{L}_{0}^{2}(\Omega)
such that the following
discrete inf-sup holds

(3.16)

\sup_{0\neq(\boldsymbol{v}_{h},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{h})\in\mathbf{V}_{h}\times\mathbf{W}_{h}}\frac{|B((\boldsymbol{v}_{h},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{h}),q_{h})|}{\|(\boldsymbol{v}_{h},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{h})\|}\geq\gamma_{0}\|q_{h}\|_{0,\Omega}\quad\forall q_{h}\in Q_{h},

where
\gamma_{0}>0
is independent of
h
.

In this way, the above inf-sup condition can be obtained
if (
\mathbf{V}_{h},Q_{h}
) is an inf-sup stable pair for
the classical Stokes problem. Moreover,
the discrete space
\mathbf{W}_{h}\subseteq\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)^{d(d-1)/2}

for the vorticity can be taken as continuous
or discontinuous polynomial space. Here
we will consider both options.

Now, we are in a position to introduce the finite element scheme
related to problem (
2.8
):
Find
((\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{\omega}_{h}),p_{h})\in(\mathbf{V}_{h}\times\mathbf{W}_{h})\times Q_{h}
such that

(3.17)

\begin{split}A((\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{\omega}_{h}),(\boldsymbol{v}_{h},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{h}))+B((\boldsymbol{v}_{h},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{h}),p_{h})=&\;F(\boldsymbol{v}_{h},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{h})\qquad\forall(\boldsymbol{v}_{h},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{h})\in\mathbf{V}_{h}\times\mathbf{W}_{h},\\
B((\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{\omega}_{h}),q_{h})=&\;0\qquad\qquad\quad\forall q\in Q_{h}.\end{split}

The next step is to establish the unique solvability
and convergence of the discrete problem (
3.17
).

Theorem 3.1
.

Assume that the hypotheses of Lemma
2.2
hold true.
Let
\mathbf{V}_{h}\subseteq\mathrm{H}_{0}^{1}(\Omega)^{d}
,
\mathbf{W}_{h}\subseteq\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)^{d(d-1)/2}

and
Q_{h}\subseteq\mathrm{L}_{0}^{2}(\Omega)
satisfy (
3.16
).
Then, there exists a unique
((\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{\omega}_{h}),p_{h})\in(\mathbf{V}_{h}\times\mathbf{W}_{h})\times Q_{h}

solution to (
3.17
).
Moreover, there exist
\hat{C}_{1},\,\hat{C}_{2}>0
,
independent of
h
, such that

\|(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{\omega}_{h})\|+\|p_{h}\|_{0,\Omega}\leq\hat{C}_{1}\|\boldsymbol{f}\|_{0,\Omega},

and

(3.18)

\begin{split}&\|(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{\omega})-(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{\omega}_{h})\|+\|p-p_{h}\|_{0,\Omega}\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\leq\hat{C}_{2}\inf_{(\boldsymbol{v}_{h},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{h},q_{h})\in\mathbf{V}_{h}\times\mathbf{W}_{h}\times\mathrm{Q}_{h}}(|\!|\!|\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{v}_{h}|\!|\!|_{1,\Omega}+\|\boldsymbol{\omega}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{h}\|_{0,\Omega}+\|p-q_{h}\|_{0,\Omega}),\end{split}

where
((\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{\omega}),p)\in(\mathrm{H}_{0}^{1}(\Omega)^{d}\times\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)^{d(d-1)/2})\times\mathrm{L}_{0}^{2}(\Omega)
is the
unique solution of (
2.8
).

3.1
Discrete subspaces and error estimates

In this section, we will define explicit families of finite element subspaces
yielding the unique solvability of the discrete scheme (
3.17
).
In addition, we derive the corresponding rate of convergence
for each family.

3.1.1
Taylor-Hood-
\mathbb{P}_{k}

We start by introducing a family based on Taylor-Hood
[
36
]
finite
elements for velocity and pressure, and continuous
or discontinuous piecewise polynomial spaces for vorticity.
More precisely, for any
k\geq 1
, we consider:

(3.19)

\begin{split}\mathbf{V}_{h}:&=\{\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\in C(\overline{\Omega})^{d}:\boldsymbol{v}_{h}|_{K}\in\mathbb{P}_{k+1}(K)^{d}\quad\forall K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\}\cap\mathrm{H}_{0}^{1}(\Omega)^{d},\\
Q_{h}:&=\{q_{h}\in C(\overline{\Omega}):q_{h}|_{K}\in\mathbb{P}_{k}(K)\quad\forall K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\}\cap\mathrm{L}_{0}^{2}(\Omega),\\
\mathbf{W}_{h}^{1}:&=\{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{h}\in C(\overline{\Omega})^{d(d-1)/2}:\boldsymbol{\theta}_{h}|_{K}\in\mathbb{P}_{k}(K)^{d(d-1)/2}\quad\forall K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\},\\
\mathbf{W}_{h}^{2}:&=\{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{h}\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)^{d(d-1)/2}:\boldsymbol{\theta}_{h}|_{K}\in\mathbb{P}_{k}(K)^{d(d-1)/2}\quad\forall K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\}.\end{split}

It is well known that
(\mathbf{V}_{h},Q_{h})
satisfies the inf-sup condition
(
3.16
)
[
15
]
.
In addition, we will consider continuous (
\mathbf{W}_{h}^{1}
)
and discontinuous (

Now, we recall the approximation properties of the
spaces specified in (
3.19
).
Assume that
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathrm{H}^{1+s}(\Omega)^{d}
,
p\in\mathrm{H}^{s}(\Omega)

and
\boldsymbol{\omega}\in\mathrm{H}^{s}(\Omega)^{d(d-1)/2}
, for some

s\in(1/2,k+1]

C>0
,
independent of

, such that

(3.20a)

\displaystyle\inf_{\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\in\mathbf{V}_{h}}|\!|\!|\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{v}_{h}|\!|\!|_{1,\Omega}

\displaystyle\leq Ch^{s}\|\boldsymbol{u}\|_{\mathrm{H}^{1+s}(\Omega)^{d}},

(3.20c)

\displaystyle\inf_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{h}\in\mathbf{W}_{h}^{1}}\|\boldsymbol{\omega}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{h}\|_{0,\Omega}

\displaystyle\inf_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{h}\in\mathbf{W}_{h}^{2}}\|\boldsymbol{\omega}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{h}\|_{0,\Omega}

The following theorem provides the rate of convergence of the augmented mixed

).

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
k\geq 1
be an integer, and let
\mathbf{V}_{h},Q_{h}
and

,

be specified by (

).
Let
(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{\omega},p)\in\mathrm{H}_{0}^{1}(\Omega)^{d}\times\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)^{d(d-1)/2}\times\mathrm{L}_{0}^{2}(\Omega)
and

(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{\omega}_{h},p_{h})\in\mathbf{V}_{h}\times\mathbf{W}_{h}^{i}\times Q_{h}
be the unique

) and
(
3.17
), respectively. Assume that

,

The proof follows from (

4
A posteriori error estimator

In this section, we propose a residual-based a posteriori
error estimator and prove its reliability and efficiency.
The analysis restricts to the two-dimensional case and using
continuous finite element approximations for vorticity. Nevertheless,
the extension to 3D and to discontinuous vorticity follows
straightforwardly.

For each
T\in{\mathcal{T}}_{h}
we let
{\mathcal{E}}(T)
be the set of edges of
T
, and we denote by
{\mathcal{E}}_{h}

the set of all edges in
{\mathcal{T}}_{h}
, that is

{\mathcal{E}}_{h}={\mathcal{E}}_{h}(\Omega)\cup{\mathcal{E}}_{h}(\Gamma),

where
{\mathcal{E}}_{h}(\Omega):=\{e\in{\mathcal{E}}_{h}:e\subset\Omega\}
, and

{\mathcal{E}}_{h}(\Gamma):=\{e\in{\mathcal{E}}_{h}:e\subset\Gamma\}
.
In what follows,
h_{e}
stands for the diameter of a given edge

e\in{\mathcal{E}}_{h}
,
\boldsymbol{t}_{e}=(-n_{2},n_{1})
, where
\boldsymbol{n}_{e}=(n_{1},n_{2})

is a fix unit normal vector of
e
.
Now, let
q\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)
such that
q|_{T}\in C(T)
for each
T\in{\mathcal{T}}_{h}
,
then given
e\in{\mathcal{E}}_{h}(\Omega)
, we denote by
[q]
the jump of
q
across
e
,
that is
[q]:=(q|_{T^{\prime}})|_{e}-(q|_{T^{\prime\prime}})|_{e}
, where
T^{\prime}
and
T^{\prime\prime}
are the triangles
of
{\mathcal{T}}_{h}
sharing the edge
e
. Moreover,
let
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)^{2}
such that
\boldsymbol{v}|_{T}\in C(T)^{2}
for each
T\in{\mathcal{T}}_{h}
.
Then, given
e\in{\mathcal{E}}_{h}(\Omega)
, we denote by
[\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{t}]
the tangential
jump of
\boldsymbol{v}
across
e
,
that is,
[\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{t}]:=\left((\boldsymbol{v}|_{T^{\prime}})|_{e}-(\boldsymbol{v}|_{T^{\prime\prime}})|_{e}\right)\cdot\boldsymbol{t}_{e}
,
where
T^{\prime}
and
T^{\prime\prime}
are the triangles
of
{\mathcal{T}}_{h}
sharing the edge
e
.

Next, let
k\geq 1
be an integer, and let
\mathbf{V}_{h},Q_{h}
and
\mathbf{W}_{h}^{1}

be given as in (
3.19
) or (
3.21
).
Let
(\boldsymbol{u},\omega,p)\in\mathrm{H}_{0}^{1}(\Omega)^{2}\times\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)\times\mathrm{L}_{0}^{2}(\Omega)
and

(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\omega_{h},p_{h})\in\mathbf{V}_{h}\times\mathbf{W}_{h}^{1}\times Q_{h}
be the unique solutions
to the continuous and discrete problems (
2.8
) and (
3.17
), respectively.
We introduce for each
T\in{\mathcal{T}}_{h}
the local
a posteriori
error indicator and its global counterpart as

\displaystyle\Theta_{T}^{2}:=

\displaystyle h_{T}^{2}\|\boldsymbol{f}-\sigma\boldsymbol{u}_{h}-\nu\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits\omega_{h}-(\boldsymbol{\beta}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{u}_{h}+2\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h})\nabla\nu-\nabla p_{h}\|_{0,T}^{2}

(4.22)

\displaystyle+\|\omega_{h}-\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\|_{0,T}^{2}+\|\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\|_{0,T}^{2},\qquad\Theta^{2}:=\sum_{T\in{\mathcal{T}}_{h}}\Theta_{T}^{2}.

Let us now establish reliability and efficiency of (
4.22
).

4.1
Reliability

We begin by recalling that the
continuous dependence result given in Theorem
2.4

is equivalent to the global inf–sup condition for the
continuous formulation (
2.8
). Then,
applying this estimate to the error

(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\omega-\omega_{h},p-p_{h})
,
we obtain

(4.23)

\|(\boldsymbol{u},\omega)-(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\omega_{h})\|+\|p-p_{h}\|_{0,\Omega}\leq C_{glob}\sup_{(\boldsymbol{v}_{h},\theta_{h},q_{h})\in}\frac{\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{v},\theta,q)}{\|(\boldsymbol{v},\theta,q)\|},

where the residual functional
\mathcal{R}
is defined by

(4.24)

\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{v},\theta,q)=A((\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\omega-\omega_{h}),(\boldsymbol{v},\theta))+B((\boldsymbol{v},\theta),{p-p_{h}})+B((\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\omega-\omega_{h}),q),

for all
(\boldsymbol{v},\theta,q)\in\mathrm{H}_{0}^{1}(\Omega)^{2}\times\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)\times\mathrm{L}_{0}^{2}(\Omega)
.

Some technical results are provided beforehand.
Let us first recall the Clément-type interpolation operator

\mathcal{I}_{h}:\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\to Y_{h}
, where

Y_{h}:=\{v_{h}\in C(\overline{\Omega})\cap\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega):v_{h}\Big|_{T}\in\mathbb{P}_{1}(T),\forall T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\}.

This operator satisfies the following local
approximation properties (cf.
[
22
]
).

Lemma 4.1
.

There exist positive constants
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
such that for all

v\in\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
there hold

(4.25a)

\displaystyle\|v-\mathcal{I}_{h}v\|_{0,T}

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}h_{T}|v|_{1,w_{T}}\quad\forall T\in\mathcal{T}_{h},

(4.25b)

\displaystyle\|v-\mathcal{I}_{h}v\|_{0,e}

\displaystyle\leq C_{2}h^{1/2}_{e}|v|_{1,w_{e}}\quad\forall e\in{\mathcal{E}}_{h}(\Omega),

where
w_{T}:=\bigcup\{T^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}_{h}:T^{\prime}\cap T\neq\emptyset\}

and
w_{e}:=\bigcup\{T^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}_{h}:T^{\prime}\cap e\neq\emptyset\}
.

The main result of this section is stated as follows.

Theorem 4.2
.

There exists a positive constant
C_{\mathrm{rel}}
,
independent of
h
, such that

(4.26)

\|(\boldsymbol{u},\omega)-(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\omega_{h})\|+\|p-p_{h}\|_{0,\Omega}\leq{C}_{\mathrm{rel}}\;\Theta.

Proof.

From (
4.24
) and the continuous
problem (
2.8
), we have that,

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{v},\theta,q)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{f}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}-\Big(A((\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\omega_{h}),(\boldsymbol{v},\theta))+B((\boldsymbol{v},\theta),p_{h})+B((\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\omega_{h}),q)\Big)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\big(\boldsymbol{f}-\sigma\boldsymbol{u}_{h}-(\boldsymbol{\beta}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{u}_{h}+2\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h})\nabla\nu\big)\cdot\boldsymbol{v}-\int_{\Omega}\nu(\omega_{h}-\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{u}_{h})\theta

\displaystyle\quad-\kappa_{1}\int_{\Omega}(\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{u}_{h}-\omega_{h})\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{v}-\kappa_{2}\int_{\Omega}\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{v}

\displaystyle\quad-\left(\int_{\Omega}\nu\omega_{h}\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{v}+\int_{\Omega}\omega_{h}(\nabla\nu\times\boldsymbol{v})\right)+\int_{\Omega}p_{h}\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{v}+\int_{\Omega}q\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{u}_{h}.

Using the identity
\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits(\nu\boldsymbol{v})=\nabla\nu\times\boldsymbol{v}+\nu\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{v}

and integration by parts on the above residual (cf. (
1.4
)), we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{v},\theta,q)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}(\boldsymbol{f}-\sigma\boldsymbol{u}_{h}-(\boldsymbol{\beta}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{u}_{h}+2\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h})\nabla\nu)\cdot\boldsymbol{v}-\int_{\Omega}\nu(\omega_{h}-\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{u}_{h})\theta

\displaystyle-\kappa_{1}\int_{\Omega}(\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{u}_{h}-\omega_{h})\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{v}-\kappa_{2}\int_{\Omega}\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{v}+\int_{\Omega}q\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{u}_{h}

\displaystyle-\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\left(\int_{T}\nu\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits\omega_{h}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}-\langle\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{t},\nu\omega_{h}\rangle_{\partial T}-\int_{T}\nabla p_{h}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}+\langle\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{n},p_{h}\rangle_{\partial T}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\int_{T}(\boldsymbol{f}-\sigma\boldsymbol{u}_{h}-\nu\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits\omega_{h}-(\boldsymbol{\beta}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{u}_{h}+2\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h})\nabla\nu-\nabla p_{h})\cdot\boldsymbol{v}

\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega}\nu(\omega_{h}-\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{u}_{h})\theta-\kappa_{1}\int_{\Omega}(\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{u}_{h}-\omega_{h})\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{v}-\kappa_{2}\int_{\Omega}\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{v}+\int_{\Omega}q\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{u}_{h},

where we have used the fact that
\omega_{h}
and
p_{h}

are piecewise continuous functions.
Hence, since from (
4.24
) we have
\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{v}_{h},\theta_{h},q_{h})=0
,
we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{v},\theta,q)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{v}_{h},\theta-\theta_{h},q-q_{h})

\displaystyle=\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\int_{T}\big(\boldsymbol{f}-\sigma\boldsymbol{u}_{h}-\nu\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits\omega_{h}-(\boldsymbol{\beta}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{u}_{h}+2\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h})\nabla\nu-\nabla p_{h}\big)\cdot(\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{v}_{h})

\displaystyle\quad-\int_{\Omega}\nu(\omega_{h}-\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{u}_{h})(\theta-\theta_{h})-\kappa_{1}\int_{\Omega}(\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{u}_{h}-\omega_{h})\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{v}_{h})

\displaystyle\quad-\kappa_{2}\int_{\Omega}\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{v}_{h})+\int_{\Omega}(q-q_{h})\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{u}_{h}.

Thus, it suffices to take
\boldsymbol{v}_{h}:=\mathcal{I}_{h}(\boldsymbol{v})
(cf. Lemma
4.1
),
and
\theta_{h}:=\Pi(\theta)
and
q_{h}:=\Pi(q)
with

\Pi
being the
L^{2}
-projection onto piecewise constants. And then, using the
Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, triangle inequality, properties
for
\mathcal{I}_{h}
given by Lemma
4.1
and
[
28
, Lemma 1.127]
,
and approximation properties for
\Pi
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{v},\theta,q)

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}h_{T}\|\boldsymbol{f}-\sigma\boldsymbol{u}_{h}-\nu\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits\omega_{h}-(\boldsymbol{\beta}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{u}_{h}+2\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h})\nabla\nu-\nabla p_{h}\|_{0,T}|\boldsymbol{v}|_{1,w_{T}}

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}(\nu_{1}+\kappa_{1})\|\omega_{h}-\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\|_{0,T}(C_{3}\|\theta\|_{0,T}+|\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{v}_{h}|_{1,T})

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}(\kappa_{2}+1)\|\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\|_{0,T}(|\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{v}_{h}|_{1,T}+C_{4}\|q\|_{0,T})

\displaystyle\leq\widehat{C}_{1}\left(\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}h_{T}^{2}\|f-\sigma\boldsymbol{u}_{h}-\nu\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits\omega_{h}-(\boldsymbol{\beta}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{u}_{h}+2\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h})\nabla\nu-\nabla p_{h}\|_{0,T}^{2}\right)^{1/2}\!\!\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{1,\Omega}

\displaystyle\quad+\widehat{C}_{2}\left(\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\|\omega_{h}-\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\|_{0,T}^{2}\right)^{1/2}(\|\theta\|_{0,\Omega}+{\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{1,\Omega}})

\displaystyle\quad+\widehat{C}_{3}\left(\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\|\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\|_{0,T}^{2}\right)^{1/2}(\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{1,\Omega}+\|q\|_{0,\Omega}).

And the proof of (
4.26
) follows from (
4.23
)
and the above estimate.

4.2
Efficiency

This subsection deals with the efficiency of the
a posteriori

error estimator. For simplicity, we will assume that
the given convective velocity
\boldsymbol{\beta}
and the viscosity
\nu

are polynomial functions both of degree
s
.
The general case can be proved by repeating the same arguments
and requiring an additional regularity for the data.

A major role in the proof of efficiency is played by element and edge bubbles (locally supported non-negative functions), whose definition we recall in what follows.
For
T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}(\Omega)
and
e\in{\mathcal{E}}(T)
, let
\psi_{T}
and
\psi_{e}
, respectively, be the interior and edge bubble functions defined as in, e.g.,
[
1
]
. Let
\psi_{T}\in\mathbb{P}_{3}(T)
with
supp(\psi_{T})\subset T,

\psi_{T}=0
on
\partial T
and

0\leq\psi_{T}\leq 1
in
T.
Moreover, let
\psi_{e}|_{T}\in\mathbb{P}_{2}(T)
with
supp(\psi_{e})\subset\Omega_{e}:=\{T^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}_{h}(\Omega):e\in{\mathcal{E}}(T^{\prime})\},

\psi_{e}=0
on
\partial T\setminus e,
and
0\leq\psi_{e}\leq 1
in
\Omega_{e}.
Again, let us recall an extension operator
E:C^{0}(e)\mapsto C^{0}(T)
that satisfies
E(q)\in\mathbb{P}_{k}(T)
and
E(q)|_{e}=q
for all
q\in\mathbb{P}_{k}(e)
and for all

k\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}.

We now summarise the properties of
\psi_{T},\psi_{e}
and
E

in the following lemma (see
[
1
,
49
]
).

Lemma 4.3
.

The following properties hold:

(i)

For
T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
and for
v\in\mathbb{P}_{k}(T)
, there is a positive constant
C_{1}
such that

\displaystyle C_{1}^{-1}\;\|v\|^{2}_{0,T}\leq\int_{T}\psi_{T}v^{2}\,\mbox{d}x\leq C_{1}\|v\|^{2}_{0,T},\qquad C_{1}^{-1}\;\|v\|^{2}_{0,T}\leq\|\psi v\|^{2}_{0,T}+h_{T}^{2}|\psi v|^{2}_{1,T}\leq C_{1}\|v\|^{2}_{0,T}.

(ii)

For
e\in{\mathcal{E}}_{h}
and
v\in\mathbb{P}_{k}(e)
, there exists a positive constant, say
C_{1}
, such that

C_{1}^{-1}\;\|v\|^{2}_{0,e}\leq\int_{e}\psi_{e}v^{2}ds\leq C_{1}\|v\|^{2}_{0,e}.

(iii)

For
T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
,
e\in{\mathcal{E}}(T)
and for
v\in\mathbb{P}_{k}(e)
, there is a positive constant,
again say
C_{1}
,
such that

\|\psi_{e}^{1/2}\;E(v)\|^{2}_{0,T}\leq C_{1}h_{e}\;\|v\|^{2}_{0,e}.

The following classical result which states
an inverse estimate will also be used.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
k,l,m\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
such that
l\leq m
. Then, there exists
\tilde{C}>0
, depending only on

k,l,m
and the shape regularity of the triangulations, such that for each triangle
T

there holds

|q|_{m,T}\leq\tilde{C}h_{T}^{l-m}|q|_{l,T}\quad\forall q\in\mathbb{P}_{k}(T).

In order to prove the efficiency of the a posteriori error estimator, we will bound
each term defining
\Theta_{T}
in terms of local errors.

Theorem 4.5
.

There is a positive constant
C_{\mathrm{eff}}
, independent of
h
, such that

C_{\mathrm{eff}}\;\Theta\leq\;\|(\boldsymbol{u},\omega)-(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\omega_{h})\|+\|p-p_{h}\|_{0,\Omega}+\mathrm{h.o.t.},

where
\mathrm{h.o.t.}
denotes higher-order terms.

Proof.

Using that
\omega-\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{u}=0
and
\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{u}=0
in
\Omega

(see (
2.5b
) and (
2.5c
), respectively), we immediately have that

\|\omega_{h}-\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\|_{0,T}+\|\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\|_{0,T}\leq\|\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h})\|_{0,T}+\|\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h})\|_{0,T}+\|\omega-\omega_{h}\|_{0,T}.

On the other hand, with the help of the
\mathrm{L}^{2}(T)^{2}
-orthogonal
projection
\mathcal{P}_{T}^{\ell}
onto
\mathbb{P}_{\ell}(T)^{2}
, for
\ell\geq(s+k+1),

with respect to the weighted
\mathrm{L}^{2}
-inner product
(\psi_{T}\boldsymbol{f},\boldsymbol{g})_{0,T}
, for

\boldsymbol{f},\boldsymbol{g}\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(T)^{2},
it now follows that

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{f}-\sigma\boldsymbol{u}_{h}-

\displaystyle\nu\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits\omega_{h}-(\boldsymbol{\beta}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{u}_{h}+2\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h})\nabla\nu-\nabla p_{h}\|_{0,T}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|\boldsymbol{f}-\mathcal{P}_{T}^{\ell}(\boldsymbol{f})+\mathcal{P}_{T}^{\ell}(\boldsymbol{f})-\sigma\boldsymbol{u}_{h}-\nu\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits\omega_{h}-(\boldsymbol{\beta}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{u}_{h}+2\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h})\nabla\nu-\nabla p_{h}\|_{0,T}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|\boldsymbol{f}-\mathcal{P}_{T}^{\ell}(\boldsymbol{f})\|_{0,T}^{2}+\|\mathcal{P}_{T}^{\ell}(\boldsymbol{f})-\sigma\boldsymbol{u}_{h}-\nu\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits\omega_{h}-(\boldsymbol{\beta}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{u}_{h}+2\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h})\nabla\nu-\nabla p_{h}\|_{0,T}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|\boldsymbol{f}-\mathcal{P}_{T}^{\ell}(\boldsymbol{f})\|_{0,T}^{2}+\|\mathcal{P}_{T}^{\ell}(\boldsymbol{f}-\sigma\boldsymbol{u}_{h}-\nu\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits\omega_{h}-(\boldsymbol{\beta}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{u}_{h}+2\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h})\nabla\nu-\nabla p_{h})\|_{0,T}^{2}.

For the second term on the right-hand side, an application of Lemma
4.3
shows that

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{P}_{T}^{\ell}(\boldsymbol{f}-\sigma\boldsymbol{u}_{h}

\displaystyle-\nu\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits\omega_{h}-(\boldsymbol{\beta}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{u}_{h}+2\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h})\nabla\nu-\nabla p_{h})\|_{0,T}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|\psi_{T}^{1/2}\mathcal{P}_{T}^{\ell}(\boldsymbol{f}-\sigma\boldsymbol{u}_{h}-\nu\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits\omega_{h}-(\boldsymbol{\beta}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{u}_{h}+2\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h})\nabla\nu-\nabla p_{h})\|_{0,T}^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{T}\psi_{T}\mathcal{P}_{T}^{\ell}(\boldsymbol{f}-\sigma\boldsymbol{u}_{h}-\nu\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits\omega_{h}-(\boldsymbol{\beta}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{u}_{h}+2\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h})\nabla\nu-\nabla p_{h})

\displaystyle\qquad\times(\boldsymbol{f}-\sigma\boldsymbol{u}_{h}-\nu\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits\omega_{h}-(\boldsymbol{\beta}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{u}_{h}+2\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h})\nabla\nu-\nabla p_{h}),

where we have used the fact that
\mathcal{P}_{T}^{\ell}
is the
L^{2}(T)^{2}
-orthogonal projection.
Thus, from the above inequality, and (
2.5a
) (cf. Remark
2.5
), we can deduce that

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{P}_{T}^{\ell}(\boldsymbol{f}

\displaystyle-\sigma\boldsymbol{u}_{h}-\nu\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits\omega_{h}-(\boldsymbol{\beta}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{u}_{h}+2\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h})\nabla\nu-\nabla p_{h})\|_{0,T}^{2}

\displaystyle{\leq}\int_{T}\psi_{T}\mathcal{P}_{T}^{\ell}(\boldsymbol{f}-\sigma\boldsymbol{u}_{h}-\nu\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits\omega_{h}-(\boldsymbol{\beta}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{u}_{h}+2\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h})\nabla\nu-\nabla p_{h})

\displaystyle\quad\times(\sigma(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h})+\nu\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits(\omega-\omega_{h})+(\boldsymbol{\beta}\cdot\nabla)(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h})-2\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h})\nabla\nu+\nabla(p-p_{h})).

Next, using that the viscosity is a polynomial function,
the bound follows by integration by parts on the
terms
\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits(\omega-\omega_{h})
and
\nabla(p-p_{h})
,
Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and an inverse inequality (cf. Lemma
4.4
).
We end the proof by observing that the required efficiency
bound follows straightforwardly from
the estimates above, and after assuming additional regularity for

\boldsymbol{f}
.

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\beta}=\boldsymbol{u},\quad\nu_{d}(x,y)=\nu_{0}+\dfrac{721}{16}(\nu_{1}-\nu_{0})x^{2}(1-x)y^{2}(1-y),\quad\nu_{0}=0.1,\quad\nu_{1}=1,

\displaystyle\nu_{e}(x,y)=\nu_{0}+(\nu_{1}-\nu_{0})\exp(-10^{12}((x-0.5)^{10}+(y-0.5)^{10})),\quad\kappa_{1}=\frac{2}{3}\nu_{0},\quad\kappa_{2}=\frac{\nu_{0}}{2},\quad\sigma=10.

Use
blue-green

5.4
Example 4: Steady blood flow in aortic arch

We finalise the set of examples with a simple simulation of pseudo-stationary blood flow in an aorta. The patient-specific geometry
[
38
,
39
]
has one inlet (a segment that connects with the pre-aortic root coming from the aortic valve in the heart) and four outlets (the left common carotid artery, the left subclavian artery, the innominate artery, and the larger descending aorta). On the inlet we impose a Poiseuille profile of magnitude 4, on the vessel walls we set no-slip conditions, and on the remaining boundaries we set zero normal stresses (more physiologically relevant boundary conditions can be considered following, e.g.,
[
24
,
32
]
). The initial unstructured mesh has 46352 tetrahedral elements.
The synthetic variable viscosity field is a smooth exponential function
\nu=\nu_{0}+(\nu_{1}-\nu_{0})\exp(-10^{3}[(x-0.1)^{6}+y-0.5)^{6}+(z-0.5)^{6}])
with
\nu_{0}=10^{-3},\nu_{1}=10
that entails an average Reynolds number of approximately 60 (computed using the inlet diameter and maximal inlet velocity), while the convecting velocity is computed as the solution of a preliminary Stokes problem (on the initial coarse mesh), and we prescribe
\sigma=1000
and
\boldsymbol{f}=\sigma\boldsymbol{\beta}
. Then we compute numerical solutions of the Oseen problem and
apply four steps of adaptive mesh refinement using a 3D version of the estimator (
4.22
) and the algorithm
described in the previous example.
The results are portrayed in Figure
5.3
, plotting pressure distribution, velocity streamlines, vorticity, and a sample of the resulting adaptive mesh which shows more refinement near the boundaries of the descending aorta. For this test we have used a conforming approximation of vorticity.
