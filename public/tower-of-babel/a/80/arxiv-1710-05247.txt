On Hashing-Based Approaches to Approximate DNF-Counting The author list has been sorted alphabetically by last name; this should not be used to determine the extent of authors’ contributions
Kuldeep S. Meel, Aditya A. Shrotri, Moshe Y. Vardi

Abstract
Propositional model counting is a fundamental problem in artificial intelligence with a wide variety of applications, such as probabilistic inference, decision making under uncertainty, and probabilistic databases. Consequently, the problem is of theoretical as well as practical interest. When the constraints are expressed as DNF formulas, Monte Carlo-based techniques have been shown to provide a fully polynomial randomized approximation scheme (FPRAS). For CNF constraints, hashing-based approximation techniques have been demonstrated to be highly successful. Furthermore, it was shown that hashing-based techniques also yield an FPRAS for DNF counting without usage of Monte Carlo sampling. Our analysis, however, shows that the proposed hashing-based approach to DNF counting provides poor time complexity compared to the Monte Carlo-based DNF counting techniques. Given the success of hashing-based techniques for CNF constraints, it is natural to ask: Can hashing-based techniques provide an efficient FPRAS for DNF counting? In this paper, we provide a positive answer to this question. To this end, we introduce two novel algorithmic techniques: Symbolic Hashing and Stochastic Cell Counting , along with a new hash family of Row-Echelon hash functions . These innovations allow us to design a hashing-based FPRAS for DNF counting of similar complexity (up to polylog factors) as that of prior works. Furthermore, we expect these techniques to have potential applications beyond DNF counting.

Preliminaries

DNF Formulas and Counting

We use Greek letters
\phi
,
\theta
and
\psi
to denote boolean formulas. A formula
\phi
over boolean variables
x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{\mathsf{n}}
is in Disjunctive Normal Form (DNF) if it is a disjunction over conjunctions of variables or their negations. We use
X
to denote the set of variables appearing in the formula. Each occurrence of a variable or its negation is called a
literal
. Disjuncts in the formula are called
cubes
and we denote the
i^{th}
cube by
\phi^{Ci}
. Thus
\phi=\phi^{C1}\lor\phi^{C2}\lor...\lor\phi^{C\mathsf{m}}
where each
\phi^{Ci}
is a conjunction of
literals
. We will use
\mathsf{n}
and
\mathsf{m}
to denote the number of variables and number of cubes in the input DNF formula, respectively. The number of literals in a cube
\phi^{Ci}
is called its
width
and is denoted by
0pt{i}
.

An assignment to all the variables can be represented by a vector
\boldsymbol{x}\in\{0,1\}^{\mathsf{n}}
with
1
corresponding to
true
and
0
to
false
.
U=\{0,1\}^{\mathsf{n}}
is the set of all possible assignments, which we refer to as the
universe
or state space interchangeably. An assignment
\boldsymbol{x}
is called a satisfying assignment for a formula
\phi
if
\phi
evaluates to
true
under
\boldsymbol{x}
. In other words
\boldsymbol{x}
satisfies
\phi
and is denoted as
\boldsymbol{x}\models\phi
. Note that an assignment
\boldsymbol{x}
will satisfy a DNF formula
\phi
if
\boldsymbol{x}\models\phi^{Ci}
for some
i
. The DNF-Counting Problem is to count the number of satisfying assignments of a DNF formula.

Next, we formalize the concept of a counting problem. Let
R\subseteq\{0,1\}^{*}\times\{0,1\}^{*}
be a relation which is decidable in polynomial time and there is a polynomial
p
such that for every
(s,t)\in R
we have
|t|\leq p(|s|)
. The decision problem corresponding to
R
asks if for a given
s
there exists a
t
such that
(s,t)\in R
. Such a problem is in NP. Here,
s
is a called the
problem instance
and
t
is called the
witness
. We denote the set of all witnesses for a given
s
by
R_{s}
. The
counting problem
corresponding to
R
is to calculate the size of the witness set
|R_{s}|
for a given
s
. Such a problem is in
\mathsf{\#P}
[
29
]
.
The DNF-Counting problem is an example of this formalism: A formula
\phi
is a problem instance and a satisfying assignment
\boldsymbol{x}
is a witness of
\phi
. The set of satisfying assignments or the solution space is denoted
R_{\phi}
and the goal is to compute
|R_{\phi}|
. It is known that the problem is
\mathsf{\#P}
-Complete, which is believed to be intractable
[
27
]
. Therefore, we look at what it means to efficiently and accurately approximate this problem.

A
fully polynomial randomized approximation scheme
(FPRAS) is a randomized algorithm that takes as input a problem instance
s
, a tolerance
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
and confidence parameter
\delta\in(0,1)
and outputs a random variable
c
such that

\Pr[\frac{1}{1+\varepsilon}|R_{s}|\leq c\leq(1+\varepsilon)|R_{s}|]\geq 1-\delta

and the running time of the algorithm is polynomial in
|s|
,
1/\varepsilon
,
\log(1/\delta)

[
17
]
.
Notably, while exact DNF-counting is inter-reducible with exact CNF-counting, the approximate versions of the two problems are not because multiplicative approximation is not closed under complementation.

Hash Functions

A hash function
h:\{0,1\}^{\mathsf{q}}\rightarrow\{0,1\}^{\mathsf{p}}
partitions the elements of of the domain
\{0,1\}^{\mathsf{q}}
into
2^{\mathsf{p}}
cells.
h(\boldsymbol{x})=\boldsymbol{y}
implies that
h
maps the assignment
\boldsymbol{x}
to the cell
\boldsymbol{y}
.
h^{-1}(\boldsymbol{y})=\{\boldsymbol{x}|h(\boldsymbol{x})=\boldsymbol{y}\}
is the set of assignments that map to the cell
\boldsymbol{y}
. In the context of counting, 2-universal families of hash functions, denoted by
H(\mathsf{q},\mathsf{p},2)
, are of particular importance. When
h
is sampled uniformly at random from
H(\mathsf{q},\mathsf{p},2)
, 2-universality entails

1.

\Pr[h(\boldsymbol{x}_{1})=h(\boldsymbol{x}_{2})]\leq 2^{-\mathsf{p}}
for all
\boldsymbol{x}_{1}\neq\boldsymbol{x}_{2}

2.

\Pr[h(\boldsymbol{x})=\boldsymbol{y}]=2^{-\mathsf{p}}
for every
\boldsymbol{x}\in\{0,1\}^{\mathsf{q}}
and
\boldsymbol{y}\in\{0,1\}^{\mathsf{p}}
.

Of particular interest is the random XOR family of hash functions, which is defined as
H_{XOR}(\mathsf{q},\mathsf{p})=\{\boldsymbol{A}.\boldsymbol{x}\oplus\boldsymbol{b}\,\,|\,\,\boldsymbol{A}[i,j]\in\{0,1\}\,\,and\,\,\boldsymbol{b}[i]\in\{0,1\}\,\,;\,\,0\leq i<\mathsf{p},\,\,0\leq j<\mathsf{q}\}
. Selecting
\boldsymbol{A}[i,j]s
and
\boldsymbol{b}[i]
s randomly from
\{0,1\}
is equivalent to drawing uniformly at random from this family. A pair
\boldsymbol{A}
and
\boldsymbol{b}
now defines a hash function
h_{\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{b}}
as follows:
h_{\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{b}}(x)=\boldsymbol{A}.\boldsymbol{x}\oplus\boldsymbol{b}
. This family was shown to be 2-universal in
[
2
]
. For a hash function
h\in H_{XOR}(\mathsf{q},\mathsf{p})
, we have that
h(\boldsymbol{x})=\boldsymbol{y}
is a system of linear equations modulo 2:
\boldsymbol{A}.\boldsymbol{x}\oplus\boldsymbol{b}=\boldsymbol{y}
. From another perspective, it can be viewed as a boolean formula
\psi=\bigwedge_{i=1}^{\mathsf{p}}(\bigoplus_{j=1}^{\mathsf{q}}(\boldsymbol{A}[i,j]\land\boldsymbol{x}[j]))\oplus\boldsymbol{b}[i]=\boldsymbol{y}[i]
. The solutions to this formula are exactly the elements of the set
h^{-1}(\boldsymbol{y})
.

Gaussian Elimination

Solving a system of linear equations over
\mathsf{q}
variables and
\mathsf{p}
constraints can be done by row reduction technique known variously as
Gaussian Elimination
or
Gauss-Jordan Elimination
. A matrix is in
Row-Echelon form
if rows with at least one nonzero element are above any rows of all zeros. The matrix is in
Reduced Row-Echelon form
if, in addition, every leading non-zero element in a row is 1 and is the only nonzero entry in its column. We refer to the technique for obtaining the
Reduced Row-Echelon
form of a matrix as Gaussian Elimination. We refer the reader to any standard text on linear algebra (cf.,
[
26
]
) for details. For a matrix in Reduced Row-Echelon form, the row-rank is simply the number of non-zero rows.

For a system of linear equations
\boldsymbol{A}.\boldsymbol{x}\oplus\boldsymbol{b}=\boldsymbol{y}
, if the row-rank of the augmented matrix is same as row-rank of
\boldsymbol{A}
, then the system is consistent and the number of solutions is
2^{\mathsf{q}-rowrank(\boldsymbol{A})}
where
\mathsf{q}
is the number of variables in the system of equations.
Moreover, if
\boldsymbol{A}
is in Reduced Row-Echelon form, then the values of the variables corresponding to leading
1
s in each row are completely determined by the values assigned to the remaining variables. The variables corresponding to the leading 1’s are called
dependent
variables and the remaining variables are
free
. Let
X_{F}
and
X\setminus X_{F}
denote the set of free and dependent variables respectively. Let
f=|X_{F}|
. Clearly
f=\mathsf{q}-rowrank(\boldsymbol{A})
. For each possible assignment to the free variables we get an assignment to the dependent variables by propagating the values through the augmented matrix in
\mathcal{O}(\mathsf{q}^{2})
time. Thus we can enumerate all
2^{f}
satisfying assignments to a system of linear equations
\boldsymbol{A}.\boldsymbol{x}\oplus\boldsymbol{b}=\boldsymbol{y}
if
\boldsymbol{A}
in Reduced Row-Echelon form.

Gray Codes

A Gray code
[
14
]
is an ordering of
2^{l}
binary numbers for some
l\geq 1
with the property that every pair of consecutive numbers in the sequence differ in exactly one bit. Thus starting from
\boldsymbol{0}^{l}
we can iteratively construct the entire Gray code sequence by flipping one bit in each step. We assume access to a procedure
nextGrayBit
that in each call returns the position of the next bit that is to be flipped. Such a procedure can be implemented in constant time by a trivial modification of Algorithm L in
[
19
]
.

Related Work

Propositional model counting has been of theoretical as well as practical interest over the years
[
16
,
17
,
23
,
27
]
. Early investigations showed that both #CNF and #DNF are #P-complete
[
29
]
. Consequently, approximation algorithms have been explored for both problems. A major breakthrough for approximate #DNF was achieved by the seminal work of Karp and Luby
[
17
]
, which provided a Monte Carlo-based FPRAS for #DNF. The proposed FPRAS was improved by follow-up work of Karp, Luby and Madras
[
18
]
and Dagum et al.
[
5
]
, achieving the best known complexity of
\mathcal{O}(\mathsf{m}\mathsf{n}\log(1/\delta)/\varepsilon^{2})
. In this work, we bring certain ideas of Karp et al. into the hashing framework with significant adaptations.

For #CNF, early work on approximate counting resulted in hashing-based schemes that required polynomially many calls to an NP-oracle
[
25
,
28
]
. No practical algorithms materialized from the these schemes due to the impracticality of the underlying NP queries. Subsequent attempts to circumvent hardness led to the development of several hashing and sampling-based approaches that achieved scalability but provided very weak or no guarantees
[
13
,
11
]
. Due to recent breakthroughs in the design of hashing-based techniques, several tools have been developed recently that can handle formulas involving hundreds of thousands of variables while providing rigorous formal guarantees. Overall, these tools can be broadly classified by their underlying hashing-based technique as: (i) obtain a constant factor approximation and then use identical copies of the input formula to obtain
\varepsilon
approximations
[
10
]
, or (ii) directly obtain
\varepsilon
guarantees
[
3
,
4
]
. The first technique when applied to DNF formulas is not an FPRAS. In contrast, Chakraborty, Meel and Vardi
[
4
]
recently showed that tools based on the latter approach, such as
\mathsf{ApproxMC2}
, do provide FPRAS for #DNF counting. Chakraborty et al. did not analyze the complexity of the algorithm in their work. We now provide a precise complexity analysis of
\mathsf{ApproxMC2}
for #DNF. To that end, we first describe the
\mathsf{ApproxMC}
framework on which
\mathsf{ApproxMC2}
is built.

3.1
ApproxMC Framework

Chakraborty et al. introduced in
[
3
]
a hashing-based framework called
\mathsf{ApproxMC}
that requires linear (in
\mathsf{n}
) number of
\mathsf{SAT}
calls. Subsequently in
\mathsf{ApproxMC2}
, the number of
\mathsf{SAT}
calls was reduced from linear to logarithmic (in
\mathsf{n}
). The core idea of
\mathsf{ApproxMC}
is to employ
2-
universal hash functions to partition the solution space into
roughly equal small
cells, wherein a cell is called
small
if it has less than or equal to
\mathsf{hiThresh}
solutions, such that
\mathsf{hiThresh}
is a function of
\varepsilon
. A
\mathsf{SAT}
solver is employed to check if a cell is small by enumerating solutions one-by-one until either there are no more solutions or we have already enumerated
\mathsf{hiThresh}+1
solutions. Following the terminology of
[
3
]
, we refer to the above described procedure as
\mathsf{BSAT}
(bounded SAT). To determine the number of cells,
\mathsf{ApproxMC}
performs a search that requires
\mathcal{O}(\log\mathsf{n})
steps and the estimate is returned as the count of the solutions in a randomly picked small cell scaled by the total number of cells. To amplify confidence to the desired levels of
1-\delta
,
\mathsf{ApproxMC}
invokes the estimation routine
\mathcal{O}(\log\frac{1}{\delta})
times and reports the median of all such estimates. Hence, the number of
\mathsf{BSAT}
invocations is
\mathcal{O}(\log\mathsf{n}\log(\frac{1}{\delta}))
.

FPRAS for #DNF

The key insight of Chakraborty et al.
[
4
]
is that the
\mathsf{BSAT}
procedure can be done in polynomial time when the input formula to
\mathsf{ApproxMC}
is in DNF. In particular, the input to every invocation of
\mathsf{BSAT}
is a formula that is a conjunction of the input DNF formula and a set of XOR constraints derived from the hash function. Chakraborty et al. observed that one can iterate over all the cubes of the input formula, substitute each cube into the set of XOR constraints separately, and employ Gaussian Elimination to enumerate the solutions of the simplified XOR constraints. Note that at no step would one have to enumerate more than
\mathsf{hiThresh}
solutions. Since Gaussian Elimination is a polynomial-time procedure,
\mathsf{BSAT}
can be accomplished in polynomial time as well. Chakraborty et al. did not provide a precise complexity analysis of
\mathsf{BSAT}
. We now provide such an analysis. We defer all proofs to the appendix. The following lemma states the time complexity of the
\mathsf{BSAT}
routine.

Lemma 1
.

The complexity of
\mathsf{BSAT}
when the input formula to
\mathsf{ApproxMC2}
is in DNF is
\mathcal{O}(\mathsf{m}\mathsf{n}^{3}+\mathsf{m}\mathsf{n}^{2}/\varepsilon^{2})
.
∎

We can now complete the complexity analysis:

Lemma 2
.

The complexity of
\mathsf{ApproxMC2}
is
\mathcal{O}((\mathsf{m}\mathsf{n}^{3}+\mathsf{m}\mathsf{n}^{2}/\varepsilon^{2})\log\mathsf{n}\log(1/\delta))
when the input formula is in DNF.
∎

4
Efficient Hashing-based DNF Counter

We now present three key novel algorithmic innovations that allow us to design hashing-based FPRAS for #DNF with complexity similar to Monte Carlo-based state-of-the-art techniques. We first introduce a new family of 2-universal hash functions that allow us to circumvent the need for expensive Gaussian Elimination. We then discuss the concept of Symbolic Hashing, which allows us to design hash functions over a space different than the assignment space, allowing us to achieve significant reduction in the complexity of search procedure for the number of the cells. Finally, we show that
\mathsf{BSAT}
can be replaced by an efficient stochastic estimator. These three techniques allow us to achieve significant reduction in the complexity of hashing-based DNF counter without loss of theoretical guarantees.

4.1
Row-Echelon XOR Hash Functions

The complexity analysis presented in Section
3
shows that the expensive Gaussian Elimination contributes significantly to poor time complexity of
\mathsf{ApproxMC2}
. Since the need for Gaussian Elimination originates from the usage of
H_{XOR}
, we seek a family of 2-universal hash functions that circumvents this need. We now introduce a Row-Echelon XOR family of hash functions defined as
H_{REX}(\mathsf{q},\mathsf{p})=\{\boldsymbol{A}.\boldsymbol{x}\oplus\boldsymbol{b}\mid\boldsymbol{A}^{[\mathsf{p}\times\mathsf{q}]}=[\boldsymbol{I}^{[\mathsf{p}\times\mathsf{p}]}\,\,:\,\,\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D}}^{[\mathsf{p}\times(\mathsf{q}-\mathsf{p})]}]\}
where
\boldsymbol{I}
is the identity matrix,
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D}}
and
\boldsymbol{b}
are random 0/1 matrix and vector respectively. In particular, we ensure that for every
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D}}[i,j]
and
\boldsymbol{b}[i]
we have
\Pr[\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D}}[i,j]=1]=\Pr[\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D}}[i,j]=0]=0.5
and also
\Pr[\boldsymbol{b}[i]=1]=\Pr[\boldsymbol{b}[i]=0]=0.5
. Note that
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D}}
and
\boldsymbol{b}
completely define a hash function from
H_{REX}
. The following theorem establishes the desired properties of universality for
H_{REX}
. The proof is deferred to Appendix.

Theorem 3
.

H_{REX}
is 2-universal.
∎

Algorithm 1

\mathsf{enumNextREX}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D}},\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v},k)

1:

\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime}\leftarrow\boldsymbol{v}
;

2:

\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime}[k]\leftarrow\neg\boldsymbol{v}[k]
;

3:

\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime}\leftarrow\boldsymbol{u}\oplus\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D}}[.,k]
;

4:

return

(\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime})

The naive way of enumerating satisfying assignments for a given

4.3
Stochastic Cell-Counting

To estimate the number of solutions in a cell, we need to check for every tuple
(\boldsymbol{x},\phi^{Ci})
generated using symbolic hash function as described above: if
(\boldsymbol{x},\phi^{Ci})\in R_{\phi}^{\prime}
. Such a check would require iteration over cubes
\phi^{Cj}
for
1\leq j\leq(i-1)
and returning no if
\boldsymbol{x}\models\phi^{Cj}
for some
j
and yes otherwise. This would result in procedure with
O(\mathsf{m}\mathsf{n})
complexity.

Our key observation is that a precise count of the number of solutions in a cell is not required and therefore, one can employ a stochastic estimator for the number of solutions in a cell. We proceed as follows: we define the
coverage
of an assignment
\boldsymbol{x}
as
cov(\boldsymbol{x})=\{j|\boldsymbol{x}\models\phi^{Cj}\}
. Note that
\sum_{(\boldsymbol{x},\phi^{Ci})\in U^{\prime}}\frac{1}{|cov(\boldsymbol{x})|}=|R_{\phi}|
.

We define a random variable
\mathsf{c_{\boldsymbol{x}}}
as the number of steps taken to uniformly and independently sample from
\{1,2,\ldots,\mathsf{m}\}
, a number
j
such that
\boldsymbol{x}\models\phi^{Cj}
. For a randomly chosen
j
, the probability
\Pr[\boldsymbol{x}\models\phi^{Cj}]=|cov(\boldsymbol{x})|/\mathsf{m}
, which follows the Bernoulli distribution. The random variable
\mathsf{c_{\boldsymbol{x}}}
is the number of Bernoulli trials for the first success, which follows the geometric distribution. Therefore,
\mathsf{E}[\mathsf{c_{\boldsymbol{x}}}]=\mathsf{m}/|cov(\boldsymbol{x})|
, and
\mathsf{E}[\mathsf{c_{\boldsymbol{x}}}/\mathsf{m}]=1/|cov(\boldsymbol{x})|
. The estimator
\mathsf{c_{\boldsymbol{x}}}/\mathsf{m}
has been previously employed by Karp et al.
[
18
]
. Here, we show that it can also be used for Stochastic Cell-Counting: we define the estimator for the number of solutions in a cell as
\Omega_{\boldsymbol{y}}=\sum_{(\boldsymbol{x},\phi^{Ci})\in h^{-1}(\boldsymbol{y})}\mathsf{c_{\boldsymbol{x}}}/\mathsf{m}
.

Conclusion

Hashing-based techniques have emerged as a promising approach to obtain counting algorithms and tools that scale to large instances while providing strong theoretical guarantees. This has led to an interest in designing hashing-based algorithms for counting problems that are known to be amenable to fully polynomial randomized approximation schemes. The prior hashing-based approach
[
4
]
provided FPRAS for DNF but with complexity much worse than state-of-the-art techniques. In this work, we introduced (i) Symbolic Hashing, (ii) Stochastic Cell-Counting, and (iii) a new 2-universal family of hash functions, and obtained a hashing-based FPRAS for #DNF with complexity similar to state-of-the-art.

Given the recent interest in hashing-based techniques and generality of our contributions, we believe concepts introduced in this paper can lead to design of hashing-based techniques for other classes of constraints. For example, all prior versions of
\mathsf{ApproxMC}
relied on deterministic SAT solvers for exactly counting the solutions in a cell for #CNF. The technique of Stochastic Cell-Counting opens up the door for the usage of probabilistic SAT solvers for #CNF. Furthermore, a salient feature of the
H_{REX}
family is the sparsity of its hash functions. In fact, the sparsity increases with the addition of constraints. Sparse hash functions have been shown to be desirable for efficiently solving CNF+XOR constraints
[

,

]
. An interesting direction for future work is to test
H_{REX}
