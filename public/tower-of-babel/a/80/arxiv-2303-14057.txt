Reciprocals of thinned exponential series
John Engbers, David Galvin, Cliff Smyth

Abstract
The reciprocal of e − x \displaystyle e^{-x} has a power series about 0 \displaystyle 0 in which all coefficients are non-negative. Gessel [ Ges19 ] considered truncates of the power series of e − x \displaystyle e^{-x} , i.e. polynomials of the form ∑ n = 0 r ( − 1 ) n ​ x n n ! \displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{r}(-1)^{n}\frac{x^{n}}{n!} , and established combinatorially that the reciprocal of the truncate has a power series with all coefficients non-negative precisely when r \displaystyle r is odd. Here we extend Gessel’s observations to arbitrary “thinned exponential series”. To be precise, let A ⊆ { 1 , 3 , 5 , … } \displaystyle A\subseteq\{1,3,5,\ldots\} and B ⊆ { 2 , 4 , 6 , … } \displaystyle B\subseteq\{2,4,6,\ldots\} , and consider the series 1 − ∑ a ∈ A x a a ! + ∑ b ∈ B x b b ! . 1-\sum_{a\in A}\frac{x^{a}}{a!}+\sum_{b\in B}\frac{x^{b}}{b!}. We consider conditions on A \displaystyle A and B \displaystyle B that ensure that the reciprocal series has all coefficients non-negative. We give combinatorial proofs for a large set of conditions, including whenever 1 ∈ A \displaystyle 1\in A and the endpoints of the maximal consecutive intervals in A ∪ B \displaystyle A\cup B are odd integers. In particular, the coefficients in the reciprocal series can be interpreted as ordered set partitions of [ n ] \displaystyle[n] with block size restrictions, or in terms of permutations with restricted lengths of maximally increasing runs, suitably weighted.



1
Introduction and Statement of Results

The power series
\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}\frac{x^{n}}{n!}

has a reciprocal with only non-negative coefficients; specifically, for real
\displaystyle x
, we have

\left(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}\frac{x^{n}}{n!}\right)^{-1}=\left(e^{-x}\right)^{-1}=e^{x}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{x^{n}}{n!}.

Gessel
[
Ges19
]
observed that whether this non-negativity phenomenon extends to the reciprocals of partial sums of
\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}\frac{x^{n}}{n!}
seems to depend on the parity of the point of truncation — in particular, computation suggests that for all
\displaystyle m\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
\displaystyle\left(\sum_{n=0}^{2m}(-1)^{n}\frac{x^{n}}{n!}\right)^{-1}
has some negative coefficients, while
\displaystyle\left(\sum_{n=0}^{2m-1}(-1)^{n}\frac{x^{n}}{n!}\right)^{-1}
has only non-negative coefficients. Gessel gives a lovely combinatorial proof of this latter fact, demonstrating that if the sequence
\displaystyle(c_{n})_{n\geq 0}
is defined via

\left(\sum_{n=0}^{2m-1}(-1)^{n}\frac{x^{n}}{n!}\right)^{-1}=\sum_{n\geq 0}c_{n}\frac{x^{n}}{n!},

then
\displaystyle c_{n}
counts the number of permutations of
\displaystyle[n]:=\{1,\ldots,n\}
in which
every increasing run has length congruent to
\displaystyle 0
or
\displaystyle 1
modulo
\displaystyle 2m
. This is a special case (
\displaystyle b=1,r=2
) of the following more general result from
[
Ges19
]
:

Theorem 1.1
.

(
[
Ges19
, Proposition 2.4]
)
Let
\displaystyle m
,
\displaystyle b
and
\displaystyle r\geq 2
be positive integers. The coefficient of
\displaystyle x^{n}/n!
in

\left(\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}\left(\frac{x^{krb}}{(krb)!}-\frac{x^{(kr+1)b}}{((kr+1)b)!}\right)\right)^{-1}

is the number of permutations of
\displaystyle[n]
in which every maximal increasing run has length congruent to one of
\displaystyle 0,b,\ldots,(r-1)b
modulo
\displaystyle mrb
.

In particular, the coefficients in the reciprocal series are all non-negative.

Inspired by this result, we take a combinatorial approach to extend the scope of Gessel’s observations.

To state our results precisely, let
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}=\{1,3,5,\ldots\}
denote the odd natural numbers and
\displaystyle\mathcal{E}=\{2,4,\ldots\}
denote the positive even natural numbers, and let
\displaystyle A\subseteq\mathcal{O}
and
\displaystyle B\subseteq\mathcal{E}
. Consider the power series

F_{A,B}(x)=1-\sum_{a\in A}\frac{x^{a}}{a!}+\sum_{b\in B}\frac{x^{b}}{b!},

and let

G_{A,B}(x)=(F_{A,B}(x))^{-1}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_{n}\frac{x^{n}}{n!}

be the reciprocal of
\displaystyle F_{A,B}(x)
. A natural question to ask is: under what conditions does the power series representation of
\displaystyle G
about
\displaystyle 0
have all coefficients
\displaystyle c_{n}
non-negative? And when
\displaystyle G
does have all coefficients
\displaystyle c_{n}
non-negative, is there a natural set of objects that
\displaystyle c_{n}
counts? For results on these questions and their combinatorial interpretations in the context of
compositional
inverses, see e.g.
[
EGS19
]
.

If we are content to interpret
\displaystyle c_{n}
as the number of elements in a signed set (a set in which each element has weight either
\displaystyle 1
or
\displaystyle-1
), or, equivalently, as the difference between the sizes of two sets, then the matter is straightforward, as we show next. Write
\displaystyle{\mathcal{P}}^{\rm pos}_{n,A,B}
for the set of ordered set partitions
\displaystyle P=P_{1}/\cdots/P_{\ell}
of
\displaystyle[n]
, in which all block sizes are in
\displaystyle A\cup B
, and which have an even number of blocks with sizes from
\displaystyle B
. Write
\displaystyle{\mathcal{P}}^{\rm neg}_{n,A,B}
for those with an odd number of blocks with sizes from
\displaystyle B
.
Note that

G_{A,B}(x)=\frac{1}{F_{A,B}(x)}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left(\sum_{a\in A}\frac{x^{a}}{a!}-\sum_{b\in B}\frac{x^{b}}{b!}\right)^{k}.

The coefficient of
\displaystyle x^{n}/n!
in the final expression above is a sum over compositions of
\displaystyle n
with all blocks coming from
\displaystyle A\cup B
; the sign of the summand corresponding to a composition is positive if the composition has an even number of blocks from
\displaystyle B
, and negative otherwise, and the magnitude is the multinomial coefficient
\displaystyle\binom{n}{a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,b_{1},b_{2},\ldots}
, where the
\displaystyle a_{i}
’s and
\displaystyle b_{i}
’s are the blocks of the composition. It immediately follows that we have that the coefficient of
\displaystyle x^{n}/n!
in
\displaystyle(F_{A,B}(x))^{-1}
is

|\mathcal{P}^{\rm pos}_{n,A,B}|-|\mathcal{P}^{\rm neg}_{n,A,B}|.

The drawback to this interpretation is that it gives no immediate information about the sign of the coefficient, nor any obvious combinatorial explanation of why the sign might be always non-negative for certain choices of
\displaystyle A
and
\displaystyle B
. One strategy for showing that
\displaystyle G_{A,B}(x)
has all coefficients non-negative is to exhibit an injection
\displaystyle i
from
\displaystyle{\mathcal{P}}^{\rm neg}_{n,A,B}
into
\displaystyle{\mathcal{P}}^{\rm pos}_{n,A,B}
or, equivalently, an involution
\displaystyle\tau
of the restricted partitions in
\displaystyle{\mathcal{P}}^{\rm neg}_{n,A,B}\cup{\mathcal{P}}^{\rm pos}_{n,A,B}
that moves all elements in
\displaystyle{\mathcal{P}}^{\rm neg}_{n,A,B}
to elements in
\displaystyle{\mathcal{P}}^{\rm pos}_{n,A,B}
and fixes the elements in
\displaystyle{\mathcal{P}}^{\rm pos}_{n,A,B}\setminus\tau({\mathcal{P}}^{\rm neg}_{n,A,B})
.

As discussed earlier, Gessel
[
Ges19
]
observes that in particular when
\displaystyle A\cup B=\{1,\ldots 2m-1\}
then
\displaystyle G_{A,B}(x)
has all coefficients non-negative. We obtain a broad generalization of this (Theorem
1.4
below). We will need the following definitions.

Definition 1.2
.

For
\displaystyle A\subseteq\mathcal{O}
and
\displaystyle B\subseteq\mathcal{E}
, we say that
\displaystyle A\cup B
is
odd-ended
if, when it is written as the union of maximal intervals of consecutive integers, the endpoints of each interval are odd numbers. A maximal element in a maximal interval is called a
top element
and a minimal element in a maximal interval is called a
bottom element
.

Example 1.3
.

The set
\displaystyle\{1,\ldots,2m-1\}
is odd-ended, and only contains one maximal interval, with top element
\displaystyle 2m-1
and bottom element
\displaystyle 1
.

The set
\displaystyle\{1,5,9,10,11,12,13,19,20,21,22,\ldots\}=\{1\}\cup\{5\}\cup[9,13]\cup[19,\infty)
is odd-ended and has four maximal intervals. The top elements are
\displaystyle 1
,
\displaystyle 5
, and
\displaystyle 13
, while the bottom elements are
\displaystyle 1
,
\displaystyle 5
,
\displaystyle 9
, and
\displaystyle 19
.

Our first main result is as follows.

Theorem 1.4
.

Suppose that
\displaystyle A\subseteq\mathcal{O}
and
\displaystyle B\subseteq\mathcal{E}
with
\displaystyle A\cup B
odd-ended and
\displaystyle 1\in A
.

Define
\displaystyle(c_{n})_{n\geq 0}
via

\left(1-\sum_{a\in A}\frac{x^{a}}{a!}+\sum_{b\in B}\frac{x^{b}}{b!}\right)^{-1}=\sum_{n\geq 0}c_{n}\frac{x^{n}}{n!}.

Then the values
\displaystyle c_{n}
are all non-negative integers, and moreover have a natural combinatorial interpretation as the number of ordered set partitions of
\displaystyle[n]
whose blocks are either size
\displaystyle 1
or size that is an endpoint of the maximal intervals, and these block sizes appear in particular allowable orders as described in Corollary
3.6
.

In Corollary
3.12
, we illustrate that these coefficients count the permutations with particular increasing run lengths, suitably weighted, which extends the results of Gessel
[
Ges19
]
. The coefficients
\displaystyle c_{n}
for
\displaystyle A\cup B=[3]
are given as sequence A317111 in OEIS. Those for
\displaystyle A\cup B=[5]
,
\displaystyle[7]
, and
\displaystyle[9]
are given as sequences A322262, A322282, and A322283 in OEIS, respectively
[
OEI23
]

\displaystyle A\cup B=\{1,3\}
or
\displaystyle A\cup B=\{1,2,3,5\}
are simple examples where
\displaystyle A\cup B
is odd-ended whose corresponding coefficient sequences do not appear in OEIS.

We also incorporate the integer parameters
\displaystyle b\geq 1
and
\displaystyle r\geq 2
into our results, which we refer to as the
\displaystyle b
-stretched
and
\displaystyle r
-stretched
versions of our main result. The idea is to stretch the set
\displaystyle\{1,2,3,4,\ldots\}
to
\displaystyle\{1,r,r+1,2r,\ldots\}
, and then multiply each entry by
\displaystyle b
to obtain
\displaystyle\{b,br,b(r+1),2br,\ldots\}
. We then suppose that
\displaystyle A\subseteq{\mathcal{O}}^{*}:=\{b,b(r+1),\ldots\}
(the stretched odds) and
\displaystyle B\subseteq{\mathcal{E}}^{*}:=\{br,2br,\ldots\}
(the stretched evens). We will again say that
\displaystyle A\cup B
is
odd-ended
if the endpoints of the (stretched) maximal intervals again are “odd”;
in particular
\displaystyle A\cup B
is odd-ended if
\displaystyle kr\in B
implies
\displaystyle kr+1\in A
and
\displaystyle(k-1)r+1\in A
. We make similar modifications to definitions throughout in the stretched context.

Theorem 1.5
.

Suppose that
\displaystyle A\subseteq{\mathcal{O}}^{*}
and
\displaystyle B\subseteq{\mathcal{E}}^{*}
with
\displaystyle b\in A
and
\displaystyle A\cup B
odd-ended. Define
\displaystyle(c_{n})_{n\geq 0}
via

\left(1-\sum_{a\in A}\frac{x^{a}}{a!}+\sum_{b\in B}\frac{x^{b}}{b!}\right)^{-1}=\sum_{n\geq 0}c_{n}\frac{x^{n}}{n!}.

Then the values
\displaystyle c_{n}
are all non-negative integers, and moreover have a natural combinatorial interpretation as the number of ordered set partitions of
\displaystyle[n]
whose blocks are either size
\displaystyle 1
or size that is an endpoint of the stretched maximal intervals, and these block sizes appear in particular allowable orders as stated in Corollary
4.5
.

In Corollary
4.8
, we illustrate that these coefficients count the permutations with particular increasing run lengths, suitably weighted.

We give an alternate proof Theorem
1.4
and Theorem
1.5
in Section
6
. That proof apeals to the “Run Theorem” in non-commutative symmetric function theory (Theorem
6.1

[
Ges77
]

and

given in Sections

Note that the case
\displaystyle b=1
and
\displaystyle r=2
in Theorem

is simply Theorem
1.4
. The specifics of our combinatorial interpretations, which are direct extensions of the

When we consider the

\displaystyle b=1

as a series whose coefficients count the number of permutations on

Gessel considers the following more general situation (see
[

).
Suppose
\displaystyle a(x)=a_{0}+a_{1}x+\cdots+a_{d}x^{d}
with
\displaystyle a_{0}=1

\displaystyle 1/a(x)

Returning to the non-stretched setting, an obvious question is to determine necessary and sufficient conditions (on
\displaystyle A,B
) for
\displaystyle G_{A,B}(x)
to be non-negative. We show a necessary condition, under the requirement that
\displaystyle 1\in A
, in the following. Recall that when
\displaystyle A\cup B=\{1,2,3,4,\ldots\}
, equivalently when
\displaystyle\{1,2,3,\ldots\}\setminus(A\cup B)=\varnothing

Theorem 1.6
.

Suppose that
\displaystyle A\subseteq\mathcal{O}
and
\displaystyle B\subseteq\mathcal{E}
with
\displaystyle 1\in A
.
Define
\displaystyle(c_{n})_{n\geq 0}
via

\left(1-\sum_{a\in A}\frac{x^{a}}{a!}+\sum_{b\in B}\frac{x^{b}}{b!}\right)^{-1}=\sum_{n\geq 0}c_{n}\frac{x^{n}}{n!}.

If the smallest positive number in
\displaystyle\{1,2,3,\ldots\}\setminus(A\cup B)
is
\displaystyle 2m+1
, then the coefficient
\displaystyle c_{2m+2}
is negative.

In other words, if the initial interval in
\displaystyle A\cup B
ends in an even number, then the reciprocal has a negative coefficient. This provides a combinatorial proof of Gessel’s initial observation in
[
Ges19
]
when
\displaystyle A\cup B=[2m]
.

Computational evidence suggests that (still in the special case
\displaystyle 1\in A
) if just the initial interval ends in an odd number, then the reciprocal has non-negative coefficients.

Conjecture 1.7
.

Suppose that
\displaystyle A\subseteq\mathcal{O}
and
\displaystyle B\subseteq\mathcal{E}
with
\displaystyle 1\in A
.

Define
\displaystyle(c_{n})_{n\geq 0}
via

\left(1-\sum_{a\in A}\frac{x^{a}}{a!}+\sum_{b\in B}\frac{x^{b}}{b!}\right)^{-1}=\sum_{n\geq 0}c_{n}\frac{x^{n}}{n!}.

If the smallest positive number in
\displaystyle\{1,2,3,\ldots\}\setminus(A\cup B)
is
\displaystyle 2m
, then all coefficients
\displaystyle c_{n}
are non-negative.

We are able to reduce Conjecture
1.7
to the particular case where
\displaystyle A\cup B=[2m-1]\cup\{2m+2,2m+4,\ldots\}
for some positive integer
\displaystyle m
; see Corollary
5.2
. We then use analytic arguments to prove Conjecture
1.7
in the cases where
\displaystyle m=1
and
\displaystyle m=2
, see Theorems

and
5.4
.

These analytic techniques do not seem to support extending this argument much further, as we discuss in Section

after the proofs. Further evidence in favor of Conjecture

is provided by computation that shows that if
\displaystyle m\leq 250
then
\displaystyle c_{n}\geq 0
for
\displaystyle n\leq 2500
.

Note that while the Run Theorem, Theorem
6.1
, can be used to prove Theorems
1.4
and
1.5
, it cannot be used to resolve this conjecture. See Section

for the details.

Our combinatorial proofs all rely on an involution on the ordered set partitions of
\displaystyle[n]
that
fixes the underlying permutation

2
Proof Ideas

In this section, we briefly sketch an algorithmically defined involution
\displaystyle i
, that is involved in proving Theorems
1.4
and
1.5
. We then illustrate how it works on some inputs.

Given a partition
\displaystyle P
of
\displaystyle[n]
whose elements in a block
are listed in increasing order, the algorithm attempts to perform one of two operations:

•

if the block furthest to the right has a single element, it attempts to merge it with the one to its left (subject to some conditions), or

•

if the block furthest to the right has more than one element, it splits off its furthest right element into a new singleton block.

If it cannot do either of these (e.g. because they will create blocks of forbidden sizes), it will move to the next block of
\displaystyle P
to the left; occasionally it will need to also skip this next block too. It takes some care to ensure the involution is well-defined and prove that it has the properties we need. We will do this in Section 3, but first we illustrate the involution operating on some particular inputs.

Example 2.1
.

Suppose
\displaystyle A=\{1,3\}
,
\displaystyle B=\{2\}
, and
\displaystyle n=9
. This means we have ordered partitions of the set
\displaystyle[9]
into blocks, with the elements inside of a block written in increasing order. Furthermore, the blocks can only contain
\displaystyle 1
,
\displaystyle 2
, or
\displaystyle 3
elements.

•

The partition
\displaystyle P=234/6/1/57/89
of
\displaystyle[9]
will map to
\displaystyle 234/6/1/57/8/9
, and this latter partition will map back to the former partition
\displaystyle 234/6/1/57/89
.

For future reference, we also note here that the underlying permutation for
\displaystyle P
is
\displaystyle 234615789
, and this is the same as the underlying partition for the image of
\displaystyle P
. This property will always hold for our algorithm.

•

The partition
\displaystyle P=234/1/56/78/9
will map to
\displaystyle 234/1/56/789
, and this latter partition will map back to the former partition.

•

The partition
\displaystyle P=234/1/56/79/8
will skip the first singleton (as the entries of merging blocks
\displaystyle 79
and
\displaystyle 8
are not in order), and then map to
\displaystyle 234/1/56/7/9/8
. This latter partition will again skip the first singleton (as the entries of merging blocks
\displaystyle 9
and
\displaystyle 8
are out of order) and then map back to the former partition.

•

The partition
\displaystyle P=134/28/567/9
will skip the first singleton (since blocks of size
\displaystyle 4
are not allowed). It will then also
skip the block
\displaystyle 567
and move to considering block
\displaystyle 28
.

The reason for this somewhat surprising second skip, briefly, is that if not, the partition
\displaystyle P
will instead map to
\displaystyle 134/28/56/7/9
, and this latter partition will
not
map back to the former under this procedure (it will map to
\displaystyle 134/28/56/79
to be consistent with previously defined rules, and so our algorithm will not be an involution). Therefore, to make this algorithm an involution, we also skip the block
\displaystyle 567
.

We also note that being odd-ended means that any even-sized block will be able to either add or subtract a singleton and still have a block size in
\displaystyle A\cup B
, thus mapping elements of
\displaystyle{\mathcal{P}}^{\rm neg}_{n,A,B}
to elements of
\displaystyle{\mathcal{P}}^{\rm pos}_{n,A,B}
.

Finally, we briefly indicate how the procedure will change when considering the values
\displaystyle b>1
and
\displaystyle r>2
. When we have
\displaystyle b>1
, we will view each individual number in
\displaystyle[n]
as a group of size
\displaystyle b
, and when we have a value
\displaystyle r>2
we will translate from the set
\displaystyle\{1,2,3,4,5,\ldots\}
to the set
\displaystyle\{1,r,r+1,2r,2r+1,\ldots\}
.

3
Proof of Theorem
1.4
and Combinatorial Interpretations

In this section, we prove Theorem
1.4
and provide the combinatorial interpretations of the coefficients of the reciprocal series. We begin with the proof.

3.1
Proof of Theorem
1.4

Suppose that
\displaystyle A\subseteq{\mathcal{O}}
and
\displaystyle B\subseteq{\mathcal{E}}
satisfy that
\displaystyle 1\in A
and
\displaystyle A\cup B
is odd-ended.

For each non-negative integer
\displaystyle n
we construct an injection from
\displaystyle{\mathcal{P}}^{\rm neg}_{n,A,B}
into
\displaystyle{\mathcal{P}}^{\rm pos}_{n,A,B}

by constructing inductively for each
\displaystyle n\geq 0
a sign-reversing involution
\displaystyle i_{n}
on
\displaystyle{\mathcal{P}}^{\rm pos}_{n,A,B}\cup{\mathcal{P}}^{\rm neg}_{n,A,B}
, all of whose fixed points lie in
\displaystyle{\mathcal{P}}^{\rm pos}_{n,A,B}
. By sign-reversing, we mean that if
\displaystyle i(P)\neq P
then
\displaystyle i(P)\in{\mathcal{P}}^{\rm pos}_{n,A,B}
if
\displaystyle P\in{\mathcal{P}}^{\rm neg}_{n,A,B}
, and
\displaystyle i(P)\in{\mathcal{P}}^{\rm neg}_{n,A,B}
if
\displaystyle P\in{\mathcal{P}}^{\rm pos}_{n,A,B}
.
Furthermore, we will show that the underlying permutation on
\displaystyle i_{n}(P)
is the same as the underlying permutation on
\displaystyle P
(i.e. the relative order of the elements of
\displaystyle[n]
in the ordered partition is not changed by
\displaystyle i_{n}
). The inductive procedure can equivalently be thought of as an iterative algorithm on a given ordered partition.

Constructing
\displaystyle i_{0}
: When
\displaystyle n=0
there is a unique ordered partition, the empty partition, which is in
\displaystyle{\mathcal{P}}^{\rm pos}_{0,A,B}
as it has zero blocks with sizes from
\displaystyle B
. The involution is the identity map, and the relative order condition is trivially satisfied.

Constructing
\displaystyle i_{1}
: When
\displaystyle n=1
there is again a unique ordered partition, with one singleton block
\displaystyle\{1\}
, which is in
\displaystyle{\mathcal{P}}^{\rm pos}_{1,A,B}
, as it has zero blocks with sizes from
\displaystyle B
. The involution is again the identity map, and also the relative order condition is trivially satisfied.

Constructing
\displaystyle i_{n}
for
\displaystyle n\geq 2
: We take as our inductive hypothesis that for
\displaystyle m<n
, a sign-reversing involution
\displaystyle i_{m}
on
\displaystyle{\mathcal{P}}^{\rm pos}_{m,A,B}\cup{\mathcal{P}}^{\rm neg}_{m,A,B}

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
\displaystyle n\geq 1
. With the involution
\displaystyle i_{n}
defined above, if
\displaystyle i_{n}(P)=P
, then all block sizes of
\displaystyle P
are
\displaystyle 1
or a top element or a bottom element that is not equal to
\displaystyle 1
.

Furthermore, the necessary and sufficient conditions on the order of the block sizes of
\displaystyle P
are that, (i), the top elements that are not also bottom elements must be part of a freeze skip pair, and, (ii), the bottom elements that are not also top elements must not have a singleton to the right (whose elements are collectively in increasing order) that is not in a freeze skip pair.

We note that
\displaystyle 1
is a bottom element, but we separate it out in Lemma
3.3
to emphasize its importance in the algorithm. To better understand condition (ii), suppose that a bottom element that is not a top element has a singleton to the right whose elements are in increasing order. If that singleton is in a freeze-skip pair, then it must be the block that is “frozen” and so has a second singleton to its right (whose elements are collectively in increasing order), and therefore we must have
\displaystyle 2\notin A\cup B
. So, if
\displaystyle 2\in A\cup B
, then (ii) simply says that if you have a bottom element that is not a top element, then it does not have a singleton to the right (whose elements are collectively in increasing order). Whereas if
\displaystyle 2\notin A\cup B
, then the second condition inductively says that if you have a bottom element that is not a top element, then it does not have an odd number of singletons to the right (whose elements are collectively all in increasing order).

Proof of Lemma
3.3
.

To prove the first statement, we proceed by induction on
\displaystyle n
, with the result trivial for base case
\displaystyle n=1
. Assume that the ordered partition
\displaystyle P=P_{1}/\cdots/P_{\ell}
has at least one block size not equal to
\displaystyle 1
or a top element or a bottom element. We argue that
\displaystyle i_{n}(P)\neq P
. This is obvious in Cases A and D as here we immediately have
\displaystyle i_{n}(P)\neq P
. In Case B, we skip a block with size
\displaystyle 1
, in Case C we skip blocks of size
\displaystyle 1
and a top element, and in Case E we skip a block with size being a bottom element. In each of these three Cases, we are left with a partition that still has at least one block size not equal to
\displaystyle 1
or a top element or a bottom element, and so by induction we obtain
\displaystyle i_{n}(P)\neq P
.

The second statement is clear from the algorithm, as it is describing the properties needed so that Cases A and D never occur when considering a fixed block, and so
\displaystyle P
is fixed. Note that condition (ii) ensures that case A can’t happen and (i) ensures that case D can’t happen.
∎

3.2
General Combinatorial Interpretations

With the terminology from the proof and the characterization of the fixed points of
\displaystyle i_{n}
, we have the following as a Corollary of the proof of Theorem
1.4
and Lemma
3.3
.

Definition 3.5
.

Suppose that
\displaystyle A\subseteq\mathcal{O}
and
\displaystyle B\subseteq\mathcal{E}
with
\displaystyle 1\in A
. Say that an ordered set partition of
\displaystyle[n]
is
\displaystyle A,B
-good if:

•

all parts are either size
\displaystyle 1
or a size that is an endpoint of the maximal intervals of
\displaystyle A\cup B
,

•

writing the elements of a block in increasing order, within an increasing run in the underlying permutation the following holds:

–

all blocks with size equal to a top element that is not a bottom element is part of a freeze skip pair, and

–

all blocks with size equal to a bottom element that are not also top elements do not have a singleton immediately to the right that is not in a freeze skip pair.

In light of Lemma
3.3
, when
\displaystyle A\cup B
is odd-ended, the ordered set partitions of
\displaystyle[n]
that are
\displaystyle A,B
-good are exactly those which the involution
\displaystyle i_{n}
leaves fixed.

Corollary 3.6
.

Suppose that
\displaystyle A\subseteq\mathcal{O}
and
\displaystyle B\subseteq\mathcal{E}
with
\displaystyle A\cup B
odd-ended and
\displaystyle 1\in A
.
Define
\displaystyle(c_{n})_{n\geq 0}
via

\left(1-\sum_{a\in A}\frac{x^{a}}{a!}+\sum_{b\in B}\frac{x^{b}}{b!}\right)^{-1}=\sum_{n\geq 0}c_{n}\frac{x^{n}}{n!}.

Then the values
\displaystyle c_{n}
are all non-negative integers, and moreover have a natural combinatorial interpretation as the number of
\displaystyle A,B
-good ordered set partitions of
\displaystyle[n]
.

Instead of looking at ordered set partitions, we can also imagine the underlying permutation being fixed, with a particular permutation being counted multiple times based on the number of ways of partitioning the maximal increasing runs into allowable blocks with an
\displaystyle A,B
-good ordered set partition.

Let
\displaystyle A\cup B=\{1,3\}

(a)

\displaystyle\sigma

\displaystyle[n]

\displaystyle\sigma

\displaystyle w_{\sigma}

\displaystyle\sigma

Given a permutation
\displaystyle\sigma

\displaystyle[n]

\displaystyle A,B

\displaystyle\sigma

\displaystyle w_{\sigma}

\displaystyle\sigma

\displaystyle\sigma=12\ldots n

\displaystyle w_{n}

\displaystyle A,B

\displaystyle\sigma

\displaystyle\sigma

3.3
Recovering Gessel’s Interpretation Involving Increasing Runs

In the special case
\displaystyle A\cup B=[2m-1]
for some positive integer
\displaystyle m
, we now show how we can recover Gessel’s interpretation that
\displaystyle c_{n}
is the number of permutations of
\displaystyle[n]
whose maximal increasing runs all have length congruent to
\displaystyle 0
or
\displaystyle 1
mod
\displaystyle 2m
.

When
\displaystyle m=1
, the reciprocal is
\displaystyle(1-x)^{-1}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}x^{n}
, which has exponential generating function coefficient
\displaystyle n!
. Here Gessel’s characterization is trivial as every integer is congruent to
\displaystyle 0
or
\displaystyle 1
mod
\displaystyle 2
, and so all
\displaystyle n!
permutations are counted and
\displaystyle c_{n}=n!
.

For
\displaystyle m>1
, the element
\displaystyle 1
is a bottom element that is not a top element, and the element
\displaystyle 2m-1
is a top element that is not a bottom element. Therefore, by Corollary
3.12
and Definition
3.5
, an increasing run is made up from blocks with sizes
\displaystyle 1
and
\displaystyle 2m-1
; further, the blocks of size
\displaystyle 2m-1
must have a block of size
\displaystyle 1
to the right, and there are no consecutive blocks of size
\displaystyle 1
.

It follows that the the maximal increasing runs in the permutation must have the blocks of the ordered set partition appear in blocks that occur in freeze skip pairs, with the extra possibility of one more singleton block on the left of these pairs. Since freeze skip pairs have
\displaystyle 2m
elements in them, if there are
\displaystyle j
freeze skip pairs there are either
\displaystyle 2mj
elements in the increasing run (if there is no singleton block to the left) or
\displaystyle 2mj+1
elements in the increasing run (if there is a singleton block to the left). This shows that all increasing runs must have length
\displaystyle 0
or
\displaystyle 1
mod
\displaystyle 2m
.
Finally, every permutation whose increasing runs have length
\displaystyle 0
or
\displaystyle 1
mod
\displaystyle 2m
has a unique associated ordered set partition
\displaystyle P
where the runs consist of freeze skip pairs (with a possible singleton block on the left). Therefore the weight of each such maximal increasing run length is
\displaystyle 1
, and so the weight of those permutations where all run lengths are
\displaystyle 0
or
\displaystyle 1
mod
\displaystyle 2m
is
\displaystyle 1
(with any other permutation having weight
\displaystyle 0
).

General conditions for non-negativity

In this section, we consider the necessary and sufficient conditions for non-negativity of

and
\displaystyle B\subseteq\mathcal{E}

5.1
Proof of Theorem
1.6

Suppose that
\displaystyle A\subseteq\mathcal{O}
and
\displaystyle B\subseteq\mathcal{E}
with
\displaystyle 1\in A

\displaystyle A\cup B=[2m]
.
We will consider the general case (where sizes at least
\displaystyle 2m+2
are also allowed in
\displaystyle A\cup B
) in the last paragraph of the proof.

We focus on the value
\displaystyle c_{2m+2}
. Recall that the algorithm from the proof of Theorem
1.4
considers all ordered set partitions of
\displaystyle[2m+2]
so that the block sizes are at most
\displaystyle 2m
, and so that the elements in each block are in increasing order and so have an associated underlying permutation. From the algorithm, the term
\displaystyle c_{2m+2}
counts the ordered set partitions of
\displaystyle[2m+2]
that are fixed points of the involution, with the signs of each fixed partition based on the sizes of the blocks in the partition. So we count the signed fixed points of
\displaystyle i_{2m+2}

1.4
showed that all fixed points came with a positive sign, but we are not odd-ended and so must consider the signs of the fixed points.

Any block of size
\displaystyle 2
through
\displaystyle 2m
, if considered by the algorithm (as in, it has not been skipped in a freeze skip pair), will split off a singleton block in Case D, and so such a ordered set partition will not be fixed by the algorithm. In particular, the fixed points of the algorithm must start with a singleton block
\displaystyle P_{\ell}
.

Further, any block of size
\displaystyle 1
will perform a non-skip move under the algorithm unless either:

1.

its entry in the underlying permutation is in increasing order with the entry to the left, and the next block has size
\displaystyle 2m
(so those two blocks will be in a freeze skip pair), or

2.

its entry in the underlying permutation is not in increasing order with the entry to the left (so the singleton block will be skipped).

We consider the possibilities separately.

If the entries of the singleton
\displaystyle P_{\ell}
and the block
\displaystyle P_{\ell-1}
to its left are in order, then
\displaystyle P_{\ell-1}
must be of size
\displaystyle 2m
, so
\displaystyle P_{\ell-2}=P_{1}
is a singleton. The only restriction on the entries of the permutation is that the last
\displaystyle 2m+1
entries — encompassing the blocks
\displaystyle P_{\ell-1}
and
\displaystyle P_{\ell}
— must be increasing, so there are exactly
\displaystyle 2m+2
such ordered partitions into blocks with sizes
\displaystyle 1
,
\displaystyle 2m
,
\displaystyle 1
, with the last two blocks having entries in the underlying permutation in increasing order.

If the entries of
\displaystyle P_{\ell}
and
\displaystyle P_{\ell-1}
are not in increasing order, then by Case B the singleton block
\displaystyle P_{\ell}
is skipped. Again, the next block
\displaystyle P_{\ell-1}
will split unless it is also singleton. By the same argument above,
\displaystyle P_{\ell-1}
will do a freeze skip if its entries are in increasing order with the entries of
\displaystyle P_{\ell-2}
and
\displaystyle P_{\ell-2}
has size
\displaystyle 2m
; the same count above gives
\displaystyle 2m+1
ways this can occur (note that the last two entries in the underlying permutation must not be increasing, by assumption).

If instead the block
\displaystyle P_{\ell-1}
has entries that are not in increasing order with those in
\displaystyle P_{\ell-2}
, then
\displaystyle P_{\ell-1}
is skipped. Now there are
\displaystyle 2m
elements remaining,
and to be a fixed point of the involution, all remaining blocks must do block skips. Therefore all blocks are singletons whose entries are not in increasing order, and so
\displaystyle P
is partitioned into
\displaystyle 2m+2

We now consider the signs associated with these fixed points. Those partitions with a block with size
\displaystyle 2m
have a negative sign. The partition of all singletons has a positive sign, and so
\displaystyle c_{2m+2}=-(2m+2)-(2m+1)+1=-2(2m+1)<0

Lastly, suppose there are also blocks with size at least
\displaystyle 2m+2

Since any blocks with size
\displaystyle 2m+2

\displaystyle 2m+3
do not alter the count for partitions of
\displaystyle[2m+2]

\displaystyle c_{2m+2}
is negative in this case (with value unchanged if
\displaystyle 2m+2\notin A\cup B

\displaystyle c_{2m+2}=-2(2m+1)-1

\displaystyle 2m+2\in A\cup B
).

5.2
Sufficient Condition Limitations via Combinatorial Algorithm

In this section we describe the limitations of our current proof technique in relation to Conjecture
1.7
.

Note that our algorithm fixes the underlying permutation. We will find a particular permutation for a particular
\displaystyle A\subseteq\mathcal{O}
and
\displaystyle B\subseteq\mathcal{E}
so that the net count for this permutation is negative, and so our method of finding non-negativity by proving it for each fixed permutation (via a sign-reversing involution that only fixes elements that count positively) cannot work.

Consider
\displaystyle A\cup B=\{1,4,6,8,10\ldots\}

and
\displaystyle n=5
, and fix the permutation 12345. Then only blocks with size
\displaystyle 1
and
\displaystyle 4
can be used, and there is one way to use all singleton blocks, namely
\displaystyle 1/2/3/4/5
, whereas there are two ways to use a block with size
\displaystyle 4
on this fixed permutation:
\displaystyle 1/2345
and
\displaystyle 1234/5
. So the net count for this fixed permutation is
\displaystyle-1
. Any sign-reversing involution that fixes the underlying permutation will have a fixed point that counts negatively for this particular permutation.

We note that this does not imply that there are negative coefficients in the reciprocal series, since other permutations of
\displaystyle[5]
have positive counts; it only shows that we cannot produce a proof where the underlying permutations stay the same and we have an involution leaving only positive blocks for each fixed permutation.

5.3
Sufficient Condition via Analytic Techniques

We next show analytically how Conjecture
1.7
can be reduced to specific sets
\displaystyle A\cup B
for each positive integer
\displaystyle m
. We use this to prove Conjecture
1.7
in the cases where
\displaystyle m=1
and
\displaystyle m=2

To begin, we have the following general lemma.

Lemma 5.1
.

Suppose that
\displaystyle f(x)
and
\displaystyle g(x)
satisfy
\displaystyle f(0)=1
and
\displaystyle g(0)=0
and furthermore suppose that the series for
\displaystyle\frac{1}{f(x)}
and the series for
\displaystyle g(x)
have all coefficients non-negative.

Then the coefficients of the series for
\displaystyle\frac{1}{f(x)-g(x)}
are also non-negative.

Proof.

We compute

\frac{1}{f(x)-g(x)}=\frac{1}{f(x)}\cdot\frac{1}{1-g(x)/f(x)}=\left(\frac{1}{f(x)}\right)\left(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left(g(x)\cdot\frac{1}{f(x)}\right)^{k}\right),

and by assumption both
\displaystyle\frac{1}{f(x)}
and
\displaystyle g(x)\cdot\frac{1}{f(x)}
have all coefficients non-negative. This completes the proof.
∎

This leads to the following.

Corollary 5.2
.

Suppose that
\displaystyle A\subseteq\mathcal{O}
and
\displaystyle B\subseteq\mathcal{E}
with
\displaystyle 1\in A
, and suppose that the smallest positive number in
\displaystyle\{1,2,3,\ldots\}\setminus(A\cup B)
is
\displaystyle 2m
.

If the series for

\frac{1}{1+\sum_{k=1}^{2m-1}\frac{(-1)^{k}x^{k}}{k!}+\sum_{k=m+1}^{\infty}\frac{x^{2k}}{(2k)!}},

corresponding to coefficients in
\displaystyle[2m-1]\cup\{2m+2,2m+4,\ldots\}
, has all coefficients non-negative, then the series for

\frac{1}{1-\sum_{a\in A}\frac{x^{a}}{a!}+\sum_{b\in B}\frac{x^{b}}{b!}}

has all coefficients non-negative.

Proof.

For each positive
\displaystyle m\geq 1
let

f_{m}(x)=1+\sum_{k=1}^{2m-1}\frac{(-1)^{k}x^{k}}{k!}+\sum_{k=m+1}^{\infty}\frac{x^{2k}}{(2k)!}.

By assumption, suppose also that
\displaystyle\frac{1}{f_{m}(x)}
has all non-negative coefficients.

Consider now the arbitrary
\displaystyle A\cup B
with
\displaystyle 1\in A
and with
\displaystyle 2m
the smallest positive integer missing from
\displaystyle A\cup B
. Let
\displaystyle C
be the set of even numbers, strictly greater than
\displaystyle 2m
, that are
missing from

\displaystyle B
, together with the set of odd numbers, strictly greater than
\displaystyle 2m
, that are
in

\displaystyle A

\frac{1}{1-\sum_{a\in A}\frac{x^{a}}{a!}+\sum_{b\in B}\frac{x^{b}}{b!}}=\frac{1}{f_{m}(x)-\sum_{c\in C}\frac{x^{c}}{c!}}

has all coefficients non-negative by Lemma

.
∎

So to prove Conjecture
1.7
, by Corollary

it suffices to consider

A\cup B=\{1,2,\ldots,2m-1,2m+2,2m+4,\ldots\}

for each positive integer
\displaystyle m
. This leads to the following proof of Conjecture
1.7
when
\displaystyle m=1
.

Theorem 5.3
.

Let
\displaystyle f(x)=1-x+\sum_{k\mbox{ even, }k\geq 4}x^{k}/k!
. Then the reciprocal
\displaystyle\frac{1}{f(x)}
has a series with all non-negative coefficients.

Proof.

Let
\displaystyle a>0
. We’ll show that there is a value for
\displaystyle a
so that
\displaystyle f(x)g(x)=1-p(x)
, where
\displaystyle g(x)=1+ax+a^{2}x^{2}+\cdots
and
\displaystyle p(x)
is a non-negative series with
\displaystyle p(0)=0
. Then the reciprocal
\displaystyle 1/f(x)=g(x)\cdot(1/(1-p(x)))
is a product of two non-negative series and is hence non-negative.

Let
\displaystyle n
be a positive integer. Then

\displaystyle\displaystyle[x^{n}]f(x)g(x)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle a^{n}-a^{n-1}+\sum_{k\mbox{ even, }4\leq k\leq n}a^{n-k}/k!

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle a^{n}\left(1-1/a+\sum_{k\mbox{ even, }4\leq k\leq n}(1/a)^{k}/k!\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\displaystyle a^{n}f(1/a).

If we find
\displaystyle a>0
so that
\displaystyle f(1/a)<0

\displaystyle[x^{n}]f(x)g(x)\leq 0
for all
\displaystyle n\geq 1

But
\displaystyle f(x)=\frac{e^{x}+e^{-x}}{2}-x-x^{2}/2

∎

With similar reasoning, we have a proof when
\displaystyle m=2
.

Theorem 5.4
.

Let
\displaystyle f(x)=1-x+\frac{x^{2}}{2!}-\frac{x^{3}}{3!}+\sum_{k\mbox{ even, }k\geq 6}x^{k}/k!

\displaystyle\frac{1}{f(x)}
has a series with all non-negative coefficients.

Proof.

Let
\displaystyle f(x)=1-x+\frac{x^{2}}{2!}-\frac{x^{3}}{3!}+\sum_{k\mbox{ even, }k\geq 6}\frac{x^{k}}{k!}

We’ll again show that there is an
\displaystyle a>0
so that

\displaystyle g(x)=1+x+\frac{x^{2}}{2!}+\frac{x^{3}}{3!}+a^{4}x^{4}+a^{5}x^{5}+\cdots
and
\displaystyle p(x)
is a non-negative series with
\displaystyle p(0)=0

We compute

and

\displaystyle\displaystyle\left[x^{4}\right]f(x)g(x)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

and let
\displaystyle A=F\langle\langle X_{1},X_{2},\ldots\rangle\rangle
be the algebra of formal power series in the noncommuting variables
\displaystyle X_{i}
for
\displaystyle i\geq 1
.

Let
\displaystyle n\geq 0
be an integer. We say that
\displaystyle L
is a composition of
\displaystyle n
, written
\displaystyle L\models n
, if
\displaystyle L
is a sequence
\displaystyle L=(L_{1},\ldots,L_{k})
of positive integers that sum to
\displaystyle n
where
\displaystyle k\geq 0
is an integer. Note that the only composition of
\displaystyle 0
is the empty composition, the
\displaystyle 0
-tuple
\displaystyle L=()
.

We will now define the subalgebra
\displaystyle\mathbf{Sym}
of
\displaystyle A
of noncommutative symmetric functions. Let

{\bf h}_{n}=\sum_{i_{1}\leq i_{2}\leq\cdots\leq i_{n}}X_{i_{1}}X_{i_{2}}\cdots X_{i_{n}}

be the complete noncommutative symmetric function. For any composition
\displaystyle L=(L_{1},\ldots,L_{k})
, let
\displaystyle{\bf h}_{L}={\bf h}_{L_{1}}\cdots{\bf h}_{L_{k}}
. We take
\displaystyle{\bf h}_{L}=1
for the empty composition
\displaystyle L=()
.
\displaystyle\mathbf{Sym}
is defined to be the subalgebra of
\displaystyle A
consisting of all (possibly infinite) linear combinations of the
\displaystyle{\bf h}_{L}
with coefficients in
\displaystyle F
. By this definition,
\displaystyle\mathbf{Sym}
has basis
\displaystyle H=\{{\bf h}_{L}:L\mbox{ a composition}\}
when it is viewed as a vector space over
\displaystyle F
.

If
\displaystyle s=(i_{1},\ldots,i_{n})
is a sequence of positive integers, a run of
\displaystyle s
is a maximal non-decreasing subsequence of consecutive entries of
\displaystyle s
. Let
\displaystyle L(s)\models n
be the run composition of
\displaystyle s
, i.e.
\displaystyle L=(L_{1},\ldots,L_{k})
where
\displaystyle L_{i}
is the length (i.e. number of elements) of the
\displaystyle i
th run in
\displaystyle s
. For any composition
\displaystyle L\models n
, we define the ribbon non-commutative symmetric function

{\bf r}_{L}=\sum_{s=(i_{1},\ldots,i_{n}):L(s)=L}X_{i_{1}}X_{i_{2}}\cdots X_{i_{n}}.

As shown in
[
GZ14
]
,
\displaystyle R=\{{\bf r}_{L}:L\mbox{ a composition}\}
is also a basis for
\displaystyle\mathbf{Sym}
.

Theorem 6.1

(The Run Theorem)
.

Let
\displaystyle F
be a field of characteristic
\displaystyle 0
.
Let
\displaystyle(a_{i})_{i=0}^{\infty}
be a sequence in
\displaystyle F
with
\displaystyle a_{0}=1
. In
\displaystyle\mathbf{Sym}
, the algebra of noncommutative symmetric functions over
\displaystyle F
, we have

\left(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}{\bf h}_{n}\right)^{-1}=\sum_{L}w_{L}{\bf r}_{L}

where the sequence
\displaystyle(w_{i})_{i=0}^{\infty}
in
\displaystyle F
is defined by the equation

\left(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}x^{n}\right)^{-1}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}w_{n}x^{n}

in the ring
\displaystyle F[[x]]
of power series in
\displaystyle x
with coefficients in
\displaystyle F
, the sum on the right is over all compositions
\displaystyle L
, and
\displaystyle w_{L}=w_{L_{1}}\cdots w_{L_{k}}
for all compositions
\displaystyle L=(L_{1},\ldots,L_{k})
.

The Run Theorem was first proved in an equivalent form in
[
Ges77
, Theorem 5.2]
. The form we give here follows the presentations given in
[
Zhu16
, Theorem 1]
and
[
GZ14
, Theorem 11]
. Note that the coefficients
\displaystyle a_{i}
and
\displaystyle w_{i}
may actually be taken to lie in a unital
\displaystyle F
-algebra (see
[
Zhu16
, Theorem 1]
), but the form we give is sufficient for our needs.

As noted in
[
GZ14
, Section 4.2]
, there is a homomorphism
\displaystyle\Phi:\mathbf{Sym}\to F[[x]]
for which
\displaystyle\Phi({\bf h}_{n})=x^{n}/n!
and
\displaystyle\Phi({\bf r}_{L})=\beta(L)x^{n}/n!
where
\displaystyle\beta(L)
is the number of permutations
\displaystyle\pi
in
\displaystyle S_{n}
with run-composition
\displaystyle L(\pi)=L
, i.e. with consecutive run-lengths as given by
\displaystyle L
. Applying this
\displaystyle\Phi
to the reciprocal relation in
\displaystyle\mathbf{Sym}
in Theorem
6.1
gives the following power series version of the Run Theorem.

Corollary 6.2
.

Let
\displaystyle F
be a field of characteristic
\displaystyle 0
. Let
\displaystyle(a_{i})_{i=0}^{\infty}
be a sequence in
\displaystyle F
with
\displaystyle a_{0}=1
. In the ring of power series
\displaystyle F[[x]]
we have

\left(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}\frac{x^{n}}{n!}\right)^{-1}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}b_{n}\frac{x^{n}}{n!}

where

b_{n}=\sum_{L\models n}w_{L}\beta(L),

\displaystyle w_{L}=w_{L_{1}}\cdots w_{L_{k}}
for all compositions
\displaystyle L=(L_{1},\ldots,L_{k})
, and the sequence
\displaystyle(w_{i})_{i=0}^{\infty}
in
\displaystyle F
is defined by the equation

\left(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}x^{n}\right)^{-1}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}w_{n}x^{n}

in
\displaystyle F[[x]]
.

We can use Corollary
6.2
to give another proof of Theorem
1.5
with an equivalent combinatorial formula for the coefficient of the reciprocal. We first need the following standard result on reciprocals in
\displaystyle F[[x]]
.

Theorem 6.3
.

Let
\displaystyle F
be a field of characteristic
\displaystyle 0
. Let
\displaystyle(a_{i})_{i=0}^{\infty}
be a sequence in
\displaystyle F
with
\displaystyle a_{0}=1
. Then

\left(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}x^{n}\right)^{-1}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}w_{n}x^{n},

where

w_{n}=\sum_{L\models n}(-1)^{|L|}a_{L},

and
\displaystyle a_{L}=a_{L_{1}}\cdots a_{L_{k}}
and
\displaystyle|L|=k
for each composition
\displaystyle L=(L_{1},\ldots,L_{k})
.

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle\displaystyle\left(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}x^{n}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\left(1-\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}(-a_{\ell})x^{\ell}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=1+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left(\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}(-a_{\ell})x^{\ell}\right)^{k}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=1+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\sum_{(L_{1},\ldots,L_{k})}(-1)^{k}a_{L_{1}}\cdots a_{L_{k}}x^{L_{1}+\cdots+L_{k}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=1+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(\sum_{L\models n}(-1)^{|L|}a_{L}\right)x^{n}

We use the formula for the geometric series in
\displaystyle F[[x]]
for the second equality above. Note that
\displaystyle w_{0}=1
if we adopt the usual convention that the only composition of
\displaystyle 0
is the empty composition
\displaystyle L=()
and that
\displaystyle a_{L}=1
for this composition.
∎

In particular, we use the following corollary of Theorem
6.3
.

Corollary 6.4
.

Let
\displaystyle F
be a field of characteristic
\displaystyle 0
. Let
\displaystyle S
be a subset of the positive integers. Then

\left(1-\sum_{s\in S}x^{s}\right)^{-1}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}w_{n}x^{n},

where
\displaystyle w_{n}
is the number of compositions of
\displaystyle n
, all of whose block sizes lie in
\displaystyle S
.

Proof.

We apply Theorem
6.3
with
\displaystyle a_{0}=1
and for all positive integers
\displaystyle n
,
\displaystyle a_{n}=-1
if
\displaystyle n\in S
and
\displaystyle a_{n}=0
if
\displaystyle n\not\in S
. Then for any composition
\displaystyle L=(L_{1},\ldots,L_{k})
,
\displaystyle(-1)^{|L|}a_{L}=1
if
\displaystyle L
is a composition, all of whose blocks are in
\displaystyle S
, and
\displaystyle(-1)^{|L|}a_{L}=0
otherwise. Thus
\displaystyle w_{n}
is the number of compositions of
\displaystyle n
, all of whose block sizes lie in
\displaystyle S
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.5
.

Let
\displaystyle{\cal O}
be the set of odd positive integers and
\displaystyle{\cal E}
be the set of even positive integers. We define the function
\displaystyle n^{*}
on the non-negative integers by setting
\displaystyle n^{*}(k)=krb/2
if
\displaystyle k
is even, and
\displaystyle n^{*}(k)=((k-1)r/2+1)b
if
\displaystyle k
is odd. Thus
\displaystyle n^{*}(0)=0
,
\displaystyle n^{*}({\cal O})=\{(jr+1)b:r\geq 0,j\geq 0\}={\cal O}^{*}
, and
\displaystyle n^{*}({\cal E})=\{jrb:r\geq 1,j\geq 1\}={\cal E}^{*}
.

Suppose
\displaystyle A\subseteq{\cal O}^{*}
,
\displaystyle B\subseteq{\cal E}^{*}
,
\displaystyle b\in A
, and
\displaystyle A\cup B
is odd-ended. Then
\displaystyle A\cup B
is the image under the map
\displaystyle n^{*}
of the union of a set of finitely or infinitely many intervals of integers of the form
\displaystyle I_{1}=[c_{1},d_{1}]
,
\displaystyle I_{2}=[c_{2},d_{2}],\ldots
satisfying the following conditions:

(i)

\displaystyle c_{1}=0
but otherwise
\displaystyle c_{i}
and
\displaystyle d_{i}
are odd for all
\displaystyle i
,

(ii)

\displaystyle 0=c_{1}<d_{1}<c_{2}\leq d_{2}<c_{3}\leq d_{3}<\cdots
,

(iii)

if there are finitely many intervals, the last interval
\displaystyle I_{m}
may also be of the form
\displaystyle I_{m}=[c_{m},\infty)
.

Let

f(x)=1-\sum_{a\in A}\frac{x^{a}}{a!}+\sum_{b\in B}\frac{x^{b}}{b!}=\sum_{k}\sum_{c_{k}\leq i\leq d_{k}}(-1)^{i}\frac{x^{n^{*}(i)}}{n^{*}(i)!}

and

g(x)=1-\sum_{a\in A}x^{a}+\sum_{b\in B}x^{b}=\sum_{k}\sum_{c_{k}\leq i\leq d_{k}}(-1)^{i}x^{n^{*}(i)}.

Briefly, we will use algebraic manipulations to show that ordinary generating function (o.g.f.)
\displaystyle g(x)
has a reciprocal whose o.g.f. has non-negative coefficients
\displaystyle w_{n}
. Corollary
6.2
then implies that the exponential generating function (e.g.f.)
\displaystyle f(x)
has a reciprocal whose e.g.f. has non-negative coefficients
\displaystyle b_{n}
. We will use Corollary
6.4
to show that
\displaystyle w_{n}
counts the number of compositions of a certain form. Thus the formula for
\displaystyle b_{n}
given by Corollary
6.2
will be a count of permutations that are combinatorially weighted according to their run-lengths. We now fill in the details.

Let
\displaystyle h(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(x^{krb}-x^{(kr+1)b})
. Note that

\displaystyle\displaystyle h(x)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=(1-x^{b})\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}x^{krb}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=(1-x^{b})/(1-x^{rb})

\displaystyle\displaystyle=1/(1+x^{b}+x^{2b}+\cdots+x^{(r-1)b}).

Thus we have

1/h(x)=1+x^{b}+x^{2b}+\cdots+x^{(r-1)b}

and

1-1/h(x)=-(x^{b}+x^{2b}+\cdots+x^{(r-1)b}).

Note that

\sum_{c_{1}\leq i\leq d_{1}}(-1)^{i}x^{n^{*}(i)}=h(x)\left(1-x^{n^{*}(d_{1}+1)}\right)

and that for all
\displaystyle k\geq 2

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{c_{k}\leq i\leq d_{k}}(-1)^{i}x^{n^{*}(i)}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=h(x)\left(x^{n^{*}(c_{k}-1)}-x^{n^{*}(d_{k}+1)}\right)-x^{n^{*}(c_{k}-1)}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=h(x)\left((1-1/h(x))x^{n^{*}(c_{k}-1)}-x^{n^{*}(d_{k}+1)}\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=h(x)\left(-(x^{b}+x^{2b}+\cdots+x^{(r-1)b})x^{n^{*}(c_{k}-1)}-x^{n^{*}(d_{k}+1)}\right)

Thus
\displaystyle g(x)=h(x)(1-\sum_{s\in S}x^{s})
where

\displaystyle\displaystyle S

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\{n^{*}(c_{k}-1)+jb:k\geq 2,1\leq j\leq r-1\}\cup\{n^{*}(d_{k}+1):k\geq 1\}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\{n^{*}(c_{k})+jb:k\geq 2,0\leq j\leq r-2\}\cup\{n^{*}(d_{k}+1):k\geq 1\}.

So

1/g(x)=(1+x^{b}+x^{2b}+\cdots x^{(r-1)b})\left(1-\sum_{s\in S}x^{s}\right)^{-1}.

By Corollary
6.4
,

1/g(x)=\sum_{n\geq 0}w_{n}x^{n},

where
\displaystyle w_{n}
is the number of compositions of
\displaystyle n
whose block sizes must lie in
\displaystyle S
, where we also allow the first block to have size
\displaystyle b,2b,\ldots,(r-1)b
. Clearly
\displaystyle w_{n}
is non-negative.

We then apply Corollary
6.2
to get that

1/f(x)=\sum_{n\geq 0}b_{n}\frac{x^{n}}{n!}

where

b_{n}=\sum_{L\models n}w_{L}\beta(L).

Clearly
\displaystyle b_{n}
is also non-negative. This formula can also be considered a combinatorial formula for
\displaystyle b_{n}
. One obtains
\displaystyle b_{n}
by summing over all permutations
\displaystyle\pi
of
\displaystyle[n]
so that each permutation
\displaystyle\pi
is weighted by
\displaystyle w_{L}=w_{L_{1}}\cdots w_{L_{k}}
if it has run-composition
\displaystyle L(\pi)=(L_{1},\ldots,L_{k})
.
∎

Note that this proof of Theorem
1.5
appeals to the theory of non-commutative symmetric functions as well as algebraic manipulations of generating functions whereas the proofs of Theorems
1.4
and
1.5
given in Sections
3
and
4
are purely combinatorial. Those proofs also appeal to sign-reversing involutions, a tactic used in
[
EGS19
]
to give non-negativity results and combinatorial formulas for the coefficients of the
compositional inverses
of a large class of power series.

Note also that the combinatorial count given by the Run Theorem is of a slightly different form that the ones given in Corollaries
4.5
and
4.8
. The translation from this interpretation to that in Corollary
4.8
amounts to considering up to
\displaystyle r-1
singleton blocks at the end of the algorithm (which are fixed), along with viewing freeze skip pairs as a single block (with size
\displaystyle n^{*}(d_{k}+1)
), and viewing the blocks with at most
\displaystyle r-2
singletons to the right as another single block (with size
\displaystyle n^{*}(c_{k})+jb
).

Lastly, we note that it is not possible to use the Run Theorem to answer Conjecture
1.7
as the corresponding o.g.f.s often have some negative coefficients.
