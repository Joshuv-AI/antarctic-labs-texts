Stiefel-Whitney Classes of representations of some finite groups of lie type
Neha Malik, Steven Spallone

Abstract
. In this note we present the Stiefel-Whitney classes (SWCs) for orthogonal representations of several finite groups of Lie type, namely for G = SL ⁡ ( 2 , q ) , G=\SL(2,q), SL ⁡ ( 3 , q ) , \SL(3,q), Sp ⁡ ( 4 , q ) \Sp(4,q) , and Sp ⁡ ( 6 , q ) \Sp(6,q) , with q q odd. We also describe the SWCs for G = SL ⁡ ( 2 , q ) G=\SL(2,q) when q q is even.



1.
Introduction

The theory of Stiefel-Whitney classes (SWCs) of vector bundles is an old unifying concept in geometry. Real representations, or equivalently, orthogonal complex representations of a group
G
give rise to
flat
vector bundles over the classifying space
BG
. Via this construction one defines SWCs of representations, which live in the group cohomology
H^{*}(G,\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})
. There do not seem to be many explicit calculations of such SWCs, especially when
G
is nonabelian. In this note we give formulas for SWCs in terms of character values, for the groups mentioned in the abstract.

Formulas for cyclic groups are well-known; we review this below. The second SWCs of representations of
S_{n}
and related groups were found in
[
GS20
]
.
The case of
\GL(2,q)
with
q
odd was completed recently in
[
GJ21
]
; their results are analogous to ours.

This note is intended only as an announcement of our calculations; the work will appear elsewhere.

2.
Notations & Preliminaries

2.1.
Orthogonal Representations

Let
G
be a finite group. We say that a representation
(\pi,V)
, with
V
a complex finite-dimensional vector space, is
orthogonal
, provided there exists a non-degenerate
G
-invariant symmetric bilinear form
B:V\times V\to\mathbb{C}
. From
[
BtD95
, Chapter II, Section 6]
, we note:

Proposition 1
.

If
(\pi,V)
is orthogonal, then there is a
(\pi_{0},V_{0})
, with
V_{0}
a real vector space, so that
\pi_{0}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{C}\cong\pi
. Moreover,
\pi_{0}
is unique up to isomorphism.

2.2.
Stiefel-Whitney Classes

In this section we define Stiefel-Whitney classes for orthogonal complex representations of finite groups. This is equivalent to the definition in
[
Ben98
]
or
[
GKT89
]
, given for real representations.

Recall from
[
MS16
]
that a (
d
-dimensional real) vector bundle
\mathcal{V}
over a paracompact space
B
is assigned a sequence

w_{1}(\mathcal{V}),\ldots,w_{d}(\mathcal{V})

of cohomology classes, with each
w_{i}(\mathcal{V})\in H^{*}(B,\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})
.

For every finite group
G
there is a classifying space
BG
with a principal
G
-bundle
EG
, unique up to homotopy. From a representation
(\pi,V)
one can form the associated vector bundle
EG[V]
over
BG
. Then one puts

w_{i}^{\mathbb{R}}(\pi)=w_{i}(EG[V]);

see for instance
[
Ben98
]
or
[
GKT89
]
.
Moreover the singular cohomology
H^{*}(BG,\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})
is isomorphic to the group cohomology
H^{*}(G,\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})
.

Hence, from a real representation
\rho
, we can associate cohomology classes
w_{i}^{\mathbb{R}}(\rho)\in H^{i}(G,\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})
, called Stiefel-Whitney classes (SWCs).
We prefer to work with an orthogonal complex representation
\pi
, which by Proposition
1
comes from a unique real representation
\pi_{0}
.
We thus define

w_{i}(\pi):=w_{i}^{\mathbb{R}}(\pi_{0}),

for
0\leq i\leq\deg\pi
.

At this point we simply write
H^{*}(G)=H^{*}(G,\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})
. A group homomorphism
\varphi:G_{1}\to G_{2}
induces a map

\varphi^{*}:H^{*}(G_{2})\to H^{*}(G_{1}).

The first few
w_{i}(\pi)
have nice interpretations. Firstly,
w_{0}(\pi)=1\in H^{0}(G)
. Moreover, the first SWC, applied to linear characters
G\to\{\pm 1\}\cong\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
, is the well-known isomorphism

w_{1}:\Hom(G,\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})\overset{\sim}{\to}H^{1}(G,\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}).

More generally, if
\pi
is an orthogonal representation, then
w_{1}(\pi)=w_{1}(\det\pi)
, where
\det\pi
is simply the composition of
\pi
with the determinant map.
When
\det\pi=1
, then
w_{2}(\pi)
vanishes iff
\pi
lifts to the corresponding spin group. (See
[
GS20
]
for details.)

The SWCs are functorial in the following sense: If
\rho
is an orthogonal representation of
G_{2}
, then

(1)

\varphi^{*}(w(\pi))=w(\pi\circ\varphi).

3.
Group Cohomology

In this section we review explicit descriptions of group cohomology for several important groups. Later, we will describe SWCs in terms of these descriptions.
Throughout this note, we put
k=\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
, and write
\Theta_{\pi}
for the character of a representation
\pi
.

3.1.
Cyclic Groups

Let
n
be even, and
G=C_{n}
, the cyclic group of order
n
. Let
g
be a generator of
G
, and
\chi_{\bullet}
the linear character of
G
with
\chi_{\bullet}(g)=e^{\frac{2\pi i}{n}}
. Write ‘sgn’ for the linear character of order 2.
It is known
[
Kam67
]
that

H^{*}(C_{n})=\begin{cases}k[s,t]/(s^{2}),&n\equiv 0\text{ (mod }4)\\
k[v],&n\equiv 2\text{ (mod }4)\end{cases}

where
s=w_{1}(\sgn),

t=w_{2}(\chi_{\bullet}\oplus\chi_{\bullet}^{-1})
for
n\equiv 0\text{ (mod }4)
, and
v=w_{1}(\sgn)
when
n\equiv 2\text{ (mod }4)
.

Since the cyclic case is so easy, we can give our first example of expressing SWCs in terms of character values.

Proposition 2
.

Let
\pi
be an orthogonal representation of
C_{n}
, and
b_{\pi}=\frac{1}{4}\left(\deg\pi-\Theta_{\pi}(g^{n/2})\right)
.

(1)

If
n\equiv 2\mod 4
, then

w(\pi)=(1+v)^{2b_{\pi}}.

(2)

If
n\equiv 0\mod 4
, and
\det\pi=1
, then

w(\pi)=(1+t)^{b_{\pi}}.

(3)

If
n\equiv 0\mod 4
, and
\det\pi\neq 1
, then

w(\pi)=(1+s)(1+t)^{b_{\pi}}.

Let
C_{n}^{r}
be the
n
-fold product of
C_{n}
, with projection maps
\pr_{i}:C_{n}^{r}\to C_{n}
for
1\leq i\leq r
. By Künneth, we have

H^{*}(C_{n}^{r})=\begin{cases}k[s_{1},\dots,s_{r},t_{1},\dots,t_{r}]/(s_{1}^{2},\ldots,s_{r}^{2}),&n\equiv 0\text{ (mod }4)\\
k[v_{1},\dots,v_{r}],&n\equiv 2\text{ (mod }4)\end{cases}

where we put
s_{i}=w_{1}(\sgn\circ\pr_{i})
and
t_{i}=w_{2}((\chi_{\bullet}\oplus\chi_{\bullet}^{-1})\circ\pr_{i})
for
n\equiv 0\text{ (mod }4)
, and
v_{i}=w_{1}(\sgn\circ\pr_{i})
, for
n\equiv 2\text{ (mod }4)
. (Here
1\leq i\leq r
.)

3.2.
Special Linear Groups

From
[
Qui72
]
, we have for odd
q
,

H^{*}(\SL(2,q))=k[e]\otimes_{k}k[b]/b^{2},

where
\deg(b)=3
and
e
is the unique non-zero element in
H^{4}(\SL(2,q))
.

Let
\mathcal{Z}
be the center of
\SL(2,q)
, which has order
2
. The restriction map

\displaystyle H^{4}(\SL(2,q))

\displaystyle\to H^{4}(\mathcal{Z})

\displaystyle e

\displaystyle\mapsto v^{4}

is a group isomorphism.

3.3.
Symplectic Groups

Fix the matrix
J=\begin{pmatrix}0&&0&&\ldots&&0&&1\\
0&&0&&\ldots&&-1&&0\\
\vdots&&\vdots&&\iddots&&\vdots&&\vdots\\
0&&1&&\ldots&&0&&0\\
-1&&0&&\ldots&&0&&0\\
\end{pmatrix}
,

and let
G=\Sp(2n,q):=\{A\in\GL(2n,q)\mid A^{t}JA=J\}
, with
q
odd. Write
X
for the subgroup of matrices in
G
, all of whose nonzero entries lie either on the diagonal or the antidiagonal.
Thus

X=\begin{pmatrix}\square&0&0&\ldots&0&0&\square\\
0&\ddots&&&&\iddots&0\\
0&&\square&&\square&&0\\
\vdots&&\vdots&\scalebox{3}{$\square$}_{2\times 2}&\vdots&&\vdots\\
0&&\square&&\square&&0\\
0&\iddots&&&&\ddots&0\\
\square&0&0&\ldots&0&0&\square\\
\end{pmatrix}.

Note that
X
is isomorphic to the direct product of
n
copies of
\SL(2,q)
.
So there are projections

\text{pr}_{j}:X\to\SL(2,q)\text{ ; }1\leq j\leq n,

and by Künneth we have

H^{*}(X)\cong k[e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}]\otimes_{k}k[b_{1},\ldots,b_{n}]/(b_{1}^{2},\ldots,b_{n}^{2}),

where
e_{j}=\text{pr}^{*}_{j}(e)
and
b_{j}=\text{pr}^{*}_{j}(b)
for
1\leq j\leq n.

4.
Detection of Cohomology

Let
G
be a finite group. We say a subgroup
G^{\prime}

detects
the mod-2 cohomology of
G
when the restriction map

H^{*}(G)\to H^{*}(G^{\prime})

is an injection.

Suppose this is the case, and that
\pi
is an orthogonal representation of
G
. Then
w(\pi)
is specified by its image in
H^{*}(G^{\prime})
, which is actually the SWC of the restriction of
\pi
to
G^{\prime}
. In our formulas in the next section, when there is a detecting subgroup, we will simply again write
w(\pi)
for this image.

Lemma 1

(
[
AM04
]
, Cor. 5.2, Ch. II)
.

A Sylow 2-subgroup of
G
detects its mod-2 cohomology.

Proposition 3

(
[
Qui72
]
)
.

When
q
is odd, the diagonal subgroup
A
of
\GL(n,q)
detects its mod-2 cohomology.

From the above one can deduce:

Proposition 4
.

When
n
and
q
are odd, the diagonal subgroup
A_{1}
of
\SL(n,q)
detects its mod-2 cohomology.

Finally, there is:

Proposition 5

(
[
AM04
]
, Lemma 6.2)
.

For odd
q
, the subgroup
X
detects the mod-2 cohomology of
\Sp(2n,q)
.

5.
Main Theorems

Now we are ready to describe the promised SWCs.

5.1.
G=\SL(2,q)
,
q
odd

Theorem 1
.

The total SWC of an orthogonal representation
\pi
of
\SL(2,q)
is

(2)

w(\pi)=(1+e)^{r_{\pi}},

where
e
is as defined in Section
3.2
, and
r_{\pi}=\frac{1}{8}(\Theta_{\pi}(\mathbbm{1})-\Theta_{\pi}(-\mathbbm{1})).

We can say more when
\pi
is irreducible; write
\omega_{\pi}
for its central character.

Proposition 6

(
[
Gow85
]
, Theorem 1)
.

If
\pi
is irreducible orthogonal, then
\omega_{\pi}(-\mathbbm{1})=1
. If
\pi
is irreducible symplectic, then
\omega_{\pi}(-\mathbbm{1})=-1
.

Thus if
\pi
is irreducible orthogonal, then
\Theta_{\pi}(-\mathbbm{1})=\Theta_{\pi}(\mathbbm{1})
, and so
r_{\pi}=0
. Therefore:

Corollary 1.1
.

Let
\pi
be an irreducible orthogonal representation of
G
. Then
w(\pi)=1
.

On the other hand, let
\rho
be an irreducible symplectic representation of
G
. (By this we mean that
\rho
is an irreducible representation on a complex vector space
V
admitting a non-degenerate
G
-invariant
antisymmetric

B:V\times V\to\mathbb{C}
.) Its double
\pi=\rho\oplus\rho
is orthogonal. Then

\Theta_{\pi}(-\mathbbm{1})=-\Theta_{\pi}(\mathbbm{1})
, and so

\begin{split}r_{\pi}&=\frac{1}{8}\cdot 2\Theta_{\pi}(\mathbbm{1})\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\deg\rho.\\
\end{split}

Therefore

w(\rho\oplus\rho)=(1+e)^{\frac{1}{2}\deg\rho}.

5.2.
G=\SL(2,q)
,
q
even

Now let
q
be even, say
q=2^{r}
.
Let
N
be the subgroup of upper unitriangular matrices in
G
. Being a Sylow 2-subgroup,
N
detects the mod-2 cohomology of
G
.

Now
N\cong(\mathbb{F}_{q},+)
is an elementary abelian 2-group, so from Section
3.1
, we have

H^{*}(N)\cong H^{*}(C_{2}^{r})\cong k[v_{1},\ldots,v_{r}].

Set
n_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}1&&1\\
0&&1\end{pmatrix}\in N.

Every representation
\pi
of
G
is orthogonal by the main result in
[
Vin20
]
.

Theorem 2
.

The total SWC of
\pi
is

w(\pi)=\Big(\prod_{v\in H^{1}(N)}(1+v)\Big)^{s_{\pi}}\in H^{*}(N),

where
s_{\pi}=\frac{1}{q}(\Theta_{\pi}(1)-\Theta_{\pi}(n_{1})).

The expansion of this product is well-known. We have

(3)

\prod_{v\in H^{1}(N)}(1+v)=1+\sum_{i=0}^{r-1}c_{r,i}\in H^{*}(N),\\

with
c_{r,i}
the famous Dickson invariants described in
[
Wil83
]
. Note that
\deg(c_{r,i})=2^{r}-2^{i}
.

5.3.
G=\SL(3,q)
,
q
odd

Let
q
be odd, and put
a_{1}=\diag(-1,-1,1)\in\SL(3,q)
. (Meaning, as usual, the diagonal matrix with these diagonal entries.)

Theorem 3
.

Let
m_{\pi}=\frac{1}{8}\big(\Theta_{\pi}(\mathbbm{1})-\Theta_{\pi}(a_{1})\big).
Then,

w(\pi)=\begin{cases}\big((1+t_{1})(1+t_{2})(1+t_{1}+t_{2})\big)^{m_{\pi}}\;\;,&q\equiv 1\text{ (mod }4)\\
\big((1+v_{1})(1+v_{2})(1+v_{1}+v_{2})\big)^{2m_{\pi}}\;,&q\equiv 3\text{ (mod }4).\end{cases}

Remark 1
.

Again we encounter Dickson products as in (
3
), but where the vector space
H^{1}(N)
is replaced with
\Span\{t_{1},t_{2}\}
or
H^{1}(A)
.

5.4.
G=\Sp(4,q)
,
q
odd

Let
q
be odd, and
G=\Sp(4,q)
. Put
g_{1}=\diag(1,-1,-1,1)\in\Sp(4,q).

Theorem 4
.

The total SWC of an orthogonal representation
\pi
of
G
is

w(\pi)=((1+e_{1})(1+e_{2}))^{r_{\pi}}(1+e_{1}+e_{2})^{s_{\pi}},

where

\displaystyle r_{\pi}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{16}\Big(\Theta_{\pi}(\mathbbm{1})-\Theta_{\pi}(-\mathbbm{1})\Big),

\displaystyle s_{\pi}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{16}\Big(\Theta_{\pi}(\mathbbm{1})+\Theta_{\pi}(-\mathbbm{1})-2\Theta_{\pi}(g_{1})\Big).

Again from Theorem 1 of
[
Gow85
]
, we deduce that when
\pi
is irreducible orthogonal, we have

w(\pi)=(1+e_{1}+e_{2})^{s_{\pi}},

where

s_{\pi}=\frac{1}{8}\left(\deg\pi-\Theta_{\pi}(g_{1})\right).

5.5.
G=\Sp(6,q)
,
q
odd

Let

\displaystyle g_{1}

\displaystyle=\text{diag}(1,1,-1,-1,1,1),

\displaystyle g_{2}

\displaystyle=\text{diag}(1,-1,-1,-1,-1,1)\in G.

Theorem 5
.

The total SWC of an orthogonal representation
\pi
of
G
is

w(\pi)=\Big(\prod_{1\leq i\leq 3}(1+e_{i})\Big)^{r_{\pi}}\Big(\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq 3}(1+e_{i}+e_{j})\Big)^{s_{\pi}}(1+e_{1}+e_{2}+e_{3})^{t_{\pi}},

where

\displaystyle r_{\pi}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{32}\big(\Theta_{\pi}(\mathbbm{1})+\Theta_{\pi}(g_{1})-\Theta_{\pi}(g_{2})-\Theta_{\pi}(-\mathbbm{1})\big)

\displaystyle s_{\pi}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{32}\big(\Theta_{\pi}(\mathbbm{1})-\Theta_{\pi}(g_{1})-\Theta_{\pi}(g_{2})+\Theta_{\pi}(-\mathbbm{1})\big),\text{ and}

\displaystyle t_{\pi}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{32}\big(\Theta_{\pi}(\mathbbm{1})-3\Theta_{\pi}(g_{1})+3\Theta_{\pi}(g_{2})-\Theta_{\pi}(-\mathbbm{1})\big).

Once more Theorem 1 of
[
Gow85
]
allows simplification: when
\pi
is irreducible orthogonal, we have

w(\pi)=\Big(\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq 3}(1+e_{i}+e_{j})\Big)^{s_{\pi}},

where

s_{\pi}=\frac{1}{16}\left(\deg\pi-\Theta_{\pi}(g_{1})\right).
