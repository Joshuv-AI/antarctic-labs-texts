A trajectorial approach to relative entropy dissipation of McKean–Vlasov diffusions: gradient flows and HWBI inequalities
Bertram Tschiderer, Lane Chun Yeung

Abstract
Abstract. We formulate a trajectorial version of the relative entropy dissipation identity for McKean–Vlasov diffusions, extending the results of the papers [ FJ16 , KST20a ] , which apply to non-interacting diffusions. Our stochastic analysis approach is based on time-reversal of diffusions and Lions’ differential calculus over Wasserstein space. It allows us to compute explicitly the rate of relative entropy dissipation along every trajectory of the underlying diffusion via the semimartingale decomposition of the corresponding relative entropy process. As a first application, we obtain a new interpretation of the gradient flow structure for the granular media equation, generalizing the formulation in [ KST20a ] developed for the linear Fokker–Planck equation. Secondly, we show how the trajectorial approach leads to a new derivation of the HWBI inequality, which relates relative entropy (H), Wasserstein distance (W), barycenter (B) and Fisher information (I). MSC 2020 subject classifications: Primary 60H30, 60G44; secondary 60J60, 94A17 Keywords and phrases: relative entropy dissipation, gradient flow, McKean–Vlasov diffusion, granular media equation, HWBI inequality



1
Introduction

We are interested in the relative entropy dissipation of McKean–Vlasov stochastic differential equations of the form

\mathrm{d}X_{t}=-\big(\nabla V(X_{t})+\nabla(W\ast\mathrm{P}_{\kern-0.81949ptt})(X_{t})\big)\,\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2}\,\mathrm{d}B_{t}\,,\qquad 0\leqslant t\leqslant T,

(1.1)

where
X_{0}
has some given initial distribution
\mathrm{P}_{\kern-0.81949pt0}
on
\mathds{R}^{n}
. Here, the functions
V,W\colon\mathds{R}^{n}\rightarrow[0,\infty)
play the roles of confinement and interaction potentials and are assumed to be suitably regular,
\mathrm{P}_{\kern-0.81949ptt}\coloneqq\operatorname{Law}(X_{t})
denotes the distribution of the random vector
X_{t}
, the symbol
\ast
stands for the standard convolution operator, and
(B_{t})_{0\leqslant t\leqslant T}
is a standard
n
-dimensional Brownian motion. In particular, this SDE is non-local (or non-linear) in the sense that the drift term depends on the distribution of the state variable. Non-local equations of this form arise in the modeling of weakly interacting diffusion equations, after the seminal work of McKean
[
McK66
]
.

Since the work of Carrillo–McCann–Villani
[
CMV03
,
CMV06
]
, relative entropy dissipation has been known to be an effective method for studying convergence rates to equilibrium and propagation of chaos of McKean–Vlasov equations. Some notable examples include the works
[
CJM+01
,
Mal03
,
BGG13
,
Tug13
,
CA14
,
CGPS20
]
. In a broader context,
[
MMN18
,
HRŠS20
]
recently applied entropy methods to the mean-field theory of neural networks.

We denote by
\mathscr{P}_{\mathrm{ac}}(\mathds{R}^{n})
the set of absolutely continuous probability measures on
\mathds{R}^{n}
, which we will often identify with their corresponding probability density functions with respect to Lebesgue measure. The
free energy functional

\mathscr{P}_{\mathrm{ac}}(\mathds{R}^{n})\ni p\longmapsto\mathscr{F}(p)\coloneqq\mathcal{U}(p)+\mathcal{V}(p)+\mathcal{W}(p)

(1.2)

is defined as the sum of the energy functionals

\mathcal{U}(p)\coloneqq\int_{\mathds{R}^{n}}p(x)\log p(x)\,\mathrm{d}x,\quad\mathcal{V}(p)\coloneqq\int_{\mathds{R}^{n}}V(x)\,p(x)\,\mathrm{d}x,\quad\mathcal{W}(p)\coloneqq\tfrac{1}{2}\int_{\mathds{R}^{n}}(W\ast p)(x)\,p(x)\,\mathrm{d}x

(1.3)

corresponding to internal (
\mathcal{U}
), potential (
\mathcal{V}
) and interaction (
\mathcal{W}
) energy, respectively. Defining the
relative entropy dissipation functional

\mathscr{P}_{\mathrm{ac}}(\mathds{R}^{n})\ni p\longmapsto\mathscr{D}(p)\coloneqq\int_{\mathds{R}^{n}}|\nabla\log p(x)+\nabla V(x)+\nabla(W\ast p)(x)|^{2}\,p(x)\,\mathrm{d}x,

(1.4)

the well-known relative entropy dissipation identity takes the form

\mathscr{F}(p_{t})-\mathscr{F}(p_{t_{0}})=-\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\mathscr{D}(p_{u})\,\mathrm{d}u.

(1.5)

This identity is of a deterministic nature: it only depends on the curve of probability density functions
(p_{t})_{0\leqslant t\leqslant T}
, but not on the trajectories of the underlying process
(X_{t})_{0\leqslant t\leqslant T}
itself. It is then natural to ask whether there is a process-level analogue of the relative entropy dissipation identity
(
1.5
)
, depending directly on the trajectories of the McKean–Vlasov process
(X_{t})_{0\leqslant t\leqslant T}
. The main contribution of this paper is to give an affirmative answer to this question, by formulating a trajectorial version of the relative entropy dissipation identity via a stochastic analysis approach.

Before going into details, let us briefly describe the main ideas. We draw inspiration from prior literature
[
FJ16
,
KST20a
]
based on a simpler (linear) setting without interaction, i.e.,
W\equiv 0
. In this case, the McKean–Vlasov SDE
(
1.1
)
reduces to a Langevin–Smoluchowski diffusion equation of the form

\mathrm{d}X_{t}=-\nabla V(X_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2}\,\mathrm{d}B_{t}\,,\qquad 0\leqslant t\leqslant T.

(1.6)

In particular, the drift term does not depend on the distribution of
X_{t}
. Moreover, there is an explicit stationary distribution (also known as the Gibbs distribution) with density proportional to
q(x)\coloneqq\mathrm{e}^{-V(x)}
. Defining the likelihood ratio function (or Radon–Nikodym derivative)
\ell_{t}(x)\coloneqq p_{t}(x)/\penalty q(x)
, the free energy at time
t
can be expressed as
\mathscr{F}(p_{t})=\mathds{E}[\log\ell_{t}(X_{t})]
, and the resulting stochastic process

\log\ell_{t}(X_{t})=\log p_{t}(X_{t})+V(X_{t})\,,\qquad 0\leqslant t\leqslant T

(1.7)

is called
free energy
or
relative entropy process
. As shown in
[
FJ16
,
KST20a
]
, the time-reversal
\log\ell_{T-s}(X_{T-s})_{0\leqslant s\leqslant T}
of this process is a submartingale, and Itô calculus can be used to obtain its Doob–Meyer decomposition

\log\ell_{T-s}(X_{T-s})-\log\ell_{T}(X_{T})=M_{T-s}+F_{T-s}.

(1.8)

Here,
(M_{T-s})_{0\leqslant s\leqslant T}
is a martingale and
(F_{T-s})_{0\leqslant s\leqslant T}
is an increasing process of finite first variation, both with explicit expressions. This decomposition describes exactly the rate of relative entropy dissipation along every trajectory of the Langevin–Smoluchowski diffusion. Therefore, it can be viewed as a trajectorial analogue of the (deterministic) relative entropy dissipation identity
(
1.5
)
.

Let us now return to our McKean–Vlasov setting. In order to take into account the interaction potential
W
, it is natural to consider a generalized relative entropy process of the form

\log p_{t}(X_{t})+V(X_{t})+\tfrac{1}{2}(W\ast\mathrm{P}_{\kern-0.81949ptt})(X_{t})\,,\qquad 0\leqslant t\leqslant T.

(1.9)

The task is now to compute the semimartingale decomposition of this process. We will provide a detailed analysis of this extension, which is subtler than might appear at first sight. The main difficulty is that, even when it exists, the stationary distribution of the McKean–Vlasov diffusion does not have a closed-form expression in general. This prevents us from defining the likelihood ratio function in a straightforward manner as in the setting of Langevin–Smoluchowski diffusions, where one can rely on the invariant Gibbs distribution. An appropriate definition of the generalized likelihood ratio function turns out to be that
(
1.9
)
should be viewed as a function of the form
\log\ell_{t}(X_{t},\mathrm{P}_{\kern-0.81949ptt})
, depending explicitly on the distribution
\mathrm{P}_{\kern-0.81949ptt}
of
X_{t}
itself, in addition to the state
X_{t}
. This form of generalized likelihood ratio function allows us to take the L
-derivative
with respect to the probability distribution
\mathrm{P}_{\kern-0.81949ptt}
. The notion of L-differentiation for functions of probability measures was introduced by Lions
[
Lio08
]
. We refer to the monograph
[
CD18
, Chapter 5]
for a detailed discussion of differential calculus and stochastic analysis over spaces of probability measures. In particular, we will use a generalized form of Itô’s formula for functions of curves of measures, to derive the dynamics of the time-reversal of the relative entropy process
(
1.9
)
, in terms of the semimartingale decomposition

\log\ell_{T-s}(X_{T-s},\mathrm{P}_{\kern-0.81949ptT-s})-\log\ell_{T}(X_{T},\mathrm{P}_{\kern-0.81949ptT})=M_{T-s}+F_{T-s}\,,\qquad 0\leqslant s\leqslant T,

(1.10)

where
(M_{T-s})_{0\leqslant s\leqslant T}
is a martingale and
(F_{T-s})_{0\leqslant s\leqslant T}
is a process of finite first variation, both of which will be explicitly computed. Similar to the case of Langevin–Smoluchowski dynamics, this decomposition can be viewed as the trajectorial rate of relative entropy dissipation. The classical (deterministic) identity
(
1.5
)
can then be recovered by taking expectations.

1.1
Gradient flow structure of the granular media equation

As a first application of our trajectorial approach we obtain a new interpretation of the gradient flow structure of the
granular media equation

\partial_{t}p_{t}(x)=\operatorname{div}\Big(\nabla p_{t}(x)+p_{t}(x)\nabla V(x)+p_{t}(x)\nabla(W\ast p_{t})(x)\Big)\,,\qquad(t,x)\in(0,T)\times\mathds{R}^{n},

(1.11)

which describes the evolution of the curve of probability density functions
(p_{t})_{0\leqslant t\leqslant T}
corresponding to the McKean–Vlasov diffusion
(X_{t})_{0\leqslant t\leqslant T}
of
(
1.1
)
. When
n=1
, this PDE appears in the modeling of the time evolution of granular media
[
BCCP98
,
Vil06
,
CGM08
]
; in that context, the granular medium is modeled as system of particles performing inelastic collisions, and
p_{t}(x)
is regarded as the velocity of a representative particle in the system at time
t
and position
x
, while
V
and
W
represent the friction and the inelastic collision forces, respectively. Note that in the interaction-free case
W\equiv 0
, the equation
(
1.11
)
reduces to a linear
Fokker–Planck equation
. As is well known from
[
CMV03
,
CMV06
]
, this curve of probability densities can be characterized as a
gradient flow
in
\mathscr{P}_{\mathrm{ac},2}(\mathds{R}^{n})
, the space of absolutely continuous probability measures with finite second moments. Roughly speaking, this is an optimality property stating that the curve
(p_{t})_{0\leqslant t\leqslant T}
evolves in the direction of steepest possible descent for the free energy functional
(
1.2
)
with respect to the quadratic Wasserstein distance

W_{2}(\mu,\nu)=\Big(\,\inf_{\scriptscriptstyle Y\sim\mu,Z\sim\nu}\mathds{E}|Y-Z|^{2}\,\Big)^{1/2}\,,\qquad\mu,\nu\in\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathds{R}^{n}).

(1.12)

The Wasserstein gradient flow structure of the linear Fokker–Planck equation was first discovered by Jordan, Kinderlehrer and Otto in the seminal work
[
JKO98
]
. In the paper
[
OV00
]
, Otto and Villani developed a formal Riemannian structure on the space of probability measures with finite second moments, leading to heuristic proofs of gradient flow properties as in
[
Ott01
]
, where the porous medium equation was studied. This pioneering approach is often referred to as “Otto calculus”. Later, a rigorous framework based on minimizing movement schemes and curves of maximal slope was introduced in
[
AGS08
]
. Recently, a trajectorial approach to the gradient flow properties of Langevin–Smoluchowski diffusions
[
KST20a
]
and Markov chains
[
KMS20
]
was established. We will follow this approach and adapt it to our McKean–Vlasov setting. For gradient flows of McKean–Vlasov equations on discrete spaces we refer to
[
EFLS16
]
.

Returning to the setting of this paper, our main result leads to a new formulation of the gradient flow property of the granular media equation. To show this steepest descent property, the main idea is to consider a
perturbed
McKean–Vlasov diffusion of the form

\mathrm{d}X_{t}=-\big(\nabla V(X_{t})+\nabla\bm{\beta}(X_{t})+\nabla(W\ast\mathrm{P}_{\kern-0.81949ptt}^{\bm{\beta}})(X_{t})\big)\,\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2}\,\mathrm{d}B_{t}^{\bm{\beta}}\,,\qquad t_{0}\leqslant t\leqslant T

(1.13)

which is constructed by adding a perturbation
\bm{\beta}\colon\mathds{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathds{R}
to the confinement potential
1
1

1

As we will see, the steepest descent property is already visible by perturbing the confinement potential from
V
to
V+\bm{\beta}
, thus we avoid complicating the setup further by adding another perturbation to the interaction potential
W
.
of the original McKean–Vlasov SDE
(
1.1
)
. In other words, from time
t_{0}
onward, the perturbed diffusion drifts in a direction different from that of the original diffusion, hence the perturbed curve of time-marginal distributions
(\mathrm{P}^{\bm{\beta}}_{\kern-0.81949ptt})_{t_{0}\leqslant t\leqslant T}
also evolves differently from the unperturbed curve
(\mathrm{P}_{\kern-0.81949ptt})_{t_{0}\leqslant t\leqslant T}
. In parallel with the unperturbed case, we may compute the dynamics of the perturbed relative entropy process associated with
(
1.13
)
. As a consequence, we derive the rate of relative entropy dissipation for the perturbed McKean–Vlasov diffusion. On the other hand, the rate of change of the Wasserstein distance along the perturbed curve
(\mathrm{P}^{\bm{\beta}}_{\kern-0.81949ptt})_{t_{0}\leqslant t\leqslant T}
can be computed based on the general theory of metric derivative of absolutely continuous curves, see
[
AGS08
]
. Finally, comparing these two rates in both the perturbed and unperturbed settings, allows us to establish the gradient flow property.

1.2
The HWBI inequality

The second application of our trajectorial approach deals with the HWBI inequality
[
AGK04
, Theorem 4.2]
, which is an extension of the HWI inequality
[
OV00
]
. It relates not only relative entropy (H), Wasserstein distance (W), and relative Fisher information (I), but also barycenter (B). These quantities are defined as follows: for two probability measures
\nu,\mu\in\mathscr{P}(\mathds{R}^{n})
, the
relative entropy
of
\nu
with respect to
\mu
is defined by

H(\nu\,|\,\mu)\coloneqq\begin{cases}\int_{\mathds{R}^{n}}\frac{\mathrm{d}\nu}{\mathrm{d}\mu}\log(\frac{\mathrm{d}\nu}{\mathrm{d}\mu})\,\mathrm{d}\mu,\,&\text{if }\nu\ll\mu\\
+\infty,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(1.14)

the
relative Fisher information
of
\nu
with respect to
\mu
is given by

I(\nu\,|\,\mu)\coloneqq\begin{cases}\int_{\mathds{R}^{n}}|\nabla\log(\frac{\mathrm{d}\nu}{\mathrm{d}\mu})|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu,\,&\text{if }\nu\ll\mu\\
+\infty,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(1.15)

and the
barycenter
of a probability measure
\nu\in\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathds{R}^{n})
is defined as
b(\nu)\coloneqq\int_{\mathds{R}^{n}}x\,\mathrm{d}\nu(x)\in\mathds{R}^{n}
, where the integral is understood as a Bochner integral. Informally, the HWBI inequality then states that any two probability measures
\nu_{0},\nu_{1}\in\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathds{R}^{n})
satisfy

H(\nu_{0}\,|\,\mu_{0})-H(\nu_{1}\,|\,\mu_{1})\leqslant\sqrt{I(\nu_{0}\,|\,\mu_{0}^{\uparrow})}\,W_{2}(\nu_{0},\nu_{1})-\tfrac{\kappa_{V}+\kappa_{W}}{2}\,W_{2}^{2}(\nu_{0},\nu_{1})+\tfrac{\kappa_{W}}{2}\,|b(\nu_{0})-b(\nu_{1})|^{2},

(1.16)

where
\mu_{0},\mu_{1},\mu_{0}^{\uparrow}
are some appropriate
\sigma
-finite reference measures depending on the potentials
V,W
(see
Subsection
3.3
for the details), and
\kappa_{V},\kappa_{W}\in\mathds{R}
are the moduli of uniform convexity for
V,W
. This inequality describes the evolution of the relative entropy along the
displacement interpolation

(\nu_{t})_{0\leqslant t\leqslant 1}
between
\nu_{0}
and
\nu_{1}
. Compared with the HWI inequality, there are two additional terms on the right-hand side of
(
1.16
)
contributed by the interaction energy functional
\mathcal{W}
of
(
1.3
)
. Intuitively, the
\kappa_{W}
-uniform convexity of
W
leads to the first additional term
-\tfrac{\kappa_{W}}{2}W_{2}^{2}(\nu_{0},\nu_{1})
, which alone would correspond to the
\kappa_{W}
-uniform displacement convexity of
\mathcal{W}
along
(\nu_{t})_{0\leqslant t\leqslant 1}
. But since
\mathcal{W}(p)
is invariant under any translation of
p
, the functional
\mathcal{W}
might fail to be uniformly displacement convex when the barycenter shifts. This suggests that the barycentric shift along
(\nu_{t})_{0\leqslant t\leqslant 1}
should be factored out of the consideration of the displacement convexity of
\mathcal{W}
, which is intuitively why the second additional term
\frac{\kappa_{W}}{2}|b(\nu_{0})-b(\nu_{1})|^{2}
in
(
1.16
)
appears.

Coming back to our second application, we illustrate how our approach yields a trajectorial proof of the inequality
(
1.16
)
, in the slightly strengthened form of
[
CEGH04
, Theorem 4.1]
and
[
FK06
, Theorem D.50]
. Much of this consists of arguments similar in spirit to our main result
(
1.10
)
, but with one key difference: instead of the time-marginals of the McKean–Vlasov diffusion, we apply the trajectorial approach to the displacement interpolation
(\nu_{t})_{0\leqslant t\leqslant 1}
. In this regard, our derivation can be seen as a generalization of the trajectorial proof of the HWI inequality in
[
KST20a
, Section 4.2]
; see also
[
KMS20
, Section 9.4]
, where the same idea was used to derive a discrete version of the HWI inequality in a Riemannian-geometric framework.

In the literature, similar trajectorial approaches have also been applied in the context of martingale inequalities
[
ABP+13
,
BS15
]
, functional inequalities
[
Cat04
,
Leh13
,
BCGL20
,
GLRT20
]
, and their stability estimates
[
ELS20
,
EM20
]
. In particular, we refer to
[
BCGL20
, Corollary 1.4]
for a related HWI inequality derived from the entropic interpolation of the mean-field Schrödinger problem.

2
The probabilistic framework

2.1
The setting

We fix a terminal time
T\in(0,\infty)
and let
\Omega\coloneqq C([0,T];\mathds{R}^{n})
be the path space of
\mathds{R}^{n}
-valued continuous functions defined on
[0,T]
. We denote by
(X_{t})_{0\leqslant t\leqslant T}
the canonical process defined by
X_{t}(\omega)\coloneqq\omega(t)
for
\omega\in\Omega
, and fix a probability distribution
\mathrm{P}_{\kern-0.81949pt0}\in\mathscr{P}_{\mathrm{ac},2}(\mathds{R}^{n})
.

As will be shown in
Lemma
2.2
, under the
Assumptions
2.1
below, the SDE
(
1.1
)
with initial distribution
\mathrm{P}_{\kern-0.81949pt0}
has a unique strong solution, when it is posed on an arbitrary filtered probability space. This implies that there exists a probability measure
\mathds{P}
on
\Omega
and a
\mathds{P}
-Brownian motion
(B_{t})_{0\leqslant t\leqslant T}
such that the SDE
(
1.1
)
holds. We write
\mathds{F}=(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{0\leqslant t\leqslant T}
for the right-continuous augmentation of the canonical filtration.

For each time
t\in[0,T]
, we denote by
\mathrm{P}_{\kern-0.81949ptt}\coloneqq\mathds{P}\circ X_{t}^{-1}
the distribution of
X_{t}
under
\mathds{P}
, and by
p_{t}
the corresponding probability density function on
\mathds{R}^{n}
. The density functions
(p_{t})_{0\leqslant t\leqslant T}
then solve the granular media equation
(
1.11
)
.

2.2
Regularity assumptions

Assumptions 2.1
.

The following regularity assumptions will be used frequently.

(i)

The functions
V,W\colon\mathds{R}^{n}\rightarrow[0,\infty)
are smooth and have Lipschitz continuous gradients with Lipschitz constants
\|\nabla V\|_{\textnormal{Lip}},\|\nabla W\|_{\textnormal{Lip}}
. All derivatives of
V
and
W
grow at most exponentially as
|x|
tends to infinity, and the first derivatives are of linear growth. The latter condition means that there exists a constant
C>0
such that

\forall\,x\in\mathds{R}^{n}\colon\qquad|\nabla V(x)|\leqslant C(1+|x|)\,,\qquad|\nabla W(x)|\leqslant C(1+|x|).

(2.1)

Furthermore, the function
W
is even (in other words, symmetric), i.e.,
W(x)=W(-x)
for all
x\in\mathds{R}^{n}
.

(ii)

The probability distribution
\mathrm{P}_{\kern-0.81949pt0}
is an element of the space
\mathscr{P}_{\kern-0.81949pt\mathrm{ac},2}(\mathds{R}^{n})
and the corresponding probability density function
\mathds{R}^{n}\ni x\mapsto p_{0}(x)
is strictly positive. Moreover, the initial free energy
\mathscr{F}(p_{0})
is finite.

These assumptions ensure that the equation
(
1.1
)
belongs to a broad class of strongly solvable McKean–Vlasov SDEs. We relegate the proof of the following result to
Subsection
5.1
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Suppose
Assumptions
2.1
hold. Then on an arbitrary filtered probability space, the McKean–Vlasov SDE
(
1.1
)
has a pathwise unique, strong solution
(X_{t})_{0\leqslant t\leqslant T}
satisfying

\mathds{E}\bigg[\sup_{0\leqslant t\leqslant T}|X_{t}|^{2}\bigg]<\infty.

(2.2)

Moreover, its marginal distributions
(\mathrm{P}_{\kern-0.81949ptt})_{0\leqslant t\leqslant T}
belong to
\mathscr{P}_{\kern-0.81949pt\mathrm{ac},2}(\mathds{R}^{n})
, and the corresponding curve of probability density functions
(p_{t})_{0\leqslant t\leqslant T}
is a classical solution of the granular media equation
(
1.11
)
.

2.3
Probabilistic representations of gradient flow functionals

To set up our framework, the first step is to express the free energy as well as the relative entropy dissipation functional in probabilistic terms. To this end, we introduce the generalized potential
\Psi\colon\mathds{R}^{n}\times\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathds{R}^{n})\to[0,\infty)
and its close relative
\Psi^{\uparrow}
given by

\Psi(x,\mu)\coloneqq V(x)+\tfrac{1}{2}(W\ast\mu)(x)\,,\qquad\Psi^{\uparrow}(x,\mu)\coloneqq V(x)+(W\ast\mu)(x)\,

(2.3)

for
(x,\mu)\in\mathds{R}^{n}\times\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathds{R}^{n})
. Furthermore, we define the density functions

q(x,\mu)\coloneqq\mathrm{e}^{-\Psi(x,\mu)}\ ,\qquad\qquad q^{\uparrow}(x,\mu)\coloneqq\mathrm{e}^{-\Psi^{\uparrow}(x,\mu)}\ ,\qquad\qquad q^{\downarrow}(x)\coloneqq\mathrm{e}^{-V(x)}

(2.4)

and the corresponding generalized likelihood ratio functions

\ell_{t}(x,\mu)\coloneqq\frac{p_{t}(x)}{q(x,\mu)}\ ,\qquad\qquad\ell_{t}^{\uparrow}(x,\mu)\coloneqq\frac{p_{t}(x)}{q^{\uparrow}(x,\mu)}\ ,\qquad\qquad\ell_{t}^{\downarrow}(x)\coloneqq\frac{p_{t}(x)}{q^{\downarrow}(x)}

(2.5)

for
t\in[0,T]
. Note that if
W\equiv 0
, these three likelihood ratio functions coincide.

For each time
t\in[0,T]
, we introduce
\sigma
-finite measures on the Borel sets of
\mathds{R}^{n}
, given by

\mathrm{Q}_{t}(A)\coloneqq\int_{A}q(x,\mathrm{P}_{\kern-0.81949ptt})\,\mathrm{d}x\,,\qquad\mathrm{Q}_{t}^{\uparrow}(A)\coloneqq\int_{A}q^{\uparrow}(x,\mathrm{P}_{\kern-0.81949ptt})\,\mathrm{d}x\,,\qquad A\in\mathscr{B}(\mathds{R}^{n}).

(2.6)

Intuitively, these measures are (unnormalized) time-dependent Gibbs distributions. If
W\equiv 0
, they coincide with the true Gibbs distribution of the Langevin–Smoluchowski equation
(
1.6
)
, which is also its stationary distribution (when normalized to a probability measure).

With these definitions, we can now write the gradient flow functionals
\mathscr{F}
and
\mathscr{D}
, introduced in
(
1.2
)
and
(
1.4
)
, in probabilistic terms: the relative entropy (defined in
(
1.14
)
) of
\mathrm{P}_{\kern-0.81949ptt}
with respect to
\mathrm{Q}_{t}
and the relative Fisher information (defined in
(
1.15
)
) of
\mathrm{P}_{\kern-0.81949ptt}
with respect to
\mathrm{Q}_{t}^{\uparrow}
can be expressed respectively as

H(\mathrm{P}_{\kern-0.81949ptt}\,|\,\mathrm{Q}_{t})=\mathds{E}_{\mathds{P}}\big[\log\ell_{t}(X_{t},\mathrm{P}_{\kern-0.81949ptt})\big]\,,\qquad I(\mathrm{P}_{\kern-0.81949ptt}\,|\,\mathrm{Q}_{t}^{\uparrow})=\mathds{E}_{\mathds{P}}\big[|\nabla\log\ell_{t}^{\uparrow}(X_{t},\mathrm{P}_{\kern-0.81949ptt})|^{2}\big]\,;

(2.7)

and we have the relations
H(\mathrm{P}_{\kern-0.81949ptt}\,|\,\mathrm{Q}_{t})=\mathscr{F}(\mathrm{P}_{\kern-0.81949ptt})
as well as
I(\mathrm{P}_{\kern-0.81949ptt}\,|\,\mathrm{Q}_{t}^{\uparrow})=\mathscr{D}(\mathrm{P}_{\kern-0.81949ptt})
. In particular, the relative entropy
H(\mathrm{P}_{\kern-0.81949ptt}\,|\,\mathrm{Q}_{t})
can be written as the
\mathds{P}
-expectation of the
relative entropy process

\log\ell_{t}(X_{t},\mathrm{P}_{\kern-0.81949ptt})=\log p_{t}(X_{t})+V(X_{t})+\tfrac{1}{2}(W\ast\mathrm{P}_{\kern-0.81949ptt})(X_{t})\,,\qquad 0\leqslant t\leqslant T.

(2.8)

The dynamics of this stochastic process, together with its perturbed counterpart to be introduced in
Subsection
3.2
below, will be our main objects of interest.

Remark 2.3
.

If the reference measure
\mathrm{Q}_{t}
in
(
2.6
)
is a
probability
measure, then the expression
(
2.7
)
matches the classical definition of relative entropy given in
(
1.14
)
. In the general case when
\mathrm{Q}_{t}
is a
\sigma
-finite measure, the definition
(
1.14
)
is also valid under the condition that
\mathrm{P}_{\kern-0.81949ptt}
has finite second moment, with the only difference that the range of the function
t\mapsto H(\mathrm{P}_{\kern-0.81949ptt}\,|\,\mathrm{Q}_{t})
is extended from
[0,\infty]
to
(-\infty,\infty]
; we refer to
[
KST20b
, Appendix C]
or
[
Léo14b
, Section 3]
for the details.

3
Main results

3.1
Trajectorial dissipation of relative entropy for McKean–Vlasov diffusions

Our first main result is the semimartingale decomposition of the relative entropy process
(
2.8
)
. It describes the dissipation of relative entropy along every trajectory of a particle undergoing the McKean–Vlasov dynamics
(
1.1
)
. In the same spirit as the trajectorial approaches of
[
FJ16
]
and
[
KST20a
]
, we shall study the dynamics of the relative entropy process in the backward direction of time. Concretely, we consider for arbitrary, fixed
T\in(0,\infty)
the time-reversed canonical process

\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{X}\overline{\phantom{X}}$}X_{s}\coloneqq X_{T-s}\,,\qquad 0\leqslant s\leqslant T

(3.1)

on the filtered probability space
(\Omega,\mathds{G},\mathds{P})
, where
\mathds{G}=(\mathcal{G}_{s})_{0\leqslant s\leqslant T}
is the
\mathds{P}
-augmented filtration generated by
(\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{X}\overline{\phantom{X}}$}X_{s})_{0\leqslant s\leqslant T}
.

In order to formulate
Theorem
3.1
below, we introduce the time-reversed
Fisher information process

\displaystyle\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{I}\overline{\phantom{I}}$}I_{s}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\Big(\big|\nabla\log\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{\ell}\overline{\phantom{\ell}}$}\ell_{s}^{\downarrow}\big|^{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}\big|\nabla(W\ast\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{\mathrm{P}}\overline{\phantom{\mathrm{P}}}$}\mathrm{P}_{\kern-0.81949pts})\big|^{2}+\Big\langle\tfrac{1}{2}\nabla(W\ast\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{\mathrm{P}}\overline{\phantom{\mathrm{P}}}$}\mathrm{P}_{\kern-0.81949pts})\,,\,2\nabla\log\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{\ell}\overline{\phantom{\ell}}$}\ell_{s}^{\downarrow}+\nabla V\Big\rangle\Big)(\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{X}\overline{\phantom{X}}$}X_{s})

(3.2)

\displaystyle-\mathds{E}_{\tilde{\mathds{P}}}\Big[\Big\langle\tfrac{1}{2}\nabla W(\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{X}\overline{\phantom{X}}$}X_{s}-\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{Y}\overline{\phantom{Y}}$}Y_{s})\,,\Big(2\nabla\log\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{\ell}\overline{\phantom{\ell}}$}\ell_{s}^{\downarrow}-\nabla V+\nabla(W\ast\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{\mathrm{P}}\overline{\phantom{\mathrm{P}}}$}\mathrm{P}_{\kern-0.81949pts})\Big)(\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{Y}\overline{\phantom{Y}}$}Y_{s})\Big\rangle\Big]

for
0\leqslant s\leqslant T
. Here, the process
(\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{Y}\overline{\phantom{Y}}$}Y_{s})_{0\leqslant s\leqslant T}
is defined on another probability space
(\tilde{\Omega},\tilde{\mathds{G}},\tilde{\mathds{P}})
such that the tuple
(\tilde{\Omega},\tilde{\mathds{G}},\tilde{\mathds{P}},(\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{Y}\overline{\phantom{Y}}$}Y_{s})_{0\leqslant s\leqslant T})
is an exact copy of
(\Omega,\mathds{G},\mathds{P},(\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{X}\overline{\phantom{X}}$}X_{s})_{0\leqslant s\leqslant T})
. A bar over a letter means that time is reversed as in
(
3.1
)
.

We also define the time-reversed
cumulative Fisher information process
as the time integral

\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{F}\overline{\phantom{F}}$}F_{s}\coloneqq\int_{0}^{s}\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{I}\overline{\phantom{I}}$}I_{u}\,\mathrm{d}u\,,\qquad 0\leqslant s\leqslant T.

(3.3)

This process will act as the compensator in the semimartingale decomposition of the relative entropy process
(
2.8
)
. Its relation with the relative Fisher information
(
2.7
)
will be given in
(
3.7
)
below.

Theorem 3.1
.

Suppose
Assumptions
2.1
hold. On
(\Omega,\mathds{G},\mathds{P})
, the time-reversed relative entropy process

\log\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{\ell}\overline{\phantom{\ell}}$}\ell_{s}(\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{X}\overline{\phantom{X}}$}X_{s},\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{\mathrm{P}}\overline{\phantom{\mathrm{P}}}$}\mathrm{P}_{\kern-0.81949pts})=\log\ell_{T-s}(X_{T-s},\mathrm{P}_{\kern-0.81949ptT-s})\,,\qquad 0\leqslant s\leqslant T

(3.4)

admits the semimartingale decomposition

\log\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{\ell}\overline{\phantom{\ell}}$}\ell_{s}(\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{X}\overline{\phantom{X}}$}X_{s},\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{\mathrm{P}}\overline{\phantom{\mathrm{P}}}$}\mathrm{P}_{\kern-0.81949pts})-\log\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{\ell}\overline{\phantom{\ell}}$}\ell_{0}(\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{X}\overline{\phantom{X}}$}X_{0},\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{\mathrm{P}}\overline{\phantom{\mathrm{P}}}$}\mathrm{P}_{\kern-0.81949pt0})=\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{M}\overline{\phantom{M}}$}M_{s}+\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{F}\overline{\phantom{F}}$}F_{s}.

(3.5)

Here
(\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{M}\overline{\phantom{M}}$}M_{s})_{0\leqslant s\leqslant T}
is the
L^{2}(\mathds{P})
-bounded martingale

\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{M}\overline{\phantom{M}}$}M_{s}\coloneqq\int_{0}^{s}\Big\langle\nabla\log\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{\ell}\overline{\phantom{\ell}}$}\ell_{u}(\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{X}\overline{\phantom{X}}$}X_{u},\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{\mathrm{P}}\overline{\phantom{\mathrm{P}}}$}\mathrm{P}_{\kern-0.81949ptu})\,,\,\sqrt{2}\,\mathrm{d}\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{B}\overline{\phantom{B}}$}B_{u}\Big\rangle,

(3.6)

with
(\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{B}\overline{\phantom{B}}$}B_{s})_{0\leqslant s\leqslant T}
a
\mathds{P}
-Brownian motion of the backward filtration
\mathds{G}
, and the compensator
(\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{F}\overline{\phantom{F}}$}F_{s})_{0\leqslant s\leqslant T}
satisfies

\mathds{E}_{\mathds{P}}\big[\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{F}\overline{\phantom{F}}$}F_{s}\big]=\int_{0}^{s}I\big(\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{\mathrm{P}}\overline{\phantom{\mathrm{P}}}$}\mathrm{P}_{\kern-0.81949ptu}\,\big|\,\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{\mathrm{Q}}\overline{\phantom{\mathrm{Q}}}$}\mathrm{Q}_{u}^{\uparrow}\big)\,\mathrm{d}u=\mathds{E}_{\mathds{P}}\bigg[\int_{0}^{s}\big|\nabla\log\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{\ell}\overline{\phantom{\ell}}$}\ell_{u}^{\uparrow}(\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{X}\overline{\phantom{X}}$}X_{u},\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{\mathrm{P}}\overline{\phantom{\mathrm{P}}}$}\mathrm{P}_{\kern-0.81949ptu})\big|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}u\bigg]<\infty.

(3.7)

3.1.1
Examples

We give two concrete examples to illustrate
Theorem
3.1
.

Example 3.2
.

We set
n=1
and specialize
Theorem
3.1
to the case of quadratic confinement potential
V(x)=\frac{x^{2}}{2}
and no interaction potential
W\equiv 0
. The initial position
X_{0}
is chosen to be independent of
(B_{t})_{0\leqslant t\leqslant T}
and to be normally distributed with mean
0
and variance
\sigma_{0}^{2}>0
. In this case, the SDE of
(
1.1
)
becomes

\mathrm{d}X_{t}=-X_{t}\,\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2}\,\mathrm{d}B_{t}\,,\qquad 0\leqslant t\leqslant T

(3.8)

and its solution is given by the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process

X_{t}=\mathrm{e}^{-t}X_{0}+\sqrt{2}\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{e}^{u-t}\,\mathrm{d}B_{u}\,,\qquad 0\leqslant t\leqslant T

(3.9)

with probability density function

p_{t}(x)=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma_{t}^{2}}}\exp\big(-\tfrac{x^{2}}{2\sigma_{t}^{2}}\big)\,,\qquad\sigma_{t}^{2}\coloneqq 1+\mathrm{e}^{-2t}(\sigma_{0}^{2}-1).

(3.10)

Recalling
(
2.3
)
–
(
2.5
)
and using
(
3.10
)
, we see that in this setting the cumulative Fisher information process of
(
3.3
)
is explicitly given by

\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{F}\overline{\phantom{F}}$}F_{s}^{\mathrm{OU}}=\int_{0}^{s}\Big(\nabla\log\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{\ell}\overline{\phantom{\ell}}$}\ell_{u}^{\downarrow}(\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{X}\overline{\phantom{X}}$}X_{u})\Big)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}u=\int_{0}^{s}\Big(\nabla\log\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{p}\overline{\phantom{p}}$}p_{u}(\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{X}\overline{\phantom{X}}$}X_{u})+\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{X}\overline{\phantom{X}}$}X_{u}\Big)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}u=\int_{0}^{s}\big(1-\tfrac{1}{\overline{\sigma}_{u}^{2}}\big)^{2}\,\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{X}\overline{\phantom{X}}$}X_{u}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}u

(3.11)

for
0\leqslant s\leqslant T
. In particular, the non-negativity of the integrand in
(
3.11
)
implies that the relative entropy decreases along almost every trajectory.

Now we set
T=1
and
\sigma_{0}^{2}=0.1
. The blue lines in
Figure
1
below are ten simulated trajectories
s\mapsto\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{F}\overline{\phantom{F}}$}F_{s}^{\mathrm{OU}}(\omega_{i})
, for
i=1,\ldots,10
. The thick black line plots the expected path
s\mapsto\mathds{E}_{\mathds{P}}[\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{F}\overline{\phantom{F}}$}F_{s}^{\mathrm{OU}}]
of all possible trajectories.

Simulations of the cumulative Fisher information process
(
3.11
)

for the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck diffusion
(
3.8
)
.

Example 3.3
.

We set again
n=1
and now consider the case of no confinement potential
V\equiv 0
, quadratic interaction potential
W(x)=\frac{x^{2}}{2}
, and a centered Gaussian initial position
X_{0}
with variance
\sigma_{0}^{2}>0
, which is independent of the Brownian motion
(B_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
. In this case, for any
t\geqslant 0
, the drift term of the SDE of
(
1.1
)
is

-\nabla(W\ast\mathrm{P}_{\kern-0.81949ptt})(X_{t})=-\int_{\mathds{R}^{n}}\nabla W(X_{t}-y)p_{t}(y)\,\mathrm{d}y=-\big(X_{t}-\mathds{E}[X_{t}]\big).

(3.12)

In particular, the drift term depends on the distribution
\mathrm{P}_{\kern-0.81949ptt}
only through its mean. Substituting it into
(
1.1
)
,
this SDE reduces to

\mathrm{d}X_{t}=-\big(X_{t}-\mathds{E}[X_{t}]\big)\,\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2}\,\mathrm{d}B_{t}\,,\qquad 0\leqslant t\leqslant T.

(3.13)

This type of nonlinear, self-interacting SDE has been studied since
[
BRTV98
]
, where it was shown that its solution is also given by the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process of
(
3.9
)
. Therefore, similar computations as in
Example
3.2
show that in this setting the cumulative Fisher information process is given by

\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{F}\overline{\phantom{F}}$}F_{s}^{\mathrm{NL}}=\int_{0}^{s}\Big(\big(\tfrac{1}{\overline{\sigma}_{u}^{4}}+\tfrac{1}{2}-\tfrac{1}{\overline{\sigma}_{u}^{2}}\big)\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{X}\overline{\phantom{X}}$}X_{u}^{2}+\mathds{E}_{\tilde{\mathds{P}}}\Big[\tfrac{1}{2}(\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{X}\overline{\phantom{X}}$}X_{u}-\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{Y}\overline{\phantom{Y}}$}Y_{u})\big(\tfrac{2}{\overline{\sigma}_{u}^{2}}\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{Y}\overline{\phantom{Y}}$}Y_{u}-\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{Y}\overline{\phantom{Y}}$}Y_{u}\big)\Big]\Big)\,\mathrm{d}u

(3.14)

for
0\leqslant s\leqslant T
. Using the fact that
(\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{X}\overline{\phantom{X}}$}X_{u})_{\#}(\tilde{\mathds{P}})=(\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{Y}\overline{\phantom{Y}}$}Y_{u})_{\#}(\tilde{\mathds{P}})=\mathcal{N}(0,\overline{\sigma}_{u}^{2})
, which we have from
(
3.10
)
, we can compute the expectation appearing in
(
3.14
)
and obtain

\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{F}\overline{\phantom{F}}$}F_{s}^{\mathrm{NL}}=\int_{0}^{s}\Big(\big(\tfrac{1}{\overline{\sigma}_{u}^{4}}+\tfrac{1}{2}-\tfrac{1}{\overline{\sigma}_{u}^{2}}\big)\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{X}\overline{\phantom{X}}$}X_{u}^{2}+\tfrac{\overline{\sigma}_{u}^{2}}{2}-1\Big)\,\mathrm{d}u.

(3.15)

Clearly,
\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{F}\overline{\phantom{F}}$}F_{s}^{\mathrm{OU}}\neq\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{F}\overline{\phantom{F}}$}F_{s}^{\mathrm{NL}}
. In particular, the integrand in
(
3.15
)
is non-negative if and only if

\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{X}\overline{\phantom{X}}$}X_{u}^{2}\geqslant\big(\tfrac{1}{\overline{\sigma}_{u}^{4}}+\tfrac{1}{2}-\tfrac{1}{\overline{\sigma}_{u}^{2}}\big)^{-1}\big(1-\tfrac{\overline{\sigma}_{u}^{2}}{2}\big).

In other words, as opposed to
Example
3.2
, relative entropy only decreases along a trajectory if
\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{X}\overline{\phantom{X}}$}X_{u}
is far from its mean. However, after taking expectations in
(
3.11
)
and
(
3.15
)
, we see that the expected rate of relative entropy dissipation in both cases is equal to

\mathds{E}_{\mathds{P}}\big[\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{F}\overline{\phantom{F}}$}F_{s}^{\mathrm{OU}}\big]=\mathds{E}_{\mathds{P}}\big[\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{F}\overline{\phantom{F}}$}F_{s}^{\mathrm{NL}}\big]=\int_{0}^{s}\big(\overline{\sigma}_{u}-\tfrac{1}{\overline{\sigma}_{u}}\big)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}u\,,\qquad 0\leqslant s\leqslant T.

(3.16)

Now we set again
T=1
and
\sigma_{0}^{2}=0.1
. In the same vein as in
Figure
1
, we plot in
Figure
2
below the paths of ten simulated trajectories
s\mapsto\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{F}\overline{\phantom{F}}$}F_{s}^{\mathrm{NL}}(\omega_{i})
, for
i=1,\ldots,10
. We observe that some of the red lines describing the paths of these trajectories indeed take negative values. In other words, the cumulative Fisher information process of
(
3.15
)
, and hence its integrand, can both be negative. Finally, the thick black line in
Figure
2
follows the expected path
s\mapsto\mathds{E}_{\mathds{P}}[\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{F}\overline{\phantom{F}}$}F_{s}^{\mathrm{NL}}]
of all possible trajectories. According to
(
3.16
)
, this is the same black line as in
Figure
1
.

for the nonlinear, self-interacting diffusion

)
.

3.1.2
Consequences of
Theorem
3.1

We now return to the general statement of
Theorem
3.1
and deduce some direct consequences. By averaging the trajectorial result of
Theorem
3.1
according to the path measure
\mathds{P}
, we derive the well-known
relative entropy identity

(
3.17
)
and the
dissipation of relative entropy

(
3.18
)
below. A sketch of proof for the latter result was first given in
[
CMV03
, Proposition 2.1]
.

Corollary 3.4
.

Suppose
Assumptions
2.1
hold. For all
t,t_{0}\in[0,T]
, we have the relative entropy identity

H(\mathrm{P}_{\kern-0.81949ptt}\,|\,\mathrm{Q}_{t})-H(\mathrm{P}_{\kern-0.81949ptt_{0}}\,|\,\mathrm{Q}_{t_{0}})=-\int_{t_{0}}^{t}I(\mathrm{P}_{\kern-0.81949ptu}\,|\,\mathrm{Q}_{u}^{\uparrow})\,\textnormal{d}u.

(3.17)

In particular, the relative entropy function
t\mapsto H(\mathrm{P}_{\kern-0.81949ptt}\,|\,\mathrm{Q}_{t})
is monotonically decreasing. Furthermore, for Lebesgue-a.e.
t\in[0,T]
, the relative Fisher information
I(\mathrm{P}_{\kern-0.81949ptt}\,|\,\mathrm{Q}_{t}^{\uparrow})
is finite, and the rate of relative entropy dissipation is given by

\tfrac{\textnormal{d}}{\textnormal{d}t}\,H(\mathrm{P}_{\kern-0.81949ptt}\,|\,\mathrm{Q}_{t})=-I(\mathrm{P}_{\kern-0.81949ptt}\,|\,\mathrm{Q}_{t}^{\uparrow}).

(3.18)

Proof.

The identity
(
3.17
)
follows by taking expectations with respect to the probability measure
\mathds{P}
in
(
3.5
)
, recalling
(
2.7
)
, using
(
3.7
)
, and invoking the fact that the
\mathds{P}
-expectation of the martingale
(
3.6
)
vanishes. Finally, applying the Lebesgue differentiation theorem to the monotone function
t\mapsto H(\mathrm{P}_{\kern-0.81949ptt}\,|\,\mathrm{Q}_{t})
gives
(
3.18
)
.
∎

Remark 3.5
.

The relation
(
3.18
)
describes the temporal dissipation of relative entropy at the
ensemble
level. It asserts that the rate of decay of the relative entropy
t\mapsto H(\mathrm{P}_{\kern-0.81949ptt}\,|\,\mathrm{Q}_{t})
is given by the relative Fisher information
I(\mathrm{P}_{\kern-0.81949ptt}\,|\,\mathrm{Q}_{t}^{\uparrow})
.

Finally, let us place ourselves again on the filtered probability space
(\Omega,\mathds{G},\mathds{P})
as in
Theorem
3.1
and emphasize that this trajectorial result is valid along almost every trajectory
s\mapsto\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{X}\overline{\phantom{X}}$}X_{s}(\omega)
of the underlying McKean–Vlasov process. As a consequence, by taking conditional expectations, we can generalize
(
3.18
)
and deduce the following trajectorial rate of relative entropy dissipation.

Corollary 3.6
.

Suppose
Assumptions
2.1
hold and
\int_{0}^{T}\mathds{E}_{\mathds{P}}[|\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{I}\overline{\phantom{I}}$}I_{u}|]\,\mathrm{d}u<\infty
. For
\mathds{P}
-a.e.
\omega\in\Omega
there exists a Lebesgue null set
N_{\omega}\subseteq[0,T]
such that for any
s_{0}\in[0,T]\setminus N_{\omega}
we have

\lim_{s\downarrow s_{0}}\frac{\mathds{E}_{\mathds{P}}\big[\log\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{\ell}\overline{\phantom{\ell}}$}\ell_{s}(\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{X}\overline{\phantom{X}}$}X_{s},\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{\mathrm{P}}\overline{\phantom{\mathrm{P}}}$}\mathrm{P}_{\kern-0.81949pts})\,|\,\mathcal{G}_{s_{0}}\big](\omega)-\log\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{\ell}\overline{\phantom{\ell}}$}\ell_{s_{0}}\big(\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{X}\overline{\phantom{X}}$}X_{s_{0}}(\omega),\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{\mathrm{P}}\overline{\phantom{\mathrm{P}}}$}\mathrm{P}_{\kern-0.81949pts_{0}}\big)}{s-s_{0}}=\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{I}\overline{\phantom{I}}$}I_{s_{0}}(\omega).

(3.19)

Remark 3.7
.

Recalling
(
3.7
)
, we observe that
\mathds{E}_{\mathds{P}}[\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{I}\overline{\phantom{I}}$}I_{s_{0}}]=I(\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{\mathrm{P}}\overline{\phantom{\mathrm{P}}}$}\mathrm{P}_{\kern-0.81949pts_{0}}\,|\,\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{\mathrm{Q}}\overline{\phantom{\mathrm{Q}}}$}\mathrm{Q}_{s_{0}}^{\uparrow})
. Therefore the limiting assertion
(
3.19
)
can indeed be viewed as a trajectorial version of the deterministic relative entropy dissipation identity
(
3.18
)
.

Proof.

We let
0\leqslant s_{0}\leqslant s\leqslant T
. By
(
3.5
)
,
(
3.3
)
and Fubini’s theorem, we have for
\mathds{P}
-a.e.
\omega\in\Omega

\mathds{E}_{\mathds{P}}\big[\log\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{\ell}\overline{\phantom{\ell}}$}\ell_{s}(\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{X}\overline{\phantom{X}}$}X_{s},\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{\mathrm{P}}\overline{\phantom{\mathrm{P}}}$}\mathrm{P}_{\kern-0.81949pts})\,|\,\mathcal{G}_{s_{0}}\big](\omega)-\log\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{\ell}\overline{\phantom{\ell}}$}\ell_{s_{0}}\big(\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{X}\overline{\phantom{X}}$}X_{s_{0}}(\omega),\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{\mathrm{P}}\overline{\phantom{\mathrm{P}}}$}\mathrm{P}_{\kern-0.81949pts_{0}}\big)=\int_{s_{0}}^{s}\mathds{E}_{\mathds{P}}\big[\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{I}\overline{\phantom{I}}$}I_{u}\,|\,\mathcal{G}_{s_{0}}\big](\omega)\,\mathrm{d}u.

(3.20)

Furthermore, Jensen’s inequality gives

\mathds{E}_{\mathds{P}}\bigg[\int_{s_{0}}^{s}\big|\mathds{E}_{\mathds{P}}\big[\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{I}\overline{\phantom{I}}$}I_{u}\,|\,\mathcal{G}_{s_{0}}\big]\big|\,\mathrm{d}u\bigg]\leqslant\int_{s_{0}}^{s}\mathds{E}_{\mathds{P}}\big[|\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{I}\overline{\phantom{I}}$}I_{u}|\big]\,\mathrm{d}u<\infty,

(3.21)

which implies

\int_{s_{0}}^{s}\big|\mathds{E}_{\mathds{P}}\big[\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{I}\overline{\phantom{I}}$}I_{u}\,|\,\mathcal{G}_{s_{0}}\big](\omega)\big|\,\mathrm{d}u<\infty\qquad\textnormal{ for }\mathds{P}\textnormal{-a.e. }\omega\in\Omega.

(3.22)

By the Lebesgue differentiation theorem, for every such
\omega
there exists a Lebesgue null set
N_{\omega}\subseteq[0,T]
so that the limiting assertion

\lim_{s\downarrow s_{0}}\frac{\int_{s_{0}}^{s}\mathds{E}_{\mathds{P}}\big[\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{I}\overline{\phantom{I}}$}I_{u}\,|\,\mathcal{G}_{s_{0}}\big](\omega)\,\mathrm{d}u}{s-s_{0}}=\mathds{E}_{\mathds{P}}\big[\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{I}\overline{\phantom{I}}$}I_{s_{0}}\,|\,\mathcal{G}_{s_{0}}\big](\omega)=\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{I}\overline{\phantom{I}}$}I_{s_{0}}(\omega)

(3.23)

holds for every
s_{0}\in[0,T]\setminus N_{\omega}
. Finally, combining
(
3.20
)
and
(
3.23
)
proves
(
3.19
)
.
∎

3.2
Gradient flow structure of the granular media equation

In this subsection we apply the trajectorial approach of
Subsection
3.1
in order to formulate the gradient flow property of the granular media equation
(

3.3
A trajectorial proof of the HWBI inequality

In this subsection, we show how our trajectorial approach can be adapted to give a simple proof of the HWBI inequality. While the techniques that will be used are similar, the setting of this section is independent from the rest of the paper.

We fix two probability measures
\nu_{0}
and
\nu_{1}
in
\mathscr{P}_{\mathrm{ac},2}(\mathds{R}^{n})
. By Brenier’s theorem
[
Bre91
]
, there exists a convex function
\varphi\colon\mathds{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathds{R}
such that

W^{2}_{2}(\nu_{0},\nu_{1})=\int_{\mathds{R}^{n}}|x-\nabla\varphi(x)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{0}(x).

(3.58)

The displacement interpolation of McCann
[
McC97
]
between
\nu_{0}
and
\nu_{1}
is given by

\nu_{t}\coloneqq(T_{t})_{\#}\nu_{0}\,,\qquad T_{t}(x)\coloneqq(1-t)x+t\nabla\varphi(x)\,,\qquad 0\leqslant t\leqslant 1.

(3.59)

In particular, since the endpoints
\nu_{0}
and
\nu_{1}
belong to
\mathscr{P}_{\mathrm{ac},2}(\mathds{R}^{n})
, each
\nu_{t}
has a probability density function
\rho_{t}
; see, e.g.,
[
Vil03
, Remarks 5.13 (i)]
.

As before, we consider a confinement potential
V
and an interaction potential
W
. For each
t\in[0,1]
, we then define by analogy with
(
2.6
)
, the
\sigma
-finite measures

\mu_{t}(A)\coloneqq\int_{A}q(x,\nu_{t})\,\mathrm{d}x\,,\qquad\mu_{t}^{\uparrow}(A)\coloneqq\int_{A}q^{\uparrow}(x,\nu_{t})\,\mathrm{d}x\,,\qquad A\in\mathscr{B}(\mathds{R}^{n}),

(3.60)

where we recall the definitions of the density functions
q
and
q^{\uparrow}
in
(
2.4
)
. In parallel to the likelihood ratio functions in
(
2.5
)
, we define

r_{t}(x,\nu)\coloneqq\frac{\rho_{t}(x)}{q(x,\nu)}\,,\qquad r_{t}^{\uparrow}(x,\nu)\coloneqq\frac{\rho_{t}(x)}{q^{\uparrow}(x,\nu)}\,,\qquad(t,x,\nu)\in[0,1]\times\mathds{R}^{n}\times\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathds{R}^{n}).

(3.61)

Then the relative entropy of
\nu_{t}
with respect to
\mu_{t}
is given by

H(\nu_{t}\,|\,\mu_{t})=\int_{\mathds{R}^{n}}\rho_{t}(x)\log r_{t}(x,\nu_{t})\,\mathrm{d}x

(3.62)

and the relative Fisher information of
\nu_{t}
with respect to
\mu_{t}^{\uparrow}
is equal to

I\big(\nu_{t}\,|\,\mu_{t}^{\uparrow}\big)=\int_{\mathds{R}^{n}}|\nabla\log r_{t}^{\uparrow}(x,\nu_{t})|^{2}\,\rho_{t}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x.

(3.63)

We impose the following regularity conditions for
Proposition
3.17
, noting that the strong assumptions placed on
\rho_{0}
and
\rho_{1}
are only temporary and will be removed in
Assumptions
3.18
of
Theorem
3.19
.

Assumptions 3.16
.

The functions
V,W\colon\mathds{R}^{n}\rightarrow[0,\infty)
are smooth and
W
is symmetric. The probability density functions
\rho_{0}
and
\rho_{1}
are smooth, compactly supported and strictly positive in the interior of their respective supports.

Proposition 3.17
.

Suppose
Assumptions
3.16
hold. Along the displacement interpolation
(\nu_{t})_{0\leqslant t\leqslant 1}
, the rate of relative entropy dissipation at time
t=0
, with respect to the “reference curve of probability measures”
(\mu_{t})_{0\leqslant t\leqslant 1}
, is given by

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\Big|_{t=0}^{+}\,H(\nu_{t}\,|\,\mu_{t})=\int_{\mathds{R}^{n}}\Big\langle\nabla\log r_{0}^{\uparrow}(x,\nu_{0})\,,\nabla\varphi(x)-x\Big\rangle\,\rho_{0}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x.

(3.64)

Combining
Proposition
3.17
with the displacement convexity results of McCann
[
McC97
]
, we obtain the following generalization of the HWBI inequality. Equivalent versions of this inequality can be found in
[
CEGH04
, Theorem 4.1]
and
[
FK06
, Theorem D.50]
.

Assumptions 3.18
.

The functions
V,W\colon\mathds{R}^{n}\rightarrow[0,\infty)
are smooth and
W
is symmetric. Furthermore,
V
and
W
are uniformly convex, i.e., there exist real constants
\kappa_{V}
and
\kappa_{W}
such that

\mathrm{Hess}(V)\geqslant\kappa_{V}I_{n}\,,\qquad\mathrm{Hess}(W)\geqslant\kappa_{W}I_{n}.

(3.65)

Theorem 3.19
.

Suppose
Assumptions
3.18
hold and the relative entropy
H(\nu_{1}\,|\,\mu_{1})
is finite. Then

\displaystyle H(\nu_{0}\,|\,\mu_{0})-H(\nu_{1}\,|\,\mu_{1})\leqslant

\displaystyle-\int_{\mathds{R}^{n}}\Big\langle\nabla\log r_{0}^{\uparrow}(x,\nu_{0})\,,\nabla\varphi(x)-x\Big\rangle\,\rho_{0}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x

(3.66)

\displaystyle-\tfrac{\kappa_{V}+\kappa_{W}}{2}\,W_{2}^{2}(\nu_{0},\nu_{1})+\tfrac{\kappa_{W}}{2}\,|b(\nu_{0})-b(\nu_{1})|^{2}.

(3.67)

Remark 3.20
.

By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, the right-hand side of
(

can be bounded from above by

\sqrt{\int_{\mathds{R}^{n}}\big|\nabla\log r_{0}^{\uparrow}(x,\nu_{0})\big|^{2}\,\rho_{0}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x}\ \sqrt{\int_{\mathds{R}^{n}}|\nabla\varphi(x)-x|^{2}\,\rho_{0}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x}=\sqrt{I(\nu_{0}\,|\,\mu_{0}^{\uparrow})}\ W_{2}(\nu_{0},\nu_{1}),

(3.68)

and we obtain the usual form of the HWBI inequality
(

)
; see also
[

, Theorem 4.2]
