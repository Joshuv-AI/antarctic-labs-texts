Which differential equations correspond to the Lindblad equation?
Victor Kasatkin, Larry Gu, Daniel A. Lidar

Abstract
The Lindblad master equation can always be transformed into a first-order linear ordinary differential equation (1ODE) for the coherence vector. We pose the inverse problem: given a finite-dimensional, non-homogeneous 1ODE, does a corresponding Lindblad equation exist? If so, what are the corresponding Hamiltonian and Lindblad operators? We provide a general solution to this problem, including a complete positivity test in terms of the parameters of the 1ODE. We also derive a host of properties relating the two representations (master equation and 1ODE) of independent interest.

Introduction

The Gorini-Kossakowski-Lindblad-Sudarshan (GKLS) master equation
Gorini et al. 1976
;

\displaystyle=-i[H,\cdot]

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{a}[\cdot]

II
Main result

Consider a finite-dimensional Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
, with

, and the Banach space (a complete, normed vector space)
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
of operators acting on

\langle A,B\rangle\equiv\Tr(A^{\dagger}B)

Definition 1
.

A “nice operator basis” for
\mathcal{H}
is a set
\{F_{j}\}_{j=0}^{J}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
,

F_{0}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}I

\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})

\langle F_{j},F_{k}\rangle=\Tr(F_{j}F_{k})=\delta_{jk}\ ,\quad 0\leq j\leq J\ .

(4)

Definition 2
.

Any equation of the form

Eq.

1
, where
H=H^{\dagger}
is a Hermitian operator in
B(\mathcal{H})
and

a=a^{\dagger}
is a Hermitian
J\times J
matrix, is a “Markovian quantum master equation”. In this case, the superoperator
\mathcal{L}
in
Eq.

1
is called a “Liouvillian”.

When, in addition,
a\geq 0
,
Eq.

1
is a
“Lindblad master equation”, “Lindblad equation”,
or “completely positive Markovian quantum master equation”.
In this case, the superoperator
\mathcal{L}
is called a “Lindbladian” or “completely positive Liouvillian”.

The superoperator
\mathcal{L}_{a}
is called the

.

Whenever we refer to
Eq.

1
or the superoperator
\mathcal{L}
without
explicitly specifying complete positivity, we assume the former case, i.e.,
we only assume the conditions

,

.

Our main result is the following:

Theorem 1
.

For any
J\times J
matrix
G
and any vector
\vec{c}
of length
J
with real
coefficients there is a pair
(H,a)
describing a Markovian quantum master equation

Eq.

1
which transforms to
Eq.

3
with these
G
and
\vec{c}
.
If, in addition to the above,
we require

to be traceless, then such a pair

is unique and
can be computed as follows:

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2id}\sum_{m,n=1}^{J}G_{nm}\left[F_{m},F_{n}\right],

(5a)

\displaystyle a_{mn}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{J}\Tr\bigg[\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{J}G_{ij}F_{j}+c_{i}I\bigg)F_{m}F_{i}F_{n}\bigg].

(5b)

Moreover, the matrix

is positive semidefinite
[i.e.,

if and only if (iff) for all traceless

\sum_{i=1}^{J}\Tr\bigg[\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{J}G_{ij}F_{j}+c_{i}I\bigg)B^{\dagger}F_{i}B\bigg]\geq 0\ .

(6)

Condition (

Note that adding a constant, i.e., a term of the form

.
Therefore, if the requirement that

can
only be recovered from

III
Background

Let
M(d,F)
denote the vector space of
d\times d
matrices with coefficients
in
F
, where
F\in\{\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}\}
.
For our purposes it suffices to identify
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
with
M(d,\mathbb{C})
. Quantum states are represented by density operators
\rho\in\mathcal{B}_{+}(\mathcal{H})
(positive trace-class operators acting on
\mathcal{H}
) with unit trace:
\Tr\rho=1
.
Elements of
\mathcal{B}[\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})]
, i.e., linear transformations
\mathcal{E}:\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})\to\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
, are called superoperators, or maps.

Complete positivity of a superoperator
\mathcal{E}
is equivalent to the statement that
\mathcal{E}
has a Kraus representation
Kraus 1983
:
\forall X\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
,

\mathcal{E}(X)=\sum_{i}K_{i}XK_{i}^{\dagger}\ ,

(7)

where the
\{K_{i}\}
are called Kraus operators. When they satisfy
\sum_{i}K_{i}^{\dagger}K_{i}=I
, the map
\mathcal{E}
is trace preserving.

A quantum dynamical semigroup is defined as a one-parameter family of strongly continuous CP maps, satisfying
\Lambda_{0}=I
(the identity operator), and the Markov property
\Lambda_{s}\Lambda_{t}=\Lambda_{s+t}
for all
s,t\geq 0
.
3
3

3

Strong continuity means that
\forall X\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
,
\|\Lambda_{t}X-X\|\to 0
as
t\searrow 0
, i.e., the map
\Lambda_{t}
is continuous in the strong operator topology.

We can more formally state the GKLS theorem(s)
Gorini et al. 1976
;
Lindblad 1976

as follows: a superoperator
\mathcal{L}:\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})\to\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
is the generator of a quantum dynamical semigroup iff it is of the form given in
Eq.

1
, with
a\geq 0
. I.e., when
\mathcal{L}
is in the form of
Eq.

1
, the solution of
\dot{\rho}=\mathcal{L}\rho
is
\rho(t)=\Lambda_{t}\rho(0)
, where
\Lambda_{t}=e^{\mathcal{L}t}
is an element of a quantum dynamical semigroup.

III.1
General superoperators over a nice operator basis

Given a nice operator basis, let us “coordinatize” the operator
X\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
in the nice operator basis as

X=\sum_{j=0}^{J}\boldsymbol{X}_{j}F_{j},

(8)

where we use the notation
\boldsymbol{X}=\{\boldsymbol{X}_{j}\}
for the vector of coordinates of the operator
X
(we interchangeably use the
\vec{X}
and
\boldsymbol{X}
notations to denote a vector). I.e., for any
X\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
:

\boldsymbol{X}_{i}=\langle F_{i},X\rangle=\Tr(F_{i}X)\ .

(9)

In particular, applying
Eqs.

8
and
9
to
|{k}\rangle\!\langle l|
we obtain

|{k}\rangle\!\langle l|=\sum_{i=0}^{J}(F_{i})_{lk}F_{i},

(10)

or

\sum_{i=0}^{J}F_{i\,lk}F_{i\,k^{\prime}l^{\prime}}=\delta_{ll^{\prime}}\delta_{kk^{\prime}}.

(11)

Proposition 1
.

The operator
X\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
is Hermitian iff
\boldsymbol{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{d^{2}}
in a nice operator basis.

Proof.

If
X
is Hermitian, then
\boldsymbol{X}_{i}^{*}=\langle F_{i},X\rangle^{*}=\langle X,F_{i}\rangle=\Tr(XF_{i})=\Tr(F_{i}X)=\boldsymbol{X}_{i}
. Vice-versa, if all the coefficients
\boldsymbol{X}_{i}
are real, then the operator
X=\sum_{j=0}^{J}\boldsymbol{X}_{j}F_{j}
is Hermitian because it is a sum of Hermitian operators
F_{j}
with real coefficients.
∎

More generally, for Hermitian matrices, the inner product becomes
\langle A,B\rangle=\Tr(AB)
. Such matrices have
d^{2}=J+1
real parameters [
d
real diagonal elements plus
(d^{2}-d)/2
independent complex off-diagonal elements], so they can be represented in terms of vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{d^{2}}
. Moreover, if
A
and
B
are both Hermitian operators then:

\langle A,B\rangle=\sum_{{j,k=0}}^{J}\boldsymbol{A}_{j}\boldsymbol{B}_{k}\Tr(F_{j}F_{k})=\sum_{{j,k=0}}^{J}\boldsymbol{A}_{j}\boldsymbol{B}_{k}\delta_{jk}=\boldsymbol{A}^{t}\boldsymbol{B}\ ,

(12)

so that in these coordinates, the inner product
\langle A,B\rangle

corresponds to the standard inner product in
\mathbb{R}^{d^{2}}
.

The matrix elements of a superoperator
\mathcal{E}:\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})\to\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
are given by

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{ij}=\langle F_{i},\mathcal{E}(F_{j})\rangle=\Tr[F_{i}\mathcal{E}(F_{j})]\ .

(13)

The action of a general (not necessarily CP) superoperator
\mathcal{E}\in\mathcal{B}[\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})]
can always be represented for any
A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
as

\mathcal{E}(A)=\sum_{{i,j=0}}^{J}c_{ij}F_{i}AF_{j},

(14)

where
c_{ij}\in\mathbb{C}
(see
Appendix

D
). Thus the matrix
c=\{c_{ij}\}
specifies
\mathcal{E}
in the given basis
\{F_{i}\}
. CP maps are a special case of Eq. (
14
), where
c
is positive semidefinite.

For superoperators we denote the Hilbert-Schmidt adjoint of
\mathcal{E}
by
\mathcal{E}^{\dagger}
, which is defined via

\langle\mathcal{E}^{\dagger}(A),B\rangle\equiv\langle A,\mathcal{E}(B)\rangle\quad\forall A,B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}).

(15)

Definition 3
.

\mathcal{E}
is
Hermiticity-preserving
iff:

\mathcal{E}^{\dagger}\left(A^{\dagger}\right)=\mathcal{E}(A)\ \quad\forall A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}).

(16)

\mathcal{E}
is
Hermitian
iff

\mathcal{E}^{\dagger}\left(A\right)=\mathcal{E}(A)\ \quad\forall A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}).

(17)

Let us find explicit formulas for
[\mathcal{E}(A^{\dagger})]^{\dagger}
and
\mathcal{E}^{\dagger}(A)
in terms
of the general representation of a superoperator given by
Eq.

14
.
First, from Eq. (
14

[\mathcal{E}(A^{\dagger})]^{\dagger}=\left(\sum_{ij}c_{ij}F_{i}A^{\dagger}F_{j}\right)^{\dagger}=\sum_{ij}c^{*}_{ij}F_{j}AF_{i}.

(18)

Second, substituting
Eq.

14
into
the complex conjugate of
Eq.

15
yields:

\displaystyle\left<B,\mathcal{E}^{\dagger}(A)\right>=\left<\mathcal{E}(B),A\right>=\Tr\left(\sum_{ij}(c_{ij}F_{i}BF_{j})^{\dagger}A\right)

(19a)

\displaystyle=\Tr\left(\sum_{ij}c_{ij}^{*}F_{j}B^{\dagger}F_{i}A\right)=\Tr\left(B^{\dagger}\sum_{ij}c_{ij}^{*}F_{i}AF_{j}\right)

(19b)

\displaystyle=\langle B,\sum_{ij}c_{ij}^{*}F_{i}AF_{j}\rangle.

(19c)

Since this equality holds for every
B
, we have:

\mathcal{E}^{\dagger}(A)=\sum_{ij}c_{ij}^{*}F_{i}AF_{j}.

(20)

It will turn out to be useful to have another representation of
\mathcal{E}^{\dagger}(A)
. Consider two sets of arbitrary operators
\{L_{p}\}
and
\{M_{q}\}
, which we can expand in the nice operator basis as
L_{p}=\sum_{i=0}^{J}l_{ip}F_{i}
and
M_{q}=\sum_{i=0}^{J}m_{iq}F_{i}
. In addition, let
b
be some arbitrary matrix in
M(d^{2},\mathbb{C})
and let
c=lbm^{\dagger}
. Then, using
Eq.

14
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(A)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=0}^{J}[\sum_{p,q}l_{ip}b_{pq}m^{*}_{jq}]F_{i}AF_{j}

(21a)

\displaystyle=\sum_{p,q}b_{pq}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{J}l_{ip}F_{i}\right)A\left(\sum_{j=0}^{J}m_{jq}^{*}F_{j}\right)

(21b)

\displaystyle=\sum_{p,q}b_{pq}L_{p}AM_{q}^{\dagger}\ ,

(21c)

and, using
Eq.

20
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{\dagger}(A)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=0}^{J}[\sum_{p,q}l_{ip}b_{pq}m^{*}_{jq}]^{*}F_{i}AF_{j}

(22a)

\displaystyle=\sum_{p,q}b_{pq}^{*}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{J}l_{ip}^{*}F_{i}\right)A\left(\sum_{j=0}^{J}m_{jq}F_{j}\right)

(22b)

\displaystyle=\sum_{p,q}b_{pq}^{*}L_{p}^{\dagger}AM_{q}\ .

(22c)

Proposition 2
.

\mathcal{E}
is simultaneously Hermiticity-preserving and Hermitian iff

in
Eq.

14
is real-symmetric, i.e.,
c_{ij}=c_{ji}=c^{*}_{ij}

\forall i,j
.

Proof.

This follows immediately by equating the right-hand sides of

and

, both of which are then equal to

by
3

∎

Proposition 3
.

\mathcal{E}
is Hermiticity-preserving iff
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\in M(d^{2},\mathbb{R})
.

Proof.

Using Eq. (
13
), we obtain:

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{ij}^{*}=\Tr\left(\left[\mathcal{E}\left(F_{j}\right)\right]^{\dagger}F_{i}^{\dagger}\right)=\Tr\left(\mathcal{E}\left(F_{j}\right)F_{i}\right)=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{ij}\ .

(23)

In the other direction, assume

.

\mathcal{E}(A)
is represented in a nice operator basis as

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(A)

\displaystyle=\sum_{{i,j=0}}^{J}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{ij}\boldsymbol{A}_{j}F_{i}\ ,

(25b)

III.2
General properties of a Liouvillian

Since
a
in
Eq.

1
is Hermitian, it can be written as

a=u^{{\dagger}}\tilde{\gamma}u
where
u
is unitary and
\tilde{\gamma}
is diagonal
with the eigenvalues
\{\gamma_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha=1}^{J}
of
a
on its diagonal.
The
\gamma_{\alpha}
’s are always real when
Eq.

1
is a Markovian quantum master equation. When
a\geq 0
, i.e., when
Eq.

1
is a Lindblad equation, they are non-negative. Defining

L_{\alpha}=\sum_{j=1}^{J}u^{*}_{\alpha j}F_{j}\ ,

(26)

we have
F_{i}=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{J}u_{\alpha i}L_{\alpha}
. Then:

\displaystyle\sum_{{i,j=1}}^{J}a_{ij}F_{i}\rho F_{j}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\alpha,\beta=1}}^{J}L_{\alpha}\rho L_{\beta}^{\dagger}\sum_{{i,j=1}}^{J}u_{\alpha i}a_{ij}u^{\dagger}_{\beta l}

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\alpha=1}}^{J}{\gamma}_{\alpha}L_{\alpha}\rho L_{\alpha}^{\dagger}\ ,

(27a)

\displaystyle\sum_{{i,j=1}}^{J}a_{ij}F_{j}^{\dagger}F_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\alpha,\beta=1}}^{J}L_{\beta}^{\dagger}L_{\alpha}\sum_{{i,j=1}}^{J}u_{\alpha i}a_{ij}u^{\dagger}_{\beta l}

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\alpha=1}}^{J}{\gamma}_{\alpha}L_{\alpha}^{\dagger}L_{\alpha}\ .

(27b)

Therefore the Markovian quantum master equation [
Eq.

1
] becomes:

\displaystyle\dot{\rho}={\cal L}\rho

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}_{H}\rho+\mathcal{L}_{a}\rho

(28a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{H}[\cdot]

\displaystyle=-i[H,\cdot]\ ,

(28b)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{a}[\cdot]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{J}\gamma_{\alpha}\left(L_{\alpha}\cdot L^{{\dagger}}_{\alpha}-\frac{1}{2}\{L^{{\dagger}}_{\alpha}L_{\alpha},\cdot\}\right)\ .

(28c)

The
L_{\alpha}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
are called
Lindblad operators
.

We remark that the representation of the dissipator
\mathcal{L}_{a}
in
the form (
28c
) need not be unique:
for example, if
a=I
(the identity matrix), it can be written
as
u^{\dagger}Iu
for any unitary
u
, but [as can be seen from

Eq.

26
] different choices of
u
lead to different Lindblad operators. More generally,
\tilde{\gamma}
is unique only up to permutations, and any permutation redefines the Lindblad operators by modifying the diagonalizing unitary matrix
u
.

Proposition 4
.

The Liouvillian is Hermiticity-preserving.

Proof.

To show this we can directly compare
[\mathcal{L}(X)]^{\dagger}
with
\mathcal{L}(X^{\dagger})
using
Eqs.

28b
and
28c
.
For the Hamiltonian part, we have the following:

\displaystyle[\mathcal{L}_{H}(X)]^{{\dagger}}

\displaystyle=i(HX-XH)^{{\dagger}}=i(X^{{\dagger}}H^{{\dagger}}-H^{{\dagger}}X^{{\dagger}})

(29a)

\displaystyle=-i(HX^{{\dagger}}-X^{{\dagger}}H)=\mathcal{L}_{H}(X^{{\dagger}})\ ,

(29b)

while for the dissipative part,

\displaystyle[\mathcal{L}_{a}(X)]^{{\dagger}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{J}\gamma_{\alpha}\left((L_{\alpha}XL_{\alpha}^{{\dagger}})^{{\dagger}}-\frac{1}{2}\{L_{\alpha}^{{\dagger}}L_{\alpha},X\}^{\dagger}\right)

(30a)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{J}\gamma_{\alpha}\left((L_{\alpha}X^{{\dagger}}L_{\alpha}^{{\dagger}})-\frac{1}{2}\{L_{\alpha}^{{\dagger}}L_{\alpha},X^{{\dagger}}\}\right)

(30b)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}_{a}(X^{{\dagger}})\,.

(30c)

Thus,

\mathcal{L}\left(X^{\dagger}\right)=[\mathcal{L}\left(X\right)]^{\dagger}\ .

(31)

∎

Corollary 1
.

The Markovian quantum master equation
\dot{\rho}=\mathcal{L}\rho

is a real-valued linear ordinary differential equation (ODE) for the vector

whose coordinates are

:

\dot{\boldsymbol{\rho}}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{L}}\boldsymbol{\rho}\ ,\quad\boldsymbol{\rho}\in\mathbb{R}^{d^{2}}\ ,\quad\boldsymbol{\mathcal{L}}\in M(d^{2},\mathbb{R})\ .

(32)

Proof.

This follows directly from

,
3
and
4
with
X=\rho
and
\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{L}
.
∎

Proposition 5
.

The Liouvillian maps any operator
X
to a traceless operator.
That is, for any operator
X

\Tr[\mathcal{L}(X)]=0.

(33)

Proof.

From

1
we have

\displaystyle\Tr[\mathcal{L}_{H}(X)]

\displaystyle=-i\Tr([H,X]){}

\displaystyle=-i[\Tr(HX)-\Tr(XH)]=0,

(34)

and, using
\Tr(AB)=\Tr(BA)
:

\Tr(\mathcal{L}_{a}(X))=\sum_{i,j=1}^{J}a_{ij}\Tr\left(F_{i}XF_{j}-\frac{1}{2}\left\{F_{j}F_{i},X\right\}\right)\\
=\sum_{i,j=1}^{J}a_{ij}\left(F_{j}F_{i}X-\frac{1}{2}F_{j}F_{i}X-\frac{1}{2}F_{j}F_{i}X\right)=0.

(35)

∎

Proposition 6

VI
The rarity of Lindbladians

As posed in the title, the question that motivated this work can be interpreted as the probability
that a given pair
(G,\vec{c})
will give rise to a Lindbladian (given a natural choice of a
distribution over the pairs
(G,\vec{c})
). Alternatively, one can use a natural choice of a
distribution for
(H,a)
in order to induce the distribution on the pairs
(G,\vec{c})
and
compute a probability that a given pair
(G,\vec{c})
with that distribution
gives rise to a Lindbladian. In the remainder of this section, we provide partial answers
to these questions and describe the difference between these two distributions.

VI.1
Natural distribution on the pairs of
(G,\vec{c})

Suppose all elements of
G
and
\sqrt{d}\vec{c}
are picked
independently from a standard normal distribution. Such a distribution
is known as the Ginibre Orthogonal Ensemble (GinOE)
Byun and Forrester 2023
.
One might be interested
in the following question: “How likely is it that these
G
and
\vec{c}

generate a Lindbladian, i.e.
a\geq 0
?”. Let us denote this probability
as
\tilde{P}^{\text{GinOE}}_{J}
. While we do not know the asymptotics
of
\tilde{P}^{\text{GinOE}}_{J}
, here we provide a non-rigorous attempt
at deriving the asymptotics of an upper bound. Recall that a necessary condition for
a\geq 0
is
that all eigenvalues of
G
have a non-positive real part.
Denoting the probability of the latter by
P^{\text{GinOE}}_{J}
, this implies

\tilde{P}^{\text{GinOE}}_{J}\leq P^{\text{GinOE}}_{J}.

(183)

The distribution of eigenvalues in the GinOE is known: see, e.g.
(
Byun and Forrester 2023
, Eq. (1.7))
. One can use either the methods of

Dean and Majumdar 2008
or some rigorous alternative to those methods
to estimate the asymptotics of
P^{\text{GinOE}}_{J}
, which would be an upper bound for
\tilde{P}^{\text{GinOE}}_{J}
due to
Eq.

183
.

More specifically, assuming the same (non-rigorous) argument as in Ref.
Dean and Majumdar 2008
applies to the GinOE, we can state that

P^{\text{GinOE}}_{J}=\exp(-\theta^{\text{GinOE}}J^{2}+O(J)).

(184)

We discuss the estimation of the positive constant
\theta^{\text{GinOE}}
in
Appendix

E
. The important point about
Eq.

184
is that the probability decays rapidly in the Hilbert space dimension
d
(recall that
J=d^{2}-1
).

VI.2
Natural distribution on
a

In this subsection, we examine the prevalence of Lindbladians within the set of all Liouvillians.

Specifically, we attempt to answer the following question:
“Given a random
H
and
a
, what is the probability that
\mathcal{L}_{H,a}
is a Lindbladian?”
Since the condition
a\geq 0
that guarantees a Liouvillian is a Lindbladian depends only on
a
and not on
H
,
defining the distribution in the space of Hermitian matrices
a
is sufficient to answer this question.
Additionally, the scale of
a
is irrelevant; changing
a
to
\alpha a
(where
\alpha>0
can be randomly selected from a distribution which may depend on
a
)
does not alter the answer to
a\geq 0
.

One natural choice for the distribution is the Gaussian Unitary Ensemble (GUE), also known as the
\beta=2
Gaussian Ensemble.
In this ensemble, the real and imaginary parts of each component of a
J\times J
matrix
A
are independently selected from
a normal distribution with a mean of
0

and
5
5

5

We choose the normalization to be consistent with Ref.
Forrester 2010
. An alternative normalization used in other works is variance
=1
, i.e., the standard normal distribution.

a variance of
1/\beta=1/2
.
The matrix
a
is then computed as
a=(A+A^{\dagger})/2
. The condition

a\geq 0
is equivalent to
\lambda_{j}\geq 0
for
j=1,\dots,J
,
where
\lambda=\{\lambda_{j}\}_{j=1}^{J}
is the vector of eigenvalues of
a
. Let us denote the
probability of this event as
P^{\text{GUE}}_{J}
.

The joint probability density function (PDF) of eigenvalues of a matrix from the GUE is well known to be:

\displaystyle p(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{G_{\beta,J}}e^{-\frac{\beta}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\lambda_{j}^{2}}\prod_{1\leq j<k\leq J}\!\!\!\!\left|\lambda_{k}-\lambda_{j}\right|^{\beta}

(185a)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{G_{\beta,J}}e^{-\beta E(\lambda)}\ ,

(185b)

where
E(\lambda)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\lambda_{j}^{2}-\sum_{1\leq j<k\leq J}\ln\left|\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{k}\right|
and
G_{\beta,J}
is the normalization factor (partition function);
see
(
Forrester 2010
, Eq. (1.4))
for further details.
Thus, the distribution of
\lambda
can be interpreted as the canonical ensemble of a two-dimensional gas (in
\mathbb{C}
)
with
J
charged particles (each of unit charge with the same sign), constrained to the real line in a harmonic potential at temperature
1/\beta=1/2
, also known as a log-gas.
The probability
P^{\text{GUE}}_{J}
in this interpretation is the probability that, by random chance, all particles happen to be to the right of the origin.

P^{\text{GUE}}_{J}
is known as the probability of atypically large fluctuations of extreme value statistics of the GUE,
and was estimated in Ref.
Dean and Majumdar 2008
as:

P^{\text{GUE}}_{J}=3^{-J^{2}/2+O(J)}\ .

(186)

The estimate uses non-rigorous techniques from statistical mechanics, including
the replacement of particle density (sum of delta functions) with a smooth function
and the use of functional integrals and functional Fourier transforms.
While rigorously proving this estimate is left to future research,
Eq.

186

shows that the probability of a randomly picked Liouvillian being a Lindbladian
decays rapidly with
d
, just like
Eq.

184
for the GinOE. For example, already for
d=4
, the estimate gives

P^{\text{GUE}}_{J}\sim 10^{-54}
, demonstrating that it is extremely unlikely to find a
Lindbladian for
d=4
by picking a random
a
from the GUE.

Due to the linearity of

VIII
Summary and Outlook

A standard approach to solving a (time-independent quantum) Markovian quantum
master equation is to vectorize it and solve the corresponding nonhomogeneous
first-order linear ODE for the coherence vector. Here we posed the inverse
problem: when does such
a 1ODE of the form
\dot{\vec{v}}=G\vec{v}+\vec{c}
correspond to a
Markovian quantum master equation? When does it correspond to a completely positive Markovian quantum master equation, i.e., a Lindblad equation? What are the
parameters, i.e.,
a
and
H
, of such master equations in terms of the parameters
G
and
\vec{c}
of the 1ODE? Finally, how ubiquitous are Lindbladians?

We have shown that the answer to the first question is “always”. We also expressed
the parameters of such Markovian quantum master equations using an expansion in a nice
operator basis, which yields explicit expressions for the Hamiltonian and the matrix
a

of coefficients of the dissipator in terms of the parameters
(G,\vec{c})
of the 1ODE; see
1
. In essence, this means that every 1ODE of the form
\dot{\vec{v}}=G\vec{v}+\vec{c}
is directly representable as a Markovian quantum master equation,
Eq.

1
. However, complete positivity (i.e., whether the result is a Lindblad equation) is not guaranteed and must be checked on a case-by-case basis. Toward this end, we have also formulated the complete positivity condition directly in terms of
(G,\vec{c})
; see
Eq.

6
. This condition is equivalent to the positive semidefiniteness of
a
, which is simpler to check in practice after
a
has been computed from
G
and
\vec{c}
using
Eq.

5b
.

Our work assumed the setting of finite-dimensional Hilbert spaces. We left open for future research the problem of connecting 1ODEs to quantum master equations in the infinite-dimensional setting. We also left open the complete answer to the question of the ubiquity of Lindbladians: namely, while we have argued that the condition of positivity of
a
makes Lindbladians extremely rare when viewed from the perspective of random matrix theory, the answer to what is the probability that
a\geq 0
for all real
G
(sampled from the Ginibre Orthogonal Ensemble (GinOE)
Byun and Forrester 2023
) such that
G
has non-positive eigenvalues, is still open. Answering this question will quantify the probability that a randomly selected 1ODE results in a Lindblad master equation.

Appendix A
Solution of
Eq.

3

for the coherence vector

Eq.

3
is a linear, first-order, nonhomogeneous differential equation (1ODE). We provide the solution in two parts, first for diagonalizable and invertible
G
, then for general
G
.

A.1
Solution for diagonalizable and invertible

G

Let us first assume that
G
is diagonalizable over
\mathbb{R}^{J}
and also invertible.
We look for a solution in the form

\vec{v}(t)=\vec{v}^{(0)}(t)+\vec{v}^{(\infty)}\ ,

(196)

where
\vec{v}^{(0)}(t)
is the homogeneous part and
\vec{v}^{(\infty)}
is the nonhomogeneous (time-independent) part. Let
\vec{x}^{(k)}
and
\lambda_{k}
represent the eigenvectors and (possibly degenerate and complex) eigenvalues of
G
, i.e.,

G\vec{x}^{(k)}=\lambda_{k}\vec{x}^{(k)}\ ,\qquad k=1,\dots,J\ .

(197)

It is then straightforward to check by direct differentiation and substitution that

\displaystyle\vec{v}^{(0)}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{M}s_{k}e^{\lambda_{k}t}\vec{x}^{(k)}

(198a)

\displaystyle\vec{v}^{(\infty)}

\displaystyle=-G^{-1}\vec{c}

(198b)

in the solution in
Eq.

3
.
The coefficients
s_{k}
are determined by the initial condition
\vec{v}(0)
:

\vec{v}^{(0)}(0)=\sum_{k=1}^{M}s_{k}\vec{x}^{(k)}=X\vec{s}\ ,\qquad\text{col}_{k}(X)=\vec{x}^{(k)}\ ,

(199)

i.e.,
X
is the matrix whose columns are the eigenvectors of
G
. Also,
\vec{v}^{(0)}(0)=\vec{v}(0)-\vec{v}^{(\infty)}
. Thus

\vec{s}=X^{-1}(\vec{v}(0)+G^{-1}\vec{c})\ .

(200)

The eigenvalues can be decomposed as

\lambda_{k}=\real(\lambda_{k})+i\imag(\lambda_{k})
. The imaginary part describes a rotation of
\vec{v}
. The real part is constrained by complete positivity and trace preservation to be non-positive, or else the norm of the coherence vector would not be bounded [recall
Eq.

42
].

A.2
Solution for general
G

The general case is where
G
is not diagonalizable over
\mathbb{R}^{J}
and may not be invertible. In this case, we can still use a similarity transformation
S
to transform
G
into Jordan canonical form:

G_{J}=SGS^{-1}=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}J_{1}&&\\
&\ddots&\\
&&J_{q}\\
\end{array}\right)\ ,

(201)

where the
q
Jordan blocks have the form

J_{j}=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccccc}\mu_{j}&1&&&\\
&\mu_{j}&\ddots&&\\
&&\ddots&\ddots&\\
&&&\mu_{j}&1\\
&&&&\mu_{j}\\
\end{array}\right)=\mu_{j}I+K_{j}\ .

(202)

The
\mu_{j}
’s are the (possibly degenerate, complex) eigenvalues, and
K_{j}
are nilpotent matrices:
K_{j}^{d_{j}}=0
, where
d_{j}
is the dimension of
J_{j}
. When all
d_{j}=1
,
G
is diagonalizable, and
G_{J}
reduces to the diagonalized form of
G
. When one or more of the
d_{j}>1
, then
G
is not diagonalizable, meaning that a similarity transformation does not exist that transforms
G
into a diagonal matrix. The eigenvalues are the solutions of
\det{(G-\mu I)}=0
.

Applying
S
from the left to
Eq.

3
yields

S\dot{\vec{v}}=SGS^{-1}S\vec{v}+S\vec{c}\quad\implies\quad\dot{\vec{w}}=G_{J}\vec{w}+\vec{c}^{\prime}\ ,

(203)

where
\vec{w}=S\vec{v}
and we defined
\vec{c}^{\prime}=S\vec{c}
.
We again look for a solution in the form

\vec{w}(t)=\vec{w}^{(0)}(t)+\vec{w}^{(\infty)}\ ,

(204)

where now
\vec{w}^{(0)}(t)
is the homogeneous part and
\vec{w}^{(\infty)}
is the nonhomogeneous (time-independent) part.

First, let us solve for the homogeneous part.
Since the Jordan blocks are decoupled, the general solution of the homogenous part is

\vec{w}^{(0)}(t)=\bigoplus_{j=1}^{q}\vec{w}_{j}^{(0)}(t)\ ,

(205)

where we use the direct sum notation to denote that the summands must be stacked into a single-column vector. Each of the
q
summands satisfies a differential equation of the form

\dot{\vec{w}}_{j}^{(0)}=J_{j}{\vec{w}}_{j}^{(0)}\ .

(206)

Claim 1
.

The solution for a general
d_{j}
dimensional Jordan block is a vector

\vec{w}_{j}^{(0)}=({w}_{j,1}^{(0)},\dots,{w}_{j,k}^{(0)},\dots,{w}_{j,d_{j}}^{(0)})^{T}

(207)

with components:

\displaystyle{w}_{j,k}^{(0)}(t)

\displaystyle=e^{\mu_{j}t}p_{d_{j}-k}(t)

(208a)

\displaystyle p_{d_{j}-k}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=k}^{d_{j}}{w}_{j,n}^{(0)}(0)\frac{t^{n-k}}{(n-k)!}\ ,\quad k=1,\dots,d_{j}\ .

(208b)

Here
p_{d_{j}-k}(t)
denotes a polynomial of degree
d_{j}-k
. The highest degree of such polynomials is
d_{j}-1
, of the last component
{w}_{j,d_{j}}^{(0)}
.

Proof.

From the structure of the
j
’th Jordan block
J_{j}
, it is clear that

\displaystyle\dot{w}_{j,d_{j}}^{(0)}

\displaystyle=\mu_{j}{w}_{j,d_{j}}^{(0)}

(209a)

\displaystyle\dot{w}_{j,k}^{(0)}

\displaystyle=\mu_{j}{w}_{j,k}^{(0)}+{w}_{j,k+1}^{(0)}\ ,\qquad k\in\{1,\dots d_{j}-1\}\ ,

(209b)

and if we differentiate Eq. (
208
) we obtain, first for
k=d_{j}
:

\dot{w}_{j,d_{j}}^{(0)}=\mu_{j}e^{\mu_{j}t}p_{0}=\mu_{j}{w}_{j,d_{j}}^{(0)}\ ,

(210)

and second, for
k<d_{j}
:

\displaystyle\dot{w}_{j,k}^{(0)}

\displaystyle=\mu_{j}{w}_{j,k}^{(0)}+\sum_{n=k+1}^{d_{j}}{w}_{j,n}^{(0)}(0)\frac{t^{n-k-1}}{(n-k-1)!}

(211a)

\displaystyle=\mu_{j}{w}_{j,k}^{(0)}+{w}_{j,k+1}^{(0)}\ ,\qquad k\in\{1,\dots d_{j}-1\}\ .

(211b)

∎

Note that we can be certain that for all
d_{j}>1
, the corresponding
\real(\mu_{j})<0
, since a positive or zero real part would violate
Eq.

42
.

Next, let us solve for the nonhomogeneous part.
As before, it is determined by the solution form (
204
) subject to the boundary conditions

\displaystyle\vec{w}(0)

\displaystyle=\vec{w}^{(0)}(0)+\vec{w}^{(\infty)}

(212a)

\displaystyle\vec{w}(\infty)

\displaystyle=\vec{w}^{(\infty)}\,.

(212b)

On the one hand,
\dot{\vec{w}}=\dot{\vec{w}}^{(0)}
, and on the other hand
\dot{\vec{w}}=G_{J}(\vec{w}^{(0)}(t)+\vec{w}^{(\infty)})+\vec{c}^{\prime}=G_{J}\vec{w}^{(0)}(t)+G_{J}\vec{w}^{(\infty)}+\vec{c}^{\prime}
. Since only the homogeneous part is time-dependent, we must have
\dot{\vec{w}}^{(0)}=G_{J}\vec{w}^{(0)}(t)
, so that the particular solution satisfies

G_{J}\vec{w}^{(\infty)}=-\vec{c}^{\prime}\ .

(213)

This linear system has a solution for
\vec{w}^{(\infty)}
iff
\text{rank}(G_{J})=\text{rank}(G_{J}|\vec{c}^{\prime})
(the augmented matrix).

The solution is unique only if, in addition,
G_{J}
is full rank (equal to
J
); otherwise, the solution for
\vec{w}^{(\infty)}
is under-determined.
Since
G_{J}
is in Jordan form, it is in full-rank row-echelon form unless there are Jordan blocks with
\mu_{j}=0
.
However, as a consequence of the norm upper bound
Eq.

42
, any such Jordan blocks must have a dimension
d_{j}=1
, since otherwise, the polynomials in
Eq.

208
would grow unboundedly.
Therefore, the kernel dimension
\dim(G_{J})-\mathrm{rank}(G_{J})
must be the number of Jordan blocks with
\mu_{j}=0
and
d_{j}=1
, and in these blocks,
Eq.

203
becomes the trivial scalar differential equation
\dot{w}_{j}=c^{\prime}_{j}
, whose solution is
w_{j}(t)=c^{\prime}_{j}t+w_{j}(0)
. But this means that
c^{\prime}_{j}=0
since otherwise
w_{j}(t)
is unbounded. Thus, in each block with
\mu_{j}=0
we have
w_{j}(t)=w_{j}(0)
.
Such Jordan blocks represent frozen degrees of freedom that do not evolve in time.

Proof.

To prove injectivity we need to show that
\mathcal{F}(a_{1})=\mathcal{F}(a_{2})\implies a_{1}=a_{2}
, but since
\mathcal{F}
is linear it suffices to show that
\mathcal{F}(a)=(0,\vec{0})\implies a=0
.

Let us take a matrix
a
such that
\mathcal{F}(a)=(0,\vec{0})
. From
4
:

\mathcal{L}_{a}=\mathcal{L}_{0}=0,

(215)

where
\mathcal{L}_{a}
is the dissipative Liouvillian in
Eq.

1
. Let
\ket{j}
be the vector with the
j
-th component equal to
1
and all other components equal to
0
. Then, from
Eq.

215
,
\forall j,k=1,\dots,d
we have
\mathcal{L}_{a}(|{j}\rangle\!\langle k|)=0
. In particular, using
Eq.

1c
:

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{d}\bra{i}\mathcal{L}_{a}(|{j}\rangle\!\langle k|)\ket{k}

(216a)

\displaystyle=\,\sum_{{m,n=1}}^{J}\,a_{mn}\big[\bra{i}F_{m}\ket{j}\sum_{k=1}^{d}\bra{k}F_{n}\ket{k}-\frac{1}{2}\bra{i}F_{n}F_{m}\ket{j}\sum_{k=1}^{d}\langle{k}|{k}\rangle

\displaystyle\qquad-\frac{1}{2}\langle{i}|{j}\rangle\sum_{k=1}^{d}\bra{k}F_{n}F_{m}\ket{k}\big]

(216b)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m,n=1}^{J}a_{mn}\big[\bra{i}F_{m}\ket{j}\Tr(F_{n})-\frac{d}{2}\bra{i}F_{n}F_{m}\ket{j}

\displaystyle\qquad-\frac{1}{2}\delta_{ij}\Tr(F_{n}F_{m})\big]

(216c)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\delta_{ij}\Tr(a)-\frac{d}{2}\sum_{m,n=1}^{J}a_{mn}\bra{i}F_{n}F_{m}\ket{j}\ ,

(216d)

where we used
\Tr(F_{n})=0
and
\Tr(F_{n}F_{m})=\delta_{nm}
(
1
). For the same reason,
\sum_{i=1}^{d}\bra{i}F_{n}F_{m}\ket{i}=\Tr(F_{n}F_{m})=\delta_{nm}
, so that after summing
Eq.

216d
over
i=j=1,\ldots,d
we obtain

0=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\Tr(a)-\frac{d}{2}\sum_{m,n=1}^{J}a_{mn}\delta_{nm}=-d\Tr(a)\ ,

(217)

i.e.,
\Tr(a)=0
. From
Eq.

216d
it follows that
\bra{i}A\ket{j}=0

\forall i,j
, where
A\equiv\sum_{m,n=1}^{J}a_{mn}F_{n}F_{m}
, i.e.,
A=0
.
Hence, we have shown that
\forall X
:

\mathcal{L}_{a}(X)=\sum_{m,n=1}^{J}a_{mn}F_{n}XF_{n}\ .

(218)

We can now express the matrix elements
a_{mn}
in terms of
\mathcal{L}_{a}
, as follows:

\displaystyle a_{mn}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m^{\prime},n^{\prime}=1}^{J}a_{m^{\prime}n^{\prime}}\Tr(F_{n}F_{n^{\prime}})\Tr(F_{m^{\prime}}F_{m})

(219a)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j,k=1}^{d}\bra{j}F_{n}\big[\sum_{m^{\prime},n^{\prime}=1}^{J}a_{m^{\prime}n^{\prime}}F_{n^{\prime}}|{j}\rangle\!\langle k|F_{m^{\prime}}\big]F_{m}\ket{k}

(219b)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j,k=1}^{J}\bra{j}F_{n}\mathcal{L}_{a}(|{j}\rangle\!\langle k|)F_{m}\ket{k}=0\ ,

(219c)

since
\mathcal{L}_{a}=0
. That is,
a=0
.
∎

Appendix C
Facts from convex geometry

In this section, we summarize the facts from convex geometry
needed for this work.

Let
M_{{+}}(J,\mathbb{C})
be the set
of positive semidefinite
J\times J
matrices with complex coefficients:

M_{{+}}(J,\mathbb{C})=\left\{a\in M_{\textrm{sa}}(J,\mathbb{C}):\forall b\in\mathbb{C}^{J}\;b^{\dagger}ab\geq 0\right\}.

(220)

This equation describes
M_{{+}}(J,\mathbb{C})
as the intersection
of infinitely many half-spaces in
M_{\textrm{sa}}(J,\mathbb{C})
,
whose boundaries all pass through
the origin
a=0
. Therefore,
M_{{+}}(J,\mathbb{C})
is a closed convex
cone in
M_{\textrm{sa}}(J,\mathbb{C})
.

Let

M_{{+},1}(J,\mathbb{C})=\{a\in M_{{+}}(J,\mathbb{C})\colon\;\Tr(a)=1\},

(221)

i.e., the set of unit-trace, positive-semidefinite matrices.

Lemma 6
.

M_{{+}}(J,\mathbb{C})
is a closed convex cone with a compact base, which
can be chosen to be
M_{{+},1}(J,\mathbb{C})
.

Proof.

As noted above,
M_{{+}}(J,\mathbb{C})
is a closed convex cone.
By definition,
M_{{+},1}(J,\mathbb{C})
is an intersection of that
cone and a hyperplane not passing through the origin.
Thus, it remains to prove that

1.

any of the rays in
M_{{+}}(J,\mathbb{C})
passes through

M_{{+},1}(J,\mathbb{C})
, i.e., every non-zero element of
M_{{+}}(J,\mathbb{C})

is proportional to an element of
M_{{+},1}(J,\mathbb{C})
with a positive
coefficient;

2.

M_{{+},1}(J,\mathbb{C})
is compact.

To prove the first statement, let
a\neq 0,a\in M_{{+}}(J,\mathbb{C})
.
Since
a
is Hermitian
it can be diagonalized.
Eq.

220
implies that all its eigenvalues
are non-negative, hence
\Tr(a)\geq 0
. In fact, at least one of the
eigenvalues has to be positive, otherwise
a=0
, hence
\Tr(a)>0
.
Therefore
a=\Tr(a)(a/\Tr(a))
where the coefficient
\Tr(a)>0
and
the element
a/\Tr(a)\in M_{{+},1}(J,\mathbb{C})
.

Since
M_{{+},1}(J,\mathbb{C})
is an intersection of closed sets,
it is closed. Thus, it remains to prove it is bounded, e.g.:

\forall a\in M_{{+},1}(J,\mathbb{C})\quad\sum_{i,j=1}^{J}\left|a_{ij}\right|^{2}\leq 1\ .

(222)

Let
\{\lambda_{i}\}_{i=1}^{J}
be the eigenvalues of such an
a
. Then

\sum_{i=1}^{J}\lambda_{i}=1
and
0\leq\lambda_{i}\leq 1
. Therefore, indeed,

\sum_{i,j=1}^{J}\left|a_{ij}\right|^{2}=\Tr(a^{\dagger}a)=\sum_{i=1}^{J}\lambda_{i}^{2}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{J}\lambda_{i}=1\ .

(223)

∎

Lemma 7
.

The extreme rays of
M_{{+}}(J,\mathbb{C})
are the rays generated by rank 1
matrices.

Note: this is
(
R.A. Horn and C.R. Johnson 1999
, Exercise 7.5.15)
. Here we provide a proof for
completeness.

Proof.

Let
\{\alpha a:\alpha\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\}
be an extreme ray in

M_{{+}}(J,\mathbb{C})
. Here
a\in M_{{+}}(J,\mathbb{C})\setminus\{0\}
.
Since
a
is Hermitian, we can diagonalize it by a unitary,
hence write it in the form

a=\sum_{i=1}^{k}b_{i}b_{i}^{\dagger}

(224)

for some
b_{i}\in\mathbb{C}^{J}
orthogonal to each other, where

k=\rank(a)
. Since
a\neq 0
,
k\geq 1
. If
k\geq 2
then

Eq.

224
represents
a
as a sum of non-proportional
elements of
M_{{+}}(J,\mathbb{C})
, contradicting the assumption
that
a
lies on an extreme ray in
M_{{+}}(J,\mathbb{C})
. Thus,
k=1
.

Vice-versa, if
\rank(a)=1
,
a\in M_{{+}}(J,\mathbb{C})
, we want to
prove that
a
lies on an extreme ray of
M_{{+}}(J,\mathbb{C})
.
Assume to the contrary that

a=a_{1}+a_{2}

(225)

where
a_{1},a_{2}\in M_{{+}}(J,\mathbb{C})
and
a_{1}
is not proportional to

a
. We can write
a=\alpha bb^{\dagger}
for some

\alpha\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
,
b\in\mathbb{C}^{J}
with

\left\|b\right\|=1
,
\alpha>0
. Then for
i=1,2
, we can decompose

a_{i}/\alpha=\beta_{i}bb^{\dagger}+\tilde{a}_{i}

(226)

where
\beta_{i}=b^{\dagger}a_{i}b/\alpha
,
b^{\dagger}\tilde{a}_{i}b=0
.

Eq.

225
implies that
\beta_{1}+\beta_{2}=1
,

\tilde{a}_{1}+\tilde{a}_{2}=0
. By our assumption
\tilde{a}_{1}\neq 0

(otherwise
a_{1}
would be proportional to
a
).
Therefore, there is a vector
c
s.t.
c^{\dagger}\tilde{a}_{1}c\neq 0
.
Let
c=\gamma b+c_{\perp}
, where
b^{\dagger}c_{\perp}=0
.
Note that

c_{\perp}^{\dagger}\tilde{a}_{1}c_{\perp}=c_{\perp}^{\dagger}a_{1}c_{\perp}/\alpha
,
hence
c_{\perp}^{\dagger}\tilde{a}_{1}c_{\perp}\geq 0
. Similarly,

c_{\perp}^{\dagger}\tilde{a}_{1}c_{\perp}=-c_{\perp}^{\dagger}a_{2}c_{\perp}/\alpha
,
hence
c_{\perp}^{\dagger}\tilde{a}_{1}c_{\perp}\leq 0
. Thus,

c_{\perp}^{\dagger}\tilde{a}_{1}c_{\perp}=0
.

For any
\delta\in\mathbb{C}
we have

(b+\delta c_{\perp})^{\dagger}a_{1}(b+\delta c_{\perp})\geq 0\ ,

(227)

and we have just shown that the quadratic term in this expression is zero. Since
the inequality
Eq.

227
has to hold for all
\delta
, the
linear term is zero too, i.e.,
b^{\dagger}a_{1}c_{\perp}=0
.
Thus,
c^{\dagger}\tilde{a}_{1}c=0
, contradicting the assumption.
∎

Lemma 8
.

M_{{+}}(J,\mathbb{C})
is the convex hull of its extreme rays.

Here we present two alternative ways to see this.

Proof 1.

Any positive semidefinite matrix can be diagonalized. Such diagonalization
results
(
R.A. Horn and C.R. Johnson 1999
, Theorem 7.5.2)
in a decomposition of the matrix into
rank-
1
positive semidefinite matrices [as in
Eq.

224
], which – according to
7
–
lie on the extreme rays of
M_{{+}}(J,\mathbb{C})
.
∎

Proof 2.

In the context of finite dimensional vector spaces,
Krein-Milman (also known as Minkowski’s theorem, see, e.g.,

(
Rockafellar 1970
, Corollary 18.5.1)
) states that
any compact convex set is the convex hull of its extreme points.
As a corollary any convex cone with a compact base is the
convex hull of its extreme rays. As follows from
6
,

M_{{+}}(J,\mathbb{C})
is such a cone.
∎

Appendix D
Representation of a superoperator using a nice operator basis

Proposition 19
.

Any superoperator

\mathcal{E}\in\mathcal{B}[\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})]

\mathcal{E}(A)=\sum_{{i,j=0}}^{J}c_{ij}F_{i}\bullet F_{j}\ .

This is
(
Gorini et al. 1976
, Lemma 2.2)
.

It can also be seen directly by noting that any linear operator can be

\mathcal{E}(A)_{kn}=\sum_{l,m=1}^{d}\mathcal{E}_{klmn}A_{lm}

tensor
\mathcal{E}_{klmn}

\mathcal{E}_{klmn}
can be seen as a
matrix in 2 ways: first, as a function of indices
k
and
l
, and second, as a
function of indices
m
and
n
. Applying “coordinatization”
[
Eqs.

8
and
9
] twice to
\mathcal{E}_{klmn}
we get
c_{ij}
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{E}
be any superoperator in
\mathcal{B}[\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})]

Denoting

11

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(A)_{kn}=\sum_{l,m=1}^{d}\mathcal{E}_{klmn}A_{lm}

Appendix E
Computation of the constant
\theta^{\text{GinOE}}

The constant
\theta^{\text{GinOE}}
in
Eq.

184
can be computed as

\theta^{\text{GinOE}}=\inf_{\rho\in\mathcal{S}_{{+}}}E(\rho)-\inf_{\rho\in\mathcal{S}}E(\rho),

(231)

where

\displaystyle E(\rho)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{C}}\left(\left|z\right|^{2}-\int_{\mathbb{C}}\ln\left|z-w\right|\rho(w)d^{2}w\right)\rho(z)d^{2}z,

(232a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}

\displaystyle=\Bigl\{\rho\in C_{0}(\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}):\int_{\mathbb{C}}\rho(z)d^{2}z=1,

\displaystyle\qquad\forall z\;\rho(z)=\rho(z^{*})\Bigr\},

(232b)

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{{+}}

\displaystyle=\left\{\rho\in\mathcal{S}:\forall z\;\real(z)<0\Rightarrow\rho(z)=0\right\},

(232c)

\displaystyle d^{2}z

\displaystyle=d\real(z)d\imag(z)=\frac{i}{2}dzdz^{*},

(232d)

where
C_{0}(X\to Y)
is the set of continuous functions from
X
to
Y

with compact support. One can then compute that

\inf_{\rho\in\mathcal{S}}E(\rho)=5/8-\ln(2)/4
and is achieved
near
\rho(z)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\mathbb{I}(\left|z\right|^{2}<2)
, where
\mathbb{I}
is the indicator function. One can then try to argue that the
\inf_{\rho\in\mathcal{S}_{{+}}}E(\rho)

is achieved for
\rho
of the following form:

\rho(x+iy)=g(y)\delta(x)+\frac{1}{2\pi}\mathbb{I}(0\leq x\leq f(y)).

(233)

Note that while the function
Eq.

233
is not continuous, it can be approximated by
a continuous function in
\mathcal{S}_{{+}}
as soon as
f
and
g
are non-negative continuous functions with compact support and

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(g(y)+\frac{f(y)}{2\pi}\right)dy=1.

(234)

For such
\rho
we have

E(\rho)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}dy_{1}\Biggl(\frac{f(y_{1})^{3}}{12\pi}+\frac{y_{1}^{2}f(y_{1})}{4\pi}+g(y_{1})\frac{y_{1}^{2}}{2}-\\
\int_{\mathbb{R}}dy_{2}\biggl(g(y_{1})g(y_{2})\ln\left|y_{1}-y_{2}\right|/2+\\
\frac{1}{2\pi}g(y_{2})I_{1}(f(y_{1}),\left|y_{2}-y_{1}\right|)+\\
\frac{1}{8\pi^{2}}I_{2}(f(y_{1}),f(y_{2}),y_{2}-y_{1})\biggr)\Biggr),

(235)

where

\displaystyle I_{1}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{x}dx_{1}\ln\left|x_{1}+iy\right|,

(236a)

\displaystyle I_{2}(x_{1},x_{2},y)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{x_{1}}dx_{3}\int_{0}^{x_{2}}dx_{4}\ln\left|x_{2}-x_{4}+iy\right|.

(236b)

One can then solve this optimization problem numerically (or attempt to find an analytical solution) to find
\theta^{\text{GinOE}}
.
