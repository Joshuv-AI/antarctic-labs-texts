A New Matrix Truncation Method for Improving Approximate Factorisation Preconditioners
Andreas A. Bock, Martin S. Andersen

Abstract
In this experimental work, we present a general framework based on the Bregman log determinant divergence for preconditioning Hermitian positive definite linear systems. We explore this divergence as a measure of discrepancy between a preconditioner and a matrix. Given an approximate factorisation of a given matrix, the proposed framework informs the construction of a low-rank approximation of the typically indefinite factorisation error. The resulting preconditioner is therefore a sum of a Hermitian positive definite matrix given by an approximate factorisation plus a low-rank matrix. Notably, the low-rank term is not generally obtained as a truncated singular value decomposition (TSVD). This framework leads to a new truncation where principal directions are not based on the magnitude of the singular values, and we prove that such truncations are minimisers of the aforementioned divergence. We present several numerical examples showing that the proposed preconditioner can reduce the number of PCG iterations compared to a preconditioner constructed using a TSVD for the same rank. We also propose a heuristic to approximate the proposed preconditioner in the case where exact truncations cannot be computed explicitly (e.g. in a large-scale setting) and demonstrate its effectiveness over TSVD-based approaches.



Keywords—
preconditioner, low-rank approximation, Bregman log determinant divergence.

(1)

\|x-x_{k}\|_{S}\leq 2\Big(\frac{\sqrt{\kappa_{2}(S)}-1}{\sqrt{\kappa_{2}(S)}+1}\Big)^{k}\|x-x_{0}\|_{S},

for some initial guess

In this paper, we study a different nearness measure for low-rank approximations,

Definition 1
.

The Bregman matrix divergence

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\phi}(X,Y)

\displaystyle=\phi(X)-\phi(Y)-\langle\nabla\phi(Y),X-Y\rangle.

The log determinant matrix divergence, induced by

\mathcal{D}_{\textnormal{LD}}(X,Y)=\Tr\big(XY^{-1}\big)-\log\det(XY^{-1})-n,

A key property of the Bregman log determinant divergence

and
invertible

\mathcal{D}_{\textnormal{LD}}(X,Y)=\mathcal{D}_{\textnormal{LD}}(QXQ^{*},QYQ^{*}).

(4)

This invariance is central to our contribution in this paper. Indeed, we
can write the matrix

from

1

S=Q(I+\tilde{E})Q^{*},\hskip 9.24994pt\text{where}\hskip 9.24994pt\tilde{E}=Q^{-1}EQ^{-*}.

This leads us to study the candidate preconditioners of the form

P=Q(I+W)Q^{*}\in{\mathbb{H}^{n}_{++}},\hskip 9.24994pt\rank W\leq r,\hskip 9.24994ptW\in\mathbb{H}^{n},

(6)

\mathcal{D}_{\textnormal{LD}}(S,P)=\mathcal{D}_{\textnormal{LD}}(I+\tilde{E},I+W).

The aim is to determine the variable

by minimising the divergence above
over a suitable set of low-rank matrices. It is not known which rank

This leads to the main contribution of this work. We will show that such

]
, and it is not always clear which

[

introduced the

We refer to the recent work
[

]
for overviews of relevant

The design of preconditioners using a combination of approximate factorisations
and low-rank approximations has been studied before in

In this reference the authors assume that

\llbracket X\rrbracket_{r}=\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.49998pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}X\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.49998pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}_{r}=\{\mskip-5.0mu\{X\}\mskip-5.0mu\}_{r}
.

Proof.

The eigenvalues of
I+\tilde{X}
are bounded from below by
1
when
X\in{\mathbb{H}^{n}_{+}}

and since
\gamma
(resp.
\nu
) is an increasing function on
[1,\infty)

the index set
\mathcal{I}_{X}^{\star}
(resp.
\mathcal{J}_{X}^{\star}
)
constructed in
Equation

19
(resp.
Equation

23
)
selects the same
r

\llbracket X\rrbracket_{r}=\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.49998pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}X\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.49998pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}_{r}
.

\nu
is an increasing function on
[1,\infty)
, so
\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.49998pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}X\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.49998pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}_{r}=\{\mskip-5.0mu\{X\}\mskip-5.0mu\}_{r}

since

∎

3
A preconditioner with Bregman log determinant truncation

We propose the preconditioner

{S}_{r}^{\textnormal{Br}}=Q(I+\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.49998pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\tilde{E}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.49998pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}_{r})Q^{*}

(25)

based on a BLD truncation of
\tilde{E}
. As we show next, this preconditioner
is a solution to an optimisation problem.

Theorem 2
.

{S}_{r}^{\textnormal{Br}}
is a minimiser of

\displaystyle\minimise_{P\in{\mathbb{H}^{n}_{++}}}\hskip 9.24994pt

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\textnormal{LD}}(S,P)

(26a)

\displaystyle\subto\hskip 9.24994pt

\displaystyle P=Q(I+W)Q^{*},

(26b)

\displaystyle W\in\mathbb{H}^{n},

(26c)

\displaystyle\rank(W)=r.

(26d)

Proof.

Since the
\mathcal{D}_{\textnormal{LD}}
is invariant to congruence transformations,

\mathcal{D}_{\textnormal{LD}}(S,P)=\mathcal{D}_{\textnormal{LD}}(I+\tilde{E},I+W),

where
\tilde{E}=Q^{-1}EQ^{-*}
. The result follows from

Theorem

1
.
∎

Remark 1
.

Obviously, the (reverse) Bregman truncation is exact if
\rank(\tilde{E})\leq r
. This highlights two interesting aspects of designing a preconditioner
for
S
within the framework presented here. Suppose we choose
Q
as an
incomplete Cholesky factor of
S
. A fill-reducing algorithm can be used
in such a procedure, in which case the factors are typically given up to
a permutation matrix
\mathcal{P}

Q=\mathcal{P}^{-1}C,

which balances numerical accuracy against sparsity of
Q
. The permutation
matrix
\mathcal{P}
will have an effect on
\tilde{E}
(and its truncations).
Indeed, rank is not well-behaved under addition in the sense that for two
Hermitian full rank matrices:

0\leq\rank(\tilde{E})=\rank(S-QQ^{*})\leq n.

When applying the proposed framework, it may therefore also be of interest
to consider the interplay between
\mathcal{P}
and the rank of
\tilde{E}
.
Roughly speaking, we therefore have three objectives: numerical
stability of
Q
, its structure or size (e.g. sparsity) and a reduction in
the rank of the term
S-QQ^{*}
.

We define a preconditioner analogous to
Equation

25

but using the RBLD truncation in
Definition

3
.

{S}_{r}^{\textnormal{RBr}}=Q(I+\{\mskip-5.0mu\{\tilde{E}\}\mskip-5.0mu\}_{r})Q^{*}.

(27)

Theorem 3
.

{S}_{r}^{\textnormal{RBr}}
is a minimiser of

\displaystyle\minimise_{P\in{\mathbb{H}^{n}_{++}}}\hskip 9.24994pt

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\textnormal{LD}}(P,S)

(28a)

\displaystyle\subto\hskip 9.24994pt

\displaystyle P=Q(I+W)Q^{*},

(28b)

\displaystyle W\in\mathbb{H}^{n},

(28c)

\displaystyle\rank(W)=r.

(28d)

Proof.

The proof is similar to that of
Theorem

2
.
∎

We show empirically how these preconditioners perform in
Section

6.1

for modestly-sized matrices and develop some intuition for when they may differ from a
preconditioner based on a TSVD as in
Equation

9
.
The preconditioners in
Equations

25
and
27

are expensive to construct in general, in particular for large matrices.
In
Section

4
, we describe approximate truncations and analyse the
resulting preconditioners in
Section

5
(computational cost, storage,
application, etc.).

4
Approximating the Bregman log determinant truncation

The aim in this section is to present a heuristic to approximate
the BLD and RBLD truncations from
Section

2
.

We can regard
\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.49998pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\tilde{E}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.49998pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}_{r}
as consisting of two terms stemming
from the positive and negative semi-definite parts of
\tilde{E}
that they
approximate. This can be viewed as splitting the "budget"
r
into two
positive (and generally unknown) integers

r=r^{\oplus}+r^{\ominus},

(29)

so that we can write a BLD truncation of
\tilde{E}
as

\displaystyle\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.49998pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\tilde{E}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.49998pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}_{r}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{r^{\oplus}}\theta_{i}v_{i}v_{i}^{*}+\sum_{i=n-r^{\ominus}+1}^{n}\theta_{i}v_{i}v_{i}^{*}=:\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.49998pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\tilde{E}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.49998pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}_{r}^{\oplus}+\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.49998pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\tilde{E}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.49998pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}_{r}^{\ominus}.

(30)

The main goal of this section is to describe how to approximate these
matrices, which subsequently give rise to the preconditioner analysed
in
Section

5
.

As mentioned, the values of
r^{\oplus}
and
r^{\ominus}
in
Equation

29
are not
known beforehand, so we introduce the parameter
\alpha\in[0,1]
and, with a
slight abuse of notation, define the integers

\displaystyle r^{\oplus}(\alpha)

\displaystyle:=\lfloor\alpha r\rfloor,

(31a)

\displaystyle r^{\ominus}(\alpha)

\displaystyle:=r-r^{\oplus}(\alpha),

(31b)

representing the proportion of the rank
r
budget to be allocated to
approximating the largest positive part of the spectrum of
\tilde{E}
.
For a given
\alpha
, we define the approximations
\tilde{E}_{r^{\oplus}}(\alpha)
and

\tilde{E}_{r^{\ominus}}(\alpha)

\displaystyle\tilde{E}_{r^{\oplus}}(\alpha)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{i=1}^{r^{\oplus}(\alpha)}\theta_{i}v_{i}v_{i}^{*},

\displaystyle\tilde{E}_{r^{\ominus}}(\alpha)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{i=n-r^{\ominus}(\alpha)+1}^{n}\theta_{i}v_{i}v_{i}^{*},

and set

\tilde{E}_{r}(\alpha):=\tilde{E}_{r^{\oplus}}(\alpha)+\tilde{E}_{r^{\ominus}}(\alpha).

(32)

In general, the values of
\alpha
for which
\tilde{E}_{r}(\alpha)=\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.49998pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\tilde{E}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.49998pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}_{r}
or
\tilde{E}_{r}(\alpha)=\llbracket\tilde{E}\rrbracket_{r}
are not
known. We return to this in
Section

6.2
.
What remains of this section is dedicated to describing how the two terms in
the approximation
Equation

32
can be computed in practice.
The construction of
\tilde{E}_{r^{\oplus}}(\alpha)
and
\tilde{E}_{r^{\ominus}}(\alpha)

will be discussed in
Section

4.1
and

Section

4.2
, respectively.

4.1
Estimating the largest eigenvalues

In this section, we describe how to compute approximations of the matrix

\tilde{E}_{r^{\oplus}}(\alpha)
in
Equation

32
which represent
the
r^{\oplus}(\alpha)
largest positive eigenvalues of
\tilde{E}
. Note that, by definition,

\tilde{E}=Q^{-1}SQ^{-*}-I,

(33)

so once
\alpha
is prescribed, we can compute only with the HPD matrix

Q^{-1}SQ^{-*}
in practice.
Instead, we estimate the largest eigenvalues of
Q^{-1}SQ^{-*}
using
a Krylov–Schur method
[
38
]
, which only requires
matrix-vector products with
Q^{-1}
,
Q^{-*}
and
S
. This
method is also used in
Matlab
2023b’s implementation of
eigs
.
Since
S
is symmetric, this is a special case of the
thick-restart
Lanczos method
of
[
42
]
. This method provides a matrix

H^{\oplus}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times r^{\oplus}(\alpha)}
and a diagonal matrix

D^{\oplus}\in\mathbb{R}^{r^{\oplus}(\alpha)\times r^{\oplus}(\alpha)}
approximating
the largest eigenvalues of
Q^{-1}SQ^{-*}
.

In view of
Equation

33
, we therefore use the following approximation in

Section

6.2
:

\tilde{E}_{r^{\oplus}}(\alpha):=H^{\oplus}\big(D^{\oplus}-I\big)(H^{\oplus})^{*}.

(34)

4.2
Smallest eigenvalues

Approximating
\tilde{E}_{r^{\ominus}}(\alpha)
in

Equation

32
is tantamount to approximating the eigenspaces
of
Q^{-1}SQ^{-*}
associated with its smallest eigenvalues.
These can be estimated using a
shift-and-invert

power method
[
19
]
which, for some
\eta>0
requires
the solution of linear systems of equations involving the matrix
\eta I-Q^{-1}SQ^{-*}
. This can be as expensive as solving the original
problem
Equation

1
to begin with. Provided an estimate
\eta
of
the largest eigenvalue
\theta_{1}
of
Q^{-1}SQ^{-*}
such that

\eta\geq\theta_{1}
, the matrix

\eta I-Q^{-1}SQ^{-*}

(35)

is positive semi-definite. The eigenvalues
\theta_{1}\geq\ldots\geq\theta_{n}
of
\tilde{E}
satisfy

\theta_{n-i+1}=\eta-\lambda_{i}(\eta I-Q^{-1}SQ^{-*})-1,\hskip 9.24994pti=1,\ldots n,

(36)

where
\lambda_{i}(\eta I-Q^{-1}SQ^{-*})
denotes the
i^{\textnormal{th}}

largest eigenvalue of
\eta I-Q^{-1}SQ^{-*}
.
We can therefore estimate the smallest negative eigenvalues of
\tilde{E}

by computing the largest eigenvalues of the shifted matrix

Equation

35
, which obviates the need for a shift-and-invert
approach.

The aim is to solve the eigenvalue
problem

\big(\eta I-Q^{-1}SQ^{*}\big)x=\lambda x,

for the
r^{\ominus}(\alpha)
largest eigenvalues and eigenvectors. We can then
compute
\tilde{E}_{r^{\ominus}}(\alpha)
via
Equation

36
. The Krylov–Schur
method depends on several parameters; the maxmimum size of the subspace,
number of iterations and a tolerance at which eigenvalues are deemed to have
converged. We comment on these in
Section

6.2
.

The strategy for computing
\tilde{E}_{r^{\ominus}}(\alpha)
is summarised below.

1.

Estimate
\eta\approx\theta_{1}
, which may already be available from
\tilde{E}_{r^{\oplus}}(\alpha)
(or can be computed using the steps in
Section

4.1
).

2.

Compute
H^{\ominus}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times r^{\ominus}(\alpha)}
with orthonormal columns and a diagonal matrix
D^{\ominus}\in\mathbb{R}^{r^{\ominus}(\alpha)\times r^{\ominus}(\alpha)}
representing the largest eigenvalues of
\eta I-Q^{-1}SQ^{*}
using the Krylov–Schur method mentioned above. This means that
H
and
D
approximately satisfy

H^{\ominus}D^{\ominus}(H^{\ominus})^{*}\approx\llbracket\eta I-\tilde{E}\rrbracket_{r}.

3.

Undo the shift in
Equation

36
and set

\tilde{E}_{r^{\ominus}}(\alpha):=H^{\ominus}\Big((\eta-1)I-D^{\ominus}\Big)(H^{\ominus})^{*}.

In principle, a Nyström approximation
[
31
]
could also be used in step 2, but we have
found that it does not result in good approximations of
\llbracket\eta I-\tilde{E}\rrbracket_{r}
.
We believe this is because the gap between its largest eigenvalues (resp. the smallest
eigenvalues of
\tilde{E}
) is small owing to
Equation

14
and
therefore makes it difficult to compute them accurately.
The choice of method used in step 2 depends on the specific matrix
S
and the
level of fill in the approximate factor
Q
. The same applies to the
approximation in
Section

4.1
, which enters the approximation
of
\tilde{E}_{r^{\ominus}}(\alpha)
through
\eta
.

5
Approximating the proposed preconditioner

Equation

32
introduced
\tilde{E}_{r}(\alpha)=\tilde{E}_{r^{\oplus}}(\alpha)+\tilde{E}_{r^{\ominus}}(\alpha)
as a way of approximating
\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.49998pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\tilde{E}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.49998pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}_{r}
as a function
of
\alpha\in[0,1]
. An approximation of the proposed preconditioner
in
Equation

25
is therefore given by

{S}_{r}^{\textnormal{Br}}({\alpha})=Q(I+\tilde{E}_{r}(\alpha))Q^{*}.

(37)

In practice, the matrix in
Equation

37
is not
formed explicitly, and only the applications of
Q^{-1}
, its Hermitian
adjoint, and
(I+\tilde{E}_{r}(\alpha))^{-1}
are necessary. Given a spectral
decomposition of
\tilde{E}_{r}(\alpha)
, the cost of applying the latter is

O(nr)
by the Woodbury matrix identity. As a result, applying the preconditioner
to a vector costs

O(\pi_{Q}+nr).

Cost of storage and construction are linked through the choice of
Q
. A sparse
approximation
QQ^{*}
to
S
may reduce storage requirements and time
to apply the inverse of
Q
(and its Hermitian adjoint), possibly at the expense
of a larger error
\tilde{E}
. For instance, if
Q
were to be computed as an
incomplete Cholesky with a non-zero drop threshold (i.e. entries of the Cholesky
factors of
S
above this threshold are included in
Q
), then we would expect
a better approximate factorisation for smaller values of such a tolerance at
the cost of potentially denser factors. We therefore expect to pay for better
approximations
QQ^{*}\approx S
by increasing storage cost through
Q
,
which possibly reduces both the magnitude of the extremal eigenvalues of
\tilde{E}

and its rank. Irrespective of
\alpha
, the preconditioner requires storage of

O(\texttt{nnz}(Q)+nr)
.

.
If the numerical rank of
\tilde{E}
is large, we may also require a large

r
in the preconditioner
Equation

37
.
Even if the numerical rank of
\tilde{E}
is small, there is no guarantee
that the largest positive and smallest negative eigenvalues are small in magnitude.
Roughly speaking, a steep slope in the spectrum can make it easier for Krylov–Schur
to converge, as the largest eigenvalues are more isolated from the rest of the spectrum.
Knowledge of the spectrum of
\tilde{E}
can therefore inform the choice of the
method used to construct
\tilde{E}_{r^{\oplus}}(\alpha)
.

\alpha
also affects construction costs through
Equation

31
.
We expect that it is easier and cheaper to compute
\tilde{E}_{r^{\oplus}}(\alpha)
than

\tilde{E}_{r^{\ominus}}(\alpha)
, but it depends on the spectrum, as mentioned above.
Its construction costs
O(r^{3})
operations due to the inverse needed to apply
(I+\tilde{E}_{r}(\alpha))^{-1}
in excess of costs
discussed in
Sections

4.1
and
4.2
.

In summary, if
S
is large and sparse, a good candidate
Q
for use in

Equation

37
keeps both the numerical rank of

\tilde{E}
small and reduces the condition number of
Q^{-1}SQ^{-*}
.
From what precedes, we know that the Bregman log determinant divergence takes on
large values for small negative eigenvalues of
\tilde{E}
close to
-1
(cf.

Figures

1
and
15
). As a
result, an approximate factorisation should try to eliminate these from the
error so that the
\tilde{E}
is "as close to" positive semi-definite as possible.
In this case, we will expect the Nyström approximation to perform well and be
a good alternative to the Krylov–Schur method mentioned above. We describe this
method in more detail in the next section.
The authors are not aware of methods of generating
Q
that can control the
inertia of the
S-QQ^{*}
, although this would of course
be of interest.

5.1
Randomised methods

Another option to approximate
\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.49998pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\tilde{E}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\mkern 2.0mu\kern-3.49998pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}_{r}
is to adopt a randomised
approach. Randomised linear algebra
[
23
]
encompasses a popular
family of algorithms that can yield good low-rank approximations. These are
particularly useful when it is prohibitively expensive to compute a truncated
SVD directly, since they only require the action of a matrix. The
randomised
SVD

[
31
, Algorithm 8]
(RSVD) is applicable to general
square matrices
X
of order
n
. To construct a RSVD,
X
is first
sketched

by multiplication with an
n\times r
matrix
\Omega
with e.g. normal Gaussian
i.i.d. entries:

Y=X\Omega.

(38)

A reduced
QR
decomposition of
Y
yields a
n\times r
matrix
\mathcal{O}

with orthonormal columns whose range approximates that of
X
. Using

Matlab
-style notation, we can compute a reduced SVD

\mathbf{\hat{U}}\mathbf{\Sigma}\mathbf{V}^{*}=\texttt{svd\_econ}(X,\,r).

Setting
\mathbf{U}:=\mathcal{O}\mathbf{\hat{U}}
we obtain the approximation

X\approx\mathbf{U}\mathbf{\Sigma}\mathbf{V}^{*}.

We denote by
\pi_{X}
the cost of computing matrix-vector products with
X
.
The randomised SVD comes at a cost of
O(\pi_{X}r+nr^{2})
which is an
improvement on the naïve
O(n^{3})
cost of truncating a full SVD.

For Hermitian matrices, one of the most popular methods in randomised linear
algebra is the
Nyström
approximation

[
18
,
15
,
34
]
. We
define the rank
r
Nyström approximation of
X\in\mathbb{H}^{n}
with respect to a
Gaussian sketching matrix
\Omega\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times k}
,
k\geq r
:

[
40
]

{X}^{\text{Nys}}\langle\Omega\rangle:=X\Omega\llbracket\Omega^{*}X\Omega\rrbracket_{r}^{+}(X\Omega)^{*}.

(39)

The cost of this approximation is
O(\pi_{X}k+k^{3})

in construction cost and requires only
k

products with the matrix. This approximation can be derived as range-restricted
matrix approximation that is optimal in the Bregman divergence

[
7
]
which corresponds to a single iteration of
Bregman’s algorithm for rank-deficient matrices with no constraints

[
30
, Section 4.3]
. The approaches above can be combined with
oversampling and power iterations, and can also be adapted to use only a single
"view" of the matrix via
Equation

38
. See

[
31
,
23
]
for comprehensive reviews.

Modifications can be made to the Nyström approximation in order
to produce a numerically robust procedure for indefinite Hermitian matrices

[
32
]
. It is similar
to
Equation

39
, where the sketching matrix
\Omega
is a
n\times cr

matrix for some appropriate oversampling
c>1
(e.g.
c=1.5
,
c=2
). We
repeat the definition for convenience:

{X}^{\text{Indef}}\langle\Omega\rangle:=X\Omega\llbracket\Omega^{*}X\Omega\rrbracket_{r}^{+}(X\Omega)^{*}.

(40)

By more generous oversampling and truncating in the inner
matrix,
\llbracket\Omega^{*}X\Omega\rrbracket_{r}
we avoid taking the inverse of very
small eigenvalues, which could blow up and render the approximation

{X}^{\text{Indef}}\langle\Omega\rangle
inaccurate. In
Section

6.2
,
a comparison will be made between a preconditioner based on this approximation
along with a numerical study of how different choices of
\alpha
in

Equation

37
affects the performance of PCG.

deteriorates as
\alpha

capturing the eigensubspace of the error
\tilde{E}
corresponding to the
smallest eigenvalues can be valuable over the that with largest singular values, which
aligns well with our observations from

There are also several cases where the opposite is true and
{S}_{r}^{\textnormal{KrySVD}}

outperforms

case where

{S}_{r}^{\textnormal{Br}}({\alpha})\approx{S}_{r}^{\textnormal{KrySVD}}.

The results, however, suggest that there may be significant gains in allocating
computational budget towards other truncations than a TSVD, although it is difficult
to determine an appropriate value of
\alpha
in such cases.

Overall, the preconditioners
{S}_{r}^{\textnormal{Br}}({\alpha})
and
{S}_{r}^{\textnormal{KrySVD}}

{S}_{r}^{\textnormal{KrySVD}}
and
{S}_{r}^{\textnormal{Br}}({\alpha})

\tilde{E}
may be a dense matrix (but is never formed), the
application of
\tilde{E}
to a vector may be cheap if both triangular solves with

Q
can be done efficiently and the matrix
S
is sparse. The computation of

{S}_{r}^{\textnormal{Br}}({\alpha})
can therefore be fast for certain instances.
Construction time of the
{S}_{r}^{\textnormal{Br}}({\alpha})
increases with the number
of required matrix-vector products, which for small
\alpha
generally is larger than
if
\alpha
is small. There is an obvious trade-off between PCG convergence and the
time spent on the construction of
{S}_{r}^{\textnormal{Br}}({\alpha})
, although the
interplay between
r
and
\alpha
is not known in general.
If a given application requires multiple solves with the same matrix, then the
cost of construction of the preconditioner
{S}_{r}^{\textnormal{Br}}({\alpha})
is amortised
and therefore may be an attractive option.

Empirically, it appears
that
{S}_{r}^{\textnormal{Br}}({\alpha})

can reduce the number of PCG iterations, but this depends on the spectrum of
\tilde{E}
.
Knowledge of the spectrum, together with the overall computational budget (rank
r
,
fill of
Q
, cost of matrix-vector products and access model of
S
), informs the
value of
\alpha
.

Summary & outlook

We have presented preconditioners based on low-rank truncations constructed as
minimisers of the Bregman log determinant divergence.
The motivation for this work was to systematically account for factorisation errors

S-QQ^{*}
when designing preconditioners for the iterative solution of
linear systems
Equation

1
involving the matrix
S
.
The proposed preconditioners are intended for "black box" computations, i.e. they do
not rely on any particular structure of
S
.

In
Section

2
, we showed how the Bregman log determinant divergence
generally leads to principal directions of a matrix that are different from a TSVD, and

Section

3
presented the associated preconditioner. We proposed an approximation
of the BLD truncation in
Section

4
based on a combination of the
Nyström approximation and a Krylov–Schur method and analysed the resulting preconditioner
in
Section

5
.

Section

6
contained numerous numerical examples comparing the exact
BLD truncations to an TSVD-based preconditioner (
Section

6.1
)
as well as demonstrating the effectiveness of approximations to our preconditioner in

Section

6.2
.

Our results depend heavily on the choice of factor
Q
and how closely

QQ^{*}
resembles the target matrix
S
(cf. the discussion in

Section

5
), and approximate factorisations come in many
variants
[
36
]
. For instance, off-diagonal entries of a
Cholesky factorisation that are below a certain threshold can be set to zero in
the factorisation to force a degree of sparsity. An advantage of the framework
is that while storage costs of
Q
may be unpredictable as a function of drop
tolerance, the
nr
storage of the low-rank compensation term is deterministic.
For a given problem, it may be the case that specialised procedures alleviate
the need for compensation using a low-rank matrix. However, this may not be true
in general, and our results provide a novel framework for this should it be necessary.

In any case, it can be desirable to
construct
Q
in a way that ensures positive semi-definiteness of
\tilde{E}

leading to the preconditioner
{S}_{r}^{\textnormal{SVD}}
conceived in

[
7
]
, which is much easier to approximate using
the Nyström approximation when
\tilde{E}
is positive semi-definite. Another advantage
is that sketching can easily be parallelised, whereas it is not obvious to do so
for the Krylov–Schur method adopted above.
Investigating sparse approximate inverse preconditioners

[
6
]
could also be valuable in this context.
This relates to a major disadvantage of the proposed preconditioner. An
appropriate choice of rank
r
that balances computational cost of the
associated truncations with any gain related to convergence of PCG is not
known beforehand, and, as the experiments, show depends heavily on the
spectrum of
\tilde{E}
, which in turn depends on
Q
. A thorough
computational study comparing the preconditioners above to other
preconditioners in the literature is also necessary.

Theory is necessary to fully understand the nature of the truncations,
connections to PCG, and the results in
Section

6
.
The experiments indicate that it is not always preferable to select
truncations based on the largest singular values when considering preconditioners
for iterative solvers. One objective would be to understand the difference
between
{S}_{r}^{\textnormal{Br}}
and
{S}_{r}^{\textnormal{RBr}}
, which is given by their
respective truncations of the error
\tilde{E}
. Using similar approaches
to
[
24
]
, we leave a more detailed theoretical and numerical
study on how the choice of
Q
affects the proposed preconditioner as future
work.

As alluded to in
Remark

1
, investigating the effect of permutations
on the preconditioner in line with
[
16
]
could also be interesting.
When
Q=\mathcal{P}^{-1}C
is defined in terms of a fill-reducing permutation,
a reduction of the rank of
S-\mathcal{P}^{-1}CC^{*}\mathcal{P}^{-\top}

could be sought as part of the approximate factorisation. We can express this
problem as follows, where
\mathcal{S}_{n}
is the group of symmetric permutations:

\minimise_{P\in\mathcal{S}_{n}}\hskip 9.24994pt\rank(S-\mathcal{P}^{-1}CC^{*}\mathcal{P}^{-\top}).

(43)

Using the Ky-Fan norm, we can pose a convex relaxation of
Equation

43

\displaystyle\minimise_{P\in\mathcal{S}_{n}}\hskip 9.24994pt

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{i}(S-\mathcal{P}^{-1}CC^{*}\mathcal{P}^{-\top}),

the solution of which is subject to future research. Chordal completions could also
be relevant to the problem at hand. Suppose
QQ^{*}
has a chordal
sparsity pattern given by the adjacency matrix
E
, then there exists a
perturbation matrix
P
for which
PAP^{\top}
has a zero fill Cholesky
factorisation. Finding minimal fill of a factorisation can therefore be
viewed as a minimum chordal completion problem
[
41
]
.
