On a result by Baillon, Bruck, and Reich
Heinz H. Bauschke and Yuan Gao

Abstract
It is well known that the iterates of an averaged nonexpansive mapping may only converge weakly to fixed point. A celebrated result by Baillon, Bruck, and Reich from 1978 yields strong convergence in the presence of linearity. In this paper, we extend this result to allow for flexible relaxation parameters. Examples are also provided to illustrate the results.

1
Introduction

Throughout, we assume that

X
is
a real Hilbert space

(1)

with inner product

\left\langle{\cdot},{\cdot}\right\rangle\colon X\times X\to\mathbb{R}
,
and induced norm
\|\cdot\|
.
We also throughout assume that

R\colon X\to X
is nonexpansive,

(2)

i.e.,
(\forall x\in X)(\forall y\in X)

\|Rx-Ry\|\leq\|x-y\|
, and with a nonempty
fixed point set

\operatorname{Fix}R=\big\{{x\in X}~\big|~{Rx=x}\big\}\neq\varnothing.

(3)

Finding a point in
\operatorname{Fix}R
is a basic task in optimization and
variational analysis because the solutions to many optimization problems can often be understood as fixed point sets of nonexpansive mappings; see, e.g.,
[
3
]
.
To find a point in
\operatorname{Fix}R
, one employs fixed point iterations.
Iterating
R
is not guaranteed to work as the case
R=-\operatorname{Id}
shows.
However, iterating
underrelaxations
of
R
is a successful strategy as Krasnosel’skiĭ
[
10
]
and Mann
[
11
]
demonstrated.
Many extensions (see the recent monograph
[
8
]
) exist; here, we present here one that is quite flexible and based upon a parameter sequence

(\lambda_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\text{~in $\mathbb{R}$,}

(4)

which we fix from now on.
Given
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, we set

T_{\lambda}:=(1-\lambda)\operatorname{Id}+\lambda R

(5)

or
T_{\lambda,R}
if we need to stress
R
.
This allows us to concisely describe the following
result:

Fact 1.1

(Reich)
.

Suppose that
\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}(1-\lambda_{n})\lambda_{n}=+\infty

and let
x_{0}\in X
.
Then the sequence generated by

(\forall{n\in{\mathbb{N}}})\hskip 9.24994ptx_{n+1}:=T_{\lambda_{n}}x_{n}

(6)

converges
weakly
to a point in
\operatorname{Fix}R
.
Moreover,
x_{n}-Rx_{n}\to 0
and
(x_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}

is Fejér monotone with respect to
\operatorname{Fix}R
.

Proof
. This is
[
12
, Theorem 2]
. (See also
[
3
, Theorem 5.15]
.)

\hfill\hskip 9.24994pt\blacksquare

In contrast,
strong
convergence and identification of the limit is possible when
R
is
linear
but the parameter sequence is
constant
.

Fact 1.2

(Baillon-Bruck-Reich)
.

Suppose that
R
is linear and that
\lambda\in\left]0,1\right[
.
Let
x_{0}\in X
.

(\forall{n\in{\mathbb{N}}})\hskip 9.24994ptx_{n+1}:=T_{\lambda}x_{n}

(7)

Then

x_{n}\to P_{\operatorname{Fix}R}x_{0}.

(8)

Proof
. This is
[
3
, Example 5.29]
; however,
the main ideas of the proof are in

[
1
]
and
[
7
]
.

\hfill\hskip 9.24994pt\blacksquare

We are now ready to present our main result, which substantially generalizes
Fact

1.2
and which we will prove in
Section

2
:

Theorem 1.3

(main result)
.

Suppose that
R
is linear and that there exists

\varepsilon>0
such that

(\forall{n\in{\mathbb{N}}})\hskip 9.24994pt\varepsilon\leq\lambda_{n}\leq 1-\varepsilon.

(9)

Let
x_{0}\in X
and generate the sequence
(x_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
by

(\forall{n\in{\mathbb{N}}})\hskip 9.24994ptx_{n+1}:=T_{\lambda_{n}}x_{n}.

(10)

Then

x_{n}\to P_{\operatorname{Fix}R}x_{0}.

(11)

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows.
In
Section

2
, we provide the proof of
Theorem

1.3
.
Variants of
Theorem

1.3
are discussed in
Section

3
.
The notation we employ in this paper is fairly standard and
follows largely
[
3
]
.

2
Proof of the main result

From now on, we additionally assume that

R
is linear and
R\neq\operatorname{Id}
.

(12)

The idea for the next result can be traced back to a paper by Gearhard and Koshy
(see
[
9
, Acceleration 3.2]
and also
[
4
]
):

Proposition 2.1
.

Suppose that
x\in X\smallsetminus\operatorname{Fix}R
.
Set

\lambda_{x}:=\left\langle{x},{x-Rx}\right\rangle/\|x-Rx\|^{2}.

(13)

Then
\lambda_{x}\geq\tfrac{1}{2}
.
Let
\varepsilon\in\bigl]0,\tfrac{1}{2}\bigr]
.
Then

[\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon]\subseteq[\varepsilon,2\lambda_{x}-\varepsilon]\hskip 9.24994pt\text{and}\hskip 9.24994pt(\forall\lambda\in[\varepsilon,2\lambda_{x}-\varepsilon])\;\;\|T_{\lambda_{x}}x\|\leq\|T_{\lambda}x\|\leq\|T_{\varepsilon}x\|.

(14)

Proof
. Recalling
5
, we define the quadratic
f
by

\displaystyle f(\lambda)

\displaystyle:=\|T_{\lambda}x\|^{2}=\|x+\lambda(Rx-x)\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\lambda^{2}\|x-Rx\|^{2}+2\lambda\left\langle{x},{Rx-x}\right\rangle+\|x\|^{2}.

Completing the square yields

f(\lambda)=\|x-Rx\|^{2}\Big(\lambda-\frac{\left\langle{x},{x-Rx}\right\rangle}{\|x-Rx\|^{2}}\Big)^{2}+\|x\|^{2}-\frac{\left\langle{x},{x-Rx}\right\rangle^{2}}{\|x-Rx\|^{2}}.

Hence the unique minimizer of
f
is
\lambda_{x}
and

\min f(\mathbb{R})=\|x\|^{2}-\left\langle{x},{x-Rx}\right\rangle^{2}/\|x-Rx\|^{2}
.
Because
R
is nonexpansive and
0\in\operatorname{Fix}R
, we have

\|Rx\|\leq\|x\|

\Leftrightarrow

0\leq\|x\|^{2}-\|Rx\|^{2}=\left\langle{x+Rx},{x-Rx}\right\rangle=\left\langle{2x-(x-Rx)},{x-Rx}\right\rangle

\Leftrightarrow

2\left\langle{x},{x-Rx}\right\rangle\geq\|x-Rx\|^{2}

\Leftrightarrow

\lambda_{x}\geq\tfrac{1}{2}

as claimed.
This yields
2\lambda_{x}-\varepsilon\geq 1-\varepsilon
and so

[\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon]\subseteq[\varepsilon,2\lambda_{x}-\varepsilon]
, also as claimed.
On the other hand,

f^{\prime}(\lambda)=2\|x-Rx\|^{2}(\lambda-\lambda_{x})
.
Altogether,

f
is a convex quadratric,

f
strictly decreases on
\left]-\infty,\lambda_{x}\right]
,

f
strictly increases on
\left[\lambda_{x},+\infty\right[
, and

(\forall\delta\geq 0)

f(\lambda_{x}-\delta)=f(\lambda_{x}+\delta)
.
Finally, let
\lambda\in[\varepsilon,2\lambda_{x}-\varepsilon]

and set
\delta:=\lambda_{x}-\varepsilon\geq\tfrac{1}{2}-\varepsilon\geq 0
.
Then
\lambda_{x}-\delta=\varepsilon
,

\lambda_{x}+\delta=2\lambda_{x}-\varepsilon
,
and therefore

f(\lambda_{x})\leq f(\lambda)\leq f(\varepsilon)=f(2\lambda_{x}-\varepsilon)
.

\hfill\hskip 9.24994pt\blacksquare

From now on, we set

\overline{\lambda}:=\inf_{x\in X\smallsetminus\operatorname{Fix}R}\frac{\left\langle{x},{x-Rx}\right\rangle}{\|x-Rx\|^{2}}.

(15)

If we wish to stress
R
, we also write
\overline{\lambda}_{R}
for
\overline{\lambda}
.

Corollary 2.2
.

We have

\overline{\lambda}\geq\tfrac{1}{2}\;\;\text{and}\;\;\bigl(\forall\mu\in\bigl]0,\tfrac{1}{2}\bigr]\bigr)(\forall\lambda\in[\mu,2\overline{\lambda}-\mu])(\forall x\in X)\hskip 9.24994pt\|T_{\lambda}x\|\leq\|T_{\mu}x\|.

(16)

Proof
. Adopt the notation from
Proposition

2.1
.
Then
\overline{\lambda}=\inf_{x\in X\smallsetminus\operatorname{Fix}R}\lambda_{x}\geq\tfrac{1}{2}
by
Proposition

2.1
, and also

\overline{\lambda}<+\infty
.
Next, let
\mu\in\bigl]0,\tfrac{1}{2}\bigr]
,
let
\lambda\in[\mu,2\overline{\lambda}-\mu]
, and
let
x\in X
.
Then
\lambda\leq 2\lambda_{x}-\mu
and

Proposition

2.1
yields

\|T_{\lambda}x\|\leq\|T_{\mu}x\|
as claimed.

\hfill\hskip 9.24994pt\blacksquare

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
\lambda,\mu
be in
\mathbb{R}
.
Then

T_{\lambda}T_{\mu}=T_{\mu}T_{\lambda}.

(17)

Proof
. Indeed,

\displaystyle T_{\lambda}T_{\mu}

\displaystyle=\big((1-\lambda)\operatorname{Id}+\lambda R\big)\big(1-\mu)\operatorname{Id}+\mu R\big)

\displaystyle=(1-\lambda)(1-\mu)\operatorname{Id}+\big((1-\lambda)\mu+\lambda(1-\mu)\big)R+\lambda\mu R^{2}

\displaystyle=T_{\mu}T_{\lambda}

and we are done.

\hfill\hskip 9.24994pt\blacksquare

Proposition 2.4
.

Suppose
(\mu_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is a sequence in
[0,1]
such that

(\forall{n\in{\mathbb{N}}})

\|T_{\lambda_{n}}(\cdot)\|\leq\|T_{\mu_{n}}(\cdot)\|
.
Then

(\forall x_{0}\in X)(\forall y\in\operatorname{Fix}R)(\forall{n\in{\mathbb{N}}})\hskip 9.24994pt\big\|T_{\lambda_{n}}\cdots T_{\lambda_{1}}T_{\lambda_{0}}x_{0}-y\big\|\leq\big\|T_{\mu_{n}}\cdots T_{\mu_{1}}T_{\mu_{0}}x_{0}-y\big\|.

(18)

Proof
. Let
x_{0}\in X
,

y\in\operatorname{Fix}R
,
and
{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
.
Then

\displaystyle\big\|T_{\lambda_{n}}\cdots T_{\lambda_{1}}T_{\lambda_{0}}x_{0}-y\big\|

\displaystyle=\big\|T_{\lambda_{n}}\big(T_{\lambda_{n-1}}\cdots T_{\lambda_{1}}T_{\lambda_{0}}(x_{0}-y)\big)\big\|

(because
y\in\operatorname{Fix}R
)

\displaystyle\leq\big\|T_{\mu_{n}}\big(T_{\lambda_{n-1}}\cdots T_{\lambda_{1}}T_{\lambda_{0}}(x_{0}-y)\big)\big\|

(by assumption)

\displaystyle=\big\|T_{\lambda_{n-1}}\big(T_{\lambda_{n-2}}\cdots T_{\lambda_{1}}T_{\lambda_{0}}T_{\mu_{n}}(x_{0}-y)\big)\big\|

(by
Lemma

2.3
)

\displaystyle\leq\big\|T_{\mu_{n-1}}\big(T_{\lambda_{n-2}}\cdots T_{\lambda_{1}}T_{\lambda_{0}}T_{\mu_{n}}(x_{0}-y)\big)\big\|

(by assumption)

\displaystyle=\big\|T_{\lambda_{n-2}}\big(T_{\lambda_{n-3}}\cdots T_{\lambda_{1}}T_{\lambda_{0}}T_{\mu_{n}}T_{\mu_{n-1}}(x_{0}-y)\big)\big\|

(by
Lemma

2.3
)

\displaystyle\;\,\,\vdots

\displaystyle\leq\big\|T_{\mu_{n}}\cdots T_{\mu_{1}}T_{\mu_{0}}(x_{0}-y)\big\|

\displaystyle=\big\|T_{\mu_{n}}\cdots T_{\mu_{1}}T_{\mu_{0}}x_{0}-y\big\|

(because
y\in\operatorname{Fix}R
)

as claimed.

\hfill\hskip 9.24994pt\blacksquare

We now restate the main result (for the reader’s convenience) and prove it:

Theorem 2.5

(main result)
.

Suppose that there exists

\varepsilon>0
such that

(\forall{n\in{\mathbb{N}}})

\varepsilon\leq\lambda_{n}\leq 1-\varepsilon
.
Let
x_{0}\in X
and
generate the sequence
(x_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
by

(\forall{n\in{\mathbb{N}}})

x_{n+1}:=T_{\lambda_{n}}x_{n}
.
Then

x_{n}\to P_{\operatorname{Fix}R}x_{0}
.

Proof
. Applying
Corollary

2.2
with
\mu=\varepsilon
yields

(\forall{n\in{\mathbb{N}}})

\|T_{\lambda_{n}}(\cdot)\|\leq\|T_{\varepsilon}(\cdot)\|
.
Next, we apply
Proposition

2.4
with
y=P_{\operatorname{Fix}R}x_{0}
and

(\mu_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}=(\varepsilon)_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
to deduce that

(\forall{n\in{\mathbb{N}}})\hskip 9.24994pt\|x_{n}-P_{\operatorname{Fix}R}x_{0}\|\leq\|T_{\varepsilon}^{n+1}x_{0}-P_{\operatorname{Fix}R}x_{0}\|.

(19)

On the other hand,

T_{\varepsilon}^{n}x_{0}\to P_{\operatorname{Fix}R}x_{0}

(20)

by
Fact

1.2
.
The conclusion follows by combining
19
and
20
.

\hfill\hskip 9.24994pt\blacksquare

Remark 2.6
.

There are numerous papers that use
the Baillon-Bruck-Reich result (
Fact

1.2
).
Whenever this is the case, there is the potential to obtain a more powerful
result by using the more general
Theorem

1.3
instead.
For instance, in the recent paper
[
6
]
,
the authors study several recent splitting methods
applied to normal cone operators of closed linear subspaces. A key ingredient was to apply
Fact

1.2
to deduce

T_{\lambda}^{n}x_{0}\to P_{\operatorname{Fix}R}x_{0},

(21)

where
\lambda\in\left]0,1\right[
. A closer inspection of the proofs shows that one may instead work with flexible parameters such as those of
Theorem

1.3

and one thus obtains a more general result.

3
Variants

3.1
Averaged mappings

Recall that a nonexpansive mapping
S\colon X\to X
is
\kappa
-averaged,
if
S=(1-\kappa)\operatorname{Id}+\kappa N
for some nonexpansive mapping
N
and
\kappa\in[0,1]
. The number

\kappa(S):=\min\big\{{\kappa\in[0,1]}~\big|~{\text{$S$ is $\kappa$-averaged}}\big\}

(22)

is called the
modulus of averagedness
of
S
.
If
\kappa(S)<1
, then one says that
S
is averaged.
Recalling
15
, it follows from
[
2
, Lemma 2.1]

that

\overline{\lambda}_{R}=\inf_{x\in X\smallsetminus\operatorname{Fix}R}\frac{\left\langle{x},{x-Rx}\right\rangle}{\|x-Rx\|^{2}}=\frac{1}{2\sup_{x\in X\smallsetminus\operatorname{Fix}R}\frac{\|x-Rx\|^{2}}{2\left\langle{x},{x-Rx}\right\rangle}}=\frac{1}{2\kappa(R)}.

(23)

Hence we have the equivalence

\overline{\lambda}_{R}>\tfrac{1}{2}\;\;\Leftrightarrow\;\;\text{$R$ is \emph{averaged}.}

(24)

This allows us to derive the following variant of
Theorem

2.5
:

Theorem 3.1

(main result — averaged mapping version)
.

Suppose that
R
is
\kappa
-averaged for some
\kappa\in\left]0,1\right[
.
Suppose that
\delta>0
and that
(\mu_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
satisfies

(\forall{n\in{\mathbb{N}}})

\delta\leq\mu_{n}\leq\tfrac{1}{\kappa}-\delta
.
Given
x_{0}\in X
, generate
(x_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
by

(\forall{n\in{\mathbb{N}}})

x_{n+1}:=T_{\mu_{n}}x_{n}
.
Then
x_{n}\to P_{\operatorname{Fix}R}x_{0}
.

Proof
. Because

R
is
\kappa
-averaged,
the mapping

N:=\frac{R-(1-\kappa)\operatorname{Id}}{\kappa}

(25)

is nonexpansive, with
\operatorname{Fix}N=\operatorname{Fix}R
(and
\kappa(N)=1
),
and

T_{\lambda,N}=T_{\lambda/\kappa,R}=T_{\lambda/\kappa}.

(26)

Now set
\varepsilon:=\kappa\delta
and
(\forall{n\in{\mathbb{N}}})

\lambda_{n}:=\kappa\mu_{n}
.
Then
(\forall{n\in{\mathbb{N}}})

\varepsilon\leq\lambda_{n}\leq 1-\varepsilon
.
By
Theorem

2.5
,

T_{\lambda_{n},N}\cdots T_{\lambda_{1},N}T_{\lambda_{0},N}x_{0}\to P_{\operatorname{Fix}N}x_{0}.

(27)

On the other hand,
(\forall{n\in{\mathbb{N}}})

T_{\mu_{n}}=T_{\lambda_{n},N}
and

\operatorname{Fix}N=\operatorname{Fix}R
.
Altogether, the result follows.

\hfill\hskip 9.24994pt\blacksquare

3.2
Affine mappings

In this subsection, we suppose that
b\in X
and

S\colon X\to X\colon x\mapsto Rx+b\;\;\text{with}\;\;\operatorname{Fix}S\neq\varnothing.

(28)

Then the following can be seen easily (see also
[
5
, Lemma 3.2]
)

Fact 3.2
.

There exists a point
a\in X
such that
b=a-Ra
and the following hold:

(i)

\operatorname{Fix}S=a+\operatorname{Fix}R
.

(ii)

(\forall x\in X)

P_{\operatorname{Fix}S}x=a+P_{\operatorname{Fix}R}(x-a)
.

(iii)

(\forall x\in X)

Sx=a+R(x-a)
.

Corollary 3.3
.

Let
x_{0}\in X
.
Then for every
{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
, we have

T_{\lambda_{n},S}\cdots T_{\lambda_{0},S}x_{0}=a+T_{\lambda_{n},R}\cdots T_{\lambda_{0},R}(x_{0}-a),

(29)

where
a
is as in
Fact

3.2
.

Proof
. Let
x\in X
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
.
By
Fact

3.2
(iii)
,

\displaystyle T_{\lambda,S}x

\displaystyle=(1-\lambda)x+\lambda Sx=(1-\lambda)x+\lambda(a+R(x-a))

(30a)

\displaystyle=(1-\lambda)(x-a)+\lambda R(x-a)+a

(30b)

\displaystyle=a+T_{\lambda,R}(x-a).

(30c)

We now prove
29
by induction on
n
.
The base case
n=0
is clear from
30
.
Now assume that
29
holds for some
{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
.
Then

\displaystyle T_{\lambda_{n+1},S}T_{\lambda_{n},S}\cdots T_{\lambda_{0},S}x_{0}

\displaystyle=T_{\lambda_{n+1},S}\big(T_{\lambda_{n},S}\cdots T_{\lambda_{0},S}x_{0}\big)

\displaystyle=a+T_{\lambda_{n+1},R}\big(T_{\lambda_{n},S}\cdots T_{\lambda_{0},S}x_{0}-a\big)

(using
30
)

\displaystyle=a+T_{\lambda_{n+1},R}T_{\lambda_{n},R}\cdots T_{\lambda_{0},R}(x_{0}-a)

(using
29
)

and we are done.

\hfill\hskip 9.24994pt\blacksquare

We now obtain the following affine generalization of
Theorem

1.3
:

Theorem 3.4

(main result — more general affine version)
.

Suppose that there exists

\varepsilon>0
such that

(\forall{n\in{\mathbb{N}}})\hskip 9.24994pt\varepsilon\leq\lambda_{n}\leq 1-\varepsilon.

(31)

Let
x_{0}\in X
and generate the sequence
(x_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
by

(\forall{n\in{\mathbb{N}}})\hskip 9.24994ptx_{n+1}:=T_{\lambda_{n},S}x_{n}.

(32)

Then

x_{n}\to P_{\operatorname{Fix}S}x_{0}.

(33)

Proof
. Let
a
be as in
Fact

3.2
and
Corollary

3.3
.
By
Theorem

1.3
, we have

T_{\lambda_{n},R}\cdots T_{\lambda_{0},R}(x_{0}-a)\to P_{\operatorname{Fix}R}(x_{0}-a).

(34)

Then

\displaystyle x_{n+1}

\displaystyle=T_{\lambda_{n},S}\cdots T_{\lambda_{0},S}x_{0}

(using
32
)

\displaystyle=a+T_{\lambda_{n},R}\cdots T_{\lambda_{0},R}(x_{0}-a)

(using
29
)

\displaystyle\to a+P_{\operatorname{Fix}R}(x_{0}-a)

(using
34
)

\displaystyle=P_{\operatorname{Fix}S}x_{0}

(using
Fact

3.2
(ii)
)

as claimed.

\hfill\hskip 9.24994pt\blacksquare

3.3
aBBR: an adaptive variant of Baillon-Bruck-Reich

Theorem

1.3
opens the door for the following adaptive version of the
Baillon-Bruck-Reich result (
Fact

1.2
), which we call

adaptive Baillon-Bruck-Reich
or
aBBR
for short:

Theorem 3.5

(aBBR)
.

Suppose
R
is linear, and let
\varepsilon\in\bigl]0,\tfrac{1}{2}\bigr]
and

x_{0}\in X
. Given
{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
, generate the next iterate
x_{n+1}

from the current iterate
x_{n}
as follows:
If
x_{n}\in\operatorname{Fix}R
, then stop.
Otherwise, compute

\lambda_{x_{n}}=\frac{\left\langle{x_{n}},{x_{n}-Rx_{n}}\right\rangle}{\|x_{n}-Rx_{n}\|^{2}}\hskip 9.24994pt\text{and }\hskip 9.24994pt\lambda_{n}:=\min\{\lambda_{x_{n}},1-\varepsilon\},

(35)

and update

x_{n+1}:=T_{\lambda_{n}}x_{n}.

(36)

Then

x_{n}\to P_{\operatorname{Fix}R}x_{0}.

(37)

Proof
. This is a consequence of
Theorem

1.3

because
\lambda_{n}\in\bigl[\tfrac{1}{2},1-\varepsilon\bigr]
by
Proposition

2.1
.

\hfill\hskip 9.24994pt\blacksquare

We conclude this paper with the following numerical experiment.
Suppose
R\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}
is nonexpansive with
\operatorname{Fix}R=\{0\}
.
Given a starting point
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\smallsetminus\{0\}
, we know
that both the standard Baillon-Bruck-Reich algorithm, which we abbreviate
as BBR, as well as aBBR produces sequences that converge to
P_{\operatorname{Fix}R}(x_{0})=0

(by
Fact

1.2
and
Theorem

3.5
). We experimented with various instances of
R

and found that the behaviour essentially follows two patterns which we illustrate
in
Fig.

1
. These plots were generated as follows: Given
R
, we randomly generated
100 nonzero starting points
x_{0}
and we counted how many iterations are need for
BBR and aBBR to reach
\|x_{n}\|<\varepsilon:=10^{-6}
. For BBR, we
varied the constant
\lambda
in the interval
[\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon]
.
It then happens either that the optimal
\lambda
for BBR is
1-\varepsilon

in which case the performance of BBR and aBBR is very similar (although
it takes slightly more work to compute the iterates generated by aBBR).
Or the optimal
\lambda
is smaller than
1-\varepsilon
in which case it really pays off
to run aBBR. In
Fig.

1
(a), the matrix is

R=\begin{pmatrix}0.7&0\\
0&0.2\end{pmatrix}

while for
Fig.

1
(b), we have

R=\begin{pmatrix}-0.9&0\\
0&0.5\end{pmatrix}
. This simple experiment suggests that it might be beneficial to run aBBR rather than straight BBR.
