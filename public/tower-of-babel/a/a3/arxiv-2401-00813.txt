Ultraspherical/Gegenbauer polynomials to unify 2D/3D Ambisonic directivity designs
Franz Zotter

Abstract
This report on axisymmetric ultraspherical/Gegenbauer polynomials [ Geg77 ] and their use in Ambisonic directivity design in 2D and 3D presents an alternative mathematical formalism to what can be read in, e.g., my and Matthias Frank’s book on Ambisonics or Jérôme Daniel’s thesis, Gary Elko’s differential array book chapters, or Boaz Rafaely’s spherical microphone array book. My original—but discarded—intention was to already include ultraspherical polynomials in the Ambisonics book to pursue a simple thought: retrieving suitable axisymmetric continuous-direction functions that serve the discrete-direction metrics for experimental psychoacoustic findings in spatial audio uniformly, for any number D \mathrm{D} of space dimensions, of course typically D = 2 , 3 \mathrm{D}=2,3 . But the cost for this natural way to understand spherical/circular polynomials and their directional sampling was too high. This would have required carrying out both the derivations of two entire formalisms from scratch, the ultraspherical/Gegenbauer polynomials and the circular/spherical harmonics, of which only the latter ones define the Ambisonic format. Nevertheless, ultraspherical/Gegenbauer polynomials are highly valuable when designing axisymmetric beams and understanding spherical t t designs so that this report will shed some light on what circular, spherical, and ultraspherical axisymmetric polynomials are. While mathematically interesting by themselves already, they can be useful in spherical beamforming as described in the literature on spherical and differential microphone arrays, e.g. [ Raf19 , Elk00 , Elk04 ] . What are polynomials bases for axisymmetric functions, say axisymmetric harmonics (or ultraspherical or Gegenbauer) polynomials? How can they be utilized to define Ambisonic order weightings or spherical beamformers? In this report, these ultraspherical/Gegenbauer polynomials will be used to uniformly derive for arbitrary dimensions D \mathrm{D} the various directivity designs or Ambisonic order weightings known from literature: max-DI/basic [ Dan01 ] , max- r E r_{\mathrm{E}} [ Dan01 ] , supercardioid [ Raf19 , Elk00 , Elk04 ] , cardioid/inphase [ Dan01 ] . Is there a way to relate higher-order cardioids and supercardioids? How could one define directivity patterns with an on-axis flatness constraint?



1

Axisymmetric directivity functions

In the seventies, Cooper
[
CS72
]
described the idea of using angular amplitude functions to control the auditory event, which could be thought of as an extension to mid/side decomposition, or as recording with figure-of-eight and omnidirectional directivity patterns.
Stemming from this approach and from this time, the notion and technology of Ambisonics was developed by Felgett
[
Fel74
]
, Gerzon
[
Ger75
]
, and Craven
[
CG77
]
. Since then, the term Ambisonics became common for technology using angular harmonic functions to express surround sound. Read more about all of this in the open Ambisonics book at Springer

https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-030-17207-7
.

Appendix H
shows how 2D Ambisonic panning can be expressed in terms of a finite-order Fourier series using the exponentials
e^{\mathrm{i}m\phi}
with real coefficients, i.e., a finite cosine
\cos m\phi
series in the azimuthal distance
\phi=\varphi-\varphi_{s}
between array angles
\varphi=\upvarphi_{l}
and the given angle
\varphi_{s}
. For this symmetric finite-resolution function, a regular circular polygon of sufficiently many loudspeaker directions would automatically yield correct aiming of the vector measures
\bm{r}_{\mathrm{E}}
and
\bm{r}_{\mathrm{V}}
towards the panning direction
\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{s}}
and panning-invariant loudness and width magnitudes
E
,
P
,
\|\bm{r}_{\mathrm{E}}\|
,
\|\bm{r}_{\mathrm{V}}\|
, and a certain directivity factor
Q
.

Instead, we desire defining axisymmetric functions around the panning direction
\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{s}}
in a space of arbitrarily many dimensions
\mathrm{D}
, and their decomposition into suitable limited degree harmonic functions. The projection of the variable direction vector
\bm{\theta}
on given direction
\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{s}}
yields the cosine of the enclosed angle to the axis
\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{s}}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{\theta}=\cos\phi
, and hereby expresses the only dimension of the axisymmetric function,
x=\cos\phi
. As the
m^{\mathrm{th}}
power thereof,
\cos^{m}\phi
, can be expressed in terms of a cosine series up to the
m^{\mathrm{th}}
degree, a power series in
x
is a reasonable representation of the panning functon on the sphere.

To do this properly, we will first learn about the term harmonics, solve for axisymmetric harmonics, establish the panning function on the circle using these harmonics, and then finally come to the spherical panning function.

1.1

Axisymmetric harmonics: Gegenbauer polynomials

The Laplacian is defined in the
\mathrm{D}
-dimensional Cartesian space as

\displaystyle\bigtriangleup

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{\mathrm{D}}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{j}^{2}}.

(1)

Solving the eigenvalue problem
\bigtriangleup=-m^{2}\,f=0
for one dimension
\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x^{2}}f=-m^{2}f
yields with the exponential guess
f=e^{ax}
, inserted and differentiated to
a^{2}\,f=-m^{2}\,f
,
harmonic functions

f=e^{\pm\mathrm{i}mx}
with
m\in\mathbb{Z}
if the function should be periodic
f(x)=f(x+2\pi\,k)
,
k\in\mathbb{Z}
. If only a limited number of eigenvalues
m\leq K
is used, this yields a limited-degree expansion into
harmonics
, a band-limited Fourier series. It is useful to limit the resolution of a represented function.

For 2 dimensions
Appendix H
defines panning function with limited
m
, defined using a series of real part of this complex exponential
e^{\mathrm{i}m\phi}=\cos(m\phi)
with the polar angle
\phi=\arctan\frac{y}{x}
enclosed with the
x
axis. For 3 dimensions, these trigonometric or complex-exponential angular harmonics are not useful anymore, and lose some beneficial properties, for instance,
\cos(m\phi)
has no uniformly limited resolution and is not analytic around the poles of the sphere.

Generally, homogeneous solutions to the Laplacian
\bigtriangleup g=0
are called harmonics. In case of a spherical coordinate system with a unit-direction vector
\bm{\theta}
and a radius
r
and a separation approach
g=f(\bm{\theta})\,h(r)
, we obtain
\bigtriangleup g=f(\bm{\theta})\bigtriangleup h(r)+h(r)\bigtriangleup f(\bm{\theta})=0
, for which there needs to be a separation constant
\lambda=\frac{\bigtriangleup h}{h}
defining Laplacian eigensolutions
\bigtriangleup f=-\lambda\,f
for the directional part. For suitable Eigenvalues
-\lambda
, these
directional harmonics
span an orthogonal set of basis functions as solutions.

Chosing the last Cartesian coordinate axis as panning direction
\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{s}}=[\dots,\,0,\,1]^{\mathrm{T}}
, and writing the direction vector as
\bm{\theta}=\frac{[x_{1},\,\dots,\,x_{\mathrm{D}}]^{\mathrm{T}}}{\sqrt{\sum_{j}x_{j}^{2}}}=\frac{[x_{1},\,\dots,\,x_{\mathrm{D}}]^{\mathrm{T}}}{r}
, i.e.
\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{s}}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{\theta}=\frac{x_{\mathrm{D}}}{r}=\cos\phi
,
a purely axisymmetric panning function
defined on
x:=\cos\phi
becomes

\displaystyle f

\displaystyle=f(\cos\phi)=f(x).

By this choice, the
directional harmonics
become
axisymmetric harmonics
with regard to the enclosed angle
\phi
to some directional axis. To find their formal solution, we re-write the Laplacian to the axial dimension
-1\leq x\leq 1
. Such harmonics are useful to represent limited-resolution patterns of rotational symmetry with regard to their axis.
As derived in the
Appendix A
, the Laplacian with regard to the axial coordinate
x=\cos\phi
becomes:

\displaystyle r^{2}\bigtriangleup_{x}=(1-x^{2})\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}x^{2}}-(\mathrm{D}-1)\,x\,\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}.

(2)

The Ggegenbauer/ultraspherical differential equation: Laplacian eigenvalue problem for axisymmetric functions.

With the above, we establish the ultraspherical differential equation
(r^{2}\bigtriangleup_{x}+\lambda)f=0
with the eigenvalue
-\lambda
to the Laplacian

\displaystyle(1-x^{2})f^{\prime\prime}-(\mathrm{D}-1)\,x\,f^{\prime}+\lambda\,f=0,

(3)

in the hope that if a limited set of discrete eigenvalues
\lambda_{n}\leq K
is used, then we obtain harmonics orthogonal on the interval
-1\leq x\leq 1
that contain only limited powers of
x^{n}
with
n\leq\mathrm{N}
, given a suitable of
K
and
\lambda_{n}
. We know trigonometrically that such functions can only contain oscillations up to
\cos(\mathrm{N}\phi)
, thus have limited angular resolution.

We solve the equation with the power series approach
f=\sum_{k}c_{k}\,x^{k}
, see
Appendix C
, and get an interrelation of its coefficients
c_{k+2}=\frac{k^{2}+k(\mathrm{D}-2)-\lambda}{(k+1)(k+2)}\;c_{k}
.
Choosing
\lambda=\lambda_{n}=n^{2}+(\mathrm{D}-2)n
, the power series terminates at
k=n
, so that
c_{k>n}=0
, hence it becomes an
n^{\mathrm{th}}
-degree polynomial
f=\mathcal{P}_{n}
, and the relation between the coefficients of
\mathcal{P}_{n}
remains

\displaystyle c_{k+2}^{(n)}=\frac{(k-n)(k+n+\mathrm{D}-2)}{(k+1)(k+2)}\;c_{k}^{(n)}.

(4)

Also solutions with different
\lambda
could be acceptable, but their infinite series may diverge, e.g., at
x=1
or
x=-1
, so we disregard. We re-write the above equation accordingly:

\displaystyle(1-x^{2})\mathcal{P}_{n}^{\prime\prime}-(\mathrm{D}-1)\,x\,\mathcal{P}_{n}^{\prime}+n(n+\mathrm{D}-2)\,\mathcal{P}_{n}=0.

(5)

The above equation is called the
ultraspherical
or
Gegenbauer
differential equation
[
Geg77
]
, and solutions can be constructed from
\mathcal{P}_{0}(x)=1
, and
\mathcal{P}_{1}(x)=x
with the recurrence derived in
subsection E.3
, eq. (
87
) with the standardization
\mathcal{P}_{n}(1)=1
of the
ultraspherical
polynomials, therefore differing from Gegenbauer’s typical definition

[
Geg77
]
, cf.

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{n+1}(x)=\frac{2n+\mathrm{D}-2}{n+\mathrm{D}-2}\;x\,\mathcal{P}_{n}(x)-\frac{n}{n+\mathrm{D}-2}\mathcal{P}_{n-1}(x).

(6)

1.2

Continuous axisymmetric directivity/panning patterns in D dimensions

The axisymmetric directivity/panning function is based on the decomposition of a Dirac delta pointing towards the panning direction
\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{s}}
,

\displaystyle\delta(1-\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{s}}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{\theta})

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\infty,&\text{for $\bm{\theta}=\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{s}}$}\\
0,&\text{otherwise}\end{cases},

\displaystyle\text{with }\int_{\mathbb{S}^{\mathrm{D}-1}}\delta(1-\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{s}}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{\theta})\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\theta}

\displaystyle=1,

(10)

into infinitely many axisymmetric harmonics

\displaystyle\delta(1-\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{s}}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{\theta})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{S_{\mathrm{D}-2}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\gamma_{n}\,\mathcal{P}_{n}(\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{s}}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{\theta}),

(11)

where the factor
\frac{1}{S_{\mathrm{D}-2}}
accounts for normalization with regard to the integrals over the independent, axisymmetric coordinates, whose value corresponds to the surface of the next lesser dimensional unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{\mathrm{D}-2}
.

The desired axisymmetric panning function is based on the representation of the Dirac delta, but with limited degree and involving a sidelobe-suppressing weight
a_{n}
,

\displaystyle g(\bm{\theta})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{S_{\mathrm{D}-2}}\sum_{n=0}^{\mathrm{N}}a_{n}\,\gamma_{n}\,\mathcal{P}_{n}(\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{s}}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{\theta}).

(12)

Simple
\gamma_{n}
for axisymmetric Dirac delta at
x=x_{0}
.

Given a Dirac delta located at
x=x_{0}
as
\frac{\delta(x_{0}-x)}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}^{\mathrm{D}-3}}
which is correctly normalized to the axial coordinate to fulfill
\int_{-1}^{1}\delta(x_{0}-x)\;\,\mathrm{d}x=1
, we use the transform integral eq. (
9
) to find its coefficients
\gamma_{m}
:

\displaystyle\frac{\delta(x_{0}-x)}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}^{\mathrm{D}-3}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\gamma_{n}\,\mathcal{P}_{n}(x),

\displaystyle\gamma_{n}=\frac{1}{N_{n}^{2}}\;\int_{-1}^{1}\delta(x_{0}-x)\,\mathcal{P}_{n}(x)\;\mathrm{d}x=\frac{\mathcal{P}_{n}(x_{0})}{N_{n}^{2}}.

(13)

The resulting expression
\frac{\delta(x_{0}-x)}{w(x)}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\mathcal{P}_{n}(x_{0})\mathcal{P}_{n}(x)}{N_{n}^{2}}
may be simplified by the Christoffel-Darboux identity eq. (
94
) for summation over a limited degree
n\leq\mathrm{N}
.

When located at the pole
x_{0}=1
, the ultraspherical/Gegenbauer polynomials normalized to
\mathcal{P}_{n}(1)=1
yield a simple expansion coefficient, cf. eq. (
8
), with
a_{n}=\mathcal{P}_{n}(x_{0})=1
,

\displaystyle\gamma_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N_{n}^{2}},

\displaystyle g(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{S_{\mathrm{D}-2}\,}\sum_{n=0}^{\mathrm{N}}\frac{a_{n}}{N_{n}^{2}}\,\mathcal{P}_{n}(x),

(14)

and
a_{n}=1
results from eq. (
13

a_{n}\neq 1

1.3

Optimal discretization: spherical
t
design

Assume that we sample a polynomial in
\left(\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{s}}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{\theta}\right)^{n}
limited in degree by
n\leq t
,
t\in\mathbb{N}
. The discretization uses the directions
\{\bm{\uptheta}_{l}\}
at
\mathrm{L}
points and that this is called
t
design whenever discrete summation and integral are equivalent, i.e., discrete summation accurately determines the direct component (DC) of the polynomial despite the variable orientation of
\bm{\theta}_{s}
, according to the definition in
[
DGS77
]
,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{S}_{\mathrm{D}-1}}\mathcal{P}_{n}\left(\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{s}}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{\theta}\right)\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\theta}

\displaystyle={\textstyle\frac{S_{\mathrm{D}-1}}{\mathrm{L}}}\sum_{l=1}^{\mathrm{L}}\mathcal{P}_{n}\left(\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{s}}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{\uptheta}_{l}\right).

(30)

This simplistic criterion of just estimating the DC correctly is relevant for all models:

\bullet

In the calculation of
P
, an integral over the
\mathrm{N}^{\mathrm{th}}
-degree polynomial pattern
g(\bm{\theta})
is evaluated, which is why at least a
t\geq\mathrm{N}
design should be used to get correct results for
P
by summing up the discretized pattern
g(\bm{\uptheta}_{l})
times
\mathrm{d}\Omega=\frac{S_{D-1}}{\mathrm{L}}
.

\bullet

In the calculation of
\bm{r}_{\mathrm{V}}
, the
\mathrm{N}^{\mathrm{th}}
-degree pattern
g(\bm{\theta})
is multiplied by
\bm{\theta}^{\mathrm{T}}_{\mathrm{s}}\bm{\theta}
, causing an integral over an
(\mathrm{N}+1)^{\mathrm{th}}
-degree polynomial, therefore discretization requires at least a
t\geq\mathrm{N}+1
design for the numerator of
\bm{r}_{\mathrm{V}}
to sum up
\bm{\theta}^{\mathrm{T}}_{\mathrm{s}}\bm{\theta}\,g(\bm{\uptheta}_{l})
correctly.

\bullet

For calculating
E
, the squared continuous pattern
g(\bm{\theta})^{2}
is integrated, a
2\mathrm{N}^{\mathrm{th}}
-degree polynomial, thus discretization should at least consider a
t\geq 2\mathrm{N}
design so that summing up the discrete pattern
g(\bm{\uptheta}_{l})^{2}
times
\mathrm{d}\Omega=\frac{S_{D-1}}{\mathrm{L}}
correctly estimates
{E}
.

\bullet

In the calculation of
\bm{r}_{\mathrm{E}}
, the squared continuous function
g(\bm{\theta})^{2}
multiplied by
\bm{\theta}^{\mathrm{T}}_{\mathrm{s}}\bm{\theta}
is integrated, a
(2\mathrm{N}+1)^{\mathrm{th}}
-degree polynomial, so discretization should consider a
t\geq 2\mathrm{N}+1
design for correct results for the numerator of
\bm{r}_{\mathrm{E}}
that sums up
\bm{\theta}^{\mathrm{T}}_{\mathrm{s}}\bm{\theta}\,g(\bm{\uptheta}_{l})^{2}
.

In conclusion, using at least a
t\geq 2\mathrm{N}+1
design ensures that
E
,
P
,
\bm{r}_{\mathrm{V}}
, and
\bm{r}_{\mathrm{E}}
obtained by from discrete patterns
g(\bm{\uptheta}_{l})
complies with results for the continuous function
g(\bm{\theta})
.

Note that the FBR measure is an exception because its lower integration limit may exclude a varying number of discretization nodes, depending on orientation and is therefore more accurate when solved with the analytic values
g_{nm}
of the Gram matrix, see
subsection F.1
.

Appendix A
Laplacian in axisymmetric coordinates

A function in the last, normalized Cartesian coordinate
x:=\frac{x_{\mathrm{D}}}{r}=\cos\vartheta
is axisymmetric with regard to
\mathbf{i}_{\mathrm{D}}=[0,\dots,0,1]^{\top}
, and the generalized chain rule yields for the Laplacian

\displaystyle\bigtriangleup=\sum_{j}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{j}^{2}}=\sum_{j}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}\left(\frac{\partial x}{\partial x_{j}}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}\right)=\sum_{j}\frac{\partial^{2}x}{\partial x_{j}^{2}}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}+\sum_{j}\left(\frac{\partial x}{\partial x_{j}}\right)^{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}x^{2}}.

(54)

The derivative of
\frac{1}{r}
keeps re-appearing. It is with regard to any of the coordinates

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}\frac{1}{r}=-\frac{1}{r^{2}}\frac{\partial r}{\partial x_{j}}=-\frac{1}{r^{2}}\frac{\partial[\sum_{j}x_{j}^{2}]^{\frac{1}{2}}}{\partial x_{j}}=-\frac{1}{r^{2}}\frac{[\sum_{j}x_{j}^{2}]^{-\frac{1}{2}}}{2}\frac{\partial\sum_{j}x_{j}^{2}}{\partial x_{j}}=-\frac{1}{r^{3}}\frac{1}{2}2x_{j}=-\frac{x_{j}}{r^{3}}.

(55)

The derivatives of
x:=\frac{x_{\mathrm{D}}}{r}
are for
j\neq 1
:

(57)

The first derivative of
x

x_{\mathrm{D}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{\mathrm{D}}}\frac{x_{\mathrm{D}}}{r}=-\frac{x_{\mathrm{D}}^{2}}{r^{3}}+\frac{1}{r}=\frac{r^{2}-x_{\mathrm{D}}^{2}}{r^{3}}=\frac{\sum_{j\backslash\mathrm{D}}x_{j}^{2}}{r^{3}}=\frac{r_{\mathrm{D}-1}^{2}}{r^{3}},

\displaystyle\sum_{j}\left(\frac{\partial x}{\partial x_{j}}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{r_{\mathrm{D}-1}^{4}}{r^{6}}+\frac{x_{\mathrm{D}}^{2}\sum_{j\backslash\mathrm{D}}x_{j}^{2}}{r^{6}}=\frac{(r^{2}-x_{\mathrm{D}}^{2})r_{\mathrm{D}-1}^{2}}{r^{6}}+\frac{x_{\mathrm{D}}^{2}r_{\mathrm{D}-1}^{2}}{r^{6}}=\frac{r^{2}r_{\mathrm{D}-1}^{2}}{r^{6}}=\frac{r_{\mathrm{D}-1}^{2}}{r^{4}},

(61)

\displaystyle r^{2}\bigtriangleup_{x}=(1-x^{2})\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}x^{2}}-(\mathrm{D}-1)\,x\,\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}.

(62)

We can re-write the axisymmetric Laplacian (which can be proven using the chain rule), involving the definition

Appendix B
Jacobi determinant of axisymmetric coordinates

For the axisymmetric coordinate
x
, we re-write the integration constants
\prod_{j=1}^{\mathrm{D}}\mathrm{d}x_{j}=|\bm{J}|\,\mathrm{d}x\,\prod_{j=1}^{\mathrm{D}-1}\mathrm{d}\tilde{x}_{j}
with the determinant of the
Jacobian
matrix that describes how the surface element is stretched. The Jacobi matrix is filled with derivatives of the old coordinates with regard to the new ones. One of the new coordinates is
x
that defines
x_{\mathrm{D}}=r\,x
. The other
\mathrm{D}-1
coordinates are just copies of
x_{j}
but normalized to preserve the information about the radius as the new radius
r=\sqrt{\sum_{i}\tilde{x}_{i}^{2}}
of the
(\mathrm{D}-1)
-dimensional subspace. This defines

\displaystyle x_{\mathrm{D}}=x\,r,

\displaystyle x_{j}

\displaystyle=\tilde{x}_{j}\,\sqrt{1-x^{2}},

(65)

and herewith the derivatives for
\bm{J}
are

\displaystyle\bm{J}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}[\frac{\partial x_{j}}{\partial\tilde{x}_{i}}]_{i,j<\mathrm{D}},&[\frac{\partial x_{\mathrm{D}}}{\partial\tilde{x}_{i}}]_{i<\mathrm{D}}\\
[\frac{\partial x_{j}}{\partial x}]_{j<\mathrm{D}}&\frac{\partial x_{\mathrm{D}}}{\partial x}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{I}\sqrt{1-x^{2}},&\bigl[\frac{x\,\tilde{x}_{i}}{r}\bigr]_{i<\mathrm{D}}\\
\Bigl[-\frac{\tilde{x}_{j}\,x}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}\Bigr]_{j<\mathrm{D}}&r\end{bmatrix},

(66)

which makes the determinant particularly simple to calculate: (i) the determinant of a product of matrices is the product of the determinants, (2) for a block-triangular matrix, the product of the diagonal blocks would be the determinant. A block-matrix
\bm{E}
can be made block-triangular by LU decomposition

\displaystyle\bm{J}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{A}&\bm{B}\\
\bm{C}&\bm{D}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{A}&\bm{0}\\
\bm{C}&\bm{I}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\bm{I}&\bm{A}^{-1}\bm{B}\\
\bm{0}&\bm{D}-\bm{C}\bm{A}^{-1}\bm{B}\end{bmatrix},

\displaystyle\text{so that }\det\bm{J}

\displaystyle=\det\bm{A}\,\det(\bm{D}-\bm{C}\bm{A}^{-1}\bm{B}).

(67)

We get the weight of the integration constant
|\bm{J}|\,\mathrm{d}x
:

\displaystyle|\bm{J}|

\displaystyle=\sqrt{1-x^{2}}^{\mathrm{D}-1}\;\left(r+\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}\,\sum_{j}\frac{x\,\tilde{x}_{j}}{r}\frac{x\,\tilde{x}_{j}}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}\right)

(68)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}^{\mathrm{D}-1}}{r}\left(1+\frac{x^{2}r^{2}}{r^{2}(1-x^{2})}\right)=\frac{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}^{\mathrm{D}-3}}{r}=\frac{w(x)}{r}.

(69)

Appendix C
Solving the ultraspherical differential equation:

Gegenbauer polynomials

With
\alpha=\frac{\mathrm{D}}{2}-1
, the Laplacian eigenfunction problem
r^{2}\bigtriangleup y(x)=-\lambda\,y(x)
is the ultraspherical or Gegenbauer differential equation for the purely axisymmetric function
y

\displaystyle(1-x^{2})y^{\prime\prime}-(2\alpha+1)\,x\,y^{\prime}+\lambda\,y=0.

It is solved by a power series (Frobenius Ansatz)

\displaystyle y=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}c_{k}\;x^{k}.

Inserted into the differential equation, we can simplify, shift the indices
k
to fit the powers
x^{k}
, and find by comparison of coefficients.

\displaystyle(1-x^{2})\,\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}c_{k}\,k\,(k-1)\,x^{k-2}-(2\alpha+1)\,x\,\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}c_{k}\,k\,x^{k-1}+\lambda\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}c_{k}\,x^{k}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}c_{k}\,k\,(k-1)\,x^{k-2}-\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}c_{k}\,k\,(k-1)\,x^{k}-(2\alpha+1)\,\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}c_{k}\,k\,x^{k}+\lambda\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}c_{k}\,x^{k}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}c_{k+2}\,(k+1)\,(k+1)\,x^{k}-\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}c_{k}\,k\,(k-1)\,x^{k}-(2\alpha+1)\,\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}c_{k}\,k\,x^{k}+\lambda\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}c_{k}\,x^{k}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\Bigl\{\bigl[\lambda-k\,(k-1)\,u[k-2]-(2\alpha+1)\,k\,u[k-1]\bigr]\,c_{k}+(k+1)\,(k+2)\,c_{k+2}\Bigr\}x^{k}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle c_{k+2}=\frac{(2\alpha+1)\,k+k\,(k-1)-\lambda}{(k+1)(k+2)}\;c_{k}.\quad\text{for $k\geq 2$}

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow c_{k+2}=\frac{k(k+2\alpha)-\lambda}{(k+1)(k+2)}\;c_{k}.\quad\text{for $k\geq 2$}

Choosing
\lambda=\lambda_{n}=n(n+2\alpha)
, the series terminates at
k=n
.
The recurrence of the coefficients for a certain
n
can be re-written to

\displaystyle c_{k+2}^{(n)}=\frac{k^{2}+2\alpha\,k-n^{2}-2\alpha\,n}{(k+1)(k+2)}\;c_{k}=-\frac{(n-k)(n+k+2\alpha)}{(k+1)(k+2)}\;c_{k}^{(n)}.

For even
n=2m
, odd-degree coefficients vanish
c_{2l+1}^{(2m)}=0
and for odd
n=2m+1
, coefficients of even powers vanish
c_{2l}^{(2m+1)}
.
The non-zero coefficient series are composed factorials and double factorials (containing every second factor) when applying the recurrence repeatedly, and they are either based on a starting value
c_{0}^{(n)}
or
c_{1}^{(n)}
.

We may replace the constants
k+2=n-2l
and arrive at

\displaystyle c_{n-2l}^{(n)}

\displaystyle=-4(l+1)(n-l-1+\alpha)\frac{c_{n-2l-2}^{(n)}}{(n-2l)(n-2l-1)}

(70)

Starting from
l=0
, re-iterate the recurrence until arriving at
c_{n-2\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor}^{(n)}
after
2l\leq n
iterations, or with round-off operator
l\leq\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor
. For
l=\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor
, the term
n-l
becomes
n-\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor=\lceil\frac{n}{2}\rceil
. We use rising factorials or Gamma functions
(\lceil\frac{n}{2}\rceil+\alpha-1)^{\overline{\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor-l}}=\frac{\Gamma(n-l+\alpha)}{\Gamma(\lceil\frac{n}{2}\rceil+\alpha)}=(\lceil\frac{n}{2}\rceil+\alpha-1)\dots(n-l+\alpha-2)(n-l+\alpha-1)
to generalize the ratio of factorials to real arguments

\displaystyle c_{n-2l}^{(n)}

\displaystyle=\frac{(-1)^{l}\,2^{n-2l}\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor!(\lceil\frac{n}{2}\rceil+\alpha-1)^{\overline{\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor-l}}}{l!(n-2l)!}c_{n-2\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor}^{(n)}

\displaystyle=\frac{(-1)^{l}\,2^{n-2l}\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor!}{l!(n-2l)!}\frac{\Gamma(n-l+\alpha)}{\Gamma(\lceil\frac{n}{2}\rceil+\alpha)}c_{n-2\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor}^{(n)}.

The typical choice for the coefficient
c_{n-2\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor}^{(n)}=\frac{\Gamma(\lceil\frac{n}{2}\rceil+\alpha)}{\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor!\Gamma(\alpha)}
removes clutter depending on integer division of
n
and keeps the values of the Gamma functions
\frac{\Gamma(n-l+\alpha)}{\Gamma(\alpha)}
finite, or more slim with the factorial
(\alpha)^{\overline{n-l}}
rising from
(\alpha)
to
(\alpha+n-l-1)

\displaystyle c_{n-2l}^{(n)}

\displaystyle=\frac{(-1)^{l}\,2^{n-2l}}{l!(n-2l)!}\frac{\Gamma(n-l+\alpha)}{\Gamma(\alpha)}=\frac{(-1)^{l}\,2^{n-2l}(\alpha)^{\overline{n-l}}}{l!(n-2l)!},

(71)

defining the Gegenbauer polynomials
[
Geg77
]

\displaystyle C_{n}^{(\alpha)}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=0}^{\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor}\frac{(-1)^{l}\,2^{n-2l}(\alpha)^{\overline{n-l}}}{l!(n-2l)!}\,x^{n-2l},

(72)

and for
\alpha=0
the rising factorial simplifies to
(\alpha)^{\overline{n-l}}=(n-l-1)!
.

Appendix D
Orthogonality of axisymmetric harmonics

The axisymetric Laplacian differential equation

(1-x^{2})\mathcal{P}^{\prime\prime}_{n}-(\mathrm{D}-1)\,x\,\mathcal{P}^{\prime}_{n}+\lambda_{n}\,\mathcal{P}_{n}=0
with
\lambda_{n}=n(n+2\alpha)
can be re-written compactly as

\displaystyle\frac{1}{w(x)}\left[(1-x^{2})\,w(x)\,\mathcal{P}^{\prime}_{n}\right]^{\prime}+\lambda_{n}\mathcal{P}_{n}=0.

(73)

Multiplied by another solution
\mathcal{P}_{m}
and integrated over
w(x)\,\mathrm{d}x
it becomes

\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}\left[(1-x^{2})\,w(x)\,\mathcal{P}^{\prime}_{n}\right]^{\prime}\mathcal{P}_{m}\,\mathrm{d}x+\lambda_{n}\,\int_{-1}^{1}\mathcal{P}_{n}\,\mathcal{P}_{m}\,w(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=0.

The first expression can be dealt with using partial integration

\int f^{\prime}g=fg-\int fg^{\prime}:

\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}\left[(1-x^{2})\,w(x)\,\mathcal{P}^{\prime}_{n}\right]^{\prime}\mathcal{P}_{m}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=(1-x^{2})\,w(x)\,\mathcal{P}^{\prime}_{n}\,\mathcal{P}_{m}\Big|_{-1}^{1}-\int_{-1}^{1}(1-x^{2})\,w(x)\,\mathcal{P}^{\prime}_{n}\,\mathcal{P}^{\prime}_{m}\,\mathrm{d}x.

To show that the solutions
\mathcal{P}_{n}
and
\mathcal{P}_{m}
with
n\neq m
are orthogonal, we subtract a similar equation, the one with the roles of
\mathcal{P}_{n}
and
\mathcal{P}_{m}
interchanged, and get:

\displaystyle(1-x^{2})\,w(x)[\mathcal{P}_{n}^{\prime}\mathcal{P}_{m}-\mathcal{P}_{m}^{\prime}\mathcal{P}_{n}]\bigg|_{-1}^{1}+(\lambda_{n}-\lambda_{m})\,\int_{-1}^{1}\mathcal{P}_{n}\,\mathcal{P}_{m}\,w(x)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=0,

(74)

because the two integrals
\int(1-x^{2})\,w(x)\,\mathcal{P}_{n}^{\prime}\mathcal{P}_{m}^{\prime}\mathrm{d}x
are identical. With the integration boundaries
-1\leq x\leq 1
, the factor
(1-x^{2})\,w(x)|_{\pm 1}=0
in front of the mixed derivatives vanishes, which happens for any
-\frac{1}{2}\geq\alpha
, i.e.,
\mathrm{D}\geq 1
. We get:

\displaystyle(\lambda_{n}-\lambda_{m})\,\int_{-1}^{1}\mathcal{P}_{n}\,\mathcal{P}_{m}\,w(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=0.

(75)

If the indices match
n=m
, the first term vanishes and the integral needs not vanish. For different indices
n\neq m
our eigenvalues are unique as
\lambda_{n}=n(n+\mathrm{D}-2)
, hence
\lambda_{n}\neq\lambda_{m}
, so the first term won’t vanish, consequently the integral must vanish:

Any pair of solutions
\mathcal{P}_{n}
and
\mathcal{P}_{m}
with different index
n\neq m
is orthogonal under the integration weight
w(x)=(1-x^{2})^{\alpha-\frac{1}{2}}
.

Appendix E
Orthogonal polynomials

A set of orthogonal polynomials
\mathcal{P}_{n}(x)
sorted by their degree
n
, up to which they contain powers of the variable
\mathcal{P}_{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}c_{n,k}\,x^{k}
can naturally be used to re-expand any other limited-degree polynomial
\mathscr{P}_{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}c_{k}\,x^{k}
:

\displaystyle\mathscr{P}_{n}(x)=\sum_{m=0}^{n}\gamma_{m}\,\mathcal{P}_{m}(x).

(76)

This is not surprising. What makes orthogonal polynomials useful is their orthogonality on the interval
[a,b]
:

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}\mathcal{P}_{n}(x)\mathcal{P}_{m}(x)\;w(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=\begin{cases}0\qquad\text{for $n\neq m$},\\
N_{n}^{2}\qquad\text{for $n=m$}.\end{cases}

(77)

Here,
N_{n}^{2}=\int_{a}^{b}\mathcal{P}_{n}^{2}(x)\;w(x)\,\mathrm{d}x
is the squared magnitude of each function integrated over the entire interval.

Note that by the ability of limited-degree polynomials
\mathcal{P}_{m<n}
to express any
m^{\mathrm{th}}
-degree polynomial with
m<n
, the orthogonality automatically extendeds to
any
lower-degree polynomial
\mathscr{P}_{m<n}=\sum_{l=0}^{m}\gamma_{l}\,\mathcal{P}_{l}

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}\mathcal{P}_{n}(x)\mathscr{P}_{m<n}(x)\;w(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=0.

(78)

E.1

The transform integral: a benefit of orthogonality

The orthogonality allows to decompose any given function
g(x)
into coefficients
\gamma_{m}
of the orthogonal polynomials rather easily by an integral transformation. We write down the decomposition that we desire and integrate over a test function
\mathcal{P}_{n}(x)w(x)\mathrm{d}x\int
. Orthogonality simplifies the expression that we get

\displaystyle g(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\gamma_{m}\,\mathcal{P}_{m}(x)\qquad\qquad\Big|\;\mathcal{P}_{n}(x)w(x)\mathrm{d}x\int_{a}^{b}

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}g(x)\,\mathcal{P}_{n}(x)\;w(x)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\gamma_{m}\,\underbrace{\int_{a}^{b}\mathcal{P}_{m}(x)\mathcal{P}_{n}(x)\;w(x)\,\mathrm{d}x}_{N_{n}^{2}\delta_{nm}}=N_{n}^{2}\;\gamma_{n},

We hereby found the expansion coefficients by the integral without matrix inversion. The transform pair is:

\displaystyle g(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\gamma_{m}\,\mathcal{P}_{m}(x)\qquad\qquad\gamma_{n}=\frac{1}{N_{n}^{2}}\;\int_{a}^{b}g(x)\,\mathcal{P}_{n}(x)\;w(x)\,\mathrm{d}x.

(79)

As infinitely many orthogonal polynomials consist of any power
x^{k}
with
k=0,1,2,\dots,\infty
, the representation is able to represent any function that can be described by an infinite power series.

E.2

Symetries and interval
-1\leq x\leq 1
.

If
-a=b=1
and
w(x)=w(-x)
, the integration range and integration weight is symmetrical.
We can re-write the orthogonality condition

\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}\mathcal{P}_{n}\,\mathcal{P}_{m}\;w\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\phantom{-}\int_{-1}^{0}\mathcal{P}_{n}(x)\,\mathcal{P}_{m}(x)\;w(x)\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{0}^{1}g(x)\,\mathcal{P}_{n}(x)\;w(x)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{-1}\mathcal{P}_{n}(x)\,\mathcal{P}_{m}(x)\;w(x)\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{0}^{1}\mathcal{P}_{n}(x)\,\mathcal{P}_{m}(x)\;w(x)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{1}\mathcal{P}_{n}(-x)\,\mathcal{P}_{m}(-x)\;w(-x)\,\mathrm{d}(-x)+\int_{0}^{1}\mathcal{P}_{n}(x)\,\mathcal{P}_{m}(x)\;w(x)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\phantom{-}\int_{0}^{1}\mathcal{P}_{n}(-x)\,\mathcal{P}_{m}(-x)\;w(x)\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{0}^{1}\mathcal{P}_{n}(x)\,\mathcal{P}_{m}(x)\;w(x)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\phantom{-}\int_{0}^{1}\underbrace{\Bigl[\mathcal{P}_{n}(-x)\,\mathcal{P}_{m}(-x)+\mathcal{P}_{n}(x)\,\mathcal{P}_{m}(x)\Bigr]}_{=0\text{ for $n\neq m$}}\;w(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=N_{n}^{2}\,\delta_{nm}.

(80)

The expression in brackets
[\mathcal{P}_{n}(-x)\,\mathcal{P}_{m}(-x)+\mathcal{P}_{n}(x)\,\mathcal{P}_{m}(x)]
vanishes whenever exclusively one of the two functions is odd, either
\mathcal{P}_{n}(-x)=-\mathcal{P}_{n}(x)
or
\mathcal{P}_{m}(-x)=-\mathcal{P}_{m}(x)
, while the other one is even. The highest power in a polynomial corresponds to its degree, odd powers of
x^{2k+1}
to odd function, even powers of
x^{2k}
to even functions, so consequently orthogonal polynomials for the interval
-1\leq x\leq 1
and weight
w(-x)=w(x)
alternate between even and odd in
m
to obtain orthogonality:
Odd-degree orthogonal polynomials contain only odd powers, even-degree orthogonal polynomials only even powers.

E.3

Three-term recurrence of orthogonal polynomials

Normally, we define
\mathcal{P}_{0}(x)=1
and any higher-degree polynomial by a simple recurrence relation, hence based on the lower degree polynomials. Finding the recurrence relation requires some consideration.

Orthogonality yields that we don’t need an orthogonal polynomial of the
n^{\mathrm{th}}
degree
\mathcal{P}_{n}(x)
to represent an arbitrary lower-degree polynomial
\mathscr{P}_{m<n}(x)
. This becomes clear when expanding the polynomial
\mathscr{P}_{m<n}(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{m<n}\beta_{k}\,\mathcal{P}_{k}(x)
and considering the transform integral with
\mathcal{P}_{n}
vanishing
\int\mathcal{P}_{k<n}\mathcal{P}_{n}=0
for every term because of orthogonality. We may write

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}\mathscr{P}_{m<n}(x)\,\mathcal{P}_{n}(x)\;w(x)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=0.

(81)

After multiplication with
x
, an orthogonal polynomial of degree
n-1
becomes an
n^{\mathrm{th}}
-degree polynomial

\displaystyle x\,\mathcal{P}_{n-1}(x)=\mathscr{P}_{n}(x).

(82)

We can find the expansion coefficients,
\beta_{m}
, thereof into orthogonal polynomials by integrating
x\,\mathcal{P}_{n-1}(x)
over
\mathcal{P}_{m}(x)
. By applying the knowledge from above, we see which coefficients must vanish:

\displaystyle\beta_{m<n-2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N_{m<n-2}^{2}}\int_{a}^{b}\overbrace{\mathcal{P}_{m<n-2}(x)\,x}^{\mathscr{P}_{m<n-1}(x)}\,\mathcal{P}_{n-1}(x)\;w(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=0,

\displaystyle\beta_{m>n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N_{m>n}^{2}}\int_{a}^{b}\mathcal{P}_{m>n}(x)\,\underbrace{x\,\mathcal{P}_{n-1}(x)}_{\mathscr{P}_{n}(x)}\;w(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=0.

Obviously,
x\,\mathcal{P}_{n-1}(x)
only consists of
\mathcal{P}_{m}(x)
with
m=n-2,n-1,n
:
Any set of orthogonal polynomials obeys a three-term recurrence:

\displaystyle x\,\mathcal{P}_{n-1}(x)

\displaystyle=\beta_{n}^{(n)}\,\mathcal{P}_{n}(x)+\beta_{n-1}^{(n)}\,\mathcal{P}_{n-1}(x)+\beta_{n-2}^{(n)}\,\mathcal{P}_{n-2}(x)

(83)

that can be used as
\mathcal{P}_{n}(x)=\frac{1}{\beta_{n}^{(n)}}\Bigl[\bigl(x-\beta_{n-1}^{(n)}\bigr)\,\mathcal{P}_{n-1}(x)-\beta_{n-2}^{(n)}\,\mathcal{P}_{n-2}(x)\Bigr]
to calculate higher-degree polynomials based on the lower-degree ones.
The recurrence reduces for
-a=b=1
and
w(-x)=w(x)
as this requires
\mathcal{P}_{n}
to only contain even or odd powers of
x
,

\displaystyle x\,\mathcal{P}_{n-1}(x)=\beta_{n}^{(n)}\,\mathcal{P}_{n}(x)+\beta_{n-2}^{(n)}\,\mathcal{P}_{n-2}(x).

(84)

E.4

Explicit three-term recurrence

For the Gegenbauer polynomials
C_{n}^{(\alpha)}(x)=\sum_{l=0}^{\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor}\frac{(-1)^{l}\,2^{n-2l}(\alpha)^{\overline{n-l}}}{l!(n-2l)!}\,x^{n-2l}
of eq. (

), we can obtain an explicit three-term recurrence by comparing the highest powers; divided by common factors
\frac{2^{n}(\alpha)^{\overline{n}}}{0!n!}
and
\frac{2^{n-1}(\alpha)^{\overline{n-1}}}{0!(n-2)!}
in the columns on the right

\displaystyle C_{n+1}^{(\alpha)}

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{n+1}(\alpha)^{\overline{n+1}}}{0!(n+1)!}x^{n+1}

\displaystyle-\frac{2^{n-1}(\alpha)^{\overline{n}}}{1!(n-1)!}x^{n-1}

\displaystyle\dots\bigg|

\displaystyle\frac{2(\alpha+n)}{n+1}

\displaystyle x^{n+1}\bigg|

\displaystyle-(\alpha+n-1)

\displaystyle x^{n-1}

\displaystyle xC_{n}^{(\alpha)}

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{n}(\alpha)^{\overline{n}}}{0!n!}x^{n+1}

\displaystyle-\frac{2^{n-2}(\alpha)^{\overline{n-1}}}{1!(n-2)!}x^{n-1}

\displaystyle\dots\bigg|

\displaystyle x^{n+1}\bigg|

\displaystyle-\frac{n-1}{2}

\displaystyle x^{n-1}

\displaystyle C_{n-1}^{(\alpha)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2^{n-1}(\alpha)^{\overline{n-1}}}{0!(n-2)!}x^{n-1}

\displaystyle\dots\bigg|

\displaystyle\phantom{x^{n+1}}\bigg|

\displaystyle x^{n-1}

Combining
(n+1)C_{n+1}^{(\alpha)}-2(\alpha+n)xC_{n}^{(\alpha)}
obviously cancels the power

and leaves
-(n+1)(\alpha+n-1)+(n-1)(\alpha+n)=-2\alpha-n+1
to cancel by

. We get

\displaystyle 2(n+\alpha)\,x\,C_{n}^{(\alpha)}=(n+1)C_{n+1}^{(\alpha)}+(n+2\alpha-1)C_{n-1}^{(\alpha)}.

(85)

For standardization to
\mathcal{P}_{n}(1)=1

If we standardize the Gegenbauer polynomials to ultraspherical ones
\mathcal{P}_{n}(1)=1
for all

, eq. (

) imposes the condition that
\beta_{n}^{(n)}+\beta_{n-2}^{(n)}=1
, so that
\beta_{n-2}^{(n)}=1-\beta_{n}^{(n)}
. We skip the superscript and write
\beta_{n}
for the recurrence coefficient,

\displaystyle x\,\mathcal{P}_{n-1}(x)=\beta_{n}\,\mathcal{P}_{n}(x)+(1-\beta_{n})\,\mathcal{P}_{n-2}(x).

(86)

We obtain the recurrence coefficient
\beta_{n}
from comparing coefficients of the highest powers in
\mathcal{P}_{n}
and
\mathcal{P}_{n-1}
. This is done based on the coefficient recurrence for
c_{n}^{(n)}
,

\displaystyle\beta_{n}\,c_{n}^{(n)}

\displaystyle=c_{n-1}^{(n-1)}\Longrightarrow\beta_{n}=\frac{c_{n-1}^{(n-1)}}{c_{n}^{(n)}}.

From above we know about the existence and nature of three-term recurrences.
For the polynomials with
\mathcal{P}_{n}(1)=1
in eqs.(
72
) (
98
), we get a ratio of the
l=0
coefficients for
n
and
n+1
as

\beta_{n+1}=\frac{c_{n}^{(n)}}{c_{n+1}^{(n+1)}}=\frac{2^{n}\,\Gamma(n+\alpha)}{\Gamma(n+2\alpha)}\frac{\Gamma(n+2\alpha+1)}{2^{n+1}\,\Gamma(n+\alpha+1)}=\frac{n+2\alpha}{2(n+\alpha)}
and
1-\beta_{n+1}=\frac{n}{2(n+\alpha)}
for a recurrence as in eq. (
86
)

\displaystyle 2(n+\alpha)\,x\,\mathcal{P}_{n}

\displaystyle=(n+2\alpha)\mathcal{P}_{n+1}+n\mathcal{P}_{n-1}.

(87)

E.5

Squared norm of orthogonal polynomials

According to Law and Sledd
[
LS75
]
, the recurrence coefficient
\beta_{n}
can also be used to evaluate the normalization
N_{n}^{2}=\int_{-1}^{1}[\mathcal{P}_{n}(x)]^{2}\,w(x)\,\mathrm{d}x
of the polynomial recursively.
This is done by re-formulating the three-term recurrence to

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{n}

\displaystyle=(A_{n}x+B_{n})\mathcal{P}_{n-1}-C_{n}\mathcal{P}_{n-2}.

(88)

Law and Sledd’s derivation is based on explicitly evaluating only the first term of the recurrence until
\mathcal{P}_{0}=1
, while the rest must stay some polynomial of the degree
n-1

\mathcal{P}_{n}=A_{n}x\mathcal{P}_{n-1}+\mathscr{A}_{n-1}=A_{n}A_{n-1}x^{2}\mathcal{P}_{n-2}+\mathscr{B}_{n-1}=A_{n}A_{n-1}\dots A_{1}x^{n}+\mathscr{C}_{n-1},

or more neatly

\displaystyle x^{n}=(A_{n}A_{n-1}\dots A_{1})^{-1}\mathcal{P}_{n}+\mathscr{P}_{n-1}.

(89)

Integration over
\mathcal{P}_{n}\,w\,\mathrm{d}x\,\int_{a}^{b}
yields

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}x^{n}\mathcal{P}_{n}\,w\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=(A_{n}A_{n-1}\dots A_{1})^{-1}\underbrace{\int_{a}^{b}(\mathcal{P}_{n})^{2}\,w\,\mathrm{d}x}_{=N_{n}^{2}}+\underbrace{\int_{a}^{b}\mathscr{P}_{n-1}\,\mathcal{P}_{n}\,w\,\mathrm{d}x}_{=0}

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}x^{n}\mathcal{P}_{n}\,w\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=(A_{n}A_{n-1}\dots A_{1})^{-1}\,N_{n}^{2}.

(90)

x^{n}\mathcal{P}_{n}
is re-expressed by re-arranging eq. (
88
) to

x\mathcal{P}_{n}=A_{n+1}^{-1}[\mathcal{P}_{n+1}-B_{n+1}\mathcal{P}_{n}+C_{n+1}\mathcal{P}_{n-1}]

and multiplication with
x^{n-1}=(A_{n-1}\dots A_{1})^{-1}[\mathcal{P}_{n-1}+\mathscr{P}_{n-2}]
from eq. (
89
),

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}x^{n}\mathcal{P}_{n}\,w\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\frac{\int_{a}^{b}[\mathcal{P}_{n+1}-B_{n+1}\mathcal{P}_{n}+C_{n+1}\mathcal{P}_{n-1}][\mathcal{P}_{n-1}+\mathscr{P}_{n-2}]\,w\,\mathrm{d}x}{A_{n+1}(A_{n-1}\dots A_{1})}

\displaystyle=\frac{C_{n+1}}{A_{n+1}(A_{n-1}\dots A_{1})}\underbrace{\int_{a}^{b}(\mathcal{P}_{n-1})^{2}\,w\,\mathrm{d}x}_{=N_{n-1}^{2}}.

(91)

Together with eq. (
90
), we get for

\displaystyle N_{n}^{2}=\frac{C_{n+1}A_{n}}{A_{n+1}}N_{n-1}^{2},

(92)

which is in our
\mathcal{P}_{n}(1)=1
standardized case with

\beta_{n}\mathcal{P}_{n}(x)=x\mathcal{P}_{n-1}-(1-\beta_{n})\mathcal{P}_{n-2}

hence
A_{n}=\frac{1}{\beta_{n}}
, and
C_{n}=\frac{1-\beta_{n}}{\beta_{n}}
, as well as
\mathcal{P}_{0}=1
and
\int_{-1}^{1}(\mathcal{P}_{0})^{2}\,w(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=\frac{S_{\mathrm{D}-1}}{S_{\mathrm{D}-2}}
, and so

\displaystyle N_{n}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1-\beta_{n+1}}{\beta_{n}}\,N_{n-1}^{2}=\frac{S_{\mathrm{D}-1}}{S_{\mathrm{D}-2}}\prod_{k=1}^{n}\frac{1-\beta_{k+1}}{\beta_{k}};

(93)

see Appdx.
E.11
for more details.

E.6

Christoffel-Darboux formula

From the general three-term recurrence

\displaystyle A_{n+1}x\mathcal{P}_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{P}_{n+1}(x)-B_{n+1}\mathcal{P}_{n}(x)+C_{n+1}\mathcal{P}_{n-1}(x)

one finds the Cristoffel-Darboux formula by expanding with
\mathcal{P}_{n}(y)
and
(x-y)

\displaystyle A_{n+1}(x-y)\mathcal{P}_{n}(x)\mathcal{P}_{n}(y)

\displaystyle=[\mathcal{P}_{n+1}(x)\mathcal{P}_{n}(y)-\mathcal{P}_{n}(x)\mathcal{P}_{n+1}(y)]

\displaystyle+C_{n+1}[\mathcal{P}_{n-1}(x)\mathcal{P}_{n}(y)-\mathcal{P}_{n}(x)\mathcal{P}_{n-1}(y)].

Multiplying with
\frac{1}{A_{n+1}N_{n}^{2}}
yields with the normalization recurrence
\frac{A_{n+1}}{C_{n+1}}N_{n}^{2}=A_{n}\,N_{n-1}^{2}
from eq. (
92
)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathcal{P}_{n}(x)\mathcal{P}_{n}(y)}{N_{n}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\frac{\mathcal{P}_{n+1}(x)\mathcal{P}_{n}(y)-\mathcal{P}_{n}(x)\mathcal{P}_{n+1}(y)}{(x-y)\,A_{n+1}N_{n}^{2}}}_{\mathcal{F}_{n}(x,y)}+\underbrace{\frac{\mathcal{P}_{n-1}(x)\mathcal{P}_{n}(y)-\mathcal{P}_{n}(x)\mathcal{P}_{n-1}(y)}{(x-y)\,A_{n}N_{n-1}^{2}}}_{-\mathcal{F}_{n-1}(x,y)}.

Adding all expressions of smaller
n
until
n=0
, where
\mathcal{F}_{-1}(x,y)=0
, yields the Christoffel-Darboux formula decomposing an axisymmetric Dirac delta
\delta(x-y)

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\mathrm{N}}\frac{\mathcal{P}_{n}(x)\mathcal{P}_{n}(y)}{N_{n}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{N}+1}(x)\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{N}}(y)-\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{N}}(x)\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{N}+1}(y)}{(x-y)\,A_{\mathrm{N}+1}N_{\mathrm{N}}^{2}}.

(94)

If
y=x
, the fraction exhibits a pole and the confluent Christoffel-Darboux identity is obtained by
y=x+\Delta x
and taking the limit
\lim_{\Delta x\rightarrow 0}

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\mathrm{N}}\frac{[\mathcal{P}_{n}(x)]^{2}}{N_{n}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\Delta x\rightarrow 0}\frac{\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{N}+1}(x)\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{N}}(x+\Delta x)-\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{N}}(x)\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{N}+1}(x+\Delta x)}{-\Delta x\,A_{\mathrm{N}+1}N_{\mathrm{N}}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{N}}(x)\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{N}+1}^{\prime}(x)-\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{N}+1}(x)\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{N}}^{\prime}(x)}{A_{\mathrm{N}+1}N_{\mathrm{N}}^{2}},

(95)

which is obviously finite.
This equation is useful to simplify the finite-degree representation of a Dirac delta at
y=1
, as, e.g., found in Rafaely’s book
[
Raf19
]
. For normalization at
x=1
, all factors can be disregarded and using the derivative at
x=1
in eq. (
99
), we find
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{N+1}}^{\prime}(1)-\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{N}}^{\prime}(1)=\frac{(\mathrm{N}+1)(\mathrm{N}+\mathrm{D}-1)-\mathrm{N}(\mathrm{N}+\mathrm{D}-2)}{\mathrm{D}-1}=\frac{2\mathrm{N}+\mathrm{D}-1}{\mathrm{D}-1}
so that
g(1)=1
and elsewhere eq. (
94
) holds with the newly obtained factor

\displaystyle g(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{D}-1}{2\mathrm{N}+\mathrm{D}-1}\frac{\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{N}+1}(x)-\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{N}}(x)}{x-1}.

(96)

E.10

Rodrigues’ formula for Gegenbauer polynomials.

The French mathematician Rodrigues provided a formula for the Legendre polynomials that are orthogonal using the integral measure
w(x)=1
. A general Rodrigues formula for a more arbitrary measure
w(x)=(1-x^{2})^{\alpha-\frac{1}{2}}
can be given to specify the corresponding orthogonal polynomials
\mathcal{P}_{n}
.

The ultraspherical (Gegenbauer) differential equation for axisymmetric harmonic solutions in
\alpha=\frac{\mathrm{D}}{2}-1
dimensions is

\displaystyle(1-x^{2})\mathcal{P}_{n}^{\prime\prime}(x)-(2\alpha+1)x\mathcal{P}_{n}^{\prime}(x)+n(n+2\alpha)\mathcal{P}_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=0,

(102)

and it is solved by the generalized Rodrigues formula

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{n}(x)=e_{n}^{-1}(1-x^{2})^{-\alpha+{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{n}}{\mathrm{d}x^{n}}(1-x^{2})^{\alpha-{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}+n}.

Proof.

To show this, we start with the inner-most function

f_{n}=(1-x^{2})^{\alpha-{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}+n}

that the solution is constructed of and derive it

f_{n}^{\prime}=(n+\alpha-{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}})(1-x^{2})^{\alpha-\frac{1}{2}+n-1}(-2x)=-2(n+\alpha-{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}})x(1-x^{2})^{-1}f_{n},

yielding a differential equation after multiplication with
(1-x^{2})
:

(1-x^{2})f_{n}^{\prime}+(2n+2\alpha-1)xf_{n}=0.

Deriving again yields a second-order differential equation

\displaystyle(1-x^{2})f_{n}^{\prime\prime}-2xf_{n}^{\prime}+(2n+2\alpha-1)xf_{n}^{\prime}+(2n+2\alpha-1)f_{n}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle(1-x^{2})f_{n}^{\prime\prime}+(2n+2\alpha-3)xf_{n}^{\prime}+(2n+2\alpha-1)f_{n}

\displaystyle=0.

Now this second-order differential equation in
f_{n}=(1-x^{2})^{\alpha-\frac{1}{2}+n}
is derived
n
times requiring for the first two expressions the Leibniz rule for repeated differentiation of products
\frac{\mathrm{d}^{n}}{\mathrm{d}x^{n}}(f\,g)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{n!}{k!(n-k)!}f^{(k)}\,g^{(n-k)}
,

\displaystyle(1-x^{2})f_{n}^{(n+2)}-2nxf_{n}^{(n+1)}-n(n-1)f_{n}^{(n)}

\displaystyle+(2n+2\alpha-3)xf_{n}^{(n+1)}+n(2n+2\alpha-3)f_{n}^{(n)}

\displaystyle+(2n+2\alpha-1)f_{n}^{(n)}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle(1-x^{2})f_{n}^{(n+2)}+(2\alpha-3)xf_{n}^{(n+1)}+\left[n(n+2\alpha)+(2\alpha-1)\right]f_{n}^{(n)}

\displaystyle=0.

The above differential equation is second-order for
f_{n}^{(n)}=\frac{\mathrm{d}^{n}}{\mathrm{d}x^{n}}(1-x^{2})^{n}
, which is related to the Rodrigues solution by
f_{n}^{(n)}=e_{n}(1-x^{2})^{\alpha-\frac{1}{2}}\,\mathcal{P}_{n}
, which upon insertion and omission of
e_{n}
becomes

\displaystyle(1-x^{2})[(1-x^{2})^{\alpha-\frac{1}{2}}\,\mathcal{P}_{n}]^{\prime\prime}+(2\alpha-3)x[(1-x^{2})^{\alpha-\frac{1}{2}}\,\mathcal{P}_{n}]^{\prime}

\displaystyle+\left[n(n+2\alpha)+(2\alpha-1)\right](1-x^{2})^{\alpha-\frac{1}{2}}\,\mathcal{P}_{n}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle(1-x^{2})[(1-x^{2})^{\alpha-\frac{1}{2}}\,\mathcal{P}_{n}^{\prime\prime}-2(2\alpha-1)x(1-x^{2})^{\alpha-\frac{3}{2}}\mathcal{P}_{n}^{\prime}\quad

\displaystyle-(2\alpha-1)(1-x^{2})^{\alpha-\frac{3}{2}}\mathcal{P}_{n}+(2\alpha-1)(2\alpha-3)x^{2}(1-x^{2})^{\alpha-\frac{5}{2}}\mathcal{P}_{n}]

\displaystyle+(2\alpha-3)x[(1-x^{2})^{\alpha-\frac{1}{2}}\,\mathcal{P}_{n}^{\prime}-(2\alpha-1)x(1-x^{2})^{\alpha-\frac{3}{2}}\,\mathcal{P}_{n}]

\displaystyle+\left[n(n+2\alpha)+(2\alpha-1)\right](1-x^{2})^{\alpha-\frac{1}{2}}\,\mathcal{P}_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=0\quad\Big|(1-x^{2})^{-\alpha+\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle(1-x^{2})[\mathcal{P}_{n}^{\prime\prime}-2(2\alpha-1)x(1-x^{2})^{-1}\mathcal{P}_{n}^{\prime}\quad

\displaystyle-\cancel{(2\alpha-1)(1-x^{2})^{-1}\mathcal{P}_{n}}+\cancel{(2\alpha-1)(2\alpha-3)x^{2}(1-x^{2})^{-2}\mathcal{P}_{n}}]

\displaystyle+(2\alpha-3)x[\mathcal{P}_{n}^{\prime}-\cancel{(2\alpha-1)x(1-x^{2})^{-1}\,\mathcal{P}_{n}}]

\displaystyle+\left[n(n+2\alpha)+\cancel{(2\alpha-1)}\right]\,\mathcal{P}_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle(1-x^{2})\mathcal{P}_{n}^{\prime\prime}(x)-(2\alpha+1)x\mathcal{P}_{n}^{\prime}(x)+n(n+2\alpha)\mathcal{P}_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=0.

As this is the ultraspherical (Gegenbauer) differential equation, and we obtained it by the
(n+2)^{\mathrm{th}}
derivative of
f_{n}=(1-x^{2})^{\alpha-\frac{1}{2}+n}
and its substitution
f_{n}^{(n)}=e_{n}(1-x^{2})^{\alpha-\frac{1}{2}}\mathcal{P}_{n}
, we hereby proved Rodrigues’ solution

\mathcal{P}_{n}=e_{n}^{-1}(1-x^{2})^{-\alpha+\frac{1}{2}}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{n}}{\mathrm{d}x^{n}}(1-x^{2})^{\alpha-\frac{1}{2}+n}
.

To find the normalization factor
e_{n}
for standardization of
\mathcal{P}_{n}(1)
, here for
\mathcal{P}_{n}(1)=1
, we apply Leibniz’s rule for repeated differentiation

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=e_{n}^{-1}(1-x^{2})^{-\alpha+\frac{1}{2}}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{n}}{\mathrm{d}x^{n}}[(1-x)^{\alpha-\frac{1}{2}+n}(1+x)^{\alpha-\frac{1}{2}+n}]

\displaystyle=e_{n}^{-1}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{n!}{k!(n-k)!}\frac{(1+x)^{-\alpha+\frac{1}{2}}\mathrm{d}^{k}(1+x)^{\alpha-\frac{1}{2}+n}}{\mathrm{d}x^{k}}\frac{(1-x)^{-\alpha+\frac{1}{2}}\mathrm{d}^{n-k}(1-x)^{\alpha-\frac{1}{2}+n}}{\mathrm{d}x^{n-k}}

\displaystyle=e_{n}^{-1}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{n!}{k!(n-k)!}\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+n+\frac{1}{2})(1+x)^{n-k}}{\Gamma(\alpha+n-k+\frac{1}{2})}\underbrace{\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+n+\frac{1}{2})(-1)^{n-k}(1-x)^{k}}{\Gamma(\alpha+k+\frac{1}{2})}}_{\neq 0:x=1:k=0}

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{n}(x)\Big|_{1}

\displaystyle=e_{n}^{-1}\,\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+n+\frac{1}{2})(2)^{n}(-1)^{n}}{\Gamma(\alpha+\frac{1}{2})}=1

\displaystyle e_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{(2)^{n}\Gamma(\alpha+n+\frac{1}{2})}{(-1)^{n}\Gamma(\alpha+\frac{1}{2})}=\frac{2^{n}(\alpha+\frac{1}{2})^{\overline{n}}}{(-1)^{n}}.

(103)

Hereby, Rodrigues’ formula for Gegenbauer polynomials standardized to ultraspherical ones
\mathcal{P}_{n}(1)=1
becomes:

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{n}(x)=\frac{(-1)^{n}}{2^{n}(\alpha+\frac{1}{2})^{\overline{n}}}(1-x^{2})^{-\alpha+\frac{1}{2}}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{n}}{\mathrm{d}x^{n}}(1-x^{2})^{\alpha-\frac{1}{2}+n},

(104)

and alternatively we could write with the integration weight
w(x)=(1-x^{2})^{\alpha-\frac{1}{2}}
inserted

\mathcal{P}_{n}(x)=\frac{(-1)^{n}}{(2\alpha+1)^{\overline{n}}\,w(x)}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{n}}{\mathrm{d}x^{n}}\left[(1-x^{2})^{n}\,w(x)\right]
.

The typical Gegenbauer standardization is
C_{n}^{(\alpha)}(1)=\frac{\Gamma(n+2\alpha)}{n!\Gamma(2\alpha)}=\frac{(2\alpha)^{\overline{n}}}{n!}
as in eq. (
97
), and its Rodrigues’ formula is obtained by multiplying the above with
\frac{(2\alpha)^{\overline{n}}}{n!}
, cf. eq. (
97
),

\displaystyle C_{n}^{(\alpha)}(x)=\frac{(-1)^{n}(2\alpha)^{\overline{n}}}{n!2^{n}(\alpha+\frac{1}{2})^{\overline{n}}}(1-x^{2})^{-\alpha+\frac{1}{2}}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{n}}{\mathrm{d}x^{n}}(1-x^{2})^{\alpha-\frac{1}{2}+n}.

(105)

E.11

Norm of Gegenbauer polynomials

For the transform integral, the squared norm

As the zeroth-degree polynomial is

, its norm

should be easiest to find. The others follow from eq. (
92
).

Finding

.

Using all coordinates of the
\mathrm{D}
-dimensional space, the integral over
x
the last one of multiple integrals.
In the sequence of the
(\mathrm{D}-1)
-fold integration over
1
to determine the surface
S_{\mathrm{D}-1}
of the
\mathbb{S}^{\mathrm{D}-1}
unit sphere in a
\mathrm{D}
-dimensional space, the integral over
x
delivers the distinguishing factor between the surfaces
S_{\mathrm{D}-1}
and
S_{\mathrm{D}-2}
, the one of the next lower-dimensional unit sphere, cf.
[
HC37
, vol.2, p.387]
,

\displaystyle S_{\mathrm{D}-1}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\iiint\,\mathrm{d}S_{\mathrm{D}-2}}_{=S_{\mathrm{D}-2}}\cdot\,\underbrace{\int_{-1}^{1}\,\sqrt{1-x^{2}}^{\mathrm{D}-3}\,\mathrm{d}x}_{=N_{0}^{2}}.

(106)

With
S_{\mathrm{D}-1}=\frac{2\,\sqrt{\pi}^{\mathrm{D}}}{\Gamma(\frac{\mathrm{D}}{2})}

[
HC37
, vol.2, p.387]
, we conclude that

\displaystyle N_{0}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{S_{\mathrm{D}-1}}{S_{\mathrm{D}-2}}=\frac{\sqrt{\pi}\,\Gamma(\frac{\mathrm{D}-1}{2})}{\Gamma(\frac{\mathrm{D}}{2})}.

(107)

For 2 dimensions
\mathrm{D}=2
we get

, and for 3 dimensions
\mathrm{D}=3

, for both kinds
\mathcal{P}_{n}
and
C_{n}^{(\alpha)}

Squared norm for any degree
N_{n}^{2}
for
C_{n}^{(\alpha)}
.

eq. (
92
) obtains the norm
N_{n}^{2}=\frac{C_{n+1}A_{n}}{A_{n+1}}N_{n-1}^{2}
of higher-degree polynomials recursively from

.
We use the recurrence
C_{n}^{(\alpha)}=\underbrace{\textstyle\frac{2(n+\alpha-1)}{n}}_{A_{n}}x\,C_{n-1}^{(\alpha)}-\underbrace{\textstyle\frac{n+2\alpha-2}{n}}_{C_{n}}\,C_{n-2}^{(\alpha)}

to find the squared norm, and
\alpha=\frac{\mathrm{D}}{2}-1

\displaystyle\bigg[\sum_{n=0}^{\mathrm{N}-1}\frac{\mathcal{P}_{n}(r_{\mathrm{E}})\mathcal{P}_{n+1}(r_{\mathrm{E}})}{S_{\mathrm{D}-2}N_{n+1}^{2}}(1-\beta_{n+2})+\sum_{n^{\prime}=0}^{\mathrm{N}-1}\frac{\mathcal{P}_{n^{\prime}+1}(r_{\mathrm{E}})\mathcal{P}_{n^{\prime}}(r_{\mathrm{E}})}{S_{\mathrm{D}-2}N_{n^{\prime}+1}^{2}}\beta_{n^{\prime}}\bigg]

\displaystyle=r_{\mathrm{E}}\bigg[\sum_{n=0}^{\mathrm{N}}\frac{\mathcal{P}_{n}(r_{\mathrm{E}})\mathcal{P}_{n}(r_{\mathrm{E}})}{S_{\mathrm{D}-2}N_{n}^{2}}\bigg]

We’d nearly precisely get the same right-hand-side term after expanding one of the
\mathcal{P}_{n}(r_{\mathrm{E}})
terms in the sum by the factor
r_{\mathrm{E}}
from outside the sum, using the recurrence
r_{\mathrm{E}}\,\mathcal{P}_{n}(r_{\mathrm{E}})=\beta_{n+1}\mathcal{P}_{n+1}(r_{\mathrm{E}})+(1-\beta_{n+1})\mathcal{P}_{n-1}(r_{\mathrm{E}})
. And yet, there is a decisive difference: no sum cuts away the degree
\mathrm{N}+1
expression, which wasn’t part of the sums on the left. Therefore, a feasible finite-degree solution for
r_{\mathrm{E}}
requires a vanishing term

\displaystyle\beta_{\mathrm{N}+1}\,\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{N}+1}(r_{\mathrm{E}})\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{N}}(r_{\mathrm{E}})=0.

(134)

Because
a_{\mathrm{N}}\neq 0
should not vanish and
\beta_{\mathrm{N}+1}\neq 0
, the feasibility constraint is that

\displaystyle\arg\max\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{N}+1}(r_{\mathrm{E}})=0.

(135)

Therefore, as defined in
[
DRP99
,
Dan01
]
, the largest root of
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{N}+1}
defines the coefficients
a_{n}
for a maximum-length
\bm{r}_{\mathrm{E}}
vector.

Interestingly, this corresponds to decomposing an axisymmetric Dirac delta
\delta(x-\max r_{\mathrm{E}})
: a concentric ring (3D) or a pair of points (2D) around the panning direction, which could be expressed in short by the Christoffel-Darboux identity eq. (
94
) with
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{N}+1}(\max r_{\mathrm{E}})=0

\displaystyle g(\cos\vartheta)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{N}+1}(\cos\vartheta)\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{N}}(\max r_{\mathrm{E}})}{\cos\vartheta-\max r_{\mathrm{E}}}.

(136)

F.5

(Ultra-)Spherical cap function

For the coefficients of a spherical cap function, one needs to integrate the polynomial over
w(x)
and the corresponding segment of the sphere
x_{0}\leq x\leq 1
.

The coefficient needed for expanding a spherical cap function is obtained by integrating
a_{n}=\int_{x_{0}}^{1}\mathcal{P}_{n}(x)\,w(x)\,\mathrm{d}x
, with the weight function
w(x)=(1-x^{2})^{\alpha-\frac{1}{2}}
.

We can manipulate Rodrigues’ formula eq. (
104
) in the form with
n+1

\displaystyle\frac{2^{n+1}(\alpha+\frac{1}{2})^{\overline{n+1}}}{(-1)^{n+1}}\;w(x)\,\mathcal{P}_{n+1}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{d}^{n+1}}{\mathrm{d}x^{n+1}}(1-x^{2})^{\alpha+n+\frac{1}{2}},

by carrying out one of the derivatives on the right to get

\displaystyle\frac{2^{n+1}(\alpha+\frac{1}{2})^{\overline{n+1}}}{(-1)^{n+1}}\,

\displaystyle w(x)\,\mathcal{P}_{n+1}(x)=(\alpha+n+{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}})\frac{\mathrm{d}^{n}}{\mathrm{d}x^{n}}(-2x)(1-x^{2})^{\alpha+n-\frac{1}{2}},

\displaystyle=-(2\alpha+2n+1)\bigg[\underbrace{x\frac{\mathrm{d}^{n}(1-x^{2})^{\alpha+n-\frac{1}{2}}}{\mathrm{d}x^{n}}}_{\frac{2^{n}(\alpha+\frac{1}{2})^{\overline{n}}}{(-1)^{n}}x\,w(x)\,\mathcal{P}_{n}(x)}+n\,\underbrace{\frac{\mathrm{d}^{n-1}(1-x^{2})^{\alpha+n-\frac{1}{2}}}{\mathrm{d}x^{n-1}}}_{\frac{2^{n}(\alpha+\frac{1}{2})^{\overline{n}}}{(-1)^{n}}\int\mathcal{P}_{n}(x)\,w(x)\,\mathrm{d}x}\bigg]

\displaystyle\frac{2\alpha+2n+1}{2\alpha+2n+1}\;w(x)\,

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{n+1}(x)=x\,w(x)\,\mathcal{P}_{n}(x)+n\,\int\mathcal{P}_{n}(x)\,w(x)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{n+1}(x)=x\,\mathcal{P}_{n}(x)+\frac{n}{w(x)}\,\int\mathcal{P}_{n}(x)\,w(x)\,\mathrm{d}x

(138)

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow\int\mathcal{P}_{n}(x)\,w(x)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\frac{w(x)}{n}\,\bigg[\mathcal{P}_{n+1}(x)-x\,\mathcal{P}_{n}(x)\bigg].

(139)

The recurrence
x\mathcal{P}_{n}(x)=\frac{n+2\alpha}{2n+2\alpha}\mathcal{P}_{n+1}(x)+\frac{n}{2n+2\alpha}\mathcal{P}_{n-1}(x)
yields the coefficients

\displaystyle a_{n}=\int_{x_{0}}^{1}\mathcal{P}_{n}(x)\,w(x)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\frac{w(x)}{2n+2\alpha}\,\bigg[\mathcal{P}_{n+1}(x)-\mathcal{P}_{n-1}(x)\bigg]\Bigg|_{x_{0}}^{1}

(140)

\displaystyle=\frac{w(x_{0})}{2n+2\alpha}\,\bigg[\mathcal{P}_{n-1}(x_{0})-\mathcal{P}_{n+1}(x_{0})\bigg].

For the exception
n=0
, the integral to solve is
a_{0}=\int_{x_{0}}^{1}w(x)\,\mathrm{d}x
, which is

\displaystyle a_{0}=\int_{x_{0}}^{1}\sqrt{1-x^{2}}^{\mathrm{D}-3}\,\mathrm{d}x=\begin{cases}\arccos(x_{0})=\upvarphi_{0},&\mathrm{D}=2\\
1-x_{0},&\mathrm{D}=3\\
F(\frac{1}{2},\frac{2-\mathrm{D}}{2};\frac{3}{2},1)-x_{0}\,F(\frac{1}{2},\frac{2-\mathrm{D}}{2};\frac{3}{2},x_{0}^{2}),&\text{any $\mathrm{D}$}\end{cases}

(141)

using Gauß’ hypergeometric function
F(a,b;c,z)
.

F.6

Inphase/higher-order cardioid pattern

A higher-order cardioid pattern has an
\mathrm{N}
-fold zero at
x=-1
and is defined as

\displaystyle g_{\mathrm{N}}

\displaystyle={\textstyle\frac{1}{2^{\mathrm{N}}}}\,(1+x)^{\mathrm{N}},

(142)

and we want to find the coefficients
a_{n}
for its expansion

\displaystyle g_{\mathrm{N}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{a_{n}}{N_{n}^{2}}\,\mathcal{P}_{n}(x).

(143)

Deriving
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}(1+x)^{\mathrm{N}+1}=(\mathrm{N}+1)(1+x)^{\mathrm{N}}
, and then integrating it from its zero at
x=-1
to
x
, we se that
g_{\mathrm{N}}
can be obtained from the integral equation
(1+x)\,y=(\mathrm{N}+1)\int_{-1}^{x}y\;\mathrm{d}x
.
We use the integration and multiplication recurrences:

2(n+\alpha)\int_{-1}^{x}\mathcal{P}_{n}\mathrm{d}x=\frac{n+2\alpha}{n+1}\mathcal{P}_{n+1}-\frac{n}{n+2\alpha-1}\mathcal{P}_{n-1}
, and

2(n+\alpha)(1+x)\,\mathcal{P}_{n}=(n+2\alpha)\mathcal{P}_{n+1}+2(n+\alpha)\mathcal{P}_{n}+n\mathcal{P}_{n-1}

to write
(\mathrm{N}+1)\int y\,\mathrm{d}x-(1+x)\,y=0
:

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\mathrm{N}}{\textstyle\frac{a_{n}}{N_{n}^{2}2(n+\alpha)}}\,[{\textstyle\frac{(\mathrm{N}+1)(n+2\alpha)-(n+1)(n+2\alpha)}{n+1}}\mathcal{P}_{n+1}-2(n+\alpha)\mathcal{P}_{n}-{\textstyle\frac{(\mathrm{N}+1)n+(n+2\alpha-1)n}{n+2\alpha-1}}\mathcal{P}_{n-1}]

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\mathrm{N}}{\textstyle\frac{a_{n}}{N_{n}^{2}}}\,[{\textstyle\frac{(\mathrm{N}-n)(n+2\alpha)}{2(n+\alpha)(n+1)}}\mathcal{P}_{n+1}-\mathcal{P}_{n}-{\textstyle\frac{(\mathrm{N}+n+2\alpha)n}{2(n+\alpha)(n+2\alpha-1)}}\mathcal{P}_{n-1}]

\displaystyle=0,

and by applying orthogonality when integrating over
\int_{-1}^{1}\mathcal{P}_{n^{\prime}}\mathcal{P}_{n}\mathrm{d}x=N_{n}^{2}\delta_{nn^{\prime}}
we find
after using

N_{n}^{2}N_{n-1}^{-2}=\frac{(n+\alpha-1)n}{(n+\alpha)(n+2\alpha-1)}
and

N_{n}^{2}N_{n+1}^{-2}=\frac{(n+\alpha+1)(n+2\alpha)}{(n+\alpha)(n+1)}
the difference equation

\displaystyle{\textstyle\frac{(\mathrm{N}-n+1)(n+2\alpha-1)}{2(n+\alpha-1)n}}{\textstyle\frac{N_{n}^{2}\,a_{n-1}}{N_{n-1}^{2}}}-{\textstyle\frac{N_{n}^{2}\,a_{n}}{N_{n}^{2}}}-{\textstyle\frac{(\mathrm{N}+n+2\alpha+1)(n+1)}{2(n+\alpha+1)(n+2\alpha)}}{\textstyle\frac{N_{n}^{2}\,a_{n+1}}{N_{n+1}^{2}}}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\textstyle(\mathrm{N}-n+1)\,a_{n-1}-2(n+\alpha)\,a_{n}-(\mathrm{N}+n+2\alpha+1)\,a_{n+1}

\displaystyle=0,

and we can guess that
a_{n}
must contain
(\mathrm{N}-n)^{-1}
to cancel the factor
(\mathrm{N}-n+1)
with an index shift to
a_{n-1}
and
(\mathrm{N}+n+2\alpha)^{-1}
to cancel the factor
(\mathrm{N}+n+2\alpha+1)
with an index shift to
a_{n+1}
. We guess a decaying solution of factorials ending at these numbers as denominator, we insert and divide by the solution to test

\displaystyle 1/a_{n}

\displaystyle=(\mathrm{N}-n)!(\mathrm{N}+n+2\alpha)!

\displaystyle(\mathrm{N}+n+2\alpha)-2(n+\alpha)-(\mathrm{N}-n)

\displaystyle=0,\qquad\blacksquare

We express the solution normalized to its first weight
a_{0}=1
and with the dimensionality constant written as

2\alpha=\mathrm{D}-2
as

\displaystyle a_{n}=\frac{\mathrm{N}!(\mathrm{N}+\mathrm{D}-2)!}{(\mathrm{N}-n)!(\mathrm{N}+n+\mathrm{D}-2)!}.

(144)

The
\mathrm{N}
-fold zero at
x=-1
can be modulated to
x=1
by sign alteration for the odd polynomials, i.e.
a_{n}=(-1)^{n}\frac{\mathrm{N}!(\mathrm{N}+\mathrm{D}-2)!}{(\mathrm{N}-n)!(\mathrm{N}+n+\mathrm{D}-2)!}
.

F.7

Max-flat pattern: generalized higher-order cardioid

This was inspired by the maxflat FIR filter
firflatLp(N,K,M)

in Schüßler’s DSP2 book
[
Sch10
, p.92]
.
A polynomial with
\mathrm{L}
-fold zero at
x=1
and
\mathrm{M}
-fold zero at
x=-1
integrated yields a pattern that is zero with
\mathrm{L}
-degree flatness at
x=-1
and a positive constant with
\mathrm{M}
-degree flatness at
x=1
, with
\mathrm{N}=\mathrm{L}+\mathrm{M}+1
, a Butterworth design

\displaystyle g_{\mathrm{N}}

\displaystyle=\frac{(\mathrm{L+M}+1)!}{2^{\mathrm{L+M}+1}\,\mathrm{L}!\,\mathrm{M}!}\int_{-1}^{x}(1-x)^{\mathrm{L}}(1+x)^{\mathrm{M}}\,\mathrm{d}x.

(145)

The factor provides
g_{\mathrm{N}}(1)=1
and is obtained by applying partial integration recursively,

\displaystyle(m+1)\int_{-1}^{1}(1-x)^{l}(1+x)^{m}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=(1-x)^{l}(1+x)^{m+1}\big|_{-1}^{1}+l\,\int_{-1}^{1}(1-x)^{l-1}(1+x)^{m+1}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle(m+1)\,g_{lm}

\displaystyle=g_{l,m+1}^{\prime}+l\,g_{l-1,m+1}

\displaystyle(m+1)(m+2)\,g_{lm}

\displaystyle=(m+2)g_{l,m+1}^{\prime}+l\,g_{l-1,m+2}^{\prime}+l\,(l-1)\,g_{l-2,m+2}

\displaystyle\frac{(m+l+1)!}{m!}g_{lm}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\sum_{k=0}^{l}\frac{(m+l+1)!\,l!}{(m+k+1)!(l-k)!}\,g_{l-k,m+k+1}^{\prime}}_{g_{l-k,m+k+1}^{\prime}|_{1}=0\forall k\neq l,\hskip 8.19447ptg_{l-k,m+k+1}^{\prime}|_{-1}=0\forall k}+0\,g_{-1,m+l+1}

\displaystyle\frac{(m+l+1)!}{m!}\,g_{lm}\big|_{1}

\displaystyle=l!\,g_{0,m+l+1}^{\prime}\big|_{1}=l!\,2^{m+l+1}

\displaystyle g_{lm}\big|_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{l+m+1}\,l!\,m!}{(l+m+1)!}.

We can call
g_{\mathrm{N}}=y
and derive twice to get

(1-x^{2})\;y^{\prime\prime}=\mathrm{M}(1-x)\;y^{\prime}-\mathrm{L}(1+x)\;y^{\prime}
, i.e.
(1-x^{2})\;y^{\prime\prime}+(\mathrm{M}+\mathrm{L})\,x\,y^{\prime}-(\mathrm{M}-\mathrm{L})\,y^{\prime}=0
.
We can insert
y=\sum_{n=0}^{\mathrm{N}}\frac{a_{n}}{N_{n}^{2}}\mathcal{P}_{n}

and get:

\displaystyle(1-x^{2})\sum_{n=0}^{\mathrm{N}}\frac{a_{n}}{N_{n}^{2}}\mathcal{P}_{n}^{\prime\prime}+(\mathrm{M}+\mathrm{L})\,x\sum_{n=0}^{\mathrm{N}}\frac{a_{n}}{N_{n}^{2}}\mathcal{P}_{n}^{\prime}+(\mathrm{M}-\mathrm{L})\sum_{n=0}^{\mathrm{N}}\frac{a_{n}}{N_{n}^{2}}\mathcal{P}_{n}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=0,

(146)

and make use of

(1-x^{2})\mathcal{P}_{n}^{\prime\prime}=(1+2\alpha)\,x\,\mathcal{P}_{n}^{\prime}-n(n+2\alpha)\,\mathcal{P}_{n}
,

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\mathrm{N}}\frac{a_{n}}{N_{n}^{2}}\,\left[(\mathrm{M}+\mathrm{L}+2\alpha+1)x\mathcal{P}_{n}^{\prime}-n(n+2\alpha)\,\mathcal{P}_{n}+(\mathrm{M}-\mathrm{L})\,\mathcal{P}_{n}^{\prime}\right]=0,

(147)

We integrate to get

\sum_{n=0}^{\mathrm{N}}\frac{a_{n}}{N_{n}^{2}}\,\bigg\{(\mathrm{M}+\mathrm{L}+2\alpha+1)\int x\,\mathcal{P}_{n}^{\prime}\,\mathrm{d}x-n(n+2\alpha)\,\int\mathcal{P}_{n}\,\mathrm{d}x+(\mathrm{M}-\mathrm{L})\,\mathcal{P}_{n}\bigg\}=C,

(148)

and make use of

2(n+\alpha)\int_{-1}^{x}x\,\mathcal{P}_{n}^{\prime}\,\mathrm{d}x=n(n+2\alpha)[\frac{1}{n+1}\mathcal{P}_{n+1}+\frac{1}{n+2\alpha-1}\mathcal{P}_{n-1}]

2(n+\alpha)\int_{-1}^{x}\mathcal{P}_{n}\mathrm{d}x=\frac{(n+2\alpha)}{n+1}\mathcal{P}_{n+1}-\frac{n}{n+2\alpha-1}\mathcal{P}_{n-1}

\textstyle\sum_{n=0}^{\mathrm{N}}\frac{a_{n}}{N_{n}^{2}}\,\bigg\{\frac{n(n+2\alpha)(\mathrm{M}+\mathrm{L}-n+1)}{(n+1)2(n+\alpha)}\,\mathcal{P}_{n+1}+\frac{n(n+2\alpha)(\mathrm{M}+\mathrm{L}+n+2\alpha+1)}{(n+2\alpha-1)2(n+\alpha)\,}\,\mathcal{P}_{n-1}+(\mathrm{M}-\mathrm{L})\,\mathcal{P}_{n}\bigg\}=C.

(149)

With the orthogonality when integrating over
\mathcal{P}_{n}

\textstyle\frac{N_{n}^{2}a_{n-1}}{N_{n-1}^{2}}\,\frac{(n-1)(n+2\alpha-1)(\mathrm{M}+\mathrm{L}-n+2)}{2n(n+\alpha-1)}+\frac{N_{n}^{2}a_{n+1}}{N_{n+1}^{2}}\,\frac{(n+1)(n+2\alpha+1)(\mathrm{M}+\mathrm{L}+n+2\alpha+2)}{2(n+2\alpha)(n+\alpha+1)}+a_{n}\,(\mathrm{M}-\mathrm{L})=E\,\delta_{n}.

(150)

N_{n}^{2}N_{n-1}^{-2}=\frac{2(n+\alpha-1)n}{2(n+\alpha)(n+2\alpha-1)}
and

N_{n}^{2}N_{n+1}^{-2}=\frac{2(n+\alpha+1)(n+2\alpha)}{2(n+\alpha)(n+1)}
yield
the difference equation

\textstyle a_{n-1}\,(n-1)(\mathrm{M}+\mathrm{L}-n+2)+a_{n+1}\,(n+2\alpha+1)(\mathrm{M}+\mathrm{L}+n+2\alpha+2)\\
+a_{n}\,(\mathrm{M}-\mathrm{L})2(n+\alpha)=E\,\delta_{n}.

(151)

As the resulting sequence
a_{n}
should be limited to the degree
\mathrm{N}=\mathrm{M}+\mathrm{L}+1
, we can abbreviate the recurrence by
\mathrm{N}
and
\Delta\mathrm{N}
as

\displaystyle\textstyle(\mathrm{N}-n+1)(n-1)\,a_{n-1}+2\Delta\mathrm{N}(n+\alpha)\,a_{n}+(\mathrm{N}+n+2\alpha+1)(n+2\alpha+1)\,a_{n+1}=E\,\delta_{n}.

We can focus on using the recurrence for
n>0
so that we can omit
E\,\delta_{n}
and write

\displaystyle a_{n+1}=-\frac{(\mathrm{N}-n+1)(n-1)\,a_{n-1}+2\Delta\mathrm{N}(n+\alpha)\,a_{n}}{(\mathrm{N}+n+2\alpha+1)(n+2\alpha+1)},\quad n\geq 1.

(152)

It is useful to start from assuming
a_{1}=1
and obtaining
a_{n>1}
from the above iteration with
n=1,\dots,\mathrm{N}-1
.
The missing value for
a_{0}
can be obtained as the offset at
x=-1
, where
g_{\mathrm{N}}(-1)=0
should have a zero, and where
\mathcal{P}_{n}(-1)=(-1)^{n}
consequently yields

\displaystyle a_{0}

\displaystyle=-N_{0}^{2}\sum_{n=1}^{\mathrm{N}}\frac{a_{n}}{N_{n}^{2}}\mathcal{P}_{n}(-1)=-\sum_{n=1}^{\mathrm{N}}\frac{(-1)^{n}(2n+\mathrm{D}-2)\;(n+\mathrm{D}-3)!}{n!}\,a_{n}.

(153)

Scaling can be fixed to unity by dividing all the
a_{n}\leftarrow a_{n}/b
by
b=g_{\mathrm{N}}(1)-g_{\mathrm{N}}(-1)
,

\displaystyle b

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{\mathrm{N}}\frac{[1-(-1)^{n}](2n+\mathrm{D}-2)\;(n+\mathrm{D}-3)!}{n!}\,a_{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor\frac{\mathrm{N}-1}{2}\rfloor}\frac{(4k+\mathrm{D})\;(2k+\mathrm{D}-2)!}{(2k+1)!}\,a_{2k+1}.

(154)

For a non-recurrent, closed-form solution as for the non-generalized higher-order cardioid, only a simplified setting
\mathrm{M}=\mathrm{L}
helps so that the
a_{n}
term vanishes. We get

\displaystyle\textstyle(n-2)(\mathrm{N}-n+2)\,a_{n-2}+(n+2\alpha)(\mathrm{N}+n+2\alpha)\,a_{n}=0,

(155)

\displaystyle\textstyle a_{n}=\frac{(-1)^{\frac{n}{2}}(n-2)!!(2\alpha)!!(\mathrm{N}+2\alpha)!!\mathrm{N}!!}{(n+2\alpha)!!(\mathrm{N}+n+2\alpha)!!(\mathrm{N}-n)!!}

(156)

\displaystyle\textstyle\frac{(n-2)(\mathrm{N}-n+2)}{(n+2\alpha)(\mathrm{N}+n+2\alpha)}\frac{(n-4)!!}{(n+2\alpha-2)!!(\mathrm{N}+n+2\alpha-2)!!(\mathrm{N}-n+2)!!}-\frac{(n-2)!!}{(n+2\alpha)!!(\mathrm{N}+n+2\alpha)!!(\mathrm{N}-n)!!}=0\quad\blacksquare

(157)

Appendix H
2D Ambisonic panning as Fourier series

Appendices
A
to
G
derived the ultraspherical polynomials that uniformly satisfy arbitrary dimensions
\mathrm{D}
. Appendices
H
.1 until
H
.4 here are an optional, reassuring meditation/verification exercise that prove the results for
\mathrm{D}=2
based on the Fourier series.

H.1

Panning function as Fourier series

Assuming that we first try to find a continuous panning function, we can regard the Fourier series as suitable means to express this function. The Fourier coefficient
a_{|m|}\,e^{-\mathrm{i}m\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}}
is able to control the direction and length of the
\bm{r}_{\mathrm{E}}
- and
\bm{r}_{\mathrm{V}}
vector perfectly:

\displaystyle g(\varphi)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty}a_{|m|}\,e^{-\mathrm{i}m\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}}\,e^{\mathrm{i}m\varphi}.

(158)

While
a_{|m|}=1
, yields a directional Dirac delta
g(\varphi)=\delta(\varphi-\varphi_{\mathrm{s}})
, values for
a_{|m|}
tending to zero for large
|m|
yield a smoother curve
g(\varphi)
that is easier to sample.

Before looking into sampling the panning function at the loudspeaker positions, we seek whether the definitions of the linear and quadratic loudness and vector models are invariant with regard to the panning direction
\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}
, and what their relation is to
a_{|m|}
.

For the loudness models, this is easily shown by replacing the integration variable
\varphi
by
\phi=\varphi-\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}
, and for the quadratic measure by considering orthogonality:

\displaystyle P

\displaystyle=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}g(\varphi)\,\mathrm{d}\varphi=\sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{a_{m}}{2\pi}\underbrace{\int_{-\pi-\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}}^{\pi-\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}}e^{\mathrm{i}m\phi}\,\mathrm{d}\phi}_{:=2\pi\,\delta_{m0}}=a_{0},

(159)

\displaystyle E

\displaystyle=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}|g(\varphi)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\varphi=\sum_{m^{\prime}=-\infty}^{\infty}\sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{a_{m}a_{m^{\prime}}^{*}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\underbrace{\int_{-\pi-\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}}^{\pi-\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}}e^{\mathrm{i}(m-m^{\prime})\phi}\,\mathrm{d}\phi}_{:=2\pi\,\delta_{mm^{\prime}}}=\sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{|a_{m}|^{2}}{2\pi}.

(160)

Obviously the quadratic loudness measure involves all of the Fourier terms
a_{m}
(Parseval’s theorem) while the linear one only considers
a_{0}
.

The notation as complex phasors (
x
as real and
y
as imaginary part) simplifies the calculation of the vector models, i.e. using
\bm{\theta}=e^{\mathrm{i}\varphi}
. Orthogonality yields further simplifications:

\displaystyle{\bm{r}_{\mathrm{V}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}g(\varphi)\,e^{\mathrm{i}\varphi}\,\mathrm{d}\varphi}{V}=\frac{\sum_{m}\frac{a_{m}}{2\pi}\,e^{-\mathrm{i}m\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}}\overbrace{\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}e^{\mathrm{i}(m+1)\varphi}\mathrm{d}\varphi}^{2\pi\delta_{m+1,0}}}{a_{0}}=\frac{a_{1}}{a_{0}}e^{\mathrm{i}\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}},

(161)

\displaystyle{\bm{r}_{\mathrm{E}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}|g(\varphi)|^{2}\,e^{\mathrm{i}\varphi}\,\mathrm{d}\varphi}{E}=\frac{\sum_{m,m^{\prime}}\frac{a_{m}a_{m^{\prime}}^{*}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,e^{-\mathrm{i}(m-m^{\prime})\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}}\overbrace{\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}e^{\mathrm{i}(m-m^{\prime}+1)\varphi}\mathrm{d}\varphi}^{2\pi\delta_{m-m^{\prime}+1,0}}}{\sum_{m}\frac{|a_{m}|^{2}}{2\pi}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty}a_{m}a_{m+1}^{*}}{\sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty}|a_{m}|^{2}}\,e^{\mathrm{i}\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}}.

(162)

In all of the above results, the vectors point exactly into the desired direction. The vector component
e^{-\mathrm{i}m\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}}
just undergoes a sign change but remains untouched otherwise. Note that this is largely fulfilled independently of what the weights
a_{m}
exactly look like.

The lengths of both vectors is independent of the panning direction
\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}
. This can also be logically concluded from two facts, see
subsection H.2
: (1)
g(\varphi)
is symmetric around
\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}
, (2) and its shape is preserved under rotation. The length
\|\mathrm{r}_{\mathrm{V}}\|
only depends on the weights
a_{0}
and
a_{1}
, while the length
\|\bm{r}_{\mathrm{E}}\|
depends on all the Fourier coefficient weights
a_{m}
involved, which seems to be important as the
\bm{r}_{\mathrm{E}}
vector was an excellent predictor in the listening experiments about direction and width.

Surely, both the quadratic criteria,
E
and
\bm{r}_{\mathrm{E}}
are the more critical ones when considering the continuous panning function as they involve all of the weights
a_{m}
.

H.2

Finite-order Fourier series

Rotation invariant shape.

The represented shape of a
2\pi
-periodic function in a finite-order Fourier series is rotation-invariant. For
2\pi\,g(\varphi)=\sum_{m=-\mathrm{N}}^{\mathrm{N}}a_{|m|}\,e^{\mathrm{i}m(\varphi-\varphi_{\mathrm{s}})}
with arbitrary aiming
\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}
, rotation by
-\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}
is losslessly expressed by modified coefficients
a_{|m|}\,e^{+\mathrm{i}m\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}}
(
shift theorem
), proved by
\sum_{m=-\mathrm{N}}^{\mathrm{N}}a_{|m|}\,e^{\mathrm{i}m\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}}\,e^{\mathrm{i}m(\varphi-\varphi_{\mathrm{s}})}=\sum_{m=-\mathrm{N}}^{\mathrm{N}}a_{|m|}\,e^{\mathrm{i}m\varphi}
, which obviously is
2\pi\,g(\varphi+\varphi_{\mathrm{s}})
, the shape of the original function rotated to aim at
0
.

Symmetry.

A real-valued and symmetric Fourier coefficient
a_{|m|}=\Re\{a_{\pm m}\}
yields a symmetric real shape

\sum_{m=-\mathrm{N}}^{\mathrm{N}}a_{m}\,e^{\mathrm{i}m\phi}=a_{0}\,e^{\mathrm{i}0\phi}+\sum_{m=1}^{\mathrm{N}}\left(a_{-m}\,e^{-\mathrm{i}m\phi}+a_{m}\,e^{\mathrm{i}m\phi}\right)
. Symmetry yields for the second term
\sum_{m=1}^{\mathrm{N}}2a_{m}\Re\{e^{-\mathrm{i}m\phi}\}=\sum_{m=1}^{\mathrm{N}}2a_{m}\cos(m\phi)
. Such a cosine series is symmetric around
\phi=\varphi-\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}
, i.e. around the panning direction.

Is
\gamma_{m}=a_{|m|}
a symmetric rectangular function, the finite geometric sum lets us obtain

\displaystyle g(\varphi)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_{m=-N}^{N}e^{\mathrm{i}m\phi}=\frac{e^{-\mathrm{i}m\phi}-e^{\mathrm{i}(m+1)\phi}}{1-e^{\mathrm{i}\phi}}=\frac{\sin[(m+\frac{1}{2})\phi]}{2\pi\sin\frac{\phi}{2}},

(163)

the periodic sinc function shifted to
\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}
. However, there are better ways to define
a_{|m|}
.

H.3

Finite-order series with maximum
\bm{r}_{\mathrm{E}}
vector length

The maximization of the
\bm{r}_{\mathrm{E}}
vector length by optimal Fourier weights
a_{m}
yields a narrower perceived width and beneficial sidelobe suppression
[
DRP99
]
.
For this purpose, the length
r_{\mathrm{E}}=\frac{\sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty}a_{m}a_{m+1}}{\sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty}a_{m}^{2}}
is differentiated with regard to the weight
a_{m}
and equated to zero.

The numerator can be re-written to
\sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty}a_{m}a_{m+1}=\sum_{m^{\prime}=0}^{\infty}a_{m^{\prime}}a_{m^{\prime}+1}+a_{m^{\prime}}a_{m^{\prime}-1}
and the denominator to

\sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty}a_{m}^{2}=\sum_{m^{\prime}=0}^{\infty}(2-\delta_{m^{\prime}})a_{m^{\prime}}^{2}
.
Formally, the differentiated fraction equated to zero
r_{\mathrm{E}}=\frac{A}{B}
can be simplified to
r_{\mathrm{E}}^{\prime}=\frac{A^{\prime}}{B}-\frac{AB^{\prime}}{B^{2}}=\frac{A^{\prime}}{B}-\frac{r_{\mathrm{E}}\,B^{\prime}}{B}
, and we may require
A^{\prime}-r_{\mathrm{E}}\,B^{\prime}=0

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial a_{m}}\Biggl(\sum_{m^{\prime}=0}^{\infty}a_{m^{\prime}}a_{m^{\prime}+1}+a_{m^{\prime}}a_{m^{\prime}-1}\Biggr)-r_{\mathrm{E}}\;\frac{\partial}{\partial a_{m}}\Biggl(\sum_{m^{\prime}=0}^{\infty}(2-\delta_{m^{\prime}})\,a_{m^{\prime}}^{2}\Biggr)

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle a_{m+1}+a_{m-1}-(2-\delta_{m})\,r_{\mathrm{E}}\,a_{m}

\displaystyle=0.

(164)

As a result to our optimization problem we obviously got a recurrence relation for both
a_{m}
and the minimum
r_{\mathrm{E}}
. We find a recurrence in
