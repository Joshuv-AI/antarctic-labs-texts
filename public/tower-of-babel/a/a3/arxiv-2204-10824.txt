Scalable symmetric Tucker tensor decomposition
Ruhui Jin, Joe Kileel, Tamara G. Kolda, and Rachel Ward

Abstract
We study the best low-rank Tucker decomposition of symmetric tensors. The motivating application is decomposing higher-order multivariate moments. Moment tensors have special structure and are important to various data science problems. We advocate for projected gradient descent (PGD) method and higher-order eigenvalue decomposition (HOEVD) approximation as computation schemes. Most importantly, we develop scalable adaptations of the basic PGD and HOEVD methods to decompose sample moment tensors. With the help of implicit and streaming techniques, we evade the overhead cost of building and storing the moment tensor. Such reductions make computing the Tucker decomposition realizable for large data instances in high dimensions. Numerical experiments demonstrate the efficiency of the algorithms and the applicability of moment tensor decompositions to real-world datasets. Finally we study the convergence on the Grassmannian manifold, and prove that the update sequence derived by the PGD solver achieves first- and second-order criticality.

2.2
Problem statement

Let
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{X}}\in\mathbb{S}^{d}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
be a symmetric tensor. Fix a user-specified rank

\min_{\boldsymbol{\mathscr{C}}\in\mathbb{S}^{d}(\mathbb{R}^{r})\atop\mathbf{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}}\left\|\boldsymbol{\mathscr{X}}-\boldsymbol{\mathscr{C}}\cdot\left(\mathbf{Q}^{\top},\dots,\mathbf{Q}^{\top}\right)\right\|^{2},~~\text{subject~to}~\mathbf{Q}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}=\mathbf{I}_{r}.

(2)

In fact, the minimization (

\mathbf{Q}
that maximizes the following cost function:

\max_{\mathbf{Q}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}=\mathbf{I}_{r}}F(\mathbf{Q})\equiv\left\|\boldsymbol{\mathscr{X}}\cdot\left(\mathbf{Q},\dots,\mathbf{Q}\right)\right\|^{2}.

(
*

Commonly, there is a huge amount of data in exceedingly high dimensions. We hence further turn to the streaming strategy to speed up the computations.
Here data is revealed as a sequence of sample batches
\{\mathbf{X}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{X}_{t},\dots\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n\times b}
with batch size
b
(
b\ll p
).
We read each batch as it arrives but discard it afterwards. This streaming model is called the turnstile model
[
50
,
42
,
63
]
.

Therefore, as the
t
-th
(t\geq 1)
batch
\mathbf{X}_{t}=[\mathbf{x}_{t,1},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{t,b}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times b}
arrives, the cost function (
11
) is recast stochastically:

F_{t}(\mathbf{Q}):=\left\|\frac{1}{b}\sum_{i=1}^{b}\left(\mathbf{Q}^{\top}\mathbf{x}_{t,i}\right)^{\otimes d}\right\|^{2}.

(12)

In light of (
10
), we can compute the stochastic gradient in a tensor-free manner:

\begin{array}[]{ll}\nabla F_{t}(\mathbf{Q})&\displaystyle=2d\,\langle\frac{1}{b}\sum_{i=1}^{b}\mathbf{x}_{t,i}\otimes\left(\mathbf{Q}^{\top}\mathbf{x}_{t,i}\right)^{\otimes(d-1)},\frac{1}{b}\sum_{j=1}^{b}\left(\mathbf{Q}^{\top}\mathbf{x}_{t,j}\right)^{\otimes d}\rangle_{-1}\\
&\displaystyle=\frac{2d}{b^{2}}\,\sum_{i=1}^{b}\sum_{j=1}^{b}\mathbf{x}_{t,i}\langle\mathbf{Q}^{\top}\mathbf{x}_{t,i},\mathbf{Q}^{\top}\mathbf{x}_{t,j}\rangle^{d-1}\mathbf{x}_{t,j}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}\\
&\displaystyle=\frac{2d}{b^{2}}\,\mathbf{X}_{t}\left(\mathbf{X}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{Q}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{t}\right)^{[d-1]}\mathbf{X}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}\,\,\in\,\,\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}.\end{array}

(13)

Here,
\left(~\cdot~\right)^{[d-1]}
means raising each element of the input matrix to the
(d-1)
-th power.
Computing the gradient implicitly via online samples reduces the cost to
\mathcal{O}(rnb+rb^{2})
flops per iteration. This improves upon
\mathcal{O}(rnp+rp^{2})
using the full amount of samples, and a cost that is exponential in
d

if we operate on the explicit tensor in
\mathbb{S}^{d}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

Moreover, a scalable HOEVD solver,

4.1
Set-up

In these tests, we generate moment tensors with approximate low-rank symmetric Tucker structure (
3
). One way is to assume data instances
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
follow a linear factor model:

\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{B}\mathbf{f}+\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}.

(15)

Here we fix the factor loading matrix
\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r_{\text{true}}}
.
The latent factor
\mathbf{f}\in\mathbb{R}^{r_{\text{true}}}
is drawn from a coordinate-independent, zero-mean and unit variance normal-inverse Gaussian (NIG) distribution
[
5
,
6
]
. This distribution is skewed and heavy-tailed, aligning with some motivations discussed in
Section

1
.
The noise
{\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is Gaussian, following
\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}_{n})
.

Note that in (
15
), the
d
-th moment of
\mathbf{x}
roughly admits a low-rank Tucker format:

\begin{array}[]{lll}\boldsymbol{\mathscr{M}}^{d}&\approx\mathbb{E}\left(\mathbf{x}^{\otimes d}\right)&\approx\,\,\,\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left((\mathbf{B}\mathbf{f})^{\otimes d}\right)\\
&&\displaystyle=\,\,\,\mathbb{E}\left((\mathbf{R}^{*}\mathbf{f})^{\otimes d}\right)\cdot\left(\mathbf{Q}^{*\top},\dots,\mathbf{Q}^{*\top}\right).\end{array}

The matrices
\mathbf{Q}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r_{\text{true}}},\mathbf{R}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{r_{\text{true}}\times r_{\text{true}}}
are from the QR factorization of
\mathbf{B}
.

We define the inverse signal-to-noise ratio

\texttt{SNR}^{-1}:=\frac{\|\mathbb{E}\left(\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}\right)\|}{\|\mathbb{E}\left(\mathbf{B}\mathbf{f}\right)\|}=\frac{\sigma\sqrt{n}}{\|\mathbf{B}\|}.

(16)

The above quantity (rather than the deviation
\sigma
) is to measure the model’s noise level.

Remark 6
.

Our goal is to simply perform the symmetric Tucker decomposition of the sample moment tensor
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{M}}^{d}\in\mathbb{S}^{d}(\mathbb{R}^{n}).
This is different from inference procedures on the linear factor model, e.g., independent component analysis (ICA)
[
14
]
.

Remark 7
.

Besides the model (
15
) or other heavy-tailed datasets, we emphasize that our proposed algorithms are able to efficiently decompose
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{M}}^{d}
when the sample vectors come from any probabilistic model.

4.5

We establish

\displaystyle=2\operatorname{sym}\Big((\mathbf{I}_{n}-\phi(\mathbf{P}))\left(\Delta\mathbf{P}+2\gamma w(\mathbf{P})\Delta\mathbf{P}+2\gamma v(\mathbf{P})[\Delta\mathbf{P}]\right)(\mathbf{P}+2\gamma w(\mathbf{P})\mathbf{P})^{{\dagger}}\Big).\end{array}

\operatorname{Gr}(n,r)

is measure-zero in
\operatorname{Gr}(n,r)

Given that the step size satisfies

\phi
is a local diffeomorphism by

for all

As

, the set

has zero measure for each

.
Then
\mathcal{J}

is a countable union of measure-zero sets, so has zero measure.

To conclude,
define

as the pre-image of

\operatorname{Gr}(n,r)

For all initializations

7
Conclusion and future work

In this paper, we developed the PGD method (
Algorithm

1
) for symmetric Tucker tensor decomposition. It has a simple update scheme compared to other iterative solvers running on Riemannian manifolds.
We further designed the SPGD method (
Algorithm

2
) and SHOEVD solver (
Algorithm

3
) to decompose the sample moment tensor (
1
). The algorithms are free of constructing and storing moment tensors, and their iterations are conducted by streaming only a small subset of samples.
Tremendous computational savings were seen in numerical experiments with minimal loss of accuracy.
We also showed the applicability of the Tucker decomposition of moment tensors in comparison to the CP format, as well as to detect anomalies and allocate assets on real datasets.
Finally, utilizing Riemannian manifold optimization theory, we derived theoretical guarantees for the PGD sequence on the Grassmannian. The sequence always converges to a first-order critical point, and almost surely to a second-order critical point.

There are a number of directions that would be interesting to study in the future.

1.

Implementation for cumulants.

Cumulant tensors are central to non-Gaussian data analysis.
They are nonlinear combinations of moments.
It would be useful to derive scalable implementations for sample cumulants.

2.

Extending the analysis.
It is certainly worth investigating convergence guarantees in the streaming setting with adaptive step sizes of
Algorithms

2
and
3
.
We also want to explore under what conditions is the limiting point guaranteed to be a global optimizer.

3.

Estimating the core without fresh samples.
Currently, the core tensor
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{C}}
is computed using independent data samples after the subspace
\mathbf{Q}
has been calculated. We would like to figure out a good way of updating
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{C}}
while updating
\mathbf{Q}
, using the same data stream.

Appendix B
Proof of
Proposition

6.3

Proof.

We first compute the Riemannian gradient,
\operatorname{grad}f(\mathbf{P})\in\text{T}_{\mathbf{P}}\operatorname{Gr}(n,r)
. It is the orthogonal projection of the Euclidean gradient
\nabla f(\mathbf{P})\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}
onto the tangent space at
\mathbf{P}
.
The projection
\pi_{\mathbf{P}}:\mathbb{S}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\to\text{T}_{\mathbf{P}}\operatorname{Gr}(n,r)
is computed as follows
[
27
, Proposition 2.1]
:

\pi_{\mathbf{P}}(\mathbf{X})=\text{ad}^{2}_{\mathbf{P}}(\mathbf{X})\in\text{T}_{\mathbf{P}}\operatorname{Gr}(n,r)~\text{with}~\text{ad}_{\mathbf{P}}^{2}=\text{ad}_{\mathbf{P}}\circ\text{ad}_{\mathbf{P}}~\text{and}~\text{ad}_{\mathbf{P}}(\mathbf{X})=\mathbf{P}\mathbf{X}-\mathbf{X}\mathbf{P}

for
\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{S}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Here,
ad
stands for the adjoint operator.

The Euclidean gradient of
f
(
22
) is

\begin{array}[]{ll}\nabla f(\mathbf{P})&=d\left\langle\boldsymbol{\mathscr{X}}\cdot\left(\mathbf{I}_{n},\mathbf{P},\dots,\mathbf{P}\right),\boldsymbol{\mathscr{X}}\cdot\left(\mathbf{P},\dots,\mathbf{P}\right)\right\rangle_{-1}\\
&~~+d\left\langle\boldsymbol{\mathscr{X}}\cdot\left(\mathbf{P},\dots,\mathbf{P}\right),\boldsymbol{\mathscr{X}}\cdot\left(\mathbf{I}_{n},\mathbf{P},\dots,\mathbf{P}\right)\right\rangle_{-1}\\
&=w(\mathbf{P})\mathbf{P}+\mathbf{P}w(\mathbf{P}).\end{array}

(51)

Hence the Riemannian gradient equals

\begin{array}[]{ll}\operatorname{grad}f(\mathbf{P})&=\text{ad}^{2}_{\mathbf{P}}\left(\nabla f(\mathbf{P})\right)\\
&=\text{ad}_{\mathbf{P}}\left(\mathbf{P}\nabla f(\mathbf{P})-\nabla f(\mathbf{P})\mathbf{P}\right)\\
&=\mathbf{P}\left(\mathbf{P}\nabla f(\mathbf{P})-\nabla f(\mathbf{P})\mathbf{P}\right)-\left(\mathbf{P}\nabla f(\mathbf{P})-\nabla f(\mathbf{P})\mathbf{P}\right)\mathbf{P}\\
&=\mathbf{P}\left(\nabla f(\mathbf{P})\right)-2\mathbf{P}\left(\nabla f(\mathbf{P})\right)\mathbf{P}+\left(\nabla f(\mathbf{P})\right)\mathbf{P}\end{array}

Substituting (
51
) into the above equation,

\begin{array}[]{ll}\operatorname{grad}f(\mathbf{P})&=\mathbf{P}\left(w(\mathbf{P})\mathbf{P}+\mathbf{P}w(\mathbf{P})\right)\\
&~~-2\mathbf{P}\left(w(\mathbf{P})\mathbf{P}+\mathbf{P}w(\mathbf{P})\right)\mathbf{P}+\left(w(\mathbf{P})\mathbf{P}+\mathbf{P}w(\mathbf{P})\right)\mathbf{P}\\
&=\mathbf{P}w(\mathbf{P})\left(\mathbf{I}_{n}-\mathbf{P}\right)+\left(\mathbf{I}_{n}-\mathbf{P}\right)w(\mathbf{P})\mathbf{P}=\operatorname{sym}\Big(2\left(\mathbf{I}_{n}-\mathbf{P}\right)w(\mathbf{P})\mathbf{P}\Big).\end{array}

(52)

Next, we relate the gradients of
f(\mathbf{P})
and
F(\mathbf{Q})
. The Euclidean gradient of
F
is

\begin{array}[]{ll}\nabla F(\mathbf{Q})&=2d\left\langle\boldsymbol{\mathscr{X}}\cdot\left(\mathbf{I}_{n},\mathbf{Q},\dots,\mathbf{Q}\right),\boldsymbol{\mathscr{X}}\cdot\left(\mathbf{Q},\dots,\mathbf{Q}\right)\right\rangle_{-1}\\
&=2d\left\langle\boldsymbol{\mathscr{X}}\cdot\left(\mathbf{I}_{n},\mathbf{P},\dots,\mathbf{Q}\right),\boldsymbol{\mathscr{X}}\cdot\left(\mathbf{I}_{n},\mathbf{P},\dots,\mathbf{P}\right)\right\rangle_{-1}\mathbf{Q}\\
&=2d\left\langle\boldsymbol{\mathscr{X}}\cdot\left(\mathbf{I}_{n},\mathbf{P},\dots,\mathbf{P}\right),\boldsymbol{\mathscr{X}}\cdot\left(\mathbf{I}_{n},\mathbf{P},\dots,\mathbf{P}\right)\right\rangle_{-1}\mathbf{Q}=2w(\mathbf{P})\mathbf{Q}.\end{array}

(53)

Therefore,

\left(\mathbf{I}_{n}-\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{Q}^{\top}\right)\nabla F(\mathbf{Q})\mathbf{Q}^{\top}=2\left(\mathbf{I}_{n}-\mathbf{P}\right)w(\mathbf{P})\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{Q}^{\top}=2\left(\mathbf{I}_{n}-\mathbf{P}\right)w(\mathbf{P})\mathbf{P}.

By (
52
), we have

\operatorname{grad}f(\mathbf{P})=\operatorname{sym}\Big(\left(\mathbf{I}_{n}-\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{Q}^{\top}\right)\nabla F(\mathbf{Q})\mathbf{Q}^{\top}\Big).

The proof of
Proposition

6.3
is complete.

\left\|\operatorname{grad}f(\mathbf{P})\right\|=\sqrt{2}\left\|\operatorname{grad}f(\mathbf{P})\mathbf{P}\right\|.

(67)

Then due to triangle inequality and (
65
),

\begin{array}[]{ll}\|\operatorname{grad}f(\mathbf{P})\|&\leq\sqrt{2}\left\|\operatorname{grad}f(\mathbf{P})\alpha_{t}(\mathbf{P})^{\dagger}\right\|+\sqrt{2}\left\|\operatorname{grad}f(\mathbf{P})\left(\mathbf{P}-\alpha_{t}(\mathbf{P})^{\dagger}\right)\right\|\\
&\displaystyle=\sqrt{2}\left\|\operatorname{grad}f(\mathbf{P})\alpha_{t}(\mathbf{P})^{\dagger}\right\|+\sqrt{2}\left\|\operatorname{grad}f(\mathbf{P})\alpha_{t}(\mathbf{P})^{\dagger}\left(\mathbf{P}-\alpha_{t}(\mathbf{P})^{\dagger}\right)\right\|\\
&\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{2}\left\|\operatorname{grad}f(\mathbf{P})\alpha_{t}(\mathbf{P})^{\dagger}\right\|\left(1+\left\|\mathbf{P}-\alpha_{t}(\mathbf{P})^{\dagger}\right\|\right)\\
&\displaystyle=\frac{3\sqrt{2}}{2}\left\|\operatorname{grad}f(\mathbf{P})\alpha_{t}(\mathbf{P})^{\dagger}\right\|.\end{array}

Inserting (
66
) shows the last inequality. Thus,

\left\|\operatorname{grad}f(\mathbf{P})\alpha_{t}(\mathbf{P})^{\dagger}\right\|\geq\frac{\sqrt{2}}{3}\left\|\operatorname{grad}f(\mathbf{P})\right\|.

On the other hand, as
\alpha_{t}(\mathbf{P})^{\dagger}=\mathbf{P}\alpha_{t}(\mathbf{P})^{\dagger},
it holds

\begin{array}[]{ll}\left\|\operatorname{grad}f(\mathbf{P})\alpha_{t}(\mathbf{P})^{\dagger}\right\|&\leq\left\|\operatorname{grad}f(\mathbf{P})\mathbf{P}\right\|\left\|\alpha_{t}(\mathbf{P})^{\dagger}\right\|\\
&\leq\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{2r}}{2}\left\|\operatorname{grad}f(\mathbf{P})\right\|.\end{array}

The results (
67
) and (
63
) give the last derivation. Combining the above two inequalities completes the proof of
Lemma

6.5
.
∎

\begin{array}[]{l}\left\langle\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\bf 0&\mathbf{H}_{i}^{\top}+2\gamma\lambda_{i}(\mathbf{I}_{r}+2\gamma\mathbf{A})^{-1}\mathbf{H}_{i}^{\top}\\
\mathbf{H}_{i}+2\gamma\lambda_{i}\mathbf{H}_{i}(\mathbf{I}_{r}+2\gamma\mathbf{A})^{-1}&\bf 0\end{array}\right],\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\bf 0&\mathbf{H}_{j}^{\top}\\
\mathbf{H}_{j}&\bf 0\end{array}\right]\right\rangle\\
\\
=2\,\left\langle\mathbf{H}_{i}+2\gamma\lambda_{i}\mathbf{H}_{i}(\mathbf{I}_{r}+2\gamma\mathbf{A})^{-1},\mathbf{H}_{j}\right\rangle\\
\\
=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lc}4\gamma\lambda_{i}\left\|\mathbf{H}_{i}(\mathbf{I}_{r}+2\gamma\mathbf{A})^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right\|^{2}+1&i=j\\
4\gamma\lambda_{i}\left\langle\mathbf{H}_{i}(\mathbf{I}_{r}+2\gamma\mathbf{A})^{-\frac{1}{2}},\mathbf{H}_{j}(\mathbf{I}_{r}+2\gamma\mathbf{A})^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right\rangle&i\neq j,\end{array}\right.\end{array}

(82)

where we use

\left\langle\mathbf{H}_{i},\mathbf{H}_{j}\right\rangle=\frac{1}{2}\,\left\langle\Delta\mathbf{P}_{i},\Delta\mathbf{P}_{j}\right\rangle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lc}\frac{1}{2},&i=j\\
0,&i\neq j.\end{array}\right.

We set

\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathbf{L}&=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\text{vec}\left(\mathbf{H}_{1}(\mathbf{I}_{r}+2\gamma\mathbf{A})^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right)^{\top}\\
\vdots\\
\text{vec}\left(\mathbf{H}_{r(n-r)}(\mathbf{I}_{r}+2\gamma\mathbf{A})^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right)^{\top}\end{array}\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{r(n-r)\times r(n-r)},\\
\\
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}&=4\gamma\text{diag}\left(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{r(n-r)}\right)\in\mathbb{S}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{r(n-r)}).\end{array}

(83)

Here
vec
denotes the vectorization operator.
Combining (
82
) and (
83
), we have a short-hand expression for
\mathbf{M}
:

\mathbf{M}=\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\mathbf{L}\mathbf{L}^{\top}+\mathbf{I}_{r(n-r)}.

The linear independence of
\{\mathbf{H}_{i}\}
implies the linear independence of
\{\mathbf{H}_{i}(\mathbf{I}_{r}+2\gamma\mathbf{A})^{-\frac{1}{2}}\}.
Then
\mathbf{L}
is invertible because its rows are linearly independent.
Hence
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\mathbf{L}\mathbf{L}^{\top}
is similar to
\mathbf{L}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\mathbf{L}=\mathbf{L}^{\top}\left({\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}\mathbf{L}\mathbf{L}^{\top}\right)\mathbf{L}^{-\top}
.
As

\mathbf{L}^{\top}{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}\mathbf{L}
is symmetric,

\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\mathbf{L}\mathbf{L}^{\top}
is diagonalizable with the same eigenvalues as
\mathbf{L}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\mathbf{L}.
Then
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
and
\mathbf{L}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\mathbf{L}
are congruent.
So their numbers of positive eigenvalues match.
But there is a strictly positive eigenvalue of
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
, namely
4\gamma\lambda_{1}>0
.
It follows that
\mathbf{M}=\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\mathbf{L}\mathbf{L}^{\top}+\mathbf{I}_{r(n-r)}
has an eigenvalue strictly greater than 1.
The proof of
Lemma

6.7
is complete.
∎

Proof.

We prove
Lemma

6.8
by showing that
\phi
is
C^{\infty}
and
\text{D}\phi(\mathbf{P})
is invertible for all
\mathbf{P}\in\operatorname{Gr}(n,r)

\gamma
is small enough.

First we claim that
\Phi
is
C^{\infty}
on
\operatorname{St}(n,r)
.
It is a composite of
orth
and
\mathbf{Q}\mapsto\mathbf{Q}+\gamma\nabla F(\mathbf{Q})
. The latter is a polynomial map, only taking full-rank values by
C3
of
Section

6.2
.
Meanwhile,
orth
is
C^{\infty}
at full-rank inputs by
[

.
So
\Phi
is
C^{\infty}
.
Consequently
\phi

C^{\infty}
by
[
40

\mathbf{Q}\mapsto\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{Q}^{\top}
is a quotient map from
\operatorname{St}(n,r)
to
\operatorname{Gr}(n,r)
.

Next we show
\text{D}\Phi(\mathbf{Q})
is invertible for all
\mathbf{Q}\in\operatorname{St}(n,r)
. Define
h:\operatorname{St}(n,r)\times\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
by

h(\mathbf{Q},\gamma):=\sigma_{\min}\left(\text{D}\Phi(\mathbf{Q})\right)=\sigma_{\min}\left(\text{D}\texttt{orth}(\mathbf{Q}+\gamma\nabla F(\mathbf{Q}))\right),

where

\Phi(\mathbf{Q})

\Phi(\mathbf{Q})

\mathbf{Q},\gamma

\mathbf{Q},\gamma
.
As the smallest singular value is also continuous,
h
is jointly continuous in
\mathbf{Q},\gamma
.
When
\gamma=0,

\Phi

\operatorname{St}(n,r)
, so
h(\cdot,0)\equiv 1
. Because the Stiefel manifold is compact, there exists a constant
\Gamma^{**}(\boldsymbol{\mathscr{X}},r)>0

. W.l.o.g. assume
\Gamma^{**}

\Gamma^{*}
in
Theorem

6.1
.
Then
\text{D}\Phi:\text{T}_{\mathbf{Q}}\operatorname{St}(n,r)\to\text{T}_{\phi(\mathbf{Q})}\operatorname{St}(n,r)

To conclude, differentiate the update diagram

The proof of
