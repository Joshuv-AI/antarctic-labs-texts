Efficient Learning of Optimal Individualized Treatment Rules for Heteroscedastic or Misspecified Treatment-Free Effect Models
Weibin Mo, Yufeng Liu

Abstract
Recent development in data-driven decision science has seen great advances in individualized decision making. Given data with individual covariates, treatment assignments and outcomes, researchers can search for the optimal individualized treatment rule (ITR) that maximizes the expected outcome. Existing methods typically require initial estimation of some nuisance models. The double robustness property that can protect from misspecification of either the treatment-free effect or the propensity score has been widely advocated. However, when model misspecification exists, a doubly robust estimate can be consistent but may suffer from downgraded efficiency. Other than potential misspecified nuisance models, most existing methods do not account for the potential problem when the variance of outcome is heterogeneous among covariates and treatment. We observe that such heteroscedasticity can greatly affect the estimation efficiency of the optimal ITR. In this paper, we demonstrate that the consequences of misspecified treatment-free effect and heteroscedasticity can be unified as a covariate-treatment dependent variance of residuals. To improve efficiency of the estimated ITR, we propose an Efficient Learning (E-Learning) framework for finding an optimal ITR in the multi-armed treatment setting. We show that the proposed E-Learning is optimal among a regular class of semiparametric estimates that can allow treatment-free effect misspecification. In our simulation study, E-Learning demonstrates its effectiveness if one of or both misspecified treatment-free effect and heteroscedasticity exist. Our analysis of a Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus (T2DM) observational study also suggests the improved efficiency of E-Learning. Keywords and Phrases: Double robustness; Heteroscedasticity; Individualized treatment rules; Model misspecification; Multi-armed treatments; Semiparametric efficiency

Introduction

Individualized decision making is very essential in various scientific fields. One of the common goals is to find the optimal
individualized treatment rule (ITR)
mapping from the individual characteristics or contextual information to the treatment assignment, that maximizes the expected outcome, known as the
value function

(
manski2004statistical
;
qian2011performance
)
. Such a goal can be seen from applications in many different areas. In disease management, the physician needs to decide whether to introduce or switch a therapy based on patients’ characteristics in order to optimize his/her clinical outcome
(
bertsimas2017personalized
)
. In public management, decision makers may seek for a policy that allocates resources based on individual profiles and maximizes the overall efficiency
(
kube2019allocating
)
. In a context-based recommender system, contextual information such as time, location and social connection can be incorporated to increase effectiveness of the recommendation
(
aggarwal2016recommender
)
.

There is a vast literature on estimating an optimal ITR. Among various existing methods, there are two main strategies. The first strategy is to estimate the outcome mean model given covariates and treatment, which is often referred as the
model-based
approach. The optimal ITR can be induced by maximizing the mean outcome over treatment conditional on covariates. Existing methods including Q-Learning
(
watkins1989learning
;
qian2011performance
)
, A-Learning
(
murphy2003optimal
;
lu2013variable
;
shi2018high
)
,
dynamic Weighted Ordinary Least Square (dWOLS)

(
wallace2015doubly
)
and
Robust D-Learning (RD-Learning)

(
meng2020robust
)
all fall into this category. Some related approaches focus on a transformed outcome model, such as the Subgroup Identification approach
(
tian2014simple
;
chen2017general
)
and D-Learning
(
qi2018d
;
qi2020multi
)
. The second strategy, known as the
direct-search
approach, is to estimate the value function nonparametrically, and maximize the value function estimate over a prespecified ITR class to obtain an optimal ITR. A well-known example using this strategy is the
inverse-probability weighted estimate (IPWE)

(
zhao2012estimating
;
zhou2017residual
;
liu2018augmented
;
kitagawa2018should
)
. For these two strategies, the model-based approach relies on a correctly specified outcome mean model, while the direct-search approach based on the IPWE requires correctly estimating the propensity score function. In order to improve these two strategies, various papers proposed to combine the strength of both. In particular,
zhang2012robust
;
zhao2019efficient
;
athey2021policy
considered to combine the outcome model with the IPWE to obtain the
augmented IPWE (AIPWE)
of the value function. Such an estimate can be more robust to the model specification for the outcome model or the propensity score model.

Among the aforementioned approaches, the
double robustness
property has been studied and advocated to protect from potential model misspecifications. In the model-based approaches, the optimal ITR only depends on the interaction effect between covariates and treatment within the outcome mean model. Then the treatment-free effect that only depends on covariates can be a nuisance component.
robins2004optimal
investigated the incorrectly specified parametric model for the treatment-free effect, and introduced the G-estimating equation that can incorporate additional information from the propensity score. The G-estimator can be doubly robust in the sense that the estimate remains consistent even if one of the treatment-free effect model and the propensity score model is misspecified. As special cases,
lu2013variable
;
ertefaie2021robust
developed least-squares approaches that can equivalently solve the G-estimating equation and enjoy double robustness.
wallace2015doubly
;
meng2020robust
took a different approach to hedge the risk of treatment-free effect misspecification. Specifically, they proposed the weighted least-squares problem that utilizes the propensity score information to construct balancing weights, and the resulting estimates can also be doubly robust. In the direct-search approaches, the AIPWE of the value function is doubly robust in a slightly different way. Specifically, the AIPWE incorporates the outcome mean function and the propensity score function. When estimating the outcome mean and propensity score functions, even if one of their model specifications is incorrect, the corresponding AIPWE can still remain consistent.

The double robustness property has also been widely studied in the causal inference literature
(
robins1994estimation
;
robins1995analysis
;
ding2018causal
)
. One problem of particular interest is to study the case when one of or both model misspecifications happen.
kang2007demystifying
provided a comprehensive empirical study on how model misspecification can affect the resulting estimates. They concluded that the misspecified outcome mean model can be generally more harmful than the misspecified propensity score model. When both models are misspecified, the doubly robust estimate can perform even worse than the IPWE. Later studies further developed improved estimates and inference procedures to overcome such challenges
(
tan2010bounded
;
rotnitzky2012improved
;
vermeulen2015bias
;
benkeser2017doubly
)
. These studies have also motivated some improvement of the AIPWE for the ITR problem. Specifically, when the outcome mean model is incorrectly specified,
cao2009improving
proposed an optimal estimation strategy for the misspecified outcome mean model in the sense that the resulting AIPWE can have the smallest variance.
pan2021improved
further extended this work to the ITR problem, and utilized augmented inverse-probability weighted estimating equations for the outcome mean model estimation.

Motivated from
kang2007demystifying
that the misspecified treatment-free effect can have more severe consequence, we focus on addressing this challenge. In our study, we find that the misspecified treatment-free effect in the model-based approach can have a consequence similar to heteroscedasticity
(
carroll1982adapting
)
. More specifically, both misspecified treatment-free effect and heteroscedasticity can cause the variance of residuals being dependent on covariates and treatment. Therefore, we take the approach of semiparametric efficient estimation under heteroscedasticity
(
ma2006efficient
)
and propose an
Efficient Learning (E-Learning)
framework for the optimal ITR in the multi-armed treatment setting. Our proposed E-Learning can enjoy the following properties:

1.

When nuisance models are correctly specified, E-Learning performs semiparametric efficient estimation. Our framework can allow the variance of outcome depends on covariates and treatment, and hence is more general than existing semiparametric efficient procedures such as G-Estimation and its equivalents;

2.

E-Learning is doubly robust with respect to the treatment-free effect model and the propensity score model;

3.

In presence of misspecified treatment-free effect, E-Learning is optimal with the minimal
\sqrt{n}
-asymptotic variance among a regular class of semiparametric estimates based on the given working treatment-free effect function. Our optimality incorporates the standard semiparametric efficiency
(
tsiatis2007semiparametric
)
as a special case for the ITR problem.

This paper contributes to existing literature in terms of the followings:

1.

Parallel to the improved doubly robust procedure in
pan2021improved
for direct-search approaches, E-Learning is an improved doubly robust method for model-based approaches. Specifically, E-Learning performs optimal efficiency improvement when one of or both misspecified treatment-free effect and heteroscedasticity exist;

2.

E-Learning incorporates many existing approaches as special cases, including Q-Learning, G-Estimation, A-Learning, dWOLS, Subgroup Identification, D-Learning and RD-Learning. It provides a more general framework to study the double robustness and estimation efficiency for these methods;

3.

We develop E-Learning for the setting with multiple treatments. In particular, E-Learning utilizes a generalized equiangular coding of multiple treatment arms to develop the efficient estimating function. This can be the first work to incorporate equiangularity in the semiparametric framework among those utilizing the equiangular coding
(
zhang2014multicategory
;
zhang2020multicategory
;
qi2020multi
;
meng2020near
;
xue2021multicategory
)
;

4.

In our simulation study, our proposed E-Learning demonstrates superior performance over existing methods when one of or both misspecified treatment-free effect and heteroscedasticity exist, which confirms the superior performance of the proposed E-Learning. In the analysis of a
Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus (T2DM)
observational study, E-Learning also demonstrates its improved efficiency compared to other methods.

R
code for the implementation of this paper is available at
https://github.com/harrymok/E-Learning.git
.

2.2
A Motivating Example

We introduce a motivating example to demonstrate that several existing approaches, including Q-Learning, G-Estimation, A-Learning, dWOLS, Subgroup Identification, D-Learning and RD-Learning, may not be optimal if either the treatment-free effect
\mu_{0}(\bm{X})
is misspecified, or the variance function
\mathbb{E}(\epsilon^{2}|\bm{X},A)
depends on
(\bm{X},A)
. In contrast, the E-Learning estimate can be much more efficient. All these methods are compared in Section
3
.

Consider the covariate
X
with a symmetric distribution on
\mathbb{R}
, the treatment
A\sim\mathsf{Bernoulli}(1/2)
, and the error term
\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
, where
X,A,\epsilon
are mutually independent. Suppose the outcome
Y
is generated by

Y=\underbrace{c_{1}|X|}_{\text{treatment-free effect}}+\underbrace{(A-1/2)\beta_{0}}_{\text{interaction effect}}+\sqrt{\underbrace{1+2c_{2}^{2}AX^{2}}_{\text{variance function}}}\epsilon,

for some
\beta_{0}\geq 0
. When estimating from the training data, suppose that we specify
X\eta
for the treatment-free effect with
\eta
to be estimated, and
(A-1/2)\beta
for the interaction effect with
\beta
to be estimated. If
c_{1}=0
, then the treatment-free effect is correctly specified, with the true parameter
\eta=0
; otherwise, the treatment-free effect is misspecified. If
c_{2}=0
, then the variance function is
1
, and homogeneous with respect to
(X,A)
; otherwise, we have a heteroscedastic model with the variance of error depending on
(X,A)
.

Denote
\mathbb{E}_{n}
as the empirical average over the training dataset of size
n
. Then for this particular example, Q-Learning
(
watkins1989learning
)
, G-Estimation
(
robins2004optimal
)
, A-Learning
(
murphy2003optimal
)
, dWOLS
(
wallace2015doubly
)
, Subgroup Identification
(
tian2014simple
)
, D-Learning
(
qi2018d
)
and RD-Learning
(
meng2020robust
)
are equivalent to the following
Ordinary Least-Squares (OLS)
problem:

\displaystyle(\widehat{\eta}_{n},\widehat{\beta}_{n})\in\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{\eta,\beta\in\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{E}_{n}[Y-X\eta-(A-1/2)\beta]^{2}.

(1)

Note that if
c_{1}=c_{2}=0
with correctly specified treatment-free effect and homoscedasticity, then
\widehat{\beta}_{n}
is semiparametric efficient. For the general
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
, the OLS estimates
\widehat{\beta}_{n}
and
\widehat{\eta}_{n}
are asymptotically independent, with
\sqrt{n}\widehat{\eta}_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{D}}}{{\to}}\mathcal{N}(0,\nu^{2})
for some
\nu^{2}>0
and

\sqrt{n}(\widehat{\beta}_{n}-\beta_{0})=\sqrt{n}[\mathbb{E}(A-1/2)^{2}+\mathchoice{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{O}}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{O}}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{O}}}{\scalebox{.5}{$\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{O}$}}_{\mathbb{P}}(1)]^{-1}\mathbb{E}_{n}\left[(A-1/2)\left(c_{1}|X|+\sqrt{1+2c_{2}^{2}AX^{2}}\epsilon\right)\right]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{D}}}{{\to}}\mathcal{N}(0,v^{2}),

where the
\sqrt{n}
-asymptotic variance of
\widehat{\beta}_{n}
is given by
v^{2}=4\mathbb{E}[1+(c_{1}^{2}+c_{2}^{2})X^{2}]=4\mathbb{E}(1+c^{2}X^{2})
with
c^{2}:=c_{1}^{2}+c_{2}^{2}
.
Notice that the residual is
\widehat{e}=Y-X\widehat{\eta}_{n}-(2A-1)\widehat{\beta}_{n}=c_{1}|X|+\sqrt{1+2c_{2}^{2}AX^{2}}\epsilon+\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}}(n^{-1/2})
. Then we have
\mathbb{E}(\widehat{e}^{2}|X)=1+c^{2}X^{2}+\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}}(n^{-1})
, which clearly depends on
X
.

Motivated from the heteroscedastic residual, we define
\widecheck{v}_{\epsilon}(x):=4(1+c^{2}x^{2})
. Consider the solutions to the generalized least-squares problem

\displaystyle(\widehat{\eta}_{\mathrm{eff},n},\widehat{\beta}_{\mathrm{eff},n})\in\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{\eta,\beta\in\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{E}_{n}\Big\{\widecheck{v}_{\epsilon}^{-1}(X)[Y-X\eta-(A-1/2)\beta]^{2}\Big\}.

(2)

Then
\widehat{\beta}_{\mathrm{eff},n}
and
\widehat{\eta}_{\mathrm{eff},n}
are asymptotically independent, with
\sqrt{n}\widehat{\eta}_{\mathrm{eff},n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{D}}}{{\to}}\mathcal{N}(0,\widetilde{\nu}^{2})
for some
\widetilde{\nu}^{2}>0
,

\sqrt{n}(\widehat{\beta}_{\mathrm{eff},n}-\beta_{0})=\sqrt{n}\left\{\mathbb{E}\left[{(A-1/2)^{2}\over 4(1+c^{2}X^{2})}\right]+\mathchoice{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{O}}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{O}}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{O}}}{\scalebox{.5}{$\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{O}$}}_{\mathbb{P}}(1)\right\}^{-1}\mathbb{E}_{n}\left[{(A-1/2)\left(c_{1}|X|+\sqrt{1+2c_{2}^{2}AX^{2}}\epsilon\right)\over 4(1+c^{2}X^{2})}\right]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{D}}}{{\to}}\mathcal{N}(0,v_{\mathrm{eff}}^{2}),

where the
\sqrt{n}
-asymptotic variance of
\widehat{\beta}_{\mathrm{eff},n}
is given by
v_{\mathrm{eff}}^{2}=4\left[\mathbb{E}(1+c^{2}X^{2})^{-1}\right]^{-1}
.
The asymptotic relative efficiency of
\widehat{\beta}_{\mathrm{eff},n}
with respect to
\widehat{\beta}_{n}
is
{v^{2}/v_{\mathrm{eff}}^{2}}=\mathbb{E}(1+c^{2}X^{2})\mathbb{E}\left({1\over 1+c^{2}X^{2}}\right)\geq 1
.
That is,
\widehat{\beta}_{\mathrm{eff},n}
has a smaller
\sqrt{n}
-asymptotic variance than
\widehat{\beta}_{n}
. The strict inequality generally holds if
c\neq 0
and
X
is non-degenerate.

Next we consider an extreme case to illustrate that
\widehat{\beta}_{\mathrm{eff},n}
can be much more efficient than
\widehat{\beta}_{n}
. Suppose
X\sim qf^{(M)}(x)+(1-q)f^{(\infty)}(x)
, where
f^{(M)}(x)
is a symmetric
probability density function (PDF)
with compact support on
[-M,M]
,
f^{(\infty)}(x)
is a symmetric PDF on
\mathbb{R}
with
\int_{\mathbb{R}}x^{2}f^{(\infty)}(x)\mathrm{d}x=+\infty
, and

is the mixture probability. Then for

,

, while

implies that
\widehat{\beta}_{n}
cannot even be
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}}(n^{-1/2})
, while in contrast,
\widehat{\beta}_{\mathrm{eff},n}
has a bounded
\sqrt{n}
-asymptotic variance

. Therefore, if either the treatment-free effect is misspecified
(c_{1}\neq 0)
, or the variance function is not homogeneous
(c_{2}\neq 0)
, then
\widehat{\beta}_{n}
can have much worse perforamance than the more efficient estimate
\widehat{\beta}_{\mathrm{eff},n}
.

From the motivating example above, we can conclude that the efficiency of many existing approaches can be improved when either misspecified treatment-free effect or heteroscedasticity happens. In fact, our example shows that misspecified treatment-free effect or heteroscedasticity can cause the dependency of
\mathbb{E}(\widehat{e}^{2}|X)=1+c^{2}X^{2}
on
X
. Motivated from efficient estimation under heteroscedasticity
(
ma2006efficient
)
and our motivating example, we introduce the working variance function
v_{\mathrm{opt}}(X)=1+c^{2}X^{2}
, and consider the generalized least-squares estimate as in (
2
). The estimation efficiency can be greatly improved in this case.

xiao2019robust
pointed out a phenomenon similar to our finding in Section
2.2
, while their methodology and theoretical properties differ from ours. To be specific,
xiao2019robust
replaced the squared loss by general robust loss functions. Under the assumption
\epsilon\mathchoice{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\displaystyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\displaystyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\textstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\textstyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\scriptstyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptscriptstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}}}A|\bm{X}
, their estimate based on the quantile loss function can be shown consistent and
\sqrt{n}
-asymptotic normal. However, it remains unclear whether the
\sqrt{n}
-asymptotic normality still holds, and if so, how large the corresponding
\sqrt{n}
-asymptotic variance is, when treatment-free effect misspecification and heteroscedasticity exist. In contrast, we show in Theorem
that, under a more general setting, our proposed estimation strategy using the working variance function
\widecheck{v}_{\epsilon}(\bm{x})
is optimal, with the smallest
\sqrt{n}
-asymptotic variance, for heteroscedastic and misspecified treatment-free effect models. This implies that E-Learning is more general with better optimality guarantee than
xiao2019robust
.

The methodology introduced in this section is special in the sense that the treatment assignment is binary,
i.e.

A\in\{0,1\}
. For multiple treatment options
A\in\{1,2,\cdots,K\}
with
K>2
, the estimation problem is no longer an inverse-variance weighted least-squares problem. We will motivate our general methodology from the semiparametric efficient estimate of Model (
2.1
).

2.3
Semiparametric Efficient Estimate

In this section, we derive the semiparametric efficient estimate of

2.3.1
Efficient Score

In order to obtain the corresponding estimating equation, we first show the procedures to calculate the semiparametric efficient score following

We first derive the nuisance tangent space with respect to
\eta
following

Lemma 1

(Nuisance Tangent Space)
.

Consider Model (

). Define
\mathcal{H}:=\{\bm{h}(\bm{X},A,\epsilon)\mid\bm{h}:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{p},~\mathbb{E}\bm{h}(\bm{X},A,\epsilon)=\mathbf{0},~\mathbb{E}\|\bm{h}(\bm{X},A,\epsilon)\|_{2}^{2}<+\infty\}
, which is equipped with the norm
\|\cdot\|:=(\mathbb{E}\|\cdot\|_{2}^{2})^{1/2}
. Then the nuisance tangent space is

\Lambda=\Big\{\bm{H}\in\mathcal{H}:\mathbb{E}(\bm{H}\epsilon|\bm{X},A)=\mathbb{E}(\bm{H}\epsilon|\bm{X})\Big\}.

The proof of Lemma
1
is included in the Supplementary Material.

Next we discuss how to obtain the efficient score of Model (
2.1
). The efficient score is defined as the projection of the score vector onto the orthogonal complement
\Lambda^{\perp}
of the nuisance tangent space.
Notice that the moment restriction in Lemma
1
is equivalent to

\mathbb{E}(\bm{H}\epsilon|\bm{X},A=1)=\mathbb{E}(\bm{H}\epsilon|\bm{X},A=2)=\cdots=\mathbb{E}(\bm{H}\epsilon|\bm{X},A=K).

Then we can introduce a set of coding vectors
\{\bm{\omega}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{K-1}
, such that
\sum_{k=1}^{K}c_{k}\bm{\omega}_{k}=\mathbf{0}
if and only if
c_{1}=c_{2}=\cdots=c_{K}
. Equivalently, we can let
\Omega:=\sqrt{1-1/K}[\bm{\omega}_{1},\bm{\omega}_{2},\cdots,\bm{\omega}_{K}]^{\intercal}\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times(K-1)}
, and require that
(1/\sqrt{K})\mathbf{1}_{K\times 1}
is the only left singular vector corresponding to the singular value
0
of
\Omega
. In the following Lemma
2
, we show that any coding vectors satisfying such a requirement are equiangular up to normalization.

Lemma 2

(Equiangularity)
.

Let
\Omega:=\sqrt{1-1/K}[\bm{\omega}_{1},\bm{\omega}_{2},\cdots,\bm{\omega}_{K}]^{\intercal}\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times(K-1)}
such that
(1/\sqrt{K})\mathbf{1}_{K\times 1}
is the only left singular vector corresponding to the singular value
0
. Then
\{(\Omega^{\intercal}\Omega)^{-1/2}\bm{\omega}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
are equiangular.

The equiangular coding representation in
zhang2014multicategory
;
zhang2020multicategory
;
qi2020multi
is an example that satisfies Lemma
2
. The equiangular coding vectors
\{\bm{\omega}_{k}\}_{K=1}^{K}
can be useful to define the following
\mathbb{R}^{K-1}
-valued decision function associated with the interaction effect.

Lemma 3

(Angle-Based Decision Function)
.

Consider Model (
2.1
). For the coding vectors
\{\bm{\omega}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{K-1}
as in Lemma
2
, define an
\mathbb{R}^{K-1}
-valued decision function
\vec{\bm{f}}(\bm{x};\bm{\beta}):=(\Omega^{\intercal}\Omega)^{-1}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\gamma(\bm{x},k;\bm{\beta})\bm{\omega}_{k}
. Then we have

\gamma(\bm{x},k;\bm{\beta})=\left(1-{1\over K}\right)\langle\bm{\omega}_{k},\vec{\bm{f}}(\bm{x};\bm{\beta})\rangle;\quad 1\leq k\leq K.

Moreover, the optimal ITR is given by

\displaystyle d^{\star}(\bm{x})\in\mathop{\mathrm{argmax}}_{1\leq k\leq K}\langle\bm{\omega}_{k},\vec{\bm{f}}(\bm{x};\bm{\beta})\rangle.

(3)

Without loss of generality, assume that
\|\bm{\omega}_{k}\|_{2}=1
for
1\leq k\leq K
. For ease of notation, we denote
\vec{\bm{f}}=\vec{\bm{f}}(\bm{x};\bm{\beta})
. Then the angle between
\bm{\omega}_{k}
and
\vec{\bm{f}}
satisfies
\cos\angle(\bm{\omega}_{k},\vec{\bm{f}})=\langle\bm{\omega}_{k},\vec{\bm{f}}\rangle/\|\vec{\bm{f}}\|_{2}
. The decision rule (
3
) is equivalent to
\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{1\leq k\leq K}\angle(\bm{\omega}_{k},\vec{\bm{f}})
. That is, among
K
coding vectors
\{\bm{\omega}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
, the decision function
\vec{\bm{f}}
seeks for the arm that the corresponding coding vector has the least angle with respect to
\vec{\bm{f}}
.

Based on the coding vectors, the tangent space in Lemma
1
can be rewritten as

\Lambda=\left\{\bm{H}\in\mathcal{H}:\mathsf{O}_{p\times(K-1)}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbb{E}(\bm{H}\epsilon|\bm{X},A=k)\bm{\omega}_{k}^{\intercal}=\mathbb{E}\left({\bm{H}\bm{\omega}_{A}^{\intercal}\epsilon\over p_{\mathscr{A}}(A|\bm{X})}\middle|\bm{X}\right)\right\}.

Then we can obtain
\Lambda^{\perp}
and the projection operator onto it as in the following Lemma
4
. For a vector
\bm{a}
, we denote
\bm{a}^{\otimes 2}:=\bm{a}\bm{a}^{\intercal}
.

Lemma 4

(Projection onto
\Lambda^{\perp}
)
.

Let
\Lambda
be the tangent space in Lemma
1
,
\{\bm{\omega}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{K-1}
be the coding vectors satisfying
\sum_{k=1}^{K}c_{k}\bm{\omega}_{k}=\mathbf{0}
if and only if
c_{1}=c_{2}=\cdots=c_{K}
. Then

\Lambda^{\perp}=\left\{{\mathsf{H}(\bm{X})\bm{\omega}_{A}\epsilon\over p_{\mathscr{A}}(A|\bm{X})}\middle|\mathsf{H}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}^{p\times(K-1)}\right\}.

Furthermore, the projection operator onto
\Lambda^{\perp}
is

\mathbb{E}(\bm{H}|\Lambda^{\perp})=\mathbb{E}\left\{{\bm{H}\bm{\omega}_{A}^{\intercal}\epsilon\over p_{\mathscr{A}}(A|\bm{X})}\middle|\bm{X}\right\}\mathsf{V}_{\epsilon}(\bm{X})^{-1}{\bm{\omega}_{A}\epsilon\over p_{\mathscr{A}}(A|\bm{X})},

where
\mathsf{V}_{\epsilon}(\bm{X}):=\sum_{k=1}^{K}{\sigma^{2}(\bm{X},k)\bm{\omega}_{k}^{\otimes 2}\over p_{\mathscr{A}}(k|\bm{X})}\in\mathbb{R}^{(K-1)\times(K-1)}
. Here, if
\mathsf{V}_{\epsilon}(\bm{X})
is degenerate, then
\mathsf{V}_{\epsilon}(\bm{X})^{-1}
represents its measurable generalized inverse.

The efficient score of the semiparametric model (
2.1
) is defined as the projection of the score vector, the gradient of the log-likelihood with respect to
\bm{\beta}
, onto
\Lambda^{\perp}

(
tsiatis2007semiparametric
)
. Proposition
2
provides the explicit form of the efficient score.

Proposition 2

(Efficient Score)
.

Consider Model (
2.1
), the coding vectors
\{\bm{\omega}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{K-1}
as in Lemma
2
, and the angle-based representation in Lemma
3
. The semiparametric efficient score is

\bm{S}_{\mathrm{eff}}(\bm{\beta})=\dot{\mathsf{F}}(\bm{X};\bm{\beta})^{\intercal}\Omega^{\intercal}\Omega\mathsf{V}_{\epsilon}(\bm{X})^{-1}\times{\bm{\omega}_{A}\over p_{\mathscr{A}}(A|\bm{X})}\times\epsilon,

where
\dot{\mathsf{F}}(\bm{X};\bm{\beta}):=(\partial/\partial\bm{\beta}^{\intercal})\vec{\bm{f}}(\bm{X};\bm{\beta})\in\mathbb{R}^{(K-1)\times p}
, and
\mathsf{V}_{\epsilon}(\bm{X})^{-1}
is the same as in Lemma
4
.

As a consequence of Proposition
2
, we can finally define the efficient estimating function:

\displaystyle\bm{\phi}_{\mathrm{eff}}(\bm{\beta};\widecheck{\mu}_{0},\widecheck{p}_{\mathscr{A}},\widecheck{\sigma}^{2})

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\underbrace{\left[Y-\widecheck{\mu}_{0}(\bm{X})-\left(1-{1\over K}\right)\langle\bm{\omega}_{A},\vec{\bm{f}}(\bm{X};\bm{\beta})\rangle\right]}_{\text{residual}}\times\underbrace{\dot{\mathsf{F}}(\bm{X};\bm{\beta})^{\intercal}\Omega^{\intercal}\Omega\left[\sum_{k=1}^{K}{\widecheck{\sigma}^{2}(\bm{X},k)\bm{\omega}_{k}^{\otimes 2}\over\widecheck{p}_{\mathscr{A}}(k|\bm{X})}\right]^{-1}{\bm{\omega}_{A}\over\widecheck{p}_{\mathscr{A}}(A|\bm{X})}}_{\text{efficient instrument}},

(4)

which depends on the nuisance functions
\widecheck{\mu}_{0}(\bm{X})
,
\widecheck{p}_{\mathscr{A}}(A|\bm{X})
and
\widecheck{\sigma}^{2}(\bm{X},A)
. In particular,
\bm{S}_{\mathrm{eff}}(\bm{\beta})=\bm{\phi}_{\mathrm{eff}}(\bm{\beta};\mu_{0},p_{\mathscr{A}},\sigma^{2})
. That is, if the parameters
\bm{\beta}
of interest and all nuisance functions
(\mu_{0},p_{\mathscr{A}},\sigma^{2})
match with the truth in Model (
2.1
), then the estimating function becomes the efficient score.

2.4
E-Learning

In Section
2.3
, we have obtained the efficient estimating function
\bm{\phi}_{\mathrm{eff}}(\bm{\beta};\widecheck{\mu}_{0},\widecheck{p}_{\mathscr{A}},\widecheck{\sigma}^{2})
from (
4
).
An E-Learning estimate of
\bm{\beta}
solves

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{n}[\bm{\phi}_{\mathrm{eff}}(\bm{\beta};\widehat{\mu}_{0,n},\widehat{p}_{\mathscr{A},n},\widehat{\sigma}_{n}^{2})]=\mathbf{0},

(5)

where
(\widehat{\mu}_{0,n},\widehat{p}_{\mathscr{A},n})
are the finite-sample estimates of treatment-free effect and treatment assignment probability in Model (
2.1
). Furthermore,
\widehat{\sigma}_{n}^{2}(\bm{X},A)
is an estimate of the optimal variance function

\displaystyle\sigma_{\mathrm{opt}}^{2}(\bm{X},A;\widehat{\mu}_{0,n}):=[\widehat{\mu}_{0,n}(\bm{X})-\mu_{0}(\bm{X})]^{2}+\sigma^{2}(\bm{X},A).

(6)

The optimality of
\sigma_{\mathrm{opt}}^{2}(\bm{X},A;\widehat{\mu}_{0,n})
is justified in Theorem
in Section
. However, (
6
) can depend on the true treatment-free effect function
\mu_{0}(\bm{X})
and variance function
\sigma^{2}(\bm{X},A)
, which are unknown. Motivated from the example in Section
2.2
, we can consider the working residual
\widehat{e}:=Y-\widehat{\mu}_{0,n}(\bm{X})-\gamma(\bm{X},A;\bm{\beta})
, such that
\mathbb{E}(\widehat{e}^{2}|\bm{X},A,\widehat{\mu}_{0,n})=[\widehat{\mu}_{0,n}(\bm{X})-\mu_{0}(\bm{X})]^{2}+\sigma^{2}(\bm{X},A)=\sigma_{\mathrm{opt}}^{2}(\bm{X},A;\widehat{\mu}_{0,n})
. Therefore,
\widehat{\sigma}_{n}^{2}(\bm{X},A)
can be obtained by regressing
\widehat{e}^{2}
on
(\bm{X},A)
.

Similar to the general methodology in
davidian1987variance
, the E-Learning estimate of
\bm{\beta}
can be solved by the following three steps:

Step 1.

Obtain a consistent estimate
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{n}^{(0)}
of
\bm{\beta}
. This can be done by solving
(
5
)
with
\widehat{\sigma}_{n}^{(0)2}=1
that results in a consistent estimate of
\bm{\beta}
. The consistency is guaranteed by Proposition
3
;

Step 2.

Obtain
\widehat{\sigma}_{n}^{2}(\bm{X},A)
. Specifically, we first compute the working residual
\widehat{e}=Y-\widehat{\mu}_{0,n}(\bm{X})-\gamma(\bm{X},A;\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{n}^{(0)})
, and then
perform a nonparametric regression using
\widehat{e}^{2}
as the response and
(\bm{X},A)
as the covariates to estimate the optimal working variance function;

Step 3.

Solve (
5
) again using
\widehat{\sigma}_{n}^{2}(\bm{X},A)
from
Step 2
to obtain the E-Learning estimate
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{n}
.

More implementation details are discussed in Section
2.5
.

Note that the estimation procedure in this section relies on the parametric model for the interaction effect
\gamma(\bm{X},A;\bm{\beta})
. A typical parametric assumption is
\gamma(\bm{X},A;\mathsf{B}):=(1-1/K)\langle\bm{\omega}_{A},\mathsf{B}^{\intercal}\bm{X}\rangle
, where the angle-based decision function in Lemma
3
is modeled linearly as
\vec{\bm{f}}(\bm{X};\mathsf{B})=\mathsf{B}^{\intercal}\bm{X}
with
\mathsf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times(K-1)}
. However, this should not restrict the applicability of E-Learning. When the true interaction effect is nonlinear, we can still consider the basis expansion of
\bm{X}
. For example, we can use
\bm{X}^{(3)}:=(X_{1},X_{1}^{2},X_{1}^{3};\cdots,X_{p},X_{p}^{2},X_{p}^{3})^{\intercal}
as the covariate vector instead. In the Supplementary Material Section
, we demonstrate the effectiveness of E-Learning with the linear and cubic polynomial basis. Although the true interaction effect may not be correctly specified by the linear or cubic polynomial, our results can still show the strong advantage of cubic E-Learning when compared to other methods based on the same corresponding function basis.

2.5
Implementation

For the implementation of E-Learning, we first need to estimate the treatment assignment probabilities
\{p_{\mathscr{A}}(k|\bm{X})\}_{k=1}^{K}
and the treatment-free effect
\mu_{0}(\bm{X})
. Then we follow the three-step procedures in Section
2.4
for E-Learning estimation.

2.5.1
Estimating the Propensity Score Function

Suppose the treatment assignment probability
p_{\mathscr{A}}
is unknown. The first approach of estimating
p_{\mathscr{A}}
is to consider the penalized multinomial logistic regression
(
friedman2010regularization
)
. Specifically, consider the multinomial logistic working model
\widecheck{p}_{\mathscr{A}}(k|\bm{X};\bm{\tau}_{1},\bm{\tau}_{2},\cdots,\bm{\tau}_{K}):={\exp(\bm{\tau}_{k}^{\intercal}\bm{X})\over\sum_{k^{\prime}=1}^{K}\exp(\bm{\tau}_{k^{\prime}}^{\intercal}\bm{X})}
. The propensity score parameters
\bm{\tau}_{1},\bm{\tau}_{2},\cdots,\bm{\tau}_{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
can be estimated by the following penalized log-likelihood maximization:

\max_{\bm{\tau}_{1},\cdots,\bm{\tau}_{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\Bigg\{\mathbb{E}_{n}\left[\sum_{k=1}^{K}\bm{\tau}_{k}^{\intercal}\bm{X}\mathbb{I}(A=k)-\log\left(\sum_{k^{\prime}=1}^{K}e^{\bm{\tau}_{k^{\prime}}^{\intercal}\bm{X}}\right)\right]-\lambda_{\mathscr{A}}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}\tau_{jk}^{2}\right)^{1/2}\Bigg\},

where the group-LASSO penalty
\sum_{j=1}^{p}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}\tau_{jk}^{2}\right)^{1/2}
takes
\{\tau_{jk}\}_{k=1}^{K}
for the
j
-th variable across all treatments as a group, and
\lambda_{\mathscr{A}}
is a tuning parameter and can be chosen using cross validation.

In observational studies, the propensity scores can be vulnerable to model misspecification. Another approach for estimating
p_{\mathscr{A}}
is to consider flexible nonparametric regression using the regression forest
(
athey2019generalized
)
. Specifically, for each
1\leq k\leq K
, we run a regression forest using
\mathbb{I}(A=k)
as the response and
\bm{X}
as the covariates. Then each fitted regression forest provides a prediction for
\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{I}(A=k)|\bm{X}]
. The final estimate of
p_{\mathscr{A}}(k|\bm{X})
is the prediction after normalization such that the summation over
k=1,\cdots,K
is one.

2.5.2
Estimating the Treatment-Free Effect Function

Similar to Section
2.5.1
, the treatment-free effect function
\mu_{0}
can be estimated from a parametric model or nonparametric regression. For parametric estimation, we consider the linear working model
\widecheck{\mu}_{0}(\bm{X};\bm{\eta})=\bm{\eta}^{\intercal}\bm{X}
. In this case, the outcome mean model in (
2.1
) is fully parametrized. For example, if
\gamma(\bm{X},A;\mathsf{B})=(1-1/K)\langle\bm{\omega}_{A},\mathsf{B}^{\intercal}\bm{X}\rangle
, then we can consider the following joint penalized inverse-probability weighted least-squares problem with the
\ell_{1}
-penalty:

\min_{\bm{\eta}\in\mathbb{R}^{p},\mathsf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times(K-1)}}\left\{\mathbb{E}_{n}\left[{1\over\widehat{p}_{\mathscr{A},n}(A|\bm{X})}\left(Y-\bm{\eta}^{\intercal}\bm{X}-\left(1-{1\over K}\right)\langle\bm{\omega}_{A},\mathsf{B}^{\intercal}\bm{X}\rangle\right)^{2}\right]+\lambda_{\mu_{0}}\left(\|\bm{\eta}\|_{1}+\|\mathsf{B}\|_{1}\right)\right\},

where
\widehat{p}_{\mathscr{A},n}
is the estimated treatment assignment probability,
\lambda_{\mu_{0}}
is a tuning parameter and can be chosen using cross validation. Here, if
\widehat{p}_{\mathscr{A},n}(A|\bm{X})
is the correct treatment assignment probability, then the above estimate for
\bm{\eta}
can be consistent even if the model for the interaction effect
\gamma(\bm{X},A;\bm{\beta})
is incorrect. If the model for the interaction effect
\gamma(\bm{X},A;\bm{\beta})
is correct, then the above estimate for
\bm{\eta}
can also be consistent for any arbitrary
\widehat{p}_{\mathscr{A},n}
besides the correct one.

For nonparametric regression, we first divide the data into
K
subsets according to the received treatments. For each
1\leq k\leq K
, we use
Y
as the response and
\bm{X}
as the covariates to fit a regression forest on the data subset
\{(\bm{X}_{i},Y_{i}):A_{i}=k\}
. Then each fitted regression forest corresponds to the prediction of
\mathbb{E}(Y|\bm{X},A=k)
. We average the predictions over
k=1,\cdots,K
to obtain the treatment-free effect estimate.

2.5.3
Estimating the Variance Function

Suppose
\widehat{e}
is the working residual in
Step 2
. In order to estimate the variance function, we specifically consider the regression forest using
\widehat{e}^{2}
as the response and
(\bm{X},A)
as the covariates. Then
\widehat{\sigma}_{n}^{2}(\bm{X},k)
is the regression forest prediction at
(\bm{X},k)
for
1\leq k\leq K
.

In the simulation study in Section
, we also study another two nonparametric regression methods, the
Multivariate Adaptive Regression Splines (MARS)

(
friedman1991multivariate
)
and the
COmponent Selection and Smoothing Operator (COSSO)

(
lin2006component
)
. Here, the COSSO estimate of the working variance function is based on the following
Smoothing Spline ANalysis Of VAriance (SS-ANOVA)
model:
\mathbb{E}(\widehat{e}^{2}|\bm{X},A)=\nu_{0}+\sum_{j=1}^{p}f_{j}(X_{j})+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\alpha_{k}+\sum_{j=1}^{p}\sum_{k=1}^{K}f_{jk}(X_{j})+u
, where
\nu_{0}
is the global main effect,
\{f_{j}(X_{j})\}_{j=1}^{p}
are the covariate main effects,
\{\alpha_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
are the treatment main effects,
\{f_{jk}(X_{j})\}_{1\leq j\leq p,1\leq k\leq K}
are the covariate-treatment interaction effects, and
u
is the remainder term that is not modeled.

2.5.4
Solving the Regularized E-Learning Estimating Equation

In this section, we consider further regularization
J(\bm{\beta})
on the parameters of interest. One example from
qi2020multi
is to consider the linear angle-based decision function
\vec{\bm{f}}(\bm{X};\mathsf{B})=\mathsf{B}^{\intercal}\bm{X}
in Lemma
3
, where the covariate vector
\bm{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
can be high-dimensional. They introduced the row-wise group-LASSO penalty on the matrix coefficient
\mathsf{B}=[\beta_{jk}]_{p\times(K-1)}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times(K-1)}
as
J(\mathsf{B}):=\|\mathsf{B}\|_{2,1}=\sum_{j=1}^{p}(\sum_{k=1}^{K-1}\beta_{jk}^{2})^{1/2}
, which encourages sparsity among input covariates. Another example can be the extension to nonlinear modeling of the decision function
\vec{\bm{f}}(\bm{X})
, where a functional penalty
J(\vec{\bm{f}})
is applied.

To incorporate regularization in E-Learning from (
5
), we solve the penalized estimating equations
(
johnson2008penalized
)
:

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\left\{{1\over 2}\left\|\mathbb{E}_{n}[\bm{\phi}_{\mathrm{eff}}(\bm{\beta};\widehat{\mu}_{0,n},\widehat{p}_{\mathscr{A},n},\widehat{\sigma}_{n}^{2})]\right\|^{2}_{\mathsf{W}}+\lambda J(\bm{\beta})\right\},

(7)

where
\|\bm{x}\|_{\mathsf{W}}^{2}:=\bm{x}^{\intercal}\mathsf{W}\bm{x}
with some weighting matrix
\mathsf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
. A typical choice of
\mathsf{W}
can be
\mathsf{I}_{p\times p}
or the inverse of the empirical information matrix
\left\{\mathbb{E}_{n}[(\partial/\partial\bm{\beta}^{\intercal})\bm{\phi}_{\mathrm{eff}}(\bm{\beta};\widehat{\mu}_{0,n},\widehat{p}_{\mathscr{A},n},\widehat{\sigma}_{n}^{2})]\right\}^{-1}
.
Problem (
7
) can be solved by the accelerated proximal gradient method
(
nesterov2013gradient
)
with the gradient
\bm{\beta}\mapsto\mathbb{E}_{n}[\bm{\phi}_{\mathrm{eff}}(\bm{\beta};\widehat{\mu}_{0,n},\widehat{p}_{\mathscr{A},n},\widehat{\sigma}_{n}^{2})]
. A comprehensive lists of the proximal operators on various penalties
J(\bm{\beta})
can be found in
mo2021supervised
.
For a fixed
\lambda
, the estimation procedure follows the three steps in Section
2.4
. The parameter
\lambda
can be further tuned by cross validation. The IPWE of the value function is used as the tuning criteria. Denote
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{n}(\lambda)
as the solution to (
7
). The corresponding ITR becomes
\widehat{d}_{n}(\bm{X};\lambda):=\mathop{\mathrm{argmax}}_{1\leq k\leq K}\gamma(\bm{X},k;\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{n}(\lambda))
. Let
\{(\bm{X}_{i},A_{i},Y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n_{\rm valid}}
be the validation dataset. Then the criteria for
\lambda
is
{1\over n_{\rm valid}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\rm valid}}{\mathbb{I}[\widehat{d}_{n}(\bm{X}_{i};\lambda)=A_{i}]\over\widehat{p}_{\mathscr{A},n}(A_{i}|\bm{X}_{i})}Y_{i}
, which is larger the better.

More implementation details for E-Learning are discussed in Sections
and
in the Supplementary Material.

3
Connections to Existing Literature

In this section, we discuss the connection of the E-Learning estimating function (
4
) to several methods in the existing literature. It can be shown that with more assumptions in addition to Model (
2.1
), several existing methods can be equivalent to (
4
). That is, E-Learning can incorporate these methods as special cases. The motivating example in Section
2.2
is such a special case. In Sections
3.1
and
3.2
, we discuss the equivalence and the specific additional assumptions. In Section
3.3
, we further provide the general comparisons for these methods and some other nonparametric methods in the literature.

3.1
Binary Treatment

We first consider the binary treatment case
K=2
and relate the efficient estimating function (
4
) to some existing methods. We follow the convention to denote
\mathcal{A}=\{0,1\}
. Then we have one-dimensional coding for two treatment arms as
\omega_{0}
,
\omega_{1}
, which satisfies
c_{0}\omega_{0}+c_{1}\omega_{1}=0
if and only if
c_{0}=c_{1}
. Then we have
\omega_{1}=-\omega_{0}
. Without loss of generality, we can assume that
\omega_{1}=1
and
\omega_{0}=-1
, which become the sign coding of treatments. Then
\Omega^{\intercal}\Omega=1
.

The variance matrix from Proposition
2
becomes a scalar:
v_{\epsilon}(\bm{X}):={\sigma^{2}(\bm{X},1)\over p_{\mathscr{A}}(1|\bm{X})}+{\sigma^{2}(\bm{X},0)\over p_{\mathscr{A}}(0|\bm{X})}
. The decision function
f(\bm{X};\bm{\beta})
is
\mathbb{R}
-valued, such that
\gamma(\bm{X},A;\bm{\beta})=(1/2)\omega_{A}f(\bm{X};\bm{\beta})
. Then the E-Learning efficient estimating function (
4
) becomes

\displaystyle\bm{\phi}_{\mathrm{eff}}(\bm{\beta};\widecheck{\mu}_{0},\widecheck{p}_{\mathscr{A}},\widecheck{\sigma}^{2})=[Y-\widecheck{\mu}_{0}(\bm{X})-(1/2)\omega_{A}f(\bm{X};\bm{\beta})]{\widecheck{v}_{\epsilon}^{-1}(\bm{X})\omega_{A}\over\widecheck{p}_{\mathscr{A}}(A|\bm{X})}\dot{\mathbf{f}}(\bm{X};\bm{\beta}),

(8)

where
\dot{\mathbf{f}}(\bm{X};\bm{\beta}):=(\partial/\partial\bm{\beta})f(\bm{X};\bm{\beta})\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
. Moreover, (
8
) is also equivalent to the following weighed least-squares problem:

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\mathbb{E}_{n}\left\{{\widecheck{v}_{\epsilon}^{-1}(\bm{X})\over\widecheck{p}_{\mathscr{A}}(A|\bm{X})}[Y-\widecheck{\mu}_{0}(\bm{X})-(1/2)\omega_{A}f(\bm{X};\bm{\beta})]^{2}\right\}.

(9)

There are some connections for this formulation to several methods in the existing literature.

Q-Learning

Consider the additional assumptions: (a) homoscedasticity
\sigma^{2}(\bm{X},1)=\sigma^{2}(\bm{X},0)=\sigma^{2}
; and (b) complete-at-random treatment assignment
p_{\mathscr{A}}(1|\bm{X})=p_{\mathscr{A}}(0|\bm{X})=1/2
. Then E-Learning (
9
) reduces to an OLS problem. If we also assume that: (c) the treatment-free effect satisfies
\mu_{0}(\bm{X})=\bm{X}^{\intercal}(\bm{\eta}+\bm{\beta}/2)
, where
(\bm{\beta},\bm{\eta})
are jointly estimated, then E-Learning (
9
) can be equivalent to the standard
Q-Learning

(
watkins1989learning
)
in this case:

\min_{\bm{\eta},\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\mathbb{E}_{n}(Y-\bm{X}^{\intercal}\bm{\eta}-A\bm{X}^{\intercal}\bm{\beta})^{2}.

G-Estimation, A-Learning and dWOLS

Consider the additional assumption: (a) homoscedasticity
\sigma^{2}(\bm{X},1)=\sigma^{2}(\bm{X},0)=\sigma^{2}
. Then
v_{\epsilon}^{-1}(\bm{X})=\sigma^{-2}p_{\mathscr{A}}(1|\bm{X})p_{\mathscr{A}}(0|\bm{X})
. Without loss of generality, we can further assume that
\sigma^{2}=1
. Denote
\pi_{\mathscr{A}}(\bm{X}):=p_{\mathscr{A}}(1|\bm{X})=\mathbb{E}(A|\bm{X})
. Then we have
{v_{\epsilon}^{-1}(\bm{X})\omega_{A}\over p_{\mathscr{A}}(A|\bm{X})}=A-\pi_{\mathscr{A}}(\bm{X})=|A-\pi_{\mathscr{A}}(\bm{X})|\omega_{A}
and
{v_{\epsilon}^{-1}(\bm{X})\over p_{\mathscr{A}}(A|\bm{X})}=|A-\pi_{\mathscr{A}}(\bm{X})|
.

robins2004optimal
proposed the
G-Estimation
strategy for dynamic treatment regimes, which is equivalent to the standard
A-Learning

(
murphy2003optimal
)
in the single-stage setting. In particular, G-Estimation solves the estimating equation

\mathbb{E}_{n}\Big\{\left[Y-\widehat{\mu}_{0,n}(\bm{X})-A\bm{X}^{\intercal}\bm{\beta}\right]\left[A-\widehat{\pi}_{\mathscr{A},n}(\bm{X})\right]\bm{X}\Big\}=\mathbf{0},

while A-Learning is equivalent to the estimating equation

\mathbb{E}_{n}\Big\{\left[Y-\widehat{m}_{0,n}(\bm{X})-\big(A-\widehat{\pi}_{\mathscr{A},n}(\bm{X})\big)\bm{X}^{\intercal}\bm{\beta}\right]\left[A-\widehat{\pi}_{\mathscr{A},n}(\bm{X})\right]\bm{X}\Big\}=\mathbf{0}.

Then G-Estimation and A-Learning are equivalent to E-Learning (
8
) in this case up to reparametrization, where
\widehat{\mu}_{0,n}(\bm{X})
is replaced by
\widehat{m}_{0,n}(\bm{X})-\widehat{\pi}_{\mathscr{A},n}(\bm{X})\bm{X}^{\intercal}\bm{\beta}
.

wallace2015doubly
proposed the dWOLS method. In the single-stage setting, they considered the following weighted least-squares problem:

\min_{\bm{\eta},\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\mathbb{E}_{n}\Big\{w(\bm{X},A)(Y-\bm{X}^{\intercal}\bm{\eta}-A\bm{X}^{\intercal}\bm{\beta})^{2}\Big\},

where
w(\bm{X},A)
satisfies the balancing condition
\pi_{\mathscr{A}}(\bm{X})w(\bm{X},1)=[1-\pi_{\mathscr{A}}(\bm{X})]w(\bm{X},0)
. Note that
w(\bm{X},A)=|A-\pi_{\mathscr{A}}(\bm{X})|
meets this balancing condition. Assume that: (b) the treatment assignment probability
\pi_{\mathscr{A}}(\bm{X})=p_{\mathscr{A}}(1|\bm{X})
is known; and (c) the treatment-free effect satisfies
\mu_{0}(\bm{X})=\bm{X}^{\intercal}(\bm{\eta}+\bm{\beta}/2)
, where
(\bm{\beta},\bm{\eta})
are jointly estimated. Then dWOLS with
w(\bm{X},A)=|A-\pi_{\mathscr{A}}(\bm{X})|
is equivalent to E-Learning (
9
):

\min_{\bm{\eta},\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\mathbb{E}_{n}\Big\{|A-\pi_{\mathscr{A}}(\bm{X})|(Y-\bm{X}^{\intercal}\bm{\eta}-A\bm{X}^{\intercal}\bm{\beta})^{2}\Big\}.

Subgroup Identification, D-Learning and RD-Learning

Consider the additional assumptions: (a) the variance function satisfies
v_{\epsilon}(\bm{X})={\sigma^{2}(\bm{X},1)\over p_{\mathscr{A}}(1|\bm{X})}+{\sigma^{2}(\bm{X},0)\over p_{\mathscr{A}}(0|\bm{X})}=v_{\epsilon}
, which is a constant; (b) the treatment assignment probability
p_{\mathscr{A}}(A|\bm{X})
is known; and (c) the treatment-free effect satisfies
\mu_{0}(\bm{X})=0
. Then E-Learning (
9
) is equivalent to the standard
Subgroup Identification

(
tian2014simple
;
chen2017general
)
and the binary
D-Learning

(
qi2018d
)
:

\min_{\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\mathbb{E}_{n}\left\{{1\over p_{\mathscr{A}}(A|\bm{X})}[Y-(1/2)\omega_{A}\bm{X}^{\intercal}\bm{\beta}]^{2}\right\}.

If both (b) and (c) are relaxed, then E-Learning (
9
) is equivalent to the augmented Subgroup Identification
(
chen2017general
, Web Appendix B)
and the binary
RD-Learning

(
meng2020robust
)
:

\min_{\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\mathbb{E}_{n}\left\{{1\over\widehat{p}_{\mathscr{A},n}(A|\bm{X})}[Y-\widehat{\mu}_{0,n}(\bm{X})-(1/2)\omega_{A}\bm{X}^{\intercal}\bm{\beta}]^{2}\right\}.

3.2
Multiple Treatments and Partially Linear Model

For general
K\geq 3
, we consider the linear decision function
\vec{\bm{f}}(\bm{X};\mathsf{B})=\mathsf{B}^{\intercal}\bm{X}
, where
\mathsf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times(K-1)}
is a parameter matrix. By Lemma
3
, Model (
2.1
) becomes

\displaystyle Y=\mu_{0}(\bm{X})+\left(1-{1\over K}\right)\langle\bm{\omega}_{A},\mathsf{B}^{\intercal}\bm{X}\rangle+\epsilon;\quad\mathbb{E}(\epsilon|\bm{X},A)=0;\quad\sigma^{2}(\bm{X},A)=\mathbb{E}(\epsilon^{2}|\bm{X},A)<+\infty,

(10)

which is a
Heteroscedasticitic Partially Linear Model (HPLM)

(
ma2006efficient
)
.

Denote
\mathsf{Vec}(\mathsf{B})\in\mathbb{R}^{p(K-1)}
as the vectorization of
\mathsf{B}
. The we further have
\vec{\bm{f}}(\bm{X};\mathsf{B})=(\mathbf{I}_{(K-1)\times(K-1)}\otimes\bm{X})^{\intercal}\mathsf{Vec}(\mathsf{B})
and
\dot{\mathsf{F}}(\bm{X};\mathsf{B})=[\partial/\partial\mathsf{Vec}(\mathsf{B})^{\intercal}]\vec{\bm{f}}(\bm{X};\bm{\beta})=(\mathbf{I}_{(K-1)\times(K-1)}\otimes\bm{X})^{\intercal}
, where
\otimes
denotes the Kronecker product.
The E-Learning efficient estimating function (
4
) becomes

\displaystyle\bm{\phi}_{\mathrm{eff}}(\mathsf{B};\widecheck{\mu}_{0},\widecheck{p}_{\mathscr{A}},\widecheck{\sigma}^{2})=\underbrace{[(\Omega^{\intercal}\Omega)\otimes\mathbf{I}_{d\times d}]}_{\text{constant matrix}}\times\left[Y-\widecheck{\mu}_{0}(\bm{X})-\left(1-{1\over K}\right)\langle\bm{\omega}_{A},\mathsf{B}^{\intercal}\bm{X}\rangle\right]\times{\widecheck{\mathsf{V}}_{\epsilon}(\bm{X})^{-1}\bm{\omega}_{A}\over\widecheck{p}_{\mathscr{A}}(A|\bm{X})}\otimes\bm{X},

(11)

where
\widecheck{\mathsf{V}}_{\epsilon}(\bm{X}):=\sum_{k=1}^{K}{\widecheck{\sigma}^{2}(\bm{X},k)\bm{\omega}_{k}^{\otimes 2}\over\widecheck{p}_{\mathscr{A}}(k|\bm{X})}
, and
\widecheck{\mathsf{V}}_{\epsilon}(\bm{X})^{-1}
denotes the generalized inverse if not invertible.

Consider the additional assumption: (a) the variance function satisfies
\mathsf{V}_{\epsilon}(\bm{X})=\sum_{k=1}^{K}{\sigma^{2}(\bm{X},k)\bm{\omega}_{k}^{\otimes 2}\over p_{\mathscr{A}}(k|\bm{X})}=\mathsf{V}_{\epsilon}
, which is a constant matrix. Then E-Learning (
11
) is equivalent to the multi-arm RD-Learning:

\min_{\mathsf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times(K-1)}}\mathbb{E}_{n}\left\{{1\over\widehat{p}_{\mathscr{A},n}(A|\bm{X})}\left[Y-\widehat{\mu}_{0,n}(\bm{X})-\left(1-{1\over K}\right)\langle\bm{\omega}_{A},\mathsf{B}^{\intercal}\bm{X}\rangle\right]^{2}\right\}.

Notice that the multi-arm D-Learning
(
qi2020multi
)
cannot be equivalent to E-Learning. In fact, D-Learning solves the following vectorized least-squares problem:

\displaystyle\min_{\mathsf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times(K-1)}}\mathbb{E}_{n}\left\{{1\over 2Kp_{\mathscr{A}}(A|\bm{X})}\left\|KY\bm{\omega}_{A}-\mathsf{B}^{\intercal}\bm{X}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right\}.

(12)

The estimating function of (
12
) is

\underbrace{\left[Y-\left(1-{1\over K}\right)\langle\bm{\omega}_{A},\mathsf{B}^{\intercal}\bm{X}\rangle\right]\times{\bm{\omega}_{A}\over p_{\mathscr{A}}(A|\bm{X})}\otimes\bm{X}}_{\text{efficient estimating function if (a) and $\mu_{0}(\bm{X})=0$}}+\underbrace{{1\over p_{\mathscr{A}}(A|\bm{X})}\left[\left(1-{1\over K}\right)\bm{\omega}_{A}^{\otimes 2}-{1\over K}\mathsf{I}_{(K-1)\times(K-1)}\right]\mathsf{Vec}(\bm{X}^{\otimes 2}\mathsf{B})}_{:=\bm{\phi}_{\rm D}(\bm{X},A)}.

Note that
\mathbb{E}[\bm{\phi}_{\rm D}(\bm{X},A)|\bm{X}]=\mathbf{0}
and
\mathbb{E}[\bm{\phi}_{\rm D}(\bm{X},A)^{\otimes 2}]
is strictly positive definite, which contributes an extra term to the
\sqrt{n}
-asymptotic variance of the D-Learning estimate. This suggests that when
K\geq 3
, the D-Learning estimate can generally have a larger asymptotic variance than E-Learning.

4.1
Asymptotic Properties

We first focus on estimation properties of the proposed E-Learning. In Proposition

4

Proposition 3

(Double Robustness)
.

Consider Model (
2.1
) and the estimating function (
4
). Suppose
\widecheck{\mu}_{0}(\bm{X})
,
\widecheck{p}_{\mathscr{A}}(A|\bm{X})
and
\widecheck{\sigma}^{2}(\bm{X},A)
are arbitrary nuisance functions. Then we have

\mathbb{E}[\bm{\phi}_{\mathrm{eff}}(\bm{\beta};\widecheck{\mu}_{0},p_{\mathscr{A}},\widecheck{\sigma}^{2})]=\mathbb{E}[\bm{\phi}_{\mathrm{eff}}(\bm{\beta};\mu_{0},\widecheck{p}_{\mathscr{A}},\widecheck{\sigma}^{2})]=\mathbf{0}.

If either
\widecheck{\mu}_{0}=\mu_{0}
or
\widecheck{p}_{\mathscr{A}}=p_{\mathscr{A}}
, then
\mathbb{E}[\bm{\phi}_{\mathrm{eff}}(\bm{\beta};\widecheck{\mu}_{0},\widecheck{p}_{\mathscr{A}},\widecheck{\sigma}^{2})]=\mathbf{0}
at the true parameter
\bm{\beta}
in Model (
2.1
). By assuming the positivity of the information matrix at
\bm{\beta}
(Assumption
), the consistency of
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{n}\in\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{\bm{\beta}\in\mathscr{B}}{1\over 2}\left\|\mathbb{E}_{n}[\bm{\phi}_{\mathrm{eff}}(\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{n};\widecheck{\mu}_{0},\widecheck{p}_{\mathscr{A}},\widecheck{\sigma}^{2})]\right\|_{2}^{2}
can be established by the consistency of an M-estimator
(
van1996weak
, Corollary 3.2.3)
. This implies the doubly robust property of
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{n}
. If
(\widecheck{\mu}_{0},\widecheck{p}_{\mathscr{A}},\widecheck{\sigma}^{2})
are replaced by their finite-sample estimate
(\widehat{\mu}_{0,n},\widehat{p}_{\mathscr{A},n},\widehat{\sigma}_{n}^{2})

can be further applied to obtain consistency. Based on the connections from Section
3
, Proposition
3
provides a more general framework to explain the double robustness property discussed in
robins2004optimal
;
lu2013variable
;
wallace2015doubly
;
meng2020robust
.

Our next goal is to study how model specifications can affect estimation efficiency. In Section
