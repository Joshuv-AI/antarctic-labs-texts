Quasi-Monte Carlo finite element approximation of the Navier–Stokes equations with random initial data
Seungchan Ko, Guanglian Li, and Yi Yu

Abstract
In this work, we investigate the numerical approximation of the Navier–Stokes equations on a bounded polygonal domain in ℝ 2 \mathbb{R}^{2} , where the initial condition is modeled by a log-uniform random field. We propose a novel numerical scheme to compute the expected value of linear functionals of the solution to the Navier–Stokes equations. The scheme is based on the finite element discretization in space, backward Euler method in time, truncated Karhunen–Loève expansion for realizing the random initial condition, and lattice-based quasi-Monte Carlo (QMC) for estimating the expected values over the parameter space (i.e., randomly-shifted lattice rules for the integration over a high-dimensional hypercube). We present a rigorous error analysis of the numerical scheme, including bounds on the mean squared error, and especially establish that the QMC converges optimally at an almost-linear rate with the constant independent of the dimension of integration. This provides one rigorous theoretical justification of the use of the QMC sampling strategy for quantifying the uncertainties of nonlinear PDEs subject to random inputs.



Keywords:
quasi-Monte Carlo method, finite element method, uncertainty quantification, Navier–Stokes equations, random initial data, Karhunen–Loève expansion

65D40, 65D32, 65N30, 76D05

Introduction

Uncertainty in the input data of mathematical models has received much recent attention due to its importance in various applications. The uncertainty can arise from different sources, e.g., coefficients, boundary conditions, initial conditions, and external forces. The impact of uncertainty on various quantities of interest sheds valuable insights into the inherent variability of diverse physical and engineering problems. To describe and analyze uncertainty, probability theory offers a flexible framework where uncertain inputs are treated as random fields, and it is especially useful in characterizing the randomness associated with physical quantities of a given system.

In this work, we investigate the incompressible Navier-Stokes equations with random initial data.
Let
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a bounded convex polygonal domain. Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
be a probability space,
where
\Omega
is a sample space consisting of all possible outcomes,
\mathcal{F}\subset 2^{\Omega}
a
\sigma

\mathbb{P}:\mathcal{F}\rightarrow[0,1]

\boldsymbol{u}:[0,T]\times D\times\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
and a random pressure field

such that the following equations hold
\mathbb{P}
-almost surely (a.s.):

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u}+(\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{u}-\Delta\boldsymbol{u}

\displaystyle=-\nabla p,\quad

\displaystyle\mbox{in $(0,T]\times D\times\Omega$},

(1.1)

\displaystyle\text{div}\,{\boldsymbol{u}}

\displaystyle=0,\quad

\displaystyle\mbox{in $(0,T]\times D\times\Omega$},

(1.2)

where the symbols
\nabla
and
\Delta
denote differential operators with respect to the spatial variable
\boldsymbol{x}\in D
, and
\partial_{t}
denotes the time derivative. The system is subject to the homogeneous Dirichlet boundary condition and random initial condition:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}(t,\boldsymbol{x},\omega)

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{0},\quad{\rm{on}}\,\,(0,T]\times\partial D\times\Omega,

(1.3)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}(0,\boldsymbol{x},\omega)

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{u}^{0}(\boldsymbol{x},\omega),\quad{\rm{in}}\,\,D\times\Omega.

(1.4)

In this work, we consider the following initial random field

\boldsymbol{u}^{0}(\boldsymbol{x},\omega)=\nabla^{\perp}\exp(Z(\boldsymbol{x},\omega))=\left(-\partial_{x_{2}}\exp(Z(\boldsymbol{x},\omega)),\partial_{x_{1}}\exp(Z(\boldsymbol{x},\omega))\right),

(1.5)

)-(

,
35
]
of the random initial data

, and obtain a truncated problem with a finite-dimensional random

denote by

\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{G}(\boldsymbol{u}(t_{J}))]

using quasi-Monte Carlo (QMC) methods
[
3
,
43
,
44
]

\varphi(y)=\exp(-y^{2}/2)/\sqrt{2\pi}
be the probability density function (PDF) of the standard normal distribution, and
\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{s}
the cumulative
distribution function (CDF) of the standard normal random vector of length

\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{G}(\boldsymbol{u}^{J}_{s,h})]=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{s}}\mathcal{G}(\boldsymbol{u}^{J}_{s,h}(\cdot,\boldsymbol{y}))\prod_{i=1}^{s}\varphi(y_{i})\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{y}=\mathbb{E}[F^{J}_{s,h}]=\int_{(0,1)^{s}}F^{J}_{s,h}(\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{-1}_{s}(\boldsymbol{v}))\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{v}.

(1.6)

To approximate (

\mathcal{Q}_{s,N}(F^{J}_{s,h};\boldsymbol{\Delta})\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum^{N}_{i=1}F^{J}_{s,h}\left(\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{-1}_{s}\left({\rm{frac}}\left(\frac{i\boldsymbol{z}}{N}+{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}\right)\right)\right),

where
\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{N}^{s}

is a random shift uniformly distributed over

The design and analysis of QMC methods for linear PDEs with random coefficients,
where the QMC methods are utilized to estimate the expected values of linear functionals of the
exact or approximate solution from the PDEs, has been well investigated in the past ten years

[
30
,
12
,
31
,
16
]
. The expected values can be transformed
into an infinite or high dimensional integral over the parameter domain, which corresponds to
the randomness induced by the random coefficient. A proper function space for a given integrand
should be designed to guarantee that the associated high-dimensional integration problem is tractable.
The ”standard” function spaces in the QMC analysis are weighted Sobolev spaces on
(0,1)^{s}
,
which consists of functions with square-integrable mixed first derivatives. If the integrand
lies in a suitable weighted Sobolev space, then RSLRs can be constructed so as to (nearly) achieve
the optimal convergence rate
\mathcal{O}(n^{-1})
; see
[
5
,
6
,
9
,
27
,
42
]

and recent surveys
[
8
,
29
]
.

In this work, we provide a thorough analysis of the numerical approximation
\mathcal{Q}_{s,N}(F^{J}_{s,h};\boldsymbol{\Delta})
. This is achieved by decomposing
the error into the finite element error, the error due to KL
truncation, and the error due to the QMC quadrature. In Theorem
5.9
, we establish a bound
on the root-mean-square error at each time level
J
, i.e.,

(\mathbb{E}^{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}[(\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{G}(\boldsymbol{u}(t_{J}))]-\mathcal{Q}_{s,N}(F^{J}_{s,h};\boldsymbol{\Delta}))^{2}])^{1/2}
,
where
\mathbb{E}^{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}
denotes taking expectation with respect to the random shift
\boldsymbol{\Delta}
. The main technical
challenge in the analysis is to bound the mixed derivatives of the velocity field with respect to the stochastic variable in suitable weighted Sobolev norms. This issue is highly challenging due to the nonlinearity of the mathematical model. These estimates are established in Theorem
5.3
, which allows deriving an error estimate for the QMC sampling strategy applied to the Navier–Stokes equations.

Now we situate the study within existing works on QMC for nonlinear problems. So far despite the extensive studies on QMC for uncertainty quantification, most theoretical analyses on QMC methods are confined to uncertainty quantification for linear PDEs. Further, Harbrecht et al.
[
15
]
studied the computing of nonlinear quantities of interest for a linear PDE with log-normally distributed random coefficients using a deterministic QMC rule. In sharp contrast, the study on nonlinear PDEs is fairly scarce, largely due to a striking lack of study on the related solution regularity theory. In an interesting piece of work, Cohen et al.
[
4
]
also investigate the stationary Navier–Stokes equations with random parameters, and establish the holomorphic dependence of the velocity and the pressure on the domain, with the parameterized families of domain mappings over the uniform distribution. When compared with the work
[
4
]
, we investigate the non-stationary Navier-Stokes equations with random initial conditions, and established new mixed derivative estimates for the solutions. Very recently, Harbrecht et al.
[
17
]
establish regularity bounds for a parametric semilinear elliptic PDE with the nonlinearity appearing on the lowest order term. This result was shown under an
s
-Gevrey assumption using the implicit mapping theorem. This analysis technique differs markedly from the strategy in this work. This work contributes new regularity estimates, and furnishes compelling evidence that QMC methods have significant potential for the uncertainty quantification of nonlinear PDEs.

Weak formulation of the parametric problem

We begin with the notations for useful function spaces. For
m\geq 0
and
1\leq p\leq\infty
, we denote by
L^{p}(D)
and
W^{m,p}(D)
the standard Lebesgue and Sobolev spaces, and when
p=2
, we write
H^{m}(D):=W^{m,2}(D)
. We denote by
H^{1}_{0}(D)
the space of functions in
H^{1}(D)
with zero trace on
\partial D
and by

the space of
C^{\infty}
functions with compact support in
D
. The notation
X(D)^{d}
denotes the

X(D)
, and
\boldsymbol{a}\cdot\boldsymbol{b}
denotes the scalar product of two vectors
\boldsymbol{a}
and
\boldsymbol{b}
. Also,

Next, we define the following functional spaces that are frequently used in the study of incompressible fluid flow:

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}

\displaystyle=\{\boldsymbol{u}\in C^{\infty}_{0}(D)^{2}:{\rm{div}}\,\boldsymbol{u}=0\},\quad V=\overline{\mathcal{V}}^{H^{1}_{0}(D)^{2}},\quad\mbox{and}\quad H=\overline{\mathcal{V}}^{L^{2}(D)^{2}}.

The space
H
is a Hilbert space with the
L^{2}(D)
inner product
(\cdot,\cdot)
, and the space
V
is equipped with the scalar product

a(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})=\int_{D}\nabla\boldsymbol{u}:\nabla\boldsymbol{v}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}.
Further,
we define a trilinear form
B:V\times V\times V\to\mathbb{R}
by

B[\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w}]=\frac{1}{2}\int_{D}\big(((\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{v})\cdot\boldsymbol{w}-((\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{w})\cdot\boldsymbol{v}\big)\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}.

(2.1)

This modified convective term conserves the skew symmetry of the discrete divergence, cf. (
3.2
) below.
Note that
B[\cdot,\cdot,\cdot]
coincides with the trilinear form associated with the
convection term in (

We have the following well-posedness of problem (
1.1
)-(
1.4
) in the 2D case
[
45
]
.

Theorem 2.1
.

For any given
\boldsymbol{u}^{0}\in H
, there exists a unique
\boldsymbol{u}\in L^{\infty}(0,T;H)\cap L^{2}(0,T;V)
such that

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} (\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})+B[\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}]+a(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})&=0,\quad\forall\boldsymbol{v}\in V,\\
\boldsymbol{u}(0)&=\boldsymbol{u}^{0}.\end{aligned}\right.

(2.2)

Furthermore, the following energy estimate holds

\sup_{0<t<T}\|\boldsymbol{u}(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(D)}+2\int^{T}_{0}\|\nabla\boldsymbol{u}(s)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(D)}\,\mathrm{d}s\leq\|\boldsymbol{u}^{0}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(D)}.

(2.3)

It is direct to show that
\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u}\in L^{2}(0,T;V^{\prime})
, which implies
\boldsymbol{u}\in C([0,T];H)
. Thus the identity

Here, the kernel

The integral operator

is self-adjoint and compact from

]

Throughout, we make the following regularity assumption on

.

Assumption 2.2
.

For some

Under Assumption

\mu_{j}\lesssim j^{-k-1}\mbox{ for $j\in\mathbb{N}$ sufficiently large}.

(2.6)

Under Assumption

\|\xi_{j}\|_{H^{\theta k}(D)}\lesssim j^{\frac{\theta(k+1)}{2}}

(2.7)

We define the sequence
\boldsymbol{b}=\{b_{j}\}_{j\geq 1}

(2.8)

For the QMC analysis in Section

Assumption 2.3

There exists some

If

Next, we define the following admissible parameter set

U_{\boldsymbol{b}}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\Big\{\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{\infty}:\sum_{j\geq 1}b_{j}|y_{j}|<\infty\Big\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{\infty}.

Note that the set

is not a product of subsets of

. However,

holds for some

, the set is

. Since

, we can use

. Note that

is not a product domain. But we can define a product measure, e.g.,

3
Finite element approximation

We employ the implicit Euler method in time, and conforming FEM approximation in space for the variational formulation (
2.10
) of the Navier–Stokes system. Let
\mathcal{T}_{h}
be a shape-regular partition of the domain
D
with a mesh size
h
. We define conforming FEM spaces for the velocity
H_{h}\subset H^{1}_{0}(D)^{2}
and the pressure
Q_{h}\subset L^{2}_{0}(D)
by

\displaystyle H_{h}

\displaystyle=\{\mathbf{W}\in C(\overline{D})^{2}:\mathbf{W}|_{K}\in P_{i}(K)^{2},\,\,\forall K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\,\,{\rm{and}}\,\,\mathbf{W}|_{\partial D}=\boldsymbol{0}\},

\displaystyle L_{h}

\displaystyle=\{\Pi\in L^{2}_{0}(D):\Pi|_{K}\in P_{j}(K),\,\,\forall K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\},

where
P_{i}(K)
denotes the space of polynomials of degree
i
on the triangle
K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
.
The FEM spaces
H_{h}
and
L_{h}
are assumed to satisfy the discrete inf-sup condition:

\|q_{h}\|_{L^{2}(D)}\leq C\sup_{\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\in H_{h}\setminus\{\boldsymbol{0}\}}\frac{(\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{v}_{h},q_{h})}{\|\nabla\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\|_{L^{2}(D)}},\quad\forall q_{h}\in L_{h},

(3.1)

where the constant
C>0
is independent of
h
. This condition holds for several finite element spaces,
e.g., Taylor-Hood element or MINI element
[
11
]
. Also we define a discrete divergence-free subspace of
H_{h}
by

V_{h}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\{\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\in H_{h}:(\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{v}_{h},q_{h})=0,\quad\forall q_{h}\in Q_{h}\}.

(3.2)

Given
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
, we divide the interval
[0,T]
into a uniform grid

0=t_{0}<t_{1}<\cdots<t_{\ell}=T
, with
t_{j}=j\tau
,
j=0,1,\cdots,\ell
and the time step size
\tau:=T/{\ell}
.
We employ the following implicit Euler conforming finite element approximation: find
\boldsymbol{u}^{j+1}_{h}\in V_{h}
for
j\in\{0,\cdots,\ell-1\}
such that

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} \tau^{-1}(\boldsymbol{u}^{j+1}_{h}-\boldsymbol{u}^{j}_{h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h})+B[\boldsymbol{u}^{j+1}_{h},\boldsymbol{u}^{j+1}_{h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h}]+a(\boldsymbol{u}^{j+1}_{h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h})=0,&\quad\forall\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\in V_{h},\\
(\boldsymbol{u}_{h}^{0},\boldsymbol{v}_{h})=(\boldsymbol{u}^{0},\boldsymbol{v}_{h}),&\quad\forall\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\in V_{h}.\end{aligned}\right.

(3.3)

The scheme (
3.3
) is well-posed
[
11
,
45
]
, and satisfies the following stability result
[
11
]
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let the time step size
\tau>0
be sufficiently small. Then the discrete problem (
3.3
) has a unique solution
\boldsymbol{u}_{h}^{j}\in V_{h}
,
and furthermore the following stability estimate holds

\|\boldsymbol{u}^{j+1}_{h}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(D)}+\tau\sum^{j}_{k=0}\|\nabla\boldsymbol{u}^{k+1}_{h}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(D)}\leq\|\boldsymbol{u}^{0}_{h}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(D)},\quad\forall j=0,1,\cdots,\ell-1.

(3.4)

Now we discuss the error estimate for the scheme (
3.3
), which has a
long and rich history (see, e.g.,
[
1
,
11
,
45
,
25
]
for
a rather incomplete list).
The scheme has a first-order convergence in time,
while the convergence order for spatial discretization depends on the regularity of solutions.
More precisely, we decompose the error
\boldsymbol{u}(t_{J})-\boldsymbol{u}^{J}_{h}
of the fully-discrete scheme (
3.3
) into

\boldsymbol{u}(t_{J})-\boldsymbol{u}^{J}_{h}=(\boldsymbol{u}(t_{J})-\boldsymbol{u}_{h}(t_{J}))+(\boldsymbol{u}_{h}(t_{J})-\boldsymbol{u}^{J}_{h}),

where
\boldsymbol{u}_{h}
is the solution to the semi-discrete scheme

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} (\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h})+B[\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h}]+a(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h})&=0,\quad\forall\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\in V_{h},\\
(\boldsymbol{u}^{0}_{h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h})&=(\boldsymbol{u}_{0},\boldsymbol{v}_{h}),\quad\forall\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\in V_{h}.\end{aligned}\right.

The terms
\boldsymbol{u}(t_{J})-\boldsymbol{u}_{h}(t_{J})
and
\boldsymbol{u}_{h}(t_{J})-\boldsymbol{u}^{J}_{h}
denote the spatial and
time discretization errors, respectively. For the spatial error, if

\|\boldsymbol{u}^{0}\|_{H^{1}(D)}\leq M
for some
M>0
, then
[
24
, Theorem 4.5]
gives

\|\boldsymbol{u}(t)-\boldsymbol{u}_{h}(t)\|_{L^{2}(D)}\leq Ct^{-\frac{1}{2}}h^{2},\quad\forall t\in(0,T],

(3.5)

where the constant
C:=C(M,T,D)
depends only on
M
,
T
and
D
.
Note that the initial data
\boldsymbol{u}^{0}
also depends on the stochastic variable
\boldsymbol{y}\in U_{b}
. To make the spatial discretization error independent of the stochastic variable
\boldsymbol{y}\in U_{b}
,
we further assume that there exists some constant
M>0
such that

\displaystyle\sup_{\boldsymbol{y}\in U_{\boldsymbol{b}}}\|\boldsymbol{u}^{0}(\boldsymbol{y})\|_{H^{1}(D)}<M.

(3.6)

For the temporal error, there are several results depending on the type of temporal discretization
[
20
,
21
,
45
,
11
,
25
]
, under different time regularity assumptions on the solution. Since a detailed discussion of the finite element approximation of the Navier–Stokes equations is not our main focus, we will not explicitly specify the regularity assumptions. Instead, we assume the following condition: for any sufficiently small
h>0
,

\mathbb{E}\left[\|\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u}_{h}(\boldsymbol{y})\|_{L^{2}(0,T;V)}+\|\partial_{tt}\boldsymbol{u}_{h}(\boldsymbol{y})\|_{L^{2}(0,T;V^{\prime})}\right]\leq C,

(3.7)

for some
C>0
independent of
\boldsymbol{y}\in U_{\boldsymbol{b}}
. See, e.g.,
[
21
,
19
,
18
]
for discussions on similar properties of
\boldsymbol{u}_{h}
.
Then following the argument of
[
45
,
11
]
, we obtain

\mathbb{E}\left[\|\boldsymbol{u}_{h}(t_{J})-\boldsymbol{u}^{J}_{h}\|_{L^{2}(D)}\right]\leq C\tau,\quad\forall J>0,

(3.8)

where the constant
C>0
is independent of
\boldsymbol{y}\in U_{\boldsymbol{b}}
.

Summarizing the above discussion and using the linearity of
\mathcal{G}
and Hölder’s inequality (for
the term related to
\mathcal{G}
), we have the following result.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a convex polygonal domain and
\mathcal{G}\in(L^{2}(D)^{2})^{\prime}
, and let (
3.6
) and (
3.7
) hold.
Then there exists a
C>0
independent of
\boldsymbol{y}\in U_{\boldsymbol{b}}
such that

\mathbb{E}[|\mathcal{G}(\boldsymbol{u}(t_{J}))-\mathcal{G}(\boldsymbol{u}^{J}_{h})|]\leq C(t_{J}^{-\frac{1}{2}}h^{2}+\tau).

4
Truncation of the infinite-dimensional problem

The second discretization step reduces the parametric dimension: we truncate the infinite series (
2.4
) to a finite-dimensional summation, and obtain a truncated initial data

\boldsymbol{u}^{0}_{s}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})=(-\partial_{x_{2}}\exp Z_{s},\partial_{x_{1}}\exp Z_{s}),

(4.1)

where
Z_{s}(\cdot,\cdot)
is the truncated KL expansion of the random field
Z(\cdot,\cdot)
, given by

Z_{s}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}):=\sum_{j=1}^{s}\sqrt{\mu_{j}}\xi_{j}(\boldsymbol{x})y_{j}.

Note that
\boldsymbol{u}^{0}_{s}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})
can be identified as the initial data
\boldsymbol{u}^{0}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})
evaluated at the vector
\boldsymbol{y}=(y_{1},\cdots,y_{s},0,0,\cdots)
. For any set of ‘active’ coordinates
\kappa\subset\mathbb{N}
, we denote the vectors
\boldsymbol{y}\in U_{\boldsymbol{b}}
with
y_{j}=0
for
j\notin\kappa
by
(\boldsymbol{y}_{\kappa};\boldsymbol{0})
.

Then consider the following scheme for the truncated problem with the initial data
\boldsymbol{u}^{0}_{s}
:
Given
\boldsymbol{u}^{j}_{s,h}\in V_{h}
, and
s\in\mathbb{N}
, find
\boldsymbol{u}^{j+1}_{s,h}\in V_{h}
such that

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} \tau^{-1}(\boldsymbol{u}^{j+1}_{s,h}-\boldsymbol{u}^{j}_{s,h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h})+B[\boldsymbol{u}^{j+1}_{s,h},\boldsymbol{u}^{j+1}_{s,h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h}]+a(\boldsymbol{u}^{j+1}_{s,h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h})=0,&\quad\forall\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\in V_{h},\\
(\boldsymbol{u}^{0}_{s,h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h})=(\boldsymbol{u}^{0}_{s},\boldsymbol{v}_{h}),&\quad\forall\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\in V_{h},\end{aligned}\right.

(4.2)

where
\boldsymbol{u}^{0}_{s,h}
is the
L^{2}(D)
-projection of
\boldsymbol{u}^{0}_{s}
into
V_{h}
.
The well-posedness of the scheme (
4.2
) follows by the argument in Section
3
.
The next result gives the truncation error.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let Assumption
2.2
hold and
\mathcal{G}\in(L^{2}(D)^{2})^{\prime}
. If the time step size
\tau>0
is sufficiently small, i.e.,

\displaystyle\tau\|\boldsymbol{u}^{j}_{h}\|^{4}_{L^{4}(D)}<1,\quad\forall j>0\text{ and }h>0,

(4.3)

then for any time level
j\in\{0,1,\cdots,\ell\}
, mesh size
h>0
and parametric dimension
s\in\mathbb{N}
,
there exists
C>0
independent of
j,h
and
s
such that

\mathbb{E}[|\mathcal{G}(\boldsymbol{u}^{j}_{h})-\mathcal{G}(\boldsymbol{u}^{j}_{s,h})|]\leq Cs^{-\frac{k}{2}+\frac{3}{2}}.

Proof.

First, by Fernique’s theorem in Theorem
A.3
, for any
s\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

\|\exp(Z)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega;C(D))}\lesssim 1\quad{\rm{and}}\quad\|\exp(Z_{s})\|_{L^{2}(\Omega;C(D))}\lesssim 1,

(4.4)

(see, e.g., Proposition 5.6 in
[
14
]
).
Let
\boldsymbol{u}^{j}_{h}
and
\boldsymbol{u}^{j}_{s,h}
be the solution to the scheme (
3.3
) and problem (
4.2
),
respectively, and
\boldsymbol{d}^{i}_{s,h}:=\boldsymbol{u}^{i}_{h}-\boldsymbol{u}^{i}_{s,h}
.
Then subtracting (
3.3
) from (
4.2
) and setting
\boldsymbol{v}_{h}=\boldsymbol{d}^{j+1}_{s,h}\in V_{h}
yield

\tau^{-1}(\boldsymbol{d}^{j+1}_{s,h}-\boldsymbol{d}^{j}_{s,h},\boldsymbol{d}^{j+1}_{s,h})+(\nabla\boldsymbol{d}^{j+1}_{s,h},\nabla\boldsymbol{d}^{j+1}_{s,h})=B[\boldsymbol{u}^{j+1}_{h},\boldsymbol{u}^{j+1}_{h},\boldsymbol{d}^{j+1}_{s,h}]-B[\boldsymbol{u}^{j+1}_{s,h},\boldsymbol{u}^{j+1}_{s,h},\boldsymbol{d}^{j+1}_{s,h}].

By the skew symmetry of
B[\cdot,\cdot,\cdot]
, Hölder’s inequality,
Ladyzhenskaya’s inequality in Lemma
A.1
and Young’s inequality, we deduce

\displaystyle\big|B\big[\boldsymbol{u}^{j+1}_{h},\boldsymbol{u}^{j+1}_{h},\boldsymbol{d}^{j+1}_{s,h}]-B\big[\boldsymbol{u}^{j+1}_{s,h},\boldsymbol{u}^{j+1}_{s,h},\boldsymbol{d}^{j+1}_{s,h}\big]\big|

(4.5)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\big|B\big[\boldsymbol{d}^{j+1}_{s,h},\boldsymbol{u}^{j+1}_{h},\boldsymbol{d}^{j+1}_{s,h}\big]+B\big[\boldsymbol{u}^{j+1}_{s,h},\boldsymbol{d}^{j+1}_{s,h},\boldsymbol{d}^{j+1}_{s,h}\big]\big|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\big|-B\big[\boldsymbol{d}^{j+1}_{s,h},\boldsymbol{d}^{j+1}_{s,h},\boldsymbol{u}^{j+1}_{h}\big]\big|\lesssim\|\boldsymbol{d}^{j+1}_{s,h}\|_{L^{4}(D)}\|\nabla\boldsymbol{d}^{j+1}_{s,h}\|_{L^{2}(D)}\|\boldsymbol{u}^{j+1}_{h}\|_{L^{4}(D)}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{d}^{j+1}_{s,h}\|^{1/2}_{L^{2}(D)}\|\nabla\boldsymbol{d}^{j+1}_{s,h}\|^{3/2}_{L^{2}(D)}\|\boldsymbol{u}^{j+1}_{h}\|_{L^{4}(D)}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\tfrac{c}{4}\|\boldsymbol{u}^{j+1}_{h}\|^{4}_{L^{4}(D)}\|\boldsymbol{d}^{j+1}_{s,h}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(D)}+\tfrac{3}{4}\|\nabla\boldsymbol{d}^{j+1}_{s,h}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(D)}.

Using the identity

\tau^{-1}(\boldsymbol{d}^{j+1}_{s,h}-\boldsymbol{d}^{j}_{s,h},\boldsymbol{d}^{j+1}_{s,h})=(2\tau)^{-1}(\|\boldsymbol{d}^{j+1}_{s,h}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(D)}-\|\boldsymbol{d}^{j}_{s,h}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(D)}+\|\boldsymbol{d}^{j+1}_{s,h}-\boldsymbol{d}^{j}_{s,h}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(D)}),

and summing up the previous results from
j=0
to
j=k-1
for
k\geq 1
yield

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{d}^{k}_{s,h}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(D)}+\frac{\tau}{2}\sum^{k-1}_{j=0}\|\nabla\boldsymbol{d}^{j+1}_{s,h}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(D)}\lesssim

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{d}^{0}_{s,h}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(D)}+\frac{\tau}{2}\sum^{k-1}_{j=0}\|\boldsymbol{u}^{j+1}_{h}\|^{4}_{L^{4}(D)}\|\boldsymbol{d}^{j+1}_{s,h}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(D)}.

Then (
4.3
) and the discrete Gronwall’s inequality in Lemma
A.2
imply

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{d}^{k}_{s,h}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(D)}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|\boldsymbol{d}^{0}_{s,h}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(D)}\exp\left(\tau\sum^{k-1}_{j=0}\frac{\|\boldsymbol{u}^{j+1}_{h}\|^{4}_{L^{4}(D)}}{1-\tau\|\boldsymbol{u}^{j+1}_{h}\|^{4}_{L^{4}(D)}}\right)

\displaystyle\lesssim\|\boldsymbol{d}^{0}_{s,h}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(D)}\exp\left(C\tau\sum^{k-1}_{j=0}\|\boldsymbol{u}^{j+1}_{h}\|^{4}_{L^{4}(D)}\right).

Note that the exponential term on the right-hand side is uniformly bounded in
k\geq 1
. Indeed,
Proposition
3.1
and Ladyzhenskaya’s inequality (cf. Lemma
A.1
in the appendix)
yield the following
L^{4}(D)
-estimate on
\boldsymbol{u}_{h}^{j}

\tau\sum^{j-1}_{k=0}\|\boldsymbol{u}^{k+1}_{h}\|^{4}_{L^{4}(D)}\leq 2\|\boldsymbol{u}^{0}_{h}\|^{4}_{L^{2}(D)}.

(4.6)

Further, by taking
\boldsymbol{v}_{h}=\boldsymbol{u}^{0}_{h}-\boldsymbol{u}^{0}_{s,h}
in the definition of
L^{2}
-projection, i.e.,

(\boldsymbol{u}^{0}-\boldsymbol{u}^{0}_{s},\boldsymbol{v}_{h})=(\boldsymbol{u}^{0}_{h}-\boldsymbol{u}^{0}_{s,h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h})
, and Hölder’s inequality, we deduce

\|\boldsymbol{u}^{0}_{h}-\boldsymbol{u}^{0}_{s,h}\|_{L^{2}(D)}\leq\|\boldsymbol{u}^{0}-\boldsymbol{u}^{0}_{s}\|_{L^{2}(D)}\lesssim\|\boldsymbol{u}^{0}-\boldsymbol{u}^{0}_{s}\|_{C(D)},

since the domain
D
is bounded. To estimate
\|\boldsymbol{u}^{0}-\boldsymbol{u}^{0}_{s}\|_{C(D)}
, we derive
from the definitions of
\boldsymbol{u}^{0}
and
\boldsymbol{u}^{0}_{s}
that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\|\boldsymbol{u}^{0}-\boldsymbol{u}^{0}_{s}\|_{C(D)}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\|(-\partial_{x_{2}}Z\exp(Z)+\partial_{x_{2}}Z_{s}\exp(Z_{s}),\partial_{x_{1}}Z\exp(Z)-\partial_{x_{1}}Z_{s}\exp(Z_{s}))\|_{C(D)}\right]

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\partial_{x_{i}}Z\exp(Z)-\partial_{x_{i}}Z_{s}\exp(Z_{s})\|_{C(D)}\right].

Then the triangle inequality, Hölder’s inequality, Assumption
2.2
and the Sobolev embedding yield

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt\mathbb{E}\left[\|\partial_{x_{i}}Z\exp(Z)-\partial_{x_{i}}Z_{s}\exp(Z_{s})\|_{C(D)}\right]

\displaystyle\lesssim\mathbb{E}\left[\|\partial_{x_{i}}Z\exp(Z)-\partial_{x_{i}}Z\exp(Z_{s})\|_{C(D)}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\|\partial_{x_{i}}Z\exp(Z_{s})-\partial_{x_{i}}Z_{s}\exp(Z_{s})\|_{C(D)}\right]

\displaystyle\lesssim\mathbb{E}\left[\|\partial_{x_{i}}Z\|_{C(D)}\|\exp(Z)-\exp(Z_{s})\|_{C(D)}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\|\exp(Z_{s})\|_{C(D)}\|\partial_{x_{i}}Z-\partial_{x_{i}}Z_{s}\|_{C(D)}\right]

\displaystyle\lesssim\mathbb{E}\left[\|\exp(Z)-\exp(Z_{s})\|_{C(D)}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\|\exp(Z_{s})\|_{C(D)}^{2}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}\times\mathbb{E}\left[\|\partial_{x_{i}}Z-\partial_{x_{i}}Z_{s}\|^{2}_{C(D)}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=:{\rm{I}}+{\rm{II}}\times{\rm{III}}.

Note that under Assumption
2.2
, for sufficiently large
s\in\mathbb{N}
, there holds
[
14
]

\|Z-Z_{s}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega;C(D))}\lesssim s^{{-\frac{k}{2}}+1}.

(4.7)

Thus, for the term
{\rm{I}}
, it follows from the inequality
|e^{x}-e^{y}|\leq|x-y|(e^{x}+e^{y})
for
any
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
, Hölder’s inequality, and (
4.4
) that

\displaystyle{\rm{I}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\mathbb{E}\left[\|Z-Z_{s}\|_{C(D)}\|\exp(Z)+\exp(Z_{s})\|_{C(D)}\right]

\displaystyle\lesssim\|Z-Z_{s}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega;C(D))}\|\exp(Z)+\exp(Z_{s})\|_{L^{2}(\Omega;(C(D))}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|Z-Z_{s}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega;C(D))}\left(\|\exp(Z)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega;C(D))}+\|\exp(Z_{s})\|_{L^{2}(\Omega;C(D))}\right)\lesssim s^{-\frac{k}{2}+1}.

Meanwhile, (
4.4
) directly implies
{\rm{II}}\lesssim 1
. Since
\{y_{j}(\omega)\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
is
orthonormal in
L^{2}(\Omega)
, using the estimates (
2.6
) and (
2.7
) leads to

\displaystyle{\rm III}

\displaystyle=\bigg\|\sum_{j>s}\sqrt{\mu_{j}}\partial_{x_{i}}\xi_{j}y_{j}\bigg\|_{L^{2}(\Omega;C(D))}\lesssim\bigg\|\sum_{j\geq s}\sqrt{\mu_{j}}\|\partial_{x_{i}}\xi_{j}\|_{C(D)}y_{j}\bigg\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\lesssim\bigg(\sum_{j>s}\mu_{j}\|\partial_{x_{i}}\xi_{j}\|^{2}_{C(D)}\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}\lesssim\bigg(\sum_{j>s}j^{-k-1}j^{3}\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}\lesssim\left(\int^{\infty}_{s}t^{-k+2}\,\mathrm{d}t\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\lesssim s^{-\frac{k}{2}+\frac{3}{2}},

upon noting
k>2
in Assumption
2.2
. Finally, we derive

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|\mathcal{G}(\boldsymbol{u}^{j}_{h})-\mathcal{G}(\boldsymbol{u}^{j}_{s,h})|]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[|\mathcal{G}(\boldsymbol{u}^{j}_{h}-\boldsymbol{u}^{j}_{s,h})|]\lesssim\mathbb{E}[\|\mathcal{G}\|_{(L^{2}(D))^{\prime}}\|\boldsymbol{u}^{j}_{h}-\boldsymbol{u}^{j}_{s,h}\|_{L^{2}(D)}]

\displaystyle\lesssim\mathbb{E}[\|\boldsymbol{u}^{0}-\boldsymbol{u}^{0}_{s}\|_{L^{2}(D)}]\lesssim s^{-\frac{k}{2}+\frac{3}{2}}.

Combining the preceding estimates proves the desired assertion.
∎

Remark 4.2
.

The estimate (
4.6
) implies

\tau\|\boldsymbol{u}^{j}_{h}\|^{4}_{L^{4}(D)}\leq 2\|\boldsymbol{u}^{0}\|^{4}_{L^{2}(D)}
for all
j\geq 0
and
h>0
.
Below we shall assume the smallness of initial data
\|\boldsymbol{u}^{0}\|_{L^{2}(D)}\leq\varepsilon\approx\tau^{1/2}
. Thus,
for sufficiently small

, the condition (
4.3
) holds independently of
j\geq 1
and

.

5
Quasi-Monte Carlo integration

Now we use QMC to approximate the linear functional
\mathcal{G}\in(L^{2}(D)^{2})^{\prime}
, i.e.,

\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{G}(\boldsymbol{u}^{J}_{s,h})]
. More precisely, given the parametric
dimension
s\in\mathbb{N}
, time level
J\in\{1,\cdots,\ell\}
and mesh size
h>0
, we
approximate
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{G}(\boldsymbol{u}^{J}_{s,h})]
by the following integral

I_{s}(F^{J}_{s,h})\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{s}}F^{J}_{s,h}(\boldsymbol{y})\prod^{s}_{j=1}\phi(y_{j})\,{\textrm{d}}\boldsymbol{y},\quad{\rm{with}}\,\,\,F^{J}_{s,h}(\boldsymbol{y})=\mathcal{G}(\boldsymbol{u}^{J}_{s,h}(\cdot,\boldsymbol{y})).

(5.1)

Here,
\phi(y){\color[rgb]{0,0,0}=}\exp(-y^{2}/2)/\sqrt{2\pi}
and
\Phi(y)=\int^{y}_{-\infty}\exp(-t^{2}/2)/\sqrt{2\pi}\,\mathrm{d}t

is the standard Gaussian probability density function and the cumulative normal distribution function, respectively. To apply QMC methods, we transform the integral (
5.1
)
over the unbounded domain
\mathbb{R}^{s}
to a hyper-cube
[0,1]^{s}
by introducing new variables
\boldsymbol{y}=\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{s}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{v})
, where
\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{s}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{v})
is the inverse cumulative normal distribution function for
\boldsymbol{v}\in[0,1]^{s}
. Then changing variables yields

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{G}\left(\boldsymbol{u}^{J}_{s,h}\right)\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[F^{J}_{s,h}\right]=\int_{(0,1)^{s}}F^{J}_{s,h}(\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{-1}_{s}(\boldsymbol{v}))\,{\textrm{d}}\boldsymbol{v}.

We approximate the integral on
(0,1)^{s}
by a randomly-shifted lattice rule (RSLR):

\mathcal{Q}_{s,N}(F^{J}_{s,h};\boldsymbol{\Delta})\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum^{N}_{i=1}F^{J}_{s,h}\left(\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{-1}_{s}\left({\rm{frac}}\left(\frac{i\boldsymbol{z}}{N}+{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}\right)\right)\right),

(5.2)

where
\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{N}^{s}
is a deterministic generating vector and
{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}
is
a uniformly distributed random shift over
[0,1]^{s}
. We shall establish an
\mathcal{O}(N^{-1})
bound on the root-mean-square error (RMSE)

\textrm{RMSE}_{\rm qmc}(N):=(\mathbb{E}^{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}[(\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{G}(\boldsymbol{u}^{J}_{s,h})]-\mathcal{Q}_{s,N}(F^{J}_{s,h};\boldsymbol{\Delta}))^{2}])^{1/2},

where
\mathbb{E}^{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}
denotes taking expectation in the random shift
{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}\in[0,1]^{s}
, with a constant independent of
s\in\mathbb{N}
,
J\in\mathbb{N}
and
h>0
.

5.1
Solution regularity in the stochastic variables

First we bound mixed first derivatives of
\boldsymbol{u}_{s,h}^{J}(\boldsymbol{y})
with respect to the parametric variable
\boldsymbol{y}\in U_{\boldsymbol{b}}
. This represents the main technical challenge in the QMC analysis. Below
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}=(\mathfrak{u}_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
denotes the standard multi-index of non-negative integers with
|\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}|=\sum_{j\geq 1}\mathfrak{u}_{j}<\infty
, and
\partial^{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}}\boldsymbol{u}
denotes the mixed derivative of
\boldsymbol{u}
with respect to all variables in the multi-index
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}
. We focus on the mixed first derivative
\partial^{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}}
, i.e.,
\mathfrak{u}_{j}\in\{0,1\}
for all
j\in\mathbb{N}
.
By Assumption
2.3
, there exists
N_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
sufficiently large such that

j>N_{0}
implies
b_{j}\leq\frac{1}{2}
. Then we define a sequence of positive numbers
\{C_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
by

C_{j}:=\left\{\begin{aligned} &\max\{2b_{j},1\}&&\text{ if }j\in\{1,\cdots,N_{0}\};\\
&1&&\text{ if }j>N_{0}.\end{aligned}\right.

(5.3)

Then the constant
C^{*}>0
, defined by

\displaystyle C^{*}:=\prod_{j\geq 1}C_{j}=\prod_{j=1}^{N_{0}}\max\{2b_{j},1\},

(5.4)

is finite, i.e.,
C^{*}<\infty
.

Next, we estimate the mixed first derivatives of the discrete truncated initial data
\boldsymbol{u}^{0}_{s,h}
.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
\partial^{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}}
be a mixed first derivative with respect to
\boldsymbol{y}
and let Assumption
2.3
hold. Then the following estimate holds

\|\partial^{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}}\boldsymbol{u}^{0}_{s}\|_{L^{2}(D)}\leq 2^{-|\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}|}(C^{*}+2\sqrt{2})|\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}|\|\exp Z_{s}(\cdot,\boldsymbol{y})\|_{H^{1}(D)}.

(5.5)

Proof.

By the definition of
L^{2}(D)
-projection
\boldsymbol{u}^{0}_{s,h}(\boldsymbol{y})
, for each
\boldsymbol{y}\in U_{\boldsymbol{b}}
, we have

(\boldsymbol{u}^{0}_{s,h}(\boldsymbol{y}),\boldsymbol{v}_{h})=(\boldsymbol{u}^{0}_{s}(\boldsymbol{y}),\boldsymbol{v}_{h}),\quad\forall\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\in V_{h}.

Taking
\partial^{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}}
on both sides
and then setting
\boldsymbol{v}_{h}=\partial^{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}}\boldsymbol{u}^{0}_{s,h}\in V_{h}
yield that for each
\boldsymbol{y}\in U_{\boldsymbol{b}}
,

\left\|\partial^{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}}\boldsymbol{u}^{0}_{s,h}(\boldsymbol{y})\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(D)}=\left(\partial^{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}}\boldsymbol{u}^{0}_{s}(\boldsymbol{y}),\partial^{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}}\boldsymbol{u}^{0}_{s,h}(\boldsymbol{y})\right)\leq\left\|\partial^{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}}\boldsymbol{u}^{0}_{s}(\boldsymbol{y})\right\|_{L^{2}(D)}\left\|\partial^{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}}\boldsymbol{u}^{0}_{s,h}(\boldsymbol{y})\right\|_{L^{2}(D)}.

Hence, we have

\|\partial^{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}}\boldsymbol{u}^{0}_{s,h}(\boldsymbol{y})\|_{L^{2}(D)}\leq\|\partial^{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}}\boldsymbol{u}^{0}_{s}(\boldsymbol{y})\|_{L^{2}(D)}.

(5.6)

With the notation
j\in\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}
when
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}_{j}=1
, by the product rule,

\displaystyle\|\partial^{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}}\boldsymbol{u}^{0}_{s}\|_{L^{2}(D)}=\|\partial^{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}}\partial_{x}\exp Z_{s}\|_{L^{2}(D)}=\bigg\|\partial_{x}\bigg[\bigg(\prod_{j\in\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}}\sqrt{\mu_{j}}\xi_{j}\bigg)\exp Z_{s}\bigg]\bigg\|_{L^{2}(D)}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\bigg\|\bigg(\prod_{j\in\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}}\sqrt{\mu_{j}}\xi_{j}\bigg)\partial_{x}\exp Z_{s}\bigg\|_{L^{2}(D)}\!\!\!+\bigg\|\sum_{i\in\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}}\sqrt{\mu_{i}}\partial_{x}\xi_{i}\bigg(\prod_{j\in\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}},\,j\neq i}\sqrt{\mu_{j}}\xi_{j}\bigg)\exp Z_{s}\bigg\|_{L^{2}(D)}\!\!\!

\displaystyle=:

\displaystyle{\rm{I}}+{\rm{II}}.

By (
5.3
) and (
5.4
), the following inequality holds

\prod_{j\in\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}}b_{j}=\prod_{j\in\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}},\,j>N_{0}}b_{j}\times\prod_{j\in\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}},\,j\leq N_{0}}b_{j}\leq\prod_{j\in\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}},\,j>N_{0}}\frac{1}{2}\times\prod_{j\in\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}},\,j\leq N_{0}}\frac{C_{j}}{2}\leq\frac{C^{*}}{2^{|\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}|}}.

Then we can bound the first term
\rm{I}
by

{\rm{I}}\leq\bigg(\prod_{j\in\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}}b_{j}\bigg)\|\partial_{x}\exp Z_{s}\|_{L^{2}(D)}\leq\frac{C^{*}}{2^{|\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}|}}\|\partial_{x}\exp Z_{s}\|_{L^{2}(D)}.

For the term
{\rm{II}}
, we discuss the cases
|\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}|\geq 2
and
|\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}|=1

Remark 5.2

The small data assumption (

]

that satisfies the smallness assumption (

close to 1 for the specific choice of

5.2

Most QMC methods in the literature are applied and analyzed with the class of functions defined over the unit hypercube. In this work, the suitable function spaces in the QMC analysis are weighted Sobolev spaces on

To be more precise, it is known from the classical theory that if the integrand lies in a suitable weighted Sobolev space, randomly-shifted lattice rules (RSLRs) can be constructed so as to (nearly) achieve the optimal convergence rate
\mathcal{O}(n^{-1})
; see
[
5
,
6
,
9
,
27
,
42
]
and recent surveys
[
8
,
29
]
. Since the integral in (
5.1
) is defined over
\mathbb{R}^{s}
, we have first transformed the domain to
(0,1)^{s}
and obtain

. However, the resulting integrand may be unbounded near the boundary
\partial(0,1)^{s}
,
and thus the standard QMC theory is not directly applicable. The function space setting for the integral of the type (
5.1
) has been studied in
[
23
,
34
,
46
,
47
,
32
,
38
]
, and the optimal convergence rate has been obtained using RSLRs. The corresponding weighted Sobolev norm is defined by
[
32
,
38
]

\|F\|^{2}_{\mathcal{W}_{s}}:=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}\subset[s]}\frac{1}{\gamma_{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{|\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}|}}\!\!\!\!\Big(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{s-|\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}|}}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\partial^{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}}F(\boldsymbol{y}_{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}};\boldsymbol{y}_{[s]\setminus\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}})\prod_{j\in[s]\setminus\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}}\!\!\!\!\phi(y_{j})\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{y}_{[s]\setminus\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}}\Big)^{2}\prod_{j\in\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}}\psi^{2}_{j}(y_{j})\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{y}_{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}},

(5.13)

where
[s]=\{1,2,\cdots,s\}

denotes the mixed first derivative with respect to each “active” variables

for

) is called “unanchored” since the inactive variables are integrated out as opposed to being “anchored” at a certain fixed value, e.g.,
0
.

For each
j\geq 1
, the continuous weight function
\psi_{j}:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
in (
5.13
) should be
properly chosen in order to handle the singularities for the active variables. The analysis in
[
38
]
indicates
that
\psi^{2}_{j}(y)
should decay slower than the standard Gaussian density in (
5.1
) as
|y|\rightarrow\infty
: for some
a_{j}>0
, such that

\psi^{2}_{j}(y)=\exp(-2a_{j}|y|).

(5.14)

Throughout, we assume that for some constants
0<a_{\min}<a_{\max}<\infty
,

a_{\min}<a_{j}\leq a_{\max},\quad j\in\mathbb{N}.

(5.15)

For each index
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}\subset\mathbb{N}
with finite cardinality
|\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}|<\infty
, we associate
a weight parameter vector
\gamma_{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}}>0
to indicate the relative importance
of the variables. We write
\boldsymbol{\gamma}=(\gamma_{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}})_{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}\subset\mathbb{N}}
and
let
\gamma_{\emptyset}:=1
. In
[
32
]
, only “products weights” were considered,
i.e., there exists a sequence
\gamma_{1}\geq\gamma_{2}\geq\cdots>0
, where each
\gamma_{j}
is
associated with an integral variable
y_{j}
and let
\gamma_{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}}:=\prod_{j\in\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}}\gamma_{j}
.
See also
[
38
]
for further generalizations.
The choice of the weight parameters
\gamma_{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}}
is important to guarantee that the constant
in the QMC error bound does not grow exponentially as
s\rightarrow\infty
. In this work,
we employ the so-called “product and order dependent weights” (“POD” weights) introduced
in
[
30
]
. Specifically, we consider two different sequences
\Gamma_{0}=\Gamma_{1}=1
,

\Gamma_{2},\cdots,\Gamma_{s}
and
\gamma_{1}\geq\gamma_{2}\geq\cdots\geq\gamma_{s}>0
such that

\gamma_{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}}:=\Gamma_{|\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}|}\prod_{j\in\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}}\gamma_{j}
.

5.3
Error bound for randomly-shifted lattice rules

To bound the error of the QMC integration, we define the worst-case error
e^{\rm{wor}}_{s,N}(\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{\Delta})
of
the shifted lattice rule (
5.2
): for a generating vector
\boldsymbol{z}
and a random shift
\boldsymbol{\Delta}
,

e^{\rm{wor}}_{s,N}(\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{\Delta}):=\sup_{\|F\|_{\mathcal{W}_{s}}\leq 1}|I_{s}(F)-\mathcal{Q}_{s,N}(F;\boldsymbol{\Delta})|
,
where
I_{s}
is the integral defined in (
5.1
).
By the linearity of integration, we have

|I_{s}(F^{J}_{s,h})-\mathcal{Q}_{s,N}(F^{J}_{s,h};\boldsymbol{\Delta})|\leq e^{\rm{wor}}_{s,N}(\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{\Delta})\|F^{J}_{s,h}\|_{\mathcal{W}_{s}}.

In this work, we consider the root-mean-square error (RMSE) for QMC:

{\rm RMSE}_{\rm qmc}(N)\leq e^{\rm{sh}}_{s,N}(\boldsymbol{z})\|F^{J}_{s,h}\|_{\mathcal{W}_{s}},

(5.16)

with
e^{\rm{sh}}_{s,N}(\boldsymbol{z}):=(\int_{[0,1]^{s}}(e^{\rm{wor}}_{s,N}(\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{\Delta}))^{2}\,{\rm{d}}\boldsymbol{\Delta})^{1/2}
(often known as shift-averaged worst case error). By (
5.16
), we can decouple the dependence on
\boldsymbol{z}
from that on the integrand
F^{J}_{s,h}
.

A generating vector
\boldsymbol{z}=(z_{1},z_{2},z_{3},\cdots)
can be constructed by a component-by-component algorithm which determines
z_{1},z_{2},z_{3},\cdots
sequentially, using
e^{\rm{sh}}_{s,N}(\boldsymbol{z})
as the search criterion: given that
z_{1},\cdots,z_{i}
are already determined,
z_{i+1}
is chosen from the set
\{1\leq z\leq N-1:{\rm{gcd}}(z,N)=1\}
to minimize
e^{\rm{sh}}_{i+1,N}(z_{1},\cdots,z_{i+1})
. See
[
38
]
for the precise formula for
e^{\rm{sh}}_{s,N}(\boldsymbol{z})
for general weight functions
\psi_{j}
and weight parameters
\gamma_{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}}
:

\left(e^{\rm{sh}}_{s,N}(\boldsymbol{z})\right)^{2}=\sum_{\emptyset\neq\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}\subset[s]}\frac{\gamma_{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}}}{N}\sum^{N}_{i=1}\prod_{k\in\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}}\theta_{k}\left(\left\{\frac{iz_{k}}{N}\right\}\right),

with

\theta_{k}(f)=\int^{\infty}_{\Phi^{-1}(f)}\frac{\Phi(t)-f}{\psi^{2}_{k}(t)}\,\mathrm{d}t+\int^{\infty}_{\Phi^{-1}(1-f)}\frac{\Phi(t)-1+f}{\psi^{2}_{k}(t)}\,\mathrm{d}t-\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}\frac{\Phi^{2}(t)}{\psi^{2}_{k}(t)}\,\mathrm{d}t.

See also
[
38
]
for the following choices of
\phi
and
\psi_{k}
with
a nearly
\mathcal{O}(N^{-1})
convergence rate: the cumulative normal distribution function and weight functions
in (
5.14
). Below we use a result from
[
12
]
. Note here that
\varphi(p)=p-1
for
p
prime, and it is known that
\frac{1}{\varphi(N)}<\frac{9}{N}
for any
N\leq 10^{30}
. Hence, in practice, we can replace
\varphi(N)
by
\frac{C}{N}
for some
C>0
.

Theorem 5.4
.

For given
h>0
,
s,J,N\in\mathbb{N}
, weight parameters
\boldsymbol{\gamma}=(\gamma_{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}})_{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}\subset\mathbb{N}}
, Gaussian density function
\phi
and weight function
\psi_{j}
defined in (
5.14
), an RSLR with
N
points can be constructed by a component-by-component algorithm satisfying for any
\lambda\in(1/2,1]
,

{\rm RMSE}_{\rm qmc}(N)\leq\Big(\sum_{\emptyset\neq\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}\subset\{1:s\}}\gamma^{\lambda}_{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}}\prod_{i\in\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}}\varrho_{i}(\lambda)\Big)^{\frac{1}{2\lambda}}\left[\varphi(N)\right]^{-\frac{1}{2\lambda}}\|F^{J}_{s,h}\|_{\mathcal{W}_{s}}

with

\varrho_{i}(\lambda):=2\left(\frac{\sqrt{2\pi}\exp(a^{2}_{i}/\eta)}{\pi^{2-2\eta}(1-\eta)\eta}\right)^{\lambda}\zeta\left(\lambda+\frac{1}{2}\right)\quad{\rm{and}}\quad\eta:=\frac{2\lambda-1}{4\lambda},

(5.17)

where
\varphi(n):=|\{1\leq z\leq n-1:{\rm{gcd}}(z,n)=1\}|
is the Euler totient function and
\zeta(x):=\sum^{\infty}_{k=1}k^{-x}
denotes the Riemann zeta function.

5.4
Estimate for the weighted Sobolev norm

The next result shows that for each
s\in\mathbb{N}
and choice of
\gamma_{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}}
, we have
\|F^{J}_{s,h}\|_{\mathcal{W}_{s}}<\infty
for all
J\in\mathbb{N}
and
h>0
. Thus, together with Theorem
5.4
, we obtain an estimate for the
\rm RMSE_{qmc}
with a nearly
\mathcal{O}(N^{-1})
convergence rate. However, the bound may depend on the parametric dimension
s\in\mathbb{N}
. In Section
5.5
, we will prove that a careful choice of
\gamma_{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}}
can remove the dependency on
s\in\mathbb{N}
.

Theorem 5.5
.

Let the weight functions
\psi_{j}
be defined in (
5.14
) and
F^{J}_{s,h}
the
integrand in (
5.1
) for any
J=1,\cdots,\ell
and
h>0
. Then
F^{J}_{s,h}\in\mathcal{W}_{s}
,
and with constants
a_{i}
and
b_{i}
defined in (
5.14
) and (
2.8
), its
\mathcal{W}_{s}
norm is bounded by

\|F^{J}_{s,h}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{W}_{s}}\leq(2\sqrt{2}+2C^{*})^{2}\|\mathcal{G}\|_{(L^{2}(D))^{\prime}}^{2}\sum_{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}\subset[s]}\frac{(|\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}|!)^{2}2^{2|\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}|}}{\gamma_{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}}}\prod_{i\in\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}}\frac{b_{i}^{2}}{a_{i}}.

Proof.

By Theorem
5.3
and the linearity of
\mathcal{G}
, we obtain for each
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{s}
,

|\partial^{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}}F^{J}_{s,h}(\boldsymbol{y})|\leq\|\mathcal{G}\|_{(L^{2}(D))^{\prime}}\|\partial^{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}}\boldsymbol{u}^{J}_{s,h}(\boldsymbol{y})\|_{2}\leq\widetilde{C}|\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}|!2^{|\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}|}\bigg(\prod_{j\in\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}}b_{j}\bigg)\|\exp Z_{s}(\boldsymbol{y})\|_{H^{1}(D)},

with
\widetilde{C}:=(2\sqrt{2}+2C^{*})\|\mathcal{G}\|_{(L^{2}(D))^{\prime}}
. Further, the smallness of the truncated log-normal random field (
5.8
) implies that
\|\exp Z_{s}(\boldsymbol{y})\|_{H^{1}(D)}\leq 1
for all
s\in\mathbb{N}
and
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{s}
. Then the definition of the weighted Sobolev norm (
5.13
) implies

\displaystyle\|F^{J}_{s,h}\|_{\mathcal{W}_{s}}^{2}\leq

\displaystyle\widetilde{C}^{2}\sum_{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}\subset[s]}\frac{(|\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}|!)^{2}2^{2|\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}|}}{\gamma_{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}}}\!\!\Big(\prod_{j\in\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}}b_{j}\Big)^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{|\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}|}}\!\!\Big(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{s-|\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}|}}\!\!\!\!\prod_{j\in[s]\setminus\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}}\!\!\phi(y_{j})\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{y}_{[s]\setminus\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}}\Big)^{2}\prod_{j\in\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}}\psi^{2}_{j}(y_{j})\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{y}_{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\widetilde{C}^{2}\sum_{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}\subset[s]}\frac{(|\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}|!)^{2}2^{2|\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}|}}{\gamma_{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}}}\prod_{j\in\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}}\frac{b_{j}^{2}}{a_{j}},

since
\int_{\mathbb{R}}\phi(y)\,{\rm{d}}y=1
and
\int_{\mathbb{R}}\psi^{2}_{j}(y)\,{\rm{d}}y=\frac{1}{a_{j}}
for all
j\in\mathbb{N}
.
∎

Theorems
5.4
and
5.5
together give the following RMSE estimate.

Theorem 5.6
.

Let
F^{J}_{s,h}
be the integrand defined in (
5.1
) and let
\psi_{j}
be a weight function defined in (
5.14
). For given
s,J,N\in\mathbb{N}
with
N\leq 10^{30}
,
h>0
, weights
\boldsymbol{\gamma}=(\gamma_{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}})_{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}\subset\mathbb{N}}
and standard Gaussian density function
\phi
, we can construct an RSLR with
N
points in
s
dimensions by a component-by-component algorithm such that for any
\lambda\in(1/2,1]
,

{\rm RMSE}_{\rm qmc}(N)\leq 9(2\sqrt{2}+2C^{*})\|\mathcal{G}\|_{(L^{2}(D))^{\prime}}K_{\boldsymbol{\gamma},s}(\lambda)N^{-\frac{1}{2\lambda}},

(5.18)

with
\varrho_{i}(\lambda)
defined in (
5.17
),

K_{\boldsymbol{\gamma},s}(\lambda):=\Big(\sum_{\emptyset\neq\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}\subset[s]}\gamma^{\lambda}_{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}}\prod_{i\in\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}}\varrho_{i}(\lambda)\Big)^{\frac{1}{2\lambda}}\Big(\sum_{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}\subset[s]}\frac{(|\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}|!)^{2}2^{2|\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}|}}{\gamma_{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}}}\prod_{i\in\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}}\frac{b_{i}^{2}}{a_{i}}\Big)^{1/2}.

(5.19)

In general,
K_{\boldsymbol{\gamma},s}(\lambda)
may grow with the parametric dimension
s
. In order to bound
K_{\boldsymbol{\gamma},s}(\lambda)
uniformly with respect to
s\in\mathbb{N}
, we need to choose
\gamma_{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}}
carefully so that

K_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(\lambda):=\Big(\sum_{|\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}|<\infty}\gamma^{\lambda}_{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}}\prod_{i\in\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}}\varrho_{i}(\lambda)\Big)^{\frac{1}{2\lambda}}\Big(\sum_{|\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}|<\infty}\frac{(|\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}|!)^{2}2^{2|\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}|}}{\gamma_{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}}}\prod_{i\in\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}}\frac{b_{i}^{2}}{a_{i}}\Big)^{1/2}<\infty.

(5.20)

If (
5.20
) holds, then the estimate
K_{\boldsymbol{\gamma},s}(\lambda)\leq K_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(\lambda)<\infty
holds for any
s\in\mathbb{N}
, and accordingly the error bound (
5.18
) is independent of the dimension
s
.

5.5
Choice of weight parameters
\gamma_{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}}

For any
\lambda\in(1/2,1]
, we follow the strategy in
[
30
,
12
]
to choose weight parameters
\gamma_{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}}
that minimize the constant
K_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(\lambda)
, cf. (
5.20
), and that
K_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(\lambda)
is finite.
Note that the constant
K_{\boldsymbol{\gamma},s}(\lambda)
in (
5.19
) and the uniform bound
K_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(\lambda)
in (
5.20
) have the same form as the function appearing in Lemma
A.4
. Therefore, we can infer the proper form of the weight parameters
\gamma_{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}}
. Below we specify the parameter
\lambda>0
so that the constant
K_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(\lambda)
is finite in our setting and to obtain a good convergence rate.

Theorem 5.7
.

Let
\psi_{j}
be the weight functions defined in (
5.14
) with
a_{j}
satisfying (
5.15
), and Assumption
2.3
hold for some
p\leq 1
. When
p=1
, additionally,

\sum_{j\geq 1}b_{j}\leq\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{a_{\min}}{\varrho_{\max}(1)}},

(5.21)

with
\varrho_{\max}(\lambda)
defined by replacing
a_{i}
in (
5.17
) by
a_{\max}
in (
5.15
). Then for each fixed
\lambda\in(1/2,1]
, the weight

\gamma_{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}}=\gamma^{*}_{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}}(\lambda):=\Big((|\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}|!)^{2}2^{2|\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}|}\prod_{j\in\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}}\frac{b_{j}^{2}}{a_{j}\varrho_{j}(\lambda)}\Big)^{1/(1+\lambda)}

(5.22)

is the minimizer of
K_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(\lambda)
if the minimum is finite. Additionally, if we choose

\lambda=\lambda_{*}:=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2-2\delta},&{\rm{if}}\,\,p\in(0,2/3],\\
\frac{p}{2-p},&{\rm{if}}\,\,p\in(2/3,1),\\
1,&{\rm{if}}\,\,p=1,\end{cases}

(5.23)

for arbitrary
\delta\in(0,1/2]
, and set
\gamma_{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}}=\gamma_{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}}^{*}(\lambda_{*})
, then
K_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(\lambda)<\infty
. Moreover, an RSLR can be constructed by a component-by-component algorithm such that

\mathrm{RMSE}_{\rm qmc}\lesssim N^{-\chi},\quad\mbox{with }\chi=(2\lambda_{*})^{-1},

in other words,

\mathrm{RMSE}_{\rm qmc}\lesssim\begin{cases}N^{-(1-\delta)}\quad&{\rm{if}}\,\,p\in(0,2/3],\\
N^{-(1/p-1/2)}\quad&{\rm{if}}\,\,p\in(2/3,1),\\
N^{-\frac{1}{2}}\quad&{\rm{if}}\,\,p=1,\end{cases}

where the constants are independent of the truncation dimension
s\in\mathbb{N}
, but may depend on
p\in(0,1]
and
\delta\in(0,1/2]
if relevant.

Proof.

First, note that the finite subsets of
\mathbb{N}
in (
5.20
) can be ordered and the specific choice of ordering is not important, since the convergence is unconditional. Therefore, by Lemma
A.4
, the choice of weights (
5.22
) minimizes
K_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(\lambda)
, cf
[
30
,
12
]
. Next, we prove that
K_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(\lambda)
is finite when the weight and the parameter
\lambda
are given by (
5.22
) and (
5.23
), respectively. Indeed, define the following quantity

S_{\lambda}:=\sum_{|\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}|<\infty}(\gamma_{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}}^{*})^{\lambda}\prod_{j\in\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}}\varrho_{j}(\lambda)=\sum_{|\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}|<\infty}\Big((|\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}|!)^{2}2^{2|\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}|}\prod_{j\in\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}}\frac{[\varrho_{j}(\lambda)]^{1/\lambda}b_{j}^{2}}{a_{j}}\Big)^{\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda}}.

(5.24)

Then
K_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(\lambda)=S_{\lambda}^{1/(2\lambda)+1/2}
, and hence it suffices to show that
S_{\lambda}
is finite.
From (
5.17
), for each
\lambda
,
\varrho_{j}(\lambda)
monotonically increases with respect to
a_{j}
, and thus we obtain
\varrho_{j}(\lambda)\leq\varrho_{\max}(\lambda)
for any
j\geq 1
. Consequently,

S_{\lambda}\leq\sum_{|\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}|<\infty}(|\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}|!)^{2\lambda/(1+\lambda)}\prod_{j\in\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}}\left(\frac{4[\varrho_{\max}(\lambda)]^{1/\lambda}}{a_{\min}}b_{j}^{2}\right)^{\lambda/(1+\lambda)}.

(5.25)

Now we consider the cases
\lambda\in(1/2,1)
and
\lambda=1
separately. For
\lambda\in(1/2,1)
, we have

2\lambda/(1+\lambda)<1
. Next, we multiply and divide the right-hand side of (
5.25
) by
\prod_{j\in\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}}A_{j}^{2\lambda/(1+\lambda)}
, with
A_{j}>0
to be specified. By Hölder’s inequality with Hölder conjugate exponents
(1+\lambda)/(2\lambda)
and
(1+\lambda)/(1-\lambda)
, we obtain

\displaystyle S_{\lambda}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{|\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}|<\infty}(|\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}|!)^{2\lambda/(1+\lambda)}\prod_{j\in\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}}A_{j}^{2\lambda/(1+\lambda)}\prod_{j\in\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}}\left(\frac{4[\varrho_{\max}(\lambda)]^{1/\lambda}}{a_{\min}}\frac{b_{j}^{2}}{A_{j}^{2}}\right)^{\lambda/(1+\lambda)}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\sum_{|\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}|<\infty}|\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}|!\prod_{j\in\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}}A_{j}\right)^{2\lambda/(1+\lambda)}\left(\sum_{|\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}|<\infty}\prod_{j\in\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}}\left(\frac{4[\varrho_{\max}(\lambda)]^{1/\lambda}}{a_{\min}}\frac{b_{j}^{2}}{A_{j}^{2}}\right)^{\lambda/(1-\lambda)}\right)^{(1-\lambda)/(1+\lambda)}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{1}{1-\sum_{j\geq 1}A_{j}}\right)^{\frac{2\lambda}{1+\lambda}}\exp\left(\frac{1-\lambda}{1+\lambda}\left(\frac{4[\varrho_{\max}(\lambda)]^{1/\lambda}}{a_{\min}}\right)^{\frac{\lambda}{1-\lambda}}\sum_{j\geq 1}\left(\frac{b_{j}}{A_{j}}\right)^{\frac{2\lambda}{1-\lambda}}\right).

where the last step follows from Lemma
A.5
, which holds if

\sum_{j\geq 1}A_{j}<1\quad{\rm{and}}\quad\sum_{j\geq 1}(b_{j}/A_{j})^{2\lambda/(1-\lambda)}<\infty.

(5.26)

Upon choosing

A_{j}:=b_{j}^{p}/\alpha
for some
\alpha>\sum_{j\geq 1}b_{j}^{p}
, Assumption
2.3
implies
\sum_{j\geq 1}A_{j}<1
. Further, Assumption
2.3
also implies
\sum_{j\geq 1}b_{j}^{q}
for any
q\geq p
. Hence the second sum in (
5.26
) converges provided that

\frac{2\lambda}{1-\lambda}(1-p)\geq p
if and only if
\lambda\geq\frac{p}{2-p}
.
Since
\lambda\in(1/2,1)
, if
p\in(0,2/3)
we have
\frac{1}{2}\geq\frac{p}{2-p}
and hence we can choose
\lambda=1/(2-2\delta)
for some
\delta\in(0,1/2)
, so that
\frac{p}{2-p}\leq\frac{1}{2}<\lambda<1
. If
p\in(2/3,1)
, we have
\frac{1}{2}<\frac{p}{2-p}<1
, and we may choose
\lambda=p/(2-p)
. When
p=1
, we choose
\lambda=1
. Then by Lemma
A.5
, we have from (
5.25
) that

S_{1}\leq\sum_{|\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}|<\infty}|\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}|!\prod_{j\in\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}}\left(\frac{4\varrho_{\max}(1)}{a_{\min}}b_{j}^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq\Big(1-\sum_{j\geq 1}2b_{j}\Big(\frac{\varrho_{\max}(1)}{a_{\min}}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\Big)^{-1},

which is finite by assumption (
5.21
). This completes the proof of the theorem.
∎

Last, we give a proper choice of
a_{j}
which will be used in numerical experiments.

Corollary 5.8
.

Let
\lambda=\lambda_{*}
and
\gamma_{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}}=\gamma_{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}}^{*}(\lambda_{*})
be defined in (
5.23
) and (
5.22
), respectively. Then the constant
K_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(\lambda)
in (
5.20
) is minimized when

a_{j}=\sqrt{\frac{2\lambda_{*}-1}{8\lambda_{*}}}
for all
j\geq 1
.

Proof.

In the proof of Theorem
5.7
, we have
K_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(\lambda)=S_{\lambda}^{1/(2\lambda)+1/2}
, with
S_{\lambda}
defined in (
5.24
). Since all terms in (
5.24
) are positive, it is sufficient to minimize each
[\varrho_{j}(\lambda)]^{1/\lambda}/a_{j}
with respect to
a_{j}
, in order to minimize
K_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(\lambda)
with respect to
a_{j}
. By definition, we have
[\varrho_{j}(\lambda)]^{1/\lambda}=c\exp(a_{j}^{2}/\eta_{*})
for some
c>0
independent of
a_{j}
and
\eta_{*}=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{4\lambda}
. Then direct computation shows that the choice of
a_{j}
minimizes
S_{\lambda}
, and hence
K_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(\lambda)
.
∎

Last, we give the total error of the approximation
\mathcal{Q}_{s,N}(F^{J}_{s,h};\boldsymbol{\Delta})
.

Theorem 5.9
.

Let the assumptions of Theorems
3.2
,
4.1
and
5.7
hold. Then the RMSE of
\mathcal{Q}_{s,N}(F^{J}_{s,h};\boldsymbol{\Delta})
with respect to the random shift
\boldsymbol{\Delta}\in[0,1]^{s}
is bounded by

(\mathbb{E}^{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}[(\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{G}(\boldsymbol{u}(t_{J}))]-\mathcal{Q}_{s,N}(F^{J}_{s,h};\boldsymbol{\Delta}))^{2}])^{1/2}\leq C(t_{J}^{-\frac{1}{2}}h^{2}+\tau+s^{-\frac{k}{2}+\frac{3}{2}}+N^{-\chi}),

(5.27)

where the constant
C>0
is independent of positive parameters
h
,
\tau
,
s
and
N
.

Proof.

The error
\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{G}(\boldsymbol{u}(t_{J}))\right]-\mathcal{Q}_{s,N}(F^{J}_{s,h};\boldsymbol{\Delta})
can be decomposed into

\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{G}(\boldsymbol{u}(t_{J}))-\mathcal{G}(\boldsymbol{u}^{J}_{h})]+\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{G}(\boldsymbol{u}^{J}_{h})-\mathcal{G}(\boldsymbol{u}^{J}_{s,h})]+(\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{G}(\boldsymbol{u}^{J}_{s,h})]-\mathcal{Q}_{s,N}(F^{J}_{s,h};\boldsymbol{\Delta})),

where the expectation
\mathbb{E}
is with respect to
\boldsymbol{y}\in U_{\boldsymbol{b}}
.
Then the desired bound on
(\mathbb{E}^{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}[(\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{G}(\boldsymbol{u}(t_{J}))]-\mathcal{Q}_{s,N}(F^{J}_{s,h};\boldsymbol{\Delta}))^{2}])^{1/2}
follows from
Theorems
3.2
,
4.1
and
5.7
directly.
∎

6
Numerical experiments

Since Theorem
5.7
is our main novel theoretical finding, we only conduct several numerical tests to complement the analysis in Theorem
5.7
, we present some numerical results for the system (
1.1
)-(
1.4
) in the domain
[0,T]\times D\times\Omega
, with
T=1
and
D=(0,1)^{2}
. All computations were performed using MATLAB on HPC system at The University of Hong Kong, using 64 cores, each with 3GB memory.

In the computation, we take a time step size
\tau=0.1
. We divide the square domain
D
into
1/h^{2}
congruent small squares (with
h=1/16
), and then further divide each small square into two right triangles, obtaining a shape-regular triangulation
\mathcal{T}_{h}
. To solve problem (
2.10
), we employ the Taylor-Hood element on the mesh
\mathcal{T}_{h}
, i.e., a conforming piecewise quadratic element for the velocity
\boldsymbol{u}
and a conforming piecewise linear element for the pressure
p
. The resulting FEM spaces are given by

\displaystyle H_{h}

\displaystyle:=\{\mathbf{W}\in C(\overline{D})^{2}:\mathbf{W}|_{K}\in{P}_{2}(K)^{2}\,\,\forall K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\,\,{\rm{and}}\,\,\mathbf{W}|_{\partial D}=\boldsymbol{0}\},

\displaystyle Q_{h}

\displaystyle:=\{\Pi\in C(\overline{D}):\Pi|_{K}\in{P}_{1}(K)\,\,\forall K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\text{ and }\int_{D}\Pi\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}=0\}.

This choice satisfies the discrete inf-sup condition (
3.1
).
To handle the divergence-free subspace, we employ a continuous bilinear form
c(\cdot,\cdot)
on
H_{h}\times Q_{h}
by

c(\boldsymbol{v},q)=-\int_{D}q\,(\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{v})\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}
. The trilinear term
B[\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w}]
(
3.3
), arising from the convective term
(\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{u}
, is nonlinear. We employ Picard’s method to linearize the problem, which constructs a sequence of approximations
(\boldsymbol{u}^{j+1,k}_{s,h},p^{j+1,k}_{s,h})
by solving

\left\{\begin{split}\tau^{-1}(\boldsymbol{u}^{j+1,k}_{s,h}&-\boldsymbol{u}^{j}_{s,h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h})+a(\boldsymbol{u}^{j+1,k}_{s,h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h})\\
&+B[\boldsymbol{u}^{j+1,k-1}_{s,h},\boldsymbol{u}^{j+1,k}_{s,h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h}]+c(\boldsymbol{v}_{h},p^{j+1,k}_{s,h})=0,\quad\forall\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\in H_{h},\\
c(\boldsymbol{u}^{j+1,k}_{s,h},q_{h})&=0,\quad\forall q_{h}\in Q_{h}.\end{split}\right.

This formulation is equivalent to the pressure-free formulation for the FEM approximation in Section
3
, since we use an inf-sup stable velocity-pressure pair for the discrete solutions. We take the initialization

\boldsymbol{u}^{j+1,0}_{s,h}=\boldsymbol{u}^{j}_{s,h}
. The iteration process is terminated when the relative
L^{2}(D)
-norm between the solutions of the current iteration and the previous one falls below a given tolerance
\eta=10^{-7}
. Once the iteration converges after
K
steps, we assign
\boldsymbol{u}_{s,h}^{j+1}=\boldsymbol{u}_{s,h}^{j+1,K}
and
p_{s,h}^{j+1}=p_{s,h}^{j+1,K}
.

7
Conclusion

In this paper, we have investigated the Navier-Stokes equations with random initial data in a
bounded polygonal domain
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
. We have developed a scheme for
approximating the expected value of the solution, by combining Galerkin FEM, truncated Karhunen-Loève
expansion of the log-normal initial random field, and quasi-Monte Carlo (QMC) method. Further, we
have derived a bound on the root-mean-square error, including the finite element error, the dimension
truncation error, and the error from the QMC quadrature. The numerical experiments show that
the scheme enjoys fast convergence with respect to the number of sampling points.
The present investigation indicates that the QMC methods have enormous potentials for solving nonlinear PDEs with uncertainties.

Theoretically, it is of interest to extend the analysis to a more general class of PDEs. Of particular interest are non-Newtonian fluid flow models, describing the motion of fluids with a more general structure
[
36
,
37
]
. The extension requires a new way to control the fully non-linear diffusion term, and a more refined analysis for the convective term. Of equal interest is to relax the smallness condition, which plays a crucial role in the overall analysis. Numerically the condition does not appear very critical for the empirical performance. From an algorithmic point of view, it is of much interest to consider the multilevel and/or changing dimension algorithms
[
22
,
33
,
39
,
40
]
, with the QMC algorithm applied to the PDE problems with random data
[
31
,
7
,
28
,
10
]
. Adapting these algorithms to the Navier–Stokes problem is an intriguing topic.

\|\boldsymbol{u}\|_{L^{4}(D)}\leq 2^{\frac{1}{4}}\|\boldsymbol{u}\|^{\frac{1}{2}}_{L^{2}(D)}\|\nabla\boldsymbol{u}\|^{\frac{1}{2}}_{L^{2}(D)},\quad\forall\boldsymbol{u}\in H^{1}_{0}(D)^{2}.

(A.1)

.

Lemma A.2
.

Let the non-negative numbers

and

,
b_{j}
,
c_{j}
,
\gamma_{j}
satisfy

a_{n}+k\sum^{n}_{j=0}b_{j}\leq k\sum^{n}_{j=0}\gamma_{j}a_{j}+k\sum^{n}_{j=0}c_{j}+B
, for all

If

for all

Theorem A.3
.

Let

[

,
12
]
.

Lemma A.4
.

Fix
m\in\mathbb{N}
,
\lambda>0
and
A_{i}
,
B_{i}>0
for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
. Then the quantity

\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}x^{\lambda}_{i}A_{i}\right)^{\frac{1}{\lambda}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{B_{i}}{x_{i}}\right)

(A.2)

is minimized over any sequences
(x_{i})_{1\leq i\leq m}
when

x_{i}=c\left({B_{i}/A_{i}}\right)^{\frac{1}{1+\lambda}}\quad{\rm{for}}\,\,{\rm{all}}\,\,c>0.

(A.3)

For

, the function (
A.2
) is minimized provided that

is defined by (
A.3
) for each

and it is finite if and only if
\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}(A_{i}B_{i}^{\lambda})^{1/(1+\lambda)}
converges.

Lemma A.5
.

Fix
A_{j}>0
for all
j\in\mathbb{N}
and
\sum_{j\geq 1}A_{j}<1
. Then we have

\sum_{|\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}|<\infty}|\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}|!\prod_{j\in\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}}A_{j}\leq\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\bigg(\sum_{j\geq 1}A_{j}\bigg)^{k}=\frac{1}{1-\sum_{j\geq 1}A_{j}}.

Furthermore, for any
B_{j}>0
with
\sum_{j\geq 1}B_{j}<\infty
, we also have

\sum_{|\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}|<\infty}\prod_{j\in\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}}B_{j}=\prod_{j\geq 1}(1+B_{j})=\exp\bigg(\sum_{j\geq 1}\log(1+B_{j})\bigg)\leq\exp\bigg(\sum_{j\geq 1}B_{j}\bigg).
