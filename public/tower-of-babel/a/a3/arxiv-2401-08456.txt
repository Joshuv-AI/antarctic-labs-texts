An elementary description of nef cone for irreducible holomorphic symplectic manifolds
Anastasia V. Vikulova

Abstract
. We describe MBM classes for irreducible holomorphic symplectic manifolds of K3 \mathrm{K3} and Kummer types. These classes are the monodromy images of extremal rational curves which give the faces of the nef cone of some birational model. We study the connection between our results and A. Bayer and E. Macrì’s theory. We apply the numerical method of description due to E. Amerik and M. Verbitsky in low dimensions to the K3 \mathrm{K3} type and Kummer type cases.



1.
Introduction

There is a great interest in studying ample cones in algebraic geometry in the context of minimal model program. Much had been done to find ample or Kähler cone of irreducible holomorphic symplectic manifolds. The simplest example of an irreducible holomorphic symplectic manifolds is a
\mathrm{K3}
surface. The self-intersection of smooth rational irreducible curves on
\mathrm{K3}
surfaces is
-2.
The Kähler cone of a
\mathrm{K3}
surface consists of the classes of type
(1,1)
in the second cohomologies with real coefficients, which have positive self-intersection and which positively intersect with
(-2)
-curves. The ample cone of a projective
\mathrm{K3}
surface is characterized in the same way in the Néron-Severi group with real coefficients. So orthogonal hyperplanes to the classes of square
-2
in the positive cone give the chamber decomposition of the positive cone, where the Kähler cone (or the ample cone) is one of the chambers (see, for example, the paper
[
20
, Corollary 3.4]
).

However, it is not so easy to describe the ample or Kähler cone for other irreducible holomorphic symplectic manifolds. The recent results of A. Bayer and E. Macrì use the difficult technique of the theory of Bridgeland stability conditions. There is a connection between wall-crossing for stability conditions and walls of the positive cone of a moduli space of
H
-Gieseker stable sheaves thanks to a natural morphism from the set of chambers to the Néron-Severi group, constructed by A. Bayer and E. Macrì in the paper
[
6
]
. This technique was applied to all known examples of holomorphic symplectic manifolds (see
[
7
]
,
[
10
]
,
[
24
]
,
[
32
]
). A. Bayer and E. Macrì described the chamber decomposition of the positive cone of the moduli space of semistable sheaves on a
\mathrm{K3}
surface with primitive Mukai vector. In fact, such a moduli space is deformation equivalent to the Hilbert scheme of points on a
\mathrm{K3}
surface. We mention two theorems from the papers
[
7
]
and
[
32
]
, which describe the ample cones of
\mathrm{K3}
and Kummer types manifolds.

Before we formulate the theorems we first introduce the following notations:

Definition 1.1
.

Let
X
be a smooth projective surface. Denote by
H^{*}_{\text{alg}}(X,\mathbb{Z})
the algebraic part of the ring of even cohomologies, i.e.

H^{*}_{\text{alg}}(X,\mathbb{Z})\,=\,H^{0}(X,\mathbb{Z})\oplus\mathrm{NS}(X)\oplus H^{4}(X,\mathbb{Z}).

For an element
v\in H^{*}_{\text{alg}}(X,\mathbb{Z})
denote by
v^{\perp}
the subspace of elements in
H^{*}_{\text{alg}}(S,\mathbb{Z})
which are orthogonal to
v
with respect to the Mukai pairing given by

((r_{1},\alpha_{1},s_{1}),(r_{2},\alpha_{2},s_{2}))\,=\,\alpha_{1}\cdot\alpha_{2}-r_{1}\cdot s_{2}-r_{2}\cdot s_{1},

where
(r_{i},\alpha_{i},s_{i})\in H^{*}_{\text{alg}}(X,\mathbb{Z})
with
r_{i}\in H^{0}(X,\mathbb{Z}),

\alpha_{i}\in\mathrm{NS}(X)
and
s_{i}\in H^{4}(X,\mathbb{Z}).

Theorem 1.2

(
[
7
, Theorem 12.1]
)
.

Let
S
be a
\mathrm{K3}
surface,
v\in H^{*}_{\text{alg}}(X,\mathbb{Z})
be a fixed primitive Mukai vector and

be the moduli space of
\sigma
-semistable sheaves on

with the Mukai vector

and the stability condition

. Consider the set

of elements

such that

and
0\leqslant(v,a)\leqslant\frac{v^{2}}{2}
with respect to the Mukai pairing, where the element

is identified with its image under the isometry

\theta:v^{\perp}\xrightarrow{\sim}\mathrm{NS}(M_{\sigma}(v))

which is defined in
[
33
, (1.6)]
. Then the set of
a^{\perp}
with
a\in W
defines a chamber decomposition of the positive cone of
M_{\sigma}(v).
The ample cone
\mathrm{Amp}(M_{\sigma}(v))
is one of the chambers:
\eta\in\mathrm{Amp}(M_{\sigma}(v))
if and only if
(\eta,a)>0
for all
a\in W.

Theorem 1.3

(
[
32
]
)
.

Let
A
be an abelian surface,
v\in H^{*}_{\text{alg}}(A,\mathbb{Z})
be a fixed primitive Mukai vector,

be the moduli space of
\sigma

\displaystyle M_{\sigma}(v)

\displaystyle\longrightarrow A\times A^{\vee}

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}

\displaystyle\longmapsto\left(\mathrm{Alb}(c_{2}(\mathcal{F})),\det(\mathcal{F})\right)

over zero with
A^{\vee}\,=\,\mathrm{Pic}^{0}(A),
where
\mathrm{Alb}:\mathrm{CH}_{0}(A)\to A
is a summation map. Consider the set
W
of elements
a\in H^{*}_{\text{alg}}(A,\mathbb{Z})
such that
a^{2}\geqslant 0
and
0<(v,a)\leqslant\frac{v^{2}}{2}
with respect to the Mukai pairing, where the element
a
is identified with its image under the isometry

\theta:v^{\perp}\xrightarrow{\sim}\mathrm{NS}(\mathrm{Kum}_{\sigma}(v))

which is defined in
[

. Then the set of

The ample cone
\mathrm{Amp}(\mathrm{Kum}_{\sigma}(v))

Remark 1.4

(cf.
[
8
,
21
]
)
.

If
S
is a
\mathrm{K3}
surface, then the Mukai vector
v=(1,0,0)
defines the moduli space
M_{\sigma}(v)\simeq S
for any
\sigma.
If
A
is a complex two-dimensional torus, then the Mukai vector
v=(1,0,-2)
defines the Kummer surface
\mathrm{Kum}_{\sigma}(v)
isomorphic to the blowup of the quotient of
A
by the natural involution for any
\sigma
.

For a
\mathrm{K3}
surface
S
and Mukai vector
v=(1,0,-n+1)
the moduli space
M_{\sigma}(v)
is isomorphic to the Hilbert scheme of
n
points
\mathrm{Hilb}^{n}(S)
for any
\sigma.
For a complex two-dimensional torus
A
and Mukai vector
v=(1,0,-n-1)
the variety
\mathrm{Kum}_{\sigma}(v)
is isomorphic to the Kummer variety
\mathrm{Kum}^{n}(A)
for any
\sigma.
Recall that the Kummer variety for the two-dimensional torus
A
is the fibre over
0\in A
of the summation map
\mathrm{Hilb}^{n+1}(A)\to A.

All these theorems work for projective surfaces. In the non-algebraic case the ample cone is empty. However, the Kähler cone does always exist for irreducible holomorphic symplectic manifolds and we have
Amp(X)\,=\,\mathcal{K}(X)\cap\mathrm{NS}(X)_{\mathbb{R}},
where
Amp(X)
is the ample cone,
\mathcal{K}(X)
is the Kähler cone and
\mathrm{NS}(X)
is the Néron-Severi group. The Kähler cone of irreducible holomorphic symplectic manifolds was studied by E. Amerik, D. Huybrechts, E. Markman, M. Verbitsky, and others (see, for instance,
[
2
,
17
,
22
]
).

The structure of the ample cone for the Hilbert scheme of points on
\mathrm{K3}
surface was originally conjectured in the papers of B. Hassett and Y. Tschinkel (for instance, see
[
18
]
). A refinement of their conjecture is established by A. Bayer and E. Macrì. In the paper of E. Amerik and M. Verbitsky
[
2
]
there is more elementary description of the boundary of the Kähler cone for
\mathrm{K3}
type manifolds (i.e. deformation of the Hilbert scheme of points on a
\mathrm{K3}
surface) for small dimensions. This means that they describe MBM classes, i.e. the classes which correspond to the extremal rays of the Mori cone on some deformation of the variety (see Section
2
). The point is that these classes are easy to characterise numerically without appealing to Bayer-Macrì’s machinery. In this paper we try to provide a similar elementary description of the Kähler cone for
\mathrm{K3}
and Kummer types varieties of arbitrary dimension which are deformations of the Hilbert scheme of points on a
\mathrm{K3}
surface or Kummer variety, respectively. Recall the definition.

Definition 1.5
.

Let
X
be a
2n
-dimensional IHSM. We call it a
\mathrm{K3}
type manifold if
X
is deformation equivalent to
\mathrm{Hilb}^{n}(S)
for some
\mathrm{K3}
surface
S.
We call it a Kummer type manifold if
X
is deformation equivalent to
\mathrm{Kum}^{n}(A)
for some complex two-dimensional torus
A.

So the goal of this paper is to give a simple and self-contained proof of the following theorems about the walls of the Kähler cone. Before we state these theorems, let us give some definitions. Let
X
be an IHSM. Let
\alpha\in H^{2}(X,\mathbb{Z})
be a primitive class. Let
q
be the Beauville-Bogomolov form on
X.
We call the number

d(\alpha)=\min\{d>0\mid q(\alpha,\beta)=d\;\text{for}\;\beta\in H^{2}(X,\mathbb{Z})\}

the
divisibility
of
\alpha.
By
\delta(\alpha)
we denote the image of
\alpha/d(\alpha)
by the natural homomorphism

H^{2}(X,\mathbb{Z})\otimes\mathbb{Q}\to H^{2}(X,\mathbb{Z})^{*}/H^{2}(X,\mathbb{Z}),

where the quotient
H^{2}(X,\mathbb{Z})^{*}/H^{2}(X,\mathbb{Z})
is the discriminant group (see Definition
3.1
).

Definition 1.6
.

Let
X=\mathrm{Hilb}^{n}(B)
(respectively,
X=\mathrm{Kum}^{n}(B)
) for a
\mathrm{K3}
surface
B
(respectively, for a complex two-dimensional torus
B
). Let
\Delta
be the exceptional divisor of the Hilbert–Chow morphism

\mathrm{Hilb}^{n}(B)\to\mathrm{Sym}^{n}(B).

Then we call the divisor
\Delta/2
(respectively,
\Delta\cap\mathrm{Kum}^{n-1}(B)/2
)
half of the exceptional divisor.

Theorem 1.7
.

Let
X
be an IHSM of
\mathrm{K3}
type of dimension
2n.
Let
\alpha\in H^{2}(X,\mathbb{Z})
be a primitive class and denote by
\widehat{\alpha}\in H_{2}(X,\mathbb{Z})
the dual homology class of
\alpha.
Denote by
q
the Beauville–Bogomolov form on the lattice
H^{2}(X,\mathbb{Z})
and its extension form on the dual lattice
H_{2}(X,\mathbb{Z})\hookrightarrow H^{2}(X,\mathbb{Z})\otimes\mathbb{Q}.
Let
d(\alpha)
be the divisibility of
\alpha
and
\delta(\alpha)
be the image of
\alpha/d(\alpha)
in the discriminant group of the lattice
H^{2}(X,\mathbb{Z}).
Then
\alpha
is MBM if and only if there is an integer

(1.1)

b\in[0,n-1]

such that

(1.2)

q(\widehat{\alpha})=2a-\frac{b^{2}}{2(n-1)},

where
a
is an integer number satisfied the inequalities

(1.3)

-2\leqslant 2a<\frac{b^{2}}{2(n-1)},

and
\pm\delta(\alpha)=b.
Moreover, the integer numbers
a
and
b,
satisfying (
1.1
), (
1.2
) and (
1.3
), uniquely determine the monodromy orbit of
\alpha.

In the proof of the theorem we obtain as a byproduct the following corollary:

Corollary 1.8
.

Under the conditions of Theorem
1.7
we have:

(1)

If
b=1
and
a=0,
then the cohomological class
\alpha
is the class of half of the exceptional divisor on some deformation
Y=\mathrm{Hilb}^{n}(S)
of
X
with Picard group

\mathrm{Pic}(Y)=\mathrm{Pic}(S)\oplus\mathbb{Z}e,

where
S
is a
\mathrm{K3}
surface and
e
is a class of half of exceptional divisor on
Y
.

(2)

If
(a,b)\neq(0,1),
then the cohomological class
\alpha
up to a rational multiple can be represented by

2(n-1)\cdot x-be

on some deformation
Y=\mathrm{Hilb}^{n}(S)
of
X
with Picard group

\mathrm{Pic}(Y)=\mathrm{Pic}(S)\oplus\mathbb{Z}e,

such that
\mathrm{Pic}(S)=\mathbb{Z}x,

x^{2}=2a\geqslant-2
and
e
is a class of half of the exceptional divisor on
Y.

.

Let
X
be an IHSM of Kummer type of dimension

(1.4)

Again, as in the

2.
Preliminaries

In this section we recall some theory about irreducible holomorphic symplectic manifolds.

Definition 2.1
.

A smooth compact Kähler manifold
X
is called
an irreducible holomorphic symplectic manifold
(or just
IHSM
) if it has trivial
\pi_{1}(X)
and

H^{0}(X,\Omega_{X}^{2})\simeq\mathbb{C}\sigma

with a non-degenerate holomorphic 2-form
\sigma
.

A few examples of such manifolds are known. Two series of examples were described by A. Beauville in
[
8
]
. They are the Hilbert scheme of
n
points on a
\mathrm{K3}
surface and the Kummer variety which is defined as follows.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
A
be a complex two-dimensional torus. Then the
Kummer variety
of dimension
2n
is the fibre over zero of the summation map

s:\mathrm{Hilb}^{n+1}(A)\to A.

We denote the Kummer variety of dimension
2n
by
\mathrm{Kum}^{n}(A).

Other two examples were found by O’Grady in
[
28
]
and
[
29
]
. All mentioned series of examples are not pairwise birational, because they have different second Betti numbers.

Beauville–Bogomolov form and Mukai lattice.

The IHS manifolds have an interesting feature which, in general, only surfaces have. If
X
is an IHSM, then there is a non-degenerate quadratic form
q
on the second integral cohomology
H^{2}(X,\mathbb{Z})
which is called
Beauville–Bogomolov form
. This form is of signature
(3,b_{2}(X)-3)
and its restriction on
H^{1,1}(X,\mathbb{R})
is of signature
(1,b_{2}(X)-3).
This form is uniquely up to multiplication by a non-zero constant.

Theorem 2.3

(
[
14
, Theorem 4.7]
and
[
17
, Proposition 23.14]
)
.

Let
X
be a IHSM of dimension
2n.
Denote by
q
the Beauville–Bogomolov form. Then there is a constant
c\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
depending only on the manifold
X
such that for
\alpha\in H^{2}(X,\mathbb{Z})
we get

(2.1)

\int_{X}\alpha^{2n}\,=\,cq(\alpha)^{n}.

In particular,
q
can be renormalized such that
q
is a primitive integral quadratic form on
H^{2}(X,\mathbb{Z}).

In fact, the primitive integral Beauville-Bogomolov form is uniquely defined by (
2.1
) and signature on
H^{1,1}(X,\mathbb{R}).
This means that the quadratic form
q
depends only on topology of the manifold (see
[
17
, Remark 23.15]
). Thus, it is invariant under the deformations. We always assume that the Beauville-Bogomolov form is primitive integral form on
H^{2}(X,\mathbb{Z}).

Remark 2.4
.

The second cohomology group of an IHSM equipped with Beauville–Bogomolov form is a lattice. We have the embedding
H_{2}(X,\mathbb{Z})\hookrightarrow H^{2}(X,\mathbb{Z})\otimes\mathbb{Q}
which is given by the lattice duality.

Let us give the description of the Beauville–Bogomolov form for the Hilbert scheme of points on a
\mathrm{K3}
surface and the Kummer variety. First of all, we recall the definition and some properties of the Mukai lattice, which will be extremely important below. Let

L\,=\,H^{0}(X,\mathbb{Z})\oplus H^{2}(X,\mathbb{Z})\oplus H^{4}(X,\mathbb{Z})

be the lattice of even cohomologies of a surface
X
with the quadratic form which is called Mukai pairing and given by

((r_{1},\alpha_{1},s_{1}),(r_{2},\alpha_{2},s_{2}))\,=\,\alpha_{1}\cdot\alpha_{2}-r_{1}\cdot s_{2}-r_{2}\cdot s_{1},

where
(r_{i},\alpha_{i},s_{i})\in L
such that
r_{i}\in H^{0}(X,\mathbb{Z}),

\alpha_{i}\in H^{2}(X,\mathbb{Z})
and
s_{i}\in H^{4}(X,\mathbb{Z}).
Let

H^{*}_{alg}(X,\mathbb{Z})\,=\,H^{0}(X,\mathbb{Z})\oplus\mathrm{NS}(X)\oplus H^{4}(X,\mathbb{Z}).

It has the following geometric meaning: for the Grothendieck group of the category of coherent sheaves
\mathcal{K}_{0}(X)
we have a surjective homomorphism

v:\mathcal{K}_{0}(X)\twoheadrightarrow H^{*}_{alg}(X)

which maps the class of a sheaf
\mathcal{F}
to the Mukai vector
v(\mathcal{F})\,=\,ch(\mathcal{F})\sqrt{td(X)},
where
ch(\mathcal{F})
is the Chern character and
td(X)
is the Todd class of tangent bundle of
X
. In the case of a
\mathrm{K3}
surface we get

v(\mathcal{F})\,=\,(rk(\mathcal{F}),c_{1}(\mathcal{F}),\chi(\mathcal{F})-rk(\mathcal{F}))

and for a complex two-dimensional torus we have

v(\mathcal{F})\,=\,(rk(\mathcal{F}),c_{1}(\mathcal{F}),\chi(\mathcal{F})),

where
rk(\mathcal{F})
is the rank,
c_{1}(\mathcal{F})
is the first Chern class and

\chi(\mathcal{F})\,=\,\sum_{i}(-1)^{i}\dim\mathrm{Ext}^{i}(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{F})

is the Euler characteristic.

The Hilbert scheme of
n
points on the
\mathrm{K3}
surface
S
is a moduli space of ideal sheaves of
n
points on
S
with respect to a stability condition
\sigma
. These are sheaves with the Mukai vector
v\,=\,(1,0,-n+1).
The orthogonal complement of
v
with respect to the Mukai quadratic form is isomorphic to the Néron-Severi group of
M_{\sigma}(v).
So we have

v^{\perp}\simeq\mathrm{NS}(S)\oplus\mathbb{Z}e,

where
e\,=\,(1,0,n-1)
and
e^{2}\,=\,-2(n-1).
This means that the restriction of the Beauville–Bogomolov form for
\mathrm{Hilb}^{n}(S)
on
\mathrm{NS}(S)
coincides with the intersection form on
\mathrm{NS}(S),
and
e
is orthogonal to
\mathrm{NS}(S).

The same description works for a complex two-dimensional torus
A
. In this case we consider the moduli space
M_{\sigma}(v)
for the Mukai vector
v\,=\,(1,0,-n-1).
Every sheaf
\mathcal{F}\in M_{\sigma}(v)
can be written as
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{L}\otimes I_{T},
where
\mathcal{L}\in\mathrm{Pic}^{0}(A)=A^{\vee}
and
T\in\mathrm{Hilb}^{n+1}(A).
The generalized Kummer variety
\mathrm{Kum}^{n}(A)
is the fibre over zero of the Albanese map

\displaystyle M_{\sigma}(v)

\displaystyle\longrightarrow A\times A^{\vee}

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}

\displaystyle\longmapsto\left(\mathrm{Alb}(T),\mathcal{L}\right)

where
\mathrm{Alb}:\mathrm{Hilb}^{n+1}(A)\to A
is a summation map. We have the isomorphism

v^{\perp}\simeq\mathrm{NS}(A)\oplus\mathbb{Z}e,

where
e=(1,0,n+1)
and
e^{2}=-2(n+1).
This means that the restriction of the Beauville–Bogomolov form for
\mathrm{Kum}^{n}(A)
on
\mathrm{NS}(A)
coincides with the intersection form on
\mathrm{NS}(A)
and
e
is orthogonal to
\mathrm{NS}(A).

The vector
e
has the following geometric description in both
\mathrm{K3}
and Kummer cases.

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
\Delta
be the exceptional divisor of the Hilbert–Chow morphism

\mathrm{Hilb}^{n}(B)\to\mathrm{Sym}^{n}(B),

where
B
is either
\mathrm{K3}
surface, or a complex two-dimensional torus. If
B
is a
\mathrm{K3}
surface and
e=(1,0,n-1),
then the class of
\Delta
is
2e.
If
B
is a complex two-dimensional torus and
e=(1,0,n+1),
then the class of
\Delta\cap\mathrm{Kum}^{n-1}(B)
is
2e.

Proof.

The proposition follows directly from
[
8
, Remark after Proposition 6]
for
\mathrm{K3}
case and
[

Definition 2.6
.

We denote by
\widehat{e}
the class of a general fibre on the exceptional divisor of the Hilbert–Chow morphism in

It satisfies the equation

Remark 2.7
.

Note, that if

(respectively,

) for a

surface (respectively, for a complex two-dimensional torus

\displaystyle H^{2}(X,\mathbb{Z})\simeq H^{2}(B,\mathbb{Z})\oplus\mathbb{Z}e;

The space of deformations.

Now we recall some definitions and facts of deformation theory.

Definition 2.8
.

Let
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
be complex manifolds. We say that they are
deformation equivalent
if there is a smooth proper holomorphic morphism
\mathcal{X}\to B
such that
B
is connected and there are two points
b_{1},b_{2}\in B
such that
\mathcal{X}_{b_{1}}\simeq X_{1}
and
\mathcal{X}_{b_{2}}\simeq X_{2}.
Here the variety
B
is called
a space of deformations.

Now we give the description of the deformation space of IHSM which will be convenient for us.

Definition 2.9
.

Let
X
be an IHSM,
\text{Diff}_{0}(X)
be a connected component of its diffeomorphism group,
Comp
be the space of complex structures of the Kähler type on
X.
Then
\text{Teich}\,=\,\text{Comp}/\text{Diff}_{0}(X)
is the
Teichmüller space
.

Note that the Teichmüller space is non-Hausdorff. However, there is an important result which is given in
[
19
]
.

Theorem 2.10

(
[
19
, Theorem 4.3]
)
.

Let
X
and
X^{\prime}
be IHSMs which are diffeomorphic. Assume that they are inseparable in the space
\mathrm{Teich}.
Then
X
and
X^{\prime}
are birational.

It is convenient to consider the
birational Teichmüller space

\text{Teich}_{b}=\text{Teich}/\sim,
where
X\sim X^{\prime}
if and only if they are inseparable in
\mathrm{Teich}.
The birational Teichmüller space is the Hausdorff reduction of the Teichmüller space. By Theorem
2.10
every point of
\mathrm{Teich}
over any
x\in\text{Teich}_{b}
corresponds to some birational model of
X.

Cones associated to an irreducible holomorphic symplectic manifold.

Now let us recall some facts about the positive cone for IHSM.

Definition 2.11
.

Let
X
be an IHSM. Both the set of
\alpha\in\mathrm{NS}(X)_{\mathbb{R}}
for projective
X
and the set of
\alpha\in H^{1,1}(X)_{\mathbb{R}}
for Kähler
X,
such that
q(\alpha)>0,
have two connected components. The positive cone is the component that contains ample or Kähler classes, respectively. We denote both of them by
\mathrm{Pos}(X)
when there is no confusion, otherwise we denote by
\mathrm{Pos}_{\text{amp}}(X)
and
\mathrm{Pos}_{\text{K\"{a}h}}(X)
the positive cones of ample and Kähler classes, respectively.

Remark 2.12
.

Note that
\mathrm{Pos}_{\text{amp}}(X)
and
\mathrm{Pos}_{\text{K\"{a}h}}(X)
are different, because in general the inclusion

\mathrm{NS}(X)_{\mathbb{R}}=H^{1,1}(X,\mathbb{Z})_{\mathbb{R}}\subset H^{1,1}(X,\mathbb{R})

is strict. However, the intersection of
\mathrm{Pos}_{\text{K\"{a}h}}(X)
with
\mathrm{NS}(X)_{\mathbb{R}}
gives us
\mathrm{Pos}_{\text{amp}}(X)
. This is the reason why we will mainly deal with the positive cone
\mathrm{Pos}_{\text{K\"{a}h}}(X).

Definition 2.13
.

Let
X
be a Kähler manifold. The Kähler cone

\mathcal{K}(X)\subset H^{1,1}(X,\mathbb{R})

is an open convex cone of Kähler classes.

Definition 2.14
.

Let
X
be a projective manifold. The ample cone

\mathrm{Amp}(X)\subset\mathrm{NS}(X)_{\mathbb{R}}

is the open cone of finite sums
\sum_{i}a_{i}L_{i},
where
a_{i}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
and
L_{i}
are ample classes in
\mathrm{NS}(X).
The nef cone
\mathrm{Nef}(X)\subset\mathrm{NS}(X)_{\mathbb{R}}
is the set of classes
\mathrm{NS}(X)_{\mathbb{R}}
such that their intersections with curves on
X
is non-negative.

The closure of the ample cone is the nef cone, i.e. we have

\mathrm{Amp}(X)=\mathrm{Int}\mathrm{Amp}(X)\subset\mathrm{Nef}(X)=\overline{\mathrm{Amp}(X)},

where
\mathrm{Int}\mathrm{Amp}(X)
is the interior of the ample cone of
X.
We have the following result.

Theorem 2.15

(
[
9
]
)
.

The Kähler cone of an IHSM
X
consists of all elements
\alpha
in
\mathrm{Pos}(X)
such that
q(\alpha,C)\,>\,0
for all rational curves
C
.

According to Remark
2.4
, one may view homology classes as cohomology classes with rational coefficients, so that
q(\alpha,C)
is a well-defined rational number. Theorem
2.15
is a generalization of the description of the Kähler cone for a
\mathrm{K3}
surface. In the two-dimensional case the positivity of the intersection with
(-2)
-curves defines ample or Kähler classes. However, in the higher-dimensional case the situation is more complicated. By Theorem
2.15
, the walls of the Kähler cone are given by the orthogonal elements to the classes of rational curves, but now the value of the quadratic form can be different on different rational curves.

Nevertheless, it is clear that for
\alpha\in\mathrm{Pos}(X)
to check that it lies in the Kähler cone we only need to check the positivity of
q(\alpha,C)
on rational curves
C
with negative square. Indeed, as Beauville–Bogomolov form has signature
(1,b_{2}-3)
on
(1,1)
-classes we get that the orthogonal hyperplane to a curve with non-negative square does not intersect the positive cone.

MBM classes.

A rational curve
C
is called
extremal
if its cohomological class
[C]
cannot be written as
[C]=Z_{1}+Z_{2},
where
Z_{1}
and
Z_{2}
are two non-proportional effective cycles in
\mathrm{NS}(X).
By Theorem
2.15
, the walls of the Kähler cone are given by the orthogonal hyperplanes to the extremal rational curves. It turns out that the chamber decomposition of the positive cone is given by the orthogonal hyperplane to the class with negative Beauville–Bogomolov square represented by a rational curve on some deformation of
X.
We recall some definitions and results about these classes.

Definition 2.16

(
[
23
, Definition 1.5]
)
.

Let
X
be an IHSM. An automorphism
g
of
H^{2}(X,\mathbb{Z})
is called a
monodromy operator
, if there exists a family

\pi:\mathcal{X}\to B

of IHSM such that
\psi:X\xrightarrow{\sim}\mathcal{X}_{b_{0}}
for some
b_{0}\in B
and
g
belongs to the image of
\pi_{1}(B,b_{0})
under the monodromy representation. In other words, the automorphism
g\in\mathrm{Aut}(H^{2}(X,\mathbb{Z}))
is a monodromy operator if there is a loop
\gamma:[0,1]\to B
such that
\gamma(0)=\gamma(1)=b_{0}
and the parallel transport in the local system
R^{2}\pi_{*}\mathbb{Z}
along
\gamma
induces the homomorphism

\psi_{*}\circ g\circ\psi^{*}:H^{2}(\mathcal{X}_{b_{0}},\mathbb{Z})\to H^{2}(\mathcal{X}_{b_{0}},\mathbb{Z}).

The monodromy group
\mathrm{Mon}(X)
is a subgroup of
\mathrm{Aut}(H^{2}(X,\mathbb{Z}))
generated by all monodromy operators. By
[
31
, Theorem 7.2]
the subgroup
\mathrm{Mon}(X)
coincides with the natural image of the mapping class group
\Gamma=\mathrm{Diff}(X)/\mathrm{Diff}_{0}(X)
in
\mathrm{Aut}(H^{2}(X,\mathbb{Z})),
where
\mathrm{Diff}(X)
is the group of diffeomorphisms of the manifold
X
and
\mathrm{Diff}_{0}(X)
is its connected component containing the identity, i.e. the group of isotopies.

Definition 2.17

(
[
1
, Definition 1.13]
)
.

A negative rational class

\alpha\in H^{1,1}(X,I)\cap H^{2}(X,\mathbb{Q})

on
(X,I)
is called
MBM
if for some
\gamma\in\mathrm{Mon}(X)
the subspace
\gamma(\alpha)^{\perp}\subset H^{1,1}(X,I)
contains a wall of the Kähler cone of a birational model
(X,I^{\prime})
of
(X,I).

By Remark
2.4
it makes sense to speak of
(1,1)
-classes represented by curves.

Definition 2.18
.

Let
X
be an IHSM and let us fix the Kähler class
\omega.
Let
U\subset X
be an irreducible uniruled subvariety. A rational curve
C\subset U
is called
minimal
if
\int_{C}\omega
is minimal among all curves in
U
passing through a general point of
U.

Definition 2.19
.

Let
X
be an IHSM. A rational curve
C\subset X
is called
MBM
if it is minimal and its cohomology class
[C]\in H^{2}(X,\mathbb{Z})
has negative Beauville–Bogomolov square.

There is a convenient criterion that allow to check the minimality of a rational curve on IHSM.

Theorem 2.20

(
[
1
, Corollaries 4.5 and 4.6]
and
[
4
, Remark 1.2]
)
.

Let
X
be an IHSM of dimension
2n
and let
C\subset X
be a rational curve. Then
C
is minimal if and only if
C
deforms in
(2n-2)
-parameter family.

However we will use the following criterion that allow to check the minimality of a rational curve on IHSM. First of all, recall the definition.

Definition 2.21
.

Let
Z
be a Kähler manifold covered by rational curves. Let

r\colon Z\dashrightarrow Q

be an almost holomorphic fibration such that its general fibre is rationally connected. Then the fibration
r
is called
the rational quotient
of
Z.

Theorem 2.22

(
[
1
, Theorem 4.4]
)
.

Let
X
be an IHSM and
C\subset X
be a rational curve. Let
Z
be an irreducible component of the locus covered by the deformations of
C
on
X.
Then
Z
is a coisotropic subvariety of
X.
The fibres of the rational quotient of the desingularization of
Z
have dimension equal to the codimension of
Z
in
X.

The following proposition gives the convenient description of MBM classes, which will be used in the classification of MBM classes on
\mathrm{K3}
and Kummer types manifolds (see Proposition 2.1 and the discussion after Proposition 2.2 in
[
3
]
).

Proposition 2.23
.

The class
\alpha\in H^{1,1}(X,I)\cap H^{2}(X,\mathbb{Q})
is the MBM class if and only if its dual class
\widehat{\alpha}\in H_{2}(X,\mathbb{Q})
is represented, up to rescaling, by a minimal rational curve on some deformation of
X.

Proposition 2.24

(
[
1
, Corollary 5.13]
)
.

A negative class
\alpha\in H^{2}(X,\mathbb{Z})
is MBM or not simultaneously in all complex structures, where it is of type (1,1).

Remark 2.25
.

By definition, an MBM class
\alpha
on an IHSM
X
up to a sign is represented by a curve on a sufficiently general deformation
X^{\prime}
of
X.
This means that it must be represented by a possibly reducible curve on all deformations.

Definition 2.26
.

Let
X
be an IHSM. Let
C
be an MBM curve and let
B
be the space of all deformations of
C.
We call the
MBM locus
of
C
the union of all curves parametrized by
B.

Definition 2.27
.

Let
X
be an IHSM. Let
\alpha\in H^{2}(X,\mathbb{Z})
be an MBM class. Then we call the
MBM locus
of the class
\alpha
the union of all MBM loci of MBM curves
C
such that
[C]
is proportional to
\alpha.

Remark 2.28
.

From Theorems
2.20
and
2.22
and
[
4
, Remark 1.2]
we get that to prove the minimality of the rational curve
C
in IHSM
X
one should find MBM locus
Z
of
C
and prove that it is a rational quotient such that its fibres of the desingularization of
Z
have dimension equal to the codimension of
Z
in
X
and the base of this fibration is not unirational.

Now we recall the theorem which states that MBM loci on the deformation equivalent IHSM are real analytic isomorphic.

Theorem 2.29

(
[
4
, Theorem 1.8]
)
.

Let
X
be an IHSM of
\mathrm{K3}
or Kummer type and let
\alpha\in H^{2}(X,\mathbb{Q})
be MBM and
T
be the MBM locus on
X
of the class
\alpha.
Let
X^{\prime}
be a deformation of

in
\mathrm{Teich}
such that
\alpha
is
(1,1)
and is the class of a minimal rational curve on both

Let

be an MBM locus on

of

Then

and

are real analytic isomorphic.

Now we recall the definition of the period map and the theorem about chamber decomposition of the positive cone.

Definition 2.30
.

Let
X
be an IHSM. Denote by
Per
the map

\mathrm{Per}:\mathrm{Teich}\to\mathbb{P}H^{2}(X,\mathbb{C})

such that
\text{Per}(I)=H^{2,0}(X,I)\in\mathbb{P}H^{2}(X,\mathbb{C})
for a complex structure
I\in\text{Teich}.
The image of
Per
is

\mathbb{P}\text{er}=\{l\in\mathbb{P}H^{2}(X,\mathbb{C})\mid q(l)=0,\;q(l+\bar{l})>0\}.

Definition 2.31
.

Let
\alpha
be an MBM class. The space
\text{Teich}_{\alpha}
consists of complex structures
I
such that
\alpha
is of type (1,1) with respect to
I.
We have

\text{Teich}_{\alpha}=\text{Per}^{-1}(\alpha^{\perp}).

Theorem 2.32

(
[
1
, Theorem 1.19]
,
[
22
, Theorem 1.4]
)
.

Let
X
be an IHSM and
I
be its complex structure. Then the points in
\textup{Per}^{-1}\left(\textup{Per}(I)\right)
are in one-to-one correspondence with the Kähler chambers, i.e. the connected components of

\mathcal{BK}_{X}=\mathrm{Pos}(X)\setminus\bigcup_{z\in\textup{MBM classes}}z^{\perp}.

This means that the connected components (or chambers) of
\mathrm{Pos}(X)
are Kähler (or ample) cones of
X
and its birational models, as well as their monodromy images (see
[
2
]
and other papers of the authors). Here we use the following theorem:

Theorem 2.33

(
[
22
, Theorem 1.3]
)
.

Let
X
and
Y
be IHSM which are deformation equivalent. Then they are bimeromorphic if and only if there is a parallel transport operator
f:H^{2}(X,\mathbb{Z})\to H^{2}(Y,\mathbb{Z})
which is an isomorphism of integral Hodge structures.

So our main goal is to find numerical characterization for these MBM classes for Beauville examples. For more details about the Teichmüller space see
[
2
, Section 3]
.

Torelli theorem.

For IHSM there is a generalization of Global Torelli Theorem for
\mathrm{K3}
surfaces, namely

Theorem 2.34

(
[
31
, Theorem 1.18]
)
.

For each connected component of the birational Teichmüller space
\mathrm{Teich}_{b}^{0},
the period map
\textup{Per}:\textup{Teich}_{b}^{0}\to\mathbb{P}\textup{er}
is a diffeomorphism.

Corollary 2.35
.

Let
(X,I)
be an IHSM with a complex structure
I.
Consider the lattice
L=H^{2}(X,\mathbb{Z})
equipped with the Beauville–Bogomolov form. Suppose that

\alpha\in L\cap H^{1,1}(X,I)

is a negative vector, i.e.
q(\alpha)<0,
and
\alpha\in L^{\prime}\subset L,
where
L^{\prime}
is a primitive sublattice of
L
and
(L^{\prime\perp})_{\mathbb{R}}
contains a positive
2
-plane. Then there is a deformation
(X,I^{\prime})
of
(X,I)
in
\mathrm{Teich}_{\alpha}
such that
\mathrm{Pic}(X,I^{\prime})=L^{\prime}.

Proof.

Let
M
be a positive
2
-plane in
L^{\prime\perp}.
It is not hard to see that we can find a vector
l\in M\otimes\mathbb{C}
such that
q(l)=0
and
q(l+\bar{l})>0.
By Theorem
2.34
there is a complex structure
I^{\prime}\in\mathrm{Teich}_{\alpha}
such that

\mathrm{Per}(I^{\prime})=l\in\mathbb{P}H^{2}(X,\mathbb{C}).

If such a positive
2
-plane
M
is generic, then

L\cap H^{1,1}(X,I^{\prime})=L^{\prime},

but as
\mathrm{Pic}(X,I^{\prime})=L\cap H^{1,1}(X,I^{\prime}),
we get the desired.

∎

Corollary 2.36
.

Let
(X,I)
be a
\mathrm{K3}
(respectively, Kummer) type manifold and let

L=H^{2}(X,\mathbb{Z})

be the lattice equipped with the Beauville–Bogomolov form. Suppose that there is a primitive
\alpha\in L
such that
\alpha\in H^{1,1}(X,I)
and
q(\alpha)<0.
Then the manifold
X
can be deformed to
Y
in
\textup{Teich}_{\alpha}
such that
Y=\mathrm{Hilb}^{n}(S)
(respectively,
Y=\mathrm{Kum}^{n}(A)
), where
S
is a
\mathrm{K3}
surface with cyclic Picard group (respectively,
A
is a complex two-dimensional torus with cyclic Néron-Severi group).

Proof.

Let
e
be a class of half of the exceptional divisor on some deformation
Y
of
X
which is the Hilbert scheme of a
\mathrm{K3}
surface (respectively, Kummer manifold). Note that the lattice
\langle\alpha,e\rangle
is not positive definite, since both
q(\alpha)
and
q(e)
are negative. Thus, applying Corollary
2.35
to the lattice
\langle\alpha,e\rangle
we obtain a deformation
Y^{\prime}
of
X,
such that

\mathrm{Pic}(Y^{\prime})=\langle\alpha,e\rangle.

Such a manifold
Y^{\prime}
has the same period point in
\mathbb{P}\mathrm{er}
as the Hilbert scheme of points on a
\mathrm{K3}
surface (respectively, the Kummer variety). Indeed, the period points of Hilbert schemes (respectively, Kummer varieties) in
\mathbb{P}\mathrm{er}
form the union of the orthogonal hyperplanes to
e
and to its monodromy images. Hence
Y^{\prime}
is unseparable from Hilbert scheme
Y=\mathrm{Hilb}^{n}(S)
for some
\mathrm{K3}
surface
S
(respectively, unseparable from Kummer variety
Y=\mathrm{Kum}^{n}(A)
for some complex two-dimensional torus
A
) in the Teichmüller space. In other words, we can deform
Y^{\prime}
to
Y
which is the Hilbert scheme of points on a
\mathrm{K3}
surface (respectively, the Kummer variety) such that
Y
and
Y^{\prime}
have the same Picard group.

∎

3.
Lattices of
\mathrm{K3}
and Kummer types manifolds

In this section we collect some basic facts about lattices.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
L
be a lattice and
L^{\vee}
be its dual lattice, i.e.

L^{\vee}=\{l^{*}\in L\otimes\mathbb{Q}\mid(l^{*},l)\in\mathbb{Z}\;\text{for all}\;l\in L\}.

Then the quotient group
L^{\vee}/L
is called the discriminant group. For an element
l\in L
denote by
d(l)
the divisibility of
l,
i.e. the positive integer number such that

(l,L)=d(l)\mathbb{Z}.

For
l\in L
denote by
\delta(l)
the image of
l/d(l)
in the discriminant group
L^{\vee}/L.

Definition 3.2
.

Let
L
be a lattice. If
\alpha\in L
is a primitive element, then the element

\widehat{\alpha}=\frac{\alpha}{d(\alpha)}\in L^{\vee}

is called the dual element to
\alpha.

Example 3.3
.

The lattice of the second cohomology group for
\mathrm{K3}
type manifolds of dimension
2n
is
L=U^{\oplus 3}\oplus E(-8)^{\oplus 2}\oplus\langle-2(n-1)\rangle.
Thus, we have

L^{\vee}/L\simeq\mathbb{Z}/{2(n-1)}\mathbb{Z}.

Example 3.4
.

Similarly, the lattice of the second cohomology group for Kummer type manifolds of dimension
2n
is
L=U^{\oplus 3}\oplus\langle-2(n+1)\rangle.
Thus, we have

L^{\vee}/L\simeq\mathbb{Z}/{2(n+1)}\mathbb{Z}.

Recall that the lattice
(L,q)
of the second cohomology of IHSM with Beauville–Bogomolov form is the indefinite lattice of signature
(3,b_{2}-3),
where
b_{2}
is the second Betti number.

Denote by
O(L^{\vee}/L)
the subgroup of
\mathrm{Aut}(L^{\vee}/L)
consisting of multiplication by all elements of
t\in L^{\vee}/L
(viewed as
\mathbb{Z}/2k\mathbb{Z}
for natural number
k
) such that
t^{2}=1.
Let
\mathrm{Gr}^{+}(L)
be the Grassmannian parametrizing maximal positive definite subspaces in
L_{\mathbb{R}}
and let
\mathrm{Gr}^{+,\mathrm{or}}(L)
be the oriented Grassmannian parametrizing oriented maximal positive definite subspaces in
L_{\mathbb{R}}.
Note that
\mathrm{Gr}^{+,\mathrm{or}}(L)
is a double cover of
\mathrm{Gr}^{+}(L).
For the Grassmannian
\mathrm{Gr}^{+}(L)
we have the following proposition.

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
(L,q)
be an indefinite lattice. Then
\mathrm{Gr}^{+}(L),
parametrizing maximal positive definite subspaces in
L_{\mathbb{R}},
is contractible.

Proof.

Let
L_{\mathbb{R}}=V_{+}\oplus V_{-}
be a decomposition of the vector space
L_{\mathbb{R}}
in the sum of vector subspaces such that
q|_{V_{+}}
is positive definite and
q|_{V_{-}}
is negative definite. Assume that
V_{+}\perp V_{-}
with respect to
q.
Let
U\subset L_{\mathbb{R}}
be a maximal positive subspace. Then
U
can be seen as a graph of some linear map
V_{+}\to V_{-}.
Indeed, the map
f:U\to V_{+}\times V_{-}
can be defined as

U\ni u\mapsto(\mathrm{pr}_{+}(u),\mathrm{pr}_{-}(u)),

where
\mathrm{pr}_{+}
is a projection to the subspace
V_{+}
and
\mathrm{pr}_{-}
is a projection to the subspace
V_{-}.
Obviously, the image is isomorphic to
V_{+},
because the kernel of the composition
\mathrm{pr}_{+}\circ f
is zero, since
U\cap V_{-}=0
and
\dim U=\dim V_{+}.

So let
U
be the graph of the linear map
\phi.
Then
U
is positive definite if and only if for any non-zero
v\in V_{+}
we get

(3.1)

q(v+\phi(v))>0.

As (
3.1
) holds for any
t\phi,
where
t\in[0,1],
we obtain the contraction of
\mathrm{Gr}^{+}(L).

∎

Therefore, since
L
is indefinite, by Proposition
3.5
we get that
\mathrm{Gr}^{+}(L)
is contractible and, thus, the Grassmannian
\mathrm{Gr}^{+,\mathrm{or}}(L)
has two connected components. Denote by
O^{+}(L)
the subgroup of
O(L)
acting trivially on
\pi_{0}\left(\mathrm{Gr}^{+,\mathrm{or}}(L)\right)
and by
SO^{+}(L)
the subgroup of
O^{+}(L)
which consists of the elements with determinant equal to
1.
Let

\widetilde{O(L)}\,=\,\{g\in O(L)\mid g(l^{*})\equiv l^{*}\mod L\quad\text{for all}\;l^{*}\in L^{\vee}\}

and let
\widetilde{SO^{+}(L)}
be the subgroup of
\widetilde{O(L)}
which consists of the element with determinant equal to
1
and acting trivially on
\pi_{0}\left(\mathrm{Gr}^{+,\mathrm{or}}(L)\right).

Now let us recall a basic theorem about lattices, which we shall use next.

Theorem 3.6

(
[
16
, Lemma 3.5]
and
[
27
, Lemma 2.6]
)
.

Let
L
be a lattice containing two orthogonal isotropic planes. Then the
\widetilde{SO^{+}(L)}
-orbit of a primitive vector
l\in L
is uniquely determined by two invariants:
q(l)
and
\delta(l).

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
L=H^{2}(X,\mathbb{Z})
be a lattice of second cohomologies with Beauville–Bogomolov form, where
X
is a
\mathrm{K3}
or Kummer type manifold. Then the monodromy orbit of a primitive vector
l\in L
is uniquely determined by two invariants:
q(l)
and
\pm\delta(l).

Proof.

First of all, let us consider
\mathrm{K3}
type case. Recall that by Example
3.3
the lattice of the second cohomology is

L=U^{\oplus 3}\oplus E(-8)^{\oplus 2}\oplus\langle-2(n-1)\rangle.

Consider

\pi:O^{+}(L)\to O(L^{\vee}/L),

Then, according to
[
22
, Lemma 9.2]
, the group
\mathrm{Mon}(X)
is equal to
\pi^{-1}(\{1,-1\}).
Let
m,l\in L
lie in the same monodromy orbit. This means that there is
\gamma\in\mathrm{Mon}(X)
such that
m=\gamma(l).
Therefore, by the definition of
\gamma
we get that
q(l)=q(\gamma(l))
and
\delta(l)=\pm\delta(\gamma(l)).

In the opposite direction, assume that there are primitive elements
l
and
m
which satisfy

q(m)=q(l)\quad\text{and}\quad\delta(m)=\pm\delta(l).

Then if
\delta(m)=\delta(l),
by Theorem
3.6
there is
\gamma\in\widetilde{SO^{+}(L)}
such that
m=\gamma(l)
and we are done. Otherwise, if
\delta(m)=-\delta(l),
then
\delta(m)=\delta\left(\mathrm{R}_{e}(l)\right)
and by Theorem
3.6
there is
\gamma\in\widetilde{SO^{+}(L)}
such that
m=\gamma(\mathrm{R}_{e}(l))
and we are done.

Now let us consider the Kummer case. For Kummer variety the lattice is equal to
L=U^{\oplus 3}\oplus\langle-t\rangle.
Let
\pi:O^{+}(L)\to O(L^{\vee}/L).
According to
[
26
, Theorem 2.3]
the monodromy group consists of two types of elements, namely,

\pi^{-1}(1)\cap SO^{+}(L)\quad\text{and}\quad\pi^{-1}(-1)\cap(O^{+}(L)\setminus SO^{+}(L)).

Let
m,l\in L
lie in the same monodromy orbit. This means that there is
\gamma\in\mathrm{Mon}(X)
such that
m=\gamma(l).
Again by the definition of
\gamma,
it is obvious that

q(l)=q(\gamma(l))\;\;\text{and}\;\;\delta(l)=\pm\delta(\gamma(l)).

In the opposite direction, assume that there are primitive elements
l
and
m
which satisfy

q(m)=q(l)\quad\text{and}\quad\delta(m)=\pm\delta(l).

Then if
\delta(m)=\delta(l),
by Theorem
3.6
there is
\gamma\in\widetilde{SO^{+}(L)}
such that
m=\gamma(l)
and we are done. Otherwise, if
\delta(m)=-\delta(l),
then
\delta(m)=\delta\left(\mathrm{R}_{e}(l)\right)
and by Theorem
3.6
there is
\gamma\in\widetilde{SO^{+}(L)}
such that
m=\gamma(\mathrm{R}_{e}(l))
and we are done, because

\gamma\mathrm{R}_{e}\in O^{+}(L)\setminus SO^{+}(L)\quad\text{and}\quad\det\left(\gamma(\mathrm{R}_{e}(l))\right)=1.

∎

4.
Description of nef cone for
\mathrm{K3}
type manifolds

In this section we prove Theorem
1.7
. Let
X
is a
\mathrm{K3}
type manifold of dimension
2n.
Denote by
e\in H^{2}(X,\mathbb{Z})
the class of half of the exceptional divisor on a deformation of
X
which is the Hilbert scheme of
n
points on some
\mathrm{K3}
surface. We identify
H^{2}(X,\mathbb{Z})
and
H^{2}(Y,\mathbb{Z})
for any deformation
Y
of
X
in
\mathrm{Teich},
since topologically they are the same. Half of the exceptional divisors on a deformation of
X
which is a Hilbert scheme of points on a
\mathrm{K3}
surface is then
\gamma(e)
for some
\gamma\in\mathrm{Mon}(X).
By
\hat{e}\,=\,\frac{e}{2(n-1)}\in H_{2}(X,\mathbb{Z})
we denote the dual class of
e.
First of all, we need the following lemma (cf.
[
12
, Lemma 2.1 and Remark 2.2]
).

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
S
be a
\mathrm{K3}
surface. Let
C\subset S
be a smooth curve of genus
g.
Assume that there is a linear system
g^{1}_{k}
on
C
with
1\leqslant k\leqslant n
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}.
Then there is a map
f\colon C\to\mathrm{Hilb}^{n}(S)
such that its image is a rational curve
R
which is numerically equivalent to
C-(g-1+k)\hat{e}
in
\mathrm{N}_{1}(\mathrm{Hilb}^{n}(S),\mathbb{Z}).

Proof.

The lemma follows from the Riemann-Hurwitz formula. The map
f
is induced by a linear system
g^{1}_{k}
on
C
and is given by

C\stackrel{{\scriptstyle g^{1}_{k}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathbb{P}^{1}\subset\mathrm{Sym}^{k}(C)\hookrightarrow\mathrm{Sym}^{k}(S)\hookrightarrow\mathrm{Sym}^{n}(S),

where the last inclusion
\mathrm{Sym}^{k}(S)\hookrightarrow\mathrm{Sym}^{n}(S)
is given by

\mathrm{Sym}^{k}(S)\ni x_{1}+\ldots+x_{k}\mapsto x_{1}+\ldots+x_{k}+y_{k+1}+\ldots+y_{n}\in\mathrm{Sym}^{n}(S)

for
y_{k+1},\ldots,y_{n}\in S
which are distinct fixed points out of
C.
The ramification points are exactly the intersection of the rational curve
f(C)
with the exceptional divisor. Let us write
R\equiv C-m\hat{e}
with

C\in N_{1}(S)\hookrightarrow N_{1}(\mathrm{Hilb}^{n}(S))

and
m\geqslant 0.
We have
q(e,\hat{e})\,=\,-1,
thus, we get

m\,=\,q(R,e)\,=\,\frac{1}{2}q(R,\Delta),

where
\Delta\,=\,2e
is a class of the diagonal in
\mathrm{Sym}^{n}(S).
At the same time, we get

\frac{1}{2}q(R,\Delta)\,=\,\frac{1}{2}2(g-1+k)\,=\,(g-1+k),

where the first equality is true by Riemann-Hurwitz formula.

∎

We have the following important property which will be very useful for proving the minimality of curve in the process of proving Theorem
1.7
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
M=\mathrm{Hilb}^{n}(S)
for a
\mathrm{K3}
surface
S
such that
\mathrm{Pic}(S)=\langle x\rangle
and let
C\subset S
be a smooth curve of genus
g
which corresponds to the class
x\in H^{2}(S,\mathbb{Z}).
Let
t=2(n-1).
Let
e\in H^{1,1}(M,\mathbb{Z})
be a class of half of the exceptional divisor on
M.
Assume that
\beta=rx-be\in H^{1,1}(M,\mathbb{Z})
is a primitive class such that
r\in\mathbb{N},

(4.1)

b\in[0,\frac{r}{2}]\quad\text{is an integer number,}

and

(4.2)

q(\beta)<0.

Then
k=\frac{bt}{r}-g+1>2g-2.

Proof.

If
g=0
or
1,
we are done since
\frac{br}{t}>0.
So assume that
g\geqslant 2.
By (
4.2
) we obtain

(4.3)

\sqrt{\frac{2g-2}{t}}<\frac{b}{r}.

So by (
4.1
) we get

(4.4)

\sqrt{\frac{t}{2g-2}}>2.

By (
4.3
) and (
4.4
) we have the following inequalities

k=\frac{bt}{r}-g+1>\sqrt{\frac{2g-2}{t}}-g+1=(2g-2)\left(\sqrt{\frac{t}{2g-2}}-\frac{1}{2}\right)>2g-2.

∎

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
M=\mathrm{Hilb}^{n}(S)
for a
\mathrm{K3}
surface
S
such that
\mathrm{Pic}(S)=\langle x\rangle
and let
C\subset S
be a smooth curve of genus
g
which corresponds to the class
x\in H^{2}(S,\mathbb{Z}).
Let
t=2(n-1).
Let
e\in H^{1,1}(M,\mathbb{Z})
be a class of half of the exceptional divisor on
M.
Assume that
\beta=rx-be\in H^{1,1}(M,\mathbb{Z})
is a primitive class such that
r
divides
t,

b\in[0,\frac{r}{2}]
is an integer number, and
q(\beta)<0.
Then there is a rational curve
R\subset M
corresponds to the class
\beta.
Moreover, the curve
R
varies in the variety
\mathcal{Z}\subset M
which is a rational quotient such that dimension of its fibres is equal to codimension of
\mathcal{Z}
in
M.

Proof.

Let us consider
\widehat{\beta}=x-\frac{b}{r}e\in H_{2}(X,\mathbb{Q}).
Let
m=\frac{t}{r}
We have

\widehat{\beta}=x-\frac{mb}{t}e\equiv C-mb\hat{e}.

Let us prove that there is a linear system
g^{1}_{k}
on
C
such that

(4.5)

k=mb-g+1=\frac{bt}{r}-g+1

and
k\leqslant n.
The inequality is clear, because this means that

\frac{bt}{r}-g+1\leqslant\frac{t}{2}+1=n

or, equivalently,
\frac{b}{r}\leqslant\frac{1}{2}+\frac{g}{t}
which is true according to the conditions on
b.

By Lemma
4.2
we get

(4.6)

k>2g-2.

If we prove that the gonality of
C
is less than or equal to
k,
then by Lemma
4.1
we get that the linear system
g^{1}_{k}
gives a rational curve
R
on
M.
To do this we need to use the inequality for the gonality in
[
15
, Chapter 2, page 261]
. In other words, we have to prove that

\left\lfloor\frac{g+1}{2}\right\rfloor+1\leqslant k.

For
g\geqslant 2
this immediately follows from Lemma
4.2
. For
g=0
it is enough to prove that
k\geqslant 1.
Form (
4.5
) it is enough to prove that
\frac{bt}{r}+1\geqslant 1,
which is true. For
g=1
it is enough to prove that
k\geqslant 2.
Form (
4.5
) it is enough to prove that
\frac{bt}{r}\geqslant 1,
which is true. This means that there is a rational curve
R
which corresponds to the class
\beta.

Now let us find the MBM locus
\mathcal{Z}
of the curve
R.
Assume that the genus of the curve
C
is
g>0.
By (
4.6
) we get that
\mathrm{Sym}^{k}(C)
is a projective bundle over the Jacobian
J(C)
of
C.
Also by Hirzebruch-Riemann-Roch theorem we get that

\dim H^{0}(S,\mathcal{O}_{S}(C))=g+1

and so
\mathbb{P}^{g}
parametrizes curves of genus
g
on the surface
S.
Thus, we have the fibration over
\mathbb{P}^{g}
with the Jacobians as the fibres. In other words, we get the fibration

(4.7)

j\colon\mathcal{J}\to\mathbb{P}^{g},

such that the fibre of
j
over a point
D\in\mathbb{P}^{g},
where
D\subset S
is a curve of genus
g,
is equal to the Jacobian of
D.
Let

\phi\colon\mathcal{P}\to\mathcal{J}

be a fibration such that the fibre over a point
J\in\mathcal{J}
is
\mathbb{P}^{k-g}
which gives us the fibration

\mathrm{Sym}^{k}(D)\xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}^{k-g}}J(D)

for a curve
D\in\mathbb{P}H^{0}(S,\mathcal{O}_{S}(C))^{\vee}
of genus
g.
The completion of
\mathcal{Z}
is
\mathcal{P}\times\mathrm{Sym}^{n-k}(S).
Note that the fibration (
4.7
) is called the Beauville-Mukai system and it is known that
\mathcal{J}
is deformation equivalent to
\mathrm{Hilb}^{g}(S)
(see
[
30
, Section 2.3]
). In other words, the desingularization of
\mathcal{Z}
is deformation equivalent to
\mathbb{P}^{k-g}
-bundle over IHSM. The dimension of
\mathcal{Z}
is
2n-2k+g+g+k-g=2n-k+g.
The dimension of the fibre of its rational quotient is equal to
k-g
which is the codimension of
\mathcal{Z}
in
M.

If the genus of
C
is
g=0,
then the MBM locus
\mathcal{Z}
of
R
is

\mathrm{Sym}^{k}(C)\times\mathrm{Sym}^{n-k}(S)\simeq\mathbb{P}^{k}\times\mathrm{Sym}^{n-k}(S).

Its dimension is equal to
2n-k
and the dimension of the fibre of its rational quotient is equal to the codimension of
\mathcal{Z}
in
M.
This completes the proof.

∎

Now we are ready to prove Theorem

.

Proof of Theorem

.

Let
\alpha
be a primitive element in

First of all, assume that the class
\alpha\in H^{2}(X,\mathbb{Z})

\mathbb{P}^{1}
-bundle. Therefore, the class
\alpha

\mathrm{Hilb}^{n}(S)
with

\mathrm{Pic}(\mathrm{Hilb}^{n}(S))\,=\,\langle e\rangle,

does not have curves. We have

q(\widehat{e})=-\frac{1}{2(n-1)}=q(\widehat{\alpha}).

Now assume that
\alpha
and
e
are not in the same monodromy orbit. We divide the proof into several steps.

Step 1.

In this step we recall that there is a deformation
Y

within
\textup{Teich}_{\alpha}

\mathrm{K3}

for a
\mathrm{K3}

\mathrm{Pic}(S^{\prime})=\langle y\rangle=e^{\perp}\;\text{in}\;\langle\alpha,e\rangle.

Step 2.

In this step we deform

to

\textup{Teich}_{\alpha}
such that

and

in a certain way.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let

Assume that

\widetilde{\alpha}\,=\,ry^{\prime}+ce\quad\text{and}\quad{\mathchoice{\widetilde{\hbox{$\displaystyle\widetilde{\alpha}$}}}{\widetilde{\hbox{$\textstyle\widetilde{\alpha}$}}}{\widetilde{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\widetilde{\alpha}$}}}{\widetilde{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\widetilde{\alpha}$}}}}\,=\,rx+(c-lr)e

Step 3.

In this step we give necessary condition for

\mathcal{Z}

2n+2-2k+2+g+g-2-2+k-g=2n-k+g.

The dimension of the fibre of its rational quotient is equal to
k-g
which is the codimension of the MBM locus in
M.

Assume that
g=1.
Then from the exact sequence

0\to\mathcal{O}_{A}\to\mathcal{O}_{A}(C)\to\mathcal{O}_{C}(C)\to 0

we get that
\dim H^{0}(A,\mathcal{O}_{A}(C))
is either
1,
or
2.
If it is equal to
2,
we get that
A
is an elliptic fibre over
\mathbb{P}^{1},
which is impossible. So we get that

\dim H^{0}(A,\mathcal{O}_{A}(C))=1.

This means that the completion of
\mathcal{Z}
is a product of a fibre over zero of the summation map
\mathrm{Sym}^{k}(C)\to C
and
\mathrm{Sym}^{n+1-k}(A).
In other words, the completion of
\mathcal{Z}
is isomorphic to

\mathbb{P}^{k-1}\times\mathrm{Sym}^{n+1-k}(A).

Note that in this case
C
is a subgroup in
A.
So one does not need to take into account the translations of
C,
since these parameters are in the component
\mathrm{Sym}^{n+1-k}(A)
in this case. The dimension of
\mathcal{Z}
is equal to
2n-k+1
and the dimension of the fibre of its rational quotient is equal to the codimension of
\mathcal{Z}
in
M.
This completes the proof.

∎
