Dimension-Preserving Reductions Between SVP and CVP in Different p p -Norms
Divesh Aggarwal, Yanlin Chen, Rajendra Kumar, Zeyong Li, Noah Stephens-Davidowitz

Abstract
We show a number of reductions between the Shortest Vector Problem and the Closest Vector Problem over lattices in different ℓ p \ell_{p} norms ( SVP p \textsf{SVP}_{p} and CVP p \textsf{CVP}_{p} respectively). Specifically, we present the following 2 ε ​ m 2^{\varepsilon m} -time reductions for 1 ≤ p ≤ q ≤ ∞ 1\leq p\leq q\leq\infty , which all increase the rank n n and dimension m m of the input lattice by at most one: • a reduction from O ~ ​ ( 1 / ε 1 / p ) ​ γ \widetilde{O}(1/\varepsilon^{1/p})\gamma -approximate SVP q \textsf{SVP}_{q} to γ \gamma -approximate SVP p \textsf{SVP}_{p} ; • a reduction from O ~ ​ ( 1 / ε 1 / p ) ​ γ \widetilde{O}(1/\varepsilon^{1/p})\gamma -approximate CVP p \textsf{CVP}_{p} to γ \gamma -approximate CVP q \textsf{CVP}_{q} ; and • a reduction from O ~ ​ ( 1 / ε 1 + 1 / p ) \widetilde{O}(1/\varepsilon^{1+1/p}) - CVP q \textsf{CVP}_{q} to ( 1 + ε ) (1+\varepsilon) -unique SVP p \textsf{SVP}_{p} (which in turn trivially reduces to ( 1 + ε ) (1+\varepsilon) -approximate SVP p \textsf{SVP}_{p} ). The last reduction is interesting even in the case p = q p=q . In particular, this special case subsumes much prior work adapting 2 O ⁡ ( m ) 2^{O(m)} -time SVP p \textsf{SVP}_{p} algorithms to solve O ⁡ ( 1 ) O(1) -approximate CVP p \textsf{CVP}_{p} . In the (important) special case when p = q p=q , 1 ≤ p ≤ 2 1\leq p\leq 2 , and the SVP p \textsf{SVP}_{p} oracle is exact, we show a stronger reduction, from O ⁡ ( 1 / ε 1 / p ) ​ - CVP p O(1/\varepsilon^{1/p})\text{-}\textsf{CVP}_{p} to (exact) SVP p \textsf{SVP}_{p} in 2 ε ​ m 2^{\varepsilon m} time. For example, taking ε = log ⁡ m / m \varepsilon=\log m/m and p = 2 p=2 gives a slight improvement over Kannan’s celebrated polynomial-time reduction from m ​ - CVP 2 \sqrt{m}\text{-}\textsf{CVP}_{2} to SVP 2 \textsf{SVP}_{2} . We also note that the last two reductions can be combined to give a reduction from approximate- CVP p \textsf{CVP}_{p} to SVP q \textsf{SVP}_{q} for any p p and q q , regardless of whether p ≤ q p\leq q or p > q p>q . Our techniques combine those from the recent breakthrough work of Eisenbrand and Venzin [ EV20 ] (which showed how to adapt the current fastest known algorithm for these problems in the ℓ 2 \ell_{2} norm to all ℓ p \ell_{p} norms) together with sparsification-based techniques.

A lattice

The two most important computational problem on lattices are the Shortest Vector Problem (

Given a basis for a lattice

1.1
Our results and techniques

Our results are summarized in Figure

[
EV20
]
(though our presentation differs quite a bit from theirs). We also note that all of our reductions are randomized, and they all either preserve the rank and dimension of the input lattice exactly or increase each by exactly one. (We sometimes informally refer to reductions that increase the rank and dimension by one as “dimension- and rank-preserving,” since for algorithmic purposes the distinction between maintaining these values exactly and increasing them by one is unimportant.)

SVP to SVP.

Our first main result shows that
O(1)\text{-}\textsf{SVP}_{p}
does in fact reduce to
O(1)\text{-}\textsf{SVP}_{2}
for all
p\geq 2
(including
p=\infty
), in time
2^{\varepsilon m}
. The running time of
2^{\varepsilon m}
of course makes this a non-standard reduction. But, in the most interesting settings, the fastest known algorithms run in time
2^{\Omega(n)}=2^{\Omega(m)}
(and we have some complexity-theoretic evidence suggesting that
2^{o(m)}
-time algorithms are impossible, as well as many cryptographic constructions that rely on the assumption that our algorithms cannot be improved by much), so that this type of non-standard reduction is “almost as good as a polynomial-time reduction.”

In fact, our reduction is significantly more general. We reduce
O(\gamma)\text{-}\textsf{SVP}_{q}
to
\gamma\text{-}\textsf{SVP}_{p}
for any
1\leq p\leq q\leq\infty
in time
2^{\varepsilon m}
, and more generally still, we show a smooth tradeoff between the running time of the reduction and the approximation factor. This shows in a very strong sense that
\textsf{SVP}_{q}
is no harder than
\textsf{SVP}_{p}
(up to a constant in the approximation factor).

Theorem 1.1

(Informal, see Theorem
3.2
)
.

For
p\leq q
, there is a
2^{\varepsilon m}
-time dimension- and rank-preserving reduction from
\gamma^{\prime}\text{-}\textsf{SVP}_{q}
to
\gamma\text{-}\textsf{SVP}_{p}
, where

\gamma^{\prime}=\widetilde{O}(1/\varepsilon^{1/p})\cdot\gamma\;.

To prove this result, we combine (slight generalizations of) the techniques from
[
EV20
]
together with a technique called lattice sparsification (originally due to Khot
[
Kho05
]
), which is a method of sampling a randomly chosen “sparsified” sublattice of a lattice.

We first describe the relevant ideas from Eisenbrand and Venzin.
[
EV20
]
observe that the
\textsf{SVP}_{2}
subprocedure that they use actually outputs a list of
2^{\Omega(m)}
lattice vectors that all lie inside the smallest
\ell_{2}
ball that contains a shortest non-zero vector in the
\ell_{q}
norm. Furthermore, this list is sampled from a distribution satisfying a certain technical non-degeneracy condition. For our purposes, it suffices to think of this non-degeneracy condition as follows: either (1) a shortest vector in the
\ell_{q}
norm is likely to be in their list of vectors (in which case we are done); or (2) the list is likely to contain many distinct lattice vectors inside this
\ell_{2}
ball. For
q\geq 2
, Eisenbrand and Venzin then use a covering argument to show that any list of
2^{\varepsilon m}
distinct vectors in this
\ell_{2}
ball must contain a pair of distinct vectors that lie in an appropriately small
\ell_{q}
ball. (We note that this covering argument is reminiscent of the elegant
M
-ellipsoid technique in
[
DPV11
]
.) This immediately yields an algorithm, by checking all pairwise differences of the vectors in the list and outputting the result with the smallest (non-zero)
\ell_{q}
norm.

The main technical contribution behind our result can be seen as a method for converting
any

\gamma\text{-}\textsf{SVP}_{p}
oracle into an oracle that samples a list of lattice vectors with appropriately short
\ell_{p}
norms with a distribution satisfying a similar non-degeneracy condition. Combining this with a simple extension of the
[
EV20
]
covering argument to all
\ell_{p}
norms immediately yields the above result.

As an important special case, notice that vectors sampled independently from the uniform distribution over lattice vectors inside an appropriate
\ell_{p}
ball certainly satisfy this condition.
4
4

4

To see this, notice that if the number of vectors in the ball is smaller than
N
, then
N
samples will likely be enough to find any fixed vector in the ball—including the shortest vector in the
\ell_{q}
norm. But, if there are many vectors in this ball, then
N
independent samples from this distribution are likely to contain many distinct vectors.

[
Ste16a
]
showed that the shortest vector in an appropriately sparsified lattice is more-or-less a uniformly random (primitive) lattice vector in a ball, so that an
exact

\textsf{SVP}_{p}
oracle can be used to sample a nearly uniformly random (primitive) lattice vector from an
\ell_{p}
ball of any radius—by simply calling the oracle on an appropriately sparsified sublattice. So, it is not
too
hard to use the ideas from
[
EV20
]
and
[
Ste16a
]
to show that
O_{\varepsilon}(1)\text{-}\textsf{SVP}_{q}
reduces to
exact

\textsf{SVP}_{p}
in
2^{\varepsilon m}
time.

To make this idea work with an
approximate

\textsf{SVP}_{p}
oracle is rather delicate because, unlike an exact
\textsf{SVP}_{p}
oracle, an approximate oracle will typically have a choice between many different vectors for its output. In spite of this, we show that the lattice vector returned by the approximate
\textsf{SVP}_{p}
over a randomly sparsified sublattice satisfies essentially the same non-degeneracy conditions. Specifically, we show (using ideas from
[
Ste16a
]
) that the only vectors that the oracle can choose with “unexpectedly high” probability are vectors that are integer multiples of some shortest vector
\boldsymbol{v}
, which are as good as the shortest vector from our perspective. On the other hand, if there does not exist a vector that is output with “unexpectedly high” probability, then the list of vectors sampled contains many distinct lattice vectors inside the
\ell_{p}
ball.

CVP to SVP

Our second main result is a reduction from constant-factor approximate
\textsf{CVP}_{p}
to
\textsf{SVP}_{q}
for any
p,q\in[1,\infty]
in time
2^{\varepsilon m}
. In fact, this reduction works with a
(1+\varepsilon)
-approximate
\textsf{SVP}_{q}
oracle as well, and even with a
(1+\varepsilon)
-unique
\textsf{SVP}_{p}
oracle. Unique
\textsf{SVP}_{p}
is a potentially easier variant of
\textsf{SVP}_{p}
, in which we are promised that there is only one solution (up to sign) to the
(1+\varepsilon)\text{-}\textsf{SVP}_{p}
instance. (For example unique
\textsf{SVP}_{p}
is no harder than
\mathsf{GapSVP}_{p}
, the decision variant of
\textsf{SVP}_{p}
, under an efficient rank-, dimension-, and approximation-factor-preserving reduction
[
LM09
]
. And,
(1+\varepsilon)
-unique SVP is not even known to be NP-hard for constant
\varepsilon
.)

Theorem 1.2

(Informal, see Corollaries
4.5
and
4.8
)
.

For any
p,q
, there is a
2^{O(\varepsilon m)}
-time reduction from
\gamma
-
\textsf{CVP}_{q}
to
(1+\varepsilon)
-unique
\textsf{SVP}_{p}
, where

\gamma=\widetilde{O}(1/\varepsilon^{1+1/\min\{p,q\}})\;.

The reduction only calls its
SVP
oracle on lattices with rank
n+1
and dimension
m+1
, where
n
and

are the rank and dimension of the input lattice respectively.

Again, our main technical tools are lattice sparsification and (different) ideas introduced by Eisenbrand and Venzin
[
EV20
]
. In more detail, many
\textsf{CVP}_{p}
algorithms start with the observation that one can use an
\textsf{SVP}_{p}
oracle to find a lattice vector that is close to
k\boldsymbol{t}
for some small integer
k
by calling the
\textsf{SVP}_{p}
oracle on the lattice “with
\boldsymbol{t}
embedded in it,” i.e., the lattice generated by

\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{B}&-\boldsymbol{t}\\
0&s\end{pmatrix}\;.

(1)

Notice that short lattice vectors of the form
(\boldsymbol{v}-k\boldsymbol{t},ks)
in this new lattice correspond to lattice vectors close to
k\boldsymbol{t}
in the lattice generated by
\mathbf{B}
. (This idea is originally due to Kannan
[
Kan87
]
and is often referred to as Kannan’s embedding.)

A lot of work on
\textsf{CVP}_{p}
algorithms therefore naturally focuses on ways to force the algorithm to yield a solution with
k=1
. (E.g., this is how
[
AKS02
]
showed how to reduce
O(1)
-
\textsf{CVP}_{2}
to uniformly sampling short lattice vectors in time
2^{O(n)}
, and it is what we do in Section
5
.)

Eisenbrand and Venzin observed that it suffices to simultaneously find a lattice vector close to
k\boldsymbol{t}
and a lattice vector close to
(k-1)\boldsymbol{t}
. By triangle inequality, the difference of these two vectors will be close to
\boldsymbol{t}
.
5
5

5

As far as the authors know,
[
EV20
]
was the first work to use the very natural idea, even in the case when
p=q
.
In fact, when one is reducing
\textsf{CVP}_{q}
to
\textsf{SVP}_{p}
for
p\neq q
, this method even seems preferable, since the fact that this technique outputs the difference of vectors that are close in the
\ell_{p}
norm allows
[
EV20
]
to apply the ideas that we described above (i.e., the covering argument and the idea of non-degenerate distributions) to this setting.

With this in mind, the high-level idea behind our reduction is quite simple: we use Kannan’s embedding as in Eq. (
1
) (with a carefully chosen
s
), sample many random short lattice vectors from the resulting lattice using our
\textsf{SVP}_{p}
oracle and sparsification, and look for pairs of vectors whose difference is of the form
(\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{t},s)
with small
\ell_{q}
norm.

However, many subtleties arise here, even when reducing to exact
\textsf{SVP}_{p}
. For example, if
\boldsymbol{x}
is in the sparsified lattice, so are
-\boldsymbol{x},\pm 2\boldsymbol{x},\pm 3\boldsymbol{x},\ldots,
. So, the event that
\boldsymbol{x}
is in the sparsified lattice is of course not independent of the event that
2\boldsymbol{x}
is in the sparsified lattice. These correlations are not an issue when trying to solve
SVP
, since it never makes sense to output
k\boldsymbol{x}
for
|k|\neq 1
as a solution to
SVP
anyway. But, to make the above technique work, we might actually prefer, e.g.,
2\boldsymbol{x}
to
\boldsymbol{x}
, and we therefore must account for these correlations. We do so by using yet another sparsification-based technique—this one from
[
DRS14
]
—which allows us to limit the number of integer multiples of
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{L}
that can lie in an appropriately sized
\ell_{p}
ball.

A more difficult issue is that it no longer suffices (as it did in the case of
\textsf{SVP}_{p}
) to show that there is some small
\ell_{q}
ball that contains many vectors. Instead, we must show that there are many vectors close to
k\boldsymbol{t}
(in the
\ell_{p}
norm) and many vectors close to
(k-1)\boldsymbol{t}
, whose pairwise differences are close to
\boldsymbol{t}
in the
\ell_{q}
norm. This requires us to relate the number of vectors in two different groups, those close to
k\boldsymbol{t}
and those close
(k-1)\boldsymbol{t}
(and to worry about the
\ell_{q}
distance to
\boldsymbol{t}
of their difference). To do so, we observe that, simply by triangle inequality, each lattice vector at distance
r
from
k\boldsymbol{t}
naturally corresponds to a lattice vector at distance
r^{\prime}=r+\mathrm{dist}(\boldsymbol{t},\mathcal{L})
from
(k-1)\boldsymbol{t}
. We then very carefully choose parameters to argue that we can find a suitable not-too-large radius
r
and choice of
k
for which the number of vectors at distance
r
from
(k-1)\boldsymbol{t}
is not much smaller than the number of vectors at distance
r^{\prime}
from
(k-1)\boldsymbol{t}
. More specifically, we must choose our radius
r
so that the total number of vectors in the embedded lattice generated by Eq. (
1
) that have
\ell_{p}
norm at most
r
is at most
2^{\varepsilon m}
times the number of vectors with norm
r^{\prime}
. A packing argument shows that such a radius
r
at which the number of lattice points “grows slowly” in this way must exist with
r\lesssim\mathrm{dist}(\boldsymbol{t},\mathcal{L})/\varepsilon
.

Finally, in order to make our reduction work with an approximate (unique)
\textsf{SVP}_{p}
oracle, we show (using ideas from
[
Ste16b
]
) that the solution to the sparsified
(1+\varepsilon)\text{-}\textsf{SVP}_{p}
instance will be unique with probability roughly
2^{-\varepsilon m}
if (and only if) the number of lattice points “grows slowly” at
r
. So, conveniently, this slow-growing property proves to be exactly what we need to resolve two different issues.

Intuitively, we cannot hope to work with a
\gamma\text{-}\textsf{SVP}_{p}
oracle for larger values of
\gamma
because, e.g., our oracle might only output vectors of the form
(\boldsymbol{v}-2k\boldsymbol{t},2ks)
. In contrast, Eisenbrand and Venzin are able to use a specific
\gamma\text{-}\textsf{SVP}_{2}

algorithm
(rather than a generic oracle) for large constant
\gamma
by taking advantage of specific properties of the algorithm that prevent this from happening.

CVP to SVP for
p=q
.

Our reduction from
\textsf{CVP}_{p}
to
\textsf{SVP}_{q}
is even interesting in the case when
p=q
and when the
SVP
oracle is exact. Indeed, many works (such as
[
AKS02
,
BN07
,
ADRS15
]
) have shown how to adapt specific
2^{Cm}
-time algorithms for
\textsf{SVP}_{p}
to work for constant-factor-approximate
\textsf{CVP}_{p}
with the same or nearly the same running time. Our result shows that this can be done generically, up to a factor of
2^{\varepsilon m}
in the running time. Given this importance, we study this special case separately and prove the following theorem, which gives quantitatively stronger results than Theorem
1.2
for
1\leq p\leq 2
when the
\textsf{SVP}_{p}
oracle is exact. In particular, plugging in
p=2
and
\varepsilon=\log m/m
strictly improves on Kannan’s celebrated polynomial-time reduction from
\sqrt{m}\text{-}\textsf{CVP}_{2}
to
\textsf{SVP}_{2}
.

Theorem 1.3

(Informal, see Theorem
5.1
)
.

For
1\leq p\leq 2
, there is a
2^{\varepsilon m}
-time reduction from
\gamma\text{-}\textsf{CVP}_{p}
to (exact)
\textsf{SVP}_{p}
, where

\gamma=O(1/\varepsilon^{1/p})\;.

The reduction calls its
\textsf{SVP}_{p}
oracle on lattices with rank
n+1
and dimension
m+1
, where
n
and
m
are the rank and dimension of the input lattice respectively.

Behind this theorem are generalizations of discrete-Gaussian-based techniques from
[
ADRS15
]
and sparsification techniques from
[
Ste16a
]
. Specifically, we show how to use sparsification and an (exact)
\textsf{SVP}_{p}
oracle to sample from an
\ell_{p}
analogue of a Gaussian distribution over a lattice, which was shown in
[
Ste16a
]
for the case
p=2
(and sketched for the more general case). We then show that
2^{\varepsilon m}
such samples from the embedded lattice discussed above (with the width of the distribution chosen appropriately) suffices to solve
O(1/\varepsilon^{1/p})\text{-}\textsf{CVP}_{p}
, which was shown for the case
p=2
and a specific choice of
\varepsilon
in
[
ADRS15
]
. This can also be seen as a variant of the original
[
AKS02
]

2^{O(n)}
-time reduction from
O(1)\text{-}\textsf{CVP}_{2}
to the problem of sampling nearly uniformly from lattice points with bounded norm (and Blömer and Naewe’s generalization to
\ell_{p}
norms
[
BN07
]
), in which we use sparsification to do the random sampling and choose our radius very carefully (using the oracle).

The result only holds for
1\leq p\leq 2
for a rather technical reason: the
\ell_{p}
analogue
e^{-\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{p}^{p}}
of the Gaussian is a positive definite function if and only if
1\leq p\leq 2
. It is unclear whether this issue is inherent.

CVP to CVP

Our final main result is a reduction between
CVP
in different norms. Specifically, we reduce
O_{\varepsilon}(\gamma)\text{-}\textsf{CVP}_{p}
to
\gamma\text{-}\textsf{CVP}_{q}
in
2^{\varepsilon m}
time, for
p\leq q
. (Notice that our
SVP
reduction went from
q
to
p
, i.e., from big to small, while our
CVP
reduction goes from
p
to
q
, or from small to big.)

Theorem 1.4

(Informal, see Theorem
4.7
)
.

For
1\leq p\leq q\leq\infty
, there is a dimension- and rank-preserving
2^{\varepsilon m}
-time reduction from
\gamma^{\prime}\text{-}\textsf{CVP}_{p}
to
\gamma\text{-}\textsf{CVP}_{p}
, where

\gamma^{\prime}=\widetilde{O}(1/\varepsilon^{1/p})\cdot\gamma\;.

The reduction behind Theorem
1.4
uses a very simple and natural idea that, to the authors’ knowledge, was also first published in
[
EV20
]
. The idea is to randomly perturb the input target point
\boldsymbol{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
to
\boldsymbol{t}^{\prime}
and then to call our
\textsf{CVP}_{q}
oracle on the perturbed point. One needs to choose the method of perturbation so that with probability
2^{-\varepsilon m}
, the perturbed target will be very close in
\ell_{q}
norm to a closest lattice vector to
\boldsymbol{t}
in the
\ell_{p}
norm. Though they do not describe it this way, the algorithm in
[
EV20
]
for
p<2
can already be viewed as such a reduction for
q=2
. Therefore, our contribution here is largely the extension to arbitrary
q
, as well as a simpler reduction and analysis.

Preliminaries

2.1
Notation

We use
\real,\mathbb{Z},\nat
to denote the real numbers, integers and natural numbers respectively. For any natural number
m
, we use
[m]
to denote the set
\{1,2,\ldots,m\}
. For any
p\in[1,\infty)
, the
\ell_{p}
norm on
\real^{m}
is defined as

\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{p}=\left(\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert x_{i}\right\rvert}^{p}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}.

The
\ell_{\infty}
norm is defined as
\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{\infty}=\mathrm{max}_{i\in[m]}\{x_{i}\}
. For any
1\leq p\leq\infty
, we use
\mathcal{B}_{p}^{m}
to denote the closed unit ball in
\ell_{p}
norm in
\real^{m}
i.e.

\mathcal{B}_{p}^{m}=\{\boldsymbol{x}\in\real^{m}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\;\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{p}\leq 1\}.

For a matrix
\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
with rank
n
, we abuse notation and write
\mathbf{B}^{-1}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=(\mathbf{B}^{T}\mathbf{B})^{-1}\mathbf{B}^{T}
for the left inverse of
\mathbf{B}
. In particular,
\mathbf{B}^{-1}\mathbf{B}\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{x}
for all
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

2.2
Lattices

For any set of
n
linearly independent vectors
\mathbf{B}=\{\boldsymbol{b_{1}},\ldots,\boldsymbol{b_{n}}\}
from
\real^{m}
, the lattice
\mathcal{L}
generated by basis
\mathbf{B}
is

\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{B})=\left\{\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}z_{i}\boldsymbol{b_{i}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}z_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}\right\}.

We call
n
the
rank
of the lattice
\mathcal{L}
and
m
the
dimension
. The vectors
\mathbf{B}=\{\boldsymbol{b}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{b}_{n}\}
forms a
basis
of the lattice. Given a basis
\mathbf{B}
, we use
\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{B})
to denote the lattice generated by
\mathbf{B}
.
If
n=m
, we say the lattice
\mathcal{L}
is
full rank
. We define the length of the shortest non-zero vector under
\ell_{p}
norm by

\lambda_{1}^{(p)}(\mathcal{L})=\underset{{\boldsymbol{x\in\mathcal{L}\setminus\{\boldsymbol{0}\}}}}{\mathrm{min}}\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{p}.

For any
\boldsymbol{t}\in\real^{n}
, we define the distance to the closet lattice vector under
\ell_{p}
norm by

\mathrm{dist}_{p}(\boldsymbol{t,\mathcal{L}})=\underset{{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{L}}}{\mathrm{min}}\;\|\boldsymbol{x-t}\|_{p}.

We also write

\lambda_{2}^{(p)}(\mathcal{L})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\min\{r\ \mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\ \dim(\mathrm{span}(\mathcal{L}\cap\,r{\mathcal{B}}_{p}^{m}))\geq 2\}\;.

(I.e.,
\lambda_{2}^{(p)}(\mathcal{L})
is the minimal length of a vector that is linearly independent from at least one shortest vector.)

A vector
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathcal{L}
is a
non-primitive
lattice vector if there exists an
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{L}
and a
z>1
such that
\boldsymbol{v}=z\boldsymbol{x}
. Otherwise it is
primitive
. For any lattice
\mathcal{L}
, we use
\mathcal{L}^{prim}
to denote the set of all the primitive lattice vectors in
\mathcal{L}
.

Claim 2.1
.

For any lattice
\mathcal{L}\subset\real^{m}
, radius
r>0
, and set
{\mathcal{S}}\subseteq\mathcal{L}_{\neq\boldsymbol{0}}\cap\,r\mathcal{B}_{p}^{m}
, then

|{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}|\geq\frac{\lambda_{1}^{(p)}(\mathcal{L})}{r}|{\mathcal{S}}|

where
{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathcal{L}^{prim}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\exists k\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0},k\boldsymbol{v}\in{\mathcal{S}}\}
.

Proof.

For any primitive vector
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathcal{L}^{prim}
, the number of vectors in
\mathcal{L}\cap\,r\mathcal{B}_{p}^{m}
that are a positive integer multiple of
\boldsymbol{v}
is at most
\frac{r}{\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{p}}\leq\frac{r}{\lambda_{1}^{(p)}(\mathcal{L})}
. The claim follows.
∎

For any
p\geq 1
, set of vectors
\mathcal{A}
, target vector
\boldsymbol{t}
and radius
r>0
, we use
N_{p}(\mathcal{A},r,\boldsymbol{t})
to denote the number of vector in set
\mathcal{A}
whose
\ell_{p}
norm is at most
r
.

N_{p}(\mathcal{A},r,\boldsymbol{t})=|\{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{A}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\|\boldsymbol{x-t}\|_{p}\leq r\}|\;.

We omit the parameter
\boldsymbol{t}
, when
\boldsymbol{t}=\boldsymbol{0}
.

Lemma 2.2

(
[
Ste16a
]
, Corollary 2.3)
.

For any lattice
\mathcal{L}\subset\mathbb{Q}^{m}
, with basis
(\boldsymbol{b}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{b}_{n})
,
\boldsymbol{t}\in\mathbb{Q}^{m}
and radius
r>0
, let
\ell
be the bound on the
bit length of the
\boldsymbol{b}_{i}

for all
i
in the natural representation of rational numbers. Then,

|(\mathcal{L}-\boldsymbol{t})\cap\,r\mathcal{B}_{p}^{m}|\leq 1+(2+r)^{\mathrm{poly}(m,\ell)}\;.

We will also need the following simple claim.

Lemma 2.3
.

For any lattice
\mathcal{L}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
(for
n\geq 2
) and
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{L}_{\neq\boldsymbol{0}}
, let
\pi(\mathcal{L})
be the lattice obtained by projecting
\mathcal{L}
orthogonal to
\boldsymbol{x}
. Then,

\lambda_{1}^{(2)}(\pi(\mathcal{L}))\geq\frac{3}{4}\cdot\frac{\lambda_{1}^{(2)}(\mathcal{L})^{2}}{\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{2}}

Proof.

Let
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathcal{L}
such that
\pi(\boldsymbol{y})\neq\boldsymbol{0}
, and let
\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})
be the lattice generated by
\boldsymbol{x}
and
\boldsymbol{y}
, which has determinant
\det(\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}))=\|\pi(\boldsymbol{y})\|\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{2}
. It is, however, well known that any two-dimensional lattice
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
satisfies
\lambda_{1}^{(2)}(\mathcal{L}^{\prime})^{2}\leq 4/3\cdot\det(\mathcal{L}^{\prime})
.
Therefore,

\lambda_{1}^{(2)}(\mathcal{L})^{2}\leq\lambda_{1}^{(2)}(\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}))^{2}\leq\frac{4}{3}\cdot\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{2}\|\pi(\boldsymbol{y})\|_{2}\;.

Rearranging shows that
\|\pi(\boldsymbol{y})\|_{2}\geq 3\lambda_{1}^{(2)}(\mathcal{L})^{2}/(4\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{2})
, as needed.
∎

2.3
The discrete supergaussian distributions

For any
\ell_{p}
norm, we define the function
f_{p}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\real^{m}\mapsto\real
as
f_{p}(\boldsymbol{x})=\exp(-\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{p}^{p})
, which is also known as a
supergaussian
. For a discrete set
\mathcal{A}\subset\real^{m}
, we define
f_{p}(\mathcal{A})=\sum\limits_{\boldsymbol{x}\in A}f_{p}(\boldsymbol{x})
.

Definition 2.4
.

For a
n
-rank lattice
\mathcal{L}\subset\real^{m}
, we define
D_{\mathcal{L},p}
as the probability distribution over
\mathcal{L}
such that probability of drawing
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{L}
is proportional to
f_{p}(\boldsymbol{x})
:

\underset{X\sim D_{\mathcal{L},p}}{Pr}[X=\boldsymbol{x}]=\frac{f_{p}(\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{x}})}{f_{p}(\mathcal{L})}.

We will need the following tail bound on
D_{\mathcal{L},p}
for
1\leq p\leq 2
, proven by
[
MS19
]
. It is a generalization of Banaszczyk’s celebrated result for
p=2
.

Lemma 2.5

(Lemma 3.11,
[
MS19
]
)
.

For any lattice
\mathcal{L}\subset\real^{m}
,
0<p\leq 2
and
a\geq 1
,

\sum\limits_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{L},\,\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{p}\geq a(m/p)^{1/p}}f_{p}(\boldsymbol{x})\leq(ea^{p}e^{-a^{p}})^{m/p}\cdot f_{p}(\mathcal{L})\;.

We will also need the following lemma. The special case of
p=2
is again a celebrated inequality due to Banaszczyk
[
Ban93
]
.

Lemma 2.6
.

For any
1\leq p\leq 2
, any lattice
\mathcal{L}\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
, and any
\boldsymbol{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
,

f_{p}(\boldsymbol{t})f_{p}(\mathcal{L})\leq f_{p}(\mathcal{L}+\boldsymbol{t})\leq f_{p}(\mathcal{L})\;.

Proof.

The upper bound follows from the fact that
f_{p}
is a positive-definite function. (See, e.g.,
[
MS19
]
.)
For the lower bound, we have

f_{p}(\mathcal{L}+\boldsymbol{t})=f_{p}(\mathcal{L}+\boldsymbol{t})/2+f_{p}(\mathcal{L}-\boldsymbol{t})/2=\sum_{\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathcal{L}}(e^{-\|\boldsymbol{v}+\boldsymbol{t}\|_{p}^{p}}/2+e^{-\|\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{t}\|_{p}^{p}}/2)\;.

Then,

e^{-\|\boldsymbol{v}+\boldsymbol{t}\|_{p}^{p}}/2+e^{-\|\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{t}\|_{p}^{p}}/2=e^{-\|\boldsymbol{v}+\boldsymbol{t}\|_{p}^{p}/2-\|\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{t}\|_{p}^{p}/2}\cosh(\|\boldsymbol{v}+\boldsymbol{t}\|_{p}^{p}/2-\|\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{t}\|_{p}^{p}/2)\geq e^{-\|\boldsymbol{v}+\boldsymbol{t}\|_{p}^{p}/2-\|\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{t}\|_{p}^{p}/2}\;.

It therefore suffices to prove that
|v+t|^{p}/2+|v-t|^{p}/2\leq|v|^{p}+|t|^{p}

for all
v,t\in\mathbb{R}
.

We may assume without loss of generality that
0\leq t\leq v
.
Then, the necessary inequality follows from the fact that for such
v
and
t
,

\displaystyle(v+t)^{p}/2+(v-t)^{p}/2-v^{p}

\displaystyle=t^{p}\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{t^{2i-p}}{v^{2i-p}}\cdot\frac{(p)_{2i}}{(2i)!}

\displaystyle\leq t^{p}\;.

Here, the equality is the Taylor series around
t=0
(which converges for
|t|\leq v
), using the notation
(a)_{i}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=a(a-1)\cdots(a-i+1)
. The inequality follows from direct computation, or it can be derived from the fact that the worst case is clearly the case when
t=v
, in which case the result is trivial.
∎

3
A reduction from

We first show our reduction from
\textsf{SVP}_{p}

\textsf{SVP}_{q}
for

Lemma 3.1
.

For any
q\geq p\geq 1
, and
\alpha\geq e
,
m^{1/p-1/q}\mathcal{B}_{p}^{m}
can be covered by
(e^{4}\alpha^{p})^{m/\alpha^{p}}

\alpha\mathcal{B}_{q}^{m}

Proof.

(e^{4}\alpha^{p})^{m/\alpha^{p}}

r\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=m^{1/p}/\alpha

Let

\boldsymbol{t}\in rB_{p}^{m}
, there exists
\boldsymbol{z}\in S
such that
\|\boldsymbol{z}-\boldsymbol{t}\|_{q}\leq m^{1/q}
. (This point can be found explicitly by simply rounding all of the coordinates of
\boldsymbol{t}
towards zero.) In other words, translates of
m^{1/q}B_{q}^{m}
centered at the points in
S
cover all of
rB_{p}^{m}
, and it therefore suffices to bound
|S|
.

Let

\Theta_{p}(\tau)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sum_{z\in\mathbb{Z}}e^{-\tau|z|^{p}}\;.

For any
\tau>0
, we have

|S|\leq e^{\tau r^{p}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}}e^{-\tau\|\boldsymbol{z}\|_{p}^{p}}=e^{\tau r^{p}}\Theta_{p}(\tau)^{m}\;,

where the equality follows from the fact that
f(\boldsymbol{z})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=e^{-\tau\|\boldsymbol{z}\|_{p}^{p}}=\prod_{i}e^{-\tau|z_{i}|^{p}}
is a product measure (and the inequality follows from an averaging argument).

It remains to bound
\Theta_{p}(\tau)
. Indeed,

\Theta_{p}(\tau)\leq 1+2e^{-\tau}+2\int_{1}^{\infty}e^{-\tau|x|^{p}}{\rm d}x\leq 1+2e^{-\tau}+2\int_{1}^{\infty}x^{p-1}e^{-\tau|x|^{p}}{\rm d}x=1+2e^{-\tau}\cdot(1+1/(p\tau))\;.

Plugging in
\tau\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=p\log\alpha
gives

|S|\leq\alpha^{pm/\alpha^{p}}\cdot(1+2\alpha^{-p}(1+1/(p^{2}\log\alpha))^{m}\leq(e^{4}\alpha^{p})^{m/\alpha^{p}}\;,

as needed,
where we have used the inequality,

(1+2(1+1/(p^{2}\log\alpha))/\alpha^{p})\leq e^{2(1+1/(p^{2}\log\alpha))/\alpha^{p}}\leq e^{4/\alpha^{p}}\;,

valid for
\alpha\geq e^{1/p^{2}}
.
∎

We can now present our reduction between SVP in different norms. The proof as presented below is a simplification (suggested by Moritz Venzin) of our original proof.

Theorem 3.2
.

For any (efficiently computable)
\varepsilon=\varepsilon(n,m)\in(0,1/100)
,
q\geq p\geq 1
, and
\gamma=\gamma(n,m)\geq 1
, there is a
(\gamma^{4}\cdot 2^{\varepsilon m}\mathrm{poly}(m))
-time reduction from
\gamma^{\prime}\text{-}\textsf{SVP}_{q}
to
\gamma\text{-}\textsf{SVP}_{p}
, where

\gamma^{\prime}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=100\log^{1/p}(1/\varepsilon)\cdot\gamma/\varepsilon^{1/p}\;.

The reduction preserves dimension and rank and only calls its oracle on sublattices of the input lattice.

Proof.

We show a reduction that runs in polynomial time and finds a sufficiently short vector with probability at least
2^{-\varepsilon m}/(C\gamma^{4}m^{8})
. The result follows by running this algorithm
2^{\varepsilon m}\gamma^{4}\cdot\mathrm{poly}(m)
times.

The reduction takes as input a basis
\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
for a lattice
\mathcal{L}\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
and behaves as follows. It first finds a prime
Q
with
10\gamma m^{2}2^{\varepsilon m/4}\leq Q\leq 20\gamma m^{2}2^{\varepsilon m/4}
and samples
\boldsymbol{z}_{1},\boldsymbol{z}_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}_{Q}^{n}
uniformly and independently at random. The reduction then sets

\mathcal{L}_{i}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathcal{L}\ \mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\ \langle\boldsymbol{z}_{i},\mathbf{B}^{-1}\boldsymbol{y}\rangle=0\bmod Q\}\;,

and calls its
\gamma
-
\textsf{SVP}_{p}
oracle on
\mathcal{L}_{1}
and
\mathcal{L}_{2}
, receiving as output
\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\boldsymbol{v}_{2}
. The reduction then outputs either
\boldsymbol{v}_{1}-\boldsymbol{v}_{2}
or
\boldsymbol{v}_{1}/k
where
k\geq 1
is maximal such that
\boldsymbol{v}_{1}/k\in\mathcal{L}
—whichever is has smaller
\ell_{q}
norm. (If
\boldsymbol{v}_{1}=\boldsymbol{v}_{2}
, then the reduction outputs
\boldsymbol{v}_{1}/k
.)

The reduction clearly runs in polynomial time. It suffices to show that it succeeds with probability at least
1/Q^{4}
.

To prove correctness, let
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{L}
be a shortest non-vector in the
\ell_{q}
norm, i.e.,
\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{q}=\lambda_{1}^{(q)}(\mathcal{L})
, and let
r_{p}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=m^{1/p-1/q}\lambda_{1}^{(q)}(\mathcal{L})\geq\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{p}
and
r_{q}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\gamma^{\prime}\lambda_{1}^{(q)}(\mathcal{L})
. Notice that
\mathcal{L}_{1}
and
\mathcal{L}_{2}
are independent and identically distributed random variables. It follows that
\boldsymbol{v}_{1}
and
\boldsymbol{v}_{2}
are also independent and identically distributed. By the correctness of the oracle, whenever
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{L}_{i}
, we must have
\|\boldsymbol{v}_{i}\|_{p}\leq\gamma r_{p}
. We will therefore study the distribution of
\boldsymbol{v}_{i}
conditioned on the event that
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{L}_{i}
. It follows immediately from Lemma
2.13
that
\Pr[\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{L}_{i}]=1/Q
, so that we can afford to condition on this event. We then divide our analysis into two cases: one for when this distribution has a high-probability vector and one when it does not.

First, suppose there exists a
\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathcal{L}\cap\,\gamma r_{p}B_{p}^{m}
such that

\Pr[\boldsymbol{v}_{i}=\boldsymbol{w}\text{ and }\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{L}_{i}]>1/Q^{2}\;.

By Lemma
2.13
, we must have
\boldsymbol{w}=\alpha\boldsymbol{x}\bmod Q\mathcal{L}
for some
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{Q}
, i.e.,
\boldsymbol{w}=\alpha\boldsymbol{x}+Q\boldsymbol{y}
, where
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathcal{L}
. We claim that
\boldsymbol{w}
must be a scalar multiple of
\boldsymbol{x}
(not just modulo
Q
). If this is the case, then we see that with probability at least
1/Q^{2}
,
\boldsymbol{v}_{1}=\boldsymbol{w}
in which case
\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{v}_{1}/k
, and we are done. So, suppose not. Then,

\|\boldsymbol{w}\|_{2}\geq Q\|\pi_{\boldsymbol{x}^{\perp}}(\boldsymbol{w})\|_{2}=Q\|\pi_{\boldsymbol{x}^{\perp}}(\boldsymbol{y})\|_{2}\geq Q\lambda_{1}^{(2)}(\pi_{\boldsymbol{x}^{\perp}}(\mathcal{L}))\;.

By Lemma
2.3
, this implies that
\|\boldsymbol{w}\|_{2}\geq(3/4)\cdot Q\lambda_{1}^{(2)}(\mathcal{L})^{2}/\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{2}>(Q/\sqrt{m})\lambda_{1}^{(2)}(\mathcal{L})
, i.e.
\|\boldsymbol{w}\|_{p}>\gamma\lambda_{1}^{(p)}(\mathcal{L})
. This contradicts the correctness of the oracle. So,
\boldsymbol{w}
must be a multiple of
\boldsymbol{x}
, as claimed.

Second, suppose that

\Pr[\boldsymbol{v}_{i}=\boldsymbol{w}\text{ and }\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{L}_{i}]\leq 1/Q^{2}

for all
\boldsymbol{w}
.
In this case, it suffices to show that there exists some
\boldsymbol{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
such that

\Pr[\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\boldsymbol{v}_{2}\in S_{\boldsymbol{c}}\text{ and }\boldsymbol{v}_{1}\neq\boldsymbol{v}_{2}]\geq 1/Q^{4}\;,

where
S_{\boldsymbol{c}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=((r_{q}/2)\mathcal{B}_{q}^{m}+\boldsymbol{c})
.
Indeed, whenever this event occurs,
\boldsymbol{v}_{1}-\boldsymbol{v}_{2}\in\mathcal{L}
is a short non-zero vector.

By Lemma
3.1
,
\gamma r_{p}\mathcal{B}_{p}^{m}
can be covered by
(e^{4}(\gamma^{\prime}/(2\gamma))^{p})^{m(2\gamma/\gamma^{\prime})^{p}}\leq Q/2
translated copies of
(r_{q}/2)\cdot B_{q}^{m}
. Since whenever
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{L}_{i}
, we have
\boldsymbol{v}_{i}\in\gamma r_{p}\mathcal{B}_{p}^{m}
, it follows from the pigeonhole principle that there must exist some
\boldsymbol{c}
with

\Pr[\boldsymbol{v}_{i}\in S_{\boldsymbol{c}}\ |\ \boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{L}_{i}]\geq 2/Q\;.

Therefore,

\Pr[\boldsymbol{v}_{i}\in S_{\boldsymbol{c}}\text{ and }\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{L}_{i}]\geq 2/Q^{2}\;.

Finally, we conclude that

\displaystyle\Pr[\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\boldsymbol{v}_{2}\in S_{\boldsymbol{c}}\text{ and }\boldsymbol{v}_{1}\neq\boldsymbol{v}_{2}]

\displaystyle\geq\Pr[\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\boldsymbol{v}_{2}\in S_{\boldsymbol{c}}\text{ and }\boldsymbol{v}_{1}\neq\boldsymbol{v}_{2}\text{ and }\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{L}_{1}\cap\,\mathcal{L}_{2}]

\displaystyle\geq\Pr[\boldsymbol{v}_{1}\in S_{\boldsymbol{c}}\text{ and }\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{L}_{1}]

\displaystyle\qquad\cdot\big(\Pr[\boldsymbol{v}_{2}\in S_{\boldsymbol{c}}\text{ and }\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{L}_{2}]-\max_{\boldsymbol{w}}\Pr[\boldsymbol{v}_{2}=\boldsymbol{w}\text{ and }\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{L}_{2}]\big)

\displaystyle\geq(2/Q^{2})\cdot(2/Q^{2}-1/Q^{2})

\displaystyle\geq 1/Q^{4}\;,

as needed, where the second inequality uses the independence of
(\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\mathcal{L}_{1})
from
(\boldsymbol{v}_{2},\mathcal{L}_{2})
.
∎

4
Reductions from CVP to SVP (and to CVP) in different norms

In this section, we present a reduction from
\gamma
-
\textsf{CVP}_{q}
to (
(1+\varepsilon)
-unique)
\textsf{SVP}_{p}
for any
p
and
q
. We also present a reduction from
\gamma^{\prime}
-
\textsf{CVP}_{p}
to
\gamma
-
\textsf{CVP}_{q}
where
1\leq p\leq q\leq\infty
and
\gamma^{\prime}=O(\gamma)
.

We will first need a basic lemma about the growth of the number of lattice points in a convex body as we increase the radius of the body. We include the simple packing-based proof for completeness.

Lemma 4.1
.

For any radii
0<r<R
, any lattice
\mathcal{L}\subset\real^{n}
and any symmetric convex body
\mathcal{K}\subset\real^{n}

|\mathcal{L}\cap\,R\mathcal{K}|\leq\left(1+\frac{2R}{r}\right)^{n}|\mathcal{L}\cap\,r\mathcal{K}|.

Proof.

Notice that for any
\boldsymbol{x}\in\real^{n}
,

\left|\{\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathcal{L}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\boldsymbol{x}\in\boldsymbol{y}+\frac{r}{2}\mathcal{K}\}\right|=\left|\mathcal{L}\cap\,\left(\frac{r}{2}\mathcal{K}+\boldsymbol{x}\right)\right|\leq|\mathcal{L}\cap\,r\mathcal{K}|\;.

From the above, we see that

\displaystyle\mathrm{Vol}\left(\bigcup_{\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathcal{L}\cap\,R\mathcal{K}}\left(\boldsymbol{y}+\frac{r}{2}\mathcal{K}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\geq|\mathcal{L}\cap\,r\mathcal{K}|^{-1}\sum\limits_{\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathcal{L}\cap\,R\mathcal{K}}\mathrm{Vol}\left(\boldsymbol{y}+\frac{r}{2}\mathcal{K}\right)

\displaystyle=|\mathcal{L}\cap\,r\mathcal{K}|^{-1}|\mathcal{L}\cap\,R\mathcal{K}|\left(\frac{r}{2}\right)^{n}\mathrm{Vol}(\mathcal{K})\;.

(2)

On the other hand,

\bigcup_{\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathcal{L}\cap\,R\mathcal{K}}\left(\boldsymbol{y}+\frac{r}{2}\mathcal{K}\right)\subset\left(R+\frac{r}{2}\right)\mathcal{K}\;,

so that

\mathrm{Vol}\left(\bigcup_{\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathcal{L}\cap\,R\mathcal{K}}\left(\boldsymbol{y}+\frac{r}{2}\mathcal{K}\right)\right)\leq\left(R+\frac{r}{2}\right)^{n}\mathrm{Vol}(\mathcal{K})\;.

(3)

By Eqs. (
2
) and (
3
), we get

\left(R+\frac{r}{2}\right)^{n}\geq|\mathcal{L}\cap\,r\mathcal{K}|^{-1}|\mathcal{L}\cap\,R\mathcal{K}|\left(\frac{r}{2}\right)^{n}\;,

and the result follows.
∎

Using the above lemma, we derive the following proposition, which will help us find a radius
r^{\dagger}
such that “
N_{p}(\mathcal{L},r)
does not grow too quickly for
r\approx r^{\dagger}
.”

Proposition 4.2
.

For any lattice
\mathcal{L}\subset\real^{m}
,
c\geq 2
, radius
r>0
and symmetric convex body
\mathcal{K}\subset\real^{m}
there exists
c^{\dagger}
such that
c\geq c^{\dagger}>c/2
and

\frac{|\mathcal{L}\cap\;c^{\dagger}r\mathcal{K}|}{|\mathcal{L}\cap\;(c^{\dagger}-1)r\mathcal{K}|}\leq 2^{\varepsilon m}\;,

where
\varepsilon=\frac{\log 5}{\lfloor c/2\rfloor}
.

Proof.

Let
c^{\prime}=c-\lfloor\frac{c}{2}\rfloor
. By Lemma
4.1
, we know that

\frac{|\mathcal{L}\cap\,cr\mathcal{K}|}{|\mathcal{L}\cap\,c^{\prime}r\mathcal{K}|}\leq 5^{m}.

Let
N_{i}=|\mathcal{L}\cap\,(c^{\prime}+i)r\mathcal{K}|
and we get

\frac{N_{1}}{N_{0}}\cdot\frac{N_{2}}{N_{1}}\cdot\ldots\cdot\frac{N_{\lfloor\frac{c}{2}\rfloor}}{N_{\lfloor\frac{c}{2}\rfloor-1}}=\frac{N_{\lfloor\frac{c}{2}\rfloor}}{N_{0}}=\frac{|\mathcal{L}\cap\,cr\mathcal{K}|}{|\mathcal{L}\cap\,c^{\prime}r\mathcal{K}|}\leq 5^{m}.

By the pigeonhole principle there must exists a
1\leq j\leq\lfloor\frac{c}{2}\rfloor
, for which

\frac{N_{j}}{N_{j-1}}\leq 5^{m/\lfloor c/2\rfloor}=2^{\frac{m\log 5}{\lfloor c/2\rfloor}}\;.\qed

Finally, we will need the following rather technical lemma.

Lemma 4.3
.

For any
\varepsilon>0
,
p\geq 1
, lattice
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{B})\subset\real^{m}
, vectors
\boldsymbol{t},\boldsymbol{x}\in\real^{m}
,
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathcal{L}
, and radius
r>0
. Let
{\mathcal{S}}\subseteq\mathcal{L}\cap\,(\boldsymbol{x}+r{\mathcal{B}}_{p}^{m})
. Then the set
{\mathcal{T}}={\mathcal{S}}+\boldsymbol{v}\subseteq\mathcal{L}
satisfies the following:

{\mathcal{T}}-{\mathcal{S}}-\boldsymbol{t}\subseteq\left(2r+\|\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{t}\|_{p}\right){\mathcal{B}}_{p}^{m}\;,

and if
\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{0}
, then

{\mathcal{T}}-\boldsymbol{t}\subseteq\left(r+\|\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{t}\|_{p}\right){\mathcal{B}}_{p}^{m}\;.

Proof.

Clearly, any vector in
{\mathcal{T}}-{\mathcal{S}}-\boldsymbol{t}
is contained in
\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{t}+2r{\mathcal{B}}_{p}^{m}
, which in turn is contained in
\left(2r+\|\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{t}\|_{p}\right){\mathcal{B}}_{p}^{m}
.

Also, any vector in
{\mathcal{T}}-\boldsymbol{t}
is contained in
\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{t}+r{\mathcal{B}}_{p}^{m}
, which in turn is contained in
\left(r+\|\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{t}\|_{p}\right){\mathcal{B}}_{p}^{m}
.
∎

Theorem 4.4
.

For any
\varepsilon=\varepsilon(n,m)\in(0,1/100)
,

, and

(2,\gamma)

(1+\delta)

p=q
, we can take
\gamma=80/\varepsilon
.

The reduction calls its
\textsf{uSVP}_{p}

m+1
and rank
n+1
.

Proof.

Let
\kappa\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=m^{\left(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q}\right)}

\frac{2^{-\varepsilon m}}{\mathrm{poly}(m\ell/\varepsilon)}

\ell

\mathrm{poly}(m\ell/\varepsilon)\cdot 2^{\varepsilon m}

\mathbf{B}\in\real^{m\times n}

\mathcal{L}\subset\real^{m}

\boldsymbol{t}\in\real^{m}

\mathbf{B}^{\dagger}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{B}&-\boldsymbol{t}\\
\boldsymbol{0}&1\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{(m+1)\times(n+1)}\;,

\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{B}^{\dagger})
and
\ell
be the number of bits required for the natural representation of
\mathbf{B}^{\dagger}
.
It follows from Proposition
4.2
that by sampling

appropriately, the algorithm can guess a radius

\frac{N_{p}({\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}},r)}{N_{p}({\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}},r-2(m+1))}\leq 2^{\varepsilon m/4}

(4)

with probability at least

.
Similarly, it follows from Lemma
2.2
that with probability
1/\mathrm{poly}(m,\ell)

(5)

The reduction does the following. It first uses its
(1+\delta)\text{-}\textsf{uSVP}_{p}

2.14

, with input lattice
\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}

The reduction then finds integers

.

The reduction clearly runs in time polynomial in the size of the input.
We now show that it outputs a vector at the desired distance from the target in the

\frac{1}{2^{\varepsilon m}\cdot\mathrm{poly}(\ell,m/\varepsilon)}
. Specifically, we will find sets
{\mathcal{S}},{\mathcal{T}}\subset\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}

.
Let
r^{\prime}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=r-2(m+1)
. For an integer

\mathcal{L}_{k}^{\dagger}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{(\boldsymbol{v}-k\boldsymbol{t},k)\in\mathbb{R}^{m+1}\ \mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\ \boldsymbol{v}\in\mathcal{L}\}

\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}

\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}\cap\,r^{\prime}{\mathcal{B}}_{p}^{m+1}=\bigcup_{|k|\leq r^{\prime}}(\mathcal{L}_{k}^{\dagger}\cap\,r^{\prime}{\mathcal{B}}_{p}^{m+1})\;.

Therefore, there must exist a
-r^{\prime}\leq k^{*}\leq r^{\prime}

N_{p}(\mathcal{L}_{k^{*}}^{\dagger},r^{\prime})\geq N_{p}(\mathcal{L}^{\dagger},r^{\prime})/(2r^{\prime}+1)

.

Let
\alpha=\left(\frac{10}{\varepsilon}\log(1/\varepsilon)\right)^{1/p}
. From Lemma
3.1
, we know that
\mathcal{L}_{k^{*}}^{\dagger}\cap\,r^{\prime}\mathcal{B}_{p}^{m+1}

2^{\varepsilon m/4}
translated copies of the
\ell_{q}
ball with radius

\frac{\alpha}{\kappa}\cdot r^{\prime}=\frac{\alpha}{\kappa}\cdot(r-2(m+1))<\gamma/2\cdot\mathrm{dist}_{q}(\boldsymbol{t},\mathcal{L})-\left(\frac{2m}{\kappa}\right)

Hence, there exists a vector
\boldsymbol{x}\in\real^{m+1}

{\mathcal{S}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mathcal{L}_{k^{*}}^{\dagger}\cap\,\Big(\boldsymbol{x}+\frac{\alpha r^{\prime}}{\kappa}{\mathcal{B}}_{q}^{m+1}\Big)\;,

\frac{N_{p}(\mathcal{L}^{\dagger},r^{\prime})}{2^{\varepsilon m/4}(2r^{\prime}+1)}

{\mathcal{T}}-{\mathcal{S}}\subseteq\mathcal{L}_{1}^{\dagger}\;.

(6)

From Lemma
4.3

{\mathcal{T}}-{\mathcal{S}}\subseteq(2\frac{\alpha}{\kappa}r^{\prime}+\frac{m}{\kappa}+1)\mathcal{B}_{q}^{m+1}\;,\text{ and }{\mathcal{T}}\subseteq(r^{\prime}+m+1)\mathcal{B}_{p}^{m+1}.

(7)

Let
{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathcal{L}^{\dagger,prim}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\exists k\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0},k\boldsymbol{v}\in{\mathcal{S}}\}

. By the definition of

\lambda_{1}^{(p)}(\mathcal{L})\geq\lambda_{1}^{(q)}(\mathcal{L})\geq\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{dist}_{q}(\boldsymbol{t},\mathcal{L})>\frac{m}{4\kappa}

\lambda_{1}^{(p)}(\mathcal{L}^{\dagger})\geq\min\{1,\lambda_{1}^{(p)}(\mathcal{L})\}\geq\min\Big\{1,\frac{m}{4\kappa}\Big\}\geq\frac{1}{4}.

(8)

Applying Claim
2.1
then gives

\min\{|\mathcal{T^{\prime}}|,|\mathcal{S^{\prime}}|\}\geq\frac{|\mathcal{S}|}{4r}\geq\frac{N_{p}(\mathcal{L}^{\dagger},r^{\prime})}{\mathrm{poly}(m/\varepsilon)\cdot 2^{\varepsilon m/4}}\;.

(9)

Let
f(m)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=m\frac{N_{p}(\mathcal{L}^{\dagger,prim},r)}{N_{p}(\mathcal{L}^{\dagger,prim},r-m-1)}
.
Notice that
{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime},{\mathcal{T}}^{\prime}\in(r-m-1)\mathcal{B}_{p}^{m+1}
,
\frac{r}{r-m-1}\geq 1+\delta
,
f(m)\cdot N\leq 2^{\mathrm{poly}(\ell,m/\varepsilon)}
, and

. We have

\displaystyle\Pr[\boldsymbol{v}_{1}\in\mathcal{T^{\prime}}]

\displaystyle\geq\frac{|{\mathcal{T}}^{\prime}|\cdot N_{p}(\mathcal{L}^{\dagger,prim},r-m-1)}{\mathrm{poly}(\ell,m/\varepsilon)\cdot N\cdot N_{p}(\mathcal{L}^{\dagger,prim},r)}

(Theorem

)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{|{\mathcal{T}}^{\prime}|}{\mathrm{poly}(\ell,m/\varepsilon)\cdot N_{p}(\mathcal{L}^{\dagger,prim},r)}

(Eq. (
5

\displaystyle\geq\frac{N_{p}(\mathcal{L}^{\dagger},r^{\prime})}{\mathrm{poly}(\ell,m/\varepsilon)\cdot 2^{\varepsilon m/4}\cdot N_{p}(\mathcal{L}^{\dagger,prim},r)}

(Eq. (

4.1
A reduction from CVP to CVP

We next show a reduction between
CVP
in different norms. To that end, we will first need the following simple lemma. For
p\geq 1
, let
D^{(p)}
be the probability distribution over
\real^{m}
whose probability density function is
e^{-C_{p}\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{p}^{p}}
, where
C_{p}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=2^{p}\Gamma(1+1/p)^{p}=\Theta(2^{p})
is such that this yields a probability distribution (i.e.,
\int_{\real^{m}}e^{-C_{p}\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{p}^{p}}{\rm d}\boldsymbol{x}=1
). We also define
D^{(\infty)}
as the uniform distribution over
[-1,1]^{m}
.

Lemma 4.6
.

For any
1\leq p<\infty
and
1\leq q\leq\infty
,
\boldsymbol{y}\in\real^{m}
, and
\varepsilon\in(0,1/100)
,

\Pr_{\boldsymbol{X}\sim D^{(p)}}[\|\boldsymbol{X}-\boldsymbol{y}\|_{q}\leq m^{1/q}r]\geq e^{-\varepsilon m-C_{p}\|\boldsymbol{y}\|_{p}^{p}}\;,

where
r\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=10\log^{1/p}(1/\varepsilon)/C_{p}^{1/p}
. In particular, if
\|\boldsymbol{y}\|_{p}<(\varepsilon m/C_{p})^{1/p}
, then this probability is at least
e^{-2\varepsilon m}
.

Proof.

Notice that
D^{(p)}
is a product distribution. Therefore, we have

\Pr[\|\boldsymbol{X}-\boldsymbol{y}\|_{q}\leq rm^{1/q}]\geq\prod_{i=1}^{m}\Pr[|X_{i}-y_{i}|\leq r]\;.

If
|y_{i}|>r/2>1
, then

\Pr[|X_{i}-y_{i}|\leq r]\geq e^{-C_{p}|y_{i}|^{p}}\;.

Otherwise,

\displaystyle\Pr[|X_{i}-y_{i}|\leq r]

\displaystyle\geq\Pr[-r/2\leq X_{i}\leq r/2]

\displaystyle=1-2\int_{r/2}^{\infty}e^{-C_{p}x^{p}}{\rm d}x

\displaystyle\geq 1-2^{p}/r^{p-1}\cdot\int_{r/2}^{\infty}x^{p-1}e^{-C_{p}x^{p}}{\rm d}x

\displaystyle=1-\frac{2^{p}}{pC_{p}r^{p-1}}e^{-C_{p}r^{p}/2^{p}}

\displaystyle\geq e^{-\varepsilon}\;.

The result follows.
∎

Given a basis

for lattice

and target vector

as input, the reduction behaves as follows. By randomly rescaling appropriately, we may assume that

Let

1.

2.

Let
\boldsymbol{v}

m

We want to lower bound the probability of

\mathcal{G}
consists of vectors that we are interested in. We start by upper bounding

\displaystyle f_{p}(\mathcal{L}^{\dagger})

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}\sum_{\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{L}}f_{p}\left(\frac{\boldsymbol{u}-k\boldsymbol{t}}{\alpha},k\right)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}f_{p}(k)\sum_{\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{L}}f_{p}\left(\frac{\boldsymbol{u}-k\boldsymbol{t}}{\alpha}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum\limits_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}f_{p}(k)\cdot f_{p}\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}\mathcal{L}\right)

\displaystyle\leq c\cdot f_{p}\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}\mathcal{L}\right)\;,

is a constant. Notice that the second-to-last inequality follows from Lemma
2.6

\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathcal{L}
be a vector such that
\|\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{t}\|_{p}=\mathrm{dist}_{p}(\boldsymbol{t},\mathcal{L})

\displaystyle f_{p}(\mathcal{G})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{L}}f_{p}\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}\boldsymbol{u}+\frac{1}{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{t}),1\right)

\displaystyle\geq f_{p}\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{t}),1\right)\cdot\sum_{\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{L}}f_{p}\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}\boldsymbol{u}\right)

\displaystyle\geq e^{-1-\frac{\|\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{t}\|_{p}^{p}}{\alpha^{p}}}\cdot f_{p}\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}\mathcal{L}\right)

\displaystyle\geq e^{-1-m/\alpha^{p}}\cdot f_{p}\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}\mathcal{L}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{3}e^{-m/\alpha^{p}}f_{p}\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}\mathcal{L}\right)\;,

where the first inequality follows from Lemma

Therefore,

\Pr[\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{G}]=\frac{f_{p}(\mathcal{G})}{f_{p}(\mathcal{L}^{\dagger})}\geq\frac{1}{3c}e^{-m/\alpha^{p}}\;.

Next, by Lemma
2.5

\Pr[\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{p}\geq tm^{1/p}]\leq(e^{-t^{p}+1/p}\cdot t\cdot p^{1/p})^{m}\;.

(11)

so that the above probability is at most

It follows that each vector output by the
\delta
-
\textsf{DSS}_{p}^{M}
oracle will yield a correct solution with probability at least

∎

Lemma A.1

The upper bound follows from the observation that

\Pr[\langle\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{y}_{1}+\boldsymbol{c}\rangle=0\bmod Q]\leq\Pr[\boldsymbol{y}_{1}+\boldsymbol{c}=\boldsymbol{0}\bmod Q]+\Pr[\langle\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{y}_{1}+\boldsymbol{c}\rangle=\boldsymbol{0}\bmod Q\ |\ \boldsymbol{y}_{1}+\boldsymbol{c}\neq\boldsymbol{0}]=\frac{1}{Q^{n}}+\frac{1}{Q}\;.

The result then immediately follows from the observation that
\Pr[\langle\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{y}_{1}+\boldsymbol{c}\rangle=0\bmod Q]\geq 1/Q
and union bound.

Indeed, the statement for the
\boldsymbol{y}_{i}
is immediate from the observation that for any fixed
i>1

\boldsymbol{y}_{1}+\boldsymbol{c}^{\prime}\neq\boldsymbol{v}_{i}\bmod Q

.

p\geq 1
, a basis
\mathbf{B}\in\real^{m\times n}
for a lattice
\mathcal{L}\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
, algorithm first finds a prime
Q
such that
100f(m)N\log(f(m)N)\leq Q\leq 200f(m)N\log(f(m)N)
. It then samples
\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{Z}_{Q}^{n}
uniformly at random and sets

\mathcal{L}^{\prime}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathcal{L}\ \mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\ \langle\boldsymbol{z},\mathbf{B}^{-1}\boldsymbol{v}\rangle=0\bmod Q\}\;,

and calls its
\gamma\text{-}\textsf{uSVP}_{p}
oracle on
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
. If the oracle outputs
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathcal{L}
such that
\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{p}\leq r
, the algorithm outputs
\pm\boldsymbol{v}
(where the sign is chosen randomly). Otherwise, it outputs
\boldsymbol{0}
.

The running time of the algorithm is clear. Let
\boldsymbol{x}_{0}\in\mathcal{L}^{prim}\cap\,\mathcal{B}_{p}(r)
. Let
\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}_{M}\in\mathcal{L}
be all distinct primitive lattice vectors with
\|\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\|\leq\gamma r
and
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\neq\pm\boldsymbol{x}_{0}
. By Theorem
A.3
, we have

\displaystyle\Pr[\boldsymbol{x}_{0}\in\mathcal{L}^{\prime}\text{ and }\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\notin\mathcal{L}^{\prime},\ \forall i>0]

\displaystyle=Pr[\langle\boldsymbol{z},\mathbf{B}^{-1}\boldsymbol{v_{0}}\rangle=0\bmod Q\text{ and }\langle\boldsymbol{z},\mathbf{B}^{-1}\boldsymbol{v_{i}}\rangle\neq 0\bmod Q,\ \forall{i}>0]

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{Q}-\frac{M}{Q^{2}}\;.

Notice that whenever this event occurs, the oracle must output
\pm\boldsymbol{x}_{0}
.
By assumption,
M<N_{p}(\mathcal{L}^{prim},\gamma(n)r)\ll Q/2
, so this probability is at least
1/(2Q)\geq 1/(1000f(m)N\log(f(m)N))
, and the result follows.
∎

Proof of Theorem
2.15

See
2.15

Proof.

The reduction takes as input a
\mathbf{B}\in\real^{m\times n}
for a lattice
\mathcal{L}\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
, and a target vector
\boldsymbol{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
. If
\lambda_{1}^{(p)}(\mathcal{L})>\mathrm{dist}_{p}(\boldsymbol{t},\mathcal{L})
, then the reduction is trivial. So, we may assume that
\lambda_{1}^{(p)}(\mathcal{L})\leq\mathrm{dist}_{p}(\boldsymbol{t},\mathcal{L})
. Let
r\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mathrm{dist}_{p}(\boldsymbol{t},\mathcal{L})/\tau
. We may also assume without loss of generality that the reduction takes as input a prime number
Q\geq 101
such that and
100\cdot N_{p}(\mathcal{L},r)\leq Q\leq 200N_{p}(\mathcal{L},r)
, since the reduction can guess this value with probability at least
1/\mathrm{poly}(m)
.

The reduction then samples
\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{Z}_{Q}^{n}
and
\boldsymbol{c}\in\mathbb{Z}_{Q}^{n}
uniformly and independently at random, sets
\boldsymbol{t}^{\prime}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\boldsymbol{t}+\boldsymbol{y}
where
\boldsymbol{y}
is any lattice vector with
\langle\boldsymbol{z},\mathbf{B}^{-1}\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{c}\rangle=0\bmod Q
and

\mathcal{L}^{\prime}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathcal{L}\ \mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\ \langle\boldsymbol{z},\mathbf{B}^{-1}\boldsymbol{v}\rangle=0\bmod Q\}\;.

It then calls its
(\alpha,\gamma)\text{-}\textsf{BDD}_{p}
oracle on input
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
and
\boldsymbol{t}^{\prime}
, receiving as output
\boldsymbol{v}
. Finally, the reduction outputs
\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{y}
.

The running time of the reduction is clear. For correctness, it suffices to show that with positive constant probability, we have both that
\mathrm{dist}_{p}(\boldsymbol{t}^{\prime},\mathcal{L}^{\prime})\leq(1+1/\tau)\mathrm{dist}_{p}(\boldsymbol{t},\mathcal{L})
and
\mathrm{dist}_{p}(\boldsymbol{t}^{\prime},\mathcal{L}^{\prime})\leq(1+\tau)\lambda_{1}^{(p)}(\mathcal{L}^{\prime})
.

To that end, let
\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{v}_{N}\in\mathcal{L}
be all distinct non-zero vectors with length at most
r
, and let
\boldsymbol{y}_{i}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\boldsymbol{v}_{i}+\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathcal{L}
, where
\boldsymbol{w}
is a closest lattice vector to
\boldsymbol{t}
. Notice that
\lambda_{1}^{(p)}(\mathcal{L}^{\prime})>r
if and only if
\boldsymbol{v}_{i}\notin\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
for all
i
. Furthermore, if there exists a
\boldsymbol{y}_{i}\in\mathcal{L}^{\prime}+\boldsymbol{y}
, then
\mathrm{dist}_{p}(\boldsymbol{t}^{\prime},\mathcal{L}^{\prime})\leq\|\boldsymbol{y}_{i}-\boldsymbol{t}\|_{p}\leq\mathrm{dist}_{p}(\boldsymbol{t},\mathcal{L})+r
. If both of these events occur simultaneously with non-negligible probability, then we are done.

Notice that
\boldsymbol{v}_{i}\notin Q\mathcal{L}
, since otherwise
\boldsymbol{v}_{i}/Q,2\boldsymbol{v}_{i}/Q,\ldots,Q\boldsymbol{v}_{i}/Q\in\mathcal{L}\cap\,B_{p}^{n}(r)
, contradicting the assumption that
N_{p}(\mathcal{L},r)<Q
. Therefore, we may apply Lemma
A.1
to
\mathbf{B}^{-1}\boldsymbol{v}_{i}
and
\mathbf{B}^{-1}\boldsymbol{y}_{i}
to see that for each
i
,

\Pr[\boldsymbol{y}_{i}\in\mathcal{L}+\boldsymbol{y}\text{ and }\boldsymbol{v}_{j}\notin\mathcal{L}\ \forall j\text{, and }\boldsymbol{y}_{j}\notin\mathcal{L}^{\prime}+\boldsymbol{y}\ \forall j\neq i]\geq\frac{1}{Q}-\frac{2N}{Q^{2}}-\frac{N}{Q^{n}}\;.

Notice that these are disjoint events, so that the probability of at least one happening is exactly the sum of each probability. It follows that

\Pr[\exists i,\ \boldsymbol{y}_{i}\in\mathcal{L}+\boldsymbol{y}\text{ and }\boldsymbol{v}_{j}\notin\mathcal{L}^{\prime}\ \forall j]\geq\frac{N}{Q}+-\frac{2N^{2}}{Q^{2}}-\frac{N^{2}}{Q^{n}}\geq\frac{1}{1000}\;,

as needed.
∎

A.3
Proof of Theorem
5.4

Theorem A.5

(Theorem 4.5,
[
Ste16a
]
)
.

For any efficiently computable function
f(m)
with
1\leq f(m)\leq poly(m)
, there is a polynomial-time reduction from
(\beta,\gamma)
-
\textsf{GapPVCP}_{p}
to
\textsf{SVP}_{p}
where
\beta=\frac{1}{f(m)}
and
\gamma=1+\frac{1}{f(m)}
. The reduction preserves rank and dimension and only calls the
\textsf{SVP}_{p}
oracle on sublattices of the input lattice.

See
5.4

Proof.

On input
\mathcal{L}\subset\mathbb{Q}^{m}
, the reduction behaves as follows. First it computes
\lambda_{1}^{(p)}(\mathcal{L})
using its
\textsf{SVP}_{p}
oracle. For
i=0,\ldots,\ell=200\cdot m^{2}f(m)
, let

r_{i}=\left(\left(\lambda_{1}^{(p)}(\mathcal{L})\right)^{p}+\frac{i}{100\cdot mf(m)}\right)^{1/p}.

Let
\gamma=1+1/f(m)
. For each
i
, the reduction uses
\textsf{SVP}_{p}
oracle (using the procedure from Theorem
A.5
) to compute
N_{i}
such that

\gamma^{-1/10}N_{p}(\mathcal{L}^{prim},r_{i})\leq N_{i}\leq N_{p}(\mathcal{L}^{prim},r_{i})

or
N_{i}=1
if
\lambda_{1}^{(p)}(\mathcal{L})\leq\frac{r_{i}}{100\cdot m^{2}f(m)\cdot N_{p}(\mathcal{L}^{prim},r_{i})}
.
Let
w_{\ell}=f_{p}(\mathbb{Z}_{\neq 0}r_{\ell})
and for
i=0,\ldots,\ell-1
, let

w_{i}=f_{p}(\mathbb{Z}_{\neq 0}r_{i})-f_{p}(\mathbb{Z}_{\neq 0}r_{i+1})
.

Let
W=\sum_{i=0}^{\ell}N_{i}w_{i}
. Then the reduction outputs
\boldsymbol{0}
with probability
\frac{1}{1+W}
. Otherwise it choose an index
0\leq k\leq\ell
, assigning to each index
i
probability
\frac{N_{i}\cdot w_{i}}{W}
. If
N_{k}>1
, the reduction chooses a vector
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{L}^{prim}
that is uniformly distributed over
\mathcal{L}^{prim}\cap\,\mathcal{B}_{p}(r_{k})
, up to a factor of
\gamma^{\pm 1/10}
. If
N_{k}=1
, the reduction simply sets
\boldsymbol{x}=\textsf{SVP}_{p}(\mathcal{L})
. Finally, it samples an integer
z
from
D_{\mathbb{Z}_{\neq 0}\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{p},p}
and returns
\boldsymbol{v}=z\cdot\boldsymbol{x}
.

First, we note that the reduction runs in expected polynomial time. In particular, the
N_{i}
have polynomial bit length by Lemma
2.2
, and the various subprocedures have expected running times that are polynomial in the length of their input.

We now prove correctness. Let
\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}
be the set of all lattice vectors that are integer multiples of a primitive lattice vector whose length is at most
m^{1/p}
. By the definition, it is enough to bound the probability of all the vector from
\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}
. Then,

f_{p}(\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}\setminus\{\boldsymbol{0}\})=\sum\limits_{\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}\setminus\{\boldsymbol{0}\}}f_{p}(\boldsymbol{y})=\sum\limits_{\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathcal{L}^{prim}\cap\,m^{1/p}\mathcal{B}_{p}}f_{p}(\mathbb{Z}_{\neq 0}\|\boldsymbol{y}\|_{p})\;.

For any
\boldsymbol{y}
with
r_{i-1}\leq\|\boldsymbol{y}\|_{p}\leq r_{i}
, we have

f_{p}(\mathbb{Z}_{\neq 0}r_{i})\leq f_{p}(\mathbb{Z}_{\neq 0}\|\boldsymbol{y}\|_{p})\leq\gamma^{1/10}f_{p}(\mathbb{Z}_{\neq 0}r_{i})\;.

From the definition of
w_{i}
, we have

\sum\limits_{i=0}^{\ell}w_{i}\cdot N_{p}(\mathcal{L}^{prim},r_{i})\leq f_{p}(\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}\setminus\{0\})\leq\gamma^{1/10}\sum\limits_{i=0}^{\ell}w_{i}\cdot N_{p}(\mathcal{L}^{prim},r_{i})\;.

Now we would like to say that
N_{i}\approx N_{p}(\mathcal{L}^{prim},r_{i})
. This is true by definition except when
N_{i}=1
and
N_{p}(\mathcal{L}^{prim},r)>1
, i.e., when
\lambda_{1}^{(p)}(\mathcal{L})<\frac{r_{i}}{100m^{2}f(m)N_{p}(\mathcal{L}^{prim},r_{i})}
and
\lambda_{2}^{(p)}(\mathcal{L})\leq r_{i}
. Notice that

N_{p}(\mathcal{L}^{prim},r_{i+1})\geq\frac{r_{i+1}-\lambda_{2}^{(p)}(\mathcal{L})}{\lambda_{1}^{(p)}(\mathcal{L})}\geq\frac{1}{200m\cdot f(m)\lambda_{1}^{(p)}(\mathcal{L})}.

This implies that for
j>i
, we get
\lambda_{1}^{(p)}(\mathcal{L})>\frac{r_{j}}{100m^{2}\cdot f(m)N_{p}(\mathcal{L}^{prim},r_{j})}
.
It follows that, for any
i<\ell
, we have

\gamma^{-1/5}\cdot\sum\limits_{j\geq i}w_{j}\cdot N_{p}(\mathcal{L}^{prim},r_{j})\leq\sum\limits_{j\geq i}w_{j}\cdot N_{j}\leq\sum\limits_{j\geq i}w_{j}\cdot N_{p}(\mathcal{L}^{prim},r_{j})\;.

Therefore, we have that,

\gamma^{-1/5}f_{p}(\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}\setminus\{\boldsymbol{0}\})\leq W\leq\gamma^{1/5}f_{p}(\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}\setminus\{\boldsymbol{0}\})\;.

So, the probability that reduction outputs
\boldsymbol{0}
is
\frac{1}{1+W}
, which is a good approximation to the correct probability of
\frac{1}{f_{p}(\mathcal{L}^{\dagger})}
.

Now, for any
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathcal{L}^{prim}
, it follows from Corollary
A.4
that

\gamma^{-1/2}\frac{f_{p}(\mathbb{Z}_{\neq 0}\|\boldsymbol{y}\|_{p})}{f_{p}(\mathcal{L}^{\dagger})}\leq\Pr[\boldsymbol{x}=\pm\boldsymbol{y}]\leq\gamma^{1/2}\frac{f_{p}(\mathbb{Z}_{\neq 0}\|\boldsymbol{y}\|_{p})}{f_{p}(\mathcal{L}^{\dagger})}\;.

(12)

Finally, for any
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}\setminus\{\boldsymbol{0}\}
, Let
\boldsymbol{y}
be one of the primitive lattice vector that are scalar multiples of
\boldsymbol{u}
and let
z^{\prime}
be such that
\boldsymbol{u}=z^{\prime}\boldsymbol{y}
. Then,

\Pr[\boldsymbol{v}=\boldsymbol{u}]=\Pr[\boldsymbol{x}=\pm\boldsymbol{y}]\cdot\Pr[z=z^{\prime}|\boldsymbol{x}=\pm\boldsymbol{y}]

=\Pr[\boldsymbol{x}=\pm\boldsymbol{y}].\frac{f_{p}(\boldsymbol{u})}{f_{p}({\|\boldsymbol{y}\|_{p}\cdot\mathbb{Z}_{\neq 0}})}\;.

The result follows from plugging the above equation into Eq. (
12
).

∎
