Theoretical Analysis and Numerical Approximation for the Stochastic thermal quasi-geostrophic model
D Crisan, DD Holm, O Lang, PR Mensah, W Pan

Abstract
. This paper investigates the mathematical properties of a stochastic version of the balanced 2D thermal quasigeostrophic (TQG) model of potential vorticity dynamics. This stochastic TQG model is intended as a basis for parametrisation of the dynamical creation of unresolved degrees of freedom in computational simulations of upper ocean dynamics when horizontal buoyancy gradients and bathymetry affect the dynamics, particularly at the submesoscale (250m–10km). Specifically, we have chosen the SALT (Stochastic Advection by Lie Transport) algorithm introduced in [ 1 ] and applied in [ 2 , 3 ] as our modelling approach. The SALT approach preserves the Kelvin circulation theorem and an infinite family of integral conservation laws for TQG. The goal of the SALT algorithm is to quantify the uncertainty in the process of up-scaling, or coarse-graining of either observed or synthetic data at fine scales, for use in computational simulations at coarser scales. The present work provides a rigorous mathematical analysis of the solution properties of the thermal quasigeostrophic (TQG) equations with stochastic advection by Lie transport (SALT) [ 4 , 5 ] .



1.
Introduction

The deterministic TQG (thermal QG) model augments the standard QG (quasigeostrophic) model for quasigeostrophically balanced planar incompressible fluid flow. Namely, TQG augments the QG balance between pressure gradient and Coriolis force for small Rossby number by also introducing the thermal gradient. See, e.g.,
[
6
,
7
,
5
]
for the history and details about how the TQG model can be derived via a balanced asymptotic expansion in small dimension-free ocean dynamics parameters, as well as results of initial computational simulations.

In TQG, as in other models of planar incompressible fluid flow, the divergence-free vector field
\mathbf{u}(x,y,t)
representing horizontal fluid velocity may be defined in terms of its
stream function

\psi(x,y,t)
as

(1.1)

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}=\nabla^{\perp}\psi:={\bm{\widehat{z}}}\times\nabla\psi\,.

The defining expression for the divergence-free TQG velocity vector field
\mathbf{u}
is an assumed balance among Coriolis force, hydrostatic pressure force and also the horizontal gradient of buoyancy, which arises at order
O(Ro)
in the
formal
asymptotic expansion in Rossby number
Ro\ll 1
of the thermal rotating shallow water (TRSW) equations. Namely,

(1.2)

\mathbf{u}:={{\bm{\widehat{z}}}}\times\nabla\psi=:{{\bm{\widehat{z}}}}\times\nabla\left(\zeta+\frac{1}{2}b\right)+O(Ro)\,.

In the asymptotic expansion approach to deriving the TQG equation, one now discovers a linear relation among the stream function
\psi
, the surface elevation
\zeta
and the buoyancy
b
at order
O(Ro)
as

(1.3)

\displaystyle\psi=\zeta+b/2+O(Ro)\,.

Imposing the linear relation (
1.3
) that arises from the assumed balance relation (
1.2
) at order
O(Ro)
in the asymptotic expansion of the TRSW equations now yields the TQG equations for the dynamics of potential vorticity (PV)
q
in the horizontal
(x,y)
plane which also involves the dynamics of the buoyancy
b
. The resulting TQG equations are given by
[
5
]

(1.4)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\partial_{t}q+\bm{u}\cdot\nabla q&={\bm{\widehat{z}}}\cdot\nabla\Big(\zeta-\frac{1}{2}h_{1}\Big)\times\nabla b=:{\bm{\widehat{z}}}\cdot\nabla\big(\psi-h_{1}/2\big)\times\nabla b\,,\\
\partial_{t}b+{\mathbf{u}}\cdot\nabla b&=0\quad\hbox{with}\quad q:=(\Delta-1)\psi+f_{1}\,,\end{split}

in which
f_{1}={\mathbf{\widehat{z}}}\cdot{\rm curl}{\mathbf{R}}(x,y)
with local rotation velocity
{\mathbf{R}}(x,y)
is the Coriolis parameter and
h_{1}(x,y)
is the bathymetry, both of whose gradients are assumed to be of order
O(Ro)
. Figure

shows a snapshot of the solution of the TQG equations for potential vorticity

The Kelvin circulation theorem for TQG

To write the Kelvin circulation theorem for TQG, we first introduce the convolution kernel
K
defined by its action on fluid velocity

(1.5)

\displaystyle K*\mathbf{u}=\big(1-\Delta^{-1}\big)\mathbf{u}\,.

The utility of the kernel
K
is that its curl summons the Helmholtz operator; namely,

(1.6)

\displaystyle{\rm curl}(K*\mathbf{u})=\big(\Delta-1\big)\psi\,.

The property (
1.6
) under the curl of the kernel
K
in (
1.5
) facilitates writing the Kelvin circulation theorem for TQG as

(1.7)

\displaystyle\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\oint_{c(u)}\big((K*{\mathbf{u}})+{\mathbf{R}}(x,y)\big)\cdot\,\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{x}}=\oint_{c(u)}\big(\psi-h_{1}/2\big)\nabla b\cdot\,\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{x}}\,.

Upon applying the Stokes theorem to the Kelvin circulation theorem for TQG in (
1.7
), one obtains the rate of change of the flux of potential vorticity
q
though a planar surface patch whose boundary follows the Kelvin loop,
c(u)
. Namely, with
{\bm{\widehat{z}}}\cdot{\rm curl}{\mathbf{R}}(x,y)=:f_{1}(x,y)
, one finds

(1.8)

\displaystyle\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\partial S=c(u)}\big((\Delta-1)\psi+f_{1}\big)\,\mathrm{d}x\wedge\,\mathrm{d}y=\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\partial S=c(u)}q\,\,\mathrm{d}x\wedge\,\mathrm{d}y=\int_{\partial S=c(u)}\,\mathrm{d}\big(\psi-h_{1}/2\big)\wedge\,\mathrm{d}b\,.

1.1.
Hamiltonian Stochastic Advection by Lie Transport (SALT) for TQG

Hamiltonian Stochastic Advection by Lie Transport (SALT) can be derived for TQG by augmenting its deterministic Hamiltonian in (
A.1
) to add a stochastic Hamiltonian whose flow under the Lie-Poisson bracket (
A.3
) induces a Stratonovich stochastic flow along the characteristics of the symplectic vector field generated by the stochastic Hamiltonian process
\langle\varpi\,,\,\varsigma(x,y)\rangle\circ dW_{t}
. The semimartingale total Hamiltonian is then given by

(1.11)

\displaystyle{\rm d}h={{\mathcal{H}}}_{TQG}(\varpi,b)\,\mathrm{d}t+\int_{{\mathcal{D}}}\varpi\,\varsigma(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y\circ\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}\,,

whose Lie-algebra valued variations with respect to the
dual
Lie-algebra valued variables
(\varpi;b)\in(f_{1}\circledS f_{2})^{*}
for TQG are expressed in semimartingale form as

(1.12)

\displaystyle\delta({\rm d}h)=\int\Big(\psi{\rm d}t+\varsigma(x,y)\circ\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}\Big)\delta\varpi+\Big(\frac{1}{2}(b+h_{1})\,\mathrm{d}t-\big(\psi{\rm d}t+\varsigma(x,y)\circ\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}\big)\Big)\delta b\,\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y\,,

with

(1.13)

\displaystyle\frac{\delta({\rm d}h)}{\delta\varpi}=\psi{\rm d}t+\varsigma(x,y)\circ\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}\quad\hbox{and}\quad\frac{\delta({\rm d}h)}{\delta b}=\frac{1}{2}(b+h_{1})\,\mathrm{d}t-\big(\psi{\rm d}t+\varsigma(x,y)\circ\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}\big)\,.

SALT TQG dynamics is then expressed geometrically using the coadjoint operator
{\rm ad}^{*}:\mathfrak{g}\times\mathfrak{g}^{*}\to\mathfrak{g}^{*}
in (
A.8
) in stochastic integral form as

(1.14)

\displaystyle\big({\rm d}\varpi;{\rm d}b\big)=-\,{\rm ad}^{*}_{\big({\delta({\rm d}h)}/{\delta\varpi}\,;\,{\delta({\rm d}h)}/{\delta b}\big)}\big(\varpi;b\big)\,.

Thus, SALT TQG solutions evolve by stochastic coadjoint motion under right semidirect-product action of symplectic diffeomorphisms on the dual of its Lie algebra with dual (momentum-map) variables
(\varpi;b)\in(f_{1}\circledS f_{2})^{*}
. The symplectic semimartingale vector fields

({\delta({\rm d}h)}/{\delta\varpi}\,;\,{\delta({\rm d}h)}/{\delta b})\in f_{1}\circledS f_{2}
whose characteristic curves generate the stochastic coadjoint solution behaviour are given in equations (
1.13
).

One may write the SALT TQG equations (
1.14
) in Hamiltonian matrix operator form as, cf. equation (
A.2
),

(1.15)

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\rm d}\begin{bmatrix}\varpi\\
b\end{bmatrix}&=\begin{bmatrix}J(\varpi,\,\cdot\,)&J(b,\,\cdot\,)\\
J(b,\,\cdot\,)&0\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}{\delta({\rm d}h)}/{\delta\varpi}=\psi{\rm d}t+\varsigma(x,y)\circ\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}\\
{\delta({\rm d}h)}/{\delta b}=\frac{1}{2}(b+h_{1})\,\mathrm{d}t-(\psi{\rm d}t+\varsigma(x,y)\circ\,\mathrm{d}W_{t})\end{bmatrix}\\
{\rm d}\begin{bmatrix}q\\
b\end{bmatrix}&=\begin{bmatrix}J\big(q-b,\,\psi{\rm d}t+\varsigma(x,y)\circ\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}\big)+J\big(b,\,\frac{1}{2}h_{1}\,\mathrm{d}t\big)\\
J\big(b,\,\psi{\rm d}t+\varsigma(x,y)\circ\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}\big)\end{bmatrix}\\
&=-\begin{bmatrix}\big(\bm{u}\,\mathrm{d}t+{\bm{\xi}}(x,y)\circ\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}\big)\cdot\nabla\big(q-b\big)+\mathbf{u}_{h}\cdot\nabla b\,\mathrm{d}t\\
\big(\bm{u}\,\mathrm{d}t+{\bm{\xi}}(x,y)\circ\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}\big)\cdot\nabla b\end{bmatrix}\,.\end{split}

In the last step here, we have introduced notation for the fixed symplectic vector field
{\bm{\xi}}(x,y):=\nabla^{\perp}\varsigma
that in principle must be obtained from observed data. Discussions of the methods for acquiring these fixed symplectic vector fields from observed data are beyond the scope of the present paper. However, our intention is to follow the methods of data analysis and data assimilation in
[
2
,
3
]
developed in our previous work for 2D Euler fluid equations and standard QG. Before passing to data analysis, though, one must study the solution properties of the SALT TQG equations. These solution properties of the Hamiltonian stochastic TQG equations in (
1.15
) will be investigated in the remainder of this paper.

The SALT approach via Hamilton’s variational principle preserves all of the geometric structure of the underlying deterministic equations
[
1
]
. However, as we have seen for TQG, a derivation of the deterministic equations using Hamilton’s variational principle may not always be available, especially when asymptotic expansions are applied to approximate higher level mathematical models. For TQG we have met this situation by using the residual Lie-Poisson Hamiltonian structure possessed by the TQG equations. Before we pass to the analysis of the solution properties of the SALT TQG equations, let us briefly mention an alternative approach to developing stochastic fluid equations in the QG family whose dynamics exactly preserves energy.

The SALT approach preserves ideal fluid flow properties such as the Kelvin circulation theorem and conservation laws arising from the Lie algebraic structure and Lie-Poisson Hamiltonian properties. These fluid circulation properties and conservation laws are physical principles which benefit both the theoretical analysis and the physicality of computational simulation results.

Remark 1.1

(Energy preservation arising from Stochastic Forcing by Lie Transport (SFLT))
.

Instead of SALT, one could have introduced an energy-preserving form of stochasticity for TQG. The energy-preserving SFLT approach can be written by taking the Lie-Poisson Hamiltonian matrix operator in (
1.15
) to be a semimartingale, as follows
[
8
]

(1.16)

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\rm d}\begin{bmatrix}\varpi\\
b\end{bmatrix}&=\begin{bmatrix}J(\varpi\,\mathrm{d}t+\xi(x,y)\circ\,\mathrm{d}W_{t},\,\cdot\,)&J(b\,\mathrm{d}t+\xi(x,y)\circ\,\mathrm{d}W_{t},\,\cdot\,)\\
J(b\,\mathrm{d}t+\xi(x,y)\circ\,\mathrm{d}W_{t},\,\cdot\,)&0\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}{\delta{{\mathcal{H}}}_{TQG}}/{\delta\varpi}=\psi\\
{\delta({{\mathcal{H}}}_{TQG})}/{\delta b}=\frac{1}{2}(b+h_{1})-\psi\end{bmatrix}\\
&=-\begin{bmatrix}(\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla)\big((\varpi-b)\,\mathrm{d}t+\xi(x,y)\circ\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}\big)-\nabla^{\perp}\frac{1}{2}(b+h_{1})\cdot\nabla\big(b\,\mathrm{d}t+\xi(x,y)\circ\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}\big)\\
(\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla)\big(b\,\mathrm{d}t+\xi(x,y)\circ\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}\big)\end{bmatrix}\,,\end{split}

with velocity
\mathbf{u}=\nabla^{\perp}\psi
. Because the Hamiltonian matrix operator for the Lie-Poisson bracket in (
1.16
) is skew-symmetic under the

pairing, one has energy conservation in the form
{\rm d}{{\mathcal{H}}}_{TQG}=0
.
Thus, SFLT preserves the deterministic energy Hamiltonian,
{{\mathcal{H}}}_{TQG}
, although its dynamics is stochastic. Here we do not follow the alternative SFLT approach, though, because it does not preserve the deterministic Casimir conservation laws in (
1.10
) that are preserved in the SALT approach by taking the deterministic form of the Lie-Poisson operator while taking the Hamiltonian to be a semimartingale. This approach has been applied to introduce SFLT into the 3D Primitive Equations for ocean circulation dynamics in
[
9
]

1.2.
Content and plan of the paper

In the sections above, we have explained the underlying geometric mechanics background for stochastic modelling with SALT in the case of TQG. This is done in parallel with the stochastic modelling with SALT of the classic problem of Euler-Boussinesq convection (EBC), which is considerably simpler than the case of TQG. Geometrically, the dynamics of both TQG and EBC are understood as coadjoint Hamiltonian motion generated by the semidirect-product action of the Lie algebra
(f_{1}\circledS f_{2})
on function pairs
(f_{1};f_{2})\in(f_{1}\circledS f_{2})^{*}
. This is the proper geometric framework for the application of the SALT approach to stochastic modelling in fluid dynamics.

In Section
2
, we begin by making precise the notion of a solution that we wish to construct, after which we state our main analytical results. Here, we are interested in the construction of a unique maximal strong pathwise solution to the SALT TQG equation. This solution is strong in both the stochastic and deterministic sense.

The proof of the existence of the solution described above will rely on ideas developed in
[
10
,
11
,
12
,
13
,
14
,
15
]
. In particular, the book
[
11
]
on the mathematical analysis of stochastic compressible fluids will serve as our main guide.
In this regard, we begin the proof of our main result in Section
2.3
where we construct a strong pathwise solution for an approximation of the SALT TQG whose nonlinearities have been truncated and are subjected to a subclass of initial conditions that have finite moments.
Here, we follow the classical Yamada–Watanabe-type argument where the pathwise solution is derived from the construction of a stochastically weak solution and the establishment of pathwise uniqueness for the truncated system. The construction of the stochastically weak solution is achieved from a finite-dimensional approximation.

Our next goal will be to show the existence of a strong pathwise solution for the original system by removing the cut-off functions introduced into the system as well as the restriction that the initial condition is of bounded moments. This is carried out in Section
2.4
. To begin with, we trade an appropriate stopping time for the cut-off function for the nonlinear terms. In so doing, we obtain a local strong pathwise solution for the original system in place of the solution obtained for the truncated system. Having removed the cut-offs, we then proceed to remove the additional boundedness assumption imposed on our initial conditions. This is done by introducing yet another cut-off function, but one which cuts the initial conditions rather than the nonlinear terms. This cut-off allows us to deduce the existence of a local strong pathwise solution for the original system subject to general initial conditions, by piecing together ‘smaller’ local solutions. Next, we deduce maximality of this local solutions after which we show uniqueness of these maximal solutions.
This is a straightforward adaptation of the earlier uniqueness result for the truncated system to subspaces
\Omega_{K}
of the sample space
\Omega

K\rightarrow\infty
to complete the proof of our main result in Theorem

In Section

which has since been extended to the stochastic setting by Crisan, Flandoli and Holm

(\bm{\xi}_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
,
\mathbf{u}_{h}
and
f
be given time-independent functions representing a collection of symplectic vector fields obtained from observed data, the skew bathymetry gradient, and the Coriolis parameter of a fluid, respectively.

The equations of Stochastic Advection by Lie Transport for the Thermal Quasi-Geostrophic (SALT TQG), or simply, the Stochastic Thermal Quasi-Geostrophic (STQG) equations which are written in matrix form in (
1.15
) may also be expressed equivalently as a pair of coupled equations governing the evolution of the buoyancy
b
and the potential vorticity
q
in the following way

(2.9)

\displaystyle\,\mathrm{d}b+(\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla)b\,\mathrm{d}t+(\bm{\xi}_{i}\cdot\nabla)b\circ\,\mathrm{d}W^{i}=0,

(2.10)

\displaystyle\,\mathrm{d}q+(\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla)(q-b)\,\mathrm{d}t+(\bm{\xi}_{i}\cdot\nabla)(q-b)\circ\,\mathrm{d}W^{i}=-(\mathbf{u}_{h}\cdot\nabla)b\,\mathrm{d}t,

(2.11)

\displaystyle b(0,\bm{x})=b_{0}(\bm{x}),\qquad q(0,\bm{x})=q_{0}(\bm{x}),

where we have applied the Einstein convention of summing repeated indices over their range and where

(2.12)

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}=\nabla^{\perp}\psi,\qquad\mathbf{u}_{h}=\frac{1}{2}\nabla^{\perp}h,\qquad q=(\Delta-1)\psi+f.

Here,
\psi
is the streamfunction and
h
is the spatial variation around a constant bathymetry profile.

Our given set of data is
((\bm{\xi}_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}},\mathbf{u}_{h},f,b_{0},q_{0})
with the following properties:

(2.13)

\displaystyle\bm{\xi}_{i}

\displaystyle\in W^{4,\infty}_{\mathrm{div}}(\mathbb{T}^{2}),\quad\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\|\bm{\xi}_{i}\|_{4,\infty}<\infty,\quad\mathbf{u}_{h}\in W^{3,2}_{\mathrm{div}}(\mathbb{T}^{2}),\quad f\in W^{2,2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle(b_{0},q_{0})\in W^{3,2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})\times W^{2,2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})\text{ is a pair of }\mathcal{F}_{0}\text{-measurable random variables.}

Remark 2.3
.

The assumption on the time-independent divergence-free
vector fields
\bm{\xi}_{i}:\mathbb{T}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
are to
ensure that the infinite sum of stochastic integrals in (
2.9
)–(
2.10
)
are well-defined.

Remark 2.4
.

Since we are working on the torus, and the velocity fields are defined by (
2.12
), we have in
particular,
\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\mathbf{u}_{h}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}=0
and
\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\mathbf{u}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}=0
.
Consequently, for simplicity, we will assume that all
functions under consideration have zero averages.

Remark 2.5
.

For our theoretical analysis, it is more convenient to express (
2.9
)–(
2.10
) in Itô form. To see clearly how Stratonovich to Itô conversion works for these equations, it is useful to rewrite them in the following compact form

(2.14)

\displaystyle\,\mathrm{d}\binom{b}{q}=\mathcal{A}\begin{pmatrix}b\\
q\end{pmatrix}\,\mathrm{d}t+\mathcal{G}_{i}\begin{pmatrix}b\\
q\end{pmatrix}\circ\,\mathrm{d}W^{i},\qquad\mathcal{A}:=-\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla&0\\[3.00003pt]
(\mathbf{u}_{h}-\mathbf{u})\cdot\nabla&\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla\end{bmatrix},\quad\mathcal{G}_{i}:=-\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\xi}_{i}\cdot\nabla&0\\[3.00003pt]
-\bm{\xi}_{i}\cdot\nabla&\bm{\xi}_{i}\cdot\nabla\end{bmatrix}.

Furthermore, the conversion from the Stratonovich to Itô integral
\mathcal{G}_{i}\mathbf{g}\circ\,\mathrm{d}W^{i}\mapsto\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{G}_{i}^{2}\mathbf{g}\,\mathrm{d}t+\mathcal{G}_{i}\mathbf{g}\,\mathrm{d}W^{i}
,
\mathbf{g}:=(b,q)^{T}
yields the following equivalent form for (
2.9
)–(
2.10
)

(2.15)

\displaystyle\,\mathrm{d}b+(\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla)b\,\mathrm{d}t-\frac{1}{2}(\bm{\xi}_{i}\cdot\nabla)(\bm{\xi}_{i}\cdot\nabla)b\,\mathrm{d}t+(\bm{\xi}_{i}\cdot\nabla)b\,\,\mathrm{d}W^{i}=0,

(2.16)

\displaystyle\,\mathrm{d}q+(\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla)(q-b)\,\mathrm{d}t-\frac{1}{2}(\bm{\xi}_{i}\cdot\nabla)(\bm{\xi}_{i}\cdot\nabla)(q-2b)\,\mathrm{d}t+(\bm{\xi}_{i}\cdot\nabla)(q-b)\,\mathrm{d}W^{i}=-(\mathbf{u}_{h}\cdot\nabla)b\,\mathrm{d}t.

Our main goal is to construct a solution of (
2.9
)–(
2.12
) that lives on a maximal time interval and that is strong in both PDE and probabilistic sense. To make this clearer, let us first make the following definitions.

Definition 2.6

(Local strong pathwise solution)
.

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0},\mathbb{P})
be a stochastic basis and
(W^{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
a sequence of independent one-dimensional Brownian motions adapted to the complete and right-continuous filtration
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
. Let
((\bm{\xi}_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}},\mathbf{u}_{h},f,b_{0},q_{0})
be a dataset satisfying (
2.13
). A triplet
(b,q,\tau)
is called a
local strong pathwise solution
of (
2.9
)–(
2.12
) if:

•

\tau
is a
\mathbb{P}
-a.s. strictly positive
(\mathcal{F}_{t})
-stopping time;

•

(b,q)
is a pair of
(\mathcal{F}_{t})
-progressively measurable stochastic processes such that

\displaystyle b(\cdot\wedge\tau)\in C([0,T];W^{3,2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})),\qquad q(\cdot\wedge\tau)\in C([0,T];W^{2,2}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))\quad\mathbb{P}\text{-a.s.;}

•

the equations

\displaystyle b(t\wedge\tau)=

\displaystyle b_{0}-\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau}(\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla)b\,\mathrm{d}\sigma+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau}(\bm{\xi}_{i}\cdot\nabla)(\bm{\xi}_{i}\cdot\nabla)b\,\mathrm{d}\sigma-\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau}(\bm{\xi}_{i}\cdot\nabla)b\,\mathrm{d}W^{i}_{\sigma},

\displaystyle q(t\wedge\tau)=

\displaystyle q_{0}-\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau}(\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla)(q-b)\,\mathrm{d}\sigma-\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau}(\mathbf{u}_{h}\cdot\nabla)b\,\mathrm{d}\sigma+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau}(\bm{\xi}_{i}\cdot\nabla)(\bm{\xi}_{i}\cdot\nabla)(q-{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}\bf{2}}b)\,\mathrm{d}\sigma

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau}(\bm{\xi}_{i}\cdot\nabla)(q-b)\,\mathrm{d}W^{i}_{\sigma},

hold
\mathbb{P}
-a.s. for all
t\in[0,T]
.

Definition 2.7

(Maximal strong pathwise solution)
.

Fix the preamble as contained in Definition
2.6
. A set
(b,q,(\tau_{R})_{R\in\mathbb{N}},\tau)
is called a
maximal strong pathwise solution
of (
2.9
)–(
2.12
) if:

•

\tau
is a
\mathbb{P}
-a.s. strictly positive
(\mathcal{F}_{t})
-stopping time;

•

(\tau_{R})_{R\in\mathbb{N}}
is an increasing sequence of
(\mathcal{F}_{t})
-stopping times such that
\tau_{R}<\tau
on the set
[\tau<T]
,
\lim_{R\rightarrow\infty}\tau_{R}=\tau
a.s. and

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,\tau_{R}]}\big(\|\nabla b(t)\|_{\infty}+\|\nabla\mathbf{u}(t)\|_{\infty}+\|q(t)\|_{\infty}\big)\geq R\quad\text{on}\quad[\tau<T];

•

each
(b,q,\tau_{R}),R\in\mathbb{N}
is a local strong pathwise solution.

Remark 2.8
.

Here,
\tau
marks the maximal lifespan of the solution, which is determined by the time of explosion of the
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
-norm of either the buoyancy gradient, the velocity gradient, or the potential vorticity. Indeed, by constructing a log-Sobolov estimate in the spirit of Beale–Kato–Madja
[
16
, Eq. (15)]
for the Euler equation, one may control the lifespan of the STQG by just the buoyancy gradient or the potential vorticity.

Remark 2.9
.

Our maximal solution is defined within the fixed, but arbitrary, interval
[0,T]
. By piecing together these finite interval maximal solutions, we can obtain a maximal solution without a constraint on a final time
T
.

With these definitions in hand, we are now in the position to state our main result.

Theorem 2.10

(Existence of a unique maximal solution)
.

For
((\bm{\xi}_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}},\mathbf{u}_{h},f,b_{0},q_{0})
satisfying (
2.13
), there exists a unique maximal strong pathwise
solution
(b,q,(\tau_{R})_{R\in\mathbb{N}},\tau)
of (
2.9
)–(
2.12
).

Remark 2.11
.

Uniqueness of a maximal strong pathwise solution as stated in our main result is to be understood in the sense that only the triplet
(b,q,\tau)
is unique.

In the following, we state a weak stability result for a superset of the maximal strong pathwise solutions to be constructed in Theorem
2.10
. This will consist of solutions that emanate from a subset of the dataset (
2.13
) with bounded-in-
\omega
initial conditions
(b_{0},q_{0})
.

Theorem 2.12

(Weak continuity with respect to bounded initial data)
.

Define
G(t)
as follow

(2.17)

\displaystyle G(t):=c\,\bigg(1+\|\mathbf{u}_{h}\|_{3,2}+\|f\|_{2,2}+\sum_{i=1}^{2}\|b_{i}(t)\|_{3,2}^{3}+\sum_{i=1}^{2}\|q_{i}(t)\|_{2,2}^{3}\bigg).

Any pair of maximal strong pathwise solutions
(b^{j},q^{j},(\tau_{R}^{j})_{R\in\mathbb{N}},\tau^{j})
,
j=1,2
with respective dataset
((\bm{\xi}_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}},\mathbf{u}_{h},f,b_{0}^{j},q_{0}^{j})
,
j=1,2
satisfying (
2.13
) and
(b_{0}^{j},q_{0}^{j})\in L^{\infty}(\Omega;W^{3,2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})\times W^{2,2}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))
,
i=1,2
will satisfy the bound

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[e^{-\int_{0}^{t}G(\sigma)\,\mathrm{d}\sigma}\big(\|(b^{1}-b^{2})(t)\|_{2,2}^{2}+\|(q^{1}-q^{2})(t)\|_{1,2}^{2}\big)\Big]\leq\mathbb{E}\big(\|b^{1}_{0}-b^{2}_{0}\|_{2,2}^{2}+\|q^{1}_{0}-q^{2}_{0}\|_{1,2}^{2}\big)

\mathbb{P}
-a.s. for all
t\in[0,\tau^{1}\wedge\tau^{2})
.

We now devote the following subsections to the proof of Theorem
2.10
.

2.3.
Strong pathwise solutions of the truncated system

In the following, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we let
P_{n}
be the orthogonal projection operator mapping
L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
onto
X_{n}:=\mathrm{span}\{\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{n}\}
where
\{\phi_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is a complete
orthonormal system, see
[
11
, Page 104]
,
[
22
, Chapter 3]
for further details. In particular, we recall that the
P_{n}
’s are continuous operators on all Hilbert spaces under consideration.
Finally, for a fixed
R>0
, we let
\theta_{R}:[0,\infty)\rightarrow[0,1]
be a smooth cut-off function satisfying

\displaystyle\theta_{R}(z)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lr}1&:0\leq z\leq R,\\
0&:z\geq R+1.\end{array}\right.

Now we consider the dataset
((\bm{\xi}_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}},\mathbf{u}_{h},f,b_{0},q_{0})
satisfying

(2.20)

\displaystyle

\displaystyle\bm{\xi}_{i}\in W^{4,\infty}_{\mathrm{div}}(\mathbb{T}^{2}),\qquad\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\|\bm{\xi}_{i}\|_{4,\infty}<\infty,\qquad\mathbf{u}_{h}\in W^{3,2}_{\mathrm{div}}(\mathbb{T}^{2}),\qquad f\in W^{2,2}(\mathbb{T}^{2}),

\displaystyle(b_{0},

\displaystyle q_{0})\in L^{\infty}\big(\Omega;W^{3,2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})\times W^{2,2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})\big)\text{ is a pair of }\mathcal{F}_{0}\text{-measurable random variables.}

Our goal now is to construct a solution
(b,q)
of

(2.21)

\displaystyle\,\mathrm{d}b+\theta_{R}[(\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla)b]\,\mathrm{d}t+(\bm{\xi}_{i}\cdot\nabla)b\circ\,\mathrm{d}W^{i}=0,

(2.22)

\displaystyle\,\mathrm{d}q+\theta_{R}[(\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla)(q-b)]\,\mathrm{d}t+(\mathbf{u}_{h}\cdot\nabla)b\,\mathrm{d}t+(\bm{\xi}_{i}\cdot\nabla)(q-b)\circ\,\mathrm{d}W^{i}=0,

(2.23)

\displaystyle b(0,x)=b_{0}(x),\qquad q(0,x)=q_{0}(x)

in
W^{3,2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})\times W^{2,2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
for the given dataset (
2.20
) and where

(2.24)

\displaystyle\theta_{R}:=\theta_{R}(\|\nabla b\|_{\infty}+\|\nabla\mathbf{u}\|_{\infty}+\|q\|_{\infty})

is the cut-off function (
2.3
).
As before,

(2.25)

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}=\nabla^{\perp}\psi,\qquad\mathbf{u}_{h}=\frac{1}{2}\nabla^{\perp}h,\qquad q=(\Delta-1)\psi+f.

We will achieve this goal by using a Galerkin approximation. In this regard, for the given data (
2.20
), we can first construct a finite-dimensional solution
(b_{n},q_{n})\in L^{2}(\Omega;C([0,T];X_{n}\times X_{n})
of

(2.26)

\displaystyle\,\mathrm{d}b_{n}+\theta_{R}^{n}P_{n}[(\mathbf{u}_{n}\cdot\nabla)b_{n}]\,\mathrm{d}t+\frac{1}{2}P_{n}\big[(\bm{\xi}_{i}\cdot\nabla)P_{n}[(\bm{\xi}_{i}\cdot\nabla)b_{n}]\big]\,\mathrm{d}t+P_{n}[(\bm{\xi}_{i}\cdot\nabla)b_{n}]\,\mathrm{d}W^{i}=0,

\displaystyle\,\mathrm{d}q_{n}+\theta_{R}^{n}P_{n}[(\mathbf{u}_{n}\cdot\nabla)(q_{n}-b_{n})]\,\mathrm{d}t+\frac{1}{2}P_{n}\big[(\bm{\xi}_{i}\cdot\nabla)P_{n}[(\bm{\xi}_{i}\cdot\nabla)(q_{n}-2b_{n})]\big]\,\mathrm{d}t+P_{n}[(\mathbf{u}_{h}\cdot\nabla)b_{n}]\,\mathrm{d}t

(2.27)

\displaystyle+P_{n}[(\bm{\xi}_{i}\cdot\nabla)(q_{n}-b_{n})]\,\mathrm{d}W^{i}_{t}=0,

(2.28)

\displaystyle b_{0,n}:=b_{n}(0,x)=P_{n}b_{0}(x),\qquad q_{0,n}:=q_{n}(0,x)=P_{n}q_{0}(x),

where

(2.29)

\displaystyle\theta_{R}^{n}:=\theta_{R}(\|\nabla b_{n}\|_{\infty}+\|\nabla\mathbf{u}_{n}\|_{\infty}+\|q_{n}\|_{\infty})

and where

(2.30)

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}_{n}=\nabla^{\perp}\psi_{n},\qquad\mathbf{u}_{h}=\frac{1}{2}\nabla^{\perp}h,\qquad q_{n}=(\Delta-1)\psi_{n}+f.

Indeed, since this is a finite dimensional system of SDEs with locally Lipschitz drift coefficients and globally Lipschitz diffusion coefficients, by a standard theorem, see for instant
[
23
]
, we can infer the existence of a unique local solution
(b_{n},q_{n})
.
The fact that the solutions are global will follow from the following a priori estimates.

2.3.1.
Uniform estimates

We now aim to show uniform bounds for
(b_{n},q_{n})
in
W^{3,2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})\times W^{2,2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
.
To do this, we apply
\partial^{\beta}
to (
2.26
) with
|\beta|\leq 3
to obtain

(2.31)

\displaystyle\,\mathrm{d}\partial^{\beta}b_{n}

\displaystyle+\theta_{R}^{n}P_{n}[(\mathbf{u}_{n}\cdot\nabla\partial^{\beta})b_{n}]\,\mathrm{d}t+\frac{1}{2}\partial^{\beta}P_{n}\big[(\bm{\xi}_{i}\cdot\nabla)P_{n}[(\bm{\xi}_{i}\cdot\nabla)b_{n}]\big]\,\mathrm{d}t+\partial^{\beta}P_{n}[(\bm{\xi}_{i}\cdot\nabla)b_{n}]\,\mathrm{d}W^{i}_{t}=S_{1}\,\mathrm{d}t

where

\displaystyle S_{1}

\displaystyle:=\theta_{R}^{n}\big[P_{n}(\mathbf{u}_{n}\cdot\partial^{\beta}\nabla)b_{n}-\partial^{\beta}P_{n}((\mathbf{u}_{n}\cdot\nabla)b_{n})\big]

is such that

(2.32)

\displaystyle\|S_{1}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\theta_{R}^{n}\big(\|\nabla\mathbf{u}_{n}\|_{\infty}\|\nabla b_{n}\|_{2,2}+\|\nabla b_{n}\|_{\infty}\|\mathbf{u}_{n}\|_{3,2}\big)\lesssim\theta_{R}^{n}\big(\|b_{n}\|_{3,2}+(1+\|q_{n}\|_{2,2})\big)

holds uniformly in
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Indeed, the constant only depends on
\|f\|_{2,2}
through Lemma
2.1
applied to (
2.30
).
If we now apply Itô’s formula to the mapping
t\mapsto\|\partial^{\beta}b_{n}(t)\|_{2}^{2}
with
|\beta|\leq 3
, we obtain

(2.33)

\displaystyle\|\partial^{\beta}b_{n}(t)\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|\partial^{\beta}b_{n}(0)\|_{2}^{2}-2\int_{0}^{t}\theta_{R}^{n}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}[(\mathbf{u}_{n}\cdot\nabla\partial^{\beta})b_{n}]\partial^{\beta}b_{n}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma

\displaystyle+2\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}S_{1}\partial^{\beta}b_{n}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma-2\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\partial^{\beta}[(\bm{\xi}_{i}\cdot\nabla)b_{n}]\partial^{\beta}b_{n}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\,\mathrm{d}W^{i}_{\sigma}

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{t}\big\|P_{n}\partial^{\beta}[(\bm{\xi}_{i}\cdot\nabla)b_{n}]\big\|_{2}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma-\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\partial^{\beta}\big[(\bm{\xi}_{i}\cdot\nabla)P_{n}[(\bm{\xi}_{i}\cdot\nabla)b_{n}]\big]\,\partial^{\beta}b_{n}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma

\mathbb{P}
-a.s for all
t\in[0,T]
. By squaring the resulting equation above, we obtain the following inequality

(2.34)

\displaystyle\|\partial^{\beta}b_{n}(t)\|_{2}^{4}

\displaystyle\leq\|\partial^{\beta}b_{n}(0)\|_{2}^{4}+\bigg(2\int_{0}^{t}\theta_{R}^{n}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}[(\mathbf{u}_{n}\cdot\nabla\partial^{\beta})b_{n}]\partial^{\beta}b_{n}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma\bigg)^{2}

\displaystyle+\bigg(2\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}S_{1}\partial^{\beta}b_{n}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\bigg)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma+\bigg(2\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\partial^{\beta}[(\bm{\xi}_{i}\cdot\nabla)b_{n}]\partial^{\beta}b_{n}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\,\mathrm{d}W^{i}_{\sigma}\bigg)^{2}

\displaystyle+\bigg(\int_{0}^{t}\big\|P_{n}\partial^{\beta}[(\bm{\xi}_{i}\cdot\nabla)b_{n}]\big\|_{2}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\partial^{\beta}\big[(\bm{\xi}_{i}\cdot\nabla)P_{n}[(\bm{\xi}_{i}\cdot\nabla)b_{n}]\big]\,\partial^{\beta}b_{n}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma\bigg)^{2}

for all
t\in[0,T]
. However, we note that by integration by parts,

\displaystyle 2\int_{0}^{t}\theta_{R}^{n}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}[(\mathbf{u}_{n}\cdot\nabla\partial^{\beta})b_{n}]\partial^{\beta}b_{n}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma=0

since our fluid is incompressible. Now, if we use (
2.32
), we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}

\displaystyle\sum_{|\beta|\leq 3}\bigg(\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}S_{1}\partial^{\beta}b_{n}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma\bigg)^{2}\lesssim\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{T}\theta_{R}^{n}\big(1+\|b_{n}\|_{3,2}^{4}+\|q_{n}\|_{2,2}^{4}\big)\,\mathrm{d}\sigma.

Next, by the Burkholder–Davis–Gundy inequality and (
2.20
), we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{|\beta|\leq 3}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\bigg|\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\partial^{\beta}[(\bm{\xi}_{i}\cdot\nabla)b_{n}]\partial^{\beta}b_{n}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\,\mathrm{d}W^{i}_{\sigma}\bigg|^{2}\bigg]

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{|\beta|\leq 3}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{0}^{T}\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\partial^{\beta}[(\bm{\xi}_{i}\cdot\nabla)b_{n}]\partial^{\beta}b_{n}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\bigg)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\bigg]

\displaystyle\lesssim\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{T}\|b_{n}\|_{3,2}^{4}\,\mathrm{d}t.

Finally, by using Lemma
2.2
, we also have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\sum_{|\beta|\leq 3}\bigg(\int_{0}^{t}\big\|P_{n}\partial^{\beta}[(\bm{\xi}_{i}\cdot\nabla)b_{n}]\big\|_{2}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\partial^{\beta}\big[(\bm{\xi}_{i}\cdot\nabla)P_{n}[(\bm{\xi}_{i}\cdot\nabla)b_{n}]\big]\,\partial^{\beta}b_{n}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma\bigg)^{2}\lesssim\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{T}\|b_{n}\|_{3,2}^{4}\,\mathrm{d}t.

By summing over
|\beta|\leq 3
,
we can therefore conclude from (
2.34
) that

(2.35)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}

\displaystyle\|b_{n}(t)\|_{3,2}^{4}\leq\mathbb{E}\|b_{n}(0)\|_{3,2}^{4}+c\,\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{T}\big(1+\|b_{n}\|_{3,2}^{4}+\|q_{n}\|_{2,2}^{4}\big)\,\mathrm{d}\sigma

where

.

Next, we apply
\partial^{\beta}
to (
2.27
) with
|\beta|\leq 2
to get

\displaystyle\,\mathrm{d}\partial^{\beta}q_{n}

\displaystyle+\theta_{R}^{n}P_{n}(\mathbf{u}_{n}\cdot\nabla\partial^{\beta})(q_{n}-b_{n})\,\mathrm{d}t+P_{n}(\mathbf{u}_{h}\cdot\nabla\partial^{\beta})b_{n}\,\mathrm{d}t+\frac{1}{2}\partial^{\beta}P_{n}\big[(\bm{\xi}_{i}\cdot\nabla)P_{n}[(\bm{\xi}_{i}\cdot\nabla)(q_{n}-2b_{n})]\big]\,\mathrm{d}t

where

\displaystyle S_{2}

\displaystyle:=-\theta_{R}^{n}\big[P_{n}(\mathbf{u}_{n}\cdot\partial^{\beta}\nabla)b_{n}-\partial^{\beta}P_{n}(\mathbf{u}_{n}\cdot\nabla b_{n})\big],

\displaystyle S_{3}

\displaystyle:=\theta_{R}^{n}\big[P_{n}(\mathbf{u}_{n}\cdot\partial^{\beta}\nabla)q_{n}-\partial^{\beta}P_{n}(\mathbf{u}_{n}\cdot\nabla q_{n})\big],

\displaystyle S_{4}

\displaystyle:=-\big[P_{n}(\mathbf{u}_{h}\cdot\partial^{\beta}\nabla)b_{n}-\partial^{\beta}P_{n}(\mathbf{u}_{h}\cdot\nabla b_{n})\big]

are such that

(2.36)

\displaystyle\|S_{2}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\theta_{R}^{n}\big(\|\nabla\mathbf{u}_{n}\|_{\infty}\|\nabla b_{n}\|_{1,2}+\|\nabla b_{n}\|_{\infty}\|\mathbf{u}_{n}\|_{2,2}\big)\lesssim\theta_{R}^{n}\big(\|b_{n}\|_{3,2}+(1+\|q_{n}\|_{2,2})\big),

\displaystyle\|S_{3}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\theta_{R}^{n}\big(\|\nabla\mathbf{u}_{n}\|_{\infty}\|\nabla q_{n}\|_{1,2}+\|\nabla q_{n}\|_{4}\|\nabla\mathbf{u}_{n}\|_{1,4}\big)

\displaystyle\lesssim\theta_{R}^{n}\big(\|\nabla\mathbf{u}_{n}\|_{\infty}\|\nabla q_{n}\|_{1,2}+\|q_{n}\|_{\infty}^{1/2}\|\nabla q_{n}\|_{1,2}^{1/2}\|\nabla\mathbf{u}_{n}\|_{\infty}^{1/2}\|\nabla\mathbf{u}_{n}\|_{2,2}^{1/2}\big)

(2.37)

\displaystyle\lesssim\theta_{R}^{n}\big(1+\|q_{n}\|_{2,2}\big),

(2.38)

Also, by the Burkholder–Davis–Gundy inequality and (

(2.41)

\displaystyle\sum_{|\beta|\leq 2}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\bigg|2\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\partial^{\beta}[(\bm{\xi}_{i}\cdot\nabla)(q_{n}-b_{n})]\partial^{\beta}q_{n}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\,\mathrm{d}W^{i}_{\sigma}\bigg|\bigg]^{2}

.
Finally, we also obtain from Lemma
2.2

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\sum_{|\beta|\leq 2}\bigg(\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}

\displaystyle\|q_{n}(t)\|_{2,2}^{4}\leq\mathbb{E}\|q_{n}(0)\|_{2,2}^{4}+c\,\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{t}\big(1+\|b_{n}\|_{3,2}^{4}+\|q_{n}\|_{2,2}^{4}\big)\,\mathrm{d}\sigma

where

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}

\displaystyle+c\,\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{T}(\theta_{R}^{n})^{2}\big(1+\|b_{n}(\sigma)\|_{3,2}^{4}+\|q_{n}(\sigma)\|_{2,2}^{4}\big)\,\mathrm{d}\sigma

\displaystyle\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbb{E}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}

\displaystyle\big(\|b_{n}(t)\|_{3,2}^{4}+\|q_{n}(t)\|_{2,2}^{4}\big)\lesssim 1.

2.3.2.
Hölder continuity in time

We can now proceed to show Hölder continuity in time of
\{(b_{n},\omega_{n})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
. It will follow from the next lemma in conjunction with Kolmogorov’s continuity result.

Lemma 2.13
.

For
((\bm{\xi}_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}},\mathbf{u}_{h},f,b_{0},q_{0})
satisfying (
2.20
), we let
\{(b_{n},q_{n})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be the corresponding family of Galerkin approximations solving (
2.26
)–(
2.30
). Then for all
s,t\in[0,T]
,
s<t
, the estimate

(2.47)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|b_{n}(t)-b_{n}(s)\|_{2}^{4}+\mathbb{E}\|q_{n}(t)-q_{n}(s)\|_{2}^{4}\lesssim_{R,T}|t-s|^{2}

holds uniformly in
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof.

In the following, we will only show that

(2.48)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|q_{n}(t)-q_{n}(s)\|_{2}^{4}\lesssim_{R,T}|t-s|^{2}

since the estimate for the buoyancy difference is similar and in fact easier.

From (
2.27
), we observe that for any
s,t\in[0,T]
,
s<t

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|q_{n}(t)-q_{n}(s)\|_{2}^{4}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\Big\|\int_{s}^{t}\theta_{R}^{n}P_{n}(\mathbf{u}_{n}\cdot\nabla)(q_{n}-b_{n})\,\mathrm{d}\sigma\Big\|_{2}^{4}+\mathbb{E}\Big\|\int_{s}^{t}P_{n}\frac{1}{2}[(\bm{\xi}_{i}\cdot\nabla)(\bm{\xi}_{i}\cdot\nabla)](q_{n}-b_{n})\,\mathrm{d}\sigma\Big\|_{2}^{4}

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}\Big\|\int_{s}^{t}P_{n}(\mathbf{u}_{h}\cdot\nabla)b_{n}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma\Big\|_{2}^{4}+\mathbb{E}\Big\|\int_{s}^{t}P_{n}(\bm{\xi}_{i}\cdot\nabla)(q_{n}-b_{n})\,\mathrm{d}W^{i}_{\sigma}\Big\|_{2}^{4}

\displaystyle=:I_{1}+I_{2}+I_{3}+I_{4}

holds
\mathbb{P}
-a.s. where by the continuity property of
P_{n}
,

(2.49)

\displaystyle I_{1}

\displaystyle\lesssim|t-s|^{4}\,\mathbb{E}\sup_{\sigma\in[0,T]}\theta_{R}^{n}\|(\mathbf{u}_{n}(\sigma)\cdot\nabla)(q_{n}(\sigma)-b_{n}(\sigma))\|_{2}^{4}

\displaystyle\lesssim T^{2}|t-s|^{2}\,\mathbb{E}\sup_{\sigma\in[0,T]}\theta_{R}^{n}\|\mathbf{u}_{n}(\sigma)\|_{\infty}^{4}\|\nabla(q_{n}(\sigma)-b_{n}(\sigma))\|_{2}^{4}

\displaystyle\lesssim_{R,T}|t-s|^{2}\,\mathbb{E}\sup_{\sigma\in[0,T]}\big(\|b_{n}(\sigma)\|_{3,2}^{4}+\|q_{n}(\sigma)\|_{2,2}^{4}\big)

\displaystyle\lesssim_{R,T}|t-s|^{2}

where the last estimate follow from (
2.46
). The same estimate holds for
I_{2}
and
I_{3}
.
For the stochastic term, we use Burkholder–Davis–Gundy inequality and the continuity of
P_{n}
to obtain

(2.50)

\displaystyle I_{4}

\displaystyle\lesssim\mathbb{E}\bigg(\int_{s}^{t}\|(\bm{\xi}_{i}\cdot\nabla)(q_{n}-b_{n})\|_{2}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma\bigg)^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim_{R}|t-s|^{2}\,\mathbb{E}\sup_{\sigma\in[0,T]}\|(\bm{\xi}_{i}\cdot\nabla)(q_{n}-b_{n})\|_{2}^{4}

\displaystyle\lesssim_{R}|t-s|^{2}\,\mathbb{E}\sup_{\sigma\in[0,T]}\big(\|b_{n}(\sigma)\|_{3,2}^{4}+\|q_{n}(\sigma)\|_{2,2}^{4}\big)

\displaystyle\lesssim_{R}|t-s|^{2}.

Summing the two estimates above yields our desired result.
∎

We observe that due to Kolmogorov’s continuity theorem, an immediate consequence of Lemma
2.13
is the following.

Corollary 2.14
.

For
((\bm{\xi}_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}},\mathbf{u}_{h},f,b_{0},q_{0})
satisfying (
2.20
), we let
\{(b_{n},q_{n})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be the corresponding family of Galerkin approximations solving (
2.26
)–(
2.30
). Then there exists a representation of
(b_{n},q_{n})
with
\mathbb{P}
-a.s. Hölder continuous trajectories
of exponent
\gamma\in(0,1/4)
. Furthermore, the estimate

(2.51)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|b_{n}\|_{C^{\gamma}([0,T];L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}^{4}+\mathbb{E}\|q_{n}\|_{C^{\gamma}([0,T];L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}^{4}\lesssim 1

holds uniformly in
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

2.3.3.
Compactness

Our goal now is to define a family of measures associated with
\{(b_{n},q_{n},\{W^{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and then show that these measures are tight in suitable spaces.
In order to achieve our goal, we first define the path space

\displaystyle\chi=C([0,T];W^{s,2}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))\times C([0,T];W^{s-1,2}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))\times W^{3,2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})\times W^{2,2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})\times C([0,T])^{\mathbb{N}}.

where
s\in(2,3)
is close enough to
3
. Here,
S^{\mathbb{N}}
denotes the
\mathbb{N}
th product space of
S
. Also note that the embeddings
W^{s,2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})\hookrightarrow W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
and
W^{s-1,2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})\hookrightarrow L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
are continuous.
We then define the following probability measures:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}[{b_{n}}]\text{ is the law of }b_{n}\text{ on }C([0,T];W^{s,2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})),

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}[{q_{n}}]\text{ is the law of }q_{n}\text{ on }C([0,T];W^{s-1,2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})),

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}[b_{0,n}]\text{ is the law of }b_{0,n}\text{ on }W^{3,2}(\mathbb{T}^{2}),

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}[q_{0,n}]\text{ is the law of }q_{0,n}\text{ on }W^{2,2}(\mathbb{T}^{2}),

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}[W^{i}]\text{ is the law of }W^{i}\text{ on }C([0,T]),

for each
i\in\mathbb{N}
.
Now, by combining (
2.46
) and Corollary
2.14
with the abstract Arzelá–Ascoli theorem
[
24
, Theorem 1.1.1.]
, we can obtain the following result.

Lemma 2.15
.

The family of laws
\{\mathcal{L}[{b_{n}}]:n\in\mathbb{N}\}
is tight on
C([0,T];W^{s,2}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))
.
The family of laws
\{\mathcal{L}[{q_{n}}]:n\in\mathbb{N}\}
is tight on
C([0,T];W^{s-1,2}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))
.
The family of laws
\{\mathcal{L}[b_{0,n}]:n\in\mathbb{N}\}
is tight on
W^{3,2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
.
The family of laws
\{\mathcal{L}[q_{0,n}]:n\in\mathbb{N}\}
is tight on
W^{2,2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
.

Proof.

Due to the compact embedding

\displaystyle C([0,T];W^{3,2}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))\cap C^{\gamma}([0,T];L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))\hookrightarrow C([0,T];W^{s,2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})),

which holds for
\gamma>0
and
s<3
, see
[
24
, Proposition 5.2.5.]
, it follows that for any fixed
L>0
, the set

\displaystyle B_{L}:=\big\{b\in C([0,T];W^{3,2}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))\cap C^{\gamma}([0,T];L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))\,:\,\|b\|_{C([0,T];W^{3,2}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))\cap C^{\gamma}([0,T];L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}\leq L\big\}

is compact in
C([0,T];W^{s,2}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))
. Moreover, by Chebyshev’s inequality,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}[b_{n}](B_{L}^{c})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\big(\|b_{n}\|_{C([0,T];W^{3,2}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))\cap C^{\gamma}([0,T];L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}>L\big)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\Big(\|b_{n}\|_{C([0,T];W^{3,2}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}>L/2\big)+\mathbb{P}\big(\|b_{n}\|_{C^{\gamma}([0,T];L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}>L/2\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{16}{L^{4}}\mathbb{E}\Big(\|b_{n}(t)\|_{C([0,T];W^{3,2}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}^{4}+\|b_{n}(t)\|_{C^{\gamma}([0,T];L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}^{4}\Big)

so that by (
2.46
) and Corollary
2.14
,

\displaystyle\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\mathcal{L}[b_{n}](B_{L}^{c})\lesssim\frac{1}{L}\rightarrow 0

as
L\rightarrow\infty
. This completes the proof of the first part of the lemma. The second part can be done analogously.
For the third part, we use the continuity property of the projection
P_{n}
to get that
b_{0,n}:=P_{n}b_{0}\rightarrow b_{0}
a.s. This implies convergence in law which further implies tightness due to Prokhorov’s theorem. The fourth part follows the same argument.
∎

We also note that since for each
i\in\mathbb{N}
,
\{\mathcal{L}[W^{i}]\}
is a singleton, it is trivially
weakly compact and hence tight on
C([0,T])
.
An immediate consequence of this remark and the lemma above is that:

Corollary 2.16
.

The family of measures
\{\mathcal{L}[{b_{n},q_{n},b_{0,n},q_{0,n},\{W^{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}}]:n\in\mathbb{N}\}
is tight on
\chi
.

With tightness of the measures in hand, we can now proceed to identify the limits of the sequence of functions by way of the Jakubowski–Skorokhod representation theorem
[
25
]
. See also,
[
11
, Theorem 2.7.1]
.

Proposition 2.17
.

There exists a complete probability space
(\tilde{\Omega},\tilde{\mathcal{F}},\tilde{\mathbb{P}})
with
\chi
-valued
Borel measurable random variables
\{(\tilde{b}_{n},\tilde{q}_{n},\tilde{b}_{0,n},\tilde{q}_{0,n},\{\tilde{W}^{i}_{n}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(\tilde{b},\tilde{q},\tilde{b}_{0},\tilde{q}_{0},\{\tilde{W}^{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}})
such that (up to a subsequence, not relabelled):

•

the law of
\{(\tilde{b}_{n},\tilde{q}_{n},\tilde{b}_{0,n},\tilde{q}_{0,n},\{\tilde{W}^{i}_{n}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}

the following convergences

(2.52)

\displaystyle\tilde{b}_{n}\rightarrow\tilde{b}\qquad\text{in}\qquad C([0,T];W^{s,2}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))

(2.53)

\displaystyle\tilde{q}_{n}\rightarrow\tilde{q}\qquad\text{in}\qquad C([0,T];W^{s-1,2}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))

(2.54)

\displaystyle\tilde{b}_{0,n}\rightarrow\tilde{b}_{0}\qquad\text{in}\qquad W^{3,2}(\mathbb{T}^{2}),

(2.55)

\displaystyle\tilde{q}_{0,n}\rightarrow\tilde{q}_{0}\qquad\text{in}\qquad W^{2,2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})

(2.56)

\displaystyle\tilde{W}^{i}_{n}\rightarrow\tilde{W}^{i}\qquad\text{in}\qquad C([0,T])

hold
\tilde{\mathbb{P}}
-a.s. as
n\rightarrow\infty
.

\displaystyle\check{b}_{n_{k}},\check{q}_{n_{k}},\check{b}_{0,n_{k}},\check{q}_{0,n_{k}},\{\overline{W}^{i}_{k}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}),\check{b},\check{q},\check{b}_{0},\check{q}_{0},\{\overline{W}^{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}.

3.
Blowup of the Stochastic Thermal Quasigeostrophic Equations

Let
(b,q,(\tau_{R})_{R\in\mathbb{N}},\tau)
be the unique maximal strong pathwise solution of (
2.9
)–(
2.12
) as constructed in Section
2
above for a given set of data.
As a consequence of this construction, we have seen that, recall Remark
2.8
, if
\tau<\infty
, then

\displaystyle\limsup_{t\nearrow\tau}\big(\|\nabla b(t)\|_{\infty}+\|\nabla\mathbf{u}(t)\|_{\infty}+\|q(t)\|_{\infty}\big)=\infty\qquad\mathbb{P}\text{-a.s.}

Thus, the three quantities announces the blowup of solution or numerical simulation at the finite time
\tau
. This is however too restrictive and it turns out that we do better.

In the following, we want to generalize this to a blowup criteria of Beale–Kato–Majda-type
[
16
]
.
In particular, we show the following result.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
(b,q,(\tau_{R})_{R\in\mathbb{N}},\tau)
be a unique maximal strong pathwise solution of (
2.9
)–(
2.12
) with a dataset

((\bm{\xi}_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}},\mathbf{u}_{h},f,b_{0},q_{0})
satisfying:

(3.1)

\displaystyle\bm{\xi}_{i}

\displaystyle\in W^{4,\infty}_{\mathrm{div}}(\mathbb{R}^{2}),\quad\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\|\bm{\xi}_{i}\|_{4,\infty}<\infty,\quad\mathbf{u}_{h}\in W^{3,2}_{\mathrm{div}}(\mathbb{R}^{2}),\quad f\in W^{2,2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle(b_{0},q_{0})\in W^{3,2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\times W^{2,2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\text{ is a pair of }\mathcal{F}_{0}\text{-measurable random variables.}

For
M,N\in\mathbb{N}
,
let
\tau_{M}^{1}
and
\tau_{N}^{2}
be
defined as

\displaystyle\tau_{M}^{1}=\inf\bigg\{t\geq 0\,:\,\int_{0}^{t}\big(\|\nabla b(s)\|_{\infty}+\|q(s)\|_{\infty}\big)\,\mathrm{d}s\geq M\bigg\},

\displaystyle\tau_{N}^{2}=\inf\big\{t\geq 0\,:\,\|b(t)\|_{3,2}+\|q(t)\|_{2,2}\geq N\big\},

with
\tau^{1}=\lim_{M\rightarrow\infty}\tau_{M}^{1}
and
\tau^{2}=\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\tau_{N}^{2}
.
Then

(3.2)

\displaystyle\tau^{1}=\tau^{2}=\tau

\mathbb{P}
-a.s.

To prove Theorem
3.1
, we require some preparations. We begin by constructing a suitable Green’s function for the elliptic equation at the end of (
2.12
). This in turn will help us derive a log-Sobolev estimate for the Lipschitz norm of the velocity which is the key to solving Theorem
3.1
.

3.1.
Log-Sobolev estimate for velocity gradient

In the following, we let

\nabla_{0}:=(\partial_{x_{1}},\partial_{x_{2}},0)^{T}
and
\nabla_{0}^{\perp}:=(-\partial_{x_{2}},\partial_{x_{1}},0)
be the three-dimensional extensions of the two-dimensional differential operators
\nabla=(\partial_{x_{1}},\partial_{x_{2}})^{T}
and
\nabla^{\perp}:=(-\partial_{x_{2}},\partial_{x_{1}})
by zero respectively. Our goal is to find an estimate for
\mathbf{u}
that solves

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}=\nabla^{\perp}\psi,\qquad(\Delta-1)\psi=w,\qquad w=q-f

where
h\in W^{2,2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
is given. In particular, inspired by
[
16
]
, we aim to show that

(3.3)

\displaystyle\|\nabla\mathbf{u}\|_{\infty}\lesssim 1+(1+2\ln^{+}(\|w\|_{2,2}))\|w\|_{\infty}

where
\ln^{+}a=\ln\,a
if
a\geq 1
and
\ln^{+}a=0
otherwise.

We start with
G^{per}
defined as

\displaystyle G^{per}(x_{1},x_{2})=-\sum_{(k_{1},k_{2})\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\frac{e^{i(k_{1}x_{1}+k_{2}x_{2})}}{k_{1}^{2}+k_{2}^{2}+1},\qquad(x_{1},x_{2})\in\mathbb{T}^{2}.

Observe that
G^{per}(0,0)=-\infty
and that
G^{per}\in L^{2}({\mathbb{T}^{2}})
as

\|G^{per}\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{(k_{1},k_{2})\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\frac{1}{(k_{1}^{2}+k_{2}^{2}+1)^{2}}<\infty.

One can check that

\displaystyle(\Delta-1)G^{per}(\mathbf{x})=\delta_{0}.

More precisely

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}G^{per}(\mathbf{x})(\Delta-1)w(\mathbf{x})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}=w(0)

for any periodic function
w\in W^{2,2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
. In particular

(3.4)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}G^{per}(\mathbf{x})(\Delta-1)e^{i(l_{1}x_{1}+l_{2}x_{2})}\,\mathrm{d}x_{1}\,\mathrm{d}x_{2}=-(l_{1}^{2}+l_{2}^{2}+1)\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}G^{per}(\mathbf{x})e^{i(l_{1}x_{1}+l_{2}x_{2})}\,\mathrm{d}x_{1}\,\mathrm{d}x_{2}=1

for any
(l_{1},l_{2})\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
.
Note that (
3.4
) uniquely identifies
G^{per}(\mathbf{x})
. That is, if
\overline{G}(\mathbf{x})
is periodic, in
L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
and

(3.5)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\overline{G}(\mathbf{x})(\Delta-1)e^{i(l_{1}x_{1}+l_{2}x_{2})}\,\mathrm{d}x_{1}\,\mathrm{d}x_{2}=1.

Then
G^{per}(\mathbf{x})=\overline{G}(\mathbf{x})
,
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{T}^{2}
. That is because
G^{per}-\overline{G}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
will be perpendicular on all elements of the basis of
L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
, and therefore it must be 0.

Next let

\displaystyle G^{free}(\mathbf{x})=-\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{e^{-\sqrt{|\mathbf{x}|^{2}+r^{2}}}}{\sqrt{|\mathbf{x}|^{2}+r^{2}}}\,\mathrm{d}r\qquad(x_{1},x_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}.

Observe that
G^{free}(0,0)=-\infty
and that
G^{free}\in L^{2}_{loc}({\mathbb{R}^{2}})
as For
\mathbf{x}\neq(0,0)
,
r=|\mathbf{x}|\overline{r}
,

\displaystyle|G^{free}(\mathbf{x})|=\frac{1}{4\pi}\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{e^{-|\mathbf{x}|\sqrt{\overline{r}^{2}+1}}}{\sqrt{\overline{r}^{2}+1}}\,\mathrm{d}\overline{r}\right|\lesssim\frac{1}{\sqrt{|x|}},

since
\sup_{y\geq 0}\sqrt{y}e^{-y}<\infty
. One can check that

\displaystyle(\Delta-1)G^{free}(\mathbf{x})=\delta_{0}.

More precisely, this means that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}G^{per}(\mathbf{x})(\Delta-1)w(\mathbf{x})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}=w(0)

for any smooth function
w
with compact
support. Note the different way in which we interpret the fundamental solution of the Helmholtz equation on the torus as opposed of that on whole space.

Consider
\xi=1
in a neighbourhood of
(0,0)
and
\xi=0
in a neighbourhood of the boundary on
\mathbb{T}^{2}=[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}]\times[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}]
. Say
\xi(x_{1},x_{2})=\eta(|x_{1}|)\eta(|x_{2}|)
where
\eta:[0,\infty)\rightarrow[0,1]
is a smooth decreasing function such that

\displaystyle\eta(x)=\begin{cases}1&:|x|\leq\frac{1}{6}\\
0&:|x|\geq\frac{1}{3}\end{cases}.

Then
\xi G^{free}
is periodic and
\xi G^{per}\in L^{2}({\mathbb{T}^{2}})
. Moreover, we have that

(3.6)

\displaystyle(\Delta-1)(\xi(\mathbf{x})G^{free}(\mathbf{x}))=(\Delta\xi(\mathbf{x}))G^{free}(\mathbf{x})+2\nabla\xi(\mathbf{x})\cdot\nabla G^{free}(\mathbf{x})+\xi(\mathbf{x})(\Delta-1)G^{free}(\mathbf{x}),

where (
3.6
) is understood in the sense of (
3.4
). For smooth
w
, the last term in (
3.6
) can be explicitly computed as

\displaystyle\xi(\mathbf{x})(\Delta-1)G^{free}(\mathbf{x})=\delta_{0}

because

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\xi(\mathbf{x})(\Delta-1)G^{free}(\mathbf{x})w(\mathbf{x})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}(\Delta-1)G^{free}(\mathbf{x})\xi(\mathbf{x})w(\mathbf{x})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}(\Delta-1)G^{free}(\mathbf{x})\overline{w}(\mathbf{x})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle=\overline{w}(0)=\xi(0)w(0)=w(0),

where

\displaystyle\overline{w}(\mathbf{x})=\begin{cases}\xi(\mathbf{x})w(\mathbf{x})&\mathbf{x}\in[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}]\times[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}],\\
0&\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}]\times[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}].\end{cases}

Define next
\varphi:\mathbb{T}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
.

\displaystyle\varphi(\mathbf{x})=(\Delta\xi(\mathbf{x}))G^{free}(\mathbf{x})+2\nabla\xi(\mathbf{x})\cdot\nabla G^{free}(\mathbf{x}),\qquad x\in\mathbb{T}^{2}.

Then
\varphi
is periodic, smooth, bounded and equal to zero in the neighbourhood of the boundary
[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}]\times[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}]
as well as on

[-\frac{1}{4},\frac{1}{4}]\times[-\frac{1}{4},\frac{1}{4}]
.
Let
\overline{\varphi}
be the solution of

\displaystyle(\Delta-1)\overline{\varphi}(\mathbf{x})=-\varphi(\mathbf{x}),

where the above equation is interpreted as a PDE on the torus. Then
\overline{\varphi}
is also smooth periodic function on the torus. Define

Appendix A
Geometric considerations of TQG flows

The TQG conservation laws in (
1.10
) for divergence-free flow are in the same form as would be satisfied in the dynamics of semidirect-product Lie-Poisson bracket for fluid flow in the variables
(q,b)

[
32
]
. Thus, one asks whether TQG can be cast by a change of variables into Hamiltonian form and endowed with a semidirect-product Lie-Poisson bracket. If this is possible, then even though it does not follow from Hamilton’s principle the TQG model would fit into the standard Hamiltonian framework for all ideal fluids with advected quantities, see
[
32
]
.

We introduce the variable
\varpi
, given by

\varpi:=(1-\Delta)\psi+b=f_{1}-q+b\quad\hbox{so that}\quad\psi=(1-\Delta)^{-1}(\varpi-b)\,.

Consequently, when written in the variables
(\varpi,b)
, the energy in (
1.9
) becomes
the TQG Hamiltonian,

(A.1)

{\mathcal{H}}_{TQG}(\varpi,b)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{{\mathcal{D}}}(\varpi-b)(1-\Delta)^{-1}(\varpi-b)+(b+h_{1})^{2}/2\ \,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y\,.

One computes the variational derivatives of the Hamiltonian
{\mathcal{H}}_{TQG}(\varpi,b)
as

\delta{\mathcal{H}}_{TQG}=\int_{{\mathcal{D}}}\psi\cdot\delta\varpi+\Big((b+h_{1})/2-\psi\Big)\delta b\ \,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y\,.

Now, in the new notation, we can rewrite the TQG equations in standard Hamiltonian form, with a Lie-Poisson bracket defined on the dual to a semidirect-product Lie algebra, as discussed for example in
[
32
]
. Explicitly, this is

(A.2)

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\begin{bmatrix}\varpi\\
b\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}J(\varpi,\,\cdot\,)&J(b,\,\cdot\,)\\
J(b,\,\cdot\,)&0\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}{\delta{\mathcal{H}}_{TQG}}/{\delta\varpi}=\psi\\
{\delta{\mathcal{H}}_{TQG}}/{\delta b}=(b+h_{1})/2-\psi\end{bmatrix}\,.

The Hamiltonian operator form of the TQG equations in (
A.2
) is identical to that of the Euler-Boussinesq convection (EBC) equations in
[
33
]
.
We are now in a position to explain the conservation laws for Hamilton matrix operator in (
A.2
). Namely, those conservation laws comprise Casimir functions
C_{\Phi,\Psi}
whose variational derivatives are null eigenvectors of the Hamilton matrix operator in (
A.2
) which defines the semidirect-product Lie-Poisson bracket as

(A.3)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}{\mathcal{F}}(\varpi,b)&=\int_{{\mathcal{D}}}\begin{bmatrix}{\delta{\mathcal{F}}}/{\delta\varpi}\\
{\delta{\mathcal{F}}}/{\delta b}\end{bmatrix}^{T}\begin{bmatrix}J(\varpi,\,\cdot\,)&J(b,\,\cdot\,)\\
J(b,\,\cdot\,)&0\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}{\delta{\mathcal{H}}}/{\delta\varpi}\\
{\delta{\mathcal{H}}}/{\delta b}\end{bmatrix}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y=:\Big\{{\mathcal{F}}\,,\,{{\mathcal{H}}}\Big\}(\varpi,b)\\
&=-\int_{{\mathcal{D}}}\varpi\,J\bigg(\frac{\delta{\mathcal{F}}}{\delta\varpi},\frac{\delta{\mathcal{H}}}{\delta\varpi}\bigg)+b\Bigg(J\bigg(\frac{\delta{\mathcal{F}}}{\delta b},\frac{\delta{\mathcal{H}}}{\delta\varpi}\bigg)-J\bigg(\frac{\delta{\mathcal{F}}}{\delta\varpi},\frac{\delta{\mathcal{H}}}{\delta b}\bigg)\Bigg)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y\,.\end{split}

In particular, for
{\mathcal{F}}(\varpi,b)=\int_{{\mathcal{D}}}\Phi(b)+\varpi\Psi(b)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y
we have

(A.4)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}{\mathcal{F}}(\varpi,b)&=\Big\{{\mathcal{F}}\,,\,{{\mathcal{H}}}\Big\}(\varpi,b)\\
&=-\int_{{\mathcal{D}}}\varpi\,J\bigg(\Psi(b),\frac{\delta{\mathcal{H}}}{\delta\varpi}\bigg)+\Bigg(bJ\bigg(\Phi^{\prime}(b)+\varpi\Psi^{\prime}(b),\frac{\delta{\mathcal{H}}}{\delta\varpi}\bigg)-bJ\bigg(\Psi(b),\frac{\delta{\mathcal{H}}}{\delta b}\bigg)\Bigg)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y\\
&=-\int_{{\mathcal{D}}}\frac{\delta{\mathcal{H}}}{\delta\varpi}J\bigg(\varpi,\Psi(b)\bigg)+\Bigg(\frac{\delta{\mathcal{H}}}{\delta\varpi}J\bigg(b,\Phi^{\prime}(b)+\varpi\Psi^{\prime}(b)\bigg)-\frac{\delta{\mathcal{H}}}{\delta b}J\bigg(b,\Psi(b)\bigg)\Bigg)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y\,.\\
&=-\int_{{\mathcal{D}}}\frac{\delta{\mathcal{H}}}{\delta\varpi}\Big(J\big(\varpi,b\big)+J\big(b,\varpi\big)\Big)\Psi^{\prime}(b)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y\\
&=0\quad\hbox{for all}\quad{{\mathcal{H}}}\,.\end{split}

This explains the conservation of
C_{\Phi,\Psi}
in (
1.10
). These quantities are Casimir functions which are conserved for
every
Hamiltonian
{\mathcal{H}}(\varpi,b)
for the semidirect-product Lie-Poisson bracket in (
A.3
). This conservation occurs because Lie-Poisson brackets generate coadjoint orbits, and coadjoint orbits are level sets of the bracket’s Casimir functions. The requirement that TQG motion takes place on level sets of the Casimir functions limits the function space available to TQG solutions. In particular, the TQG solutions are restricted to stay on the same level set as their initial conditions.

Proposition A.1
.

The Lie-Poisson bracket in (
A.3
) satisfies the Jacobi identity.

Proof.

This proposition could be demonstrated by direct computation using the well-known properties of the Jacobian of function pairs. The proof given here, though, will illustrate the geometric properties of the TQG system in (
1.2
) and, thus, place it into the wider class of ideal fluid dynamics with advected quantities. In particular, the proof will identify the Poisson bracket in (
A.3
) as being defined over domain
{\mathcal{D}}
on functionals of the
dual
3
3

3

Dual with respect to the
L^{2}
pairing on
{\mathcal{D}}

(f_{1}\circledS f_{2})^{*}
of the Lie algebra
(f_{1}\circledS f_{2})
of semidirect-product symplectic transformations. Thus, TQG dynamics is understood as coadjoint motion generated by the semidirect-product action of the Lie algebra

The Lie algebra commutator

\displaystyle{\rm ad}_{\big(\overline{f}_{1};\overline{f}_{2}\big)}\big(f_{1};f_{2}\big)=\Big[\big(f_{1};f_{2}\big),\big(\overline{f}_{1};\overline{f}_{2}\big)\Big]:=\Big(\big[f_{1},\overline{f}_{1}\big];\big[f_{1},\overline{f}_{2}\big]-\big[\overline{f}_{1},f_{2}\big]\Big)\,,

which is also the commutator of symplectic vector fields. Thus, the adjoint (ad) action in (

The definition in (

in which the angle brackets

) by identifying its Lie-Poisson bracket with the coadjoint action of the semidirect-product Lie group of symplectic transformations defined in equation (
A.8
). Namely, the solutions of the TQG system in (
A.8
) evolve by undergoing coadjoint motion along a time-dependent path on the Lie group manifold of semidirect-product symplectic diffeomorphisms acting on the domain of flow
{\mathcal{D}}
. This association of the TQG system with the smooth flow of symplectomorphisms on
{\mathcal{D}}
bodes well for the analytical properties of TQG solutions. Indeed, the preservation of these smooth flow properties obtained via the SALT approach provides a geometric framework for the determination of the corresponding analytical properties for the stochastic counterpart of TQG, which is the primary aim of the present paper.

We show that

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{L}_{\bm{\xi}_{i}}(q-b),\mathcal{L}_{\bm{\xi}_{i}}(q-b)\rangle_{k-1,2}+\langle\mathcal{L}_{\bm{\xi}_{i}}^{2}(q-2b),q\rangle_{k-1,2}\lesssim_{k}\|q\|_{k-1,2}^{2}+\|b\|_{k,2}^{2}

.

and a multi-index

Appendix C
Spatial discretisation

In this appendix section, we describe the finite element (FEM) method we use for the STQG system. Our setup applies to problems on bounded domains
\mathcal{D}
with Dirichlet boundary conditions

(C.1)

\psi=0,\qquad\text{on }\partial\mathcal{D}.

For
\mathcal{D}=\mathbb{T}^{2}
, the boundary flux terms in the discretised equations are set to zero.

C.0.1.
The stream function equation

Let
H^{1}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)
denote the Sobolev
W^{1,2}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)

space and let
\left\|.\right\|_{\partial\mathcal{D}}
denote
the
L^{2}\left(\partial\mathcal{D}\right)
norm.
Define the space

(C.2)

W^{1}\left(\mathcal{D}\right):=\left\{\nu\in H^{1}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)\left|\left\|\nu\right\|_{\partial\mathcal{D}}=0\right.\right\}.

We look for numerical solutions to the Helmholtz elliptic problem (
2.12
) in the space
W^{1}(\mathcal{D})
.
Define the functionals

(C.3)

\displaystyle L(v,\phi)

\displaystyle:=\langle\nabla v,\nabla\phi\rangle_{\mathcal{D}}+\langle v,\phi\rangle_{\mathcal{D}}

(C.4)

\displaystyle F_{\cdot}(\phi)

\displaystyle:=-\langle\cdot,\phi\rangle_{\mathcal{D}}

for
v,\phi\in W^{1}(\mathcal{D})
, then the Helmholtz problem can be written as

(C.5)

L(\psi,\phi)=F_{q-f}(\phi).

We discretise (
C.5
) using a continuous Galerkin (CG) discretisation scheme.

Let
\delta
be the discretisation parameter, and let
\mathcal{D}_{\delta}
denote a space filling triangulation of the domain, that consists of geometry-conforming non-overlapping elements.
Define the approximation space

(C.6)

W_{\delta}^{k}(\mathcal{D}):=\left\{\phi_{\delta}\in W^{1}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)\ :\ \phi_{\delta}\in C\left(\mathcal{D}\right),\left.\phi_{\delta}\right|_{K}\in\Pi^{k}\left(K\right)\text{ each }K\in\mathcal{D}_{\delta}\right\}.

in which
C(\mathcal{D})
is the space of continuous functions on
\mathcal{D}
, and
\Pi^{k}\left(K\right)
denotes the space of polynomials of degree at most
k
on element
K\in\mathcal{D}_{\delta}
.

For (
C.5
), given
f_{\delta}\in W_{\delta}^{k}(\mathcal{D})
and
q_{\delta}\in V_{\delta}^{k}(\mathcal{D})
(see (
C.8
) for the definition of
V_{\delta}^{k}(\mathcal{D})
),
our numerical approximation is the solution
\psi_{\delta}\in W_{\delta}^{k}(\mathcal{D})
that satisfies

(C.7)

L(\psi_{\delta},\phi_{\delta})=F_{q_{\delta}-f_{\delta}}(\phi_{\delta})

for all test functions
\phi_{\delta}\in W_{\delta}^{k}(\mathcal{D})
.
For a detailed exposition of the numerical algorithms that solves the discretised problem (
C.7
)
we point the reader to
[
34
,
35
]
.

To handle the noise terms in the hyperbolic equations, let
\zeta_{i}\in W^{1}(\mathcal{D})
denote the stream function of
\bm{\xi}
, i.e.
\zeta_{i}=\nabla^{\perp}\bm{\xi}_{i}
. So the discretised
\zeta_{i}^{\delta}
are in the same space as
\psi_{\delta}
.

C.0.2.
Hyperbolic equations

We choose to discretise the hyperbolic buoyancy (
2.9
) and potential vorticity (
2.10
) equations using a discontinuous Galerkin (DG) scheme. For a detailed exposition of DG methods, we refer the interested reader to
[
30
]
.

Define the DG approximation space, denoted by
V_{\delta}^{k}(\mathcal{D})
, to be the element-wise polynomial space,

(C.8)

V_{\delta}^{k}(\mathcal{D})=\left\{\left.v_{\delta}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{D})\right|\forall K\in\mathcal{D}_{\delta},\ \exists\phi_{\delta}\in\Pi^{k}(K):\ \left.v_{\delta}\right|_{K}=\left.\phi_{\delta}\right|_{K}\right\}.

We look to approximate
b
and
q
in the space
V_{\delta}^{k}(\mathcal{D})
. Essentially, this means our approximations of
b
and
q
are each an direct sum over the elements in
\mathcal{D}_{\delta}
. Additional constraints on the numerical fluxes across shared element boundaries are needed to ensure conservation properties and stability.
Further, note that
W^{k}_{\delta}(\mathcal{D})\subset V^{k}_{\delta}(\mathcal{D})
. This inclusion is needed for ensuring numerical conservation of Casimirs (
1.10
).

For the buoyancy equation (
2.9
), we obtain the following variational formulation

(C.9)

\displaystyle\langle\,\mathrm{d}b,\nu_{\delta}\rangle_{K}

\displaystyle=\langle b({\bf u}\,\mathrm{d}t+\bm{\xi}_{i}\circ\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}^{i}),\nabla\nu_{\delta}\rangle_{K}-\langle b({\bf u}\,\mathrm{d}t+\bm{\xi}_{i}\circ\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}^{i})\cdot{\bf n},\nu_{\delta}\rangle_{\partial K},\quad K\in\mathcal{D}_{\delta}

where
\nu_{\delta}\in V^{k}_{\delta}(\mathcal{D})
is any test function,
\partial K
denotes the boundary of
K
, and
\bf n
denotes the unit normal vector to
\partial K
. Let
b_{\delta}
be the approximation of
b
in
V_{\delta}^{k}
, and let
\mathbf{u}_{\delta}=\nabla^{\perp}\psi_{\delta}
for
\psi_{\delta}\in W^{k}_{\delta}
. Similarly, let
\bm{\xi}_{i}^{\delta}=\nabla^{\perp}\zeta_{i}^{\delta}
for
\zeta_{i}^{\delta}\in W^{k}_{\delta}
. Our discretised buoyancy equation over each element is given by

(C.10)

\langle\,\mathrm{d}b_{\delta},\nu_{\delta}\rangle_{K}=\langle b_{\delta}({\bf u}_{\delta}\,\mathrm{d}t+\bm{\xi}_{i}^{\delta}\circ\,\mathrm{d}W^{i}),\nabla\nu_{\delta}\rangle_{K}-\langle b_{\delta}({\bf u}_{\delta}\,\mathrm{d}t+\bm{\xi}_{i}^{\delta}\circ\,\mathrm{d}W^{i})\cdot{\bf n},\nu_{\delta}\rangle_{\partial K},\quad K\in\mathcal{D}_{\delta}.

Similarly, let
q_{\delta}\in V_{\delta}^{k}(\mathcal{D})
be the approximation of
q
, and let
h_{\delta}\in W_{\delta}^{k}
. We obtain the following discretised variational formulation that corresponds to (
2.10
),

\displaystyle\langle\,\mathrm{d}q_{\delta},\nu_{\delta}\rangle_{K}

\displaystyle=\langle(q_{\delta}-b_{\delta})({\bf u}_{\delta}\,\mathrm{d}t+\bm{\xi}_{i}^{\delta}\circ\,\mathrm{d}W^{i}),\nabla\nu_{\delta}\rangle_{K}-\langle(q_{\delta}-b_{\delta})({\bf u}_{\delta}\,\mathrm{d}t+\bm{\xi}_{i}^{\delta}\circ\,\mathrm{d}W^{i})\cdot{\bf n},\nu_{\delta}\rangle_{\partial K}

(C.11)

\displaystyle\qquad-\frac{1}{2}\langle\nabla\cdot(b_{\delta}\nabla^{\perp}h_{\delta}),\nu_{\delta}\rangle_{K},\qquad K\in\mathcal{D}_{\delta},

for test function
\nu_{\delta}\in V_{\delta}^{k}(\mathcal{D})
.

At this point, we only have the discretised problem on single elements. To obtain the global approximation, we sum over all the elements in
\mathcal{D}_{\delta}
. In doing so, the
\partial K
terms in (
C.10
) and (
C.11
) must be treated carefully.
Let
\partial K_{\text{ext}}
denote the part of cell boundary that is contained in
\partial\mathcal{D}
. Let
\partial K_{\text{int}}
denote the part of the cell boundary that is contained in the interior of the domain
\mathcal{D}\backslash\partial\mathcal{D}
.
On
\partial K_{\text{ext}}
we simply impose the PDE boundary conditions. However, on
\partial K_{\text{int}}
we need to consider the contribution from each of the neighbouring elements.
By choice, the approximants
\psi_{\delta},\zeta_{i}^{\delta}\in W_{\delta}^{k}
are continuous on
\partial K
. And since

(C.12)

(\mathbf{u}_{\delta}+\bm{\xi}_{i}^{\delta})\cdot\bf n=\nabla^{\perp}(\psi_{\delta}+\zeta_{i}^{\delta})\cdot\bf n=-\nabla(\psi_{\delta}+\zeta_{i}^{\delta})\cdot\hat{\tau}=-\frac{\,\mathrm{d}(\psi_{\delta}+\zeta_{i}^{\delta})}{\,\mathrm{d}\hat{\tau}},

where
\hat{\tau}
denotes the unit tangential vector to
\partial K
,
(\mathbf{u}_{\delta}+\bm{\xi}_{i}^{\delta})\cdot\bf n
is also continuous. This means
(\psi_{\delta}+\zeta_{i}^{\delta})\cdot\bf n
in (
C.10
) (also in (
C.11
)) is single valued. However, due to the lack of global continuity constraint in the definition of
V_{k}(\mathcal{D})
,
b_{\delta}
and
q_{\delta}
are multi-valued on
\partial K_{\text{int}}
. Thus, as our approximation of
q
and
b
over the whole domain is the sum over
K\in\mathcal{D}_{\delta}
, we have to constrain the flux on the set
\left(\bigcup_{K\in\mathcal{D}_{\delta}}\partial K\right)\setminus\partial\mathcal{D}
. This is done using appropriately chosen numerical flux fields in the boundary terms of (
C.10
) and (
C.11
).

Let

\nu^{-}:=\lim_{\epsilon\uparrow 0}\nu({\bf x}+\epsilon\bf n)

and

\nu^{+}:=\lim_{\epsilon\downarrow 0}\nu({\bf x}+\epsilon\bf n)
,
for
{\bf x}\in\partial K
, be the inside and outside (with respect to a fixed element
K
) values respectively, of a function
\nu
on the boundary.
Let
\hat{f}
be a
numerical flux
function
that satisfies the following properties:

(i)

consistency

(C.13)

\hat{f}(\nu,\nu,(\mathbf{u}_{\delta}\,\mathrm{d}t+\bm{\xi}_{i}^{\delta}\circ\,\mathrm{d}W^{i})\cdot{\bf n})={\nu}(\mathbf{u}_{\delta}\,\mathrm{d}t+\bm{\xi}_{i}^{\delta}\circ\,\mathrm{d}W^{i})\cdot\bf n

(ii)

conservative

(C.14)

\hat{f}(\nu^{+},\nu^{-},(\mathbf{u}_{\delta}\,\mathrm{d}t+\bm{\xi}_{i}^{\delta}\circ\,\mathrm{d}W^{i})\cdot{\bf n})=-\hat{f}(\nu^{-},\nu^{+},-(\mathbf{u}_{\delta}\,\mathrm{d}t+\bm{\xi}_{i}^{\delta}\circ\,\mathrm{d}W^{i})\cdot\bf n)

(iii)

L^{2}
stable in the enstrophy norm with respect to the buoyancy equation, see
[
36
, Section 6]
.

With such an
\hat{f}
, we replace

b_{\delta}(\mathbf{u}_{\delta}\,\mathrm{d}t+\bm{\xi}_{i}^{\delta}\circ\,\mathrm{d}W^{i})\cdot\bf n
by the numerical flux
\hat{f}(b_{\delta}^{,+},b_{\delta}^{,-},(\mathbf{u}_{\delta}\,\mathrm{d}t+\bm{\xi}_{i}^{\delta}\circ\,\mathrm{d}W^{i})\cdot\bf n)
in (
C.10
).
Similarly, in (
C.11
), we replace
(q_{\delta}-b_{\delta})(\mathbf{u}_{\delta}\,\mathrm{d}t+\bm{\xi}_{i}^{\delta}\circ\,\mathrm{d}W^{i})\cdot\bf n
by
\hat{f}((q_{\delta}-b_{\delta})^{+},(q_{\delta}-b_{\delta})^{-},(\mathbf{u}_{\delta}\,\mathrm{d}t+\bm{\xi}_{i}^{\delta}\circ\,\mathrm{d}W^{i})\cdot\bf n)
.

Remark C.1
.

For a general nonlinear conservation law, one has to solve what is called the
Riemann problem
for the numerical flux, see
[
30
]
for details.
In our setup, we use the following local Lax-Friedrichs flux, which is an approximate Riemann solver,

(C.15)

\hat{f}(\nu^{+},\nu^{-},(\mathbf{u}_{\delta}\,\mathrm{d}t+\bm{\xi}_{i}^{\delta}\circ\,\mathrm{d}W^{i})\cdot{\bf n})=(\mathbf{u}_{\delta}\,\mathrm{d}t+\bm{\xi}_{i}^{\delta}\circ\,\mathrm{d}W^{i})\cdot{\bf n}\{\{\nu\}\}-\frac{|(\mathbf{u}_{\delta}\,\mathrm{d}t+\bm{\xi}_{i}^{\delta}\circ\,\mathrm{d}W^{i})\cdot{\bf n}|}{2}\llbracket\nu\rrbracket

where

(C.16)

\{\{\nu\}\}:=\frac{1}{2}(\nu^{-}+\nu^{+}),\qquad\llbracket\nu\rrbracket:={\bf n}^{-}\nu^{-}+{\bf n}^{+}\nu^{+}.

Finally, our goal is to find
b_{\delta},q_{\delta}\in V_{\delta}^{k}(\mathcal{D})
such that for all
\nu_{\delta}\in V_{\delta}^{k}(\mathcal{D})
we have

(C.17)

\displaystyle\sum_{K\in\mathcal{D}_{\delta}}\langle\,\mathrm{d}b_{\delta},\nu_{\delta}\rangle_{K}

\displaystyle=\sum_{K\in\mathcal{D}_{\delta}}\big\{\langle b_{\delta}\nabla^{\perp}\tilde{\psi}_{\delta},\nabla\nu_{\delta}\rangle_{K}-\langle\hat{f}_{b}(b_{\delta}^{,+},b_{\delta}^{,-},\nabla^{\perp}\tilde{\psi}_{\delta}\cdot{\bf n}),\nu_{\delta}^{-}\rangle_{\partial K}\big\},

(C.18)

\displaystyle\sum_{K\in\mathcal{D}_{\delta}}\langle\,\mathrm{d}q_{\delta},\nu_{\delta}\rangle_{K}

\displaystyle=\sum_{K\in\mathcal{D}_{\delta}}\big\{\langle(q_{\delta}-b_{\delta})\nabla^{\perp}\tilde{\psi}_{\delta},\nabla\nu_{\delta}\rangle_{K}-\frac{1}{2}\langle\nabla\cdot(b_{\delta}\nabla^{\perp}h_{\delta}),\nu_{\delta}\rangle_{K}

\displaystyle\qquad-\langle\hat{f}_{q}((q_{\delta}-b_{\delta})^{+},(q_{\delta}-b_{\delta})^{-},\nabla^{\perp}\tilde{\psi}_{\delta}\cdot{\bf n}),\nu_{\delta}^{-}\rangle_{\partial K}\big\}

where
\tilde{\psi}_{\delta}:=\psi_{\delta}\,\mathrm{d}t+\zeta_{i}^{\delta}\circ\,\mathrm{d}W^{i}
was introduced for notation simplicity.

Remark C.2
.

In (
C.17
) and (
C.18
) we do not explicitly distinguish external and internal boundaries,
\partial K_{\text{ext}}
and
\partial K_{\text{int}}
, because for the boundary condition (
C.1
), the
\partial K_{\text{ext}}
terms vanish.
