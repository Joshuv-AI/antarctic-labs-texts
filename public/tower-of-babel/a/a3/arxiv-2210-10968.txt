Abstract
We study divide-and-conquer recurrences of the form f ⁡ ( n ) = α ​ f ​ ( ⌊ n 2 ⌋ ) + β ​ f ​ ( ⌈ n 2 ⌉ ) + g ⁡ ( n ) ( n ⩾ 2 ) , f(n)=\alpha f\bigl({\bigl\lfloor\tfrac{n}{2}\bigr\rfloor}\bigr)+\beta f\bigl({\bigl\lceil\tfrac{n}{2}\bigr\rceil}\bigr)+g(n)\qquad(n\geqslant 2), with g ⁡ ( n ) g(n) and f ⁡ ( 1 ) f(1) given, where α , β ⩾ 0 \alpha,\beta\geqslant 0 with α + β > 0 \alpha+\beta>0 ; such recurrences appear often in analysis of computer algorithms, numeration systems, combinatorial sequences, and related areas. We show that the solution satisfies always the simple identity f ⁡ ( n ) = n log 2 ⁡ ( α + β ) ​ P ​ ( log 2 ⁡ n ) − Q ⁡ ( n ) f(n)=n^{\log_{2}(\alpha+\beta)}P(\log_{2}n)-Q(n) under an optimum (iff) condition on g ⁡ ( n ) g(n) . This form is not only an identity but also an asymptotic expansion because Q ⁡ ( n ) Q(n) is of a smaller order. Explicit forms for the continuity of the periodic function P P are provided, together with a few other smoothness properties. We show how our results can be easily applied to many dozens of concrete examples collected from the literature, and how they can be extended in various directions. Our method of proof is surprisingly simple and elementary, but leads to the strongest types of results for all examples to which our theory applies.

Recurrences with or without floors and ceilings.

While the divide-and-conquer paradigm with evenly divided parts is
widely used in computer algorithms, our formulation of the
divide-and-conquer recurrence (
1.4
), as well as the very
precise identity (
1.2
), is surprisingly rare in the computer
algorithm literature; instead one finds predominantly a recurrence of
the form

\displaystyle f(n)=(\alpha+\beta)f\bigl({\tfrac{n}{2}}\bigr)+g(n),

(1.5)

or more generally

\displaystyle f(n)=\sum_{0\leqslant j<d}\alpha_{j}f\bigl({\tfrac{n}{q_{j}}}\bigr)+g(n),

(1.6)

where
\alpha,\alpha_{j}>0
,
d=1,2,\dots
and
q_{j}>1
. According to
the first edition of Cormen et al.’s widely used textbook on
Algorithms
[
10
, p. 54]
: “
When we state and solve
recurrences, we often omit floors, ceilings, and boundary conditions.
We forge ahead without these details and later determine whether or
not they matter. …we shall address some of these details to show
the fine points of recurrence solution methods.
”

Such a simplifying approach also appears in most publications on
Algorithms. One of our aims in this paper is to show that

retaining floors and ceilings is not much more complicated than
omitting them, and with various advantages that are mostly unnoticed
in the literature.
This suggests that one main reason of omitting
floors and ceilings in handling a divide-and-conquer recurrence lies
more in methodological deficiencies than simply technical
conventions; the approach proposed in this paper will then complete
to some extent the required methodological developments.

More precisely, when
\alpha,\beta,g(n)\geqslant 0
, typical approaches
adopted in the computer algorithms community to solving the
recurrence (
1.1
) include

•

dropping floor and ceiling in (
1.1
) by assuming
n
to be
a power of
2
, resulting in the closed-form expression

\displaystyle f(2^{m})=\sum_{1\leqslant j\leqslant m}(\alpha+\beta)^{m-j}g(2^{j})+(\alpha+\beta)^{m}f(1),

(1.7)

and

•

lower- and upper-bounding
f
by keeping only floor and only
ceiling function in (
1.1
), leading to

\displaystyle f_{-}(n):=(\alpha+\beta)f_{-}\bigl({\lfloor\tfrac{n}{2}\rfloor}\bigr)+g(n),

(1.8)

and

\displaystyle f_{+}(n):=(\alpha+\beta)f_{+}\bigl({\lceil\tfrac{n}{2}\rceil}\bigr)+g(n),

(1.9)

respectively.

While simple and effective in estimating the growth order of
f(n)

for large
n
, both approaches suffer from subtle oversights and
intrinsic limitations, with different shortcomings.

Monotonicity.

First, in either of the approaches (
1.7
) and
(
1.8
)–(
1.9
), the next crucial property used in
estimating the asymptotic growth of
f(n)
is

which in the first approach is of the form
f(2^{m})\leqslant f(2^{m}+\ell)\leqslant f(2^{m+1})
for

, and
f_{-}(n)\leqslant f(n)\leqslant f_{+}(n)

in the second approach. However, these inequalities may not hold in
general due to the periodic nature of the recurrences (
1.1
),
(
1.8
) and (
1.9
). For example, take
\alpha=\beta=1

and
g(n)=1+\mathbf{1}_{n\text{ odd}}
with
f(1)=f_{+}(1)=0
.
Then
8=f(7)>f(8)=7
, and
f(15)=17>f_{+}(15)=16

monotonicity is more subtle than it is generally taken to be.

On the other hand, when

Integer Sequences

[

Thus not only monotonicity fails but also the growth order oscillates
violently, although our result yields that

Discontinuity.

Apart from monotonicity, there is yet another deeper reason why the
original sequence (
1.1

versions (
1.8

1.9

The reason is because the periodic function

1.1

Smoothness of the periodic function.

While the periodic equivalence is introduced to identify the same
fluctuating part of different sequences satisfying the
same

be non-differentiable points), and there are other classes of
functions (such as Lipshitz and Hölder continuous) between the
class of continuous functions and that of continuously differentiable
functions. We will thus clarify the different, characteristic,
inherent types of Hölder continuity of the interpolated function

f(x)
and the periodic function
P(t)
when
(\alpha,\beta)
varies.

A generating function viewpoint.

To see how the general cases differ from the special case

(\alpha,\beta)=(1,1)
or more generally
\alpha=\beta
, we consider
the generating function

A(z):=\sum_{n\geqslant 1}f(n)z^{n},

(1.10)

which, by (
1.1
) satisfies the functional equation

A(z)=\frac{\beta+(\alpha+\beta)z+\alpha z^{2}}{z}\,A(z^{2})+B(z),

(1.11)

where

B(z):=f(1)(1-\beta)z+\sum_{n\geqslant 2}g(n)z^{n}.

(1.12)

We see that if
\alpha=\beta
, then the functional equation becomes

A(z)=\frac{\alpha(1+z)^{2}}{z}\,A(z^{2})+B(z),

(1.13)

so that the generating function
\bar{A}(z):=\frac{(1-z)^{2}}{z}\,A(z)

of the second difference of
f(n)
satisfies the simpler equation

\bar{A}(z)=\alpha\bar{A}(z^{2})+\bar{B}(z),

(1.14)

where
\bar{B}(z):=\frac{(1-z)^{2}}{z}\,B(z)
. Assuming for simplicity
(without real loss of generality) that
\bar{B}(0)=0
, we then
obtain, by iteration, the exact solution

A(z)=\frac{z}{(1-z)^{2}}\sum_{k\geqslant 0}\alpha^{k}\bar{B}\bigl({z^{2^{k}}}\bigr).

(1.15)

Such a neat representation is the basis of the analytic approach
introduced in
[
20
]

when
\alpha\neq\beta
. We will therefore use a different method.

(2.8)

for

Remark 2.2
.

Conversely, it is immediate that if (
2.3
) holds for the
extensions of
f
and
g
defined by (
2
), then (
2.1
)
holds.

In the case
\alpha=\beta=1
treated in
[
27
]
, the system of
equations (
2.4
) has the solution
\varphi(t)=t
. In general, it is
not obvious that a continuous solution to (
2.4
) exists. We now
show that this is the case, and that this
\varphi
is unique.

Lemma 2.3
.

If
\alpha,\beta>0
, then there exists a unique continuous
function
\varphi(t)=\varphi_{\alpha,\beta}(t)
on
[0,1]
such that

\varphi(0)=0
,
\varphi(1)=1
and (
2.4
) holds. Moreover,

\varphi
is strictly increasing.

Proof.

The equation (
2.4
) and the conditions
\varphi(0)=0
,
\varphi(1)=1
define recursively
\varphi(t)
uniquely for dyadic rational

t\in[0,1]
; hence, there is at most one continuous
\varphi

satisfying these requirements. The existence of such a
\varphi
can
be proved in several different ways.

First proof (probabilistic).

Let
\varphi
be the distribution function
\varphi(t):=\Pr(X\leqslant t)
of the
random variable
X\in[0,1]
defined by the binary expansion

X=0.B_{1}B_{2}\dots
, where the bits
B_{1},B_{2},\dots
are independent and
with
\Pr(B_{i}=1)=\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta}
. Since the random variable

X^{\prime}:=0.B_{2}B_{3}\dots=2X-B_{1}
has the same distribution as
X
, we see
that

\begin{split}\varphi(t)&:=\Pr(X\leqslant t)\\
&=\begin{cases}\Pr(B_{1}=0)\Pr(X^{\prime}\leqslant 2t),&t\in[0,\frac{1}{2}],\\
\Pr(B_{1}=0)+\Pr(B_{1}=1)\Pr(X^{\prime}\leqslant 2t-1),&t\in[\frac{1}{2},1],\end{cases}\\
&=\begin{cases}\frac{\beta}{\alpha+\beta}\,\varphi(2t),&t\in[0,\frac{1}{2}],\\
\frac{\beta}{\alpha+\beta}+\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta}\,\varphi(2t-1),&t\in[\frac{1}{2},1],\end{cases}\end{split}

(2.9)

which verifies (
2.4
). Furthermore,
\varphi
is continuous and
strictly increasing on
[0,1]
, with
\varphi(0)=0
and
\varphi(1)=1
.

Second proof (digital sums).

An alternative approach begins with an explicit construction, which
will also be useful later. For
t=\sum_{j\geqslant 1}b_{j}2^{-j}\in[0,1]
, where
b_{j}\in\{0,1\}
, define

\displaystyle\varphi(t):=\sum_{j\geqslant 1}b_{j}\left(\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\right)^{b_{1}+\cdots+b_{j-1}}\biggl(\frac{\beta}{\alpha+\beta}\biggr)^{j}.

(2.10)

Equivalently, define, for
t=\sum_{k\geqslant 1}2^{-e_{k}}\in[0,1]
, where

1\leqslant e_{1}<e_{2}<\cdots
is a finite or infinite sequence,

\varphi\biggl(t=\sum_{k\geqslant 1}2^{-e_{k}}\biggr):=\sum_{k\geqslant 1}\frac{\alpha^{k-1}\beta^{e_{k}-k+1}}{\left(\alpha+\beta\right)^{e_{k}}}.

(2.11)

We first show that both (
2.10
) and (
2.11
) are well defined
for dyadic rational
t
with two different representations, namely,

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leqslant i\leqslant k}2^{-e_{i}}=\sum_{1\leqslant j<k}2^{-e_{j}}+\sum_{j\geqslant 1}2^{-(e_{k}+j)}.

(2.12)

The value in (
2.11
) for the last term on the left-hand side of
(
2.12
) is
\frac{\alpha^{k-1}\beta^{e_{k}-k+1}}{\left(\alpha+\beta\right)^{e_{k}}}
; the value for the sum on the right-hand
side equals

\sum_{j\geqslant 1}\frac{\alpha^{(k+j-1)-1}\beta^{e_{k}+j-(k+j-1)+1}}{(\alpha+\beta)^{e_{k}+j}}=\frac{\alpha^{k-1}\beta^{e_{k}-k+1}}{(\alpha+\beta)^{e_{k}}}\sum_{j\geqslant 1}\frac{\alpha^{j-1}\beta}{(\alpha+\beta)^{j}}=\frac{\alpha^{k-1}\beta^{e_{k}-k+1}}{(\alpha+\beta)^{e_{k}}},

(2.13)

since the final geometric series sums to 1. Hence, the two
representations in (
2.12
) yield the same sum (
2.11
), which
always equals the sum (
2.10
), and thus
\varphi
is well-defined
on
[0,1]
.

Clearly,
\varphi(0)=0
and
\varphi(1)=1
, again by summing the same
geometric sum. Moreover, (
2.4
) follows easily from (
2.10
),
considering the cases
b_{1}=0
and
b_{1}=1
separately.

Next, if
t=\sum_{1\leqslant j\leqslant N}b_{j}2^{-j}
is a dyadic rational in

[0,1)
, and
s:=\sum_{1\leqslant j\leqslant N}b_{j}
, then, by (
2.4
) and
induction on
N\geqslant 0
, we have

\displaystyle\varphi\bigl(t+2^{-N}\bigr)-\varphi(t)=\frac{\alpha^{s}\beta^{N-s}}{(\alpha+\beta)^{N}}.

(2.14)

It follows from (
2.14
) that
\varphi
is strictly increasing on the
set of dyadic rationals in
[0,1]
. Furthermore, suppose that

t_{1}=j2^{-N}
is a dyadic rational in
[0,1)
, and let

t_{2}:=t+2^{-N}
. If
t\in[t_{1},t_{2}]
, then both
t
and
t_{2}
have
binary expansions beginning with the expansion of
t_{1}
(choosing the
infinite representation for
t_{2}
), and it follows from (
2.11
)
that
\varphi(t_{1})\leqslant\varphi(t)\leqslant\varphi(t_{2})
. Now, suppose
0\leqslant t<u\leqslant 1
,
and choose
N
such that
2^{1-N}<u-t
. Then there exist
j
and
k

with
j2^{-N}\leqslant t\leqslant(j+1)2^{-N}<k2^{-N}\leqslant u\leqslant(k+1)2^{-N}
.
Hence, by what was just shown,
\varphi(t)\leqslant\varphi\bigl((j+1)2^{-N}\bigr)<\varphi\bigl(k2^{-N}\bigr)\leqslant\varphi(u)
. Thus,
\varphi
is
strictly increasing.

The monotonicity implies that any discontinuity of
\varphi
must be a
jump. Define

\displaystyle\Delta_{+}\varphi(t):=\lim_{u\searrow t}\varphi(u)-\varphi(t),\qquad t\in[0,1),

(2.15)

the jump to the right at
t
, and let
R:=\sup_{t\in[0,1)}\Delta_{+}(t)
.
It follows from (
2.4
) that
R=\frac{{\alpha\lor\beta}}{\alpha+\beta}R
,
and thus
R=0
. Hence,
\varphi
is right-continuous. Similarly,
\varphi

is left-continuous.

Third proof (recursive construction).

Yet another alternative, similar to the construction of Koch’s

is weakly increasing; it is

∎

is not only continuous but always locally Hölder continuous.

In this section, starting from the recursion (

assume that
\alpha,\beta>0
and

,

and

\displaystyle\lambda:=\log_{2}\frac{\alpha+\beta}{\alpha\vee\beta}\in(0,1].

(3.1)

Bounded variation, Lipschitz continuity and Hölder
continuity.

We recall some standard definitions. A function
\phi
is

Lipschitz continuous
on an interval
[a,b]
if there exists
a positive number
C
such that

|\phi(x)-\phi(y)|\leqslant C|x-y|\quad\text{(}x,y\in[a,b]\text{).}

(3.2)

This definition extends to
Hölder continuity
by replacing
the last inequality by

|\phi(x)-\phi(y)|\leqslant C|x-y|^{\gamma}\quad\text{(}x,y\in[a,b]\text{)},

(3.3)

for some
0<\gamma\leqslant 1
. Let
\mathrm{H}_{\gamma}[a,b]
be the
space of functions
\phi
on
[a,b]
such that the seminorm

\|\phi\|^{\prime}_{\mathrm{H}_{\gamma}[a,b]}:=\sup_{a\leqslant x<y\leqslant b}\frac{|\phi(x)-\phi(y)|}{|x-y|^{\gamma}}

(3.4)

is finite; this is a Banach space with the norm

\|\phi\|_{\mathrm{H}_{\gamma}[a,b]}:=\|\phi\|^{\prime}_{\mathrm{H}_{\gamma}[a,b]}+\sup_{x\in[a,b]}|\phi(x)|.

(3.5)

A function
\phi
is of
bounded variation
on
[a,b]
if its
total variation is bounded. Such a function is differentiable almost
everywhere. Let
\mathrm{BV}[a,b]
be the space of functions on

[a,b]
of bounded variation, with the norm

\|\phi\|_{\mathrm{BV}[a,b]}:=V(\phi;a,b)+\sup_{[a,b]}|\phi|,

(3.6)

where
V
denotes the total variation.

Note that both
\mathrm{BV}[a,b]
and
\mathrm{H}_{\gamma}[a,b]
are
Banach algebras. Also
\mathrm{H}_{1}\subset\mathrm{BV}
, namely,
Lipschitz continuity implies bounded variation.

4.1
Periodic equivalence

We introduce a simple notion here, very useful in identifying the
relation between sequences. The main case is when two sequences
f_{1}

and
f_{2}
both satisfy Theorem
2.10
(with the same
\varrho
), and
the corresponding periodic functions
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
are the same, or
more generally proportional. It will be convenient to be a bit more
general, and regard polynomial terms as trivial. We thus define:

Definition 4.1
.

Two sequences
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
are said to be
periodically
equivalent
if, for some
\varrho
,
f_{j}(n)=n^{\varrho}P_{j}(\log_{2}n)+p_{j}(n)
,

j=1,2
, where
p_{j}(n)
are polynomials and
P_{j}
are periodic
functions such that
{P}_{1}(k)=c{P}_{2}(k)
for some constant
c\neq 0
.
For simplicity, we write
f_{1}\Bumpeq f_{2}
.

It is possible to extend the definition to
asymptotically
periodic equivalence
by allowing
f_{j}(n)=n^{\varrho}P_{j}(\log_{2}n)+p_{j}(n)+o(n^{\varrho})
for
j=1,2
, but the above definition
without
o(1)
-term is sufficient for our use in this paper.

We begin with the simplest cases when
\Lambda_{\alpha,\beta}[f](n)=g(n)=0
.
Such cases cover also the situation when
g(n)=c
for
n\geqslant 2
because
normalising
f
by
\bar{f}(n):=f(n)+\frac{c}{\alpha+\beta-1}

yields the recurrence
\Lambda_{\alpha,\beta}[\bar{f}]=0
with

\bar{f}(1)=f(1)+\frac{c}{\alpha+\beta-1}
. For convenience of
reference, we state this observation, in a somewhat generalised form,
as a lemma.

Lemma 4.2
.

If
\alpha+\beta>1
, then two sequences defined by

\Lambda_{\alpha,\beta}[f_{1}]=0
with
f_{1}(1)\neq 0
and

\Lambda_{\alpha,\beta}[f_{2}]=c
are connected by

\displaystyle f_{2}(n)=\left(f_{2}(1)+\frac{c}{\alpha+\beta-1}\right)\frac{f_{1}(n)}{f_{1}(1)}-\frac{c}{\alpha+\beta-1}.

(4.3)

Hence, if
f_{2}(1)+\frac{c}{\alpha+\beta-1}\neq 0
, the sequences

f_{1}(n)
and
f_{2}(n)
are periodically equivalent with the underlying
periodic function
P
satisfying
P\in\mathrm{H}_{\lambda}[0,1]\cap\mathrm{BV}[0,1]
,
where
\lambda
is defined in (
3.1
).

Proof.

It is easy to verify that (
4.3
) satisfies the recursion

\Lambda_{\alpha,\beta}[f_{2}]=c
. and thus (
4.3
) holds by
induction. Thus
f_{1}\Bumpeq f_{2}
if
f_{2}(1)+\frac{c}{\alpha+\beta-1}\neq 0
. The Hölder continuity and bounded
variation of
P
follow from Lemma
3.4
.
∎

Lemma 4.3
.

Write
f\sim\widehat{\Lambda}_{\alpha,\beta}[c;d,e]
if

\Lambda_{\alpha,\beta}[f]=cn+\begin{cases}d,&\text{$n$ even}\\
e,&\text{$n$ odd}\end{cases}

Appendix C
Recurrences with minimisation or maximisation

Consider the class of sequences satisfying recurrences of the form

\displaystyle u(n)=\min_{1\leqslant k\leqslant\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor}\left\{\alpha u(k)+\beta u(n-k)\right\}\qquad(n\geqslant 2),

(C.1)

with
u(1)=1
.

This was studied in
[
5
]
, where it was shown that if

\alpha
and
\beta
are positive integers with
\beta\geqslant\alpha
, then the minimum in (
C.1
) is reached at

k=\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor
and the solution is given
by

u(n)=1+(\alpha+\beta-1)\sum_{1\leqslant j<n}w(j),

(C.2)

where

w(j):=\alpha^{L_{j}-\nu_{0}(j)}\beta^{\nu_{0}(j)},

(C.3)

and
\nu_{0}(j)
denotes the number of zeros in the binary expansion
of
j
. We will extend this result and the arguments in

[
5
]
, and prove the following.

Proposition C.1
.

Let
\alpha,\beta>0
be real numbers such that either

(i)

\beta\geqslant\alpha
and
\beta\geqslant 1
, or

(ii)

\alpha\geqslant\beta
and
\alpha+\beta\leqslant 1
.

Then the minimum in (
C.1
) is reached at
k=\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor
. Hence, (
C.1
) reduces to

\Lambda_{\alpha,\beta}[u]=0
, and thus (
C.1
) is solved by

u(n)=S_{\alpha,\beta}(n)
.

Proof.

We note first that
S_{\alpha,\beta}(n)
is given by the formula in (
C.2
)
for any
\alpha,\beta
. This follows by (
2.44
), (
2.22
), and
(
2.14
) in Section
2
, or by the proof in

[
5
]
.

It thus remains to show that if
u(n)
is defined by (
C.2
),
then

\displaystyle\alpha u(k)+\beta u(n-k)\geqslant\alpha u\bigl({\left\lfloor\tfrac{n}{2}\right\rfloor}\bigr)+\beta u\bigl({\left\lceil\tfrac{n}{2}\right\rceil}\bigr)

(C.4)

for
1\leqslant k<\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor
. By (
C.2
),
the difference between the two sides of (
C.4
) is

\displaystyle(\alpha+\beta-1)\left(\beta\sum_{\left\lceil\frac{n}{2}\right\rceil\leqslant j<n-k}w(j)-\alpha\sum_{k\leqslant j<\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor}w(j)\right).

(C.5)

To prove that this is non-negative, we will show that

\beta\sum_{\left\lceil\frac{n}{2}\right\rceil\leqslant j<n-k}w(j)\geqslant\alpha\sum_{k\leqslant j<\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor}w(j)

(C.6)

if
(i)
holds, and that (
C.6
) holds with the inequality
reversed if
(ii)
holds. The key is the following claim:

(a)

If
\beta\geqslant\alpha
and
\beta\geqslant 1
, then

\displaystyle\beta w(n+2^{j})\geqslant\alpha w(n),\quad\text{for all }n\geqslant 1\text{ and }j\geqslant 0.

(C.7)

(b)

If
\alpha\geqslant\beta
and
\beta\leqslant 1
, then

\displaystyle\beta w(n+2^{j})\leqslant\alpha w(n),\quad\text{for all }n\geqslant 1\text{ and }j\geqslant 0.

(C.8)

Proof of the claim.
From (
C.3
), we have

\displaystyle\frac{\beta w(n+2^{j})}{\alpha w(n)}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\beta}{\alpha}\right)^{1+\nu_{0}(n+2^{j})-\nu_{0}(n)}\alpha^{L_{n+2^{j}}-L_{n}}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\beta}{\alpha}\right)^{1+\nu_{0}(n+2^{j})-\nu_{0}(n)-(L_{n+2^{j}}-L_{n})}\beta^{L_{n+2^{j}}-L_{n}}.

(C.9)

It is easily seen that

1+\nu_{0}(n+2^{j})-\nu_{0}(n)\geqslant L_{n+2^{j}}-L_{n}\geqslant 0.

(C.10)

Both parts of the claim thus follow from (
C
). ∎

To show (
C.6
), or its converse in case
(ii)
, which will
then complete the proof of the proposition, we combine the claim
above with a pairing between the sets, with

m:=\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor-k
,

\left\{\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor-m,\cdots,\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor-1\right\}\quad\text{and}\quad\left\{\left\lceil\frac{n}{2}\right\rceil,\cdots,\left\lceil\frac{n}{2}\right\rceil+m-1\right\},

(C.11)

such that the difference between the elements of each pair is a power
of
2
. In other words, the proof is completed by applying the
following lemma (with a translation).
∎

Lemma C.2
.

Let
n\geqslant 1
and

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle:=\{1,2,\cdots,n\},

(C.12)

\displaystyle C_{1}

\displaystyle:=\{n+1,n+2,\cdots,2n\},

(C.13)

\displaystyle C_{2}

\displaystyle:=\{n+2,n+3,\cdots,2n+1\}.

(C.14)

There exist one-to-one mappings

h_{1}:A\rightarrow C_{1}\quad\text{and}\quad h_{2}:A\rightarrow C_{2},

(C.15)

such that for each
k\in A
there exist
j_{1},j_{2}
with

\displaystyle h_{1}(k)

\displaystyle=k+2^{j_{1}},\quad\text{for some }j_{1}\geqslant 0,

(C.16)

\displaystyle h_{2}(k)

\displaystyle=k+2^{j_{1}},\quad\text{for some }j_{2}\geqslant 0.

(C.17)

Proof.

We prove the existence of
h_{1}
by induction. Write
n=2^{\ell}+j

for some
\ell\geqslant 0
and
0\leqslant j<2^{\ell}
. (Thus,

\ell=L_{n}
.) We want to show that there exist a one-to-one mapping

h_{1}:\{1,\cdots,2^{\ell}+j\}\rightarrow\{2^{\ell}+j+1,\cdots,2^{\ell+1}+2j\},

(C.18)

such that for each
1\leqslant k\leqslant 2^{\ell}+j
, (
C.16
)
holds.

If
j=0
, we simply define
h_{1}(k):=k+2^{\ell}
.

If
j\geqslant 1
, we first define
h_{1}(k)
for
k\leqslant 2j
by

h_{1}(k):=k+2^{\ell+1}
. This gives a mapping from
\{1,2,\cdots,2j\}
to
\{2^{\ell+1}+1,\cdots,2^{\ell+1}+2j\}
. We remove these
two blocks, and it remains to define a one-to-one mapping satisfying
(
C.16
) between
\{2j+1,\cdots,2^{\ell}+j\}
and

\{2^{\ell}+j+1,\cdots,2^{\ell+1}\}
. By subtracting
2j
from each
term, it is equivalent to showing that there exist such a mapping
from
\{1,\cdots,m\}
to
\{m+1,\cdots,2m\}
, where

m=2^{\ell}-j
; this is true by the induction hypothesis.

The proof of the existence of
h_{2}
is similar (but with

n+1=2^{\ell}+j
); we omit the details.
∎

If we replace
\min
by
\max
in (
C.1
), we obtain a similar
result; see also
[
32
]
for
\alpha>\beta=1
.

Proposition C.3
.

Let
\alpha,\beta>0
be real numbers such that
\alpha\geqslant\beta
,

\beta\leqslant 1
, and
\alpha+\beta\geqslant 1
. Then the maximum

\displaystyle u(n)=\max_{1\leqslant k\leqslant\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor}\left\{\alpha u(k)+\beta u(n-k)\right\}\qquad(n\geqslant 2),

(C.19)

with

, is attained at

. Hence, (
C.19
) reduces to

\Lambda_{\alpha,\beta}[u]=0
, and thus (
C.19
) is solved by

.

Proof.

The proof above shows that under these conditions, (
C.8
)
holds, and hence (
C.6
) holds in the opposite direction. Thus the
difference in (
C.5
) is
\leqslant 0
. (We do not obtain a second case
with
\alpha+\beta<1
; we then would need (
C.7
), but the condition

\beta\geqslant 1
in
(a)
above is incompatible with

∎

The results above can be extended to the recurrences of the form

\displaystyle u(n)=\min_{1\leqslant k\leqslant\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor}\left\{\alpha u(k)+\beta u(n-k)\right\}+c\qquad(n\geqslant 2),

(C.20)

with the same conditions as above on
\alpha
and
\beta
; see

[

]
for more general versions. The solution
for the recurrence

\displaystyle u(n)=\alpha u\left(\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor\right)+\beta u\left(\left\lceil\frac{n}{2}\right\rceil\right)+c

(C.21)

is, with
w(j)
as in (

)–(

) above (see also

Appendix D
Nowhere differentiability of
P_{\text{A006581}}(t)

We prove in this appendix the fractal nature of the periodic function

P(t)
arising from A006581 (discussed in Example
5.6
with Fourier
expansion given in (
5.20
)), namely,

\Lambda_{2,2}[f]=g
where
g(n):=\{\frac{n}{2}\}(n-1)
:

\displaystyle f(n)=n^{2}P(\log_{2}n),

(D.1)

where, with
\bar{g}(x):=g(x)/x^{2}
,

\displaystyle P(t)=\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}4^{-m-t}g(2^{m+t})=\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\bar{g}(2^{m+t}).

(D.2)

Here
g(x)
is extended from
g(n)
as in (
2
) with
\varphi(t)=t
:

\displaystyle\bar{g}(x)=\frac{1}{2x^{2}}\times\begin{cases}\{x\}\lfloor x\rfloor,&\text{if }\lfloor x\rfloor\text{ is even};\\
(1-\{x\})(\lfloor x\rfloor-1),&\text{if }\lfloor x\rfloor\text{ is odd}.\end{cases}

(D.3)

Since
\bar{g}(2^{m+t})=0
for
t\in[0,1]
and
m\leqslant 0
, we have

\displaystyle P(t)=\sum_{m\geqslant 1}\bar{g}(2^{m+t})\qquad(0\leqslant t\leqslant 1).

(D.4)

The method of proof used here to prove the nowhere differentiability
of
P
is standard and similar to that for the Takagi function given
in the survey paper
[
1
]
.

Let
t\in[0,1)
, and define

\displaystyle\tau_{n}:=\log_{2}\frac{\lfloor 2^{n+t}\rfloor}{2^{n}}\quad\text{and}\quad\tau_{n}^{\prime}:=\log_{2}\frac{\lfloor 2^{n+t}\rfloor+1}{2^{n}}.

(D.5)

To prove that
P
does not have a finite derivative at
t
, it
suffices to show that the sequence

\displaystyle\frac{P(\tau_{n})-P(\tau_{n}^{\prime})}{\tau_{n}-\tau_{n}^{\prime}}=\sum_{1\leqslant m\leqslant n}\frac{\bar{g}(2^{m+\tau_{n}})-\bar{g}(2^{m+\tau_{n}^{\prime}})}{\tau_{n}-\tau_{n}^{\prime}}

(D.6)

does not converge to a finite limit. Here we used the relation

\bar{g}(2^{m+\tau_{n}})=\bar{g}(2^{m+\tau_{n}^{\prime}})=0
for
m>n
. Now
for
\theta\in[0,1)

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} \bar{g}(2k+\theta)&=\frac{k\theta}{(2k+\theta)^{2}}\\
\bar{g}(2k+1+\theta)&=\frac{k(1-\theta)}{(2k+1+\theta)^{2}}\end{aligned}\right.

(D.7)

so that, taking the right derivative at integer points here and below,

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} \bar{g}^{\prime}(2k+\theta)&=\frac{k(2k-\theta)}{2(2k+\theta)^{3}}\\
\bar{g}^{\prime}(2k+1+\theta)&=-\frac{k(2k+3-\theta)}{(2k+1+\theta)^{3}}.\end{aligned}\right.

(D.8)

If
1\leqslant m\leqslant n
, then

\displaystyle\lfloor 2^{m+\tau_{n}}\rfloor\leqslant 2^{m+\tau_{n}}\leqslant 2^{m+t}<2^{m+\tau^{\prime}_{n}}\leqslant\lfloor 2^{m+\tau_{n}}\rfloor+1.

(D.9)

It follows that
h(x):=\bar{g}(2^{x})
is infinitely differentiable on

[m+\tau_{n},m+\tau_{n}^{\prime}]
, and it is easily seen from (
D.8
) that

h^{\prime\prime}(x)=O(1)
(uniformly in
m
and
n
). We have, for some

\tau^{\prime\prime}_{n}\in(\tau_{n},\tau^{\prime}_{n})
,

\displaystyle\frac{\bar{g}(2^{m+\tau_{n}})-\bar{g}(2^{m+\tau_{n}^{\prime}})}{\tau_{n}-\tau_{n}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\frac{h(m+\tau_{n})-h(m+\tau_{n}^{\prime})}{\tau_{n}-\tau_{n}^{\prime}}=h^{\prime}(m+\tau^{\prime\prime}_{n})

\displaystyle=h^{\prime}(m+t)+O(|\tau^{\prime\prime}_{n}-t|)=h^{\prime}(m+t)+O(|\tau^{\prime}_{n}-\tau_{n}|)

\displaystyle=h^{\prime}(m+t)+O(2^{-n}).

(D.10)

Hence, (
D.6
) implies that

\displaystyle\frac{P(\tau_{n})-P(\tau_{n}^{\prime})}{\tau_{n}-\tau_{n}^{\prime}}=\sum_{1\leqslant m\leqslant n}h^{\prime}(m+t)+O(n2^{-n}),

(D.11)

and thus, if
P
is differentiable at
t
, then the sum

\displaystyle\sum_{m\geqslant 1}h^{\prime}(m+t)

(D.12)

converges (and equals
P^{\prime}(t)
).

On the other hand, it follows easily from (
D.8
) that if

x\geqslant 2
, then
x\bar{g}^{\prime}(x)\geqslant\frac{1}{9}
for even
\lfloor x\rfloor
and
x\bar{g}^{\prime}(x)\leqslant-\frac{1}{4}
for odd
\lfloor x\rfloor
. Hence,
|h^{\prime}(m+t)|=|2^{m+t}\bar{g}^{\prime}(2^{m+t})\log 2|\geqslant\frac{\log 2}{9}
for all
m\geqslant 1
. Consequently,
the sum (
D.12
) diverges for any
t
, and thus
P
is nowhere
differentiable.

Moreover, we note that if
2^{t}
is a dyadic rational, then for all
large
m
,
2^{m+t}
is an even integer, and thus

h^{\prime}(m+t)\geqslant\frac{\log 2}{9}
. Hence, in this case the sum (
D.12
)
diverges to
+\infty
, and thus so does

\bigl(P(\tau_{n})-P(\tau^{\prime}_{n})\bigr)/(\tau_{n}-\tau^{\prime}_{n})
in
(
D.6
); consequently,
P
is not Lipschitz.

We do not know whether
P
is Hölder continuous, and leave that
as an open problem. Note that Lemma
3.4
does not apply since
(
3.10
) does not hold.
