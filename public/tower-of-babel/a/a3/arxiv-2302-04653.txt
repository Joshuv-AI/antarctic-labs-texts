Introduction to rough paths theory
M. Ghani Varzaneh, S. Riedel

Abstract
. These notes are an extended version of the course “Introduction to rough paths theory” given at the XXV Brazilian School of Probability in Campinas in August 2022. Their aim is to give a concise overview to Lyons’ theory of rough paths with a special focus on applications to stochastic differential equations.



2020 Mathematics Subject Classification
60L20

1.
Introduction

Rough paths theory, as we know it today, originates from a series of papers T. Lyons wrote in the 90s, cf.
[
Lyo98
]
and the references therein. In his work, Lyons obtained a deep understanding of paths with low regularity and their interaction within nonlinear systems. One strong motivation for a study of irregular paths is their ubiquity in stochastic analysis. In fact, there are various examples of rescaled random systems that converge to objects with “rough” behaviour. The most prominent example is, of course, the
Brownian motion
(Bm) which is a rescaled version of a whole class of random walks. Due to its universality, the Brownian motion plays a key role in stochastic modelling. An important example is a stochastic differential equation in which the “noise” is modelled by the (formal) derivative of a Brownian motion. For instance, let us look at the equation

(1.1)

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}Y_{t}=\sigma(Y_{t})\,\mathrm{d}B_{t}(\omega)

where
B(\omega)
is the trajectory of a Brownian motion and
\sigma
a nonlinear function. Although this equation might look like an innocent non-autonomous random ordinary differential equation, it constitutes a great challenge if we want to analyze it with the tools of classical analysis (we will see in these notes some explanations why this is the case). A major contribution to the understanding of the equation (
1.1
) was made in the 50s by K. Itō who gave a rigorous meaning to it using a
stochastic integral
that nowadays bears his name. Itō did not view (
1.1
) as an ordinary differential equation for every trajectory (what is called a
pathwise
point of view) but put forward the
probabilistic
properties of the Brownian motion, namely, its martingale property. His integral is defined using an isometry on a space of martingales, not referring to single trajectories anymore. Eventually, he understood (
1.1
) as an equation on a space of stochastic processes. Itō’s stochastic calculus was (and is!) extremely successful. Still, a pathwise understanding of (
1.1
) is desirable in many situations, and one of Lyons’ goals was to provide the ground for it.

The present text focuses on defining solutions to
rough differential equations
for which (
1.1
) is a prototype. On the journey to this overall goal, we will touch several key aspects of rough paths theory. These notes are almost self-contained as we give formal proofs of the stated results wherever possible. However, some calculations will be omitted in order not to overload the reader with technical details, but references are given in that case, though. We hope that the reader can use this text to get an idea of what rough paths theory is about, why it was invented and what it can be used for.

There are some branches of rough paths theory we were not able to discuss in these notes, and we want to mention two of them here. The first concerns applications of rough paths in the field of stochastic
partial
differential equations. The most famous result here is probably M. Hairer’s solution to the KPZ-equation that was constructed with the help of rough paths theory
[
Hai13
]
. Later, Hairer systematically expanded his ideas and built a whole solution theory for a class of stochastic partial differential equations that he called the theory of
regularity structures

[
Hai14
]
. The reader who is interested in these topics is referred to
[
FH20
, Chapter 12 – 15]
and
[
Hai15
]
for an overview. A second complex we were not able to touch concerns the relationship between rough paths theory and machine learning. In fact, the so-called
signature method
is a very powerful tool that can be used to analyze and forecast very different kinds of data streams. For an introduction to this method, the reader may consult
[
CK16
]
and
[
LM22
]
.

Several monographs about rough paths theory are available now, cf. e.g.
[
LQ02
,
LCL07
,
FV10b
,
FH20
]
. The structure of our notes has some similarities to
[
LCL07
]
, but our notation and the proofs we present are closer to
[
FH20
]
. In particular, we wanted to present the important notion of a
controlled path
introduced by Gubinelli
[
Gub04
]
, since this concept plays a prominent role also in regularity structures. In this context, we discuss a more recent result about the geometry of controlled paths in Section
6.1
. In this form, these results did not appear elsewhere yet.

1.1.
Notation

A
path
denotes a continuous function defined on a compact interval with values in a topological space. If
E
is a topological vector space and
X\colon[0,T]\to E
a path, we call
X_{t}-X_{s}
with
s,t\in[0,T]
an
increment
of the path. We will use the notation
\delta X_{s,t}\coloneqq X_{t}-X_{s}
. If
(E,|\cdot|)
is a normed space, we define for a function
\Xi
defined on a simplex
\Xi\colon\{0\leq s\leq t\leq T\}\to E
and
\alpha>0
the quantity

\displaystyle\|\Xi\|_{\alpha}\coloneqq\sup_{s<t}\frac{|\Xi_{s,t}|}{|t-s|^{\alpha}}.

If
X\colon[0,T]\to E
is a path and
\alpha\in(0,1]
,
\|X\|_{\alpha}\coloneqq\|\delta X\|_{\alpha}
is the usual
\alpha
-Hölder seminorm. A
partition
of an interval
[s,t]
is a finite set of points
\mathcal{P}=\{s=t_{0}<\ldots<t_{N}=t\}
. We will also view the partition
\mathcal{P}
as a set of closed intervals
\mathcal{P}=\{[t_{i},t_{i+1}]\,:\,i=0,\ldots,N-1\}
. The
mesh size
of
\mathcal{P}
is defined as
|\mathcal{P}|\coloneqq\max_{[u,v]\in\mathcal{P}}|v-u|
. For two Banach spaces
V
and
W
,
L(V,W)
denotes the space of continuous linear functions from
V
to
W
. The space
L(V,W)
itself is equipped with the operator norm

\displaystyle\|\Phi\|\coloneqq\sup_{v\neq 0}\frac{|\Phi v|}{|v|},\quad\Phi\in L(V,W).

By
C
, we will mostly mean a generic constant that depends on the
aforementioned parameters. If we want to emphasize the dependence on a certain parameter
p
, we use the notation
C_{p}
. In a series of (in-)equalities, the actual value of this constant may change from line to line.

2.
Motivation: Fractional Brownian motion

In this section, we present some background about the
fractional Brownian motion
. These processes form a natural generalization of the
Brownian motion
and were first introduced by Mandelbrot and van Ness in
[
MVN68
]
. Let us first recall the definition of a Gaussian process.

Definition 2.1
.

A stochastic process
X\colon[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
is called
Gaussian
if for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
and every
t_{1},\ldots,t_{k}\in[0,\infty)
, the random variable
(X_{t_{1}},\ldots,X_{t_{k}})
is a multivariate Gaussian random variable.

Note that the law of a Gaussian process is completely determined by the mean function
\mathbb{E}(X_{t})
,
t\in[0,\infty)
, and the covariance function
\operatorname{cov}(X_{s},X_{t})
,
s,t\in[0,\infty)
, of the process.

Definition 2.2

(Mandelbrot, van Ness ’68)
.

Let
H\in(0,1)
. The
fractional Brownian motion
(fBm) is a continuous zero mean Gaussian process
B^{H}\colon[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
starting at
0
with covariance function given by

\displaystyle R(s,t)\coloneqq\operatorname{cov}(B^{H}_{s},B^{H}_{t})=\mathbb{E}(B^{H}_{s}B^{H}_{t})=\frac{1}{2}\left(|t|^{2H}+|s|^{2H}-|t-s|^{2H}\right).

The parameter
H\in(0,1)
is called
Hurst parameter
.

Remark 2.3
.

For
H=\frac{1}{2}
, one obtains
R(s,t)=\min\{s,t\}
, i.e.
B^{H}
is the usual Brownian motion (Bm).

Below, we show typical trajectories of the fractional Brownian motion with different Hurst parameters.

Figure 1.
Trajectories of a fractional Brownian motion

For later purposes, we will list some properties of the fractional Brownian motion here. These and others can be found e.g. in
[
Nua06
, Chapter 5]
and
[
BHOZ08
]
.

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
B^{H}\colon[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
be a fractional Brownian motion with Hurst parameter
H\in(0,1)
. Then the following holds:

(i)

B^{H}
has stationary increments, i.e. for every
s\geq 0
, we have

\displaystyle(B^{H}_{t+s}-B^{H}_{s})_{t\geq 0}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{D}}}{{=}}(B^{H}_{t})_{t\geq 0}.

(ii)

B^{H}
is self-similar with index
H
, i.e. for every
a>0
,

\displaystyle(B^{H}_{at})_{t\geq 0}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{D}}}{{=}}(a^{-H}B^{H}_{t})_{t\geq 0}.

Proof.

Exercise.
∎

The Hurst parameter describes the behaviour of the process. One easy observation is the following:

Proposition 2.5
.

The increments of the fractional Brownian motion are

(1)

uncorrelated for
H=\frac{1}{2}
,

(2)

positively correlated for
H>\frac{1}{2}
,

(3)

negatively correlated for
H<\frac{1}{2}
.

Remark 2.6
.

•

In stochastic modelling, the term
noise
usually denotes the formal derivative of the Brownian motion
B
. To give a rigorous definition,
\dot{B}
is understood as a random
generalized function
or
distribution
. More precisely,

\displaystyle\langle\dot{B},\phi\rangle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\phi(s)\mathrm{d}B_{s}

for every smooth function
\phi\colon[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
with compact support. In particular,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(\langle\dot{B},\phi\rangle\langle\dot{B},\psi\rangle)=\mathbb{E}\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}\phi(s)\,\mathrm{d}B_{s}\int_{0}^{\infty}\psi(s)\,\mathrm{d}B_{s}\right)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\phi(s)\psi(s)\,\mathrm{d}s.

This suggests that
\mathbb{E}(\dot{B}_{t})=0
and

(2.1)

\displaystyle\operatorname{cov}(\dot{B}_{s},\dot{B}_{t})=\mathbb{E}(\dot{B}_{s}\dot{B}_{t})=\delta_{st}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }s=t,\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

for every
s,t\in[0,\infty)
. Note, however, that these identities are only formal since the indicator functions
\mathbbm{1}_{[0,t]}
are not smooth and thus not a valid choice for
\phi
and
\psi
. Still, (
2.1
) justifies the name
white noise
for
\dot{B}
. For the fractional Brownian motion
B^{H}
, one can make a similar (formal) calculation that indicates that the process
\dot{B}^{H}
is stationary but has non-vanishing correlations for
H\neq\frac{1}{2}
. Sometimes, this kind of noise is called
colored
.

•

Using the fractional Brownian motion instead of the Brownian motion for modelling random phenomena can be more realistic in case of models with memory. For instance, it was used to model price processes in illiquid markets (electricity markets, gas markets etc.)

A generic form of a stochastic differential equation (SDE) driven by a fractional Brownian motion is

(2.2)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathrm{d}Y_{t}&=b(Y_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sigma_{i}(Y_{t})\,\mathrm{d}B^{H;i}_{t}\\
Y_{0}&=y_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\end{split}

where
B^{H}=(B^{H;1},\ldots,B^{H;d})
is a
d
-dimensional fractional Brownian motion
, i.e. a vector of independent one-dimensional fractional Brownian motion,
b,\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{d}\colon\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
is a collection of vector fields and
Y\colon[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
is a stochastic process we aim to call a solution to (
2.2
).
The fundamental problem is:
How should we interpret (
2.2
)?
Or, in other words:
What properties should the process
Y
satisfy to call it a
solution
to the stochastic differential equation (
2.2
)
?

First attempt:
If the trajectories of
B^{H}
, i.e. the paths
t\mapsto B^{H}_{t}(\omega)
,
\omega\in\Omega
, were differentiable, we could interpret (
2.2
)
pathwise
as a random (non-autonomous) ordinary differential equation (ODE):

(2.3)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}Y_{t}}{\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle=b(Y_{t})+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sigma^{i}(Y_{t})\frac{\mathrm{d}B^{H;i}_{t}(\omega)}{\mathrm{d}t}.

However, we will see now that this attempt fails.

Lemma 2.7
.

For a fractional Brownian motion
B^{H}
and
p>0
, we have

(2.4)

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{2^{n}}|B^{H}_{j2^{-n}}-B^{H}_{(j-1)2^{-n}}|^{p}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbb{P}}}{{\to}}\begin{cases}0&\text{if }pH>1,\\
\infty&\text{if }pH<1\end{cases}

as
n\to\infty
.

Proof.

Define

\displaystyle Y_{n}\coloneqq\sum_{j=1}^{2^{n}}|B^{H}_{j2^{-n}}-B^{H}_{(j-1)2^{-n}}|^{p}(2^{n})^{pH-1}.

By the scaling property,

\displaystyle Y_{n}=\sum_{j=1}^{2^{n}}|B^{H}_{j2^{-n}}-B^{H}_{(j-1)2^{-n}}|^{p}(2^{n})^{pH-1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{D}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{j=1}^{2^{n}}|B^{H}_{j}-B^{H}_{(j-1)}|^{p}\eqqcolon\tilde{Y}_{n}.

Since the fractional Brownian motion has stationary increments, the sequence
(B^{H}_{j}-B^{H}_{(j-1)})_{j\geq 1}
is stationary. Therefore, by Birkhoff’s ergodic theorem,

\displaystyle\tilde{Y}_{n}\to\mathbb{E}(|B^{H}_{1}|^{p})\eqqcolon c_{p}>0

almost surely and in
L^{1}
as
n\to\infty
. It follows that
Y_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{D}}}{{\to}}c_{p}
and, consequently,
Y_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbb{P}}}{{\to}}c_{p}
as
n\to\infty
. From this, the claim follows.

∎

Proposition 2.8
.

On any interval
[0,T]
, the fractional Brownian motion is not continuously differentiable almost surely.

Proof.

By rescaling, we can assume w.l.o.g. that
[0,T]=[0,1]
. Assume that
B^{H}
is continuously differentiable with positive probability on
[0,1]
. Then there is a random constant
C>0
that is finite with positive probability such that
|B^{H}_{t}-B^{H}_{s}|\leq C|t-s|
for every
s,t\in[0,1]
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{2^{n}}|B^{H}_{j2^{-n}}-B^{H}_{(j-1)2^{-n}}|\leq\frac{C}{2^{n}}\sum_{j=1}^{2^{n}}j-(j-1)=C<\infty

for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
with positive probability which is a contradiction to Lemma
2.7
.
∎

Remark 2.9
.

There is a stronger statement saying that the fractional Brownian motion is nowhere differentiable with probability one that can be deduced from a general result on Gaussian processes, cf.
[
KK71
]
. However, we will not need this stronger statement here.

Motivated by partial differential equations, one might have the idea to weaken the notion of differentiability in order to give a meaning to (
2.3
). We could interpret
\frac{\mathrm{d}B^{H;i}_{t}(\omega)}{\mathrm{d}t}
as a
weak derivative
, i.e. as a
distribution
or
generalized function

[
Str03
,
Eva10
]
. However, this will lead to another problem: the equation (
2.3
) contains products
\sigma^{i}(Y_{t})\cdot\frac{\mathrm{d}B^{H;i}_{t}(\omega)}{\mathrm{d}t}
of non-smooth functions with distributions, and such products are (in general) not well defined
[
Sch54
]
.

Second attempt:
In stochastic analysis, the Itō integral
\int Y\,\mathrm{d}X
is defined in case of
X
being a semimartingale and
Y
being adapted to the filtration generated by
X
. One could try to interpret (
2.2
) as an integral equation

\displaystyle Y_{t}=Y_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}b(Y_{s})\,\mathrm{d}s+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\int_{0}^{t}\sigma^{i}(Y_{s})\,\mathrm{d}B^{H,i}_{s}

where the stochastic integral is understood in Itō-sense. However, one can prove the following:

Proposition 2.10
.

The fractional Brownian motion
B^{H}
is not a semimartingale unless
H=\frac{1}{2}
.

Proof.

This is another consequence of Lemma
2.7
: If the fractional Brownian motion was a semimartingale, the sum (
2.4
) would converge in probability for
p=2
to the quadratic variation process evaluated at
1
. Since this random variable is finite almost surely, this is a contradiction to Lemma
2.7
in the case
H<\frac{1}{2}
. For
H>\frac{1}{2}
, Lemma
2.7
implies that the quadratic variation process equals 0 almost surely. This means that the martingale part in the semimartingale decomposition vanishes and that the fractional Brownian motion has almost surely paths of finite variation. This, however, is a contradiction to Lemma
2.7
when choosing
p=1
.
∎

This shows that the classical Itō approach is not applicable to the fractional Brownian motion, too.

Third attempt:
In 1936, L.C. Young introduced a notion of an integral that generalizes Riemann-Stieltjes integration
[
You36
]
. More concretely, he defined an integral for functions
f,g\colon[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}
that are Hölder continuous with Hölder index
\alpha\in(0,1]
resp.
\beta\in(0,1]
of the form
\int f\,\mathrm{d}g
provided
\alpha+\beta>1
. To employ this approach, we first need to understand the regularity of the fractional Brownian motion. The following theorem is a classical result:

Theorem 2.11

(Kolmogorov-Chentsov)
.

Let
X\colon[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}
be a continuous stochastic process,
q\geq 2
,
\beta>\frac{1}{q}
and assume that

\displaystyle\|X_{t}-X_{s}\|_{L^{q}}\leq C|t-s|^{\beta}

for a constant
C>0
and any
s,t\in[0,T]
. Then for all
\alpha\in[0,\beta-1/q)
, there is a random variable
K_{\alpha}\in L^{q}
such that

\displaystyle|X_{t}-X_{s}|\leq K_{\alpha}|t-s|^{\alpha}

for all
s,t\in[0,T]
. In particular, the trajectories of
X
are almost surely
\alpha
-Hölder continuous.

Proof.

The proof is classical and can be found e.g. in
[
RY99
, (2.1) Theorem]
. Since we will use similar arguments later for proving Theorem
4.10
, we provide a full proof here.

Without loss of generality, we can assume that
T=1
. Set

\displaystyle D_{n}=\{k2^{-n}\,:\,k=0,\ldots,2^{n}\}\quad\text{and}\quad D=\cup_{n\geq 0}D_{n}.

We further define the random variables

\displaystyle K_{n}\coloneqq\sup_{t\in D_{n}}|\delta X_{t,t+2^{-n}}|,\quad\delta X_{t,t+2^{-n}}=X_{t+2^{-n}}-X_{t}.

Then it holds that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(K^{q}_{n})\leq\mathbb{E}\sum_{t\in D_{n}}|\delta X_{t,t+2^{-n}}|^{q}\leq\frac{1}{|D_{n}|}C^{q}|D_{n}|^{q\beta}=C^{q}|D_{n}|^{q\beta-1}

where
|D_{n}|=2^{-n}
. Fix
s<t\in D
and choose
m
such that
|D_{m+1}|<t-s\leq|D_{m}|
. Going from coarser to finer partitions successively, we can find
\tau_{0},\ldots,\tau_{N}\in\cup_{n\geq m+1}D_{n}
such that

\displaystyle s=\tau_{0}<\tau_{1}<\ldots<\tau_{N}=t

with the property that at most two intervals of the form
[\tau_{i},\tau_{i+1}]
have the same length.
With this choice, it follows that

\displaystyle|\delta X_{s,t}|\leq\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}|\delta X_{\tau_{i},\tau_{i+1}}|\leq 2\sum_{n\geq m+1}K_{n}.

We thus obtain

\displaystyle\frac{|\delta X_{s,t}|}{|t-s|^{\alpha}}\leq\sum_{n\geq m+1}\frac{2K_{n}}{|D_{m+1}|^{\alpha}}\leq\sum_{n\geq m+1}\frac{2K_{n}}{|D_{n}|^{\alpha}}\leq 2\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{K_{n}}{|D_{n}|^{\alpha}}\eqqcolon K_{\alpha}.

Therefore, we have shown that

\displaystyle|\delta X_{s,t}|\leq K_{\alpha}|t-s|^{\alpha}

for every
s,t\in D
. By continuity of
X
, this bound holds in fact for every
s,t\in[0,1]
. It remains to check that
K_{\alpha}
is in
L^{q}
. Indeed,

\displaystyle\|K_{\alpha}\|_{L^{q}}\leq 2\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{\|K_{n}\|_{L^{q}}}{|D_{n}|^{\alpha}}\leq 2C\sum_{n\geq 0}|D_{n}|^{\beta-\frac{1}{q}-\alpha}

which is summable by assumption on
\alpha
. This proves the theorem.
∎

Remark 2.12
.

Often, the formulation of the Kolmogorov-Chentsov theorem does not assume that
X
is continuous. The statement then says that
X
has a Hölder-continuous
modification

\tilde{X}
, i.e.
X_{t}=\tilde{X}_{t}
almost surely for every
t
. Note that the proof above yields the same statement: instead of using continuity of
X
, we define a process
\tilde{X}
to coincide with
X
on the dyadic numbers
D
and extend it continuously to the whole interval
[0,1]
. One can check that
\tilde{X}
is a modification of
X
.

Using the Kolmogorov-Chentsov theorem, we can deduce an important property concerning the trajectories of a fractional Brownian motion:

Corollary 2.13
.

The trajectories of the fractional Brownian motion are almost surely
\alpha
-Hölder continuous for every
\alpha<H
.

Proof.

By definition,

\displaystyle\quad\|B^{H}_{t}-B^{H}_{s}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=\mathbb{E}\big((B^{H}_{t}-B^{H}_{s})^{2}\big)=\mathbb{E}(B^{H}_{t}B^{H}_{t})-2\mathbb{E}(B^{H}_{t}B^{H}_{s})+\mathbb{E}(B^{H}_{s}B^{H}_{s})=|t-s|^{2H}

for every
s,t
. Since
B^{H}
is Gaussian, all
L^{q}
-norms are equivalent. Therefore,

\displaystyle\|B^{H}_{t}-B^{H}_{s}\|_{L^{q}}^{q}\leq C_{q}^{q}(\|B^{H}_{t}-B^{H}_{s}\|_{L^{2}}^{2})^{\frac{q}{2}}=C_{q}^{q}|t-s|^{qH},

for every
q\geq 2
. The result now follows from Theorem
2.11
.
∎

Corollary
2.13
opens the possibility to understand the integral that appears in the integrated equation (
2.2
) using Young’s integration theory. We will follow this approach in the next section.

3.
Sewing lemma and Young’s integral

3.1.
The Sewing lemma

In rough path theory, the Sewing lemma is one of the cornerstones which will allow us to define integrals. In this part, we present this result and show how it can be used to define Young integrals. Before doing this, we will introduce some more notation.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
W
be a Banach space.

(1)

\mathcal{C}([0,T],W)
will denote the space of continuous functions
f\colon[0,T]\to W
.

(2)

For
\alpha\in(0,1]
,
\mathcal{C}^{\alpha}([0,T],W)
is defined as the space of
\alpha
-Hölder continuous functions
f\colon[0,T]\to W
, i.e.
f\in\mathcal{C}^{\alpha}([0,T],W)
if and only if

\displaystyle\|f\|_{\alpha}=\sup_{s<t}\frac{|\delta f_{s,t}|}{|t-s|^{\alpha}}<\infty.

(3)

The space
\mathcal{C}_{2}^{\alpha,\beta}([0,T],W)
denotes the space of functions
\Xi
defined on the simplex
\{(s,t)\in[0,T]^{2}\,:\,s\leq t\}
such that
\Xi_{t,t}=0
and

\displaystyle\|\Xi\|_{\alpha,\beta}\coloneqq\|\Xi\|_{\alpha}+\|\delta\Xi\|_{\beta}<\infty

where

\displaystyle\delta\Xi_{s,u,t}\coloneqq\Xi_{s,t}-\Xi_{s,u}-\Xi_{u,t},\qquad\|\delta\Xi\|_{\beta}\coloneqq\sup_{s<u<t}\frac{|\delta\Xi_{s,u,t}|}{|t-s|^{\beta}}.

We can now formulate the Sewing lemma.

Lemma 3.2

(Sewing lemma)
.

Let
0<\alpha\leq 1<\beta
. Then there exists a unique continuous linear map
\mathcal{I}\colon\mathcal{C}^{\alpha,\beta}_{2}([0,T],W)\to\mathcal{C}^{\alpha}([0,T],W)
such that
(\mathcal{I}\Xi)_{0}=0
and

(3.1)

\displaystyle|\delta\mathcal{I}\Xi_{s,t}-\Xi_{s,t}|\leq\|\delta\Xi\|_{\beta}\big[2^{\beta}(\zeta(\beta)-1)+1\big]|t-s|^{\beta}

where
C>0
depends on
\beta
and
\zeta
denotes the Riemann zeta function

\displaystyle\zeta(s)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n^{s}}.

Moreover,

\displaystyle\delta\mathcal{I}\Xi_{s,t}=\lim_{|\mathcal{P}|\to 0}\sum_{[u,v]\in\mathcal{P}}\Xi_{u,v}.

Proof.

Let us prove uniqueness first. Assume that
I
and
\tilde{I}
both satisfy (
3.1
). Then it holds that

\displaystyle|(I-\tilde{I})_{t}-(I-\tilde{I})_{s}|\leq C|t-s|^{\beta}.

Since
\beta>1
and
I-\tilde{I}
is a path,
I-\tilde{I}
is constant. Since
I_{0}=\tilde{I}_{0}=0
, uniqueness follows.
Now fix an interval
[s,t]
and a partition
\mathcal{P}=\{s=u_{0}<u_{1}<\ldots<u_{r}=t\}
of this interval.
We set

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{P}}\Xi\coloneqq\sum_{[u,v]\in\mathcal{P}}\Xi_{u,v}.

The idea is now to establish a
maximal inequality
for
\int_{\mathcal{P}}\Xi
by successively removing distinguished points from the partition
\mathcal{P}
. We claim that if
r\geq 3
, there exists a point
u\in\mathcal{P}
such that for its neighbouring points
u_{-}<u<u_{+}\in\mathcal{P}
,

\displaystyle|u_{+}-u_{-}|\leq\frac{2}{r-1}|t-s|.

Indeed, otherwise we would have

\displaystyle 2|t-s|\geq\sum_{u\in\mathcal{P}\setminus\{u_{0},u_{r}\}}|u_{+}-u_{-}|>2|t-s|

which is a contradiction. Note that for
r=2
, clearly
|u_{+}-u_{-}|\leq|t-s|
.
With this choice for
\#\mathcal{P}=r+1\geq 4
, we obtain

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\mathcal{P}}\Xi-\int_{\mathcal{P}\setminus\{u\}}\Xi\right|=|\delta\Xi_{u_{-},u,u_{+}}|\leq\|\delta\Xi\|_{\beta}|u_{+}-u_{-}|^{\beta}\leq\|\delta\Xi\|_{\beta}\frac{2^{\beta}|t-s|^{\beta}}{(r-1)^{\beta}}.

By successively removing points, we arrive at the uniform bound

(3.2)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\sup_{\mathcal{P}}\left|\int_{\mathcal{P}}\Xi-\Xi_{s,t}\right|&\leq 2^{\beta}|t-s|^{\beta}\|\delta\Xi\|_{\beta}\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}\frac{1}{k^{\beta}}+|t-s|^{\beta}\|\delta\Xi\|_{\beta}\\
&=\|\delta\Xi\|_{\beta}\big[2^{\beta}(\zeta(\beta)-1)+1\big]|t-s|^{\beta}\end{split}

where the right hand side is finite since
\beta>1
. We aim to define
\mathcal{I}\Xi
as the limit
\lim_{|\mathcal{P}|\to 0}\int_{\mathcal{P}}\Xi
for which we have to prove the existence now. It suffices to show that

\displaystyle\sup_{\max\{|\mathcal{P}|,|\mathcal{P}^{\prime}|\}\leq\varepsilon}\left|\int_{\mathcal{P}}\Xi-\int_{\mathcal{P}^{\prime}}\Xi\right|\to 0\quad\text{as }\varepsilon\to 0.

By adding and subtracting
\int_{\mathcal{P}\cup\mathcal{P}^{\prime}}\Xi
, we can assume without loss of generality that
\mathcal{P}\subset\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
. In this case,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{P}}\Xi-\int_{\mathcal{P}^{\prime}}\Xi=\sum_{[u,v]\in\mathcal{P}}\left(\Xi_{u,v}-\int_{\mathcal{P}^{\prime}\cap[u,v]}\Xi\right).

For
\max\{|\mathcal{P}|,|\mathcal{P}^{\prime}|\}=|\mathcal{P}|\leq\varepsilon
, we can use the maximal inequality (
3.2
) to see that

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\mathcal{P}}\Xi-\int_{\mathcal{P}^{\prime}}\Xi\right|

\displaystyle\leq\|\delta\Xi\|_{\beta}\big[2^{\beta}(\zeta(\beta)-1)+1\big]\sum_{[u,v]\in\mathcal{P}}|v-u|^{\beta}=\mathcal{O}(|\mathcal{P}|^{\beta-1})=\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{\beta-1}).

This finishes the proof.
∎

3.2.
Young’s integral and differential equations driven by Hölder paths

We are now ready to state Young’s result that generalizes Riemann–Stieltjes integration.

Theorem 3.3

(Young integral)
.

Let
V
and
W
be Banach spaces,
g\in\mathcal{C}^{\alpha}([0,T],V)
and
f\in\mathcal{C}^{\beta}([0,T],L(V,W))
. Assume that
\alpha+\beta>1
. Then the integral

\displaystyle\int_{s}^{t}f_{u}\,\mathrm{d}g_{u}\in W

exists as a limit of Riemann sums for every
s<t\in[0,T]
and we call it the
Young integral
. Moreover, we have the estimate

(3.3)

\displaystyle\left|\int_{s}^{t}f_{u}\,\mathrm{d}g_{u}-f_{s}(g_{t}-g_{s})\right|\leq C\|f\|_{\beta}\|g\|_{\alpha}|t-s|^{\alpha+\beta}

where
C>0
depends
\alpha+\beta
.

Proof.

Set

\displaystyle\Xi_{s,t}\coloneqq f_{s}(g_{t}-g_{s}).

Then we have

\displaystyle\delta\Xi_{s,u,t}

\displaystyle=f_{s}(g_{t}-g_{s})-f_{s}(g_{u}-g_{s})-f_{u}(g_{t}-g_{u})

\displaystyle=-(f_{u}-f_{s})(g_{t}-g_{u}),

thus
\|\Xi\|_{\alpha}\leq\|f\|_{\infty}\|g\|_{\alpha}<\infty
and

\displaystyle\|\delta\Xi\|_{\alpha+\beta}\leq\|f\|_{\beta}\|g\|_{\alpha}<\infty.

We can therefore apply the Sewing lemma and set

\displaystyle\int_{s}^{t}f_{u}\,\mathrm{d}g_{u}=\delta\mathcal{I}\Xi_{s,t}.

∎

Interpreting the integral in this way, we can give meaning to differential equations driven by sufficiently regular Hölder paths. Before we formulate the statement, we define a class of functions that will be important for us.

Definition 3.4
.

For
k\geq 0
,
\mathcal{C}^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{m},L(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{m}))
denotes the space of bounded
k
-times continuously differentiable functions
\sigma\colon\mathbb{R}^{m}\to L(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{m})
with bounded derivatives, i.e.
\sigma=(\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{d})
and every
\sigma_{i}\colon\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
is bounded and
k
-times continuously differentiable with all derivatives being bounded. For
\sigma\in\mathcal{C}^{k}=\mathcal{C}^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{m},L(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{m}))
, we set

\displaystyle\|\sigma\|_{\mathcal{C}^{k}}\coloneqq\max_{l=0,\ldots,k}\|D^{l}\sigma\|_{\infty}.

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
X\in\mathcal{C}^{\alpha}([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{d})
for some
\alpha>\frac{1}{2}
and let
\sigma\in\mathcal{C}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{m},L(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{m}))
. Then the integral equation

(3.4)

\displaystyle Y_{t}

\displaystyle=y+\int_{0}^{t}\sigma(Y_{t})\,\mathrm{d}X_{t};\quad t\in[0,T]

possesses a unique solution
Y\in\mathcal{C}^{\alpha}([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{m})
for every initial condition
y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
. The integral is understood as a Young integral.

Remark 3.6
.

The integral equation (
3.4
) is often called
Young differential equation
although Young never used his integral to solve differential equations. To the authors knowledge, T. Lyons was the first who studied differential equations involving the Young integral in
[
Lyo94
]
.

Before we give the proof of Theorem
3.5
, we state a technical result that will be needed.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
\sigma\in\mathcal{C}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{m},L(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{m}))
and
T\leq 1
. Then there exists a constant
C_{\alpha,K}
such that for every
X,Y\in\mathcal{C}^{\alpha}([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
\|X\|_{\alpha}\vee\|Y\|_{\alpha}\leq K
,

\displaystyle\|\sigma(X)-\sigma(Y)\|_{\alpha}\leq C_{\alpha,K}\|\sigma\|_{\mathcal{C}^{2}}\left(|X_{0}-Y_{0}|+\|X-Y\|_{\alpha}\right).

Proof.

It follows by using Taylor’s theorem repeatedly, cf.
[
FH20
, Lemma 7.5]
for details.
∎

Proof of Theorem
3.5
.

The proof is classical and uses a fixed point argument. For
0<T_{0}\leq T
and
Y\in\mathcal{C}^{\alpha}([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{m})
with
Y_{0}=y
, we set

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{T_{0}}(Y)\coloneqq\left(t\mapsto y+\int_{0}^{t}\sigma(Y_{s})\,\mathrm{d}X_{s}\,;\,t\in[0,T_{0}]\right).

Since
\sigma
is Lipschitz, the path
t\mapsto\sigma(Y_{t})
is
\alpha
-Hölder continuous and the integral is defined as a Young integral.
Thus,
\mathcal{M}_{T_{0}}
is in fact a map from
\mathcal{C}_{y}^{\alpha}([0,T_{0}],\mathbb{R}^{m})
to itself where
\mathcal{C}_{y}^{\alpha}([0,T_{0}],\mathbb{R}^{m})
is the complete metric space of
\alpha
-Hölder paths starting in
y
. We aim to show that it is a contraction. We will not do this on the whole space, but restrict ourselves to the closed unit ball

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{T_{0}}\coloneqq\{Y\in\mathcal{C}_{y}^{\alpha}([0,T_{0}],\mathbb{R}^{m})\,:\,\|Y\|_{\alpha}\leq 1\}.

that is still a closed metric space with the induced metric. We first show that
\mathcal{M}_{T_{0}}
leaves
\mathcal{B}_{T_{0}}
invariant for
T_{0}>0
sufficiently small, i.e.
\mathcal{M}_{T_{0}}\colon\mathcal{B}_{T_{0}}\to\mathcal{B}_{T_{0}}
.
From Theorem
3.3
,

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{M}_{T_{0}}\|_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\|\int_{0}^{\cdot}\sigma(Y_{s})\,dX_{s}\|_{\alpha}

\displaystyle\leq C\|X\|_{\alpha}(T^{\alpha}\|\sigma(Y)\|_{\alpha}+\|\sigma(Y)\|_{\infty})

\displaystyle\leq C\|X\|_{\alpha}(T^{\alpha}\|\sigma\|_{\mathcal{C}^{1}}\|Y\|_{\alpha}+\|\sigma\|_{\mathcal{C}^{0}})

\displaystyle\leq C\|X\|_{\alpha}

where
\|X\|_{\alpha}
denotes the
\alpha
-Hölder norm on
[0,T_{0}]
. We aim to choose
T_{0}
sufficiently small such that
C\|X\|_{\alpha}\leq 1
. However, it is in general
not
true that
\|X\|_{\alpha}
gets small as
T_{0}
tends to
0
(to see this, take the
\frac{1}{2}
-Hölder norm for the square root function, for instance). To solve this issue, we choose
\alpha^{\prime}
such that
\frac{1}{2}<\alpha^{\prime}<\alpha
and repeat the calculation for
\alpha^{\prime}
. If
X
is
\alpha
-Hölder, it follows that
\|X\|_{\alpha^{\prime}}\to 0
as
T_{0}\to 0
, thus we can choose
T_{0}
small enough to conclude that
\|\mathcal{M}_{T_{0}}\|_{\alpha^{\prime}}\leq 1
and therefore
\mathcal{M}_{T_{0}}\colon\mathcal{B}_{T_{0}}\to\mathcal{B}_{T_{0}}
. We proceed showing that
\mathcal{M}_{T_{0}}
is a contraction. For
Y,\tilde{Y}\in\mathcal{B}_{T_{0}}
, Theorem
3.3
implies that

\displaystyle\left|\delta\mathcal{M}_{T_{0}}(Y)_{s,t}-\delta\mathcal{M}_{T_{0}}(\tilde{Y})_{s,t}\right|

\displaystyle=\

\displaystyle\left|\int_{s}^{t}\sigma(Y_{u})-\sigma(\tilde{Y}_{u})\,\mathrm{d}X_{u}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\

\displaystyle C\left(\|\sigma(Y)-\sigma(\tilde{Y})\|_{\infty}+\|\sigma(Y)-\sigma(\tilde{Y})\|_{\alpha^{\prime}}\right)\|X\|_{\alpha^{\prime};[0,T_{0}]}|t-s|^{\alpha^{\prime}}.

Note that, since
Y_{0}=\tilde{Y}_{0}
,

\displaystyle\|\sigma(Y)-\sigma(\tilde{Y})\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq|\sigma(Y_{0})-\sigma(\tilde{Y}_{0})|+T_{0}^{\alpha^{\prime}}\|\sigma(Y)-\sigma(\tilde{Y})\|_{\alpha^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=T_{0}^{\alpha^{\prime}}\|\sigma(Y)-\sigma(\tilde{Y})\|_{\alpha^{\prime}}.

From Lemma
3.7
,

\displaystyle\|\sigma(Y)-\sigma(\tilde{Y})\|_{\alpha^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\leq C_{\alpha^{\prime},K}\|\sigma\|_{\mathcal{C}^{2}}(|Y_{0}-\tilde{Y}_{0}|+\|Y-\tilde{Y}\|_{\alpha^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=C_{\alpha^{\prime},K}\|\sigma\|_{\mathcal{C}^{2}}\|Y-\tilde{Y}\|_{\alpha^{\prime}}

where
K>0
satisfies
\|Y\|_{\alpha}\vee\|\tilde{Y}\|_{\alpha}\leq K
. Since
Y,\tilde{Y}\in\mathcal{B}_{T_{0}}
,
C
can be chosen independently of
Y
and
\tilde{Y}
. Therefore, we arrive at an estimate of the form

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{M}_{T_{0}}(Y)-\mathcal{M}_{T_{0}}(\tilde{Y})\|_{\alpha^{\prime}}\leq C\|X\|_{\alpha^{\prime}}\|Y-\tilde{Y}\|_{\alpha^{\prime}}

and choosing
T_{0}>0
smaller if necessary, we obtain
C\|X\|_{\alpha^{\prime}}<1
, i.e.
\mathcal{M}_{T_{0}}
is a contraction on the space
\mathcal{B}_{T_{0}}
. It follows that the equation possesses a unique solution on the interval
[0,T_{0}]
. We can now repeat the argument on the interval
[T_{0},2T_{0}]
with initial condition
Y_{T_{0}}
and glue together the solutions. Iterating this sufficiently often, we eventually obtain a unique solution
Y
on the interval
[0,T]
. A posteriori, the estimates for the Young integral in Theorem
3.3
show that
Y
is not only
\alpha^{\prime}
Hölder, but even
\alpha
-Hölder continuous. This finishes the proof.
∎

Combining Corollary
2.13
and Theorem
3.5
, we can show:

Theorem 3.8
.

Let
B^{H}
be a fractional Brownian motion with
H>\frac{1}{2}
. Assume that
\sigma\in\mathcal{C}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{m},L(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{m}))
. Then for every
y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, the stochastic differential equation

(3.5)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathrm{d}Y_{t}&=\sigma(Y_{t})\,\mathrm{d}B^{H}_{t}(\omega);\quad t\in[0,T],\\
Y_{0}&=y,\end{split}

can be interpreted as an integral equation using the Young integral and possesses a unique solution
Y
for almost every trajectory.

Remark 3.9
.

The solution theory just presented is
pathwise
, meaning that one can solve the stochastic differential equation (
3.5
) path-by-path. In particular, if the fractional Brownian motion is
\alpha
-Hölder continuous outside a set
\mathcal{N}\subset\Omega
of measure zero, the equation (
3.5
) can be solved outside exactly that set. This is in contrast to Itō’s theory of stochastic differential equations which is
not
pathwise. The solution of an Itō stochastic differential equation is defined outside a set of measure zero
that depends on the whole equation
, e.g. on
y
and on
\sigma
, too. Considering another initial condition
\tilde{y}
will create a new set of measure zero outside of which the solution is defined. Since the set of allowed initial conditions is not countable, it is a priori not clear whether there exists a set of full measure on which an Itō stochastic differential equation can be solved for every initial condition
y
. In fact, assuming that
\sigma
is globally Lipschitz continuous, such a universal set always exists, but there are examples of solutions to Itō stochastic differential equations that fail to have this property, cf.
[
LS11
]
. For a pathwise solution theory, this cannot happen.

3.3.
Limitations of the Young integral

While the Young integral can be used successfully for the fractional Brownian motion in the case
H>\frac{1}{2}
, it cannot be applied even to the Brownian motion. The main obstacle is the lack of sufficient regularity, which is essential for defining the integral. We indeed have the following statement for the fractional Brownian motion:

Proposition 3.10
.

The fractional Brownian motion
B^{H}
does not have
\alpha
-Hölder continuous trajectories on
[0,T]
almost surely for
\alpha>H
.

Proof.

Can be deduced from Lemma
2.7
. The details are left to the reader.
∎

It is natural to look for an extension of the Young integral that works for paths with low Hölder-regularity, too. More generally, the minimal condition on the notion of an integral would be that we can apply it to the Brownian motion. We make the following (very general) definition:

Definition 3.11
.

Let
E
be a Banach space of paths in
\mathbb{R}
and
(C_{n})_{n\geq 1}
and
(S_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be two series of independent standard Gaussian random variables. Let
c_{n}(t)\coloneqq\cos(2\pi nt)
and
s_{n}(t)\coloneqq\sin(2\pi nt)
. We say that
E

carries the Wiener measure
if and only if
s_{n}
and
c_{n}
belong to
E
and if the series

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{C_{n}c_{n}+S_{n}s_{n}}{2\pi n}

converges in
E
almost surely.

Example 3.12
.

Let us motivate Definition
3.11
by showing that
L^{2}[0,1]
carries the Wiener measure. Assume that
B=(B_{t})_{0\leq t\leq 1}
is a Brownian motion, i.e.
\mathbb{E}(B_{t})=0
and
\mathbb{E}(B_{s}B_{t})=\min\{s,t\}
for every
s,t\in[0,1]
. Recall that
\{1,\sqrt{2}c_{n},\sqrt{2}s_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
is an orthonormal basis for
L^{2}[0,1]
. By
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
, we denote the inner product of two functions in
L^{2}[0,1]
. If we expand
B
with respect to this basis, we obtain

\displaystyle B=\int_{0}^{1}B_{s}\,\mathrm{d}s+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left[\langle B,\sqrt{2}c_{n}\rangle\sqrt{2}c_{n}+\langle B,\sqrt{2}s_{n}\rangle\sqrt{2}s_{n}\right]

almost surely in
L^{2}([0,1])
. Since
B
is a zero mean Gaussian process, it is easy to check that
\big\{\langle B,1\rangle,\langle B,\sqrt{2}c_{n}\rangle,\langle B,\sqrt{2}s_{n}\rangle\big\}_{n\geq 1}
is a family of normal random variables with zero mean. Therefore, the law of each random variable is determined by its second moment. We calculate

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big(\langle B,\sqrt{2}c_{n}\rangle^{2}\big)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left(\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}{2B_{t}B_{s}}\cos(2\pi nt)\cos(2\pi ns)\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}s\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}2\min\{s,t\}\cos(2\pi nt)\cos(2\pi ns)\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{s}2t\cos(2\pi nt)\cos(2\pi ns)\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{1}\int_{s}^{1}2s\cos(2\pi nt)\cos(2\pi ns)\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi n)^{2}}.

Similarly,
\mathbb{E}(\langle B,\sqrt{2}s_{n}\rangle^{2})=\frac{1}{(2\pi n)^{2}}.
Proceeding with similar calculations, we see that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big(\langle B,1\rangle\langle B,\sqrt{2}c_{n}\rangle\big)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\big(\langle B,1\rangle\langle B,\sqrt{2}s_{n}\rangle\big)=0\quad\text{for every }n\geq 1,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big(\langle B,\sqrt{2}c_{n}\rangle\langle B,\sqrt{2}s_{m}\rangle\big)

\displaystyle=0\quad\text{for every }n,m\geq 1\text{ and}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big(\langle B,\sqrt{2}c_{n}\rangle\langle B,\sqrt{2}c_{m}\rangle\big)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\big(\langle B,\sqrt{2}s_{n}\rangle\langle B,\sqrt{2}s_{m}\rangle\big)=0\quad\text{for every }n\neq m.

These calculations reveal that the elements
\big\{\langle B,1\rangle,\langle B,\sqrt{2}c_{n}\rangle,\langle B,\sqrt{2}s_{n}\rangle\big\}_{n\geq 1}
are uncorrelated and therefore, since they are normal, independent. Setting

\displaystyle C_{n}\coloneqq 2\pi n\langle B,\sqrt{2}c_{n}\rangle\quad\text{and}\quad S_{n}\coloneqq 2\pi n\langle B,\sqrt{2}s_{n}\rangle,

we can thus represent
B
by

\displaystyle B=\int_{0}^{1}B_{s}\,\mathrm{d}s+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{C_{n}c_{n}+S_{n}s_{n}}{\sqrt{2}\pi n}

almost surely in
L^{2}[0,1]
. In fact, with more work, one can even show that the convergence of the series holds uniformly almost surely. This shows that
L^{2}[0,1]
and
\mathcal{C}[0,1]
carry the Wiener measure. One can also show that
\mathcal{C}^{\alpha}[0,1]
carries the Wiener measure for
\alpha<\frac{1}{2}
but not for
\alpha\geq\frac{1}{2}
.

The following result is taken from
[
LCL07
]
.

Theorem 3.13

(Lyons)
.

Let
E
be a Banach space that carries the Wiener measure. Then there is no continuous bilinear map
I\colon E\times E\to\mathbb{R}
such that if
x
and
y
are trigonometric functions,
I(x,y)=\int_{0}^{1}x_{t}\,\mathrm{d}y_{t}
.

Proof.

For
N\geq 1
, we define

\displaystyle W^{N}\coloneqq\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{C_{n}c_{n}+S_{n}s_{n}}{2\pi n}\quad\text{and}\quad\tilde{W}^{N}\coloneqq\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{C_{n}s_{n}-S_{n}c_{n}}{2\pi n}

where in both definitions, we take the same random variables
S_{n}
and
C_{n}
. Note that
W^{N}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{D}}}{{=}}\tilde{W}^{N}
and that both
W^{N}
and
\tilde{W}^{N}
converge almost surely to processes
W
resp.
\tilde{W}
in
E
by assumption. We assume that a bilinear map
I\colon E\times E\to\mathbb{R}
satisfying the stated conditions exists. Then we have
I(W^{N},\tilde{W}^{N})\to I(W,\tilde{W})
almost surely as
N\to\infty
. On the other hand,

\displaystyle I(W^{N},\tilde{W}^{N})=\int_{0}^{1}W^{N}_{t}\,\mathrm{d}\tilde{W}^{N}_{t}=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{C_{n}^{2}+S_{n}^{2}}{2\pi n}

diverges almost surely as
N\to\infty
which is a contradiction.
∎

Remark 3.14
.

In view of Example
3.12
, we see that the processes
W
and
\tilde{W}
are both (essentially) the sum of a Brownian motion and a smooth random function. Note that the nonexistence of the integral
\int_{0}^{1}W_{t}\,\mathrm{d}\tilde{W}_{t}
does not contradict Itō’s theory of stochastic integration: the processes
W
and
\tilde{W}
are highly correlated and
W
is not adapted to the filtration generated by
\tilde{W}
.

Theorem
3.13
shows that we cannot expect to find a linear theory of deterministic integration that is rich enough to handle Brownian sample paths. The fact that a pathwise approach to stochastic differential equations driven by a Brownian motion seems impossible underlines the importance of Itō’s theory of stochastic integration and is one of many reasons for its tremendous success.

4.
Rough paths and linear equations

Our goal is to solve differential equations driven by paths with regularity less than Brownian sample paths. We already saw that a direct approach using the Young integral will not work. To simplify the problem, we will consider linear equations first. For a
d
-dimensional path
X=(X^{1},\ldots,X^{d})
, we look at the equation

(4.1)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathrm{d}Y_{t}&=Y_{t}\,\mathrm{d}X_{t}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}Y_{t}\,\mathrm{d}X^{i}_{t};\qquad t\geq 0,\\
Y_{0}&=y\in\mathbb{R}.\end{split}

Formally
, a solution to (
4.1
) is given by

\displaystyle Y_{t}=y

\displaystyle+y\sum_{i=1}^{d}\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}X^{i}_{s}+y\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{s}\mathrm{d}X^{i}_{u}\mathrm{d}X^{j}_{s}

\displaystyle+\ldots+y\sum_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{n}=1}^{d}\int_{0<t_{1}<\cdots<t_{n}<t}\mathrm{d}X_{t_{1}}^{i_{1}}\cdots\mathrm{d}X_{t_{n}}^{i_{n}}+\ldots

The problem is, of course, that there is no good notion of an integral we can use to define the iterated integrals for irregular paths
X
. On the other hand, there are situations where iterated integrals are given in a non-pathwise manner. For instance, in stochastic analysis, the Itō and the Stratonovich integral are defined for a Brownian motion. The idea of rough paths theory is to just
assume
that the iterated integrals exist and satisfy some key properties. In this chapter, we will discuss these properties and see how linear equations can be solved. Eventually, we will consider the case of a Brownian motion.

4.1.
Iterated integrals and rough paths

What are the properties that characterize an iterated integral? To answer this question, let us start with the second iterated integral. For smooth
X
, we use the notation

\displaystyle\mathbb{X}^{ij}_{s,t}\coloneqq\int_{s}^{t}\int_{s}^{u}\mathrm{d}X^{i}_{v}\,\mathrm{d}X^{j}_{u}.

One basic algebraic property is additivity of the integral, i.e.
\int_{s}^{u}+\int_{u}^{t}=\int_{s}^{t}
for
s<u<t
. For the iterated integral, this leads to

\displaystyle\mathbb{X}^{ij}_{s,t}

\displaystyle=\int_{s}^{t}(X^{i}_{v}-X^{i}_{s})\,\mathrm{d}X^{j}_{v}=\int_{s}^{u}(X^{i}_{v}-X^{i}_{s})\,\mathrm{d}X^{j}_{v}+\int_{u}^{t}(X^{i}_{v}-X^{i}_{s})\,\mathrm{d}X^{j}_{v}

\displaystyle=\int_{s}^{u}(X^{i}_{v}-X^{i}_{s})\,\mathrm{d}X^{j}_{v}+\int_{u}^{t}(X^{i}_{v}-X^{i}_{u})\,\mathrm{d}X^{j}_{v}+\int_{u}^{t}(X^{i}_{u}-X^{i}_{s})\,\mathrm{d}X^{j}_{v}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{X}^{ij}_{s,u}+\mathbb{X}^{ij}_{u,t}+\int_{s}^{u}\,\mathrm{d}X^{i}_{v}\int_{u}^{t}\,\mathrm{d}X^{j}_{v}.

Setting
\mathbb{X}_{s,t}\coloneqq(\mathbb{X}^{ij}_{s,t})_{i,j=1,\ldots,d}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\cong\mathbb{R}^{d}\otimes\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the above equality reads

\displaystyle\mathbb{X}_{s,t}=\mathbb{X}_{s,u}+\mathbb{X}_{u,t}+\delta X_{s,u}\otimes\delta X_{u,t}.

To describe the corresponding property for the higher-order iterated integrals, we will introduce some more notations. Note that the
n
-th order iterated integral of a
d
-dimensional smooth path can be understood as an element in
(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{\otimes n}
. Thus the collection of all iterated integrals will be an element in the direct product of all tensor products.

Definition 4.1
.

The direct product

\displaystyle T((\mathbb{R}^{d}))\coloneqq\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(\mathbb{R}^{d}\otimes\mathbb{R}^{d})\times\cdots\times(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{\otimes n}\times\cdots=\prod_{n=0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{\otimes n}

where
(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{\otimes 0}=\mathbb{R}
,
(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{\otimes 1}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
, is called
extended tensor algebra
. The maps

\displaystyle\pi_{n}\colon T((\mathbb{R}^{d}))\to(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{\otimes n}

are the usual projection maps.

Definition 4.2
.

For elements
a,b\in T((\mathbb{R}^{d}))
, we define an element
a\otimes b\in T((\mathbb{R}^{d}))
by setting

\displaystyle\pi_{n}(a\otimes b)\coloneqq\sum_{i+j=n}\pi_{i}(a)\otimes\pi_{j}(b)

for every
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. We also define

\displaystyle 1\coloneqq(1,0,0,\ldots)\in T((\mathbb{R}^{d})).

Note that the extended tensor algebra carries a natural vector space structure. It becomes a
associative unital algebra
with product
\otimes
and
1
as the unit.

Definition 4.3
.

Let
X\colon[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a smooth path. We define the
(canonical) lift

\mathbf{X}
of
X
as a map
\mathbf{X}\colon\Delta\to T((\mathbb{R}^{d}))
,
\Delta\coloneqq\{(s,t)\in[0,T]^{2}\,:\,s\leq t\}
, by setting

\displaystyle\pi_{n}(\mathbf{X}_{s,t})\coloneqq\mathbb{X}_{s,t}^{(n)}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\int_{s<u_{1}<\cdots<u_{n}<t}\mathrm{d}X_{u_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathrm{d}X_{u_{n}}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\left(\int_{s<u_{1}<\cdots<u_{n}<t}\mathrm{d}X^{i_{1}}_{u_{1}}\cdots\mathrm{d}X^{i_{n}}_{u_{n}}\right)_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{n}\in\{1,\ldots,d\}}\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{\otimes n}

for
n\geq 1
and
\pi_{0}(\mathbb{X}_{s,t})\coloneqq 1
.

We can now prove an algebraic property called
Chen’s identity
that is satisfied by iterated integrals.

Theorem 4.4

(Chen)
.

Let
X\colon[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
be smooth and
\mathbf{X}
its canonical lift. Then

\displaystyle\mathbf{X}_{s,t}=\mathbf{X}_{s,u}\otimes\mathbf{X}_{u,t}

for every
s<u<t
.

Proof.

We have to show that for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{X}_{s,t}^{(n)}=\sum_{i+j=n}\mathbb{X}_{s,u}^{(i)}\otimes\mathbb{X}_{u,t}^{(j)}.

We do this by induction. For
n=1
, the statement is obvious. For arbitrary
n\geq 2
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{X}_{s,t}^{(n)}

\displaystyle=\int_{s}^{t}\mathbb{X}_{s,v}^{(n-1)}\,\otimes\mathrm{d}X_{v}

\displaystyle=\int_{s}^{u}\mathbb{X}_{s,v}^{(n-1)}\,\otimes\mathrm{d}X_{v}+\int_{u}^{t}\mathbb{X}_{s,v}^{(n-1)}\,\otimes\mathrm{d}X_{v}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{X}_{s,u}^{(n)}+\sum_{i+j=n-1}\int_{u}^{t}\mathbb{X}_{s,u}^{(i)}\otimes\mathbb{X}_{u,v}^{(j)}\,\otimes\mathrm{d}X_{v}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{X}_{s,u}^{(n)}+\sum_{i+j=n-1}\mathbb{X}_{s,u}^{(i)}\otimes\int_{u}^{t}\mathbb{X}_{u,v}^{(j)}\,\otimes\mathrm{d}X_{v}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{X}_{s,u}^{(n)}+\sum_{i+j=n-1}\mathbb{X}_{s,u}^{(i)}\otimes\mathbb{X}_{u,t}^{(j+1)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i+j=n}\mathbb{X}_{s,u}^{(i)}\otimes\mathbb{X}_{u,t}^{(j)}

and the claim is shown.

∎

From Chen’s theorem, we can deduce that

\displaystyle\mathbb{X}^{(2)}_{s,t}=\pi_{2}(\mathbf{X}_{s,u}\otimes\mathbf{X}_{u,t})=\mathbb{X}^{(2)}_{s,u}+\mathbb{X}^{(2)}_{u,t}+\delta X_{s,u}\otimes\delta X_{u,t}

which we calculated “by hand” above.

Definition 4.5
.

For
N\geq 0
, the direct sum

\displaystyle T^{N}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\coloneqq\mathbb{R}\oplus\mathbb{R}^{d}\oplus(\mathbb{R}^{d}\otimes\mathbb{R}^{d})\oplus\cdots\oplus(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{\otimes N}=\bigoplus_{n=0}^{N}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{\otimes n}

if called
truncated tensor algebra
of level
N
. The truncated tensor product
\otimes^{N}:T^{N}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\times T^{N}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\rightarrow T^{N}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

is then defined similarly as in Definition
4.2
by truncating each term of the product to level
N
. Abusing notation, we will still use the symbol
\otimes
instead of
\otimes^{N}
on the truncated tensor algebra.

The truncated tensor algebra is also an associative unital algebra with sum and product induced by the extended tensor algebra.

Definition 4.6
.

A map
\mathbf{X}\colon\Delta\to T^{N}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
satisfying the
Chen relation

\displaystyle\mathbf{X}_{s,t}=\mathbf{X}_{s,u}\otimes\mathbf{X}_{u,t}

for every
s<u<t
is called a
multiplicative functional
.

Multiplicative functionals satisfy an
algebraic
property that we expect from iterated integrals. There is also an
analytic
property an iterated integral should satisfy. Recall that for the Young integral, we showed that for an
\alpha
-Hölder path
X
with
\alpha>\frac{1}{2}
,

\displaystyle|\delta X_{s,t}|

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{s}^{t}\,\mathrm{d}X_{u}\right|=\mathcal{O}(|t-s|^{\alpha}),

\displaystyle\left|\int_{s}^{t}(X_{u}-X_{s})\,\otimes\mathrm{d}X_{u}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{s<u_{1}<u_{2}<t}\mathrm{d}X_{u_{1}}\,\otimes\mathrm{d}X_{u_{2}}\right|=\mathcal{O}(|t-s|^{2\alpha}).

What is the regularity of higher order iterated integrals? We consider the third order first. We set
\mathbb{X}^{(2)}_{s,t}=\int_{s}^{t}(X_{u}-X_{s})\,\otimes\mathrm{d}X_{u}
and define

\displaystyle\Xi_{u,v}\coloneqq\mathbb{X}^{(2)}_{0,u}\otimes\delta X_{u,v}+\delta X_{0,u}\otimes\mathbb{X}^{(2)}_{u,v}\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{\otimes 3}.

By assumption,
\|\Xi\|_{\alpha}<\infty
. Using the Chen identity, for
u<v<w
,

\displaystyle\delta\Xi_{u,v,w}

\displaystyle=(\mathbb{X}^{(2)}_{0,u}-\mathbb{X}^{(2)}_{0,v})\otimes\delta X_{v,w}+\delta X_{0,u}\otimes(\mathbb{X}^{(2)}_{u,w}-\mathbb{X}^{(2)}_{u,v})-\delta X_{0,v}\otimes\mathbb{X}^{(2)}_{v,w}

\displaystyle=-(\mathbb{X}^{(2)}_{u,v}+\delta X_{0,u}\otimes\delta X_{u,v})\otimes\delta X_{v,w}+\delta X_{0,u}\otimes(\mathbb{X}^{(2)}_{v,w}+\delta X_{u,v}\otimes\delta X_{v,w})

\displaystyle\quad-\delta X_{0,v}\otimes\mathbb{X}^{(2)}_{v,w}

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{X}^{(2)}_{u,v}\otimes\delta X_{v,w}-\delta X_{u,v}\otimes\mathbb{X}^{(2)}_{v,w}.

Therefore,
\|\delta\Xi\|_{3\alpha}<\infty
. From the Sewing lemma,

\displaystyle\delta\mathcal{I}\Xi_{s,t}=\lim_{|\mathcal{P}|\to 0}\sum_{[u,v]\in\mathcal{P}}\Xi_{u,v}

\displaystyle=\lim_{|\mathcal{P}|\to 0}\sum_{[u,v]\in\mathcal{P}}\mathbb{X}^{(2)}_{0,u}\otimes\delta X_{u,v}+\delta X_{0,u}\otimes\mathbb{X}^{(2)}_{u,v}

\displaystyle=\lim_{|\mathcal{P}|\to 0}\sum_{[u,v]\in\mathcal{P}}\mathbb{X}^{(2)}_{0,u}\otimes\delta X_{u,v}

\displaystyle=\int_{s}^{t}\mathbb{X}^{(2)}_{0,u}\,\otimes\mathrm{d}X_{u}

exists. In the second equality, we used that
|\mathbb{X}^{(2)}_{u,v}|=\mathcal{O}(|v-u|^{2\alpha})
and
2\alpha>1
. The Sewing lemma also tells us that

\displaystyle\left|\int_{s<u_{1}<u_{2}<u_{3}<t}\mathrm{d}X_{u_{1}}\,\otimes\mathrm{d}X_{u_{2}}\otimes\mathrm{d}X_{u_{3}}\right|=\mathcal{O}(|t-s|^{3\alpha}).

Our goal is now to deduce the right regularity of iterated integrals of any order. As we will see in the sequel, we can repeat the previous argument by applying the Sewing lemma. Another property of iterated integrals we know from smooth functions is that their value decays very quickly when considering higher orders. To prove this property in our context, we need the
neo-classical inequality
that we cite now.

Theorem 4.7

(Neo-classical inequality)
.

For
\alpha\in(0,1]
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
s,t>0
,

\displaystyle\alpha\sum_{0\leq j\leq n}\frac{s^{j\alpha}t^{(n-j)\alpha}}{(j\alpha)!((n-j)\alpha)!}\leq\frac{(t+s)^{n\alpha}}{(n\alpha)!},

where
(j\alpha)!\coloneqq\Gamma(1+j\alpha)
and

\displaystyle\Gamma(z)=\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{z-1}\exp(-t)\,\mathrm{d}t,\ \ \ z\in\mathbb{C}\ \ \text{and}\ \ \mathcal{R}(z)>0.

Proof.

HH10
]
.
∎

We can now prove a first important result in rough paths theory, the Extension theorem.

Theorem 4.8

(Lyon’s extension theorem)
.

Let
\mathbf{X}\colon\Delta\to T^{N}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be a multiplicative functional with

\displaystyle{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\mathbf{X}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\alpha}\coloneqq\max_{n=1,\ldots,N}\sup_{0\leq s<t\leq T}\frac{|\mathbb{X}^{(n)}_{s,t}|}{|t-s|^{n\alpha}}<\infty

for
N+1>\frac{1}{\alpha}
. Then
\mathbf{X}
has a unique extension to a multiplicative functional
\tilde{\mathbf{X}}\colon\Delta\to T((\mathbb{R}^{d}))
with the same regularity. More precisely,
\tilde{\mathbf{X}}
satisfies Chen’s relation,
\tilde{\mathbb{X}}^{(n)}=\mathbb{X}^{(n)}
for every
n=0,\ldots,N
and there are constants
M
and
\beta
such that

\displaystyle\sup_{0\leq s<t\leq T}\frac{|\tilde{\mathbb{X}}^{(n)}_{s,t}|}{|t-s|^{n\alpha}}\leq\frac{M^{n}}{\beta(n\alpha)!}

holds for every
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.

Proof.

Existence
: We use an induction argument. Let
n\geq N
, and assume for every
1\leq j\leq n
,
\mathbb{X}^{(j)}
is well-defined and for some
M,\beta>0
satisfying

(4.2)

\displaystyle\sup_{0\leq s<t\leq T}\frac{|\mathbb{X}^{(j)}_{s,t}|}{|t-s|^{j\alpha}}\leq\frac{M^{j}}{\beta(j\alpha)!}\ .\ \ \ \

Also, assume for
2\leq k\leq n

(4.3)

\displaystyle\mathbb{X}^{(k)}_{s,t}=\mathbb{X}^{(k)}_{s,u}+\mathbb{X}^{(k)}_{u,t}+\sum_{1\leq i\leq k-1}\mathbb{X}^{(i)}_{s,u}\otimes\mathbb{X}^{(k-i)}_{u,t},\ \ \ \forall s,u,t\in[0,T]:\ s\leq u\leq t.

First, we show one can apply the Sewing lemma to define the following integrals

\displaystyle\Gamma^{n+1}(s,t):=\int_{s}^{t}\mathbb{X}^{n}_{0,\sigma}\otimes\mathrm{d}X_{\sigma},\ \ \ \ X_{\sigma}:=\mathbb{X}_{0,\sigma}^{{1}}\ .

Set

\displaystyle\Xi^{(n+1)}_{s,t}=\sum_{1\leq j\leq n}\mathbb{X}^{(n+1-j)}_{0,s}\otimes\mathbb{X}^{(j)}_{s,t},

then for
u<v<w
, from (
4.3
)

(4.4)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\delta\Xi^{(n+1)}_{u,v,w}=\Xi^{(n+1)}_{u,w}-\Xi^{(n+1)}_{u,v}-\Xi^{(n+1)}_{v,w}=\sum_{1\leq j\leq n}\mathbb{X}^{(n+1-j)}_{0,u}\otimes[\mathbb{X}^{(j)}_{u,w}-\mathbb{X}^{(j)}_{u,v}]-\sum_{1\leq j\leq n}\mathbb{X}^{(n+1-j)}_{0,v}\otimes\mathbb{X}^{(j)}_{v,w}\\
&\quad=\sum_{1\leq j\leq n}\mathbb{X}_{0,u}^{(n+1-j)}\otimes\mathbb{X}^{(j)}_{v,w}+\sum_{2\leq j\leq n}\sum_{1\leq i\leq j-1}\mathbb{X}^{(n+1-j)}_{0,u}\otimes[\mathbb{X}^{(j-i)}_{u,v}\otimes\mathbb{X}^{(i)}_{v,w}]-\sum_{1\leq j\leq n}\mathbb{X}^{(n+1-j)}_{0,u}\otimes\mathbb{X}^{(j)}_{v,w}\\
&\quad-\sum_{1\leq j\leq n}\mathbb{X}^{(n+1-j)}_{u,v}\otimes\mathbb{X}^{(j)}_{v,w}-\sum_{1\leq j\leq n-1}\sum_{1\leq i\leq n-j}[\mathbb{X}^{(n+1-j-i)}_{0,u}\otimes\mathbb{X}^{(i)}_{u,v}\otimes]\otimes\mathbb{X}^{(j)}_{v,w}\\
&\quad=-\sum_{1\leq j\leq n}\mathbb{X}^{(n+1-j)}_{u,v}\otimes\mathbb{X}^{(j)}_{v,w}\ .\end{split}

From our induction assumption (
4.2
) and the neo-classical inequality,

(4.5)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&|\delta\Xi^{(n+1)}_{u,v,w}|_{(n+1)\beta}\leq\sum_{1\leq j\leq n}|\mathbb{X}^{(n+1-j)}_{u,v}||\mathbb{X}^{(j)}_{v,w}|\leq\frac{M^{n+1}}{\beta^{2}}\sum_{1\leq j\leq n}\frac{(v-u)^{(n+1-j)\alpha}(w-v)^{j\alpha}}{(j\alpha)!((n+1-j)\alpha)!}\\
&\quad\leq\frac{M^{n+1}(w-v)^{(n+1)\alpha}}{\alpha\beta^{2}((n+1)\alpha)!}.\end{split}

We finally define

(4.6)

\displaystyle\mathbb{X}^{(n+1)}_{s,t}:=\delta\mathcal{I}\Xi_{s,t}^{(n+1)}-\Xi_{s,t}^{(n+1)},\ \ \ s,t\in[0,T].

Note that from (
4.5
) and (
3.1
),

\displaystyle|\mathbb{X}^{(n+1)}_{s,t}|\leq\big(1+2^{(n+1)\alpha}(\zeta((n+1)\alpha)-1)\big)(t-s)^{(n+1)\alpha}\|\delta\mathcal{I}\Xi^{(n+1)}\|

\displaystyle\quad\leq\frac{2^{(N+1)\alpha}(\zeta((N+1)\alpha)-1)+1}{\alpha\beta^{2}}\times\frac{M^{n+1}}{((n+1)\alpha)!}.

For
\beta
satisfying

\displaystyle\beta\geq\frac{2^{(N+1)\alpha}(\zeta((N+1)\alpha)-1)+1}{\alpha^{2}},

we choose
M>0
such that

\displaystyle\forall j,\ 1\leq j\leq N:\ \ \sup_{0\leq s<t\leq T}\frac{|\mathbb{X}^{(j)}_{s,t}|}{|t-s|^{j\alpha}}\leq\frac{M^{j}}{\beta(j\alpha)!}

and our claim is proved. It only remains to prove that

\displaystyle\mathbb{X}^{(n+1)}_{s,t}=\mathbb{X}^{(n+1)}_{s,u}+\mathbb{X}^{(n+1)}_{u,t}+\sum_{1\leq j\leq n}\mathbb{X}^{(j)}_{s,u}\otimes\mathbb{X}^{(n+1-j)}_{u,t},\ \ \ \forall s,u,t\in[0,T]:\ s\leq u\leq t,

which again follows by an induction argument. Indeed, by (
4.4
) and (
4.6
),

\displaystyle\mathbb{X}^{(n+1)}_{s,t}-\mathbb{X}^{(n+1)}_{s,u}-\mathbb{X}^{(n+1)}_{u,t}=-\delta\Xi^{(n+1)}_{s,u,t}=\sum_{1\leq j\leq n}\mathbb{X}^{(n+1-j)}_{s,u}\otimes\mathbb{X}^{(j)}_{u,t}.

Uniqueness
: Assume that
\tilde{\mathbf{X}}
and
\hat{\mathbf{X}}
are two extensions of
\mathbf{X}
that agree up to some level
n\geq N
. Set

\displaystyle\Psi_{s,t}\coloneqq\tilde{\mathbb{X}}^{(n+1)}_{s,t}-\hat{\mathbb{X}}^{(n+1)}_{s,t}.

From Chen’s identity, for
s<u<t
,

\displaystyle\Psi_{s,t}

\displaystyle=\pi_{n+1}(\tilde{\mathbf{X}}_{s,u}\otimes\tilde{\mathbf{X}}_{u,t})-\pi_{n+1}(\hat{\mathbf{X}}_{s,u}\otimes\hat{\mathbf{X}}_{u,t})

\displaystyle=\tilde{\mathbb{X}}^{(n+1)}_{s,u}+\tilde{\mathbb{X}}^{(n+1)}_{u,t}-\hat{\mathbb{X}}^{(n+1)}_{s,u}-\hat{\mathbb{X}}^{(n+1)}_{u,t}

\displaystyle=\Psi_{s,u}+\Psi_{u,t}.

It follows that
t\mapsto\Psi_{t}:=\Psi_{0,t}
is a path that has
(n+1)\alpha
-Hölder regularity. Since
(n+1)\alpha>1
,
\Psi_{t}
is constant, thus
\Psi_{s,t}=0
for every
s<t
which shows
\tilde{\mathbb{X}}^{(n+1)}=\hat{\mathbb{X}}^{(n+1)}
. Therefore, our claim about the uniqueness is proved.
∎

The Extension theorem gives us the exact regularity of iterated Young integrals of any order. Moreover, it tells us that multiplicative functionals having a certain regularity up to a sufficiently high level can be uniquely extended to
T^{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
for any other integer
M>\frac{1}{\alpha}
. A rough path will be a multiplicative functional that has such an extension.

Definition 4.9
.

Let
\alpha\in(0,1]
. An
\alpha
-Hölder rough path

\mathbf{X}
is a multiplicative functional
\mathbf{X}\colon\Delta\to T^{N}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that

\displaystyle{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\mathbf{X}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\alpha}\coloneqq\max_{n=1,\ldots,N}\sup_{0\leq s<t\leq T}\frac{|\mathbb{X}^{(n)}_{s,t}|}{|t-s|^{n\alpha}}<\infty

where

\displaystyle\lfloor 1/\alpha\rfloor\coloneqq\max\left\{n\in\mathbb{N}\,:\,n\leq\frac{1}{\alpha}\right\}=N,

i.e.
N\leq\frac{1}{\alpha}<N+1
. The set of
\alpha
-Hölder rough paths is denoted by
\mathscr{C}^{\alpha}([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{d})
or simply by
\mathscr{C}^{\alpha}
. If
X\colon[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is an
\alpha
-Hölder path and
\mathbf{X}
a rough path with
\mathbb{X}^{(1)}=\delta X
, we call
\mathbf{X}
a
rough path lift
of
X
. If
\mathbf{X}
is a rough path, the unique extension
\tilde{\mathbf{X}}
provided by Theorem
4.8
is called the
Lyons lift
of
\mathbf{X}
.

4.2.
Linear equations driven by a rough path

We now return to linear equations. In fact, we will see now that if
\mathbf{X}
is an
\alpha
-Hölder rough path, we can solve linear equations driven by this path. We will first describe the equation we are looking at. Let
A_{1},\ldots,A_{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
and define a linear map
A\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
by setting

\displaystyle Av\coloneqq A_{1}v^{1}+\ldots+A_{d}v^{d},\qquad v=(v^{1},\ldots,v^{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Set

\displaystyle\sigma:\mathbb{R}^{m}\rightarrow L(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{m}),

\displaystyle\sigma(Z)(W):=(AW)Z,\ \ \ Z\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\ \text{and}\ \ W\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

We aim to solve

(4.7)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathrm{d}Y_{t}&=\sigma(Y_{t})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{X}_{t};\qquad t\in[0,T],\\
Y_{0}&=y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}.\end{split}

A natural candidate for a solution to (
4.7
) is

(4.8)

\displaystyle Y_{t}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}A^{\otimes n}\left(\int_{0<s_{1}<\ldots<s_{n}<t}\mathrm{d}X_{s_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathrm{d}X_{s_{n}}\right)(y)

where we write
\int_{0<s_{1}<\ldots<s_{n}<t}\mathrm{d}X_{s_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathrm{d}X_{s_{n}}
for the element
\mathbb{X}^{(n)}_{0,t}
that is uniquely defined for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
due to Lyons’ Extension theorem. In the expression above,
A^{\otimes n}\colon(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{\otimes n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
is the linear map defined by
A^{\otimes 0}(v)=I_{m}
and

\displaystyle A^{\otimes n}(e_{i_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes e_{i_{n}})=A_{i_{1}}\cdots A_{i_{n}},\quad n\geq 1,

where
\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{d}\}
denotes the Euclidean basis of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. For example, if
A_{1}=\cdots=A_{d}=I_{m}
,

\displaystyle Y_{t}=y

\displaystyle+y\sum_{i=1}^{d}\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}X^{i}_{s}+\ldots+y\sum_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{n}=1}^{d}\int_{0<t_{1}<\cdots<t_{n}<t}\mathrm{d}X_{t_{1}}^{i_{1}}\cdots\mathrm{d}X_{t_{n}}^{i_{n}}+\ldots

where we use the notation
\int_{0<t_{1}<\cdots<t_{n}<t}\mathrm{d}X_{t_{1}}^{i_{1}}\cdots\mathrm{d}X_{t_{n}}^{i_{n}}=\mathbb{X}_{0,t}^{(n);i_{1},\ldots,i_{n}}
. Note that the infinite sum (
4.8
) indeed converges due to the superexponential decay of the iterated integrals
\mathbb{X}^{(n)}
deduced in Theorem
4.8
.

4.3.
Brownian motion as a rough path

We saw that rough paths can be used to solve linear equations, or linear
rough differential equations
. The natural question is now how we can use this result to solve linear stochastic differential equations pathwise. This would be possible if we could show that a given stochastic process can be “naturally extended” to a rough paths valued process. The most important process in stochastic analysis is the Brownian motion. Let
B=(B^{1},\ldots,B^{d})
be a
d
-dimensional Brownian motion, i.e. the
B^{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,d
, are independent, real valued Brownian motions. We know that the Brownian motion has trajectories that are
\alpha
-Hölder continuous for every
\alpha<\frac{1}{2}
. Therefore, we can construct a rough paths valued process if we determine the second iterated integral. There are (at least) two natural candidates: First, we can set
\mathbf{B}^{\text{It\={o}}}=(1,B,\mathbb{B}^{\text{It\={o}}})
where

\displaystyle\mathbb{B}^{\text{It\={o}}}_{s,t}=\int_{s}^{t}(B_{u}-B_{s})\,\otimes\mathrm{d}B_{u}.

The integral is understood as an Itō integral. Another choice would be
\mathbf{B}^{\text{Strat}}=(1,B,\mathbb{B}^{\text{Strat}})
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{B}^{\text{Strat}}_{s,t}=\int_{s}^{t}(B_{u}-B_{s})\,\otimes\circ\mathrm{d}B_{u}=\mathbb{B}^{\text{It\={o}}}_{s,t}+I_{d}\frac{(t-s)}{2}

where the integral is understood as Stratonovich integral. Since both the Itō and the Stratonovich integral satisfy
\int_{s}^{t}=\int_{s}^{u}+\int_{u}^{t}
for
s<u<t
, they satisfy Chen’s relation, thus they are multiplicative functionals almost surely. It remains to check that also the iterated integrals have the right Hölder-regularity. To prove this regularity, we first state the following version of the Kolmogorov-Chentsov theorem:

Theorem 4.10

(Kolmogorov-Chentsov theorem for multiplicative functionals)
.

Let
\mathbf{X}\colon\Delta\to T^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be a random continuous multiplicative functional,
q\geq 2
,
\beta>\frac{1}{q}
and assume that

\displaystyle\|\mathbb{X}^{(1)}_{s,t}\|_{L^{q}}\leq C|t-s|^{\beta}\quad\text{and}\quad\|\mathbb{X}^{(2)}_{s,t}\|_{L^{q/2}}\leq C|t-s|^{2\beta}

for a constant
C>0
and any
s,t\in[0,T]
. Then for all
\alpha\in[0,\beta-1/q)
, there are random variables
K^{1}_{\alpha}\in L^{q}
and
K^{2}_{\alpha}\in L^{\frac{q}{2}}
such that

\displaystyle|\mathbb{X}^{(1)}_{s,t}|\leq K^{1}_{\alpha}|t-s|^{\alpha}\quad\text{and}\quad|\mathbb{X}^{(2)}_{s,t}|\leq K^{2}_{\alpha}|t-s|^{2\alpha}

for all
s,t\in[0,T]
. In particular,
{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\mathbf{X}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\alpha}<\infty
almost surely.

Proof.

The proof is similar to the one of the classical Kolmogorov-Chentsov theorem that we already saw in Theorem
2.11
. We proceed as in
[
FH20
, Theorem 3.1]
. We assume that
T=1
. Defining
D_{n}
and
D
as in the proof of Theorem
2.11
, we set

\displaystyle K_{n}\coloneqq\sup_{t\in D_{n}}|\mathbb{X}^{(1)}_{t,t+2^{-n}}|\quad\text{and}\quad\tilde{K}_{n}\coloneqq\sup_{t\in D_{n}}|\mathbb{X}^{(2)}_{t,t+2^{-n}}|.

As before, one can check that
\mathbb{E}(K^{q}_{n})\leq C^{q}|D_{n}|^{q\beta-1}
and
\mathbb{E}(\tilde{K}^{q/2}_{n})\leq C^{q/2}|D_{n}|^{q\beta-1}
. Fix
s,t\in D
and choose
m
with
|D_{m+1}|<|t-s|\leq|D_{m}|
. Furthermore, choose

\displaystyle s=\tau_{0}<\tau_{1}<\ldots<\tau_{N}=t

as in the proof of Theorem
2.11
. Then,

\displaystyle|\mathbb{X}^{(1)}_{s,t}|\leq\max_{0\leq i\leq N-1}|\mathbb{X}^{(1)}_{s,\tau_{i+1}}|\leq\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}|\mathbb{X}^{(1)}_{\tau_{i},\tau_{i+1}}|\leq 2\sum_{n\geq m+1}K_{n}.

Using the Chen relation repeatedly gives

\displaystyle|\mathbb{X}^{(2)}_{s,t}|

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\mathbb{X}^{(2)}_{\tau_{i},\tau_{i+1}}+\mathbb{X}^{(1)}_{s,\tau_{i}}\otimes\mathbb{X}^{(1)}_{\tau_{i},\tau_{i+1}}\right|\leq\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}|\mathbb{X}^{(2)}_{\tau_{i},\tau_{i+1}}|+|\mathbb{X}^{(1)}_{s,\tau_{i}}||\mathbb{X}^{(1)}_{\tau_{i},\tau_{i+1}}|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}|\mathbb{X}^{(2)}_{\tau_{i},\tau_{i+1}}|+\max_{0\leq i\leq N-1}|\mathbb{X}^{(1)}_{s,\tau_{i+1}}|\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}|\mathbb{X}^{(1)}_{\tau_{i},\tau_{i+1}}|\leq 2\sum_{n\geq m+1}\tilde{K}_{n}+\left(2\sum_{n\geq m+1}K_{n}\right)^{2}.

In the proof of Theorem
2.11
, we have already seen that this implies that for every
s<t

\displaystyle\frac{|\mathbb{X}^{(1)}_{s,t}|}{|t-s|^{\alpha}}\leq 2\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{K_{n}}{|D_{n}|^{\alpha}}\eqqcolon K^{1}_{\alpha}

and
K^{1}_{\alpha}\in L^{q}
. Similarly,

\displaystyle\frac{|\mathbb{X}^{(2)}_{s,t}|}{|t-s|^{2\alpha}}\leq 2\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\tilde{K}_{n}}{|D_{n}|^{2\alpha}}+\left(2\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{K_{n}}{|D_{n}|^{\alpha}}\right)^{2}=K^{2}_{\alpha}+(K^{1}_{\alpha})^{2}

where

\displaystyle K^{2}_{\alpha}\coloneqq\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\tilde{K}_{n}}{|D_{n}|^{2\alpha}}.

It is then straightforward to check that
K^{2}_{\alpha}\in L^{\frac{q}{2}}
which finishes the proof.

∎

Corollary 4.11
.

We have
{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\mathbf{B}^{\text{It\={o}}}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\alpha}<\infty
and
{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\mathbf{B}^{\text{Strat}}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\alpha}<\infty
almost surely for every
\alpha<\frac{1}{2}
.

Proof.

Using Brownian scaling, one can show that the conditions of Theorem
4.10
hold for the Itō- and for the Stratonovich lift of the Brownian motion for
\beta=\frac{1}{2}
and every
q\geq 2
.
∎

From Corollary (
4.11
), we know that
\mathbf{B}^{\text{It\={o}}}
and
\mathbf{B}^{\text{Strat}}
are both rough path valued stochastic processes. Therefore, we can use them both to solve linear stochastic differential equations driven by a Brownian motion. However, choosing the Itō or the Stratonovich rough path lift leads to different solutions, which is natural and well known in stochastic analysis. In fact, the choice of the rough path lift should be regarded as another parameter in the equation and depends on the problem one aims to find a model for.

5.
The space of rough paths

5.1.
Metrics on rough paths spaces and separability

In the previous section, we defined the set
\mathscr{C}^{\alpha}([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{d})
of
\alpha
-Hölder rough paths. Note that there is no meaningful notion of the sum of two rough paths, i.e.
\mathscr{C}^{\alpha}
is not a linear space. We will see now that it is still a metric space.

Definition 5.1
.

Let
\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y}\in\mathscr{C}^{\alpha}
. Then we define

\displaystyle\varrho_{\alpha}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})\coloneqq\sum_{n=1}^{\lfloor 1/\alpha\rfloor}\sup_{0\leq s<t\leq T}\frac{|\mathbb{X}^{(n)}_{s,t}-\mathbb{Y}^{(n)}_{s,t}|}{|t-s|^{n\alpha}}.

It is not hard to see that
\varrho_{\alpha}
is a metric on
\mathscr{C}^{\alpha}
. Moreover, one can prove the following:

Proposition 5.2
.

For every
\alpha\in(0,1]
, the space
(\mathscr{C}^{\alpha},\varrho_{\alpha})
is a complete metric space.

Proof.

The arguments are the same as those used for proving that the usual Hölder spaces are complete. We leave the details to the reader. A detailed proof can be found in
[
LQ02
, Lemma 3.3.3]
.
∎

Sometimes, it is desirable to work with separable rough paths spaces. However, since Hölder spaces are not separable, we cannot expect that the spaces
\mathscr{C}^{\alpha}
are separable. To solve this issue for Hölder spaces, one often considers
little Hölder spaces
that are defined as the closure of the space of smooth functions in the
\alpha
-Hölder metric. A similar definition works for rough paths spaces, too.

Definition 5.3
.

Let
X\colon[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
be smooth (e.g. piecewise continuously differentiable) and
\alpha\in(0,1]
. Then we call
\mathbb{X}\in\mathscr{C}^{\alpha}
with

\displaystyle\mathbb{X}_{s,t}^{(n)}=\int_{s<u_{1}<\ldots<u_{n}<t}\mathrm{d}X_{u_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathrm{d}X_{u_{n}}

the
canonical lift
of
X
to an
\alpha
-Hölder rough path. Rough paths
\mathbf{X}\in\mathscr{C}^{\alpha}
of this form are also called
smooth rough paths
. The space
\mathscr{C}^{\alpha}_{g}
is defined as the closure of smooth rough paths in the metric
\varrho_{\alpha}
. The elements in
\mathscr{C}^{\alpha}_{g}
are called
geometric rough paths
.

Proposition 5.4
.

For every
\alpha\in(0,1]
, the space
(\mathscr{C}_{g}^{\alpha},\varrho_{\alpha})
is a complete separable metric, i.e.
Polish
space.

Proof.

Completeness follows by definition. The idea to show separability is to find a complete separable space of smooth paths containing all piecewise
\mathcal{C}^{1}
-paths for which the canonical lift map is continuous. An example is the space obtained by taking the closure of arbitrarily often differentiable paths with respect to the total variation distance. Details can be found in
[
BRS17
, Appendix A and B]
.
∎

Proposition 5.5
.

The process
\mathbf{B}^{\mathrm{Strat}}=(1,B,\mathbb{B}^{\mathrm{Strat}})
takes values in the space
\mathscr{C}^{\alpha}_{g}
for every
\frac{1}{3}<\alpha<\frac{1}{2}
almost surely.

Proof.

For simplicity,
T=1
. Choose
\alpha^{\prime}
such that
\alpha<\alpha^{\prime}<\frac{1}{2}
. We know that

\displaystyle{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\mathbf{B}^{\text{Strat}}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\alpha^{\prime}}<\infty.

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we define
B(n)
to be the piecewise-linear approximation of
B
at the dyadic points
0<2^{-n}<\cdots<(2^{n}-1)2^{-n}<1
, i.e.

\displaystyle B_{t}(n)=B_{k2^{-n}}+2^{n}(t-k2^{-n})(B_{(k+1)2^{-n}}-B_{k2^{-n}}),\qquad t\in[k2^{-n},(k+1)2^{-n}].

Let
\mathbf{B}(n)
be the canonical lift of
B(n)
to an
\alpha
-Hölder rough path. With some basic calculations, one can show that

\displaystyle\|\delta B(n)_{s,t}\|_{L^{2}}\leq C|t-s|^{\frac{1}{2}}\quad\text{and}\quad\|\mathbb{B}(n)_{s,t}\|_{L^{2}}\leq C|t-s|

holds for every
s<t
for a constant
C
that is independent of
n
. Since
B
is Gaussian, the same estimates also hold for the
L^{q}
-norm for every
q\geq 2
. The Kolmogorov-Chentsov theorem for multiplicative functionals implies that

\displaystyle\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\mathbf{B}(n)\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\alpha^{\prime}}<\infty.

a.s. To prove that
\varrho_{\alpha}(\mathbf{B}^{\text{Strat}},\mathbf{B}(n))\to 0
, by the Arzelà-Ascoli theorem, it is sufficient to show that
B(n)\to B
and
\mathbb{B}(n)\to\mathbb{B}^{\text{Strat}}
pointwise as
n\to\infty
. The first statement is clear. For the second, we first note that

\displaystyle\int_{s}^{t}(B^{i}_{u}(n)-B^{i}_{s}(n))\,\mathrm{d}B^{i}_{u}(n)=\frac{(B_{t}(n)-B_{s}(n))^{2}}{2}\to\frac{(B_{t}-B_{s})^{2}}{2}=\int_{s}^{t}(B^{i}_{u}-B^{i}_{s})\,\circ\mathrm{d}B^{i}_{u}.

Define

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{n}\coloneqq\sigma(B_{k}\,:\,k\in\{0,2^{-n},\ldots,(2^{n}-1)2^{-n},1\}).

Then
(\mathcal{F}_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is a filtration. Fix
t\in[0,T]
. By Gaussian conditioning, one can check that
B_{t}(n)=\mathbb{E}[B_{t}\,|\,\mathcal{F}_{n}]
. From the martingale convergence theorem, it follows that

\displaystyle B_{t}(n)=\mathbb{E}[B_{t}\,|\,\mathcal{F}_{n}]\to B_{t}

almost surely and in
L^{p}
for any
p\geq 1
as
n\to\infty
(which yields an alternative proof of what we already know). For
i\neq j
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\int_{0}^{t}B^{i}_{s}\,\mathrm{d}B^{j}_{s}\,|\,\mathcal{F}_{n}\right)

\displaystyle=\lim_{|\mathcal{P}|\to 0}\sum_{[u,v]\in\mathcal{P}}\mathbb{E}\left(B^{i}_{u}\delta B^{j}_{u,v}\,|\,\mathcal{F}_{n}\right)=\sum_{[u,v]\in\mathcal{P}}B^{i}_{u}(n)\delta B^{j}_{u,v}(n)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}B^{i}_{s}(n)\,\mathrm{d}B^{j}_{s}(n).

Therefore, the martingale convergence theorem yields that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}B^{i}_{s}(n)\,\mathrm{d}B^{j}_{s}(n)=\mathbb{E}\left(\int_{0}^{t}B^{i}_{s}\,\mathrm{d}B^{j}_{s}\,|\,\mathcal{F}_{n}\right)\to\int_{0}^{t}B^{i}_{s}\,\mathrm{d}B^{j}_{s}

almost surely and in
L^{p}
for any
p\geq 1
as
n\to\infty
, which finishes the proof.
∎

A natural question is whether
\mathbf{B}^{\text{It\={o}}}
has geometric rough paths trajectories, too. We will see in the next section that this is not the case.

5.2.
Shuffles and the signature

There is also an important algebraic property satisfied by geometric rough paths that is inherited from smooth rough paths. In fact, multiplying two iterated integrals of smooth paths yields a linear combination of iterated integrals. For example,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\mathrm{d}X_{s}\cdot\int_{0}^{T}\mathrm{d}Y_{s}=\int_{0<s_{1}<s_{2}<T}\mathrm{d}X_{s_{1}}\,\mathrm{d}Y_{s_{2}}+\int_{0<s_{1}<s_{2}<T}\mathrm{d}Y_{s_{1}}\,\mathrm{d}X_{s_{2}}.

Note that this is a property that does not hold for every rough path. For example, the Itō integral satisfies the identity

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}B_{t}\,\mathrm{d}B_{t}=\frac{B^{2}_{t}}{2}-\frac{t}{2}

which shows that the sample paths of
\mathbf{B}^{\text{It\={o}}}
behave differently.

We aim to give a more detailed description of the product of iterated integrals of smooth paths. To do this, we introduce some more notation.

Definition 5.6
.

The direct sum

\displaystyle T(\mathbb{R}^{d})\coloneqq\mathbb{R}\oplus\mathbb{R}^{d}\oplus(\mathbb{R}^{d}\otimes\mathbb{R}^{d})\oplus\cdots=\bigoplus_{n=0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{\otimes n}

is called
tensor algebra
.

One can show that the extended tensor algebra is the (algebraic) dual of the tensor algebra. We will identify the basis elements
e_{i_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes e_{i_{n}}
in the tensor algebra
T(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with the words
{i_{1}\cdots i_{n}}
composed by the letters
1,\ldots,d
. The empty word will be denoted by
{\epsilon}
. For two words, we can define their shuffle product:

Definition 5.7
.

Let
{u}
,
{v}
be words and
{a}
,
{b}
be letters. The
shuffle product
is defined recursively by

\displaystyle u\shuffle\epsilon

\displaystyle=\epsilon\shuffle u=u,

\displaystyle ua\shuffle vb

\displaystyle=(u\shuffle vb)a+(ua\shuffle v)b.

The shuffle product is extended bilinearly to a product

\displaystyle\shuffle\colon T(\mathbb{R}^{d})\times T(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to T(\mathbb{R}^{d}).

Example 5.8
.

(1)

For example,

\displaystyle{12}\shuffle{3}

\displaystyle={123}+{132}+{312},

\displaystyle{12}\shuffle{24}

\displaystyle=2\cdot{1224}+{1242}+{2124}+{2142}+{2412}.

(2)

Let
X\colon[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a smooth path (e.g.
\mathcal{C}^{1}
) and

(5.1)

\displaystyle\mathbf{X}_{0,T}:=\left(1,\int_{0}^{T}\mathrm{d}X_{s},\ldots,\int_{0<s_{1}<\cdots<s_{n}<T}\mathrm{d}X_{s_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathrm{d}X_{s_{n}},\ldots\right)\in T((\mathbb{R}^{d})).

With the notation we introduced above, we have, for example,

\displaystyle\langle{121},\mathbf{X}_{0,T}\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{0<s_{1}<s_{2}<s_{3}<T}\mathrm{d}X^{1}_{s_{1}}\,\mathrm{d}X^{2}_{s_{2}}\,\mathrm{d}X^{1}_{s_{3}},

\displaystyle\langle\sqrt{3}\cdot{12}-2\cdot{21},\mathbf{X}_{0,T}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sqrt{3}\int_{0<s_{1}<s_{2}<T}\mathrm{d}X^{1}_{s_{1}}\,\mathrm{d}X^{2}_{s_{2}}-2\int_{0<s_{1}<s_{2}<T}\mathrm{d}X^{2}_{s_{1}}\,\mathrm{d}X^{1}_{s_{2}}.

The main observation is the following:

Theorem 5.9
.

For
\mathbf{X}
defined as in (
5.1
), for every
l_{1},l_{2}\in T(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,

\displaystyle\langle l_{1},\mathbf{X}_{0,T}\rangle\langle l_{2},\mathbf{X}_{0,T}\rangle=\langle l_{1}\shuffle l_{2},\mathbf{X}_{0,T}\rangle.

Proof.

Let
u
and
v
be words and
a
and
b
be letters from the alphabet
\{1,\ldots,d\}
. The proof is by induction over the length of the words. Using the induction hypothesis, we have

\displaystyle\langle ua,\mathbf{X}_{0,T}\rangle\langle vb,\mathbf{X}_{0,T}\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\langle u,\mathbf{X}_{0,t}\rangle\,\mathrm{d}X^{a}_{t}\cdot\int_{0}^{T}\langle v,\mathbf{X}_{0,s}\rangle\,\mathrm{d}X^{b}_{s}

\displaystyle=\int_{0<s,t<T}\langle u,\mathbf{X}_{0,t}\rangle\langle v,\mathbf{X}_{0,s}\rangle\,\mathrm{d}X^{a}_{t}\,\mathrm{d}X^{b}_{s}

\displaystyle=\int_{0<t<s<T}\langle u,\mathbf{X}_{0,t}\rangle\langle v,\mathbf{X}_{0,s}\rangle\,\mathrm{d}X^{a}_{t}\,\mathrm{d}X^{b}_{s}+\int_{0<s<t<T}\langle u,\mathbf{X}_{0,t}\rangle\langle v,\mathbf{X}_{0,s}\rangle\,\mathrm{d}X^{b}_{s}\,\mathrm{d}X^{a}_{t}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\langle ua,\mathbf{X}_{0,s}\rangle\langle v,\mathbf{X}_{0,s}\rangle\,\mathrm{d}X^{b}_{s}+\int_{0}^{T}\langle u,\mathbf{X}_{0,t}\rangle\langle vb,\mathbf{X}_{0,t}\rangle\,\mathrm{d}X^{a}_{t}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\langle ua\shuffle v,\mathbf{X}_{0,s}\rangle\,\mathrm{d}X^{b}_{s}+\int_{0}^{T}\langle u\shuffle vb,\mathbf{X}_{0,t}\rangle\,\mathrm{d}X^{a}_{t}

\displaystyle=\langle(ua\shuffle v)b,\mathbf{X}_{0,T}\rangle+\langle(u\shuffle vb)a,\mathbf{X}_{0,T}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle(ua\shuffle v)b+(u\shuffle vb)a,\mathbf{X}_{0,T}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle ua\shuffle vb,\mathbf{X}_{0,T}\rangle.

∎

The following corollary is immediate.

Corollary 5.10
.

Let
\mathbf{X}\in\mathscr{C}^{\alpha}_{g}
be a geometric rough path. We identify
\mathbf{X}
with its Lyons-lift to a path with values in
T((\mathbb{R}^{d}))
. Then for every
l_{1},l_{2}\in T(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,

\displaystyle\langle l_{1},\mathbf{X}_{0,T}\rangle\langle l_{2},\mathbf{X}_{0,T}\rangle=\langle l_{1}\shuffle l_{2},\mathbf{X}_{0,T}\rangle.

Remark 5.11
.

Let
\mathbf{X}
be a geometric rough path. As usual, we identify
\mathbf{X}
with its Lyons lift to a path with values in
T((\mathbb{R}^{d}))
. Then the element
\mathbf{X}_{0,T}\in T((\mathbb{R}^{d}))
is called the
signature
of the rough path
\mathbf{X}
. The signature is important since it contains all (necessary) information about the rough path. Indeed, in a series of papers, it was shown that the signature determines a geometric rough path completely up to so-called “tree-like” excursions
[
Che58
,
HL10
,
BGLY16
]
. If
\mathbf{X}
is random, the
expected signature
determines the law of
\mathbf{X}
and can be seen as a Laplace transform for measures on path spaces
[
CL16
,
CO22
]
. The (truncated) signature also plays an important role in machine learning as a way to extract characteristic features from a data stream, cf.
[
CK16
,
LM22
]
for an overview.

6.
Controlled paths and rough integral

We aim to solve non-linear rough differential equations of the form

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}Y_{t}

\displaystyle=\sigma(Y_{t})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{X}_{t};\quad t\in[0,T]

\displaystyle Y_{0}

\displaystyle=y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}.

As for the Young case, we want to interpret the equation as an integral equation:

\displaystyle Y_{t}=y+\int_{0}^{t}\sigma(Y_{s})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{X}_{s};\quad t\in[0,T].

We want to find a notion of an integral that coincides with the Young integral in case the integrand is smooth. That is, for a smooth function
f
and a Brownian motion
B
, we would like to have that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}f(s)\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{B}_{s}=\int_{0}^{T}f(s)\,\mathrm{d}B_{s}.

Here,
\mathbf{B}
may either denote
\mathbf{B}^{\text{It\={o}}}
or
\mathbf{B}^{\text{Strat}}
. If we want to perform a fixed point argument to solve the equation, it is desirable to look for a Banach space
E
containing smooth functions such that the map

\displaystyle f\mapsto\big(t\mapsto\int_{0}^{t}f(s)\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{B}_{s}\big)

is a continuous map from
E
to itself. A minimal requirement for
E
would be that it contains the trajectories of the Brownian motion, otherwise we would not be able to integrate constant functions. However, one can show that such a space
E
does not exist:

Theorem 6.1
.

There is no space of functions
E
carrying the Wiener measure on which we can define a continuous map
I\colon E\to E
that coincides with the pathwise defined integral

\displaystyle I(f)=\big(t\mapsto\int_{0}^{t}f(s)\,\mathrm{d}B_{s}\big)

for smooth functions
f
on a set of full measure.

Proof.

Same idea as in the proof of Theorem
3.13
.
∎

The solution to this issue proposed by rough paths theory is that we allow the space
E
to depend on the trajectory of the Brownian motion, i.e. we will define spaces
\{E_{\omega}\}_{\omega\in\Omega}
for which
B(\omega)\in E_{\omega}
with the property that

\displaystyle E_{\omega}\ni f\mapsto\big(t\mapsto\int_{0}^{t}f(s)\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{B}_{s}(\omega)\big)\in E_{\omega}

extends the integral map on smooth paths and is continuous. Our goal is to define a “rough integral” of the form

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}Y_{t}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{X}_{t},

for a given rough path
\mathbf{X}\in\mathscr{C}^{\alpha}
. Before moving forward, let us assume the following assumptions:

Assumption 6.2
.

For the sake of simplicity, we will assume
\alpha\in(1/3,1/2]
from now on.

Remember that we deduced the regularity of a 3-times iterated Young integral by introducing a “compensator”:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{X}_{0,t}^{(2)}\,\mathrm{d}X_{t}=\sum_{|\mathcal{P}|\to 0}\sum_{[u,v]\in\mathcal{P}}\mathbb{X}_{0,u}^{(2)}\otimes\delta X_{u,v}+\delta X_{0,u}\otimes\mathbb{X}_{u,v}^{(2)}.

This motivates the following ansatz: for given
\mathbf{X}=(1,X,\mathbb{X})\in\mathscr{C}^{\alpha}
and
Y\colon[0,T]\to L(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{m})
, we assume that there exists a path
Y^{\prime}\colon[0,T]\to L(\mathbb{R}^{d}\otimes\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{m})
for which we can define the limit

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}Y_{t}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{X}_{t}=\sum_{|\mathcal{P}|\to 0}\sum_{[u,v]\in\mathcal{P}}Y_{u}\delta X_{u,v}+Y^{\prime}_{u}\mathbb{X}_{u,v}.

As before, we will use the Sewing lemma to prove the existence of the limit. Set

\displaystyle\Xi_{u,v}\coloneqq Y_{u}\delta X_{u,v}+Y^{\prime}_{u}\mathbb{X}_{u,v}.

Clearly,
\|\Xi\|_{\alpha}<\infty
. We have to make sure that
\|\delta\Xi\|_{\beta}<\infty
for some
\beta>1
. After some lines of calculations, we see that

\displaystyle\delta\Xi_{s,u,t}=-(\delta Y_{s,u}-Y^{\prime}_{s}\delta X_{s,u})X_{u,t}-\delta Y^{\prime}_{s,u}\mathbb{X}_{u,t}.

Therefore, we arrive at the conditions

\displaystyle|\delta Y^{\prime}_{s,t}|

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}(|t-s|^{\gamma})\quad\text{where}\quad\quad\gamma+2\alpha>1\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle\quad|\delta Y_{s,t}-Y^{\prime}_{s}\delta X_{s,t}|

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}(|t-s|^{\tilde{\gamma}})\quad\text{with}\quad\tilde{\gamma}+\alpha>1.

These conditions are in particular satisfied for
\gamma=\alpha
and
\tilde{\gamma}=2\alpha
. This observation motivates the following definition that was introduced by Gubinelli in
[
Gub04
]
first.

Definition 6.3
.

Let
\mathbf{X}\in\mathscr{C}^{\alpha}([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
\alpha\in(1/3,1/2]
. A path
Y\in\mathcal{C}^{\alpha}([0,T],W)
is said to be
controlled by
\mathbf{X}
if there exists a path
Y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}^{\alpha}([0,T],L(\mathbb{R}^{d},W))
such that the remainder term
R^{Y}
given by

\displaystyle R^{Y}_{s,t}\coloneqq\delta Y_{s,t}-Y^{\prime}_{s}\delta X_{s,t}

satisfies
\|R^{Y}\|_{2\alpha}<\infty
. The path
Y^{\prime}
is called a
Gubinelli-derivative
of
Y
. The set of all controlled paths
(Y,Y^{\prime})
is denoted by
\mathscr{D}^{\alpha}_{X}([0,T],W)
. If
(Y,Y^{\prime})\in\mathscr{D}^{\alpha}_{X}([0,T],W)
, we set

\displaystyle\|Y,Y^{\prime}\|_{X,\alpha}\coloneqq\|Y^{\prime}\|_{\alpha}+\|R^{Y}\|_{2\alpha}.

Example 6.4
.

(1)

If
\mathbf{X}=(1,\delta X,\mathbb{X}^{(2)})\in\mathscr{C}^{\alpha}
, the path
X
is controlled by
\mathbf{X}
. A Gubinelli-derivative is given by the constant function
Y^{\prime}=I_{d}
.

(2)

If
Y
is smooth or, more precisely,
2\alpha
-Hölder continuous, the path is controlled by
\mathbf{X}
with Gubinelli-derivative
Y^{\prime}=0
.

It is easily seen that the space of controlled paths is a linear space for every fixed rough path
\mathbf{X}\in\mathscr{C}^{\alpha}
. Moreover, one can prove the following:

Proposition 6.5
.

The spaces
\mathscr{D}^{\alpha}_{X}([0,T],W)
are Banach spaces with a norm given by

\displaystyle(Y,Y^{\prime})\mapsto|Y_{0}|+|Y^{\prime}_{0}|+\|Y,Y^{\prime}\|_{X,\alpha}\eqqcolon{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|Y,Y^{\prime}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{X,\alpha}.

Proof.

Straightforward.
∎

Remark 6.6
.

Gubinelli derivatives are not unique, in general. Indeed, if
Y
is smooth, we can choose
Y^{\prime}=0
. But if
X
is smooth, too, we can in fact choose any
\mathcal{C}^{\alpha}
-path as a Gubinelli-derivative. On the contrary, if
X
is not smooth, one can show uniqueness of the Gubinelli-derivative, cf.
[
FH20
, Proposition 6.4]
.

The most important fact about controlled paths is that they are good integrands.

Theorem 6.7
.

Let
\mathbf{X}\in\mathscr{C}^{\alpha}([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
(Y,Y^{\prime})\in\mathscr{D}^{\alpha}_{X}([0,T],L(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{m}))
.

(1)

The integral

\displaystyle\int_{s}^{t}Y_{u}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{X}_{u}:=\lim_{|\mathcal{P}|\to 0}\sum_{[u,v]\in\mathcal{P}}Y_{u}\delta X_{u,v}+Y^{\prime}_{u}\mathbb{X}_{u,v}

(6.1)

(2)

The path
t\mapsto\int_{0}^{t}Y_{u}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{X}_{u}
is a controlled path with Gubinelli-derivative
Y

\displaystyle(Y,Y^{\prime})\mapsto\left(\int_{0}^{\cdot}Y_{u}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{X}_{u},Y\right)\eqqcolon(Z,Z^{\prime})

is a continuous linear map from

\displaystyle\|Z,Z^{\prime}\|_{X,\alpha}\leq\|Y\|_{\alpha}+\|Y^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\|\mathbb{X}\|_{2\alpha}+CT^{\alpha}(\|X\|_{\alpha}\|R^{Y}\|_{2\alpha}+\|\mathbb{X}\|_{2\alpha}\|Y^{\prime}\|_{\alpha}).

Proof.

As already indicated above, we use the Sewing lemma with

From

we see that

\displaystyle\|\delta\Xi\|_{3\alpha}\leq\|R^{Y}\|_{2\alpha}\|X\|_{\alpha}+\|Y^{\prime}\|_{\alpha}\|\mathbb{X}\|_{2\alpha}

is

6.1.
Controlled paths as a field of Banach spaces

The statements discussed in this section are simplified versions of the more general results obtained in
[
GVRST22
]
. Recall that we defined a Banach space of controlled paths for every rough path
\mathbf{X}
. The question we would like to answer now is whether the indexed spaces
\{\mathscr{D}^{\alpha}_{X}([0,T],W)\}_{\mathbf{X}\in\mathscr{C}^{\alpha}}
have more structure than being just a collection of isolated spaces. This will also have practical relevance. From Theorem
6.7
, we know that rough integration induces bounded linear maps

\displaystyle\Phi(\mathbf{X},\cdot)\colon\mathscr{D}^{\alpha}_{X}([0,T],W)\to\mathscr{D}^{\alpha}_{X}([0,T],\bar{W}).

If
\mathbf{X}
is a stochastic process (i.e. a random rough path), the operator norm

\displaystyle\|\Phi(\omega)\|\coloneqq\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}(Z,Z^{\prime})\in\mathscr{D}^{\alpha}_{X(\omega)}([0,T],W)\\
(Z,Z^{\prime})\neq 0\end{subarray}}\frac{{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\Phi(\mathbf{X}(\omega),(Z,Z^{\prime}))\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}}{{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|Z,Z^{\prime}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}}

is a natural quantity to consider (note that we dropped the lower indices for the norms on controlled rough paths spaces on the right hand side of the equation to ease notation). One seemingly basic question to answer first is the measurability of this random number. We will see that having some additional structure on the space of controlled paths will help us to answer this question.
We make the following definition:

Definition 6.8
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a topological space and
\{E_{x}\}_{x\in\mathcal{X}}
a collection of Banach spaces.
\{E_{x}\}_{x\in\mathcal{X}}
is called a
separable continuous field of Banach spaces
if there exists a countable set of sections
\Delta\subset\prod_{x\in\mathcal{X}}E_{x}
, i.e. every
g\in\Delta
is a map
g\colon\mathcal{X}\to\bigcup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}E_{x}
with
g(x)\in E_{x}
for every
x\in\mathcal{X}
, that has the following properties:

(1)

For every
g\in\Delta
,
x\mapsto\|g(x)\|_{E_{x}}\in\mathbb{R}
is continuous.

(2)

For every
x\in\mathcal{X}
, the set
\{g(x)\,:\,g\in\Delta\}
is dense in
E_{x}
.

Remark 6.9
.

The usual definition of a continuous field of Banach spaces in the literature differs slightly from the one we gave in Definition
6.8
. In
[
Dix77
]
, the definition of a continuous field of Banach spaces assumes the existence of a
linear subspace
of sections
\Delta^{\prime}
satisfying (1) and (2). Separability in
[
Dix77
]
means that there is a countable subset
\Delta\subset\Delta^{\prime}
satisfying (2.). It is clear that our definition is equivalent since a linear subspace of sections can be just obtained by considering the linear span of
\Delta
. Also,
[
Dix77
]
assumes a third property for
\Delta^{\prime}
that is as follows:

(3’)

Let
\tilde{g}\in\prod_{x\in\mathcal{X}}E_{x}
. If for every
y\in\mathcal{X}
and
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
g_{y}\in\Delta^{\prime}
such that
\|\tilde{g}(x)-g_{y}(x)\|_{E_{x}}\leq\varepsilon
in some neighbourhood of
y
in
\mathcal{X}
, then
\tilde{g}\in\Delta^{\prime}
.

However, one can show that having a
\Delta^{\prime}
satisfying only (1) and (2), one can take some “completion” of
\Delta^{\prime}
that satisfies (3’), too
[
Dix77
, 10.2.3. Proposition]
. Therefore, the definition we gave here could also be called a separable continuous
pre-field
of Banach spaces.

The question we want to answer now is whether the spaces of controlled paths form a separable continuous field of Banach spaces. However, we cannot expect that separability holds since the spaces of controlled paths are equipped with Hölder-type norms that make them not separable themselves. Nevertheless, we will see that a slightly weaker result holds. Inspired by the little Hölder and geometric rough paths spaces, we define:

Definition 6.10
.

Let
\mathbf{X}\in\mathscr{C}^{\beta}
,
\beta\in(1/3,1/2]
and
\alpha\leq\beta
. We define
\mathscr{D}^{\alpha,\beta}_{X}([0,T],W)
to be the closure of the space
\mathscr{D}^{\beta}_{X}([0,T],W)
in the
{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\cdot\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\alpha}
-Norm.

The key result is the following lemma.

Lemma 6.11
.

Let
\mathbf{X}\in\mathscr{C}^{\gamma}
and
\frac{1}{3}<\alpha<\beta\leq\gamma\leq\frac{1}{2}
. Then the set

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}:=\left\{(Z,Z^{\prime})\,:\,Z_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}\phi_{u}\,\mathrm{d}X_{u}+\psi_{t},\ Z^{\prime}_{t}=\phi_{t}\,:\,\phi\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}([0,T],L(\mathbb{R}^{d},W)),\ \psi\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}([0,T],W)\right\}

is dense in
\mathscr{D}^{\alpha,\beta}_{X}([0,T],W)
. The integral here is defined as a Young integral.

Proof.

It suffices to proof that
\mathcal{Z}
is dense in
\mathscr{D}^{\beta}_{X}([0,T],W)
equipped with the norm
{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\cdot\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\alpha}
. Let
(\xi,\xi^{\prime})\in\mathscr{D}^{\beta}_{X}([0,T],W)
with remainder
R^{\xi}
, i.e.
\|\xi^{\prime}\|_{\beta}<\infty
and
\|R^{\xi}\|_{2\beta}<\infty
. Let

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}=\{0=t_{0}<t_{1}<\ldots<t_{n}=T\}

be a partition with
|\mathcal{P}|=|t_{i+1}-t_{i}|\eqqcolon\theta>0
for all
i=0,\ldots,n-1
. Define
\bar{\xi}^{\prime}\colon[0,T]\to W
to be the piecewise-linear approximation of
\xi^{\prime}
w.r.t. to
\mathcal{P}
, i.e.

\displaystyle\bar{\xi}^{\prime}_{t}\coloneqq\xi^{\prime}_{t_{i}}+\frac{t-t_{i}}{\theta}(\xi^{\prime}_{t_{i+1}}-\xi^{\prime}_{t_{i}}),\quad t\in[t_{i},t_{i+1}].

Our goal is to find a function
\psi
with
\psi_{0}=\xi_{0}
such that for

\displaystyle\bar{\xi}_{t}\coloneqq\int_{0}^{t}\bar{\xi}^{\prime}_{u}\,\mathrm{d}X_{u}+\psi_{t},

we have
{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\xi,\xi^{\prime})-(\bar{\xi},\bar{\xi}^{\prime})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\alpha}=\|(\xi,\xi^{\prime})-(\bar{\xi},\bar{\xi}^{\prime})\|_{X,\alpha}\leq\varepsilon
for any given
\varepsilon>0
as
\theta\to 0
. Set
\eta:=\xi^{\prime}-\bar{\xi}^{\prime}
. It is straightforward to show that
\|\eta\|_{\alpha}\to 0
as
\theta\to 0
. It remains to show that

\displaystyle\|R^{\xi}-R^{\bar{\xi}}\|_{2\alpha}\to 0

as
\theta\to 0
where

\displaystyle R^{\bar{\xi}}_{s,t}=\delta\bar{\xi}_{s,t}-\bar{\xi}^{\prime}_{s}\delta X_{s,t}=\int_{s}^{t}\bar{\xi}^{\prime}_{u}\,\mathrm{d}X_{u}-\bar{\xi}^{\prime}_{s}\delta X_{s,t}+\delta\psi_{s,t}

for a
\psi
still to be chosen. For
s,t\in[0,T]
, we define

\displaystyle\rho_{s,t}\coloneqq\int_{s}^{t}\bar{\xi}^{\prime}_{u}\,\mathrm{d}X_{u}-\bar{\xi}^{\prime}_{s}\delta X_{s,t}=\int_{s}^{t}\delta\bar{\xi}^{\prime}_{s,u}\,\mathrm{d}X_{u}.

If
s,t\in[t_{i},t_{i+1}]
, we have

\displaystyle\rho_{s,t}=\frac{\delta\xi^{\prime}_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}}{\theta}\int_{s}^{t}(u-s)\,\mathrm{d}X_{u}.

Using the estimate for the Young integral in Theorem
3.3
, we see that

\displaystyle\|\rho\|_{2\alpha;[t_{i},t_{i+1}]}\leq C\|\xi^{\prime}\|_{\beta}\|X\|_{\gamma}\theta^{\gamma+\beta-2\alpha}.

Now we take
t_{j},t_{k}\in\mathcal{P}
,
k<j
. Then,

\displaystyle\rho_{t_{k},t_{j}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\leq i<j}\left[\int_{t_{i}}^{t_{i+1}}\bar{\xi}^{\prime}_{t_{i},u}\,\mathrm{d}X_{u}+\delta\bar{\xi}^{\prime}_{t_{k},t_{i}}\delta X_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\leq i<j}\left[\rho_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}-R^{\xi}_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}+\delta\xi_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}-\xi_{t_{k}}\delta X_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\leq i<j}\left[\rho_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}-R^{\xi}_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}\right]+R^{\xi}_{t_{k},t_{j}}.

Setting
\tilde{\rho}_{s,t}\coloneqq R^{\xi}_{s,t}-\rho_{s,t}
, the calculation above implies that

(6.2)

\displaystyle\tilde{\rho}_{t_{k},t_{j}}=\sum_{k\leq i<j}\left[\rho_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}-R^{\xi}_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}\right].

We define
\tilde{\psi}
to be the continuous, piecewise-linear function satisfying
\tilde{\psi}_{0}=\xi_{0}
and

\displaystyle\delta\tilde{\psi}_{s,t}=\frac{t-s}{t_{i+1}-t_{i}}(R^{\xi}_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}-\rho_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}),\quad s,t\in[t_{i},t_{i+1}].

With this choice,

\displaystyle R^{\bar{\xi}}_{s,t}=\int_{s}^{t}\bar{\xi}^{\prime}_{u}\,\mathrm{d}X_{u}-\bar{\xi}^{\prime}_{s}\delta X_{s,t}+\delta\tilde{\psi}_{s,t}=\rho_{s,t}+\delta\tilde{\psi}_{s,t}.

Now let
s,t\in\mathcal{P}
with
t_{k}\leq s\leq t_{k+1}\leq\cdots\leq t_{j}\leq t\leq t_{j+1}
. By (
6.2
),

\displaystyle\delta\tilde{\psi}_{s,t}=\delta\tilde{\psi}_{s,t_{k+1}}+\delta\tilde{\psi}_{t_{k+1},t_{k+2}}+\ldots+\delta\tilde{\psi}_{t_{j},t}=\delta\tilde{\psi}_{s,t_{k+1}}+\delta\tilde{\psi}_{t_{j},t}+\tilde{\rho}_{t_{k+1},t_{j}}.

Furthermore,

\displaystyle\rho_{s,t}=\rho_{s,t_{k+1}}+\rho_{t_{k+1},t_{j}}+\rho_{t_{j},t}+\delta\bar{\xi}^{\prime}_{s,t_{k+1}}\delta X_{t_{k+1},t_{j}}+\delta\bar{\xi}^{\prime}_{t_{k+1},t_{j}}\delta X_{t_{j},t}

and

\displaystyle\tilde{\rho}_{s,t}=\tilde{\rho}_{s,t_{k+1}}+\tilde{\rho}_{t_{k+1},t_{j}}+\tilde{\rho}_{t_{j},t}+\delta\eta_{s,t_{k+1}}\delta X_{t_{k+1},t_{j}}+\delta\eta_{t_{k+1},t_{j}}\delta X_{t_{j},t}.

Thus, we obtain that

\displaystyle R^{\bar{\xi}}_{s,t}-R^{\xi}_{s,t}

\displaystyle=\delta\tilde{\psi}_{s,t}-\tilde{\rho}_{s,t}

\displaystyle=\delta\tilde{\psi}_{s,t_{k+1}}+\delta\tilde{\psi}_{t_{j},t}-\tilde{\rho}_{s,t_{k+1}}-\tilde{\rho}_{t_{j},t}-\delta\eta_{s,t_{k+1}}\delta X_{t_{k+1},t_{j}}-\delta\eta_{t_{k+1},t_{j}}\delta X_{t_{j},t}.

Each term can now be estimated separately and we can conclude that indeed

\displaystyle\|R^{\bar{\xi}}-R^{\xi}\|_{2\alpha}\to 0

as
\theta\to 0
. It remains to argue that we can replace the piecewise smooth functions
\bar{\xi}^{\prime}
and
\tilde{\psi}
by genuine smooth functions. This, however, does not cause any problems since we can approximate any continuous function arbitrarily close my smooth functions in the Hölder metric. Therefore, our claim is proved.
∎

For every

, an open set

Open tube

Fortunately, in the case of controlled paths, the tube topology is completely metrizable with an explicit metric. We state this result now.

\alpha<\beta\leq\frac{1}{2}

to obtain that
d^{\flat}_{\alpha,\beta}((Y,Y^{\prime}),(\tilde{Y},\tilde{Y}^{\prime}))<\varepsilon
. This proves claim 1.

Claim 2:
For given
W(g,U,\delta)
and
(Y,Y^{\prime})\in W(g,U,\delta)
, there is an
\varepsilon>0
such that

\displaystyle B_{\varepsilon}(Y,Y^{\prime})\subseteq W(g,U,\delta).

To see this, let
\mathbf{X}=p(Y,Y^{\prime})
. By definition,
\mathbf{X}\in U
and since
U
is open, there is an
\eta>0
such that
B_{\eta}(\mathbf{X})\subseteq U
. Let
(\tilde{Y},\tilde{Y}^{\prime})\in B_{\varepsilon}(Y,Y^{\prime})
be arbitrary and
\tilde{\mathbf{X}}=p(\tilde{Y},\tilde{Y}^{\prime})
. If
0<\varepsilon<\eta
, it follows that

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{X}}\in B_{\eta}(\mathbf{X})\subseteq U.

In remains to show that choosing
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small, we can obtain that

\displaystyle{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\tilde{Y},\tilde{Y}^{\prime})-g(p(\tilde{Y},\tilde{Y}^{\prime}))\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\tilde{X},\alpha}<\delta.

Note that

\displaystyle\left|{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\tilde{Y},\tilde{Y}^{\prime})-g(p(\tilde{Y},\tilde{Y}^{\prime}))\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\tilde{X},\alpha}-{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(Y,Y^{\prime})-g(p(Y,Y^{\prime}))\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{X,\alpha}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\

\displaystyle{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(Y,Y^{\prime});(\tilde{Y},\tilde{Y}^{\prime})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\alpha}+{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|g(p(Y,Y^{\prime}));g(p(\tilde{Y},\tilde{Y}^{\prime}))\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\alpha}.

Using again (
6.3
) and the assumption, the right hand side gets small when
\varepsilon
is chosen small. Therefore, for any given
\nu>0
, we can choose
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small to obtain

\displaystyle{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\tilde{Y},\tilde{Y}^{\prime})-g(p(\tilde{Y},\tilde{Y}^{\prime}))\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\tilde{X},\alpha}\leq\nu+{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(Y,Y^{\prime})-g(p(Y,Y^{\prime}))\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{X,\alpha}.

Since
{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(Y,Y^{\prime})-g(p(Y,Y^{\prime}))\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{X,\alpha}<\delta
, we can find a
\nu>0
such that

\displaystyle\nu+{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(Y,Y^{\prime})-g(p(Y,Y^{\prime}))\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{X,\alpha}<\delta.

From these observations, we can deduce the second claim. Both claims together prove that
d^{\flat}_{\alpha,\beta}
indeed metrizes the tube topology. The fact that
\mathscr{D}
is complete with respect to
d^{\flat}_{\alpha,\beta}
follows from completeness of the space
\mathscr{C}^{\beta}
with respect to
\varrho_{\beta}
and completeness of the spaces
\mathscr{D}_{X}^{\alpha,\beta}
. Separability follows from separability of the respective spaces.

∎

We can now prove an important stability result for rough integration.

Theorem 6.15
.

Let
\mathbf{X},\tilde{\mathbf{X}}\in\mathscr{C}^{\beta}
,
(Y,Y^{\prime})\in\mathscr{D}^{\alpha,\beta}_{X}
and
(\tilde{Y},\tilde{Y}^{\prime})\in\mathscr{D}^{\alpha,\beta}_{\tilde{X}}
. Set

\displaystyle Z\coloneqq\int_{0}^{\cdot}Y_{u}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{X}_{u},\quad Z^{\prime}\coloneqq Y

and define
(\tilde{Z},\tilde{Z}^{\prime})
similarly. Then, locally,

\displaystyle d^{\flat}_{\alpha,\beta}((Z,Z^{\prime}),(\tilde{Z},\tilde{Z}^{\prime}))\leq C\,d^{\flat}_{\alpha,\beta}((Y,Y^{\prime}),(\tilde{Y},\tilde{Y}^{\prime})).

In other words: the integration map

\displaystyle(Y,Y^{\prime})\mapsto\left(\int Y\,\mathrm{d}p(Y,Y^{\prime}),Y\right)

is locally Lipschitz continuous.

Proof.

It suffices to establish a bound for
\|R^{Z}-R^{\tilde{Z}}\|_{2\alpha}
. Recall that

\displaystyle R^{Z}_{s,t}=\int_{s}^{t}Y_{u}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{X}_{u}-Y_{s}\delta X_{s,t}=(\mathcal{I}\Xi)_{s,t}-\Xi_{s,t}+Y^{\prime}_{s}\mathbb{X}_{s,t}

where
\Xi_{u,v}=Y_{u}\delta X_{u,v}+Y^{\prime}_{u}\mathbb{X}_{u,v}
and
\mathcal{I}
is the integration map provided by the Sewing lemma. A similar decomposition holds for
R^{\tilde{Z}}_{s,t}
with
\Xi
replaced by
\tilde{\Xi}_{u,v}=\tilde{Y}_{u}\delta\tilde{X}_{u,v}+\tilde{Y}^{\prime}_{u}\tilde{\mathbb{X}}_{u,v}
. Setting
\Psi\coloneqq\Xi-\tilde{\Xi}
, linearity of
\mathcal{I}
yields

\displaystyle|R^{Z}_{s,t}-R^{\tilde{Z}}_{s,t}|\leq|(\mathcal{I}\Psi)_{s,t}-\Psi_{s,t}|+|Y^{\prime}_{s}\mathbb{X}_{s,t}-\tilde{Y}^{\prime}_{s}\tilde{\mathbb{X}}_{s,t}|.

The Sewing lemma gives us the bound

\displaystyle|(\mathcal{I}\Psi)_{s,t}-\Psi_{s,t}|\leq C\|\delta\Psi\|_{3\alpha}|t-s|^{3\alpha}.

We have

\displaystyle\delta\Psi_{s,u,t}=\delta\Xi_{s,u,t}-\delta\tilde{\Xi}_{s,u,t}=R^{\tilde{Y}}_{s,u}\tilde{X}_{u,t}+\delta\tilde{Y}^{\prime}_{s,u}\tilde{\mathbb{X}}_{u,t}-R^{Y}_{s,u}X_{u,t}-\delta Y^{\prime}_{s,u}\mathbb{X}_{u,t}.

Therefore, by using the triangle inequality:

\displaystyle\|\delta\Psi\|_{3\alpha}\leq Cd^{\flat}_{\alpha,\beta}((Y,Y^{\prime}),(\tilde{Y},\tilde{Y}^{\prime})).

The triangle inequality also yields

\displaystyle|Y^{\prime}_{s}\mathbb{X}_{s,t}-\tilde{Y}^{\prime}_{s}\tilde{\mathbb{X}}_{s,t}|\leq C|t-s|^{2\alpha}d^{\flat}_{\alpha,\beta}((Y,Y^{\prime}),(\tilde{Y},\tilde{Y}^{\prime}))

which concludes the proof.
∎

Corollary 6.16
.

For every
g\in\Delta
, the map

\displaystyle\mathbf{X}\to\left\|\int g(X)\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{X}\right\|_{\mathscr{D}^{\alpha,\beta}_{X}}

is continuous.

Proof.

For
\mathbf{X},\tilde{\mathbf{X}}\in\mathscr{C}^{\beta}
, the reverse triangle inequality for Hölder norms gives

\displaystyle\left|\left\|\int g(X)\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{X}\right\|_{\mathscr{D}^{\alpha,\beta}_{X}}-\left\|\int g(\tilde{X})\,\mathrm{d}\tilde{\mathbf{X}}\right\|_{\mathscr{D}^{\alpha,\beta}_{\tilde{X}}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq d^{\flat}_{\alpha,\beta}\left(\int g(X)\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{X},\int g(\tilde{X})\,\mathrm{d}\tilde{\mathbf{X}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\ C\,d^{\flat}_{\alpha,\beta}(g(X),g(\tilde{X}))

locally. Recall that

\displaystyle g(X)=\left(\int\phi\,\mathrm{d}X+\psi,\phi\right)

for some smooth functions
\phi
and
\psi
. Therefore, we can use continuity of the Young integral to see that

\displaystyle d^{\flat}_{\alpha,\beta}(g(X),g(\tilde{X}))\leq C\varrho_{\beta}(\mathbf{X},\tilde{\mathbf{X}})

locally and continuity follows.
∎

7.
Rough differential equations

Having defined the rough integral, we can now say how a general
rough differential equation
should be understood.

Definition 7.1
.

Let
\mathbf{X}\in\mathscr{C}^{\alpha}
,
\frac{1}{3}<\alpha\leq\frac{1}{2}
,
\sigma=(\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{d})
a collection of vector fields
\sigma_{i}\colon\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
. We call
Y\colon[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
a
solution to the rough differential equation
(RDE)

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}Y_{t}

\displaystyle=\sigma(Y_{t})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{X}_{t};\quad t\in[0,T],

\displaystyle Y_{0}

\displaystyle=y,

if and only if
t\mapsto\sigma(Y_{t})
is controlled by
\mathbf{X}
and satisfies the integral equation

(7.1)

\displaystyle Y_{t}=y+\int_{0}^{t}\sigma(Y_{s})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{X}_{s}

where the integral is understood as a rough integral.

Since rough integrals are also controlled paths, any solution
Y
that satisfies (
7.1
) will be controlled by
\mathbf{X}
, too. A natural candidate for a Gubinelli derivative of
Y
is
\sigma(Y)
. We would therefore like to consider the map

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(Y,Y^{\prime}):=\left(y+\int_{0}^{\cdot}\sigma(Y_{s})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{X}_{s},\sigma(Y)\right)

as a map from the space of controlled paths to itself and try show that that it is a contraction on a small time interval. To properly define this map, one has to show that the composition of a controlled path with a sufficiently smooth function
\sigma
is again controlled.

Lemma 7.2
.

Let
\mathbf{X}\in\mathscr{C}^{\alpha}
,
(Y,Y^{\prime})\in\mathscr{D}^{\alpha}_{X}([0,T],W)
and let
\varphi\colon W\to\bar{W}
be twice continuously differentiable. Then the path
t\mapsto\varphi(Y_{t})
is again controlled by
X
with a Gubinelli derivative given by
\varphi(Y)^{\prime}_{t}=D\varphi(Y_{t})Y^{\prime}_{t}
. Moreover, if
\varphi
is bounded with bounded derivatives, the estimate

\displaystyle\|\varphi(Y),\varphi(Y)^{\prime}\|_{X,\alpha}\leq C(\|Y\|_{\alpha}+\|Y\|^{2}_{\alpha}+\|Y,Y^{\prime}\|_{X,\alpha})

holds where
C
depends on
\|\varphi\|_{\mathcal{C}^{2}}
.

Proof.

It suffices to consider the case of
\sigma
being bounded with bounded derivatives, the general case follows by localization. We have

\displaystyle\|\varphi(Y)\|_{\alpha}\leq\|D\sigma\|_{\infty}\|Y\|_{\alpha}

and

\displaystyle\|\varphi(Y)^{\prime}\|_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\|D\varphi(Y)Y^{\prime}\|_{\alpha}\leq\|D\varphi(Y)\|_{\alpha}\|Y^{\prime}\|_{\infty}+\|D\varphi(Y)\|_{\infty}\|Y^{\prime}\|_{\alpha}

\displaystyle\leq\|D^{2}\sigma\|_{\infty}\|Y\|_{\alpha}+\|D\sigma\|_{\infty}\|Y^{\prime}\|_{\alpha}.

This shows that
\varphi(Y),\varphi(Y)^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}^{\alpha}
. We have to prove that

\displaystyle R^{\varphi}_{s,t}

\displaystyle\coloneqq R^{\varphi(Y)}_{s,t}\coloneqq\delta\varphi(Y)_{s,t}-\sigma(Y)^{\prime}_{s}\delta X_{s,t}

\displaystyle=\delta\varphi(Y)_{s,t}-D\varphi(Y_{s})Y^{\prime}_{s}\delta X_{s,t}

is
2\alpha
-Hölder. Since

\displaystyle R^{\varphi}_{s,t}=\varphi(Y_{t})-\varphi(Y_{s})-D\varphi(Y_{s})\delta Y_{s,t}+D\varphi(Y_{s})R^{Y}_{s,t},

Taylor’s theorem yields the bound

\displaystyle\|R^{\varphi}\|_{2\alpha}\leq\frac{1}{2}\|D^{2}\varphi\|_{\infty}\|Y\|_{\alpha}^{2}+\|D\varphi\|_{\infty}\|R^{Y}\|_{2\alpha},

which shows that indeed
(\varphi(Y),\varphi(Y)^{\prime})
is controlled by
X
and the desired bound.
∎

Next, we formulate the main theorem about the non-linear rough differential equations.

Theorem 7.3
.

Let
\mathbf{X}\in\mathscr{C}^{\alpha}([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{d})
for
\frac{1}{3}<\alpha\leq\frac{1}{2}
,
y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
\sigma\in\mathcal{C}^{3}(\mathbb{R}^{m},L(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{m}))
. Then there exists a unique controlled path
(Y,Y^{\prime})\in\mathscr{D}^{\alpha}_{X}([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{m})
with
Y^{\prime}=\sigma(Y)
that satisfies

\displaystyle Y_{t}=y+\int_{0}^{t}\sigma(Y_{s})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{X}_{s};\quad t\in[0,T].

Proof.

The proof is very similar to the one we gave in Theorem
3.5
, i.e. we will show that a properly defined mapping has a fixed point. For
(Y,Y^{\prime})\in\mathscr{D}^{\alpha}_{X}
and
0<T_{0}\leq T
, set

\displaystyle(Z_{t},Z^{\prime}_{t})\coloneqq(\sigma(Y_{t}),D\sigma(Y_{t})Y^{\prime}_{t})\in\mathscr{D}^{\alpha}_{X};\quad t\in[0,T_{0}].

We define the map

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(Y,Y^{\prime}):=\left(y+\int_{0}^{t}Z_{s}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{X}_{s},Z_{t};\ t\in[0,T_{0}]\right).

The expected solution will be a fixed point of this map. We will not define this map on the whole space of controlled paths but on the closed unit ball

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{T_{0}}\coloneqq\left\{(Y,Y^{\prime})\in\mathscr{D}^{\alpha}_{X}\,:\,Y_{0}=y,Y^{\prime}_{0}=\sigma(y),\|Y,Y^{\prime}\|_{X,\alpha}\leq 1\right\}

of controlled paths starting in
(y,\sigma(y))
. We will have to prove two things:

(1)

\mathcal{M}
leaves
\mathcal{B}_{T_{0}}
invariant, i.e.
\mathcal{M}\colon\mathcal{B}_{T_{0}}\to\mathcal{B}_{T_{0}}
is a well defined map,

(2)

\mathcal{M}
is a contraction.

We start with the first point. Clearly,
\mathcal{M}(Y,Y^{\prime})_{0}=(y,\sigma(y))
. To prove that
\|Y,Y^{\prime}\|_{X,\alpha}\leq 1
, we use the estimate for the rough integral given in Theorem
6.1
:

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{M}\|_{X,\alpha}

\displaystyle=\|\int_{0}^{\cdot}Z_{s}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{X}_{s},Z\|_{X,\alpha}

\displaystyle\leq\|Z\|_{\alpha}+\|Z^{\prime}\|_{\alpha}\|\mathbb{X}\|_{2\alpha}+CT_{0}^{\alpha}(\|X\|_{\alpha}\|R^{Z}\|_{2\alpha}+\|\mathbb{X}\|_{2\alpha}\|Z^{\prime}\|_{\alpha})

\displaystyle\leq\|Z\|_{\alpha}+\|Z,Z^{\prime}\|_{X,\alpha}\|\mathbb{X}\|_{2\alpha}+CT^{\alpha}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\mathbf{X}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\alpha}\|Z,Z^{\prime}\|_{X,\alpha}.

We have
\|Z\|_{\alpha}\leq C\|Y\|_{\alpha}
and

\displaystyle\|Y\|_{\alpha}

\displaystyle\leq\|Y^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\|X\|_{\alpha}+T_{0}^{\alpha}\|R^{Y}\|_{2\alpha}

\displaystyle\leq|Y^{\prime}_{0}|\|X\|_{\alpha}+T_{0}^{\alpha}\|Y^{\prime}\|_{\alpha}\|X\|_{\alpha}+T^{\alpha}\|R^{Y}\|_{2\alpha}

\displaystyle\leq C\|X\|_{\alpha}+T^{\alpha}_{0}(1+\|X\|_{\alpha})\|Y,Y^{\prime}\|_{X,\alpha}

\displaystyle\leq C\|X\|_{\alpha}+T^{\alpha}_{0}(1+\|X\|_{\alpha}).

To estimate
\|Z,Z^{\prime}\|_{X,\alpha}
, we use Lemma
7.2
:

\displaystyle\|Z,Z^{\prime}\|_{X,\alpha}

\displaystyle\leq C(\|Y\|_{\alpha}+\|Y\|^{2}_{\alpha}+\|Y,Y^{\prime}\|_{X,\alpha})

\displaystyle\leq C(1+\|Y\|_{\alpha}+\|Y\|^{2}_{\alpha}).

Note that we already estimated
\|Y\|_{\alpha}
above. To summarize, we see that
\|\mathcal{M}\|_{X,\alpha}
gets small if
T_{0}
and
{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\mathbf{X}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\alpha}
are getting small. As in the proof of Theorem
3.5
, we will therefore assume first that
\mathbf{X}
is smoother than only being
\alpha
-Hölder continuous to assure that
{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\mathbf{X}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\alpha}
gets small as
T_{0}\to 0
. In total, we can thus guarantee that
\mathcal{M}
leaves
\mathcal{B}_{T_{0}}
invariant for a sufficiently small
T_{0}>0
. It remains to prove that
\mathcal{M}
is a contraction on
\mathcal{B}_{T_{0}}
. To do this, we have to estimate the difference between two rough integrals in the
{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\cdot\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{X,\alpha}
-norm. Note that we do not have to use Theorem
6.15
since the driving rough path
\mathbf{X}
is fixed. The complete proof for the contraction property is a bit long, but does not provide many new insights, that is why we will not present it here. It can be found in
[
FH20
, Theorem 8.3.]
.
∎

There is also a stability result for solutions to rough differential equations that we want to cite here. To formulate it, we define the metric

\displaystyle d^{\flat}_{\alpha}((Y,Y^{\prime}),(\tilde{Y},\tilde{Y}^{\prime}))

\displaystyle\coloneqq d^{\flat}_{\alpha,\alpha}((Y,Y^{\prime}),(\tilde{Y},\tilde{Y}^{\prime}))

\displaystyle\coloneqq\varrho_{\alpha}(\mathbf{X},\tilde{\mathbf{X}})+\|Y^{\prime}-\tilde{Y}^{\prime}\|_{\alpha}+\|R^{Y}-R^{\tilde{Y}}\|_{2\alpha}

\displaystyle\quad+|Y_{0}-\tilde{Y}_{0}|+|Y^{\prime}_{0}-\tilde{Y}^{\prime}_{0}|

for
(Y,Y^{\prime})\in\mathscr{D}_{X}^{\alpha}
and
(\tilde{Y},\tilde{Y}^{\prime})\in\mathscr{D}_{\tilde{X}}^{\alpha}
which is a metric on the total space
\mathscr{D}=\sqcup_{\mathbf{X}\in\mathscr{C}^{\alpha}}\mathscr{D}_{X}^{\alpha}
.

Theorem 7.4

(Stability of RDE solutions)
.

Let
(Y,Y^{\prime})
and
(\tilde{Y},\tilde{Y}^{\prime})
be solutions to

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}Y_{t}=\sigma(Y_{t})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{X}_{t};\ Y_{0}=y\quad\text{resp.}\quad\mathrm{d}\tilde{Y}_{t}=\sigma(\tilde{Y}_{t})\,\mathrm{d}\tilde{\mathbf{X}}_{t};\ \tilde{Y}_{0}=\tilde{y}

with
Y^{\prime}=\sigma(Y)
and
\tilde{Y}^{\prime}=\sigma(\tilde{Y})
. Then

\displaystyle d^{\flat}_{\alpha}((Y,Y^{\prime}),(\tilde{Y},\tilde{Y}^{\prime}))\leq C(|y-\tilde{y}|+\varrho_{\alpha}(\mathbf{X},\tilde{\mathbf{X}}))

locally.

Proof.

[
FH20
, Theorem 8.5]
. ∎

7.1.
Rough differential equations driven by a Brownian motion

In this part, we discuss how to employ rough theory in stochastic analysis. Let us start with the following proposition which, loosely speaking, claims that Itō (resp. Stratonovich) integration coincides with rough integration against the enhanced Itō (resp. Stratonovich) Brownian motion.

Proposition 7.5
.

Let
B=(B^{1},\ldots,B^{d})
be a
d
-dimensional Brownian motion and
\mathbf{B}^{\text{It\={o}}}
resp.
\mathbf{B}^{\text{Strat}}
its Itō resp. Stratonovich lift to a rough paths valued process. For
\frac{1}{3}<\alpha<\frac{1}{2}
, assume that
(Y(\omega),Y^{\prime}(\omega))\in\mathscr{D}^{\alpha}_{X(\omega)}
almost surely and that
(Y,Y^{\prime})
is adapted to the filtration generated by
B
. Then

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}Y_{s}\,\mathrm{d}B_{s}=\int_{0}^{T}Y_{s}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{B}^{\text{It\={o}}}_{s}\quad\text{and}\quad\int_{0}^{T}Y_{s}\,\circ\mathrm{d}B_{s}=\int_{0}^{T}Y_{s}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{B}^{\text{Strat}}_{s}

almost surely.

Proof.

We will only prove the Itō-case, the identity for Stratonovich integral can be found in
[
FH20
, Corollary 5.2]
. It is known that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}Y_{s}\,\mathrm{d}B_{s}=\lim_{|\mathcal{P}|\to 0}\sum_{[u,v]\in\mathcal{P}}Y_{u}\delta B_{u,v}

in probability. Passing to a subsequence, we may assume that there is a sequence of partitions such that the convergence holds almost surely. It suffices to prove that

\displaystyle\lim_{|\mathcal{P}|\to 0}\sum_{[u,v]\in\mathcal{P}}Y^{\prime}_{u}\mathbb{B}^{\text{It\={o}}}_{u,v}=0

in
L^{2}(\Omega)
. We will assume that
\|Y^{\prime}(\omega)\|\leq M
almost surely, the general case follows by a stopping argument. Fix a partition
\mathcal{P}=\{0=\tau_{0}<\ldots\tau_{N}=T\}
. One can check that
(S_{k})
with
S_{0}=0
and
S_{k+1}-S_{k}=Y^{\prime}_{\tau_{k}}\mathbb{B}^{\text{It\={o}}}_{\tau_{k+1},\tau_{k}}
is a discrete martingale. Since its increments are uncorrelated,

\displaystyle\left\|\sum_{[u,v]\in\mathcal{P}}Y^{\prime}_{u}\mathbb{B}^{\text{It\={o}}}_{u,v}\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=\sum_{[u,v]\in\mathcal{P}}\left\|Y^{\prime}_{u}\mathbb{B}^{\text{It\={o}}}_{u,v}\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq M\sum_{[u,v]\in\mathcal{P}}\left\|\mathbb{B}^{\text{It\={o}}}_{u,v}\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=\mathcal{O}(|\mathcal{P}|)

and the claim follows.
∎

Corollary 7.6
.

For
\sigma\in\mathcal{C}^{3}(\mathbb{R}^{m},L(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{m}))
, the solutions to

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}Y_{t}=\sigma(Y_{t})\,\mathrm{d}B_{t}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathrm{d}Y_{t}=\sigma(Y_{t})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{B_{t}}^{\text{It\={o}}}

resp.

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}Y_{t}=\sigma(Y_{t})\,\circ\mathrm{d}B_{t}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathrm{d}Y_{t}=\sigma(Y_{t})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{B_{t}}^{\text{Strat}}

with the same initial conditions coincide almost surely.

We can now prove an important theorem in stochastic analysis, the
Wong-Zakai theorem
, that connects stochastic differential equations to random ordinary differential equations.

Theorem 7.7
.

Let
\sigma\in\mathcal{C}^{3}(\mathbb{R}^{m},L(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{m}))
,
y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
B=(B^{1},\ldots,B^{d})
be a Brownian motion defined on
[0,1]
and
B(n)
and be its piecewise-linear approximation at the the dyadic points
0<2^{-n}<\ldots<(2^{n}-1)2^{-n}<1
. Then the solutions
Y(n)
to the random ordinary differential equations

(7.2)

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}Y_{t}(n)=\sigma(Y_{t}(n))\,\mathrm{d}B_{t}(n);\quad Y_{0}(n)=y

converge in the
\alpha
-Hölder metric for any
\frac{1}{3}<\alpha<\frac{1}{2}
to the solution of the Stratonovich stochastic differential equation

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}Y_{t}=\sigma(Y_{t})\,\circ\mathrm{d}B_{t};\quad Y_{0}=y

almost surely as
n\to\infty
.

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{B}(n)
be the canonical lift of
B(n)
to an
\alpha
-Hölder rough path. Then the solutions
Y(n)
to the random ordinary differential equations (
7.2
) coincide with the solutions to the rough differential equations

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}Y_{t}(n)=\sigma(Y_{t}(n))\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{B}_{t}(n);\quad Y_{0}(n)=y.

From Corollary
7.6
, the solution
Y
of the Stratonovich stochastic differential equation coincides almost surely with the solution to the random rough differential equation

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}Y_{t}=\sigma(Y_{t})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{B}^{\text{Strat}}_{t};\quad Y_{0}=y.

In the proof of Proposition
5.5
, we have seen that
\varrho_{\alpha}(\mathbf{B}(n),\mathbf{B}^{\text{Strat}})\to 0
as
n\to\infty
. From the stability result on RDE solutions (Theorem
7.4
), it follows that

\displaystyle d^{\flat}_{\alpha}((Y,Y^{\prime}),(Y(n),Y^{\prime}(n)))\to 0

almost surely as
n\to\infty
. In particular,
Y(n)\to Y
almost surely as
n\to\infty
in the
\alpha
-Hölder metric.

∎

7.2.
Rough differential equations driven by a fractional Brownian motion.

We will come back now to our motivating problem, i.e. the question of how to define a meaningful solution to a stochastic differential equation driven by a fractional Brownian motion
B^{H}
. For
H>\frac{1}{2}
, we can use Young’s integration theory to solve such equations. In the case
H=\frac{1}{2}
, we can either use Itō’s theory of stochastic integration or rough paths theory as we saw in the previous section. What about
H<\frac{1}{2}
? It turns out that a similar result as seen in the proof of Proposition
5.5
holds for the fractional Brownian motion, too, provided
H>\frac{1}{4}
. To formulate it, let
B^{H}(n)
denote a piecewise-linear approximation of
B^{H}
. Since
B^{H}(n)
has smooth sample paths, the canonical lift
\mathbf{B}^{H}(n)
to an
\alpha
-Hölder rough path exists. With much more involved arguments as in Proposition
5.5
(cf.
[
CQ02
,
FV10a
]
), it can be shown that
(\mathbf{B}^{H}(n))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a Cauchy sequence almost surely in the space of geometric
\alpha
-Hölder rough paths for
\alpha<H
. Since the space of geometric rough paths is complete, the sequence converges to a limit
\mathbf{B}^{H}
which is then called the
natural lift
of the fractional Brownian motion. This result allows to study stochastic equations driven by a fractional Brownian motion with a Hurst parameter
H>\frac{1}{4}
. There are many works in which such equations are studied, the interested reader is referred to
[
FH20
, Chapter 10]
and the comments at the end of this chapter.

A natural question is whether there is a meaningful lift in the case of
H\leq\frac{1}{4}
, too. In
[
CQ02
]
, it is shown that the approach we just described here does not work for
H\leq\frac{1}{4}
because the natural lifts
\mathbf{B}^{H}(n)
will diverge in this case. To the authors’ knowledge, it is currently not clear whether a meaningful rough path lift can be defined in the regime
H\in(0,1/4]
.

8.
Discussion and Outlook

In these notes, we gave a brief introduction to the theory of rough paths. We emphasized its application in stochastic analysis, discussing, in particular, its ability to solve stochastic differential equations driven by a fractional Brownian motion.

Rough path theory is nowadays a mature theory that found many applications in various fields of mathematics. At the end of these notes, we would like to discuss further branches of research in which rough paths theory plays a role. We are aware that the choice of topics we present here reflects our personal interests, and there are many important subjects we are not going to discuss here. In particular, we want to repeat that we will not touch the numerous applications of rough paths theory in the context of stochastic partial differential equation, a topic far beyond the scope of these notes.

•

Gaussian rough paths and rough differential equations driven by Gaussian signals were studied extensively. The foundations were laid in the articles
[
CQ02
,
FV10a
,
FGGR16
]
, cf. also
[
FV10b
, Chapter 15]
and
[
FH20
, Chapter 10]
. The continuity of the solution map, cf. Theorem
7.4
, allows to give an easy proof for the Freidlin-Wentzell large deviation principle and the Stroock-Varadhan support theorem
[
LQZ02
]
. These theorems have natural extensions to stochastic differential equations driven by Gaussian rough paths, too
[
FV10b
, Chapter 19]
.

•

A famous theorem from Hörmander characterizes second order hypoelliptic differential operators by stating a condition on the iterated Lie brackets of the involved vector fields
[
Hör67
]
. This result has an equivalent formulation in terms of stochastic differential equations: Hörmander’s theorem says that if the vector fields of an SDE driven by a Brownian motion satisfy the bracket condition, the solution to the SDE obtains a smooth density at every time point
t>0
. In
[
Mal78
]
, Malliavin gave a proof of Hörmander’s theorem using a form of stochastic analysis on the Wiener space. Today, this calculus is called
Malliavin calculus
. One core idea of Malliavin was to prove that the solution map to a stochastic differential equation is differentiable in certain directions of the noise. It turns out that the solution map of a rough differential equation enjoys a similar regularity
[
CFV09
]
. This motivated the study of Hörmander’s theorem in the context of rough differential equations driven by Gaussian rough paths. In a series of papers, it was shown that Hörmander’s bracket condition is indeed sufficient for the solution to a rough differential equations driven by a Gaussian process to admit a smooth density
[
CF10
,
CLL13
,
FR13
,
CHLT15
]
. This density was further investigated in
[
BOT14
,
BOZ15
,
Ina16
,
BNOT16
,
GOT20
,
IN21
,
GOT23
,
GOT22
]

•

In stochastic analysis, the Markov property ususally plays an important role. Different aspects for rough paths valued stochastic processes having the Markov property, known as
Markovian rough paths
, were studied in
[
FV08
,
Lej06
,
Lej08
,
CO17
,
CO18
,
Che18
]
.

•

In the classical texts about rough paths theory, one usually considers continuous paths exclusively. However, rough paths theory can be generalized to non-continuous paths, too, and is able to study stochastic processes with càdlàg sample paths such as Lévy processes or general semimartingales, cf.
[
FS13
,
FS17
,
FZ18
,
LP18
,
CF19
]
.

•

Solving rough differential equations numerically can be a challenging problem. A natural numerical scheme to solve a rough differential equation can be deduced from the (formal) Taylor expansion of the solution, cf.
[
Dav07
]
and
[
FV10b
, Chapter 10]
. These schemes usually contain iterated integrals of, at least, order 2. Since these integrals are notoriously difficult to simulate, in particular if the driving signal is not a Brownian motion, several alternatives were studied. For instance, the
simplified
or
implementable Milstein scheme
replaces the iterated integral by a product of increments, cf.
[
DNT12
,
FR14
]
. If this scheme is used in combination with Monte Carlo simulations, a complexity reduction can be obtained by using a multilevel Monte Carlo method, cf.
[
BFRS16
]
. General Runge-Kutta schemes were studied in
[
RR22
]
. Many articles study numerical schemes that are specifically designed to solve rough differential equations driven by a fractional Brownian motion and use some probabilistic properties of this process, cf.
[
Nag15
]
for the (implicit) Crank-Nicolson scheme or
[
LT19
]
for a first order Euler scheme with deterministic correction term.

•

Expanding the solution to an ordinary differential equation leads to a so-called
B-series
. The B-series expansion of a rough differential equation motivates the notion of a
branched rough path
that was introduced by Gubinelli in
[
Gub10
]
. A branched rough path does not only contain iterated integrals, but also integrated products of iterated integrals. The difference to a geometric rough path is that for branched rough paths, no product rule is assumed, i.e. the shuffle property in Corollary
5.10
does not hold for branched rough paths. For example, the product iterated integrals of the Brownian motion in Itō-sense constitute a branched rough path, but not a geometric one. It turns out that there is a kind of embedding of the space of branched rough paths into a larger space of geometric rough paths, cf.
[
HK15
,
BC19
]
. The geometry of branched rough paths was further studied in
[
TZ20
]
. It turns out that the space of branched rough paths also form a continuous field of Banach spaces seen in Section
6.1
, cf.
[
GVRST22
]
.

•

Studying the long-time behaviour of the solution to a rough differential equation is a natural problem. However, if the solution is non-Markovian, well established strategies fail. We would like to mention two approaches here that do not rely on the Markov property and were quite successful in this context. The first one was invented by Hairer to study ergodicity of stochastic differential equations driven by a fractional Brownian motion
[
Hai05
,
HO07
,
HP11
,
HP13
]
. Hairer defines a structure that he calls
stochastic dynamical system
(SDS) to study these equations. An SDS has certain similarities to L. Arnold’s notion of a
random dynamical system

[
Arn98
]
(see below), but it is closer to the classical Markovian framework. In Hairer’s theory, invariant measures can be similarly defined as for classical Markov processes. Existence and uniqueness of these measures can be proven with techniques (e.g. the coupling method) that are well-known in the Markovian world. Other researchers adopted this framework and studied, for instance, the convergence rate towards the equilibrium
[
FP17
,
DPT19
]
or used it to study an estimator for the drift coefficient in an equation driven by a fractional Brownian motion
[
PTV20
]
. Another approach is to study the random dynamical system (RDS) in the sense of L. Arnold
[
Arn98
]
that is generated by a stochastic differential equation. A rough differential equations generates an RDS whenever the driving rough paths valued process has stationary increments
[
BRS17
]
. This is the case, for instance, for the fractional Brownian motion. In the theory of RDS, different objects can be defined that describe the long time behaviour of the solution to a rough differential equation. For example, one can study random attractors
[
Duc22
]
, random center manifolds
[
NK21
]
or random stable and unstable manifolds for rough delay equations
[
GVRS22
,
GVR21
]
.

•

As we already mentioned in Remark
5.11
, the signature of a rough path is an important object that is still studied a lot. One interesting problem is to find an algorithm that reconstructs the path from a given signature effectively. This question was discussed e.g. in
[
LX18
,
LX17
,
CDNX17
,
Gen17
]
. Due to its generalilty, the signature also plays a role in model-free mathematical finance, cf.
[
LNPA19
,
LNPA20
,
KLA20
,
BHRS23
,
CGSF23
]
. We already mentioned that the signature is an important object in machine learning and time series analysis, but we are unable to summarize the corresponding vast literature in these notes. Instead, we refer the reader to the overview articles
[
CK16
,
LM22
]
.

•

We saw in these lecture notes that the Sewing lemma (Lemma
3.2
) is one of the cornerstones in rough paths theory. In the work
[
Lê20
]
, Lê proves a stochastic version of it that he called
Stochastic sewing lemma
, see also
[
FH20
, Section 4.6]
. With the Stochastic sewing lemma, it is possible to prove that certain Riemann-type sums involving random variables converge to a limit in a stochastic sense, taking into account stochastic cancellations. For instance, it is well-known that the Itō-integral that integrates an adapted process with respect to a Brownian motion can be approximated by Riemann sums in probability, but this fact cannot be proven with the classical Sewing lemma that only looks at the regularity of the sample paths and neglects the probabilistic structure. With the Stochastic sewing lemma, however, this is possible. The Stochastic sewing lemma proved to be a very helpful tool and could be used in various settings, e.g. in the context of the regularization by noise phenomenon
[
HP21
,
HL22
,
Ger23
]
or for the analysis of numerical methods for singular SDEs
[
BDG21
,
BDG23
,
DGL23
]
.
