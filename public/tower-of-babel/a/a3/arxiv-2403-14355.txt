Toya Kumagai, Tomohiro Okuma

Abstract
. Let V V be an affine algebraic variety, and let p ∈ V p\in V be a singular point. For a regular function g g on V V such that g ⁡ ( p ) = 0 g(p)=0 and for a positive integer n n , we consider the cyclic covering ϕ n : V n → V \phi_{n}\colon V_{n}\to V of degree n n branched along the hypersurface defined by g g . We will prove that for sufficiently large n n , the tangent cone of V n V_{n} at ϕ n − 1 ​ ( p ) \phi_{n}^{-1}(p) is, as an affine variety, the product of the tangent cone of the branch locus and the affine line. In particular, the multiplicity of the singularity ϕ n − 1 ​ ( p ) ∈ V n \phi_{n}^{-1}(p)\in V_{n} , which is a function of n n determined by V V and g g , remains constant for sufficiently large n n . This result generalizes Tomaru’s theorem for normal surface singularities.

1.
Introduction

Let
V
be an affine algebraic variety over an algebraically closed field
K
and let
p\in V
be a singular point.
Then we call the pair
(V,p)
a singularity.
We denote by
A
the local ring
\mathcal{O}_{V,p}
of the variety
V
at
p
, which is called the local ring of the singularity
(V,p)
.
The
multiplicity

\mult(V,p)
of the singularity
(V,p)
is defined as the (Hilbert-Samuel) multiplicity of the local ring
A
(see
[
3
, §14]
).
The multiplicity is a fundamental invariant playing a crucial role in singularity theory.
Let
\mathfrak{m}
denote the maximal ideal of
A
, and let
d=\dim(V,p):=\dim A
.
If
\{f_{1},\dots,f_{d}\}\subset A
generates an
\mathfrak{m}
-primary ideal, then it defines a local finite morphism
(V,p)\to(\mathbb{A}_{K}^{d},o)
(cf.
[
3
, Theorem 14.5]
). From a geometric point of view,
\mult(V,p)
can be interpreted as the minimum of the degree of such morphisms (cf.
[
2
, Exercises 6.3.6]
,
[
3
, Theorems 14.13, 14.14]
and
[
8
, Ch. VIII, §10, Corollary 2]
).

In this paper, we investigate the multiplicity of singularities on branched cyclic coverings of a given singularity.
Let
g
be a non-zero regular function on
V
such that
g(p)=0
, and let
y
be the coordinate function of the affine line
\mathbb{A}^{1}_{K}
.
For
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
, the natural projection
\phi_{n}\colon V_{n}:=\{y^{n}=g\}\subset V\times\mathbb{A}^{1}_{K}\to V
defines a cyclic covering of degree
n
branched along the hypersurface
\{g=0\}\subset V
. Clearly, the fiber
\phi_{n}^{-1}(p)
consists of the point
p_{n}:=p\times\{0\}\in V_{n}
.
From the geometric perspective, we have the inequality
\mult(V_{n},p_{n})\leq n\cdot\mult(V,p)
.
We will show the following.

Theorem 1.1
.

There exists a positive integer
N
such that the function
\mult(V_{n},p_{n})
of
n
is constant for
n>N
.

This result is motivated by Tomaru’s result
[
7
]
which proves the theorem above for normal complex analytic singularities of dimension two.
Tomaru used the fact that if
M
represents the maximal ideal cycle on a resolution with nice property, then the multiplicity coincides with
-M^{2}
.
To prove the theorem, he constructed a branched cyclic covering
\psi_{n}\colon\tilde{V}_{n}\to\tilde{V}
of resolution spaces of
V_{n}
and
V
, and computed the maximal ideal cycle on
\tilde{V}_{n}
from data of
\tilde{V}
via
\psi_{n}
.
While this method is effective for analyzing the maximal ideal cycles, it becomes somewhat intricate when we focus just on the function
\mult(V_{n},p_{n})
.
In this paper, we adopt the standard bases of ideals of the localization of the polynomial rings, and prove a stronger result as follows (see Theorem
3.1
and Remark
3.4
).

Theorem 1.2
.

There exists a positive integer
N
, which is determined by a standard basis of an ideal defining the singularity
(V,p)
and the regular function
g
, such that the affine tangent cone of
(V_{n},p_{n})
is isomorphic to the product of the affine tangent cone of the subscheme of
V
defined by
g
at
p
and the affine line
\mathbb{A}^{1}_{K}
.

For these results, we do not make assumptions about the Cohen-Macaulayness or normality of the singularity.
Since the multiplicity is determined by the tangent cone, Theorem
1.2
implies Theorem
1.1
. The main theorem may also reveal which invariants of singularities are not determined (or bounded) by the invariants of their tangent cones (see Remark
3.5
).

In Section
2
, we provide a brief overview of the algorithm and criterion for the standard basis and the description of the tangent cones, both of which are crucial for the proof of the main theorem in Section
3
.

2.
Standard bases and tangent cones

In this section, we introduce some notation and provide a brief description of the standard bases, as well as the tangent cone of a local ring in terms of standard bases.
A standard basis is called a Gröbner basis if the monomial ordering is global.
We refer to
[
2
]
for basics about the standard basis and related results.
In the following, we denote an ideal generated by
F=\{f_{1},\dots,f_{m}\}
as
\langle F\rangle
or
\langle f_{1},\dots,f_{m}\rangle
.

Let
K
be an algebraically closed field and let
K[x]=K[x_{1},\dots,x_{r}]
denote the polynomial ring in
r
variables over
K
.
Let
R=K[x]_{\langle x\rangle}
denote the localization of
K[x]
with respect to the maximal ideal
\langle x\rangle=\langle x_{1},\dots,x_{r}\rangle
.
We may regard
R
as a subring of the power series ring
K[[x]]
in a natural way.
A monomial
\prod_{i=1}^{r}x_{i}^{\alpha_{i}}\in K[x]
is denoted by
x^{\alpha}
, where
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{r})\in(\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0})^{r}
,
and the degree of
x^{\alpha}
is defined to be
\deg x^{\alpha}:=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\alpha_{i}
.
Let
\mon(x)=\mon(x_{1},\dots,x_{r})
denote the semigroup of monomials, namely,
\mon(x)={\left\{\left.x^{\alpha}\,\right|\,\alpha\in(\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0})^{r}\right\}}
.
A total ordering
>
on
\mon(x)
is called a
monomial ordering
if
x^{\alpha}>x^{\beta}
implies
x^{\alpha}x^{\gamma}>x^{\beta}x^{\gamma}
for all
x^{\alpha},x^{\beta},x^{\gamma}\in\mon(x)
. A monomial ordering
>
is said to be
global
(resp.
local
)
if
x^{\alpha}>1
(resp.
x^{\alpha}<1
) for all
x^{\alpha}\in\mon(x)\setminus\{1\}
.
If
\deg x^{\alpha}<\deg x^{\beta}
implies
x^{\alpha}>x^{\beta}
, the ordering
>
is called a
local degree ordering
.
Local orderings play a crucial role in the analysis of ideals in the local ring
R
.
As an example, we introduce the
negative degree reverse lexicographical ordering

>_{ds}
, which is defined as follows:

\displaystyle x^{\alpha}>_{ds}x^{\beta}\overset{\text{def}}{\Longleftrightarrow}

\displaystyle\deg x^{\alpha}<\deg x^{\beta},\quad\text{or}

\displaystyle\deg x^{\alpha}=\deg x^{\beta}\ \text{and there exists $i$ such that}

\displaystyle\quad\alpha_{n}=\beta_{n},\dots,\alpha_{i+1}=\beta_{i+1},\alpha_{i}<\beta_{i}.

In the following, we fix a local degree ordering
>
on
\mon(x)
.

Let
R^{*}
denote the set of units of
R
.
Let
f\in R\setminus\{0\}
.
Then there is
u\in R^{*}\cap K[x]
with
u(0)=1
such that
uf\in K[x]
.
Let us write it as

uf=\sum_{\alpha}a_{\alpha}x^{\alpha}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}x^{\alpha(i)},\quad a_{i}\neq 0\ \ \text{for $1\leq i\leq n$},\quad x^{\alpha(1)}>x^{\alpha(2)}>\dots

Then we define:

•

\lm(f):=x^{\alpha(1)}
, the
leading monomial
of
f
,

•

\lt(f):=a_{1}x^{\alpha(1)}
, the
leading term
of
f
,

•

\lc(f):=a_{1}
, the
leading coefficient
of
f
,

•

\ord(f):=\min{\left\{\left.\deg x^{\alpha}\,\right|\,a_{\alpha}\neq 0\right\}}=\deg x^{\alpha(1)}
, the
order
of
f
,

•

\displaystyle\In(f):=\sum_{\deg x^{\alpha}=\ord(f)}a_{\alpha}x^{\alpha}
, the
initial form
of
f
.
Let
\In(0)=0
.

Obviously, for
f\in K[x]\setminus\{0\}
,
\lm(f)
appears in
\In(f)
.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
f,g\in R\setminus\{0\}
, and write
\lm(f)=x^{\alpha}
and
\lm(g)=x^{\beta}
.
Let

\gamma=\lcm(\alpha,\beta):=(\max(\alpha_{1},\beta_{1}),\dots,\max(\alpha_{r},\beta_{r})).

Then the
s
-polynomial
of
f
and
g
is defined to be

\spo(f,g)=x^{\gamma-\alpha}f-\frac{\lc(f)}{\lc(g)}x^{\gamma-\beta}g.

Clearly,
\spo(f,g)\in K[x]
if
f,g\in K[x]
.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
I\subset R
be an ideal and
G\subset R
a subset.

(1)

The ideal

L(G):=\left\langle\lm(g)\mid g\in G\setminus\{0\}\right\rangle\subset K[x]

is called the
leading ideal
of
G
.

(2)

If
G
is a finite subset of
I
and
L(I)=L(G)
, then
G
is called a
standard basis
of
I
.

(3)

The
initial ideal
of
I
is defined to be

\In(I)=\left\langle\In(f)\mid f\in I\setminus\{0\}\right\rangle\subset K[x].

Proposition 2.3

(cf.
[
2
, 5.5.11–5.5.12]
)
.

Let
I\subset\langle x\rangle\subset K[x]
be an ideal, and let
A=R/IR
and
\mathfrak{m}\subset A
the maximal ideal of
A
.

(1)

If
\{f_{1},\dots,f_{s}\}
is a standard basis of
I
, then
\In(I)=\langle\In(f_{1}),\dots,\In(f_{s})\rangle
.

(2)

The tangent cone
\gr_{\mathfrak{m}}(A)=\bigoplus_{n\geq 0}\mathfrak{m}^{n}/\mathfrak{m}^{n+1}
is isomorphic to
K[x]/\In(I)
as graded
K
-algebra.

In particular, the multiplicity of the local ring
A
is determined by the initial forms of a standard basis of
I
.

We provide a very brief overview of the criterion for the standard bases and an algorithms to compute them with respect to a local ordering. These are essentially Buchberger’s algorithms but have been generalized by Mora (
[
4
]
).

Definition 2.4

(cf.
[
2
, 1.6.4–1.6.5]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
denote the set of all finite subsets of
R
.
A map

\nf\colon R\times\mathcal{G}\to R,\quad(f,G)\mapsto\nf(f\mid G),

is called a
weak normal form
on
R
if the following are satisfied:

(1)

For all
G\in\mathcal{G}
,
\nf(0\mid G)=0
.

(2)

For all
f\in R
and
G\in\mathcal{G}
,
\nf(f\mid G)\neq 0
implies
\lm(\nf(f\mid G))\not\in L(G)
.

(3)

For all
f\in R
and
G=\{g_{1},\dots,g_{s}\}\in\mathcal{G}
, there exists a unit
u\in R^{*}
such that
uf-\nf(f\mid G)
(or, by abuse of notation,
uf
) has a standard representation with respect to
\nf(-\mid G)
, namely,

uf-\nf(f\mid G)=\sum_{i=1}^{s}a_{i}g_{i}\quad(a_{i}\in R)

with the property that
\lm(\sum_{i=1}^{s}a_{i}g_{i})\geq\lm(a_{i}g_{i})
for all
i
such that
a_{i}g_{i}\neq 0
.

A weak normal form
\nf
is called
polynomial
if, whenever
f\in K[x]
and
G\subset K[x]
, in the condition (3) above,
u
and
a_{i}
can be chosen as elements of
K[x]
.

Definition 2.5

(cf.
[
4
, 2.6]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
denote the set of all finite subsets of
R
.
Let
h\in R
and
G\in\mathcal{G}
. Assume that there exist sequences
\{h_{0}=h,h_{1},\dots,h_{m}\}\subset R
and
\{G_{0}=G,G_{1},\dots,G_{m}\}\subset\mathcal{G}
such that

\displaystyle\exists g_{i}\in G_{i},\ \lm(g_{i})\mid\lm(h_{i}),\ h_{i+1}=\spo(h_{i},g_{i}),\ G_{i+1}=G_{i}\cup\{h_{i}\},

\displaystyle h_{m}=0\text{ \ or \ $\lm(g)\nmid\lm(h_{m})$ for all $g\in G_{m}$.}

Then we call
h_{m}
an
s
-normal form
of
h
with respect to
G
.
Clearly, we have
\{h_{i}\}\subset K[x]
if
h\in K[x]
and
G\subset K[x]
.
We write as

\snf(h\mid G)=0

to mean that an
s
-normal form of
h
with respect to
G
exists and is zero.

Note that in general, a sequence
\{h_{0}=h,h_{1},\dots,h_{m}\}\subset R
with the property in Definition
2.5
does not exist; in other words, the procedure may not terminate (cf.
[
2
, §1.7]
).

Buchberger’s criterion for Gröbner bases is also effective for standard bases with local ordering.

Theorem 2.6

(Buchberger’s Criterion (cf.
[
2
, 1.7.3]
))
.

Let
I\subset R
be an ideal and
G=\{g_{1},\dots,g_{s}\}\subset I
.
Let
\nf
be a weak normal form on
R
with respect to
G
.
Then the following are equivalent:

(1)

G
is a standard basis of
I
.

(2)

\nf(f\mid G)=0
for all
f\in I
.

(3)

Each
f\in I
has a standard representation with respect to
\nf(-\mid G)
.

(4)

G
generates
I
and
\nf(\spo(g_{i},g_{j})\mid G)=0
for
i,j=1,\dots,s
.

(5)

G
generates
I
and
\nf(\spo(g_{i},g_{j})\mid G_{ij})=0
for
a suitable subset
G_{ij}\subset G
and
i,j=1,\dots,s
.

The following proposition is also useful in the proof of our main theorem.

Proposition 2.7

(See
[
4
, 2.5-2.6, 3.1]
)
.

Let
I\subset R
be an ideal and
G\subset I
a finite subset generating
I
.
Then
G
is a standard basis of
I
if
\snf(\spo(g,g^{\prime})\mid G)=0
for any
g,g^{\prime}\in G
.

There is an algorithm, proposed by Mora (
[
4
]
, cf.
[
2
, 1.7.6]
), that yields a polynomial weak normal form.
When employing a global ordering,
\nf(f\mid G)
is obtained as the last element of a sequence
h_{0}=f,h_{1},\dots,h_{m}
such that
h_{i+1}=\spo(h_{i},h_{i}^{\prime})
, where
h_{i}^{\prime}
is a suitable element of
G
. Mora’s normal form with respect to a local ordering is obtained in a similar manner, but
h_{i}^{\prime}
is an element of
G\cup\{h_{0},\dots,h_{i-1}\}
.
For the convenience of the readers, we state the algorithm.

For
f=\sum_{\alpha}a_{\alpha}x^{\alpha}\in K[x]\setminus\{0\}
, let

\ec(f)=\max{\left\{\left.\deg x^{\alpha}\,\right|\,a_{\alpha}\neq 0\right\}}-\min{\left\{\left.\deg x^{\alpha}\,\right|\,a_{\alpha}\neq 0\right\}}.

Proposition 2.8

(cf.
[
2
, 1.7.6]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
denote the set of all finite subsets of
K[x]
.
Let
f\in K[x]\setminus\{0\}
and
G\in\mathcal{G}
.
We construct sequences
\{h_{0}=f,h_{1},\dots\}\subset K[x]
and
\{T_{0}=G,T_{1},\dots\}\subset\mathcal{G}
as follows:
Suppose that we obtain
\{h_{0},\dots,h_{i}\}
and
\{T_{0},\dots,T_{i}\}
.
If
h_{i}=0
or
T^{\prime}_{i}:={\left\{\left.g\in T_{i}\,\right|\,\lm(g)\mid\lm(h_{i})\right\}}=\emptyset
, then we stop here.
In case
h_{i}\neq 0
and
T^{\prime}_{i}\neq\emptyset
, take
g\in T^{\prime}_{i}
with
\ec(g)=\min{\left\{\left.\ec(g^{\prime})\,\right|\,g^{\prime}\in T^{\prime}_{i}\right\}}
, let
T_{i+1}=T_{i}\cup\{h_{i}\}
if
\ec(g)>\ec(h_{i})
and
T_{i+1}=T_{i}
otherwise, and let
h_{i+1}=\spo(h_{i},g)
.
This algorithm terminates after a finite number of steps, and the last one in the sequence
\{h_{0}=f,h_{1},\dots\}
is a polynomial weak normal form of
f
with respect to
G
.

Note that Mora’s normal form is also an
s
-normal form. The difference between them is whether the invariant
\ec
is used in the process to obtain the normal forms.

Using Mora’s normal form, we can compute the standard basis as follows (see
[
2
, 1.7.1, 1.7.8]
).

Proposition 2.9
.

Let
I\subset R
be an ideal generated by a finite subset
G_{0}\subset K[x]
and let
\nf
denote Mora’s polynomial weak normal form.
We construct a sequence
\{G_{0}\subsetneq G_{1}\subsetneq\dots\}
of finite subsets of
K[x]
as follows: if we obtain
\{G_{0},\dots,G_{i}\}
and
if there exists
f,g\in G_{i}
such that
h:=\nf(\spo(f,g)\mid G_{i})\neq 0
, then define
G_{i+1}
to be
G_{i}\cup\{h\}
.
This algorithm terminates after a finite number of steps, and the last subset in the sequence is a standard basis for
I
.

3.
The main results

In this section, we will prove the main theorem.
We basically use the notation from the preceding section.
Let
I\subset\langle x\rangle\subset K[x]
be a prime ideal and
V\subset\mathbb{A}^{r}_{K}
the affine variety defined by
I
.
Let
A
denote the local ring of the variety
V
at the origin
o\in V
;
namely,
A=R/IR
.
Let
K[x,y]=K[x_{1},\dots,x_{r},y]
denote the polynomial ring in
r+1
variables and let
B=K[x,y]_{\langle x,y\rangle}
, where
\langle x,y\rangle=\langle x_{1},\dots,x_{r},y\rangle
.
Let
g\in\langle x\rangle\setminus I
.
For
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
, let
I_{n}=I+\langle g-y^{n}\rangle\subset K[x,y]
and
B_{n}=B/I_{n}B
.
Then
B_{n}
is the local ring of the singularity
(V_{n},p_{n})
in the Introduction, and Theorem
1.1
immediately follows from Proposition
2.3
and the following.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\{f_{1},\dots,f_{m}\}\subset K[x]\setminus\{0\}
be a standard basis of
I
and let
G_{0}=\{f_{1},\dots,f_{m},g\}
.
Let
\{g_{1},\dots,g_{t}\}
be a standard basis of
I+\langle g\rangle\subset K[x]
which is obtained as the last element
G_{k}
of the sequence
G_{0}\subsetneq\dots\subsetneq G_{k}\subset K[x]
, as described in Proposition
2.9
.
Let
N=\max{\left\{\left.\ord(g_{i})\,\right|\,i=1,\dots,t\right\}}
.
Then, for
n>N
, the tangent cone of the local ring
B_{n}
is isomorphic to
K[x,y]/\langle\In(g_{1}),\dots,\In(g_{t})\rangle
.

Proof.

Assume that
n>N
.
By Proposition
2.3
, it is sufficient to prove that the ideal
I_{n}
has a standard basis
\mathcal{G}
such that
{\left\{\left.\In(f)\,\right|\,f\in\mathcal{G}\right\}}=\{\In(g_{1}),\dots,\In(g_{t})\}
.

Note that all polynomials in
G_{k}
and those appearing in the algorithm for obtaining
G_{k}
from
G_{0}
are represented as linear combinations of polynomials in
G_{0}
with coefficients in
K[x]
.
For each
f
of those polynomials, we fix such a representation
f=\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}f_{i}+a_{m+1}g
, and define
\bar{f}\in K[x,y]
as the polynomial obtained from
f
by replacing
g
with
g-y^{n}
in the representation; namely,
\bar{f}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}f_{i}+a_{m+1}(g-y^{n})
.
Let us express these procedures more precisely.
For
0\leq i<k
, we may assume that
h^{(i)}\in G_{i+1}\setminus G_{i}
is a unique element and
h^{(i)}=\nf(\spo(p_{1}^{(i)},p_{2}^{(i)})\mid G_{i})
with
p_{1}^{(i)},p_{2}^{(i)}\in G_{i}
.
Let
\{h_{0}^{(i)}=\spo(p_{1}^{(i)},p_{2}^{(i)}),h_{1}^{(i)},\dots,h_{\ell_{i}}^{(i)}=h^{(i)}\}
be a sequence obtained by the algorithm in Proposition
2.8
.
Let

\mathcal{F}=\left(\bigcup_{i=0}^{k}G_{i}\right)\cup\left(\bigcup_{i=0}^{k-1}\{h_{0}^{(i)},h_{1}^{(i)},\dots,h_{\ell_{i}}^{(i)}\}\right)

and let
K[x][s]:=K[x][s_{1},\dots,s_{m+1}]
be the polynomial ring in
m+1
variables with coefficient ring
K[x]
.
We fix a map
\Phi\colon\mathcal{F}\to K[x][s]
such that
\Phi(f)=\Phi(f)(s_{1},\dots,s_{m+1})
is of the form

\sum_{i=1}^{m+1}a_{i}(f)s_{i}
, where
a_{i}(f)\in K[x]
, and that

f=\Phi(f)(f_{1},\dots,f_{m},g)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}(f)f_{i}+a_{m+1}(f)g\in K[x].

Moreover assume that
\Phi(f_{i})=s_{i}
for
i=1,\dots,m
,
and
\Phi(g)=s_{m+1}
.
For each
f\in\mathcal{F}
, we define a polynomial
\overline{f}
by

\overline{f}=\Phi(f)(f_{1},\dots,f_{m},g-y^{n})\in K[x,y].

Let
\overline{G}_{i}={\left\{\left.\bar{f}\,\right|\,f\in G_{i}\right\}}\subset K[x,y]
.
Then
I_{n}=\langle\overline{G}_{0}\rangle
.

We consider the following condition for
0\leq j\leq k
:

C(j)
:

\In(f)\in K[x]
for any
f\in\overline{G}_{i}
for
0\leq i\leq j
.

We will prove that the condition
C(k)
holds.
Consequently, we obtain

{\left\{\left.\In(f)\,\right|\,f\in\overline{G}_{k}\right\}}={\left\{\left.\In(f)\,\right|\,f\in G_{k}\right\}}=\{\In(g_{1}),\dots,\In(g_{t})\}.

Assume that
C(i)
with
i<k
holds.
Let
p_{1}=p_{1}^{(i)},p_{2}=p_{2}^{(i)}\in G_{i}
,
\ell=\ell_{i}
and
h_{j}=h_{j}^{(i)}
(
j=1,\dots,\ell
).
For any
f(x,y)\in K[x,y]
, we write
f(x,0)=f|_{y=0}
.
Note that
\ord(f)\leq N<n
for each
f\in\mathcal{F}
.
Since
\lm(\bar{p}_{1})=\lm(p_{1})
and
\lm(\bar{p}_{2})=\lm(p_{2})
by the assumption, we have
\lm(\spo(\bar{p}_{1},\bar{p}_{2}))=\lm(\spo(p_{1},p_{2}))
and
\spo(\bar{p}_{1},\bar{p}_{2})|_{y=0}=\spo(p_{1},p_{2})
.
Suppose that
\In(\bar{h}_{t})\not\in K[x]
for some
0\leq t\leq\ell
and
t
is the minimum among such numbers, and let
\Phi(h_{t})=\sum_{i=1}^{m+1}a_{i}s_{i}
.
Then we have the following representation:

\bar{h}_{t}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}f_{i}+a_{m+1}(g-y^{n})=h_{t}-a_{m+1}y^{n}.

Since
\ord(h_{t})<n
, we have
h_{t}=0
; however, it contradicts that
h_{j}\neq 0
for every
0\leq j\leq\ell
as
h_{\ell}\neq 0
.
Therefore,
\In(\bar{h}_{j})\in K[x]
for
0\leq j\leq\ell
, and thus
C(i+1)
holds.
Hence the condition
C(k)
also holds.

Now, we have to prove that
\overline{G}_{k}
is a standard basis of
I_{n}
.
To this end, it suffices to show that
\snf(\spo(f,f^{\prime})\mid\overline{G}_{k})=0
for any
f,f^{\prime}\in\overline{G}_{k}
by Proposition
2.7
.
Let
\bar{p}_{1},\bar{p}_{2}\in\overline{G}_{k}
with
p_{1},p_{2}\in G_{k}
.
We may assume that
\spo(\bar{p}_{1},\bar{p}_{2})\neq 0
.
Let us use the notation
h_{j}
,
\bar{h}_{j}
,
0\leq j\leq\ell
as above (we extend
\Phi
to a function on
\mathcal{F}\cup\{h_{0},\dots,h_{\ell}\}
).
Since
G_{k}
is a standard basis of
I+\langle g\rangle
, it follows from Theorem
2.6
that
h_{\ell}=\nf(\spo(p_{1},p_{2})\mid G_{k})=0
.
By the argument above, we have that
\In(\bar{h}_{j})\in K[x]
for
0\leq j<\ell
.
Since the sequence
\{h_{0},\dots,h_{\ell}\}
is obtained by taking
s
-polynomials and
\lm(h_{i})=\lm(\bar{h}_{i})
for
0\leq j<\ell
, the sequence
\{\bar{h}_{0},\dots,\bar{h}_{\ell-1}\}
satisfies the property in Definition
2.5
; however,
\bar{h}_{\ell-1}
might not be an
s
-normal form.
Assume that
h_{\ell}=\spo(h_{\ell-1},h^{\prime})
for an element
h^{\prime}\in G_{k}\cup{\left\{\left.h_{i}\,\right|\,i<\ell-1\right\}}
, and put
h=\spo(\bar{h}_{\ell-1},\bar{h}^{\prime})
.
Let
h=\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}f_{i}+a_{m+1}(g-y^{n})
,
a_{i}\in K[x]
, be a representation obtained naturally from
\bar{h}_{\ell-1}
and
\bar{h}^{\prime}
.
Then
h|_{y=0}=h_{\ell}=0
, and thus
h=-a_{m+1}g=\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}f_{i}\in\langle f_{1},\dots,f_{m}\rangle=I
.
Hence
a_{m+1}\in I
, because
I
is a prime ideal and
g\not\in I
.
Recall that
F:=\{f_{1},\dots,f_{m}\}
is a standard basis of
I
.
By Theorem
2.6
, we have
\nf(a_{m+1}\mid F)=0
.
Since
F\subset\overline{G}_{k}
by the assumption on
\Phi
, it follows that
\snf(a_{m+1}\mid\overline{G}_{k})=0
.
By multiplying
y^{n}
, we obtain an
s
-normal form of
a_{m+1}y^{n}=-h
, that is,
\snf(h\mid\overline{G}_{k})=0
.
Finally, we obtain a sequence as in Definition
2.5
starting from
\bar{h}_{0}=\spo(\bar{p}_{1},\bar{p}_{2})
via
h
, and ending at
0
. That is,
\snf(\spo(\bar{p}_{1},\bar{p}_{2})\mid\overline{G}_{k})=0
.
∎

Remark 3.2
.

In the proof above, the assumption that
I
is a prime ideal is only applied to show that
a_{m+1}g\in I
implies
a_{m+1}\in I
.
Therefore, this assumption can be replaced with a weaker condition.
In fact, it suffices to assume that
g
is
K[x]/I
-regular, that is, if
a\in K[x]
and
ag\in I
, then
a\in I
.

Example 3.3
.

We show some simple examples with
r=2
.
We use the variables
(x,y,z)
instead of
(x_{1},x_{2},y)
and the monomial ordering

.

(1)
Let
I=\langle xy\rangle\subset K[x,y]
and

Then

Then

Remark 3.4
.

In the situation of Theorem
3.1
, we have

K[x,y]/\langle\In(g_{1}),\dots,\In(g_{t})\rangle\cong(K[x]/\langle\In(g_{1}),\dots,\In(g_{t})\rangle)[y].

Hence the affine tangent cone of
B_{n}
with
n>N
is isomorphic to the product of the affine tangent cone of
R/(I+\langle g\rangle)R
and the affine line
\mathbb{A}^{1}_{K}
.

Remark 3.5
.

If
A
is normal and the image of
g
in
A
is reduced, then
B_{n}
are normal for every
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
(
[
6
]
).
Let us consider the case that
\spec B_{n}
are normal two-dimensional singularities. Then, as
n
approaches infinity, the geometric genus
p_{g}(\spec B_{n})
tends towards infinity (cf.
[
1
, §2]
).
Therefore, the tangent cone of a normal two-dimensional singularity cannot provide upper bounds of the geometric genus, even though any singularity is a small deformation of its tangent cone (cf.
[
5
