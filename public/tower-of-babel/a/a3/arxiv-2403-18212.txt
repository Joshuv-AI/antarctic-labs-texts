Preference-Based Planning in Stochastic Environments: From Partially-Ordered Temporal Goals to Most Preferred Policies
Hazhar Rahmani, Abhishek N. Kulkarni, Jie Fu

Abstract
Partial order preferences arise naturally in human and AI decision-making when the agents are unable or unwilling to express their preferences. We study automatic synthesis of policies in Markov decision processes (MDP) that maximize satisfaction of preferences represented as a partial order on a set of Linear Temporal Logic on Finite Traces (LTLf) formulas. Partial order preferences on LTLf formulas introduce two key challenges to preference-satisfying policy synthesis: unranked outcomes make traditional total-order-based approaches inapplicable, and identifying a non-dominated strategy requires evaluating all probability distributions over finite paths in the MDP, where each path satisfies a subset of LTLf formulas, for which efficient computational models remain unexplored. We present two key contributions: First, we introduce a new computational model called the preference deterministic finite automaton (PDFA) to represent partial orders on subsets of LTLf formulas. We present a procedure to construct a PDFA for a given partial order on a set of LTLf formulas. Second, we identify three types of stochastic orders studied in order theory that transform a partial order on LTLf formulas to a partial order on policies in MDP. For these stochastic orders, we show that synthesizing preference-satisfying policy is equivalent to finding a Pareto optimal policy in a multi-objective MDP, constructed from the original MDP, the PDFA, and the stochastic order. We prove the correctness of our procedure and show its applicability using a robot motion planning example in a gridworld.

Introduction

The rise of AI and foundational models has made human-AI interactions more intuitive by enabling autonomous systems to understand human’s preferences in natural language.
However, human preferences are not always expressible by a total order.
They often need to admit a
partial
order because of (a)
Inescapability
: agents must make decisions with limited information under time constraints, and
(b)
Incommensurability
: some comparisons, such as deciding between minimizing harm to one individual versus saving multiple lives in scenarios like the trolley problem
[
28
]
, lack a common basis.
While there exists a wide body of literature on preference-based planning under partial order preferences in deterministic environments (see
[
4
]
and the references within), this problem remains under-explored for probabilistic environments.

We study a variant of the policy synthesis problem in a
mdp
(
mdp
)
[
2
]
, where the goal is to synthesize a policy that maximally satisfies a user preference expressed as a partial order on a set of
ltlf
(
ltlf
) formulas.

As a motivating example, consider Figure
1
, which shows a garden that belongs to Bob. He grows three kinds of flowers: Tulips, daisies, and orchids. To pollinate the flowers, he uses a bee robot with limited battery. The environment is uncertain due to the presence of another agent (bird), the weather, and the robot dynamics.

Bob has a preference for how the robot should achieve the task of pollination. Compared to the other types, tulips have a shorter life span, so Bob considers four outcomes:

(
p_{1}
)

pollinate tulips first, then at least one other flower type;

(
p_{2}
)

pollinate two types of flowers, with the first being either daisies or orchids;

(
p_{3}
)

pollinate only tulips; and

(
p_{4}
)

pollinate either daisies or orchids or no flowers,

over which, his preference is shown in Figure
1
b, using a preference graph, where the nodes represent the outcomes, and each directed edge from
p_{i}
to
p_{j}
represents that Bob strictly prefers outcome
p_{j}
over
p_{i}
.
According to this graph,
p_{1}
is the most preferred outcome, and
p_{4}
is the least preferred, while
p_{2}
and
p_{3}
are incomparable with each other. As the robot has a limited battery life and the system is stochastic, it might not achieve the most preferred outcome with probability one. Thus, we are interested to answer the following questions: Given all the uncertainties in the environment, how to compute a robot’s policy that maximally satisfies Bob’s preference?

The problem poses a fundamental question:
Given a partial order on
ltlf
formulas, how to induce a partial order on the set of policies in MDP?

Answering this question is particularly challenging because each policy in
mdp
induces a probability distribution over the set of finite paths in
mdp
and each finite path in
mdp
satisfies a set of
ltlf
formulas.
Consequently, comparing any two policies amounts to comparing two probability distributions over subsets of
ltlf
formulas.

We present a two step approach to address this challenge.
First, we introduce a computational model called
pdfa
(
pdfa
), which encodes a partial order on the subsets of
ltlf
formulas as a partial order on the states of a semi-automaton, i.e., a deterministic finite automaton without final states.

Second, we build upon the notion of stochastic orders
[
21
]
to compare policies by comparing the distributions induced by them over a family of subsets of
ltlf
formulas.
Specifically, we focus on three types of stochastic orders: strong, weak, and weak
∗
, each of which defines distinct families of subsets.
Given a partial order on a set
U
, the strong order consists of subsets
U
that are not dominated by any element in
U
not in the subset.
A policy is strictly preferred to another under the strong stochastic order if the probability of satisfying some of the undominated subsets in this family is strictly greater under the first policy than the second, while remaining equal for all others.
In fact, the notion of strong stochastic order coincides widely studied concept of stochastic dominance, but is known to result in conservative outcomes
[
21
]
.

Weak and weak
∗
stochastic orders offer less conservative way to compare policies.
For each
ltlf
formula, the family of subsets induced by the weak stochastic order includes a subset containing all
ltlf
formulas
at least as good as
as the formula under given partial order on
ltlf
formulas.
Whereas, the weak
∗
stochastic order accounts for incomparable outcomes by including a subset for each
ltlf
formula containing all
ltlf
formulas
no worse than
the formula.

The three stochastic orders satisfy a special condition: Any non-dominated policy under these stochastic order is a Pareto optimal policy in a multi-objective
mdp
, which is constructed using the original
mdp
and the
pdfa
.
As a result, to synthesize a strategy that maximally satisfies a given preference, we solve for Pareto-optimal strategies in the corresponding multi-objective
mdp
, where
maximally satisfies
is interpreted under one of the three orders.

Our contributions in this paper as follows: (1) We introduce a new computational model called
Preference Deterministic Finite Automaton (PDFA)
, which models a user’s (possibly partially-ordered) preference over temporally extended goals. (2) We introduce an algorithm that translates a set of partially ordered LTL
f
formulas, each representing a temporal goal, to a PDFA. (3) We establish a connection between the PBP in stochastic systems and the notions of stochastic orders
[
21
]
. This connection allows us to rank policies given their induced probabilistic distribution over possible outcomes.
Hence, it
reduces probabilistic planning with partially-ordered preferences over temporal goals to computing the set of nondominated policies for a multi-objective
mdp
, constructed as a product of the
mdp
modeling the environment and the PDFA specifying the user preference over the temporal goals.
(4) We employ the property of weak-stochastic nondominated policies to design multiple objective functions in the product
mdp
and prove that a Pareto-optimal policy in the resulting multi-objective product
mdp
is weak-stochastic nondominated respecting the preference relation. Thus,
the set of weak-stochastic nondominated policies can, then, be computed using any off-the-shelf solver that computes Pareto optimal policies.

1.1
Related Work

Preference-based planning (PBP) has been well-studied for deterministic systems given both total and partial preferences. See the survey by
[
4
]
.
For preferences over temporal goals in deterministic systems, several works
[
29
,
30
,
26
,
25
]
proposed minimum-violation planning methods that decide which low-priority constraints should be violated.
[
1
]
formulate and solve a two-objective optimal planning problem where one objective is to minimize the total cost of a plan, while the other aims to optimize the costs of individual temporal goals ordered by the user preference.
In
[
22
]
, authors associate weights with Boolean and temporal operators in signal temporal logic to specify the importance of satisfying the sub-formulas and priority in the timing of satisfaction. This reduces the PBP problem to that of maximizing the weighted satisfaction in deterministic dynamical systems.
For planning under this new specification language,
[
8
]
propose an algorithm based on mixed linear integer programming.
However, the solutions to PBP for deterministic systems cannot be applied to stochastic systems (such as MDPs/POMDPs).
This is because in stochastic systems, even a deterministic policy yields a distribution over outcomes. Hence, to determine a better policy, we need comparison of distributions—a task a deterministic planner cannot do.

Preference-based planning for stochastic systems has been less studied until recently. In

[
18
]
, the authors consider to revise a given specification to improve the probability of satisfaction of the specification. They
develop an
mdp
planning algorithm that trades off minimizing the cost of revision and maximizing the probability of satisfying the revised formula.
In

[
7
]
, the authors focus on planning with infeasible LTL specifications in MDPs.
Their problem’s objective is to synthesize a policy that, in decreasing order of importance, 1) provides a desired guarantee to satisfy the task, 2) satisfies the specifications as much as possible, and 3) minimizes the implementation cost of the plan.
In
[
19
]
, a fuzzy logic is introduced to capture a class of preferences over temporal goals constructed using prioritized conjunction and ordered disjunction. The authors show that these formulas can be equivalently expressed by weighted automata. They then provide a probabilistic planning algorithm that maximizes the expected degree of satisfaction.
However, all these works assume that the preference relation to be
total
.
To the best of our knowledge, only
[
14
]
and
[
17
]
have studied probabilistic planning with incomplete preferences.
Ref.
[
17
]
focus on the qualitative version of the problem, synthesizing strategies that identify and exploit opportunities to improve the most preferred achievable outcome with either positive probability or probability one. This is achieved by reducing the problem to reactive synthesis
[
20
,
3
]
. In
[
14
]
, the author introduced the notion of the value of preference satisfaction for planning within a pre-defined finite time duration and developed a mixed-integer linear program to maximize the satisfaction value for a subset of preference relations. In comparison, our work resorts to the notion of stochastic ordering to rank the policies in the stochastic system with respect to the partial order of temporal goals and allows the time horizon to be finite, but unbounded.

The paper is organized as follows.
In Section
2
, we present preliminaries and our problem definition.
In Section
3
, we introduce
pdfa
, and in Section
4
, we present our algorithm for converting a preference model of a set of LTL
f
formulas into a
pdfa
.
In Section
5
, we present our algorithm for computing a nondominated a policy, given the
pdfa
specifying the user’s preference over temporal goals.
In Section
6
, we present a case study and our detailed analysis.

We presented a preliminary version of this paper at the 2023 IEEE International Conference
on Robotics and Automation

]
.
In addition to revisions made throughout the paper, we have included several new results: (1) Our preliminary version assumed the
pdfa
is given by the user, but in this version we assume the user’s preference is specified using a partially ordered set of LTL
f

; (2) the preliminary version considered only the notion of weak-stohastic ordering for comparing policies, but in this version we added two additional notions of stochastic ordering, strong-stochastic ordering and weak

2.1
The System and its Policy

We model the system using a variant of
mdp
.

Definition 1

(Terminating Labeled MDP)
.

A
tlmdp
(
tlmdp
), or a terminating MDP for short, is a tuple

M=\langle S,A:=\bigcup_{s\in S}A_{s},\mathbf{P},s_{0},s_{\bot},\mathcal{AP},L\rangle,

where

S
is a finite set of states;

A
is a finite set of actions, where for each state
s\in S
,
A_{s}
is the set of available actions at
s
;

\mathbf{P}:S\times A\rightarrow\mathcal{D}(S)
is the probabilistic transition function, where for each
s,s^{\prime}\in S
and
a\in A
,
\mathbf{P}(s,a,s^{\prime})
is the probability that the MDP transitions to
s^{\prime}
after taking action
a
at
s
;
s_{0}\in S
is the initial state;

s_{\bot}\in S
is the
termination state
, which is a unique
sink
state and
A_{s_{\bot}}=\emptyset
;
\mathcal{AP}
is a finite set of atomic propositions; and

L:S\rightarrow 2^{\mathcal{AP}}\cup\{\epsilon\}
is a labeling function that assigns to each state
s\in S\setminus\{s_{\bot}\}
, the set of atomic propositions
L(s)\subseteq\mathcal{AP}
that hold in
s
.
Only the terminating state is labeled the empty string, i.e.,
L(s)=\epsilon
if and only if
s=s_{\bot}
.

Though this definition assumes a single sink state, we do not lose generality, as one can always convert an MDP with multiple sink state into an equivalent MDP with a single sink state by keeping only a single sink state and redirecting all the transitions to other sink states to that sink state.

The robot’s interaction with the environment in a finite number
k
of steps produces an
execution

\varrho=s_{0}a_{0}s_{1}a_{1}\cdots s_{k-1}a_{k-1}s_{k}
, where
s_{0}
is the initial state and at each step
0\leq i\leq k
, the system is at state
s_{i}
, the robot performs
a_{i}\in A_{s_{i}}
, and then the system transitions to state
s_{i+1}
, picked randomly based on the distribution
\mathbf{P}(\cdot\mid s_{i},a_{i})
.
This execution produces a
path
defined as
\rho=s_{0}s_{1}\cdots s_{k}\in S^{*}
, and the
trace
of this path is defined as the finite word
\operatorname{trace}(\rho)=L(s_{0})L(s_{1})L(s_{2})\cdots L(s_{k})\in(2^{\mathcal{AP}})^{*}
.
Path
\rho
is called
terminating
if
s_{k}=s_{\bot}
.
The set of all terminating paths in
M
is denoted
\operatorname{Paths}_{\bot}(M)
.
A policy for
M
is a function
\pi:\mathscr{D}\rightarrow\mathscr{C}
with
\mathscr{D}\in\{S,S^{*}\}
and
\mathscr{C}\in\{A,\mathcal{D}(A)\}
, and it is called
memoryless
if
\mathscr{D}=S
;
finite-memory
if
\mathscr{D}=S^{*}
;
deterministic
if
\mathscr{C}=A
, and
randomized
if
\mathscr{C}=\mathcal{D}(A)
.

In a terminating
mdp
, a policy is
proper
if it guarantees that the termination state
s_{\bot}
will be reached with probability one
[
5
]
. The set of all randomized, finite-memory, proper polices for
M
is denoted
\Pi_{prop}^{M}
. In this paper, we consider only the
tlmdp
s for which all the policies are proper. In other words, the system always terminates after a finite time. This restriction is due to that (1)
many applications require the robot to finish its execution in a finite time, and 2) the preference specification, defined next, is restricted to a partially ordered set of finite traces.

2.2
Specifying the Temporal Goals

The temporal goals in the MDP are specified formally using the following language.

Definition 2

(Syntax of
ltlf

[
11
]
)
.

Given a finite set
\mathcal{AP}
of atomic propositions, a formula in
ltlf

\mathcal{AP}
is generated by the following grammar:

\varphi\coloneqq p\mid\neg\varphi\mid\varphi\land\varphi\mid\bigcirc\,\varphi\mid\varphi\mbox{$\,{\sf U}\,$}\varphi,

where
p\in\mathcal{AP}
is an atomic proposition,
\neg
and
\land
are the standard Boolean operators negation and conjunction, respectively, and
\bigcirc\,
and

are temporal operators “Next” and “Until”, respectively.

The temporal operators are interpreted over sequences of time instants.
The formula
\bigcirc\,\varphi
means at the next time instant,
\varphi
holds true. Formula
\varphi_{1}\mbox{$\,{\sf U}\,$}\varphi_{2}
holds true at the current time instant if there exists a future time instant at which
\varphi_{2}
holds true and for all time instants from the current time until that future time,
\varphi_{1}
holds true.
An additional temporal operator “Eventually” (
\Diamond\,
) is defined as
\Diamond\,\varphi:=\mathsf{true}\mbox{$\,{\sf U}\,$}\varphi
.
Formula
\Diamond\,\varphi
means there exists a future time instant at which
\varphi
holds true.
The dual of Eventually operator is the “Always” (
\Box\,
). It is defined as
\Box\,\varphi:=\neg\Diamond\,\neg\varphi
.
Formula
\Box\,\varphi
means
\varphi
holds true at the current instant and all future instants.
For formal semantics of
ltlf
, see
[
11
]
.

\displaystyle\neg t\mbox{$\,{\sf U}\,$}((o\wedge\bigcirc\,\Diamond\,(d\vee t))\vee(d\wedge\bigcirc\,\Diamond\,(o\vee t))),

(

pollinate only tulips;

\displaystyle(\neg d\wedge\neg o)\mbox{$\,{\sf U}\,$}(t\wedge\Box\,(\neg d\wedge\neg o)),

(

Given an

The language of

(Deterministic Finite Automaton)
.

A

(

\mathcal{A}=\langle Q,\Sigma,\delta,q_{0},F\rangle

2.3
Rank the Policies

We introduce a computational model that captures the user’s preference over different temporal goals.

Definition 4
.

A preference model is a tuple
\langle U,\succeq\rangle
where
U
is a countable set of outcomes and
\succeq
is
preorder
–a reflexive and transitive binary relation–on
U
.

Given
u_{1},u_{2}\in U
, we write
u_{1}\succeq u_{2}
if
u_{1}
is
weakly preferred to
(i.e., is at least as good as)
u_{2}
; and
u_{1}\sim u_{2}
if
u_{1}\succeq u_{2}
and
u_{2}\succeq u_{1}
,
that is,
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
are
indifferent
.
We write

u_{1}\succ u_{2}
to mean that
u_{1}
is
strictly preferred
to
u_{2}
, i.e.,
u_{1}\succeq u_{2}
and
u_{1}\not\sim u_{2}
. We write

u_{1}\nparallel u_{2}
if
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
are
incomparable
.

Definition 5
.

Given a preference model
\langle U,\succeq\rangle
,
let
X
be a subset of
U
, the
upper closure of
X

is defined by

X^{\uparrow}=\{y\in U\mid y\succeq x\text{ for some }x\in X\},\text{ and }

the
lower closure of
X

is defined by

X^{\downarrow}=\{y\in U\mid y\preceq x\text{ for some }x\in X\}.

A set
X
is called an

if
X=X^{\uparrow}
.

In
[
21
]
, three different stochastic orderings are introduced, called

,

, and

∗

of

Definition 6
.

[
21
]

Let

\mathfrak{E}_{wk}(U)
, and

denote the strong, weak, and weak* orderings, respectively.
It is defined that

\displaystyle\mathfrak{E}_{st}(U)

\displaystyle=\{\text{ all increasing sets in $2^{U}$}\},

\displaystyle\mathfrak{E}_{wk}(U)

\displaystyle=\{\{x\}^{\uparrow}\mid x\in U\}\cup\{U,\emptyset\},

\displaystyle\mathfrak{E}_{wk*}(U)

\displaystyle=\{E\setminus\{x\}^{\downarrow}\mid x\in U\}\cup\{U,\emptyset\}.

These stochastic orderings allow us to rank probability measures defined over
U
according to the partially ordered set
U
.
Let
\mathfrak{E}(U)
be
a family of subsets
of
U
that includes

\mathfrak{E}\in\{\mathfrak{E}_{st},\mathfrak{E}_{wk},\mathfrak{E}_{wk*}\}

Let

P_{2}\geq_{\mathfrak{E}}P_{1}

.
It is proven that if the partial order

, and

[

]

However, for a partial order, the three stochastic orderings may differ.

To illustrate, consider the following example.

Example 2
.

Let
U=\{a,b,c,d\}

\succeq=\{(a,b),(b,d),

(c,d),(a,c),(a,d)\}\cup I_{U}

, and

, and

.

We have

\mathfrak{E}_{st}(U)=\{\{a\},\{a,b\},\{a,c\},\{a,b,c\},\{a,b,c,d\},\emptyset\}.

Accordingly,

\displaystyle[P_{1}[X]]_{X\in\mathfrak{E}_{st}(U)}

\displaystyle=[0.5,0.8,0.7,1,1,0],

\displaystyle[P_{2}[X]]_{X\in\mathfrak{E}_{st}(U)}

\displaystyle=[0,0.5,0.3,1,1,0],

\displaystyle[P_{3}[X]]_{X\in\mathfrak{E}_{st}(U)}

\displaystyle=[0.3,0.5,0.3,0.5,1,0].

Accordingly,

\displaystyle[P_{1}[X]]_{X\in\mathfrak{E}_{wk}(U)}

\displaystyle=[0.5,0.8,0.7,1,0],

\displaystyle[P_{2}[X]]_{X\in\mathfrak{E}_{wk}(U)}

\displaystyle=[0,0.5,0.3,1,0],\text{and}

\displaystyle[P_{3}[X]]_{X\in\mathfrak{E}_{wk}(U)}

.

\displaystyle=[0.8,0.7,1,1,0],

In our context, because we are interested in sequential decision-making and planning problems with a finite time termination, the set

\Sigma^{\ast}
, or the set of finite traces generated by the system and its labeling function.
Based on the ranking of probability measures induced by each one of the stochastic orderings for
\Sigma^{\ast}

in the

Specifically, note that a proper policy

produces a distribution over the set of all terminating paths in the MDP

is the probability of generating

Each terminating path

\operatorname{trace}(\rho)=L(s_{0})L(s_{1})\ldots
,
and therefore,

3
Modeling Preference over LTL
f
Goals

In this section, we consider the case when the user defines his preference
\langle\Sigma^{\ast},\succeq\rangle
using a ranking over temporal goals.

Definition 9
.

An
ltlf
preference model
is a preference model
\langle\Phi,\trianglerighteq\rangle
in which
\Phi=\{\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{N}\}
is a finite set of
distinct

ltlf
formulas over a set of atomic propositions
\mathcal{AP}
and
\trianglerighteq
is a partial order—a reflexive, transitive, and an antisymmetric—relation on
\Phi
.

Two
ltlf
formulas
\varphi
and
\varphi^{\prime}
are distinct if
\mathcal{L}(\varphi)\neq\mathcal{L}(\varphi^{\prime})
, where
\mathcal{L}(\varphi)
is the language of the formula, i.e., the set of words satisfying the formula.

Assumption 1
.

We assume that
\bigcup_{1\leq i\leq N}\mathcal{L}(\varphi_{i})=\Sigma^{\ast}
, meaning that for each word
w\in\Sigma^{\ast}
, there
is at least one
\varphi\in\Phi
such that
w\in\mathcal{L}(\varphi)
.
Note that if
\bigcup_{1\leq i\leq N}\mathcal{L}(\varphi_{i})\subset\Sigma^{\ast}
, then the assumption will hold by adding the formula
\varphi=\bigwedge_{1\leq i\leq N}\neg\varphi_{i}
to
\Phi
.

Assumption 2
.

The preference model
\langle\Phi,\trianglerighteq\rangle
is a partial order relation over
\Phi
, which means the following properties are satisfied:

•

Reflexive:
\varphi\trianglerighteq\varphi
for all
\varphi\in\Phi
.

•

Antisymmetric:
\varphi\trianglerighteq\varphi^{\prime}
and
\varphi^{\prime}\trianglerighteq\varphi
implies
\varphi=\varphi^{\prime}
.

•

Transitive:
\varphi_{1}\trianglerighteq\varphi_{2}
and
\varphi_{2}\trianglerighteq\varphi_{3}
implies
\varphi_{1}\trianglerighteq\varphi_{3}
.

Remark 1
.

Note that any preference model
\langle\Phi,\trianglerighteq\rangle
in which
\trianglerighteq
is a preorder, i.e., a partial order without the requirement of being antisymmetric, can be converted into a preference model
\langle\Phi^{\prime},\trianglerighteq^{\prime}\rangle
in which
\trianglerighteq^{\prime}
is a partial order.
The idea is to iteratively refine the preference model until the resulting model has no pair of indifferent formulas.
In each step, two indifferent formulas
\varphi_{i}
and
\varphi_{j}
are replaced by their disjunction
\varphi_{i}\lor\varphi_{j}
, after which the preference relation is altered accordingly.

The model
\langle\Phi,\trianglerighteq\rangle
is a combinative preference model, as opposed to an exclusionary one. This is because we do not assert the exclusivity condition
\varphi_{i}\land\varphi_{j}=\mathsf{false}
. This allows us to represent a preference such as “Visiting A and B is preferred to visiting A,” (
\Diamond\,A\land\Diamond\,B\trianglerighteq\Diamond\,A
) where the less preferred outcome must be satisfied first in order to satisfy the more preferred outcome. In literature, it is common to study exclusionary preference models (see
[
4
,
6
]
and the references within) because of their simplicity
[
16
]
.
However, we focus on planning with combinative preferences since they are more expressive than the exclusionary ones
[
15
]
. In fact, every exclusionary preference model can be transformed into a combinative one, but the opposite is not true.

When a combinative preference model is interpreted over finite words, the agent needs a way to compare the sets of temporal logic objectives satisfied by two words.
For instance, consider the preference that “Visiting A and B is preferred to visiting A,” and let
w_{1}=\emptyset\{A\}\emptyset\{B\}
and
w_{2}=\emptyset\{A\}\emptyset
be two finite
words.
Note that
w_{1}
has both
A
and
B
evaluated true, each at some point in time, and
w_{2}=\emptyset\{A\}\emptyset
only has
A
evaluated true.
Therefore,
w_{1}\models\varphi_{1}\land\varphi_{2}
, whereas
w_{2}
satisfies only
\varphi_{2}
. To determine the preference between
w_{1}
and
w_{2}
, the agent compares the set
\{\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2}\}
with
\{\varphi_{2}\}
to conclude that
w_{1}
is preferred over
w_{2}
. However, suppose the given preference is that “visiting A is preferred over visiting B,” i.e., (
\Diamond\,A\trianglerighteq\Diamond\,B
). Then the two words
w_{1}
and
w_{2}
are indifferent since both satisfy the more preferred objective
\Diamond\,A
. To formalize this notion, we define the notion of most-preferred outcomes.

Given a non-empty subset
\mathbb{X}\subseteq\Phi
, let
\mathsf{MP}(\mathbb{X})\triangleq\{\varphi\in\mathbb{X}\mid\nexists\varphi^{\prime}\in\mathbb{X}:\varphi^{\prime}\trianglerighteq\varphi\}
denote the set of most-preferred outcomes in
\mathbb{X}
.

Definition 10
.

Given an
ltlf
preference model
\langle\Phi,\trianglerighteq\rangle
and a finite word
w\in\Sigma^{\ast}
, the set of most-preferred formulas satisfied by
w
is given by
\mathsf{MP}(w)\coloneqq\mathsf{MP}(\{\varphi\in\Phi\mid w\models\varphi\})
.

By definition, there is no outcome included in
\mathsf{MP}(w)
that is weakly preferred to any other outcome in
\mathsf{MP}(w)
. Thus, we have the following result.

Lemma 1
.

For any word
w\in\Sigma^{\ast}
, formulas in
\mathsf{MP}(w)
are incomparable to each other.

Proof.

By contradiction. Suppose that the set
\mathsf{MP}(w)
contains two formulas
\varphi_{1}
and
\varphi_{2}
that are comparable. Then, since
\trianglerighteq
is a partial order, one of the following cases must be true:

1)

\varphi_{1}\triangleright\varphi_{2}
, or

2)

\varphi_{2}\triangleright\varphi_{1}
.

Consider the first case. By definition of
\mathsf{MP}
operator, only
\varphi_{1}
is included in
\mathsf{MP}(w)
. Similarly, in second case, only
\varphi_{2}
is included in
\mathsf{MP}(w)
. This is a contradiction.
∎

Now, we formally define the interpretation of
\langle\Phi,\trianglerighteq\rangle
in terms of the preference relation it induces on
\Sigma^{\ast}
.

Definition 11
.

An
ltlf
preference model
\langle\Phi,\trianglerighteq\rangle
induces the preference model
\langle\Sigma^{\ast},\succeq\rangle
where for any
w_{1},w_{2}\in\Sigma^{\ast}
,

•

w_{1}\succeq w_{2}
if and only if for every formula
\varphi\in\mathsf{MP}(w_{1})
, there exists a formula
\varphi^{\prime}\in\mathsf{MP}(w_{2})
such that
\varphi\trianglerighteq\varphi^{\prime}
,

•

w_{1}\sim w_{2}
if and only if
\mathsf{MP}(w_{1})=\mathsf{MP}(w_{2})
, and

•

w_{1}\nparallel w_{2}
, otherwise.

The following set of properties can be shown.

Lemma 2
.

Let
\langle\Sigma^{\ast},\succeq\rangle
be the preference model induced by
\langle\Phi,\trianglerighteq\rangle
. For any
w_{1},w_{2}\in\Sigma^{\ast}
, if
w_{1}\succeq w_{2}
, then
there does not exist a pair of outcomes
\varphi\in\mathsf{MP}(w_{1})
and
\varphi^{\prime}\in\mathsf{MP}(w_{2})
such that
\varphi^{\prime}\trianglerighteq\varphi
.

Proof.

By contradiction.
Let
\mathsf{MP}(w_{1})=\{\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{m}\}
and
\mathsf{MP}(w_{2})=\{\psi_{1},\ldots,\psi_{t}\}
.
Suppose there exists
\psi\in\mathsf{MP}(w_{2})
such that
\psi\trianglerighteq\varphi
for some
\varphi\in\mathsf{MP}(w_{1})
.
Given the assumption
w_{1}\succeq w_{2}
, by Definition
11
, there exists
\psi^{\prime}\in\mathsf{MP}(w_{2})
such that
\varphi\trianglerighteq\psi^{\prime}
.
As a result,
\psi\trianglerighteq\varphi\trianglerighteq\psi^{\prime}
, implying that
\psi\trianglerighteq\psi^{\prime}
. This contradicts the result in Lemma
1
which imposes
\mathsf{MP}(w_{2})
to contain only incomparable formulas.
Thus, the assumption that
\psi\trianglerighteq\varphi
is contradicted.
∎

Lemma 3
.

If
w_{1}\sim w_{2}
, then
\mathsf{MP}(w_{1})=\mathsf{MP}(w_{2})
.

Proof.

By way of contradiction, suppose
\mathsf{MP}(w_{1})\neq\mathsf{MP}(w_{2})
.
Without loss of generality, let
\varphi\in\mathsf{MP}(w_{1})\setminus\mathsf{MP}(w_{2})
.
Given
w_{1}\succeq w_{2}
and
\varphi\in\mathsf{MP}(w_{1})
, there must exist a formula
\psi\in\mathsf{MP}(w_{2})
such that
\varphi\trianglerighteq\psi
.
Also, because
w_{2}\succeq w_{1}
, there exists a formula
\phi\in\mathsf{MP}(w_{1})
such that
\psi\trianglerighteq\phi
.
Due to the transitivity of
\trianglerighteq
,
\varphi\trianglerighteq\psi\trianglerighteq\phi
and thus
\varphi\trianglerighteq\phi
and that
\varphi,\phi\in\mathsf{MP}(w_{1})
, contradicting Lemma
1
.
Since
\varphi
is chosen arbitrarily, witnessing this contradiction implies that
\mathsf{MP}(w_{1})=\mathsf{MP}(w_{2})
.
∎

Lemma 4
.

The preference model
\langle\Sigma^{\ast},\succeq\rangle
induced by
\langle\Phi,\trianglerighteq\rangle
is a preorder.

Proof.

For any
w\in\Sigma^{\ast}
,
w\succeq w
because for any
\varphi\in\mathsf{MP}(w)
,
\varphi\trianglerighteq\varphi
.
Thus,
\langle\Sigma^{\ast},\succeq\rangle
is reflexive. For the transitivity, supposing
w_{1}\trianglerighteq w_{2}
and
w_{2}\trianglerighteq w_{3}
, we need to show that
w_{1}\trianglerighteq w_{3}
.
Let for each
t\in\{1,2,3\}
,
\mathsf{MP}(w_{t})=\{\varphi_{t,i}\mid i=1,\ldots,n_{t}\}
be the most prefered formulas satisfied by
w_{t}
.
Given that
w_{1}\succeq w_{2}
, for any
\varphi_{1,i}\in\mathsf{MP}(w_{1})
, there exists
\varphi_{2,j}\in\mathsf{MP}(w_{2})
such that
\varphi_{1,i}\trianglerighteq\varphi_{2,j}
.
Also, because
w_{3}\succeq w_{3}
, for any such
\varphi_{2,j}
, there exists
\varphi_{3,k}\in\mathsf{MP}(w_{3})

such that
\varphi_{2,j}\trianglerighteq\varphi_{3,k}
.
Using the transitivity property of
\trianglerighteq
,
\varphi_{1,i}\trianglerighteq\varphi_{3,k}
holds.
As a result,
w_{1}\succeq w_{3}
.
∎

Note that the preference relation
\langle\Sigma^{\ast},\succeq\rangle
needs not to be antisymmetric since there might exist two words
w_{1}\neq w_{2}
such that
\mathsf{MP}(w_{1})=\mathsf{MP}(w_{2})
.
For example, with
\Phi=\{\Diamond\,a,\Diamond\,b\}
, consider two words
\emptyset\emptyset\{a\}\{b\}
and
\emptyset\{a\}\emptyset\{b\}
.
Since they both satisfy both
\Diamond\,a
and
\Diamond\,b
,
w_{1}\succeq w_{2}
and
w_{2}\succeq w_{1}
, while
w_{1}\neq w_{2}
, showing an example where
\succeq
is not antisymmetric.

Proof for the case where

is constructed for weak*-stochastic ordering, that is, where

For strong-stochastic ordering, we first construct for all subset

∎

Given the

Effect of Weight Vector.

The proposed synthesis approach implicitly manages a trade-off between the preference of satisfying an outcome with the likelihood that it can enforce its satisfaction given the uncertainties.
For instance, on rows

Since the probability to satisfy these conditions given the environment dynamics is less than
0.25

p_{1}
under any strategy regardless of weight is smaller than
0.25

On the other hand, consider the eighth and tenth rows in Table 2, where the subsets

Effect of Stochasticity.

As the stochasticity increases, the synthesized policy becomes more conservative, assuming the same stochastic ordering and weight vector.

Under weak-stochastic ordering with the weight vector

7
Conclusions and Future Work

In this paper, we introduced a formal language for specifying user’s partially-ordered preferences over temporal goals expressed in LTL
f
. We developed an algorithm to convert the user preference over LTL
f
formulas into an automaton with a preorder over the acceptance conditions. To synthesize a most preferred policy in a stochastic environment, we utilized stochastic ordering to translate a partially-ordered user’s preference to a preorder over probabilistic distributions over the system trajectories. This allowed us to rank the policies based on the partially-ordered user’s preference.
Leveraging the automaton structure, we proved that computing a most-preferred policy can be reduced to finding a Pareto-optimal policy in a multi-objective
mdp
augmented with the automaton states.

This work provides fundamental algorithms and principled approach for preference-based probabilistic planning with partially-ordered temporal logic objectives in stochastic systems.
A direction for future work will be to extend the planning with preference over temporal goals that are satisfied in infinite time, for instance, recurrent properties and other more general properties in temporal logic. For robotic applications, it would be of practical interest to design a conversational-AI interface that elicits human preferences and translating natural language specifications into the preference model, and thus facilitate human-on-the-loop planning.
