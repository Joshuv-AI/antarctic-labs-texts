Abstract
Simulators are vital in science and engineering, as they faithfully model the influence of design parameters on real-world observations. Optimizing design parameters to minimize a desired objective function is notoriously challenging since simulators are often non-differentiable black boxes. Moreover, each simulation incurs significant compute time, thus doing such optimizations naively may be prohibitively expensive. Furthermore, in experiment design settings, practitioners are typically required to solve the same optimization problem for various input distributions before proceeding to real-world experiments. Starting these related optimizations from scratch each time can be inefficient if the forward simulation model is expensive to evaluate. To address these challenges, this paper introduces a novel method for solving black-box optimization problems on various input distributions by learning a policy that guides the training of a differentiable surrogate. The surrogate’s gradients are then used to optimize the simulation parameters with SGD. After training the policy, the cost for downstream optimization of problems involving black-box simulators is amortized, and we require up to 10 × 10\times fewer expensive simulator calls compared to baselines such as previous local surrogate-based approaches, numerical optimization, and Bayesian optimization.

We aim to optimize the simulation parameters of a black-box simulator using stochastic gradient descent (SGD).
The black-box simulator,

\displaystyle=\argmin_{\bm{\psi}}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{L}(\bm{y})\right]

\displaystyle=\argmin_{\bm{\psi}}\int\mathcal{L}(\bm{y})\,p(\bm{y}|\bm{\psi},\bm{x})\,q(\bm{x})\,d\bm{x}\,d\bm{y}

Policy-based Black-Box Optimization

Following
Shirobokov et al. (2020)
, we also perform an iterative optimization based on the surrogate gradients obtained in
section 3
. Starting from an initial parameter
\bm{\psi}_{0}
, we may train a local surrogate for the current parameter value. To that aim, we start by sampling nearby parameter values within a so-called trust-region, a box of fixed size
2\epsilon
, centered around the current
\bm{\psi}
:
U_{\epsilon}^{\bm{\psi}}=\{\bm{\psi}^{\prime};|\bm{\psi}^{\prime}-\bm{\psi}|\leq\epsilon\}
. These are combined with input samples from
q({\bm{x}})
and noise
\bm{z}
, and the simulator
f_{\phi}(\bm{\psi},\bm{x},\bm{z})
is called to obtain the corresponding
\bm{y}
values. The surrogate is then trained on these local samples. To improve data efficiency and to allow surrogates to continuously improve in difficult regions, the samples are also stored in a history buffer
H
. Then, surrogate training for a point
\bm{\psi}
reuses all datapoints in
H
that lie within
U_{\epsilon}^{\bm{\psi}}
. The overall process required to generate new samples from the black-box simulator is what we refer to as a “
simulator call
”. Such a simulator call requires running the forward process on
10^{4}
to
10^{5}
total
(\bm{\psi},\bm{x})
tuples, depending on the precise setting.

Policy-based Approach

We propose to reduce the number of simulator calls required for an optimization run with an
RL
-based approach. Our method involves utilizing a learned policy
\pi_{\theta}
, with learnable parameters
\theta
to: i) decide whether a simulator call should be performed to retrain the local surrogate; and ii) define how to sample surrogate training data from the black-box simulator. This allows surrogates to be retrained – which involves expensive simulator calls – only when (and how) the policy chooses it. Thus, if the policy considers the current local surrogate reliable, we can use it for gradient estimation and avoid training a new local surrogate for this purpose.

States

We formalize the sequential optimization process as an episodic
MDP
(
MDP
). The state
s_{t}
at timestep
t
is given by the tuple
(\bm{\psi}_{t},t,l_{t},\sigma_{t})
, where
\bm{\psi}_{t}
is the current parameter value,
l_{t}
is the number of simulator calls already performed in the episode, and
\sigma_{t}
is some measure of uncertainty produced by the surrogate. These features allow the policy to decide based on how long the optimization has been going for and how reliable the surrogate estimates are – see Appendix
B.3
for a discussion regarding observability in the
MDP
.

Actions

Actions
a_{t}
consist of binary valued variables
b\in\{0,1\}
, sampled from a Bernoulli distribution, where
1
represents the decision to perform a simulator call. We also train policies to output, as part of the action, the trust region size
\epsilon
for sampling new values for
\bm{\psi}
. State transitions of the
MDP
are by the Adam optimizer
(
Kingma and Ba, 2014
)
, which updates the current state by doing a single step in the direction of the surrogate gradient estimate.

Reward design

Episodes end under one of three conditions:
\mathcal{A)}
the optimization reaches a parameter for which
\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{L}(\bm{y})\right]
is below the target value
\tau
(we call this
termination
- see Appendix
D.3
for details regarding the choice of
\tau
);
\mathcal{B)}
the maximum number of timesteps
T
is reached; or
\mathcal{C)}
the policy hits the available budget for simulator calls
L
. To incentivize reducing the number of simulator calls, rewards
r(s_{t},a_{t},s_{t+1})
are
0
if
b=0
and
-1
if
b=1
. In addition, a reward penalty is added when
\mathcal{B)}
or
\mathcal{C)}
occurs to promote termination. The penalty is
-(L-l_{t})-1
when
\mathcal{B)}
occurs and
-1
when
\mathcal{C)}
occurs. This ensures the sum-of-rewards for non-terminating episodes is

D.2

Determining whether the surrogate is reliable is not straightforward using the available features. To simplify this task, we give the policy an extra feature

To construct

Metrics

We report experimental results on two metrics: the Average Minimum of the Objective function (AMO) for a specific budget of simulator calls and the Average Number of simulator Calls (ANC) required to terminate an episode. The first quantity tells us the objective function value achievable as a function of simulator calls; this is an indicator of the efficacy of each simulator call. The second quantity tells us the average simulator call budget required to solve a black-box optimization problem; this indicates how well the policy leverages the surrogate during optimization. For more details, see Appendix
C.5

5.1
Benchmark Functions

We experiment with fixed and varying input distributions for each benchmark function. The latter setup solves an entire family of related optimization problems, each characterized by a different input distribution,
q({\bm{x}})
. In particular, during training of the policy and evaluation of all methods for a given simulator, each function to optimize is induced by a different input distribution. We include a short description of our benchmark functions below; more details – including those on how the input distributions vary – can be found in Appendix
C.1
and Appendix
C.3
.

Probabilistic Three Hump Problem

In the Probabilistic Three Hump problem, the goal is to find the 2-dimensional
{\bm{\psi}}^{*}
such that:
{\bm{\psi}}^{*}=\mathrm{arg\ min}_{\bm{\psi}}\ \mathbb{E}[\sigma(y-10)-\sigma(y)]
, where
\sigma
is the sigmoid function and
{\bm{y}}
, the observation vector, is sampled from a distribution that depends on
{\bm{x}}
and
{\bm{\psi}}
.
Since this function is two-dimensional in
{\bm{\psi}}
, its optimization trajectory is amenable to visualization. Figure
11
of Appendix
D.1
illustrates such a trajectory, where a fully trained policy heavily exploits the local surrogate for gradient estimation, only performing simulator calls when the model is far from the initial training location (the red square in Figure
11
denotes the initial trust region
U_{0.5}^{{\bm{\psi}}_{0}}
). Intuitively, we expect the surrogate model to provide meaningful gradients in proximity to the
{\bm{\psi}}
region where it was previously trained. As the optimization moves away from that region, we expect the quality of the gradients to decline until a simulator call is triggered and the local surrogate is re-trained. Of course, moving away from the last training region is not the only condition that may trigger a simulator call. For instance, towards the end of the trajectory, the policy calls the simulator twice to gather more data and update the surrogate. It may, for instance, have learned that a rapidly changing loss landscape should trigger a simulator call; these nuances are up to the policy to learn.

Rosenbrock Problem

The aim is to optimize
{\bm{\psi}}\in\mathbb{R}^{10}
such that:
{\bm{\psi}}^{*}=\mathrm{arg\ min}_{\bm{\psi}}\ \mathbb{E}[y]
; where
y
is given by:
y\sim\mathcal{N}(y;\ \gamma+x,\ 1)
, with
\gamma=\sum^{n-1}_{i=1}\left[(\psi_{i}-\psi_{i+1})^{2}+(1-\psi_{i})^{2}\right]
. This is a higher-dimensional benchmark function that differs in character from the Three Hump problem, allowing us to check that our design is not overoptimized to the Three Hump case.

Nonlinear Submanifold Hump Problem

This problem is formulated similarly to the Probabilistic Three Hump problem. We consider the embedding
\hat{{\bm{\psi}}}={\bm{B}}\ \mathrm{tanh}({\bm{A}}{\bm{\psi}})
, where
{\bm{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{16\times 40}
and
{\bm{B}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 16}
, of the vector
{\bm{\psi}}\in\mathbb{R}^{40}
. Subsequently,
\hat{{\bm{\psi}}}
is used in place of
{\bm{\psi}}
in the Probabilistic Three Hump problem definition. This essentially embeds the 2-dimensional surface into a 40-dimensional space, allowing us to test the policy’s performance on higher-dimensional problems.

Wireless Communication: Indoor Transmitting Antenna Placement

We study the problem of optimally placing a transmitting antenna in indoor environments to maximize the signal strength at multiple receiver locations.
Determining the signal strength in such a scenario requires a wireless ray tracer (

Optimally placing the transmit antenna is typically slow, as it amounts to naively and slowly sweeping over transmit location choices

Instead, we employ our approach to “backpropagate” through the surrogate and perform gradient descent steps on the location

Specifically, we consider two indoor scenes for this experiment and investigate how to use our approach to find an optimal transmit location that maximizes signal strength in the 3D scene (Figure

6
Conclusion

We propose a novel method for minimizing the number of
simulator calls
required to solve optimization problems involving black-box simulators using (local) surrogates. The core idea of our approach is to learn an active learning policy that controls
when
the black-box simulator is used and
how
to sample data to train the local surrogates. We describe three policy model variations and present experiments showing they outperform previous methods, including local surrogate methods
(
Shirobokov et al., 2020
)
, numerical differentiation, and Bayesian approaches
(
Oh et al., 2018
)
in a variety of setups that include benchmark functions and real-world black-box simulators. In particular, we observe a significant reduction in the number of
simulator calls
up to
10\times
. Our results suggest that local surrogate-based optimization of problems involving black-box forward processes benefits from the guidance of both simple policies and learned sampling strategies.

Appendix A
Impact statement, limitations and future work

A.1
Impact statement

This paper proposes a novel policy-based approach to guide local surrogate-based problem optimization with black-box simulators. We believe the potential societal consequences of our work are chiefly positive, as it has the potential to accelerate experiment design in various scientific domains, particularly those involving optimization procedures involving black-box, non-differentiable, forward processes. However, it is crucial to exercise caution and thoroughly comprehend the behavior of the models to obtain tangible benefits. Application of the models outside of their domain may lead to inefficiently designed real-life experiments, with all its associated costs.

A.2
Limitations and future work

Gradient-based optimization may get stuck in local optima of the loss surface
\mathbb{E}_{p(\bm{y}|\bm{\psi},\bm{x})}\left[\mathcal{L}(\bm{y})\right]
. Investigating whether introducing a policy into the optimization can help avoid such local minima is an interesting direction of future research. The Probabilistic Three Hump problem has no local minima but does contain a few flat regions, where gradient-based optimization is more challenging. Exploratory experiments have provided weak evidence that the policy may learn to avoid such regions.

Hyperparameter tuning has mostly involved reducing training variance through tuning the number of episodes used for a PPO iteration, as well as setting learning rates and the KL-threshold. Little effort has been spent optimizing the policy or surrogate architectures; we expect doing so to further improve performance. Surrogates (and thus policies) can likely be trained using fewer simulator datapoints per simulation call, which should be explored in future work. Additionally, it would be interesting to investigate whether the semi-local surrogate obtained from training
\pi_{AL}^{G}
-E can be used for simulator-less optimizations: i.e., not retraining the surrogate during optimization.

More recent algorithms for policy training may offer advantages compared to
PPO
with a value function critic, such as improved planning and off-policy learning for more data-efficient training
[
Haarnoja et al., 2018
,
Neumann et al., 2023
]
.

When the policy decides how (with what data) the surrogate should be updated, it does so using information provided by the surrogate itself. This is an example of active learning
[
Settles, 2009
]
, where the current instance of a task model (the surrogate) affects the data it sees in future training iterations. In particular, our policies are instances of
learning active learning
, where a separate model (our policy) is trained to suggest the data that the task model should be trained on
[
Hsu and Lin, 2015
,
Konyushkova et al., 2017
,
Fang et al., 2017
,
Ravi and Larochelle, 2018
,
Pang et al., 2018
,
Liu et al., 2018
,
Konyushkova et al., 2018
,
Bakker et al., 2023
]
. Progress in the field of (learning) active learning may be applicable in our setting as well.

In this work, we studied the generalization of our policies to a varying distribution of input variables
{\bm{x}}
. Another valuable setting is one in which the simulator optimization objective changes between runs, such as when doing sequential sensor placement. This is currently out of scope of our work, as properly integrating such dynamics into the policy would involve training it on such sequential optimization trajectories. However, there may be a reason to expect our policies to be somewhat robust to distribution shifts generally, namely that they only interact with the distribution shift through the surrogate uncertainty feature. Thus, if such distribution shifts do not strongly influence the relationship between surrogate uncertainty and good policy decisions, performance may be retained. Such a condition may, for instance, hold when the policy is implementing a threshold function based on surrogate uncertainty (e.g., uncertainty above a threshold leads to calling the simulator). We do not study this here, however.

The

When training a policy for downstream optimization of many related black-box optimization problems, it may be helpful to train a global surrogate simultaneously for such a problem setting. B.3
Full observability of the MDP

Because the state of our reinforcement learning framework consists of the fully observed variables
(\bm{\psi}_{t},t,l_{t},\sigma_{t})

\bm{\psi}
of a simulator is known at all times, since

\sigma_{t}
is generated using separate surrogate models. Training these surrogate models requires

\bm{x}
and
\bm{x}
are user-generated and
\bm{y}
is observed simulator output. If
\bm{\psi}
is not observed, then backpropagation through the surrogate w.r.t.
\bm{\psi}
is not possible, and our method is not applicable. However, note that – even in black-box optimisation settings – the simulator parameter settings
\bm{\psi}
are generally input values specified by the user, and thus observed.

Benchmark functions

We randomly sample the upper,
u_{x}
, and lower,
l_{x}
, bounds of the
{\bm{x}}
distribution for each optimization run. Specifically, we assume the boundaries were normally distributed and sampled their value at each run. Once we obtain the bounds, we use them to construct a uniform distribution,
q({\bm{x}})=U_{l_{x},u_{x}}
, to sample
{\bm{x}}
values. In all cases, the Normal distributions on the bounds are centered on the value used in the fixed
{\bm{x}}
-distribution setting; e.g., for the Three Hump problem, we sample
(l_{x_{1}},u_{x_{1}})
from Normals centered at -2 and 2 respectively, since
x_{1}\sim U_{-2,2}
originally. The Normals have standard deviation 0.5 for
x_{1}
and 1 for
x_{2}
in Three Hump and Submanifold Three Hump, and standard deviation 2 for Rosenbrock.

Wireless Communication: Indoor Transmitting Antenna
Placement

To simulate a varying input distribution to the simulator, we randomly sample the carrier frequency
x_{f_{c}}
passed to the ray tracer for each optimization episode. The main challenge in such a setup comes from the different behaviors of electromagnetic waves at different wavelengths. Specifically, we sampled frequencies uniformly in the
[4,8]
GHz range.

C.5
Metrics

As we mentioned in Section
5
, we use two metrics to compare our models against the baselines: the Average Minimum of the Objective function (AMO) for a specific budget of simulator calls and the Average Number of simulator Calls (ANC) required to terminate an episode. We now delve deeper into both of them. The meaning of the latter is quite straightforward. We consider the average number of simulator calls to solve the problem. We compute the average across evaluation episodes and random seeds. In contrast, the AMO is slightly less intuitive to interpret. One might question whether the value of the ANC should align with the maximum value on the
x
-axes for the AMO. In other words, assuming that for a given model, the ANC is equal to, e.g. 10,
should one expect that at a value
x=10
, the AMO will be equal to the termination value?

Generally speaking, the answer is
no
. To explain why that is the case, we can report the following example. Let us assume that, for a given model, we have the following three episodes, each characterized by a specific length and value of the objective function at each simulator call:

•

Episode 1: [20, 12, 7, 5, 3, 1]

•

Episode 2: [18, 6, 1]

•

Episode 3: [15, 5, 1]

We assumed the target value,
\tau
, to be 1. For simplicity, we used integers for the objective value. As we can see from the example, we have
\mathrm{ANC}=4
. Now, if we examine the AMO for
x=4
, we find that it is equal to 5 since only the first episode contributes to it, which is greater than
\tau
. Therefore, one cannot directly map the
x
-axis from the AMO to the
y
-axis of the ANC. Such a one-to-one mapping would exist only when all episodes always require the same number of simulator calls, which is not the case. We hope that our explanation has clarified the interpretation of the results we reported in the main corpus of the paper.

D.2
Reward Design

The reward function is chosen to incentivize the policy to reduce the number of simulator calls. This is achieved by giving a reward of -1 every time the policy opts to call the simulator, contrasting with a reward of 0 when it does not. However, with this reward function the policy could achieve maximum return (of zero) by never calling the simulator even if this leads to non-terminating episodes. An extra term is required to make any non-terminating episodes worse than any terminating one. Since the minimum return is
-L
, corresponding to the maximum number of simulator calls for an episode, that is achieved by setting a reward penalty of
-(L-l_{t})-1
whenever the episode ends for reasons other than reaching the target value
\tau
: if the simulator call budget has been exhausted, then
l_{t}=L
and the penalty is
-1
; if the timestep budget has been exhausted, then we have accumulated
-l_{t}
return already. In both cases, adding this penalty leads to a total return of
-L-1<-L
.

D.3
Termination Value

Termination values
\tau
for the Probabilistic Three Hump and Rosenbrock problems are chosen to trade-off episode length and optimization precision. Selecting values very close to the exact minimum value of the objective function
\mathcal{L}
leads to extremely long episodes, due to the stochastic nature of the optimization process. Moreover, varying the distribution of the
{\bm{x}}
variables changes the (expected) objective value minimum, such that choosing a too low value for
\tau
leads to episodes that cannot terminate even in theory. Computing the minimum of
\mathcal{L}
on the fly for the various
{\bm{x}}
-distributions is not trivial, and so we opted for a
\tau
that generally suffices for good performance across parameterizations of a given problem. These values are chosen by manually inspecting L-GSO runs.

D.4
Compute Resources

Performing a single optimization trajectory for the benchmark functions does not require a significant amount of computational resources and can be conducted using any commercially available NVIDIA GPU. A single optimization can be easily fitted on a single GPU.
On the contrary, both wireless and physics experiments require extensive computing resources for running simulations. Especially for the physics setting, although it is still feasible to run the entire optimization on a single machine, simulation takes a considerable amount of time when simulating thousands of particles. The primary bottleneck for such experiments is the Geant4
[
Agostinelli and others, 2003
]
simulator, which is highly CPU-demanding. Since the simulations of individual particles are independent of each other, they can be run in parallel without communication between processes. In our experiments, we split up each simulation into chunks of 2000 particles, which resulted in run times of 5-15 minutes per simulation on single CPU core, depending on the exact hardware.

D.5
Number of simulations

Since our main idea is to train a policy for efficient optimization of downstream tasks, it is natural to ask whether the training costs are worth the efficiency gains. In our setup, we aim to minimize the number of simulator calls, since these are often the most expensive part of simulator optimization. Here, we provide an empirical upper bound on the number of ‘simulator calls’ (as defined in section 4 of the main text) for the wireless and physics settings. Our policy is trained for a number of iterations
K
, using
G
episodes per iteration, each of which use at most
L
simulator calls (following the notation of Algorithm 1). Thus, we will at most employ
KGL
simulator calls for policy training. In practice, we do not need the full
K
iterations to train the policy (e.g., see how the sum of rewards stabilizes in Figure 12), and episodes will not use the full
L
simulator calls (as shown by the ANC metric);
KGL
is an upper bound. For the particle physics setting, we use
K=5
,
G=8
, and
L=30
, which equates to 1200 simulator calls to train the policy. Each simulator call involves a (parallel) computation of
5\cdot 10^{4}
events, so training the policy amounts to
6\cdot 10^{7}
events. This is more than
10\times
less than the
10^{9}
reported for optimizing a
single input distribution
by
Massoli [2015]
,
Baranov et al. [2017a]
. Training a policy seems to be very worthwhile in this setting. For the wireless setting, we originally use
K=20
,
G=10
, and
L=50
, which amounts to
10^{4}
simulator calls. Each call involves
3000
parallel events, so
3\cdot 10^{7}
events total. These numbers were chosen for expedience and are not optimized. Inspecting our training runs, we have noticed that the policy only requires (on average) 10 simulator calls for termination in the wireless setting. With
L=15
the number of total calls drops to
3000
. The policy converges rapidly and reaches its final performance already after only 10 iterations. Setting
K=10
further reduces the number of simulator calls to 1500. Table 1 shows that LGSO needs 23 simulator calls to optimize this setting, and our policies need 7 (
\pi
-E) or 8 (
\pi_{AL}
-E). Thus, the break-even point is at around
1500/(23-8)=100
optimizations.
