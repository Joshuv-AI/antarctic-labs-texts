Transporting Higher-Order Quadrature Rules
Ilja Klebanov, T. J. Sullivan ,

Abstract
Abstract. Integration against, and hence sampling from, high-dimensional probability distributions is of essential importance in many application areas and has been an active research area for decades. One approach that has drawn increasing attention in recent years has been the generation of samples from a target distribution ℙ tar \mathbb{P}_{\textup{tar}} using transport maps: if ℙ tar = T ♯ ​ ℙ ref \mathbb{P}_{\textup{tar}}=T_{\sharp}\mathbb{P}_{\textup{ref}} is the pushforward of an easily-sampled probability distribution ℙ ref \mathbb{P}_{\textup{ref}} under the transport map T T , then the application of T T to ℙ ref \mathbb{P}_{\textup{ref}} -distributed samples yields ℙ tar \mathbb{P}_{\textup{tar}} -distributed samples. This paper proposes the application of transport maps not just to random samples, but also to quasi-Monte Carlo points, higher-order nets, and sparse grids in order for the transformed samples to inherit the original convergence rates that are often better than N − 1 / 2 N^{-1/2} , N N being the number of samples/quadrature nodes. Our main result is the derivation of an explicit transport map for the case that ℙ tar \mathbb{P}_{\textup{tar}} is a mixture of simple distributions, e.g. a Gaussian mixture, in which case application of the transport map T T requires the solution of an explicit ODE with closed-form right-hand side. Mixture distributions are of particular applicability and interest since many methods proceed by first approximating ℙ tar \mathbb{P}_{\textup{tar}} by a mixture and then sampling from that mixture (often using importance reweighting). Hence, this paper allows for the sampling step to provide a better convergence rate than N − 1 / 2 N^{-1/2} for all such methods. Keywords. Introduction

When estimating the expected value
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{\textup{tar}}}[f]\coloneqq\int f(y)\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}_{\textup{tar}}(y)
of some function
f\in L^{1}(\mathbb{P}_{\textup{tar}})
with respect to some target probability distribution
\mathbb{P}_{\textup{tar}}
, which is a crucial task in many areas of applied mathematics and statistics such as Bayesian inference, one often faces one or more of the following challenges:

•

high dimension: for a given desired level of accuracy, the number of grid points required by classical quadrature rules grows exponentially with the dimension (‘curse of dimensionality’), rendering such methods infeasible;

•

multimodal distributions
\mathbb{P}_{\textup{tar}}
that cannot be well approximated by more simple distributions such as a normal distribution;

•

the probability density of
\mathbb{P}_{\textup{tar}}
is only given up to an unknown normalisation constant.

By far the most widely used methodologies that can tackle these issues are Monte Carlo and Markov chain Monte Carlo methods, which approximate the expected value by an empirical mean or ergodic average

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{\textup{tar}}}[f]\approx\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}f(Y_{n}).

(1.1)

In the case of ‘vanilla’ Monte Carlo, the points
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{N}
are independent and identically distributed with law
\mathbb{P}_{\textup{tar}}
, if
\mathbb{P}_{\textup{tar}}
is simple enough to draw direct samples from it;
in the case of Markov chain Monte Carlo,
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{N}
form a Markov chain with asymptotic distribution
\mathbb{P}_{\textup{tar}}
as
N\to\infty
.
In both cases, the central limit theorem (CLT) and ergodicity arguments guarantee that the approximation error
\left\lvert\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{\textup{tar}}}[f]-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}f(Y_{n})\right\rvert
has a convergence rate of
N^{-1/2}
(see e.g.
Meyn and Tweedie 2009
), independently of the dimension
d
— although, via the variance of the integrand
f
,
d
may still appear implicitly as a premultiplier of the convergence rate.
While this breaks the curse of dimensionality, the convergence is still rather slow, particularly in applications where
f
is costly to evaluate.
Under suitable regularity assumptions on
f
, there exist several well-known alternatives to Monte Carlo methods with better convergence rates:

•

quasi-Monte Carlo methods
(
Niederreiter 1992
;
Fang and Wang 1994
;
Caflisch 1998
;
Dick et al. 2013
)
;

•

higher-order digital nets
(
Dick and Pillichshammer 2010
)
;

•

sparse grids (SG;
Smolyak 1963
;
Zenger 1991
;
Gerstner and Griebel 1998
).

We will refer to these types of point sequences as
higher-order points
.
However, such point sequences have only been constructed for very few, simple distributions
\mathbb{P}_{\textup{tar}}
such as uniform distributions on the cube,
\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{U}}=\Unif([0,1]^{d})
, and (standard) normal distributions,
\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{N}}=\mathcal{N}(0,\Id_{d})
.
In this paper, we suggest constructing
higher-order points
for other distributions by the application of transport maps, an approach already suggested for Monte Carlo samples as an alternative to Markov chain Monte Carlo
(
El Moselhy and Marzouk 2012
;
Marzouk et al. 2016
;
Parno and Marzouk 2018
)
:
If a sequence
X_{1},\dots,X_{N}
with the desired convergence rate can be constructed for a simple distribution
\mathbb{P}_{\textup{ref}}
and
T
is a measurable map such that
T_{\sharp}\mathbb{P}_{\textup{ref}}=\mathbb{P}_{\textup{tar}}
, then the sequence given by
Y_{n}\coloneqq T(X_{n})
will inherit this convergence rate for the distribution
\mathbb{P}_{\textup{tar}}
.
The reason for this is the change of variables formula,

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{\textup{tar}}}[f(Y)]=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{\textup{ref}}}[f\circ T],\qquad\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}f(Y_{n})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}f\circ T(X_{n}),

(1.2)

which provides a
dual viewpoint
on the transport idea:
Integrating the function
f
against a complicated distribution
\mathbb{P}_{\textup{tar}}
, which we try to rewrite as the pushforward of a simple distribution
\mathbb{P}_{\textup{ref}}
under a transport map
T
, is equivalent to integrating the transformed function
f\circ T
against the simple distribution
\mathbb{P}_{\textup{ref}}

(and similarly, applying the function
f
to transformed points
Y_{n}=T(X_{n})
is equivalent to applying the transformed function
f\circ T
to the original points
Y_{n}
, which really is a tautology), cf.
Figure

5.1
.
This changes the task of sampling the target distribution into the task of constructing a suitable transport map, which is typically extremely challenging, especially in high dimensions.
A further note of caution is called for:
The regularity assumptions needed for establishing the convergence rate of quadrature using the points
X_{n}
now have to be verified for the function
f\circ T
instead of
f
, which may modify the class of functions
\mathcal{C}
to which such methods are applicable;
cf.
Section

3
).
This paper does not attempt to give a comprehensive answer to this issue.
However, in most practical applications, there is no reason to presume that it is more likely for
\mathcal{C}
to contain
f
rather than
f\circ T
(unless one is interested in very specific quantities of interest, such as the mean or (co-)variance of
\mathbb{P}_{\textup{tar}}
, in which case
f
is a polynomial).

This paper’s approach to the construction of transport maps is based on ordinary differential equations and the corresponding continuity equations:
The initial
\mathbb{P}_{\textup{ref}}
-distributed points are transported to
\mathbb{P}_{\textup{tar}}
-distributed points by a flow map
\Phi_{t}
of an ordinary differential equation with appropriately chosen right-hand side, which guarantees that the final flow map at
t=1
defines a transport map from
\mathbb{P}_{\textup{ref}}
to
\mathbb{P}_{\textup{tar}}
.
While this idea has a long history
(
Moser 1965
;
Dacorogna and Moser 1990
)
with several recent implementations
(
Heng et al. 2021
;
Reich 2011
;
Reich 2019
;
Liu 2017
)
, to the best of our knowledge it has not yet been applied to higher-order points.

The main contribution of this paper is the derivation of an ordinary differential equation with
analytic
right-hand side for the case that
\mathbb{P}_{\textup{tar}}
is a mixture of simple distributions such that its flow map
\Phi_{t}
for
t=1
is an exact transport map from
\mathbb{P}_{\textup{ref}}
to
\mathbb{P}_{\textup{tar}}
.
This case should by no means be seen as a toy example.
Due to their flexibility, mixture modes have proven themselves extremely useful in a variety of applications in e.g. machine learning and data analysis
(
Bishop 2006
;
McLachlan et al. 2019
)
.
While most practical distributions (e.g. Bayesian posteriors) are not mixtures, many methodologies concentrate on approximating them by mixture distributions.
Such methods include

•

variational inference
(
Blei et al. 2017
)
;

•

all variants of layered adaptive importance sampling (LAIS;
Martino et al. 2017
;
Bugallo et al. 2017
;
Martino et al. 2017
);

•

a variety of kernel methods including kernel herding
(
Chen et al. 2010
;
Lacoste-Julien et al. 2015
)
.

Subsequently, expected values with respect to the original distribution can be estimated by empirical means of samples from the mixture, possibly using importance reweighting (
Rubinstein and Kroese 2016
, Section 5.7
;
Robert and Casella 2004
, Section 3.3
) in order to correct for the approximation error.
Again, mixture distributions are favorable as importance sampling distributions due to their flexibility to approximate e.g. multimodal distributions.
It appears lucrative to perform the second step using (transported) higher-order points in place of random samples in order to obtain better convergence rates.
We demonstrate this procedure by performing layered adaptive importance sampling with transported quasi-Monte Carlo points in
Section

6.2
.
Furthermore, this approach extends naturally to copulas of mixture distributions
(
Gunawan et al. 2021
)
.

Remark 1.1
.

One might object that an easier approach to obtain the desired convergence rate is to apply quasi-Monte Carlo or sparse grids to each mixture component
separately
as sketched in
Algorithm

3
below.
In fact, such methods are the topic of current research
(
Cui et al. 2023
)
and work well for small and moderate numbers of mixture components.
Note, however, that
the affordable budget
N
of quadrature points might be comparable or even smaller than the number of mixture components
J
, in which case the convergence rate of the above approach appears pointless, cf.
Figure

6.2
.

The paper is structured as follows.
After commenting on related work in
Section

2
,
Section

3
discusses the transportation of point sets for numerical quadrature and reviews how the convergence rates of such quadrature schemes are assessed.

After giving some brief preliminaries on the construction of transport maps in
Section

4
, in
Section

5
we discuss how higher-order points from a mixture of simple distributions can be produced by means of transport.
In particular, we introduce and compare the performance of transported Monte Carlo, transported QMC, and transported SG.
Using the example of layered adaptive importance sampling, we show how this procedure can be combined with several state-of-the-art inference methods to yield practically relevant methodologies.

Section

6
illustrates our approach on a suite of numerical test cases with some implementation details given in
Section

7
.
We test both transported QMC on its own, and transported QMC within layered adaptive importance sampling, against an extensive suite of integrands with dimensions
d
varying from
2
to
50
.

Section

8
gives some closing remarks, and some technical supporting results can be found in
Appendix

A
.

Related Work

Prior to this work, surprisingly few papers have addressed the application of transport maps to quasi-Monte Carlo sequences.

Basu and Owen 2016
considered such transport maps in the case where both
\mathbb{P}_{\textup{ref}}
and
\mathbb{P}_{\textup{tar}}
are uniform distributions on different but fairly simple domains, such as a simplex or a sphere.
Their focus lies on building low discrepancy sequences on domains
\mathcal{X}
different from the unit cube, not to represent given probability distributions in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Kuo et al. 2010
and
Nichols and Kuo 2014
analyse a transport map from the uniform distribution on a cube to a product measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, such as the standard normal distribution, cf.
Example

3.4
.
The intricate analysis of the class of functions to which such transported quasi-Monte Carlo sequences are applicable, which only holds true for this rather simple class of distributions (in particular, due to the product structure, the corresponding transport map acts in a coordinatewise manner), shows how difficult this step turns out to be.
An algorithm for the transport of QMC points to mixture distributions has recently been suggested by
Cui et al. 2023
.
While their approach to treat each mixture component separately works for a small number
J
of mixture components (compared to the number
N
of evaluation points), it is likely to fail when
J
is of the order of (or even larger than)
N
, in which case a genuine (non-componentwise) transport map to the mixture appears necessary.

To the best of our knowledge, the transport of sparse grids to other probability distributions has been discussed only in special cases in dimension
d=3
by
Rodríguez et al. 2008
for the approximation of certain integrals arising in computational chemistry.

The idea of using flow maps of suitably constructed ordinary differential equations as transport maps in order to sample from some target distribution is not new.

Reich 2011
,
Daum and Huang 2012
and
Heng et al. 2021
suggested several approaches that are typically summarised as
particle flow methods
.
Further algorithms can be viewed as time-discretised versions of such particle flows, moving from a tractable distribution to some target distribution via a sequence of intermediate distributions.
These include
sequential Monte Carlo methods

(
Del Moral 2004
;
Del Moral et al. 2006
)
,
annealed importance sampling

(
Neal 2001
)
, and so-called
particle-based variational inference

(
Liu et al. 2019
;
Wang and Li 2022
;
Chen et al. 2018a
;
Chen et al. 2018b
;
Chen et al. 2019
)
, with Stein variational gradient descent (SVGD;
Liu and Wang 2016
;
Liu 2017
;
Duncan et al. 2023
) as its arguably most prominent method.
However, most of particle flow methods involve some sort of interaction between the particles, which might break the delicate structure of higher-order points and eliminate the desired gain in convergence rate.
This might be the reason why such methods have not yet been applied to higher-order points.
Note that the ODE we construct in this paper involves no particle interactions (in fact, each point can be transported separately), hence such issues do not arise in our case.

3
Transporting Points for Numerical Quadrature

Definition 3.1
.

Let
(\Omega_{0},\mathcal{A}_{0})
and
(\Omega_{1},\mathcal{A}_{1})
be measurable spaces.
The
pushforward
of a probability measure
\mathbb{P}_{0}
on
(\Omega_{0},\mathcal{A}_{0})
through a measurable map
T\colon\Omega_{0}\to\Omega_{1}
is the probability measure
T_{\sharp}\mathbb{P}_{0}
on
(\Omega_{1},\mathcal{A}_{1})
defined by

(T_{\sharp}\mathbb{P}_{0})(B)\coloneqq\mathbb{P}_{0}(T^{-1}(B))\quad\text{for each $B\in\mathcal{A}_{1}$.}

(3.1)

We also call the map
T
a
transport map
from
\mathbb{P}_{0}
to
\mathbb{P}_{1}
when
\mathbb{P}_{1}
is the pushforward of
\mathbb{P}_{0}
under
T
, i.e. when
T_{\sharp}\mathbb{P}_{0}=\mathbb{P}_{1}
or
T_{\sharp}\colon\mathbb{P}_{0}\mapsto\mathbb{P}_{1}
.

Whenever
\Omega_{0}
(resp.
\Omega_{1}
) is a Borel-measurable subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{A}_{0}
(resp.
\mathcal{A}_{1}
) is assumed to be the corresponding Borel
\sigma
-algebra.

Remark 3.2
.

As a consequence, if
X
is a
\mathbb{P}_{0}
-distributed random variable,
X\sim\mathbb{P}_{0}
, and
T
is a transport map from
\mathbb{P}_{0}
to
\mathbb{P}_{1}
, then
Y\coloneqq T(X)
is
\mathbb{P}_{1}
-distributed,
Y\sim\mathbb{P}_{1}
.

Remark 3.3
.

This paper focusses on
deterministic
transport maps
T
from
\mathbb{P}_{0}
to
\mathbb{P}_{1}
.
There is also significant interest in
stochastic
transports, i.e. in transition kernels
\kappa\colon\Omega_{0}\times\mathcal{A}_{1}\to[0,1]
such that

(\kappa_{\sharp}\mathbb{P}_{0})(B)\coloneqq\int_{\Omega_{0}}\int_{B}\kappa(\omega_{0},\mathrm{d}\omega_{1})\,\mathbb{P}_{0}(\mathrm{d}\omega_{0})\quad\text{for each $B\in\mathcal{A}_{1}$}

(3.2)

satisfies
\kappa_{\sharp}\mathbb{P}_{0}=\mathbb{P}_{1}
.
(The deterministic case is the special case
\kappa(\omega_{0},\mathrm{d}\omega_{1})=\delta_{T(\omega_{0})}(\mathrm{d}\omega_{1})
, where
\delta_{a}
denotes the unit Dirac measure centred at
a
.)

Reich 2019
offers a review of such methods in the context of Bayesian data assimilation.

If
\Omega_{0},\Omega_{1}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
are Borel subspaces and
d=1
, then a transport map
T
with
T_{\sharp}\colon\mathbb{P}_{0}\mapsto\mathbb{P}_{1}
can be defined using the cumulative distribution functions
F_{0}
of
\mathbb{P}_{0}
and
F_{1}
of
\mathbb{P}_{1}
via
T\coloneqq F_{1}^{-1}\circ F_{0}
, where
F_{1}^{-1}(x)\coloneqq\inf\{y\in\mathbb{R}\mid F_{1}(y)\geqslant x\}
, an approach known as the inverse transform method
(
Rubinstein and Kroese 2016
, Chapter 2.3.1)
.
In dimension
d>1
, a similar construction can be performed which is called the Knothe–Rosenblatt rearrangement
(
Villani 2009
, Chapter 1)
, but it becomes computationally infeasible in high dimension except for certain special cases.
One such case occurs when
\mathbb{P}_{\textup{ref}}
and
\mathbb{P}_{\textup{tar}}
factorise (that is, they are product measures of one-dimensional distributions) and the inverse transform method can be applied in each dimension separately, as in the following classical example:

Example 3.4
.

Let
\mathbb{P}_{0}\coloneqq\Unif((0,1)^{d})
and
\mathbb{P}_{1}\coloneqq\mathcal{N}(0,\Id_{d})
.
The transformation
T^{\mathcal{N}}\colon(0,1)^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
given by

T^{\mathcal{N}}(x)\coloneqq\big(\sqrt{2}\,\erf^{-1}\left(2x_{j}-1\right)\big)_{j=1,\dots,d}

(3.3)

is a transport map from
\mathbb{P}_{0}
to
\mathbb{P}_{1}
, where
\erf(z)\coloneqq\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{0}^{z}e^{-t^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}t
is the error function and
\erf^{-1}
is its inverse.
The action of
T^{\mathcal{N}}
on an equidistant grid is illustrated in
Figure

3.1
.

Figure 3.1
:
In order to illustrate the transformation
T^{\mathcal{N}}
from (
3.3
) in
Example

3.4
, we apply it to an equidistant grid on
(0,1)^{2}
.
(Since
T^{\mathcal{N}}
is defined on the open cube
(0,1)^{d}
, there must be no grid points on the boundary. Therefore, the grid is chosen as
G\times G
, where
G\coloneqq\varepsilon+\{(1-2\varepsilon)k/(K-1)\mid k=0,\dots,K-1\}
, with
K=15
and
\varepsilon=10^{-3}
.)

For more complicated distributions, e.g. Bayesian posteriors in high dimension, transport maps often have to be approximated, which we discuss in
Section

4
.
In
Section

5
we will construct transport maps to certain mixture distributions.

As indicated in
Remark

3.2
, a transport map
T
from
\mathbb{P}_{0}
to
\mathbb{P}_{1}
can be used to generate
\mathbb{P}_{1}
-distributed samples once we are able to sample from
\mathbb{P}_{0}
.
When
X_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textsc{iid}}}{{\sim}}\mathbb{P}_{0}
for
n\in\mathbb{N}
, it follows that
Y_{n}\coloneqq T(X_{n})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textsc{iid}}}{{\sim}}\mathbb{P}_{1}
.
Similarly to independent samples, one can consider transporting other point sequences such as sparse grids and quasi-Monte Carlo points, in order to obtain a better convergence rate of certain numerical methods, e.g. quadrature.
We first give a general definition of convergence rates of (sequences of) deterministic or stochastic quadrature rules:

Definition 3.5
.

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
,
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a domain in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, let
(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\mathbb{P})
be a probability space, let
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{\Omega}
be a class of functions on
\Omega
, let
\mathfrak{N}=(N_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
be an increasing sequence in
\mathbb{N}
and let
\mathfrak{w}=(w^{(N_{k})})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of weight vectors
w^{(N_{k})}=(w_{n}^{(N_{k})})_{n=1,\dots,N_{k}}
(i.e.
w_{n}^{(N_{k})}\in\mathbb{R}
with
\sum_{n=1}^{N_{k}}w_{n}^{(N_{k})}=1
for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
).
We say that a sequence
\mathfrak{X}=(X^{(N_{k})})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
of families
X^{(N_{k})}=(X_{n}^{(N_{k})})_{n=1,\dots,N_{k}}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where
X_{n}^{(N_{k})}
can either be deterministic points or random variables, has
(\mathbb{P},\mathcal{C},\mathfrak{N},\mathfrak{w})
-convergence rate

r\colon\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}
, if, for any
f\in\mathcal{C}
,

\left\lvert\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\mathbb{P}}[f(X)]-\sum_{n=1}^{N_{k}}w_{n}^{(N_{k})}f(X_{n}^{(N_{k})})\right\rvert=\mathcal{O}_{p}(r(N_{k})).

(3.4)

In this case, we will refer to
\mathfrak{X}
as
higher-order points with distribution
\mathbb{P}
and
(\mathbb{P},\mathcal{C},\mathfrak{N},\mathfrak{w})
-convergence rate
r
.

Remark 3.6
.

To simplify notation, we will often drop the
\mathfrak{N}
dependence of the convergence rate and write
N
in place of
N_{k}
as though
(
3.4
)
would hold for any
N\in\mathbb{N}
.
This is, however, an abuse of notation since the most advantageous performance of a quadrature rule is often attained only along specific sequences
\mathfrak{N}
, quasi-Monte Carlo quadrature rules being a good example of this phenomenon
(
Owen 2022
)
as are sparse gridss, the sizes of which result from the specific constructions after choosing a certain ‘level’
(
Novak and Ritter 1996
)
.
Furthermore, we will often write
X_{n}
and
w_{n}
instead of
X_{n}^{(N_{k})}
and
w_{n}^{(N_{k})}
, respectively (in the case of quasi-Monte Carlo points,
X_{n}^{(N_{k})}
is, in fact, independent of
N_{k}
).
Finally, we will often omit the prefix
(\mathbb{P},\mathcal{C},\mathfrak{N},\mathfrak{w})
- if it is clear which quantities are being considered.

By the central limit theorem, MC samples are therefore higher-order points with rate
r(N)=N^{-1/2}
.
Remarkably, this holds for the class
\mathcal{C}=L^{2}(\mathbb{P})
of all
\mathbb{P}
-square-integrable functions and independently of the dimension
d
.
The convergence rates for sparse grids and QMC points are also well established and there are various results for different types of constructions. Here are two classical examples:

Example 3.7

(Convergence rate for sparse grids)
.

Consider
\Omega=[-1,1]^{d}
,
\mathbb{P}=\Unif(\Omega)
and the class of functions of regularity
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}
,

\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{F}_{d}^{\alpha}(\Omega)\coloneqq\{f\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{R}\mid D^{\beta}f\text{ is continuous if $\beta_{i}\leqslant\alpha$ for all }i\}.

(3.5)

Then the sparse grid
A(q,d)
of
Novak and Ritter 1996
(see also
Wasilkowski and Wozniakowski 1995
) has the following convergence rate
(
Novak and Ritter 1996
, Corollary 1)
:

r(N)=N^{-\alpha}(\log N)^{(d-1)(\alpha+1)}.

(3.6)

Here, the sequences
\mathfrak{w}
and
\mathfrak{N}
of weight vectors and point numbers are defined by the corresponding sparse grid construction.

Similar convergence rates hold for sparse grids designed for other product measures
\mathbb{P}
on possibly unbounded domains
\Omega
, e.g. the sparse grids based on Gauss–Hermite points when
\mathbb{P}
is a standard normal distribution
(
Novak and Ritter 1997
;
Novak et al. 1999
;
Novak and Ritter 1999
)
.

Example 3.8

(Convergence rate for quasi-Monte Carlo)
.

Considering
\Omega=[0,1]^{d}
,
\mathbb{P}=\Unif(\Omega)
and the class of functions with a bounded variation in the sense of Hardy and Krause
(
Owen 2005
)
,
\mathcal{C}=\BVHK(\Omega)
, the
d
-dimensional Halton sequence has the following convergence rate
(
Caflisch 1998
, Section 5.6)
:

r(N)=N^{-1}(\log N)^{d}.

(3.7)

Here, the weights are uniform and
\mathfrak{N}=\mathbb{N}
.

Remark 3.9
.

More recent QMC constructions, such as (randomly shifted) lattice rules, rely on weighted Sobolev and Korobov spaces acting as reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces rather than on function classes of bounded variation;
see e.g.
Dick et al. 2013
for an overview.
Similar to
Example

3.8
, convergence rates of almost
N^{-1}
can be obtained in these cases;
see
Dick et al. 2013
for corresponding results.

The following simple proposition justifies our approach of transporting higher-order points:

Proposition 3.10

(Transfer of convergence rate)
.

Let
(\Omega_{0},\mathcal{A}_{0},\mathbb{P}_{0})
and
(\Omega_{1},\mathcal{A}_{1},\mathbb{P}_{1})
be two probability spaces,

Remark 3.11
.

Note that we make no statement about the properties of the class of functions
\mathcal{C}_{T}
.
In the case of sparse grids with
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{F}_{d}^{\alpha}(\Omega_{0})
as in (

), if

is a

-diffeomorphism with

, then

(

, Section 7.4, Corollary 5)
.
In the case of QMC and
\mathcal{C}=\BVHK(\Omega_{0})
it is much harder to find suitable conditions on

for

to hold.
Partial answers are given by

and

.
For certain product measures on

,
Kuo et al. 2010
and

discuss how the resulting function class

can be characterised for the corresponding transport map

, which in this case acts in a coordinatewise manner similar to

in

.
The sophisticated derivations in these papers suggest that the analysis of the function class
\mathcal{C}_{T}
for the more complicated (in particular, non-coordinatewise) transport maps established in this manuscript justifies a separate paper.
It remains an open problem for future research.

Remark 3.12
.

In most of our examples,
\mathbb{P}_{\textup{tar}}
will be a Gaussian mixture defined on the whole Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, which is not homeomorphic to a closed cube.
While for sparse grids there exist constructions based on Gauss–Hermite points which guarantee convergence rates similar to (

) on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, one has to be particularly careful when working with QMC constructions based on
\BVHK([0,1]^{d})
.
However, more recent QMC approaches mentioned in

3.9
, such as shifted lattice rules based on certain Sobolev spaces rather than on
\BVHK

(0,1)^{d}
in place of the closed one.

4
Construction of Transport Maps: Background

Explicit transport maps from
\mathbb{P}_{\textup{ref}}
to
\mathbb{P}_{\textup{tar}}
can only be constructed in very special cases such as the one discussed in
Example

3.4
.
In most other cases, numerical approximations are necessary.
There are several approaches to the construction of approximate transport maps.
One is to define a finite-dimensional class of possible maps and to optimise a certain functional with respect to the parameters
(
El Moselhy and Marzouk 2012
;
Marzouk et al. 2016
)
.
Another approach, which we are going to focus on in this manuscript, is the construction of transport maps via ordinary differential equations.
The idea is to define an ordinary differential equation

\dot{x}(t)=v_{t}(x(t)),\qquad t\in[0,1],

(4.1)

with flow maps
\Phi^{t}
,
t\in[0,1]
, such that
\Phi^{1}
is the desired transport map from
\mathbb{P}_{\textup{ref}}
to
\mathbb{P}_{\textup{tar}}
.
If
x(0)=x_{0}\sim\mathbb{P}_{\textup{ref}}
, then consequently
x(1)=\Phi^{1}(x_{0})\sim\mathbb{P}_{\textup{tar}}
by
Remark

3.2
.

For this purpose, a typical strategy is to define a family of ‘intermediate’ probability densities
(\rho_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
with
\rho_{0}=\rho_{\textup{ref}}
and
\rho_{1}=\rho_{\textup{tar}}
, e.g. via
\rho_{t}\propto\rho_{\textup{ref}}^{1-t}\,\rho_{\textup{tar}}^{t}
, and find a family of velocity fields
(v_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
that satisfies the continuity equation

\partial_{t}\rho_{t}=-\diver(\rho_{t}v_{t}),

(4.2)

which guarantees that
x(t)
has probability density
\rho_{t}
for each
t\in[0,1]
, in particular
\Phi_{\sharp}^{1}\mathbb{P}_{\textup{ref}}=\mathbb{P}_{\textup{tar}}
.
See
Ambrosio 2008
and
Ambrosio et al. 2008
for a precise statement and proof of the continuity equation and its consequences.

The main difficulty of this strategy, which has been implemented by e.g.
Heng et al. 2021
, lies in approximating the velocity fields
v_{t}
that solve the continuity equation (
4.2
), especially in high dimension.
In
Section

5
we will construct an explicit formula for
v_{t}
for certain mixture distributions
\mathbb{P}_{\textup{tar}}
and (intermediate) probability densities
(\rho_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
.

Remark 4.1
.

There is another class of methods that construct an ordinary differential equation with the property
\Phi_{\sharp}^{\tau}\mathbb{P}_{\textup{ref}}=\mathbb{P}_{\textup{tar}}
for either
\tau=1
or
\tau=\infty
, without (a priori) defining intermediate probability densities
(\rho_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
.
Instead, the velocity field
v_{t}
is chosen in such a way that
\rho_{t}
gets closer to
\rho_{\textup{tar}}
in some metric, i.e.
x(t)
is ‘pushed’ in the ‘right’ direction.
This approach comes with its own challenges such as obtaining some knowledge about the current probability density
\rho_{t}
, with Stein variational gradient descent (SVGD;
Liu and Wang 2016
) providing one elegant solution.
Typically, the constructed ODE is solved for a large number of particles simultaneously and requires some form of particle interaction, which can break the delicate structure of many higher-order point constructions.
The present paper does not follow this approach.

5.1
Mixture Distributions with Negative Weights

In certain situations, probability densities are approximated by mixture distributions with weights

that are not necessarily positive.
Such approximations arise e.g. in the context of reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces, the conditional density operator of
(

)

Note that the proof of

remains legitimate for arbitrary weights as long as we can guarantee

However, it does hold in the case of Gaussian densities

G[m,C](x)\coloneqq\frac{|\det C|^{-1/2}}{(2\pi)^{d/2}}\exp\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}(x-m)^{\top}C^{-1}(x-m)\right),

if we do not allow scaling, i.e.
A_{j}=\Id_{d}
for all
j=1,\dots,J

Proposition 5.6
.

Let
\rho_{\textup{ref}}=G[0,C]
be a centered Gaussian density on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with covariance matrix
C
and let
\rho_{\textup{tar}}
be given by (
5.1
) with
A_{j}=\Id_{d}
for all
j=1,\dots,J
.
If
\rho_{\textup{tar}}
is strictly positive, then the densities

\rho_{t}=\sum_{j=1}^{J}w_{j}\rho_{j,t},\qquad\rho_{j,t}\coloneqq\rho_{\textup{ref}}(x-ta_{j}),\qquad t\in[0,1],

are strictly positive for all
t\in[0,1]
.

Proof.
If
\rho_{\textup{tar}}
is positive, then so are the densities

g_{t}(x)\coloneqq t^{-d}\rho_{\textup{tar}}\left(t^{-1}x\right)=\sum_{j=1}^{J}w_{j}G[ta_{j},t^{2}C](x),\qquad t\in[0,1].

Hence,

\rho_{t}=\sum_{j=1}^{J}w_{j}\rho_{j,t}=\sum_{j=1}^{J}w_{j}G[ta_{j},C]=g_{t}\ast G[0,(1-t^{2})C]

is also positive for each
t\in[0,1]
.

\blacksquare

6.2
Transported quasi-Monte Carlo Points within layered adaptive importance sampling

As mentioned in the introduction, the transport to mixture distributions should not be viewed as a toy example since it can be combined with various state-of-the-art importance sampling algorithms.
In this section, we demonstrate its practical importance by combining TQMC with layered adaptive importance sampling (LAIS;
Martino et al. 2017
).
layered adaptive importance sampling and its various versions
(
Martino et al. 2017
;
Bugallo et al. 2017
;
Martino et al. 2017
)
are powerful and up-to-date Monte Carlo methods that build an approximation to the target distribution
\mathbb{P}_{\textup{tar}}
by a mixture distribution in a first step (‘upper layer’), typically by running one or more Markov chains, and using it as an importance sampling distribution (
Rubinstein and Kroese 2016
, Section 5.7
;
Robert and Casella 2004
, Section 3.3
) in a second step (‘lower layer’).

Leaving the upper layer untouched, we can modify the sampling of the lower layer by replacing the random samples with transported higher-order points.
To ensure a comparison fair to layered adaptive importance sampling, we illustrate the benefits of this modification on a multimodal two-dimensional target distribution, visualised in
Figure

6.3
, that corresponds exactly to the one used by
Martino et al. 2017
, as do the Markov chains used in the upper layer and the importance sampling distribution built from them.
Further, we run layered adaptive importance sampling using the ‘full deterministic mixture approach’
(
Martino et al. 2017
)
described below and from now on abbreviated by DM-layered adaptive importance sampling, which gives the best results in terms of the variance of the estimator at the price of a higher computational cost, thus getting the best out of layered adaptive importance sampling.

Figure 6.3
:
Target density used by
Martino et al. 2017
, together with the centers
m_{i}
and DM-LAIS samples
z_{n}
(left) compared to transported QMC-layered adaptive importance sampling points
\tilde{z}_{n}
(right).
As expected, the transported QMC-layered adaptive importance sampling points are more evenly spaced, resulting in a higher convergence rate of the corresponding Monte Carlo estimator, as shown in
Figure

6.4
.

DM-layered adaptive importance sampling is based on the samples
m_{1},\dots,m_{CT}
produced by
C\in\mathbb{N}
parallel Metropolis–Hastings chains that are run for
T\in\mathbb{N}
time steps (upper layer).
These samples are then used to build the importance sampling density
\hat{\rho}=(CT)^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{CT}q(\hbox to5.71527pt{\hss$\cdot$\hss}|m_{i})
, where
q(\hbox to5.71527pt{\hss$\cdot$\hss}|m_{i})
are easily sampled probability density functions (oftentimes these are chosen to coincide with the proposal densities
(
Schuster and Klebanov 2021
)
; the reader may think of Gaussian kernels centered at
m_{i}
).
Hereafter,
M\in\mathbb{N}
independent and identically distributed samples are produced from each of the components
q(\hbox to5.71527pt{\hss$\cdot$\hss}|m_{i})
, resulting in a so-called
stratified
sampling
(z_{1},\dots,z_{CTM})
from
\hat{\rho}

(
Rubinstein and Kroese 2016
, Chapter 5.5)
, which, compared to independent samples from
\hat{\rho}
produced by
Algorithm

1
, result in a smaller variance of the Monte Carlo estimator
(
Rubinstein and Kroese 2016
, Proposition 5.5.1)
.
These samples are then used in a standard (self-normalised) importance sampling scheme to approximate the expected value
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{\textup{tar}}}[f]
of some function
f\in L^{1}(\mathbb{P}_{\textup{tar}})
by

\hat{S}^{\textup{snIS}}[f]=\frac{\sum_{n=1}^{CTM}\omega_{n}f(z_{n})}{\sum_{n=1}^{CTM}\omega_{n}},\qquad\omega_{n}=\frac{\rho_{\textup{tar}}(z_{n})}{\hat{\rho}(z_{n})}.

We suggest to replace the samples
z_{n}
by transported QMC points
\tilde{z}_{n}
transported to the mixture distribution
\hat{\rho}
as in
Theorem

5.1
(
Algorithm

2
) and term the resulting algorithm transported QMC-layered adaptive importance sampling.
Naturally, for this to work we require
\hat{\rho}
to be of the form
Equation

5.1
, i.e. the densities
q(\hbox to5.71527pt{\hss$\cdot$\hss}|m_{i})
have to be scaled and shifted copies of
\rho_{\textup{ref}}
.
While any other higher-order points can be used, we employed quasi-Monte Carlo points as a proof of concept.
For practical applications with extremely long Markov chains, it is meaningful to
thin
the chains
(
Owen 2017
;
Riabiz et al. 2022
)
in order to keep the number of mixtures (and thereby the computational cost) manageable.

We make two comparisons between DM-layered adaptive importance sampling and transported QMC-layered adaptive importance sampling.
First, we fix the number of chains
C=10
and number of time steps
T=20
and vary the number of samples per mixture component
M
.
Second, we fix the number of chains
C=10
and number of samples per mixture component
M=100
and vary the number of time steps
T
.
In both cases we plot the approximation error
\|\hat{S}^{\textup{snIS}}[f]-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{\textup{tar}}}[f]\|
for
f(x)=x
over the total number of samples
N=CTM
, illustrated in
Figure

6.4
.
We observe a higher convergence rate of transported QMC-layered adaptive importance sampling compared to DM-layered adaptive importance sampling.

8
Conclusion and Outlook

In this paper, we have derived an explicit transport map
T\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
from some probability distribution
\mathbb{P}_{\textup{ref}}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
to a mixture
\mathbb{P}_{\textup{tar}}
of scaled and shifted versions of
\mathbb{P}_{\textup{ref}}
(
Theorem

5.1
and
Algorithm

2
).
By ‘explicit’ we mean that it is the solution of an ODE with
analytic
right-hand side.
This transport map can be used to transport higher-order points and thereby quadrature rules previously established for
\mathbb{P}_{\textup{ref}}
to such a mixture, allowing for super-root-
N
convergence of the corresponding empirical mean towards the analytic mean
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{\textup{tar}}}[f]\coloneqq\int f(y)\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}_{\textup{tar}}(y)
for certain integrands
f
.

While most distributions in practice, such as Bayesian posteriors, are not mixture distributions, many methodologies approximate the distribution of interest by such a mixture, e.g. layered adaptive importance sampling as well as certain versions of variational inference, such as variational boosting.
Hence, combining the above approach with such methods by using an importance sampling estimator allows to approximate expected values with respect to more complicated target distributions with improved convergence rates.

Since the right-hand side of the constructed ODE is explicit,
T
can be evaluated for each point separately, without any particle interactions, that are typical for most particle flow methods.
Hence, our approach is trivially parallelisable and can be easily extended to adaptive schemes, such as adaptive sparse grids.

We demonstrated the advantages of our approach for a simple mixture of three Gaussian distributions in dimension
d=2
and performed an extensive study over different dimensions
d=2,5,20,50
and for various numbers of mixture components.
In addition, we showed how LAIS can benefit from using transported higher-order points in place of random samples in the sampling step (’lower layer’).

In order to establish the theory for transported higher-order points, one crucial step remains as an open problem, namely characterisation of the class
\mathcal{C}_{T}
of admissible integrands
f
.
As argued in
Remark

3.11
, the corresponding detailed analysis goes beyond the scope of this paper.

Lemma A.1
.

Let
X\sim\mathbb{P}_{X}
, where
\mathbb{P}_{X}
is a probability distribution with density
\rho_{X}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0}
, and
a\in\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0}^{d}
,
A\in\GL(d,\mathbb{R})
, with
\GL(d,\mathbb{R})
denoting the
d
-dimensional general linear group.
Then
Y=AX+a
has the probability density

\rho_{Y}(y)=\left\lvert\det A\right\rvert^{-1}\rho_{X}(A^{-1}(y-a)).

Proof.
This is simply the linear change of variables formula for probability densities.

\blacksquare

Theorem A.2

(
Ambrosio et al. 2008
)
.

Let
(\mathbb{P}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
,
T>0
, be a narrowly continuous family of Borel probability measures on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d\in\mathbb{N}
, solving the continuity equation
\partial_{t}\mathbb{P}_{t}=-\diver(v_{t}\mathbb{P}_{t})
on
(0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
with respect to a Borel vector field
v_{t}
satisfying

(i)

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\lVert v_{t}(x)\right\rVert\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}_{t}\,\mathrm{d}t<\infty

(ii)

\int_{0}^{T}\sup_{B}\left\lVert v_{t}\right\rVert+\mathrm{Lip}(v_{t},B)\,\mathrm{d}t<\infty

for every compact
B\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

where
\mathrm{Lip}(f,A)
denotes a Lipschitz constant of the function or vector field
f
in the set
A
.
Then, for
\mathbb{P}_{0}
-almost every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the characteristic system

\tfrac{\partial}{\partial t}\Phi_{t}(x)=v_{t}(\Phi_{t}(x)),\qquad\Phi_{0}(x)=x,

admits a globally-defined solution
(\Phi_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
and, for each
t\in[0,T]
,
\mathbb{P}_{t}=(\Phi_{t})_{\sharp}\mathbb{P}_{0}
.

Proof of
Theorem

5.1
.

Ambrosio et al. 2008
show that
(\mathbb{P}_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
is a narrowly continuous family of Borel probability measures.
We will now show that it solves the continuity equation.
First note that, by a technical but straightforward calculation, each family
(\rho_{j,t})_{t\in[0,1]}
solves the continuity equation

\partial_{t}\rho_{j,t}=-\diver(\rho_{j,t}v_{j,t}),\qquad\rho_{j,0}=\rho_{\textup{ref}},\qquad t\in[0,1],\ j=1,\dots,J.

(A.1)

See
Remark

A.3
for an alternative justification of (
A.1
).
Therefore, by linearity of differentiation, the family of probability densities
(\rho_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
solves the continuity equation

\partial_{t}\rho_{t}=\partial_{t}\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{J}w_{j}\rho_{j,t}\Big)=-\diver\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{J}w_{j}\rho_{j,t}v_{j,t}\Big)=-\diver(\rho_{t}v_{t}),\qquad\rho_{0}=\rho_{\textup{ref}},\qquad t\in[0,1].

Now let us verify conditions
(i)
and
(ii)
on the vector field
v_{t}
from
Theorem

A.2
.
Using the assumption on the first moment of
\mathbb{P}_{\textup{ref}}
and the change of variables
z=A_{j,t}^{-1}(x-ta_{j})
, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\lVert v_{t}(x)\right\rVert\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}_{t}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{j=1}^{J}w_{j}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\rho_{j,t}(x)\,v_{j,t}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{J}w_{j}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\rho_{\textup{ref}}(z)\,\left\lVert a_{j}+(A_{j}-\Id_{d})z\right\rVert\,\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{j=1}^{J}w_{j}\big(\left\lVert a_{j}\right\rVert+\left\lVert A_{j}-\Id_{d}\right\rVert M\big).

Since the bound on the right-hand side is independent of
t\in[0,1]
, this proves
(i)
.
In order to prove
(ii)
, fix a compact subset
B\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
and consider the function

V\colon[0,1]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d},\qquad V(t,x)=v_{t}(x),

which is continuously differentiable by construction.
Hence, it attains its maximum on
[0,1]\times B
, i.e.
\hat{V}\coloneqq\sup_{[0,1]\times B}V<\infty
, and, by the mean value theorem,
\hat{L}\coloneqq\mathrm{Lip}(V,[0,1]\times B)<\infty
.
It follows that
\int_{0}^{1}\sup_{B}\left\lVert v_{t}\right\rVert+\mathrm{Lip}(v_{t},B)\,\mathrm{d}t\leqslant\hat{V}+\hat{L}<\infty
, proving
(ii)
.
Since
\rho_{0}=\rho_{\textup{ref}}
and
\rho_{1}=\rho_{\textup{tar}}
,
Theorem

A.2
proves the claim.

\blacksquare

Remark A.3
.

In place of the technical verification of (
A.1
), we can give the following much more intuitive justification:
For fixed

and
