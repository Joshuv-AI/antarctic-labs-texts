Stability of Weyl node merging processes under symmetry constraints
Gabriele Naselli, György Frank, Dániel Varjas, Ion Cosma Fulga, Gergő Pintér, András Pályi, Viktor Könye

Abstract
Changes in the number of Weyl nodes in Weyl semimetals occur through merging processes, usually involving a pair of oppositely charged nodes. More complicated processes involving multiple Weyl nodes are also possible, but they typically require fine tuning and are thus less stable. In this work, we study how symmetries affect the allowed merging processes and their stability, focusing on the combination of a two-fold rotation and time-reversal ( C 2 ​ 𝒯 C_{2}\mathcal{T} ) symmetry. We find that, counter-intuitively, processes involving a merging of three nodes are more generic than processes involving only two nodes. Our work suggests that multi-Weyl-merging may be observed in a large variety of quantum materials, and we discuss SrSi 2 \text{SrSi}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}} and bilayer graphene as potential candidates.



Introduction —

Weyl semimetals are a class of gapless topological materials with robust two-fold band crossing points called
Weyl points
(or nodes) in their Brillouin zone (BZ)
Nielsen and Ninomiya 1981
;
Murakami 2007
;
Wan et al. 2011
;

;

;

;

;

Weyl points are characterized by a linear low energy dispersion relation, and have
\pm 1

topological charge
(often referred to as chirality) given by the value of the Chern number calculated for a surface enclosing them.

Since the topological charge in a region of momentum space with gapped spectrum on its boundary is a conserved quantity, isolated Weyl points cannot be gapped, but can only be moved or tilted by a small perturbation

;
Soluyanov et al. 2015
.
For larger perturbations though, it is possible to annihilate two Weyl points with opposite charges
Volovik 2018
.
The phenomenon of pairwise creation and annihilation has been thoroughly investigated in the presence of various perturbations, including disorder

;
Roy et al. 2018
;
Pixley et al. 2016
;
Pixley and Wilson 2021
, magnetic fields, changes in magnetization
Zhang et al. 2017
;
Cano et al. 2017
;
Ghimire et al. 2019
;
Ray et al. 2022
, strain

;
Liu and Vanderbilt 2014
;
Slager et al. 2016
;
Facio et al. 2018
, phonons
Singh et al. 2020
or high-frequency illumination
Fu et al. 2017
;
Fu et al. 2019
.

Motivated by the prediction of merging processes involving three Weyl nodes (two positive and one negative)

;
Guba et al. 2023
in
\text{MoTe}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}

Tamai et al. 2016
, we are interested in studying the general stability conditions of various merging processes in the presence of
C_{2}\mathcal{T}
symmetry – the combination between a two-fold rotation (
C_{2}
) and time-reversal (
\mathcal{T}
) symmetry.
Thus, we expect our work will be relevant to a large class of quantum materials, including time-reversal invariant Weyl semimetals which host at least one two-fold rotation axis, but also magnetic systems in which
C_{2}\mathcal{T}
symmetry is preserved even though
C_{2}
and
\mathcal{T}

Adding symmetry constrains to conventional Weyl semimetals not only stabilizes higher-charge multi-Weyl points

, but it also restricts the possible positions and charges of the Weyl points in the BZ.
With

symmetry, the configuration of Weyl points in the BZ is symmetric with respect to a
C_{2}\mathcal{T}
-invariant plane normal to the
C_{2}
axis, which we take to be the
k_{y}=0
momentum plane. The symmetry-related Weyl nodes at momenta
\mathbf{k}=(k_{x},\pm k_{y},k_{z})
have the same topological charge, and unpaired Weyl points are only allowed in the invariant plane and can only leave the plane by meeting another node of the same charge forming a pair with zero Euler class
Ahn et al. 2019
;
Bouhon et al. 2020

Consider processes in which the position of Weyl cones changes as a function of some control parameter,
t
. A pair of same-charge Weyl nodes start outside the
C_{2}\mathcal{T}
-invariant plane, and end as two (symmetry-unrelated) nodes in the plane.
One way such a process can occur is by moving the two nodes into the plane, forming a charge-two double-Weyl node, which then splits into two Weyl nodes within the plane.
This we call the
two-node process
, see Fig.
1
(a).
There is, however, a different option: Before the nodes reach the
k_{y}=0
plane, a pair creation occurs in the plane.
The two original nodes merge with the oppositely charged member of the created pair, forming a charge 1 cubic Weyl point, which then becomes one in-plane Weyl point, while the other final node is the other member of the pair.
This we call the
three-node process
, see Fig.
1
(b).

We will show that, contrary to the naive expectation, the three-node process is stable, while the two-node process is not, explaining why only the former was observed in the numerical studies of
\text{MoTe}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
.
First we present an intuitive pictorial argument through Fig.
1
.
The processes are represented by “Weyl world-lines” in the
(t,k_{x},k_{y})
space (restricting all nodes to
k_{z}=0
without loss of generality) by two parabolas that touch at a single point where the nodes enter the plane.
In order to get a two-node process the parabolas must touch at their extremal points, while any other touching results in a three-node process, meaning that the two-node process requires fine tuning.

In this work we employ the mathematical framework of
contact equivalence

Mond and Nuño-Ballesteros 2020
;
Teramoto et al. 2017

to prove the above statement about the stability of merging processes with
C_{2}\mathcal{T}
symmetry.
Stable processes are of experimental interest, as they can be accessed by only varying a single control parameter.
Then, we consider whether it is possible to observe both types of processes in the same system, without fine-tuning.
The answer lies in adding further symmetries, which make the two-node process stable, and can be broken by perturbations to produce the three-node process.
We showcase two physical examples, using simplified tight-binding models of
\text{SrSi}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
and bilayer graphene.

Setting up the problem —

In the following, we refer to the crystal quasimomenta
\mathbf{k}
as

and call any further parameters such as magnetic field or strain as

The phase diagram divides the control parameter space into different regions, distinguished by the number and charges of Weyl points.
Generically, the borders between different regions correspond to pairwise creation and annihilation processes
where an unstable
quadratic Weyl point
is formed which has zero topological charge (neutral) and its dispersion is quadratic in one direction.

stable

\mathbf{t}

For fixed parameter values, we use the notation
\mathbf{d}_{\mathbf{t}}(\mathbf{k})

\mathbf{d}_{\mathbf{t}}(\mathbf{k})=\mathbf{0}

We only consider processes that are local in
\mathbf{k}

More precisely, we only consider a compact

To formalize the intuitive definition, that Hamiltonians are qualitatively the same when they only differ, for example, in the locations and velocities of the Weyl nodes, we introduce the concept of
contact equivalence
(or
\mathcal{K}
-equivalence)
Mond and Nuño-Ballesteros 2020

Loosely speaking, we consider Hamiltonians equivalent if they can be transformed into each other by a smooth local reparametrization of
\mathbf{k}
-space and a smooth local
\mathbf{k}
-dependent reparametrization of the
\mathbf{d}
-space (inducing a deformation of the Hamiltonian that does not introduce new gap closings).
Introducing this equivalence relation has the advantage that it reduces the infinite-dimensional space of smooth functions
\mathbf{d}(\mathbf{k})
to a function space which is finite-dimensional in our examples.
For precise definitions see App.
A
and the Supplemental Material (SM), Sec. I

A useful concept we will use is the
codimension
of degeneracy points with respect to the contact equivalence.
Intuitively, it gives the number of parameters that have to be fine-tuned in order to find a given degeneracy point.
A codimension 0 degeneracy point is called
stable
. The stable degeneracy points in this sense are exactly the Weyl points.
For a higher, but finite codimension degeneracy point, it is possible to construct a
versal unfolding

\mathbf{d}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{t})
of
\mathbf{d}_{0}(\mathbf{k})=\mathbf{d}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{0})
, where the space of control parameters
\mathbf{t}
has the same dimension as the codimension of
\mathbf{d}_{0}(\mathbf{k})
(for details see App.
B
).
The family of degeneracy point configurations given by
\mathbf{d}_{\mathbf{t}}(\mathbf{k})
covers all the possible perturbations of
\mathbf{d}_{0}(\mathbf{k})
up to contact equivalence, enabling us to make highly general statements using models with a finite number of parameters.
We refer to a specific choice of the versal unfolding
\mathbf{d}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{t})
as a
normal form
of the degeneracy point.

A single Weyl node, for example, has the normal form
\mathbf{d}(\mathbf{k})=(k_{x},k_{y},k_{z})
, where no tuning parameters appear, it being a stable (codimension 0) configuration.
As a second example, consider the neutral quadratic Weyl node, with normal form
\mathbf{d}(\mathbf{k},t)=(k_{x}^{2}+t,k_{y},k_{z})
.
The degeneracy point of
\mathbf{d}_{0}(\mathbf{k})
has codimension 1, and can be accessed by tuning only one parameter.
For
t>0
a gap opens, while for
t<0
the degeneracy point splits into two oppositely charged Weyl nodes, realizing the creation/annihilation process.

We call a merging process
stable
, if it connects two stable configurations, and does not pass through any configurations with codimension greater than 1, this guarantees that a small perturbation to the path in control parameter space does not change which configurations appear along the path.

Merging processes in
{}\mathrm{SrSi}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}

The three-node process has been theoretically predicted in the

\text{MoTe}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}

As we argued above, the two-node process is unstable, explaining why only the three-node process was observed in a realistic model.
This raises the question, whether it is possible to observe the two-node process without fine-tuning parameters.

We propose the Weyl semimetal candidate
\text{SrSi}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}

Merging processes in bilayer graphene —
As a second example, we show the possibility of observing two- and three-node merging processes with the Dirac cones of bilayer graphene, that are analogous to Weyl cones in Weyl semimetals.

Conclusion —

We have shown that merging processes of multiple Weyl nodes can be observed in Weyl semimetals with
C_{2}\mathcal{T}
symmetry.
Counter-intuitively, the simultaneous merging of three Weyl nodes is generic, while two-node processes and the formation of double-Weyl nodes are unstable, since they require either additional symmetry constraints or fine tuning of the Hamiltonian parameters.

We used the Weyl semimetal candidate
\text{SrSi}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
as a case study.
By using an effective tight-binding model we have shown that it is possible to reduce the symmetry of the system to
C_{2}\mathcal{T}
and to observe a stable three-node process using strain and magnetic field.
Since
C_{2}\mathcal{T}
-symmetric Weyl semimetals are ubiquitous in the literature
Tamai et al. 2016
;
Xu et al. 2018
;
Koepernik et al. 2016
;
Weng et al. 2016
;
Chen et al. 2022
;
Chen et al. 2023
, it could be possible to observe the same type of merging in other materials under the right conditions.
For example, a two Weyl node merging process was predicted in ReO
3

Chen et al. 2023
in the presence of strain.
By adding perturbations which lower the three
C_{2}\mathcal{T}
symmetries to only one, it could be possible to observe triple Weyl merging processes. Furthermore, since the three node process requires only a local
C_{2}\mathcal{T}
symmetry, similar merging processes could be observed in systems with a broader set of symmetries away from high symmetry points.

We have shown that similar processes can be realized in two-dimensional chiral Dirac semimetals in the presence of
M\mathcal{T}
symmetry, since the symmetry constraints are analogous to the three-dimensional case.
This can be seen by applying strain to bilayer graphene
Mucha-Kruczyński et al. 2011
;
Mucha-Kruczyński et al. 2011
;
Son et al. 2011
;
Mariani et al. 2012
;
Varlet et al. 2015
. In the presence of a skew hopping and strain, the Dirac cones will generically enter or exit the
M\mathcal{T}
symmetry axis by creating a new pair of Dirac cones and undergoing a stable three-point process.

Our results also have applications beyond the study of topologically-protected band degeneracies in semiemtals.
For instance, they can be used to explore merging processes between analog Weyl points in meta-materials, such as interacting spin systems
Scherübl et al. 2019
;
Frank et al. 2020
, multiterminal Josephson junctions
Van Heck et al. 2014
;
Riwar et al. 2016
and spring systems
Guba et al. 2023
, and between other topological points, e.g magnetic Bloch points in spin systems
Tapia et al. 2024
;
Kuchkin et al. 2024
.

In this paper, only the presence of a single
C_{2}\mathcal{T}
symmetry was considered. The formalism presented here can be applied to study other lattice symmetries, providing a foundation for exploring merging processes involving multiple Weyl nodes.

Appendix A
Definitions for contact equivalence of degeneracy points

We treat isolated two-fold degeneracy points in three-dimensional band-structures.
We consider traceless
2\times 2
Hamiltonians, as any Hamiltonian can be smoothly deformed into a traceless one by introducing a
\mathbf{k}
-dependent energy shift.
The restriction to
2\times 2
Hamiltonians is without loss of generality: Starting from a larger Hamiltonian that has a twofold degeneracy point, we can perform quasi-degenerate perturbation theory restricted to the two bands involved in the crossing, and construct an effective
2\times 2
Hamiltonian in this subspace.
As long as these two bands are separated from all other bands by a finite gap in some vicinity of the degeneracy point, small perturbations of the full Hamiltonian change the effective Hamiltonian continuously, and cannot create degeneracies between these bands and any others.

Hence the degeneracy points are studied by writing

H(\mathbf{k})={\bf\it\sigma}\cdot\mathbf{d}(\mathbf{k}),

(4)

and analyzing the
\mathbf{d}:\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
function.
As we are only interested in properties in the vicinity of the degeneracy point, which we fix to be at
\mathbf{k=0}
, we treat
\mathbf{d}
as a
map germ
, i.e. an equivalence class of functions that are equal in an open neighborhood of
\mathbf{0}
(for example a ball
|\mathbf{k}|<\epsilon
with fixed
\epsilon
).
We denote by
\mathbf{d}:(\mathbb{R}^{3},\mathbf{0})\to(\mathbb{R}^{3},\mathbf{d}(\mathbf{0}))
the germ
\mathbf{d}
at
\mathbf{0}
.
Since we consider degerenacy points, we only consider germs with
\mathbf{d}(\mathbf{0})=\mathbf{0}
.

We call two Hamiltonians described by the map germs
\mathbf{d}(\mathbf{k})
and
\mathbf{d}^{\prime}(\mathbf{k})
contact equivalent (
\mathcal{K}
-equivalent), if there exists a germ of diffeomorphism
\Psi:(\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3},0)\to(\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3},0)
in the special form
\Psi(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{d})=({\bf\it\phi}(\mathbf{k}),{\bf\it\psi}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{d}))
such that
{\bf\it\phi}:(\mathbb{R}^{3},0)\to(\mathbb{R}^{3},0)
is a germ of diffeomorphism with
{\bf\it\phi}(\mathbf{0})=\mathbf{0}
, and
{\bf\it\psi}(\mathbf{k},\cdot):\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
are locally defined
\mathbf{k}
-dependent diffeomorphisms with
{\bf\it\psi}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{0})=\mathbf{0}
, and

\mathbf{d}^{\prime}(\mathbf{k})={\bf\it\psi}\left(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{d}\!\left({\bf\it\phi}\left(\mathbf{k}\right)\right)\right).

(5)

Intuitively,

{\bf\it\phi}
is a smooth and invertible reparametrization of
\mathbf{k}
-space, while
{\bf\it\psi}
is a smooth and invertible,
\mathbf{k}
-dependent reparametrization of the space of Hamiltonians (
\mathbf{d}
-space).
We get equivalent results if we restrict to
{\bf\it\psi}
’s that are linear in their second argument
(
Mond and Nuño-Ballesteros 2020
, Prop. 4.2.)
:

{\bf\it\psi}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{d})=A(\mathbf{k})\mathbf{d},

(6)

where
A
is a smooth map germ taking values from invertible
3\times 3
matrices,
A:\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\textnormal{GL}(3,\mathbb{R})
.
(Note that
\mathbf{d}
without an explicit
\mathbf{k}
-dependence simply denotes a variable in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, not a map germ.)
With this, the contact equivalence condition reads

\mathbf{d}^{\prime}(\mathbf{k})=A(\mathbf{k})\mathbf{d}\!\left({\bf\it\phi}\left(\mathbf{k}\right)\right).

(7)

Following
(
Mond and Nuño-Ballesteros 2020
, Lemma 4.1.)
(see also Ref.
Teramoto et al. 2017
) we investigate infinitesimal
\mathcal{K}
-equivalence transformations, and construct the tangent space corresponding to transformations that do not leave the orbit of
\mathcal{K}
-equivalence.
Consider a one-parameter family of transformations given by
A_{t}(\mathbf{k})
and
{\bf\it\phi}_{t}(\mathbf{k})
where
t\in\mathbb{R}
, and
A_{0}(\mathbf{k})=\mathbbm{1}
and
{\bf\it\phi}_{0}(\mathbf{k})=\mathbf{k}
corresponding to the identity transformation.
Note that by definition
{\bf\it\phi}_{t}
fixes the origin, i.e.,
{\bf\it\phi}_{t}(\mathbf{0})=\mathbf{0}
holds for every
t
.
An infinitesimal transformation on
\mathbf{d}(\mathbf{k})
reads

\displaystyle\left.\frac{d}{dt}\mathbf{d}_{t}(\mathbf{k})\right|_{t=0}

\displaystyle=\left.\frac{d}{dt}\left[A_{t}(\mathbf{k})\mathbf{d}_{0}\!\left({\bf\it\phi}_{t}\left(\mathbf{k}\right)\right)\right]\right|_{t=0}

(8)

\displaystyle=B(\mathbf{k})\mathbf{d}_{0}(\mathbf{k})+J(\mathbf{k})\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{k}),

(9)

where

\displaystyle B(\mathbf{k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left.\frac{d}{dt}A_{t}(\mathbf{k})\right|_{t=0},

\displaystyle\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left.\frac{d}{dt}{\bf\it\phi}_{t}(\mathbf{k})\right|_{t=0},

(10)

\displaystyle J(\mathbf{k})_{ij}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\partial d_{0i}}{\partial k_{j}}(\mathbf{k}).

The functions
B(\mathbf{k})
and
\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{k})
are arbitrary matrix and vector-valued smooth functions (power series) with
\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{0})=\mathbf{0}
and
J(\mathbf{k})
is the Jacobian of
\mathbf{d}(\mathbf{k})
.
All the infinitesimal transformations that can be written in the form of (
8
) remain at first order in the orbit of
\mathcal{K}
-equivalence, forming the
\mathcal{K}
-tangent space.
Power series that cannot be represented as (
8
) form
transverse perturbations
of
\mathbf{d}_{0}
, which leave the orbit of
\mathcal{K}
-equivalence.
The space of transverse perturbations fixing the origin is formed by the quotient of the space of all power series with zero constant term, with the space of power series of the form (
8
). The dimension of the quotient space is called the
\mathcal{K}
-codimension of the germ
\mathbf{d}(\mathbf{k})
.

However, we want to consider perturbations which possibly move the origin.
For it we need the extended tangent space (
\mathcal{K}_{e}
-tangent space), defined in the same way as the right side of (
8
), but without the restriction
\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{0})=\mathbf{0}
.
Hence we allow for perturbed functions with
\mathbf{d}_{\mathbf{t}}(\mathbf{0})\neq\mathbf{0}
for
\mathbf{t}\neq\mathbf{0}
.
The space of all perturbations transverse to the extended tangent space is formed by the quotient of the space of all power series (with possibly non-zero constant term) with the space of power series of the form (
8
) (possibly with
\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{0})\neq\mathbf{0}
).
The dimension of the quotient space is called the
\mathcal{K}_{e}
-codimension of the germ
\mathbf{d}(\mathbf{k})
, the subscript refers to ‘extended’.

We demonstrate the calculation of the
\mathcal{K}_{e}
-codimension and transverse perturbations using elementary techniques in Sec. I of the SM
SM
.
In general this calculation can be carried out in the following way:
First we show that all vector valued monomials above a certain degree can be generated by a suitable choice of
B(\mathbf{k})
and
\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{k})
.
In the next step, we restrict
B(\mathbf{k})
and
\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{k})
to be polynomials of this maximal order, restricting them to a finite dimensional subspace of the vector and matrix valued polynomial ring.
Then we evaluate (
8
) for a set of basis functions in this space, and expand the result (also restricted to this finite dimensional subspace) in a basis, resulting in a matrix equation between the free coefficients in
B(\mathbf{k})
and
\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{k})
and the coefficients of the resulting
\frac{d}{dt}\mathbf{d}_{t}(\mathbf{k})
.
Calculating the rank deficiency of this matrix (the dimension of its null space) gives the codimension, and a basis of the complement of its image yields the transverse perturbations.

Appendix B
Unfoldings and versality

Perturbations or deformations of germs are formulated via the notion of
unfoldings
. An unfolding of
\mathbf{d}(\mathbf{k})
is a germ in form
\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{t})=(\mathbf{d}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{t}),\mathbf{t})
, where
\mathbf{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
for some
n
and
\mathbf{d}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{t}=\mathbf{0})=\mathbf{d}(\mathbf{k})
. Its first component
\mathbf{d}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{t})
is the perturbation or deformation corresponding to the control parameter
\mathbf{t}
.
Note that for a fixed
\mathbf{t}
value, the perturbation
\mathbf{k}\mapsto\mathbf{d}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{t})
is a map of
\mathbf{k}
, but not a germ. However,
\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{t})
is a germ thanks to the inclusion of the trivial
\mathbf{t}
component.
We fix a representative map of the unfolding
\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{t})
with a sufficiently small neighborhood
|\mathbf{k}|<\epsilon
and
|\mathbf{t}|<\delta
, such that
\mathbf{d}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{t})=0
has no solutions with
|\mathbf{t}|<\delta
and
|\mathbf{k}|=\epsilon
, i.e., the gap does not close on the boundary of the
\epsilon
-ball in the
\mathbf{k}
-space. This makes the analysis shown in Fig.
2
in the main text precise.

An unfolding
\mathbf{D}_{v}\!\left(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{t}\right)=(\mathbf{d}_{v}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{t}),\mathbf{t})
(
\mathbf{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
) is
versal
(more precisely,
\mathcal{K}
-versal), if every other unfolding can be induced from it up to contact equivalence: given any unfolding
\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{t})
(
\mathbf{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
), there exists a base-change function (pull-back)
\mathbf{h}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
such that

\widetilde{\mathbf{D}}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{t}):=(\mathbf{d}_{v}\!\left(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{h}(\mathbf{t})),\mathbf{t}\right)

(11)

agrees with
\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{t})
up to
contact equivalence of unfoldings
, see Ref.
Wall 1981
;
Damon 1984
for a precise definition. It is also
miniversal
, if there exists no versal unfolding with a lower number of parameters.

By the
unfolding theorem

Wall 1981
;
Damon 1984
, a miniversal unfolding can be constructed as follows:
First we choose a basis for perturbations
\mathbf{e}_{i}(\mathbf{k})
transverse to the
\mathcal{K}_{e}
-tangent space as described in the previous section.
The number of basis elements is the
\mathcal{K}_{e}
-codimension of
\mathbf{d}(\mathbf{k})
by definition.
Adding the basis elements to
\mathbf{d}(\mathbf{k})
with arbitrary parameters (
\mathbf{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
where
n
equals the codimension), we get an unfolding:

\mathbf{D}_{v}\!\left(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{t}\right)=\left(\mathbf{d}(\mathbf{k})+\sum_{i=1}^{n}t_{i}\mathbf{e}_{i}(\mathbf{k}),\mathbf{t}\right).

(12)

The unfolding theorem states that this unfolding is miniversal.

Appendix C
Symmetry constraints

We modify the above construction of the tangent space, transverse perturbations, and unfoldings to be applicable in the presence of symmetries, by restricting to the subspace of functions that respect the prescribed symmetries.

A general symmetry poses a restricion on
\mathbf{d}(\mathbf{k})
of the form

V\mathbf{d}(\mathbf{k})=\mathbf{d}(R\mathbf{k}),

(13)

where
V
and
R
are
3\times 3
orthogonal matrices acting in
\mathbf{d}
and
\mathbf{k}
space respectively.
We demand that the contact equivalence transformation of Eq. (
7
) preserves this symmetry, which results in the constraints on the symmetry-compatible transformations described by
A(\mathbf{k})
and
{\bf\it\phi}\!\left(\mathbf{k}\right)
:

\displaystyle VA(\mathbf{k})=

\displaystyle A(R\mathbf{k})V,

(14)

\displaystyle R\,{\bf\it\phi}\!\left(\mathbf{k}\right)=

\displaystyle{\bf\it\phi}\!\left(R\mathbf{k}\right).

(15)

Constraints of the same form also apply to
B(\mathbf{k})
and
\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{k})
respectively.
The equivalence defined by such symmetry-restricted transformations is called
equivariant contact equivalence
.
The computation of the codimension and normal form follows the same procedure as without symmetry, except that the spaces of available functions are restricted.

Although in our examples these restrictions are straightforward (see Sec. II of the SM
SM
), for the general theory we refer to Ref.
Roberts 1985
,
Wall 1985
.
Note that this restriction guarantees that
any
symmetric
\mathbf{d}(\mathbf{k})
is mapped to a symmetric one by the allowed contact equivalence transformations.
There are cases where two symmetric
\mathbf{d}(\mathbf{k})
’s are connected by an unrestricted contact equivalence transformation, but not by a restricted one.
We give a non-trivial example of this situation in Sec. II of the SM
SM
.
In the specific case of of
C^{y}_{2}\mathcal{T}
symmetry introduced in the main text, the function
\mathbf{d}(\mathbf{k})
is restricted as:

\displaystyle d_{x}(k_{x},-k_{y},k_{z})

\displaystyle=d_{x}(k_{x},k_{y},k_{z})

\displaystyle d_{y}(k_{x},-k_{y},k_{z})

\displaystyle=-d_{y}(k_{x},k_{y},k_{z})

(16)

\displaystyle d_{z}(k_{x},-k_{y},k_{z})

\displaystyle=d_{z}(k_{x},k_{y},k_{z}).

In the notation above this corresponds to
V=R=\operatorname{diag}(1,-1,1)
.
