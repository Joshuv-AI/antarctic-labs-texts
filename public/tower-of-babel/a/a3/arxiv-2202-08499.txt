The Complexity of SPEs in Mean-payoff Games
Léonard Brice, Jean-François Raskin, Marie van den Bogaard

Abstract
We establish that the subgame perfect equilibrium (SPE) threshold problem for mean-payoff games is 𝐍𝐏 \mathbf{NP} -complete. While the SPE threshold problem was recently shown to be decidable (in doubly exponential time) and 𝐍𝐏 \mathbf{NP} -hard, its exact worst case complexity was left open.

Introduction

Nash equilibria (NEs), a fundamental solution concept in game theory, are defined as strategy profiles such that no player can improve their payoff by changing unilaterally their strategy. So NEs can be interpreted as self-inforcing contracts from which there is no incentive to deviate unilaterally. Unfortunately, NEs are known to suffer, in sequential games like infinite duration games played on graphs, from the issue of
non-credible threats
: to enforce a NE, some players may threaten other players to play irrationally in order to punish deviations. This is allowed by the definition of NEs, as in case of deviation from one player, the other players are not bound to rational behaviors anymore and they can therefore play irrationally w.r.t. their own objectives in order to sanction the deviating player.
This drawback of NEs has triggered the introduction of the notion of
subgame-perfect equilibria
(SPEs)
[
21
]
, a more complex but more natural solution concept for sequential games.
A strategy profile is an SPE if after every history, i.e. in every
subgame
, the strategies of the players still form an NE.
Thus, SPEs impose rationality even after a deviation and only rational behaviors can be used to coerce the behavior of other players.

In this paper, we study the complexity of SPE problems in infinite-duration sequential games played on graphs with mean-payoff objectives.
While NEs always exist in those games, as proved in
[
7
]
, SPEs do not always exist as shown in
[
22
,
6
]
.
The
SPE threshold problem
, i.e. the problem of deciding whether a given mean-payoff game admits an SPE satisfying some constraints on the payoffs it grants to the players, has recently been proved to be decidable in
[
3
]
.
However, its worst-case computational complexity is open:
[
3
]
provides only an
\mathbf{NP}
lower bound and a
2\mathbf{ExpTime}
upper bound.
In this paper, we close this complexity gap and prove that the problem is actually
\mathbf{NP}
-complete.

Contributions.

The starting point of our algorithm is the characterization of SPEs recently presented in
[
3
]
, based on the notions of
requirement
and
negotiation function
.
A requirement on a game
G
is a function
\lambda:V\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{\pm\infty\}
, where
V
is the state space of
G
.
For a given state
v
, the value
\lambda(v)
should be understood as the minimal payoff that the player controlling the state
v
will require in a play traversing
v
in order to avoid deviating.
A requirement captures, therefore, some level of
rationality
of the players.
The negotiation function transforms each requirement
\lambda
into a (possibly stronger) requirement
\mathsf{nego}(\lambda)
, such that
\mathsf{nego}(\lambda)(v)
is the best payoff that the player controlling
v
can ensure, while playing against a coalition of the other players that play rationally with regards to the requirement
\lambda
.
A play is the outcome of an SPE if and only if it satisfies the requirement
\lambda^{*}
, the least fixed point of the negotiation function — or equivalently, one of its fixed points.
We recall that result in Lemma
5
.
In order to obtain our nondeterministic polynomial time algorithm, the rest of the paper constructs a notion of witness recognizing the positive instances of the SPE threshold problem.
Such witnesses admit three pieces.
First, we show that the size of
\lambda^{*}
can be bounded by a polynomial function of the size of the game (Theorem
1
).
This result is obtained by showing that the set of fixed points of the negotiation function can be characterized by a finite union of polyhedra that in turn can be represented by linear inequations.
While the number of inequations that are needed for that characterization may be large (it cannot be bounded polynomially), we show that each of those inequations have coefficients and constants whose binary representations can be bounded polynomially.
As the least fixed point is the minimal value in this set, it is represented by a vertex of one of those polyhedra.
Then this guarantees, using results that bounds the solutions of linear equalities, that the least fixed point has a binary representation that is polynomial and so it can be guessed in polynomial time by a nondeterministic algorithm: it will be the first piece of our notion of witness, in the non-deterministice algorithm we design to solve the SPE threshold problem.
Second, we define a witness of polynomial size for the existence of a play, consistent with a given requirement, which generates a payoff vector between the desired thresholds (Theorem
2
). This play is not guaranteed to be regular.
Third, we define a witness of polynomial size to prove that a requirement is indeed a fixed point of the negotiation function.
This notion of certificate relies on a new and more compact game characterization of the negotiation function called the
reduced negotiation game
(Definition
26
, Theorem
3
).
These results are far from trivial as we also show that SPEs may rely on strategy profiles that are not regular and require infinite memory. As both the least fixed point and its two certificates can be guessed and verified in polynomial time, we obtain
\mathbf{NP}
membership for the threshold problem, closing the complexity gap left open in
[
3
]
(Theorem
4
).

Additionally, all the previous results do also apply to
\varepsilon
-SPEs, a quantitative relaxation of SPEs.
In particular, Theorem
4
does also apply to the
\varepsilon
-SPE threshold problem.

Related works.

Non-zero sum infinite duration games have attracted a large attention in recent years, with applications targeting reactive synthesis problems, see e.g.
[
1
,
8
,
9
,
13
,
18
]
and their references.
We now detail other works more closely related to our contributions.

In
[
6
]
, Brihaye et al. introduce and study the notion of weak SPE, which is a weakening of the classical notion of SPE. This weakening is equivalent to the original SPE concept on reward functions that are
continuous
.
This is the case for example for the quantitative reachability reward function, on which Brihaye et al. solve the SPE threshold problem in
[
5
]
.
The mean-payoff cost function is not continuous and the techniques used in
[
6
]
, and generalized in
[
10
]
, cannot be used to characterize SPEs for the mean-payoff reward function.

In
[
20
]
, Meunier develops a method based on Prover-Challenger games to solve the problem of the existence of SPEs on games with a finite number of possible payoffs. In mean-payoff games, the number of possible payoffs is uncountably infinite.

In
[
14
]
, Flesch and Predtetchinski present another characterization of SPEs on games with finitely many possible payoffs, based on a game structure with infinite state space.
In
[
3
]
, Brice et al. define the notions of requirements and negotiation function. They prove that the negotiation function is characterized by a zero-sum two-player game called
abstract negotiation game
, which is similar to the game introduced in the characterization of Flesch and Predtetchinski. As a starting point for algorithms, they also provide an effective representation of this game, called
concrete negotiation game
, which turns out to be a zero-sum finite state multi-mean-payoff games
[
23
]
.
Finally, they use those tools to prove that the SPE threshold problem is decidable for mean-payoff games.
They left open the question of its precise complexity: they provide a
\mathbf{NP}
lower bound and a
2\mathbf{ExpTime}
upper bound.
In
[
4
]
, the same authors use those tools to close the complexity gap for the SPE threshold problem in parity games, which had been proved to be
\mathbf{ExpTime}
-easy and
\mathbf{NP}
-hard by Ummels and Grädel in
[
15
]
.
They prove that the problem is actually
\mathbf{NP}
-complete.
The techniques used in that paper heavily rely on the fact that parity objectives are
\omega
-regular, which is not the case of mean-payoff games in general.

In
[
12
]
, Chatterjee et al. study mean-payoff
automata
, and give a result that can be translated into an expression of all the possible payoff vectors in a mean-payoff game.
In
[
2
]
, Brenguier and Raskin give an algorithm to build the Pareto curve of a multi-dimensional two-player zero-sum mean-payoff game.
To do so, they study systems of equations and of inequations, and they prove that they always admit simple solutions (with polynomial size).
Those technical results will be used along this paper.

Structure of the paper.

In Section 2, we introduce the necessary background.
Section 3 recalls the notions of requirement and negotiation function, and link them to NEs and SPEs.
Section 4 recalls results about the size of solutions of systems of equations or inequations, and use them to bound the size of the least fixed point of the negotiation function.
Section 5 defines a witness for the existence of a
\lambda
-consistent play between two given thresholds.
Section 6 introduces the reduced negotiation game that is a new compact characterization of the negotiation function.
Finally, Section 7 applies those results to prove the
\mathbf{NP}
-completeness of the SPE threshold problem on mean-payoff games.
Due to the page limit, some proofs are not given in the main body of the text: they appear in a well-identified appendix.

Background

Games, strategies, equilibria.

In all what follows, we study infinite duration turn-based quantitative games on finite graphs with complete information.

Definition 1

(Game)
.

A
non-initialized game
is a tuple

G=\left(\Pi,V,(V_{i})_{i\in\Pi},E,\mu\right)
, where:

•

\Pi
is a finite set of
players
;

•

(V,E)
is a directed graph, called the
underlying graph
of
G
, whose vertices are sometimes called
states
and whose edges are sometimes called
transitions
, and in which every state has at least one outgoing transition.
For the simplicity of writing, a transition
(v,w)\in E
will often be written
vw
;

•

(V_{i})_{i\in\Pi}
is a partition of
V
, in which
V_{i}
is the set of states
controlled
by player
i
;

•

\mu:V^{\omega}\to\mathbb{R}^{\Pi}
is an
payoff function
, that maps each infinite word
\rho
to the tuple
\mu(\rho)=(\mu_{i}(\rho))_{i\in\Pi}
of the players’
payoffs
.

An
initialized game
is a tuple
(G,v_{0})
, often written
G_{\upharpoonright v_{0}}
, where
G
is a non-initialized game and
v_{0}\in V
is a state called
initial state
.
We often use the word

, alone, for both initialized and non-initialized games.

Definition 2

(Play, history)
.

A
play
(resp. history) in the game
G
is an infinite (resp. finite) path in the graph
(V,E)
.
It is also a play (resp. history) in the initialized game
G_{\upharpoonright v_{0}}
, when
v_{0}
is its first vertex.
The set of plays (resp. histories) in the game
G
(resp. the initialized game
G_{\upharpoonright v_{0}}
) is denoted by
\mathsf{Plays}G
(resp.
\mathsf{Plays}G_{\upharpoonright v_{0}},\mathsf{Hist}G,\mathsf{Hist}G_{\upharpoonright v_{0}}
).
We write
\mathsf{Hist}_{i}G
(resp.
\mathsf{Hist}_{i}G_{\upharpoonright v_{0}}
) for the set of histories in
G
(resp.
G_{\upharpoonright v_{0}}
) of the form
hv
, where
v
is a vertex controlled by player
i
.

Given a play
\rho
(resp. a history
h
), we write
\mathsf{Occ}(\rho)
(resp.
\mathsf{Occ}(h)
) the set of vertices that appear in
\rho
(resp.
h
), and
\mathsf{Inf}(\rho)
the set of vertices that appear infinitely often in
\rho
.
For a given index

, we write
\rho_{\leq k}
(resp.
h_{\leq k}
), or
\rho_{<k+1}
(resp.
h_{<k+1}
), the finite prefix
\rho_{0}\dots\rho_{k}
(resp.

\rho_{\geq k}
(resp.

(resp.

Definition 3

(Strategy, strategy profile)
.

A
strategy
for player

in the initialized game
G_{\upharpoonright v_{0}}
is a function
\sigma_{i}:\mathsf{Hist}_{i}G_{\upharpoonright v_{0}}\to V
, such that

is an edge of

for every

A history

is
compatible

if and only if

. A play

A
strategy profile
for

is a tuple
\bar{\sigma}_{P}=(\sigma_{i})_{i\in P}
, where each
\sigma_{i}
is a strategy for player
i

G_{\upharpoonright v_{0}}
.
A play or a history is
compatible
with
\bar{\sigma}_{P}
if it is compatible with every
\sigma_{i}
for
i\in P
.
A
complete
strategy profile, usually written
\bar{\sigma}
, is a strategy profile for
\Pi
.
Exactly one play is compatible with a complete strategy profile: we write it
\langle\bar{\sigma}\rangle
, and call it the
outcome
of
\bar{\sigma}
.

When
i
is a player and when the context is clear, we will often write
-i

\Pi\setminus\{i\}
.
When
\bar{\tau}_{P}
and

(\bar{\tau}_{P},\bar{\tau}^{\prime}_{Q})
the strategy profile
\bar{\sigma}_{P\cup Q}

\sigma_{i}=\tau_{i}
for

, and
\sigma_{i}=\tau^{\prime}_{i}
for

.

Before moving on to SPEs, let us recall that an NE is a strategy profile such that no player has an incentive to deviate unilaterally.

Definition 4

(Nash equilibrium)
.

Let
G_{\upharpoonright v_{0}}
be a game. The strategy profile
\bar{\sigma}
is a
Nash equilibrium
— or
NE
for short — in
G_{\upharpoonright v_{0}}
if and only if for each player
i
and for every strategy
\sigma^{\prime}_{i}
, called

\sigma_{i}
, we have the inequality
\mu_{i}\left(\langle\sigma^{\prime}_{i},\bar{\sigma}_{-i}\rangle\right)\leq\mu_{i}\left(\langle\bar{\sigma}\rangle\right)
.

An SPE is a strategy profile whose all substrategy profiles are NEs.

Definition 5

(Subgame, substrategy)
.

Let
hv
be a history in the game
G
. The
subgame
of
G
after
hv
is the game
\left(\Pi,V,(V_{i})_{i},E,\mu_{\upharpoonright hv}\right)_{\upharpoonright v}
, where
\mu_{\upharpoonright hv}
maps each play to its payoff in
G
, assuming that the history
hv
has already been played: formally, for every
\rho\in\mathsf{Plays}G_{\upharpoonright hv}
, we have
\mu_{\upharpoonright hv}(\rho)=\mu(h\rho)
.
If
\sigma_{i}
is a strategy in
G_{\upharpoonright v_{0}}
, its
substrategy
after
hv
is the strategy
\sigma_{i\upharpoonright hv}
in
G_{\upharpoonright hv}
, defined by
\sigma_{i\upharpoonright hv}(h^{\prime})=\sigma_{i}(hh^{\prime})
for every
h^{\prime}\in\mathsf{Hist}_{i}G_{\upharpoonright hv}
.

Remark
.

The initialized game
G_{\upharpoonright v_{0}}
is also the subgame of
G
after the one-state history
v_{0}
.

Definition 6

(Subgame-perfect equilibrium)
.

Let
G_{\upharpoonright v_{0}}
be a game.
The strategy profile
\bar{\sigma}
is a
subgame-perfect equilibrium
— or
SPE
for short — in
G_{\upharpoonright v_{0}}
if and only if for every history

in

\bar{\sigma}_{\upharpoonright h}
is a Nash equilibrium in the subgame
G_{\upharpoonright h}
.

The notion of subgame-perfect equilibrium refines the notion of Nash equilibrium and excludes coercion by non-credible threats.

Mean-payoff games.

We now turn to the definition of mean-payoff objectives.

Definition 8

In a graph

Two-player zero-sum games.

We now recall several definitions and two classical results about two-player zero-sum games.

Definition 12

(Two-player zero-sum game)
.

A
two-player zero sum game
is a game
G
with
\Pi=\{1,2\}
and
\mu_{2}=-\mu_{1}
.

Definition 13

(Borel game)
.

A game

is
Borel
if the function
\mu
, from the set
V^{\omega}
equipped with the product topology to the Euclidian space
\mathbb{R}^{\Pi}
, is Borel, i.e. if, for every Borel set

Remark
.

Mean-payoff games are Borel (see
[
11
]
).

Lemma 3

(Determinacy of Borel games,
[
19
]
)
.

Let
G_{\upharpoonright v_{0}}
be a zero-sum Borel game, with
\Pi=\{1,2\}
. Then, we have the following equality:

\sup_{\sigma_{1}}~\inf_{\sigma_{2}}~\mu_{1}(\langle\bar{\sigma}\rangle)=\inf_{\sigma_{2}}~\sup_{\sigma_{1}}~\mu_{1}(\langle\bar{\sigma}\rangle).

That quantity is called

, denoted by
\mathsf{val}_{1}(G_{\upharpoonright v_{0}})
.

Definition 14

(Optimal strategy)
.

Let
G_{\upharpoonright v_{0}}
be a zero-sum Borel game, with
\Pi=\{1,2\}
.
The strategy
\sigma_{1}
is
optimal
in
G_{\upharpoonright v_{0}}
if
\inf_{\sigma_{2}}\mu_{1}(\langle\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}\rangle)=\mathsf{val}_{1}(G_{\upharpoonright v_{0}})
.

Let us now define memoryless strategies, and a condition under which they can be optimal.

Definition 15

(Memoryless strategy)
.

A strategy
\sigma_{i}
in a game
G_{\upharpoonright v_{0}}
is
memoryless
if for every vertex
v\in V_{i}
and for all histories
h
and
h^{\prime}
, we have
\sigma_{i}(hv)=\sigma_{i}(h^{\prime}v)
.

We usually write
\sigma_{i}(\cdot v)
for the state
\sigma_{i}(hv)
for every
h
.
For every game
G_{\upharpoonright v_{0}}
, we write
\mathsf{ML}\left(G_{\upharpoonright v_{0}}\right)
for the set of memoryless strategies in
G_{\upharpoonright v_{0}}
.

Definition 16

(Shuffling)
.

Let
\rho,\eta
and
\theta
be three plays in a game
G
.
The play
\theta
is a
shuffling
of
\rho
and
\eta
if there exist two sequences of indices
k_{0}<k_{1}<\dots
and
\ell_{0}<\ell_{1}<\dots
such that
\eta_{0}=\rho_{k_{0}}=\eta_{\ell_{0}}=\rho_{k_{1}}=\eta_{\ell_{1}}=\dots
, and:

\theta=\rho_{0}\dots\rho_{k_{0}-1}\eta_{0}\dots\eta_{\ell_{0}-1}\rho_{k_{0}}\dots\rho_{k_{1}-1}\eta_{\ell_{0}}\dots\eta_{\ell_{1}-1}\dots.

Definition 17

(Convexity, concavity)
.

A function
f:\mathsf{Plays}G\to\mathbb{R}
is
convex
if every shuffling
\theta
of two plays
\rho
and
\eta
satisfies
f(\theta)\geq\min\{f(\rho),f(\eta)\}
.
It is
concave
if
-f
is convex.

Remark
.

Mean-payoff functions, defined with a limit inferior, are convex.

Lemma 4
.

In a two-player zero-sum game played on a finite graph, every player whose payoff function is concave has an optimal strategy that is memoryless.

Proof.

According to
[
17
]
, this result is true for qualitative objectives, i.e. when
\mu
can only take the values
0
and
1
.
It follows that for every
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
, if a player
i
, whose payoff function is concave, has a strategy that ensures
\mu_{i}(\rho)\geq\alpha
(understood as a qualitative objective), then they have a memoryless one.
Hence the equality:

\mathsf{val}_{1}(G_{\upharpoonright v_{0}})=\sup_{\sigma_{1}\in\mathsf{ML}(G_{\upharpoonright v_{0}})}~\inf_{\sigma_{2}}~\mu_{1}(\langle\bar{\sigma}\rangle).

Since the underlying graph
(V,E)
is finite, memoryless strategies exist in finite number, hence the supremum above is realized by a memoryless strategy
\sigma_{1}
that is, therefore, optimal.
∎

Requirements and negotiation

We now recall some notions and results from
[
3
]
, which are the starting point of our algorithm.

Requirements.

In the sequel, we write
\overline{\mathbb{R}}
the set
\mathbb{R}\cup\{\pm\infty\}
.

Definition 18

(Requirement)
.

A
requirement
on the game
G
is a mapping
\lambda:V\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
.

For a given state
v
, the quantity
\lambda(v)
represents the minimal payoff that the player controlling
v
will require in a play traversing the state
v
.

Definition 19

(
\lambda
-consistency)
.

Let
\lambda
be a requirement on a game
G
. A play
\rho
in
G
is
\lambda
-consistent
if and only if, for all
i\in\Pi
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
with
\rho_{n}\in V_{i}
, we have
\mu_{i}(\rho_{\geq n})~\geq~\lambda(\rho_{n})
.
The set of
\lambda
-consistent plays from a state
v
is denoted by
\lambda\mathsf{Cons}(v)
.

Remark
.

The set
\lambda\mathsf{Cons}(v)
can be empty, and is not regular in general.

Definition 20

(
\lambda
-rationality)
.

Let
\lambda
be a requirement on a mean-payoff game
G
. Let
i\in\Pi
. A strategy profile
\bar{\sigma}_{-i}
is
\lambda
-rational
if and only if there exists a strategy
\sigma_{i}
such that, for every history
hv
compatible with
\bar{\sigma}_{-i}
, the play
\langle\bar{\sigma}_{\upharpoonright hv}\rangle
is
\lambda
-consistent.
We then say that the strategy profile
\bar{\sigma}_{-i}
is
\lambda
-rational
assuming

\sigma_{i}
.
The set of
\lambda
-rational strategy profiles in
G_{\upharpoonright v}
is denoted by
\lambda\mathsf{Rat}(v)
.

Negotiation.

In mean-payoff games, as well as in a wider class of games (see
[
3
]
and
[
14
]
), SPEs are characterized by the fixed points of the
negotiation function
, a function from the set of requirements into itself.
We always use the convention
\inf\emptyset=+\infty
.

Definition 21

(Negotiation function)
.

Let
G
be a game.
The
negotiation function
is the function that transforms each requirement
\lambda
on
G
into a requirement
\mathsf{nego}(\lambda)
on
G
defined, for each
i\in\Pi
and
v\in V_{i}
, by:

\mathsf{nego}(\lambda)(v)=\inf_{\bar{\sigma}_{-i}\in\lambda\mathsf{Rat}(v)}\sup_{\sigma_{i}}\mu_{i}(\langle\bar{\sigma}\rangle).

The quantity
\mathsf{nego}(\lambda)(v)
is the best payoff the player controlling the state
v
can enforce if the other players play rationally with regards to the requirement
\lambda
.

Remark
.

The negotiation function satisfies the following properties.

•

It is monotone: if
\lambda\leq\lambda^{\prime}
(for the pointwise order), then
\mathsf{nego}(\lambda)\leq\mathsf{nego}(\lambda^{\prime})
.

•

It is also non-decreasing: for every
\lambda
, we have
\lambda\leq\mathsf{nego}(\lambda)
.

•

There exists a
\lambda
-rational strategy profile from
v
against the player controlling
v
if and only if
\mathsf{nego}(\lambda)(v)\neq+\infty
.

Link with SPEs.

The SPE outcomes in a mean-payoff game are characterized by the fixed points of the negotiation function, or equivalently by its least fixed point.
That result can be extended to
\varepsilon
-SPEs.
To that end, we recall the notion of
\varepsilon
-fixed points of a function.

Definition 22

(
\varepsilon
-fixed point)
.

Let
\varepsilon\geq 0
, let

be a finite set and let

be a mapping. A tuple
\bar{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
is a
\varepsilon
-fixed point
of

if for each

, for
\bar{y}=f(\bar{x})
, we have
y_{d}\in[x_{d}-\varepsilon,x_{d}+\varepsilon]
.

Remark
.

A
0
-fixed point is a fixed point, and conversely.

Lemma 5

(
[
3
]
)
.

Let

be a mean-payoff game, and let
\varepsilon\geq 0
.
The negotiation function on

has a least
\varepsilon
-fixed point
\lambda^{*}
, and given a play

\rho
is
\lambda

\varepsilon
-fixed point

is
\lambda^{*}
-consistent.

The abstract negotiation game.

Given
\lambda
and

, the quantity
\mathsf{nego}(\lambda)(u)
can be characterized as the value of a

, a two-player zero-sum game opposing the player
Prover
, who simulates a
\lambda
-rational strategy profile and wants to minimize player

’s payoff, and the player
Challenger
, who simulates player
i
’s reaction by accepting or refusing Prover’s proposals.
Two negotiation games were defined in
[
3
]
.
Conceptually simpler, the
abstract negotiation game

\mathsf{Abs}_{\lambda i}(G)_{\upharpoonright u}
unfolds as follows:

•

from the state

, Prover chooses a
\lambda
-consistent play
\rho
from the state

If Prover has no play to propose, the game is over and Challenger gets the payoff

.

•

Once a play
\rho

Or he can

, and choose a prefix
\rho_{\leq k}
with
\rho_{k}\in V_{i}
and a new transition
\rho_{k}w\in E
.

•

In the former case, the game is over.
In the latter, it starts again from the state

.

If Challenger finally accepts a proposal

, then his payoff is
\mu_{i}(\rho)
.
If he deviates infinitely often, then Prover’s proposals and his deviations construct a play
\dot{\pi}=\rho^{(0)}_{\leq k_{0}}\rho^{(1)}_{\leq k_{1}}\rho^{(2)}_{\leq k_{2}}\dots
.
Then, Challenger’s payoff is
\mu_{i}(\dot{\pi})
.
It has been proved in
[
3
]
that the equality

Thus, the abstract negotiation game captures a first intuition on how the negotiation function can be computed.

The concrete negotiation game.

The abstract negotiation game cannot be directly used for an algorithmic purpose, since it has an infinite state space.
However, it can be turned into a game on a finite graph if Prover does not propose plays as a whole, but edge by edge.
In the
concrete negotiation game

\mathsf{Conc}_{\lambda i}(G)_{\upharpoonright(u,\{u\})}
, the states controlled by Prover have the form
(v,M)
, where
M\subseteq V
memorizes the states seen since the last time Challenger did deviate, in order to control that the play Prover is constructing since that moment is effectively
\lambda
-consistent: for each
u\in M
, Prover has to give to the player controlling
u
at least the payoff
\lambda(u)
.
Similarly, the states controlled by Challenger are of the form
(vv^{\prime},M)
, where
vv^{\prime}\in E
is an edge proposed by Prover.
The game unfolds as follows:

•

from the state
(v,M)
, Prover chooses a transition
vv^{\prime}
and proposes it to Challenger.

•

Once a transition
vv^{\prime}
has been proposed, Challenger can accept it.
Or, if
v\in V_{i}
, he can
deviate
, and choose a new transition
vw
.

•

If the former case, the game starts again from the state
(v^{\prime},M\cup\{v^{\prime}\})
.
In the latter, it starts from the state
(w,\{w\})
.

For every play
\pi=(\rho_{0},M_{0})(\rho_{0}\rho^{\prime}_{0},M_{0})(\rho_{1},M_{1})(\rho_{1}\rho^{\prime}_{1},M_{1})\dots
in
\mathsf{Conc}_{\lambda i}(G)
, we write
\dot{\pi}=\rho_{0}\rho_{1}\dots
the play in
G
constructed by Prover’s proposals and Challenger’s deviations.
Then, Challenger’s payoff in the play
\pi
is either
+\infty
if there exists an index
k
such that the suffix
\pi_{\geq 2k}
contains no deviation and
\dot{\pi}_{\geq k}
is not
\lambda
-consistent, and
\mu_{i}(\dot{\pi})
otherwise.

In
[
3
]
, a first algorithm was proposed to solve the
\varepsilon
-SPE threshold problem, using the fact that in the concrete negotiation game, Challenger has a memoryless optimal strategy, to design a complete representation of the negotiation function, and to compute its least
\varepsilon
-fixed point.
However, that algorithm requires doubly exponential time, because it needs to enumerate all the memoryless strategies available for Challenger, whose number is exponential in the size of the concrete game, itself exponential in the size of
G
.
Here, we make use of the concrete negotiation game only to bound the size of the least
\varepsilon
-fixed point: our algorithm will use a third negotiation game, the
reduced negotiation game
.

4
Size of the least
\varepsilon
-fixed point

In this section, after having recalled some results about the sizes of solutions to linear equations and inequations, we prove that the least
\varepsilon
-fixed point of the negotiation function in a game
G
has a size that is polynomial in the size of
G
and
\varepsilon
.
The first piece of the witnesses identifying positive instances of the
\varepsilon
-SPE threshold problem will then be an
\varepsilon
-fixed point of the negotiation function of polynomial size.

About size, equations and inequations.

We define here the notion of size that we use.

Definition 23

(Size)
.

The
size
of a rational number
r=\frac{p}{q}
, where
p,q\in\mathbb{Z}
are co-prime, is the quantity
\|r\|=1+\lceil\log_{2}(|p|+1)\rceil+\lceil\log_{2}(|q|+1)\rceil
.
The size of an irrational number is
+\infty
.
The size of the infinite numbers is
\|+\infty\|=\|-\infty\|=1
.
The size of a tuple
\bar{x}\in O^{D}
, where
D
is a set and
O
is a set of objects for which the notion of size has been defined, is the quantity
\mathsf{card}D+\sum_{d\in D}\|x_{d}\|
.
Similarly, the size of a function
f:D\to X
is the quantity
\mathsf{card}D+\sum_{d\in D}\|f(d)\|
, and the size of a set
X\subseteq O
is the quantity
\mathsf{card}X+\sum_{x\in X}\|x\|
.

The proof of Theorem
1
below requires the manipulation of
polytopes
, e.g. downward sealings of convex hulls (from Lemma
2
), expressed as solution sets of
systems of linear inequations
.

Definition 24

(Linear equations, inequations, systems)
.

Let
D
be a finite set.
A
linear equation
in
\mathbb{R}^{D}
is a pair
(\bar{a},b)\in\left(\mathbb{R}^{D}\setminus\left\{\bar{0}\right\}\right)\times\mathbb{R}
.
The
solution set
of the equation
(\bar{a},b)
is the set
\mathsf{Sol}_{=}(\bar{a},b)=\{\bar{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}~|~\bar{a}\cdot\bar{x}=b\}
, where
\cdot
denotes the canonical scalar product on the euclidian space
\mathbb{R}^{D}
.
A set
X\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{D}
is a
hyperplane
of
\mathbb{R}^{D}
if it is the solution set of some linear equation.
A
system of linear equations
is a finite set
\Sigma
of linear equations.
The
solution set
of the system
\Sigma
is the set
\mathsf{Sol}_{=}\Sigma=\bigcap_{(\bar{a},b)\in\Sigma}\mathsf{Sol}_{=}(\bar{a},b)
.
A set
X\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{D}
is a
linear subspace
of
\mathbb{R}^{D}
if it is the solution set of some system of linear equations.

A
linear inequation
in
\mathbb{R}^{D}
is a pair
(\bar{a},b)\in\left(\mathbb{R}^{D}\setminus\left\{\bar{0}\right\}\right)\times\mathbb{R}
.
The
solution set
of the inequation
(\bar{a},b)
is the set
\mathsf{Sol}_{\geq}(\bar{a},b)=\{\bar{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}~|~\bar{a}\cdot\bar{x}\geq b\}
.
A set
X\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{D}
is a
half-space
of
\mathbb{R}^{D}
if it is the solution set of some linear inequation.
A
system of linear inequations
is a finite set
\Sigma
of linear inequations.
The
solution set
of the system
\Sigma
is the set
\mathsf{Sol}_{\geq}\Sigma=\bigcap_{(\bar{a},b)\in\Sigma}\mathsf{Sol}_{\geq}(\bar{a},b)
.
A set
X\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{D}
is a
polyhedron
of
\mathbb{R}^{D}
if it is the solution set of some system of linear inequations
\Sigma
.
A
vertex
of
X
is a point
\bar{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
such that
\{\bar{x}\}=\mathsf{Sol}_{=}(\Sigma^{\prime})
for some subset
\Sigma^{\prime}\subseteq\Sigma
.
A
polytope
is a bounded polyhedron.

Remark
.

Polyhedra are closed sets.
The polytopes of
\mathbb{R}^{D}
are exactly the sets of the form
\mathsf{Conv}(S)
, where
S
is a finite subset of
\mathbb{R}^{D}
.

Lemma 6

(
[
12
]
)
.

Let
\Sigma
be a system of inequations, and let
X=\mathsf{Sol}_{\geq}(\Sigma)
.
The set
\!\,{}^{\llcorner}X
is itself a polyhedron, and there exists a system of inequations
\Sigma^{\prime}
such that
\!\,{}^{\llcorner}X=\mathsf{Sol}_{\geq}(\Sigma^{\prime})
and that for every
(\bar{a}^{\prime},b^{\prime})\in\Sigma^{\prime}
, there exists
(\bar{a},b)\in\Sigma
with
\lVert(\bar{a}^{\prime},b^{\prime})\rVert\leq\lVert(\bar{a},b)\rVert
.

Lemma 7

(
[
2
]
, Theorem 1)
.

There exists a polynomial
P_{1}
such that, for every system of equations
\Sigma
, there exists a point
\bar{x}\in\mathsf{Sol}_{=}\Sigma
, such that
\lVert\bar{x}\rVert\leq P_{1}\left(\max_{(\bar{a},b)\in\Sigma}\lVert(\bar{a},b)\rVert\right)
.

Corollary 1
.

For every system of inequations
\Sigma
, each vertex
\bar{x}
of the polyhedron
\mathsf{Sol}_{\geq}(\Sigma)
has size
\lVert x\rVert\leq P_{1}\left(\max_{(\bar{a},b)\in\Sigma}\lVert(\bar{a},b)\rVert\right)
.

Note that in Lemma
6
, in Lemma
7
and in Corollary

, the number of equations or inequations has no influence.
A consequence of Lemma

is the following result.

Lemma 8

(App.
B
)
.

There exists a polynomial
P_{2}
such that, for each finite set
D
and every finite subset
X\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{D}
, there exists a system of linear inequations
\Sigma
, such that
\mathsf{Sol}_{\geq}(\Sigma)=\mathsf{Conv}(X)
and
\lVert(\bar{a},b)\rVert\leq P_{2}(\lVert X\rVert)
for every
(\bar{a},b)\in\Sigma
.

Size of the least
\varepsilon
-fixed point.

The following theorem bounds the size of the least fixed point of the negotiation function.
As we used the notation
\bar{x}
for tuples so far, we use the notation
\bar{\bar{x}}
for tuples of tuples.

Theorem 1

(App.
C
)
.

There exists a polynomial
P_{3}
such that for every mean-payoff game
G
, the least
\varepsilon
-fixed point
\lambda^{*}
of the negotiation function has size
\lVert\lambda^{*}\rVert\leq P_{3}(\lVert G\rVert+\lVert\varepsilon\rVert)
.

Proof sketch.

It has been proved in
[
3
]
that Challenger has a memoryless optimal strategy in every concrete negotiation game.
Given a requirement
\lambda
, a player
i
, a state
v\in V_{i}
and a memoryless strategy
\tau_{\mathbb{C}}
, we can construct the set of payoff vectors
\mu(\dot{\pi})
, where
\pi
is a play in
\mathsf{Conc}_{\lambda i}(G)_{\upharpoonright(v,\{v\})}
compatible with
\tau_{\mathbb{C}}
, as a union of polytopes defined using Lemma
2
.
If we intersect the upward closures of those sets, then
\mathsf{nego}(\lambda)(v)
is equal to the least value
x_{i}
, where
\bar{x}
belongs to that intersection.
Therefore, if
X_{\lambda}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{V\times\Pi}
is the product of those intersections, then for each
i
and
v\in V_{i}
, we have
\mathsf{nego}(\lambda)=\inf\{x_{vi}~|~\bar{\bar{x}}\in X_{\lambda}\}
.

To each tuple of tuples
\bar{\bar{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{V\times\Pi}
, we associate the requirement
\lambda_{\bar{\bar{x}}}
defined by
\lambda_{\bar{\bar{x}}}(v)=x_{vi}-\varepsilon
for each
i\in\Pi
and
v\in V_{i}
.
Then, we define
X=\left\{\bar{\bar{x}}~\left|~\bar{\bar{x}}\in X_{\lambda_{\bar{\bar{x}}}}\right.\right\}
, and we show that any requirement
\lambda
is an
\varepsilon
-fixed point of the negotiation function if and only
\lambda=\lambda_{\bar{\bar{x}}}
for some
\bar{\bar{x}}\in X
.
Then, the set
X
is itself a union of polyhedra, hence the linear mapping
\bar{\bar{x}}\mapsto\sum_{v}\lambda_{\bar{\bar{x}}}(v)
has its minimum over
X
on some vertex
\bar{\bar{x}}
of one of those polyhedra.
The requirement
\lambda^{*}
is equal to
\lambda_{\bar{\bar{x}}}
, hence its size can be bounded using Corollary
1
.
∎

5
Constrained existence of a
\lambda
-consistent play

We claim that a non-deterministic algorithm can recognize the positive instances of the
\varepsilon
-SPE threshold problem by guessing an
\varepsilon
-fixed point
\lambda
of the negotiation function.
Once
\lambda
has been guessed, according to Lemma
5
, two assertions must be proved: on the one hand, that there exists a
\lambda
-consistent play between the two desired thresholds, and on the other hand, that
\lambda
is actually an
\varepsilon
-fixed point of the negotiation function.
The latter will be handled later through the concept of
reduced negotiation game
.
Now, we tackle the former, and provide the second piece of our notion of witness: to prove the existence of a
\lambda
-consistent play
\rho

6
The reduced negotiation game

The abstract negotiation game has an infinite (and uncountable) state space in general, and the concrete negotiation game has an exponential one.
In
[
4
]
, the infiniteness of the abstract negotiation game has been handled in the case of parity games, by proving that Prover has an optimal strategy that is memoryless, and that proposes only simple plays with a finite representation.
Unfortunately, this result does not apply to mean-payoff games, where Prover needs infinite memory in general.
That fact is illustrated in the next example.

Example 4
.

In the game of Figure
7
, the requirement
\lambda
defined by
\lambda(a)=\lambda(b)=1
is a fixed point of the negotiation function (it is actually the least fixed point).
Indeed, from the state
a
(the situation is symmetrical from the state
b
), consider the strategy for Prover that proposes always, from the state
v
, the play
vb^{|h|^{2}}(a^{3}b^{3})^{\omega}
, where
h
is the history that has already been constructed by her proposals and Challenger’s deviations.
If Challenger accepts such a play, then he gets the payoff
1
.
If he deviates infinitely often, then Prover loops longer and longer on the state
b
, and he also gets the payoff
1
.
The loop on
b
corresponds to what we will call later a
punishing cycle
.
Now, if Prover uses only finite memory, Challenger can get a payoff better than
1
by always deviating and go to
b
as soon as he can: then, edges giving to player
\Circle
the reward
2
will occur with a nonzero frequency.

However, the plays proposed by Prover in the previous example are very similar: only the number of repetitions of the loop
b
does increase.
More generally, one observes that Prover can play optimally while always proposing a play of the form
hc^{n}\rho
, where
h
,
c
and
\rho
are constant, and only the number
n
increases, quadratically with the time — so that Challenger’s payoff is dominated by the mean-payoff
\mathsf{MP}_{i}(c)
if he deviates infinitely often.

Remark
.

Reduced negotiation games are Borel, and are played on a finite graph.

Link with the negotiation function.

We will now prove that the reduced negotiation game captures the negotiation function, as do the abstract and concrete ones.
For that purpose, we first need the following key result.

Lemma 9
.

In a reduced negotiation game, Prover has a memoryless optimal strategy.

Proof.

This lemma is a consequence of Lemma
4
: the payoff function
\nu_{\mathbb{C}}
is concave.
Indeed, let
\xi
be a shuffling of two plays
\pi
and
\chi
.
If either
\pi
or
\chi
reaches the state
\bot
(in which case both do), then we immediately have
\nu_{\mathbb{C}}(\dot{\xi})\leq\max\{\nu_{\mathbb{C}}(\dot{\pi}),\nu_{\mathbb{C}}(\dot{\chi})\}=+\infty
.
Otherwise, the play
\dot{\xi}
is a shuffling of
\dot{\pi}
and
\dot{\chi}
, and since mean-payoff objectives defined with a limit inferior are convex, we have
\nu_{\mathbb{C}}(\dot{\xi})\leq\max\{\nu_{\mathbb{C}}(\dot{\pi}),\nu_{\mathbb{C}}(\dot{\chi})\}
.
∎

This lemma enables us to prove that the reduced negotiation game is equivalent to the other negotiation games.

Theorem 3
.

There exists a polynomial
P_{4}
such that for every mean-payoff game
G
, every requirement
\lambda
with rational values, each player
i
and each
v_{0}\in V_{i}
, for every
\beta\geq P_{4}(\lVert G\rVert+\lVert\lambda\rVert)
, we have
\mathsf{nego}(\lambda)(v_{0})=\mathsf{val}_{\mathbb{C}}\left(\mathsf{Red}^{\beta}_{\lambda i}(G)_{\upharpoonright v_{0}}\right).

Proof.

For every mean-payoff game
G
and every requirement
\lambda
, we assume:

\beta\geq P_{1}\left(P_{2}\left(\lVert\left\{\mathsf{MP}(c)~|~c\in\mathsf{SC}(G)\right\}\rVert\right)\right)

and for each
v\in V
:

\beta\geq\lVert\lambda(v)\rVert+3,

which are indeed quantities that are bounded by a polynomial of
\lVert G\rVert+\lVert\lambda\rVert
.

•

First direction:
\mathsf{nego}(\lambda)(v_{0})\geq\mathsf{val}_{\mathbb{C}}\left(\mathsf{Red}^{\beta}_{\lambda i}(G)_{\upharpoonright v_{0}}\right).

Let
\bar{\sigma}_{-i}
be a strategy profile in
G
that is
\lambda
-rational assuming a strategy
\sigma_{i}
, and let
x=\sup_{\sigma^{\prime}_{i}}\mu_{i}(\langle\bar{\sigma}_{-i},\sigma^{\prime}_{i}\rangle)
.
We wish to prove that there exists a strategy
\tau_{\mathbb{P}}
in the reduced negotiation game such that
\sup_{\tau_{\mathbb{C}}}\nu_{\mathbb{C}}(\langle\bar{\tau}\rangle)\leq x
.
Thus, we will have proved that the quantity
\mathsf{val}_{\mathbb{C}}\left(\mathsf{Red}^{\beta}_{\lambda i}(G)_{\upharpoonright v_{0}}\right)
is smaller than or equal to every such
x
, and therefore smaller than or equal to
\mathsf{nego}(\lambda)(v_{0})
.

Let us define simultaneously the strategy
\tau_{\mathbb{P}}
and a mapping
\varphi:\mathsf{Hist}_{\mathbb{P}}\mathsf{Red}^{\beta}_{\lambda i}(G)_{\upharpoonright v_{0}}\to\mathsf{Hist}G_{\upharpoonright v_{0}}
, such that for each history
H
, the punishment family
\tau_{\mathbb{P}}(H)
will be defined from the play
\langle\bar{\sigma}_{\upharpoonright\varphi(H)}\rangle

We guarantee inductively that if

is compatible with
\tau_{\mathbb{P}}
, then
\varphi(H)\in\mathsf{Hist}G_{\upharpoonright v_{0}}
is compatible with
\bar{\sigma}_{-i}
.
First, let us define
\varphi(v_{0})=v_{0}
.

Let
H\in\mathsf{Hist}_{\mathbb{P}}\mathsf{Red}^{\beta}_{\lambda i}(G)_{\upharpoonright v_{0}}
be a history compatible with
\tau_{\mathbb{P}}
as it has been defined so far, and such that
\varphi(H)
has already been defined.
Let
\eta^{0}=\langle\bar{\sigma}_{\upharpoonright\varphi(H)}\rangle
.
By induction hypothesis, the history
\varphi(H)
is compatible with
\bar{\sigma}_{-i}
, hence the play
\eta^{0}
is
\lambda
-consistent, and satisfies
\mu_{i}(\eta^{0})\leq x
.

Let
\eta^{0}_{\leq\ell}
be the shortest prefix of
\eta^{0}
that is not simple, i.e. such that there exists
k<\ell

\eta^{0}_{k}=\eta^{0}_{\ell}
.
If
\mathsf{MP}_{i}\left(\eta^{0}_{k+1}\dots\eta^{0}_{\ell}\right)\leq x
, then we define
\tau_{\mathbb{P}}(H)=\left[\eta^{0}_{\leq k}\left(\eta^{0}_{k+1}\dots\eta^{0}_{\ell}\right)^{\infty}\rho\right]
, where
\rho
is a play such that
\mathsf{Occ}(\rho)=\mathsf{Occ}\left(\eta^{0}_{>\ell}\right)
, that
\mu_{i}(\rho)\leq x
, and that
\lVert\mu(\rho)\rVert\leq\beta
.
Such a play exists, because the polytope:

Z=\left\{\mu(\rho)~\left|~\begin{matrix}\forall j,\forall v\in V_{j}\cap\mathsf{Occ}(\eta^{0}),\mu_{j}(\rho)\geq\lambda(v),\\
\mathrm{and~}\mathsf{Occ}(\rho)=\mathsf{Occ}\left(\eta^{0}_{>\ell}\right)\end{matrix}\right.\right\}

Otherwise, if

As a consequence, it effectively defines the proposal

Then, for each prefix

Let us now prove that

Second direction:
\mathsf{nego}(\lambda)(v_{0})\leq\mathsf{val}_{\mathbb{C}}\left(\mathsf{Red}^{\beta}_{\lambda i}(G)_{\upharpoonright v_{0}}\right).

.

.
Such a history is called a

.
After other histories, the strategy profile can be defined arbitrarily.
To that end, we construct a mapping
\psi
which maps each bud history to a history
\psi(hv)\in\mathsf{Hist}_{\mathbb{P}}\mathsf{Red}_{\lambda i}^{\beta}(G)_{\upharpoonright v_{0}}
that is compatible with
\tau_{\mathbb{P}}
.
This mapping will induce a definition of
\bar{\sigma}
: since
y\neq+\infty
, we have
\tau_{\mathbb{P}}(\psi(hv))\neq\bot
: let then
[h^{\prime}c^{\infty}\rho]=\tau_{\mathbb{P}}(\psi(hv))
.
We then define
\langle\bar{\sigma}_{\upharpoonright hv}\rangle=h^{\prime}c^{|hh^{\prime}|^{2}}\rho
, which is a
\lambda
-consistent play since
h^{\prime}c^{\infty}\rho
is a
\lambda
-consistent punishment family, by definition of the reduced negotiation game.

Let now
h_{0}v
be a bud history: we assume that
\bar{\sigma}
has been defined on every prefix of
h_{0}
, but not on
h_{0}v
itself.
If
h_{0}
is empty, that is if
h_{0}v=v_{0}
, then we define
\psi(h_{0}v)=v_{0}
.
Otherwise, let us write
h_{0}=h_{1}wh_{2}
, where
h_{1}w
is the longest prefix of
h_{0}
that is a bud history — that is, its longest prefix such that
\psi(h_{1}w)
has been defined, or its shortest prefix such that
wh_{2}
is compatible with
\bar{\sigma}_{\upharpoonright h_{1}w}
.
Let
H=\psi(h_{1}w)
, and let
[hc^{\infty}\rho]=\tau_{\mathbb{P}}(H)
.
We have defined
\langle\bar{\sigma}_{\upharpoonright h_{1}w}\rangle=hc^{|h_{1}h|^{2}}\rho
, and consequently, the history
wh_{2}
is a prefix of that play.
If it is a prefix of the history
hc
, then we define
\psi(h_{0}v)=H[hc^{\infty}\rho][wh_{2}v]v
.
Otherwise, we define
\psi(h_{0}v)=H[hc^{\infty}\rho](c,v)v
.

Now, the strategy profile
\bar{\sigma}
has been defined, and since all the punishment families proposed by Prover are
\lambda
-consistent, the strategy profile
\bar{\sigma}_{-i}
is
\lambda
-rational assuming
\sigma_{i}
.
Let
\eta
be a play compatible with
\bar{\sigma}_{-i}
, and let us prove that
\mu_{i}(\eta)\leq y
.
If
\eta
has finitely many prefixes that are bud histories, then let
\eta_{\leq n}
be the longest one: we have
\eta_{\geq n}=hc^{n+|h|}\rho
, where
[hc^{\infty}\rho]=\tau_{\mathbb{P}}(\psi(\eta_{\leq n}))
.
Then, we have
\mu_{i}(\eta)=\mu_{i}(\rho)\leq y
.

Now, if
\eta
has infinitely many such prefixes, then there exists a unique play
\pi
in the reduced negotiation game such that for any prefix
\eta_{\leq n}
of
\eta
that is a bud history, the history
\psi(\eta_{\leq n})
is a prefix of
\pi
.
Then, if
\pi
contains finitely many post-cycle deviations, then there exist two indices
m
and
n
such that
\eta_{\geq m}=\dot{\pi}_{\geq n}
, hence
\mu_{i}(\eta)=\mu_{i}(\dot{\pi})\leq y
.

Finally, if
\pi
contains infinitely many post-cycle deviations, i.e. infinitely many occurrences of a state of the form
(c,v)
, then let us choose such state that minimizes the quantity
\mathsf{MP}_{i}(c)
.
The play
\eta
has the form:

\eta=h_{0}c^{k_{0}^{2}}h_{1}c^{k_{1}^{2}}h_{2}\dots,

where for each
n
, we have
k_{n}=\left|h_{0}c^{k_{0}^{2}}\dots c^{k_{n-1}^{2}}h_{n}\right|
.
Then, if we write
M=\max r_{i}
, we have:

\mathsf{MP}_{i}\left(h_{0}c^{k_{0}^{2}}\dots h_{n}c^{k_{n}^{2}}\right)\leq\frac{1}{k_{n}+k_{n}^{2}|c|-1}\left(k_{n}M+\left(k_{n}^{2}|c|-1\right)\mathsf{MP}_{i}(c)\right),

which converges to
\mathsf{MP}_{i}(c)
when
n
tends to
+\infty
, hence
\mu_{i}(\eta)\leq\mathsf{MP}_{i}(c)
.
Now, since
\tau_{\mathbb{P}}
is memoryless, there exists a play of the form
HC^{\omega}
that is compatible with it, and such that
(c,v)\in\mathsf{Occ}(C)\subseteq\mathsf{Inf}(\pi)
; and by definition of
y
, we have
\mathsf{MP}_{i}(\dot{C})=\nu_{\mathbb{C}}(HC^{\omega})\leq y
.
By minimality of
\mathsf{MP}_{i}(c)
, we have
\mathsf{MP}_{i}(\dot{C})=\mathsf{MP}_{i}(c)
, hence
\mathsf{MP}_{i}(c)\leq y
, and therefore
\mu_{i}(\eta)\leq y
. ∎

Thus, a given requirement
\lambda
is an
\varepsilon
-fixed point of
\mathsf{nego}
if and only if for each
i
and
v\in V_{i}
, there exists a memoryless strategy
\tau_{\mathbb{P}}
in the game
\mathsf{Red}_{\lambda i}^{\beta}
, with
\beta=P_{4}(\lVert G\rVert+\lVert\lambda\rVert)
, such that
\sup_{\tau_{\mathbb{C}}}\nu_{\mathbb{C}}(\langle\bar{\tau}\rangle)\leq\lambda(v)+\varepsilon
.
The reduced negotiation game has an exponential size, but it contains only
\mathsf{card}V
states that are controlled by Prover: memoryless strategies for Prover are therefore objects of polynomial size.
Thus, such memoryless strategies constitute the third and last piece of our notion of witness.

7
Algorithm and complexity

We are now in a position to define formally our notion of witness.

Definition 27

(Witness)
.

Let
I=(G_{\upharpoonright v_{0}},\bar{x},\bar{y},\varepsilon)
be an instance of the
\varepsilon
-SPE threshold problem.
A
witness
for
I
is a tuple
\left(W,W^{\prime},\bar{\bar{\alpha}},\lambda,(\tau^{v}_{\mathbb{P}})_{v}\right)
, where
W\subseteq W^{\prime}\subseteq V
;
\bar{\bar{\alpha}}\in[0,1]^{\Pi\times\mathsf{SC}(W)}
;
\lambda
is a requirement; and each
\tau^{v}_{\mathbb{P}}
is a memoryless strategy in the game
\mathsf{Red}^{\beta}_{\lambda i}(G)_{\upharpoonright v}
, where
\beta=P_{4}(\lVert G\rVert+\lVert\lambda\rVert)
.
A witness is
valid
if:

•

each strategy
\tau^{v}_{\mathbb{P}}
satisfies the inequality
\sup_{\tau_{\mathbb{C}}}\nu_{\mathbb{C}}(\langle\tau^{v}_{\mathbb{P}},\tau_{\mathbb{C}}\rangle)\leq\lambda(v)+\varepsilon
;

•

the sets
W
and
W^{\prime}
and the tuple of tuples
\bar{\bar{\alpha}}
satisfy the hypotheses of Theorem
2
.

Remark
.

The sets
W
and
W^{\prime}
, as well as the tuple of strategies
(\tau^{v}_{\mathbb{P}})_{v}
, have polynomial size.
In order to bound the size of witnesses by a polynomial, we only have to bound
\lVert\lambda\rVert
and
\left\lVert\bar{\bar{\alpha}}\right\rVert
.

The
\varepsilon
-SPE threshold problem will be
\mathbf{NP}
-easy if we show, first, that there exists a valid witness of polynomial size if and only if the instance is positive, and second, that the validity of a witness can be decided in polynomial time.
The former is a consequence of Lemma
5
, Theorem
1
, Theorem
2
, Theorem
3
, and Lemma
9
:

Lemma 10
.

There exists a polynomial
P_{5}
such that an instance
I
of the
\varepsilon
-SPE threshold problem admits a valid witness of size
P_{5}(\lVert I\rVert)
if and only if it is a positive instance.

Let us now tackle the latter.

Lemma 11
.

Given an instance of the
\varepsilon
-SPE threshold problem and a witness for it, deciding whether that witness is valid is
\mathbf{P}
-easy.

Proof.

The validity of a witness is defined by two conditions.
As regards the second one, all the hypotheses of Theorem
2
can be checked in polynomial time with classical algorithms.
Let us now show how the first condition can also be checked in polynomial time.

Let
n=\mathsf{card}V
.
Given a memoryless strategy
\tau^{v}_{\mathbb{P}}
of Prover in a reduced negotiation game, one can construct in a time polynomial in
\lVert\tau^{v}_{\mathbb{P}}\rVert
the graph
\mathsf{Red}_{\lambda i}^{\beta}(G)[\tau^{v}_{\mathbb{P}}]
, defined as the underlying graph of
\mathsf{Red}_{\lambda i}^{\beta}(G)
where all the transitions that are not compatible with
\tau_{\mathbb{P}}^{v}
have been omitted, as well as all the states that are, then, no longer accessible from the state
v
.
That graph has indeed a polynomial size, because it is composed only of:

•

at most
n
vertices of the form
w\in V
;

•

at most
n
vertices of the form
\tau_{\mathbb{P}}(\cdot w)
(either equal to
\bot
or of the form
[hc^{\infty}\rho]
);

•

at most
n^{2}
vertices of the form
(c,w)
;

•

at most
2n^{2}
vertices of the form
[h^{\prime}w^{\prime}]
, where
h^{\prime}
is a prefix of the history
hc
for some punishment family
[hc^{\infty}\rho]=\tau_{\mathbb{P}}(\cdot w)
;

•

possibly the state
\top
.

We call this connected graph the
deviation graph
.
Note that if among those vertices, there is the vertex
\bot
, then since the vertices that are not accessible have been removed, we have
\sup_{\tau_{\mathbb{C}}}\nu_{\mathbb{C}}(\langle\bar{\tau}\rangle)=+\infty
and the problem can be solved immediately.
In what follows, we assume that it is not the case, i.e. that for each
w
, the state
\tau_{\mathbb{P}}(\cdot w)
has the form
[hc^{\infty}\rho]
.
Deciding whether
\sup_{\tau_{\mathbb{C}}}\nu_{\mathbb{C}}(\langle\bar{\tau}\rangle)\leq\alpha
is then equivalent to deciding whether there exists a path
\pi
, in that graph, such that
\nu_{\mathbb{C}}(\pi)>\alpha
.
Such a play can have three forms.

•

It can end in the state
\top
, i.e. with Challenger accepting Prover’s proposal.
The existence of such a play can be decided immediately, by checking whether in the deviation graph, there exists a vertex of the form
[hc^{\infty}\rho]
with
\mu_{i}(hc^{\infty}\rho)>\alpha
.

•

It can avoid the state
\top
, and comprise finitely many post-cycle deviations.
This is the case if and only if there exists a cycle
C
in the deviation graph, without post-cycle deviations, such that
\mathsf{MP}_{i}(\dot{C})>\alpha
.
The existence of such a cycle can be decided in polynomial time with Karp’s algorithm (see
[
16
]
).

•

It can avoid the state
\top
, and comprise infinitely many post-cycle deviations.
In that case, we have
\nu_{\mathbb{C}}(\pi)\leq\mathsf{MP}_{i}(c)
for each state of the form
(c,w)
appearing infinitely often along
\pi
; then, there exists a cycle
C
in the deviation graph, such that every state of the form
(c,w)
along
C
satisfies
\mathsf{MP}_{i}(c)>\alpha
.
Conversely, if such a cycle exists, then
\pi
exists.
The existence of such a cycle can be decided in polynomial time with Karp’s algorithm.

Therefore, the existence of such a play is decidable in polynomial time.
∎

Thus, given an instance of the
\varepsilon
-SPE threshold problem, a valid witness can be guessed and checked in polynomial time.
Since the
\varepsilon
-SPE threshold problem has been proved to be
\mathbf{NP}
-hard in
[
3
]
, we finally obtain the following theorem:

Theorem 4
.

The
\varepsilon
-SPE threshold problem in mean-payoff games is
\mathbf{NP}
-complete.

To improve readability, we have chosen to recall the statements that appeared in the main body of the paper before giving their detailed proofs in order to ease the work of the reader.

Appendix B
Proof of Lemma
8

Lemma
8
.

There exists a polynomial
P_{2}
such that, for each finite set
D
and every finite subset
X\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{D}
, there exists a system of linear inequations
\Sigma
, such that
\mathsf{Sol}_{\geq}(\Sigma)=\mathsf{Conv}(X)
and
\lVert(\bar{a},b)\rVert\leq P_{2}(\lVert X\rVert)
for every
(\bar{a},b)\in\Sigma
.

Proof.

First, let us recall the notion of
facet
: a facet of a polytope
P
is a subset of
P
of dimension
\dim P-1
and of the form
P\cap H
, where
H
is a hyperplane defined by an equation
(\bar{a},b)
, such that
P\subseteq\mathsf{Sol}_{\geq}(\bar{a},b)
.

Each facet of the polytope
\mathsf{Conv}(X)
is of the form
\mathsf{Conv}\{\bar{x}_{1},\dots,\bar{x}_{n}\}
, where
\bar{x}_{1},\dots,\bar{x}_{n}
are vertices of
X
and
n\geq d=\mathsf{card}D
.
Let
\Phi
be the set of those facets.
We can then write:

\mathsf{Conv}(X)=\mathsf{Sol}_{\geq}\left\{(\bar{a}_{F},b_{F})~|~F\in\Phi\right\},

where for each
F
, the equation
(\bar{a}_{F},b_{F})
defines the hyperplane to which the facet
F
belongs.
Let us now study the complexity of each of those equations (or inequations).

For a given facet
F=\mathsf{Conv}\{\bar{x}_{1},\dots,\bar{x}_{n}\}
, let us choose
d
points
\bar{y}_{1},\dots,\bar{y}_{d}\in\{\bar{x}_{1},\dots,\bar{x}_{n}\}
that are linearly independent.
The equation
(\bar{a}_{F},b_{F})
has the points
\bar{y}_{1},\dots,\bar{y}_{d}
among its solutions, i.e. it satisfies:

\forall i\in\{1,\dots,d\},\sum_{j=1}^{d}a_{Fj}y_{ij}-b_{F}=0.

Those
d
equalities can themselve be understood as equations on the pair
(\bar{a}_{F},b_{F})
.
Let us add a
(d+1)
-th equation: for some dimension
i_{0}
, we have
a_{Fi_{0}}\neq 0
, and by multiplying if needed by a nonzero factor, we can assume
a_{Fi_{0}}=1
.
By Lemma
7
, there exists a pair
(\bar{a},b)
satisfying that system of
d+1
equations and the inequality
\lVert(\bar{a},b)\rVert\leq P_{1}\left(2d+5+\max_{i}\left(\sum_{j}\lVert x_{ij}\rVert\right)\right)\leq P_{1}(\lVert X\rVert)
.
That pair is an equation of the hyperplane containing
F
.

As a consequence, the polynomial
P_{2}=P_{1}
satisfies the desired inequalities.
∎

Appendix C
Proof of Theorem
1

Theorem
1
.

There exists a polynomial
P_{3}
such that for every mean-payoff game
G
, the least
\varepsilon
-fixed point
\lambda^{*}
of the negotiation function has size
\lVert\lambda^{*}\rVert\leq P_{3}(\lVert G\rVert+\lVert\varepsilon\rVert)
.

Proof.

This proof uses the concrete negotiation game to construct the sets we wish to construct as polyhedra.
We write
S=V\times 2^{V}\cup E\times 2^{V}
the state space
\mathsf{Conc}_{\lambda i}(G)
, and
\nu_{\mathbb{C}}
for Challenger’s payoff function.

•

Size of mean-payoffs of cycles

Without loss of generality, we assume that all the rewards
r_{i}(uv)
for
i\in\Pi
and
uv\in E
are integers — otherwise, we can multiply all the rewards by the least common denominator, compute the least fixed point in the resulting game, and finally divide each of its values by the least common denominator.

Let now
i\in\Pi
and
v\in V_{i}
, let
\lambda
be a requirement, and let us consider the corresponding concrete negotiation game
\mathsf{Conc}_{\lambda i}(G)_{\upharpoonright(v,\{v\})}
.
It is shown in
[
3
]
that Challenger has a memoryless optimal strategy.
Let then
\tau_{\mathbb{C}}
be a memoryless strategy for Challenger in
\mathsf{Conc}_{\lambda i}(G)_{\upharpoonright v}
and let
K
be a strongly connected component of the graph
\mathsf{Conc}_{\lambda i}(G)[\tau_{\mathbb{C}}]
, i.e. the underlying graph of
\mathsf{Conc}_{\lambda i}(G)
in which the transitions that are not compatible with
\tau_{\mathbb{C}}
have been omitted.
Let
c=c_{1}\dots c_{n}
be a simple cycle of
K
: necessarily, we have
n\leq\mathsf{card}S=\mathsf{card}V2^{\mathsf{card}V}
.
Therefore, we have, for each player
j
:

\displaystyle\lVert\mathsf{MP}_{j}(\dot{c})\rVert

\displaystyle=\left\lVert\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}}r_{j}(\dot{c}_{k}\dot{c}_{k+1})\right\rVert

\displaystyle=1+\left\lceil\log_{2}\left(\left|\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}}r_{j}(\dot{c}_{k}\dot{c}_{k+1})\right|+1\right)\right\rceil+\lceil\log_{2}(n)\rceil

\displaystyle\leq 1+\left\lceil\log_{2}\left(\left|2^{\mathsf{card}V}\sum_{e\in E}r_{j}(e)\right|+1\right)\right\rceil+\lceil\mathsf{card}V\log_{2}(\mathsf{card}V)\rceil

\displaystyle\leq 1+\mathsf{card}V+\sum_{e\in E}\left\lceil\log_{2}\left(|r_{j}(e)|+1\right)\right\rceil+(\mathsf{card}V)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq(\mathsf{card}E)^{3}+\sum_{e\in E}\left\lceil\log_{2}\left(|r_{j}(e)|+1\right)\right\rceil

\displaystyle\leq\lVert r_{j}\rVert^{3}\leq\lVert G\rVert^{3}.

Feasible payoff vectors in a strongly connected component

Let us now define the polytope:

F_{K}=\!\,^{\llcorner}\left(\underset{c\in\mathsf{SC}(K)}{\mathsf{Conv}}\mathsf{MP}(\dot{c})\right).

By Lemma
2
, the polytope
F_{K}
is exactly the set of the payoff vectors of plays in the strongly connected component
K
.
By Lemma
8
, the polytope:

\underset{c\in\mathsf{SC}(K)}{\mathsf{Conv}}\mathsf{MP}(\dot{c})

is the solution set of a system of inequations whose sizes are bounded by a polynomial function of
\lVert\{\mathsf{MP}(\dot{c})~|~c\in\mathsf{SC}(K)\}\rVert
, i.e., according to the previous point, of
\lVert G\rVert
.
By Lemma
6
, this is also the case for the polytope
F_{K}
.

Consistent payoff vectors in a strongly connected component

If
K
contains no deviation, then there is a set
M_{K}\subseteq V
such that all the states of
K
have the form
(\cdot,M_{K})
.
If
K
contains a deviation, we define
M_{K}=\emptyset
.
Given a requirement
\lambda
, we can now define a new polytope:

C_{K\lambda}=\left\{\bar{x}\in F_{K}~|~\forall j,\forall u\in V_{i}\cap M_{K},x_{i}\geq\lambda(u)\right\}.

That polytope is the set of the tuples
\bar{x}
such that there exists a play
\pi
in
K
realizing
\mu(\dot{\pi})=\bar{x}
,
and

\nu_{\mathbb{C}}(\pi)\neq+\infty
.
Note that we have:

\mathsf{nego}(\lambda)(v)=\sup_{\tau_{\mathbb{C}}}\inf_{K}\inf_{\bar{x}\in C_{K\lambda}}x_{i}.

The inequations defining
C_{K\lambda}
are the same as
F_{K}
, plus the inequations of the form
x_{i}\geq\lambda(u)
.

Union, intersection and product

Let us now work in the space
\mathbb{R}^{V\times\Pi}
.
Given a requirement
\lambda
, we define the set:

X_{\lambda}=\prod_{i\in\Pi,v\in V_{i}}\bigcap_{\tau_{\mathbb{C}}\in\mathsf{ML}\left(\mathsf{Conc}_{\lambda i}(G)_{\upharpoonright(v,\{v\})}\right)}\bigcup_{K}C_{K\lambda}^{\uparrow},

where
Y\mapsto Y^{\uparrow}
is the upward closure operation, i.e.
Y^{\uparrow}=\{z~|~\exists y\in Y,z\geq y\}
.
Then, for every tuple of tuples
\bar{\bar{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{V\times\Pi}
, we have
\bar{\bar{x}}\in X_{\lambda}
if and only if for each
i
and
v\in V_{i}
, for every memoryless strategy of Challenger, there exists a play
\pi
compatible with
\tau_{\mathbb{C}}
satisfying
\nu_{\mathbb{C}}(\pi)\neq+\infty
and
\mu(\dot{\pi})\leq\bar{x}_{v}
.
Therefore, for each
i
and
v\in V_{i}
, we have
\mathsf{nego}(\lambda)(v)=\inf\left\{x_{vi}~\left|~\bar{\bar{x}}\in X_{\lambda}\right.\right\}
.

In terms of inequations, the set
X_{\lambda}
is a union of polyhedra which are all defined by inequations that are inequations defining some
C_{K\lambda}
, padded with
0
to fit with the dimension change.

•

\varepsilon
-fixed points

To each tuple of tuples
\bar{\bar{x}}
, we associate the requirement
\lambda_{\bar{\bar{x}}}
defined, for each
i\in\Pi
and
v\in V_{i}
, by
\lambda_{\bar{\bar{x}}}(v)=x_{vi}-\varepsilon
.
Now, let us consider the set:

X=\left\{\bar{\bar{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{V\times\Pi}~\left|~\bar{\bar{x}}\in X_{\lambda_{\bar{\bar{x}}}}\right.\right\}.

In terms of inequations, the set
X
is a union of polyhedra defined by the same inequations as
X_{\lambda}
, but where the inequations of the form
x_{ui}\geq\lambda(v)
are replaced by equations of the form
x_{ui}\geq x_{vi}
, of size
2+4\mathsf{card}V\mathsf{card}\Pi
.

Let
\lambda
be a requirement.
Then, it is an
\varepsilon
-fixed point of the negotiation function if and only if
\lambda=\lambda_{\bar{\bar{x}}}
for some
\bar{\bar{x}}\in X
.

–

Indeed, if there exists
\bar{\bar{x}}\in X
such that
\lambda=\lambda_{\bar{\bar{x}}}
, then we have
\bar{\bar{x}}\in X_{\lambda_{\bar{\bar{x}}}}
, and by the previous point, for each
i
and
v\in V_{i}
, we have
\mathsf{nego}(\lambda_{\bar{\bar{x}}})(v)\leq x_{vi}=\lambda_{\bar{\bar{x}}}(v)+\varepsilon
.
Therefore,
\lambda
is an
\varepsilon
-fixed point of the negotiation function.

–

Conversely, if for each
i
and
v\in V_{i}
, we have
\mathsf{nego}(\lambda)(v)\leq\lambda(v)+\varepsilon
, according to the previous point and since the set
X_{\lambda}
is closed, there exists a tuple of tuples
\bar{\bar{x}}^{(v)}\in X_{\lambda}
such that
x^{(v)}_{vi}=\lambda(v)+\varepsilon
.
Then, since
X_{\lambda}
is defined as a cartesian product over
v
, the tuple of tuples
\bar{\bar{x}}=\left(\bar{x}^{(v)}_{v}\right)_{v}
does also belong to
X_{\lambda}
, and satisfies
\lambda=\lambda_{\bar{\bar{x}}}
.

Then, in particular, the least
\varepsilon
-fixed point
\lambda^{*}
is the (unique) minimal element in the set
\left\{\lambda_{\bar{\bar{x}}}~\left|~\bar{\bar{x}}\in X\right.\right\}
.
The set
X
is itself a union of polyhedra: consequently, the linear mapping
\bar{\bar{x}}\mapsto\sum_{v}\lambda_{\bar{\bar{x}}}(v)
has its minimum over
X
on some vertex
\bar{\bar{x}}
of one of those polyhedra, which is therefore such that
\lambda^{*}=\lambda_{\bar{\bar{x}}}
.
By Corollary
1
, that vertex has size bounded by a polynomial function of the maximal size of the inequations defining
X
, and therefore a polynomial function of
\lVert G\rVert+\lVert\varepsilon\rVert
.
