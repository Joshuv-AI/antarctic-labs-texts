On the action of the Weyl group on canonical bases
Fern Gossow, Oded Yacobi

Abstract
. We study representations of simply-laced Weyl groups which are equipped with canonical bases. Our main result is that for a large class of representations, the separable elements of the Weyl group W W act on these canonical bases by bijections up to lower-order terms. Examples of this phenomenon include the action of separable permutations on the Kazhdan–Lusztig basis of irreducible representations for the symmetric group, and the action of separable elements of W W on dual canonical bases of weight zero in tensor product representations of a Lie algebra. Our methods arise from categorical representation theory, and in particular the study of the perversity of Rickard complexes acting on triangulated categories. Keywords. Weyl groups, representation theory, canonical bases, categorification



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Set-up

Let
I
be a simply-laced Dynkin diagram of finite type, and
W=W_{I}
its associated Weyl group. In this paper we’ll be interested in the following situation: we have a (complex) representation
\pi:W\to\mathrm{GL}(U)
equipped with a canonical basis
\mathbb{B}\subset U
, usually of geometric or categorical origin.

We ask the following:

Question 1.1
.

Can we extract interesting symmetries of
\mathbb{B}
from the action of
w\in W
?

The origins of this question go back to the 1990s, when the longest element
w_{0}\in W
was studied in this context: Lusztig investigated its action on the canonical basis elements of weight zero in irreducible representations of the Lie algebra
[
31
]
, Mathas studied its action on the Kazhdan–Lusztig basis of the Hecke algebra
[
34
]
, and Berenstein–Zelevinsky and Stembridge studied the case of the longest permutation acting on the Kazhdan–Lusztig basis of irreducible Specht modules
[
2
,
41
]
. More recently, Rhoades studied the case of a particular Coxeter element in the symmetric group (the “long cycle”) acting on Specht modules corresponding to rectangular partitions
[
35
]
.

The main aims of our work are two-fold. Firstly, we show that these results are very special cases of a much wider phenomenon: for a large class of elements (the “separable” elements of
W
) and for a large class of representations, the action of
w
encodes interesting symmetries of
\mathbb{B}
. These symmetries arise from an underlying crystal structure in the sense of Kashiwara
[
25
]
. Secondly, our methods are rooted in categorical representation theory, and in particular we show how perverse autoequivalences of triangulated categories can be well-suited to study and explain interesting combinatorial phenomena arising in Lie theory.

1.2.
The symmetric group

We consider first the prototypical examples of the kinds of results we have in mind. Set
I=\mathrm{A}_{n-1}
, i.e.
W=S_{n}
is the symmetric group, and for a partition
\lambda\vdash n
let
S^{\lambda}
be the corresponding Specht module. Recall that as we range over all partitions, these give a complete list of the irreducible representations of
S_{n}
.

The Kazhdan–Lusztig (KL) basis of the Hecke algebra (cf. Appendix
B
) descends to a canonical basis of the representations
S^{\lambda}
(cf. Section
5
). We denote this basis by
\mathbb{K}\mathbb{L}_{\lambda}=\{C_{T}\;|\;T\in\mathrm{SYT}(\lambda)\}
, where
\mathrm{SYT}(\lambda)
is the set of standard Young tableaux of shape
\lambda
. Then, by Berenstein–Zelevinsky and Stembridge
[
2
,
41
]
, for any
\lambda\vdash n
and any
T\in\mathrm{SYT}(\lambda)
, we have
w_{0}\cdot C_{T}=\pm C_{\mathsf{ev}(T)}
, where
\mathsf{ev}:\mathrm{SYT}(\lambda)\to\mathrm{SYT}(\lambda)
is the evacuation operator (cf. Example
5.7
). Thus Question
1.1
has an affirmative answer in the case when
w
is the longest element acting on the KL basis of a Specht module.

Rhoades proved a similar result for the “long cycle”
c_{n}=(1,2,\ldots,n)\in S_{n}
: in the case when
\lambda
is rectangular,
c_{n}
acts on the KL basis by the promotion operator
\mathsf{pr}
, another well-studied symmetry of
\mathrm{SYT}(\lambda)

[
35
]
. Using this, Rhoades established a cyclic sieving phenomenon for the action of promotion on rectangular tableaux.

It’s easy to see that Rhoades’ Theorem is false for non-rectangular partitions. Nevertheless, we were recently able to show that the theorem holds for all partitions if one allows for a more general framework
[
18
]
. Indeed, let
\lambda\vdash n
be any partition, and define a preorder on
\mathrm{SYT}(\lambda)
by
R\leq T
if the box of
R
containing
n
is in a higher row than the box of
T
containing
n
. We proved that
c_{n}\cdot C_{T}=\pm C_{\mathsf{pr}(T)}

up to lower-order terms
.

Upon reviewing these results, it is natural to try to refine Question
1.1
. For this we define:

Definition 1.2
.

Let
U
be a vector space with basis
\mathbb{B}
,
w:U\to U
an operator,
\xi:\mathbb{B}\to\mathbb{B}
a bijection, and
\leq
a preorder on
\mathbb{B}
. Then
w
acts on
(U,\mathbb{B},\leq)
by
\xi

up to lower-order terms (l.o.t.)
if for every
x\in\mathbb{B}
there are
a_{y}\in\mathbb{Z}
such that:

w(x)=\pm\xi(x)+\sum_{y<x}a_{y}\xi(y),

and the sign of
\xi(x)
only depends on the equivalence class of
x
under
\leq
.

In some cases, we will leave the bijection and preorder unspecified and simply say that
w
acts on
(U,\mathbb{B})
by some bijection up to lower-order terms. This is reasonable since the bijection is uniquely specified by the action of
w
on
\mathbb{B}
(cf. Lemma
A.1
). There is also a compact description of the above definition in terms of QR decompositions (cf. Lemma
A.2
).

In light of this definition, we now ask:

Question 1.3
.

Which permutations
w\in S_{n}
act on
(S^{\lambda},\mathbb{K}\mathbb{L}_{\lambda})
by some bijection up to l.o.t.? Under the association of
\mathbb{K}\mathbb{L}_{\lambda}
with
\mathrm{SYT}(\lambda)
, how can we describe the bijections which appear in terms of tableaux?

Our first main result is that a large and well-studied class of permutations, namely the separable permutations, act on the KL basis by bijections up to l.o.t.

Separable permutations are constructed as follows.
Given
x\in S_{m}
,
y\in S_{n}
with permutation matrices
[x],[y]
, define
x\oplus y,x\ominus y\in S_{m+n}
by:

\displaystyle[x\oplus y]=\begin{pmatrix}[x]&0\\
0&[y]\end{pmatrix},\quad\quad[x\ominus y]=\begin{pmatrix}0&[x]\\
[y]&0\end{pmatrix}.

The set
S_{n}^{\mathrm{sep}}
of separable permutations is given by those permutations obtainable from
(1)\in S_{1}
by repeated applications of
\oplus
and
\ominus

[
28
]
.
In particular
S_{n}^{\text{sep}}
contains both
w_{0}
and
c_{n}
. Note that
S_{n}^{\text{sep}}
can be alternatively defined as the set of permutations avoiding the patterns
2413
and
3142
, and they are counted by the large Schröder numbers
1
1

1

Large Schröder numbers appear as sequence A006318 in the OEIS:
1,2,6,22,90,394,1806,8558,\ldots
.

We prove:

Theorem 1.4
.

Fix
w\in S_{n}^{\mathrm{sep}}
and
\lambda\vdash n
arbitrary. Then
w
acts on
(S^{\lambda},\mathbb{K}\mathbb{L}_{\lambda})
by a bijection up to lower-order terms.

We discuss this theorem in detail in Section
5
, where in particular we describe the bijection and preorder associated to
w
through the combinatorics of tableaux. For an example of the new type of results that arise from this, in Section
5.4
we specify an element
g_{n}\in S_{n}
which is the product of long elements for the tower of symmetric groups
S_{n}\supset S_{n-1}\supset\cdots\supset S_{1}
. We show that
g_{n}
acts on
(S^{\lambda},\mathbb{K}\mathbb{L}_{\lambda})
up to l.o.t. by an operation we term “nested evacuation”.

We note that non-separable permutations don’t appear to encode any permutations of standard Young tableaux (cf. Example
5.23
). We conjecture that in a precise sense Theorem
1.4
is the best possible solution to Question
1.3
(cf. Conjecture
5.24
). If true, this gives a new and very different type of characterisation of separable permutations.

1.3.
Other Weyl groups

When
W
is a simply-laced Weyl group, we use categorical methods to study a much broader class of
W
-modules arising from representations of Lie algebras. To describe this, let
I
be the Dynkin diagram of
W
, and for a subdiagram
J\subseteq I
, let
W_{J}\subseteq W
denote the parabolic subgroup. Let
w_{J}\in W_{J}
denote the longest element. We define the set of separable elements of
W
by

W^{\mathrm{sep}}:=\{w_{I_{1}}\cdots w_{I_{r}}\in W\mid I\supseteq I_{1}\supseteq\cdots\supseteq I_{r}\}.

Remark 1.5
.

By splitting each
I_{j}
into connected components, commuting disjoint factors and cancelling appearances of
w_{J}^{2}
, we are able to rewrite separable elements as a product over
w_{J}
for
J\in\mathcal{N}
, where
\mathcal{N}
is a nested set of
I
as in
[
15
, Theorem 7]
. Hence, our characterisation of separable elements matches the definition given by Gaetz and Gao
[
16
]
. In particular, when
W=S_{n}
, then
W^{\mathrm{sep}}
is the set of separable permutations.

We’ll now exhibit the above phenomenon in
W
-modules arising from representations of Lie algebras, such as tensor products of irreducible highest weight modules.
We prove that separable elements of
W
act by bijection up to lower-order terms on the canonical bases of weight zero. Moreover, the canonical bases of such representations have a natural crystal structure, and the bijection and preorder have an explicit crystal-theoretic description.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be the complex semisimple Lie algebra with diagram
I
, and let
X^{+}
be the set of dominant integral weights. For
\lambda\in X^{+}
we let
L(\lambda)
be the irreducible representation of
\mathfrak{g}
with highest weight
\lambda
. Given a list of dominant weights
\underline{\lambda}=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})
, let
L(\underline{\lambda})=L(\lambda_{1})\otimes\cdots\otimes L(\lambda_{n})
be the tensor product representation.

Tensor product representations
L(\underline{\lambda})
carry various canonical bases, constructed originally by Lusztig
[
32
]
, which possess amazing positivity properties. In this introduction we’ll focus on the dual canonical basis, but similar results hold also for the canonical basis (cf. Section
4.3
).

Recall that there is a natural action of
W
on the zero weight space
L(\underline{\lambda})_{0}
. Let
\mathbb{B}(\underline{\lambda})\subset L(\underline{\lambda})_{0}
denote the set of dual canonical basis elements of weight zero.

Theorem 1.6
.

Let
w\in W^{\mathrm{sep}}
and let
\underline{\lambda}
be a sequence of dominant weights. Then
w
acts on
(L(\underline{\lambda})_{0},\mathbb{B}(\underline{\lambda}))
by a bijection up to lower-order terms.

We discuss this in detail in Section
6
. The bijection and preorder are defined using the crystal structure on the set of dual canonical basis elements. We emphasise that as representations of
W
, the spaces
L(\underline{\lambda})_{0}
are very mysterious in general, even in type A and for
n=1
.

1.4.
Methodology

Our methods come from categorical representation theory.
The starting point is work of Khovanov–Lauda and Rouquier
[
27
,
35
]
who developed the foundations of this theory for
U(\mathfrak{g})
and the quantum group
U_{q}(\mathfrak{g})
.

The definition of a categorical representation is recalled in Section
2.3
. For the present discussion, the salient points are as follows. We will consider representations of
U(\mathfrak{g})
on an abelian category
\mathcal{C}
. By definition, the category is a direct sum of full subcategories
\mathcal{C}_{\mu}
indexed by weights, just as a finite dimensional representation is a direct sum of weight spaces. From the categorical structure, the complexified Grothendieck group
V=[\mathcal{C}]_{\mathbb{C}}
is endowed with an action of
U(\mathfrak{g})
, and the classes of simple objects in
\mathcal{C}
descend to a distinguished basis of
V
. In the cases of interest to us, this is the dual canonical basis.

Chuang and Rouquier constructed the Rickard complex, which is a functor of bounded derived categories:
\Theta_{i}:D^{b}(\mathcal{C}_{\mu})\to D^{b}(\mathcal{C}_{s_{i}(\mu)})
. Here
s_{i}\in W_{I}
is the simple reflection associated to a node
i\in I
. They proved that
\Theta_{i}
is an equivalence of triangulated categories, and used this to prove Broué’s conjecture for symmetric groups
[
12
]
. Many important equivalences in algebraic geometry and related areas turn out to be Rickard complexes, for example the spherical twists that appear in Mirror Symmetry.

The autoequivalences
\Theta_{i}
on
D^{b}(\mathcal{C}_{\mu})
satisfy the braid relations (up to isomorphism)
[
8
]
. Hence, given
w\in W_{I}
we have an autoequivalence
\Theta_{w}:D^{b}(\mathcal{C}_{\mu})\to D^{b}(\mathcal{C}_{w(\mu)})
, which is defined up to isomorphism.

Focusing now on the case
\mu=0
, the connection to our work arises since the autoequivalence
\Theta_{w}
categorifies the operator
w:V_{0}\to V_{0}
. Therefore, to study the action of
w
on canonical bases of
V_{0}
, we’d like to understand the image of simple objects in
\mathcal{C}_{0}
under
\Theta_{w}
.

It turns out that the theory of perverse equivalences, also due to Chuang and Rouquier
[
11
]
, provides the correct setting to extract this information. Indeed, a perverse equivalence is essentially a categorification of Definition
1.2
(cf. Lemma
3.4
).
More precisely, an equivalence
\Theta:D^{b}(\mathcal{C})\to D^{b}(\mathcal{C}^{\prime})
is perverse if it preserves some filtrations on the domain and codomain categories such that on each subquotient the induced equivalence is t-exact up to shift. Crucially, from a perverse equivalence one can extract a bijection of the simple objects by keeping track of the t-exact equivalences on each subquotient. The details of this theory are explained in Section
3.1
.

The main result that underlies our work is the following.

Theorem 1.7
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a categorical representation of
U(\mathfrak{g})
and let
w\in W^{\mathrm{sep}}
be a separable element. Then
\Theta_{w}
is a perverse equivalence.

The case
w=w_{I}
was studied in
[
22
]
. There is a subtlety in extending this case to all separable elements, since compositions of perverse equivalences are not in general perverse. In Section
3.3
, we develop the necessary techniques to prove Theorem
1.7
, and in doing so we precisely describe the necessary filtrations and resulting bijections. By investigating the consequences of this theorem on the level of Grothendieck groups, we obtain (generalisations of) Theorems
1.4
and
1.6
.

1.5.
Organisation of paper

We give a brief overview of the paper:

In Section
2
we recall some of the basic representation-theoretic objects which play a key role throughout the paper. Particularly important is the notion of an ordered
I
-filtration (Definition
2.1
), which is a categorical analogue of an isotypic filtration. This will be crucially used in the construction of perverse equivalences that follow.

Section
3
is devoted to the theory of perverse equivalences. The main result is Theorem
3.12
, which is the precise formulation of Theorem
1.7
above.

In Section
4
we explain how to deduce combinatorial consequences from perversity. Theorem
4.9
states a general result: in any
W
-module which arises from categorification (cf. Equation (
4.1
)), separable elements act by bijections up to l.o.t. We also explain how to deduce analogous results about dual bases (cf. Corollary
4.11
).

In Section
5
, we prove Theorem
1.4
as a special case of Theorem
1.6
. We then develop the combinatorics of type A crystals in order to find explicit descriptions for
\xi
and
\leq
on
\mathbb{K}\mathbb{L}_{\lambda}
in terms of tableaux. We consider a number of special cases, including the generalisation of Rhoades’ Theorem concerning the action of the long cycle (cf. Proposition
5.16
).

In Section
6
, we consider canonical bases of tensor product representations. We give several examples of Theorem
1.6
, including the action of the long element of
S_{n}
on the dual canonical basis in the
d
-fold tensor power of the standard representation, and the actions of separable permutations
(1,3),(1,2,3)\in S_{3}
on the canonical and dual canonical basis of the tensor square of the adjoint representation of
\mathfrak{sl}_{3}
.

Appendix
A
gives a number of general results about operators which act by bijections up to l.o.t., including the connection to QR decompositions. In Appendix
B
, we generalise the definition of separable elements to arbitrary Coxeter groups, and extend known results about the Kazhdan–Lusztig cell modules to prove an analogue of Theorem
1.4
for the left regular representation of a Coxeter group (cf. Theorem
B.1
). This argument does not require categorical methods.

2.
Representations, crystals and categorifications

In this section we recall the necessary background about representation theory (classical and categorical), and associated combinatorial objects which we will need in the rest of the paper. This section also serves to fix notation.

2.1.
Basic objects

Let
I
be a simply-laced Dynkin diagram of finite type. For nodes
i,j\in I
we write
i\sim j
if they are connected by an edge, and
i\not\sim j
otherwise.

Let
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{g}_{I}
be the complex semisimple Lie algebra associated to
I
, with Chevalley generators
e_{i},f_{i},h_{i}
for
i\in I
, and Cartan subalgebra
\mathfrak{h}_{I}=\Span_{\mathbb{C}}(\{h_{i}\mid i\in I\})
. We denote by
X=X_{I}
its integral weight lattice, and let
\langle h,\lambda\rangle\in\mathbb{Z}
denote the natural pairing between elements of the integral Cartan subalgebra (i.e. integral linear combinations of the
h_{i}
) and integral weights.

Let
X^{+}=X^{+}_{I}
be the cone of integral dominant weights, and
R=R_{I}\subset X
the root lattice. The latter is a free
\mathbb{Z}
-module generated by simple roots
\alpha_{i}
for
i\in I
. For
\alpha=\sum_{i}n_{i}\alpha_{i}\in R
we let
\mathrm{ht}(\alpha):=\sum_{i}n_{i}
. The simple roots define a partial order
\preceq
(denoted
\preceq_{I}
) on
X
given by
\mu\preceq_{I}\mu^{\prime}\Longleftrightarrow\mu^{\prime}-\mu\in\sum_{i\in I}\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\alpha_{i}
.

We will be working exclusively with complex finite-dimensional representations of
\mathfrak{g}
. For such a representation
V
and for
\mu\in X
, we let
V_{\mu}
denote its
\mu
weight space, so that
V=\bigoplus_{\mu\in X}V_{\mu}
is the weight decomposition of
V
.
For
\lambda\in X^{+}
we let
L(\lambda)
be the irreducible representation with highest weight
\lambda
. For any representation
V
, there is a canonical isomorphism

\bigoplus_{\lambda\in X^{+}}\big(L(\lambda)\otimes\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathfrak{g}}(L(\lambda),V)\big)\longrightarrow V,

and we let
\mathsf{Iso}_{\lambda}(V)
denote the image of the
\lambda
-summand under this map. This is the
\lambda
-isotypic component of
V
, and we say
V
is an isotypic representation of highest weight
\lambda
if
V=\mathsf{Iso}_{\lambda}(V)
.

Given a subdiagram
J\subseteq I
, we consider the Lie subalgebra
\mathfrak{g}_{J}\subseteq\mathfrak{g}
. For a representation
V
of
\mathfrak{g}
, we let
\mathsf{Res}_{J}(V)
denote the restriction of
V
to
\mathfrak{g}_{J}
.

The Weyl group
W=W_{I}
is generated by
\{s_{i}\mid i\in I\}
subject to the relations:

(1)

s_{i}^{2}=1
,

(2)

s_{i}s_{j}=s_{j}s_{i}
if
i\not\sim j
,

(3)

s_{i}s_{j}s_{i}=s_{j}s_{i}s_{j}
if
i\sim j
.

Relations (2) and (3) are known as the braid relations, and they are the defining relations of the braid group
B=B_{I}
with generators
\{\sigma_{i}\mid i\in I\}
.

Recall that the Weyl group naturally acts on the weight lattice. Let
w_{I}\in W_{I}
be the longest element. This element induces a diagram automorphism
\theta_{I}:I\to I
, given by
w_{I}\cdot\alpha_{i}=-\alpha_{\theta(i)}
. For a subdiagram
J\subseteq I
, we regard
W_{J}\subseteq W_{I}
as a parabolic subgroup. For a representation
U
of
W_{I}
, we let
\mathsf{Res}_{J}(U)
denote its restriction to
W_{J}
.

2.2.
Crystals

We can canonically associate a combinatorial object called an
I
-crystal to any representation of
\mathfrak{g}
. For us, an
I
-crystal consists of a finite set
\mathbb{S}
together with “Kashiwara operators”
\widetilde{e_{i}},\widetilde{f_{i}}:\mathbb{S}\dashrightarrow\mathbb{S}
for all
i
(the
\dashrightarrow
signifies a partially defined map), and a weight function
\mathsf{wt}:\mathbb{S}\to X
. These satisfy the following properties: for any
b,b^{\prime}\in\mathbb{S}

(1)

\widetilde{e_{i}}(b)=b^{\prime}
if and only if
b=\widetilde{f_{i}}(b^{\prime})
,

(2)

if
\widetilde{e_{i}}(b)
is defined then
\mathsf{wt}(\widetilde{e_{i}}(b))=\mathsf{wt}(b)+\alpha_{i}
, and if
\widetilde{f_{i}}(b)
is defined then
\mathsf{wt}(\widetilde{f_{i}}(b))=\mathsf{wt}(b)-\alpha_{i}
.

Since we will only be interested in “normal” crystals, i.e. crystals arising from finite dimensional representations of
\mathfrak{g}
, no additional data is necessary to define the crystal (the functions usually denoted
\varepsilon_{i},\varphi_{i}
are already determined).

Kashiwara originally discovered the theory of crystals
[
25
]
, and showed how to construct a crystal associated to any integrable representation of the quantum group. There are other means of constructing crystals, for example via algebraic geometry
[
24
]
or category theory (which we recall below)
[
29
]
.

An important point shared by all these approaches is that they are closely related to the construction and existence of canonical bases of various types, and the underlying set of the crystal is in natural bijection with such bases.
In particular, the crystal
\mathbb{S}
associated to the representation
V
satisfies the property that
\dim(V_{\mu})=\#\{b\in\mathbb{S}\mid\mathsf{wt}(b)=\mu\}
.

There are some basic operations on crystals which mirror analogous operations on representations.
For example, the direct sum
\mathbb{S}\oplus\mathbb{S}^{\prime}
is a crystal whose underlying set is obtained by disjoint union, and the additional data is defined in the obvious way. Additionally, given a subdiagram
J\subseteq I
, the restriction of an
I
-crystal
\mathbb{S}
is a
J
-crystal denoted
\mathsf{Res}_{J}(\mathbb{S})
. The underlying set of
\mathsf{Res}_{J}(\mathbb{S})
is the same as
\mathbb{S}
, but we forget the Kashiwara operators indexed by
i\in I\setminus J
, and the weight of
b\in\mathsf{Res}_{J}(\mathbb{S})
is the restriction of
\mathsf{wt}(b)
to
\mathfrak{h}_{J}
. Given
I
-crystals
\mathbb{S}
and
\mathbb{S}^{\prime}
one can form their tensor product
\mathbb{S}\otimes\mathbb{S}^{\prime}
and external tensor product
\mathbb{S}\boxtimes\mathbb{S}^{\prime}
. In both cases, the result is an
I
-crystal whose underlying set is
\mathbb{S}\times\mathbb{S}^{\prime}
(we refer the reader to
[
7
]
for the precise definitions).

We let
\mathbb{L}(\lambda)
be the crystal of
L(\lambda)
. Note that there are unique highest and lowest weight elements in
\mathbb{L}(\lambda)
, which we denote
b_{\lambda}^{\text{high}}
and
b_{\lambda}^{\text{low}}
.
The crystal associated to the representation
V
is isomorphic to
\bigoplus_{\lambda\in X^{+}}\mathbb{L}(\lambda)^{\oplus m(\lambda)}
, where
m(\lambda)=\dim(\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathfrak{g}}(L(\lambda),V))
. We let
\mathsf{Iso}_{\lambda}(\mathbb{S}):=\mathbb{L}(\lambda)^{\oplus m(\lambda)}
. This is the crystal analogue of an isotypic component.

We can picture an
I
-crystal as an
I
-labelled directed graph, whose vertices are given by the underlying set of
\mathbb{S}
, with an
i
-labelled edge from
b
to
b^{\prime}
if
\widetilde{f_{i}}(b)=b^{\prime}
.
See Figure
1
an example in type A. Note that the crystal graph of
\mathsf{Res}_{J}(\mathbb{S})
is obtained from the crystal graph of
\mathbb{S}
by deleting all edges labelled by
i\in I\setminus J
.

We recall the generalised Schützenberger involution
\xi_{I}:\mathbb{S}\to\mathbb{S}
, which will play an important role in our work. To define this set map, it suffices to consider a simple crystal
\mathbb{L}(\lambda)
, since as we described above,
\mathbb{S}
is isomorphic to a direct sum of simple crystals.
The map
\xi_{I}:\mathbb{L}(\lambda)\to\mathbb{L}(\lambda)
is determined by the following properties:

(1)

\mathsf{wt}(\xi_{I}(b))=w_{I}\cdot\mathsf{wt}(b)
,

(2)

\xi_{I}(\widetilde{e_{i}}(b))=\widetilde{f}_{\theta_{I}(i)}(\xi_{I}(b))
,

(3)

\xi_{I}(\widetilde{f_{i}}(b))=\widetilde{e}_{\theta_{I}(i)}(\xi_{I}(b))
.

Note that by (1),
\xi_{I}
swaps
b_{\lambda}^{\text{high}}
and
b_{\lambda}^{\text{low}}
, and
\xi_{I}
is then determined on the rest of the crystal by properties (2) and (3). Although
\xi_{I}
is not a map of crystals, it is clear that its square is a crystal morphism, and hence must be the identity map.

We now have the map
\xi_{I}:\mathbb{S}\to\mathbb{S}
, and so for any
J\subseteq I
we also get maps
\xi_{J}:\mathsf{Res}_{J}(\mathbb{S})\to\mathsf{Res}_{J}(\mathbb{S})
. Since these are just set maps, we can write
\xi_{J}:\mathbb{S}\to\mathbb{S}
without confusion.

2.3.
Categorical representation theory

Categorical representations will play an important role for us.
The foundations of this theory are due to Rouquier
[
37
]
and Khovanov–Lauda
[
26
]
. Although there are some differences in their approach, later work of Cautis–Lauda
[
9
]
and Brundan
[
5
]
has clarified their relationships, and the latter, in particular, proves that their definitions agree.

In this work we will use categorical representations of
U(\mathfrak{g})
following Rouquier’s approach. We note that everything we do has analogues in the graded setting, i.e. for the quantum group and the associated Hecke algebra, but that will not be our focus here.

Let
[\mathcal{A}]_{\mathbb{C}}
denote the complexified Grothendieck group of an abelian category
\mathcal{A}
, and recall that an exact functor
F:\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
induces a linear map
[F]:[\mathcal{A}]_{\mathbb{C}}\to[\mathcal{A}^{\prime}]_{\mathbb{C}}
. Recall also that a Serre subcategory
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{A}
is a full subcategory such that for any exact sequence
A\to B\to C
in
\mathcal{A}
, if
A,C\in\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
then
B\in\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
.

Let
\mathbf{k}
be the ground ring. A categorical representation of
U(\mathfrak{g})
consists of a family of
\mathbf{k}
-linear abelian categories
\mathcal{C}_{\mu}
where
\mu\in X
, exact linear functors
\mathsf{E}_{i}\mathsf{1}_{\mu}:\mathcal{C}_{\mu}\to\mathcal{C}_{\mu+\alpha_{i}}
and
\mathsf{F}_{i}\mathsf{1}_{\mu}:\mathcal{C}_{\mu}\to\mathcal{C}_{\mu-\alpha_{i}}
, and natural transformations:

(1)

x:\mathsf{E}_{i}\mathsf{1}_{\mu}\longrightarrow\mathsf{E}_{i}\mathsf{1}_{\mu}
,

(2)

t:\mathsf{E}_{i}\mathsf{E}_{j}\mathsf{1}_{\mu}\longrightarrow\mathsf{E}_{j}\mathsf{E}_{i}\mathsf{1}_{\mu}
,

(3)

\eta:\mathsf{1}_{\mu}\longrightarrow\mathsf{F}_{i}\mathsf{E}_{i}\mathsf{1}_{\mu}
,

(4)

\varepsilon:\mathsf{E}_{i}\mathsf{F}_{i}\mathsf{1}_{\mu}\longrightarrow\mathsf{1}_{\mu}
.

These satisfy relations, which depend on some further choices of scalars, which we won’t directly use and so don’t specify. Details can be found in
[
37
,
5
]
. In practice, we write
\mathcal{C}
for a categorical representation, leaving the remaining data implicit.

There are three important consequences of these relations which we mention:

(1)

There are functorial isomorphisms

\displaystyle\mathsf{E}_{j}\mathsf{F}_{i}\mathsf{1}_{\mu}\cong\mathsf{F}_{i}\mathsf{E}_{j}\mathsf{1}_{\mu}\text{ if }i\neq j,

\displaystyle\mathsf{E}_{i}\mathsf{F}_{i}\mathsf{1}_{\mu}\cong\mathsf{F}_{i}\mathsf{E}_{i}\mathsf{1}_{\mu}\oplus\mathsf{1}_{\mu}^{\oplus\langle h_{i},\mu\rangle}\text{ if }\langle h_{i},\mu\rangle\geq 0,

\displaystyle\mathsf{E}_{i}\mathsf{F}_{i}\mathsf{1}_{\mu}\oplus\mathsf{1}_{\mu}^{\oplus-\langle h_{i},\mu\rangle}\cong\mathsf{F}_{i}\mathsf{E}_{i}\mathsf{1}_{\mu}\text{ if }\langle h_{i},\mu\rangle\leq 0

(2)

The functors
(\mathsf{E}_{i}\mathsf{1}_{\mu},\mathsf{F}_{i}\mathsf{1}_{\mu+\alpha_{i}})
are bi-adjoint. One adjunction is part of the definition (the unit and counits are given by
\eta
and
\varepsilon
), and the other is a consequence
[
5
, Theorem 4.3]
.

(3)

The natural transformations
x
and
t
define an action of the quiver Hecke (alias KLR) algebra on compositions of the
\mathsf{E}_{i}\mathsf{1}_{\mu}
functors.

The first point above implies that from a categorical representation
\mathcal{C}
one can produce a classical representation.
This is a representation on the complex
X
-graded vector space
V=[\mathcal{C}]_{\mathbb{C}}:=\bigoplus_{\mu}[\mathcal{C}_{\mu}]_{\mathbb{C}}
. Define linear maps
\pi(e_{i}):V\to V
by

\pi(e_{i})([Z])=[\mathsf{E}_{i}\mathsf{1}_{\mu}(Z)],

for
Z\in\mathcal{C}_{\mu}
, and similarly
\pi(f_{i}):V\to V
. Letting
\pi(h_{i})
be the diagonal operator which acts by the scalar
\langle h_{i},\mu\rangle
on
[\mathcal{C}_{\mu}]_{\mathbb{C}}
, the resulting representation is given by
\pi:U(\mathfrak{g})\to\End(V)
. We say that
\mathcal{C}
categorifies the representation
V
.

The third point can be used to show that there is an isomorphism of functors
\mathsf{E}_{i}^{n}\mathsf{1}_{\mu}\cong(\mathsf{E}_{i}^{(n)}\mathsf{1}_{\mu})^{\oplus n!}
, where
\mathsf{E}_{i}^{(n)}\mathsf{1}_{\mu}
is a subfunctor of
\mathsf{E}_{i}^{n}\mathsf{1}_{\mu}
. The functor
\mathsf{E}_{i}^{(n)}\mathsf{1}_{\mu}
is therefore called the divided-power of
\mathsf{E}_{i}
.

Now, suppose
\mathcal{C}
is a categorical representation which categorifies a representation
V
. We recall that the crystal of
V
can be extracted from
\mathcal{C}
(
[
12
, Proposition 5.20]
and
[
6
, Theorem 4.31]
). The construction proceeds as follows: the underlying set
\mathbb{S}
of the crystal is the set of isomorphism classes of simple objects in
\mathcal{C}
. Then for
[L]\in\mathbb{S}
, set
\mathsf{wt}([L])=\mu
for
L\in\mathcal{C}_{\mu}
, and define the Kashiwara operators by
\widetilde{e}_{i}([L])=\text{socle}(\mathsf{E}_{i}(L))
and
\widetilde{f}_{i}([L])=\text{socle}(\mathsf{F}_{i}(L))
(recall that the socle of an object in an abelian category is its maximal semisimple subobject).

If
\mathcal{C}
categorifies an isotypic representation, we say that
\mathcal{C}
is an isotypic categorification.
Analogous to the isotypic decomposition of a representation, any categorical representation
\mathcal{C}
has an isotypic filtration
[
22
, Section 3.3]
:

Definition 2.1
.

An
I
-filtration of
\mathcal{C}
is a filtration by Serre subcategories

0=\mathcal{C}(0)\subset\mathcal{C}(1)\subset\cdots\subset\mathcal{C}(n)=\mathcal{C}

satisfying the following properties:

(1)

For every
i
,
\mathcal{C}(i)\subset\mathcal{C}
is a categorical subrepresentation of
\mathfrak{g}_{I}
, i.e. it is invariant under all the data defining the categorical representation on
\mathcal{C}
.

(2)

The representation on the Serre quotient
\mathcal{C}(i)/\mathcal{C}({i-1})
is an isotypic categorification.

An
I
-filtration is ordered if, letting
\lambda_{i}\in X^{+}
denote the highest weight of
\mathcal{C}(i)/\mathcal{C}({i-1})
, the list
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n}
consists of distinct highest weights such that if
\lambda_{i}\prec_{I}\lambda_{j}
then
i<j
.

Note that in an
I
-filtration of
\mathcal{C}
, different subquotients can be isotypic categorifications of the same highest weight, but this cannot happen in an ordered
I
-filtration.

Any categorical representation has an ordered
I
-filtration.
Indeed, by
[
22
, Remark 3.9]
an ordered
I
-filtration can be constructed from
\mathbb{S}
. Suppose that
\mathbb{S}
decomposes into components

\mathbb{S}=\mathsf{Iso}_{\lambda_{1}}(\mathbb{S})\sqcup\cdots\sqcup\mathsf{Iso}_{\lambda_{n}}(\mathbb{S}),

where
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n}\in X^{+}+
are distinct dominant integral weights. Further, we arrange the weights so that
\lambda_{i}\prec_{I}\lambda_{j}
implies
i<j
. Let
\mathcal{C}(i)
be the Serre subcategory of
\mathcal{C}
generated by simple objects
L
such that
[L]\in\mathsf{Iso}_{\lambda_{j}}(\mathbb{S})
for
j\leq i
. Then
0\subset\mathcal{C}(1)\subset\mathcal{C}(2)\subset\cdots\subset\mathcal{C}(n)=\mathcal{C}
is an ordered
I
-filtration of
\mathcal{C}
.

Now we move on to consider triangulated equivalences that arise from categorical representations. Let
D^{b}(\mathcal{C})
denote the bounded derived category. For
i\in I
the Rickard complex
\Theta_{i}:D^{b}(\mathcal{C}_{\mu})\to D^{b}(\mathcal{C}_{s_{i}(\mu)})
was constructed by Chuang and Rouquier, who initially used it to prove Broué’s abelian defect conjecture for symmetric groups
[
12
]
. By definition,
\Theta_{i}
is a complex of functors concentrated in non-positive degrees:

\Theta_{i}=\big(\cdots\longrightarrow(\Theta_{i})^{-1}\longrightarrow(\Theta_{i})^{0}\big),

where for
r\geq 0
and
\mu_{i}:=\langle h_{i},\mu\rangle
,

(\Theta_{i})^{-r}=\begin{cases}\mathsf{E}_{i}^{(-\mu_{i}+r)}\mathsf{F}_{i}^{(r)}\mathsf{1}_{\mu}&\text{ if }\mu_{i}\leq 0\\
\mathsf{E}_{i}^{(\mu_{i}+r)}\mathsf{F}_{i}^{(r)}\mathsf{1}_{\mu}&\text{ if }\mu_{i}\geq 0\end{cases}

The differential
(\Theta_{i})^{-r}\longrightarrow(\Theta_{i})^{-r+1}
is defined using the counit
\mathsf{E}_{i}\mathsf{F}_{i}\mathsf{1}_{\mu}\longrightarrow\mathsf{1}_{\mu}
arising from the adjunction. The fundamental results about Rickard complexes are:

(1)

For every
\mathcal{C},i\in I
, and
\mu\in X
,
\Theta_{i}:D^{b}(\mathcal{C}_{\mu})\to D^{b}(\mathcal{C}_{s_{i}(\mu)})
is an equivalence of triangulated categories
[
12
]
.

(2)

The Rickard complexes satisfy the braid relations, i.e.
\Theta_{i}\Theta_{j}\cong\Theta_{j}\Theta_{i}
if
i\not\sim j
, and
\Theta_{i}\Theta_{j}\Theta_{i}\cong\Theta_{j}\Theta_{i}\Theta_{j}
if
i\sim j

[
8
, Section 6]
.

Therefore, for any
w\in W
, we can define
\Theta_{w}:=\Theta_{i_{1}}\circ\cdots\circ\Theta_{i_{\ell}}
, where
w=s_{i_{1}}\cdots s_{i_{\ell}}
is any reduced expression. This is well-defined up to isomorphism.

3.
Perverse equivalences

3.1.
Definition and properties

We begin with the set-up for the definition of a perverse equivalence.
Let
\mathcal{A}
be an abelian category, and let
\mathbb{B}
be the set of simple objects in
\mathcal{A}
up to equivalence. For any collection of objects
\mathbb{B}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{A}
, we let
\langle\mathbb{B}^{\prime}\rangle\subseteq\mathcal{A}
be the Serre subcategory generated by
\mathbb{B}^{\prime}
. Recall that
\langle\mathbb{B}^{\prime}\rangle
is the smallest full subcategory of
\mathcal{A}
containing
\mathbb{B}^{\prime}
which is closed under quotients and extensions.

Given a subcategory
\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathcal{A}
, let
D^{b}_{\mathcal{B}}(\mathcal{A})\subseteq D^{b}(\mathcal{A})
denote the subcategory consisting of complexes with cohomology supported in
\mathcal{B}
. Equivalently,
D^{b}_{\mathcal{B}}(\mathcal{A})
is the thick subcategory of
D^{b}(\mathcal{A})
generated by
\mathcal{B}
. We set
D^{b}_{\mathbb{B}^{\prime}}(\mathcal{A}):=D^{b}_{\langle\mathbb{B}^{\prime}\rangle}(\mathcal{A})
.

Now suppose we have a chain of abelian categories
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathcal{A}
, and the induced chain of triangulated categories:

\mathcal{T}=D^{b}_{\mathcal{C}}(\mathcal{A})\;\subseteq\;\mathcal{S}=D^{b}_{\mathcal{B}}(\mathcal{A})\;\subseteq\;\mathcal{R}=D^{b}(\mathcal{A}).

Let
Q:\mathcal{S}\to\mathcal{S}/\mathcal{T}
denote the quotient functor, and let
(\mathcal{R}^{\leq 0},\mathcal{R}^{\geq 0})
denote the natural t-structure on
\mathcal{R}
. We have induced t-structures on
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{S}/\mathcal{T}
given by
(\mathcal{R}^{\leq 0}\cap\mathcal{S},\mathcal{R}^{\geq 0}\cap\mathcal{S})
and
(Q(\mathcal{R}^{\leq 0}\cap\mathcal{S}),Q(\mathcal{R}^{\geq 0}\cap\mathcal{S}))
. Note that the heart of
\mathcal{S}
is
\mathcal{B}
and the heart of
\mathcal{S}/\mathcal{T}
is
\mathcal{B}/\mathcal{C}
.

Now suppose we have two chains of subsets of
\mathbb{B}
:

(3.1)

\displaystyle\mathbb{B}(1)\subseteq\mathbb{B}(2)\subseteq\cdots\subseteq\mathbb{B}(s)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{B}

(3.2)

\displaystyle\mathbb{B}^{\prime}(1)\subseteq\mathbb{B}^{\prime}(2)\subseteq\cdots\subseteq\mathbb{B}^{\prime}(s)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{B}

Setting
\mathcal{A}(i):=\langle\mathbb{B}(i)\rangle
and
\mathcal{T}(i):=D^{b}_{\mathcal{A}(i)}(\mathcal{A})
and similarly for
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(i)
and
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}(i)
, we have induced chains of categories:

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(1)\subseteq\mathcal{A}(2)\subseteq\cdots\subseteq\mathcal{A}(s)

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}(1)\subseteq\mathcal{T}(2)\subseteq\cdots\subseteq\mathcal{T}(s)

and similarly for the primed versions. Set
\mathcal{T}:=D^{b}(\mathcal{A})
. Note that
\mathcal{T}=\mathcal{T}(s)=\mathcal{T}^{\prime}(s)
.

Definition 3.3
.

Suppose
F:\mathcal{T}\to\mathcal{T}
is an autoequivalence. Let
p:\{1,\ldots,s\}\to\mathbb{Z}
. Then
F
is a perverse equivalence with respect to
(\mathcal{A}(\bullet),\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(\bullet),p)
if for every
i
:

(1)

F(\mathcal{T}(i))=\mathcal{T}^{\prime}(i)
, and

(2)

the induced equivalence
F[-p(i)]:\mathcal{T}(i)/\mathcal{T}(i-1)\to\mathcal{T}^{\prime}(i)/\mathcal{T}^{\prime}(i-1)
is t-exact.

Given a perverse equivalence
F
, and carrying the notation from the definition, we obtain by Condition (2) abelian equivalences
\mathcal{A}(i)/\mathcal{A}(i-1)\to\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(i)/\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(i-1)
for every
i
. Since abelian equivalences must map simple objects to simple objects, these yield bijections

\mathbb{B}(i)\setminus\mathbb{B}(i-1)\leftrightarrow\mathbb{B}^{\prime}(i)\setminus\mathbb{B}^{\prime}(i-1).

Patching these together we obtain a bijection
\varphi_{F}:\mathbb{B}\to\mathbb{B}
.

The functor
F
induces an endomorphism
f:[\mathcal{A}]_{\mathbb{C}}\to[\mathcal{A}]_{\mathbb{C}}
. For
x,y\in\mathbb{B}
, write
x\leq y
if
x\in\mathbb{B}(i)\setminus\mathbb{B}(i-1)
and
y\in\mathbb{B}(j)\setminus\mathbb{B}(j-1)
for
i\leq j
.

Lemma 3.4
.

The operator
f
acts on
([\mathcal{A}]_{\mathbb{C}},\mathbb{B},\leq)
by
\varphi_{F}
up to lower-order terms.

Proof.

Suppose
x\in\mathbb{B}(i)\setminus\mathbb{B}(i-1)
. Then in
[\mathcal{A}]_{\mathbb{C}}
we have an equality

f(x)=\pm\varphi_{F}(x)+\sum_{x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{B}^{\prime}(i-1)}a_{x^{\prime}}x^{\prime},

where
a_{x^{\prime}}\in\mathbb{Z}
. Note that
\varphi_{F}(x)\in\mathbb{B}^{\prime}(i)\setminus\mathbb{B}^{\prime}(i-1)
, and since by construction
\varphi_{F}(\mathbb{B}(i-1))=\mathbb{B}^{\prime}(i-1)
, we can rewrite this as

f(x)=\pm\varphi_{F}(x)+\sum_{y\in\mathbb{B}(i-1)}a_{y}\varphi_{F}(y).

∎

3.2.
Prior results

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a categorical representation of
\mathfrak{g}
with associated
\mathfrak{g}
-crystal
\mathbb{S}
(so the underlying set of
\mathbb{S}
is the isomorphism classes of simple objects in
\mathcal{C}
). We recall two results of Halacheva, Licata, Losev and Yacobi:

Theorem 3.5

(Corollary 6.7 in
[
22
]
)
.

Suppose
\mathcal{C}
is an isotypic categorical representation of type
\lambda
. Then for any weight
\mu\in X
, the derived equivalence
\Theta_{w_{I}}[-\mathrm{ht}(w_{I}(\lambda)-\mu)]:D^{b}(\mathcal{C}_{\mu})\to D^{b}(\mathcal{C}_{w_{I}(\mu)})
is t-exact.

Let
\mathcal{C}(1)\subset\cdots\subset\mathcal{C}(s)=\mathcal{C}
be an
I
-filtration of
\mathcal{C}
such that
\mathcal{C}(i)/\mathcal{C}(i-1)
categorifies an isotypic representation of type
\lambda_{i}
. For
\mu\in X
, let
\mathcal{C}_{\mu}(i)=\mathcal{C}_{\mu}\cap\mathcal{C}(i)
. Recall that the Schützenberger involution
\xi_{I}:\mathbb{S}\to\mathbb{S}
satisfies the property that
\xi_{I}(\mathbb{S}_{\mu})=\mathbb{S}_{w_{I}(\mu)}
.

Theorem 3.6

(Theorem 6.8 in
[
22
]
)
.

The autoequivalence
\Theta_{w_{I}}:D^{b}(\mathcal{C}_{\mu})\to D^{b}(\mathcal{C}_{w_{I}(\mu)})
is a perverse equivalence with respect to
(\mathcal{C}_{\mu}(\bullet),\mathcal{C}_{w_{I}(\mu)}(\bullet),p)
, where
p(i)=\mathrm{ht}(w_{I}(\lambda_{i})-\mu)
. Moreover, the bijection between
\mathbb{S}_{\mu}
and
\mathbb{S}_{w_{I}(\mu)}
induced by
\Theta_{w_{I}}
is equal to
\xi_{I}
.

Fix
J\subseteq I
, and let
\mathcal{D}
denote the restriction of
\mathcal{C}
to
\mathfrak{g}_{J}
. That is,
\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{C}
as categories, but
\mathcal{D}
is regarded as a categorical representation of
U(\mathfrak{g}_{J})
. The weight subcategories are
\mathcal{D}_{\gamma}
for
\gamma\in X_{J}
, where
\mathcal{D}_{\gamma}=\bigoplus_{\mu}\mathcal{C}_{\mu}
and the sum ranges over all
\mu\in X
such that
\mu|_{\mathfrak{h}_{J}}=\gamma
.

Suppose
\mathcal{D}(1)\subset\cdots\subset\mathcal{D}(s)=\mathcal{D}
is a
J
-filtration of
\mathcal{D}
such that
\mathcal{D}(j)/\mathcal{D}(j-1)
categorifies an isotypic representation of type
\kappa_{j}
. For
\gamma\in X_{J}
define
\mathcal{D}_{\gamma}(j)
as above, and for
\mu\in X
let
\mathcal{C}_{\mu}^{J}(j)=\mathcal{D}(j)\cap\mathcal{C}_{\mu}
. Recall that
w_{J}
is the longest element of
W_{J}\subseteq W
.

Corollary 3.7
.

The autoequivalence
\Theta_{w_{J}}:D^{b}(\mathcal{C}_{\mu})\to D^{b}(\mathcal{C}_{w_{J}(\mu)})
is a perverse equivalence with respect to
(\mathcal{C}_{\mu}^{J}(\bullet),\mathcal{C}_{w_{J}(\mu)}^{J}(\bullet),p)
, where
p(j)=\mathrm{ht}(w_{J}(\kappa_{j})-\gamma)
and
\gamma=\mu|_{\mathfrak{h}_{J}}\in X_{J}
. Moreover, the associated bijection is equal to
\xi_{J}
.

Proof.

We know that
F:=\Theta_{w_{J}}:D^{b}(\mathcal{C}_{\mu})\to D^{b}(\mathcal{C}_{w_{J}(\mu)})
is an equivalence, and we must show it is a perverse equivalence. By Theorem
3.6
, we have that
G:=\Theta_{w_{J}}:D^{b}(\mathcal{D}_{\gamma})\to D^{b}(\mathcal{D}_{w_{J}(\gamma)})
is a perverse equivalence with respect to
(\mathcal{D}_{\gamma}(\bullet),\mathcal{D}_{w_{J}(\gamma)}(\bullet),q)
, where
p(j)=\mathrm{ht}(w_{J}(\kappa_{j})-\gamma)
, and the associated bijection is
\xi_{J}:\res_{J}(\mathbb{S})_{\gamma}\to\res_{J}(\mathbb{S})_{w_{J}(\gamma)}
.

Now, note that

\displaystyle D^{b}_{\mathcal{D}_{\gamma}(j)}(\mathcal{D}_{\gamma})\cap D^{b}(\mathcal{C}_{\mu})=D^{b}_{\mathcal{C}_{\mu}^{J}(j)}(\mathcal{C}_{\mu}).

This follows from an easy fact: if
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B},\mathcal{C}
are abelian subcategories of some larger, unspecified abelian category, with
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{B}
, then
D^{b}_{\mathcal{A}\cap\mathcal{B}}(\mathcal{B})\cap D^{b}(\mathcal{C})=D^{b}_{\mathcal{A}\cap\mathcal{C}}(\mathcal{C})
. Therefore the data defining the perverse equivalence
G
restricts to define perversity data for
F
(with the same perversity function) as claimed. Furthermore, the associated bijection is the same as
\xi_{J}
above, but the domain and codomain are suitably shrunk to
\mathbb{S}_{\mu}
and
\mathbb{S}_{w_{J}(\mu)}
.
∎

The case where
\mu=0
is of most interest to us, so henceforth we set
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{C}_{0}
and
\mathbb{B}=\mathbb{S}_{0}
. For the longest element
w_{I}
we set
\Theta_{I}=\Theta_{w_{I}}
, and more generally
\Theta_{J}=\Theta_{w_{J}}
for any
J\subseteq I
.

Note that in the set up of Theorem
3.6
,
\Theta_{I}
is perverse with respect to
(\mathcal{A}(\bullet),\mathcal{A}(\bullet),p)
, i.e. the filtrations in the domain and codomain agree. Moreover the perversity function in this case simplifies to
p(i)=\mathrm{ht}(\lambda_{i})
. The analogous fact holds for any
J\subseteq I
.

3.3.
Perversity of
\Theta_{w}
for separable elements

3.3.1.

In this section we prove some technical results in preparation for the study of
\Theta_{w}
for separable elements
w\in W
.

We consider the following set-up:
\mathcal{A}
is an abelian category and

\mathcal{D}\subset\mathcal{C}\subset\mathcal{B}\subset\mathcal{A}

is a chain of Serre subcategories. We suppose we have an equivalence
F:D^{b}(\mathcal{A})\to D^{b}(\mathcal{A})
which satisfies perversity-like conditions with respect to the filtration
\mathcal{D}\subset\mathcal{B}\subset\mathcal{A}
. Specifically, we have:

(1)

F(D^{b}_{\mathcal{B}}(\mathcal{A}))=D^{b}_{\mathcal{B}}(\mathcal{A})
,

(2)

F(D^{b}_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathcal{A}))=D^{b}_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathcal{A})
, and

(3)

the induced autoequivalence of
D^{b}_{\mathcal{B}}(\mathcal{A})/D^{b}_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathcal{A})
(which we denote by
\overline{F}
) is t-exact up to shift
b\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Notice that
F
is not actually perverse since we don’t require any exactness on
D^{b}_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathcal{A})
nor on the quotient
D^{b}(\mathcal{A})/D^{b}_{\mathcal{B}}(\mathcal{A})
.

Let
\mathbb{B}_{\mathcal{B}}
(respectively
\mathbb{B}_{\mathcal{C}},\mathbb{B}_{\mathcal{D}}
) be the set of simple objects up to equivalence of
\mathcal{B}
(respectively
\mathcal{C},\mathcal{D}
). Let
\varphi:\mathbb{B}_{\mathcal{B}}\setminus\mathbb{B}_{\mathcal{D}}\to\mathbb{B}_{\mathcal{B}}\setminus\mathbb{B}_{\mathcal{D}}
be the bijection induced by (3). Define

\mathcal{C}^{\prime}:=\left\langle\mathcal{D},\varphi(L)\;|\;L\in\mathbb{B}_{\mathcal{C}}\setminus\mathbb{B}_{\mathcal{D}}\right\rangle.

Let
Q:D^{b}_{\mathcal{B}}(\mathcal{A})\to D^{b}_{\mathcal{B}}(\mathcal{A})/D^{b}_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathcal{A})
be the natural quotient functor. Note that the restriction of
Q
to
\mathcal{B}
is the natural quotient functor
\mathcal{B}\to\mathcal{B}/\mathcal{D}
.

Lemma 3.8
.

The category
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
is the full subcategory of
\mathcal{B}
with objects

\left\{B\in\mathcal{B}\;|\;Q(B)\in\overline{F}[b](\mathcal{C}/\mathcal{D})\right\}.

Proof.

Denote by
\mathcal{C}^{\prime\prime}
the full subcategory of
\mathcal{B}
defined in the statement of the lemma. We first show that
\mathcal{C}^{\prime\prime}
is a Serre subcategory. Consider an exact sequence
A_{1}\to A_{2}\to A_{3}
where
A_{1},A_{3}\in\mathcal{C}^{\prime\prime}
. Applying
Q
we obtain
Q(A_{1})\to Q(A_{2})\to Q(A_{3})
. Since
\overline{F}[b]
restricts to an autoequivalence of
\mathcal{B}/\mathcal{D}
, we have that
\overline{F}[b](\mathcal{C}/\mathcal{D})
is a Serre subcategory of
\mathcal{B}/\mathcal{D}
. By hypothesis
Q(A_{1}),Q(A_{3})\in\overline{F}[b](\mathcal{C}/\mathcal{D})
, and therefore so is
Q(A_{2})
. This shows that
A_{2}\in\mathcal{C}^{\prime\prime}
.

Since both
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
and
\mathcal{C}^{\prime\prime}

Proposition 3.9

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces D^{b}_{\mathcal{B}}(\mathcal{A})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces D^{b}_{\mathcal{B}}(\mathcal{A})/D^{b}_{\mathcal{C}^{\prime}}(\mathcal{A})}

4.
Combinatorial applications

4.1.
The general set-up

We are now in position to deduce combinatorial consequences from Theorem
3.12
. First we describe a general schema for extracting combinatorial information about the action of separable elements on canonical bases. We will then apply this to study two situations: the Kazhdan–Lusztig basis of Specht modules in Section
5
, and the canonical bases in the zero weight space of tensor product representations in Section
6
.

To describe the general schema, let
U
be a representation of
W
equipped with a basis
\mathbb{B}\subset U
. We suppose that the pair
(U,\mathbb{B})
arises via categorification in the following sense: there is a categorical representation
\mathcal{C}
of
U(\mathfrak{g})
, and an isomorphism of
W
-modules

(4.1)

\displaystyle U\cong[\mathcal{C}_{0}]_{\mathbb{C}},

such that the set
\mathbb{B}
is identified with the set of isomorphism-classes of simple objects in
\mathcal{C}_{0}
.

We note that the
W
-module structure on
[\mathcal{C}_{0}]_{\mathbb{C}}
is a consequence of the
U(\mathfrak{g})
-module structure on
[\mathcal{C}]_{\mathbb{C}}
, and moreover, the
W
-action is categorified by the Rickard complexes. Indeed, the action of
s_{i}
on the zero weight space of a
U(\mathfrak{g})
-module is well-known to be given by

(4.2)

\displaystyle s_{i}=\sum_{k\geq 0}(-1)^{k}e_{i}^{(k)}f_{i}^{(k)},

where
x^{(k)}=x^{k}/k!
is the divided-power.

Remark 4.3
.

It is possible to develop our theory also for other weight spaces, and deduce results about the action of a cover of the Weyl group (the “Tits group”) on canonical basis elements in nonzero weight spaces. We’ve opted to focus on the zero weight space for simplicity.

4.2.

\mathbb{B}

Let

be the

-crystal arising from the categorical representation

. Recall that the crystals which arise in this manner are “normal”, i.e. they are isomorphic to a direct sum of irreducible crystals

(for

For

. Let
\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}=(I_{2},\ldots,I_{r})
. The induced filtration on this subquotient is then obtained from a normal ordering of the
\sim_{\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}}
equivalence classes of the crystal for
\mathcal{C}_{A}(k)/\mathcal{C}_{A}(k-1)
. By induction this is a
\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}
-filtration, so by definition
\mathcal{C}(\bullet)
is a
\mathcal{Z}
-filtration.
∎

We return to the general set-up described in Section
4.1
: we have a based representation
(U,\mathbb{B})
of
W
which arises from the categorical representation
\mathcal{C}
of
U(\mathfrak{g})
.

Theorem 4.9
.

Let
w\in W^{\mathrm{sep}}
. Then
w
acts on
(U,\mathbb{B},\leq_{\mathcal{Z}})
by
\xi_{\mathcal{Z}}
up to l.o.t., where
\mathcal{Z}=(I_{1},\ldots,I_{r})
is a chain such that
w:=w_{I_{1}}\cdots w_{I_{r}}
.

Proof.

We consider a normal ordering of
\mathbb{S}
, and the resulting filtration
\mathscr{C}
as defined above Lemma
4.8
. By the Lemma this is a
\mathcal{Z}
-filtration. Let
\mathcal{A}(\bullet)
be the associated
\mathcal{Z}
-filtration of
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{C}_{0}
. By Theorem
3.12
,
\Theta_{w}:D^{b}(\mathcal{A})\to D^{b}(\mathcal{A})
is a perverse equivalence with respect to
\mathcal{A}(\bullet)
and
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(\bullet)
(the latter filtration is defined above Theorem
3.12
), and the associated bijection is
\xi_{\mathcal{Z}}
. The result now follows from Lemma
3.4
.
∎

Remark 4.10
.

The bijections which arise in this manner are closley related to the cactus group
C_{I}

[
23
]
. This is a group generated by
c_{J}
, where

is a connected subdiagram, satisfying the following relations:

(1)

c_{J}^{2}=1
, for all
J\subseteq I
,

(2)

c_{J}c_{K}=c_{K}c_{J}
if
J\cap K=\varnothing
and there is no edge connecting any

to any

,

(3)

c_{J}c_{K}=c_{K}c_{\theta_{K}(J)}
if

.

The assignment
c_{J}\mapsto\xi_{J}
defines an action of

on

[
22
, Theorem 7.7]
.

4.3.
The action of separable elements on the dual basis

In this section we describe how to obtain an analogue of Theorem
4.9
for a basis dual to
\mathbb{B}
.
Indeed, in our set-up (
4.1
) the basis
\mathbb{B}
is often Lusztig’s “dual canonical basis”. Moreover, the representation
U
is equipped with a non-degenerate symmetric form
(\cdot,\cdot):U\times U\to\mathbb{C}
, and the basis
\mathbb{B}^{\prime}
dual to
\mathbb{B}
with respect to
(\cdot,\cdot)
is the “canonical basis” (cf. Section
6
).

For example, consider the case where
U
is the zero weight space of an irreducible representation
L(\lambda)
with a fixed highest weight vector
v_{\lambda}
. The category
\mathcal{C}_{0}
is equivalent to
A\mathrm{-mod}
(the category of finite dimensional
A
-modules) for an appropriate algebra
A
(cf. Section
5.2
). Then
\mathbb{B}
corresponds to simple
A
-modules, and is Lusztig’s dual canonical basis.

On the other hand,
U
is also categorified by
A\mathrm{-pmod}
(the category of finitely generated projective
A
-modules) and in this model the projective indecomposable modules give rise to a different basis
\mathbb{B}^{\prime}
(the canonical basis). These bases are orthogonal with respect to the symmetric bilinear form descended from the hom-pairing on
\mathcal{C}_{0}
: for
X\in A\mathrm{-pmod},Y\in A\mathrm{-mod}
,
([X],[Y])=\dim\operatorname{Hom}(X,Y)
. Note that this is precisely the
Shapavalov form and satisfies the properties:

(1)

(v_{\lambda},v_{\lambda})=1
,

(2)

(e_{i}\cdot u,v)=(u,f_{i}\cdot v)
for all
i\in I
and any
u,v\in U
.

To state our result, let
V=[\mathcal{C}]_{\mathbb{C}}
be the representation of
U(\mathfrak{g})
categorified by
\mathcal{C}
(so that
U=V_{0}
).
Note that we don’t require
V
to be irreducible (e.g.
V
could be a tensor product of irreducible representations, cf. Section
6
).
Suppose that
V
is equipped with a non-degenerate symmetric form satisfying Property (2) above.
We let
(\cdot,\cdot)
denote the restriction of this form to
U
.

Write
\mathbb{B}=\{x_{\mathbf{t}}\mid\mathbf{t}\in\mathcal{X}\}
for the basis of
U
arising from simple objects under (
4.1
), indexed by a set
\mathcal{X}
. Let
\mathbb{B}^{\prime}=\{x_{\mathbf{t}}^{\prime}\mid\mathbf{t}\in\mathcal{X}\}
be the dual basis with respect to
(\cdot,\cdot)
.

Given a chain of diagrams
\mathcal{Z}=(I_{1},\ldots,I_{r})
, define:
\xi_{\mathcal{Z}}(\mathbf{t})=\mathbf{s}
if and only if
\xi_{\mathcal{Z}}(x_{\mathbf{t}})=x_{\mathbf{s}}
, and
\mathbf{t}\leq_{\mathcal{Z}}\mathbf{s}

if and only if
x_{\mathbf{t}}\leq_{\mathcal{Z}}x_{\mathbf{s}}
.

Let
w:=w_{I_{1}}\cdots w_{I_{r}}
be the separable element corresponding to
\mathcal{Z}
, and write
\xi=\xi_{\mathcal{Z}}
.

Corollary 4.11
.

Let
\mathbf{t}\in\mathcal{X}
. Then there exist
b_{\mathbf{r}}\in\mathbb{Z}
such that

w\cdot x_{\mathbf{t}}^{\prime}=\pm x^{\prime}_{\xi^{-1}(\mathbf{t})}+\sum_{\xi^{-1}(\mathbf{r})\;>_{\mathcal{Z}}\;\xi^{-1}(\mathbf{t})}b_{\mathbf{r}}x^{\prime}_{\xi^{-1}(\mathbf{r})}.

Lemma 4.12
.

Any
w\in W
is self-adjoint with respect to
(\cdot,\cdot):U\times U\to\mathbb{C}
.

Proof.

We need to show that for any
u,v\in U
we have that
(w\cdot u,v)=(u,w\cdot v)
, and it suffices to prove this for simple generators
s_{i}\in W
. The action of
s_{i}
on
U
can be expressed in terms of the Lie algebra action (
4.2
).
Therefore self-adjointness follows from Property (2).
∎

Proof of Corollary
4.11
.

By Theorem
4.9
we can write for any
\mathbf{s}\in\mathcal{X}
:

w\cdot x_{\mathbf{s}}=\sum_{\mathbf{r}}a_{\mathbf{r}\mathbf{s}}x_{\xi(\mathbf{r})},

where
a_{\mathbf{s}\mathbf{s}}=\pm 1
and
a_{\mathbf{r}\mathbf{s}}=0
if
\mathbf{r}>_{\mathcal{Z}}\mathbf{s}
.
Define
b_{\mathbf{r}\mathbf{t}}\in\mathbb{Z}
by

w\cdot x^{\prime}_{\mathbf{t}}=\sum_{\mathbf{r}}b_{\mathbf{r}\mathbf{t}}x^{\prime}_{\xi^{-1}(\mathbf{r})}.

Then

\displaystyle b_{\mathbf{r}\mathbf{t}}=(x_{\xi^{-1}(\mathbf{r})},w\cdot x_{\mathbf{t}}^{\prime})=(w\cdot x_{\xi^{-1}(\mathbf{r})},x_{\mathbf{t}}^{\prime})=a_{\xi^{-1}(\mathbf{t})\xi^{-1}(\mathbf{r})}

Now,
a_{\xi^{-1}(\mathbf{t})\xi^{-1}(\mathbf{r})}=\pm 1
if
\mathbf{r}=\mathbf{t}
and
a_{\xi^{-1}(\mathbf{t})\xi^{-1}(\mathbf{r})}=0
if
\xi^{-1}(\mathbf{r})<_{\mathcal{Z}}\xi^{-1}(\mathbf{t})
. Therefore

\displaystyle w\cdot x_{\mathbf{t}}

\displaystyle=\pm x^{\prime}_{\xi^{-1}(\mathbf{t})}+\sum_{\xi^{-1}(\mathbf{r})\;>_{\mathcal{Z}}\;\xi^{-1}(\mathbf{t})}b_{\mathbf{r}\mathbf{t}}x^{\prime}_{\xi^{-1}(\mathbf{r})}.

∎

5.
The Kazhdan–Lusztig Basis for the Specht Module

In this section, we construct the Specht module in two ways. For
\lambda\vdash n
, let
(S^{\lambda},\mathbb{K}\mathbb{L}_{\lambda})
be the irreducible representation for the symmetric group
S_{n}
indexed by the partition
\lambda
equipped with its Kazhdan–Lusztig basis. The classical realisation of this based representation comes from cell modules (cf. Section
B.1
) in type A. We recall below that this basis also appears as the dual canonical basis of
L(\lambda)_{0}
, where
L(\lambda)
is the irreducible
\mathfrak{sl}_{n}
-representation of highest weight
\lambda
.

We present both of these constructions, and then use Theorem
4.9
to deduce the action of separable permutations on
\mathbb{K}\mathbb{L}_{\lambda}
, and prove Theorem
1.4
. Using the combinatorics of type A crystals, we determine explicit descriptions for the bijection
\xi_{\mathcal{Z}}
and preorder
\leq_{\mathcal{Z}}
on
\mathbb{K}\mathbb{L}_{\lambda}
in terms of standard tableaux.

5.1.
The Specht module as a cell module

Following
[
3
]
, we realise each Specht module as a cell module in type A. Fix
n\geq 1
and set
I=\mathrm{A}_{n-1}
, so that
W=S_{n}
is the symmetric group. See Appendix
B
for the general construction of cell modules. Let
\mathbb{K}\mathbb{L}=\{C_{w}\mid w\in S_{n}\}
denote the Kazhdan–Lusztig basis for
\mathbb{C}[S_{n}]
and let
\leq^{\mathrm{L}}
denote the preorder
\leq_{I}^{\mathrm{L}}
(Definition
B.2
) on
S_{n}
, and
\sim^{\mathrm{L}}
its induced equivalence relation. The equivalence classes under this relation are known as left cells. Each left cell
\mathcal{C}
has an associated based
S_{n}
-representation
(V(\mathcal{C}),\mathbb{K}\mathbb{L}_{\mathcal{C}})
which we call a cell module of
S_{n}
. We call
\mathbb{K}\mathbb{L}_{\mathcal{C}}=\{[C_{x}]\mid x\in\mathcal{C}\}
the KL basis for
V(\mathcal{C})
.

We first give a combinatorial description of the left cells. Recall that the RSK correspondence gives a bijection

x\mapsto(P(x),Q(x))

between elements of
S_{n}
and pairs of standard Young tableaux of the same shape and with
n
boxes each. For the following two results, see Section 6.5 in
[
3
]
.

Lemma 5.1
.

Permutations
x,y\in S_{n}
are equivalent under
\sim^{\mathrm{L}}
if and only if
Q(x)=Q(y)
.

Hence, the left cells of
S_{n}
are naturally indexed by standard tableaux. For a partition
\lambda\vdash n
and
Q\in\mathrm{SYT}(\lambda)
, we define the left cell

\mathcal{C}_{Q}:=\{w\in S_{n}\mid Q(w)=Q\}.

Write
[C_{P,Q}]
for the unique KL basis element
[C_{x}]\in\mathbb{K}\mathbb{L}_{\mathcal{C}_{Q}}
satisfying
P(x)=P
. It turns out that each cell module
V(\mathcal{C}_{Q})
is an irreducible
S_{n}
-representation:

Lemma 5.2
.

Fix
\lambda\vdash n
and
Q,Q^{\prime}\in\mathrm{SYT}(\lambda)
. The linear extension of the map
[C_{P,Q}]\mapsto[C_{P,Q^{\prime}}]
for every
P\in\mathrm{SYT}(\lambda)
is an isomorphism of
S_{n}
-representations
V(\mathcal{C}_{Q})\xrightarrow{\sim}V(\mathcal{C}_{Q^{\prime}})
. Moreover,
V(\mathcal{C}_{Q})
is isomorphic to the Specht module
S^{\lambda}
.

Hence, we fix
Q\in\mathrm{SYT}(\lambda)
and write
C_{P}
for
C_{[P,Q]}
. Then
S^{\lambda}
has a basis

\mathbb{K}\mathbb{L}_{\lambda}:=\{C_{P}\mid P\in\mathrm{SYT}(\lambda)\}

which we call the KL basis, and this construction is independent of the choice of
Q
.

5.2.
The Specht module descends to the dual canonical basis

Consider the partition
\lambda
as a highest weight for
\mathfrak{sl}_{n}
and let
L(\lambda)
denote the irreducible representation of
\mathfrak{sl}_{n}
with this highest weight. Recall that the Weyl group of
\mathfrak{sl}_{n}
is
S_{n}
, and hence
S_{n}
acts on the zero weight space
L(\lambda)_{0}
. By Schur–Weyl duality we have that
L(\lambda)_{0}\cong S^{\lambda}
.

To apply Theorem
4.9
we require a categorification of
L(\lambda)
. This is provided by the cyclotomic KLR algebras, as introduced by Khovanov–Lauda and Rouquier. These algebras can be defined either by generators and relations
[
27
]
, or via the geometry of Lusztig quiver varieties
[
38
]
. We will not need the precise definition of these algebras.

We let
\mathcal{L}(\lambda)
denote the category of their finite dimensional modules. Specifically, in the notation of
[
27
]
, we consider the category

\mathcal{L}(\lambda)=\bigoplus_{\nu}R^{\lambda}(\nu)\mathrm{-mod},

where
\nu
runs over elements of the root lattice of
\mathfrak{sl}_{n}
. We need the following theorems:

Theorem 5.3
.

Fix
\lambda\vdash n
.

(1)

There is a categorical
\mathfrak{sl}_{n}
-action on
\mathcal{L}(\lambda)
categorifying
L(\lambda)

[
27
]
.

(2)

Under the isomorphism
[\mathcal{L}(\lambda)]_{\mathbb{C}}\cong L(\lambda)
, the equivalence classes of simple modules are identified with the dual canonical basis of
L(\lambda)

[
43
]
.

To complete the realisation of
(S^{\lambda},\mathbb{K}\mathbb{L}_{\lambda})
via categorification we recall the following folkloric result:

Proposition 5.4
.

Under the isomorphism of
S_{n}
-modules
L(\lambda)_{0}\cong S^{\lambda}
, the dual canonical basis corresponds to the Kazhdan–Lusztig basis.

Proof.

There are several ways to prove this; we take an explicit approach and use Skandera’s construction of the dual canonical basis via immanents
[
40
]
. More precisely, by Equation (4.3) of
[
35
]
the action of simple transpositions on the dual canonical basis elements in
L(\lambda)_{0}
agrees with their action on KL basis elements. It follows that these bases agree up to a global scalar.
∎

We apply Theorem
4.9
to this situation:

Theorem 5.5
.

Fix
\lambda\vdash n
. For
w\in S_{n}^{\mathrm{sep}}
, choose a chain
\mathcal{Z}=(I_{1},\ldots,I_{r})
in
\mathrm{A}_{n-1}
such that
w=w_{\mathcal{Z}}:=w_{I_{1}}\cdots w_{I_{r}}
. Then
w
acts on
(S^{\lambda},\mathbb{K}\mathbb{L}_{\lambda},\leq_{\mathcal{Z}})
by
\xi_{\mathcal{Z}}
up to lower-order terms.

5.3.
Crystal combinatorics

We make Theorem
5.5
more explicit by giving combinatorial descriptions of
\leq_{\mathcal{Z}}
and
\xi_{\mathcal{Z}}
using standard tableaux. We consider the case of a single parabolic subgroup
I^{\prime}\subseteq I
, and generalise to chains via induction. These results will follow from the construction of type A crystals, and the reader can find more information in
[
7
]
.

5.3.1.
Irreducible crystals

If
1\leq a<b\leq n
, the subset
I_{[a,b]}:=\{a,a+1,\dots,b-1\}\subseteq I
generates the standard parabolic subgroup
S_{[a,b]}\leq S_{n}
permuting the elements

. Every nontrivial standard parabolic subgroup of
S_{n}
splits into irreducibles as
I_{[a_{1},b_{1}]}\sqcup\cdots\sqcup I_{[a_{m},b_{m}]}
for
1\leq a_{1}<b_{1}<\cdots<a_{m}<b_{m}\leq n
.

For a partition
\lambda
, denote by
\mathbb{L}_{n}(\lambda)
the crystal of
L(\lambda)
. The objects in this crystal are identified with
\mathrm{SSYT}_{n}(\lambda)

\lambda
containing entries from
\{1,\dots,n\}
. The weight map is given by
\mathsf{wt}(T)=(\eta_{1},\dots,\eta_{n})
, where
\eta_{i}
counts the number of occurences of boxes with label
i
in the tableau

. When
\lambda\vdash n
, the zero-weight subspace

is indexed by the standard tableaux

.

The raising (respectively, lowering) operators

(respectively,

) for

are described by replacing a single box

with

(respectively,

with

5.3.2.
Skew crystals

It is useful to extend our definition of
\mathbb{L}_{n}(\lambda)
to allow skew shapes. If
\mu
is a skew partition, let
\mathbb{L}_{n}(\mu)
be the crystal with objects indexed by
\mathrm{SSYT}_{n}(\mu)
, the skew semistandard tableaux of shape
\mu
on labels
\{1,\dots,n\}
. The weight map and action of the operators
e_{i}
and
f_{i}
are defined as above. We note that
\mathbb{L}_{n}(\mu)
is a normal
I
-crystal, and so is isomorphic to a disjoint union of crystals each of the form
\mathbb{L}_{n}(\lambda)
for some partitions
\lambda
.

We recall a number of definitions from
[
21
]
. Given a skew semistandard tableau
X
, the rectification of
X
is the semistandard tableaux
\mathsf{rect}(X)
obtained by removing all inside empty boxes via jeu-de-taquin slides. Two skew tableaux
X
and
Y
are called dual equivalent if and only if they have the same shape after performing any sequence of slides to the same outside or inside boxes of both tableaux, and we write
X\approx Y
. Note that
X\approx Y
implies
\sh(X)=\sh(Y)
, where
\sh
denotes the shape of a (skew) tableaux.

For
X\in\mathbb{L}_{n}(\mu)
, let
\mathbb{L}_{n}(\mu)[X]
denote the connected component of
\mathbb{L}_{n}(\mu)
containing
X
.

Proposition 5.8
.

The objects of
\mathbb{L}_{n}(\mu)[X]
are identified with the dual equivalence class of
X
. Moreover,
\mathsf{rect}(-)
induces a crystal isomorphism
\mathbb{L}_{n}(\mu)[X]\xrightarrow{\sim}\mathbb{L}_{n}(\nu)
, where
\nu:=\sh(\mathsf{rect}(X))
.

Proof.

For any
1\leq i\leq n
, the operator
f_{i}
on tableaux commutes with jeu-de-taquin slides
[
42
, Theorem 3.3.1]
. Hence,
\mathsf{rect}(f_{i}\cdot X)=f_{i}\cdot\mathsf{rect}(X)
since
\mathsf{rect}(-)
can be described as a composition of slides. The same holds for
e_{i}
. Moreover, it is easy to see that rectification preserves the weight. Finally, this map is an isomorphism since
\mathsf{rect}(-)
is a bijection between the dual equivalence class of
X
and
\mathrm{SSYT}_{n}(\nu)

[
21
, Theorem 2.13]
.
∎

Corollary 5.9
.

The crystal
\mathbb{L}_{n}(\mu)[X]
has highest weight
\sh(\mathsf{rect}(X))
, and
\xi_{I}
acts on
\mathbb{L}_{n}(\mu)
by sending
X
to the unique tableau
Y
such that
Y\approx X
and
\mathsf{rect}(Y)=\mathsf{ev}(\mathsf{rect}(X))
.

For a semistandard (skew) tableau
X
, set
\mathsf{ev}(X):=\xi_{I}\cdot X
. We can calculate
\mathsf{ev}(-)
by rectifying, evacuating, and then ‘unrectifying’ backward along the same path to preserve dual equivalence. We continue to call this map evacuation for skew tableaux, although the term
reversal
is also used in the literature
[
1
]
.

6.
Canonical bases in tensor product representations

6.1.

Let
I
be a finite type simply-laced Dynkin diagram, and let
W=W_{I}
be the associated Weyl group.
In this section we apply Theorem
4.9
to study the action of separable elements of
W
on the dual canonical basis elements (of weight zero) in tensor product representations of
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{g}_{I}
.

Recall that
X^{+}
denotes the set of integral dominant weights of
\mathfrak{g}
, and for
\lambda\in X^{+}
,
L(\lambda)
denotes the irreducible representation of
\mathfrak{g}
of highest weight
\lambda
.
Given a sequence
\underline{\lambda}=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})
of dominant weights, we define
L(\underline{\lambda}):=L(\lambda_{1})\otimes\cdots\otimes L(\lambda_{n})
.

These tensor product representations possess (dual) canonical bases constructed in
[
32
,
33
]
. These bases have remarkable properties such as positivity and compatibility with isotypic filtrations, and important connections to the BGG category
\mathcal{O}
and quantum topology.

Both the canonical and dual canonical basis are indexed by the crystal of the representation. More precisely, the underlying set of
\mathbb{S}(\lambda)
indexes two bases of
L(\lambda)
: the dual canonical basis
\{x_{b}\mid b\in\mathbb{S}(\lambda)\}
and the canonical basis
\{x^{\prime}_{b}\mid b\in\mathbb{S}(\lambda)\}
. This follows from
[
20
]
, and also follows from the categorical point of view.

Indeed, as mentioned in Section
4.3
, elements of the dual canonical basis correspond to classes of simple modules in a categorication of
L(\lambda)
, which can be constructed via the category of finite-dimensional modules over cyclotomic KLR algebras. If one instead considers the category of finitely-generated projective modules over the same algebras, this produces another categorification of
L(\lambda)
but now the classes of indecomposable projective modules correspond to the canonical basis. This theory is well-explained in
[
43
]
.

Consequently, the dual canonical and canonical bases of
L(\underline{\lambda})
are indexed by the crystal
\mathbb{S}(\underline{\lambda}):=\mathbb{S}(\lambda_{1})\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathbb{S}(\lambda_{n})
, and we denote these
\{x_{b}\mid b\in\mathbb{S}(\underline{\lambda})\}
and
\{x^{\prime}_{b}\mid b\in\mathbb{S}(\underline{\lambda})\}
as above.
Note that these are not simply the tensor product of bases, e.g. in
L(\lambda)\otimes L(\lambda^{\prime})
, it is not necessarily true that
x_{b\otimes b^{\prime}}=x_{b}\otimes x_{b^{\prime}}
for
b\in\mathbb{S}(\lambda)
and
b^{\prime}\in\mathbb{S}(\lambda^{\prime})
. However, we do have that
x_{b\otimes b^{\prime}}=x_{b}\otimes x_{b^{\prime}}+\text{ lower-order terms}
, where the order on tensor products of bases is defined via the partial order on weights
[
33
, Section 27.3]
.

6.2.

Consider the zero weight space
U(\underline{\lambda}):=L(\underline{\lambda})_{0}
, which as usual we view as a
W
-module.
We let
\mathbb{B}(\underline{\lambda})\subset U(\underline{\lambda})
denote the set of dual canonical basis elements of
L(\underline{\lambda})_{0}
.

Theorem 6.1

(Webster’s Theorem)
.

The pair
(U(\underline{\lambda}),\mathbb{B}(\underline{\lambda}))
arises via categorification.

Proof.

Webster constructs KLRW algberas
T^{\underline{\lambda}}_{\nu}
, which are generalisations of KLR algebras, in order to categorify the representations
L(\underline{\lambda})
. Specifically, the category

\mathcal{L}(\underline{\lambda})=\bigoplus_{\nu}T^{\underline{\lambda}}_{\nu}\mathrm{-mod},

carries an action of
\mathfrak{g}
(and the corresponding quantum group), and this representation categorifies
L(\underline{\lambda})
.
By
[
43
, Corollary 8.10]
, if we instead consider the additive categorification given by taking the categories of projective modules above, then the classes of indecomposable projectives correspond to the canonical basis of
L(\underline{\lambda})
. Then, by
[
43
, Corollary 2.4]
, we obtain that the classes of simple modules in
\mathcal{L}(\underline{\lambda})
correspond to the dual canonical basis elements of
L(\underline{\lambda})
.
∎

We are in the position to apply Theorem
4.9
. Consider a chain
\mathcal{Z}
of subdiagrams of
I
, and let
w=w_{\mathcal{Z}}\in W
be the corresponding separable element,
\leq_{\mathcal{Z}}
the partial order on
\mathbb{B}(\underline{\lambda})
, and
\xi_{\mathcal{Z}}
the bijection of
\mathbb{B}(\underline{\lambda})
. Then

w
acts on
(U(\underline{\lambda}),\mathbb{B}(\underline{\lambda}),\leq_{\mathcal{Z}})
by
\xi_{\mathcal{Z}}
up to l.o.t.

6.3.

We investigate this result through some examples, and begin by considering the longest element
w=w_{I}
. In the simplest case
\underline{\lambda}=(\lambda)
, i.e. when
w
acts on the zero weight space of the irreducible representation
L(\lambda)
, we recover, and slightly refine, a classical result of Lusztig
[

More generally, consider the

be the Schützenberger involution.
Since the dominance order
\prec

, we have for every

w\cdot x_{b}=\pm x_{\xi(b)}+v_{b},\;\text{where }v_{b}\in\Span_{\mathbb{Z}}(\{\mathbb{B}_{\mu}\mid\mu\prec\lambda\}).

Appendix A
Acting by bijections up to lower-order terms

In this section we discuss a number of general results concerning linear operators which act by bijections up to lower-order terms, as introduced in Definition
1.2
.

For the following, let
U
be a vector space over
\mathbb{C}
with a fixed basis
\mathbb{B}
, and let
w:U\to U
be a linear operator. Our first result is that if
w
acts on
(U,\mathbb{B})
by a bijection up to lower-order terms, this bijection is unique.

Lemma A.1
.

For
i=1,2
, let
\xi_{i}:\mathbb{B}\to\mathbb{B}
be bijections and let
\leq_{i}
be preorders on
\mathbb{B}
such that
w
acts on
(U,\mathbb{B},\leq_{i})
by
\xi_{i}
up to lower-order terms. Then
\xi_{1}=\xi_{2}
.

Proof.

Suppose for a contradiction that
\xi_{1}\neq\xi_{2}
, and let
x\in\mathbb{B}
be minimal under
\leq_{1}
such that
\xi_{1}(x)\neq\xi_{2}(x)
. By definition, we have:

w(x)=\pm\xi_{1}(x)+\sum_{y<_{1}x}a_{y}\xi_{1}(y)

and we also know that
\xi_{2}(x)
appears with coefficient
\pm 1
in
w\cdot x
. Since
\xi_{1}(x)\neq\xi_{2}(x)
by assumption, we must have
\xi_{2}(x)=\xi_{1}(y)
for some
y<_{1}x
. Since
x
was minimal, we conclude that
\xi_{1}(y)=\xi_{2}(y)
and hence
y=x
, a contradiction.
∎

Next, we interpret bijections up to lower-order terms through the QR decomposition of a matrix. Recall that a square matrix
A
with real coefficients has a unique decomposition into a product of square matrices
A=QR
, where
Q
is orthogonal and
R
is upper-triangular with positive diagonal entries.

Suppose
(U,\mathbb{B})
is a finite-dimensional based vector space. Given a preorder
\leq
on
\mathbb{B}
, let
\preceq
be a linearisation of this order, which is a total order satisfying
x\prec y
for
x,y\in\mathbb{B}
whenever
x<y
. For an operator
w:U\to U
, let
[w]_{(\mathbb{B},\preceq)}
be the matrix of
w
with respect to the ordered basis
(\mathbb{B},\preceq)
.

Lemma A.2
.

Suppose that
w
acts on
(U,\mathbb{B},\leq)
by
\xi
up to lower-order terms. Then
[w]_{(\mathbb{B},\preceq)}
has a unique
QR
decomposition, and the orthogonal part
Q
is a generalised permutation matrix for
\xi
.

Proof.

Since
[w]_{(\mathbb{B},\preceq)}
is a square matrix with integer entries, it has a unique QR decomposition. For every
x\in\mathbb{B}
we can find
a_{y}\in\mathbb{Z}
such that

w(x)=\pm\xi(x)+\sum_{y<x}a_{y}\xi(y).

Define maps
\xi^{\prime}:U\to U
and
w^{\prime}:U\to U
by the linear extensions of
\xi^{\prime}(x):=\pm\xi(x)
and

w^{\prime}(x):=x\pm\sum_{y<x}a_{y}y

for every
x\in\mathbb{B}
, where the sign in both cases matches the sign of
\xi(x)
in
w(x)
. Then
w=\xi^{\prime}w^{\prime}
,
[\xi^{\prime}]_{(\mathbb{B},\preceq)}
is a generalised permutation matrix for
\xi
with entries
\pm 1
, and
[w^{\prime}]_{(\mathbb{B},\preceq)}
is an upper-triangular matrix whose diagonal entries are all
1
, since the order
<
implies
\prec
by construction. Permutation matrices with nonzero entries
\pm 1
are orthogonal, so

[w]_{(\mathbb{B},\preceq)}=[\xi^{\prime}]_{(\mathbb{B},\preceq)}[w^{\prime}]_{(\mathbb{B},\preceq)}

is the QR decomposition as required.
∎

We note that a decomposition of this form is extremely rare in general, since permutation matrices with entries
\pm 1
form a finite subset of the space of orthogonal matrices.

Given some based vector space
(U,\mathbb{B})
and an operator
w:U\to U
, we would like to determine whether
w
acts by
some
bijection up to lower-order terms. Suppose
A:=[w]_{(\mathbb{B},\preceq)}
, where
\preceq
is an arbitrary ordering on
\mathbb{B}
.

Corollary A.3
.

The operator
w:U\to U
acts by some bijection on
(U,\mathbb{B})
up to lower-order terms if and only if there exist permutation matrices
P_{1},P_{2}
such that
P_{1}AP_{2}
is an upper-triangular integer matrix with diagonal entries
\pm 1
.

Proof.

By the Lemma,
w
acts by a bijection on
(U,\mathbb{B})
by a bijection up to l.o.t. if and only if there is some ordering
(\mathbb{B},\preceq^{\prime})
of the basis such that
[w]_{(\mathbb{B},\preceq^{\prime})}=PR
, where
P
is a permutation matrix and
R
is upper-triangular with integer entries and diagonal entries
\pm 1
. Since reordering the basis corresponds to conjugating by a permutation matrix, this is equivalent to the existence of a permutation matrix
P_{0}
such that

P_{0}AP_{0}^{-1}=PR.

Setting
P_{1}:=P^{-1}P_{0}
,
P_{2}:=P_{0}^{-1}
and multiplying by
P^{-1}
on both sides completes the proof.
∎

This is the problem of ‘upper-triangularisation’: asking whether the rows and columns of a matrix
A
can be permuted to achieve upper-triangularity (although we also impose some additional constraints on the entries). When
A
is a binary matrix, the computational complexity of this problem has been shown to be NP-complete
[
14
]
.

Appendix B
The Kazhdan–Lusztig basis for arbitrary Coxeter groups

In this section, we prove that separable elements of a Coxeter group act on the Kazhdan-Lusztig basis of the left regular representation by bijections up to lower-order terms. Our argument is combinatorial, and builds on work of Mathas
[
34
]
, Geck
[
17
]
and Roichman
[
35
]
in relative Kazhdan-Lusztig theory. We note that a version of these results for representations of Hecke algebras (with equal parameters) can be similarly developed using recent work by Bonnafé
[
4
]
, although this involves a number of technicalities which we choose to avoid for the sake of exposition.

For the general theory of Coxeter groups and their Kazhdan–Lusztig representations, see
[
3
]
. We begin by making the necessary definitions to state our main result.

Let
W:=(W,I)
be a Coxeter system. That is,
I
is a finite set, which is the vertex set of a simple, undirected graph with edges labelled
\{3,4,\dots,\infty\}
. Given such an
I
, the associated Coxeter group
W
is generated by
s_{i}
for
i\in I
satisfying
s_{i}^{2}=1
and
(s_{i}s_{j})^{m_{ij}}=1
, where
m_{ij}
is the weight of the edge between
i
and
j
. If no such edge exists, we set
m_{ij}:=2
so that
s_{i}
and
s_{j}
commute. If
m_{ij}=\infty
, no relation is imposed. Note that every Weyl group can be realised as a finite Coxeter group.

An expression for
x\in W
is a word
(i_{1},\dots,i_{r})
with
i_{j}\in I
such that
x=s_{i_{1}}\cdots s_{i_{r}}
. We call this expression reduced if it is of minimal length among all expressions for
x
. The length of such an expression is called the length of
x
.

For
J\subseteq I
, we let
(W_{J},J)
be the induced Coxeter system. Then
W_{J}\leq W
is the subgroup generated by
\{s_{i}\mid i\in J\}
. When
W_{J}
is a finite group, it contains a unique longest element which we denote
w_{J}
. It turns out that this element is always an involution. We use this to extend the definition of separable elements of Weyl groups from Section
1.3
to all Coxeter groups by

W^{\mathrm{sep}}:=\{w_{I_{1}}\cdots w_{I_{r}}\in W\mid I\supseteq I_{1}\supseteq\cdots\supseteq I_{r},\,W_{I_{1}}\text{ finite}\}.

In the following section, we construct a canonical basis for the regular representation
\mathbb{C}[W]
, which we call the Kazhdan–Lusztig (KL) basis. Our main result will be as follows:

Theorem B.1
.

Let
W
be a Coxeter group and
\mathbb{K}\mathbb{L}
the KL basis of
\mathbb{C}[W]
. Every
w\in W^{\mathrm{sep}}
acts on
(\mathbb{C}[W],\mathbb{K}\mathbb{L})
by a bijection up to lower-order terms.

B.1.
Setup

In this section we construct the KL basis
\mathbb{C}[W]
following
[
3
]
. As before, let
W:=(W,I)
be an arbitrary Coxeter system.

Denote by
\mathcal{H}:=\mathcal{H}(W;q)
the Hecke algebra, which is the
\mathbb{Z}[q^{\pm 1/2}]
-algebra generated by
\{T_{i}\mid i\in I\}
satisfying the relations
(T_{i}T_{j})^{m_{ij}}=1
, and the quadratic relation
T_{i}^{2}=(q-1)T_{i}+q
. If
w=s_{i_{1}}\cdots s_{i_{r}}
is a reduced expression, define
T_{w}:=T_{i_{1}}\cdots T_{i_{r}}
(this is independent of the chosen reduced expression). Observe that
\mathcal{H}
is spanned over
\mathbb{Z}[q^{\pm 1/2}]
by
\{T_{w}\mid w\in W\}
, and each
T_{w}
is invertible.

There is a ring involution on
\mathcal{H}
given by the
\mathbb{Z}
-linear extension of
q^{1/2}\mapsto q^{-1/2}
and
T_{w}\mapsto T_{w^{-1}}^{-1}
for every
w\in W
. By work of Kazhdan and Lusztig
[
26
]
, there is a unique basis
\{C^{\prime}_{w}(q)\mid w\in W\}
for
\mathcal{H}
which is invariant under this involution, and satisfies some additional properties which we won’t explicitly need.

To obtain representations of
W
, we specialise this construction to
q\mapsto 1
. Observe that there is a ring isomorphism

\mathcal{H}(W;1)\xrightarrow{\sim}\mathbb{Z}[W]

sending
T_{w}
to
w
. We let
C_{w}
denote the image of
C^{\prime}_{w}(1)
under this isomorphism, and write
\mathbb{K}\mathbb{L}=\{C_{w}\mid w\in W\}
for the induced basis of the regular representation
\mathbb{C}[W]=\mathbb{C}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{Z}[W]
, which we call the Kazhdan–Lusztig (KL) basis.

The construction of a fixed basis for
\mathbb{C}[W]
gives a natural filtration of subrepresentations. We generalise this to determining a filtration of
\mathsf{Res}_{J}\mathbb{C}[W]:=\mathsf{Res}_{W_{J}}\mathbb{C}[W]
for every
J\subseteq I
, whose subquotients can be given a KL basis in a natural way. When
W_{J}
is finite, we will see that the longest element
w_{J}\in W_{J}
acts by bijection (up to sign) on the KL basis of these subquotients.

For
x,y\in W
and
i\in I
, write
x\xleftarrow{i}y
if
C_{x}
appears with nonzero coefficient in the expansion of
s_{i}\cdot C_{y}
with respect to the KL basis.

Definition B.2
.

Fix
J\subseteq I
. For
x,y\in W
, set
x\leq_{J}^{\mathrm{L}}y
if and only if there exists a chain

x=x_{0}\xleftarrow{i_{1}}x_{1}\xleftarrow{i_{2}}x_{2}\xleftarrow{i_{3}}\cdots\xleftarrow{i_{r}}x_{r}=y

for
x_{0},x_{1},\dots,x_{r}\in W
and
i_{1},\dots,i_{r}\in J
. Write
\sim_{J}^{L}
for the equivalence relation induced by this preorder.

Note that
C_{x}
appears with nonzero coefficient in
w\cdot C_{x}
for some
w\in W_{J}
only if
x\leq_{J}^{\mathrm{L}}y
, and that
\leq_{J^{\prime}}^{\mathrm{L}}
implies
\leq_{J}^{\mathrm{L}}
whenever
J^{\prime}\subseteq J\subseteq I
. Let
\mathcal{C}
be an equivalence class of
W
under
\leq_{J}^{\mathrm{L}}
, which we call a
J
-relative left cell. For
x\in W
, write
x\leq_{J}^{\mathrm{L}}\mathcal{C}
if
x\leq_{J}^{\mathrm{L}}y
for some (equivalently, for all)
y\in\mathcal{C}
. Define the subspace

\mathcal{C}^{\leq}:=\Span_{\mathbb{C}}(\{C_{x}\mid x\leq_{J}^{\mathrm{L}}\mathcal{C}\})\subseteq\mathbb{C}[W]

which is a subrepresentation of
\mathsf{Res}_{J}\mathbb{C}[W]
. We define
x<_{J}^{\mathrm{L}}\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{C}^{<}
similarly. Observe that
\mathcal{C}^{<}
is a
\mathsf{Res}_{J}\mathbb{C}[W]
-subrepresentation of
\mathcal{C}^{\leq}
, and so we can define the quotient representation

V(\mathcal{C}):=\mathcal{C}^{\leq}/\mathcal{C}^{<}.

This is a
W_{J}
-representation, and we call such representations
J
-relative cell modules of
W
. For
x\in\mathcal{C}
, let
[C_{x}]_{J}
be the image of the KL basis element
C_{x}\in\mathcal{C}^{\leq}
under the natural projection map
\mathcal{C}^{\leq}\twoheadrightarrow V(\mathcal{C})
. Observe that
\mathbb{K}\mathbb{L}_{\mathcal{C}}:=\{[C_{x}]_{J}\mid x\in\mathcal{C}\}
is a basis for
V(\mathcal{C})
, which we call the KL basis.

When
J=I
, we write
\leq^{\mathrm{L}}
(respectively
\sim^{\mathrm{L}}
) instead of
\leq_{I}^{\mathrm{L}}
(respectively
\sim_{I}^{\mathrm{L}}
), and
[C_{x}]
instead of
[C_{x}]_{I}
. We often drop the prefix ‘
I
-relative’ for cells and modules and refer simply to left cells and cell modules of
W
.

Remark B.3
.

When
W=S_{n}
is the symmetric group, the cell modules are irreducible (see Section
5.1
). However, cell modules of general Coxeter groups will be neither irreducible nor finite-dimensional in general.

B.2.
Actions on cell modules

Let
(W,I)
be an arbitrary Coxeter system. Our goal is to show that for any
J\subseteq I
with
W_{J}
finite, the element
w_{J}
acts on
\mathbb{C}[W]
by an involution on the KL basis.

We begin with the action of the longest element on cell modules for finite Coxeter groups, which forms the basis for the remainder of our calculations.

Proposition B.4

(Theorem 3.1 in
[
34
]
)
.

Suppose
W
is finite and
\mathcal{C}
is a left cell of
W
. Then there is an involution
\psi_{\mathcal{C}}:\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{C}
such that the longest element
w_{I}\in W
acts on the KL basis of
V(\mathcal{C})
by

w_{I}\cdot[C_{x}]=\pm[C_{\psi_{\mathcal{C}}(x)}]

for every
x\in\mathcal{C}
, where the sign is constant.

Remark B.5
.

In
[
34
]
, Mathas states this result only for Weyl groups, since the general case relies on the so-called ‘positivity conjecture’ to hold in each Hecke algebra. However, this conjecture has since been proven in the case of equal parameters
[
13
]
.

To extend this to parabolic subgroups, we need to compare the
J
-relative left cells of
W
to the left cells of
W_{J}
, which is possible due to a number of observations of Roichman
[
36
]
.

Fix
(W,I)
an arbitrary Coxeter system and
J\subseteq I
a subdiagram. Every
x\in W
has a unique decomposition
x=ua
, where
u\in W_{J}
and
a
is the minimal-length element of
W_{J}x\subseteq W
. We define special notation for this decomposition by setting
\mathsf{Res}_{J}(x):=u
and
\pi_{J}(x):=a
.

Lemma B.6

(Theorem 5.2 in
[
36
]
)
.

Fix
w\in W_{J}
,
\mathcal{C}
a
J
-relative left cell and
x,y\in\mathcal{C}
. Then
[C_{x}]_{J}\in V(\mathcal{C})
appears in
w\cdot[C_{y}]_{J}
with nonzero coefficient only if
\pi_{J}(x)=\pi_{J}(y)
. If this is the case, the coefficient with which it appears matches the coefficient of
C_{\res_{J}(x)}\in\mathbb{C}[W_{J}]
in the action of
w\cdot C_{\res_{J}(y)}
.

In particular, for every
J
-relative left cell
\mathcal{C}
of
W
, there is a left cell
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
of
W_{J}
such that
\res_{J}(-):\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
is a bijection, and the map
[C_{x}]_{J}\mapsto[C_{\res_{J}(x)}]
for every
x\in\mathcal{C}
linearly extends to an isomorphism
V(\mathcal{C})\to V(\mathcal{C}^{\prime})
of
W_{J}
-represetations.

By this Lemma, we are able to deduce the action of
w_{J}
on the KL basis of
\mathbb{C}[W]
up to lower-order terms. We first define the bijection that
w_{J}
will act by. As usual, we define this bijection on
W
and use the natural association of
x\in W
with
C_{x}\in\mathbb{K}\mathbb{L}
.

Definition B.7
.

Fix
J\subseteq I
with
W_{J}
finite. For
x\in W
, define
\psi_{J}:W\to W
by

\psi_{J}(x):=\psi_{\mathcal{C}}(\mathsf{Res}_{J}(x))\pi_{J}(x),

where
\mathcal{C}
is the left cell of
W_{J}
containing
\mathsf{Res}_{J}(x)
and
\psi_{\mathcal{C}}
is the involution from Proposition
B.4
.

In particular, we note that
\psi_{J}
is an involution which preserves
J
-relative left cells setwise, and hence permutes the KL basis elements of each
J
-relative cell module.

Proposition B.8
.

For any
J\subseteq I
with
W_{J}
finite, the longest element
w_{J}\in W_{J}\leq W
acts on
(\mathbb{C}[W],\mathbb{K}\mathbb{L},\leq_{J}^{\mathrm{L}})
by
\psi_{J}
up to l.o.t.

Proof.

Fix
x,y\in W
. Since
w_{J}\in W_{J}\leq W
,
C_{x}
appears in
w_{J}\cdot C_{y}
only if
x\leq_{J}^{\mathrm{L}}y
. By the first part of Lemma
B.6
, we have
x\sim_{J}^{\mathrm{L}}y
if and only if
\pi_{J}(x)=\pi_{J}(y)
and
\res_{J}(x)\sim^{\mathrm{L}}\res_{J}(y)
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be the left cell of
W_{J}
containing
u:=\res_{J}(x)
. By Proposition
B.8
,
w_{J}
acts on the KL basis element
[C_{u}]\in V(\mathcal{C})
by

w_{J}\cdot[C_{u}]=\pm[C_{\psi_{\mathcal{C}}(u)}]

with constant sign. Note that
\psi_{J}(x)=\psi_{\mathcal{C}}(u)\pi_{J}(x)
by definition. Hence, by applying the second part of Lemma
B.6
we obtain that there exist
b_{y}\in\mathbb{Z}
such that

w_{J}\cdot C_{x}=\pm C_{\psi_{J}(x)}+\sum_{y<_{J}^{\mathrm{L}}x}b_{y}C_{y}

in
\mathbb{C}[W]
. The sign only depends on
\pi_{J}(x)
and the left cell of
W_{J}
containing
\res_{J}(x)
, and hence is constant on equivalence classes of
\sim_{J}^{\mathrm{L}}
. Finally, setting
a_{y}:=b_{\psi_{J}(y)}
and noting that
y\sim_{J}^{\mathrm{L}}\psi_{J}(y)
, we can reindex the sum to obtain

w_{J}\cdot C_{x}=\pm C_{\psi_{J}(x)}+\sum_{y<_{J}^{\mathrm{L}}x}a_{y}C_{\psi_{J}(y)}

as required.
∎

We now extend this to separable elements. For a chain
\mathcal{Z}=(I_{1},\dots,I_{r})
with
I_{1}\supseteq\cdots\supseteq I_{r}
, we define
\leq_{\mathcal{Z}}^{\mathrm{L}}
in analogy with Definition
4.5
.

Definition B.9
.

If
r=1
, define
\leq_{\mathcal{Z}}^{\mathrm{L}}
equivalently to
\leq_{I_{1}}^{\mathrm{L}}
. Otherwise, for
x,y\in W
, set
x\leq_{\mathcal{Z}}^{\mathrm{L}}y
if and only if
x<_{I_{1}}^{\mathrm{L}}y
, or
x\sim_{I_{1}}^{\mathrm{L}}y
and
x\leq_{(I_{2},\dots,I_{r})}^{\mathrm{L}}y
.

Since
\leq_{I_{1}}^{\mathrm{L}}
is implied by
\leq_{I_{2}}^{\mathrm{L}}
which is implied by
\leq_{I_{3}}^{\mathrm{L}}
and so on, we have that
x<_{\mathcal{Z}}^{\mathrm{L}}y
for
x,y\in W
whenever
x<_{I_{a}}^{\mathrm{L}}y
for any
1\leq a\leq r
. Similarly, if
\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}:=(I_{2},\dots,I_{r})
, then
x<_{\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}}^{\mathrm{L}}y
implies
x<_{\mathcal{Z}}^{\mathrm{L}}y
(note that the analogous statements do not hold for the crystal orderings).

For an arbitrary separable element
w\in W^{\mathrm{sep}}
, there is a chain
\mathcal{Z}=(I_{1},\dots,I_{r})
such that
W_{I_{1}}
is finite and
w=w_{\mathcal{Z}}:=w_{I_{1}}\cdots w_{I_{r}}
. Set
\psi_{\mathcal{Z}}:=\psi_{I_{1}}\cdots\psi_{I_{r}}
, which is a bijection on
W
preserving
I_{1}
-relative left cells setwise.

Proof of Theorem
B.1
.

Define
\psi_{\mathcal{Z}}
and
\leq_{\mathcal{Z}}^{\mathrm{L}}
on
\mathbb{K}\mathbb{L}
by identifying
x
with
C_{x}
. We prove that
w_{\mathcal{Z}}
acts on
(\mathbb{C}[W],\mathbb{K}\mathbb{L},\leq_{\mathcal{Z}}^{\mathrm{L}})
by
\psi_{\mathcal{Z}}
up to l.o.t. for any chain
\mathcal{Z}=(I_{1},\dots,I_{r})
with
W_{I_{1}}
finite.

We prove this by induction on
r
. Proposition
B.8
gives the
r=1
case. Now suppose
r>1
and set
\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}:=(I_{2},\dots,I_{r})
. By assumption
w_{\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}}
acts on
\mathbb{K}\mathbb{L}
by

w_{\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}}\cdot C_{x}=\pm C_{\psi_{\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}}(x)}+\sum_{y<_{\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}}^{\mathrm{L}}x}b_{y}C_{\psi_{\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}}(y)}

for every
x\in W
. Apply
w_{I_{1}}
to both sides. On the left-hand side, we have
w_{\mathcal{Z}}\cdot x
by definition. Suppose
C_{\psi_{\mathcal{Z}}(z)}=C_{\psi_{I_{1}}(\psi_{\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}}(z))}
for
z\in W
appears with nonzero coefficient after acting on the left-hand side by
w_{I_{1}}
. There are two cases to consider.

(1)

C_{\psi_{\mathcal{Z}}(z)}
appears in
w_{I_{1}}\cdot C_{\psi_{\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}}(x)}
with nonzero coefficient. Then either
z=x
(in which case
C_{\psi_{\mathcal{Z}}(z)}
appears with coefficient
\pm 1
) or
\psi_{\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}}(z)<_{I_{1}}^{\mathrm{L}}\psi_{\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}}(x)
. In the latter case, we have
z<_{I_{1}}^{\mathrm{L}}x
and so
z<_{\mathcal{Z}}^{\mathrm{L}}x
.

(2)

C_{\psi_{\mathcal{Z}}(z)}
appears in
w_{I_{1}}\cdot C_{\psi_{\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}}(y)}
with nonzero coefficient for some
y<_{\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}}^{\mathrm{L}}x
(that is,
y<_{\mathcal{Z}}^{\mathrm{L}}x
). Using the same argument as in (1), we observe that
z=y
or
z<_{I_{1}}^{\mathrm{L}}y
. In either case, we have
z\leq_{\mathcal{Z}}^{\mathrm{L}}y<_{\mathcal{Z}}^{\mathrm{L}}x
as required.

Finally, the coefficient of each
C_{\psi_{\mathcal{Z}}(z)}
is an integer by the construction of the KL basis, and the sign with which
C_{\psi_{\mathcal{Z}}(x)}
appears depends only on the
\sim_{I_{1}}^{\mathrm{L}}
and
\sim_{\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}}^{\mathrm{L}}
equivalence classes of
x
which are uniquely determined by the
\sim_{\mathcal{Z}}^{\mathrm{L}}
equivalence class.
∎

Remark B.10
.

From this one can deduce that the cactus group of
W
(defined for general Coxeter groups as generated by
c_{J}
for
J\subseteq I
connected and
W_{J}
finite, with relations in Remark
4.10
) acts on
W
by
c_{J}\cdot x=\psi_{J}(x)
, recovering a result in
[
4
,
30
]
.

We extend this theorem to
J
-relative cell modules of
W
. For this result to make sense,
J
must be chosen large enough so that
W_{J}
contains
w_{\mathcal{Z}}
.

Corollary B.11
.

Fix
I\supseteq J\supseteq I_{1}\supseteq\cdots\supseteq I_{r}
with
W_{I_{1}}
is finite and set
\mathcal{Z}:=(I_{1},\dots,I_{r})
. Let
\mathcal{C}
be a
J
-relative left cell. Then
w_{\mathcal{Z}}
acts by
\psi_{\mathcal{Z}}
on
(V(\mathcal{C}),\mathbb{K}\mathbb{L}_{\mathcal{C}},\leq_{\mathcal{Z}}^{\mathrm{L}})
up to lower-order terms.

Proof.

We define
\psi_{\mathcal{Z}}
and
\leq_{\mathcal{Z}}^{\mathrm{L}}
on
\mathbb{K}\mathbb{L}_{\mathcal{C}}
under the association of
x\in\mathcal{C}
with
[C_{x}]_{J}\in\mathbb{K}\mathbb{L}_{\mathcal{C}}
.

By the proof of Theorem
B.1
, for every
x\in\mathcal{C}
we have

w_{\mathcal{Z}}\cdot C_{x}=\pm C_{\psi_{\mathcal{Z}}}(x)+\sum_{y<_{\mathcal{Z}}^{\mathrm{L}}x}a_{y}C_{\psi_{\mathcal{Z}}(y)}

with
a_{y}\in\mathbb{Z}
and a sign constant on equivalence classes under
\sim_{\mathcal{Z}}^{\mathrm{L}}
. Since
\psi_{\mathcal{Z}}(y)
preserves
\sim_{I_{1}}^{\mathrm{L}}
equivalence classes and hence
\sim_{J}^{\mathrm{L}}
equivalence classes, the above formula is also valid in the subrepresentation
\mathcal{C}^{\leq}\subseteq\mathsf{Res}_{J}\mathbb{C}[W]
. Applying the projection map
\mathcal{C}^{\leq}\twoheadrightarrow V(\mathcal{C})
we obtain

w_{\mathcal{Z}}\cdot[C_{x}]_{J}=\pm[C_{\psi_{\mathcal{Z}}(x)}]_{J}+\sum_{y<_{\mathcal{Z}}^{\mathrm{L}}x}a_{y}[C_{\psi_{\mathcal{Z}}(y)}]_{J}

for every
x\in\mathcal{C}
. Since
\psi_{\mathcal{Z}}
preserves
\mathcal{C}
setwise, the only nonzero elements in the above sum are of the form
[C_{\psi_{\mathcal{Z}}(y)}]_{J}
for
y\in\mathcal{C}
. In particular,
[C_{\psi_{\mathcal{Z}}(x)}]_{J}=\psi_{\mathcal{Z}}([C_{x}]_{J})
.
∎

We consider this result in the case
W=S_{n}
and
